Psychedelics and collective trauma: multisystemic resilience and recovery pathways among Nova festival survivors – a qualitative study
Authors: Guy Simon, Maya Gal-Birman, Nir Tadmor, Demian Halperin, Ziv Ben-Zion
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTObjective: On 7 October 2023, a mass-casualty attack at the Nova festival in Israel created an unprecedented convergence of collective trauma and altered states of consciousness. Many survivors were under the influence of psychedelics or other psychoactive substances at the time of the attack. This study explored how survivors made sense of and navigated recovery, with a focus on relational, community, and cultural processes.Method: We conducted semi-structured interviews with 45 Israeli survivors (25 male, 20 female), recruited through a nonprofit organization that provides trauma support. All participants had been under the influence of at least one psychoactive substance during the attack and were engaged in psychological support afterward. Data were analysed using Reflexive Thematic Analysis (Braun & Clarke [2019]. Reflecting on reflexive thematic analysis. Qualitative Research in Sport, Exercise and Health, 11(4), 589–597) with a phenomenological orientation to lived experience. Reflexivity was supported by journaling, peer debriefing, and a positionality statement.Results: Two interrelated recovery pathways emerged from survivors’ narratives: (1) Interpersonal and Therapeutic Supports (psychotherapy, therapeutic alliance, and peer solidarity) and (2) Collective Healing Practices (grassroots initiatives, community rituals, and culturally meaningful commemorations).Conclusions: Recovery from the Nova attack appears to be rooted in multisystemic and culturally significant contexts. In fact, psychedelic experiences became part of survivors’ trauma stories not in isolation but when supported by therapeutic, peer, and community frameworks. These findings underscore the importance of culturally sensitive, system-level approaches to collective trauma recovery and emphasize the need for trauma services that incorporate community-led care and nonjudgmental engagement with altered states of consciousness.KEYWORDS: Multisystemic resilience, collective trauma, psychedelics, community healing, psychedelic-informed therapy

Introduction

On 7 October 2023, a mass-casualty attack at the Nova festival in southern Israel created an unprecedented collision between altered states of consciousness and collective trauma. The assault began near sunrise (6:29 AM), when dancefloors at outdoor trance events typically reach their peak, precipitating a chaotic flight across open terrain. Survivor testimonials describe extended hiding in fields, bushes, and culverts, long escapes on foot or by vehicle, and acts of extreme violence perpetrated during the attacks. These factors compound traumatic burden (Halperin et al., 2025). This attack represents part of a broader pattern of vulnerability that music festivals face globally – research spanning five decades has identified 312 terrorist attacks targeting arts venues, with 23 specifically aimed at music concerts, including notable examples such as the 2015 Bataclan attack in Paris, which killed 89 attendees during an Eagles of Death Metal concert, and the 2017 Route 91 Harvest Festival shooting in Las Vegas, which resulted in 60 fatalities and hundreds of injuries (BBC News, 2015; Las Vegas Metropolitan Police Department, 2018; Liang et al., 2022). However, the Nova attack presents a unique dimension within this broader context. Targeting approximately 3,500 festival goers, it resulted in hundreds of deaths and left thousands of survivors grappling with psychological distress. Contemporaneous reports suggest that a substantial proportion of attendees were under the influence of psychoactive substances at the time of the attack, including classic psychedelics (e.g. LSD, psilocybin), empathogens (particularly MDMA), and dissociatives (ketamine) (Karp Barnir et al., 2025). This convergence of acute trauma and altered states represents a unique and underexplored context for examining trauma responses and recovery.

Trauma exposure can profoundly reshape an individual's psychological and neurobiological well-being. Globally, an estimated 3.9% of the population will develop post-traumatic stress disorder (PTSD) in their lifetime, rising to 5.6% among those directly exposed to trauma (Hayes et al., 2012; Liberzon & Abelson, 2016). Trauma-related alterations span multiple domains, including threat processing, emotional regulation, and memory consolidation, which often manifest as hypervigilance, emotional dysregulation, intrusive memories, and avoidance of trauma reminders (Hayes et al., 2012; Liberzon & Abelson, 2016; Shalev et al., 2017). Yet, considerable individual variability exists in trauma processing and adaptation, with some individuals developing persistent psychopathology while others demonstrate resilience (Bonanno & Mancini, 2012; Galatzer-Levy et al., 2018; Yehuda et al., 2015). This heterogeneity contributes to the persistent clinical challenge of trauma-related disorders, as conventional treatments often struggle to address the complex interplay of physiological, cognitive, and emotional responses (Haagen et al., 2015; Krystal et al., 2017). This variability has motivated extensive research into the factors that confer vulnerability or resilience, with much of this work focusing on individual-level mechanisms. While this focus on individual differences has advanced our understanding of trauma responses, the heterogeneity observed in outcomes likely emerges from the dynamic interaction between individuals and their social environments, not solely from intrinsic characteristics. Indeed, the capacity for emotional regulation, cognitive flexibility, or physiological stability may be fundamentally shaped by one's relationships, community resources, and societal structures.

Much of the existing literature on traumatic stress vulnerability and resilience has therefore emphasized individual-level factors, including psychological characteristics (e.g. regulatory flexibility, positive appraisal style) (Bonanno & Burton, 2013; Riepenhausen et al., 2022), cognitive functioning (e.g. executive functions, cognitive flexibility) (Ben-Zion et al., 2018; Schultebraucks et al., 2022; Yang et al., 2021), physiological markers (e.g. HRV, GSR) (Walker et al., 2017) and neural factors (e.g. brain structure and function) (Ben-Zion et al., 2022, 2023; Hinojosa et al., 2024; Roeckner et al., 2021). These studies have provided valuable insights into mechanisms of risk and recovery; however, they reflect a broader tendency to conceptualize resilience primarily as an individual trait or capacity. In contrast, far fewer investigations have examined how resilience is shaped by social and societal systems (Feder et al., 2019; Sippel et al., 2015). Social resilience refers to interpersonal and relational resources (e.g. social support and family cohesion), while societal resilience reflects broader structural and environmental conditions like institutional trust, healthcare access, and perceived safety (see recent review: Schäfer et al., 2024).

To address this complexity, we draw on multisystemic resilience theory (Ungar, 2013, 2018, 2021; Ungar & Theron, 2020), which conceptualizes resilience as a dynamic process emerging across multiple ecological levels. Ungar (2021) defines multisystemic resilience as ‘the capacity of systems to adapt successfully to challenges that threaten the function, survival, or future development of those systems’ (p. 10). This perspective emphasizes that resilience cannot be understood in isolation from the family, community, and cultural systems surrounding an individual. Adaptive responses, therefore, arise through person-in-environment interactions, where personal coping skills are scaffolded or constrained by relational and community supports. This framework provides a valuable lens for analysing how Nova survivors navigated recovery across interpersonal, community, and cultural systems.

Cultural context significantly shapes trauma responses and resilience pathways (Raghavan & Sandanapitchai, 2019; Ungar, 2017). Survivors of the Nova attack were connected, to varying degrees, to the Israeli psychedelic trance milieu, a cultural scene characterized by ideals of unity, collective effervescence, and expanded consciousness (Kajanová & Mrhálek, 2019; Turner, 2017). While not all survivors identified strongly with this subculture, many encountered its cultural idioms and practices. Psychedelics themselves have been shown to facilitate experiences of oneness and mystical connectedness that may support resilience and post-traumatic growth (Kettner et al., 2021; Krediet et al., 2020). These dynamics resonate with Hirschberger’s (2018) argument that collective trauma fosters processes of shared identity and meaning-making, shaping not only individual responses but also communal bonds.

At the same time, survivors of the Nova rave confronted what may be termed a ‘double stigma’: the combined social burden of being trauma victims and of being under the influence of illicit hallucinogens at the time of victimization. Israeli society exhibits significant stigmatization toward both mental health treatment following traumatic events and psychedelic drug use, creating barriers for trauma survivors who may have engaged with psychedelics. Research demonstrates that Israeli trauma survivors frequently avoid seeking mental health care due to stigma concerns, with treatment stigma serving as a significant mediator in the relationship between traumatic exposure and psychological symptoms (Haim-Nachum et al., 2025). Additionally, psychedelic users in Israeli society face substantial social condemnation, with rave culture participants experiencing ‘stigmatization as drug addicts by society and law enforcement agencies’ and expressing concerns about being perceived negatively due to Israel's strict drug policies (Milshteyn & Bensimon, 2023). This dual stigmatization may lead survivors who have used psychedelics to fear social judgment and dismissal, potentially hindering their recognition as legitimate victims requiring care (Halperin et al., 2025). Such stigma, well documented in the trauma and mental health literature (Rüsch et al., 2005), poses obstacles to help-seeking and underscores the importance of culturally responsive interventions. These cultural and clinical challenges intersect with the evolving concept of psychedelic integration, typically understood as an individual process of making sense of psychedelic experiences and applying insights into daily life (Aixalà, 2022; Bathje et al., 2022). While integration is often framed intrapsychically, trauma research emphasizes that recovery is deeply embedded in social worlds. Social connectedness is a well-established protective factor against PTSD (Charuvastra & Cloitre, 2008; Dinenberg et al., 2014). Narrative approaches also demonstrate how collective storytelling can reduce isolation and foster a sense of belonging (Sloan et al., 2013). These insights align with Ungar’s (2008) argument that resilience requires not only individual agency but also supportive families, communities, and cultures that provide resources in culturally meaningful ways.

In light of this, we reconceptualize psychedelic integration in the context of the Nova attack as a multisystemic process, simultaneously involving intrapsychic, relational, communal, and cultural aspects. In such contexts, recovery may be shaped by multiple layers of support, ranging from individual therapeutic relationships to broader community and cultural practices, highlighting the need to consider resilience as a process co-constructed within specific cultural ecologies.

Accordingly, this study aims to explore how Nova festival survivors who were under the influence of psychedelics during the attack made sense of their trauma and recovery. We approach this through Reflexive Thematic Analysis with a phenomenological orientation, interpreting survivors’ narratives within a multisystemic resilience framework. Our objective is to examine how resilience can be understood across intrapsychic, relational, community, and cultural domains in the context of collective trauma and altered states of consciousness.

Background

Multisystemic resilience theory

Resilience has evolved from a static trait model to a dynamic, interactional process. Multisystemic resilience theory emphasizes that adaptive capacity arises from the interplay of individual, relational, community, and cultural systems (Ungar, 2021; Ungar & Theron, 2020). Ungar (2021) defines multisystemic resilience as ‘the capacity of systems to adapt successfully to challenges that threaten the function, survival, or future development of those systems’ (p. 10). In this view, an individual’s resilience cannot be understood in isolation from the family, community, and cultural systems around them. Resilience thus emerges through person-in-environment interactions; for example, a survivor’s coping skills are bolstered (or hindered) by their community's support and resources. This framework provides a useful interpretive lens for understanding how survivors may navigate recovery across social networks, community organizations, and culturally embedded resources.

Cultural context and collective trauma

Cultural context profoundly shapes responses to trauma and resilience pathways (Raghavan & Sandanapitchai, 2019; Ungar, 2017). The meaning of trauma and the acceptability of coping strategies can vary significantly across cultures. Ungar (2008) emphasized that resilience requires individual agency and a family, community, and culture that can provide support ‘in culturally meaningful ways.’ The participants in this study were connected, to varying degrees, to the Israeli psychedelic trance milieu, characterized by core values such as unity and the pursuit of expanded consciousness (Kajanová & Mrhálek, 2019), principles that can meaningfully influence both their experience of trauma and their pathways to recovery. The communal ethos of this group, often described as ‘communitas’ or collective effervescence (Kettner et al., 2021; Newson et al., 2024; Turner, 2017), fosters deep social bonding through shared rituals and altered states of consciousness. Psychedelics, in this context, have been shown to promote mystical experiences and a sense of oneness that may support emotional resilience and post-traumatic growth (Krediet et al., 2020).

The Nova festival attack occurred within Israeli psytrance culture, a prominent global electronic music scene particularly embedded among post-military service adults seeking transcendence following mandatory conscription (Gristina, 2019; Schmidt, 2012). Drug use, particularly psychedelics, constitutes a normalized, integral aspect of this experience (Bonny-Noach et al., 2023). This scene exemplifies what St John (2012) terms ‘neotribal’ formations, liminal spaces of ‘ecstatic exile’ where alternative norms enable exploration of ‘being-in-transit’ within supportive structures. The community has developed sophisticated harm reduction protocols, framing consciousness-altering substances as tools for processing Israeli society's unique stressors, including security threats and intergenerational trauma (Bonny-Noach et al., 2023; Milshteyn & Bensimon, 2023; Schmidt, 2012). The attack violated both individual safety and a subcultural sanctuary where participants trusted their community to support them through vulnerable consciousness states. Experiencing extreme violence while in altered states compounded trauma, affecting memory formation and emotional processing. Israeli society simultaneously celebrates rave culture as innovative while stigmatizing drug use (Schmidt, 2012), creating complex challenges for survivors who fear disclosing their altered states might diminish their perceived legitimacy as victims (Halperin et al., 2025).

Cultural factors pose significant challenges to the recovery processes of trauma survivors (Bryant-Davis, 2019). Both psychedelic drug use and trauma-related mental health conditions carry substantial stigma in contemporary society. While research has demonstrated that even highly educated mental health professionals harbour lingering cultural stigma about psychedelics (Davis et al., 2022), more recent evidence suggests a shift, with mental health professionals beginning to endorse positive views about their potential (Kucsera et al., 2023). Nevertheless, stigmatization remains associated with increased PTSD risk and psychological distress among trauma survivors (Schneider et al., 2018). Participants in this study confronted what can be characterized as a ‘double stigma": the burden of being trauma survivors who experienced victimization, while under the influence of illicit hallucinogenic substances. This compound stigmatization manifested as fear of societal judgment, with survivors expressing concern that therapists and the broader community might perceive them as ‘drugged-out party kids’ rather than legitimate victims deserving of compassion and support. Such apprehensions potentially inhibited help-seeking behaviours, consistent with research showing that stigma acts as a barrier to treatment utilization (Rüsch et al., 2005).

Psychedelic integration as a community practice

In clinical contexts, psychedelic integration typically refers to the process by which individuals make sense of their psychedelic experiences and incorporate insights into their daily lives (Aixalà, 2022). This process is often framed as an individual endeavour, facilitated through journaling, personal reflection, or one-on-one psychotherapy. For example, Bathje et al. (2022) define integration as ‘a process in which, through effort and support, one can incorporate and retain beneficial elements of a psychedelic experience and apply them to daily life, thus moving toward greater balance and wholeness’ (p. 2). While this definition acknowledges the importance of support, the emphasis remains on personal transformation.

However, this individualized framing can obscure the inherently relational dimensions of integration, especially in the context of trauma. Social support is a well-documented protective factor in trauma recovery: Perceived social connectedness has been repeatedly shown to mitigate the development and severity of PTSD symptoms (Dinenberg et al., 2014). The ‘social ecology of PTSD’ framework (Charuvastra & Cloitre, 2008) underscores this point, highlighting how both vulnerability and recovery are deeply embedded in social networks and cultural contexts.

In light of this, integration can be reconceptualized as not merely an individual practice but a collective process rooted in shared meaning-making. For individuals navigating both trauma and substance use histories, integration often requires sustained attention to attachment dynamics, with attachment-focused interventions harnessing the healing power of safe interpersonal relationships to support long-term transformation (Lander et al., 2013). Recognizing this, our study aims to extend current integration models by exploring how they may unfold within communal, relational, and culturally embedded frameworks, particularly when trauma is a central aspect of the psychedelic experience.

Narrative approaches to trauma integration

Narrative approaches to trauma provide a valuable framework for understanding how collective integration processes function therapeutically. By creating structured spaces where individuals construct and share their stories, these approaches enable trauma survivors to have their experiences witnessed and validated by others. The power of collective processing becomes particularly evident in family contexts: Kiser et al. (2010) found that when families engage in shared trauma narrative processes, they naturally develop ways to work through difficult experiences together, interweaving individual perspectives into a cohesive collective story. This collaborative storytelling serves multiple therapeutic functions. It fosters continuity and belonging elements, especially crucial when trauma intersects with collective or intergenerational experiences. Through these shared narratives, what Hirschberger (2018) describes as a ‘transgenerational collective self’ emerges, strengthening both individual identity and social bonds. Ultimately, these narrative approaches address a core challenge in PTSD treatment: the fragmentation of traumatic memory.

Research demonstrates that in PTSD, traumatic memories remain poorly integrated within the broader framework of autobiographical memory. When asked to recall their most distressing traumatic experiences, individuals with PTSD require significantly more time to retrieve non-traumatic autobiographical memories compared to those without the disorder (Kleim et al., 2008). These traumatic memories exist in relative isolation, inadequately elaborated and disconnected from their temporal context, lacking meaningful links to the events that preceded or followed them, as well as to other personal memories (Kleim et al., 2008). Narrative approaches directly address this fragmentation by helping patients weave isolated traumatic memories into coherent life stories. Narrative Exposure Therapy, for instance, has demonstrated robust efficacy with large effect sizes both at post-treatment (g = 1.18) and follow-up (g = 1.37) (Robjant & Fazel, 2010), guiding patients to construct chronological narratives that reintegrate traumatic experiences into their broader biographical context. This integration process operates at both individual and collective levels: van de Ven's (2020) research on peer support groups reveals how ‘identity work is collectively done’ through co-constructed narratives, while in Rwanda, narrative therapy's alignment with indigenous oral traditions has proven particularly effective for genocide survivors, enabling communities to reclaim their collective narratives and facilitate healing across personal and communal dimensions (Murengera et al., 2025).

The success of these narrative treatments depends critically on the therapeutic alliance. Both patient and therapist ratings of this alliance predict PTSD symptom improvement across various treatment modalities (Saraiya et al., 2023). This relationship proves especially vital in trauma-focused work, where a strong alliance creates the safe environment necessary for clients to explore and process their most difficult experiences (Chen et al., 2020).

Purpose of the current research

Trauma research has primarily focused on individual resilience factors, overlooking the role of social and cultural systems in shaping recovery (Harvey, 1996; Schäfer et al., 2024; Ungar & Theron, 2020). This individualistic approach views resilience as a personal capacity rather than a dynamic process that unfolds across multiple levels of human experience (Ungar, 2021).

The intersection of altered states of consciousness and collective trauma remains a critical gap in therapeutic research. Current psychedelic integration practices rely predominantly on individual therapy models, failing to address the relational dimensions essential to collective trauma recovery. These individuals confront a compounding ‘double stigma’, both as trauma victims and as users of illicit substances, which creates significant barriers to accessing appropriate care.

This study examines how survivors navigate recovery after experiencing collective trauma during psychedelic use, exploring their meaning-making processes across personal, relational, community, and cultural domains. We ask: (1) how survivors reconstruct meaning from traumatic psychedelic experiences and integrate them into their broader life narratives; (2) the roles of therapeutic relationships, peer support networks, and community resources in fostering resilience; and (3) the influence of cultural practices and frameworks in shaping recovery pathways.

Method

Research design

This study employed Reflexive Thematic Analysis (RTA) (Braun & Clarke, 2019) with a phenomenological orientation. RTA was chosen because it allows for identifying patterned meanings across a relatively large dataset (N = 45), while remaining flexible enough to capture subjective accounts of trauma and recovery. A phenomenological orientation guided the analytic focus on participants’ lived experiences of trauma under the influence of psychedelics. However, the study did not adhere to strict idiographic traditions, such as Interpretative Phenomenological Analysis (IPA). Instead, we aimed to explore both individual meaning-making and shared experiential patterns across survivors.

Our epistemological stance was critical realism: participants’ narratives were treated as reflecting real experiences of trauma and recovery, while also acknowledging that meaning is co-constructed through language, cultural frameworks, and the researcher's interpretation.

Participants and recruitment

Participants were 45 Israeli survivors of the October 7th Nova festival attack (25 males, 20 females, ages late teens to mid-40s). All were under the influence of at least one psychoactive substance (e.g. LSD, MDMA, ketamine) during the attack and subsequently sought psychological support.

Participant recruitment was facilitated through Lev Batuach (SafeHeart), a trauma-informed care organization established by mental health professionals and harm-reduction specialists with extensive connections to Israel's psychedelic and festival communities. This organization served as the primary recruitment pathway due to its unique position as a trusted provider of ‘psychedelic-informed therapy’ services to Nova Festival survivors. SafeHeart's therapeutic approach, which integrates compassionate presence, trauma-informed care methods, substance/set/setting awareness, and active engagement with psychedelic experience content, enabled the organization to establish therapeutic relationships with individuals who might otherwise avoid traditional mental health services. Survivors contacted the research team directly to express interest. The researchers approached no participants without prior consent. Inclusion criteria: (1) presence at the Nova festival on 7 October 2023; (2) use of at least one psychoactive substance during the attack; (3) current post-traumatic stress symptoms and engagement in therapy; (4) Hebrew fluency; and (5) capacity to provide informed consent. Exclusion criteria were: (1) acute psychosis, (2) severe psychiatric instability, (3) inability to recall the event, and (4) current substance dependence requiring immediate intervention. The target sample (N = 45) was set to ensure information power sufficient for cross-case thematic analysis, given a focused study aim, a specific and information-rich survivor population, strong interview dialogue, use of an interpretive framework at the second analytic stage, and a cross-case (not idiographic) analytic strategy (Malterud et al., 2016).

Through our inductive analysis, we identified two primary experiential recovery pathways: (1) Interpersonal and Therapeutic Supports and (2) Collective Healing Practices. These pathways highlight the multisystemic nature of resilience, as survivors drew on relational, community, and cultural resources in their recovery.

Data collection

Interviews were conducted between November 2023 and August 2024 using a semi-structured interview guide. Topics included: immediate experiences during the attack, coping in the aftermath, relationships and support networks, community involvement, and the role of psychedelic experiences in recovery.

All interviews were carried out by the first author, a licensed clinical social worker and psychotherapist with over ten years’ experience in trauma therapy and familiarity with altered states of consciousness. While trained as a therapist, the interviewer approached the conversations as a qualitative researcher, emphasizing open-ended prompts and avoiding therapeutic interventions. The interviewer was additionally trained in qualitative interviewing (Høffding & Martiny, 2016) and followed trauma-informed interviewing practices in line with ISTSS guidelines. Interviews were conducted in Hebrew via secure video call, lasting between 42 and 118 min. They were audio-recorded with consent, transcribed verbatim in Hebrew, and translated into English by bilingual team members. Translations were cross-checked to ensure accuracy. Participants were reminded they could pause or stop at any point; most described the process as cathartic.

In keeping with reflexive thematic analysis (RTA), we did not calculate inter-rater reliability or treat coding consensus as a marker of quality, because RTA conceptualizes meaning as interpretive and researcher-situated rather than a single ‘true’ codebook to be reliably applied. Instead, quality was pursued through transparent documentation (audit trail, reflexive memos), regular analytic dialogues/peer debriefs, and attention to theoretical coherence between our critical-realist stance and interpretive practice. Co-coding of a subset functioned as a ‘critical friend’ exercise to extend reflexivity rather than to produce agreement statistics.

Interviewer positionality, quality assurance, and bias mitigation

The first author conducted all interviews and holds ‘insider’ knowledge of psychedelic and festival communities. This positionality facilitated rapport and access, but it also risks expectancy effects and social desirability. To surface and manage these risks, the interviewer maintained a reflexive journal before and after each interview (noting assumptions, emotional responses, and decision points) and kept an audit trail documenting adaptations to prompts, ordering, and follow-up questions. A semi-structured guide with standard opening/closing scripts and non-leading probes promoted consistency across participants; all interviews reiterated voluntariness and welcomed uncertainty or disagreement. Interviews followed trauma-informed principles (choice, pacing, titration of traumatic content, options to pause/stop, and resource-oriented closures). Team discussions functioned as peer debriefs to scrutinize potential interviewer influence and to refine prompts for clarity while avoiding suggestive formulations.

In addition to the above, the co-authors reviewed five early interviews in full during peer debrief meetings, providing structured feedback on the neutrality of prompts, the balance of follow-ups, and conversational pacing. Minor wording adjustments were made to the guide, which were logged in the audit trail. After each set of approximately five interviews, we examined the interviewer’s log to verify coverage of core topics (guide adherence) and noted any systematic ‘drift’; no drift was detected. We also monitored simple indicators of interview balance to identify bias; no systematic bias was observed. All participants in our study met with a licensed psychiatrist to assess their mental health status.

Ethical considerations

The Institutional Review Board at Bar Ilan University approved the study. Written informed consent was obtained. Identifying details were removed to protect confidentiality within the small survivor community. Participants were provided with referrals for ongoing support if needed.

Data analysis

We followed Braun and Clarke’s six-phase approach to RTA: (1) immersion in transcripts, (2) generating initial codes, (3) searching for themes, (4) reviewing and refining themes, (5) defining and naming themes, and (6) writing up. Codes were initially inductive, grounded in participants’ language and meanings. In a second analytic stage, themes were interpreted through Multisystemic Resilience Theory (Masten, 2021; Ungar, 2021), which functioned as a sensitizing framework to organize patterns across ecological levels (relational, community, cultural). We report this qualitative study in line with the Consolidated Criteria for Reporting Qualitative Research (COREQ) checklist for interviews (Tong et al., 2007).

Our analytic process proceeded in two stages. First, we conducted an entirely inductive coding of the transcripts, staying close to participants’ words to capture their lived experiences without imposing prior theoretical categories. This stage generated preliminary codes and candidate themes rooted in the data. Across the dataset, this inductive stage produced 278 initial codes, which were refined into 34 candidate themes. Through iterative review, these were consolidated into eight thematic clusters, which, in the second stage, were reinterpreted through multisystemic resilience theory and organized under two overarching recovery pathways. In a second stage, we conducted a more interpretive analysis by considering how these emergent themes aligned with the principles of multisystemic resilience theory (Masten, 2021; Ungar, 2021). In this sense, the framework functioned as a sensitizing concept (Blumer, 1954): it helped organize and deepen interpretation without constraining the inductive findings. This two-step process ensured that participants’ voices were foregrounded in the analysis, while also situating the findings within a broader ecological and cultural model of resilience.

Two researchers co-coded a subset of transcripts to establish analytic reflexivity, with differences resolved through discussion. Coding was then completed by the first author using MAXQDA software. Constant comparison techniques were employed not in the sense of Grounded Theory, but to ensure that codes were iteratively refined and applied consistently across transcripts. The first author occupies a dual role as both clinician and researcher. As a psychotherapist with experience in trauma and psychedelic integration, and as someone familiar with the Israeli psychedelic community, the author approached the research with insider knowledge. To manage this positionality, the author engaged in reflexive journaling, maintained discussions with co-researchers from diverse disciplinary backgrounds, and conducted member checks with five participants to confirm the resonance of the themes. This reflexive process helped guard against over-identification with participants’ perspectives and ensured transparency in interpretation.

Results

Pathway 1: interpersonal and therapeutic supports (psychotherapy, therapeutic alliance, and peer solidarity)

Interpersonal/social support, particularly therapeutic alliances and peer solidarity, played a critical role in survivors’ post-trauma adaptation. These connections provided not only emotional and practical aid but also a form of epistemic and cultural anchoring in the aftermath of a trauma experienced during psychedelic intoxication. Consistent with the idea that resilient systems maintain and restore connections (Norris et al., 2008; Ungar, 2021), recovery in this context was not solitary or purely intrapsychic, but instead embedded in relational and communal matrices.

Psychedelic-Informed therapy as cultural bridging

Nineteen (∼42%) survivors initially struggled to engage with conventional mental health services. Their hesitation stemmed not from resistance to receive help per se but from a perception that standard therapy was ill-equipped to understand their experiences, especially those shaped by altered states of consciousness. Out of the nineteen, two survivors (11%) also mentioned fears of surveillance or other institutional repercussions that compound it. One male survivor admitted:

Regarding psychological treatments, I will tell you this: If I felt safe enough to speak with complete openness, without worrying that things are being recorded and filed, without fearing consequences for my insurance, maybe it would help … That's what prevents me from truly opening up. And without genuine openness in front of a psychologist, what meaningful treatment can you receive?

This quote shows the survivor’s fear of being judged or even penalized (e.g. via insurance or legal consequences) if he speaks openly. The therapy space was not seen as neutral, but as potentially punitive or record-keeping – a perception that understandably inhibits ‘truly opening up’ and thus undermines therapy. Others described a cultural or generational disconnect with mainstream therapy, as one female survivor said: ‘We are a generation more involved in the spiritual world today: energy, spirit, consciousness … these are the treatments we need to undergo … more spiritual, higher, practical’. Here, the desire is not just for acceptance, but for resonance: therapy should speak the language of those it serves. Practices aligned with energetic, spiritual, or embodied ways of knowing, such as breathwork, somatic therapies, and transpersonal approaches, were not peripheral interests but central to this participant's conception of healing.

Of the survivors who struggled with therapy (n = 19), nine (∼47%) feared therapists unfamiliar with psychedelics would misunderstand them, writing off their altered-state experiences as hallucinations or pathology. This concern led seven of them to delay seeking help or to self-censor during therapy. In contrast, they could ‘share without judgment’ and felt validated in peer settings. In response, grassroots initiatives led by therapists and harm-reduction activists from the psychedelic community began offering care that treated altered states as meaningful rather than pathological. This approach gave survivors a genuine feeling of being understood and culturally accepted.

One participant explained what he gained from therapy: ‘First of all, to share with non-judgment, and [learn] ways of coping, how one can truly cope with it and practical tools … that’s what helped.’ Therapy provided recognition of their experience and practical strategies for managing post-traumatic symptoms, combining validation with tangible coping tools. For seven participants (∼37%), this was their first experience of psychotherapy, as a young man reflected: ‘I was never really a person who talks about myself or shares too much … so [therapy] gave me some place to do that, to release and unload this pressure that I feel.’ Thirteen survivors (∼68%) specifically stated that they would not have sought mental health care without this crisis and might not have engaged without the culturally relevant services, which spoke their language and lowered both psychological and symbolic barriers to therapy.

Participants described encountering unprecedented situations that challenged traditional therapeutic frameworks. Nineteen participants (∼47.4%) disclosed having self-medicated during the attack; for instance, one man reported taking ketamine to numb his terror while hiding. Another credited these substances with their survival, stating: ‘I'm sure that if half of us hadn't eaten something, we wouldn't have had the strength to run.’

Given these unique circumstances, participants deemed traditional therapeutic models inappropriate for their crisis. Instead, they pursued approaches that fostered profound acceptance and understanding of their altered-state experiences. This need for culturally attuned therapy became evident as participants described how psychedelics facilitated crucial breakthroughs. One interviewee reported that a DMT experience led to ‘self-awareness, self-love, and self-acceptance.’ Effective therapeutic engagement, according to participants, required bridging conventional mental health treatment with their altered-state realities in the time of the attack. They emphasized that successful integration depended on creating an appropriate ‘safe space’ and encouraging genuine self-honesty. As one participant stressed, therapeutic benefits rely on adherence to ‘set and setting’ and ‘being honest with yourself.’ These factors, drawn from psychedelic culture rather than traditional psychiatry, were considered essential for processing both their trauma and their substance-influenced survival experiences.

Peer support as collective holding

Alongside formal therapy, peer relationships proved equally vital. Survivors consistently reported that only others who had ‘been there’ could truly understand what they went through. Peer support played a dual role: It was life-saving during the traumatic experience and foundational throughout long-term recovery. During the attack itself, peer presence was protective. Whether hiding together or fleeing, survivors described co-regulating each other through touch, eye contact, and unspoken attunement. One male survivor recalled: ‘Primarily, I was not alone; I remained with my partner throughout this entire duration. I believe we provided reciprocal support to one another during the attack, even without being consciously aware of that’. Others noted that MDMA had intensified a sense of unity among those at the festival, a feeling that persisted even when they were physically alone, as pointed out by a male survivor on MDMA: ‘MDMA is a substance that induces feelings of unity … I felt close to everyone because we all shared the same fate, something profoundly deep.’ This sense of mystical connectedness dissolved isolation and generated a feeling of being part of a collective consciousness. In this case, MDMA’s empathy-enhancing effects operated as a kind of pharmacologically mediated social glue under crisis conditions.

In the aftermath, survivors gravitated toward each other. Many withdrew, at least initially, from friends or family who had not been at Nova, feeling that those who ‘weren’t there’ could not understand. As one woman survivor explained:

What supports me the most, and what heals me the most, is being with friends who were with me at the party … I can sit with [others] and feel I don’t belong … But when I’m with friends who were with me … even if we don’t talk about it, the feeling is that everything is okay, everything is like it was before.

In this account, simply being with fellow survivors restores a sense of normalcy without the need for words. The shared experience offers a kind of existential recognition and a temporary reconnection to the world as it was before the trauma shattered it. Another female survivor noted the reassurance of knowing that peer support was always available: ‘If I need to escape to someone, I know I’ll find someone who understands me.’ In contrast, when engaging with non-survivors, many felt the need to self-censor, manage others’ reactions, or hide their symptoms. Among peers, there was an ease of expression and affective safety that did not require explanation or justification.

This peer support extended beyond one-on-one friendships. Participants spoke of forming new group chats, holding regular meetups, and even living communally for extended periods. As one participant described:

It was like that for a few months: a bunch of us stayed together, showered together, ran together. Even if a person is running now, let's say, to the toilet, the second one stands with him. I don't know what, just in case something happens, so we are together.

They created safe social spaces where no one had to pretend they were ‘okay.’ One survivor explained the contrast:

I experience a lot of disconnections from the environment … I can sit with family or friends or my partner, and feel unconnected or feel the need to go home or run away … but again, when I am with the friends who were with me at the party … the feeling is that everything is okay, just like it was before.

As participants recounted, they established informal networks so that people could reach out ‘at any hour’ to vent or seek reassurance. Acts like checking in on each other, sitting quietly together, cooking meals, or just hanging out became small, daily acts of co-regulation that cumulatively created a holding environment for their trauma.

Community infrastructure also emerged in more organized ways. One participant described a dedicated drop-in centre for Nova survivors: ‘It helps me to belong to the community … there’s this Nova healing compound, there are groups. We connected and became a community.’ Another female survivor described this coming together as a turning point in her recovery: ‘The community started to connect, to become a real community, and then everything improved. Everything has improved since then.’ Being part of a community provided practical support and helped survivors rebuild their sense of identity and stability.

Psychedelic-informed therapy offered survivors a rare space of cultural resonance, while peer support provided embodied solidarity and an implicit sense of safety. In this context, healing was not an individual task but a co-created, culturally situated process sustained by webs of trust, shared experiences, and mutual recognition. These peer efforts laid the groundwork for more organized community healing initiatives to grow.

Pathway 2: collective healing practices (grassroots initiatives, community rituals, and culturally meaningful commemorations)

In the wake of the 7 October events, an urgent need for trauma response catalyzed rapid mobilization among trauma professionals and community members attuned to the unique cultural dynamics of the affected population. A constellation of NGO-led and grassroots initiatives emerged to provide community-based support systems that combined professional care with cultural attunement. Organizations such as Safe Heart (https://www.safeheartil.com/en) offered comprehensive services, including individual and group therapy, psychiatric support, retreats, and complementary medicine. Others, including Adama Tova (https://www.adamatova.org), Marpeh Space (https://www.leanaor.com/healingspace), and Izun Arena and Izun Nova (https://izun.org.il/about-us), have developed open therapeutic community spaces that emphasize group processing and somatic therapy. Still others, like Layla K'Yom Yair (https://www.laylakeyomyair.org/en), Nefesh Hofshit (https://www.freespiritexperience.org), and Slow-ness Tribe (https://slow-ness.com/activity/slowness-tribe), focus on immersive healing retreats, enabling extended, shared meaning-making in supportive and spiritually resonant environments. All were founded by community members, for the community.

Community-level resilience manifests through collective meaning-making processes and shared narrative reconstruction, unlike individual coping or interpersonal support. This mobilization included the emergence of grassroots therapeutic organizations, healing retreats, and collective rituals that facilitated what sociologist Kai Erikson (1991) termed ‘gatherings of the wounded,’ spaces where trauma survivors collectively process experiences and rebuild social fabric.

From passive recipient to active agent: community-based care as cultural infrastructure and organized routines

The grassroots therapeutic initiatives mentioned above evolved into a central component of the community’s healing infrastructure. A key element of this approach was the inclusion of multi-day healing retreats in tranquil settings, which offered dynamic group work, somatic practices, and rituals. One male survivor described how this structure itself was healing:

They created a daily schedule for us: you arrive here, you go there, you meet here … I wasn’t interested in what the treatments were. The act of waking up with something to do, a specific purpose … that’s what completely saved me.

This quote suggests that having an externally organized routine can help counter the trauma-induced collapse of time and purpose. In his view, healing came from therapy modalities and being held in a predictable environment where care was already arranged. The imposed rhythm of the day gave a sense of stability when his internal world felt chaotic. Retreats also allowed survivors to realize they were not alone in their struggles, reducing the isolating belief that their reactions were unique or ‘crazy’. As one participant said, acknowledging ‘you’re not the only one’ was ‘a bit comforting’ and gave him perspective. Beyond talk therapy, these organizations offered a range of integrative practices, including breathwork, yoga, music and dance, nature excursions, and art therapy, reflecting the holistic ethos of the community. One male survivor described building a personal toolkit from these offerings: ‘I learned all kinds of breathing exercises and techniques … and I created for myself a sort of basket of tools, a toolkit that works for me and helps me.’ This shift, from passive recipient to active agent, illustrates the transformative potential of community care that centres on empowerment through embodied practice.

Collective rituals and psychedelic reclamation

In addition to organized care, survivors engaged in various collective rituals and informal ceremonies to reframe their experience. These included memorial events where participants processed grief collectively, with one survivor sharing that ‘I concentrated on others … not on myself, but rather concentrated on others’ sadness’ during the first week, using collective mourning as a way to manage overwhelming personal grief; organized group ceremonies that participants repeatedly called ‘closing a circle’, where survivors deliberately gathered for shared healing; shared psychedelic sessions that facilitated crucial emotional processing through group structures as anoter one desrcibed: ‘We had a closing a circle, with preparation and declaration of intentions beforehand, and the MDMA was also a part’. Survivors articulated an intention to re-enter the trance party scene not merely as leisure, but as a collective act of reclamation and continuity, returning to the very spaces where trauma and community intersected. One participant described their decision to revisit Universo Paralello (Trance music festival) after the attack as profoundly transformative, explaining:

I really struggled with whether to go or not … but the very fact that I experienced MDMA again there with my friends … and that I was in a place like Universo Paralello again … it was a profound experience for me. Like … I was a different person afterward.

Similarly, another survivor emphasized maintaining their connection to the trance community through future participation, stating: ‘I even got tickets for Ozora [a trance festival], which I wanted to go to all my life … So, I will take substances at Ozora with other survivors, but to tell you how that will affect me … we can talk afterward.’ Another Nova survivor shared: ‘Next weekend we have an event of the Nova survivors, and that is truly the place where I want to take the LSD for the first time again after the attack, we are all going to do that to heal ourselfs.’

These shared psychedelic sessions often incorporated ceremonial elements. Participants described creating ‘a ceremony of intentions’ before taking substances together, transforming individual drug use into a ritualized collective practice. One group of survivors who gathered for mushrooms described their ritual: ‘My friends and I, when we finish the mushrooms, we do a kind of summary … The whole trip was a sign of gratitude, like, thank you that we are alive, thank you that we are here.’ Another person at a therapeutic retreat shared, ‘We all decided to do it on the last day … because of the shared fate … There was something very therapeutic, from the heart, and we talked honestly all the time.’ The emphasis on ‘shared fate’ highlights how these rituals served as a communal means of processing collective trauma.

One participant described how, during a shared Psilocybin session, ‘the body of the other person was moving for about two hours, like an animal releasing trauma … it was simply a return to life." Such practices were not merely recreational but functioned as rituals of repair, symbolically re-signifying what was broken and restoring a sense of collective agency. One female survivor participant described intentionally retaking MDMA at a retreat as part of a planned, collective action aimed at emotional rehabilitation. She stated her intention was: ‘It was to create a positive and corrective experience with people I feel comfortable with … we were together … in this incredibly supportive and encompassing container.’ Here, the same substance that was present during the trauma was reintroduced under radically different conditions: a safe setting (a retreat with other survivors), a shared therapeutic intention, and a collectively maintained supportive environment. The goal was explicitly defined as achieving a ‘corrective experience’ through communal processing. The act of intentionally engaging with the substance under these collectively controlled circumstances became a form of recontextualization, allowing survivors to reassert control over a drug and memory that had once been associated with terror. What was once linked to horror was intentionally woven into a narrative of redemptive self-inquiry, focusing on therapeutic progress and confronting emotional needs. Still, crucially, this was done within supportive group contexts. Groups of survivors also framed their collective returns to trance music festivals as communal rituals of defiance and reclamation. As participants who returned to parties together expressed: ‘we are not allowed to give up on the things we love, and we always need to rejoice and dance,’ while another recognized that hearing trance music again ‘is the thing that will heal me the most.’ These returns were deliberately planned as a collaborative act, with participants emphasizing the importance of facing the trauma together. Such acts served as rituals of normalization, not minimizing the trauma but collectively refusing to let it foreclose life.

This way, collective rituals became bridges between mere survival and a renewed communal engagement with the world. They allowed survivors to reclaim aspects of their lives (psychedelic use, dance, gathering) that trauma could have permanently tainted, transforming these experiences into sources of collective healing rather than reminders of horror. They also provided communal ways to metabolize grief through what participants described as structured practices of mutual support and shared meaning-making.

Discussion

This study examined how survivors of the Nova festival attack navigated trauma recovery while under the influence of psychedelics. Survivors described two main pathways: (1) Interpersonal and Therapeutic Supports (psychotherapy, therapeutic alliances, and peer solidarity) and (2) Collective Healing Practices (community-led retreats, grassroots initiatives, and culturally meaningful rituals). These findings show that resilience was not experienced as a solitary trait but as a process embedded within interpersonal, communal, and cultural systems.

Multisystemic resilience and relational pathways

Applying multisystemic resilience theory (Ungar, 2021; Ungar & Theron, 2020), survivors’ accounts highlight how relational systems provided crucial scaffolding for recovery. This multisystemic framework, which Ungar (2021) describes as operating across interconnected biological, psychological, social, and ecological domains simultaneously, helps explain how survivors negotiated resilience through multiple pathways rather than singular interventions. In this study, we showed how therapeutic relationships became safe spaces for survivors to discuss altered states without fear of judgment, bridging conventional trauma care with psychedelic experiences. This aligns with Tadmor et al. (2025), who found that mental health professionals with personal psychedelic experiences developed ‘a more non-directive, patient-centered approach, and report a heightened appreciation for the potential of non-ordinary states of consciousness in bettering their clinical practice’ (Tadmor et al., 2025, p. 7), suggesting that clinicians with firsthand knowledge of psychedelic states are uniquely positioned to create therapeutic environments where patients can discuss altered-state experiences without fear of judgment or pathologization.

Peer support emerged as a distinctive resilience pathway, operating through mechanisms that diverged significantly from conventional social support models. While social support consistently predicts trauma recovery (Bonanno et al., 2010; Norris et al., 2008), the Nova survivors’ experience reveals how shared altered states during collective trauma created what might have created an intersubjective field where boundaries between self and other dissolve, facilitating forms of mutual aid that transcend typical helper-helped dynamics. During the attack itself, survivors described navigating dual realities: the hypervigilance demanded by mortal threat alongside the expanded consciousness and heightened empathy characteristic of psychedelic states. This paradoxical state, described by one survivor as ‘being completely open while completely under terror’, required peers to function as anchors across multiple dimensions of experience simultaneously. They provided not just physical protection or emotional comfort, but what might be termed ‘ontological scaffolding’, helping each other maintain coherent reality-testing while processing extreme violence through dramatically altered perceptual frameworks. This multidimensional peer support aligns with and extends Lahad’s (2017) BASIC PH model beyond its conventional applications in community crisis response. The model identifies six coping channels, Belief, Affect, Social, Imagination, Cognition, and Physiology, that individuals and communities mobilize under stress. While traditionally applied to contexts such as war, terrorism, and natural disasters, the Nova survivors’ narratives suggest that these channels operated simultaneously and synergistically in ways particular to psychedelic states of consciousness.

The Social channel, for instance, was not limited to a sense of belonging or mutual aid but took on qualities that participants described as ‘feeling each other’s experience,’ a form of heightened intersubjectivity or emotional contagion. Under psychedelic influence, this could exacerbate collective panic, yet when skilfully navigated, it fostered remarkable solidarity and co-regulation, echoing Lahad’s emphasis on natural support systems but extending it into altered states of perception. Similarly, the Imagination channel, typically encompassing creativity, improvisation, and humour, was amplified in the psychedelic state into a literal survival tool: survivors reported co-creating protective narratives in real time, using the state’s enhanced meaning-making capacity to maintain psychological coherence amid chaos.

These accounts suggest that psychedelic consciousness does not merely activate existing coping channels but interweaves them dynamically, generating hybrid modes of resilience that are both culturally grounded and neurophenomenologically distinct. In this sense, the survivors’ collective coping illustrates an extension of the BASIC PH model into terrains where crisis and altered states of consciousness converge, highlighting the need to adapt resilience frameworks to such hybrid contexts.

Community healing and cultural infrastructures

At the community level, grassroots initiatives such as Safe Heart, Adama Tova, and other organizations developed infrastructures of care that integrated psychotherapy, somatic practices, retreats, and rituals. Survivors described these spaces as stabilizing, offering predictable routines, normalization of symptoms, and opportunities to build ‘toolkits’ for coping. This reflects Ungar's principle that resilient systems are those with redundancy, complexity, and cultural fit (Ungar, 2021). Community-based infrastructures provided multiple entry points for survivors to engage in healing, making recovery less dependent on formal institutions that were often perceived as inaccessible or stigmatizing.

The emergence of these grassroots healing infrastructures resonates with patterns documented in other post-disaster contexts. Aldrich's (2012) extensive research across multiple disasters demonstrates that communities with robust pre-existing social networks, which he terms ‘social capital,’ coordinate recovery more effectively and minimize population out-migration. However, the Nova survivors’ experience adds a distinctive dimension: the role of altered states in facilitating what Kettner et al. (2021) term ‘psychedelic communitas.’ Their research with 886 participants found that communitas during group psychedelic experiences significantly predicted increases in psychological well-being and social connectedness four weeks later. This suggests that the shared experience of trauma while in altered states may have created unique conditions for sustained mutual support networks among Nova survivors.

At the cultural level, survivors engaged in rituals of repair and symbolic re-signification. Practices such as memorial gatherings, collective storytelling, and intentional re-engagement with psychedelic use in safe, supportive contexts enabled survivors to reclaim experiences once associated with terror. These testimonies resonate with Durkheim's (1912) insight in The Elementary Forms of Religious Life that ritual practice serves not merely as social ceremony, but as the fundamental mechanism through which collective consciousness transforms individual suffering into sacred meaning, creating what he termed ‘collective effervescence’, the transformative energy that emerges when communities gather to process shared experience. This process of ritual meaning-making aligns powerfully with Alexander's (2004) theory of cultural trauma, which emphasizes that traumatic events do not carry inherent meaning but become socially integrated only when ‘carrier groups’ engage in the complex cultural work of symbolic representation and collective narrative construction. Alexander's (2004) framework provides another theoretical grounding for understanding how Nova survivors transformed individual suffering into collective meaning. He argues that traumatic events require active interpretation by ‘carrier groups’, reflexive social agents who broadcast symbolic representations and make claims about the shape of social reality. These groups function as collective meaning-makers who engage in what Alexander terms the ‘trauma process’, the cultural work of bridging the gap between raw event and social representation. Carrier groups create compelling master narratives that give significance to suffering while forging new collective identities capable of incorporating traumatic experience into ongoing social life. The community organizations and ritual practices described by survivors appear to function as such carrier groups, transforming the immediate terror of events into sources of renewed solidarity, shared purpose, and collective resilience through the deliberate cultivation of healing spaces and meaning-making practices that honour both loss and survival.

Implications for clinical practice

The findings from this study of Nova festival survivors suggest several critical adaptations for clinicians treating individuals who experienced collective trauma while in altered states of consciousness. Practitioners should establish explicitly non-judgmental therapeutic frameworks that acknowledge altered-state experiences as legitimate aspects of the traumatic event rather than dismissing them as hallucinations or pathological phenomena, as this validation significantly reduces self-censorship and improves therapeutic engagement. Given the profound therapeutic value of peer solidarity demonstrated in survivors’ narratives, clinicians should actively facilitate connections to survivor-specific peer networks alongside individual therapy, recognizing that specific healing processes occur most effectively through shared experiences with others who have undergone similar circumstances. The incorporation of somatic and experiential modalities, including breathwork, movement therapy, and other embodied practices, aligns with survivors’ expressed needs for concrete ‘toolkits’ of portable self-regulation strategies that extend beyond traditional talk therapy. For clients experiencing post-traumatic disorganization, referral to structured recovery programmes or retreats that provide external scaffolding through predictable routines may prove particularly beneficial. Clinicians must also address the compound stigma faced by survivors who were using substances during traumatic events by creating explicitly safe therapeutic spaces free from judgment about substance use.

Finally, practitioners should remain open to clients’ engagement with collective rituals, commemorative practices, or culturally meaningful recovery activities when conducted within supportive frameworks, helping assess safety while respecting the therapeutic value of these community-based healing processes. These adaptations require bridging conventional trauma treatment with culturally attuned approaches that honour the unique intersection of collective trauma and consciousness-altering experiences.

Limitations and future directions

This qualitative study has several limitations. First, retrospective self-reports are vulnerable to recall bias, particularly given the effects of psychedelics on memory encoding (Schimmenti et al., 2017). Second, the context of a mass-casualty event at a psychedelic festival limits transferability. Recovery pathways described here may be culturally specific and not fully generalizable to other trauma settings. Third, participants were recruited through an NGO, and self-selection may have led to overrepresentation of survivors who were more engaged, connected, or resilient, while the perspectives of more isolated individuals remain underrepresented. Fourth, the study did not measure clinical outcomes quantitatively, limiting conclusions about the relationship between reported resilience pathways and symptom change. Finally, relying on a single interviewer with community embeddedness may have influenced disclosures through perceived shared understanding. To mitigate this risk and enhance credibility, dependability, and confirmability, we employed reflexive and trauma-informed procedures, maintained an audit trail, and conducted peer debriefing.

Future research should address these limitations by using longitudinal and mixed methods designs, incorporating more diverse and less connected survivor populations, and systematically examining the balance between risks and benefits when trauma and psychedelics converge. Comparative work across cultural contexts and studies integrating quantitative outcome measures would clarify whether the relational and communal recovery pathways described here reflect dynamics specific to the trance milieu or broader patterns of trauma recovery in psychedelic contexts.

Conclusion

This study revealed that Nova festival survivors navigated trauma recovery through two interrelated pathways: Interpersonal and Therapeutic Supports (e.g. psychotherapy, therapeutic alliance, peer solidarity) and Collective Healing Practices (e.g. grassroots initiatives, community rituals, culturally meaningful commemorations). These multisystemic processes included psychedelic-informed therapy, validation of altered-state experiences, peer networks fostering solidarity, and community infrastructures providing structured support. Cultural rituals further enabled survivors to transform traumatic psychedelic experiences into shared sources of resilience. Taken together, these findings reconceptualize psychedelic integration as an inherently relational and culturally embedded process, extending beyond individual therapy to encompass collective systems of care. The therapeutic potential of trauma processing during altered states appears to depend on the cultural and social frameworks that hold those experiences. This challenges individualistic models of both trauma recovery and psychedelic integration, underscoring the need for system-level interventions that bridge clinical practice with community-based, culturally attuned approaches.
