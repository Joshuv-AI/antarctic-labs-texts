Molecular characterization and seasonal, sex-associated expression patterns of HSP genes in Eurygaster integriceps (Hemiptera: Scutelleridae)
Authors: Fatemeh Abdollahi, Azam Amiri, Ali R Bandani, Natraj Krishnan
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractSeasonal environmental variation may be associated with heat-shock protein (HSP) transcription in insects with annual diapause cycles. The Sunn pest, Eurygaster integriceps (Puton), occupies contrasting field and shelter habitats across its univoltine life cycle. We combined transcriptome-guided annotation, motif and conserved-domain analysis, localization prediction, fragment-based structural modeling, phylogenetic placement, and RT-qPCR to characterize partial Hsp60, Hsp70, and Hsp90 sequences and compare their whole-body transcript abundance among 4 field-sampled groups and between sexes. The translated fragments contained family-associated motifs and conserved-domain matches, showed high similarity to hemipteran homologs, and clustered with corresponding Halyomorpha halys Stål sequences, supporting provisional annotation of the 3 partial transcripts. Expression patterns differed among genes. Hsp70 abundance was higher in the summer-sampled group than in the other sampling groups across sexes, whereas Hsp90 showed a sex-dependent pattern, with higher abundance in summer-, autumn-, and winter-sampled males than in spring-sampled males and lower abundance in winter-sampled females than in spring-sampled females. Hsp60 also showed a sex-by-sampling-group pattern, but within-sex comparisons with the spring group were not supported after multiple-comparison adjustment. Because sampling phase covaried with habitat, altitude, cohort, and physiological context, these results describe associations among field-collected groups rather than isolated effects of season or diapause. The study supports provisional sequence-based annotations and identifies gene- and sex-associated transcript patterns that can guide controlled, tissue-resolved investigations of HSP regulation in E. integriceps.Keywords: diapause, Eurygaster integriceps, gene expression, heat shock protein

Introduction

Insects that inhabit temperate agroecosystems must repeatedly confront dramatic seasonal swings in temperature, humidity, and resource availability. Many species have evolved diapause—a hormonally and transcriptionally regulated dormancy program—to survive these predictable environmental extremes (Koštál et al. 2017). Diapause is a multistage, ecophysiological process (induction → preparation → maintenance → termination → post-diapause) rather than a simple metabolic “off” switch, and its phases are associated with distinct transcriptional and proteostatic states (Denlinger 2002, Koštál 2006, Koštál et al. 2017, Alborzi et al. 2025, Alborzi et al. 2026). In insects, seasonal diapause underpins population persistence across harsh summer and winter intervals and therefore strongly shapes population dynamics and economic impact on crop production (Cambron-Kopco et al. 2022, Torson et al. 2023).

Understanding how stress-responsive genes vary across the annual life cycle is a fundamental question in insect physiology. In the Sunn pest, Eurygaster integriceps (Puton), adults migrate between wheat fields and seasonal shelter habitats, and diapause-associated periods form a major component of its seasonal ecology and population dynamics. Characterizing heat-shock protein (HSP) expression across field-collected groups therefore provides a descriptive basis for investigating molecular variation throughout the annual cycle of this agriculturally important pest (Amiri and Bandani 2013, Davari and Parker 2018).

Molecular chaperones of the HSP superfamilies (notably Hsp60, Hsp70, and Hsp90) are central to cellular proteostasis during environmental stress and life-history transitions. HSPs mediate folding, prevent aggregation, and regulate turnover of client proteins; their regulation is finely tuned by developmental stage, sex, and physiological state (King and MacRae 2015, Banfi et al. 2025). In insects, HSP families display diverse roles: Hsp70 and Hsp90 are typically stress-inducible and linked to acute thermotolerance and recovery, whereas Hsp60 (a GroEL-type chaperonin) is commonly associated with mitochondrial protein homeostasis and metabolic stability (Rinehart et al. 2007, King and MacRae 2015).

Importantly, diapause entails differential regulation of HSPs: in some species, HSP transcription is suppressed during profound metabolic suppression, while in others, certain HSPs are maintained or even upregulated to safeguard critical proteins during extended dormancy (Rinehart et al. 2007, Quan et al. 2022, Torson et al. 2023). Contextualizing HSP sequence features, subcellular targeting, and seasonal expression, therefore, provides mechanistic insight into diapause physiology and stress resilience. Understanding which HSPs are mobilized during diapause—and how this mobilization differs between sexes—offers insight into the proteostatic strategies that underlie survival and post-diapause recovery.

Previous work on insect HSPs has documented both conserved architecture and highly plastic expression responses to abiotic stressors. Cross-taxon surveys show conserved N-terminal ATPase domains in Hsp70/Hsp90 families and GroEL signatures in Hsp60, while expression studies demonstrate rapid upregulation of Hsp70 and Hsp90 in response to thermal shock and variable seasonal regulation associated with diapause phases (King and MacRae 2015, Ding et al. 2021, Kmiecik and Mayer 2022, Quan et al. 2022). Recent insect studies have also emphasized sex-specific differences in stress responses and post-diapause recovery, with females often showing stronger or earlier HSP induction in contexts that intersect with reproductive preparation (Barman et al. 2023, Zhang et al. 2022).

For hemipterans specifically, phylogenetic proximity among HSP homologs suggests conserved sequence motifs across related pest species, which aids annotation and comparative interpretation (Sievers et al. 2011, Mitchell et al. 2019).

The Sunn pest E. integriceps is an agricultural pest of critical importance across West Asia and adjacent regions; its univoltine life cycle includes obligatory migration and diapause phases, with adults aestivating or overwintering in mountain shelters and subsequently recolonizing wheat fields (Davari and Parker 2018). Survival through these contrasting environments requires precise thermal adaptation, yet the molecular mechanisms underlying this capacity remain largely unknown. Despite the pest’s economic significance, molecular data on stress-protective pathways in E. integriceps remain limited.

Preliminary findings on Hsp60 and Hsp70 sequence identification and whole-body expression in E. integriceps were previously presented in a conference poster abstract (Abdollahi et al. 2024). Building on that preliminary report and the remaining knowledge gaps, the present study aimed to (i) recover and annotate partial transcript sequences corresponding to the Hsp60, Hsp70, and Hsp90 families in E. integriceps; (ii) evaluate their family-level assignment using sequence similarity, conserved motif and domain analyses, phylogenetic placement, and fragment-based structural prediction; and (iii) quantify differences in their whole-body transcript abundance among 4 field-sampling groups and between sexes. Relative to the conference abstract, the present study adds Hsp90 and provides a substantially expanded analysis integrating sequence annotation, conserved features, broader phylogenetic placement, fragment-based structural prediction, and formal sampling-phase-by-sex comparisons. These analyses provide a descriptive and hypothesis-generating basis for future studies of HSP regulation across the annual life cycle of E. integriceps.

Materials and Methods

Insect Collection and Preservation

Adult males and females of E. integriceps were collected during 4 field-sampling phases, hereafter referred to as the spring, summer, autumn, and winter groups. The spring group comprised post-overwintering adults collected from lowland wheat-growing areas near Nazarabad, Karaj, Iran (35.86°N, 50.61°E), after their return from mountain overwintering shelters to wheat fields and before the main oviposition period. The summer group comprised newly emerged adults of the current year’s generation collected while feeding in lowland wheat fields before or near crop harvest. The autumn group, collected in September, comprised adults of the same generation sampled from mid-elevation seasonal shelter habitats at 1,500 to 2,200 m. These adults had left the harvested wheat fields and migrated to seasonal shelters; thus, their collection context was consistent with diapause entry. The winter group comprised overwintering adults collected from mountain slopes above 2,200 m in the Khor Karaj region (36.06°N, 50.72°E). At the time of collection, the insects were sheltered beneath Astragalus and Dendrostellera shrubs, a context consistent with diapause maintenance.

This study used an observational field-sampling design rather than a controlled seasonal experiment. Sampling phase was inherently associated with collection location, altitude, habitat, and potentially different microclimatic conditions. The sampled groups also differed in cohort and life-history context, whereas exact adult age, reproductive and physiological condition, and diapause status were not directly measured. Diapause-related assignments were inferred from collection timing, habitat, and established species phenology. Because these factors were not independently manipulated, their individual effects cannot be separated. Accordingly, the labels spring, summer, autumn, and winter identify the field-sampling groups and should not be interpreted as independent causal effects of season, temperature, altitude, geography, or diapause.

Immediately after collection, the insects were flash-frozen in liquid nitrogen to preserve RNA integrity and limit post-collection transcriptional changes. Samples remained frozen during transport to the laboratory and were stored at −80 °C until RNA extraction.

RNA Extraction and Quantification

Total RNA was extracted from frozen adult insects using TRIzol reagent (Invitrogen, Thermo Fisher Scientific, United States) according to the manufacturer’s protocol. Each extraction utilized whole-body homogenates from 3 biological replicates per treatment group. RNA concentration and purity were measured using a NanoDrop 2000 spectrophotometer (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Wilmington, Delaware, United States), while RNA integrity was confirmed by 1% agarose gel electrophoresis and verified with an Agilent 2100 Bioanalyzer (Agilent Technologies, Santa Clara, California, United States).

Samples exhibiting an A260/A280 ratio between 1.9 and 2.1 and RNA integrity number ≥7.0 were selected for downstream analyses. Residual genomic DNA was removed by RNase-free DNase I treatment (Takara Bio, Shiga, Japan).

cDNA Synthesis

First-strand cDNA was synthesized from 1 µg of total RNA using the AddScript cDNA Synthesis Kit (AddBio, South Korea) in a 20 µl reaction containing 4 µl of 5× reaction buffer, 1 µl of enzyme mix, 2 µl of dNTPs (10 mM each), 2 µl of oligo(dT) primers, and 11 µl of RNA template. Reactions were incubated at 25 °C for 10 min, then reverse-transcribed at 50 °C for 60 min, and the enzyme was inactivated at 80 °C for 5 min.

All cDNA samples were diluted 5-fold with nuclease-free water and stored at −20 °C. Negative control reactions without reverse transcriptase were included to confirm the absence of genomic DNA contamination. The reference gene β-actin (GenBank accession no. OR783484.1) was amplified to verify cDNA quality before quantitative analyses.

Primer Design and Validation

Gene-specific primers were designed from transcriptome-derived sequences using Primer3Plus version 2.4.0 (Table 1) with melting temperatures of 58 to 62 °C and amplicon lengths of 100 to 150 bp. Thermodynamic properties and secondary structures were verified using OligoAnalyzer (Integrated DNA Technologies, United States). Primer specificity was confirmed via NCBI Primer-BLAST against E. integriceps transcriptomic data, ensuring unique 3′-end binding and absence of cross-hybridization. Amplification efficiency (95% to 105%) and linearity (R² > 0.99) were confirmed via standard curves in quantitative PCR assays.

Each primer pair yielded a single, specific amplicon with clean melt curves and single peaks, consistent with MIQE (Minimum Information for Publication of Quantitative Real-Time PCR Experiments) standards.

Bioinformatic Analysis

Partial cDNA sequences for Hsp60, Hsp70, and Hsp90 were extracted from the E. integriceps transcriptome. Open reading frames (ORFs) were determined using ORF Finder (NCBI, https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/orffinder/). Theoretical molecular mass and isoelectric point (pI) were calculated using ExPASy Compute pI/Mw (https://web.expasy.org/compute_pi/).

Signal peptides and cleavage sites were predicted using SignalP 6.0 (https://services.healthtech.dtu.dk/service.php? SignalP), while subcellular localization was inferred using DeepLoc 2.0 (https://services.healthtech.dtu.dk/services/DeepLoc-2.0/) (Almagro Armenteros et al. 2017).

Homology searches were performed using BLASTX (https://blast.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/Blast.cgi) against the NCBI nonredundant protein database (Altschul et al. 1990). Multiple sequence alignments of E. integriceps HSPs and orthologous sequences from other insects were generated using Clustal Omega (EMBL-EBI, https://www.ebi.ac.uk/Tools/msa/clustalo/), and alignments were visualized using GeneDoc (Nicholas et al. 1997). Conserved motifs were identified using MEME Suite v5.5.3, with parameters set to detect 3 motifs of 15 to 50 amino acids. Conserved domains were verified using the NCBI Conserved Domain Database (CDD) with an E-value cutoff of 0.01.

Phylogenetic relationships were inferred from aligned amino acid sequences using the maximum-likelihood method implemented in MEGA12 under the Jones–Taylor–Thornton model with a discrete gamma distribution. Branch support was assessed using 1,000 bootstrap replicates (Kumar et al. 2024).

3D protein structure predictions were performed using ColabFold v1.5.5 (Mirdita et al. 2022), an implementation of AlphaFold2 (Jumper et al. 2021) optimized for Google Colab. The top-ranked model (model rank_1) with the highest confidence score (pLDDT > 85) was selected for structural visualization.

All bioinformatic analyses were based on the available partial nucleotide or translated sequences. Consequently, motif, domain, signal-peptide, localization, phylogenetic, and structural outputs were interpreted only for the represented sequence regions and were not considered evidence of complete protein architecture, definitive cellular localization, secretion status, or biochemical function.

Quantitative Real-Time PCR

Quantitative expression analyses of Hsp60, Hsp70, and Hsp90 were performed using a Rotor-Gene Q system (Qiagen, Hilden, Germany) with RealQ Plus 2× Master Mix Green (Ampliqon, Denmark). We used the primers listed in Table 2. Each 20 µl reaction contained 10 µl of master mix, 0.5 µl of each primer (10 µM), 2 µl of cDNA template, and nuclease-free water to a final volume of 20 µl. Cycling parameters were as follows: initial denaturation at 95 °C for 15 min, followed by 40 cycles of 95 °C for 15 s, 62 °C for 20 s, and 72 °C for 20 s.

A melting curve analysis (65 to 94 °C, 1 °C increments) confirmed the specificity of the amplification products. Relative expression levels were normalized to β-actin, and reactions were performed with 3 biological and 3 technical replicates per treatment.

Gene Expression Quantification

Relative transcript abundance was determined using the 2^–ΔΔCt method (Livak and Schmittgen 2001). Expression levels for each season were compared against spring samples (calibrator = 1.0). ΔCt values were calculated as (Ct_target − Ct_reference), and ΔΔCt values as (ΔCt_sample − ΔCt_calibrator). Fold changes were expressed as 2^−ΔΔCt.

To minimize variability, all samples within a given treatment were analyzed simultaneously under identical thermal cycling conditions, and amplification efficiency was validated prior to analysis.

Statistical Analysis

All statistical analyses were performed using R (version 4.3.0) with the following packages: ggplot2 for data visualization, dplyr for data manipulation, gridExtra for arranging plots, ggpubr for publication-ready graphics, and broom for statistical model outputs. Relative gene expression was calculated using the 2^(−ΔΔCt) method, with spring samples serving as the calibrator (baseline = 1× fold-change). Statistical analyses of sampling-phase, sex, and interaction effects were performed on ΔCt values. When the sex × sampling-phase interaction was significant, Tukey-adjusted pairwise comparisons were performed among all 8 sex × sampling-phase cell means. The results report the biologically relevant same-phase sex contrasts and within-sex contrasts against spring. Data are presented as mean ± SE from 3 biological replicates. Two-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) was conducted for each gene to assess the effects of sex, season, and their interaction on expression levels. Post-hoc comparisons were performed using Tukey’s HSD test when significant main effects or interactions were detected (P < 0.05).

Results

Molecular Characterization of E. integriceps Heat Shock Protein Genes

Partial cDNA sequences corresponding to the Hsp60, Hsp70, and Hsp90 families were recovered from the E. integriceps transcriptome. The available Hsp60, Hsp70, and Hsp90 fragments were 1,160, 1,077, and 470 bp in length and were deposited in GenBank under accession numbers PQ560713.1, PQ560714.1, and PQ560715.1, respectively.

The partial Hsp60 cDNA encoded a deduced polypeptide of 571 amino acids with a calculated molecular mass of 60.37 kDa and an pI of 5.29, suggesting an overall acidic character. ORF Finder analysis predicted a putative ORF of 1,716 bp. DeepLoc 2.0 assigned the highest localization probability to the mitochondrion, whereas SignalP 6.0 detected no classical signal peptide or predicted cleavage site within the available translated fragment (Supplementary Fig. S1). Because the sequence is partial and may lack the extreme N-terminus, these computational predictions do not establish the localization or secretion status of the corresponding protein.

The partial Hsp70 cDNA had a deduced protein size of 722 amino acids, a molecular mass of 83.05 kDa, and a pI of 4.99. The predicted putative ORF was 2,169 bp. DeepLoc 2.0 assigned the highest localization probability to the cytoplasm, whereas SignalP 6.0 detected no classical signal peptide within the available translated sequence (Fig. 1A and B). These computational predictions do not experimentally establish the intracellular localization or secretion status of the corresponding protein.

Predicted subcellular localization and signal-peptide analysis of Hsp70 from E. integriceps. The partial cDNA of Hsp90 was 470 bp, encoding 173 amino acids with a molecular weight of 19.46 kDa and a pI of 4.67. The putative ORF was estimated at 522 bp. DeepLoc 2.0 assigned the highest localization probability to the cytoplasm, whereas SignalP 6.0 detected no classical signal peptide within the available translated fragment (Supplementary Fig. S2). Because the sequence is partial, these predictions do not establish the localization or secretion status of the corresponding protein.

Together, these analyses identified sequence features consistent with provisional family-level annotation of the 3 E. integriceps HSP fragments, including acidic predicted pIs, fragment-level localization predictions, and no detected classical signal peptides. These computational outputs do not establish the complete protein architecture, cellular localization, secretion status, or biochemical function of the corresponding proteins.

Sequence Similarity and BLAST Analysis

BLASTX homology searches revealed high sequence identity between E. integriceps HSPs and homologs from other Hemipteran species. EiHsp90 shared 97.79% amino acid identity with Halyomorpha halys (XP_014284945.1), 97.06% with Corythucha ciliata (ARW29612.1), and 96.32% with Tytthus chinensis (AXU24973.1) (Fig. 2A). EiHsp70 showed 94.81% identity with H. halys (XP_014284945.1), 88.24% with Cimex lectularius (XP_014260468.1), and 87.73% with Periplaneta americana (XP_069692673.1) (Supplementary Fig. S3). EiHsp60 exhibited 95.48% identity with H. halys (XP_014273033.1), 89.25% with T. chinensis (AXU24980.1), and 87.70% with Laodelphax striatellus (QGP73637.1) (Supplementary Fig. S4).

Conserved Domains and Motif Analysis

Sequence analysis of the translated EiHsp90 fragment identified conserved sequence features characteristic of the Hsp90 family. Three conserved motifs were identified: motif 1 (TGIGMTKADLVNNLGTIAKSFMEALQGADISMIGQFGVGFYSAYL), motif 2 (ADKVTVSSKHNDEQYVSAWNRERIMKQALRDSSTMGYMAAKKHLEIN), and motif 3 (RLV DSPCCVLTSQYQWTANMERIMKQALRDSSTMGYMAAKKHLEINPDH) (Fig. 2B). CDD analysis of the available partial Hsp90-derived sequences detected significant matches to an HATPase-like ATP-binding domain and an Hsp90-family domain, providing additional support for their assignment to the Hsp90 family (Supplementary Fig. S5). Because the sequences are incomplete, these results do not establish the full N-terminal, middle, and C-terminal domain architecture or demonstrate ATP hydrolysis, client–protein interaction, dimerization, or chaperone activity.

Amino acid sequence alignment of the translated EiHsp70 fragment with homologous insect Hsp70 sequences revealed conserved regions characteristic of the Hsp70 family. Specifically, 3 conserved motifs were identified: motif 1 (HQGERMA…IVHSARD), motif 2 (WQVGSDGK…FVNDSQ), and motif 3 (QGIDVTKD…GSPKHMN) (Supplementary Fig. S3). CDD analysis detected a significant match to the Hsp70/actin-like ATPase superfamily, supporting assignment of the available fragment to the Hsp70 family. However, these sequence-based results do not establish the complete nucleotide-binding, substrate-binding, and lid-domain organization or demonstrate ATP-dependent conformational cycling, substrate recognition, or protein-refolding activity (Supplementary Fig. S3).

Alignment of the translated EiHsp60 fragment with homologous insect sequences identified conserved regions characteristic of the GroEL-like chaperonin family. MEME analysis identified 3 conserved motifs: motif 2 (YAKDVRFGPEVRGLMLQGVDILADAVAVTMGPKGRNVILEQSWGSPKITK), motif 1 (MKFDRGYISPYFINTSKGAKVEFQDCLLLLSEKKISSVQSIIPALELANS), and motif 3 (MPCMTIAQNAGVDAAVVVNKVLDSEGDMGYDAMNNEYVNMIERGIIDPTK) (Supplementary Fig. S4). CDD analysis detected a significant GroEL-like chaperonin-superfamily match, providing further support for assignment of the fragment to the Hsp60 family. Although these motifs occur within conserved chaperonin regions, their presence alone does not establish the complete domain architecture, mitochondrial localization, oligomerization, substrate interaction, or chaperone activity of the corresponding full-length protein.

Motif and conserved-domain analyses identified family-associated sequence features within the available EiHsp60, EiHsp70, and EiHsp90 fragments. These findings support fragment-level assignment to the corresponding Hsp families but do not establish the complete structures, cellular localization, biochemical activity, or physiological functions of the full-length proteins.

Within the available sequence region, the EiHsp90 model was compatible with the conserved core architecture of the Hsp90 family (Fig. 3). Likewise, the EiHsp70 and EiHsp60 models were consistent with Hsp70-family and GroEL-like chaperonin core folds, respectively (Supplementary Figs. S6 and S7). Phylogenetic Support for Provisional HSP-Family Annotation

Maximum-likelihood analyses placed each E. integriceps HSP fragment with hemipteran homologs. In all 3 trees, the E. integriceps sequence formed a sister pair with the corresponding H. halys homolog, with bootstrap support of 98% for HSP90 and 100% for both HSP70 and HSP60 (Fig. 4; Supplementary Figs. S8 and S9). These placements were concordant with the BLASTX and conserved-feature results and were used only as complementary support for provisional family-level annotation. No species-level or broader evolutionary relationships were inferred from these fragment-based analyses.

Whole-Body HSP Transcript Abundance across Sampling Phases and Sexes

Two-way ANOVA of ΔCt values revealed gene-specific associations with sex and sampling phase (Fig. 5; Table 3). For Hsp60, the sex × sampling-phase interaction was significant (F(3,16) = 4.952, P = 0.0128, partial η² = 0.481), as was the sex effect (F(1,16) = 36.237, P = 1.78 × 10⁻⁵, partial η² = 0.694). The sampling-phase effect was not significant (F(3,16) = 1.768, P = 0.194). Given the significant interaction, the overall sex effect was interpreted together with the Tukey-adjusted cell-mean comparisons. On the ΔCt scale, transcript abundance was higher in females than in males in spring (adjusted P = 0.000690). The same-phase sex contrasts were not significant in summer, autumn, or winter (all adjusted P ≥ 0.0965).

The display-normalized summer means for Hsp70 were 55.31 ± 7.70 in males and 16.36 ± 2.81 in females. These values were calculated using separate sex-specific spring calibrators. They therefore describe the summer departure from the corresponding spring baseline within each sex and should not be interpreted as a direct quantitative comparison of the response magnitude between males and females.

For Hsp90, sampling phase (F(3,16) = 23.274, P = 4.45 × 10⁻⁶, partial η² = 0.814) and the sex × sampling-phase interaction (F(3,16) = 24.840, P = 2.92 × 10⁻⁶, partial η² = 0.823) were significant. The overall sex effect was not significant (F(1,16) = 1.392, P = 0.255). On the ΔCt scale, the same-phase comparisons indicated higher normalized transcript abundance in females than in males in spring (adjusted P = 0.000111). In contrast, abundance was higher in males in summer (adjusted P = 0.0196) and winter (adjusted P = 0.0345). The sexes did not differ significantly in autumn (adjusted P = 0.825). This crossover pattern accounts for the significant interaction and the absence of an overall sex effect.

Within males, Hsp90 transcript abundance was higher than in spring during summer (adjusted P = 4.09 × 10⁻⁷), autumn (adjusted P = 0.000659), and winter (adjusted P = 0.00287). The summer mean also exceeded the autumn (adjusted P = 0.00550) and winter means (adjusted P = 0.00124). In females, summer and autumn did not differ significantly from spring, whereas normalized winter abundance was lower (adjusted P = 0.00116). The corresponding display-normalized means were 50.54, 8.92, and 6.45 in summer, autumn, and winter males and 0.92, 0.40, and 0.12 in females, respectively, relative to the within-panel spring baseline of 1.00.

Model diagnostics did not detect significant departures from residual normality (Shapiro–Wilk P = 0.127 to 0.625) or homogeneity of cell variances (Brown–Forsythe P = 0.688 to 0.999). These diagnostic tests have limited sensitivity because each sex × sampling-phase cell contained only 3 biological replicates. Table 3 therefore presents the complete ΔCt-based ANOVA results, whereas the fold-change values in Fig. 5 are retained for descriptive within-panel visualization.

Discussion

The sequence analyses provide a reliable molecular foundation for interpreting the quantitative real-time PCR results. The agreement among BLASTX similarity, conserved motifs and domains, fragment-based structural models, and maximum-likelihood placement supports the provisional assignment of the recovered sequences to the Hsp60, Hsp70, and Hsp90 families. In all 3 trees, the close association of the E. integriceps fragments with the corresponding H. halys sequences provides consistent, hemipteran-focused support for these annotations. Because HSP sequences are strongly conserved, the phylogenetic results are best viewed as confirmatory evidence rather than as a separate evolutionary finding. The principal biological contribution of this study is therefore the identification of distinct gene-, sampling-phase-, and sex-associated transcript-abundance patterns in E. integriceps (King and MacRae 2015).

Among the 3 genes, Hsp70 showed the clearest sampling-phase pattern shared by both sexes. Its transcript abundance was higher in the summer-sampled group than in the spring-, autumn-, and winter-sampled groups. The nonsignificant sex × sampling-phase interaction indicates that the general structure of this phase-associated pattern was similar in males and females. At the same time, the significant sex effect, with higher normalized abundance in females across sampling phases, indicates an overall difference between sexes superimposed on the shared phase pattern. HSP70 proteins have a central role in stabilizing, refolding, and protecting proteins under conditions that disrupt cellular proteostasis. Controlled studies have similarly demonstrated rapid Hsp70 induction following thermal exposure and have associated this response with improved thermotolerance and recovery in insects (Jin et al. 2020, Quan et al. 2022). The elevated abundance detected in summer-sampled E. integriceps is therefore compatible with increased deployment of HSP70-mediated cellular protection under the environmental and physiological conditions represented by this group (King and MacRae 2015, Quan et al. 2022).

Hsp90 exhibited the most pronounced sex-dependent pattern. Females showed higher transcript abundance than males in spring, whereas males showed higher abundance in summer and winter. Within males, abundance was higher in the summer-, autumn-, and winter-sampled groups than in the spring-sampled group, with the strongest elevation occurring in summer. Females followed a different trajectory, including lower abundance in winter than in spring. This crossover pattern explains the significant interaction and shows that sex is an important component of Hsp90 variation across annual-cycle sampling contexts. HSP90 supports the stability and maturation of client proteins involved in signal transduction, development, and responses to environmental challenges. Its transcript abundance can vary substantially among temperatures, developmental stages, and tissues in insects (Ding et al. 2021, Ruan et al. 2024).

Gene-specific thermal regulation of HSPs has also been reported in the hemipteran Bemisia tabaci, supporting the relevance of differentiated HSP responses within Hemiptera (Barman et al. 2023, Ruan et al. 2024). The strong sex-dependent pattern identified here makes Hsp90 a particularly informative candidate for future studies of tissue-specific and physiological transitions in E. integriceps.

Hsp60 displayed a third and distinct profile. Its significant sex × sampling-phase interaction was expressed primarily through higher transcript abundance in females than in males in spring, followed by an absence of statistically supported sex differences during the other sampling phases. Although the male means increased numerically from spring to summer, autumn, and winter, none of the within-sex comparisons with spring remained significant after adjustment for multiple comparisons.

This result is biologically informative because Hsp60 did not reproduce either the shared summer-associated pattern of Hsp70 or the pronounced sex-dependent trajectory of Hsp90. HSP60-family chaperonins contribute to protein folding and cellular proteostasis, but individual HSP families may differ markedly in the magnitude and timing of their transcriptional responses (King and MacRae 2015). Controlled experiments have likewise shown that Hsp60 can exhibit a weaker or shorter transcriptional response than Hsp70 and Hsp90, while diapause studies have demonstrated that HSP families are not necessarily regulated in parallel (Rinehart et al. 2007, Quan et al. 2022).

The Hsp60 findings therefore provide an important internal contrast and reinforce the conclusion that HSP regulation in E. integriceps is gene-specific.

Taken together, the 3 transcript profiles reveal a differentiated HSP signature across the sampled portions of the annual life cycle. Hsp70 showed a broadly shared phase-associated pattern, Hsp90 exhibited strong sex-dependent reorganization, and Hsp60 showed a more restricted pattern dominated by the spring sex difference. This heterogeneity agrees with evidence that insect HSP families respond independently to thermal exposure, diapause, and recovery rather than undergoing a uniform transcriptional shift (Rinehart et al. 2007, Quan et al. 2022). For an agriculturally important pest that moves between crop fields and seasonal shelters, these results provide a species-specific molecular baseline for examining cellular-protection pathways across contrasting life-history contexts. They also identify Hsp70 and Hsp90 as priority candidates for investigating the molecular physiology associated with field activity, migration, shelter occupancy, and overwintering in E. integriceps (King and MacRae 2015, Davari and Parker 2018).

Several limitations define the scope of these interpretations. The observational sampling design means that sampling phase covaried with habitat, altitude, cohort, feeding conditions, reproductive status, and probable diapause state. Accordingly, the observed transcript patterns represent associations among field-collected groups and cannot be attributed to a single environmental factor. Whole-body RNA analysis does not identify the tissues contributing to the sex-associated patterns.

Moreover, the 3 biological replicates per sex × sampling-phase group limit the precision of effect estimates, and 4 discrete sampling phases do not resolve continuous temporal dynamics. Transcript abundance also does not necessarily correspond directly to protein abundance or chaperone activity (Buccitelli and Selbach 2020, Teets et al. 2023). These considerations define clear directions for controlled common-environment experiments, tissue-resolved expression analyses, larger independent samples, and protein-level validation, while the statistically supported gene-specific patterns provide a strong foundation for these investigations.

This study establishes provisional molecular annotations for partial Hsp60, Hsp70, and Hsp90 transcripts and demonstrates that HSP transcript variation in field-collected E. integriceps is structured by gene identity, sampling phase, and sex. The shared summer-associated elevation of Hsp70, the pronounced sex-dependent pattern of Hsp90, and the distinct profile of Hsp60 collectively show that the HSP system is a differentiated component of annual physiological variation in this species. These findings expand the molecular information available for an economically important hemipteran pest and identify specific HSP genes for targeted investigation of stress physiology and annual life-history transitions. By extending established models of gene-specific HSP regulation to E. integriceps, this study provides a valuable foundation for future mechanistic studies and a clearer understanding of molecular variation across the species’ annual cycle.
