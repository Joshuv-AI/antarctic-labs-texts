Deep learning driven early detection of lung cancer from CT images using transfer learning approaches
Authors: Vishwas V. Patange, Jagadish B. Jadhav, Sanjay L. Nalbalwar, Priyanka V. Deshmukh
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Lung cancer remains one of the foremost causes of cancer-related mortality worldwide, primarily due to its frequent late-stage diagnosis and the limited efficacy of available treatments at advanced stages. This study seeks to advance early detection through the application of deep learning techniques for the automated classification of lung CT images into benign, malignant, and normal categories. A comprehensive methodological framework was adopted, encompassing data acquisition, preprocessing, model development, training, validation, and performance evaluation, with patient-level data splitting employed to prevent slice-level data leakage. Among six tested architectures (VGG16, Custom CNN, MobileNetV2, ResNet50, InceptionV3, and EfficientNetB0), the fully fine-tuned VGG16 model optimized via transfer learning and trained on the IQ-OTH/NCCD lung cancer dataset exhibited the strongest overall performance, achieving a mean test accuracy of 89.39% ± 2.10% (test loss 0.329) across three independent runs, and 89.70% ± 2.75% under 5-fold patient-grouped cross-validation. One-way ANOVA (F = 17.55, p < 0.0001) followed by Tukey’s HSD post-hoc test confirmed that VGG16 significantly outperformed ResNet50 and EfficientNetB0. To enhance interpretability, Grad-CAM visualizations were generated for all three classes, indicating that the model’s attention broadly corresponded to anatomically relevant lung regions. Trained and evaluated on a Google Colab GPU environment, the proposed system demonstrates potential as an assistive tool for automated lung cancer screening, warranting further validation on larger and multi-institutional datasets before clinical application.

Introduction

Lung cancer remains one of the most prevalent and lethal cancers globally, consistently ranking among the leading causes of cancer-related deaths. Its high mortality rate primarily results from late-stage detection, when available treatments such as surgery, chemotherapy, and radiotherapy are far less effective. Therefore, early and accurate diagnosis is essential to improving survival outcomes. Traditional diagnostic methods, including chest X-rays, Computed Tomography (CT) scans, and tissue biopsies, face several limitations such as inconsistent accuracy, high cost, limited availability in low-resource settings, and risks related to radiation and invasiveness. These challenges emphasize the necessity for more advanced, precise, and non-invasive diagnostic alternatives.

In recent years, Artificial Intelligence (AI), particularly Machine Learning (ML), has revolutionized medical diagnostics by enabling data-driven disease detection and decision support. ML algorithms are capable of analyzing extensive and complex medical datasets, facilitating predictive analytics and automated diagnosis. By integrating radiological images, genetic data, and clinical histories, these systems extract intricate patterns that often remain undetected through traditional analysis.

Among ML methods, Deep Learning (DL) a specialized subset has demonstrated outstanding performance in medical imaging. Convolutional Neural Networks (CNNs), in particular, are effective at automatically learning hierarchical features from raw image data, enabling the identification of subtle abnormalities in medical scans. CNN-based models have proven highly effective for segmentation, nodule detection, and classification of lung lesions1.

Despite remarkable progress, most existing studies on lung cancer detection emphasize binary classification distinguishing benign from malignant nodules. In clinical practice, however, a more nuanced classification is necessary to address the diverse spectrum of lung abnormalities. To bridge this gap, the present study focuses on developing advanced deep learning architectures for multi-class classification of lung nodules in CT images. Specifically, it categorizes nodules into three clinically significant groups: Benign, Malignant, and Normal, aligning more closely with real-world diagnostic requirements and enabling improved treatment planning.

A publicly available annotated dataset of lung CT images was sourced from Kaggle2, providing a rich and diverse foundation for model training and validation. Using this dataset, several architectures MobileNetV2, ResNet50, EfficientNetB0, and a custom CNN were implemented and compared based on their training and validation accuracy and loss. This evaluation enabled a detailed analysis of each model’s diagnostic performance and generalization capabilities.

The overarching aim of this research is to develop a robust, efficient, and clinically applicable deep learning framework for automated lung cancer detection. By enhancing screening precision, accelerating diagnostic workflows, and minimizing false positives, the proposed system seeks to support clinicians in informed decision-making ultimately improving patient care and clinical outcomes3.

Literature review

Lung cancer detection using computed tomography (CT) scans has been extensively explored due to the high spatial resolution CT provides for visualizing pulmonary nodules and other lung abnormalities. As one of the most aggressive cancers worldwide, lung cancer demands early and accurate detection strategies to improve survival rates. Despite the widespread clinical use of traditional diagnostic approaches including chest X-rays, CT scans, and biopsies, these methods suffer from several drawbacks. Their diagnostic accuracy can be inconsistent, particularly in early-stage detection, and accessibility to advanced imaging technology remains limited in many regions. Moreover, conventional diagnostics are costly and often involve invasive procedures, exposing patients to risks such as ionizing radiation and procedural complications. These limitations have fueled research into advanced computational approaches, particularly machine learning (ML) and deep learning (DL), to support automated diagnosis, early detection, and improved clinical outcomes in lung cancer care. Machine learning techniques have demonstrated promising results in medical image analysis by leveraging pattern recognition and data-driven insights. A critical factor in enhancing ML model performance is the selection of relevant features from imaging data. Feature selection not only improves classification accuracy but also reduces model complexity, making the algorithms more interpretable and computationally efficient. Syed et al.4 highlighted the role of feature selection in lung cancer detection by mapping features extracted from CT scans to specific lung regions. Their study applied traditional ML algorithms to these selected features, which significantly enhanced the models’ diagnostic performance. Building upon this concept, Dash et al.5 proposed a hybrid classification framework combining the Semantic Network Classifier and Naïve Bayes. This multi-classifier approach underscored the advantage of ensemble methods in improving prediction precision, showcasing how combining classifiers could mitigate individual model limitations. Beyond individual classifiers, researchers have explored the comparative performance of various ML algorithms to identify optimal models for lung cancer diagnosis. Günaydin et al.6 conducted a comprehensive comparison involving classifiers like Principal Component Analysis (PCA), k-Nearest Neighbors (KNN), and Support Vector Machines (SVM). Their findings revealed that Artificial Neural Networks (ANNs) and SVMs outperformed other classifiers in terms of diagnostic accuracy, positioning them as viable tools for clinical deployment. In tackling the challenge of detecting small-sized pulmonary nodules, often a critical factor in early diagnosis, Makaju et al.7 employed watershed segmentation. Their method successfully enhanced the detection rate of small nodules, highlighting the importance of advanced image segmentation techniques in early-stage lung cancer detection, where traditional methods often fall short. The rise of deep learning has ushered in transformative changes in medical imaging analysis. Convolutional Neural Networks (CNNs), a class of DL models, have been particularly effective in learning hierarchical feature representations directly from raw image data without the need for manual feature extraction. These models can capture intricate spatial patterns, enabling them to outperform traditional ML methods in various image-based diagnostic tasks. Zhang et al.8 introduced a multi-scene deep learning model that utilized a CNN with four input channels, allowing for the analysis of multiple image structures simultaneously. This architecture improved detection accuracy by accounting for the diverse visual characteristics of pulmonary nodules across different CT slices. Complementing this, Ausawalaithong et al.9 implemented a deep CNN with 121 layers, utilizing transfer learning to leverage pre-trained model weights. Their approach achieved an accuracy of 74.43% and provided heatmap visualizations, which offered interpretability — a crucial consideration in clinical environments where understanding the model’s decision-making process is essential for trust and adoption. In a related direction, Wang et al.10 proposed a deep feature fusion approach combining a graph convolutional network with a CNN for chest CT classification, demonstrating the benefit of integrating relation-aware graph-based representations with conventional convolutional features for CT-based diagnosis. Similarly, Wang et al.11 introduced a graph rank-based average pooling neural network for explainable diagnosis from chest CT images, explicitly linking model attention to clinically interpretable regions — an approach conceptually aligned with the Grad-CAM-based explainability adopted in the present study. Further extending DL applications, studies have addressed more specialized diagnostic challenges, such as cancer subtype classification and treatment response prediction. Li et al.12 focused on the classification of lung adenosquamous carcinoma subtypes using histopathological images integrated with feature selection techniques. Their model achieved high subtype differentiation accuracy, underscoring DL’s potential for personalized medicine applications. In a parallel line of work on a different cancer type, Banupriya and Sethukarasi13 proposed a deep learning-driven multi-omics sequential diagnosis framework, Hybrid-OmniSeq, for breast cancer, integrating sequential feature selection with deep neural networks to improve molecular subtype classification accuracy. Extending this direction, Banupriya and Sethukarasi14 further introduced a multi-instance deep multimodal attention network (M-DMAN) that fuses gene expression and biomarker data (ER, PR, HER2) for breast cancer association prediction, achieving over 96% classification accuracy and highlighting the broader applicability of multimodal deep learning frameworks for cancer diagnosis beyond imaging-only approaches. Moreover, Yu et al.15 presented the Adaptive Hierarchical Heuristic Mathematical Model (AHHMM), blending DL with sophisticated image pre-processing to predict tumor growth patterns. This model also aimed to minimize radiation side effects, reflecting a broader vision for DL in treatment planning and longitudinal patient monitoring, extending beyond mere diagnostic tasks. However, despite the remarkable performance of DL models, several persistent challenges remain. The effectiveness of these models is heavily reliant on the availability of large, diverse, and accurately annotated datasets — a prerequisite that is often difficult to meet in the medical domain due to privacy concerns, annotation costs, and data heterogeneity. Thanoon et al.16 reviewed contemporary DL applications in lung cancer screening, emphasizing the need for expansive datasets to ensure model generalizability and reliability. They also stressed the importance of addressing issues like model transparency and bias mitigation, which are critical for safe clinical deployment. Complementing these observations, Muntasir et al.17 conducted a comparative study on leading DL architectures such as Inception V3, Xception, and ResNet-50 in the context of lung cancer detection. Their findings, reporting a diagnostic accuracy of 92% and an AUC of 98.21%, reaffirmed DL’s potential in outperforming conventional diagnostic approaches, especially when supported by comprehensive datasets and robust model training. In conclusion, while ML and DL techniques have significantly advanced the capabilities of automated lung cancer detection systems, their successful translation into clinical practice necessitates addressing key challenges. These include ensuring the availability of high-quality annotated datasets, improving model interpretability for clinician trust, minimizing algorithmic bias, and integrating these systems seamlessly into existing healthcare workflows. Ongoing research and collaborative efforts between clinicians, data scientists, and AI researchers will be pivotal in bridging the gap between experimental models and real-world clinical applications, ultimately contributing to more accurate, accessible, and early detection of lung cancer.

Recent advances in lung cancer detection

Over the past decade, the integration of artificial intelligence (AI) into medical diagnostics has significantly transformed the field of disease detection, particularly in oncology. Lung cancer, consistently ranking among the leading causes of cancer-related mortality worldwide, remains a primary focus of AI-driven innovations. Techniques based on machine learning (ML) and deep learning (DL) have emerged as powerful tools for improving the accuracy, consistency, and efficiency of lung cancer detection, particularly in the analysis of computed tomography (CT) images. The early work of Altarawneh18 explored the application of basic image processing techniques for lung cancer detection, demonstrating how fundamental enhancement and segmentation methods could aid in identifying pulmonary nodules. This foundational study paved the way for the adoption of advanced computational approaches in clinical diagnostics. Building on these early findings, Lakshmanaprabu et al.19 developed an optimized deep learning model for classifying lung cancer in CT images, highlighting the importance of model architecture design and hyperparameter tuning. Their research demonstrated that well-structured deep learning models could outperform traditional ML techniques in complex medical image classification tasks. Addressing the common issue of class imbalance in medical datasets, Mez et al.20 introduced a hybrid learning approach incorporating the Synthetic Minority Over-sampling Technique (SMOTE), which improved the balance between benign and malignant cases during model training and led to more stable and reliable classification outcomes. This work emphasized the importance of robust dataset preparation in AI model development. Further comparative studies of deep learning architectures were conducted by Ashhar et al.21, who systematically evaluated various convolutional neural network (CNN) models for lung cancer classification. Their findings confirmed that deeper CNN architectures generally yield higher classification accuracy, although they require greater computational resources, highlighting the trade-off between model complexity and clinical applicability. Complementing these advancements, Cengil and Cinar22 proposed a practical deep learning-based approach that focused on efficient model training and applicability in real-world clinical settings, underscoring the potential of AI-assisted diagnostics in everyday healthcare workflows. A critical factor facilitating these research advancements has been the availability of publicly accessible, annotated datasets. The Kaggle CT Scan Images for Lung Cancer dataset3 has provided a valuable resource for training, validation, and benchmarking of AI models, promoting reproducibility and collaborative research within the medical AI community. Despite significant progress, challenges remain. These include ensuring model generalization across diverse patient populations, enhancing model interpretability to foster clinician trust, and integrating AI systems seamlessly into existing healthcare infrastructures. Addressing these challenges requires continuous interdisciplinary collaboration among AI researchers, clinicians, and healthcare professionals, which is essential for translating technological advancements into practical, patient-centered diagnostic solutions aimed at achieving early and accurate lung cancer detection.

AI-driven image analysis techniques for CT-based lung cancer diagnosis

The evolution of lung cancer detection methods has been deeply influenced by advancements in medical image analysis, particularly through the application of machine learning (ML) and deep learning (DL) approaches. The diagnostic workflow for lung cancer using computed tomography (CT) imaging generally follows a structured pipeline, including data acquisition, pre-processing, segmentation, feature extraction, and classification each stage critically contributing to the enhancement of diagnostic precision and clinical applicability18.

Data collection and pre-processing

The foundation of this pipeline lays in the collection of diverse, high-quality CT datasets, which are crucial for training AI models with strong generalization capabilities. Publicly available repositories such as LIDC-IDRI, NLST, and TCGA-LUAD have been widely utilized for lung cancer detection research19. These datasets, often complemented by institutional archives, capture a wide variety of patient demographics and pathological conditions, contributing to more robust model performance. Pre-processing steps, including noise reduction via Gaussian filtering or anisotropic diffusion, intensity normalization through histogram equalization, and standardized image resizing, help minimize variations from different imaging protocols and enhance model training efficiency20. Effective pre-processing not only improves model convergence during training but also ensures consistent diagnostic accuracy across heterogeneous imaging datasets.

Image segmentation

Accurate segmentation of regions of interest (ROIs), particularly pulmonary nodules, is a pivotal step in the diagnostic process. Traditional methods, such as Otsu’s thresholding and watershed segmentation, have been applied for basic delineation tasks21. However, these conventional techniques often fall short when dealing with complex anatomical structures or low-contrast nodules. To overcome these limitations, deep learning-based segmentation models like U-Net and Mask R-CNN have been widely adopted for their high precision in medical image segmentation tasks22. Recent advancements, including the use of three-dimensional convolutional neural networks (3D CNNs) and transformer-based architectures, have demonstrated significant success in segmenting volumetric CT data and detecting subtle or irregularly shaped nodules23. Nevertheless, challenges such as inter-patient variability in nodule morphology continue to drive ongoing research into more adaptive and resilient segmentation models.

Feature extraction

Feature extraction converts segmented nodule regions into quantifiable attributes that serve as critical inputs for subsequent classification tasks. In traditional image analysis, feature extraction primarily focuses on handcrafted features that capture distinct morphological and textural characteristics of lung nodules. Morphological features include measurable properties such as shape, size, compactness, and aspect ratio, and border irregularity, parameters often associated with malignancy in clinical diagnostics. Textural features, on the other hand, are designed to quantify variations in pixel intensity and spatial patterns within the nodule region. Techniques such as the Gray-Level Co-occurrence Matrix (GLCM), which captures second-order statistical textures, and Local Binary Patterns (LBP), which encode local texture descriptors, have been widely adopted for this purpose20,24. These handcrafted features have been successfully utilized in traditional machine learning models like Support Vector Machines (SVM) and Random Forests for benign versus malignant classification tasks. However, the advent of deep learning (DL) has revolutionized the feature extraction process by enabling automatic learning of complex, hierarchical feature representations directly from raw image data. Convolutional Neural Networks (CNNs), in particular, have demonstrated the capability to capture multi-level features from simple edge detectors in initial layers to highly abstract patterns in deeper layers without requiring manual feature engineering. This automatic feature learning not only streamlines the workflow but also allows the model to discover intricate patterns that may be imperceptible to human experts. To further enhance feature extraction and generalization, transfer learning techniques have been employed using pre-trained models such as EfficientNet24, ResNet25, and DenseNet26. These models, originally trained on large-scale image datasets like ImageNet, can be fine-tuned on medical imaging datasets, leveraging learned feature hierarchies to improve performance on specific tasks like lung nodule classification. Transfer learning reduces the need for extensive annotated medical data and accelerates model convergence, making it a practical approach in medical AI applications. Moreover, these pre-trained architectures have been instrumental in extracting deep features for both classification and further downstream tasks, such as visualization using Grad-CAM for interpretability. Overall, the shift from manual to automated feature extraction has significantly improved the accuracy, scalability, and robustness of lung cancer detection models, paving the way for more efficient and clinically applicable diagnostic systems.

Classification and model evaluation

The classification phase involves analysing the extracted features to determine the malignancy status of detected nodules. Traditional ML classifiers, including Support Vector Machines (SVM), Random Forests, and Decision Trees, have been effectively employed for structured datasets27. However, deep learning classifiers, particularly CNN-based architectures such as ResNet25, DenseNet26, and advanced 3D CNN models23, have demonstrated superior performance in lung cancer classification tasks. Recently, hybrid models combining CNNs with transformer-based networks have shown promise, leveraging the spatial feature extraction capabilities of CNNs alongside the global contextual modelling strength of transformers to enhance classification accuracy and robustness27. Model performance is typically evaluated using standard metrics such as accuracy, sensitivity, specificity, and the Area Under the Receiver Operating Characteristic Curve (AUC-ROC). These metrics provide comprehensive insights into a model’s diagnostic reliability and its potential applicability in clinical practice, ensuring that AI-assisted systems can effectively contribute to improved lung cancer detection and patient care outcomes.

Data acquisition

This study utilizes a publicly available dataset sourced from Kaggle3, titled “CT Scan Images for Lung Cancer”. This Kaggle repository hosts the IQ-OTH/NCCD Lung Cancer Dataset (Version 2), originally curated by the Iraq-Oncology Teaching Hospital and the National Center for Cancer Diseases. The dataset comprises 1,190 thoracic CT scan images collected from 110 patients, representing a mix of healthy individuals and patients diagnosed with lung cancer at various stages. All images have been annotated by experienced oncologists and radiologists, ensuring reliable ground truth for supervised machine learning model training and evaluation.

The CT scans, originally in DICOM format, were acquired using a SOMATOM scanner (Siemens) with protocols standardized at 120 kV, 1 mm slice thickness, and window settings between 350 and 1200 HU width and 50–600 HU centre during full inspiration breath-hold. Each case consists of 80 to 200 slices, providing comprehensive volumetric chest imaging from different angles and perspectives.

The dataset participants were drawn from diverse socio-demographic backgrounds, including government employees, farmers, and labourers across Baghdad, Wasit, Diyala, Salahuddin, and Babylon provinces of Iraq. Ethical considerations were upheld — all images were fully de-identified prior to analysis, with institutional ethical approvals in place and written consent waived by the oversight review board.

To prevent slice-level data leakage, a patient-identifier was extracted from each image filename, and all CT slices belonging to a given patient were assigned exclusively to a single data partition (training, validation, or test). Splitting was performed at the patient level using stratified sampling by class, yielding an approximate 64/16/20 split of patients across training, validation, and test sets respectively. This ensures that no patient’s slices appear in more than one partition, addressing a key methodological concern regarding the validity of reported performance metrics in medical imaging studies.

Data pre-processing

To enhance the consistency and learning efficiency of the deep learning models, a structured pre-processing pipeline was applied to all CT scan images before training. The process began with noise reduction, where techniques like Gaussian filtering and anisotropic diffusion were utilized to suppress background noise while retaining essential anatomical features. This was followed by intensity normalization through histogram equalization, which standardized contrast and brightness levels across different images, minimizing discrepancies arising from varying acquisition parameters. Subsequently, all images were resized to standardized dimensions, ensuring compatibility with the input requirements of the deep learning architectures employed in this study. In addition to these steps, data augmentation techniques were implemented to artificially expand the dataset and improve model generalization. The augmentation strategy included random rotations (up to 20 degrees), horizontal flips, width and height shifts (up to 10%), zoom operations (up to 10%), and nearest-neighbour filling for handling image edges. These transformations helped the models learn robust features and mitigated overfitting by exposing them to a wider variety of training samples. Collectively, these pre-processing and augmentation techniques played a pivotal role in enhancing model stability, improving classification accuracy, and supporting effective learning during training and validation phases.

Processed images

The raw lung CT scan images collected from the IQ-OTHNCCD dataset were subjected to systematic preprocessing before being used for model training. Initially, all images were resized uniformly to 224 × 224 pixels, the standard input dimension for the VGG16 and other ImageNet-pretrained architectures used in this study. Following this, normalization was applied by scaling pixel intensity values to a [0, 1] range, facilitating faster convergence during training. Any images containing noise, incomplete data, or corrupted pixels were carefully excluded from the dataset to maintain the overall quality and reliability of the input data. To represent the diagnostic categories, each image was encoded with a numerical class label corresponding to one of three categories: 0 for Benign cases, 1 for Malignant cases, and 2 for Normal cases. Furthermore, to enhance dataset variability and improve model generalization, data augmentation was applied exclusively to the training set, using random rotations within ± 20 degrees, width and height shifts of ± 10%, zoom operations up to ± 10%, and random horizontal flipping, with nearest-neighbour fill for image boundaries; validation and test sets were left unaugmented. To address the class imbalance class weights inversely proportional to class frequency were computed from the training set and applied during loss calculation, penalizing misclassification of the minority Benign class more heavily. Finally, the dataset was split at the patient level into three subsets: approximately 64% for training, 16% for validation, and 20% for testing, as described above. The preprocessing approach ensured that the model received high-quality, leakage-free input samples, contributing to the performance achieved by the proposed deep learning models, especially the VGG16-based model in the lung cancer classification task.

Model design

In this study, a transfer learning-based approach was employed for lung cancer classification using the VGG16 architecture, pre-trained on the ImageNet dataset. The VGG16 model was loaded without its fully connected (top) layers, retaining its strong convolutional feature extraction capability. To tailor it for lung cancer prediction, a Global Average Pooling 2D layer was added after the final convolutional block to reduce the spatial dimensions and prevent overfitting. This was followed by a Dense (fully connected) layer with 128 neurons and ReLU activation, aimed at capturing task-specific features. To enhance generalization and minimize overfitting, a Dropout layer with a rate of 0.6 was incorporated. Finally, a dense output layer with 3 neurons and SoftMax activation was used to classify the images into Benign, Malignant, or Normal categories. For fine-tuning, all convolutional layers of the VGG16 backbone were made trainable (full fine-tuning), allowing the network to adapt its learned features to the domain-specific characteristics of lung CT imagery. This configuration was selected following an ablation study comparing four configurations: all layers frozen, only the last four convolutional layers trainable, full fine-tuning, and full fine-tuning without augmentation; full fine-tuning with augmentation was adopted as it provided a favourable balance between predictive performance and generalization stability. The model was compiled using the Adam optimizer (learning rate = 1e-5) and trained with the Sparse Categorical Cross-Entropy loss function suitable for multi-class classification, with class weights applied to address class imbalance. Classification accuracy was used as the primary evaluation metric, supplemented by precision, recall, F1-score, and AUC. This architectural strategy effectively combined the pre-trained strengths of VGG16 with task-specific adaptations for lung cancer detection, enabling efficient learning on the pre-processed CT image dataset.

Model training

The model training phase involved fine-tuning the VGG16-based architecture on the preprocessed and augmented dataset. The model was compiled using the Adam optimizer with an initial learning rate of 1e-5, and the sparse categorical cross-entropy loss function was employed due to the multi-class nature of the problem. Training was conducted for up to 50 epochs with a batch size of 16, incorporating early stopping (patience = 6 epochs, monitoring validation accuracy) and a learning-rate reduction schedule (factor 0.5, patience 3 epochs, monitoring validation loss) to prevent overfitting and aid convergence. To assess the stability and reproducibility of the results, each of the six architectures evaluated in this study was independently trained three times using different random seeds (42, 43, 44), and mean ± standard deviation of training, validation, and test accuracy were reported. The model’s performance was continually monitored on the validation set, and the best-performing weights were retained for final evaluation on the unseen test data.

Model validation and testing

The model was validated using a dedicated validation dataset, separated from the training set during preprocessing. Throughout the training phase, validation accuracy and validation loss were continuously monitored to track model performance and mitigate overfitting. An early stopping strategy was implemented to halt training once the validation accuracy ceased to improve.

After training, the model underwent testing on a separate unseen test dataset to evaluate its generalization ability. The test accuracy (the percentage of correctly classified samples) and test loss (the categorical cross-entropy loss) were computed as primary performance indicators. Additionally, confusion matrix and classification reports were generated to analyze the model’s class-wise prediction performance. As a supplementary robustness check, 5-fold patient-grouped stratified cross-validation was also performed, in which patients were partitioned into five folds with no patient appearing in more than one fold, providing an additional estimate of the model’s generalization performance independent of the primary train/validation/test split. These combined metrics ensured a thorough assessment of the model’s reliability before moving to the result and discussion phase.

Cancer prediction

After successful training and validation, the model was employed for lung cancer prediction on unseen CT images. The prediction workflow began by preprocessing the input CT image, resizing it to the model’s input dimensions, and normalizing pixel values. The pre-processed image was then passed through the trained model, which computed class probabilities via the SoftMax activation layer. The class with the highest probability whether benign, malignant, or normal was selected as the final prediction. To aid in clinical interpretability, Grad-CAM (Gradient-weighted Class Activation Mapping) was applied, highlighting significant regions in the CT image that influenced the model’s decision. This approach not only provided an automated prediction but also offered visual insights supporting clinical assessment.

Results and discussion

The proposed VGG16-based transfer learning model was evaluated on the IQ-OTHNCCD lung cancer CT image dataset comprising benign, malignant, and normal cases, using a patient-level 64/16/20 train/validation/test split. Across three independent runs with different random seeds, the model achieved a mean training accuracy of 85.54% ± 8.60%, validation accuracy of 88.45% ± 7.24%, and test accuracy of 89.39% ± 2.10%, with a mean test loss of 0.329. The best-performing run (seed 44) reached a training accuracy of 97.86%, validation accuracy of 95.45%, and test accuracy of 96.36%. As shown in Fig. 2, the training and validation accuracy of this run improved steadily across epochs without divergence between the two curves. The corresponding training and validation loss curves in Fig. 3 depict a continuous decrease, consistent with the absence of overfitting and a stable learning pattern. To further assess robustness, 5-fold patient-grouped stratified cross-validation was performed, yielding a mean test accuracy of 89.70% ± 2.75%, closely matching the held-out test performance and providing additional evidence against data leakage or overfitting.

Limitations

Several limitations of this study should be acknowledged. First, the IQ-OTH/NCCD dataset, while patient-diverse, is relatively small (110 patients) and exhibits substantial class imbalance, with only 15 Benign patients compared to 40 Malignant and 55 Normal patients. This imbalance is the most likely explanation for the model’s comparatively low Benign-class recall (0.250, Tables 1 and 3); despite the use of class weighting and augmentation, the limited number of unique Benign patients constrains the diversity of Benign examples available for training. Second, all experiments were conducted on a single dataset acquired from a single imaging center using a single CT scanner protocol; the model’s performance on data from other scanners, institutions, or patient populations has not been evaluated, and external multi-center validation is an important direction for future work. Third, while Grad-CAM visualizations (Fig. 5) suggest that the model attends to anatomically plausible regions, no formal quantitative validation against radiologist-annotated regions of interest was performed; such validation, ideally involving inter-rater agreement metrics between model attention maps and expert annotations, would strengthen claims of clinical interpretability. Fourth, the strong claims of “seamless clinical integration” present in earlier drafts of this manuscript have been removed; the present results should be interpreted as demonstrating technical feasibility and potential as an assistive screening tool, subject to the prospective clinical validation, regulatory review, and multi-center studies that would be required before any clinical deployment. Future work will address these limitations through dataset expansion (particularly for the Benign class, potentially via synthetic augmentation or SMOTE-based techniques as demonstrated by Zhang et al.28 for addressing class imbalance in medical image classification), multi-center external validation, and formal expert evaluation of model interpretability outputs.

Experimental environment

The experimental evaluation was conducted on Google Colaboratory (Colab Pro), leveraging its cloud-based computational resources for efficient model training and testing. The runtime environment was configured with Python 3 and an NVIDIA A100 GPU hardware accelerator (high-RAM runtime), which provided significant computational speedup compared to a standard CPU runtime. All models were trained and evaluated using single-precision (FP32) arithmetic with a batch size of 16 for training and a batch size of 1 for inference-time measurements (Table 4). The model implementation and training were performed using TensorFlow and Keras libraries.

Conclusion

This study presented a deep learning framework for the classification of lung CT images into Benign, Malignant, and Normal categories, with particular attention to methodological rigor through patient-level data splitting, multi-seed evaluation, statistical significance testing, and cross-validation. Among six evaluated architectures, the fully fine-tuned VGG16 model achieved the highest mean test accuracy (89.39% ± 2.10%) and the lowest test loss (0.329), with 5-fold patient-grouped cross-validation (89.70% ± 2.75%) closely corroborating this result. The model demonstrated near-perfect sensitivity and specificity for the Malignant class (AUC = 1.000), which is the most clinically critical category, while performance on the Benign class was comparatively limited, reflecting the small number of Benign patients in the available dataset. Grad-CAM visualizations indicated that the model’s attention broadly corresponds to anatomically plausible lung regions for each class, supporting its interpretability, though formal radiologist validation remains future work. Overall, this work demonstrates the technical feasibility of VGG16-based transfer learning for multi-class lung cancer classification and highlights its potential as an assistive screening tool, while underscoring the need for larger, multi-institutional, and class-balanced datasets, together with prospective clinical validation, before such methods could be considered for clinical deployment.
