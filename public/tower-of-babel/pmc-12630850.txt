Dietary and oral factors associated with eating slowly and chewing well: a National web-based study
Authors: Midori Ishikawa, Masanori Iwasaki, Rumi Tano, Tetsuji Yokoyama, Yuichi Ando
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractSeveral studies have investigated manner of eating, such as “eating slowly” or “chewing well,” to prevent being overweight and obese. In Japan, one of the goals of the “Basic Act on Shokuiku” (Food and Nutrition Education), is “to increase the number of people who eat slowly and chew their food well.” Therefore, this cross-sectional study aimed to clarify the factors associated with eating slowly and chewing well. Survey forms were randomly distributed to national-based monitors from an online survey company by gender and for age group from the 40s to 70s. The survey included items on dietary and health behaviors (12 items), dental and oral conditions (14 items), and socioeconomic factors (6 items). A total of 1,644 people (822 men and women each) were evaluated using logistic regression analysis. For men and women in nearly all age groups, “eating slowly and chewing well” was associated with “savoring while eating” followed by “not eating with one’s mouth full.” It was also associated with the absence of bone loss around the teeth for men and the absence of toothache for women. Therefore, “eating slowly and chewing well” may be encouraged by “savoring food while eating.”Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-025-17631-9.Keywords: Eating slowly and chewing well, Dietary behaviors, Dental and oral conditions, Eating while savoring, ShokuikuSubject terms: Dentistry, Health policy, Health services, Nutrition, Public health

Introduction

Recently, several studies have investigated dietary behaviors associated with eating to stimulate satiety or appetite, such as “eating speed”1–4 and “chewing well”4–6, to prevent gaining weight and obesity. Many reports have been produced on the relationship between “eating fast” and “overweight and obesity (25.0 ≤ BMI)”1–3. They suggest that “eating slowly” may help control appetite and prevent excessive food intake3,4. Additional factors associated with “eating slowly” are “not eating until full”1,3 and “eating food while chewing thoroughly”5.

Additionally, “chewing well” refers to increasing the number of times food is chewed, implying that it may take longer to eat when one eats more slowly. This stimulates postprandial insulin secretion and hormones that promote insulin secretion, which may help prevent being overweight and obesity3,5,6. It is reported that people “do not chew well” due to problems with their dental and oral condition, such as periodontal disease, causing an “inability to chew”7,8. Additionally, dental and oral functions decline with aging in adulthood, and people are more likely to “not chew properly,” suggesting they may change manner of eating7–9.

In Japan, which is becoming a super-aged society, the promotion of food and nutrition education in the “Basic Act on Shokuiku”10 aims to increase the number of people who “eat slowly and chew their food well,” including their respective indicators11.

Similarly, the Diabetes Treatment Guidelines for 2024 stated that one of the key points of diet therapy is to “eat slowly and chew well”12. However, the percentage of people who engage in “eating slowly and chewing well” has mainly plateaued since 2015 (49.2%) and slightly decreased to 47.3% in 202013. In the Ministry of Agriculture, Forestry and Fishery (MAFF), the Fourth Basic Plan for the Promotion of Shokuiku (2020–2025) aims to raise it to 55% or more by 202511.

The MAFF states that “savoring food slowly (Yukkuri Ajiwau in Japanese) will allow for lightly seasoned flavors and an appropriate amount to give a sense of satisfaction”14. As part of the Shokuiku efforts, various savoring initiatives are being implemented15, including activities that teach “how to savor ingredients of locally produced foods,” such as savoring whole rice16 and Japanese school meals with “dashi (umami soup)”17.

Although previous research has suggested a relationship between savoring food and dental and oral conditions18, few studies reported a relationship between “savoring food while eating” and “eating slowly or chewing well.”

As mentioned above, while several previous studies have focused on Japanese people and either “eating slowly” or “chewing well,” there are very few research reports on the factors associated with “eating slowly and chewing well.”

Therefore, this study aimed to clarify the dietary behaviors and dental and oral conditions associated with “eating slowly and chewing well.”

Results

This study hypothesized that “eating slowly and chewing well” was associated with dietary behaviors and dental and oral factors. The results indicate that, for men and women in nearly all age groups, “eating slowly and chewing well” was strongly associated with “savoring while eating” followed by “not eating with one’s mouth full.” In addition, it was associated with “the absence of bone loss around the teeth” for men and “the absence of toothache (pain in teeth)” for women. Moreover, “eating slowly and chewing well” was related with “having children” for several age groups in terms of socioeconomic factors. Previous studies have reported that BMI is associated with eating fast1–4. In the current study, however, BMI values were self-reported instead of measured. For men in their 40s and 70s, “not eating slowly and chewing food well” was associated with being overweight and obesity (BMI ≥ 25.0) and being slender (BMI < 18.5). No similar relationship was observed for women.

Table 1 shows the results for men. For all age groups, the groups who ate slowly and chewed well had more people who “ate slowly “and “chewed their food “than the groups whose members did not eat slowly and chew well (p < 0.001).

Regarding socioeconomic factors, the groups that ate slowly and chewed well had a higher proportion of people who did not have children (p = 0.019) and were highly educated (p = 0.038) in their 50s, people who have children (p = 0.048) in their 60s, lived in single-person households (p = 0.005) in their 70s, compared to the group whose members did not eat slowly and chewed well.

Table 2 shows the results for women. For all age groups, the groups whose members ate slowly and chewed well had more people who “ate slowly” and “chewed their food” than the groups whose members did not eat slowly and chew well (p < 0.001).

The groups that ate slowly and chewed well had a higher proportion of people who were without children (p = 0.006) and who lived alone or were in a married couple household (p = 0.001) in their 40s, compared to the groups whose members did not eat slowly and chew well.

For Model 2, in terms of dietary and health behaviors among men and women, “eating slowly and chewing well” was significantly associated with “savoring food while eating” (men: OR: 11.20, 95% CI: 5.28–23.76, p < 0.0001; women: OR: 11.48, 95% CI: 4.63–28.46, p < 0.0001) followed by “not eating with one’s mouth full” (men: OR: 3.34, 95% CI: 2.23–5.00, p < 0.0001; women: OR: 2.60, 95% CI: 1.65–4.10, p < 0.0001).

In addition, regarding dental and oral conditions, “eating slowly and chewing well” was associated with “the absence of bone loss around the teeth” (OR: 2.06, 95% CI: 1.22–3.50, p = 0.007) for men and “not having pain in the teeth” (OR: 2.72, 95% CI: 1.44–5.16, p = 0.002) for women.

Furthermore, the results of logistic regression analyses by age group indicate that for men and women in nearly all age groups, “eating slowly and chewing well” was significantly associated with “savoring food while eating” followed by “not eating with one’s mouth full.” In the age groups 60s and 70s, it was associated with good dental and oral conditions such as the absence of severe periodontal disease or oral frailty (Supplementary Tables: ST 2 and ST 3).

Discussion

This study identified that, for men and women in nearly all age groups, “savoring food while eating” was associated with “eating slowly and chewing well.” Previous studies reported that dietary behaviors and dental and oral conditions were associated with each indicator such as “eating slowly” or “chewing well”1–7,13. However, few studies highlighted factors associated with “eating slowly and chewing well,” which includes both meanings.

The Japanese word “savor (Ajiwau)” means trying the taste, fully appreciating the flavor of food, and enjoying food19. The MAFF encourages children to eat while savoring agricultural, forestry and fishery products from an early age, which will help cultivate healthy minds and bodies throughout their lives, develop rich humanity10,20. “Savoring local food” will deepen awareness and understanding of the agriculture, forestry and fishery industries and the food they produce11.

The results of this study, which targeted people in their 40s and older, suggested that “savoring food while eating” may encourage eating slowly and chewing well, regardless of age. This behavior may be worthy of attention when formulating approaches for groups with little interest in health behaviors. In the future, studies should be conducted on whether similar results can be obtained in younger age groups. Thus, “savoring food” throughout the life course may be necessary to “eat slowly and chew well.”

This study also showed that in almost all age groups, “not eating with one’s mouth full” associated with “eating slowly and chewing well” in men and women. Given that previous research21 demonstrated that eating with one’s mouth full is an eating behavior, we set it as an indicator of dietary behavior. Other studies have shown that taking large bites reduces the number of times one chews, whereas eating small amounts increases the number of times one chews per meal3,21. Other studies have also reported on the importance of controlling the eating speed and chewing frequency by the amount of food you put in one bite22,23. The results of this study suggest that to chew well, it may be important not to eat with one’s mouth full in the first place. Further research is required because the phrase “not eating one’s mouth full” may mean an oral condition.

Previous studies provided limited reports on dietary behavioral factors associated with “not chewing food well.” The results of this study confirmed a relationship between “eating slowly and chewing well” and “eating with family members.” In particular, for women in their 40s and 50s, “eating slowly and chewing well” was associated with the frequency of breakfast/dinner and eating with the family. Mothers may teach their children the importance of eating slowly and chewing well at the breakfast or dinner table at home. However, no results on this relationship were found for men. Further investigation is needed, including the background factors of gender differences.

It was also found that dental and oral conditions associated with “eating slowly and chewing well,” such as the lack of severe periodontal disease, especially for individuals those in their 60s and 70s. Additionally, differences were observed between men and women24. The associated factors for men in their 60s and 70s were “not losing bone around their teeth.” For women in their 60s, “not experience of loose teeth,” and in their 70s, it is “not difficult to eat tough foods,” and “not bothered by a dry mouth.” These results suggested that “chewing slowly and chewing well” may be related to having fewer dental and oral problems.

Furthermore, the current study found no association between dietary variety and eating slowly and chewing well. However, a previous study observed a relationship among eating speed, chewing well, and intake of processed food, such as processed meat, as well as food preference and gender difference24. As the current study excluded these items, further investigation is required.

The strength of this study lies in its use of logistic regression analysis to identify factors of dietary behaviors and dental and oral conditions that associated with “eating slowly and chewing well,” which had largely been unexplored. However, this study also has some limitations.

First, this survey was conducted using an internet-based questionnaire. All items were based on self-reporting, not actual measurements. Thus, the results should be interpreted considering these limitations. However, because the respondents included both men and women in all age groups from the 40s to the 70s, as well as region of residence, household income, and educational background, these variables were adjusted for the analysis.

Second, given the cross-sectional nature of the study, it was unable to demonstrate the causal relationship between “eating slowly and chewing well” and “savoring food while eating” over time, which would also require further research.

In conclusion, despite these limitations, for both men and women in almost all age groups from the 40s to the 70s, “savoring food while eating,” followed by “not eating with one’s mouth full,” was significantly associated with “eating slowly and chewing well.” Furthermore, “chewing slowly and chewing well” was associated with the absence of bone loss around the teeth for men and the absence of toothache for women, which may be due to positive dental and oral conditions. The MAFF’s “savoring local food” initiatives might be promoted to “eat slowly and chew well” to create healthy food habits and improve oral health.

Methods

Study population and procedure

This cross-sectional observational study was conducted at the National Institute of Public Health in Japan as part of a research project promoting dental and oral health in Shokuiku (food and nutrition education). The participants were approximately 1.3 million people who anonymously registered as monitors for a major web survey company in Japan (Macromill, Inc., Tokyo, Japan)25. The participants were screened using an online questionnaire, and those who were deemed capable of answering the survey (capable of accessing the survey site and receiving survey-related information) were selected. The first part of the questionnaire explained the target criteria, and the questionnaire was designed such that only those who met the conditions stipulated by the study could complete it. A computer program of the web survey company was used to randomly distribute the questionnaire to potential respondents aged in their 40s, 50s, 60s, and 70s across Japan. Based on this selection process, there was a low probability of selecting respondents with dementia or other diseases, who may encounter difficulty in answering the survey. The respondents were asked to complete a self-administered questionnaire. The survey was continued until the number of respondents in each age group exceeded 200. A total of 1,648 people, 206 men and women each age group, participated in the survey. The company obtained informed consent from all participants. This study accessed the company database, ensuring the anonymity of the participants. Data were provided in electronic form through an established procedure and were encrypted and stored on a specific computer as per the information security policy of the institutions of the researchers. This study was conducted per the guidelines established in the Declaration of Helsinki, and all procedures involving research study participants were approved by the Ethics Committee of the National Institute of Public Health, Wako, Saitama, Japan (NIPH-IBRA#23024, November 30, 2023).

Measurement

The authors of this study developed the questionnaire. For the survey items, the study selected indicators that were previously validated. The sources of these indicators include the Fourth Basic Plan for the Promotion of Shokuiku11,13,14, the National Health and Nutrition Survey conducted by the Ministry of Health, Labor and Welfare (MHLW; respondents were aged 20 years and older)26, surveys on general health checkups conducted by the MHLW (respondents were aged 40 years and older)27, the Survey of the Current Status and Awareness of Shokuiku conducted by the MAFF to evaluate and monitor the Basic Plan for the Promotion of Shokuiku (respondents were aged 20 years and older)28, and previous reports and national guidelines (respondents were aged 40–79 years)1–7,29–37.

Objective variable

The objective variable was the indicator of the Basic Plan for the Promotion of Shokuiku, “Do you eat slowly and chew your food well?” Participants chose one of the following answers: “eating slowly and chewing well, eating somewhat slowly and chewing somewhat well, not eating somewhat slowly and chewing somewhat well, and not eating slowly and chewing well” [11,0.13].

This question has two meanings: “How fast do you eat food?” and “Do you often eat your food without chewing?” Therefore, the following two questions also were posed: “How fast do you eat food?” (answer choices: very or somewhat slowly, normally, and very or somewhat fast)26, and “Do you often eat your food without chewing?” (yes or no)27.

Explanatory variables

The explanatory variables were dietary and health behaviors (12 items) and dental and oral conditions (14 items), as follows:

Dietary and health behaviors

The following dietary and health behaviors were included: manner of eating (3 items), meals/snacks (3 items), dietary behaviors (3 items), and health behaviors (3 items).

Manner of eating (3 items) included, “Do you savor your food when eating?” (response options: savor, somewhat savor, somewhat do not savor, do not savor)28, “Do you eat until you are full?” (response options: yes or no)1,29, and “Do you stuff your mouth when you eat?” (response options: yes or no)21.

For meals and snacks (3 items), the respondents were asked, “Do you eat snacks or drink sweet beverages between meals, i.e., breakfast, lunch, and dinner?” (daily, sometimes, or rarely) and “How often do you eat at least two well-balanced meals daily consisting of a staple dish, a main dish, and side dishes?” (almost every day, 4–5 days a week, 2–3 days a week, rarely)26–28.

Dietary variety was evaluated using Dietary Variety Score (DVS)30,31, a self-administered questionnaire for the weekly frequency of food consumption. DVS comprises 10 food groups: fish and shellfish, meat, eggs, milk, soybean/soybean products, green and yellow vegetables, seaweeds, potatoes, fruits, and fats and oils. The response “eat almost daily” was scored a value of 1, whereas “eat once every two days,” “eat twice a week,” and “rarely eat” were scored a value of 0. Thus, the total scores ranged from 0 to 10. Based on the association with oral frailty, the score was divided into two groups: 0–3 points (low) and 4–10 points (high).

For dietary behaviors (3 items), the participants were asked, “Do you skip breakfast more than 3 times a week?” (yes or no), “How often do you eat breakfast with your family members?” (almost every day, 4–5 days a week, 2–3 days a week, about once a week or rarely), and “How often do you eat dinner with your family members?” (almost every day, 4–5 days a week, 2–3 days a week, about once a week, or rarely)28. In the analysis, the responses to questions about eating breakfast/dinner with family were reclassified into three responses “almost every day,” “2–5 days a week,” and “less than once a week.”

Health behaviors (3 items) included, “Are you currently a heavy smoker?” (yes or no), “How often do you drink (sake, shochu, beer, wine, whiskey, or brandy, etc.)?” (every day, occasionally, hardly drink, or cannot drink),” and “Are you in the habit of doing exercise in which you sweat lightly for over 30 min at a time, twice weekly, for over a year?” (yes or no)26,27.

Dental and oral conditions

Dental and oral conditions (13 items) included the chewing function (2 items), dental caries and periodontal disease (3 items), screening for severe periodontal disease (4 items) and checking for oral frailty (5 items).

For the chewing function (2 items), participants were asked, “Which best describes your condition while eating and chewing food?” (I can chew any food or I can’t chew some foods)26,30,31 and “Can you bite with both left and right molars?” (yes or no)32.

Dental caries and periodontal disease (3 items) included the following questions: “Do you have pain in your teeth or gums?” (yes or no), “Do you have any untreated carious cavities or teeth with missing dental fillings?” (yes or no), and “Do you use removable dentures?” (yes or no)8.

Screening items for severe periodontal disease (4 items)7,27 included the following questions: “Do you think you may have gum disease?” (yes or no), “Have you ever had naturally loose teeth (not due to injury)?” (yes or no), “Have you ever been told by a dental professional that you have lost bone around your teeth (lost bone)?” (yes or no), and “Have you had bleeding gums in the past three months?” (rarely or never, sometimes, always/often)7.

For the answers to questions about severe periodontal disease (4 points), “yes” or “always/often” was assigned a score of 1, and “no” or “other” was assigned a score of 0. The total score was then calculated, after which “no possibility” (0–1 point) or “possible” (2–4 points) were assessed33.

The following questions examined oral frailty2,34–37: “How many of your natural teeth are left?” (self-reported) (20 or more or fewer than 19), “Do you have any difficulties eating tough food now compared with 6 months ago?” (yes or no), “Have you recently choked on your tea or soup?” (yes or no), “Do you often experience dry mouth?” (yes or no) and “Have you recently had difficulty pronouncing clearly?” (yes or no).

The oral frailty score (5 points) was calculated by adding up the answers, with “yes” assigned a score of 1 and “no“ assigned a score of 0, after which “no possibility” (0–1 points)“ or “possible” (2–5 points) “was assessed34.

Physical and health status

Questions were asked to assess how physical and health status (7 items) was associated with “eating slowly and chewing well.”

For physical status, after participants self-reported their height and weight, body mass index (BMI = weight (kg) ÷ (height (m)²)) was calculated and they were classified as underweight (BMI < 18.5 kg/m2), normal weight (18.5 kg/m2 ≤ BMI < 25.0 kg/m2), or overweight/obese (25.0 kg/m2 ≤ BMI).

Regarding health status26, the participants were asked and answered yes or no, “Do you currently take medication to reduce blood pressure?” “Do you currently take medication to reduce blood sugar or insulin injection?” “Do you currently take medication to reduce your cholesterol level?” “Have you ever been told by the doctor that you have had a stroke (cerebral hemorrhage, brain infarction, etc.) and received treatment?” “Have you ever been told by the doctor you have a heart disease (angina pectoris, myocardial infarction, etc.) and received treatment?” “Have you ever been diagnosed as having chronic kidney disease or kidney failure and received treatment (dialysis therapy)?” and “Have you ever been diagnosed with anemia?”

Socioeconomic factors (six items) for all participants included residence area, marital status, whether or not participants had children, whether they live together with family members, household income, and highest level of education.

Study period

The survey was conducted from January 5th to January 6th, 2024.

Statistical analysis

Subjects for analysis

Among all respondents, those whose BMI values were outliers (BMI < 10.0 or 100 < BMI) were excluded (2 men, 2 women), with a total of 1,644 persons (822 men, 822 women) analyzed.

Statistical analysis

All analyses were performed by gender and age group (40s, 50s, 60s, and 70s). Because in previous studies, eating fast was associated with obesity in middle aged men6, and the items related to oral frailty were related to eating slowly or chewing well in the older age groups9,35; thus, we analyzed the data based on the age groups.

First, in the objective variable “eating slowly and chewing well,” the relationship was analyzed between the four answer groups (eating slowly and chewing well, eating somewhat slowly and chewing somewhat well, not eating somewhat slowly or chewing somewhat well and not eating slowly or chewing well) and the indicators (how fast they eat food, and whether eat their food without chewing). The relationships were then analyzed with physical and health status and socioeconomic factors using the Cochran–Mantel–Haenszel test.

Then, the relationships between these four groups and dental and oral conditions (13 items), dietary behavior (9 items), and health behavior (3 items) were then analyzed using the Cochran–Mantel–Haenszel test.

Second, the four answer options for “eat slowly and chew well” were reclassified into two categories: “Eat slowly and chew well” and “Do not eat slowly or chew well.”

The study then calculated the odds ratio of a situation favoring “eating slowly and chewing well” using logistic regression analysis (forced entry method) with the explanatory variables, including dietary and health behaviors (12 items), dental and oral conditions (14 items), and socioeconomic factors (6 items) (Model 1).

Finally, the study calculated the odds ratio of a situation favoring “eating slowly and chewing well” via logistic regression analysis (stepwise method) with the same objective variables as in Model 1 including dietary and health behaviors (12 items), dental and oral conditions (14 items), and socioeconomic factors (6 items) that were analyzed by the forced entry method (Model 2).

In both analyses, the “reference” was assigned an option that represented unfavorable situations (e.g., not savoring food while eating, eating food by stuffing one’s mouth, having painful gums, or having untreated dental caries), and the odds ratio of a favorable situation to “eating slowly and chewing well” was calculated.

All statistical analyses were performed using the SAS software, version 9.4 (SAS Institute, Inc., Cary, NC, USA). A p-value of < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.
