Development of an immunocompetent cutaneous squamous cell carcinoma model identifies VISTA and CTLA-4 as targetable immune checkpoints
Authors: Alanis E Rodriguez Rosario, Roberto Rangel, Nicholas Balbin, Zohra N Nizami, Jaafar Hadi, Ahmed Noor, Liping Dong, Arnoldo Corona, Nikitha Bhavani, Ricardo M Cruz Sanchez, Gemalene M Sunga, Ratna Veeramachaneni, Ganiraju C Manyam, Jing Wang, Wendong Yu, Andrew G Sikora, Jeffrey N Myers, Roberto Rangel
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundImmunotherapeutic approaches for cutaneous squamous cell carcinoma (cSCC) remain limited to programmed cell death protein 1 (PD-1) blockade. Although genomics studies have characterized key driver mutations in cSCC, preclinical models that faithfully recapitulate both the genetic landscape and immune microenvironment of the human disease, that could drive the development of novel, effective therapies, are lacking.MethodsTo address this need, we generated genetically engineered mouse models harboring inducible p53wmR172H expression and Cdkn2a deletion in stratified epithelia. These mice spontaneously developed well-differentiated and spindle cell cSCCs, whose histopathological and immunological features mimicked those observed in patients. We established syngeneic cell lines and characterized their genomic and transcriptomic profiles through whole exome sequencing and RNA sequencing. We used these syngeneic tumor models to test the effect of STING agonist, PD-1, VISTA and CTLA-4 blockade applied at different schedules and combinations. Finally, immune checkpoint molecules detected in the syngeneic models were validated in human cSCC tissue arrays.ResultsWhole exome sequencing confirmed alterations overlapping with human cSCC, including mutations in Notch, interferon signaling, and cytokine pathways. Transcriptomic analysis revealed the upregulation of immunoregulatory genes, including Cd274, Lgals9, and Il33, suggesting a suppressive tumor immune microenvironment (TIME). Preclinical therapeutic evaluation in this model demonstrated that PD-1 blockade and the treatment with a STING agonist elicited partial responses. The combination of STING agonist with checkpoint inhibition targeting either PD-1/CTLA-4 or PD-1/VISTA significantly enhanced antitumor immunity in tumors resistant to anti-PD-1 monotherapy.ConclusionsThese results establish a robust, genetically defined, immunocompetent platform for modeling cSCC and evaluating novel immunotherapeutic strategies for treating patients with this disease. Our findings also identify VISTA and CTLA-4 as promising immune checkpoints to target in cSCC and support clinical evaluation of this combination of immune checkpoint blockade to overcome immune checkpoint inhibitor resistance and improve clinical outcomes.Keywords: Immune Checkpoint Inhibitor, Skin Cancer, Immunotherapy, Immunosuppression

Introduction

Cutaneous squamous cell carcinoma (cSCC) is the second most common skin cancer worldwide, accounting for 20% of non-melanoma skin cancer-related deaths in immunocompetent individuals.1–5 Although most early-stage cSCCs are curable by surgical excision, locally advanced or metastatic forms have aggressive behavior and poor clinical outcomes.6 7 The pathogenesis of cSCC is driven primarily by chronic ultraviolet (UV) exposure, which leads to accumulated DNA damage and high mutational burden, with recurrent inactivation of tumor suppressor genes such as TP53 and CDKN2A, which are mutated in over 80% and 50% of human cSCCs, respectively.8 9

Immune checkpoint inhibitors targeting programmed cell death protein 1 (PD-1), such as cemiplimab and pembrolizumab, have shown clinical efficacy and are now US Food and Drug Administration-approved for patients with unresectable or metastatic cSCC.10–15 However, a subset of patients fails to respond or develops resistance to these inhibitors, highlighting the need to understand the immunological mechanisms driving therapeutic resistance and identify novel immune-based targeting. The tumor immune microenvironment (TIME) in patients with cSCC often contains immunosuppressive myeloid cells as myeloid-derived suppressor cells (MDSC) and tumor-promoting macrophages, regulatory T cells (Tregs), and exhausted T cells with increased expression of immune checkpoints such as PD-1, CTLA-4, LAG3, and VISTA.16–20 Although recent studies have identified VISTA as a key modulator of immune evasion in multiple cancer types, including skin cancers,21 22 its role in cSCC remains underexplored owing to a lack of preclinical models that faithfully recapitulate both the genetic landscape and cellular composition of the TIME of human cSCC.

Despite advances in genomic profiling through efforts such as The Cancer Genome Atlas and other large-scale sequencing studies,8 9 23 preclinical models that faithfully recapitulate both the genetic landscape and characterization of human cSCC remain limited. Mouse models generated using chemical carcinogens, radiation, three-dimensional biofabrication, transplantation or skin-on-a-chip approaches often lack the genetic precision or immune competency needed for immunotherapeutic studies.24–29

To overcome these limitations, we developed genetically engineered mouse models (GEMMs) with inducible expression of mutant p53R172H and deletion of Cdkn2a in stratified epithelium. Spontaneous tumors arising in these mice include well-differentiated and spindle cell cSCC, mimicking the histopathological subtypes observed in patients. To facilitate therapeutic testing, we established syngeneic tumor cell lines from spontaneous tumors and characterized their genomic, transcriptomic, and immunological features. Whole-exome sequencing (WES) confirmed that these cells have recurrent mutations overlapping with those identified in human cSCC, including alterations in Notch, interferon signaling, and cytokine pathways. Functional analyses revealed differential expression of immunomodulatory genes and tumor-intrinsic factors that shape the TIME. Using these models, we evaluated responses to immune checkpoint blockade, and treatment with STING agonist and found that the co-inhibition of VISTA and CTLA-4 has a synergistic antitumor effect, particularly in tumors with partial responsiveness to anti-PD-1 monotherapy. Collectively, our findings establish a robust genetically defined, syngeneic cSCC platform that models both the tumor-intrinsic and immune-evasive features of human disease and identifies VISTA as a promising immunotherapeutic target in cSCC.

Material and methods

Mice

We used GEMMs in which p53wmR172H-gain-of-function (GOF; provided by Dr Guillermina Lozano) contains a partial wild-type Trp53 complementary DNA (cDNA) encoding exons 5–11, flanked by loxP sites and inserted into intron 4 of the endogenous Trp53 locus. Following K14-Cre-mediated recombination, the inserted cDNA cassette is excised, allowing expression of the mutant Trp53R172H/R172H allele under control of the endogenous Trp53 promoter; these mice are hereafter referred to as p53-GOF.30 The p53-loss-of-function (LOF; The Jackson Laboratory strain #008462) mice harbor loxP sites flanking exons 1 and 11 of the Trp53 gene. After Cre-mediated recombination, the Trp53 locus is deleted, generating p53-LOF mice.31 The Cdkn2aflox/flox (fl/fl) mice (NCI, strain #01XBU) harbor loxP sites flanking exon 1α of the Cdkn2a gene. On Cre-mediated recombination, exon 1α is deleted, resulting in loss of Cdkn2a expression; these mice are hereafter referred to as Cdkn2a-LOF.32 The Trp53 and Cdkn2a alleles were activated or deleted in epithelial cells using K14-Cre mice (The Jackson Laboratory strain #018964) which target gene recombination to stratified epithelia. All mouse strains were backcrossed onto the C57BL/6 background using marker-assisted speed congenics in MD Anderson’s Laboratory Animal Genetic Services core facility. The following mouse cohorts were generated: p53-GOF (K14CreTg/+, p53R172H/R172H), p53-LOF (K14CreTg/+, p53fl/fl), Cdkn2a-LOF (K14 CreTg/+; Cdkn2afl/fl) and p53-GOF; Cdkn2a-LOF (K14 CreTg/+; p53R172H/R172H, Cdkn2afl/fl). Genotypes were confirmed by PCR using genomic DNA from tail biopsy specimens. Mice were monitored weekly for 2 years to detect spontaneous tumor development. Mice were humanely euthanized after displaying severe morbidity related to disease burden and/or treatment toxicity or unknown causes in those that did not develop tumors. All animal procedures were approved by the University of Texas MD Anderson Cancer Center Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee (IACUC), under protocol number 00000950-RN04.

Establishment of syngeneic mouse cSCC cell lines

Skin tumors were aseptically excised, rinsed and washed three times with phosphate-buffered saline (PBS) with 500 U/mL penicillin-streptomycin. Tumor tissues were minced and incubated with a collagenase-dispase I enzyme mix at 37°C for 30 min. The resulting cell suspension was washed once with PBS, followed by a second enzymatic digestion using trypsin and DNase I at 37°C for 10 min. The reaction was then neutralized with a soybean trypsin inhibitor solution. The suspension was filtered through a 70 µm cell strainer, and the cells were cultured in Dulbecco’s Modified Eagle Medium (DMEM) supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS), L-glutamine, sodium pyruvate, non-essential amino acids, and 50 U/mL penicillin-streptomycin at 37°C in 5% CO₂. After 2–3 weeks, outgrowing tumor cells formed colonies, eventually yielding four cell lines we designated ROCSkin-1, ROCSkin-2, ROCSkin-3, and ROCSkin-5. The cells were expanded to larger plates and enriched by differential trypsinization for fibroblast removal. ROCSkin-1 and ROCSkin-5 cells were from p53-GOF/Cdkn2a-LOF tumor-bearing mice, ROCSkin-2 cells were derived from tumors from p53-LOF mice, and ROCSkin-3 cells came from tumors in p53-GOF mice. All cell lines were negative for Mycoplasma contamination and frozen cell stocks were stored in liquid nitrogen. Short tandem repeat profiling performed by IDEXX BioAnalytics (Columbia, Missouri, USA) confirmed the murine origin of the cells and ruled out cross-contamination. Short tandem repeat marker information is available in online supplemental table S1.

Cell harvesting and chromosome banding analysis

Metaphase chromosome preparations were generated from exponentially growing ROCSkin cell lines using standard cytogenetic techniques. Cells were treated with colcemid at 0.04 µg/mL for 3 hours to arrest cells in metaphase, followed by hypotonic treatment with 0.075 M KCl for 15 min at room temperature. Cells were then fixed in methanol:acetic acid solution at a 3:1 ratio and washed three times with fresh fixative. Fixed cell suspensions were dropped onto wet glass slides to prepare metaphase spreads. G-banding was performed by treating the preparations with Trypsin-EDTA solution, followed by staining with 4% Giemsa in buffer. Metaphase images were captured using a Nikon 80i microscope equipped with HiBand karyotyping software from Applied Spectral Imaging (Vista, California, USA). For each cell line, at least 20–25 Giemsa-banded metaphase spreads were analyzed, and 10 complete karyotypes were prepared.

Establishment of cSCC tumor models

2 million of tumor cells (ROCSkin-1, ROCSkin-2, ROCSkin-3, or ROCSkin-5) suspended in 100 µL of sterile PBS were injected subcutaneously into 8–10 weeks old male and female C57BL/6J mice (The Jackson Laboratory, strain #000664) and RAG−/− mice (B6.129S7-Rag1tm1Mom/J, The Jackson Laboratory, strain #002216). Tumor size was measured with calipers two times a week and the tumor volume was calculated using the formula tumor volume=(length×width2/2). Mice were euthanized immediately on reaching IACUC-approved humane endpoint criteria, including tumor ulceration or other veterinary-defined welfare endpoints, irrespective of tumor volume.

Next generation sequencing

Genomic DNA was isolated with a Blood and Cell Culture DNA Mini Kit (Qiagen, cat. #13323), and total RNA was isolated with RNA Miniprep Plus (Zymo Research, Irvine, California, USA) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. DNA and RNA concentrations were determined using the NanoDrop system (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Wilmington, Delaware, USA). WES, messenger RNA (mRNA) library preparation and NovaSeqX+150 PE sequencing were performed by Psomagen (Rockville, Maryland, USA). WES reads were mapped to the mouse genome assembly mm10 using the Burrows-Wheeler Aligner (default parameter setting). Nucleotide mutations were called using VarScan2 (parameter setting: -strand-filter 1-min-coverage 30-p value 0.01-min-freq-for-hom 0.9–output-vcf 1-variants 1) based on the output generated by samptools mpileup of bam files. The VCF files of mutations for downstream analysis were annotated by the refGene database and filtered by the snp142 database. The RNA sequencing reads were mapped against the GRCm38 reference genome for Mus musculus strain C57BL/6J using TopHat2 (V.2.1.1), and the mapped reads per gene were counted using HTSeq (V.0.11.0) based on gene annotation of GENCODE M19. The reads counts were scaled/normalized by transcripts per million.

Histology and immunohistochemistry

Tumors, lymph nodes, liver and lungs were fixed in 10% neutral-buffered formalin at room temperature for 24 hours, transferred to 70% ethanol, and embedded in paraffin. Histologic sections (5 µm) were stained with H&E or processed for immunohistochemistry (IHC). The primary antibodies used for IHC were against cytokeratin (1:500; Cell Signaling Technology; cat. #83957), vimentin (1:200; Cell Signaling Technology; cat. #5741), and p53 (1:200; Leica Biosystems; cat. #NCL-L-p53-CM5p). Slides were scanned using the APERIO system (Leica Biosystems). Formalin-fixed, paraffin-embedded tissue microarray (Novus Biologicals Cat No. NBP2-30229) containing cores from cSCC (n=33), basal carcinomas (n=5), verrucous carcinoma (n=1), Bowen’s disease (n=1) and normal skin (n=9) were used for immunohistochemical analysis. Other information such as age, sex, organ, diagnosis, pTNM grade information is in online supplemental table S2. Tissue microarray (TMA) glass slide was baked at 60°C overnight prior to staining. Next, deparaffinized in xylene and rehydrated through graded ethanol to distilled water. Antigen retrieval was performed using heat-induced epitope retrieval in citrate-based buffer using an EZ-retriever microwave (BioGenex). Endogenous peroxidase activity was quenched with 3% hydrogen peroxide, followed by blocking with 5% bovine serum albumin in 1× PBS to reduce non-specific binding. Glass slide was incubated with rabbit monoclonal anti-VISTA antibody (Cell Signaling Technology, cat no. 54979) at 1:250 dilution overnight at 4°C, followed by incubation with anti-rabbit HRP-conjugated secondary antibody. Signal detection was performed using 3,3′-diaminobenzidine (DAB) as chromogen, and sections were counterstained with hematoxylin, dehydrated, and cover slipped. Detailed description of the age, sex, organ, diagnosis and pTMn is in online supplemental table S2 (information provided by the vendor). Whole-slide image of the TMA was acquired using APERIO slide scanner (Leica Biosystems), and immunohistochemical staining was quantified using ImageJ software (NIH, Bethesda, Maryland, USA; Fiji distribution), only cSCC with skin origin (n=28) and normal skin (n=9) were evaluated. Under identical imaging conditions, for each tissue core case, image analysis was performed in a blinded manner. The RGB images were subjected to color deconvolution using the H-DAB plugin to separate hematoxylin and DAB signals. The DAB channel was used for quantification, and a fixed threshold was applied uniformly across all samples to identify positive staining. Positive staining was quantified as the percentage of DAB-positive area relative to the total tissue area for each core. IHC measurements obtained by ImageJ were stratified into four expression categories based on the percentage of DAB-positive area per tissue core: negative (0–3.5%), low (3.5–8%), moderate (9–15%), and high (15–50%). Thresholds were defined a priori and applied uniformly across all samples. This approach enabled semi-quantitative comparison of VISTA expression levels between normal skin and cSCC tissue cores.

Western blotting

Cells were lysed in radioimmunoprecipitation assay buffer (50 mM Tris-HCl, pH 7.4, 150 mM NaCl, 0.1% SDS, 0.5% sodium deoxycholate, 1% Triton X-100) with protease inhibitors (Roche). Protein concentrations were measured with Bradford assay (Bio-Rad). Lysates were resolved on 4–20% Sodium Dodecyl Sulfate-Polyacrylamide Gel Electrophoresis (SDS-PAGE) gels (Bio-Rad) and transferred to nitrocellulose membranes. Membranes were blocked in 5% milk/tris-buffered saline with Tween 20, incubated with primary antibodies and then incubated with horseradish peroxidase-conjugated secondary antibodies (1:2,000). Detection was done using enhanced chemiluminescence reagents (Amersham). The primary antibodies used for western blotting were against p53 (1:1,000; Cell Signaling Technology; cat. #32532), β-actin (1:5,000; Santa Cruz Biotechnology; cat. #sc81178).

Quantitative PCR

Total RNA was purified and treated with DNase using the RNeasy Mini Kit (Qiagen). cDNA synthesis was performed using a SuperScript III Reverse Transcriptase kit (Life Technologies), according to the manufacturer’s protocol. A CFX96 Touch Real-Time PCR Detection System and SsoAdvanced Universal SYBR Green Supermix (Bio-Rad) were used to perform quantitative PCR (qPCR) testing. qPCR was performed using an initial denaturation at 94°C for 2 min, 40 cycles of denaturation at 94°C for 15 s and annealing and extension at 60°C for 1 min. Relative RNA expression levels were calculated using the formula RQ=2−ΔΔCq, where RQ is the relative quantification, and Cq is the quantification cycle. Each reaction was performed in triplicate. All signals were normalized to Gapdh as an internal expression control. The primers used are listed in online supplemental table S3.

Flow cytometric analysis of tumor samples

Freshly excised skin tumors were harvested in ice-cold Roswell Park Memorial Institute Media (RPMI-1640) or DMEM (without FBS or EDTA), minced into fragments <1 mm³, and enzymatically digested in RPMI-1640 containing Collagenase I (10 mg/mL), Collagenase IV (2500 U/mL), and DNase I (200 U/mL) at 37°C for 1 hour with gentle agitation. Digestion was quenched with RPMI-1640 supplemented with 5% FBS and 2 mM EDTA, and cell suspensions were passed through a 70 µm cell strainer. Cells were pelleted by centrifugation, washed two times, and resuspended in Dulbecco’s Phosphate-Buffered Saline (DPBS) to generate single-cell suspensions. Single-cell suspensions were plated into 96-well U-bottom plates. Fc receptors were blocked with Fc block for 20 min at 4°C, followed by staining with a 2X concentrated surface antibody cocktail combined with a fixable viability dye and incubated for 30 min in the dark at room temperature or 4°C (online supplemental table S4). Subsequently, the cells were washed two times with FACS buffer, fixed and permeabilized, and incubated overnight at 4°C protected from light. The following day, cells were washed with permeabilization buffer and stained with 1×intracellular antibody cocktail prepared in permeabilization buffer for 30 min in the dark at 4°C, followed by washes in permeabilization buffer and Flow-Activated Cell Sorting (FACS) buffer. Cells were resuspended in FACS buffer, transferred to flow cytometry tubes, and analyzed on a Cytek Aurora spectral flow cytometer. Spectral unmixing was performed using single-stained compensation beads for each fluorophore, and unstained and Live/Dead controls. For Aurora-based acquisition, all antibodies were centrifuged prior to use, and Brilliant Stain Buffer was used at a 1:1 ratio with PBS for antibody cocktails containing multiple Brilliant Violet-conjugated fluorophores. Flow cytometry data were analyzed using FlowJo software (V.10), with immune cell populations identified through sequential gating on singlets, live cells, and CD45+ leukocytes, followed by lineage and subset-specific markers, as detailed in online supplemental figure 1.

COMET Lunaphore multiplex immunohistochemistry

The multiplex IHC (mIHC) panel included the following markers: FOXP3, CD206, CD4, CD8, CD86, LY6C, CD31, PD-1, CD11C, B220, CTLA-4, VISTA, CD3E, KI67, F4/80, CD11B, PANCK, and VIM. Antibody information is provided in online supplemental table S5. The antibodies were validated by MD Anderson’s Flow Cytometry and Cellular Imaging Core Facility. The secondary antibodies anti-mouse Alexa Fluorophore 555 (Thermo Fisher Scientific) and anti-rabbit Alexa Fluorophore 647 (Thermo Fisher Scientific) were used at dilutions of 1:200 and 1:400, respectively. Image registration after staining was performed using COMET Control Software. Markers were pseudocolored for visualization in the Lunaphore viewer and quantified using Visiopharm software.

Immunotherapy

All antibodies were purchased from Bio X Cell (Lebanon, New Hampshire, USA): anti‐mouse PD-1 (clone RPMI-14, cat. #BP0146), rat IgG2a isotype control (clone: 2A3, cat. #BP0089), anti-CTLA-4 (clone 9H10, cat. #BE0131), Syrian hamster IgG isotype (clone: polyclonal, cat. #BE0087), anti-VISTA (clone: 13F3, cat. #BE0310), Armenian hamster IgG (Clone: polyclonal; cat. #BE0091). Mice were randomized in this study. The anti-PD-1 antibody was administered as intraperitoneal injections of 10 µg/g body weight every 2 days for 2-week 18 s. The anti-CTLA-4, anti-VISTA, and IgG control antibodies were administered as intratumoral injections of 20 µg per tumor two times a week for 2 weeks. STING agonist c-di-GMP (InvivoGen, cat. #tlrl-nacdg) was administered intratumoral injections of 20 µg two times a week for 2 weeks.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analysis was performed using GraphPad Prism V.10.3.1 (GraphPad Software). All data are presented as means±SEMs. Two-tailed Student’s t-tests and one-way analysis of variance were used to detect significant differences between control and treatment groups. The log-rank test was used for the tumor-free survival analysis. P values<0.05 were considered statistically significant.

Histopathological analysis revealed that tumors from p53-GOF mice were well to moderately differentiated cSCC, whereas tumors from p53-GOF/Cdkn2a-LOF mice predominantly exhibited a spindle cell cSCC phenotype (figure 1C). These spontaneous well to differentiated cSCC tumors and spindle cell cSCC tumors displayed differential expression of cytokeratin, vimentin, and mutant p53 (figure 1C).

Generation of a syngeneic cSCC mouse tumor model

To establish a syngeneic mouse tumor model, we sterilely harvested and placed tumor fragments of spontaneous primary skin tumors into tissue culture conditions. Of six tumor fragments isolated from different mutant mice placed in culture, four gave rise to stable tumor cell lines. Four cell lines were selected for assessing tumorigenicity in vivo: ROCSkin-1 (p53-GOF; Cdkn2a-LOF), ROCSkin-2 (p53-LOF), ROCSkin-3 (p53-GOF), and ROCSkin-5 (p53-GOF; Cdkn2a-LOF). These lines were injected subcutaneously into both immunodeficient B6. RAG1 knockout mice and immunocompetent C57BL/6J mice (figure 2A). All tumor cell lines formed tumors in both strains, except for ROCSkin-3, which failed to grow in C57BL/6J mice. Morphologically, the tumors showed spindle cell carcinomas (online supplemental figure S2), resulting in highly vascularized and exhibited robust proliferative capacity (figure 2B,C). No evidence of distant metastasis was observed in the lymph nodes, lungs or liver of any of the mice (online supplemental figure S3). Furthermore, morphological and molecular characteristics of our model relative to other syngeneic murine cSCC cell lines are summarized in online supplemental table S6.

Genomic and transcriptomic analysis reveals mutational overlap with human cSCC and enrichment of immunosuppressive pathways

Previous studies from our group and others have contributed to elucidating the genomic mutation landscape of cSCC.8 9 Based on these datasets in cBioportal, we performed a comparative mutational analysis of the ROCSkin cell lines focusing on genes frequently mutated in the cSCC cohort (figure 3A). As expected, ROCSkin cell lines harbored p53 and Cdkn2a mutations; other frequently altered genes in the cSCC dataset, such as Fat3, Notch2, Col4a3, Itgae, and Krt20, also showed high mutation frequencies. The presence of p53 mutations was further validated by both Sanger sequencing and WES (figure 3B) and confirmed at the protein level by western blotting (figure 3C). To further characterize the genomic architecture of the ROCSkin cell lines, we performed conventional G-banded karyotype analysis. ROCSkin-1 exhibited marked chromosomal instability with chromosome numbers ranging from 62 to 140 chromosomes, with modal populations centered at 67 and 80 chromosomes. The presence of a Y chromosome confirmed a male origin of the tumor. A marker chromosome (M) was identified, and multiple non-clonal Robertsonian and tandem translocations were observed throughout the cell population, indicating ongoing chromosomal rearrangement and genomic instability (online supplemental figure S4). ROCSkin-2 displayed chromosome numbers from 67 to 138 chromosomes, with a median chromosome number of 98, consistent with a highly aneuploid genome. Cytogenetic analysis confirmed a female origin of the cell line. Marker chromosome M1 represents a derivative chromosome containing an inversion involving chromosome 12. An additional marker chromosome (M2) was identified in independent metaphase spreads. Furthermore, the presence of double-minute chromosomes, indicative of gene amplification events, highlights the extensive genomic instability and ongoing chromosomal evolution of this tumor line (online supplemental figure S5). ROCSkin-5 G-banded karyotype showed chromosome numbers ranged from 73 to 111 chromosomes, with a median chromosome number of 78, consistent with a highly aneuploid genome. Cytogenetic analysis demonstrated a female-derived cell line with widespread numerical chromosomal abnormalities (online supplemental figure S6). Like ROCSkin-1 and ROCSkin-2, ROCSkin-5 exhibited extensive aneuploidy, supporting the role of p53 pathway disruption in promoting chromosomal instability during cSCC progression. Collectively, G-banded karyotyping analysis revealed profound aneuploidy and chromosomal instability across all ROCSkin cell lines, characterized by broad numerical chromosome alterations, marker chromosomes, structural rearrangements, and evidence of ongoing clonal evolution.

We next analyzed specific genes harboring frameshift deletions, in-frame insertions, or nonsense mutations. ROCSkin-1 and ROCSkin-5 cells shared mutations in 12 interferon-related genes, while ROCSkin-2 cells exhibited nonsense mutations in genes associated with cell proliferation and endoplasmic reticulum-endosome trafficking (online supplemental table S7).

Transcriptomic profiling of ROCSkin-1 cells revealed differential gene expression of Cd274, Bst2, Lgals9, and Il33, which are implicated in tumor immunosuppression (figure 3D, online supplemental table S8). In addition, the cells overexpressed Casp1 and Pycard suggesting the potential suppression of the cGAS–STING signaling pathway. Pathway enrichment analysis indicated the upregulation of p53, chemokine, TNF, Notch, toll-like receptor, and cytosolic DNA-sensing pathways (online supplemental figure S7). Gene expression analysis of ROCSkin-2 cells revealed elevated levels of Il34, Ccl9, and Serpine2, all of which are involved in the recruitment and maturation of myeloid cells (figure 3E, online supplemental table S9), as well as significant expression of Bmp4 and Tgfbi, which are linked to tumor invasion and proliferation. Enriched pathways included glycolysis/gluconeogenesis, pyruvate metabolism, oxidative phosphorylation, fatty acid degradation, cell cycle regulation, and apoptosis pathways (online supplemental figure S8). Finally, ROCSkin-5 cells expressed the immune checkpoint molecules Bst2 and Lgals9, as well as the chemokines Cxcl14 and Cxcl5, which promote neutrophil recruitment and activation. Other upregulated genes included Anpep and Egfr, which are associated with pathological angiogenesis, and Gpx3, which enhances cellular viability by mitigating oxidative stress (figure 3F, online supplemental table S10). Pathway analysis revealed enrichment in chemokine signaling, TGF-β, cGMP–PKG, and chemical carcinogenesis pathways (online supplemental figure S9). To validate the RNA sequencing (RNA-seq) results, we performed quantitative real-time polymerase chain reaction (qRT-PCR) analysis of the top 10 differentially expressed genes in each ROCSkin cell line. Overall, 50–80% of genes showed concordant expression patterns between qRT-PCR and RNA-seq. Trp53 expression was consistent across both platforms and further confirmed by western blotting. Minor discrepancies likely reflect methodological differences in transcript detection and normalization. These validation data are presented in online supplemental figure S10.

Overall, these syngeneic ROCSkin cell lines exhibit genomic alterations in key tumor suppressors, activation of oncogenic and immunosuppressive signaling pathways, and expression of immune checkpoint regulators, providing a valuable platform for modeling tumor progression and immune evasion in cSCC.

Distinct cytokine and chemokine programs define a myeloid-dominant immune landscape across ROCSkin tumor models

To characterize the immune landscape of the tumor microenvironment, we analyzed the expression of cytokines, myeloid/lymphoid cell markers, and immune checkpoints in ROCSkin-1, ROCSkin-2, and ROCSkin-5 tumors. Total RNA was isolated from three independent tumors per cell line and analyzed by qPCR. All three tumor models exhibited similar expression of Il6, Csf1, S100a9, and Ccl9. ROCSkin-1 tumors had elevated Il33 expression (online supplemental figure S11A,B), and ROCSkin-2 tumors showed high levels of Ccl5, a chemokine implicated in inflammation and the recruitment of both myeloid and lymphoid cells. The expression of Ccl2, which promotes monocyte and macrophage recruitment, was restricted to ROCSkin-2 and ROCSkin-5 tumors. All three tumor types had elevated levels of Cd68 and Itgam (encoding CD206), suggesting a predominance of M2-like tumor-associated macrophages (M2-TAMs) with high levels of immune checkpoints (online supplemental figure S11C,D). In contrast, all three tumor types also had low expression of Cd3, Cd4, Cd8, and Foxp3 suggesting limited T-cell infiltration compared with the myeloid population (online supplemental figure S11E).

Tumor immune microenvironment differs markedly across ROCSkin syngeneic cSCC models

Comprehensive flow cytometry profiling revealed differences in the TIME among ROCSkin-1, ROCSkin-2, and ROCSkin-5 tumors. Analysis of CD45+ tumor-infiltrating leukocytes demonstrated that all three models were predominantly myeloid-rich; however, ROCSkin-1 tumors exhibited a lower relative abundance of lymphoid populations compared with ROCSkin-2 and ROCSkin-5, which showed increased proportions of suppressive myeloid cells (figure 4A,B). ROCSkin-1 tumors displayed increased proliferation (Ki67+), elevated programmed death-ligand 1 (PD-L1) expression, and markedly increased VISTA expression within CD45⁻ cells, including higher VISTA mean fluorescence intensity in PD-L1+ CD45⁻ cells (online supplemental figure S12A). In contrast, ROCSkin-2 and ROCSkin-5 tumors exhibited reduced proliferation and immune checkpoint expression phenotype (online supplemental figure S12A). Within the CD4+ T-cell compartment, ROCSkin-1 tumors showed increased frequencies of FoxP3+ Tregs, including activated CD44+CTLA-4+ effector Tregs, whereas ROCSkin-2 tumors were enriched for interferon (IFN)-γ+ “fragile” Tregs (figure 4C). CD8+ T-cell analysis showed enhanced cytotoxic activity in ROCSkin-2 tumors, with higher frequencies of granzyme B+ IFN-γ+ effector cells. In addition, ROCSkin-2 and especially ROCSkin-5 tumors showed lower expression of exhaustion and inhibitory markers, including PD-1 and VISTA (figure 4D, online supplemental figure S12B). Finally, while ROCSkin-1 showed higher frequencies of proliferative CD8a+T cells, the CD8 T-cell compartment also had higher frequencies of cells expressing VISTA, PD-1 and Tim-3 exhaustion markers compared with ROCSkin-2 and ROCSkin-5 (figure 4D, online supplemental figure 12C). Interestingly, natural killer (NK) cells in ROCSkin-1 tumors exhibited significantly increased expression of inhibitory immune checkpoint molecules PD-1 and VISTA, indicative of an exhausted or dysfunctional phenotype, despite preserved cytotoxic marker expression (online supplemental figure S12D). Myeloid cell profiling revealed that while TAMs were the predominant immune population across all models, ROCSkin-1 tumors were enriched for iNOS+ M1-like macrophages, whereas ROCSkin-2 and ROCSkin-5 tumors exhibited increased CD206+ Arg1+ M2-like macrophages (figure 4E). Specifically, ROCSkin-5 showed significantly reduced VISTA expression, consistent with an immunosuppressive macrophage phenotype (figure 4E). Additionally, granulocytes were significantly enriched in ROCSkin-1 tumors, but no differences were observed in Arg1 and VISTA expression levels across the three tumor models (figure 4F). Interestingly, ROCSkin-2 tumors show elevated dendritic cell infiltration (online supplemental figure S12E). Collectively, these data demonstrate that ROCSkin-1 tumors exhibit higher proliferation and immune checkpoint expression in their non-immune cell compartment with elevated granulocyte infiltration, whereas ROCSkin-2 and ROCSkin-5 tumors display progressively immune-suppressed phenotypes characterized by diminished T-cell cytotoxicity and enhanced suppressive myeloid populations. These findings establish ROCSkin models as a spectrum of immune states relevant for studying mechanisms of immune evasion and therapeutic response in cSCC.

We further assessed immune cell infiltration using mIHC COMET-Lunaphore platform. All tumor types showed infiltration of CD3+CD4+FoxP3+ Tregs, with varying expression of immune checkpoints (online supplemental figure S13A). ROCSkin-2 tumors harbored a higher proportion of PD-1+ Tregs, whereas ROCSkin-5 tumors contained more VISTA+ Tregs. Cytotoxic T-lymphocyte associated protein 4 (CTLA-4), a critical regulator of Treg function, was expressed at similar levels across all tumor types (online supplemental figure 13B). We detected low levels of infiltrating CD3+CD8+ cytotoxic T cells, the majority co-expressed the exhaustion markers PD-1 and VISTA, consistent with a dysfunctional or suppressed phenotype (online supplemental figure 13C). We detected an elevated infiltration of CD11b+ myeloid cells in ROCSkin-1 and ROCSkin-5 tumors (online supplemental figure 13D). Collectively, these data indicate that the syngeneic ROCSkin models exhibit an immunosuppressive tumor microenvironment characterized by M2-TAM infiltration, Tregs, and exhausted CD8+ T cells.

cSCC tumor models exhibit differential responses to immunotherapy

Anti-PD-1 agents such as cemiplimab and pembrolizumab are currently approved as first-line therapies for patients with advanced cSCC. Given the high infiltration of Tregs, M2-TAMs, and PD-1 expression in our cSCC models, we tested the efficacy of anti-PD-1 antibody therapy alone or in combination with a STING agonist (c-di-GMP). Prior studies from our lab have demonstrated that this combination can elicit robust responses in immune-excluded “cold” tumors.33

ROCSkin-1, ROCSkin-2 and ROCSkin-5 cells were subcutaneously injected into the flanks of immunocompetent C57BL/6 mice. The mice were randomized to receive PBS/IgG isotype control, the STING agonist alone, the anti-PD-1 antibody alone, or a combination of the anti-PD-1 and STING agonist. Treatment began when tumors reached 100–150 mm³. ROCSkin-1 and ROCSkin-5 tumors were resistant to all treatments, including the combination, with no significant improvement in survival (figure 5A,C). In contrast, ROCSkin-2 tumors were sensitive to the combination treatment only, and had a longer survival compared with monotherapy (figure 5B).

To further investigate immune resistance mechanisms, we analyzed the expression of immune checkpoints in the ROCSkin tumor microenvironment. VISTA expression was high across all three models, while CTLA-4 expression was lower but detectable in each tumor model (figure 4, online supplemental figure S11). These findings are notable given that VISTA is expressed in multiple immune compartments, including macrophages, CD8+ T cells, and CD11b+ cells, as well as in tumor cells.34 Thus, anti-VISTA antibody treatment could target both tumor cells and immunosuppressive populations. Additionally, anti-CTLA-4 therapy may offer complementary benefit by modulating regulatory CD4+ T cells within the tumor microenvironment.

Based on the partial responsiveness of the ROCSkin-2 model to the STING agonist and anti-PD-1 antibody, we selected this model to further evaluate combinations with anti-VISTA and anti-CTLA-4 antibodies. We hypothesized that intratumorally administered anti-VISTA and anti-CTLA-4 antibodies would synergize locally with the STING agonist, reducing systemic effects while anti-PD-1 treatment would further enhance antitumor efficacy (figure 6A). As shown in figure 6B,C, most of the treatments exhibited antitumor activity by the time tumor controls reached the end point (online supplemental table S11). Mice receiving the STING agonist alone or in combination with an immunotherapeutic antibody, with the exception of the anti-CTLA-4 antibody showed a significant partial response. Combination treatment with anti-PD-1 plus anti-CTLA-4 or anti-PD-1 plus anti-VISTA did not significantly improve survival compared with untreated controls (online supplemental figure S20). With respect to tumor growth, the triple combination therapies (STING+anti-PD-1+anti-CTLA-4 and STING+anti-PD-1+anti-VISTA) showed significantly reduced tumor burden compared with control and several single and dual therapies; however, their effect was comparable to the STING+anti-VISTA dual-therapy (online supplemental table S11). In contrast, pairwise survival analysis demonstrated that both triple combination therapies were associated with significantly better overall survival relative to control and dual therapies, including STING+anti-VISTA (Benjamini-Hochberg adjusted p=0.0053 for both comparisons; figure 6D, online supplemental table S12). These findings suggest that although STING+anti-VISTA achieves tumor-volume control similar to the triple-combination therapies, the addition of anti-PD-1 conferred a greater survival benefit. Collectively, these results support the potential of stimulating the innate immune response in combination with immune checkpoint-targeting antibodies to overcome resistance in cSCC.

VISTA is a potential therapeutic target in human cSCC

To assess the clinical relevance of VISTA expression in human cSCC, we performed IHC using human cSCC tissue microarrays of patients samples of normal skin and cSCC. In normal skin samples, VISTA expression was largely absent; 87.5% of samples were negative, and only 12.5% exhibited low protein levels. In contrast, VISTA was frequently expressed in cSCC samples, with 38.2% showing high expression, 29.4% showing moderate expression, 23.5% showing low expression, and only 8.8% showing no expression (figure 7A–C). These findings demonstrate a significant upregulation of VISTA in cSCC compared with normal skin and highlight VISTA as a potentially promising immunotherapeutic target in PD-1 refractory tumors.

Discussion

In this study, we developed GEMMs and generated syngeneic tumor cell lines for cSCC based on conditional mutations in p53 and Cdkn2a, two of the most frequently mutated genes in human cSCC. Our findings demonstrate that the dual loss of Trp53 and Cdkn2a in keratinized epithelium leads to spontaneous skin tumor formation in immunocompetent mice, with increased incidence and accelerated tumor onset compared with single-mutant counterparts. The use of Krt14-driven Cre recombinase enabled tissue-specific recombination, while backcrossing into the C57BL/6J background provided compatibility for immune-oncology studies.

Histologically, the tumors that arose in these models ranged from well-differentiated cSCC to spindle cell cSCC, reflecting the heterogeneity observed in human cSCC. Tumors derived from p53-GOF/Cdkn2a-LOF mice exhibited a more mesenchymal phenotype, consistent with prior studies linking p53 mutations and Cdkn2a deletion to epithelial-to-mesenchymal transition (EMT) and increased tumor aggressiveness.35 Previous work showed that p53 mutations in the stratified epithelium increase susceptibility to cSCC development following UV treatment.36 Additionally, p53 inactivation has been shown to be required for spontaneous cSCC formation in Rb1 mutant mice,37 and mice with p53 and Cdkn2a mutations with a mixed genetic background have been shown to spontaneously develop different types of tumors, demonstrating the cooperation of both genes in tumor development.38 In agreement with this, our present data demonstrate that p53 and Cdkn2a mutations in the skin epithelium can drive the development of cSCC.

Like the development of CIT, PDVC57, Pam212T, and 9AS1 cSCC cell lines,39–44 our generation of B6 syngeneic ROCSkin tumor cell lines from spontaneous tumors enabled the establishment of transplantable models that retain key molecular and immune features. These models provide a robust platform for mechanistic studies and therapeutic interrogation. Genomic and transcriptomic profiling of the ROCSkin-1, ROCSkin-2, and ROCSkin-5 cell lines revealed both shared and distinct mutations and different gene expression profiles across pathways implicated in human cSCC, including interferon signaling, cytokine/chemokine responses, Notch signaling, and DNA sensing pathways. ROCSkin-1 and ROCSkin-5 cells shared mutations in several IFN-related genes, whereas ROCSkin-2 cells harbored unique mutations in genes involved in cellular proliferation and endosomal sorting. These data suggest that distinct mutational profiles among ROCSkin tumors may drive differences in tumor progression, immune evasion, and therapeutic response.

Recent studies using inducible mutant mouse models demonstrated that EMT-driven chromosomal instability, in cooperation with p53 and Cdkn2a mutations, disrupts the cluster of interferon ligand genes on chromosome 4C4 (syntenic to human 9p21), primarily through intraexonic alterations that compromise gene integrity.45 Additional studies showed that the inactivation of interferon gene clusters or deletion of human 9p21 enhances tumor evasion mechanisms and promotes the development of cold immune-excluded tumors.46 47 These findings are particularly relevant, as interferon genes play a critical role in the innate immune response in early tumorigenesis and can influence resistance to immunotherapy, as observed in our ROCSkin tumor models.

Flow cytometry studies indicate that ROCSkin-2 tumors are myeloid rich but retain an appreciable T-cell infiltrate with checkpoint restrained features, including PD-1+ T cells and an activated CTLA-4+ regulatory compartment. In addition, ROCSkin-2 are enriched for IFN-γ+ “fragile” Tregs, and CD11c+ MHC+ dendritic cells; these cell subsets are often reprogrammed by immunotherapy to stop suppressing the immune system, thereby boosting antitumor immunity.48 49 We therefore interpret ROCSkin2 as an immune-suppressed but immunologically targetable model. STING agonist likely supplies the inflammatory and antigen-presenting signals needed to enhance T-cell recruitment and priming, while PD-1 blockade restores effector function. The additional benefit of VISTA or CTLA-4 targeting is consistent with non-redundant inhibitory programs within the myeloid and Treg compartments, respectively, and may prevent compensatory suppression during STING-induced inflammation, providing a mechanistic basis for the therapeutic sensitivity of ROCSkin2 to these combinations.

ROCSkin-1 and ROCSkin-5 tumors exhibited resistance to STING agonist plus anti-PD-1 therapy through distinct immune mechanisms. ROCSkin-1 tumor stroma displayed a high proliferation rate and elevated inhibitory checkpoint expression, including PD-L1 and VISTA, with a high infiltration of Ly6G+ granulocytes. In this context, STING-driven activation may amplify compensatory inhibitory pathways, rendering PD-1 blockade alone insufficient to restore effective antitumor immunity. In contrast, ROCSkin-5 tumors exhibited reduced immune activation and limited effector T-cell function, consistent with a poorly primed or partially immune-excluded state in which PD-1 blockade lacks sufficient effector cells. Together, these findings indicate that resistance to STING plus PD-1 therapy arises from either checkpoint redundancy in immunosuppressed tumors or inadequate immune priming, highlighting the need for tailored combination strategies in ROCSkin-1 and ROCSkin-5 tumor models.

The ROCSkin tumor models exhibited high levels of immunosuppressive myeloid cells, particularly M2-TAMs. These models recapitulate the key immunosuppressive features of human cSCC, including the high infiltration of M2-TAMs, MDSCs and Tregs.50–54 Such features likely contribute to the cold, immune-excluded phenotype, which is associated with poor response to immunotherapy. Approximately 50% of patients with locally advanced and metastatic cSCC and 65% of those with recurrent or metastatic head and neck SCC do not have a response or develop resistance to anti-PD-1 therapy.10 13 55–57

An important finding was the high expression of the immune checkpoint molecule VISTA in ROCSkin tumors. VISTA plays multiple roles in supporting the function of MDSCs, regulating NK cell activation, promoting Treg survival, limiting antigen presentation by antigen-presenting cells, and maintaining T cells in a quiescent state.58–60 Recent studies using single-cell RNA-seq and spatial transcriptomics provided a comprehensive view of the immune landscape of human cSCC, confirming the high infiltration of MDSCs, macrophages, and various subsets of exhausted T cells.61 VISTA is broadly expressed in myeloid, lymphoid and tumor cells, making it an attractive target for cancer immunotherapy. Therapeutic evaluation of our ROCSkin models revealed variable responses to immunotherapy. While ROCSkin-1 and ROCSkin-5 tumors were resistant to the anti-PD-1 antibody and STING agonist combination, ROCSkin-2 tumors exhibited partial responsiveness, indicating inherent differences in immune landscape and therapeutic sensitivity. These findings support the rationale for combinatorial strategies targeting multiple immune checkpoints and innate immune pathways to overcome resistance in cold immune-excluded tumors, as demonstrated in previous studies.62 63

The combination of anti-VISTA and anti-CTLA-4 antibodies synergized with PD-1 blockade and STING agonism in ROCSkin-2 tumors, significantly enhancing antitumor responses and improving overall survival. The blockade of CTLA-4 effectively disrupted immunosuppressive mechanisms in cSCC, suggesting that patients with detectable CTLA-4 expression may particularly benefit from such therapeutic strategies.64 Consistent with findings in human cSCC, the ROCSkin tumors had M2-TAMs, Tregs, CD8+ T lymphocytes, and tumor cells with high levels of VISTA expression, making VISTA a compelling target in both the tumor and stromal compartments of the tumor microenvironment. Supporting this rationale, studies in melanoma mouse models showed that the peritumoral delivery of anti-VISTA antibody in combination with anti-PD-1 and anti-4-1BB antibody elicited significant tumor regression.65

Our results further highlight the clinical relevance of targeting VISTA in cSCC. VISTA expression was detected in a large proportion of patients with cSCC samples but rarely observed in normal skin, supporting its potential as a selective therapeutic target. These findings are consistent with recent reports of VISTA expression in clinical studies of non-melanoma skin cancers.19

There are some limitations to our study. A weakness of this study is that the mutant mouse model does not recapitulate the UV-induced mutational burden and neoantigen landscape characteristic of most human cSCCs. Consequently, responses to immune checkpoint blockade observed in this model should be interpreted within the context of a genetically engineered, low-tumor mutational burden system rather than as a direct representation of UV-driven human cSCC. While our syngeneic models human cSCC in terms of genetics and immune contexture, they did not show metastasis, which may limit their use in studying mechanisms of dissemination and resistance in metastatic disease settings. Furthermore, while STING agonism showed promise in combination regimens, the pharmacodynamics and delivery methods may require further optimization to achieve consistent results across tumor types. Local delivery of hydrogels loaded with STING agonists, in combination with immunotherapeutic antibodies, may offer the advantage of using lower compound doses while achieving greater efficacy at the disease site.66 67

In summary, we present a suite of genetically defined, immunocompetent mouse models of cSCC with key features of the human disease. Our findings identify VISTA as an important immune checkpoint in cSCC and provide strong preclinical evidence supporting immunotherapy with combinations of STING agonist and PD-1, CTLA-4, STING, and VISTA blockade. These models offer valuable tools for future studies of tumor-immune interactions, mechanisms of immune escape, and rational immunotherapy design in cutaneous malignancies.
