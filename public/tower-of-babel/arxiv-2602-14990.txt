A formula for the Euler class of foliations
Alessandro V. Cigna

Abstract
Given a cooriented branched surface ℬ \mathcal{B} fully carrying a foliation ℱ \mathcal{F} , we use the dual graph of ℬ \mathcal{B} to define a simplicial 1-cycle Γ ⁡ ( ℬ ) \Gamma(\mathcal{B}) representing the Poincaré dual of the Euler class of ℱ \mathcal{F} . This construction provides an effective way to track how the Euler class of a foliation carried by a hierarchy changes when the orientation of a decomposing disk is reversed. We also show how our formula generalises previous results of Lackenby and Dunfield.



1
Introduction

In the study of taut foliations on 3-manifolds, the Euler class plays a central role.
Given a cooriented foliation
\mathcal{F}
on a compact
3
-manifold
M
, the
Euler class

e(\mathcal{F})\in H^{2}(M;\Z)
is the obstruction to finding a nonvanishing section for the plane bundle
T\mathcal{F}\to M
. When
M
has toroidal boundary and
\mathcal{F}
is transverse to it, we can fix a nonvanishing section
s
of
TF|_{\partial M}
and define a relative Euler class
e(\mathcal{F},s)\in H^{2}(M,\partial M;\Z)
. This time,
e(\mathcal{F},s)
is the obstruction to extending
s
to a nonvanishing section over
M
. When the section
s
is not explicitly defined, we will just write
e(\mathcal{F})=e(\mathcal{F},s)\in H^{2}(M,\partial M;\Z)
and we will understand that
s
is an outward-pointing section.

In
[
20
]
, Thurston showed that the Euler class of a taut foliation is related to the topological complexity of the surfaces sitting inside
M
:

Theorem 1.1

(Thurston’s Inequality)
.

Let
\mathcal{F}
be a taut foliation of a compact oriented
3
-manifold
M
, with
\mathcal{F}
transverse to
\partial M
. Let
S\subset M
be a properly embedded oriented surface such that each component of
\partial S
is either a leaf of or transverse to
\mathcal{F}\cap\partial M
. Then the following inequality holds:

|\langle e(\mathcal{F}),[S]\rangle|\leq|\chi(S)|

with
e(\mathcal{F})\in H^{2}(M,\partial M;\Z)
and
[S]\in H_{2}(M,\partial M;\Z)
. Moreover, the equality is achieved if
S
is a union of leaves of
\mathcal{F}
oriented coherently.

Another way to think of Thurston’s Inequality is through the
Thurston norm
. The Thurston norm of a homology class
\alpha\in H_{2}(M,\partial M;\Z)
is the minimal realisable value of
|\chi(S^{\prime})|
among all properly embedded orientable surfaces
S
representing
\alpha
, and
S^{\prime}
is
S
minus its sphere or disk components
[
20
]
. Thurston’s Inequality implies that the relative Euler class of a taut foliation lies in the unit ball of the dual Thurston norm on
H^{2}(M,\partial M;\Z)
. If the taut foliation has a compact leaf of negative Euler characteristic, then its Euler class actually lies on the boundary of the dual Thurston ball.

The
Euler class-one Conjecture
, posed by Thurston in
[
20
]
, stated that every class in
H^{2}(M,\partial M;\Z)
with unit dual Thurston norm and subject to a necessary parity condition is the relative Euler class of some taut foliation on
M
. The conjecture was proved for vertices of the unit ball of the dual Thurston norm by Gabai
[
11
,
6
]
. Then the conjecture was disproved by Gabai and Yazdi in
[
21
]
and
[
6
]
. By means of the
Fully Marked Surface Theorem
, Yazdi exhibited infinitely many examples of closed hyperbolic
3
-manifolds for which the conjecture does not hold. Lately, Liu showed that counterexamples to the conjecture are not sporadic. Indeed, every oriented closed hyperbolic
3
-manifold
N
admits some finite cover
M
for which the conjecture does not hold
[
17
]
.

The Thurston norm is also a valuable tool in its own right. The shape of its unit ball has a close relationship with many other remarkable objects associated with the manifold: fibrations, pseudo-Anosov flows, representations, and Floer homologies are just some of them. Consult
[
14
]
for an overview on the Thurston norm and some of its applications. Although this norm is notoriously difficult to compute, Thurston’s Inequality forces the unit ball to lie between the two hyperplanes
\{\alpha\in H_{2}(M,\partial M;\R)\,|\;\langle e(\mathcal{F}),\alpha\rangle=\pm 1\}
.

Understanding the Euler class of a foliation therefore becomes a key problem, both from a conceptual and a computational point of view. The contribution of this paper is a formula for the Euler class of a foliation in terms of a branched surface that carries it.

If
\mathcal{B}
is a cooriented branched surface whose exterior consists of product balls, we endow the dual graph of
\mathcal{B}
with a weights structure on its edges and refer to this data as the
maw dual graph

\Gamma(\mathcal{B})
(Definition
4.3
). Theorem
4.5
and Lemma
4.4
together imply the following result.

Theorem 1.2
.

Let
\mathcal{B}
be a cooriented branched surface in
M
whose exterior consists of product balls. Let
\mathcal{F}
be a foliation carried by
\mathcal{B}
. The maw dual graph
\Gamma(\mathcal{B})
represents in
H_{1}(M)
the Poincaré dual to the relative Euler class
e(\mathcal{F})\in H^{2}(M,\partial M)
.

A special case is when
\mathcal{B}
is the branched surface associated with a taut sutured manifold hierarchy ending in a number of product balls. Suppose that the last step of a taut sutured manifold hierarchy
\mathcal{H}_{+}
is a decomposition along a disk
D
. We can consider a different hierarchy
\mathcal{H}_{-}
that differs from
\mathcal{H}_{+}
just in the chosen orientation of
D
. How much do the Euler classes of the two obtained foliations differ from each other? This question is answered by the following lemma.

Lemma
4.8
.

Let
\mathcal{F_{+}}
be a taut foliation on
M
arising from the groomed sutured manifold hierarchy
\mathcal{H}_{+}
given by

(M,\partial M)\rightsquigarrow...\rightsquigarrow(M_{r},\gamma_{r})\overset{D}{\rightsquigarrow}\text{product sutured balls}

where
D
is a decomposing disk. Let
\mathcal{H}_{-}
be the groomed sutured manifold hierarchy coinciding with
\mathcal{H}_{+}
until
(M_{r},\gamma_{r})
, yet ending with

(M_{r},\gamma_{r})\overset{\bar{D}}{\rightsquigarrow}\text{product sutured balls.}

Let
\mathcal{F}_{-}
be a taut foliation carried by
\mathcal{H}_{-}
. Then the difference
e(\mathcal{F}_{+})-e(\mathcal{F_{-})}
is Poincaré dual to
(2-|D\cap\gamma_{r}|)i_{*}\delta
, where
i_{*}\delta\in H_{1}(M)
is the image under the inclusion induced map
i_{*}:H_{1}(M_{r})\to H_{1}(M)
of the Poincaré-Lefschetz dual
\delta
to
[D]\in H_{2}(M_{r},\partial M_{r})
.

As discussed above, the Euler classes of taut foliations are closely related to the Thurston norm. Ongoing work by the author uses the maw dual graph to compute the Thurston norm for certain families of arborescent links and to produce new counterexamples to the Euler class–one Conjecture for manifolds with toroidal boundary. In fact, one can completely classify which classes of dual Thurston norm one arise as Euler classes of taut foliations in the exterior of positive
2
-bridge links.

The Euler class is also a useful tool for studying the left-orderability of the fundamental group of a rational homology sphere. Indeed, if such a manifold admits a taut foliation with vanishing Euler class, its fundamental group is left-orderable
[
3
, Theorem 8.1]
. Although the converse is not true in general
[
2
]
, it would be of interest to understand when the maw dual graph represents the zero class in homology.

The idea of defining a graph dual to the Euler class of a foliation is not new. Lackenby used a graph dual to an ideal triangulation to mimic the role of the Euler class of a taut foliation
[
15
]
. In
[
5
]
, given a foliar orientation on a one-vertex triangulation of a
3
-manifold, Dunfield represented the Euler class of the corresponding taut foliation by a cocycle supported on a graph. In Section
5
, the relation between Lackenby and Dunfield’s constructions and the maw dual graph is investigated.

Structure of the paper.
Sections
2
and
3
are devoted to recalling the definitions and some of the main properties related to sutured manifolds, branched surfaces and Euler classes. In Section
4
we define the maw dual graph and we prove Theorem
4.5
. Then we apply it to establish Lemma
4.8
. Finally, in Section
5
, we study the relation between the maw dual graph and previous work linking dual graphs and taut foliations.

Notation.
If
P
is a manifold,
|P|
is the number of connected components of
P
. If oriented,
\bar{P}
will denote
P
with reversed orientation. If
S
is a properly embedded two-sided surface in an oriented
3
-manifold
M
, we use
N(S)
to refer to a closed small tubular neighbourhood
S\times[-1,1]
of
S
in
M
. The symbol
\partial N(S)
will indicate
S\times\{\pm 1\}
, whilst
\overset{\circ}{N}(S)
will indicate
S\times(-1,1)
.

2
Sutured manifolds and branched surfaces

Two classical ways to construct foliations on a
3
-manifold
M
are the following:

•

Find a taut sutured manifold hierarchy for
M
.

•

Define a branched surface
\mathcal{B}
on
M
and show it fully carries a lamination. If the exterior of
\mathcal{B}
in
M
is a product sutured manifold, the lamination can be extended to a foliation. Moreover, by adding hypotheses on
\mathcal{B}
(e.g., cooriented, taut, etc.), one can ensure the constructed foliations have good properties.

We now present sutured manifold hierarchies and branched surfaces. As we will see, the second approach extends the first and is in fact more general. The advantage of the sutured manifold hierarchies point of view is that it requires no need to analyse the properties of the underlying branched surface.

2.1
Sutured manifolds and their hierarchies

Sutured manifolds were introduced by Gabai
[
8
]
to construct taut foliations on
3
-manifolds.

Definition 2.1

(Sutured manifold)
.

A
sutured manifold
is a pair
(M,\gamma)
, where
M
is a compact oriented
3
-manifold and
\gamma=A(\gamma)\cup T(\gamma)
is a subset of
\partial M
satisfying the following conditions. The set
A(\gamma)
is a union of pairwise disjoint annuli and
T(\gamma)
is a union of pairwise disjoint tori. A
suture
is the core of an annulus of
A(\gamma)
, considered as an oriented simple closed curve. Every region of
R(\gamma):=\partial M-\overset{\circ}{\gamma}
has a normal orientation. We call
R_{+}(\gamma)
the union of the components of
R(\gamma)
whose normal vector points out of
M
, and
R_{-}(\gamma)
the union of the remaining components. The last requirement for
(M,\gamma)
is that the normal orientations on
R(\gamma)
are coherent with the orientation of the sutures: if
\delta\subset\partial R(\gamma)
is a boundary component with the induced orientation, then
\delta
is homologous to a suture in
H_{1}(\gamma)
.

We will say that
(M,\gamma)
is a
product sutured manifold
, if the sutured manifold
(M,\gamma)
is homeomorphic to
(R\times[0,1],\partial R\times[0,1])
for some compact oriented surface
R
with non-empty boundary.

If
\partial M
is a union of tori and
S
is a properly embedded oriented surface, call
(M(S),\gamma(S))
the sutured manifold with
M(S):=M-\overset{\circ}{N}(S)
and
\gamma(S):=\partial M-\overset{\circ}{N}(S)
.

Definition 2.2

(Taut foliations)
.

A
foliation
on a sutured manifold
(M,\gamma)
is a family
\mathcal{F}
of disjoint injectively immersed surfaces - called
leaves
of
\mathcal{F}
- that altogether cover
M
and such that a small neighbourhood of each point is foliated as an open subset of
\R\times[0,+\infty)\times[0,+\infty)
subdivided into horizontal half-planes
\R\times[0,+\infty)\times\{z\}
. Bearing this local model in mind, we ask points interior to
\gamma
to look like points on
\R\times\{0\}\times(0,+\infty)
, points interior to
R(\gamma)
as points on
\R\times(0,+\infty)\times\{0\}
, and points on
\partial\gamma
as points on
\R\times\{0\}\times\{0\}
.

The foliation
\mathcal{F}
is
taut
if it is cooriented with normal orientation pointing inward on
R_{-}(\gamma)
and outward on
R_{+}(\gamma)
, and every leaf intersects either a closed transversal or a properly embedded transverse arc with endpoints on
R(\gamma)
. We furthermore require that a taut foliation
\mathcal{F}
on
(M,\gamma)
has no
2
-dimensional Reeb components
at the boundary, see Figure
1
.

Figure 1
:
A
2
-dimensional Reeb component is a subset of leaves of the foliation covering an annulus. The boundary of the annulus is composed of leaves with nonparallel coorientation. The interior leaves are lines.

The idea behind sutured manifolds is simple: assume
S
is a properly embedded oriented surface in a manifold
M
with toroidal boundary. Suppose that we want to construct a foliation of
M
where
S
is a leaf. Cut
M
open along
S
to obtain the sutured manifold
(M(S),\gamma(S))
. Here, both
R_{+}(\gamma(S))
and
R_{-}(\gamma(S))
are copies of
S
. So, if we find a foliation of
M(S)
transverse to
\gamma(S)
and tangent to
R_{+}(\gamma(S))
and
R_{-}(\gamma(S))
, then we can construct a foliation of
M
with leaf
S
, by just gluing back
R_{+}(\gamma(S))
and
R_{-}(\gamma(S))
together.

Potentially, this technique can be iterated: we find a new surface
S^{\prime}
in
M(S)
and cut
M(S)
open along
S^{\prime}
. Then we define a new sutured manifold
(N^{\prime},\gamma^{\prime})
, where
N^{\prime}=M(S)-\overset{\circ}{N}(S^{\prime})
and
\gamma^{\prime}
is defined so that a foliation of
N^{\prime}
transverse to
\gamma^{\prime}
and tangent to
R(\gamma^{\prime})
can be modified to get a foliation of
(M(S),\gamma(S))
. Then we can decompose
(N^{\prime},\gamma^{\prime})
along a new surface
S^{\prime\prime}
and so on and so forth.

Finally, suppose that at some point we get a product sutured manifold
(R\times[0,1],\partial R\times[0,1])
. Then the process is done, because we can just choose the product foliation on this last sutured manifold.

This philosophy stands behind the notions of
sutured manifold decompositions
and
sutured manifold hierarchies
.

, Definition 3.1]
corrected as in

, Correction 0.3]
.

Definition 2.3

(Surface decompositions)
.

Let
(M,\gamma)
be a sutured manifold. A
decomposing surface
for
(M,\gamma)
is a properly embedded oriented surface
S\subset M
such that for every component
\lambda
of
S\cap\gamma
one of the following holds:

(i)

\lambda
is a properly embedded nonseparating arc in
\gamma
;

(ii)

\lambda
is a simple closed curve in an annular component
A
of
\gamma
in the same homology class as
A\cap s(\gamma)
;

(iii)

\lambda
is a homotopically nontrivial curve in a toral component
T
of
\gamma
, and if
\delta
is another component of
T\cap S
, then
\lambda
and
\delta
represent the same homology class in
H_{1}(T)
.

We further require that:

(iv)

No component of
\partial S
bounds a disk in
R(\gamma)
and no component of
S
is a disk
D
with
\partial D\subset R(\gamma)
.

The decomposing surface
S
defines a
sutured manifold decomposition

(M,\gamma)\overset{S}{\rightsquigarrow}(M^{\prime},\gamma^{\prime}),

where
M^{\prime}=M-\overset{\circ}{N}(S)
and
R(\gamma^{\prime})
is obtained by smoothing together
\partial N(S)
to
R(\gamma)\cap M^{\prime}
according to the coorientation, as in Figure
2
. More precisely:

\displaystyle\gamma^{\prime}

\displaystyle=(\gamma\cap M^{\prime})\cup N(S^{\prime}_{+}\cap R_{-}(\gamma))\cup N(S_{-}^{\prime}\cap R_{+}(\gamma)),

\displaystyle R_{+}(\gamma^{\prime})

\displaystyle=((R_{+}(\gamma)\cap M^{\prime})\cup S^{\prime}_{+})-\overset{\circ}{N}(A(\gamma)),

\displaystyle R_{-}(\gamma^{\prime})

\displaystyle=((R_{-}(\gamma)\cap M^{\prime})\cup S^{\prime}_{-})-\overset{\circ}{N}(A(\gamma)),

where
S_{\pm}^{\prime}
are the two components of
N(S)
, labelled according to coorientation.

Definition 2.4

(Hierarchies)
.

A
taut sutured manifold hierarchy
for the sutured manifold
(M,\gamma)
is a sequence of sutured manifold decompositions

(M,\gamma)\overset{S_{1}}{\rightsquigarrow}...\overset{S_{n}}{\rightsquigarrow}(M_{n},\gamma_{n})

such that
(M_{n},\gamma_{n})
is a product sutured manifold.

The hierarchy above is said
complete
if
(M_{n},\gamma_{n})
is a union of product balls.

Theorem 2.5
.

[
7
]
If
(M,\partial M)
has a taut sutured manifold hierarchy, then
(M,\partial M)
admits a taut foliation.

Definition 2.6
.

A
product disk
for
(M,\gamma)
is a decomposing disk
D
with
|\partial D\cap\gamma|=2
.

Remark 2.7
.

•

Every product sutured manifold can decomposed into a disjoint union of product balls through a disjoint collection of product disks. In particular, every taut sutured manifold hierarchy can be completed, meaning that it can be extended to a complete hierarchy.

•

The converse holds as well: if
(M,\gamma)
is a sutured manifold with
R(\gamma)\neq\emptyset
and it is completely decomposable through product disks, then
(M,\gamma)
is a product sutured manifold
[
8
]
.

•

Decomposing along product disks is never a gamble: by
[
7
, Lemma 3.12]
, in a product disk decomposition
(M,\gamma)\overset{D}{\rightsquigarrow}(M^{\prime},\gamma^{\prime})
,
(M,\gamma)
admits a taut foliation if and only if
(M^{\prime},\gamma^{\prime})
does.

2.2
Branched surfaces.

Definition 2.8

(Branched surface)
.

A
branched surface
in a
3
-manifold
M
is a
2
-complex
\mathcal{B}
that locally looks like an open subset of the models in Figure
3
. Notice that a branched surface is naturally endowed with a tangent space at every point. Moreover, we always assume the branched surface to be transverse to
\partial M
.

The
branching locus
of
\mathcal{B}
is the set
\brl(\mathcal{B})
of nonmanifold points of
\mathcal{B}
. It is a properly embedded graph on
\mathcal{B}
, whose vertices have degree either one or four, and the degree-four vertices are called
triple points
. The branching locus
\brl(\mathcal{B})
is naturally endowed with the
maw vector field
: this is any nonvanishing smooth vector field tangent to
\mathcal{B}
and always pointing in the branching direction. See again Figure
3
. Once a normal orientation on
\partial M
is chosen, we extend the maw vector field on
\partial\mathcal{B}
by requiring it always points in the normal direction.

A
sector
of
\mathcal{B}
is the metric closure of any connected component of
\mathcal{B}-\brl(\mathcal{B})
.

The branched surface
\mathcal{B}
is
cooriented
if there is a choice of normal direction on each sector of
\mathcal{B}
that is coherent at the branching locus. Equivalently,
\mathcal{B}
is cooriented if its tangent bundle
T\mathcal{B}
is.

Definition 2.9

(
I
-fibered neighbourhood and exterior)
.

An
I
-fibered neighbourhood
of a branched surface
B
in a
3
-manifold
M
is a regular neighborhood
N(B)
foliated by interval fibers that intersect
B
transversely, see Figure
4
. The portion
N(\mathcal{B})\cap\partial M
of
N(\mathcal{B})
is an interval-bundle over
\mathcal{B}\cap\partial M
too. We will think of
\mathcal{B}
as embedded inside
N(\mathcal{B})
, and endowed with a fiber-collapsing map
\pi_{\mathcal{B}}:N(\mathcal{B})\to\mathcal{B}
.

The
exterior
of
\mathcal{B}
in
M
is the sutured manifold
(M(\mathcal{B}),\gamma(\mathcal{B}))
where:

•

M(\mathcal{B}):=M-\overset{\circ}{N}(\mathcal{B})
;

•

R(\gamma(\mathcal{B}))
is given by the points on
\partial N(\mathcal{B})
transverse to the
I
-fibers. This set is usually called the
horizontal boundary
of
N(\mathcal{B})
, and indicated with
\partial_{h}N(\mathcal{B})
;

•

\gamma(\mathcal{B})
can be decomposed as the union
(\partial M-\partial N(\mathcal{B}))\cup\partial_{v}N(\mathcal{B})
. The
vertical boundary

\partial_{v}N(\mathcal{B})
consists of the portion of
\partial N(\mathcal{B})
interior to
M
and tangent to the interval fibers.

The connected components of
M(\mathcal{B})
are called
complementary regions
of
\mathcal{B}
.

:

N(\mathcal{B})
of a branched surface
\mathcal{B}
close to a triple point of the branching locus. The grey intervals are some fibers. The small shaded areas are part of the vertical boundary
N_{v}(\mathcal{B})
.

Definition 2.10

(Taut branched surfaces)
.

A cooriented branched surface
\mathcal{B}\subset M
is
taut
if its exterior is a taut sutured manifold and for every sector
s
of
\mathcal{B}
there is a closed loop in
M
passing through
s
and intersecting
\mathcal{B}
transversely always with the same sign.

Definition 2.11

(Laminations)
.

A
lamination
in a compact
3
-manifold
M
is a family of disjoint injectively immersed surfaces which altogether foliate a closed subset of
M
.

Given a branched surface
\mathcal{B}\subset M
, we say that a lamination
\mathcal{L}
is
carried
by
\mathcal{B}
if it can be isotoped to lie inside an
I
-fibered neighbourhood
N(\mathcal{B})
in a way that each leaf of
\mathcal{L}
is transverse to the
I
-fibers. We say that
\mathcal{L}
is
fully carried
by
\mathcal{B}
if, furthermore, each interval fiber intersects at least one leaf of
\mathcal{L}
.

As already anticipated, branched surfaces can be used to construct foliations. Indeed, suppose that
\mathcal{B}\subset M
is a branched surface fully carrying a lamination
\mathcal{L}
. Then we can isotope
\mathcal{L}
so to assume that the horizontal boundary
\partial_{h}N(\mathcal{B})
is contained in a union of leaves of
\mathcal{L}
. Since
\mathcal{L}
is fully carried by
\mathcal{B}
, when we cut
N(\mathcal{B})
along
\mathcal{L}
, we get an interval-bundle over a surface. In particular,
\mathcal{L}
can be extended to a foliation
\mathcal{F}_{0}
of
N(\mathcal{B})
transverse to the interval fibers, and such that
\mathcal{F}_{0}
restricts to a product foliation on the vertical boundary
\partial_{v}N(\mathcal{B})
. If the exterior of
\mathcal{B}
is a product sutured manifold, we can further extend
\mathcal{F}_{0}
to a foliation
\mathcal{F}
on
M
through the product foliations of the complementary regions. We will briefly say that
\mathcal{B}
carries the foliation
\mathcal{F}
.

If
\mathcal{B}
is cooriented, then
\mathcal{F}
is cooriented too. If
\mathcal{B}
is taut, then the same holds for
\mathcal{F}
.

The reasoning above motivates the importance of determining whether a branched surface fully carries a lamination. A very general criterion to obtain an affirmative answer consists in verifying that the branched surface is
laminar
, and we refer the interested reader to
[
16
]
for deeper insight.

2.3
From sutured manifold hierarchies to branched surfaces.

Let

(M,\gamma)\overset{S_{1}}{\rightsquigarrow}(M_{1},\gamma_{1})\overset{S_{2}}{\rightsquigarrow}...\overset{S_{n}}{\rightsquigarrow}(M_{n},\gamma_{n})

be a sequence of sutured manifold decompositions. We can imagine each
M_{i}
to be naturally embedded in
M
, and each surface
S_{i}\subset M
with
\partial S_{i}=S_{i}\cap(\partial M\cup S_{1}\cup...\cup S_{i-1})
. For every
i=1,..,n
, we want to define a cooriented branched surface
\mathcal{B}_{i}\subset M
so that
(M(\mathcal{B}_{i}),\gamma(\mathcal{B}_{i}))=(M_{i},\gamma_{i})
. This is the content of
[
10
, Construction 4.16]
.

Set
\mathcal{B}_{1}=S_{1}
. Suppose then that
\mathcal{B}_{i}
has been defined, and let us construct
\mathcal{B}_{i+1}
. After possibly a small isotopy, we can suppose
\partial S_{i+1}=S_{i+1}\cap(\partial M\cup\mathcal{B}_{i})
and that at most triple self-intersection points arise on
\mathcal{B}_{i}\cup S_{i+1}
. The coorientation on
\mathcal{B}_{i}
and on
S_{i+1}
gives instructions on how to smooth out

close to

3
Euler classes of plane bundles

Given an oriented circle bundle
\pi:E\to X
on a cellular complex, the Euler class
e(\pi)
is an element of
H^{2}(X)
measuring the obstruction to finding a section of
\pi
, hence how far
\pi
is from being the trivial circle bundle. We will now define the Euler class and state the main properties that we will need throughout the paper. A good resource to learn the details of these results is
[
4
, Chapter 4]
, which the reader is suggested to consult in case of further interest.

Let
\pi:E\to X
be an oriented circle bundle over a finite
n
-dimensional cellular complex
X
. Let
X^{i}
be the
i
-th skeleton of
X
. We attempt to define a section
s
to
\pi
inductively on the skeletons. Choose arbitrarily a section
s_{0}
on the vertices
X^{0}
, then extend it continuously to a section
s_{1}
on
X^{1}
. Given a
2
-cell
\Delta\subset X^{2}
, we would like to extend the section
s_{1}
already defined on
\partial\Delta
over the whole
\Delta
. Since
\Delta
is contractible, the restriction of
\pi
to it is trivial, and therefore isomorphic to
\Delta\times S^{1}
. Through this identification, the section
s_{1}
on the boundary induces a map
\partial\Delta\to S^{1}
with degree
c_{s_{1}}(\Delta)
. Once
c_{s_{1}}(\Delta)
is defined for every
2
-cell
\Delta
, we have a cellular
2
-cochain
c_{s_{1}}
in
C^{2}_{CW}(X)
.

The following properties are classical. For a proof, consult for example
[
4
, Section 4.3]
.

Theorem 3.1
.

The following properties hold:

1.

The
2
-cochain
c_{s_{1}}
does not depend on the choice of the trivializations
E_{|\Delta}\cong\Delta\times S^{1}
.

2.

c_{s_{1}}
is in fact a
2
-cocycle, hence representing a class
[c_{s_{1}}]\in H^{2}(X)
.

3.

If
t_{1}
is another section of
\pi:E_{|X^{1}}\to X^{1}
, then
c_{s_{1}}
and
c_{t_{1}}
are cohomologous.

4.

The section
s_{1}
can be extended to a section of
E_{|X^{2}}
if and only if
[c_{s_{1}}]=0\in H^{2}(X)
, if and only if it can be extended to a section of
\pi:E\to X
.

Suppose now that we know a section
\sigma:Y\to E_{|Y}
defined on a subcomplex
Y
of
X
. We might wonder whether
\sigma
can be extended to a section over the whole
X
and proceed in a similar fashion as above. Namely, pick an arbitrary section
s_{1}
on
X^{1}\cup Y
that coincides with
\sigma
on
Y
and define for every
2
-cell
\Delta
in
X-Y
the number
c_{s_{1}}(\Delta)
. As we are fixing the section on
Y
and we just care about cells not in
Y
, the integer-valued function
c_{s_{1}}
gives this time a cellular relative cochain in
C^{2}_{CW}(X,Y)
. The analogue of Theorem
3.1
holds also in this more general context, guaranteeing that
c_{s_{1}}
induces a relative cocycle in
H^{2}(X,Y)
that vanishes if and only if
\sigma
can be extended to a section over
X
.

The Poincaré–Hopf Theorem

Let
S
be a compact oriented surface, possibly with boundary, and endowed with a Riemannian metric. Consider the unit tangent bundle
\pi:T_{1}S\to S
and a section
m
defined on
\partial S
. The Poincaré–Hopf Theorem establishes that one can compute the Euler class of
\pi
by just drawing a suitable vector field on
S
. We now quickly review the significant definitions, and we address the reader to
[
1
]
for detailed proofs.

Definition 3.2

(Index)
.

Let
X
be a vector field on
S
possibly with isolated zeroes in the interior of
S
. If
z\in S
is a zero of
X
, then pick a small disk
D_{z}\subset S
having just the zero
z
in it. Pick a trivialisation of
\pi
over
D_{z}
and consider the induced map
X_{|\partial D_{z}}:\partial D_{z}\to S^{1}
. The
index
of
X
at
z
is the degree of
X_{|\partial D_{z}}
. Call
index sum
of
X
the number
\iota_{X}
given by the sum of all the indices of
X
at its zeroes.

Lemma 3.3
.

Let
S
be a disk and
m
a nonvanishing vector field on
\partial s
. Pick a trivialisation for
\pi:T_{1}S\to S
and call
c_{m}(S)
the degree of the resulting map
m_{|\partial S}:\partial S\to S^{1}
. If
X
is a smooth vector field on
S
with isolated zeroes in the interior of
S
and coinciding with
m
at the boundary, then

c_{m}(S)=\iota_{X}.

Proof.

Pick a small disk
D_{z}
for every singularity
z
of
X
, as for the previous definition. After possibly shrinking, assume the
D_{z}
s to be disjoint from each other. The restriction of
X
to
B:=S-\cup_{z}\overset{\circ}{D_{z}}
is nonvanishing, hence induces a trivialisation of the tangent bundle of
B
. In particular, we obtain a map
B\to S^{1}
. Since
B
gives a bordism between
\partial S
and
\cup_{z}\partial D_{z}
, the lemma follows for example from
[
1
, Proposition 12.5]
.
∎

The following is a very well-known result in differential topology and several proofs are easy to access among the literature. One such source is
[
1
, Theorem 14.4]
.

Theorem 3.4

(Poincaré–Hopf Theorem)
.

Let
S
be a compact oriented surface and let
X
be a smooth vector field on
S
transverse to the boundary. If
X
has isolated zeroes, then

e(\pi)=\iota_{X}=\chi(S),

where
e(\pi)\in H^{2}(S,\partial S)\cong\Z
is the Euler class of
\pi:T_{1}(S)\to S
relative to any boundary section transverse to the boundary.

Euler classes of taut foliations.

Definition 3.5

(Euler class of a foliation)
.

Let
M
be a compact oriented manifold with toroidal boundary, and let
\mathcal{F}
be a foliation on
M
transverse to
\partial M
. Fix a cellular structure on
M
and consider the tangent bundle
T\mathcal{F}
on
M
. By means of a Riemannian metric for
T\mathcal{F}\to M
, we can study the unit circle bundle
\pi:T_{1}\mathcal{F}\to M
. Since
\mathcal{F}
is transverse to
\partial M
, it admits an outward pointing section
\sigma
for
\pi_{|\partial M}
. The
Euler class
of
\mathcal{F}
is the relative
2
-cocycle

e(\mathcal{F})=[c_{s_{1}}]\in H^{2}(M,\partial M)

where
s_{1}
is any section of
\pi
on
M^{1}\cup\partial M
coinciding with
\sigma
on
\partial M
. In other words,
e(\mathcal{F})
is the Euler class of
\pi
relative to an outward pointing section on
\partial M
.

We might as well define the Euler class
e(\mathcal{F},-\sigma)
relative to the inward pointing section
-\sigma
on
\partial M
. The vector fields
\sigma
and
-\sigma
are homotopic through sections of
\pi_{|\partial M}
. Indeed, they differ by a
180^{o}
clockwise rotation on each circle of the oriented bundle
T_{1}\mathcal{F}|_{\partial M}
. Therefore, the identity

e(\mathcal{F},\sigma)=e(\mathcal{F},-\sigma)

must hold.

4
The maw dual graph

Let
M
be a compact oriented
3
-manifold whose boundary is a (possibly empty) union of tori. Fix a transverse orientation for
\partial M
. Let
\mathcal{B}
be a cooriented branched surface in
M
whose exterior is a union of sutured product balls. In this section, we construct a
1
-cycle
\Gamma(\mathcal{B})\subset M
transverse to
\mathcal{B}
. When
\mathcal{B}
fully carries a lamination, then such a lamination can be extended to a cooriented foliation
\mathcal{F}
of
M
, and the
1
-cycle
\Gamma(\mathcal{B})\in H_{1}(M)
is dual to the relative Euler class
e(\mathcal{F})\in H^{2}(M,\partial M)
. For example, this is the case when
\mathcal{B}
comes from a sutured manifold hierarchy, or when
\mathcal{B}
is laminar.

Here is the idea of the construction: since the exterior of
\mathcal{B}
is a union of balls, we can define a cellular structure on
M
having
\mathcal{B}
as
2
-dimensional skeleton. The sectors of
\mathcal{B}
are a union of
2
-cells, the branched locus and
\partial B
are part of the
1
-skeleton. If
\mathcal{B}
carries a foliation
\mathcal{F}
, such foliation is (after a local isotopy) tangent to
\mathcal{B}
and the maw vector field gives a nonvanishing section of the restriction of the plane field to most of the
1
-skeleton.
The cycle
\Gamma(\mathcal{B})
is an embedded graph such that, for any sector
s
of
\mathcal{B}
, the algebraic intersection number between
\Gamma(\mathcal{B})
and
s
equals the obstruction to extend the maw vector field from
\partial s
to the interior of
s
. Such obstruction is what we will call the
maw Euler characteristic
of
s
.

Definition 4.1

(Maw Euler characteristic)
.

Let
s
be a sector of a branched surface
\mathcal{B}
and let
v
be a vertex of the branched locus
\brl(\mathcal{B})
on
\partial s
, i.e.,
v
is either a triple point on
\partial s
or an extreme of
s\cap\partial M
. We say that
v
is a
double corner
(or double vertex) for
s
if the maw vector field points inward on one edge at
v
and outward on the other (e.g.,
v
is a triple point and
s
is a mixed sector at
v
). We say that
v
is a
smoothable corner
(or smoothable vertex) otherwise. See Figure
6
left. Indicate with
\dc(s)
the number of double corners on
\partial s
. The
maw Euler characteristic
of
s
is

\chi_{m}(s):=\chi(s)-\frac{1}{2}\dc(s).

\partial s
, where every smoothable vertex can indeed be smoothed (therefore inducing no corners), and each double vertex gives rise to a couple of right angles.

Lemma 4.2
.

Let
s
be a sector of a branched surface
\mathcal{B}
. Let
m
be the maw vector field on the boundary of
s
. Suppose
X
is a smooth vector field with isolated zeroes in
s
that coincides with
m
on
\partial s
. Then

\iota_{X}=\chi_{m}(s)

(1)

where
e(Ts,m)\in H^{2}(s,\partial s)\cong\Z
is the relative Euler class of the tangent bundle of
s
relative to the boundary section
m
.

Proof.

Consider
s
as a surface with corners by introducing two right angles on
\partial s
for every double vertex as in Figure
6
right, where we have slightly isotoped
m
so that each edge of
\partial s
is either transverse or tangent to it. Call
\partial_{\tau}s
the union of the new edges of
\partial s
which are tangent to
m
. Slightly modify
X
too in a neighbourhood of
\partial s
so to coincide with the new

. Consider the smooth surface
ds=s\cup\bar{s}
(without corners) obtained by doubling
s
along
\partial_{\tau}s
, as in Figure

. Here
\bar{s}
indicates
s
with opposite orientation. Let
\bar{X}
be the vector field on
\bar{s}
equal to
-X
on each point of
\bar{s}
. The vector fields
X
and
\bar{X}
patch together in
ds
to a well defined vector field

transverse to the boundary. By construction
\iota_{X}=\iota_{\bar{X}}
. The Poincaré–Hopf Theorem implies

\chi(ds)=\iota_{X}+\iota_{\bar{X}}=2\iota_{X}.

As

Now, we define the graph candidate to represent the Poincaré dual of the Euler class of a foliation carried by the branched surface
\mathcal{B}
.

Definition 4.3

(Maw dual graph)
.

Let
\mathcal{B}
be a cooriented branched surface in an ambient
3
-manifold
M
whose complement consists of
3
-balls. Fix a base point on each sector and inside each complementary region of
\mathcal{B}
. The
maw dual graph
of
\mathcal{B}
is the weighted oriented graph
\Gamma(\mathcal{B})
in
M
constructed as follows:

•

Vertices: the vertices of
\Gamma(\mathcal{B})
are the base points in the complementary regions to
\mathcal{B}
;

•

Edges: for every sector
s
of
\mathcal{B}
, there is an arc
a(s)
joining the vertices corresponding to the complementary regions to
\mathcal{B}
adjacent at
s
. The arc
a(s)
intersects
s
transversely and only in its base point. It is oriented as the coorientation of
s
and carries a weight equal to
\chi_{m}(s)
.

Lemma 4.4
.

Let
\mathcal{B}
be a cooriented branched surface in
M
whose exterior is a union of sutured product balls. The maw dual graph
\Gamma(\mathcal{B})
is a
1
-cycle.

Proof.

We need to show that at each vertex
v
of
\Gamma(\mathcal{B})
, the total weight of the edges pointing into
v
is the same as the total weight of the edges pointing out of it. The vertex
v
is the base point of a product sutured ball
B
in the exterior of
\mathcal{B}
. The edges pointing out of
v
correspond altogether with the sectors of
\mathcal{B}
that carry
R_{+}(B)
, which is a disk. As vector fields on the sectors of
\mathcal{B}
restricting to the maw vector field at the boundary patch together to a vector field on their union and the index formula is additive, the total weight pointing out of
v
is
\chi(R_{+}(B))
(in this case equal to
1
). Similarly, the total weight pointing into
v
is
\chi(R_{-}(B))
. As
\chi(R_{+}(B))=\chi(R_{-}(B))
the claim follows.
∎

Theorem 4.5
.

Let
\mathcal{B}\subset M
be a cooriented branched surface whose exterior is a union of product balls. Let
\mathcal{F}
be a foliation fully carried by
\mathcal{B}
. Let
\Gamma
be an oriented weighted graph in
M
. Suppose that
\Gamma
is a
1
-cycle and intersects each sector
s
exactly
\chi_{m}(s)
times according to orientation (e.g., if
\Gamma=\Gamma(\mathcal{B})
). Then
\Gamma
represents in
H_{1}(M)
the Poincaré dual of the relative Euler class
e(\mathcal{F})\in H^{2}(M,\partial M)
.

Proof.

As the exterior of
\mathcal{B}
is a union of taut sutured balls, each sector of
\mathcal{B}
embeds in the tangential boundary of some such ball. Therefore, each sector of
\mathcal{B}
is a planar surface with boundary. On every sector, choose a finite family of disjoint properly embedded arcs whose complement is a
2
-cell. By adding these arcs to the branching locus
\brl(\mathcal{B})
and to
\partial\mathcal{B}
, we obtain the
1
-skeleton of a cellular structure on
M
having one
2
-cell
s_{0}
for every sector
s
of
\mathcal{B}
, and one
3
-cell for every complementary ball. Extend the maw vector field to the
1
-skeleton of this cellulation by choosing arbitrarily a vector field transverse to the new arcs and tangent to
\mathcal{B}
. In order to carry out this extension, we first might need to slightly isotope the maw vector field close to the endpoints of the newly added arcs. Call the extended vector field
m
again. By definition of the Euler class, there is a relative
2
-cochain
c_{m}
representing
e(\mathcal{F})
, and such that
c_{m}(s_{0})
is the obstruction to find a nonvanishing section of
T\mathcal{F}
on
s_{0}
that coincides with
m
on
\partial s_{0}
. Since
s_{0}
is contained in
\mathcal{B}
, the plane bundle
T\mathcal{F}|_{s_{0}}
is isotopic to the tangent bundle of
s_{0}
and so

c_{m}(s_{0})=\iota_{X}=\chi_{m}(s_{0})=\chi_{m}(s),

where
X
is a smooth vector field with isolated zeroes in
s
that coincides with
m
on
\partial s
. The first equality comes from Lemma
3.3
, the second equality is the content of Lemma
4.2
. Since
c_{m}
and the algebraic intersection cochain
\langle\cdot,\Gamma\rangle
coincide on the free basis of the cellular
2
-chains of
M
, we conclude that
e(\mathcal{F})=\langle\cdot,[\Gamma]\rangle
.
∎

Corollary 4.6
.

Let

(M,\partial M)\overset{S_{1}}{\rightsquigarrow}...\overset{S_{n}}{\rightsquigarrow}(M_{n},\gamma_{n})

be a complete groomed sutured manifold hierarchy, and let
\mathcal{B}\subset M
be the induced branched surface. If
\mathcal{F}
is a taut foliation carried by the hierarchy, then the Poincaré dual of the relative Euler class
e(\mathcal{F})\in H^{2}(M,\partial M)
is represented by the maw dual graph
\Gamma(\mathcal{B})
.

Producing nonisotopic foliations

Sometimes, we might want to understand how the Euler class of a foliation changes when it is carried by a hierarchy, and we reverse the orientation to one of its decomposing surfaces. The following lemma establishes the case for “depth-most” decomposing disks. Here, by

we mean that the decomposing surface is the one of the last decomposition in a given complete sutured manifold hierarchy.

Lemma 4.8
.

Let
\mathcal{F_{+}}
be a taut foliation on

(2)

y-x
and
w-z
are homologous to
\delta
, we get
a_{+}(c)-a_{-}(c)
is homologous to
2\delta
. Right: the local view of
\mathcal{B}_{+}
around a vertex
c\in D\cap\partial B_{+}
. Here,
a_{+}(c)=x
and
a_{-}(c)=y

x-y

.

5
Previous work on graphs and taut foliations

In the present section, we overview some results by Lackenby
[
15
]
and Dunfield
[
5
]
. In both works, graphs were used to play the role of Euler classes of taut foliation. We will explain how our construction is related to theirs.

Taut ideal triangulations of
3
-manifolds.

In
[
15
]
, Lackenby introduced the notion of
taut triangulations
for a
3
-manifold
M
with nonempty boundary. Roughly speaking, a taut triangulation of
M
is a triangulation of
M-\partial M
by ideal
3
-simplices with a special angled structure. Two faces of each
3
-simplex have normal orientation pointing inward, and the other two have normal orientation pointing outward. An edge between two coherently cooriented faces has interior angle
\pi
, otherwise it has zero interior angle. It is understood that the glueing map between two taut
3
-simplices must respect their normal orientations. Moreover, the total angle around every edge of the triangulation should be
2\pi
. See Figure
10
left for a local picture of
M
close to an edge of the triangulation.

Figure 10
:
Left: local view of the branched surface carried by a taut triangulation close to an edge. Right: the resulting branched surface after a flattening operation. A new rectangular sector is introduced (dark grey in the picture). The maw vector field is now well-defined on the branched surface, as indicated by the red arrows.

The
2
-skeleton of a taut triangulation yields a branched surface
\mathcal{B}
in
M
. Unfortunately, the branched locus of
\mathcal{B}
is not
generic
, i.e. it is not modelled as in Figure
3
. However, as no triple points arise, the branched surface fully carries a lamination
\mathcal{L}
. Notice that we can flatten
\mathcal{B}
out close to each edge as in Figure
10
by introducing a new rectangular sector. After this procedure, the new branched surface still fully carries
\mathcal{L}
, and a maw vector field is now well-defined.

The cusped structure of taut simplices makes the exterior of
\mathcal{B}
a union of product balls. In particular,
\mathcal{B}
carries a cooriented foliation that is taut if
M
is compact (e.g., the graph
G
defined below can be split into a number of closed transversals intersecting every leaf of the foliation).

For this reason, taut ideal triangulations were employed by Lackenby to give foliation-free proofs of classical results on manifolds admitting taut foliations. For instance, it was shown in
[
15
]
that manifolds admitting taut triangulations are irreducible and
\partial
-irreducible. Moreover, a new proof of Gabai’s Theorem that the minimal genus of a knot equals its singular genus was presented.

In order to substitute the Euler class of the foliation carried by a taut triangulation with a combinatorial analogue, Lackenby uses the graph
G
in
M
dual to the taut triangulation. Every edge of
G
inherits the normal orientation of the
2
-simplices of the triangulation. Since every vertex of
G
has exactly two incoming edges and exactly two outgoing ones,
G
is a cycle and hence represents an element of
H_{1}(M)
. Through a study of the singular normal surfaces carried by the triangulation, Lackenby shows:

Proposition 5.1
.

[
15
, Proposition 11]
Let
M
be a compact
3
-manifold with a taut triangulation and let
G
be the graph described above. For every compact oriented surface
F
without disk or sphere components, and for every continuous map
(F,\partial F)\to(M,\partial M)
, we have

|\langle[G],[F]\rangle|\leq-2\chi(F).

As the equality is achieved by a surface carried by the branched surface associated with the triangulation, such a surface minimises the topological complexity among (singular) surface representatives of its homology class. We show that:

Lemma 5.2
.

Let
M
be a compact
3
-manifold with a taut triangulation,
\mathcal{F}
a taut foliation carried by it, and
G
the dual graph described above.
The relative Euler class
e(\mathcal{F})\in H^{2}(M,\partial M)
is Poincaré dual to
-\frac{1}{2}[G]\in H_{1}(M)
.

Proof.

First flatten the branched surface induced by the triangulation close to every edge, as in Figure
10
. Call
\mathcal{B}
the resulting branched surface. Endow
\partial M
with outward-pointing normal orientation. Since
\mathcal{B}
has a well-defined maw vector field, the proofs of Lemma
4.4
and of Theorem
4.5
can be carried out verbatim. The associated maw dual graph
\Gamma_{+}
represents the Poincaré dual to
e(\mathcal{F})
. The sectors of
\mathcal{B}
are of two types: hexagons arising as truncated
2
-simplices or rectangles arising from the flattening procedure. The branching direction points outside at every edge of the hexagons and points inside at every edge of the rectangle. As
\partial M
is normally oriented outwardly, hexagonal sectors have
\chi_{m}=+1
whilst rectangular sectors have
\chi_{m}=-1
. In particular,

[\Gamma_{+}]=[G]+[\beta],

where
\beta\subset M
is a simple closed curve intersecting every rectangular sector once with negative sign, see Figure
11
.

Figure 11
:
Left: the dual graph
G
coloured red. Right: the subdivision of the boundary of a
3
-simplex into sectors after the flattening operation. The local arc of the curve
\beta
is coloured in blue.

Now consider the inward-pointing normal orientation on
\partial M
. This time, hexagonal sectors of
\mathcal{B}
have
6
double corners, hence
\chi_{m}=-2
. Rectangular sectors are sink sectors this time, and
\chi_{m}=+1
. The maw dual graph
\Gamma_{-}
associated with the new orientation of the boundary satisfies

[\Gamma_{-}]=-2[G]-\beta.

Since the two boundary sections are homotopic, they induce the same relative Euler class
e(\mathcal{F})
. Therefore
[\Gamma_{+}]=[\Gamma_{-}]
implies
[\beta]=-\frac{3}{2}[G]
and

[\Gamma_{+}]=[G]+[\beta]=[G]-\frac{3}{2}[G]=-\frac{1}{2}[G].

∎

Proposition
5.1
then gives a new foliation-free proof of Thurston’s Inequality for taut foliations carried by taut ideal triangulations and singular surfaces:

Theorem 5.3
.

Let
M
be a compact manifold admitting a taut triangulation, and let
\mathcal{F}
be a carried foliation. Then for every compact oriented surface
S
without disk or sphere components, and for every continuous map
(S,\partial S)\to(M,\partial M)
, we have

|\langle e(\mathcal{F}),[S]\rangle|\leq|\chi(S)|.

(3)

Expression (
3
) is commonly referred to as
Thurston’s Inequality
. Thurston first proved it in
[
20
]
for taut foliations and properly embedded surfaces. The main idea was to isotope the surface so that only saddle tangencies occurred with the foliation. Gabai extended this proof to singular surfaces, first for finite-depth foliations
[
7
]
, then in the general case
[
12
]
. As Gabai explains in the introduction of
[
12
]
, for
C^{2}
taut foliations, a minimal surface proof of the most general result was already known by putting together works of Sullivan
[
19
]
, Schoen and Yau
[
18
]
and Hass
[
13
]
.

Foliar orientations.

In
[
5
, Section 7]
, Dunfield used a special kind of triangulation to construct taut foliations on closed
3
-manifolds. Given a triangulation
\mathcal{T}
of a closed
3
-manifold
M
, an
edge orientation
is a choice of orientation on the edges of
\mathcal{T}
. An edge orientation is
acyclic
if no
2
-simplex of
\mathcal{T}
has a directed cycle as boundary orientation. In particular, every triangle has two
short edges
and one
long edge
, see Figure 6 of
[
5
]
. The existence of an acyclic edge orientation guarantees we can define a cooriented branched surface
\mathcal{B}
in
M
dual to the triangulation, see Figure 7 of
[
5
]
. An acyclic edge orientation is
foliar
if the associated branched surface is laminar in the sense of Li
[
16
]
and its exterior consists of product balls. In fact, the property of being foliar can be completely described just in terms of the edge orientation, i.e., without referring to the branched surface
[
5
]
. It follows that if
\mathcal{T}
admits a foliar orientation, then
M
has a taut foliation.

If the manifold
M
has a taut foliation with vanishing Euler class, then its fundamental group is left-orderable, and this is of significant interest for the L-space Conjecture
[
5
]
. Dunfield then shows how to compute the Euler class of a taut foliation induced by a foliar orientation. This is now explained.

Suppose that the triangulation
\mathcal{T}
on
M
is endowed with a foliar orientation. An edge
\epsilon
of
\mathcal{T}
is
mixed
for the
3
-simplex
\Delta
if
\epsilon
appears on the
1
-skeleton of
\Delta
as the long edge of a face and a short edge for another face.

Since the sectors of
\mathcal{B}
generate the simplicial
2
-chains of
M
, we can define a simplicial
2
-cochain
\phi
by assigning an integer to each of them. For every sector
D
of
\mathcal{B}
, we put

\phi(D):=1-\frac{1}{2}\text{mixed}(\epsilon),

where
\epsilon
is the edge of
\mathcal{T}
dual to
D
, and
\text{mixed}(\epsilon)
counts the number of times
\epsilon
appears as a mixed edge in every tetrahedron of
\tau
. After this definition, Dunfield shows:

Theorem 5.4
.

[
5
, Theorem 9.2]
Suppose
\mathcal{F}
is the foliation of
M
associated to a foliar orientation of a
1
-vertex triangulation
\mathcal{T}
. Then the cochain
\phi
is a cocycle representing the Euler class
e(\mathcal{F})\in H^{2}(M)
.

The original proof of this result consists in defining a vector field on
\mathcal{B}
to compute
e(\mathcal{F})
.

Notice that our formula for the Euler class can also be used to prove the same theorem, also after removing the assumption that the triangulation has exactly one vertex. Indeed, given a sector
D
, the triple point on
\partial D
inside a tetrahedron
\Delta
gives rise to a double corner for
D
if and only if the dual edge
\epsilon
to
D
is mixed for
\Delta
, see Figure 7 of
[
5
]
. Since
D
is a disk,
1-\frac{1}{2}\text{mixed}(\epsilon)
is exacty the maw Euler characteristic of
D
.
