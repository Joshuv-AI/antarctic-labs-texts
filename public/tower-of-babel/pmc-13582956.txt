Reverse bioengineering of the liver: developmental principles for next-generation liver-on-a-chip systems
Authors: Ken-ichiro Kamei
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractDespite major advances in liver-on-a-chip and organoid technologies, most current in vitro liver models remain limited. Here, it is argued that these limitations are fundamentally conceptual rather than purely technical. Reverse bioengineering is introduced as a unifying design framework for liver-on-a-chip systems, in which human liver development is treated as the primary engineering blueprint rather than adult hepatic phenotype as the endpoint. Existing cell sources, liver organoids, and liver-on-a-chip platforms are critically evaluated, demonstrating that each capture complementary but incomplete aspects of liver development. Pluripotent stem cells uniquely enable access to intrinsic developmental programs and cellular diversity, organoids partially reconstruct early developmental trajectories through self-organization, and microphysiological systems provide extrinsic cues without fully encoding developmental execution. Finally, futured directions are outlined, highlighting developmentally coordinated multi-organ systems, quantitative developmental benchmarks, and the emerging role of multi-omics-enabled Digital Twins and artificial intelligence in guiding and interpreting liver-on-a-chip design.Subject terms: Stem-cell biotechnology, Stem-cell differentiation, Tissue engineering

Introduction

The liver is a central metabolic and homeostatic organ whose functions extend far beyond xenobiotic detoxification (Fig. 1). It integrates nutrient sensing, lipid and glucose metabolism1, endocrine signaling2, immune surveillance3, and systemic inflammatory responses4, while dynamically coordinating with the gut5, pancreas6, vasculature7, and immune system. Reflecting this centrality, liver dysfunction underlies a broad spectrum of human diseases, ranging from acute drug-induced liver injury (DILI)8,9 to chronic conditions such as metabolic dysfunction-associated steatotic liver disease (MASLD10; formerly NAFLD11), fibrosis9,12, cirrhosis9, and primary liver cancers13. Despite decades of intensive research, the ability to model human liver physiology and pathology in vitro with sufficient predictive power remains limited, creating a major bottleneck for fundamental biology, drug development, and safety assessment.

Liver-on-a-chip and other microphysiological systems (MPSs) have emerged as promising platforms to address these challenges by offering controlled microenvironments, perfusion, and multi-cellular organization that surpass conventional static cultures14,15. Substantial progress has been made in engineering microfluidic architectures, optimizing flow and oxygenation, integrating multiple hepatic cell types, and benchmarking functional outputs such as albumin secretion and cytochrome P450 (CYP) activity. Parallel advances in stem-cell biology have enabled the generation of hepatocyte-like cells and liver organoids from pluripotent stem cells, further expanding the toolkit available for in vitro liver modeling16,17. Together, these developments have fueled optimism that engineered liver models may eventually replace or substantially reduce reliance on animal studies.

However, despite increasing technical sophistication, most in vitro liver models continue to exhibit limited functional stability, immature phenotypes, poor metabolic zonation, and inconsistent disease manifestations15,18,19. Importantly, incremental improvements in materials, device design, or perfusion alone have not resolved these challenges. This persistent gap suggests that the limitations of current liver-on-a-chip platforms are not merely technical, but conceptual. Most systems are designed to reproduce selected aspects of adult liver physiology, implicitly assuming that recreating steady-state conditions is sufficient for hepatic function to emerge.

In contrast, in vivo, the liver is not assembled as a static entity, but is progressively constructed through a tightly orchestrated developmental process. Human liver development proceeds from foregut endoderm specification to liver bud formation, endothelial-mediated expansion, and gradual acquisition of metabolic and structural specialization20. Many defining features of adult liver function, including metabolic zonation, vascular specialization, and stress responses, are not pre-programmed endpoints, but emergent properties of this developmental trajectory shaped by temporal signaling, mechanical forces, vascularization, and inter-organ interactions. Ignoring these origins risks engineering systems that appear phenotypically correct yet lack the regulatory logic required for robustness and predictivity.

In this perspective, it is argued that advancing liver-on-a-chip technology requires a fundamental shift in design philosophy, from reproducing adult hepatic phenotypes to reconstructing the developmental processes by which liver function arises. Reverse bioengineering is introduced as a unifying framework that treats human liver development as the primary blueprint for engineering design. Using this perspective, existing in vitro liver models, including cell-based systems, organoids, and microfluidic platforms, are critically evaluated through the lens of developmental fidelity. Finally, how a development-first approach reshapes applications in disease modeling, drug discovery, and toxicity testing is discussed, and future directions toward developmentally coordinated, multi-organ, and computationally integrated liver-on-a-chip systems are outlined.

Reverse bioengineering: reconstructing liver development as a design principle

The analyses above reveal a fundamental asymmetry in current in vitro liver models. Pluripotent stem cell-derived systems capture intrinsic developmental programs but fail to complete maturation. Organoids self-organize into multicellular architectures yet stall at early developmental states21,22. Liver-on-a-chip platforms impose physiological control but typically assume adult liver phenotypes as fixed targets. These approaches address complementary aspects of liver biology, but none of them alone reconstructs the process by which liver structure and function arise in vivo.

To overcome this fragmentation, reverse bioengineering is proposed as a unifying framework for next-generation liver-on-a-chip systems. Reverse bioengineering inverts the conventional design logic: rather than starting from available engineering tools and asking which liver functions can be reproduced, it begins with in vivo liver development and asks which biological and physical principles must be reconstructed to enable those functions to emerge.

Defining reverse bioengineering: what reverse bioengineering is—and is not

Reverse bioengineering is a development-first design philosophy in which engineering specifications are derived from biological processes rather than imposed independently of them as static targets. Although conventional organoid technology typically relies on creating a “permissive niche,” identifying what signals are sufficient to trigger self-organization, reverse bioengineering focuses on the precise temporal, spatial, and mechanical execution of those signals to ensure developmental completion23 (Fig. 2a). This approach differs from the “narrative engineering” or “organoid-by-design” concepts by shifting the focus from identifying sufficient starting conditions to reconstructing the actual causal pathways of human organogenesis24 (Fig. 2b).

Reverse bioengineering shares the goal of recreating developmental signals in vitro with permissive-niche organoid culture, but differs in three operational respects. First, reverse bioengineering initiates from a quantified human developmental trajectory, a temporally ordered set of cellular states, signaling environments, and mechanical conditions, rather than from an empirically optimized growth-factor cocktail25,26. Second, every engineered cue is treated as a controllable design variable whose value is benchmarked against a developmental reference (e.g., human fetal liver single-cell atlases20,27) rather than against a phenotypic endpoint such as albumin secretion28. Third, reverse bioengineering is closed-loop: Deviations from the reference trajectory feed back into platform design, including timing of growth-factor delivery, ECM stiffness ramps, and perfusion onset. Permissive niches define what signals are sufficient; reverse bioengineering defines when, where, and how they should be delivered (Fig. 2).

The goal is not to mimic adult liver outputs directly, but to reconstruct the causal pathways that generate them.

Liver development as an engineering blueprint

Human liver development provides a rich and underutilized blueprint for in vitro system design. Liver organogenesis begins with foregut endoderm specification, followed by liver bud formation through intimate interactions with endothelial and mesenchymal tissues20,29 (Fig. 3). Endothelial cells invade the nascent liver bud and provide instructive signals that drive hepatoblast expansion, polarization, and differentiation. As development proceeds, blood flow and cardiac motion introduce mechanical forces that shape tissue architecture and metabolic specialization, ultimately giving rise to spatial zonation and functional heterogeneity.

Several key principles emerge from this developmental sequence. First, function follows structure: metabolic competence and zonation arise only after vascular organization is established30. Second, timing matters28,31: Signals that promote early expansion inhibit later maturation if applied out of sequence. Third, mechanics are instructive, not permissive: Shear stress and tissue deformation actively regulate hepatic gene expression and specialization32,33.

Reverse bioengineering translates these principles into engineering constraints, requiring liver-on-a-chip platforms to support staged differentiation, evolving mechanical environments, and dynamic cell–cell interactions rather than static culture conditions.

Integrating intrinsic and extrinsic cues across model classes

Within a reverse-bioengineering framework, pluripotent stem cells, organoids, and microfluidic platforms are no longer competing technologies but interdependent components. Human pluripotent stem cells (hPSCs) provide access to early developmental states and genetic programmability20,30. Organoids enable self-organization and multicellular patterning, revealing which developmental processes are cell-intrinsic20,34–36. Liver-on-a-chip systems supply the extrinsic cues, perfusion, gradients, mechanics, and spatial constraint, that are absent in organoid cultures but essential for later maturation37,38.

Crucially, reverse bioengineering emphasizes temporal integration. Developmental cues must be applied sequentially and adaptively, rather than simultaneously. For example, endothelial co-culture may be required early to promote liver bud expansion, whereas stable perfusion and oxygen gradients become critical only after tissue architecture is established. This temporal logic is largely absent from current platforms, which typically impose adult-like conditions from the outset.

By encoding developmental timing into chip design, reverse bioengineering enables systematic testing of how specific cues drive transitions between developmental states, an approach that is particularly powerful when combined with human induced pluripotent stem cell (hiPSC)-based systems and gene-editing technologies.

Reverse bioengineering beyond the liver: inter-organ development as a design constraint

Reverse bioengineering naturally extends beyond the reconstruction of liver development in isolation. In vivo, liver organogenesis is inseparable from the coordinated development of surrounding organs and systems, including the foregut, vasculature, heart, pancreas, and immune compartment20,29. These inter-organ interactions are not merely supportive; they actively shape hepatic specification, maturation, and long-term functional specialization.

During embryogenesis, hepatic progenitors emerge from the foregut endoderm in close spatial and temporal coordination with gut tube patterning20,29. Endothelial cells derived from adjacent mesoderm invade the liver bud before the establishment of perfused vasculature, providing instructive signals that regulate hepatoblast expansion and differentiation. Concurrently, cardiac development and the onset of circulation introduce mechanical forces and metabolic fluxes that influence hepatic morphogenesis. These early inter-organ couplings establish the architectural and regulatory foundations upon which adult liver function is built.

From a reverse-bioengineering perspective, these observations impose a critical design constraint: liver development cannot be faithfully reconstructed without accounting for its developmental coupling to other organs. Engineering liver-on-a-chip systems without this context risks recreating isolated hepatic phenotypes that lack the systemic regulatory logic inherent to liver biology.

Importantly, inter-organ interactions are governed not only by molecular signaling, but also by temporal alignment. Developmental timelines differ across organs, and premature or asynchronous coupling can distort maturation trajectories. Reverse bioengineering, therefore, emphasizes staged and directional integration, wherein inter-organ communication is introduced only when developmentally appropriate. This principle stands in contrast to many existing multi-organ-on-chip systems, which connect mature tissues without regard for developmental sequencing.

In this framework, multi-organ liver-on-a-chip platforms are not an optional extension, but a logical consequence of development-first design18,39–41. Gut–liver41–43, pancreas–liver18,44, immune–liver45,46, and vascular–liver47,48 couplings can be introduced incrementally, guided by developmental milestones rather than adult physiological endpoints. Such systems enable interrogation of how systemic cues shape hepatic maturation, metabolic programming, and disease susceptibility in a human-specific context.

Reverse bioengineering as a roadmap, not a single platform

Importantly, reverse bioengineering should not be interpreted as a single technology or device. Rather, it is a conceptual roadmap that guides the co-development of cell sources, differentiation strategies, and engineering platforms. Success is measured not by maximal expression of adult markers, but by faithful progression through developmental stages and emergence of stable, context-appropriate liver function.

Rather than representing discrete categories, these models form a continuum wherein increasing structural and microphysiological control does not necessarily correspond to improved reconstruction of developmental processes. As detailed below, each class of system captures specific aspects of liver biology while omitting others.

Cell sources: strengths, limitations, and developmental potential

From a reverse-bioengineering perspective, cell source selection fundamentally determines which aspects of liver development can be accessed and reconstructed in vitro. While early liver models emphasized robustness, scalability, and endpoint function, advancing toward mechanistic and predictive systems requires developmentally competent cell sources. In this context, hPSCs, including human embryonic and induced pluripotent stem cells (hESCs49 and hiPSCs50, respectively), offer unique advantages.

Immortalized and primary hepatic cells: functional utility without developmental plasticity

Immortalized hepatic cell lines (e.g., HepG2, HepaRG) and primary human hepatocytes (PHHs) have long served as workhorses in liver research and drug development51. HepG2 cells provide reproducibility and ease of use, HepaRG cells exhibit enhanced metabolic competence, and PHHs remain the closest in vitro approximation of adult liver function.

However, all three cell sources lack developmental plasticity. Immortalized lines are constrained by transformed or progenitor-like regulatory states, while PHHs are terminally differentiated and rapidly lose polarity, zonation, and transcriptional stability ex vivo52. Crucially, none can traverse developmental trajectories or respond dynamically to cues governing liver organogenesis, maturation, or spatial specialization.

Pluripotent stem cell-derived hepatic cells: developmental access, cellular diversity, and incomplete execution

In contrast, hPSCs provide experimental access to both early hepatic specification and the emergence of cellular diversity characteristic of the developing liver53–56. During human liver development20,29,57, hepatoblasts generate heterogeneous parenchymal and non-parenchymal populations—including hepatocytes, cholangiocytes, endothelial cells, stellate cells, and immune cells—that co-emerge and interact within a shared developmental context. This diversity underpins key liver features such as zonation, vascular specialization, bile transport, and immune surveillance.

Developmentally guided hPSC differentiation protocols routinely recapitulate this early diversity. From a reverse-bioengineering lens, heterogeneous outcomes should therefore be interpreted not as off-target differentiation, but as partial reconstruction of intrinsic liver developmental programs, an interpretation that conceptually aligns hPSC-derived cultures with liver organoids (Section “Liver development as an engineering blueprint”).

However, as with organoids, developmental execution remains incomplete20,30,58. Although lineage specification occurs, coordinated maturation, spatial organization, and long-term stability are rarely achieved. Hepatocyte-like cells stall at fetal stages, endothelial-like cells lack sinusoidal specialization, and mesenchymal populations remain unstable59,60. In short, cellular diversity is generated but not developmentally organized.

This limitation reflects the absence of extrinsic developmental cues—such as perfusion, biomechanical forces, spatial confinement, and temporally staged signaling—that canalize intrinsic programs in vivo. Consequently, hPSC-derived hepatic systems initiate development but fail to complete it, mirroring developmental arrest in static organoids and phenotype stabilization in many chip-based systems.

Importantly, this limitation is diagnostic rather than detrimental. By revealing where intrinsic programs stall, hPSC-derived systems identify the specific extrinsic cues that must be engineered to drive maturation, stability, and integration. In this sense, hPSCs function not as immature hepatocyte substitutes, but as developmentally competent substrates that expose the requirements for reverse bioengineering.

hiPSCs: bridging developmental biology and precision medicine

Beyond developmental relevance, hiPSCs enable genetic personalization53–56. Patient-derived hiPSCs preserve disease-associated genetic backgrounds, facilitating studies of inter-individual variability in pharmacokinetics61,62, idiosyncratic DILI8,9, and inherited metabolic disorders63–65.

Combined with gene-editing technologies, hiPSCs allow generation of isogenic cell pairs differing at defined loci66,67, enabling causal dissection of how genetic variants influence developmental trajectories and hepatic function. When integrated into liver-on-a-chip platforms, hiPSC-based systems thus support a convergence of developmental biology and precision medicine, enabling longitudinal, genotype-resolved interrogation under controlled microphysiological conditions.

Implications of cell source choice for development-informed liver-on-a-chip design

Collectively, these considerations position hiPSCs as the central cell source for developmentally informed liver-on-a-chip systems. While adult and immortalized cells remain valuable for benchmarking and near-term applications, only hiPSCs enable reconstruction of the developmental processes underlying liver structure, function, and adaptability. Their integration with microfluidic platforms creates opportunities to encode temporal sequencing, mechanics, and multicellular organization—key requirements for predictive liver models.

Liver organoids: partial reconstructions of developmental programs

Liver organoids represent partial reconstructions of intrinsic liver developmental programs. By leveraging pluripotent stem cell-derived self-organization, organoids recapitulate early organogenesis, including lineage diversification, epithelial organization, and rudimentary polarity. They occupy a conceptual middle ground between reductionist cultures and engineered MPSs. In the conceptual landscape shown in Fig. 4, liver organoids occupy an intermediate position (Tier 3), where intrinsic developmental programs are activated but remain insufficiently coordinated by extrinsic cues.

A key strength of organoids lies in their reliance on intrinsic developmental logic. In permissive conditions, heterogeneous hepatic and non-parenchymal lineages co-emerge without imposed spatial constraints68,69, revealing cell-autonomous aspects of liver development such as hepatoblast expansion70 and early fate bifurcation71–74.

However, organoids also expose the limits of intrinsic programs. Until recently, developmental execution consistently stalls at fetal or early postnatal stages, with weak metabolic maturation, unstable CYP activity, and absent zonation. Recent advances are narrowing both limitations: multi-zonal organoids self-assemble periportal- and pericentral-like compartments with zone-specific xenobiotic responses40, adult-hepatocyte organoids preserve mitochondrial and CYP activity at levels approaching primary cells22,75, and long-term expansion strategies now extend functional persistence beyond what was achievable a decade ago21. These advances reinforce, rather than contradict, the case for reverse bioengineering. Each advantage was achieved by re-encoding a specific developmental cue (Wnt gradient, mitogen tuning, and mechanical confinement). These failures reflect missing extrinsic cues, vascular perfusion, hemodynamics, gradients, and mechanical forces, that are inseparable from liver maturation in vivo.

Notably, rapid maturation following transplantation into vascularized environments underscores that the missing elements are extrinsic rather than intrinsic. From a reverse-bioengineering perspective, organoid immaturity is therefore informative, identifying developmental transitions requiring engineered intervention.

3D bioprinting: structural fidelity without developmental execution

Three-dimensional (3D) bioprinting enables precise control over tissue geometry, cellular placement, and architecture76–80, allowing recreation of lobule-inspired structures, vascular-like networks, and compartmentalized domains47,79–82. This structural fidelity exceeds that achievable by self-organizing systems alone.

However, most bioprinting approaches impose final architecture from the outset, assembling pre-differentiated or partially differentiated cells and evaluating performance based on adult-like function. This strategy bypasses the developmental trajectories through which liver structure normally emerges, leading to limited maturation, adaptability, and stability despite spatial patterning.

These limitations do not reflect deficiencies in printing resolution, but rather the absence of temporally staged developmental cues. Within a reverse-bioengineering framework, bioprinting is therefore best understood as a tool for imposing boundary conditions, geometry and spatial constraints, that can guide development when combined with developmentally competent cells and dynamic microphysiological environments.

Accordingly, organoids and bioprinting are complementary: organoids initiate intrinsic programs, while bioprinting constrains and standardizes spatial organization. Their integration within liver-on-a-chip systems enables structural canalization of development rather than static anatomical mimicry.

Liver-on-a-chip platforms: extrinsic control without developmental trajectories

Liver-on-a-chip platforms excel at imposing extrinsic cues, including perfusion, shear stress, controlled geometry, and compartmentalized co-culture15,16,82–84. These features enhance nutrient transport, polarization, and short-term functional stability, particularly for adult hepatocytes51,84–90. As illustrated in Fig. 4, conventional liver-on-a-chip platforms align with Tier 4, where extrinsic control through perfusion and microenvironmental regulation is enhanced, yet developmental trajectories remain only partially reconstructed.

However, most designs assume that adult liver function is a stable state that can be maintained by supplying appropriate conditions from the outset. Consequently, chips often bypass developmental trajectories, focusing on endpoint phenotypes rather than staged maturation.

This limitation becomes evident when PSC-derived cells or organoids are incorporated. Although perfusion and shear can transiently enhance function, they rarely drive coordinated maturation across lineages because extrinsic cues are applied without temporal alignment to intrinsic programs. Developmental cues are typically added modularly rather than as components of a staged program.

Consequently, liver-on-a-chip platforms provide extrinsic control without developmental execution, complementing but not replacing organoids. Their full potential emerges only when used to organize intrinsic cellular diversity into coherent developmental trajectories.

From a translational perspective, liver-on-a-chip platforms ultimately serve as the convergence point for reverse bioengineering, integrating intrinsic programs, cellular diversity, and extrinsic physical cues within a scalable and controllable framework. They are enabling infrastructures that operationalize developmental logic for disease modeling, drug discovery, and regulatory testing.

Synthesis: integrating intrinsic programs, cellular diversity, structure, and extrinsic cues

As summarized in Fig. 4, current in vitro liver models occupy distinct but overlapping regions within a landscape defined by developmental fidelity and engineering control. Sections “Cell sources: strengths, limitations, and developmental potential”–“Liver-on-a-chip platforms: extrinsic control without developmental trajectories” collectively demonstrate that these systems capture complementary but incomplete aspects of liver development: hPSCs enable developmental initiation and cellular diversity; organoids partially reconstruct intrinsic trajectories (Tier 3); bioprinting imposes reproducible structure (Tier 2); and liver-on-a-chip platforms deliver extrinsic control (Tier 4).

Reverse bioengineering clarifies that liver development cannot be reconstructed through intrinsic programs, imposed structure, or extrinsic cues in isolation. Developmental fidelity emerges only through their deliberate integration along defined temporal trajectories. Aligning developmentally competent cells, structural constraints, and microphysiological control establishes the foundation for next-generation liver-on-a-chip systems that are mechanistically grounded, functionally robust, and translationally predictive.

Therefore, progression toward high tiers in this landscape, particularly organoid-on-a-chip and development-programmed systems (Tiers 5–6), requires not incremental optimization of individual components, but their coordinated integration through temporally structured developmental programs.

Applications revisited through the lens of developmental fidelity

Liver-on-a-chip platforms are increasingly deployed across a wide range of applications, including fundamental biology, disease modeling, drug discovery, and toxicity testing (Fig. 5). Traditionally, the performance of these systems has been evaluated based on their ability to reproduce selected adult hepatic phenotypes. From a reverse-bioengineering perspective, however, the utility and predictivity of liver-on-a-chip platforms depend more fundamentally on developmental fidelity—that is, on whether intrinsic developmental programs and cellular diversity are properly organized and executed through appropriate extrinsic cues along defined developmental trajectories. Here, major application areas are re-examined through this lens.

Fundamental understanding of human liver development and function

Developmentally informed liver-on-a-chip platforms offer a powerful experimental window into human liver development, which remains difficult to interrogate in vivo37,47. By combining hPSC-derived intrinsic developmental programs with engineered extrinsic developmental cues, these systems enable controlled reconstruction of developmental trajectories that underlie liver morphogenesis and functional maturation87,91,92. Concrete examples of such cues include staged presentation of developmental morphogens28,31, temporally controlled oxygen or perfusion gradients87, progressive programming of matrix mechanics or viscoelasticity93–95, and timed endothelial/angiocrine induction during hepatoblast expansion and early tissue assembly71,72. Such platforms make it possible to dissect causal relationships between intrinsic lineage programs and extrinsic regulators, including endothelial signaling, perfusion-induced mechanical forces, and spatial gradients of oxygen and nutrients. In this context, liver-on-a-chip systems function not merely as culture devices, but as developmental testbeds for human liver biology, with direct implications for precision medicine and predictive modeling96,97.

Reverse bioengineering of liver diseases

Whereas conventional liver disease models typically superimpose pathological insults onto developmentally static tissues, reverse bioengineering treats disease as a deviation from normal developmental execution18,,98,99. From a reverse-bioengineering perspective, many liver diseases are best understood not as isolated defects of adult hepatic function but as failures in the execution, maintenance, or adaptation of developmental programs. Metabolic liver diseases, fibrotic remodeling, cholangiopathies, and vascular disorders frequently reflect disruptions in cellular differentiation, spatial organization, or intercellular communication that originate during development or re-emerge through maladaptive remodeling. Conventional in vitro disease models typically impose pathological stimuli onto developmentally static tissues, limiting their ability to capture disease initiation and progression.

Reverse-bioengineered liver-on-a-chip systems offer a fundamentally different approach by reconstructing liver diseases along defined developmental trajectories. By guiding hPSC–derived hepatic tissues through staged differentiation under controlled extrinsic cues, disease-associated perturbations—genetic, metabolic, inflammatory, or mechanical—can be introduced at specific developmental windows. This enables identification of critical transition points at which intrinsic developmental programs diverge toward pathology, revealing causal mechanisms that are obscured in endpoint-focused models.

Importantly, this framework allows disease phenotypes to emerge through interactions among multiple hepatic and non-parenchymal lineages, rather than being imposed as predefined outcomes. In this context, liver-on-a-chip platforms function not only as disease models but as experimental systems for dissecting how developmental fidelity, cellular diversity, and inter-organ signaling jointly determine susceptibility, progression, and reversibility of liver disease.

Together, these developmentally grounded approaches to both normal physiology and disease establish a mechanistic foundation for translational applications. In particular, they enable liver-on-a-chip systems to move beyond descriptive modeling toward predictive frameworks that integrate genetic variation, developmental state, and functional outcome.

Precision medicine and predictive modeling: a developmental framework

Reverse bioengineering also reshapes the role of liver-on-a-chip platforms in precision medicine. Patient-specific hiPSCs53–56,100,101, combined with gene editing53,56, allow disease-associated variants to be introduced at defined developmental stages and tracked through liver formation and maturation. This capability enables investigation of how genetic perturbations influence not only adult hepatic function but also the developmental processes that establish it.

From a pharmacological perspective, this framework provides a path toward predictive modeling89,102–106. Drug metabolism, stress responses, and functional maturation are tightly coupled with developmental state, zonation, and tissue organization, features that are poorly captured in conventional systems. By reconstructing these aspects, reverse-engineered liver-on-a-chip platforms enable accurate identification of human-specific drug responses and idiosyncratic effects.

Broadly, these systems integrate genetic background, developmental dynamics, and tissue architecture into a unified experimental context, positioning them as predictive platforms that link genotype to functional outcome in a mechanistically coherent manner. Building on this foundation, reverse bioengineering reshapes the role of liver-on-a-chip platforms in precision medicine.

Drug discovery: predictivity through developmental reconstruction

Within this predictive framework, drug responses are strongly influenced by developmental state, cellular diversity, and spatial organization within the liver107–110. Enzyme expression, transporter activity, and stress-response pathways evolve along developmental trajectories and vary across hepatic zones. Liver-on-a-chip platforms that emphasize phenotype stabilization without developmental reconstruction therefore risk misrepresenting drug efficacy and liability.

By contrast, reverse-bioengineered liver-on-a-chip systems support drug testing within a developmentally grounded framework. Intrinsic developmental programs generate relevant cellular diversity, while extrinsic cues guide maturation and spatial specialization. This approach enables evaluation of pharmacological responses in tissues that more closely approximate adult human liver function, improving predictivity for metabolism, efficacy, and off-target effects. Integration of patient-specific and genetically edited hPSC lines further enables genotype-resolved drug discovery within a developmentally faithful context.

Toxicity testing: developmental fidelity as a requirement for safety assessment

Extending these considerations to safety assessment, DILI8,9,111 often depends on zonation-specific metabolism, cumulative stress responses, and long-term adaptation—features that emerge only through proper developmental execution. Static or developmentally incomplete models frequently fail to capture these processes, contributing to poor translational performance in toxicity screening.

Liver-on-a-chip systems designed through a reverse-bioengineering lens enable toxicity testing under conditions that support sustained function, spatial heterogeneity, and dynamic response8,36,112,113. By organizing intrinsic cellular diversity through appropriate extrinsic cues, these platforms allow detection of delayed, dose-dependent, and region-specific toxicities that are invisible in short-term assays. In this context, developmental fidelity is not an abstract goal, but a practical requirement for reliable safety assessment.

Scalability and standardization through developmental benchmarks

A common concern is that developmentally complex liver-on-a-chip platforms may compromise scalability and standardization113,114. Reverse bioengineering reframes this challenge by shifting standardization from fixed phenotypic endpoints to developmental benchmarks. Instead of enforcing uniform marker expression at arbitrary time points, platforms can be evaluated based on progression through defined developmental states and successful execution of key transitions, such as vascularization, zonation, and functional stabilization.

This strategy supports scalable manufacturing while preserving biological relevance, enabling developmentally faithful liver-on-a-chip systems to meet the reproducibility demands of industrial and regulatory applications.

Perspective

Collectively, these applications underscore a unifying principle: The predictive power of liver-on-a-chip platforms scales with their ability to reconstruct developmental trajectories that organize intrinsic cellular diversity through appropriate extrinsic cues. However, translating this principle into robust and widely deployable systems is not straightforward. In the following section, the key design constraints and translational challenges that must be addressed for reverse-bioengineered liver-on-a-chip platforms to move from conceptual promise to practical impact are examined.

Design constraints and translational challenges for reverse-bioengineered liver-on-a-chip systems

The adoption of reverse bioengineering as a guiding framework for liver-on-a-chip systems opens avenues for biological insight and translational impact, but also introduces conceptual, technical, and practical challenges. Addressing these challenges will be essential for advancing developmentally faithful liver models from proof-of-concept platforms to standardized, widely deployable technologies.

Encoding temporal developmental logic into engineered platforms

A central challenge in reverse bioengineering is the explicit incorporation of developmental timing into liver-on-a-chip design. Most existing platforms impose static or quasi-static culture conditions, whereas liver development in vivo unfolds through tightly regulated, sequential stages. Future systems will therefore enable time-resolved control of biochemical signals, mechanical forces, and cellular interactions, allowing developmental cues to be introduced, withdrawn, or reweighted in synchrony with intrinsic lineage progression20,28,115.

Achieving this will require advances in programmable microfluidics68, dynamic biomaterials116, and automated culture control117. Importantly, temporal complexity must be balanced against usability; platforms that are biologically faithful but operationally intractable risk limited adoption. Defining minimal yet sufficient developmental programs will therefore be as important as increasing biological complexity. As the resulting design space rapidly exceeds what can be optimized empirically, an atlas-guided, iterative benchmark-and-prune strategy118 may offer a practical path forward. In analogous cell-culture systems, automated on-chip optimization119, active learning120, and Bayesian optimization121 have already reduced the experimental burden required to search large parameter spaces, suggesting that similar closed-loop strategies could help operationalize developmental fidelity as a measurable design objective in liver-on-a-chip engineering.

Quantitative metrics of developmental fidelity

As liver-on-a-chip platforms become more developmentally complex, defining objective metrics of success becomes increasingly important. Traditional readouts—such as marker expression or single-time-point functional assays—are insufficient to capture developmental progression. Future evaluation frameworks must incorporate dynamic, trajectory-based metrics, including temporal transcriptomic and proteomic profiles, spatial organization, and functional maturation over time.

Single-cell and spatial omics technologies, combined with live imaging and computational modeling, will play a central role in establishing developmental benchmarks. However, translating these high-dimensional datasets into practical quality-control criteria suitable for industrial or regulatory contexts remains a significant challenge.

Scalability, standardization, and manufacturing constraints

Reverse-engineered liver-on-a-chip systems must ultimately reconcile biological fidelity with scalability and reproducibility. hiPSC-based platforms introduce additional sources of variability, including donor-to-donor differences and batch effects. While genetic editing and isogenic controls can mitigate some variability, standardized protocols and reference materials will be required to ensure consistency across laboratories and applications.

Manufacturing considerations—including device fabrication, cell production, and long-term culture stability—will shape the translational trajectory of these systems. Developmentally informed platforms must therefore be designed with manufacturability in mind, ensuring that complexity serves a clear biological purpose rather than becoming an end in itself.

Ethical and regulatory considerations

Developmentally faithful liver models raise important ethical and regulatory questions114,122. As in vitro systems increasingly recapitulate early human developmental processes, clear governance frameworks will be needed to define acceptable experimental boundaries. While liver-on-a-chip platforms do not pose the same ethical challenges as embryo research, their increasing biological sophistication warrants proactive engagement with ethical guidelines.

From a regulatory perspective, developmentally informed liver-on-a-chip systems challenge existing validation paradigms, which are largely designed around static assays and endpoint measurements. Regulatory acceptance will depend on demonstrating not only functional relevance, but also reproducibility, transparency, and clear links between developmental fidelity and predictive performance.

Conceptual challenge: avoiding overengineering

Finally, a key conceptual challenge is avoiding the trap of overengineering. Reverse bioengineering does not imply maximal complexity, but rather purposeful reconstruction of causally relevant processes. Identifying which developmental cues are essential, and which can be abstracted or omitted, will require iterative cycles of biological hypothesis testing and engineering refinement.

Success will depend on close collaboration between developmental biologists, stem cell researchers, and bioengineers, as well as on a willingness to abandon phenomenological benchmarks in favor of mechanistic understanding.

Addressing these constraints clarifies both the limits and the opportunities of reverse bioengineering. While challenges related to temporal control, quantitative benchmarking, scalability, and regulatory alignment are substantial, they also define the design space within which developmentally faithful liver-on-a-chip systems can be realized. With this foundation in place, the broader outlook for the field is considered next, focusing on how developmentally coordinated multi-organ platforms, Digital Twins, artificial intelligence, and multi-omics integration may collectively redefine the future of liver-on-a-chip technology (Fig. 6).

Outlook: toward developmentally coordinated, intelligent liver-on-a-chip systems

The shift from phenotype-driven liver models to developmentally grounded systems represents more than a technical evolution, it reflects a conceptual redefinition of how liver biology should be reconstructed and interrogated in vitro. Reverse bioengineering reframes liver-on-a-chip platforms as dynamic systems that must recapitulate adult hepatic functions and the developmental processes by which those functions are assembled, coordinated, and stabilized over time. Looking ahead, the convergence of developmental biology, microphysiological engineering, and computational intelligence is poised to define the next phase of the field.

Liver-on-a-chip as an experimental surrogate for human liver development

Developmentally programmed liver-on-a-chip systems are uniquely positioned to serve as experimental surrogates for otherwise inaccessible stages of human liver development. Ethical and technical constraints severely limit direct investigation of early and mid-gestational human organogenesis. By reconstructing intrinsic developmental programs within controllable microengineered environments, liver-on-a-chip platforms enable systematic perturbation of signaling pathways, mechanical forces, and cellular composition across defined developmental windows. In doing so, they transform liver-on-a-chip systems from static maintenance devices into experimental engines for human developmental biology.

Developmentally coordinated multi-organ and body-on-a-chip systems

Reverse bioengineering naturally extends beyond the liver in isolation. Hepatic maturation is inseparable from gut-derived metabolites, pancreatic endocrine signaling, vascular development, and immune colonization. Future body-on-a-chip platforms will therefore move beyond static coupling of mature organ modules toward co-developmental architectures, wherein multiple tissues are differentiated, matured, and integrated along compatible developmental timelines. Such systems preserve directionality and hierarchy in inter-organ communication, enabling mechanistic interrogation of systemic disease emergence, pharmacokinetics, and whole-body drug responses.

Digital twins and AI-guided reconstruction of liver development

As developmentally grounded liver-on-a-chip platforms increase in biological and temporal complexity, parallel advances in computational frameworks become essential. Developmental Digital Twins—computational representations that evolve alongside physical liver-on-a-chip systems—offer a powerful approach to model, predict, and interrogate developmental trajectories in silico114,123–125. By continuously assimilating experimental data spanning molecular profiles, cellular states, tissue architecture, and functional outputs, Digital Twins enable hypothesis testing, parameter optimization, and failure-mode analysis without exhaustive empirical iteration.

The rapid maturation of multi-omics technologies is a critical enabler of this paradigm. Single-cell and spatial transcriptomics, epigenomics, proteomics, and metabolomics have revealed that liver development proceeds through continuous, state-dependent trajectories rather than discrete lineage endpoints8,20,58,126,127. When applied longitudinally to liver-on-a-chip systems, these data anchor Digital Twins to trajectory-level biological ground truth, constraining AI models to operate within biologically meaningful manifolds. This enables mechanistically interpretable, development-aware predictions rather than purely correlative inference.

Developmentally informed precision medicine and translation

The integration of hiPSC technology, gene editing, and AI-enabled Digital Twins opens avenues for developmentally informed precision medicine. Patient-specific genetic variants can be introduced into developmentally competent systems and tracked across hepatic differentiation, maturation, and inter-organ interaction. Digital Twins can then be used to simulate alternative developmental paths, predict disease susceptibility, and explore intervention strategies at stages that precede overt pathology. This approach shifts precision medicine upstream, from treating established disease phenotypes to identifying and correcting developmental vulnerabilities.

At the translational level, the success of intelligent liver-on-a-chip platforms will depend on their scalability, interpretability, and regulatory alignment. While AI can accelerate optimization, its outputs must remain experimentally verifiable and biologically constrained. Reverse bioengineering provides this constraint by evaluating models not solely on predictive accuracy, but on their ability to respect known developmental logic and generate testable hypotheses. Over time, this trajectory-based framework may also reshape regulatory validation, aligning naturally with emerging New Approach Methodologies.

From platforms to paradigms

In summary, the future of liver-on-a-chip technology lies in the integration of developmental biology, microengineering, and computational intelligence. By treating liver development as an engineering blueprint, inter-organ coordination as a design constraint, and Digital Twins as adaptive companions to physical systems, reverse bioengineering offers a path toward in vitro liver models that are not only more realistic, but also more predictive, controllable, and transformative.

Conclusion

Liver-on-a-chip technologies have matured rapidly, yet their predictive power remains constrained by an emphasis on reproducing adult hepatic phenotypes rather than the developmental processes that generate them. In this perspective, I propose developmental fidelity as the unifying design principle linking pluripotent stem cell biology, organoid research, and MPSs. Liver-on-a-chip technologies have matured genetic programmability, organoids reveal the potential and limits of self-organization, and microfluidic platforms supply the extrinsic cues required for maturation and spatial specialization. Guided by developmental principles, their integration can transform static assay platforms into dynamic, mechanistically grounded models of liver, disease, and therapeutic response.

Because liver development is coordinated with the vasculature, gut, pancreas, and immune system, developmentally faithful models will ultimately require staged inter-organ integration. By treating liver development as an engineering blueprint reverse bioengineering provides a roadmap toward more predictive, controllable, and scalable liver-on-a-chip systems.
