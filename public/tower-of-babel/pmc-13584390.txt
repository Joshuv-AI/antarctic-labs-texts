The relationship between partner attachment, psychological well-being, and menopausal symptoms in postmenopausal women
Authors: Şener Çetin Nurdilan, Simge Öztürk, Çetin Yasin
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ObjectiveThis research aimed to determine the relationship between menopausal symptoms experienced by postmenopausal women and their levels of spousal attachment and psychological well-being.MethodsThis descriptive research was conducted with 1334 postmenopausal women. Data were collected using the Personal Introduction Form, Experiences in Close Relationships Inventory-II (ECR-R), Psychological Well-Being Scale (PWS) and Menopause Rating Scale (MRS).Descriptive statistics and Pearson correlation analysis were used to analyze the data. Data were analyzed using descriptive statistics, Pearson correlation, and Cronbach alpha reliability coefficients.ResultsThe mean age of the participants was 58.38 ± 7.41 years, and the mean age at menopause was 48.9 ± 3.73 years. A low-level positive correlation was found between menopausal symptoms and both anxious (r = .286, p < .001) and avoidant spousal attachment (r = .182, p < .001). Conversely, a low-level negative correlation was identified between menopausal symptoms and psychological well-being (r=−.220, p < .001). These findings indicate that higher insecure partner attachment is associated with increased menopausal symptoms, whereas higher psychological well-being is associated with fewer symptoms.ConclusionThe results highlight the importance of psychosocial factors in the menopausal experience. Higher insecure partner attachment was associated with increased menopausal symptoms, whereas higher psychological well-being was associated with fewer symptoms. Interventions targeting emotional support, relationship quality, and psychological well-being may be relevant areas for future research. Future studies should include diverse populations and examine additional factors such as social support, marital satisfaction, and lifestyle characteristics to better understand their relationship with menopausal health. Introduction

Menopause is a complex physiological process related to the transition from the reproductive phase to aging for women. Defined by the permanent loss of menstruation, menopause occurs as a result of follicle depletion and lack of estrogen secretion from the ovaries [1]. Menopause occurs between the ages of 45 and 55 and appears on average at the age of 51 worldwide. In Turkey, the average age of menopause is between 46 and 49 years old [2]. Menopause is commonly described in three stages: premenopause, perimenopause, and postmenopause. The perimenopausal period, which represents the transition to menopause, is particularly characterized by hormonal fluctuations and the emergence of vasomotor and menstrual changes, while the postmenopausal period is marked by the persistence of symptoms and long-term health risks [3]. Postmenopausal problems are divided into three categories: short-term changes such as hot flashes, sweating, psychological complaints, insomnia, and joint pain; medium-term complications such as vaginal and urinary symptoms and sexual problems; and long-term symptoms such as osteoporosis and cardiovascular disease [4]. Although these changes that occur during menopause do not threaten women’s lives, they can negatively affect their quality of life and lead to an increase in morbidity and mortality rates. Due to these complaints experienced during menopause, improving women’s well-being and protecting and improving their quality of life is extremely important [5]. Menopausal symptoms are strongly associated with reduced marital happiness, particularly when it comes to mood and relationship dynamics. Research reveals that sexual dysfunction develops throughout the menopausal transition, which might severely affect marital satisfaction. Women with mild symptoms often reported very good relationships, while those with severe symptoms reported good or poor relationship [6].

Attachment refers to relatively stable patterns of expectations, emotions, and behaviors that individuals develop in close relationships based on early relational experiences and that continue to shape interpersonal functioning in adulthood [7, 8]. In adult intimate relationships, attachment style reflects the degree to which individuals perceive their partners as emotionally available, supportive, and responsive in times of need, thereby influencing how stress is regulated and how threats to physical and psychological well-being are managed. In later life, when social networks tend to narrow and the partner relationship becomes more central, attachment patterns may become even more salient in shaping emotional security, coping processes, and vulnerability to stressors such as health problems and relational conflicts [9]. Insecure attachment has been associated with dysregulated physiological stress responses, somatization, and increased susceptibility to physical symptoms, suggesting that attachment-related differences in affect regulation may influence how bodily complaints are experienced and interpreted [10–12]. Within this framework, menopausal symptoms—particularly those related to somatic discomfort, sexual functioning, and emotional distress—may be more intensely perceived and more difficult to manage among women with insecure attachment patterns [13].

Psychological well-being, defined as a positive evaluation of one’s functioning in life, including autonomy, purpose, personal growth, and the quality of interpersonal relationships, represents a key resource for coping with developmental and health-related challenges [14]. Evidence indicates that women who experience more severe menopausal symptoms tend to report lower levels of psychological well-being, highlighting the close interconnection between emotional resources and symptom burden during the postmenopausal period [15]. Marital relationships often deteriorate during menopause due to changes in intimacy, communication, and sexual health [6]. Therefore, examining partner attachment patterns together with psychological well-being may provide a more comprehensive understanding of individual differences in the severity and experience of menopausal symptoms.

Previous research has predominantly examined menopausal symptoms in relation to either psychological factors or general relationship outcomes such as marital satisfaction. However, the specific role of partner attachment patterns, together with psychological well-being, in shaping menopausal symptom experience has not been sufficiently investigated, particularly among postmenopausal women.

By simultaneously examining partner attachment and psychological well-being, this research provides a more integrative perspective on the psychosocial determinants of menopausal symptoms. Accordingly, this research aims to examine the independent and combined effects of spousal attachment and psychological well-being on menopausal symptoms in postmenopausal women.

Methods

Type of research

The research is a Cross-sectional correlational research. Therefore, the findings reflect associations between variables and do not allow for causal inferences.

Universe and sample

The universe of the research consisted of postmenopausal women living in Elazıg the east of Turkey. The research sample was recruited using an online snowball sampling method. The snowball sampling method was chosen because it facilitates access to hard-to-reach groups. Initially, the researchers shared the survey link with a group of participants who met the inclusion criteria (women who could understand Turkish, have a partner, were not diagnosed with any mental illness, had not menstruated in the last year), and these participants were then asked to forward the survey to other eligible individuals within their social networks. This approach facilitated access to a wider population in a cost-effective and time-efficient manner. The sample size to be used in the research has been calculated using the G*Power version 3.1.9.7 power analysis program. A t-test power analysis was performed for two-way Pearson correlation with G*Power; alpha = 0.05, power = 0.95, expected correlation r = .20, and the required sample size was calculated as at least 384 [16, 17]. Because the online snowball sampling method was used in the research, the data collection process was not limited to a minimum sample size and data collection continued. Oversampling was intentionally allowed to increase statistical power, reduce sampling error, and mitigate potential biases inherent in non-probability sampling methods 1368 people who agreed to participate in the research completed the research. However, 34 participants reported having menstruated within the past 12 months and were excluded from the research. The research included 1334 women. The inclusion criteria for the research were; women who could understand Turkish, not having her period for 12 months, have a partner (Only women with a current partner were included, as the research aimed to examine spousal attachment and perceived partner-related factors in relation to menopausal symptoms.), were not diagnosed with any mental illness, had not menstruated in the last year, and agreed to participate in the research. Women who did not meet these criteria were excluded from the research.

Data collection form

The data of the research were collected using the Personal Introduction Form, Experiences in Close Relationships Inventory-II (ECR-R), Psychological Well-Being Scale (PWS) and Menopause Rating Scale (MRS). All data were obtained directly from participants through these instruments, and no governmental databases or official statistics were used.

Personal introduction form

This form was developed by the researchers to collect participants’ sociodemographic and clinical characteristics, including age, menopause duration (years since menopause), age at menopause (years), body mass index, level of education, employment status, income level, place of residence, and marital status. The English version of the Personal Introduction Form developed for this research is provided as Supplementary File 1.

Experiences in Close Relationships Inventory-II (ECR-R)

The validity and reliability research of the scale developed by Fraley and Shaver (2000) [18] in Turkey was conducted by Selçuk et al. (2005). The scale has a total of 36 items in the seven-point Likert type, 18 of which are in the anxiety and 18 in the avoidance sub-factor. The score obtained from each sub-dimension varies between 18 and 126, and it is said that as the score obtained from the scale increases, avoidant attachment or attachment anxiety increases. The avoidant attachment dimension is calculated by adding the even numbered items, and the anxious attachment dimension is calculated by adding the odd numbered items. On the scale 4, 8, 16, 17, 18, 20, 21, 22, Items 24, 26, 30, 32, 34, and 36 are scored reversely. While the Cronbach alpha coefficient of the avoidant attachment sub-dimension is 0.90, the Cronbach alpha coefficient of the anxious attachment sub-dimension is 0.86 [19]. In the research, the Cronbach alpha of the avoidant attachment sub-dimension was calculated as 0.92 and that of the anxious attachment sub-dimension was calculated as 0.87.

Psychological well-being scale (PWS)

The psychological well-being scale was developed by Diener et al. (2010) and consists of eight items and a single dimension [20]. The Turkish validity and reliability studies of the scale were conducted by Telef (2012). The scale defines important concepts such as positive relationships, feelings of competence, and having a meaningful and purposeful lifeIt is a single-factor scale consisting of 8 items with a 7-point Likert type. Scores range from 8 to 56. A high score obtained from the scale indicates that the individual’s psychological well-being level is high. The Cronbach’s alpha coefficient was 0.80 [20, 21]. In this research, the Cronbach’s alpha coefficient was found to be 0.90.

Menopause Rating Scale (MRS)

Menopause Rating Scale (MRS), was developed by Schneider and colleagues to measure the severity of menopausal symptoms [22] and was adapted into Turkish by Gürkan in 2005. The Likert-type scale, which consists of 11 items covering menopausal complaints, has the options “0 = None”, “1 = Mild”, “2 = Moderate”, “3 = Severe” and “4 = Very severe” for each item. The total score of the scale is calculated based on the scores given for each item. The lowest possible score is 0, and the highest score is 44. An increase in the total score obtained from the scale indicates an increase in the severity of the complaints experienced. The Cronbach Alpha coefficient for the entire scale is 0.84. In this research, the Cronbach Alpha coefficient was determined as 0.84. The scale consists of three sub-dimensions: somatic, psychological and urogenital complaints. The Cronbach alpha reliability coefficient of the original scale is 0.84. The Cronbach alpha values of the subgroups were 0.65 for somatic complaints, 0.79 for psychological complaints and 0.72 for urogenital complaints [23]. In this research, the MRS Cronbach alpha was found to be 0.85 for the total scale, 0.69 for somatic complaints, 0.80 for psychological complaints and 0.69 for urogenital complaints.

Data collection

The online survey was distributed through convenience sampling by sharing the survey link via social media platforms such as WhatsApp and Facebook, both in women’s groups and through direct messaging to eligible participants. Participants were encouraged to share the survey link with other postmenopausal women. Data was collected between January 2025 and March 2025.

Analysis of data

Data were analyzed using the Statistical Package for the Social Sciences (SPSS) 25.0 analysis program. For data analysis skewness and kurtosis were used to assess normality. The values were found to be between 0.025 and − 0.554. Subsequently, percentage, mean, standard deviation, Pearson correlation (the variables were continuous and met the assumptions of normal distribution.), Hierarchical Regression Analysis and Cronbach’s alpha reliability coefficient were used for data analysis. The results were evaluated at a 95% confidence interval and a p < .05 significance level. Before conducting the hierarchical regression analysis, regression assumptions were evaluated. Multicollinearity was assessed using tolerance and variance inflation factor (VIF) values, independence of errors was examined using the Durbin–Watson statistic, and normality, linearity, and homoscedasticity were assessed through visual inspection of standardized residual plots and normal probability (P–P) plots. Influential observations were evaluated using Cook’s distance. All assumptions were considered to be satisfactorily met before performing the regression analysis.

Ethical aspects of the research

For the research, ethics committee permission was obtained from the X University Non-Interventional Ethics Committee (date: 01.11.2024; number: 28590) and an information note was written on the survey form for the researchers, asking the participants to click the checkbox if they agreed to participate in the research. In this way, the women’s written informed consent was obtained. An informed consent form was prepared in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki and their approvals were obtained. Care was taken to comply with the “Principle of Autonomy” by stating that the participants could withdraw from the research at any time, and the “Principle of Protection of Confidentiality and Privacy” by stating that individual information would be protected after being shared with the researcher. The “Principle of Anonymity and Security” was fulfilled by stating that the information obtained and the identities of the respondents would be kept confidential. No artificial intelligence was used other than language editing.

Prior to interpreting the hierarchical regression analysis, the underlying assumptions of ordinary least squares (OLS) regression were rigorously evaluated. To assess multicollinearity, Tolerance and Variance Inflation Factors (VIF) were examined for all predictors. The VIF values ranged between 1.07 and 1.42, and all Tolerance values were well above the conservative threshold of 0.10 (ranging from 0.69 to 0.95), indicating the absence of multicollinearity issues. The Durbin-Watson statistic was 1.95, confirming that the assumption of independent errors was met. Furthermore, visual inspections of the normal P-P plots and scatterplots of standardized residuals against standardized predicted values verified the assumptions of normality and homoscedasticity. Lastly, Cook’s distance values for all cases were below 1.0, indicating that the results were not skewed by influential outliers. A hierarchical regression analysis was subsequently conducted to determine the predictors of menopausal symptoms (Table 4). In the first step (Model 1), sociodemographic and clinical characteristics (age, BMI, economic status, duration of menopause, education level, and employment status) were entered as control variables. These variables collectively accounted for 4.4% of the variance in menopausal symptoms (R 2 = 0.044, F(6,1327) = 10.230, p<.001). Within this model, BMI (β = 0.10, t = 3.71, p<.001), economic status (β=−0.13, t = − 4.83, p<.001), and education level (β=−0.08, t = − 2.80, p<.01) emerged as significant predictors. In the second step (Model 2), the inclusion of attachment styles (avoidance and anxiety) and psychological well-being significantly improved the model, explaining an additional 9.7% of the variance (ΔR 2 = 0.097, ΔF(3,1324) = 49.70, p<.001), and bringing the total explained variance to 14.1% (R 2 = 0.141, F(9,1324) = 24.075, p<.001). After controlling for sociodemographic and clinical factors, attachment anxiety significantly and positively predicted menopausal symptoms (β = 0.25, t = 9.15, p<.001), whereas psychological well-being emerged as a significant negative predictor (β=−0.14, t = − 4.64, p<.001). Conversely, attachment avoidance did not significantly contribute to the regression model (β=−0.02, t = − 0.68, p=.496).

Discussion

This research examined the relationships between partner attachment styles, psychological well-being, and menopause symptoms in postmenopausal women. A weak but positive correlation was found between MRS subdimensions—somatic (r = .107, p < .001), psychological (r = .231, p < .001), and urogenital (r = .102, p < .001)—and avoidant and anxious attachment scores, as well as ECR-R total scores (r = .182, p < .001). In contrast, a weak but negative correlation was found between MRS subdimensions—somatic (r = − .125, p < .001), psychological (r = − .248, p < .001), and urogenital (r = − .170, p < .001)—and psychological well-being scores (r = − .220, p < .001).

These findings indicate statistically significant but weak associations between attachment patterns, psychological well-being, and menopausal symptoms. Although higher insecure attachment scores were associated with higher symptom levels and higher psychological well-being scores were associated with lower symptom levels, the observed effect sizes were small and indicate limited explanatory and clinical relevance. Therefore, these findings should be interpreted as associative rather than indicative of strong relationships. Furthermore, the hierarchical regression model explained only 14.1% of the variance in menopausal symptoms, suggesting that most variability in symptom severity remains unexplained and is likely attributable to biological, psychosocial, behavioral, and health-related factors not included in the present study.

The anxiety and avoidance dimensions of insecure attachment are associated with fear of abandonment, emotional distance, and reduced perceived support, particularly in close relationships [24, 25]. This research found weak but significant associations between levels of anxious and avoidant attachment and menopausal symptom severity in postmenopausal women. These findings suggest a possible association between insecure attachment patterns and menopausal symptoms rather than a definitive relationship and may reflect differences in perceived spousal support and relational dynamics (Table 2). Although these correlations were statistically significant, their magnitude was relatively small (r < .2–0.3), indicate limited practical interpretation. It should also be noted that potential confounding variables such as marital satisfaction, physical health status, comorbid conditions, medication use, and broader social support were not controlled for in this research, which may have influenced the observed associations. Marital relationships may become strained during menopause in Türkiye [26] which may also contribute to variability in symptom perception; however, this was not directly assessed. Consequently, the observed associations may partly reflect the influence of these unmeasured variables rather than independent associations between partner attachment, psychological well-being, and menopausal symptoms.

Previous studies have suggested that insecure attachment may be associated with stress-related responses and adverse psychological outcomes in postmenopausal women [8, 11, 27]. In addition, a relationship has been stated between insecure attachment and somatization and sexual desire [13]. Receiving little support from one’s partner has been associated with the anxiety and avoidance dimensions of insecure attachment [28]. Spousal support has been shown to alleviate menopausal symptoms [2, 29, 30].

Taken together, these findings suggest that insecure attachment may be indirectly related to menopausal symptoms, possibly in relation to lower perceived spousal support. However, these interpretations should be considered cautiously. It is also possible that the relationship is bidirectional, where symptom severity may influence psychological well-being and perceptions of partner attachment. Therefore, causal conclusions cannot be drawn due to the cross-sectional design.

Psychological well-being is a positive cognitive and emotional state that integrates self-acceptance, positive relationships, autonomy, environmental mastery, life purpose, and personal growth, and is considered a fundamental attitude for maintaining a healthy life [29, 31]. In this research, higher levels of psychological well-being were associated with fewer menopausal symptoms. Although statistically significant, this relationship was weak, suggesting a limited effect size. This indicates that psychological well-being is associated with symptom perception but is unlikely to be a primary determinant of symptom severity. These findings may reflect a general coping-related association rather than a direct effect. Menopause is a natural stage of life that affects all women and involves biological, psychological, and social changes [32]. These factors may partly explain the weak magnitude of the observed associations.

Rabiee et al., (2019) determined in their research with 400 women in the premenopausal period that as the psychological well-being of women increased, the severity of menopausal symptoms decreased [17]. Lee and Lee (2022) reported that the level of psychological well-being reduced menopausal symptoms in the structural equation model research they conducted to test the importance of positive and negative factors related to psychological health during menopause [33]. The present findings are consistent with the literature; however, the strength of the associations observed in this research appears to be lower than those reported in some previous research. This difference may be related to sample characteristics or methodological variations. In addition, the weak effect sizes in the present research further suggest limited explanatory power of psychological well-being in menopausal symptom severity.

Insecure attachment styles have been shown to be associated with lower psychological well-being and difficulties in interpersonal relationships. Certain dimensions of psychological well-being, particularly positive relationships and environmental control, have been identified as mediating mechanisms in the relationship between attachment patterns and psychological outcomes [34]. However, in the present research, mediation was not tested; therefore, no conclusions regarding indirect effects can be drawn.

Future research should adopt longitudinal and experimental designs to strengthen causal inferences between variables. Longitudinal studies can capture temporal relationships, while intervention studies may help establish causality by testing targeted interventions. Furthermore, incorporating additional variables such as social support, marital satisfaction, physical health status, lifestyle behaviors, and comorbidities would contribute to a more comprehensive and robust understanding of menopausal symptoms.

This research has some strengths. One of the most important strengths of this research is its large sample. However, the findings should be interpreted in light of the research’s limitations, particularly the cross-sectional design and the weak strength of the observed associations. Despite these limitations, this research contributes to the literature by providing preliminary evidence on psychosocial correlates of menopausal symptoms in a large sample of postmenopausal wome.

Conclusion

In conclusion, this research highlights the importance of psychological well-being and partner attachment in the management of menopausal symptoms among postmenopausal women. Although the observed associations were modest, the findings suggest that psychological well-being and partner attachment may be relevant psychosocial factors associated with menopausal symptom experience among postmenopausal women. Supportive partner relationships can mitigate feelings of loneliness and facilitate emotional regulation, thereby improving overall quality of life during the postmenopausal period. Although the present findings suggest that psychological well-being and partner attachment may be relevant psychosocial correlates of menopausal symptoms, the explanatory power of the regression model was modest. Therefore, these findings should be interpreted cautiously and regarded as preliminary. Additional longitudinal and interventional studies are needed before specific clinical recommendations or psychosocial interventions can be routinely recommended.

Healthcare professionals may consider being aware of the potential role of psychosocial factors when providing holistic menopause care; however, the present findings alone are insufficient to support routine psychosocial screening or partner-based interventions. In addition, structured psychosocial interventions—such as counseling, psychoeducation, and cognitive-behavioral approaches—may be implemented to enhance emotional regulation and psychological well-being.

Future intervention studies should investigate whether partner-inclusive or psychosocial interventions improve menopausal outcomes. Raising awareness about menopausal symptoms and their association with psychological changes and partner attachment may empower women to better understand and manage their experiences. In this context, involving partners in menopausal care may represent a practical and effective strategy to promote holistic well-being.

Future research should extend beyond cross-sectional designs to clarify causal relationships between attachment styles, psychological well-being, and menopausal symptoms. Longitudinal and interventional studies are needed to determine whether improving psychological well-being and relationship dynamics leads to meaningful reductions in symptom severity. Additionally, incorporating broader psychosocial and health-related variables—such as social support, marital satisfaction, physical activity, comorbidities, and lifestyle factors—may contribute to a more comprehensive understanding of menopausal health. Therefore, the findings should be interpreted cautiously and considered preliminary rather than definitive evidence regarding the psychosocial correlates of menopausal symptoms.

The use of online snowball sampling may have introduced selection bias and limited the representativeness of the sample. Participants with higher educational levels, greater internet access, and increased awareness of menopausal symptoms may have been more likely to participate in the research. These characteristics may have influenced the reporting of attachment styles, psychological well-being, and menopausal symptoms. Therefore, the findings should be interpreted with caution and may not be generalizable to all postmenopausal women.

Limitations

This research has several limitations that should be considered when interpreting the findings. First, the research was conducted in a single city in eastern Turkey, which may limit the generalizability of the results to different geographical regions and cultural contexts. Second, the cross-sectional design allows for the identification of associations but does not permit causal inferences. Third, data were collected using self-reported questionnaires, which may be subject to recall and social desirability biases. Additionally, the use of online data collection may have excluded individuals with limited digital literacy or lack of internet access and may have contributed to a non-representative sample. The use of the snowball sampling method in research may introduce significant selection bias and limit the representativeness of the sample due to the homogeneous nature of social networks. Importantly, several potential confounding variables were not measured or controlled for in this research, including physical health status, comorbid conditions, lifestyle factors, marital satisfaction, and sources of social support beyond the partner. These unmeasured variables may have influenced the observed associations. Another limitation of the research is that the etiology of menopause (natural, surgical, or premature) was not distinguished. Therefore, some participants—particularly younger women—may have experienced premature ovarian insufficiency or surgical menopause, which differ from natural menopause in psychological and physiological aspects. This may have introduced variability in menopausal symptom scores. Finally, the research primarily focused on partner attachment and psychological well-being and did not comprehensively assess other factors that may affect menopausal symptoms. Overall, these limitations, particularly the use of snowball sampling and online recruitment, may have introduced selection bias and limited the representativeness of the sample; therefore, the findings should be interpreted with caution, and generalizations to the wider population should be avoided. Although major sociodemographic and clinical factors were statistically controlled in our hierarchical regression model, other potential confounders such as marital satisfaction, specific comorbidities, medication use, sexual function, and smoking/lifestyle factors were not assessed. Therefore, the observed associations may be influenced by unmeasured or uncontrolled factors.
