Selecting between UKA and HTO in the grey zone
Authors: Jie Shen, Huigen Lu, Gang Chen, Kejian Fu
Published: 2026

Dear Editor,

We appreciate Guo et al.'s review on deciding between unicompartmental knee arthroplasty (UKA) and high tibial osteotomy (HTO) [1]. This grey area is one of the most challenging aspects of knee preservation and arthroplasty. For considering many preoperative factors, the authors deserve complimentary remarks.

However, we would like to propose that the grey area be further refined to distinguish between broad general prognostic elements and genuine procedure selection elements that are more actionable. Several factors in the review may influence the postoperative outcome, but they are not in the domain of determining in which direction the patient should go between UKA and HTO. The grey area question is ultimately who is likely to be better off with one of the procedures. Comprehensive frameworks are good, but they are still lacking in the concrete actionable specifics that will assist in answering that question.

Another aspect that may deserve some focus is the mechanical phenotype. The ESSKA consensus guidance states that the indications for osteotomy in the case of a painful degenerative varus knee are wider than previously assumed but still require deformity analysis and planning [2]. The most recent studies have demonstrated that patients undergoing UKA and medial open-wedge HTO have different patterns in terms of alignment, severity of osteoarthritis, and bony morphology [3]. Therefore, in any grey zone algorithm, factors related to coronal alignment, joint line, and the respective degree of contribution of the femoral and tibial deformity should be prioritized over demographic factors.

Third, endpoint selection needs to prioritize patient-centered outcomes as well as the downstream burden of revision surgeries. A recent meta-analysis showed that for UKAs revision rates, postoperative pain issues and complications were better than HTOs, although the functional outcomes were the same [4]. Conversely, recent studies from the Dutch Arthroplasty Register show that HTOs had more required revision components and less revision-free survival than UKAs [5]. Furthermore, pain and function postoperatively were improved with HTOs, but sport-related functional improvement was limited by coronal alignment and the importance of sport to the patient [6] AKUMA grey-zone patients. Symptom relief should not be the primary reason for selecting a surgical procedure; instead, the downstream complexity of future conversions, activity-related expectations, and patient-centered outcomes should be prioritized.

We therefore believe that Guo et al. have provided a valuable platform for more individualised decision-making, but that future refinements should more clearly separate factors that predict overall prognosis from those that modify the relative advantage of UKA versus HTO. With this, the proposed framework will be more applicable by shifting emphasis to the relevant mechanical phenotype and patient-centered outcomes.
