Deciphering Interfacial Electronic Interactions Between Iridium Oxide and Ni‐Based Supports for High‐Current‐Density Anion Exchange Membrane Water Electrolysis
Authors: Yeongbin Lee, Seohyeon Jang, Seokjin Hong, Seoyeon Jeong, Ju Young Kim, Wooseok Jeong, Hyeonseok Lee, Gyuhyeon Kim, Hyungbin Son, Sang Hyun Ahn, Inho Nam, Don‐Hyung Ha
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTEnhancing the oxygen evolution reaction (OER) performance of Ir‐based catalysts while minimizing Ir usage is crucial for advancing anion exchange membrane water electrolyzers (AEMWEs). Herein, we present binder‐free iridium oxide (IrO2)‐Ni‐based electrodes prepared via a liquid‐phase synthesis, which directly anchors an ultralow quantity of IrO2 nanoparticles onto versatile Ni‐based foams. This design enables systematic investigation of how Ni‐based support chemistry modulates interfacial electronic interactions and thereby dictates catalytic performance. Electrochemical measurements reveal that IrO2 on nickel phosphide (Ni2P) exhibits the highest activity and durability, with a significantly low overpotential of 232 mV and stable operation over 400 h at 10 mA cm−2. Spectroscopic and density functional theory (DFT) analyses reveal that strong interfacial charge transfer between IrO2 and Ni2P enriches the electron density around Ir and downshifts the Ir d‐band center, thereby optimizing the adsorption energetics of OER intermediates and accelerating the reaction kinetics. The binder‐free configuration enhances mechanical integrity and mass transport, which enables high‐current‐density operation (3.1 A cm−2 at 2.0 Vcell) under practical AEMWE conditions. Thus, the Ni‐based support material enables tuning of the interfacial electronic coupling and catalytic behavior of IrO2, thereby supporting the development of binder‐free low Ir‐loaded electrodes for cost‐effective and durable AEMWEs.Keywords: Anion exchange membrane water electrolyzer, Binder‐free electrode, Catalyst–support interaction, Iridium oxide, Oxygen evolution reaction

Introduction

The oxygen evolution reaction (OER) is a key half‐reaction in water electrolysis, yet its sluggish kinetics require the electrocatalyst to be highly active and durable [1]. Iridium oxide (IrO2) remains the benchmark OER catalyst in practical electrolyzer systems due to its exceptional structural robustness and corrosion resistance [2, 3]. However, the scarcity and high cost of Ir, along with its lower intrinsic activity compared to RuO2, highlight the need for innovative strategies that drastically enhance the catalytic efficiency of IrO2 while maximizing Ir utilization [4, 5].

Beyond intrinsic activity, another significant bottleneck arises from the conventional fabrication of Ir‐based electrodes is caused by mixing catalyst powders with polymeric binders and conductive additives before coating them onto substrates [6]. The inherent drawbacks of this method are i) polymeric binders such as Nafion or PTFE can partially block active sites and impede ion/electron transport [7], ii) the catalyst layers often exhibit poor adhesion and are prone to mechanical degradation during prolonged operation [8], and iii) hydrophobic binder domains impair electrolyte penetration and gas bubble release, particularly under high‐current loads [9, 10]. These factors collectively lead to premature performance degradation in binder‐containing electrodes under the demanding conditions (e.g., electric current density > 1 A cm−2) required in practical water electrolyzers.

Binder‐free architectures, in which IrO2 nanoparticles (NPs) are directly anchored onto conductive and high‐surface area supports, have therefore emerged as a compelling strategy to address the aforementioned limitations [11, 12]. Such heterogeneous catalyst‐support systems enable IrO2 NPs to disperse uniformly while suppressing NP agglomeration and substantially reducing noble‐metal loading [13, 14, 15]. Furthermore, strong interfacial coupling between the IrO2 NPs and the underlying support can modulate the electronic structure of Ir, thereby tailoring the adsorption energetics of OER intermediates and improving both activity and durability [8, 16]. In this context, the choice of support is not merely structural but plays a decisive role in governing interfacial charge redistribution, catalyst activity, and long‐term operation under harsh anodic conditions.

Carbon‐based supports, widely employed due to their high surface area and electrical conductivity, face intrinsic limitations under anodic OER conditions. Carbon materials are thermodynamically unstable and susceptible to electrochemical corrosion, particularly at potentials exceeding 1.4 V vs. RHE [17]. Progressive carbon oxidation not only deteriorates structural integrity but also weakens catalyst–support interactions, leading to catalyst detachment and accelerated performance decay. Moreover, the relatively inert electronic structure of carbon restricts the extent of interfacial charge redistribution with IrO2, often limiting the catalyst–support interaction to physical anchoring rather than electronic coupling [18].

Among the various conductive support materials, Ni foam is widely recognized as an optimal platform for binder‐free electrode fabrication owing to its high electrical conductivity, interconnected porous architecture, and excellent mechanical robustness [19, 20]. This unique 3D framework not only facilitates efficient charge transport and rapid gas diffusion but also offers a chemically stable and modifiable surface for heterogeneous catalyst growth. More importantly, the Ni framework provides a chemically versatile foundation that can be tuned through surface modification to form functional Ni‐derived compounds, thereby allowing systematic investigation of how interfacial chemistry governs catalytic behavior [21].

Building upon this versatile Ni platform, functional Ni compounds such as nickel sulfides and phosphides have emerged as promising catalytic supports owing to their metallic conductivity, adjustable electronegativity, and diverse bonding characteristics [22]. These properties suggest the potential for stronger orbital hybridization and sustained interfacial charge transfer when coupled with noble‐metal catalysts. In contrast, conventional oxide support materials (e.g., TiO2, WO3, and V2O5) are chemically stable but generally exhibit relatively low electrical conductivity and limited electronic tunability [23, 24, 25]. Although oxide supports can induce strong metal–support interactions, the extent of interfacial charge redistribution is often constrained without alloying, intentional doping, or bulk compositional modification. By comparison, nickel phosphides and sulfides can facilitate more extensive band‐level hybridization with supported IrO2, enabling more pronounced and tunable redistribution of electron density at the interface. Such support‐induced electronic modulation can shift the Ir d‐band center and optimize the adsorption energetics of key OER intermediates [26, 27, 28, 29]. Despite this potential, systematic studies directly correlating support identity, interfacial electronic interactions, and catalytic performance in binder‐free IrO2 systems remain scarce. Establishing these correlations is essential for developing fundamental design principles for rational electrode engineering in practical electrolyzers.

In the present study, we developed binder‐free IrO2–Ni‐based electrodes via a liquid‐phase synthesis process that enabled precise control over the IrO2 nanoparticle morphology and ultralow Ir loading (< 0.02 mgIr cm−2), independent of the support material. A comparative investigation revealed that nickel phosphide (Ni2P) was a particularly effective IrO2‐catalyst‐support material, which in contrast to Ni and nickel sulfide (Ni3S2), enriched the electron density around Ir, accompanied by a downshift of the Ir d‐band center. This electronic modulation optimized the adsorption strength of key OER intermediates (OH*, O*, and OOH*) within the adsorbate evolution mechanism pathway, resulting in markedly enhanced catalytic activity. Density functional theory (DFT) calculations further proved strong interfacial coupling between IrO2 and Ni2P mediated by stable interfacial bonds and Ir─O linkages, which collectively resulted in the catalyst's prolonged operational stability. This integrated Ni‐based platform provides a well‐defined comparative framework to elucidate how support chemistry governs the interfacial electronic structure and catalytic performance. More broadly, our findings support a generalizable strategy for interfacial support engineering in binder‐free noble‐metal catalysts, thereby paving the way toward cost‐effective and durable electrodes for next‐generation water electrolyzers.

Results and Discussion

The preparation of IrO2 deposited on Ni‐based foams via liquid‐phase synthesis and deposition is illustrated in Figure 1a [30]. To investigate interactions between IrO2 and the Ni‐based support materials, various types of Ni‐based compounds were first fabricated by adjusting the reaction conditions and adopting specific anion sources. Using Ni foam as the starting material, Ni3S2 or Ni2P were grown directly on Ni foam via sulfurization or phosphorization by utilizing di‐tert‐butyl disulfide (TBDS) or Triphenyl phosphite (TPP) as the anion source, respectively. These self‐supported Ni‐based compounds exhibited characteristic surface morphologies and crystal structures. Subsequently, IrO2 NPs were deposited by immersing the support material (Ni, Ni3S2, or Ni2P foam) in NaOH solution (pH 11–12) containing an Ir3+ precursor for 2 h at 80°C, thereby producing the analogous morphological features of the resulting IrO2 NPs. These three types of electrode materials provided a platform to thoroughly explore the interfacial interactions between the IrO2 and Ni‐based compounds, which could influence their various properties.

The X‐ray diffraction (XRD) patterns in Figure 1b verify the growth of Ni‐based compounds (Ni3S2 and Ni2P) on Ni foam, as well as the preservation of the overall crystal structure of support materials after the IrO2 deposition process. While pristine Ni foam exhibited only two prominent peaks at 44.5° and 51.8°, additional characteristics peaks in the patterns for the Ni3S2‐ and Ni2P‐based foams clearly indicate the formation of nickel sulfides and phosphides by aligning well with the reference peaks for heazlewoodite Ni3S2 and hexagonal Ni2P, respectively, thereby confirming the self‐supported growth of these compounds on Ni foam via the liquid‐phase synthesis. In addition, the XRD pattern for the Ni2P foam contained minor peaks at 32.73°, 46.96°, and 48.96°, which matched the (310), (240), and (321) planes of the Ni12P5 phase, respectively, signifying that nickel phosphide consisted of Ni2P as the major phase and Ni12P5 as a minor phase. Moreover, after the IrO2 deposition process, clearly identical crystal structures were maintained in all samples without the evolution of additional crystalline structure and structural transformations of Ni, Ni3S2, and Ni2P. Notably, diffraction peaks indicating Ir‐related species were not present in the patterns of any of the samples, which could be due to the extremely low loading amounts of Ir therein.

Ni3S2 and Ni2P were successfully grown on Ni foam, with each displaying distinct surface morphological characteristics, as discernible in the scanning electron microscopy (SEM) images in Figure 1c–e. Compared to the smooth surface of pristine Ni foam (Figure 1c), that of the Ni3S2 foam exhibited a vertically oriented, triangular pyramid‐like structure (Figure 1d) with an Ni/S atomic ratio of 60.03:39.97 (Table S1), which is consistent with the Ni3S2 phase. Meanwhile, the surface of the Ni2P foam featured self‐supported triangular prism structures composed of individual clusters (Figure 1e) with an Ni/P atomic ratio of 70.04:29.96 (Table S1), thus confirming the complete coverage of the Ni foam surface by the Ni‐based compounds. Further structural analysis of Ni3S2 and Ni2P via high‐resolution transmission electron microscopy (HRTEM) revealed atomic arrangements corresponding to the (110) plane of Ni3S2 and the (111) plane of Ni2P, with interplanar spacings of 2.89 and 2.21 Å, respectively (Figure S1a,b). Moreover, the scanning transmission electron microscopy‐energy‐dispersive X‐ray spectroscopy (STEM‐EDS) results confirm the uniform dispersion of elements (Figure S1c,d) and consistent atomic ratios (Table S2), thereby providing clear evidence of the formation of Ni‐based compounds through the liquid‐phase synthesis.

Although the unique morphologies of Ni‐based foam remained largely intact after IrO2 deposition, the surfaces became noticeably coarser, likely due to surface oxidation occurring in the NaOH solution (Figure 1f–h). Elemental analysis using SEM‐EDS substantiated the presence and uniform distribution of Ir species across all samples with no significant clustering or aggregation of Ir at specific sites (Figure S2a–c). Moreover, the preserved Ni/S (61.8:37.9) and Ni/P (70.5:29.2) atomic ratios indicate that the support structures were not noticeably altered during the deposition process (Table S3).

Raman spectroscopy was employed to probe the vibrational characteristics of the catalysts and to examine possible surface oxidation and IrO2 formation (Figure S3). All samples exhibited a broad band in the ∼450–550 cm−1 region, which was attributed to Ni–O vibrations associated with nickel oxide/oxyhydroxide species formed via partial oxidation of the Ni‐based foam [31, 32]. In addition, a weak band appearing at ∼720–750 cm−1 corresponded to the A1g phonon mode of rutile IrO2, arising from the symmetric stretching vibration of Ir–O bonds, confirming the presence of IrO2 on the catalyst surfaces [33].

Distinct vibrational features corresponding to the support frameworks were also observed. For IrO2–Ni2P, Raman bands in the ∼100–200 cm−1 region was assigned to Ni–P lattice vibrations, while a high‐wavenumber band near ∼1000 cm−1 corresponds to P–O stretching vibrations, indicating partial oxidation of phosphorus species [34, 35]. In the case of IrO2–Ni3S2, peaks in the ∼200–400 cm−1 region were attributed to Ni–S lattice vibrations [36], together with a band near ∼1000 cm−1 corresponding to S–O stretching vibrations, suggesting the formation of oxidized sulfur species [37]. For IrO2–Ni, a prominent band at ∼520 cm−1 is observed, which is attributed to Ni–O vibrations associated with surface NiOOH species, commonly formed on Ni‐based electrodes. Since metallic Ni is Raman inactive, the observed Raman signals mainly originate from surface oxide/hydroxide species and the deposited IrO2, confirming successful IrO2 deposition and slight surface oxidation of the catalyst surface.

HRTEM imaging provides additional evidence for the successful deposition of IrO2 onto the Ni‐based form while emphasizing the consistent morphological features of the catalyst, regardless of the support type. Upon reaction with Ir3+ in NaOH solution, uniformly distributed ∼3 nm‐sized spherical nanoparticles were observed on the Ni, Ni3S2, and Ni2P surfaces (Figure 2a–c, respectively). The interplanar spacing of these spherical NPs corresponded to the (200) plane of IrO2 (a d‐spacing value of ∼2.25 Å [38]), thus verifying the formation of IrO2 via the deposition method. While previous studies have reported that layered double hydroxide (LDH) structures typically incorporate Ir in its non‐oxidized form, the formation of oxidized Ir on the Ni‐based foams (Ni, Ni3S2, and Ni2P) suggests that the reaction pathway may vary depending on the support structure [39, 40]. Furthermore, the STEM images and corresponding elemental maps demonstrate the uniform distribution of individual elements across all samples, thereby substantiating the homogeneous deposition process (Figure 2d–f). Inductively coupled plasma‐mass spectrometry (ICP‐MS) analysis determined the precise loading amounts of Ir species (Table S4): 0.010, 0.012, and 0.016 mgIr cm−2 for IrO2‐Ni, IrO2‐Ni3S2, and IrO2‐Ni2P, respectively, highlighting the ultralow Ir loading in these materials. Overall, the well‐controlled size and morphology of the IrO2 NPs enabled a systematic investigation of their interaction with the Ni‐based support materials, which is critical for understanding their effects on the electronic structure and, consequently, catalytic performance.

Since catalytic reactions are predominantly governed by the surface chemical states, with surface atoms directly participating in the overall reactions, the surface chemical states of the catalysts were analyzed by using X‐ray photoelectron spectroscopy (XPS) to explore the electronic interactions between IrO2 and the three types of support material (Ni, Ni3S2, and Ni2P). Notably, a comparison of the Ir 4f spectra for IrO2‐Ni, IrO2‐Ni3S2, and IrO2‐Ni2P in Figure 3a uncovered the influence of the Ni‐based compounds on the electronic structure of Ir, as evidenced by variations in peak positions as well as the relative chemical state proportions; the primary peaks (Ir 4f7/2 and 4f5/2) appeared at the lowest binding energies in the spectrum for IrO2‐Ni2P (62.2 and 65.2 eV, respectively), followed by IrO2‐Ni (62.48 and 65.38 eV, respectively) and IrO2‐Ni3S2 (62.58 and 65.58 eV, respectively), indicating differences in the oxidation state of Ir depending on the support material. These distinct shifts in the Ir oxidation states suggest that the Ni2P support material likely donates electron density to the Ir atoms, thereby reducing their valence states. Furthermore, deconvolution of the Ir 4f7/2 and 4f5/2 peaks confirms the absence of metallic Ir, with only Ir3+, Ir4+, and satellite peaks typical of IrO2 present [41, 42]. While the peak positions for Ir3+ (62.8 and 66 eV) and satellites (∼64 and ∼67 eV) did not significantly change in the spectra for the samples, the binding energies for Ir4+ differed depending on the support material: 62.3 and 65.3 eV for IrO2‐Ni, 62.4 and 65.45 eV for IrO2‐Ni3S2, and 62.1 and 65.1 eV for IrO2‐Ni2P. These shifts further corroborate the finding that Ir in IrO2‐Ni2P attained the lowest oxidation state. Since prior studies have shown that a higher proportion of the lower‐valence state (3+) is favorable for catalyzing the OER [43, 44], the ratios for the Ir3+ and Ir4+ states were calculated to quantify these observations (Table S5). Among the samples, IrO2‐Ni2P had the highest Ir3+/Ir4+ ratio (0.61), followed by IrO2‐Ni (0.41) and IrO2‐Ni3S2 (0.31). These opposing shifts provide direct evidence of strong electron donation from the Ni2P support to IrO2. As will be further elucidated by DFT calculations, this electron‐rich environment at the Ir active sites downshifts the Ir d‐band center, thereby optimizing the adsorption strength of OER intermediates [45, 46, 47]. Furthermore, the lower oxidation state of Ir in IrO2‐Ni2P is expected to enhance catalytic efficiency and durability under the harsh conditions of the OER by mitigating Ir over‐oxidation and subsequent dissolution [48].

XPS (a) Ir 4f, (b) Ni 2p, and (c) O 1s spectra for IrO2‐Ni, IrO2‐Ni3S2, and IrO2‐Ni2P.

The Ni 2p spectra for the samples in Figure 3b contain prominent peaks corresponding to Ni2+, Ni3+, and their satellites, along with characteristic peaks indicative of the chemical states for Ni metal (852.6 eV), nickel sulfide (853 eV), or nickel phosphide (853.5 eV) species. In the Ni 2p spectra for Ni3S2 and Ni2P, two pairs of intense peaks appeared in the higher binding energy region compared to Ni metal (Ni0; 852.6 eV) [49], which is attributable to Ni‐S bonds (853.05 and 870.35 eV for Ni3S2 [50]) and Ni─P bonds (853.5 and 870.73 eV for Ni2P [28]) (Figure S4a). Additional peaks corresponding to Ni2+ (855.9 and 873.66 eV for Ni3S2; 856.05 and 873.52 eV for Ni2P) and satellites (861.25 and 880.02 eV for Ni3S2; 860.91 and 878.6 eV for Ni2P) were also present. Notably, in the spectra for IrO2‐Ni, IrO2‐Ni3S2, and IrO2‐Ni2P, the emergence of Ni3+ peaks (857.5 and 874.9 eV for IrO2‐Ni; 857.5 and 874.8 eV for IrO2‐Ni3S2; and 857.9 and 875.3 eV for IrO2‐Ni2P) likely originated from NiOOH species formed via surface oxidation during IrO2 deposition in NaOH solution. In addition to the Ni3+ state, peaks corresponding to Ni2+ were observed at a higher binding energy in IrO2‐Ni2P (856 and 873.5 eV) compared to IrO2‐Ni (855.8 and 873.3 eV) and IrO2‐Ni3S2 (855.8 and 873.2 eV), thereby indicating a reversed trend relative to the Ir 4f spectra. The O 1s spectra for all of the samples reveal the presence of four distinct oxygen species: lattice O (530.5 eV), Ir─OH (∼531.15 eV), Ni─OH (∼531.9 eV), and adsorbed H2O (∼532.9 eV) (Figure 3c) [51, 52]. In addition, after IrO2 deposition, the S 2p and P 2p spectra in Figures S4b,c, and S5 are characteristic of typical nickel sulfides [26]. and phosphides [53], respectively, confirming the structural integrity of the support materials. Despite the similar morphology of IrO2 NPs across the different support materials, the electronic structures of Ir and Ni were significantly affected by the type of support, thus indicating that IrO2‐support interactions are likely to affect catalytic properties.

The OER activity of IrO2 deposited on Ni‐based supports, along with the bare supports and commercial IrO2 on carbon paper (CP) (IrO2/CP) reference, was evaluated in 1 M KOH electrolyte using a three‐electrode system. To minimize interference from non‐OER currents arising from the Ni oxidation of the Ni foam substrate, all OER polarization curves were extracted from the reverse scan to enable accurate comparison among the catalysts [54]. As shown in Figure 4a, IrO2‐Ni2P demonstrated remarkably higher OER performance than the other samples across the entire current density range. Specifically, IrO2‐Ni2P achieved the lowest overpotentials of 232 and 309 mV at current densities of 10 and 100 mA cm−2, respectively, thus reflecting the optimized surface Ir chemical states, which are highly favorable for catalyzing the OER. Furthermore, the higher activity of IrO2‐Ni (287 mV at 10 mA cm−2) compared to IrO2‐Ni3S2 (306 mV at 10 mA cm−2) highlights the strong correlation between surface chemical states and catalytic activity. Although the commercial IrO2/CP reference (305 mV at 10 mA cm−2) displayed slightly higher activity than IrO2‐Ni3S2 at a low current density, this trend was reversed at higher current density, highlighting the superior IrO2–support interactions of our catalyst compared to the conventional counterpart in high‐current regions. Among the support materials, Ni2P exhibited the highest activity (365 mV at 10 mA cm−2), which emphasizes the importance of engineering highly active support materials (408 mV at 10 mA cm−2 for Ni3S2 and 435 mV at 10 mA cm−2 for Ni) (Figure S6). Thus, the interaction between IrO2 and Ni2P led to exceptional OER performance, thus highlighting the critical role of the chemical states therein in determining the catalytic properties.

To provide insights into the reaction kinetics and charge‐transfer behavior resulting in the distinguished catalytic properties of IrO2‐Ni2P, Tafel and electrochemical impedance spectroscopy (EIS) analyses were further conducted (Figure 4b,c). Although the complex OER mechanism limits the direct elucidation of the reaction pathway from a Tafel slope alone, the remarkably low Tafel slope (71.6 mV dec−1) and charge‐transfer resistance (Rct = 4.86 Ω) of IrO2‐Ni2P ascertained its rapid reaction kinetics and efficient charge transfer, both of which are closely correlated with its exceptional catalytic efficiency. Furthermore, the observed trends in Tafel slopes (77.1 mV dec−1 for IrO2‐Ni, 79.4 mV dec−1 for IrO2/CP, and 86.7 mV dec−1 for IrO2‐Ni3S2) and Rct values (5.53 Ω for IrO2‐Ni, 5.92 Ω for IrO2/CP and 6.44 Ω for IrO2‐Ni3S2) are consistent with the OER activity since a lower Tafel slope and charge‐transfer resistance are indicative of enhanced electrocatalytic performance. To gain a deeper understanding of the charge‐transfer kinetics during the OER, in situ EIS was conducted under various applied potentials (1.20—1.55 V) (Figure 4d and Figure S7) [55]. The Bode phase plots in Figure 4e indicate that IrO2‐Ni2P featured a more rapid abatement of the phase angle in the low frequency region with an increase in applied potential compared to IrO2‐Ni and IrO2‐Ni3S2. Such behavior highlights the crucial role of the support material in improving the electrocatalytic kinetics and directly influencing the OER activity [56].

Given the varying influence of surface morphology on OER performance depending on the electrodes, the intrinsic catalytic activity therein was evaluated to focus on the inherent catalytic properties of the materials. Cyclic voltammetry (CV) curves were obtained over an identical potential range at different scan rates, exhibiting capacitor‐like behavior in the electrocatalysts (Figure S8). The linear relationship between current density and scan rate was used to determine the surface area of each catalyst (quantified by using the slope), which corresponds to the electrochemical double‐layer capacitance (Cdl ) (Figure 4f). The significantly larger Cdl value for IrO2‐Ni2P (6.80 mF cm−2) indicates the presence of abundant active areas that contribute to its superior catalytic activity. In addition, the IrO2‐deposited samples exhibited higher Cdl values compared to Ni foam (1.22 mF cm−2), with IrO2‐Ni3S2 (3.54 mF cm−2) having a slightly larger value than IrO2‐Ni (2.77 mF cm−2), likely due to the morphological effects of the support materials. The intrinsic activity was subsequently compared based on Cdl , which exhibited a proportional relationship with the electrochemically active surface area (ECSA); the latter was estimated by dividing Cdl by the specific capacitance (Cs ) of a flat surface electrode, for which we took the measured value of 1.22 mF cm−2 for Ni foam. Notably, the ECSA‐normalized polarization curves confirm that IrO2‐Ni2P exhibited the highest intrinsic current density at an overpotential of 300 mV (13.5 mA cmECSA −2), followed by IrO2‐Ni (6.85 mA cmECSA −2) and IrO2‐Ni3S2 (2.85 mA cmECSA −2), thereby highlighting the distinguished catalytic efficiency of the active sites in IrO2‐Ni2P (Figure 4g and Figure S9a). Furthermore, IrO2‐Ni2P featured an outstanding mass activity of 4.6 A mg−1 at 1.53 V, far surpassing that of previously reported noble‐metal‐based OER catalysts (Figure 4g and Figure S9b, and Table S6). This exceptional mass activity, achieved with extremely low Ir loading, highlights the efficient utilization of Ir atoms (an essential factor in noble‐metal‐based electrocatalysts). Moreover, the remarkable inherent catalytic activity of IrO2‐Ni2P placed an emphasis on modulating the surface chemical states arising from the synergistic interactions between IrO2 and Ni2P.

The beneficial effects of IrO2–support interfacial interactions were further corroborated by comparing the catalytic activity of chemically deposited IrO2‐Ni2P with that of physically attached commercial IrO2 on Ni2P foam (Com. IrO2‐Ni2P). While ensuring the same Ir‐loading amount (0.016 mg cm−2), the Com. IrO2‐Ni2P electrode was fabricated by simply drop‐casting a commercial IrO2‐containing solution onto Ni2P foam, signifying a system with only physical interactions between the components. A comparison of OER activity revealed that the interfacial interactions between IrO2 and Ni2P significantly improved catalytic performance, with IrO2‐Ni2P exhibiting superior OER performance compared to Com. IrO2‐Ni2P (Figure S10). The active surface area and inherent activity were also investigated while considering differences in the size and morphology of commercial IrO2 and chemically deposited IrO2 NPs (Figure S11). The Cdl value for IrO2‐Ni2P (6.80 mF cm−2) was approximately twice that for Com. IrO2‐Ni2P (3.39 mF cm−2), while the intrinsic activity of the former (13.5 mA cmECSA −2 at 1.53 V) was nearly four times higher than that of the latter (3.30 mA cmECSA −2 at 1.53 V) (Figure S12). The increments in intrinsic activity and the enlarged active surface area seen in IrO2‐Ni2P are attributed to the chemical deposition process, which not only created favorable chemical states for catalytic reactions but also facilitated strong mutual interactions between IrO2 and Ni2P, thereby significantly enhancing the catalytic properties (Figure S13).

In addition to the remarkable activity, various stability tests under harsh conditions verified the catalytic robustness of IrO2‐Ni2P. During the accelerated degradation test (ADT), IrO2‐Ni2P exhibited the smallest deviation in OER polarization curves before and after 2,000 CV cycles at a scan rate of 100 mV s−1, with only a 10 mV increase in overpotential at 10 mA cm−2 (Figure 4e). In contrast, IrO2‐Ni and IrO2‐Ni3S2 showed significant attenuation, with an overpotential increase of approximately 60 mV, exhibiting their vulnerability to a rapidly fluctuating voltage. Although the performance of IrO2/CP at 10 mA cm−2 remained nearly unchanged, the polarization curve exhibited a shift toward lower activity, thereby suggesting a significant deterioration in the reaction kinetics therein. As well as stable operation during multi‐step chronopotentiometry test, IrO2‐Ni2P sustained ∼90% of its initial catalytic performance after 400 h of testing at 10 mA cm−2, while the others were lower (∼66% for IrO2‐Ni, ~77% for IrO2‐Ni3S2, and ∼82% IrO2‐CP), thereby confirming the exceptional durability of IrO2‐Ni2P (Figure 4f).

After OER analysis figured out the structural inertness and changes in surface chemical states of the samples, as well as uncovering the underlying factors contributing to the superior electrocatalytic performance of IrO2‐Ni2P. While the surfaces of IrO2‐Ni, IrO2‐Ni3S2, and IrO2‐Ni2P appeared to have undergone inevitable oxidation during OER, the SEM images confirm that their morphological characteristics were well preserved (Figure S14a–f). In addition, there were no noticeable variations in XRD patterns before and after OER test (Figure S15), which highlights the structural robustness of the electrodes crucial for ensuring long‐term operability. The long‐lasting catalytic behavior of IrO2‐Ni2P could be linked to the low oxidation state of Ir, as demonstrated via XPS (Figure S16a). During the OER process, the surface chemical states underwent inevitable modulation due to the gradual oxidation of metal species [38]. Despite an overall increase in the oxidation state of Ir, the primary peaks (Ir 4f7/2 and 4f5/2) were located at lower binding energies in the XPS profile for IrO2‐Ni2P (62.3 and 65.3 eV) than seen in those for IrO2‐Ni (62.6 and 65.6 eV) and IrO2‐Ni3S2 (62.7 and 65.7 eV). This relatively low oxidation state of Ir in IrO2‐Ni2P contributed to the retention of its catalytic properties during the OER by effectively mitigating over‐oxidation that could otherwise cause Ir dissolution and a subsequent decline in activity. Meanwhile, the binding energies of each chemical state in the Ni 2p spectra for the samples after the OER remained nearly unchanged, although an increase in signal in the Ni 3p region was observed in the Ir 4f spectra (Figure S16b). In addition, although the O 1s spectra for the samples consistently revealed four distinct oxygen species (lattice O, Ir‐OH, Ni‐OH, and adsorbed H2O) at similar binding energies before and after the OER, the relative peak areas varied (Figure S16c). The proportion of lattice oxygen was well preserved in IrO2‐Ni2P, whereas it decreased in IrO2‐Ni and IrO2‐Ni3S2, implying enhanced stability of IrO2‐Ni2P, due to the robustness of its surface oxygen species (Figure S17) [4]. Our findings suggest that Ni2P is an ideal support material for interacting with IrO2, thereby optimizing the chemical states that facilitate the OER and long‐term operation.

DFT calculations were performed to elucidate the intrinsic influence of the Ni‐based supports on the OER performance of IrO2. Prior to the calculations, electrochemical analysis using tetramethylammonium hydroxide (TMAOH) as the electrolyte was conducted to distinguish between the adsorption evolution mechanism (AEM) and the lattice oxygen oxidation mechanism (LOM). It has been well established that TMA+ can selectively interact with surface O2 2−/O−‐like intermediates, which are characteristic signatures of the LOM pathway. Such interactions are known to suppress OER kinetics when TMAOH is introduced into the electrolyte, due to the stabilization or blocking of lattice oxygen–related reactive species [57, 58]. However, the polarization curves measured in 1 m TMAOH exhibited no significant current decrease compared to those in the KOH environment, suggesting that the LOM pathway is not activated (Figure S18). Furthermore, to thermodynamically analyze the experimental observations derived from the TMAOH electrolyte system, we performed DFT calculations to evaluate the free energy profiles of both the AEM and the LOM on IrO2‐Ni, IrO2‐Ni3S2, and IrO2‐Ni2P surfaces (Figure S19) [59]. The calculated OER overpotentials (ηOER) are consistently lower for the AEM than for the LOM across all samples. This indicates that the oxygen intermediates bind very strongly to the catalyst surface, making the formation of oxygen vacancies (VO) thermodynamically uphill [60]. Consequently, the generation of LOM‐specific reactive intermediates (e.g., O2 2−/O−) is significantly suppressed, which prevents interaction with TMA+. These experimental observations strongly suggest that the OER in this catalyst system predominantly follows the AEM pathway. Accordingly, subsequent DFT calculations were performed based on the AEM.

Given the ultralow loading amount of IrO2 in the experimental system, the computational model adopted a rutile IrO2 (110) cluster as an island supported on Ni (111), Ni2P (001), and Ni3S2 (0001) substrates (Figure S20). The on‐top sites of the Ir atoms in IrO2‐Ni, IrO2‐Ni3S2, and IrO2‐Ni2P served as the active centers for binding OER intermediates *O, *OH, and *OOH (Figure 5a–c). The computed free‐energy profiles of the OER steps at U = 0 and 1.23 V for IrO2‐Ni, IrO2‐Ni3S2, and IrO2‐Ni2P in Figure 5d reveal that the rate‐determining step (RDS) corresponds to the formation of the Ir‐OOH* intermediate from Ir─O* across all of the calculated configurations, which is consistent with the conventional adsorbate evolution mechanism of IrO2. Importantly, the IrO2‐Ni2P system exhibited the lowest free‐energy barrier of 2.2 eV, underscoring its thermodynamically favorable pathway for the OER. By contrast, IrO2–Ni3S2 and IrO2–Ni both showed higher free‐energy barriers of 2.5 eV, which is in agreement with the experimental observations. As summarized in Table S7, IrO2‐Ni2P also showed the weakest oxygen adsorption energy (E O* = 2.0 eV compared to 1.7 and 1.8 eV for IrO2‐Ni and IrO2‐Ni3S2, respectively). Since excessively strong O adsorption can hinder O─O coupling and limit the overall reaction rate, the moderate weakening of M─O* binding observed in IrO2‐Ni2P effectively lowers the barrier for M─OOH* formation and thereby enhances the OER kinetics [61].

Further investigations into the interfacial electronic interactions provided critical insights into the exceptional catalytic behavior of IrO2 supported on Ni2P. Unlike the other supports, IrO2‐Ni2P forms distinct bonding interactions between the oxygen atoms in IrO2 and both the phosphorus and nickel atoms in the Ni2P support (Figure S21). Bader charge analysis revealed that IrO2‐Ni2P accumulates a greater electron density on the IrO2 surface (5.6) compared with those in IrO2‐Ni and IrO2‐Ni3S2 (4.6 and 4.7 electrons, respectively) (Figure 5e–h). This result indicates more efficient electron transfer from the Ni2P support to IrO2 than from Ni or Ni3S2, thereby enriching the electron density of Ir sites and modulating their electronic structures. This theoretical finding perfectly aligns with our earlier XPS observations (Figure 3). Specifically, the opposing shifts of the Ir 4f7/2 peak to the lowest binding energy (62.2 eV) and the Ni 2p3/2 peak to the highest binding energy (856 eV) provide direct experimental evidence of this electron donation. Consequently, the highest Ir3+/Ir4+ ratio (0.61) observed in IrO2‐Ni2P corroborates the establishment of an electron‐rich environment at the Ir active sites. Such electron enrichment was also reflected in the position of the Ir d‐band center downshifting to −3.1 eV in IrO2‐Ni2P compared with −3.0 eV in IrO2‐Ni and IrO2‐Ni3S2 (Figure 5i). A lower d‐band center corresponds to a higher occupancy of antibonding states, which weakens the binding strength between IrO2 and the oxygenated intermediates. Accordingly, this moderation of adsorption energetics facilitates oxygen release and accelerates the overall OER process [25].

The electronic alignment and interfacial bonding stability at the IrO2‐support interfaces were evaluated by calculating the energy difference (ΔE_dp) between the Ni d‐band center in supports and the O p‐band center in IrO2 (Figure 5j). The IrO2‐Ni2P interface featured the smallest ΔE_dp of 1.20 eV (1.53 and 1.51 eV for the IrO2‐Ni and IrO2‐Ni3S2 interfaces, respectively), which is indicative of the best orbital alignment and most stable interfacial bonding. This result was further corroborated by the calculated Ir–O bond length variation relative to pure IrO2, which followed the order IrO2‐Ni2P (102.0%) < IrO2‐Ni (102.8%) < IrO2‐Ni3S2 (105.6%) (Table S8). The minimal lattice distortion observed in IrO2‐Ni2P is expected to enhance the structural compatibility and greater preservation of Ir–O bond integrity within the IrO2 lattice. These favorable structural and orbital characteristics fundamentally explain why Ni2P acts as the most highly efficient electron donor among the supports. This optimized interface facilitates a more effective pathway for electron transfer, thereby enriching the electron density on the IrO2 surface and inducing a downward shift of the Ir d‐band center. This electronic reconfiguration allows the O intermediate to achieve an optimal adsorption strength, being neither too strong nor too weak. Ultimately, from the fundamental interfacial orbital alignment to the minimized RDS free energy barrier, underpins its superior catalytic activity and durability during the OER.

Building on the theoretical predictions, the applicability of IrO2‐Ni2P was evaluated as a practical anode in an anion exchange membrane water electrolyzer (AEMWE) system. Guided by the half‐cell OER results and DFT insights, the IrO2‐Ni2P sample was integrated as the anode catalyst in an AEMWE system, and its performance and durability were benchmarked against a commercial IrO2/CP electrode (Figure 6a). The AEMWE single‐cell polarization curves were obtained for two different membrane electrode assembly (MEA) configurations over a potential range of 1.0–2.0 Vcell, flowing 1.0 m KOH (preheated to 80°C) as the electrolyte. Remarkably, the IrO2‐Ni2P || Pt/C/CP configuration achieved a current density of 3.1 A cm−2 @ 2.0 Vcell, whereas the IrO2/CP || Pt/C/CP configuration reached only 1.9 A cm−2 under identical conditions (Figure 6b). The performance gap was particularly pronounced at current densities exceeding 1 A cm−2, which was attributed to the enhanced mass transport in the self‐supported IrO2‐Ni2P electrode not requiring a polymeric binder. The AEMWE single‐cell test results clearly demonstrate the advantages of the binder‐free IrO2‐Ni2P architecture, particularly in achieving high activity at large current densities, which is particularly challenging for conventional powder‐based electrodes. Moreover, the IrO2/CP || Pt/C/CP exhibited superior single‐cell performance compared with previously reported noble‐metal‐based AEMWE systems (Table S9).

To further elucidate the mass‐transport characteristics, contact angle measurements were conducted to assess electrode surface hydrophilicity. Although IrO2 is inherently hydrophilic, the incorporation of polytetrafluoroethylene (PTFE) binder (commonly used in conventional electrode fabrication) significantly reduced such property, resulting in a hydrophobic surface with a large contact angle exceeding 100° (Figure 6c and Figure S22) [62]. In contrast, the binder‐free electrodes (IrO2‐Ni, IrO2‐Ni3S2, and IrO2‐Ni2P) preserved the intrinsic hydrophilicity of IrO2 by eliminating binder‐induced surface modifications, resulting in markedly lower contact angles, with the IrO2‐Ni2P electrode displaying the lowest one (21.4°) (Figure 6c and Table S10). This highlights the crucial role of both support selection and a binder‐free fabrication strategy to simultaneously optimize the physicochemical and catalytic properties.

To better understand the performance characteristics of each AEMWE single cell, the overall cell overpotential was subdivided into three distinct components: ohmic overpotential (η ohm ), kinetic overpotential (η kin ), and mass‐transfer overpotential (η mass ) (Figure 6d,e). Ohmic resistance (Rohm ) values were extracted from Nyquist plots and used to calculate η ohm according to the relationship η ohm = iRohm (Figure S23). η kin values were determined from Tafel plots of iRohm ‐corrected I‐V curves (Figure S24). Subsequently, η mass values were calculated by subtracting other components from the total cell voltage via the equation η mass = Ecell − Erev − η ohm − η kin . A notable difference between the two AEMWE systems was identified in the ohmic and mass‐transport overpotential regions, which contributed to the enhanced performance of the AEMWE system employing the IrO2‐Ni2P anode. The IrO2‐Ni2P electrode showed a significantly lower ohmic resistance (Rohm ) (0.05 Ω cm2) in the AEMWE system than that observed with the spray‐coated IrO2/CP anode (0.11 Ω cm2), resulting in a smaller ohmic overpotential over the entire current density range (Figure S25). In addition, the AEMWE with the IrO2‐Ni2P anode exhibited a lower η mass at high‐current densities, which could have originated from the hydrophilic nature of the binder‐free system facilitating rapid KOH transport to the catalytic sites and detachment of oxygen bubbles [63]. Although the IrO2‐Ni2P || Pt/C/CP configuration showed a somewhat higher η kin than IrO2/CP || Pt/C/CP, presumably due to the considerably higher Ir loading in the latter (3.0 mg cm−2), the substantial reduction in ohmic and mass‐transport overpotentials in the IrO2‐Ni2P anode enabled the AEMWE system to deliver a markedly higher overall performance, particularly at current densities >1 A cm−2 (Figure S26). Long‐term durability test further underscored this advantage. At 1 A cm−2, the IrO2/CP || Pt/C/CP system suffered severe performance degradation (34.1 mV h−1), potentially due to mechanical stress on the catalyst layers and subsequent delamination under vigorous gas evolution [64]. On the contrary, the IrO2‐Ni2P‐based AEMWE maintained stable operation with a much lower decay rate of 7.84 mV h−1, reflecting the mechanical robustness of the binder‐free IrO2‐Ni2P electrode. Taken together, these results highlight the remarkable activity and durability of IrO2‐Ni2P in both half‐cell and single‐cell configurations. The findings confirm that the rational support design combined with binder‐free catalyst growth offers an effective pathway to overcome the intrinsic limitations of conventional powder noble‐metal‐based catalysts and to realize a highly efficient practical system.

Conclusion

In this work, we demonstrated a polymeric binder‐free approach for constructing IrO2‐Ni‐based electrodes with ultralow Ir loading (<0.02 mgIr cm−2) that achieved both high catalytic activity and robust stability toward the OER. Among the investigated supports, Ni2P was identified as the most effective by providing strong interfacial coupling with IrO2 that optimized electron density, inducing a downshift of the Ir d‐band center, and moderating the binding strength of key reaction intermediates. DFT calculations corroborated these findings, revealing that the IrO2‐Ni2P configuration exhibited the lowest free‐energy barrier for OER and the most stable interfacial bonding environment. These favorable physicochemical interactions led to outstanding electrochemical performance. The IrO2‐Ni2P electrode delivered remarkable activity (232 mV @ 10 mA cm−2) in half‐cell OER test and achieved 3.1 A cm− 2 at 2.0 Vcell with stable operation under high‐current density conditions when integrated into an AEMWE. The binder‐free IrO2‐Ni2P system also demonstrated superior mass transport, reduced ohmic losses, and preserved hydrophilicity compared to a polymeric binder‐containing Ir‐based standard electrode, underscoring the crucial role of support selection and electrode architecture in overcoming the intrinsic limitations of the latter. Overall, this study not only establishes IrO2‐Ni2P as a promising anode for high‐performance AEMWEs but also presents a generalizable design principle for engineering durable and efficient Ir‐based OER catalysts. The integration of rational support engineering with binder‐free fabrication offers a viable pathway toward reducing noble‐metal usage while achieving the performance metrics required for practical large‐scale water electrolyzers.

Experimental Section

Chemicals and Materials

Ni foam (thickness: 0.08 mm) was purchased from MTI Korea. Oleylamine (OLAM; technical grade, 70%), 1‐octadecne (ODE; technical grade, 90%), and di‐tert‐butyl disulfide (TBDS, 97%), Nafion 117 solution (∼5% in a mixture of lower aliphatic alcohols and water) were obtained from Sigma‐Aldrich. Triphenyl phosphite (TPP; 97%), iridium (III) chloride hydrate (IrCl3· xH2O; 99.8%), and IrO2 (Premion, 99.99%) were purchased from Thermo Fisher Scientific. Sodium hydroxide (NaOH; powder, 97%) and hydrochloric acid (HCl; 35–37%) were purchased from Daejung Chemicals. Commercial Pt/C on carbon paper (CP) (Pt/C/CP; 20 wt.% Pt/C, Pt loading: 0.5 mg cm− 2) and IrO2 on CP (IrO2/CP; IrO2 loading: 3.0 mg cm− 2) were purchased from Fuel Cell Earth and Dioxide Materials, respectively. All chemicals were used as received without further purification.

Synthesis of Ni‐Based Foam

Before use, Ni foam (1 cm × 1.5 cm, 3 pieces) was soaked in 1 M HCl and sonicated for 15 min to eliminate the surface oxide layer. Afterward, the Ni foams were thoroughly rinsed with deionized (DI) water and ethanol at least 3 times each to ensure that the Ni foam surface was clean, followed by vacuum drying. To synthesize Ni3S2 foam, the pre‐treated Ni foam, 10 mL OLAM, 10 mL ODE, and an equivalent molar amount of TBDS relative to Ni were placed in a three‐neck flask. The solution was degassed under gentle stirring for 30 min to remove volatile impurities and subsequently purged with N2. The solution was then heated to 190°C and maintained at this temperature for 20 min to synthesize Ni3S2 on Ni foam. After naturally cooling the solution to room temperature, the Ni3S2 foam was collected from the flask and thoroughly rinsed several times with hexane and ethanol. The resulting Ni3S2 foam was vacuum‐dried and annealed at 450°C for 1 h under a 4% H2/Ar atmosphere to remove residual organic molecules. The process to fabricate the Ni2P foam was similar except that 2.7 mL TPP was used instead of TBDS, and the reaction conditions were adjusted to 300°C for 2 h.

IrO2 Deposition on the Ni, Ni3S2, and Ni2P Foams

For each of the foams, Ir precursor (corresponding to 0.1 mol% of Ni) was dissolved in 20 mL of 0.1 M NaOH, and the homogeneous solution was then heated to 80°C. Once the target temperature had stabilized, the as‐prepared Ni‐based foams were immersed in the solution and kept for 2 h. Afterward, the samples were removed from the solution, sufficiently washed with DI water, and dried under a vacuum. The detailed formation mechanism of IrO2 on Ni‐based foam is proposed in Supplementary Note 1.

For comparison, a commercial IrO2‐deposited Ni2P electrode (denoted as Com. IrO2‐Ni2P) was prepared by drop‐casting IrO2 catalyst ink onto a piece of Ni2P foam. Specifically, 5 mg of commercial IrO2 powder was dispersed in a solution containing 100 µL of Nafion, 490 µL of DI water, and 4.41 mL of ethanol (volume ratio = 20:480:500). The mixture was then sonicated for at least 1 h to ensure that the resulting solution was homogeneous. Afterward, the IrO2 ink was drop‐casted onto a 1 × 1 cm piece of Ni2P foam to achieve the same Ir loading amount as IrO2‐Ni2P (i.e., 0.016 mgIr cm−2). The resulting Com. IrO2‐Ni2P electrode was naturally dried for 12 h prior to use.

Material Characterization

XRD analysis was conducted using a Bruker AXS New D8 Advance diffractometer equipped with a Cu Kα radiation source and a LynxEye line detector. HRTEM, STEM, and EDS mapping were performed using a field‐emission transmission electron microscope (JEM–F200, JEOL, Tokyo, Japan) operating at an accelerating voltage of 200 kV. SEM and EDS analyses were carried out with a Carl Zeiss SIGMA 300 microscope (Carl Zeiss, Oberkochen, Germany). The Ir‐loading amount was determined via ICP‐MS (NexION 350D, PerkinElmer, USA). XPS was conducted using a K‐alpha+ system (Thermo Fisher Scientific) with an Al Kα radiation source to examine the surface chemical states of the nanoparticles. The XPS spectra were calibrated by referencing the C─C peak in the C 1s region to a binding energy of 284.8 eV. A contact‐angle‐measuring device (DSA100S, KRÜSS) was used to measure the water contact angles on the electrodes. Raman spectra were collected using a commercial micro‐Raman spectrometer (XperRam 200, Nanobase) equipped with a 532 nm laser (1 mW) and a 5× objective.

Electrochemical Characterization

All electrochemical measurements were conducted using a three‐electrode system, with the catalyst electrode as the working electrode (1 × 1 cm2), an Hg/HgO electrode as the reference electrode, and Pt wire as the counter electrode in a 1 M KOH solution as the electrolyte. The potential was converted to the reversible hydrogen electrode (RHE) scale using the relation E RHE = E Hg/HgO + 0.927 V in 1 M KOH (pH = 14).

Alkaline OER activity was evaluated by using linear sweep voltammetry (LSV) over the potential range of 0 to 0.9 V (vs. Hg/HgO) at a scan rate of 5 mV s−1. All reported OER polarization curves correspond to the reverse scan to ensure accurate evaluation of intrinsic catalytic activity. EIS was carried out at 0.58 V (vs. Hg/HgO) with an AC signal amplitude of 10 mV over a frequency range of 100 kHz to 0.1 Hz. Ohmic resistance (Rs) was obtained from Nyquist plots and used to compensate for ohmic losses in all of the polarization curves. To determine the Cdl , CV was conducted in the potential range of –0.1 to 0.1 V (vs. Hg/HgO) at various scan rates (40, 60, 80, 100, 120, 160, and 200 mV s−1). ADT was performed using 2,000 cycles of CV within the potential range of 0 to 0.9 V (vs. Hg/HgO) at a scan rate of 100 mV s−1. Chronopotentiometry measurements were carried out under both sequentially varying current densities (20, 50, 100, 50, and 20 mA cm−2) and a constant current density of 10 mA cm−2.

An AEMWE single cell was constructed by integrating MEA, bipolar plates, current distributors, and end plates. The MEA was designed by sandwiching the anion exchange membrane (AEM (PiperION, 20 µm) between the cathode (commercial Pt/C/CP) and anode (IrO2‐Ni2P or IrO2/CP): IrO2/CP was used as a commercial reference anode. To ensure proper sealing and spacing, a 150 µm‐thick gasket was used on the cathode side, while 160 and 245 µm‐thick gaskets were employed for the IrO2‐Ni2P and IrO2/CP anode, respectively. The single cell was operated at 80°C with the 1.0 M KOH solution preheated to 80°C and supplied to the anode and cathode at flow rates of 20‐ and 2‐mL min−1, respectively. Current‐voltage (I‐V) curves were obtained in the voltage range of 1.0–2.0 Vcell, and Chronopotentiometry test was conducted at a current density of 1 A cm−2 for 12 h to evaluate the stability. EIS analysis was carried out across a current density range of 0.05–2 A cm−2 to determine the Rohm of the AEMWE single cell.

Computational Details

All DFT calculations in this study were conducted using the Vienna Ab initio Simulation Package (VASP) [65, 66, 67, 68]. The Perdew–Burke–Ernzerhof (PBE) generalized gradient approximation (GGA) was applied for the exchange–correlation functional, and the projector augmented wave (PAW) method was employed to model core electrons [65, 69, 70, 71]. In consideration of both convergence and computational efficiency, the plane‐wave energy cutoff was set to 400 eV. Electronic structure optimization was performed until the total energy change in the self‐consistent field (SCF) cycle was less than 10−5 eV, while structural optimization proceeded until the residual force was below 0.05 eV/Å.

Slab models of Ni2P (3 × 3 × 1), Ni3S2 (3 × 3 × 1), Ni (4 × 4 × 1), and IrO2 (1 × 1 × 1) were constructed, with the in‐plane cell size adjusted to ensure the surface areas of the supports were as similar as possible. A vacuum layer of 30 Å was applied to all models, and IrO2 was positioned on the Ni, Ni2P, and Ni3S2 supports in an island configuration. The Brillouin zone was sampled using a 1 × 1 × 1 Monkhorst–Pack k‐point grid to minimize interactions between periodic images.
