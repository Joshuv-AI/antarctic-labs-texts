Machine learning prediction of mortality in pediatric fungemia using the Candida score
Authors: Khouloud Abdulrahman Al-Sofyani, Ibrahim Hussain Ali Muzaffar, Abdulrahman Mohammedsaeed Baqasi, Saleh Al Fulayyih, Mohammed Shahab Uddin
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Pediatric fungemia in pediatric intensive care units (PICUs) carries high mortality. We evaluated whether the Candida Score, combined with clinical variables, predicts mortality after diagnosis using a prespecified multivariable logistic regression (primary model) and benchmarked discrimination against Random Forest and Gradient Boosting Machine. We analyzed 85 pediatric fungemia cases from a PICU (2016–2020). The prespecified primary model was multivariable logistic regression with predefined covariates; Random Forest and Gradient Boosting Machine were exploratory comparators. Discrimination was evaluated on a held-out test set and by 10-fold cross-validation and bootstrapping. In 85 cases, the median age was 6 months and median weight 4.8 kg; 62.4% were male. Candida albicans was the most prevalent species (37.6%). Of the subjects, 39 (45.9%) died and 46 (54.1%) survived. On the held-out test set (n = 17), logistic regression achieved accuracy 0.735 and AUC 0.800. Random Forest achieved AUC 0.861 (precision 1.000; recall 0.778), and Gradient Boosting achieved AUC 0.847 (precision 0.875; recall 0.778). Internal validation (10-fold cross-validation and bootstrap resampling) supported model stability.Conclusion Integrating the Candida Score with clinical predictors shows potential for mortality risk stratification after fungemia diagnosis. In this single-center cohort, Random Forest yielded the highest discrimination on the test set. Findings are exploratory and require external validation in larger, multicenter studies before clinical use.

Introduction

Candida sepsis presents a significant challenge in pediatric intensive care units (PICUs), associated with high mortality, prolonged hospitalizations, and increased healthcare costs1,2. Candida bloodstream infections (CBSIs) are the most common type of invasive fungal infection in children and represent approximately 10–15% of all healthcare-associated bloodstream infections in the PICU3. Research indicates that CBSIs significantly escalate mortality rates to 20–49% and contribute to approximately 15% of all PICU fatalities4. A striking 207% rise in fungal sepsis cases from 1979 to 2000 highlights the escalating threat5, with recent studies confirming a continued increase in such infections6,7. Piqueras et al. reported an incidence rate of 1.97 per 1,000 patient-days, contrasting with Hsu et al.’s findings of 0.21 to 10.5 occurrences per thousand admissions8,9.

In pediatric intensive care units (PICUs), the risk of developing candidiasis increases with the length of stay, with infections often manifesting after the first week of admission. Key risk factors well-documented in the pediatric population include immunosuppression, steroid use, dialysis, total parenteral nutrition (TPN), central venous catheterization, recent major surgery, and broad-spectrum antibiotic usage4,10. While also relevant in adults, factors such as prolonged neutropenia, prematurity, urinary catheter use, malnutrition, and burns are particularly significant contributors to risk in critically ill children11–13.

Despite advances in infection management, blood cultures remain the standard for diagnosing fungemia, but their 24–48‑hour turnaround and non‑specific clinical symptoms in children can still delay therapy. While timely diagnosis is important, there is an equally pressing need for risk models that help stratify mortality once fungemia is confirmed8,9. Prognostic tools designed for the post‑diagnosis phase would support targeted interventions and resource allocation in critically ill children, complementing existing diagnostic practices rather than aiming to detect infection earlier14,15. By focusing on mortality risk after diagnosis, such tools can deepen understanding of disease progression and guide care in this vulnerable population16.

A pertinent question is why a new model is needed, given the widespread use of general severity-of-illness scores like the Pediatric Risk of Mortality (PRISM) score. However, such scores are calculated upon admission and are designed to predict overall ICU mortality, proving less effective for forecasting outcomes from complications like fungemia, which often develop later during the ICU stay. In fact, our analysis confirms the findings of others that the initial PRISM score is not significantly correlated with subsequent mortality from fungemia. This highlights the critical need for dynamic, condition-specific prognostic tools tailored to challenges that arise during a patient’s ICU treatment.

The potential of machine learning in healthcare is immense, yet the integration of such technologies into clinical practice is challenged by the need for high-quality, diverse datasets and adherence to ethical considerations like patient privacy and data security17.The Candida Score is a validated bedside tool that estimates the risk of invasive candidiasis in critically ill—particularly ICU—patients18,19 and guides when to initiate empiric antifungal therapy in high-risk individuals without confirmed infection20.

While the Candida Score was developed to predict the risk of acquiring invasive candidiasis, its utility as a prognostic tool for predicting mortality after an infection is established remains unexplored, particularly in children. Given that the physiological and immunological differences between children and adults are significant3,18,21, there is a need for prognostic tools tailored specifically for the pediatric population. Our study, therefore, aims to investigate whether the Candida Score, when combined with other clinical risk factors in a pediatric-specific model, can accurately predict fungemia-related mortality in children already diagnosed with the condition. By evaluating its role in prognostication, we address a different clinical question than its original purpose. By improving mortality risk prediction and addressing a substantial need in pediatric intensive care, this research could transform patient outcomes through improved risk stratification and more targeted resource utilization.

Methods

Study design and setting

We conducted a retrospective study using electronic health records to evaluate predictors of mortality in pediatric patients with fungemia at the pediatric intensive care units (PICUs) of King Abdulaziz University Hospital, Jeddah, Saudi Arabia. The analysis encompassed data from 2016 to 2020. The PICUs manage ~ 500 admissions annually, providing a robust dataset for evaluating the influence of the Candida Score and other clinical risk factors on outcomes. As a tertiary referral center, the hospital manages a high volume of medically complex pediatric patients from across the region.

Ethical considerations

This study adhered to the ethical standards outlined in the Declaration of Helsinki (1964) and its subsequent amendments. Ethical approval was obtained from the Institutional Review Board (IRB) of King Abdulaziz University Hospital, Jeddah, Saudi Arabia (Reference No. 566 − 23). Owing to the retrospective nature of the analysis, the IRB waived the requirement for informed consent. Data anonymization and security measures were implemented to ensure rigorous protection of patient confidentiality and anonymity. Patient identifiers were removed from the dataset prior to the analysis and replaced with unique anonymization codes. Electronic data were encrypted and stored on secure, password-protected servers that were accessible only to authorized research personnel. Physical copies of data were kept in locked cabinets in secure facilities, adhering to the highest standards of privacy and ethical integrity and minimizing any risk of data breaches while maintaining the utility of the data for research purposes.

Study population

The study cohort comprised of pediatric patients (age 1 month–14 years) admitted to the PICU with a blood culture positive for a Candida species. Patients with incomplete medical records—defined as records missing any of: mortality at PICU discharge; Candida Score components (total parenteral nutrition, recent surgery, multifocal colonization, severe sepsis); duration of mechanical ventilation; PRISM score; length of PICU stay; or the dates required to derive durations (antibiotics and hospital stay prior to fungemia)—were excluded. Neonates (< 1 month) were not included because our institution has a separate Neonatal Intensive Care Unit (NICU). Additional exclusions were: patients who developed fungemia after transferring out of the PICU; those who received antifungal treatment before PICU admission; PICU stay < 24 h; and co-existing conditions where mortality was deemed imminent and independent of fungemia (e.g., terminal malignancy under a palliative plan, end-stage organ failure not amenable to transplant).

Key variables

Demographic variables (age, sex, weight), clinical scores (PRISM), and clinical measurements, including duration of mechanical ventilation (DOMV); length of PICU stay (LOPICU); duration of antibiotics before fungal infection (DOAbBFI); length of hospital stay before fungal infection (LHSBFI); Candida Score (CScore); and presence of Candida albicans (Calbicans).

Diagnosis of fungemia

Blood cultures were processed using the BacT/ALERT automated system (bioMérieux, France). For each sample, 5 mL of blood were inoculated into a pediatric culture bottle, incubated continuously, and flagged positives were subcultured onto Sabouraud dextrose agar for identification. Yeast species identification was performed by VITEK MS, with antifungal susceptibility by VITEK®2 (bioMérieux, France). If no growth was detected after five days, cultures were finalized as negative.

Candida score

We computed the 4-item Candida Score (total parenteral nutrition = 1, recent surgery = 1, multifocal Candida colonization = 1, severe sepsis = 2); scores ≥ 3 indicated high risk. In this study, the Candida Score was evaluated as a predictor of mortality after fungemia diagnosis, not as a diagnostic tool.

Outcome measures

The primary outcome was mortality at PICU discharge.

Statistical analysis

Statistical programs and data handling

Analyses were performed in Python 3.8. Logistic regression was fitted using statsmodels 0.13.5. Random Forest and Gradient Boosting Machine were implemented with scikit-learn 1.0.2. Data wrangling and computation used pandas 1.5.3 and numpy 1.23.5.

Overview of modeling and validation

Our primary analysis used multivariable logistic regression (LR) with pre-specified covariates. Random Forest22 and Gradient Boosting Machine23were secondary, exploratory models to benchmark discrimination. For performance reporting, we used: (a) an 80/20 train–test split for held-out test-set metrics, (b) 10-fold cross-validation on the full dataset for internal stability, and (c) bootstrap (1,000 resamples) and permutation tests as additional internal validation/negative-control procedures.

Descriptive statistics

Demographic and clinical features of the pediatric patients were summarized using descriptive statistics. Continuous variables were presented as medians with interquartile ranges or means with standard deviations based on their distribution. Categorical variables were described using percentages and frequencies to clarify their distribution within the study population.

Data processing for model development

Data were complete (no imputation). Outliers were capped at 1.5× IQR. Min–max scaling normalized continuous features to [0,1]. The categorical variable sex was one-hot encoded (binary indicator) for inclusion in regression. (Dataset allocation is described in the Overview of modeling and validation and not repeated here.)

Multivariable logistic regression analysis

Logistic regression estimated associations with mortality. For each predictor, we report odds ratios (OR) and 95% confidence intervals (CI); p < 0.05 denoted statistical significance. Pre-specified covariates were CScore, DOMV, LOPICU, age, weight, PRISM, DOAbBFI, LHSBFI, sex, and Calbicans. All covariates were entered simultaneously to assess independent associations while avoiding biases of automated stepwise selection.

Machine learning model development

In addition to logistic regression, we developed two ensemble learning models: Random Forest (RF) and Gradient Boosting Machine (GBM). For the RF model, we used 100 decision trees (n_estimators = 100) and the Gini impurity criterion to measure split quality. For the GBM model, a learning rate of 0.1 and 100 boosting stages were used. The models were implemented in Python using the scikit-learn library (version 1.0.2). Default parameters were used for other hyperparameters, as extensive tuning is often not feasible or reliable with small datasets. The goal was to compare the performance of these standard, well-established models against logistic regression.

Model validation

We assessed internal validity using three complementary procedures applied to the full analytic dataset unless otherwise noted. Ten-fold cross-validation partitioned the data into 10 folds; models were trained on nine folds and evaluated on the held-out fold, iterating across all folds, with performance summarized as mean and standard deviation of fold-level metrics. Bootstrap resampling (1,000 resamples) drew samples of size n with replacement; models were fit within each resample and performance was summarized as mean (SD) across resamples. Permutation testing (1,000 permutations) randomly permuted outcome labels and retrained the model for each permutation to generate a null distribution of performance, providing a negative-control benchmark.

Model performance metrics

For binary mortality prediction, we report accuracy, precision, recall (sensitivity), F1 score, and area under the receiver operating characteristic curve (ROC AUC). Unless otherwise stated, a probability threshold of 0.50 was used to derive class labels and the confusion matrix. For resampling procedures (cross-validation, bootstrap, permutation), performance is summarized as mean (SD) across folds/resamples/permutations.

Comparative model analysis

Comparisons between multivariable logistic regression (primary), Random Forest, and Gradient Boosting Machine (exploratory) were conducted using the same metric set defined above. Hyperparameters followed standard defaults: RF with 100 trees (Gini criterion); GBM with learning rate 0.1 and 100 boosting stages.

Feature importance analysis

Feature importance was analyzed for each model to identify the most influential predictors of mortality. For the logistic regression model, feature importance was determined by the absolute value of the standardized regression coefficients. For the Random Forest and Gradient Boosting Machine models, we used the standard Mean Decrease in Impurity (MDI) method. MDI calculates the importance of a feature by measuring the total reduction in the Gini impurity criterion brought by that feature, averaged over all trees in the ensemble. Features that contribute to the largest impurity decrease are considered most important. All calculations were performed using the built-in functions of the scikit-learn library in Python.

Results

Baseline characteristics of the study population (Figure-1)

The median age was 6.0 months (IQR 2.0–12.0) and the median weight was 4.8 kg (IQR 3.43–8.0 kg). The PRISM score had a median of 10.0 (IQR 5.8–14.0). The duration of mechanical ventilation (DOMV) was 16.0 days (IQR 6.0–28.0 days), and the length of PICU stay (LOPICU) was 27.0 days (IQR 14.0–38.0 days). The duration of antibiotics before fungal infection (DOAbBFI) was 14.0 days (IQR 10.0–20.0 days). The Candida Score (CScore) was 2.0 (IQR 1.0–4.0), and the length of hospital stay before fungal infection (LHSBFI) was 25.0 days (IQR 15.0–52.0). There were 53 males (62.4%) and 32 females (37.6%). Forty‑six patients (54.1%) survived, and 39 (45.9%) died. Overall, non‑albicans Candida accounted for 53/85 (62.4%) and Candida albicans for 32/85 (37.6%). The species distribution was C. albicans 32 (37.6%), C. tropicalis 22 (25.9%), C. parapsilosis 16 (18.8%), C. glabrata 5 (5.9%), C. lusitaniae 3 (3.5%), C. guilliermondii 2 (2.4%), C. famata 2 (2.4%), C. krusei.

Data processing and model development

Of 85 eligible patients, 68 were assigned to the training set and 17 to the test set. Baseline characteristics were comparable between sets, with no statistically significant differences across assessed variables (all p > 0.05). Median age was similar (training 6.0 months [IQR 2.0–13.5] vs. test 5.0 months [IQR 2.0–10.0]; p = 0.99), as were weight, PRISM score, duration of mechanical ventilation (DOMV), and length of PICU stay (LOPICU) (all p ≥ 0.40). The length of hospital stay before fungal infection (LHSBFI) was slightly longer in the training set (median 25.0 days) than the test set (22.0 days), but this did not reach significance (p = 0.06). Gender distribution and presence of Candida albicans also did not differ (gender p = 0.26; C. albicans p = 0.60). Mortality was similar between the training and test sets (34/68 [50.0%] vs. 5/17 [29.4%]; p = 0.18). These findings support the comparability of the two datasets used for model development and evaluation (Table 1).

Multivariable logistic regression analysis (Fig. 2). In the multivariable model, the Candida Score (CScore) was significantly associated with mortality (OR 2.18, 95% CI 1.383–3.45; p = 0.001). The duration of mechanical ventilation (DOMV) was also associated with mortality (OR 1.10, 95% CI 1.02–1.18; p = 0.010). Length of PICU stay (LOPICU) showed an inverse trend but was not statistically significant (OR 0.95, 95% CI 0.90–1.01; p = 0.090). Other variables, including age, weight, PRISM score, duration of antibiotics before bloodstream fungal infection (DOAbBFI), length of hospital stay before bloodstream fungal infection (LHSBFI), sex, and the presence of Candida albicans, were not statistically significant predictors of mortality in this model.

Discussion

In our single-center study of 85 pediatric patients with fungemia, a multivariable logistic regression model identified two key independent predictors of mortality. Candida Score was associated with mortality (OR 2.185; p = 0.0008), and duration of mechanical ventilation was also associated (OR 1.10; p = 0.010). This finding is consistent with prior research identifying mechanical ventilation as a marker for severe illness and an independent predictor of death in patients with fungemia24–26. Conversely, factors like age and weight were not significantly associated with outcomes, aligning with previous studies3,5,27–29. Exploratory machine learning models, including Random Forest and Gradient Boosting Machine, showed similar performance and feature importance on a held-out test set.

A key finding of our research is the novel application of the Candida Score. Originally developed by León et al. to predict the risk of acquiring invasive candidiasis and guide pre-emptive antifungal therapy in critically ill, non-neutropenic adult ICU patients21,30, its use was later extended to other non-neutropenic patients as well31. Our study repositions the score from a tool for infection-risk to a prognostic instrument for predicting mortality after fungemia has already been diagnosed in a pediatric population. This utility is plausible since its components—parenteral nutrition, recent surgery, multifocal colonization, and severe sepsis—reflect illness complexity relevant to post-diagnosis outcomes20,32,33. Our results align with a recent study by Juneja et al., which also found a high Candida Score predicted mortality in adults with confirmed candidemia, reinforcing its potential utility in risk stratification post-diagnosis34.

A high Candida Score reflects the presence of severe sepsis and other systemic factors, highlighting how illness complexity influences mortality risk after fungemia is diagnosed35,36. Similarly, longer durations of mechanical ventilation generally mark prolonged organ dysfunction rather than acting as independent causal drivers of mortality. Standard care bundles for hemodynamic support and ventilator management are already used in PICUs to address these underlying issues37,38. Prospective studies should assess whether incorporating risk scores like the CScore into clinical workflows alters management decisions or improves outcomes, recognising that mortality is influenced by the trajectory of the patient’s overall organ dysfunction rather than the infection alone39,40.

Across all three approaches, CScore and DOMV ranked among the most influential variables. In logistic regression, importance reflects the absolute standardized coefficients, whereas in Random Forest and Gradient Boosting Machine it reflects mean decrease in impurity; these scales are not directly comparable. Nonetheless, the convergence on CScore and DOMV supports their relevance for risk stratification after fungemia confirmation. Other covariates (e.g., PRISM, LOPICU, age) contributed variably across models, consistent with differing modeling assumptions.

On the held-out test set, ensemble models (Random Forest, Gradient Boosting Machine) showed numerically higher discrimination than logistic regression, while logistic regression provided greater interpretability. Given the modest sample size and single-center setting, these differences should be interpreted cautiously; external validation with larger, multicenter datasets is needed to assess reproducibility and clinical relevance. Internal resampling (10-fold cross-validation, bootstrap) produced consistent estimates, and permutation results were near chance, supporting that observed discrimination exceeds random performance.

Clinical implications

The models developed in this study are intended for risk stratification after fungemia is diagnosed—not to guide antifungal initiation. In practice, identifying patients at higher risk of mortality could prompt more intensive monitoring, earlier consultation with specialists, or more informed prognostic discussions with families. However, given the exploratory nature of our analysis, this potential utility requires prospective evaluation and should not be assumed.

Strengths and limitations

Key strengths include a prespecified primary model with clinically grounded covariates, transparent reporting of train/test and resampling results, and comparison with established ensemble methods. Limitations include the small sample size, single‑center design, and retrospective nature of the study, which restrict generalizability. Our cohort’s crude mortality rate was 39 deaths (45.9%)—54.1% of patients survived. This rate may differ from those reported by other institutions and should be considered contextual rather than a primary finding. We did not perform extensive hyperparameter tuning to avoid overfitting in a small dataset; larger multicenter studies are needed to refine calibration, threshold selection, and decision‑analytic evaluation.

Future directions

Building on this exploratory study, the most critical next step is external validation of our findings in a larger, multicenter pediatric cohort. This is essential to confirm the generalizability of the model across different clinical settings and patient populations. Future research should also prospectively evaluate the clinical impact of implementing such a risk prediction tool. Specifically, studies could assess whether real-time risk scores influence clinical management (e.g., frequency of monitoring, use of adjunctive therapies) and subsequently affect patient outcomes. Finally, incorporating novel biomarkers of host immune response or fungal virulence into future models may further enhance their predictive accuracy.

Conclusions

The Candida Score and duration of mechanical ventilation were consistently associated with mortality after fungemia in this exploratory single‑center study. Ensemble models offered modest gains in discrimination, but logistic regression remained interpretable and clinically meaningful. These results warrant validation in larger, multicenter cohorts before clinical implementation.
