Comparative Analysis of the Accuracy of Large Language Models in Addressing Common Pulmonary Embolism Patient Questions
Authors: Akiva Rosenzveig, Joseph Kassab, Lidiya Sul, Dana Angelini, Pulkit Chaudhury, Ashish Sarraju, Leben Tefera
Published: 2024

Large language models (LLMs) have gained considerable attention for the potential to make medical knowledge more accessible to patients, particularly for answering patient questions. 1 Pulmonary embolism (PE) is the third leading cause of cardiovascular death in the United States. 2 It is not well known if LLMs can answer questions related to PE, a prevalent and morbid cardiovascular condition. Our study sought to evaluate how 2 widely used LLM chatbots, ChatGPT‐4.0 and Google Gemini, respond to common patient questions revolving around PE care.

To develop questions reflective of patient concerns, we developed 15 patient‐centered questions using Google Trends to identify the most frequently searched terms associated with PE from 2019 to 2024, noted as “related injuries” in Google Trends. These terms were then used to develop a list of questions. Examples of such terms included “treatment for pulmonary embolism,” “what's a pulmonary embolism,” and “pulmonary embolism cause.” The questions were developed by internal medicine residents and reviewed by board‐certified physicians. For example, 1 of the frequently queried terms was “side effects of blood thinners.” Thus, we formulated a related question, “what are the side effects of blood thinners?” These questions were then submitted to the chatbots. In early February of 2024, ChatGPT‐4.0 and Google Gemini were evaluated because of their prevalent use among the general public. We presented the responses to 3 experienced staff physicians at a tertiary care center (Cleveland Clinic, Cleveland, OH). We included a vascular medicine physician, a cardiologist, and a hematologist, all well versed in the treatment of PE and members of an institutional PE response team. Institutional review board approval was waived.

The blinded physicians individually reviewed and graded each question response as accurate or inaccurate. Responses were considered accurate if deemed to be complete without any missing information and aligned with current clinical guidelines. Responses were considered inaccurate if they were partially or completely incorrect according to the physicians' clinical judgment. For example, as reflected in the Figure, although clot burden and location may play a role in the decision of which advanced therapies are appropriate, clinical guidelines do not take clot burden into consideration when risk stratifying or determining the severity of a PE. After grading each individual response, the physicians chose the more appropriate response provided by the chatbots based on individual clinical judgment. The agreement level among reviewers was determined using the free‐marginal Fleiss κ coefficient. In cases of physician disagreement, grades were modified by the first author after further discussions among the assessors to ensure appropriate alignment with current PE guidelines.

Following discussion and resolution of discrepancies, 93.3% (95% CI, 0.68–1.0) (14/15) of ChatGPT 4.0–generated answers were deemed accurate by the physicians. The free‐marginal Fleiss κ agreement coefficient was 0.64 (95% CI, 0.34–0.95), indicating good agreement (82.2% overall agreement). For Gemini, 67% (95% CI, 0.38–0.88) (10/15) of answers were considered accurate, with the remaining 33% (95% CI, 0.12–0.62) (5/15) viewed as inaccurate. The κ coefficient for the generated answers was 0.82 (95% CI, 0.58–1.00) (91.1% overall agreement), indicating very good agreement. When comparing both models and after discussion and discrepancy resolution, ChatGPT 4.0 responses were deemed more appropriate for 73% (95% CI, 0.45–0.92) (11/15) of the questions. The data that support the findings of this study are available from the corresponding author on reasonable request.

This study demonstrates high accuracy of 2 commonly used chat‐based LLMs for addressing common patient questions revolving around PE as adjudicated by clinician experts. ChatGPT 4.0 outperformed Google Gemini. Burnout among physicians has been well documented and an increasing concern. 3 Physician burnout has been associated with increased adoption of electronic health records and, specifically, increasing in‐basket messages are associated with a higher probability of burnout as well as a higher probability of desire to reduce clinical work time. 4 LLMs that may reduce physician workflow burdens in relation to patient messages may have a positive impact on physician burnout risk.

This study has certain limitations. First, we used the current versions of ChatGPT 4.0 and Google Gemini, which may evolve over time. Second, we acknowledge that although ChatGPT performed better than Gemini in the instances we evaluated (11 of 15), the CI's overlap with 50% highlights uncertainty about the generalizability of these results. Third, the rating system and physician‐rated response appropriateness used may be subjective and individual clinician driven. Third, responses were evaluated by clinicians but not patients, thereby limiting the preference of patients. Although promising, the application of LLMs in clinical settings requires further investigation. LLMs need to undergo more detailed safety and efficacy evaluations before real‐world implementation to answer patient questions.
