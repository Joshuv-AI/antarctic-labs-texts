Multidisciplinary Team Nursing Care During Peri-Transplantation for ALK-Negative Anaplastic Large Cell Lymphoma: A Case Report
Authors: Xiaoying Hong, Genqun Wang, Li Cai, Yuanjiao Lin, Yi Zhao
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractThis case report describes the nursing experience of a patient with ALK‑negative anaplastic large cell lymphoma undergoing autologous hematopoietic stem cell transplantation. A multidisciplinary team model was established, integrating professionals from haematology, rehabilitation, nutrition, psychology to deliver comprehensive peri‑transplant care. An individualized treatment and nursing plan was developed for the peri-transplant period. This plan included pre-transplant physical and psychological preparation, environmental readiness, and management of complications during transplantation, such as infection, bleeding, gastrointestinal symptoms, malnutrition, and depressive mood. Over a 26-day period, successful hematopoietic reconstitution was achieved, and the patient was discharged smoothly from the isolation unit. Post-transplantation, continuous nursing support was provided in infection control, diet, and exercise, along with regular follow-up for two months. The patient’s laboratory parameters returned to normal, and both physical and psychological conditions recovered well.Keywords: multidisciplinary team, ALK negative anaplastic large cell lymphoma, autologous hematopoietic stem cell transplantation, nursing, case report

Introduction

Anaplastic large cell lymphoma (ALCL) is a rare and highly aggressive subtype of Peripheral T-cell lymphomas (PTCLs) that predominantly affects males and accounts for approximately 2–3% of adult non-Hodgkin lymphoma cases.1,2 Based on the expression of anaplastic lymphoma kinase (ALK), ALCL is classified into ALK-positive and ALK-negative subtypes.3 ALK-negative ALCL is more commonly observed in middle-aged and elderly populations, with an incidence of 5.5% in Western countries and 4.51% in China.2,4 Most patients present at an advanced stage at diagnosis (approximately 65% at stage III–IV), and the prognosis is generally poor, with a 5-year overall survival (OS) rate of 40–60%.5–7 Clinically, patients with ALK-negative ALCL often present with systemic symptoms such as fever, weight loss, and night sweats. Extranodal involvement is common, with 54.2% of patients exhibiting two or more extranodal lesions, most frequently affecting the skin and soft tissues, bone, liver, and lungs.3,8,9 Due to its refractory, relapsing, and high-risk nature, conventional chemotherapy alone often yields suboptimal outcomes. Therefore, both domestic and international guidelines recommend autologous hematopoietic stem cell transplantation (ASCT) as a first-line consolidation therapy.10,11 ASCT refers to the process in which patients receive high-dose chemotherapy followed by reinfusion of previously collected hematopoietic stem cells to achieve hematopoietic reconstitution.12 Studies have shown that patients who undergo ASCT consolidation after achieving complete remission with induction chemotherapy can achieve improved survival outcomes, with 5-year progression-free survival (PFS) and overall survival (OS) rates reaching 61% and 70%, respectively.13 However, during the transplantation process, patients are highly susceptible to complications due to profound immunosuppression, increased nutritional demands, and significant psychological stress. Common issues include infection, malnutrition, and psychological disorders, all of which may adversely affect transplantation outcomes. Among these, infection and bleeding remain the leading causes of mortality in transplant recipients.14,15

In this study, we report the peri-transplant nursing management of a patient with ALK-negative ALCL. Given the rarity, high aggressiveness, and substantial physical and psychological burden of this disease, and to address the complex challenges of multi-system complications, nutritional imbalance, and psychological distress during transplantation, our team established an integrated medical–nursing–technical multidisciplinary modelinvolving hematology, rehabilitation, nutrition, psychology, and stomatology. This model enabled individualized, whole-process management throughout the peri-transplant period, resulting in favorable patient recovery.

Case Description

A 21-year-old male patient was admitted on August 6, 2025, for autologous hematopoietic stem cell transplantation (ASCT) following a diagnosis of ALK-negative anaplastic large cell lymphoma for over 8 months. The patient initially presented on November 23, 2024, with right hip pain and was subsequently diagnosed with a pathological fracture of the right femoral neck. On November 27, 2024, he underwent proximal femoral tumor resection and joint replacement. Postoperative pathological examination confirmed ALK-negative anaplastic large cell lymphoma, stage IVA. From December 2024 to July 2025, the patient received eight cycles of chemotherapy, including CHOP, brentuximab vedotin plus CHP (BV+CHP), CHP, and BV+CHP regimens, along with four intrathecal injections. Treatment response was evaluated as complete remission. The patient had no prior history of hypertension, coronary heart disease, or diabetes mellitus. He denied any history of hepatitis, tuberculosis, trauma, blood transfusion, or drug allergies. He was unmarried and had no children. His family history was unremarkable, with both parents and siblings in good health.

Pre-Transplantation Preparation Period (August 6–12, 2025)

Within one week before transplantation, the patient underwent a comprehensive pre-transplant evaluation in accordance with institutional protocols. The assessment included laboratory testing of blood, urine, and stool samples; evaluation of cardiopulmonary and gastrointestinal function; and screening for infectious diseases. Multidisciplinary consultations were completed with specialists from the departments of otolaryngology, ophthalmology, proctology, nutrition, psychiatry, stomatology, joint surgery, and orthopedic oncology. Following completion of these assessments, the transplantation team convened a pre-transplant multidisciplinary meeting to review the patient’s clinical status and to formulate an individualized transplantation treatment and nursing care plan. This process was conducted to ensure that the patient was physically and psychologically prepared for transplantation. The results of the pre-transplant health assessment were as follows.

Disease-Related Indicators

White blood cell count was 2.76×109/L, neutrophil count was 1.73×109/L, hemoglobin level was 97 g/L, and platelet count was 210 × 109/L. T-cell subsets showed an absolute T lymphocyte count of 498.69/μL, B lymphocytes of 15.37/μL, and NK lymphocytes of 43.85/μL. Whole-body positron emission tomography–computed tomography (PET/CT) demonstrated complete response/complete metabolic response (CR/CMR) following surgery and chemotherapy for right femoral lymphoma. Compared with the previous PET/CT examination (March 11, 2025):(a) postoperative changes after left hip arthroplasty with mild surrounding inflammatory changes were observed, with decreased metabolic activity compared to before; no evidence of hypermetabolic tumor recurrence was found in the surgical area;

(b) previously noted bone destruction in the left acetabulum and the third thoracic vertebra (T3) showed no increased metabolic activity (Deauville score: 1), suggesting suppressed tumor activity after treatment, with evidence of an old compression fracture of T3. Overall therapeutic response was evaluated as complete response (CR).

General Condition and Functional Assessment

Body temperature was 36.5°C, pulse rate was 75 beats/min, respiratory rate was 19 breaths/min, and blood pressure was 108/65 mmHg. The patient was 165 cm in height and weighed 55 kg, with a body mass index (BMI) of 20.20 kg/m2. Physical examination revealed a pale appearance consistent with anemia. A curved surgical scar of approximately 50 cm was observed on the lateral aspect of the right thigh. The left ear was obstructed by secretions, and one dental caries was noted on the left side of the oral cavity. Muscle strength of the limbs was graded as level IV, with limited mobility of the right lower limb; the patient required crutches for ambulation. Emotional instability was noted, and the patient was relatively withdrawn with limited verbal communication. Functional assessment showed a Barthel Index score of 70 (mild dependence), a Morse Fall Scale score of 75 (high risk), a high risk of bleeding, and a Nutritional Risk Screening 2002 (NRS-2002) score of 3 (indicating nutritional risk). The Padua Prediction Score for venous thromboembolism was 0 (low risk).

After discussion by the multidisciplinary transplantation team, the patient was confirmed to meet the indications for autologous hematopoietic stem cell transplantation. The patient was transferred to the transplantation unit on August 13, 2025, and subsequently underwent the conditioning phase, bone marrow suppression phase, and hematopoietic reconstitution phase.

Conditioning Phase (August 13–19, 2025)

After entering the transplantation unit on August 13, the patient received high-dose chemotherapy with the BV+TEAM regimen (brentuximab vedotin, thiotepa, etoposide, and melphalan). During this period, protective isolation nursing and symptomatic supportive care were provided, including prophylactic anti-infective therapy, continuous electrocardiographic monitoring, antiemetic treatment, gastric and hepatic protection, hydration, urine alkalinization, appropriate diuresis, oral cryotherapy, monitoring of body weight and abdominal circumference, and recording of 24-hour fluid intake and output. On August 16, the patient developed oral mucositis, which resolved after treatment with nystatin mouthwash and local ultraviolet therapy.

Stem Cell Reinfusion and Bone Marrow Suppression Phase (August 20 to September 15, 2025)

On August 20, the patient successfully received reinfusion of 342.5 mL of autologous hematopoietic stem cells, with a cell viability of 84.14%. Following reinfusion, the patient entered a phase of profound bone marrow suppression. From August 22, the patient developed loose to watery stools, with a volume of approximately 100–300 mL per episode, occurring 1–3 times per day. Repeated stool cultures showed no abnormalities. On August 23, laboratory results showed a white blood cell count of 0.01 × 109/L, neutrophil count of 0.01 × 109/L, hemoglobin level of 94 g/L, and platelet count of 34 × 109/L. C-reactive protein (CRP) was 142 mg/L, and procalcitonin (PCT) was 2.27 μg/L. The patient’s body temperature fluctuated between 36.5°C and 38.8°C. Blood cultures subsequently identified Escherichia coli and Enterococcus faecium. Enoxaparin was discontinued, and treatment was initiated with thrombopoietin (TPO) to increase platelet counts and prevent bleeding. Anti-infective therapy included meropenem combined with vancomycin and voriconazole. Additional supportive treatments included blood transfusion and parenteral nutritional support. On September 6, the patient developed significant depressive symptoms, including marked emotional distress, aggressive speech, and self-harm tendencies. A psychiatric consultation resulted in a diagnosis of depressive state. Interventions included intensified psychological support, 24-hour close observation, and oral administration of escitalopram oxalate, after which the patient’s emotional status gradually stabilized.

Hematopoietic Reconstitution Phase (September 15 to Discharge)

On September 15, laboratory results showed a white blood cell count of 0.69 × 109/L, neutrophil count of 0.61 × 109/L, hemoglobin level of 58 g/L, and platelet count of 17 × 109/L, with CRP at 53.47 mg/L. Granulocytic hematopoietic recovery was achieved, and the patient was released from isolation. On September 22, the patient’s condition was stable, with a platelet count of 19 × 109/L, and he was discharged with medications. Following discharge, a 2-month period of continuous nursing intervention was implemented. During follow-up, the patient’s laboratory parameters gradually returned to normal, psychological status stabilized, and both self-care ability and physical activity levels improved significantly.

Discussion

Multidisciplinary Team-Based Management for the Peri-Transplant Period

The patient in this case was a 21-year-old male diagnosed with stage IVA ALK-negative anaplastic large cell lymphoma, with a disease course of 11 months. Despite achieving complete remission after surgery and multiple lines of chemotherapy, the highly aggressive nature of the disease and residual complications from prior treatments posed multiple challenges during transplantation. First, the risk of infection was high, as prolonged bone marrow suppression during transplantation, combined with pre-existing potential infectious foci, including chronic erosive gastritis, dental caries, and left ear secretion retention, significantly increased susceptibility to infection. Second, the risk of bleeding was elevated due to severe thrombocytopenia following conditioning therapy and delayed platelet recovery. Third, the patient was at risk of malnutrition; chemotherapy-related gastrointestinal reactions and oral mucositis led to insufficient intake, with an NRS-2002 score of 3 at admission indicating existing nutritional risk. Fourth, psychological disturbances were evident, with prolonged confinement within the transplantation unit contributing to depressive symptoms and suicidal tendencies. Fifth, functional impairment was present; the patient had limited mobility due to a postoperative pathological fracture of the right femur and a compression fracture of the T3 vertebra, and reduced food intake and physical weakness during transplantation further compromised self‑care ability, with the Barthel Index decreasing from 70 (mild dependence) before entering the transplantation unit to 55 (moderate dependence).

Given the complexity of this case, an integrated multidisciplinary team (MDT) comprising physicians, nurses, rehabilitation therapists, dietitians, and psychologists was established. Prior to transplantation, a comprehensive multidisciplinary assessment was conducted. Focusing on five key domains, infection control, bleeding management, nutritional support, psychological intervention, and functional rehabilitation, an individualised, whole-process management plan for the peri-transplant period was developed. Regular weekly MDT meetings were held to dynamically adjust the plan. This MDT model exhibited two distinctive features compared with routine multidisciplinary care. First, nursing-led-full-cycle coordination was implemented: the nursing team actively participated in pre-transplant risk stratification, contributed to the formulation of care plans across the five domains, and coordinated inputs from all team members throughout the peri-transplant trajectory, serving as the structural backbone that connected specialist inputs into a coherent, patient-centred care pathway. Second, proactive involvement of rehabilitation therapists, dietitians, and psychologists was embedded into daily bedside care rather than delivered through episodic consultations. Rehabilitation therapists provided early mobilisation programmes and functional exercise guidance tailored to the patient’s condition at each phase. Dietitians delivered personalised nutritional assessments and interventions to address the increased metabolic demands during transplantation. Psychologists conducted regular mental health evaluations and offered cognitive behavioural support to alleviate depressive symptoms. Furthermore, considering the lack of knowledge regarding post-transplant home-based care and the burden on family caregivers, continuous nursing care was incorporated into the MDT framework. Before discharge, the team jointly developed a home based rehabilitation plan to ensure continuity of care. This model may offer practical value for centres seeking to optimise peri-transplant care delivery, particularly in settings where systematic integration across disciplines remains challenging. However, its feasibility and generalisability require further evaluation through larger scale studies.

Infection Prevention and Control: Risk-Stratified Protective Isolation and Targeted Nursing Care

Environmental and Equipment Management

The patient was admitted to the transplantation unit under protective isolation. The floor was disinfected daily using a chlorine-containing disinfectant (500 mg/L), and environmental surfaces were cleaned daily with gamma disinfectant wipes. Clothing and bedding were replaced daily and sent for high-pressure steam sterilization. Tableware was boiled and disinfected for 30 minutes. All items entering the transplantation unit were exposed to ultraviolet irradiation for 60 minutes one day in advance.

Enhanced Basic Nursing Care

Before entering the transplantation unit, the patient underwent hair washing, hair removal, medicated bathing, and changed into sterile patient clothing. During transplantation, daily body cleansing was performed using 2% chlorhexidine gluconate wipes, and levofloxacin eye drops and ear drops were administered twice daily.

Mucositis is a well‑recognised complication of high‑dose chemotherapy, with reported incidence rates of 70–99% in patients undergoing HSCT,16 highlighting the need for effective preventive oral care strategies in this population. For this patient, preventive measures based on expert consensus were implemented. These included alternating mouth rinses with metronidazole and nystatin solution six times daily, along with oral care twice daily. In addition, oral cryotherapy was applied prior to melphalan infusion (ice chips held in the mouth), twice daily for 1 hour each time, for a duration of one week. After the development of oral mucositis, treatment included lidocaine mouthwash for analgesia, topical iodine glycerin application, and local ultraviolet irradiation.17

Perianal Care

Perianal infection is a common complication in transplant patients, associated with neutropenia and the unique anatomical features of the anorectal region. Systematic perianal care—including sitz baths, topical medication, and maintenance of hygiene—has been shown to significantly reduce infection risk.18 The patient received sitz baths twice daily using a 1:5000 potassium permanganate solution. Hemorrhoidal suppositories were administered rectally. After defecation, the perianal area was gently cleaned using wet wipes with a pressing technique, followed by application of a liquid dressing spray to form a protective barrier.

Outcome Evaluation

During transplantation, the patient experienced prolonged neutropenia for 25 days, with a nadir of 0.01 × 109/L. Sepsis developed, with a peak body temperature of 38.8°C and elevated inflammatory markers, including C-reactive protein (CRP) of 142 mg/L, procalcitonin (PCT) of 2.67 μg/L, and interleukin-6 (IL-6) of 1160 pg/mL. Following anti-infective treatment and hematopoietic recovery (neutrophil count 0.61 × 109/L), body temperature gradually normalized, and inflammatory markers declined. At discharge, CRP was 0.5 mg/L, PCT was 0.098 μg/L, and IL-6 was 3.46 pg/mL. On August 16, oral mucositis developed, presenting as erythema on the tongue and ulceration on the right lateral border (Figure 1). On August 23, erythema and ulceration developed on the tongue surface and the hard palate (Figure 2). Following the above interventions, the lesions healed by August 28 (Figure 3).

Bleeding Risk Management: A Stepwise Protective Strategy Based on Dynamic Monitoring

Following conditioning therapy, the patient’s platelet count decreased to a nadir of 34 × 109/L. A stratified bleeding risk management approach was implemented as follows: (1) Platelet counts and signs of bleeding were monitored daily. (2) When platelet counts were ≤50 × 109/L, the patient was instructed to remain on bed rest, avoid physical trauma, and use a soft-bristle toothbrush. (3) When platelet counts were ≤30 × 109/L, strict bed rest was enforced. Tooth brushing was discontinued and replaced with mouth rinses. Activities such as toothpicking, forceful defecation, eye rubbing, and nasal picking were strictly prohibited. (4) Oral eltrombopag was administered, along with platelet transfusion.

Outcome evaluation: During the transplantation period, no signs of bleeding were observed, including petechiae, ecchymosis, gingival bleeding, epistaxis, hematuria, or melena. Platelet engraftment was successfully achieved.

Nutritional Support: Integrated Dynamic Management of Screening, Intervention, and Monitoring

Patients undergoing hematopoietic stem cell transplantation (HSCT) are at high risk of malnutrition, which may impair immune function and delay hematopoietic and immune reconstitution.19 Therefore, effective nutritional management is essential in HSCT patients. At admission, the patient had an NRS-2002 score of 3, indicating nutritional risk. A personalized nutritional plan was developed with the involvement of the nutrition team.

(1) Pre-transplant phase: A high-protein, high-vitamin diet was provided, supplemented with oral enteral nutrition preparations (eg, Nutrison, Ensure, and hydrolyzed protein powder), aiming to achieve a daily energy intake of 25–30 kcal/kg. (2) During transplantation: Nutritional strategies were adjusted according to the condition of the oral mucosa. When the oral mucosa was intact, additional enteral nutrition (Nutrison, 12 scoops mixed with 250 mL warm water, three times daily) was provided after meals. When oral intake was impaired due to mucositis-related pain, Nutrison was prepared with sterile cold water, and the intake of hydrolyzed protein powder was increased. Parenteral nutrition was administered as needed, including amino acids, lipid emulsions, and albumin supplementation. (3) Management of gastrointestinal symptoms: For nausea and vomiting, ondansetron was administered intravenously as prescribed. Patients were instructed to avoid food intake 30 minutes before and after infusion and to consume small, frequent meals with adequate hydration. (4) Nutritional monitoring: Body weight was measured daily in the morning under fasting conditions. Serum albumin, prealbumin, hemoglobin, and lymphocyte counts were monitored every 2 days. The nutritional plan was adjusted when intake fell below 80% of the target requirement.

Outcome evaluation: The patient’s body weight decreased from 55 kg at admission to 49.8 kg during the mid-transplant period, followed by gradual recovery after discharge from the transplantation unit. At 2 months post-discharge, body weight increased to 54.5 kg, and BMI improved from 18.29 kg/m2 to 20.02 kg/m2. The NRS-2002 score decreased from 5 (high risk) during the mid-transplant period to 3 (reduced risk) at discharge. Serum albumin, hemoglobin, and other nutritional indicators showed gradual improvement with nutritional support.

Integrated Psychosocial Support: Cognitive Behavioral Therapy Combined with Pharmacological Intervention

Depressive symptoms are prevalent among patients undergoing HSCT, with reported rates of approximately 35% during the transplantation period and up to 43% within 6 months after discharge,20 underscoring the importance of psychological assessment and support in this population. In addition, younger adult lymphoma patients tend to experience higher levels of fear of recurrence.21 Non‑pharmacological approaches, such as cognitive behavioural therapy and narrative nursing, have been reported to alleviate negative emotional states.22 These considerations informed the psychological support strategy for the present case.

After admission to the transplantation unit, the patient developed low mood and reduced verbal communication due to environmental confinement and lack of family companionship. A psychiatric assessment revealed a Self-Rating Depression Scale (SDS) score of 65, indicating moderate depression, with suicidal ideation.

An integrated psychological intervention strategy was implemented as follows:

(1) Pre-transplant education: Detailed explanations of disease progression, treatment strategies, transplantation procedures, and successful cases were provided to correct the patient’s cognitive bias that “transplantation carries extremely high risk”. (2) Daily supportive communication: Dedicated nursing staff engaged in 20–30 minutes of daily communication using techniques such as active listening, empathy, and reassurance. Family members were encouraged to maintain daily video communication with the patient. (3) Peer support: Online sharing sessions were arranged with patients who had successfully undergone transplantation to provide experiential support. (4) Psychiatric Intervention: psychiatric intervention was initiated for psychological assessment. Oral escitalopram oxalate 5 mg once daily was started on September 7. After one week of treatment, psychiatric re‑evaluation showed improvement in the patient’s mood, sleep, and appetite compared with baseline, with a Self‑Rating Depression Scale (SDS) score of 57. The medication was discontinued on September 14, and the patient was discharged from protective isolation on September 15, with bedside companionship and emotional support from family members.

Outcome evaluation: The patient’s emotional state gradually stabilized, with increased willingness to communicate with healthcare providers and improved treatment adherence. At the 2-month follow-up after discharge, the SDS score decreased to 33 (within the normal range). Sleep quality improved, and the patient resumed a normal daily routine.

Continuity of Care: Home-Based Rehabilitation Guidance Under MDT Collaboration

One week prior to discharge, the multidisciplinary team (MDT) developed and implemented an individualized home-based rehabilitation plan centered on five key domains:

(1) Infection prevention and control: Family members were instructed to prepare disinfection supplies, including ultraviolet lamps, disinfectant wipes, and 75% alcohol. A WeChat-based follow-up system was established, with supervision conducted twice weekly to reinforce hand hygiene, mask use, and environmental disinfection. Clear instructions were provided regarding the management and hospital referral process for emergency situations such as fever. (2) Bleeding risk prevention: Home environment modifications were recommended, including installation of bathroom handrails, use of anti-slip mats, and placement of protective padding on sharp edges. The patient was advised to use a soft-bristle toothbrush and avoid strenuous physical activities. (3) Nutritional support: Oral enteral nutrition (eg, Nutrison) was continued and gradually reduced as appetite improved. Body weight was monitored weekly, and serum albumin and hemoglobin were re-evaluated at 1 and 3 months post-discharge. (4) Psychological support: Weekly voice follow-ups were conducted to dynamically assess emotional status, with referral to online psychiatric consultation when necessary. (5) Functional rehabilitation: A rehabilitation therapist provided video-based guidance for progressive functional training. Patients were instructed to avoid excessive hip flexion and rotation, and correct movement patterns were reinforced through online follow-up.

Outcome evaluation: At the 2-month follow-up after discharge, the patient’s muscle strength recovered to grade V, with a Barthel Index of 100 (complete independence), and the patient was able to walk independently.

Conclusion

This case report demonstrates that, under a multidisciplinary team (MDT) collaboration model, whole-process management during the peri-transplant period can be effectively implemented for a patient with stage IVA ALK-negative anaplastic large cell lymphoma undergoing autologous hematopoietic stem cell transplantation. Targeted nursing interventions were carried out focusing on four key domains, namely infection prevention, bleeding management, nutritional support, and psychological care. While continuity of care was ensured during the home-based rehabilitation period through structured follow-up. The patient successfully overcame the bone marrow suppression phase, with gradual recovery of clinical indicators, improvement in nutritional status, stabilization of psychological condition, and full restoration of self-care ability. In the future, further efforts are needed to explore and refine a “hospital–community–home” integrated care model, with clearly defined roles and efficient information-sharing pathways across different levels of care. Establishing a system of regular follow-up and dynamic assessment may facilitate more comprehensive and continuous health management. This case suggests that such an approach may offer a practical framework, and this experience could inform future practice.
