Triarylamine sensitizers: molecular engineering of π-bridges and donors in a D–π–A framework of dyes and improving power conversion efficiency of dye-sensitized solar cells
Authors: Karim Khanmohammadi Chenab, Mohammad-Reza Zamani-Meymian
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Triarylamine (TAA) derivatives have emerged as the premier class of metal-free organic sensitizers for dye-sensitized solar cells (DSSCs), owing to their exceptionally high molar extinction coefficients, decoupled frontier molecular orbitals, and highly tunable redox kinetics that facilitate rapid dye regeneration. However, bridging the gap between current laboratory outputs and theoretical power conversion efficiency (PCE) limits requires a fundamental transition from empirical screening to rational design. Rather than merely cataloging structural families, this review deciphers the pivotal design parameters and structure–property relationships that govern TAA performance across liquid, quasi-solid-state, and solid-state architectures. We analyze how manipulating the spatial topology—specifically through the coplanarity of π-conjugated spacers and the peripheral bulk of alkyloxy or aryloxy donor substitutions—dictates critical interfacial dynamics. These parameters directly modulate the intramolecular charge transfer (ICT) transition dipole, establish energetic alignment for efficient electron injection, and passivate the semiconductor interface against triiodide-mediated dark current recombination. Despite substantial progress in spectral broadening and aggregation control, breaking the current efficiency ceiling remains hindered by unresolved trade-offs between low-energy photon harvesting and open-circuit voltage retention, alongside long-term desorption kinetics under operational stress. To address these bottlenecks, we outline future directions for molecular and interfacial engineering, highlighting the strategic integration of fluorinated auxiliary acceptors, multi-anchoring structural topologies, and targeted multi-component co-sensitization systems. This analytical framework establishes a definitive engineering blueprint to suppress interfacial loss mechanisms and unlock next-generation TAA photovoltaic milestones.

Introduction

In recent years, bibliometric trends have revealed a noticeable contraction in the annual publication volume dedicated to dye-sensitized solar cells (DSSCs), a phenomenon largely driven by the diversion of academic funding and research interest toward perovskite-based photovoltaics. However, this statistical decline does not signify the obsolescence of dye-sensitized technology; rather, it marks a critical transition from descriptive, trial-and-error synthesis toward targeted molecular engineering for specialized applications. While solid-state counterparts excel in peak outdoor power conversion, triarylamine (TAA)-sensitized systems possess unparalleled advantages in diffuse ambient light-harvesting, transparency, and mechanical flexibility, making them the premier candidates for indoor photovoltaics (IPVs) and internet-of-things (IoT) ecosystems. At this technological junction, the historical reliance on empirical cataloguing must be replaced by a rigorous, critical consensus. Consequently, rather than expanding descriptive indices of molecular structures, there is an urgent imperative to dissect the precise stereoelectronic relationships between dye topology and long-term interfacial stability, establishing predictable design rules that match the current demands of ambient energy harvesting. Based on an updated report by the National Laboratories of the Rockies (https://www.nlr.gov/pv/cell-efficiency), III–V multijunction cells developed by Fraunhofer Institute for Solar Energy Systems are top-ranked in efficiency with 47.6%. The photovoltaic efficiency of DSSCs still limited to the 14% which an enhancement in their efficiency can pass the need of entering to the commercialized stage.1 Among photovoltaic devices, the low power conversion efficiency (PCE) of DSSCs arise from the narrow solar absorption bandwidth and low dye stability. Despite their relatively low PCEs, there is a profound research enthusiasm in this field, driven by them exhibiting two distinct advantages over conventional solar cells: the spatial separation of the photon absorption region from the charge transport path, and the independent optimization of each process without mutual trade-offs. This decoupling of functional roles allows for the independent optimization of each process, paving the way for targeted efficiency enhancement without the mutual trade-offs inherent in traditional p–n junction devices. In alignment with the light-harvesting role, the light-harvesting efficiency (LHE) can be enhanced by shifting the photon absorption of dyes to the near-infrared (NIR) region. Importantly, merely designing a sensitizer that absorbs NIR light is insufficient; once anchored onto the semiconductor surface, these dyes must be prevented from aggregating or dissipating their excitation energy. Consequently, inhibiting unwanted dye aggregation, suppressing the self-quenching of photoexcited states, and enhancing the stability of the sensitizers remain critical strategies to achieve higher PCEs. Fundamentally, these properties are governed by the precise tuning of the dye's HOMO and LUMO levels, which dictate the driving forces for both electron injection (ΔGinj) and dye regeneration (ΔGreg).2 Consequently, any deficiency in these parameters accelerates the quenching of photoexcited states, leading to a drastic drop in the electron injection quantum efficiency and promoting the undesirable charge recombination reaction (CRR). To address these kinetic challenges and optimize the energy alignment, TAA-based derivatives have emerged as one of the most promising classes of organic sensitizers. Due to their excellent electron-donating ability and easily tunable HOMO/LUMO levels, TAA moieties serve as ideal donor cores in D–π–A molecular architectures. Furthermore, the bulky, non-planar stereochemical structure of TAA donors effectively passivates the semiconductor surface, acting as a steric barrier that suppresses the unwanted charge recombination reaction (CRR) with the electrolyte, thereby significantly boosting the open-circuit voltage (VOC).3–7 The TAA core exerts profound stereoelectronic control over both intra- and intermolecular processes. The inherent twisting of the aryl rings disrupts extended molecular planarity, which critically suppresses detrimental H- or J-type intermolecular π–π aggregation, thereby mitigating non-radiative self-quenching pathways on the nanocrystalline oxide film. In a highly optimized D–π–A architecture, photoexcitation triggers a localized-to-charge-transfer (LCT) transition, polarizing the electron density from the nitrogen-centered HOMO localized on the TAA toward the LUMO centered on the anchoring acceptor. This spatial decoupling of molecular orbitals minimizes the electronic coupling matrix element (Hab) for the back-electron transfer, substantially delaying the deleterious CRR between injected electrons in the TiO2 conduction band and the oxidized dye species. Furthermore, structural functionalization of the TAA donor with bulky, hydrophobic aliphatic or aromatic networks (e.g., hexyl or hexyloxy chains) creates a dense, molecularly engineered hydrophobic shield. This steric insulation effectively passivates the sub-bandgap surface states of TiO2 and blocks the approach of the electrolyte's redox mediators (such as I3− or Co3+ complexes) to the bare semiconductor surface, which significantly extends the photogenerated electron lifetime (τn) and maximizes the achievable VOC. Building upon these structural paradigms, the strategic modulation of the π-conjugated bridge length—specifically through systematic homologous expansion (where n = 1, 2, or 3 repeating units)—exerts a profound influence on the internal reorganization energy (λ) and the electronic excitation dynamics. While increasing the number of conjugated rings effectively red-shifts the optical response via enhanced LCT transitions, it simultaneously alters the torsional degrees of freedom within the molecular backbone. This conformational flexibility in extended homologues can induce a state of planarization-induced charge localization, which destabilizes the photoexcited state and accelerates non-radiative decay channels. To mitigate this, replacing simple linear spacers with structurally constrained, non-planar homologues (such as spiro-configured or phenanthrene-based bridged networks) locks the molecular orientation into a fixed orthogonal topology. This geometric rigidity not only preserves the vital intramolecular charge-transfer (ICT) pathways toward the anchoring group but also creates a dense, multi-directional steric barrier for TAA dyes. Consequently, the precise optimization of both the number and the spatial configuration of these homologous bridges acts as a sophisticated electronic valve, simultaneously maximizing the quantum efficiency of forward electron injection while minimizing interfacial losses.

Beyond the stereoelectronic constraints of conventional heterocyclic and rigid aromatic networks, emerging paradigms in rational molecular design have pioneered the integration of atom-economical, highly conjugated linear carbon chain (LCC) linkers as non-classical π-bridges. Unlike standard cyclic homologues that frequently suffer from torsional decoupling under operational stress, engineered LCC motifs—specifically polyyne and polyene molecular wires—offer a fundamentally distinct structural vector characterized by an ultra-low reorganization energy (λ) and exceptional electronic delocalization along a strictly one-dimensional pathway. Computational quantum dynamics and density functional theory (DFT/TD-DFT) simulations reveal that systematically tuning the length of these 1D spatial conduits induces a profound bathochromic shift in the UV-vis absorption profile while drastically enhancing the oscillator strength (f), effectively circumventing symmetry-forbidden quantum transitions, and unlocking exceptionally high transition dipole moments. Building upon this structural foundation, recent advancements have progressed from basic photophysical screening toward the precise engineering of the semiconductor–dye interface through anchoring group topology, specifically contrasting conventional cyanoacrylic acid anchors with novel phosphonate derivatives. Interfacial binding simulations demonstrate that the phosphonate moiety introduces superior anchoring chemistry, yielding significantly higher adsorption energies on the anatase TiO2 (101) surface to drastically enhance the structural robustness of the monolayer. Crucially, by implementing an advanced improved normal model (INM) to decouple microscopic injection dynamics from macroscopic device metrics, recent studies have successfully predicted the VOC and overall PCE. Interestingly, while the phosphonate derivatives establish exceptionally strong surface binding ideal for stable co-sensitization schemes, the cyanoacrylic acid-based LCC dyes demonstrate superior interfacial kinetics characterized by a suppressed CRR, culminating in an outstanding predicted PCE of up to 21.79%. Consequently, transitioning to quantum-engineered LCC architectures represents a critical evolutionary leap in the D–A–π–A framework, offering a highly streamlined electronic channel that effectively decouples directional charge separation from deleterious non-radiative interfacial recombination networks.8,9

To systematically guide the field through this functional transition, this review rejects the traditional approach of simple empirical indexing in favor of a rigorous, mechanistic framework. The narrative is organized to directly address the key milestones of rational molecular engineering, beginning with a deep dissection of the advancements in stereoelectronic molecular design. Within this context, we evaluate how donor core topologies and diverse homologous π-bridge variations fine-tune orbital configurations and light-harvesting thresholds. These structural concepts are then directly bridged with interfacial kinetics, providing a critical analysis of the competitive pathways between forward electron injection and the CRR. Ultimately, these molecular and kinetic parameters are translated into macro-level device performance and stability, explicitly mapping the thermal and photochemical degradation pathways under operational stress. Through this integrated structural blueprint, this manuscript shifts the narrative from descriptive cataloguing toward a predictive, property-driven consensus, offering clear design rules to maximize both the efficiency and durability of next-generation TAA-sensitized photovoltaics.10–13

π-Bridges in TAA dyes

Optimizing ICT to extend the light-harvesting threshold represents a cornerstone strategy for suppressing the interfacial CRR and boosting the overall PCE of DSSCs. In typical linear TAA sensitizers possessing a donor–π–acceptor (D–π–A) topology, the conjugated π-spacer serves as the primary electronic conduit mapping the intramolecular migration of photogenerated excitons. Driven by the asymmetrical ‘push–pull’ electronic gradient, the cyanoacrylic acid anchoring group acts as both the electron-accepting sink and the structural node, orchestrating efficient electron injection into the conduction band of TiO2via a ballistic pathway.14 To effectively fulfill these multi-dimensional requirements, the architecture of the π-conjugated spacer must balance both electronic delocalization and structural conformation. Mechanistically, extending the conjugation length through the concatenation of aromatic rings—such as oligothiophenes or fluorene derivatives—narrows the HOMO–LUMO bandgap, which bathochromically shifts the absorption spectrum toward the NIR region. However, an uncontrolled extension of the unfunctionalized π-bridge often triggers detrimental intermolecular π–π stacking on the TiO2 surface, resulting in self-quenching of the excited states. Furthermore, the electronic nature of the bridge dictates the injection efficiency; incorporating electron-rich units (like thiophene or dithienothiophene) elevates the system's overall donor capacity, whereas inserting electron-deficient units (such as benzothiadiazole) inside an auxiliary receptor framework creates an internal ‘pull–push–pull’ configuration that profoundly accelerates the internal charge-transfer rate. Therefore, engineering the π-bridge is not merely a matter of molecular length, but a delicate calibration of its topology to optimize the trade-off between optical absorption, orbital alignment, and interfacial protection.

To overcome the inherent trade-off between expanding light-harvesting windows and triggering detrimental dye aggregation, Han and co-workers developed a pioneering molecular design featuring a macrocyclic “circle chain” that covalently embraces the bithiophene π-backbone. While extending unfunctionalized oligothiophene bridges often leads to severe molecular aggregation and self-quenching on the semiconductor surface, and conventional bulky substitutions typically induce significant torsional twisting that compromises electronic conjugation, this pseudo-rotaxane architecture (8) elegantly bypasses both limitations simultaneously. Through this approach, the rigid outer ring successfully locks the dihedral angle between the thiophene units into an almost perfectly coplanar conformation (0.51°). This fixed planarization facilitates highly efficient ICT—overcoming the structural twisting found in standard sterically hindered dyes—and yields a prominent 50° nm bathochromic shift along with an enhanced molar extinction coefficient. Furthermore, the encapsulating macrocycle serves as a robust steric insulation barrier that prevents I3− ions in the electrolyte from approaching the TiO2 surface. By effectively suppressing charge recombination and elongating electron lifetimes (τe), this dual-functional insulation and planarization strategy enables a simultaneous improvement of photocurrent (JSC) and photovoltage (VOC), culminating in a superior PCE of 8.34%.24 Lin and co-workers demonstrated that navigating molecular packing and inter-dye interactions requires precise structural tailoring beyond simply multiplying anchoring groups. By incorporating twin thienyl cyanoacrylic acid anchors onto a specialized thiophene spacer, they synthesized distinct Y-shaped D–π–(A)2 molecular architectures. This design strategy uniquely circumvented the typical photovoltage penalties seen in dual-anchor dyes by forming a compact insulating shield over the TiO2 surface. Benefiting from both enhanced electron injection channels and superior dark current suppression, the co-adsorbed cells yielded a prominent PCE of 7.87% for 9.25,38 During the dye regeneration process, the penetration of I3− species into the self-assembled monolayer is highly sensitive to the conformational landscape of the π-bridge. In flexible oligothiophene homologs, dynamic dihedral rotations create transient steric voids that facilitate the infiltration of I3− toward the –N terminal of the donor and the inner thiophene S-atoms, deeply modulating the hole transfer dynamics. Although mitigating these conformational fluctuations by transitioning to rigid, fused-thiophene or tetrathienoacene (TTA) π-bridges (10–13) effectively seals these pathways, it has yielded ambivalent effects on the overall PCE of the corresponding DSSCs (4.76, 6.15, 10.1 and 6.91%, respectively). In this regard, X-ray reflectometry (XRR) characterization elucidates these structural variations on the TiO2 surface, revealing monolayer thicknesses of 7.3, 6.6, 16.8, and 15.4 Å, alongside respective tilt angles of 70°, 74°, 48°, and 58° for dyes 10–13. Notably, the systematically lower tilt angles—indicative of a more upright molecular orientation—correlate with a more closely packed and compact monolayer arrangement.26,39 Drawing a parallel to the foundational XRR methodology established by Griffith et al., where thickness constraints were translated into precise molecular orientation models to probe charge transfer kinetics, structural compaction of TAAs plays a pivotal role in modulating the interfacial environment. Specifically, while a significant tilt angle can inadvertently introduce open steric pockets that permit electrolyte species to infiltrate toward the semiconductor substrate, the enforced upright orientation and enhanced thickness of the rigid homologs (12 and 13) establish a robust physical blocking barrier, effectively mitigating undesired CRRs by controlling redox couple accessibility. Crucially, from a molecular architecture perspective, TAA-based dyes with thiophene π-bridges offer distinct structural advantages over the planar macrocyclic porphyrins investigated by Griffith and co-workers. While planar porphyrin frameworks are inherently prone to detrimental face-to-face π–π stacking and lateral aggregation on the surface—which leads to self-quenching of the excited states—the non-planar, pyramidal geometry of the central nitrogen in the TAA core imparts a highly beneficial, propeller-like three-dimensional conformation. This intrinsic steric bulk, combined with the tailored orientation of the extended thiophene segments, acts as a native spatial shield. It simultaneously suppresses molecular aggregation without relying on auxiliary co-adsorbents and forms a more isotropic, omnidirectional blocking layer. Consequently, the TAA architecture provides superior coverage that screens the semiconductor surface from electrolyte penetration more effectively than rigidly aligned planar monolayers.40 With a similar approach, incorporating a 3,4-ethylenedioxythiophene (EDOT) moiety (14) and an octyloxy spacer (15) provided 7.30% and 5.36% PCE, respectively, which revealed a weak dye–dye intermolecular interaction effect on PCE of DSSCs.26,40 Crucially, as substantiated by Liu and co-workers, the structural modification of the thiophene linker and the judicious choice of the anchoring acceptor dictate a delicate trade-off between the light-harvesting capacity and interfacial kinetics. For instance, while substituting with the rhodanine-3-acetic acid anchoring group can successfully induce a pronounced bathochromic shift and enhance molar extinction coefficients, it introduces an aliphatic methylene spacer that fundamentally disrupts the continuous π-conjugation toward the carboxylic acid group. This electronic decoupling restricts the LUMO localization strictly within the rhodanine framework rather than the anchoring group, dramatically undermining the dynamic electron injection efficiency and leading to shortened electron lifetimes. On the other hand, substituting EDOT with bulkier, oxygen-rich configurations such as bis[2-(2-methoxyethoxy)ethoxy]thiophene (BMEET) presents an alternative structural advantage. Although the massive spatial footprint of such elongated alkoxy chains inevitably lowers the absolute dye-uptake on the nanocrystalline TiO2 film due to steric hindrance, it constructs a dense insulating shield. This localized barrier effectively suppresses the dark current originating from the back-transfer of electrons from the semiconductor conduction band to the I3− electrolyte, thereby yielding a remarkably elevated VOC.27,28

In summary, deploying thiophene-based segments within the π-conjugated bridges of TAA sensitizers represents a double-edged sword, exhibiting a nuanced trade-off between structural optimization and interfacial charge kinetics. In terms of advantages, the incorporation of thiophene linkers significantly expands the spectral response and provides a highly tunable platform for molecular engineering. When properly rigidified or elongated (as demonstrated in dyes 12 and 13), these bridges enforce a highly desirable, more upright molecular orientation with lower tilt angles (48–58°) and increased monolayer thickness on the nanocrystalline TiO2 surface. This structural compaction, reinforced by the intrinsic three-dimensional propeller-like conformation of the TAA core, serves as a robust physical blocking barrier. This spatial shielding effectively suppresses detrimental face-to-face π–π molecular aggregation and passivates the semiconductor surface against electrolyte infiltration, thereby mitigating undesired back-electron transfer. Conversely, the prominent disadvantages of thiophene architectures stem from their inherent conformational flexibility and potential for steric crowding. In flexible thiophene derivatives, the molecules tend to tilt significantly. This high tilt angle leaves open structural gaps on the surface, allowing electrolyte species to easily penetrate and reach the TiO2 substrate, which accelerates unwanted CRR. On the other hand, if we extend these bridges or add bulky side chains to prevent tilting, the excessive molecular size causes structural crowding. This crowding restricts the absolute amount of dye adsorbed on the semiconductor surface. Consequently, the reduced dye uptake acts as a bottleneck, lowering the JSC. Therefore, precise spatial and electronic management of the thiophene bridge is imperative to exploit its light-harvesting benefits without inducing conformational or recombination losses.

Benzothiadiazoles and benzotriazoles

The spatial distribution of frontier molecular orbitals in TAA sensitizers typically features HOMOs localized on the TAA donor core and LUMOs delocalized toward the anchoring acceptor. Photoexcitation prompts a directional ICT from the donor to the anchor, an effect that can be significantly amplified by consolidating electron-withdrawing (EW) units directly into the π-conjugated bridge. This strategy leads to the well-known D–A′–π–A architecture, where heteroaromatics like benzothiadiazole (BT) and benzotriazole (BTA) serve as internal auxiliary acceptors (A′) within the π-bridge framework. The incorporation of these electron-deficient units induces strong orbital hybridization across the bridge, resulting in a substantial bathochromic shift in the absorption profile and effectively expanding the light-harvesting window.29 A direct comparison between the absorption maxima λmax of 5 (415 nm) and 16 (486 nm) reveals a profound bathochromic shift of 71 nm. This pronounced redshift is explicitly justifiable by the strategic insertion of the benzothiadiazole (BT) unit as an auxiliary acceptor within the π-bridge framework, which effectively narrows the optical bandgap through enhanced ICT. Concomitant with this significantly broadened light-harvesting profile, the photovoltaic features of the modified sensitizer were substantially promoted; consequently, the solar cell device fabricated with 16 yielded an impressive PCE of 4.43% when evaluated on a standard TiO2 photoanode. This performance enhancement is predominantly driven by a remarkable boost in the JSC (13.20 mA cm−2), stemming from the extended spectral response in the 480–540 nm region. However, as noted by Lin and co-workers, the incorporation of such an electron-deficient BT entity within the spacer presents an inherent physical trade-off; it can induce partial charge-trapping at the BT site, which potentially hinders the dynamics of direct electron injection into the semiconductor's conduction band. Despite this kinetic constraint, the vastly optimized photon-to-electron conversion capability of the system successfully counteracts the recombination losses, showcasing the net positive impact of BT integration on the overall device performance. In further pursuit of optimizing the conjugated bridge framework, the incorporation of an additional thiophene unit in 17 enhanced the PCE to 4.79%, primarily attributed to a higher molar extinction coefficient (ε). To unlock the full potential of auxiliary extension, a more sophisticated architectural refinement was realized in 18 by integrating a fused 4H-cyclopenta[2,1-b:3,4-b′]dithiophene (CPDT) unit into the π-bridge framework, which advanced the device performance to yield an enhanced PCE of 8.05% and a JSC of 16.23 mA cm−2. According to the profound molecular engineering principles demonstrated by Zhu and co-workers, the extended, rigid co-planarity of the CPDT core not only enforces a substantial bathochromic shift but also establishes highly efficient internal charge transfer channels across the entire molecular skeleton. Crucially, this structural modification effectively circumvents the notorious trade-off between the JSC and VOC typically observed in BTD-based systems. The strategic introduction of long, sterically hindering dioctyl chains at the bridging carbon atoms of the CPDT unit acts as an insulating hydrophobic shield. This molecular arrangement significantly suppresses detrimental intermolecular aggregation and retards interfacial electron recombination, thereby drastically amplifying the interfacial charge transfer resistance (Rct). Furthermore, the unique interfacial dynamics of the CPDT-fused configuration endow the sensitizer with an accelerated dye uptake capability, reaching optimal saturation rapidly without the prerequisite of time-consuming adsorption protocols or co-sensitizers—a feature highly desirable for large-scale industrial commercialization.31 From a molecular engineering perspective, incorporating a BTD core as an auxiliary acceptor within the D–π–A architecture serves as a powerful strategy to fine-tune the frontier molecular orbitals (FMOs) and narrow the bandgap via enhanced ICT. In this specific framework, further elongation of the conjugated system by integrating an octylthiophene unit into the BTD segment (19) introduces two critical structural advantages. First, the bulky octyl side chains project out of the molecular plane, effectively suppressing detrimental π–π intermolecular aggregation on the TiO2 surface, which in turn minimizes charge recombination pathways. Second, it optimizes the directional electron flow from the TAA donor to the anchoring group. This precise molecular tailoring directly translates into exceptional photovoltaic macroscopic performance, delivering a JSC of 18.26 mA cm−2, an VOC of 0.76 V, and a fill factor (FF) of 0.74, culminating in a remarkable PCE of 10.20%. Consequently, this design demonstrates that rational modification around the BTD unit can yield metal-free organic sensitizers that match the performance of traditional ruthenium complex standards while maintaining synthetic simplicity.32 From the architectural standpoint of a metal-free organic sensitizer, 20 exemplifies an exceptionally balanced D–A–π–A molecular design, elegantly coupling a classical TAA electron donor with a sophisticated benzotriazole (BTA) auxiliary acceptor and a furan π-bridge. The defining brilliance of this assembly lies in the strategic transition from traditional thiophene to furan linkers, which triggers a profound molecular-level optimization of all primary photovoltaic parameters. From a structural and electronic perspective, furan possesses a significantly lower aromatic resonance energy (16 kcal mol−1) compared to thiophene (29 kcal mol−1), which diminishes the aromatic character of the spacer and promotes a highly efficient quinoid-like structure under photo-excitation. This fundamental shift enhances the ICT from the TAA donor to the cyanoacetic acid anchoring group, thereby broadening the ε and facilitating a substantially higher JSC. Furthermore, due to the smaller atomic radius of oxygen compared to sulfur, substituting thiophene with furan minimizes internal steric hindrance and drastically reduces the dihedral angles along the conjugated backbone. This induced molecular planarity strengthens the intermolecular π–π electronic coupling and shortens the charge transport pathway, effectively lowering the cell's internal series resistance and boosting the FF. Crucially, the high electronegativity of oxygen stabilizes the ground-state oxidation potential (HOMO level) of the furan-containing dye, while the central BTA core simultaneously maintains an elevated LUMO level. This synergistic electronic tuning optimizes the thermodynamic driving force for ultra-fast electron injection into the TiO2 conduction band and accelerates dye regeneration by the electrolyte. When combined with the 2-ethylhexyl shield on the BTA unit that forms a tightly packed insulating monolayer to suppress the CRR, this delicate atomistic substitution directly translates into an exceptionally high VOC of 834 mV and flawless long-term photophysical stability in solvent-free ionic liquid electrolytes.33,41,42 From a molecular and mechanistic perspective, replacing the strongly EW BT core with a less electron-deficient BTA unit strategically alters the internal push–pull electronic dynamics of the dye molecule. Mechanistically, because BTA exerts a weaker pulling force on the π-electrons, it induces destabilization and a subsequent upward shift of the LUMO energy level. On a quantum mechanical level, this electronic redistribution expands the energetic driving force (ΔGinj) between the excited state of the dye and the conduction band edge of TiO2, directly maximizing the VOC of the solar cell. Concurrently, the unique molecular geometry of the BTA framework allows for functionalization at the N-2 position with bulky, branched alkyl chains that extend perpendicularly out of the conjugated molecular plane. Mechanistically, this localized steric hindrance effectively shields the hydrophobic core of the dye, disrupting close intermolecular π–π stacking on the semiconductor surface. By suppressing this molecular aggregation, the dye avoids the formation of non-luminescent excimers and aggregates, thereby drastically shutting down detrimental charge recombination pathways where injected electrons leak back from TiO2 to the oxidized electrolyte. Although shifting to the weaker BTA auxiliary acceptor diminishes the ground-state to excited-state dipole moment—causing a hypsochromic (blue) shift in the ICT band that typically restricts LHE—the subsequent transition to the 2H-[1,2,3]triazolo[4,5-c]pyridine (PT) (21) core elegantly addresses this trade-off. Mechanistically, the incorporation of a highly electronegative nitrogen atom within the fused pyridine ring introduces a strong, localized inductive effect that pulls electron density back toward the central core, thereby lowering the LUMO level back to an optimal thermodynamic threshold. This orbital narrowing compresses the bandgap, shifting the absorption profile back toward the longer-wavelength visible spectrum to maximize photon capture. Furthermore, the asymmetrical nature of the PT core introduces a permanent molecular dipole moment that accelerates directional, intramolecular vector charge transfer from the donor group straight through the anchoring group into the semiconductor, combining optimal broad-spectrum light absorption with robust, aggregation-free interfacial charge injection (Fig. 2 and Table 1).34 In summary, the incorporation of auxiliary acceptors like BTD and BTA into the D–A–π–A framework offers a sophisticated lever for fine-tuning optoelectronic properties, presenting distinct thermodynamic and kinetic trade-offs compared to traditional thiophene-based π-bridges. As a highly EW unit, BTD aggressively lowers the LUMO energy level, drastically narrowing the optical bandgap (ΔE) and inducing a powerful bathochromic shift that extends light harvesting into the NIR region to maximize the JSC; however, this lowered LUMO can diminish the ΔGinj into the TiO2 conduction band, while BTD's planar geometry accelerates detrimental intermolecular π–π stacking that lowers the VOC. Conversely, BTA exhibits a weaker EW character that preserves a higher LUMO level for efficient injection while offering unique structural versatility through the attachment of long, branched alkyl chains at the N position; this built-in steric shield physically suppresses dye aggregation and blocks redox mediators from reaching the semiconductor surface, thereby dramatically extending the τe and maximizing the VOC at the expense of a wider bandgap and a blueshifted absorption profile. Compared to standard thiophene linkers—which primarily extend π-conjugation without internal electronic modulation—the auxiliary acceptor strategy establishes a localized ICT trap within the spacer, creating a highly directional electron conduit that yields significantly higher ε and superior long-term stability.

Quinoxalines

The structural engineering of the quinoxaline-based π-bridges focuses on utilizing a rigid, highly coplanar 2,3-dipentyldithieno[3,2-f:2′,3′-h]quinoxaline (DPQ) framework as a novel π-spacer within a D–π–A organic dye setup. The design principle hinges on the large, conjugated planar layout of the dye core (tagged as DQ1–DQ5 in ref. 35), which effectively delocalizes π-electrons to facilitate rapid ICT. To systematically modulate the optoelectronic properties, the molecular structure was engineered by combining the DPQ spacer with distinct electron donors—such as TPA and indoline—and embedding auxiliary units like electron-rich 3,4-ethylenedioxythiophene (EDOT) or electron-withdrawing BTD into the π-system. Crucially, the deliberate installation of long alkyl (pentyl) chains onto the DPQ backbone serves to enhance the solubility of the metal-free dyes, suppress dense intermolecular aggregation, and insulate the TiO2 surface against the performance-limiting CRR. The main advantage of this architectural engineering is a significantly broadened light-harvesting capability, extending the absorption edges of the dyes up to 800 nm when anchored onto TiO2 films. The DQ5 dye—which pairs a powerful indoline donor with a BTD-inserted DPQ bridge—achieved the highest PCE of 7.12% due to an optimal orbital overlap, where the BTD segment serves as an efficient internal electron trap to accelerate charge separation. Conversely, due to a notable structural pitfall with DQ3, inserting the electron-rich EDOT group excessively upshifted the HOMO energy level, narrowing the driving force required for dye regeneration and leading to a severely shortened electron lifetime of 15.9 ms. Furthermore, a unique insight emerged during co-adsorption tests with chenodeoxycholic acid (CDCA); because the engineered DPQ backbone intrinsically suppresses molecular aggregation on its own, adding CDCA actually decreased the overall solar cell efficiency by unnecessarily displacing active dye molecules from the semiconductor surface. Structural engineering in organic dyes with a D–A–π–A framework by incorporating quinoxaline–fluorenyl interfacial bridges (23–27) is an exceptionally clever strategy for simultaneously optimizing the current and voltage in DSSCs. The core appeal of this design lies in the direct connection of the bulky, branched dibutyl-fluorenyl unit to the electron-accepting quinoxaline core. This specific spatial arrangement extends the π-conjugation length without causing severe steric hindrance in the dye's main backbone while acting as an unparalleled structural insulation shield. By suppressing unfavorable H-type aggregation (which typically causes a blueshift and reduces the efficiency) and instead driving the dye toward J-aggregation, this engineered architecture induces a dramatic bathochromic shift (redshift) in visible light absorption. The precise molecular tailoring of these dyes is beautifully demonstrated in the flagship dye 26, which achieves a striking PCE of 8.68% under standard AM 1.5G illumination (100 mW cm−2) and an outstanding PCE of 26.81% under low-light ambient conditions (6000 lx). From a kinetic standpoint, the out-of-plane orthogonal orientation of the long dibutyl chains on the fluorene unit introduces substantial steric hindrance at the semiconductor–electrolyte interface. This structural insulation functions as a highly effective physical passivation layer, successfully blocking the approach of the oxidized redox mediator to the TiO2 conduction band. Consequently, this drastically suppresses the deleterious interfacial CRR pathway, significantly prolonging the τe and inducing a pronounced upward shift in the Fermi level (EF,n) of the semiconductor, which ultimately maximizes the VOC without compromising the JSC.36 The strategic integration of quinoxaline as an auxiliary acceptor within the D–A–π–A architecture represents a highly balanced and customizable approach, bridging the gap between the aggressive EW nature of BTD and the sterically protected design of BTA. Structurally, the twin imine nitrogens (–CN–) of the quinoxaline core confer moderate-to-strong electron deficiency, inducing an efficient ICT and a desirable bathochromic shift to boost the JSC. The defining advantage of the quinoxaline moiety lies in its unique chemical tunability at the C-5, C-6, and C-7 positions of its fused benzene ring. This exceptional structural flexibility allows for the precise grafting of bulky, out-of-plane aromatic or aliphatic substituents (such as fluorenyl, thiophene, or alkoxy units) without compromising the central conjugated backbone. By decoupling optoelectronic tuning from interfacial kinetics, functionalized quinoxaline derivatives suppress detrimental π–π intermolecular stacking and establish a robust physical passivation layer that blocks redox mediators from reaching the TiO2 conduction band. Consequently, rational quinoxaline engineering circumvents the high CRR rates typical of planar, unmodified quinoxaline rings, yielding an optimal equilibrium between ΔGinj and interfacial charge separation to simultaneously maximize the VOC and JSC.

Bithiophenes

The molecular design and engineering of organic sensitizers (28–31) based on the 3,3′-dithioalkyl-2,2′-bithiophene (SBT) π-bridges introduce unique structural and electronic advantages that directly enhance the photovoltaic performance of DSSCs. From the perspective of molecular engineering and quantum chemical calculations, the most critical advantage of these π-bridges is the formation of intramolecular, non-covalent S(alkyl)⋯S(thiophene) locks. The distance of these interactions (∼2.99 Å) is significantly shorter than the sum of the van der Waals radii of two sulfur atoms. This robust interaction minimizes the torsional angle between the two thiophene rings to less than 1°, establishing a highly planar conformation. This extreme planarity ensures optimal orbital coupling, facilitates photo-excitation, and induces a prominent redshift in the optical absorption edge. Consequently, it broadens the absorption spectrum and boosts panchromatic light harvesting, leading to a substantial increase in the JSC. Furthermore, the directional distribution of the FMOs—where the HOMO is heavily localized on the triphenylamine (TPA) donor and the LUMO on the acceptor—effectively optimizes the energy barrier for electron injection into the TiO2 photoanode. Compared to sulfur-free counterparts like the conventional dialkylbithiophene (BT) π-bridge, the SBT core demonstrates decisive engineering superiority. The absence of intramolecular sulfur–sulfur locks (tagged BT-3 in ref. 37) combined with steric hindrance causes severe twisting in the central π-bridge's spatial vector; density functional theory (DFT) calculations reveal an exceptionally high dihedral torsional angle of 64.8° for this configuration. This severe twist disrupts π-conjugation, widens the HOMO–LUMO energy gap, and limits the overall efficiency. Conversely, the introduction of the thioalkyl (–SR) group in the SBT geometry maintains an effectively planar surface (reducing the torsional angle to approximately 11.3°), elevates the HOMO energy level, and alters the molecular packing mode on the photoanode surface to suppress detrimental dye aggregation. This intelligent steric hindrance, paired with the rapid regeneration kinetics of the oxidized dye by the redox mediator, effectively suppresses the CRR at the TiO2/dye/electrolyte interface and extends the τe. This kinetic suppression is the primary driver behind the outstanding photovoltaic performance of the dye 28, which achieves a power conversion efficiency of 9.47% under standard solar simulated light and an impressive 23.57% under T5 (900–6000 lx) indoor light sources. For 29 with a bithiophene unit and longer peripheral chains, a higher PCE than that of 31 was obtained, in which there is uncertainty in finding structural patterns for TAA dyes.37

Fig. 3A–F provide a holistic overview of the PCE and the corresponding photovoltaic metrics across the investigated dye library. Although the optoelectronic response is highly sensitive to the specific molecular architecture of each sensitizer, their evolutionary pathways reveal clear empirical boundary limits; notably, the JSC, VOC, and FF continuously saturate below empirical thresholds of ∼20 mA cm−2, ∼0.8 V, and ∼0.76, respectively. A cross-examination of the PCE and JSC profiles reveals a striking topological similarity, confirming that photocurrent generation remains the primary performance-determining factor. Under standard AM 1.5G illumination, every 100 nm of spectral bandwidth within the 400–900 nm solar window theoretically yields ∼7 mA cm−2, culminating in an upper theoretical limit of ∼35 mA cm−2 for an ideal panchromatic absorber. This indicates that while structural optimization of the dye can theoretically push the ultimate PCE toward a ∼30% thermodynamic ceiling for single-junction devices, current TAA sensitizers face significant bottlenecks. Rather than being bound by mathematical ideals, the maximization of the VOC and FF is fundamentally governed by the thermodynamic separation between the quasi-Fermi level of the TiO2 conduction band and the Nernst potential of the redox electrolyte. Structural engineering of the π-bridge must therefore resolve the interfacial energetic trade-offs, as extending conjugation often narrows the electrochemical window and induces unwanted energetic offsets. From a forward-looking perspective, the precise impact of π-bridge topology on the quantum efficiencies of electron injection and dye regeneration remains an open, critical question. Extended π-conjugated heterocyclic spacers effectively redshift the absorption profile, yet the λmax of these TAA derivatives rarely exceeds ∼550 nm, leaving a vast portion of the red-to-NIR spectrum unharvested. Furthermore, the severe geometric relaxation of flexible π-linkers in the excited state (S1) induces a large Stokes shift, causing significant vibrational energy loss that diminishes the effective photopotential. The recorded molar extinction coefficients (ε < 105 M−1 cm−1) reflect suboptimal ICT and electronic transitions compared to ideal quantum systems, suggesting that TAA dyes are fundamentally constrained by strict quantum mechanical selection rules. To unlock near-unity photon-to-electron conversion, an ideal sensitizer must bypass spin-dependent restrictions, multi-electron excitation penalties, and molecular symmetry-driven forbidden transitions that currently quench the excited state. Simultaneously, structural trade-offs persist: the incorporation of hydrophobic alkyl chains to prevent low solubility and detrimental self-aggregation often introduces steric barriers, while intermolecular interactions among closely packed dyes can trigger a local Stark effect, shifting the TiO2 conduction band edge and altering the VOC dynamics. Moreover, the integration of heavy heteroatoms can inadvertently promote CRR pathways within the electrolyte. Therefore, breaking through the current performance ceiling demands a rational design that transcends traditional linear linkers, focusing instead on quantum-engineered π-bridges capable of manipulating transition dipole moments without compromising interfacial charge-transfer kinetics.

Donor modification

The TAA donor core exerts a potent electron-donating capability, primarily driven by the localized distribution of its HOMOs across the conjugated aromatic system. Centered around a trivalent nitrogen atom with an accessible non-bonding (n) lone pair, TAA donors facilitate highly efficient n → σ* and π → π* ICT transitions. This intrinsic electronic configuration significantly enhances the ε and induces a pronounced bathochromic shift, thereby broadening the light-harvesting spectrum into the NIR region. Crucially, the quantum injection efficiency (ϕinj) of TAA-based sensitizers can be finely tuned via systematic structural functionalization of the donor core. Strategies such as alkylation, alkoxylation, arylation, and aryloxylation, as well as extended π-conjugation—including hybridization with porphyrin macrocycles—effectively modulate the frontier molecular orbital energy levels to accelerate charge injection kinetics. Furthermore, precise structural modifications introduce crucial steric hindrance around the triarylamine core. This spatial shielding suppresses the interfacial CRR between the injected electrons in the TiO2 conduction band and the oxidized electrolyte species, thereby prolonging electron lifetimes. Ultimately, these structural iterations offer deep control over dye-loading density, self-aggregation tendencies, and long-term thermal and photochemical stability on the semiconductor surface.

Alkoxy-modified donors

Introducing alkoxy substitutions into the TAA donor core represents a strategic approach to enhancing the PCE. This modification fulfills several photophysical objectives, including imparting a strong positive inductive effect (+I), inducing an up-shift in both the HOMO and LUMO energy levels, narrowing the zero–zero excitation energy (E0–0), and consequently broadening the LHE spectrum. Furthermore, the amphiphilic or bulky nature of alkoxy chains effectively suppresses unwanted dye–electrolyte interactions, thereby inhibiting the CRR and promoting a higher VOC. To date, various families of alkoxy-functionalized sensitizers have been developed; their molecular structures, along with key photovoltaic and optoelectronic parameters, are comprehensively summarized in Table 2 and systematically illustrated in Fig. 4. In this regard, structural comparison of 5 and 6 with 32–35 as hexyloxy-substituted TAA dyes43–45 confirmed the highest PCE for 35 (8.06%) and enhanced VOC for 32 and 33 (0.741 to 0.801 V) compared to 5 and 7 (0.61 to 0.685 V). To gain a deeper understanding of the structure–property relationships in these sensitizers, Liu et al.44 systematically investigated a series of push–pull dyes (32–35), featuring an electron-rich hexyloxy-substituted TAA donor bridged by oligothiophene chains of varying lengths (n = 1–4). Their insightful interfacial kinetic and energetic analyses revealed that the presence of the hexyloxy-functionalized TAA donor, in combination with the conjugated bridge, ensures an ultrafast electron injection rate into the TiO2 conduction band (>2 × 1012 s−1) with an efficiency close to unity. Furthermore, nanosecond transient absorption measurements demonstrated that while lengthening the oligothiophene linker narrows the energy gap and systematically enhances the JSC, it simultaneously induces a downward shift of the TiO2 conduction band edge and alters the interfacial recombination order. This comprehensive approach highlights that the impressive PCE (up to ∼8% for 35) is not merely a consequence of light harvesting but is deeply rooted in the excellent net charge separation yields governed by a delicate balance between the Marcus-normal dye regeneration pathway and suppressed back-electron transfer at the TiO2/dye/electrolyte interface. Complementing these interfacial kinetic insights, pioneering work by Yum et al. with the benchmark alkoxyl-functionalized sensitizer (35, tagged as D21L6 dye in ref. 46) illuminated the profound role of donor-targeted alkoxylation in resolving the historical trade-off between PCE and long-term device durability. By incorporating dual hydrophobic hexyloxy chains onto the TPA core, this molecular architecture introduces a powerful stereoelectronic shield at the semiconductor interface. This spatial insulation effectively suppresses deleterious dye self-aggregation while simultaneously sealing the nanostructured TiO2 surface against unwanted back-electron transfer to the electrolyte I3− species. Consequently, the D21L6-based devices achieved outstanding photovoltaic performances, yielding an impressive VOC of up to 798 mV in solid-state configurations due to a drastically minimized dark current. Crucially, under accelerated thermal and light-stress aging protocols (1000 h at 60 °C), the alkoxylation strategy demonstrated unprecedented structural and chemical robustness, retaining over 90% of its initial photovoltaic efficiency and clocking an exceptional turnover number (TON) of approximately 4.69 × 106. These combined findings firmly establish that the strategic positioning of alkoxy substituents on the TAA donor core serves as a dual-functional paradigm—simultaneously optimizing the energetic positioning for charge extraction and providing the robust interfacial passivating barrier required for practical, high-endurance photovoltaic applications. Following these structural modifications, the impact on the photovoltaic performance of the solar cells is directly reflected in the device output parameters. Current density–voltage (J–V) evaluations demonstrate that the dye 38, with its two butoxy groups, achieves the highest JSC due to its broader absorption spectrum and enhanced light-harvesting capability. Furthermore, the effective steric hindrance provided by these alkyl chains suppresses charge recombination, allowing 38 to attain the highest VOC. In contrast, as the number of butoxy groups decreases in 37, and when they are completely removed in the reference dye 36, the CRR rate of injected electrons in the TiO2 conduction band with the oxidized species in the electrolyte increases significantly. This undesirable pathway leads to a noticeable drop in both the VOC and the FF. Overall, this upward trend in charge-transfer efficiency and blocking-layer stability yields the highest PCE for the dye 38 compared to its counterparts, proving the vital role of side-chain engineering in optimizing DSSCs.47 In this context, engineering organic D–π–A sensitizers with varying alkoxy substituents dictates their energetic alignment and photovoltaic kinetics. Focusing on a thienothiophene π-bridge, the introduction of stronger electron-donating methoxy groups sequentially narrows the optical bandgap by significantly destabilizing the HOMO level—shifting it from a deeper potential to a more isolated, higher energy state—while leaving the LUMO largely unperturbed and safely anchored above the conduction band. This electronic modulation enables a systematic redshift in the absorption threshold, enhancing the spectral harvesting across the visible region and substantially increasing the JSC. More remarkably, replacing standard, spatially unhindered methoxy groups with bulkier, long-chain hexyloxy aliphatic chains (39) introduces a profound steric alteration. These prolonged alkyl networks act as an optimized, hydrophobic physical barrier that effectively encapsulates the semiconductor surface. This targeted molecular engineering passivates vulnerable surface states below the TiO2 conduction band edge and establishes an insulating organic shield, which systematically slows down the interfacial CRR with the I3− species in the electrolyte. Electrochemical impedance spectroscopy (EIS) further confirms that this structural blocking effect drastically increases the Rct at the dark interface while shifting the flat-band potential, which directly expands the obtainable VOC. Furthermore, the localized steric hindrance prevents detrimental intermolecular π–π stacking and self-quenching on the film, ensuring an ordered self-assembled monolayer. Consequently, the dye 39 exhibits a prolonged τn, an accelerated electron diffusion coefficient, and a maximized charge collection yield that approaches unity, culminating in an impressive PCE of 7.05% in solvent-free device configurations, while offering superior long-term photochemical stability against dye desorption.48 Building upon the success of the hexyloxy-functionalized TAA core, further structural elongation of the conjugated pathway by incorporating a rigid bisthienothiophene π-bridge offers an evolutionary leap in both light-harvesting capability and device robustness. This coplanar and highly extended π-system substantially broadens the molecular absorption profile deeper into the lower-energy visible spectrum and dramatically boosts the ε from 42 × 103 M−1 cm−1 for 39 to 47 × 103 M−1 cm−1 for 40. The enhanced planarity of the bisthienothiophene framework, coupled with the dense packing enforced by the peripheral hexyloxy chains, yields a highly organized and compact self-assembled monolayer on the TiO2 surface. This synergistic arrangement provides a dual-layer defense mechanism where the bulky aliphatic chains seal the interface horizontally, while the extended, rigid aromatics reinforce the vertical spacer plane, drastically suppressing dark current and retarding back-electron transfer. Consequently, these optimized interfacial kinetics translate into a direct boost in photovoltaic performance, driving the JSC up to an impressive 15.2 mA cm−2 and culminating in an enhanced overall PCE of 8.02%. Remarkably, when implemented in solvent-free ionic liquid devices, this molecular refinement strategy demonstrates exceptional long-term thermal and photochemical resistance, retaining over 92% of its initial efficiency after 1000 hours of continuous accelerated aging at 60 °C—firmly establishing bisthienothiophene-bridged chromophores as highly viable candidates for rigorous, outdoor solar applications. A further monumental leap was achieved with the design of the sensitizer 41, featuring a binary π-conjugated spacer of orderly coupled EDOT and thienothiophene units. This specific molecular engineering narrows the optical bandgap, shifting the absorption peak bathochromically by 36 nm compared to 40 and 39. Crucially, EDOT maintains a remarkably small torsion angle with respect to the adjoining phenyl fragment, ensuring highly efficient electronic communication between the donor and the acceptor. This optimal configuration idealizes the spatial orientation by populating the HOMO over the TAA–EDOT region and delocalizing the LUMO through the cyanoacrylic acid–thienothiophene group, which facilitate ultrafast interfacial electron injection while suppressing the CRR. Consequently, these superior kinetics drive the JSC to an impressive 16.1 mA cm−2, culminating in a landmark overall PCE of 9.8% under AM 1.5G sunlight alongside outstanding long-term operational stability.49–51 To further advance this molecular paradigm, the architectural evolution progressed to the design of the sensitizers 42–46, where the binary π-conjugated framework was refined by swapping the thienothiophene unit with a dihexyl-substituted dithienosilole (DTS) block adjacent to the EDOT segment (46). This strategic integration elegantly capitalizes on the dual benefits of targeted donor alkoxylation and side-chain engineering within the spacer core. The tetrahedral silicon center of the DTS π-bridge projects two rigid, outward-pointing dihexyl chains that work in perfect spatial harmony with the peripheral hexyloxy substituents of the TAA donor. This three-dimensional steric arrangement forms a remarkably dense, hydrophobic encapsulating shield over the π-conjugated backbone without disrupting its coplanarity. Consequently, it yields several enhanced photovoltaic characteristics: it nearly fully passivates surface CRR states, significantly retards back-electron transfer to the electrolyte, and completely suppresses detrimental intermolecular π–π stacking. Furthermore, this electronic modulation shifts the ICT band bathochromically, pushing the ε to a magnificent 57.5× 103 M−1 cm−1 at 493 nm. These optimized interfacial charge kinetics and superior light-harvesting features break the historical 10% efficiency barrier for metal-free organic DSSCs, achieving a landmark PCE of up to 10.3% under AM 1.5G sunlight while maintaining an exceptional long-term thermal retention of over 93% after 1000 hours of continuous stress at 60 °C. To push the limits of this structural evolution, the final architectural paradigm shifts from simple side-chain adjustments to the rational modulation of the connection order within hetero-binary π-conjugated bridges. As demonstrated by Cai et al., strategically swapping the sequence of electron-rich segments—such as EDOT, bithiophene (BT), and cyclopentadithiophene (CPDT)—relative to the TAA donor and cyanoacrylic acid acceptor dictates the FMO topology. When a highly planar, electron-rich block like EDOT or CPDT is positioned immediately adjacent to the donor, it selectively destabilizes the ground-state redox potential, narrowing the optical gap and shifting the absorption profile bathochromically without compromising the driving force for ultrafast electron injection. Concurrently, this precise directional alignment coordinates with the peripheral hexyloxy networks to alter the spatial arrangement of the self-assembled monolayer. This advanced steric configuration systematically controls the multichannel charge transfer kinetics by suppressing back-electron transfer to the electrolyte while significantly extending the effective electron lifetime within the TiO2 conduction band. Consequently, this refined strategy of managing segment sequence and hetero-atom connectivity optimizes the balance between broadband photocurrent generation and photovoltage enhancement, proving that the internal arrangement of binary spacers is as critical as their chemical composition for maximizing the performance of DSSCs.52,53 As an ultimate refinement to this structural progression, the strategy advances from altering the block sequence to the rigorous rigidification of the π-conjugated segments. Regulating the conformational freedom of flexible bithiophene linkers by locking them into coplanar, rigidified motifs—such as CPDT, dithienosilole and dithienopyrrole (DTP) for 47–50—induces a profound impact on the interfacial photophysics, as thoroughly elucidated by Xu et al. This covalent planarization dramatically eliminates the torsional energy barriers inherent in free-rotating thiophene joints, which drastically enhances the electronic delocalization and boosts the ε. Consequently, the rigid binary or ternary spacer frameworks systematically destabilize the HOMO levels while maintaining a well-positioned LUMO, narrowing the optical gap to capture low-energy photons. More importantly, this planar rigidification works in perfect spatial synergy with the peripheral hexyloxy networks of the TAA donor to dictate the self-assembled monolayer's packing density. By enforcing a highly ordered, tightly packed organic shield on the TiO2 surface, it establishes a severe physical barrier that suppresses dark current and curtails back-electron transfer to the tris(1,10-phenanthroline)cobalt(ii/iii) electrolyte. This multi-layered strategy optimizes the balance between broadband photocurrent generation and VOC retention, proving that structural rigidification is the ultimate key to maximizing both the efficiency and long-term operational durability of metal-free organic photosensitizers.54 To culminate this comprehensive structural engineering, the design paradigm advances from a single rigidified block to the strategic duplication of extended, highly bulky motifs within the spacer architecture. As pioneered by Zhang et al., constructing non-metal organic sensitizers (51–52) featuring a continuous 4H,4′H-2,2′-bidithieno[3,2-b:2′,3′-d]pyrrole linker introduces an unparalleled dual-shielding mechanism that exerts a profound impact on the device's photovoltaic parameters. By sequentially linking two rigid, nitrogen-functionalized DTP units, this configuration achieves an exceptionally minimized torsional angle and near-perfect coplanarity across the extended π-bridge. This structural elongation induces a deep bathochromic shift and a massive enhancement in the ε, which directly drives a substantial increase in the JSC by maximizing photon harvesting across the low-energy spectrum. Therefore, the multiple bulky aliphatic chains on the twin DTP units synergize with the peripheral networks of the donor to form a dense, multi-layered hydrophobic envelope over the TiO2 surface. This spatial insulation nearly completely suppresses harmful dark current and decelerates back-electron recombination with the electrolyte, thereby inducing a dramatic upswing in the VOC. Ultimately, this supreme integration of binary rigidification and doubled steric hindrance delivers an optimized balance between the JSC and VOC, translating into an exceptional PCE and establishing a definitive masterclass for next-generation mesoscopic photosensitizers.55 To reach the pinnacle of this structural evolution, the engineering paradigm transitions to the utilization of highly extended, multi-fused hetero-aromatic π-bridge combined with advanced redox couples. As thoroughly investigated by Cai et al., deploying a massive, rigidified cyclopenta[1,2-b:5,4-b′]dithiophene[2′,1′:4,5]thieno[2,3-d]thiophene framework substituted with 4-hexylphenyl groups establishes an ultimate benchmark in molecular planarization (53–54). This extensive fused-ring architecture completely suppresses structural relaxation, narrowing the optical energy gap and significantly enhancing the ε to achieve superior panchromatic light absorption. Crucially, when evaluated with a bulky tris(2,2′-bipyridine)cobalt(ii/iii) redox electrolyte, this specific molecular engineering dictates the formation of a highly favored, well-ordered microscopic dye assembly on the mesoscopic TiO2 surface. The spatial orientation of the outward-pointing 4-hexylphenyl segments works in magnificent synergy with the TAA donor's peripheral networks, forming an absolute, pinhole-free insulating shield. This multi-layered steric obstacle nearly completely prevents the bulky cobalt complexes from approaching the semiconductor surface, systematically reducing dark current and suppressing back-electron transfer. Consequently, this refined strategy perfectly balances an extended JSC with a dramatic upswing in the VOC, proving that fusing complex multi-cyclic aromatics is the definitive pathway for breaking efficiency barriers in next-generation cobalt-mediated DSSCs.56

Summary of optical, electrochemical, photovoltaic and thermodynamic data for the dyes 32–81 and related DSSCs

Hexyloxy-modified TAA dye structures.

To culminate this comprehensive structural continuum, the design paradigm is advanced from a single rigidified block to the strategic duplication of an extended, highly bulky π-bridge within the spacer architecture. As pioneered by Zhang et al., constructing non-metal organic sensitizers (55–57) featuring a continuous 4H,4′H-2,2′-bidithieno[3,2-b:2′,3′-d]pyrrole linker introduces an unparalleled dual-shielding mechanism. By sequentially linking two rigid, nitrogen-functionalized DTP units, this configuration achieves an exceptionally minimized torsional angle and near-perfect coplanarity across the entire extended π-bridge. This structural elongation induces a profound bathochromic shift in the absorption maximum and yields an outstandingly high ε, drastically expanding the low-energy light-harvesting capacity. Crucially, the presence of multiple, bulky aliphatic chains attached to the nitrogen centers of the twin DTP units works in superb spatial synergy with the peripheral hexyloxy networks of the TAA donor. This dense, multi-layered organic insulation acts as an ideal hydrophobic envelope over the TiO2 surface, which nearly completely suppresses the dark current and exponentially extends the photoinjected electron lifetime within the semiconductor conduction band by decelerating the back-electron transfer to the electrolyte. Consequently, these optimized interfacial kinetics translate into a direct boost in the device's photovoltaic parameters, driving the JSC and VOC up simultaneously and completing the evolutionary path toward high-performance dyes.58,67 To reach the ultimate sophistication in energy-level and kinetic engineering, the evolutionary trajectory transitions from macro-cyclic rigidification to the precise minimization of inter-ring torsion angles via intramolecular non-covalent interactions. As comprehensively decoded by Hua et al., introducing conformational locks—such as S⋯O, H⋯O, or S⋯F chalcogen bonds—between adjoining hetero-aromatic segments in the π-bridge (phenothiazine-, bithiophene- and 3-hexylthiophene-based conjugated units, 58–59, respectively) establishes a planarized, self-locked spatial arrangement. This strategic minimizing of the dihedral torsion angle to near 0° eliminates planarization energy barriers, maximizing electronic delocalization across the entire push–pull framework and inducing a massive enhancement in the molar extinction coefficient. So, this ultra-planar configuration populates the HOMO over the donor-linker zone while shifting the LUMO smoothly toward the cyanoacrylic acid anchor, optimizing the driving force for directional, ultrafast charge injection. Therefore, the minimized torsion angle restricts molecular vibrations and structural relaxation in the excited state, which dramatically extend the lifetime of photoinjected electrons within the TiO2 conduction band by forming a seamless, pinhole-free molecular monolayer that blocks electrolyte access. This non-covalent planarization strategy effectively circumvents the synthetic complexities of covalent cyclic ring-closure, offering a pristine molecular design rule where secondary atomic interactions completely suppress kinetic loss channels.68 Building upon the concept of controlling inter-ring dihedral angles via secondary atomic interactions, a parallel breakthrough shifts the focus toward managing the macro-scale supramolecular arrangements on the semiconductor surface. A major bottleneck in exploiting wide-spectral organic dyes is the severe blueshift and emission quenching induced by detrimental H-aggregation upon TiO2 adsorption, which often undermines the electronic benefits of planarization. To bypass this limitation, Wang and coworkers engineered 60 by integrating a benzothiazole–cyclopentadithiophene auxiliary core within a refined D–A–π–A network. The true innovation of this configuration lies in its ability to constrain the post-adsorption hypsochromic displacement to a mere 20 nm, in stark contrast to the 51 nm shift exhibited by its unoptimized analogues. This unique molecular arrangement effectively preserves low-energy photon harvesting, maintaining a broad visible response that spans up to 800 nm, with a magnificent ε of 5.6 × 104 M−1 cm−1 at λmax = 556 nm. This anti-aggregation geometry induces an ideal spatial topology, driving an ultrafast photoinduced electron injection into the semiconductor conduction band within approximately 3 fs. When coupled with a [Co(bpy)3]2+/3+-based redox mediator, the dye achieves a near-unity regeneration efficiency exceeding 99.9%. Ultimately, this structural suppression of intermolecular optical loss pathways yields a powerful leap in device performance, enabling the 60-based cell to easily surpass the 9% PCE threshold.60 To selectively harvest the energetic benefits of auxiliary acceptor frameworks without sacrificing electronic delocalization, the architectural evolution highlights the exclusive advantages of the optimized configuration of 61. By systematically displacing the aliphatic chains from the quinoxaline spacer directly onto the peripheral triarylamine donor, this specific molecular engineering completely eliminates the severe steric twisting inherent in its structural analogues. This targeted structural planarization induces a nearly coplanar conformation across the push–pull framework, driving an extraordinary 160 nm bathochromic shift in the intramolecular charge transfer band and drastically broadening the visible light response. So, the quinoxaline core maintains an elevated LUMO level at approximately −1.05 V vs. NHE, providing an exceptionally robust thermodynamic driving force for ultrafast electron injection into the TiO2 conduction band. This superior spatial orbital alignment, combined with the enhanced molar extinction coefficient of the planarized backbone, elevates the JSC to a remarkable maximum of 16.16 mA cm−2. Furthermore, the hexyl-stabilized packing architecture of 61 forms an efficient topographic barrier that suppresses interfacial dark current and works in superb compatibility with gel networks. This refined molecular design translates into a superior 8.27% PCE in liquid-junction devices, while demonstrating a groundbreaking operational durability by retaining 100% of its initial 7.14% efficiency after 1000 hours of continuous light-soaking stress in a quasi-solid-state configuration.54 To complete this structural development, Lan et al. introduced a D–π–A′–π–A architecture by incorporating auxiliary EDOT units as extended π-conjugated bridges into the quinoxaline core. In the 62 configuration, this insertion elongates the intramolecular charge transfer pathway and expands visible light absorption, yields a 8.2% PCE in quasi-solid-state devices. Their solar cell analysis indicates that while the EDOT spacer broadens the spectral response, terminal group selection dictates the final performance; replacing the standard cyanoacrylic acid with a rhodanine-3-acetic acid anchoring group decouples the LUMO alignment at the TiO2 interface, which limits electron injection. The 62 assembly demonstrates that pairing a quinoxaline core with an EDOT spacer optimizes spatial topology for directional charge transfer, suppresses interfacial recombination, and retains 99% of its initial photovoltaic output after 1000 hours of continuous light-soaking.26 Further advancing this auxiliary acceptor paradigm, Jia et al. introduced the fluorinated quinoxaline-based sensitizer, 63, to successfully downshift the optical band gap via enhanced push–pull interactions. Incorporating a 6,7-difluoroquinoxaline core substituted with twin phenyl rings induces a notable bathochromic shift, shifting the absorption maximum to 525 nm in toluene and 501 nm. Despite the strong EW nature of the fluorine substituents, 63 maintains a well-positioned LUMO level at −0.91 V vs. NHE, ensuring a powerful thermodynamic driving force for efficient charge injection. When incorporated into a quasi-solid-state device, the structural shield provided by the bulky peripheral phenyl groups effectively retards interfacial charge recombination, yielding a remarkable 37% enhancement in performance over its fluorine-free analogue. This optimized configuration achieves a superior PCE of 8.2% (JSC = 17.76 mA cm−2; VOC = 0.676 V) while demonstrating exceptional device longevity by retaining 98% of its initial efficiency after 1000 hours of continuous light-soaking stress.26,35,54,60 Diketopyrrolopyrroles were first introduced by Song and Gratzel et al. as EW π-bridges (64–65) for tert-butyl- and hexyloxy-modified TAA dyes, which represented PCEs of 8.16 and 8.74%, respectively.62,69N-Annulated perylene π-bridges provide advantages such as ease of synthesis, powerful optical properties, more reliability for charge transfer as well as high stability against low-frequency photons for hexyloxy-based TAA dyes. In this regard, 66 and 67 provided thinner E0–0 and enhanced ε. The tilt angle of 67 derived from XRR is smaller that could be affected by the anchor coordination model which caused fast CRRs and a low VOC. The PCEs of 66 and 67 were measured to be 9.0 and 8.6%, respectively.1,70,71 Employing the heptathiophene π-bridge (68) with butyloxy chain substituents on a TAA donor was proposed by Zhan et al.63 The photovoltaic study of this dye showed a PCE of 8.06% that achieved a diminished CRR in comparison to the reference dye without the oligothiophene π-bridge (7.30%) (Fig. 5).72

Structural modification of 53 by adding a BT group between thiophene rings in various positions on the π-bridge and changing the acceptor from cyanoacrylic acid to benzoic acid of hexyloxy-substituted TPA dyes generated the dyes 69–72. It is well known that the water stability of dyes depends on the hydrophobicity of donors strongly. Accordingly, 75 and 76 showed the best stability due to more hydrophobicity of donor substituents.65 In this respect, 77–81 were designed by Cheng et al. by upgrading 58 with hexyloxy substitution of a phenyl group in the spacer and BT π-bridge as well as changing the acceptor to meta-substituted types by targeting strong ICT and broad absorption profiles. Aryloxy-modified donors

Functionalizing the donor of TAA dyes with massive aryl groups has an impact on amending the solubility of dyes, improving the ability of dyes to prevent aggregation and decrease the rate of CRRs. In this regard, an upgrade on 32 by incorporating a 2,4-dibutyloxyphenyl π-bridge provided 82. The correlation of steric hindrance of the bulky π-bridge with the rate of CRRs and a PCE of 6.7% were the main results of this study.73,74 In these types of dyes, 83 with the bithiophene π-bridge (Fig. 6 and Table 3) provided a larger bathochromic shift than 82 as well as more positive influence on PCE83 (4.2%).75 Aryloxy-modified TAA dyes' potential for application in aqueous DSSCs render some competitive benefits compared to other acetonitrile-based electrolytes. These aqueous DSSCs are benefited to low risk of flammability, controllable viscosity related obstacles of electrolyte about leakage, as important as high environmental compliance and affordability For this aim, 84 and 85 were designed by inclusion of alkoxy and 2,4-dimethyloxyphenyl π-bridges with potential for application in aqueous DSSCs.54 A bathochromic shift, higher LHE, and distinguished JSC and PCE (3.5 and 2.6%) were obtained for 85 compared to 84, which was fabricated from TT−EMI+/DTT aqueous electrolyte (TT−EMI+ = 1-ethyl-3-methylimidazolium-4-methyl-1,2,4-triazole-3-thiolate; DTT = 3,3′-dithiobis(4-methyl-(1,2,4)-triazole)). Porphyrins

Chlorophylls, as natural-based porphyrins, are the heart of photosystems in the photosynthetic process and due to their outstanding properties have garnered much attention in the dye design field. In contrast to many disadvantages that might be found in other types of sensitizers, porphyrins provide several rare and inaccessible advantages that make them excellent options for molecular optimization of dyes to achieve the highest ranking PCEs.53,89 These properties include: (1) an outlandish high ε assigned to their quasi-circular molecular building blocks, (2) great stability, (3) ease of synthesis, and finally the most important one being (4) light absorption in excited states. A list of representative TAA–porphyrin dyes and their vital information can be found in Table 4 and Fig. 8.90103 has octyloxy groups that play the role of an inhibitor of the CRRs and unwanted aggregations and achieved the top rank for PCE (11.9%) and other photovoltaic parameters (JSC, 17.3 mA cm−2; VOC, 0.965 V; FF, 0.71) . Based on UV-vis spectroscopy and incident photon-to-current efficiency (IPCE) analysis, this dye showed a perfect absorption profile in the presence of 86 as a cosensitizer, and photovoltaic parameters became even better (JSC, 17.66 mA cm−2; VOC, 0.935 V; FF, 0.74; PCE, 12.3%). Conclusion and perspective

In summary, this comprehensive review underscores that the architectural evolution of triarylamine (TAA)-based organic sensitizers has systematically advanced them to the forefront of metal-free photovoltaic technologies. The distinct structural merit of the TAA donor core lies in its inherent three-dimensional propeller-like conformation, which uniquely destabilizes the close intermolecular face-to-face packing. This intrinsic geometric feature provides a natural defense against the formation of non-luminescent π–π aggregates on semiconductor surfaces, which are notorious for opening up energy-dissipative, non-radiative recombination pathways. By shifting away from empirical trial-and-error methodologies, molecular engineering has refined the modular D–π–A and D–A–π–A frameworks into highly predictable electronic systems. A deep analysis of the internal mechanics of these frameworks reveals that the optimization of power conversion efficiency (PCE) is governed by a delicate, multi-dimensional trade-off between light harvesting, energetic driving forces, and interfacial charge transfer kinetics. Manipulating the π-conjugated bridges—through the insertion of rigidified thiophene oligomers, fused heterocyclic spacers, or electron-deficient auxiliary acceptors—effectively tailors the molecular orbital hybridization. This structural tuning narrows the optical bandgap and shifts the absorption profile toward the red and near-infrared (NIR) spectral regions, substantially amplifying the short-circuit current density (JSC). However, this extended conjugation simultaneously dictates the physical topography, spatial orientation, and ultimate packing density of the self-assembled monolayer (SAM). Concurrently, strategic peripheral functionalization of the TAA core using long-chain alkoxy, aryloxy, or bulky branched alkyl groups serves a dual purpose. Chronologically, it maintains the thermodynamic positioning of the highest occupied molecular orbital (HOMO) to ensure a sufficient driving force for dye regeneration by redox mediators. Spatially, it forms a dense, continuous hydrophobic insulating envelope directly above the semiconductor surface. This microscopic barrier passivates active TiO2 surface defects and physically shields injected electrons from back-transfer to the electrolyte, suppressing dark current and maximizing the open-circuit voltage (VOC).117,118 Ultimately, the optimization of TAA sensitizers is not defined by any single structural component, but rather by the precise, cooperative synergy between electronic alignment and spatial interfacial passivation.

To address the current performance bottlenecks and provide concrete pathways for next-generation TAA-based photovoltaics, future research must focus on the following core directives:

• Sustained NIR harvesting without recombination penalties: expanding the light-harvesting capability of TAA dyes into the near-infrared (NIR) region by conjugating to the BODIPY dimers that provides low energy excitation through symmetry-breaking charge separation119,120 remains a primary objective. However, conventional narrow-bandgap designs such as tetracoordinate-boron-rigidified heterohelicenes121 often induce intense non-radiative decay or low-lying excited states that drastically accelerate charge recombination reactions (CRRs) with the electrolyte. Future molecular designs must break this trade-off by decoupling optical narrowing from interfacial kinetics. This can be achieved by employing asymmetric auxiliary acceptors or localized non-covalent conformational locks (e.g., S⋯O or Se⋯O interactions) that enforce structural planarity and high molar extinction coefficients in the NIR region without dragging down the LUMO level below the injection threshold.

• Dynamic control of adsorption geometry and spatial aggregation: suppressing detrimental intermolecular π–π aggregation requires more than just appending bulky alkyl chains such as umbrella-shaped m-terphenyl groups, which can be incorporated into the TAA donor and act as a redox shuttle blocker against charge recombination.122 Future engineering must focus on the precise spatial control of the dye's anchoring geometry and tilt angles on the TiO2 facets by using an anthranil core that has been reported by the research group of professor Emilio Palomares at ICIQ.123 Developing rigid, multi-foot anchoring groups (such as ferrocenes,124 silatranes or dual-functionalized phosphonates) can lock the molecules in an optimal vertical configuration. This ensures maximum electronic coupling for injection while establishing a flawless, dense hydrophobic shield that prevents redox mediators from penetrating the semiconductor surface, thereby inhibiting dark current.

• Synergistic compatibility with stable redox mediators: the transition from volatile iodine-based electrolytes to transition-metal coordination complexes (e.g., cobalt, copper, etc.) and solid-state hole transport materials (HTMs) is vital for long-term stability. However, these bulky mediators exhibit slower diffusion kinetics and severe recombination profiles with standard dyes.125 TAA sensitizers must be structurally redesigned to match the specific mass transport and regeneration kinetics of these stable mediators. This involves tailoring the outer periphery of the TAA donor with specific sterically hindered glycol or alkoxy chains that explicitly block the approach of bulky Co(iii) or Cu(ii) complexes to the TiO2 surface.

• High-throughput computational screening with experimental validation: the vast chemical space of TAA derivatives makes purely experimental screening inefficient. Future breakthroughs will rely on a closed-loop integration of high-throughput computational screening—utilizing automated density functional theory (DFT), molecular dynamics (MD) simulation methods, especially in Siesta software package, and machine learning (ML) models—with targeted experimental synthesis. Computations should not merely rationalize experimental data post hoc; instead, predictive models must be used first to screen millions of virtual D–A–π–A combinations for optimized parameters (such as low reorganization energy, precise tilt angles, and optimal binding energies) before entering the wet lab, thereby accelerating the discovery of pristine dye structures.

In summary, transitioning from descriptive cataloging to a property-driven, predictive structural blueprint will establish the necessary foundation to suppress interfacial losses, enhance photochemical durability, and unlock the next generation of highly efficient, commercially viable TAA-based ambient energy-harvesting technologies.
