The effectiveness of mandala coloring on the psychological and physiological symptoms in adult hospitalized patients: a meta-analysis
Authors: Seda Pehli̇van, Öznur Erbay Dalli
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundHospitalization often imposes significant psychological and physiological demands on patients. Mandala coloring may help reduce these challenges. This meta-analysis aimed to evaluate the effectiveness of mandala coloring interventions across psychological and physiological symptoms in adult hospitalized patients.MethodsA comprehensive search was conducted using nine databases. Randomized controlled and controlled studies comparing mandala coloring with control groups were included. Data extraction was performed independently by two authors, and risk of bias was assessed using the Cochrane RoB tool. GRADE Pro software was used to assess the quality of the evidence. Standardized mean differences (SMD) with 95% confidence intervals (CIs) were pooled using RevMan (version 5.4). Subgroup analyses were performed by session frequency and duration.ResultsSeventeen studies with a total of 987 participants were included. Mandala coloring significantly reduced anxiety (SMD = − 2.16, 95% CI [–3.38, − 0.95]) and stress (SMD = − 2.45, 95% CI [–3.06, − 1.85]). Improvements were also observed in well-being (SMD = 4.51, 95% CI [0.81, 8.21]) and hope (SMD = 0.42, 95% CI [0.09, 0.75]). No significant effects were found for resilience, pain, fatigue, nausea, comfort, or vital parameters. Subgroup analyses indicated that brief (≤ 30 min) and multiple-session interventions were especially effective in alleviating anxiety. The level of evidence, as assessed using GRADE, was, however, low.ConclusionMandala coloring provides nurses and healthcare professionals with an inexpensive, low-resource, and patient-friendly method to enhance psychological well-being during hospitalization. Incorporating short, repeated sessions into routine care may strengthen resilience and patient comfort. Further large-scale and high-quality trials are required to establish standardized protocols and clarify effects on physiological symptoms.Trial registration PROSPERO meta-analysis registration: CRD420251130565Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12906-026-05404-4.

Background

Hospitalization often imposes a dual burden of psychological and physiological challenges that can adversely affect patients’ recovery and overall quality of life [1, 2]. On the psychological side, uncertainty about the course of illness, disruption of daily routines, and the loss of personal autonomy frequently give rise to anxiety, stress, and depressive symptoms. At the same time, physiological symptoms such as pain, fatigue, nausea, and sleep disturbances are common, either as direct consequences of the underlying condition or as side effects of treatment [2, 3]. Although pharmacological treatments are commonly used to alleviate symptoms such as pain, nausea, or anxiety, their effectiveness can be limited and may sometimes be accompanied by side effects or interactions [4, 5]. For this reason, there has been increasing interest in supportive, non-pharmacological approaches that can help address patients’ psychological and physiological needs without adding to the pharmacological burden. Among these, creative and art-based methods have attracted attention for their potential to promote relaxation, enhance emotional regulation, and contribute to overall well-being in both healthy populations and patients [6–10].

Among the wide range of creative interventions, mandala coloring has received growing attention as a simple yet structured practice that can foster psychological stability [11, 12]. The act of coloring within repetitive, geometric designs is thought to promote attentional focus, mindfulness, and emotional regulation, thereby reducing anxiety and stress while enhancing positive affect [13, 14]. Unlike other forms of art therapy that often require trained therapists or extensive resources, mandala coloring is inexpensive, requires minimal supervision, and can be easily implemented at bedside [15]. These characteristics make it a particularly feasible option for hospitalized patients, offering a non-invasive strategy that can complement conventional care.

To date, systematic reviews and meta-analyses evaluating mandala coloring have been limited in number and scope. Existing syntheses have largely concentrated on psychological outcomes, particularly anxiety, and often did not extend their analyses to other relevant domains such as pain, fatigue, or physiological indicators [16, 17]. Moreover, several reviews have combined different populations, including healthy adults, children, and individuals with trauma-related conditions, thereby restricting the applicability of their conclusions to hospitalized adults [17–19]. These methodological and population-related limitations have hindered the ability to draw comprehensive and generalizable conclusions. Consequently, there remains a need for a rigorous meta-analytic synthesis that evaluates both psychological and physiological symptoms of mandala coloring in hospitalized adults while also exploring potential sources of heterogeneity through subgroup analyses.

The aim of this meta-analysis was to quantitatively synthesize the evidence on the effectiveness of mandala coloring interventions in hospitalized adults across psychological and physiological symptoms. By addressing the methodological limitations of previous reviews and incorporating findings from randomized controlled trials (RCTs) and controlled clinical trials (CCTs), this study seeks to provide a more comprehensive understanding of the therapeutic potential of mandala coloring in clinical populations.

Methods

Design

The present study was conducted as a meta-analysis. The reporting process was structured in line with the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses (PRISMA) guidelines (Supplementary File 1). To ensure methodological transparency, the study protocol was prospectively registered in the International Prospective Register of Systematic Reviews (PROSPERO) (CRD420251130565).

Search strategy

A comprehensive literature search was independently carried out by two authors between 14 and 22 August 2025, without applying language restrictions. The following electronic databases were examined: PubMed, Web of Science, Cochrane Library, CINAHL, PsycINFO, Embase, Scopus, and national databases (ULAKBIM and the Turkish Higher Education Thesis Database) to identify studies evaluating the effects of mandala coloring interventions in adult hospitalized patients.

The search strategy combined free-text keywords and controlled vocabulary terms related to mandala-based interventions (e.g., mandala coloring/painting/drawing, mandala art, mandala therapy, art-based mandala coloring), target populations (e.g., adult inpatients, hospitalized patients, surgical patients, medical patients), and relevant psychological and physiological symptoms (e.g., stress, anxiety, depression, well-being, hope, fatigue, pain, nausea, comfort). Boolean operators (AND, OR), truncation, and database-specific subject headings (e.g., MeSH in PubMed, CINAHL Headings, EMTREE in Embase) were used as appropriate. Detailed search strategies adapted for each database are presented in Supplementary File 2.

In addition, reference lists of included articles and related systematic reviews were manually screened to identify further eligible studies. Grey literature sources, including national theses and dissertations, were also searched. Any discrepancies in the search or study selection process were resolved through discussion, with consultation from an external reviewer when necessary.

Inclusion and exclusion criteria

The eligibility criteria for this systematic review were formulated in accordance with the PICOS framework, which considers Participants (P), Interventions (I), Comparators (C), Outcomes (O), and Study designs (S). This structured approach ensured a transparent and rigorous process for selecting studies relevant to the review objectives [20]. The detailed inclusion and exclusion criteria are summarized in Table 1. Both RCTs and CCTs were included to provide a comprehensive synthesis of the available evidence, particularly given the limited number of randomized studies in this emerging field. Regarding outcomes, anxiety and stress in this study refer to situational psychological responses to hospitalization rather than clinical anxiety disorders (e.g., generalized anxiety disorder, panic disorder, phobias) or chronic stress conditions (e.g., episodic, environmental).

Study selection and data extraction

All titles and abstracts retrieved through the search process were independently assessed by two authors. Duplicate references were identified and removed using Mendeley Reference Manager. In cases where either reviewer judged a record to be potentially relevant, the full text was obtained for further evaluation. Any discrepancies in study selection were resolved through discussion, and consultation with an external reviewer was sought when consensus could not be achieved. Additionally, inter-rater agreement was assessed using Cohen’s kappa, yielding values of 0.91 for title/abstract screening and 0.88 for full-text evaluation, indicating almost perfect agreement.

For data extraction, the Cochrane Collaboration’s standardized form entitled “Data collection form for intervention reviews: RCTs and non-RCTs” was employed [21]. The form was pilot tested with three randomly selected studies, and minor revisions were applied to enhance clarity and contextual relevance. Extracted information included: study characteristics (authors, year, country, language, design), participant details (sample size, age, gender), eligibility criteria, intervention characteristics (format of mandala coloring, facilitator, setting, frequency, duration, delivery method, content), comparator conditions, symptom measures (psychological and physiological symptoms, assessment tools, timing of assessments), methodological features (power analysis, intention-to-treat application), as well as adverse events, funding sources, and conflict of interest declarations. When data were missing or unclear, study authors were contacted for clarification.

Quality assessment

The methodological quality and potential risk of bias of the included studies were independently evaluated by two authors using the Cochrane Risk of Bias (RoB) tool [22]. The tool considers seven domains: random sequence generation, allocation concealment, blinding of participants and personnel, blinding of outcome assessment, incomplete outcome data, selective reporting, and other potential sources of bias. Each domain was rated as low, unclear, or high risk of bias according to the criteria outlined in the Cochrane Handbook.

Disagreements between reviewers were resolved through discussion, and if consensus was not achieved, an external reviewer was consulted. All judgments were made strictly on the basis of information available in the published reports, supplemented by study protocols or trial registrations when such sources were accessible.

Certainty of evidence

The overall certainty of evidence for each outcome was assessed using the Grading of Recommendations Assessment, Development and Evaluation (GRADE) approach [23]. This framework evaluates the confidence in pooled estimates across five domains: risk of bias, inconsistency, indirectness, imprecision, and other considerations (e.g., publication bias). Because the meta-analysis included both RCTs and CCTs, the initial level of evidence was determined according to the predominant study design contributing to each outcome. All evaluations were performed using the GRADEpro Guideline Development Tool.

Data synthesis

All statistical analyses were performed using Review Manager (RevMan, version 5.4). When essential data were missing, study authors were contacted for clarification; if no response was obtained, available data were estimated using the RevMan calculator whenever feasible.

For each outcome, pooled effect sizes were expressed as mean difference (MD) when studies applied identical measurement tools, or as standardized mean difference (SMD) when different scales were used. SMDs were evaluated using Cohen’s rule of thumb, whereby values around 0.2 indicate a small effect, values around 0.5 indicate a moderate effect, and values of 0.8 or above indicate a large effect [24]. All estimates were reported with 95% confidence intervals (CIs). In studies with multiple intervention or control arms, the data were split to avoid double-counting participants in the meta-analysis. When repeated measures were reported, the last post-intervention score was used for analysis.

Between-study heterogeneity was evaluated using Cochran’s Q test and Higgins’ I² statistic. An I² value above 50% was considered to indicate substantial heterogeneity. Random-effects models were employed when heterogeneity was significant, while fixed-effect models were applied otherwise. A p-value < 0.05 was deemed statistically significant. Results were illustrated using forest plots. When ten or more studies were available for an outcome, publication bias was examined visually through funnel plots.

We conducted subgroup analyses according to session frequency (single [SS] vs. multiple sessions [MS]) and intervention duration (≤ 30 vs. > 30 min) to evaluate the potential influence of intervention characteristics. These subgroup analyses were specified a priori, given the clinical and theoretical relevance of intervention dose in psychosocial therapies.

A sensitivity analysis was conducted by separating RCTs from CCTs to examine the robustness of the pooled estimates and evaluate the potential influence of study design on effect sizes.

Quantitative syntheses, including overall and subgroup meta-analyses, were conducted only when at least two eligible studies reported the same outcome. In cases where fewer than two studies were available, findings were described narratively.

Results

Study selection

The study selection process is illustrated in Fig. 1. A total of 523 records were identified through database searches (PubMed, Embase, Scopus, Cochrane Library, Web of Science, PsycINFO, CINAHL, ULAKBIM, and the Turkish Thesis Database), and an additional 58 records were retrieved from grey literature and other sources. After the removal of 342 duplicates, 181 records remained for screening. Following title and abstract screening, 138 records were excluded as they did not meet the eligibility criteria. The full texts of the remaining 43 reports were assessed for eligibility, of which 34 were excluded for not meeting the inclusion criteria. Ultimately, 9 studies from database searches and 8 from other sources were included, yielding a total of 17 studies in the qualitative and quantitative syntheses.

Across studies, the total sample was 987 participants (range 24–118 per study). The proportion of female participants ranged from 32.6% (S12) to 100% (e.g., S5, S9, S11, S16); one study did not report gender distribution (S14). Clinical populations included oncology (breast or gynecologic: S5, S9, S11, S14, S16; hematologic: S6, S15; various cancer: S10), hemodialysis (S7, S8, S12, S13, S17), psychiatry (S1), multiple sclerosis (S2), COVID-19 (S3), burn (S14), and surgical settings (S4).

The most frequently evaluated symptoms were anxiety (e.g., S2, S3, S5, S7, S9, S11, S14, S15, S17), stress (S2, S11, S15), well-being (S1, S13, S15), hopelessness/hope (S1, S11, S15), pain (S4, S9, S11, S14), comfort (S10, S12), resilience (S1, S8), nausea/vomiting (S10, S16), vital parameters (heart rate, blood pressure [BP]; S4, S11); additional outcomes included sleep quality (S6), coping (S13), satisfaction and waiting time (S11, S4), and multi-domain mood (S4) or quality of life (S16). The State-Trait Anxiety Inventory (STAI) was the most frequently applied tool for anxiety (S3, S5, S7, S9, S11, S15). Pain, stress and satisfaction were often measured using the Visual Analogue Scale (VAS) (S9, S11, S14). The Psychological Well-Being Scale (PWBS) was employed for well-being (S13, S15) (Table 2).

Most interventions employed mandala coloring (n = 10; S2, S3, S5, S6, S7, S8, S10, S13, S14, S17), followed by mandala art therapy (n = 4; S1, S11, S12, S16) (Table 3). Interventions were predominantly researcher-led (n = 15; S2–S10, S12–S17), while two were delivered by clinical/mandala-therapy personnel or teams (S1, S11). Delivery was face-to-face in all studies; format was individual in nearly all (n = 15; S3–S17).

Settings were hospital-based and varied: hemodialysis/dialysis units (n = 5; S7, S8, S12, S13, S17), CT units (S5, S10), oncology/gynecologic oncology/radiotherapy services (S9, S16), hematology (S6), internal medicine/infectious disease wards (S3), neuropsychiatry (S1), operating preparation room (S4), burn unit (S14), and bone marrow transplant unit (S15). Intervention “dose” ranged from 1 to 12 sessions. Six-session (S3, S7, S8, S11, S16) and eight-session formats (S1, S12, S13, S17) were most common; single-session protocols were used peri-procedurally (S4, S5, S10, S14), with additional three- (S9), seven- (S6, S15), and twelve-session designs (S2) (Table 3).

Content commonly comprised pre-selected mandala templates (often round patterns) and coloring media (frequently 6–12 colored pencils/markers); several studies used structured booklets or A4 sheets, with practical aids (e.g., laptop/practice tables in S5, S10, S17). One study implemented a mobile mandala-coloring software (S2) (Table 3). None of the included studies documented adverse events related to mandala coloring, and the intervention was consistently reported as safe and well accepted by participants.

Risk of bias

The risk of bias assessment for the included studies is summarized in Figs. 2 and 3. Overall, the majority of studies demonstrated either high or unclear risk in at least one domain, with the most frequent concerns related to allocation concealment, performance bias, and blinding of outcome assessment [25–41]. The following provides detailed considerations for each domain:

Risk of bias graph

Fig. 3Risk of bias summary

Risk of bias summary

Random sequence generation (selection bias)

Several studies adequately described randomization methods, such as computer-generated sequences or random number tables, and were rated as low risk [26, 27, 32, 33, 35, 38, 39]. However, other trials provided insufficient detail or applied quasi-experimental approaches, resulting in judgments of high risk.

Allocation concealment (selection bias)

Most studies did not use or report sufficient details regarding concealment procedures (e.g., sealed opaque envelopes, central randomization), and thus were rated as high or unclear risk [25–33, 35–41]. Only one study described robust allocation concealment strategies [34].

Blinding of participants and personnel (performance bias)

Given the nature of mandala coloring interventions, blinding of participants and facilitators was generally not feasible. Consequently, all studies were judged to be at high risk of performance bias.

Blinding of outcome assessment (detection bias)

This domain was frequently rated as high or unclear risk because outcomes were primarily self-reported (e.g., stress, anxiety, pain, fatigue, well-being). In most cases, outcome assessors were not blinded. A small number of studies attempted partial blinding, such as involving independent analysts [32, 39].

Incomplete outcome data (attrition bias)

Most studies were rated as low risk for attrition bias, as dropout rates were minimal and adequately reported, and intention-to-treat analyses were performed where appropriate.

Selective reporting (reporting bias)

All studies were judged to be at low risk of selective reporting, as prespecified outcomes were reported consistently in line with study objectives. In particular, studies with registered protocols demonstrated clear alignment between planned and reported outcomes [27, 34, 37–39].

Other bias

This domain was assessed according to the reporting of baseline characteristics, data collection procedures, funding sources, sample size justifications, and conflict of interest declarations. Studies were judged as unclear risk if one of these elements was missing and as high risk when more than one was absent. While most studies provided sufficient methodological transparency, others were rated as unclear or high risk due to one or more unreported items.

Certainty of the evidence

The certainty of evidence ranged from low to very low across outcomes. Anxiety was supported by low-certainty evidence, whereas most psychological and physiological outcomes were supported by very low-certainty evidence due to risk of bias, heterogeneity, and imprecision. No high-certainty evidence was identified. Detailed GRADE assessments are presented in the Supplementary File 3.

Effects of mandala coloring on psychological symptoms

Anxiety

As presented in Fig. 4, the meta-analysis of nine studies (5 RCTs, 4 CCTs) revealed a significant large effect of mandala coloring in reducing anxiety compared to control groups (SMD = − 2.16, 95% CI [–3.38, − 0.95], p = 0.0005; I² = 97%).

Subgroup analysis revealed that interventions with a duration of ≤ 30 min significantly reduced anxiety compared with control (SMD = − 1.62, 95% CI [–3.02, − 0.22], p = 0.02; I² = 96%), whereas those lasting > 30 min did not show a significant effect (SMD = − 3.20, 95% CI [–6.57, 0.18], p = 0.06; I² = 98%). Similarly, multiple-session interventions were associated with significant reductions in anxiety (SMD = − 2.40, 95% CI [–3.91, − 0.88], p = 0.002; I² = 97%), while single-session interventions demonstrated no significant effects (SMD = − 1.38, 95% CI [–4.02, 1.26], p = 0.31; I² = 97%) (Fig. 4).

Stress

The pooled analysis of three studies demonstrated a significant reduction in stress levels among participants in the mandala coloring groups compared with control (SMD = − 2.45, 95% CI [–3.06, − 1.85], p < 0.00001; I² = 50%) (Fig. 5).

Other psychological symptoms (well-being, hope, and resilience)

As shown in Fig. 6, the meta-analysis of three studies (1 RCT, 2 CCTs) revealed that mandala coloring significantly improved well-being compared with control (SMD = 4.51, 95% CI [0.81, 8.21], p = 0.02; I² = 97%). For hope, two studies demonstrated a significant positive small effect in favor of mandala coloring (SMD = 0.42, 95% CI [0.09, 0.75], p = 0.01; I² = 0%). However, the pooled analysis of two studies assessing resilience did not show a significant effect (SMD = 2.45, 95% CI [–2.28, 7.18], p = 0.31; I² = 98%).

Pain

As presented in Fig. 7, the meta-analysis of five studies (2 RCTs, 3 CCTs) found no significant effect of mandala coloring on pain symptoms compared to control (SMD = − 0.35, 95% CI [–1.18, 0.47], p = 0.40; I² = 92%).

Subgroup analysis showed no significant effects for interventions lasting ≤ 30 min (SMD = − 1.03, 95% CI [–3.78, 1.72], p = 0.46; I² = 98%) or > 30 min (SMD = 0.02, 95% CI [–0.44, 0.48], p = 0.94; I² = 65%). Similarly, multiple-session (SMD = 0.02, 95% CI [–0.44, 0.48], p = 0.94; I² = 65%) and single-session interventions (SMD = − 1.03, 95% CI [–3.78, 1.72], p = 0.46; I² = 98%) did not demonstrate statistically significant effects on pain symptoms (Fig. 7).

Fatigue

The pooled analysis of three studies (1 RCT, 2 CCTs) suggested a reduction in fatigue in the mandala coloring groups; however, the effect was not statistically significant (SMD = − 3.09, 95% CI [–6.60, 0.42], p = 0.08; I² = 98%) (Fig. 8).

Other physiological symptoms (nausea, comfort, and vital parameters)

The pooled analysis of two studies (1 RCT, 1 CCT) found no significant effect of mandala coloring on nausea (SMD = − 0.08, 95% CI [–0.45, 0.30], p = 0.69; I² = 0%). Similarly, no significant differences were observed for comfort in two included studies (2 RCTs) (SMD = 0.46, 95% CI [–0.03, 0.94], p = 0.06; I² = 43%) (Fig. 9).

Mandala coloring did not produce significant effects on vital parameters (2 CCTs for all), including systolic BP (MD = − 0.84, 95% CI [–14.05, 12.38], p = 0.90; I² = 92%), diastolic BP (MD = − 0.75, 95% CI [–9.79, 8.28], p = 0.87; I² = 88%), and pulse (MD = 1.95, 95% CI [–1.55, 5.45], p = 0.27; I² = 52%) (Fig. 9).

Sensitivity analysis by study design

Sensitivity analyses were conducted only for outcomes with a sufficient number of RCTs to evaluate the robustness of the pooled estimates after excluding CCTs. For anxiety, the pooled analysis restricted to RCTs continued to demonstrate a statistically significant reduction in anxiety levels (SMD = − 2.03, 95% CI [–3.33, − 0.74], p = 0.002; I² = 94%). For pain, the sensitivity analysis including only RCTs showed no statistically significant difference between the mandala coloring and control groups (SMD = − 0.93, 95% CI [–3.89, 2.02], p = 0.54; I² = 98%). Forest plots of the sensitivity analyses are presented in Supplementary File 4.

Discussion

This meta-analysis evaluated the effects of mandala coloring interventions on psychological and physiological symptoms in hospitalized adult populations. Among the evaluated outcomes, anxiety demonstrated the most consistent and methodologically supported evidence. The pooled analysis revealed a significant reduction in anxiety, and sensitivity analyses restricted to RCTs produced comparable effect estimates, supporting the robustness of this finding and suggesting that the observed benefit was not primarily driven by non-randomized study designs. Stress also showed a significant reduction; however, the smaller number of available studies positions this outcome as supportive rather than confirmatory evidence. For other symptoms, including well-being, hope, fatigue, resilience, comfort, nausea, and physiological parameters, meta-analyses were feasible but were generally based on a limited number of studies, often with few randomized trials. This constraint reduces the reliability and generalizability of these findings and warrants cautious interpretation. Notably, sensitivity analysis for pain similarly demonstrated no statistically significant effect after restricting the analysis to randomized trials, supporting the stability of the observed lack of effect while also suggesting that the current evidence base may be underpowered to detect clinically meaningful differences. When interpreted through the GRADE framework, the overall certainty of evidence ranged from low to very low, indicating that these findings should be interpreted cautiously despite the observed effects.

Heterogeneity was substantial across most analyses, likely reflecting the clinical and methodological diversity of the included studies. Variations in patient populations (e.g., oncology, surgical, psychiatric, dialysis settings), intervention protocols (session duration, frequency, and format), outcome measurement tools, and study designs may all have contributed to the observed variability. Importantly, high heterogeneity is frequently reported in meta-analyses evaluating complex psychosocial and art-based interventions, where contextual and implementation factors inherently influence outcomes [16–19]. Notably, the very large effect sizes observed for certain psychological outcomes, particularly anxiety and well-being, should be interpreted with caution in light of this substantial heterogeneity. Clinical diversity, small sample sizes, non-blinded outcome assessment, and the inclusion of non-randomized designs may have contributed to amplified pooled estimates. Although subgroup analyses explored selected intervention characteristics, residual heterogeneity remained, indicating that pooled estimates should be interpreted cautiously and may not be uniformly generalizable across clinical contexts. However, these subgroup findings should be interpreted cautiously, as multiple comparisons may increase the risk of type I error. Future RCTs should prioritize methodological standardization, including clearly defined intervention protocols, consistent outcome measures, and adequately powered samples, to enhance comparability across studies and reduce unexplained variability. The substantial variability in intervention characteristics also highlights the need for greater standardization in future trials. Developing structured intervention frameworks, such as clearly defined session length and frequency, appropriate timing within the care process, facilitator training, and consistent use of coloring materials, may enhance reproducibility and improve comparability across studies. Greater protocol consistency would ultimately support more precise estimates of intervention effectiveness.

Consistent with prior research on art-based and mindfulness-oriented interventions, mandala coloring appears to produce psychological benefits [42–44]. However, the very large effect sizes observed in some outcomes should be interpreted with caution, as methodological factors such as small sample sizes and lack of blinding may have contributed to effect inflation. The significant improvements in anxiety, stress, well-being, and hope align with the premise that structured coloring promotes attentional focus, facilitates mindfulness, and creates a state of relaxation through rhythmic and repetitive engagement [45, 46]. Similar benefits of mandala coloring and related art-therapy approaches have been reported among diverse groups including students [10, 47], pediatric patients [48, 49], adolescents [50, 51], caregivers [15, 52], and working professionals [53]. These findings highlight the capacity of mandala-based interventions to buffer emotional distress, foster positive affect, and provide patients with a sense of agency during hospitalization. Subgroup analysis demonstrated that shorter sessions (≤ 30 min) delivered repeatedly were particularly effective in reducing anxiety. Clinically, this finding suggests that mandala coloring can be delivered in brief, structured formats that are feasible within routine hospital care. Mechanistically, concise sessions may reduce cognitive strain, while repetition may support emotional regulation. Such an interpretation aligns with mindfulness evidence showing that short but repeated practice alleviates anxiety [54], as well as art-based intervention studies in older adults reporting a dose–response effect favoring frequent repetition over extended single sessions [44]. These results underscore the importance of systematically examining intervention dose and structure in future trials to support the development of standardized protocols. Notably, longer sessions (> 30 min) and single-session interventions did not yield statistically significant effects on anxiety, which may reflect reduced engagement or insufficient exposure to achieve meaningful psychological change. In contrast to anxiety, subgroup analyses for pain revealed no significant effects regardless of session duration or frequency. This discrepancy may be attributed to the multifactorial nature of pain, which is shaped by biological, pharmacological, and psychosocial factors that brief art-based interventions may not sufficiently address [55]. These findings suggest that while mandala coloring appears to be a promising approach for psychological symptoms, its applicability to physiological outcomes may require longer intervention periods or integration with multimodal treatment strategies.

Regarding physiological symptoms, the meta-analyses did not demonstrate significant effects of mandala coloring on pain, fatigue, nausea, or vital parameters. These findings should be interpreted in light of the complex and multifactorial nature of physical symptoms. Pain and fatigue are influenced by disease severity, pharmacological management, and comorbidities, which may outweigh the potential impact of a brief art-based intervention [55]. Evidence from a pilot mindfulness-based art therapy trial in cancer outpatients similarly demonstrated that while psychological and spiritual well-being improved, physical symptoms including pain and fatigue remained largely unaffected [56]. Nausea is likewise shaped by complex physiological and treatment-related mechanisms that may not be sufficiently altered by short-term psychological approaches [57, 58]. Vital parameters, including blood pressure and pulse, are inherently variable and highly sensitive to situational and environmental influences, making them less suitable for detecting subtle regulatory changes [59]. The absence of significant physiological effects may also reflect inconsistencies in outcome measurement and the relatively short duration of many interventions, which may have limited the ability to detect meaningful biological changes. Establishing standardized physiological endpoints and incorporating longer intervention periods in future trials may improve measurement sensitivity and enhance comparability across studies. Furthermore, evidence for some physiological outcomes relied primarily on non-randomized studies, further limiting certainty. Taken together, while the psychological benefits of mandala coloring, particularly for anxiety, appear relatively consistent, the effects on physiological outcomes remain uncertain. Larger, rigorously designed RCTs incorporating objective physiological indicators, such as heart rate variability (HRV), cortisol levels, inflammatory markers, or other biomarkers, are needed to provide a more comprehensive understanding of the potential biological mechanisms underlying mandala coloring and to determine whether its benefits extend beyond psychological domains.

Strengths and limitations

This meta-analysis has several important strengths. It was prospectively registered in PROSPERO, conducted in accordance with PRISMA guidelines, and based on a comprehensive search strategy covering multiple international and national databases. Both RCTs and CCTs were included, allowing for a broader synthesis of the available evidence. Furthermore, the study evaluated a wide range of psychological and physiological symptoms, thus providing a multidimensional perspective on the potential effects of mandala coloring interventions in hospitalized patients. Sensitivity analyses were conducted for outcomes with a sufficient number of randomized trials, which strengthened confidence in the robustness of the primary findings.

Nonetheless, some limitations should be acknowledged. The number of included studies was small for several symptoms, particularly fatigue, resilience, comfort, and nausea, which may have reduced the statistical power of pooled analyses. Considerable heterogeneity was observed across studies in terms of patient populations (oncology, dialysis, surgical, psychiatric, burn care), intervention protocols (session length, number, frequency, coloring format), delivery modes, and outcome measurement instruments. Such diversity likely influenced the consistency of effect sizes and complicates the interpretation of pooled results. Although subgroup and sensitivity analyses were performed, meta-regression and outlier analyses were not feasible due to the limited number of studies per outcome, and therefore, residual heterogeneity could not be fully explained. Additionally, as the number of studies for each outcome was below ten, formal assessment of publication bias using funnel plots was not feasible, in accordance with established recommendations. The inclusion of CCTs alongside RCTs may have introduced potential design-related bias, as non-randomized studies are inherently more vulnerable to selection bias and residual confounding. Due to the participatory nature of mandala coloring, blinding of participants and facilitators was not feasible, leading to a high risk of performance bias across all studies. This methodological constraint is inherent in art-based and psychosocial interventions and should be considered when interpreting the results. Additionally, the majority of included studies were conducted in Türkiye and Iran, which may introduce regional bias and limit the generalizability of the findings to broader multicultural healthcare settings. Cultural factors may influence patients’ perceptions of art-based interventions, their level of emotional engagement, and their willingness to participate, potentially affecting observed outcomes. Therefore, the results should be interpreted within their cultural context, and further research across diverse geographic and cultural settings is needed to strengthen the cross-context applicability of these findings. Furthermore, the included studies did not consistently report whether data were collected in private or public healthcare settings, limiting the ability to examine potential differences in patient outcomes across healthcare contexts. Finally, although SMDs were used to account for differences in measurement tools, pooling across diverse psychological instruments (e.g., different stress or anxiety scales) may have introduced conceptual heterogeneity and affected interpretability. Furthermore, several pooled outcomes yielded unusually large effect sizes that may have been influenced by methodological characteristics, including small sample sizes, lack of blinding, and the inclusion of non-randomized designs, potentially leading to overestimation of intervention effects.

Implications for clinical practice and research

The findings of this meta-analysis suggest that mandala coloring may represent a feasible supportive approach for addressing psychological distress in hospitalized adults, particularly in relation to anxiety and stress. As a safe, low-cost, and culturally adaptable activity, it has the potential to complement conventional care when psychological support is indicated. However, these implications should be interpreted cautiously given the heterogeneity of the included studies and the limited number of high-quality randomized trials. At present, the evidence is insufficient to support definitive clinical recommendations or standardized implementation.

From a research perspective, the results underscore the need for rigorously designed, multicenter RCTs with larger and more diverse samples to strengthen the evidence base. Future investigations should prioritize the development of standardized intervention protocols, evaluate optimal session frequency and duration, and incorporate objective physiological measures such as cortisol or HRV to better capture the multidimensional impact of mandala coloring. Additionally, studies examining patient selection and contextual factors may further support the development of evidence-informed clinical frameworks.

Conclusion

This meta-analysis suggests that mandala coloring may serve as a supportive intervention for alleviating psychological distress in hospitalized adults, with the most consistent evidence observed for reductions in anxiety and, to a lesser extent, stress. Evidence for other psychological outcomes, such as well-being and hope, remains limited due to the small number of available studies. As a simple and low-cost approach, mandala coloring may complement routine care when psychological support is needed. However, the evidence for physiological outcomes such as pain, fatigue, nausea, comfort, and vital parameters remains limited and inconclusive, largely due to the small number of available studies and the absence of long-term follow-up. Therefore, these findings should be interpreted cautiously and should not yet be broadly generalized to clinical practice. Larger, methodologically rigorous RCTs incorporating sensitive physiological measures are required to determine whether the benefits of mandala coloring extend beyond psychological domains.
