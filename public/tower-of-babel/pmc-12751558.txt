Differences in the utilization of physical therapy for outpatients with non-specific shoulder pain labels and specific shoulder pain labels
Authors: Yujian Liu, Kangzhe Peng, Jian Lu, Guangwu Liu
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundNational data on the characteristics of ambulatory visits with non-specific shoulder pain (NSSP) labels are lacking. The aim of this study was to identify the differences in the utilization of physical therapy (PT) for visits with NSSP labels and visits with specific shoulder pain (SSP) labels by using National Ambulatory Medical Care Survey (NAMCS) database.MethodsThe NAMCS was queried from 2007 to 2016 to identify the ambulatory visits for shoulder pain. The study population was stratified according to the top 3 diagnoses, including NSSP group and SSP group. Data on patient, practice and visit characteristics were included, as variables in both descriptive and adjusted analyses. Differences in these characteristics and clinical management for the two groups were tested. Of these, 24.31 million visits (28.1%) were assigned to NSSP labels. Combining all years, PT was prescribed in approximately 10.8% of visits with NSSP labels, which was significantly lower than visits with SSP labels (22.2%). Background

Shoulder pain is a highly prevalent musculoskeletal complaint in the United States (US). A wide spectrum of bone and soft tissue pathologies can cause shoulder pain. The extensive existing diagnostic labels are based on the traditional pathoanatomic diagnostic classification, which attempts to identify a specific tissue pathology responsible for shoulder symptoms [1]. However, making a definitive specific diagnosis of shoulder pain is challenging at both primary and specialist care levels [2]. The immediate challenges surrounding the pathoanatomic diagnosis of shoulder pain include limited diagnostic value of physical examination tests, weak association between imaging findings and symptoms, co-existence of multiple pathoanatomic conditions [3, 4]. Numerous reviews have highlighted the poor reliability of physical examination tests in the assessment of patients with shoulder pain [5–8]. Hegedus et al. examined the diagnostic accuracy of numerous shoulder physical examination tests and concluded that no single clinical test was sufficiently reliable to establish a pathognomonic diagnosis [7]. In addition, many studies have questioned the weak correlation between clinical shoulder symptoms and radiologic abnormalities [9, 10]. Barreto et al. prospectively evaluated bilateral magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) findings in 123 individuals with unilateral shoulder pain and demonstrated that most abnormalities seen on MRI were not different in frequency between symptomatic and asymptomatic shoulders [9].

Consequently, the use of the term non-specific shoulder pain (NSSP) as a non-specific diagnostic label has been proposed [3]. The definition of NSSP in the published literature is shoulder pain without specific recognizable pathology or physical signs [11]. The extensive use of NSSP labels reflects uncertainty in traditional pathoanatomic labeling for shoulder pain and intention to overcome the limitations of traditional approaches to diagnosis [3, 4]. NSSP might be considered as a common independent disease entity, rather than an indeterminate diagnosis that needs to be clarified [3]. However, comprehensive national data on the characteristics of ambulatory visits with NSSP labels are lacking.

The value of physical therapy (PT) as the first-line treatment of specific shoulder pain (SSP) is widely accepted [12, 13]. The published clinical practice guidelines (CPGs) strongly recommended PT to improve pain, mobility, and function in patients with SSP, such as rotator cuff injuries and adhesive capsulitis of shoulder [12, 13]. Although, the widely recognized CPGs for NSSP have not yet been established, the evidence supporting the PT as an effective intervention for patients with NSSP is also increasing and strengthening [2, 14, 15]. Recently, a new Norwegian guideline strongly recommended simple clinical examination and advice for exercises in the primary care of NSSP [2]. Dolder et al. assessed the effectiveness of exercise for the treatment of NSSP and demonstrated high quality evidence that exercise therapy was effective to relieve the shoulder pain [14].

There is limited research focused on the use of PT for patients with NSSP compared to SSP. Therefore, the aim of our study was to identify the differences in the utilization of PT for ambulatory visits with NSSP labels and SSP labels, by using the 2007–2016 National Ambulatory Medical Care Survey (NAMCS) database.

Methods

Data source

The NAMCS launched by the National Center for Health Statistics (NCHS) provides a nationally representative estimate of office-based ambulatory care visits in the US. This annual survey captures information on the characteristics of patients and visits, such as patients’ age, sex, race, expected source of payment, metropolitan statistical area (MSA) status, reasons for visit (RFVs), major reason for this visit (new problem, chronic problem routine, chronic problem flare-up), continuity of care (new patient, established patient), clinical diagnoses, medical comorbidities. The NAMCS data also include the diagnostic screening and examination, education/counseling, and therapeutic services ordered or provided during the visits. Given the publicness and de-identification of NAMCS database, this study did not require institutional review board approval.

Study population

The NAMCS was queried from 2007 to 2016 to identify the ambulatory visits for shoulder pain. The sample consisted of visits by adults with a diagnosis of shoulder disorder in the top 3 diagnoses and a complaint of shoulder pain in the top 3 RFVs. We excluded visits involving diagnoses of pregnancy, malignancy or neoplasm, infectious diseases, visits classified as preventative care, pre-surgery or post-surgery. From this sample population, we further excluded the visits for infectious or inflammatory arthropathy of shoulder, pathological fracture of shoulder, open fracture or open dislocation of shoulder, because of the significant heterogeneity in the management of these diseases.

The shoulder related diagnoses were dichotomized as NSSP and SSP based on the International Classification of Diseases, Ninth Edition, Clinical Modification (ICD-9-CM) and International Classification of Diseases, Tenth Edition, Clinical Modification (ICD-10-CM) codes. A diagnostic label described a symptom without specific pathology was classified as NSSP, including effusion of joint, shoulder region (ICD-9 code: 71901; ICD-10 code: M25411, M25412), hemarthrosis, shoulder region (ICD-9 code: 71911; ICD-10 code: M25011, M25012), pain in joint, shoulder region (ICD-9 code: 71941; ICD-10 code: M25511, M25512), stiffness of joint, not elsewhere classified, shoulder region (ICD-9 code: 71951; ICD-10 code: M25611, M25612), other symptoms referable to joint, shoulder region (ICD-9 code: 71961), unspecified disorder of joint, shoulder region (ICD-9 code: 71991; ICD-10 code: M259). A diagnostic label identified a clear structural pathoanatomic origin of shoulder pain was classified as SSP. The comprehensive NSSP and SSP diagnosis codes were listed in Table 1. If none of the top 3 diagnoses was a SSP diagnosis, this visit would be assigned to the NSSP group. If one of the top 3 diagnoses was a SSP diagnosis, this visit would be assigned to the SSP group. To limit the visits mainly for shoulder pain, we used top 3 RFVs in addition to the top 3 diagnoses to identify the study sample. A complaint of shoulder pain was determined based on the RFV classification code number (19400–19405,19450–19455) and the top 3 RFVs were reviewed in our analysis. According to the RFV classification, 19,400 represents shoulder symptoms, 19,401 represents pain, ache, soreness, discomfort of shoulder, 19,402 represents cramps, contractures, spasms of shoulder, 19,403 represents limitation of movement, stiffness, tightness of shoulder, 19,404 represents weakness of shoulder, and 19,405 represents swelling of shoulder, 19,450 represents arm symptoms, 19,451 represents pain, ache, soreness, discomfort of arm, 19,452 represents cramps, contractures, spasms of arm, 19,453 represents limitation of movement, stiffness, tightness of arm, 19,454 represents weakness of arm, and 19,455 represents swelling of arm.

Study variables

Data on patient sociodemographic characteristics such as age, sex (male, female), race (white, black, and other), expected source of payment for this visit (private insurance, Medicare/Medicaid, worker’s compensation, and other), and MSA status (yes, no) were included, as variables in both descriptive and adjusted analyses. We also included the practice and visit characteristics such as the major reason for this visit (new problem, chronic problem routine, chronic problem flare-up), presence of injury related to current visit (yes, no), referred for this visit (yes, no), continuity of care (new patient, established patient), number of medical comorbidities (0, 1, 2, ≥ 3) and physician specialty (primary care specialty, surgical care specialty, medical care specialty). The variables with missing rate exceeding 20% were excluded from the statistical models because of the inability to make reliable estimates using these variables, which included tobacco use (missing rate 26.96%), temperature (missing rate 78.33%), body mass index (missing rate 41.36%), systolic blood pressure (missing rate 47.89%), and diastolic blood pressure (missing rate 47.96%) (Supplementary Table 1).

Considering the information on a variety of services and treatments captured in NAMCS, this study focused on the differences in the utilization of PT for ambulatory visits with NSSP labels and visits with SSP labels. In addition, this study also described the use of diagnostic imaging included x-ray, MRI, ultrasound, computed tomography (CT), as well as pain medications across both groups. Pain medications were classified into four categories according to the Multum drug classification, including nonsteroidal anti-inflammatory drugs (NSAIDs) (drug codes 061 and 278), narcotic analgesics (drug codes 060 and 191), skeletal muscle relaxants (drug codes 178 and 179), and other pain medications (drug codes 059, 062 and 063) [16]. Recommendations for diet/nutrition, exercise, injury prevention, stress management, tobacco use/exposure, weight reduction were all included as health education/counselling services. Mental health counseling and psychotherapy were combined into one variable for analysis.

Statistical analysis

In order to identify the differences between the two groups, all analyses were stratified by NSSP group versus SSP group. We described the sample according to the patient and visit characteristics. Differences in these characteristics and clinical management for two groups were tested, by using the Rao-Scott Chi-square test for categorical variables and the Student’s t-test for continuous variables. We constructed multivariable logistic regression analyses to evaluate the influence of diagnostic labels on PT utilization. The covariables in the regression analysis included age, sex, race, expected source of payment for this visit, MSA status, major reason for this visit, presence of injury related to current visit, referred for this visit, continuity of care, number of medical comorbidities, and physician specialty. The results of regression analyses were presented as odds ratio (OR) with the corresponding 95% confidence interval (CI). To accommodate the multistage probability sampling design of NAMCS, all estimates were weighted to yield nationally representative estimates. The multiple imputations were applied to address missing data by using the MICE (Multivariate Imputation by Chained Equations) package.

In the sensitivity analysis, we conducted the regression analysis again by excluding the visits with closed fracture and closed dislocation of shoulder. Subgroup analyses and interaction tests were performed using the adjusted model to explore heterogeneity in the associations across different subgroups. All statistical analyses were conducted with the statistical computing language R software (version 4.1.3), with p values < 0.05 considered as the threshold for statistical significance.

Results

From 2007 to 2016, approximately 62.17 million (71.9%) visits were assigned to SSP labels, while the remaining 24.31 million visits (28.1%) were assigned to NSSP labels. Significant visit characteristic differences were observed between NSSP group and SSP group (Table 2). The proportion of visits with the presence of injury in NSSP group was 9.9%, which was significantly lower than the proportion in the SSP group (42.4%). 20.6% of NSSP group were referred for this visit, which was lower than proportion in the SSP group (36.0%). The proportion of established patients in NSSP group (85.1%) was higher than proportion in the SSP group (78.3%). For the physician specialty, the proportion of primary care specialty in the NSSP group was significantly higher than the proportion in the SSP group (56.0% versus 25.8%), and proportion of surgical care specialty in the NSSP group was significantly lower than the proportion in the SSP group (25.1% versus 54.3%). The proportion of visits with no comorbidities was 40.1% in the NSSP group, which was significantly higher than the proportion in the SSP group (21.2%). Besides that, no statistically significant differences were identified with regard to patient age, sex, race, expected source of payment for this visit, MSA status, or the major reason for this visit between two groups (Table 2).

For visits with NSSP labels, x-ray (23.7%) was the most common imaging examination, followed by MRI (11.3%), ultrasound (0.3%) and CT (0.3%). It was interesting to see visits with NSSP labels received far fewer ultrasound compared with visits with SSP labels (0.3% versus 4.1%). Besides that, the use of medical imaging was similar for visits with SSP labels, and no differences were observed in use of x-ray, MRI, or CT between the two groups (Table 2).

For both NSSP and SSP groups, the most frequent medications prescribed were NSAIDs, and the use of NSAIDs were similar in the NSSP (31.9%) group and SSP (27.6%) group. It was noteworthy that visits with NSSP labels had a significant higher frequency of narcotic analgesics prescriptions compared to visits with SSP labels (25.8% versus 15.6%). Skeletal muscle relaxants occurred at 11.5% of visits with NSSP labels and 8.7% of visits with SSP labels. The health education/counselling was more common in the SSP group (23.2%) than that in the NSSP group (14.5%). No difference in the mental health counseling between the two groups was identified (Table 2).

Combining all years, PT was prescribed in approximately 19.0% of the overall adult ambulatory visits for shoulder pain (Table 2). Our results revealed a significant lower frequency of PT for visits with NSSP labels (10.8%) versus visits with SSP labels (22.2%). The baseline characteristics of sample population stratified by utilization of PT were presented in Table 3. Multivariable logistic regression analysis was employed to identify the factors associated with ambulatory utilization of PT for shoulder pain (Table 4). Visits assigned to NSSP labels had significantly lower odds of prescribing PT compared to visits assigned to SSP labels (adjusted OR = 0.62, 95% CI: 0.41–0.94), after adjustment for covariates including age, sex, race, expected source of payment, MSA, major reason for this visit, injury related, referred for this visit, continuity of care, number of medical comorbidities, and physician specialty. Meanwhile, the surgical care specialty had higher odds of prescribing PT compared to primary care specialty (adjusted OR = 2.31, 95% CI: 1.62–3.29). Visits covered by worker’s compensation had higher odds of receiving PT than visits covered by private insurance (adjusted OR = 2.08, 95% CI: 1.30–3.31).

In the sensitivity analysis, visits assigned to NSSP labels still had significantly lower odds of prescribing PT compared to visits assigned to SSP labels (adjusted OR = 0.60, 95% CI: 0.39–0.92), after adjustment for covariates and excluding the visits with closed fracture and closed dislocation of shoulder (Table 5). The results of subgroup analyses among shoulder pain visits are shown in Fig. 1. A significant negative association was observed between NSSP group and PT among age less than 60 years old, female, white, those from MSA, with routine chronic problem, without referred for this visit. Additionally, no significant interactions were identified between diagnosis labels and any covariates (all P values for interaction > 0.05, Fig. 1).

Discussion

Our research was the first study focused on the differences in the utilization of PT for visits with NSSP labels and visits with SSP labels by using NAMCS database. The most noteworthy finding was that visits assigned to NSSP labels had significantly lower frequency of PT compared to visits assigned to SSP labels (10.8% versus 22.2%). Logistic regression analyses identified the influence of non-specific diagnostic labels on the utilization of PT after adjusting for the covariates. However, the evidence of a lower use of PT coupled with higher prescribed narcotic analgesics for visits with NSSP labels compared to visits with SSP labels needed to be interpreted with caution.

Accurate and prompt diagnosis is crucial for clinical management of shoulder pain [17]. Currently, the majority of diagnostic labels assigned to patients with shoulder pain are based on the pathoanatomic diagnostic classification [1]. In typical ambulatory shoulder care, physicians often rely on physical examination tests and imaging modalities to reach a definitive diagnosis of shoulder pain [1, 3, 4]. However, achieving a definitive and specific diagnosis of shoulder pain is challenging, and the basis for them has been questioned [5, 9, 10]. Many studies have highlighted the poor reliability of physical examination tests in the field of shoulder disorders [5, 7]. May et al. systematically reviewed the reliability of physical examination procedures in 2010 and found that majority of studies indicated poor reliability for the procedures used in the clinical examination of patients with shoulder pain [5]. Several studies suggested that combinations of physical examination tests might provide better diagnostic accuracy [7, 18, 19]. Given the limited diagnostic value of physical examination tests for shoulder pain, diagnostic imaging has become increasingly important for detecting shoulder disorders [20, 21]. According to the American College of Radiology (ACR) appropriateness criteria for evaluating atraumatic shoulder pain, MRI is considered to be the gold standard with its excellent ability in depicting the soft-tissue streuctures [21]. However, abnormal radiological findings may not be the source of clinical symptoms [9, 10]. Barreto et al. found that abnormalities on MRI were not different in frequency between symptomatic and asymptomatic shoulders, after evaluating bilateral MRI findings in 123 individuals [9]. Eliason et al. recruited 115 consecutive patients with unilateral subacromial pain syndrome from primary care centers and reported ultrasound findings of structural abnormalities in almost 90% of the asymptomatic shoulder [10].

Consequently, the use of the term NSSP as a non-specific diagnostic label has been proposed [3]. NSSP has been defined in various ways, the most common definition of NSSP is shoulder pain without specific recognizable pathology or physical signs. In our study, a diagnostic label described a symptom without specific pathology was classified as NSSP. A diagnostic label identified a clear structural pathoanatomic origin of shoulder pain was classified as SSP, such as rotator cuff tear or rupture, impingement syndrome, and adhesive capsulitis of shoulder. During 2007–2016, about 28.1% of ambulatory visits for shoulder pain were assigned to NSSP labels in the US. This occurrence rate of NSSP was consistent with literature reports and clinical practice [11, 22]. Non-specific diagnostic labels have traditionally been considered as the consequence of inadequate expertise to diagnose the specific structural cause of symptoms, which may be different in the field of shoulder pain [3]. Sometimes, it was difficult to establish a specific diagnosis at the initial visit for shoulder pain, and further examinations were needed. However, these assumptions were not supported by the results of our study. There were no significant differences in the ordering of x-ray, MRI, or CT between the two groups, which indicated that visits with NSSP labels did not undergo more imaging. Meanwhile, the proportion of established patients in the NSSP group was higher than the proportion in the SSP group (85.1% versus 78.3%), that indicated the majority of visits with NSSP labels were made by established patients, rather than new patients. These results could be attributed to the uncertainty in traditional pathoanatomic labeling for shoulder pain and the improvement in understanding NSSP diagnosis in clinical practice. NSSP can be considered as a common independent disease entity, rather than an indeterminate diagnosis that needs to be clarified, which is supported by our research findings and published literature [3, 4].

In addition to the lack of national data on the management of NSSP, there is limited research comparing the utilization of PT for NSSP and SSP. Feleus et al. performed a large prospective cohort study on the differences in management of different diagnostic groups in non-traumatic arm, neck and shoulder complaints by general practitioner in the Netherlands, and found that patients with a non-specific diagnosis were more frequently referred for physiotherapy [23], which was contrasted with our results. This difference reflected variation in research characteristics and populations observed, also reflected the different beliefs of physicians about the treatment of NSSP from different academic backgrounds and regions. Many factors can influence the utilization of PT, including clinical diagnosis, seriousness of illness, PT supply, insurance policy, physician experience and training [24, 25]. In our study, the physician specialty could be a significant potential confounder to the association between the diagnosis type and PT use. To evaluate the potential impact of physician specialty, we included the physician specialty as a covariable in the regression model, and found that visits assigned to NSSP labels had significantly lower odds of prescribing PT compared to visits assigned to SSP labels (adjusted OR = 0.62, 95% CI: 0.41–0.94).

Our result could be partially explained by the perception and recognition of the effectiveness of PT for NSSP and SSP in the clinical practice. PT has a fundamental role in the management of SSP, such as rotator cuff injuries and adhesive capsulitis of shoulder [12, 13]. Doiron-Cadrin et al. assessed the methodological quality of 9 CPGs on the management of rotator cuff disorders and found all CPGs recommended active treatment modalities, such as an exercise program in the management of rotator cuff disorders [26]. A recent systematic review and meta-analysis conducted by Mertens et al. found that range of motion, function, and pain in patients with frozen shoulder improved with both solely exercises and programs with exercises [27]. In recent years, the evidence supporting PT as an effective intervention for patients with NSSP is also increasing and strengthening [2, 14, 15]. Dolder et al. assessed the effectiveness of exercise for the treatment of NSSP and demonstrated high quality evidence that exercise therapy was effective to relieve the shoulder pain [14]. Abdulla et al. conducted a systematic review and suggested that supervised strengthening and stretching were equally effective to corticosteroid injections or multimodal care for NSSP [28]. Recently, a new Norwegian guideline strongly recommended simple clinical examination and advice for exercises in the primary care of NSSP [2]. The gap in physicians’ awareness and understanding of NSSP, might lead to the lower utilization rate of PT for NSSP. Our study might identify this gap in the utilization of PT for NSSP and provided valuable information on improving the ambulatory management of NSSP.

Findings from recent studies indicate that diagnostic labels for shoulder pain may influence patients’ perceived seriousness of the condition, need for imaging or surgery, recovery expectations, and clinical outcomes [29–31]. Zadro et al. analyzed 2024 responses and found labeling as rotator cuff tear increased perceived need for surgery, imaging, absence from work and to see a specialist compared to labelling as bursitis [30]. Cuff et al. suggested that using the term subacromial impingement syndrome (SIS) potentially contributed to the negative influence on the expectations of positive outcome with physiotherapy [31]. Patients assigned to SSP labels might have higher perceived treatment need compared to patients assigned to NSSP labels, leading to a significantly higher use of PT for SSP compared to NSSP. The influence of non-specific diagnostic labels on the treatment intentions might explain the lower rate of PT for NSSP, but it was unable to explain the higher rate of narcotic analgesics prescriptions for NSSP compared to SSP, which was a contradiction needed further investigation.

Limitations

These limitations are worth noting. Firstly, due to the retrospective and cross-sectional nature of our study, no causal relationship can be inferred between the risk factors and the use of PT. Secondly, the accuracy of the diagnoses recorded in the NAMCS database could not be determined, and the recorded diagnoses more reflected the doctors’ diagnosis and treatment mode. Thirdly, variation exists across the current management of NSSP due to the lack of recognized CPGs for NSSP. Despite these limitations, the differences we observed in the utilization of PT for NSSP compared with SSP may signal gaps in the ambulatory management of NSSP, which warrants further investigation.

Conclusion

The results of this retrospective cross-sectional study identified the significant differences in the utilization of PT for visits with NSSP labels and SSP labels by using NAMCS database. Multivariable logistic regression analyses confirmed the influence of non-specific diagnostic labels on utilization of PT. After adjusting for the covariates, visits assigned to NSSP labels had significantly lower odds of prescribing PT compared to visits assigned to SSP labels. These findings may help improve physician prescribing practices, diminish management disparities, and improve appropriate use of PT in the ambulatory management of shoulder pain.
