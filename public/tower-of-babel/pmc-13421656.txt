Natural Frequencies of Levodopa‐Induced Dyskinesia in Parkinson's Disease
Authors: Ioannis U Isaias, Federica Avantaggiato, Gianni Pezzoli, Andrea Canessa
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTObjectivesAbnormal involuntary movements, known as dyskinesias, are common complications of levodopa treatment in patients with Parkinson's disease and can significantly impair quality of life. The underlying pathophysiology remains unclear, and current therapeutic options are limited. This study aimed to investigate the oscillatory dynamics of the supplementary motor area during levodopa administration and the emergence of dyskinesias.MethodsWe applied transcranial magnetic stimulation to directly perturb the supplementary motor area and used high‐density electroencephalography to measure its natural oscillatory frequency during levodopa administration and the emergence of dyskinesias.ResultsLevodopa intake increased cortical excitability in both dyskinetic and non‐dyskinetic parkinsonian patients. Transcranial magnetic stimulation consistently evoked dominant beta‐gamma oscillations in both groups. Notably, dyskinetic patients exhibited a shift of event‐related spectral perturbation toward beta low frequencies, paralleling the clinical manifestation of peak‐of‐dose dyskinesias.InterpretationThese findings support the view of dyskinesias as disorders of oscillatory local and network activity and suggest that targeted neuromodulation strategies could be developed to mitigate these motor complications.Keywords: circuit, dyskinesia, EEG, oscillations, Parkinson's disease, transcranial magnetic stimulation

Introduction

Since its introduction, levodopa has remained the most effective treatment in Parkinson's disease (PD) [1]. However, its chronic use is associated with the appearance of complications in most patients, such as motor fluctuations and levodopa‐induced dyskinesias (LID). The latter are often dose‐limiting [2] and can substantially affect quality of life while increasing overall healthcare costs [3]. LID represents one of the most common indications for second‐line treatments.

Despite the high prevalence of dyskinesias [4], their pathophysiology remains unclear. They likely result from multiple dopaminergic and non‐dopaminergic mechanisms that disrupt cortico‐striatal plasticity [5, 6]. Dopamine loss, the hallmark of PD, has several consequences in the parkinsonian brain that are associated with the occurrence of LID. First, it causes a progressive reduction of dopamine storage and buffering capacity, leading to levodopa‐induced fluctuations in central dopamine levels and LID [7, 8]. This is accompanied by the development of compensatory postsynaptic and presynaptic adjustments, such as supersensitivity of dopamine receptors, long‐lasting alterations in gene expression and epigenetic regulation of striatal neurons [9], increased dopamine turnover, sprouting of residual dopaminergic terminals, downregulation of presynaptic transporters, and a shift of levodopa metabolism to alternative pathways (serotoninergic, noradrenergic and cholinergic neurotransmission) [10, 11].

These molecular derangements can directly alter the membrane conductance properties and firing rates of cortical and subcortical neurons [12, 13], ultimately influencing the electrical activity at different nodes of the motor cortico‐striato‐thalamic circuitry. Abnormal network oscillatory dynamics have been implicated in several clinical manifestations (reviewed by Petersson et al. [14]), with the most consistent being the subthalamic beta rhythm in PD [15, 16]. At the cortical level, however, no consistent pattern of oscillatory response to oral treatment has yet been identified [17, 18]. Nevertheless, increased narrowband gamma oscillatory activity has been observed in the primary sensorimotor cortex (SM1) of patients with PD during LID episodes, supporting cortical electrical rearrangements in the dyskinetic brain [19, 20] and underscoring the concept of LID as neural “oscillopathies” [12, 21].

Besides SM1, increasing evidence suggests an important role of other frontal areas in the pathophysiology of LID, including the primary motor cortex (M1), the supplementary motor areas (SMA), pre‐SMA, and the inferior frontal gyri (IFG) [22, 23]. Specifically, aberrant activation of these regions in the medicated state has been shown to reliably correlate with the occurrence of LID across different functional imaging techniques [24, 25, 26, 27].

In our own previous work in PD patients, combining transcranial magnetic stimulation with electroencephalography (TMS‐EEG), we demonstrated increased cortical excitability in the SMA of the more dopamine‐depleted brain hemisphere following administration of levodopa [28]. This increase paralleled the degree of clinical improvement and was significantly greater in patients with LID compared to non‐dyskinetic subjects when analyzed during the timeframe of the occurrence of peak‐of‐dose dyskinesias [29].

Brain‐navigated TMS‐EEG can also be used to characterize the oscillatory activity of cortical areas [30, 31] by directly applying controlled magnetic perturbations and subsequently examining the spectral content of the triggered EEG changes—namely the total oscillatory response, which encompasses both phase‐locked‐ and non‐phase‐locked‐induced EEG activity by the conditioning stimulus [32]. With this approach, it is possible to identify the natural oscillatory frequency (eigenfrequency) of the stimulated region [30], which is thought to reflect underlying local and network properties [33].

In the present study, we extend our previous findings by investigating the TMS‐induced oscillatory EEG dynamics of the SMA in patients with PD, both with and without LID.

Methods

Subjects

Patients with predominantly akinetic‐rigid PD were enrolled in the study. Diagnosis was assessed according to the UK Parkinson Disease Brain Bank criteria and the following three supportive criteria: (i) progressive disorder, (ii) clinical course ≥ 10 years, and (iii) levodopa response for > 5 years [34]. Cognitive impairment and depression were excluded using the Mini‐Mental State Examination (MMSE > 25), the Frontal Assessment Battery (FAB > 11), and the Beck Depression Inventory (BDI < 8). Only patients on stable dopaminergic treatment for at least two months prior to the study were included. Exclusion criteria comprised biphasic dyskinesia, off‐dystonia, other movement or neuropsychiatric disorders, ongoing treatments with medications known to modify cortical excitability (e.g., anti‐seizure drugs, antidopaminergic drugs, antiepileptics), or a history of neurosurgical procedures.

The clinical diagnosis of idiopathic PD was supported by a pathological single‐photon emission computed tomography (SPECT) with [123I]N‐ω‐fluoropropyl‐2β‐carbomethoxy‐3β‐(4‐iodophenyl)nortropane. Based on SPECT findings, we identified the most dopamine‐depleted hemisphere for each subject, and this information was used to guide the side selected for TMS‐EEG evaluation.

Clinical severity at baseline and during the study protocol was assessed using the Unified Parkinson's Disease Rating Scale motor part (UPDRS‐III) and the Abnormal Involuntary Movement Scale (AIMS). Patients were evaluated with a clinical and electrophysiological assessment in the morning after withdrawal of all anti‐parkinsonian medications for at least 12 h (meds‐off), challenged with a single dose of soluble levodopa/benserazide (200/50 mg), and reassessed at 30 (meds‐on30), 60 (meds‐on60), and 180 (meds‐on180) min after medication intake.

Long‐acting dopamine agonists were discontinued three days before the experiment. Other medications (e.g., MAO‐B or COMT inhibitors) were not discontinued to avoid patient discomfort or sleep disturbances during the nights preceding the experiment, which could have confounded the TMS‐EEG assessment (see Section 3). Levodopa equivalent daily dose (LEDD) was calculated as described by Tomlinson et al. [35].

In accordance with the declaration of Helsinki, all participants gave written informed consent before entering the study, which was approved by the local ethics committee (Area 1).

TMS/EEG Recordings and Data Processing and Analysis

Methods for TMS delivery and EEG recording have been described elsewhere [28, 29]. The present analyses are based on the same dataset previously investigated in [29], in which group differences in TMS‐evoked potential (TEP) amplitude were reported. In the current study, we extend those findings by specifically characterizing the spectral dynamics of the TMS‐evoked response.

Briefly, we used a Navigated Brain Stimulation (NBS) system coupled with a TMS‐compatible 60‐channel EEG amplifier (Nexstim Ltd., Helsinki, Finland [36]) to target the individually MRI‐defined SMA (middle‐caudal portion of the superior frontal gyrus—Brodmann area 6) of the most dopamine‐depleted hemisphere. Stimulation intensity was individually calibrated to induce a fixed estimated maximum electric field of 120 V/m at the cortical surface, rather than being defined in terms of stimulator output [30]. Multiple trials of single pulse TMS were delivered at each timepoint at randomly jittering intervals of 1500–1800 ms. We always checked that stimulation intensity did not evoke muscle twitches. A customized masking noise [37] was played to the patients through inserted earplugs to reduce contamination by auditory evoked activity. Stimulation parameters were controlled using a NBS system (Nexstim Ltd., Helsinki, Finland) equipped with a 3D infrared optical tracking system (Polaris, Northern Digital Inc., Waterloo, Canada) and integrated with individual T1‐weighted MRI scans. Spatial accuracy and reproducibility of stimulation coordinates within and across sessions were ensured through continuous real‐time monitoring of coil position and orientation relative to the intended target, with TMS pulses automatically inhibited when spatial deviation exceeded ±2 mm.

Data pre‐processing and analysis was carried out using MATLAB (2020, The MathWorks Inc.). hdEEG channels affected by bad scalp‐electrode contact were visually identified and replaced with spherical spline interpolation. In the channels near the stimulated sites and in the homologous contralateral area, signal quality was always acceptable, and we did not apply any interpolation. Trials with sporadic artifacts were excluded by visual inspection. Stereotypical artifacts (e.g., blinks, heartbeat, and muscle tension) were removed by Independent Component Analysis [36]. Each trial was baseline corrected, band‐pass filtered between 2 and 80 Hz, down‐sampled to 725 Hz, and re‐referenced to the common average reference.

As a first step to study the natural frequency of the stimulated region in response to TMS, we identified a Region of Interest (ROI) representing the maximal cortical‐evoked activity. The ROI was defined as described in [29], by computing the z‐score of the Immediate Response Area (IRA) of the TMS‐evoked potential (TEP) response across all EEG channels.

TEPs were computed for each EEG channel as the average across trials for each session, from a minimum of 120 artifact‐free single trials (mean across all TMS‐EEG measurements: 195 trials; range: 120–270 trials). For each trial, the first 5 ms following the TMS pulse were excluded from the analysis due to residual stimulation artifacts. To characterize the spatial distribution of the early cortical response, local mean field power (LMFP) was computed for each channel as a measure of the local TEP amplitude. The area under the curve of the first positive LMFP peak (IRA) was then calculated, and a z‐score transformation was applied to the resulting topographic IRA map [29]. Channels with z‐score > 2 were selected for each session, resulting in subject‐specific ROIs composed of fronto‐central EEG channels showing the strongest early cortical response to TMS.

The topographic distribution of ROIs was consistent across sessions within each subject. Therefore, a single subject‐specific ROI was defined by merging the ROIs obtained across sessions. ROI channels consistently clustered over the central frontal region, ipsilateral to the most affected hemisphere, compatible with the expected scalp projection of the SMA (see Figure S1).

Consistent with our previous analysis focusing on TEP amplitude [29], in the present study TEPs were primarily used to identify channels exhibiting the strongest early cortical response to TMS and to define the ROI for subsequent time–frequency analyses.

To characterize the local dominant frequency of the cortical response to TMS beyond amplitude measures, we analyzed TMS responses in the time–frequency domain by computing the event‐related spectral perturbation (ERSP). For each TMS session, individual trials were transformed using time–frequency decomposition with 43 Morlet wavelets in the 8–50 Hz range (central frequency = 1 Hz; full width at half maximum = 3 s). Power was averaged across trials, and the ERSP was normalized by subtracting and dividing by the mean baseline power spectrum computed between −200 and −10 ms relative to stimulation.

For each experimental condition, ERSP matrices were averaged across all channels belonging to the subject‐specific ROI, yielding the local ERSP (LERSP). To extract the natural frequency of the cortical response, the LERSP was integrated over the post‐stimulus time window from 10 to 200 ms, and the frequency corresponding to the maximum power was identified from the resulting power spectral modulation (PSM) profiles. The 10–200 ms post‐stimulus window was selected to capture early cortical responses while minimizing the contribution of late, non‐local components. To quantify frequency modulation across experimental conditions, the peak frequency extracted from the PSM profile was computed for each subject and condition. Relative frequency modulation (FM) was defined as the relative deviation of the condition‐specific peak frequency from the subject‐specific mean peak frequency averaged across all experimental conditions. Specifically, for each condition, FM was computed as the difference between the peak frequency and the mean peak frequency, normalized by the mean peak frequency.

To investigate the relationship between cortical frequency modulation and motor symptom severity, hypothesis‐driven correlation analyses were performed between FM measures at the 30‐ and 60‐min time points and AIMS and UPDRS scores using Pearson's correlation coefficient.

In addition, a relative motor impairment index was computed by normalizing AIMS_30 to the individual maximum AIMS value across sessions, yielding a percentage measure of symptom severity (AIMS_rel). The analysis relating AIMS_rel to the maximum FM observed across the 30‐ and 60‐min time points was considered exploratory and aimed at providing an overall index of peak spectral modulation.

To evaluate whether group or medication effects were detectable in ongoing SMA activity independently of TMS, we performed an additional analysis of the pre‐stimulus EEG. Using the same time–frequency decomposition described above, the pre‐stimulus power spectral density (PSD) was obtained by integrating the time–frequency maps within the interval −250 to −50 ms relative to the TMS pulse and averaging across channels belonging to the subject‐specific SMA ROI.

To properly analyze specific power changes, we decided to parameterize the estimated PSD to disentangle the simultaneous presence of an aperiodic background with 1/f PSD superimposed to the true oscillatory periodic components. Following Donoghue and coll. [38], we modeled the mean PSD as the sum of putative, periodic oscillatory components parameterized by their central frequency, power, and bandwidth, as measured from Gaussian mixture model fits, plus an aperiodic component, as described from a power law fit.

To better isolate oscillatory components, the aperiodic (1/f) component of the PSD was estimated in log–log space by fitting a robust linear model to log10(power) as a function of log10(frequency) over the 8–50 Hz range. Frequency bins corresponding to prominent spectral peaks were iteratively down‐weighted during fitting to minimize peak‐induced bias. Following the parameterization procedure, we removed the fitted aperiodic component from the PSD to obtain an aperiodic‐adjusted spectrum to analyze only the emphasized true oscillatory components.

From the adjusted spectra, we identified two frequency peaks, one in the low beta range and one in the high beta–low gamma range, for each subject and condition. Group (PD vs. PDSK), Timepoint (meds‐off vs. meds‐on60), and their interaction were assessed using repeated‐measures ANOVA.

General Statistical Analysis

Statistical significance for demographic, clinical, or molecular imaging variables was evaluated using nonparametric Wilcoxon rank‐sum tests. Multiple comparisons were controlled using a false discovery rate (FDR) correction. Within‐subject cluster‐based analyses were used to test (separately for the two patient populations) the presence of significant spectral modulations between the LERSP matrix in the meds‐off condition and the LERSP matrices in the meds‐on30, meds‐on60, and meds‐on180 conditions, with an alpha level set at p < 0.05.

Differences among natural frequencies for each of the four medication conditions and for the two patient groups were investigated running a two‐way rmANOVA, where the dependent variable was the peak frequency of the PSM, the between‐subject factor was the “group” and the within‐subject factor was the “medication session”. We verified the normality of the data using the Anderson‐Darling test. Significant effects and interaction were followed up by pair‐wise comparisons, with significance level set at p < 0.05 and Tukey Honest Significant Difference method correction. Statistical analyses were conducted using JAMOVI statistical package and JMP (SAS Institute).

Results

Demographic, Clinical, and Pharmacological Characteristics

Twenty‐three patients were included (13 PD without dyskinesia and 10 PDSK). The two groups were comparable in age, handedness, side of stimulation, overall levodopa equivalent daily dose (LEDD), relative contribution of levodopa and dopamine agonists, and motor severity assessed by UPDRS‐III across medication states. Disease duration was longer in the PDSK group, whereas sex distribution showed a non‐significant trend toward a higher proportion of females in PDSK. Dyskinesias were present only in the PDSK group, were choreiform‐predominant, and followed a typical peak‐of‐dose temporal profile after levodopa intake. Concomitant dopaminergic and non‐dopaminergic medications were broadly comparable between groups, including MAO‐B inhibitors, COMT inhibitors, benzodiazepines, antidepressants, and low‐dose quetiapine, with differences reflecting individual clinical needs rather than systematic group effects (see also Supporting Information).

Neuroimaging Findings

Both PD and PDSK patients exhibited similarly reduced pathological dopaminergic uptake in the caudate nucleus and putamen bilaterally, with no significant differences between groups (see also Supporting Information).

Spectral Features of the TMS‐Evoked Response and Medication‐Related Modulation

As previously reported in our prior analysis of this dataset [29], levodopa administration modulated cortical excitability, as reflected by changes in TMS‐evoked potential (TEP) amplitude in both groups, with a larger effect in PDSK patients, peaking at 60 min after medication intake and returning to baseline value at 180 min. These amplitude findings have been described in detail elsewhere [29] and are summarized here to provide context for the present spectral analyses.

In the present study, we specifically focused on the spectral content of the TMS‐evoked response. Accordingly, the local event‐related spectral perturbation (LERSP) at baseline was characterized in both groups by the presence of two frequency peaks, one in the high beta–low gamma range and one in the low beta range (Figure 1).

Following levodopa administration, no significant spectral modulation was detected in the PD group, both in terms of LERSP power amplitude and power spectral modulation (PSM) frequency peak (Figure 1). In contrast, PDSK patients presented a significantly greater (p < 0.05, cluster‐based analysis) expression of LERSP power amplitude in the low beta frequencies range after levodopa intake at the 60 min timepoint, and subsequent normalization at 180 min (Figure 1B).

Medication‐ and Group‐Dependent Modulation of Natural Frequencies

Interestingly, the PSM of the PDSK group demonstrated consistent slowing of cortical oscillatory activity at the 30 and 60 min time points, within a similar low beta frequencies range, with a peak frequency that shifted toward the alpha range (Figure 1C). These features were consistently observed at a single‐patient level (see also Figures S2 and S3). To characterize this frequency modulation from the PSM at the individual‐subject level, we identified the two main frequency peaks: one in the high beta–low gamma range and one in the low beta range. For each frequency range, we performed a repeated measures (rm)ANOVA analysis to investigate changes in the peak frequency across the medication session and groups (Figure 2).

For the high beta range, the two‐way rmANOVA revealed no significant medication × group interaction on the peak frequency location (F(3, 63) = 1.57, p = 0.206), indicating that the effect of levodopa on the high beta eigenfrequency was comparable between PD and PDSK patients (Figure 2A). Consistently, no significant main effect of medication session (F(3, 63) = 1.36, p = 0.264) or group (F(1, 21) = 0.118, p = 0.735) was observed. The high beta eigenfrequency was overall similar in the PD (mean 34.2 Hz) and PDSK (mean 34.7 Hz) groups and remained stable across medication conditions.

For the low beta range, the two‐way rmANOVA revealed a significant medication × group interaction on the peak frequency location (F(3, 63) = 8.79, p < 0.001) (Figure 2B, bottom), indicating that the effect of levodopa on the low beta eigenfrequency differed between PD and PDSK patients.

Follow‐up analyses of the simple main effect of medication showed that, in the PDSK group, levodopa induced a significant and time‐dependent modulation of the low beta peak frequency, characterized by a progressive shift toward the alpha range that reached a minimum at 60 min and returned to baseline at 180 min (meds‐off vs. meds‐on30, p = 0.024; meds‐off vs. meds‐on60, p < 0.001; meds‐off vs. meds‐on180, p = 0.99; meds‐on180 vs. meds‐on30, p = 0.029; meds‐on180 vs. meds‐on60, p < 0.001).

In contrast, no significant effect of medication on the low beta peak frequency was observed in the PD group across time points (all p > 0.05), indicating a stable eigenfrequency throughout the medication session.

Main effects of medication (F(3, 63) = 9.8, p < 0.001) and group (F(1, 21) = 0.118, p = 0.735) are reported for completeness but are not further interpreted given the presence of a significant interaction.

Robustness of Natural Frequency Estimation to Variations in the Integration Window

The estimation of the natural frequency from the LERSP involves integration over a post‐stimulus time window. To assess whether the identified PSM peak frequencies depended on the specific choice of the integration interval, we systematically varied the integration window around the main interval used in the study (10–200 ms), extending or shortening its upper bound by up to ±100 ms.

For both PD and PDSK groups, varying the integration window resulted in expected changes in the absolute power of the PSM profiles; however, the frequency at which the PSM peaked remained remarkably stable. Specifically, the peak frequencies in the low beta range and in the high beta–low gamma range were preserved across all tested integration windows (Figure 3).

These findings indicate that the identification of the natural frequency is robust to moderate variations in the temporal integration window and does not critically depend on the specific choice of the 10–200 ms interval. Rather, the peak frequency reflects a stable spectral property of the TMS‐evoked cortical response. These remained stable also at a single patient level (see also Figures S4 and S5).

Pre‐Stimulus PSM Profiles Across Medication Conditions

To assess whether SMA oscillatory abnormalities were detectable in ongoing EEG independently of TMS, we analyzed the pre‐stimulus oscillatory components of the power spectral density within the subject‐specific SMA ROI (−250 to −50 ms relative to the TMS pulse).

In both groups, pre‐stimulus oscillatory profiles exhibited lower overall modulation and reduced peak prominence compared to the post‐stimulus interval, consistent with a flatter and more variable spectral profile. Importantly, pre‐stimulus profiles showed no systematic shift in peak frequency nor evidence of medication‐dependent differences (Figures S2 and S3).

A repeated‐measures ANOVA revealed no significant main effect of Condition (F(1, 21) = 1.15, p = 0.296), of Group (F(1, 21) = 1.06, p = 0.316), and no significant Group × Condition interaction (F(1, 21) = 2.02, p = 0.170) for the low‐beta peak frequency range. These results indicate that low‐beta peak frequency did not differ between PD and PDSK patients at rest and was not modulated by levodopa in the absence of TMS. Similar results were obtained for the high beta–low gamma range (Condition: F(1, 21) = 2.717, p = 0.114; Group: F(1, 21) = 0.441, p = 0.514; Group × Condition: F(1, 21) = 0.509, p = 0.483).

These findings indicate that the medication‐related effects reported in the main analyses predominantly arise from the TMS‐evoked spectral response, rather than reflecting changes in ongoing baseline oscillatory activity within the short pre‐stimulus window examined here. Given the limited duration of the pre‐stimulus interval, baseline estimates should be regarded as exploratory.

Motor Symptom Severity Correlates With Cortical Frequency Modulation in Dyskinetic Patients

Correlation analyses were conducted to examine the relationship between motor symptom severity and the modulation of the peak frequency of the PSM.

Given that significant modulation of the PSM peak frequency was observed exclusively in PDSK, all correlation analyses were restricted to this group.

At 30 min after pharmacological intake, a moderate negative association was observed between AIMS30 scores and frequency modulation of the PSM peak (FM30), although this relationship did not reach statistical significance (R = −0.561, p = 0.092), (Figure 4, left). At 60 min no significant correlation was found between AIMS60 scores and the corresponding frequency modulation values (FM60; R = 0.214, p = 0.553), (Figure 4, middle).

In an exploratory analysis, motor impairment was also expressed as a relative measure normalized to the individual maximum AIMS value (AIMSrel). We then examined its relationship with the maximum (absolute) frequency modulation observed across meds‐on30 and meds‐on60 experimental conditions. This analysis revealed a negative association (R = −0.664, 95% CI [−0.91, −0.06]) (Figure 4, right), which should be interpreted cautiously given the exploratory nature of this analysis.

To assess whether frequency modulation was related to overall motor impairment severity, additional correlation analyses were performed using the motor section of the Unified Parkinson's Disease Rating Scale (UPDRS‐III). No significant correlations were observed between UPDRS‐III scores and any frequency modulation measure.

Discussion

We characterized the spectral content of increased SMA excitability following levodopa intake in PD patients. Notably, patients with PD and dyskinesia displayed frequency modulation with a transient shift toward beta low oscillations, paralleling the dyskinetic effect of levodopa. Aberrant cortico‐subcortical oscillatory dynamics have been identified in several aspects of movement disorders neurophysiology [39, 40]. Our work adds to the literature by describing neural oscillatory alterations in the dyskinesia‐producing network, linking aberrant SMA electrophysiology to the pathogenesis of LID.

Notably, the dominant frequency of the SMA oscillatory response in all patients was in the beta high to gamma range, consistent with previous findings in healthy subjects [30]. This indicates that in PD, structural damage caused by dopaminergic denervation does not necessarily result in a fixed oscillatory derangement, but instead leads to an altered cortical oscillatory response to pharmacological stimuli (e.g., levodopa) and to the tuning required for proper execution of motor or non‐motor tasks [41, 42].

The selective beta low frequency modulation observed in the dyskinetic state could reflect several local and network‐level pathophysiological mechanisms. The oscillatory content of brain responses to TMS is generally thought to depend on the functional state of the targeted area, unmasking defining oscillatory properties and functional architecture specific to that area [30, 32, 43, 44, 45, 46, 47]. Accordingly, we hypothesize that dyskinetic patients exhibit degraded cortical processing and altered responses to levodopa, resulting from aberrant plasticity under chronic dopamine denervation and pharmacological stimulation. This may also include cortical maladaptive compensatory mechanisms or their exhaustion [48]. In this context, levodopa‐related reductions in oscillatory activity may reflect the emergence of local reentrant activity that directly disrupts SMA function, particularly with regard to action initiation and inhibition [49]. Impairments in internally guided motor control [50] and in the sequential execution of movements [51] may ultimately lead to dyskinesias.

At a network level, reentrant connectivity links cortical areas to the thalamus [52]. In this context, our finding may reflect levodopa‐related strengthening of long‐range thalamo‐cortical oscillatory activity involved in sensorimotor processing [23, 53]. In dyskinetic patients, levodopa could entrain the SMA into beta low idling rhythms favoring incoming sensorimotor information. Poor online filtering would lead to a failure of selection between competing motor responses, resulting in dyskinetic movements. The striato‐thalamic cholinergic system may play a significant role in this process, as it is relatively preserved in dyskinetic versus non‐dyskinetic PD patients [54] and is known to modulate thalamo‐cortical slow oscillations [47].

As a third, not‐exclusive hypothesis, the responsiveness of the SMA with beta low oscillations following TMS perturbation may reflect entrainment of the subthalamic nucleus [55], which has been proposed to contribute significantly to the development of LID [56]. Interestingly, a shift toward 4–10 Hz oscillatory activity has been shown in subthalamic local field potentials of dyskinetic PD patients in response to medication [57].

Although several studies have extensively investigated beta oscillatory dynamics, their functional role in motor control remains incompletely understood. In the context of PD, beta oscillations, particularly in the beta low range, are generally considered anti‐kinetic [58]. This view is difficult to reconcile with our results, which show transient dopamine‐dependent beta low oscillatory activity that parallels the dyskinetic state. One possible explanation would posit a role for beta low oscillations in dynamically adjusting somatosensory gain thresholds in PD, thereby favoring the selection of specific motor and non‐motor tasks, particularly under low‐dopamine conditions [42, 59].

The correlation data from this study are particularly informative. Specifically, we did not observe a correlation between dyskinesia severity and the magnitude of the SMA frequency response; instead, a larger frequency shift was associated with a greater proportion of the maximal dyskinesia severity of each individual patient already reached at 30 min. These findings suggest that, more than peak dyskinesia severity per se, for which we nevertheless observed a significant trend, levodopa‐related aberrant oscillatory properties of the SMA determine the rapidity of dyskinesia development. Furthermore, the absence of correlations with disease severity for akinetic–rigid symptoms (i.e., UPDRS‐III score) or with dopaminergic deficit (SPECT data) underscores the selectivity of this effect for the levodopa‐related dyskinetic component.

The use of combined TMS‐EEG is increasingly being explored as a valuable tool to index cortical excitability, connectivity, and neuroplasticity. However, studies applying this approach in patients with PD remain limited.

An increased beta (15–30 Hz) oscillatory response to TMS over the primary motor cortex (M1) was reported in medicated PD patients who had previously undergone ventrolateral thalamotomy [2, 45]. In that study, the beta response was stronger and longer lasting in the unoperated hemisphere compared with the hemisphere subjected to thalamotomy. In contrast, in 15 medicated PD patients, beta power oscillations following M1 TMS were found to be comparable to those recorded in age‐matched healthy controls [31]. More recently, in a study including 46 medicated PD patients Zhu et al. [60], reported reduced beta oscillations at the latency of the P180 component in the right parietal region following TMS over the left M1. In the same study, PD patients exhibited higher P30 amplitudes following M1 stimulation compared with healthy subjects, with P30 amplitude positively correlating with the severity of motor symptoms.

In a study of 28 drug‐naïve (de novo) PD patients Leodori et al. [61], showed reduced M1 TEP P30 amplitude compared with healthy controls, while pre‐SMA TEP N40 amplitudes were similar between groups. In these patients, TEPs did not correlate with either clinical or objectively measured bradykinesia (assessed by kinematic recordings of finger‐tapping movements), nor with reduced fractional anisotropy (measured using 3T MRI) of the white matter tracts connecting M1 to the ventral lateral anterior thalamic nucleus. These findings suggested the presence of intracortical changes or alterations in functional connectivity within cortico‐subcortical circuits. In a previous study by the same authors, conducted in 20 more advanced unmedicated PD patients, smaller P30 responses were observed over both the contralateral and ipsilateral M1 relative to the most bradykinetic side, together with increased pre‐SMA N40 amplitudes. In that cohort, contralateral M1 P30 amplitude correlated with bradykinesia severity. Dopaminergic therapy normalized both M1 P30 and pre‐SMA N40 amplitudes [61].

Interpreting our findings in the context of these previous studies is challenging, due both to the heterogeneity of pharmacological conditions across cohorts and to the fact that patients with PD and levodopa‐induced dyskinesias (LID) were excluded from prior investigations. Notably, none of the previous studies specifically investigated the SMA. In addition, several methodological variables –such as target selection and neuronavigation strategies– remain open issues that warrant dedicated investigation beyond the scope of the present study [62, 63, 64].

An important limitation of this study is the absence of a dedicated movement control condition, as PDSK patients were examined during dyskinesia while PD patients remained largely immobile. However, several factors mitigate this concern, including the automatic interruption of stimulation in the presence of movement‐related misalignment, the lack of correlation between LID amplitude and SMA excitability [29] and converging fMRI evidence demonstrating SMA hyperactivity in medicated patients with LID even before the emergence of overt dyskinesias [25, 27].

Additional limitations include heterogeneity in non‐dopaminergic medications, which were intentionally not withdrawn to avoid patient discomfort and the introduction of poorly characterized acute effects on TMS–EEG measures; however, careful matching of dopaminergic treatment between groups was ensured.

Differences in disease duration may also represent a confound, although this factor is intrinsically linked to dyskinesia prevalence.

Regarding two main methodological limitations, namely the choice of the temporal integration window for natural frequency estimation and the relationship between TMS‐evoked spectral signatures and ongoing SMA activity, natural frequency estimation proved robust to variations in the post‐stimulus window (Figure 3), and pre‐stimulus analyses revealed no systematic medication‐related differences (see Supporting Information and Figures S2 and S3).

In conclusion, we have identified levodopa‐induced frequency modulation of SMA cortical beta oscillations using a TMS‐EEG paradigm in patients with PD and peak‐of‐dose dyskinesias. This marker of transition to the dyskinetic state, possibly reflecting local and network derangements, further supports the interpretation of dyskinesia as primarily a disorder of oscillatory circuit function that could be monitored and treated using personalized approaches. Future studies investigating frequency‐specific, directed functional connectivity may help disentangle the hierarchical organization of other network nodes involved in this aberrant activity, shed light on the mechanisms of its generation, and guide the development of new neuromodulation therapies.
