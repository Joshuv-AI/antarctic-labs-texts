Eye movements are a low-cost orienting behavior relative to key presses
Authors: Rania Qais, Robert T. Knight, Shlomit Yuval-Greenberg
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
SummaryIn laboratory-based studies of visual working memory (VWM), observers typically rely on internal storage to manipulate information that is no longer perceptually available. However, when information remains accessible, observers often prefer to explore and resample the environment rather than depend on memory. Evidence suggests that this trade-off depends on the cost of the sampling action. Here, we examined whether the type of orienting behavior modulates this cost, specifically whether eye movements are a lower cost means of orienting than key presses. Across two experiments, we compared sampling through eye movements or key presses while participants performed a VWM task. Participants preferred external sampling when access occurred via eye movements but relied more on internal storage when access required a key press. These findings suggest that the cost of sampling information reflects not only computational and motor demands but also how naturally the orienting behavior fits within the human behavioral repertoire.

Introduction

Visual working memory (VWM) is the cognitive system responsible for storing and manipulating visual information over brief intervals.1 Over the past decades, extensive research has examined the nature and limits of VWM capacity, with a particular focus on how much visual information can be maintained and kept accessible for ongoing tasks.2,3 Some accounts describe VWM as a flexible resource that varies with context and attentional allocation,3 whereas others propose a more discrete and relatively fixed capacity limit.2,4 Despite these theoretical differences, there is broad agreement that VWM utilization depends on multiple factors, including the type of information stored,5 individual differences in capacity and control,6,7 and task and environmental demands.6,8 Under typical laboratory conditions, healthy adults are estimated to maintain approximately three to four items over a brief delay.9,10,11

Traditional VWM research has largely relied on highly controlled paradigms in which perceptual input disappears and reappears while participants’ information-seeking behaviors are constrained. In these settings, observers must rely primarily on internal storage. More recently, this approach has been extended to more naturalistic contexts, where observers are free to move and interact with their environment.12 Such contexts better approximate real-world vision, in which visual information typically remains available after it has been viewed, allowing observers to actively access and resample it as needed.12,13,14,15,16,17,18 Across these studies, a consistent pattern emerges: when visual information remains accessible, observers rely less on internal storage and instead repeatedly resample the environment. For example, Ballard et al. examined VWM during an active visuomotor copying task.13 When participants memorized a block configuration and reproduced it from memory, performance aligned with the canonical three-to-four-item capacity. However, when they could freely shift their gaze between the model and the workspace, they tended to encode only one to two items at a time and frequently returned to the model. This pattern reflects a strategic underutilization of VWM when external information is readily available. When observers can access information directly in the environment, there is little need to maximize internal storage, as they can resample relevant details on demand. Accordingly, Van der Stigchel proposed that under natural conditions, the visual environment functions as an “external memory” providing information on demand.19

Critically, the balance between internal storage and external sampling depends on the cost of accessing information in the environment. Draschkow et al. demonstrated this in immersive virtual reality by manipulating the physical distance between the model and the workspace in an object-copying task.12 Increasing the distance required greater locomotive effort to access visual information, whereas reducing it lowered access costs. As effort increased, participants relied more on VWM; when effort decreased, they relied more on perceptual resampling. Similarly, Somai et al. increased the time cost of eye movements by delaying the reappearance of a visual template following a saccade.18 Longer delays led to fewer saccades to the model and longer dwell times on it, suggesting a shift away from frequent resampling and toward greater reliance on internal representations. A recent meta-analysis of 28 experiments using related paradigms20 further supports this conclusion, showing that this effect is robust across different cost manipulations and dependent measures.

Different aspects of orienting behavior can determine its cognitive cost. Cost can depend on the time required to execute the action,18 the physical effort it entails,12,21 or the degree of cognitive computation it demands.22 It may also depend on how natural the behavior is within the human behavioral repertoire, which can influence how readily it is initiated in a given context. Importantly, prior studies examining the trade-off between internal storage and external access have manipulated cost by varying the effort or time required to perform the same type of orienting behavior.12,18 In such paradigms, the mode of access, typically eye and head movements, remains constant, while only its extent or the effort required to produce it changes. Here, we propose a complementary approach. Rather than manipulating the magnitude of effort within a single orienting behavior, we use the storage-access trade-off to test whether different orienting behaviors inherently differ in cost. To this end, we operationally define the cognitive cost of an orienting behavior as the threshold for choosing internal storage over external sampling and apply this approach to investigate the role of eye movements in natural information seeking.

Eye movements are the primary and evolutionarily grounded mechanism through which humans acquire high-resolution visual information. Because detailed visual processing is restricted to the fovea (the center of vision), observers continuously shift their gaze so that relevant regions of the environment fall onto this high-acuity area. Eye movements have often been described as relatively “cheap” in attentional terms, as they are used continuously and largely automatically to sample the environment, which seemingly requires little attentional effort23,24 and only minimal muscular fatigue.

However, recent studies suggest that eye movements are not entirely without cost.25 They involve multiple forms of cost, including perceptual costs, such as temporary loss of visual information during saccades26 as well as planning and execution costs associated with selecting a saccade target and preparing a goal-directed eye movement toward it.27 These costs can influence when and where observers choose to move their eyes.28,29 Although it is now clear that eye movements are not cost-free, it remains largely unknown how their cost compares with those associated with other information-acquisition behaviors. This question is particularly relevant in modern computerized environments, where access to information is often mediated by relatively unnatural actions such as pressing a key or moving a cursor. It remains an open question how costly eye movements are relative to these likely simpler, yet less natural, actions that can provide comparable access to visual information.

We examined this question by comparing the trade-off between internal storage and external sampling while manipulating the action used to access external information: eye movements, or key presses. The rationale of the study, based on previous literature, is that when visual access is achieved through a less costly action, observers will rely more on external sampling and less on VWM storage, whereas more costly actions will promote greater reliance on internal storage. In this way, the storage-access trade-off can serve as an implicit measure of the relative cost of different information-seeking behaviors. To test this, we used a task inspired by the game Dobble (Spot It! in the US). In the first experiment, participants were asked to identify a common item between two sets, presented either simultaneously, allowing free eye movements between them, or sequentially in the center, with switching between sets achieved via key presses (Figure 1). A second experiment included the same task but controlled for differences between conditions in the peripheral input. In both experiments, the rate of switches between the sets, whether by eye movements or key presses, was taken as an index of the extent to which observers sampled external visual information rather than relied on internal storage. In contrast, the number of small saccades performed within a set between switches (“gathering” saccades) was taken as an index of reliance on internal VWM storage.Figure 1Procedure of Experiment 1(A) Simultaneous condition. Based on the assumption that lower cost orienting behaviors promote greater reliance on external sampling, we hypothesized that a condition characterized by more frequent switching and fewer gathering saccades than another condition would reflect lower access costs. Conversely, fewer switches and more gathering saccades would indicate greater reliance on internal storage and, therefore, higher access costs. We hypothesized that eye movements are a lower-cost visual orienting behavior than key presses and, therefore, predicted that there would be more switches between sets and fewer gathering saccades within a set when eye movements, rather than key presses, were used to access visual information.

The findings confirmed this hypothesis: when switching was achieved via eye movements, observers performed more switches and fewer gathering saccades. This indicated greater reliance on external information and reduced reliance on internal storage, suggesting that eye movements are a less costly means of accessing information than key presses. These findings highlight how modern methods of accessing information, although common in our highly computerized world, may impose greater cognitive costs and consequently shape how we interact with the environment.

In addition, we analyzed the classical behavioral measurements: accuracy rates, and reaction times (RTs), to examine the subsidiary hypothesis that accessing information through eye movements, rather than key presses, would lead to faster and more accurate search performance. Furthermore, we predicted that the condition showing greater reliance on VWM storage, reflected by more gathering saccades and a lower rate of switches, would also exhibit steeper changes in RTs and accuracy as the number of items increased. To test these predictions, we compared accuracy rates and RTs across the two conditions.

All four dependent measurements were analyzed separately using four repeated-measures 3 × 2 ANOVAs, with set size (3, 5, or 7) and display condition (simultaneous or sequential) as factors. Benjamini-Hochberg false discovery rate (FDR) correction was applied (q = 0.05) on the main effects and interactions, and the corrected positive FDR (pFDR) values are reported along with the uncorrected p values.

RTs

The RT findings were consistent with the accuracy effects, indicating that there were no speed-accuracy trade-offs. As expected, larger set sizes resulted in longer RTs (main effect of set size: F(2, 38) = 293.34, p < 0.001, pFDR < 0.001, ƞ2p = 0.94; positive linear trend: t(19) = 17.96, p < 0.001, Cohen’s d = 2.19). Participants took longer to find the target in the sequential condition than in the simultaneous condition (main effect of display condition: F(1, 19) = 59.69, p < 0.001, pFDR = 0.012, ƞ2p = 0.76). A significant interaction indicated that the effect of set size on RT varied between display conditions (F(2, 38) = 11.74, p < 0.001, pFDR = 0.016, ƞ2p = 0.38).

The interaction was interpreted by performing the analysis separately on the two conditions. In both conditions, there was a significant main effect of set size (simultaneous: F(2, 38) = 232.38, p < 0.001, ƞ2p = 0.92; sequential: F(2, 38) = 152.49, p < 0.001, ƞ2p = 0.89) with a positive linear trend (simultaneous: t(19) = 17.51, p < 0.001, Cohen’s d = 2.68; sequential: t(19) = 12.83, p < 0.001, Cohen’s d = 2.07). Examining the effect of condition on each set size separately revealed that it took more time to find the target in the sequential vs. the simultaneous condition at all set sizes (3 items: F(1, 19) = 38.76, p < 0.001, ƞ2p = 0.76; 5 items: F(1, 19) = 92.07, p < 0.001, ƞ2p = 0.83, and 7 items: F(1, 19) = 37.70, p < 0.001, ƞ2p = 0.66; Figure 3B).

Switching rate

Switches between the two sets were performed via eye movements in the simultaneous condition or key presses in the sequential condition (Figure 2). The rate of switching within a trial was used as an index of the extent of external sampling occurring in that trial: more switches indicate greater reliance on repeatedly accessing information from the visual display rather than maintaining the information internally.

The analysis revealed a main effect of set size (F(2, 38) = 97.24, p < 0.001, pFDR = 0.033, ƞ2p = 0.84), indicating that as set size increased, participants performed fewer switches per second, either saccades in the simultaneous condition or key presses in the sequential condition (simultaneous: negative linear trend: t(19) = −12.23, p < 0.001, Cohen’s d = −0.87; sequential: negative linear trend: t(19) = −8.10, p < 0.001, Cohen’s d = −0.50). There was also a main effect of the display condition (F(1, 19) = 37.04, p < 0.001, pFDR = 0.037, ƞ2p = 0.66), with a higher rate of switching in the simultaneous condition than in the sequential condition.

Although there was no evidence for an interaction between the factors (F(2, 38) = 1.98, p = 0.151, pFDR = 0.054; Figure 3C), we performed the analysis separately on the two conditions. This analysis revealed a significant effect of set size in both conditions (simultaneous: F(2, 38) = 79.41, p < 0.001, ƞ2p = 0.81, negative linear trend: t(19) = −12.23, p < 0.001, Cohen’s d = −0.87; sequential: F(2, 38) = 48.05, p < 0.001, ƞ2p = 0.72, negative linear trend: t(19) = −8.10, p < 0.001, Cohen’s d = −0.50). Analyzing the three set sizes separately revealed that there was more switching in the simultaneous than the sequential condition for all set sizes (3 items: F(1, 19) = 30.67, p < 0.001, ƞ2p = 0.62; 5 items: F(1, 19) = 39.10, p < 0.001, ƞ2p = 0.67; 7 items: F(1, 19) = 40.61, p < 0.001, ƞ2p = 0.68).

Gathering

The small saccades performed within a set between each pair of switches (“gathering” saccades) reflect the number of items that were fixated within a set before switching to the next set (Figure 2). While there are various cognitive functions that may influence gazing behavior within a task, we refer to the gathering saccades as an index that is tightly linked to the reliance on VWM storage within a trial.

There was a significant main effect of display condition, with a lower gathering index for the simultaneous condition relative to the sequential condition (F(1, 19) = 32.99, p < 0.001, pFDR = 0.025, ƞ2p = 0.63; Figure 3D), suggesting lower reliance on internal storage in this condition. We also found a significant main effect of set size (F(2, 38) = 35.06, p < 0.001, pFDR = 0.020, ƞ2p = 0.65), with gathering positively associated with set size in both display conditions (simultaneous: F(2, 38) = 108.07, p < 0.001, ƞ2p = 0.85, positive linear trend: t(19) = 12.52, p < 0.001, Cohen’s d = 0.97; sequential: F(2, 38) = 24.28, p < 0.001, ƞ2p = 0.56, positive linear trend: t(19) = 5.17, p < 0.001, Cohen’s d = 0.72). A significant interaction was found between set size and display condition (F(2, 38) = 15.01, p < 0.001, pFDR = 0.029, ƞ2p = 0.44). When analyzing the three set sizes separately, we found less gathering in the simultaneous than the sequential condition for all set sizes, but this effect was smaller for the 7-items condition (3 items: F(1, 19) = 34.04, p < 0.001, ƞ2p = 0.64; 5 items: F(1, 19) = 34.56, p < 0.001, ƞ2p = 0.64; 7 items: F(1, 19) = 27.83, p < 0.001, ƞ2p = 0.59).

Task performance strategies

The different task-performance strategies and their corresponding switching and gathering patterns are illustrated in Figure 4. Examining individual participants’ gaze patterns reveals a consistent pattern in the simultaneous condition. Most participants exhibit behavior closely resembling the predicted pattern of the “one-to-one” strategy (Supplementary Material S2), which means a single item is selected from one card and compared with a single item on the other card before switching again. Specifically, their performance is characterized by minimal gathering, typically 0–1 saccades per switch-episode, and an absence of a clear dependency between consecutive episodes. In contrast, gaze patterns in the sequential condition are considerably less consistent. There are substantial individual differences, and the observed patterns do not systematically resemble any of the predefined strategies. Example participants are shown in Figure 5, and data from the remaining participants are presented in Supplementary Material S2. The grand-average pattern (Figure 5) reflects the same overall phenomenon: a pattern fitting the “one-to-one” strategy in the simultaneous condition and no coherent strategy in the sequential condition. Notably, the positive linear trend observed in the grand-average trace may arise from two distinct sources: genuine positive within-participant dependencies between consecutive episodes, or stable between-participant differences in overall gathering behavior, as some participants consistently produced more gathering saccades than others. This can generate a positive slope in the aggregated data even in the absence of a systematic within-participant effect. In summary, the analyses provide statistical support for the “one-to-one” strategy over the other two strategies in both the simultaneous and sequential conditions. This conclusion is based on a preference for lower rather than higher gathering rates, which is inconsistent with the “set-to-set” strategy, and the absence of negative correlations between consecutive episodes, which is inconsistent with the “one-to-set” strategy. Nevertheless, the descriptive data indicate a clear and robust fit to the “one-to-one” strategy in the simultaneous condition, whereas the sequential condition shows greater variability and a weaker correspondence to the predicted pattern. We therefore conclude that participants reliably adopt the “one-to-one” strategy in the simultaneous condition, whereas behavior in the sequential condition is less consistent and only partially aligned with this strategy.

Summary of Experiment 1 results

Experiment 1 compared two display conditions. In the simultaneous condition, with two cards presented side by side, switching between them was achieved through large saccades. In the sequential condition, only one card was presented at a time, and switching occurred via a key press. The simultaneous condition mimicked natural viewing, in which eye movements are used to gather and compare perceptual information. In contrast, the sequential condition resembled a computerized setting, in which data are accessed through a key press rather than through natural visual exploration.

The findings show that in the sequential condition, participants performed more gathering saccades and fewer switching than in the simultaneous condition. This indicates that in the simultaneous condition, there was less reliance on VWM and more on foveal perceptual input. In other words, participants preferred to switch their gaze back and forth between the cards to compare the items one by one, rather than fixate on a few items within one card, store them in VWM, and then compare them to items on the other card. These findings supported our hypothesis that eye movements represent a low-cost behavior for accessing information relative to key presses. Therefore, when eye movements are used to access perceptual input, observers rely on the external environment rather than on VWM storage, and the opposite is true when key presses are used to access information.

However, a serious limitation of Experiment 1 was that it did not control for peripheral input. In the sequential condition, participants viewed only one card at a time, whereas in the simultaneous condition, they viewed both. This means that in the simultaneous condition, peripheral perceptual input was available, whereas in the sequential condition it was not. An alternative explanation for the findings is that participants prefer to switch more and gather less when peripheral information is available. The purpose of the second experiment was to address this question by providing identical peripheral input in both conditions. In the sequential condition of this experiment, two cards were presented: one at the center and one in the periphery. Participants were instructed to fixate only on the central card, and this was enforced with a gaze-contingent procedure. They could switch between the cards using the left and right arrow keys. Upon pressing the key, the central card moved to the periphery, and the peripheral card moved to the center (keeping the right card always on the right and the left on the left) as shown in Figure 7. Discussion

The study examined how different means of accessing visual information shape the trade-off between reliance on internal storage and external sampling. Across two experiments, participants searched for a common item between two sets, with access to the sets achieved either through eye movements (simultaneous condition) or key presses (sequential condition). Large saccades between sets (in the simultaneous condition) or key presses shifting from one set to another (in the sequential condition) were taken as indices of external sampling. Smaller saccades performed within a set (“gathering” saccades) were taken as an index of reliance on internal VWM storage.

The findings showed that when information was accessed via eye movements, participants relied minimally on VWM. They typically performed only one or two gathering saccades before making a switching saccade to the other set. This pattern indicates greater reliance on external sampling than on internal storage in this condition. In contrast, when access required a key press, participants made more saccades, approximately three to five, before switching. This pattern indicates greater reliance on VWM than on external sampling. Importantly, this effect was evident even when peripheral input was fully controlled across conditions.

Eye movements provide a relatively low-cost means of accessing information

We based the rationale of this study on previous findings showing that the trade-off between external sampling of visual information and internal storage depends on the cognitive cost of the behavior used to access information. When access is easy, participants prefer external resampling over internal storage.12,18 As access becomes more costly, reliance on VWM increases. Sahakian et al. suggested that this cost-benefit balance reflects not only changes in encoding but also changes in the decision threshold for relying on stored information rather than resampling the environment.16 In the present study, we examined this interplay as an index for comparing the cognitive cost of two different behaviors, eye movements and key presses.

The finding of lower reliance on internal storage and greater reliance on external sampling, when eye movements were used to access information, supports the hypothesis that eye movements provide a relatively low-cost mechanism for acquiring task-relevant information.18,19,20 This raises the question of what makes eye movements, in the present setting, a less costly means of accessing information than key presses. We propose that eye movements are less costly because they represent a more natural orienting behavior in evolutionary terms.

To support this claim, we first consider a few alternative explanations. Specifically, we examine three factors that may affect the cost of a behavior—locomotive effort, access time, and computational complexity—and explain why these factors are unlikely to account for the observed difference.

The first possibility is locomotive effort. Previous studies have shown that increasing locomotive effort shifts the balance away from external sampling toward greater reliance on internal storage.12 It could be the case that eye movements are less costly because they require less locomotor effort than button presses. Indeed, recent oculomotor research suggests that even subtle differences in motor cost can influence orienting behavior. For example, using pupil size as an index of effort,31 researchers demonstrated that the cost of eye movements varies as a function of movement direction, and that observers tend to prefer lower effort eye movements even when searching complex natural scenes or performing cognitively demanding tasks.27,29 Thus, we cannot fully exclude the possibility that eye movements were favored in the present task because they carried a small motor advantage. However, given that both eye movements and key presses require very little muscular effort,32,33 locomotive effort is unlikely to account for the robust effects observed here.

A second potential source of difference is the time required to gain access to information following the orienting behavior. Previous studies have shown that when the orienting action (e.g., an eye movement) provides immediate access to information, observers rely more on external access than on internal storage. However, this balance shifts when the delay before information becomes available increases.18 In our design, we attempted to control for the reduced visual sensitivity during saccades by perceptually equating the visual access time across behaviors. Specifically, in the sequential condition, each key press was followed by a 50 ms blank interval before presentation of the second set. This interval was intended to approximate the duration of a saccade, which lasts approximately 50 ms based on our preliminary measurements and previous accounts.32,34

Although this manipulation likely provided the closest possible temporal match between conditions, we cannot fully rule out the possibility that effects associated with saccadic suppression extend beyond the eye movement itself. This possibility appears unlikely in light of recent evidence suggesting that the transient reduction in visual sensitivity during saccades may arise primarily from the processing of motion blur rather than from an active neural suppression mechanism.35 Furthermore, even if suppression lingers beyond the eye movement, this would make saccades more, rather than less, costly. These considerations support the conclusion that differences in access time are unlikely to explain the observed effects.

A third possible factor is the computational complexity of the behavior. An eye movement is a goal-directed action that requires computation of a ballistic movement toward a precise spatial target. In contrast, a key press involves a simple downward finger movement. Therefore, eye movements likely involve more complex motor computation, which would again make them a more costly behavior than key presses.

Taken together, these considerations suggest that the most likely explanation for the lower cost of eye movements is that they evolved as a natural means of acquiring visual information. In contrast, key presses, although common in digital environments, are evolutionarily recent and did not evolve for orienting purposes. This suggests that the cost of an orienting action depends not only on motor, perceptual, or computational demands but also on how natural the action is within the human behavioral repertoire. Within the repertoire of human orienting behaviors, eye movements hold a central position.

The conclusion that eye movements represent a low-cost orienting behavior is consistent with previous evidence suggesting that eye movements are the default, highly practiced mechanism for acquiring high-resolution visual information.23,36,37 Saccades are supported by highly specialized subcortical circuitry that enables their rapid and efficient execution, making them well-suited for continuous visual exploration.38,39 Moreover, during natural vision, eye movements are deployed routinely and often with limited attention and conscious awareness.40,41 While these previous studies provide support for the view that eye movements are efficient and relatively low-cost behaviors, the present study extends this literature by directly comparing eye movements with a behavior that is simpler in its motor demands, namely, a key press, but evolutionarily recent and not developed for orienting purposes.

A previous study by Li et al. used a task similar to the present one and compared a condition in which visual information was fully accessible to a condition in which it was revealed only when the computer mouse hovered above it.15 Consistent with our findings, they showed greater VWM use in the unnatural (mouse) condition than in the natural free-view condition. Although their findings align with our hypothesis, that study was not designed to isolate the effect of free exploration or to examine the role of eye movements in VWM utilization. First, eye movements were not restricted in the mouse condition and likely followed the cursor, resembling the free-view condition. Second, the free-view and mouse conditions differed not only in motor responses but also in the availability of visual information, which was fully visible in the free-view condition but largely occluded in the mouse condition. In our design, full access to visual information was provided in both conditions, but in one it was achieved through natural orienting behavior (eye movements), and in the other through an artificial behavior (key presses). This design allowed us to examine the link between the means of accessing information and VWM utilization.

A related perspective comes from decision-making research showing that the format through which information is accessed can influence information sampling and integration. Evidence suggests that when information access is restricted or effortful, for example, when information must be revealed through mouse clicks, people rely more on sequential and deliberate sampling strategies. In contrast, when information is openly available and easily accessible, integration becomes faster and more automatic.42,43 Although these studies focused on decision-making rather than VWM, they converge with the present findings in suggesting that cognitive strategy depends not only on the availability of information but also on the means by which this information is accessed.

The present study extends this idea to VWM by showing that even when task-relevant information is available, the means of access shape whether observers rely more on external sampling or internal storage. Specifically, we show that when information is easily accessed through eye movements, participants exploit the availability of external information, relying more on external sampling and less on VWM. In contrast, when access is more effortful and requires key presses, exploration becomes more sequential and memory-dependent. Consistent with Glöckner and Betsch’s42 argument that apparent cognitive limitations may partly arise from the experimental method used to access information, the present findings suggest that computerized access methods may artificially increase reliance on internal memory.

Scanning strategies

Examining the strategies used to perform this task revealed that in both conditions, the dominant strategy was the “one-to-one” strategy, which involved sampling one item from one card and comparing it with one item from the other card. This strategy is consistent with relatively low reliance on VWM storage, as only a small amount of information is maintained before switching to the next set, and with relatively high reliance on external sampling, reflected in frequent switching between cards. Although this was the dominant strategy in both conditions, it matched the gaze patterns more robustly and consistently in the simultaneous condition than in the sequential condition. This indicates that while observers relied more heavily on external sampling than on VWM storage in both conditions, this tendency was stronger and more consistent in the simultaneous condition.

An important question that remains only partially addressed in the present study concerns individual differences in scanning strategies. In the simultaneous condition, participants showed remarkably similar scanning patterns, with relatively little variability between individuals. In contrast, the sequential condition revealed substantial individual differences and a much less coherent pattern of behavior. Participants in this condition often exhibited mixed or inconsistent scanning patterns that did not clearly correspond to a single strategy.

One possible interpretation is that orienting through eye movements, which evolved as a natural orienting behavior, is more deeply embedded in the cognitive architecture and therefore produces more consistent patterns both within and between individuals. In contrast, key presses represent a relatively recent and less natural human behavior, and may therefore be less constrained by stable orienting mechanisms, resulting in greater variability across individuals.

Future studies using larger samples and broader assessments of individual characteristics could examine this question more directly. For example, it would be valuable to test whether specific personality traits and sensory abilities are associated with different exploration strategies or with greater reliance on external sampling versus internal storage.

The effect of voluntary control on VWM utilization

Our findings showed that observers performed an average of three to five gathering saccades in the sequential condition. This may appear inconsistent with the findings of Kvitelashvili and Kessler, who used an adapted change detection task and found that participants preferred repeated perceptual sampling over reliance on VWM. In their study, switching was executed via key presses, as in our sequential condition, yet they observed underutilization of VWM.14

This difference is likely explained by differences in task demands. Our task required visual search and comparison between two equally informative displays, whereas their task required recall and reconstruction. In their paradigm, switching often meant moving from a meaningful source of information (the model) to a blank or partially completed reconstruction screen. This reliance on precise recall and reproduction may have encouraged serial resampling rather than sustained memory storage. In contrast, our task allowed for partial encoding strategies, such as scanning for likely matches, which supported more flexible use of VWM depending on switching costs. This illustrates how task structure and stimulus characteristics can critically shape VWM strategies, underscoring the need for further research on the interaction between motor control and working memory.

The effect of item number on VWM utilization

The present findings show that as the number of items increased, participants relied more on their VWM and less on perceptual resampling in both display conditions. This effect was stronger in the sequential than the simultaneous condition. The link between cognitive load and reliance on VWM is consistent with Draschkow et al., who used a visuomotor task in an immersive environment and found that reliance on VWM increased as task demands grew.12 Similarly, Droll et al. demonstrated that participants flexibly adapted their reliance on perception and VWM depending on task requirements: when a single feature was relevant, they relied primarily on perceptual input, but when multiple features were relevant, they stored information in advance.44 Together with the present results, these findings align with Baddeley’s model, which proposes that working memory engagement scales with cognitive demand.45 Our study extends this idea by showing that the relationship between load and VWM utilization is weaker when eye movements are used to access information. In the simultaneous condition, participants maintained a strong preference for external sampling even as cognitive load increased. This suggests that the availability of eye movements sustained reliance on external information, reducing the need to store information internally even under higher load.

Visual search and foraging

The present findings are consistent with research on visual foraging tasks, in which participants search for multiple targets. Studies have examined the effect of eye movements on visual foraging by permitting or restricting eye movements in different groups while they were performing a visual search of multiple targets. Consistent with our findings, these studies show that when observers can move their eyes freely, they exhibit more efficient oculomotor dynamics, which facilitate natural visual exploration and minimize memory load. In contrast, when eye movements are restricted, oculomotor behavior shifts toward a more ambient exploration mode, relying on covert attention and increased dependence on internal memory.46,47 In the study by Jóhannesson et al., participants performed a multiple-target foraging task by either tapping targets with their fingers or fixating them with their eyes to compare how different response modalities affected attentional selection and foraging behavior.46 Results showed notable differences between finger foraging and gaze foraging in how observers select multiple targets. Finger foraging showed a strong dissociation: participants switched easily between target categories when targets differed by a single feature but tended to make long runs of the same category during conjunction foraging, indicating more constrained switching under higher attentional demands. In contrast, gaze foraging demonstrated much less difference between feature and conjunction conditions, with a higher proportion of observers switching randomly between categories even under conjunction conditions.

These findings reinforce the idea that eye movements are not merely the overt manifestation of attention but serve as a natural mechanism through which information is gathered and updated. Constraining eye movements, therefore, alters cognitive processes. Similar to our finding that eye movements are a low-cost orienting behavior that reduces reliance on VWM, visual foraging studies demonstrate that overt exploration governs the balance between external sampling and internal maintenance.

Stability and instability in retinotopic-based vs. world-based reference maps

An important question raised by our experimental design concerns the reference frame in which items are encoded, stored, and processed. Experiment 2 was designed to hold the retinotopic mapping constant, while the world-based coordinates of the observed items inevitably differed between conditions. In the simultaneous condition, the spatial layout of the two cards remained stable throughout the trial, and switching between cards was achieved by moving the eyes within this stable visual scene. In contrast, in the sequential condition of Experiment 1, the two cards occupied the same world-based location, rendering the visual scene unstable and dependent on key presses that changed the currently available visual input. Consequently, in Experiment 1, the world-based coordinates of the observed items remained stable in the simultaneous condition but became unstable in the sequential condition.

This difference between conditions reflects a fundamental distinction between eye movements and other, less natural, behaviors that can be used to access visual information. Although all of these behaviors enable high-acuity visual processing by bringing relevant visual input to the center of vision, eye movements are unique in that they shift the retinotopic coordinates while preserving the external environment. This is one of the core characteristics that makes eye movements the dominant and most natural mechanism of visual orienting, and therefore it cannot be fully controlled through an experimental design.

It should be noted, however, that while this difference between eye movements and key presses is inherent to their functionality, it also complicates the interpretation of the effects that we observed here. Specifically, the lack of world-based stability in the sequential condition may have increased the cost of key press access by imposing additional demands on spatial updating, reorienting, or maintaining a stable world-centered representation of the display. The stable world-based layout in the simultaneous condition may have facilitated the planning of switches and preserved continuity throughout the exploration process. In contrast, in the sequential condition, the visual scene changed with every key press, requiring observers to repeatedly reorient to the newly presented card and update the relation between the two sets, thereby disrupting the continuity of exploration. This instability of the sequential condition could have encouraged participants to rely more on gathering within a set than on switching to the other set.

Because the defining property of eye movements is precisely that they alter retinal coordinates while preserving world-based stability, these factors cannot be fully dissociated in natural viewing. It therefore remains an open question whether the observed superiority of eye movements as an orienting behavior for accessing information reflects their lower cost as a natural exploration mechanism, their support for maintaining a stable visual scene, or a combination of both. Nevertheless, future studies could manipulate world-based stability by disrupting the continuity of visual input in the simultaneous condition. For example, gaze-contingent paradigms could introduce visual instability into the eye-movement condition by rendering the second card visible only when fixated, thereby mimicking the instability of the key press condition. Although such a manipulation would compromise the naturalness of eye movements, it could help clarify the contribution of visual stability to VWM utilization.

Function of gathering saccades

Another important question concerns how the difference in visual stability between conditions may have affected exploration patterns. Specifically, it raises the question of how visual stability may have modified the functional role of the small within-set saccades that we refer to as “gathering saccades”. Although we interpret these small saccades primarily as an index of reliance on VWM storage, it is possible that not all of them reflect the gathering of information into memory. Some may instead reflect processes such as visual search, exploration, or attentional orienting that do not necessarily result in the encoding of information into VWM. If the proportion of such non-VWM-related saccades differed systematically between conditions, this could complicate the interpretation of the observed effects.

One possibility is that the visual transient produced by each key press in the sequential condition altered scanning patterns relative to the simultaneous condition. However, we believe that this factor alone is unlikely to account for the observed increase in gathering saccades. First, previous studies have shown that scene onset transiently modifies scanning behavior, initially promoting an “ambient” mode of exploration that later shifts toward more focused processing.48,49 Importantly, the early ambient stage is characterized primarily by large exploratory saccades rather than by small saccades. Since the gathering saccades in our study were, by definition, small and performed within a single set, they are unlikely to reflect ambient orienting alone.

Second, the visual transient generated by each key press in the sequential condition may be comparable, at least in part, to the transient produced when a saccade lands on a new region of the display in the simultaneous condition. Previous studies suggest that visual transients associated with saccades are processed by the visual system in ways that resemble responses to externally generated visual events,50,51 and that subsequent eye-movement behavior following these transients can show comparable dynamics.52 Therefore, although onset-related orienting processes may have contributed to within-card eye movements in the sequential condition, they are unlikely to fully explain the substantial differences observed between conditions.

The findings of this study position eye movements as an efficient, low-cost form of active exploration that directly supports cognitive processing. By showing that eye movements determine the trade-off between internal storage and external sampling, the present findings highlight the unique role of eye movements as the most natural orienting behavior in humans. The findings challenge the common assumption that cognitive processes operate independently of the oculomotor system and expose a limitation of the widespread practice of studying cognition under fixed fixation. The results demonstrate that basic cognitive functions such as VWM are action-dependent and underscore the importance of investigating cognition under naturalistic, free-viewing conditions that more closely approximate real-world behavior.

Limitations of the study

The present study has several limitations that also point to important directions for future research. First, our comparison was restricted to two means of accessing visual information, eye movements and key presses. While these behaviors differ in their naturalness and produced robust differences in the reliance on external sampling versus internal storage, future studies should examine a broader range of orienting behaviors, such as mouse movements and head movements, to determine whether the present findings generalize across different forms of information access. Second, although we interpret the lower cost of eye movements as reflecting their status as a natural orienting behavior, naturalness is likely only one of several factors contributing to access costs. Future work should aim to dissociate the contributions of naturalness, motor demands, computational complexity, temporal delays, and visual scene stability using experimental manipulations that independently vary these factors. Finally, the present experiments were conducted in controlled laboratory settings. Extending this approach to more naturalistic environments will help establish how the means of accessing information shape the balance between external sampling and VWM in real-world behavior.

Experimental model and study participant details

Twenty participants (16 females; age range 20–34, M = 24.67, SD = 3.29, all Caucasians) took part in Experiment 1. Another twenty participants (13 females; age range 19–25, M = 22.2, SD = 1.63, all Caucasians) took part in Experiment 2. The sample size of Experiment 1 was determined based on a power analysis, using G∗Power 3.1.53 We examined the main effect of display condition on the gathering variable as a dependent-samples two-tailed t test, with α = 0.05, targeting 80% power to detect a moderate main effect of 0.7. This led to a minimal requirement of 19 participants, which we expanded to 20. The sample size for Experiment 2 was determined to follow that of Experiment 1. All participants reported normal or corrected-to-normal vision and no history of neurological disorders. Method details

Experiment 1

Stimuli

The stimulus set consisted of 184 colored drawings of familiar visual objects (size 2.6°–2.7°), chosen from Snodgrass and Vanderwart’s object pictorial set54 and Multipic database.55 In each trial, 3–7 such items were presented on large white circles referred to as “cards” (diameter 9°). Items were spatially distributed across the card surface. The background was always mid-gray.

Procedure

Participants were seated in a sound-proof chamber, with their head resting on a chinrest at a distance of 100 cm from the display monitor (24″ LCD ASUS VG248QE, 1,920 × 1,080 pixels resolution, 120 Hz refresh rate, mid-gray luminance 110 cd/m2). The experiment was generated and controlled using PsychoPy software in Python.56 The procedure and design parameters were examined and validated in a preliminary study.

Each trial began with a central black fixation cross (0.35 ° × 0.35 °) presented for 1.5 s, which participants were asked to fixate. This was followed either by the presentation of two cards placed symmetrically to the left and right of center at a distance of 7.72° from each other (simultaneous condition) or by the presentation of one central card, which could be switched to another centrally presented card by pressing either the right or the left key (sequential condition). The distance between the cards in the simultaneous condition was determined in a preliminary study and set to the largest possible separation permitted by the monitor’s width, in order to minimize peripheral information from the non-foveated card.

In the sequential condition, each key press was followed by a 50 ms blank screen, after which the second card was presented. Pressing the key again resulted in another 50 ms blank screen, followed by the first card, and so on. This brief blank was included to equate the sequential condition with the simultaneous condition, in which switching between cards required a saccade of approximately 50 ms on average. Since saccades are accompanied by a transient reduction in visual sensitivity, the blank screen was intended to mimic this perceptual interruption. The 50 ms duration was determined based on a preliminary study as the average duration of switching saccades in the simultaneous condition and was later confirmed in the current experiment (mean switching saccade duration = 51.06 ms). Equating the conditions in this way was important because the delay between an access action and the resulting visual input can influence its perceived cost and the storage-access trade-off.18

Although the 50 ms blank interval was intended to approximate the perceptual interruption associated with saccades, it was not intended to equate the full motor planning and execution processes of eye movements and key presses. We acknowledge that the latency associated with preparing and initiating a key press may differ from that associated with planning a saccade. However, because the precise timing of motor preparation could not be directly measured in the present design, our manipulation focused specifically on matching the period during which visual information was transiently unavailable. Importantly, if key presses indeed involve longer preparation or execution latencies than eye movements, this would constitute an additional access cost in the sequential condition and would therefore be consistent with our broader interpretation that accessing information through key presses is more costly than accessing it through eye movements.

In both conditions, the pair of cards (presented either simultaneously or sequentially) contained the same set size (3, 5, or 7) and exactly one common item (the target). Participants could move their eyes freely to search for the common item and were instructed to press the spacebar as soon as they identified it. After pressing the spacebar, the two cards disappeared and a screen displaying randomly selected items from that trial (including the target) appeared, with each item numbered. Participants were instructed to press the corresponding number key to indicate the target. The two conditions were presented in separate blocks, with order counterbalanced between participants. The main experimental session consisted of 450 trials, 10 blocks of 45 trials each - 5 blocks for the simultaneous condition and 5 blocks for the sequential condition. The trial procedure of both conditions is illustrated in Figure 1.

Eye tracking

Binocular gaze position was monitored using a remote infrared video-oculographic system (Eyelink 1000 Plus; SR Research, Canada), with a spatial resolution <0.01° and average accuracy of 0.25°–0.5° using a chinrest (manufacturer specifications). Raw gaze positions were sampled at 1000 Hz and converted into degrees of visual angle using a 9-point-grid calibration, performed at the start of the session and repeated between blocks when necessary.

Task performance strategies

Unlike previous model-copying paradigms, the Dobble task does not include a clear distinction between model and workspace. Instead, the two cards are symmetrical and must be compared with one another. This symmetry allows for multiple possible scanning strategies. Participants may scan a full set of items on one card and then switch to the other card to compare sets (“set-to-set” strategy, Figure 4A). Alternatively, they may select a single item from one card and compare it sequentially to all items on the other card (“one-to-set” strategy, Figure 4B). A third possibility is to select one item from one card and compare it with only one item on the other card before switching again (“one-to-one” strategy, Figure 4C), repeating this process until the match is found. Participants may also adopt no consistent strategy, or switch between strategies across card-switches, trials, or conditions.

To differentiate between these strategies, we examine the gaze patterns they predict. Specifically, we analyze the number of gathering saccades, defined as saccades occurring within a single card, during a switch-episode, defined as the interval between two card switches. This measure reflects how many items are scanned on one card before switching to the other. Figures 4D-4F present the relationship between the number of gathering saccades in episode N and the average number of gathering saccades in the subsequent episode (N+1). A detailed explanation of these predictions is provided in Supplementary Material S1.

To provide qualitative support for one strategy over the others, we conducted two complementary analyses. First, we compared the distribution of switch-episodes across different gathering rates (0, 1, 2, 3, 4, 5, 6, or 7 gathering saccades). Observing a greater number of episodes with low gathering rates than with high gathering rates would support the “one-to-one” strategy over the “set-to-set” strategy, which predicts predominantly high gathering rates, and over the “one-to-set” strategy, which predicts an approximately equal number of low- and high-gathering episodes. Second, we examined the dependency between consecutive switch-episodes by computing the Pearson correlation between the number of gathering saccades in episode N and the number in episode N+1. This analysis was conducted within participants, across all switch-episodes within each condition. The “one-to-set” strategy predicts a negative correlation, reflecting an alternating pattern between low- and high-gathering episodes, whereas the “one-to-one” and the “set-to-set” strategies predict little to no dependency between consecutive episodes. Together, these analyses allow us to evaluate which strategy best accounts for the observed gaze patterns.

Experiment 2

Stimuli

The stimuli were the same as in Experiment 1, except that the cards and the items were smaller (card diameter 4.58°; items size: 0.98°) and the items were in grayscale.

Procedure

After Experiment 1, we replaced the display monitor with a 24.5″ BENQ XL2566K (1920 × 1080 pixels resolution, 360 Hz refresh rate). This experiment was almost identical to Experiment 1, except that the two cards were presented simultaneously even in the “sequential” condition. In the sequential condition, one card was presented at the center of the screen, and the other was placed 7.72° to the right or left of fixation. Participants were asked to look only at the central card and received a real-time warning if they looked away. Upon a key press, the two cards changed positions in the following way: the peripheral card shifted to the center and the central card shifted to the opposite peripheral side to preserve the distance of 7.72° as shown in Figure 7. This design change was intended to mimic the retinal image shift caused by a saccade in the simultaneous condition, thereby controlling for the effects of peripheral vision in the two conditions.

As in Experiment 1, to match the perceptual gap in the sequential condition to the saccade duration in the simultaneous condition, each key press in the sequential condition was followed by a 50 ms blank screen before the second card appeared. In the present study, the mean saccade duration in the simultaneous condition was 47.8 ms, again closely corresponding to the 50-ms blank interval.

Behavioral analysis

Mean reaction times (RTs) and accuracy rates were calculated separately for each participant and condition. RT was defined as the time from card presentation to spacebar press. In Experiment 1, RTs were calculated only for correct trials. In Experiment 2, due to an error in the experimental code, response correctness was not saved for 12 out of 20 participants; therefore, RTs were analyzed for all trials. Accuracy rates represented the proportion of correct target identifications in each condition.

Eye movement analysis

Gaze positions were segmented from trial onset and until the spacebar press. Eye tracking samples with lost pupil tracking, due to blinks or faulty recording, were interpolated. Saccades were detected using a published algorithm.57 Only saccades exceeding 1° of visual angle were included, in order to exclude microsaccades within single items and focus on gaze shifts between items. Eye tracking analysis focused on two main measurements: the switching rate index, reflecting how often participants switched between the two cards, and the gathering index, approximating the number of items from one card gathered into working memory before switching to the next card, thus reflecting the reliance on internal storage. The two conditions differed in the means by which switching was done: while in the sequential condition each key press counted as a switch, in the simultaneous condition, a switch was defined as a large saccade crossing the vertical midline of the screen. In both cases, switching rate was calculated per trial as the number of switches divided by trial duration in seconds. The gathering index was calculated similarly for the two conditions and defined as the average number of saccades performed within one card (i.e., did not cross the vertical midline) between two switches, including saccades from trial onset until the first switch. Both indices were calculated per trial and then averaged per participant and condition. Example eye movement patterns are shown in Figure 2.

Quantification and statistical analysis

Eye movement and behavioral analyses were processed in MATLAB R2022b (The MathWorks Inc., Natick, MA, USA). Statistical analyses were conducted in JASP 0.19.1. Behavioral and eye movement measurements were analyzed separately using repeated-measures 3 × 2 ANOVAs, with set size (3, 5, or 7) and display condition (simultaneous or sequential) as within-subject factors. Benjamini–Hochberg false discovery rate correction was applied (q = 0.05) to the main effects and interactions, and corrected pFDR values are reported together with uncorrected p-values.

Task strategy analyses included one-way ANOVAs with planned contrasts, Pearson correlation analyses, Fisher z-transformation of correlation coefficients, one-sample t-tests, and two-way repeated-measures ANOVAs on Fisher z-transformed values. Complete statistical results for all figures and tables are provided in Tables 1 and 2.

Statistical significance was defined as p < 0.05. Effect sizes are reported throughout the manuscript. Exact sample sizes, statistical tests, p-values, and effect sizes are reported in the Results section and figure/table legends. Assumptions of repeated-measures ANOVA were evaluated where applicable.
