Sex difference in the neural correlates of volitional eyes closing
Authors: Yi Zhang, Yuhao Ruan, Panpan Han, Guiyang Lv, Junhan Wang, Jun Gu, Longhui Li, Zemin Xu, Minfang Huang, Jianing Dai, Shuchan Wang, Xiaohong Wu, Xinyu Li
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractVolitional eyes closing (EC), which shifts brain’s information processing modes from the “exteroceptive” to “interoceptive” state, can promote behaviors (such as episodic memory) that exhibit significant sex difference. Although this act is known to reconfigure a large-scale brain network as its neural correlates, sex difference in such network reconfiguration remains unexplored. Here, we compared between genders the EC-effects on functional network, focusing on the intersubject variability in functional connectivity (IVFC), modularity index, local efficiency, and clustering coefficient. While reliable EC-effects were observed in both genders, females demonstrated greater alterations in IVFC and modularity index than males. Moreover, the female-biased effects of EC were supported by a large-scale cortical basis, primarily affecting the default-mode and visual networks. Finally, a positive relationship was discovered between the sex difference of EC-effects and that of brain anatomy. Jointly, our findings suggested that biological sex serves as a potential factor influencing EC’s neural correlates, with widespread impacts on cognitive systems and close link to brain anatomy.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-025-25442-1.Keywords: Sex difference, Volitional eyes closing, Functional brain network, Default-mode and visual networksSubject terms: Psychology, Human behaviour

Introduction

Volitional eyes closing (EC) is a behavior rarely observed in animals but commonly seen in humans1. It is believed to shift brain’s information processing from an “exteroceptive” to an “interoceptive” mode2, thereby enhancing abilities such as memory3, imagination4, and creativity5 to address complex cognitive tasks6. Recently, great efforts have been made to investigate the neural correlates of volitional EC. Findings indicated that EC induces large-scale brain network reconfiguration, characterized by altered topological properties. Specifically, studies have reported reductions in segregation metrics—such as modularity6, local efficiency, and clustering coefficient2—suggesting a shift toward a more integrated network organization. Notably, the integrated network basis of EC exhibits substantial inter-individual variability6. This variability indicates that the neural correlate of EC may be readily impacted by specific physiological or cognitive traits7,8, leading to potential subtypes across distinct populations9. However, previous studies have primarily focused on identifying the typical neural patterns associated with EC, with insufficient attention to the external factors that may modulate these patterns.

This study explored biological sex as a potential factor influencing EC’s neural correlates. Prior studies have typically treated the influence of biological sex as a covariate when analyzing the neural correlates of EC10. However, direct comparisons between males and females in this context remain unexplored. Indeed, biological sex robustly modifies the brain organization in structure11 and function12, which contributes to sex differences across various cognitive-behavioral domains13,14. Importantly, certain cognitive functions affected by biological sex also undergo significant modulation during the EC condition. For example, the females exhibit a pronounced advantage in episodic memory15, a cognitive capacity that could be enhanced during EC; the males demonstrate superior performance in visual-motor processing tasks16,17, which typically require the practitioners to close their eyes to achieve optimal performance18,19. Furthermore, the brain areas that support those sex-biased behaviors are frequently implicated in the neural processes underlying EC. For example, the default-mode network (DMN), which supports the female-biased advantage in episodic memory20, plays a pivotal role in enhancing modular integration during EC6; the visual cortices, associated with the male-biased proficiency in visual-motor processing21, has been repeatedly identified as a key contributor to the neural adaptations observed during EC22,23. These findings highlight the necessity to investigate sex differences in the neural correlates of EC, and their potential links to the sex-biased brain anatomy.

To address these issues, we first examined the EC-effects on functional brain network separately for males and females, with a focus on inter-individual variability24 and graph-theoretical metrics2,6 previously identified as significant—specifically, the modularity index, local efficiency, and clustering coefficient. Next, we investigated sex differences in these EC-effects and identified the functional connections that are “critical” for these sex-specific biases. Finally, we investigated the relationship between the sex-biased effects of EC and sex-related differences in brain anatomy.

Materials and methods

Participants

This study utilized openly available datasets on the EC/EO conditions (https://www.nitrc.org/projects/eceo_rsfmri_9). As outlined in our previous research24, the original dataset included nine groups of subjects25,26. However, only five groups (groups 1, 4, 6, 7, and 8) were selected for analysis in the current study. The remaining four groups were excluded for the following reasons: group 2, 3 and 5 were collected from the same cohort as group 1 (over different visit), and 4 (with different TR), respectively; group 9 were scanned with different parameters from the others. Additionally, 14 subjects were excluded due to poor image quality, resulting in a final sample of 134 subjects for analysis. The subjects were categorized into female (N = 67) and male (N = 67) groups for subsequent analysis. The two groups were age-matched (p > 0.05), thereby minimizing potential confounding effects related to age.

A detailed description of the EC/EO dataset is provided in Yue et al.25 and Zhang et al.26. Briefly, participants underwent two consecutive scanning sessions under EC and EO conditions, with the session order counterbalanced to ensure balanced distribution. Each session lasted eight minutes, during which participants were instructed to keep their eyes naturally closed or open without focusing on any specific thoughts. After each session, the experimenter briefly conversed with the participant to confirm they had remained awake throughout.

Image acquisition and preprocessing

The scanning parameters for the EC/EO dataset are detailed Yue et al.25 and Zhang et al.26. Briefly, imaging data were acquired using a 3 T MRI clinical scanner equipped with an eight-channel head coil. Resting-state scans were obtained using a standard gradient-echo echo planar imaging sequence, lasting eight minutes and comprising 240 volumes. Additionally, a high-resolution anatomical scan was collected for each participant using a 3D T1-weighted magnetization-prepared rapid gradient echo (MPRAGE) sequence. Data preprocessing was conducted using FreeSurfer (v7.3.2; https://surfer.nmr.mgh.harvard.edu), ANTs (v2.3.5; http://stnava.github.io/ANTs/), and AFNI (v23.2.08; https://afni.nimh.nih.gov), with the pipeline openly accessible at https://github.com/lufee10/myPrep.

Details for the processing were described in Zhang et al.24. Briefly, FreeSurfer’s recon-all was applied to the anatomical volume of each participant to estimate tissue maps, from which lateral ventricle and white matter masks were generated and converted into NIFTI format using @SUMA_MakeSpec_FS27. Commands from ANTs were executed on the anatomical volumes to perform intensity non-uniformity correction (N4BiasFieldCorrection), image denoising (DenoiseImage), skull stripping (antsBrainExtraction.sh), and non-linear registration to the MNI template (antsRegistration).

Specifically, the Schaefer’s parcellation30 was transformed (mri_surf2surf) into the individual space of each subject, according to which the GMV was computed (mris_anatomical_stats) for each ROI. To validate the findings, this study also identified the functional systems with considerable ROIs deemed “essential” for the sex-biased EC-effects. Specifically, the proportion of “critical FCs” associated with each ROI was first calculated. Then brain regions with the top N% regional proportion were designated as the “essential ROIs”, where the N% range from 2.5% to 10% with interval of 0.25% (i.e., top 10 to 40 ROIs with interval of 1). Finally, for each threshold of N%, the ratio of “essential ROIs” was computed for each functional module and compared to its ratio of all ROIs within the entire network. We expected that functional systems with considerable “essential ROIs” (i.e., ratio of “essential ROIs” > ratio of all ROIs) would align with those identified in earlier analyses.

Relation to the sex difference of brain anatomy

This study evaluated the sex-biased GMV for each ROI, and analyzed its spatial correlation to the sex difference of EC-effects. Specifically, the sex-biased GMV of an ROI was calculated by subtracting the average GMV (See Section “Image acquisition and preprocessing”) of males from that of females (i.e., female > male, referred to as female-biased GMV). The sex difference of EC-effects was measured by subtracting the regional EC-effects (See Section ““Critical FCs” for the sex-biased EC-effects”) of males from that of females (i.e., female-biased EC-effects). The spatial correlation between the female-biased GMV and EC-effects was quantified with Pearson’s correlation coefficient. Such correlation assessed the similarity for the spatial patterns between the two metrics. To obtain a comprehensive result, this correlation was separately computed across “essential ROIs” and other ROIs.

Results

Both females and males showed reliable EC-effects on functional network

First, we sought to examine the EC-effects on functional brain network, separately for males and females. Previous studies have indicated that the enhanced individual difference2 and functional integration6 constitutes two primary impacts of EC on functional network. Therefore, this study evaluated the two factors as proxies for the EC-effects on functional brain network. As a preliminary step, we replicated the EC-effects (Figs. 1A, S1A) using the entire samples, which supported the validity of our dataset.

Furthermore, significant EC effects were observed in both females (Figs. 1B, S1B) and males (Figs. 1C, S1C). Specifically, paired t-tests revealed that the EC condition significantly enhanced IVFC (t (66) = 6.349, p < 0.001 for females; t (66) = 2.624, p = 0.011 for males). Additionally, the EC condition significantly reduced the modularity index (6% graph density: t (66) = − 5.900, p < 0.001 for females; t (66) = − 2.275, p = 0.026 for males), local efficiency (6% graph density: t (66) = − 3.787, p < 0.001 for females t (66) = − 2.235, p = 0.028 for males), and clustering coefficient (6% graph density: t (66) = − 2.563, p = 0.012 for females; t (66) = − 2.756, p = 0.008 for males) across distinct graph densities. These findings underscored the widespread EC-effects on functional network across genders.

Females demonstrated enhanced EC-effects in IVFC and network modularity

Second, we aimed to investigate the sex difference of EC-effects on functional network. Independent t-tests indicated that females exhibited greater EC-effects than males, in the IVFC (Fig. 2A) (t (132) = 2.390, p = 0.018) and modularity index (Figs. 2B, S2A) (6% graph density: t (132) = 2.331, p = 0.021). In contrast, no sex difference of EC-effects was observed in the local efficiency (Figs. 2C, S2B) (6% graph density: t (132) = 1.236, p = 0.219) and clustering coefficient (Figs. 2D, S2C) (6% graph density: t (132) = − 0.085, p = 0.932).

The EC-effects on functional network exhibited sex difference. Females exhibited greater EC-effects than males, in the IVFC (A) and modularity index (B). However, no significant sex difference was observed in the local efficiency (C) and clustering coefficient (D). Error bars indicate standard error of the mean. ∗p < 0.05.

These results were further replicated across multiple parcellation resolutions, with ROI numbers ranging from 100 to 1,000 (Figs. S3, S4). To further validate the findings, we conducted a 2 (sex: male vs. female) × 2 (condition: EC vs. EO) mixed ANOVA on both IVFC and modularity index (400ROIs and 6% graph density). Significant sex-by-condition interactions were observed in both metrics (IVFC: F (1,132) = 7.784, p = 0.006, partial η2 = 0.056; modularity index: F (1,132) = 6.828, p = 0.011, partial η2 = 0.049), confirming sex-dependent EC effects. Together, these findings provided convincing evidence for the sex difference of EC-effects in the individual difference and modular segregation of brain network.

Next, we attempted to identify the FCs that were “critical” for the sex difference of EC-effects on functional network. Connectivity-wise tests on the “loading” for sex difference identified a total of 2753 FCs (3.45% FCs within the whole brain network) as “critical FCs”. These FCs linked 98.75% nodes of the whole network (Fig. 3A), suggesting a large-scale cortical basis for the sex difference. Notably, removing these “critical FCs” from networks precluded the sex difference of EC-enhanced IVFC (t (132) = 1.539, p = 0.126, Fig. 3B left panel) but not affected the significant EC-effects in either females (t (66) = 5.196, p < 0.001, Fig. 3B middle panel) or males (t (66) = 3.294, p = 0.002, Fig. 3B right panel). Consistently, removing the “critical FCs” eliminated the sex difference of EC-reduced modularity (t (66) = 1.111, p = 0.268, Fig. 3C left panel, Fig. S5A), without changing the significant EC-effects in either females (6% graph density: t (66) = − 2.735, p = 0.008, Fig. 3C middle panel, Fig. S5B) or males (6% graph density: t (66) = − 2.538, p = 0.013, Fig. 3C right panel, Fig. S5C). To ensure the robustness of our findings, we performed a permutation-based analysis on the sex-modulated effect of EC. Specifically, we compared the observed sex differences after removing the 2,753 “critical FCs” with a null distribution generated by removing 1,000 randomly selected sets of functional connections of equal size. As anticipated, the observed sex-modulated effects on both IVFC and the modularity index were greater than all values obtained from the random permutations (both p < 0.001), suggesting that the specific removal of the critical FCs led to a significantly greater reduction in sex differences than expected by chance. These observations demonstrated that, despite their small proportion, the “critical FCs” were essential for the sex difference of EC-effect on function network.

Sex difference of EC-effects is closely linked to that of brain anatomy

Finally, we intended to explore the relationship between sex difference of EC-effects and that of brain anatomy. To achieve so, we first evaluated the sex-biased GMV for each brain region. As shown in Fig. 5A, the GMV was female-biased in the prefrontal and superior parietal cortices and male-biased in the ventral occipitotemporal regions, which aligned with observations reported in previous studies31.

Then we computed the spatial correlation between the female-biased brain anatomy and EC-effects. Analysis of the “essential” ROIs revealed significant correlations (p < 0.05) in 70% of cases and marginally significant correlations (p < 0.08) in 30% across various thresholds (Fig. 5B left panel), indicating a consistent spatial relationship between female-biased brain anatomy and EC-effects. In contrast, no significant correlations were observed in other ROIs at any threshold (Fig. 5B right panel), which underscored the specificity of the observed spatial consistency. To rule out the possibility that the significant correlation arises from a particular gender, we separately correlated the GMV of female/male to the sex difference of EC-effects. As expected, no significance was identified for either gender (Fig. 5C). Thus, our findings suggested that sex-biased brain anatomy may represent a potential underlying factor contributing to sex differences of EC-effects.

Discussion

This study explored the sex difference of EC-effects on functional brain network. Our main findings are as follows: (1) both females and males would exhibit reliable EC-effects on brain network, characterized by altered individual variability and functional segregation; (2) the magnitude of EC-effects was not uniformly comparable between genders, where females exhibited greater effect in IVFC and modularity index compared to males; (3) the female-biased effects of EC was underpinned by a large-scale cortical basis, with predominant impacts on the default-mode and visual networks; (4) a close connection existed between the sex difference of EC-effects and that of brain anatomy. Jointly, our findings suggested that biological sex serve as a potential factor influencing EC’s neural correlates, with widespread impact on cognitive systems and close link to brain anatomy.

Sex difference in the EC-effects on functional network

Volitional EC has been demonstrated to elicit large-scale reconfiguration of functional network2,6. Recent studies further proposed that the neural adaptation during EC represents a phenomenon widely observed across individuals, rather than merely reflecting a group-level central tendency24. The present research corroborates this proposal by showing that both females and males would exhibit reliable EC-effects on functional network (Fig. 1B,C).

However, the magnitude of EC-effects was not uniformly comparable between genders. On the one hand, females exhibited greater EC-effects in IVFC (Fig. 2A) and modularity index (Fig. 2B) compared to males. These findings remained robust in a wide range of thresholds for obtaining networks, with graph density varying from 1 to 10% (Figs. S3, S4). While prior studies often treated biological sex as a covariate when analyzing the neural correlates of EC10, this study provides, to the best of our knowledge, the first statistical evidence for sex differences in this context. The results support our hypothesis that the neural correlate of EC could be readily impacted by specific physiological factors. Moreover, the observed sex-biased reconfiguration of brain network implied that EC may offer distinct advantages over EO in exploring neural processes underlying psychological or psychiatric conditions characterized by sex differences. Future research in this area is encouraged to examine the intrinsic brain organization in both EC and EO conditions, with particular emphasis on changes in IVFC and network modularity.

On the other hand, no sex difference of EC-effects was observed in the local efficiency (Fig. 2C) and clustering coefficient (Fig. 2D). The findings highlighted potential discrepancies between EC-enhanced communication efficiency and modular integration. Indeed, recent study have reported that EC-promoted communication efficiency was not significantly correlated with EC-enhanced modular integration6. Although the precise neurobiological mechanism behind this phenomenon remains unclear, it has been suggested that the communication efficiency does not capture information about the intermediate scale of brain organization, which is more comprehensively described by the network modularity38. Given the inconsistent findings on the sex-biased small-world properties of brain network39,40, one could speculate that the EC-relevant neural processes may encode rich information about certain functional modules (such as the default-mode and visual network), which are particularly sensitive to biological sex. Further discussion on this topic will be provided in the subsequent section.

Key players in the sex difference of EC-effects: default-mode and visual network

The identification of “critical” FCs revealed a large-scale cortical basis underlying the sex-biased EC-effects (Fig. 3A), with predominant impacts on the default-mode and visual networks (Fig. 4A). These findings provide additional evidence for the network-based foundations of EC, and underscored the rich information encoded by certain functional modules that are sensitive to biological sex.

Focusing on the high-level cognitive modules, our data indicated that most “critical” FCs (Fig. 4A,B) and “essential ROIs” were associated to the DMN (Fig. 4C). This observation aligned with prior studies showing the DMN’s active involvement in the neural mechanisms underlying EC2,6. Moreover, the findings suggested that EC-induced functional adaptation within DMN imply some difference between genders. Indeed, DMN demonstrated sex difference in IVFC41 and dynamic FCs12. The FC profile of DMN was even recognized as a key contributor to gender classification42. Our study thus extended the sex-biased functional properties of DMN into the context of volitional EC. The DMN is known to involve in multiple forms of advanced cognition43, including the episodic memory20 and creative thinking44 that could be enhanced during EC5. Given the established sex difference in these cognitive processes45, our results also suggested that the EC-improved behaviors may exhibit sex-specific variations. We encourage future studies with relevant behavior tests to confirm the hypothesis.

From the perspective of task-relevant modules, our data showed that VN also significantly involved in the sex-biased effects of EC, given substantial “critical” FCs and “essential ROIs” related to the network. VN serves as the primary functional module responsible for processing and integrating visual information46. This network has been consistently observed to increase its activity because the EC involves extensive visual processing of various sensory experiences47. Findings from our recent study suggested that the enhanced functional variability of VN may support the rich content of interoceptive thoughts during EC24. Accordingly, the female-biased promotion of IVFC may imply greater diversity in the internal thoughts than males during EC. Notably, previous studies on the sex-biased visual processing have highlighted its significant value in elucidating the neural mechanisms underlying mental disorders such as autism spectrum disorder21 and depression48. Thus, we proposed that future studies on these issues could benefit from examining the sex-biased neural correlates of EC identified within the VN.

Moreover, this neural shift from exteroceptive to interoceptive processing during eye closure may be meaningfully linked to individual differences in interoceptive ability. Tools such as the Multidimensional Assessment of Interoceptive Awareness (MAIA-2)49 and the Heartbeat Detection Task50 have been widely used to characterize such variability. Notably, prior studies have reported sex-related differences in interoceptive accuracy and awareness, which may reflect both biological factors and sociocultural influences51. Incorporating these behavioral interoceptive assessments in future neuroimaging research could help disentangle the neural and psychosocial mechanisms underlying the observed sex differences in EC-related functional dynamics.

A close connection between the sex difference of EC-effects and that of brain anatomy

This study conducted an exploratory investigation into the relationship between sex difference of EC-effects and brain anatomy. Specifically, we evaluated the sex-biased GMV for each ROI (Fig. 5A), and examined its spatial correlation to the sex difference of EC-effects. It was discovered that only the “essential” ROIs (Fig. 5B), not the other ROIs, displayed significant correlations between these metrics. Additionally, neither female nor male (Fig. 5C) alone demonstrated significant correlation, ruling out the possibility that the observed spatial similarity arises from a particular gender. Thus, our results suggested that the sex-biased brain anatomy may serve as a potential cause for the sex difference of EC effects. Although these results should be interpreted as indicative rather than conclusive, they align with recent studies emphasizing the pivotal role of brain anatomy in shaping sex-biased cortical functions52. It also highlighted that the anatomical structures may partially account for interindividual differences of EC-effects, an area that remains largely unexplored in prior studies. Indeed, it has been proposed that diverse brain functions emerge from fixed anatomical structures53. The coupling between structure and function in macroscale human brain networks has become a prominent topic in neuroscience54. Thus, we expected future studies could pay more attention to the anatomical foundations of the EC-relevant neural and cognitive processes.

Limitation

Several limitations exist in the current study. First, multiple comparison correction was not applied when examining the relationship between the sex-biased effects of EC and brain anatomy. As a result, the findings should be interpreted as providing an anatomically interpretable overview of how EC-related effects are spatially distributed across the cortex, rather than statistically significant inferences at the individual regional level. We hope that future studies will incorporate appropriate correction procedures to enable rigorous statistical inference across multiple tested regions.

Second, the increased variability observed in the EC condition, particularly within the visual network, may alternatively explained by the reduced standardization of visual input across participants. In contrast to the EO condition, where participants consistently viewed the interior of the scanner, the EC condition may introduce greater heterogeneity in visual experience. Future studies could mitigate this potential confound by adopting more controlled experimental designs, such as implementing standardized light exposure protocols, to better disentangle interoceptive and exteroceptive contributions to brain activity.

Third, the primary analysis combined data acquired from multiple sites with slight variations in scanner models and acquisition protocols. Such heterogeneity may influence signal properties and contribute to inter-individual variability, even when the observed effects appear stable. Future studies with harmonized acquisition settings or statistical harmonization techniques are warranted to further minimize potential site-related confounds.

Conclusion

This study systematically investigated the sex differences of the EC-effects on functional network. While reliable effects of EC were observed in both genders, the magnitude of EC-effects was not uniformly comparable between genders. Specifically, females demonstrated greater alterations in IVFC and modularity index than males. Moreover, the female-biased effect of EC was supported by a large-scale cortical basis, primarily affecting the default-mode and visual networks. Finally, a positive relationship existed between the sex difference of EC-effects and that of GMV. Jointly, our findings suggested that biological sex serve as a potential factor influencing EC’s neural correlates, with widespread impacts on cognitive systems and close link to brain anatomy.
