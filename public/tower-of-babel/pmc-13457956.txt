Rapid resolution of variants of uncertain significance (VUS). A complementary role for zebrafish in the era of multi-million-dollar therapies
Authors: J Giacomotto
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractGenomic screening increasingly reveals variants of uncertain significance, forcing clinicians to make high-stakes decisions as million-dollar drug and gene-replacement therapies emerge. Rapid in vivo zebrafish assays can help to resolve variant pathogenicity within weeks, providing organism-level evidence that informs treatment decisions, prevents unnecessary interventions, and strengthens precision medicine.Subject terms: Diseases, Drug discovery, Genetics, Medical research

The growing challenge of interpreting variants of uncertain significance (VUS)

The expansion of newborn screening and whole-genome sequencing has fundamentally reshaped clinical genetics1–5. Increasingly, novel and rare variants in genes associated with known diseases are identified in individuals who are otherwise healthy and asymptomatic before symptom onset. While this progress has enabled earlier diagnosis and access to disease-modifying therapies, it has also amplified an emerging challenge: the clinical interpretation of variants of uncertain significance (VUS) in situations where decisions cannot be deferred6–8. Importantly, the presence of a rare variant does not necessarily equate to disease, and a substantial proportion of individuals identified through population-level screening may remain asymptomatic throughout life due to incomplete penetrance, variable expressivity, or residual gene function6,9–14. As a result, clinicians are increasingly confronted with situations in which therapeutic decisions must be made despite considerable uncertainty, raising the risk of implementing costly and potentially harmful interventions in individuals who might otherwise have remained healthy (Fig. 1).

Spinal muscular atrophy illustrates the clinical stakes

In two recent studies published in EMBO Molecular Medicine and The American Journal of Human Genetics, we addressed this challenge in the context of spinal muscular atrophy (SMA)15,16. We demonstrated that the zebrafish can serve as a rapid, robust and complementary “VUS-resolution” tool for geneticists and clinicians requiring urgent functional evidence to support time-critical therapeutic decisions. While zebrafish have long been used to investigate gene function through transient mRNA and transgenic complementation assays17,18 ( > 80% of human disease-associated genes have a zebrafish orthologue19,20), and more recently for the study of patient-derived variants21–23, the presented case studies15,16 showcase how the zebrafish and established experimental paradigm can have direct real-world impacts on urgent clinical decision-making and human lives.

In these studies, newborn screening identified SMN1 variants predicted to be pathogenic and associated with a high risk of severe disease. However, no clinical evidence or robust functional assays were available to guide clinicians on whether to initiate therapy immediately or delay treatment until symptom onset -which, in SMA, is unfortunately associated with irreversible neurodegeneration (time equals neurons for patients).

By implementing immediate gene complementation assays using a zebrafish smn1 loss-of-function models, we demonstrated that these variants retained substantial biological activity. Importantly, this approach provided clinically relevant evidence in less than six weeks. Going beyond our initial expectations, by testing the limits of this approach using a panel of known pathogenic and hypomorphic variants (variants associated with different severities of the disease)1, we found that the degree of phenotypic rescue reflected residual biological activity, demonstrating that the assay is robust enough to distinguish hypomorphic from complete loss-of-function alleles. A finding of considerable significance for the field.

These experimental results were subsequently supported by longitudinal clinical follow-up, with affected children remaining asymptomatic well beyond infancy. Together, these findings supported formal VUS reclassification, relieved family anxiety, avoided potential harm from life-long therapies, and saved millions in healthcare costs.

Multi-million-dollar therapies amplify diagnostic uncertainty

Although focused on SMA, these studies illustrate a rising clinical dilemma. The emergence of highly effective drugs and gene replacement therapies across a growing number of genetic diseases is creating a strong need for early, presymptomatic diagnosis and intervention24–28. At the same time, these therapies are associated with substantial biological risks, unknown long-term consequences, and unprecedented costs, often reaching millions of dollars per patient29–33. When variant pathogenicity is uncertain, clinicians are required to make real, irreversible decisions in the absence of definitive functional evidence.

In this context, diagnostic uncertainty is not benign. Initiating therapy based on incomplete or indirect evidence may expose children to unnecessary biological risk, while delaying treatment in truly pathogenic cases may permit irreversible disease progression. Beyond individual patients and families, such uncertainty has broader implications for healthcare systems, particularly as ultra-expensive drug and gene-replacement therapies become available for an expanding range of conditions29–34. Even a small proportion of unnecessary interventions can translate into significant economic burden when multiplied across population-level screening programs.

Rapid in vivo zebrafish assays as a functional bridge

Current variant interpretation frameworks rely primarily on population frequency data, computational predictions, and in vitro or cell-based assays7,35,36. While essential, these approaches often provide limited insight into organism-level function, compensatory mechanisms, or phenotypic thresholds that determine early disease expression. Traditional mammalian models, although highly informative, are rarely compatible with the time constraints imposed by newborn screening and early clinical decision-making.

The studies reported15,16 demonstrate that rapid in vivo zebrafish functional assays can help complement and bridge this gap. By testing exact patient-specific variants in a genetically relevant loss-of-function context, these assays can distinguish pathogenic, hypomorphic, and functionally benign alleles within days rather than months or years. Crucially, this information was generated early enough to inform real clinical decisions, rather than retrospectively validating outcomes after treatment had already been initiated or withheld, and saving millions of dollars in healthcare costs.

Beyond SMA: implications for precision medicine

Beyond SMA, the implications of this approach are broader and extend to genetic diseases more generally. As precision medicine advances, the number of rare and novel variants identified through population-level screening will continue to rise. Without scalable strategies to generate timely functional evidence, the gap between variant detection and confident clinical interpretation will widen. This challenge is particularly acute for disorders in which treatment must be initiated early and where unnecessary intervention may itself constitute harm.

Rapid in vivo assays also intersect with increasing ethical and regulatory pressure to reduce animal use in research. Small vertebrate models such as the zebrafish enable high-content phenotypic analysis while limiting invasive procedures, reducing the number of animals used and shortening experimental timelines, aligning with the principles of replacement, reduction, and refinement37. In contrast, reliance on slower mammalian models may increase animal use and animal welfare burden while delaying clinically actionable insight.

Integrating rapid functional assays into clinical genomics

We do not suggest that zebrafish in vivo functional testing should replace established diagnostic frameworks or be applied indiscriminately. Rather, our findings support its use as a complementary tool in specific clinical scenarios: when variant interpretation is uncertain, therapeutic stakes are high, and decisions must be made within a narrow timeframe. In such settings, the absence of timely functional evidence represents a tangible limitation of current precision medicine pipelines.

Importantly, zebrafish should be proactively considered as part of variant resolution strategies, particularly when a validated and disease-relevant model already exists. In these contexts, rapid in vivo functional testing can provide organism-level insight that is otherwise unattainable within clinically meaningful timelines. When an appropriate genetic and phenotypic framework is available, integrating zebrafish assays into diagnostic workflows may significantly strengthen evidence for or against pathogenicity and help guide therapeutic decision-making with greater confidence.

Together, the two presented studies16,38 provide proof-of-principle that rapid in vivo functional testing using the zebrafish can meaningfully support clinical variant interpretation, disease severity prediction and decision-making. As genomic screening continues to expand and therapeutic options multiply, integrating such approaches into translational workflows may help support diagnostic decisions, prevent unnecessary harm to patients, limit avoidable healthcare expenditure, and ensure that precision medicine delivers benefits proportionate to its promise. The zebrafish has its place in clinical settings.

Limitations

To provide rapid VUS resolution, this approach requires pre-existing, well-characterized zebrafish disease models (or LOF models) displaying a robust and quantifiable phenotype that can first be rescued by wild-type human mRNA. Numerous zebrafish disease models are already available, and clinicians are encouraged to engage with zebrafish experts when variants arise in genes with established models. However, when no appropriate model exists, generating and validating a new disease model may require several months before functional testing can be implemented -nevertheless, although this requirement extends the timeline for VUS resolution, the resulting disease models become valuable long-term resources for mechanistic studies and therapeutic screening/ discovery, greatly extending their utility beyond VUS interpretation-. Likewise, the rapid turnaround described in our SMA studies relies on transient mRNA complementation, which limits functional assessment to early developmental stages because injected mRNA and protein are progressively degraded. Consequently, disorders with late-onset phenotypes or genes that cannot be readily assessed through ubiquitous early expression may require more complex approaches, including stable transgenic expression or CRISPR/Cas9-based genome editing. Despite these limitations, when an appropriate disease model is available, zebrafish provides a unique opportunity to generate organism-level functional evidence within clinically meaningful timeframes and should be considered as a complementary component of VUS interpretation pipelines.
