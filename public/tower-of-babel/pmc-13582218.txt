Level-dependent changes in cervical multifidus composition following anterior cervical discectomy and fusion: bridging quantitative MRI and clinical perspectives
Authors: Tom Folkerts, Ria Tripathi, Thomas Caffard, Bruno Verna, Jiaqi Zhu, Andrew A. Sama, Frank P. Cammisa, Alexander P. Hughes
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
IntroductionFatty infiltration (FI) of the cervical paraspinal muscles is associated with greater disease severity and less favorable outcomes in degenerative cervical spine disease. However, longitudinal data on whether muscle composition changes following anterior cervical discectomy and fusion (ACDF) are scarce. This study aimed to evaluate changes in FI and functional cross-sectional area (fCSA) of the cervical multifidus and rotatores after ACDF and to assess whether these changes differ across surgical levels.Materials and MethodsThis single-center retrospective study analyzed patients who underwent ACDF for degenerative cervical stenosis between 2015 and 2018. Patients were included if both preoperative and postoperative cervical spine MRI (obtained 1–5 years after surgery) were available. The multifidus and rotatores colli were segmented bilaterally on T2-weighted axial images. Measurements were categorized by position within the fusion construct as upper (most cranial fused vertebra), middle (intervening vertebrae), and lower (most caudal fused vertebra) levels. FI and fCSA were quantified using Otsu thresholding. P-values were adjusted for multiple testing using the Benjamini-Hochberg procedure.ResultsThirty-five patients met inclusion criteria (median age 58 years, 40% female, 1–4 fused levels). Bilateral FI decreased significantly at the lower level (52.1 ± 15.4% vs. 45.0 ± 14.8%, adjusted p = 0.039), with no significant changes at the upper (adjusted p = 0.858) or middle level (adjusted p = 0.354). Bilateral fCSA decreased significantly at the middle level (165.2 [119.4–196.3] vs. 109.4 [92.0–171.6] mm², adjusted p = 0.018), with no significant changes at the upper (adjusted p = 0.194) or lower level (adjusted p = 0.382)ConclusionsACDF was associated with level-dependent changes in cervical multifidus composition, including a reduction in FI at the lower surgical level and a loss of fCSA at the middle level. These findings highlight that postoperative muscle remodeling varies by position within the fusion construct, which may have implications for the development of level-specific, targeted postoperative rehabilitation strategies.

Introduction

Cervical spinal stenosis (CSS) is a common cause of neurological dysfunction in adults, arising from progressive narrowing of the cervical spinal canal that compromises neural structures [1–3]. Although congenital predisposition and traumatic injury are recognized contributors, degenerative changes constitute the principal etiology, driven by intervertebral disc degeneration, facet joint hypertrophy, and progressive thickening of the ligamentum flavum [4–8]. Depending on the anatomical location of narrowing, the resulting neural compression may manifest as pain, numbness, and tingling in the affected dermatomes [9].

Anterior cervical discectomy and fusion (ACDF) is a well-established surgical treatment for CSS, involving removal of diseased intervertebral discs, compressive osteophytes, and the posterior longitudinal ligament, with subsequent placement of an interbody cage to restore disc height and achieve segmental fusion [10, 11]. Accounting for over 60% of all cervical spine surgeries performed in the United States, ACDF remains the most commonly utilized cervical spine procedure and is associated with favorable long-term clinical outcomes [12, 13].

Beyond its well-characterized clinical manifestations, CSS increasingly draws attention for its impact on the cervical paraspinal muscles, which are essential for maintaining head position, sagittal alignment, and segmental stability [14–16]. The superficial extensors primarily generate torque for gross cervical motion, whereas the deep extensors provide segmental stiffness and proprioceptive feedback critical for intervertebral control [17–19]. Among the latter, the multifidus and rotatores are of particular interest as the deepest muscular layer with direct attachments to adjacent vertebrae, serving as key local stabilizers of the cervical spine [19–21]. The cervical multifidus receives predominantly segmental innervation through the medial branches of the dorsal rami, with overlap across adjacent levels, an arrangement that may render these muscles particularly susceptible to morphological changes in the presence of cervical pathology [20, 22, 23]. However, previous imaging studies have frequently analyzed the cervical extensor musculature as a composite group, thereby limiting insight into changes occurring specifically within these deep segmental stabilizers [15, 24, 25].

Fatty infiltration (FI) of the cervical paraspinal muscles has emerged as a clinically relevant marker of muscle quality in degenerative cervical spine disease [22, 26]. Greater severity of foraminal and central stenosis is associated with higher FI of the cervical multifidus and rotatores, and elevated FI has in turn been linked to greater neck disability, impaired physical function, and less favorable postoperative outcomes following ACDF [14, 23, 26]. These findings highlight the importance of paraspinal muscle composition as both a correlate of disease severity and a potential determinant of surgical outcome.

While cervical paraspinal FI has been well characterized in degenerative cervical disease and linked to clinical outcomes, little is known about how muscle composition changes following ACDF. Therefore, this study aimed to evaluate longitudinal changes in FI and functional cross-sectional area (fCSA) of the cervical multifidus and rotatores following ACDF and to assess whether these changes are distributed uniformly across the fused cervical segments.

Methods and materials

Patient population

This single-center retrospective study analyzed all patients who underwent ACDF for cervical radiculopathy, myelopathy, or both secondary to degenerative cervical stenosis between 2015 and 2018 at a tertiary academic spine institution.

All procedures were performed by four board-certified surgeons with more than ten years of experience through a standard anterior cervical approach, comprising anterior exposure, discectomy, and placement of an interbody cage. Radiographic fusion was confirmed on postoperative radiographs in accordance with established clinical criteria [27–29].

Patients were included if they were 18 years of age or older and had both preoperative and postoperative cervical spine magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) available, with the postoperative MRI acquired between one and five years after surgery. This interval was selected to allow sufficient time for postoperative muscle remodeling, as several studies have demonstrated that measurable changes in paraspinal muscle composition may require at least 12 months to manifest, while minimizing the potential confounding influence of adjacent segment disease, which has been reported to increase in incidence beyond four to five years after ACDF [30–35].

Patients were excluded if they had undergone prior cervical spine surgery, including revision procedures, if ACDF was performed for traumatic, neoplastic, or infectious pathology, or if documented pseudarthrosis was present prior to postoperative MRI acquisition. Demographic and surgical characteristics, including age, sex, and the number of fused levels, were recorded for each patient.

Muscle measurements

Muscle measurements were performed on T2-weighted axial MRI images. Measurements were obtained at the axial slice corresponding to the midpoint of each vertebral body, with the measurement plane oriented parallel to the inferior endplate to minimize variability resulting from cervical lordosis or kyphosis. Postoperative images were visually inspected for metal artifact prior to segmentation.

The multifidus and rotatores colli muscles were segmented bilaterally at vertebral levels C3 through C7 using ITK-SNAP software (version 3.8.0; www.itksnap.org; Fig. 1). Consistent with established methodology, the multifidus and rotatores colli were segmented as a single composite region, reflecting their close anatomical integration as a continuous deep extensor layer that cannot be reliably delineated on axial MRI [24, 36].

Consistent with prior level-specific fusion analyses, measurements were categorized according to their position within the fusion construct [37, 38]. The upper level was defined as the most cranial vertebra included in the fusion and the lower level as the most caudal vertebra. In multilevel constructs, the vertebra or vertebrae located between the upper and lower levels were designated as middle levels. For example, in a C4–C7 fusion, C4 represented the upper level, C7 the lower level, and C5–C6 the middle levels. Patients who underwent single-level ACDF (n = 6) had only an upper and a lower level and were therefore excluded from the middle-level analysis. When multiple middle levels were present, values were averaged to obtain a single representative value per patient, thereby avoiding overrepresentation of patients with longer fusion constructs.

To differentiate intramuscular fat from muscle tissue within each segmented region, Otsu thresholding was applied to maximize interclass variance between signal intensities [39]. A customized MATLAB script (version R2019a; The MathWorks, Natick, MA, USA) was then used to calculate functional cross-sectional area (fCSA), intramuscular fat area (FAT), and total cross-sectional area (CSA) for each segmented region, with CSA defined as the sum of fCSA and FAT. All segmentations were performed by a single trained rater, and all reported values are based on this rater’s measurements. To assess interrater reliability, a second rater, an orthopedic surgery resident with subspecialty training in spine surgery, independently re-segmented a randomly selected subset of approximately 50% of patients (n = 18). Interrater reliability was evaluated using two-way random-effects, single-measures, absolute-agreement intraclass correlation coefficients (ICC).

Statistical analysis

Preoperative and postoperative FI and fCSA were compared at the upper, middle, and lower levels as defined above. The Shapiro-Wilk test was used to assess normality of continuous variables. Normally distributed data were reported as mean ± standard deviation and compared using paired t-tests, whereas non-normally distributed data were reported as median and interquartile range (IQR) and compared using the Wilcoxon signed-rank test. Because six a priori comparisons were performed (FI and fCSA at the upper, middle, and lower surgical levels), p-values were additionally adjusted for multiple testing using the Benjamini-Hochberg procedure to control the false discovery rate, in accordance with recommendations for multiplicity adjustment in health research [40, 41]. Both unadjusted and adjusted p-values are reported. Statistical significance was defined as a two-sided p-value < 0.05. As an exploratory analysis, the association between the interval from surgery to postoperative MRI and the change in FI and fCSA (postoperative minus preoperative values) at each surgical level was assessed using Spearman rank correlation coefficients. All statistical analyses were performed using R (version 4.4.2; R Foundation for Statistical Computing, Vienna, Austria).

Results

Patient characteristics

A total of 35 patients met the inclusion criteria. The median age was 58 years (IQR 48.9–68.5), and 14 patients (40.0%) were female. The number of fused levels ranged from one to four, with two-level fusions being the most common (n = 15, 42.9%). Six patients underwent single-level ACDF and were therefore excluded from the middle-level analysis, leaving 29 patients for comparison at this level. All 35 patients demonstrated radiographic evidence of fusion on postoperative flexion-extension radiographs; no cases of pseudarthrosis or revision surgery were identified prior to postoperative MRI acquisition. The median interval between surgery and postoperative MRI was 22.4 months (IQR 14.3–36.6). Patient characteristics are summarized in Table 1.

Changes in muscle composition

Interrater reliability for muscle segmentation was assessed in a randomly selected subset of 18 patients (51.4%), including both preoperative and postoperative images. The ICC was 0.88 (95% confidence interval [CI], 0.75–0.94) for fCSA and 0.94 (95% CI, 0.89–0.97) for FI.

Bilateral FI of the multifidus and rotatores decreased significantly at the lower surgical level between the preoperative and postoperative periods (adjusted p = 0.039). No significant changes in FI were observed at the upper (adjusted p = 0.858) or middle (adjusted p = 0.354) surgical levels (Table 2).

Exploratory Spearman correlation analyses demonstrated no significant association between the postoperative follow-up interval and the change in FI at the upper (ρ = 0.034, p = 0.845), middle (ρ = 0.199, p = 0.309), or lower (ρ = 0.151, p = 0.386) surgical level, or the change in fCSA at the upper (ρ = 0.057, p = 0.746), middle (ρ = −0.345, p = 0.067), or lower (ρ = −0.184, p = 0.291) surgical level.

Discussion

To our knowledge, this is the first level-specific longitudinal study on changes in FI and fCSA of the cervical multifidus and rotatores following ACDF, analyzed separately at the upper, middle, and lower surgical levels. The principal findings are: (1) FI decreased significantly at the lower surgical level, while no significant changes were observed at the upper or middle levels; (2) a concurrent significant reduction in fCSA was confined to the middle surgical level, with the upper and lower levels remaining unchanged; (3) these divergent patterns suggest that the effects of ACDF on paraspinal muscle composition are not uniform across the fusion construct but vary according to the biomechanical and neuroanatomical position of each level within the fused segment.

While the association between cervical paraspinal FI and clinical outcomes has received increasing attention in recent years, longitudinal data on postoperative changes in muscle composition following anterior cervical surgery are scarce [42, 43]. A recent systematic review identified only six studies examining the relationship between paraspinal muscle degeneration and functional outcomes after anterior cervical spine surgery, and none of these specifically evaluated longitudinal changes in FI or fCSA at individual surgical levels [43]. To date, existing literature has predominantly focused on preoperative muscle quality as a predictor of postoperative outcomes, rather than how surgery itself alters muscle morphology over time [42, 44]. The present study addresses this gap by contributing level-specific longitudinal data on postoperative changes in cervical multifidus composition following ACDF, thereby broadening the currently limited understanding of muscle remodeling after surgical decompression and fusion and providing a basis for future prospective investigations.

The significant reduction in FI at the lower surgical level is a notable finding. Several mechanisms may contribute to this observation. First, the lower level represents the caudal boundary of the fusion construct, where the transition from fused to mobile segments occurs. Biomechanical studies have demonstrated that motion compensation after cervical fusion is not symmetrically distributed, with greater compensatory motion at the inferior than at the superior adjacent segment [45, 46]. This increased mechanical loading at the caudal transition zone may promote muscle remodeling. Second, neural decompression achieved through ACDF may contribute to changes in muscle composition at this level. Given that the cervical multifidus receives predominantly segmental innervation through the medial branches of the dorsal rami, decompression of the most caudal nerve root may accordingly influence the muscle fibers at this level. Preclinical evidence suggests that nerve compression-related FI may be partially reversible following decompression, with FI resorption mediated through specific cellular mechanisms [47, 48]. However, the present study cannot distinguish between these potential mechanisms, and the observed changes may also reflect natural disease course, postoperative activity modifications, or other unmeasured confounders. Notably, the reduction in FI at the lower level was not accompanied by a corresponding increase in fCSA, suggesting that reversal of FI does not necessarily coincide with muscle hypertrophy within the observed follow-up period.

The significant decrease in fCSA at the middle level, in the absence of a corresponding change in FI, suggests a different process. The middle level is located between two fused boundaries and is therefore subject to the greatest restriction in segmental motion. Experimental and clinical studies have consistently demonstrated that spinal fusion leads to paraspinal muscle atrophy at fused segments, likely as a consequence of reduced mechanical demand and disuse [31, 49, 50]. Hu et al. showed in a rabbit model that fusion resulted in significant reduction in muscle fiber size and decreased electromyographic activity specifically within the fused region, while adjacent segments demonstrated increased muscular activity [49]. Similarly, clinical studies of posterior lumbar fusion have reported marked multifidus volume loss at operated levels [31].

The decrease in fCSA at the middle level in the present study is consistent with these findings and may reflect disuse-related atrophy in a segment that is maximally immobilized within the construct. Notably, FI did not increase significantly at the middle level despite the loss of muscle area, which may suggest that the atrophic process at this level is primarily characterized by a reduction in muscle fiber volume rather than by progressive fatty replacement.

The interpretation of these level-specific findings should, however, be considered in the context of the variable postoperative follow-up interval (1 to 5 years, median 22.4 months), as the time elapsed since surgery may plausibly influence the degree of muscle remodeling observed. Evidence from lumbar fusion studies indicates that postoperative changes in paraspinal muscle composition are time dependent, with multifidus volume loss and increased fat infiltration detectable within the first postoperative year and further progression beyond two years [31, 35, 51]. Whether the patterns observed in the present cohort represent a stable endpoint or an intermediate stage of ongoing remodeling therefore cannot be determined from a single postoperative time point. In exploratory analyses, the follow-up interval was not significantly correlated with the change in FI or fCSA at any surgical level, suggesting that the observed changes do not appear to be primarily attributable to differences in follow-up duration. However, these analyses were not powered to detect modest time-dependent effects, and imaging was obtained on clinical indication rather than at protocol-defined intervals. The temporal trajectory of muscle remodeling after ACDF thus remains incompletely understood and should be investigated prospectively with standardized imaging at predefined intervals.

These findings carry potential clinical implications. The observation that FI may decrease at certain surgical levels following ACDF suggests that pathological muscle changes associated with cervical stenosis are not necessarily irreversible. While the reduction in FI at the lower level suggests a favorable effect of ACDF on muscle quality, the simultaneous loss of muscle area at the middle level highlights the potential need for postoperative cervical strengthening programs to mitigate disuse-related atrophy within the fused segments. Recent evidence suggests that structured, phase-based rehabilitation programs initiated early after ACDF, including cervical muscle strengthening, postural correction, and home-based exercises, can significantly improve pain, disability, and functional outcomes [52–54]. A finite element analysis further demonstrated that postoperative muscle strengthening reduces adjacent segment disc pressure and implant stress, while muscle atrophy increases the risk of adjacent segment degeneration and implant failure [55]. These data collectively support the integration of targeted cervical muscle rehabilitation into postoperative ACDF care, particularly given the level-specific muscle changes observed in the present study.

The clinical relevance of paraspinal muscle composition in the context of ACDF is further supported by studies demonstrating that preoperative FI is associated with less Neck Disability Index (NDI) improvement, greater progression of sagittal vertical axis, and worse patient-reported outcomes following cervical spine surgery [14, 42, 44]. In patients with chronic nonspecific neck pain, FI of the cervical multifidus has been shown to correlate with neck disability and pain severity [56–58]. These associations highlight the potential value of monitoring paraspinal muscle quality both before and after surgery. However, future prospective studies incorporating patient-reported outcome measures such as the NDI are needed to establish whether postoperative muscle remodeling corresponds to measurable clinical improvement.

This study has several important limitations that must be acknowledged. First, the sample size of 35 patients limits statistical power and precludes meaningful subgroup analyses, such as comparisons between single-level and multilevel ACDF or between different fusion levels. Second, FI and fCSA were analyzed as pooled bilateral values, as the sample size was insufficient for adequately powered side-stratified analyses. Potential asymmetric muscle remodeling may therefore have been attenuated. Third, the retrospective design introduces inherent selection bias. Postoperative MRI was not obtained routinely but only when persistent or recurrent symptoms warranted further evaluation. Consequently, the study cohort represents a selected subgroup of ACDF patients who may have had more persistent pathology or symptoms than the general ACDF population, and the results may not be generalizable to all patients undergoing this procedure. Fourth, since the timing of postoperative MRI was determined by clinical indication, the interval between surgery and imaging differed between patients, precluding definitive conclusions regarding the temporal evolution of the observed muscle changes. Fifth, the study did not include patient-reported outcome measures such as the NDI or Visual Analog Scale (VAS), precluding correlation of the observed muscle changes with clinical improvement. Sixth, all patients received early postoperative physical therapy according to institutional standards. However, long-term rehabilitation protocols and adherence were not systematically documented and therefore could not be accounted for in the analysis. Seventh, cervical sagittal alignment parameters and the presence of adjacent segment disease were not assessed, both of which may influence paraspinal muscle morphology. Eighth, the variable number of fused levels (one to four) introduces heterogeneity that may confound level-specific comparisons. Finally, while the study proposes potential mechanisms for the observed changes, including neural decompression and altered biomechanical loading, these remain hypothesis-generating and cannot be confirmed from imaging data alone.

Conclusion

ACDF was associated with level-dependent changes in cervical multifidus composition, including a significant reduction in FI at the lower surgical level and a significant loss of fCSA at the middle level. These findings suggest that FI of the cervical multifidus and rotatores may be partially reversible following surgical decompression and fusion, with potential implications for targeted postoperative rehabilitation. Further prospective studies incorporating patient-reported outcomes are warranted to build on these findings and delineate the relationship between surgical decompression, muscle remodeling, and clinical recovery.
