How do financial considerations influence medical decision-making? A qualitative-empirical study in German cancer medicine
Authors: Julia Felicitas Leni König, Birthe Aufenberg, Gereon Brei, Sophia Reitmayer, Sabine Sommerlatte, Wolfgang Greiner, Jan Schildmann, Eva Caroline Winkler, Katja Mehlis
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractIntroductionThe question of how financial considerations influence medical decision-making is coming to a head in cancer medicine, as cancer medicine is getting more expensive and extensive. As the debate continues, empirical data on specific decision-making situations that are influenced by financial consideration and on the characterisation of financial influence in cancer medicine remain scarce.MethodsWe conducted a qualitative semistructured interview study with physicians who had experience in controlling and budgeting in different settings of cancer care in Germany. Qualitative content analysis was performed to identify specific decision-making situations in cancer medicine influenced by financial considerations and to characterise the financial influence.Resultsn=16 interviews were conducted. We identified 21 medical decision-making situations influenced by financial considerations specific to cancer medicine, which were characterised into four subcategories of financial influence derived from the data: (1) no reimbursement, (2) reimbursement that does not completely cover costs, (3) reimbursement that exceeds the costs and (4) costs covered for treatments with questionable cost-effectiveness. These results imply that medical decisions in cancer medicine could be influenced by financial considerations.ConclusionsThe findings support the hypothesis that medical decision-making in cancer medicine can be influenced by financial considerations. They provide specific examples and a characterisation of the influence as starting points for further quantitative studies on the extent of the financial influence and on the possible effect on high-risk patient groups. Further, they underline the need for ethical analysis regarding the implications for quality of care.Keywords: Qualitative Research, ethics, economics

Introduction

The debate on how financial considerations may influence medical decision-making is ongoing. Critics argue that financial influence on medical decision-making at the physician-patient level changes the core of medicine and increases existing disparities.1 In contrast, proponents emphasise the need for financial consideration due to rising healthcare costs, limited resources and the responsibility for efficiency.2 Extended global life expectancy is exacerbating the discussion.3 The heterogeneously used terminology further complicates the debate: financial influence is often referred to inter alia as ‘economisation’ or ‘commercialisation’ without clearly defining the meaning and implication of these terms.4

To counter the adverse unintended consequences of financial considerations on medical decision-making in the German healthcare system, the German Ethics Council issued a position statement in 2016. In response to what they described as the growing economic influence, they declared patient welfare as an ethical standard for hospitals.5 Similarly, the Association of Scientific Medical Societies published a strategy paper titled ‘Medicine and Economy: Measures for a Science Based, Patient-Centred and Resource Conscious Care’ in 2019.6 Given the constraints of limited resources, the German Medical Association underscores the need to avoid wastefulness in its theses on the economisation of the medical profession.7

These issues are coming to a head in cancer medicine, as it requires an increasing proportion of resources. The efficient and effective use of resources in cancer medicine is especially relevant as patients are getting older, cancer incidence increases with age and cancer treatment is becoming more expansive and expensive.8 In other words, cancer is becoming a chronic disease, and its treatment requires greater resources. These developments place economic and financial considerations at the centre of questions of allocation—especially given the finite nature of these resources.

Still, the concept of financial influence on medical decision-making remains vague and empirical evidence is scarce.9 The politically and normatively important publications provide only a broad description. A gap exists on a more detailed description and analysis of specific challenges regarding specific decision-making situations.9 This gap is particularly pertinent in Germany due to the mix of inpatient diagnosis-related group (DRG)-funded care and out-patient fee-for-service/private practice arrangements, which create unique incentive structures.

Closing the existing gap is important because the concerns that financial considerations may have a negative influence on medical decision-making and affect quality of care persist.9 Given these concerns, there is a need to specify financial influence on medical decisions in cancer medicine.10 11

Therefore, it is important to understand which decision-making situations may be influenced by financial considerations and how that influence can be described. To identify and describe the financial influence on medical decision-making in cancer medicine, we conducted a qualitative-empirical study addressing the following research questions:

Which specific medical decision-making situations in cancer medicine are influenced by financial considerations?

Methods and materials

Context of the study

This study is part of the project ‘Mapping and dealing with economic influencing factors in the treatment of cancer patients (ELABORATE) - A joint project of medical ethics, oncology and health economics’ funded by the German Cancer Aid (Deutsche Krebshilfe).

The project defined economic influencing factors as financial factors influencing diagnostic and therapeutic measures. A qualitative design was chosen. A qualitative study design allows for the collection of comprehensive data while remaining flexible and participant-driven. It allows for deeper explanation and exploration than quantitative approaches, making it particularly suitable for understanding the complex and often vague concept of economic and financial influence on medical decision-making.9 12 13

The results of this qualitative-empirical study inform the subsequent quantitative and normative analysis of the ELABORATE project.

Recruitment and participants

Semistructured interviews were conducted. To answer the research questions, knowledge of cancer medicine, the respective medical decisions involved in cancer medicine and expertise in medical budgeting and controlling were required. Therefore, criteria for participation were (1) being a physician who had experience in budgeting and controlling and (2) working in a medical specialty of cancer medicine. Participants were recruited using purposive sampling with maximum variation, convenience and snowball sampling. Those sampling strategies were chosen to recruit in all specificities of the German healthcare system (outpatient and inpatient sector, different federal states and academic and non-academic setting) with different financing and reimbursement systems.14 We compiled a list of all cancer centres (‘Onkologische Zentren’) certified by the German Cancer Society (‘Deutsche Krebsgesellschaft’) in Microsoft Excel. We applied the random number selection function to sample cancer centres, stratifying by financial sponsor and location across different federal states.

The coordination teams of the sampled cancer centres were contacted via email and asked to assist in contacting the physician responsible for budgeting and controlling at their cancer centre. To cover the most diverse sample possible with different financing and reimbursement systems in the healthcare system, we aimed to include providers from all financial backgrounds. Since not all providers and most outpatient practices in cancer medicine are not part of a certified cancer centre, the strategy was to contact another non-certified haematology and oncology provider in the same city if the randomly selected certified cancer centre declined to participate. In these cases, we contacted the respective head of the haematology and oncology department or the owner of the outpatient practice to facilitate contact with the physician responsible for budgeting and controlling. This sampling decision was based on qualitative sampling principles (maximum variation) and best practice recommendations for transparency in qualitative studies.12

Data collection: semistructured interviews

Guided by the research questions, the questions for the semistructured interview guide were derived from the superordinate research interest, relevant literature,15,17 the results of a prior survey on this topic11 and discussions within the project team.

The key questions addressed the perceived economic influence and incentives on medical decision-making regarding diagnostic and therapeutic measures, as well as how they were managed. The interview guide started with an open question about economic influences in cancer medicine, followed by more specific questions about situations, decision-making and strategies. Questions addressed both negative and positive aspects. The interview guide was adapted after n=5 interviews to focus on positive and negative financial influence.

The formal structure of the interview guide was adopted from Mai et al18 and adapted and condensed to be more workable.

The interviews were conducted by a female interviewer (JFLK; MD, junior researcher, little experience in qualitative research, experienced in communicating with physicians in cancer medicine, prior work relationship with two of the participants) who was working as a physician and researcher at the time. The interviewer had received training in patient communication at medical school and had worked as a physician for 8 years. She had familiarised herself with methodological practice13 and was instructed and supervised by a senior scientist (KM; Ph.D. social science, vast experience in qualitative research, experienced in communicating with physicians in cancer medicine, no prior relationship with participants). The interviews took place at the interviewees’ workplace, except for one interview that was conducted via videocall. All interviews were in German and audio recorded. They were transcribed verbatim using f4x and proof-read by JFLK and SR (student assistant). Each interview was attributed an ID to pseudonymise. Verbatim interview quotes were translated into English and translation was cross-checked using DeepL.

No field notes were taken, and no repeat interviews were carried out. No prior relationship was established. Transcripts were not returned to participants for comment or correction, but participants received a copy of the transcript if they wished.

Data analysis: qualitative content analysis

Qualitative content analysis following Kuckartz12 was conducted using MAXQDA11 and MAXQDA 2020. All transcripts were coded by JFLK; two interviews were double coded by KM for verification. Relevant text passages were highlighted. Categories and subcategories were developed deductively and inductively. The analysis was carried out in five steps as described in figure 1.

Steps of the qualitative content analysis.

Two deductive thematic categories were derived from the interview guide: (1) medical decision-making situation and (2) financial influence. All data were coded according to these two categories, and the coded data were extracted. The extracted data were paraphrased and condensed into focused thematic summaries that described the specific decision-making situations and the respective incentives as the original data consisted of long paragraphs of verbatim quotes (boxes14).12 Wording and paraphrases of the focused thematic summaries were discussed among the junior researchers of the ELABORATE project (JFLK, GB, BA, SS) and reviewed and evaluated by the senior researchers (KM, ECW, WG, JS). Each focused thematic summary described the respective decision-making situation and financial influence. To understand the implication an influence had on medical decision-making situations, inductive analytic subcategories were derived from the focused thematic summaries that characterise the financial influence.12 All focused thematic summaries were then coded with these inductive analytic subcategories.

Rigour

Common strategies in data collection, analysis and reporting described by Adler et al ensured trustworthiness.19

Researchers’ involvement in interviews and data interpretation was repeatedly discussed to identify potential biases, such as researcher bias or selection bias. To ensure reflexivity and triangulation, all interviews were recorded and timely transcribed verbatim, allowing for an iterative data analysis process. KM reviewed several audio records to provide feedback on interviewing techniques. Findings were discussed among the junior researchers (BA, GB, SS, JFLK) and evaluated and supervised by the senior researchers (KM, ECW, JS, WG). Triangulation involved an interdisciplinary team with expertise in cancer medicine, health economics, socio-empirical research and medical ethics to discuss emerging divergences and enhance study reliability and validity. Results were discussed in relation to current research and study limitations. Purposive sampling strategies (maximum variation, snowball sampling and convenience sampling) ensured data richness. The predefined stratification was considerate of the particularities of the German healthcare system.

Ethical considerations

The study was approved by the local ethics commission (Ethics Committee of the Medical Faculty of Heidelberg University; S-691/2022; approved 08.12.2022). All participants gave written informed consent. The study is reported in accordance with the Consolidated Criteria for Reporting Qualitative Research (COREQ) and followed the Declaration of Helsinki 2023.20 21

Results

Interviews took place between February and August 2023 and lasted between 20 and 90 min. n=45 providers were contacted. 17 physicians with experience in budgeting and controlling agreed to be interviewed and we conducted 16 semistructured interviews in nine federal states.

Four financial sponsors and five different subspecialties of cancer medicine were included. For descriptive characteristics, see online supplemental table S1.

Based on the deductive main categories, we identified 21 medical decision-making situations that were influenced by financial considerations and were specific to cancer medicine. For each decision-making situation, we also identified the respective financial incentives (boxes14). The identified financial incentives were used to develop subcategories of financial influence. The focused thematic summaries of the financial influence described the actions that the incentives motivated, not how the interviewed participants dealt with the influence. Therefore, financial incentives were deducted theoretically.12

The following subcategories of financial influence on medical decision-making in cancer medicine were identified inductively and characterised the financial influence: (1) no reimbursement, (2) reimbursement that does not completely cover costs, (3) reimbursement that exceeds the costs and (4) costs covered for treatments with questionable cost-effectiveness.

The results suggest that financial considerations might affect the quality of patient care as we outline in detail in the following discussion.

Discussion of specific medical decision-making situations and their incentives

Even though the medical decision-making situations are quite specific to the German healthcare system, similar decision-making situations influenced by comparable financial considerations also arise in other healthcare systems,22,25 as shown by the ELABORATE project’s scoping review.26

A selective search by Lückmann et al summarised the state of the current research on economisation in inpatient care in Germany. They described that despite its negative connotation for most physicians, it remains unclear to what extent economic and financial considerations influence medical decision-making.9 Especially the evidence on the extent of the impact of economic and financial considerations, the impact on quality of care and the impact on high-risk patient groups is insufficient.9

To our knowledge, no empirical collection of specific decision-making situations that are influenced by financial considerations in cancer medicine in Germany has been presented yet. Therefore, the results of this study provide concrete use cases for quantifying the financial influence in cancer medicine and addressing the identified lack of evidence and, thereby, contributing to closing the evidence gap.9

Some of the identified medical decision-making situations contain potential effects on the quality of care, such as withholding indicated, beneficial measures for cost reasons. For instance, in situations 9 (where the costs of bone marrow puncture are not completely covered) and 12 (where the costs of brachytherapy are not completely covered), physicians might be incentivised to not offer the indicated procedure. Both examples illustrate how financial influence may reduce the quality of care by limiting available treatment and diagnostic options based on reimbursement policies.

The results point to possible patient groups that might be particularly affected by financial considerations like patients who receive novel or off-label treatment (compare situations 3 and 4). For both patient groups, it is unclear whether their treatment is reimbursed, which may incentivise the respective physicians not to prescribe the treatment and may lead to patients not getting the treatment they need in time or even at all.

The 21 decision-making situations provide use cases that can be operationalised, quantified and validated. The next step for future research would be an operationalisation with a subsequent quantification on an independent sample to generate evidence on the extent and impact of financial considerations on medical decision-making in cancer medicine.

Discussion of the four categories of financial influence

The four subcategories of financial influence we describe in our findings are: (1) no reimbursement, (2) reimbursement that does not completely cover costs, (3) reimbursement that exceeds the costs and (4) covered costs for treatments with questionable cost-effectiveness. They are not limited to the German healthcare context.27 28 Borras et al and Epstein et al found similar patterns in other European countries when focusing on specific decision-making situations in breast cancer or radiation therapy.

Our results complement findings from other studies in Germany. In a quantitative survey, Fernau et al asked physician members of the German Society of Hematology and Oncology how cost pressure was affecting their clinical practice. Physicians reported that cost considerations and negotiations gained increasing importance.11 Krause et al showed that cost considerations and reimbursement were often given as reasons for withholding expensive treatments.29 Wehkamp and Naegler conducted a qualitative study on the perceptions of physicians and chief executive officers regarding the influence of economic considerations on medical decision-making. They found that the financial pressure on chief executive officers to generate high profits resulted in operational guidelines that indirectly affected physicians’ actions and decision-making.17 Strech et al identified financial prerequisites and difficulties with DRG as subcategories of aggravating circumstances and influencing factors in their qualitative analysis of how financial scarcity influences medical decision-making.15 These findings underline the influence of reimbursement strategies on medical decision-making and are in line with our results.

The four subcategories of financial influences demonstrate that reimbursement strategy characterises the financial influence on medical decision-making in cancer medicine. Compared with the other studies, we showed that the characterisation by reimbursement strategy illustrates and defines the financial influence clearly. Further, it creates a better understanding of the financial influence and allows generalisation to different healthcare settings and systems.

As shown above, the results provide reasons to assume that financial considerations might influence the quality of cancer medicine. The characterisation by reimbursement strategy is a starting point for future research that analyses the normative implications financial influence might have.

Discussion of ethical consequences

The subcategories of financial influence show that reimbursement has an impact on medical decision-making because it creates a financially motivated incentive. The incentives invite specific actions on the respective decisions. That raises the question: Do they promote quality of cancer medicine, or do they reduce it? Many oncologists are concerned that the quality of care will suffer due to cost considerations.10 11 If empirical evidence points to a decline in quality of care, financial considerations may restrict the a-priori principle of medicine ‘“salus aegroti suprema lex’ and question whether physicians are still their patients’ keepers.30 That could affect the very core of physicians’ professional ethos and raise important issues of distributive justice.

But these questions can only partially be answered empirically. A subsequent normative-ethical analysis is needed to provide theoretical contributions and ethical insights on practical implications that guide policymakers and inform recommendations for physicians’ daily practices on how to deal with financial considerations. Therefore, a subsequent normative-ethical analysis of the results of this study is planned as part of ELABORATE. This combined empirical-ethical approach is a distinctive strength of the project and increases the translational potential of the findings for policy and practice.

Limitations

Our study has some limitations. Due to low recruitment rates, we had to resort to snowball and convenience sampling after purposive sampling. As many requests for interview participation went unanswered, the reasons for non-participation remained unclear. When contacted via telephone or email, lack of time or sensitivity of the topic was sometimes given as reasons for not participating.

All interview participants were of male gender and white, which is characteristic of the interviewed population. Experience in budgeting and controlling usually comes with a leadership position which in Germany is occupied predominantly by male and white persons.

Instead of an inter-rater approach, a single-coder approach with partial verification was applied. This was chosen because the interviewer and interviewees, both physicians specialised in cancer medicine, provided very detailed, but also very technical descriptions of how financial considerations can influence medical decision-making. The project team included no other person with similar knowledge and practical experience in cancer medicine and the same availability as the first coder. Therefore, we decided on an extended stepwise and iterative multiperspective approach to the thematic summaries including all project members who critically examined and discussed each extracted decision-making situation. To increase trustworthiness despite the single-coder approach, we applied peer debriefing and iterative team discussions. The evaluation and discussion ensured plausibility and were done with recourse to foundational data if needed to increase rigour, transferability and further verification.

Theoretical saturation was not reached due to the stratification approach, and therefore, the results are not fully comprehensive for all federal states and the different financial providers. That was anticipated while planning, and we decided to risk not reaching theoretical saturation for the gain of stratifying a diverse sample and including different perspectives. To reach theoretical saturation, separate studies on different financial providers and different federal states are needed.

Conclusion

The results support the hypothesis that medical decision-making can be influenced by financial consideration. The extracted decision-making situations provide specific starting points for further quantitative and ethical analysis for policy recommendations on how to deal with financial considerations in healthcare. They are significant use cases for future research to assess the quantitative effect financial influence might have on the quality of patient care. Qualitative and quantitative data taken together can provide an empirical foundation that underscores normative-ethical analysis to determine how to counter unintended consequences and limitations on quality of care. Therefore, the results are an important step towards data that can inform clinical guidelines, medical practice, choosing wisely initiatives and policy recommendations on how to deal with financial influence on medical decision-making in cancer medicine.
