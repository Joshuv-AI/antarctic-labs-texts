Large Language Models for Cancer Communication: Evaluating Linguistic Quality, Safety, and Accessibility in Generative AI
Agnik Saha, Victoria Churchill, Anny D. Rodriguez, Ugur Kursuncu, Muhammed Y. Idris

Abstract
Effective communication about breast and cervical cancers remains a persistent health challenge, with significant gaps in public understanding of cancer prevention, screening, and treatment, potentially leading to delayed diagnoses and inadequate treatments. This study evaluates the capabilities and limitations of Large Language Models (LLMs) in generating accurate, safe, and accessible cancer-related information to support patient understanding. We evaluated five general-purpose and three medical LLMs using a mixed-methods evaluation framework across linguistic quality, safety and trustworthiness, and communication accessibility and affectiveness. Our approach utilized quantitative metrics, qualitative expert ratings, and statistical analysis using Welch’s ANOVA, Games-Howell, Hedges’ g. Our results show that general-purpose LLMs produced outputs of higher linguistic quality and affectiveness, while medical LLMs demonstrate greater communication accessibility. However, medical LLMs tend to exhibit higher levels of potential harm, toxicity, and bias, reducing their performance in safety and trustworthiness. Our findings indicate a duality between domain-specific knowledge and safety in health communications. The results highlight the need for intentional model design with targeted improvements, particularly in mitigating harm and bias, and improving safety and affectiveness. This study provides a comprehensive evaluation of LLMs for cancer communication, providing critical insights for improving AI-generated health content and informing future development of accurate, safe, and accessible digital health tools.

Introduction

Cancer remains a leading cause of morbidity and mortality among women in the U.S., making it a critical public health issue. One in three women in the U.S. will be diagnosed with cancer in her lifetime, and one in six will die from the disease. Breast cancer is the most commonly diagnosed cancer among women, with an estimated
310,720
new cases and
42,250
deaths projected in 2024
(
American Cancer Society, (2023)
)
. Despite improvements in screening and treatment, disparities in cancer outcomes persist. For instance, Black women experience a 40% higher breast cancer mortality rate than White women, despite similar incidence rates, largely due to systemic inequities in screening access, delayed diagnoses, and unequal healthcare
(
Siegel et al., (2023)
;
Warner et al., (2012)
;
Moore et al., (2023)
)
. Cervical cancer, the most common cancer among women in the U.S., had
13,360
new cases in 2025
(
American Cancer Society, (2024)
)
, with Black women facing a mortality rate 200% higher than White women and Hispanic women experiencing a 51% higher incidence rate
(
Olusola et al., (2019)
;
Moore de Peralta et al., (2017)
;
Spencer et al., (2023)
)
. These disparities are rooted in broader structural and social barriers, including financial hardship, limited geographic access, and psychological challenges
(
Consedine et al., (2004)
)
.

Emerging technologies, such as Large Language Models (LLMs), offer promising generative AI applications to support potentially more effective and equitable health communications for breast and cervical cancers. LLMs have the potential to improve access to reliable, understandable, and personalized health information. However, their rapid advancement has outpaced research into their real-world effectiveness and safety in clinical and public health settings. Existing studies provide limited evaluation of how well LLMs deliver cancer-related information that is accurate, unbiased, and accessible
(
Johnson et al., (2016)
;
Grilo et al., (2025)
)
. Experts strongly encourage that before we commit to this new frontier in cancer communications, we need to address accuracy, safety, and privacy
(
Zitu et al., (2025)
)
. These identified gaps are particularly concerning given the risks of misleading or incorrect information, which can delay diagnoses, influence harmful treatment decisions, and erode trust in health institutions
(
Swire-Thompson et al., (2020)
)
.

Given the urgency of addressing the need for high-quality communication tools in cancer care, this study was conducted to evaluate the quality and safety of LLM-generated content in the context of breast and cervical cancer. Our rationale stems from the critical importance of ensuring that AI-driven tools do not exacerbate existing disparities or contribute to patient harm. The specific objectives of this work are two-fold:

•

Develop a comprehensive evaluation framework that systematically assesses LLMs across three dimensions: linguistic quality, safety and trustworthiness, and communication accessibility and affectiveness;

•

Characterize the relative strengths and limitations of general-purpose and specialized medical LLMs for cancer communications.

In this study, we developed an evaluation framework for effective cancer communication with
quantitative
and
qualitative
elements, which span three main categories: (i) Linguistic Quality, (ii) Safety & Trustworthiness, and (iii) Communication Accessibility & Affectiveness. Five general‑purpose LLMs (Llama 3, Gemma, Alpaca, Mistral, Vicuna) and three specialized medical LLMs (MedAlpaca, BioMistral, Meditron) were utilized for evaluation. For linguistic quality, we used quantitative metrics for semantic coherence and consistency, and hallucinations, as well as qualitative ratings by domain experts assessing accuracy, coherence, jargon control, and reasoning. Safety and trustworthiness were assessed using quantitative toxicity and bias metrics, complemented by expert evaluations of harm and user trust. Finally, communication accessibility and affective impact were measured with readability indices, reflection score, and qualitative assessments of clarity, empathy, compassion, actionability, domain relevance, and user acceptability.

Our findings show that general-purpose LLMs, particularly Llama 3 and Gemma, outperformed specialized medical models in Linguistic Quality. In contrast, medical LLMs, such as MedAlpaca and BioMistral demonstrated better Communication Accessibility, generating outputs with lower grade-level requirements and reduced complexity. While general-purpose LLMs, especially Llama 3, demonstrated more affective communication, general-purpose models, such as Alpaca and Llama 3, consistently outperformed medical LLMs in Safety and Trustworthiness, producing responses that were safer, less toxic, and less biased.

2
Related Work

Recent research has increasingly focused on evaluating LLMs in healthcare across both general-purpose and domain-specific settings. A systematic review identified over 760 LLM evaluations, with more than 93% involving general-purpose models such as GPT-3.5 and GPT-4, and fewer than 6% assessing specialized medical LLMs
(
Shool et al., (2025)
)
. Google’s Med-PaLM series achieved 86.5% on USMLE-style exams and outperformed clinicians on eight of nine physician preference axes
(
Singhal et al., (2025)
)
. Other proprietary models, such as Claude, have also been assessed along with the GPT family of LLMs; in oncology-focused evaluations, the responses from GPT-3.5, GPT-4, and Claude were rated higher than those from physicians in empathy, quality, and readability
(
D. Chen et al., (2024)
)
. Another comparative analysis in the breast cancer context showed that GPT-4 outperformed GPT-3.5 and Claude 2 in accuracy, relevance, and clinical applicability across assessment, treatment planning, and psychosocial care
(
Deng et al., (2024)
)
.

In oncology, LLM applications have spanned patient education, triage, and physician note drafting, with nearly half of oncology studies focusing on patient communication, followed by diagnostic support (20%) and treatment recommendations (17%)
(
D. Chen et al., (2025)
)
. Studies such as Chen et al. used panels of oncologists and LLMs (GPT-3.5, GPT-4, Claude) to evaluate responses to real-world patient questions, underscoring LLMs’ potential as virtual cancer consultants
(
D. Chen et al., (2024)
)
. In primary care, evaluations have included patient forum moderation
(
Ayers et al., (2023)
)
, licensing exam simulations
(
Thirunavukarasu et al., (2023)
)
, and randomized controlled trials assessing diagnostic assistance
(
Goh et al., (2024)
)
.

Common evaluation metrics included accuracy (22% of studies), relevance, completeness, readability (e.g., Flesch-Kincaid), empathy, bias, factual consistency, and safety
(
D. Chen et al., (2025)
;
Ayers et al., (2023)
)
. Med-PaLM 2 has been rated comparably safe to physician responses
(
Singhal et al., (2025)
)
, and GPT-4 consistently outperforms GPT-3.5 on clinical tasks
(
Singhal et al., (2025)
)
. As social media increasingly serves as a platform for mental health communications
(
Sinha et al., (2023)
)
, recent work has shown that prompting and fine-tuning LLMs with mental health–related social media data can improve linguistic quality, but may also increase toxicity and reduce emotional appropriateness
(
Kursuncu et al., (2025)
)
. However, limitations remain. For example, adversarial benchmarks like Cancer-Myth highlight persistent failures in identifying false presuppositions in patient queries
(
Zhu et al., (2025)
)
, and randomized trials suggest that LLM integration into clinical workflows does not uniformly improve diagnostic outcomes
(
Goh et al., (2024)
)
. Together, these studies reveal both the promise and current boundaries of LLM applications in oncology and primary care.

Despite growing interest in LLMs for healthcare, critical gaps remain in evaluating their performance for cancer-specific applications. Prior studies have largely focused on general medical benchmarks or isolated oncology tasks; in contrast, our study provides the first systematic evaluation of eight general-purpose and medical LLMs specifically for breast and cervical cancer communication. While prior work has studied biases in healthcare AI
(
Obermeyer et al., (2019)
;
Sorin et al., (2023)
;
J. Huang et al., (2022)
;
Intahchomphoo & Gundersen, (2020)
)
, few have examined their impact in cancer-specific communications. Further, we evaluate whether LLMs can meet the needs of patients with limited health literacy, cognitive challenges, or information overload
(
Jindal & MacDermid, (2017)
;
Federman et al., (2009)
;
Morava et al., (2024)
;
Jensen et al., (2014)
)
.

3
Methods

Our approach consists of four phases. First, we developed a comprehensive evaluation framework. Second, we curated a domain-specific dataset for breast and cervical cancer. Third, we selected five general-purpose and three medical LLMs to generate responses for questions in our dataset. Finally, we applied our evaluation framework to the generated responses from each model and conducted statistical analyses for the quantitative metrics and expert qualitative ratings.

3.1
Evaluation Framework

As per the probabilistic nature of LLMs and the randomness of the generated outputs, it is essential to develop and employ a comprehensive evaluation framework, specifically designed for breast and cervical cancers and the respective patient population. This evaluation of LLMs provides a structured approach to systematically assess different aspects of the generated content, ensuring that the evaluation is thorough, consistent, and based on well-defined criteria. Recognizing the unique needs and challenges faced by women in underserved communities, the generated content on breast and cervical cancers should be clearly written, safe, and accessible to ensure it is both understandable and trustworthy
(
Abbasian et al., (2024)
)
. Our framework spans three main dimensions: Linguistic Quality, Safety and Trustworthiness, as well as Communication Accessibility and Affectiveness (See Figure
2
).

:
Comprehensive evaluation framework for evaluating general purpose and specialized medical LLMs for cancer communication.

3.1.1
Linguistic Quality

Linguistic quality focuses on assessing the accuracy, contextual relevance, and overall quality of the generated responses. We utilized three primary metrics: ROUGE, Bleurt Score, and BERTScore, which collectively scrutinize the overall fidelity of the generated content. ROUGE (Recall-Oriented Understudy for Gisting Evaluation) was employed to measure how well the key information related to a concept (e.g., breast cancer) is captured in the generated content with respect to trusted reference sources based on the overlap of n-grams
(
Lin, (2004)
)
. Bleurt Score assesses the quality of the generated text by comparing its semantic and syntactic alignment with a reference corpus utilizing a pre-trained language model
(
Sellam et al., (2020)
)
. BERTScore measures semantic similarity between the generated text and the reference text, employing BERT contextual embeddings
(
Zhang et al., (2020)
)
. Further, we measure the likelihood of ‘hallucination,’ which is the generated information by the LLM that is not grounded in the reference material
(
Zhang et al., (2023)
)
. We analyzed the entropy of named entities and nouns, most prone to hallucinations. As the model’s uncertainty is measured through entropy, higher values indicate a higher probability of inaccurate information; thus, a greater risk of generating misleading content. This hallucination score, which adjusts for varying confidence levels and is normalized, provides a safeguard against the erroneous health information generated by LLMs.

Our qualitative assessment of linguistic quality uses four complementary metrics. Accuracy was evaluated to ensure clinical correctness of the information. Coherence assessed the logical flow and internal consistency of responses. Jargon was used to identify and evaluate the use of overly technical or specialized language that might reduce comprehensibility. Finally, Understanding and Reasoning captured the model’s ability to interpret medical queries correctly and respond with logically structured, contextually appropriate answers. Together, these quantitative and qualitative measures provide a comprehensive evaluation of the linguistic soundness of LLM-generated cancer communication content.

3.1.2
Safety & Trustworthiness

This dimension critically assesses the generated content for any potential harmful elements, including biases and toxic language, which can be detrimental to patient trust and the efficacy of health communications. Specifically, we employed metrics to measure three potential risks: toxicity, gender bias, and racial bias. For toxicity, we used Perspective API to detect and quantify any harmful or inappropriate language that could undermine the safety of the communication
(
Jigsaw, (2024)
;
Erol et al., (2025)
)
. While breast cancer primarily affects women, it can also develop in men, albeit much less commonly
(
A. Huang et al., (2025)
;
Anderson et al., (2010)
)
. However, measuring potential gender bias is still essential to ensure that communication remains non-discriminatory and does not overlook male patients. To measure potential gender bias, we utilized the Genbit score
(
Sengupta et al., (2021)
)
designed to quantify gender bias within the generated content, using the average bias conditional absolute score. A higher Genbit score suggests a stronger gender association, indicating significant gender bias. We evaluate the generated content for racial bias, utilizing in-context impersonation
(
Salewski et al., (2024)
)
, following prior work
(
Levy et al., (2024)
;
Poulain et al., (2024)
)
, adding specific racial or ethnic contexts to the prompts, and analyzing the model’s responses. This approach allows us to disentangle any disparities in the content that may indicate underlying biases. Fifty representative prompts were used to ensure feasibility and diversity.

In addition to these quantitative measures, we conducted a qualitative assessment using two core metrics: Harm, Trust and Confidence. Harm was evaluated to identify content that could be emotionally distressing, misleading, or potentially medically dangerous. Trust and confidence assessed whether the tone, framing, and presentation of the information encouraged users to trust the content and feel supported in their decision-making. Including bias and toxic language in model outputs can also undermine patient trust and reduce the effectiveness of health communications.

3.1.3
Communication Accessibility & Affectiveness

Our evaluation incorporates readability indices to measure communication accessibility, along with affective metrics to assess the emotional tone and impact of the content
(
Salovey & Mayer, (1990)
;
Tomkins, (1962)
)
. To render the generated content easily understood by a diverse audience, we employed six established readability indices. More specifically, Flesch Reading Ease and Flesch-Kincaid Grade Level measure readability of text based on sentence length and syllable count, indicating the education level required to understand the information
(
Flesch, (1948)
;
Kincaid et al., (1975)
)
. Gunning Fog and SMOG indices calculate the years of education needed to understand the text based on sentence complexity and the use of polysyllabic words, including medical terms
(
Gunning, (1952)
;
McLaughlin, (1969)
)
. Automated Readability Index (ARI) and Coleman-Liau Index measure readability by analyzing character count per word and sentence length, offering a grade-level estimation necessary for comprehension
(
Senter & Smith, (1967)
;
Coleman & Liau, (1975)
)
. To evaluate affective impact of the generated content, we utilized PAIR (Prompt Aware Informative Response)
(
Min et al., (2022)
)
. This approach assesses how well a medical advisory response reflects and affirms a patient’s experiences, crucial for empathetic communication. As this method utilizes the Motivational Interviewing Reflection Dataset, it helps ensure that the responses generated by LLMs align with the emotional states of the patients by generating a reflection score.

We complemented these quantitative measures with qualitative evaluations designed to assess the emotional tone, usability, and practicality of the responses. Clarity and Empathy measured the degree to which the information was both understandable and compassionately conveyed. Compassion focused more specifically on emotional sensitivity and supportiveness. Cue to Action evaluated whether the content motivated constructive next steps, such as seeking screening or preventive care. Domain Relevance assessed whether the content remained focused on breast or cervical cancer, avoiding generic or off-topic information. Finally, Usability and Acceptability considered how practical, approachable, and suitable the information was for the target audience. Together, these metrics provide a comprehensive understanding of how effectively LLMs communicate health information that is not only accurate but also supportive and usable for real-world patients.

3.2
Dataset

We curated a domain-specific dataset for evaluating LLMs on breast and cervical cancer communication by filtering five publicly available medical datasets using the keywords “breast cancer” and “cervical cancer.” PubMedQA
(
Singhal et al., (2023)
)
, comprising biomedical Q&A pairs from PubMed abstracts, contributed 3,310 filtered instances. MedQA-USMLE
(
Pal et al., (2022)
;
Jin et al., (2021)
)
, based on U.S. medical licensing exam questions, provided 141 instances, while MedMCQA
(
Pal et al., (2022)
)
, covering Indian medical entrance exams, contributed 278 cases. From MedLFQA
(
Jeong et al., (2024)
)
, which aggregates consumer health queries from sources such as LiveQA
(
Agichtein et al., (2015)
)
, MedicationQA
(
Abacha et al., (2019)
)
, HealthSearchQA
(
Singhal et al., (2023)
)
, and K-QA
(
Manes et al., (2024)
)
, we extracted 36 relevant cases. Additionally, HealthcareMagic and iCliniq
(
Y. Li et al., (2023)
)
, both user-generated Q&A platforms, added 835 and 43 instances, respectively. The final dataset comprised 4,643 cases, offering a diverse and clinically relevant foundation to rigorously assess LLMs’ performance in generating accurate, safe, and patient-centered cancer information.

3.3
Experimental Setup: Selected LLMs

We selected both general-purpose and specialized medical LLMs to assess their effectiveness in generating accurate breast and cervical cancer information, aiming to compare general-purpose and specialized medical LLMs for their performance in the three main evaluation categories. Models were chosen based on two key criteria: having less than or equal to 8B parameters and open-source availability, ensuring accessibility and feasibility for deployment under resource constraints.

3.3.1
General Purpose LLMs

We evaluated five general-purpose LLMs: Vicuna 7B, Alpaca 7B
(
Taori et al., (2023)
)
, Llama 3 8B
(
Dubey et al., (2024)
)
, Mistral 7B
(
Jiang et al., (2023)
)
, and Gemma 7B
(
Team et al., (2024)
)
, selected for their state-of-the-art performance in generating content
(
Liang et al., (2022)
)
, and training methodologies. Vicuna was fine-tuned on user-shared conversations from ShareGPT for context-aware responses to medical queries. Alpaca was built on Llama 7B using 52,000 instruction-following examples. Llama 3 8B has demonstrated state-of-the-art results across tasks, including medical text generation
(
Leong et al., (2024)
)
. Mistral 7B and Gemma 7B were designed to capture complex linguistic patterns using diverse datasets.

3.3.2
Medical LLMs

We included three specialized medical LLMs: MedAlpaca
(
Han et al., (2023)
)
, BioMistral 7B
(
Labrak et al., (2024)
)
, and Meditron
(
Z. Chen et al., (2023)
)
, to assess domain-specific performance, particularly for breast and cervical cancer. MedAlpaca, built on Alpaca, was fine-tuned on medical datasets (e.g., ChatDoc, Anki flashcards). BioMistral, a biomedical variant of Mistral, draws on specialized literature specializing in complex medical queries. Meditron, based on Llama 2 7B, was tuned on curated medical corpora for clinical accuracy and contextual relevance. These specialized models were designed to provide better specificity and expertise in patient care.

3.4
Data Analysis

3.4.1
Statistical Analysis of Quantitative Metrics

We applied Welch’s ANOVA
(
Welch, (1951)
)
to each evaluation metric to test whether there were statistically significant differences in performance across the eight LLMs, suitable for datasets with unequal variances and sample sizes, conditions consistent with our experimental setting. For metrics that showed significance, Games-Howell post hoc tests
(
Games & Howell, (1976)
)
were used to perform pairwise comparisons between every unique pair of LLMs without assuming homogeneity of variance or equal sample sizes, for this multi-model and multi-metric comparison. . For each LLM pair, we computed Hedges’ g
(
Hedges, (1981)
)
to quantify the effect size and direction of difference. Rankings were adjusted accordingly: if the effect size was positive (indicating better performance), the first model’s rank increased and the second’s decreased, and vice versa. Statistical significance was set at
p<0.05
, with both p-values and effect sizes used to assess statistical and practical significance jointly.

3.4.2
Coding of Qualitative Data and Evaluation

Two domain experts in health communication and breast and cervical cancer (VC and AR) independently evaluated model outputs using a structured rubric aligned with the three core evaluation categories.
We collected 400 responses from eight LLMs by submitting 50 randomly selected questions to each model, creating an 8 × 50 dataset. To minimize bias, model identities were masked during evaluation. Responses were rated on multiple qualitative criteria in each category (e.g., accuracy, harm, empathy, trust, clarity, actionability) using a 3-point Likert scale. Scores from each expert were averaged for each criterion item (e.g., average score for accuracy, average score for empathy)and treated as interval data, consistent with standard practices in psychometrics and health communication research
(
Wu & Leung, (2017)
)
. This approach was selected to align with the study’s focus on category-level evaluation, reducing item-level variability, and emphasizing consistent rating patterns across categories. Inter-rater reliability was assessed using Weighted Cohen’s Kappa (
\kappa_{w}
), with quadratic weights applied to penalize larger disagreements more heavily
(
Landis & Koch, (1977)
;
M. Li et al., (2023)
)
. Descriptive statistics were reported by model and category, providing a rigorous, assessment of model performance.

Performance of LLMs in Linguistic Quality

Quantitative Evaluation.

Our analysis revealed significant differences in BLEURT, BERTScore, and ROUGE across models, indicating distinct strengths and weaknesses in linguistic fluency and content quality. As shown in Table

and

, post hoc analysis identified general LLMs, specifically Llama 3, outperforming medical LLMs, based on BLEURT (0.41), BERTScore Recall (0.86), and ROUGE-1 (0.51), indicating higher linguistic quality, fluency, and relevance. Gemma performed better in ROUGE-1 and ROUGE-L, demonstrating robust performance in capturing content overlap. Among medical LLMs, BioMistral demonstrated higher precision (BERTScore Precision and F1: 0.82), highlighting its capability for accurate, domain-specific content generation. However, LLMs, such as Alpaca and Mistral, showed elevated hallucination scores (both at 0.57), suggesting a trade-off between fluency and factuality. Llama 3 had the lowest hallucination score among general LLMs, demonstrating its robustness in factual accuracy. Meditron achieved the highest ranking and aggregate scores in BLEURT, BERTScore Recall, and all ROUGE variants, indicating overall superiority in linguistic quality.

Qualitative Evaluation Performance.
Qualitative assessments (Table

) revealed moderate to near perfect inter-rater agreement, especially for coherence (

) and accuracy (
\kappa_{w}=0.60

In contrast, MedAlpaca and Meditron scored lowest, with Meditron exhibiting poor coherence (1.13) and excessive jargon (1.57), suggesting limitations in clarity and accessibility. Alpaca and Mistral performed moderately but lagged in reasoning and accuracy. These findings indicate that general-purpose models, particularly Llama 3 and Gemma, outperformed specialized medical LLMs in generating clear and accurate cancer-related communication content.

In contrast, Llama 3 and Gemma generated significantly more complex responses, with Flesch scores below 40, Grade Levels above 12, making them more appropriate for high-literacy audiences. Meditron and Vicuna produced denser text with lower readability and greater complexity. These results suggest that Alpaca and MedAlpaca are well-suited for patient-facing communication, while general-purpose models, such as Llama 3, may require further simplification to reach broader public audiences.

Gemma and Vicuna followed with strong scores in domain relevance and usability. In contrast, MedAlpaca and Meditron underperformed, particularly in usability and motivational content, suggesting limitations in generating patient-centered outputs. Alpaca and Mistral scored moderately, with strengths in compassion but weaker usability. Overall, general-purpose LLMs, especially Llama 3, demonstrated stronger affective and communicative performance than medical LLMs.

4.4
Discussion

This study reveals distinct and complementary strengths between general-purpose and medical LLMs across linguistic quality, safety & trustworthiness, and communication accessibility & affectiveness.

General-purpose models, particularly Llama 3 and Gemma, outperformed others in generating fluent, coherent, and factually accurate responses. Medical LLMs hallucinated more frequently than general LLMs, where Llama 3 had the lowest hallucination rate. The qualitative evaluation also supported these observations with criterion items rated highest, including accuracy, coherence. jargon, and understanding. While medical LLMs were developed to be utilized for domain-specific applications, we observe that the linguistic quality of the content they generate potentially may not be satisfactory.

Further, despite their medical fine-tuning, BioMistral and Meditron exhibited higher toxicity and bias scores than general LLMs. In contrast, Alpaca and MedAlpaca showed the lowest toxicity and gender bias, as well as Llama 3 and Gemma with the lowest racial bias, indicating their potential for safer use in sensitive health domains. Llama 3 and Gemma were also found safest based on qualitative criteria for harm and trust/confidence, suggesting their reliability for safety and trustworthiness. Medical LLMs were overall not satisfactory in their performance in this category.

Medical LLMs, such as MedAlpaca, produced the most readable outputs along with a general LLM, Alpaca, suitable for public health communication. However, Llama 3 generated content that was more complex yet highly rated for empathy, clarity, and domain relevance, indicating its ability to convey emotionally resonant and actionable information despite a higher readability threshold. Overall, general LLMs were better for generating affective communications, whereas medical LLMs were better for producing more readable content.

While each of these metrics and criteria items is critical, it is surprising to observe that specialized medical LLMs perform poorly on the safety, affectiveness, and linguistic quality. General-purpose LLMs provide better linguistic fluency and affective engagement, while medical models tend to produce simpler, more accessible content, albeit often at the expense of safety, trust, and coherence.

Implications for Clinical Relevance.
Specialized medical LLMs require further scrutiny before clinical deployment or utilization. Their poor performance stem from fine-tuning strategies that prioritize domain knowledge at the expense of safety, coherence, and affectiveness; hence, these processes should account for clinical relevance and sensitivity, especially for patient-facing communication tasks. Given the probabilistic nature of LLMs, grounding their generation process with clinical communication standards, including clarity, empathy, and harm mitigation, becomes critical for broader deployment in digital health tools. Recent research suggests that integrating external structured knowledge representation help achieve these goals, enhancing the generated content
(
Xu et al., (2023)
;
Khandelwal et al., (2024)
)
. Knowledge infusion from external knowledge and distillation from larger LLMs can provide better recall and precision as well as scalability
(
Meng et al., (2021)
;
Garg et al., (2024)
)
. Hence, future directions should prioritize the development of hybrid neurosymbolic approaches that incorporate domain-specific reasoning during fine-tuning, prompt optimization, and post-processing and -training to achieve clinically robust outputs. This is particularly critical for digital health applications in patient education, preventive care, and clinical decision support.

Limitations.
This study focused on a selected set of open-source models and benchmark datasets. Results may not generalize to proprietary models or real-world patient interactions. In addition, the datasets may contain topic or language distribution biases. Factors such as cultural context, language preference, and patient-specific health literacy were not fully represented and warrant further study.

5
Conclusion

This study advances the science of health communication by systematically evaluating the capabilities and limitations of LLMs in generating breast and cervical cancer–related communicative information, with a focus on linguistic quality, safety, and communication accessibility and affectiveness. General-purpose models demonstrated superior fluency and coherence but produced more complex outputs that may challenge accessibility. In contrast, domain-specific medical models generated simpler responses albeit often at the expense of linguistic quality, affectiveness, safety. These findings highlight the complementary strengths of general-purpose and medical LLMs and highlight persistent challenges related to readability, trust, and contextual reliability. Advancing AI-driven health communication will require strategies that combine neural and symbolic approaches to support linguistic quality, safety and affectiveness along with domain accuracy to ensure safe, comprehensible, and trustworthy information delivery at scale.
