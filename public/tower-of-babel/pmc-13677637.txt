Associations of surgical breast cancer treatment at regional or community hospitals with patient-reported experience measures
Authors: Savannah R. Gonzales, Uma Ramesh, A. James O’Malley, Gabriel A. Brooks, Tracy Onega, Erika L. Moen
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
PurposeHigh-volume hospitals deliver improved outcomes for patients with breast cancer, supporting referrals for breast cancer surgical care to high-volume regional facilities. This retrospective cohort study investigated how multidisciplinary cancer care trajectories and patient care experiences varied by surgical facility classification (regional vs. community as a proxy for high- vs. low-volume) and whether effects differed by rural-residence.MethodsWe used Surveillance, Epidemiology, and End Results-Consumer Assessment of Healthcare Providers and Systems (SEER-CAHPS) data for Medicare beneficiaries who received surgery for non-metastatic breast cancer from 2009 to 2019. We mapped cancer care trajectories using facility classification and oncologist specialty and performed state sequence analysis to identify care trajectory clusters. We used linear mixed-effects models to examine associations of surgical facility classification with patient-reported experience measures and assessed effect modification by rurality.ResultsOf the 3,327 patients in our cohort, 15.4% were rural-residing and 68.8% received surgery at a regional facility. Care trajectories indicated that patients tended to receive adjuvant care from the same type of facility as their surgical treatment. Patients who received surgery at community facilities (vs. regional) were significantly more likely to report better experiences with getting needed care (Est. (95%CI) = 3.5 (0.10,6.9), p = 0.043), with no significant differences for getting care quickly, care coordination, or doctor communication. Rural-residence was not a significant effect modifier.ConclusionReceiving surgery at community facilities is associated with better patient-reported experiences for getting needed care. Moreover, surgical facility classification appears to influence the setting for subsequent cancer care, which may impact patient experiences in getting needed care. Introduction

Procedure volume-based regionalization of care has been linked to improved clinical outcomes for patients with breast cancer [1–7]. Evidence of increased cancer-specific and overall survival have encouraged policies promoting regionalization of breast cancer surgical procedures to high-volume hospitals [8, 9]. However, the need to balance the benefits of regionalization with equitable access to care is supported by literature documenting the wide geographic variation in access to specialized cancer care in the United States [10, 11]. Prior work found that 55% of the United States population resided over one hour from the nearest National Cancer Institute (NCI)-Designated Cancer Center [12]. Rural-residing patients with a common cancer traveled a median of 89 min to receive surgical care, compared to 36 min for urban-residing patients [13]. For patients facing geographic barriers to specialized cancer care, such as rural-residents, regionalization of surgical care may not only influence the initial surgical procedure [10, 14–19], but could also have unintended consequences, such as treatment delays [20] and lower adherence to guideline-concordant care [10, 18, 19, 21]. Indeed, rural-residing patients experienced greater travel burden and treatment delays compared with urban residents when referred to a higher-volume regional center over closer, lower-volume alternatives [22].

While surgical treatment is a key component of breast cancer treatment, adjuvant radiation therapy and/or chemotherapy are often indicated for guideline-concordant care. Collaboration between multiple specialists is therefore essential to receive high quality breast cancer care and reduce risk of experiencing surgical and adjuvant treatment delays [23–26]. Regionalization of surgical care may impact the management of multidisciplinary cancer treatment, yet we have limited insight into the extent to which regionalization of breast cancer surgery is associated with multidisciplinary cancer care trajectories. Furthermore, because patients with cancer often prefer to receive care closer to home [27–29], additional research on how regionalization of surgical care impacts patient experiences with care access and coordination is needed.

The objective of this study was to evaluate associations of surgical breast cancer treatment at regional or community facilities (as a proxy for hospital surgical volume) with cancer care trajectories and patient experiences. Care trajectories have been characterized using state sequence analysis for a variety of chronic conditions [30–34]. This method allows researchers to jointly capture the timing, continuity, and transitions across specialties and care settings. We applied state sequence analysis to characterize care trajectories based on facility classification (regional vs. community) and cancer provider specialty (surgery, medical oncology, or radiation oncology). We first sought to better understand how multidisciplinary care trajectories differ following surgical treatment at regional and community settings. Then, we assessed the relationship between surgical facility classification and patient-reported experiences with getting care quickly, getting needed care, care coordination, and doctor communication.

Methods

Data sources and study cohort

This study used 2009–2019 Surveillance, Epidemiology and End Results (SEER)-Medicare data for beneficiaries who responded to a Centers for Medicare & Medicaid Services (CMS) Consumer Assessment of Healthcare Providers and Systems (CAHPS) survey [35]. This data links Medicare administrative claims, the NCI’s SEER cancer registry data, and the CAHPS survey, which evaluates health care experiences among Medicare beneficiaries. We constructed a cohort of Medicare beneficiaries who were diagnosed with stage I-III breast cancer between 2009 and 2019 and received surgical treatment. Due to the nature of SEER-CAHPS data, this cohort only includes Medicare beneficiaries who responded to the CAHPS survey between 2009 and 2019. Additional cohort criteria required patients to be female, aged 66–99 years at the time of diagnosis, survive the following 9 months, live in a SEER region, and be continuously enrolled in Traditional Medicare (Parts A and B) for the 12 months preceding and 9 months following their cancer diagnosis month (Table S1).

For modeling survey outcomes, we limited the analysis to patients who completed a survey within two years of their cancer diagnosis to more closely reflect care experiences during cancer treatment. As such, the analytic cohort in the models represents a subset of the cohort used to build the cancer care trajectories.

Facility classification

We classified all facilities as “regional” or “community” to serve as a proxy for surgical volume. The Healthcare Cost and Utilization Project (HCUP) defines hospital size based on its number of beds, teaching status, rurality, and geographic region [36]. We used the NCI Hospital Characteristics File to gather these variables for the facilities listed in patient claims (see Supplemental Methods). Any facility that satisfied the HCUP criteria for a large hospital was classified as a regional facility; any facility that did not meet these criteria was classified as a community facility. All NCI-Designated Cancer Centers were classified as regional facilities. Missing facility IDs in NCH Carrier claims were imputed using Outpatient and MedPAR claims (see Supplemental Methods). Table S2 reports characteristics for regional and community facilities included in the study.

Cancer care trajectories

We used NCH Carrier claims to construct cancer care trajectories for the 9 months following the first day of a patient’s cancer diagnosis month. We chose 9 months to sufficiently capture surgical and adjuvant treatment encounters. We included claims with a diagnosis code for breast cancer (ICD-9: 174, 175; ICD-10: C50) and physician specialty listed as medical oncology, radiation oncology, or surgery (inclusive of surgical oncology, plastic/reconstructive surgery, and general surgery). We classified each patient encounter-date based on a two-dimensional state corresponding to physician specialty and facility classification (Figure S1). Each patient began in a “None” state. To reflect being under the care of their physician, patients stayed in a state for 7 days, or until another cancer care encounter, whichever occurred first. If a patient had multiple claims with different states on the same day, all their claims on that day were assigned the state called “Multiple states.” After 7 days of no cancer care encounters, patients returned to the “None” state. Sensitivity analyses for different time-units (1 day, 14 days) were explored and showed similar trajectories.

We stratified patients into two groups based on the facility classification where they received their initial surgical treatment to characterize different care trajectories within those strata (labeled “regional surgery cohort” and “community surgery cohort”). We used the R package TraMineR to perform state sequence analysis, which uses a sequence alignment algorithm to calculate dissimilarity scores for each pair of sequences. We used the dynamic hamming distance alignment algorithm because it allows for time-varying substitution costs based on the observed transition rates [34, 37]. Then, we performed agglomerative clustering on the matrix of pairwise dissimilarity scores to identify clusters with distinct characteristics and visually examined the inertia curves to determine the optimal number of clusters.

Study variables

Independent variables

Surgical facility classification

We identified the first ICD-9 or ICD-10 procedure code for lumpectomy or mastectomy (Table S3), assigned the corresponding facility ID to each patient, and determined if the facility was a regional or community facility, as described above.

Rurality

Patient rurality of residence at time of diagnosis was determined using county-level Rural-Urban Continuum Codes (RUCC) provided in the SEER-CAHPS Cancer file. We dichotomized the RUCC into metropolitan and non- metropolitan categories, as defined by the US Department of Agriculture [38].

Outcome variables

The outcomes of interest were the patient-reported CAHPS composite scores for four domains of patient experiences: getting care quickly, getting needed care, care coordination, and doctor communication. Composite scores were created based on patient responses to individual questions relating to each category. These variables were transformed using linear mean scoring and reported on a scale from 0 (worst) to 100 (best).

Patient variables

We gathered patient age at diagnosis, sex, race, ethnicity, rurality, Yost Index quintile (a composite socioeconomic status score from the American Community Survey 5-year estimates) [39], cancer stage, and month of diagnosis from the SEER-CAHPS Cancer file. From the SEER-CAHPS Survey File, we obtained patient self-reported education level, general and mental health status, Medicaid eligibility, and whether respondents received help answering survey questions. Lastly, we calculated survey timing by subtracting the number of months between each patient’s cancer diagnosis and survey completion date. We used MedPAR, Outpatient, and NCH Carrier claims to determine receipt of lumpectomy, mastectomy, chemotherapy, and radiation therapy (Table S3). We chose to focus on these components of breast cancer treatment because they require facility services and equipment in addition to encounters with cancer specialists.

Statistical analysis

We compared patient sociodemographic and clinical characteristics by surgical facility classification and cluster assignments. Continuous variable comparisons used Wilcoxon rank-sum and Kruskal-Wallis tests; categorical variable comparisons used Chi-squared and Fisher exact tests. All tests of statistical significance were two-sided with a significance level of 0.05.

We conducted a series of linear mixed-effects regression models with surgical facility classification as a categorical predictor, patient experience measures as outcomes, and surgical facility ID as a random intercept to account for variation between facilities. We included an interaction term to test whether the effect of facility classification differed by patient rurality. Covariates were selected a priori based on prior literature and their potential to confound the associations of interest. All models were adjusted for patient age at diagnosis, race/ethnicity, rurality, Yost Index quintile, cancer stage, survey year, months between cancer diagnosis and survey completion, and standard SEER-CAHPS case-mix adjustment variables (general and mental health status, education level, Medicaid eligibility, and proxy assistance).

Results

Characteristics of study cohort

The study cohort consisted of 3,327 female Medicare beneficiaries who received surgical treatment for non-metastatic breast cancer. The majority of these patients were non-Hispanic White (83.0%), diagnosed with stage I breast cancer (67.0%), and lived in a metropolitan area (84.5%) (Table 1). Most patients received their initial surgery at a regional facility (68.8%), with only 31.2% receiving their initial surgery at a community facility. Compared with those in the community surgery cohort, patients in the regional surgery cohort were less likely to be over 85 years old (10.0% vs. 12.9%, p = 0.02), more likely to be non-Hispanic White (84.2% vs. 80.5%, p = 0.009), and more likely to live in an area with high socioeconomic status (35.5% vs. 28.4%, p < 0.001).

Cancer care trajectories by surgery cohort

When we visualized the aggregate cancer care trajectories for patients in the regional and community cohorts, we observed that patients who received their initial surgical treatment at a regional facility tended to continue visiting regional facilities for adjuvant care, with similar consistency of care settings seen in the community surgery cohort (Fig. 1).

Fig. 1State distribution over time following cancer diagnosis for regional surgery cohort (left) and community surgery cohort (right)

State distribution over time following cancer diagnosis for regional surgery cohort (left) and community surgery cohort (right)

On average, patients in the regional surgery cohort spent more total days in regional states (52 days) than community states (16 days) (Fig. 2). Patients in the community surgery cohort spent more days in community states (54 days) than in regional states (16 days). We further quantified these observations by comparing facility classifications of chemotherapy and radiation treatment claims across each cohort (Tables S4 and S5). Among patients in the regional surgery cohort who received chemotherapy, 48.0% received chemotherapy at only regional facilities, 29.1% received chemotherapy at both regional and community facilities, and 22.9% received chemotherapy at only community facilities. Among the community surgery cohort, 20.9% received chemotherapy at only regional facilities, 13.2% received chemotherapy at both regional and community facilities, and 65.8% received chemotherapy at only community facilities. Likewise, among patients in the regional surgery cohort who received radiation therapy, 35.5% received radiation therapy at only regional facilities, 45.1% received radiation therapy at regional and community facilities, and 19.1% received radiation at only community settings. For patients in the community surgery cohort, 10.6% received radiation therapy at only regional facilities, 33.6% received radiation therapy at regional and community facilities, and 55.8% received radiation therapy at only community facilities.

Clusters of cancer care trajectories

Within the regional and community surgery cohorts, we identified three distinct clusters of care trajectories (Fig. 3) based on the visualized inertia curves (Figure S2). The three clusters identified within the regional surgery cohort significantly differed by age at diagnosis, cancer stage, surgical procedure, receipt of chemotherapy, and receipt of radiation (Table S6). Similar associations were observed for the three clusters in the community surgery cohort, with the addition of rurality reaching statistically significant differences across clusters. Patient race, ethnicity, and socioeconomic status were not significantly different across the clusters.

State distributions over time since cancer diagnosis for each cluster of sequences. We found three distinct clusters in the regional surgery cohort (left) and three distinct clusters in the community surgery cohort (right)

The clusters revealed complementary care trajectories across the regional and community surgery cohorts. Regional Cluster 1 and Community Cluster 1 had the highest proportions of patients treated with lumpectomy (84.7%, 82.9%) and radiation (88.5%, 82.9%), and the smallest proportions of patients older than 86 years (5.7%, 7.6%; Table S6). Regional Cluster 2 and Community Cluster 2 were characterized by fewer visits with medical and radiation oncologists. These patients were the most likely to be over 70 years old (86.4%, 81.6%) and the least likely to be treated with chemotherapy (9.1%, 7.0%) or radiation (32.3%, 22.1%). Regional Cluster 3 and Community Cluster 3 had the highest proportions of patients with stage III breast cancer (25.4%, 24.2%) and who were treated with chemotherapy (> 94.6%, > 88.9%) compared to all other clusters.

We found that the predominant cluster differed between the regional and community surgery cohorts. The largest cluster in the regional surgery cohort was Regional Cluster 2 (n = 1,343, 59%). Patients in this cluster spent an average of 233 days in the “None” state, meaning they had no cancer care visits within a week for 85% of their total care trajectory (Figure S3). The largest cluster in the community surgery cohort was Community Cluster 1 (n = 510, 49%), in which patients spent an average of 26 days in the community radiation oncology state and 189 days in the “None” state.

Associations with patient-reported experience measures

The analytic cohort of patients with non-metastatic breast cancer who completed a CAHPS survey within two years of their cancer diagnosis included 652 patients. These patients were more likely to be younger but otherwise displayed similar characteristics to the overall cohort (Table S7). The median time to survey completion was 12 months after cancer diagnosis, and survey timing within clusters did not significantly differ from the overall median (Figure S4).

We examined whether initial surgical facility classification was associated with patient-reported experience measures (Fig. 4, Table S8). We found that initial surgical treatment at a community facility was associated with higher scores for getting needed care (Est. (95%CI) = 3.5 (0.10,6.9), p = 0.043). We also observed that rural residence was associated with higher scores for getting care quickly (Est. (95%CI) = 8.8 (2.2,15), p = 0.009) and doctor communication (Est. (95%CI) = 6.3 (1.7,11), p = 0.007) as a main effect. There was no significant interaction between facility classification and rurality (p > 0.05), indicating that the effect of facility classification did not differ significantly based on rural residence.

Discussion

The aim of our study was to characterize multidisciplinary breast cancer care trajectories following surgical treatment at regional and community facilities, and to examine how surgical facility classifications were associated with patient care experiences. We found that clusters of patient care trajectories were similar following surgery at regional or community facilities, and most patients received some or all of their adjuvant chemotherapy or radiation treatment at the same facility type, particularly for patients receiving surgical treatment at a community facility. We found that patients who received their initial surgery at a community facility reported better scores for getting needed care, and the effect did not differ by rurality.

Our care trajectory analyses revealed complementary care patterns following surgery at regional vs. community facilities, yet the most common care trajectory differed based on initial surgical facility classification. Among patients who received surgical care at a regional facility, 59% were in Regional Cluster 2. We found that while two-thirds of patients in Regional Cluster 2 had a lumpectomy, only one-third received adjuvant radiation therapy. In contrast, the largest cluster among patients who received surgical treatment at community facilities was Community Cluster 1, which was characterized by receipt of lumpectomy (83% of patients) followed by adjuvant radiation (81%). These results are open to multiple interpretations. While adjuvant radiation following lumpectomy is the standard of care for many patients and reduces risk of locoregional recurrence, clinical guidelines support the omission of radiation in select low-risk cases and women over 70 years of age [40–44]. Regional facilities may be better positioned to identify such cases where women can safely omit adjuvant radiation. NCI-designation and other facility-level characteristics may influence these treatment decisions among regional facilities, though further analysis in this direction is beyond the scope of this study. On the other hand, higher rates of adjuvant radiation therapy observed in community facilities may reflect improved access to cancer care via reduced travel burden or greater colocation of care. Prior work found that patients with greater travel distance to their cancer care facility are more likely to receive a mastectomy over breast-conserving surgery, even when accounting for cancer stage [14–19]. Furthermore, other studies have found that among patients who receive breast-conserving surgery, those with lower travel burden are more likely to receive guideline-concordant adjuvant radiation therapy [10, 18, 19, 21]. Additional work is needed to better understand the different rates of radiation therapy receipt across patients receiving lumpectomy in regional and community facilities.

Our study also found that patients in the community surgery cohort reported better scores for getting needed care than patients in the regional surgery cohort, with similar associations observed for both rural- and urban-residing patients. The composite measure for getting needed care includes questions about ease of accessing specialty appointments. It is possible that referral to a regional facility introduces selection bias for more complex patients who have care needs that are more challenging to address. However, we also found that patients in the regional surgery cohort were more likely to receive adjuvant treatment across both regional and community facilities compared with patients in the community surgery cohort. This may introduce barriers to getting needed care by potentially leading to fragmented care across regional and community settings. Our data cannot discern the mechanism(s) for this finding, and we also did not observe associations between surgical facility classification and patient experiences of getting timely care, care coordination, or doctor communication. Future work integrating qualitative data could shed light on factors that contribute to barriers to getting needed care for patients receiving surgical care at a regional facility. Moreover, interventions to improve patient experiences with accessing care may especially benefit those who require complex multidisciplinary treatment following surgery at regional facilities. Patient navigators play a key role in addressing barriers to accessing needed care and coordination of care across settings, and navigation services could be targeted to patients who require multidisciplinary treatment plans or need to travel between multiple facilities [45–47].

Finally, we found that perceptions of timely care and doctor communication were higher among rural-residing patients. Interestingly, prior research has shown that rural-residing patients with cancer often report better care coordination and access to care than their urban-residing counterparts. However, higher scores for patient-reported experiences do not necessarily indicate higher quality of care. Some studies have suggested that rural residents may report higher scores due to reduced travel burden and lower expectations at their home facilities. These findings raise questions as to whether patient-reported scores for getting care quickly and getting needed care are concordant with claims-based measures of timely treatment and receipt of guideline-concordant care, and whether this concordance differs between rural- and urban-residing patients. Future work is needed to understand the mechanisms of care coordination and delivery that enhance patient experiences across all cancer care settings.

Limitations

Although SEER-CAHPS data provides a nationally representative cohort, the generalizability of our results is limited to women with breast cancer who are over age 65, reside in a SEER region, and insured by Traditional Medicare. SEER-CAHPS also provides a smaller sample size than typical SEER-Medicare studies, and our analytic cohort is further constrained by survey timing. Future analyses are needed to determine how our findings compare to those in a larger cohort of Medicare beneficiaries. Additionally, the median age at breast cancer diagnosis is 62 years, meaning over half of all cases in the United States are not represented by our sample, and younger patients may face distinct barriers to care or have different care experiences [48]. Furthermore, encrypted ZIP Codes prevent us from directly calculating travel distance, which could provide greater insight into the extent to which travel burden plays a role in patient experience measures. Lastly, many regionalization policies are based on hospital surgical volume for specific operations, but we cannot calculate these volumes because the Medicare claims available in our study only make up a small portion of all care provided in a facility. Our current methodology dichotomizes hospitals into regional and community facilities based on the general definitions for large, medium, and small hospitals provided by HCUP. Ideally, we would calculate the number of breast cancer surgeries each facility performs annually and define regional (high-volume) vs. community (low-volume) facilities based on existing policy thresholds.

Conclusions

Overall, our findings suggest that experience measures reflecting access to care among patients with non-metastatic breast cancer are enhanced when initial surgical treatment is performed at a community facility compared to a larger regional facility. Furthermore, receipt of initial surgical treatment at a regional facility is often associated with patient cancer care remaining in a regional setting and may have disparate impacts on care experiences depending on multidisciplinary treatment needs.
