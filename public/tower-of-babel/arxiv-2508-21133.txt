Impulse control in a spectrally negative Lévy model with a level-dependent intensity of bankruptcy
Dante Mata

Abstract
. We consider an optimal dividend problem with transaction costs where the surplus is modelled by a spectrally negative Lévy process in an Omega model. n this model, the surplus is allowed to spend time below the critical ruin level, but is penalised by a state-dependent intensity of bankruptcy. We show that under the spectrally negative model an optimal strategy is such that the surplus is reduced to a level c 1 c_{1} whenever they are above another level c 2 c_{2} , and that such levels are unique under the additional assumption that the Lévy measure has a log-convex tail. We describe a numerical method to compute the optimal values c 1 c_{1} and c 2 c_{2} .



1.
Introduction

In this paper, we study a version of De Finetti’s optimal dividend problem in continuous time in which a transaction cost is incurred each time a dividend payment is made. The presence of this fixed transaction cost prevents the controller from paying dividends continuously in time, and therefore only lump sum dividend payments are feasible. This class of problems is widely known in the literature as the impulse comtrol problem.

We assume that the underlying dynamics of the risk process is described by a spectrally negative Lévy process (SNLP), i.e., a process that exhibits only downwards jumps. This class of processes has been widely used in the insurance literature, as they are a generalisation of the classical Cramér-Lundberg process. Early instances of the impulse control problem can be found in the seminal works of Jeanblanc-Picqué and Shiryaev
[
4
]
and Paulsen
[
13
]
under the diffusive model; and afterwards Loeffen
[
10
]
studied the impulse control problem under the SNLP model. Recently, Moreno-Franco and Pérez
[
12
]
have studied a version of the dividend problem with transaction costs driven by an SNLP with Poissonian decision times. In these preliminary studies, it was shown that a double barrier strategy is optimal. In broad terms, a double barrier strategy requires two levels
(c_{1},c_{2})
, with
c_{1}<c_{2}
such that whenever the surplus is above the level
c_{2}
, then a lump sum is performed to bring the reserves down to level
c_{1}
. It must be remarked that each lump sum payment must cover at least the transaction cost.

These previous studies, namely
[
4
,
10
,
13
]
rely on the concept of
classical
ruin, which is defined as the first passage time of the controlled surplus below the critical level 0. Recently, this assumption has been generalised to consider more realistic models of ruin. For instance, the concept of Parisian ruin allows the surplus to remain below the critical level, however ruin is declared if the process remains negative after a certain amount of time, we refer the reader to
[
15
]
for a dividend problem under the constraint of Parisian ruin. An impulse control problem under the constraint of Parisian ruin has been studied by Czarna and Kaszubowski
[
2
]
under the
refracted
spectrally negative model, and more recently Wang et al.
[
16
]
have studied this problem under the Chapter 11 bankruptcy constraint. In this paper, we consider a more general version of Parisian ruin, the so-called
Omega
-model, where a bankruptcy rate function
\omega
is provided and if the surplus is at level
x<0
, then the rate of bankruptcy is given by
\omega(x)
. This notion of bankruptcy was defined rigorously for SNLP’s by Li and Palmowski
[
7
]
, where they developed a suite of fluctuation identities expressed in terms of the
generalised
scale functions, which are defined as the solution of an integral equation. Recently, Mata and Renaud
[
11
]
studied the classical De Finetti dividend problem under this model of ruin, and derived analytical properties of the generalised scale functions under the assumption that the Lévy measure has a log-convex tail. In the present paper we use both the fluctuation identities found in
[
8
]
and the analytical properties of the generalised scale function from
[
11
]
, to prove the optimality of a double barrier strategy and, moreover, prove the uniqueness of an optimal strategy when the tail of the Lévy measure is log-convex.

This paper is structured as follows, in Section
2
we introduce the underlying surplus process, the so-called scale functions and the class of generalised scale functions, as well as the formulation of the impulse control problem. In Section
4
we introduce the double barrier strategies, compute their performance function in term of the generalised scale functions, and propose candidate values for the optimal strategy; whereas in Section
5
we state a verification lemma and provide rigorous proofs to show that a double barrier strategy is optimal. In Section
6
we study the uniqueness of an optimal strategy under the assumption that the tail of the Lévy measure is log-convex, and we propose a method to compute the optimal barriers. Finally, in Section
7
we present numerical computations to illustrate our theoretical results.

2.
Preliminaries

2.1.
Lévy Processes

Consider a filtered probability space
\left(\Omega,\mathcal{F},\left\{\mathcal{F}_{t},t\geqslant 0\right\},\mathbb{P}\right)
such that
\mathcal{F}_{\infty}:=\bigvee_{t\geqslant 0}\mathcal{F}_{t}\subsetneq\mathcal{F}
, where
\bigvee_{t\geqslant 0}\mathcal{F}_{t}
is the smallest
\sigma
-algebra containing
\mathcal{F}_{t}
for all
t\geqslant 0
. Let
X=\left\{X_{t},t\geqslant 0\right\}
be a spectrally negative Lévy process (adapted to the filtration
\left\{\mathcal{F}_{t},t\geqslant 0\right\}
) with Laplace exponent
\theta\mapsto\psi(\theta)
given by the Lévy-Khintchine formula

(1)

\psi(\theta)=\gamma\theta+\frac{1}{2}\sigma^{2}\theta^{2}+\int^{\infty}_{0}\left(\mathrm{e}^{-\theta z}-1+\theta z\mathbf{1}_{(0,1]}(z)\right)\nu(\mathrm{d}z),

where
\gamma\in\mathbb{R}
and
\sigma\geqslant 0
, and where
\nu
is a
\sigma
-finite measure on
(0,\infty)
, called the Lévy measure of
X
, satisfying

\int^{\infty}_{0}(1\wedge x^{2})\nu(\mathrm{d}x)<\infty.

For more details on spectrally negative Lévy processes, see e.g.

[
6
]
.

In the rest of this paper, we make the following standing assumption:

Assumption 2.1
.

(1)

If the paths of
X
are of bounded variation (BV), then the Lévy measure is assumed to be absolutely continuous with respect to Lebesgue measure.

(2)

The tail of the Lévy measure, i.e., the mapping
x\mapsto\nu(x,\infty)
is assumed to be log-convex.

In what follows, we will use the following notation: the law of
X
when starting from
X_{0}=x
is denoted by
\mathbb{P}_{x}
and the corresponding expectation by
\mathbb{E}_{x}
. We write
\mathbb{P}
and
\mathbb{E}
when
x=0
.

2.2.
Optimisation problem

Let
X
be the underlying surplus process. An impulse control strategy
\pi
is represented by a non-decreasing, right-continuous and adapted stochastic process
L^{\pi}=\left\{L^{\pi}_{t},t\geqslant 0\right\}
such that
L^{\pi}_{0-}=0
, where
L^{\pi}_{t}
represents the cumulative amount of dividends paid up to time
t
under this control strategy. In addition, we assume that
L^{\pi}
is a pure-jump process, i.e.

L^{\pi}_{t}=\sum_{0\leqslant s<t}\Delta L^{\pi}_{s},\quad t\geqslant 0.

Throughout this paper, we mean
\Delta L^{\pi}_{s}=L^{\pi}_{s+}-L^{\pi}_{s}
.

For a given strategy
\pi
, the corresponding controlled process
U^{\pi}=\left\{U^{\pi}_{t},t\geqslant 0\right\}
is defined by
U^{\pi}_{t}=X_{t}-L^{\pi}_{t}
. A strategy is said to be admissible if
U^{\pi}_{t}-\Delta L^{\pi}_{t}\geqslant 0
for all
t
. Let
\Pi
be the set of admissible strategies.

In order to define the bankruptcy time of interest, let us fix a
bankruptcy rate function

\omega\colon\mathbb{R}\to[0,\infty)
(see Definition
2.1
below), that is roughly speaking a non-negative and non-increasing function having support in
(-\infty,0]
. We define the following termination time associated with
\omega
: for
\pi\in\Pi
, set

(2)

\sigma^{\pi}_{\omega}=\inf\left\{t>0\colon\int_{0}^{t}\omega(U^{\pi}_{s})\mathrm{d}s>\mathbf{e}_{1}\right\},

where
\mathbf{e}_{1}
is an exponential random variable with unit mean, independent of the surplus.

Finally, fix a time-preference rate
q>0
and a cost per transaction
\beta>0
. The performance function associated to an admissible strategy
\pi\in\Pi
is defined by

v_{\pi}(x)=\mathbb{E}_{x}\left[\int_{0}^{\sigma^{\pi}_{\omega}}\mathrm{e}^{-qt}\mathrm{d}{{\left(L^{\pi}_{t}-\sum_{0\leqslant s<t}\beta\mathbb{I}_{{{\left\{\Delta L^{\pi}_{s}>0\right\}}}}\right)}}\right],\quad x\in\mathbb{R}.

Note that due to the pure jump nature of the admissible controls, we wan rewrite the performance function as

v_{\pi}(x)=\mathbb{E}_{x}{{\left[\sum_{0\leqslant t<\sigma^{\pi}_{\omega}}\mathrm{e}^{-qt}{{\left(\Delta L^{\pi}_{t}-\beta\mathbb{I}_{{{\left\{\Delta L^{\pi}_{t}>0\right\}}}}\right)}}\right]}}.

At this point, we find relevant to present the following alternate representation of the performance function of an admissible strategy, which aloows us to interpret our problem as an infinite-horizon dividend problem where dividend payments are penalised by a level-dependent discounting. We omit the proof, as it is similar to that of Lemma 3.1 in
[
11
]
.

Lemma 2.1
.

For any
\pi\in\Pi
we have

v_{\pi}(x)=\mathbb{E}_{x}{{\left[\sum_{0\leqslant t<\infty}\mathrm{e}^{-qt}{{\left(\Delta L^{\pi}_{t}-\beta\mathbb{I}_{{{\left\{\Delta L^{\pi}_{t}>0\right\}}}}\right)}}\right]}},\quad x\in\mathbb{R}.

Our goal is to compute

(3)

v_{\ast}(x)=\sup_{\pi\in\Pi}v_{\pi}(x),\quad x\in\mathbb{R},

the value function of this control problem by finding an optimal strategy
\pi_{\ast}\in\Pi
, i.e., an admissible strategy such that

v_{\pi_{\ast}}(x)=v_{\ast}(x),\quad x\in\mathbb{R}.

2.3.
Bankruptcy rate function

We provide a proper definition of a
bankruptcy rate function
, a name that has been used already in
[
3
]
.

Definition 2.1
.

We say that
\omega\colon\mathbb{R}\to[0,\infty)
is a discounting intensity if:

(1)

it is non-increasing;

(2)

it is ultimately constant, i.e., there exist
a\in(-\infty,0]
and
\phi>0
for which
\omega(x)=\phi
for all
x\in(-\infty,a)
, and there exists
\rho\in[0,\omega(0-)]
such that
\omega(x)=\rho
for all
x\in[0,\infty)
;

(3)

it is piecewise continuous, i.e., there exists a finite partition
\{a_{1},a_{2},\dots,a_{n},a_{n+1}\}
of
[a,0]
given by
a=a_{1}<a_{2}<\cdots<a_{n}\leqslant a_{n+1}=0
and such that:

(a)

\omega
is continuous on each sub-interval
(a_{k},a_{k+1})
for
k\in\{1,2,\cdots,n\}
;

(b)

if
n\geqslant 2
, then
\omega(a_{k}-)>\omega(a_{k}+)
for each
k\in\{2,\cdots,n\}
.

Remark 2.1
.

In our model, we say that
\omega
is a bankruptcy rate function if it is a discounting intensity with
\rho=0
.

As opposed to other elements in the partition, a discounting intensity might or might not have jumps at
a
and
0
. More precisely, we have
\phi=\omega(a-)\geqslant\omega(a+)
and
\omega(0-)\geqslant\rho=\omega(0)
. Thus, if
\omega
is continuous on
(-\infty,0)
, then
n=1
and
a\leqslant 0
and thus the only possible discontinuity point is now at
0
. This is the case for example if
\omega(x)=\phi\mathbf{1}_{(-\infty,0)}(x)+\rho\mathbf{1}_{[0,\infty)}(x)
. However, it is possible for
\omega
to be continuous on
\mathbb{R}
. For example,

\omega(x)=\phi\mathbf{1}_{(-\infty,-\phi)}(x)-x\mathbf{1}_{[-\phi,0)}(x)

is a continuous bankruptcy rate function.

Remark 2.2
.

A piecewise constant bankruptcy rate function yields a termination time that is already significantly more general than a Parisian ruin time with exponential delays. Indeed, Parisian ruin with exponential rate
\phi>0
corresponds to the bankruptcy rate function
\omega(x)=\phi\mathbf{1}_{(-\infty,0)}(x)
.

3.
Scale functions

For each
q\geqslant 0
, there exists a function
W_{q}
known as the
scale function
, such that
W_{q}(x)=0
for all
x\in(-\infty,0)
, and it is strictly increasing on
(0,\infty)
. In addition, it is characterised by its Laplace transform in the following way

(4)

\int_{0}^{\infty}\mathrm{e}^{-\theta x}W_{q}(x)\mathrm{d}x=\frac{1}{\psi(\theta)-q},\quad\text{for all $\theta>\Phi(q),$}

where
\Phi(q)\sup\left\{\lambda\geqslant 0\colon\psi(\lambda)=p\right\}
. We refer the reader to
[
5
]
for a detailed analysis on the theory of scale functions.

As a consequence of item 1 from Assumption
2.1
, we have that the scale function
W_{q}
is continuously differentiable on
(0,\infty)
, for any
q\geqslant 0
; see, e.g.,
[
5
]
.

Recall that
q
is the discount rate. Since
[
1
,
9
]
, it has been widely known that the
q
-scale function
W_{q}
is an essential object in the study of the optimal dividends problem with classical ruin. When exponential Parisian ruin is considered, as in
[
15
]
, a second family of scale functions is needed. If Parisian ruin occurs at rate
\phi>0
, i.e., if
\omega(x)=\phi\mathbf{1}_{(-\infty,0)}(x)
, then
Z_{q}(\cdot;\Phi(\phi+q))
is the scale function of interest. In general, for fixed
0<q<s
, define, for each
x\in\mathbb{R}
,

(5)

\displaystyle Z_{q}(x;\Phi(s))

\displaystyle=\mathrm{e}^{\Phi(s)x}\left(1-(s-q)\int_{0}^{x}\mathrm{e}^{-\Phi(s)y}W_{q}(y)\mathrm{d}y\right)

\displaystyle=(s-q)\int_{0}^{\infty}\mathrm{e}^{-\Phi(s)y}W_{q}(x+y)\mathrm{d}y,

where for
x\leqslant 0
, we have
Z_{q}(x;\Phi(s))=\mathrm{e}^{\Phi(s)x}
. In particular, we have
Z_{q}(x;\Phi(\phi+q))=\phi\int_{0}^{\infty}\mathrm{e}^{-\Phi(\phi+q)y}W_{q}(x+y)\mathrm{d}y
.

3.1.
Omega scale functions

For the control problem under study, another
scale function
, which is a generalization of both
W_{q}(\cdot)
and
Z_{q}(\cdot;\Phi(\phi+q))
, will be needed. A theory of scale functions based on a bankruptcy rate function
\omega
(also called a
killing intensity
) has been developed in
[
7
]
. In that paper, the
\omega
-scale function
\mathcal{H}^{\omega}\colon\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R}
is defined as the solution of the following functional equation:

(6)

\mathcal{H}^{\omega}(x)=\mathrm{e}^{\Phi(\phi)(x-a)}+\int_{a}^{x}W_{\phi}(x-y)(\omega(y)-\phi)\mathcal{H}^{\omega}(y)\mathrm{d}y,\quad x\in\mathbb{R}.

See Lemma 2.1 and Section 2.4 in
[
7
]
for more details.

In the above definition and in what follows, it is assumed that, for any integrable function
f
, if
a\geqslant b
then
\int_{a}^{b}f(x)\mathrm{d}x=0
. In general, by
\int_{a}^{b}
we mean
\int_{[a,b)}
.

Remark 3.1
.

Note that (
6
) does not appear verbatim in
[
7
]
. We have performed a translation in Equations (2.22)-(2.23) of
[
7
]
to obtain (
6
). This modification is needed to be in agreement with our definition of a bankruptcy rate function.

Below we list some key results regarding the analytic properties of the
\omega
-scale function
\mathcal{H}^{\omega}
. We refer the reader to
[
11
]
for detailed proofs.

Theorem 3.1

(Theorem 2.1 in
[
11
]
)
.

Under Assumption
2.1
, the
\omega
-scale function
\mathcal{H}^{\omega}
has the following differentiability properties:

(1)

If
X
has paths of UBV, then
\mathcal{H}^{\omega}
is continuously differentiable on
\mathbb{R}
. In addition, if
\sigma>0
, then
\mathcal{H}^{\omega}
is twice continuously differentiable on
\mathbb{R}\setminus\{a_{1},a_{2},\cdots,a_{n}\}
.

(2)

If
X
has paths of BV, then
\mathcal{H}^{\omega}
is:

(a)

continuously differentiable on
\mathbb{R}\setminus\{a_{1},a_{2},\dots,a_{n}\}
if
\omega(0-)=0
;

(b)

continuously differentiable on
\mathbb{R}\setminus\{a_{1},a_{2},\dots,a_{n},a_{n+1}\}
if
\omega(0-)>0
.

Proposition 3.1

(Proposition 2.1 in
[
11
]
)
.

If the function
x\mapsto\nu(x,\infty)
is log-convex on
(0,\infty)
, then
\mathcal{H}^{\omega\prime}
is log-convex on
(0,\infty)
. In particular, it is also convex on
(0,\infty)
.

4.
Double barrier strategies

An important type of strategy in impulse control models is the so-called
(c_{1},c_{2})
strategy. This strategy is defined such that whenever the surplus process is above a given level
c_{2}
, then a lump sum payment is made which brings the surplus to level
c_{1}
.

Formally speaking, the
(c_{1},c_{2})
strategy is defined as follows. Given two constants
c_{1},c_{2}
such that
c_{2}>\beta+c_{1}
and
c_{1}\geqslant 0
, we define the sequence of stopping times
{{\left\{\tau^{c_{1},c_{2}}_{k}:k\geqslant 1\right\}}}
such that

\tau^{c_{1},c_{2}}_{k}:=\inf{{\left\{t\geqslant 0:X_{t}>[(X_{0}\vee c_{2})+(c_{2}-c_{1})(k-1)]\right\}}},\quad k\geqslant 1.

The dividend process
(L^{c_{1},c_{2}}_{t})_{t\geqslant 0}
is defined as

L^{c_{1},c_{2}}_{t}=\mathbb{I}_{{{\left\{\tau^{c_{1},c_{2}}_{1}<t\right\}}}}([X_{0}\vee c_{2}]-c_{1})+\sum_{k=2}^{\infty}\mathbb{I}_{{{\left\{\tau^{c_{1},c_{2}}_{k}<t\right\}}}}(c_{2}-c_{1}),\quad t\geqslant 0.

Consequently, the controlled process is written as
U^{c_{1},c_{2}}_{t}:=X_{t}-L^{c_{1},c_{2}}_{t}
for all
t\geqslant 0
. Note that the sequence of stopping times can be expressed in terms of
U^{c_{1},c_{2}}
as
\tau^{c_{1},c_{2}}_{1}=\inf{{\left\{t\geqslant 0:U^{c_{1},c_{2}}_{t}>c_{2}\right\}}}
and
\tau^{c_{1},c_{2}}_{k}=\inf{{\left\{t\geqslant\tau^{c_{1},c_{2}}_{k-1}:U^{c_{1},c_{2}}_{t}>c_{2}\right\}}}
for
k\geqslant 2
.

Our goal is to determine the existence of a pair
(c_{1}^{\ast},c_{2}^{\ast})
such that the associated strategy attains the supremum in (
3
), i.e., that it is an optimal strategy. Moreover, under Assumption
2.1
that the tail of the Lévy measure is log-convex, we will find conditions for uniqueness and also conditions to determine whether
c_{1}^{\ast}>0
or
c_{1}^{\ast}=0
.

4.1.
Performance function of a
(c_{1},c_{2})
strategy

Proposition 4.1
.

Fix
c_{2}>\beta+c_{1}
with
c_{1}\geqslant 0
. Let
\omega_{q}:\mathbb{R}\to[0,\infty)
be given by
\omega_{q}(x)=q+\omega(x)
, then we have

v_{c_{1},c_{2}}(x)=\begin{cases}{{\left(\frac{c_{2}-c_{1}-\beta}{\mathcal{H}^{\omega_{q}}(c_{2})-\mathcal{H}^{\omega_{q}}(c_{1})}\right)}}\mathcal{H}^{\omega_{q}}(x)&\text{if $x\in(-\infty,c_{2}]$,}\\
x-c_{1}-\beta+{{\left(\frac{c_{2}-c_{1}-\beta}{\mathcal{H}^{\omega_{q}}(c_{2})-\mathcal{H}^{\omega_{q}}(c_{1})}\right)}}\mathcal{H}^{\omega_{q}}(c_{1})&\text{if $x\in[c_{2},\infty)$.}\end{cases}

Proof.

Consider the admissible
(c_{1},c_{2})
strategy, and denote its respective controlled process by
(U^{c_{1},c_{2}}_{t})_{t\geqslant 0}
. Let
x\in(-\infty,c_{2})
, then by the strong Markov property we get

v_{c_{1},c_{2}}(x)=\mathbb{E}_{x}{{\left[e^{-q\tau_{c_{2}}^{+}};\tau_{c_{2}}^{+}<\sigma_{\omega}\right]}}v_{c_{1},c_{2}}(c_{2}),

where
\tau_{c_{2}}^{+}:=\inf{{\left\{t\geqslant 0:U^{c_{1},c_{2}}_{t}>c_{2}\right\}}}
. Note that we have the equality of events
{{\left\{\tau_{c_{2}}^{+}<\sigma_{\omega}\right\}}}={{\left\{\int_{0}^{\tau_{c_{2}}^{+}}\omega(U^{c_{1},c_{2}}_{t})\mathrm{d}t<\mathbf{e}_{1}\right\}}}
. Integrating w.r.t. the law of
\mathbf{e}_{1}
we get

\displaystyle v_{c_{1},c_{2}}(x)

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}_{x}{{\left[\exp{{\left\{-\int_{0}^{\tau_{c_{2}}^{+}}\omega_{q}(U^{c_{1},c_{2}}_{t})\mathrm{d}t\right\}}};\tau_{c_{2}}^{+}<\infty\right]}}v_{c_{1},c_{2}}(c_{2})

\displaystyle=\frac{\mathcal{H}^{\omega_{q}}(x)}{\mathcal{H}^{\omega_{q}}(c_{2})}v_{c_{1},c_{2}}(c_{2}),

where in the second equality we have used Theorem 2.5 from
[
7
]
.

On the other hand, for
x\in[c_{2},\infty)
by the definition of the
(c_{1},c_{2})
strategy there is a lump sum payment of size
x-c_{1}
at time 0 to bring the reserves to level
c_{1}
, then we have

\displaystyle v_{c_{1},c_{2}}(x)

\displaystyle=x-c_{1}-\beta+v_{c_{1},c_{2}}(c_{1})

\displaystyle=x-c_{1}-\beta+\frac{\mathcal{H}^{\omega_{q}}(c_{1})}{\mathcal{H}^{\omega_{q}}(c_{2})}v_{c_{1},c_{2}}(c_{2}).

In particular, for
x=c_{2}
we have

v_{c_{1},c_{2}}(c_{2})=c_{2}-c_{1}-\beta+\frac{\mathcal{H}^{\omega_{q}}(c_{1})}{\mathcal{H}^{\omega_{q}}(c_{2})}v_{c_{1},c_{2}}(c_{2}).

Solving for
v_{c_{1},c_{2}}(c_{2})
yields the desired expression.
∎

4.2.
A candidate optimal strategy

Equipped with the representation of
v_{c_{1},c_{2}}
in terms of the generalised scale function, our next goal is to find values
(c^{\ast}_{1},c^{\ast}_{2})
that will give the
best
strategy, i.e., the one that maximises across all admissible impulse policies and attains the supremum at (
3
). Given the expression of the performance function, our initial guess will be to find the values
(c^{\ast}_{1},c^{\ast}_{2})
that minimise

g(c_{1},c_{2})=\frac{\mathcal{H}^{\omega_{q}}(c_{2})-\mathcal{H}^{\omega_{q}}(c_{1})}{c_{2}-c_{1}-\beta},

where the domain of
g
is given by
\text{dom}(g)={{\left\{(c_{1},c_{2}):c_{1}\geqslant 0,\,c_{2}>\beta+c_{1}\right\}}}
.

Let
C^{\ast}
be the set of minimisers of
g
, i.e.

C^{\ast}={{\left\{(c_{1}^{\ast},c_{2}^{\ast})\in\text{dom}(g):g(c_{1}^{\ast},c_{2}^{\ast})=\inf_{(c_{1},c_{2})\in\text{dom}(g)}g(c_{1},c_{2})\right\}}}.

Proposition 4.2
.

The set
C^{\ast}
is non-empty and for each
(c_{1}^{\ast},c_{2}^{\ast})\in C^{\ast}
we have

\mathcal{H}^{\omega_{q}\prime}(c_{2}^{\ast})=\frac{\mathcal{H}^{\omega_{q}}(c_{2}^{\ast})-\mathcal{H}^{\omega_{q}}(c_{1}^{\ast})}{c_{2}^{\ast}-c_{1}^{\ast}-\beta}.

In addition, one of the following holds:

(1)

\mathcal{H}^{\omega_{q}\prime}(c_{2}^{\ast})=\mathcal{H}^{\omega_{q}\prime}(c_{1}^{\ast})
,

(2)

c_{1}^{\ast}=0
.

Proof.

Let
(c_{1},c_{2})\in\text{dom}(g)
, then by the mean value theorem for some
\xi\in[c_{1},c_{2}]
we have

\displaystyle g(c_{1},c_{2})

\displaystyle={{\left(\frac{c_{2}-c_{1}}{c_{2}-c_{1}-\beta}\right)}}\mathcal{H}^{\omega_{q}\prime}(\xi)

\displaystyle\geqslant{{\left(\frac{c_{2}-c_{1}}{c_{2}-c_{1}-\beta}\right)}}\min_{\xi\in[c_{1},c_{2}]}\mathcal{H}^{\omega_{q}\prime}(\xi)

\displaystyle>\min_{\xi\in[c_{1},c_{2}]}\mathcal{H}^{\omega_{q}\prime}(\xi).

From equation (17) in
[
11
]
we have that

\mathcal{H}^{\omega_{q}\prime}(\xi)=Z_{q}^{\prime}(\xi-a;\Phi(\phi))+\int_{a}^{0}W_{q}^{\prime}(\xi-y)\omega(y)\mathcal{H}^{\omega_{q}}(y)\mathrm{d}y.

It follows easily that
\mathcal{H}^{\omega_{q}\prime}(\xi)\to+\infty
as
\xi\to\infty
, which implies that the minimum of
g
is not attained when
c_{1}\to\infty
. This further implies that there exists
C_{1}>0
such that

\inf_{\text{dom}(g)}g(c_{1},c_{2})=\inf_{\text{dom}(g),c_{1}\leqslant C_{1}}g(c_{1},c_{2}).

Similarly, we show that the minimum is not attained when
c_{2}\to\infty
.

\displaystyle\inf_{c_{1}\in[0,C_{1}]}g(c_{1},c_{2})

\displaystyle=\inf_{c_{1}\in[0,C_{1}]}\frac{\mathcal{H}^{\omega_{q}}(c_{2})}{c_{2}-c_{1}-\beta}-\frac{\mathcal{H}^{\omega_{q}}(c_{1})}{c_{2}-c_{1}-\beta}

\displaystyle\geqslant\frac{\mathcal{H}^{\omega_{q}}(c_{2})}{c_{2}-\beta}-\frac{\mathcal{H}^{\omega_{q}}(C_{1})}{c_{2}-C_{1}-\beta},

it follows that the right-hand side of the inequality goes to
+\infty
as
c_{2}\to\infty
. Hence, the minimum of
g
is not attained when
c_{2}\to\infty
.

Finally, we show that the minimum is not attained when
(c_{1},c_{2})
converges to the line
c_{2}=c_{1}+\beta
. By an application of the mean value theorem we have

\displaystyle g(c_{1},c_{2})

\displaystyle\geqslant{{\left(\frac{c_{2}-c_{1}}{c_{2}-c_{1}-\beta}\right)}}\min_{\xi\in[0,C_{1}]}\mathcal{H}^{\omega_{q}\prime}(\xi)

\displaystyle\geqslant{{\left(\frac{\beta}{c_{2}-c_{1}-\beta}\right)}}\min_{\xi\in[0,C_{1}]}\mathcal{H}^{\omega_{q}\prime}(\xi),

where
\min_{\xi\in[0,C_{1}]}\mathcal{H}^{\omega_{q}\prime}(\xi)>0
. It follows that the right-hand side goes to
+\infty
as
(c_{1},c_{2})
converge to the line
c_{2}=c_{1}+\beta
, hence the minimum is not attained.

The previous analysis allows us to conclude that the minimiser
(c_{1}^{\ast},c_{2}^{\ast})
is either an interior point of
\text{dom}(g)
or is such that
c_{1}^{\ast}=0
.

In case that
(c_{1}^{\ast},c_{2}^{\ast})
is an interior point, then
g
is differentiable and satisfies

\frac{\partial g}{\partial c_{1}}(c_{1}^{\ast},c_{2}^{\ast})=0,\quad\text{and,}\quad\frac{\partial g}{\partial c_{2}}(c_{1}^{\ast},c_{2}^{\ast})=0.

After some algebraic manipulations, we observe that

\mathcal{H}^{\omega_{q}\prime}(c_{2}^{\ast})=\frac{\mathcal{H}^{\omega_{q}}(c_{2}^{\ast})-\mathcal{H}^{\omega_{q}}(c_{1}^{\ast})}{c_{2}^{\ast}-c_{1}^{\ast}-\beta},

hence item (1) follows.

In the case where
c_{1}^{\ast}=0
, then
c_{2}^{\ast}
minimises the function
g_{0}(c_{2}):=g(0,c_{2})
. It follows that
g_{0}^{\prime}(c_{2}^{\ast})=0
, hence we also have
\mathcal{H}^{\omega_{q}\prime}(c_{2}^{\ast})=\frac{\mathcal{H}^{\omega_{q}}(c_{2}^{\ast})-\mathcal{H}^{\omega_{q}}(0)}{c_{2}^{\ast}-\beta}
.
∎

Corollary 4.1
.

For each
(c_{1}^{\ast},c_{2}^{\ast})\in C^{\ast}
we have

v_{c_{1}^{\ast},c_{2}^{\ast}}(x)=\begin{cases}\frac{\mathcal{H}^{\omega_{q}}(x)}{\mathcal{H}^{\omega_{q}\prime}(c_{2}^{\ast})}&\text{if $x\in(-\infty,c_{2}^{\ast}]$,}\\
x-c_{2}^{\ast}+\frac{\mathcal{H}^{\omega_{q}}(c_{2}^{\ast})}{\mathcal{H}^{\omega_{q}\prime}(c_{2}^{\ast})}&\text{if $x\in[c_{2}^{\ast},\infty)$.}\end{cases}

Note that this representation coincides with the performance function of a barrier strategy at level
c_{2}^{\ast}
in De Finetti’s problem under state dependent ruin, as has been studied in
[
11
]
. This representation will be useful in the proofs of optimality.

5.
Verification of Optimality

We prove that the performance function of a
(c_{1}^{\ast},c_{2}^{\ast})
strategy is optimal for any
(c_{1}^{\ast},c_{2}^{\ast})\in C^{\ast}
.

To prove optimality, we need the following verification lemma which states the necessary conditions for a strategy to be optimal.

Lemma 5.1

(Verification Lemma)
.

Suppose that
\hat{\pi}
is an admissible impulse control strategy such that
v_{\hat{\pi}}
is sufficiently smooth on
\mathbb{R}
, and satisfies

(7)

\displaystyle(\Gamma-\omega_{q}(\cdot))v_{\hat{\pi}}(x)

\displaystyle\leqslant 0,

\displaystyle\text{for almost all }x\in\mathbb{R};

(8)

\displaystyle v_{\hat{\pi}}(x)-v_{\hat{\pi}}(y)

\displaystyle\geqslant x-y-\beta,

\displaystyle x\geqslant y.

Then
v_{\hat{\pi}}=v_{\ast}
for almost every
x\in\mathbb{R}
and hence
\hat{\pi}
is an optimal strategy.

Proof.

Throughout this proof we write
h=v_{\hat{\pi}}
. Let
\pi
be an admissible strategy, and
U
its associated controlled process. Since
h
is sufficiently smooth, an application of Meyer-Itô’s formula (see Theorem 70 in
[
14
]
) to
Y_{t}h(U_{t})
, with
Y_{t}:=\exp{{\left\{\int_{0}^{t}\omega_{q}(U_{s})\mathrm{d}s\right\}}}
, gives:

\displaystyle Y_{t}h(U_{t})-h(U_{0})

\displaystyle=\int_{0+}^{t}Y_{s}\left(\Gamma h(U_{s})-(\omega(U_{s})+q)h(U_{s})\right)\mathrm{d}s-\int_{0}^{t}Y_{s}h^{\prime}(U_{s})\mathrm{d}L^{c}_{s}

\displaystyle\qquad-\sum_{0\leqslant s<t}Y_{s}\left\{h(X_{s}-L_{s-})-h(X_{s}-L_{s-}-\Delta L_{s})\right\}+\int_{0+}^{t}Y_{s}\mathrm{d}M_{s},

where
t\mapsto\int_{0+}^{t}Y_{s}\mathrm{d}M_{s}
is a local martingale.

Recall that admissible strategies are pure jump processes and
\Delta L_{t}\geqslant\beta
for all
t\geqslant 0
, then applying inequality (
8
), we have

\displaystyle Y_{t}h(U_{t})-h(U_{0})

\displaystyle\leqslant\int_{0+}^{t}Y_{s}\left(\Gamma h(U_{s})-(\omega(U_{s})+q)h(U_{s})\right)\mathrm{d}s-\sum_{0\leqslant s<t}Y_{s}(\Delta L_{s}-\beta)+\int_{0+}^{t}Y_{s}\mathrm{d}M_{s}.

In addition, since for almost all
x\in\mathbb{R}\setminus\{a_{1},\dots,a_{n},0\}
we have the variational inequality (
7
) then we can further write

Y_{t}h(U_{t})-h(U_{0})\leqslant-\sum_{0\leqslant s<t}Y_{s}(\Delta L_{s}-\beta)+\int_{0+}^{t}Y_{s}\mathrm{d}M_{s}.

Now, consider
(T_{n})_{n\geqslant 1}
a localising sequence. Applying the last inequality at
t=T_{n}
and taking expectations, we can write

h(x)\geqslant\mathbb{E}_{x}{{\left[Y_{T_{n}}h(U_{T_{n}})\right]}}+\mathbb{E}_{x}{{\left[\sum_{0\leqslant s<T_{n}}Y_{s}(\Delta L_{s}-\beta)\right]}}.

Finally, by taking the limit as
n\to\infty
, we obtain

h(x)\geqslant\mathbb{E}_{x}{{\left[\sum_{0\leqslant s<\infty}Y_{s}(\Delta L_{s}-\beta)\right]}}=v_{\pi}(x),

where in the last equality we have used Lemma
2.1
. Since
\pi
is arbitrary, the result follows.
∎

Lemma 5.2
.

Let
(c_{1}^{\ast},c_{2}^{\ast})\in C^{\ast}
. Then for
x\geqslant y\geqslant 0
,

(9)

v_{c_{1}^{\ast},c_{2}^{\ast}}(x)-v_{c_{1}^{\ast},c_{2}^{\ast}}(y)\geqslant x-y-\beta.

Proof.

Firstly, we note that
v_{c_{1}^{\ast},c_{2}^{\ast}}
is an increasing function, so throughout this proof we can assume without loss of generality that
x-y>\beta
.

Now, we consider the case when
x\geqslant y\geqslant c_{2}^{\ast}
, then it follows easily that
v_{c_{1}^{\ast},c_{2}^{\ast}}(x)-v_{c_{1}^{\ast},c_{2}^{\ast}}(y)=x-y\geqslant x-y-\beta
. Second, in the case where
c_{2}^{\ast}\geqslant x\geqslant y
, using Corollary
4.1
we have

v_{c_{1}^{\ast},c_{2}^{\ast}}(x)-v_{c_{1}^{\ast},c_{2}^{\ast}}(y)=\frac{\mathcal{H}^{\omega_{q}}(x)-\mathcal{H}^{\omega_{q}}(y)}{\mathcal{H}^{\omega_{q}\prime}(c_{2}^{\ast})}.

Recall that
(c_{1}^{\ast},c_{2}^{\ast})\in C^{\ast}
, so that we have the equality

\displaystyle\mathcal{H}^{\omega_{q}\prime}(c_{2}^{\ast})

\displaystyle=\frac{\mathcal{H}^{\omega_{q}}(c_{2}^{\ast})-\mathcal{H}^{\omega_{q}}(c_{1}^{\ast})}{c_{2}^{\ast}-c_{1}^{\ast}-\beta}

\displaystyle\leqslant\frac{\mathcal{H}^{\omega_{q}}(x)-\mathcal{H}^{\omega_{q}}(y)}{x-y-\beta},

where the inequality follows simply from the fact that
(c_{1}^{\ast},c_{2}^{\ast})\in C^{\ast}
. Plugging this back into
v_{c_{1}^{\ast},c_{2}^{\ast}}(x)-v_{c_{1}^{\ast},c_{2}^{\ast}}(y)
gives

v_{c_{1}^{\ast},c_{2}^{\ast}}(x)-v_{c_{1}^{\ast},c_{2}^{\ast}}(y)\geqslant x-y-\beta.

Finally, if
x\geqslant c_{2}^{\ast}\geqslant y
, then by Corollary
4.1

\displaystyle v_{c_{1}^{\ast},c_{2}^{\ast}}(x)-v_{c_{1}^{\ast},c_{2}^{\ast}}(y)

\displaystyle=x-c_{2}^{\ast}+\frac{\mathcal{H}^{\omega_{q}}(c_{2}^{\ast})}{\mathcal{H}^{\omega_{q}\prime}(c_{2}^{\ast})}-\frac{\mathcal{H}^{\omega_{q}}(y)}{\mathcal{H}^{\omega_{q}\prime}(c_{2}^{\ast})}

\displaystyle\geqslant x-c_{2}^{\ast}+c_{2}^{\ast}-y-\beta

\displaystyle=x-y-\beta,

where in the inequality we have used once again that
(c_{1}^{\ast},c_{2}^{\ast})\in C^{\ast}
.
∎

We are ready to state the main result of this section.

Theorem 5.1
.

Suppose that there exists
(c_{1}^{\ast},c_{2}^{\ast})\in C^{\ast}
such that

\mathcal{H}^{\omega_{q}\prime}(a)\leqslant\mathcal{H}^{\omega_{q}\prime}(b),\quad\text{for all }c_{2}^{\ast}\leqslant a\leqslant b.

Then the strategy
(c_{1}^{\ast},c_{2}^{\ast})
is an optimal strategy for the impulse control problem.

Proof.

Let
(c_{1}^{\ast},c_{2}^{\ast})\in C^{\ast}
that satisfies the conditions of the statement. Firstly, thanks to Lemma
5.2
we verify that
(c_{1}^{\ast},c_{2}^{\ast})
satisfies the variational inequality (
8
).

Regarding the variational inequality (
7
), in the proof of Lemma 4.1 in
[
11
]
it was shown that

(\Gamma-\omega_{q}(\cdot))\mathcal{H}^{\omega_{q}}(x)=0,\quad x\in\mathbb{R}\setminus{{\left\{a_{1},\cdots,a_{n},a_{n+1}\right\}}},

then it follows that

(\Gamma-\omega_{q}(\cdot))v_{c_{1}^{\ast},c_{2}^{\ast}}(x)=0,\quad x\in(-\infty,c_{2}^{\ast})\setminus{{\left\{a_{1},\cdots,a_{n},a_{n+1}\right\}}}.

On the other hand, using that
\mathcal{H}^{\omega_{q}\prime}(c_{2}^{\ast})\leqslant\mathcal{H}^{\omega_{q}\prime}(a)\leqslant\mathcal{H}^{\omega_{q}\prime}(b)
, then we can proceed verbatim as in the proof of Theorem 2 in
[
9
]
to deduce

(\Gamma-\omega_{q}(\cdot))v_{c_{1}^{\ast},c_{2}^{\ast}}(x)\leqslant 0,\quad x\in(c_{2}^{\ast},\infty).

We conclude that
(c_{1}^{\ast},c_{2}^{\ast})
is an optimal strategy.
∎

6.
Optimal strategies when the tail of the Lévy measure is log-convex

The results presented in the preceding sections hold for a general class of SNLP’s, however, under our standing assumption that the tail of Lévy measure is log-convex, we are able to further characterise the optimal barrier strategy. In this section, we will prove that
C^{\ast}
consists of one element, and we will provide conditions to determine whether
c_{1}^{\ast}=0
or
c_{1}^{\ast}>0
, as well as the characterisation of
c^{\ast}_{2}
.

Under our standing Assumption
2.1
, we have that
W_{q}
is
C^{1}
on
(0,\infty)
and moreover, Proposition 2.1 in
[
11
]
gives that
x\mapsto\mathcal{H}^{\omega_{q}\prime}(x)
is log-convex on
(0,\infty)
, hence it is also convex. In addition, the log-convexity of
\mathcal{H}^{\omega_{q}\prime}
implies that there exists
a^{\ast}\in[0,\infty)
such that

\mathcal{H}^{\omega_{q}\prime}(a^{\ast})\leqslant\mathcal{H}^{\omega_{q}\prime}(x),\quad\text{for all }x\geqslant 0.

It also follows that
\mathcal{H}^{\omega_{q}\prime}
is strictly decreasing on
(0,a^{\ast})
and strictly increasing on
(a^{\ast},\infty)
. Throughout this section we will use the following function
\zeta:(0,a^{\ast})\to(a^{\ast},\infty)
, which is implicitly defined as the one that satisfies
\mathcal{H}^{\omega_{q}\prime}(x)=\mathcal{H}^{\omega_{q}\prime}(\zeta(x))
for all
x\in(0,a^{\ast})
.

Remark 6.1

(Properties of
\zeta
)
.

By its definition,
\zeta
is strictly decreasing. In addition, using that
\mathcal{H}^{\omega_{q}\prime}
is convex on
(0,\infty)
, hence left- and right-differentiable, we obtain for
a\in(0,a^{\ast})

\displaystyle\lim_{x\downarrow a}\frac{\zeta(x)-\zeta(a)}{x-a}

\displaystyle=\lim_{x\downarrow a}\frac{(\zeta(x)-\zeta(a))(\mathcal{H}^{\omega_{q}\prime}(x)-\mathcal{H}^{\omega_{q}\prime}(a))}{(x-a)(\mathcal{H}^{\omega_{q}\prime}(\zeta(x))-\mathcal{H}^{\omega_{q}\prime}(\zeta(a)))}

\displaystyle={{\left(\lim_{y\uparrow\zeta(a)}\frac{y-\zeta(a)}{\mathcal{H}^{\omega_{q}\prime}(y)-\mathcal{H}^{\omega_{q}\prime}(\zeta(a))}\right)}}{{\left(\lim_{x\downarrow a}\frac{\mathcal{H}^{\omega_{q}\prime}(x)-\mathcal{H}^{\omega_{q}\prime}(a)}{x-a}\right)}}

\displaystyle=\frac{\mathcal{H}^{\omega_{q}\prime\prime}_{+}(a)}{\mathcal{H}^{\omega_{q}\prime\prime}_{-}(\zeta(a))},

hence
\zeta
is right-differentiable. By a similar computation, we also obtain that
\zeta
is left-differentiable.

Due to convexity of
\mathcal{H}^{\omega_{q}\prime}
and the definition of
a^{\ast}
, we have that
\mathcal{H}^{\omega_{q}\prime\prime}_{+}(x)<0
for all
x\in(0,a^{\ast})
, and that
\mathcal{H}^{\omega_{q}\prime\prime}_{-}(x)>0
for
x\in(a^{\ast},\infty)
. It then follows that
\zeta^{\prime}_{+}(x)<0
for all
x\in(0,a^{\ast})
.

We state our main result.

Theorem 6.1
.

If the Lévy measure has a log-convex tail, then there exists a unique
(c^{\ast}_{1},c^{\ast}_{2})
strategy that is optimal for the impulse control problem. Moreover,
c_{1}^{\ast}=0
if and only if
\beta\geqslant\beta_{\max}
or
a^{\ast}=0
, where
\beta_{\max}
is given by

(10)

\beta_{\max}:=\zeta(0)-\frac{\mathcal{H}^{\omega_{q}}(\zeta(0))-\mathcal{H}^{\omega_{q}}(0)}{\mathcal{H}^{\omega_{q}\prime}(0+)}.

The proof of this Theorem is rather long, so we will break it down into a series of lemmas.

Lemma 6.1
.

The set
C^{\ast}
consists of only one element, hence there exists a unique impulse control strategy
(c_{1}^{\ast},c_{2}^{\ast})
that is optimal.

Proof.

Suppose there exist
(c_{1},c_{2}),(\hat{c}_{1},\hat{c}_{2})\in C^{\ast}
. Since they are minimisers of
g
, we have that
\mathcal{H}^{\omega_{q}\prime}(c_{2})=\mathcal{H}^{\omega_{q}\prime}(\hat{c}_{2})
. However, since both
c_{2},\hat{c_{2}}>a^{\ast}
and
\mathcal{H}^{\omega_{q}\prime}
is strictly increasing on
(a^{\ast},\infty)
, then we must have
c_{2}=\hat{c}_{2}
.

By a similar reasoning, if both
c_{1}
and
\hat{c}_{1}
are strictly positive, and since
c_{1},\hat{c}_{1}<a^{\ast}
and
\mathcal{H}^{\omega_{q}\prime}
is strictly decreasing on
(0,a^{\ast})
, then we must have
c_{1}=\hat{c}_{1}
.

Finally, suppose that
c_{1}=0
and
\hat{c}_{1}>0
. On the one hand we have

\mathcal{H}^{\omega_{q}\prime}(c_{1})=\frac{\mathcal{H}^{\omega_{q}}(c_{2})-\mathcal{H}^{\omega_{q}}(c_{1})}{c_{2}-c_{1}-\beta}.

On the other hand, we have

\mathcal{H}^{\omega_{q}\prime}(0)=\frac{\mathcal{H}^{\omega_{q}}(c_{2})-\mathcal{H}^{\omega_{q}}(0)}{c_{2}-\beta}.

Since they are both minimisers of
g
, we also have
\mathcal{H}^{\omega_{q}\prime}(c_{1})=\mathcal{H}^{\omega_{q}\prime}(0)
, which after some algebraic manipulations gives

\displaystyle\mathcal{H}^{\omega_{q}\prime}(c_{1})

\displaystyle=\frac{\mathcal{H}^{\omega_{q}\prime}(c_{1})(c_{2}-\beta)+\mathcal{H}^{\omega_{q}}(0)-\mathcal{H}^{\omega_{q}}(c_{1})}{c_{2}-c_{1}-\beta}

\displaystyle=\frac{\mathcal{H}^{\omega_{q}\prime}(c_{1})(c_{2}-\beta)-\mathcal{H}^{\omega_{q}\prime}(\xi)c_{1}}{c_{2}-c_{1}-\beta},

where in the second equality we have applied the mean value theorem, hence
\xi\in(0,c_{1})
. However, since
\mathcal{H}^{\omega_{q}\prime}
is strictly decreasing on
(0,c_{1})
, we obtain

\displaystyle\mathcal{H}^{\omega_{q}\prime}(c_{1})

\displaystyle=\frac{\mathcal{H}^{\omega_{q}\prime}(c_{1})(c_{2}-\beta)-\mathcal{H}^{\omega_{q}\prime}(\xi)c_{1}}{c_{2}-c_{1}-\beta}

\displaystyle<\mathcal{H}^{\omega_{q}\prime}(c_{1}),

which is a contradiction. It follows that
c_{1}
has to be equal to
\hat{c}_{1}
.
∎

In the ext result, we provide a way to compute
(c_{1}^{\ast},c_{2}^{\ast})
. To this end, we will introduce some auxiliary functions that will allow us to reduce the 2-dimensional minimisation into two auxiliary 1-dimensional minimisation problems. Let

c_{1\max}:=\inf{{\left\{c_{1}\in(0,a^{\ast}):\zeta(c_{1})-c_{1}\leqslant\beta\right\}}},

where we set
c_{1\max}=0
when
\lim_{x\downarrow 0}\zeta(x)\leqslant\beta
. Define the functions
g_{1}:(0,c_{1\max})\to(0,\infty)
and
g_{0}:(\beta,\infty)\to(0,\infty)
as

(11)

\displaystyle g_{1}(c_{1})

\displaystyle=\frac{\mathcal{H}^{\omega_{q}}(\zeta(c_{1}))-\mathcal{H}^{\omega_{q}}(c_{1})}{\zeta(c_{1})-c_{1}-\beta},

(12)

\displaystyle g_{0}(c_{2})

\displaystyle=\frac{\mathcal{H}^{\omega_{q}}(c_{2})-\mathcal{H}^{\omega_{q}}(0)}{c_{2}-\beta}.

Lemma 6.2
.

The function
g_{0}
has a unique minimiser.

Proof.

By differentiating
g_{0}
we obtain:

\displaystyle g^{\prime}_{0}(x)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{x-\beta}(\mathcal{H}^{\omega_{q}\prime}(x)-g_{0}(x)).

From these expressions we deduce the following:

(1)

g_{0}
is strictly decreasing if and only if
g_{0}<\mathcal{H}^{\omega_{q}\prime}
;

(2)

g_{0}
is strictly increasing if and only if
g_{0}>\mathcal{H}^{\omega_{q}\prime}
;

(3)

g_{0}
has a critical point at
x
if and only if
g_{0}(x)=\mathcal{H}^{\omega_{q}\prime}(x)
.

In addition, we show that
g_{0}
has a unique minimiser. On the one hand, we have that
\lim_{x\downarrow\beta}g_{0}(x)=+\infty
whereas
\mathcal{H}^{\omega\prime}(\beta)<\infty
, hence it follows that there exists an open vicinity around
\beta
such that
g_{0}>\mathcal{H}^{\omega_{q}\prime}
. On the other hand, for
\varepsilon\in(0,1)
, and
x
large enough we write

g_{0}(x)={{\left(\frac{\mathcal{H}^{\omega_{q}}(x)-\mathcal{H}^{\omega_{q}}(0)}{(1-\varepsilon)x}\right)}}{{\left(\frac{(1-\varepsilon)x}{x-\beta}\right)}}.

Note that if
x
is large enough, then
\frac{(1-\varepsilon)x}{x-\beta}\leqslant 1
. In addition, the mean value theorem implies that there exists some
\xi\in[0,x]
such that
\mathcal{H}^{\omega_{q}\prime}(\xi)=\frac{\mathcal{H}^{\omega_{q}}(x)-\mathcal{H}^{\omega_{q}}(0)}{x}
. Moreover, since
\mathcal{H}^{\omega_{q}\prime}
is increasing on
(a^{\ast},\infty)
then for
x\gg a^{\ast}
we have

g_{0}(x)<\frac{\mathcal{H}^{\omega_{q}\prime}(x)}{1-\varepsilon},

where it follows that
g_{0}(x)<\mathcal{H}^{\omega_{q}\prime}(x)
for
x
sufficiently large. This analysis implies that there exists a unique
\hat{c}\in(\beta\vee a^{\ast},\infty)
such that
g_{0}
is decreasing on
(\beta,\hat{c})
, increasing on
(\hat{c},\infty)
, and
g_{0}(\hat{c})=\mathcal{H}^{\omega_{q}\prime}(\hat{c})
.
∎

Finally, we derive a condition to determine whether
c_{1}^{\ast}>0
or
c_{1}^{\ast}=0
.

Lemma 6.3
.

Recall the parameter
\beta_{\max}
defined in (
10
). Consider the following cases:

(1)

a^{\ast}>0
and
\beta<\beta_{\max}
;

(2)

a^{\ast}>0
and
\beta\geqslant\beta_{\max}
;

(3)

a^{\ast}=0
.

In case (1) we have that
c_{1}^{\ast}>0
; whereas in cases (2) and (3) we have that
c_{1}^{\ast}=0
.

Proof.

In case (1), we will show that
c_{1}^{\ast}>0
. First, by differentiating
g_{1}
we obtain

\displaystyle g^{\prime}_{1+}(x)

\displaystyle=\frac{\zeta^{\prime}_{+}(x)-1}{\zeta(x)-x-\beta}(\mathcal{H}^{\omega_{q}\prime}(x)-g_{1}(x)),

then we deduce the following:

(1)

g_{1}
is strictly decreasing if and only if
g_{1}<\mathcal{H}^{\omega_{q}\prime}
;

(2)

g_{1}
is strictly increasing if and only if
g_{1}>\mathcal{H}^{\omega_{q}\prime}
;

(3)

g_{1}
has a critical point at
x
if and only if
g_{1}(x)=\mathcal{H}^{\omega_{q}\prime}(x)
.

Given this behaviour, we now show that
g_{1}
has a unique minimiser. Indeed, on the one hand we have

\displaystyle g_{1}(0)

\displaystyle=\frac{\mathcal{H}^{\omega_{q}}(\zeta(0))-\mathcal{H}^{\omega_{q}}(0)}{\zeta(0)-\beta}

\displaystyle<\frac{\mathcal{H}^{\omega_{q}}(\zeta(0))-\mathcal{H}^{\omega_{q}}(0)}{\zeta(0)-\beta_{\max}}

\displaystyle=\mathcal{H}^{\omega_{q}\prime}(0+),

where in the last equality we have used the definition of
\beta_{\max}
. On the other hand, we have

\lim_{x\uparrow c_{1\max}}g_{1}(x)=+\infty>\mathcal{H}^{\omega_{q}\prime}(c_{1\max}).

This analysis, together with the fact that
\mathcal{H}^{\omega_{q}\prime}
is strictly decreasing on
(0,a^{\ast})
implies that there exists a unique
\bar{c}\in(0,c_{1\max})
such that
g_{1}
is decreasing on
(0,\bar{c})
, increasing on
(\bar{c},c_{1\max})
, and that
g_{1}(\bar{c})=\mathcal{H}^{\omega_{q}\prime}(\bar{c})
.

Now we turn to prove that
(c_{1}^{\ast},c_{2}^{\ast})=(\bar{c},\zeta(\bar{c}))
. By the definition of
\bar{c}
and the fact that
\mathcal{H}^{\omega_{q}\prime}
is decreasing on
(0,a^{\ast})
we have
g_{1}(\bar{c})<\mathcal{H}^{\omega_{q}\prime}(0+)
. Recall the unique minimiser of
g_{0}
, denoted by
\hat{c}
, which we obtained in Lemma
6.2
. It follows that, if
g_{0}(\hat{c})\geqslant\mathcal{H}^{\omega_{q}\prime}(0+)
, then
g_{1}(\bar{c})<g_{0}(\hat{c})
and so
g
is minimised at
(\bar{c},\zeta(\bar{c}))
. Conversely, if
g_{0}(\hat{c})<\mathcal{H}^{\omega_{q}\prime}(0+)
, then we get

\lim_{x\downarrow 0}\frac{\partial}{\partial x}g(x,\hat{c})=\frac{g_{0}(\hat{c})-\mathcal{H}^{\omega_{q}\prime}(0+)}{\hat{c}-\beta}<0,

hence
(0,\hat{c})
is not a minimiser of
g
. We conclude that
(c_{1}^{\ast},c_{2}^{\ast})=(\bar{c},\zeta(\bar{c}))
.

In case (2), first we notice that
g_{1}(0)\geqslant\mathcal{H}^{\omega_{q}\prime}(0+)
. Now, for
x\in(0,c_{1\max})
and using the mean value theorem we have

\displaystyle g_{1}(x)

\displaystyle=\frac{\mathcal{H}^{\omega_{q}}(\zeta(x))-\mathcal{H}^{\omega_{q}}(x)}{\zeta(x)-x-\beta}

\displaystyle=\frac{\zeta(x)-x}{\zeta(x)-x-\beta}\mathcal{H}^{\omega_{q}\prime}(\xi)

\displaystyle>\mathcal{H}^{\omega_{q}\prime}(x),

where in the inequality we have used that
\mathcal{H}^{\omega_{q}\prime}
is decreasing on
(0,c_{1\max})
and that
\frac{\zeta(x)-x}{\zeta(x)-x-\beta}\geqslant 1
. It follows that
g_{1}
is strictly increasing on
(0,c_{1\max})
. Hence
c_{1}^{\ast}=0
, and even further
(c_{1}^{\ast},c_{2}^{\ast})=(0,\hat{c})
.

Finally, in case (3), first note that
c_{1\max}=0
and that
\mathcal{H}^{\omega_{q}\prime}
is strictly increasing on
(0,\infty)
. It now follows that
c_{1}^{\ast}=0
, and even further
(c_{1}^{\ast},c_{2}^{\ast})=(0,\hat{c})
.
∎

To sum up, using Lemmas (
6.1
)–(
6.3
) we have obtained that the optimal barriers
(c_{1}^{\ast},c_{2}^{\ast})
are unique, and we have derived conditions to characterise and compute them in terms of the cost parameter
\beta
and the generalised scale function. Hence, the proof of Theorem
6.1
is complete.

7.
Numerical Experiments

We provide a numerical illustration of our results. Throughout this section, we assume that our Lévy process
X
is given by

X_{t}=x+\mu t+\sigma B_{t}-\sum_{i=1}^{N_{t}}Y_{i},

where
\mu=0.075,\,\sigma=0.5,\,B=(B_{t})_{t\geqslant 0}
is a standard Brownian motion,
(N_{t})_{t\geqslant 0}
is an homogeneous Poisson process with arrival rate
\lambda=0.5
independent of
B
, and
(Y_{i})_{i\geqslant 1}
is a sequence of iid exponential random variables with parameter
\alpha=9
; finally, we let
q=0.025
. In this model, the scale function
W_{q}
can be computed explicitly as

W_{q}(x)=\sum_{i=1}^{3}D_{i}\mathrm{e}^{\theta_{i}x},

where
\theta_{i},\,i=1,2,3
are the distinct roots of
s\mapsto\psi(s)-q
satisfying
\theta_{1}>0
and
\theta_{2},\,\theta_{3}<0
, and
D_{i}
are given by
D_{i}=\frac{1}{\psi^{\prime}(\theta_{i})}
. Consequently, the second scale function
Z_{q}
can also be found explicitly by standard integration.

For this numerical illustration, we consider that the bankruptcy rate function is of the form
\omega(x)=\phi\mathbb{I}_{(-\infty,a]}+(\phi+m(x-a))\mathbb{I}_{(a,0]}
, with
a=-1
,
\phi=1.5
, and
m=-0.15
. Note that, thanks to the explicit expression for the scale functions
W_{q}
and
Z_{q}
, we are able to compute numerically the generalised scale function
\mathcal{H}^{\omega_{q}}
by the iteration scheme described in Section 2.2 in
[
11
]
.

Using these parameter values, first we find that
\beta_{\max}=0.009
, hence for our numerical experiment we take
\beta=0.001
. In Figure

\mathcal{H}^{\omega_{q}\prime}

g_{1}
(Red curve). We observe that
c_{1}^{\ast}>0
is the unique minimiser of
g_{0}
, and it is also the unique intersection point of
\mathcal{H}^{\omega_{q}\prime}
and
g_{1}
. Afterwards we find
c_{2}^{\ast}
as the one satisfying
\mathcal{H}^{\omega_{q}\prime}(c_{1}^{\ast})=\mathcal{H}^{\omega_{q}\prime}(c_{2}^{\ast})
