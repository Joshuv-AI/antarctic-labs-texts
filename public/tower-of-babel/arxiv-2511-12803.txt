Finite-Horizon Quickest Change Detection Balancing Latency with False Alarm Probability
Yu-Han Huang and Venugopal V. Veeravalli

Abstract
A finite-horizon variant of the quickest change detection (QCD) problem that is of relevance to learning in non-stationary environments is studied. The metric characterizing false alarms is the probability of a false alarm occurring before the horizon ends. The metric that characterizes the delay is latency , which is the smallest value such that the probability that detection delay exceeds this value is upper bounded to a predetermined latency level. The objective is to minimize the latency (at a given latency level), while maintaining a low false alarm probability. Under the pre-specified latency and false alarm levels, a universal lower bound on the latency, which any change detection procedure needs to satisfy, is derived. Change detectors are then developed, which are order-optimal in terms of the horizon. The case where the pre- and post-change distributions are known is considered first, and then the results are generalized to the non-parametric case when they are unknown except that they are sub-Gaussian with different means. Simulations are provided to validate the theoretical results.

Introduction

In the quickest change detection (QCD) problem, the environment undergoes a change at an unknown
change-point
, which affects the distribution of stochastic observations of the environment.
The goal for the agent is to detect the change as soon as possible, while controlling the false alarm rate.
QCD problems have been extensively studied due to the broad range of applications in engineering and science, including but not limited to biomedical signal processing, epicenter monitoring, financial market, and quality control in manufacturing. See
(
15
;
18
;
17
;
21
)
for articles and books on this topic.

QCD problems are mathematically formulated as constrained optimization problems, in which the objective is to minimize an appropriate metric of the detection delay, under a constraint on a suitable false alarm metric.
The most common false alarm metric is the
mean time to false alarm
(or equivalently, its reciprocal, the
false alarm rate
).
Detection delay is generally measured by considering the
worst-case expected detection delay
(conditioned on not having a false alarm and possibly the history of samples prior to the change-point) over all possible change-points across an infinite horizon.
These metrics, however, may not be appropriate in many practical settings. First, the horizon may be large but finite in practice, which does not fit the infinite horizon formulations common in QCD literature.
Additionally, instead of minimizing the worst-case expected delay while suppressing the false alarm rate, one might be interested in controlling the probability of false alarm over the horizon, and that of detection delay being too large, as these two “bad” events can possibly have a significant impact on the system.
Lai
(
10
, Section II.B)
addresses the false alarm aspect of such a formulation partially, by considering the goal of minimizing the worst-case expected delay while controlling the false alarm probability over a window to a prespecified level. However, the size of the window does not cover the entire horizon, and it depends on the prespecified false alarm level.
Thus, the QCD problem in which we are interested, where we balance the probability of false alarm over the horizon with
latency
, which the smallest value such that the probability the detection delay exceeds this value is upper bounded to a predetermined level, remains largely open. A key application of this formulation is in online learning in a piecewise stationary environment, which we describe next.

In a piecewise stationary environment, the underlying distribution of the environment undergoes changes at multiple change-points, and remains stationary in between. For the case of piecewise stationary bandits and piecewise stationary finite-horizon episodic Markov decision processes, there is a rich literature on algorithms that restart as soon as they detect a change in the environment
(
2
;
20
;
3
;
1
;
gerogiannis2024blackboxfeas
;
5
)
. To achieve good (order-optimal) performance, these algorithms need to control the probability of false alarm and that of detection delay being too large
(
3
;
5
)
.

In this work, we formulate a QCD problem in which the metrics are the false alarm probability and latency.
The objective is to minimize the latency (at a prespecified latency level), while maintaining the probability of false alarm below a prespecified level. Under the prespecified latency and false alarm levels, we first establish a universal lower bound on the latency that any change detector must satisfy. Then, we propose change detectors that are order-optimal in terms of the horizon (as the horizon goes to infinity) in scenarios with full knowledge of the underlying distributions. We then generalize these order-optimal change detectors to scenarios where the underlying distributions are unknown, except for being sub-Gaussian with different means. Preliminary versions of these results are described in the following conference proceedings
(
7
;
6
)
.

The remainder of the paper is organized as follows: The QCD problem of interest and the metrics for detection delay (latency) and false alarm are presented in Section
2
. A universal lower bound on latency is derived in Section
3
. Order-optimal change detectors are discussed in Section
4
. The performance of proposed change detectors is validated through simulations in Section
5
. Lastly, some concluding remarks, including avenues for future research are presented in Section
6
.

2
Problem Formulation

For any positive integers
k\leq n
, we denote the set
\left\{k,\dots,n\right\}
by
[k,n]
, and denote
[1,n]
by
[n]
for conciseness. Let
\left\{X_{n}:n\in[T]\right\}
be a set of independent random vectors observed by an agent over a finite horizon
T
sequentially. At an unknown time-step, referred to as the
change-point

\nu
, the distribution of the observations undergoes a change, i.e.,

X_{n}\sim\dcases f_{0},&n\in[\nu-1]\\
f_{1},n\in[\nu,T].

(1)

This indicates that the observations before the change-point
\nu
follow the pre-change density
f_{0}
, while those after the change-point follow the post-change density
f_{1}
. Both densities are defined with respect to a common dominating measure
\lambda
.
When
f_{0}
is unknown, the agent needs a small number of pre-change observations to estimate
f_{0}
. Thus, following
(
9
)
, we assume that the change-point does not occur within the first
m
time-steps, i.e.,
\nu>m
. These first
m
time-steps, known as the
pre-change window
, are necessary for change detectors to learn the pre-change density.

Let
\tau
denote the stopping time of a (causal) change detector, i.e., the time-step at which a change detector declares a change. Following notation common in the QCD literature, we use
\mathbb{P}_{\nu}
and
\mathbb{E}_{\nu}
to denote the probability measure and the expectation when the change occurs at
\nu
. Similarly,
\mathbb{P}_{\infty}
and
\mathbb{E}_{\infty}
represent the probability measure and the expectation when there is no change in the distribution. As discussed in Section
1
, our metric for detection delay is the smallest value such that this value is exceeded by the detection delay
\tau-\nu
with low probability, for any possible change-point. Therefore, we define the latency as follows: for some (small) latency level
\delta_{\mathrm{D}}\in(0,1)
,

\displaystyle\ell\coloneqq\min\left\{d\in[T]:\mathbb{P}_{\nu}\left(\tau\geq\nu+d\right)\leq\delta_{\mathrm{D}},\forall\,\nu\in\left[m+1,T-d\right]\right\}

(2)

where
m=0
when the pre-change density
f_{0}
is known. Then, the goal of a change detector is to minimize the latency, subject to the constraint that the false alarm probability over a finite horizon
\mathbb{P}_{\infty}\left(\tau\leq T\right)
remains below a (small) false alarm level
\delta_{\mathrm{F}}\in(0,1)
. Therefore, we propose the following formulation of the QCD problem of interest: for false alarm and latency levels
\delta_{\mathrm{F}},\delta_{\mathrm{D}}\in\left(0,1\right)
,

\underset{\tau}{\textrm{minimize}}\kern 5.0pt\ell\quad\quad\textrm{s.t.}\kern 5.0pt\mathbb{P}_{\infty}\left(\tau\leq T\right)\leq\delta_{\mathrm{F}}

(3)

where
m=0
when the pre-change density
f_{0}
is known. Note that when we develop candidate change detector for this problem, we do
not
leverage the knowledge of the horizon
T
, as this information about the horizon might not be available in general settings.

3
Universal Lower Bound on Latency

In order to design good change detectors for the problem introduced in (
3
), we first establish the theoretical lower bound on the latency that any change detector must satisfy, with the understanding that good change detectors should approach this lower bound in terms of their latency for prespecified false alarm and latency levels
(\delta_{\mathrm{F}},\delta_{\mathrm{D}})
.
The following quantity appears in the lower bound:

\displaystyle K\coloneqq\log\left(\mathbb{E}_{f_{1}}\left[\frac{f_{1}\left(X\right)}{f_{0}\left(X\right)}\right]\right)

(4)

where
\mathbb{E}_{f_{1}}
denotes the expectation with
X\sim f_{1}
. Using Jensen’s inequality, it is easily seen that
K>D(f_{1}||f_{0})>0
where
D
denotes Kullback-Liebler (KL) divergence. We further assume that
K<\infty
.

Theorem 1

(Asymptotic Lower Bound on Latency)
.

For all
\delta_{\mathrm{F}},\delta_{\mathrm{D}}\in\left(0,1\right)
such that
\delta_{\mathrm{F}}+\delta_{\mathrm{D}}<1

\ell\geq\left(\frac{1}{K}+o\left(1\right)\right)\left[\log\left(T\right)+\log\left(\frac{1}{\delta_{\mathrm{F}}}\right)+\log\left(1-\delta_{\mathrm{F}}-\delta_{\mathrm{D}}\right)+o\left(1\right)\right]

(5)

as
T\to\infty
.

The proof of Theorem
1
is given in Appendix
B
.

4
Candidate Change Detectors

Given the universal lower bound of Theorem
1
, our goal in this section is to design change detectors that approach this bound asymptotically as
T\to\infty
. We first consider the scenario with full knowledge of the underlying distributions, and then generalize to the scenario where the
underlying distributions are unknown, except for being sub-Gaussian with different
means.

4.1
Known Pre- and Post-Change Distributions

In the setting where
T=\infty
and the independent stochastic observations follow (
1
), it was demonstrated by Moustakides
(
12
)
that Page’s Cumulative Sum (CuSum) test
(
13
)
is exactly optimal under Lorden’s criterion
(
11
)
. The CuSum test employs the CuSum statistic:

C_{n}\coloneqq\sup_{k\in\left[n\right]}\sum_{i=k}^{n}\log\left(\frac{f_{1}\left(X_{i}\right)}{f_{0}\left(X_{i}\right)}\right),\;n\in\mathbb{N}.

(6)

which satisfies the recursion:

C_{n}=\max\left\{C_{n-1},0\right\}+\log\left(\frac{f_{1}\left(X_{n}\right)}{f_{0}\left(X_{n}\right)}\right),\;n\in\mathbb{N},

(7)

with
C_{0}\coloneqq 0
. The CuSum test declares a change when the CuSum statistic exceeds a constant threshold. Thus, the stopping time of the CuSum test is defined as follows:

\tau_{\mathrm{CS}}\coloneqq\inf\left\{n\in\mathbb{N}:C_{n}\geq b\right\},

(8)

where
b
is the threshold. In the same setting where
T=\infty
and observations follow (
1
), the Shiryaev–Roberts (SR) test
(
16
)
is shown to be asymptotically optimal under Pollak’s criterion
(
14
)
and under Lorden’s criterion. The SR test utilizes the statistic:

S_{n}\coloneqq\sum_{k\in\left[n\right]}\prod_{i=k}^{n}\frac{f_{1}\left(X_{i}\right)}{f_{0}\left(X_{i}\right)},\;n\in\mathbb{N},

(9)

which satisfies the recursion:

\displaystyle S_{n}=\left(S_{n-1}+1\right)\frac{f_{1}\left(X_{n}\right)}{f_{0}\left(X_{n}\right)},\;n\in\mathbb{N},

(10)

where
S_{0}\coloneqq 0
. Similar to the CuSum test, the SR test gets triggered when the SR statistic surpasses a constant threshold. The stopping time of the SR test can then be defined as follows:

\tau_{\mathrm{SR}}\coloneqq\inf\left\{n\in\mathbb{N}:S_{n}\geq b\right\}.

(11)

Due to the recursive properties in (
7
) and (
10
), the CuSum and SR tests are computationally efficient, as the computational complexity of the statistics per time-step is
\mathcal{O}(1)
.
Owing to the aforementioned optimality of the CuSum and SR tests, it is natural to speculate that these tests can attain the lower bound in Theorem
1
asymptotically as
T\to\infty
.
However, since the mean time to false alarm is finite for the CuSum and SR tests (i.e.,
\mathbb{E}_{\infty}\left[\tau_{\mathrm{CS}}\right],\mathbb{E}_{\infty}\left[\tau_{\mathrm{SR}}\right]<\infty
), the false alarm probability of these tests goes to
1
as the horizon goes to infinity (i.e.,
\lim_{T\to\infty}\mathbb{P}_{\infty}\left(\tau_{\mathrm{CS}}\leq T\right)=\lim_{T\to\infty}\mathbb{P}_{\infty}\left(\tau_{\mathrm{SR}}\leq T\right)=1
). This indicates that these tests (with a constant threshold) cannot suppress the false alarm probability over a large horizon if the threshold cannot be tuned according to the horizon. As the agent is assumed to be oblivious to the horizon, the CuSum test in (
8
) and the SR test in (
11
) are not good candidate solutions.

One way to control the false alarm rate in the CuSum and SR tests for large horizons is to increase the thresholds of these tests with time. Taking cues from the analysis in
(
8
;
3
)
, we propose the Time-Varying-Threshold CuSum (TVT-CuSum) and Time-Varying-Threshold SR (TVT-SR) tests, whose thresholds increase logarithmically with time. The stopping time of the TVT-CuSum test is defined as follows: for
r>1
,

\displaystyle\tau_{\mathrm{C},r}\coloneqq\inf\left\{n\in\mathbb{N}:C_{n}\geq\beta_{\mathrm{C}}\left(n,\delta_{\mathrm{F}},r\right)\right\},

(12)

where the time-varying threshold
\beta_{\mathrm{C}}
is:

\displaystyle\beta_{\mathrm{C}}\left(n,\delta_{\mathrm{F}},r\right)\coloneqq\log\left(\zeta\left(r\right)\frac{n^{r}}{\delta_{\mathrm{F}}}\right),\;n\in\mathbb{N},

(13)

with
\zeta\left(r\right)=\sum_{i=1}^{\infty}i^{-r}
. Similarly, the stopping time of the TVT-SR test is defined as follows: for
r>1
,

\displaystyle\tau_{\mathrm{S},r}\coloneqq\inf\left\{n\in\mathbb{N}:\log S_{n}\geq\beta_{\mathrm{S}}\left(n,\delta_{\mathrm{F}},r\right)\right\},

(14)

where the time-varying threshold
\beta_{\mathrm{S}}
has the following form:

\displaystyle\beta_{\mathrm{S}}\left(n,\delta_{\mathrm{F}},r\right)\coloneqq\beta_{\mathrm{C}}\left(n,\delta_{\mathrm{F}},r\right)+\log n,\;n\in\mathbb{N}.

(15)

In the following theorem, we establish that the TVT-CuSum and TVT-SR tests control the false alarm probability to be below the false alarm level
\delta_{\mathrm{F}}
. Furthermore, the latencies of these tests at latency level
\delta_{\mathrm{D}}
are
\mathcal{O}\left(\log T\right)
, matching the order in the lower bound in Theorem
1
. In the theorem, we use
\Lambda
to denote the cumulant generating function of
\log\frac{f_{0}\left(X\right)}{f_{1}\left(X\right)}
with
X\sim f_{1}
, i.e.,

\Lambda\left(\theta\right)=\log\left(\mathbb{E}_{f_{1}}\left[\exp\left(\theta\log\left(\frac{f_{0}\left(X\right)}{f_{1}\left(X\right)}\right)\right)\right]\right).

(16)

Theorem 2

(TVT-CuSum and TVT-SR tests)
.

Consider the TVT-CuSum test in (
12
) and the TVT-SR test in (
14
). The false alarm probability of these tests is smaller than
\delta_{\mathrm{F}}
, i.e., for any
r>1
,

\mathbb{P}_{\infty}\left(\tau_{\mathrm{C},r}\leq T\right)\leq\delta_{\mathrm{F}}\kern 5.0pt\mathrm{and}\kern 5.0pt\mathbb{P}_{\infty}\left(\tau_{\mathrm{S},r}\leq T\right)\leq\delta_{\mathrm{F}}.

(17)

In addition, the latencies of these tests are upper bounded as follows:

\displaystyle\ell\leq\inf_{\theta\in\left(0,1\right)}\left\{\frac{1}{\lvert\Lambda\left(\theta\right)\rvert}\left[\log\left(\frac{1}{\delta_{\mathrm{D}}}\right)+\theta\beta\left(T,\delta_{\mathrm{F}},r\right)\right]\right\},

(18)

with
\beta=\beta_{\mathrm{C}}
for the TVT-CuSum test and
\beta=\beta_{\mathrm{S}}
for the TVT-SR test.

The proof of Theorem
2
is presented in Appendix
A
. Note that
\beta_{\mathrm{C}}\left(T,\delta_{\mathrm{F}},r\right)
and
\beta_{\mathrm{S}}\left(T,\delta_{\mathrm{F}},r\right)
are
\mathcal{O}\left(\log T\right)
, matching the order of the lower bound in Theorem
1
. Hence, Theorem
2
indicates that the TVT-CuSum and TVT-SR tests are order-optimal with respect to the horizon
T
.

Remark 1

(Decreasing false alarm and latency levels)
.

In the setting where
\delta_{\mathrm{F}}=\mathcal{O}\left(T^{-p}\right)
and
\delta_{\mathrm{D}}=\mathcal{O}\left(T^{-q}\right)
for some
p,q>0
, the lower bound in Theorem
1
is
\Omega\left(\log T\right)
, and the upper bound on latencies in (
18
) is also
\mathcal{O}\left(\log T\right)
. This shows that the TVT-CuSum and TVT-SR tests are also order-optimal with respect to
T
in this setting, which is relevant to learning in piecewise stationary environments – see, also
(
6
)
.

4.2
Unknown Pre- and Post-Change Distributions

While the TVT-CuSum and TVT-SR tests described in Section
4.1
are order-optimal with respect to
T
, the computation of the CuSum and SR statistics requires full knowledge of
f_{0}
and
f_{1}
.
We now investigate the setting where
f_{0}
and
f_{1}
are both
\sigma^{2}
-sub-Gaussian, and that the agent knows the sub-Gaussianity parameter
\sigma^{2}
.
We stress that the assumption is not too restrictive, since bounded stochastic observations (which are commonly encountered in practice) are
\sigma^{2}
-sub-Gaussian, where the parameter
\sigma
can be derived from the support of the observations.
Let
\mu_{0}
and
\mu_{1}
represent the pre- and post-change means, respectively. Then, we define the change-gap
\Delta
as the absolute difference between the pre- and post-change means, i.e.,
\Delta\coloneqq\left\lvert\mu_{0}-\mu_{1}\right\rvert
. We assume that the change-gap is nonzero i.e.,
\Delta>0
.

To enable the TVT-CuSum and TVT-SR tests to operate without knowledge of the pre- and post-change distributions, we replace the CuSum and SR statistics with their generalized versions—a common generalization method for settings with unknown pre- and post-change distributions
(
10
;
9
)
. In particular, we replace the CuSum statistic in (
12
) with the Generalized Likelihood Ratio (GLR) statistic, defined as follows:

G_{n}\coloneqq\sup_{k\in\left[n\right]}\log\frac{\sup_{\mu_{0}^{\prime}\in\mathbb{R}}\sup_{\mu_{1}^{\prime}\in\mathbb{R}}\prod_{i=1}^{k}f_{\mu_{0}^{\prime}}\left(X_{i}\right)\prod_{i=k+1}^{n}f_{\mu_{1}^{\prime}}\left(X_{i}\right)}{\sup_{\mu\in\mathbb{R}}\prod_{i=1}^{n}f_{\mu}\left(X_{i}\right)},\;n\in\left[T\right],

(19)

where
f_{\mu}
denotes the Gaussian density with mean
\mu
and variance
\sigma^{2}
. The following lemma (whose proof is given in the Appendix) shows that the GLR statistic can be written in terms of empirical means of the stochastic observations:

Lemma 1
.

For any
n\in\mathbb{N}
and any
k\in\left[n\right]
, we have:

\displaystyle\log\left(\frac{\sup_{\mu_{0}^{\prime}\in\mathbb{R}}\prod_{i=1}^{k}f_{\mu_{0}^{\prime}}\left(X_{i}\right)\sup_{\mu_{1}^{\prime}\in\mathbb{R}}\prod_{i=k+1}^{n}f_{\mu_{1}^{\prime}}\left(X_{i}\right)}{\sup_{\mu\in\mathbb{R}}\prod_{i=1}^{n}f_{\mu}\left(X_{i}\right)}\right)

\displaystyle=kD\left(\hat{\mu}_{1:k};\hat{\mu}_{1:n}\right)+\left(n-k\right)D\left(\hat{\mu}_{k+1:n};\hat{\mu}_{1:n}\right)

(20)

where
\hat{\mu}_{m:n}
is the empirical mean of the stochastic observations
\left\{X_{m},\dots,X_{n}\right\}
and
D\left(x;y\right)\coloneqq(x-y)^{2}/(2\sigma^{2})
is the KL-divergence between two Gaussian distributions with common variance
\sigma^{2}
and different means
x,y\in\mathbb{R}
.

Thus, for any
n\in[T]
,

\displaystyle G_{n}=\sup_{k\in[n]}kD\left(\hat{\mu}_{1:k};\hat{\mu}_{1:n}\right)+\left(n-k\right)D\left(\hat{\mu}_{k+1:n};\hat{\mu}_{1:n}\right).

(21)

The corresponding GLR test has stopping time:

\displaystyle\tau_{\mathrm{GLR}}\coloneqq\inf\left\{n\in\mathbb{N}:\;G_{n}\geq\beta_{\mathrm{GLR}}\left(n,\delta_{\mathrm{F}}\right)\right\},

(22)

where the threshold function
\beta_{\mathrm{GLR}}
is defined as:

\displaystyle\beta_{\mathrm{GLR}}\left(n,\delta_{\mathrm{F}}\right)\coloneqq

\displaystyle 6\log\left(1+\log\left(n\right)\right)+\frac{5}{2}\log\left(\frac{4n^{3/2}}{\delta_{\mathrm{F}}}\right)+11,\;n\in\left[T\right].

(23)

Similarly, we can extend the SR statistic to the Generalized Shiryaev–Roberts (GSR) statistic to the setting where the pre- and post-change densities are unknown. The GSR statistic is defined as follows:

W_{n}\coloneqq\sum_{k=1}^{n}\frac{\sup_{\mu_{0}^{\prime}\in\mathbb{R}}\sup_{\mu_{1}^{\prime}\in\mathbb{R}}\prod_{i=1}^{k}f_{\mu_{0}^{\prime}}\left(X_{i}\right)\prod_{i=k+1}^{n}f_{\mu_{1}^{\prime}}\left(X_{i}\right)}{\sup_{\mu\in\mathbb{R}}\prod_{i=1}^{n}f_{\mu}\left(X_{i}\right)},\;n\in\left[T\right].

(24)

By Lemma
1
, we can write

\displaystyle W_{n}=\sum_{k=1}^{n}\exp\left(kD\left(\hat{\mu}_{1:k};\hat{\mu}_{1:n}\right)+\left(n-k\right)D\left(\hat{\mu}_{k+1:n};\hat{\mu}_{1:n}\right)\right).

(25)

The stopping time of the GSR test is given by:

\displaystyle\tau_{\mathrm{GSR}}\coloneqq\inf\left\{n\in\mathbb{N}:\;\log W_{n}\geq\beta_{\mathrm{GSR}}\left(n,\delta_{\mathrm{F}}\right)\right\},

(26)

where

\displaystyle\beta_{\mathrm{GSR}}\left(n,\delta_{\mathrm{F}}\right)\coloneqq

\displaystyle\beta_{\mathrm{GLR}}\left(n,\delta_{\mathrm{F}}\right)+\log n,\;n\in\left[T\right].

(27)

In the following theorem, we show that the GLR and GSR tests can effectively control the false alarm probability at level
\delta_{\mathrm{F}}
. Furthermore, we provide upper bounds on the latencies of the tests at latency level
\delta_{\mathrm{D}}
.

Theorem 3

(GLR and GSR tests)
.

Consider the GLR test in (
22
) and the GSR test in (
26
). The false alarm probabilities of these tests are controlled at level
\delta_{\mathrm{F}}
, i.e.,

\mathbb{P}_{\infty}\left(\tau_{\mathrm{GLR}}\leq T\right)\leq\delta_{\mathrm{F}}\kern 5.0pt\mathrm{and}\kern 5.0pt\mathbb{P}_{\infty}\left(\tau_{\mathrm{GSR}}\leq T\right)\leq\delta_{\mathrm{F}}.

(28)

In addition, with the pre-change window length

m\geq\frac{8\sigma^{2}}{\Delta^{2}}\beta\left(T,\delta_{\mathrm{F}}\right),

(29)

the latencies of these tests at latency level
\delta_{\mathrm{D}}
are upper bounded as follows:

\ell\leq\left\lceil\max\left\{\frac{8\sigma^{2}m\beta\left(T,\delta_{\mathrm{F}}\right)}{\Delta^{2}m-8\sigma^{2}\beta\left(T,\delta_{\mathrm{F}}\right)},\frac{\delta_{\mathrm{F}}^{2/3}}{2^{16/15}\delta_{\mathrm{D}}^{4/15}}-m\right\}\right\rceil

(30)

with
\beta=\beta_{\mathrm{GLR}}
in (
23
)
for the GLR test and
\beta=\beta_{\mathrm{GSR}}
in (
27
) for the GSR test.

The proof of Theorem
3
is given in Appendix
D
. Note that
\beta_{\mathrm{GLR}}\left(T,\delta_{\mathrm{F}}\right)
and
\beta_{\mathrm{GSR}}\left(T,\delta_{\mathrm{F}}\right)
are
\mathcal{O}\left(\log T\right)
. In the following corollary, we show that the GLR and GSR tests are order-optimal in
T
with an appropriate choice of
m
when
\delta_{\mathrm{F}}
and
\delta_{\mathrm{D}}
are fixed.

Corollary 1
.

If the false alarm level
\delta_{\mathrm{F}}
and the latency level
\delta_{\mathrm{D}}
are fixed, the GLR and GSR tests have latencies (at latency level
\delta_{\mathrm{D}}
) that are
\mathcal{O}\left(\log T\right)
if

m=\left\lceil\frac{16\sigma^{2}}{\Delta^{2}}\beta\left(T,\delta_{\mathrm{F}}\right)\right\rceil,

(31)

where
\beta=\beta_{\mathrm{GLR}}
in (
23
)
for the GLR test and
\beta=\beta_{\mathrm{GSR}}
in (
27
) for the GSR test.

Proof.

With the choice of
m
and fixed
\delta_{\mathrm{F}},\delta_{\mathrm{D}}\in(0,1)
, we have

\displaystyle\ell

\displaystyle=\left\lceil\max\left\{\frac{8\sigma^{2}m\beta\left(T,\delta_{\mathrm{F}}\right)}{\Delta^{2}m-8\sigma^{2}\beta\left(T,\delta_{\mathrm{F}}\right)},\frac{\delta_{\mathrm{F}}^{2/3}}{2^{16/15}\delta_{\mathrm{D}}^{4/15}}-m\right\}\right\rceil

\displaystyle\leq\left\lceil\frac{8\sigma^{2}m\beta\left(T,\delta_{\mathrm{F}}\right)}{\Delta^{2}\left\lceil\frac{16\sigma^{2}}{\Delta^{2}}\beta\left(T,\delta_{\mathrm{F}}\right)\right\rceil-8\sigma^{2}\beta\left(T,\delta_{\mathrm{F}}\right)}\right\rceil

\displaystyle\overset{(a)}{\leq}\left\lceil\frac{16\sigma^{2}}{\Delta^{2}}\beta\left(T,\delta_{\mathrm{F}}\right)\right\rceil

\displaystyle\overset{(b)}{=}\mathcal{O}\left(\log T\right),

(32)

where step
(a)
follows from the choice of
m
, and step

Experimental Results

In this section, we illustrate the performance of our change detectors through simulations. We present the empirical latencies of the TVT-CuSum, TVT-SR, and GLR tests and compare them with the lower bound in Theorem
1
. We also compare the empirical values with the upper bounds on the latencies in Theorems

and
3

To study the empirical performance, we first explain how the empirical values are determined. From the definition of latency in (
2
), when the change-point lies in
\left[m+1,T-\ell\right]
, the probability of detection delay surpassing the latency should be at most
\delta_{\mathrm{D}}
. Accordingly, we compute the empirical latencies as follows: For each change-point in a candidate change-point set
\mathcal{M}\subseteq\left[m+1,T-\ell\right]
, we conduct
2\times 10^{5}
trials and record the detection delay
\tau-\nu
in each trial. The empirical value is then set to the maximum, over all change-points in
\mathcal{M}
, of the
100\left(1-\delta_{\mathrm{D}}\right)^{\mathrm{th}}
percentiles of the
2\times 10^{5}
recorded delays. Furthermore, we take
\mathcal{M}
to be
\left\{m+1+nT/10:\;n\in\mathbb{N},\;m+1+nT/10\leq T\right\}

2\times 10^{5}
trials over all
T-\ell-m
change-points is computationally cumbersome.

We now describe the experimental setup: The pre-change distribution is
\mathcal{N}\left(0,1\right)
, and the post-change distribution is
\mathcal{N}\left(1,1\right)
. For the GLR test in (

6

Conclusions

In this work, we proposed a variant of the QCD problem, in which the goal is to minimize the latency (at a prespecified latency level), while controlling the false alarm probability. We established a lower bound on the latency, under prespecified false alarm and latency levels, demonstrating that the latency of an optimal change detector is at least
\Omega(\log T)
. We then developed two change detectors that leverage the full knowledge of the pre- and post-change distributions, and then generalized these tests to cases with minimal knowledge of the distributions. All the proposed tests attain
\mathcal{O}\left(\log T\right)
latency while successfully containing the false alarm probability.
Our simulations validate the order optimality of the tests with respect to the horizon, and also show that our tests achieve good latency performance.
As the generalized tests can be applied to scenarios where little prior information is available about the pre- and post-change distributions, these tests can be employed in a wide range of practical settings.

However, the simulation results also suggest that the lower bound on the latency in Theorem
1
and the upper bound on the latency in Theorem
3
are quite loose, and it may be worthwhile exploring if these bounds can be tightened. In addition, the gap between the latencies of the tests that utilize the knowledge of the distribution and that of the generalized versions of these tests indicates that it may be worthwhile attempting to design tests that improve upon the generalized tests. We leave these topics for future exploration.
