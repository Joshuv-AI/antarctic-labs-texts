Effect of sodium glucose cotransporter 2 inhibitors on all cause death and rehospitalization for heart failure in patients with acute myocardial infarction
Authors: Bin Xiong, Limin He, An Zhang, Zhiyu Ling
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
The impact of sodium-glucose co-transporter 2 inhibitors (SGLT2-i) on reducing the risk of all-cause mortality and rehospitalization for heart failure (HF) in patients with acute myocardial infarction (AMI) remains unclear. This study aims to evaluate the effect of SGLT2-i on all-cause mortality and rehospitalization for HF in patients diagnosed with AMI. A comprehensive search was conducted in PubMed, Web of Science, the Cochrane Library, and Embase for relevant studies published up to May 2024, following the PICOS principle. Eligible studies included randomized clinical trials and cohort studies comparing SGLT2-i with placebo regarding all-cause mortality, rehospitalization for HF, cardiovascular mortality, and the incidence of nonfatal MI in AMI patients. Patient-level data from each trial were synthesized into a pooled dataset and analyzed using a mixed-effects or random-effects model based on the I2 statistic. Ten clinical trials enrolling 15,748 participants (6913 in the SGLT2-i group and 8835 in the placebo group) were included. The follow-up duration ranged from 12 weeks to 2.1 years. SGLT2-i significantly reduced rehospitalization for HF (RR: 0.69, 95% CI 0.60–0.81, P < 0.00001, I2 = 39%) compared to placebo. However, SGLT2-i did not significantly reduce the risk of all-cause death (RR: 0.85, 95% CI 0.72–1.00, P = 0.05, I2 = 46%), cardiovascular death (RR: 0.96, 95% CI 0.78–1.18, P = 0.67, I2 = 24%) or nonfatal MI (RR: 0.71, 95% CI 0.44–1.14, P = 0.16, I2 = 64%) during follow-up. Compared to placebo, SGLT2-i significantly reduced rehospitalization for HF in patients with AMI, but did not reduce the risk of all-cause death, cardiovascular death and nonfatal MI.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-024-81954-2.

Introduction

Despite substantial progress in reperfusion strategies and secondary prevention measures, acute myocardial infarction (AMI) remains a disease with high incidence and mortality rates worldwide1. Survivors of AMI are at significant risk for subsequent fatal and nonfatal cardiovascular events. Heart failure (HF) is a common sequela of AMI and the strongest predictor of death in these patients. Therefore, early intervention to prevent post-AMI HF is imperative to reduce the morbidity and mortality burden associated with AMI. Diabetes, particularly type 2 diabetes mellitus (T2DM), is a well-known risk factor for coronary artery disease (CAD), and patients with AMI who also have T2DM face a particularly high risk of HF, cardiovascular death, and subsequent major cardiovascular events (MACE)2,3.

Sodium-glucose cotransporter 2 inhibitors (SGLT2-i) represent a novel class of oral antidiabetic agents originally developed to improve glycemic control in patients with T2DM. Their hypoglycemic effect is achieved by blocking SGLT2 in the proximal convoluted tubules of the kidney, which leads to the elimination of excess glucose4. Recent studies have indicated several cardiovascular benefits of SGLT2-i that extend beyond their primary role in glycemic control. Several large-scale randomized clinical trials (such as CANVAS5, EMPA‑REG OUTCOME6, DECLARE‑TIMI 587) and meta-analyses8–10 have demonstrated that the addition of SGLT2-i to existing oral hypoglycemic agents can reduce the incidence of myocardial infarction (MI), decrease the rehospitalization rate for HF, and lower cardiovascular mortality. Furthermore, reductions in cardiovascular death and hospitalization for HF was observed in symptomatic chronic HF patients, regardless of diabetes status, across a wide range of left ventricular ejection fraction (LVEF). The pleiotropic effects of SGLT2-i beyond glycemic control have prompted investigations into their potential utility in AMI. Preclinical studies suggest that the cardioprotective mechanisms of SGLT2-i include improvements in myocardial energetics, attenuation of oxidative stress and inflammation, modulation of cardiac remodeling, and enhancement of vascular function11. These findings lay the groundwork for clinical research to evaluate the impact of SGLT2-i on the prognosis of AMI patients.

In recent years, both preclinical experiments12,13 and clinical studies14–16 have demonstrated the cardioprotective effects of SGLT2-i in animal models of myocardial ischemia–reperfusion and patients with AMI; however, the outcomes have not been consistent16–19. Therefore, we performed a meta-analysis of data from clinical trials to evaluate the effect of SGLT2-i on all-cause death, rehospitalization for HF, cardiovascular death, and the occurrence of nonfatal MI in patients with AMI. Our findings may provide strong evidence for using SGLT2-i in patients with AMI.

Methods

Search strategy

This meta-analysis was performed in accordance with the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-analyses (PRISMA) statement20. A systemic literature search was independently conducted by two authors across the PubMed, Web of Science, The Cochrane Library, and Embase databases. The broad search keywords (“sodium-glucose co-transporter 2 inhibitors”, “SGLT2 inhibitors”, “SGLT2-i”, “empagliflozin”, “dapagliflozin”, “canagliflozin”, “acute myocardial infarction” and “AMI”) were utilized to identify eligible studies evaluating the efficacy of SGLT2 inhibitors following AMI. This study was registered with the International Prospective Register of Systematic Reviews (PROSPERO) (https://www.crd.york.ac.uk/PROSPERO/) and the registration number is CRD42024550538.

Study selection criteria

The inclusion criteria were based on the PICOS (Patients, Intervention, Comparison, Outcome, and Study design) principle: (I) P: AMI; (II) I and C: SGLT2-i vs placebo; (III) O: all-cause death, rehospitalization for HF, cardiovascular death, occurrence of nonfatal MI; and (IV) S: randomized controlled trials (RCTs) or non-RCTs. Studies published in the English language until May 2024 were eligible for inclusion. Additionally, reference lists of relevant articles were manually screened to identify further studies.

The following studies were excluded: (1) duplicated publications; (2) animal researches, case reports, clinical trial design and rationale, reviews, correspondence, and letters; (3) inappropriate outcome measures.

Study outcomes

The primary outcomes were single procedure: 1) all-cause death and 2) rehospitalization for HF. The secondary outcomes were: 1) cardiovascular death and 2) occurrence of nonfatal MI.

Data collection and quality assessment

Data were independently extracted by two authors (B X and L-M H) and reviewed by a third author (A Z). Details regarding the study, including publication year, study design, SGLT2-i category, dose of SGLT2-i, outcomes, and duration of follow-up, were collected. The quality of the included studies was assessed using the guidelines of the Cochrane Handbook and the PRISMA statement20,21.The following aspects were evaluated for quality assessment: (i) randomization; (ii) double-blinding; (iii) clearly defined study population; (iv) clearly defined groups for comparison; (v) clearly defined outcome measures; (vi) use of appropriate statistical methods; (vii) no selective loss of data; and (viii) identification of important confounders. Studies that met six or more of these criteria were considered to be of high methodological quality.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analyses were performed using the Cochrane Review Manager Version 5.4.1 (Copenhagen, The Nordic Cochrane Centre, the Cochrane Collaboration) and the SPSS 26.0 (Chicago, IL, USA). Categorical variables were described as percentages and the pooled effect size was estimated by calculating risk ratio (RR) with a two-tailed 95% confidence interval (CI). Heterogeneity among the studies was assessed using the I2 value determined by the Q test. The level of heterogeneity was classified as follows: (1) I2 = 0%–25%: low heterogeneity; (2) I2 = 25%–50%: moderate heterogeneity; (3) I2 = 50%–75%: substantial heterogeneity; and (4) I2 = 75%–100%: considerable heterogeneity22. In case of low or moderate heterogeneity (I2 < 50%), the fixed effect model was used for meta-analysis; otherwise, the random effect model was employed. The number needed to treat (NNT) values were calculated according to the method outlined in prior literature23. Two-tailed P values < 0.05 were considered indicative of statistical significance.

Primary outcomes

The pooled results showed that SGLT2-i did not significantly reduce all-cause death in AMI patients compared to placebo (RR: 0.85, 95% CI 0.72–1.00, P = 0.05, I2 = 46%, Fig. 2). Subgroup analysis

Due to moderate heterogeneity of the primary outcomes (I2 < 50%), the fixed effects model was used to pool effect sizes. However, substantial heterogeneity existed in the pooled analysis of nonfatal MI (I2 > 50%); therefore, a random effects model was used.

In the subgroup analysis of RCTs, compared to placebo, SGLT2-i did not significantly reduce the risk of all-cause death (RR: 1.00, 95% CI 0.83–1.21, P = 0.39, I2 = 0%, Fig. 2; NNT = 9083), cardiovascular death (RR: 1.04, 95% CI 0.84–1.29, P = 0.74, I2 = 0%, Fig. 3; NNT = 988), or the occurrence of nonfatal MI(RR: 1.2, 95% CI 0.73–1.71, P = 0.61, I2 not applicable, Fig. 4; NNT = 445), except that reduced the rehospitalization for HF (RR: 0.73, 95% CI 0.61–0.89, P = 0.001, I2 = 0%, Fig. 3; NNT = 84).

We performed a subgroup analysis based on SGLT2-i category to explore potential sources of heterogeneity. A significantly decreased risk of rehospitalization for HF was observed in both empagliflozin (RR: 0.72, 95% CI 0.58–0.88, P = 0.001; I2 = 0%, Fig. S2A) and dapagliflozin (RR: 0.58, 95%CI 0.37–0.91, P = 0.02, I2 = 63%, Fig. S2A) subgroups. However, there were no significant differences regarding all-cause death, cardiovascular death, and nonfatal MI (Fig. S1A, Fig. S3A, and Fig. S4A).

Subsequently, a subgroup analysis was conducted based on the duration of follow-up (less than 6 months vs. more than 6 months). The results indicated that SGLT2-i significantly reduced the risk of all-cause death (RR: 0.81, 95% CI 0.60–0.81, P = 0.01, I2 = 66%; Fig. S1B) and rehospitalization for HF (RR: 0.69, 95% CI 0.69–0.95, P < 0.00001, I2 = 59%; Fig. S2B) when the duration of follow-up exceeded 6 months. However, no significant differences were noted regarding cardiovascular death and nonfatal MI, irrespective of the duration of follow-up. (Fig. S3B and Fig. S4B).

According to the Cochrane Handbook for Systematic Reviews of Interventions, we did not conduct meta-regression analysis due to no more than 10 studies were included28.

Discussion

To our knowledge, the present study represents the first meta-analysis investigating the impact of SGLT2-i treatment on all-cause death and rehospitalization for HF in patients with AMI. Our findings demonstrate a significant reduction in rehospitalization for HF with SGLT2-i compared to placebo during the follow-up period. However, no significant risk reduction was observed for all-cause death, cardiovascular death or nonfatal MI during this time.

SGLT2 inhibitors are a class of antidiabetic medications that have been proven to reduce the risk of hospitalization for HF and cardiovascular death, regardless of the presence of diabetes or the level of LVEF29–32. Therefore, SGLT2 inhibitors are recommended for patients with T2DM and HF to mitigate the risks of HF, MACE, and cardiovascular death. Additionally, current diabetes guidelines suggest considering the use of SGLT2 inhibitors for individuals with T2DM who are at high risk of atherosclerotic cardiovascular disease or have already been diagnosed33, owing to their efficacy in reducing cardiovascular events. With the continuous emergence of findings from basic researches and clinical trials, increasing evidence supports the application of SGLT2 inhibitors in AMI, igniting new hope for treatment of AMI34,35.

Although the EMPACT-MI17 and DAPA-MI18 studies produced conflicting results on whether SGLT2-i reduced the risk of rehospitalization of HF in patients with AMI, we found in our study that SGLT2i reduced the rehospitalization of HF in patients with AMI by 31%. Post-AMI left ventricular enlargement and remodeling are known to lead to HF. Furthermore, these patients have an increased risk of cardiovascular and cerebrovascular events, poor prognosis, mortality, and hospitalization. Consequently, treatment and intervention in the acute phase of MI are particularly important. However, the exact mechanism by which SGLT2 inhibitors reduce rehospitalization of HF in patients with AMI has not been fully elucidated, and we suspect that it may be related to the following factors. Previous studies have shown that the use of SGLT2-i in patients with HF is associated with a significant reduction in all-cause mortality and hospitalization rates, potentially mediated by the favorable hemodynamic effects of SGLT2-i36. In addition, SGLT2 inhibitors have shown significant improvements in echocardiographic functional and structural parameters14. Moreover, SGLT2-i have also been associated with reductions in sympathetic nerve activity25, improvements in body fluid balance37, anti-fibrotic effects, antioxidant properties34, anti-inflammatory effects38, alleviate cardiomyocyte apoptosis and necrosis39, improve endothelial dysfunction40,41, and uninterrupted molecular interactions with cardiomyocytes, thereby significantly improving myocardial energy and activating protective downstream pathways11,34.

Our meta-analysis results also indicate that the SGLT2-i did not significantly reduce all-cause mortality or cardiovascular mortality in patients with AMI. Two studies by Paolisso et al.19 and Kwon et al.16 demonstrated that the use of SGLT2 inhibitors after AMI could lower all-cause mortality; however, both studies were cohort studies and thus carry a higher risk of bias. The two largest RCTs, EMPACT-MI17 and DAPA-MI18, found no reduction in all-cause mortality or cardiovascular mortality in AMI patients treated with SGLT2-i. Reasons for this include the exclusion of patients with diabetes or chronic HF in the DAPA-MI trial18,42, and t the EMPACT-MI trial being conducted during the COVID-19 pandemic and in regions affected by war conflict17. Additionally, the average follow-up duration for the two RCTs were less than 2 years, while previous studies, EMPA-REG OUTCOME6 and DECLARE–TIMI 587, showed significant reductions in cardiovascular events in the SGLT2-i group with follow-ups exceeding 3 years. Furthermore, timely and effective reperfusion in AMI patients salvages ischemic myocardium, and standardized secondary prevention treatment according to guidelines may diminish the additional benefits of SGLT2-i. In summary, these factors may all influence the results of this study.

Inflammation plays an important role in the pathophysiology of coronary heart disease (CHD). Previous studies38,43,44 have found that SGLT2-i can reduce the serum levels of interleukin-6, tumor necrosis factor-α, C-reactive protein, monocyte chemotactic protein-1, and intercellular adhesion molecule-1, thereby exerting anti-inflammatory effects; however, the EMMY trial did not obtained a similar results45. Another study indicated that SGLT2-i can delay the progression of atherosclerosis and improve the stability of atherosclerotic plaques by inhibiting the infiltration of mononuclear macrophages46. In addition, SGLT2-i improve coronary endothelial barrier function47–49. These effects suggest that SGLT2-i may reduce the incidence of MI and alleviate ischemia–reperfusion injury. However, in the present meta-analysis, while there was a positive downward trend in nonfatal MI in the SGLT2-i group compared to the placebo group, the between-group difference was not statistically significant. Subsequent subgroup analyses showed no significant differences in nonfatal MI between the two groups, either by SGLT2-i category or duration of follow-up. This may be attributable to the low dose of SGLT2-i and the significant improvement of prognosis of AMI patients with early percutaneous coronary intervention, which may also reduce the effect of SGLT2 inhibitors26. Further research is required to confirm this.

Limitations

Some limitations of this study should be acknowledged. First, only 6 of the 10 clinical studies included in this meta-analysis were RCTs14,17,18,24–26, while the remaining four were cohort studies15,16,19,27. This may have contributed to the observed heterogeneity. Second, different SGLT2 inhibitors were used in the included studies, which may have led to significant heterogeneity in secondary endpoint outcomes. However, subgroup analyses based on SGLT2-i category indicated no significant effect of the type of SGLT2-i on secondary outcomes. Third, the duration of follow-up was inconsistent and the timespan was large. Four studies14,24–26 involved a follow-up of less than 6 months, and 6 studies15–19,27 had a follow-up of more than 12 months. However, subgroup analysis by duration of follow-up showed no significant change in secondary outcomes. Fourth, the inconsistent timing of SGLT2-i administration between studies may lead to cautious interpretation of this meta-analysis. However, post hoc analysis of the EMMY study demonstrated that early administration of SGLT2i after AMI showed no adverse safety signal and appeared to be as effective in reducing N-terminal pro-brain natriuretic peptide (NT-proBNP) and improving structural and functional LV markers as starting treatment 2–3 days later50. Nonetheless, this study did not analyze the all-cause death, cardiovascular death and rehospitalization for HF in SGLT2-i group, and further studies may be needed to confirm this. Finally, only research articles published in English were included, which may have introduced an element of bias.

Conclusions

The present study demonstrates that SGLT2-i significantly reduced rehospitalization for HF in AMI patients compared to placebo. However, no significant risk reduction was observed for all-cause death, cardiovascular death or nonfatal MI, and the findings were further confirmed by subsequent RCTs requiring longer follow-up.
