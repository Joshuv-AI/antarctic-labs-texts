Max-Min Bilinear Completely Positive Programs: A Semidefinite Relaxation with Tightness Guarantees
Sarah Yini Gao, Xindong Tang, Yancheng Yuan

Abstract
. Max–min bilinear optimization models, where one agent maximizes and an adversary minimizes a common bilinear objective, serve as canonical saddle-point formulations in optimization theory. They capture, among others, two-player zero-sum games, robust and distributionally robust optimization, and adversarial machine learning. This study focuses on the subclass whose variables lie in the completely positive (CP) cone, capturing a broad family of mixed-binary quadratic max–min problems through the modelling power of completely positive programming. We show that such problems admit an equivalent single-stage linear reformulation over the COP–CP cone, defined as the Cartesian product of the copositive (COP) and CP cones. Because testing membership in COP cones is co-NP-complete, the resulting COP-CP program inherits NP-hardness. To address this challenge, we develop a hierarchy of semidefinite relaxations based on moment and sum-of-squares representations of the COP and CP cones, and flat truncation conditions are applied to certify the tightness. We show that the tightness of the hierarchy is guaranteed under mild conditions. The framework extends existing CP/COP approaches for distributionally robust optimization and polynomial games. We apply the framework to the cyclic Colonel Blotto game, an extension of Borel’s classic allocation contest. Across multiple instances, the semidefinite relaxation meets the flat-truncation conditions and solves the exact mixed-strategy equilibrium.

1.
Introduction

Max-min bilinear optimization problems, which pit one decision-maker’s maximization against another’s minimization over a bilinear objective, are fundamental in both mathematical theory and optimization. From a theoretical perspective, these problems, coupled with the corresponding min-max problem, solve the saddle-point solutions that equalize the max-min and min-max values under appropriate convexity-concavity conditions. Such saddle-point frameworks are central to duality in convex optimization, where solving the saddle point of a Lagrangian corresponds to addressing both primal and dual problems simultaneously. Equally important is the practical significance of max-min models: in game theory, for instance, a two-player zero-sum game can be modeled as a max-min bilinear problem (e.g. the payoff matrix game), with each player’s optimal strategy leading to a saddle-point equilibrium as ensured by von Neumann’s minimax theorem. Likewise, robust decision-making and control often employ a worst-case max-min criterion, effectively treating nature as the minimizing player to ensure solutions perform well under the most adverse conditions. In machine learning, adversarial settings such as generative adversarial networks (GANs) cast training as a max-min game between competing models, a formulation that is inherently a bilinear (player vs. opponent) optimization at its core.
The bilinear structure in all these applications is especially noteworthy: it provides a tractable yet expressive representation of strategic interactions, enabling the use of saddle-point analysis and strong equilibrium guarantees. Given the theoretical depth and broad applicability of max-min bilinear optimization problems, advancing our understanding of their structural properties, computational complexity, and algorithmic solutions remains a central and impactful direction in both optimization theory and applied decision sciences.

In this paper, we specifically consider
a max-min bilinear optimization problem over completely positive cones (CP cones).

We will formally define this problem with more details in Section
3.1

Specifically, the CP cone is the convex cone of completely positive matrices.
It has drawn significant attention in recent years due to the development of completely positive programs (CPPs), which have been shown to be powerful reformulations for many NP-hard problems. In particular, a CPP is defined as a linear program over the CP cones, as proven effective in modeling, for instance, standard quadratic programs

To solve the max-min bilinear optimization over CP cones, we show that it can be reformulated as a single-stage linear optimization problem over the

) is

(8)

\begin{array}[]{cl}\min\limits_{Y\in\mathcal{S}^{m}}&\left<\mathcal{Q}(X),Y\right>\\
{s.t.}&\left<B_{i},Y\right>={c}_{i},\quad i=1,...,J,\\
&Y\succcurlyeq_{cp}0.\end{array}

By introducing dual variables
\bm{z}\in\mathbb{R}^{J}
and
Z\in\mathcal{S}^{m}
with
Z\succcurlyeq_{co}0
, the Lagrangian of (
8
) is

L(Y,\bm{z},Z;X)=\langle\mathcal{Q}(X),Y\rangle-\sum_{i=1}^{J}z_{i}(\langle B_{i},Y\rangle-c_{i})-\langle Y,Z\rangle.

Thus, the Lagrangian dual problem of (
8
) reads

(9)

\begin{array}[]{cl}\max\limits_{\bm{z}\in\mathbb{R}^{J},Z\in\mathcal{S}^{m}}&\bm{c}^{\mathsf{T}}\bm{z}\\
{s.t.}&\displaystyle\mathcal{Q}(X)-\sum_{j=1}^{J}z_{j}B_{j}=Z,\quad Z\succcurlyeq_{co}0.\end{array}

Combining the outer maximization and the dual of the inner minimization yields the following linear program over COP-CP cone:

(10)

\begin{array}[]{ccl}\mathcal{W}_{\max}^{*}\coloneqq&\max\limits_{X,\bm{z},Z}&\bm{c}^{\mathsf{T}}\bm{z}\\
&{s.t.}&\left<A_{i},X\right>={b}_{i},\quad i=1,...,I,\\
&&\displaystyle\mathcal{Q}(X)-\sum_{j=1}^{J}z_{j}B_{j}=Z,\\
&&X\succcurlyeq_{cp}0,\ Z\succcurlyeq_{co}0,\ X\in\mathcal{S}^{n},\ \bm{z}\in{\mathbb{R}^{J}},\ Z\in\mathcal{S}^{m}.\end{array}

Similarly, for the associated min-max problem (
3
),
its COP-CP linear program counterpart is

(11)

\begin{array}[]{ccl}\mathcal{W}_{\min}^{*}\coloneqq&\min\limits_{Y,\bm{w},W}&\bm{b}^{\mathsf{T}}\bm{w}\\
&{s.t.}&\left<B_{j},Y\right>={c}_{j},\quad j=1,...,J,\\
&&\displaystyle\sum_{i=1}^{I}w_{i}A_{i}-\mathcal{Q}^{\star}(Y)=W,\\
&&Y\succcurlyeq_{cp}0,\ W\succcurlyeq_{co}0,\ Y\in\mathcal{S}^{m},\ {\bm{w}\in\mathbb{R}^{I}},\ W\in\mathcal{S}^{n}.\end{array}

Here,
\mathcal{Q}^{\star}
denotes the adjoint operator of
\mathcal{Q}
.
The following proposition establishes an ascending chain of optimal values for the above problems.

Proposition 1
.

The maximization problem (
10
) and the minimization problem (
11
) are dual to each other, and

\mathcal{W}_{\max}^{*}\leq\mathcal{Z}^{*}_{\max\min}\leq\mathcal{Z}^{*}_{\min\max}\leq\mathcal{W}_{\min}^{*}.

We remark that if Assumption
1
(a) presented below holds, then there is no duality gap between the CP program (
8
) and COP program (
9
) for any
X
, likewise, under Assumption
1
(b) for the min-max counterpart.

Assumption 1
.

(a)

For the symmetric matrices
B_{1},\ldots,B_{J}
, there exist
z_{1},\ldots,z_{J}
such that
\sum\limits_{j=1}^{J}z_{j}B_{j}\succ_{co}0
.

(b)

For the symmetric matrices
A_{1},\ldots,A_{I}
, there exist
w_{1},\ldots,w_{I}
such that
\sum\limits_{i=1}^{I}w_{i}A_{i}\succ_{co}0
.

Lemma 4
.

Under Assumption
1
(a),
\mathcal{Z}^{*}_{\max\min}=\mathcal{W}^{*}_{\max}
. Likewise, under Assumption
1
(b),
\mathcal{Z}^{*}_{\min\max}=\mathcal{W}^{*}_{\min}
.

A simple sufficient condition ensuring condition in Assumption
1
(a) holds (and thus, strong duality) is that at least one
B_{j}
for
j\in[J]
is
strictly copositive
.
By analogous reasoning, if at least one
A_{i}
for
i\in[I]
is strictly copositive, then
\mathcal{Z}^{*}_{\min\max}=\mathcal{W}_{\min}^{*}
.

3.2.
Application: Zero-Sum Bi-Quadratic Games

We illustrate an application of the bilinear optimization over CP cones to model a two-person zero-sum game with
bi-quadratic
payoff functions and mixed-binary variables.
Let
{\bm{s}}_{1}\in\mathbb{R}^{l_{1}}
and
{\bm{s}}_{2}\in\mathbb{R}^{l_{2}}
be the strategy vectors of Players 1 and 2, respectively, and let
\mathbb{B}_{1}\subseteq[{l_{1}}]
,
\mathbb{B}_{2}\subseteq[{l_{2}}]
be the index sets of binary variables. The feasible sets for
{\bm{s}}_{1}
and
{\bm{s}}_{2}
are given as
\mathcal{S}_{1}
and
\mathcal{S}_{2}
defined as follows.

\displaystyle\mathcal{S}_{1}:=

\displaystyle\{{\bm{s}}_{1}\in\mathbb{R}^{l_{1}}:G{\bm{s}}_{1}=\bm{g},\ {\bm{s}}_{1}\geq 0,\ {\bm{s}}_{1,i}\in\{0,1\}\ \ \forall i\in\mathbb{B}_{1}\},

\displaystyle\mathcal{S}_{2}:=

\displaystyle\{{\bm{s}}_{2}\in\mathbb{R}^{l_{2}}:H{\bm{s}}_{2}=\bm{h},\ {\bm{s}}_{2}\geq 0,\ {\bm{s}}_{2,j}\in\{0,1\}\ \forall j\in\mathbb{B}_{2}\}.

We consider a zero-sum game that Player 1 aims to maximize, while Player 2 wants to minimize, the following bi-quadratic payoff function:

(12)

\begin{array}[]{rll}P({\bm{s}}_{1},{\bm{s}}_{2})\coloneqq{\bm{s}}_{1}^{\mathsf{T}}C{\bm{s}}_{2}+\operatorname{vec}({\bm{s}}_{1}{\bm{s}}_{1}^{\mathsf{T}})^{\mathsf{T}}F\operatorname{vec}({\bm{s}}_{2}{\bm{s}}_{2}^{\mathsf{T}}).\end{array}

In the above,
C\in\mathbb{R}^{l_{1}\times l_{2}}
and
F\in\mathbb{R}^{l_{1}^{2}\times l_{2}^{2}}
are matrices that encode the first and the second order interactions, respectively, in the payoff function.

Typically, the game described in the above does not have a
pure strategy
solution, i.e., a pair of vectors
({\bm{s}}_{1}^{*},{\bm{s}}_{2}^{*})\in\mathcal{S}_{1}\times\mathcal{S}_{2}
such that the following holds for all :

P({\bm{s}}_{1},{\bm{s}}_{2}^{*})\leq P({\bm{s}}_{1}^{*},{\bm{s}}_{2}^{*})\leq P({\bm{s}}_{1}^{*},{\bm{s}}_{2}),\quad\forall{\bm{s}}_{1}\in\mathcal{S}_{1},\ {\bm{s}}_{2}\in\mathcal{S}_{2}.

Therefore, one usually consider
mixed strategies
for the variables
{\bm{s}}_{1}
and
{\bm{s}}_{2}
.
Let
\mathcal{M}_{1}
(respectively,
\mathcal{M}_{2}
) be the set of probability measures supported on
\mathcal{S}_{1}
(respectively,
\mathcal{S}_{2}
).
A mixed strategy pair
(\mu_{1},\mu_{2})\in\mathcal{M}_{1}\times\mathcal{M}_{2}
induces the expected payoff

\displaystyle U(\mu_{1},\mu_{2})\,:=

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{(\mu_{1},\mu_{2})}\left[{\bm{s}}_{1}^{\mathsf{T}}C{\bm{s}}_{2}+\operatorname{vec}({\bm{s}}_{1}{\bm{s}}_{1}^{\mathsf{T}})^{\mathsf{T}}F\operatorname{vec}({\bm{s}}_{2}{\bm{s}}_{2}^{\mathsf{T}})\right]

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{\mu_{1}}[{\bm{s}}_{1}]^{\mathsf{T}}C\mathbb{E}_{\mu_{2}}[{\bm{s}}_{2}]+\operatorname{vec}(\mathbb{E}_{\mu_{1}}[{\bm{s}}_{1}{\bm{s}}_{1}^{\mathsf{T}}])^{\mathsf{T}}F\operatorname{vec}(\mathbb{E}_{\mu_{2}}[{\bm{s}}_{2}{\bm{s}}_{2}^{\mathsf{T}}]).

In this sequel, we consider the
mixed-strategy bi-quadratic game
that aims to find a pair of probability measures
(\mu_{1}^{*},\mu_{2}^{*})\in\mathcal{M}_{1}\times\mathcal{M}_{2}
such that

(13)

U(\mu_{1},\mu_{2}^{*})\leq U(\mu_{1}^{*},\mu_{2}^{*})\leq U(\mu_{1}^{*},\mu_{2}),\quad\mbox{for all}\quad\mu_{1}\in\mathcal{M}_{1},\ \mu_{2}\in\mathcal{M}_{2}.

Such a pair
(\mu_{1}^{*},\mu_{2}^{*})
is called a (mixed-strategy)
Nash equilibrium
.

3.2.1.
Motivation: Finite Games with Complementary Actions

In this motivation example, we illustrate that the zero-sum bi-quadratic game formulation arises when we consider a finite two-player zero-sum game where the payoff depends on
complementary
actions, that is, the payoff is determined based on pairs of actions chosen by each player.
For such kinds of games, the strategy space is defined as containing individual actions, and the complementarity is explicitly captured in the payoff expression given as in (
12
).
We will show that the resulting bi-quadratic game admits an equivalent reformulation as a bilinear problem over CP cones, and the methods developed in this paper provide a clear path to its solution. In doing so, we offer a framework for tackling certain large-scale games with complementary effects for which no efficient algorithms may not be readily available. The following example, a cyclic Blotto game, provides a relevant context for this type of game.

Example: Cyclic Blotto Game.
The Colonel Blotto game is a two-person-zero-sum game originated from the pioneering work of
5
. The game considers two players, a defender and an attacker, simultaneously allocating limited resources across a number of locations. At each location, the player allocating more resources wins the location, and the payoff of each player depends on the total number of locations he wins.
The
cyclic Blotto game
is a variation of the classic Colonel Blotto game, defined as follows. Suppose the defender has
R_{1}
resources and the attacker has
R_{2}
resources.
They simultaneously allocate their resources to
N
locations arranged in a circle.
On each location, if the defender has more resources than the attacker, then they win that location and gain a payoff of 1; and if they have the same number of resources, then they both accrue 0 payoff.
Additionally, the defender has an advantage: if his resources on location
i
are fewer than the attacker’s, they can use surplus resources from the adjacent location on the left,
i-1
(by abuse of notation, let
i-1\coloneqq N
if
i=1
), to support location
i
.
If the combined resources exceed the attacker’s, the defender still wins the location and gains a payoff of 1. Otherwise, the attacker wins, and the defender’s payoff is
-1
.
In the case where both players have equal resources on a location after considering the defender’s support, the result is a tie, and the defender receives a payoff of 0.
It is clear that this forms a zero-sum game, such that defender aims to maximize their payoff, and the attacker aims to minimize it.
Figure 1 provides an example of a cyclic Blotto game with 5 locations, and each player has 5 units of resources. Suppose the defender’s and attacker’s resource allocations are as shown in the figure, where numbers next to each node on the inner side of the ring indicate the attacker’s resource allocation, whereas numbers on the outer side indicate the defender’s resource allocation. The defender earns a payoff of
1
at locations 1 and 3, where its resources exceed the attacker’s. At location 2, although the defender has no local resources, effective support from location 1 allows it to earn a payoff of
1
. At location 4, support from location 3 only equalizes total resources, yielding zero payoff for both players. At location 5, support from location 4 is insufficient, and the defender loses the location, receiving a payoff of
-1
.

It is well‑known that the classic Colonel Blotto game is computationally challenging due to the exponentially large number of pure strategies when they are defined as allocation profiles across
N
locations.
The cyclic variant inherits this curse of dimensionality and adds a further complication:
because resources can be redeployed around the ring,
adjacent allocations represent the complementary effect.
This complementary effect creates correlations among allocations on each location, which makes the currently proposed solution methodologies for the classic Colonel Blotto game relying on separability
(
1
, e.g.,)
not directly applicable to this cyclic extension.

In the following, we present a biquadratic game formulation of the cyclic game using binary variables.
For every
k=0,1,\ldots,E_{1}
and each
i=1,\ldots,N
,
let
s_{1,k,i}
be the indicator variable that takes the value
1
if the defender deploys
k
resources at location
i
, and it equals zero otherwise.
Similarly, we define
s_{2,l,j}
for the attacker’s allocation of
l
resources at location
j
, for each
0\leq l\leq E_{2}
and
j=1,\ldots,N
.
Denote by

\bm{s}_{1}\coloneqq(s_{1,k,i})_{k=0,1,\ldots,E_{1},\,i=1,\ldots,N},\quad\bm{s}_{2}\coloneqq(s_{2,l,j})_{l=0,1,\ldots,E_{2},\,j=1,\ldots,N}.

For feasible allocations
\bm{s}_{1}
and
\bm{s}_{2}
,
the sum of all allocations cannot exceed the total number of resources.
Moreover, for each location
i
,
\sum\limits_{k=0}^{E_{1}}s_{1,k,i}=\sum\limits_{l=0}^{E_{2}}s_{2,l,i}=1
since allocation strategies at one single location are mutually exclusive.
Thus, the feasible pure strategies for the defender and the attacker are given by the following two polyhedra, respectively:

\mathcal{S}_{1}\coloneqq\left\{\bm{s}_{1}\in\{0,1\}^{N(E_{1}+1)}\left|\ \begin{array}[]{lll}\sum\limits_{i=1}^{N}\sum\limits_{k=0}^{E_{1}}ks_{1,k,i}\leq E_{1},\ \sum\limits_{k=0}^{E_{1}}s_{1,k,1}=\cdots=\sum\limits_{k=0}^{E_{1}}s_{1,k,N}=1\par\end{array}\right.\right\},

\mathcal{S}_{2}\coloneqq\left\{\bm{s}_{2}\in\{0,1\}^{N(E_{2}+1)}\left|\ \begin{array}[]{lll}\sum\limits_{j=1}^{N}\sum\limits_{l=0}^{E_{2}}ls_{2,l,j}\leq E_{2},\ \sum\limits_{l=0}^{E_{2}}s_{2,l,1}=\cdots=\sum\limits_{l=0}^{E_{2}}s_{2,l,N}=1\end{array}\right.\right\}.

For the given feasible allocations
\bm{s}_{1}\in\mathcal{S}_{1}
and
\bm{s}_{2}\in\mathcal{S}_{2}
, if the defender allocates more resources than the attacker at location
i
,
then the defender win location
i
directly and payoff equals
1
, which can be reflected by

P_{i}^{direct}(\bm{s}_{1},\bm{s}_{2})\coloneqq\sum\limits_{k>l}s_{1,k,i}s_{2,l,i}.

Otherwise, player 1 can seek support from location
i-1
, and the payoff in these cases becomes

\begin{array}[]{c}P_{i}^{support}(\bm{s}_{1},\bm{s}_{2})\coloneqq\sum\limits_{k<l,k+{k^{\prime}}>l+l^{\prime}}s_{1,k,i}s_{1,k^{\prime},(i-1)}s_{2,l,i}s_{2,l^{\prime},(i-1)}\\
\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad-\sum\limits_{k<l,k+{k^{\prime}}<l+l^{\prime}}s_{1,k,i}s_{1,k^{\prime},(i-1)}s_{2,l,i}s_{2,l^{\prime},(i-1)}.\end{array}

Then, it is clear that the defender’s total payoff is

P(\bm{s}_{1},\bm{s}_{2})=\sum_{i=1}^{N}\big(P_{i}^{direct}(\bm{s}_{1},\bm{s}_{2})+P_{i}^{support}(\bm{s}_{1},\bm{s}_{2})\big).

The payoff function contains not only bilinear terms (given by
P_{i}^{direct}
),
but also bi-quadratic terms resulting from the complementary actions taken at two adjacent locations (i.e.,
P_{i}^{support}
).
Concrete examples with numerical results for cyclic Blotto games are presented in Section
5
.

3.2.2.
Max-Min Bilinear CPP Reformulations

Consider the bi-quadratic game defined in this section. Let
\mathcal{L}_{1}
and
\mathcal{L}_{2}
denotes the polyhedra
\{{\bm{s}}_{1}\in\mathbb{R}^{l_{1}}_{+}:G{\bm{s}}_{1}=\bm{g}\}
and
\{{\bm{s}}_{2}\in\mathbb{R}^{l_{2}}_{+}:H{\bm{s}}_{2}=\bm{h}\}
, respectively.
We first introduce the following blanket assumption, which we maintain for the rest of this sequel:

Assumption 2
.

If
u\in\mathcal{L}_{1}
and
v\in\mathcal{L}_{2}
, then
0\leq u_{i}\leq 1
,
0\leq v_{j}\leq 1
for all
i\in\mathbb{B}_{1}
and
j\in\mathbb{B}_{2}
.

We remark that Assumption
2
can be enforced without loss of generality by adding constraints
u_{i}\leq 1
and
v_{j}\leq 1
, for all
i\in\mathbb{B}_{1}
and
j\in\mathbb{B}_{2}
in
\mathcal{L}_{1}
and
\mathcal{L}_{2}
, respectively (slack variables are added to convert these constraints to equalities); see
6
.
This framework naturally includes cases where
{\bm{s}}_{1}
and
{\bm{s}}_{2}
are binary.
The linear constraints in
\mathcal{S}_{1}
and
\mathcal{S}_{2}
are general enough to incorporate side constraints on the choices of strategies.

Consider now the following
pure strategy
bilinear game over the CP cones, seeking pairs
((\bm{p},P),(\bm{q},Q))\in\mathcal{C}_{cp}^{1}\times\mathcal{C}_{cp}^{2}
, where
\mathcal{C}_{cp}^{1}
and
\mathcal{C}_{cp}^{2}
are defined as below

(14)

\mathcal{C}_{cp}^{1}=\left\{\begin{array}[]{cc}(\bm{p},P)\in\mathbb{R}^{l_{1}}\times\mathcal{S}^{l_{1}}&\left|\begin{array}[]{cc}G\bm{p}=\bm{g},\\
diag(GP{G}^{\mathsf{T}})=\bm{g}\bullet\bm{g},\\
{p}_{1,i}-P_{i,i}=0,\ \forall i\in\mathbb{B}_{1},\\
\left[\begin{array}[]{cc}1&{\bm{p}}^{\mathsf{T}}\\
\bm{p}&P\end{array}\right]\succcurlyeq_{cp}0\end{array}\right.\end{array}\right\},

(15)

\mathcal{C}_{cp}^{2}=\left\{\begin{array}[]{cc}(\bm{q},Q)\in\mathbb{R}^{l_{2}}\times\mathcal{S}^{l_{2}}&\left|\begin{array}[]{cc}H\bm{q}=\bm{h},\\
diag(HQ{H}^{\mathsf{T}})=\bm{h}\bullet\bm{h},\\
{q}_{2,j}-Q_{j,j}=0,\ \forall j\in\mathbb{B}_{2},\\
\left[\begin{array}[]{cc}1&{\bm{q}}^{\mathsf{T}}\\
\bm{q}&Q\end{array}\right]\succcurlyeq_{cp}0\end{array}\right.\end{array}\right\}.

Let
\mathcal{F}
be the linear operator such that

\left<\mathcal{F}(P),Q\right>=\left<P,\mathcal{F}^{*}(Q)\right>=\operatorname{vec}(P)^{\mathsf{T}}F\operatorname{vec}(Q)
for all
P
and
Q
.
Define the bilinear payoff function

V((\bm{p},P),(\bm{q},Q))\,:=\,\bm{p}^{\mathsf{T}}C\bm{q}+\left<\mathcal{F}(P),Q\right>=\bm{p}^{\mathsf{T}}C\bm{q}+\left<P,\mathcal{F}^{\star}(Q)\right>.

We say
((\bm{p}^{*},P^{*}),(\bm{q}^{*},Q^{*}))
is a Nash equilibrium of this bilinear CP game if

(16)

V((\bm{p},P),(\bm{q}^{*},Q^{*}))\leq V((\bm{p}^{*},P^{*}),(\bm{q}^{*},Q^{*}))\leq V((\bm{p}^{*},P^{*}),(\bm{q},Q))

for all
(\bm{p},P)\in\mathcal{C}_{cp}^{1}
and for all
(\bm{q},Q)\in\mathcal{C}_{cp}^{2}
.
The following theorem shows that this bilinear game is equivalent to the bi-quadratic game defined by (
13
).

Theorem 1
.

For the bi-quadratic game (
13
), a pair of mixed strategy
(\mu_{1}^{*},\mu_{2}^{*})\in\mathcal{M}_{1}\times\mathcal{M}_{2}
is a Nash equilibrium if and only if there exists
((\bm{p}^{*},P^{*}),(\bm{q}^{*},Q^{*}))\in\mathcal{C}_{cp}^{1}\times\mathcal{C}_{cp}^{2}
such that
((\bm{p}^{*},P^{*}),(\bm{q}^{*},Q^{*}))
is a Nash equilibrium of (
16
). Moreover, for the Nash equilibrium of the two games, we can establish that
\mathbb{E}_{\mu_{1}^{*}}[{\bm{s}}_{1}]=\bm{p}^{*}
,
\mathbb{E}_{\mu^{*}_{2}}[{\bm{s}}_{2}]=\bm{q}^{*}
.

For the bilinear game (
16
), the minimum payoff for Player 1 is

(17)

\mathcal{V}_{1}^{*}\,:=\,\max_{(\bm{p},P)\in\mathcal{C}_{cp}^{1}}\min_{(\bm{q},Q)\in\mathcal{C}_{cp}^{2}}V((\bm{p},P),(\bm{q},Q)),

and the maximum cost for Player 2 is

(18)

\mathcal{V}_{2}^{*}\,:=\,\min_{(\bm{q},Q)\in\mathcal{C}_{cp}^{2}}\max_{(\bm{p},P)\in\mathcal{C}_{cp}^{1}}V((\bm{p},P),(\bm{q},Q)).

In general, it can be difficult to solve for Nash equilibria in either the bi-quadratic game (
13
) or the bilinear CP game (
16
) directly. Instead, one often studies these paired max–min and min–max problems.
By leveraging results in Sections
3.1
, one can formulate COP-CP programs for
\mathcal{V}_{1}^{*}
and
\mathcal{V}_{2}^{*}
. For instance,
\mathcal{V}_{1}^{*}
cam be cast in a form akin to

(19)

\begin{array}[]{rcl}\mathcal{U}_{1}^{cop-cp}\,:=&\max&\quad\rho+{\bm{h}}^{\mathsf{T}}\bm{r}+(\bm{h}\bullet\bm{h})^{\mathsf{T}}\bm{v}\\
&{s.t.}&\quad{(\bm{p},P)\in\mathcal{C}_{cp}^{1}}\\
&&\quad(\rho,\bm{r},\bm{v},\bm{\vartheta})\in\mathcal{C}_{cop}^{2}(\bm{p},P).\end{array}

A symmetric construction applies to
\mathcal{V}_{2}^{*}
.

(20)

\begin{array}[]{rcl}\mathcal{U}_{2}^{cop-cp}\,:=&\min&\quad\tau+{\bm{g}}^{\mathsf{T}}\bm{t}+(\bm{g}\bullet\bm{g})^{\mathsf{T}}\bm{u}\\
&{s.t.}&\quad{(\bm{q},Q)\in\mathcal{C}_{cp}^{2}}\\
&&\quad(\tau,\bm{t},\bm{u},\bm{\kappa})\in\mathcal{C}_{cop}^{1}(\bm{q},Q),\end{array}

where
\mathcal{C}_{cp}^{1}
,
C_{cp}^{2}
, are defined in (
14
), (
15
), respectively, and (let
\mathscr{R}_{i}\coloneqq\mathbb{R}\times\mathbb{R}^{d_{i}}\times\mathbb{R}^{d_{i}}\times\mathbb{R}^{l_{i}}
)

\mathcal{C}_{cop}^{1}(\bm{q},Q)\,:=\,\left\{\begin{array}[]{c}(\tau,\bm{t},\bm{u},\bm{\kappa})\\
\in\mathscr{R}_{1}\end{array}\left|\begin{gathered}\begin{bmatrix}-\tau&\frac{\bm{q}^{\mathsf{T}}C^{\mathsf{T}}-({G}^{\mathsf{T}}\bm{t}+\bm{\kappa})^{\mathsf{T}}}{2}\\
\\
\frac{C\bm{q}-(G^{\mathsf{T}}\bm{t}+\bm{\kappa})}{2}&\mathcal{F}^{\star}(Q)-G^{\mathsf{T}}\Lambda(\bm{u})G+\Lambda(\bm{\kappa})\end{bmatrix}\succcurlyeq_{co}0,\\
{\kappa}_{i}=0,\quad\forall\ i\notin\mathbb{B}_{1}.\end{gathered}\right.\right\},

\mathcal{C}_{cop}^{2}(\bm{p},P)\,:=\,\left\{\begin{array}[]{c}(\rho,\bm{r},\bm{v},\bm{\vartheta})\\
\in\mathscr{R}_{2}\end{array}\left|\begin{gathered}\begin{bmatrix}\rho&\frac{({H}^{\mathsf{T}}\bm{r}+\bm{\vartheta})^{\mathsf{T}}-\bm{p}^{\mathsf{T}}C}{2}\\
\\
\frac{{H}^{\mathsf{T}}\bm{r}+\bm{\vartheta}-C^{\mathsf{T}}\bm{p}}{2}&{H}^{\mathsf{T}}\Lambda(\bm{v})H-\Lambda(\bm{\vartheta})-\mathcal{F}(P)\end{bmatrix}\succcurlyeq_{co}0,\\
{\vartheta}_{i}=0,\quad\forall\ i\notin\mathbb{B}_{2}.\end{gathered}\right.\right\}.

Proposition 2
.

The maximization problem (
19
) and the minimization problem (
20
) are dual to each other. Moreover,

(1)

If
{H}^{\mathsf{T}}H\succ_{co}0
, then
\mathcal{V}_{1}^{*}=\mathcal{U}_{1}^{cop-cp}
.

(2)

If
{G}^{\mathsf{T}}G\succ_{co}0
, then
\mathcal{V}_{2}^{*}=\mathcal{U}_{2}^{cop-cp}
.

The conditions in Proposition
2
are aligned with Assumption
1
. When the feasible sets
\mathcal{S}_{1}
and
\mathcal{S}_{2}
are compact, the classical results of
11
,
18
, and
51
guarantee the existence of a Nash equilibrium for the bi-quadratic game (
13
). By Theorem
1
, the corresponding pure-strategy bilinear CP game (
16
) also admits a Nash equilibrium, implying
\mathcal{V}_{1}^{*}=\mathcal{V}_{2}^{*}
. This further implies that the compactness of
\mathcal{S}_{1}
and
\mathcal{S}_{2}
, together with conditions in Proposition
2
, i.e.,
{G}^{\mathsf{T}}G\succ_{co}0

{H}^{\mathsf{T}}H\succ_{co}0
, ensure strong duality between the COP-CP programs (
19
) and (
20
), i.e.,
\mathcal{U}_{1}^{cop-cp}=\mathcal{U}_{2}^{cop-cp}
. In fact, it can be shown that the compactness of
\mathcal{S}_{1}
and
\mathcal{S}_{2}
implies
{G}^{\mathsf{T}}G\succ_{co}0

{H}^{\mathsf{T}}H\succ_{co}0
(which we will prove in the proof of the following Lemma
5
). Therefore, the compactness of the feasible sets
\mathcal{S}_{1}
and
\mathcal{S}_{2}
is sufficient to ensure strong duality between the paired COP-CP formulations, and both games have well-defined equilibrium solutions. Lemma
5
below presents this result and it further shows that the COP-CP game solution recovers the first two moments of the mixed strategy equilibrium of the biquadratic game.

Lemma 5
.

Let
(\mu_{1}^{*},\mu_{2}^{*})\in\mathcal{M}_{1}\times\mathcal{M}_{2}
be a Nash equilibrium of the bi-quadratic game, and
(\bm{p}^{*},P^{*},\rho,\bm{r},\bm{v},\bm{\vartheta})
, and
(\bm{q}^{*},Q^{*},\tau,\bm{t},\bm{u},\bm{\kappa})
are optimal solutions to Problems (
19
) and (
20
). If
\mathcal{S}_{1}
and
\mathcal{S}_{2}
are compact, then

(1)

\mathcal{V}_{1}^{*}=\mathcal{U}_{1}^{cop-cp}=\mathcal{U}_{2}^{cop-cp}=\mathcal{V}_{2}^{*}
; and

(2)

\mathbb{E}_{\mu_{1}^{*}}[\bm{s}_{1}]=\bm{p}^{*}
,
\mathbb{E}_{\mu_{2}^{*}}[\bm{s}_{2}]=\bm{q}^{*}
; and

(3)

\mathbb{E}_{\mu_{1}^{*}}[\bm{s}_{1}\bm{s}_{1}^{\mathsf{T}}]=P^{*}
,
\mathbb{E}_{\mu_{1}^{*}}[\bm{s}_{2}\bm{s}_{2}^{\mathsf{T}}]=Q^{*}
.

Remark
.

In
25
, the authors studied the zero-sum polynomial game.
Specifically, let
K_{1}\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{n_{1}}
and
K_{2}\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{n_{2}}
be two closed basic semialgebraic sets, and let
f
be a polynomial in
(\bm{s}_{1},\bm{s}_{2})\in K_{1}\times K_{2}
.
Then the zero-sum polynomial game is to solve the following minimax problem:

(21)

\max_{\mu_{1}\in\mathcal{M}(K_{1})}\min_{\mu_{2}\in\mathcal{M}(K_{2})}\int\int f(\bm{s}_{1},\bm{s}_{2})d\mu_{2}d\mu_{1}.

In contrast, the bi-quadratic game (
13
) focus on the cases where the objective function is bi-quadratic, the feasible set are given by linear equality and inequalities, and there exist binary variables.
We remark that one may characterize the binariness for the variable
\bm{s}_{i,j}\ (j\in\mathbb{B}_{i})
by imposing the equality constraint
s_{i,j}(s_{i,j}-1)=0
.
In this way, (
13
) can also be reformulated as a zero-sum polynomial game (
21
).
However, this polynomial game reformulation is different from our COP-CP reformulation (
17
).

3.3.
Application: Robust and Distributionally Robust Optimization for Mixed 0-1 Quadratic Problems with Uncertain Objectives

We now illustrate how this framework accommodates robust optimization (RO) and moment-based distributionally robust optimization (DRO) in the context of mixed 0-1 quadratic problems with uncertain objective parameters. The key results generally follow from
6
and
34
.

Let
\bm{s}\in\mathbb{R}^{l_{1}}
be the decision variable, and
\bm{\theta}\in\mathbb{R}^{l_{2}}
be the random parameter vector,
and let
\mathbb{B}_{1}\subseteq[l_{1}]
,
\mathbb{B}_{2}\subseteq[l_{2}]
be the index set of binary elements in
\bm{s}
and
\bm{\theta}

For real matrices
G

and vectors
\bm{g}

\bm{h}

let

\displaystyle\mathcal{S}:=

\displaystyle\{{\bm{s}}\in\mathbb{R}^{l_{1}}:G{\bm{s}}=\bm{g},\ {\bm{s}}_{1}\geq 0,\ s_{1,i}\in\{0,1\},\ \ \forall i\in\mathbb{B}_{1}\},

\displaystyle\Theta:=

\displaystyle\{\theta\in\mathbb{R}^{l_{2}}:H\theta=\bm{h},\ {\bm{s}}_{2}\geq 0,\ \theta_{2,j}\in\{0,1\},\ \forall j\in\mathbb{B}_{2}\}.

Consider the following RO problem, which captures both linear and quadratic terms of
{\bm{s}}
and
\bm{\theta}

\displaystyle\begin{array}[]{lll}\mathcal{V}^{ro}=\max\limits_{\bm{s}\in\mathcal{S}}\ \min\limits_{\mu\in\mathcal{M}}\ \mathbb{E}_{\mu}\left[\bm{\theta}^{\mathsf{T}}C\bm{s}+\operatorname{vec}(\bm{s}\bm{s}^{\mathsf{T}})^{\mathsf{T}}F\operatorname{vec}(\bm{\theta}\bm{\theta}^{\mathsf{T}})\right],\end{array}

where
C
and

are objective-coefficient matrices,

\bm{\theta}
supported on
\Theta
, and
\mathcal{M}

Similarly, for Problem (

\mathcal{L}_{1}\coloneqq\{{\bm{s}}\in\mathbb{R}^{l_{1}}_{+}:G{\bm{s}}=\bm{g}\},\quad\mathcal{L}_{2}\coloneqq\{\bm{\theta}\in\mathbb{R}^{l_{2}}_{+}:H\bm{\theta}=\bm{h}\},

and we assume that Assumption
2
holds throughout this subsection.
For the solution
(\bm{s}^{*},\mu^{*})
to (
3.3
), the second coordinate
\mu^{*}
is also called the worst-case distribution of
\bm{\theta}
.

3.3.1.
Robust Optimization Setting

We first consider the RO setting where
\mathcal{M}
is the set of all probability measures supported on
\Theta
.
In this RO setting, the objective is to optimize
\bm{s}
under the worst-case realization of
\bm{\theta}
.
We now show that Problem (
3.3
) reduces to a bilinear max-min problem over CP cones in the form of Problem (

Specifically, consider the following maximin bilinear problem over CP cones:

\displaystyle\begin{array}[]{lll}\mathcal{V}^{ro}_{cp}=\max\limits_{(\bm{p},P)\in\mathcal{C}_{cp}^{1}}\min\limits_{(\bm{q},Q)\in\mathcal{C}_{cp}^{2}}\bm{q}^{\mathsf{T}}C\bm{p}+\left<\mathcal{F}(P),Q\right>,\end{array}

where
\mathcal{F}
,
\mathcal{C}_{cp}^{1}
,
\mathcal{C}_{cp}^{2}
are given in a similar way as in Section
3.2.2
, and we omit the explicit illustrations.

Remark
.

Note that although the max–min conic reformulation (
3.3.1
) of the robust optimization problem shares the same structural form as that of a biquadratic game reformulation, there is a fundamental distinction between the two settings. RO can be viewed as a one-sided variant of a zero-sum game, in which the decision-maker optimizes against an adversarial nature. In this interpretation, nature is allowed to select a “mixed strategy,” corresponding to a worst-case distribution within the prescribed ambiguity set. This perspective highlights the intrinsic connection between RO and the theory of two-person zero-sum games, while emphasizing that the former represents a special, asymmetric case of the latter.

Theorem 2
.

Under Assumption
2
, if
\mathcal{M}
is the set of all probability measures supported on
\Theta
, then Problem (
3.3
) is equivalent to Problem (
3.3.1
), i.e.,
\mathcal{V}^{ro}=\mathcal{V}^{ro}_{cp}
. Furthermore, if

and

are optimal solutions to Problem (

), then

(1)

the vector

of Problem (

(2)

for the worst-case distribution

of

in Problem (

;

(b)

If

is bounded, then

This result can be similarly shown as for Theorem
1
and Lemma

,
based on the key results in

34

We omit the detailed proofs here for neatness.

Leveraging the COP-CP reformulation result in Section

(26)

\begin{array}[]{rcl}\mathcal{U}^{ro}_{cop-cp}\,:=&\max&\quad\rho+{\bm{h}}^{\mathsf{T}}\bm{r}+(\bm{h}\bullet\bm{h})^{\mathsf{T}}\bm{v}\\
&{s.t.}&\quad{(\bm{p},P)\in\mathcal{C}_{cp}^{1}},\\
&&\quad(\rho,\bm{r},\bm{v},\bm{\vartheta})\in\mathcal{C}_{cop}^{2}(\bm{p},P),\end{array}

where

is defined as in Section

A natural sufficient condition to ensure conic strong duality is demonstrated in the following, which can be shown in the same way as Proposition
2
:

Corollary 1
.

Under the assumptions of Theorem

Note that the boundedness of

In these cases, conic strong duality holds and the COP-CP reformulation (

3.3.2.
Moment-Based Distributionally Robust Optimization Setting

In the moment-based DRO setting, Problem (
3.3
) is equipped with additional moment constraints.
That is, aside from the constraint that
\mu
is supported on
\Theta
,
the first order moment vector
\mathbb{E}_{\mu}[\bm{\theta}]
and the second order moment matrix
\mathbb{E}_{\mu}[\bm{\theta}\bm{\theta}^{\mathsf{T}}]
are partially specified by some linear constraints.
Without loss of generality, we assume these linear constraints are given in the following form:

(27)

\left<L_{i},\left[\begin{array}[]{cc}1&\mathbb{E}_{\mu}[\bm{\theta}^{\mathsf{T}}]\\
\mathbb{E}_{\mu}[\bm{\theta}]&\mathbb{E}_{\mu}[\bm{\theta}\bm{\theta}^{\mathsf{T}}]\end{array}\right]\right>=d_{i},\quad i=1,\ldots,I.

Recall that the bijective correspondence of measures
\mu\in\mathcal{M}
and CP matrices in
\mathcal{C}^{2}_{cp}
is given in Theorem
2
.
Thus, such moment constraints can be transformed to linear constraints over
\mathcal{C}^{2}_{cp}
.
Define

\Omega=\left\{(\bm{q},Q)\in\mathbb{R}^{l_{2}}\times\mathcal{S}^{l_{2}}\left|\left<L_{i},\left[\begin{array}[]{cc}1&\bm{q}^{\mathsf{T}}\\
\bm{q}&Q\end{array}\right]\right>=d_{i},\quad i=1,\ldots,I\right.\right\}.

Consider the maximin bilinear problem over CP cones (
3.3.1
) with additional moment constraints

\displaystyle\begin{array}[]{lll}\mathcal{Z}^{dro}_{cp}=\max\limits_{(\bm{p},P)\in\mathcal{C}_{cp}^{1}}\min\limits_{(\bm{q},Q)\in\mathcal{C}_{cp}^{2}\cap\Omega}\bm{p}^{\mathsf{T}}C\bm{q}+\left<\mathcal{F}(P),Q\right>.\end{array}

The following result shows that (
3.3
) with moment constraints (
27
) is equivalent to (
3.3.2
)

Lemma 6
.

Let
\mathcal{M}
be the set of probability measures supported on
\Theta
that satisfies the moment constraints (
27
).
Then, Problem (
3.3
) is equivalent to Problem (
3.3.2
), i.e.,
\mathcal{V}^{ro}=\mathcal{Z}^{dro}_{cp}
.

A corresponding COP-CP reformulation for the DRO setting can be derived analogously. Similar to Theorem
2
, the conic framework is able to recover the moments information of the worst-case distribution when they are not fully prescribed in the ambiguity set under mild conditions.

Remark
.

The equivalences established here suggest that COP-CP formulations may prove valuable in a wide range of applications beyond the RO and DRO examples described above. We here comment on one possibility:
a mixed-integer copositive program
, especially when linear matrix constraints and linear constraints on integer decision variables are separable. Since a CP constraint can capture the convex hull of mixed 0-1 constraints, integer constraints can be replaced by the corresponding CP construction. Coupled with the original copositivity constraints, this yields a COP-CP formulation of the mixed-integer copositive problem. The SDP relaxation methods introduced subsequently offer a systematic approach for solving these broader classes of mixed-integer copositive programs.

4.
A Semidefinite Relaxation Method for COP-CP Programs

In this section, we propose a semidefinite relaxation approach for solving Problems (
10
) and (
11
). The method leverages the relationships between the CP/COP cones and htms/nonnegative quadratic forms.
We present both asymptotic and finite convergence results for the semidefinite relaxations, and we introduce the
flat truncation
condition for certifying the finite convergence.

4.1.
SDP Relaxations

CP and COP matrices have a close connection to htms and nonnegative quadratic forms on
\Delta^{n}
. Recall that
\mathcal{R}[\Delta^{n}]_{2}^{\hom}
is the cone of degree-2 htms supported on
\Delta^{n}
, defined by (
2
). Define a linear mapping
\varphi_{n}:\mathcal{S}^{n}\mapsto\mathbb{R}^{\mathbb{N}^{n}_{2,\hom}}
such that for all
\lambda_{1},\ldots,\lambda_{r}\in\mathbb{R}
and
\bm{x}^{(1)},\ldots,\bm{x}^{(r)}\in\Delta^{n}
, it holds

\displaystyle\varphi_{n}\big(\sum_{i=1}^{r}\lambda_{i}\bm{x}^{(i)}{\bm{x}^{(i)}}^{\mathsf{T}}\big)=\sum_{i=1}^{r}\lambda_{i}[\bm{x}^{(i)}]_{2}^{\hom}.

Then
\varphi_{n}
is an isomorphism, and by the definitions of CP matrices and
\mathcal{R}[\Delta^{n}]_{2}^{\hom}
, one sees that
X\in\mathcal{S}^{n}
is CP if and only if
\varphi_{n}({X})\in\mathcal{R}[\Delta^{n}]_{2}^{\hom}
.
Similarly, the cone of COP matrices can be identified with the dual cone of
\mathcal{R}[\Delta^{n}]_{2}^{\hom}
, i.e., the cone of nonnegative quadratic forms,
\mathcal{P}[\Delta^{n}]^{\hom}_{2}
, given by (
3
). Let
\psi_{n}:\mathcal{S}^{n}\mapsto\mathbb{R}[x]_{2}^{\hom}
be the linear map such that for any matrix
W\in\mathcal{S}^{n}
, it holds

\displaystyle\psi_{n}(W)=\bm{x}^{\mathsf{T}}W\bm{x}.

Then
\psi_{n}
is a linear isomorphism from
\mathcal{S}^{n}
to the vector space of quadratic forms, and by definition,
W\succcurlyeq_{co}0
if and only if
\psi_{n}(W)\in\mathcal{P}[\Delta^{n}]^{\hom}_{2}
.
More details on these relationships can be found in Chapter 9 of
43
.

Define analogous mappings
\varphi_{m}
and
\psi_{m}
in lieu of
\varphi_{n}
and
\psi_{n}
when we refer to cones defined on
m
dimensional space.
Using these isomorphisms, the linear maximization problem (
10
) is equivalent to

(30)

\begin{array}[]{ccl}\mathcal{W}_{\max}^{*}\coloneqq&\max\limits_{X,\bm{z},Z}&\bm{c}^{\mathsf{T}}\bm{z}\\
&{s.t.}&\left<A_{i},X\right>={b}_{i},\quad i=1,...,I\\
&&\displaystyle\mathcal{Q}(X)-\sum_{j=1}^{J}z_{j}B_{j}=Z,\\
&&\varphi_{n}(X)\in\mathcal{R}[\Delta^{n}]^{\hom}_{2},\ \psi_{m}(Z)\in\mathcal{P}[\Delta^{m}]^{\hom}_{2}.\end{array}

For degrees
k_{1},k_{2}\in\mathbb{N}_{+}
,
denote by
\bm{\xi}|_{2}^{\hom}
the homogeneous degree-2 truncation for a tms
\bm{\xi}\in\mathbb{R}^{\mathbb{N}^{n}_{2k_{1}}}
, i.e.,
\bm{\xi}|_{2}^{\hom}:=({\xi}_{\bm{\alpha}})_{\bm{\alpha}\in\mathbb{N}^{n}_{2,\hom}}\in\mathbb{R}^{\mathbb{N}^{n}_{2,\hom}}
,
and consider the following relaxation of (
30
):

(31)

\begin{array}[]{ccl}\mathcal{W}_{\max}^{(k_{1},k_{2})}\coloneqq&\max\limits_{X,\bm{z},Z,\bm{\xi}}&\bm{c}^{\mathsf{T}}\bm{z}\\
&{s.t.}&\left<A_{i},X\right>={b}_{i},\quad i=1,...,I\\
&&\displaystyle\mathcal{Q}(X)-\sum_{j=1}^{J}z_{j}B_{j}=Z,\ \varphi_{n}(X)=\bm{\xi}|^{\hom}_{2},\\
&&\bm{\xi}\in\mathscr{S}[\Delta^{n}]_{2k_{1}},\ \psi_{m}(Z)\in\mbox{\rm IQ}[\Delta^{m}]_{2k_{2}}.\end{array}

As introduced in Section
2
, the above is a semidefinite program.
Similarly, for the min-max problem (
11
), one can also derive the following semidefinite relaxation:

(32)

\begin{array}[]{ccl}\mathcal{W}_{\min}^{(k_{1},k_{2})}\coloneqq&\min\limits_{Y,\bm{w},W,\bm{\nu}}&\bm{b}^{\mathsf{T}}\bm{w}\\
&{s.t.}&\left<B_{j},Y\right>={c}_{j},\quad j=1,...,J\\
&&\displaystyle\sum_{i=1}^{I}w_{i}A_{i}-\mathcal{Q}^{\star}(Y)=W,\ \varphi_{m}(Y)=\bm{\nu}|^{\hom}_{2}\\
&&\bm{\nu}\in\mathscr{S}[\Delta^{m}]_{2k_{2}},\ \psi_{n}(W)\in\mbox{\rm IQ}[\Delta^{n}]_{2k_{1}}.\end{array}

One may verify that (
32
) is the dual of (
31
), so by weak duality,
\mathcal{W}_{\max}^{(k_{1},k_{2})}\leq\mathcal{W}_{\min}^{(k_{1},k_{2})}
.

Recall that by Lemmas
2
and
3
,
\mbox{\rm IQ}[\Delta^{n}]_{2k_{2}}
approximates the copositive cone from the inside for any
k_{2}\in\mathbb{N}_{+}
, and
\mathscr{S}[\Delta^{n}]_{2k_{1}}
approximates the completely positive cone from outside for any
k_{1}\in\mathbb{N}_{+}
.
Consequently, it is not immediately evident which bound, upper or lower, these relaxations provide for Problems (
10
) and (
11
).
Let
k_{1},k_{2}\in N_{+}
be relaxation orders.
In the following sections, we first establish the asymptotic convergence that
\mathcal{W}_{\max}^{(k_{1},k_{2})}
and
\mathcal{W}_{\min}^{(k_{1},k_{2})}
converge to
\mathcal{W}^{*}_{\max}
and
\mathcal{W}^{*}_{\min}
, respectively, as
\min\{k_{1},k_{2}\}\to\infty
.
Then, we give the results for
finite convergence
(which is also called
tightness
) and the
flat truncation
condition, a key certificate for the tightness.

4.2.
Asymptotic Convergence

We first study the asymptotic convergence properties of the semidefinite formulations (
31
)-(
32
) relative to the original problems (
10
)-(
11
). Throughout the rest of the paper, we use the following notation shorthand

(X,\bm{z},Z,\bm{\xi})^{*}\coloneqq(X^{*},\bm{z}^{*},Z^{*},\bm{\xi}^{*}),\quad(Y,\bm{w},W,\bm{\nu})^{*}\coloneqq(Y^{*},\bm{w}^{*},W^{*},\nu^{*}),

where

is the maximizer of (

is the minimizer of (

For any relaxation orders

we write

\displaystyle(X,\bm{z},Z,\bm{\xi})^{(k_{1},k_{2})}

\displaystyle\coloneqq(X^{(k_{1},k_{2})},\bm{z}^{(k_{1},k_{2})},Z^{(k_{1},k_{2})},\bm{\xi}^{(k_{1},k_{2})}),

\displaystyle(Y,\bm{w},W,\bm{\nu})^{(k_{1},k_{2})}

Let

Corollary 2
.

Note that the proof of Theorem

4.3.
Flat Truncation and Finite Convergence

We next study the finite convergence of the relaxations (
31
)-(
32
).
The hierarchy of semidefinite relaxation (
31
)-(
32
) has finite convergence if
\mathcal{W}_{\max}^{(k_{1},k_{2})}=\mathcal{W}_{\max}^{*}=\mathcal{W}_{\min}^{*}=\mathcal{W}_{\min}^{(k_{1},k_{2})}
for all
k_{1}
and
k_{2}
big enough, under which we also say the relaxation is tight.
We start with the
flat truncation
conditions, which, when satisfied, leads to finite convergence.
The following proposition from
8
demonstrates the utility of flat truncation condition, and we refer to
30
for an elegant and expository proof.

Proposition 3

(Flat Truncation)
.

Let
\bm{\xi}\in\mathscr{S}[\Delta^{n}]_{2k}
be a tms. If for some
t\leq k
,

\mbox{rank}\,M_{t}[\bm{\xi}]=\mbox{rank}\,M_{t-1}[\bm{\xi}]
, then
\bm{\xi}|_{2}^{\hom}\in\mathcal{R}[\Delta^{n}]^{\hom}_{2}
with
r_{1}=\mbox{rank}\,M_{t}[\bm{\xi}]
and

\bm{\xi}|_{2}^{\hom}=\lambda_{1}[\bm{x}^{(1)}]_{2}^{\mathrm{hom}}+\cdots+\lambda_{r_{1}}[\bm{x}^{(r_{1})}]_{2}^{\mathrm{hom}},\quad\bm{x}^{(i)}\in\Delta^{n},\quad\lambda_{i}\in\mathbb{R}_{+},\quad i=1,\ldots,r_{1}.

Similarly, if
\bm{\nu}\in\mathscr{S}[\Delta^{m}]_{2k}
and
\mbox{rank}\,M_{t}[\bm{\nu}]=\mbox{rank}\,M_{t-1}[\bm{\nu}]
for some
t\leq k
, then
\bm{\nu}|_{2}^{\hom}\in\mathcal{R}[\Delta^{m}]^{\hom}_{2}
and there exists a decomposition similar to the above.

Recall that our SDP constructions approximate the CP and COP cones from the opposite sides, which complicates direct bounding arguments. With the flat truncation condition, however, the semidefinite relaxation effectively reduces to a one-sided approximation. The following result formalizes this observation.

Proposition 4
.

For the pair of degrees
(k_{1},k_{2})
, let
(X,\bm{z},Z,\bm{\xi})^{(k_{1},k_{2})}
be the maximizer of (
31
) and let
(Y,\bm{w},W,\bm{\nu})^{(k_{1},k_{2})}
be the minimizer of (
32
).

(1)

If
\bm{\xi}^{(k_{1},k_{2})}
satisfies the flat truncation, i.e., there exists
t\leq k_{1}
such that

(33)

r_{1}:=\mbox{rank}\,M_{t}[\bm{\xi}^{(k_{1},k_{2})}]=\mbox{rank}\,M_{t-1}[\bm{\xi}^{(k_{1},k_{2})}],

then
X^{(k_{1},k_{2})}\succcurlyeq_{cp}0
and
\mathcal{W}_{\max}^{(k_{1},k_{2})}\leq\mathcal{W}_{\max}^{*}
;

(2)

If
\bm{\nu}^{(k_{1},k_{2})}
satisfies the flat truncation, i.e., there exists
s\leq k_{2}
such that

(34)

r_{2}:=\mbox{rank}\,M_{s}[\bm{\nu}^{(k_{1},k_{2})}]=\mbox{rank}\,M_{s-1}[\bm{\nu}^{(k_{1},k_{2})}],

then
Y^{(k_{1},k_{2})}\succcurlyeq_{cp}0
and
\mathcal{W}_{\min}^{(k_{1},k_{2})}\geq\mathcal{W}_{\min}^{*}
.

By Proposition
4
, once flat truncation is in place, the semidefinite relaxation hierarchy becomes one-sided: each higher level will improve the bound. Therefore, closing the remaining gap reduces to ensuring strong duality for the SDP pair as stated in Theorem
4

5.2.
Binary Cyclic Blotto Game

In Section
3.2.1
, we introduce the cyclic Blotto game, a variation of the classic Colonel Blotto game, as a representative example that can be naturally formulated as a bi-quadratic game.
In this numerical study, we begin by examining a binary version of the cyclic Blotto game, where each player’s decision at a given location is simplified to a binary choice, namely,
whether or not to occupy that location
.
This binary formulation significantly reduces the action space compared to the general game, thereby enabling sharper structural insights into the strategic interplay.

Specifically, consider
N
locations arranged in a circle.
In the first stage, both the defender and attacker simultaneously select subsets of locations to occupy, subject to respective cardinality budgets:
E_{1}
for the defender and
E_{2}
for the attacker, where
E_{1}\leq N
and
E_{2}\leq N
.
That is, the defender and the attacker can only occupy at most
E_{1}
and
E_{2}
locations, respectively.
At each location
i
. the payoff is determined as follows: if the defender occupies location

i
but the attacker does not, the defender wins that location and earns a payoff of
1
, while the attacker receives a payoff of
-1
.
If both or neither player occupies location
i
, then the outcome is a tie with zero payoff to both.
However, if the attacker occupies a location without the defender’s occupation, the defender may still neutralize the attack by redeploying support from the left-adjacent location
i-1
(with
i-1=N
if
i=1
) if defender occupies location
i-1
and attacker does not. In this case, both players get zero payoff on location
i
.
In summary, if we denote by
s_{1,i}=1
(respectively,
s_{2,i}=1
) that the defender (respectively, the attacker) occupies location
i
, and let it be zero otherwise, then

(39)

\mbox{Defender's payoff on location $i$}=\left\{\begin{array}[]{rl}1,&\quad\mbox{ if }s_{1,i}=1,\ s_{2,i}=0;\\
-1,&\quad\mbox{ if }s_{1,i-1}=s_{1,i}=0,\ s_{2,i}=1;\\
-1,&\quad\mbox{ if }s_{1,i-1}=s_{2,i-1}=s_{2,i}=1;s_{1,i}=0;\\
0,&\quad\mbox{ otherwise. }\end{array}\right.

The attacker’s payoff is the negative of the defender’s.
This support mechanism confers a structural advantage to the defender.

Similar to the general cyclic Blotto game presented in Section
3.2.1
, binary cyclic Blotto games can be formulate as biquadratic games.
For binary variables
s_{1,i},s_{2,i}
in the above,
let
\bm{s}_{1}\coloneqq(s_{1,1},\ldots,s_{1,N})
and
\bm{s}_{2}\coloneqq(s_{2,1},\ldots,s_{2,N})
.
Then the feasible sets for the defender and attacker are

\mathcal{S}_{1}\coloneqq\left\{\bm{s}_{1}\in\{0,1\}^{N}\left|\ \begin{array}[]{lll}s_{1,1}+\cdots+s_{1,N}\leq E_{1}\end{array}\right.\right\},

\mathcal{S}_{2}\coloneqq\left\{\bm{s}_{2}\in\{0,1\}^{N}\left|\ \begin{array}[]{lll}s_{2,1}+\cdots+s_{2,N}\leq E_{2}\end{array}\right.\right\}.

Moreover, for the pair of feasible strategies
(\bm{s}_{1},\bm{s}_{2})
, it follows from (
39
) that the total payoff for the defender equals (let
s_{i,0}\coloneqq s_{i,N}
)

(40)

P(\bm{s}_{1},\bm{s}_{2})=\sum\limits_{i=1}^{N}\left(s_{1,i}(1-s_{2,i})-(1-s_{1,i})(1-s_{1,i-1})s_{2,i}-(1-s_{1,i})s_{1,i-1}s_{2,i}s_{2,i-1}\right).

For the remainder of this section, we explore a series of numerical experiments using semidefinite relaxations to evaluate the defender’s optimal strategy under various configurations
(E_{1},E_{2},N)
.
We exclude trivial regimes where
E_{1}=N
or
E_{2}=N
, as the players would simply occupy all locations.
Similarly, when
E_{1}=1
or
E_{2}=1
, the optimal strategies become degenerate: randomly selecting a single location due to the game’s symmetry in this specific case. Hence, we focus on nontrivial cases where
1<E_{1}<N
and
1<E_{2}<N
to better reveal the nuanced strategic dynamics enabled by binary occupation and spatial redeployment. Moreover, given that the defender already has locational advantage over the attacker, we restrict attention to cases where attacker has more “resources” than the defender, i.e.,
E_{2}>E_{1}
.

Table
2
reports the instances solved for
N=4
and

k_{1}=k_{2}=2
.
As described in Section
4
, when this condition holds, the semidefinite relaxation is tight, and a decomposition for CP matrices can be obtained, from which we explicitly recover the pure strategies in the support of each player’s equilibrium mixed strategy and their associated probabilities.
Specifically, for each game configuration
(E_{1},E_{2},N)

p_{x}
for the defender and
p_{y}
for the attacker, each equal to the reciprocal of the number of supported pure strategies.

The rationale for first introducing the binary variant of the cyclic Blotto game lies in its ability to reveal clear and intuitive structural insights into the equilibrium. Before presenting the exact equilibrium solution, we formulate several conjectures regarding its structure.
In the absence of the defender’s advantage of leveraging support from adjacent locations, the strategies of the defender and the attacker would be symmetric and independent across locations, except for the correlation induced by the total budget constraints. In such a case, each location is indistinguishable from the others, and players’ optimal strategies would reduce to identically distributed decisions over locations, leading to a formulation equivalent to the classical Colonel Blotto game.
However, the added flexibility for the defender to receive support from the left-adjacent location alters the strategic landscape. This interdependence across locations implies that the spatial arrangement of occupied locations becomes consequential. Anticipating this, an attacker may seek to concentrate their attacks on adjacent locations, thereby reducing the effectiveness of the defender’s support mechanism. In response, a rational defender would favor spreading out their occupied locations to maximize the value of adjacent support and counter the attacker’s clustering strategy.

Our computational results align with this intuition. Consider the case where
N=5
and
E_{1}=E_{2}=2
, meaning both players are allowed to occupy up to two locations. The mixed equilibrium strategy for the defender randomizes over five pure strategies that are pairwise symmetry through cyclic permutations: each selecting two locations that are maximally spaced apart on the cycle of five nodes. These strategies are:
(1,3)

(2,4)

(3,5)

(4,1)

(5,2)

\frac{1}{5}

(1,2)

(2,3)

(3,4)
,
(4,5)

(5,1)

\frac{1}{5}

6.
Conclusions

This paper introduces a general framework for solving a class of max–min bilinear optimization problems over CP cones, which frequently arise in robust optimization, game theory, and beyond. Our key innovation is the COP–CP reformulation, which transforms the original nested optimization structure into a single-stage linear conic program over the product of COP and CP cones. This reformulation enables us to apply a systematic semidefinite relaxation approach, leveraging the moment–SOS hierarchy and the powerful flat truncation condition to certify global optimality and extract optimizers.

We provide both asymptotic and finite convergence guarantees for the proposed semidefinite relaxations and demonstrate their applicability on representative numerical examples. In particular, we highlight an application to a cyclic Colonel Blotto game, showing how the proposed method can exactly recover the equilibrium strategies and payoff when the flat truncation condition is satisfied.

There are several interesting directions for future research. One important avenue is the quantitative characterization of the relaxation gap. Although we provide conditions under which the semidefinite relaxation is tight, a deeper understanding of when the relaxation gap exists and how large it can be remains an open and important question.
Another direction pertains to scalability and computational efficiency. Our numerical study indicates that solving the semidefinite relaxation becomes increasingly time-consuming as the problem dimension grows, a common challenge for hierarchy-based relaxation methods. Since the cyclic Blotto game is used as a demonstration of the general method (rather than the primary focus of this work), we adopt standard solvers and do not design tailored algorithms for this specific instance. Future research may explore ways to exploit problem structure or develop decomposition techniques to improve scalability for large-scale applications.
Finally, extending the COP–CP reformulation to broader settings, such as problems involving tensor optimization or multi-agent equilibrium computation, may further expand the scope and impact of the proposed framework.

In summary, this paper contributes a new theoretical and algorithmic pathway for addressing max–min bilinear CP problems, with provable guarantees and practical relevance. It lays the groundwork for further advances in tractable formulations and scalable algorithms in nonconvex conic optimization.

Appendix C
More Details on The Moment-SOS hierarchy of semidefinite relaxations

We briefly review Lasserre’s Moment-SOS hierarchy of semidefinite relaxations for solving polynomial optimization problems, which was first studied in
[
26
]
and
[
44
]
.
Let
p
be a polynomial in
{\bm{x}}
and consider the polynomial optimization over the simplex

(68)

\left\{\begin{array}[]{ccl}p_{\min}\coloneqq&\displaystyle\min_{x}&\quad p({\bm{x}})\\
&{s.t.}&\quad{\bm{x}}\in\Delta^{n}.\end{array}\right.

For the integer
k
such that
2k\geq\deg(p)
, the
k
th order moment relaxation is

(69)

\left\{\begin{array}[]{ccl}p_{mom,k}\coloneqq&\displaystyle\min_{\xi}&\quad\langle p,\xi\rangle\coloneqq coef(p)^{T}\xi\\
&{s.t.}&\quad\xi_{\bf 0}=1,\ \xi\in\mathscr{S}[\Delta^{n}]_{2k}.\end{array}\right.

Its dual problem is the
k
th SOS relaxation

(70)

\left\{\begin{array}[]{ccl}p_{sos,k}\coloneqq&\displaystyle\max_{\gamma}&\quad\gamma\\
&{s.t.}&\quad p(x)-\gamma\in\mbox{\rm IQ}[\Delta^{n}]_{2k}.\end{array}\right.

To illustrate these definitions, consider the following example.

Example 2
.

Suppose
d=2
and
n=3
. A tms
\bm{\xi}
of degree 2 can be written as

(71)

\bm{\xi}=(\xi_{000},\xi_{100},\xi_{010},\xi_{001},\xi_{200},\xi_{110},\xi_{101},\xi_{020},\xi_{011},\xi_{002})^{\mathsf{T}},

Its htms of degree 2 consists of the last six entries.
If
\bm{x}=(0.5,0.5,0)^{\mathsf{T}}\in\Delta^{3}
,
then
\xi_{\alpha_{1}\alpha_{2}\alpha_{3}}=x_{1}^{\alpha_{1}}x_{2}^{\alpha_{2}}x_{3}^{\alpha_{3}}
. Therefore,

[\bm{x}]_{2}=(1,0.5,0.5,0,0.25,0.25,0,0.25,0,0)^{\mathsf{T}},\quad[\bm{x}]_{2}^{\hom}=(0.25,0.25,0,0.25,0,0)^{\mathsf{T}}.

Example 3
.

Let
{\bm{x}}\in\mathbb{R}^{3}
and let
p({\bm{x}})=2x_{1}+x_{2}^{2}-x_{1}x_{3}
.
Recall that
\bm{\xi}
is given by (
71
in Example
2
).
Then we have

\langle p,\xi\rangle=2\xi_{100}+\xi_{020}-\xi_{101}

M_{1}[\bm{\xi}]=\left[\begin{array}[]{cccc}\xi_{000}&\xi_{100}&\xi_{010}&\xi_{001}\\
\xi_{100}&\xi_{200}&\xi_{110}&\xi_{101}\\
\xi_{010}&\xi_{110}&\xi_{020}&\xi_{011}\\
\xi_{001}&\xi_{101}&\xi_{011}&\xi_{002}\end{array}\right],\quad\mathscr{V}^{(2)}_{1-\bm{e}^{\mathsf{T}}\bm{x}}[\xi]=\left[\begin{array}[]{c}\xi_{000}-\xi_{100}-\xi_{010}-\xi_{001}\\
\xi_{100}-\xi_{200}-\xi_{110}-\xi_{101}\\
\xi_{010}-\xi_{110}-\xi_{020}-\xi_{011}\\
\xi_{001}-\xi_{101}-\xi_{011}-\xi_{002}\end{array}\right],

L^{(1)}_{x_{1}}[\bm{\xi}]=\xi_{100},\quad L^{(1)}_{x_{2}}[\bm{\xi}]=\xi_{010},\quad L^{(1)}_{x_{3}}[\bm{\xi}]=\xi_{001},\quad L^{(1)}_{1-\bm{x}^{\mathsf{T}}\bm{x}}[\bm{\xi}]=\xi_{000}-\xi_{200}-\xi_{020}-\xi_{002}.

Furthermore, for
\hat{\bm{x}}=(0.5,0.5,0)^{\mathsf{T}}\in\Delta^{3}
, if we denote
\hat{\bm{\xi}}\coloneqq[\hat{\bm{x}}]_{2}
, then

M_{1}[\hat{\bm{\xi}}]=\left[\begin{array}[]{cccc}1&0.5&0.5&0\\
0.5&0.25&0.25&0\\
0.5&0.25&0.25&0\\
0&0&0&0\end{array}\right],\quad\mathscr{V}^{(2)}_{1-\bm{e}^{\mathsf{T}}\bm{x}}[\hat{\bm{\xi}}]=\left[\begin{array}[]{c}0\\
0\\
0\\
0\end{array}\right],

L^{(1)}_{x_{1}}[\hat{\bm{\xi}}]=0.5,\quad L^{(1)}_{x_{2}}[\hat{\bm{\xi}}]=0.5,\quad L^{(1)}_{x_{3}}[\hat{\bm{\xi}}]=0,\quad L^{(1)}_{1-\bm{x}^{\mathsf{T}}\bm{x}}[\hat{\bm{\xi}}]=0.5.

Both (
69
) and (
70
) are semidefinite programs.
Because
[{\bm{x}}]_{2k}\in\mathscr{S}[\Delta^{n}]_{2k}
for every
{\bm{x}}\in\Delta^{n}
, the following holds for any
k
by weak duality:

p_{\min}\geq p_{mom,k}\geq p_{sos,k}.

Indeed, one has
\lim_{k\to\infty}p_{sos,k}=p_{\min}
by Putinar Positivstellensatz (see Lemma
2
).
This property is usually referred to as
asymptotic convergence
.
Moreover, correspondence to the Putinar’s Positivstellensatz for nonnegative polynomials (Lemma
2
), there exists a characterization for the asymptotic membership for moment vectors.
Denote by
\mathbb{R}^{\mathbb{N}^{n}}
be the (infinitely dimensional) space of vectors labeled by monomial powers
\alpha\in\mathbb{N}^{n}
.

Lemma 10
.

(
[
49
]
)
Let
\bm{\xi}^{\infty}\coloneqq(\xi^{\infty}_{\bm{\alpha}})_{\bm{\alpha}\in\mathbb{N}^{n}}\in\mathbb{R}^{\mathbb{N}^{n}}
,
and let
\bm{\xi}^{(k)}\coloneqq(\xi^{\infty}_{\bm{\alpha}})_{\bm{\alpha}\in\mathbb{N}^{n}_{2k}}\in\mathbb{R}^{\mathbb{N}^{n}_{2k}}
be the subvector, for each
k=1,2,\ldots.

Then,

\bm{\xi}^{(k)}\in\mathscr{S}[\Delta^{n}]_{2k},\quad\forall k=1,2,,\ldots,

if and only if there exists a measure
\mu
supported on
\Delta^{n}
such that

\bm{\xi}^{\infty}_{\alpha}=\int_{\Delta^{n}}{\bm{x}}^{\alpha}d\mu,\quad\forall\alpha\in\mathbb{N}^{n}.

In the meanwhile, we say the Moment-SOS hierarchy is
tight
or has
finite convergence
if
p_{sos,k}=p_{\min}
for some relaxation order
k
.
It is shown in
[
40
]
that the finite convergence is guaranteed if the global minimizers of (
68
) satisfy some optimality conditions.
Finally, the finite convergence can be certified by the
flat truncation conditions.

When they are satisfied, one can extract global minimizers of (
68
) from the moment matrix at the minimizer of (
69
).
We refer the interested readers to
[
3
,
44
]
for more details regarding nonnegative and SOS polynomials, and
[
8
,
41
,
42
,
43
,
30
]
for polynomial optimization and the truncated moment problem.
