The Effects of Ambient Air Pollutants on Changes in Cardiac Structure and Function
Authors: Claire L. Leiser, Daniela Sotres-Alvarez, Haeyoon Chang, Elizabeth W. Spalt, Barry E. Hurwitz, Martha L. Daviglus, Greg Talavera, Robert Kaplan, Bonnie E. Shook-Sa, Carlos J. Rodriguez, Joel D. Kaufman
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundThe effects of air pollutants on cardiac structure and function preceding the clinical diagnosis of heart failure are not well understood.ObjectivesThe objective of the study was to determine if ambient pollutant concentrations are associated with longitudinal changes in cardiac structure and function.MethodsMeasures of cardiac structure and function were serially assessed by echocardiogram at visits 1 (2008-2011) and 2 (2015-2018), respectively, with a median follow-up of 4.3 years (IQR: 4.0-4.4 years). Outdoor air pollution concentrations at residence were estimated from spatio-temporal models, as mean concentrations the year prior to each visit. We determined longitudinal relationships between air pollution and each echocardiographic measure, adjusted for baseline and time-varying covariates.ResultsEchocardiographic data were available on 1,630 individuals; mean age was 55.3 years at visit 1. Per IQR of pollutant, higher mean residential concentrations of particulate matter air pollution <2.5 µm in aerodynamic diameter (particulate matter 2.5 [PM2.5]) and traffic-related air pollutant gas (nitrogen dioxide) were associated with a 0.52% (95% CI: 0.20-0.83) and 0.12% (95% CI: 0.03-0.20) increase in global longitudinal strain, respectively. Higher PM2.5 and nitrogen dioxide were associated with decreased left atrial volume index: −1.39 (95% CI: −2.25 to −0.54) and −0.94 (95% CI: −1.63 to −0.26) respectively. PM2.5 was associated with a decrease in left ventricular ejection fraction of −1.05% per year (95% CI: −1.73 to −0.37) and increase in relative wall thickness by 0.12 (95% CI: 0.05-0.19) unit.ConclusionsOur findings suggest that common air pollutant exposures are associated with longitudinal preclinical adverse changes in cardiac structure and function.

Ambient air pollution has been linked to overall mortality in epidemiological studies, with the majority of deaths resulting from cardiovascular disease.1 Air pollution is a ubiquitous and complex mixture of multiple gaseous compounds and particulate matter, produced by multiple sources including traffic and industrial emissions, which results in high spatial and temporal variability of exposure.2, 3, 4 There is a large body of evidence that both long-term and short-term air pollution exposure can result in adverse cardiovascular effects.1,5,6 These associations are primarily observed in epidemiological cohorts, use of retrospective electronic health records, or toxicological studies.1,5,6 Studies have observed effects of air pollution on cardiovascular diseases in mortality, stroke, myocardial infarction, atrial fibrillation, and heart failure (HF), among others.7, 8, 9, 10 However, the effects of air pollutants on cardiac structure and function preceding the clinical diagnosis of HF remain less explored.

HF in particular is of substantial interest, due to its high morbidity and mortality in the United States.11 HF is a growing public health issue, with an estimated prevalence of 6.2 million in 2020 and 20% lifetime risk for persons aged over 40 years.12 U.S. HF costs are high, with total annual costs reaching $160 billion by 2030.13 Given this significant burden, attention has increasingly shifted to HF prevention, particularly by targeting pre-HF.14,15 Pre-HF replaces American College of Cardiology/American Heart Association stage B HF as per new HF nomenclature guidelines in efforts to emphasize the preventative and actionable stage of HF similar to how clinicians use prediabetes and prehypertension. Pre-HF is indeed a “stage” before clinical HF equivalent to the older nomenclature of American College of Cardiology/American Heart Association stage B HF.

Approximately 24% to 34% of the U.S. adult population has pre-HF.14 Recent guidelines emphasize that intervening in pre-HF is crucial, to potentially forestall progression to symptomatic HF.14,15 Previous studies of air pollution on pre-HF measures have been cross-sectional in nature,16, 17, 18 thus unable to estimate rates over time. Therefore, investigating the effect of air pollution exposure on pre-HF can provide crucial information into the underlying physiological processes of air pollution on the HF cascade.

Importantly, Hispanics/Latinos represent nearly 20% of the U.S. population and are a fast growing segment of the aging population.19 They have a higher burden of risk factors for clinical HF.13 Hispanic/Latino populations have been shown to have higher particulate matter 2.5 (PM2.5)-attributable premature mortality compared to more non-Hispanic white communities as well as higher exposure levels.20 Air pollution may represent a modifiable risk factor for the reduction of HF incidence or slowing the rate from changes in structure and function to clinical diagnosis. Our primary objective was to evaluate the impacts of residential concentrations of particulate matter air pollution <2.5 microns in aerodynamic diameter (PM2.5) and a traffic-related air pollutant gas (nitrogen dioxide [NO2]) on the progression of echocardiographic preclinical cardiac structure and function measures performed at the first and second visits (V1 and V2) in the ECHO-SOL (Echocardiographic Study of Latinos, an ancillary study to the HCHS/SOL (Hispanic Community Health Study/Study of Latinos.

Methods

HCHS-SOL and ECHO-SOL have been previously described.21,22 Briefly, for HCHS-SOL, individuals aged 18 to 74 years at baseline, were recruited and enrolled from randomly selected households in 4 U.S. metropolitan cities (Chicago, IL; Miami, FL; Bronx, NY; San Diego, CA). Data for HCHS-SOL was collected during a baseline (V1) and a follow-up visit (V2). The median follow-up time was 4.3 years (IQR: 4.0-4.4 years). HCHS-SOL was approved by the Ethics and Institutional Review Boards of all institutions involved and written informed consent was obtained from all individuals. ECHO-SOL included 1,818 HCHS-SOL individuals aged ≥45 years recruited through a stratified-sampling process representative across all 4 sites during the baseline HCHS-SOL exam. During ECHO-SOL V2, 1,630 ECHO-SOL individuals underwent a repeat echocardiography exam.23,24

Individuals in this study represent 1,818 ECHO-SOL individuals seen in the 4 HCHS/SOL clinical centers. Due to our modeling structure, we also included individuals with 1 ECHO measurement, as well as those with 2. Moreover, we included individuals who had confirmed residential addresses. All individuals (N = 1,818) received echocardiograms at V1 of whom 1,630 had valid residential addresses which is required to estimate air pollution exposures. Individuals who did not have residential information (N = 188) and who did not have a repeated echocardiogram (N = 132) were excluded for analysis. Poor image quality ratings (4.8%) were part of the quality control assessment and do not necessarily indicate missing or unusable echocardiographic data;22 <2% of participants were missing the primary measures used in this analysis. A total of 1,498 participants who had both exposure information and an echocardiogram at 2 time points were included.

Exposure predictions were generated from validated national spatio-temporal models of outdoor pollutant concentrations.25 Concentrations of air pollutants were predicted by a hierarchical model in a universal kriging framework which integrates temporal trends and incorporates agency and researcher-deployed air pollution monitoring data, geographic covariates, and spatial smoothing to characterize pollutant concentrations for each 2-week period of follow-up. The cross-validated R2 for these models are 0.89 for PM2.5 and 0.87 for NO2.25 Estimates were made at the precise residential locations and weighted to reflect time living at each known address. The primary exposures in this study were the 12-month residential average PM2.5 and NO2 before each participant’s V1 and V2.

Baseline and incident cardiac structure and function among the ECHO-SOL study population have been previously described.19,26 Philips Ultrasound imaging platforms were used across all field imaging centers. Per the American Society of Echocardiography recommendations, standard echocardiography examinations were performed by experienced sonographers at each Field Imaging Center.22, 23, 24,27, 28, 29 At least 2 full cardiac cycles were recorded for each measure, and 1 sonographer was chosen per ECHO-SOL site to ensure consistency. All sonographers participated in trainings to review protocols, and all echocardiograms were analyzed and interpreted by a registered diagnostic cardiac sonographer unaware of the exposure information at a single reading center and over-read by a board-certified cardiologist with advanced echocardiography training (C.J.R.).22 Inter-reader and intrareader reproducibility as measured by intraclass correlation ranged from 0.80 to 0.99. Left atrial volume and left ventricular (LV) end-diastolic volumes had the highest intraclass correlation values (0.97-0.99). The outcome definitions included echocardiographic measurements on both left heart structure and function defined as follows:

LV mass (LVM) was determined by subtracting the LV endocardial cavity volume from the volume encompassed by the LV epicardium and multiplying the resultant myocardial volume by the myocardial density.30 LVM index (LVMI) represents LVM indexed to body surface area. Volumetric assessments used the method of discs from apical 4- and 2-chamber long-axis views to measure end-diastolic volume (EDV) and end-systolic volume. LV ejection fraction (LVEF) was calculated as (EDV−end-systolic volume)/EDV.31

Tissue Doppler imaging permitted assessment of mitral diastolic annular E′ velocities. The average of the medial and lateral annular velocities was used. The ratio of early diastolic transmitral inflow velocity (E) to E’ (E/E′) was calculated. Left atrial (LA) volume index (LAVI) represents the ratio of long-axis volumetric LA dimensions indexed to body surface area.

Relative wall thickness (RWT) was defined by the formula using 2 times the posterior wall thickness divided by LV diastolic diameter.32

LV global longitudinal strain (GLS) was obtained from speckle-tracking analysis performed offline on 2D images using TomTec Cardiac Performance Analysis package and the speckles tracked along the endocardial borders generated peak longitudinal strain for both regional and global views.22,33

A mixed effects model was used to jointly model the cross-sectional and longitudinal relationship between air pollution and myocardial strain as measured by echocardiogram. We assume a Gaussian link-function wherein the predictor equals the mean response. Study site at baseline was additionally included as a fixed effect in all models to account for geographic variability in both pollutant exposures and echocardiographic outcomes. The residential pollutant exposures during the calendar year before the echocardiogram were the primary exposure calculated. The model is given by the following equation for set of individuals (i=1,…,n) measured at 1 or more exams (v=1,…,V):Yiv=[α0+Xi0α1+ai]+[tivβ0+Wivtivβ1+tivbi]+[ϵiv]where,

Yiv outcome (e.g., LV GLS) measure for individual i at exam v.

Xi0 vector of baseline covariates (eg sex at birth) for participant i.

Wiv vector time-varying exposure of interest (eg air pollution) and time-varying covariates (eg smoking status) at visit v for participant i.

tiv time from V1 to V2 for participant i.

β0 change in outcome measures (centered i.e. Yiv - baseline mean).

β1 term of interest - coefficient for association between predictors (including air pollution per IQR increase) and rate of change in outcome measurements per year.

α0 mean measurements at exam 1 in reference group.

α1 coefficients for covariates in cross-sectional relationship (baseline).

ai subject specific random intercept (assumed to be normally distributed, mean 0).

bi random slope per participant (assumed to be normally distributed, mean 0).

ϵiv error term (assumed to be normally distributed, mean 0).

The mixed effects model is flexible with respect to the number of observations and follow-up time per participant and allows for adjustment of time-varying covariates as well as covariates associated with baseline measurements and progression from V1 to V2. In addition, the missing echocardiographic measurements relevant to pre-HF phenotypes included LVMI, RWT, LVEF, LAVI, E/E’, or GLS. These missing measures were not mutually exclusive. Because the proportion of missing data for our primary echocardiographic measures was relatively small (<2%) and mixed-effects models accommodate incomplete repeated measures data, additional formal analyses of missing data patterns were not performed. Repeated measurements were modeled as a function of time since baseline, with time-varying exposures modeled as an interaction with follow-up time to obtain a rate.

To account for the population-based recruitment strategy, and clustering and stratification within HCHS/SOL, we used complex survey sampling and established sampling weights in the analysis.21 All reported values (means and prevalence rates) were weighted to account for the disproportionate selection of the HCHS/SOL sample and to at least partially adjust for any bias effects due to differential nonresponse in the selected sample at the household and person levels.21,34 The adjusted weights were also trimmed to limit precision losses due to the variability of the adjusted weights, and calibrated to the 2010 Census characteristics by age, sex, and Hispanic/Latino background in each field site’s target population. All analyses also account for cluster sampling and the use of stratification in sample selection.

Model 1 was adjusted for time varying covariates, (alcohol use and smoking status) and nontime varying covariates from baseline interviews (body mass index [BMI], education, gender, age, pack years of smoking, and physical activity). In model 2, we sought to exclude the possibility of confounding of our results by neighborhood socioeconomic status (NSES). NSES was measured as within year standardized NSES index value, where the z-score is interpreted as the census tract level disadvantage relative to other tracts that year.35 We also evaluated an extended model (model 3) to include clinical variables including systolic blood pressure, diastolic blood pressure, presence of diabetes mellitus, and heart rate; this was not our primary model out of concern that these factors may mediate the cardiovascular effects of air pollution. Finally, we conducted a sensitivity analysis that adjusted for the use of antihypertensive medications at V1. The data supporting the findings of this study are not publicly available due to privacy restrictions. Requests of data can be submitted to the HCHS/SOL Publications Committee for purposes of reproducing the results or replicating the procedure. Additional information about request of the data may be found at the HCHS/SOL website.

Table 3 shows the results relating PM2.5 with echocardiographic parameters. All results are reported as effect estimates per difference in one IQR unit of the predictor variable. PM2.5 was associated with changes in cardiac structure and function; we observed a 0.52% increase in GLS per year of follow-up (95% CI: 0.20-0.83) and a decrease in LVEF of −1.05% per year (95% CI: -1.73 – −0.37), for every 4 μg/m3 of PM2.5 concentration. Discussion

In this study, we found associations between the ambient air pollutants PM2.5 and NO2 with pre-HF measures of cardiac structure and function (Central Illustration). Beyond well-known risk factors for HF, such as hyperlipidemia, hypertension, diabetes, and lifestyle factors, numerous epidemiological and toxicological studies demonstrate that air pollution represents a potentially important contributor to incidence and progression of clinically diagnosed HF.1,36 Our results were consistent between our modeling strategies with inclusion or exclusion of clinical variables, thus suggesting air pollution exposure may be a contributing risk factor for progression of pre-HF. Although understanding of the underlying biological mechanisms of these observed relationships is still evolving, exposure to air pollution is thought to result in oxidative stress and inflammation,5,37 potentially impacting cardiac health across the HF continuum over long-term periods.Central IllustrationAir Pollution and Cardiac Structure Progression Effects of Ambient Air Pollution on Progression of Cardiac Structure and Function in the Echocardiographic Study of LatinosHF = heart failure; HFpEF = heart failure with preserved ejection fraction; HFrEF = heart failure with reduced ejection fraction; NO2 = nitrogen dioxide; PM2.5 = particulate matter 2.5.

Our study shares some similar results but has some methodological differences with other related studies evaluating environmental and occupational exposures using echocardiographic outcomes. Burroughs Peña, et al18 assessed occupational exposure to wood smoke, vehicle exhaust, solvents, pesticides, and metals in a cross-sectional analysis of the ECHO-SOL study; they found association between exposure to burning wood at current and longest held jobs with measures of cardiac structure and function. However, wood smoke is not equivalent to traffic-related air pollution exposures evaluated in our study, and no association were observed between occupational exposure to vehicle exhaust and echocardiographic measures.18 Ambient air pollution exposure has also previously been associated with adverse cardiac function in other populations. Zheng, et al38 evaluated associations between air pollution exposure and abnormal LV diastolic function in the China Hypertension Study using annual average NO2, PM10, and PM2.5 concentrations. Yang, et al17 similarly found worse systolic dysfunction and adverse longitudinal strain patterns associated with air pollution exposure in a Flemish population. Chang et al16 found increased air pollution exposure to be associated with adverse myocardial strain in the Cardiovascular Health Study of U.S. adults. Although these existing studies demonstrated findings generally consistent with our results, they were cross-sectional in nature. In contrast, our study estimated exposures at participant residences and evaluated longitudinal progression of pre-HF measures.

In contrast,39 we found a paradoxical relationship with LAVI outcomes. Similar findings have been reported previously,39 potentially reflecting the time- or age-dependent nature of LAVI as a predictive measure, which may limit its usefulness for evaluating long-term environmental exposures, such as those investigated in our study.40 Alternatively, the inverse association between LAVI with air pollution exposure may reflect direct LA toxicity rather than diastolic LV dysfunction. Future studies need to further evaluate these potential mechanisms. In addition, although several observed associations were statistically significant, the magnitudes of effect were modest and may not necessarily reflect clinically meaningful changes. It is possible that the duration and magnitude of the exposures in this study were insufficient to produce meaningful effects on LV end-diastolic pressure resulting in a large LV restructuring.

This study used echocardiographic data to examine markers associated with pre-HF allowing us to evaluate the progression of disease. Subclinical disease measurement is crucial to understanding HF development, allows for the examination of pathophysiological changes before clinical HF, providing evidence for studies of HF prevention and disease risk.17,19 As a basis for understanding biological mechanisms for disease, it is important that researchers and clinicians understand the full range of risk factors because intervention strategies can vary at different time points in disease pathogenesis. Given our findings regarding GLS and LVEF at an early, preclinical stage, our results provide further evidence that air pollutants are emerging key risk factors for HF prevention across the spectrum of HF development.

Strengths of this study include our state-of-the-art exposure estimation and comprehensive evaluation of echocardiographic pre-HF measures, rather than select individual echocardiographic parameters, in a well-characterized epidemiologic cohort. Importantly, we add to the literature in that this study is longitudinal in nature and thus provides evidence to cardiac changes resulting from air pollution exposure which could help to tailor interventions and policies to improve disease prevalence and incidence. The use of continuous echocardiographic measurements in our study also results in higher power to detect risk associations as compared to categorizing an individual’s disease risk. In addition, we conducted this study in a population of Hispanic/Latinos, an important understudied and diverse population.

Study limitations

Important limitations to our study also exist. As in all epidemiological studies, care should be taken in generalizing these findings beyond the population in which it was conducted. Air pollution studies are prone to exposure measurement error and mismeasurement, and although we were able to characterize well the outdoor concentration at each participant’s home and these are well-correlated with indoor concentrations (people spend time in many microenvironments we did not characterize). There is potential for selection bias into ECHO-SOL from the larger HCHS/SOL study as those who participate in ECHO-SOL may be different than those who elected to not participate; however, our use of sampling weights minimizes this limitation. Although echocardiography and cardiac magnetic resonance imaging have differing and complementary strengths and limitations, future studies characterizing pre-HF by cardiac magnetic resonance imaging may be helpful. Lastly, residual confounding from unmeasured chemotherapy exposure is possible, as certain chemotherapeutic agents may adversely affect GLS and LVEF depending on the dose and duration of exposure. However, the low prevalence of self-reported cancer in this population-based cohort (3.9%) suggests minimal impact on our findings. Any unmeasured chemotherapy exposure would likely be nondifferential, potentially attenuating observed associations and biasing results toward the null.

Conclusions

To our knowledge, this is the first study to evaluate the effects of long-term air pollution exposure on longitudinal change in pre-HF measures of cardiac structure and function by serial echocardiography. Air pollution could represent a modifiable risk factor for the development and progression of HF, and this work provides preclinical insights into the role that air pollution plays in the reduction of measures of systolic function (specifically GLS and LVEF). These results can inform future studies of longitudinal environmental effects on the progression of pre-HF to clinical HF, a disease with high morbidity and mortality.PerspectivesCOMPETENCY IN MEDICAL KNOWLEDGE: Long-term exposure to ambient air pollution, including PM2.5 and NO2, was associated with longitudinal changes in echocardiographic measures of preheart failure, particularly impaired global longitudinal strain and left ventricular ejection fraction, among Hispanic/Latino adults.
