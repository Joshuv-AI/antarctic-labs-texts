Hypoxic BMSCs accelerate femur fracture healing via the METTL3/SLC2A3 m6A-glycolysis axis
Authors: Shaohai Lin, Jia Wu, Lijun Deng, Yudan Long, Wenbo Xu, Wenxu Nie, Guanghui Liu, Yajun Han, Min Zeng, Zhangyuan Lin, Yong Zhu, Haitao Long
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundHypoxia promotes the osteogenic differentiation of bone marrow mesenchymal stem cells (BMSCs), thereby accelerating fracture healing. However, the underlying molecular mechanisms remain unclear.Materials and methodsA rat fracture model was established using external force. Introduction

Fracture healing is the restoration of bone structure and function through biological and biochemical processes. A study spanning 19 countries revealed that hip fracture cases will double by 2050 owing to population aging [1]. In recent decades, advances in fundamental, translational, and clinical research have improved fracture treatment; however, 10%–15% of patients with fractures still experience delayed or failed healing [2]. Fractures impose considerable burdens, including limited physical function, pain, discomfort, reduced quality of life, economic costs, disruption of work and study, and long-term sequelae [3]. Recovery time varies with fracture type and location, patient age, health status, and treatment methods. A recent investigation revealed that among 717 patients with hip fractures, 75% were females, yet female patients regained ambulatory capacity faster than their male counterparts [4]. Promoting rapid healing could reduce disability rates and medical burden. Therefore, understanding the mechanisms of fracture healing is essential for developing new treatment methods and technologies to improve healing outcomes and clinical efficacy.

Bone marrow mesenchymal stem cells (BMSCs) are central to fracture healing because of their low immunogenicity, ease of expansion, and multipotent differentiation capacity [5, 6]. They can differentiate into osteoblasts and chondrocytes, secrete growth factors and cytokines, and thereby contribute to new bone formation and remodeling [7]. Additionally, BMSCs can promote angiogenesis and improve blood supply to healing sites, thus providing sufficient nutrition and oxygen for bone healing. Recently, hypoxic preconditioning of BMSCs has emerged as a promising approach to accelerate fracture healing [8]. The strategy promotes cell survival, proliferation, angiogenesis, and tissue remodeling, as well as regulates growth factor release and affects the inflammatory response [9]. Investigating the molecular mechanisms of hypoxic BMSCs in fracture healing will aid the development of new clinical treatment strategies.

RNA methylation modifications, such as N6-methyladenosine (m6A) modifications, are strongly associated with fracture healing by regulating cell proliferation, invasion, and apoptosis [9–12]. m6A modification is involved in hypoxia-induced biological processes [13–15]. Hypoxia alters the methylation status of BMSCs, thereby regulating their biological behavior and enhancing osteogenesis, which is crucial for fracture repair and regeneration [16]. Methyltransferase-like 3 (METTL3), which is involved in regulating m6A modification, can inhibit osteoblast aging by stabilizing Hspa1a mRNA [17]. METTL3 is also involved in promoting osteogenic differentiation of various types of stem cells [18, 19]. However, whether and how METTL3-mediated m6A modifications regulate hypoxia in BMSCs and contribute to fracture healing remains unclear.

In this study, we performed methylated RNA immunoprecipitation-sequencing (MeRIP-seq) analysis on hypoxic BMSCs to identify molecules essential for fracture healing. Furthermore, we verified the potential mechanism by which these molecules regulate hypoxia-induced BMSC function and fracture healing.

Materials and methods

BMSC isolation and hypoxia treatment

BMSCs were harvested from 4-week-old female Sprague–Dawley rats (Model Animal Research Center of Nanjing University) housed under a 12-h dark/light cycle and provided with food and water. BMSCs were isolated from bone tissues using the adherence method as previously described [20] and cultured in Dulbecco’s modified Eagle’s medium supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (Gibco, USA) at 37 ℃ and 5% CO2. Briefly, the euthanized rats were disinfected in 75% ethanol, and femurs and tibias were collected after removing the limb skin. Both bone ends were cut, and the bone marrow cavity was repeatedly flushed with culture medium to collect bone marrow. Subsequently, the suspension was sieved through a 40–100 μm cell sieve, centrifuged at 1000–1500 rpm for 5–10 min, and the supernatant discarded. The cell pellet was resuspended in culture medium and incubated at 37 ℃ with 5% CO2 for 24–48 h. Non-adherent cells were removed during the first medium change. BMSC identity was confirmed by flow cytometry (NovoCyte, Agilent, USA) using antibodies against CD29 (No. 130-119-165), CD44 (No. 130-107-802), and CD45 (No. 130-128-256; 1:100 diluted, Miltenyi Biotech, Germany). Third passage BMSCs were then divided into two groups: non-treatment (Ctrl group) and hypoxia treatment with 50 µM CoCl2 for 72 h (Hypoxia group) [21]. This study was approved by the Experimental Animal Ethics Committee of Xiangya Hospital of Central South University (No. 2024111623).

Hypoxia-treated BMSCs were transfected with overexpression plasmids for solute carrier family 2 member 3 (SLC2A3, Hypoxia + SLC2A3 group) or METTL3 (METTL3 group); small interfering RNA (siRNA) targeting SLC2A3 (Hypoxia + SLC2A3 siRNA group; targeted sequence: 5’-GGCATGATTGGCTCTTTTTCTGT-3’) or METTL3 (METTL3 siRNA group; targeted sequence: 5’-ACGTCTATAGTCCCTGAATTAGC-3’); combined SLC2A3 overexpression with METTL3 siRNA (METTL3 + SLC2A3 siRNA group); SLC2A3 overexpression with the glycolysis inhibitor 2-DG (SLC2A3 + 2-DG group). Negative control groups included sh-NC and vector-transfected cells. All transfections were performed using the Lipofectamine 2000 kit (Thermo Fisher Scientific Inc., Waltham, MA, USA) at 80%–90% confluent. Transfection efficiency was confirmed by qPCR and Western blotting (WB) analysis.

Establishment of fracture model

Twenty-four 10-week-old female rats were used to establish a fracture model. Given that fractures predominantly occur in elderly women [22], the experiment excluded male rats. All rats were anesthetized with sodium pentobarbital, and right hind limb femur fractures were induced by external force. Intramedullary pins were inserted from the distal end for fixation. The rats were divided into four groups: normal cultured BMSCs (BMSC group, n = 6), hypoxic BMSCs (HY-BMSC group, n = 6), hypoxic BMSCs transfected with vector adenovirus (HY-BMSC-vector group, n = 6), and overexpressed SLC2A3 adenovirus (HY-BMSC + SLC2A3 group, n = 6). Pre-treated cells (2 × 106) from each group were injected into the femoral cavity post-fracture. Penicillin (2 × 105 U/day) was injected into the thigh muscle to prevent infection. After 30 days, the rats were euthanized with an overdose of sodium pentobarbital, and femur and serum samples were collected. The tissues were observed using X-ray and micro-computed tomography (CT) scanning, followed by dissection and observation of the surgical site.

Tissues were perfused with heparinized physiological saline, fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde, paraffin-embedded, and coronally sectioned at 4 μm. Sections were stained with hematoxylin and eosin (H&E), methylene blue, and Annona Red O/Fast Green stainings. Finally, slides were washed, dehydrated, cleared, cover-slip-mounted using neutral balsam, and observed under a 400× microscope.

All animal experiments were conducted strictly following international ethical guidelines and the National Institutes of Health Guide for the Care and Use of Laboratory Animals. Approval was obtained from the Experimental Animal Ethics Committee of Xiangya Hospital of Central South University (No. 2024111623).

Alizarin red staining

BMSCs were cultured in osteogenic induction medium (PH Biotechnology, Wuxi, China) for 3 weeks and washed with phosphate-buffered saline (PBS) to remove residual culture medium. The cells were fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde at room temperature (20 ℃ ~25 ℃) for 15–30 min, washed 2–3 times with PBS, and stained with 1% Alizarin Red solution for 5–15 min. The cells were gently agitated using a small brush to ensure uniform staining. Excess dye was removed by washing with PBS to remove unbound dye. The cells were washed 2–3 additional times until no visible dye remained. Samples were observed under phase contrast or fluorescence microscopy. The stained area appeared red-orange, indicating mineralized calcium deposition.

Enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay (ELISA)Enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay (ELISA)

BMSCs were collected, lysed in RIPA buffer, and centrifuged at 12,000 rpm for 15 min at 4 ℃. Alkaline phosphatase (ALP) content was measured using commercial ELISA kits (Shanghai Boyun Biotech Co., Ltd.) following the manufacturer’s instructions.

Methylated RNA immunoprecipitation-sequencing

MeRIP-seq analysis was performed on Ctrl and hypoxia-treated BMSCs (n = 3). Total RNA was extracted using TRIzol reagent (Takara, Dalian, China). mRNA was isolated using poly T oligo-attached magnetic beads (Invitrogen, CA, USA) and fragmented using bovine serum albumin. Strand-specific libraries were prepared using the dUTP method, converting the eluted m6A-containing and untreated input control fragments into a final cDNA library. Finally, the library was sequenced on an Illumina NovaSeq™ 6000 platform with 2 × 150 bp paired-end reads.

Differentially expressed gene (DEG) identification and functional analyses

DEGs were identified using the “limma” package in R (version 3.4.1), with thresholds of |log2 fold change (FC)| >1 and false discovery rate < 0.01. Gene ontology (GO) and the Kyoto encyclopedia of genes and genomes (KEGG) pathway enrichment analyses were performed using the DAVID database. The ggplot2 package was used for graphing and data visualization.

WB analysis

Proteins were extracted using RIPA lysis buffer (#R0278, Sigma). The proteins were separated by sodium dodecyl sulfate-polyacrylamide gel electrophoresis with 100 V applied to the stacking gel and 120 V to the resolving gel. Following electrophoresis, the gels were transferred to membranes, washed, and blocked overnight. Membranes were incubated with primary antibodies against SLC2A3 (#A03259-2, 1:1000, BOSTER, Wuhan, China), runt-related transcription factor-2 (Runx2, #ab236639, 1:1000, Abcam, Cambridge, MA, USA), osteocalcin (OCN, #ab93876, 1:1000), Osterix (Osx, #A02077-1, 1:1000), hexokinase 2 (HK2, #ab209847, 1:2000), pyruvate kinase muscle isozyme 2 (PKM2, #ab150377, 1:2000), lactate dehydrogenase A (LDHA, #ab112996, 1:1000), METTL3 (#ab195352, 1:1000), METTL4 (#ab261735, 1:1000), METTL4 (#ab309096, 1:1000), and ACTB (#ab8226, 1:5000). Membranes were incubated at 25 ℃ with the secondary antibody HRP-labeled goat anti-rabbit IgG (#cat: ab672, Abcam). The membranes were placed in an electrogenerated chemiluminescence (ECL) solution (ECL808-25, Biomiga, San Diego, CA, USA) and observed using an X-ray machine (36209ES01, Shanghai, China). The net density of the band was calculated using Image-Pro Plus software (version 6.0), referencing ACTB.

ATP production assay

ATP content was measured using an ATP Assay kit (Ab83355, Abcam, Cambridgeshire, UK) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Briefly, BMSCs (1 × 104) were centrifuged, and the supernatants were collected. Cell lysates were mixed with the ATP reaction mix at a 1:1 ratio (v/v) and incubated for 30 min. Absorbance was measured at 570 nm using a microplate reader (Infinite 200 Pro, TECAN). Data were normalized to total protein content.

Glucose uptake measurement

Glucose uptake was determined by a Glucose Uptake Colorimetric Assay kit (ab136955, Abcam, Cambridge, MA, USA) following the manufacturer’s protocol. Briefly, BMSCs were seeded in a 6-well plate, incubated overnight at 37 ℃, digested, and centrifuged. The supernatant was collected and incubated with a detection solution for 30 min. Absorbance at 540 nm was measured using a microplate reader, with signal intensity proportional to glucose concentration.

RNA pull-down analysis

The interaction between METTL3 and SLC2A3 was evaluated using a magnetic RNA–Protein Pull-Down kit (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA). Biotinylated sense and antisense probes of SLC2A3 were synthesized by GenePharma (Shanghai, China). The cells were lysed in buffer (Ambion, Austin, TX), and lysates were incubated with magnetic beads pre-coated with the probes (Servicebio, China). The beads were washed with salt buffer and pre-cooled lysis buffer. Finally, RNA–protein complexes were eluted and analyzed by WB.

RNA Immunoprecipitation

Following the manufacturer’s instructions, RNA immunoprecipitation (RIP) was performed using the EZ-Magna RIP kit (Millipore, Billerica, MA, USA) to examine the interaction between SLC2A3 and METTL3. Cells were lysed in RIP buffer and incubated overnight with anti-METTL3 antibody and IgG (1:100; ab109489; Abcam). Proteinase K was used to digest the cells, and immunoprecipitated RNA was extracted using TRIzol–chloroform. Finally, qPCR analysis was used to determine whether RNAs were precipitated by anti-METTL3 or IgG.

MeRIP-qPCR

m6A MeRIP was conducted using the Magna MeRIP m6A kit (cat#17–10,499, Millipore) following the manufacturer’s instructions. Immunoprecipitated RNA was analyzed by qPCR using the HiScript II One Step qRT-PCR SYBR Green Kit (#Q221-01). Amplification was conducted on an ABI 7900 system (Foster City, CA, USA), and relative expression levels were calculated using the 2–ΔΔCt method.

Statistical analysis

Data are presented as mean ± standard deviation (SD). Each experiment was repeated three times. All data followed a normal distribution. Statistical significance was determined using unpaired Student’s t-tests and one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA), followed by Tukey’s post hoc test for multiple comparisons. Graphs were generated using GraphPad Prism software (version 10; San Diego, CA, USA). A P < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

Hypoxia-induced higher osteogenic capacity in BMSCs

CD29 and CD44 are typical markers of BMSCs, with high expression indicating good proliferative ability and stem cell characteristics. CD45 is a marker of other types of cells; its high expression suggests contamination and the need for further purification. In this study, BMSCs displayed high expression of CD29 and CD44 but low expression of CD45 (Fig. 1A), confirming the suitability of BMSCs for subsequent experiments. To simulate a hypoxic environment, BMSCs were stimulated with CoCl₂ for 72 h. The results of the CCK-8 assay indicated that 50 µM CoCl₂ did not affect cell viability (Fig. 1B). Osteogenic capacity was evaluated in Ctrl and Hypoxia groups using Alizarin Red staining (Fig. 1C). Cells in the hypoxia-treated group exhibited prominent red calcified deposits, indicating good osteogenic ability. The cell staining intensity in the hypoxia-treated group was significantly higher than that in the Ctrl group. The average size of the mineralized area increased considerably, indicating that osteoblast activity was markedly enhanced in the hypoxia-treated group. The results of immunofluorescence confirmed an increased expression level of HIF1α in cells after CoCl₂ stimulation (Fig. 1D).

(B) CCK-8 assay was conducted to assess the cell viability of BMSCs after stimulation with different concentrations of CoCl₂ for 72 h. *P < 0.05, **P < 0.01, ***P < 0.001, one-way ANOVA. (C) Alizarin Red staining detects the osteogenic capacity. (D) Immunofluorescence assay showed the expression level of HIF1α. (E) ELISA analysis of ALP levels in Ctrl and Hypoxia groups. (F) Runx2, OCN, and Osx biomarkers were assessed using WB. ***P < 0.001, Student’s t-test

Furthermore, cells in the hypoxia-treated group exhibited a typical osteoblast morphology, further supporting their enhanced osteogenic ability. An increase in ALP levels is generally considered a sign of increased osteoblast activity during osteogenesis. Higher ALP levels are usually associated with the proliferation and differentiation of osteoblasts, reflecting their enhanced osteogenic ability. In this study, we measured ALP levels using ELISA and observed that ALP was significantly higher in the Hypoxia group than in the Ctrl group (P < 0.001, Fig. 1E). WB analysis confirmed that Runx2, OCN, and Osx—markers of osteogenic differentiation, osteoblasts, and bone formation, respectively—were highly expressed in the Hypoxia group than in the Ctrl group (Fig. 1F), indicating enhanced osteogenic capacity under hypoxia treatments. Overall, these findings demonstrate that hypoxia enhances the osteogenic capacity of BMSCs.

MeRIP-seq revealed DEGs under hypoxia treatment

MeRIP-seq was performed on BMSCs under Ctrl and hypoxia conditions. A total of 8,079 DEGs were identified, with 3,656 upregulated and 4,423 downregulated in the Hypoxia versus Ctrl comparison. The volcano plot and heatmap are presented in Fig. 2A-B. SLC2A3, allograft inflammatory factor 1, and small ubiquitin-like modifier/sentrin-specific protease 1 were upregulated in the Hypoxia group (Fig. 2B). GO analysis revealed that DEGs were mainly enriched in processes such as RNA secondary structure unwinding, positive regulation of sodium ion transmembrane transporter activity, positive regulation of angiogenesis, DNA binding, blood vessel development, and localization to the apical plasma membrane (Fig. 2C). KEGG analysis revealed that DEGs were associated with viral myocarditis, transcriptional misregulation in cancer, Staphylococcus aureus infection, and focal adhesion (Fig. 2D). In addition, to validate the sequencing results, the RNA enriched by MeRIP was utilized for QPCR analysis. Among the top 10 DEGs with upregulated m6A modification levels, QPCR detection revealed that only six genes exhibited elevating m6A modification levels (Fig S1A). However, further QPCR analysis revealed that only four genes, SLC2A3, Sfrp1, Pappa1, and Pcdh20, exhibited concurrent increases in both mRNA expression levels and m6A modification levels in the hypoxic group (Fig S1B).

SLC2A3 enhanced osteogenic capacity through the glycolytic pathway

The role of SLC2A3 in regulating osteogenic capacity under hypoxia treatment was investigated by overexpressing or silencing SLC2A3 in BMSCs. Transfection efficiency was first verified by assessing SLC2A3 expression levels through immunofluorescence assay and WB analysis. Compared with the Ctrl group, SLC2A3 expression was upregulated in the Hypoxia group (Fig. 3A-B), consistent with MeRIP-seq results. Furthermore, expression was markedly increased in the Hypoxia + SLC2A3 group and substantially decreased in the Hypoxia + SLC2A3 siRNA group compared with Hypoxia + vector and Hypoxia + NC groups, respectively, confirming successful transfection. The osteogenic markers Runx2, OCN, and Osx were then evaluated. Their expression levels were upregulated in the Hypoxia + SLC2A3 group but suppressed in the Hypoxia + SLC2A3 siRNA group (Fig. 3C). Compared with the Hypoxia + NC group, ALP level was significantly reduced in the Hypoxia + SLC2A3 siRNA group (P < 0.01, Fig. 3D). Conversely, it was markedly increased in the Hypoxia + SLC2A3 group compared with the Hypoxia + vector group (P < 0.001). Alizarin Red staining further revealed enhanced red calcification in the SLC2A3-overexpressing group and reduced calcification in the silenced SLC2A3 group (Fig. 3E), supporting the role of SLC2A3 in osteogenic ability.

SLC2A3 enhances osteogenic capacity through the glycolytic pathway. (A) Immunofluorescence assay showed the expression level of SLC2A3. (B-C) WB analysis of SLC2A3, Runx2, OCN, and Osx expression levels in groups. (D) ELISA analysis of ALP levels in groups. (E) Alizarin Red staining illustrating osteogenic capacity. (F) ATP levels. (G) Glucose uptake. (H) WB analysis of HK2, PKM2, and LDHA expression levels in groups. (I) The detection of extracellular acidification rate (ECAR). **P < 0.01, ***P < 0.001, one-way ANOVA

Osteoblasts require substantial energy to synthesize and secrete the bone matrix, with ATP serving as the primary energy source; thus, high ATP levels typically indicate active osteoblasts. Consequently, glycolysis may be essential for osteogenic capacity. We observed that ATP levels were higher in the Hypoxia + SLC2A3 group than in the Hypoxia + vector group (P < 0.001) and lower in the Hypoxia + SLC2A3 siRNA group than in the Hypoxia + NC group (P < 0.01, Fig. 3F), indicating more active osteoblasts in the SLC2A3-overexpressing condition. Glucose uptake analysis further helps understand how osteoblasts regulate metabolism to support bone formation. The glucose uptake was considerably increased in the Hypoxia + SLC2A3 group and reduced in the Hypoxia + SLC2A3 siRNA group when compared with Hypoxia + vector or Hypoxia + NC groups (P < 0.01, P < 0.001, Fig. 3G). WB analysis revealed that the protein expression levels of HK2, PKM2, and LDHA were upregulated under hypoxia (Fig. 3H). Additionally, their expression was enhanced by SLC2A3 overexpression and suppressed by SLC2A3 silencing. Detection of extracellular acidification rate (ECAR) revealed that intervening in the expression of SLC2A3 can alter the glycolytic capacity of BMSCs under hypoxic conditions (Fig. 3I). Collectively, these findings demonstrate that SLC2A3 enhances osteogenic capacity through the glycolytic pathway.

METTL3 promotes osteogenic capacity through the glycolytic pathway

The m6A expression level was assessed in Ctrl and Hypoxia groups. m6A levels were markedly higher in the Hypoxia group than in the Ctrl group (P < 0.001, Fig. 4A). To identify the potential methyltransferase responsible, we evaluated the expression of METTL3, METTL4, and METTL14. WB analysis revealed that METTL3 was markedly upregulated in the Hypoxia group compared with the Ctrl group, whereas METTL4 and METTL14 exhibited minor differences between groups (Fig. 4B).Immunofluorescence assays also demonstrated the expression and cellular distribution of METTL3 (Fig. 4C).

Subsequently, the regulation of METTL3 on osteogenic capacity and the association between METTL3 and SLC2A3 were investigated by silencing and overexpressing METTL3 in BMSCs. WB analysis revealed that METTL3 and SLC2A3 were highly expressed in the METTL3 group and lowly expressed in the METTL3 siRNA group (Fig. 4D). Immunofluorescence assays confirmed that intervening in the expression of METTL3 can affect the protein expression of SLC2A3 (Fig. 4E). These results suggest successful transfection and indicate that SLC2A3 m6A modification may be mediated by METTL3, thereby promoting osteogenic capacity. The osteogenic markers Runx2, OCN, and Osx were considerably upregulated in the METTL3 group and downregulated in the METTL3 siRNA group (Fig. 4F), supporting a promotive role of METTL3 in osteogenesis. Compared with the NC group, ALP levels, glucose uptake, and ATP production were significantly reduced in the METTL3 siRNA group (P < 0.01), but markedly increased in the METTL3 group compared with the Vector group (P < 0.001, Fig. 4G–I). Alizarin Red staining further revealed larger calcification areas in the METTL3 group and smaller areas in the METTL3 siRNA group (Fig. 4J), reinforcing the promoting effect of METTL3 on osteogenic ability. HK2, PKM2, and LDHA were also highly expressed in the METTL3 group and reduced in the METTL3 siRNA group (Fig. 4K). Furthermore, ECAR detection indicated that high expression levels of METTL3 are associated with elevated glycolytic capacity (Fig. 4L). Collectively, these findings indicate that METTL3 promotes osteogenic capacity through the glycolytic pathway.

BMSC hypoxia upregulates METTL3, which facilitates osteogenic capacity by activating m6A modification of SLC2A3

To verify the regulatory effect of METTL3 on the expression level of SLC2A3, BMSCs were stimulated with an inhibitor of METTL3 (S-Adenosylhomocysteine, SAH). The results of the RIP assay showed that the detectable expression level of SLC2A3 decreased after SAH treatment (Fig. 5A), indicating a reduction in its m6A modification level. Meanwhile, the mRNA expression of SLC2A3 was suppressed by SAH (Fig. 5B). WB and immunofluorescence assays revealed that the protein level of SLC2A3 also decreased in the SAH-stimulated group (Fig. 5C-D). Moreover, the ECAR in the SAH-stimulated group was suppressed (Fig. 5E).

As described above, SLC2A3 expression was increased by METTL3 overexpression and suppressed by METTL3 siRNA. A rescue experiment was conducted by silencing SLC2A3 in overexpressed METTL3 transfections. First, we assessed SLC2A3 and METTL3 expression levels in Vector, METTL3, METTL3 + NC, and METTL3 + SLC2A3 siRNA groups. METTL3 expression was significantly elevated in overexpressed METTL3 groups. SLC2A3 expression was also upregulated in these groups but reduced in the METTL3 + SLC2A3 siRNA group (Fig. 5F), confirming successful transfection. Similarly, glucose uptake, ATP production, and the expression of HK2, PKM2, and LDHA were all significantly increased in METTL3 and METTL3 + NC groups but were substantially reduced when transfected with SLC2A3 siRNA (P < 0.001, Fig. 5G–I). Compared with the METTL3 + NC group, expression levels of Runx2, OCN, Osx, and ALP were notably decreased in the METTL3 + SLC2A3 siRNA group (Fig. 5J), indicating the rescuing role of SLC2A3 siRNA in regulating osteogenic capacity. The red calcification areas in the Alizarin Red stained image was increased by overexpressed METTL3, while it was decreased when transfected with SLC2A3 siRNA (Fig. 5K). WB analysis revealed that the changes in the expression levels of HK2, PKM2, and LDHA were consistent with those of SLC2A3 (Fig. 5L). Correspondingly, the ECAR was also affected by SLC2A3 siRNA (Fig. 5M). These results demonstrate that METTL3 promotes osteogenic capacity by activating m6A modification of SLC2A3 through the glycolytic pathway.

BMSC hypoxia promotes METTL3 and enhances osteogenic capacity by activating SLC2A3 m6A modification through the glycolytic pathway

Based on the above results, the binding relationship between METTL3 and its target gene SLC2A3 was confirmed using RIP and pull-down analyses. Compared with the IgG control, the METTL3 antibody significantly enriched SLC2A3 (P < 0.001, Fig. 6A). Conversely, the SLC2A3 probe enriched more METTL3 protein than the antisense probe (Fig. 6B), confirming the interaction between SLC2A3 and METTL3. To further verify the role of glycolysis in regulating osteogenic capacity, we treated BMSCs with the glycolysis inhibitor 2-DG. SLC2A3 expression was significantly elevated in SLC2A3 and SLC2A3 + 2-DG groups compared with the Vector group (Fig. 6C). However, no significant difference in SLC2A3 expression was observed between SLC2A3 and SLC2A3 + 2-DG groups, indicating that glycolysis inhibition does not directly alter SLC2A3 expression. In contrast, the expressions of Runx2, OCN, and Osx, which were upregulated by SLC2A3 overexpression, were markedly reduced by the glycolysis inhibitor (Fig. 6D). Similarly, glucose uptake, ATP production, and the expression of HK2, PKM2, and LDHA were significantly decreased in the SLC2A3 + 2-DG group compared with the SLC2A3 group (P < 0.001, Fig. 6E–G). Alizarin Red staining revealed that adding a glycolysis inhibitor reduced the areas of red calcification (Fig. 6H). These results demonstrate that BMSC hypoxia promotes METTL3 and facilitates osteogenic capacity by activating SLC2A3 m6A modification through the glycolytic pathway.

SLC2A3 overexpression facilitated fracture healing

A fracture model was established and treated with hypoxic BMSCs or hypoxic BMSCs transfected with overexpressed SLC2A3 to investigate the in vivo function of SLC2A3. On day 1 post-surgery, X-ray and micro-CT imaging revealed clear bone defects in the right leg across all four groups (Fig. 7A–C). Thirty days post-surgery, high-density signals were detected at the bone defect sites in HY-BMSC, HY-BMSC-vector, and HY-BMSC + SLC2A3 groups, indicating callus formation, whereas no such signals were observed in the BMSC group. The high-density imaging area was further enlarged in the HY-BMSC + SLC2A3 group compared with the HY-BMSC-vector group, indicating enhanced callus formation mediated by SLC2A3 overexpression. Gross examination of the dissected surgical site in SD rats confirmed the imaging results. By day 30, newly formed heterotopic bone was separated. Expressions of SLC2A3, METTL3, Runx2, HK2, PKM2, and LDHA were notably expressed in the HY-BMSC-SLC2A3 group, followed by HY-BMSC-Vector and HY-BMSC groups, compared with the BMSC group (Fig. 7D).

(B) Gross observation of the surgical site in Sprague–Dawley rats (n = 3). (C) Micro-CT analysis of fracture tissues (n = 3). The structural characteristics of fracture tissue, collagen distribution, cell activity, and healing status were evaluated using H&E staining, methylene blue, and Annona Red O/Fast Green staining. H&E staining revealed that the HY-BMSC + SLC2A3 group exhibited newly formed bone trabeculae and myeloid tissue. Some trabeculae are interconnected to create a network structure extending into the bone marrow cavity, with numerous osteoblasts aligned in an orderly cuboidal pattern along the periphery of the new bone (Fig. 8A). Methylene blue staining, which highlights cell survival and activity, revealed more abundant nuclei, regular morphology, and clearer staining features in the HY-BMSC + SLC2A3 group compared with the HY-BMSC-vector group, indicating greater proliferative capacity (Fig. 8B). More red collagen fibers were observed in the HY-BMSC + SLC2A3 group than in the HY-BMSC-vector group (Fig. 8C), indicating enhanced tissue formation. Moreover, the tissues in the HY-BMSC + SLC2A3 group were more evenly distributed and organized. These staining results suggest that SLC2A3 overexpression promotes fracture healing.

Discussion

Fracture healing is often prolonged, leading to patient discomfort and inconvenience. Therefore, accelerating healing after a fracture is a critical clinical goal. This study investigated biomarkers involved in fracture healing under hypoxic conditions and elucidated their underlying molecular mechanisms. We found that hypoxia enhances the osteogenic capacity of BMSCs. MeRIP-seq revealed that SLC2A3 is differentially expressed under hypoxia, suggesting that it may promote osteogenesis via the glycolytic pathway. METTL3 binds to SLC2A3 and enhances osteogenesis via the glycolytic pathway. Notably, CoCl2-induced hypoxia increased METTL3 expression and facilitated osteogenesis by activating SLC2A3 m6A modification via the glycolytic pathway.

BMSCs are commonly used in bone regeneration owing to their ability to differentiate into multiple lineages and their potential to enhance osteoinduction and osteogenesis [23]. BMSCs promote osteoblast differentiation, angiogenesis, inflammation regulation, and cell–cell interaction by releasing various bioactive factors. Multiple drugs can target BMSCs to accelerate fracture healing by enhancing angiogenesis and osteogenesis [24, 25]. In vivo, BMSCs are exposed to hypoxic environments; however, they are typically cultured under normoxic conditions (21% O2) in vitro, creating a discrepancy between experimental and physiological settings. Hypoxic preconditioning of BMSCs may enhance their paracrine effects, and their role has been widely studied in fractures. For example, exosomes derived from hypoxic BMSC accelerate bone fracture healing by delivering miR-126 [26]. However, the effects of hypoxia on osteogenic differentiation remain controversial. Peripheral blood mesenchymal stem cells exposed to hypoxia (1% O2) exhibit reduced osteogenic differentiation capacity [27, 28]. Although hypoxia-induced reactive oxygen species (ROS) may compromise stem cell viability, moderate ROS levels can paradoxically promote osteogenesis through HIF-1α activation [29]. This duality underscores the context-dependent effects of different hypoxia modalities on osteogenic outcomes. Notably, existing literature indicates that CoCl2-induced hypoxia preserves cellular homeostasis without significant apoptotic elevation [30]. In this study, CoCl2 treatment enhanced BMSC osteogenesis. Unlike physiological hypoxia, chemical hypoxia induced by CoCl2 mimics oxygen deprivation by interfering with cellular oxygen-sensing mechanisms, primarily by preventing HIF-1α degradation [31]. However, a recent study reported that CoCl2-induced hypoxia can promote glycolysis in cancer cells [32]. In addition, high concentrations of Co2⁺ ions exhibit cytotoxic effects [33], which means the optimal concentration of CoCl2 requires further experimental investigation across different cell types and experimental contexts. Lu et al. reported that CoCl2-induced hypoxia promotes osteogenic differentiation of ligament cells through the HIF-1α–BMP4 axis [34]. Consequently, hypoxic preconditioning may be an effective and promising strategy to enhance the therapeutic potential of BMSCs in fracture healing.

In our study, SLC2A3 was significantly upregulated under hypoxic conditions. This finding is consistent with Mimura et al. [35], who reported that SLC2A3 is a hypoxia-responsive gene. SLC2A3 is also an endogenous marker of hypoxia in prostate cancer [36]. We further investigated the role of SLC2A3 in fracture healing and, for the first time, demonstrated that SLC2A3 enhances osteogenic capacity through the glycolytic pathway. Glucose is essential for osteoblasts to meet their physiological demands [37], and proper regulation of glucose metabolism is critical for osteogenesis [38]. SLC2A3, also known as GLUT3, functions as a cellular glucose transporter. Hochrein et al. reported that SLC2A3-dependent glucose uptake regulates a metabolic-transcriptional circuit controlling Th17 cell pathogenicity [39]. SLC2A3 overexpression significantly increases glucose uptake and ATP production. Glycolytic enzymes such as HK2, PKM2, and LDHA regulate cell proliferation, differentiation, and bone matrix synthesis by regulating energy metabolism and metabolic reprogramming of osteoblasts, therefore influencing osteogenic capacity [40]. We found that SLC2A3 overexpression elevated the expression of HK2, PKM2, and LDHA, suggesting a synergistic relationship between SLC2A3 and these glycolytic genes. Consistent with our findings, Lei et al. discovered that aspirin reduced SLC2A3 expression and downregulated glycolytic enzymes, including HK2, PFKM, PKM2, and LDHA [41]. Similarly, hypoxia-induced SLC2A3 overexpression promotes cellular glucose uptake, which may facilitate glycolysis in esophageal squamous cell carcinoma cells [42]. Moreover, the transcription of HK2 and PKM2 is regulated by HIF-1α [43, 44], suggesting that SLC2A3 affects HK2, PKM2, and LDHA expression through glucose intake and HIF-1α-mediated transcriptional regulation. Other metabolic regulators, such as coactivator-associated arginine methyltransferase 1, have also been implicated in osteogenesis and osteoclast differentiation [45]. Conversely, low-glucose conditions suppress osteogenic differentiation, inhibit cell progression, and induce autophagy [37, 46]. Therefore, SLC2A3 is a crucial hypoxia-responsive marker that regulates osteoblast differentiation, function, and bone health by regulating glucose uptake and metabolism.

m6A methylation occurs during the processing of nascent pre-mRNA and is catalyzed by a methyltransferase complex that includes METTL3 [17]. Histone lactylation upregulates METTL3 and drives ligamentum flavum ossification by enhancing IGF2BP1-mediated BMP2 m6A methylation [47]. In this study, we examined methylation in BMSCs under hypoxic conditions and observed that METTL3 expression was significantly upregulated, which exhibited positive effects on osteogenic capacity. In our study, METTL3-mediated m6A methylation regulates BMSC osteogenic differentiation, consistent with previous studies [48–50]. METTL3 is recognized as the primary methyltransferase essential for m6A methylation and a key regulator of bone regeneration [51]. Alterations in METTL3 expression, whether by deletion or overexpression, affect global m6A levels and directly influence cell survival, stem cell maintenance, and lineage specification [52, 53].

RIP and pull-down analyses confirmed for the first time that METTL3 binds to SLC2A3 in fracture healing. Ning et al. reported that METTL3 may promote SLC2A3 expression in placentas exposed to hyperglycemia [54]. In contrast, Wang et al. suggested that SLC2A3 m6A methylation is regulated by another methyltransferase, METTL14, during osteoblast differentiation of MC3T3-E1 cells [55]. Our findings, together with theirs, support the conclusion that SLC2A3 participates in osteogenesis through the glycolytic pathway. METTL3’s role in regulating cellular glycolysis through m6A modification of genes has also been documented in various cancers [53, 56]. Enhancing glycolytic activity is a common mechanism for promoting stemness in cells, both in vivo and in induced pluripotent stem cells, which shift toward glycolysis to maintain stem-like properties [57]. Deng et al. [38]. demonstrated that mitofusin2 knockdown enhances BMSC osteogenic differentiation by promoting aerobic glycolysis through the Wnt/β-catenin signaling pathway. Glycolysis also contributes to three-dimensional matrix stiffness and supports stem cell osteogenesis [58]. Accordingly, glycolysis appears to be essential for regulating BMSC osteogenic capacity under hypoxia. However, differences between the CoCl2-induced and physiological hypoxia, as well as interspecies variation between humans and rats, necessitate further human studies to elucidate the precise molecular mechanisms underlying hypoxia-mediated osteogenesis. Our findings indicate that the hypoxic microenvironment specifically activates the METTL3–SLC2A3–glycolysis axis to promote osteogenesis. In the future, slow-release systems targeting fracture sites, such as hypoxia-responsive nanomaterials, can be designed to locally enhance this pathway while avoiding systemic effects.

Conclusions

This study investigated biomarkers with essential roles in promoting fracture healing. We demonstrated that hypoxia in BMSCs upregulates the methyltransferase METTL3 and mediates SLC2A3 m6A modification, thereby facilitating fracture healing through the glycolytic pathway. At the m6A modification level, we revealed the impact of hypoxia on BMSC differentiation and established a link between SLC2A3-mediated glycolysis and fracture healing. These findings broaden the theoretical foundation for developing strategies to accelerate fracture healing.
