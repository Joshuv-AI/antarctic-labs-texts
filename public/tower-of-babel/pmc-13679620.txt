Learning cancer genetics from domestic cats
Authors: Al-Hassan M Mustafa, Oliver H Krämer
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Highlights•Feline cancers resemble human cancers genetically but there are species-specific differences.•Analyzing tumors of household cats delivers insights into a natural model for cancer research.•Genomic profiling holds the promise to accelerate translational cancer research by identifying therapeutic opportunities.•The large offspring of cats, together with detailed lifetime histories, may deliver new knowledge on genetic and environmental factors of tumorigenesis.•Careful interpretation is needed because differences in drug metabolism may influence treatment responses and drug-associated toxicity.Keywords: Comparative oncology, Feline cancer genomics, Spontaneous tumor models, Cross-species tumor biology, Translational cancer therapeutics

Domestic cats, the species Felis catus, evolved from the African wildcat, Felis lybica. Possible centers of early domestication are the Neolithic Levant ∼9500 years ago and Pharaonic Egypt ∼3500 years ago [1]. As beloved companions, veterinarians see feline patients suffering from benign and malignant tumors. Francis and colleagues sequenced 493 feline tumor samples from 13 histopathologically defined tumor types [2]. Except for mammary cancers, cancerous and corresponding normal tissues were from female and male cats with a median age of 11 years [2]. Cats are regarded as middle-aged at 7–10 years, senior at 11–14 years, and geriatric once they reach 15 years of age [3]. Over 80% of the cats were nonpedigree shorthair (73.1%) and longhair cats. Twenty-six feline patients were from various breeds, including 12 Siamese (2.2%), 9 Maine Coon (1.8%), and 3 Norwegian Forest cats [2]. Hence, a strong bias on tumorigenesis due to breeding strategies is unlikely.

How can human cancer research benefit from the analysis of feline tumors? Cats that are maintained in protected environments with adequate nutrition and veterinary care frequently have litters of five or even more surviving kittens and this can happen more than once per year [4]. Professional or dedicated private breeders carefully pair animals to enrich desired traits. This can allow numerous litters from the same pairing to be followed over time. Such large numbers of offspring, detailed pedigree information, and the availability of genetically related individuals across generations facilitate the identification of hereditary cancer risk and gene-environment interactions. Moreover, the occurrence of spontaneous tumors in such pets resembles human situations more than experimentally induced tumors (e.g., in immunocompromised mice that receive tumor xenotransplants). On the other hand, variables that are not controlled under experimental laboratory conditions must be clarified. For example, were any of the 57 lung adenocarcinoma cases exposed to household tobacco smoke, or can passive smoke exposure be excluded as a contributing factor to feline tumor development? Does the same apply to the other cases? What can we conclude from this? This likewise applies to other parameters. When applicable, comprehensive life history data, together with systematic documentation of environmental exposures, diet, obesity and feeding habits, neutering status and age at neutering, reproductive history, and other living conditions —obtained as shelter records or owner-reported histories — could be invaluable for distinguishing genomically driven tumorigenesis from environmentally acquired cancers. For example, dysbiosis, which describes an imbalance of microbial homeostasis, was linked to feline oral squamous cell carcinoma [17]. Integrating microbiome profiling and dietary history into spontaneous feline tumor models could clarify to which extent distinct tumor susceptibilities are driven by diet and microbial landscape. Importantly, the study by Francis and colleagues selected common and rare tumor types that reflect human histopathological categories. Rare tumors were included because identifying similarities between feline and human tumors could uncover potential therapeutic strategies for tumors in both species.

Francis and colleagues carried out cross-species comparisons of common mutations in their feline tumor panel and human cancer datasets. A total of 1039 human cancer-associated genes was the basis for the feline cancer-associated bait set, resulting in a final panel comprising 978 feline orthologs. Using the FelCat9 assembly reference, the authors identified tumor-specific mutations, including somatic single-nucleotide variants (SNVs), multi-nucleotide variants (MNVs), and insertions/deletions (indels shorter than 100 base pairs), and they conducted whole-exome sequencing of feline mammary tumors [2]. Although these analyses must be seen as comparisons to larger numbers of human specimens, they disclose striking similarities. The tumor suppressor p53 (encoded by the TP53 gene) is mutated in 34% of all human tumors and 33% of all tumors in the feline cancer study (Fig. 1). As in humans, most of these aberrations were in the DNA-binding domain of p53 [2]. Such lesions disable the ability of this transcription factor to induce genes that promote cell cycle arrest, apoptosis, and other modes of cell death [5]. Additional recurrent signatures in 20% of feline tumors included copy number gains of the transcription factor MYC and losses in the genes encoding the tumor suppressor PTEN (which controls pro-proliferative PI3K/AKT signaling) as well as the pro-apoptotic protein FAS. Although these findings are reminiscent of the human situation, a different picture emerged for the gene encoding the small GTPase RAS, which is hyperactivated due to point mutations in 25% of human cancers (Fig. 2). Francis and colleagues found no activating point mutations in gene loci encoding H/K/N-RAS [2] (Fig. 1). Nonetheless, other mutations and copy number gains were detectable in the RAS gene loci. Basal cell carcinoma samples showed KRAS (4/39) and NRAS (4/39) copy number gains. Another study reported comparably low frequencies of activating point mutations in the RAS target BRAF. RAS proteins activate RAF kinases to induce pro-proliferative MEK-ERK signaling [6]. Although the constitutively active BRAFV600E mutation occurs in over 50% of human melanomas [6], the corresponding BRAFV597E mutation was found in only one melanoma out of 169 analyzed feline tumor samples [7]. This scenario differs in dogs, where BRAF and NRAS mutations are frequently found in melanomas [8]. What could be the reasons for such absence of RAS-activating point mutations in feline tumor specimens? There could be genomic and non-genomic reasons. Mutants of RAS are degraded upon temperature shock [9]. The normal body temperature of a cat ranges from 37.7°−39.2°C, which is higher than in humans (36.5°–37.4°C) [10]. Such higher baseline body temperatures of cats may destabilize oncogenic RAS-RAF signaling complexes because the thermostability of mutant RAS is lower than its wild-type counterpart. Consequently, cells that acquire these mutations would be counter-selected (i.e., the mutant proteins fail to function or cause proteotoxic stress, preventing clonal expansion of cells with RAS point mutants). Alternatively, the RAS gene loci in feline and human cells might harbor genetic and epigenetic traits that disfavor or favor the acquisition of mutations. Another possible explanation evolves from the finding that constitutively active RAS molecules can cause DNA replication stress due to cell hyperproliferation [11]. Failing to cope with such stress might trigger a “self-destruct” mechanism of RAS-mutated feline cancer cells. This may also be induced by an unidentified cat-specific activation of tumor suppressors in cells with hyperactive RAS. Another option is that wild-type RAS is already more active in healthy feline cells than in human cells and that this is not critical if there are no tumor-promoting mutations. Differences in diet, lifestyle (drugs, use of cosmetics and supplements), metabolism, and variable circadian rhythms [12] may also contribute to divergent patterns of gene mutations in cancer cells from household cats and humans. Clearly, more research on this topic is necessary.

Schematic model illustrates wild-type KRAS (left) and mutant KRAS (right) signaling. Wild-type RAS alternates between two distinct conformational states, the guanosine diphosphate (GDP)-bound (inactive) to the guanosine triphosphate (GTP)-bound (active). Activated RAS exchanges its GDP for GTP and drives a phosphorylation cascade of the serine/threonine mitogen-activated protein kinases (MAPKs). MAPKs phosphorylate various effector proteins and thereby stimulate cell proliferation, survival, or differentiation. Normal RAS switches itself off again by hydrolyzing its bound GTP to GDP. Cycling between GDP and GTP is facilitated by the guanine nucleotide exchange factor (GEF) and GTPase activating enzyme (GAP), which are essential for normal functioning of the RAS signaling pathway. Mutant KRAS fails to hydrolyze GTP to GDP and cannot effectively switch off and is constitutively active, resulting in abnormal hyperproliferation, increased survival, and impaired differentiation of cancer cells. Sequencing analyses generate extensive datasets with the aim of being informative on therapeutic approaches. F-box and WD-40 domain-containing protein 7 (FBXW7) is a member of the F-box protein family within the ubiquitin-proteasome system. FBXW7 acts as a tumor suppressor through its ability to promote the proteasomal degradation of proto-oncoproteins and cell cycle-activating proteins, such as MYC and cyclin E [13]. When comparing the sensitivity of FBXW7 mutant and wild-type feline mammary tumoroids to the vinca alkaloids vincristine and vinorelbine, the mutant cells were 8 to 14-fold more inhibited in their growth [2] (Fig. 1). Feline cutaneous mast cell tumors show activating mutations in the tyrosine kinase (TK) KIT and the transcription factor retinoic acid-activated receptor-α. These may render tumor cells susceptible to the TK inhibitor imatinib and pharmacologic doses of retinoic acid. Other mutations were noted in the TKs PDGFRα and JAK1 as well as in the acetyltransferase CREBBP/CBP [2]. Drugs against these enzymes are available [14,15]. Francis and colleagues summarize driver genes that are druggable and show synthetic lethality with established anti-cancer drugs (Table S12 in [2]). Caution regarding interpretations on toxicity in humans must be paid when drugs are given to cats. Cats and humans metabolize various drugs and other xenobiotics differently. Cats have lack glucuronidation and glycine conjugation systems, and cats and humans have divergent liver cytochromes for phase I biotransformation [16].

It will be exciting to see how investigating genomic landscapes of nonhuman cancers contributes to a better understanding of oncogenesis and aids in combating this disease by the discovery of life-saving interventions.
