Bimetallic nanozymes with galvanic cell effects eradicate intratumoral bacteria and tumor cells to overcome drug resistance and potentiate antitumor immune responses for tumor therapy
Authors: Jianlin Wang, Zhenning Su, Xin Zhang, Siyu Wang, Xihua Chen, Xiangbo Xu, Bin He, Yufeng Zheng
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractIntratumoral bacteria have attracted considerable attention as critical components of the tumor microenvironment (TME) that influence tumor initiation, progression, therapeutic efficacy, and immune responses. Conventional antibiotic approaches for eradicating intratumoral bacteria are limited by severe side effects, disruption of microbial homeostasis, and the emergence of multidrug resistance, while offering no direct tumoricidal effects. Here, we developed bimetallic Copper-Tin nanozymes (CuSn nanozymes) with a galvanic cell effect to overcome bacteria-associated challenges, overcome drug resistance, and enhance cancer therapy. The nanozymes can generate highly active electrons and metal ions at tumor sites without external stimulation, achieving massive reactive oxygen species (ROS) production and effective tumor eradication. The released electrons disrupt bacterial electron transport chains (ETC), thereby inhibiting energy synthesis and achieving potent antibacterial effects. The release of copper ions further enhances ROS-mediated antibacterial activity. Moreover, bacterial eradication promotes antitumor immune activation, further enhancing therapeutic outcomes. Using breast, endometrial, and liver cancer models, we demonstrated the broad applicability of this approach. This work not only offers a new paradigm for designing innovative bimetallic nanozymes with enhanced catalytic activity but also provides a potential therapeutic approach for simultaneously eliminating bacteria and tumors, overcoming chemoresistance, and offering effective treatment without reliance on conventional antibiotics or chemotherapeutics for microbiota-enriched tumors.Keywords: Alloy nanozymes, Intratumoral bacteria, Antimicrobial agents, Tumor therapy, Galvanic cells

Introduction

In recent years, there has been growing evidence that human tumors contain large numbers of live symbiotic microbial communities. These intratumoral microbiota are more likely to be integral components of the tumor tissue itself rather than accidently present because of pathogenic infection, such as in breast cancer, colorectal cancer, lung cancer, pancreatic cancer, etc. [[1], [2], [3], [4]].

The tumor microenvironment, as the habitat of tumors, influences the behavior of cancer cells, immune responses, and therapeutic outcomes. Cancer cells promote tumor metastasis and evade treatment through interactions with the tumor microenvironment and immune cells [5]. Tumor-resident bacteria are predominantly intracellular, particularly within tumor cells and immune cells, and the microbial communities vary among different tumor types [3]. Intratumoral bacteria can support tumor growth [6], enhance tumor cell viability, promote metastasis and colonization [1], and impair tumor chemotherapy sensitivity [[7], [8], [9]], thereby accelerating tumor progression. For instance, Fusobacterium nucleatum (F. nucleatum), an anaerobic bacterium significantly enriched in breast tumors, hinders the infiltration of immune cells in breast tumors and facilitates the advancement of tumor growth and metastasis [[10], [11], [12], [13]]. Colitis caused by Escherichia coli (E. coli) can promote the development of colorectal cancer [14]. Staphylococcus aureus (S. aureus) and E.coli are abundant in lung cancer and facilitated immune suppression regulated by T regulatory cells (Tregs), potentially accelerating liver and prostate tumors progression [15,16]. Intratumoral bacteria contribute to tumor initiation, progression, and metastasis. Additionally, tumor-associated bacteria, as a component of the tumor microenvironment, can directly modulate responses to chemotherapy and immunotherapy, leading to significantly increased drug resistance. This is also one of the reasons for the emergence of resistance to chemotherapy drugs and antibiotics in clinical treatment, making an already challenging cancer even more difficult to treat. Clearly, besides the well-known genetic, epigenetic, and tumor microenvironment regulatory factors, the tumor microbiota also serves as a critical mediator influencing cancer progression, metastasis, and resistance [1].

However, the most common method of eliminating bacteria is to use antibiotics, but traditional antibiotics used in clinical practice can cause serious side effects, including depletion of drug resistance, intestinal probiotic, disrupted body homeostasis, disease susceptibility, etc., especially for patients with malignant tumors. Moreover, the overuse of antibiotics can also cause the occurrence of multidrug resistant strains [17,18]. Meanwhile, in infected tumor models, antibiotics do not have a direct tumor cell killing effect and often need to be used in combination with other antitumor agents [17]. Therefore, using innovative strategies to kill cancer cells while also eliminating intratumoral bacteria, overcoming bacterial and tumor resistance, and preventing cancer progression and metastasis, the effective resolution of this critical scientific issue holds significant implications for cancer prevention and treatment.

Nanozymes are nanomaterials with intrinsic enzyme-like catalytic activities that catalyze biochemical reactions under physiological conditions. Compared with natural enzymes, nanozymes exhibit superior stability, tunable composition, scalable synthesis, and lower cost. Since Fe3O4 nanoparticles were first reported to possess intrinsic peroxidase-like activity, a variety of metal-based and alloy nanomaterials have been demonstrated to exhibit peroxidase (POD)-, catalase (CAT)-, superoxide dismutase (SOD)-, and oxidase (OXD)-like activities [19,20]. They have been universally utilized as nanozymes for tumor treatment [21]. Galvanic cells represent a classical electrochemical system that generates electrical energy through spontaneous redox reactions between two metals with different redox potentials in the presence of an electrolyte [22]. In a typical galvanic cell, the more reactive metal serves as the anode and undergoes oxidation, releasing metal ions into the surrounding medium while generating highly reactive electrons [[23], [24], [25]]. These electrons are subsequently transferred to the cathode driven by the potential difference between the two metals, thereby accelerating redox reactions within the system [26]. Importantly, this process not only enables continuous electron migration but also produces metal ions in situ, both of which can participate in catalytic and biological reactions.

Herein, we developed bimetallic CuSn nanozymes with galvanic cell effect that synergistically eliminate intratumoral bacteria and cancer cells, overcoming bacteria-related chemotherapy resistance and thereby enhancing cancer treatment. Notably, we chose Cu and Sn primarily for the following reasons: First, CuSn nanozymes have nanozyme properties and can produce large amounts of reactive oxygen species (ROS) without external stimulation, thereby inducing oxidative stress. Second, Cu and Sn form a galvanic cell structure, rapidly releasing metal ions while releasing large amounts of electrons, directly destroying biomolecules (proteins and DNA) and catalyzing reactions to produce cytotoxic substances, while also killing intratumoral bacteria and cancer cells, thereby enhancing the effectiveness of cancer treatment. Finally, Cu and Sn are essential and healthy elements in the human body, playing a crucial role in maintaining life activities and health. They exhibit excellent biocompatibility, biodegradability, and safety. As shown in Scheme 1, at the tumor sites, CuSn nanozymes efficiently produce ROS through their enzyme-like (peroxidase (POD)-like, glutathione oxidase (GSHOx)-like) properties and consume glutathione (GSH), causing redox imbalance. Moreover, CuSn nanozymes rapidly release large amounts of copper ions through their galvanic cell structure, amplifying ROS production. The resulting ROS could induce severe endoplasmic reticulum (ER) stress. Further study revealed that CuSn nanozymes could induce ferroptosis and cuproptosis via ROS generation, GSH consumption and ER stress, thereby eradicating cancers. On the other hand, the electrons released by CuSn nanozymes interfere with the electron transport chain of bacteria, resulting in rapid and efficient sterilization. In addition, the ROS caused by the release of Cu ions further enhance the antibacterial effect. More intriguingly, it was found that the dead bacteria in the tumor eliminated by CuSn nanozymes promoted antigen-presenting dendritic cells (DCs) maturation, thereby triggering T cells to destruct tumors and enhancing the antitumor immune response. Three cancer models (breast cancer, endometrial cancer, and liver cancer) were used to demonstrate the universality of this strategy. This strategy offers a practical approach for clinical treatment of cancer, enabling the simultaneous elimination of bacteria and cancer cells in tumors without the use of clinical drugs, overcoming drug resistance, and eradicating cancer, thereby simplifying the treatment process and demonstrating potential for clinical translation.

Results and discussion

Preparation and characterization of CuSn nanozymes

CuSn nanozymes were synthesized using CuCl2•2H2O and SnCl2•2H2O via a thermal solvent method (Fig. 1A) [27]. The scanning electron microscopy (SEM) image and energy dispersive spectrometry (EDS) elemental mapping of disclosed the nanostructure of CuSn nanozymes with an average diameter of approximately 200 nm (Fig. 1B; Fig. S1A, Supporting Information). According to the dynamic light scattering (DLS) analysis also indicates the hydrated particle size of CuSn nanozymes with narrow poly-dispersity index (PDI) (Fig. 1C). The zeta potential of CuSn nanozymes was −8.35 ± 4.87 (mV). Transmission electron microscopy (TEM) images revealed that the CuSn nanozymes displayed irregular particle morphologies. Moreover, similar morphologies and size distributions were observed across three independent synthesis batches, indicating that good reproducibility and consistency of the synthesis process (Fig. S2, Supporting Information). TEM and the elemental mapping of CuSn nanozymes clearly showed Cu and Sn elements in the composite, indicating the successful formation of the CuSn nanozymes and having good monodispersity. (Fig. 1C and D; Fig. S2, Supporting Information). X-ray Diffraction (XRD) analysis indicates the formation of an alloyed phase because of the shift of the main diffraction peaks relative to those of monometallic Cu and Sn diffraction patterns, thereby confirming the formation of alloy structures (Fig. 1E). X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy (XPS) was used to analyze the effect of alloying on the chemical states of CuSn alloy nanozymes. As shown in Fig. 1F, CuSn nanozymes contain relevant metallic elements (Sn, Cu). The Cu 2p and Sn 3d binding-energy peaks further confirmed the formation of CuSn nanozymes (Fig. 1F), consistent with the EDX analysis presented in Fig. S1B and C, supporting information. The valence states of Cu in CuSn nanozymes encompassed Cu0, and Cu2+. The Cu 2p3/2 and 2p1/2 for Cu0 were located at 932.17 and 951.87 eV, while those for Cu2+ are lying at 934.73 and 954.71 eV. Similarly, CuSn nanozymes exhibited the valence states of Sn0 and Sn4+. The Sn 3d3/2 and 3d5/2 for Sn0 were located at 485.00 and 493.50 eV, while those for Sn4+ are lying at 486.79 and 495.21 eV. To evaluate the structural and electrochemical stability of CuSn nanozymes in physiological environments, the CuSn nanozymes were incubated in PBS (pH 7.4). DLS analysis demonstrated negligible changes in hydrodynamic size and PDI in PBS after 7 days of storage, indicating good colloidal stability (Fig. S3, Supporting Information). TEM images after incubation confirmed that the morphological integrity of the CuSn nanozymes was largely preserved without significant aggregation or structural collapse (Fig. S2, Supporting Information). Above results show that we successfully synthesized CuSn nanozymes with alloy structures.

Multienzyme-like activities and galvanic cells properties of CuSn nanozymes

Due to the acidic nature of TME, we first evaluated the ion release efficiency of CuSn nanoenzymes in solutions at different pH values before proceeding with further applications. The ICP-MS analysis confirmed a pH-dependent release of Cu ions, with significantly accelerated ion dissolution observed under acidic conditions, indicating the pH-responsive degradability of CuSn nanoenzymes (Fig. S4, Supporting Information).

The CuSn nanozymes possess intrinsic multienzyme-like activities, similar to those of POD and GSHOx. Relatedly, as shown in Fig. 2A, the mutually reinforcing effects of these enzymatic reactions led to amplified levels of oxidative stress in tumor sites.

We comprehensively evaluated the POD-like catalytic activity of the CuSn nanozymes using the 3,3′,5,5′-tetramethylbenzidine (TMB) probe. TMB probes could react with ROS to generate blue products oxidzed-TMB (ox-TMB), displaying an obvious absorption peak value at 652 nm (Fig. 2A; Fig. S5A, Supporting Information). As shown in Fig. 2B–D, all groups exhibited ox-TMB absorption peak values at 652 nm, confirming the POD-like catalysis activities of the CuSn nanozymes in the presence of hydrogen peroxide (H2O2). As the pH decreased, the absorbance at 652 nm showed a noticeable increase, suggesting a higher generation of hydeoxyl radicals (•OH) in an acidic buffer solution (pH 5.0), whereas this production was restricted under neutral conditions (pH 7.4) (Fig. 2B). Furthermore, the ox-TMB absorbance exhibited a concentration-dependent increase when the CuSn nanozymes concentration was raised from 30 to 120 μg/mL (Fig. 2C). We also vary the concentrations of the substrate H2O2 to quantitatively assess their catalytic activities (Fig. 2D). To reveal the catalysis mechanism of POD-like enzymes, a steady-state kinetics analysis was conducted at a fixed concentration of CuSn nanozymes by changing the concentration of H2O2. The result was consistent with the classic Michaelis–Menten kinetics. Based on the corresponding Lineweaver–Burk plots, the enzyme kinetic parameters including the maximum reaction rate (Vmax) and Michaelis–Menten constant (Km) were calculated. According to Michaelis–Menten kinetic analysis in Fig. 2E and Lineweaver–Burk plot in Fig. 2F, the Km (16.35 mM) and Vmax (2.22 × 10−7 Ms−1) values indicated that CuSn nanozymes exhibited great affinity for H2O2 and could quickly convert it into •OH. The Vmax of CuSn nanozymes was 3.59-fold higher than that of classical Fe3O4 nanozyme, and a markedly lower Km value. Moreover, based on the values of KM and Vmax, the CuSn nanozymes showed superior POD-like catalytic activity compared to most of the reported nanozymes (Table S1, Supporting Information), indicating that the CuSn nanozymes possess a higher enzyme affinity and catalytic reaction rate.

The GSHOx-like activities of CuSn nanozymes can disrupt the intracellular antioxidant defense, amplifying oxidative damage. The abundant GSH in tumor cells, acting as an endogenous antioxidant, has been confirmed to scavenge ROS and diminish antitumor effects. Consequently, the GSH depletion could effectively boost the accumulation of ROS, thereby enhancing tumor treatment efficacy. We employed the consumption of GSH by CuSn nanozymes through 5,5′-dithiobis-(2-nitrobenzoic acid) (DTNB). DTNB is a reagent that could react with GSH to form yellow products, displaying an obvious absorption peak value at 412 nm (Fig. 2A, Fig. S5B, Supporting Information). Investigating the GSHOx-like function of CuSn nanozymes in the presence of GSH, the results showed that CuSn nanozymes can consume GSH (Fig. 2G). Upon increasing the concentration of CuSn nanozymes, there was a significant enhancement in GSH consumption (Fig. 2H). As shown in Fig. S6, supporting information, CuSn nanozymes induced a pH-dependent GSH depletion, with markedly greater depletion observed under acidic conditions. These results indicated that CuSn nanozymes possess GSHOx-like activity, leading to the consumption of GSH, which would exacerbate the imbalance of cellular redox for enhanced tumor therapeutic efficacy.

For CuSn nanozymes, as galvanic cells, a spontaneous redox reaction occurred when the metal electrode was placed into an aqueous environment. Specifically, electrons (e−) would flow from the negative electrode (Cu electrode, Cu-2e− = Cu2+) to the positive electrode (Sn electrode, O2 + 2H2O + 2e− = 4OH−), resulting in Cu to be etched by water and the production of Cu2+ and e− (Fig. 2I). To verify that the electrons in the conduction band of the cathode can be easily transferred to the surroundings, the catalytic ability of CuSn nanozymes in the oxygen reduction reaction was evaluated by electrochemistry analysis. As shown in Fig. 2J, the cyclic voltammogram (CV) curve of CuSn nanozymes group exhibit a greatly increased current signal, but a negligible peak was observed in the control group, demonstrating that the cathode had the superior ability to transfer electrons to the surrounding proteins, DNA, H2O2, etc. [[28], [29], [30]] The galvanic cell represents an electrochemical process of corrosion, in which a lower corrosion potential increases the likelihood of an oxidative corrosion reaction for Cu2+ production. The Tafel curve results indicated that the presence of the galvanic cell structure significantly reduced the corrosion potential, thereby elucidating the enhanced reactivity of CuSn nanozymes with water (Fig. 2K). Based on the electrochemical impedance spectroscopy (EIS) results, CuSn nanozymes showed lower electrochemical impedance and stronger electron transport capacity than Cu nanozymes (Fig. 2L). To further validate the occurrence of electron transfer and redox processes during exposure to aqueous environments, XPS analysis was performed on CuSn nanozymes before and after incubation in water. After water treatment, the Cu 2p spectrum showed a noticeable decrease in the Cu0 peak intensity accompanied by an increase in the Cu2+ signal, indicating the oxidation of Cu0 to Cu2+ (Fig. S7A, Supporting Information). However, the valence state of Sn remained virtually unchanged throughout the reaction process, indicating that Sn acted as an inert anode within the galvanic cell structure (Fig. S7B, Supporting Information). These valence state variations directly support the occurrence of spontaneous redox reactions within the CuSn nanozymes upon exposure to aqueous media, further confirming the presence of a galvanic cell structure. Then, further evaluation of cell activity showed that CuSn nanozymes had stronger cytotoxicity than Cu nanozymes (Fig. 2M). Moreover, to further prove that the galvanic-cell effect leads to enhanced ion release, intracellular copper ion detection was performed. Compared to cells treated with Cu nanozymes alone, the CuSn nanozymes group exhibited a higher level of intracellular copper ions (Fig. 2N). These data presented herein demonstrated that successfully constructed nanozymes have a metal galvanic cell structure. This enables the rapid and efficient release of active metal ions, which was previously a challenging task for biological applications.

In vitro antitumor effects of CuSn nanozymes

Encouraged by the galvanic cells structure and multienzyme activities of CuSn nanozymes, we assessed the antitumor effects of CuSn nanozymes in vitro. Biological TEM (Bio-TEM) analysis illustrated that CuSn nanozymes were effectively internalized by 4T1 cells after 4 h of co-incubation (Fig. 3A), thereby mediating the cytotoxic effects. We evaluated its cytotoxicity on three cancer cell lines of human endometrial cancer cells (Ishikawa), mouse breast cancer cells (4T1) and human liver cancer cells (Hep3b) and two normal cell lines of mouse mammary epithelium cells (HC11) and human endometrial stromal cells (HESC) by a typical cell counting kit 8 (CCK-8) assay (Fig. 3B and C; Fig. S8, Supporting Information). After being treated with 50 μg mL−1 CuSn nanozymes for 24 h, 4T1, Ishikawa, and Hep3b cells were found with a significant drop in cell viability with less than 40% of cells alive. Notably, the cell viabilities of all two normal cells were higher than 65% despite receiving the same treatment of 50 μg mL−1 CuSn nanozymes for 24 h (Fig. S9, Supporting Information). Furthermore, the CuSn nanozymes exhibited stronger killing ability against cancer cells under simulated TME conditions (weakly acidic, pH = 6.0) (Fig. 3B and C; Fig. S8, Supporting Information). Meanwhile, the cytotoxicity of CuSn nanozymes showed a dose-dependent behavior. To more visually display the tumor killing effect of CuSn nanozymes, cells treated with various concentrations of CuSn nanozymes were stained through the live/dead staining method (calcein-AM/PI) (Fig. 3D; Fig. S10, Supporting Information). The fluorescence images revealed results similar to those of previous experiments, where increasing concentrations of CuSn nanozymes led to incremental dead cells. To better verify the therapeutic efficacy, the cytotoxicity of Sn, Cu2+, and Sn + Cu2+ was measured to evaluate whether CuSn nanozymes has multi-enzyme activities and the galvanic cells effect. Experimental results indicated the treatment with Sn alone and Cu2+ exerted minimal effects on cell viability. Conversely, cell viability was significantly reduced in the groups after treatment with Sn + Cu2+ and CuSn nanozymes. Notably, among all these groups, the group after treatment with CuSn nanozymes exhibited the strongest cytotoxicity, suggesting a synergistic antitumor effect due to CuSn nanozymes-mediated enzyme activities and galvanic cells effect (Fig. 3E; Fig. S11, Supporting Information). Furthermore, the Annexin V-FITC apoptosis detection kit demonstrated the CuSn nanozymes treatment induced the most potent antitumor effect (Fig. 3F). These findings demonstrated that the combined application of CuSn nanozymes can exert a therapeutic efficacy on tumors.

To study the effect of multienzyme-like activity and galvanic cells structure of CuSn nanozymes on the intracellular ROS level. The total intracellular ROS generation properties of CuSn nanozymes were further evaluated by utilizing 2′,7′-dichlorodihydrofluorescein diacetate (DCFH-DA) as ROS probe (Fig. 3G–I; Fig. S12, Supporting Information). In comparison to the green fluorescence increased in the CuSn nanozymes and Cu nanozymes groups, demonstrating the ROS production ability of CuSn nanozymes. Compared to the control groups, a slight upregulation of green fluorescence signals was observed in the Cu nanozymes groups, potentially attributable to the chemodynamic property of Cu ions released by Cu nanozymes. The CuSn nanozyme groups exhibited the highest fluorescence intensity, verifying the synergistic action of multienzyme-like properties and galvanic cells structure in amplifying intracellular ROS levels (Fig. 3G–I; Fig. S12, Supporting Information). Subsequently, we used GSH/oxidized glutathione disulfide (GSSG) detection kit to quantitatively assess the intracellular contents of GSH. The GSH contents in intact 4T1 and Ishikawa cells treated with CuSn nanozymes were significantly decreased compared to Cu nanozymes (Fig. 3H). It confirmed that CuSn nanozymes can eliminate GSH in tumor cells and damage the antioxidant barrier and augment oxidative stress. The results confirm that the CuSn nanozymes possess a potent killing effect on a variety of tumor cells, thereby demonstrating their potential for universal application in tumor treatment.

In vitro antibacterial performance of CuSn nanozymes

To assess the antibacterial property of CuSn nanozymes in vitro, Escherichia coli (E. coli) and Staphylococcus aureus (S. aureus) were chosen as the model strains due to their widespread presence in the human body and their proven ability to metabolize intratumoral gemcitabine (Gem) and mediate chemotherapy resistance [15,31]. The antibacterial effects of CuSn nanozymes were tested in two different bacteria. To investigate the bacterial inhibition ability of CuSn nanozymes, the bacterial growth was estimated by detecting the absorbance at OD630 nm after exposing the bacteria to different concentrations of CuSn nanozymes (0-800 μg mL−1) at specific time points, it is worth noting that the CuSn nanozymes significantly suppressed the growth of E. coli and S. aureus in a dose-dependent manner within 24 h (Fig. 4A and B). The growth curve indicated that the CuSn nanozymes at a concentration of 200 μg mL−1 effectively inhibited the proliferation of E. coli and S. aureus. Further, CuSn nanozymes exhibited significant antibacterial activity at a concentration of 200 μg mL−1, whereas Cu nanozymes required a higher concentration of 400 μg mL−1 to achieve a comparable inhibitory effect (Fig. S13, Supporting Information). Moreover, the quantitative antibacterial efficacy was assessed by using a colony count assay. As seen in Fig. 4C and D, the number of colonies of the bacteria treated with CuSn nanozymes, for 6 h was remarkably reduced compared with the control and Cu nanozymes groups, which was comparable to that of ceftriaxone (a powerful broad-spectrum antibiotic in clinic). Similar results were observed in DMAO/PI staining assay (Fig. 4E), in which E. coli and S. aureus cells after CuSn nanozymes treatments exhibited strong PI red fluorescence, indicating the powerful antibacterial capacity of CuSn nanozymes.

To further investigated the antibacterial mechanisms of CuSn nanozymes, scanning electron microscopy (SEM) is applied to assess the morphological structure of treated E. coli and S. aureus. As depicted in Fig. 4F, the bacteria in the control groups exhibited normal morphology with clear boundaries as well as membrane integrity. In contrast, treatment with CuSn nanozymes induced severe disruption of the bacterial cell membrane, accompanied by cell wall fragmentation and the formation of irregularly shaped pores. These observations suggested that CuSn nanozymes group exhibited antibacterial capabilities by disrupting bacterial cell membranes and bacterial cell walls, which were endowed by the release of electrons. Next, the bacterial inhibition ability of Sn, Cu2+, and Sn + Cu2+ was assayed to evaluate whether CuSn nanozymes has the best antibacterial performance. As depicted in Fig. 4G and H, the groups treated with CuSn nanozymes exhibited the great bacterial inhibition effect. Meanwhile, the standard colony counting method was used to evaluated germicidal efficacy, as shown in Fig. 4I and J, the CuSn nanozymes groups displayed excellent antibacterial performance. The above results clearly demonstrated the superior antibacterial performance of the CuSn nanozymes. Collectively, these results demonstrate the substantial ability of CuSn nanozymes to eliminate intratumoral bacteria, which can be used to abolish their adverse effects on antitumor therapy.

In vitro intracellular antitumor performance and antibacterial performance of CuSn nanozymes

After that, the antitumor and antibacterial effects of the CuSn nanozymes were also evaluated in bacteria-infected tumor cell models. The models were built by the addition of E. coli or S. aureus (MOI = 10) into the tumor cells (4T1 cells, Ishikawa cells, and Hep3b cells). Fig. 5A and B showed that E. coli and S. aureus can invade tumor cells and coexist with them, providing a basis for subsequent bacterial resistance experiments. Then, the antitumor performance of the drugs is tested based on the built bacterium-infected cancer cells model using the CCK-8 assay. As depicted in Fig. 5C; Fig. S14, Supporting Information, E. coli and S. aureus within tumor cells could promote the drug resistance activity of tumor cells when adding chemotherapeutic agents, thereby inhibiting the cytotoxicity of chemotherapy drugs paclitaxel (PTX) on tumor cells. For cells without E. coli or S. aureus infection, the cell cytotoxicity induced by PTX was drug concentration-dependent (Fig. S14, Supporting Information). The CCK-8 assay displayed that ceftriaxone (CEF) had no remarkable influence on tumor cells, whereas the simultaneous use of CEF and PTX can reduce the resistance developed by bacteria. It is worth noting that CuSn nanozymes have the strongest cytotoxicity on bacterium-infected tumor cells. Next, after the above treatments, those bacterium-infected tumor cells were collected and homogenized. The homogenized suspensions were smeared on agar plates for 24 h, followed by plate colony counting, to further evaluate the antibacterial performance of the drug. As shown in Fig. 5E and F, the control and PTX groups were nearly full of bacterial plaques, very few bacterial plaques were observed on the plates of the CuSn nanozymes. Moreover, CEF and CEF + PTX groups observed a similar number of bacterial colonies to the control group, which may be due to the poor penetrating ability of CEF cell membranes and the acidic and hydrolytic environment of lysosomes, resulting in poor efficacy. Taken together, the above results indicate that CuSn nanozymes can effectively kill cancer cells and intracellular bacteria, overcome the chemotherapy resistance induced by intratumoral bacteria, and enhance the efficacy of antitumor treatment.

Antitumor mechanism of CuSn nanozymes

To explore the antitumor mechanism of CuSn nanozymes, RNA-sequencing (RNA-seq) was used to investigate the underlying antitumor mechanisms. Heatmap analysis highlighted significant differences in RNA expression profiles among the control and CuSn nanozymes groups (Fig. 6A). The volcano plot shows that there were 4367 significantly differentially expressed genes (DEGs) compared to the control group (Fig. 6B). Of these, 2222 genes were upregulated and 2145 genes were downregulated (Fig. 6B). To further investigate the biological and metabolic pathways in the DEGs of these cancer cells treated with CuSn nanozymes, the Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) pathway and Gene Ontology (GO) analyses were applied. GO analysis of the CuSn nanozymes groups identified DEGs were enriched in molecular function related to unfolded protein binding (Fig. 6C). It should be noted that ER stress is usually attributed to the perturbation of three major functions of ER, such as protein folding/trafficking, Ca2+ homeostasis, and lipid synthesis and processing [32]. Thus, GO analysis suggested that ER stress is the potential mechanism of cell death induced by CuSn nanozymes. KEGG pathway analyses further confirmed that the major activation pathway of the ER stress was protein processing in the endoplasmic reticulum. KEGG pathway analyses also observed ROS production, suggesting that ER stress occurrence could be caused by ROS generation. (Fig. 6D). Specifically, from the heat map, ER stress-related genes, including Rnf183, Atf4, Tmem33, and Ern1 were significantly downregulated. Conversely, ferroptosis-related genes, including glutathione peroxidase 4 (GPX4), Jun, and heme oxygenase1 (Hmox1), and cuproptosis-related genes such as ferredoxin 1 (FDX1), and dihydrolipoamide S-acetyltransferase (DLAT) were notably upregulated (Fig. 6E). Moreover, Gene Set Enrichment Analysis (GSEA) was further indicated ferroptosis was one of the potential cell death mechanisms (Fig. 6F). As shown in the above results, ER stress, ferroptosis, and cuproptosis were all involved in CuSn nanozymes-mediated cell death, and their signaling pathways were further explored by western blotting (WB). It is well-known that protein kinase RNA-like ER kinase (PERK), and activating transcription factor 4 (ATF-4), C/EBP homologous protein (CHOP), are key stress sensors of initiation of the ER stress [32]. It was found the higher expression of these proteins were detected in the CuSn nanozymes groups (Fig. 6G). These confirmed that CuSn nanozymes-mediated severe ER stress.

Cuproptosis is initiated by excessive intracellular Cu2+ accumulation, which is reduced by FDX1 to the more toxic Cu+ form. To assess intracellular Cu+ levels. We used intracellular copper ion fluorescent probe (CuprosGreen). As depicted in Fig. S15, supporting information, the intracellular Cu+ accumulation level after CuSn nanozymes treatment exhibited markedly higher than those of all other groups. The activity of cuproptosis-related proteins DLAT was further investigated. Western blot results revealed that CuSn nanozymes remarkably increased the expression of DLAT protein in 4T1 cells, indicating the activation of cuproptosis (Fig. 6G).

The lethal accumulation of lipid peroxides (LPO) is regarded as the gold-standard hallmark of ferroptosis. Intracellular LPO levels were assessed using the C11-BODIPY fluorescent probe following different treatments [33]. The significant LPO accumulation, as expected, was observed in the CuSn nanozymes group, implying the most intense ferroptosis (Fig. S16, Supporting information). Moreover, malondialdehyde (MDA), recognized as a byproduct of lipid peroxidation reaction, is generally utilized as a reliable biomarker of oxidative stress and ferroptosis [34]. Fig. S17, supporting information showed that MDA levels after the CuSn nanozymes treatments were about three folds higher than those in the untreated control group. This substantial elevation indicated a marked increase in lipid peroxidation, thereby activating the ferroptosis pathway and contributing significantly to the suppression of tumor cell viability. Furthermore, we measured the expression of key ferroptosis-related proteins, including glutathione peroxidase 4 (GPX4) and ACSL4 by using Western blot analysis. As shown in Fig. 6G, after CuSn nanozymes treatment, the inhibitory effect on the expression of GPX4 in cells was the most pronounced compared to the other groups. In contrast, ACSL4 expression was the highest. Next, to further confirm the occurrence of cuproptosis and ferroptosis, the copper chelator tetrathiomolybdate (TTM) and the ferroptosis inhibitor ferrostatin-1 (Fer-1) are applied to evaluate the impact of CuSn nanozymes on 4TI cancer cells cytotoxicity. As shown in Fig. S18, supporting information, TTM and Fer-1 markedly reversed the cell death. These results suggested that ferroptosis occurred along with cuproptosis during CuSn nanozymes-induced cell death. Taken together, these results indicated CuSn nanozymes-mediated ER stress and induced the ferroptosis, and cuproptosis in the tumor cells (Fig. 6H). Beyond directly inducing cell death, cuproptosis and ferroptosis can also elicit a potent immune response by triggering immunogenic cell death (ICD). This occurs through the release damage-associated molecular patterns (DAMPs), such as calreticulin (CRT) and high mobility group box 1 (HMGB1), which serve as critical mediators of immune activation. CRT exposure on the cell surface acts as an “eat me” signal, facilitating dendritic cell (DC) recognition and maturation. After CuSn nanozymes treatment, CRT on the cell surface significantly increased (Fig. S19A and B, Supporting Information). Moreover, the migration of HMGB1 from the nucleus to the cytoplasm and extracellular space is another indicator to evaluate the ICD. As shown in Fig. S19C and D, supporting information, intracellular HMGB1 levels were significantly reduced in the CuSn nanozymes group. Together, this dual lethal effect holds significant potential for highly effective tumor suppression in cancer therapy.

Antibacterial mechanism of CuSn nanozymes

Furthermore, the antibacterial mechanism of CuSn nanozymes were further studied. According to the literature reports, the electron transport chain (ETC) on the bacterial inner membrane acts as a crucial role in essential physiological processes, including energy supply and metabolism [35,36]. ROS generation disrupts the bacterial electron transport chain (ETC), leading to impaired respiration and energy metabolism [37]. 2′,7′-dichlorofluorescein diacetate (DCFH-DA), a fluorescent probe that reacts with ROS to generate green fluorescence, was used to monitor the real-time generation of ROS in E. coli and S. aureus under various treatments. As shown in Fig. 7A, negligible green fluorescence was observed in the control group, indicating minimal ROS production. The Sn, Cu2+, and Sn + Cu2+ groups exhibited only weak fluorescence signals. In contrast, bacteria treated with CuSn nanozymes displayed remarkably enhanced green fluorescence in both E. coli and S. aureus, demonstrating substantial intracellular ROS accumulation. These results indicate that CuSn nanozymes can effectively induce oxidative stress in bacteria, which may contribute to the disruption of the ETC. Due to the galvanic cell structure of CuSn nanozymes, which can spontaneously and rapidly release large amounts of electrons, the potential for self-supplying electrons on the surface of bacteria was further explored. In order to verify this hypothesis, the bacterial membrane potential was detected using DiBAC4(3) as the probe. As seen in Fig. 7B, there had no obvious fluorescence in the control group. Compare to the Sn, Cu2+, and Sn + Cu2+ groups, CuSn nanozymes existed an obvious fluorescence enhancement, which could be attributed to prolonged electron accumulation and ROS-mediated oxidative damage leading to disruption of ETC. The resulting membrane depolarization allowed increased dye uptake, thereby producing a stronger fluorescence signal. In addition, we measured the microbial adenosine triphosphate (ATP) synthase concentration of the bacteria treated with different formulations through an ATP assay kit. The ATP levels of bacteria decreased significantly after treatment with CuSn nanozymes, suggesting the inhibition of the bacterial ETC led to the proton pump insufficient motive force to enable ATP synthesis, thereby leading to a reduction in ATP levels (Fig. 7C). Moreover, the total bacterial DNA was extracted from different groups. The corresponding quantitative results are presented in Fig. 7D. It is witnessed that the CuSn nanozymes group display the most distinguished property of destructed bacterial DNA, indicating the tremendous electron absorbed by E. coli efficiently destroy the bacteria. The results of bacterial DNA in S. aureus are consistent with E. coli (Fig. 7D), demonstrating CuSn nanozymes possess broad-spectrum antibacterial ability. In addition, the leakage of proteins was evaluated by BCA kit to prove the destruction of bacteria. As depicted in Fig. 7E, the CuSn nanozymes groups display tremendous leakage of proteins compared with other groups, illustrating that CuSn nanozymes could efficiently kill pathogenic bacteria and lead to irreversible catastrophic damage. In summary, CuSn nanozymes exhibits a synergistic electronic-mechanical bactericidal effect. Generally, the galvanic cell structure of CuSn nanozymes can spontaneously and rapidly release large amounts of electrons, electron transfer and accumulation on the surface of bacteria promote disruption of ETC and inhibit synthesis of ATP, which kill the bacteria (Fig. 7F).

In vivo antitumor and antibacterial performance of CuSn nanozymes

Facilitated by the outstanding therapeutic results observed in vitro, the antitumor and antibacterial efficacy of CuSn nanozymes is investigated in the in vivo model. The in vivo therapeutic efficacy of CuSn nanozymes was systematically assessed by using BALB/c mice bearing 4T1 tumors (Fig. 8A). 4T1 cells were subcutaneously injected to the right backs of BALB/c mice, and then E. coli and S. aureus were peritumorally injected on the seventh day of tumor inoculation, obtaining the E. coli and S. aureus-infiltrated breast tumor-bearing mice model. The mice in the former model were randomly divided into 6 groups of five each, one of which without injected E. coli and S. aureus, five of which were intratumorally injected with E. coli and S. aureus, labeled as the control, CEF, PTX, CEF + PTX, CuSn nanozymes.

During the 14 days treatment period, the tumor volumes and body weights of the mice were observed every two days. As shown in Fig. 8E, there are no obvious changes in body weight of the mice through the whole treatment period, demonstrating the minimal systemic toxicity of CuSn nanozymes (Fig. 8E). As shown in Fig. 8B–D, intratumor bacteria can promote tumor growth. Compared with the gradually increased tumors in blank control without any intervention, tumors with bacteria infection showed much accelerated proliferation, as evidenced by the rapidly increased tumor volumes, revealing that bacteria had an auxo-action in promoting the development of cancer. With PTX treatment alone, the tumor growth showed minimal inhibition, which may be ascribed to bacterium-induced drug resistance. Similarly, CEF therapy alone had no significant tumor suppression effect. Comparatively, the combination of PTX and CEF (CEF + PTX cotreatment group) promoted tumor suppression to a certain extent, owing to the fact that CEF would act on bacteria to enhance the therapeutic index of chemotherapeutic drugs. In remarkable contrast, the CuSn nanozymes-treated group exhibited an extraordinary tumor regression with complete ablation in 3 of 5 mice in 14 days. All mice were sacrificed, and tumor tissues from different groups were collected at the end of the experiment. The above therapeutic performance was also affirmed by the corresponding photographs (Fig. 8B) and average weights (Fig. 8D) of excised tumors. Thus, a preliminary result is reached that like the in vitro test, CuSn nanozymes also have a significant antitumor performance even under bacteria-infected conditions.

To further verify the antitumor effect of the CuSn nanozymes, we conducted histological analysis, including Hematoxylin-eosin (H&E) staining, Ki-67 assays, and Terminal deoxynucleotidyl transferase-mediated dUTP nick-end Labeling (TUNEL) on tumor tissues treated with different methods. H&E and TUNEL staining displayed CuSn nanozymes caused the most serious damage to the tumor tissues and induced the most severe cell apoptosis. Ki-67 assays, a key marker associated with active cancer cell proliferation, also demonstrated that the CuSn nanozymes group exhibited the strongest suppression of tumor cell proliferation, further verifying the high therapeutic efficacy of the CuSn nanozymes-induced synergistic treatment (Fig. 8F).

To assess the ability to suppress intratumoral bacteria, tumor tissues were harvested from the mice on the 14th day, homogenized, and plated on an agar plate. The number of colonized bacteria after the treatments was quantified by counting the colonies (Fig. 8G and H). Compared with the control group, PTX exhibited a negligible performance on inhibiting intratumoral bacteria, whereas the other treatment groups displayed obvious antibacterial ability. Specifically, the group after CuSn nanozymes treatment displayed the strongest bactericidal ability against intratumor bacteria compared to CEF, CEF + PTX groups. These findings suggested that CuSn nanozymes overcome chemotherapy resistance and immunosuppression induced by tumor-associated bacteria by eliminating both the intratumoral bacteria and the tumors.

Last but not the least, it is important to investigate the biosafety of CuSn nanozymes prior to future clinical applications. After the treatment, Cu and Sn levels in the tumors of mice after being treated with CuSn nanozymes for different times were measured by inductively coupled plasma optical emission spectrometry (ICP-OES) (Fig. S20A and B, Supporting Information). The Cu and Sn content in the tumor gradually decreased within 14 days (Fig. S20A and B, Supporting Information). Moreover, Cu and Sn levels in the blood and main organs (liver, spleen, kidney, heart, and lung) were studied. As shown in Fig. S20C, supporting information, high amounts of Cu were measured in the liver at days 1 and 2, and the amounts gradually decreased over time. And the change of blood Cu levels (Fig. S20D, Supporting Information) was not significant after the treatment. In addition, there was no significant increase of Sn levels in the blood and main organs (liver, spleen, kidney, heart, and lung) after CuSn nanozymes were treated, indicating that Sn generated from the degraded CuSn nanozymes would not affect the equilibrium of Sn levels in the body (Fig. S20E and F, Supporting Information). The body weight remained normal in all treated groups during the entire assessment period (Fig. 8B). At the same time, there were no obvious abnormalities in H&E staining of major organs (heart, liver, spleen, lung, and kidney) (Fig. S21, Supporting Information), blood biochemical detection (Fig. S22, Supporting Information), testifying to the good biosafety of the CuSn nanozymes. These results confirmed CuSn nanozymes no obvious toxicity and side effects to the treated animals.

In vivo immune activation on bacteria-infected tumor model

The death of bacteria can facilitate the release of lipopolysaccharide (LPS) from gram-negative bacteria, which promotes the dendritic cells (DCs) maturation [31]. Furthermore, recent studies have shown ferroptosis and cuproptosis not only trigger immunogenic cell death (ICD) but also possess the ability to enhance the efficiency of the tumor immune checkpoint inhibitor therapy [21]. Thus, tumors tissues of mice were dissected, and the changes in the immune cell populations in the tumor tissues were analyzed by flow cytometry. The percentage of matured DCs cells in tumors treated with CuSn nanozymes have notably greater promotion capacity than the other groups (Fig. 9A and B). Matured DCs possess a robust antigen delivery capability, which facilitates the activation of cytotoxic T cells in vivo [38]. The presence of elevated CD8+ T cells within the tumor tissues represents the most direct evidence of immune response activation. As shown in Fig. 9C and D, the proportion of tumor-infiltrating CD8+ toxic T cells in CuSn nanozymes treatment groups was remarkably enhanced in CuSn nanozymes treatment groups compared to the other groups. Moreover, we analyzed the negative regulation immune cells of regulatory T cells (Tregs) and myeloid suppressor cells (MDSCs) surrounding the tumor site. Immunofluorescence (IF) staining showed that Treg cells and MDSCs in the tumor following the CuSn nanozymes was markedly lower than the others groups (Fig. 9E). Meanwhile, the levels of pro-inflammatory cytokines of tumor necrosis factor-α (TNF-α), interferon-γ (IFN-γ), and interleukin-6 (IL-6) significantly increased following CuSn nanozymes treatment compared to the other groups, while IL-10 remarkably decreased (Fig. 9F–I). Besides, to further investigate the immunological memory effects, the level of immunological memory cells in the spleen on day 14 was evaluated. The results showed the percentage of central memory T cells (Tcm, CD3+CD8+CD44+CD62L+) (Fig. 9J; Fig. S23, Supporting Information) and effector memory T cells (Tem, CD3+CD8+CD44+CD62L−) (Fig. 9K; Fig. S23, Supporting Information) in the CuSn nanozymes group was obviously higher than that in the other groups, respectively. These data collectively indicate that CuSn nanozymes can promote systemic immune response and induce the potent immunological memory effect, thereby augmenting tumor tissue destruction and metastasis.

Conclusion

In this study, bimetallic CuSn nanozymes with a galvanic cell structure were designed to reverse bacteria-mediated drug resistance for enhanced chemotherapy by integrating antibacteria with antitumor therapy. CuSn nanozymes combined enzyme-like biocatalysis, and electrocatalysis to address key limitations of conventional catalytic therapies. The CuSn nanozymes deplete intracellular substrates and amplify bursts of ROS by triggering a cascade of catalytic reactions. Furthermore, without any stimulations, CuSn nanozymes can generate highly reactive electrons and metal ions to further amplify therapeutic efficiency. Under the tumor microenvironment, the robust generation of ROS could lead to the inhibition of tumor cell growth. The generated ROS further induce ER stress, thereby mediating cuproptosis and ferroptosis to achieve tumor therapy. The released electrons of CuSn nanozymes can destroy the electron transport chain and block the energy supply of bacteria, thereby eliminating bacteria. The dead bacteria can increase the body's immunity to enhance the tumor treatment. This work proposed the strategy highlights the potential of utilizing bimetallic nanozymes with a galvanic cell strategy with dual elimination of tumor and intratumoral bacteria for novel tumor applications.
