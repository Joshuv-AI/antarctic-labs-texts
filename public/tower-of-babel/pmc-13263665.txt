Representation, ownership and funding in climate change and health research in South Asia
Authors: Inika Sharma, Soumyadeep Bhaumik
Published: 2026

Climate change is a shared threat that requires co-ordinated geo-political and scientific co-operation. South Asia is witnessing severe impacts of climate change, despite having a low carbon footprint per capita.1 With increasing climate change and health research, it is essential to understand if the knowledge ecosystem which underpins it is fair and just, in terms of representation, ownership and funding. Historically, knowledge systems positioned colonisers as the producers and gatekeepers of knowledge and the colonised as ‘subjects’ and receivers of knowledge. The legacy of colonialism is reflected in the continued social and geographical disconnect between the realm of action (research settings) and realm of power (where agendas are set and knowledge legitimised).2 In research collaborations, LMIC (low-and-middle-income country) partners are frequently excluded from key aspects of the research process and lack equitable recognition: a practice that sustains power imbalances within the global knowledge ecosystem.3,4 This is reflected through the dominance of first or corresponding authorship positions by HIC-affiliated (high-income country) researchers in studies conducted in LMIC settings.4,5 Building on evidenced disparities, we examined the funding and author affiliations in South Asian climate change and health research. We analysed a dataset that we built for an evidence map (n = 308 studies) on climate change and health studies published after 2008 and conducted in Bangladesh, Bhutan, India, Nepal, Pakistan, or Sri Lanka for this purpose.6 We classified authors as domestically affiliated if the primary institution was based in the South Asian country where the study was conducted; otherwise, it was classified as foreign-affiliated. Overall, there were an almost equal number of domestic and foreign affiliated first authors, with a marginally higher number of foreign-affiliated corresponding authors than domestic ones. In bivariate analysis, foreign-affiliated first authors had 4.2 times higher odds of being funded than domestically affiliated first authors (odds ratio [OR] = 4.2; 20.6% vs 52.0% unfunded research, respectively). Similarly, foreign-affiliated corresponding authors had 3.8 times higher odds of being funded than domestically affiliated corresponding authors (OR = 3.9; 22.6% vs 53.0% unfunded research, respectively). These funding imbalances persisted when adjusting for study design (first authors aOR = 4.8; corresponding authors aOR = 3.4). Additional analysis pertaining to multi-country studies, multiple affiliations, and funder type are reported in Appendix 1.

The findings suggest presence of systemic biases favouring funding for foreign-affiliated researchers. This is likely due to HIC institutes having greater access to resources, allowing them to set research agendas with a ‘foreign gaze’, and retain the ownership of research activities in LMICs.6,7 The leading of research by domestic institutes is not only a moral imperative but also an epistemic advantage, which can facilitate a more nuanced understanding of contextual complexities of climate change adaptation.8

This indicates the need for funders to address potentially extractive research practices through pro-justice funding initiatives, such as explicit affirmative action to mandate research being led by researchers who are accountable and connected to the setting. Multi-country collaborations should be led by institutions within the country where majority of the research is conducted. Foreign institutions can play a capacity-sharing or technical role, if necessary.9 Journal requirements of authorship reflexivity statements, that identify connections to research setting and interrogate how power, biases and social context shape research, constitute an important step. However, it is essential to ensure they do not inadvertently normalise colonial hierarchies: there is a need for reparative scholarship in addition to ongoing relational accountability.10 Finally, ethics review committees can go beyond their participant-centric role to proactively prevent epistemic injustice by including research authorship plans, research data management plans, and other actions which ensure Indigenous knowledge sovereignty. Research done in LMICs by HIC institutes should formalise actions and investments taken to reduce the power differential with LMIC collaborators, build ownership, and benefit local communities. The Global North has historically benefited from contributing more to climate change and can now leverage resources to make adaptations without impeding development; thus, HIC-led research recommendations may seem hypocritical or unfeasible in LMIC settings.11 Future research to understand the epistemological framing of climate-health research is necessary to understand the knowledge impact of foreign-affiliated authors.

Global discussions on climate change and health require a pro-justice platform for those most vulnerable. Funders need to revisit not just eligibility criteria but also evaluation criteria to advance equitable research production and enhance local responsiveness to results. A systemic transformation of funding practices is required for dismantling the feudal structure of knowledge ecosystem.
