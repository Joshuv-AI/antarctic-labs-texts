Integrated molecular networks reveal latent components distinguishing type 2-high and type 2-low asthma
Authors: Maria Ramirez, Maria Paula Diaz, Ningya Wang, Qing Liu, Huan Li, Shannon Kay, Megan Shuguli, Jana Zielonka, Gabriella Wilson, Maor Sauler, Zuoheng Wang, Xiting Yan, Geoffrey Chupp, Jose L Gomez
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundType 2 inflammation (T2) biomarkers are used to identify T2-low and T2-high endotypes of asthma in the clinic and understanding of these endotypes has helped to improve clinical management of patients. The T2-low asthma endotype remains relatively poorly characterised and these patients have few treatment options. Despite its clinical relevance, this endotype lacks specific biomarkers.ObjectiveTo identify transcriptional and microRNA signatures associated with asthma endotypes.MethodsWe studied 88 participants with asthma stratified into T2-high and T2-low asthma by blood eosinophils and/or fractional exhaled nitric oxide. We defined whole-blood microRNA and mRNA profiles for each participant and used Data Integration Analysis for Biomarker discovery using Latent Variable approaches for Omics integration to identify latent components (LCs). The results were validated in two independent cohorts of subjects with asthma, including patient samples taken before and after treatment with benralizumab.ResultsThe T2-high endotype was the most prevalent (67%, n=55) within the study population. We identified two LCs associated with T2-high and T2-low asthma. LC1 was characterised by eosinophil-derived mRNAs and included multiple T2-low microRNAs. LC2 was associated with both endotypes and non-eosinophilic cell types. Pathway analysis revealed increased representation of taste and smooth muscle pathways in the T2-low endotype. The T2-low microRNA miR-574-3p was enriched in LC1. Furthermore, we found that the LC signatures were present in two independent cohorts and modulated by benralizumab.ConclusionsT2-high and T2-low asthma are distinguished by unique transcriptomic networks and have minimal microRNA overlaps. T2-low asthma was enriched for pathways involved with smooth muscle contraction. The LCs signatures are also modulated by benralizumab.

Introduction

Asthma is a heterogeneous syndrome characterised by airway inflammation and bronchial hyperresponsiveness. Studies focused on understanding the heterogeneity of asthma have resulted in the description of various endotypes of the disease and the underlying cellular and signalling pathways. Type 2 (T2) inflammation biomarkers have been used to classify asthma patients into two main subtypes: T2-high asthma, the better-defined subset, is characterised by eosinophilia, increased airway mast cells and responsiveness to inhaled corticosteroids. T2-low asthma is characterised by levels of T2 inflammation that are within normal reference ranges and poor responsiveness to corticosteroid treatment. Otherwise, this asthma subset is poorly understood and likely comprises multiple endotypes.1 2

Asthma endotypes have helped to improve clinical management of patients, particularly those with T2-high asthma, who have benefitted from the availability of biologics that target specific molecular pathways of T2 inflammation3 and from the identification of clinical biomarkers, such as blood eosinophil levels and IgE, that are routinely used to characterise disease severity and monitor response to therapy. Despite these advances, pathways that are not targeted by biologic agents remain largely uncharacterised, particularly in T2-low asthma, where there are also a limited number of clinically useful biomarkers and patients have few therapeutic options.4 5

Transcriptional analysis of asthma endotypes has improved our understanding of the molecular pathways associated with the disease.69 For instance, the identification of distinct gene expression patterns in airway epithelial cells from patients with T2-high asthma6 led to the identification of periostin as a protein biomarker of T2 inflammation used for patient selection and stratification in clinical trials.10 11 Transcriptional analyses have been facilitated by the development of non-invasive approaches (eg, using sputum or peripheral blood) that overcome the limitations of invasive airway epithelial sampling.79

An estimated 30% of the human transcriptome is controlled by microRNAs,12 and recent studies have identified microRNAs linked to various states of airway inflammation,13 including T2-low asthma.14 15 The Let-7 family of miRNAs in human, animal and in vitro studies has been implicated in the modulation of T2 inflammation.16 17 In a mouse model of allergic inflammation, miR-126 suppression led to a decrease in T2 responses, inflammation, airways hyperreactivity, eosinophil recruitment and mucus hypersecretion.18 Two miRNAs, miR-155 and miR-221 regulate T2 responses and several cellular components of the allergic response including eosinophils,19 macrophages,20 mast cells21 in both asthma and allergic rhinitis. Several microRNAs, including miR-26, miR-133a, miR-140, miR-206 and miR-221, play active regulatory roles in airway smooth muscle cell function, proliferation and remodelling.2225 Among individuals with asthma, increased expression of a triad of microRNAs (miR-629-3p, miR-223-3p and miR-142-3p) was associated with severe neutrophilic asthma and airway obstruction.14 A previous study from our group identified a module of 12 microRNAs that correlated with clinical features of asthma, including history of hospitalisations and lung function impairment; this module was also associated with neutrophil and lymphocyte abundance in the sputum.15 By investigating paired mRNA profiles from the same patients, we identified correlated mRNA modules, including those enriched for genes associated with Toll-like receptor signalling, IL-17 responses and endoplasmic reticulum stress.15 These studies demonstrate the potential advantages of understanding asthma endotypes via analysis of microRNA expression and transcriptomic signatures. However, few other studies have integrated mRNA and microRNA profiles of patients with asthma,13 limiting our understanding of the microRNA-regulated pathways underlying different asthma endotypes.

Additional biomarkers that are broadly applicable and can be measured using minimally invasive techniques are needed to better understand the molecular determinants of asthma endotypes. Paired microRNA and mRNA profiling of patient samples has the potential to identify new molecular pathways and biomarkers, particularly those associated with T2-low asthma. To identify transcriptional networks associated with specific asthma endotypes, we integrated microRNA and mRNA expression of whole blood in individuals with asthma. This study extends our understanding of regulatory networks and pathway interactions to enable a more precise stratification of patients with asthma.

Methods

Yale centre for asthma and airway diseases cohort

A cross-sectional analysis was conducted on blood RNA samples collected from subjects with asthma and control subjects that completed the Yale Center for Asthma and Airway Diseases phenotyping protocol between September 2009 and June 2012. Subjects were 17 years of age or older, nonsmokers and with less than or equal to 10 pack-years of smoking history. The details of this cohort have been described previously.7 15 26

Inclusion criteria for asthma included history, physical examination and physiologic testing consistent with a diagnosis of asthma based on National Asthma Education and Prevention Program guidelines.27 Exclusion criteria included smoking within the past year, a history of chronic lung disease other than asthma (ie, chronic obstructive pulmonary disease, allergic bronchopulmonary aspergillosis, eosinophilic granulomatosis with polyangiitis, pulmonary vascular disease or interstitial lung disease); other severe chronic conditions including congestive heart failure, chronic kidney disease, liver disease or viral infection; or inability to safely undergo the studies required for participation.

Endotype assignment for T2-high or T2-low asthma was based on the presence or absence, respectively, of blood eosinophil counts >150 cells/microliter and/or fractional exhaled nitric oxide (FeNO) ≥25 parts per billion (ppb).28 Subjects without either of these features were assigned to the T2-low asthma endotype. We excluded individuals on systemic corticosteroids with T2-low criteria at the time of the study visit to account for the masking effect of systemic steroids on blood eosinophil counts and FeNO. The held-out validation cohort did not have blood eosinophil or FeNO values. Control subjects had a negative pulmonary history and were not on any inhalers, systemic corticosteroids or biologic agents.

Patient and public involvement

Patients or the public were not involved in the design, or conduct, or reporting, or dissemination plans of our research.

Genomic analysis

Total RNA from whole blood samples was isolated using a column-based system (total-RNA kit; Norgen), and if needed, DNA contamination was removed using a DNA clear kit (Qiagen). Haemoglobin reduction was performed on blood samples to eliminate haemoglobin-derived gene transcripts (GLOBINclear Kit; Ambion) and samples were checked by Agilent bioanalyser. Purified RNA from the blood was processed for gene expression using the Affymetrix HuGene 1.0 ST gene arrays (Affymetrix) and the Nanostring nCounter v3.0a array following the manufacturer’s protocols (Nanostring). Only samples with RNA integrity numbers >4 were analysed. The data can be obtained from the GEO database (http://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/geo/) under the accession number GSE287879 and GSE287880.

Statistical analyses

R V.4.0.2 was used for all analyses. Kruskal-Wallis tests were used to assess differences in continuous clinical, physiologic and inflammatory asthma phenotypes between the clusters, and the χ² or Cochran-Armitage test was used to assess differences in categorical phenotypes. Intensity and quality checks of Affymetrix arrays were performed with R package oligo V.1.62.2.29 Nanostring nCounter v3.0a arrays were normalised using the top 100 genes without background subtraction using the geometric mean in the nSolver Analysis Software.v3 (Nanostring). The normalised raw counts were used to identify expressed microRNAs. MicroRNAs were expressed if their overall mean signal was above the background mean plus two SD of the negative ligation controls in the array. Subsequently, the log2 count normalised matrices of mRNAs in the Affymetrix arrays and expressed microRNAs in the Nanostring arrays were batch-adjusted using the ComBat function in the sva package (V.3.46.0) and used for all subsequent analyses.

Integration of mRNA and microRNA expression was performed with Data Integration Analysis for Biomarker discovery using Latent Variable approaches for Omics studies (DIABLO), part of the mixOmics R Package V.6.12.2.30 For robust estimates during module tuning, k-fold cross-validation was performed with 10 cross-validation folds repeated 10 times using two components and class centroids. Following identification of the best tuning parameters for the mRNA and microRNA datasets, the block.splsda function in DIABLO was used in the supervised analysis of the samples using the predefined T2-low and T2-high endotypes. Bulk transcriptome deconvolution was performed with CibersortX using the LM22 matrix using the absolute run mode with 500 permutations.31 Results are reported as relative fractions normalised to 1.

Following identification of the transcripts in the latent components (LCs) in DIABLO, the Hmisc package v.4.5-0 was used to calculate the correlations between genes and microRNAs and derive the correlation networks that were used for enrichment with Ingenuity Pathways build 122 103 623 (Qiagen) using an absolute correlation threshold of 0.3 (eg, −0.3< and >0.3) and a p-value threshold of 0.05. Z scores were extracted following pathway enrichment for the discovery dataset and upstream regulator analysis for the microarray validation dataset.

Canonical correlation analysis was performed with the CCA package V.1.2.1.

TargetScan analysis

Predicted microRNA targets for the microRNAs forming the asthma LCs were obtained from the TargetScan database V.8.0 (www.targetscan.org).32

Validation studies

The original CEL files for GSE137394 were downloaded from GEO.33 The microarrays in this study were collected from patients with uncontrolled severe persistent allergic asthma in the EXTRA omalizumab study (ages 12–75 years). These CEL files were processed using the oligo package29 as described above. The processed matrix was used for subsequent analyses using Lasso regularisation on the package glmnet V.4.1-7. mRNA transcripts derived from the DIABLO analysis were used in this dataset to identify T2-high versus T2-low endotypes based on blood eosinophil count thresholds described above. The plot the receiver operator curve (pROC) package V.1.18.5 was used to ROC.

Transcriptional meta-analysis of the discovery and GSE137394 datasets was performed using the MetaIntegrator R package (V.2.1.3).34 In brief, this method uses a random-effects model to account for inter-study technical and biological variation that prevent simply combining the results of various experiments for analysis. Each gene was subjected to endotype-based analysis. For a gene to be considered differentially expressed, the results of Fisher’s sum-of-logs and DerSimonian and Laird random-effects model must both be significant. The resulting p values are false discovery rate (FDR) corrected to q-values and were considered significant if less than 0.05.

Raw counts for GSE148725 were downloaded from NCBI (www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov)35 and analysed using DESeq2.36 These files were collected prospectively in 41 patients with severe eosinophilic asthma treated with benralizumab for 4 months to identify differentially expressed genes (DEGs) between baseline and 4 months of treatment. Gene expression was defined as the presence of more than 10 counts.

Geneset Enrichment Analysis (GSEA) was performed using V.4.3.2,37 and a custom analysis of the TargetScan database targets with the clusterProfiler V.4.6.238 and GSEABase V.1.60.0 R packages. The c3.all.v2024 collection of the Molecular Signatures (MSiDB) was used for native GSEA enrichment.

Manuscript figures were generated with a combination of ggplot2 V.3.3.2, ggpubr V.0.4.0, ComplexHeatmap V.2.5.5, viridis V.0.5.1 and corrplot V.0.92.

Results

Baseline characteristics

A total of 88 subjects with asthma were included in the analysis and were classified as either T2-high (n=59) or T2-low (n=29) based on blood eosinophil counts and/or FeNO levels. 64 (73%) of subjects were female, and 64 (73%) were White. An overview of the clinical characteristics is presented in table 1 and figure 1. A higher proportion of subjects with T2-low asthma had mild disease (41% vs 15%, p=0.02), and those with T2-high asthma had higher eosinophil counts in blood and sputum, serum IgE concentrations and FeNO levels (figure 1).

Blood microRNA and mRNA profiles in T2-high and T2-low asthma endotypes

To identify the microRNA and mRNA networks in the two asthma endotypes, we measured mRNA and microRNA expression in whole blood samples and analysed the paired expression of protein-coding mRNAs and expressed microRNAs. We detected 18 363 protein-coding mRNAs and 72 expressed miRNAs in the complete study samples. Differential expression of mRNAs and microRNAs without integration is included in online supplemental table 1. Multiomic DIABLO analysis showed a strong correlation (r=0.7, p<0.05) between the matched LC scores from the mRNA and microRNA datasets (online supplemental figure 1), indicating that both capture a shared underlying structure linked to endotype biology.30 This method integrates multiple datasets, such as microRNA and mRNA, in relation to a categorical variable, in this case asthma endotypes. DIABLO constructs LCs by maximising covariances between datasets while balancing model discrimination and integration. Using DIABLO, we identified two LCs formed by a combination of mRNAs and microRNAs linked to the two asthma endotypes (figure 2A–D). The T2-low microRNA miR-376-3p was the only feature shared between LC1 and LC2, contributing to both components but in opposite directions. This indicates that while miR-376-3p influences endotype separation in both dimensions, it shows a positive association with LC1 and a negative association with LC2, reflecting distinct regulatory roles across the two components.

LC1 included 25 mRNAs, all associated with T2-high asthma, and 5 microRNAs, 4 of which were associated with T2-low asthma (figure 2A,C). All mRNAs within LC1 showed a positive correlation with blood eosinophil counts (online supplemental figure 2), whereas microRNAs within LC1 did not display a similar correlation. Within LC1, the T2-high microRNA miR-486-3p correlated with blood neutrophil counts, whereas the T2-low microRNA miR-574-3p correlated with blood lymphocyte counts (online supplemental figure 3).

LC2 included 20 mRNAs and 12 microRNAs. In contrast to LC1, half of the mRNAs in LC2 were associated with T2-low asthma (figure 2B), and no mRNA correlated with blood eosinophil counts. However, there was a correlation between LC2 mRNAs and other cell types including lymphocytes (n=7), monocytes (n=5) and neutrophils (n=2) (online supplemental figure 4). MicroRNAs in LC2 were predominantly linked to T2-low asthma (n=9) and generally showed no positive correlations with blood cell counts, except for miR-125a-5p (online supplemental figure 5).

We performed CibersortX deconvolution to determine which cell fractions contributed to the bulk signatures of T2-high and T2-low asthma. We found higher eosinophil and monocyte fractions in T2-high, and higher CD8 fractions in T2-low asthma (online supplemental table 2). CibersortX-derived cell-type coefficients showed positive correlations with automated complete blood counts for neutrophils (r=0.65, p<0.01), eosinophils (r=0.3, p<0.01) and lymphocytes (r=0.3, p<0.01). These findings indicate that transcriptional features of LCs are influenced by the cellular composition of the whole blood samples. Adjusting for measured or deconvoluted cell fractions led to overadjustment, which disproportionately reduced the signal from the most abundant cell populations and attenuated biologically relevant differences linked to key immune cell types. We present the unadjusted results here.

To determine whether the identified microRNA–mRNA networks were specific to asthma, we conducted a separate DIABLO analysis comparing individuals with asthma to 20 non-asthmatic controls. The clinical characteristics of the controls are shown in online supplemental table 3. Similar to the asthma-only analysis, microRNA and mRNA profiles also showed a strong correlation (r=0.66, p<0.05). DIABLO analysis of asthma and controls (figure 2E–H) identified unique mRNAs that did not overlap with those from the asthma-only analysis (figure 2A–D). In contrast, microRNAs were shared across both analyses. Most overlapping microRNAs were asthma-specific and included miR-1183, miR-125a-5p, miR-142-3p, miR-144-3p, miR-20a-5p+miR-20b-5p, miR-222-3p, miR-374a-5p, miR-376a-3p and miR-574-3p. MiR-574-3p was the only microRNA shared by T2-high asthma and controls. Accordingly, most of the microRNA networks identified in the asthma-only analysis were asthma-specific.

To evaluate whether the transcriptional architecture of subjects with specific asthma endotypes (n=88) is similar to that of subjects without a known endotype due to absence of data for blood eosinophil counts and/or FeNO (n=17) (online supplemental table 4), we performed canonical correlation analysis of mRNA and microRNA expression. We found that LC1 and LC2 components were perfectly correlated between subjects with asthma who had an endotype assignment and those without endotype assignment (online supplemental figures 6 and 7). This analysis shows that expression of LC components is not dependent on endotype assignments.

Molecular pathways linked to LCs

To determine how distinct molecular pathways contributed to each LC, we examined the direction and strength of microRNA-mRNA relationships using Pearson’s correlation (r>0.30) (figure 3A,B). In DIABLO, the loading coefficients reflect each variable’s contribution to the component, with their sign indicating whether expression is positively or negatively aligned with it. In LC1, T2-low and T2-high microRNAs exerted opposing directional influences on LC1 mRNAs: T2-low microRNAs (miR-574-3p and miR-942-5p) were negatively correlated, while the T2-high microRNA miR-486-3p showed a positive correlation with T2-high mRNAs (figure 3A).

LC2 exhibited a more intricate pattern of regulatory relationships between microRNAs and mRNAs

The T2-low microRNAs miR-1183, miR-491-5p, miR-513b-5p, miR-451a, miR-374a-5p, miR-1910-5p, miR-1299 and miR-142-3p were positively correlated with multiple T2-low mRNAs and negatively correlated with a subset of T2-high mRNAs, including CLSTN3, MFSD10, PMF1, CDK9, ZFAND2A and SMAD7 (figure 3B).

To characterise known pairs of microRNAs and their mRNA targets, we used TargetScan,32 which confirmed correlations between T2-low microRNAs and known target mRNAs in both LCs (online supplemental figure 8). This analysis also showed that miR-486-3p, miR-574-5p, miR-942-5p, miR-1299 and miR-513b-5p targeted the greatest number of mRNAs in the LCs. MiR-486-3p and miR-574-5p strongly aligned with T2-high mRNAs.

To identify the molecular pathways that contribute to each LC, we examined both microRNA and mRNA correlation networks with all protein-coding transcripts on the Affymetrix array. For the correlation network of each transcript, we compared pathway enrichment Z-scores (figure 3C,D). In LC1, we found associations between mRNA-microRNA networks and multiple pathways associated with T2 inflammation, including IL-4 and IL-13 signalling. In LC1, the T2-low microRNAs miR-574-3p and miR-942-5p were enriched for mitochondrial dysfunction, granzyme A signalling and expression and translocation of olfactory receptors pathways. The same microRNAs were negatively correlated with most T2-high mRNA and microRNA pathways (figure 3C, online supplemental table 5).

Analysis of LC2 revealed a more complex relationship between T2-low and T2-high transcripts and their associated pathways. In LC2, T2-low mRNAs were strongly linked to IL-17 signalling and to a lesser extent Th1 and Th2 pathways (figure 3D; online supplemental table 6). Several T2-low mRNAs also showed positive correlations with smooth muscle contraction. Another cluster of T2-low transcripts and microRNAs in LC2 was connected to neutrophil degranulation, oxidative phosphorylation and TRIM21-mediated signalling, with correlation patterns that were aligned between microRNAs and mRNAs (figure 3B).

Validation in two independent asthma cohorts

To externally validate these findings, we used a previously described cohort of subjects with asthma.33 As expected, subjects with T2-high asthma had a higher number of blood eosinophils (422 vs 103 cells/microlitre, p<0.05). We first assessed differential gene expression between T2-high (n=219) and T2-low (n=77) samples. We found 152 DEGs (absolute log fold change >0.2, FDR<0.05) (online supplemental table 7). Subjects with T2-high asthma showed upregulation of 23 genes corresponding to T2-high mRNAs associated with LCs, whereas all 10 genes corresponding to T2-low mRNAs remained unchanged in these subjects (figure 4A). DEG pathway enrichment (online supplemental figure 9) was concordant with the LCs identified in the discovery cohort (figure 3C,D). Genes upregulated in T2-high asthma were linked to Th2 pathways, particularly IL-13 signalling, while those elevated in T2-low asthma were connected to STAT3 activation and smooth muscle contraction. Furthermore, in a transcriptional meta-analysis of this validation cohort and the discovery cohort, we identified multiple DEGs overlapping both endotypes (online supplemental table 8).

Gene set enrichment analysis in the validation cohort revealed significant enrichment of the LC1 microRNA miR-574-3p signature (ES=0.78, FDR=0.03) (online supplemental figure 10, online supplemental table 9). We built a LASSO classifier using LC mRNAs and applied it to the validation blood samples, which resulted in an area under the curve of 0.75 (figure 4B). We then conducted a feature importance analysis to determine which genes contributed most to the performance of the LASSO classifier and identified 20 genes capable of distinguishing between T2-low and T2-high endotypes (figure 4C). Although T2-high genes dominated the classifier (n=16), the T2-low gene CFHR2, a member of the complement pathway, showed the highest feature importance across all mRNAs.

Since the dynamic range of transcriptomic quantification may be affected by profiling platforms, we sought to validate the integrative signature in a cohort profiled with RNA sequencing. We evaluated the integrative signature in a study of patients with severe eosinophilic asthma before and after the administration of the IL-5 receptor antagonist benralizumab.35 A total of 30 mRNAs (29 T2-high and 1 T2-low) included in the LCs were expressed in this dataset (online supplemental figure 11). Benralizumab treatment resulted in 510 DEGs that were altered over the 4-month treatment period, and twenty-two of these were part of the LCs linked to T2-high asthma (figure 4D, online supplemental table 10, online supplemental figure 12). Using these two validation analyses, we found that most T2-high transcripts were expressed in the validation samples, whereas very few T2-low transcripts were expressed. Finally, we found that a large majority of T2-high genes within the LC signature changed significantly following treatment with an anti-IL5 receptor antagonist for severe eosinophilic asthma.

Discussion

We identified novel integrated microRNA and mRNA networks distinguishing T2-high and T2-low asthma endotypes. T2-high and T2-low RNAs were both highly correlated with their respective endotypes and were specific to asthma. Notably, the microRNAs enriched in T2-low asthma targeted multiple biological pathways characteristic of this endotype, suggesting that coordinated post-transcriptional regulation contributes to its distinct molecular profile. Compared with traditional differential gene expression analyses, integrating mRNA and microRNA expression profiles offers a more comprehensive and mechanistically informative approach for biomarker discovery by capturing post-transcriptional regulatory interactions. Together, these findings expand our understanding of the T2-low asthma endotype by contrasting its molecular signatures with the better known T2-high asthma endotype.

To facilitate the clinical translation of these integrative analyses, endotypes were defined by previously established thresholds for blood eosinophils and FeNO.28 These clinical biomarkers also helped us understand the cellular composition of each LC. LC1 was predominantly eosinophilic and T2-high, consistent with the known clinical features of T2-high asthma. Several genes in LC1, including IL5RA and CLC, have been previously linked to eosinophilic inflammation in asthma, and their encoded proteins are targeted by currently available biologic therapies.29 30 Moreover, we found that benralizumab significantly inhibited expression of these two genes in an independent validation cohort of patients with severe eosinophilic asthma.

Several microRNAs within LC1 and LC2 have been previously associated with asthma and/or lung function,39 40 but none have been identified in connection with the T2-low endotype. Therefore, the regulatory networks defined by these microRNAs may help clarify the underlying mechanisms of the T2-low endotype. We identified novel microRNA networks that tightly regulate the T2-low-associated transcription factors SOX1 and SOX8 in LC2. It remains unclear how these two developmentally related transcription factors are linked to T2-low asthma, warranting further investigation.41 These findings suggest that T2-low microRNAs play a complex role in controlling gene expression by directly targeting mRNA, the primary mechanism of action for microRNAs, and indirectly by modulating transcription factors, a known mechanism of transcriptional regulation.42 Collectively, these results point to a complex role for microRNA networks in post-translational regulation of T2-low asthma.

Both LCs contained multiple T2-low microRNAs and mRNAs associated with bitter taste receptor pathways. Bitter taste receptors are expressed in airway smooth muscle and modulate intracellular calcium responses,43 thereby modulating smooth muscle cell contraction and airway reactivity. These findings suggest that T2-low asthma may be enriched for non-inflammatory pathways involved in airway hyperreactivity.

We also demonstrate how microRNA-mRNA integration can be used to identify drug responses to biological therapies. The IL-5 receptor antagonist benralizumab significantly reduced the expression of most T2-high genes in the LCs, providing additional support to the molecular role of these LCs in T2-high asthma. Furthermore, T2-low transcripts in LC2 were associated with the IL-17 pathway, highlighting a potential link between this immune pathway and the T2-low endotype.

Our study has several limitations, including the use of bulk RNA for mRNA and microRNA profiling rather than single-cell analysis. We compensated for this approach by including cell count correlations to help interpret the LCs and by performing deconvolution with CibersortX.31 Another limitation was the use of a narrow definition of the T2 endotype that did not include allergic sensitisation.28 However, we showed that the T2-high and T2-low endotypes had distinct levels of blood IgE, which is an accepted surrogate marker of allergic sensitisation. We also used the same definition when assessing the microarray data in the validation cohort. In addition, we used array technologies for profiling these individuals, which typically have a lower dynamic range compared with RNA sequencing. To address this, we used an RNA-seq validation cohort in addition to the microarray validation cohort. Finally, we could not assess the behaviour of the MSiDB over time because of the cross-sectional design of the study, but we partially compensated for this by including an analysis of patient samples taken at two timepoints following treatment with benralizumab. Future studies are needed to provide insight into the longitudinal behaviour of these signatures and functional assays to validate the regulatory interactions inferred in our analyses.

In conclusion, we delineated distinct microRNA-mRNA regulatory networks that help to define the molecular architecture of T2-high and T2-low asthma endotypes. T2-low asthma was uniquely associated with expression of taste receptors and pathways regulating smooth muscle contraction, two mechanisms directly implicated in bronchoconstriction. It is possible that T2-low asthma may benefit from existing therapeutics targeting bitter taste receptors if future studies validate our observations. Our finding that T2-high transcripts were modulated by benralizumab highlights their potential use as biomarkers of therapeutic efficacy. Together, these findings enhance our mechanistic insight into asthma heterogeneity and inform precision approaches to biologic treatment.
