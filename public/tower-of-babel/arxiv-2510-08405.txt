Routed Bell tests with arbitrarily many local parties
Gereon Koßmann, Mario Berta, René Schwonnek

Abstract
Device-independent quantum key distribution (DIQKD) promises cryptographic security based solely on observed quantum correlations, yet its implementation over long distances remains limited by the detection-efficiency loophole. Routed Bell tests have recently re-emerged as a promising strategy to mitigate this limitation by enabling local self-testing of one party’s device. However, extending this idea to self-testing both communicating parties has remained unclear. Here, we introduce a modified setup that enables local self-tests for both Alice and Bob and analyze its security against potential attacks. Employing modern tools from robust self-testing, we show that in a BB84-type protocol between the self-tested devices, the achievable key rate varies continuously with the winning probability of the local tests. In particular, we find that perfect local Bell tests can, in principle, overcome the detection-efficiency barrier, rendering the asymptotic key rate limited only by standard bit-flip errors, as in the device-dependent case.



Key distribution.

Quantum theory offers a, to date, unique opportunity to establish information-theoretic secrecy between distant parties, guaranteed by the laws of nature themselves
Ekert and Renner 2014
. Since the seminal works on
quantum key distribution
(QKD)
Bennett and Brassard 2014
;
Ekert 1991
, extensive research has sought to delineate the mathematical and experimental conditions required for a secure key-exchange protocol (see e.g.
Renner 2008
;
Scarani et al. 2009
;
Pirandola et al. 2020
and references therein). Early bipartite protocols presupposed nearly perfect devices and an infinite number of rounds to circumvent statistical finite-size corrections
Bennett et al. 1992
. It has since become clear that, in QKD, one must in principle account for all imperfections, all mismatches between theory and experiment, and all finite-size effects in order to assert secrecy of the generated key (see
Tomamichel 2012
;
Scarani et al. 2009
and subsequent works).

Device independence.

In a subsequent, now well-established, model of QKD, one seeks to avoid any assumptions about the devices themselves beyond the fundamental premise that they obey the laws of quantum theory. This gold standard of QKD is known as
device-independent quantum key distribution
(DIQKD)
Mayers and Yao 1998
. DIQKD experiments are rooted in Ekert’s protocol
Ekert 1991
and, at their core, use a Bell test — a test originally employed to probe Bell’s theorem, which essentially rules out the existence of a theory violating a Bell inequality which is at the same time empirically correct, local, and classical
Bell 1964
;
Werner and Wolf 2001
. Although DIQKD, relying solely on a Bell experiment, allows one to dispense with detailed device models and the need to incorporate experimental procedures into a security proof, one must still address all potential loopholes that could threaten the conclusions of Bell’s theorem, as these manifest in the (DI)QKD framework as possible attacks by an eavesdropper
Hensen et al. 2015
;
Nadlinger et al. 2022
;
Liu et al. 2022
;
Zhang et al. 2022
.

In this letter, we address attacks on the empirical correctness by providing a protocol featuring arbitrarily many local self-tests for each party.

Loopholes.

The loophole concerning empirical correctness in a Bell experiment is commonly termed the
detection loophole
(see e.g.
Christensen et al. 2013
for an early work on the topic). Roughly speaking, as the separation between the two parties increases, the number of events reaching the local devices decreases due to fundamental physical laws such as the Beer–Lambert law, thereby calling empirical correctness into question. In particular, without the
fair-sampling
assumption — that losses are unbiased so that the detected outcomes form a representative sample — there exists a lower bound on the
detection efficiency
required for a secure Bell test
Szangolies et al. 2017
: below this threshold, the test may lose its (approximate) empirical correctness. Because this challenge is rooted in fundamental physics, it cannot be overcome merely by more sophisticated security-proof techniques (e.g., optimizing the certified uncertainty extracted from the data), instead, it calls for new protocols. One proposed approach is the use of local Bell tests
Lim et al. 2013
;
Chaturvedi et al. 2024
;
Lobo et al. 2024
;
Le Roy-Deloison et al. 2025
;
Tan and Wolf 2024
, wherein a third party within Alice’s laboratory essentially self-tests Alice’s device, thereby lowering the threshold for long-range key extraction. Recent numerical results using techniques to estimate the conditional von Neumann entropy in a device-independent scenario indicate that introducing such a third party in Alice’s lab improves the required detection efficiencies
Tan and Wolf 2024
;
Le Roy-Deloison et al. 2025
.

Second, the locality invoked in Bell’s theorem gives rise to the well-known
locality loophole
, which requires that the underlying devices be unable to communicate faster than the measurement statistics are recorded; otherwise, one cannot claim to have probed an experiment compatible with a local theory, such as quantum theory. As the locality loophole—and its associated implications—concerns mainly experimental challenges on the road to a fully DIQKD experiment, we refer to
Kulikov et al. 2024
for up-to-date progress and references therein.

Approach.

We propose a generalized framework for examining local Bell tests that allows for an arbitrary number of local parties on each side. The central idea is to replace the switch in the proposals of
Lim et al. 2013
;
Chaturvedi et al. 2024
;
Lobo et al. 2024
;
Le Roy-Deloison et al. 2025
;
Tan and Wolf 2024
with two local sources that randomly output states within Alice’s and Bob’s laboratories. For each such state, the second subsystem is either routed to another local party in Alice’s (or Bob’s) lab or sent to an entanglement-swapping experiment between Alice and Bob (for details about entanglement swapping see
Pan et al. 1998
;
Żukowski et al. 1993

Żukowski et al. 1993
;
Gisin et al. 2010
for details). The experiment is thus governed by two local random variables,

and
T_{B}

Security assumptions.

As usual, we assume no knowledge of the devices’ internal workings beyond that they follow the laws of quantum theory. However, unlike
Le Roy-Deloison et al. 2025
;
Tan and Wolf 2024
depicted in
Figure 1
(a), we must clarify how Eve is modeled. In our setting, Alice (with her partners) and Bob (with his partners) are strictly locally separated, and Eve is spacelike separated from both laboratories; all partners operate in secure labs and are not under Eve’s control. This does not violate the overall assumptions of a DIQKD experiment.

By contrast, in
Le Roy-Deloison et al. 2025
;
Tan and Wolf 2024
Eve has access to the switch and may measure the third party in
Figure 1
(a) as well as her own system. A key obstacle to extending that model to arbitrarily many parties — especially intermediate parties between Alice and Bob — is that all relevant laboratories must be spacelike separated. Allowing Eve simultaneous access to all partners would raise the question of her location. Independent of that, our model fully aligns with standard DIQKD assumptions while placing Alice’s and Bob’s partners in secure, spacelike separated laboratories. This choice yields a substantially simpler analysis of the achievable key rate in (
3
).

To prevent attacks on the source, we explicitly discuss how to model the marginal constraints, since they are crucial. Experimentally, the key assumption is that the sources are completely decoupled from Alice’s and Bob’s devices. In particular, Alice’s (respectively Bob’s) device must not alter its internal workings when the source sends different types of states. Thus, in any concrete implementation of our protocol, it is essential to argue that this assumption holds. A similar assumption is required in the routed scenario of
Figure 1
(a), cf. the discussion below
(
Le Roy-Deloison et al. 2025
, Figure 1)

Arbitrarily many parties.

We conclude this section by explaining how to extend our techniques to arbitrarily many parties. By generalizing the switch so that it can route states to an arbitrary number of parties in
Figure 1
(b), we can perform correlation experiments between Alice and each of those partners, and analogously for Bob. These additional parties can then be used to probe different types of correlations between Alice’s measurements and those of the corresponding partner. The marginal constraint then generalizes to the fact that all marginals on Alice side equal and all marginals on Bob’s side equal.

II
Results

BB84 type protocol.

The simplest but nevertheless technologically highly relevant example demonstrating a key rate advantages by a multi routed setting is the following: We consider an enhanced entanglement base BB84 type protocol, which can be implemented using a local Bell test between Alice and Fred, and between Bob and George, together with a BB84 protocol between Alice and Bob. Numerical results using the full statistics and the methods described in the previous section are depicted in
Figure 2
. Here, we also compare to the single switch setting previously considered in literature. Albeit a single switch suffices to obtain the tight BB84 key rates of
Woodhead 2016
in the limit of a perfect self test
Tan and Wolf 2024
;
Le Roy-Deloison et al. 2025
, two switches yield a relevant key rate advantage whenever the self test is imperfect.

In order to complement our numerical observations by an analytical result on the robustness with respect to the self testing error (stated as
Theorem 1
below), we have to fix some notation. In an entanglement based BB84 protocol Alice and Bob each perform two types of measurements with binary outcomes. In an honest implementation those would be mutually unbiased qubit measurements. For convenience, we will label these measurements by

and

also for the, a priori, device independent case. Keys are extracted from correlations of in the

Z_{B}
measurements.
Following the standard analysis of an BB84 type experiment, the measurement statistics can be compressed by only considering the individual bit errors for the
X
and
Z
measurements. This is, we only ask for the probabilities that the
X_{A}
and
X_{B}
or, respectively,
Z_{A}
and
Z_{B}
measurements give coinciding outcomes.
We will refer to the random variables of these errors by

and
Q_{Z}
and to

and

as the corresponding error rates, where
h

It is well known that, at least in an honest implementation, a BB84 type protocol promises better key rates than a CHSH-Test based protocol like E91 when the Quantum bit error rate (here
h(Q_{X})
is taken as parameter for a fair comparison). Nevertheless, in a fully DI setting, the BB84 key rate will drop to zero, since the MUB measurements performed by Alice and Bob admit a local hidden variable model. In such a LHV model all local measurements would commute. The basic intuition behind the local self test is to rule out that this is the case. Invoking recent advancements in the theory of robust self-testing
Zhao 2024
, we can make this intuition precise (see also
Baccari et al. 2020
for further work on robust self-testing). We argue that the local Bell tests robustly self-test the algebras of Alice and Bob. Consequently, even under small perturbations of the local Bell tests, the overlaps of Alice’s and Bob’s algebras introduce only an
\mathcal{O}(\sqrt{\varepsilon})
error in the resulting key rate. This is remarkable in the sense that it shows that the local algebras in Alice’s and Bob’s laboratories can be made provably robust against noise.

III
Conclusion

Outlook.

Recent progress in device-independent quantum key distribution (DIQKD) indicates that the decrease in detection efficiency over long distances may become a central challenge for the next steps in device-independent security frameworks. Building on proposals for routed Bell tests
Lim et al. 2013
;
Chaturvedi et al. 2024
;
Lobo et al. 2024
;
Le Roy-Deloison et al. 2025
;
Tan and Wolf 2024
, we propose in this letter a setup that allows for arbitrarily many self-testing parties for both Alice and Bob, in comparison to other proposals, which have focused on the analysis of one party close to Alice’s lab. In order to connect to these developments, we show in
Theorem 2
in the appendix that our framework, reduced to the routed Bell scenario, is able to produce the correlations with the same conditional von Neumann entropy of a measurement in Alice’s lab conditioned on an eavesdropper. Thus, our framework is able to include the already existing framework and extends it. Moreover, the connection between recent developments in robust self-tests
Zhao 2024
enables us to give a proof-of-principle result for the robustness of protocols involving a self-test per party. As we will never be able to self-test exactly in a lab, this type of result is of central importance in order to justify security analyses involving self-tests in real-world environments. Given this, we see that similar results are achievable various other local games, as already indicated in
Zhao 2024
— which we leave for future research.

c^{\star}
to
c_{A}^{\star}
by the following observations. For binary projective measurements
Z,X
on Alice,

c^{\star}\leq 2\cdot c_{A}^{\star},

(14)

because the definition of
c^{\star}
, we can take a joint Naimark dilation
U
of
(Z,X)
to
(Z^{\prime},X^{\prime})
and a projective "slicing"
K^{\prime}=\{P_{k}\}
that commutes with
Z^{\prime},X^{\prime}
on the support of
U\sigma_{A}U^{\dagger}
. In this block decomposition, each block
k
is at most two-dimensional and the restrictions of
Z^{\prime},X^{\prime}
are binary projective measurements. Writing
p_{k}\coloneqq\operatorname{tr}[P_{k}U\sigma_{A}U^{\dagger}]
and letting
c_{k}
be the standard overlap of the two binary projectors within block
k
,
Tomamichel and Hänggi 2013
yields

\displaystyle c^{\star}=\sum_{k}p_{k}c_{k}.

(15)

On the other hand,

\displaystyle c_{A}^{\star}=\max_{a,b}\;\operatorname{tr}\bigl[\sigma_{A}\,M_{a|Z}M_{b|X}M_{a|Z}\bigr]=\max_{a,b}\sum_{k}p_{k}q_{a|k}c_{k},

(16)

where
q_{a|k}\coloneqq\operatorname{tr}\bigl[P_{k}U\sigma_{A}U^{\dagger}M^{\prime}_{a|Z}\bigr]/p_{k}
is the conditional probability of
Z
-outcome
a
inside block
k
. Since
q_{0|k}+q_{1|k}=1
, we have
\max_{a}q_{a|k}\geq\frac{1}{2}
for every
k
, whence

\displaystyle c_{A}^{\star}\geq\frac{1}{2}\sum_{k}p_{k}\,c_{k}=\frac{1}{2}c^{\star}.

(17)

This is equivalent to
c^{\star}\leq 2\,c_{A}^{\star}
. Thus, combinig (
13
) and (
14
) yields

H(Z_{A}|E)+H(X_{A}|B)\geq-\log_{2}(2c_{A}^{\star})=-1-\log_{2}c_{A}^{\star}.

(18)

By data-processing, Fano’s inequality and the observed bit-error rates, we have

\displaystyle H(X_{A}|B)\leq H(X_{A}|X_{B})\leq h(Q_{X}),\qquad H(Z_{A}|Z_{B})\leq h(Q_{Z}).

(19)

Inserting the ingredients into the Devetak–formula with key extracted from
Z
gives

\displaystyle r_{\operatorname{key}}\geq H(Z_{A}|E)-H(Z_{A}|Z_{B}).

(20)

From (
18
) we have
H(Z_{A}|E)\geq-1-\log_{2}c_{A}^{\star}-H(X_{A}|B)
, hence

\displaystyle r\geq\bigl[-1-\log_{2}c_{A}^{\star}-H(X_{A}|B)\bigr]-H(Z_{A}|Z_{B})\geq-1-\log_{2}c_{A}^{\star}-h(Q_{X})-h(Q_{Z}).

(21)

Using
c_{A}^{\star}\leq\frac{1}{4}+8\delta
from self-testing, with
\delta=\mathcal{O}(\sqrt{\varepsilon})
, we obtain

\displaystyle-1-\log_{2}c_{A}^{\star}\geq-1-\log_{2}\Bigl(\frac{1}{4}+8\delta\Bigr)=1-\mathcal{O}(\delta)=1-\mathcal{O}(\sqrt{\varepsilon}).

(22)

Therefore,

\displaystyle r\geq 1-h(Q_{X})-h(Q_{Z})-\mathcal{O}(\sqrt{\varepsilon}),

(23)

which yields in the limit
\varepsilon\to 0
the optimal asymptotic BB84 rate
r\geq 1-h(Q_{X})-h(Q_{Z})
.
∎
