Ergodicity and error estimate of laws for a random splitting Langevin Monte Carlo
Lei Li, Chen Wang, Mengchao Wang

Abstract
The random splitting Langevin Monte Carlo could mitigate the first order bias in Langevin Monte Carlo with little extra work compared other high order schemes. We develop in this work an analysis framework for the sampling error under Wasserstein distance regarding the random splitting Langevin Monte Carlo. First, the sharp local truncation error is obtained by the relative entropy approach together with the explicit formulas for the commutator of related semi-groups. The necessary pointwise estimates of the gradient and Hessian of the logarithmic density are established by the Bernstein type approach in PDE theory. Second, the geometric ergodicity is established by accommodation of the reflection coupling. Combining the ergodicity with the local error estimate, we establish a uniform-in-time sampling error bound, showing that the invariant measure of the method approximates the true Gibbs distribution with O ⁡ ( τ 2 ) O(\tau^{2}) accuracy where τ \tau is the time step. Lastly, we perform numerical experiments to validate the theoretical results.

While the fact that the randomized splitting could improve accuracy is intuitively clear, the rigorous analysis of its long-time behavior and the error bound, in particular under Wasserstein distances, have not been systematically established. We remark that the quantitative error analysis for laws of numerical schemes of SDEs and sampling methods under certain metrics (e.g., Wasserstein distances, total variation distance) becomes a central topic in recent years. Compared to the traditional weak error analysis where the bound depends on the particular choice of smooth test function

It is noted that the relative entropy has recently been proved useful to obtain the sharp error bounds using some PDE techniques and eventually yield bounds under the Wasserstein distances using the transport inequalities. The

2
Setup and some basic results

As mentioned above, the Langevin Monte Carlo introduces a first order bias of the order
O(\tau)
in the invariant distribution.

As mentioned, we propose a
random splitting method
that alternates between the drift (
\dot{X}=-\nabla U(X)
) and diffusion (
dX=\sqrt{2\beta^{-1}}dW
) steps in random orders. This randomization cancels low-order bias terms asymptotically, enabling higher-order accuracy. Note that the random splitting approach can be generalized to general splitting with
p
terms for
p\geq 2
, and can be applied to other types of SDEs like the underdamped Langevin equation.
In this work, we stick to this simple setting and propose an analysis framework for the error estimate. Our goal is to show that the splitting method has a second order sampling accuracy under some metric.
In particular, under the Wasserstein distances. The multiplicative noise case can be generalized to as well, but is much more involved.

To improve the order one may apply some second order schemes, for example, the symmetrized Trotter splitting can achieve
O(\tau^{2})
accuracy but it requires evaluation of dynamics for multiple times in one iteration.

2.1
The random splitting Langevin Monte Carlo

As mentioned, for the random splitting Langevin Monte Carlo, we split the SDE into an ordinary differential equation (ODE) and a pure Brownian diffusion, namely

\displaystyle\dot{X}=-\nabla U(X)

(2.1)

and

\displaystyle dX=\sqrt{2\beta^{-1}}\,dW.

(2.2)

The random splitting approach is to solve these two parts consecutively in a random order for each iteration. The ODE part can be solved
using any standard method that is at least second order, like the Runge-Kutta method of second order accuracy. Let such a solver with initial state
x
be denoted by

\displaystyle X(\tau)=:S(x,\tau).

(2.3)

The diffusion part is
to add simply a standard normal variable to the process. The detailed method is given in Algorithm

For notational convenience, we denote

\displaystyle b(x):=-\nabla U(x),

(2.6)

and the density evolution for the ODE is then given by

\displaystyle\partial_{t}\rho+\nabla\cdot(b(x)\rho)=0.

(2.7)

Clearly, the operator corresponding to the ODE part can be expressed as

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}_{1}(\rho)=-\nabla\cdot(b(x)\rho).

(2.8)

Similarly, the density evolution for the diffusion step is

\displaystyle\partial_{t}\rho=\beta^{-1}\Delta\rho=:\mathcal{L}_{2}(\rho).

(2.9)

The Fokker-Planck operator for the Langevin equation is clearly

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}\rho=\mathcal{L}_{1}(\rho)+\mathcal{L}_{2}(\rho)=-\nabla\cdot(b(x)\rho)+\beta^{-1}\Delta\rho.

(2.10)

To estimate the local truncation error, we need the expressions of the semigroup. Let
\phi_{t}(x)
denote the flow map for the ODE, which is the trajectory of the ODE with initial position
x
:

\displaystyle\partial_{t}\phi_{t}(x)=b(\phi_{t}(x)),\quad\phi_{0}(x)=x.

(2.11)

Moreover, we introduce the determinant of the Jacobi matrix

\displaystyle J(t,x):=\det(\nabla_{x}\phi_{t}(x)).

(2.12)

Along the ODE, it is easy to see that

\displaystyle\frac{\partial}{\partial t}\nabla_{x}\phi_{t}(x)=\nabla_{x}\phi_{t}(x)\cdot\nabla b(\phi_{t}(x)),

(2.13)

where we use the convention that

(\nabla b)_{ij}=\partial_{i}b_{j}(x).

It then follows that

\displaystyle\partial_{t}J(t,x)=\text{tr}((\nabla_{x}\phi)^{-1}\partial_{t}(\nabla_{x}\phi))J(t,x)=(\nabla\cdot b)(\phi_{t}(x))J(t,x).

(2.14)

We note the following simple fact.

Lemma 2.1
.

Let
\rho
denote the probability density function associated with the SDE (
1.1
). With the operators
\mathcal{L}_{1}
and
\mathcal{L}_{2}
defined in (

) and (

), respectively, then the semigroups are given as follows:

\displaystyle e^{t\mathcal{L}_{1}}\rho(x)=\rho(\phi_{-t}(x))J(-t,x),

(2.15)

and that

\displaystyle e^{t\mathcal{L}_{2}}\rho(x)=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\frac{1}{(4\pi\beta^{-1}t)^{\frac{d}{2}}}e^{-\frac{\beta|x-y|^{2}}{4t}}\rho(y)dy.

(2.16)

Proof.

Consider the semigroup
e^{t\mathcal{L}_{1}}
. Denote
\varrho(x,t)=e^{t\mathcal{L}_{1}}\rho
. Then,

\displaystyle\partial_{t}\varrho+b\cdot\nabla\varrho=-(\nabla\cdot b)\varrho.

(2.17)

Then, along the characteristics, it is easy to see that

\displaystyle\varrho(\phi_{t}(x),t)=\varrho(x,0)\exp\left(-\int_{0}^{t}\nabla\cdot b(\phi_{s}(x))\,ds\right).

(2.18)

By (

), one has

(e^{t\mathcal{L}_{1}}\rho)(\phi_{t}(x))=\rho(x)J(t,x)^{-1}.

Using the property of evolution group,

\phi_{-t}(\phi_{t}(x))=x\quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad J(-t,\phi_{t}(x))J(t,x)=1.

It then follows that

\displaystyle(e^{t\mathcal{L}_{1}}\rho)(\phi_{t}(x))=\rho(x)J(-t,\phi_{t}(x)).

(2.19)

The expression for the first semigroup then follows.

Since

generates the standard heat semigroup on

\displaystyle e^{t\mathcal{L}_{2}}\rho(x)=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\frac{1}{(4\pi\beta^{-1}t)^{\frac{d}{2}}}e^{-\frac{\beta|x-y|^{2}}{4t}}\rho(y)dy.

For a given random variable
\zeta

\xi

\{(1,2),(2,1)\}
. The probability of each permutation is
1/2
. The density evolution is then given by

\displaystyle\rho_{n+1}^{\xi}=e^{\tau\mathcal{L}_{\xi(2)}}e^{\tau\mathcal{L}_{\xi(1)}}\rho_{n}^{\xi},

(2.21)

where the operators are applied in random order at each step.
The law of

is then

\bar{\rho}_{n+1}=\mathbb{E}_{\xi}\rho_{n+1}^{\xi}.

Here, we consider two versions of the time-continuous density. The first is

\displaystyle\rho^{\xi}(t)=e^{(t-n\tau)\mathcal{L}_{\xi(1)}}e^{(t-n\tau)\mathcal{L}_{\xi(2)}}\rho_{n}^{\xi},\quad t\in[n\tau,(n+1)\tau],

(2.22)

while the second is

\displaystyle\rho^{\xi_{n}}(t)=e^{(t-n\tau)\mathcal{L}_{\xi(1)}}e^{(t-n\tau)\mathcal{L}_{\xi(2)}}\bar{\rho}_{n},\quad t\in[n\tau,(n+1)\tau].

(2.23)

By the linearity of the operators, one clearly has

\rho^{\xi_{n}}(t)=\mathbb{E}_{\xi_{1},\cdots,\xi_{n-1}}[\rho^{\xi}(t)|\xi_{n}].

That means the average is taken by considering all the random permutations for
t_{i},i\leq n-1
.
We then define

\displaystyle\bar{\rho}(t)=\mathbb{E}_{\xi_{n}}\rho^{\xi_{n}}(t)=\mathbb{E}_{\xi}\rho^{\xi}(t).

(2.24)

Since
\bar{\rho}(t_{n})=\bar{\rho}_{n}
which is the law of
X_{n}
.
The key quantity of interest is the approximation error between
\bar{\rho}(t)
and the law of the original Langevin dynamics
\rho
.
To quantify the approximation error, we use the
relative entropy (also Kullback-Leibler (KL) divergence). The relative entropy between two probability measures
\mu,\nu
are defined by

\displaystyle\mathcal{H}(\mu\,\|\,\nu):=\begin{cases}\displaystyle\int\log\!\Big(\frac{d\mu}{d\nu}\Big)\,d\mu,&\mu\ll\nu,\\[3.44444pt]
+\infty,&\text{otherwise},\end{cases}

(2.25)

where
\tfrac{d\mu}{d\nu}
denotes the Radon–Nikodym derivative, which rigorously captures both mass displacement and tail behavior discrepancies.
In particular, if
\mu
and
\nu
admit densities
p,q
with respect to Lebesgue measure, then

\mathcal{H}(\mu\|\nu)\;=\;\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}p(x)\,\log\!\frac{p(x)}{q(x)}\,dx.

It is known that the relative entropy has the following properties (see
[
29
,
11
]
)

1.

Nonnegativity:

\mathcal{H}(\mu\|\nu)\geq 0
, with equality if and only if
\mu=\nu
.

2.

Joint convexity:
The map
(\mu,\nu)\mapsto\mathcal{H}(\mu\|\nu)
is jointly convex.

3.

Lower semicontinuity:
For fixed
\nu
, the map
\mu\mapsto\mathcal{H}(\mu\|\nu)
is lower semicontinuous with respect to weak convergence of measures.

The relative entropy is useful because it can be used to control the Wasserstein distances through the transport inequalities.
We recall that the Wasserstein distances between two probability measures
\mu,\nu\in\mathcal{P}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
are defined by

W_{p}(\mu,\nu):=\inf_{\pi\in\Gamma(\mu,\nu)}\left(\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}\times\mathbb{R}^{d}}|x-y|^{p}\,d\pi(x,y)\right)^{1/p},\quad p\geq 1,

where
\Gamma(\mu,\nu)
denotes the set of all couplings of
\mu
and
\nu
.

(T
1
)

If
\nu
satisfies sub-Gaussian concentration for all 1-Lipschitz functions,
then there exists
C>0
such that

W_{1}(\mu,\nu)\;\leq\;\sqrt{\,2C\,\mathcal{H}(\mu\|\nu)}.

(T
2
)

If
\nu
satisfies a logarithmic Sobolev inequality with constant
C
, then

W_{2}(\mu,\nu)\;\leq\;\sqrt{2C\,\mathcal{H}(\mu\|\nu)}

Moreover, since one has
W_{1}\leq W_{2}
, hence
T_{2}(C)\Rightarrow T_{1}(C)
.

Our goal is to prove error bounds of the form
\mathcal{H}(\bar{\rho}(t)\|\rho(t))\leq C(T)\tau^{4}
under appropriate assumptions.
Later, we will obtain a uniform subGaussian estimate for
\rho(t)
so that
\rho(t)
satisfies a uniform
T_{1}(C_{T})
inequality. Then, the bound on relative entropy can give a sharp bound on the Wasserstein-1 distance of the order

.

2.2
Some assumptions and basic results

We begin by specifying the regularity assumptions on both the potential function

We will work under the following Lipschitz assumptions, which are standard for Langevin Monte Carlo analysis.

Assumption 2.1
.

The potential function

By the assumption, one has the following simple observation.

Lemma 2.2
.

Under Assumption
2.1

for some constant

\displaystyle(x-y)\cdot(b(x)-b(y))\leq-\lambda|x-y|^{2}\quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad(x-y)\cdot(\nabla U(x)-\nabla U(y))\geq\lambda|x-y|^{2}.

.

(i)

Iterating this gives the asserted uniform-in-time bound in (ii).

M\geq\|\rho\|_{L^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{d}\times[0,\infty))}
and all
t\geq 0
that:

|\nabla\log\rho(x,t)|\leq C_{1}(1-\log(\rho(x,t)/M))\leq C(1+|x|^{p_{1}}),

(2.26)

\|\nabla^{2}\log\rho(x,t)\|\leq C(1+|x|^{p_{2}}).

(2.27)

Proof.

In the proof below, we will use
C
to denote a generic constant that is independent of the parameters in consideration. The concrete meaning of
C
can change from line to line.

First order derivative estimates.

As we have shown, for all
t\geq 0
, there is a uniform upper bound for
\rho(t,x)
. Let one such upper bound be
M
.
Let
f=\log\rho
and we consider

\displaystyle v:=\frac{|\nabla f|^{2}}{(1-f+\log M)^{2}}=\frac{f_{k}f_{k}}{(1-f+\log M)^{2}}.

(2.28)

In the proof here, we use the Einstein summation convention so that summation is performed for repeated indices.
Note that

1-f+\log M\geq 1.

One has by Assumption
2.2
that (if the constant
M
does not agree with the one in Assumption
2.2
, one may enlarge the bound slightly)

\sup_{x}v(x,0)<\infty.

Since the equation for
\rho
is linear, without loss of generality, we may take
M=1
for convenience of the discussion in this section.
The equation for
f=\log\rho
(or
\log(\rho/M)
) is given by

f_{t}=\beta^{-1}f_{ii}+\beta^{-1}f_{i}f_{i}-b^{i}f_{i}+c,

where
c=\Delta U
.
Define

\mathcal{L}v:=\beta^{-1}v_{ii}-b^{i}v_{i}-v_{t}.

In the following calculations, without loss of generality, we may take

\beta=1

for convenience in this section.
Then, one can derive the following estimate

\tilde{\mathcal{L}}v:=\mathcal{L}v-\frac{2f}{1-f}f_{i}v_{i}\geq\frac{2}{3}\frac{f_{ki}f_{ki}}{(1-f)^{2}}+(1-f)v^{2}-R_{1}(x),

where

\displaystyle R_{1}(x)=\frac{2cf_{k}f_{k}}{(1-f)^{3}}+2\frac{c_{k}f_{k}-b_{k}^{i}f_{k}f_{i}}{(1-f)^{2}}.

The details are given in Appendix
B
.

Fix
x_{*}
and take the cutoff function
\phi(x)=\psi(|x-x_{*}|/(|x_{*}|+1))
with
\psi
satisfying the following: (1)
\psi\in C^{2}[0,\infty)
such that

0\leq\psi(r)\leq 1
, and is
1
on
[0,1]
,
0
for
r\geq 2
; (2)

\displaystyle\psi^{\prime}\leq 0,\quad|\psi^{\prime}|+|\psi^{\prime\prime}|\leq C_{a}\psi^{a},\quad\forall a\in(0,1).

(2.29)

Then,
\phi
is supported in
B(x_{*},2|x_{*}|+2)
and

\tilde{\mathcal{L}}(\phi v)=\phi\tilde{\mathcal{L}}v+v\tilde{\mathcal{L}}\phi+2\phi_{i}v_{i}.

From the estimates obtained, one has

\phi\tilde{\mathcal{L}}v\geq(1-f)\phi v^{2}-\phi R_{1}(x).

Moreover, due to
\phi_{i}(x)=\psi^{\prime}\left(\frac{|x-x_{*}|}{|x_{*}|+1}\right)\cdot\frac{x_{i}-x_{*i}}{(|x_{*}|+1)|x-x_{*}|}
and
|b(x)|\leq C(1+|x|)
, we find that

\begin{split}v\tilde{\mathcal{L}}\phi&=v\phi_{ii}-b^{i}v\phi_{i}-\frac{2f}{1-f}vf_{i}\phi_{i}\geq Cv(-1-\frac{|b(x)|\mathbf{1}_{|x_{*}|+1\leq|x-x_{*}|\leq 2|x_{*}|+2}}{|x_{*}|+1}-\frac{|f||f_{i}||\phi_{i}|}{1-f})\\
&\geq-Cv\left(1+\epsilon|\phi_{i}|^{2}(1-f)v+\frac{f^{2}}{1-f}\right),\end{split}

and

2\phi_{i}v_{i}=2\phi_{i}\phi^{-1}\left(\phi v\right)_{i}-2\phi_{i}^{2}\phi^{-1}v.

Using the condition
|\psi^{\prime}|\leq C_{a}\psi^{a}
with
a=\frac{1}{2}
, one has

|\phi_{i}|^{2}\leq|\psi^{\prime}(r)|^{2}\cdot\frac{1}{(|x_{*}|+1)^{2}}\leq C^{2}\phi\cdot\frac{1}{(|x_{*}|+1)^{2}}\leq C\phi.

Hence, one has

\displaystyle\tilde{\mathcal{L}}(\phi v)\geq(1-C\epsilon)(1-f)\phi v^{2}-Cv\left[1+\frac{f^{2}}{1-f}\right]+2\phi_{i}\phi^{-1}(\phi v)_{i}-M_{1}\phi,

(2.30)

where

\displaystyle M_{1}=C_{\varepsilon}\big(1+\|\partial b\|_{L^{\infty}}^{2}+\|c\|_{L^{\infty}}^{2}+\|\partial c\|_{L^{\infty}}^{2}\big).

It is then left to consider where the function
\phi v(x,t)
attains its maximum.

•

Since
\phi
vanishes for
|x-x_{*}|\geq 2(|x_{*}|+1)
, if
\phi v
reaches its maximum on the boundary of
[0,\infty)\times B(x_{*},2(|x_{*}|+1))
, then it must be the initial time line so that

v(x_{*},t)=(\phi v)(x_{*},t)\leq\sup_{x}(\phi v)(x,0)\leq\sup_{x}v(x,0).

•

If
\phi v
reaches its maximum at
\left(x^{\prime},t^{\prime}\right)
with
t^{\prime}>0
, then
x^{\prime}
must be in the interior of the ball
B(x_{*},2|x_{*}|+2)
so that
\phi_{i}\phi^{-1}
is well-defined at this point, and
(\phi v)_{i}=0
at that point. Then, at
(x^{\prime},t^{\prime})
, it holds by the parabolic maximum principle that

0\geq\tilde{\mathcal{L}}(\phi v\left(x^{\prime},t^{\prime}\right))\geq(1-C\epsilon)(1-f)\phi v^{2}-Cv\left[1+\frac{f^{2}}{1-f}\right]-M_{1}.

If
v(t^{\prime},x^{\prime})\leq 1
, then one has
v(t,x_{*})=\phi(t,x_{*})w(t,x_{*})\leq w(t^{\prime},x^{\prime})\phi(t^{\prime},x^{\prime})\leq 1
.
Otherwise,

\begin{split}&v(x_{*},t)=\phi(x_{*})v(x_{*},t)\leq\phi(x^{\prime})v(x^{\prime},t^{\prime})\\
\leq&\frac{C}{1-C\epsilon}\frac{1}{1-f}\left[1+\frac{f^{2}}{1-f}\right]+\frac{M_{1}}{(1-C\epsilon)(1-f)v(x^{\prime},t^{\prime})}\leq C.\end{split}

(2.31)

By the arbitrariness of
x_{*}
, one then has
\sup_{x,t}v(x,t)<\infty
so that

|\nabla f|\leq C(1-f+\log M)\leq C(1+|x|^{p_{1}}),

for some
p_{1}>0
as a consequence of Proposition
2.1
.

Second order derivative estimates.

Consider

\displaystyle w:=\frac{1}{q(x)}\frac{|\nabla^{2}f|^{2}}{A(1-f+\log M)^{2}-|\nabla f|^{2}}=\frac{1}{q}\frac{f_{k\ell}f_{k\ell}}{A(1-f+\log M)^{2}-f_{j}f_{j}},

(2.32)

\quad u\;:=\;\frac{|\nabla^{2}f|^{2}}{\,A(1-f+\log M)^{2}-|\nabla f|^{2}\,},\qquad w\;=\;\frac{u}{q(x)},

(2.33)

where
q(x)=1+|x|^{r}
for some
r\geq 0
to be determined such that
w
is bounded at
t=0
and some extra polynomial terms will be controlled. By the results of
v
above, we can choose
A
such that

\displaystyle\frac{A}{2}(1-f+\log M)^{2}\leq A(1-f+\log M)^{2}-|\nabla f|^{2}\leq A(1-f+\log M)^{2}.

(2.34)

Though
(1-f+\log M)^{2}
is comparable to
A(1-f+\log M)^{2}-|\nabla f|^{2}
, we do not use the former in the denominator
as we need the derivatives of
f_{k}f_{k}
to help to control certain terms which shall be clear in the proof. As before, without loss of generality, we can set again
M=1
and
\beta=1
.

Consider again the operator

\mathcal{L}u=u_{ii}+b^{i}u_{i}-u_{t}.

By direct computation (Appendix
B
provides the details), one has the following estimate

\tilde{\mathcal{L}}u:=\mathcal{L}u-4\frac{A(1-f)f_{i}+f_{k}f_{ki}}{A(1-f)^{2}-f_{j}f_{j}}u_{i}\geq\frac{f_{k\ell i}f_{k\ell i}}{A(1-f)^{2}-f_{j}f_{j}}+\frac{3}{2}u^{2}-Cq(x)-R_{2}(x),

where

R_{2}:=\frac{2f_{k\ell}(f_{i}b^{i}_{\ell k}+b^{i}_{\ell}f_{ik}+b^{i}_{k}f_{i\ell}+c_{kl})}{A(1-f)^{2}-f_{j}f_{j}}+\frac{2f_{k\ell}f_{k\ell}\left(A(1-f)c+f_{j}c_{j}\right)}{(A(1-f)^{2}-f_{j}f_{j})^{2}}.

According to Young’s inequality, one has

|R_{2}|\leq C(\|\partial^{2}c\|^{2}+\|\partial b\|^{2}+\|\partial^{2}b\|^{2})(u+v)+Cuv\leq C(1+u).

Then, one has

\tilde{\mathcal{L}}w=\frac{1}{q}\left(\mathcal{L}u-4\frac{A(1-f)f_{i}+f_{k}f_{ki}}{A(1-f)^{2}-f_{j}f_{j}}u_{i}\right)-2(\log q)_{i}w_{i}-[(\log q)_{i}^{2}+(\log q)_{ii}]w\\
+4\frac{A(1-f)f_{i}+f_{k}f_{ki}}{A(1-f)^{2}-f_{j}f_{j}}w(\log q)_{i}-b^{i}(\log q)_{i}w.

Note that the derivatives of
\log q
and

b^{i}(\log q)_{i}=\frac{r|x|^{r-2}(b\cdot x)}{1+|x|^{r}}

are bounded.
Moreover, according to Young’s inequality and the results of first-order derivative estimates, it follows that

\displaystyle\left|\frac{A(1-f)f_{i}+f_{k}f_{ki}}{A(1-f)^{2}-f_{j}f_{j}}\right|\leq C+\epsilon u.

(2.35)

Then, we obtain

\mathcal{L}w+[-4\frac{A(1-f)f_{i}+f_{k}f_{ki}}{A(1-f)^{2}-f_{j}f_{j}}+2(\log q)_{i}]w_{i}\geq(\frac{3}{2}-\epsilon)q(x)w^{2}-C-Cw-\frac{1}{q(x)}.

Next, fix again
x_{*}
and we again take the cutoff function
\phi=\psi(|x-x_{*}|)
as before. Then,

\tilde{\mathcal{L}}(\phi w)=\phi\tilde{\mathcal{L}}w+w\tilde{\mathcal{L}}\phi+2\phi_{i}\phi^{-1}(w\phi)_{i}-2\phi_{i}^{2}\phi^{-1}w.

By (
2.35
), it is easy to know that

w\tilde{\mathcal{L}}\phi\geq-w(C+\epsilon|\phi_{i}|^{2}u)\geq-\epsilon\phi q(x)w^{2}-Cw.

Hence, one has

\mathcal{L}(\phi w)+\left[-4\frac{A(1-f)f_{i}+f_{k}f_{ki}}{A(1-f)^{2}-f_{j}f_{j}}+2(\log q)_{i}\right](\phi w)_{i}\geq q(x)\phi w^{2}-Cw-C-\frac{1}{q(x)}.

Then, applying the maximum principle and repeating the same argument as for
v
, one obtains

w(t,x_{*})\leq\max\left\{\sup_{x\in B(x_{*},2(|x_{*}|+1))}\|w(0,x)\|,\quad C\right\}.

Hence,
w
is uniformly bounded and

\|\nabla^{2}f\|\leq\sqrt{Aq}(1-f+\log M)

according to (
2.34
). Applying Proposition
2.1
, the claim for the Hessian is then proved.
∎

3
Ergodicity of the random splitting LMC

For the convenience of the discussion in this section, we will abandon the time interpolation in (
2.23
), and regard the advection as a step that takes no time; that is, the diffusion is performed in
(t_{n}^{+},t_{n+1}^{-})
and the advection is performed at
\{t_{n}\}=:[t_{n}^{-},t_{n}^{+}]
. Then the random splitting LMC is then a random pulse-diffusion model.
Throughout this section, let
(\xi_{k})_{k\geq 1}
be the i.i.d. random splitting choices
and
(W_{t})_{t\geq 0}
the driving Brownian motion.
We let the process
X(t)
to experience a diffusion between
t_{n}^{+}<t<t_{n+1}^{-}
:

dX=\sqrt{2\beta^{-1}}\,dW.

If the diffusion happens first in the
n
th step, then

X(t_{n}^{+})=X_{n},\quad X_{n+1}=S(X(t_{n+1}^{-}),\tau).

Otherwise

X(t_{n}^{+})=S(X_{n},\tau),\quad X_{n+1}=X(t_{n+1}^{-}).

Consider a coupling copy of
X
, which we denote by
Y
.
Let
\theta
be the stopping time that
X=Y
. Clearly, if
X\neq Y
, then
S(X,\tau)\neq S(Y,\tau)
by the classical ODE
theory. Hence,
\theta
can only be in
(t_{n}^{+},t_{n+1}^{-})
for some
n
.
In the diffusion step, we let the dynamics of
Y
to be

\displaystyle dY=(I-2\hat{e}\otimes\hat{e})dW_{t\wedge\theta},\quad\hat{e}=\frac{X-Y}{|X-Y|},\quad t<\theta,

(3.1)

and

\displaystyle Y_{t}=X_{t},\quad t\geq\theta.

(3.2)

The dynamics of
Y
in the diffusion step is well-defined, as one can define the dynamics with stopping times
\theta_{j}
suitably

\theta_{j}=\inf_{t>t_{n}}\{|X(t)-Y(t)|=1/j\}
and then take
j\to\infty
to get the limit dynamics for the dynamics of
Y
(see
[
17
,
29
]
).

We define the discrete-time
information
\sigma
-algebra at step
n
by

\mathcal{G}_{n}:=\sigma\!\bigl(X_{0},Y_{0}\;(\xi_{k})_{k\leq n},\;(W_{s})_{s\leq t_{n}}\bigr),\qquad n\geq 0,

which collects all randomness available at time
t_{n}^{+}
.
Consequently, Brownian increments on
(t_{n}^{+},t]
are independent of
\mathcal{G}_{n}
.
We also write

Z_{t}:=X_{t}-Y_{t}

be the difference of the two processes,
Z_{n}:=Z(t_{n})
, and
Z_{n}^{\pm}:=Z(t_{n}^{\pm})
.

Then, one has

dZ=2\sqrt{2\beta^{-1}}d\zeta_{t},\quad\zeta_{t}=\int_{t_{n}\wedge\theta}^{t\wedge\theta}\frac{Z_{s}^{\otimes 2}}{|Z_{s}|^{2}}dW.

As in
[
17
,
18
,
29
]
.
We consider the function

\displaystyle f(r)=\int_{0}^{r}e^{-c_{f}(s\wedge R_{1})}\,ds,\quad R_{1}=3R.

(3.3)

The main result in this section is the following.

Theorem 3.1
.

There exists some constants
A_{0}>0
and
\tau_{0}>0
. When
c_{f}>A_{0}\beta LR_{1}
and
\tau\leq\tau_{0}
, there is some constant
\lambda>0
independent of
\tau
such that

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}f(|Z_{n+1}|)\leq e^{-\lambda\tau}\mathbb{E}f(|Z_{n}|).

(3.4)

Consequently, let
\mu_{n}
and
\nu_{n}
be the laws of the random splitting LMC with initial laws
\mu_{0}
and
\nu_{0}
, respectively. Then, it holds that

W_{1}(\mu_{n},\nu_{n})\leq Ce^{-\lambda n\tau}W_{1}(\mu_{0},\nu_{0}),

for some constant
C>0
independent of
\tau
and
n
.

To prove this theorem, we need some auxiliary lemmas. The first is the following.

Lemma 3.1
.

It holds that

\mathbb{P}(|\zeta_{t}-\zeta_{s}|\geq u)\leq 2\exp\left(-\frac{C\beta u^{2}}{\tau}\right).

This essentially is the sub-Gaussian tail bound for diffusion process, which can be derived by computing the moments using BDG inequality. See
[
29
]
for the proof.

Lemma 3.2
.

For the diffusion step, when
t_{n}^{+}<t<t_{n+1}^{-}
, one has

\frac{d}{dt}\mathbb{E}f(|Z(t)|)=4\mathbb{E}\beta^{-1}f^{\prime\prime}(|Z|)1_{t\leq\theta}=-4\beta^{-1}c_{f}\mathbb{E}e^{-c_{f}|Z|\wedge R_{1}}1_{|Z|\leq R_{1}}1_{t\leq\theta}.

This is a direct proof by Itô’s formula, by approximating
\theta
using a sequence of stopping times
\theta_{j}
. Direct computation gives

\begin{split}&\nabla f(|x|)=f^{\prime}(|x|)\frac{x}{|x|},\\
&\nabla^{2}f(|x|)=f^{\prime\prime}(|x|)\frac{x\otimes x}{|x|^{2}}+f^{\prime}(|x|)\frac{1}{|x|}(I-\frac{x\otimes x}{|x|^{2}}).\end{split}

Since
d\zeta_{t}
contains only the martingale term while the differential of the quadratic variation
d[\zeta_{t},\zeta_{t}]
is parallel
to
x
, one can obtain the result. We skip the details and refer the readers to
[
16
,
19
,
7
]
and
[
29
]
for details.

The following lemma is similar to
[
29
, Lemma 3.4]
.

Lemma 3.3
.

Let
0<a<b\leq R_{1}
and
\beta Ca^{2}/\tau>4\log 8
.
Suppose that
G\in\mathcal{G}_{n}
is an event such that
|Z_{n}|\leq a
on
G
.
Define
F=\{\exists s\in[t_{n}^{+},t]:|Z_{s}|=b\}
. Then, for all
t\in[t_{n}^{+},t_{n+1}^{-}]
,

\mathbb{E}\!\big[\mathbf{1}_{G}\mathbf{1}_{F}\mathbf{1}_{\{t<\theta\}}\big]\,\leq\,\eta_{1}(a,b,\tau)\,\mathbb{E}\!\big[\mathbf{1}_{G}\mathbf{1}_{\{t<\theta\}}\big],\quad\eta_{1}=4\exp\!\Big(-\tfrac{C\beta(b-a)^{2}}{\tau}\Big).

Moreover,

\mathbb{E}\!\big[\mathbf{1}_{G}|Z_{t}|\mathbf{1}_{\{|Z_{t}|\geq b\}}\big]\,\leq\,\eta_{2}(a,b,\tau)\,\mathbb{E}\!\big[\mathbf{1}_{G}|Z_{t}|\big],

with

\eta_{2}=\frac{6}{a}\left[b+\frac{\tau}{C\beta(b-a)}\right]\exp\!\Big(-\tfrac{C\beta(b-a)^{2}}{2\tau}\Big).

Proof.

Define the event

E=\{\exists s\in[t_{n}^{+},t],|Z_{s}|=a\}\cap G,\quad B=\{t<\theta\}.

(i). We consider the first claim. Clearly, the event
F
must be contained in
E
. We will actually show that

\mathbb{E}[1_{G\cap E}1_{F}1_{t<\theta}]\leq\eta_{1}(a,b,\tau)\mathbb{E}[1_{G\cap E}1_{t<\theta}].

The idea is that once
E
occurs, the probability of coupling before time
t
is exponentially small in
a^{2}/\tau
, so
\{t<\theta\}
holds with large probability. In fact,

\mathbb{P}(G\cap E,F,t<\theta)=\mathbb{P}(G\cap E,t<\theta)\mathbb{P}(F|G\cap E,t<\theta).

For the latter,

\mathbb{P}(F|G\cap E,t<\theta)\leq\frac{\mathbb{P}(F,G\cap E)}{\mathbb{P}(G\cap E)-\mathbb{P}(G\cap E,\theta\leq t)}.

Meanwhile,

\mathbb{P}(G,E,t\geq\theta)=\mathbb{P}(G,E)\int_{t_{n}}^{t}\mathbb{P}(t\geq\theta||Z_{s}|=a,G)\nu_{G,E}(ds),

where
\nu_{G,E}(\cdot)
is the conditional law for the first hitting time of
a
for
|Z|
with
\int_{t_{n}}^{t}\nu_{G,E}(ds)=1
.
By Lemma
3.1
, we obtain

\mathbb{P}(t\geq\theta||Z_{s}|=a,G)\leq\mathbb{P}(\sup_{s\leq t^{\prime}\leq t\wedge\theta}2\sqrt{2\beta^{-1}}|\zeta_{t^{\prime}}-\zeta_{s}|\geq a||Z_{s}|=a,G)\leq 2\exp(-\frac{C\beta a^{2}}{\tau})<\frac{2}{8^{4}}.

Hence, one has

\mathbb{P}(F|G\cap E,t<\theta)\leq 2\mathbb{P}(F|G\cap E)\leq 4\exp(-\frac{C\beta(b-a)^{2}}{\eta})=:\eta_{1}.

(ii) The proof of the second claim uses similar ideas but more involved.
Note that
\{|Z_{t}|>b\}
must be contained in
E
. We will then actually show that

\mathbb{E}[1_{G\cap E}|Z_{t}|1_{|Z_{t}|\geq b}]\leq\eta_{2}(a,b,\tau)\mathbb{E}[1_{G\cap E}|Z_{t}|].

Clearly, one has the following two expressions.

\displaystyle\begin{split}&\mathbb{E}[1_{G\cap E}|Z_{t}|1_{|Z_{t}|\geq b}]=b\mathbb{P}(|Z_{t}|\geq b,G,E)+\int_{b}^{\infty}\mathbb{P}(|Z_{t}|\geq r,G,E)dr,\\
&\mathbb{E}[1_{G\cap E}|Z_{t}|]=\int_{0}^{\infty}\mathbb{P}(|Z_{t}|\geq r,G,E)dr.\end{split}

Hence, we need to show that

b\mathbb{P}(|Z_{t}|\geq b|G,E)+\int_{b}^{\infty}\mathbb{P}(|Z_{t}|\geq r|G,E)dr\leq\eta_{2}\int_{0}^{\infty}\mathbb{P}(|Z_{t}|\geq r|G,E)dr.

Intuitively,
\mathbb{P}(|Z_{t}|\geq r|G,E)
is small for
r\geq b
and almost
1
if
r\leq a/2
.
In fact

\mathbb{P}(|Z_{t}|\geq r|G,E)=\int_{t_{n}}^{t}\mathbb{P}(|Z_{t}|\geq r||Z_{s}|=a,G,E)\nu_{G,E}(ds).

For
r\geq b
, one has

\mathbb{P}(|Z_{t}|\geq r|E,G)\leq\sup_{s}\mathbb{P}(\sup_{s\leq t^{\prime}\leq t\wedge\theta}2\sqrt{2\beta^{-1}}|\zeta_{t^{\prime}}-\zeta_{s}|\geq r-a||Z_{s}|=a,G)\leq 2\exp(-C\beta(r-a)^{2}/\tau).

Similarly, for
r\leq a/2
, one has
\mathbb{P}(|Z_{t}|>r|G,E,1_{t<\theta})=1-\mathbb{P}(|Z_{t}|\leq r|G,E,1_{t<\theta})
. Similar as above,

\mathbb{P}(|Z_{t}|\leq r|G,E,1_{t<\theta})\leq\frac{1}{1-2\exp(-C\beta a^{2}/4\tau)}\mathbb{P}(|Z_{t}|\leq r|G,E),

while

\mathbb{P}(|Z_{t}|\leq r|G,E)\leq 2\exp(-C\beta a^{2}/(4\tau)).

Thus, with high probability the process remains above
a/2
, which provides a positive lower bound for the denominator.
With all the estimates here, it suffices the following holds

\left[2b+\frac{2\tau}{C\beta(b-a)}\right]\exp\left(-\frac{C\beta}{2\tau}(b-a)^{2}\right)\leq\eta_{2}(a,b,\tau)\frac{a}{2}\frac{1-4\exp(-C\beta a^{2}/(4\tau))}{1-2\exp(-C\beta a^{2}/(4\tau))}.

Hence, taking

\eta_{2}(a,b,\tau)=\frac{2}{a}\frac{1-2/8}{1-4/8}\left[2b+\frac{2\tau}{C\beta(b-a)}\right]\exp\left(-\frac{C\beta}{2\tau}(b-a)^{2}\right)

suffices.
∎

We also have the following.

Lemma 3.4
.

Let
a>0
satisfy
2\exp(-C\beta a^{2}/\tau)<1
. Suppose that
G\in\mathcal{G}_{n}
is an event such that
2a<|Z_{n}|\leq b
for some
b>0
on
G
. Define
F=\{\exists s\in[t_{n}^{+},t],|Z_{s}-Z_{n}^{+}|=a\}
. Then, one has

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[1_{G}1_{F}f(|Z_{t}|)]\leq\eta_{3}(2a,b,\tau)\mathbb{E}[1_{G}f(|Z_{t}|)],

(3.5)

where

\eta_{3}(2a,b,\tau)=\frac{2(2b+\eta_{2}(b,2b,\tau))\exp(-C\beta a^{2}/\tau)}{f(a)(1-2\exp(-C\beta a^{2}/\tau))}.

The proof of Lemma
3.4
is almost the same as the proof of Lemma
3.3
. The estimates needed include: (1). The left hand side is controlled by

\mathbb{E}[1_{G}1_{F}|Z_{t}|]\leq[2b+\eta_{2}(b,2b,\tau)]\mathbb{P}(G,F)\leq 2[2b+\eta_{2}(b,2b,\tau)]\exp(-C\beta a^{2}/\tau)\mathbb{P}(G).

(2). The expectation on the right hand side is bounded below by

\mathbb{E}[1_{G}f(|Z_{t}|)]\geq f(a)\mathbb{P}(G)[1-\mathbb{P}(|Z_{t}|\leq a|G)]\geq f(a)\mathbb{P}(G)(1-2\exp(-C\beta a^{2}/\tau)).

We omit the details for the proof. Next, we give the proof of the main theorem.

Proof of Theorem
3.1
.

Recall that
R_{1}=3R
. We decompose the whole probability space into three parts.

\Omega_{1}=\{|Z_{n}|<\tau^{1/2-\delta}\},\quad\Omega_{2}=\{\tau^{1/2-\delta}\leq|Z_{n}|<2R\},\quad\Omega_{3}=\{|Z_{n}|\geq 2R\}.

We discuss the three cases separately. In
\Omega_{3}
, the advection step will contribute the contraction due to the convexity. When
|Z_{n}|\leq 2R
, the main idea is that the reflection coupling together with the concavity of
f
gives contraction with coefficient
c_{f}
, which is large to dominate the advection step.

Case 1

We consider
\Omega_{1}
. The main difficulty is that the probability of
1_{t\leq\theta}
may not be close to
1
.
The observation is that
f(|Z|)1_{t\leq\theta}=f(|Z|)
and
|Z|1_{t\leq\theta}=|Z|
. Moreover,
e^{-c_{f}|Z|\wedge R_{1}}
is close to
1

so that
|Z|
and
f(|Z|)
are almost the same.

Case 1.1

If the diffusion step is first, then
X(t_{n}^{+})=X(t_{n})
and
Y(t_{n}^{+})=Y(t_{n})
. We denote

F_{1}(t)=\{\exists s\in[t_{n},t]:|Z_{s}|=2\tau^{1/2-\delta}\}.

On the event
F_{1}(t)^{c}
,
|Z_{t}|\leq R_{1}
clearly holds. Then, since
\exp(-2c_{f}\tau^{1/2-\delta})\geq 3/4
, one has

\displaystyle\frac{d}{dt}\mathbb{E}1_{\Omega_{1}}f(|Z_{t}|)\leq-3\beta^{-1}c_{f}\mathbb{E}1_{\Omega_{1}\cap F_{1}(t)^{c}}1_{t<\theta}.

By the first claim in Lemma
3.3
, one has

\mathbb{E}1_{\Omega_{1}\cap F_{1}(t)^{c}}1_{t<\theta}\geq(1-\eta_{1}(\tau^{1/2-\delta},2\tau^{1/2-\delta},\tau))\mathbb{E}1_{\Omega_{1}}1_{t<\theta}.

Hence, for
\tau
small enough, one has

\displaystyle\frac{d}{dt}\mathbb{E}1_{\Omega_{1}}f(|Z_{t}|)\leq-2\beta^{-1}c_{f}\mathbb{E}1_{\Omega_{1}}1_{t<\theta}\leq-\beta^{-1}c_{f}\mathbb{E}1_{\Omega_{1}}1_{t<\theta}-\beta^{-1}c_{f}\mathbb{E}1_{\Omega_{1}}1_{t_{n+1}<\theta}.

We will use the latter to control the contribution from the subsequent advection step.
In fact, for the advection step, letting
\bar{X}_{s}:=S(X_{n+1}^{-},s)
,
\bar{Y}_{s}:=S(Y_{n+1}^{-},s)
and
W_{s}=\bar{X}_{s}-\bar{Y}_{s}
, one has

\mathbb{E}1_{\Omega_{1}}f(|Z_{n+1}|)-\mathbb{E}1_{\Omega_{1}}f(|Z_{n+1}^{-}|)\leq L\int_{0}^{\tau}\mathbb{E}1_{\Omega_{1}}1_{\theta>t_{n+1}}f^{\prime}(|W_{s}|)|W_{s}|\leq C\tau L\mathbb{E}1_{\Omega_{1}}|Z_{n+1}^{-}|,

where we have used
1_{t_{n+1}<\theta}|Z_{n+1}^{-}|=|Z_{n+1}^{-}|
. By the second claim in Lemma
3.3
, one has

\mathbb{E}1_{\Omega_{1}}|Z_{n+1}^{-}|\leq(1+\eta_{2}(\tau^{1/2-\delta},2\tau^{1/2-\delta},\tau))\mathbb{E}1_{\Omega_{1}}|Z_{n+1}^{-}|1_{|Z_{n+1}^{-}|\leq 2\tau^{1/2-\delta}}\\
\leq 2\tau^{1/2-\delta}(1+\eta_{2}(\tau^{1/2-\delta},2\tau^{1/2-\delta},\tau))\mathbb{E}1_{\theta>t_{n+1}}1_{\Omega_{1}}.

The number
\eta_{2}
is small if
\tau
is small. Hence, one can choose
c_{f}
large such that

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}1_{\Omega_{1}}f(|Z_{n+1}|)\leq\mathbb{E}1_{\Omega_{1}}f(|Z_{n}|)-\beta^{-1}c_{f}\int_{t_{n}^{+}}^{t_{n+1}^{-}}\mathbb{E}1_{\Omega_{1}}1_{t<\theta}.

Let
u(t):=\mathbb{E}1_{\Omega_{1}}f(|Z_{s}|)
. By the estimate for the diffusion step, for
t<t_{n+1}
, one has

u(t)\leq u(t_{n})-\beta^{-1}c_{f}\int_{t_{n}^{+}}^{t}\mathbb{E}1_{\Omega_{1}}1_{t<\theta}\leq u(t_{n})-\frac{\beta^{-1}c_{f}}{2\tau^{1/2-\delta}}\int_{t_{n}}^{t_{n+1}^{-}}\mathbb{E}1_{\Omega_{1}}|Z_{t}|1_{|Z_{t}|\leq 2\tau^{1/2-\delta}}\\
\leq u(t_{n})-\frac{\beta^{-1}c_{f}}{2\tau^{1/2-\delta}}(1-\eta_{2})\int_{t_{n}}^{t}u(s)\,ds,

where have used the fact
|Z_{t}|1_{t<\theta}=|Z_{t}|
here.

To apply the Grönwall’s lemma, the problem is that

\mathbb{E}1_{\Omega_{1}}f(|Z_{n+1}|)\neq u(t_{n+1}^{-})
. To resolve this, define
b(t):=\max\{\mathbb{E}1_{\Omega_{1}}f(|Z_{n+1}|),u(t)\}
.
Since
\mathbb{E}1_{\Omega_{1}}f(|Z_{n+1}|)\leq u(t_{n})
by the estimate above, one concludes that
b
is continuous and
b(t_{n})=u(t_{n})
.
It is clear that
u(t)
is monotonically decreasing and
\mathbb{E}1_{\Omega_{1}}f(|Z_{n+1}|)\leq Cu(t_{n+1}^{-})
since the advection step amplify
|Z|

by a small factor. Hence, one has
u(s)\geq C^{-1}b(s)
for some constant. Then,

b(t)\leq b(t_{n})-c\int_{t_{n}}^{t}b(s)\,ds.

By applying Grönwall’s lemma to the function
b(t)
, we conclude that

\mathbb{E}1_{\Omega_{1}}f(|Z_{n+1}|)\leq e^{-c\tau}\mathbb{E}1_{\Omega_{1}}f(|Z_{n}|).

Case 1.2
If the advection step is first, after the advection,
|Z_{n}^{+}|\leq e^{CL\tau}|Z_{n}|
.
Moreover,
f^{\prime}(|Z|)\geq 3/4
. Hence,
u(t_{n}^{+})\leq e^{C_{1}L\tau}u(t_{n})
.
The diffusion step is estimated similarly as above, and one has

u(t)\leq u(t_{n}^{+})-c\int_{t_{n}}^{t}u(s)\,ds

where
c=\frac{\beta^{-1}c_{f}}{2\tau^{1/2-\delta}}(1-\eta_{2})
which is greater than
2C_{1}L
if
\tau
is small.
Combining the two cases here, one gets

\mathbb{E}1_{\Omega_{1}}f(|Z_{n+1}|)\leq e^{-c^{\prime}\tau}\mathbb{E}1_{\Omega_{1}}f(|Z_{n}|).

Case 2
On this event, the good thing is that
\theta\leq t
will almost not happen. However,
e^{-c_{f}|Z_{t}|}
is no longer close to
1
. Since
c_{f}
is large, we cannot
bound this by
e^{-c_{f}2R}
from below here. To address this, we define

\mu_{m}:=m\tau^{1/2-\delta}

and decompose
\Omega_{2}
into the union of the following sets

\Omega_{2,m}:=\{\mu_{m}\leq|Z_{n}|<\mu_{m+1}\},\quad m=1,2,\cdots,\lceil 2R/\tau^{1/2-\delta}\rceil-1.

During the diffusion step, one has

\frac{d}{dt}\mathbb{E}1_{\Omega_{2,m}}f(|Z(t)|)=-4\beta^{-1}c_{f}\mathbb{E}1_{\Omega_{2,m}}e^{-c_{f}|Z_{t}|}1_{|Z_{t}|\leq R_{1}}1_{t\leq\theta}.

Using the fact that
e^{-CL\tau}\mu_{m}\leq|Z_{n}|\leq\mu_{m+1}e^{CL\tau}\leq 2Re^{CL\tau}
(if there is no advection step before the diffusion, the constants here are still correct), one has

\mathbb{E}[1_{\Omega_{2,m}}e^{-c_{f}|Z_{t}|}1_{|Z_{t}|\leq R_{1}}1_{t\leq\theta}]\geq e^{-c_{f}\mu_{m+1}e^{CL\tau}}\mathbb{E}[1_{\Omega_{2,m}}e^{-c_{f}|Z_{t}-Z_{n}^{+}|}1_{|Z_{t}|\leq R_{1}}1_{t\leq\theta}]\\
\geq\frac{1}{2}\frac{e^{-c_{f}\mu_{m}}}{f(R_{1})}\mathbb{E}[1_{\Omega_{2,m}}1_{|Z_{t}-Z_{n}^{+}|<a}f(|Z_{t}|)].

Clearly, if
\tau
is small enough, applying Lemma
3.4
for
a=3\tau^{1/2-\delta}/8
, one has

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}1_{\Omega_{2,m}}e^{-c_{f}|Z_{t}|}1_{|Z_{t}|\leq R_{1}}1_{t\leq\theta}\geq\frac{1}{4f(R_{1})}e^{-c_{f}\mu_{m}}\mathbb{E}[1_{\Omega_{2,m}}f(|Z_{t}|)].

If the diffusion step is first, we define
W_{s}
similarly as in Case 1. Then one has

\mathbb{E}1_{\Omega_{2,m}}f(|Z_{n+1}|)-\mathbb{E}1_{\Omega_{2,m}}f(|Z_{n+1}^{-}|)\leq L\int_{0}^{\tau}\mathbb{E}e^{-c_{f}|W_{s}|\wedge R_{1}}1_{t_{n+1}<\theta}|W_{s}|1_{\Omega_{2,m}}

It is clear that
\partial_{s}|W_{s}-Z_{n+1}^{-}|\leq L|W_{s}|
so that
|W_{s}-Z_{n+1}^{-}|\leq|Z_{n+1}^{-}|(e^{L\tau}-1)
and
|W_{s}|\leq e^{L\tau}|Z_{n+1}^{-}|
. Note that
|Z_{n+1}^{-}|1_{t_{n+1}<\theta}=|Z_{n+1}^{-}|
. Then, one has for
\tau\leq\tau_{0}
(where
\tau_{0}
depends on
c_{f},R_{1},L
etc)

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}e^{-c_{f}|W_{s}|\wedge R_{1}}1_{t_{n+1}<\theta}|W_{s}|1_{\Omega_{2,m}}\leq 2\mathbb{E}e^{-c_{f}|Z_{n+1}^{-}|\wedge R_{1}}|Z_{n+1}^{-}|1_{\Omega_{2,m}},

By the second claim in Lemma
3.3
,

\mathbb{E}e^{-c_{f}|Z_{n+1}^{-}|\wedge R_{1}}|Z_{n+1}^{-}|1_{\Omega_{2,m}}\leq(1+e^{-c_{f}R_{1}}\eta_{2}(\mu_{m+1},R_{1},\tau))\mathbb{E}e^{-c_{f}|Z_{n+1}^{-}|}|Z_{n+1}^{-}|1_{|Z_{n+1}^{-}|\leq R_{1}}1_{\Omega_{2,m}}.

Since
r/f(r)
is an increasing function on
(0,\infty)
,

\mathbb{E}e^{-c_{f}|Z_{n+1}^{-}|}|Z_{n+1}^{-}|1_{|Z_{n+1}^{-}|\leq R_{1}}1_{\Omega_{2,m}}\leq\frac{R_{1}}{f(R_{1})}\mathbb{E}e^{-c_{f}|Z_{n+1}^{-}|}f(|Z_{n+1}^{-}|)1_{|Z_{n+1}^{-}|\leq R_{1}}1_{\Omega_{2,m}}.

Applying Lemma
3.4
again,

\displaystyle\begin{split}&\mathbb{E}e^{-c_{f}|Z_{n+1}^{-}|}f(|Z_{n+1}^{-}|)1_{|Z_{n+1}^{-}|\leq R_{1}}1_{\Omega_{2,m}}\\
&\leq\eta_{3}\mathbb{E}f(|Z_{n+1}^{-}|)1_{\Omega_{2,m}}+\mathbb{E}[e^{-c_{f}|Z_{n+1}^{-}|}f(|Z_{n+1}^{-}|)1_{|Z_{n+1}^{-}-Z_{n}|<a}1_{\Omega_{2,m}}]\\
&\leq\eta_{3}\mathbb{E}f(|Z_{n+1}^{-}|)1_{\Omega_{2,m}}+2e^{-c_{f}\mu_{m}}\mathbb{E}[f(|Z_{n+1}^{-}|)1_{|Z_{n+1}^{-}-Z_{n}|<a}1_{\Omega_{2,m}}]\\
&\leq(\eta_{3}+2e^{-c_{f}\mu_{m}})\mathbb{E}f(|Z_{n+1}^{-}|)1_{\Omega_{2,m}}.\end{split}

Clearly, when
\tau
is small enough, we have
\eta_{3}\leq e^{-c_{f}\mu_{m}}
as
\eta_{3}
is exponentially small.

Hence one has

\mathbb{E}1_{\Omega_{2,m}}f(|Z_{n+1}|)\leq\left[1+A_{0}L\frac{R_{1}}{f(R_{1})}e^{-c_{f}\mu_{m}}\tau\right]\mathbb{E}[f(|Z_{n+1}^{-}|)1_{\Omega_{2,m}}]\\
\leq\left[1+A_{0}L\frac{R_{1}}{f(R_{1})}e^{-c_{f}\mu_{m}}\tau\right]\exp\left(-\beta^{-1}\frac{c_{f}}{f(R_{1})}e^{-c_{f}\mu_{m}}\tau\right)\mathbb{E}1_{\Omega_{2,m}}f(|Z_{n}|).

Note that the constant
A_{0}
here is independent of
c_{f},R_{1},L,\mu_{m}
.

If the advection step is before the diffusion step, obtaining such an estimate is similar and is slightly easier.

Here the key point is that the multiplicative factor
is strictly less than
e^{-c\tau}
for some
c>0
independent of
\tau
, if
c_{f}>A_{0}\beta LR_{1}
. Hence, choosing such
c_{f}
, one gets the contraction to obtain

\mathbb{E}1_{\Omega_{2,m}}f(|Z_{n+1}|)\leq e^{-c\tau}\mathbb{E}1_{\Omega_{2,m}}f(|Z_{n}|).

Case 3

We consider the case for
\Omega_{3}
, which is much easier as the advection step will yield the contraction. If the advection is first , the contraction is clear in the advection step since

Z_{s}\cdot(b(X_{s})-b(Y_{s}))\leq-\lambda|Z_{s}|^{2},

while
\mathbb{E}1_{\Omega_{3}}f(|Z_{t}|)

is nonincreasing in the diffusion step, one easily has

\mathbb{E}1_{\Omega_{3}}f(|Z_{n+1}|)\leq\mathbb{E}1_{\Omega_{3}}f(|Z_{n+1}^{-}|)\leq e^{-c\tau}\mathbb{E}f(|Z_{n}|).

If the diffusion step is first, then
\mathbb{E}1_{\Omega_{3}}f(|Z_{t}|)
is nonincreasing in the diffusion step. In the advection step

\mathbb{E}1_{\Omega_{3}}f(|Z_{n+1}|)=\mathbb{E}1_{\Omega_{3}}f(|Z_{n+1}|)1_{|Z_{n+1}^{-}|<\frac{3}{2}R}+\mathbb{E}1_{\Omega_{3}}f(|Z_{n+1}|)1_{|Z_{n+1}^{-}|\geq\frac{3}{2}R}.

By the contraction due to the convexity, one has

\mathbb{E}1_{\Omega_{3}}f(|Z_{n+1}|)1_{|Z_{n+1}^{-}|\geq\frac{3}{2}R}\leq e^{-c_{1}\tau}\mathbb{E}1_{\Omega_{3}}f(|Z_{n+1}^{-}|)1_{|Z_{n+1}^{-}|\geq\frac{3}{2}R}

while

\mathbb{E}1_{\Omega_{3}}f(|Z_{n+1}|)1_{|Z_{n+1}^{-}|<\frac{3}{2}R}\leq\exp(C\tau)\mathbb{E}1_{\Omega_{3}}f(|Z_{n+1}^{-}|)1_{|Z_{n+1}^{-}|<\frac{3}{2}R}.

Since
|Z_{n}|>2R
, we apply Lemma
3.4
again with
a=R/2
and obtain

\mathbb{E}1_{\Omega_{3}}f(|Z_{n+1}^{-}|)1_{|Z_{n+1}^{-}|<\frac{3}{2}R}\leq\eta_{3}\mathbb{E}1_{\Omega_{3}}f(|Z_{n+1}^{-}|).

Hence

\begin{split}\mathbb{E}1_{\Omega_{3}}f(|Z_{n+1}|)&\leq(e^{-c_{1}\tau}+(e^{C\tau}-e^{-c_{1}\tau})\eta_{3})\mathbb{E}1_{\Omega_{3}}f(|Z_{n+1}^{-}|)\\
&\leq(e^{-c_{1}\tau}+(e^{C\tau}-e^{-c_{1}\tau})\eta_{3})\mathbb{E}1_{\Omega_{3}}f(|Z_{n}|).\end{split}

When
\tau
is small,
\eta_{3}
is exponentially small so that

e^{-c_{1}\tau}+(e^{C\tau}-e^{-c_{1}\tau})\eta_{3}\leq e^{-c\tau}

for some
c
independent of
\tau
, and thus

\mathbb{E}1_{\Omega_{3}}f(|Z_{n+1}|)\leq e^{-c\tau}\mathbb{E}1_{\Omega_{3}}f(|Z_{n}|).

Summing over all disjoint events
\Omega_{1},\Omega_{2},\Omega_{3}
, we obtain

\mathbb{E}f(|Z_{n+1}|)=\sum_{i=1}^{3}\mathbb{E}1_{\Omega_{i}}f(|Z_{n+1}|)\leq e^{-c\tau}\sum_{i=1}^{3}\mathbb{E}1_{\Omega_{i}}f(|Z_{n}|)=e^{-c\tau}\mathbb{E}f(|Z_{n}|).

This proves the main inequality in the theorem.

We take the coupling measure
\Gamma
for
(X,Y)

such that

\mathbb{E}_{\Gamma}|X_{0}-Y_{0}|=W_{1}(\mu_{0},\nu_{0}),

and the coupling later is propagated by the reflection coupling
of the Brownian motions along the random splitting dynamics.
Let
\mu_{n}^{\xi}
and
\nu_{n}^{\xi}
be the laws of
X

and
Y
respectively for the random splitting LMC with given sequence of
\xi
.
By definition of the Wasserstein distance,

W_{1}(\mu_{n}^{\xi},\nu_{n}^{\xi})\leq\mathbb{E}_{\Gamma}|X_{n}-Y_{n}|=\mathbb{E}|Z_{n}|.

Since
f(r)=e^{-c_{f}(r\wedge R_{1})}r
is equivalent to
r
(there exist
c_{1},c_{2}>0
such that
c_{1}r\leq f(r)\leq c_{2}r
for all
r
), we have

\mathbb{E}|Z_{n}|\leq C\mathbb{E}f(|Z_{n}|).

Applying the contraction inequality for
\mathbb{E}f(|Z_{n}|)
yields

W_{1}(\mu_{n}^{\xi},\nu_{n}^{\xi})\leq Ce^{-\lambda n\tau}W_{1}(\mu_{0},\nu_{0}).

Since
\mu_{n}=\mathbb{E}_{\xi}\mu_{n}^{\xi}
and
\nu_{n}=\mathbb{E}_{\xi}\nu_{n}^{\xi}
,
the joint convexity of
W_{1}
(which follows immediately from its
Kantorovich–Rubinstein dual formulation, see Villani
[
58
, Particular Case 5.16]
) yields

W_{1}(\mu_{n},\nu_{n})\;\leq\;\mathbb{E}_{\xi}\,W_{1}(\mu_{n}^{\xi},\nu_{n}^{\xi}).

∎

4
Finite time error estimate

This section presents a systematic error analysis for the random splitting LMC. Through the pointwise bounds of the gradient and Hessian for the logarithmic density, we establish precise control of the temporal evolution for the relative entropy of the density with respect to the exact solution of the Fokker-Planck equation. Our analysis yields a fourth-order relative entropy bounds with explicit
T
-dependence, which is then extended to uniform-in-time control via the ergodicity properties.

4.1
Finite time error bound in relative entropy

We will get back to the time interpolation in (
2.23
).
Let us compute

E(t):=\mathcal{H}(\bar{\rho}(t)\parallel\rho(t)),

where
\rho(t)
is the solution of the Fokker-Planck equation for the original SDE. To compute the derivative, one may need the evolution of
\rho^{\xi_{n}}(t)
defined in (
2.23
).
We can compute that

\partial_{t}\rho^{\xi_{n}}=\mathcal{L}_{\xi(1)}\rho^{\xi}+e^{(t-n\tau)\mathcal{L}_{\xi(1)}}\mathcal{L}_{\xi(2)}e^{(t-n\tau)\mathcal{L}_{\xi(2)}}\bar{\rho}_{n}\\
=\mathcal{L}\rho^{\xi_{n}}+[e^{(t-n\tau)\mathcal{L}_{\xi(1)}},\mathcal{L}_{\xi(2)}]e^{(t-n\tau)\mathcal{L}_{\xi(2)}}\bar{\rho}_{n}.

(4.1)

Here, the commutator
[e^{(t-n\tau)\mathcal{L}_{\xi(1)}},\mathcal{L}_{\xi(2)}]
is defined as:

[e^{(t-n\tau)\mathcal{L}_{\xi(1)}},\mathcal{L}_{\xi(2)}]:=e^{(t-n\tau)\mathcal{L}_{\xi(1)}}\mathcal{L}_{\xi(2)}-\mathcal{L}_{\xi(2)}e^{(t-n\tau)\mathcal{L}_{\xi(1)}}.

This operator quantifies the non-commutativity between the evolution semigroup
e^{(t-n\tau)\mathcal{L}_{\xi(1)}}
and the generator
\mathcal{L}_{\xi(2)}
in our splitting scheme.
The remainder term is of order
O(\tau)
. If we consider the expectation, we have

\partial_{t}\bar{\rho}(t)=\mathcal{L}\bar{\rho}+\mathbb{E}_{\xi_{n}}[e^{(t-n\tau)\mathcal{L}_{\xi(1)}},\mathcal{L}_{\xi(2)}]e^{(t-n\tau)\mathcal{L}_{2}}\bar{\rho}_{n}.

(4.2)

We expect the remainder to reduce to
O(\tau^{2})
after expectation as the leading term in bias cancels. Of course, we need to justify this rigorously later to close the estimates.

Compute the formula for

\frac{d}{dt}\mathcal{H}(\bar{\rho}(t)\parallel\rho(t))=\int(1+\log\bar{\rho}-\log\rho(t))\partial_{t}\bar{\rho}-\frac{\bar{\rho}}{\rho(t)}\partial_{t}\rho(t).

Using (
1.1
), (
4.2
) (recalling
b=-\nabla U
), and integration by parts, it is then reduced to

\beta^{-1}\int(-\nabla\log\frac{\bar{\rho}}{\rho}\cdot\nabla\bar{\rho}+\bar{\rho}\nabla\log\frac{\bar{\rho}}{\rho}\cdot\nabla\log\rho)+\int(\log\frac{\bar{\rho}}{\rho})\mathbb{E}_{\xi}[e^{(t-n\tau)\mathcal{L}_{\xi(1)}},\mathcal{L}_{\xi(2)}]e^{(t-n\tau)\mathcal{L}_{2}}\bar{\rho}_{n}.

Simplifying this leads to

\frac{d}{dt}\mathcal{H}(\bar{\rho}(t)\parallel\rho(t))=-\beta^{-1}\int\bar{\rho}|\nabla\log\frac{\bar{\rho}}{\rho}|^{2}\,dx+r_{t},

(4.3)

where

r_{t}=\int(\log\frac{\bar{\rho}}{\rho})\mathbb{E}_{\xi}[e^{(t-n\tau)\mathcal{L}_{\xi(1)}},\mathcal{L}_{\xi(2)}]e^{(t-n\tau)\mathcal{L}_{2}}\bar{\rho}_{n}.

Next, our main goal is to estimate the remainder term. We first derive the formulas of the remainder term.
To proceed, we denote

s:=t-n\tau.

Then,

2\mathbb{E}_{\xi}[e^{s\mathcal{L}_{\xi(1)}},\mathcal{L}_{\xi(2)}]e^{s\mathcal{L}_{\xi(2)}}\bar{\rho}_{n}=(e^{s\mathcal{L}_{1}}\mathcal{L}_{2}-\mathcal{L}_{2}e^{s\mathcal{L}_{1}})h_{2}(s,x)+(e^{s\mathcal{L}_{2}}\mathcal{L}_{1}-\mathcal{L}_{1}e^{s\mathcal{L}_{2}})h_{1}(s,x)\\
=:I_{1}+I_{2},

(4.4)

where we have introduced

\displaystyle\begin{split}&h_{1}(s,x)=e^{s\mathcal{L}_{1}}\bar{\rho}_{n}(x)=\bar{\rho}_{n}(\phi_{-s}(x))J(-s,x),\\
&h_{2}(s,x)=e^{s\mathcal{L}_{2}}\bar{\rho}_{n}(x)=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\frac{1}{(4\pi\beta^{-1}s)^{d/2}}\exp(-\frac{\beta|x-y|^{2}}{4s})\bar{\rho}_{n}(y)\,dy.\end{split}

(4.5)

It follows that

I_{1}=(e^{s\mathcal{L}_{1}}\mathcal{L}_{2}-\mathcal{L}_{2}e^{s\mathcal{L}_{1}})h_{2}(s,x)\\
=\beta^{-1}(\Delta h_{2})(s,\phi_{-s}(x))J(-s,x)-\beta^{-1}\Delta_{x}\big(h_{2}(s,\phi_{-s}(x))J(-s,x)\big),

(4.6)

and

I_{2}=(e^{s\mathcal{L}_{2}}\mathcal{L}_{1}-\mathcal{L}_{\xi(1)}e^{s\mathcal{L}_{2}})h_{1}(s,x)=-\int\frac{1}{(4\pi\beta^{-1}s)^{\frac{d}{2}}}e^{-\frac{\beta|x-y|^{2}}{4s}}\nabla_{y}\cdot(b(y)h_{1}(s,y))dy\\
+\nabla_{x}\cdot\Big(b(x)\int\frac{1}{(4\pi\beta^{-1}s)^{\frac{d}{2}}}e^{-\frac{\beta|x-y|^{2}}{4s}}h_{1}(s,y)dy\Big).

(4.7)

After some computation whose details are provided in Appendix
C
, we obtain (recall
s=t-n\tau
)

\displaystyle\begin{split}r_{t}&=\frac{1}{2}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}(\log\frac{\bar{\rho}}{\rho})(I_{1}+I_{2})\,dx=\frac{\beta^{-1}s}{2}\int\nabla\log\frac{\bar{\rho}}{\rho}\cdot(\sum_{i=1}^{3}E_{i}(s,x))\,dx\\
&\leq\frac{\beta^{-1}}{8}\int|\nabla\log\frac{\bar{\rho}}{\rho}|^{2}\bar{\rho}\,dx+\frac{3(\beta^{-1}s)^{2}}{2}\int\frac{\sum_{i=1}^{3}|E_{i}(s,x)|^{2}}{\bar{\rho}(t,x)}\,dx,\end{split}

(4.8)

where the
E_{i}
are three remainder vectors fields. The remainder
E_{1}
is given by

E_{1}(s,x)=\frac{\nabla_{x}\phi_{-s}(x)-(\nabla_{x}\phi_{-s}(x))^{-T}}{s}\cdot\nabla h_{2}(s,\phi_{-s}(x))J(-s,x)\\
+\int\frac{e^{-\frac{\beta|x-y|^{2}}{4s}}}{(4\pi\beta^{-1}s)^{\frac{d}{2}}}(\overline{\nabla b}(x,y)+\overline{\nabla b}(x,y)^{T})\cdot\nabla h_{1}(s,y)dy,

(4.9)

where we have introduced

\displaystyle\overline{\nabla b}(x,y):=\int_{0}^{1}\nabla b(\lambda y+(1-\lambda)x)d\lambda.

(4.10)

Moreover,

E_{2}
is given by

E_{2}(s,x)=s^{-1}\nabla J(-s,x)h_{2}(s,\phi_{-s}(x))+\int\frac{e^{-\frac{\beta|x-y|^{2}}{4s}}}{(4\pi\beta^{-1}s)^{\frac{d}{2}}}\overline{\nabla(\nabla\cdot b)}(x,y)h_{1}(s,y)dy,

(4.11)

and
E_{3}
is given by

E_{3}(s,x)=\int\frac{e^{-\frac{\beta|x-y|^{2}}{4s}}}{(4\pi\beta^{-1}s)^{\frac{d}{2}}}\int_{0}^{1}\lambda[\Delta b(z(\lambda,y,x))-\Delta b(z(\lambda,x,y))]d\lambda h_{1}(s,y)dy,

(4.12)

where
z(\lambda,x,y):=\lambda x+(1-\lambda)y
.

By the definition of
h_{i}
, one has

\displaystyle\nabla h_{2}(s,x)=\int\frac{1}{(4\pi\beta^{-1}s)^{d/2}}\exp(-\frac{\beta|x-y|^{2}}{4s})\nabla\bar{\rho}_{n}(y)\,dy,

(4.13)

and

\displaystyle\nabla h_{1}(s,x)=\nabla\phi_{-s}(x)\cdot\nabla\bar{\rho}_{n}(\phi_{-s}(x))J(-s,x)+\bar{\rho}_{n}(\phi_{-s}(x))\nabla J(-s,x).

(4.14)

We expect that all the three terms above are of order
O(s)
so that
r_{t}
is of order
O(s^{4})
.
In fact, we are able to derive the following pointwise bounds using the pointwise estimates of the log density and its various derivatives in section
4.2
and section
4.3
.
The derivation is kind of tedious and we attach it in Appendix
C
.

Proposition 4.1
.

Suppose Assumption
2.1
on
b
and Assumption
2.2
on the initial density hold, then

\displaystyle\sum_{i=1}^{3}|E_{i}(s,x)|\leq Cs(1+|x|^{p_{1}})\bar{\rho}(t,x),

(4.15)

for some
p_{1}>0
, where
C
depends on
T
, and possibly the dimension

.

Remark 4.1
.

What is crucial to us is that the bound here depends on the coefficients

in the assumption of
\rho_{0}
, not on the particular form of
\rho_{0}
. This is important as we will apply this result to different ”initial densities” and the contraction property to get the global control.

Combining equation (
4.3
), (
4.8
), and Proposition
4.1
and the moment bounds in Lemma

, we obtain the following result.

Theorem 4.1
.

Suppose Assumption
2.1
holds. Let
\rho(t)
be the solution to the Fokker-Planck equation with initial value
\rho_{0}
satisfying Assumption
2.2
and
\bar{\rho}(t)

(4.16)

By Assumption

and the exponential moment bounds in

, the reference law

satisfies,
uniformly for

T_{1}

W_{1}(\mu,\rho(t))\;\leq\;\sqrt{\,2C_{T}\,\mathcal{H}(\mu\|\rho(t))},\qquad\text{for all probability measures $\mu$}.

Corollary 4.1

(Wasserstein–1 error bound)
.

Under the assumptions of Theorem
4.1
,
for any
T>0
there exists a constant
C(T)>0
such that, for all

W_{1}\bigl(\bar{\rho}(t),\rho(t)\bigr)\;\leq\;\sqrt{\,2C_{T}\,\mathcal{H}(\bar{\rho}(t)\|\rho(t))\,}\;\leq\;C(T)\,\tau^{2}.

In the next several subsections, we establish the bounds for the log-density and its derivatives for

4.2
The bounds on the density

This subsection establishes rigorous upper and lower bounds for the numerical solution density.

Proposition 4.2
.

Suppose Assumptions
2.1
and
2.2
hold. Then for any fixed sequence of random permutations
\boldsymbol{\xi}
and
\tau\leq 1
, the density for the random splitting LMC
\rho_{n}^{\xi}
defined in (
2.21
) satisfies

A_{3}\exp(-\alpha_{1}|x|^{p})\leq\rho_{n}^{\xi}\leq A_{4}\exp(-\alpha_{2}|x|^{2}),\quad\forall~n\tau\leq T,

where the constants
\alpha_{i}
and
A_{3},A_{4}
are independent of
\boldsymbol{\xi}
and
\tau
, but can depend on
T
and the initial data through the constants in Assumption
2.2
.

To prove this result, we first establish some simple but useful properties of the advection step and the diffusion step. The following is about the short time continuity of the transport flow map.

Lemma 4.1
.

Under Assumption
2.1
and

2.2
, the flow map
\phi_{t}
associated with SDE (
1.1
) satisfies the following properties:

|\phi_{t}(x)-x|\leq C(1+|x|)(e^{L|t|}-1),

|x|e^{-Lt}-C(1-e^{-Lt})\leq|\phi_{-t}(x)|\leq|x|e^{Lt}+C(e^{Lt}-1),

and for any two points

\displaystyle e^{-Lt}|\phi_{-t}(x)-y|\leq|x-\phi_{t}(y)|\leq e^{Lt}|\phi_{-t}(x)-y|.

(4.17)

Proof.

For the first, one notes that

\partial_{t}|\phi_{t}(x)-x|^{2}=2(\phi_{t}(x)-x)\cdot b(\phi_{t}(x))\Longrightarrow\partial_{t}|\phi_{t}(x)-x|\leq|b(\phi_{t}(x))|\leq L|\phi_{t}(x)-x|+|b(x)|.

The first claim for
t\geq 0
follows from the Grönwall’s inequality and linear growth of
b(x)
.
For
t<0
, one has similarly

\partial_{t}|\phi_{t}(x)-x|\geq-L|\phi_{t}(x)-x|-|b(x)|,

and the claim follows.

Since

\frac{d}{ds}\phi_{s}(\phi_{-t}(x))=b(\phi_{s}(\phi_{-t}(x))),

one has

\frac{d}{ds}|\phi_{s}(\phi_{-t}(x))|^{2}\leq 2L|\phi_{s}(\phi_{-t}(x))|^{2}+2|b(0)||\phi_{s}(\phi_{-t}(x))|.

It follows that

|x|\leq|\phi_{-t}(x)|e^{Lt}+|b(0)|(e^{Lt}-1)\Rightarrow|\phi_{-t}(x)|\geq|x|e^{-Lt}-C(1-e^{-Lt}).

Consider the backward flow. Differentiating explicitly,

\frac{d}{dt}|\phi_{-t}(x)|^{2}=2\phi_{-t}(x)\cdot\frac{d}{dt}\phi_{-t}(x)=-2\phi_{-t}(x)\cdot b(\phi_{-t}(x))\leq 2L|\phi_{-t}(x)|^{2}+2|b(0)|\,|\phi_{-t}(x)|.

Applying Grönwall’s inequality yields

|\phi_{-t}(x)|\leq|x|e^{Lt}+C(e^{Lt}-1).

We note that

\frac{d}{ds}|\phi_{-s}(x)-\phi_{-s}(\phi_{t}(y))|^{2}=2(\phi_{-s}(x)-\phi_{-s}(\phi_{t}(y)))\cdot[-b(\phi_{-s}(x))+b(\phi_{-s}(\phi_{t}(y)))]\\
\leq 2L|\phi_{-s}(x)-\phi_{-s}(\phi_{t}(y))|^{2},

Applying Grönwall’s inequality for
s\in[0,t]
gives the left hand side of the last inequality.
Similarly

\displaystyle\frac{d}{ds}|\phi_{s}(\phi_{-t}(x))-\phi_{s}(y)|^{2}\leq 2L|\phi_{s}(\phi_{-t}(x))-\phi_{s}(y)|^{2},

Applying Grönwall’s inequality for
s\in[0,t]
then gives the right hand side of the last inequality.
∎

The following gives the properties of the heat semigroup.

Lemma 4.2
.

Let the initial density satisfy
A_{1}\exp(-\alpha|x|^{p})\leq\rho_{0}(x)\leq Ce^{-c|x|^{2}}
where
C,c>0
and
p\geq 2
. Then

\displaystyle e^{t\mathcal{L}_{\xi(2)}}\rho(x,t)\leq\frac{C}{(1+4c\beta^{-1}t)^{d/2}}e^{-\frac{c|x|^{2}}{1+4c\beta^{-1}t}},

(4.18)

and

\displaystyle e^{t\mathcal{L}_{\xi(2)}}\rho(x,t)\geq A_{1}\exp\left(-\frac{\beta^{-1}(d+p-2)^{2}}{4}t\right)\exp(-\alpha|x|^{p}).

(4.19)

Proof.

The Gaussian upper bound is a direction computation.

Now, we consider the lower bound. It is clear that

\displaystyle e^{t\mathcal{L}_{\xi(2)}}\rho(x,t)=\mathbb{E}\rho(x+\sqrt{2\beta^{-1}}W_{t}).

(4.20)

Let
Y_{t}=x+\sqrt{2\beta^{-1}}W_{t}
. By the assumption, one then has

e^{t\mathcal{L}_{\xi(2)}}\rho(x,t)\geq A_{1}\mathbb{E}\exp(-\alpha|Y_{t}|^{p}).

Setting
u(t)=\mathbb{E}\exp(-\alpha|Y_{t}|^{p})
and applying Itô’s formula, one has

d|Y_{t}|^{p}=\sqrt{2\beta^{-1}}p|Y_{t}|^{p-2}Y_{t}\cdot dW+\beta^{-1}p(d+p-2)|Y_{t}|^{p-2}dt.

Hence,

\displaystyle du(t)=\beta^{-1}\mathbb{E}e^{-\alpha|Y_{t}|^{p}}(\alpha^{2}p^{2}|Y_{t}|^{2p-2}-\alpha p(d+p-2)|Y_{t}|^{p-2})\geq-\frac{\beta^{-1}(d+p-2)}{4}u(t).

Hence, for the lower bound in the diffusion step, the constant
\alpha_{1}
can be taken to be unchanged,
while the constant
A_{1}
is decreased by a factor
e^{-C\tau}
.

∎

Now, we prove Proposition
4.2
.

Proof of Proposition
4.2
.

We do by induction. Assume that

A_{3}^{(n)}\exp(-\alpha_{1}^{(n)}|x|^{p})\leq\rho_{n}^{\xi}\leq A_{4}^{(n)}\exp(-\alpha_{2}^{(n)}|x|^{2}),

we establish this for
\rho_{n+1}^{\xi}
.

We first consider the upper bound. If the advection step is first, then

e^{\tau\mathcal{L}_{\xi(1)}}\bar{\rho}_{n}\leq A_{4}^{(n)}\exp(-\alpha_{2}^{(n)}|\phi_{-\tau}(x)|^{2})J(-\tau,x).

By Lemma
4.1
,

|\phi_{-\tau}(x)|\geq|x|e^{-C\tau}-C\tau,

and
J(-\tau,x)\leq e^{C\tau}
since the drift
b
is globally Lipschitz.
Then, for
\tau\leq 1
,

|\phi_{-\tau}(x)|^{2}\geq|x|^{2}e^{-2C\tau}(1-C_{1}\tau)-C_{2}\tau.

This inequality holds for
|x|\leq C\tau e^{C\tau}
as well since the left hand side is obviously nonnegative. Then, after this step, one may take

\tilde{A}_{4}^{(n+1)}=A_{4}^{(n)}e^{C\tau}e^{\alpha_{2}^{(0)}C_{2}\tau},\quad\tilde{\alpha}_{2}^{(n+1)}=\alpha_{2}^{(n)}e^{-2C\tau}(1-C_{1}\tau),

since
\max\{\tilde{\alpha}_{2}^{(n)},\alpha_{2}^{(n)}\}\leq\alpha_{2}^{(0)}
.
For the diffusion (heat) step, Lemma
4.2
tells us that
we may take

A_{4}^{(n+1)}=\tilde{A}_{4}^{(n+1)},\quad\alpha_{2}^{(n+1)}=\frac{\tilde{\alpha}_{2}^{(n+1)}}{1+4\alpha_{2}^{(0)}\beta^{-1}\tau},

since
\frac{1}{(1+4\tilde{\alpha}_{2}^{(n+1)}\beta^{-1}\tau)^{d/2}}\leq 1
,
and again
\max\{\tilde{\alpha}_{2}^{(n)},\alpha_{2}^{(n)}\}\leq\alpha_{2}^{(0)}
.
If the diffusion step is first, the estimate is similar.
Then, one can take

A_{4}^{(n+1)}=A_{4}^{(n)}e^{C\tau}e^{\alpha_{2}^{(0)}C_{2}\tau},\quad\alpha_{2}^{(n+1)}=\frac{1}{1+4\alpha_{2}^{(0)}\beta^{-1}\tau}e^{-2C\tau}(1-C_{1}\tau)\alpha_{2}^{(n)}.

Hence, after
n
steps up to finite horizon
T=n\tau
, the Gaussian upper bound is preserved, with
A_{4}
enlarged at most by
(1+C\tau)^{n}\leq C(T)
and
\alpha_{2}
decreased at most by
e^{-C\tau n}\geq e^{-CT}
.

Next we consider the lower bound. For the advection step, one has

e^{\tau\mathcal{L}_{\xi(1)}}\bar{\rho}_{n}\geq A_{3}^{(n)}\exp(-\alpha_{1}^{(n)}|\phi_{-\tau}(x)|^{p})J(-\tau,x).

Lemma
4.1
ensures that

|\phi_{-\tau}(x)|\leq|x|e^{C\tau}+C\tau

and similarly

J(-\tau,x)\geq e^{-C\tau}.

Then,

|\phi_{-\tau}(x)|^{p}=|x|^{p}e^{Cp\tau}+\sum_{k=1}^{p}{p\choose k}|x|^{p-k}e^{C(p-k)\tau}C^{k}\tau^{k}\leq|x|^{p}e^{Cp\tau}(1+C\tau)+C\tau,

where
C
here depends on
p
. Hence, one may take

\tilde{A}_{3}^{(n+1)}=A_{3}^{(n)}\exp(-\alpha_{1}^{(n)}C\tau)e^{-C\tau},\quad\tilde{\alpha}_{1}^{(n+1)}=\alpha_{1}^{(n)}(1+C\tau).

In the diffusion step, Lemma
4.2
implies that we can take

A_{3}^{(n+1)}=\tilde{A}_{3}^{(n+1)}\exp(-\frac{\beta^{-1}}{4}(d+p-2)^{2}\tau),\quad\alpha_{1}^{(n+1)}=\tilde{\alpha}_{1}^{(n+1)}.

If the diffusion step is first, the estimate would be similar. Hence, no matter which case, one has

A_{3}^{(n+1)}=A_{3}^{(n)}\exp(-\alpha_{1}^{(n)}C\tau)e^{-C\tau}\exp(-\frac{\beta^{-1}}{4}(d+p-2)^{2}\tau),\quad\alpha_{1}^{(n+1)}=\alpha_{1}^{(n)}(1+C\tau).

Using this recursion relation,
\alpha_{1}^{(n)}\leq\alpha_{1}^{(0)}\exp(CT)
. Then, this further implies that
A_{3}^{(n)}\geq A_{3}^{(0)}/C(T)
for all
n\tau\leq T
.

This then shows that the upper and lower bounds can be propagated and the claims hold.
∎

4.3
Derivatives of the logarithmic density

This subsection derives uniform bounds on the first and second-order derivatives of the numerical logarithmic density. These regularity estimates provide the necessary technical tools for establishing the fourth-order convergence rate in relative entropy.

4.3.1
First order derivative estimates

Similarly as above, we consider

\displaystyle v_{n}^{\xi}:=\frac{|\nabla f_{n}^{\xi}|^{2}}{(1-f_{n}^{\xi}+\log M)^{2}},\quad f_{n}^{\xi}=\log\rho_{n}^{\xi}.

(4.21)

Here,
M
is an upper bound of
\rho_{n}^{\xi}
on
[0,T]
.
By the assumption given, it is clear that

\displaystyle A=\sup_{x}v_{0}(x)<\infty.

(4.22)

Similarly, we can set
M=1
without loss of generality.

Next, we aim to argue that this quantity stays bounded stated as follows.

Proposition 4.3
.

Suppose Assumptions
2.1
and
2.2
hold. Then, it holds that

\displaystyle\sup_{x}\sup_{t_{n}\leq T}v_{n}^{\xi}(x)\leq C(T),

(4.23)

where
C(T)
is independent of
\xi
.

To prove this claim, we first check the effects of both the advection step and the diffusion step. Then, we get the final estimate.

For the advection step, we have the following one step stability.

Lemma 4.3
.

Let
\tilde{\rho}=e^{\tau\mathcal{L}_{\xi(1)}}\rho
and
\tilde{v}
be the
v
corresponding to
\tilde{\rho}
. Then, one has

\displaystyle\sup_{x}\tilde{v}\leq(1+C\tau)\sup_{x}v+C\tau.

(4.24)

Proof.

By the assumption, since
M=1
is the uniform bound, which implies that

e^{\tau\mathcal{L}_{\xi(1)}}\rho=\rho(\phi_{-\tau}(x))J(-\tau,x)\leq 1.

Hence,
1-f(\phi_{-\tau}(x))-\log J(-\tau,x)\geq 1
.

By the fact that

|\nabla\phi_{-\tau}(x)|\leq(1+C\tau),\quad|\nabla\log J(-\tau,x)|\leq C\tau,

one has

\displaystyle\begin{split}\tilde{v}&=\frac{|\nabla\phi_{-\tau}(x)\cdot\nabla f(\phi_{-\tau}(x))+\nabla\log J(-\tau,x)|^{2}}{(1-f(\phi_{-\tau}(x))-\log J(-\tau,x))^{2}}\\
&\leq\frac{(1+C\tau)^{2}|\nabla f(\phi_{\tau}(x))|^{2}(1+C\tau)+C\tau}{(1-f(\phi_{-\tau}(x)))^{2}[1-\log J(-\tau,x)/(1-f(\phi_{-\tau}))]^{2}}.\end{split}

Here, we used
(a+C\tau)^{2}\leq(1+C\tau)a^{2}+C\tau
for
\tau\leq 1
.
Moreover, since
1-f(\phi_{-\tau}(x))\geq 1
, we have
\bigl|\tfrac{\log J(-\tau,x)}{1-f(\phi_{-\tau}(x))}\bigr|\leq C\tau
, so that for
\tau\leq 1
,

\tilde{v}(x)\leq(1+C\tau)\frac{|\nabla f(\phi_{\tau}(x))|^{2}}{(1-f(\phi_{-\tau}(x)))^{2}}+C\tau.

Hence, the claim follows.
∎

Next, we consider the diffusion step.

Lemma 4.4
.

Let
\tilde{\rho}=e^{\tau\mathcal{L}_{\xi(2)}}\rho
and
\tilde{v}
be the
v
corresponding to
\tilde{\rho}
. Then,

\sup_{x}\tilde{v}\leq\max\{\sup_{x}v,A_{1}\},

where
A_{1}
is independent of
\xi,n,\tau
and the initial value.

The proof of Lemma
4.4
follows analogously to Theorem
2.1
, where
we derived estimates for
f=\log\rho
in the continuous setting (with
\rho
satisfying
f_{t}=\beta^{-1}f_{ii}+\beta^{-1}f_{i}f_{i}
, i.e.,
b^{i}=0
,
c=0
). We omit the details.

Proof of Proposition
4.3
.

Combining Lemma
4.3
and Lemma
4.4
, for each time step
\tau
, one can directly derive the desired bound for
v
.
∎

4.3.2
Second order derivative estimates

Now, we have proved that
v_{n}^{\xi}
is bounded for
t\leq T
uniformly in
\xi
. Then, we can find
A>0
such that the following
w_{n}^{\xi}
,
in analogy with the continuous case, is well-defined

w_{n}^{\xi}:=\frac{1}{q(x)}\frac{|\nabla^{2}f_{n}^{\xi}|^{2}}{A(1-f_{n}^{\xi}+\log M)^{2}-|\nabla f_{n}^{\xi}|^{2}},\quad f_{n}^{\xi}=\log\rho_{n}^{\xi}(x)

and
q(x)=1+|x|^{r}
for some
r\geq 0
to be chosen so that
w
is bounded at
n=0
and extra polynomial terms can be controlled.

Proposition 4.4

(second order derivative estimate)
.

Under the assumptions of Proposition
4.3
, there exists some polynomial
q(x)=1+|x|^{r}
such that

\sup_{n:n\tau\leq T}\sup_{x}w_{n}^{\xi}(x)\leq C(T),

where
C(T)
is independent of
\xi
.

We first consider the effect of the advection step.

Lemma 4.5
.

Let
\tilde{\rho}=e^{\tau\mathcal{L}_{\xi(1)}}\rho
where
\rho
is any possible
\rho_{n}^{\xi}
and
\tilde{w}
be the
w
corresponding to
\tilde{\rho}
. Then there exists
C>0
independent of
\tau
such for
\tau\leq 1
that

\displaystyle\sup_{x}\tilde{w}\leq(1+C\tau)\sup_{x}w+C\tau.

(4.25)

Proof.

First, one has

\tilde{f}=\tilde{f}(\phi_{-\tau}(x))+\log J(-\tau,x).

It is clear that

\nabla^{2}\tilde{f}=\nabla\phi_{-\tau}(x)^{\otimes 2}:\nabla^{2}f(\phi_{-\tau}(x))+\nabla^{2}\phi_{-\tau}(x)\cdot\nabla f(\phi_{-\tau}(x))+\nabla^{2}J(-\tau,x).

Here

(\nabla\phi_{-\tau}(x)^{\otimes 2}:\nabla^{2}f(\phi_{-\tau}(x)))_{ij}:=\partial_{i}\phi_{m}\partial_{j}\phi_{n}\partial_{mn}f.

By Lemma
4.1
, the flow
\phi_{-\tau}
satisfies the estimates

|\nabla\phi_{-\tau}(x)-I|\leq C\tau,\qquad\|\nabla^{2}\phi_{-\tau}\|+|\nabla^{2}J(-\tau,x)|\leq C\tau.

Again, we can rescale all
\rho_{n}^{\xi}
by
M
so that we can set
M=1

and similarly,
1-f(y)\geq 1
and
1-f(\phi_{-\tau}(x))-\log J(-\tau,x)\geq 1
.

By Young’s inequality, one then has for
\tau\leq 1
that

\|\nabla^{2}\tilde{f}\|^{2}\leq(1+C\tau)\|\nabla^{2}f(\phi_{-\tau}(x))\|^{2}+C\tau\|\nabla f(\phi_{-\tau}(x))\|^{2}+C\tau.

(4.26)

For the denominator
D(\tilde{f}):=A(1-\tilde{f})^{2}-|\nabla\tilde{f}|^{2}
, one has

D(\tilde{f})=A(1-\tilde{f}(\phi_{-\tau}(x))-\log J(-\tau,x))^{2}-|\nabla\phi_{-\tau}(x)\cdot\nabla f(\phi_{-\tau}(x))+\nabla\log J(-\tau,x)|^{2}\\
\geq A(1-\tilde{f}(\phi_{-\tau}(x)))^{2}-|\nabla f(\phi_{-\tau}(x))|^{2}-C(1-\tilde{f})\tau-C\tau|\nabla f|^{2}-C\tau|\nabla f|.

As we have constructed, by the boundedness of
v
,

A(1-\tilde{f}(\phi_{-\tau}(x)))^{2}-|\nabla f(\phi_{-\tau}(x))|^{2}\geq\frac{A}{2}(1-\tilde{f}(\phi_{-\tau}(x)))^{2}\geq\max\{\frac{A}{2},\frac{1}{2}|\nabla f(\phi_{-\tau}(x))|^{2}\}.

It is straightforward to see that

D(\tilde{f})\geq\left(A(1-\tilde{f}(\phi_{-\tau}(x)))^{2}-|\nabla f(\phi_{-\tau}(x))|^{2}\right)(1-C\tau).

Hence, one has

\tilde{w}(x)\leq\frac{q(\phi_{-\tau}(x))}{q(x)}w(\phi_{-\tau}(x))(1+C\tau)+\frac{1}{q(x)}\frac{C\tau|\nabla f(\phi_{-\tau}(x))|^{2}+C\tau}{\max\{\frac{A}{2},\frac{1}{2}|\nabla f(\phi_{-\tau}(x))|^{2}\}}

Next, by Lemma
4.1
, one finds that

\frac{q(\phi_{-\tau}(x))}{q(x)}\leq 1+C\tau,

for
\tau\leq 1
where
C
depends on
r
. The second term is clearly bounded by
C\tau
.
∎

Next, we consider the diffusion step.

Lemma 4.6
.

Let
\tilde{\rho}=e^{\tau\mathcal{L}_{\xi(2)}}\rho
and
\tilde{w}
be the
w
corresponding to
\tilde{\rho}
. Then, there is some polynomial
q(x)=1+|x|^{r}
so that for a constant

independent of
\xi,n,\tau
and the initial value, it holds for all possible

that

\sup_{x}\tilde{w}\leq\max\left\{\sup_{x}w,A_{2}\right\}.

The proof of Lemma
4.6
follows analogously to Theorem
2.1
.

Proof of Proposition
4.4
.

This is a direct consequence of Lemma
4.5
and Lemma
4.6
.

4.4
The polynomial bounds of the derivatives for the logarithmic density

Based on the derivative bounds for
\rho_{n}^{\xi}
established above,
we can extend these estimates to the averaged density
\bar{\rho}(t)
.

Theorem 4.2
.

Consider the averaged interpolated density
\bar{\rho}(t)
for the random splitting Langevin Monte Carlo defined in (
2.24
).
Suppose both Assumptions
2.1
for the coefficients and
2.2
for the initial density hold. Then, for any
T>0
, there exist some
p>0
and
C(T)>0
satisfying

\sup_{t\leq T}\left(|\log\bar{\rho}(t,x)|+|\nabla\log\bar{\rho}(t,x)|+|\nabla^{2}\log\bar{\rho}(t,x)|\right)\;\leq\;C(T)\,(1+|x|)^{p}.

(4.27)

Proof.

First, we remark that the results in Proposition
4.2
, Proposition
4.3
and
Proposition
4.4
can be extended to
\rho^{\xi}(t)
defined in (
2.22
). In fact, this can be understood that the last step is performed with step size
t-t_{n}
and the proof has no difference.

First, by Proposition
4.2
,
\bar{\rho}(t)

has the same upper and lower bound and thus the claim for
\log\bar{\rho}
holds.

Moreover, by the boundedness of
v^{\xi}(t)
in Proposition
4.3
,

|\nabla\log\rho^{\xi}(t)|\leq C(1-\log\rho^{\xi}(t)+\log M)\leq C(1+|x|^{p}).

Since the mapping

(\rho,m)\mapsto m^{q}/\rho^{q-1}
for
q\geq 1
is convex, one thus has by Jensen’s inequality that

\bar{\rho}(t)|\nabla\log\bar{\rho}(t)|^{q}\leq\mathbb{E}_{\xi}[\rho^{\xi}(t)|\nabla\log\rho^{\xi}(t)|^{q}]\leq\mathbb{E}_{\xi}[\rho^{\xi}(t)(C(1+|x|^{p}))^{q}]=\bar{\rho}(t)(C(1+|x|^{p}))^{q}.

This gives the result for
|\nabla\log\bar{\rho}(t)|
.

Lastly, by the same argument,

\frac{|\nabla^{2}\bar{\rho}(t)|^{q}}{(\bar{\rho}(t))^{q-1}}\leq\mathbb{E}_{\xi}\left[\frac{|\nabla^{2}\rho^{\xi}(t)|^{q}}{(\rho^{\xi}(t))^{q-1}}\right]=\mathbb{E}_{\xi}[\rho^{\xi}(t)|\nabla^{2}\log\rho^{\xi}(t)+\nabla\log\rho^{\xi}\otimes\nabla\log\rho^{\xi}|^{q}].

Hence, one has

\bar{\rho}(t)|\nabla^{2}\log\bar{\rho}(t)|^{q}\leq\mathbb{E}_{\xi}[\rho^{\xi}(t)|\nabla^{2}\log\rho^{\xi}(t)+\nabla\log\rho^{\xi}\otimes\nabla\log\rho^{\xi}|^{q}]-\bar{\rho}(t)|\nabla\log\bar{\rho}(t)|^{2q}.

Then, by the estimate of
w^{\xi}
in Proposition
4.4
, one finds easily that

|\nabla^{2}\log\rho^{\xi}(t)|
is bounded by a polynomial uniformly in
\xi
as well. Hence, by the same argument, the bounds
on
\nabla^{2}\log\bar{\rho}
follows.
∎

5
The sampling error estimates

In this section we address the long-time behavior of the splitting scheme.
While the previous section established local error estimates on finite time intervals (Theorem
4.1
),
our goal here is to derive global error bounds that remain uniform in time.
The key observation is that the splitting scheme itself admits a unique invariant distribution and is ergodic with exponential convergence.
By combining this ergodicity property with the finite-time local error analysis,
we obtain a sharp estimate of the distance between the invariant distribution of the numerical scheme and the invariant distribution of the continuous dynamics.

Let
\mathcal{S}_{n}^{\tau}
be the evolutional operator for the laws of the random splitting LMC, i.e.,

\mathcal{S}_{n}^{\tau}\rho_{0}:=\mathrm{Law}(X_{n}),~\text{if}~\rho_{0}=\mathrm{Law}(X_{0}).

Clearly,
\mathcal{S}_{n}^{\tau}
is a linear operator. Moreover, by the Markov property,

\displaystyle\mathcal{S}_{n}^{\tau}=(\mathcal{S}^{\tau})^{n},

(5.1)

so that
\{\mathcal{S}_{n}^{\tau}\}
forms a Markov semigroup.

Theorem 5.1

(Ergodicity of the splitting scheme)
.

Under Assumptions
2.1
and
2.2
,
the Markov semigroup
S^{\tau}
associated with the random splitting LMC admits a unique invariant distribution
\bar{\rho}_{*}^{\tau}
.
Moreover, there exist constants
C,\lambda>0
, independent of
\tau
, such that for any probability measure
\mu
,

W_{1}\!\big((S^{\tau})^{n}\mu,\,\bar{\rho}_{*}^{\tau}\big)\;\leq\;Ce^{-\lambda n\tau}\,W_{1}(\mu,\,\bar{\rho}_{*}^{\tau}),

(5.2)

for all integers
n\geq 0
.

Proof.

By Theorem
3.1
, one finds that there exists some
n_{0}>0
such that
(\mathcal{S}^{\tau})^{n_{0}}
is a contraction under the
W^{1}
metric. By the contraction principle,
(\mathcal{S}^{\tau})^{n_{0}}
has a unique invariant measure
\bar{\rho}_{*}^{\tau}
. Then, it can be verified directly that

\tilde{\pi}^{\tau}:=\frac{1}{n_{0}}\sum_{i=0}^{n_{0}-1}(\mathcal{S}^{\tau})^{i}\bar{\rho}_{*}^{\tau}

is an invariant measure of
\mathcal{S}^{\tau}
.
Moreover, any invariant measure of
\mathcal{S}^{\tau}
is also an invariant measure of
(\mathcal{S}^{\tau})^{n_{0}}
and thus must be
\bar{\rho}_{*}^{\tau}
by the uniqueness of the invariant measure of
(\mathcal{S}^{\tau})^{n_{0}}
, concluding the first claim.

The second claim follows from Theorem
3.1
directly by setting
\nu=\bar{\rho}_{*}^{\tau}
.
∎

We now combine the finite-time local error estimate (Theorem
4.1
) with the ergodicity result (Theorem
5.1
).
This yields a global error estimate for the random splitting LMC uniformly in time, thus yielding the error for the invariant measure. This gives the support for sampling using the random splitting LMC.

Theorem 5.2
.

Let
\rho(t)=\mathcal{S}(t)\rho_{0}
denote the solution of the Fokker–Planck equation with initial distribution
\rho_{0}
, and let
\bar{\rho}_{n}=(\mathcal{S}^{\tau})^{n}\rho_{0}
be the law generated by the splitting scheme. Then there exists a constant
C>0
, independent of
n
and
\tau
, such that the following hold:

W_{1}\!\big(\mathcal{S}(\tau)^{n}\rho_{0},\,(\mathcal{S}^{\tau})^{n}\rho_{0}\big)\;\leq\;C\tau^{2},\qquad\text{for all }n\geq 0.

(5.3)

Moreover, the invariant measure
\bar{\rho}_{*}^{\tau}
satisfies

W_{1}(\bar{\rho}_{*}^{\tau},\rho_{\ast})\;\leq\;C\tau^{2},

(5.4)

where
\rho_{\ast}
is the unique probability invariant measure of the continuous dynamics.

Proof.

We first establish the uniform-in-time estimate (
5.3
).
From the local error analysis in relative entropy and a suitable transport–entropy inequality, one obtains that for any fixed finite horizon
T>0
,

W_{1}\!\big(\mathcal{S}(\tau)^{n}\rho,\,(\mathcal{S}^{\tau})^{n}\rho\big)\;\leq\;C(T)\,\tau^{2},\qquad\text{for all }n\tau\leq T,

for any
\rho
satisfying Assumption
2.2
where

depends on
T
and
\rho
through the coefficients in Assumption
2.2
only, and is independent of
\tau
.

To get the uniform-in-time estimate, we combine the finite-time estimate with the exponential ergodicity of the numerical dynamics, together with the uniform estimates of the density for the time-continuous dynamics.

By the exponential ergodicity of the random splitting LMC (Theorem

), there exists

such that whenever

and any probability measures

where

is independent of
\tau
.

Set

and take

,

W_{1}\!\big(\mathcal{S}(\tau)^{n}\rho_{0},\,(\mathcal{S}^{\tau})^{n}\rho_{0}\big)\\
\leq W_{1}\!\big(\mathcal{S}(\tau)^{n-m}\rho(t_{m}),\,(\mathcal{S}^{\tau})^{n-m}\rho(t_{m})\big)+W_{1}\!\big((\mathcal{S}^{\tau})^{n-m}\rho(t_{m}),\,(\mathcal{S}^{\tau})^{n-m}\bar{\rho}_{m}\big).

with
\tau_{0}

and the constant

and Theorem

The second term is bounded by

W_{1}\!\big((\mathcal{S}^{\tau})^{n-m}\rho(t_{m}),\,(\mathcal{S}^{\tau})^{n-m}\bar{\rho}_{m}\big)\;\leq\;\gamma^{\prime}W_{1}(\rho(t_{m}),\,\bar{\rho}_{m}).

which establishes (

For general

Finally, we pass to the limit

∎

6
Numerical Experiments

This section presents numerical evidence for the fourth-order convergence of the randomized splitting Langevin method (RSLMC) in relative entropy. We study three one-dimensional benchmarks and one two-dimensional multimodal example, chosen to cover light-tailed non-Gaussian targets, non-convex multi-well energy landscapes, a rigorously constructed model that satisfies all assumptions of the theory, and a higher-dimensional mixture model. Unless otherwise stated, we use
M=10^{7}
Monte Carlo trajectories, a final integration time
T=50
, and inverse temperature
\beta=1
. For each step size
\tau
, the endpoint distribution generated by RSLMC is compared against a reference distribution obtained by either exact sampling (inverse transform), rejection sampling with a Gaussian proposal, or direct mixture sampling.

Probability densities are approximated by Gaussian kernel density estimation (KDE). Given samples
\{X^{(j)}\}_{j=1}^{M}
in dimension
d
, the estimator is

\hat{\rho}(x)=\frac{1}{M\,h^{d}}\sum_{j=1}^{M}(2\pi)^{-d/2}\exp\!\left(-\frac{\|x-X^{(j)}\|^{2}}{2h^{2}}\right),

where the bandwidth
h
is chosen automatically by Silverman’s rule of thumb

h=\sigma\left(\frac{4}{d+2}\cdot\frac{1}{M}\right)^{1/(d+4)},

with
\sigma
is the sample standard deviation. In one-dimensional tests, the selected bandwidth is further scaled by a constant factor to suppress spurious oscillations; in the two-dimensional case, the unmodified rule is applied. All estimated densities are normalized over the computational grid used for entropy integration, and a small floor
\epsilon=10^{-12}
is enforced inside logarithms to stabilize the KL computation.

In RSLMC, the deterministic ODE substep follows the drift flow
\dot{x}=-\nabla U(x)
. When a closed-form flow is not available, we discretize this ODE by the second-order Heun (explicit trapezoidal) method:

Y=X+\tau f(X),\qquad X_{\text{new}}=X+\tfrac{\tau}{2}\big(f(X)+f(Y)\big),

with
f(x)=-\nabla U(x)
. For the double-well potential, the drift admits an analytic Strang splitting: write
f(x)=-4x^{3}+4x=f_{1}(x)+f_{2}(x)
, whose subflows are

\phi_{h}^{(1)}(x)=\frac{x}{\sqrt{1+8h\,x^{2}}},\qquad\phi_{h}^{(2)}(x)=x\,e^{4h}.

A step of size
h
is then the exact composition
\Phi_{h}=\phi_{h/2}^{(1)}\circ\phi_{h}^{(2)}\circ\phi_{h/2}^{(1)}
.

Estimates of the solution to Fokker-Planck

In this section, we establish bounds for the solution of the Fokker-Planck equation corresponding to the Langevin dynamics.

We consider

q_{t}(x)=\rho_{t}(x)/e^{-\beta U(x)}.

Then,

\partial_{t}q=-\nabla U\cdot\nabla q+\beta^{-1}\Delta q.

It is well know that

q_{t}(x)=\mathbb{E}q_{0}(X_{t}(x)),

where
X_{t}(x)
is the stochastic trajectory of the SDE

dX=-\nabla U(X)\,dt+\sqrt{2\beta^{-1}}\,dW.

Recall the claim that

B_{1}\exp(-C|x|^{p})\leq q_{t}(x)\leq B_{2}\exp(\beta(1-\delta)U(x)).

Proof of Proposition
2.1
.

By the assumption on
\rho_{0}
,

B_{1}^{\prime}\exp(-C^{\prime}|x|^{p})\leq q_{0}(x)\leq B_{2}^{\prime}\exp((1-\delta^{\prime})\beta U).

For the upper bound, we apply Itô’s formula to
e^{\beta(1-\delta)U}
. In fact,

dU(X)=(\beta^{-1}\Delta U-|\nabla U|^{2})\,dt+\sqrt{2\beta^{-1}}\nabla U(X)dW.

Then,

\frac{d}{dt}\mathbb{E}e^{\beta(1-\delta)U(X)}=\mathbb{E}\left\{e^{\beta(1-\delta)U(X)}[-\beta(1-\delta)\delta|\nabla U(X)|^{2}+(1-\delta)\Delta U(X)]\right\}.

By the assumptions

-\beta(1-\delta)\delta|\nabla U(X)|^{2}+(1-\delta)\Delta U(X)\leq-\lambda+C1_{|X|\leq L}.

Hence,

\frac{d}{dt}\mathbb{E}e^{\beta(1-\delta)U(X)}\leq-\lambda\mathbb{E}e^{\beta(1-\delta)U(X)}+C.

This implies that

\sup_{t\geq 0}q_{t}(x)\leq e^{\beta(1-\delta)U(x)}e^{-\lambda t}+C.

For the lower bound, we show that there exists
L>0
such that for all
x
and
t
that,

\mathbb{P}(|X_{t}(x)|\leq L(|x|+1))\geq 1/2.

In fact,
by the Markov inequality

\mathbb{P}(|X|\geq L(|x|+1))\leq[e^{\beta(1-\delta)U(x)}e^{-\lambda t}+C]/\inf_{y\in\mathbb{R}^{d}\setminus B(0,L(1+|x|))}e^{\beta(1-\delta)U(y)}\\
\leq\exp(\beta(1-\delta)[\lambda_{2}|x|^{2}-\lambda_{1}|L+L|x||^{2}])e^{-\lambda t}+C\exp(\beta(1-\delta)[-\lambda_{1}|L+L|x||^{2}]).

Then,

\inf_{t}q_{t}(x)\geq\mathbb{E}B_{1}\exp(-C|X_{t}(x)|^{p})\geq B_{1}^{\prime}\exp(-CL^{p}|x|^{p}).

-\frac{1}{2}\beta^{-1}\int(\log\frac{\bar{\rho}}{\rho})\Delta_{x}\big(h_{2}(s,\phi_{-s}(x))J(-s,x)\big)dx=:I_{11}+I_{12}.

For the first term, by change of variables

u=\phi_{-s}(x)\Rightarrow x=\phi_{s}(u),

it reduces to

\displaystyle\begin{split}I_{11}&=\frac{1}{2}\beta^{-1}\int(\log\frac{\bar{\rho}(t,\phi_{s}(u))}{\rho(t,\phi_{s}(u))})\Delta_{u}h_{2}(s,u)du\\
&=-\frac{1}{2}\beta^{-1}\int(\nabla_{u}\phi_{s}(u)\cdot\nabla\log\frac{\bar{\rho}(t,\phi_{s}(u))}{\rho(t,\phi_{s}(u))})\cdot\nabla_{u}h_{2}(s,u)du\\
&=-\frac{1}{2}\beta^{-1}\int\left((\nabla_{x}\phi_{-s}(x))^{-1}\cdot\nabla\log\frac{\bar{\rho}(t,x)}{\rho(t,x)}\right)\cdot(\nabla h_{2})(s,\phi_{-s}(x))J(-s,x)dx.\end{split}

By integration by parts, the second term is then given by

I_{12}=\frac{1}{2}\int\nabla\log\frac{\bar{\rho}(t,x)}{\rho(t,x)}\cdot\left(\nabla_{x}\phi_{-s}(x)\cdot\nabla h_{2}(s,\phi_{-s}(x))J(-s,x)+h_{2}(s,\phi_{-s}(x)\nabla J(-s,x))\right).

Hence, one has

\frac{1}{2}\int(\log\frac{\bar{\rho}}{\rho})I_{1}\,dx=\frac{1}{2}\beta^{-1}\int\nabla\log\frac{\bar{\rho}}{\rho}\cdot\Big[\nabla_{x}\phi_{-s}(x)-(\nabla_{x}\phi_{-s}(x))^{-T}\Big]\cdot\nabla h_{2}(s,\phi_{-s}(x))J(-s,x)\,dx\\
+\frac{1}{2}\beta^{-1}\int\nabla\log\frac{\bar{\rho}}{\rho}\cdot\nabla J(-s,x)h_{2}(s,\phi_{-s}(x))\,dx.

(C.1)

By integration by parts, it is relatively straightforward to see that

\begin{split}\frac{1}{2}\int(\log\frac{\bar{\rho}}{\rho})I_{2}\,dx&=\frac{1}{2}\int\nabla\log\frac{\bar{\rho}(t,x)}{\rho(t,x)}\cdot\int\frac{1}{(4\pi\beta^{-1}s)^{\frac{d}{2}}}e^{-\frac{\beta|x-y|^{2}}{4s}}\big(b(y)-b(x)\big)h_{1}(s,y)dydx\\
&=\frac{1}{2}\int\nabla\log\frac{\bar{\rho}}{\rho}\cdot\int(y-x)\cdot\overline{\nabla b}(x,y)\frac{e^{-\frac{\beta|x-y|^{2}}{4s}}}{(4\pi\beta^{-1}s)^{\frac{d}{2}}}h_{1}(s,y)dydx,\end{split}

(C.2)

where we recall

\displaystyle\overline{\nabla b}(x,y):=\int_{0}^{1}\nabla b(\lambda y+(1-\lambda)x)d\lambda.

(C.3)

Formal expansion of the semigroup indicates that this should agree with
R_{1}
in the
O(s)
order. We need to do some treatment to change this form here into a form that matches the expressions in
R_{1}
.
To this end, we split the above expression into two halves.
Noting that

\frac{1}{2}(y-x)e^{-\frac{\beta|x-y|^{2}}{4s}}=-s\beta^{-1}\nabla_{y}e^{-\frac{\beta|x-y|^{2}}{4s}}=s\beta^{-1}\nabla_{x}e^{-\frac{\beta|x-y|^{2}}{4s}},

one finds that

\begin{split}&\frac{1}{2}\int(\log\frac{\bar{\rho}}{\rho})I_{1}\,dx=\frac{s\beta^{-1}}{2}\int\nabla\log\frac{\bar{\rho}}{\rho}\cdot\Big[\int\nabla_{x}\frac{e^{-\frac{\beta|x-y|^{2}}{4s}}}{(4\pi\beta^{-1}s)^{\frac{d}{2}}}\cdot\overline{\nabla b}(x,y)h_{1}(s,y)dy\Big]dx\\
&-\frac{s\beta^{-1}}{2}\int\nabla\log\frac{\bar{\rho}}{\rho}\cdot\Big[\int\nabla_{y}\frac{e^{-\frac{\beta|x-y|^{2}}{4s}}}{(4\pi\beta^{-1}s)^{\frac{d}{2}}}\cdot\overline{\nabla b}(x,y)h_{1}(s,y)dy\Big]dx\\
&=:I_{21}+I_{22}.\end{split}

(C.4)

Integration by parts, one finds that

I_{21}=-\frac{s\beta^{-1}}{2}\int\nabla^{2}\log\frac{\bar{\rho}}{\rho}:\int\frac{e^{-\frac{\beta|x-y|^{2}}{4s}}}{(4\pi\beta^{-1}s)^{\frac{d}{2}}}\overline{\nabla b}(x,y)h_{1}(s,y)dydx\\
-\frac{s\beta^{-1}}{2}\int\nabla\log\frac{\bar{\rho}}{\rho}\cdot\int\frac{e^{-\frac{\beta|x-y|^{2}}{4s}}}{(4\pi\beta^{-1}s)^{\frac{d}{2}}}\int_{0}^{1}(1-\lambda)\Delta b(\lambda y+(1-\lambda)x)d\lambda h_{1}(s,y)dydx.

We do integration by parts again for the first term in
I_{21}
, but for the index that taking contraction with the components of
b
, to have

I_{21}=\frac{s\beta^{-1}}{2}\int\nabla\log\frac{\bar{\rho}}{\rho}\cdot\int\frac{e^{-\frac{\beta|x-y|^{2}}{4s}}}{(4\pi\beta^{-1}s)^{\frac{d}{2}}}\int_{0}^{1}(1-\lambda)\nabla(\nabla\cdot b)(\lambda y+(1-\lambda)x)d\lambda h_{1}(s,y)dydx\\
+\frac{s\beta^{-1}}{2}\int\nabla\log\frac{\bar{\rho}}{\rho}\cdot\Big[\int\overline{\nabla b}(x,y)\cdot\nabla_{x}\frac{e^{-\frac{\beta|x-y|^{2}}{4s}}}{(4\pi\beta^{-1}s)^{\frac{d}{2}}}h_{1}(s,y)dy\Big]dx\\
-\frac{s\beta^{-1}}{2}\int\nabla\log\frac{\bar{\rho}}{\rho}\cdot\int\frac{e^{-\frac{\beta|x-y|^{2}}{4s}}}{(4\pi\beta^{-1}s)^{\frac{d}{2}}}\int_{0}^{1}(1-\lambda)\Delta b(\lambda y+(1-\lambda)x)d\lambda h_{1}(s,y)dydx.

Then, we change
\nabla_{x}
in the second term above to
-\nabla_{y}
and do integration by parts and have

I_{21}=\frac{s\beta^{-1}}{2}\int\nabla\log\frac{\bar{\rho}}{\rho}\cdot\int\frac{e^{-\frac{\beta|x-y|^{2}}{4s}}}{(4\pi\beta^{-1}s)^{\frac{d}{2}}}\overline{\nabla(\nabla\cdot b)}(x,y)h_{1}(s,y)dydx\\
+\frac{s\beta^{-1}}{2}\int\nabla\log\frac{\bar{\rho}}{\rho}\cdot\Big[\int\overline{\nabla b}(x,y)\frac{e^{-\frac{\beta|x-y|^{2}}{4s}}}{(4\pi\beta^{-1}s)^{\frac{d}{2}}}\cdot\nabla h_{1}(s,y)dy\Big]dx\\
-\frac{s\beta^{-1}}{2}\int\nabla\log\frac{\bar{\rho}}{\rho}\cdot\int\frac{e^{-\frac{\beta|x-y|^{2}}{4s}}}{(4\pi\beta^{-1}s)^{\frac{d}{2}}}\int_{0}^{1}(1-\lambda)\Delta b(\lambda y+(1-\lambda)x)d\lambda h_{1}(s,y)dydx,

where similarly

\displaystyle\overline{\nabla(\nabla\cdot b)}(x,y):=\int_{0}^{1}\nabla(\nabla\cdot b)(\lambda y+(1-\lambda)x)d\lambda.

(C.5)

Direct integration by parts for
I_{22}
gives

I_{22}=\frac{s\beta^{-1}}{2}\int\nabla\log\frac{\bar{\rho}}{\rho}\cdot\int\frac{e^{-\frac{\beta|x-y|^{2}}{4s}}}{(4\pi\beta^{-1}s)^{\frac{d}{2}}}\int_{0}^{1}\lambda\Delta b(\lambda y+(1-\lambda)x)d\lambda h_{1}(s,y)dydx\\
+\frac{s\beta^{-1}}{2}\int\nabla\log\frac{\bar{\rho}}{\rho}\cdot\int\frac{e^{-\frac{\beta|x-y|^{2}}{4s}}}{(4\pi\beta^{-1}s)^{\frac{d}{2}}}\overline{\nabla b}(x,y)^{T}\cdot\nabla h_{1}(s,y)dydx.

Adding all the above up, we arrive at the expressions in (

).

C.2
Estimates of
E_{1}

By the formulas above, one has
E_{1}=E_{11}+E_{12}
, where the main term
E_{11}
is

E_{11}(s,x)=\frac{\nabla_{x}\phi_{-s}(x)-(\nabla_{x}\phi_{-s}(x))^{-T}}{s}\cdot\left(\int\frac{\exp(-\frac{\beta|\phi_{-s}(x)-y|^{2}}{4s})}{(4\pi\beta^{-1}s)^{d/2}}\nabla\bar{\rho}_{n}(y)\,dy\right)\\
+\int\frac{e^{-\frac{\beta|x-y|^{2}}{4s}}}{(4\pi\beta^{-1}s)^{\frac{d}{2}}}[\overline{\nabla b}(x,y)+\overline{\nabla b}(x,y)^{T}]\cdot\nabla\bar{\rho}_{n}(\phi_{-s}(y))J(-s,y)dy,

while the extra remainder is

+\int\frac{e^{-\frac{\beta|x-y|^{2}}{4s}}}{(4\pi\beta^{-1}s)^{\frac{d}{2}}}[\overline{\nabla b}(x,y)+\overline{\nabla b}(x,y)^{T}]\cdot(\nabla\phi_{-s}(y)-I)\cdot\nabla\bar{\rho}_{n}(\phi_{-s}(y))J(-s,y)dy\\
+\int\frac{e^{-\frac{\beta|x-y|^{2}}{4s}}}{(4\pi\beta^{-1}s)^{\frac{d}{2}}}[\overline{\nabla b}(x,y)+\overline{\nabla b}(x,y)^{T}]\cdot\bar{\rho}_{n}(\phi_{-s}(y))\nabla J(-s,y)dy.

Estimate of the remainder

Since
\partial_{i}J/J=\mathrm{tr}(\nabla\phi^{-1}\partial_{i}\nabla\phi)
, it is easy to see by the assumptions on
b
that

\|\nabla J/J\|_{\infty}\leq Cs.

where
C
is uniformly bounded if we require
|s|\leq 1
.
Hence, the last term in
E_{12}
is simply controlled by
Ce^{s\mathcal{L}_{\xi(2)}}e^{s\mathcal{L}_{\xi(1)}}\bar{\rho_{n}}s
.

Moreover, for
|s|\leq 1
, it is also easy to see that

|\nabla\phi_{-s}(y)-I|\leq C|s|,\quad|J(-s,x)-1|\leq Cs,|\frac{\nabla_{x}\phi_{-s}(x)-(\nabla_{x}\phi_{-s}(x))^{-T}}{s}|\leq C.

Hence, the first two terms are controlled by

|E_{12}(s,x)|\leq Cs\int\frac{e^{-\frac{\beta|\phi_{-s}(x)-y|^{2}}{4s}}}{(4\pi\beta^{-1}s)^{\frac{d}{2}}}|\nabla\log\bar{\rho}_{n}(y)|\bar{\rho}_{n}(y)\,dy\\
+Cs\int\frac{e^{-\frac{\beta|x-y|^{2}}{4s}}}{(4\pi\beta^{-1}s)^{\frac{d}{2}}}|\nabla\log\bar{\rho}_{n}(\phi_{-s}(y))|\bar{\rho}_{n}(\phi_{-s}(y))J(-s,y)dy.

These two terms are similar. We take the latter one as the example to show how to control.
By Lemma
4.1
and Theorem
4.2
, we find that (for
|s|\leq 1
)

|\nabla\log\bar{\rho}_{n}(\phi_{-s}(y))|\leq C(1+|\phi_{-s}(y)|^{p})\leq C(1+|y|^{p}+|b(x)|^{p}|s|^{p})\leq C(1+|x|^{p}+|x-y|^{p}),

where the concrete value of
C
has been changed.
Hence,

\int\frac{e^{-\frac{\beta|x-y|^{2}}{4s}}}{(4\pi\beta^{-1}s)^{\frac{d}{2}}}|\nabla\log\bar{\rho}_{n}(\phi_{-s}(y))|\bar{\rho}_{n}(\phi_{-s}(y))J(-s,y)dy\leq\\
C(1+|x|^{p})e^{s\mathcal{L}_{\xi(2)}}e^{s\mathcal{L}_{\xi(1)}}\bar{\rho}_{n}(x)+\int\frac{e^{-\frac{\beta|x-y|^{2}}{4s}}}{(4\pi\beta^{-1}s)^{\frac{d}{2}}}|x-y|^{p}\bar{\rho}_{n}(\phi_{-s}(y))J(-s,y)dy

For the latter, take

\bar{R}=\beta^{-1/2}\sqrt{8s}\max\left\{1,\sqrt{|\log\bar{\rho}|}\right\}.

One then has

\int_{|x-y|\leq\bar{R}}\frac{e^{-\frac{\beta|x-y|^{2}}{4s}}}{(4\pi\beta^{-1}s)^{\frac{d}{2}}}|x-y|^{p}\bar{\rho}_{n}(\phi_{-s}(y))J(-s,y)dy\leq\bar{R}^{p}e^{s\mathcal{L}_{\xi(2)}}e^{s\mathcal{L}_{\xi(1)}}\bar{\rho}_{n}(x)

while for
|x-y|\geq\bar{R}
,
\exp(-\frac{\beta|x-y|^{2}}{8s})|x-y|^{p}\leq C_{p}(s/\beta)^{p/2}\exp(-\frac{\beta|x-y|^{2}}{16s})

for some
C_{p}
independent of
s
and
x,y
so that

\displaystyle\begin{split}&\int_{|x-y|\geq\bar{R}}\frac{e^{-\frac{\beta|x-y|^{2}}{4s}}}{(4\pi\beta^{-1}s)^{\frac{d}{2}}}|x-y|^{p}\bar{\rho}_{n}(\phi_{-s}(y))J(-s,y)dy\\
\leq&C(s/\beta)^{p/2}\exp(-\frac{\beta\bar{R}^{2}}{8s})\int\frac{e^{-\frac{\beta|x-y|^{2}}{16s}}}{(4\pi\beta^{-1}s)^{\frac{d}{2}}}\bar{\rho}_{n}(\phi_{-s}(y))J(-s,y)dy.\end{split}

(C.6)

Noting that

\exp(-\frac{\beta\bar{R}^{2}}{8s})\leq\exp(-|\log\bar{\rho}|)\leq\min\{1,\bar{\rho}\},

and the fact that
\bar{\rho}_{n}
is bounded for
t\leq T
, we find that

|E_{12}(s,x)|\leq Cs(1+|x|^{p})e^{s\mathcal{L}_{\xi(2)}}e^{s\mathcal{L}_{\xi(1)}}\bar{\rho_{n}}+Cs(s/\beta)^{p/2}\max\{1,|\log\bar{\rho}|^{p/2}\}e^{s\mathcal{L}_{\xi(2)}}e^{s\mathcal{L}_{\xi(1)}}\bar{\rho_{n}}\\
+Cs(s/\beta)^{p/2}\min\{1,\bar{\rho}(t,x)\}\leq Cs(1+|x|^{p^{\prime}})\bar{\rho}(t,x),

where we used that
|\log\bar{\rho}|
has polynomial growth.

Estimate of the main term

To estimate the main terms in
E_{11}
, we first do change of variables
y^{\prime}=\phi_{-s}(y)
and split
E_{11}:=M_{1}+M_{2}
, with

M_{1}=\frac{\nabla_{x}\phi_{-s}(x)-(\nabla_{x}\phi_{-s}(x))^{-T}}{s}\cdot\\
\int\frac{1}{(4\pi\beta^{-1}s)^{d/2}}\left[\exp(-\frac{\beta|\phi_{-s}(x)-y|^{2}}{4s})-\exp(-\frac{\beta|x-\phi_{s}(y)|^{2}}{4s})\right]\nabla\bar{\rho}_{n}(y)\,dy

and

M_{2}=\int\frac{e^{-\frac{\beta|x-\phi_{s}(y)|^{2}}{4s}}}{(4\pi\beta^{-1}s)^{\frac{d}{2}}}\\
\left([\overline{\nabla b}(x,\phi_{s}(y))+\overline{\nabla b}(x,\phi_{s}(y))^{T}]+\frac{\nabla_{x}\phi_{-s}(x)-(\nabla_{x}\phi_{-s}(x))^{-T}}{s}\right)\cdot\nabla\bar{\rho}_{n}(y)dy

For
M_{1}
, by Lemma
4.1
, we find that

||x-\phi_{s}(y)|^{2}-|\phi_{-s}(x)-y|^{2}|\leq C|x-\phi_{s}(y)|^{2}s.

Since

\exp(-\frac{\beta|\phi_{-s}(x)-y|^{2}}{4s})-\exp(-\frac{\beta|x-\phi_{s}(y)|^{2}}{4s})\\
=\exp(-\frac{\beta|x-\phi_{s}(y)|^{2}}{4s})\left[\exp(\frac{\beta[|x-\phi_{s}(y)|^{2}-|\phi_{-s}(x)-y|^{2}]}{4s})-1\right]\\
=\exp(-\frac{\beta|\phi_{-s}(x)-y|^{2}}{4s})(1-\exp(\frac{\beta[|\phi_{-s}(x)-y|^{2}-|x-\phi_{s}(y)|^{2}]}{4s})).

If we simply bound

\exp(\frac{\beta[|x-\phi_{s}(y)|^{2}-|\phi_{-s}(x)-y|^{2}]}{4s})-1\leq C\exp(C|x-\phi_{s}(y)|^{2})|x-\phi_{s}(y)|^{2},

we would have trouble to deal with the extra exponential term here. To resolve this, we note that
|e^{u}-1|\leq|u|
for
u<1
. Hence, we may choose the different bounds for different cases. In particular, if
|x-\phi_{s}(y)|^{2}-|\phi_{-s}(x)-y|^{2}\leq 0
, we use the bound

|\exp(-\frac{\beta|\phi_{-s}(x)-y|^{2}}{4s})-\exp(-\frac{\beta|x-\phi_{s}(y)|^{2}}{4s})|\leq C\exp(-\frac{\beta|x-\phi_{s}(y)|^{2}}{4s})|x-\phi_{s}(y)|^{2}

Otherwise, we use the bound

C\exp\left(-\frac{\beta|\phi_{-s}(x)-y|^{2}}{4s}\right)|x-\phi_{s}(y)|^{2}.

Hence, we find

M_{1}\leq C\int\frac{1}{(4\pi\beta^{-1}s)^{d/2}}\left[\exp\left(-\frac{\beta|x-\phi_{s}(y)|^{2}}{4s}\right)+\exp\left(-\frac{\beta|\phi_{-s}(x)-y|^{2}}{4s}\right)\right]\\
|x-\phi_{s}(y)|^{2}|\nabla\log\bar{\rho}_{n}(y)|\bar{\rho}_{n}(y)\,dy

Similar as above, we use the bound for
s\leq 1
:

|\nabla\log\bar{\rho}_{n}(y)|\leq C(1+|y|^{p})\leq C(1+|\phi_{s}(y)|^{p})\leq C(1+|x|^{p}+|x-\phi_{s}(y)|^{p}).

For
|x-\phi_{s}(y)|\leq\bar{R}
, we find

\int_{|x-\phi_{s}(y)|\leq\bar{R}}\frac{|x-\phi_{s}(y)|^{2}|\nabla\log\bar{\rho}_{n}(y)|\bar{\rho}_{n}(y)}{(4\pi\beta^{-1}s)^{d/2}}\left[\exp\left(-\frac{\beta|x-\phi_{s}(y)|^{2}}{4s}\right)+\exp\left(-\frac{\beta|\phi_{-s}(x)-y|^{2}}{4s}\right)\right]\,dy\\
\leq C\beta^{-1}s[(1+|x|^{p})(1+|\log\bar{\rho}|)+(1+|\log\bar{\rho}|)^{p/2}][e^{s\mathcal{L}_{\xi(1)}}e^{s\mathcal{L}_{\xi(2)}}+e^{s\mathcal{L}_{\xi(2)}}e^{s\mathcal{L}_{\xi(1)}}]\bar{\rho}_{n}\\
\leq Cs(1+|x|^{p^{\prime}})\bar{\rho}(t,x),

where we used
[e^{s\mathcal{L}_{\xi(1)}}e^{s\mathcal{L}_{\xi(2)}}+e^{s\mathcal{L}_{\xi(2)}}e^{s\mathcal{L}_{\xi(1)}}]\bar{\rho}_{n}=2\bar{\rho}
and that
|\log\bar{\rho}|
has polynomial growth.

For
|x-\phi_{s}(y)|\geq\bar{R}
, the estimate is similar to (
C.6
)
and we find

\begin{split}&\int_{|x-\phi_{s}(y)|\geq\bar{R}}\frac{|x-\phi_{s}(y)|^{2}|\nabla\log\bar{\rho}_{n}(y)|\bar{\rho}_{n}(y)}{(4\pi\beta^{-1}s)^{d/2}}\left[\exp\left(-\frac{\beta|x-\phi_{s}(y)|^{2}}{4s}\right)+\exp\left(-\frac{\beta|\phi_{-s}(x)-y|^{2}}{4s}\right)\right]\,dy\\
\leq&Cs\min\{1,\bar{\rho}(t,x)\}(1+|x|^{p}).\end{split}

For
M_{2}
, we first note that

\nabla_{x}\phi_{-s}(x)^{-T}=\nabla\phi_{s}(\phi_{-s}(x))^{T}
, and thus

\frac{\nabla_{x}\phi_{-s}(x)-(\nabla_{x}\phi_{-s}(x))^{-T}}{s}=-\nabla b(x)-\nabla b(x)^{T}+B(s,x)s.

with
|B(s,x)|\leq C(1+|x|)
. The reminder term in

is treated similarly as before.
The main term becomes

\tilde{M}_{2}=\int\frac{e^{-\frac{\beta|x-\phi_{s}(y)|^{2}}{4s}}}{(4\pi\beta^{-1}s)^{\frac{d}{2}}}\left([\overline{\nabla b}(x,\phi_{s}(y))+\overline{\nabla b}(x,\phi_{s}(y))^{T}]-\nabla b(x)-\nabla b(x)^{T}\right)\cdot\nabla\bar{\rho}_{n}(y)dy

The difference will basically give
(y-x)
which makes the
y
derivative of the Gaussian kernel times
\beta^{-1}s
. Integration by parts, we find that

\displaystyle\tilde{M}_{2}\leq C(\|\Delta\nabla b\|_{\infty}+|\nabla^{2}b|_{\infty})s\int\frac{e^{-\frac{\beta|x-\phi_{s}(y)|^{2}}{4s}}}{(4\pi\beta^{-1}s)^{\frac{d}{2}}}(|\nabla\bar{\rho}_{n}(y)|+|\nabla^{2}\bar{\rho}_{n}(y)|)dy.

Using the polynomial growth of
