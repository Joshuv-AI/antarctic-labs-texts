Introduction

We denote by
\overline{\mathbb{Q}}
the field of algebraic numbers and by

Let us consider a non-trivial algebraic differential equation

A natural question arises: is it possible to bound

As mentioned before, Sibuya and Sperber proved an important lemma that leads to the same conclusion as our Lemma
1
. Starting from a solution
f=\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}f_{n}x^{n}\in\overline{\mathbb{Q}}[[x]]
of a non-trivial differential algebraic equation
Q(x,y,\ldots,y^{(k)})=0
with coefficientsin
\overline{\mathbb{Q}}
, they first consider a non-trivial differential equation
\widetilde{Q}(x,y,\ldots,y^{(\ell)})=0
with
\widetilde{Q}\in\overline{\mathbb{Q}}[X,Y_{0},\ldots,Y_{\ell}]
of which
f
is still a solution. They require that
\ell\geq 1
is minimal amongst all differential algebraic equations satisfied by
f
, and that the degree of
\widetilde{Q}
in
Y_{\ell}
is also minimal. Notice that
\ell
is the transcendence degree of the field generated over
\overline{\mathbb{Q}}(x)
by
f
and all its derivatives.
This in particular ensures that

\frac{\partial\widetilde{Q}}{\partial Y_{\ell}}(x,f,\ldots,f^{(\ell)})\neq 0,

which is crucial. However, Sibuya and Sperber give no way to effectively compute
\ell
and to minimize the degree in
Y_{\ell}
starting from
f
and
Q
above.
They canonically attach to
\widetilde{Q}
and
f
a linear operator
L_{0}\in\overline{\mathbb{Q}}[[x]][\frac{d}{dx}]
of order
\ell
and then consider the indicial polynomial
P_{0}
at the origin of
L_{0}
. Then
[
23
, Lemma 2.2]
says the following:

For any
c\geq 0
, there exist integers
N^{\prime}\geq 0,N\geq N^{\prime},N^{\prime\prime}\geq c
such that

is a solution of a differential equation

L(u)=x^{N^{\prime\prime}}F(x,u,u^{\prime},\ldots,u^{(\ell)})
where
F\in\mathcal{O}_{\overline{\mathbb{Q}}}[X,Y_{0},\ldots,Y_{\ell}]
and
L
is a linear differential operator in
\mathcal{O}_{\overline{\mathbb{Q}}}[x][\frac{d}{dx}]
of order
\ell

and whose leading coefficient is independent of
N^{\prime\prime}
.

Moreover, the indicial polynomial
P(X)
at the origin of

is simply
P_{0}(X+N^{\prime})
; in particular, if
P_{0}
is split over
\mathbb{Q}
, then

is as well.
From the particular form of the equation satisfied by

, Sibuya and Sperber deduce a recurrence for
(f_{n})_{n\geq 0}
which is of the form (
1.4
) with
\sigma_{1}\geq 0
(provided

is large enough, which is possible because

is arbitrary) and with
M(X)=P_{0}(X+N^{\prime}+1)
. Therefore, this gives an interpretation of
M
in terms of the indicial polynomial
P_{0}
attached to
\widetilde{Q}
and
f
. It is important to observe that
P_{0}
may be different for another solution in
\overline{\mathbb{Q}}[[x]]
of the differential equation
Q(x,y,\ldots,y^{(k)})=0
.

Sibuya and Sperber also drew a few consequences from their lemma in
[

, p. 384]
(encompassed by our Theorem
1
) when

is a constant, when
0
is an ordinary point of

, and when

(which happens when

is linear in

). In the latter case, they recovered Popken’s upper bound for the denominator of
f_{n}
in the linear case; this is in fact equivalent to what Mahler had done in
[
15
, p. 205]
.

2.2.
The main result

We are now in the position to state our main result, Theorem
2
below, which together with Lemma
1
immediately implies Theorem
1
. Indeed, the denominator

for all

for suitable positive integers

and

divides

of

divides
\delta^{n+1}(\nu n+\nu)!^{2s}
for all

.

To prove Corollary
1
, let

be a place of
\overline{\mathbb{Q}}
over a rational prime number

. From the divisibility in Theorem
1
, we deduce that

|f_{n}|_{v}\leq p^{v_{p}(\delta^{n+1})+v_{p}((\nu n+\nu)!^{2s})}\leq p^{v_{p}(\delta)(n+1)+\frac{2s\nu}{p-1}(n+1)},

because by Legendre’s formula
[
12
, p. 10]

for the

i.e.
,

v_{p}(n!)=\sum_{\ell\geq 1}\left\lfloor\frac{n}{p^{\ell}}\right\rfloor,

we have

v_{p}((\nu n+\nu)!^{2s})\leq 2s\sum_{\ell=1}^{\infty}\frac{\nu n+\nu}{p^{\ell}}=\frac{2s\nu(n+1)}{p-1}.

This proves (

).

The proof of Theorem
2
requires a technical auxiliary result which is established separately in Proposition
1
below. We emphasize that Theorem

applies to more general situations than the differential context behind Theorem

. Indeed, the only assumption we make below on the coefficients

3.1.
The linear case

The material in this subsection is well-known. We recall it for the reader’s convenience and also because it provides a simple interpretation of the condition that

A linear differential equation

Let us now assume that

3.3.

We now illustrate our method with three classical series solutions of Riccati equations.

\sqrt{1/\wp(x)}

y^{\prime\prime}+2y^{3}=0
.

3.5.
Painlevé equations

Painlevé equations produce a lot of interesting examples.
(We refer the reader to
[
3
]
for an introduction.)
In this subsection, we only consider the family of algebraic differential equations known as Painlevé PII’:

y^{\prime\prime}=\delta(2y^{3}-2xy)+\gamma(6y^{2}+x)+\beta y+\alpha,

where it is assumed that
\alpha,\beta,\gamma,\delta
are in
\overline{\mathbb{Q}}
. It is a simple task to check that
u(x):=\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}u_{n}x^{n}
is a solution of PII’, where the sequence
(u_{n})_{n\geq 0}
is given by

u_{n+1}=\frac{1}{n(n+1)}\Big(2\delta\sum_{\underset{0\leq j_{1},j_{2},j_{3}\leq n-1}{j_{1}+j_{2}+j_{3}=n-1}}u_{j_{1}}u_{j_{2}}u_{j_{3}}+6\gamma\sum_{\underset{0\leq j_{1},j_{2}\leq n-1}{j_{1}+j_{2}=n-1}}u_{j_{1}}u_{j_{2}}-2\beta u_{n-1}-2\delta u_{n-2}\Big),\;n\geq 2,

with
u_{0}
,
u_{1}
arbitrary in
\overline{\mathbb{Q}}
, and
u_{2}=-\delta u_{0}^{3}-3\gamma u_{0}^{2}-\beta u_{0}/2-\alpha/2
. This is a situation where Theorem
2
with
s=2
,
a_{1}=a_{2}=b_{1}=1
,
b_{2}=0
,
k_{0}=3
,
\sigma_{1}=1
,
\sigma_{2}=2
,
N=2
,
\Pi=1
, and
C
the common denominator of
2\delta,6\gamma,2\beta
, applies.
Letting
D\geq 1
denote the least common denominator of
u_{0},u_{1},u_{2}
, we have

C^{n}D^{2n+1}(n-1)!\,n!^{3}\,u_{n}\in\mathbb{Z},\quad n\geq 2.

3.6.
Kepler’s equation

Kepler’s equation
from Celestial Mechanics is

E-e\sin(E)=M,

where
E,M,e
are the eccentric anomaly, the mean anomaly, and the eccentricity, respectively. Inventing for the purpose of solving this equation what is now known as
Lagrange’s inversion formula
, Lagrange
[
11
]
found the well-known expansion

\varphi(e):=E-M=\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\Big(\frac{d}{dt}\Big)^{n-1}\big(\sin(t)^{n}\big)_{t=M}\cdot\frac{e^{n}}{n!},

(3.7)

which is an odd and
2\pi
-periodic function of
M
, and which converges in the disk
|e|<e_{0}
for all
M
. Here,
e_{0}=2\sqrt{\rho(1-\rho)}/(1-2\rho)\approx 0.6627
, where
\rho\approx 0.0832
satisfies
{1/\rho-1}=\exp(2/(1-2\rho))
; see the details in
[
21
]
.

It is easy to prove that

(\sin^{n}(t))^{(n-1)}=P_{n}\big(\sin(t),\cos(t)\big),

where
P_{n}(X,Y)\in\mathbb{Z}[X,Y]
is of degree
\leq n
in
X
and
\leq 1
in
Y
. Therefore, when
\sin(M)\in\overline{\mathbb{Q}}
(hence
\cos(M)\in\overline{\mathbb{Q}}
as well), there exists a positive integer
\delta
such that, for all
n\geq 1
,

\delta^{n+1}\Big(\frac{d}{dt}\Big)^{n-1}\big(\sin^{n}(t)\big)_{t=M}\in\mathcal{O}_{\overline{\mathbb{Q}}}.

Hence, from (
3.7
), we deduce in this case that the denominator of the
n
-th Taylor coefficient of
\varphi
at the origin divides
\delta^{n+1}n!
.

We now want to use this example to show how to effectively determine a recurrence like (
1.5
) in a more complex situation than in the previous examples. We change
e
to
x
and let
\varphi(x)=E-M
. Kepler’s equation reads
\sin(\varphi+M)=\varphi/x
, with
\varphi(0)=0
and
\varphi^{\prime}(0)=\sin(M)
. Differentiating this equation with respect to
x
and squaring both sides, we find

\varphi^{\prime 2}(1-(\varphi/x)^{2})=((\varphi/x)^{\prime})^{2},

(3.8)

or, in expanded form,

x^{2}(x^{2}-1)\varphi^{\prime 2}-\varphi^{2}+2x\varphi^{\prime}\varphi-x^{2}\varphi^{\prime 2}\varphi^{2}=0.

(3.9)

Let
\varphi(x)=\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\phi_{n}x^{n}
. We have
\phi_{0}=0
,
\phi_{1}=\sin(M)
, and
\phi_{2}=\sin(M)\cos(M)
. We shall assume below that
\phi_{2}\neq 0
, which
a fortiori
implies that
\phi_{1}\neq 0
. From the algebraic differential equation (
3.9
), we deduce that, for
n\geq 5
, we have

\sum_{\underset{0\leq j_{1},j_{2}\leq n-4}{j_{1}+j_{2}=n-4}}(j_{1}+1)(j_{2}+1)\phi_{j_{1}+1}\phi_{j_{2}+1}-\sum_{\underset{0\leq j_{1},j_{2}\leq n-2}{j_{1}+j_{2}=n-2}}(j_{1}+1)(j_{2}+1)\phi_{j_{1}+1}\phi_{j_{2}+1}-\sum_{\underset{0\leq j_{1},j_{2}\leq n}{j_{1}+j_{2}=n}}\phi_{j_{1}}\phi_{j_{2}}\\
+\sum_{\underset{0\leq j_{1},j_{2}\leq n-1}{j_{1}+j_{2}=n-1}}2(j_{1}+1)\phi_{j_{1}+1}\phi_{j_{2}}-\sum_{\underset{0\leq j_{1},j_{2},j_{3},j_{4}\leq n-2}{j_{1}+j_{2}+j_{3}+j_{4}=n-2}}(j_{1}+1)(j_{2}+1)\phi_{j_{1}+1}\phi_{j_{2}+1}\phi_{j_{3}}\phi_{j_{4}}=0,

which can be rewritten as

\sum_{\underset{0\leq j_{1},j_{2}\leq n-3}{j_{1}+j_{2}=n-2}}j_{1}j_{2}\phi_{j_{1}}\phi_{j_{2}}-\sum_{\underset{0\leq j_{1},j_{2}\leq n-1}{j_{1}+j_{2}=n}}j_{1}j_{2}\phi_{j_{1}}\phi_{j_{2}}-\sum_{\underset{0\leq j_{1},j_{2}\leq n}{j_{1}+j_{2}=n}}\phi_{j_{1}}\phi_{j_{2}}\\
+\sum_{\underset{0\leq j_{1}\leq n,0\leq j_{2}\leq n-1}{j_{1}+j_{2}=n}}2j_{1}\phi_{j_{1}}\phi_{j_{2}}-\sum_{\underset{0\leq j_{1},j_{2}\leq n-1,0\leq j_{3},j_{4}\leq n-2}{j_{1}+j_{2}+j_{3}+j_{4}=n}}j_{1}j_{2}\phi_{j_{1}}\phi_{j_{2}}\phi_{j_{3}}\phi_{j_{4}}=0.

This is equivalent to

\sum_{\underset{0\leq j_{1},j_{2}\leq n}{j_{1}+j_{2}=n-2}}P_{1}(n,j_{1},j_{2})\phi_{j_{1}}\phi_{j_{2}}+\sum_{\underset{0\leq j_{1},j_{2}\leq n}{j_{1}+j_{2}=n}}P_{2}(n,j_{1},j_{2})\phi_{j_{1}}\phi_{j_{2}}\\
+\sum_{\underset{0\leq j_{1},j_{2},j_{3},j_{4}\leq n}{j_{1}+j_{2}+j_{3}+j_{4}=n}}P_{3}(n,j_{1},j_{2},j_{3},j_{4})\phi_{j_{1}}\phi_{j_{2}}\phi_{j_{3}}\phi_{j_{4}}=0,

where

P_{1}(n,j_{1},j_{2})=j_{1}j_{2}{\bf 1}_{\{0\leq j_{1},j_{2}\leq n-3\}},

P_{2}(n,j_{1},j_{2})=-j_{1}j_{2}{\bf 1}_{\{0\leq j_{1},j_{2}\leq n-1\}}+2j_{1}{\bf 1}_{\{0\leq j_{2}\leq n-1\}}-1,

P_{3}(n,j_{1},j_{2},j_{3},j_{4})=-j_{1}j_{2}{\bf 1}_{\{0\leq j_{1},j_{2}\leq n-1,0\leq j_{3},j_{4}\leq n-2\}}.

This is an algebraic relation between
\phi_{0},\phi_{1},\ldots,\phi_{n}
. A simple analysis shows that neither
\phi_{n}
nor
\phi_{n-1}
appear in this relation. Indeed, in the three sums,
\phi_{n}
can appear only in terms involving
\phi_{n}\phi_{0}
or
\phi_{n}\phi_{0}^{3}
, which are equal to 0 because
\phi_{0}=0
. The coefficient
\phi_{n-1}
does not appear in the first sum, appears in the second as
(P_{2}(n,n-1,1)+P_{2}(n,1,n-1))\phi_{1}\phi_{n-1}=0
because
P_{2}(n,n-1,1)+P_{2}(n,1,n-1)=0
, and in the third sum in terms of the form
\phi_{n-1}\phi_{1}\phi_{0}\phi_{0}=0
. The coefficient
\phi_{n-2}
does not appear in the first sum, appears in terms involving
\phi_{n-2}\phi_{1}^{2}\phi_{0}=0
and
\phi_{n-2}\phi_{2}\phi_{0}^{2}=0
in the third sum, and in the second sum,
\phi_{n-2}
appears in the expression
(P_{2}(n,n-2,2)+P_{2}(n,2,n-2))\phi_{2}\phi_{n-2}=-2(n+1)\phi_{2}\phi_{n-2}
. Therefore, since we have assumed that
\phi_{2}\neq 0
, we finally obtain the recurrence (after changing
n
to
n+3
)

\phi_{n+1}=\frac{1}{2\phi_{2}(n+4)}\bigg(\sum_{\underset{0\leq j_{1},j_{2}\leq n}{j_{1}+j_{2}=n+1}}P_{1}(n+3,j_{1},j_{2})\phi_{j_{1}}\phi_{j_{2}}+\sum_{\underset{0\leq j_{1},j_{2}\leq n}{j_{1}+j_{2}=n+3}}P_{2}(n+3,j_{1},j_{2})\phi_{j_{1}}\phi_{j_{2}}\\
+\sum_{\underset{0\leq j_{1},j_{2},j_{3},j_{4}\leq n}{j_{1}+j_{2}+j_{3}+j_{4}=n+3}}P_{3}(n+3,j_{1},j_{2},j_{3},j_{4})\phi_{j_{1}}\phi_{j_{2}}\phi_{j_{3}}\phi_{j_{4}}\bigg),\quad n\geq 2.

This is a recurrence of the form (
1.5
) (with negative
\sigma_{1}
), to which we can apply Theorem
1
because
M(X)\in\mathbb{Q}[X]
is linear. We do not provide the details of the explicit result, which is of the form
\widetilde{\delta}^{n+1}(\nu n+\nu)!^{2}
for some integers
\widetilde{\delta},\nu\geq 1
, because it is weaker than the already obtained divisibility of
\delta^{n+1}n!
by the denominator of
\phi_{n}
.

Finally, instead of
\varphi
, we could consider
\psi(x):=\varphi(x)/x=\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\phi_{n+1}x^{n}
. The differential equation (
3.8
) becomes

(x\psi)^{\prime 2}(1-\psi^{2})=(\psi^{\prime})^{2}

or, in expanded form,

(1-x)\psi^{\prime 2}+2x\psi^{\prime}\psi^{3}+x^{2}\psi^{\prime 2}\psi^{2}-2x\psi^{\prime}\psi+\psi^{4}-\psi^{2}=0.

This would lead to an
a priori
different recurrence for the sequence
(\phi_{n})_{n\geq 0}
.

3.7.
The compositional inverse of the dilogarithm

The dilogarithm is
\textup{Li}_{2}(x):=\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}x^{n}/n^{2}
, with derivative
-\log(1-x)/x
, and it admits a power series
\ell
as inverse for the composition:

{\ell}(x):=\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\ell_{n}x^{n}=x-\frac{1}{4}x^{2}+\frac{1}{72}x^{3}-\frac{1}{576}x^{4}-\frac{31}{86400}x^{5}-\frac{149}{1036800}x^{6}-\cdots.

The function
\ell
is DA, for it satisfies

\ell^{\prime\prime}\ell-\ell^{\prime\prime}\ell^{2}+\ell^{\prime 3}+\ell^{\prime 2}\ell-\ell^{\prime 2}=0.

(3.10)

Indeed, we have
1=(\textup{Li}_{2}(\ell))^{\prime}=-\ell^{\prime}\log(1-\ell)/\ell
, so that, by multiplying by
\ell
and then differentiating both sides, we obtain

\ell^{\prime}=-\ell^{\prime\prime}\log(1-\ell)+\frac{\ell^{\prime 2}}{1-\ell}=\frac{\ell^{\prime\prime}\ell}{\ell^{\prime}}+\frac{\ell^{\prime 2}}{1-\ell},

which gives the above differential equation after some rearrangement. The situation is a little simpler than with Kepler’s equation because (
3.10
) is autonomous,
i.e.
, its coefficients are independent of
x
. For all
n\geq 0
, we have

\displaystyle[x^{n}](\ell^{\prime\prime}\ell)

\displaystyle=\sum_{i+j=n}(i+1)(i+2)\ell_{i+2}\ell_{j},

(3.11)

\displaystyle[x^{n}](-\ell^{\prime\prime}\ell^{2})

\displaystyle=-\sum_{i+j+k=n}(i+1)(i+2)\ell_{i+2}\ell_{j}f_{k},

\displaystyle[x^{n}](\ell^{\prime 3})

\displaystyle=\sum_{i+j+k=n}(i+1)(j+1)(k+1)\ell_{i+1}\ell_{j+1}\ell_{k+1},

(3.12)

\displaystyle[x^{n}](\ell^{\prime 2}\ell)

\displaystyle=\sum_{i+j+k=n}(i+1)(j+1)\ell_{i+1}\ell_{j+1}\ell_{k},

\displaystyle[x^{n}](-\ell^{\prime 2})

\displaystyle=-\sum_{i+j=n}(i+1)(j+1)\ell_{i+1}\ell_{j+1},

(3.13)

where in the summations all the indices run between 0 and
n
, and where as usual
[x^{n}](f)
denotes the
n
-th Taylor coefficient of a power series
f
. Proceeding as in §
3.6
, we see that
\ell_{n+2}
appears in none of these sums because it is always multiplied by
\ell_{0}=0
. The term
\ell_{n+1}
appears only in (
3.11
), (
3.12
), and in (
3.13
), with the coefficients
n(n+1)\ell_{1}
,
3(n+1)\ell_{1}^{2}
, and
-2(n+1)\ell_{1}
, respectively. This is
\ell_{1}(n+1)(n+3\ell_{1}-2)=(n+1)^{2}
in total. Therefore, we have

\ell_{n+1}=\frac{1}{(n+1)^{2}}\Big(-\sum_{\underset{i\leq n-2,j\leq n}{i+j=n}}(i+1)(i+2)\ell_{i+2}\ell_{j}+\sum_{\underset{i\leq n-2,j,k\leq n}{i+j+k=n}}(i+1)(i+2)\ell_{i+2}\ell_{j}\ell_{k}\\
-\sum_{\underset{i,j,k\leq n-1}{i+j+k=n}}(i+1)(j+1)(k+1)\ell_{i+1}\ell_{j+1}\ell_{k+1}-\sum_{\underset{i,j\leq n-1,k\leq n}{i+j+k=n}}(i+1)(j+1)\ell_{i+1}\ell_{j+1}\ell_{k}\\
+\sum_{\underset{i,j\leq n-1}{i+j=n}}(i+1)(j+1)\ell_{i+1}\ell_{j+1}\Big),\quad n\geq 0.

It follows that, for all
n\geq 0
,
\delta^{n+1}(\nu n+\nu)!^{4}\ell_{n}\in\mathbb{Z}
for some positive integers
\delta,\nu
. Numerical experiments suggest that the exponent
4
could be replaced by
2
. This would be true if it could be proved for instance that
\big(\ell(n)\big)_{n\geq 0}
satisfies a non-linear recurrence of type (
1.5
) like above but with
(n+1)^{2}
replaced by
an+b
for suitable integers
a
and
b
.

As a side remark, we point out that it is possible to determine the asymptotic behaviour of
\ell_{n}
. By Lagrange’s inversion formula
[
6
, p. 732, Theorem A.2]
, we have

\ell_{n}=\frac{1}{2i\pi n}\int_{\mathcal{C}}\frac{dz}{\textup{Li}_{2}(z)^{n}},\quad n\geq 0,

where
\mathcal{C}
is a circle centred at 0 and of sufficiently small radius. This circle can be deformed to a suitable Hankel-type contour to which the saddle point method can be applied (see
[
6
]
for similar computations). We obtain that

\ell_{n}\sim-\frac{\zeta(2)^{-n}}{n^{2}\log(n)^{2}},\quad n\to+\infty.

(3.14)

Hence, the radius of convergence of
\ell
at
x=0
is
\pi^{2}/6
, and
(\ell_{n}/\ell_{n+1})_{n\geq 0}
is a sequence of rational numbers that converge to
\pi^{2}/6
, but not especially quickly. The proof of (
3.14
) is given in our note
[
10
]
.

3.8.
Examples related to modular forms

Various automorphic functions, including certain classical modular forms, are known to satisfy algebraic differential equations of order at least 3 and of the form
Q(y)(y^{\prime})^{2}=\{y;x\}
, where
\{y;x\}:=y^{\prime\prime\prime}/y^{\prime}-\frac{3}{2}(y^{\prime\prime}/y^{\prime})^{2}
is the Schwarzian derivative with respect to
x
, and
Q
is a rational function with poles of order at most
2
. For instance, Mahler proved in
[
13
]
that the
elliptic modular invariant

F(q):=J(q^{2})=q^{-2}\Big(1+240\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}n^{3}q^{2n}(1-q^{2n})^{-1}\Big)^{3}\prod_{n=1}^{\infty}(1-q^{2n})^{-24}\\
=1/q^{2}+744+196884q^{2}+21493760q^{4}+864299970q^{6}+\cdots,

satisfies the order 3 equation

F^{\prime\prime\prime}=\frac{3q^{2}F^{\prime\prime 2}-4qF^{\prime}F^{\prime\prime}-F^{\prime 2}}{2q^{2}F^{\prime}}-F^{\prime 3}\Big(\frac{4}{9F^{2}}+\frac{3}{8(F-12^{3})^{2}}-\frac{23}{72F(F-12^{3})}\Big),

(3.15)

where the derivatives are taken with respect to
q
. He also proved that
F
cannot satisfy an algebraic differential equation of order
\leq 2
. Since the degree in
F^{\prime\prime\prime}
of (
3.15
) is 1, this equation is of the standard form to which we can apply the procedure described in
[
23
, p. 382]
. The indicial polynomial
P_{0}
of the associated differential operator
L_{0}\in\overline{\mathbb{Q}}[[x]][\frac{d}{dx}]
is
P_{0}(X)=X^{3}+8X^{2}-10X+64
, which has no rational root. Lemma 2.2 of
[
23
, p. 382]
then provides a non-linear recurrence of type (
1.4
) for the coefficients of
F
with
M(X)=P_{0}(X+m)
for some integer
m\geq 0
.
This recurrence is not of type (
1.5
), and our Theorems
1
and
2
cannot be applied to it. We do not know if there exists a recurrence of type (
1.5
) for the coefficients of
F
(which are well-known to be integers),
i.e.
, with
M
split over
\mathbb{Q}
. Let us also mention that a vast algebraico-geometric generalization of modular forms, known as mirror maps, are shown to be DA functions in
[
25
, Proposition 1]
.

3.9.
A simple example with
M
non split over
\mathbb{Q}

We conclude with an example for which
M
is non-split over
\mathbb{Q}
, and for which it is possible to make numerical experiments. The differential equation
x^{2}y^{\prime\prime}+(x-1)y^{\prime}+y-xy^{2}=1
has as solution the power series

\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}f_{n}x^{n}
with

f_{n+1}=\frac{1}{n^{2}+1}\sum_{k=0}^{n}f_{k}f_{n-k},\quad n\geq 0,\;f_{0}:=1.

Numerical experiments for values of
n
up to 2000 suggest that the denominator
d_{n}
of
f_{n}
is such that
\log(d_{n})/(n\log(n)^{2})
converges to a constant close to
0.566
. Moreover,

\frac{\log(d_{2n})}{2n\log(2n)}-\frac{\log(d_{n})}{n\log(n)}

seems to converge to a constant close to

0.39
, which tends to confirm that
\log(d_{n})/(n\log(n))
behaves more like
\log(n)
than like
\log\log(n)
.

Moreover, it seems that
\prod_{k=0}^{\lfloor n/2\rfloor}(k^{2}+1)
divides
2^{n}d_{n}
for all
n\geq 0
. If true, this conjecturally implies that there exist no integers
\delta,\nu,\mu,s\geq 0
such that
d_{n}
divides
\delta^{n+1}(\nu n+\mu)!^{s}
for all
n\geq 0
. Indeed, on the contrary, this would imply the same type of divisibility for
\prod_{k=0}^{n}(k^{2}+1)
, and we have explained in the introduction why this is not true conjecturally.

All these computations do not point towards a positive solution to Mahler’s denominator conjecture, but these experiments should be conducted for more values of
n
on this sequence and others in the non-split case to be more affirmative.
