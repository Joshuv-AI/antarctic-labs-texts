Genomic characterization and comparative analysis of a new Tequatrovirus phage infecting Escherichia coli ST131
Authors: Elsa Beurrier, Floriane Laumay, Juliette Hayer, Jacques Dainat, Mélanie Bonhomme, Mathieu Medina, Camille Kolenda, Richard Bonnet, Laurent Dortet, Frédéric Laurent, Rémy Froissart
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ObjectiveThe global rise of antimicrobial resistance with the spread of pathogenic multidrug-resistant clones such as Escherichia coli sequence type (ST) 131 represents a major challenge for public health, renewing interest in bacteriophages as therapeutical agents.ResultsWe report the isolation and characterization of a new bacteriophage, Escherichia phage vB_EcoM-V1EC45, which was isolated from wastewater and was able to infect a clinical E. coli ST131 strain. Its genome is a 170,364 bp double-stranded DNA molecule annotated with 281 coding sequences and 9 tRNAs, including multiple genes involved in antidefense systems. V1EC45 is predicted to harbour a virulent lifestyle. Comparative genomics positioned V1EC45 within the Tequatrovirus genus, and the Tsx outer membrane bacterial protein is predicted to be used as the receptor-binding protein. This work highlights the value of using genomic, structural, and evolutionary analyses to support the directed development of targeted bacteriophage therapeutics.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s13104-026-07849-9.

Introduction

Antimicrobial resistance (AMR) is a major global health challenge, causing more than 1.2 million deaths annually [1] and projected to exceed 10 million per year by 2050 [2]. Among resistant pathogens, Escherichia coli sequence type (ST) 131 has emerged as a pandemic multidrug-resistant clone, distributed worldwide in both community and hospital settings and responsible for a wide range of infections [3]. ST131 is dominated by the fluoroquinolone-resistant H30 lineage, including the H30-Rx subclone, and commonly carries ESBLs such as CTX-M-15 or CTX-M-27, which contribute to its success in both community and hospital settings [4]. Its combination of resistance and virulence determinants makes ST131 a major contributor to the global AMR threat.

Bacteriophages (phages) are being reconsidered as alternative or complementary therapeutics to antibiotics due to their lytic activity against specific bacterial hosts [5]. The genomic characterization of new phages infecting high-risk clones such as ST131 is thus of strategic interest for future phage therapy. Members of the Tequatrovirus genus (family Straboviridae) are especially promising because of their large genomes, environmental ubiquity, and their reported ability to infect a variety of E. coli strains [6]. They have been tested in clinical trials [7] and are known to rely on receptor-binding proteins (RBPs) interacting with bacterial surface structures such as outer membrane proteins or lipopolysaccharides [8].

In this study, we characterized a new phage designated as Escherichia phage vB_EcoM-V1EC45 that was extracted from wastewater samples and propagated on a clinical strain of E. coli ST131. We described its genomic architecture and anti-defense arsenal and, based on the sequence similarity of its receptor binding protein (gp38) compared to closely related Tequatroviruses, we predicted that Escherichia phage vB_EcoM-V1EC45 infects specifically its host after the recognition of the Tsx outer membrane protein.

Materials and methods

Bacteria and bacteriophages

Wastewater samples were collected at the inflow of the Neuville-sur-Saône (France) treatment plant in January 2022 (GPS: 45.868714, 4.83925). After 0.22 μm filtration, five milliliters of filtered wastewater samples were mixed with 10 µL of an overnight culture of a prophage-free strain of E. coli BLK16 described by Umenhoffer et al. [9] and 500 µL of 10X Tryptic Soy Broth (TSB) and incubated overnight at 37 °C, 180 rpm. This enrichment step was used to increase phage titers, as direct plating of wastewater did not yield sufficient plaques. After centrifugation (4,000 × g, 15 min) and 0.22 μm filtration (polyéthersulfone PES), the activity of amplified phages was tested on clinical and laboratory E. coli strains. To do so, 300 µL of an overnight bacterial culture (stationary phase) were mixed to 100 µL of serially diluted phages suspension (dilutions performed in TSB; 10⁻² to 10⁻¹⁰, 100-fold steps) and 5 mL of molten (45 °C) soft Tryptic Soy agar (TSA) (0.7% agar) without addition of divalent cations. The mixture was poured onto pre-poured TSA (1.5% agar) plates and incubated at 37 °C for 20 h. Among the 11 clinical ST131 strains tested, the clinical ST131 isolate 256G1 (provided by the French National Reference Center for Antibiotic Resistance) produced the clearest plaques (with a diameter of approximative 0.5 mm) and the highest titer (> 10⁹ PFU/mL). For titration, spot tests were performed in technical triplicates on soft TSA using 10-fold serial dilutions of phage filtrate in TSB. After one night at 37 °C, PFUs were enumerated to determine the titers in PFU/mL. This bacterial strain was therefore selected as the isolation and propagation host.

The bacteriophage was purified using the a double-layer agar method [10] based on the protocol described by Kolenda et al. [11]. To describe briefly the phage isolation step, individual plaques were picked (approx 0.5 mm, without observable halo), resuspended in 1 mL TSB, filtered (0.22 μm, PES), serially diluted, and re-plated on a bacterial lawn (256G1) using the same double-layer procedure. This purification step was repeated five times to obtain a phage clone as pure as possible. Phage stock suspension was made in liquid condition after inoculation (MOI = 10⁻⁴ phages:bacteria) of a culture of bacteria in exponential phase (OD600nm = 0.5). After overnight amplification at 37 °C with shaking at 180 rpm, the phage lysate was centrifuged (30 min at 6000 g) and filtrated (0.22 μm, PES). The stock suspension was stored at 4 °C (stability across temperatures nor media were not tested).

After amplification in liquid culture, DNA was extracted with the DNA Extractor® WB kit (Fujifilm) after DNase I and RNase A treatments exactly as described by Kolenda et al. [11]. No NaCl/PEG precipitation was used. DNA concentration was measured with Qubit™ and purity with NanoDrop™. Sequencing was performed on a NextSeq550 (Illumina) at the genEPII platform (Hospices Civils de Lyon), generating 2 × 150 bp reads. Libraries were prepared with the Illumina DNA Prep kit using a 1:10 scaled protocol [11].

De novo phage assembly and annotation

Raw reads were assessed with FastQC and cleaned with Fastp (adapter trimming, quality filtering; poly-G trimming disabled). Cleaned reads were de novo assembled with SPAdes (v.3.13.1) using default parameters, including automatic k-mer selection (21, 33, 55) based on the read length. Read-error correction was enabled and mismatchCorrector was disabled. Pilon (v1.24) was run once with default parameters, using the SPAdes assembly as input and the corresponding Illumina read alignment (sorted BAM) as fragment evidence. No mapping/subtraction against the host genome was performed. Depth estimation was refined with SAMtools.

Contigs were screened by BLASTn against NCBI nt; those matching phages were curated. The major contig (170,364 bp) was polished with Pilon [12], yielding mean coverage of 1286X. Twenty-two additional contigs (332–937 bp) were low-quality fragments with little or no viral content. CheckV [13] classified the major contig as high-quality and complete (100% completeness) based on high-confidence Average Amino Acid Identity comparisons to known viral genomes. To evaluate the presence of gaps within the assemblies, two approaches were applied. First, the total number of ambiguous nucleotides (Ns) was counted in the sequences by scanning the FASTA files while excluding header lines. Additionally, the presence of longer stretches of ambiguous bases (≥ 3 consecutive Ns) was assessed to detect potential assembly gaps. An additional screening with SeqKit [14] did not reveal any missing or duplicated regions, confirming a gapless assembly. The complete annotated genome was deposited in the European Nucleotide Archive (https://www.ebi.ac.uk/ena/browser/home) with the Accession number OZ317122 within the Study Accession PRJEB91440. The lifestyle of phage vB_EcoM-V1EC45 was predicted using the online tool PhageScope (https://phagescope.deepomics.org/) [15].

The main assembled contig was submitted to BV-BRC (https://www.bv-brc.org) for phage-specific annotation and quality assessment. Open reading frames (ORFs) were classified into functional categories (structure, DNA metabolism, lysis, immunity, etc.) using custom R scripts. GC content visualization and calculation were performed with Proksee (https://proksee.ca) [16]. Putative horizontally transferred regions were detected with AlienHunter, run within Proksee, based on sequence composition deviations.

Comparative genomics

The phage genome was compared to other phages using BLASTn (NCBI) and to a sub-collection of related phages [8]. To avoid any heterogeneity in the annotations of all genomes, we re-annotated all fasta files with Pharokka v1.7.5 [17], and the resulting GFF files were analyzed with Roary [18] for pan and core-genome reconstruction. Whole-genome analysis was performed using online VICTOR services [19]. Co-linearity, conserved blocks, and gene synteny were assessed with MAUVE [20] in Geneious Prime® 2025.1.3. Genes were categorized as present in all (0-100%), core (99–100%), soft-core (95–99%), shell (15–95%), or cloud (0–15%) genomes. Multiple sequence alignment of the core genes was performed with MAFFT [21] in the Roary workflow, and phylogeny was inferred under maximum likelihood with RAXML-NG. Bootstrapping stopped automatically after convergence (cutoff 3%, autoMRE) [22].

Detailed product references and software versions used in this study are provided in Supplementary Tables S1 and S2 (respectively) and scripts or alignments are openly available at https://src.koda.cnrs.fr/remy.froissart/v1ec45.

Results and discussion

Phage isolation and genome analysis

The isolated phage was named Escherichia phage vB_EcoM-V1EC45 (hereafter referred as V1EC45) with the accession number OZ317122. Its genome length is 170,364 bp and the annotated genome map (Fig. 1) displays a modular organization with clusters of structural, replication, and lysis genes. AlienHunter detected atypical segments, but none exceeded the threshold (29.3), preventing firm conclusions on horizontal transfer. PhageScope further predicted a virulent lifestyle for V1EC45.

Annotation identified 281 coding sequences (CDS), in which 136 CDS had assigned functions (Table S3) and nine tRNA genes and no rRNA genes. Detected genes were predicted to be involved in DNA metabolism (48; 17%), transcription (8; 2.8%), translation (10; 3.5%), structure including tail fibers (16; 5.7%) and other proteins (17; 6.0%), endonucleases (13; 4.6%), exonucleases (3; 1%), lysis (7; 2.5%), and host hijack/immunity functions (12; 4.2%). Annotation included long tail fiber and adhesin (gp38), the determinants of receptor binding and host specificity. Additionally, 39 CDS (13.8%) encode hypothetical proteins, while 106 CDS (37.7%) were annotated as general “phage proteins.” The large fraction of uncharacterized ORFs highlights the poorly understood genomic diversity of phages.

Anti-defense systems

Like in many phages [23], V1EC45 carries genes counteracting bacterial antiviral defenses (detected with DefenseFinder [24]). First, anti-restriction systems with CDS 6 encoding an Arn-like protein [25, 26], structurally mimicking DNA; CDS 57 encodes Dam DNA adenine methyltransferase, protecting phage DNA at GATC motifs [26]. Second, anti-TA (Toxin-Antitoxin) systems with CDS 64 encoding TifA, neutralizing bacterial toxins [27]; CDS 65 encoding Dmd, a broad-spectrum antitoxin acting on RNases such as LsoA and RnlA [28, 29]. Third, anti-CBASS / cyclic messenger immunity with CDS 167 encoding Acb1, a nuclease degrading cyclic nucleotides (CBASS, Pycsar) [30, 32] and CDS 145 encoding Acb2, a nucleotide-binding protein acting as a “molecular sponge” that captures these messengers to prevent CBASS activation [31].

CBASS systems occur in a minority of E. coli genomes (~ 20%, DefenseFinder), but their presence in ST131 remains poorly documented. The Acb1/Acb2 proteins of V1EC45 may therefore counter CBASS only when present. In contrast, large-scale comparative genomics indicate that ST131 retains a conserved, although reduced repertoire of TA systems. Several TA toxin groups, such as AbiEii and CbtA, are present in most of ST131 isolates [33], making them more plausible targets for the anti-TA genes carried by V1EC45.

Comparative genomics analysis

Complete genome comparison with BLASTn against the NCBI collection NCBI retrieved 484 Tevenvirinae genomes with more than 38% coverage, including 73 Tequatrovirus sequences with more than 95% coverage. V1EC45 displayed a genome length typical of Tequatrovirus, since the median of the genome length of 421 phages from this genus is 167,467 bp ± 6,364 bp, with a minimum size of 111,068 bp (Escherichia phage UoN_LDJ77_1; OK570375) and a maximum size of 172,940 bp (Escherichia phage vB_Eco_F26; OP870145). Mean GC content of V1EC45 genome was 35.3%, typical to a Tequatrovirus (median of 35.4% ± 0.1%). GC content was relatively homogeneous across V1EC45 genome except for regions harbouring more than 50% such as at loci coding for tRNAs or in the middle of the CDS 264 (coding for the receptor binding protein).

Among the 384 Tequatroviruses sharing more than 70% of coverage with V1EC45, BLASTn predicted Escherichia phage UTI-E4 to be the closest relative to V1EC45. Based on this database, whole genome analysis by VIRIDIC identified four genomes (including UTI-E4) with 95% intergenomic similarity to V1EC45. Tequatrovirus phylogenetic trees were then reconstructed using 42 genomes composed of 37 used in Maffei et al. [8] (i.e. 10 from the BASEL collection and 27 from NCBI database) with the addition of the four closest genomes according to VIRIDIC (Table S4), leveraging the BASEL dataset that experimentally defined host receptors.

To homogenize the annotations of the 42 genomes, we re-annotated all of them with Pharokka and we identified 888 genes: 130 core, 25 soft-core, 236 shell, and 497 cloud. After aligning the core genes at the nucleotide level (the alignment is available in our personal online supplementary data) and concatenating them, the Maximum-likelihood inference revealed five Tequatrovirus clades (bootstrap > 70%; Fig. 2).

V1EC45 clustered in a clade rooted by Escherichia phage vB_EcoM_ACG-C40, grouping 13 phages with > 97% identity, including UTI-E4, corroborating BLASTn and VIRIDIC results. To confirm our analysis, we inferred other phylogenetic trees based on whole genome analysis (VICTOR), that also considered UTI-E4 as the closest related genome (Fig. S1). Whole-genome alignment between V1EC45 and its closest relatives showed high CDS identity (Fig. 3), one colinear block and some genomic differences with UTI-E4 (Fig. S2): twenty CDS were unique to V1EC45 (10 phage and 7 hypothetical genes and three HNH endonucleases), and four CDS and one tRNA (tRNA-His) were unique to UTI-E4 (Table S5). Note that the twenty sequences of the CDS unique to V1EC45 compared to UTI-E4 were however detected after research using BLASTn within other Tequatroviruses. Finally, four proteins were highly diverse between the two related phages (Table S5).

Host binding molecular mechanisms are different within Tequatroviruses. On the one hand, the distal part of the long tail fiber (encoded by gp37) is responsible for host binding in T4-like phages. On the other hand, an adhesin protein (encoded by gp38) binds to the bacterial host receptor in phage related to T6 [34]. Since V1EC45 was included within a clade grouping phages (namely WilhemHis, KarlGJung and Paracelsus) using the bacterial outer membrane protein Tsx as the primary receptor, we compared the sequence of the gene gp38 of all phages that were experimentally demonstrated to use Tsx as RBP [8]. The alignment (Figure S3) revealed a high similarity of the gp38 CDS of V1EC45 and those of KarlGJung (100% similarity) and Paracelsus (99.6% similarity). Conversely, the alignment of the gp37 CDS of V1EC45 and the gp37 CDSs of five other phages using OmpC as an RBP showed very little similarity (Figure S4). These homology-based comparisons support Tsx as the most likely receptor for V1EC45, but targeted experimental validation (e.g., infection assays using a tsx knockout) remains necessary.

The V1EC45 host range should be further investigated on a broader, phylogenetically structured collection of ST131 isolates (A, B, C1, C2). Indeed, multiple studies have shown that extraintestinal E. coli infections, particularly in the urinary tract, often involve coexisting clonal variants within the same patient [35, 36]. These intrahost coexisting genotypes challenge the efficacy of monovalent antimicrobial and phage therapies and highlight the need for phages capable of addressing polyclonal populations. In this context, isolating phages able to infect diverse variants becomes urgent. Even if the natural host range is shaped by receptor compatibility, recent studies have demonstrated that experimental evolution can be used to broaden host specificity by selecting adaptive beneficial mutations [37]. When applied to V1EC45, such an approach could generate derivatives capable of targeting a wider spectrum of ST131 isolates while preserving lytic activity and genomic stability.

Limitations

This study has several limitations. First, the host range of phage V1EC45 was assessed on a limited set of E. coli ST131 isolates, preventing robust conclusions on its overall host range. Second, the prediction that V1EC45 uses the Tsx outer membrane protein as receptor-binding protein is based on sequence similarity, but awaits experimental confirmation. Finally, the therapeutic potential of V1EC45 has not been evaluated in relevant clinical or animal infection models. Further work is required to address these limitations and validate its suitability as a candidate for phage therapy.
