Abstract
. For a given λ > 0 \lambda>0 , a convex body in ℝ n \mathbb{R}^{n} is λ \lambda -convex if it is the intersection of (finitely or infinitely many) balls of radius 1 / λ 1/\lambda . In this note, we show that among all λ \lambda -convex bodies in ℝ n \mathbb{R}^{n} , n ⩾ 2 n\geqslant 2 , with a given inradius, the λ \lambda -convex lens (i.e., the intersection of two balls of radius 1 / λ 1/\lambda ) has the largest mean width. This gives an affirmative answer to the conjecture of K. Bezdek. Under an additional symmetry assumption on λ \lambda -convex bodies, we resolve the analogous inradius conjecture of Bezdek for arbitrary intrinsic volumes. We also establish an answer to the corresponding conjecture of K. Bezdek about the circumradius. In particular, we prove that the λ \lambda -convex spindle (i.e., the intersection of all balls of radius 1 / λ 1/\lambda containing a given pair of points) is the unique minimizer of the mean width among all λ \lambda -convex bodies with a fixed circumradius.



1.
Introduction

A convex body in
\mathbb{R}^{n}
is a compact convex subset with non-empty interior. For a given
\lambda>0
, a convex body
K\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}
is called
\lambda
-convex
if for every point
p\in\partial K
there exists a neighborhood
U_{p}
and a ball
B_{\lambda,p}
of radius
1/\lambda
such that
p\in\partial B_{\lambda,p}
and
U_{p}\cap K\subset B_{\lambda,p}
(see Figure
1
). By Blaschke’s rolling theorem
[
Bla
]
(see also
[
Dr3
]
and references therein), every
\lambda
-convex body can be represented as an intersection of (finitely or infinitely many) balls of radius
1/\lambda
. A
\lambda
-convex lens
in
\mathbb{R}^{n}
is the intersection of two balls of radius
1/\lambda
.

The class of
\lambda
-convex bodies appears naturally in connection with the Kneser–Poulsen conjecture
[
Be
]
, the study of the illumination conjecture
[
Be2
]
, approximation of convex bodies
[
FV
,
NV
]
; see also
[
AAF
,
AACF
,
SWY
]
for further developments on the geometry of
\lambda
-convex bodies and related questions. Recently, the study of
reverse
isoperimetric problems in the class of
\lambda
-convex bodies garnered a lot of attention. In particular, the analogue of Ball’s reverse isoperimetric inequality for
\lambda
-convex bodies was obtained in
[
FKV
,
BD2
]
in
\mathbb{R}^{2}
and in
[
DT
]
in
\mathbb{R}^{3}
. We refer the reader to the nice surveys
[
BLN
,
BLNP
]
about topics in
\lambda
-convexity.

The classical Steiner formula asserts that for every
\varepsilon>0
,

(1.1)

\Vol(K+\varepsilon{\bm{B}})=\sum\limits_{j=0}^{n}\kappa_{n-j}V_{j}(K)\varepsilon^{n-j}

where
\Vol
is the Lebesgue measure on
\mathbb{R}^{n}
,
{\bm{B}}
is the unit Euclidean ball in
\mathbb{R}^{n}
,
\kappa_{n-j}
is the volume of the unit Euclidean ball in
\mathbb{R}^{n-j}
, and
+
denotes the Minkowski addition. The coefficients
V_{j}(K)
are called the
intrinsic volumes
of
K
. It is known that
V_{n}(K)
is the volume of
K
,
V_{n-1}(K)
is (up to the scalar) the surface area of
K
,
V_{1}(K)
is (up to a scalar) the mean width of
K
, and
V_{0}(K)=\kappa_{n}
is the volume of
{\bm{B}}
. We refer readers to
[
Sch
]
for a comprehensive discussion about intrinsic volumes.

The
inradius

r(K)
of a convex body
K
is the radius of
a largest ball contained in
K
. In this paper, we answer the conjecture posed by K. Bezdek in
[
Be3
, Conjecture 5]
about minimizing the inradius in the class of
\lambda
-convex bodies with fixed mean width.
We remark that this result supplements the analogous statements for the volume due to Bezdek
[
Be3
]
, and for the surface area due to the authors
[
DT
]
.

Theorem A

(Reverse inradius inequality for mean width)
.

Let
n\geqslant 2
and let
\lambda>0
. Let
K\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}
be a
\lambda
-convex body and
L\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}
be a
\lambda
-convex lens. If
r(K)=r(L)
,
then

(1.2)

V_{1}(K)\leqslant V_{1}(L),

with equality if and only if
K
is a
\lambda
-convex lens.

For
n=2
, this result was obtained by Milka in
[
Mi
]
, and it was later generalized to all
2
-dimensional Alexandrov spaces of curvature bounded below by the first author in
[
Dr2
]
.

The circumradius
R(K)
of a convex body
K
is the radius of the smallest ball that contains
K
. K. Bezdek
[
Be3
, Conjecture 10]
conjectured that a
\lambda
-convex spindle
has the largest circumradius among all
\lambda
-convex bodies in
\mathbb{R}^{n}
with fixed mean width. A
\lambda
-convex spindle
is the intersection of all balls that contain a pair of given points. These points, called
the vertices
of the spindle, must be necessarily at a distance at most
2/\lambda
apart. Spindles appear as solutions to various optimization problems for
\lambda
-convex domains (see e.g.,
[
Be3
,
BD1
,
BM
,
Dr1
]
).

As an application of Theorem
A
and the use of the
\lambda
-duality in the class of
\lambda
-convex bodies, we obtain a solution to the partial case of Bezdek’s conjecture
[
Be3
, Conjecture 10]
for the mean width.

Theorem B

(Reverse circumradius inequality for mean width)
.

Let
n\geqslant 2
and let
\lambda>0
. Let
K\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}
be a
\lambda
-convex body and

be a
\lambda
-convex spindle. If

V_{1}(K)\geqslant V_{1}(S),

with equality if and only if

is a
\lambda
-convex spindle.

We note that the inequality of Linhart

]
gives a lower bound for

If a
\lambda
-convex body

(that is,

, Conjecture 5]

(Reverse inradius inequality for intrinsic volumes under symmetry assumption)

Let
n\geqslant 2

\lambda>0
. Let
K\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
be a
\lambda
-convex body that has inball symmetries and

be a

-convex lens. If

then

The paper is organized as follows. In Section

2.
Preliminaries

In this section, we aim to recall some general background from convex geometry and
\lambda
-convexity.

2.1.
Mixed volumes.

We refer the readers to
[
Sch
]
for a detailed study of mixed volumes and basic notions from convexity.

We work in the Euclidean space
\mathbb{R}^{n}
with scalar product
\langle\cdot,\cdot\rangle.
We denote the unit ball centered at the origin by
{\bm{B}}
, and its boundary unit sphere as
\mathbb{S}^{n-1}=\partial{\bm{B}}
.

Let
K_{1},\dots,K_{m}
be convex bodies in
\mathbb{R}^{n}
. The fundamental theorem of Minkowski describes the behavior of the volume of the Minkowski sum of convex bodies:

(2.1)

\Vol(t_{1}K_{1}+\dots+t_{m}K_{m})=\sum\limits_{i_{1},\dots,i_{n}=1}^{m}V(K_{i_{1}},\dots,K_{i_{n}})t_{i_{1}}\dots t_{i_{n}}

for any
t_{1},\dots,t_{m}\geq 0.
The coefficients
V(K_{i_{1}},\dots,K_{i_{n}})
in this polynomial are called
mixed volumes
which are important geometric functionals associated with convex bodies. These coefficients are nonnegative, continuous, and multilinear in their arguments. Moreover, mixed volumes are invariant under permutations, that is, if
\tau
is a permutation on
\{1,\ldots,n\}
, then

(2.2)

V(K_{1},\ldots,K_{n})=V(K_{\tau(1)},\ldots,K_{\tau(n)}).

As a special case, (
2.1
) implies the Steiner’s formula (
1.1
). The coefficients
V(K,\dots,K,{\bm{B}},\dots,{\bm{B}})
are called quermassintegrals
W_{j}(K)
of a convex body
K
and defined as

W_{j}(K)=V(\underbrace{K,\ldots,K}_{n-j},\underbrace{{\bm{B}},\ldots,{\bm{B}}}_{j})

for
j=0,1,\dots,n.

We will use their weighted versions
V_{j}(K)
which are called
intrinsic volumes
of
K
:

(2.3)

V_{j}(K)=\frac{{n\choose j}}{\kappa_{n-j}}W_{n-j}(K)=\frac{{n\choose j}}{\kappa_{n-j}}V(\underbrace{K,\ldots,K}_{j},\underbrace{{\bm{B}},\ldots,{\bm{B}}}_{n-j}).

It is known that
V_{n}(K)
is the
n
-dimensional volume of
K
,
2V_{n-1}(K)
is the surface area of
K
,
\frac{2\kappa_{n-1}}{n\kappa_{n}}V_{1}(K)
is the mean width of
K
, and finally,
V_{0}(K)=\kappa_{n}
is just the volume of the unit Euclidean ball
{\bm{B}}
in
\mathbb{R}^{n}
.

The support function of a convex body
K
is defined as
h_{K}(x)=\max\limits_{y\in K}\langle x,y\rangle
for every
x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
. The mixed area measure
S(K_{2},\ldots,K_{n})(\cdot)
of
K_{2},\dots,K_{n}
is a unique non-negative measure on
\mathbb{S}^{n-1}
such that

V(K_{1},\ldots,K_{n})=\frac{1}{n}\int_{\mathbb{S}^{n-1}}h_{K_{1}}(u)\,dS(K_{2},\ldots,K_{n})(u).

Note that
S({\bm{B}},\ldots,{\bm{B}})
is the standard measure
du
on
\mathbb{S}^{n-1}
, and more generally,
S(K,\ldots,K)
is the surface area measure on
\partial K
.

The mixed area measure
S(K_{2},\ldots,K_{n})(\cdot)
is invariant under ambient isometries applied simultaneously to all convex bodies:

(2.4)

S(K_{2},\ldots,K_{n})(\cdot)=S(\sigma K_{2},\ldots,\sigma K_{n})(\cdot),\quad\text{for any orientation-preserving isometry }\sigma\colon\mathbb{R}^{n}\to\mathbb{R}^{n}.

Furthermore, if each of the bodies
K_{2},\ldots,K_{n}
is rotationally symmetric with respect to an axis parallel to a given unit direction
\ell
, then the measure
S(K_{2},\ldots,K_{n})(\cdot)
is rotationally invariant in the following sense. Let
p\in\mathbb{S}^{n-1}
be the point that corresponds to
\ell
, and let
A\subset\mathbb{S}^{n-1}
be a set measurable with respect to
S(K_{2},\ldots,K_{n})(\cdot)
. Then for every rotation
\sigma
on
\mathbb{S}^{n-1}
around
p
, we have

(2.5)

S(K_{2},\ldots,K_{n})(A)=S(K_{2},\ldots,K_{n})(\sigma A).

The
width
of a convex body
K\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}
in the direction
u\in\mathbb{S}^{n-1}
is defined as
h_{K}(u)+h_{K}(-u)
. The
mean width

w(K)
of
K
is the average of the widths of
K
, that is,

(2.6)

w(K)=\frac{1}{|\mathbb{S}^{n-1}|}\int_{\mathbb{S}^{n-1}}\left(h_{K}(u)+h_{K}(-u)\right)\,du=\frac{2}{|\mathbb{S}^{n-1}|}\int_{\mathbb{S}^{n-1}}h_{K}(u)\,du,

where
|\mathbb{S}^{n-1}|
is the
(n-1)
-dimensional measure of
\mathbb{S}^{n-1}
. Thus, we can write
V_{1}(K)
as

(2.7)

V_{1}(K)=\frac{1}{\kappa_{n-1}}\int_{\mathbb{S}^{n-1}}h_{K}(u)\,du=\frac{n\kappa_{n}}{2\kappa_{n-1}}w(K),

where we used that
|\mathbb{S}^{n-1}|=n\kappa_{n}
.

The most fundamental inequality that relates mixed volumes is the classical Alexandrov–Fenchel inequality.

The Alexandrov–Fenchel Inequality
.

Let
K_{1},K_{2},\ldots,K_{n}\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}
be convex bodies. Then

V(K_{1},K_{2},K_{3},\dots,K_{n})^{2}\geqslant V(K_{1},K_{1},K_{3},\dots,K_{n})\cdot V(K_{2},K_{2},K_{3},\dots,K_{n}).

2.2.
\lambda
-convexity.

In this subsection, we collect some definitions and properties related to
\lambda
-convex bodies.

Let
\lambda>0
. A
\lambda
-convex polytope
is a
\lambda
-convex body given as the intersection of finitely many balls of radius
1/\penalty\lambda
. An
(n-1)
-dimensional
facet
of a
\lambda
-convex polytope
P
is the intersection of the corresponding ball of radius
1/\penalty\lambda
with the boundary of
P
. Two facets are either disjoint or intersect. In the latter case, their intersection is called a (lower-dimensional) face. The faces
of dimension
0
and
1
are called vertices and edges of the polytope
P
, respectively. The
\lambda
-convex lens is an example of a
\lambda
-convex polytope.

We also recall that a convex body
K\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}
is called
\lambda
-convex
if for every point
p\in\partial K
there exists a neighborhood
U_{p}
and a ball
B_{\lambda,p}
of radius
1/\lambda
such that
p\in\partial B_{\lambda,p}
and
U_{p}\cap K\subset B_{\lambda,p}
(see Figure
1
). When
\partial K
is at least
C^{2}
-smooth, this condition is equivalent to requiring that the principal curvatures
k_{i}(p)
,
i\in\{1,\ldots,n-1\}
, with respect to the inward-pointing normal at every
p\in\partial K
, satisfy
k_{i}(p)\geqslant 1
.

Figure 1.
The definition of a
\lambda
-convex body

For every
\lambda
-convex body
K
, we define the
\lambda
-convex dual

K^{\lambda}
as

K^{\lambda}=\underset{x\in K}{\bigcap}\left(\frac{1}{\lambda}{\bm{B}}+x\right).

It follows from this definition that
K^{\lambda}
is also a
\lambda
-convex body. Moreover,
\lambda
-duality is an order-reversing involution, that is,
(K^{\lambda})^{\lambda}=K
and
K^{\lambda}\subseteq M^{\lambda}
if
M\subseteq K
for any
\lambda
-convex bodies
K
and
M
. The
\lambda
-duality has been recently investigated in
[
AACF
,
BLN
,
FKV
]
.

It is known (see, for example,
[
FKV
, Proposition 2.2]
) that for a
\lambda
-convex body
K
the following relation holds

K+(-K^{\lambda})=\frac{1}{\lambda}{\bm{B}}.

Equivalently,

(2.8)

h_{K}(u)+h_{K^{\lambda}}(-u)=\frac{1}{\lambda},\qquad\text{for any }u\in\mathbb{S}^{n-1}.

The relation (
2.8
) immediately implies that

(2.9)

r(K)+R(K^{\lambda})=\frac{1}{\lambda}.

Finally, if
L
is a
\lambda
-convex lens, then
L^{\lambda}=S
is a
\lambda
-convex spindle. We refer the reader to
[
BLN
, Section 3.1]
for the detailed discussion of the
\lambda
-duality.

Remark
.

We note that
\lambda
-duality is different from the spherical duality between
\lambda
-convex and
\lambda
-concave domains in
\mathbb{S}^{2}
, which was used in
[
BD3
]
to relate the reverse isoperimetric problems in these two classes of bodies.

3.
Proof of Theorem
A

Without loss of generality, we can assume that
\lambda=1
. Let
B
be an inscribed ball for
K
. We can assume that this ball is centered at the origin and has the radius
r(K)=r(L)\leqslant 1
.

Since
B
is the inscribed ball for
K
, the intersection
\partial K\cap\partial B
contains a finite set of points
T=\{p_{0},p_{1},\ldots\}
, with
|T|\leqslant n+1
, that does not lie in any of the open hemispheres of
\partial B
(this statement is folklore, see e.g.,
[
DT
, Proposition 2.9]
; it can also be deduced from the Carathéodory theorem).

Let
\widetilde{K}
be a
1
-convex polytope given by the intersection of the unit balls tangent to
B
at the points in
T
and containing
B
in their closures. By Blaschke’s Rolling Theorem,
K\subseteq\widetilde{K}
. Furthermore, by
[
DT
, Proposition 2.9]
,
B
is the inscribed ball for
\widetilde{K}
. Thus,

r(\widetilde{K})=r(K)\quad\text{and}\quad K\subseteq\widetilde{K}.

Hence,

V_{1}(K)\leqslant V_{1}(\widetilde{K})

by the monotonicity of the intrinsic volumes, with equality if and only if
\widetilde{K}=K
.

Therefore, to establish Theorem
A
, it is enough to consider the case when
K
is a
1
-convex polytope with the property that all of its facets touch the inscribed ball at the points in the set
T=\{p_{0},p_{1},\ldots,p_{|T|}\}
,
|T|\leqslant n+1
.

For the rest of this section, we further assume that the lens
L
is a
1
-convex lens centered at the origin (and hence, sharing the same inscribed ball with
K
) and rotated so that it touches the inscribed ball in one of the points in
T
, say,
p_{0}
.

Let
\mathcal{C}:=(\underbrace{{\bm{B}},\ldots,{\bm{B}}}_{n-1})
be an
(n-1)
-tuple of unit Euclidean balls
{\bm{B}}
. Then our goal is to show

(3.1)

V(K,\mathcal{C})=\frac{1}{n}\int\limits_{\mathbb{S}^{n-1}}h_{K}(u)du\leqslant\frac{1}{n}\int\limits_{\mathbb{S}^{n-1}}h_{L}(u)du=V(L,\mathcal{C}),

with equality if and only if
K
is a lens.

Let
F_{i}
be a facet of the
1
-convex polytope
K
. Denote by
p_{i}
the point of tangency of
F_{i}
and the inscribed ball
B
. Let
\mathcal{N}_{F_{i}}\subset\mathbb{S}^{n-1}
be the radial projection of
F_{i}
onto the unit sphere
\mathbb{S}^{n-1}
with respect to the origin. Equivalently, if
Q_{1},\ldots,Q_{|T|}\subset\partial B
are the Voronoi cells of points
p_{1},\ldots,p_{|T|}\in\partial B
, then each
\mathcal{N}_{F_{i}}
is the radial projection of the corresponding
Q_{i}
onto the unit sphere
\mathbb{S}^{n-1}
.

Assume that the
1
-convex lens
L
is placed so that its center is at the origin and one of its facets, which we call
F
, touches the inscribed ball
B
at the point
p_{i}
. If
\mathcal{N}_{F}\subset\mathbb{S}^{n-1}
is a similar radial projection of
F
, then we have

(3.2)

F_{i}\subseteq F,\quad\mathcal{N}_{F_{i}}\subseteq\mathcal{N}_{F},\quad\text{with simultaneous equality if and only if }\quad F_{i}=F.

The first inclusion follows from Blaschke’s rolling theorem
[
Bla
]
, and the second inclusion follows from the symmetry and the fact that
F_{i}
and
F
are regions on the unit sphere.

Lemma 3.1
.

(3.3)

\frac{\int\limits_{\mathcal{N}_{F_{i}}}h_{K}(u)\,du}{\int\limits_{\mathcal{N}_{F_{i}}}du}\leqslant\frac{\int\limits_{\mathcal{N}_{F}}h_{L}(u)\,du}{\int\limits_{\mathcal{N}_{F}}du}=\frac{V(L,\mathcal{C})}{V({\bm{B}},\mathcal{C})}.

Moreover, the equality holds if and only if
\mathcal{N}_{F}=\mathcal{N}_{F_{i}}
(and thus, if and only if
F=F_{i}
).

Now, assume that Lemma
3.1
holds for every
i\in\{1,\ldots,|T|\}
. Then, summing over all facets of
K
, we obtain

\displaystyle V(K,\mathcal{C})

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{n}\int\limits_{\mathbb{S}^{n-1}}h_{K}(u)\,du=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i}\int\limits_{\mathcal{N}_{F_{i}}}h_{K}(u)\,du

\displaystyle\leqslant\frac{V(L,\mathcal{C})}{V({\bm{B}},\mathcal{C})}\cdot\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i}\int\limits_{\mathcal{N}_{F_{i}}}du=\frac{V(L,\mathcal{C})}{V({\bm{B}},\mathcal{C})}\cdot\frac{1}{n}\int\limits_{\mathbb{S}^{n-1}}du=V(L,\mathcal{C}),

which yields (
3.1
) and finishes the proof of Theorem
A
. In the next subsection, we prove Lemma
3.1
.

3.1.
Proof of Lemma
3.1

First observe that by (
3.2
),

h_{K}(u)\leqslant h_{L}(u),\quad\forall u\in\mathcal{N}_{F_{i}}.

Therefore, to prove (
3.3
), it is enough to establish

(3.4)

\frac{\int\limits_{\mathcal{N}_{F_{i}}}h_{L}(u)\,du}{\int\limits_{\mathcal{N}_{F_{i}}}du}\leqslant\frac{\int\limits_{\mathcal{N}_{F}}h_{L}(u)\,du}{\int\limits_{\mathcal{N}_{F}}du}.

Choose the coordinates
(t,\vartheta)
in
\mathbb{S}^{n-1}
centred at
\frac{1}{r(K)}p_{i}
such that a point
u\in\mathbb{S}^{n-1}
can be written as

u=\cos(t)\frac{p_{i}}{r(K)}+\sin(t)\,\vartheta,

where
\vartheta\in\mathbb{S}^{n-2}
(more precisely,
\vartheta\in p_{i}^{\perp}\cap\mathbb{S}^{n-1}
) and
t
is the angle between
u
and direction
op_{i}
. Then the surface area measure on
\mathbb{S}^{n-1}
is
du=\left(\sin(t)\right)^{n-2}dt\,d\vartheta
, and we set
q(t):=\left(\sin(t)\right)^{n-2}
for
t\in[0,\pi/2]
. The function
q
is strictly monotone increasing on its domain of definition. Also, denote the spherical radial function of
\mathcal{N}_{F_{i}}
in the direction
\vartheta
by

\varphi(\vartheta):=\max\limits_{t\in[0,{\pi}/2]}\left\{\cos(t)\frac{p_{i}}{r(K)}+\sin(t)\,\vartheta\in\mathcal{N}_{F_{i}}\right\}.

By rotational symmetry, the support function
h_{L}(u)=h_{L}(t)
is independent of
\vartheta
. Furthermore, the function
t\mapsto h_{L}(t)
is strictly monotone increasing on its domain of definition
t\in[0,\pi/2]
.

Then we can rewrite

\int\limits_{\mathcal{N}_{F_{i}}}h_{L}(u)\,du=\int\limits_{\mathbb{S}^{n-2}}\int\limits_{0}^{\varphi(\vartheta)}h_{L}(t)q(t)dt\,d\vartheta\qquad\text{and}\qquad\int\limits_{\mathcal{N}_{F_{i}}}du=\int\limits_{\mathbb{S}^{n-2}}\int\limits_{0}^{\varphi(\vartheta)}q(t)dt\,d\vartheta.

Also, let
\mathcal{F}:={\int_{0}^{{\pi}/2}h_{L}(t)q(t)dt}\,/\,{\int_{0}^{{\pi}/2}q(t)dt}
and consider

(3.5)

\mathcal{R}[\varphi(\vartheta)]:=\int\limits_{0}^{\varphi(\vartheta)}\left(h_{L}(t)-\mathcal{F}\right)q(t)\,dt,

where
\varphi(\vartheta)\in[0,{\pi}/2]
. Note that
\mathcal{R}[{\pi}/2]=0
.

Since

is a continuous and strictly increasing function on
[0,{\pi}/2]
, we obtain that
\mathcal{R}[\varphi(\vartheta)]\leqslant 0
for all possible
\varphi(\vartheta)\in[0,{\pi}/2]
with equality if and only if
\varphi(\vartheta)={\pi}/2
(see
[
DT
, Lemma 4.5]
). Hence,

\displaystyle\int\limits_{\mathcal{N}_{F_{i}}}h_{L}(u)\,du

\displaystyle=\int\limits_{\mathbb{S}^{n-2}}\int\limits_{0}^{\varphi(\vartheta)}h_{L}(t)q(t)dt\,d\vartheta\leqslant\mathcal{F}\int\limits_{\mathbb{S}^{n-2}}\int\limits_{0}^{\varphi(\vartheta)}q(t)dt\,d\vartheta

\displaystyle=\frac{\int\limits_{\mathbb{S}^{n-2}}\int\limits_{0}^{\frac{\pi}{2}}h_{L}(t)q(t)dt\,d\vartheta}{\int\limits_{\mathbb{S}^{n-2}}\int\limits_{0}^{\frac{\pi}{2}}q(t)dt\,d\vartheta}\int\limits_{\mathbb{S}^{n-2}}\int\limits_{0}^{\varphi(\vartheta)}q(t)dt\,d\vartheta=\frac{\int\limits_{\mathcal{N}_{F}}h_{L}(u)\,du}{\int\limits_{\mathcal{N}_{F}}du}\int\limits_{\mathcal{N}_{F_{i}}}du,

as required.

Proof of Theorem

\lambda

By (

5.
Proof of Theorem
C

For intrinsic volumes different from the volume, the surface area, and the mean width, our method allows us to derive a result similar to our Theorem
A
under the additional symmetry assumption as follows. Let
i(K)\in\partial K
be the set of points at which the inscribed ball of
K
touches the boundary
\partial K
. This set can be finite or infinite. For example, if
L
is a lens, then
i(L)
consists of two points (symmetric with respect to the center of
L
). If
S
is a spindle, then
i(S)
is a circle.

Let
\Isom^{+}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
be the group of orientation-preserving isometries in
\mathbb{R}^{n}
and
\mathscr{O}(K)<\Isom^{+}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
be the maximal subgroup of rotations around the center
o
of the inscribed ball to
K
that fixes
i(K)
. In other words, a rotation
\sigma\in\Isom^{+}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
about
o
lies in
\mathscr{O}(K)
if

i(K)=\sigma\left(i(K)\right):=\big\{\sigma(x)\,|\,x\in i(K)\big\}.

Definition 5.1

(Inball symmetries)
.

We say that
K
has
inball symmetries
if
\mathscr{O}(K)
is not equal to the identity group and it acts transitively on the set
i(K)
: for every
x,y\in i(K)
there exists
\sigma\in\mathscr{O}(K)
such that
\sigma(x)=y
.

Note that every lens has inball symmetries, and furthermore,
L
is invariant under the action of
\mathscr{O}(L)
. However, a general convex body
K
need not be invariant under the action of
\mathscr{O}(K)
.

5.1.
Proof of Theorem
C

The proof begins in a similar way to the proof of Theorem
A
. Starting with
K
, we can construct a
1
-convex polytope
\widetilde{K}
such that all of its faces are tangent to the inscribed ball
B
along the finite set
T
, and
V_{j}(K)\leqslant V_{j}(\widetilde{K})
(with equality if and only if
\widetilde{K}=K
) using the monotonicity of the intrinsic volumes. We assume that
B
is centered at the origin.

Since
K
has inball symmetries, we can further choose
T
(if there is a choice) so that
\widetilde{K}
has inball symmetries as well. Note that
\mathscr{O}(\widetilde{K})
is a subgroup of
\mathscr{O}(K)
, not necessarily proper. Since
\widetilde{K}
is a
1
-convex polytope that is completely determined by
i(\widetilde{K})
, we obtain that

(5.1)

\sigma(\widetilde{K})=\widetilde{K},\quad\quad\forall\sigma\in\mathscr{O}(\widetilde{K}),

i.e., not only are the touching points invariant under the action of
\mathscr{O}(\widetilde{K})
, but the polytope itself.

Therefore, as in the proof of Theorem
A
, in order to establish Theorem
C
, we can assume that
K
is a
1
-convex polytope that satisfies the
Standing Assumptions
: the faces of
K
touch the inscribed ball in some set
T=\{p_{0},p_{1},\ldots,p_{|T|}\}
,
|T|\leqslant n+1
, and
K
satisfies (
5.1
) with
\widetilde{K}=K
.

Also, let
L
be a
1
-convex lens centered at the origin (and hence, sharing the same inscribed ball with
K
).

For a convex body
M
and
t\in\{1,\ldots,n\}
, we will use a calligraphic version of
M
, i.e.,
\mathscr{M}_{t}
, to denote a
t
-tuple of copies of
M
, i.e.,
\mathscr{M}_{t}=(M,\ldots,M)
. In this way, for example,
V(\mathscr{M}_{t},\mathscr{C}_{n-t})=V(\underbrace{M,\ldots,M}_{t},\underbrace{C,\ldots,C}_{n-t})
. We extend this for
t=0
by setting
V(\mathscr{M}_{0},\mathscr{C}_{n})=V(\underbrace{C,\ldots,C}_{n})
.

Lemma 5.2
.

Let
K
be the
1
-convex polytope that satisfies the Standing Assumption,
L
be the
1
-convex lens, and
{\bm{B}}
be the unit Euclidean ball in
\mathbb{R}^{n}
as above. Then for every
s\in\{1,\ldots,n\}
and
t\in\{0,\ldots,s-1\}
,

(5.2)

\frac{V(\mathscr{K}_{s-t},\mathscr{L}_{t},\mathscr{B}_{n-s})}{V(\mathscr{K}_{s-t-1},\mathscr{L}_{t+1},\mathscr{B}_{n-s})}\leqslant 1.

Moreover, the equality holds if and only if
K
is a lens.

This lemma immediately yields Theorem
C
. Indeed, to show
V_{j}(K)\leqslant V_{j}(L)
for a given
j\in\{1,\ldots,n\}
, in view of (
2.3
), we need to show that

\frac{V(\mathscr{K}_{j},\mathscr{B}_{n-j})}{V(\mathscr{L}_{j},\mathscr{B}_{n-j})}\leqslant 1.

The following chain of the inequalities is obtained by applying Lemma
5.2
for
s=j
and
t\in\{0,\ldots,j-1\}

\frac{1}{V(\mathscr{L}_{j},\mathscr{B}_{n-j})}\overset{t=j-1}{\leqslant}\frac{1}{V(\mathscr{K}_{1},\mathscr{L}_{j-1},\mathscr{B}_{n-j})}\overset{t=j-2}{\leqslant}\ldots\overset{t=1}{\leqslant}\frac{1}{V(\mathscr{K}_{j-1},\mathscr{L}_{1},\mathscr{B}_{n-j})}\overset{t=0}{\leqslant}\frac{1}{V(\mathscr{K}_{j},\mathscr{B}_{n-j})}.

Multiplying the above by
V(\mathscr{K}_{j},\mathscr{B}_{n-j})
, we get

\frac{V(\mathscr{K}_{j},\mathscr{B}_{n-j})}{V(\mathscr{L}_{j},\mathscr{B}_{n-j})}\leqslant 1

as needed. The equality case follows from the equality case in Lemma
5.2
. This finishes the proof of Theorem
C
. The remainder of the section will be devoted to the proof of Lemma
5.2
.

5.2.
Proof of Lemma
5.2

Let
s\in\{1,\ldots,n\}
. We claim that it is enough to prove Lemma
5.2
for
t=s-1
. Indeed, let
t\in\{0,\ldots,s-2\}
. Then, by the Alexandrov–Fenchel inequality and the symmetry of mixed volumes, we have the following

\frac{V(\mathscr{K}_{s-t},\mathscr{L}_{t},\mathscr{B}_{n-s})}{V(\mathscr{K}_{s-t-1},\mathscr{L}_{t+1},\mathscr{B}_{n-s})}\leqslant\frac{V(\mathscr{K}_{s-t-1},\mathscr{L}_{t+1},\mathscr{B}_{n-s})}{V(\mathscr{K}_{s-t-2},\mathscr{L}_{t+2},\mathscr{B}_{n-s})}.

If
\mathcal{Q}:=(\mathscr{K}_{s-t-2},\mathscr{L}_{t},\mathscr{B}_{n-s})
, then the last inequality becomes

\frac{V(K,K,\mathcal{Q})}{V(K,L,\mathcal{Q})}\leqslant\frac{V(K,L,\mathcal{Q})}{V(L,L,\mathcal{Q})}.

Using the Alexandrov–Fenchel inequality
s-t-1\geqslant 1
times, we obtain the following sequence of inequalities:

\displaystyle\frac{V(\mathscr{K}_{s-t},\mathscr{L}_{t},\mathscr{B}_{n-s})}{V(\mathscr{K}_{s-t-1},\mathscr{L}_{t+1},\mathscr{B}_{n-s})}

\displaystyle\leqslant\frac{V(\mathscr{K}_{s-t-1},\mathscr{L}_{t+1},\mathscr{B}_{n-s})}{V(\mathscr{K}_{s-t-2},\mathscr{L}_{t+2},\mathscr{B}_{n-s})}\leqslant\frac{V(\mathscr{K}_{s-t-2},\mathscr{L}_{t+2},\mathscr{B}_{n-s})}{V(\mathscr{K}_{s-t-3},\mathscr{L}_{t+3},\mathscr{B}_{n-s})}\leqslant\ldots

\displaystyle\ldots\leqslant\frac{V(\mathscr{K}_{1},\mathscr{L}_{s-1},\mathscr{B}_{n-s})}{V(\mathscr{L}_{s},\mathscr{B}_{n-s})}.

If the claim is true for
t=s-1
then

\frac{V(\mathscr{K}_{1},\mathscr{L}_{s-1},\mathscr{B}_{n-s})}{V(\mathscr{L}_{s},\mathscr{B}_{n-s})}\leqslant 1.

This yields the required inequality for any
t\in\{0,\ldots,s-2\}
together with the equality case.

Now we prove the case
t=s-1
. Let

\mathcal{C}:=(\mathscr{L}_{s-1},\mathscr{B}_{n-s}).

We need to show that

(5.3)

\frac{V(\mathscr{K}_{1},\mathscr{L}_{s-1},\mathscr{B}_{n-s})}{V(\mathscr{L}_{s},\mathscr{B}_{n-s})}=\frac{V(K,\mathscr{L}_{s-1},\mathscr{B}_{n-s})}{V(L,\mathscr{L}_{s-1},\mathscr{B}_{n-s})}=\frac{V(K,\mathcal{C})}{V(L,\mathcal{C})}\leqslant 1,

with equality if and only if
K
is a
1
-convex lens.

Similar to the proof of Theorem
A
, let
F_{i}
be a facet of the
1
-convex polytope
K
tangent to
B
at
p_{i}
. Let
\mathcal{N}_{F_{i}}\subset\mathbb{S}^{n-1}
be the radial projection of
F_{i}
onto the unit sphere
\mathbb{S}^{n-1}
with respect to the origin.

Assume that the
1
-convex lens
L
is placed so that its center is at the origin and one of its facets, which we call
F
, touches the inscribed ball
B
at the point
p_{i}
. We denote
\mathcal{N}_{F}\subset\mathbb{S}^{n-1}
a similar radial projection of
F
on
B
. We have the same inclusion (
3.2
).

The following lemma is an analogue of Lemma
3.1
. We postpone its proof to the end of this section. The main difference to the earlier proof is that, a priori, the measure
S(\sigma_{i}\mathcal{C})(u)
might not have a smooth monotone density, and hence we cannot use the Fubini argument that we used in the proof of Lemma
3.1
. Instead, our argument will follow the approximation strategy similar to the one in
[

, Section 4]
.

Lemma 5.3
.

(5.4)

\frac{\int\limits_{\mathcal{N}_{F_{i}}}h_{K}(u)\,dS(\sigma_{i}\mathcal{C})(u)}{\int\limits_{\mathcal{N}_{F_{i}}}dS(\sigma_{i}\mathcal{C})(u)}\leqslant\frac{\int\limits_{\mathcal{N}_{F}}h_{L}(u)\,dS(\sigma_{i}\mathcal{C})(u)}{\int\limits_{\mathcal{N}_{F}}dS(\sigma_{i}\mathcal{C})(u)}=\frac{V(L,\mathcal{C})}{V({\bm{B}},\mathcal{C})}.

where
\sigma_{i}
is the rotation about the origin that moves

so that one of its faces touches the inscribed ball
B
at
p_{i}
. Moreover, the equality holds if and only if
\mathcal{N}_{F}=\mathcal{N}_{F_{i}}
(and thus, if and only if
F=F_{i}
).

By definition and our choice of
p_{0}
, each
\sigma_{i}
is an element of
\mathscr{O}(K)
that rotates
p_{0}
to
p_{i}
. We put
\sigma_{0}=id
.

We know that
\sigma_{i}(K)=K
, and thus
h_{\sigma_{i}(K)}=h_{K}
and
\mathcal{N}_{F_{i}}=\mathcal{N}_{\sigma_{i}(F_{i})}
. Hence, by this invariance and (

(5.5)

\displaystyle\int\limits_{\mathcal{N}_{F_{i}}}h_{K}(u)\,dS(\mathcal{C})(u)=\int\limits_{\mathcal{N}_{\sigma_{i}(F_{i})}}h_{\sigma_{i}(K)}(u)

\displaystyle dS(\sigma_{i}\mathcal{C})(u)=\int\limits_{\mathcal{N}_{F_{i}}}h_{K}(u)\,dS(\sigma_{i}\mathcal{C})(u),

\displaystyle\int\limits_{\mathcal{N}_{F_{i}}}dS(\mathcal{C})(u)

\displaystyle=\int\limits_{\mathcal{N}_{F_{i}}}dS(\sigma_{i}\mathcal{C})(u).

Then, summing over all facets of

and using (

) and Lemma

, we obtain:

\displaystyle V(K,\mathcal{C})

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{n}\int\limits_{\mathbb{S}^{n-1}}h_{K}(u)\,dS(\mathcal{C})(u)=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i}\int\limits_{\mathcal{N}_{F_{i}}}h_{K}(u)\,dS(\mathcal{C})(u)=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i}\int\limits_{\mathcal{N}_{F_{i}}}h_{K}(u)\,dS(\sigma_{i}\mathcal{C})(u)

\displaystyle\leqslant\frac{V(L,\mathcal{C})}{V({\bm{B}},\mathcal{C})}\cdot\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i}\int\limits_{\mathcal{N}_{F_{i}}}dS(\sigma_{i}\mathcal{C})(u)=\frac{V(L,\mathcal{C})}{V({\bm{B}},\mathcal{C})}\cdot\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i}\int\limits_{\mathcal{N}_{F_{i}}}dS(\mathcal{C})(u)

\displaystyle=\frac{V(L,\mathcal{C})}{V({\bm{B}},\mathcal{C})}\cdot\frac{1}{n}\int_{\mathbb{S}^{n-1}}dS(\mathcal{C})(u)=V(L,\mathcal{C}),

which yields (

.

5.3.
Proof of Lemma
5.3

For brevity, let us denote
\mu:=S(\sigma_{i}\mathcal{C})
. Similar to Lemma
3.1
, it is enough to establish

(5.6)

\frac{\int\limits_{\mathcal{N}_{F_{i}}}h_{L}(u)\,d\mu(u)}{\int\limits_{\mathcal{N}_{F_{i}}}d\mu(u)}\leqslant\frac{\int\limits_{\mathcal{N}_{F}}h_{L}(u)\,d\mu(u)}{\int\limits_{\mathcal{N}_{F}}d\mu(u)}=:A_{n}.

We need to show that

(5.7)

\mathcal{R}[X]:=\int\limits_{X}\left(h_{L}(u)-A_{n}\right)\,du\leqslant 0\quad\text{ for }\quad X=\mathcal{N}_{F_{i}}\subset\mathbb{S}^{n-1}.

Note that
\mathcal{R}[\mathcal{N}_{F}]=0
. Furthermore, by construction,
\mathcal{N}_{F}
is equal to the closed hemisphere in
\mathbb{S}^{n-1}
centered at
p_{i}
, while
\mathcal{N}_{F_{i}}
is a closed convex polytope in this hemisphere.

Let us denote
g(u):=h_{L}(u)-A_{n}
. The function
g
and the measure
\mu
are both rotationally invariant with respect to the rotations in
\mathbb{S}^{n-1}
about
p_{i}
.

For
\vartheta\in[0,1]
, consider a pair of oriented hyperplanes passing through
op_{i}
and making the (oriented) angle
2\pi\vartheta
. Denote by
H_{\vartheta}
the wedge of angle
2\pi\vartheta
between these hyperplanes. Finally, define
C_{\vartheta}:=H_{\vartheta}\cap\mathcal{N}_{F}
. Pick
k\in\mathbb{N}_{>0}
and let
C_{1/k}^{j}
,
j\in\{1,\ldots,k\}
, be a cyclically ordered set of
k
spherical wedges with angle
2\pi/k
that tile
\mathcal{N}_{F}
. We have

\mathcal{R}[\mathcal{N}_{F}]=\int_{\mathcal{N}_{F}}g(u)d\mu(u)=0,\quad\bigcup_{j=1}^{k}C^{j}_{1/k}=\mathcal{N}_{F}.

Hence, by rotational symmetry,

(5.8)

\int_{C_{1/k}}g(u)d\mu(u)=0,\quad\forall k\in\mathbb{N}_{>0},\forall C_{1/k}.

The function
g(u)
is strictly monotonically increasing along any geodesic ray in
\mathcal{N}_{F}
starting at
p_{i}
. The same is true for the function
h_{L}(u)
. Therefore, along any ray starting at
p_{i}
there exists a unique point
u_{0}\in\mathcal{N}_{F}
where
g(u_{0})=0
. This easily follows from the fact that
g(u)
is rotationally invariant and
\mathcal{R}[\mathcal{N}_{F}]=0
. All such points
u_{0}
form an
(n-2)
-dimensional sphere
S^{0}\subset\mathcal{N}_{F}
centered at
p_{i}
with the spherical radius
r_{0}
satisfying
g(r_{0})=0
(or equivalently,
h_{L}(r_{0})=A_{n}
). This sphere splits
\mathcal{N}_{F}
into two regions: the open ball
D^{-}
containing
p_{i}
, and a spherical annulus
D^{+}
, so that
\mathcal{N}_{F}=D^{-}\sqcup S_{0}\sqcup D^{+}
. The function
g(u)
is negative for every
u\in D^{-}
and is positive for every
u\in D^{+}
. The same conclusion is true for
C_{1/k}\cap D^{-}
and
C_{1/k}\cap D^{+}
. Therefore, for every geodesic ball
D_{t}\subset\mathbb{S}^{n-1}
of spherical radius

centered at

we have that

(5.9)

\int_{D_{t}\cap C_{1/k}}g(u)d\mu(u)\leqslant 0,\quad\text{with equality if and only if}\quad t=\frac{\pi}{2}.

Indeed, if
D_{t}\cap C_{1/k}\subset(D^{-}\sqcup S^{0})\cap C_{1/k}
, then the conclusion is obviously true because
g
is negative in
D^{-}
. If
D_{t}\cap C_{1/k}\supset(D^{-}\sqcup S^{0})\cap C_{1/k}
and
\int_{D_{t}\cap C_{1/k}}g(u)d\mu(u)\geqslant 0
, then

\int_{C_{1/k}}g(u)d\mu(u)=\int_{D_{t}\cap C_{1/k}}g(u)d\mu(u)+\int_{(D^{+}\setminus D_{t})\cap C_{1/k}}g(u)d\mu(u)>0

because

is positive in
D^{+}
; this is a contradiction to (

). Hence (
5.9
) is true.

Construct a sequence
C^{j}_{1/k}
of wedges,
j\in\{1,\ldots,k\}
, such that
C^{j}_{1/k}
’s have disjoint interiors, and a sequence

of balls such that for every

\mathcal{N}_{F}=\bigcup_{j=1}^{k}C^{j}_{1/k},\quad\quad\mathcal{N}_{F_{i}}\subset\bigcup_{j=1}^{k}\big(D_{t_{k,j}}\cap C^{j}_{1/k}\big),

and such that

\bigcup_{j=1}^{k}\big(D_{t_{k,j}}\cap C^{j}_{1/k}\big)\underset{k\to\infty}{\longrightarrow}\mathcal{N}_{F_{i}}

in the Hausdorff metric on
\mathbb{S}^{n-1}
. More specifically, for each

, we define

to be the smallest radius such that the ball

contains
\mathcal{N}_{F_{i}}\cap C^{j}_{1/k}
. This approximation is similar to the one in
[
DT
]
.

Therefore, since the function

is bounded on
\mathcal{N}_{F}
, we obtain

\int\limits_{\mathcal{N}_{F_{i}}}g(u)\,d\mu(u)=\lim_{k\to\infty}\sum_{j=1}^{k}\int\limits_{D_{t_{k,j}}\cap C^{j}_{1/k}}g(u)\,d\mu(u)\leqslant^{\eqref{Eq:Appriximation}}0,

which is the desired inequality (
5.7
).

Let us analyze the equality case. If
\mathcal{N}_{F_{i}}\subset D^{-}\sqcup S^{0}
, then
\int_{\mathcal{N}_{F_{i}}}g(u)d\mu(u)<0
. Thus, we can assume that
\mathcal{N}_{F_{i}}
intersects
D^{+}
. If
\mathcal{N}_{F}\neq\mathcal{N}_{F_{i}}
, then there exists a sufficiently large

and
j_{0}\in\{1,\ldots,k_{0}\}
, such that

(5.10)

\mathcal{N}_{F}\cap C^{j_{0}}_{1/k_{0}}\supsetneq D_{t_{k_{0},j_{0}}}\cap C^{j_{0}}_{1/k_{0}}\supseteq\mathcal{N}_{F_{i}}\cap C^{j_{0}}_{1/{k_{0}}}\supsetneq D^{-}\cap C^{j_{0}}_{1/k_{0}}.

For

\mathcal{U}_{k}:=\bigcup_{j=1}^{k}\big(D_{t_{k,j}}\cap C^{j}_{1/k}\big)
into two subsets

and
\mathcal{Y}_{k}
with disjoint interiors such that

\mathcal{X}_{k}:=\overline{C^{j_{0}}_{1/k_{0}}\cap\mathcal{U}_{k}},\quad\mathcal{Y}_{k}:=\overline{\mathcal{U}_{k}\setminus\mathcal{X}_{k}}.

By passing to a subsequence if necessary, we can assume that for

if

, then

(for example, we can choose

\int_{\mathcal{Y}_{k}}g(u)d\mu(u)\leqslant 0

\displaystyle\sum_{j=1}^{k}\int\limits_{D_{t_{k,j}}\cap C^{j}_{1/k}}g(u)\,d\mu(u)

\displaystyle=\int\limits_{\mathcal{X}_{k}}g(u)\,d\mu(u)+\int\limits_{\mathcal{Y}_{k}}g(u)\,d\mu(u)

\displaystyle\leqslant^{\eqref{Eq:Inc}}\int\limits_{D_{t_{k_{0},j_{0}}}\cap C^{j_{0}}_{1/k_{0}}}g(u)\,d\mu(u)=:E<^{\eqref{Eq:Appriximation}\eqref{Eq:Inc}}0

Therefore,
\int\limits_{\mathcal{N}_{F_{i}}}g(u)\,d\mu(u)=\lim_{k\to\infty}\sum_{j=1}^{k}\int\limits_{D_{t_{k,j}}\cap C^{j}_{1/k}}g(u)\,d\mu(u)\leqslant E<0
, thus we have the strict inequality. Thus we obtained that the only way we can achieve equality is when

. This finishes the proof of Lemma

.
