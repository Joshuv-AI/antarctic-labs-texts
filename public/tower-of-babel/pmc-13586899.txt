Residency Competency Assessments and Early-Career Primary Care Physicians’ Antibiotic Prescribing
Authors: Jesse Burk-Rafel, Hannah Park, MacIntosh Cornwell, Eric S. Holmboe, Kenji Yamazaki, Sally A. Santen, Yoon Soo Park, John S. Andrews, Sean O. Hogan, Judee Richardson
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
This cohort study assesses the association between the competency ratings received at the end of residency and antibiotic prescribing to older adults by internal medicine and family medicine physicians during their first 5 years of practice.

Introduction

Outpatient antibiotic prescribing tendencies vary across physicians, with important consequences for older adults, who receive more outpatient antibiotics than any other age group and are uniquely susceptible to antibiotic-related complications.1,2,3,4 Up to 15% of all antibiotics prescribed to older adults are deemed unnecessary,5 and nearly half of primary care physician (PCP) visits for viral respiratory infections result in antibiotic treatment.4 Understanding the sources of this between-physician variation is therefore central to antibiotic stewardship.6,7

Variation in antibiotic prescribing reflects interacting patient, practice, and physician factors—including comorbidity burden, local culture, and diagnostic uncertainty8,9,10,11,12—yet substantial variation across physicians remains unexplained. Large-scale administrative claims offer a scalable means of characterizing this variation and testing its association with modifiable factors, although they capture prescribing rates rather than clinical appropriateness. Identifying physicians and settings with persistently higher prescribing is nonetheless a first step toward targeting stewardship.

One potentially modifiable factor is residency training. Residency is a formative period that “imprints” durable practice patterns,13,14,15,16,17 particularly during the first decade of practice, and may represent a critical window for shaping prescribing behaviors.18 Prior studies demonstrate wide variation in residents’ antibiotic prescribing19,20,21 and have linked communication skills on licensing examinations to prescribing appropriateness.22,23 However, whether residency leaves a measurable imprint on antibiotic prescribing in subsequent practice remains unknown, and, to our knowledge, no studies have examined whether standardized competency assessments or other residency training factors are associated with prescribing patterns after graduation.

We therefore aimed to evaluate whether PCPs’ Accreditation Council for Graduate Medical Education (ACGME) Milestone competency ratings (hereafter competency ratings) toward the end of residency are associated with their early-career pattern of antibiotic prescribing to older adults, and to quantify the role of residency programs in prescribing variation. By linking residency assessments with national Medicare claims, we sought to identify individual- and program-level factors associated with prescribing variation. We hypothesized that higher assessed competence near the end of residency would be associated with lower antibiotic prescribing rates in early primary care practice.

Methods

Study Design and Setting

We conducted a retrospective cohort study of PCPs who graduated from US ACGME-accredited internal medicine and family medicine residency programs between January 1, 2015, and December 31, 2020. Graduates’ ACGME competency ratings and residency program information were linked to yearly, panel-level Medicare Part D antibiotic prescription claims in their first 5 years of practice. The New York University Langone Health Institutional Review Board approved this study and waived the informed consent requirement because the research involved secondary analysis of existing data, posed no more than minimal risk, and could not practicably be carried out without a waiver given the large cohort. We followed the Strengthening the Reporting of Observational Studies in Epidemiology (STROBE) reporting guideline.

Study Population and Eligibility Criteria

A total of 47 591 internal medicine and 22 833 family medicine residents who graduated between 2015 and 2020 were assessed for eligibility (eFigure 1 in Supplement 1). Graduates were excluded if they did not have a National Provider Identifier (NPI), were not practicing primary care, or did not have available Medicare Part D prescription claims. PCPs were defined as general family medicine, general internal medicine, and medicine-pediatrics practitioners according to the terminal specialty designation in the American Medical Association (AMA) Physician Professional Data (as of January 2023). Physicians with a terminal specialty of hospitalist, any subspecialty, or other non–primary care designation were excluded. Records with data quality issues (eg, missing or non–US state identifiers) were excluded.

Data Sources and Variable Definitions

Exposure Measure

Milestone competency ratings (version 1.0) for the cohort were obtained under the terms of a data-sharing agreement. The version 1.0 ratings have since been updated to version 2.0; we selected version 1.0 to ensure graduates had multiple years of early practice data. We focused on competency ratings from the middle of the final residency year (typically third postgraduate year) rather than terminal ratings because the mid-year ratings afford time for in-training interventions and have less desirability bias, with a wide-range of performance (eFigure 2 in Supplement 1).24

Competency ratings assess 6 core competency domains (patient care, medical knowledge, professionalism, interpersonal and communication skills, systems-based practice, and practice-based learning and improvement) through 22 subcompetencies on a 5-point developmental scale (eTables 1 and 2 in Supplement 1). Internal medicine and family medicine use the same domains but different behavioral anchors for each subcompetency, which are rated across developmental levels from 1 to 5, with 0.5-point rating increments.25,26 In both specialties, a rating of 4.0 is set as a graduation target indicating assessed readiness for unsupervised practice. However, this score is not a graduation requirement, and graduation readiness is at the discretion of program directors. Scores below 4.0 indicate performance that is not yet at the graduation target, while scores above 4.0 indicate aspirational performance that is typical of someone in practice for several years. Within each specialty, the criterion-based scale is consistent across residency programs, but residency programs have discretion about their processes for arriving at ratings.27 Ratings are assigned by each program’s Clinical Competency Committee through a structured review of multiple assessment sources, rather than by simple calculations of supervisor ratings or resident self-report.

Prior work indicates that inappropriate antibiotic prescribing may involve multifactorial breakdowns in physicians’ medical knowledge, system navigation, and communication skills.9,11 We thus sought to create a composite primary exposure measure reflecting overall assessed competence. Subcompetency ratings from the middle of the final residency year were averaged within each core competency domain for each resident given their collinearity, as seen in other specialties.28 The mean ratings of the 6 domains were averaged with equal weight to generate a single overall mean competency rating for each resident. To account for program-level variation in rating practices, which may represent construct-irrelevant variance,29 the overall competency rating was standardized within each residency program to a z score (setting mean [SD], 0 [1]) to serve as the primary exposure (eFigures 2-4 in Supplement 1).

Sensitivity analyses repeated the primary model using the nonstandardized overall mean competency rating and the program-standardized ratings for each of the 6 core competency domains individually. Residents’ NPI, graduation year, and associated program zip code were also abstracted from ACGME-provided data files.

Outcome Measure

We used the Centers for Medicare & Medicaid Services (CMS) Medicare Part D Prescriber Public Use Files30 from January 1, 2015, to December 31, 2022, to assess physicians’ annual antibiotic prescriptions. This dataset contains 100% of Medicare Part D final-action prescription drug events for beneficiaries enrolled in the Part D prescription drug program, which includes both standalone prescription drug plans and the Medicare Advantage Prescription Drug Plans. These beneficiaries include approximately 78.5% of all Medicare beneficiaries, who in turn consist of nearly all US adults 65 years or older.31 The annual data are aggregated at the physician-year level, including antibiotic claim count (antibiotics specified in the CMS Drug Category List32), total prescription claims, and total beneficiaries.

CMS suppresses prescription claim counts of 1 to 10 for privacy, resulting in missing-not-at-random data for 7.3% of physician-years. In sensitivity analyses, these missing values were imputed using 3 approaches: weighted random assignment based on decile ranks of the physician’s mean prescription counts across years; bounding suppressed antibiotic counts to 1, with corresponding beneficiary counts set to 10 (lower bound); and bounding suppressed antibiotic counts to 10, with beneficiary counts set to 1 (upper bound).

Only claims data from each physician’s residency graduation year to 5 years after graduation were analyzed. Data were joined via NPI.

Covariate Data Sources

Physician characteristics were obtained from the AMA,33 including self-reported sex, medical school location, medical degree type, practice location, and terminal specialty. The National Plan and Provider Enumeration System downloadable file34 was used to ascertain practice location when not available in the AMA data.

Residency program and primary care practice locations were geocoded according to rurality (county with a US Department of Agriculture rural-urban continuum code >3)35 and medically underserved or health professional shortage area (according to Health Resources & Services Administration files).36

Physician-level Medicare Part B datasets (2015-2022)37 were used to capture additional panel-level beneficiary characteristics. These characteristics were age, sex, hierarchical comorbidity conditions risk score, and prevalence of 13 relevant comorbidities such as chronic obstructive pulmonary disease or asthma and diabetes.

Statistical Analysis

The unit of analysis was physician-prescription year. Descriptive statistics were performed to analyze baseline characteristics, stratified by physician specialty. Means (SDs) were used to summarize all continuous measures. Welch 2-sample t test and Pearson χ2 test were used to compare continuous and categorical variables, respectively. Statistical significance was set at 2-sided P < .05. We did not adjust for multiple comparisons.

The primary outcome was panel-level yearly antibiotic prescription rate (the number of antibiotic prescriptions per 100 Medicare beneficiaries). We modeled antibiotic prescribing rate using negative-binomial mixed-effects models with random intercepts for physician, residency program, and practice state, which produces an adjusted incidence rate ratio (IRR). Models were estimated using the glmmTMB package in R (R Project for Statistical Computing) with the Broyden-Fletcher-Goldfarb-Shanno optimizer, with SEs based on the random-effects variance structure. Convergence diagnostics indicated stable solutions with no gradient issues, and DHARMa residual checks did not detect overdispersion. Specialty was included with other fixed effects. Physician-level, residency program–level, and state-level variance components were estimated using models with only random effects for these factors. To aid in interpretation, we converted adjusted IRRs to absolute differences per 100 beneficiaries by applying the IRR to the cohort’s mean prescribing rate. Models were fit using complete-case data.

Because the analytic sample included only physicians with Medicare antibiotic prescribing data, we conducted inverse-probability weighted analyses to assess potential selection bias. The stabilized trimmed weights were well-centered on 1 with minimal efficiency loss, and all baseline covariates were below the 0.1 standardized mean difference threshold after weighting, confirming balance.

We also analyzed residency program–level tendencies by calculating the mean unadjusted yearly antibiotic prescription rates across all graduates from each residency program. Program quintiles for unadjusted antibiotic prescribing rate were then calculated within each specialty. Quintiles were used to summarize program-level prescribing for interpretability and to estimate stability given variable graduate counts per program. In addition, we examined practice-state quintiles of unadjusted antibiotic prescribing rates to capture variation associated with where physicians ultimately practice.

Sensitivity analyses included the overall mean competency rating, program-standardized ratings for each of the 6 core competency domains, and imputed censored prescription data. Data analyses were performed between March 2024 and May 2026 using R, version 4.4.2 (R Project for Statistical Computing).

Results

Cohort Characteristics

The study cohort included 31 800 PCPs (mean [SD] age on practice entry, 32.8 [4.2] years; 15 990 females [50%], 15 807 males [50%]) from 1090 residency programs, representing 129 225 physician-years of prescribing data (Table). Among these physicians, 58% graduated from a US medical school, 90% earned an MD degree, and 51% specialized in internal medicine and 49% specialized in family medicine. Each physician panel had a mean (SD) of 169 (144) Medicare beneficiaries per year with a mean (SD) age of 70.3 (3.5) years and predominantly had female patients (100 [59%]) (eTable 3 in Supplement 1). Each physician wrote a mean (SD) of 54.1 (52.2) antibiotic prescriptions per year, with substantial variation (eFigure 5 in Supplement 1), and the cohort wrote 7 664 372 antibiotic prescriptions across the study period.

Competency Ratings and Antibiotic Prescribing Rate

The physician-level mean (SD) competency rating at the middle of the final residency year was 3.6 (0.4); 79.0% of the PCP cohort had a mean rating below the graduation target of 4.0 (eFigure 2 in Supplement 1). Each 1-SD increase in program-standardized overall competency rating was associated with a 2% lower annual antibiotic prescription rate (IRR, 0.98; 95% CI, 0.97-0.99; P < .001), while adjusting for panel-level patient comorbidities, prescribing volume, and repeated measures at the physician level, residency program level, and state level (Figure 1); the full model is provided in eTable 4 in Supplement 1. This rate corresponds to 0.7 (95% CI, 0.5-0.9) fewer antibiotic prescriptions per 100 beneficiaries per year, or approximately 1.2 fewer prescriptions annually for a typical patient panel.

Residency Program–Level Differences

Individual residency programs were associated with meaningful variation in antibiotic prescribing (Figure 2). In unadjusted analyses, graduates of internal medicine programs with the lowest prescribing rate (quintile 1) wrote 28.5 (95% CI, 27.9-29.2) antibiotic prescriptions per 100 beneficiaries in primary care practice compared with 43.4 (95% CI, 42.6-44.2) antibiotic prescriptions per 100 beneficiaries for programs with the highest prescribing rate (quintile 5), a difference of 14.9 (95% CI, 13.8-15.9; P < .001). Among graduates of family medicine programs, the corresponding prescribing rates were 21.6 (95% CI, 21.1-22.1) antibiotic prescriptions per 100 beneficiaries in quintile 1 and 40.4 (95% CI, 39.6-41.2) antibiotic prescriptions per 100 beneficiaries in quintile 5, a difference of 18.8 (95% CI, 17.9-19.7; P < .001).

Differences were also observed at the state level. Internal medicine and family medicine PCPs practicing in states with the lowest prescribing rate vs states with the highest prescribing rate wrote 8.3 (95% CI, 7.2-9.3; P < .001) and 12.8 (95% CI, 11.9-13.7; P < .001) more antibiotic prescriptions per 100 beneficiaries, respectively. Residency program–level and state-level differences were additive. Internal medicine and family medicine PCPs who graduated from programs with the highest prescribing rate and went on to practice in states with the highest prescribing rate wrote 19.2 (95% CI, 17.1-21.3; P < .001) and 22.2 (95% CI, 20.9-23.6; P < .001) more antibiotic prescriptions per 100 beneficiaries, respectively, compared with graduates from programs with the lowest prescribing rate who went on to practice in states with the lowest prescribing rate (eFigure 6 in Supplement 1).

In models unadjusted for patient factors, residency programs accounted for 9.4% of prescribing variance, whereas competency ratings accounted for 0.3% but improved model fit (eTable 5 in Supplement 1). Program-level variance decreased to 3.4% after adjustment for patient-panel characteristics (eTable 6 in Supplement 1). This relative reduction of 64% suggests that systematic differences in patient populations across programs explain much of the program-level variation, while the persisting variation may reflect the training environment and other unmeasured factors.

Other Factors

Physician factors (male sex [IRR, 1.09; 95% CI, 1.07-1.10; P < .001]; non–US medical school [IRR, 1.09; 95% CI, 1.08-1.11; P < .001]; Doctor of Osteopathic Medicine degree [IRR, 1.04; 95% CI, 1.02-1.07; P < .001]), practice characteristics (rural setting [IRR, 1.18; 95% CI, 1.15-1.21; P < .001]), and patient panels that were older (IRR, 1.02; 95% CI, 1.02-1.02; P < .001) and had more comorbidities (IRR, 1.21; 95% CI, 1.20-1.22; P < .001]) were associated with increased annual antibiotic prescribing (eTable 4 in Supplement 1).

Because these data reflect prescribing volume and not clinical appropriateness, these associations should not be interpreted as indicating inappropriate prescribing by any group. Specialty was not associated with differences in prescribing rate in adjusted analyses.

Each year of experience (ie, time since residency graduation) was associated with higher antibiotic prescribing (IRR, 1.01; 95% CI, 1.01-1.02; P < .001). The association held even when accounting for the reduction in prescribing that occurred across study years (IRR, 0.98; 95% CI, 0.97-0.98; P < .001).

Sensitivity Analyses

Findings were consistent across sensitivity analyses. Models using raw overall competency ratings yielded a comparable association (IRR, 0.95; 95% CI, 0.93-0.96; P < .001) (eTable 7 in Supplement 1). Program-standardized ratings for each of the 6 core competency domains showed similar associations, with IRRs ranging from 0.98 to 0.99 per 1-SD increase (all with P < .001) (eTables 8-13 in Supplement 1). Estimates were stable after imputing missing prescription counts, including imputation to the lower and upper bounds (IRR ranging from 0.98 to 0.99) (eTables 14-16 in Supplement 1). Inverse probability weighting to account for potential selection bias produced consistent results (IRR, 0.98; 95% CI, 0.98-0.99; P < .001) (eTable 17 in Supplement 1).

Discussion

In this national cohort of 31 800 PCPs, higher competency ratings near the end of residency were associated with fewer antibiotic prescriptions in early practice. This association was consistent across sensitivity analyses and persisted after adjustment for patient-panel, practice, and physician factors, supporting a durable association between assessed competence and subsequent prescribing.

Residency programs also accounted for meaningful variation in prescribing. Graduates of residency programs with a quintile 5 (highest) vs quintile 1 (lowest) prescribing rate wrote between 15 and 19 additional antibiotic prescriptions per 100 Medicare beneficiaries, underscoring the association between training and subsequent practice. These findings extend prior work linking relatively fixed physician and program characteristics, such as degree type or training program, to durable differences in complication rates, spending, and clinical conservatism15,16,17 and advance it by showing that standardized competency assessments obtained months before graduation are associated with prescribing behaviors. Competency ratings capture modifiable skills central to guideline-concordant prescribing, including evidence-based decision-making, patient communication, and system navigation.2,6 Programs whose graduates prescribe at higher rates may find prescribing data useful for formative self-assessment and program improvement, for example by incorporating audit and feedback, communication coaching, or simulation-based training in antibiotic prescribing. These data are not intended for summative program evaluation or accreditation given the absence of appropriateness data and potential case-mix confounding.

Recent studies have evaluated associations between competency ratings and other outcomes, such as surgical complications,38,39,40 diabetes management,41 and patient complaints,42 with mixed results. A persistent challenge in educational outcomes research is the potential dilution of training-related associations by system pressures and multifactorial attribution of outcomes to individuals.43,44,45 The association between competency and prescribing had a modest magnitude at the individual level and had a smaller effect size than the associations observed for practice location, patient comorbidity burden, and physician demographic factors. Nonetheless, modest per-physician differences accumulate at scale: applied illustratively across the cohort’s 7.6 million prescriptions, a 2% difference corresponds to approximately 150 000 prescriptions over the study period. Competency ratings thus represent one of several modifiable levers whose value lies in cumulative population impact rather than individual effect size. Antibiotic prescribing may be particularly sensitive to assessed competence because it reflects a discrete, physician-level decision. Still, competency assessments should be viewed as a tool to drive formative resident improvement rather than as a summative indicator.

Antibiotic prescribing increased with years since graduation, despite overall secular declines. This finding complements cross-sectional studies that have shown higher prescribing rates and longer durations among late-career physicians.4,46 Our longitudinal analysis suggests this may represent practice drift rather than solely generational differences. Reinforcement strategies, such as booster audit-and-feedback interventions, may be necessary to sustain lower prescribing rates observed in early practice.6

Limitations

This study has several limitations. First, we lacked visit-level indication data and therefore could not assess prescribing appropriateness. Higher prescribing rates do not necessarily indicate inappropriate use, nor do lower rates guarantee appropriateness. Second, the observational design limits causal interpretation. Third, we identified PCPs using terminal specialty designations in the AMA data; some hospitalists or subspecialists reporting general internal medicine may have been misclassified, although the requirement for a longitudinal outpatient Part D panel likely limited the inclusion of physicians with predominantly inpatient practice. Fourth, suppression of low-count prescribing in Medicare data may bias estimates toward higher-volume prescribers, although our sensitivity analyses with bounded imputation yielded consistent findings. Fifth, results may not generalize beyond Medicare beneficiaries. Sixth, this study used mid-year ratings on the version 1.0 competency rating framework, which has since been updated to version 2.0 with revised behavioral anchors; the generalizability of these findings to final ratings or the version 2.0 framework requires further study. Finally, competency rating practices may vary across programs; however, associations between overall competence and prescribing persisted after program-level standardization.

Conclusions

In this retrospective cohort study of early-career PCPs, higher assessed competence near the end of residency was associated with lower antibiotic prescribing to older adults. Both individual competency ratings and residency program characteristics were associated with prescribing patterns, with prescribing rates increasing with years since graduation despite overall secular declines. These findings suggest that standardized competency assessments could help identify prescribing variation that may inform antibiotic stewardship efforts during training and early practice, although causality was not established in this study. Future work should examine whether competency growth trajectories, rather than a single near-graduation rating, are associated with subsequent prescribing and whether higher prescribing reflects overuse. Prospective studies are needed to determine whether competency-focused interventions can alter prescribing behaviors over time.
