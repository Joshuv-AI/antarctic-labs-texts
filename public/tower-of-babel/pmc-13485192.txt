Patients' Treatment Experiences With Orthodontic Fixed Appliances, Clear Aligners and Direct‐to‐Consumer Clear Aligners: A Qualitative Study—Part II
Authors: Sherry Lee, Ben K. Daniel, Li Mei, Mauro Farella, Fiona A. Firth
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTAimUnderstanding the orthodontic patient experience is critical in providing enhanced patient‐centred care. Qualitative methods were employed to gain insight into the factors shaping an individual's orthodontic treatment experience.Objectives(1) To understand orthodontic patient experience regarding treatment progress, interactions with their treating practitioner and treatment outcome. (2) To explore the challenges patients faced during treatment.Materials and MethodsAn exploratory qualitative case study was completed. Thirty adults who completed orthodontic treatment either with fixed appliances, clear aligners, or direct‐to‐consumer clear aligners were recruited. One‐to‐one, semi‐structured interviews were conducted, and the interview transcripts were analysed with NVivo software.ResultsThree principal themes were identified: patients' experiences with orthodontic treatment, challenges encountered during treatment, and suggested improvements to enhance the patient experience. Most participants reported positive experiences with orthodontic treatment. They also reported an increase in self‐confidence following treatment. Positive interactions with the orthodontic team elevated the overall experience. In addition, participants revealed their experience of pain and trauma to the soft tissues. A few participants suggested improvements to enhance their overall experience, including flexibility in appointment times and more emphasis on the importance of elastic wear.ConclusionsPatient experience with orthodontic treatment in New Zealand was primarily positive, with few suggested improvements. There was increased self‐confidence and dental health awareness following treatment. Commonly encountered problems included gingival trauma, pain, and the effects of the COVID‐19 pandemic. Lower expectations of treatment outcomes emerged as a critical theme in the direct‐to‐consumer clear aligner group.

Introduction

Understanding the patient experience helps clinicians gain insight into treatment expectations and patient perceptions of orthodontic outcomes, both of which are critical in providing a rich and enhanced patient‐centred experience. A longitudinal qualitative study on the perspectives of 10‐to‐15‐year‐old patients on fixed orthodontic treatment reported that the perceived appearance of one's teeth was influenced by friends, family and social media [1]. Individuals of this age group were not concerned with the appearance of their braces; for some, it represented part of the adolescent stage of life [1]. Pain and discomfort, common adverse side effects of traditional fixed appliances (TFAs), were not only expected by the participants but also tolerated and normalized with time [1]. Another study reported that adolescents view braces as a “status symbol” [2]. Treatment dissatisfaction stemmed from patients' perception of their orthodontist's lack of empathy [2] and effective doctor‐to‐patient communication significantly impacted adults' satisfaction with their orthodontic treatment [3].

Challenges experienced by patients undergoing orthodontic treatment include pain [4], slow treatment progression, and longer treatment duration during the Coronavirus disease of 2019 (COVID‐19) pandemic.

Existing literature on direct‐to‐consumer (DTC) CAs is scarce as they are relatively new to the market. A survey of the user experience with DTC CAs found that 88% of the respondents were satisfied with treatment but would have preferred traditional treatment from a registered dental professional if cost and convenience were not so much of a barrier [5].

No semi‐structured interviews have examined the patient experience using CAs and DTC CAs. Thus, this qualitative study aimed to explore the factors shaping an individual's experience with orthodontic treatment in adults who completed treatment either with TFAs, CAs or DTC CAs.

Methods

Ethics

Ethical approval was obtained from the University of Otago Ethics Committee (Health) (H20/106). The rights of participants were protected.

Research Design

An exploratory case study design was employed, and the standard criteria of reporting qualitative studies: The COnsolidated criteria for REporting Qualitative Research (COREQ) checklist was followed [6].

The methodology has been presented in Part I of this study [7].

Research Sample

New Zealand‐based adults who had undergone orthodontic treatment with TFA or CAs with a registered orthodontist and DTC CA consumers. Advertisements were used on social media sites with a follow‐up phone call and email to interested participants, where their eligibility suitability was checked by a study investigator (SL). For the TFA and CA participants, emails were sent to the treating orthodontist for confirmation of treatment prior to recruitment into the study. Ten participants were recruited for each group, and participation was voluntary.

Sample Characteristics

Thirty adults, six males and 24 females aged 18–56 years, were interviewed. Ninety‐three per cent of this sample were enrolled in or had completed tertiary education. All participants in the TFA group were students, whilst most participants in the CA and DTC CA groups were working adults. Specific characteristics of each group can be found in Part I [7].

Purposive Sampling

Purposeful sampling was employed to recruit participants for the study [8]. This sampling technique was deemed relevant in this study because it enables the researcher to identify and select participants who meet some predetermined criterion of importance [9]. Full detail on the sampling method is outlined in Lee et al. [7].

Data Collection

All interviews were conducted over Zoom by a single investigator (SL), who received training in qualitative research interviews from a second study investigator (BD), an expert in qualitative research. An initial phone call was organized to inform participants of the study details and assess their participation suitability. The verification of eligibility for participation is explained in Part I [7].

A semi‐structured interview with key questions exploring the patient experience and challenges faced was conducted for all 30 participants (Supporting Information). Participants were allowed to answer each question with prompts for those who did not entirely answer the question. Interviews were up to 50 min in length. Participants were assured of data anonymity using a number identifier rather than names. All interviews were recorded on the Zoom platform and transcribed verbatim.

Data Analysis

All 30 interviews were transcribed verbatim and then analysed and coded using NVivo software (QSR International Pty Ltd. 2020) by SL. A second investigator, BD, reviewed the coding, and both investigators discussed areas that needed revision. A thematic analysis inductive approach was taken with themes and then placed into “codes” in the software to identify the most cited areas. Exceptional data were also highlighted and included in the analysis. Overall level of rigour was guided by the application of the TACT framework [10], which addresses four main dimensions in qualitative research: trustworthiness, auditability, credibility and transferability.

Theme 2: Challenges Encountered

The challenges encountered in all three groups during treatment included trauma to soft tissues and pain. Other complications included broken brackets and wires, aligner quality and fit, appointment availability, inaccessibility to care and their experience with Dental Monitoring (DM). Discussion

A multitude of factors shapes the patient experience. Understanding these factors is critical in providing a rich and enhanced patient‐centred experience. The limited number of qualitative studies focusing on the patient experience has meant that this field of orthodontics is not clearly understood.

Previous studies have reported positive psychosocial outcomes such as enhanced confidence following orthodontic treatment [11, 12], in line with our findings. Regardless of the treatment modality, most participants reported feeling satisfied and happy with their treatment outcome and would undergo orthodontic treatment again. Although the trend of high orthodontic treatment satisfaction has repeatedly been shown in the literature and our study, this may not have been the case had we explored the experiences of individuals who did not complete orthodontic treatment [2, 5, 12, 13, 14, 15].

Participants in the TFA and CA groups appreciated the quality interactions with their orthodontist and the entire orthodontic team. However, one CA participant reported limited interaction during his treatment, which led him to feel there was a lack of understanding surrounding his treatment needs. Unsurprisingly, a study which investigated factors influencing satisfaction with orthodontic treatment in adult patients found that effective communication was a key factor [3], and the doctor‐patient relationship has also been demonstrated to be associated with the level of treatment satisfaction [16].

A common theme among the DTC CA participants was their lower treatment expectations. There was a mutual understanding that for the cost of treatment, the treatment results would be of an acceptable level but not to the standards of a case treated by a dental professional. Recent studies on DTC CAs have reported that the most significant concern with DTC CAs is the quality of treatment, while this is the most significant perceived advantage of treatment provided by an orthodontist [17, 18].

Participants reported eating restrictions as a negative aspect of treatment. However, one participant acknowledged that healthier eating habits had resulted following a course of treatment, with a decrease in snacking frequency. In addition, the importance of maintaining oral hygiene during orthodontic treatment has translated to a raised awareness of dental health following treatment. Health‐related behavioural changes, including a better diet and maintaining good oral hygiene as an outcome of orthodontic treatment, have also been reported previously [11].

A DTC CA participant mentioned value for money. This participant felt that he obtained the desired result for a price that would not have been possible with professional care. Additionally, some participants in the DTC CA group were aware that DTC CA treatment was only suitable for simple malocclusions. However, this raises the issue of defining a “simple malocclusion”. Although some of our participants believed that a few crowded teeth translated to “simple treatment”, multiple other factors need to be considered. Without the proper training and expertise, one cannot assume the treatment difficulty level. Crooked teeth have been described as a motivating factor for individuals seeking orthodontic treatment [19, 20, 21], and anecdotally, patients are happy with the appearance of straight teeth. However, orthodontic alignment is only the first stage of orthodontic treatment. Correcting the occlusion and perfecting each tooth position towards the end of treatment is the challenging aspect, and there has been much discussion in the literature on finishing techniques [22, 23, 24, 25].

Patient education and awareness of occlusal correction and orthodontic finishing need to occur when treating patients to change the perceived notions of straight teeth alone being a good orthodontic outcome. A clear example of this is the treatment outcome of participant (5) from the DTC CA group, who described having only four teeth in contact as the outcome of his treatment. Although he described his teeth as straight and was happy with his outcome cosmetically, he is now seeking further treatment with an orthodontist for functional reasons.

Participants in the DTC CA group were also sceptical of the responsibility taken by the companies. Some who had no issues throughout treatment were sceptical of the support that the companies would have offered had there been a problem. Others thought that the DTC CA companies should be regulated and more accountable for the treatment they provide.

The most reported problem by participants in the TFA group was broken brackets and wires. All three groups reported trauma to the intra‐oral soft tissues in the form of ulcerations. Specifically for the DTC CA group, difficulties were noted with initial aligner insertion and aligner quality. There was a notable absence of verbal instructions on aligner insertion and care. Participants reported ill‐fitting aligners and aligners that would crack. Participants in the CA group did not report any issues with their aligners.

Inaccessibility to their treatment provider was a problem for some TFA and DTC CA participants. Those in the DTC CA group would have appreciated the ability to contact the clinician overseeing their treatment directly. Two TFA participants had a unique situation due to living rurally. Their orthodontists drove from another city weekly to provide access to orthodontic care to those in the broader region. They found it challenging to organize appointments when problems such as broken brackets arose. Unfortunately, a healthcare disparity exists between rural and urban communities, where healthcare remains much more accessible for individuals in urban settings [26, 27, 28]. Participants with TFA who lived in larger cities had breakages that were tended to within 48 h, while rurally it took 2 weeks to be seen. This undeniable phenomenon occurs worldwide and is not limited to a dental setting; the difference in care cannot be attributed to professional negligence; it is simply due to the inaccessibility of healthcare in rural communities.

Finding appointment times that suited the patient and orthodontic practice was difficult for some working adult patients in the CA group. Working adults generally prefer to have appointments at the beginning or the end of the day to minimise disruptions to their working day; however, this is understandably difficult for orthodontic practices to accommodate. Parents of school children also often request after‐school appointments, and for an orthodontic practice to run smoothly and efficiently, practices may opt to organise their appointment slots so that different parts of the day are set for different procedures, allowing for structure to their day, but reducing flexibility for patients.

The global COVID‐19 pandemic has been an abrupt interruption to our lives since 2019 and its effects have certainly extended to healthcare. A qualitative study on orthodontic patients' experiences during COVID‐19 found that dental healthcare delivery was impacted negatively [29]. This was similar to our findings where participants cited problems related to appointment times and aligner arrival delays. However, they also reported psychosocial and behavioural changes resulting from treatment disruption due to lockdowns which were not found in our study.

Remote care and DM warrant some discussion. Remote care or “tele‐orthodontics” refers to the delivery of orthodontic care through information technology [30]. Individuals who receive DTC CA treatment theoretically receive remote care, although there is arguably no evidence to suggest that a clinician oversees the entire prescribed treatment. The remote and virtual treatment suited some DTC CA participants ideally because their work commitments did not allow frequent orthodontic appointments.

A reduction in the number of in‐office visits with the DM technology has been shown in CA patients by two [31] and three appointments [32]; however, this would only result in a significant difference if the patient had a short course of treatment. A pilot study reported positive reception to the DM technology [30], although a minority of patients were dissatisfied with DM and would have preferred regular in‐office appointments [31]. Similarly, two of our participants did not enjoy DM; one disliked the battery drainage with running the app, and the second questioned the technology's accuracy. Interestingly, the accuracy of DM has been studied by two groups, who both found DM to be accurate [33, 34].

No temporomandibular joint disorders (TMD) were reported following orthodontic treatment, and those with pre‐existing issues with their temporomandibular joints noted neither improvement nor worsening of symptoms following treatment. This aligns with the current literature, which states the lack of evidence linking orthodontic treatment to TMD [35].

Most participants were satisfied with their treatment, and little discussion was offered when asked how their experiences may be improved. Participants in the CA group would prefer more flexibility in the availability of appointment times and more education regarding the importance of aligner and elastic wear. Communication was a common theme mentioned by participants in both the TFA and DTC CA groups. The DTC CA participants emphasized how they would prefer direct contact with the clinician overseeing their treatment.

The strengths of this study include an in‐depth analysis of the orthodontic experiences of 30 individuals and a rigorous process that maintained consistency throughout the process. In addition, data analysis was conducted by two SLs. However, a limitation of the data analysis process was that participants were not sent their interview transcript for editing or clarification, which may risk data misinterpretation.

The inability to extend findings beyond the sample is another limitation of the study. New Zealand adults over 18 years were recruited via advertisement over social media sites, and the data may not be generalizable to adults in other countries. In addition, we discuss the experiences of individuals receiving treatment with multiple DTC CA companies under one category—DTC CAs, and the experiences of each may differ depending on the specific DTC company and will be affected by company closure(s). Similarly, this could be the case with individuals receiving treatment from different orthodontists nationwide. As a general trend, the younger adults who made up the TFA group offered less discussion to the interview questions, and although our minimum age for inclusion into this study was 18 years, future studies could aim to recruit slightly older adults.

A further limitation of our study was the recruitment of participants who had finished their course of orthodontic treatment. This introduces a risk of memory wash‐out, with the possibility of slight inaccuracies when recalling treatment experiences. Future studies must explore the experiences of individuals who decided not to complete their entire course of treatment and the reasons behind this. There is also a need for research to systematically examine the clinical impact of DTC CA products on dental and general health beyond the aesthetic value to patients, and further qualitative studies are needed to explore the level of patient education concerning the health risks associated with the use of DTC CAs and other related commercial products.

Conclusion

This study provides insight into the patient experience with orthodontic treatment in New Zealand through three treatment modalities. Several participants expressed positive experiences with orthodontic treatment. In addition, lower expectations of treatment outcome were a theme that emerged in the DTC CA group. Following treatment, an increase in self‐confidence and dental health awareness was universally reported, and the commonly encountered challenges during treatment included trauma to the mucosal tissues, COVID‐19, and pain. Participants offered few suggestions for improvements to help enhance the individual experience with orthodontic treatment.
