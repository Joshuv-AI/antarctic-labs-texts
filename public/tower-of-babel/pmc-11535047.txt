Assessing systematic biases in farmers’ local weather change perceptions
Authors: Gaurav Arora, Hongli Feng
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
Scientific data concerning climate change are critical for designing mitigation and adaptation strategies. Equally important is how stakeholders perceive climate change because perceptions influence decision-making. In this paper, we employ spatially-delineated primary surveys to evaluate weather perception biases among corn and soybean farmers located on western frontier of the U.S. Corn Belt where substantial loss of grassland has been documented. We characterize farmers’ perception biases by measuring the gap between survey-based perception reports for three distinct weather indicators (i.e., temperature, precipitation and drought) and corresponding meteorological evidence. About 70% farmers in our sample misperceive past weather changes. Three-fourths of these misperceiving farmers over-estimate local temperatures and drought frequency and 40% of them under-estimate precipitation trends relative to past records. We further find evidence that farmers’ weather change perceptions are systematically biased in a manner that would justify past land use decisions. Particularly, higher cropping incidence on previously protected grasslands effected more farmers to under-perceive drier conditions and over-perceive wetter conditions. Our investigation of perception biases across distinct weather indicators with a reference to past economic decisions enriches the understanding of climate change perceptions and related policies.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-024-76327-8.

Introduction

Climate uncertainty is a critical risk factor in agricultural production. Large spatio-temporal datasets and sophisticated statistical models are employed to estimate the distribution of climate variables at regional, national and global scales1. Data-driven decision support systems are a centerpiece of policy designs that are intended to facilitate agricultural adaptation and mitigation amidst climate uncertainty. Meanwhile, forming climate change beliefs can be challenging given that farmers synthesize weather patterns based on local observations with a sparse sampling of extreme events such as floods, droughts and heat waves. Farmers’ beliefs are further impacted by bounded cognitive capacity2,3, exposure to mixed media signals4,5 and complex social, economic and cultural influences6–8. That farmer perceptions may diverge from climate change evidence has been recognized in the literature (e.g.,9–12).

Early studies in this literature documented farmer perceptions about future climate change (e.g.,9–11) while recent papers reported perceptions about regional-level weather changes in the past, with a greater focus on developing country contexts in Africa and Asia (e.g.,2,13,14). Climate/weather perceptions are modeled as a function of farmers’ socio-economic and institutional environments to evaluate how such factors inform their attitudes about climate change15,16. That is, whether farmers believe that climate is changing and what they perceive as climate change are understood to depend upon their social attributes such as age and education, economic situation such as income and farm size; and the willingness to mitigate climate impacts.

Despite advancements in global and local climate change estimation and the characterization of climate change perception in societies, the evidence remains rather crude for quantifying the gaps between individual perceptions and scientific data. Studies typically report an aggregate percentage measure for describing how climate perceptions compare with scientific evidence. For example, Barnes and Toma11 reported a survey of 540 Scottish dairy farmers where 46% believed that future global temperatures will increase. Similarly, Mahmood et al.13 reported that 70% of 671 farmers in northern Pakistan perceived regional temperature changes in line with the meteorological trends of past 38 years. Systematic patterns in how individual farmers’ perceptions may differ from local weather observation, and the relevant drivers, remain largely unexplored.

An understanding of heterogenous weather perceptions can inform climate policy designs by accounting for local climate information as well as farmers’ awareness and motivation to mitigate climate impacts15. Climate change and climate policies are known to generate unequal costs that depend upon local resource constraints and socio-cultural attributes of farmers. On the other hand, localized weather events can influence farmers’ climate beliefs 15–17, through the use of heuristics or psychological manipulations in processing weather information18.

In this paper we analyze potential biases in weather change perceptions, which are defined as the gap between farmer perceptions about local weather changes and corresponding data-based evidence for three weather indicators, i.e., temperature, precipitation and droughts. Our analysis seeks to address farmers’ subjective climate change attribution to a particular indicator (or a set of indicators) while many existing studies tend to examine perceptions with reference to “climate change” as a general concept9,10,19,20. We further examine farm-level heterogeneity in weather perception biases with a focus on the role of farmers’ land use decisions in comprehending such biases.

It is well-established that climate and weather perceptions influence growers’ attitudes and decisions concerning climate mitigation and adaptation15,16,21,22. For example, Mase et al.23 reported that a decision to purchase insurance or adopt technology to mitigate climate risk was associated with farmers’ climate beliefs. We emphasize that perceptions and decisions can feed into each other, i.e., perceptions affect decision making and decisions can also affect perceptions, in particular, weather perception biases. This latter channel of impacts is afforded much less attention in the literature, although some studies suggest that local experiences and economic activity can affect farmers’ weather perceptions2,24.

To our best knowledge, no study has examined whether farming decisions can lead to potentially systematic biases in weather change perceptions. We argue that, in forming weather and climate perceptions, farmers may be motivated to subconsciously distort weather signals25 through social mediation26 and exposure to market or policy shocks8,19 for validating or justifying their past land use decisions. Understanding how past decisions can contribute to perception biases would shed light on how people process climate information in a manner that might differ from objective data.

We assess the linkage between farm decisions and weather perceptions in two steps. First, we employ two-way contingency tables to test whether farmers’ weather perception biases are on-average independent of their land use decisions. In the second step, we recognize that perceptions and decisions are endogenous to each other and use economic returns as an instrument to identify the causal link between farm decisions and weather perception biases. Economic returns directly impact land use decisions; on the other hand, we expect that economic returns will affect perceptions through land use changes, and hence provide a valid instrument to estimate the impact of decisions on weather perception biases27, ch.5.

Our study makes three main contributions to the existing literature. First, we document farm-level biases in weather change perceptions by assessing spatially-delineated primary survey data on farmers’ perceptions in relation to the local weather records. Alongside weather change perceptions these surveys provide farmers’ land use information on a regional frontier between Northern Great Plains and Western Corn Belt (see Fig. 1a). Second, our inquiry moves beyond the use of a general reference to “climate change” and accounts for regional variations in distinct weather indicators (temperature, precipitation, and droughts) to test the hypothesis that perceptions are consistent with local meteorological data. Spatial heterogeneity in climate data and survey-based weather perceptions allow us to estimate farmers’ weather perception biases, i.e., the difference between perceptions (reported in primary surveys) and meteorological data, conditional on local biophysical and socio-economic contexts. Third, we recognize that perceptions of weather and climate trends can be systematically affected by past decisions, beyond the role of perceptions in affecting farming decisions. We estimate the impact of past land use decisions on farmers’ weather change (mis)perceptions. The theory of “motivated beliefs” provides a potential rationale for how farmers might subconsciously form weather perceptions to justify past decisions30,31.

Materials and methods

Data and data processing

Survey data: farmers’ weather change perceptions and land use decisions

We utilize 1026 responses from a survey of farmers located on the east of the Missouri River across 20 counties in North Dakota (ND) and 37 counties in South Dakota (SD), see Fig. 1a. Surveys were conducted from March through May in 2015 among farmers who operated 100 or more acres of land for wheat, maize, soybeans or grass/hay cultivation. Sample observations were distributed across counties in proportion of the eligible farms and the survey response rate was 36%. Farming/ranching was principal occupation of 87% respondents and 94% respondents operated the same farm/ranch for more than 10 years at the time of survey. Also, according to USDA Economic Research Service factsheets32, Dakotas experienced a steady decline in rural population shares during our study period. Thus, it is reasonable to consider our survey respondents to be long-term residents and well-aware of weather conditions in their local area.

The survey was designed to primarily study farmers’ cropland and pastureland use decisions during the commodities boom of 2004–2014, and has been utilized to shed light on important land use trends and underlying drivers28,29. Farmers were first inquired about their land use decisions and the potential drivers, followed by perceptions of past changes in local temperature, precipitation and droughts (discussed hereafter). We also collected farmland quality, income status and demographic information. The survey questionnaire pertaining to our study is provided in Supplementary Information (SM), section S1. These data were obtained within the guidelines of the Institutional Review Board of Iowa State University (please see “Availability of data and materials” statement for details).

We assess the representativeness of our survey data relative to the 2017 Census of Agriculture. Our sample constitutes farmers with 100 or more farmland acres, a criterion used to screen out very small farms or hobby farmers. With a conservative corn yield of 120 bushels per acre and corn price of $3 per bushel, a 100-acre farm generates at least $36,000 in sales. In contrast, for Census purposes, any plot that generates $1,000 or more in revenues is interpreted as a farm. Our screening criterion will imply some differences between our sample and the Census data. For example, about 25% farms in our sample were 2,000 acres or larger in size, which is well-aligned with the upper quartile of Census farms in the Dakotas. The average farm size (1,650 acres) in our surveys was larger than the average Census farm (1,450 acres). More than 95% farmers in our sample reported $50,000 or more in annual farm sales, which equals the median value of annual sales among Census farms. Farmers’ median age in our surveys, i.e., 50–59 years, was representative of the Census data. A representativeness assessment based on the 2012 Census of Agriculture revealed similar patterns of comparison29. Our surveys recorded farmers’ assessment of change in local weather patterns in 2014 compared to ten years ago. We accommodate the potential inaccuracy or bias in memory recall33–36, through alternative fuzzy delineations of ‘ten years ago’ in meteorological records (details are provided hereafter).That is, respondents were asked to select one among the following options for temperature: ‘warmer’, ‘about the same’, ‘cooler’, ‘don’t know’; precipitation: ‘more’, ‘about the same’, ‘less’, ‘don’t know’; and drought frequency: ‘more’, ‘about the same’, ‘less’, ‘don’t know’. The largest proportion of farmers perceived ‘about the same’ temperature (57% farmers), precipitation (36% farmers) and drought frequency (45% farmers) during 2004–2014. In contrast, 22% and 14% farmers perceived ‘cooler’ and ‘warmer’ temperatures, respectively; 24% and 34% farmers perceived ‘higher’ and ‘lower’ precipitation, respectively; and 29% and 19% farmers perceived ‘less’ and ‘more’ frequent droughts, respectively. About 5% responses were ‘don’t know’ and 4% farmers chose not to respond about past weather perceptions.

We evaluate a potential causal link between farmers’ weather change perceptions and their enrolment decision into the Conservation Reserve Program (CRP). The CRP is the largest land retirement program in the United States to protect highly erodible and environmentally sensitive lands37. Farmers who enroll into a CRP contract must retire their croplands for an environment-friendly use for 10–15 years, thereby giving up cropping returns in lieu of a fixed annual transfer called the ‘CRP rental payment’. The Dakotas experienced a dramatic decline in CRP acreage during 2004–201438. For example, South Dakota’s CRP coverage declined from 1.45 million acres in 2004 to 930,000 acres in 2014 when 47% of statewide CRP contracts were due to expire and up for renewal in 2007. Such a large proportion of expiring CRP contracts also implied that many farmers had the opportunity to actively consider their CRP enrolment decisions during our study period.

Cultivating on CRP lands typically entails substantial costs, including one-time land preparation cost and variable production costs during each subesquent season. Such costs would only be justified if a farmer expects high crop returns, which in turn depend on favorable weather conditions. Given that weather is uncertain and constitutes complex changes in temperature, precipiation, or both, there is scope for cognitive errors in farmers’ weather change perceptions. We will evaluate whether farmers systematically (mis)perceive local weather after they had also chosen to undertake cropping on lands that were originally protected under the CRP contract. According to the theory of motivated beliefs (explained earlier), justifying a past decision to convert CRP land might be one of the potential factors that influence weather perceptions of farmers, particularly in a manner that favors better returns from conducive weather conditions.

Weather data: temperature, precipitation and drought frequency

We match our geo-referenced farm survey data to weather station-based temperature and precipitation data, and spatial vector maps of a drought index (discussed hereafter). Daily records of temperature and precipitation levels were acquired from 825 weather stations made available by the Global Historical Climatology Network (GHCN)39. Figure 1a presents the spatial distribution of our survey respondents and GHCN stations in the area. We first assign each survey location a ‘daily temperature level’ and a ‘daily precipitation level’ as the mean value of corresponding records from the nearest three weather stations. Distribution of weather outcomes was very similar between nearest-three and nearest-five weather stations. The average value of daily temperature levels during April-August months was denoted as the ‘growing-season temperature’ whereas the sum of daily precipitation levels during April-August was denoted as the ‘growing-season precipitation’ for each survey location.

To assess farmers’ drought perceptions, we utilize vector maps made available by the U.S. Drought Monitor (USDM) on a weekly basis since the year 2000. The USDM is jointly produced by the National Drought Mitigation Centre (University of Nebraska-Lincoln), U.S. Department of Agriculture and the National Oceanic and Atmospheric Administration. These maps divide continental U.S. into six drought intensity categories: ‘exceptional drought’; ‘extreme drought’; ‘severe drought’; ‘moderate drought’; ‘abnormally dry’; and ‘no drought’. In Fig. 1b–d we illustrate drought situation in the Dakotas through USDM vector maps during the August months of 2004, 2006 (i.e., drought year) and 2014. We assign each survey location a comparison of category-wise count of drought incidences during the second week of growing-season month (April-August) in 2004 and 2014.

We intend to inquire whether past land use decisions could lead to a certain bias in farmers’ weather perceptions. That is, we ask whether farmers in our sample subconsciously ignored adverse weather information (e.g., drought) or were over-attentive towards favorable weather (e.g., higher precipitation) to validate a decision to undertake water-intensive cultivation on pre-existing grasslands? The motivated cognition theory of Benabou and Tirole30 can provide one potential economic explanation for such weather misperceptions among farmers. A farmer who converts native grassland for crop production would be motivated to expect favorable crop-based returns through beliefs that prevail over the risk of droughts in her locality. On the other hand, in case the farmer decides not to convert her grassland then she would seek to validate this decision through beliefs that support frequent drought incidence, and hence low returns from cropping. Below we formalize our hypothesis.

Hypothesis II (Weather perceptions and motivated beliefs): Farmers’ land use decisions affect their weather change perceptions. In other words, past land use decisions of farmers were systematically related to the biases in their weather change perceptions.

Assessing potential causal impact channel between land use decisions and weather perception biases

The contingency tables do not provide sufficient evidence that past CRP land conversion decisions can lead to systematic biases in farmers’ weather perceptions. Weather perceptions and land use decisions may be endogenous to each other due to reverse causality or unobservable confounders, such as farming ability, that simultaneously drive these variables. For example, farmers’ adaptive or coping capacity can influence how they perceive local weather changes (e.g.,3,23). Favorable farming abilities can also effect a costly decision to pursue crop agriculture on CRP lands. We propose to address endogeneity by specifying instruments that directly impact the CRP conversion decision but influence weather perceptions only through the land use channel27, ch. 5)].

Our choice of instruments is motivated by the local context of declining CRP acreage during 2004–2014, which coincided with sharp rise in crop prices and cropland returns44,45. Land-based returns are known to drive land use decisions, and whether to cultivate CRP land or keep it out of production crucially depends on the net returns that represent relative competitiveness of these land use choices. Based on this knowledge and data availability, we consider two IVs. First, we include CRP rental payments to account for annual returns from CRP lands. Higher CRP rent would generate disincentives for conversion. Our second instrument is the ratio of per-acre returns from croplands to those generated from grasslands which tend to have per-acre returns that are closer to CRP rental payments. Thus, this ratio can reflect relative competitiveness between croplands and CRP lands. We will provide a logic for the validity of IVs based on Wooldridge27, followed by tests for overidentifying restriction and weak IVs, with reference to a regression framework that we outline below.

Given that each farmer could only over-perceive or under-perceive change, not both, we have i ≠ j. The IV-conditioned CRP land conversion probability identifies a causal channel between land use decisions and motivated biases in weather perceptions. Evaluating the validity of instruments

Wooldridge27 provides necessary conditions for valid instruments that we adapt to the context of our study. First, the instruments must be correlated with the endogenous land use decision conditional on controls in Eq. (3). A strong correlation is desired between the instrument and land use decision to avoid misleading inference from models (4)-(5). Second, the instruments cannot be correlated with the error terms in models (4)-(5), failing which the instrument will face the same endogeneity issue as the CRP land conversion decision. Given that under CRP contracts farmers typically engage in grassland-based activities, our two IVs, namely renti and ratioi, are intended to capture the economic drivers of the choice of cultivating crops on the CRP lands against that of staying in the CRP contract. Further, it is reasonable to assume that these IVs do not directly impact weather perceptions but they can potentially generate incentives for land use conversion in the presence of cropping risks due to weather uncertainty. Therefore, we argue that the aggregate or county-level CRP rental rates and the ratio of cropland returns to grassland returns are valid instruments.

Results are organized around the testing of Hypotheses I and II, and our findings will be interpreted in the context of our study region’s weather conditions and survey-based socio-economic information of farmers. The above results suggest that farmers tend to perceive local weather indicators with regards to their impact on crop production (rather than the simple data summaries of growing-season weather). Liverpool-Tasie et al.2 also suggested that climate perceptions could be related to the degree of exposure and potential for impacts on farmers’ economic activities. However, we focus on how these perceptions compare with the data-based evidence for distinct weather indicators, which has received little attention in the existing studies. A recognition that farmer perceptions may differ across weather indicators, and with a reference to farm production activities, will help enrich climate change perspectives and climate policy debates.

Lastly, we find geographic and socioeconomic controls to be distinctly associated with farmer perceptions. Older farmers endowed with higher farm revenues and poor-drainage soils are less likely to perceive low precipitation and frequent droughts, which may reflect on these farmers’ resilience to adverse weather as derived from demographic and biophysical attributes. About 28% (283 out of 1,026) growers report beliefs that were consistent with temperature changes during 2004–2014 while such beliefs were biased for 673 farmers. Within the latter group, 531 farmers misperceived ‘lower’ temperature in 2014 to be either ‘about the same’ or ‘higher’ relative to the 2004 levels. Among these 666 farmers 267 of them misjudged ‘higher’ precipitation change to be either ‘lower’ or ‘about the same’; 253 farmers misjudged ‘lower’ precipitation to be either ‘lower’ or ‘about the same’; and 134 farmers misjudged ‘about the same’ precipitation to be either ‘lower’ or ‘higher’ relative to 2004 levels. Similarly, 33% growers exhibited consistent beliefs about drought frequency while 622 farmers misperceived them; and among them 462 farmers misperceived less frequent droughts to be ‘more frequent’ or ‘about the same’ (Table 4c).

In summary, the largest proportion of farmers misperceived past weather changes, which is in contrast with Hypothesis I. More importantly, Dakotas’ farmers predominantly over-estimated temperature and drought levels, and under-estimated precipitation levels relative to data-based records. We want to note that observing some biases in farmers’ weather perceptions is not surprising given that it is a cognitively challenging task to summarize weather trends that can evolve in multiple dimensions (e.g., temperature, precipitation, or both) and fluctuate in opposite directions. For example, a spring that is on average cooler than usual could have few very warm days. In addition, complex social, economic and political conditions drive weather perceptions4,5,8. However, it is the systematic one-sided biases whose in-depth understanding likely holds great potential for improved policy designs that are aimed at enhancing resilience in farm outcomes in response to climate uncertainty.

Issues in identifying systematic biases in weather change perceptions

Weather change misperceptions are measured as the difference between farmer perceptions and actual weather changes in their locality. Therefore, whether, and to what extent, farmers are found to have misperceptions about local weather depends on the accuracy of survey-based perception reports and past weather records. Also, our causal inference analysis relies on the instruments used in Eq. (3) and so our analytical results will be affected by issues related to the validity or strengths of the IVs used. Below we discuss the details related to the impacts of these potential issues and outline our strategies to check for robustness of our findings.

Robustness checks related to measurement of perceptions and weather records

We assess the potential issues related to the measurement of weather change misperceptions: (i) possibly inaccurate farmer recall of past weather changes, (ii) alternative sources of local weather records, and (iii) a threshold-based definition of weather changes for comparing discrete perception categories with continuous weather records (please see section "Articulating biases in past weather change perceptions").

A farmer’s perception of long-term weather trends (e.g., over a decade) can be influenced by short-term weather fluctuations or recent salient events such as droughts33,52. As a check for robustness, we account for limited recall capacity for past weather events through the use of two auxiliary measures of weather conditions at the beginning of our study period. First, we assign 2004 weather to be an average of weather records in 2003, 2004 and 2005 to evaluate \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\Delta w$$\end{document} in model (1). Second, we evaluate how recent salient events, i.e., 2006 drought and heat waves53–55 and 2011 floods56, 57, might influence farmers’ weather perceptions58,59. To do so, we calculate \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\Delta w$$\end{document} based on the initial weather conditions in 2006 and 2011, in addition to those in 2004. Tables S1–S3, SM, show that, unlike \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\Delta p^{04 - 14}$$\end{document} and \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\Delta d^{04 - 14}$$\end{document}, precipitation and drought perceptions were not significantly correlated with past weather changes during 2006–2014 and 2011–2014 periods, respectively, in a statistically manner. Second, we utilize an alternative and independent source of local weather records, i.e., PRISM weather records made available by Oregon State University to estimate model (1) and evaluate biases in farmer perceptions. Specifically, we utilize the PRISM data (Oregon State University), which is organized in 4 km × 4 km grids, besides the GHCN weather station records. We find coefficient estimates for model (1) to be very similar irrespective of the source of weather data (except that model (1) exhibits better fit with station-level data). Relevant details are provided in SM, section S2. We present our main results based on weather station data because people (including farmers) commonly learn about local weather from meteorological stations.

Finally, we assess how weather misperceptions are influenced by alternate threshold values, i.e., ± 5%, ± 10% and ± 20%, which are used to define little or no change in local weather records during 2004–2014. As discussed earlier, these thresholds are necessary to convert continuous weather indicators into categorical variables so that they are comparable with the survey-based perception categories. Obviously, the threshold values that we set will affect perception bias measurements.

We examine the robustness of our analysis to distinct threshold values in two ways. We first assess our threshold values relative to data-based range in precipitation and temperature changes during 2004–2014. For this purpose, we compare May–July weather anomalies in 2004 and 2014 against a common average weather baseline during 1991–2020, made available by PRISM web-portal. We find total precipitation and average temperatures in 2014 to be on-average lower than 2004 levels. Specifically, the spatial range of precipitation changes across counties was − 20% to − 30%, and the spatial range of temperature changes across counties was − 2.5% to − 12.5%. Please see SM, section S3 for details. Therefore, sensitivity analyses using 5%–20% as the threshold values for ‘little or no change’ category provide an appropriate spatial coverage of recorded weather changes in our study region.

Second, we conduct a sensitivity analysis and find that distribution of weather misperceptions was robust across alternate thresholds. For example, the distribution of respondents who misperceived greater precipitation as “drier” (i.e., 22% farmers), and those who misperceived less precipitation as “wetter” (i.e., 34% farmers) remain the same across the three thresholds. Moreover, the linkage between past land use decisions and perception biases also remains consistent in the 2SLS framework irrespective of threshold choice. Please see SM, section S3 for details.

Specification tests for overidentifying restrictions and weak instruments

We conduct specification tests for overidentifying restrictions and weak instruments with reference to our 2SLS framework in Eqs. (3)-(5). The Sargan test for overidentifying restrictions was implemented as described in 27, pp. 123. The concern for overidentification arises due to the fact that we include two instruments for one endogenous variable (i.e., one additional instrument than necessary) in the first stage regression Eq. (3). Null hypothesis of the Sargan test is that the instruments are correctly excluded and that these are uncorrelated with the error terms in second-stage regression models for perception biases (i.e., Eqs. (4)-(5) against the alternate hypothesis that instruments are invalid. Further, Staiger and Stock60 provided a decision rule for weak instruments such that when the regression F-statistic is less than 10 we infer that the instrument is weak. Overall, we concluded that our instrumetns, namely renti and ratioi, are correctly excluded (with 95% confidence) from the second stage regression models (4)-(5) and that these instruments are not weak for estimating the 2SLS regression system (3)-(5). Please see the Results section and SM, section S6 and Table S7 for further details.

Finally, as robustness check, we consider a different instrument, namely difference in county-level soil-specific rental rates (SRR) between 2004 and 2014, that is, we use srri in place of renti. The SRRs are based on county-average non-irrigated cropland rental rates and are further adjusted to parcel-level soil productivity61,62. The SRR of a CRP application is used to set the maximum annual payment rate of the application, thereby providing a program-based payment rate. Most CRP applications have a rental rate around the average SRR61, and we find high correlation between srri and renti (correlation coefficient = 0.76; p < 0.01). Among the CRP signups in our data, the average parcel-level srri pre-2007 period was 46$/acre and then rose sharply to 77 $/acre post-2007 (i.e. 2010 and 2011). Details of 2SLS estimation results with this distinct IV is presented in SM, Table S12. Overall, both stages of our 2SLS framework is robust to the use of alternative instruments, as particularly reflected through the distribution of \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\hat{p}_{i}^{(CRP \to crop)}$$\end{document} that remains similar irrespective of the choice of IVs.

Discussions and conclusion

There is a well-established literature that posits perceptions of climate change and weather patterns as critical inputs for designing effective policies for climate change mitigation and adaptation. A fundamental impediment in advancing the objectives of climate policy is that individual beliefs may not be consistent with scientific evidence63,64. Recent studies of climate change beliefs highlight the interactions between spatial climate patterns and heterogeneous individual interests/values that are tied to local resource constraints and socio-cultural factors (e.g.,24,65). We extend this line of research by analysing beliefs that may be distinct across weather indicators such as temperature, precipitation and droughts. The basis of our inquiry is the complex composition of climate or weather information such that individuals may attribute climate change to spatio-temporal changes in temperature, precipitation, or both, depending on the local or regional contexts.

We study farm-level heterogeneity in perception biases for three weather indicators: temperature, precipitation, and drought frequency, in relation to local meteorological evidence and farmers’ socioeconomic and geographic information. Our analysis highlights that farmers may perceive weather indicators distinctly from each other. In fact, farmers in our sample perceived local weather changes in ways that reflects on the impact of each weather indicator on their production system. We also provide evidence on the potential causal impact channel between farmers’ land use decisions and weather beliefs. Having made the decision to pursue crop production on protected lands seemed to motivate farmers to systematically ignore adverse weather signals of the past.

In the existing literature on motivated climate change reasoning, prior beliefs and political polarization were documented as drivers of climate change beliefs. Our result that links weather perceptions to past land use decisions adds a new economic perspective to the understanding of climate change beliefs and relevant communication strategies (as originally proposed by66). These authors suggested that identifying individual’s goals or motivation for belief distortion can facilitate effective climate change communication. Our findings suggest an indirect economic motivation (as mediated through land use decisions) for climate change beliefs that may present a challenge for communicating scientific evidence when high commodity prices favor crop-based production.

We point to a couple of caveats in our findings. Our analysis is based on a cross-sectional sample of farmers’ weather perceptions in a specific region. While the production setting in our study region is quite homogeneous and the sample is relatively large and representative, our cross-sectional models for weather perceptions are susceptible to non-weather confounders that may influence how farmers’ beliefs shift over time. It is also important to study weather perception biases in other regions and contexts (e.g., alternative land use types or farm management decisions). More importantly, climate and weather perceptions are based on complex cognitive processes and so in-depth examination is necessary with methods and concepts in psychology and cognitive sciences to pinpoint key factors and mechanisms that drive perceptions. Regardless, our findings suggest that climate policy designs that solely rely on objective climate and weather data might be misguided. It will be beneficial for policymakers to devise targeted climate mitigation and adaptation strategies that take into account farmers’ perceptions and beliefs.
