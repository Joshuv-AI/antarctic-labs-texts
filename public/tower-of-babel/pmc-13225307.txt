Abemaciclib Inhibits Retinoblastoma Tumor Growth by Targeting CDK1/2
Authors: Hongwei Yang, Qing Xiao, Yaoying Shen, Yudi Yao, Xiaolong Yin, Yi Sang, Yan Deng
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
PurposeThis study aimed to investigate the antitumor efficacy and molecular mechanisms of abemaciclib in retinoblastoma beyond its canonical role as a cyclin-dependent kinase (CDK) 4/6 inhibitor.MethodsTranscriptomic differences between retinoblastoma and healthy retinal tissues were analyzed using multiple Gene Expression Omnibus (GEO) datasets. Candidate gene was validated by immunohistochemistry in patient specimens and by Western blotting and quantitative real-time PCR in retinoblastoma cell lines. Cell viability and proliferation were evaluated by CCK-8, EdU incorporation, and soft agar colony formation assays. Flow cytometry was performed to analyze cell cycle distribution and apoptosis rates. RNA sequencing and Western blotting were performed to investigate the antitumor mechanisms of abemaciclib in retinoblastoma. DNA damage was specifically detected using γ-H2AX immunofluorescence staining. Additionally, subcutaneous patient-derived xenograft (PDX) and orthotopic cell line-derived xenograft (CDX) models of retinoblastoma were established in immunodeficient mice to evaluate the in vivo therapeutic efficacy.ResultsIntegrated analysis of five GEO datasets revealed that, among the CDK family members, only CDK1 and CDK2 were consistently overexpressed in retinoblastoma, a finding validated in clinical specimens and cell lines. Abemaciclib significantly inhibited retinoblastoma cell proliferation in vitro and effectively suppressed tumor growth in both PDX and orthotopic CDX models. Mechanistically, abemaciclib reduced phosphorylation of CDK1 and CDK2, resulting in G2/M cell-cycle arrest. It also increased reactive oxygen species (ROS) production, caused DNA damage, inhibited DNA damage repair, activated the p53/p21 pathway, and ultimately induced apoptosis.ConclusionsThese findings provide preclinical evidence that establishes abemaciclib as a promising novel treatment strategy for retinoblastoma.

Retinoblastoma is the most common intraocular malignancy of childhood, with an incidence of approximately one case per approximately 16,000 to 20,000 live births.1,2 In nearly all cases, tumorigenesis is driven by biallelic inactivation of the RB gene, leading to loss of functional retinoblastoma protein (pRB).3,4 In clinical practice, chemotherapy remains the standard treatment for retinoblastoma. However, systemic chemotherapy is frequently associated with severe systemic toxicities and an increased risk of secondary malignancies.5–7 Intra-arterial and intravitreal chemotherapy are associated with local ocular adverse events.8–10

Cell proliferation is tightly regulated by the cell cycle, which progresses through the G0/G1, S, G2, and M phases under the control of cyclin-dependent kinases (CDKs). Dysregulation of CDK activity is frequently observed across diverse human malignancies, disrupting cell cycle control and contributing to tumorigenesis.11 Accordingly, aberrant CDK activity has long been regarded as a rational and potentially effective target for anticancer therapy. However, despite extensive efforts to develop CDK inhibitors, early clinical trials with first-generation pan-CDK inhibitors (e.g., flavopiridol and roscovitine) and second-generation multi-CDK inhibitors (e.g., dinaciclib) yielded limited efficacy and substantial toxicity.12 To date, the US Food and Drug Administration (FDA) has approved only three selective CDK4/6 inhibitors—palbociclib, ribociclib, and abemaciclib—for clinical use.13 Among these, abemaciclib is the only agent approved as monotherapy for ER+/HER2− advanced or metastatic breast cancer following progression on endocrine therapy and chemotherapy.14,15 By inhibiting CDK4/6 activity, abemaciclib prevents phosphorylation of pRB, resulting in G1 phase arrest and inhibition of cell cycle progression.16 This canonical mechanism, however, implies a dependence on functional pRB. Consequently, in tumors such as retinoblastoma, where pRB loss is a defining feature, abemaciclib would not be expected to exert therapeutic efficacy. Thus, the potential value of abemaciclib in retinoblastoma has been largely overlooked.

Pharmacological profiling has revealed that abemaciclib, beyond its canonical inhibition of CDK4/6, also targets other CDKs, particularly CDK1 and CDK2.15,17 This broad-spectrum activity may underlie its robust single-agent efficacy.15 Preclinical studies have confirmed broad antitumor activity of abemaciclib, spanning glioblastoma, lung, pancreatic, and hematologic cancers.18–22 Notably, previous studies have shown that abemaciclib remains active in RB-negative cells, and further work has demonstrated its efficacy in RB-negative small cell lung cancer models.17,23 These findings raise the possibility that abemaciclib may also exert antitumor effects in retinoblastoma.

In this study, our bioinformatic analysis of public retinoblastoma datasets identified elevated expression of CDK1 and CDK2, a finding we confirmed in retinoblastoma tissues and cell lines. Given that abemaciclib has demonstrated a broader CDK inhibitory profile encompassing these kinases, alongside its documented efficacy in pRB-negative contexts, we hypothesized that abemaciclib would exert therapeutic effects in retinoblastoma. To this end, we investigated the effects of abemaciclib on retinoblastoma cells in vitro and assessed its antitumor efficacy in vivo using both subcutaneous patient-derived xenograft (PDX) and orthotopic cell line-derived xenograft (CDX) models.

Materials and Methods

Study Approval and Patient Samples

Research involving human retinoblastoma tissues was conducted under approval from the Ethics Committee of the Second Affiliated Hospital of Nanchang University. Paraffin-embedded samples were derived from surgical specimens of three patients with retinoblastoma patients at this institution. Two patients were boys (7 and 2 years old) and one was a girl (2 years old). In all three cases, the tumor involved the left eye (OS), and two cases were classified as stage II and one as stage III. In addition, five additional archived patient specimens were included. The detailed clinical characteristics of these cases are summarized in Supplementary Table S1.

Bioinformation, Cell Lines, and Chemicals

Expression of CDK1 and CDK2 in retinoblastoma was analyzed across five Gene Expression Omnibus (GEO) datasets: GSE58780, GSE208143, GSE24673, GSE110811, and GSE97508. The human retinoblastoma cell lines Y79 (Cat. No. FH0298) and WERI-Rb1 (Cat. No. FH0543) were purchased from FuHeng Cell Center (Shanghai, China). The human retinal pigment epithelial cell line ARPE-19 was obtained from the Shanghai Institute of Biochemistry and Cell Biology (Shanghai, China). Abemaciclib (HY-16297A; MedChemExpress, China) was dissolved in dimethyl sulfoxide (DMSO; Solarbio, Beijing, China) to prepare a 5 mM stock solution. For cell treatments, Y79 cells were exposed to abemaciclib at concentrations of 2, 3, 4, and 5 µM, with 0.1% DMSO as the vehicle control (matching the highest solvent concentration). WERI-Rb1 cells were treated with abemaciclib at 5, 6, 7, and 8 µM, and 0.16% DMSO was used as the vehicle control. The ROS scavenger N-acetylcysteine (NAC; HY-B0215), the chemotherapeutic agent carboplatin (HY-17393), and the selection antibiotic puromycin (HY-B1743A) were purchased from MedChemExpress (China). All compounds were dissolved in sterile ddH2O to prepare stock solutions.

Cell Viability Assay

Cell viability was evaluated using the Cell Counting Kit-8 (CCK-8; Yeasen, Shanghai, China). Briefly, 10,000 cells were seeded into each well of a 96-well plate and treated as indicated. At 24, 48, and 72 hours, 10 µL of CCK-8 solution was added to the corresponding wells, followed by a 2-hour incubation at 37°C under light-protected conditions. Absorbance at 450 nm was then measured using a microplate reader.

EdU Incorporation for Cell Proliferation

EdU incorporation assay was performed using the YF 488 Click-iT EdU Kit (UElandy, China) following the protocol provided by the manufacturer. EdU-positive cells were quantified by flow cytometry using a BD FACSCalibur system (Becton, Dickinson and Company, Franklin Lakes, NJ, USA), and data were analyzed with FlowJo software (version 10.9.0).

Soft Agar Colony Formation Assay

Soft agar colony formation assay was performed using low-melting agarose (S14006; Shanghai Yuanye Biological Technology, China). Briefly, cells were embedded in a 2-layer soft agar system and cultured for 14 days, after which colonies were fixed, stained with crystal violet, and imaged under a microscope. A detailed description of the soft agar assay is provided in our earlier study.24

Cell Cycle and Apoptosis

Cell cycle distribution and apoptosis were determined by flow cytometry. For cell cycle analysis, cells were stained with a PI/RNase staining kit (KGA9101; KeyGEN Biotech), and for apoptosis with an Annexin V-FITC/PI double staining kit (KGA1102; KeyGEN Biotech), according to the manufacturer's instructions. Flow cytometric data were analyzed using FlowJo software (version 10.9.0).

Detection of DNA Damage

The γ-H2AX immunofluorescence staining was performed using a DNA damage assay kit (C2035S, Beyotime, Shanghai, China) according to the manufacturer's instructions. Suspension cells were harvested, washed with PBS, fixed in fixation buffer for 10 minutes, smeared onto coverslips, and blocked at room temperature for 20 minutes. Samples were incubated with rabbit anti-γ-H2AX antibody for 1 hour at room temperature, followed by fluorescein-conjugated anti-rabbit IgG secondary antibody for 1 hour in the dark. After washing, nuclei were counterstained with DAPI, rinsed, and then mounted with antifluorescence quenching solution. Coverslips were placed to seal the samples, and fluorescence images were subsequently acquired using an Olympus IX73 microscope (Olympus, Japan).

Detection of Reactive Oxygen Species

Intracellular ROS levels were assessed using the ROS Assay Kit (R6033; Suzhou, China) following the protocol provided by the manufacturer. Briefly, cells were incubated in 10 µM 2′,7′-dichlorodihydrofluorescein diacetate (DCFH-DA; 1 mL) for 30 minutes at 37°C in the dark. After incubation, cells were washed twice with serum-free culture medium. Then, 25 µL of the cell suspension was spotted onto a glass microscope slide and imaged with an Olympus IX73 micrscope (Olympus, Japan).

Western Blotting

Western blotting was carried out using standard protocols. The antibodies for Western blot analysis were against RB (9313; CST, Danvers, MA, USA), CDK1 (ET1607-51; HUABIO, Hangzhou, China), CDK2 (ET1602-6; HUABIO, Hangzhou, China), Phospho-CDK1 (370312; ZenBio, Chengdu, China), Phospho-CDK2 (310035; ZenBio, Chengdu, China), PARP (9543; CST, Danvers, MA, USA), Cleaved-PARP (5625; CST, Danvers, MA, USA), BAX (60267-1-Ig; Proteintech, Beijing, China), BCL2 (12789-1-AP; Proteintech, Beijing, China), p53 (10442-1-AP; Proteintech, Beijing, China), p21 (2947; CST, Danvers, MA, USA), ATR (13934; CST, Danvers, MA, USA), Phospho-ATR (2853; CST, Danvers, MA, USA), Chk1 (2360; CST, Danvers, MA, USA), Phospho-Chk1 (2348; CST, Danvers, MA, USA), Rad51 (R25533; ZenBio, Chengdu, China), Phospho-Histone H2A.X (Ser139) (γH2AX, R381558; ZenBio, Chengdu, China) and β-actin (GB15003; Servicebio, Wuhan, China).

Transcriptome Sequencing

Y79 cells treated with DMSO or 5 µM abemaciclib for 48 hours were lysed in TRIzol reagent (Tiangen Biotech, Beijing, China) and shipped on dry ice to BGI Genomics (Wuhan, Hubei, China), where cDNA libraries were prepared and sequenced on the BGISEQ-500 platform. For each group, three independently cultured cell samples were processed as biological replicates. Differential transcript expression was analyzed using the provider's validated bioinformatics pipeline, applying thresholds of fold change ≥2 and adjusted P value < 0.001. Transcriptomic changes were further interpreted through heatmap visualization and enrichment analyses to identify pathways potentially associated with tumor progression.

Quantitative Real-Time Polymerase Chain Reaction

Total RNA was extracted from cells using the HP Total RNA Kit (R6812; OMAGE, USA) according to the manufacturer's instructions. Complementary DNA was generated with the PrimeScript RT reagent kit (RR047A; Takara, Shiga, Japan). Quantitative real-time PCR (qRT-PCR) was carried out on a CFX96 Real-Time PCR Detection System (Bio-Rad, USA) using the TB Green Premix Ex Taq II kit (RR820A; Takara, Shiga, Japan). GAPDH was used as the internal control. All reactions were performed in triplicate, and relative gene expression levels were calculated using the 2−ΔΔCt method. Lentivirus Transduction

To establish stable knockdown cell lines, short hairpin RNA (shRNA) sequences targeting CDK1 (5′-GGATGTGCTTATGCAGGATTC-3′) and CDK2 (5′-GCTAGCAGACTTTGGACTAGC-3′) were cloned into the LV2N (U6/Puro) lentiviral vector (GenePharma, Shanghai, China). A non-targeting scramble shRNA (5′-TTCTCCGAACGTGTCACGT-3′) was used as a negative control (LV2NC). Lentiviral particles were produced following the manufacturer's instructions and subsequently used to transduce Y79 cells. Following transduction, cells were selected with puromycin (0.5 µg/mL) for 6 days to eliminate non-transduced cells and establish stable knockdown cell lines. The knockdown efficiency was confirmed by Western blot and qRT-PCR.

Synergy Determination With SynergyFinder

To evaluate the synergistic effect between abemaciclib and carboplatin, Y79 and WERI-Rb1 cells were seeded into 96-well plates at a density of 10,000 cells/well. Cells were treated with abemaciclib and carboplatin, either as single agents or in combination, for 48 hours. The concentration gradients for the combination assays were designed based on the predetermined IC50 values of each drug using two-fold serial dilutions. Specifically, abemaciclib was tested at 0.375, 0.75, 1.5, 3, 6, and 12 µM in Y79 cells and at 0.5, 1, 2, 4, 8, and 16 µM in WERI-Rb1 cells, whereas carboplatin was tested at 3.75, 7.5, 15, 30, 60, and 120 µM in both cell lines. Cell viability was subsequently assessed using the CCK-8 assay as described above. Drug synergy was quantified using the online SynergyFinder platform (https://synergyfinder.fimm.fi). The inhibition index was utilized to generate response surface models, and synergy scores were calculated based on the Zero Interaction Potency (ZIP) reference model.25 ZIP scores less than –10 indicate antagonism, whereas ZIP scores between –10 and 10 suggest additive interactions, and ZIP scores greater than 10 indicate strong synergy.26 Synergy heatmaps were additionally generated to visualize the interaction between the two agents.

Patient-Derived Xenograft Model

Fresh retinoblastoma specimens were obtained from enucleated eyes of patients after written informed consent and were immediately placed in sterile tubes containing serum-free RPMI-1640 medium (Gibco, Waltham, MA, USA). Patient-derived tumor tissues were rinsed with PBS, minced into 2 × 2 × 2 mm3 fragments, and subcutaneously implanted into the right scapular region of 4 to 5-week-old female NOD-SCID mice (Vital River Laboratories, Beijing, China) using a trocar to establish PDX models. Tumor growth was monitored twice weekly using vernier calipers, and volumes were calculated according to the formula: V = A × B² / 2, where A represents the longest and B the shortest tumor diameter. When tumors reached approximately 1000 mm³, the mice were euthanized, and xenografts were aseptically cut into 2 × 2 × 2 mm³ pieces and serially passaged subcutaneously into new recipients up to passage 5. When the passage-5 xenografts reached a volume of 50 to 100 mm³, the mice were randomly assigned to two groups (vehicle and abemaciclib, n = 6 per group) and treated by oral gavage with abemaciclib (75 mg/kg in 0.5% hydroxyethyl cellulose in PBS) or an equal volume of vehicle once daily, 5 days per week, for 22 days. Body weight and tumor size were recorded twice weekly. One vehicle-treated mouse died accidentally during gavage and was excluded from the analysis (final n = 5 for vehicle and n = 6 for abemaciclib).

Orthotopic Xenograft Experiment

Orthotopic xenografts of retinoblastoma were generated in 4 to 5-week-old female BALB/c nude mice (Huachuang Xinnuo Pharmaceutical Science and Technology, Jiangsu, China), as described in our previous study.24 Briefly, approximately 2 × 105 Y79 cells cells in 2 µL of sterile PBS were injected into the vitreous cavity of the right eye of each mouse. The procedure was carried out using a 5 µL Hamilton microvolume syringe fitted with a 33-gauge needle (7634-01 and 7803-05; Hamilton Co., Bienne, Switzerland). At 4 days after intravitreal injection, intraocular xenografts were examined and documented using a diffuse lighting system attached to a Topcon DC4 slit lamp (Topcon, Newbury, UK) to confirm successful engraftment of Y79 cells. Eighteen tumor-bearing mice were randomly allocated into two groups: (i) abemaciclib treatment and (ii) vehicle control. Mice in the abemaciclib group received abemaciclib (75 mg/kg, formulated in 0.5% HEC in PBS) by oral gavage once daily for 14 consecutive days, whereas mice in the control group received an equal volume of vehicle solution. On the day following the final administration (day 18 post-Y79 cell transplantation), all mice were euthanized and the eyeballs were enucleated. Intraocular tumor burden was quantified histologically as the mean tumor area per sampled section, as described in our previous study.24 Briefly, whole globes were serially sectioned at 4 µm, and every 50th section along the nasal-temporal axis was selected for hematoxylin and eosin (H&E) staining. Selected sections were imaged, and tumor area in each section was quantified in ImageJ software (NIH, Bethesda, MD, USA).

At the experimental endpoint, all mice in the PDX and orthotopic CDX models were euthanized by intraperitoneal injection of pentobarbital sodium (150 mg/kg) in accordance with the AVMA Guidelines for the Euthanasia of Animals (2020).27 All animal experiments were approved by the Experimental Animal Welfare Ethics Committee of Nanchang University (approval no. NCULAE-20250928002) and adhered to the ARVO Statement for the Use of Animals in Ophthalmic and Vision Research.

Histology

H&E staining and immunohistochemistry (IHC) were performed by adapting our previous protocol,24 with key modifications introduced to optimize morphological assessment. Specifically, the eyes were fixed in FAS eye fixation fluid (Servicebio, Wuhan, China), a specialized formulation for ocular tissues, for 48 hours. The samples then underwent standard processing, including dehydration, clearing, and paraffin infiltration. Critically, a small incision was made in each eyeball prior to these steps to facilitate reagent penetration during dehydration and paraffin infiltration. This optimized processing greatly improved the preservation of delicate ocular architecture. Consequently, the high-quality sections obtained enabled accurate histological evaluation of the tumor area. The antibodies used for IHC were: CDK1 (ET1607-51, HUABIO, Hangzhou, China,), CDK2 (ET1602-6, HUABIO, Hangzhou, China), BAX (GB154122, Servicebio, Wuhan, China), and Ki67 (ZM-0166, Beijing Zhongshan Golden Bridge Biotechnology Co., Ltd., Beijing, China). Immunostaining was semi-quantitatively assessed using two subscores. The proportion of immunopositive cells was graded on an approximately 0 to 4 scale (0 = none; 1 = <25%; 2 = approximately 25–50%; 3 = approximately 51–75%; and 4 = >75%). Staining intensity was scored as 0 to 3 (0 = negative; 1 = weak; 2 = moderate; and 3 = strong). The overall IHC score was determined by multiplying these two scores.

Statistical Analysis

Descriptive data were reported as mean ± standard deviation (SD). All statistical analyses were performed using SPSS software (version 27.0; IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA). Unpaired two-tailed Student's t-tests and 1-way or 2-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) were used for statistical comparisons, with Tukey's multiple comparisons test applied for post hoc analyses. Each experiment included at least three biological replicates per group. A P value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

CDK1 and CDK2 Expression Is Increased in Retinoblastoma Tissues and Cells

Initial analysis of the GSE58780 dataset (63 retinoblastoma tumors versus 3 fetal retina samples) revealed a profound dysregulation of cell cycle programs. Gene Ontology (GO) enrichment analysis of differentially expressed genes (DEGs) identified significant terms directly implicating CDK function, including the CDK holoenzyme complex and CDK serine/threonine kinase regulator activity (Fig. 1A). Complementing this, Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) pathway analysis revealed significantly enriched pathways, including the Cell cycle and DNA replication (see Fig. 1A). Hallmark Gene Set Enrichment Analysis (GSEA) further corroborated these findings, with the top positively enriched sets being E2F targets, MYC targets V1, mTORC1 signaling, and DNA repair (Fig. 1B), collectively indicating heightened cell cycle progression and DNA repair activity. To pinpoint the specific CDKs driving this phenotype, we intersected upregulated DEGs (defined as nominal P < 0.05 and Log2FC > 0.585) across five independent retinoblastoma datasets (GSE58780, GSE208143, GSE24673, GSE110811, and GSE97508), as shown in Figures 1C to 1G. This multi-dataset consensus analysis identified CDK1 and CDK2 as the only CDKs consistently and significantly overexpressed compared with healthy retinas (Fig. 1H).

To further validate the upregulation of CDK1 and CDK2, we analyzed their expression in clinical retinoblastoma samples (n = 3) and cell lines. IHC revealed markedly stronger staining for both CDK1 and CDK2 in human retinoblastoma tissues (Fig. 2A), which was corroborated by quantitative analysis showing significantly elevated IHC scores compared with adjacent non-tumorous tissues (Fig. 2B). Furthermore, the upregulation of CDK1 and CDK2 was confirmed in five additional patient specimens (Supplementary Figs. S1, S2, S3A), and quantitative IHC score analysis further supported this finding (Supplementary Fig. S3B). Moreover, CDK1 and CDK2 mRNA and protein levels were significantly elevated in Y79 and WERI-Rb1 retinoblastoma cells relative to the human retinal pigment epithelial cell line ARPE-19 (Figs. 2C–E). Furthermore, the absence of pRB expression was confirmed in both Y79 and WERI-Rb1 cell lines, whereas it was readily detected in the control ARPE-19 cells (see Figs. 2D, 2E). Collectively, these data demonstrate overexpression of CDK1 and CDK2 in pRB-negative retinoblastoma.

Abemaciclib Suppressed Retinoblastoma Cell Viability and Reduced CDK1/2 Phosphorylation

Abemaciclib has been reported to inhibit CDK1 and CDK2.17 Given the elevated expression of CDK1 and CDK2 in retinoblastoma, we first evaluated the antiproliferative potential of abemaciclib in retinoblastoma cells. CCK-8 assays demonstrated that abemaciclib reduced the viability of Y79 and WERI-Rb1 cells in a concentration-dependent manner. The IC₅₀ values at 48 hours were 3.09 µM for Y79 cells and 5.28 µM for WERI-Rb1 cells (Figs. 3A, 3B). In contrast, the viability of healthy control cells was not significantly affected (Fig. 3C). Western blot analysis confirmed the on-target activity of abemaciclib, showing dose-dependent decreases in CDK1 (Thr161) and CDK2 (Thr160) phosphorylation (Figs. 3D–G), as reflected by reduced phospho-CDK1/CDK1 and phospho-CDK2/CDK2 ratios (Figs. 3H, 3I).

Transcriptomic Profiling Identifies Enrichment in the p53/p21 Pathway, Cell Cycle, Apoptosis, and DNA Damage Checkpoint

To gain further insight into the underlying mechanisms, we performed transcriptomic sequencing. Transcriptome analysis revealed that abemaciclib treatment significantly upregulated 1163 transcripts and downregulated 1005 transcripts (Log2FC ≥ 2 and adjusted P value < 0.001; Fig. 4A). Figure 4B depicts the hierarchical clustering of the top 40 DEGs (by adjusted P value) and reveals a marked upregulation of CDKN1A (p21) (Log₂FC = 4.12). KEGG pathway analysis of differentially expressed transcripts (Log2FC ≥ 2, adjusted P value < 0.001) identified significant enrichment in the p53 signaling pathway, cell cycle, and apoptosis (Fig. 4C). Consistently, GO biological processes were enriched for cell cycle regulation and DNA damage checkpoint (Fig. 4D). GSEA based on DEGs (Log₂FC ≥ 2, adjusted P value < 0.05) further demonstrated significant positive enrichment of p53 signaling and apoptosis gene sets (Fig. 4E). Collectively, our transcriptomic analyses suggested that abemaciclib treatment triggers cell cycle arrest and apoptosis, as well as the activation of the DNA damage checkpoint and p53/p21-mediated signaling. Accordingly, functional assays were performed to validate the transcriptomic findings.

Abemaciclib Inhibited Retinoblastoma Cell Proliferation by Inducing G2/M Phase Arrest and Apoptosis

Classically, CDK4/6 inhibition suppresses pRB phosphorylation and induces G1 arrest in pRB-positive tumor cells.16 However, the effect of abemaciclib on the cell cycle in pRB-negative retinoblastoma cells remains unclear. To determine this, Y79 and WERI-Rb1 retinoblastoma cells were treated with increasing concentrations of abemaciclib or vehicle control (DMSO) for 48 hours, and cell cycle distribution was analyzed by flow cytometry. As shown in Figures 5A and 5B, abemaciclib treatment induced a dose-dependent increase in the percentage of cells in the G2/M phase, concomitant with a decrease in G1 phase cells, indicating G2/M phase arrest in retinoblastoma cells.

Based on the transcriptomic enrichment of apoptosis pathways, along with G2/M arrest that could result in mitotic catastrophe and apoptosis, we then assessed apoptosis using flow cytometry following 48-hour treatment with abemaciclib or DMSO. Compared with DMSO treatment, abemaciclib significantly induced apoptosis in a concentration-dependent manner, as shown in Figures 5C and 5D. Moreover, we examined the expression of key apoptosis related proteins by Western blotting in retinoblastoma cells treated with DMSO or increasing concentrations of abemaciclib for 48 hours. The analysis revealed upregulated levels of cleaved PARP and the pro-apoptotic protein BAX, accompanied by downregulation of the anti-apoptotic protein BCL-2 (Figs. 5E, 5F). Collectively, these results demonstrated that abemaciclib exerted potent pro-apoptotic effects in retinoblastoma cells.

To further characterize the antiproliferative effects of abemaciclib in retinoblastoma cells, we next evaluated DNA synthesis and clonogenic survival using EdU incorporation and colony formation assays, respectively. The EdU incorporation assay demonstrated a dose-dependent reduction in DNA synthesis following abemaciclib treatment. The fraction of EdU-positive cells decreased in both Y79 and WERI-Rb1 compared with DMSO controls, as shown in Figures 5G and 5H. Consistently, in colony formation assays, abemaciclib markedly reduced the clonogenic growth of Y79 and WERI-Rb1 cells compared with DMSO-treated controls. Both colony number and colony size were decreased, as shown in Figures 5I, 5J, and 5K. Collectively, these findings confirmed that abemaciclib effectively inhibited the proliferation of retinoblastoma cells in vitro.

Abemaciclib Induced DNA Damage and Activated the p53/p21 Pathway in Retinoblastoma Cells

To assess DNA damage, we performed γ-H2AX immunofluorescence, a sensitive marker for DNA double-strand breaks.28 Abemaciclib treatment induced a dose-dependent increase in γ-H2AX foci formation in both Y79 and WERI-Rb1 cells, as quantified by relative fluorescence intensity (Figs. 6A, 6B). As increased levels of intracellular ROS can lead to DNA damage,29 we next examined whether abemaciclib induces ROS in retinoblastoma cells. The results showed that abemaciclib significantly enhanced intracellular ROS (Figs. 6C, 6D). Western blot analysis further delineated the DNA damage response, revealing that abemaciclib both increased γH2AX(Ser139) levels and suppressed key repair proteins including phospho-ATR, phospho-Chk1, and Rad51 in a dose-dependent manner (Figs. 6E–G). Collectively, our findings demonstrated that abemaciclib induced DNA damage in retinoblastoma cells, potentially by promoting ROS generation and impairing DNA damage repair.

Transcriptomic profiling indicated the potential activation of the p53/p21 pathway, as shown by significant enrichment of p53 signaling and marked upregulation of CDKN1A (p21). This transcriptional signature could be explained by the observed DNA damage. It is well established that the tumor suppressor p53 is activated by DNA damage, which in turn upregulates p21, a CDK inhibitor.30–33 To validate this pathway activation, we demonstrated that with increasing concentrations of abemaciclib, both p53 and p21 exhibited a gradual increase at the mRNA and protein levels (Figs. 6H–J). Altogether, our results demonstrated that abemaciclib caused DNA damage and activated the p53/p21 pathway in retinoblastoma cells.

CDK1/2 Knockdown Supported the On-Target Effects of Abemaciclib in Retinoblastoma Cells

To exclude potential off-target effects, we established stable CDK1 and CDK2 knockdown Y79 cell line. Knockdown efficiency was validated by qRT-PCR and Western blotting, which showed significant reductions in CDK1 and CDK2 mRNA levels, as well as decreased total and phosphorylated CDK1 and CDK2 protein levels (Supplementary Figs. S4A–S4C). Functionally, CDK1/2 knockdown significantly decreased cell viability compared with the Sh-NC group (Supplementary Fig. S4D). More importantly, CDK1/2 knockdown markedly attenuated the dose-dependent inhibition of cell viability by abemaciclib, indicating reduced drug sensitivity (Supplementary Fig. S4E). Flow cytometric analysis further showed that CDK1/2 knockdown significantly induced G2/M phase arrest relative to Sh-NC cells, whereas treatment with abemaciclib (5 µM) did not further increase the G2/M population in Sh-CDK1/2 cells (Supplementary Figs. S4F, S4G). Similarly, CDK1/2 knockdown significantly increased apoptosis, and no further increase in apoptotic rate was observed following abemaciclib treatment in Sh-CDK1/2 cells (Supplementary Figs. S4H, S4I). Collectively, these findings support that the antitumor effects of abemaciclib are largely mediated through CDK1/2 inhibition rather than nonspecific off-target effects.

Furthermore, to determine whether suppression of the ATR/CHK1/RAD51 pathway occurs downstream of CDK1/2 inhibition, we examined this signaling axis in stable CDK1/2-knockdown Y79 cells. Western blot analysis revealed that CDK1/2 knockdown markedly reduced the levels of phosphorylated ATR and phosphorylated CHK1, as well as RAD51 expression, whereas total ATR and total CHK1 protein levels remained unchanged (Supplementary Figs. S4J, S4K). These changes were consistent with those induced by abemaciclib, suggesting that suppression of ATR/CHK1/RAD51 signaling is a downstream consequence of CDK1/2 inhibition.

ROS Scavenging Attenuated Abemaciclib-Induced DNA Damage and Apoptosis

To determine whether ROS accumulation contributes causally to abemaciclib-induced DNA damage and apoptosis, we performed rescue experiments using the ROS scavenger NAC. Pretreatment of Y79 and WERI-Rb1 cells with increasing concentrations of NAC (1-8 mM) for 2 hours dose-dependently rescued cell viability following abemaciclib treatment (Supplementary Figs. S5A, S5B). As 8 mM NAC exhibited the most significant protective effect, this concentration was used in subsequent experiments. NAC pretreatment almost completely abolished abemaciclib-induced ROS accumulation in both cell lines (Supplementary Figs. S5C, S5D). Moreover, ROS scavenging by NAC markedly reduced abemaciclib-induced accumulation of γ-H2AX (Supplementary Figs. S5E, S5F). Consistently, flow cytometric analysis showed that NAC pretreatment significantly attenuated abemaciclib-induced apoptosis (Supplementary Figs. S5G, S5H). Taken together, these findings support a causal contribution for ROS accumulation in mediating DNA damage and apoptosis in abemaciclib-treated retinoblastoma cells.

Abemaciclib at a Low Dose Enhances the In Vitro Cytotoxicity of Carboplatin Against Retinoblastoma Cells

The results of the in vitro experiments showed that abemaciclib enhanced the antitumor effect of carboplatin in retinoblastoma cells. First, CCK-8 assays were performed to assess the antiproliferative effects of carboplatin in the retinoblastoma cell lines Y79 and WERI-Rb1, and dose-response curves were generated (Supplementary Figs. S6A, S6B). The IC50 values of carboplatin were 20.89 µM in Y79 cells and 34.86 µM in WERI-Rb1 cells, whereas the previously determined IC50 values of abemaciclib were 3.09 µM and 5.28 µM, respectively. These relatively high IC50 values of carboplatin are consistent with prior literature in retinoblastoma cell lines.34 To further evaluate the interaction between abemaciclib and carboplatin, drug combination analysis was performed using the online SynergyFinder platform.25 The ZIP synergy scores were 2.732 in Y79 cells and 3.499 in WERI-Rb1 cells (Supplementary Figs. S6C, S6D), indicating an additive effect between the two agents. As shown in Supplementary Figures S6C and S6D, the white rectangles mark the regions of maximum synergistic area. The concentrations of 3 µM for Y79 and 4 µM for WERI-Rb1 represented the lowest abemaciclib concentrations within these high-synergy regions and were therefore selected for subsequent combination assays. Co-treatment significantly reduced cell viability compared with carboplatin alone across the tested concentration ranges (Supplementary Figs. S6E, S6F). To further assess the long-term effect of the drug combination, soft agar colony formation assays were performed. The combination treatment produced the most pronounced suppression of colony formation compared with either monotherapy alone (Supplementary Figs. S6G, S6H). Together, these findings indicated that abemaciclib could enhance the antitumor efficacy of carboplatin in retinoblastoma cells.

Abemaciclib Inhibits Tumor Growth in Retinoblastoma PDX and Orthotopic CDX Models

To evaluate the antitumor efficacy of abemaciclib in vivo, a PDX model was initially established using the retinoblastoma tissues. As expected, abemaciclib treatment significantly suppressed tumor growth compared to the vehicle control, without inducing overt toxicity as evidenced by stable total body weight (Figs. 7A–E). IHC analysis showed increased expression of the pro-apoptotic protein BAX and decreased expression of the proliferation marker Ki-67 in PDX tumors from abemaciclib-treated mice compared with vehicle-treated controls, consistent with enhanced apoptosis and reduced tumor cell proliferation (Fig. 7F).

To further validate the antitumor efficacy of abemaciclib, an orthotopic CDX model was established by intravitreally injecting Y79 cells, as outlined in Figure 8A. Successful engraftment was confirmed by slit-lamp examination 4 days post-injection, which clearly distinguished tumor-bearing eyes from non-injected controls (Fig. 8B). Only mice with confirmed tumors were randomized into the study. Representative in vivo images of the eyes after the 14 days treatment period demonstrated robust tumor suppression in the abemaciclib group compared to the vehicle control (Fig. 8C). The treatment was well tolerated, as no significant difference in body weight was observed between the groups (Fig. 8D). The stark contrast in tumor area between the abemaciclib and vehicle groups was visualized in representative H&E images (Fig. 8E). This morphological assessment was then confirmed by quantitative analysis, which indicated significantly reduced tumor burden in the abemaciclib-treated mice (Fig. 8F). Similarly, IHC also revealed decreased Ki-67 and increased BAX expression in the abemaciclib group relative to vehicle (Figs. 8G, 8H). Moreover, histopathological examination suggested that tumors in the vehicle group were prone to aggressive outgrowths that disrupted the neighboring normal retinal architecture. Specifically, in representative sections from vehicle-treated Y79-injected eyes, marked tumor growth was accompanied by severe disorganization of the adjacent retinal lamination. In contrast, although residual tumor tissue was observed in the vitreous cavity of abemaciclib-treated eyes, the structural integrity of the neighboring retinal layers was largely preserved (Supplementary Fig. S7A). Furthermore, in uninjected eyes, no overt differences in retinal structure were observed between the vehicle- and abemaciclib-treated groups (Supplementary Fig. S7B). Collectively, these findings indicate that abemaciclib significantly inhibits retinoblastoma growth in vivo at the administered dose without eliciting overt systemic toxicity or obvious histopathological damage to the healthy retina.

Discussion

The longstanding paradigm that CDK4/6 inhibitor efficacy requires functional pRB has precluded their investigation in retinoblastoma, a malignancy defined by biallelic RB loss. Here, we challenge this convention by demonstrating that the FDA-approved CDK4/6 inhibitor abemaciclib exerts potent antitumor effects in retinoblastoma. Abemaciclib potently suppressed the proliferation of Y79 and WERI-Rb1 cells, as determined by CCK-8, EdU incorporation, and colony formation assays. Mechanistically, abemaciclib exerts its antitumor effects in retinoblastoma cells by reducing phosphorylation of CDK1 and CDK2, thereby inducing G2/M cell cycle arrest, and by causing DNA damage and activating the p53/p21 pathway, ultimately triggering apoptosis.

Notably, abemaciclib induces a pronounced G2/M arrest in retinoblastoma cells, in sharp contrast to the canonical G1 arrest elicited by CDK4/6 inhibitors in pRB-positive cells. This pattern is consistent with the notion that loss of pRB inactivates the G1/S checkpoint in retinoblastoma. Once the G1/S checkpoint is inactivated, cells no longer pause at G1 but instead repeatedly enter S phase without effective checkpoint surveillance. After completing DNA replication, they progress into G2/M and undergo successive rounds of division, resulting in unchecked cell cycle progression and rapid proliferation. Mechanistically, CDK2 drives S-phase progression and CDK1 controls the G2/M transition.35–37 Therefore, cell cycle acceleration induced by pRB loss may increase the functional demand for CDK1 and CDK2,38,39 thereby creating selective pressure for their upregulation and providing an explanation for their overexpression in retinoblastoma. By targeting CDK1 and CDK2, our study demonstrated that abemaciclib significantly decreased phosphorylation of CDK1 at Thr161 and of CDK2 at Thr160, sites required for full catalytic activity.36,40,41 This suppression of phosphorylation directly inhibits CDK1/2 activity, providing a mechanistic basis for the G2/M arrest. Consistent with this mechanism, prior studies in other pRB-negative cells have also reported that abemaciclib induces G2/M arrest, attributing this effect to suppression of CDK1 and CDK2 activity.17,23 To further substantiate this mechanism, our genetic knockdown experiments validated CDK1 and CDK2 as critical functional targets of abemaciclib. Dual depletion of CDK1/2 not only reduced cell viability and induced G2/M phase arrest and apoptosis, but more importantly, it significantly attenuated the additional effects of abemaciclib on these phenotypes. This lack of a significant additive response supports the conclusion that the antitumor activity of abemaciclib is largely mediated through on-target inhibition of CDK1 and CDK2.

Furthermore, previous research has shown that cells undergoing pRB-loss-driven malignant transformation are more dependent on CDK activity for proliferation than normal retinal progenitors.42 Specifically, using R547, a potent inhibitor of CDK1, CDK2, and CDK4, the authors found that CDK inhibition prevented retinoblastoma tumorigenesis without perturbing normal retinal progenitor cell division.42 Although this study focused on tumorigenesis prevention rather than the treatment of established tumors, it potentially supports the feasibility of targeting CDK1/2 in retinoblastoma and suggests that CDK1/2 inhibition may exert limited effects on normal retinal progenitor cells.

Moreover, abemaciclib treatment induced significant DNA damage in retinoblastoma, as evidenced by increased γ-H2AX foci formation. This DNA damage is likely attributable to the combined action of two mechanisms. First, previous studies have shown that abemaciclib increases ROS levels,43–46 which can both induce DNA damage and affect DNA damage repair.29 Consistently, we observed a marked increase in intracellular ROS after treatment, suggesting that oxidative stress may be an important contributor to the observed DNA damage. This notion was further supported by NAC rescue experiments, in which ROS scavenging attenuated abemaciclib-induced γ-H2AX accumulation and apoptosis. Second, inhibition of CDK1/2 has been reported to interfere with DNA replication and to induce DNA damage.47–51 In the present study, EdU incorporation assays showed that the proportion of cells in S phase decreased significantly with increasing concentrations of abemaciclib (see Figs. 5G, 5H). This dose-dependent reduction in S-phase cells indicates marked suppression of DNA replication and supports a potential role for CDK1/2 inhibition in the induction of DNA damage. Concurrently, the phosphorylation levels of key DNA damage response proteins, ATR and CHK1, were markedly reduced, accompanied by significantly decreased expression of Rad51, a core component of homologous recombination repair.52,53 Previous studies have reported that CDK4/6 inhibitors can induce DNA damage and impair the DNA damage repair.20,23,54 In particular, one study reported that this inhibitory effect on the DNA repair machinery is RB-dependent.55 Here, our results showed that in RB-negative retinoblastoma cells, abemaciclib not only triggered DNA damage but also compromised DNA damage repair.

The accumulation of DNA damage could subsequently activate the p53/p21 pathway.30–32 As a canonical downstream pathway of the DNA damage, p53 activation promotes mitochondrial apoptosis by upregulating the pro-apoptotic protein BAX and downregulating the anti-apoptotic protein BCL-2.56 Consistent with this mechanism, abemaciclib treatment induced robust, dose-dependent apoptosis (see Figs. 5C, 5D). Western blot analysis further confirmed activation of the apoptotic cascade, as evidenced by increased levels of cleaved PARP and BAX, together with decreased BCL-2 expression (see Figs. 5E, 5F). Furthermore, p21, a downstream effector of this axis and a broad-spectrum CDK inhibitor, could further suppress CDK1/2 activity.33,57,58 This p21-mediated inhibition is likely to cooperate with the direct inhibitory effect of abemaciclib on CDK1/2, thereby contributing to the potent G2/M phase arrest observed in our experiments.

In addition, a key downstream consequence of pRB loss in retinoblastoma is the upregulation of MDMX, an oncogenic inhibitor of p53.59 Despite functional silencing of the p53 pathway, most retinoblastoma tumors retain wild-type TP53, making them uniquely sensitive to therapeutic strategies that restore p53 function.60–62 Consistent with this, a recent study further supported the reactivation of the p53/p21 pathway as a promising therapeutic approach in retinoblastoma.63 Furthermore, a recent study elucidated the molecular mechanism by which Cip/Kip family proteins regulate cell cycle entry upon pRB inactivation.64 It demonstrated that the loss of p53, p21, and p27 proteins is a pivotal event driving the entry of pRB-negative cells into the cell cycle, whereas the upregulation of Cip/Kip proteins (e.g., p21 and p27) effectively blocks this progression. Taken together, these findings not only establish the p53/p21 pathway as a critical barrier against aberrant proliferation but also support the notion that abemaciclib exerts antitumor activity in retinoblastoma by activating this axis.

More importantly, effective systemic therapy for retinoblastoma requires adequate intraocular drug exposure, which in turn depends on penetration of the blood-retinal barrier. As a small-molecule inhibitor, abemaciclib has been shown to cross the blood-brain barrier and demonstrate antitumor activity in central nervous system malignancies,65–67 supporting the possibility that it may also achieve sufficient intraocular exposure. Therefore, after confirming the antitumor activity of abemaciclib in a subcutaneous PDX model, we further evaluated the drug in a more clinically relevant orthotopic CDX model. In this orthotopic model, abemaciclib significantly suppressed tumor growth, supporting its potential as a systemic therapy for retinoblastoma. In addition, abemaciclib treatment did not significantly affect body weight in either the PDX or orthotopic models, indicating that the dosing regimen did not induce overt systemic toxicity. This favorable preclinical tolerability is consistent with the manageable clinical safety profile of abemaciclib. In clinical use of abemaciclib, diarrhea is the most common but generally controllable adverse event, whereas neutropenia occurs at a relatively low incidence, allowing continuous dosing.15 Collectively, the robust antitumor efficacy of abemaciclib in both subcutaneous PDX and orthotopic CDX models, without overt systemic toxicity, supporting its viability as a therapeutic candidate for clinical translation.

Although our study demonstrated that abemaciclib can suppress retinoblastoma tumor growth, three main limitations should be acknowledged. First, although the effective antitumor activity of abemaciclib in the orthotopic intraocular model functionally supports its ability to reach intraocular tumors across the blood-retinal barrier, intraocular drug concentrations were not directly quantified. Dedicated pharmacokinetic studies will be required to provide definitive evidence of intraocular exposure and to inform clinical dosing strategies. Second, the present study was not primarily designed to provide a comprehensive comparison between abemaciclib and current chemotherapy agents. Although abemaciclib showed lower IC50 values than carboplatin in our in vitro assays, and enhanced carboplatin-induced cytotoxicity, these findings remain preliminary. Future studies are needed to systematically compare abemaciclib with standard treatment regimens and to explore broader combination strategies with other DNA-damaging modalities, such as multi-agent systemic chemotherapy or radiotherapy. Third, our study utilized the ARPE-19 cell line as a “normal” control. Whereas useful for establishing baseline CDK1/2 expression and assessing whether abemaciclib exerts overt cytotoxicity in nontumor cells, this retinal pigment epithelial cell line does not accurately reflect the retinal progenitors or neuronal retinal cells from which retinoblastoma originates. Future studies should incorporate more relevant retinal cell models to validate these findings.

Conclusions

Our study demonstrated that abemaciclib suppresses retinoblastoma cell proliferation by targeting CDK1/2 to induce G2/M cell cycle arrest and by inducing DNA damage with subsequent activation of the p53/p21 pathway, ultimately leading to apoptosis. In vivo, abemaciclib also showed robust antitumor efficacy in both PDX and orthotopic CDX models without overt toxicity. Altogether, these findings provide compelling preclinical evidence supporting the repurposing of the FDA-approved drug abemaciclib as a systemic therapy for retinoblastoma.
