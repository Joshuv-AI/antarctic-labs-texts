Verbenalin attenuates renal tubular epithelial ferroptosis in ischemia–reperfusion injury induced AKI by regulating the HIF-1α/HO-1 signaling pathway
Authors: Zhi-Hui Xu, Yu-Ying Li, Xiao-Li Jiang, Feng-Ying Tai, Meng Li, Ru-Fei Zhao, Xiao-Yan Wang, Yi-Xin He
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundAcute kidney injury (AKI) is a common critical disease. Ferroptosis, which is caused by unrestricted iron-dependent lipid peroxidation, is closely related to AKI progression. Verbena officinalis exhibits antioxidant activity. This study aimed to examine the therapeutic effect and mechanism of verbenalin (an active constituent of Verbena officinalis) on AKI.MethodsAn IRI-AKI mouse model was established. Verbenalin (10 or 20 mg/kg) was administered daily from 2 days before until 2 days after IRI induction. Renal function was evaluated by measuring SCr and BUN levels, while histological damage was assessed via HE and PAS staining. Ferroptosis was quantified by TUNEL assay, iron/MDA levels, and GPX4/xCT protein expression. Bioinformatic and Western blot analyses were employed to explore the mechanism, revealing the involvement of HIF-1α and HO-1. Finally, an H/R model in HK-2 cells was used for further validation.ResultsVerbenalin administration significantly improved renal dysfunction and histological damage in IRI mice. Verbenalin inhibited IRI-induced renal tubular epithelial ferroptosis. HIF-1α and HO-1 expression was upregulated in the verbenalin treatment groups. H/R-induced HK-2 cell ferroptosis was inhibited by verbenalin treatment and worsened by HIF-1α knockdown. Inhibiting HIF-1α expression abrogated the protective effect of verbenalin, and HO-1 and GPX4 expression decreased.ConclusionsVerbenalin plays a renoprotective role in IRI-AKI by inhibiting renal tubular epithelial ferroptosis, partly via the HIF-1α/HO-1 pathway.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12882-025-04643-w.

Background

Acute kidney injury (AKI) is a rapid decline in kidney function caused by various factors. The global incidence of AKI is increasing, with more than 2 million deaths per year [1]. Although AKI is an acute and transient kidney dysfunction, 35%-71% of patients do not fully recover renal function and eventually develop chronic renal disease, which results in considerable psychological distress and financial burden to these patients [2]. At present, no specific treatments are available to reduce kidney injury and promote kidney repair [3]. Therefore, improving the clinical prognosis of AKI patients requires identifying its key mechanisms and developing targeted therapeutic drugs.

The pathological hallmark of AKI is renal tubular cell death. Necrosis and apoptosis are two main forms of cell death [4]. Ferroptosis, a pivotal type of regulated necrosis, has been increasingly linked to AKI following diverse stimuli such as ischemia-reperfusion, sepsis, or toxin exposure [5]. Ferroptosis is caused by unrestricted iron-dependent lipid peroxidation [6] and is characterized by the accumulation of iron and reactive oxygen species (ROS), the depletion of glutathione, lipid peroxide deposition, and mitochondrial dysfunction [7]. In the context of AKI, the kidney is particularly susceptible to redox imbalance, suggesting that ferroptosis may represent a novel therapeutic target for AKI [8, 9].

Verbena officinalis is a medicinal plant that is widely distributed worldwide and is commonly used in folk medicine. The herb contains two types of lactone compounds: verbenalin (C17H24O10) and verbenalol (C11H14O5). Verbena officinalis (including its extracts) exhibits multiple biological activities, such as anti-inflammatory, antioxidant, anti-anxiety, neuroprotective, antitumor, anesthetic, antibacterial, and sedative effects. These biological functions have been confirmed through in vivo, in vitro, and clinical studies [10]. Among them, the antioxidant effect is a highly prominent biological activity of Verbena officinalis, as nearly all its extracts demonstrate antioxidant properties [11]. Recently, some scholars have reported that verbenalin reduces oxidative stress and ferroptosis in liver injury [12]. However, the use of Verbena officinalis or its extracts to treat renal-related diseases has not been reported.

In this study, we observed that verbenalin, an active constituent of Verbena officinalis, alleviated ischemia-reperfusion-induced acute kidney injury (IRI-AKI). Mechanistically, verbenalin played an antioxidative role and inhibited ferroptosis in renal tubular epithelial cells, an effect partly mediated by the HIF-1α/HO-1 signaling pathway. These results highlight the potential of verbenalin in AKI treatment.

Materials and methods

Animals and mouse models

Eight-week-old male C57BL/6 mice (body weight: 22–24 g) were purchased from Vital River Laboratory (Beijing, PR China). The mice were housed under a constant 12:12-h light‒dark cycle at 25 °C and allowed free access to food and water. The experimental animals were randomly divided into four groups as follows (n = 6/group): sham, IRI, IRI + low-dose verbenalin (Ver-L, 10 mg/kg) and IRI + high-dose verbenalin (Ver-H, 20 mg/kg) [29]. The mice in the verbenalin groups were treated with verbenalin (dissolved in DMSO via intraperitoneal injection) daily from 2 days before IRI to 2 days after IRI. Unilateral IRI of the left kidney was induced as previously described [13]. Pentobarbital sodium (100 mg/kg) was used as the anesthetic, and the mice were positioned on a heating system (JR-1, Techman) to maintain their core temperature at 37.0 ± 0.2 °C during the ischemic period. Briefly, the left renal pedicle was exposed through a flank incision. The renal artery was carefully clamped with a noninvasive microvascular clamp (XEC240, Jinzhong). Ischemia was induced for 25 min. At the predetermined time, the clamp was released. Ischemia and reperfusion were assessed visually. The right kidney was then removed immediately. Normal saline (300 mL/kg) was intraperitoneally injected to supplement fluid loss during the operation. The sham operation group was subjected to the same operation except for clipping of the renal artery. Twenty-four hours after IRI, the mice were briefly anesthetized, and a small amount of blood was collected from the angular vein for serum creatinine (SCr) and blood urea nitrogen (BUN) detection. On the third day after IRI, the mice were euthanized by removing circulating blood under anesthesia, and kidney samples were collected for further experiments. The mice were housed in a controlled living environment and provided adequate amounts of food and water. All animal experiments were approved by the Animal Committee of Harbin Medical University (Approval Number: sydwgzr2021-058) and performed in accordance with the National Institutes of Health Guide for the Care and Use of Laboratory Animals.

Verbenalin (Product Code: HY-N2014) was purchased from MedChemExpress. Renal function

SCr (µmol/L) and BUN (mmol/L) concentration were detected with a creatinine assay kit (C011-2-1, JianCheng) and a urea assay kit (C013-2-1, JianCheng), respectively, according to the instructions. The absorbances at 546 nm and 640 nm were measured via a microplate reader (Infinite 200 PRO, Tecan).

Tissue staining and injury scoring

Paraffin-embedded kidney sections (thickness: 2 μm) were subjected to hematoxylin‒eosin (HE) staining and periodic acid‒Schiff (PAS) staining (BA4080A, BASO) to assess renal tubular injury. Renal tubular injury was indicated and scored as described in our previous study [14].

Bioinformatic analysis

To identify the role of verbenalin in IRI-AKI, we used the keywords “IRI, AKI” to search the GEO database (https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/geo/). The GSE39548 dataset, which included four IRI-AKI samples and four sham samples, was downloaded for further analysis [15]. The R package limma was used to analyze the DEGs between the IRI-AKI samples and the sham samples in the dataset. The criteria for statistically significant differences in DEGs were a |log2-fold change (FC)|≥1 in expression and an adjusted p value < 0.05. The R package ggplot2 demonstrated the results of the differential analysis for volcano mapping, and the R package pheatmap was used for the heatmap. The target genes (TGs) of verbenalin were obtained from the SymMap database (http://www.symmap.org/) [16]. The DEGs intersected with the TGs. The intersecting genes were the key mediators of verbenalin’s effect on IRI-AKI. A Venn diagram was drawn via the ggplot2 package to visualize the intersection of key genes. GO and KEGG enrichment analyses were conducted for the key genes in R via the clusterProfiler package. An adjusted p < 0.05 was the threshold for screening the main enrichment functions and pathways of the DEGs.

Immunohistochemical staining

For immunohistochemical staining, paraffin-embedded tissue sections were processed according to routine procedures [14]. The sections were washed in PBS, subjected to antigen retrieval, and blocked with 2% BSA. The sections were then incubated with the following primary antibodies overnight at 4 °C: goat polyclonal anti-kidney injury molecule-1 (Cat. No. AF1817, R&D Systems, 1:400), rabbit polyclonal anti-HIF-1α (Cat. No. NB100-479, Novus, 1:1,000), and rabbit monoclonal anti-HO-1 (Cat. No. AB189491, Abcam, 1:200,000). After being incubated with horseradish peroxidase-conjugated anti-rabbit (PV-6001, ZSGB-BIO) or anti-goat IgG (PV-9003, ZSGB-BIO) for 20 min, the sections were developed with a DAB kit (ZLI-9018, ZSGB-BIO). Kim-1, HIF-1α and HO-1 expression levels were analyzed via ImageJ.

Cells and reagents

Human proximal renal tubule cells (HK-2, SCSP-511) were generously provided by the Cell Bank, Chinese Academy of Sciences (Shanghai, PR China) and cultured in DMEM-F-12 (No. 31331093, Gibco) supplemented with 10% FBS (F8687, Sigma). The cells were cultured at 37 °C in a humidified 5% CO2‒95% air incubator.

Cell transfection

To inhibit HIF-1α expression, siRNAs targeting HIF-1α (si-HIF1α) and the negative control (si-NC) were obtained from RiboBio and transfected into cells with the reagents in the kits. The transfected cells were further treated with verbenalin or directly subjected to hypoxia/reoxygenation (H/R) injury according to the experimental design 24 h later.

H/R models and experimental design

H/R-treated cells were placed in an anoxic chamber with a 0% O2 atmosphere until the predetermined time and were then cultured under normal conditions for reoxygenation. The control group was exposed to normoxic conditions for the same period of time as the H/R groups were exposed. HK-2 cells were divided into the following five groups (n = 3 replicates/group): control, hypoxia for 24 h and reoxygenation for 6 h (H/R), H/R + verbenalin, H/R + verbenalin + siNC, and H/R + verbenalin + si-HIF1α. Verbenalin was added at a final concentration of 20 µM 2 h before H/R.

Cell viability assays

Cell viability was assessed via cell counting kit-8 (CCK-8) assays. In brief, cells were seeded in 96-well plates (100 µL/well) and treated as indicated in the experimental design. Then, 10 µL of CCK-8 (Dojindo) solution was added to each well, and the samples were incubated for 1 h. Optical density values at 450 nm were read via a microplate reader.

TUNEL staining to detect cell death

A terminal deoxynucleotidyl transferase–mediated dUTP nick-end labeling staining kit (Roche) was used to detect in situ cell death according to the user’s manual. TUNEL-positive nuclei were counted in 10 randomly selected areas under 200× magnification.

Western blot analysis

Mouse kidney tissue and HK-2 cells were harvested from each group. Total protein was extracted, and protein expression was detected. Western blot analysis was performed as previously described [14]. The following primary antibodies were used: rabbit polyclonal anti-HIF-1α (Cat. No. NB100-479, Novus, 1:400), rabbit monoclonal anti-HO-1 (Cat. No. AB189491, Abcam, 1:20,000), GPX4 (Cat. No. AB125066, Abcam, 1:10,000), xCT (Cat. No. AB189491, Abcam, 1:10,000), and rabbit monoclonal anti-β-actin (Cat. No. AC026, ABclonal, 1:100,000).

Assays for lipid peroxidation and iron content

Renal tissues and cell samples were used to quantify malondialdehyde (MDA) level and total iron concentration via a lipid peroxidation (MDA) assay kit (AB118970, Abcam) and an iron assay kit (MAK472, Sigma‒Aldrich) according to the manufacturers’ instructions.

Statistical analysis

The data were presented as the means ± SDs. Statistical significance was determined by one-way ANOVA. For comparisons between any two groups, the Bonferroni correction was applied, whereas the Dunnett’s test was used for comparisons of multiple treatment groups against a single common control. A p-value of less than 0.05 was considered statistically significant. All the statistical analyses were conducted via GraphPad Prism 8 (GraphPad Software, San Diego, CA).

Results

Verbenalin played a renoprotective role in IRI-AKI mice

We first examined renal function in the different groups to evaluate the nephroprotective effect of verbenalin. As shown in Fig. 1A and B, at 24 h after IRI, the SCr and BUN levels in the mice in the IRI group rapidly increased to 138.00 ± 4.82 µmol/L and 30.29 ± 0.58 mmol/L, respectively (both p < 0.0001, IRI group vs. sham group). However, compared with those in the IRI group, the SCr and BUN levels in the verbenalin groups were considerably lower. We also found that the SCr and BUN levels were further reduced in the high-dose verbenalin group than in the low-dose group (p = 0.0002; p < 0.0001, Ver-H group vs. Ver-L group). Then, we compared renal histopathological changes among the different groups. HE and PAS staining of the kidney revealed that ischemia‒reperfusion caused severe renal tubular injury, which was characterized by tubular epithelial cell edema, degeneration, necrosis, cast formation, cell sloughing in the lumen and renal interstitial inflammatory cell infiltration (Fig. 1C, D). Semiquantitative analysis revealed that the renal injury score in the verbenalin groups was lower than that in the IRI group (p = 0.0054; p < 0.0001, Ver-L group vs. IRI group), and higher doses of verbenalin more effectively reduced renal tubular injury caused by ischemia‒reperfusion (p = 0.0014; p = 0.0039, Ver-H group vs. Ver-L group). KIM-1 is a biomarker of renal tubular injury. Immunohistochemical staining of renal KIM-1 revealed significantly increased KIM-1 expression in the renal tissue of IRI-AKI mice (p < 0.0001, IRI group vs. sham group), whereas verbenalin reduced KIM-1 expression (p < 0.0001, Ver-L group vs. IRI group) (Fig. 1E). These data indicated that verbenalin could attenuate IRI-AKI in mice.

Verbenalin reduced ferroptosis in renal tubular epithelial cells in IRI-AKI mice

Ferroptosis is an important form of renal tubular epithelial cell death after IRI [17]. A recent study revealed that verbenalin alleviated oxidative stress and ferroptosis in alcohol-associated steatohepatitis [12]. To further investigate the role of verbenalin in IRI-AKI, we examined ferroptosis in mouse kidneys on day 3 after ischemia‒reperfusion. We first evaluated renal tubular epithelial cell death via TUNEL staining. As shown in Fig. 2A and B, the number of TUNEL-positive tubular epithelial cells increased distinctly after IRI (p < 0.0001, IRI vs. sham). Subsequently, with an microplate reader and commercially available kits, we measured the levels of total iron (Fig. 2D) and MDA (Fig. 2C) — a lipid peroxidation product — in renal tissues across all groups, as iron-dependent lipid peroxidation is central to ferroptosis [18]. Iron and MDA levels in the IRI group were significantly greater than those in the control group, indicating that iron-dependent lipid peroxidation was increased in mouse kidneys after IRI. Conversely, the expression of the antioxidant enzyme GPX4 was reduced after renal IRI (Fig. 2E), whereas the expression of the cystine/glutamate antiporter (system xc-/xCT) was increased (Fig. 2F), indicating that the antioxidant system was disrupted by peroxidation injury. These data indicated that ferroptosis occurred in renal tubular epithelial cells after IRI in mice. These indices improved in the verbenalin treatment group. The number of TUNEL-positive cells decreased. In addition, iron levels, MDA levels and the expression of the ferroptosis marker xCT decreased, whereas expression of the core ferroptosis protein GPX4 increased. These results indicated that verbenalin could attenuate ferroptosis in renal tubular epithelial cells during IRI-AKI and that high-dose verbenalin was more effective than low-dose verbenalin.

Bioinformatic analysis revealed potential targets of verbenalin in the treatment of AKI

To further investigate the mechanism by which verbenalin affects ferroptosis in tubular epithelial cells in AKI, we conducted a bioinformatic analysis. The overall workflow of the analysis was shown in Fig. 3A. A total of 2130 DEGs were screened from the GSE39548 dataset, of which 1042 were upregulated and 1088 were downregulated in the IRI group. A heatmap and volcano map were constructed to visualize the expression changes in the upregulated and downregulated DEGs in the dataset and reveal the distribution of the gene expression data (Fig. 3B, C). We retrieved 571 TGs of verbenalin from the SymMap database. Among the initially obtained DEGs, 91 genes that overlapped with the TGs were identified as potential drug targets for the treatment of AKI (Fig. 3D). To investigate the potential signaling pathways involved, we conducted enrichment analyses on the 91 identified targets via several methods. KEGG pathway analysis revealed significant enrichment of key pathways, such as the AGE-RAGE signaling pathway in diabetic complications, the TNF signaling pathway, the HIF-1 signaling pathway, leishmaniasis, and the NF-kappa B signaling pathway, according to the enrichment score (Fig. 3E). Regarding biological process (BP), GO analysis revealed that the 91 overlapping genes were enriched in positive regulation of angiogenesis, positive regulation of miRNA transcription, cellular response to hypoxia, positive regulation of gene expression, response to xenobiotic stimulus, positive regulation of DNA-templated transcription, positive regulation of fibroblast proliferation, negative regulation of apoptotic process, cellular response to tumor necrosis factor, and response to cAMP (Fig. 3F, G). Finally, the 91 overlapping differentially expressed genes were ranked by their adjusted p-values. Figure 3H displays the top 10 genes, which served as the basis for our further investigation. To enhance the robustness of the findings from the GEO dataset, we subjected the newly obtained transcriptome sequencing data from a mouse IRI-AKI renal tissue model to the identical analytical pipeline. Notably, this independent validation yielded highly concordant results (Supplementary Figure S1). The concordance between the original dataset and our new independent sequencing data improved the reliability of the identified targets.

Verbenalin promoted HIF-1α/HO-1 expression in the kidneys of IRI-AKI mice

According to the results of the analysis above, HIF-1α/HO-1 signaling pathway might play an important role in the treatment of AKI with verbenalin. HIF-1α is a key factor that regulates oxygen metabolism [19]. Our previous study demonstrated that earlier and higher HIF-1α expression was associated with adaptive repair in AKI [13]. As a key downstream effector of HIF-1α, HO-1 regulates GPX4 expression and has an established link to ferroptosis [20–23]. To explore the renoprotective mechanism of verbenalin, we investigated HIF-1α and HO-1 expression in mouse kidneys on day 3 after IRI. As shown in Fig. 4A, renal HIF-1α expression was increased in the IRI group (p = 0.0028, IRI group vs. sham group). In the verbenalin treatment group, HIF-1α expression levels further increased (p < 0.0001, Ver-L group vs. IRI group), and the level increased with increasing verbenalin concentration (p < 0.0001, Ver-H group vs. Ver-L group). As shown in Fig. 4B, the changes in HO-1 expression were similar to those of HIF-1α. The results of renal IHC staining were further confirmed by Western blotting, as shown in Fig. 4C and D. These data suggested that HIF-1α and HO-1 might participate in the biological process by which verbenalin protects the kidney during IRI.

The HIF-1α/HO-1 signaling pathway contributed to the protective effects of verbenalin against H/R-induced ferroptosis in HK-2 cells

To investigate how verbenalin inhibits ferroptosis in renal tubular epithelial cells, we constructed a hypoxia/reoxygenation (H/R) model in HK-2 cells. Compared to normal cultured cells, H/R-treated cells showed significantly inhibited viability (p < 0.0001; Fig. 5A). Treatment with verbenalin at multiple concentrations (10, 20, 100, and 200µM) enhanced cell viability in a concentration-dependent manner in the H/R-injured HK-2 model (p < 0.0001; Fig. 5B). As shown in Fig. 6A-F, H/R stimulation significantly increased the percentage of TUNEL-positive cells, MDA levels, and total iron content, while decreasing GPX4 protein expression and increasing xCT. These changes indicate impaired antioxidant capacity and enhanced iron-dependent lipid peroxidation, consistent with ferroptosis. Verbenalin treatment markedly reversed these effects, reducing TUNEL-positive cells, MDA, and iron levels, while restoring GPX4 and modulating xCT expression. To investigate whether verbenalin’s anti-ferroptotic effects involve the HIF-1α/HO-1 pathway, we knocked down HIF-1α expression in HK-2 cells. Under H/R conditions, HIF-1α knockdown exacerbated ferroptosis, as evidenced by increased TUNEL positivity, iron and MDA levels, and xCT expression, along with decreased GPX4. This partial reversal of verbenalin’s protective effect suggests that HIF-1α contributes to its mechanism. Furthermore, HIF-1α knockdown reduced the expression of both HO-1 (Fig. 6G) and GPX4, supporting the involvement of this pathway. In summary, our results indicate that verbenalin attenuates H/R-induced ferroptosis in HK-2 cells, partially through mechanisms involving the HIF-1α/HO-1 signaling pathway.

Discussion

In this study, we employed an IRI model to simulate clinical AKI etiologies such as hemorrhagic shock, cardiac surgery, and kidney transplantation. Verbenalin administration effectively improved renal function and histological damage in the IRI mice, demonstrating its renoprotective effect. Further analysis revealed that verbenalin suppressed renal tubular ferroptosis, as evidenced by reduced TUNEL-positive cells, lower iron and MDA levels, and restored GPX4 and xCT expression. Based on bioinformatic analysis indicating the HIF-1α/HO-1 axis as a potential target of verbenalin, we proceeded to examine its functional role. Verbenalin treatment upregulated HIF-1α and HO-1 expression, while HIF-1α knockdown exacerbated H/R-induced ferroptosis and diminished verbenalin’s protective effect, accompanied by decreased HO-1 and GPX4 levels. Together, these results suggest that verbenalin ameliorates IRI-AKI by inhibiting renal tubular ferroptosis, a process potentially mediated, at least in part, through the HIF-1α/HO-1 signaling pathway.

In folic acid-induced AKI, the ferroptosis inhibitor Fer-1 reduces tubular epithelial death and improves renal outcomes [24]. Another study revealed that inducible Gpx4(-/-) mice died from lipid oxidation-induced acute renal failure. These mice were rescued by eliminating lipid peroxide, further confirming that ferroptosis is fatal during AKI [9]. Guerrero-Hue et al. demonstrated that ferroptosis-sensitive cell death can be inhibited by curcumin (a powerful antioxidant) in rhabdomyolysis-induced AKI [25]. These studies have established ferroptosis—an iron-dependent cell death pathway driven by lipid peroxidation—as a critical mechanism in various forms of AKI [26, 27]. This recognition positions it as a promising therapeutic target. Verbena officinalis and its extracts exhibit potent antioxidant activity. The beneficial effects of verbenalin have been observed in animal studies of lipopolysaccharide-induced acute lung inflammatory injury and focal cerebral ischemia–reperfusion [28, 29]. Based on the understood mechanisms of tubular injury in AKI and the known properties of Verbena officinalis, we hypothesized that verbenalin could improve AKI outcomes by inhibiting ferroptosis. Our preliminary experimental results confirmed this hypothesis.

The HIF family of transcription factors activates adaptive gene expression under hypoxia or ischemia and has been shown to exert protective effects in various AKI models [30]. While the link between HIF and ferroptosis remains incompletely understood, emerging evidence indicates a regulatory role. For instance, Wang et al. reported that HIF-1α attenuates inflammation and ferroptosis in IRI-AKI by negatively regulating the acyl-CoA synthetase long-chain family (ACSL4), a known promotor of ferroptosis [31]. Separately, HIF-1α has been found to protect renal cells from ferroptosis by mitigating mitochondrial oxidative stress and damage during AKI [19]. In the present study, bioinformatic analysis suggested a potential role for the HIF-1α/HO-1 signaling pathway in the renoprotective effects of verbenalin. This hypothesis was subsequently validated in our preliminary in vitro experiments. However, HIF-1α also promotes ferroptosis in some chronic diseases, such as diabetic nephropathy [32]. Our prior work suggests that HIF’s effects vary with disease stages and pathophysiological changes [13]. Therefore, more research is needed to confirm the factors that regulate the expression of HIF signaling pathway to maintain a stable beneficial effect of it on AKI.

There were several limitations in our study. First, although our results suggested a dose-dependent effect of verbenalin, the non-toxic dose range and the optimal therapeutic dosage remain undetermined. Second, in the animal studies, verbenalin was administered from 2 days before to 2 days after IRI induction. This precludes distinguishing whether its efficacy stems primarily from a preventive or a therapeutic action. Third, the observation period for the IRI-AKI model was limited to 3 days post-surgery. The longer follow-up (e.g., 14 days or more) is necessary to determine whether verbenalin could improve the renal outcomes. Finally, regarding the mechanism, while verbenalin possesses multiple bioactivities (e.g., antioxidant, anti-inflammatory) and our bioinformatic analysis suggested other potential pathways like NF-kappa B, these aspects were not experimentally explored in the present work.

In conclusion, our results demonstrate that the verbenalin attenuates IRI-AKI through the inhibition of tubular ferroptosis, partly via the HIF-1α/HO-1 pathway. Addressing the current limitations and deepening the mechanistic insight will provide stronger evidence for developing verbenalin as a potential therapeutic agent for AKI.
