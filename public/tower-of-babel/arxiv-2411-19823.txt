Color Avoidance for Monotone Paths
Dmitrii Zakharov

Abstract
In 2014, Moshkovitz and Shapira determined the tower height for hypergraph Ramsey numbers of tight monotone paths. We address the color-avoiding version of this problem in which one no longer necessarily seeks a monochromatic subgraph, but rather one which avoids some colors. This problem was previously studied in uniformity two by Loh and by Gowers and Long. We show, in general, that the tower height for such Ramsey numbers requires one less exponential than in the usual setting. The transition occurs at uniformity three, where the usual Ramsey numbers of monotone paths of length n n are exponential in n n , but the color-avoiding Ramsey numbers turn out to be polynomial.

Introduction

1.1
Background

It was noticed a little more than a decade ago
[
7
,
12
]
that two seminal theorems of Erdős and Szekeres from their 1935 paper
[
8
]
—the
monotone subsequence theorem
and the
cups-caps lemma
—can be cast as Ramsey theoretic statements about monotone paths in two- and three-uniform hypergraphs, respectively.
A
tight monotone path of length
n
in a
k
-uniform hypergraph on
[N]
is the set of
n
edges which contain
k
consecutive vertices from
v_{1}<v_{2}<\dots<v_{n+k-1}
in
[N]
, and we will denote by
MS_{k}(n;r)
the least integer
N
such that every
r
-coloring of
[N]^{(k)}
contains a tight
monochromatic
monotone path of length
n
, i.e. a monotone path in which every edge receives the same color.
(Here and throughout, for a natural number
N
, we will denote by
[N]
the set
\{1,2,\dots,N\}
, and for a set
X
and a natural number
k
, by
X^{(k)}
the set
\{e\subseteq X:|e|=k\}
.)
In this language, the theorems of Erdős and Szekeres read as follows.

Theorem 1.1

(Erdős–Szekeres, 1935
[
8
]
)
.

For all positive integers
n\geq 3
and
r\geq 2
, we have

MS_{2}(n;2)=n^{2}+1.

Also, for
n\geq 4
,

MS_{3}(n;2)=\binom{2n}{n}+1.

In
[
12
]
, the authors identified this unifying framework and asked for sharp estimates on
MS_{k}(n;r)
for larger values of
k
and
r
.
Soon after, the problem was almost entirely resolved by Moshkovitz and Shapira
[
18
]
, who established a connection between Ramsey numbers of monotone paths and certain types of higher-order down-sets as well as high-dimensional integer partitions.
They proved an exact equality between
MS_{k}(n;r)
and the number of these down sets which yielded the following quantitative estimates.
To state them, we recursively define the
tower function

T_{k}(m)
by
T_{0}(m)=m
and
T_{k+1}(m)=2^{T_{k}(m)}
; note that the subscript denotes the number of 2’s in the tower.

Theorem 1.2

(Moshkovitz–Shapira, 2014
[
18
]
)
.

For all
k\geq 3
,
r\geq 2
, and
n\geq 2
,

MS_{k}(n;r)\leq T_{k-2}(2n^{r-1}).

Moreover, for
n
sufficiently large,

MS_{k}(n;r)\geq T_{k-2}(n^{r-1}/2\sqrt{r}).

Remark 1.3
.

The second and third authors have recently shown
[
19
]
that this lower bound gives the correct order of magnitude, i.e.,
MS_{k}(n;r)=T_{k-2}(\Theta(n^{r-1}/\sqrt{r}))
when
r
is large.
See also
[
11
]
.

On the other hand, another line of research which has received considerable attention concerns Ramsey problems in which the desired structure now
avoids
some number of colors instead of using only one color.
Note that this generalizes the classical problem since a subgraph in a
r
-colored graph which avoids
r-1
colors is necessarily monochromatic.
This problem was introduced in a 1965 paper by Erdős, Hajnal, and Rado
[
9
]
, and was improved several years later by Erdős and Szemerédi
[
10
]
.
For cliques, the problem has seen a number of new results in the past several years
[
3
,
5
,
6
]
.

In the context of monotone paths, such color-avoiding Ramsey problems were first considered by Loh
[
14
]
in 2015.
More precisely, he studied the parameter
A_{2}(n;3,2)
which we shall define in greater generality as follows.

Definition 1.4
.

For positive integers,
n\geq k
and
r>s
, let
A_{k}(n;r,s)
denote the least
N
such that every
r
-coloring of
[N]^{(k)}
contains a tight monotone path on
n
edges which uses at most
s
colors.

A trivial bound for
A_{2}(n;3,2)
comes from the observation that a
3
-coloring of
[N]^{(2)}
can be regarded as a
2
-coloring of
[N]^{(2)}
if one pretends that two of the colors are the same, and that a monochromatic path in the latter setting is a path which avoids the usage of a color in the original
3
-coloring. The Erdős–Szekeres theorem then implies that

A_{2}(n;3,2)\leq MS_{2}(n;2)=n^{2}+1.

In
[
14
]
, Loh noted that
A_{2}(n;3,2)\geq n^{3/2}
and used the proof of the Erdős–Szekeres theorem due to Seidenberg
[
21
]
together with the triangle removal lemma of Ruzsa and Szemerédi
[
20
]
to show that
A_{2}(n;3,2)=o(n^{2})
holds. In a remarkable paper
[
13
]
, Gowers and Long further showed that there exists an absolute constant
\varepsilon>0
such that
A_{2}(n;3,2)\leq n^{2-\varepsilon}
and conjectured that
A_{2}(n;3,2)=\Theta(n^{3/2})
.

1.2
Our results

A more general first estimate on
A_{k}(n;r,s)
can be obtained in a similar fashion from Theorem
1.2
by noticing that an
r
-coloring of
[N]^{(k)}
can be regarded as a
\lceil r/s\rceil
-coloring of
[N]^{(k)}
if one pretends that
\lceil r/s\rceil
disjoint groups of
s
colors each represent a single color, and that a monochromatic tight path in the latter setting is a tight path which uses at most
s
colors in the original
r
-coloring. For example, when
k=3
,
r=3
, and
s=2
, this gives

A_{3}(n;3,2)\leq MS_{3}(n;2)=\binom{2n}{n}+1.

In general, for every
n\geq k\geq 3
,
r>s\geq 1
,

A_{k}(n;r,s)\leq MS_{k}(n;\lceil r/s\rceil)\leq T_{k-2}(2n^{\lceil r/s\rceil}).

Our main result is an improvement on the height of the tower in these upper bounds by one exponential, in the case when
k\geq 3
and
s>r/2
. For example, when
k=3
, we show the following:

Theorem 1.5
.

There exists an absolute constant
C>0
such that for all positive integers
n\geq 3
and
r
,
s
with
s>r/2
,

A_{3}(n;r,s)\leq n^{C\binom{r}{s}^{2}\log\binom{r}{s}}.

Moreover, for the particular combination
r=3
and
s=2
, our proof gives the stronger bound
A_{3}(n;3,2)\leq n^{9}
. Qualitatively speaking, Theorem
1.5
shows that both
A_{2}(n;r,s)
and
A_{3}(n;r,s)
are polynomial in
n
for
s>r/2
. We also show that for uniformity
k\geq 4
, the tower height continues to require one less exponential than the trivial bound from the theorem of Moshkovitz and Shapira.

Theorem 1.6
.

There exists an absolute constant
C>0
such that for all positive integers
n\geq k\geq 3
and
r
,
s
with
s>r/2
, we have

A_{k}(n;r,s)\leq T_{k-3}\left(n^{C\binom{r}{s}^{2}\log\binom{r}{s}}\right).

1.3
Organization

The remainder of the paper is organized as follows.
In Section
2
, we give an overview of some preliminaries which will be used extensively in our proofs.
In Section
3
, we focus on the problem in uniformity three, i.e., on
A_{3}(n;r,s)
and Theorem
1.5
.
We give first a proof that
A_{3}(n;3,2)\leq n^{9}
followed by the general proof, as it is a direct generalization of the argument for
r=3
and
s=2
.
In Section
4
, we prove Theorem
1.6
by adapting the method of Section
3
, and in Section
5
we give a characterization of
A_{k}(n;r,s)
in terms of what we call “generalized
S
-increasing sequences”.
Finally, in Section
6
, we make some closing remarks and state some open problems.

2
Preliminaries

In this subsection, we would like to first revisit the fact that

MS_{3}(n;2)\leq\binom{2n}{n}+1

(1)

holds for every
n\geq 4
. In
[
8
]
, Erdős and Szekeres originally proved this result in the language of cups and caps by establishing a recursive inequality in the spirit of the classical recursive inequality for diagonal Ramsey numbers (also originally from
[
8
]
). In
[
4
]
and
[
18
]
, however, Chvátal–Komlós and Moshkovitz–Shapira note that it is also possible to prove (
1
) by appropriately extending the Seidenberg argument
[
21
]
. Our proof of Theorem
1.5
(and that of Theorem
1.6
) will also adopt this perspective, so for the sake of self-containment we will now recall this beautiful idea below.

2.1
Seidenberg’s argument in uniformity three.

Let
\Delta:[N]^{(3)}\to\{\operatorname{red},\operatorname{blue}\}
be a two-coloring of the triples of
[N]
with no tight monotone monochromatic path of length
n
.
For each
u<v
, define the vector

P(u,v)=\begin{bmatrix}\ell_{\text{red}}(u,v)+1\\
\ell_{\text{blue}}(u,v)+1\end{bmatrix},

where
\ell_{\text{red}}(u,v)
and
\ell_{\text{blue}}(u,v)
denote the lengths of the longest tight monotone paths in red and blue, respectively, which end at
u,v
.
Since there is no tight monotone path of length
n
, note that the point
P(u,v)
lies in the grid
[n]^{2}
. Now, consider the map

\Theta:v\longmapsto D(v)=\{\vec{x}\in[n]^{2}:\vec{x}\leq P(u,v)\text{ for some }u<v\},

which assigns elements of
[N]
down-sets of
[n]^{2}
. Here the
\leq
relation on
[n]^{2}
denotes the usual “less than or equal to in both coordinates” partial order on
[n]^{2}
. One can check that
D(v)
is indeed a down-set: for every
\vec{x}\in D(v)
we have that
\vec{x}^{\prime}\in D(v)
for every
\vec{x}^{\prime}\leq\vec{x}
.

The main observation is that the map
\Theta
is injective: if
u<v
and
D(u)=D(v)
, we have
P(u,v)\in D(v)=D(u)
, so by definition
P(u,v)\leq P(w,u)
for some
w<u
; but this is a contradiction since the longest path ending at
u,v
in color
\Delta(w,u,v)
is strictly longer than the one ending at
w,u
.
Since the number of down-sets in
[n]^{2}
equals the number of antichains in
[n]^{2}
which is
\binom{2n}{n}
, we have that
N\leq\binom{2n}{n}
. We refer to
[
18
]
for more details regarding the latter claims.

The main idea behind Theorem
1.5
is that in order to upper bound
A_{3}(n;r,s)
one can construct an injection of
[N]
into a significantly smaller space than the collection of down-sets of
[n]^{2}
. To define this map, we will require the concept of a dominating set in a majority tournament.

2.2
Majority tournaments

A
tournament
is a directed graph
T=(V,E)
where every two vertices
u,v\in V
are connected by one edge, i.e.
|E\cap\left\{(u,v),(v,u)\right\}|=1
. If the edge is
(u,v)
(denoted by
u\to v
) we say that
u

dominates

v
.

Definition 2.1
.

For an integer
K\geq 2
, a tournament
T
is called a
K
-
majority tournament
if there exist
2K-1
linear (total) orders
\prec_{1},...,\prec_{2K-1}
on the set of vertices of
T
such that
u\to v
in
T
if and only if
v\prec_{i}u
holds for at least
K
values of
i\in\left\{1,\ldots,2K-1\right\}
.

These types of tournaments have been studied in social science
[
15
]
as well as combinatorics, where previous researchers studied extremal problems regarding the sizes of induced transitive subtournaments
[
16
]
and dominating sets
[
1
]
. If
T
is a tournament and
X,Y
are subsets of vertices of
T
, we say that
X

dominates

Y
if for every vertex
y
in
Y\setminus X
there exists an
x\in X
such that
x
dominates
y
. If
Y
is the full set of vertices of
T
, then we call
X
a
dominating set
of the tournament
T
. The
domination number

f(T)
of
T
is the smallest cardinality of a dominating set of
T
. It is a classical fact (often attributed to Erdős, see for example
[
17
]
) that every tournament
T
on
n
vertices satisfies
f(T)\leq\lceil\log_{2}{n}\rceil
. It is also known
[
2
]
that random tournaments on
n
vertices do not contain any smaller dominating sets, so this estimate cannot be improved upon in general. Nevertheless, it turns out that
K
-
majority tournaments
behave quite differently than random tournaments when it comes to their domination number
f(T)
. In
[
1
]
, Alon, Brightwell, Kierstead, Kostochka, Winkler proved the following result:

Theorem 2.2
.

If
T
is a
2
-majority tournament, then

f(T)\leq 3.

In general, for every
K\geq 2
, if
T
is a
K
-majority tournament, then

f(T)\leq CK\log K

holds for an absolute constant
C>0
.

It turns out it is this phenomenon which is primarily responsible for the drop in the tower height in our estimates from Theorem
1.5
and Theorem
1.6
when compared to the estimate from Theorem
1.2
.

3
Proof of Theorem
1.5

3.1
The proof for
r=3
,
s=2
.

We first prove Theorem
1.5
for
r=3
and
s=2
, in order to showcase the main idea in the clearest way possible. Our proof for this regime actually gives the stronger bound
A_{3}(n;3,2)\leq n^{9}
.

Let
\Delta:[N]^{(3)}\to[3]
be a 3-coloring of the triples of
[N]
in which every tight monotone path of length
n
receives all three colors.
Define for each
u<v
in
[N]
the vector

P(u,v)=\begin{bmatrix}\ell_{1,2}(u,v)+1\\
\ell_{2,3}(u,v)+1\\
\ell_{1,3}(u,v)+1\end{bmatrix}\in[n]^{3},

where
\ell_{i,j}(u,v)
is the length of the longest tight monotone path ending at
u,v
which uses only colors
i
and
j
.

Like in the argument from Subsection
2.1
, let us consider the map

\displaystyle\Theta_{1}:v\longmapsto S(v)=\{P(u,v)\in[n]^{3}:u<v\},

associating elements of
[N]
with subsets of
[n]^{3}
.
To define an injection from
[N]
into a space of size smaller than
{2n\choose n}
, we define a 2-majority tournament on the elements of
S(v)
with linear orders
\prec_{1},\prec_{2},\prec_{3}
as follows.
If
\vec{x}=(x_{1},x_{2},x_{3}),\vec{y}=(y_{1},y_{2},y_{3})\in S(v)
, set

\displaystyle 1.\hskip 7.11317pt\vec{x}\prec_{1}\vec{y}\text{ if }\begin{cases}x_{1}<y_{1};\text{ or}\\
x_{1}=y_{1},x_{2}<y_{2};\text{ or}\\
x_{1}=y_{1},x_{2}=y_{2},x_{3}<y_{3}\end{cases}

\displaystyle 2.\hskip 7.11317pt\vec{x}\prec_{2}\vec{y}\text{ if }\begin{cases}x_{2}<y_{2};\text{ or}\\
x_{2}=y_{2},x_{3}<y_{3};\text{ or}\\
x_{2}=y_{2},x_{3}=y_{3},x_{1}<y_{1}\end{cases}

\displaystyle 3.\hskip 7.11317pt\vec{x}\prec_{3}\vec{y}\text{ if }\begin{cases}x_{3}<y_{3};\text{ or}\\
x_{3}=y_{3},x_{1}<y_{1};\text{ or}\\
x_{3}=y_{3},x_{1}=y_{1},x_{2}<y_{2}\end{cases}

In words,
\prec_{i}
is the lexicographic order on
S(v)\subseteq[n]^{3}
in which the
i
th coordinate is the most important, followed by the
(i+1)
-st and then the
(i+2)
-nd (with addition modulo 3).
By Theorem
2.2
,
S(v)
has a total dominating set
D(v)
of size at most three, i.e., for every
\vec{y}\in S(v)
, there exists
\vec{x}\in D(v)
with
\vec{x}\to\vec{y}
in the tournament defined above.

Now consider a second map

\Theta_{2}:v\longmapsto D(v)\subseteq S(v).

Since
D(v)\subseteq S(v)\subseteq[n]^{3}
, note that
\Theta_{2}
is a map from
[N]
to the set of subsets of size at most
3
of
[n]^{3}
. We claim that
\Theta_{2}
is also an injection.
Suppose, on the contrary, that
u<v
but
D(u)=D(v)
.
Then each element
\vec{x}\in D(v)=D(u)\subseteq S(u)
by definition has the form
\vec{x}=P(w,u)
for some
w<u
;
but
\vec{x}=P(w,u)\prec_{i}P(u,v)
for at least the two coordinates
i
corresponding to paths in which the color
\Delta(w,u,v)
may be used, and so
P(u,v)\to\vec{x}
for all
\vec{x}\in D(v)
, contradicting the fact that
D(v)
totally dominates
S(v)
in the 2-majority tournament defined by the
\prec_{i}
’s.

Therefore,
we have
N\leq\binom{n^{3}}{3}+\binom{n^{3}}{2}+\binom{n^{3}}{1}+\binom{n^{3}}{0}\leq n^{9}
as promised. ∎

3.2
General
r
and
s
.

Now we prove Theorem
1.5
for all combinations
r,s
with
s>r/2
.
The proof is a straightforward generalization of the one given in the previous subsection.

Let
\Delta:[N]^{(3)}\to[r]
be an
r
-coloring of the triples of
[N]
in which every tight monotone path on
n
edges receives at least
s+1
colors (i.e., no such path uses at most
s
colors).
As before, define for each
u<v
the vector

P(u,v)=\langle 1+\ell_{S}(u,v)\rangle_{S\in[r]^{(s)}}\in[n]^{\binom{r}{s}},

where
\ell_{S}(u,v)
denotes the length of the longest monotone path ending at
u
and
v
which only uses colors from the set
S\in[r]^{(s)}
,
and consider the map

\Theta_{1}:v\longmapsto S(v)=\{P(u,v)\in[n]^{\binom{r}{s}}:u<v\}

associating elements of
[N]
with subsets of
[n]^{\binom{r}{s}}
.

Let
K
be the positive integer satisfying
\binom{r}{s}=2K-1
if
\binom{r}{s}
is odd and
\binom{r}{s}=2(K-1)
if
\binom{r}{s}
is even.
For each
v\in[N]
, we define a
K
-majority tournament on the elements of
S(v)
with linear orders
\prec_{1},\prec_{2},\dots,\prec_{\binom{r}{s}}
as follows.
If
\vec{x}=(x_{1},x_{2},\dots,x_{\binom{r}{s}}),\vec{y}=(y_{1},y_{2},\dots,y_{\binom{r}{s}})\in S(v)
and
1\leq i\leq\binom{r}{s}
(with addition in the indices considered modulo
\binom{r}{s}
), set

\vec{x}\prec_{i}\vec{y}\text{ if }\begin{cases}x_{i}<y_{i};\text{ or}\\
x_{i}=y_{i},x_{i+1}<y_{i+1};\text{ or}\\
x_{i}=y_{i},x_{i+1}=y_{i+1},x_{i+2}<y_{i+2};\text{ or}\\
\vdots\\
x_{i}=y_{i},x_{i+1}=y_{i+1},x_{i+2}=y_{i+2},\dots,x_{i+\binom{r}{s}-2}=y_{i+\binom{r}{s}-2},x_{i+\binom{r}{s}-1}<y_{i+\binom{r}{s}-1}\end{cases}

Note that if
\binom{r}{s}=2K-1
is odd then this defines a
K
-majority tournament on
S(v)
.
If
\binom{r}{s}=2(K-1)
is even, then we will force the above construction to be a
K
-majority tournament by adding an additional arbitrary linear order
\prec_{*}
with no relation to the others.
By Theorem
2.2
, each
S(v)
has a total dominating set
D(v)
in the tournament defined above, i.e., for every
\vec{y}\in S(v)
, there exists
\vec{x}\in D(v)
with
\vec{x}\to\vec{y}
, with
|D(v)|\leq CK\log K
.

Now consider a second map

\Theta_{2}:v\longmapsto D(v)\subseteq S(v).

We argue that
\Theta_{2}
is again an injection.
To see this, assume otherwise that for some
u<v
in
[N]
,
D(u)=D(v)
.
Each
\vec{x}\in D(v)=D(u)\subseteq S(u)
has the form
P(w,u)
for some
w<u
.
Now
\vec{x}=P(w,u)\prec_{i}P(u,v)
in all of the
\binom{r-1}{s-1}
coordinates
i\in[\binom{r}{s}]
which correspond to subsets
S\subseteq[r]
containing the color
\Delta(w,u,v)
.
By our hypothesis that
s>r/2
, we have

2(K-1)\leq\binom{r}{s}=\frac{r}{s}\cdot\binom{r-1}{s-1}<2\cdot\binom{r-1}{s-1},

which gives (regardless of parity, since all values are integers) that

\binom{r-1}{s-1}\geq K.

Thus, each such
\vec{x}
has
\vec{x}\prec_{i}P(u,v)
for at least
K
of the orders
\prec_{i}
,
1\leq i\leq\binom{r}{s}
, and so
P(u,v)\to\vec{x}
in the
K
-majority tournament on
S(v)
for all
\vec{x}\in D(v)
, contradicting the fact that
D(v)
is a total dominating set of
S(v)
.

Therefore, since each
D(v)
has size at most
CK\log K=C^{\prime}\binom{r}{s}\log\binom{r}{s}
, we have

N\leq\binom{n^{\binom{r}{s}}}{\leq C^{\prime}\binom{r}{s}\log\binom{r}{s}}\leq n^{C^{\prime}\binom{r}{s}^{2}\log\binom{r}{s}}.

∎

4
Proof of Theorem
1.6

The argument is an extension of the one in uniformity three, but we no longer define an injection from the singletons into the subsets of
[n]^{\binom{r}{s}}
of size at most
C\binom{r-1}{s-1}\log\binom{r-1}{s-1}
.
Instead, we define such a map on
[N]^{(k-2)}
and show that it contains no 2-edge monotone path in which both edges are mapped to the same set, or, in other words, we define a coloring of
[N]^{(k-2)}
with
n^{C\binom{r}{s}\binom{r-1}{s-1}\log\binom{r-1}{s-1}}
colors and no monochromatic monotone path of length two.
This allows us to use Theorem
1.2
to get an improved upper bound for
A_{k}(n;r,s)
over the trivial
MS_{k}(n;\lceil r/s\rceil)
.
Similar ideas were used in
[
12
,
18
]
to upper bound
MS_{k}(n,r)
.

Proof of Theorem
1.6
.
Let
\Delta:[N]^{(k)}\to[r]
be an
r
-coloring of the
k
-subsets of
[N]
in which every tight monotone path on
n
edges receives at least
s+1
colors.
For
v_{1}<v_{2}<\dots<v_{k-1}
, define

P(v_{1},v_{2},...,v_{k-1})=\langle 1+\ell_{S}(v_{1},v_{2},...,v_{k-1})\rangle_{S\in[n]^{(r)}}\in[n]^{\binom{r}{s}},

where as above
\ell_{S}(v_{1},...,v_{k-1})
denotes the length of the longest tight monotone path ending at
v_{1},...,v_{k-1}
which only uses colors in the set
S\in[r]^{(s)}
.

Consider the function

\displaystyle\Theta_{1}:\{v_{1},...,v_{k-2}\}\longmapsto S(v_{1},...,v_{k-2})=\{P(u,v_{1},...,v_{k-2})\in[n]^{\binom{r}{s}}:u<v_{1}\}

mapping elements of
[N]^{(k-2)}
into subsets of
[n]^{\binom{r}{s}}
.
We can define the exact same
K
-majority tournament as above on each
S(v_{1},...,v_{k-2})
and obtain a total dominating set
D(v_{1},...,v_{k-2})\subseteq S(v_{1},...,v_{k-2})
of size at most
CK\log K
.
With this, again consider a second map

\Theta_{2}:\{v_{1},...,v_{k-2}\}\longmapsto D(v_{1},...,v_{k-2})\subseteq S(v_{1},...,v_{k-2}).

Now let
S\subseteq[n]^{\binom{r}{s}}
have size
|S|\leq CK\log K
.
We claim that
\Theta_{2}^{-1}(S)
has no tight
(k-2)
-uniform monotone path of length two.
Indeed, if it had such a path on vertices
v_{1}<v_{2}<...<v_{k-1}
, then each
\vec{x}\in D(v_{2},...,v_{k-1})=S=D(v_{1},...,v_{k-2})\subseteq S(v_{1},...,v_{k-2})
by definition has
\vec{x}=P(v_{0},v_{1},...,v_{k-2})
for some
v_{0}<v_{1}
; but then

\vec{x}=P(v_{0},...,v_{k-2})\prec_{i}P(v_{1},...,v_{k-1})\in S(v_{2},...,v_{k-1})

in the
\binom{r-1}{s-1}\geq K
coordinates
i
corresponding to paths which are allowed to contain the color
\Delta(v_{0},...,v_{k-1})
,
and so
P(v_{1},\dots,v_{k-1})\to\vec{x}
for all
\vec{x}\in D(v_{2},\dots,v_{k-1})
, contradicting the fact that
D(v_{2},...,v_{k-1})
was chosen as a total dominating set for
S(v_{2},...,v_{k-1})
.

In other words, then, the partition

[N]^{(k-2)}=\bigcup_{S\subseteq[n]^{\binom{r}{s}},|S|\leq CK\log K}\Theta_{2}^{-1}(S)

defines an
\binom{n^{\binom{r}{s}}}{\leq CK\log K}
-coloring of the
(k-2)
-tuples of
[N]
with no monochromatic monotone path of length two.
Thus, by Theorem
1.2
, when
k\geq 4
we have

N\leq MS_{k-2}\left(2;n^{C^{\prime}\binom{r}{s}^{2}\log\binom{r}{s}}\right)\leq T_{k-4}\left(2^{n^{C^{\prime}\binom{r}{s}^{2}\log\binom{r}{s}}}\right)=T_{k-3}\left(n^{C^{\prime}\binom{r}{s}^{2}\log\binom{r}{s}}\right).

∎

5
A characterization of
A_{k}(n;r,s)

5.1
Generalized
S
-increasing sequences

We will give a characterization of
A_{k}(n;r,s)
in terms of what we call “generalized
S
-increasing sequences” which simultaneously generalizes Loh’s characterization for the 2-uniform problem (
[
14
, Lemma 2.1]
) and Moshkovitz and Shapira’s characterization for their problem in all uniformities
k
(
[
18
, Theorem 5]
).
These generalized
S
-increasing sequences extend the (2-uniform) notion of
S
-increasing sequences, which were studied extensively by Gowers and Long in
[
13
]
.

Definition 5.1
.

Let
S<R
and
n
be positive integers.
We define
M_{k}(n;R,S)
and
m_{k}(n;R,S)
by recursion on
k
as follows.

•

For
\vec{x}=(x_{1},x_{2},\dots,x_{R}),\vec{y}=(y_{1},y_{2},\dots,y_{R})\in[n]^{R}
, we say that
\vec{x}
is
S
-less
than
\vec{y}
, denoted
\vec{x}<_{S}^{(2)}\vec{y}
, if
x_{\ell}<y_{\ell}
for at least
S
coordinates
\ell\in[R]
.
A sequence
(\vec{x}_{i})
in
[n]^{r}
is
S
-increasing
if
\vec{x}_{i}<_{S}^{(2)}\vec{x}_{j}
for all
i<j
.
Let
M_{2}(n;R,S)
be the set of all
S
-increasing sequences in
[n]^{R}
, and let
m_{2}(n;R,S)
denote the length of the longest sequence in
M_{2}(n;R,S)
.

•

For
k\geq 2
, let
\mathcal{P}^{(k-2)}([n]^{R})
denote the
(k-2)
-fold power set
\mathcal{P}(\dots(\mathcal{P}([n]^{R})))
of
[n]^{R}
, with
\mathcal{P}^{(0)}([n]^{R})=[n]^{R}
, and recursively assume that we have defined a relation
<_{S}^{(k)}
on
\mathcal{P}^{(k-2)}([n]^{R})
.
We define the relation
<_{S}^{(k+1)}
on
\mathcal{P}^{(k-1)}([n]^{R})
as follows:
for two sets
\mathcal{F},\mathcal{F}^{\prime}\in\mathcal{P}^{(k-1)}([n]^{R})
, we put
\mathcal{F}<_{S}^{(k+1)}\mathcal{F}^{\prime}

if there exists a distinguished element
F^{\prime}\in\mathcal{F}^{\prime}
such that
F<_{S}^{(k)}F^{\prime}
for all
F\in\mathcal{F}
.
We will set
M_{k+1}(n;R,S)
to be the set of all sequences
(\mathcal{F}_{i})
in
\mathcal{P}^{(k-1)}([n]^{R})
with the property that for all
i<j
,
\mathcal{F}_{i}<_{S}^{(k+1)}\mathcal{F}_{j}
.
Sequences in
M_{k+1}(n;R,S)
will also be called
S
-increasing
, and we will denote by
m_{k+1}(n;R,S)
the length of the longest sequence in
M_{k+1}(n;R,S)
.

Example 5.2
.

Here are explanations in words for
k=3
and
k=4
of what is meant in Definition
5.1
:

•

Elements of
M_{3}(n;R,S)
are sequences
(F_{1},F_{2},\dots)
of
subsets

F_{i}\subseteq[n]^{R}
such that for all
i<j
,
F_{i}<_{S}^{(3)}F_{j}
, i.e., there exists
\vec{x}^{\prime}\in F_{j}
with
\vec{x}<_{S}^{(2)}\vec{x}^{\prime}
for every
\vec{x}\in F_{i}
;

•

Elements of
M_{4}(n;R,S)
are sequences
(\mathcal{F}_{1},\mathcal{F}_{2},\dots)
of
families of subsets

\mathcal{F}_{i}\subseteq 2^{[n]^{R}}
with the property that for all
i<j
,
\mathcal{F}_{i}<_{S}^{(4)}\mathcal{F}_{j}
, i.e., there is some
F^{\prime}\in\mathcal{F}_{j}
for which
F<_{S}^{(3)}F^{\prime}
holds for all
F\in\mathcal{F}_{i}
.

Our next theorem shows that
A_{k}(n;r,s)
is always upper bounded by
m_{k}(n;\binom{r}{s},\binom{r-1}{s-1})+1
, and moreover that this upper bound is sharp for both the usual Ramsey regime
s=1
(i.e.,
MS_{k}(n;r)
) and the Erdős-Szemerédi color-avoiding regime
s=r-1
.

Theorem 5.3
.

For all integers
n\geq k\geq 2
and
r>s\geq 1
,
A_{k}(n;r,s)\leq m_{k}(n;\binom{r}{s},\binom{r-1}{s-1})+1
.
When
s\in\{1,r-1\}
, the lower bound
A_{k}(n;r,s)>m_{k}(n;\binom{r}{s},\binom{r-1}{s-1})
also holds.

Proof that
A_{k}(n;r,s)\leq m_{k}(n;r,s)+1
.

Let
\Delta:[N]^{(k)}\to[r]
be a coloring in which every tight monotone path on
n
edges uses at least
s+1
colors.
Define maps
P_{k-1},P_{k-2},\dots,P_{1}
by reverse-recursion as follows:

(1)

Let
P_{k-1}:[N]^{(k-1)}\longrightarrow[n]^{\binom{r}{s}}
for
v_{2}<\dots<v_{k}
be given by

P_{k-1}(v_{2},\dots,v_{k})=\langle 1+\ell_{S}(v_{2},\dots,v_{k})\rangle_{S\in[r]^{(s)}}\in[n]^{\binom{r}{s}},

where
\ell_{S}(v_{2},\dots,v_{k})
denotes the longest monotone path ending at
v_{2}<\dots<v_{k}
which only uses colors in the set
S\in[r]^{(s)}
;

(2)

For
2\leq i\leq k-1
, define
P_{k-i}:[N]^{(k-i)}\longrightarrow\mathcal{P}^{(i-1)}([n]^{\binom{r}{s}})
for
v_{i+1}<\dots<v_{k}
by

P_{k-i}(v_{i+1},\dots,v_{k})=\{P_{k-i+1}(u,v_{i+1},\dots,v_{k})\in\mathcal{P}^{(i-2)}([n]^{\binom{r}{s}}):u<v_{i+1}\}.

Notice that
P_{1}
is a map from
[N]
into
\mathcal{P}^{(k-2)}([n]^{\binom{r}{s}})
.
We claim that the sequence
(P_{1}(v))_{v=1}^{N}
is in
M_{k}(n;{r\choose s},{r-1\choose s-1})
which would give (setting
N=A_{k}(n+1;r,s)-1
)

A_{k}(n;r,s)-1\leq m_{k}\left(n;\binom{r}{s},\binom{r-1}{s-1}\right).

To see this, suppose that
u<v
are in
[N]
.
We need to show
P_{1}(u)<_{\binom{r-1}{s-1}}^{(k)}P_{1}(v)
.
First, note that for any
s_{1}<\dots<s_{k-2}<u
, we have

P_{k-1}(s_{1},\dots,s_{k-2},u)<_{\binom{r-1}{s-1}}^{(2)}P_{k-1}(s_{2},\dots,s_{k-2},u,v)

in
[n]^{\binom{r}{s}}
, as the longest paths ending at
(s_{1},\dots,s_{k-2},u)
which only use colors in
S\in[r]^{(s)}
with
\Delta(s_{1},\dots,s_{k-2},u,v)\in S
can be extended one edge longer to end at
(s_{2},\dots,s_{k-2},u,v)
while still only using colors in
S
;
as such, the longest paths ending at
(s_{2},\dots,s_{k-2},u,v)
only using colors in these
\binom{r-1}{s-1}
sets
S
will be strictly longer than those ending at
(s_{1},\dots,s_{k-2},u)
.

Now assume inductively that for
1\leq i\leq k-1
, we have shown

P_{k-i}(s_{i},\dots,s_{k-2},u)~<_{\binom{r-1}{s-1}}^{(i+1)}~P_{k-i}(s_{i+1},\dots,s_{k-2},u,v)

holds for any choice of
s_{i}<s_{i+1}<\dots<s_{k-2}<u
.
Since

P_{k-i}(s_{i+1},\dots,s_{k-2},u,v)\in P_{k-(i+1)}(s_{i+2},\dots,s_{k-2},u,v)

and every
F\in P_{k-(i+1)}(s_{i+1},\dots,s_{k-2},u)
has the form
F=P_{k-i}(s_{i},s_{i+1},\dots,s_{k-2},u)
for some
s_{i}<s_{i+1}
, we get that

P_{k-(i+1)}(s_{i+1},\dots,s_{k-2},u)<_{\binom{r-1}{s-1}}^{(i+2)}P_{k-(i+1)}(s_{i+2},\dots,s_{k-2},u,v)

with distinguished element
F^{\prime}=P_{k-i}(s_{i+1},\dots,s_{k-2},u,v)
immediately from our inductive hypothesis. To finish, setting
i=k-1
gives
P_{1}(u)<_{\binom{r-1}{s-1}}^{(2)}P_{1}(v)
, as desired.
∎

Proof that
A_{k}(n;r,s)>m_{k}(n;\binom{r}{s},\binom{r-1}{s-1})
for
s\in\{1,r-1\}
.

Let
m_{k}(n;\binom{r}{s},\binom{r-1}{s-1})=N
and let
(\mathcal{F}_{i})_{i=1}^{N}
be a maximum-length sequence in
M_{k}(n;\binom{r}{s},\binom{r-1}{s-1})
.
We define an
r
-coloring
\Delta:[N]^{(k)}\to[r]
in which no tight monotone path on
n
edges uses at most
s
colors.

Let
i_{1}<i_{2}<\dots<i_{k}
be elements of
[N]
, and consider
\mathcal{F}_{i_{1}},\dots\mathcal{F}_{i_{k}}
.
Since the sequence of
\mathcal{F}_{i}
’s is
\binom{r-1}{s-1}
-increasing, we know that for each
j\in\{2,\dots,k\}
, there exists
F_{i_{j-1},i_{j}}\in\mathcal{F}_{i_{j}}
such that
F<_{\binom{r-1}{s-1}}^{(k)}F_{i_{j-1},i_{j}}
for all
F\in\mathcal{F}_{i_{j-1}}
.
In particular, we have

F_{i_{1},i_{2}}<_{\binom{r-1}{s-1}}^{(k)}F_{i_{2},i_{3}}<_{\binom{r-1}{s-1}}^{(k)}\dots<_{\binom{r-1}{s-1}}^{(k)}F_{i_{k-1},i_{k}}.

(2)

Now repeat this
k-2
more times — each time, we find distinguished elements in all but the first set which satisfy (
2
).
At the end we are left with
\vec{x}_{i_{1},\dots,i_{k-1}}<_{\binom{r-1}{s-1}}^{(2)}\vec{x}_{i_{2},\dots,i_{k}}
in
[n]^{\binom{r}{s}}
.

•

If
s=1
:
Then
\binom{r-1}{s-1}=1
, and so
\vec{x}_{i_{1},\dots,i_{k-1}}<_{1}^{(2)}\vec{x}_{i_{2},\dots,i_{k}}
, i.e., there is some coordinate in which
\vec{x}_{i_{1},\dots,i_{k-1}}
is strictly less than
\vec{x}_{i_{2},\dots,i_{k}}
, and we can define our coloring
\Delta
by setting
\Delta(i_{1},\dots,i_{k})
to be the least coordinate where this occurs.
Then any monotone tight path on the
t
edges on vertices
i_{1}<\dots<i_{t+k-1}
using at most one color
c\in[\binom{r}{1}]=[r]
(i.e., which is monochromatic) would yield a sequence

\vec{x}_{i_{1},\dots,i_{k-1}}<_{1}^{(2)}\vec{x}_{i_{2},\dots,i_{k}}<_{1}^{(2)}\dots<_{1}^{(2)}\vec{x}_{i_{t+1},\dots,i_{t+k-1}}

which strictly increases in the coordinate
c
, whence
t\leq n-1
.

•

If
s=r-1
: Then
\binom{r-1}{s-1}=r-1
and
\vec{x}_{i_{1},\dots,i_{k-1}}<_{r-1}^{(2)}\vec{x}_{i_{2},\dots,i_{k}}
, so in all but one coordinate the entry in
\vec{x}_{i_{1},\dots,i_{k-1}}
is strictly less than the one in
\vec{x}_{i_{2},\dots,i_{k}}
.
Therefore, we can define our coloring
\Delta
by setting
\Delta(i_{1},\dots,i_{k})
to be the (lexicographically) least
(r-1)
-set of coordinates on which
\vec{x}_{i_{1},\dots,i_{k-1}}<_{r-1}^{(2)}\vec{x}_{i_{2},\dots,i_{k}}
holds.
Now any monotone tight path on the
t
edges on vertices
i_{1}<\dots<i_{t+k-1}
using at most
r-1
colors yields a sequence

\vec{x}_{i_{1},\dots,i_{k-1}}<_{r-1}^{(2)}\vec{x}_{i_{2},\dots,i_{k}}<_{r-1}^{(2)}\dots<_{r-1}^{(2)}\vec{x}_{i_{t+1},\dots,i_{t+k-1}}

where there are at most
r-1
choices for the set of
r-1
coordinates where consecutive elements strictly increase.
Since this means that at most
r-1
of the
\binom{r}{r-1}=r
coordinates can be left out by one of these sets, there is some coordinate which is in all of them, and the
\vec{x}
’s must strictly increase on this coordinate.
Hence, we again have
t\leq n-1
. ∎

6
Concluding remarks

There are several problems related to this paper which remain open.
The most immediate is, of course, to give good lower bounds for
A_{k}(n;r,s)
.
To this end, we pose the following question.

Question 6.1
.

For all
n
sufficiently large,
k\geq 4
, and
r>s>r/2
, is it true that

A_{k}(n;r,s)\geq T_{k-3}(n^{c})

for some
c=c(r,s)>0
?

In other words, is there a construction showing that the tower height from our Theorem
1.6
cannot be improved?
We know that this is the case in uniformities
k=2
and
k=3
, but we do not know what the truth is in uniformity
k\geq 4
.
By Theorem
5.3
, for
s=r-1
it is equivalent to study the function
m_{k}(n;\binom{r}{s},\binom{r-1}{s-1})=m_{k}(n;r,r-1)
introduced in Section
5
.
It is worth pointing out while the usual approach to obtain tower-type lower bounds for hypergraph Ramsey numbers, known as the
stepping-up lemma
of Erdős and Hajnal
[
9
]
, adapts very well for the problem of lower bounding the function
MS_{k}(n;r)
(the lower bound in Theorem
1.2
above; see
[
12
,
18
]
), but fails for interesting reasons for this problem.

The corresponding color-avoiding problem for cliques is also open. Stepping-up lemmas for various combinations of
r
,
s
, and
k
were proved in
[
6
]
, but the quantitative aspects of the problem with
r=3
,
s=2
are still poorly understood even in uniformity three.
In this regime, a first-moment method argument yields an exponential-type lower bound, while standard arguments give a double-exponential upper bound, so the tower height remains undetermined.
Based on how our result distinguishes
A_{3}(n;3,2)
from
MS_{3}(n;2)
, we would like to make the following conjecture about the behavior of the three-uniform color-avoiding Ramsey function for
r=3
and
s=2
:

Conjecture 6.2
.

Let
R_{3}(n;3,2)
be the least integer
N
with the property that in every three-coloring of
E(K_{N}^{(3)})
, there exists a copy of
K_{n}^{(3)}
which uses at most two colors.
Then

R_{3}(n;3,2)\leq 2^{n^{c}}

for some
c>0
.

Another potentially fruitful direction would be to explore the case when
s\leq r/2
(for both the
A_{3}(n;3,2)
and
R_{3}(n;3,2)
). In this regime, Theorem
2.2
no longer applies, so it is an interesting problem to determine whether or not
A_{3}(n;r,s)
still exhibits a drastically different behavior than
MS_{3}(n;\lceil r/s\rceil)
, for example.

Last but not least, we would also like to mention recent work of Suk and Zeng
[
22
]
which establishes a connection between Ramsey numbers of 3-uniform monotone paths and the Erdős-Schur problem.
