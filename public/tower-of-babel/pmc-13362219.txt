Integrative metabolite–protein interaction networks reveal potential pathways and biomarkers in sepsis
Authors: Elham Amjad, Babak Sokouti
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Sepsis is a significant factor in morbidity and mortality, and the list of biomarkers that could be used to help identify and manage it is limited. We have herein, combined a set of 50 metabolites related to sepsis with a set of 171 proteins to build a protein-protein interaction network and then clustered the proteins included in the interaction network, performed pathway enrichment analysis, and provided a significance score (SScore) to rank the genes. The SScore is determined as the minus logarithm based 10 of the smallest false discovery rate-adjusted p-value in all the clusters, where a protein is located; the larger the result is the more the evidence of the relevance of the protein to sepsis. The use of this framework resulted in the prioritization of 125 higher-ranking Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) pathways that included known mechanisms, which is complement and coagulation cascades and platelet activation, and novel mechanisms, including sphingolipid metabolism and glycosylphosphatidylinositol (GPI)-anchor biosynthesis. The SScore highlighted existing sepsis-relevant genes, including, but not limited to, APOA1 and A2M, and the appearance of new candidates, including, but not limited to, IL18RAP and THBS1, capable of being used as previously unknown biomarkers. Such results provide a ranked list of pathways and genes that may guide future research on biomarker development and treatment of sepsis.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12879-026-13547-5.

Introduction

The problem of sepsis is a major health issue in the population due to its high incidence and a high mortality rate [1]. Sepsis has been defined as one of the unmet medical needs in the world by the World Health Organization (WHO) [2]. It is estimated to account for approximately 20% of all global deaths annually (about 11 million deaths) [1]. Each year, sepsis affects approximately 48.9 million people of all ages and locations worldwide [1]. Pathophysiologically, sepsis occurs when an infectious insult overwhelms the host causing a multi-system organ failure. Early identification and timely treatment are the most crucial factors to reducing morbidity in patients [3, 4], and a lack of timely diagnosis and treatment worsens morbidity, especially in healthcare facilities with limited resources.

Sepsis is characterized by an overactive immune response to infection manifested by systemic inflammation that causes organ damage [5, 6]. The triggering event is a cytokine storm that is induced by pathogen-associated molecular patterns (PAMPs) and damage-associated molecular patterns (DAMPs), which facilitate the production of pro-inflammatory cytokines including TNF- α, IL-1, IL-6, and IFN- gamma [5, 6]. Higher levels of cytokines are associated with a higher risk of mortality [5, 6] and endothelial damage, partly through the ADAM10 pathway, leads to coagulopathy and septic shock [7, 8]. The natural immune response is often biphasic, i.e. starting with a hyper-inflammatory, and afterwards immunosuppressive, phase, with T-lymphocytes becoming exhausted and T-regulatory cells becoming commonly increased [9, 10]. Such dualistic pattern makes it difficult to apply therapeutic strategies, and requires a fine balance between the inhibition of harmful inflammation and recovery of immune competence [11]. New evidence points to ferroptosis and metabolic perturbations as further causes of sepsis pathogenesis [12].

Metabolomics can provide a powerful platform of biomarker prioritization in sepsis because metabolite profiles can provide an insight into early diagnosis, prognostication, and underlying molecular processes [13]. The predictive value of panels of metabolites like cis-4-decenoylcarnitine and lactate has been demonstrated to predict clinical outcome, and the further analytical capabilities of nuclear magnetic resonance (NMR) and liquid chromatography-mass spectrometry (LC-MS) to discriminate disease conditions and predict mortality in pediatric septic shock [14]. Furthermore, the metabolomic studies contribute to the knowledge about neonatal sepsis and can underpin translational studies using animal models [15].

In spite of these developments, new sepsis biomarkers tend to have a restricted clinical utility because their sensitivity, specificity, and practicality limitations are suboptimal [16]. Traditional biomarkers, as procalcitonin (PCT) and C-reactive protein (CRP), indicate systemic inflammation, but lack sufficient diagnostic specificity and robust prognostic accuracy, with a risk of false positivity and limited outcome prediction [16–18]. The heterogeneous, dynamic nature of sepsis also makes interpretation more difficult, and no gold standard diagnostic reference has been established yet [18]. The high expenses, special equipment, and lack of standard protocols also hinder the extended application [16, 18].

Protein-protein interaction (PPI) networks and systems biology methods are essential in explaining the processes of sepsis and the identification of new biomarkers [19]. Multi-omics data-based on metabolomic data incorporated into PPI frameworks can shed light on the key molecular events and can also guide potential therapeutic targets [19]. Weighted Gene Co-expression Network Analysis (WGCNA) and curated databases like ConsensusPathDB are analytical algorithms that can be used to identify key nodes and pathways [20, 21]. Machine-learning methods can then be used to reconcile multi-omic datasets to predict results [22–24]. However, many sepsis pathways are still poorly mapped, including neutrophil inflammatory responses in acute respiratory distress syndrome, mitochondrial involvement, energy metabolism alteration disrupting organ functions, post-translational changes in inflammation, immune checkpoint controls, the interaction of endothelial activation and coagulopathy, and neuroinflammatory mechanisms [25–31]. Previously conducted studies have often focused on individual metabolites, genes, or proteins, which limits the creation of integrated information.

We used a metabolite-first approach in order to overcome these constraints and focus on biologically salient associations. Metabolites are phenotype-proximal biomarkers of sepsis-induced dysregulation, including energetic failure, lipid imbalance, and oxidative stress, and, therefore, yield robust signals across both heterogeneous patient groups, unlike proteomic or transcriptomic data that have very high variability with regard to timing of sampling, tissue origin, and patient-specific factors [13–15]. Metabolites provide targeted points of network initiation by reproducing downstream functional outputs well correlated with clinical phenotypes that can detect real-time biochemical changes. We have accordingly curated a collection of 50 sepsis-related metabolites of the KNApSAcK Human Biomarker Database, and selected those entries containing high-confidence human associations such as a change in metabolite levels, prognostic significance, and mechanistic connections [32]. Protein partners were mapped using direct associations reported in the Human Metabolome Database (HMDB) and using KEGG pathways [33, 34], and 171 unique proteins were deduplicated. This metabolite-based paradigm is opposed to both protein-first approaches that can fail to consider upstream metabolic drivers and symmetric multi-omics integration plans which are at risk of increasing the complexity of responses to the heterogeneous data feeds. Even though publicly accessible sepsis proteomic and metabolomic data present a large scale, they often represent a significant amount of heterogeneity, such as differing analytical platforms, unequal sampling times, different patient groups, batch effects, and technical noise, which undermines reliable network inference and can be biased [16, 18]. These problems are alleviated by the literature-supported curated seed list, which then is used to build the protein-protein interaction (PPI) network with high confidence using HIPPIE v2.0 [35], to identify the overlap between clusters with a protein using DPClusO [36–38], to enrich pathways using DAVID [39–41], and to prioritize the candidate biomarkers with the SScore (defined as -log10 of the minimum false-discovery-rate adjusted p-value in a cluster containing the protein). This approach can be used to identify new processes (e.g., sphingolipid metabolism, glycosylphosphatidylinositol-anchor biosynthesis) and candidate genes (e.g., IL18RAP, THBS1) on which to build on biomarkers and therapeutic studies beyond already known biomarkers like CRP and PCT.

This combined platform is the initial attempt to synthesize a curated metabolite panel and high-confidence PPI clustering and SScore analysis in order to potentially identify the biological processes and candidate targets that were previously uncharacterized in sepsis.

Materials and methods

The methodology of this study was performed using a systematic approach in multiple steps. The data on biomarker metabolites was obtained using a trustworthy database of literature, so that all are covered. We then searched the known databases, including HMDB and KEGG, for metabolite-related proteins. Subsequently, a sepsis-related PPI network was established using HIPPIE v2. 0 database to infer the relationships between the detected proteins. The generated PPI network was further clustered using the DPClusO algorithm to identify important clusters/modules within the network. Finally, pathway enrichment analysis was performed using the DAVID tool to explain the biological meaning and pathways of the protein clusters. This combined protocol allowed profiling the sepsis metabolite biomarkers and their protein networks in more detail (Fig. 1).

Data collection of metabolite biomarkers

A list of fifty metabolites related to sepsis was obtained by curation using the KNApSAcK Human Biomarker Database [32], using the filter to identify entries in the sepsis disease page of the database that were under the category of Metabolite. Recurring hits totaled fifty four yet, this was diminished to fifty after the removal of duplicate hits. Associations of high confidence, including altered metabolite levels, prognostic relevance, and mechanistic associations, were prioritized in the selection process and ambiguous or non-metabolite entries were not selected. The final inventory, including CID, name, relation, and a related KNApSAcK reference is given in the Supplementary Table S1. Direct association between proteins in HMDB and KEGG pathways produced 171 different proteins or genes (Fig. 2b and Supplementary Table S2). We entered this database into our work because of its particularly extensive and cleaned up information beyond the broadest data source available at present, incorporating 4,539 disease-biomarker associations in 18 major disease classes. It was obtained through the selectively curated information on high-quality sources, such as NCBI, published patents, and scientific conferences, thus giving a high degree of confidence in the data to be further processed. In addition, it classifies biomarkers into hierarchical groups (e.g., proteins and metabolites) and associates them with standardized disease classification. This intelligent format allows for the regular extraction of biomarkers for a certain disease, such as sepsis, and the study of interrelated diseases through common pathways of biomolecules.

Identification of metabolite-associated proteins

It was based on a metabolite-centric approach, as metabolites provide a phenotype-proximal readout of the deep metabolic dysregulation that defines sepsis, which is energy crisis, lipid imbalance and oxidative stress. These metabolomic signatures can be considered more reproducible across studies than proteomic or transcriptomic based studies. The list of curated, high-confidence metabolites obtained with KNApSAcK can be used to start with a reduction of the noise that comes with heterogeneous omics datasets and focus on biologically-relevant entities. Proteins were then identified by direct interaction in the Human Metabolome Database (HMDB) and limited to KEGG pathways, which involved using known pathway data to generate interpretable protein-protein interaction seeds. This method is in contrast to protein-first or symmetric multi-omic integration, which makes the analysis less complex and enables the identification of new associations in the context of sepsis.

Protein identification involved manual searching against each metabolite in the HMDB web interface, querying all enzymes, transporters, carriers or other functional proteins associated with it. Other mappings were acquired through KEGG pathways when possible [33, 34]. All identifiers were then normalized to UniProt accession numbers/gene symbols and 171 unique proteins/genes were obtained following the elimination of overlaps. The last dataset is provided in Supplementary Table S2 and Fig. 2b. As a result of the curation by hand performed by HMDB, there were no intermediary file linkages between the details of each metabolite. The process is reproducible by repeating the HMDB searches of each metabolite and following the same standardization measures.

Building of the sepsis-associated PPI network

An interaction data in the HIPPIE v2.0 database that combined experimentally validated human PPIs with confidence scores in each interaction was used to assemble a sepsis-specific PPI network [35]. The network was used with the proteins identified in the above section as seed nodes. The inclusion of interactions was done based on the following criteria: all interactions between seed proteins were considered regardless of score, but interactions between seed proteins and non seed proteins were only considered when the HIPPIE confidence score was greater than 0.7 thereby ensuring high quality edge selection. The resultant undirected PPI network was then visualised and analysed in cytoscape software. The most important topological characteristics such as the number of nodes, the density of edges, the average path length, the clustering coefficient, and the distribution of the degree were calculated to describe the network structure.

Clustering of the PPI network

The protein protein interaction (PPI) network of sepsis was clustered with the densitybased overlapping clustering algorithm DPClusO [36–38]. The DPClusO was run at density thresholds of 0.1 through 0.9 at a step of 0.1 to produce a total of nine results. Each density value was analyzed using singleton nodes to test statistical enrichment of sepsis-associated proteins with Fisher exact test, and the p-values were adjusted with specifically the false discovery rate (FDR) of the Benjamini-Hochberg. Groups whose FDR adjusted p-value was less than 0.05 were considered statistically significant. The inclusion of singleton nodes in the enrichment assessment was due to the capability of DPClusO to produce singleton elements; a large singleton would indicate that a protein is strongly related to sepsis to the absence of any multi-protein module, which however, were not very common in this richly connected network. The density threshold of 0.4 was selected because this is the maximum density threshold, which has the highest discriminatory power, assessed using the balanced metric (true-positive rate minus false-positive rate) or seen in Fig. 3.

Each gene was assigned a metric score known as SScore (Significance Score), which indicated the confidence of the prediction of the gene based on the p-values of the clusters that the gene belongs to. Mathematically, SScore = -log10(p-value) where p-value < 0.05. Since DPClusO forms overlapping clusters a gene can be in several clusters and thus be related to a number of p-values. The minimum p-value between its associated clusters was used to obtain the SScore of each gene (i.e., SScore = -log10(min FDR-adjusted p -value across clusters)).

Biologically, a larger SScore implies that the protein is part of at least one cluster with strong sepsis enrichment, thus being part of a protein network module with the highest statistical over-representation of known sepsis-associated proteins compared to the background. The scoring scheme is based on the priorities of the candidates that are located in the most discriminative subnetworks, instead of using topological measures (e.g., degree centrality) alone or average p-values across clusters [42, 43]. Thus, the technique underlines proteins that have strong indications of sepsis applicability to the combined metabolite-protein network.

The efficacy of the density thresholds was assessed by how well the density thresholds were able to distinguish known sepsis proteins against background controls using balanced performance analysis. The optimal setting was chosen as the threshold that gave the maximum value of the balance metric (0.4).

Pathway enrichment analysis

The biological pathway analysis was conducted to determine the functional relevance of the established clusters of genes. It was achieved by way of the Database of Annotation, Visualization, and Integrated Discovery (DAVID) in which every cluster was considered as a distinct set of genes to determine the over-represented KEGG pathways [39–41]. A pathway was considered significantly enriched when it included at least two input cluster genes, with an EASE score of ≤ 0.1 and a false discovery rate adjusted P-value of ≤ 0.05. The top three pathways in each cluster were selected. To combine these findings, a bipartite network was created between the clusters and pathways. The frequency of enrichment was computed to rank the pathways based on relevance and strength within the sepsis network; the number of statistically significant clusters, covering all the central sepsis-related genes, (false discovery rate 0.05) in which each KEGG pathway was found significant in DAVID. The pathways which had appeared in several important clusters and/or interacted with significant proteins/genes were then given preference as being more central to the integrated network biology. This approach serves as a custom proxy measure of pathway robustness across modules, avoiding dependence on the p-value when performing an individual enrichment analysis.

External validation of network predictions

To assess the topological predictions of our Sscore metric, we re-analysed the transcriptomic data of the independent sepsis dataset, GSE28750 (containing 10 samples from sepsis patients and 20 from healthy controls). We performed differential expression analysis using the NCBI GEO2R web tool, with patients and healthy controls [44, 45]. Of special interest to us were the Log Fold Change (LogFC) and p-values of our top-ranked network candidates (IL18RAP and THBS1) for verification of their dysregulation in an independent cohort.

Results

Summary of metabolite-associated gene and network construction

A total of 50 sepsis-related metabolites were matched to 171 exclusive genes and proteins based on the Human Metabolome Database (HMDB) and KEGG. To create a sepsis-specific PPI network, the proteins detected in the above section were used with HIPPIE v2.0. The network had 2692 nodes and 3435 edges, an average connectivity of 2.619, a clustering coefficient of 0.015, and an average shortest path length of 4.563. The analysis of degree distribution showed that the network followed a power-law distribution, which is one of the characteristics of a scale-free topology, as is common in biological systems (Fig. 2a).

Clustering of the PPI network

The PPI network was clustered using DPClusO. Performance assessment measured by the balanced metric curve (Fig. 3) showed a less-than-optimal but informative performance, probably because of the reference set of known genes associated with sepsis. From the results of the classification model performance under different density settings, we can see that the balanced metric value changes significantly, indicating the model’s discriminative ability. The balanced metric (i.e., the absolute value of the difference between TPR and FPR), which indicates performance, reaches its maximum around the middle-density labels (density = 0.3 to 0.5), and greater or lesser values are obtained at both extremes (density = 0.7 to 0.9). This observation implies that the model is most sensitive and specific at intermediate levels of density, but the performance declines at very low and very high-density regimes. The use of a sketch-style drawing for the lines has the effect of emphasizing these trends very nicely, and one can easily see how the model accuracy changes with density. This observation guides the use of the right density parameter to maximize the level of classification in real-life scenarios. The cluster set achieved the highest value in the balanced metric when the density threshold was 0.4. Thus, gene prediction was performed using this parameter. Genes within these two significant clusters were extracted, and the p-value was adjusted using Benjamini-Hochberg [46] for multiple testing. Genes with adjusted p-values < 0.05 were regarded as sepsis-specific predictive genes. Importantly, the threshold density of 0.4 presented a wide range of clusters (i.e., 787). Among them, 183 clusters were significant by the Fisher exact test (FDR adjusted p-value < 0.05). These inflammatory clusters which included proteins with putative role in sepsis were of different sizes.

Functional interpretation of major pathways

Most of these enriched pathways suggest a direct relationship with the immunopathogenesis of sepsis. For example, the complement and coagulation cascade pathway (seven clusters) are involved in host defense and thrombosis, both of which are characteristic of sepsis development. Platelet activation pathway and cholesterol metabolism pathway (6 and 11 clusters, respectively) are significant processes related to inflammation, endothelial dysfunction, and immunity. The TGF-ß, PPAR and insulin-resistance signaling pathway enrichment is another example of metabolic and immunomodulatory abnormalities associated with organ failure caused by sepsis. The high presence of cell-adhesion junction pathways of bacterial invasion of the epithelial cells and tight junctions suggest integrity of the barrier and pathogen-host interactions.

Gene prioritization using SScore

To identify sepsis-related genes of top priority, an SScore algorithm was implemented in proteins of the PPI network with the density threshold set at 0.4. The genes were initially filtered using Fisher’s exact test (p-value < 0.05), and we obtained 221 disease-associated and 196 non-disease-associated candidate genes for further analysis. The latter were ordered by SScore quantifies statistical significance based on the strongest cluster-level enrichment. This method allowed the prioritization of sepsis-related genes with high confidence using the analytical centrality and strength of enrichment of the network. The APOA1, ACACA, and ADCY10 are highly ranked genes in the disease group and A2M, IL18RAP, THBS1 for the non-disease group. Although APOA1 and A2M as individual proteins have been associated with sepsis, our pipeline places them together with lesser-known candidates (IL18RAP, THBS1), indicating that they are a high-confidence set of biomarkers that have not been previously ranked among the highest by network-based methods.

Sscore analysis distribution of scores between disease and non-disease groups

In order to test the discriminative power of the SScore, p-values were converted to -log10(p-value) and plotted on a group-by-group basis. The distribution change was also clearly observed, where disease-associated genes were concentrated on lower p-values, which means that they are more relevant. As expected, the sepsis-related proteins were significantly higher in terms of SScore (which reflects a more pronounced cluster enrichment) than the background proteins (Fig. 5). This was statistically significant (Mann–Whitney U test, p-value < 0.01), and this indicates that the SScore is able to discriminate genes that are more likely to be related to sepsis as compared to background noise (Fig. 6).

External validation of predicted biomarkers

In order to confirm that the network-prioritized genes are biologically relevant in the disease, we mined the independent data (GSE28750). As shown in Supplementary Table S4 and the entire analysis in Fig. 7, the gene candidates showed significant upregulation (i.e., THBS1: LogFC = 2.77, p-value = 7.07 × 10⁻⁸ and L18RAP: LogFC = 1.99, p-value = 3.99 × 10⁻⁹). This suggests that the genes found to be structurally important in the current metabolite-seeded network are also the most dysregulated ones in the patients with sepsis.

Discussion

Besides confirming previously known mechanistic pathways, such as the complement and coagulation cascades, our integrative approach provided new results based on structural network topology. High-confidence sepsis-associated genes were not selected based on expression fold-changes but were ranked by their topological centrality within the metabolite-seeded PPI network and clusters of DPClusO via the SScore as well as familiar candidates like APOA1 and A2M. This method effectively promoted the ranks of IL18RAP and THBS1, identifying them as structurally critical nodes in the sepsis metabolic landscape. The reason why IL18RAP and THBS1 attained the high SScore rankings is that they are located in clusters with the most FDR-adjusted p-values, which serve as strong network-level signals of sepsis relevance in our network, but are limited by the general PPI data, and are not experimentally validated (see Limitations subsection). In addition, the excessive representation of pathways like glycosylphosphatidylinositol (GPI)-anchor biosynthesis and sphingolipid metabolism, which are otherwise rarely mentioned in connection with sepsis, are also marked by shortcomings of general PPI data, and are experimentally unproven (see Limitations). These were predicted association pathways found in four statistically significant clusters (Table 1; Fig. 3) that are a result of our metabolite-seeding PPI network (50 sepsis-associated metabolites, 171 proteins, HIPPIE interactions DPClusO clustering density = 0.4) and prevents a focus on single omics layers. We interpret such new findings in the framework of validated high-frequency pathways including cholesterol metabolism (frequency = 11), complement/ coagulation (frequency = 7) and platelet activation (frequency = 6) and processes related to sepsis pathophysiology.

Sepsis is also associated with a disrupted cholesterol metabolism causing a decrease in total cholesterol, LDL, and HDL levels that are related to unfavorable outcomes [47, 48]. Systemic inflammation inhibits the production of hepatic cholesterol and leads to hypocholesterolemia, which is associated with increased mortality and severe organ dysfunction [47]. The decreased levels of HDL are associated with increased inflammation and worse prognoses, and maladaptive HDL obtains pro-inflammatory features [49, 50]. The centrality of this pathway in the metabolite-protein network, with the top frequency (11 clusters) and integration with high–SScore proteins (e.g., APOA1 the highest-ranking gene in the disease group) indicates its centrality in our analysis, in which the seed metabolites are mapped on to lipid-related nodes, which mediate immune dysregulation and endothelial dysfunctions.

Clinical activation of complement is a central event during sepsis because anaphylatoxins C3a and C5a trigger the onset of inflammatory responses, activate immune cells, and cause tissue damage [51, 52]. C5a inhibits neutrophil activity, but opsonisation has the potential to inhibit over-activation that can also cause complement-mediated organ failure [51–53]. The uncontrolled complement activity adds to hypercoagulable condition and disseminated intravascular coagulation (DIC) [54, 55]. We have highlighted complement and coagulation cascades (frequency = 7) in large groups (FDR) and the hub form of the network (average degree = 2.619, scale-free topology) confirms that there is amplification of thromboinflammation by central proteins.

Platelets are very important in septic shock and affect immunity and coagulation. The platelets become activated and interlink with neutrophils, which promotes the development of neutrophil extracellular traps (NETs) that help to damage tissues [56]. They also produce pro-inflammatory cytokines, attract immune cells and increase hypercoagulability/DIC [57, 58]. Thrombocytopenia is associated with the worsened outcomes [58], and metrics like the mean platelet volume (MPV) can be used as the biomarkers [59]. In direct association with our clusters, platelet activation (frequency = 6) strengthens the metabolite-mediated vascular injury module in the PPI network, which is directly associated with coagulation pathways.

Sepsis has a significant effect on metabolism. The initial hyperinflammatory state induce glucose and fatty acid metabolism disorders, which eventually turn into lactate production [60, 61]. Further stages of immune-tolerance include mitochondrial inhibition and pathogen-clearance defects [62, 63]. This is often accompanied by abnormal glucose metabolism and insulin resistance [64, 65], and a metabolic transition to fatty acid oxidation [60, 66]. These metabolic transitions are reflected in our high-frequency enrichments (frequency 6) of pyruvate/carbon metabolism across three clusters, and in metabolite seeds which suggest energetic crisis in sepsis clusters.

The interrelation between coagulation and inflammation is at the heart of sepsis in which DIC, organ failure, and high mortality will result. Tissue factor is expressed by pro-inflammatory cytokines, thus triggering coagulation [67, 68]. Thrombin also prolongs inflammation in a feedback loop [67, 69]. Natural anticoagulants are broken down, which throws the hemostatic scale towards clotting [70, 71]. It is monitored by clinical indicators like D-dimer and platelet counts [59, 72, 73]. We have summed up this interaction with overlapping clusters that include complement/coagulation and platelet pathways and SScore prioritization has been used to emphasize the most relevant proteins.

Based on these confirmed mechanisms, our analysis concentrates specifically on new directions in a unique way. Sphingolipid metabolism (hsa00600; frequency = 4) is highly enriched, which indicates a disturbed bioactive lipid balance (e.g., pro-inflammatory ceramide vs. anti-inflammatory sphingosine-1-phosphate) which enhances inflammation, impairs immune cell migration, impairs the endothelium, and leads to lymphopenia, and results in acute respiratory distress syndrome-like effects mechanisms that are supported by new sepsis studies but rarely [74–78]. The metabolite mappings and cluster significance enhance the sphingolipid metabolism as an overlooked crossroads between the metabolic stress and the immune dysregulation.

GPI-anchor biosynthesis (hsa00563; frequency = 4) is also overrepresented. GPI fixes tether critical complement regulators including CD55 and CD59 to cellular membranes, which helps to protect the cell against attack by complement [79–81]. The mechanism has mostly not been highlighted in the sepsis transcriptomics or proteomics literature [82], and our metabolite-seeded system presents a new process in which metabolic stress and complement dysregulation meet. The loss of GPI - anchor biosynthesis during sepsis may expose host cells to complement-mediated damage, which could contribute to widespread cell death a hypothesis recently proposed by our lipid-metabolite seeds and enriched clusters previously not analyzed.

The prioritization provided by SScore supports the potential predicted association of such genes as IL18RAP (enriched in the non-disease group) by cluster-membership enrichment [83], and supports the hypothesis that amplified IL-18 signalling could increase patient heterogeneity and cytokine storms [84, 85]. THBS1 has been linked to thrombotic and inflammatory reactions and dysfunction of organs, such as myocardial damage [86–88]. Unlike expression-based methods that rely on variable transcriptomic data, our metric identifies these genes based on their position within the fixed protein-protein interaction network. Both genes are products of metabolite protein integration, so they bridge these gaps in the traditional omics where these candidates are given less priority.

Our design replicates the known mechanisms, and identifies new unexplored genes (IL18RAP, THBS1) and pathways (GPI-anchor biosynthesis, sphingolipid metabolism) as promising new targets of biomarkers and treatment. The disadvantages include the dependence on the generic PPI data and the lack of the experimental validation. Future studies must be assessments of the best candidates in models of the experiment and patient populations, combine dynamic omics, and use our pipeline to support precision-medicine strategies.

Notably, the gene candidates THBS1 and IL18RAP exhibited highly significant upregulation (LogFC > 1.9, p < 10⁻⁸) in the independent GSE28750 cohort, confirming that our network-based prioritization captures clinically relevant expression changes. The fact that independent cohort analysis reports changes of IL18RAP and THBS1 in sepsis serves as a crucial biological anchor, confirming that our topological ranking aligns with known pathophysiology. This suggests that while expression levels may vary across cohorts, the structural centrality of these genes within the metabolic network is a more robust indicator of their role in sepsis.

Limitations

It is based on an exploratory hypothesis-generating framework, which is an in silico, network-based methodology, which in turn has limitations. The protein protein interaction (PPI) network was constructed using the general HIPPIE v2.0 database, which does not have a disease and tissue specific resolution, includes computationally predicted or low-confidence interactions, and is biased against proteins that are commonly studied in specific experiments as opposed to high-throughput assays. This means that hubs of good character (e.g. APOA1) are more likely to be over-represented and sepsis-specific or context-dependent interactions will be less likely to be detected.

The analysis will start with an existing collection of 50 sepsis-related metabolites analyzed in KNApSAcK with a lot of confidence on the choice of seeds, but perhaps missing the bigger signals in large-scale proteomic, metabolomic, or lipidomic repositories. Mapping these metabolites to 171 proteins by HMDB and KEGG pathways confined to at most two reaction steps relies on the completeness of these databases and requires manual curation and so cannot be fully automated, introducing the risk of small inconsistencies.

The use of DPClusO clustering and SScore prioritization is a heuristic parameter sensitive technique (e.g. density thresholds, confidence cutoffs). Even though a balance metric analysis (see Fig. 3) allowed to define the optimal density of 0.4, the same clustering algorithms or scoring schemes could give different prioritizations. Also, although the SScore is used to assess statistical cluster enrichment, it does not test causality, functional influence or directionality in the undirected PPI network.

The genes and pathways that are prioritized (IL18RAP, THBS1) and lack direct experimental or clinical validation in this work are the genes and pathways in the final stage. These factors, though consistent with emerging sepsis literature, e.g. sphingolipid dysregulation as a cause of endothelial barrier dysfunction and inflammation, require validation in terms of biomarker or therapeutic value by specific proteomics, functional assays, independent patient cohorts or through animal models.

In spite of these limitations, the targeted metabolite-protein interaction provides a biologically meaningful structure of the priorities of the under-investigated mechanisms in sepsis and generates high-confidence candidates to be further successfully validated.

Conclusions

Taken together, the integrative metabolite protein network analysis supported the known pathophysiology of sepsis, in addition to identifying new signaling pathways and new possible gene targets based on network topology rather than expression levels. The use of clustering algorithms, pathway enrichment analysis, and the SScore ranking metric was helpful to identify biomarkers that were previously underappreciated, such as IL18RAP and THBS1. Because our method relies on structural connectivity within a curated metabolite network consisting of several clusters, it provides a stable framework for prioritizing candidates like IL18RAP and THBS1, independent of the noise often found in transcriptomic datasets. It is worth noting that the metabolic processes were also emphasized, including sphingolipid metabolism, which is less represented in the existing literature. These results do not only confirm the prevailing paradigms in biology but also give systematic guidance of ranking new therapeutic and diagnostic candidates. Therefore, the most prominent genes that include the IL18RAP and THBS1 should be assessed in clinical cohorts and in preclinical models in future studies. In addition, a strong machine-learning method can be used to rank lists of genes hierarchically and identify dynamic sepsis markers through the combination of metabolomic and transcriptomic data. In addition to the validation of canonical pathways, the less-characterized mechanisms, including the glycosylphosphatidylinositol (GPI)-anchor biosynthesis and the sphingolipid metabolism can be explored to reveal potential therapeutic opportunities. Overall, it can be inferred that such integrative approaches have the capacity to convert computational predictions into clinically useful biomarkers.
