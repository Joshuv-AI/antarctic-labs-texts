The effect of multimodal temperature management on the incidence of perioperative hypothermia in patients undergoing general anesthesia: a randomized controlled trial
Authors: Na Gong, Wenfei Dong, Jianyu Zhang, Siyang Li, Liyuan Meng, Xin Bi, Xiang Wu, Bing Shao
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundInadvertent Perioperative Hypothermia (IPH) remains a common clinical problem. This study aimed to evaluate the efficacy of a multimodal temperature management strategy versus passive insulation in reducing the incidence of IPH among patients undergoing general anesthesia.MethodsThis was a single-center, randomized, assessor-blinded randomized trial conducted at our hospital from November to December 2025. This study enrolled 206 patients scheduled for 4–8 h surgeries. They were randomly assigned to either a multimodal group (preoperative warming, forced-air warming blanket, fluid warming, increased ambient temperature, pharmacological intervention) or a control group (passive insulation with cotton quilts). Core body temperature was monitored at multiple perioperative time points. The primary outcome was the incidence of intraoperative hypothermia (core temperature < 36 °C). Secondary outcomes included inflammatory markers, blood gas parameters, fluid balance, pain scores, postoperative complications, and hospital length of stay.ResultsCompared with the control group, the multimodal group had a significantly lower incidence of intraoperative hypothermia starting at 90 min after surgery initiation (T5: control 60.6% vs. multimodal 48.0%; adjusted OR 0.37, 95% CI 0.15–0.93) and continuing to the end of surgery (T11: control 69.2% vs. multimodal 27.5%; adjusted OR 0.08, 95% CI 0.03–0.23). The multimodal intervention also reduced the incidence of hypothermia at operating room discharge (28% vs. 72%; adjusted OR 0.12, 95% CI 0.06–0.24) and suggest a potential reduction in the risk of postoperative cardiac adverse events (0% vs. 3.85%; P = 0.045). The multimodal group showed a greater increase in inflammatory markers (TNF-α, NLR, PLR), less pain at 48 h postoperatively, and a smaller reduction in pH.ConclusionThis multimodal temperature management bundle significantly lowers the incidence of perioperative hypothermia in patients undergoing prolonged surgery under general anesthesia, with benefits starting at 90 min and strengthening over time. It stabilizes metabolic parameters and may reduce cardiac complications and pain at 48 h postoperatively. Introduction

Inadvertent Perioperative Hypothermia (IPH), defined as a core body temperature below 36 °C in the perioperative period, is a common but preventable condition that negatively impacts patient recovery. IPH can lead to complications such as surgical site infection, cardiovascular events, coagulopathy, delayed recovery, altered drug metabolism, increased blood loss, and higher healthcare costs [1]. Although current guidelines recommend active warming strategies [2], clinical compliance remains suboptimal, and the incidence of hypothermia persists [3].

Several guidelines advocate for the continuous application of active warming throughout the perioperative period, routinely including forced-air warming [4]. Radiative heat loss primarily depends on the temperature gradient between the patient’s skin and the environment [5]. After redistribution, ambient temperature has a minimal effect on core temperature in warmed patients. It is widely recommended internationally to maintain operating room temperatures no lower than 21–23 °C. The combination of passive insulation measures and ambient temperature control constitutes composite warming. Existing research indicates that composite warming is more effective than single active warming methods [6]. Based on this concept, perioperative multimodal temperature management has emerged. Multimodal temperature protection integrates various warming modalities—preoperative warming [7], forced-air warming, fluid warming, and ambient temperature control [8]—to synergistically maintain normothermia. Although previous studies support the efficacy of individual warming methods, evidence from randomized controlled trials on integrated multimodal approaches remains limited. This study aimed to compare the effects of multimodal versus passive temperature protection on the incidence of IPH and related clinical outcomes in patients undergoing prolonged surgery under general anesthesia.

Methods

Study design and participants

This study is reported in accord with the CONSORT reporting guideline. This was a single-center, randomized, assessor-blinded randomized trial conducted at our hospital from November to December 2025. The study protocol was approved by the Institutional Review Committee (IRB) of the General Hospital of Northern Theater Command (Approval No: LUN SHEN-Y-2025-193) and followed the tenets of the Declaration of Helsinki. The trial was registered at the Chinese Clinical Trial Registry (ChiCTR2500112554; https://www.chictr.org.cn). Inclusion criteria: patients aged ≥ 18 years, American Society of Anesthesiologists Physical Status Classification System (ASA) physical status I-III, scheduled for elective surgery lasting 4–8 h under general anesthesia. Exclusion criteria: preoperative temperature > 38 °C or < 36 °C, antipyretic use within 24 h preoperatively, Body Mass Index (BMI) > 28 kg/m² or < 16 kg/m², pregnancy, active infection, psychiatric disorders precluding cooperation, neuropathy, peripheral vascular disease, thyroid disease, or requirement for controlled hypothermia. Withdrawal criteria: patient request to terminate participation, intraoperative occurrence of shock, massive hemorrhage, cardiac arrest, or other severe complications.

Included surgical types

All enrolled patients underwent elective major abdominal surgery (including gastrointestinal surgery, hepatobiliary surgery, and retroperitoneal surgery) under general anesthesia.

Surgical duration

All surgeries lasted 4–8 h as predefined in the inclusion criteria.

Heterogeneity management

Only major abdominal surgeries were included to minimize surgical type heterogeneity.

Statistical models (GEE and logistic regression) were adjusted for baseline temperature, and sensitivity analyses were further adjusted for age and BMI to reduce potential confounding from clinical heterogeneity.

Sample size calculation

Through literature review and preliminary experiments, the incidence of perioperative hypothermia in the control group and multi-mode group patients was 26% and 10%, respectively. Assuming bilateral α = 0.05, the test efficacy of 1- β was 0.8. The total sample size was calculated using Medinc software to be 174 cases. Considering a potential dropout rate of 10% (rather than the initial 20% conservative estimate), we aimed to enroll 208 participants to ensure adequate power.

Randomization and blinding

A computer-generated randomization sequence was generated using SPSS version 20.0. A complete randomization (simple) design was used, without blocking or stratification. Allocation concealment was achieved using sequentially numbered, opaque, sealed envelopes. Upon patient enrollment, clinical nursing staff opened the corresponding numbered envelope to reveal the assignment. Patients and personnel responsible for data analysis and outcome assessment were blinded to group allocation, while the clinical staff applying the interventions could not be.

Interventions

Control group

Received passive insulation using cotton quilts; ambient temperature was set at 22 °C. Active warming was initiated only if core temperature dropped below 36 °C.

Multimodal group

Received multimodal temperature protection with continuous intervention. The operating room temperature was set to 22 °C, but could be pre-warmed to 28 °C for patients at high risk of hypothermia (defined as age ≥ 70 years, BMI < 20 kg/m², preoperative core temperature < 36.2 °C, or presence of cardiovascular comorbidity). Interventions included preoperative warming using a forced-air warming blanket (initial temperature 40 °C), intraoperative adjustment of forced-air warming, warmed intravenous and irrigation fluids (40 °C), and additional insulation for exposed areas (a small quilt approximately 100 cm×60 cm×2 cm covering the shoulders and neck, and wrapping the feet to minimize heat loss). If patient temperature persistently exceeded 37.5 °C, warming was stopped immediately, the forced-air warmer was switched to cooling mode if available, and ice packs were applied if necessary, until temperature decreased to 37 °C.

Data collection

24 h preoperatively

Peripheral blood was drawn to measure inflammatory mediators (IL-1, IL-6, TNF-α), and values were recorded. Neutrophil-to-Lymphocyte Ratio (NLR) and Platelet-to-Lymphocyte Ratio (PLR) were calculated.

Monitoring site

Distal esophageal temperature was used as the core body temperature, which is a reliable and recommended site for core temperature monitoring during general anesthesia.

Monitoring device

A disposable esophageal temperature probe (YSI 400 series) connected to a patient monitoring system (Philips IntelliVue MP70) was used for continuous real‑time recording.

Monitoring protocol

Core temperature was measured and recorded at all predefined time points (T0 to T11) with a precision of 0.1 °C. All measurements were performed by trained anesthesiologists to ensure consistency and accuracy.

T0: Recorded baseline temperature upon entering the operating room.

T1: Measured temperature after 30 min of insulation with quilts.

T2: Measured temperature immediately after successful anesthesia induction.

T3: Measured temperature 30 min after surgery start; arterial blood gas analyzed for pH, PaO₂, PaCO₂.

T4: Measured temperature 60 min after surgery start.

T5: Measured temperature 90 min after surgery start.

T6: Measured temperature 120 min after surgery start.

T7: Measured temperature 150 min after surgery start.

T8: Measured temperature 180 min after surgery start.

T9: Measured temperature 210 min after surgery start.

T10: Measured temperature 240 min after surgery start.

T11 (End of surgery): Measured temperature upon leaving the operating room; arterial blood gas analyzed for pH, PaO₂, PaCO₂; recorded total intraoperative fluid input, urine output, and blood loss.

12 h postoperatively

Patients were visited; pain intensity at rest and during movement was assessed using the Numeric Rating Scale (NRS) and recorded.

24 h postoperatively

Patients were visited; NRS pain scores at rest and during movement were recorded; peripheral blood was drawn to measure IL-1, IL-6, TNF-α, and values recorded; Neutrophil-to-Lymphocyte Ratio (NLR) and Platelet-to-Lymphocyte Ratio (PLR) were calculated.

48 h postoperatively

Patients were visited; NRS pain scores at rest and during movement were recorded.

Other records

Incidence of postoperative cardiac adverse events (myocardial infarction, cardiac arrest, death), incidence of postoperative surgical site infection, and time from surgery end to hospital discharge were recorded.

Outcome measures

The primary outcome was the incidence of hypothermia at each intraoperative time point (T1-T11). The trajectory of hypothermia incidence over time. Hypothermia was defined as a patient temperature < 36 °C at any time point.

Secondary outcomes included: ① Incidence of hypothermia upon leaving the operating room (T11). ② Change in peripheral blood levels of IL-1, IL-6, TNF-α, Neutrophil-to-Lymphocyte Ratio (NLR) and Platelet-to-Lymphocyte Ratio (PLR) from preoperatively to 24 h postoperatively. ③ Change in arterial blood pH, PaO₂, and PaCO₂ from surgery start (T3) to surgery end (T11). ④ Intraoperative fluid input, blood loss, and urine output. ⑤ Numeric Rating Scale (NRS) pain scores at 12, 24, and 48 h postoperatively. ⑥ Time from surgery end to hospital discharge. ⑦ Incidence of postoperative cardiac adverse events (myocardial infarction, cardiac arrest, death) and surgical site infection. Numeric Rating Scale (NRS) scores were dichotomized: 0 representing no pain; ≥1 representing mild or greater pain. See Fig. 1.

Statistical analysis

Categorical variables are presented as frequency (%). Continuous variables are presented as mean and standard deviation (SD), or median and interquartile range (IQR).

For the primary outcome, based on the modified intention-to-treat principle, Generalized Estimating Equations (GEE) were used to analyze the effect of multimodal temperature protection on the incidence of perioperative hypothermia. An exchangeable working correlation structure was specified. The Generalized Estimating Equations (GEE) model included the following independent variables: group assignment, measurement time (T1-T11), interaction between group and time, and baseline temperature (T0, continuous variable). The dependent variable (hypothermia yes/no) was included as a binary variable.

For all secondary outcomes, a per-protocol analysis principle was applied. Logistic regression was used to analyze the effect of multimodal protection on hypothermia incidence at T11, adjusting for baseline temperature (T0, continuous variable). For other secondary outcomes: Shapiro-Wilk test was used to test for normality. continuous data conforming to normal or approximately normal distribution were compared between groups using the independent samples t-test; otherwise, non-parametric tests were used. Categorical data were compared using the χ2 test.

A sensitivity analysis was conducted for the primary outcome: the Generalized Estimating Equations (GEE) model was additionally adjusted for age and Body Mass Index (BMI) beyond the baseline temperature (T0) to assess the robustness of the findings. Generalized Estimating Equations (GEE) analysis was performed using the `geepack` package in R (version 4.5.2). All other statistical analyses were performed using SPSS (version 26.0). All P-values were two- sided, and P < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Two participants in the multimodal group withdrew voluntarily, leaving 102 in that group for the modified intention-to-treat analysis. See Fig. 2. The groups were similar in baseline age, Body Mass Index (BMI), preoperative 24-hour inflammatory markers, and temperature upon entering the operating room (T0). Baseline comparisons showed no significant between‑group differences in age, BMI, anesthesia time, or surgical duration (all P > 0.05). Details are shown in Table 1.

Effect of multimodal temperature protection on the incidence of perioperative hypothermia

A significant group-by-time interaction was observed, indicating that the effect of multimodal management developed over time, consistent with a time-dependent protective effect. Specifically, at early time points (T2-T4), the intervention effect was not significant. However, starting from T5 (90 min after surgery start), the risk of hypothermia in the multimodal group was significantly lower than in the control group, and this protective effect strengthened over time. At T5, multimodal protection reduced the risk of hypothermia by 63% adjusted 0.37(0.15–0.93); at T6 by 70% adjusted 0.30(0.12–0.76); and by T11, the risk reduction reached 92% adjusted 0.08(0.03–0.23). Results are shown in Fig. 3; Table 2. The main and individual effects of group and time factors are shown in Supplementary Table S1-3.

In sensitivity analysis, after further adjustment for age, the protective effect at T5 was no longer observed, but it remained significant from T6 to T11. Further adjustment for BMI did not materially alter these results, indicating robustness. See Supplementary Table S4.

Differences in secondary outcomes between multimodal and control groups

Hypothermia incidence upon leaving OR

Hypothermia incidence was 72% in the control group vs. 28% in the multimodal group. Logistic regression showed multimodal intervention significantly reduced the risk of hypothermia upon leaving the OR adjusted 0.12(0.06–0.24). See Fig. 4 and Supplementary Table S5.

Discussion

As a randomized controlled trial, this study systematically evaluated the impact of a multimodal temperature protection strategy on the incidence of perioperative hypothermia and related clinical outcomes in patients undergoing prolonged surgery under general anesthesia. The results demonstrate that compared to passive insulation, the multimodal intervention significantly reduces the incidence of intraoperative and post-anesthesia care unit hypothermia, and shows potential benefits in modulating inflammatory response, maintaining metabolic stability, reducing postoperative pain, and lowering the risk of cardiac adverse events. These findings provide strong evidence-based support for optimizing perioperative temperature management to improve patient outcomes. Explicitly stating that the individual contributions of each component (e.g., pre-warming vs. forced-air warming) cannot be determined from this study, explicitly acknowledge that individual component effects cannot be separated. We have also added a brief discussion on which components are likely most influential based on existing literature (pre-warming and forced-air warming) and have suggested future component-analysis or factorial design studies to optimize the bundle’s cost-effectiveness.

Effectiveness of multimodal protection against perioperative hypothermia

This study confirms that multimodal protection significantly reduces hypothermia incidence starting from 90 min after surgery (T5), with the protective effect strengthening over time 0.08(0.03–0.23) at T11. This time-dependent effect aligns with physiological mechanisms [5, 9]: redistribution hypothermia after anesthesia induction and continuous heat loss during surgery are primary causes of IPH. The multimodal strategy, through preoperative warming [10] to build heat reserve and intraoperative use of forced-air warming and warmed fluids [11] to continuously supply heat, effectively counteracts cumulative heat loss during prolonged surgery. This result is consistent with previous studies [12, 13], but our study further quantifies time course of the protective effect, emphasizing the clinical necessity of early and sustained multimodal warming in patients with anticipated surgery durations exceeding 1.5 h. The high incidence of hypothermia upon leaving the OR in the control group (69.2%) again highlights the inadequacy of relying solely on passive insulation or “as-needed” warming.

Impact of multimodal intervention on inflammatory and metabolic responses

Exploratory analysis suggests that the postoperative increase in TNF-α, Neutrophil-to-Lymphocyte Ratio (NLR) and Platelet-to-Lymphocyte Ratio (PLR) was more pronounced in the multimodal group. This finding warrants a dual interpretation. Traditionally, excessive inflammation is considered harmful. However, in the perioperative setting, a moderate inflammatory response is necessary for tissue repair and defense against potential infection. Hypothermia has been shown to suppress immune cell function [14]. Therefore, the more marked increase in inflammatory markers in the multimodal group may reflect that maintaining normothermia helps preserve or promote necessary postoperative immune activation, rather than inducing harmful hyper-inflammation. This aligns with the finding that the multimodal group did not have an increased infection rate [15]. It may reflect preserved immune responsiveness and that the clinical implications remain uncertain and require further investigation. Furthermore, the multimodal group showed a smaller decline in intraoperative arterial pH, suggesting it may help maintain a more stable internal environment and metabolic state, reducing the risk of tissue hypoperfusion and acidosis associated with hypothermia. These findings should be interpreted with caution pending confirmation in larger studies.

Benefits of multimodal intervention on postoperative recovery outcomes

Our study observed two key clinical improvements associated with the multimodal intervention: reduced pain intensity at 48 h postoperatively and a significantly lower incidence of postoperative cardiac adverse events (myocardial infarction, cardiac arrest). Reduced pain may be related to multimodal warming: hypothermia can cause shivering, increase catecholamine release, and slow drug metabolism, thereby exacerbating pain perception; whereas normothermia may optimize analgesic drug metabolism and reduce shivering-related discomfort. The reduction in cardiac events is clinically significant. Hypothermia can induce sympathetic stimulation, increase peripheral vascular resistance, promote arrhythmias, and disrupt coagulation. By maintaining normothermia, multimodal protection may stabilize the cardiovascular system, reducing cardiac oxygen demand and event risk. This finding provides direct evidence for temperature management as part of perioperative cardiac protection strategies [16]. This finding suggests a potential benefit that warrants investigation in a larger, adequately powered trial.

Strengths and limitations

Strengths of this study include a rigorous randomized controlled trial design, use of modified intention-to-treat analysis, and application of Generalized Estimating Equations (GEE) for longitudinal repeated measures data. The detailed intervention protocol and multiple time-point monitoring provide a high-resolution view of the intervention’s effect. Limitations include: First, this is a single-center study, and the sample size may be underpowered for some secondary outcomes (e.g., specific complications). Second, the intervention was implemented as a “bundle”, making it difficult to distinguish the relative contribution of individual components (e.g., preoperative warming, fluid warming). Third, reliance primarily on core temperature monitoring without simultaneous skin temperature monitoring limits a more comprehensive assessment of thermal balance. Fourth, patients and personnel responsible for data analysis and outcome assessment were blinded to group allocation, while the clinical staff applying the interventions could not be. Fifth, regarding multiple comparisons, we acknowledge that no adjustment was made for secondary outcomes, as they were considered exploratory. Sixth, although the registered protocol included various surgical types, the final study population was restricted to major abdominal surgeries to minimize clinical heterogeneity. This should be considered when generalizing our findings to other surgical populations. Finally, the postoperative follow-up period was relatively short, precluding assessment of long-term outcomes.

Clinical implications and future directions

Our results suggest that routine use of a multimodal active temperature protection strategy should become part of standard care for patients undergoing prolonged surgery under general anesthesia, rather than a rescue measure applied only after temperature drops. This strategy not only effectively prevents hypothermia but may also improve postoperative recovery quality and safety through multiple pathways. Future research should: conduct multicenter, large-scale studies to validate generalizability; perform component analysis studies to optimize the cost-effectiveness of warming strategies; explore the benefits of temperature management in specific high-risk populations (e.g., elderly, cardiovascular patients); and extend follow-up to assess impact on functional recovery after discharge and long-term survival.

Conclusion

In conclusion, this study confirms that, compared with passive insulation, multimodal temperature management significantly reduces the incidence of perioperative hypothermia in patients undergoing prolonged surgery under general anesthesia. Importantly, this strategy offers benefits beyond temperature control alone, including potential pleiotropic effects such as stabilized metabolism, modulating postoperative inflammatory response, reduced late postoperative pain, and possibly lower risk of cardiac complications. These findings strongly support incorporating proactive, preventive multimodal temperature management into standard comprehensive perioperative care to improve overall outcomes in surgical patients.
