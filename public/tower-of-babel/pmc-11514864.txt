Survival and predictors of mortality in patients with heart failure in the cardiology department of the Center Hospitalier Basse Terre in Guadeloupe: historical cohort study
Authors: Eric Tanckwe Mumbulu, Aliocha Natuhoyila Nkodila, Veauthyelau Saint-Joy, Narcisse Moussinga, Jean-Robert Rissassi Makulo, Nathan Bimbi Buila
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundDespite the progress made in recent years in the treatment of Acute Heart Failure (AHF), its prognosis remains poor in the developing country. The objective of this study is to analyze the survival and predictors of mortality of patients with acute heart failure in the cardiology department of the Basse Terre Hospital Center in Guadeloupe.Methodsthis was a historical cohort study carried out over a period from June 2021 to June 2022, targeting all acute heart failure patients undergoing cardiac monitoring in the cardiology department of the Basse Terre Hospital Center in Guadeloupe. Sociodemographic, clinical, biological characteristics and outcome (recovery or death) were studied. Survival was described using the Kaplan Meier method α = 5%.Resultsthis study involved 242 acute heart failure patients whose median age was 75 years and the majority were male (sex ratio 2 M/1F). Among these patients, 14.9% died, the most common cause of death was cardiogenic shock (52.8%). After adjustment, tobacco consumption (aHR: 2.90; 95% CI: 1.36–8.09), Chronic Kidney Disease (aHR: 2.52; 95% CI: 1.22–5.20), infection (aHR: 2.14; 95CI %: 1.99–4.58), hyponatremia (aHR: 1.90; 95% CI: 1.10–2.86), mitral regurgitation (aHR: 3.04; 95% CI: 1.98–9.47) and N-terminal pro Brain Natriuretic Peptide > 10000ng/ml (aHR: 2.57; 95% CI: 1.21–5.49) were independently associated with the risk of death in heart failure patients.ConclusionAcute heart failure leads to high mortality, mainly due to cardiogenic shock and factors of multiple organ failure.Keywords: Acute heart failure, Mortality, Survival, Predictors

Introduction

Despite the progress made in recent years in the treatment of Acute Heart Failure (AHF), its prognosis remains poor, with a mortality of around 50% at 4 years in non-severe forms, and beyond 50%. at 1 year in severe forms [1]. In hospitalized recruitment, the one-year mortality rate is much more variable, ranging from 14 to 36%. However, the numbers are on average quite similar, and show that AHF is as serious as many cancers [2, 3]. According to the European Society of Cardiology, AHF is defined as the presence of symptoms at rest or during exercise including dyspnea, lower limb edema, jugular vein turgor, hepatomegaly with hepato-jugular reflux, associated with objective evidence (preferably echocardiography) of systolic and/or diastolic cardiac dysfunction (at rest) and, in case of diagnostic doubt, associated with a favorable response to the usual treatment of AHF [4]. Africa, AHF constitutes the main circumstance for the discovery of cardiovascular diseases. The prevalence of AHF is more or less well known in the West, but in Africa it remains less well known despite the epidemiological transition leading to the emergence of cardiovascular diseases. Data from hospitalization statistics from several English-speaking African countries show that acute HF represents 3 to 7% of causes of admission [5]. Also a study carried out in 7 French-speaking African countries in 2006 reported a proportion of 27.5% of patients hospitalized urgently for acute HF [6]. There is certainly an underestimation of the prevalence of AHF in the African environment because the countries do not have a national registry system dedicated to AHF, much less sentinel networks of stakeholders in this field. And most of the studies published on this subject are hospital series [7, 8].

In the Democratic Republic of Congo (DRC), the study conducted by Mupinsie in 2005 in Kinshasa found intra-hospital mortality at 18.9%, Kibikiabo in 2013 in Lubumbashi found hospital prevalence at 6% with intra-hospital mortality of 18% and that of %, Ntima in 2015 in Kinshasa an intra-hospital mortality of 42.8% (unpublished data). Despite the high frequency of mortality linked to heart failure, predictive factors have not been found. The objective of this study is to analyze the survival and predictors of mortality of patients with acute heart failure in the cardiology department of the Basse Terre Hospital Center in Guadeloupe.

Patients and methods

Study population and design

This is a historical cohort study extended from June 2021 to June 2022. Any patient aged 18 or over, hospitalized in the Center Hospitalier Basse Terre (CHBT) cardiology department for ICA during the study period. This is an exhaustive sampling of all patients meeting our inclusion criteria.

Inclusion criteria: hospitalized patient aged 18 or over, hospitalized in the CHBT cardiology department for ICA.

Inclusion criteria: hospitalized patient aged 18 or over, hospitalized in the CHBT cardiology department for ICA.

Exclusion criteria: any patient with acute heart failure with an unusable file, patient whose vital outcome is not known.

Exclusion criteria: any patient with acute heart failure with an unusable file, patient whose vital outcome is not known.

Data collection and procedure

The data was collected using an Excel file from elements of patient files. The parameters of interest of the study were : sociodemographic, clinical, paraclinical, therapeutic characteristics and Patient outcome: favorable or unfavorable evolution during hospitalization (death, cardiogenic shock).

Statistical analysis

The information collected was encoded on an Excel database (Microsoft, corporation, USA, 2013). Then, they were imported into the Data Analysis module of SPSS for Windows version 25 software for their analysis. The results obtained were expressed as absolute frequencies (n) and relative frequencies (%), as well as central measurements (mean and median) and dispersion (standard deviation, minimum, maximum, interquartiles). Normality of distributions was assessed with the Kolmogorov-Smirnov test. Comparison of means was made using Student’s t test. In the absence of normality of the distributions, the comparison of the medians was carried out using the Mann-Whitney U test. Comparison of proportions was made using Pearson’s chi-square test or Fisher’s exact test for numbers < 5. Patient survival was studied using Kaplan Meier curves; the comparison of Kaplan Meier curves was carried out using the Logrank test. The Cox proportional hazard regression model was used to investigate the predictors of mortality. The step-by-step method was used in the search for prediction factors. Only variables that were statistically significant in univariate analysis (p < 0.05) were introduced into the final model. The Hazard Ratio (HR) and adjusted HR (aHR) were determined to assess the risk of association between independent variables and mortality. For all tests used, the p value < 0.05 was considered as the threshold for statistical significance.

Results

During the study period there were a total of 1159 hospitalizations in the cardiology department, including 242 for acute heart failure, representing a frequency of 21% of cases of AHF.

Mortality rate

Regarding the evolution or outcome of patients hospitalized for acute heart failure, this series found a mortality of 14.9% (Fig. 1) and 175 patients were rehospitalized during the year, a rehospitalization rate by 72% in the year. Among the 175 patients readmitted, 75, or 42%, were readmitted in the month following their first hospitalization and 60, or 34%, had at least 2 hospitalizations during the year.

Survival study

The mean and median survival times of patients with heart failure were, respectively, 9.5 and 8 [5–13] days. This survival varied depending on the hospitalization stay. At two, five, eight and 14 days, it was, respectively, 96.7%, 92.6%, 89.8% and 86.8%; it increased to 85.1% on the 21st day and remained at this value until the 41st day of hospitalization of the last patient (Fig. 2A). In the same figure (Fig. 2B), we note that survival was significantly (p = 0.002) shorter in patients with low cardiac output. This survival was also significantly shorter in patients with mitral insufficiency (p = 0.023; Fig. 2C) and in patients with NTproBNP > 10,000pig/ml (p = 0.001, Fig. 2D).

In univariate analysis, stay in the SICU, presence of CKD, tobacco consumption, presence of infection, dyspnea classified NYHA 4, low blood flow, hyponatremia, mitral insufficiency and NTproBNP > 10000ng/ml were the predictors of mortality in patients with heart failure.

The multivariate analysis, after adjustment, shows that tobacco consumption [(aHR: 2.90; 95% CI: 1.36–8.09); p = 0.043], MRC [(aHR: 2.52; 95% CI: 1.22–5.20); p = 0.013], infection [(aHR: 2.14; 95% CI: 1.99–4.58); p = 0.015], hyperkalemia [(aHR: 1.90; 95% CI: 1.10–2.86); p < 0.001], mitral regurgitation [(aHR: 3.04; 95% CI: 1.98–9.47); p = 0.005] and NTproBNP > 10000ng/ml [(aHR: 2.57; 95% CI: 1.21–5.49); p = 0.014] were independently and significantly associated with the risk of death in patients with heart failure (Table 4).

Discussion

Acute heart failure remains a syndrome with high mortality and a severe prognosis; this requires numerous procedures for the management of this disease. In the present series of cases the death rate is 14.8%, which still remains high despite the overall decline in mortality due to heart failure. According to data from the National Heart, Lung and Blood Institute, the death rates at one month, 1 year and five years after hospitalization for heart failure were 10.4%, 22%, and 42.3% respectively [14–16]. The present study showed that the high level of NT-pro BNP was a poor prognostic factor for the occurrence of death in heart failure. Indeed, several studies have also shown that the increase in NT-pro BNP is a major negative factor [17, 18]. A meta-analysis including 19 studies shows that an increase of 100 ng/l in BNP corresponds to an increase of 35% of the relative risk of mortality, suggesting that its dosage is a good prognostic marker [19]. Indeed, the 2016 ESC recommendations award grade IIa to the carrying out of dosages of these natriuretic peptides to exclude alternative causes of dyspnea and obtain information on the prognosis of heart failure [11] Among the determining factors of death that emerged in the present study is the presence of CKD. Its presence associated with the ICA multiplies by 2 the risk of death. In several publications, renal failure is established as a reported poor prognosis factor [20]. The increase in creatinine or the decrease in glomerular filtration rate is found in several studies as a major negative factor in heart failure [21–23].

The present study highlighted the presence of infection or sepsis as poor prognosis factors on the survival of patients hospitalized for heart failure. Infected patients had a shorter survival curve compared to those who were not infected. Other studies have not demonstrated this factor as a determining factor in death. Cardiogenic shock was the main cause of death in our series. Sepsis, with its detrimental effect on hemodynamics, particularly by lowering blood pressure, can increase the risk of shock and low flow in the HF.

Furthermore, a lowered systolic BP is intrinsically a bad sign, but also a low BP limits the possibilities of prescribing treatment for HF such as beta-blockers and ACE inhibitors. On the other hand, it is often associated with impaired kidney function. Several studies show that high systolic blood pressure has a major and sometimes linear beneficial prognostic impact on short-term, medium-term and longer-term survival [24–27].

Among the factors associated with poor prognosis in the present study we note that tobacco consumption was associated with shorter survival when comparing those who had smoking as a cardiovascular risk factor and those who did not. The presence of this risk factor doubled the risk of dying from AHF. However, studies that have studied the prognosis of risk factors on ICA have not shown the unfavorable effect of smoking on ICA [28].

Hypokalemia in the present study was found to be a factor associated with an unfavorable outcome in heart failure patients. Effect the presence of hypokalemia doubled the risk of death in heart failure patients. The treatments prescribed in the ICA have the power to modify serum potassium either in the direction of hypokalemia or hyperkalemia. There are studies that have shown the existence of a U-shaped relationship between serum potassium and mortality in AHF. A study conducted in Denmark showed an adjusted hazard ratio of 3.16 for subjects who had hypokalemia [29]. Ahmed and his colleagues also demonstrated in their study that hypokalemia increased the risk of cardiovascular mortality, particularly sudden death due to ventricular arrhythmia [30].

Heart failure patients with mitral regurgitation etiology had a shorter survival time compared to those without MI. The presence of MI increased the risk of dying from AHF by 3. A meta-analysis demonstrated that the presence of even secondary MI worsens the prognosis of heart failure [31, 32].

This study has some limitations. These limitations are linked firstly to the data collection technique used which was a retrospective documentary review. This technique leads to several biases namely the information bias due to the non-standardization in the measurement of variables as well as the reliability of certain measurements. This technique also exposes to selection bias because there are patient files which sometimes lack data on certain variables of interest.

Conclusion

Acute heart failure is more common in hospitals and leads to more deaths. The complications it causes are predictive factors of this mortality. The correct management of acute heart failure and its complications can further reduce the mortality of these patients. In clinical practice, in developing countries, the etiologies of acute heart failure must be taken into account because they are the basis of death of patients with heart failure. It would be important upon admission of patients to properly verify these etiologies and to better manage these etiologies to reduce the death rate of patients.
