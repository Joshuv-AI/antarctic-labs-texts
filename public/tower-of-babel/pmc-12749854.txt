Structural and functional brain alterations associated with pain in Parkinson’s disease
Authors: Erlei Wang, Yujing Jia, Pubing Yuan, Jin Shi, Yiqing Bao, Yang Ya, Junkang Shen, Aijun Wang, Chengjie Mao, Yuanchao Zhang, Guohua Fan
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractPain is a common non-motor symptom in Parkinson’s disease (PD), but its neural basis remains unclear. This study included 65 PD patients with pain (PDP), 55 without pain (nPDP), and 35 healthy controls (HC), who underwent structural, functional, and diffusion MRI. Both PD groups showed reduced local gyrification index (LGI) in the left anterior insula (aINS) and decreased degree centrality (DC) in the left superior temporal gyrus/temporoparietal junction (STG/TPJ), with greater reductions in PDP. Fractional anisotropy (FA) was reduced in the ventral tegmental area/substantia nigra and bilateral caudate in both PD groups. Functional connectivity between the aINS and STG/TPJ was also reduced. In PDP patients, LGI and FA correlated negatively with pain intensity and positively with each other. These findings may enhance our understanding of the neural mechanisms underlying pain in PD and offer mechanistic insights that could inform the development of more targeted interventions for pain management in this population.Subject terms: Parkinson's disease, Neurological manifestations

Introduction

Parkinson’s disease (PD) is a neurodegenerative disorder primarily associated with motor deficits, but it also often involves sensory abnormalities, including pain, throughout its progression1. Epidemiological studies suggest that up to two-thirds of PD patients experience painful sensations—such as aching, cramping, coolness, numbness, burning, or tingling—which significantly impairs quality of life2,3. These pain syndromes are often considered secondary to motor impairments and may improve with dopaminergic therapy; however, some may precede motor onset or respond poorly to medication, suggesting a multifactorial pathogenesis of pain in PD4,5. Despite its high prevalence and clinical relevance, the pathophysiological mechanisms underlying pain in PD remain insufficiently understood2.

Recent neuroimaging studies have increasingly implicated dopaminergic dysfunction in the pathophysiology of pain in PD. For instance, dopaminergic denervation in subcortical regions such as the caudate nucleus, as well as in cortical areas including the insula and posterior cingulate cortex, has been associated with musculoskeletal pain and altered heat pain thresholds in PD patients6,7. Levodopa administration is found to attenuate pain-induced activation in several nociceptive brain regions, including the insula, prefrontal cortex, and anterior cingulate cortex, in both PD patients with and without pain8,9. In addition, cortical thinning and disrupted functional connectivity within dopaminergic regions or networks, such as the dorsolateral prefrontal cortex, nucleus accumbens, anterior insula and basal ganglia network, have been associated with pain in PD10–12. Beyond dopaminergic abnormalities, several studies have highlighted the potential involvement of non-dopaminergic neurotransmitter systems, particularly the noradrenergic and serotonergic pathways, in the modulation of pain in PD13–15. Taken together, these findings suggest that pain in PD may arise from complex and distributed dysfunctions across multiple neuromodulatory systems and brain regions.

Cortical gyrification, reflecting the complexity of cortical folding shaped by neural connectivity16, has been increasingly investigated in PD. Reductions in the local gyrification index (LGI) have been linked to PD progression and to a broad spectrum of motor and non-motor symptoms—including the akinetic-rigid subtype, depression, and impulse control disorders—suggesting that LGI may serve as a sensitive imaging marker for detecting subtle pathological changes and differentiating clinical phenotypes in PD17–20. Notably, LGI reductions frequently co-occur with compromised white matter integrity, as indexed by reduced fractional anisotropy (FA), highlighting a close coupling between cortical morphology and subcortical structural connectivity17,18. Degree centrality (DC), a voxel-wise graph-theoretical measure, quantifies the strength of functional connectivity by assessing the correlation of each voxel with all others across the brain21. Reduced DC has been observed in PD patients with freezing of gait, mild cognitive impairment, and fatigue, indicating disrupted network integration in these individuals22–24. Although LGI, FA, and DC are derived from distinct imaging modalities—structural, diffusion, and functional respectively—they converge in capturing different yet complementary aspects of brain network connectivity. Given the distributed and multifactorial nature of pain processing, an integrative multimodal approach incorporating LGI, FA, and DC may offer a more comprehensive understanding of the neural substrates underlying pain in PD.

In this study, we employed a multimodal MRI approach to investigate alterations in LGI, FA, and DC in PD patients with pain (PDP), compared to PD patients without pain (nPDP) and healthy controls (HC). Specifically, whole-brain analysis of covariance (ANCOVA) was performed for each imaging metric to assess group differences, controlling for age and sex. Brain regions showing significant group differences in each metric were subsequently extracted and defined as a region of interest (ROI). ROI-to-ROI functional connectivity and cross-modal correlation analyses were then performed to examine the functional interactions among regions and the interrelationships among imaging metrics, respectively. Based on previous findings, we hypothesized that PD patients with pain would show (1) reduced cortical gyrification in regions implicated in pain processing, including the insula, prefrontal cortex, and anterior cingulate cortex; (2) impaired functional integration within pain-related networks, such as basal ganglia and salience network; and (3) compromised microstructural integrity within the dopaminergic pathways.

LGI analysis

Vertex-wise ANCOVA revealed significant differences in LGI across groups within the left anterior insula (aINS) (cluster size = 5275 vertices, peak Talairach coordinates: x = –30.81, y = 22.96, z = 8.54, peak F value = 9.157). Subsequent post hoc t-tests showed that both the PDP group (3.876 ± 0.304) and the nPDP group (3.995 ± 0.257) had significantly reduced LGI compared to HC (4.131 ± 0.291) (PDP vs HC: t = 4.064, false discovery rate (FDR)-corrected p < 0.001; nPDP vs HC: t = 2.314, FDR-corrected p = 0.023), and the PDP group also exhibited significantly lower LGI than the nPDP group (t = 2.306, FDR-corrected p = 0.023) (Fig. 1). DC analysis

Voxel-wise ANCOVA revealed significant differences in DC across groups within the left superior temporal gyrus/temporoparietal junction (STG/TPJ) (cluster size = 135 vertices, peak MNI coordinates: x = –48, y = –45, z = 27, peak F value = 9.851). Subsequent post hoc t-tests showed that both the PDP group (0.006686 ± 0.2195) and the nPDP group (0.2064 ± 0.2325) had significantly reduced DC compared to HC (0.3529 ± 0.2453) (PDP vs HC: t = 5.964, FDR-corrected p ＝ 0.003; nPDP vs HC: t = 2.853, FDR-corrected p = 0.005), and the PDP group also exhibited significantly lower DC than the nPDP group (t = 3.378, FDR-corrected p = 0.002) (Fig. 2). FA analysis

Voxel-wise ANCOVA revealed significant differences in FA across groups in the ventral tegmental area/substantia nigra (VTA/SN) (cluster size = 361 vertices, peak MNI coordinates: x = 0, y = –16, z = –10, peak F value = 19.512), as well as in the bilateral caudate nucleus (right: cluster size = 319 vertices, peak MNI coordinates: x = 12, y = –6, z = 12, peak F value = 11.805; left: cluster size = 151 vertices, peak MNI coordinates: x = –18, y = –18, z = 24, peak F value = 11.562). Subsequent post hoc t-tests showed that both the PDP group (0.3215 ± 0.0174) and the nPDP group (0.3264 ± 0.0215) had significantly reduced mean FA compared to HC (0.3438 ± 0.0185) (PDP vs HC: t = 5.768, FDR-corrected p < 0.001; nPDP vs HC: t = 3.822, FDR-corrected p < 0.001), while no significant difference was found between the PDP and nPDP groups (t = 1.373, FDR-corrected p ＞ 0.05) (Fig. 3). The average FA maps for the three groups are presented in Fig. S3 of the Supplementary Materials.

ROI-to-ROI functional connectivity analysis

Functional connectivity analysis between the aINS and STG/TPJ revealed significantly reduced connectivity in PDP (0.3346 ± 0.1828) and nPDP (0.3523 ± 0.1219) compared to HC (0.4543 ± 0.1663) (PDP vs HC: t = 3.222, FDR-corrected p = 0.0017; nPDP vs HC: t = 3.354, FDR-corrected p = 0.0012), with no significant difference between the two patient groups (t = 0.6119, FDR-corrected p ＞ 0.05) (Fig. 4A). No other significant differences in functional connectivity were found between regions showing group differences in LGI, DC, or FA.

Associations between imaging measures and clinical variables

A significant positive correlation between LGI and FA was observed in PDP patients (r = 0.4508, p = 0.0002), whereas no significant correlation was identified in nPDP patients (r = 0.2644, p = 0.0510). In PDP patients, both LGI and FA were significantly negatively correlated with Visual Analogue Scale (VAS) scores (r = −0.2539, p = 0.0413; r = −0.2684, p = 0.00349, respectively) (Fig. 4B–D). No significant correlations involving DC were detected in any analysis.

Discussion

This study used a multimodal MRI approach to investigate alterations in LGI, DC, and FA, as well as their interrelationships in PDP patients compared to nPDP patients and HC. The main findings are summarized as follows. First, both PDP and nPDP patients showed significantly reduced LGI in the left aINS and decreased DC in the left STG/TPJ compared to HC, with PDP patients exhibiting greater reductions than nPDP in both measures. Second, both nPDP and PDP patients showed lower FA in the VTA/SN and bilateral caudate nucleus compared to HC, with no significant FA differences observed between the two patient groups. Third, functional connectivity between the aINS and STG/TPJ was diminished in both PDP and nPDP compared to HC, with no significant difference between patient groups. Interestingly, in PDP patients, a significant positive correlation was observed between LGI and FA, while both of them were negatively correlated with pain intensity (VAS scores). These findings may provide valuable insights into the pathophysiological mechanisms of pain in PD.

Both PDP and nPDP patients exhibited reduced LGI in the aINS compared to HC. This observation is consistent with our previous findings of significant LGI reductions across multiple brain regions, including the insular cortex, in both longitudinal and cross-sectional PD datasets17, emphasizing the critical role of this region in the pathophysiology of PD25. Notably, PDP patients showed more pronounced LGI reductions in the aINS compared to nPDP patients, and these reductions were negatively correlated with VAS scores, indicating a potential involvement of the aINS in the subjective experience of pain in PD. These findings are consistent with recent research showing that both reduced functional connectivity and pain-induced activation of the insula are correlated with VAS scores in PD patients with and without pain8,11. Beyond PD, structural atrophy in the aINS has been frequently reported in various chronic pain populations, often in association with increased pain intensity26–28. As a key region of the medial pain pathway, the aINS plays a crucial role in processing the cognitive-emotional dimensions of pain29. Furthermore, the aINS is also a core node of the salience network, responsible for salience attribution and the integration of emotional, interoceptive, and cognitive responses to behaviorally relevant stimuli30,31. Hence, reduced gyrification in the aINS may reflect impaired cognitive-emotional pain processing and disrupted salience network integration, thereby contributing to aberrant pain perception in PD.

Compared to HC, both PDP and nPDP groups showed lower DC in the left STG/TPJ, with PDP patients exhibiting more prominent reductions than nPDP patients. These findings are in line with prior studies frequently reporting reduced DC in PD patients experiencing symptoms such as freezing of gait, mild cognitive impairment, and fatigue22–24. Extending this evidence, our results suggest that altered DC in the left STG/TPJ may play a role in the pathophysiology of pain in PD. Specifically, reduced DC in the STG corresponds well with previous findings of decreased pain-induced activation in this region among PD patients without pain, which suggests a potential role of the STG in processing pain-related unpleasantness32. Supporting this, a virtual lesion study using transcranial magnetic stimulation demonstrated that disrupting STG activity reduces the exaggeration of pain-related unpleasant memories, providing causal evidence for its involvement in emotional encoding of pain memory33. The involvement of the TPJ in PDP is consistent with prior reports of reduced pain-related activation and decreased gyrification in the supramarginal gyrus—a core constituent part of the TPJ—in individuals with headache34,35. Moreover, gray matter alterations in this region have also been linked to impaired cognitive control of pain in patients with chronic low back pain36. As a hub of the salience network, the TPJ plays a critical role in integrating salient sensory input with contextual information to support pain processing37,38. Considering its extensive neural connectivity with large-scale networks—including the salience, executive control, and default mode networks—reduced DC in this region may reflect disrupted dynamic interactions among these systems, resulting in atypical pain processing in PDP38.

Compared to HC, both PDP and nPDP groups exhibited significantly reduced FA in the VTA/SN and bilateral caudate nucleus. These findings are consistent with previous reports of compromised white matter integrity in these regions17,39,40, likely reflecting the characteristic pathological involvement of the nigrostriatal and mesocorticolimbic dopaminergic systems in PD41,42. More interestingly, FA reductions in these regions are linked to higher VAS scores in the PDP group, potentially reflecting the involvement of dopaminergic pathway integrity—particularly within the VTA/SN and bilateral caudate nucleus—in pain perception. This result aligns with a previous study indicating a significant association between caudate dopaminergic dysfunction and musculoskeletal pain severity in PD6. Furthermore, reduced dopamine metabolism, volume and functional connectivity of the VTA and SN have also been frequently reported in other chronic pain conditions43–45. As the primary origins of the dopamine system, the VTA and SN send axonal projections to the striatum—including the caudate nucleus—as well as to multiple cortical regions, thereby facilitating the top-down cognitive and affective modulation of pain46,47. Hence, our findings reinforce the critical role of dopaminergic microstructural integrity in the pathophysiology of pain in PD. Additionally, no significant differences in FA were found between the two patient groups, which is consistent with a previous study in PD patients with persistent pain10. This finding, along with the significant FA reductions observed in both PD groups relative to HC, likely reflects a floor effect, whereby subtle pain-related alterations are masked by the more prominent neurodegenerative changes associated with PD. However, the exact neural mechanisms underlying these findings remain unclear and warrant further investigation.

In this study, we employed three distinct neuroimaging measures—LGI, DC, and FA—to comprehensively examine both structural and functional alterations associated with pain in PD. Although all three metrics reflect neuronal connectivity, we did not observe overlapping alterations across modalities. This may be attributed to the fact that these measures capture different neurobiological properties16,17,21. Nevertheless, each modality offered complementary insights, collectively highlighting pain-related abnormalities within the salience network, including the aINS, TPJ, and VTA/SN48. Furthermore, to explore the functional interactions among regions and the interrelationships among imaging metrics, ROI-to-ROI functional connectivity and cross-modal correlation analyses were performed, respectively. Specifically, ROI-to-ROI functional connectivity analysis revealed reduced functional connectivity between the left aINS and the left STG/TPJ in both PDP and nPDP groups compared to HC, with no significant differences observed between the two PD subgroups. These results likely suggest a major effect of PD pathologies, yet a subtle effect of pain on the functional connectivity between aINS and STG/TPJ. In addition, cross-modal correlation analysis revealed a positive correlation between LGI in the aINS and FA in the VTA/SN and caudate nucleus in the PDP group. This result is consistent with our previous longitudinal study in PD, which showed significant correlations between LGI and FA across three time points17. These findings demonstrate a robust interrelationship between these two features, in keeping with Van Essen’s mechanical tension model of cortical folding49. Given the dense dopaminergic innervation of the aINS—particularly from the VTA—and its critical role in pain processing via dopaminergic receptors50, our finding may suggest a mechanistic pathway whereby disrupted dopaminergic circuit integrity and insular gyrification reductions act in concert to influence pain processing in PDP. However, we cannot rule out the possibility that non-dopaminergic neurotransmitter systems may also be involved, as the aINS is additionally innervated by other neuromodulatory pathways, including noradrenergic, serotonergic, and glutamatergic inputs13,51,52.

Our study has several limitations. First, the assessment of pain in our study was limited to a unidimensional measure, the VAS, which primarily captures the intensity of pain53. This approach does not address important dimensions such as pain subtypes, duration, or multidimensional characteristics, all of which are particularly relevant in PD. Future studies incorporating comprehensive, multidimensional tools—such as the King’s Parkinson’s Disease Pain Scale—may offer a more nuanced understanding of pain in PD and enable more precise correlations with structural and functional brain alterations54. Second, the interpretation of our findings—particularly regarding the role of the aINS in pain processing—should be taken with caution. Given the high prevalence of depression and anxiety in PD and the critical role of this region in salience and affective processing, the observed cortical gyrification changes may, to some extent, have an emotional component in addition to pain-related mechanisms. Future studies incorporating standardized affective assessments as covariates will be important to better differentiate pain-specific neural correlates from broader emotional effects. Third, the majority of patients in both the PD and nPDP groups were receiving dopaminergic medications, such as levodopa, pramipexole, and rasagiline. Although the two PD subgroups were matched for levodopa equivalent daily dose, the potential effects of dopaminergic therapy on pain perception and functional connectivity cannot be completely ruled out. Future research focusing on drug-naïve, newly diagnosed PD patients with pain would help minimize medication-related confounding and better characterize the neural mechanisms underlying pain in PD. Finally, due to the cross-sectional design of this study, we were unable to characterize the dynamic trajectories of structural and functional brain alterations associated with pain in PD. Longitudinal studies are needed to further investigate these evolving alterations.

In summary, this study identified significant structural and functional brain alterations associated with pain in PD. In particular, reduced gyrification in the aINS and compromised microstructural integrity in the dopaminergic pathways were linked to pain intensity in PDP patients. These findings may enhance our understanding of the neural mechanisms underlying pain in PD and suggest potential mechanistic pathways that could inform the development of more targeted interventions for pain management in this population.

Methods

Participants

This study included 65 PDP, 55 PD nPDP and 35 HC, all recruited at the Second Affiliated Hospital of Soochow University between September 2021 and May 2023. The clinical diagnosis of PD was made by an experienced movement disorder specialist according to the diagnostic criteria of the International Parkinson and Movement Disorder Society55. Exclusion criteria included atypical Parkinsonian syndromes, severe psychiatric or neurological comorbidities, a history of head trauma or stroke, dementia, and contraindications for MRI. PDP patients were identified based on the International Association for the Study of Pain criteria for chronic pain, defined as unexplained somatic pain lasting longer than three months56. Both nPDP patients and HC were required to be pain-free at enrollment. Additionally, HC participants had no history of neurological or neuropsychiatric disorders. Pain intensity was assessed only in PDP patients using the VAS. Specifically, participants were asked to mark a point on a 100-mm horizontal line representing their average pain intensity over the past seven days, where 0 indicated “no pain” and 10 indicated “the worst imaginable pain“53. Disease severity and motor impairment in PD patients were assessed using the H&Y staging scale57 and the motor section of the UPDRS-III58. Global cognitive function was assessed using the MMSE59.

The study was approved by the Medical Ethics Committee of the Second Affiliated Hospital of Soochow University. Written informed consent was obtained from all participants. All procedures were conducted in accordance with the ethical standards of the institutional review board and the 1964 Declaration of Helsinki, as well as its subsequent amendments.

MRI data acquisition

MRI images were acquired using a 3.0 T Siemens Prisma scanner (Siemens, Prisma, Germany) with a 64-channel head and neck coil. Earplugs and foam pads were used to reduce scanner noise and head movement, respectively. The MRI protocols were as follows: T1-weighted MRI data were acquired using a magnetization-prepared rapid-acquisition gradient echo sequence with the following parameters: repetition time (TR) = 2300 ms; echo time (TE) = 2.34 ms; inversion time = 900 ms; flip angle = 8°; field of view (FOV) = 256 × 256 mm²; matrix size = 256 × 256; slice thickness = 1.0 mm. Diffusion Tensor Imaging (DTI) data were acquired using a half-coverage Cartesian q-space grid sampling procedure with a radial grid size of 4128 diffusion-weighted images, and 18 different b values (0 to 3000 s/mm²). Acquisition parameters were as follows: TR = 3900 ms; TE = 72 ms; flip angle = 90°; FOV = 220 × 220 mm²; matrix size = 110 × 110; slice thickness = 2.0 mm. Resting-state fMRI data were acquired using a multiband echo-planar imaging sequence with the following parameters: TR = 1240 ms; TE = 32 ms; flip angle = 67°; FOV = 215 × 215 mm²; matrix size = 86 × 86; slice thickness = 2.5 mm; 300 time points were collected.

LGI analysis

T1-weighted MRI data were preprocessed with the FreeSurfer package following standard procedures17,18. Initially, non-uniform intensity normalization, skull stripping, affine registration to standard space, and segmentation of gray/white matter tissue were applied. Next, the pial surface was reconstructed based on the gray/white matter boundary for each cortical hemisphere. Finally, the LGI map was derived by measuring the ratio of the area of a circular region on the pial surface to that of a corresponding region on the triangulated convex hull of the pial surface. Prior to statistical analysis, individual LGI maps were resampled to the “fsaverage” template provided by FreeSurfer and further smoothed using a Gaussian kernel with a full width at half maximum (FWHM) of 15 mm.

DC analysis

Resting-state fMRI data were preprocessed using the Data Processing & Analysis of Brain Imaging (DPABI)60,61 (http://rfmri.org/dpabi) on MATLAB R2018b (MathWorks) as follows: (1) removing the first 10 time points; (2) slice-timing correction; (3) realignment of head motion; (4) co-registration of individual T1-weighted images to the mean functional image; (5) gray and white matter segmentation using DARTEL and spatial normalization of the T1 images to the Montreal Neurological Institute (MNI) space; (6) spatial normalization of resting-state fMRI images to the MNI space; (7) band-pass filtering (0.01–0.08 Hz); and (8) regression of covariates, including 24 head motion parameters and signals from brain white matter and cerebrospinal fluid. Participants with head motion translation greater than 2 mm or rotation greater than 2° were excluded from the analysis.

DC, a graph theory-based approach, measures each voxel’s connectivity with all other brain voxels, providing objective, comprehensive insights into functional connectivity changes across the entire brain network21. The DC is calculated in three main steps. First, a Pearson’s correlation coefficient matrix is computed by assessing the temporal correlation between a given voxel and all other voxels within the gray matter mask. Second, a threshold of 0.25 for Pearson’s correlation coefficient is applied to compute the weighted sum of positive correlations22–24. This threshold was adopted based on prior DC studies to exclude voxels exhibiting low temporal correlations due to signal noise21. Third, the voxel-wise DC map is transformed into a z-score map for normalization and then spatially smoothed using a 6 mm FWHM Gaussian kernel.

FA analysis

DTI data were processed using the FMRIB’s Software Library (FSL; https://fsl.fmrib.ox.ac.uk/fsl/)62. Briefly, data were corrected for head motion and distortions induced by eddy currents using the eddy function in FSL, with non-brain structures removed from the eddy-corrected images using the brain extraction tool. Subsequently, the FA map was generated by fitting a diffusion tensor model to each voxel using the DTIFIT tool. Finally, the resulting FA map was aligned to the MNI space through nonlinear registration and smoothed with a Gaussian kernel (FWHM = 8 mm).

ROI-to-ROI functional connectivity analysis

To investigate functional interactions among brain regions exhibiting significant group differences in LGI, DC, and FA, we performed ROI-to-ROI functional connectivity analysis. Specifically, brain regions showing significant group differences in the ANCOVA for each metric were defined as a ROI. The mean time series of each ROI was then extracted from the preprocessed resting-state fMRI data. Pairwise Pearson correlation coefficients were then computed between the time series of all ROI pairs. The resulting r values were Fisher z-transformed to normalize the distribution prior to statistical analysis.

Statistical analysis

Demographic and clinical variables among the PDP, nPDP, and HC groups were compared using SPSS 22.0 (SPSS Inc., Chicago, IL, USA) with one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA), Chi-square tests, and independent sample t tests. Statistical significance was defined as p < 0.05.

Vertex-wise ANCOVA was conducted on the LGI maps across the three groups using the SurfStat package (math.mcgill.ca/keith/surfstat/), adjusting for age and sex. Clusters of interest were identified with a vertex-wise p < 0.005 and then corrected for multiple comparisons at the cluster level using random field theory (RFT), with the significance level set at RFT-corrected p < 0.05. A similar voxel-wise ANCOVA model was applied using DPABI for both DC and FA maps, with results reported at a height threshold of p < 0.005 for DC and p < 0.001 for FA, and a cluster threshold of p < 0.05, corrected using the Gaussian random field (GRF) method. For significant clusters identified through ANCOVA, their global mean values were extracted, and pairwise comparisons were conducted using independent two-sample t-tests. The results were corrected for multiple comparisons using the FDR approach. For the ROI-to-ROI functional connectivity analysis, pairwise two-sample t-tests were similarly employed to compare connectivity strength among the PDP, nPDP, and HC groups, with FDR correction applied. In addition, Pearson’s correlation coefficients were computed to assess associations between imaging measures and clinical variables, as well as interrelationships among different imaging metrics.
