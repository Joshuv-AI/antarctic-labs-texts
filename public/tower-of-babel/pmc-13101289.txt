Flavor and metabolite responses of Ganoderma lucidum to different drying methods: integrated electronic sensing, volatilomics, and metabolomics analysis
Authors: Yongtao Xia, Fuyuan Liu, Fulin Dong, Tingting Wang, Fuming He, Rui Ni, Ziyu Yang, Chenjing Li, Qing Jing, Qinghong Lu, Ni Guo, Qi Wang, Mingchi Wang, Zhihua Sun, Neng Gao, Li Zhang, Xinyong Guo
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractThis study systematically investigated the effects of four drying methods, freeze vacuum drying (FD), microwave vacuum drying (MVD), hot air drying (HD), and natural drying (ND), on the flavor profile and metabolite composition of Ganoderma lucidum (G. lucidum). FD appears to best preserve natural color, thermolabile flavor compounds, and bioactive constituents, resulting in a flavor profile most similar to that of fresh samples. HD significantly altered the profile of volatile compounds, and the marked increase in 5′-IMP likely contributed to the enhanced umami intensity. Integrated correlation analysis and weighted gene co-expression network analysis suggest that 1-nonen-3-one and 1-octen-3-ol may be key volatiles driving odor differences among treatments. Nitrogen-containing heterocycles and triterpenoids were associated with bitterness, whereas 5′-IMP, specific amino acids and derivatives were the main contributors to umami. These findings demonstrate how drying methods differentially shape G. lucidum flavor through coordinated modulation of volatile and non-volatile metabolite networks.Keywords: Drying methods, Electronic nose, Electronic tongue, Ganoderma lucidum, Non-volatile compounds, Volatile compounds, Weighted gene co-expression network analysis

Introduction

Ganoderma lucidum (G. lucidum) is a macrofungus of significant medicinal and nutritional value, widely recognized in Asian countries, particularly China and Japan, for its rich array of bioactive compounds, including polysaccharides and triterpenoids (Gao et al., 2025). These bioactive components not only demonstrate antioxidant, immunomodulatory, antitumor, and hepatoprotective properties (Galappaththi et al., 2023; Sijia Wu et al., 2024), but also contribute critically to the distinctive flavor profile of G. lucidum. Research on G. lucidum has predominantly emphasized the medicinal properties of its macromolecular bioactive components, with relatively little attention devoted to its flavor. In recent years, G. lucidum has been incorporated into functional foods, beverages, and health products, rendering its flavor an important quality attribute. However, fresh G. lucidum has high water content and vigorous post-harvest metabolism, making it susceptible during storage and transport to microbial contamination, enzymatic browning, and nutrient degradation, thereby compromising its commercial value and edible quality (Yi-jun et al., 2021). Drying is an essential post-harvest processing method for reducing moisture content and extending shelf life, thereby preserving the nutritional and flavor quality of G. lucidum.

Different drying methods, varying in temperature, humidity, and drying rate, directly influence water migration and microstructure, affect the retention of bioactive compounds, and alter the profile and abundance of flavor compounds, thereby modulating sensory quality (Liao et al., 2025). Hot air drying (HD) is widely adopted for its low cost and operational simplicity. While heating facilitates the formation of heterocyclic compounds and contributes to flavor development, prolonged exposure to high temperatures can induce nutrient degradation and substantial alterations in flavor composition (Li et al., 2023); Microwave vacuum drying (MVD) offers short drying time, high dehydration efficiency, and better retention of flavor and nutrient components compared with conventional methods (Li et al., 2024; Zeng et al., 2025). Freeze vacuum drying (FD) effectively preserves the nutritional components, structural integrity, and flavor compounds of mushroom, but its high energy consumption hinders large-scale application (Meng et al., 2026; Zeng et al., 2022); Natural drying (ND) is the most economical drying method, yet its long drying time and low efficiency hinder large-scale application.

Electronic nose (E-nose) and electronic tongue (E-tongue) are emerging sensory tools for rapid, objective, and comprehensive food flavor profiling, overcoming the subjectivity and poor repeatability of conventional sensory evaluation. Gas chromatography-mass spectrometry (GC–MS) and liquid chromatography-mass spectrometry (LC-MS) enable comprehensive qualitative and quantitative metabolite profiling in food samples. These techniques have been widely applied to investigate drying-induced changes in volatile and non-volatile compounds in edible mushrooms. The effects of drying methods on Stropharia rugoso-annulata were evaluated using E-nose and GC–MS. FD yielded products with higher overall nutritional value, whereas ND enhanced volatile aroma compound formation (Jiang et al., 2023). Liao et al. used GC–MS and LC-MS to show that combining MVD with HD shortened Morchella sextelata drying time and increased levels of soluble sugars, 5′-nucleotides, and total free amino acids (Liao et al., 2025). Zhang et al. used GC–MS to compare volatile metabolites in fresh and dried Boletus edulis, identifying 3-(methylthio) propanal, 1-octen-3-one, and pyrazines as key aroma compounds based on relative odor activity values (rOAV) (Zhang et al., 2018). Gao et al. combined sensory evaluation with GC–MS to identify 1-octen-3-ol as the key aroma compound in G. lucidum (Gao et al., 2025).

Analysis of UPLC-MS metabolite data is computationally intensive and time-consuming. Weighted gene co-expression network analysis (WGCNA) is a high-throughput systems biology approach designed to detect coordinated expression or abundance patterns in large-scale molecular profiles. Its core principle involves constructing weighted correlation networks, identifying modules of highly interconnected features that exhibit synchronized quantitative changes, and statistically associating these modules with external phenotypic traits. Therefore, it is applicable not only to transcriptomic data but also to various other omics data types, including metabolomics and flavors. For instance, (Wu et al., 2025) combined UPLC-MS-based metabolomics with E-tongue sensory data to study aging-induced changes in Huangjiu; applying WGCNA, they identified 22 metabolites potentially associated with umami taste and 18 potentially associated with bitterness. Similarly, (Zhang et al., 2025) applied WGCNA to analyze the relationship between metabolites and phenotypes in Huangqin tea subjected to different baking treatments, revealing that flavonoid glycosides and phospholipid derivatives jointly regulate color attributes, while nitrogen-containing compounds and iridoid glycosides mainly control flavor characteristics.

However, the effects of drying methods on G. lucidum flavor and metabolism remain unclear. To determine the most suitable drying method for G. lucidum, we systematically evaluated four drying methods (FD, MVD, HD, and ND) using E-nose and E-tongue for comprehensive sensory profiling. Volatile and non-volatile metabolites were identified and quantified by GC–MS and UPLC-MS. Correlation analysis and WGCNA identified key flavor-associated metabolites in G. lucidum, supporting standardized postharvest processing and quality control.

Materials and methods

Sample treatment

Fresh G. lucidum was harvested in Tacheng Prefecture, Xinjiang, transported at 4 °C to the College of Life Sciences, Shihezi University on the day of harvest, and stored at 4 °C until drying. Samples were sliced to 1 cm thickness, evenly spread, and subjected to one of four drying methods.

Drying conditions and equipment

FD: Samples were frozen at −80 °C for 24 h, then lyophilized in a vacuum freeze dryer (ScientZ-10ND, Scientz Biotechnology Co., Ningbo, China) at −40 °C, 10 Pa, with a cold trap at −70 °C for 24 h.

MVD: Samples were dried in a microwave vacuum dryer (RWBZ-08S, Nanjing Saenuri Drying Equipment Co., Nanjing, China) at 65 °C, 475 W, and 0.08 to 0.09 MPa for 10 min.

HD: Samples were dried in an electric hot air oven (HY-9008, Shanghai Han Yu Technology Co., Shanghai, China) at 55 °C for 6 h.

ND: Samples were air-dried in a ventilated, cool room at 25 ± 1 °C and 50 ± 2% relative humidity for 72 h.

All experiments were conducted under controlled indoor conditions to minimize environmental variability and ensure experimental reproducibility. Upon completion of each treatment, samples were immediately sealed in aluminum-laminated pouches under ambient conditions, flash-frozen in liquid nitrogen, and stored at −80 °C until subsequent analysis.

E-nose determination

Volatile profiles of G. lucidum fruiting bodies were analyzed using a PEN3 E-nose (AIRSENSE Analytics GmbH, Germany), equipped with ten metal-oxide sensors: W1C, W3C, W5C, and W2W (aromatic compounds); W5S (nitrogen oxides); W6S (hydrogen); W1S (methane); W1W (sulfides); W2S (ethanol); and W3S (alkanes).

After homogenization, triplicate samples of fresh and dried G. lucidum (0.5 g and 1.0 g, respectively) were weighed into 15 mL headspace vials, sealed, and equilibrated for ≥30 min prior to analysis.

Instrumental parameters: sampling and sensor cleaning time, 120 s; sensor zeroing time, 5 s; sample preparation time, 5 s; carrier gas flow rate, 300 mL/min; analysis time, 120 s. Data acquired during the analysis period were used for subsequent processing.

E-tongue determination

Taste profiles of G. lucidum fruiting bodies were assessed using an SA402B E-tongue (INSENT Inc., Japan). After homogenization, triplicate samples were prepared: dried samples (15 g) and fresh samples (30 g) were each suspended in 150 mL of ultrapure water at 37 °C, vortexed, and equilibrated for 20 min. The supernatants were filtered and loaded onto the instrument via an autosampler. Taste signals were acquired and processed using the integrated pattern recognition and data acquisition system.

Test procedure: Electrodes were immersed in cleaning solution for 90 s, then sequentially rinsed with two reference solutions (120 s each). Following a 30 s equilibration period, the sample measurement commenced and lasted 30 s. Subsequently, electrodes were rinsed with each reference solution for 3 s and then immersed in a fresh reference solution to assess aftertaste over 30 s.

Volatile metabolomics analysis

Volatile metabolites were extracted and analyzed by Wuhan Metware Biotechnology Co., Ltd. The detailed experimental procedures are described below.

G. lucidum samples were ground to powder under liquid nitrogen. A 500 mg aliquot was immediately transferred to a 20 mL headspace vial (Agilent, Palo Alto, CA, USA) containing saturated NaCl solution to suppress enzymatic activity. The vial was sealed with a screw cap (Agilent) fitted with a PTFE‑silicone septum. For solid-phase microextraction (SPME), vials were incubated at 60 °C for 5 min, followed by exposure of a 120 μm DVB/CAR/PDMS fiber (Agilent) to the headspace at 60 °C for 15 min.

After sampling, volatile organic compounds (VOCs) adsorbed on the fiber were thermally desorbed in the GC inlet (Agilent 8890) at 250 °C for 5 min in splitless mode. Separation and detection were performed using an Agilent 8890 gas chromatograph coupled to a 7000D triple quadrupole mass spectrometer (Agilent), equipped with a 30 m × 0.25 mm × 0.25 μm DB-5MS capillary column (5% phenyl-polydimethylsiloxane). Helium carrier gas was delivered at a constant linear velocity of 1.2 mL/min. The inlet temperature was 250 °C. The oven program was: 40 °C (3.5 min); ramped at 10 °C/min to 100 °C; then at 7 °C/min to 180 °C; finally at 25 °C/min to 280 °C, held for 5 min. Mass spectra were acquired in electron ionization (EI) mode at 70 eV. The quadrupole analyzer, ion source, and transfer line temperatures were 150 °C, 230 °C, and 280 °C, respectively. Analytes were quantified in selected ion monitoring (SIM) mode.

For each compound, one quantitative ion and two or three qualitative ions were selected. During analysis, these ions were monitored in scheduled time windows aligned to the expected retention times of their respective peaks. A compound was identified when its observed retention time matched that of the reference standard within an acceptable tolerance, and all selected ions were detected in the sample mass spectrum after background subtraction.

Non-volatile metabolomics analysis

Non-volatile metabolites were extracted and analyzed by Wuhan Metware Biotechnology Co., Ltd. The detailed experimental procedures are described below.

G. lucidum samples were lyophilized (Scientz-100F), then homogenized to powder using a mixer mill (MM 400, Retsch) at 30 Hz for 90 s. A 50 mg aliquot was weighed on an analytical balance (MS105DU, Mettler Toledo) and suspended in 1200 μL of ice-cold 70% (v/v) methanol-water containing internal standard (≤50 mg internal standard per 50 mg sample). The suspension was vortexed every 30 min (30 s each) for six cycles. After centrifugation at 12,000 rpm for 3 min, the supernatant was collected and filtered through a 0.22 μm nylon membrane into an LC-MS vial for UPLC-MS/MS analysis.

Sample extracts were analyzed using a UPLC-ESI-MS/MS system (UPLC: ExionLC™ AD, SCIEX; MS: AB Sciex 6500+ QTRAP, SCIEX). Chromatographic separation was performed on an Agilent SB-C18 column (2.1 mm × 100 mm, 1.8 μm) at 40 °C. The mobile phase consisted of solvent A (water with 0.1% formic acid, v/v) and solvent B (acetonitrile with 0.1% formic acid, v/v), delivered at a flow rate of 0.35 mL/min. The gradient program was: 95% A / 5% B (0–0.5 min); linear decrease to 5% A / 95% B over 9 min (0.5–9.5 min); hold at 5% A / 95% B for 1 min (9.5–10.5 min); linear increase to 95% A / 5% B over 1.1 min (10.5–11.6 min); hold at 95% A / 5% B for 2.9 min (11.6–14.5 min). Injection volume was 2 μL. Effluent was directed to an electrospray ionization (ESI) QTRAP mass spectrometer operating in scheduled multiple reaction monitoring (sMRM) mode.

Electrospray ionization (ESI) source parameters were: source temperature, 500 °C; ion spray voltage, +5500 V (positive mode) or −4500 V (negative mode); gas I (GS1), gas II (GS2), and curtain gas (CUR), 50, 60, and 25 psi, respectively; collision-activated dissociation (CAD), high. Quantitative analysis was performed using scheduled multiple reaction monitoring (sMRM) on a triple quadrupole mass spectrometer. Nitrogen was used as the collision gas at medium pressure. Declustering potential (DP) and collision energy (CE) for each transition were optimized individually. Metabolite-specific MRM transitions were monitored within predefined retention time windows.

Statistical analysis

All experiments followed standardized protocols and were performed in triplicate using independent biological replicates to ensure data reliability and reproducibility. Statistical analysis was performed using SPSS 26.0 (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA). One-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) followed by Duncan's multiple range test was used to assess significant differences among groups; significance was defined as p < 0.05. All data are presented as mean ± standard deviation (SD). Principal component analysis (PCA), orthogonal partial least squares discriminant analysis (OPLS-DA), Pearson correlation analysis, volcano plot generation, bar chart construction, and radar chart visualization were performed using default software parameters. The relative odor activity value (rOAV) of compound is calculated as rOAVi = Ci/Ti, where Ci denotes the concentration of compound (μg/g) and Ti represents its odor threshold (μg/g). A compound is considered to make a direct sensory contribution to the sample's aroma when its rOAV ≥1. WGCNA was conducted using a signed network topology, with pairwise Pearson correlation coefficients computed as similarity measures. The soft-thresholding power was set to 18, selected to achieve scale-free network topology (R2 ≥ 0.85). Modules were identified via the dynamic tree cut algorithm with a mergeCutHeight of 0.15 and a minimum module size of 30. All analyses were executed on the Metware Cloud platform.

Results and discussion

E-nose analysis

Volatile flavor compounds constitute a key quality indicator for assessing G. lucidum products. Radar chart visualization of sensor response intensities (Fig. 1B) revealed that the responses of sensors W1C, W3C, W6S, W5C, and W3S remained consistently near unity across all treatment groups, indicating low and relatively invariant concentrations of their corresponding volatile compounds in the samples. CK exhibited significantly higher responses than all other treatment groups on sensors W1W, W2W, W2S, and W5S, indicating that the four drying treatments markedly reduced sulfur-containing compounds, alcohols, and nitrogen oxides in G. lucidum. Among the four drying treatments, HD exhibited the highest response on sensor W5S, whereas MVD yielded significantly higher responses than all other groups on sensors W1W and W2W. These findings indicate that MVD better preserves sulfur-containing volatile compounds in G. lucidum, likely attributable to its short-duration heating mechanism, which minimizes thermal degradation and oxidation of flavor compounds (Li, Yi, He, Dong, & Duan, 2024).

Appearance, E-nose, and E-tongue Analysis of Ganoderma lucidum. A: slices—fresh sample (CK), freeze vacuum drying (FD), microwave vacuum drying (MVD), hot air drying (HD), natural drying (ND); B: electronic nose radar plot; C: electronic nose PCA score plot; D: electronic tongue radar plot; E: electronic tongue PCA score plot.

To further elucidate intergroup differences in odor profiles, PCA was applied to the E-nose response data (Fig. 1C). PC1 and PC2 accounted for 94.76% and 4.36% of the total variance, respectively, yielding a cumulative explained variance of 99.12%. In the PCA score plot, the CK group was distinctly separated from all drying-treated groups along PC1, consistent with its unprocessed status and preservation of native volatile compounds unaffected by thermal or physical drying stress. The ND markedly diverging from the FD, MVD, and HD. This separation likely reflects prolonged ambient exposure and gradual moisture loss during natural drying, which may promote oxidative degradation of labile flavor volatiles in G. lucidum (Zhang et al., 2025). In contrast, the MVD and HD exhibited proximal positioning in the score space, suggesting mechanistic parallels in their drying processes and consequently greater similarity in resulting volatile profiles.

E-tongue analysis

Samples of G. lucidum subjected to different drying treatments exhibited distinct taste profiles (Fig. 1D). Relative to the four drying groups, the CK group showed significantly lower sensor responses across bitterness, bitterness aftertaste, umami, and umami aftertaste. Among the dried samples, HD elicited the highest intensities in umami and richness, suggesting its potential to promote the formation or retention of taste-active compounds associated with savory depth and mouthfeel. In contrast, FD yielded the most pronounced responses in bitterness and bitterness aftertaste (b), likely attributable to the selective enrichment and reduced thermal degradation of bitter secondary metabolites under low-temperature vacuum conditions. Responses for MVD and ND remained comparatively moderate across all measured taste dimensions. Notably, sensor responses to sourness, sweetness, and saltiness fell below the instrument's detection limit in all groups, implying minimal influence of drying method on these fundamental taste modalities.

PCA further validated the discriminability of G. lucidum taste profiles across drying treatments (Fig. 1E). PC1 and PC2 accounted for 89.99% and 5.26% of the total variance, respectively, cumulatively explaining 95.25%. PC1 primarily discriminated the CK from all dried samples, confirming that drying induces substantial shifts in the overall taste profile. Partial overlap among the FD, MVD, and ND suggests moderate similarity in their taste signatures, whereas HD was clearly segregated along the direction of PC2, consistent with its uniquely elevated umami and richness intensities. Loadings analysis revealed that bitterness, bitterness aftertaste, umami, umami aftertaste, and saltiness exhibited the highest absolute loadings on PC1; saltiness and bitterness aftertaste dominated PC2. Collectively, these five taste attributes constitute the primary drivers of intergroup discrimination in the E-tongue data.

Changes of volatile flavor components

Pearson correlation analysis revealed strong intra-group correlations among replicate samples subjected to the same treatment, whereas inter-group correlations across different treatment methods were comparatively weak (Fig. S1). To assess both overall metabolic divergence among treatment groups and within-group variability, PCA was performed. The first two principal components (PC1 and PC2) collectively explained 70.46% of the total variance, accounting for 48.08% and 22.38%, respectively, and effectively separated fresh samples from those processed by distinct drying methods (Fig. 2B).

A: circle plot; B: PCA plot; C: classification bar chart; D: rOAV-based odor scatter plot; E: rOAV distribution stacked bar chart.

A total of 748 VOCs were identified from all G. lucidum samples and classified into 15 categories, including 28 acids (3.74%), 96 alcohols (12.83%), 58 aldehydes (7.75%), 16 amines (2.14%), 14 aromatics (1.87%), 122 esters (16.31%), 132 ethers (1.34%), 6 halogenated hydrocarbons (0.8%), 61 heterocyclic compounds (8.16%), 62 hydrocarbons (8.29%), 92 ketones (12.3%), 14 nitrogen compounds (1.87%), 20 phenols (2.67%), 3 sulfur compounds (0.4%), and 146 terpenoids (19.52%) (Fig. 2A, Table S1). Additionally, this study quantified the relative abundances of the aforementioned VOCs in fresh G. lucidum and in samples subjected to four distinct drying treatments. Compared with fresh controls, dried samples exhibited a significant reduction in total VOC content, following the descending order: CK > FD > HD > ND > MVD. Notably, substantial compositional shifts were observed in the VOC profiles between fresh and dried samples; specifically, the concentrations of alcohols, aldehydes, esters, and ketones were significantly lower in all dried treatments (Fig. 2C).

To identify the key VOCs responsible for the characteristic aroma of G. lucidum, this study systematically compared and analyzed metabolite profiles across fresh samples and those subjected to four distinct drying treatments. In pairwise comparisons of CK-FD, CK-MVD, CK-HD, and CK-ND, 351, 341, 485, and 395 differentially accumulated metabolites (DAMs) were identified, respectively (Fig. S2A–D). Notably, the CK-HD group exhibited the greatest number and magnitude of metabolic alterations, suggesting that prolonged high-temperature treatment exerts a pronounced regulatory effect on both the composition and abundance of VOCs in G. lucidum. To elucidate the mechanistic basis underlying the impact of drying treatments on the aroma quality of G. lucidum, this study systematically characterized the flavor-associated profiles of DAMs across pre- and post-drying stages. Radar chart analysis revealed that the DAM-derived aroma profile of G. lucidum was predominantly defined by grassy, fruity, and sweet notes. Integration of DAMs with their sensory flavor annotations demonstrated that, relative to the undried control, all four drying methods induced significant reductions in the levels of metabolites associated with fruity, sweet, and grassy aromas. Based on the flavor wheel framework, a total of 95 DAMs directly associated with the three characteristic aroma categories—fruity, green, and sweet—were identified across the five control groups. Key DAMs contributing to each aroma category were identified as follows: 1-octen-3-ol and 1-Butanol, 3-methyl-, propanoate for fruity notes; 2-octenal, and hexanal for green notes; and 1-octen-3-ol and 3-octen-2-one for sweet notes.

Alcohols are characteristic volatile compounds widely present in mushroom. Notably, 1-octen-3-ol imparts a distinctive mushroom-like aroma and is considered the primary contributor to the characteristic odor of mushrooms (Aisala, Sola, Hopia, Linderborg, & Sandell, 2019). However, octanols exhibit limited stability during the drying process due to their susceptibility to oxidation, enzymatic degradation, and other chemical transformations (Tian, Zhao, Huang, Zeng, & Zheng, 2016). CK exhibited the highest concentration of 1-octen-3-ol among all samples. However, this compound underwent substantial depletion upon drying: residual levels were 158 μg/g (FD), 16 μg/g (MVD), 16 μg/g (HD), and 20 μg/g (ND), corresponding to only 2%, 0.4%, 0.4%, and 0.5% of the total volatile flavor compounds in the respective treatments. Similar result were observed for 1-octanol and 2-octanol. These results indicate that extended drying duration (ND) and elevated thermal exposure (HD) promote volatilization, oxidative degradation, and structural transformation of octanols (Wang et al., 2023). In contrast, FD circumvents both high temperature and prolonged processing time. Under its low-temperature, low-pressure conditions, water sublimes directly from the frozen matrix, thereby minimizing thermal and mechanical stress on cellular architecture; this preservation of tissue integrity facilitates superior retention of thermolabile octanol compounds (Polat, Guclu, Kelebek, Keskin, & Selli, 2022).

Aldehydes are generated via the oxidation of unsaturated fatty acids and constitute key contributors to the characteristic aroma profile of mushrooms. The CK exhibited the highest aldehyde content (1886 μg/g), followed by the MVD (747 μg/g), HD (678 μg/g), ND (634 μg/g), and FD (544 μg/g). Among the four dried G. lucidum samples, benzaldehyde, 4-methyl- and 2-hexenal were the most abundant aldehydes, and both contribute significantly to the characteristic grassy, sweet, and bitter sensory attributes of G. lucidum. Notably, benzaldehyde retention was highest in the HD, while 2-hexenal was best preserved in the MVD, phenomena potentially attributable to Strecker degradation of amino acids under elevated thermal conditions (HD) and microwave-induced non-thermal effects (MVD) (Delgado, Hidalgo, & Zamora, 2016). These findings collectively account for the comparatively higher total aldehyde content observed in the MVD and HD treatments relative to the other drying methods.

Ketones are key contributors to the characteristic aroma of mushrooms. Quantitative analysis revealed a marked reduction in the relative abundance of several ketones—including 2,3-octanedione, 1-nonen-3-one, and 2-octanone—following drying of G. lucidum. In contrast, 1-octen-3-one, and 3-octen-2-one—exhibited significant post-drying accumulation. These compounds are primarily generated via amino acid degradation, oxidation of unsaturated fatty acids, and Maillard reactions (Sun, Lin, Li, & Li, 2024). Among the four drying treatments, FD sample yielded the highest concentration of 1-octen-3-one (25.43 μg/g), a compound associated with mushroom-like and metallic.

To investigate the aroma-contributing potential of VOCs in G. lucidum, we calculated the relative rOAVs of VOCs in the fresh sample and under four drying treatments (Fig. 2D; Table S2). Compounds with rOAV ≥1 were defined as aroma-active constituents. A total of 255, 255, 254, 239, and 250 aroma-active VOCs were identified in CK, FD, MVD, HD, and ND, respectively (Fig. 2E). Notably, ketones play a predominant role in shaping the characteristic aroma profile of G. lucidum. The rOAVs of ketones across the fresh sample and the four drying treatments follow the descending order: CK > FD > MVD > HD > ND. Among all ketones identified, 1-nonen-3-one exhibits higher relative abundance and possesses the lowest odor threshold. In CK, its rOAV reaches 1.4 × 108, contributing pungent and mushroom aroma. However, at elevated concentrations, 1-nonen-3-one imparts a pungent off-note that suppresses the native grassy and fruity nuances of G. lucidum. Under all four drying treatments, its rOAV declines markedly, with the lowest value observed in HD (9.9 × 106). In CK, 3-buten-2-one, 4-(2,6,6-trimethyl-1-cyclohexen-1-yl)- exhibited the second-highest rOAV, accounting for the characteristic sweet and fruity aroma of raw G. lucidum. Among the four drying treatments, 1-octen-3-one displayed the second-highest rOAV and is known to impart a distinct mushroom-like odor, suggesting that drying enhances the mushroom-like olfactory profile of G. lucidum. Additionally, 1-octen-3-ol, 2-octenal, 2,4-nonadienal, 2,6-nonadienal, (E,Z)-, and methional also exhibits a high rOAV, collectively indicating their substantial contribution to the characteristic aroma profile of G. lucidum. FD preserves these odor-active compounds most effectively. In summary, 1-nonen-3-one emerges as the dominant aroma-active compound in G. lucidum, followed by 3-buten-2-one, 4-(2,6,6-trimethyl-1-cyclohexen-1-yl)- and 1-octen-3-one. Additional contributors to the overall aroma profile include 1-octen-3-ol, 2-octenal, 2,4-nonadienal, 2,6-nonadienal, (E,Z)-, and methional. As corroborated by prior analytical findings, the drying process markedly diminishes fruity, sweet, and grassy odor notes, while concurrently enhancing mushroom-like aroma intensity. Among the evaluated methods, FD best retains the characteristic aroma profile of fresh G. lucidum. In contrast, HD promotes greater formation of volatile flavor compounds, whereas ND results in substantial loss of aroma-active volatiles and induces off-flavors attributable to prolonged exposure and inefficient moisture removal (Li et al., 2023).

Changes of non-volatile flavor components

To systematically elucidate how different drying methods affect the flavor profile of G. lucidum, we performed a broad-targeted metabolomic analysis using UPLC-MS/MS. As shown in Fig. 3A, a total of 2696 non-volatile metabolites were identified and classified into 13 categories, including 533 amino acids and derivatives (19.77%), 446 terpenoids (16.54%), 312 alkaloids (11.57%), 304 lipids (11.28%), 169 phenolic acids (6.27%), 158 organic acids (5.86%), 142 nucleotides and derivatives (5.27%), 124 flavonoids (4.6%), 54 lignans and coumarins (2%), 18 quinones (0.67%), 18 steroids (0.67%), 7 tannins (0.26%), and 411 others (15.24%). Based on the metabolite profiles shown in Fig. 3B, PC1 and PC2 account for 41.37% and 20.49% of the total variance, respectively, revealing pronounced inter-sample differences in non-volatile metabolite composition across the four drying treatments.

Non-volatile profiling analysis of Ganoderma lucidum across drying methods by UPLC–MS, CK: fresh sample; FD: freeze vacuum drying; MVD: microwave vacuum drying; HD: hot air drying; ND: natural drying. A: circle plot; B: PCA plot; C: heatmap; D: stacked bar chart.

The heatmap in Fig. 3C illustrates the differential responses of major non-volatile metabolite classes to five samples. Amino acids and derivatives, nucleotides and derivatives, and lipids exhibited pronounced sensitivity to drying conditions, showing substantial variation across treatments. In contrast, organic acids and terpenoids displayed relatively modest changes, whereas alkaloids, steroids, flavonoids, lignans, coumarins, quinones, and tannins remained largely unaffected. Overall, the total non-volatile metabolite content varied significantly across treatments, following the descending order: CK > FD > HD > MVD > ND (Fig. 3D).

OPLS-DA was applied to discriminate G. lucidum samples processed by different drying methods, with results presented in Fig. S3A–D. All control-group models exhibited high model validity: cumulative explained variances (R2X and R2Y) and cumulative cross-validated predictive ability (Q2) exceeded 0.9, confirming robust explanatory power and reliable group separation. Differential metabolites were rigorously identified using the following orthogonal criteria: fold change (FC) ≥ 2 (up-regulated) or ≤0.5 (down-regulated), variable importance in projection (VIP) > 1, and p < 0.05. A total of 1200 significantly altered metabolites were detected across all comparisons (Table S3). Volcano plot analysis revealed 1002 (CK vs FD), 1191 (CK vs MVD), 1083 (CK vs HD), and 1248 (CK vs ND) differential metabolites in the respective pairwise comparisons. These metabolites included 95 lipids, 362 amino acids and derivatives, 54 nucleotides and derivatives, 58 organic acids, and 47 saccharides.

Lipids constitute a vital class of bioactive compounds in mushroom, critically influencing their nutritional value, flavor profile, and textural properties (Xie et al., 2024). Among the 95 differentially accumulated metabolites, 64 were free fatty acids, 14 were lysophosphatidyl ethanolamines, 10 were sphingolipids, 6 were lysophosphatidylcholines, and 1 was glycerol ester. The lipid content in the CK was significantly higher than that observed under the four drying treatments. This difference is attributable to drying-induced activation or modulation of endogenous enzymes, including lipoxygenase (LOX) and phospholipase, which catalyze the oxidation of polyunsaturated fatty acids and the hydrolysis of phospholipids. Moreover, oxidation of unsaturated fatty acids, including oleic acid and linoleic acid, generates volatile compounds such as alcohols, ketones, and aldehydes, which contribute to the characteristic flavor profile of dried G. lucidum (Wu et al., 2021). However, excessive degradation may lead to the formation of off-flavor compounds and compromise the nutritional quality (Kong et al., 2025). FD preserved the highest lipid content among all drying methods, with marked enrichment of 18 unsaturated fatty acids, including punicic acid, stearidonic acid, and 9,10,11-trihydroxy-12-octadecenoic acid, relative to HD, MVD, and ND. This superior preservation stems from FD's dual inhibition of enzymatic hydrolysis and non-enzymatic oxidation under ultra-low temperature and vacuum, effectively safeguarding thermolabile lipids such as polyunsaturated fatty acids and phospholipids (Z. Zeng et al., 2022).

Amino acids and derivatives constitute essential cellular constituents in living organisms and serve as key precursors for flavor compound biosynthesis in mushroom (Qin et al., 2024). The abundance of amino acids and derivatives across drying treatments followed the rank order: CK > FD > HD > ND > MVD. This gradient reflects differential protein degradation during processing, which directly influences flavor quality by modulating both the composition and concentration of free amino acids (Chen et al., 2020). Among the four dried samples, FD exhibited significantly higher levels of amino acids and derivatives compared with HD, ND, and MVD, whereas no statistically significant differences were observed among the latter three. This pattern is likely attributable to thermal acceleration of the Maillard reaction under heating-based drying methods (HD, MVD), wherein amino acids condense with reducing sugars to form melanoidins and other non-volatile products (Zeng et al., 2022). Furthermore, moisture loss during drying facilitates Strecker degradation: a decarboxylation-deamination cascade that converts α-amino acids into corresponding aldehydes bearing characteristic side-chain moieties, thereby depleting free amino acids (Liu et al., 2022). Free amino acids contribute to multiple taste modalities, including umami, bitterness, astringency, and sweetness, and serve as direct precursors for aroma compound biosynthesis, thereby playing a pivotal role in shaping the flavor profile (Wu et al., 2025). Fig. 4D, and Table S4 present the quantitative profiles of 19 free amino acids across the four drying treatments. Among these, FD exhibited the highest overall abundance of free amino acids, likely attributable to the suppression of thermally driven reactions, particularly the Maillard reaction, under FD's ultra-low temperature and vacuum conditions (Liao et al., 2025). Notably, unlike most mushroom, wherein umami-associated amino acids predominantly define the flavor signature of dried products (Li et al., 2015; Zhang, Venkitasamy, Pan, & Wang, 2013), bitterness is the dominant sensory attribute governing flavor quality in dried samples. Key bitter-tasting amino acids include L-valine, methionine, L-isoleucine, L-leucine, L-phenylalanine, L-histidine, and L-arginine (Wen et al., 2022). Their concentrations were maximal in FD and minimal in CK, indicating that FD-induced enrichment of these compounds constitutes the primary biochemical basis for the pronounced bitterness characteristic of freeze-dried G. lucidum. Furthermore, L-aspartic, the principal umami contributor, reached its highest concentration in CK, exceeding that in ND by 1.5-fold; in contrast, no statistically significant differences in L-aspartic levels were observed among FD, MVD, and HD.

Non-volatile profiling analysis of Ganoderma lucidum across drying methods by UPLC–MS, CK: fresh sample; FD: freeze vacuum drying; MVD: microwave vacuum drying; HD: hot air drying; ND: natural drying. A: soluble sugar heatmap; B: organic acid heatmap; C: 5′-nucleotide heatmap; D: free amino acid heatmap.

Nucleotides acids and derivatives critically influence the taste profile of mushrooms. Consistent with prior findings, HD yielded the highest abundance of nucleotides and their derivatives among the five processing methods (Luo et al., 2021). 5′-Nucleotides are key contributors to the characteristic umami flavor of mushroom. As shown in Fig. 4C, Table S4, five 5′-nucleotides were identified: 5′-CMP, 5′-UMP, 5′-GMP, 5′-IMP, and 5′-AMP. HD exhibited the greatest 5′-nucleotide concentration, likely resulting from heat-induced depolymerization of nucleic acids and deoxyribonucleic acids, coupled with residual nuclease activity under elevated temperature (Zhao, Wei, Gong, Xu, & Xin, 2020). In contrast, the ND displayed significantly lower 5′-nucleotide levels than those in HD and MVD, attributable to the slow initial dehydration rate during ND, which sustains elevated water activity over an extended period and thereby facilitates nuclease-mediated degradation of 5′-nucleotides. Notably, 5′-GMP and 5′-IMP act as potent umami synergists that markedly enhance the savory perception of monosodium glutamate; 5′-AMP, while less umami-active, mitigates bitterness, enhances sweetness, and contributes modestly to overall umami intensity (D. Luo et al., 2021). This study demonstrates that umami-active 5′-nucleotides (5′-GMP, 5′-IMP, and 5′-AMP) were significantly enriched in all four dried samples relative to CK, with HD showing the highest accumulation, 4.15-, 1.61-, 1.55-, and 1.05-fold greater than CK, FD, ND, and MVD, respectively. These results collectively indicate that HD enhances the umami character of dried G. lucidum.

Organic acids are key contributors to the sour and astringent taste attributes of G. lucidum. The total abundance of organic acids across sample groups followed the order: CK > FD > HD > MVD > ND. While the overall organic acid content remained relatively stable among the FD, HD, and MVD, it was markedly lower in the ND. This reduction in ND is attributable to decarboxylation reactions promoted by its high initial moisture content, creating favorable conditions for enzymatic activity during drying. As shown in Fig. 4B, and Table S4, malic and succinic acids decreased across all dried samples, whereas citric, isocitric, and quinic acids exhibited significant declines in ND. Notably, citric and malic acids are heat-stable organic acids (Qiu, Vuist, Boom, & Schutyser, 2018), explaining their retention in MVD and HD; in contrast, the low-temperature, hypoxic environment of FD suppressed organic acid biosynthesis (Z. Zeng et al., 2022). Collectively, however, the total organic acid content does not appear to be the dominant determinant of G. lucidum's flavor profile.

Saccharides are naturally present in fungal fruiting bodies and exhibit diverse biological activities; they also constitute the primary determinants of sweetness (Liu et al., 2022). In this study, the total relative saccharide content in the FD was significantly higher than that in the MVD, HD, and ND, a finding consistent with prior reports (Liao et al., 2025). This disparity may arise from Maillard reactions and thermal or enzymatic sugar hydrolysis occurring during high-temperature and ambient-temperature drying processes, both of which alter saccharide composition and stability in G. lucidum (Zhang, Li, et al., 2025). Sucrose, trehalose, maltose, fructose, mannose, glucose, and arabinose have been identified as the principal sweet-tasting saccharides in mushroom (Hu, Feng, Huang, Ibrahim, & Liu, 2020; Liao et al., 2025; Yanmei Zhang et al., 2023). Notably, lactose, though less commonly reported in basidiomycetes, was detected at relatively high levels among soluble sugars in this study. As shown in Fig. 4A, and Table S4, arabinose, mannose, fructose, and glucose exhibited significantly higher abundances in ND compared to FD, MVD, and HD, whereas trehalose abundance was markedly reduced. This reduction is likely attributable to the activation of trehalase under ND conditions, an enzyme that catalyzes the hydrolysis of trehalose into two glucose units (Liao et al., 2025). In contrast, trehalose, sucrose, maltose, and lactose levels were significantly elevated in MVD relative to HD and ND, potentially reflecting the shorter drying duration in MVD, which better preserves thermolabile and hydrolysis-sensitive saccharides (Zeng et al., 2025).

Correlation analysis of E-nose and volatile flavor compounds

A strong and statistically significant correlation was observed between VOCs and the signal intensities of E-nose sensors. Correlation analysis was performed between the top 50 VOCs ranked by rOAV and sensor responses. In the resulting correlation network, red and green edges denote positive and negative correlations, respectively, while edge thickness reflects correlation magnitude (Fig. 5). Joint analysis revealed that sensors W5S, W1W, W2W, W1S, and W2S exhibited significant positive correlations with v1 (1-nonen-3-one), v5 (1-octen-3-ol), v10 (2-octenal), v13 (methional), v39 (1-octanol), v43 (2-butenal, 3-methyl-), v45 (3,5-octadien-2-one, (E,E)-), and v49 (5-octen-1-ol, (Z)-), and significant negative correlations with v8 (3-octene-2-one) and v29 (phenol, 2-methyl-). Additionally, W1S, W1W, and W2W showed significant positive correlations with v24 (octane, 1-iodo-); W2S and W2W displayed significant negative correlations with v7 (1-butanol, 3,3-dimethyl-) and v21 (hexanal); and W2W alone was significantly negatively correlated with v47 (3-methylthiobutanal).

Correlation network linking E-nose sensor responses to the top 50 volatile metabolites ranked by rOAV.

1-nonen-3-one exhibits an exceptionally low odor threshold and displays the highest rOAV across all drying treatment groups. It has been consistently identified as a potential key contributor to the characteristic aroma of G. lucidum, aligning with prior studies (Tian et al., 2016). However, its overwhelming olfactory potency may compromise the E-nose's discrimination capacity for co-eluting or structurally similar volatiles (Feng et al., 2025). In contrast, 1-octen-3-ol, 2-octenal, and 1-octanol, well-established mushroom-type aroma compounds (Tian et al., 2016), were confirmed as dominant volatile flavor contributors in G. lucidum. Sulfur-containing volatiles are widely recognized as major aroma-active constituents in dried mushrooms (Tian et al., 2016); The combined analysis detected two such compounds: methional and 3-methylthiobutanal. The former is reported as the primary aroma determinant in cooked pine mushrooms (Zhang et al., 2018), yet its content declined significantly following all four drying treatments. Conversely, 3-methylthiobutanal abundance increased markedly post-drying, suggesting that thermal processing may modulate sulfur-volatile profiles in ways that enhance overall aroma quality. Additionally, 3-octen-2-one was identified as a flavor compound associated with the characteristic aroma of G. lucidum, potentially generated through oxidation of unsaturated fatty acids and Maillard reactions (Sun et al., 2024). Notably, several high-rOAV aroma-active compounds such as 1-octen-3-one, 3-buten-2-one, 4-(2,6,6-trimethyl-1-cyclohexen-1-yl)-, 2,4-nonadienal, 2,6-nonadienal, (E,Z)-, and (E,E)-2,4-nonadienal, were not resolved by the E-nose. This detection gap likely stems from two interrelated factors: (i) signal masking due to the exceptionally high rOAV and sensor saturation caused by 1-nonene-3-one, and (ii) inherent limitations in current sensor selectivity, necessitating further optimization to improve resolution and identification accuracy.

Correlation analysis of E-tongue and non-volatile flavor compounds

WGCNA was employed to construct a co-expression network, integrating 1200 differentially accumulated metabolites with five sensory attributes of G. lucidum, namely, bitterness, bitter aftertaste, umami, umami aftertaste, and saltiness. The analysis partitioned these metabolites into five distinct co-expression modules: blue, brown, turquoise, green, and yellow (Fig. 6A). As illustrated in Fig. 6B, both the brown and turquoise modules exhibited statistically significant correlations with all five taste attributes. Specifically, the brown module showed a strong positive correlation with umami (r = 0.92), moderate-to-strong positive correlations with bitterness (r = 0.72), bitter aftertaste (r = 0.75), and umami aftertaste (r = 0.79), and a moderate negative correlation with saltiness (r = −0.64). In contrast, the turquoise module displayed an inverse association pattern: it was strongly negatively correlated with umami (r = −0.95) and umami aftertaste (r = −0.85), moderately negatively correlated with bitterness and bitter aftertaste (both r = −0.77), and positively correlated with saltiness (r = 0.64). The blue module also demonstrated notable associations, positively correlated with umami (r = 0.64) and negatively correlated with saltiness (r = −0.68). Collectively, the three biologically most relevant modules, turquoise, blue, and brown, encompassed 493 metabolites: 282 in the turquoise module, 128 in the blue module, and 83 in the brown module.

WGCNA of E-nose signals and 1200 differentially abundant non-volatile metabolites: A, cluster dendrogram; B, module–trait relationships.

Metabolite annotation of the brown module (Table S5), which exhibited the strongest associations with bitterness and umami, revealed that it was predominantly enriched in alkaloids (n = 19), amino acids and derivatives (n = 14), and nucleotides and derivatives (n = 11). Bitterness in G. lucidum is widely attributed to secondary metabolites, including triterpenoids, alkaloids, flavonoids, and coumarins (Luo et al., 2025). Notably, thermal drying induces the Maillard reaction between carbonyl and amino compounds, generating nitrogen- and oxygen-containing heterocyclic compounds, such as pyrazines, furans, reductones, and pyrrolines, that contribute significantly to perceived bitterness (Yang et al., 2020). Within the brown module, nitrogen-containing heterocycles, including 4-hydroxypyridine, 3-methyldioxyindole, and 1-aminocyclopentanecarboxylate, were identified as metabolites potentially associated with bitterness. Coumarins and flavonoids followed in contribution, while triterpenoids showed comparatively lower but still notable associations. Although triterpenoids are conventionally regarded as primary contributors to G. lucidum bitterness, their overall abundance remained largely unaffected across drying treatments in this study; Only madecassic acid showed a statistically significant response to drying method, suggesting its potential involvement in modulating bitterness under thermal processing conditions.

Umami flavor in G. lucidum is potentially associated with 5′-IMP, whose content in the HD was higher than in all other treatment groups. This elevation is likely driven by thermal degradation and enzymatic hydrolysis of nucleic acids, including both RNA and DNA, under high-temperature drying conditions (Zhao et al., 2020). In addition, ATP was identified as a hub umami-related metabolite in the brown module. As a central energy carrier in cellular metabolism, ATP can be sequentially hydrolyzed to ADP, AMP, and ultimately 5′-AMP, a well-established umami compound, thereby sustaining umami intensity over time. Notably, however, 5′-AMP itself did not emerge as a statistically significant hub metabolite in the brown module. Furthermore, the brown module was enriched with numerous amino acids and derivatives, such as l-glutamine and l-lysine, that are known to mitigate polyphenol-induced astringency through molecular interaction or pH buffering (Zhong et al., 2023). The blue module (Table S6), which exhibited strong positive correlation with umami perception, was characterized by 32 amino acid derivatives, predominantly dipeptides and tripeptides such as L-asparagine, L-glutamyl-L-glutamic acid, and 5-L-glutamyl-L-alanine. Accumulating evidence indicates that short-chain peptides contribute substantially to both umami and bitter taste; specifically, peptides bearing hydrophilic residues at either terminus tend to elicit umami, whereas those featuring an alkaline residue paired with a hydrophobic residue, or hydrophobic residues at both termini, are more commonly associated with bitterness (D. Wu et al., 2025). Collectively, these findings demonstrate that umami flavor in G. lucidum arises not from a single compound, but from a coordinated interplay among nucleotide derivatives, energy metabolites, free amino acids, and short-chain peptides, whose relative abundances are distinctly modulated by drying method, thereby underpinning process-dependent flavor differentiation.

Conclusions

This study systematically investigates the effects of four drying methods (FD, MVD, HD, and ND) on the volatile compounds, non-volatile compounds, and sensory quality of G. lucidum, employing an integrated analytical approach based on E-nose, E-tongue, GC–MS, and UPLC-MS. Through multi-index detection and comprehensive analysis, the comparative performance and context-dependent applicability of each drying method were elucidated. Results indicate that FD effectively preserves the natural color, thermolabile flavor compounds, and bioactive constituents of G. lucidum, yielding a flavor profile most closely resembling that of the fresh material. However, its prolonged drying duration, high energy demand, and elevated operational cost substantially constrain industrial scalability. MVD and HD exhibit comparable profiles in volatile and non-volatile compounds as well as sensory attributes. MVD is characterized by a shortened drying cycle and is suited to rapid, small-batch processing; HD, conversely, subjects the material to sustained high-temperature exposure, thereby enhancing flavor intensity and enriching taste complexity. Specifically, HD markedly suppresses the pungent odorant 1-nonen-3-one while promoting the release of mushroom-characteristic volatiles, such as 1-octen-3-one, 3-buten-2-one. Furthermore, the relatively high abundance of 5′-IMP may contribute to the enhanced umami flavor. In contrast, although ND is more cost-effective, it suffers from extended processing time, low initial moisture removal efficiency, and off-odor generation. Correlation analysis and WGCNA indicates that odor differences in G. lucidum across drying methods primarily arise from alterations in key volatiles including 1-nonen-3-one, 1-octen-3-ol, 2-octenal, methional, 1-octanol, and 3-octen-2-one, consistent with findings reported in (Gao et al., 2025). Drying methods significantly influence both bitter and umami taste attributes. Bitterness is predominantly attributable to nitrogen-containing heterocycles, coumarins, flavonoids, and triterpenoids; umami perception is chiefly driven by 5′-IMP alongside free amino acids and derivatives.

Based on the above analysis, and considering practical factors such as processing time, energy efficiency, economic feasibility, sensory attributes of G. lucidum, and metabolite profile changes, HD appears to be the most industrially feasible drying method for this species. These results shed light on how drying methods influence the sensory attributes and metabolite profiles of G. lucidum, offering practical insights for optimizing the processing and preservation of high-quality G. lucidum.
