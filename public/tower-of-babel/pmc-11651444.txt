Ankylosing spondylitis and cardiovascular disease: A two-sample Mendelian randomization analysis
Authors: Huaigang Chen, Lang Hong, Hong Wang, Bin Li, Liu Yang
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
Epidemiological research has demonstrated that people suffering from ankylosing spondylitis (AS) have a greater chance of developing cardiovascular disease (CVD), though the potential link between AS genetics and CVD risk is uncertain. This research examined the potential link between CVD outcomes and AS which is genetically determined. A two-sample Mendelian randomization analysis was conducted using data from European population genome-wide association study of AS and CVD. We selected single nucleotide polymorphisms closely associated with AS genetic susceptibility as instrumental variables, and used inverse variance weighted as the main method to analyze the causal effects of AS on 7 CVDs (heart failure, atrial fibrillation, hypertension, ischemic stroke, etc). To further reinforce our results, we conducted replication analyses, sensitivity analyses, and instrumental variable strength assessments. In the European population, it was observed that AS genetic susceptibility was correlated with a higher risk of heart failure (odds ratios [OR] = 1.0128, 95% confidence intervals [CI]: 1.0037–1.0219, P = .0058), hypertension (OR = 1.0143, 95% CI: 1.0038–1.0248, P = .0073), and ischemic stroke (OR = 1.0151, 95% CI: 1.0049–1.0255, P = .0036). However, it was not associated with coronary heart disease, cardiomyopathy, valvular heart disease, or atrial fibrillation (all P > .05). A sensitivity analysis was conducted to validate the reliability of the results. Evidence from the European population has indicated a correlation between AS and heart failure, hypertension, and ischemic stroke. Further investigations should be conducted to explore the underlying mechanisms of action.

1. Introduction

Cardiovascular disease (CVD) is a general term for all cardiovascular and cerebrovascular diseases related to vascular lesions, mainly including heart failure, coronary heart disease, arrhythmia, and stroke. Globally, CVD is the leading cause of death and disability, resulting in increased social and economic burden.[1] This urgently requires joint efforts to identify the risk factors for CVD, and studies have shown that the occurrence and development of CVD may be driven by genetic or environmental factors.[2–4] Epidemiological studies have indicated a link between ankylosing spondylitis (AS) and cardiovascular outcomes.[5]

AS is a chronic progressive inflammatory disease that mainly affects the spine and can involve the sacroiliac joint and peripheral joints to varying degrees, with persistent joint pain as the main clinical feature.[6] In the course of AS development, it often invades multiple systems throughout the body, such as the heart, lungs, and eyes. More than 54 million people worldwide suffer from AS, with 18.6, 18, and 12.2 cases of AS per 10,000 people in European, Asian, and Latin American populations, respectively.[7] Research has indicated that those with AS are more likely to suffer from CVDs.[8–10] Elevated levels of C-reactive protein, inflammatory cytokines, immunoglobulins G and M, antineutrophil cytoplasmic antibodies, vascular endothelial growth factor, and interleukins in patients with AS induce oxidative stress, impair endothelial function, create a prothrombotic state, and disrupt microvascular and macrovascular integrity, thereby facilitating the development of atherosclerosis and increasing the risk of cardiovascular events.[11,12] A meta-analysis of available research demonstrated that individuals with AS had a significantly heightened risk of myocardial infarction and stroke, yet there was no marked difference in overall mortality rates.[13] As a result, more research is necessary to determine the causal connection between AS and CVD. Randomized controlled trials can eliminate selection bias, and blind analysis can bolster the dependability of outcomes; however, these trials are expensive, labor-intensive, and require a significant amount of resources and time, and may have ethical restrictions.

Mendelian randomization (MR) is a form of instrumental variable analysis that makes use of genetic variants as instrumental variables to identify and measure the causal relationship of interest.[14,15] MR is based on Mendel second law of inheritance, which divides the study population into groups according to whether they carry a certain genetic variant, and then compares the occurrence of outcomes between the 2 groups. This method of grouping can prevent the confounding effects of other characteristics, environment, and socioeconomic status of the population from influencing the results. At the same time, since the formation of the exposure part explained by the genetic variant precedes the outcome, the interference of reverse causality can also be excluded, effectively compensating for the shortcomings of traditional methods. This research utilized two-sample MR analysis of extensive publicly available genome-wide association study (GWAS) data to investigate the genetic connection between AS and CVD outcomes.

We hypothesize that this MR study will allow us to more precisely evaluate the causal relationship between AS and CVD risk, as well as elucidate potential biological mechanisms underlying this association, which could have important theoretical and practical implications for clinical practice.

2. Materials and methods

2.1. Data sources

The summary data of AS were obtained from the FinnGen public database (https://www.finngen.fi/fi), which included 166,144 participants of mainly European ancestry (cases: 1462, controls: 164,682). Large-scale GWAS summary datasets of 7 CVD outcomes phenotypes were obtained from FinnGen, MRC-IEU, Neale lab, and CARDIoGRAMplusC4D (Table 1).[16–19] A GWAS study by Shah et al included 47,309 cases and 930,014 controls from 26 studies of the Heart Failure Molecular Epidemiology for Therapeutic Targets Consortium in European populations.[17] Malik et al performed a genome-wide association meta-analysis of 440,328 Europeans (34,217 ischemic stroke patients and 406,111 controls), identifying 22 new stroke risk loci.[19] The genetic data used in this study were all downloadable from the MRC-IEU Opengwas Project database (https://gwas.mrcieu.ac.uk/). The MR study was performed by analyzing GWAS summary data, which were ethically supported and freely downloadable and usable on public websites.

2.2. Screening and extraction criteria

Single nucleotide polymorphisms (SNPs) associated with the exposure phenotype were screened from the GWAS data of the exposure phenotype. Eligible SNPs were considered to be those that had a genome-wide significant association with the phenotype (P < 5 × 10−8), a minor allele frequency > 1%, and no obvious linkage disequilibrium (r2 < 0.01).[20] Following strict selection, these SNPs were utilized in the MR analysis.

2.3. Statistical analysis

2.3.1. MR assumptions

The genetic variants in classical MR studies must satisfy the following 3 core assumptions. The key assumptions underlying this approach are as follows: (1) the relevance assumption: the selected SNPs exhibit robust associations with the exposure of interest; (2) the independence assumption: the chosen SNPs are independent of potential confounding factors influencing the exposure–outcome relationship; (3) the exclusion restriction assumption: the identified SNPs affect the outcome exclusively through the exposure, and do not impact the outcome via alternative causal pathways (see Fig. 1). Only when the above 3 core assumptions are met, MR studies can effectively correct for unknown confounders. The F statistic (F = R2(N − 1 − k)/(1 − R2)k) was used to evaluate the relevance assumption, where R2 reflects the proportion of variance in the exposure variable explained by SNPs, N is the sample size, and k is the number of SNPs. When F > 10, it was considered that the relevance assumption was met, that is, there was no weak instrument bias in the study.[21] The exclusion assumption was tested using the MR Egger regression model, and when the intercept term of the MR Egger regression model was zero, it was considered that the MR study met the exclusion assumption.

2.3.2. MR analysis

Employing two-sample MR, a powerful statistical technique, we were able to deduce the causal relationship between 2 phenotypes. To obtain the MR estimates, inverse variance weighted (IVW) meta-analysis was performed on each Wald ratio, which was considered to be the most reliable when there was no evidence of directional pleiotropy. We performed sensitivity analyses using MR Egger, weighted median, simple mode, weighted mode, and leave-one-out methods.[22–24] The causal estimates were given as odds ratios (OR) and their 95% confidence intervals (CI), and P < .05 indicated statistical significance.

2.3.3. Heterogeneity and pleiotropy tests

To test the heterogeneity of SNPs, Cochran Q statistic was utilized; if the P value of the Q statistic was <.05, it indicated that there was heterogeneity among the SNPs included. Upon the detection of heterogeneity, MR-PRESSO method was employed to identify the SNPs which caused the heterogeneity, with the number of tests set to 5000 times. Simultaneously, the distortion test method calculated the P value, and when it was >.05, it indicated that the heterogeneity had no considerable effect on the MR results. Egger_intercept was used to evaluate the pleiotropy of SNPs, and if the P value was <.05, it indicated that there was pleiotropy among the included SNPs, that is, there were confounders that could not be removed between the exposure phenotype and the outcome, which would affect the final results.[25]

The statistical analysis used in this study was done in the two-sample MR and MR-PRESSO software packages of R software (version 4.1.3).

3. Result

3.1. SNPs selected for MR

We finally obtained 13 SNPs that had a significant genetic association with AS (P < 5 × 10−8, r2 < 0.01, kb = 10,000) (see Table S1, Supplemental Digital Content, http://links.lww.com/MD/O202). Meanwhile, the table presents the chosen SNPs and their characteristics, including their association and directionality with AS. To eliminate confounders, a total of 3 SNPs were removed from AS (rs11065898, rs4129267, and rs9901869), because these SNPs were closely related to phenotypes such as coronary atherosclerosis, blood lipids, blood pressure, etc (see Table S2, Supplemental Digital Content, http://links.lww.com/MD/O202).

3.2. Risk between AS and CVD

In the IVW model, there was a positive causal relationship between AS and the risk of heart failure (OR = 1.0128, 95% CI: 1.0037–1.0219, P = .0058), hypertension (OR = 1.0143, 95% CI: 1.0038–1.0248, P = .0073), and ischemic stroke (OR = 1.0151, 95% CI: 1.0049–1.0255, P = .0036). No meaningful correlation was observed between AS and atrial fibrillation, coronary heart disease, cardiomyopathy, and valvular heart disease (P > .05). The results of the detailed analysis are presented in Table 2 and depicted in the forest plot (Fig. 2). These results suggest that AS may lead to adverse cardiovascular outcomes, such as an increased risk of heart failure, hypertension, and ischemic stroke. This offers novel objectives and approaches for the avoidance and management of CVD.

3.3. Sensitivity analysis results

In the sensitivity analysis, Cochran Q test showed that there was heterogeneity in the association between AS and hypertension (Q = 31.7425, P = .0015), while there was no heterogeneity in other cardiovascular outcomes (see Table S3, Supplemental Digital Content, http://links.lww.com/MD/O202). Further analysis using the MR-PRESSO method revealed that rs62394289 was the SNP responsible for the observed heterogeneity (P < .0026), as indicated in Table S4, Supplemental Digital Content, http://links.lww.com/MD/O202. The distortion test (P = .6658) revealed the presence of heterogeneity, however this heterogeneity did not substantially impact the observed association with the outcome, as detailed in Table S4, Supplemental Digital Content, http://links.lww.com/MD/O202. The horizontal pleiotropy between SNPs and outcomes was evaluated by MR Egger regression, and the results showed no evidence of horizontal pleiotropy (all P > .05) (see Table S5, Supplemental Digital Content, http://links.lww.com/MD/O202). The funnel plot of AS and heart failure, hypertension, and ischemic stroke (Fig. 3) demonstrated a symmetrical distribution of SNPs, suggesting that there was no remarkable horizontal pleiotropy. The MR test results showed agreement in direction, and the intercept close to the origin further suggested that there was no pleiotropy (as can be seen in Fig. 4). The final result of leave-one-out analysis indicated the stability of the causal relationship between SNPs and cardiovascular outcome events (Fig. 5). The sensitivity analysis outcomes confirmed our primary conclusions, namely, that AS is linked to heart failure, hypertension, and ischemic stroke.

3.4. Results and implications of reverse MR analysis

When CVDs are considered as the exposure factor, reverse MR analysis shows that heart failure, hypertension, and ischemic stroke have no significant association with the risk of developing AS, with OR values of 0.836 (95% CI: 0.482–1.452, P = .525), 0.986 (95% CI: 0.853–1.139, P = .845), and 0.962 (95% CI: 0.704–1.315, P = .809). The results, which did not reach statistical significance, indicate that the previous positive associations were not confirmed in the reverse model. This emphasizes the need for further exploration of the complex relationships between AS and these CVDs (see Table S6, Supplemental Digital Content, http://links.lww.com/MD/O202 and Figure S1, Supplemental Digital Content, http://links.lww.com/MD/O201, Figure S2, Supplemental Digital Content, http://links.lww.com/MD/O201, and Figure S3, Supplemental Digital Content, http://links.lww.com/MD/O201).

4. Discussion

4.1. AS and CVD

The present study has demonstrated that there is an increased risk of heart failure in patients with AS. This finding is consistent with the results previously reported by Liu and colleagues.[26] Furthermore, our study has identified an association between AS and both hypertension and ischemic stroke. The absence of significant heterogeneity or multidimensionality in our study, as verified using various statistical models, provides additional support for the robustness of our findings.

Previous studies have shown a correlation between AS and CVD. In a longitudinal cohort study of AS patients across Korea, Bae et al demonstrated that the AS cohort harbored an elevated risk of congestive heart failure compared to controls (0.79% vs 0.32%, P < .0001).[27] The adjusted ORs for congestive heart failure and mortality in the AS group were 2.28 (95% CI: 1.80–2.89) and 1.66 (95% CI: 1.42–1.95), respectively, which were consistent with our positive causal relationship. A cohort study of the British population matched 3809 newly diagnosed AS patients and found that the risk of ischemic heart disease increased with age in female AS patients (HR = 1.20, 95% CI: 0.97–1.48, P < .05).[28] However, another follow-up cohort study found that newly diagnosed young spondylitis patients had a significantly increased risk of ischemic heart disease.[29] This could not further explain whether the cause of the increased incidence of ischemic heart disease was age or concomitant AS, while in this MR study, the influence of age and gender was well avoided, indicating that there was no causal relationship between AS and coronary heart disease.

Several studies have reported that AS patients seem to be more prone to cardiac valve problems.[30,31] In a study by Ward et al, outcomes were compared between 42,327 AS patients and 19,211,703 non-AS patients. Their findings demonstrated that AS patients harbored a mildly elevated risk of cardiac valve disease, particularly aortic valve involvement.[31] However, this significant difference was based on the increase of age. Prior studies have not identified an increased risk of cerebrovascular accident in patients with AS. However, Keller JJ et al compared 1479 AS and 5916 non-AS patients, and after adjusting for hypertension, coronary heart disease, atrial fibrillation and economic ability, the risk of stroke in AS patients increased, with an OR of 2.3 (95% CI: 1.9–2.8).[32] This was consistent with our study’s conclusion, and the difference between the study design results can be explained by the susceptibility of confounding factors such as socioeconomic, dietary, and lifestyle behavior factors in traditional observational studies. In addition, Mei J et al’s MR study further found that AS was associated with stroke risk only among specific subtypes, including large artery stroke and cardioembolic stroke.[33]

Our study findings demonstrated no causal association between AS and atrial fibrillation risk (IVW: OR = 1.0002, 95% CI: 0.9883–1.0122, P = .9765), congruent with the prior MR study by Chen et al which similarly found no causal relationship between AS and atrial fibrillation (IVW: OR = 0.983, 95% CI: 0.894–1.080, P = .718).[34] However, in the nationwide studies by Bengtsson and Moon et al, AS patients were found to have a higher risk of new-onset atrial fibrillation, which was contradictory to our conclusion.[35,36] Although both studies tried to adjust for some confounding factors (such as age, gender, and classic cardiac metabolic risk factors), the observational study design had inherent flaws, such as selection bias and reverse causality, which also made it extremely difficult to distinguish causal relationships from confounder-mediated associations. Our study results also found a causal association between AS and hypertension risk (OR = 1.0143, 95% CI: 1.0038–1.0248, P = .0073), which was consistent with the real-world study results.[37] At the same time, in the ASAS-COMOSPA cohort study, it was also found that the risk of hypertension was significantly associated with the course of spondyloarthritis, and for every 5 years of increase in the course, the OR of hypertension events was 1.129 (95% CI: 1.072–1.189, P < .001).[38]

A study by Masi et al demonstrated that patients with AS exhibited significantly decreased high-density lipoprotein cholesterol levels compared to healthy controls.[39] In addition, some basic studies have shown that AS, driven by pro-inflammatory cytokines such as tumor necrosis factor-a and interleukins, causes endothelial dysfunction, induces glucose and lipid metabolism abnormalities, and promotes CVD.[40,41] Our study further confirmed the causal association between AS and some CVDs, which means that in clinical treatment, more attention should be paid to the possible cardiovascular events of AS patients, and early prevention, early detection and early intervention should be achieved.

4.2. Advantages and limitations

This study represents the first application of MR analysis to investigate the causal relationship between AS and CVD risk. Key strengths of this approach include: (1) leveraging the randomized assortment of alleles at conception to overcome confounding bias inherent in conventional epidemiological studies; (2) adequate sample sizes amongst European ancestry populations for MR analyses, circumventing population stratification biases; (3) consistent findings across multiple analytical methods, with sensitivity analyses and quantitative strength assessments confirming results were not impacted by bias.

However, this study has some limitations warranting consideration. The data utilized for analysis were derived from GWAS datasets rather than original data, precluding more detailed assessments of the association between AS disease course and CVD risk. Secondly, while this study establishes causal links between AS and cardiovascular outcomes, the specific biological mechanisms underlying these associations remain undetermined. Finally, the modest magnitude of the observed OR merits cautious interpretation of the results.

5. Conclusion

In summary, in this study, we used two-sample MR analysis to evaluate the causal relationship between AS and the increased risk of CVD outcomes. Our research found that AS had a direct correlation to heart failure, hypertension, and ischemic stroke, however, it did not appear to have any effect on atrial fibrillation, heart valve disease, cardiomyopathy, and coronary heart disease. In the future, new phenotypes need to be discovered using extended GWAS studies to draw more reliable conclusions.
