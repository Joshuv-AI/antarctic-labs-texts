Effects of adding Bacillus coagulans to the diet on diarrhea, gut microbiota, and metabolites in yak
Authors: Chenyang Zhang, Dunzhu Luosan, YongFu La, Xiaoming Ma, Xiaoyun Wu, Min Chu, Xian Guo, Zhandui Pincuo, Chunnian Liang
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
The role of Bacillus coagulans in enhancing animal growth performance and immune function is increasingly recognized. However, its effects on diarrhea, gut microbiota, and metabolites in yaks in China’s high-altitude regions remain unclear. This study randomly divided 20 two-year-old male yaks of similar body weight into two groups of 10 animals each. One group served as the control group (CG) fed a basal diet, while the other group received the basal diet supplemented with 1 g Bacillus coagulans per kg of base concentrate (live bacteria count ≥ 5 × 10⁹ CFU/kg) as the experimental group (CP) for 60 days. The Probiotic supplementation significantly reduced the incidence of diarrhea and diarrhea index in the CP group compared to the CG group (P < 0.05), decreasing by 2.34% and 2.2, respectively. Additionally, CP group levels of IgA, IgM, IgG, IL-10, NOS, and T-Aoc were significantly elevated (p < 0.05) relative to CG. Notably, Bacillus coagulans supplementation significantly increased beneficial gut bacteria (Phascolarctobacterium, Agathobacter, Roseburia) in the CP group while suppressing harmful bacteria (e.g., Clostridium). It also modulated metabolic pathways, promoting the production of anti-inflammatory metabolites such as 5-(4-Methoxybenzylidene)-1,3-thiazolidine-2,4-dione, (8’S)-Latochrome, and N-Lauroyl Tyrosine. Consequently, the CP group exhibited higher levels of short-chain fatty acids (SCFAs), including lactic acid, butyric acid, valeric acid, and propionic acid, compared to the CG group. Ultimately, this enhanced yak immune function and reduced diarrhea incidence. Overall, these results suggest Bacillus coagulans may serve as an effective alternative to antibiotic growth promoters for improving yak diarrhea and gut health.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12917-025-05205-w.

Introduction

Yaks (Bos grunniens), as the most representative cold-adapted ruminants in the Qinghai-Tibet Plateau ecosystem, are not only a keystone species for maintaining ecological balance but also a core resource supporting local pastoral economies [1]. Their semi-wild grazing habits have enabled their gut microbiomes to adapt to long-term exposure to cold, low-oxygen, and nutrient-fluctuating environments, forming unique microbe-host coevolutionary relationships [2, 3]. However, in recent years, with climate change and intensified farming practices, the incidence of yak diarrhea has shown a significant upward trend. Statistics from the Ministry of Agriculture and Rural Affairs indicate that in some areas of the Qinghai-Tibet Plateau, the annual incidence of calf diarrhea reaches as high as 35%, with a mortality rate of 20% [4, 5]. Diarrhea not only disrupts the intestinal epithelial barrier function but also impairs growth performance by interfering with nutrient absorption and immune homeostasis, becoming a major bottleneck constraining the sustainable development of plateau animal husbandry [6].

Traditional diarrhea control relies on antibiotic therapy, but long-term misuse has exacerbated intestinal microbiota disruption, led to the proliferation of drug-resistant strains, and caused drug residue issues, severely threatening yak health and product safety [7]. Probiotics, as active microbial preparations that function by regulating host gut microecological balance, show potential in preventing and controlling diarrhea in ruminants [8, 9]. Studies indicate that probiotics such as lactic acid bacteria and Bacillus species can alleviate diarrhea symptoms by competitively adhering to the intestinal mucosa, secreting antimicrobial substances, and modulating immune responses [10, 11]. However, existing research has primarily focused on common cattle and sheep populations, leaving significant knowledge gaps regarding the mechanisms of probiotic action in the specialized yak species. Furthermore, the high-altitude, low-oxygen environment may influence probiotic colonization efficiency and metabolic activity, this may lead to different outcomes [12, 13].

Recent advances in omics technologies have provided new perspectives for elucidating the probiotic-microbiome-host interaction mechanisms. Metagenomic sequencing can systematically reveal the structural dynamics of the gut microbiota, while non-targeted metabolomics enables comprehensive identification of changes in the metabolite profile regulated by probiotics [14]. However, existing studies are largely confined to single-omics analyses, lacking integrated associations between microbial community structure and metabolite function. Furthermore, the molecular pathways through which probiotics regulate host immune responses remain unclear.

Addressing these scientific questions, this study proposes the following hypothesis: Specific probiotic formulations can alleviate diarrhea symptoms by reshaping yak gut microbial diversity and metabolome profiles, thereby suppressing pathogen proliferation and activating anti-inflammatory pathways. To address this, we selected yak from the Qinghai-Tibet Plateau as subjects. Through a 60-day feeding trial combined with metagenomic sequencing and non-targeted metabolomics, we systematically evaluated the effects of adding Bacillus coagulans to feed on diarrhea incidence, gut microbiota structure, and metabolites. The findings not only provide theoretical support for green prevention and control of yak diarrhea but also enrich the understanding of gut microbiome regulation in ruminants under alpine environments, demonstrating significant scientific value and application potential.

Materials and methods

All the procedures involving animals were performed according to the guidelines of the China Council on Animal Care and the Ministry of Agriculture of the People’s Republic of China. The Animal Care and Use Committee of the Lanzhou Institute of Husbandry and Pharmaceutical Sciences Chinese Academy of Agricultural Sciences approved all yak handling procedures (Permit No: 1610322020018). We confrm that the study was conducted in accordance with ARRIVE guidelines.

Animals and experimental design

The experiment was conducted at a farm in Shigatse City, Tibet Autonomous Region, China. Live Bacillus coagulans bacteria with a count ≥ 5 × 10⁹ CFU/g were sourced from Henan Putei Pharmaceutical Co., Ltd. The basal diet was a total mixed ration (TMR) with a concentrate-to-roughage ratio of 1:3. The control group (CG) received the basal diet, while the experimental group (CP) had 1 g Bacillus coagulans per kg of base concentrate added to the concentrate portion of the basal diet. Twenty 2-year-old male yaks in good body condition and of similar weight were selected as experimental subjects. Using R software (version 4.1.2), 20 yaks were randomly divided into two treatment groups. For metagenomic and metabolomic sequencing, five yaks were randomly selected from each group, yielding a total of 10 fecal samples for sequencing. All other analyses (immunological markers, short-chain fatty acids, etc.) were based on samples collected from all 20 animals. Feeding management was as follows: During the trial period, feeding occurred twice daily at 8:00 and 16:00. After each feeding, remaining feed was collected and weighed the following morning before the next feeding to calculate daily intake. Feed rations were adjusted based on production requirements to ensure all experimental yaks had ad libitum access to feed and water. The remaining feed after each feeding was maintained at approximately 5% to guarantee adequate feeding. Other husbandry practices followed the farm’s management protocols, including regular disinfection and barn cleaning. Experimental groups were established as follows: the CG group (fed basal concentrate) and the CP group (fed basal concentrate + 1 g Bacillus coagulans per kg of base concentrate), each comprising 10 yaks. Nutritional analysis of feed

The experiment consists of a pre-trial phase (15 days) and a formal trial phase (60 days). The pre-trial phase aims to help the experimental animals adapt to the supplemental feed. Following the conclusion of the pre-trial phase, the formal trial commences.

Determination of serum indicators

Following the conclusion of the trial, blood samples were collected via venipuncture using red-top tubes and sterile EP tubes from yaks in each group in the morning while they were in a fasting state. The samples were left at room temperature until the blood clotted, then centrifuged at 4 °C and 2000 g for 10 min. The supernatant was collected and stored at −20 °C for later use in measuring indicators such as IgA, IgM, IgG, IL-10,T-Aoc, GSH-px, SOD, MDA, NOS, and TP. Experimental procedures were performed according to the instructions provided in the biochemical or ELISA kit manuals(The kit brands are Nanjing Jiancheng and ELK Biotechnology.).

Diarrhea incidence among yaks in each experimental group

Observe calf fecal consistency daily before morning feeding and conduct fecal scoring. Record diarrhea status for each calf. Cattle diarrhea scoring: 0 = formed, pellet-like or cylindrical, 1 = loose, unformed, 2 = watery, separated but normal color, 3 = watery, separated with abnormal color. Diarrhea is confirmed when the score is 1 or higher.This standard was developed with reference to the commonly used clinical definitions in animal diarrhea research [15]. To ensure the consistency and objectivity of the scoring, all observers underwent standardized training prior to the experiment. Inter-observer reliability was assessed using the intraclass correlation coefficient (ICC). The ICC value for diarrhea scores among the three independent observers was 0.89, indicating excellent agreement and confirming the robustness of the scoring method.When diarrhea occurs, record the onset time and the calf’s identification number.

Determination of short-chain fatty acids

Take an appropriate amount of sample, add 200 µL of acetonitrile (containing internal standards [2H9]-Pentanoic acid and [2H11]-Hexanoic Acid) (pre-chilled at 4 °C), the selection of these deuterated internal standards was based on their structural similarity to the target analytes, which effectively corrects for matrix effects and potential losses during sample preparation and ionization.

The UPLC-ESI-MS/MS method was fully validated prior to sample analysis. Key validation parameters included specificity, linearity, accuracy, precision, and recovery. Calibration curves for all SCFAs showed excellent linearity (R² > 0.995) across the expected concentration range. The accuracy and precision, determined by analyzing quality control samples at low, medium, and high concentrations, showed intra-day and inter-day relative standard deviations (RSD) of less than 10% and recoveries within 85–115%, meeting the acceptance criteria for bioanalytical method validation. then grind for 3 min. Perform ultrasonic extraction in an ice-water bath for 10 min. Centrifuge the extracted sample (10 min, 4 °C, 12000 rpm). Transfer 80 µL of the standard solution into an injection vial. Add 40 µL of 200 mM 3-NPH (prepared as a 50% acetonitrile aqueous solution, v/v) and 40 µL of 120 mM EDC-6% pyridine (prepared as a 50% acetonitrile aqueous solution, v/v). React at 40 °C for 30 min. Cooled on ice for 1 min. Then, 160 µL of the supernatant was aspirated with a syringe, filtered through a 0.22 μm organic-phase needle filter, and transferred to a brown injection vial. Target metabolites were qualitatively and quantitatively analyzed using the UPLC-ESI-MS/MS method.

UHPLC-MS/MS analysis was performed at Novogene Co., Ltd. (Beijing, China) using a Vanquish UHPLC system (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Germany) and an Orbitrap QExactive™ HF-X mass spectrometer (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Germany). Samples were injected onto a Hypersil Gold column (100 × 2.1 mm, 1.9 μm) using a 17-minute linear gradient at a flow rate of 0.2 mL/min. In positive mode, the eluents were Eluent A (0.1% formic acid aqueous solution) and Eluent B (methanol). For negative ion mode, the eluents were Eluent A (5 mM ammonium acetate, pH 9.0) and Eluent B (methanol): 2–85% B, 3 min: 100% B, 10 min: 100-2% B, 10.1 min: 2% B, 12 min. The QExactive™ HF mass spectrometer operated in both positive and negative ion modes with a spray voltage of 3.2 kV, capillary temperature of 320 °C, sheath gas flow rate of 40 arb, auxiliary gas flow rate of 10 arb, funnel RF level of 40, and auxiliary gas heater temperature of 350 °C.

DNA extraction and metagenomic sequencing

Total microbial DNA was extracted from fecal samples using the QIAamp® Fast DNA Tool Mini Kit (Qiagen, Hilden, Germany). DNA concentration and integrity were assessed via NanoDrop 2000 spectrophotometer (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA) and agarose gel electrophoresis. DNA fragmentation was performed using S220 Focused-ultrasonicators (Covaris, USA), followed by purification with Agencourt AMPure XP Beads (Beckman Coulter Co., USA). Library preparation was conducted using the TruSeq Nano DNA LT Sample Preparation Kit (Illumina, San Diego, CA, USA).

All samples were sequenced on the Illumina NovaSeq 6000 platform, achieving an average sequencing depth of 10x, generating 150 bp paired-end reads. Raw sequencing data (FastQ files) were processed using fastp (v0.20.1) for adapter removal, low-quality base filtering, and exclusion of reads containing N bases (uncertain bases). bbmap (v38.93-0) was used to align the reads against the host genome, and host sequences were removed. After obtaining valid reads, MEGAHIT (v1.2.9) was employed for metagenomic assembly. ORF prediction is performed on the assembled scaffolds using Prodigal (v2.6.3), translating them into amino acid sequences. Non-redundant gene sets are constructed for all predicted genes across samples using MMSeqs2 (v13.45111), with clustering parameters set to 95% sequence identity and 90% coverage. The longest gene within each cluster was selected as the representative sequence for that gene set. After obtaining the representative sequences, Salmon (v1.8.0) was used to align the clean reads from each sample against the non-redundant gene set (95% identity), thereby quantifying gene abundance within the corresponding samples.

Metagenomic data processing

Species annotations are obtained from the taxonomic information database corresponding to the NR database, and the abundance of each species is then comprehensively calculated using the gene abundance associated with that species. Species abundance is statistically analyzed at the Phylum and Genus taxonomic levels across all samples, thereby constructing abundance profiles at the corresponding taxonomic levels. The DIAMOND software (v0.9.10.111) is used to annotate the representative sequences (amino acid sequences) of gene sets by aligning them against databases such as NR, KEGG, and GO. The BLAST alignment parameter is set with an expected e-value of 1e-5. The corresponding tool hmmscan (v3.1) from the CAZy database was used to align gene sets against the CAZy database, obtaining carbohydrate-active enzyme annotation information for genes. The abundance of each carbohydrate-active enzyme was then calculated by summing the gene abundances corresponding to that enzyme.

Perform PCA analysis and visualization on species abundance profiles or functional abundance profiles using the R package (v4.1.2). Calculate distance matrix results such as PCoA and NMDS for graphical analysis. Conduct differential analysis using the Kruskal-Wallis test within the R package. Perform differential analysis on species abundance profiles or functional abundance profiles using LEfSe.

Whole-spectrum metabolomics sequencing

The metabolomics detection platform employs Thermo Fisher Scientific’s UHPLC Q-Exactive HF-X ultra-high performance liquid chromatography-Fourier transform ion-pair mass spectrometry system for metabolomics analysis. Sample preparation procedure: Pipette precisely 200 µL of the biological sample into a 1.5 mL centrifuge tube. Add 800 µL pre-chilled extraction solvent (methanol: acetonitrile = 1:1, v/v) and vortex for 30 s to mix. Then perform ultrasonic extraction at 5 °C and 40 kHz for 30 min. After standing at −20 °C for 30 min to allow precipitation, centrifuge at 4 °C and 13,000 g for 15 min (using an Eppendorf 5430R centrifuge). Thoroughly dry the supernatant using a Shanghai JXDC-20 nitrogen evaporator. Add 120 µL of acetonitrile: water (1:1, v/v) resuspension buffer and sonicate at 5 °C for 5 min to ensure complete dissolution. Centrifuge again for 10 min under the same conditions. Transfer the supernatant to a dedicated sample vial and prepare a 20 µL mixed quality control (QC) sample.

Optimized chromatographic-mass spectrometry (LC-MS) analysis parameters are as follows:

Chromatographic separation system: Waters ACQUITY UPLC HSS T3 column (100 mm × 2.1 mm). Mobile phase composition: A: 95% water, 5% acetonitrile, containing 0.1% formic acid. B: 47.5% acetonitrile, 47.5% isopropanol, 5% water, containing 0.1% formic acid. Injection volume: 3 µL Column temperature: 40 °C.

Mass Spectrometry Detection System: Data acquisition employed dual-mode switching between positive and negative ions, with spray voltage set at ± 3500 V. The use of both positive and negative ion modes is a standard practice in untargeted metabolomics to maximize metabolite coverage. Positive ion mode is optimal for detecting basic compounds (e.g., amines, alkaloids), while negative ion mode is more sensitive for acidic metabolites (e.g., organic acids, nucleotides), thereby providing a more comprehensive profile of the metabolome.The mass scan range was m/z 70–1050, and resolution was set to 70,000 FWHM (measured at m/z 200). For quality control, one control sample was inserted after every 5 to 15 analyte samples to monitor system performance.

Whole-spectrum metabolomics data processing

Metabolome raw data underwent systematic processing using the Progenesis QI professional analysis platform from Waters Corporation (Milford, USA). Following multi-step data calibration and feature extraction, a high-dimensional data matrix was constructed containing three-dimensional information: chromatographic retention time, molecular mass-to-charge ratio (m/z), and ion peak intensity. Signal drift and batch effects were corrected using the LOESS (Locally Estimated Scatterplot Smoothing) normalization algorithm within the Progenesis QI software. The pooled quality control (QC) samples, injected at regular intervals throughout the analytical run, were used to model and correct the systematic technical variance across the acquisition sequence. This matrix comprehensively reflects the physicochemical characteristics and quantitative information of metabolic components, providing a standardized dataset for subsequent statistical analysis. During processing, features with missing values exceeding 20% in any group were first excluded. The remaining missing values were imputed using the minimum value. Subsequently, data normalization was performed by summing all numerical values. To ensure data quality, only metabolite features with a relative standard deviation (RSD) ≤ 30% in quality control (QC) samples were retained. The filtered dataset underwent a log10 transformation to normalize the data distribution for subsequent statistical analysis. Principal Component Analysis (PCA) was applied for dimensionality reduction and visualization, clearly presenting the spatial distribution characteristics of samples across different experimental groups. To further distinguish between groups, Partial Least Squares Discriminant Analysis (PLS-DA) was employed to assess the degree of sample separation. Variable importance in projection (VIP) values for each metabolite were obtained from the OPLS-DA model. The Kruskal-Wallis H test was employed to screen for metabolites differing between the two groups. To elucidate the effects of Bacillus coagulans supplementation on metabolites, differentially expressed metabolites were identified and validated using the Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG).

Integrated analysis of the Microbiome and metabolome

Based on VIP > 2 and p < 0.05, analyze the correlation between the top 10 dominant bacterial communities in the genus category and various metabolites and target metabolites in feces. Employ Bray-Curtis distance analysis to assess Spearman correlations between dominant bacterial communities and different metabolites and target metabolites, and visualize the results using a clustering heatmap.

Statistical analysis

Using Excel to process yak diarrhea data, calculate the diarrhea rate and diarrhea index. Grouping served as the independent variable, while biochemical indicators and short-chain fatty acids acted as dependent variables. Prior to statistical comparisons, all continuous data (including biochemical indicators and short-chain fatty acids) were tested for normality of distribution using the Shapiro-Wilk test and for homogeneity of variances using Levene’s test For data that met both the normality and homoscedasticity assumptions, one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) was employed to assess intergroup differences.For data that violated either the normality assumption or the homogeneity of variances assumption, the non-parametric Kruskal-Wallis H test was used for group comparisons.All statistical analyses were performed using SPSS software (Version 27), and results are presented as means ± SEM. A p-value of less than 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Metagenomic data statistics

The two treatment groups yielded a total of 758,869,524 raw reads across 10 samples, averaging 75,886,952 ± 4,648,758 raw reads per sample. After quality control, 758,449,858 clean reads were retained, averaging 75,844,985 ± 4,654,408 clean reads per sample. De novo assembly of the retained data generated 45,349,270 contigs, averaging 453,492 ± 133,938 contigs per sample. The N50 length for each sample was 1,453 ± 215 bp. Gene prediction identified a total of 7,328,240 open reading frames (ORFs) across all samples (Supplementary Material S1).

Microbial profiles of the two treatment groups

Metagenomic sequencing revealed differences in α-diversity indices between the experimental and control groups of yak fecal microbiota. The experimental group exhibited higher ACE, Chao1, and Simpson indices for fecal microbiota (Fig. 2 A, B, C), while the Simpson index was lower than that of the control group (Fig. 2D). This indicates that compared to the control group, adding Bacillus coagulans to the diet significantly increased species richness in the yak gut microbiota while simultaneously reducing community evenness. This pattern of change suggests that probiotic intervention does not uniformly promote the growth of all microorganisms. Instead, it may selectively enrich bacterial groups with specific functions, transforming them into new dominant populations and thereby leading to a restructuring of the community architecture. To further assess microbial community relationships across groups, we calculated β-diversity distances between samples using Bray-Curtis. PCA analysis revealed intra-group clustering and inter-group separation at the genus and phylum levels within fecal microbiomes, indicating robust clustering for subsequent studies (Fig. 3 A, B). Discussion

This study demonstrates that supplementing yak diets in high-altitude regions with Bacillus coagulans can enhance systemic immunity and antioxidant function by reshaping gut microbiota structure and promoting the production of beneficial metabolites such as short-chain fatty acids and specific anti-inflammatory substances, ultimately significantly reducing diarrhea incidence. This provides crucial theoretical and practical support for utilizing probiotics as a green control measure against yak diarrhea under the context of antibiotic restriction. A 60-day feeding trial comparing the CP and CG groups revealed that adding Bacillus coagulans to the feed significantly reduced the incidence of diarrhea in yaks in the CP group. While there was no significant difference in diarrhea frequency between the two groups during the first ten days, this may be because the colonization of the gut microbial niche and the restoration of homeostasis require time [16]. The experimental group showed a 33% reduction in recurrence rate compared to the CG group between days 10 and 30. and a 39% reduction from day 30 to day 60. This indicates that adding Bacillus coagulans to feed can reduce diarrhea incidence in yaks. To verify this, we analyzed the yaks’ blood biochemical indicators. Research shows that adding probiotics to animal diets can effectively eliminate free radicals and enhance the body’s antioxidant capacity [17]. Total Antioxidant Capacity (T-AOC) is a key indicator of overall antioxidant capacity [18]. Malondialdehyde (MDA), a major end-product of lipid peroxidation, reflects the extent of lipid peroxidation and thus indirectly indicates cellular damage [19]. Superoxide Dismutase (SOD) and Glutathione Peroxidase (GSH-Px) are primary antioxidant enzymes in the body; higher activity correlates with stronger free radical scavenging capacity [20]. Research indicates that supplementing lamb diets with Bacillus subtilis and selenium-enriched yeast significantly reduces MDA levels [21]. Additionally, adding Enterobacter faecalis enhances T-AOC levels in broiler chicken muscle, boosting antioxidant capacity [22]. Another study demonstrated that feeding Listeria acidophilus FT28 to piglets significantly increased superoxide dismutase activity in their red blood cells [23]. The results of this experiment indicate that T-AOC levels in the experimental group were significantly higher than those in the control group. While SOD and GSH-Px levels were also higher in the experimental group, the difference was not significant. Furthermore, MDA levels in the experimental group were significantly lower than those in the control group. These findings align with previous studies, confirming that feeding yak with Bacillus coagulans significantly enhances their antioxidant capacity.

Serum immunoglobulin concentrations reflect immune function and play a crucial role in protecting animals from viral infections [24]. IgM is the antibody produced during the initial phase of an immune response, while IgG plays a crucial role in systemic immune responses as the primary antibody in humoral immunity. IgA is the main antibody in mucosal immunity, primarily functioning to reduce pathogens with the assistance of non-specific immune defense mechanisms [25]. Furthermore, the concentration of immune cytokines in the blood can also reflect the state of cellular immunity [26]. In this experiment, the levels of IgM, IgG, IgA, and IL-10 in the experimental group were significantly higher than those in the control group. Research indicates that probiotics can activate mucosa-associated lymphoid tissue in the intestine, stimulate the secretion of secretory immunoglobulins, enhance immune recognition capabilities, induce immune cells to produce cytokines, activate the systemic immune system, and thereby strengthen the body’s immune function [27]. Moreover, elevated serum immunoglobulin levels are directly associated with reduced diarrhea incidence [28]. Additional research indicates that IL-10 selectively inhibits pro-inflammatory factor synthesis and receptor binding, mitigating inflammatory damage to the body [29]. These findings align with our study, demonstrating that supplementing feed with Bacillus coagulans enhances yak immune function and consequently lowers diarrhea rates.

The gut microbiota plays a crucial role in livestock health management, particularly in the prevention and control of diarrhea [30, 31]. Therefore, we conducted metagenomic analysis of yak feces and found that at the genus level, Phascolarctobacterium, Agathobacter, Roseburia, and others were significantly higher in the experimental group than in the control group (P < 0.05). Phascolarctobacterium is a microorganism capable of producing short-chain fatty acids (SCFAs), which play a vital role in maintaining gut health and regulating inflammatory responses. Research indicates that increased levels of this microorganism help improve the intestinal environment, enhance antioxidant capacity, and reduce inflammatory responses, thereby lowering the incidence of diarrhea [5]. Meanwhile, the microbes Agathobacter and Roseburia are important butyrate-producing genera in the gut. In animal studies, Roseburia significantly reduced inflammatory cytokines and elevated the anti-inflammatory factor IL-10 in DSS-induced colitis mice, alleviating diarrhea [32]. Agathobacter also produces propionic acid and lactic acid, which synergize with butyrate to maintain an acidic intestinal environment and inhibit pathogenic bacterial proliferation [33, 34]. In the control group, Clostridium abundance was significantly higher than in the experimental group. Clostridium is a primary pathogen causing hospital-acquired diarrhea. Its toxins A (TcdA) and B (TcdB) disrupt the intestinal epithelial barrier by inactivating host GTPases, leading to inflammation, diarrhea, and even pseudomembranous colitis [35, 36]. These findings indicate that adding Bacillus coagulans to feed promotes gut microbiota health likely by increasing the abundance of beneficial bacteria such as Phascolarctobacterium, Agathobacter, and Roseburia while reducing the abundance of harmful Clostridium. Furthermore, since Phascolarctobacterium, Agathobacter, and Roseburia can modulate inflammatory responses by increasing short-chain fatty acid (SCFA) levels, we also measured SCFA concentrations. Results revealed that the concentration of valeric acid in the experimental group was significantly higher than that in the control group (P < 0.05). Furthermore, the concentrations of propionic acid, lactic acid, succinic acid, butyric acid, and isobutyric acid in the experimental group were also higher than those in the control group, though not significantly (P > 0.05). Lactic acid, in particular, can lower intestinal pH, inhibit the growth of pathogenic microorganisms such as Escherichia coli and Salmonella, thereby reducing the incidence of diarrhea [37]. Short-chain fatty acids (SCFAs), including propionic acid, butyric acid, isobutyric acid, and valeric acid, are crucial regulators of intestinal health. These SCFAs provide energy to intestinal epithelial cells, enhance intestinal barrier function, regulate immune responses, and reduce diarrhea incidence [38–40]. These results indicate that adding Bacillus coagulans to feed can increase the abundance of short-chain fatty acid-producing microbial genera, thereby elevating short-chain fatty acid content, strengthening intestinal barrier function, and reducing diarrhea incidence.

We further analyzed the metabolomics data and found that the primary category of upregulated differential metabolites was Organic acids and their derivatives. KEGG analysis of these differential metabolites revealed the most significantly enriched pathways, including the biosynthesis of arginine and proline metabolism, histidine metabolism, glycerophospholipid metabolism, lysine metabolism, tryptophan metabolism, and β-alanine metabolism. Furthermore, we identified several anti-inflammatory metabolites among the upregulated compounds, including 5-(4-Methoxybenzylidene)−1,3-thiazolidine-2,4-dione, (8’S)-Latochrome, and N-Lauroyl Tyrosine. Among these, 5-(4-Methoxybenzylidene)−1,3-thiazolidine-2,4-dione belongs to the thiazolidinedione (TZD) class, known to suppress inflammation by activating PPARγ to inhibit the NF-κB pathway. In diarrhea models, TZDs may alleviate symptoms by regulating energy metabolism (e.g., the TCA cycle) and inflammatory responses [41, 42]. N-Lauroyl Tyrosine is a tyrosine derivative, and research indicates that tyrosine derivatives exhibit significant potential in antioxidant and anti-inflammatory effects, reducing reactive oxygen species (ROS) production and inhibiting the release of inflammatory mediators [43].

The above results indicate that Bacillus coagulans may alleviate diarrhea by increasing the abundance of certain microorganisms, thereby upregulating anti-inflammatory metabolites. To this end, we conducted a combined analysis of the metagenome and metabolome, as well as the metagenome and targeted metabolome. We found that the microbes Phascolarctobacterium, Agathobacter, and Roseburia were positively correlated with the anti-inflammatory metabolites 5-(4-Methoxybenzylidene)−1,3-thiazolidine-2,4-dione, (8’S)-Latochrome, and N-Lauroyl Tyrosine in the metabolome. Additionally, Phascolarctobacterium, Agathobacter, and Roseburia were positively correlated with lactic acid, butyric acid, valeric acid, and propionic acid.

In summary, we infer that adding Bacillus coagulans to feed increases the abundance of Phascolarctobacterium, Agathobacter, and Roseburia in yak intestines while reducing harmful Clostridium species. This, in turn, upregulates anti-inflammatory metabolites such as 5-(4-Methoxybenzylidene)−1,3-thiazolidine-2,4-dione, (8’S)-Latochrome, and N-Lauroyl Tyrosine, while increasing levels of short-chain fatty acids like lactic acid, butyric acid, valeric acid, and propionic acid. This improved the immune function and antioxidant capacity of the yaks, consequently reducing the incidence of diarrhea.

Although there are some findings, this study has several limitations that should be acknowledged. The primary limitation is the relatively small sample size (n = 10 per group), which, although sufficient to detect statistically significant changes in our primary outcomes, may limit the generalizability of the findings to the broader yak population and increase the susceptibility to outliers or chance findings. Future studies should include larger and more diverse cohorts from different geographical regions to validate and extend our conclusions.

Conclusions

Based on the research findings, we observed increased abundance of beneficial bacteria alongside upregulation of anti-inflammatory metabolites and reduced incidence of diarrhea. This suggests that Bacillus coagulans supplementation may alleviate diarrhea by modulating the gut ecosystem. Future studies should conduct fecal microbiota transplantation trials to directly validate this causal hypothesis. Additionally, subsequent research should prioritize expanding sample sizes to investigate the effects of different probiotic combinations and dose gradients on yak populations, thereby determining the optimal formulation.
