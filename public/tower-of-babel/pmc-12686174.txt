Thermal ablation versus surgical resection for colorectal liver metastases: a Grading of Recommendations, Assessment, Development, and Evaluation (GRADE)-assessed systematic review, survival meta-analysis, and meta-regression
Authors: Muhammad Ansab, Sepideh Razi, Shree Rath, Umama Alam, Noor Ul Huda Ramzan, Muhammad Burhan, Asad Iqbal, Anwaar Saeed
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundColorectal cancer is a leading cause of cancer-related mortality worldwide, with liver metastases occurring in nearly 50% of patients during the disease course. This study aims to compare survival outcomes, including overall survival (OS) and disease-free survival (DFS), and tumor recurrence risks between thermal ablation and surgical resection for colorectal liver metastases (CRLMs), incorporating recent randomized trial data to provide updated recommendations.MethodsSearches were performed across PubMed, Embase, and Web of Science to identify relevant studies comparing thermal ablation with surgical resection for CRLM. Meta-analyses were performed using random-effects models for pooled hazard ratios (HRs) and risk ratios (RRs) with corresponding 95% confidence intervals (CIs). Heterogeneity was assessed using I2 statistics, and publication bias was evaluated using funnel plot asymmetry and Egger’s regression test.ResultsA total of 29 studies involving 5,719 patients were included in the analysis. Thermal ablation was associated with significantly worse OS compared to surgical resection (HR =1.56; 95% CI: 1.25–1.94). Subgroup analyses revealed that radiofrequency ablation (RFA) notably increased mortality risk (HR =1.63; 95% CI: 1.26–2.51), whereas microwave ablation (MWA) demonstrated non-inferiority to surgical resection. For tumors <3 cm, no significant survival difference was observed between the interventions, but for lesions ≥3 cm, surgical resection showed improved survival (HR =1.49; 95% CI: 1.16–1.91). DFS analysis showed a higher recurrence risk with thermal ablation (HR =1.93; 95% CI: 1.45–2.57). Local tumor recurrence was over three times more frequent following ablation compared to resection (RR =3.03; 95% CI: 1.99–4.61), while distal recurrence rates did not differ significantly. Heterogeneity was substantial for survival outcomes (I2=68.0%) and recurrence outcomes (I2=74.9%).ConclusionsSurgical resection remains superior to thermal ablation in terms of OS and DFS, particularly for larger lesions (>3 cm). While thermal ablation, especially MWA, offers a promising alternative for smaller lesions, its increased risk of local recurrence warrants careful patient selection and precise procedural execution. These findings underscore the need for personalized, lesion-specific approaches to CRLM management, integrating advancements in ablation technology and hybrid surgical-ablation strategies.Keywords: Radiofrequency ablation (RFA), colorectal cancer, liver metastasis, surgery

Highlight box

Key findings

• Surgical resection provides superior overall survival (OS) and disease-free survival (DFS) compared to thermal ablation for colorectal liver metastases (CRLMs).

• Subgroup analysis showed that surgical resection was superior for lesions ≥3 cm, while for tumors <3 cm, there was no significant survival difference between interventions.

• Local tumor recurrence was over three times more frequent following ablation compared to resection, while no significant difference was observed in distal recurrence rates.

What is known and what is new?

• Surgical resection has traditionally been the gold standard for treating CRLM, but thermal ablation has emerged as a minimally invasive alternative.

• This meta-analysis, incorporating recent trial data, confirms that surgical resection remains superior to thermal ablation in terms of OS and DFS, especially for larger lesions (≥3 cm). It also highlights that microwave ablation (MWA) shows non-inferiority to surgical resection for smaller lesions, but thermal ablation carries an increased risk of local recurrence.

What is the implication, and what should change now?

• These findings underscore the need for a personalized, lesion-specific approach to CRLM management.

• Surgical resection remains the superior treatment, particularly for lesions >3 cm, while thermal ablation, specifically MWA, can be a promising alternative for smaller lesions.

• Careful patient selection and precise procedural execution are crucial due to the increased risk of local recurrence with thermal ablation.

Introduction

Background

Colorectal cancer is a leading cause of cancer-related mortality worldwide, with liver metastases occurring in nearly 50% of patients during the course of the disease (1). Surgical resection has traditionally been the gold standard for treating colorectal liver metastases (CRLMs), especially when metastases are few and small (≤3 cm) (2). However, in recent years, thermal ablation, including radiofrequency ablation (RFA) and microwave ablation (MWA), has emerged as a minimally invasive alternative that offers comparable local control with lower morbidity and shorter hospital stays (3). This shift in treatment paradigms has prompted a reassessment of whether or not thermal ablation may be equivalent to surgical resection in small-size CRLM, particularly when long-term outcomes such as overall survival (OS) and disease-free survival (DFS) are considered (2,4,5).

Rationale and knowledge gap

Historically, survival outcomes were thought to favor surgical resection, but recent data challenge this assumption. The landmark COLLISION trial, a multicenter randomized phase 3 study, provided robust evidence that thermal ablation is non-inferior to surgical resection in terms of OS, DFS, and local tumor progression-free survival (LTPFS) in patients with CRLM ≤3 cm (6). This builds on prior work by Puijk et al., who reported improved outcomes for thermal ablation in small-sized lesions in a 10-year registry analysis (2). Chlorogiannis et al. further emphasized that precise ablation margins are critical to achieving oncologic outcomes comparable to surgery (4). Other studies reinforce these findings while highlighting important clinical considerations. Filippiadis et al. reviewed the role of percutaneous ablation and concluded that thermal techniques are especially effective when lesions are small and limited in number (7). A recent study explored the prognostic role of biomarkers in patients undergoing MWA, demonstrating that certain biological factors may predict survival and recurrence, assisting in patient selection (8). These studies together challenge the notion that resection is categorically superior and point toward a nuanced, lesion-specific treatment approach. While prior meta-analyses have evaluated local recurrence and technical success, few have comprehensively addressed survival parameters particularly in the context of recent randomized data (9). Additionally, earlier reviews often restricted inclusion to specific modalities, limiting generalizability.

Objective

The clinical implications of this updated meta-analysis are substantial, as it includes recently published trials and cohort studies with robust OS and DFS data, while defining thermal ablation broadly to encompass RFA, MWA, and combined modalities as applicable. This inclusive Population, Intervention, Comparision, and Outcome framework allows for a more representative synthesis of contemporary evidence. Thermal ablation continues to gain favor as a first-line option for patients ineligible for surgery or seeking less invasive alternatives. With improving image guidance and ablation protocols, its role may further expand as data accumulates, showing comparable survival to surgery. Therefore, given the growing equivalence in survival endpoints and the expanding indications for thermal ablation, this review will provide an updated evidence-based benchmark to guide multidisciplinary decision-making in liver oncology care by incorporating survival endpoints and new trial data. We present this article in accordance with the PRISMA reporting checklist (10) (available at https://tcr.amegroups.com/article/view/10.21037/tcr-2025-1726/rc).

Methods

Study design and protocol registration

This systematic review was conducted following the Cochrane Collaboration guidelines (11). The review addressed study design, stepwise execution, data analysis, and the presentation of results. Furthermore, the study protocol was registered in International Prospective Register of Systematic Reviews (PROSPERO) under the registration number CRD420251082240.

Search strategy and databases

An electronic search of PubMed, Embase, and Web of Science was conducted, covering all available entries from their inception to May 2025, without any language restrictions. The following keywords were used: (“Percutaneous Microwave Ablation” OR “Microwave Ablation” OR “Thermal Ablation” OR “Ablation Therapy”) AND (“Open Surgical Resection” OR “Surgical Resection” OR “Hepatic Resection” OR “Liver Surgery” OR “Surgery”) AND (“Colorectal Cancer” OR “Colorectal Neoplasms” OR “Colorectal Carcinoma” OR “Colon Cancer” OR “Rectal Cancer”) AND (“Liver Metastasis” OR “Hepatic Metastasis” OR “Colorectal Liver Metastasis” OR “CRLM”). A detailed description of the search strategy can be found in Table S1.

Study selection and eligibility criteria

All studies identified through the online search were imported into Rayyan software for screening, and duplicate records were removed. The remaining studies were initially screened based on their titles and abstracts. Full-text articles were retrieved for further assessment if either reviewer found the abstract potentially relevant. Two independent reviewers (U.A. and A.I.) evaluated the eligibility of each study according to predefined inclusion criteria. Any disagreements were resolved through discussion and consultation with a third reviewer (M.A.).

Studies were included if they met the following criteria: (I) involved patients with colorectal cancer having metastasis to the liver; (II) thermal, MWA, or radioablation as the intervention; (III) surgical resection as the control; (IV) reported at least one relevant outcome.

Studies were excluded if they met any of the following criteria: (I) overlapping populations, identified by the same institutions and recruitment periods; (II) populations not relevant to the study’s focus; (III) previously published reports; (IV) protocols lacking reported outcomes; (V) reviews, abstracts, case reports or series, background articles, expert opinions, or in vitro/in vivo experiments; (VI) duplicate data originating from the same clinical trial; or (VII) absence of a comparator group.

Data extraction and outcomes

Two independent authors (S.R. and U.A.) extracted data from the included studies into an Excel sheet using a pre-piloted form. Study characteristics included study type, country, follow-up duration, ablation type, and sample size. Baseline data included age, tumor size, colon involvement, synchronous metastasis, metachronous metastasis, solitary metastasis, multiple metastases, open approach, percutaneous approach, and anatomical resection. The outcomes of this study included OS, DFS, local recurrence, and distal recurrence.

Quality assessment

The methodological quality of the included studies was evaluated using tools appropriate to each study design. For randomized controlled trials (RCTs), we employed the Revised Cochrane Risk of Bias Tool for Randomized Trials (RoB 2) (12), which examines five domains: the randomization process, deviations from intended interventions, missing outcome data, outcome measurement, and selective reporting. Each trial was assigned an overall risk of bias as low, some concerns, or high.

Observational studies were appraised using the Newcastle-Ottawa Scale (NOS) (13), which considers three aspects: selection, comparability, and outcomes. The NOS scores were converted to AHRQ quality standards (good, fair, poor) for consistency. Two reviewers (A.I. and M.B.) independently conducted the assessments, and any disagreements were resolved through discussion or consultation with a third reviewer (U.A.). In the NOS, the selection domain could receive up to four stars, comparability up to two stars, and outcomes up to three stars. Studies scoring 7–9 stars were considered low risk of bias, those with 5–6 stars were rated as having some concerns, and studies scoring below 5 stars were classified as high risk, ensuring a thorough evaluation of study quality.

Grade assessment

Two independent authors (A.I. and U.A.) assessed the certainty of evidence in this meta-analysis using the Grading of Recommendations, Assessment, Development, and Evaluation (GRADE) approach via the GRADEpro Guideline Development Tool (14). Evidence was classified into four levels, from high to very low (15). Any disagreements between the reviewers were resolved through discussion and consensus.

Statistical analysis

Pooled effect estimates with 95% confidence intervals (CIs) were calculated using the inverse variance method. A random-effects model was applied for outcomes exhibiting substantial heterogeneity, while a fixed-effect model was used when heterogeneity was minimal. Survival outcomes (OS and DFS) were pooled as hazard ratios (HRs), whereas outcomes such as proximal and distal tumor recurrence were pooled as risk ratios (RRs) with corresponding 95% CIs and presented using forest plots.

Between-study heterogeneity was assessed by estimating the between-study variance (τ2) using the restricted maximum likelihood (REML) method. CIs for both τ2 and τ were derived using the Q-profile method. Heterogeneity was interpreted using I2 statistics, categorized as low (0–<25%), moderate (25–50%), or substantial (>50%).

To evaluate studies affecting the heterogeneity disproportionately, influential diagnostics were performed, including leave-one-out sensitivity analysis. Publication bias was assessed using contour-enhanced funnel plots, which demarcate regions of statistical significance to improve interpretability. These plots also incorporate study-level power assessments, displaying the median power across studies, and present the results of the test for excess significance, as well as the Replicability Index (16). Funnel plot asymmetry was statistically evaluated using Egger’s regression test (17). All statistical analyses were conducted using R (version 4.4.2) with RStudio (2024.10.31 + 87279; R Core Team, Vienna, Austria).

Results

Study selection

Using the aforementioned search strategies on different databases, a total of 2,259 records were identified from duplicate records (n=667), and 1,592 studies remained for title and abstract screening. Following the screening process, 1,433 studies were excluded based on title and abstract evaluation. One hundred fifty-nine full-text articles were assessed for eligibility; finally, 29 studies were included in the qualitative and quantitative synthesis. Study designs varied, including retrospective cohorts (n=24) (18-41), prospective cohorts (n=4) (42-45), and one RCT (5). Meta-analysis of outcomes

Survival parameters

OS

A total of 15 studies were included in the quantitative synthesis comparing OS between thermal ablation and hepatic resection for CRLM. The pooled HR for OS significantly favored hepatic resection over ablation, with an HR of 1.56 (95% CI: 1.25–1.94; P<0.001), indicating a 56% increased risk of mortality associated with ablation (Figure 2). Statistical heterogeneity was substantial (I2=68.0%), reflecting notable between-study variability. Influence analysis did not identify any single study as the primary driver of this heterogeneity (Figures S1,S2).

Forest plot of OS comparing thermal ablation and surgical resection for CRLMs. CI, confidence interval; CRLMs, colorectal liver metastases; HR, hazard ratio; IV, inverse variance; OS, overall survival; SE, standard error.

When stratified by ablation modality, the survival benefit of hepatic resection remained consistent in comparison to RFA, with a pooled HR of 1.63 (95% CI: 1.26–2.11; P<0.001). However, no statistically significant difference in OS was observed when hepatic resection was compared to MWA (Figure 3A). The test for subgroup differences was significant (P<0.001), suggesting that ablation modality significantly modifies the treatment effect. Notably, heterogeneity decreased to I2=0% in the MWA subgroup, whereas it remained moderate (I2=50%) in the RFA subgroup.

Subgroup meta-analyses of OS comparing thermal ablation and hepatic resection for CRLMs, stratified by (A) ablation modality and (B) tumor size. CI, confidence interval; CRLMs, colorectal liver metastases; HR, hazard ratio; IV, inverse variance; MWA, microwave ablation; OS, overall survival; RFA, radiofrequency ablation; SE, standard error.

Tumor size also emerged as an effective covariate modifying the outcome results. For tumors <3 cm in diameter, there was no statistically significant difference in OS between resection and ablation. In contrast, for tumors ≥3 cm, hepatic resection was associated with significantly improved survival (HR =1.49; 95% CI: 1.16–1.91; P<0.001) (Figure 3B). Age-stratified analyses revealed a survival benefit for hepatic resection, predominantly in patients aged <65 years. In this subgroup, the likelihood of survival was 76% higher for patients undergoing hepatic resection compared to ablation (Figure S3).

To explore potential sources of heterogeneity, meta-regression was conducted using ablation type as a moderator across 13 studies. RFA was found to be a statistically significant moderator of the effect size. The regression coefficient for RFA was 0.4124 (P=0.022; 95% CI: 0.0608–0.7640), indicating a higher HR associated with RFA use, and thus increased mortality risk (Figure S4). This model explained a substantial proportion of between-study heterogeneity (R2=40.2%), suggesting that ablation modality partially accounts for the observed variability in outcomes. A separate regression analysis using tumor size as a continuous moderator demonstrated a non-significant trend (P=0.072; QM =3.2364), with limited explanatory power for residual heterogeneity (I2=19.7%). Similar results were seen with age as a moderator, contributing 18.28% to heterogeneity, with a non-significant trend (QM =3.29; P=0.07) (Figures S5,S6).

Egger’s regression test did not indicate significant small-study effects (P=0.793). However, inspection of the power-enhanced sunset funnel plot revealed an overrepresentation of small, potentially underpowered studies, raising concerns regarding the robustness of pooled estimates. These findings highlight the need for cautious interpretation, as the overall evidential base may be influenced by publication bias and methodological limitations (Figure S7).

DFS

Seven studies reported HRs for DFS comparing thermal ablation with hepatic resection in patients with CRLM. The pooled analysis showed a significantly higher risk of disease recurrence or progression following ablation (HR =1.93; 95% CI: 1.45–2.57; P<0.001), favoring hepatic resection (Figure 4).

Forest plot of DFS comparing thermal ablation versus hepatic resection in patients with CRLMs. CI, confidence interval; CRLMs, colorectal liver metastases; DFS, disease-free survival; HR, hazard ratio; IV, inverse variance; SE, standard error.

Substantial heterogeneity was observed across studies (I2=74.9%). Influence analysis identified one study (Kim et al., 2011) (33) as a potential outlier; exclusion of this study reduced heterogeneity to 61.7%, while exclusion of other studies had minimal impact (Figures S8,S9).

Meta-regression using mean tumor size as a continuous covariate did not explain a significant proportion of this heterogeneity (QM =1.47; P=0.22), accounting for 16% of between-study variance. Meta-regression using ablation type (QM =0.39, P=0.53) and age (QM =0.47; P=0.49) did not account for heterogeneity as well (Figures S10-S12). Power-enhanced funnel plot analysis showed no major asymmetry, and median statistical power was high (90.1%), suggesting that the results are unlikely to be affected by publication bias. The R-index of 100% further supports the robustness and replicability of the results (Figure S13).

Tumor recurrence

Proximal (local) tumor recurrence

A total of 16 studies reported data on local or proximal tumor recurrence. Pooled analysis demonstrated that patients undergoing thermal ablation had a significantly higher risk of local tumor recurrence compared to those undergoing hepatic resection, with an RR of 3.03 (95% CI: 1.99–4.61; P<0.001). This finding indicates a more than threefold increased risk of local recurrence following ablation (Figure 5).

Forest plot comparing the risk of local or proximal tumor recurrence between thermal ablation and hepatic resection. CI, confidence interval; IV, inverse variance.

Substantial heterogeneity was observed across studies (I2=65.5%). Sensitivity analysis revealed that exclusion of the study by van der Lei et al. [2025] (5) reduced heterogeneity to 52.3%, whereas the omission of any other individual study did not significantly impact heterogeneity (Figures S14,S15). Assessment for small-study effects using Egger’s regression test was non-significant (P=0.068), and the contour-enhanced funnel plot showed that most studies were located in regions of high statistical power, suggesting robustness of the observed effect (Figure S16).

Distal tumor recurrence

Thirteen studies reported on distal recurrence. In contrast to local recurrence, the risk of distal tumor recurrence did not differ significantly between thermal ablation and hepatic resection (RR =1.07; 95% CI: 0.85–1.33; P=0.57) (Figure 6).

Forest plot comparing the risk of distal tumor recurrence between thermal ablation and hepatic resection. CI, confidence interval; IV, inverse variance.

Heterogeneity among studies was substantial (I2=68%), which decreased to 54.9% upon exclusion of the study by Abdalla et al. [2004]. No other individual study substantially influenced the between-study variance (Figures S17,S18).

Egger’s test indicated potential small-study effects (P=0.047), and the contour-enhanced funnel plot suggested that many included studies were underpowered to detect differences in distal recurrence risk, limiting the interpretability of this outcome (Figure S19).

Quality assessment

The risk of bias of the RCT assessed using the RoB 2 tool classified Lie 2025 as having some concerns due to issues in the measurement of outcomes domain. The bias in the observational studies, as assessed through the NOS, was rated as low, with studies scoring between 7 and 9. A detailed assessment is presented in Figures S20,S21 and Table S2.

Certainty of evidence

The certainty of evidence for comparing thermal ablation with surgical resection in patients with CRLM was assessed using the GRADE framework via the GRADE Guideline Development Tool. Overall, the quality of evidence ranged from very low to moderate. Outcomes such as proximal recurrence were supported by moderate-certainty evidence, while distal recurrence was based on low-certainty evidence. In contrast, key survival outcomes, including OS and DFS, were rated as having very low certainty. The primary reasons for downgrading included: serious inconsistency, due to substantial heterogeneity (I2>50%), imprecision, particularly due to wide CIs crossing the null effect, study design limitations, since all included studies were non-randomized. Detailed GRADE ratings and explanatory notes are provided in Table S3.

Discussion

This comprehensive meta-analysis included evidence from 29 studies comparing thermal ablation and surgical resection for CRLM, with a focus on survival outcomes and tumor recurrence patterns. Our findings suggest that surgical resection remains superior in terms of OS and DFS across most patient subgroups.

Our results indicate a statistically significant survival advantage for surgical resection over thermal ablation, with a pooled HR of 1.56 for OS. This suggests that, on average, patients undergoing thermal ablation had a 56% increased risk of mortality compared to those undergoing resection. These findings align with prior retrospective analyses (46,47), yet they also provide updated evidence in light of the recent COLLISION trial (5). Notably, this meta-analysis improves upon previous ones by incorporating recent RCTs such as COLLISION, evaluating a broader spectrum of ablation modalities, and applying the GRADE framework to assess the certainty of evidence. The survival difference was predominantly observed with RFA, while MWA demonstrated non-inferiority to resection in subgroup analyses, likely due to its ability to achieve higher intratumoral temperatures and more uniform ablation margins (42,48).

Moreover, tumor size acted as a critical effect modifier. In patients with metastases <3 cm, no significant survival difference was observed, reinforcing the COLLISION trial’s non-inferiority findings. This suggests that small lesions may be optimally managed with either modality, provided appropriate patient selection and technical execution. In contrast, larger tumors (≥3 cm) were associated with significantly worse outcomes following ablation. This is consistent with findings by Tan et al. (49), who emphasized that lesion size >3 cm is predictive of incomplete ablation and recurrence.

Age also emerged as an important clinical factor. In our age-stratified analysis, the survival advantage of surgical resection was most evident in patients under 65 years, where resection was associated with a 76% higher likelihood of survival compared to ablation. This is consistent with the general consensus that younger patients derive greater oncologic benefit from surgery due to better physiological reserve and fewer comorbidities. In older patients ≥65 years), the survival difference between modalities narrowed, possibly reflecting the greater influence of competing risks, perioperative morbidity, and a preference for less invasive strategies in this group (50).

To further explore effect heterogeneity, we conducted a meta-regression using ablation type as a moderator. RFA was significantly associated with poorer survival outcomes, suggesting that its use contributed to the higher pooled HR observed for ablation. This is consistent with a recent analysis that showed superior local control and long-term survival with MWA compared to RFA, likely due to MWA’s capacity to generate higher and more homogeneous intratumoral temperatures (51).

Thermal ablation was also associated with inferior DFS, with a pooled HR of 1.93, indicating nearly twice the risk of disease recurrence compared to resection. Notably, this finding persisted across various subgroups and sensitivity analyses. The worse DFS observed with ablation may reflect microscopic residual disease, which surgical resection can address more definitively, particularly when anatomical resections are performed. These outcomes mirror those reported in Gomez et al. (52) and may reflect the limitations of current imaging modalities to delineate exact tumor boundaries during percutaneous procedures (53). However, we observed substantial heterogeneity across studies (I2=74.9%). Influence analysis identified the study by Kim et al. (33) as a potential outlier. Excluding this study reduced heterogeneity to 61.7%, while the exclusion of other studies had a minimal impact. Although Kim et al. reported significantly worse DFS following RFA for tumors ≥3 cm, their findings did not show a difference in DFS or OS for tumors <3 cm. This tumor-size stratification supports our subgroup results but may have exaggerated heterogeneity due to the study’s retrospective design, heterogeneous population, and treatment era spanning over a decade earlier than several other included studies. Additionally, the study focused exclusively on RFA and was conducted at a time when imaging guidance and ablation techniques were less advanced, potentially contributing to poorer outcomes and greater variability in effect size.

Our results confirm a significantly higher risk of local (proximal) tumor recurrence following ablation (RR =3.03), consistent with prior reviews (54). This emphasizes the importance of achieving adequate ablation margins of ideally ≥10 mm, especially when using RFA, which is more susceptible to heat-sink effects near major vessels. MWA, by contrast, may offer better local control due to reduced sensitivity to these effects (55). Substantial heterogeneity was present (I2=65.5%), largely influenced by the recent RCT by van der Lei et al. [2025] (5), whose exclusion reduced heterogeneity to 52.3%. Notably, van der Lei et al. reported no significant differences in LTPFS between ablation and resection groups on both per-patient (HR =0.82; 95% CI: 0.44–1.45; P=0.53) and per-tumor analyses (HR =0.82; 95% CI: 0.47–1.43; P=0.48), with median local progression-free survival not reached in either group. Furthermore, local control analyses favored ablation: the experimental ablation group was non-inferior per patient (HR =0.13; 95% CI: 0.02–1.06; P=0.057) and superior per tumor (HR =0.09; 95% CI: 0.01–0.74; P=0.024) compared to resection. These findings highlight a divergence from prior retrospective studies and meta-analyses that have consistently shown higher local recurrence risk following ablation, particularly with RFA.

Interestingly, the risk of distal recurrence did not differ significantly between the groups, suggesting that systemic disease biology rather than local treatment dictates extrahepatic progression, supporting the need for adjuvant systemic therapy irrespective of local modality.

Our findings advocate for a personalized, lesion-specific approach to CRLM management. Surgical resection should remain the preferred strategy in patients with favorable surgical profiles, especially when lesions exceed 3 cm or when complete anatomical resection is feasible. However, in selected patients, particularly those with solitary, small (<3 cm), peripheral lesions, thermal ablation, especially MWA, represents a viable alternative with comparable long-term outcomes and lower morbidity. Emerging technologies, including stereotactic navigation and intra-procedural imaging, may improve the precision of ablation, potentially narrowing the efficacy gap between ablation and surgery (56,57). Moreover, hybrid approaches combining resection and ablation are gaining traction for multifocal disease, as they allow for organ preservation while achieving complete local control (58).

Despite the inclusion of recent high-quality studies, this meta-analysis is limited by several factors. The majority of included studies were observational, introducing potential for residual confounding despite statistical adjustments. While meta-regression and subgroup analyses partially accounted for heterogeneity, unmeasured variables such as margin status, anatomical location, comorbidity burden, and surgeon/interventionist expertise could not be uniformly controlled. The overall certainty of evidence for survival outcomes was rated as “very low” by GRADE, showing both the predominance of non-randomized studies and the substantial heterogeneity observed. Between-study heterogeneity was particularly notable for OS (I2=68%) and DFS (I2=75%), which underscores that pooled results should be interpreted cautiously and primarily as hypothesis-generating rather than definitive evidence. Influence analyses identified the Kim et al. (33) and van der Lei et al. (5) studies as significant contributors to this variability. The Kim study, with its earlier treatment era and retrospective design, reported more extreme effects on DFS and local recurrence, contributing to increased heterogeneity. Conversely, the van der Lei trial, while of high methodological quality, reported comparatively more favorable local control and safety outcomes following ablation, which tempered the pooled effect sizes and introduced variability in the opposite direction. These findings highlight how evolving imaging modalities, improved intraprocedural navigation, and the shift from RFA toward more effective technologies, such as MWA, may progressively decrease heterogeneity and narrow outcome differences with surgery. Hence, while current evidence supports resection as superior for larger tumors and younger patients, continued refinement of ablation protocols and real-time image-guided techniques could change the therapeutic landscape over time.

Conclusions

In conclusion, while thermal ablation, especially MWA, offers a compelling, minimally invasive alternative for select patients with CRLM, surgical resection remains superior in terms of OS and DFS, particularly in larger tumors and younger patients. The local recurrence risk remains higher with ablation, although distal recurrence rates are comparable. These findings support a nuanced, individualized approach to CRLM management that considers tumor size, patient age, modality used, and evolving technology. High-quality randomized trials comparing modern MWA protocols to surgery, as well as biomarker-driven stratification, are urgently needed to refine the therapeutic algorithm and optimize outcomes.
