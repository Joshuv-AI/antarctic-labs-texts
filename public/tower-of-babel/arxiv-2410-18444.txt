Evaluating Automatic Speech Recognition Systems for Korean Meteorological Experts
ChaeHun Park Hojun Cho Jaegul Choo

Abstract
This paper explores integrating Automatic Speech Recognition (ASR) into natural language query systems to improve weather forecasting efficiency for Korean meteorologists. We address challenges in developing ASR systems for the Korean weather domain, specifically specialized vocabulary and Korean linguistic intricacies. To tackle these issues, we constructed an evaluation dataset of spoken queries recorded by native Korean speakers. Using this dataset, we assessed various configurations of a multilingual ASR model family, identifying performance limitations related to domain-specific terminology. We then implemented a simple text-to-speech-based data augmentation method, which improved the recognition of specialized terms while maintaining general-domain performance. Our contributions include creating a domain-specific dataset, comprehensive ASR model evaluations, and an effective augmentation technique. We believe our work provides a foundation for future advancements in ASR for the Korean weather forecasting domain. 1 1 1 Our code and dataset will be publicly available.

Introduction

Meteorological experts utilize diverse data sources for weather forecasting.
The database systems they use are vast and complex, making it challenging for those without extensive system knowledge to access the necessary information.
Natural language query systems can assist the experts by allowing them to input queries in natural language.
The system translates these queries into database queries (e.g., SQL) to search the database
(
Zhong et al. 2017
;
Kim et al. 2020
;
Deng et al. 2022
)
.
This process enables forecasters to efficiently access and interpret complex databases without writing complex and structured queries themselves.
Notably,
Jo et al. 2023
developed an integrated search system for Korean weather data, enabling users to query extensive meteorological information using natural language.
This system simplifies data retrieval and thereby improves operational efficiency.

Incorporating Automatic Speech Recognition (ASR) into these systems can improve efficiency by enabling voice-activated queries, which is beneficial for meteorologists who often multitask.
However, developing an effective ASR system for the Korean weather domain presents challenges. Most existing ASR models, primarily trained on general domains, may struggle with the specialized vocabulary and pronunciation of meteorological terms
(
Yadav and Sitaram 2022
)
.
Additionally, despite being trained in multiple languages, many recent multilingual ASR models often underperform in non-English languages, including Korean, due to their unique linguistic characteristics and limited representation in training data
(
Li et al. 2021
;
Radford et al. 2023
;
Ferraz et al. 2024
;
Song et al. 2024
)
. The agglutinative nature and phonetic complexity of Korean add to these challenges
(
Lee et al. 2019
;
Cho et al. 2020
)
. Therefore, the combination of Korean language characteristics and domain-specific vocabulary in meteorology makes developing an effective ASR system particularly difficult.

To investigate the extent of these challenges, we constructed a specialized evaluation dataset to assess how well ASR models perform in the Korean weather domain.
We recorded 2,000 spoken queries derived from the natural language dataset created by
Jo et al. 2023
.
Five native Korean speakers recorded the queries, following pronunciation guidelines from domain experts to ensure accurate pronunciation of domain-specific terms.
Our evaluation dataset underwent rigorous manual validation to ensure its accuracy and reliability, making it suitable for evaluating ASR systems specifically in the meteorological domain.

Using this dataset, we evaluated popular multilingual ASR models
(
Radford et al. 2023
)
in different configurations.
Training ASR models with general-domain Korean speech data
(
Bang et al. 2020
)
led to decreased performance, highlighting the challenges posed by distributional shifts in specialized domains.
For instance, as shown in Fig.
1
, both a zero-shot model and a model fine-tuned on a general domain dataset failed to accurately transcribe domain-specific terms (e.g.,
weather map
,
average wind speed
, and
KIM
(
Korean Integrated Model
)) and unit system (e.g.,
hPa
).
These results revealed performance issues with domain-specific terminology, underscoring the need for models tailored to this specialized vocabulary.
To resolve this issue, we employed a text-to-speech-based data augmentation method, which improved the recognition of specialized terms while maintaining performance on general-domain ASR tasks.
We believe our findings provide valuable insights for developing effective domain-specific ASR systems, particularly within the Korean weather forecasting domain. Our contributions can be summarized as follows:

•

We developed a specialized evaluation dataset for Korean weather-related ASR tasks by recording and validating spoken queries from native Korean speakers.

•

We conducted evaluations using different model configurations, identifying areas where performance could be improved with domain-specific dataset creation.

•

We proposed and demonstrated the effectiveness of a text-to-speech-based data augmentation method to improve ASR performance in recognizing specialized meteorological terms.

Related Work

Speech Recognition for Specialized Domains

Speech recognition technology has been extensively studied in various specialized domains, such as medical
(
Le-Duc 2024
)
and financial fields
(
O’Neill et al. 2021
)
.
One major challenge is accurately handling domain-specific terminology.

Zheng et al. 2021
demonstrated that synthetic speech data enhances the recognition of out-of-vocabulary words in domain-specific contexts.

Shamsian et al. 2024
propose a keyword-guided adaptation to improve the recognition accuracy of models for specialized terms.
Our research specifically focuses on the Korean meteorological domain, creating an evaluation dataset tailored to assess ASR performance in this specialized field.

Speech Recognition for Korean

Research on ASR for non-English languages, including Korean, presents unique challenges.
Multilingual ASR models, such as Whisper
(
Radford et al. 2023
)
, often underperform with languages like Korean due to unique linguistic characteristics and limited representation in training datasets
(
Li et al. 2021
;
Yadav and Sitaram 2022
;
Ferraz et al. 2024
;
Song et al. 2024
)
.
This performance drop is often attributed to the lower proportion and diversity of non-English data in training sets.
Efforts to improve ASR for Korean include datasets like
KsponSpeech
, a spontaneous speech corpus, and
ClovaCall
, a goal-oriented dialog speech corpus, provide valuable resources for developing and evaluating Korean ASR systems
(
Bang et al. 2020
;
Ha et al. 2020
)
.
Our research evaluates ASR performance specifically within the Korean meteorological domain, using a tailored evaluation dataset to identify and address domain-specific performance limitations.

Dataset Construction

This study aims to develop a specialized dataset for evaluating Automatic Speech Recognition (ASR) systems within the Korean meteorological domain.
The dataset comprises 5,500 spoken queries derived from natural language questions.
The construction process includes several key steps: utilizing the natural language question dataset from
Jo et al. 2023
, obtaining pronunciation guidelines for specialized terms from domain experts, collecting speech data from native speakers, and verifying the correctness of the recorded audio.
The detailed data construction pipeline (
section

3.1
) and the analysis of the resulting dataset (
section

3.2
) are as follows.

3.1
Data Construction Pipeline

Acquiring Natural Language Questions

We began by utilizing the natural language question dataset released by
Jo et al. 2023
.
This dataset includes a diverse range of questions relevant to the Korean meteorological domain, providing a robust foundation for our spoken query dataset.
The dataset consists of two subsets: URL and SQL.
We selected 3,575 questions from the URL subset and 1,925 questions from the SQL subset to ensure comprehensive coverage of various meteorological topics that forecasters typically seek.
Note that the original dataset in
Jo et al. 2023
contains natural language queries mapped to their corresponding structured queries (e.g., SQL).
For our purposes, we used only natural language queries, focusing on how these questions would be articulated by users in real-world scenarios.
For more details about natural language questions, please refer to the original paper of the dataset
(
Jo et al. 2023
)
.

Obtaining Pronunciation Guidelines from Domain Experts

To ensure the accurate pronunciation of specialized meteorological terminology, we collaborated with domain experts. These experts provided comprehensive guidelines on how to pronounce specific terms correctly. For example, the acronym
"BUFR"
should be pronounced as
"buffer"
and
"AMI"
as
"A-M-I."
Additionally, terms such as
"33009 station"
could be pronounced as
"three three zero zero nine station"
or
"thirty-three thousand and nine station."
These detailed instructions were crucial for maintaining the linguistic integrity of the dataset, ensuring that all domain-specific terms were articulated accurately by the speakers.

Collecting Speech Data from Native Speakers

Eleven native Korean speakers were recruited to record the spoken queries.
Seven were male, and four were female, all in their twenties.
Each participant was given the pronunciation guidelines and detailed instructions on how to record the questions.
Participants were compensated at a rate of 16,000 KRW (approximately 11.6 USD) per 100 queries.
The recordings were conducted in controlled environments to minimize background noise and ensure high audio quality.
This phase resulted in a substantial collection of audio recordings that correspond to the natural language questions.

Verifying the Recorded Voices

Following the collection of speech data, a rigorous verification process was implemented.
Human verifiers listened to each recording to ensure accuracy and adherence to the pronunciation guidelines.
They also checked for clarity and the absence of background noise or errors.
Two of the authors are employed for this verification process.
This process was essential to guarantee the quality and reliability of the dataset for subsequent ASR evaluation.

4
Experimental Setup

4.1
Evaluation Datasets

We use our Korean ASR dataset for the weather domain to evaluate different ASR models.
The dataset comprises 5,500 spoken queries, which we randomly split into 500 samples for the development set and 5,000 samples for the test set.
Additionally, we utilize the
eval-clean
test set from the
KsponSpeech
dataset
(
Bang et al. 2020
)
to evaluate the models in a general conversation domain.
This dataset consists of 3,000 audio and their corresponding answer transcriptions.
This approach allows us to compare models across both domain-specific and general ASR tasks.

4.2
Metrics

We use Character Error Rate (CER) and Word Error Rate (WER) as evaluation metrics to measure the distances between the prediction and ground-truth transcription.
Additionally, we employ a space-normalized Word Error Rate (sWER)
(
Bang et al. 2020
)
, which accounts for the flexibility and variations of space rules in Korean.

4.3
Models and Training Datasets

Zero-shot Multilingual Models

For our experiments, we used the multilingual Whisper model family

Radford et al. 2023
)

medium
(769M), and
large-v2
(1550M). These pre-trained models were assessed in a zero-shot manner to evaluate their performance without additional fine-tuning on our dataset. The target language and task in Whisper’s prefix tokens were set to
Korean
and
transcribe
, respectively. Additionally, for evaluation in the weather domain, we tested Google’s commercial speech-to-text API

Data Augmentation for Meteorological Domain

Inspired by
Zheng et al. 2021
, we hypothesize that teaching the model the pronunciation of specialized terms in the weather domain is crucial for accurate transcription.
To facilitate this, we used a simple text-to-speech (TTS) system to generate audio for natural language queries in the weather domain, which was then used for training.
We selected 10k and 9.8k instances from the URL and SQL subset of
Jo et al. 2023
as the training queries.
To avoid test set leakage, the natural language queries in our human-recorded evaluation dataset were excluded from these training queries.
Using the Google TTS service
3
3

3

https://gtts.readthedocs.io/
, we generated audio for the selected queries.
English words in the queries were converted to their Korean pronunciations before being provided to the TTS service based on expert guidelines described in Section
3.1
.
The resulting TTS-generated audio dataset was used as a training dataset.
We fine-tuned the Whisper large-v2 either solely on the TTS-based datasets or on both the TTS-based datasets and the KsponSpeech dataset, resulting in two different ASR models.

Post-processing with Unimodal LLMs

To evaluate the effectiveness of large language models (LLMs) in refining ASR transcriptions without utilizing acoustic information
(
Chen et al. 2023
;
Hu et al. 2024a
)
, we conducted an experiment where GPT-4o-mini
(
Achiam et al. 2023
)
post-processed the top-1 transcription from Whisper-large-v2. Specifically, we provided the LLM with the single best output from the ASR model. The model was tested in both a zero-shot setting and a 20-shot setup using examples from the development set. We adopted the text prompt from
(
Chen et al. 2023
)
and tailored it to the Korean meteorological domain to ensure corrections aligned with domain-specific terminology. (i.e.,
"Below is the best-hypotheses transcribed from speech recognition system for Korean Meteological experts. Please try to revise it and write the response for the true transcription."
) This experiment allows us to assess how effectively an LLM can improve ASR accuracy in a specialized domain when limited to a single-hypothesis input.

4.4
Implementation Details

All fine-tuned models are parameter-efficiently trained using LoRA
(
Hu et al. 2022
)
with
r
=32.
The fine-tuning process involved 3 epochs of training with a batch size of 48, a learning rate of 1e-3, and a warmup ratio of 0.1 with AdamW optimizer
(
Loshchilov and Hutter 2017
)
.
For fine-tuned models, we saved the checkpoint after each epoch and selected the best one based on the lowest CER score from the development set of our dataset.
The greedy decoding
(
Holtzman et al. 2020
)
is used as a decoding algorithm for all models.
All models are implemented with Transformers framework
(
Wolf et al. 2020
)
and PyTorch
(
Paszke et al. 2019
)
.

5
Results and Analyses

We present the key findings from our ASR evaluation on the Korean meteorological dataset. Table
3
summarizes the results across different setups.

Zero-shot ASR models usually struggle with domain-specific terminology

Whisper models exhibit high CER and WER when transcribing meteorological queries, particularly for specialized terms. While larger models (e.g., Whisper-large-v2) reduce errors compared to smaller variants, they still struggle with domain-specific terminology, reinforcing the need for adaptation.

Fine-tuning improves general performance, but domain adaptation is crucial

Fine-tuning Whisper-large-v2 on KsponSpeech enhances general transcription accuracy, lowering CER and WER across datasets. However, performance gains on meteorological queries remain limited, suggesting that general-domain adaptation alone is insufficient for specialized terminology.

Synthetic data (TTS) effectively enhances domain adaptation

Training with TTS-generated weather-domain data significantly improves transcription accuracy for meteorological terms. The model fine-tuned on both KspSpeech and synthetic weather data achieves the lowest CER (8.68%) and WER (22.58%) on the test set, demonstrating the effectiveness of controlled synthetic augmentation.

LLM-based post-processing does not consistently improve ASR output

As shown in Figure
3
, the LLM-based post-processing does not consistently improve meteorological term recognition. The CER, WER, and sWER remain higher than those of the zero-shot Whisper model, indicating that text-based refinement alone is insufficient for domain-specific transcription without additional acoustic training. While training a specialized GEC model on the target dataset or leveraging large-N hypothesis generation could potentially enhance performance
(
Chen et al. 2023
;
Hu et al. 2024b
)
, these approaches would require a large-scale training corpus and significantly increase computational costs during inference.

Commercial ASR systems underperform in specialized tasks

Google’s STT model, a widely used commercial ASR system, yields the highest error rates (e.g., 20.38% on Weather-test set), performing worse than zero-shot Whisper-large-v2. This highlights the limitations of generic ASR systems in handling meteorological speech and underscores the necessity of domain-specific adaptation.

These results emphasize the importance of fine-tuning ASR models with domain-specific data. While general LLMs can aid transcription refinement, substantial performance gains require dedicated acoustic adaptation.

Case Study

To illustrate common ASR challenges, we examine qualitative examples from Table
4
.
One issue arises with numerical and meteorological term recognition. In a wind forecast transcription, "지상 시계열 바람장미 풍속계급별 판촉횟수 CALM 0 5m/s 순별 하순 211 인제"
("Ground-level wind rose classified by wind speed, CALM 0–5m/s, late-stage 211 Inje")
, zero-shot Whisper and Google STT misinterpret "CALM", a standard meteorological term for low wind speeds, distorting critical numerical values.
Another case involves unintended style shifts. In "8월 30일 증가압과 기온 평년값은 뭐니"
("What are the historical averages for pressure and temperature on August 30?")
, the post-processed output rephrases it as "8월 30일 증가압과 기온 평년값은 무엇인가요?", making the tone more formal. While this improves fluency, it may be undesirable for contexts requiring colloquial speech.
These cases highlight two key challenges: (1) accurately preserving domain-specific terms and numerical values, and (2) balancing grammatical refinement with maintaining the intended speech style. Addressing these requires targeted domain adaptation and context-aware correction strategies.

6
Conclusion

This work constructs a specialized evaluation dataset for assessing ASR models in the Korean meteorological domain, addressing the lack of domain-specific benchmarks. Our results show that pre-trained ASR models struggle with meteorological terminology, and while fine-tuning on general-domain data improves accuracy, domain adaptation remains essential for handling specialized vocabulary. While our dataset provides a valuable benchmark, future work should expand it to cover more diverse speech conditions, such as spontaneous and noisy environments, to better reflect real-world forecasting scenarios. By making this dataset available, we aim to drive further advancements in domain-adapted ASR systems for meteorological applications.

Experts provided detailed pronunciation guidelines for 256 words and expressions frequently used in the Korean weather domain.
These guidelines were given to annotators as an initial reference and were available throughout the recording process.
