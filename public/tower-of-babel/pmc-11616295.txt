Genetic diversity analysis and core collection construction of tea plant from the Yunnan Province of China using ddRAD sequencing
Authors: Weihua Sun, Chunlin Chen, Linlin Xu, Lingling Tao, Xiaoyan Tong, Yiping Tian, Huibing Jiang, Linbo Chen, Huilin Wen, Shengrui Liu, Chaoling Wei, Junyan Zhu
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractTea plants are economically important woody plants that originated in southwestern China. The Yunnan Province in China is regarded as the central origin of tea plants owing to the abundance and diversity of the tea germplasm present in this region. However, there is a lack of knowledge regarding the genetic diversity and evolutionary relationships among tea plants in this region. Here, a total of 266,397 high-quality single nucleotide polymorphisms were obtained using double digest restriction-site associated DNA sequencing from 468 tea accessions collected from Yunnan. The phylogenetic relationship and population structure of the 468 tea accessions revealed remarkable inter- and intraspecific introgression across different sect. Thea species. We found that Camellia taliensis (W. W. Smith) Melchior is the main genetic donor that greatly contributed to the domestication of C. sinensis (L.) O. Kuntze, as evidenced by the frequent generation of genetic intermediates derived from the hybridization between C. sinensis and C. taliensis in Lincang, Xishuangbanna, and Pu’er. Notable genetic differences were observed across the populations from distinct regions of Yunnan. Additionally, the Lancang River may have attenuated the genetic interflow between Lincang and Pu’er. Finally, a core collection of 50 tea accessions was constructed based on the genetic diversity of the 468 tea accessions. Our results provide novel insights into the evolutionary and domestication history of tea plants in Yunnan Province and can facilitate the development of strategies for the conservation, breeding, and utilization of the core collection.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12870-024-05821-y.Keywords: Tea plant, Theaceae, ddRAD-seq, Genetic diversity, Core collection, Yunnan Province

Introduction

Tea is a popular non-alcoholic beverage worldwide, processed and produced using leaves from tea plants (section Thea within the genus Camellia) as the primary material, and contains numerous secondary metabolites that confer health benefits [1, 2]. The origin and domestication of tea plants are complex and challenging owing to their intricate taxonomy and widescale geographical distribution [3]. Large-scale sequencing data have collectively revealed that the cultivated tea plants were originated from southwest China and further spread worldwide, such as to Laos, Iran, and Azerbaijan [4]. Yunnan Province, in particular, is the main tea-growing region of China and contains massive and diverse tea germplasms; it is thus thought to be a crucial region for elucidating the origin of tea plants [5, 6]. According to the well-known taxonomy proposed by Ming for the sect. Thea [7], the species Camellia sinensis (L.) O. Kuntze has six different wild relatives (C. taliensis (W. W. Smith), C. crassicolumna H. T. Chang, C. kwangsiensis H. T. Chang, C. gymnogyna H. T. Chang, C. grandibracteata H.T. Chang et F. L. Yu, and C. tachangensis F. C. Zhang) and three varieties (C. sinensis var. assamica (Masters) Kitamura, C. sinensis var. pubilimba H. T. Chang, and C. sinensis var. dehungensis (H. T. Chang et Chen) Ming) widely distributed in Yunnan.

High self-incompatibility and long-term species cross-pollination of tea plants have led to high genetic heterozygosity and an expanded genome size (~ 3 Gb), resulting in abundant diversification and discrepancies in genetics, morphology, and chemistry among different tea accessions [1, 8]. Single nucleotide polymorphisms (SNPs) are efficient molecular markers for exploring the genetic diversity, phylogenetic relationships, and population structure of tea accessions from different regions [4, 9, 10]. For example, genome re-sequencing of 190 Camellia accessions has revealed that C. sinensis var. sinensis and C. sinensis var. assamica have undergone independent evolutionary paths and parallel domestication events [10]. For decades, genetic analysis and SNP-based construction of population structures have indicated extensive intra- and interspecific introgressions in tea plant accessions, contributing to improved genetic diversity in Yunnan [11, 12]. Previously, a genome-wide SNP analysis of 450 tea accessions has reported a significantly higher genetic diversity of tea plants in Yunnan Province compared with other provinces [6]. Moreover, frequent interspecific introgression across diverse tea plants has generated genetic intermediates in Yunnan; for instance, the transition type of C. sinensis var. assamica is a hybrid between C. taliensis and C. sinensis var. assamica [12]. Similar observations have been reported in the semi-wild “Bangwei” population cultivated in Yunnan, which is possibly a hybrid between C. taliensis and C. sinensis [13]. Although some studies have provided insights into the domestication of tea plants in Yunnan [4, 5], there remains a large knowledge gap regarding the phylogenetic relationship and genetic diversity of tea plants in Yunnan, as representative and diverse tea accessions from this region are lacking.

Core collection construction is considered an effective approach for germplasm conservation and breeding; it involves creating a subset of germplasm that adequately represents the genetic variability of the entire germplasm [14, 15]. Recently, some region-specific core collections of tea plants have been constructed based on the amplification and classification of simple sequence repeat (SSR) and SNP markers, such as the core collections from Sri Lanka, India, and Hunan, Anhui, and Guizhou provinces of China [16–19]. For example, an Anhui-specific core collection consisting of 115 tea germplasms has previously been established, which only accounts for 20% of the primary collection containing 573 tea germplasms [18]. Furthermore, core collection construction of tea plants has contributed to molecular marker-assisted breeding, genome-wide association analysis (GWAS), and germplasm protection and utilization [17, 20]. Nine SNPs associated with leaf size traits have been identified using GWAS based on the core collection of tea germplasms in Guizhou [17]. However, a core collection of tea plants from Yunnan has not been constructed yet because of limited knowledge of their genetic diversity.

In the present study, we conducted double digest restriction-site associated DNA sequencing (ddRAD-seq) to explore the phylogenetic relationship and population structure of 468 tea accessions collected from Yunnan Province, where genetic introgression was found across different sect. Thea species. We then constructed a Yunnan-specific representative core collection comprising only 50 tea accessions. This study reveals the evolutionary relationship and genetic diversity of tea plants in Yunnan Province, thereby providing a knowledge basis for elucidating the origin and domestication of cultivated tea plants.

Materials and methods

Sample collection and DNA extraction

A total of 468 tea accessions were collected from the Yunnan Province and were categorized according to the Ming’s taxonomy system [7]. The number of tea germplasms from different regions and detailed information on the materials are listed in Supplementary Tables S1 and S2. Fresh tea leaves were collected, dried with silica gel, and then frozen at − 80 °C until DNA extraction. Genomic DNA was extracted from the tea leaves using the cetyltrimethylammonium bromide (CTAB) method [21]. The purity and quality of the extracted DNA were confirmed by electrophoresis on a 0.8% agarose gel. Subsequently, its concentration was determined using a NanoDrop 2000 spectrophotometer.

ddRAD-seq library construction and sequencing

To determine the two optimal restriction enzymes for segmenting genomic DNA into applicable fragments for ddRAD-seq library construction, DNA of 100 ng was digested using Mse I, Pst I, EcoR I, and Sbf I at 37 °C for 1 h. The quality of the digested DNA was assessed using 0.8% agarose gel electrophoresis. The results and treatment of each group in the enzyme digestion experiment are shown in Supplementary Fig. S1. Mse I and EcoR I fully digested genomic DNA to produce a number of fragments and were hence determined as the optimal restriction enzymes for ddRAD-seq library construction. Subsequently, 100 ng genomic DNA of each tea accession was digested with Mse I and EcoR I at 37 °C for 1 h. A Solexa P1 adapter was used for sample tracking. After adapter ligation, the library of each sample was pooled and randomly sheared to an average size of 300–500 bp using a sonicator. The sheared DNA library was separated using 1.0% agarose gel electrophoresis, and targeted 300–500 bp DNA fragments were recovered. The dsDNA was modified with 3′-adenine and ligated with a Solexa P2 adapter. Finally, the DNA products were amplified using PCR as described previously [22]. The constructed DNA library was then sequenced using the Illumina HiSeq X Ten platform and was available in NCBI database (accession number, PRJNA1157005).

SNP filtering and calling

The raw sequencing data was filtered using SOAPnuke (v 2.1.0) to obtain the clean data; the reads containing > 30% low-quality bases with a quality value of ≤ 7 were removed [23]. The clean data were aligned to the reference genome of C. sinensis cv. ‘Tieguanyin’ using the BWA software, and the resulting alignment was transformed into a BAM file [10, 24]. Based on the BAM file, SNPs in each sample were detected using GATK (v 4.1.2) to generate a genomic variant call format (gvcf) data file for each sample [25]. Using GATK, the gvcf file was sorted and merged into a vcf file containing all SNPs of the 468 tea accessions, which was then filtered with default parameters (--filter-expression “QD < 2.0 || MQ < 40.0 || FS > 60.0 || SOR > 3.0 || MQRankSum < -12.5 || ReadPosRankSum < -8.0”) to obtain high-quality SNPs. Finally, the high-quality SNPs were filtered using vcftools with the following parameters: --max-missing 0.85 --maf 0.05 --min-alleles 2 --max-alleles 2 [26].

Phylogenetic tree, population structure, and principal component analysis

Based on high-quality SNPs identified from 468 tea accessions, we constructed a phylogenetic tree using the IQ-TREE software based on an optimal phylogenetic estimate model (GTR + F + ASC + I + G4) with bootstrapping (1000 replicates) and default parameters [27]. The ADMIXTURE software was used to analyze the population structure of the 468 tea accessions [28]. The number of presumed subpopulations (K) was set from 2 to 10. The optimal K value was determined based on the cross validation (CV) error, which was calculated using the minimum value. Plots of population structure was developed using the “pophelper” package in R studio. Principal component analysis (PCA) was performed using the PLINK and GCTA software [29, 30], and PCA plot was visualized using the “ggplot2” package in R studio.

Genetic diversity analysis

After normalization and sorting of high-quality SNPs using PLINK with default parameters (plink --vcf file.vcf --recode compound-genotypes --allow-extra-chr --out file.geno) [30], multiple genetic diversity indexes across different populations were calculated using “snpReady” package in R studio [31], included observed heterozygosity (Ho), expected heterozygosity (He), polymorphic information content (PIC), and minor allele frequency (MAF). The fixation index (Fst) of the regional population containing accessions greater than 10 was calculated using VCF tools [26], a sliding window approach was employed with a window size of 100 kb and a step size of 10 kb. The genome-wide of these window-based Fst values were calculated as average values to evaluate the population differentiation.

Core collection construction

Genetic distance (GD) of primary collection with 468 tea accessions were calculated using IQ-TREE software with default parameters. Moreover, a core collection was constructed using “Core hunter” package in R studio [32]. The genetic diversity of core collection was evaluated by conducting Student’s t-test between primary and core collection using SPSS software.

Results

Generation and detection of high-quality SNPs in 468 tea accessions

To investigate the genetic diversity of tea plants in Yunnan province, a total of 468 tea accessions belonged to the Sect. Theaceae of genus Camellia, were collected from 12 cities that was thoughted as the main growing regions in Yunnan province (Fig. 1). According to the Chang’s taxonomy system for the Sect. Thea, 468 tea accessions were classified into 7 species, included 215 C. sinensis, 194 C. taliensis, 31 C. crassicolumna, 19 C. kwangsiensis, 5 C. gymnogyna, 3 C. grandibracteata, and 1 C. tachangensis. Additionally, 215 C. sinensis were divided into three different varieties, consisted of 175 C. sinensis var. assamica, 25 C. sinensis var. pubilimba, and 15 C. sinensis var. dehungensis. Notably, larger amounts of tea accessions were found in southwestern Yunnan (Xishuangbanna, Lincang, and Pu’er), which mainly comprised of C. sinensis var. assamica and C. taliensis, accounted for 55.6% of a total of 468 tea accessions (Fig. 1 and Supplementary Table. S1).

Based on ddRAD libraries construction and sequencing for 468 tea accessions, a total of 920.49 Gb clean data was generated, corresponding to an average of 1.97 Gb clean data per samples, the information of clean data referred to Supplementary Table S3. The main quality control index for clean data was evaluated, which Q20 and Q30 higher than 95% and 90% for each sample, indicated that the high accuracy and confidence of data obtained from ddRAD-Seq libraries (Supplementary Table. S3). Furthermore, on basis of the chromosome-level sequence of tea plant (C. sinensis cv. Tieguanyin), a total of 23,821,601 primary SNPs were identified through mapped the 920.49 Gb clean data of 468 tea accessions to the reference genome. After filtering for primary SNPs using GATK software, a total of 266,397 high-quality SNPs were generated. To assess the genomic landscape of these high-quality SNPs, the distribution and density of SNPs were calculated and visualized among 15 pairs of chromosomes of tea plant (Fig. 2). A significant difference on the amounts of SNPs were observed among 15 chromosomes, which a minimum and maximum of 12,532 and 23,737 SNPs were detected on chromosome 15 and 1, respectively. The average density of one SNP every is 0.088 kb among these 15 chromosomes, ranged from 0.070 kb (Chr 7) to 0.094 kb (Chr11). Notably, we also found that some blocks exhibited low-density SNPs, especially an average density was low to 0.004 kb SNP distance on chromosome 7 ranged from 0 Mb to 66.4 Mb. These SNP heterogeneities were found in the BSA-Seq and BSR-Seq analysis of F1 population hybridized between ‘ZJ’ and ‘Jinxuan’ [33], which may be resulted from the deficient assembly of chromosome 7 in ‘Tieguanyin’ reference genome [10].

Phylogenetic relationship and principal component analysis of 468 tea accessions

To investigate the phylogenetic relationship among the 468 tea accessions collected from the Yunnan Province, 266,397 high-quality SNPs were used to construct a phylogenetic tree based on the maximum-likelihood method, with Camellia oleifera (OT) serving as the outgroup (Fig. 3a). Based on the phylogenetic tree, the 468 accessions were divided into five groups. Group 2 comprised the highest percentage of accessions (47.2%; 221 out of 468 accessions), followed by Group 3 (21.4%), Group 4 (18.4%), Group 1 (7.5%), and Group 5 (5.5%). In addition, nine main subgroups (subgroups I–IX) were further classified according to the internal phylogenetic relationships of the five groups (Supplementary Fig. S2). For instance, the 221 accessions in Group 2 were constituted by subgroups III (35 accessions, 15.8%) and IV (186 accessions, 84.2%), including 176 C. taliensis, 23 C. crassicolumna, 21 C. sinensis var. assamica, and 1 C. tachangensis. Notably, subgroups dominated by multiple species showed a significantly higher proportion of specific sect. Thea species; for example, C. kwangsiensis, C. taliensis, C. sinensis var. assamica, C. sinensis var. dehungensis, and C. sinensis var. assamica were predominant in subgroups II, IV, VII, VIII, and IX, respectively. Furthermore, 75 C. sinensis var. assamica plants were present in subgroup VII, accounting for 98.7% of 76 accessions. In contrast, C. taliensis was predominant in subgroups III (31.4%) and VI (28.3%) and exhibited close phylogenetic relationships with C. crassicolumna and C. sinensis var. assamica in subgroups III and VI, respectively. Similarly, subgroup V mainly included three different varieties of C. sinensis and was characterized as a classical interspecies population, with 25 C. sinensis var. pubilimba, 17 C. sinensis var. assamica, 3 C. grandibracteata, and 2 C. sinensis var. dehungensis, accounting for 53.2%, 36.2%, 6.4%, and 4.2% of 47 accessions, respectively.

To clarify the cluster and classification of the 468 different tea accessions, PCA based on 266,397 high-quality SNPs was performed to develop an overview of the genetic differences among these accessions (Fig. 3b). The resulting PCA showed that the first two principal components accounted for 32.5% of the total variation (PC1: 25.8%; PC2: 6.7%). Consistent with the phylogenetic tree of the 468 accessions, five distinct groups (1–5) and nine subgroups (I–IX) were clearly classified and clustered.

Population structure revealed regional and species introgression in Yunnan

To further explore gene introgression among the 468 accessions from Yunnan Province, 266,397 SNPs were used to reveal the population structure based on the admixture model. CV error values were calculated to assess the optimal population size (K = 2–10). The results of the calculations indicated that K = 9 showed a minimum CV error of 0.38 (Supplementary Fig. S3). Accordingly, the 468 accessions were divided into nine groups, which were characterized and classified according to the taxonomy and geographical distribution of the dominant species (Fig. 4, Supplementary Fig. S4 and Table. S4). Notably, C. taliensis served as the main donor parent, contributing to the intensive interspecific introgression with C. sinensis var. assamica, C. sinensis var. pubilimba, and C. crassicolumna in G2, G9, and G6, respectively. Intraspecific introgression also frequently occurred within C. taliensis populations derived from different regions. For example, the genetic diversity of C. taliensis within G1 was found not only in Lingcang but also in Xishuangbanna, Pu’er, and Baoshan (Fig. 4 and Supplementary Fig. S5). In contrast, conserved genetic relationship were observed in G5, G7, and G8. For example, C. taliensis collected from Xishuangbanna and Pu’er shared similar genetic background and characteristics and were collectively grouped into G7 (Fig. 4 and Supplementary Fig. S5). Moreover, accessions from different varieties of C. sinensis also exhibited significant interspecific introgression, such as the generation genetic intermediates of G2, attributed to introgression between C. sinensis var. assamica and C. sinensis var. dehungensis (Fig. 4). These results suggest that the various and frequent inter- and intraspecific introgressions among different species or regions contributed to the genetic diversity and complexity of tea plants in the Yunnan Province.

Genetic variation and differentiation of different population

To evaluate the genetic diversity of the populations collected from the nine different regions of Yunnan, the genetic parameters Ho, He, PIC, and MAF were calculated based on 266,397 SNPs. The mean values of He, Ho, PIC, and MAF across the nine regional populations ranged from 0.36 to 0.39, 0.35 to 0.54, 0.28 to 0.31, and 0.25 to 0.30, respectively (Table 1). Among the nine populations, the Baoshan population showed the highest allelic polymorphism (MAF = 0.30, PIC = 0.31), whereas the Yuxi population exhibited the lowest allelic polymorphism (MAF = 0.25, PIC = 0.28) (Table 1). In addition, Ho was significantly higher than He in the Baoshan (Ho = 0.54, He = 0.39) and Lincang (Ho = 0.42, He = 0.36) populations (Table 1), suggesting excessive heterozygosity in these two populations.

Construction and evaluation of a core collection

A Yunnan-specific core collection of tea plants was developed using the R package Core Hunter to extract tea accessions with significantly high genetic diversity from the primary collection, which consisted of 468 tea accessions. A core collection comprising a minimum of 50 tea accessions, which accounted for only 10.7% of the primary collection. Additionally, 64.4% of 266,397 high-quality SNPs (171,569) were retained to construct the core collection based on a comparison of GDs among the primary collection (Table 3). Furthermore, the geographical distribution and taxonomic classification of tea accessions from the core collection are illustrated on the map of Yunnan Province. In particular, populations of C. sinensis var. assamica and C. taliensis exhibited considerable dominance relative to the other species of sect. Thea, accounting for 46.0% and 36.0% of the primary collection, respectively (Supplementary Table S5). Additionally, 32.0%, 28.0%, and 12.0% of the tea accessions in the core collection were obtained from Lincang, Xishuangbanna, and Pu’er, respectively, suggesting that the genetic diversity of tea plants in southwestern Yunnan is comparatively higher (Supplementary Fig. S6).

To evaluate the sufficiency and genetic diversity of the core collection, typical genetic parameters, including MAF, Ho, He, and PIC, were determined. Discussion

The Yunnan Province of China, which contains thousands of wild and ancient tea plants owing to its geographical characteristics and diverse climate conditions from subtropical to tropical, is considered one of the crucial regions for studying the origin of tea plants [34, 35]. However, comparing with significant advances in genetic diversity studies of other tea-producing regions in China [17–19, 36], the evolutionary and domestication processes of tea plants from Yunnan Province remain unclear. In the present study, we determined the phylogenetic relationship, population structure, and genetic differentiation of 468 tea accessions collected from Yunnan. A Yunnan-specific core collection was constructed, comprising 50 tea accessions that collectively represented the genetic variance of the primary collection. These findings can provide comprehensive insights into the evolutionary history of tea plants in Yunnan and guide the efficient conservation and utilization of tea germplasms.

Frequent inter- and intraspecific hybridization has driven and accelerated the genetic, chemical, and morphological diversity of tea plants, thereby contributing to the development of modern cultivar breeding [6, 9, 10]. Numerous well-known cultivars have been derived from the hybridization of C. sinensis var. sinensis and C. sinensis var. assamica, such as ‘Fuyun 6’, ‘Yingshuang’, and ‘Zhenong 113’ [37]. Large-scale re-sequencing analyses of 190 and 363 tea accessions have collectively demonstrated that genetic introgression has occurred between C. sinensis and its wild relatives [10, 38]. Similarly, in this study, inter- and intraspecific introgression was found not only among different sect. Thea plants but within varieties of C. sinensis (Figs. 3a and 4). The genetic background of C. taliensis was frequently observed in the populations of C. crassicolumna and C. sinensis var. assamica, suggesting that C. taliensis may act as an intermediate contributor to the genetic diversity of tea plants in Yunnan through distant hybridization with other sect. Thea plants (Figs. 3a and 4). This notion is supported by previous studies indicating that some C. taliensis accessions show high similarity to C. sinensis var. assamica, acting as a donor to facilitate genetic introgression of the latter [5, 12]. Moreover, introgression has been observed to occur frequently across tea plant populations in adjacent regions [18, 19, 39], such as the two geographically close tea plant populations (DY and SQ) in Guizhou Province, which shared a similar genetic background and then developed independently [11]. Additionally, C. taliensis was found to exhibit long-term genetic interflow between Lincang and Baoshan (Fig. 4 and Supplementary Fig. S5), which was consistent with the results of Zhao et al. [40]. Notably, the genetic diversity of these 468 tea accessions has expanded our knowledge on the domestication of tea plants in Yunnan Province, but a comprehensive approach is essential in future work, including genetic, chemical, and morphological investigations.

The examination and evaluation of genetic diversity within and between populations are important indicators for investigating the migration and evolution of tea populations [6, 18]. A previous genetic diversity analysis of C. sinensis var. assamica using nuclear microsatellite SSR (nSSR) markers has indicated a lower value of Ho than He in the Lincang population [12]. In contrast, the ddRAD-seq analysis in the present study revealed that the Lincang population exhibited excess heterozygosity with a higher Ho value than He (Table 1). The notable discrepancies observed may be attributed to the distinct populations of Lincang utilized in the two studies, in which C. taliensis was analyzed as the dominant species rather than C. sinensis var. assamica. Furthermore, the Pu’er population showed a conserved evolutionary pattern with the Xishuangbanna and Chuxiong populations, with significantly lower values of Fst and GD (Table 2). However, remarkably elevated values of Fst and GD were found between the Pu’er and Lincang populations, which may be due to the presence of the Lancang River, which acts as a natural barrier to impede gene flow between the two populations. These findings are consistent with the perspective that the Lancang River has a spatial influence on the population differentiation of diverse species, from plants to animals [41–43].

Core collection construction is essential for identifying the genetic potential of a germplasm and facilitating its efficient conservation [32]. Compared with morphology- and metabolite-based methods, high-throughput reduced-representation sequencing has emerged as an efficient cost-effective approach for establishing a core collection of tea plants [20, 44, 45]. In the present study, the Yunnan-specific core collection of tea plants (50 tea accessions) was constructed using ddRAD-seq method, comprising 10.7% of the primary collection (468 tea accessions) (Table 3 and Supplementary Table S5). This retention rate was significantly lower than that of the core collections of Hunan (20.0%), Anhui (20.1%), and Guizhou (35.7%) [18, 19, 36]. Additionally, 72% of the core accessions were derived from southwestern Yunnan, such as Lincang, Xishuangbanna, and Pu’er. This preferred geographical distribution may have resulted from the high genetic diversity of tea accessions in southwestern Yunnan (Supplementary Fig. S6). Similar results have been found in the core collection of Anhui, where the diverse genetics of accessions in the Huangshan district resulted in a high retention rate within the core collection [18]. Moreover, in contrast to the predominance of C. sinensis var. sinensis in Hunan and Anhui [18, 19], C. sinensis var. assamica and C. taliensis constituted a considerable proportion (82.0%) of the core collection in Yunnan (Supplementary Table S5), suggesting an abundant and complex genetic variation in Yunnan. Nevertheless, further evaluation of the contribution and impact of C. sinensis var. assamica and C. taliensis on the genetic diversity in Yunnan at a large-scale population level is required.

Conclusion

We conducted ddRAD-Seq on 468 representative tea accessions to elucidate the phylogenetic and population evolutionary relationship among tea plants in Yunnan Province. Our results revealed that inter- and intraspecific introgression was frequently observed among different sect. Thea plants contributing to the abundant genetic diversity of tea plants in Yunnan. Furthermore, we developed a Yunnan-specific core collection comprising the most diverse genotypes, which is considered an essential germplasm for effective conservation and utilization. These findings provide novel insights into the evolutionary and domestication patterns of tea plants in Yunnan Province.
