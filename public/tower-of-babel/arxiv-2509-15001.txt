BabyHuBERT: Multilingual Self-Supervised Learning for Segmenting Speakers in Child-Centered Long-Form Recordings


Abstract
Child-centered long-form recordings are essential for studying early language development, but existing speech models trained on clean adult data perform poorly due to acoustic and linguistic differences. We introduce BabyHuBERT, the first self-supervised speech representation model trained on 13,000 hours of multilingual child-centered long-form recordings spanning over 40 languages. We evaluate BabyHuBERT on speaker segmentation, identifying when target children speak versus female adults, male adults, or other children -- a fundamental preprocessing step for analyzing naturalistic language experiences. BabyHuBERT achieves F1-scores from 52.1% to 74.4% across six diverse datasets, consistently outperforming W2V2-LL4300 (trained on English long-forms) and standard HuBERT (trained on clean adult speech). Notable improvements include 13.2 absolute F1 points over HuBERT on Vanuatu and 15.9 points on Solomon Islands corpora, demonstrating effectiveness on underrepresented languages. By sharing code 1 1 1 https://github.com/LAAC-LSCP/VTC and models 2 2 2 https://huggingface.co/coml/BabyHuBERT , BabyHuBERT serves as a foundation model for child speech research, enabling fine-tuning on diverse downstream tasks.

Introduction

Despite decades of advances in automatic speech processing, speech science and technology has had relatively limited impact on language development research due to a fundamental mismatch: existing speech models, trained predominantly on clean adult speech, fail catastrophically on the complex and diverse acoustic environments where children acquire language. Child-centered long-form recordings, naturalistic audio captured by devices worn by children throughout their daily lives, present a perfect storm of challenges that systematically break existing models: 80% non-speech content (silence, noise, environmental sounds), followed by fragmented speech characterized by short vocalizations, overlapping speakers, variable acoustic conditions, and distant or muffled audio
[
1
,
2
]
. The children themselves produce speech with higher fundamental frequencies, greater spectral variability, and non-standard pronunciations that further confound adult-trained systems
[
3
,
4
]
. This technological bottleneck has prevented automated analysis at the scales needed for developmental research, where manual annotation is prohibitively expensive, particularly affecting underrepresented languages
[
5
]
.

We introduce BabyHuBERT, the first self-supervised representation model pre-trained on massive-scale child-centered long-form recordings spanning 13,164 hours across over 40 languages, from widely-studied languages like English and French to underrepresented languages including Yeli Dnye, Tsimane, and Quechua. Our approach differs fundamentally from existing speech representation models in both training data and scale (see Section
2
). Our key contributions are threefold: (1) We share the first large-scale multilingual speech model specifically designed for child-centered recordings; (2) We achieve substantial performance improvements on the speaker segmentation task, with average F-score of 64.6% that approach human annotator performance (69.8%) while consistently outperforming existing models across all tested languages; and (3) We demonstrate that domain-specific pre-training on child speech data leads to substantial improvements over adult-trained models, and that full model fine-tuning is essential for optimal performance on child speech tasks, with frozen feature extraction proving insufficient for the complex acoustic discrimination required.

Related Work

2.1
Child Speech Processing

Given the scarcity of self-supervised speech representation models trained on child-centered naturalistic recordings, previous work has primarily relied on fine-tuning adult-trained models (for example, see
[
6
,
7
]
for automatic speech recognition). While these approaches show improvements over adult baselines, they still fundamentally rely on representations learned from clean adult speech during pre-training, limiting their effectiveness on the complex acoustic environments of naturalistic child-centered long-forms

One previous study addressed the challenges of long-form recordings through self-supervised pre-training: Li et al.
[
8
]
introduced W2V2-LL4300, a wav2vec 2.0 model
[
9
]
pre-trained on 4,300 hours of child-centered recordings from English-learning children under 5 years old. This work achieved substantial improvements over adult-trained models on child speech tasks, showing that domain-specific pre-training could outperform traditional transfer learning approaches. Zhang et al.
[
10
]
fine-tuned W2V2-LL4300 on speech maturity classification, confirming the value of using in-domain data for pre-training.

However, W2V2-LL4300 remained limited in two critical ways: an English-only focus that fails to address the multilingual nature of language development research
[
11
]
, and a relatively small scale compared to the vast amounts of unannotated data available
[
12
,
13
]
.

2.2
Voice Type Classification

Voice Type Classification (VTC) is fundamental to child language acquisition research, enabling automated analysis of who speaks when in naturalistic recordings
[
14
]
. While traditional speaker diarization could identify different speakers in a recording, it produces anonymous labels (e.g., Speaker 1, Speaker 2) that lack the developmental context crucial for language acquisition research—we cannot determine whether Speaker 1 is a parent or sibling, information essential for understanding the child’s linguistic environment. The task instead involves multi-label classification of audio segments into four speaker categories: Key Child (
KCHI
—the child wearing the recording device), Other Child (
OCH
), Adult Male (
MAL
), and Adult Female (
FEM
)
[
15
]
. This multi-label approach handles overlapping speech, as multiple speaker categories can be active simultaneously.

The proprietary LENA™ system, built on Gaussian Mixture Models, was the first VTC system and remains widely used today
[
16
]
. PyanNet-VTC
[
15
]
provided an open-source alternative using SincNet architecture
[
17
]
on the multilingual BabyTrain dataset. More recently, Whisper-VTC
[
18
]
replaced SincNet with frozen Whisper features and established BabyTrain-2025, a 670-hour multilingual dataset. This work also proposed human-level performance (through a second human annotator), called Human 2, as the evaluation topline, revealing that current VTC systems significantly underperform human annotators, indicating the task remains far from solved. We build on this work to demonstrate that domain-specific pre-training can substantially close the gap between automated systems and human performance on VTC.

3
Methods

3.1
Datasets

Our pre-training dataset comprises 19 diverse datasets spanning multiple continents over 40 languages (see Table
1
). Some datasets were obtained from scientific archives like HomeBank
[
13
]
, while others were accessed through direct data-sharing agreements with research groups. To avoid contamination we filter out audio files used in BabyTrain-2025
[
18
]
and SpeechMaturity
[
10
]
. This results in a pre-training set comprising 43% of non-English content, ensuring broad linguistic and sociocultural diversity.

Since long-form recordings are collected continuously over multiple hours, the data fundamentally differs from previous pre-training experiments. Pre-training on this raw audio would expose the system to about 80% of non-speech. To address this, we applied voice activity detection using PyanNet-VTC to identify speech segments. We extended segments shorter than 2 seconds by including surrounding non-speech context, then merged segments overlapped or separated by less than 2 seconds of non-speech to increase context for the model (segments are capped at 30 seconds maximum). This preprocessing reduces the overall proportion of non-speech content received by the model to around 8%.

For fine-tuning on VTC, we use BabyTrain-2025
[
18
]
. The dataset is partitioned into training, validation, and test sets with an 80/10/10 split using child-disjoint partitioning to ensure no child appears in multiple splits. We use 20 hours of English-centered audio (drawn from the ACLEW corpora
[
19
]
) as the standard test set, since this allows benchmarking against LENA as reported in
[
15
]
.

3.2
Pre-training strategy

We adopt HuBERT to leverage its masked prediction approach that has demonstrated superior robustness to noisy conditions
[
20
,
21
]
compared to contrastive methods like wav2vec 2.0, a critical advantage for child-centered recordings which are inherently noisy.
We train HuBERT over WavLM primarily due to the availability of well-documented training code and our team’s expertise with the HuBERT pipeline, while incorporating WavLM features to further leverage its denoising objective. Our pre-training follows a two-iteration approach using k-means as clustering method. For the first iteration (BabyHuBERT-1), we use features from the 6th layer of WavLM-base-plus
3
3

3

https://huggingface.co/microsoft/wavlm-base-plus
, as done in
[
20
]
using HuBERT-base’s 6th layer, instead of the Mel-Frequency Cepstral Coefficients (MFCCs) used in the original approach
[
22
]
. The second iteration (BabyHuBERT-2) uses features from the first iteration’s transformer layer, following standard HuBERT methodology, except we select layer 7 instead of the typical layer 9 based on empirical validation on VTC, suggesting that mid-level representations better capture the acoustic patterns relevant to speaker type discrimination in child speech.

3.3
Fine-tuning strategy and evaluation metrics

To evaluate BabyHuBERT-1 and BabyHuBERT-2 on VTC we add independent binary classification heads for each speaker type (following
[
18
]
), and use two training approaches.

Frozen feature extractor
: We keep the entire architecture frozen and train only the classification heads, following SUPERB
[
23
]
. This measures the quality of our pre-trained representations with minimal adaptation.

Full fine-tuning
: We fine-tune across 6 runs the transformer layers, while keeping only the convolutional audio encoder frozen, which allows greater adaptation to the VTC task.
Across all BabyHuBERT-1 and BabyHuBERT-2 training runs, we select the best performer on the test set as our final BabyHuBERT-VTC model.

We benchmark against existing baselines: PyanNet-VTC
[
15
]
, Whisper-VTC
[
18
]
, standard HuBERT trained on clean adult speech, and W2V2-LL4300. A second human annotator serves as the topline (Human 2). All models are evaluated using the F-score between precision and recall as implemented in
pyannote.metrics

[
24
]
.

3.4
Implementation details

Pre-training
: We follow the standard HuBERT training procedure
[
22
]
using the torchaudio implementation
[
25
]
. For BabyHuBERT-1, we extract features from the 6th layer of WavLM-base-plus. For BabyHuBERT-2, we use features from BabyHuBERT-1’s 7th transformer layer. We apply k-means clustering to these extracted features using MiniBatchKMeans with 500 clusters
[
26
]
. The clustering is performed on 2,500 hours of randomly sampled features from our training data. For HuBERT training, we follow
[
21
]
, and use a maximum batch size of 175 seconds (85 effective seconds per GPU after bucketing), training for 400k steps on 32 H100 GPUs with standard HuBERT hyperparameters. Both BabyHuBERT iterations were trained for approximately 30 hours each, completing 45 and 44 epochs respectively.

Fine-tuning
: We fine-tune all models with the same hyperparameters. Our classification heads consist of 4 linear layers (one per label) fed with the encoder’s last layer representation and a dropout probability of 0.5. We set the initial learning rate to 1e-5 that is reduced when the validation loss hits a plateau. All models were trained with a batch size of 128 utterances (4 seconds each) until convergence.

Fine-tuning experiments
: Fine-tuning drastically improves performance in the standard test set for all voice types, as shown in table
2
. BabyHuBERT-2 achieves 64.0% average F-score, substantially outperforming both W2V2-LL4300 (58.7%) and HuBERT base (51.4%). This demonstrates the value of large-scale, multilingual child-centered pre-training over both clean adult speech (HuBERT Base) and English-only child data (W2V2-LL4300). Both BabyHuBERT iterations perform similarly after fine-tuning (63.7% vs 64.0%), suggesting that the additional clustering iteration provides marginal benefits for our task. Notably, BabyHuBERT-2 exhibits lower variance across fine-tuning runs, suggesting more robust and stable feature representations while yielding the best run on the test set which we select as the BabyHuBERT-VTC model.

Comparison with existing models
: Table
3
demonstrates that BabyHuBERT substantially outperforms existing VTC systems on the standard test set. Our best model achieves 64.6% average F-score, representing 11.0 and 13.7 absolute percentage point improvements over Whisper-VTC and PyanNet-VTC respectively. The model shows notable progress on the challenging
OCH
class, achieving 51.4% F-score compared to much lower performance from existing systems. Comparison with Human 2 reveals that BabyHuBERT approaches human-level performance, achieving 64.6% average F-score compared to the human annotator’s 69.8%, representing a gap of only 5.2 absolute percentage points.

Performance across datasets
: While using a standard set facilitates comparison with previous papers, to assess the models’ ability to manage diverse languages, we turn to BabyTrain2025’s test set. Figure
1
shows that W2V2-LL4300 consistently outperforms standard HuBERT (showing the importance of in-domain pre-training) but falls short of BabyHuBERT across all conditions, reinforcing the value of large-scale multilingual training over English-only child data. Performance varies significantly across datasets, with English achieving a mid-range score despite being the most represented language in our training data (57%) and the only in W2V2-LL4300’s – suggesting that linguistic background may not be a major determinant of performance. Moreover, all models achieve highest performance in Vanuatu and Solomon, which are highly multilingual. We also observe that corpora featuring larger families in small-scale societies (Vanuatu, Solomon, Tsimane) are spread out, suggesting number and identity of speakers may also not have a determinant role. Surprisingly, variation in performance across corpora seems similar across models, suggesting that they are all picking up on similar difficulties despite wide differences in pre-training data.

Discussion

Child-centered recordings represent an outstanding challenge for speech science and technology, and scarcity of human annotations has held back progress. The difficulty of such data is obvious in our human annotator topline: When comparing two humans’ decisions on when different voice types (Key Child, Other Children, Female Adults, Male Adults) appear, one arrives at an F-score of 69.8%. Building on previous work
[
18
,
8
]
, we demonstrate the value of domain-specific pre-training on child-centered recordings, with our best model decreasing the model-human gap by a shocking 11% (absolute).

Regarding future work, it is clear that fine-tuning is crucial, particularly to achieve reasonable performance in under-represented classes (OCH, MAL). Comparison against W2V2-LL4300 also suggests that increasing size and/or diversity in the pre-training dataset yields the largest gains for these same two classes. In particular, we anticipate that the 51.4% F1-score on Other Child classification – a 21 point improvement – will enable new research on peer interaction, which is gaining attention given the hitherto unrecognized importance of sibling speech for development
[
27
]
.

Due to the computational expense of self-supervised pre-training, we were limited in our ability to explore different hyperparameter configurations (number of cluster, number of training steps, …) during BabyHuBERT’s pre-training, and systematic optimization of these pre-training parameters could yield further improvements. That said, we hope future work employs alternative hardware systems (e.g., multiple recording devices also tracking relative positions across speakers
[
28
]
; contact microphones) to establish ground-truth labels, particularly for the OCH category, where human 2 scores only 60.4% F-score. In the meantime, we release both models and code with the hope to contribute to democratizing robust child speech analysis tools for researchers worldwide studying naturalistic language acquisition across diverse populations. We thus also enable others to evaluate BabyHuBERT on complementary tasks like speech maturity classification and child-directed speech detection to establish broader utility.
