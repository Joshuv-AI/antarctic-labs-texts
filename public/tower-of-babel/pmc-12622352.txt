Exosomal miR-29a-3p derived from bone marrow stromal cells suppresses malignant behavior of NSCLC by regulating DNMT3A/JAK2/STAT3 axis
Authors: Chao Su, Xiaoliang Han, Kang Zhang
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTMicroRNAs (miRNAs) can be transported to tumor cells through exosomes secreted by bone marrow mesenchymal stem cells (BMSC-Exos) and exert regulatory functions within cells. Here, we aim to investigate the functional mechanism of miR-29a-3p carried by BMSC-Exos in the treatment of NSCLC. Based on the miRNA/mRNA gene expression data in the UCSC dataset (1029 NSCLC and 110 normal samples), bioinformatics analysis predicted the expression levels of miR-29a-3p and DNMT3A in NSCLC samples and their association with prognosis. Exosomes were isolated from BMSCs and characterized. BMSC-Exos with expressed or knocked out miR-29a-3p were treated A549 cells, and their biological effects on cells were evaluated, including proliferation, migration, and apoptosis. Western blotting was employed to explore the involvement of DNMT3A and JAK2/STAT3 signaling pathways. Furthermore, the binding between miR-29a-3p and DNMT3A was verified through dual-luciferase reporter assay. Following transfection with miR-29a-3p mimic and DNMT3A overexpression vectors, their roles in the biological processes of NSCLC were analyzed. In NSCLC, decreased expression of miR-29a-3p or increased expression of DNMT3A was closely associated with poor prognosis. miR-29a-3p can be transferred from BMSCs to A549 cells via exosomes, thereby inhibiting cell proliferation and migration while promoting apoptosis. DNMT3A was identified as a target gene of miR-29a-3p. Mechanistically, miR-29a-3p upregulation led to decreased DNMT3A expression and impaired JAK2/STAT3 signaling pathway. Overall, this study demonstrated that BMSC-derived exosomal miR-29a-3p restrained NSCLC by reducing DNMT3A/JAK2/STAT3 axis. These findings may provide new insights for the development of NSCLC treatment strategies.KEYWORDS: Bone marrow stromal cells, DNMT3A, exosomal miR-29a-3p, NSCLC

Introduction

Lung cancer is traditionally divided into two histological subtypes: non-small cell lung cancer (NSCLC) and small cell lung cancer, with NSCLC accounting for approximately 85% of all cases.1 In the last decade, major improvements in the prognosis of NSCLC patients have been achieved owing to the spread of early screening and the application of new therapies.2 Statistically, the 5-y overall survival rate for patients with NSCLC in 2024 is approximately 25%–30%; unfortunately, the 5-y survival rate for advanced patients is still less than 10%.3 Currently, cancer metastasis and chemotherapy resistance are the two major obstacles to lung cancer treatment.4 To overcome these problems, finding effective and specific therapeutic protocols for NSCLC is necessary.

Exosomes are extracellular vesicles with an average diameter of about 100 nm that are encapsulated by a lipid bilayer. It serves as a novel intercellular communication system that can play various functions in both healthy and disease states (such as cancer) by carrying and delivering signaling molecules such as nucleic acids and proteins.5 There is growing evidence that exosomes carry biological compounds such as therapeutic noncoding RNAs into target cells to alter the tumor microenvironment and enhance anticancer immune reactions, thereby improving the prognosis of patients.6 Exosomes currently used in cancer therapy are usually derived from mesenchymal stem cells (MSCs), which have several unique advantages. On the one hand, MSCs have anti-inflammatory effects and regulate the immune response.7 On the other hand, MSCs preferentially tend toward cancer cells in a paracrine manner and are internalized into the tumor stroma.8 Hence, this therapeutic approach of targeting MSC-derived exosomes loaded with therapeutic cargoes (such as miRNAs) to cancer cells is very promising for improving cancer.9 In NSCLC, Wu et al. found that bone MSC-derived exosomes (BMSC-Exos) effectively prevent NSCLC progression by carrying miR-30b-5p​​​​;10 Yang et al. revealed that LXY30 peptide-modified BMSC-Exo encapsulating miR-181b or miR-30c function to suppress NSCLC.11 However, the use of BMSC-Exos in NSCLC therapy is still in its infancy, and more studies are required to accelerate their use in clinical practice.

miRNAs participate in the regulation of various cellular processes via negatively controlling gene expression at the posttranscriptional level. It has also received great attention as an anticancer drug in the treatment of lung cancer.12 Recent articles have highlighted that miR-29a-3p exhibits antifibrotic activity by negatively regulating collagen, and its overexpression exerts an anti-NSCLC benefit.13,14 Moreover, the exosomal miR-29a-3p derived from MSCs can be used as a supplement to antiangiogenic therapy with antiglioma potential.15 Moreover, some of the functions of miR-29a-3p may be dependent on the downregulation of DNMT3A, such as the maintenance of hematopoietic stem cell self-renewal.16 However, whether and how miR-29a-3p inhibits the occurrence and progression of NSCLC through BMSC-Exos has not been fully investigated.

Therefore, we sought to elucidate the expression pattern of miR-29a-3p in NSCLC and assessed whether BMSC-Exos inhibit NSCLC cell viability and migration via the miR-29a-3p/DNMT3A axis. This study may improve our understanding of the role of BMSC-Exos in anti-NSCLC.

Materials and methods

Bioinformatic analysis

Gene expression profiles and information related to NSCLC were accessed from the UCSC database for differential and prognostic analyses. This dataset included 1029 NSCLC and 110 normal samples. First, the Wilcoxon rank-sum test was used to calculate the difference in hsa-miR-29a or DNMT3A levels between NSCLC and normal tissues. The relationship between hsa-miR-29a and DNMT3A was also analyzed by Pearson. Second, the surv_cutpoint function (R survminer, version 0.4.9) was employed to categorize patients into low- and high-expression groups according to the hsa-miR-29a or DNMT3A levels. Then, the survival differences between the two groups (low & high) were compared by the Kaplan‒Meier (KM) method combined with the log-rank test. Finally, we assessed the association between hsa-miR-29a or DNMT3A expression and clinicopathologic parameters in NSCLC patients using the Wilcoxon rank-sum test. The above bioinformatics analysis and visualization were realized by R software.

Cell lines and culture

Human BMSCs (hBMSCs), human bronchial epithelial cells (16HBE), and NSCLC cells (A549) were provided by Pricella Biotechnology Co., Ltd. (Wuhan, China). All the cells were grown in DMEM medium, containing 1% Pen–Strep and 10% FBS. Cell culture was conducted at 37 °C in a 5% CO2 incubator.

Cell transfection

To explore the potential function of miR-29a-3p in NSCLC cells, miR-29a-3p mimics, or negative control (miR-NC) were transiently transfected into A549 cells with HighGene reagent (RM09014, ABclonal, Wuhan, China). In a rescue assay, miRNA mimics with DNMT3A overexpression plasmid were transfected together into A549 cells. After 48 h, the cells were harvested for further assays.

Extraction and characterization of BMSC-Exos

The extraction of BMSC-Exos was performed as previously reported.17 Briefly, the supernatant of 3-d cultured hBMSCs was collected and then sequentially centrifuged at 2000 × g for 30 min and 12000 × g for 45 min to eliminate dead cells, cell debris, and large extracellular vesicles. The supernatant was further filtered using a 0.45 μm filter membrane and centrifuged at 100,000 × g for 120 min to collect the exosomes. The BMSC-Exo pellet was resuspended in 1× PBS (50 μL) and then stored at −80 °C. The morphology of BMSC-Exos was photographed under a Hitachi transmission electron microscope, and its size distribution was evaluated by a nanoparticle tracking analyzer. Moreover, exosomal biomarkers (CD63, CD81, and calnexin) were detected by western blotting. We also transfected miR-29a-3p mimics/inhibitors into hBMSCs using riboFECT™ CP transfection reagent (C10511-05, RiboBio, Guangzhou, China) and then isolated the exosomes.

Exosome labeling and uptake analysis

Cell coculture experiments were conducted to determine whether the extracted BMSC-Exos could enter NSCLC cells. A549 cells (3 × 10⁴ cells/well) were inoculated in 24-well plates and treated with 1 µM Dil (C1036, Beyotime, Shanghai, China)-labeled BMSC-Exos for 24 h. After coculture, the cells were washed and fixed, followed by DAPI staining (C1005, Beyotime, Shanghai, China). Photomicrographs were taken by fluorescence microscopy (TCS SP8, Leica, Weztlar, Germany).

Exosome treatment

We further assessed the effects of BMSC-Exos on the biological functions of NSCLC cell lines. BMSC-Exos transfected with miR-29a-3p mimic, miR-29a-3p inhibitor, and corresponding NC were named BMSCmiR-29a-3p mimic-Exos, BMSCmiR-29a-3p inhibitor-Exos, and BMSCmiR-NC-Exos, respectively. Following, BMSC-Exos (post-transfection) or PBS were supplemented with A549 cells. After incubation for 48 h, the cells were obtained for functional analysis.

Luciferase reporter experiment

The wild-type (WT) or mutant (MUT) 3ʹUTR of DNMT3A was cloned into the pmirGLO vector to establish dual-luciferase reporter vectors. Then, with the assistance of HighGene transfection reagents (RM09014, ABclonal), the recombinant vectors were transfected into A549 cells with miR-29a-3p mimics or miR-NC, followed by incubation at 37 °C and 5% CO2. After reacting for 48 h, the cells were collected/lysed and centrifuged at 15,000 × g for 5 min. The luciferase signal intensity in the supernatant was measured using the Pierce™ Cypridina-Firefly Luciferase Dual Assay Kit (16184, Thermo, Waltham, MA, USA). The fluorescence signal was recorded using the SpectraMax M4 multifunctional microplate reader (Molecular Devices, Shanghai, China).

RT-qPCR

Total RNA was separated from exosomes or cells using Trizol reagents and converted into cDNA using a dedicated kit (KR118-02, TIANGEN, Beijing, China). Next, RT-qPCR was conducted on the CFX96 system using a SYBR Green kit (A4004M, Lifeint, Xiamen, China). U6 or GAPDH was used as a normalization control, with quantification by 2−ΔΔCT. The primers used were as follows (5ʹ-3ʹ):

miR-29a-3p (F): ATGACTGATTTCTTTTGGTC;

miR-29a-3p (R): CTAGCACCATCTGAAATCGGTTAT;

U6 (F): CTCGCTTCGGCAGCAC;

U6 (R): AACGCTTCACGAATTTGCGT;

DNMT3A (F): CTTTTGCGTGGAGTGTGTGG;

DNMT3A (R): ATGCCAACGGCCTGTTCATA;

GAPDH (F): CCTGCACCACCAACTGCTTA;

GAPDH (R): TGAGTCCTTCCACGATACCA.

Western blotting

Proteins were extracted from the lysed exosomes or cells and confirmed with a BCA kit. The protein samples (25 μg) were separated on 10% SDS polyacrylamide gel electrophoresis and then transferred to polyvinylidene difluoride membranes. After blocking, the membrane was incubated overnight at 4 °C with the following primary antibodies: CD63 (AF5117, Affinity, Jiangsu, China), CD81 (DF2306, Affinity), Calnexin (AF5362, Affinity), DNMT3A (3598, CST, Shanghai, China), JAK2 (3230, CST), p-JAK2 (3771, CST), STAT3 (4904, CST), p-STAT3 (9145, CST), and GAPDH (2118, CST). Next, the TBST-washed membranes were treated with the secondary antibody (goat anti-rabbit IgG H&L, Abcam, Cambridge, MA, USA) for 60 min at room temperature. The signals were visualized using ECL luminescent solution, and the membranes were imaged using a gel imaging system. Finally, the data were analyzed with Image J.

Cell activity, migration, and apoptosis experiments

The cell activity was measured by the CCK-8 method. Here, transfected A549 cells (2 × 104 cells/well) were seeded into 96-well plates and placed in an incubator for 48 h. Then, CCK-8 (10 μL, Beyotime) was added to each well, and reacted for 2 h. The absorbance was measured at 450 nm with a microplate reader, and the cell activity was calculated.

The migration ability of A549 cells was evaluated by using the Transwell assay. A549 cells suspended in medium (serum-free, 200 µL) were placed in the top chamber, and 10% FBS medium was supplemented in the bottom chamber. After 24 h, the migrated cells in the bottom chamber were fixed with anhydrous methanol and stained with crystal violet. Three fields of view (200×) were chosen, and the cells were counted under a microscope.

Apoptosis was tested by using Annexin V-FITC/PI staining combined with flow cytometry. Briefly, PBS-resuspended A549 cells were incubated with Annexin V-FITC/PI staining solution (C1062S, Beyotime) in a dark environment for 25 min (room temperature). Immediately afterward, the cells were assessed by flow cytometry. Cell populations exhibiting early apoptosis (Annexin V+/PI−) and late apoptosis (Annexin V+/PI+) are defined as apoptotic.

Statistical analysis

Bioinformatics analysis was conducted using R software. Overall survival analysis was completed by the KM curve with log-rank test, and the correlation between mRNA and miRNA expression was analyzed by Pearson. Quantitative data are expressed as mean ± standard deviation. The cell experimental data were statistically analyzed using GraphPad Prism (version 8.0), with comparison of different groups by ANOVA or t-test. p < 0.05 was defined as significant.

Results

Aberrant expression of miR-29a and DNMT3A is associated with poor prognosis in NSCLC

Based on the TCGA database, hsa-miR-29a exhibited significant downregulation in NSCLC samples, and cases with low hsa-miR-29a expression tended to have poor overall survival (Figure 1A and B). In contrast, DNMT3A expression was elevated in NSCLC tissues, and the survival time of patients with high DNMT3A expression was unfavorable (Figure 1C and D). In addition, in NSCLC, hsa-miR-29a levels were negatively correlated with DNMT3A levels (Figure 1E). We further explored the association between hsa-miR-29a or DNMT3A with common clinicopathologic features of NSCLC. In brief, hsa-miR-29a was highly expressed in female patients with early-stage disease (T1) and lymph node-negative (N0) disease (Figure 1F). DNMT3A was highly expressed in elderly male patients (>65 y), without being related to different pathologic stages (Figure 1G). These evidences initially confirmed that hsa-miR-29a and DNMT3A are valuable prognostic biomarkers in NSCLC.

Characterization of miR-29a and DNMT3A in NSCLC tissues from TCGA databases. (A) Violin plot showing the expression levels of hsa-miR-29a in normal and NSCLC samples. (B) Kaplan‒Meier curve comparing overall survival in NSCLC patients with high and low expression of hsa-miR-29a. (C) Violin plot showing the expression levels of DNMT3A in normal and NSCLC samples. (D) Kaplan‒Meier plot comparing overall survival in NSCLC patients with high and low expression of DNMT3A. (E) Scatter plot showing the correlation between hsa-miR-29a and DNMT3A. (F and G) Box plots revealing the association between hsa-miR-29a or DNMT3A expression and common clinical features (age, gender, and pathologic_T, N, M) of NSCLC. *p < 0.05, **p < 0.01, ***p < 0.001, and ****p < 0.0001.

BMSC-derived exosomes inhibit NSCLC cell survival/migration and promote apoptosis

We isolated exosomes from the culture medium of BMSC and then cocultured them with A549 cells to investigate the functions of BMSC-Exos on NSCLC. As observed by TEM, the particle isolated from the supernatant of BMSC presented as an elliptical vesicle with a double-layer membrane, indicating that it was indeed exosome (Figure 2A). Its average diameter was 123 nm (Figure 2B). Western blotting showed that the presence of exosomal marker proteins (CD63 and CD81) in exosomes without the endoplasmic reticulum molecule Calnexin (Figure 2C). After incubating with A549 cells, the Dil-labeled exosomes emitted red fluorescence in the cells, revealing that the exosomes could be effectively internalized by A549 cells (Figure 2D). Afterward, BMSC-Exo treatment obviously repressed the activity and migration of A549 cells, while promoted the apoptosis rate of the cells (Figure 2E–G). Thus, BMSC-derived exosomes can be taken up by cancer cells and slow down the progression of NSCLC.

BMSC-derived exosomes inhibit the malignant phenotype of NSCLC cells. (A) TEM displaying the appearance of BMSC-Exos. (B) A nanoparticle tracking analyzer was used to detect the diameter of BMSC-Exos particles. (D) Representative immunofluorescence image showing that DiI-labeled BMSC-Exos (red) were internalized by A549 cells (DAPI, blue). BMSC-Exos regulate the malignant phenotype of NSCLC cells by releasing miR-29a-3p

Given the positive role of miR-29a-3p in antitumor activity, we investigated the biological functions of BMSC-exosomal miR-29a-3p in NSCLC. RT-qPCR assay revealed an obviously elevated level of miR-29a-3p in BMSC-Exos compared to A549 cells (Figure 3A). Next, we transfected miR-29a-3p inhibitors or mimics into BMSC and then extracted exosomes. As shown in Figure 3B, the addition of miRNA mimics or inhibitors to BMSCs markedly promoted or suppressed miR-29a-3p expression in exosomes, respectively. Following functional experiments indicated that BMSCmiR-29a-3p mimic-Exos further notably suppressed the viability and migration ability of cancer cells, whereas increasing the apoptosis rate compared with those of the control groups (BMSCmiR-NC-Exos). Undoubtedly, miR-29a-3p deletion in BMSC-Exos substantially weakened the inhibitory action of BMSC-Exos on A549 cells (Figure 3C–G).

Further, we explored the specific mechanism of BMSC-Exos in NSCLC treatment. We detected the expression changes of DNMT3A (a target gene of miR-29a-3p) among the different groups. BMSC-Exos transfected with miR-29a-3p mimics markedly suppressed DNMT3A level in A549 cells, whereas exosomes transfected with the miRNA inhibitor caused the elevation of DNMT3A expression in A549 cells (Figure 3H and I). The evidence suggests that the upregulation of DNMT3A can activate the JAK2/STAT3 signaling pathway, which is involved in tumor angiogenesis, so targeting this pathway is a powerful strategy in antiangiogenic therapy for NSCLC.18,19 Western blotting results indicated that BMSCmiR-29a-3p mimic-Exos treatment significantly inhibited the activity of the JAK2/STAT3 pathway compared to control groups (PBS and BMSCmiR-NC-Exos), as evidenced by the decrease in p-JAK2 and p-STAT3 expression. However, miR-29a-3p-deficient exosomes exerted the opposite effect, dramatically activating the phosphorylation of JAK2 and STAT3 in A549 cells (Figure 3H,J,K). These results confirm that BMSC-Exo-derived miR-29a-3p controls NSLCLC progression by inhibiting the JAK2/STAT3 signaling pathway.

miR-29a-3p affects the NSCLC cell phenotype by suppressing DNMT3A and modulating the JAK2/STAT3 pathway

To validate these findings, we also explored the potential mechanism and downstream signals of miR-29a-3p in NSCLC. RT-qPCR analysis showed A549 cells exhibiting low miR-29a-3p expression and high DNMT3A expression compared to normal cells (16HBE) (Figure 4A and B). Figure 4C displays the potential binding sites between miR-29a-3p and DNMT3A. This finding was further confirmed by a dual-luciferase reporter experiment. As shown in Figure 4D, miRNA mimics caused a decrease in the luciferase activity of the DNMT3A-WT, whereas had a negligible effect on the DNMT3A-MUT, revealing a direct link between DNMT3A and miR-29a-3p. Following this, the cell phenotype was observed in the miR-29a-3p mimics or miR-29a-3p mimics + OE-DNMT3A-transfected A549 cells. CCK-8 revealed that miR-29a-3p mimics notably inhibited cell viability, but this effect was reversed by DNMT3A overexpression (Figure 4E). Similarly, the inhibitory influence of miR-29a-3p mimics on cell migration was obviously weakened after DNMT3A overexpression (Figure 4F). DNMT3A overexpression also counteracted the promotion of the apoptosis rate in A549 cells by miR-29a-3p mimics (Figure 4G). Besides, miR-29a-3p mimic treatment downregulated DNMT3A protein expression, further confirming the negative control of DNMT3A by miR-29a-3p (Figure 4H and I). For the JAK2/STAT3 pathway, DNMT3A overexpression treatment attenuated the control effect of miRNA mimics on p-JAK2 and p-STAT3 expression (Figure 4H,J,K).

Discussion

Exosomes can regulate signaling in cancer by participating in multiple biological processes associated with cancer onset, progression, and metastasis, and this ability offers a wealth of new approaches for cancer treatment.20 Among them, BMSC-Exos can naturally target inflammatory sites and easily fuse with cancer cells to modulate their behavior.21 As abundant elements in exosomal cargoes, miRNAs can effectively regulate gene expression levels and influence NSCLC biological properties.22 Besides, BMSC is easily transfected by lentivirus, ensuring stable and long-term transcription of miRNAs.23 Thus, miRNAs within BMSC-Exo are promising areas for NSCLC treatment and will help to improve the prognosis and prediction of cancer patients in clinical practice.

We observed that miR-29a level was reduced in cancer tissues and its high expression was observed in early-stage NSCLC patients (T1 and N0). Notably, high miR-29a expression predicted a superior prognosis for NSCLC patients. Subsequent functional experiments determined that miR-29a-3p distinctly inhibited the malignant phenotype of A549 cells, revealing its anticancer effects. miR-29a has been found to be downregulated in proliferation and drug resistance in various cancers, such as lung cancer and myeloid leukemia, with higher levels strongly associated with longer overall survival.24 This is consistent with our findings. Although 3p and 5p arms exist in miR-29a, the miRNA of the 3p arm is the most abundant in the human miR-29 family, so most cancer-related studies have focused on miR-29a-3p.25 No exception, in this work, miR-29a-3p was researched in cell experiments. We found that BMSC-Exos could release miR-29a-3p. BMSC-Exos as well as BMSC-Exos containing miR-29a-3p overexpression promoted apoptosis and delayed cell growth in A549 cells. Inversely, deletion of miR-29a-3p in the BMSC-Exos eliminated the anticancer effect of the exosomes, suggesting that the exosomal miR-29a-3p plays a key role in repressing the malignant progression of NSCLC. However, the regulatory mechanism of miR-29a-3p as a tumor suppressor in NSCLC remains to be elucidated. It is known that genomic instability is a key feature of cancer and promotes malignant transformation.26 In this process, defects in DNA damage response genes exacerbate genomic instability.27 The connection between miRNAs and DNA damage response has been discovered.28 Early evidence described that the overexpression of miRNAs such as miR-16 can cause DNA damage at the G1/S checkpoint, leading to apoptosis in cancer cells.29 Based on this, we speculate that miR-29a-3p may serve as a potential target for cancer treatment by regulating DNA damage signals. This remains to be further explored.

Further, we researched the anti-NSCLC mechanism of exosomal miR-29a-3p. Previous studies have confirmed that DNMT3A is a direct target of miR-29a-3p and the tumor suppressor function of miR-29a-3p may be disabled by the overexpression of DNMT3A.16,30,31 In this study, we observed that DNMT3A was markedly enriched in NSCLC samples and its high expression predicted poor survival of patients. DNMT3A, a key enzyme responsible for epigenetic regulation, is frequently upregulated in lung cancer and linked to poor prognosis.32 Liu et al. demonstrated that miR-708-5p can reduce DNA methylation by targeting DNMT3A, thus effectively inhibiting lung cancer cell stemness.33 These findings further support our results. Afterward, we revealed that miR-29a-3p negatively regulated the expression of DNMT3A. Moreover, miR-29a-3p can inhibit the malignant behavior of NSCLC cells by downregulating DNMT3A expression, while the overexpression of DNMT3A reversed this effect. Hence, the negative regulation of DNMT3A by miR-29a-3p is essential for the maintenance of its anticancer function. However, a recent article presented contradictory results, that is, DNMT3A has a tumor suppressor function in lung cancer, and DNMT3A deletion reduces the CpG methylation level of the HDAC6 promoter, leading to the upregulation of HDAC6, thus promoting the malignant phenotype of lung cancer.34 This contradictory result reflects the complexity of tumor biology and that the expression levels and mutation types of DNMT3A in different patients are highly heterogeneous. Notably, DNMT3A exhibits the highest mutation rate among the DNMT family, and its mutations are frequently observed in various cancers, such as lung cancer, carrying significant clinical implications.35,36 Ricciuti et al. found that DNMT3A mutations serve as independent predictors for improving progression-free survival and overall survival in NSCLC.37 Specifically, pathogenic DNMT3A mutations lead to reduced RNA and protein expression levels; these NSCLC patients with lower DNMT3A expression exhibit enhanced immune infiltration and demonstrate a higher response rate to immunotherapy. Furthermore, multiple analyses have revealed that numerous DNMT3A mutations are predicted to be loss-of-function events, which are expected to introduce premature termination codons and then trigger nonsense-mediated mRNA decay, ultimately reducing mRNA transcription levels in the mutant allele.38,39 However, among the available NSCLC cell lines, not all DNMT3 mutation models display reduced DNMT3A expression levels, suggesting that mechanisms beyond mutation-induced inactivation may contribute to gene expression alterations.37 In addition, it remains unconfirmed whether A549 cells (the cell line used in this study) carry oncogenic DNMT3A gene mutations. Therefore, a detailed examination of DNMT3A mutation/expression status in patients with NSCLC is essential for elucidating its function. Nevertheless, it is undeniable that based on the existing evidence, DNMT3A inhibition may emerge as a potential therapeutic strategy for NSCLC.

Activation of the JAK2/STAT3 pathway is known to initiate and promote tumor progression by promoting uncontrolled cell proliferation and stimulating the angiogenesis process.40 This phenomenon has also been observed in NSCLC, and this pathway has emerged as a powerful target for NSCLC therapy.19 There is evidence that the anticolorectal cancer stemness effect of Wu Mei Wan (a Chinese medicine compound) is related to the DNMT3A-mediated JAK2/STAT3 pathway.41 miR-29a also promotes apoptosis by inhibiting the JAK2/STAT3 pathway in myoblasts.42 Therefore, we explored whether miR-29a-3p regulated changes in the JAK2/STAT3 signaling pathway by targeting DNMT3A. Western blotting analysis revealed that miR-29a-3p mimic suppressed p-JAK2 and p-STAT3 expression, which was reversed by DNMT3A overexpression. These results emphasize that miR-29a-3p controls NSCLC cell fate through the DNMT3A-mediated JAK2/STAT3 pathway.

For the first time, this work reveals that BMSC-derived exosomal miR-29a-3p inhibits the malignant progression of NSCLC by suppressing the DNMT3A/JAK2/STAT3 axis. However, our findings are mainly from cell experiments, and further studies with animal or clinical samples are needed to confirm these results. Besides, there are complex interactions among tumor, immune, and stromal cells in the tumor microenvironment, and future research should elucidate the specific regulatory mechanisms of BMSC-derived exosomal miR-29a-3p in the immune microenvironment of NSCLC. Additionally, although BMSC-Exos have emerged as a promising platform for cancer therapy because of their excellent biocompatibility, low toxicity, and minimal immunogenicity,43 there are still many challenges in translating this therapeutic approach into clinical practice. For example, how to achieve large-scale production of highly exosomes and how to ensure they maintain high tumor-targeting efficiency in vivo. Overcoming these translational challenges is critical to its successful clinical application.

Conclusion

Overall, this study emphasizes the critical function of BMSC-derived exosomal miR-29a-3p in cellular communication, which may be responsible for regulating NSCLC aggressiveness through the downstream DNMT3A/JAK2/STAT3 axis. These findings reveal that BMSC-derived exosomal miR-29a-3p may be a promising biomarker for improving NSCLC and provide new options for NSCLC therapy.
