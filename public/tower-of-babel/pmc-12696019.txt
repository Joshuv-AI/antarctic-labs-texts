Decomposition of socioeconomic inequalities in zero-dose children aged 12–23 months in India
Authors: Mriganka Dolui, Sanjit Sarkar, Anupkumar Chambhare, Sameer Kumar Jena, Nawazish Hasan Kazmi
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractChildren who do not receive the preliminary dose of the DTP-containing vaccine are likely to be known as zero-dose, occurring due to limited access to healthcare services and several sociodemographic patterns. In India, 6.67% points (pp) of children remain zero-dose, and a significant inequality exists due to socioeconomic factors. Therefore, this study measures the magnitude of the socioeconomic gap and quantifies the key determinants that contribute to the zero-dose inequality using the National Family Health Survey fifth round (NFHS-5) data. A cross-sectional sampling data of 41,132 children aged 12–23 months was considered to employ several descriptive statistics, including a chi-squared test. We utilized the Wagstaff concentration curve (WCC) and concentration index (WCI) to represent the zero-dose inequality. Afterwards, WCI was decomposed to determine the major contributing factors to the socioeconomic gap in zero-dose. The overall prevalence of zero-dose children was 6.67 pp, and a socioeconomic gap in zero-dose by the household wealth index was 3.68 pp between the poorest and the richest. The WCI value for zero-dose due to the wealth index was − 0.12 (p < 0.001), revealed 25.15% of the inequality was contributed by the wealth index, 12.48% by maternal education, 11.57% by media exposure, 10.74% by antenatal care (ANC) visits, and 10.05% by place of children’s delivery. To reduce zero-dose among children, multi-dimensional interventions and strategies are needed to address not only economic deprivation but also disparities in maternal health education, access to healthcare, and informational reach. While wealth-related inequality is evident, the wealth index captures broader household assets and living conditions beyond income. Similarly, media exposure may reflect extended access to health-related knowledge, including immunization. Introduction

Immunization at an early age is a crucial public health intervention to prevent morbidity and mortality associated with several bacterial and viral diseases, such as Diphtheria, Pertussis, Tetanus (DTP/DPT), Polio, Measles, Rubella, etc1,2. An Extended Program on Immunization (EPI) was launched by the World Health Organization (WHO) in 1974, and to date, vaccination has saved 154 million lives and has contributed to reducing 40% infant mortality. Moreover, it prevents four-five million deaths annually, with an additional 1.5 million deaths that can be averted worldwide by improving immunization coverage. In addition, the massive immunization coverage in the community has the potential to protect the rest of the unvaccinated people in the community, which is referred to as the ‘herd immunity’. Achieving the massive immunization coverage fosters the ‘herd immunity’, which serves as an effective mechanism to limit disease transmission within communities3. According to a recent report by WHO & United Nations Children’s Fund (UNICEF) (2025), nearly 14.3 million children remained unvaccinated globally despite steady coverage in immunization. It also has broader gains in terms of economic and educational development1.

Globally, 84% of the infants have been immunized against the diphtheria-tetanus-pertussis (DTP3) vaccine4. Indeed, there are significant geographical and regional inequalities in vaccination coverage across countries like Nigeria (56.4%), Angola (55.2%), Pakistan (46.6%), Myanmar (45.2%), and Bangladesh (26.9%), showing the low- and middle-income countries (LMICs) falling behind in immunization rates4,5. Nearly 14.3 million newborns missed out on receiving their first dose of the DTP-containing vaccine, and six million infants received only partial vaccination, indicating limited access to immunization and healthcare services. Among the total of 20.5 million affected infants, nearly 60% of them reside in ten selected countries, namely Angola, Brazil, the Democratic Republic of the Congo, Ethiopia, India, Indonesia, Mozambique, Nigeria, Pakistan, and the Philippines4,6.

The vaccination status, categorized as either under-vaccinated or unvaccinated, is determined using the DTP as a proxy indicator. The DTP is preferred because household surveys assessing measles and polio coverage may include both routine and campaign doses, while Bacillus Calmette–Guérin (BCG) vaccination is not universally part of the national schedule of each country and is administered over various channels. Unvaccinated children, or those classified as zero-dose, have not yet received the initial dose of the DTP-containing vaccine (DTP1) as per the operational definition of zero-dose children from Immunization Agenda 2030 (IA2030) and UNICEF, indicating limited access to immunization services7,8. On the other hand, under-vaccinated children are those who have received DTP1 but have not completed the full course or received the third dose of the DTP-containing vaccine (DTP3), which suggests deficiencies or gaps in the performance of immunization programs2,8,9. Children who remain unvaccinated or under-vaccinated reflect not just access issues but often are more profound structural deprivation, including poor education, healthcare, rural and remote residence, broader social and economic exclusion10–13. Zero-dose in children had a greater chance of higher morbidity and mortality because of suboptimal outcomes related to zero-dose. The majority of all child mortality from disease, including whooping cough and measles, is more due to an unstable immune system resulting from zero-dose or delayed vaccination14. Previous literature suggests that the morbidity rate related to zero-dose was significantly associated with the rate of immunization delay15. The children being zero-dose or declining vaccination would be precarious, as the antibody or immune system declines, resulting in childhood infectious diseases, morbidity and developmental delay16. The high proportion of zero-dose children is most likely associated with developmental delay17.

In India’s Universal Immunisation Programme (UIP), the Government of India has decided that the DTP-containing vaccine is given as the Pentavalent vaccine in three doses at 6, 10, and 14 weeks18. A subsequent booster dose of DTP is administered at 16–24 months. For operational and reporting purposes, zero-dose children are defined as children who have not received the first dose of the pentavalent vaccine (DTP-containing vaccine 1 st dose) in India2,6. A significant proportion of zero-dose children, comprising two-thirds, originate from families living below the international poverty level of $1.9 per day, highlighting the absence of vital services in these marginalized communities2,19. These do not merely indicate a lack of vital services, but often could also reflect systemic issues such as limited social security, poor healthcare infrastructure, and persistent marginalization, which hinder or zero-dose among the poorest households20. Therefore, providing immunization and connecting these families with other health, economic, and social benefits can address various Sustainable Development Goals (SDGs), including poverty reduction (SDG 1), improved nutrition (SDG 2), good health and well-being (SDG 3), enhanced educational outcomes (SDG 4), and diminished inequalities (SDG 10)21. Targeting the zero-dose children for vaccination can have a cascading effect, leading to increased coverage of full immunization2,22. This approach holds substantial potential for reducing child mortality, as almost half of deaths from vaccine-preventable diseases occur in zero-dose children2.

In India, the annual target of the UIP is approximately 26 million, and the WHO and the UNICEF estimate that National Immunization Coverage, DTP-1 (Penta-1) vaccine coverage reached 94% in 2019, representing about 1.4 million children who had not received any dose2,6. By 2020, the number of zero-dose children in the country doubled, reaching 2.9 million due to disruptions caused by the COVID-19 pandemic6. A countrywide prior study revealed that the routine immunization rate dipped during COVID, and a significant number of zero-dose children remain in India23. Even the rate of receipt of the DTP vaccine on time was 3%−5% lower among COVID-affected children relative to unaffected children in India24. It is crucial for countries to monitor immunization data at local levels to prioritize and customize vaccination strategies and plans effectively. This approach is essential for bridging the immunization gaps and ensuring life-saving vaccines reach everyone6,25. A prior study conducted in 92 low- and middle-income countries (LMICs) based on the Demographic and Health Survey (DHS) and Multiple Indicator Cluster Surveys (MICS) showed that children born outside health facilities had a higher prevalence of zero-dose vaccinations due to factors such as a lack of antenatal care (ANC) visits or missed maternal tetanus vaccinations26. This indicates that children who have not received any vaccine dose reside in households, settings, and environments where vaccine access is lacking and access to other essential health services is critically absent.

However, the trend of zero-dose children has decreased by approximately one million, as reported in 2022. The National Family Health Survey (NFHS) also reported a declining trend in the prevalence of zero-dose children over the years, dropping from 33.4% in NFHS-1 (1992–1993) to 6.4% in NFHS-5 (2019–2021)6,27. In India, national immunisation efforts such as the Universal Immunisation Programme (UIP), Mission Indradhanush (2014) and its intensified phases, and digital tracking mechanisms have played a critical role in improving vaccination coverage and reducing zero-dose prevalence over time28. These interventions have been supported by strengthened cold-chain infrastructure, wider deployment of community health workers such as Accredited Social Health Activists (ASHAs) and Auxiliary Nurse Midwives (ANMs), and targeted outreach in underserved areas. Despite these advances, the pandemic and persistent barriers such as geographic inaccessibility and health system gaps contributed to a rise in zero-dose children in India. Socioeconomic inequities remain a critical concern for zero-dose children in India, where demographic characteristics associated with multiple deprivation, including wealth status, education, and access to health care services, are significantly associated with disparities in immunization rates2,29. Prior evidence suggests that at the national level, which pointed out that zero-dose occurrence was higher among children from the poorest quintile, underprivileged social groups and mothers with no formal education30,31. However, the structural barriers such as poverty, geographic isolation, and limited access to maternal health services may exacerbate this inequality, while social determinants—such as caste-based marginalization and lack of media exposure further compound the risk of zero-dose children in India32. However, to the best of our knowledge, all the previous studies have shown the inequality related to immunization in India; while there is no study explaining the socioeconomic inequality in zero-dose children and the contributing factors to the inequality in the currently available dataset in the Indian context. Thus, using the most recent nationally representative samples of children collected by NFHS, the objective of the study was to measure socioeconomic disparity among zero-dose children and identify the factors contributing to inequality. Therefore, in order to fill the gap in the existing literature, the extent of wealth-related inequality in zero-dose rates among children in India must be quantified to guide vaccination efforts.

Methods

Data source

We have used data from the NFHS, round 5 (NFHS-5; 2019–2021), which provides vital insights into various health and social domains across India, conducted by the International Institute for Population Sciences (IIPS) under the Ministry of Health and Family Welfare (MoHFW)27. The survey encompasses all 28 states and eight union territories, including statistics on reproductive and child health, women’s autonomy, and nutrition. This data is in line with many SDGs. It sampled 636,699 households, 101,839 men (ages 15–54), 724,115 women (ages 15–49), and 232,920 children, offering detailed information on population, family planning, maternal health, child immunization and nutrition, adult health, and domestic violence for India and its 707 districts.

This study focuses on and is restricted to children aged 12–23 months in India. In India, the national immunization schedule recommends that the DTP-containing vaccine be given in three doses at 6, 10, and 14 weeks till one year (12 months) of age18. Followed by a subsequent booster dose of DTP is administered at 16–24 months. Further, a child has been recommended to complete the first DTP dose before they reach their first birthday, even if they have missed the recommended schedule of DTPs. Therefore, any children who have missed their first DTP dose before they reach twelve months are considered the zero-dose children. Considering the exposure time of twelve months to receive at least one DTP, the prevalence of zero doses has been estimated for the age group 12–23 months of children. This definition has also been used in WHO’s IA2030, UNICEF, and several other studies7,8,33. The NFHS-5 collected information on 232,920 children at the age of 0–59 months, including immunization information. Among them, 187,821 were excluded from the dataset, those children not under the age of 12–23 months (children aged below 12 months and above 23 months). Afterwards, from the remaining 45,099 children aged 12–23 months, 2,327 children were excluded due to missing data in social groups (caste). Subsequently, from the remaining 42,772 children aged 12–23 months, 1,640 were excluded due to missing information in the DTP1 dose. Therefore, the final sample for the study was 41,132 children aged 12–23 months after excluding all the missing values in different socioeconomic characteristics (please see Supplementary Table 1). A detailed methodology with a sampling procedure for the data is available in another publication27.

Outcome variable

Children aged 12–23 months were considered to have received all basic vaccinations if, according to a vaccination card or their mother’s report. The outcome variable of the study was children’s zero-dose, defined by WHO’s IA2030 and UNICEF as children who have not received the first dose of the diphtheria, tetanus and pertussis (DTP) vaccine, are described as zero-dose7,8. In this study, we have calculated the number of zero-dose children as all alive children from the 12–23 months age group who did not receive the first dose of the pentavalent vaccine (DTP-containing vaccine: DTP1) (h3), else considered as vaccinated. As our outcome variable is binary in nature, zero-dose children were coded as 0 for ‘vaccinated’ and 1 for ‘zero-dose.’

Explanatory variables

This study used a set of explanatory variables to explore the determinants of zero-dose among children 12–23 months. Based on previous literature, we have included children’s age (in months), sex of the child, mother’s age, place of delivery, antenatal care (ANC) visits, castes, religion, place of residence, region, mother’s education, wealth index (quantile), place of vaccine, problems of distance to the health center, getting permission for access to care, getting money for access to care, and taking transportation for access to care, and media exposure as explanatory variables in the study31,34–38. Statistical analysis

In this study, we used descriptive statistics to calculate the sample distribution and preliminary outcome. The bivariate analysis was employed to explore the associations between zero-dose status and selected demographic and socioeconomic variables. These analyses are descriptive in nature and do not imply causal relationships. Furthermore, a binary logistic regression model was carried out to identify the key determinants of zero-dose among children. After that, the Wagstaff concentration index (WCI) was measured, which provides the extent of socioeconomic inequality in zero-dose, and this inequality was visually represented by the Wagstaff concentration curve (WCC). Furthermore, a decomposition analysis of the WCI was conducted to identify the crucial factors contributing to the socioeconomic inequality in zero-dose. The detailed explanation of the binary logistic regression model, WCI, WCC, and decomposition of WCI is explained in the following segment.

After estimating the logistic regression model, we transformed the coefficient from the log-odds scale into marginal effects to facilitate transparency and interpretation. Marginal effects are computed to explain the effect on the conditional mean of y of a change in one of the regressors, say 41. This will often provide a robust estimation of the amount of the change in Y that will be produced by one unit change in . With a binary outcome variable such as zero-dose, it offers some advantages that the Linear Probability Model gives a single number that expresses the effect of a variable P(Y = 1)41. Hence, the results of the regression analysis are presented as an adjusted odds ratio (AOR) with a p-value at a 95% confidence interval (CI), and the marginal effects.

The inequality analysis is constructed on the WCI and WCC. The WCI measure the magnitude of socioeconomic inequality in zero-dose and to which socioeconomic groups it is concentrated, whether among wealthier or poorer groups. The value of WCI quantifies the magnitude of the inequality, ranging from − 1 to + 1. A positive value indicates the concentration of the children’s zero-dose among advantaged groups (wealthier) individuals, whereas a negative indices values indicate the concentration of the children’s zero-dose among disadvantaged groups (poorer) individuals.

We have plotted the concentration curve to visualize socioeconomic (wealth-based) inequality in zero-dose. The concentration curve illustrates the relationship between the cumulative proportion of individuals with zero-dose children and the cumulative percentage of the population, ranked according to their wealth index position (score), with deviations from the 45-degree line of equality indicating inequality.

Finally, the Wagstaff decomposition analysis was employed to quantify the magnitude of the contribution of each variable to income-based inequality in the zero-dose42. However, the value of WCI reflects the degree of inequality; it does not provide the pathway through which the inequality occurs43. Decomposing this inequality is crucial to explore how the determinant factors proportionately contribute to socioeconomic inequality in health outcomes43–45. Hence, to determine the contributing factors to the socioeconomic gap in zero-dose, we decompose the WCI to measure the contribution in percentage of each covariate to this inequality. For decomposing the socioeconomic inequality, we applied the Wagstaff decomposition analysis following the regression model that links the health outcome variable (Y) to a set of key determinants (Xk) as proposed by Wagstaff46,47. A negative contribution explains that an independent variable contributes towards a poorer distribution of zero-dose, while a positive contribution indicates that an explanatory variable operates towards the richest distribution of zero-dose42. Additionally, we have performed a variance inflation factor (VIF) to check the multicollinearity across the variables50, and there is no multicollinearity in the analysis (mean VIF=1.33; minimum VIF=1.00 and maximum VIF=2.08) (please see Supplementary Table 2). Results

Prevalence of zero-dose

Table 2 shows the prevalence of zero-dose among children aged 12–23 months by various socioeconomic and demographic characteristics. Children aged 20–23 months exhibit a slightly higher zero-dose prevalence (6.78%) compared to those aged 12–15 months (6.62%) and 16–19 months (6.62%). Likewise, female children (7.00%) and children born to mothers aged 39–49 years have a significantly higher zero-dose prevalence (10.61%) compared to male children (6.37%) and those born to mothers aged 15–22 years (6.39%). Moreover, children born at home have a substantially higher zero-dose prevalence (12.96%) compared to those born in government (5.72%) or private (6.72%) facilities. Similarly, children’s mothers with no ANC visits and children from Scheduled tribe (ST) social groups had a higher prevalence of zero-dose at 12.03% and 7.82%, respectively. The prevalence of zero-dose among children was significantly higher among those who were from a Muslim religious background and resided in the northeastern region at 9.99% and 9.82%, respectively. Another crucial factor is the mother’s education, and the zero-dose prevalence was higher among the mothers with no education at 10.43%. Likewise, the zero-dose prevalence was higher among the children from the poorest wealth index category at 8.85%, followed by the children from the poorer wealth index category (7.16%). Moreover, children’s vaccination places at private institutions and distance to the health centre are a big problem, had a substantially higher zero-dose prevalence at 40.95% and 7.56%, respectively. The prevalence of children’s zero-dose is higher among those mothers who reported that getting permission for access to care is a big problem, getting money for access to care is a big problem, and taking transportation for access to care is a big problem at 8.88%, 8.28%, and 8.06%, respectively. Media exposure is yet another significant factor that is significantly associated with the occurrence of zero-dose; mothers who had no media exposure had a higher prevalence of zero-dose at 9.36%.

Determinants and association of children’s zero-dose

Table 3 shows the determinants and associations of zero-dose children aged 12–23 months in India. The results revealed that the children whose place of delivery was at home and mothers with no ANC visits were 1.8 times (AOR: 1.804; CI:1.523–2.138; p < 0.001) and 1.79 times (AOR: 1.797; CI:1.570–2.058; p < 0.001) more likely to have zero-dose occurrence compared to children who born at private health instituitions and mothers with more than four ANC visits, respectively. The children from Scheduled Tribes (ST) social groups and Muslim religion were 1.16 times (AOR: 1.160; CI: 0.985–1.365; p < 0.01) and 1.4 times (AOR: 1.402; CI: 1.236–1.591; p < 0.001) more likely to be zero-dose children, than those are from Scheduled caste (ST) and hindu religion, respectively. The children reside in rural areas, and the eastern region had 17.2% (AOR: 0.828; CI: 0.735–0.934; p < 0.001) and 32.4% (AOR: 0.676; CI: 0.555–0.823; p < 0.001) less likely to be zero-dose children, compared to the children from the urban and northern region. The lower maternal education is significantly associated with zero-dose children; especially mothers with no education, who were 1.94 times (AOR: 1.944; CI: 1.615–2.340; p < 0.001) more likely to have zero-dose children compared to mothers with higher educational status. Similarly, children from poor economic status, especially children from the poorest wealth index, were 2.13 times (AOR: 2.133; CI: 1.719–2.646; p < 0.001) more likely to be zero-dose children compared to children from the richest wealth index. The place of vaccination, yet another significant factor of zero-dose, where children vaccinated in private places were 31.68 times (AOR: 31.683; CI: 28.393–35.355; p < 0.001) more likely to be zero-dose children, compared to the public place of vaccination. The mothers who reported getting permission for access to care, getting money for access to care, and taking transportation for access to care is a big problem, the likelihood of zero-dose of their children was higher at 1.19 times (AOR: 1.186; CI: 1.041–1.352; p < 0.05), 1.18 times (AOR: 1.180; CI: 1.034–1.347; p < 0.05) and 1.19 times (AOR: 1.191; CI: 1.031–1.378; p < 0.001), compared to those reported there is no problem in getting permission, in getting money, and taking transportation for access to care, respectively. The media exposure of the mothers was significantly associated with children’s zero-dose, and media exposure by the mothers was 18% (AOR: 0.820; CI: 0.741–0.906; p < 0.001) less likely to be zero-dose among their children compared to the children whose mothers had no media exposure.

Concentration index and decomposition of the socioeconomic inequality in zero-dose children

The Wagstaff decomposition analysis, reflecting socioeconomic inequality in zero-dose children by the WCI. Additionally, it provides decomposition results, aiming to understand how various background characteristics contribute to the observed inequality in no vaccination coverage (zero-dose) among children. The results revealed the inequality in the distribution of healthcare services across a population, with positive values indicating a concentration among the wealthy and negative values among the poor. It reveals that due to wealth, children from poorer households are more likely to have zero dozers, as indicated by the negative concentration indices of −0.1213 (SE: 0.01139; p-value: <0.001) for wealth, signifying that there is a wealth-based inequality exists in zero-dose among children in India (Table 4). This inequality was captured by the concentration curve in Fig. 3. The curve depicts a significant association between the cumulative share of zero-dose children in the vertical axis, ranked by the cumulative share of the population, ranked by the wealth index, in the horizontal axis. The concentration curve for the zero-dose lies above the line of equality (45-degree line), indicating that zero-dose occurrence is majorly concentrated among children from poorer households.

Discussion

The primary objective of the study was to measure the socioeconomic disparity and quantify the magnitude of this inequality in zero-dose status among children aged 12–23 months in India. Further, it determined the different factors which significantly associated with children’s zero-dose. This unique study employed the concentration index and decomposition analysis to quantify and explain socioeconomic inequality in zero-dose.

The findings revealed that zero-dose occurrence is majorly concentrated among children from socioeconomically poor backgrounds, which is consistent with previous investigations53–56. The Household wealth status is a significant contributor by 24.35 pp in wealth-based inequality among children with zero-dose. The rising socioeconomic disparities in immunization coverage in India may contribute to a wider wealth disparity in childcare services35. Prior studies have shown similar trends, where immunization uptake in India has been strongly shaped by economic inequalities, with poorer households persistently lagging behind wealthier counterparts in achieving full coverage57. Hence, poor housing is a significant predictor of zero-dose, along with increasing income gap and wealth inequality22,58. In India, a higher household income has been found to correlate with full vaccination coverage and a lower rate of zero-dose prevalence, echoing broader evidence of health services inequalities tied to socioeconomic status10,57. A few global studies have also reported that child vaccination is higher among children born to the richest households than the poorest ones59,60. This underscores a complex set of structural and behavioural mechanisms in the household, where poorer households often face direct and indirect costs, including transportation, missed wages and service fees that deter immunization services uptake14,38,57,61. Moreover, the zero-dose children from poorer households are marked as missed communities, also experience multiple forms of deprivation, such as inadequate housing, food insecurity, and limited access to basic necessities, which collectively push preventive care like immunization down their immediate priorities2,62–64. This calls for multisectoral interventions, including mobile vaccination drives and travel subsidies for the removal of financial barriers and strengthening equitable access to immunization in India.

The maternal education exacerbated the disparity in vaccination rates between higher and lower-income groups38. Maternal education emerged as the second-place contributor to socioeconomic inequality in zero-dose accounting for 12.06 pp. Consistent with prior studies, mothers with less or no education were more likely to miss or delay their child’s65. Global studies suggest that parental literacy improves awareness of immunisation schedules to ensure their children are properly immunized, particularly for the first dose of the DTP-containing vaccine (DTP1)59,66. In India, maternal education has been repeatedly shown to be a strong determinant of immunisation coverage: children born to mothers with higher education are significantly more likely to be vaccinated compared to those born to mothers with no schooling11,57,63,64,67. This signifies that mothers with primary education and above possess greater knowledge related to vaccines, and educated mothers are more likely to immunize their children compared to illiterate mothers10. Now, the impact of increasing maternal education may have consequences for decreasing zero-dose phenomena among children in India31,36,68,69. Mothers with no education or less education are more likely to miss the vaccination of their children or hesitate to do so65. Vaccine hesitancy sometimes becomes the cause of zero doses due to delays in acceptance or refusal of vaccines, despite the availability of vaccination services tends to limit full immunization for children70,71. Validating this, the most common reasons for vaccine hesitancy in India are literacy and their knowledge of the vaccination schedule71. A long-term approach may overcome vaccine hesitancy that involves the education of mothers and adolescents on the basics of immunization and critical thinking, and its consequences72. Beyond awareness, maternal education also enhances women’s autonomy and decision-making abilities, which are critical in determining healthcare-seeking behaviours, including visits to health centres and routine immunization in India10,63,73. Thus, investing in female education has the potential to generate intergenerational benefits by reducing the prevalence of zero-dose children in India.

The study explicitly highlighted that media exposure by the mothers significantly contributes to wealth-based inequality among children’s zero-dose in India, accounting for 10.88%. Consistent with prior findings, our study revealed that mothers with media exposure were less likely to have zero-dose children compared to those without access to mass media37,74,75. Media exposure enhances the awareness of vaccination schedules, the benefits of immunization, and empowers mothers to evaluate the efficacy of vaccine-preventable diseases and to make informed decisions regarding their children’s health63,76. In the Indian context, where disparities in literacy and access to health information continue, media campaigns, including television, radio, and mobile messaging, play a critical role in promoting vaccine uptake and reducing zero-dose prevalence in India, particularly among socioeconomically disadvantaged populations57,64. These findings underscore the importance of leveraging mass media and digital platforms to target underserved populations, mitigate inequities and strengthen immunization services in India.

The study empirically demonstrates that maternal ANC visits significantly contributed to wealth-based inequality in children’s zero-dose, accounting for 10.61 pp. The possible reasons were the connections with the institutional health centre where women received ANC during pregnancy, and one might be readily persuaded regarding the necessity of immunization77. The contribution of ANC access to inequality in zero-dose may or may not contribute to healthcare, but is instrumental to vaccination, which may as well be an issue of access rather than counselling. Limited ANC utilisation, particularly in rural and underserved areas, may reflect barriers to access rather than a lack of awareness, as long distances to health facilities, transportation challenges, and indirect costs can hinder attendance in India, which can potentially affect zero-dose63,64,78. Consequently, regular ANC visits promote both the acceptability and accessibility of immunization, which reduces the number of zero-dose immunization. Furthermore, zero-dose prevalence is closely associated with health care utilisation, including institutional delivery, which accounts for 9.79 pp in wealth-based inequality in zero-dose. The mothers who gave birth in any institution (public or private) are significantly more likely to have higher immunizations compared to those delivering at home, reflecting the integration of immunization counselling and services during institutional care57,76,79.

Social stratification (castes) is significantly associated with zero-dose children in India, which contributes 2.18 pp to the socioeconomic (wealth-based) inequality in zero-dose. This result consistently aligned with prior studies, indicating that children from marginalized communities, especially Scheduled Tribes (STs), consistently exhibit higher zero-dose prevalence compared to other social groups, reflecting that the impact of social groups or ethnicity has persisted in the occurrence of zero-dose among children in India30,57. In association with several castes in India, the differences in immunization have been recorded, and a lower rate of immunization in children has been identified among the ST than other social groups80. In India, children who belong to the ST are less likely to be vaccinated, and a higher occurrence of zero-dose is observed among children from the ST81. These disparities are compounded by structural and socioeconomic barriers, including lower maternal education, economic constraints, social marginalization, limited awareness and knowledge of healthcare services, which collectively attributable to less access to immunization in India10,30,63,64. In India, historical inequities in healthcare infrastructure and service delivery in tribal and remote regions further exacerbate the zero-dose phenomenon76. This highlights that caste-based differences remain a significant determinant of zero-dose immunization in the Indian context, contributing to persistent socioeconomic inequalities in vaccine uptake27.

Mothers who reported difficulties in accessing healthcare, such as taking transportation, getting money, or obtaining permission for access to healthcare, were significantly associated with zero-dose children, and these problems contributed 2%, 1.8% and 0.84% respectively, to the wealth-based inequality in zero-dose. These barriers underscore broader structural and operational challenges in the Indian healthcare system, particularly for the immunization of children82. These challenges are more intrinsic with financial insecurity, such as indirect costs of travel, wage loss, and a poor road network and transportation difficulties, which may appear to be a critical potential barrier to the ability to take children’s vaccination13,57,64,83,84. The decision regarding healthcare utilisation by mothers is significantly associated with the behavioural and infrastructural barriers to healthcare services in India, especially when the husband is not present at the household73,85,86. This is evident in states of India with pronounced gender inequalities, where women require permission to travel outside the home or to use household resources for healthcare27. Hence, the lack of women’s autonomy to independently make healthcare decisions for their children may be associated with vaccination delay or zero-dose among the children10. However, the travel distance to health care does not appear to impact the extent of immunization coverage.

The geographic heterogeneity in zero-dose immunization among children is a major concern in India. Our results show that geographical region contributes to wealth-based inequality in zero-dose, at 0.33 pp, underscoring the spatial dimension of immunization inequities. While global evidence consistently linked geographical barriers, such as distance to health facilities, with lower immunization coverage85,87. India-specific studies reveal that remoteness, poor connectivity, and uneven distribution of health infrastructure are particularly pronounced challenges in rural, hilly, and tribal areas, and mothers are more concerned in these regions61,64,88–90. The spatial distribution of zero-dose prevalence reveals that 16 out of 707 districts in India are predominantly in the northeast and northern regions, reporting the highest prevalence of zero-dose. These disparities are shaped not only by accessibility but also by three major determinants that explain diffidence as the “3Cs: confidence (trust in vaccines and health systems), complacency (the perceived importance of vaccination), and convenience (availability, affordability, and quality of vaccination services)63. The remote communities and irregularities in geographical location often suffer from inadequate outreach, irregular vaccine sessions, and limited health worker availability, further reinforcing geographic inequities in immunization. Addressing these gaps requires a stronger emphasis on equitable spatial coverage of immunization services with targeted outreach in hard-to-reach districts where zero-dose prevalence is concentrated in India.

Moreover, religion is yet another demographic characteristic that significantly contributes to disparities in children’s immunization rates. The results show that Muslim children were 40% more likely to be zero-dose compared to Hindu children, with religion contributing − 0.51 pp to the wealth-based inequality in children’s zero-dose. A global study reported on religious affiliation and immunization, highlighting that religious affiliation can shape health-seeking behaviours through differences in norms, lifestyle and beliefs2,91. In India, children from a Muslim religious background had a lower immunization coverage than Hindu or other religions, which might be affected by the religious beliefs and refusal to vaccinate80,81,92. A prior study in India found that Mulsim children had worse vaccination outcomes in some states and also had the lowest performance in full-immunization in some states, including “Jammu & Kashmir, Uttar Pradesh, Uttarakhand, Rajasthan, Manipur, Haryana, Karnataka, and Kerala”93. This precarious scenario of lower immunization or zero-dose rates in certain religions has often been attributed to a complex interplay of cultural practices, limited access to social benefits programmes, misinformation, and community-specific barriers, limited knowledge and perception of morbidity situations and religious leaders’ influences91,94,95. Additionally, the Muslim religion has found a potential blockade to immunization and a leading factor in children’s zero-dose, which is skeptical of ‘faith-based beliefs’ rather than health-based concerns by the religious leaders, which often spread within religious communities91,96. The concerns regarding vaccine safety, misconceptions, and mistrust of public health services are disproportionately higher among Muslim households, contributing to delayed or missed vaccinations in India97. In this regard, the relationship between religion and vaccine hesitancy is contextualized as an attitude of doubt or disbelief towards vaccines due to an inadequate understanding of the rationale for immunization, which may contribute to delay or rejection of vaccination98,99. The persistence of faith-based skepticism over health-based reasoning, reinforced through informal community networks, continues to pose challenges for achieving universal immunization among religious groups in India80,92,97,99.

Presently, global and national immunisation strategies are focused endeavours to tackle the zero-dose children and reduce the significant disparity in vaccination services. This is reflected in the WHO’s Immunisation Agenda 2030 (IA2030) and Gavi’s 2021–2025 strategic plan explicitly emphasise reaching missed communities to “leave no one behind”62,100. Despite progress under the Global Vaccine Action Plan (2011–2020), momentum was hindered by the COVID-19 pandemic, underscoring the urgency of renewed action. The Global Vaccine Action Plan (2011–2020) envisioned a world free from vaccine-preventable diseases, emphasizing country ownership, shared state-nation responsibilities, partnerships, equity, integration, sustainability, and innovation. The current global policy, IA2030, envisions its impact goals, including reducing morbidity and mortality from vaccine-preventable diseases, ensuring Sustainable Development’s promise to “Leave no one behind (LNOB),” and strengthening immunization services through primary healthcare. This contributes to universal health coverage and the SDGs, particularly SDG 321. In alignment with the IA2030, the GOI developed the zero-dose implementation plan to address the challenge of zero-dose children and missed communities, particularly those with large clusters of under-vaccinated children and vulnerability to vaccine-preventable disease outbreaks.

Findings from this study provide clear guidance for India-specific interventions. First, children with less educated mothers and those from the poorest backgrounds exhibited higher zero-dose prevalence, highlighting the need for subnational targeting and prioritization of disadvantaged communities. Expanding Information, Education, and Communication (IEC) campaigns in local languages and engaging religious and community leaders can address misconceptions and fear around vaccines. While providing incentives to frontline health workers and families may offset the opportunity costs, such as wage loss. Second, children born at home, those whose mothers had no ANC visits, reliance on private facilities for vaccinations, and poor accessibility to health services, including taking transportation, getting money, and permission issues for access to care, exhibit a higher occurrence of zero-dose children, indicating a significant gap in public health service delivery. Therefore, strengthening the continuum of care through early pregnancy registration, ensuring ANC and PNC visits, and linking institutional delivery with immunisation registration can reduce early missed opportunities. Outreach and fixed-session vaccination services at government facilities close to beneficiaries are essential, particularly in underserved and remote areas. Third, service delivery gaps call for addressing human resource deployment and innovative solutions for difficult geographies. Strategic recruitment and redistribution of health personnel, delivery of vaccines and logistics through innovations such as drone-supported vaccine delivery in hard-to-reach or conflict-affected areas, could enhance equity in service provision. Finally, improving maternal healthcare utilisation, expanding digital tracking systems for child immunisation, and reinforcing monitoring and evaluation will strengthen accountability and programme performance as a significant factor in children’s immunisation outcomes. Together, these measures - grounded in the determinants identified in our analysis can accelerate India’s progress toward universal immunisation, ensuring that every child is protected and contributing to SDG 3 and IA2030’s equity agenda.

Limitations of the study

The study has certain limitations which need to be acknowledged. First, the study is based on the cross-sectional survey data from NFHS-5, which inherently limits the ability to establish causal inference and conclusions from the observed associations. Second, the reliance on self-reported child vaccination information by maternal recall may introduce recall bias and misclassification of the vaccination status. This could distort associations between vaccination status and explanatory variables, particularly those related to healthcare access, such as place of delivery or ANC visits. Third, the exclusion of deceased children may introduce survivorship bias, which could potentially underestimate zero-dose prevalence in high-mortality regions and low-immunization settings. Fourth, the sampling frame of NFHS-5 was derived from the 2011 census framework, which may not fully capture recent socioeconomic and demographic shifts or newly formed administrative units, possibly leading to sampling bias. Finally, the analysis does not account for the underrepresentation of populations in conflict-affected regions or due to the COVID-19 impact, where disruptions to routine immunization could have substantially increased the number of zero-dose children, leading to an underestimation of the true burden.

Conclusion

The present study underscores a significant socioeconomic inequality in zero-dose among children in India and the key contributing factors to the inequality. This inequality is concentrated among children from poorer households, and such concentration is majorly contributed to by the wealth of the households, maternal education, exposure to mass media by the mothers, ANC visits by mothers, place of delivery of the children, caste, and problems related to transport, getting money, and permission to access health care. These findings suggest that targeted intervention focusing on maternal education, healthcare accessibility, and awareness of mass media among mothers, particularly socially and economically marginalized groups, needs to be strengthened. The study discussed that the education of mothers and mass media exposure are attributable to having a greater understanding of the importance of vaccination. This research will aid in the realignment of structural changes in policy and strategic advancement to reach the zero-dose or under-immunized children in India. This will also amplify to achieve the goals of ‘LNOB’, minimize zero-dose incidence, and enhance the vaccination coverage in India.
