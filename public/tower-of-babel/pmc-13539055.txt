An Acute Gastroenteritis Outbreak Linked to Serving Hot Meals Inside Polybag During a Religious Ceremony, Bangladesh‐2022: A Retrospective Cohort Study
Authors: Immamul Muntasir, Mohammad Rashedul Hassan, Md Omar Qayum, Quazi Ahmed Zaki, Mahbubur Rahman, Tahmina Shirin
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTBackground and AimsFoodborne outbreaks frequently manifest as acute gastroenteritis (AGE) and are threat for public health in Bangladesh. On 17 November 2022, the health‐manager of Delduar sub‐district, Tangail reported an AGE outbreak to the Institute of Epidemiology, Disease Control and Research (IEDCR). A team was deployed to investigate the source and risk factors of the outbreak and contain it.MethodsWe conducted a retrospective cohort study after verifying that all cases ate food of a religious ceremony (Mahfil) on 13 November, 2022. We defined a case as any person from Gojiabari who developed acute watery diarrhea with or without fever, vomiting, or abdominal cramp after eating food from the Mahfil. We interviewed every person who ate the Mahfil‐food with structured questionnaires. Stool and water samples were analyzed for enteric pathogens. Food‐specific attack rates (AR) and relative risks (RR) were calculated to identify risk factors.ResultsOverall AR was 57% (229/400). Median incubation period was 13 h [Inter‐Quartile‐Range (IQR): 10–19 h]. Most common symptoms were diarrhea (100%), fever (75%) and vomiting (34%). The epidemic curve suggested a point‐source outbreak. 224 of 329 persons who ate khichuri (rice and lentils) served in a polybag (AR = 68%, RR = 9.7, 95% CI = 4.1–22.6) developed illness compared to 5 of 70 who ate on plate (AR = 7%, RR = 0.1, 95% CI = 0.05–0.25). Khichuri was placed on Banana‐leaves immediately after cooking in a kitchen with inadequate sanitation and packaged in polybags without proper cooling. Stool and water samples were negative for Vibrio cholerae, Salmonella and Shigella.ConclusionsThe outbreak was strongly associated with packaging hot khichuri in polybags. To prevent a similar outbreak, we recommend maintenance of hygiene during food processing and proper cooling of food before packaging.Keywords: Bangladesh, disease outbreaks, food packaging, foodborne diseases, Gastroenteritis, Retrospective studies

Introduction

Foodborne transmission of pathogens is recognized as a global hazard [1]. Unsafe food is responsible for foodborne diseases, which cause 600 million cases and 420,000 deaths annually. According to World Health Organization (WHO), eating unsafe food triggers a global loss of 33 million healthy life years annually. This substantial disease burden from food implies the cruciality of food safety, particularly in Southeast Asia and Africa [2]. However, foodborne diseases are notably underreported in most countries [3].

The most common type of foodborne disease is diarrheal disease, which is responsible for more than half of the global burden of foodborne diseases. Other common foodborne diseases include typhoid fever, hepatitis A, tapeworm infections, and aflatoxin poisoning [4, 5].

Bangladesh has a high burden of foodborne diseases. Here, around 30 million people suffer from foodborne illnesses each year [6, 7, 8]. Diarrheal diseases are the most common manifestation of foodborne illnesses in Bangladesh. Traditional food preparation practices, improper holding temperatures, and unhygienic food handlers are among the key contributors to contamination [7].

On 15 November 2022, many people from Gojiabari village in sub‐district Delduar in Tangail, a district of Bangladesh, sought medical care for acute gastroenteritis from the Upazila Health Complex (UHC), Delduar. According to the statements of these patients and claims from local print media, this event started following a religious ceremony (Mahfil) that took place in the same village on the night of 13 November.

Consequently, on 17 November 2022, the Institute of Epidemiology, Disease Control and Research (IEDCR) was notified by the Upazila Health and Family Planning Officer (UHFPO) of Delduar, Tangail that an increased number of acute gastroenteritis (AGE) patients were admitted in the health complex. In response, IEDCR deployed a national rapid response team to investigate the outbreak. The aim of this investigation was to confirm the outbreak, identify its source and mode of transmission, determine the risk factors associated with illness, and guide appropriate control measures to prevent further transmission.

Materials and Methods

Study Design, Period and Location

We initially conducted a descriptive study to understand the outbreak and generate a hypothesis, which was later tested via a retrospective cohort study design to identify the source and risk factors. The investigation was conducted from 17 to 23 November 2022 in the Upazila Health Complex, Delduar, Tangail, and Gojiabari village, Atia, Delduar.

Descriptive Study

Confirmation of the Outbreak

We visited and interviewed the AGE patients who were still on admission at the hospital. We interviewed Upazila health officials to obtain information on the nature of the situation.

We also reviewed records from emergency, outdoor and indoor departments and found that 2–3 patients were admitted daily and treated for AGE during the first half of November 2022. However, from 16 November there was a sudden surge in the number of AGE patients admitted.

Based on the record‐review of hospital admission books and analysis of data obtained from interviews with admitted patients, we identified an AGE outbreak that originated in a village named Gojiabari which is located in the Atia union of Delduar upazila. We also found that all the patients had a history of eating khichuri cooked for a religious ceremony that took place on the night of 13 November.

Data Collection Method, Case Finding and Line Listing

We developed a semi‐structured questionnaire to collect the clinical features and exposure history of all AGE patients. We surveyed the affected community in search of cases and constructed a line list template to collect all the cases through face‐to‐face interviews to understand the extent of the disease. Based on our findings, we constructed the following case definition for this investigation.

Case definition: Any person from Gojiabari who developed acute watery diarrhea, with or without fever, vomiting, or abdominal cramp after eating food from the Mahfil, from 14 November 2022 until the end of the investigation.

Laboratory Study

We collected seven stool samples from hospitalized AGE patients and three water samples from the house where the religious ceremony occurred. All the stool samples were tested via rapid diagnostic test (RDT) for cholera and sent to the IEDCR for further testing (culture and sensitivity). All three water samples were tested at International Center for Diarrheal Disease Research, Bangladesh (icddr,b).

Environmental Study

We investigated the hosting house where the religious ceremony took place to explore the hygiene conditions of the house. We examined the kitchen and other places where the food was processed and served. We observed the sources of water used for food preparation.

Data Analysis and Hypothesis Testing

We initially analyzed the outbreak data descriptively by time, place and person. To perform a univariate analysis, categorical variables were presented as counts and percentages. Continuous variables were presented with central location and dispersion. On the basis of our findings, we hypothesized that there was an association between the outbreak and consumption of Khichuri, which was cooked and served in a polybag during the religious ceremony on the night of November 13. To test this hypothesis, we employed a retrospective cohort study.

Exposure status was determined separately for each food item. Persons who consumed a given food item from the Mahfil, whether or not they attended the Mahfil, were considered exposed to that item, while those who did not consume the specific food item were considered unexposed. Risk ratios (RR) with 95% confidence intervals (CIs) were calculated to identify significant risk factors.

We determined the overall attack rate (AR), sex specific ARs, food specific ARs and RRs. We plotted an epidemic curve to demonstrate the magnitude and the course of the illness. The bivariate analysis involved calculation of the RRs and their corresponding 95% CIs associated with eating each food item. We determined that variables were significantly associated with foodborne disease at a significance level of 0.05. Data analysis was carried out with Stata version 17.0. RRs and 95% CIs were calculated using standard cohort‐study formulae [9]; a 95% CI for the risk ratio that excluded 1 was considered to indicate a statistically significant association. All statistical tests were two‐sided.

Ethical Approval

As this was an immediate response to an emergency public health event and not considered research, this investigation was not subject to ethical review board approval. However, written informed consent was obtained from the participants and guardians of the minors before the data and samples were collected. In addition, absolute confidentiality and data security were ensured by using a password‐protected device.

Results

Descriptive Epidemiology

A total of 229 cases were identified out of the 400 people who ate food of that religious ceremony. The overall attack rate was 57.25% (229/400) with one death. A total of 42.79% (98/229) of the cases required hospitalization.

Among the cases, 56.77% (130/229) were females (Table 1). The sex specific attack rates were 52.66% (99/188) and 61.32% (130/212) for males and females respectively.

Analytical Study Findings

Upon conducting a retrospective study, we calculated the food‐specific attack rates for all the items cooked and served in the Mahfil and determined that the AR and RR was 68% and 9.7, respectively, among people eating khichuri (rice and lentils) served in a polybag compared with 5 of 70 who ate on plate (AR = 7% and RR = 0.1) (Table 3). No other food item served at the Mahfil was significantly associated with illness.

Laboratory Findings

The initial RDT for Vibrio cholerae was performed in the field for all seven stool samples collected and only two samples were positive. Notably, that all these samples were obtained from patients who had already been treated with antibiotics. Further culture and sensitivity tests of stool samples at the IEDCR revealed no enteric pathogens. The water samples were negative for Vibrio cholerae .

Environmental Findings

The house where the ceremony took place was tin‐shed, and the environment around the kitchen was unhygienic. The cooking apparatus had been rented for the ceremony and was returned before the investigation began. A cattle‐shed (goats) was located in the vicinity of the kitchen, and some kitchen staff were not appropriately dressed. Interviews with food handlers and volunteers who worked on the night of the Mahfil revealed several deviations from food safety practices and utensils were left uncovered on the floor. Khichuri was kept on banana leaves immediately after cooking in a kitchen with inadequate sanitation and packaged in polybags without adequate cooling. Water used in the kitchen was drawn from two tube wells inside the house. Food handlers reported no recent history of diarrhea or any other illness, although valid health certification could not be confirmed for kitchen staff.

Discussion

The occurrence of foodborne disease outbreaks from mass meal are not unusual [7, 10, 11, 12]. This outbreak occurred in Delduar, Tangail was strongly associated with eating khichuri served in polybags. Clinical and epidemiological findings are suggestive of enterotoxins of microbiological origin. However, laboratory testing could not provide any evidence for the responsible pathogen.

On the basis of the incubation period, clinical symptoms and duration of illness, we suspected that the potential organism responsible for this outbreak was enterotoxigenic Escherichia coli (ETEC). This attribution is speculative, as no pathogen was laboratory‐confirmed as the causative agent in this investigation. ETEC produces a heat‐labile toxin (LT) and/or heat‐stable toxin (ST), which can cause acute diarrhea, abdominal cramps, nausea, vomiting, and fever. The illness is usually self‐limiting and lasts for 2–3 days, with recovery without any treatment [13, 14, 15].

ETEC is a common cause of traveler's diarrhea, which is typically acquired through the ingestion of contaminated food or water. The bacteria can contaminate food and water through fecal‐oral transmission, such as through contaminated hands or surfaces [13, 16].

Other pathogens could also plausibly explain this clinical presentation. In particular, Clostridium perfringens is a recognized cause of foodborne outbreaks with a relatively short incubation period and is classically associated with foods that are cooked in bulk and then held or cooled slowly, as occurred with the khichuri in this outbreak. This organism could not be evaluated, however, because stool cultures were not tested for Clostridium perfringens, and it remains, like ETEC, an unconfirmed possibility [17].

The lack of sanitation in the kitchen compound and unhygienic practices of food processing, such as using water from tube well with compromised quality and using banana leaves to keep khichuri after cooking might allow contamination of food with ETEC. Subsequently, serving khichuri in a polybag before proper cooling results in a temperature inside the polybag known as the “danger zone” which might propagate the rapid growth of bacteria in the polybags. The outbreak may be avoided if clean water and utensils are used during food processing and proper cooling is performed before packaging [18, 19].

The single fatality, which occurred in an elderly patient with underlying comorbid conditions, underscores the importance of prompt and proper management of acute gastrointestinal illness in vulnerable individuals. However, owing to the rapid response to affected area by UHFPO of Delduar, early management of these large numbers of patients were ensured.

Laboratory confirmation of the specific causative pathogen is important, as other bacteria or viruses can produce a similar clinical picture. However, because all stool samples in this investigation were collected after patients had already received at least one dose of antibiotics, the likelihood of detecting the causative organism, if present, was substantially reduced.

This investigation reveals strong epidemiological and clinical evidence of a significant statistical association between exposure, such as eating khichuri served inside polybag and the development of illness. Additionally, the findings of environmental investigation suggest contamination during food preparation.

Public Health Action

Immediately following identification of the outbreak, local health authorities implemented a series of public health response measures aimed at controlling further transmission and reducing morbidity. Information, Education, and Communication (IEC) materials focusing on diarrheal disease prevention, hand hygiene, safe food handling, and environmental sanitation were distributed within the affected community. Health workers conducted awareness sessions to reinforce key preventive messages.

Community members were advised to seek prompt medical care at the nearest health facility if symptoms of diarrhea developed. Emphasis was placed on early oral rehydration therapy to prevent dehydration, and guidance was provided on the preparation and appropriate use of oral rehydration solution at home.

In addition, risk communication activities were undertaken to sensitize food handlers, community leaders, and event organizers about the importance of maintaining proper hygiene during food preparation, ensuring safe water use, avoiding unsafe packaging practices, and adhering to appropriate food cooling and storage procedures during mass gatherings.

Limitation

This investigation was subject to several limitations. Laboratory confirmation of the causative agent was not possible, as stool samples were collected after antibiotic administration and no food samples were available for testing. Recall bias may have affected exposure histories because interviews were conducted after the event. Additionally, environmental assessments were limited by post‐event cleaning and unavailability of cooking utensils. Despite these constraints, the epidemiological evidence strongly supports the identified exposure as the most likely source of the outbreak.

Recommendation

Short term: In the event of any diarrhea, community members were instructed to seek early hospitalization and to properly hydrate themselves by drinking oral rehydration fluid

Medium term: Information, education & communication (IEC) materials for the prevention of diarrheal disease prevention were distributed by local health officials.

Long term: Food handlers and other kitchen staff should be trained in proper food handling and hygiene practices to prevent future outbreaks.

Conclusion

This outbreak of acute gastroenteritis in Delduar, Tangail was most likely associated with consumption of khichuri served in polybags during a religious ceremony. Epidemiological findings, including the point‐source epidemic pattern and significantly higher attack rate among those consuming food packaged in polybags, indicate contamination during food handling and improper temperature control. The investigation highlights the critical importance of maintaining hygiene, ensuring safe water use, and allowing adequate cooling of cooked food before packaging—particularly during mass gatherings. Strengthening food safety practices and community awareness is essential to prevent similar outbreaks in the future.
