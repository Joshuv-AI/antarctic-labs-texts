Indications of over- and undertreatment of hypothyroidism in primary care: a scoping review and evidence mapping
Authors: Birgitte Norrel Sloth, Magnus Bye Blumenfeld, Jette Kolding Kristensen, Jan Brink Valentin, Allan Carlé, Janus Laust Thomsen
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractIntroductionHypothyroidism is a prevalent endocrine disorder predominantly managed in primary care. Improper treatment can lead to significant clinical and public health consequences. Despite the availability of clear clinical guidelines, over- and undertreatment frequently occur. The aim was to explore and map indications of over- and undertreatment of hypothyroidism in a primary care setting.MethodsWe conducted a scoping review guided by Arksey and O’Malley. The search was conducted in PubMed, Embase, CINAHL, Scopus, and the Cochrane Library. We included peer-reviewed studies exploring over- and undertreatment of hypothyroidism in primary care and included both registry/audit and survey studies. Data were extracted across four structured stages, capturing study characteristics and treatment-related outcomes. Data on indications were compared across studies to identify recurring patterns.ResultsNineteen studies were included. The findings show that the majority of studies with larger study populations report a moderate prevalence of over- and undertreatment. Overtreatment was associated with early initiation of therapy, absence of confirmatory testing, and suppressed TSH levels during follow-up. Undertreatment was linked to incomplete diagnostic evaluation, delayed treatment initiation, and inadequate follow-up. Survey studies reported similar patterns of inappropriate management but higher adherence to clinical guidelines.ConclusionThis scoping review found consistent evidence of both over- and undertreatment of hypothyroidism in primary care. A discrepancy between clinicians’ reported intentions and observed practice suggests the need to attend to behavioural and organisational factors, and to provide greater support for individualised decision-making to improve care.Keywords: Hypothyroidism, overtreatment, undertreatment, general practice, primary care, prescribing patterns, guideline adherence

Introduction

Hypothyroidism is a prevalent disorder defined by insufficient thyroid hormone production, which leads to a variety of clinical manifestations [1,2]. Hypothyroidism is most commonly managed in primary care, and its standard treatment is thyroid hormone replacement therapy, most commonly with levothyroxine (L-T4), aimed at restoring euthyroid status, alleviating symptoms, and preventing complications due to longstanding hypothyroidism [3–6]. While hypothyroidism is generally straightforward to diagnose and monitor, improper treatment can lead to significant health risks [1,3]. Current clinical guidelines recommend treatment for overt hypothyroidism. In contrast, management of subclinical hypothyroidism should be individualized, with many guidelines advising a cautious approach, particularly in cases of only mild biochemical abnormalities or in older adults [7,8].

Yet, even with well-established diagnostic and treatment guidelines, real-world management often deviates from these standards [9,10]. A central challenge in managing hypothyroidism is to maintain an appropriate balance, avoiding both over- and undertreatment [11,12]. Overtreatment occurs when thyroid hormone levels exceed physiological needs, potentially leading to subclinical or overt hyperthyroidism. This can lead to adverse effects, including palpitations, anxiety, arrhythmias, osteoporosis, and increased cardiovascular morbidity [13]. Undertreatment arises when thyroid hormone levels are insufficient to restore normal metabolic function, leaving patients with persistent symptoms of hypothyroidism, such as fatigue, depression, impaired cognitive function, and diminished quality of life [11], while also increasing the risk for long-term complications like cardiovascular disease, infertility, and dementia [14,15]. In recent years, thyroid-stimulating hormone (TSH measurements have become a routine component of standard blood test panels in primary care and general health screenings [12,16,17]. As a result, more cases of hypothyroidism, especially mild or subclinical forms, are being treated, even in asymptomatic individuals [16,18,19], even without pretreatment biochemical confirmation [20]. Although formal diagnostic thresholds have remained unchanged, the increased detection of borderline TSH elevations has, in practice, led to earlier initiation of L-T4 therapy and a steady rise in prescriptions, despite limited evidence of benefit in such cases [20–22]. Early proper diagnosis and intervention are beneficial for patients, but expanded use of L-T4 to patients without a clear diagnosis raises concerns about overtreatment, particularly in individuals with subclinical thyroid dysfunction, for whom therapy may not yield significant improvements [23]. Conversely, undertreatment remains a concern for patients whose medications are not appropriately adjusted or whose treatment plans are insufficiently personalized to meet their individual needs [21,24].

This scoping review aims to explore indications of overtreatment and undertreatment of hypothyroidism in primary care settings by examining existing literature on prescribing patterns, adherence to diagnostic and treatment recommendations, and factors influencing clinical decision-making.

Methods

This scoping review was conducted in accordance with the methodological framework proposed by Arksey and O’Malley [25] and is reported following the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses extension for Scoping Reviews (PRISMA-ScR) [26].

Eligibility criteria

To be eligible for inclusion in this scoping review, studies had to focus on treating hypothyroidism, encompassing both overt and subclinical hypothyroidism, by either 1) examining diagnostic criteria, monitoring patterns, follow-up intervals, laboratory values, or prescribing practices (e.g. appropriateness of levothyroxine initiation or dose titration), or 2) examine general practitioners’ perceptions, attitudes, or interpretation of hypothyroidism, including their use of guidelines and clinical decision-making. Eligible studies were also required to involve adult participants aged 18 years or older, be published in English, and be conducted in a primary care setting.

Primary care settings were defined as healthcare environments that provide first-contact, continuous, and generalist medical care [27]. These include general practices, family medicine clinics, community health centers, and outpatient clinics where clinicians work in a generalist role. Studies were included only when the majority of the clinical activity reflected primary care functions, such as routine monitoring or general practice–based care, rather than specialist endocrinology services.

Exclusion criteria: 1) studies addressing thyroid disorders unrelated to primary hypothyroidism, such as congenital hypothyroidism, pituitary (secondary) hypothyroidism, and hyperthyroidism, and studies exclusively examining hypothyroidism in pregnant women, 2) clinical guidelines and reviews, and 3) studies based on data collected prior to or studies published before the year 2000.

Information sources and search strategy

To ensure a comprehensive identification of relevant studies, a systematic search was conducted across five major bibliographic databases: PubMed, Embase, CINAHL, Scopus, and the Cochrane Library. These databases were selected for their broad coverage of health sciences literature and relevance to the topic under investigation. The search strategy was tailored to each database using appropriate controlled vocabulary (e.g., MeSH in PubMed and Emtree in Embase) and free-text terms. In addition to the systematic database searches, we identified additional studies through other sources, including citation tracking, reference list screening, and expert knowledge. The literature search was initiated on April 19th, 2025. See Appendix 1 for search strategy.

Selection of sources of evidence

Study selection was managed using Covidence, a web-based software designed for collaborative literature reviews (Covidence Systematic Review Software, Veritas Health Innovation, Melbourne, Australia. Available at www.covidence.org). All references retrieved from the database searches were imported into Covidence, where duplicates were automatically identified and removed. Screening was conducted in two stages: initial title and abstract screening, followed by full-text screening by two independent reviewers (BNS and MBB). Any disagreements during screening were resolved through discussion, with input from the senior author (JLT) during consensus meetings.

Data charting process

Data from all included studies were charted in Covidence, employing a customized adaptation of its standard data extraction template for systematic reviews. The two reviewers (BNS and MBB) who had previously conducted the source selection independently extracted the data. Any discrepancies were addressed and resolved through a follow-up discussion, with agreement required before moving forward. Due to the nature of this review, we did not assess the quality or risk of bias of the included studies.

Diagnostic criteria and reference ranges

The diagnosis of hypothyroidism is based on measurement of serum thyroid-stimulating hormone (TSH) and total thyroxine (T4) or free thyroxine (FT4) levels. Overt hypothyroidism is defined by an elevated TSH concentration above the upper reference limit, commonly 4.0–5.0 mIU/L, accompanied by a reduced T4-estimate. Reference levels are usually 9–19 pmol/L or 0.7–1.5 ng/dL in terms of fT4 (7,28,29) and 60–140 nmol/L (4.5–10.9 µg/dL) with regard to total T4 levels.

Subclinical hypothyroidism is characterized by an elevated TSH with FT4 within the normal range. The TSH reference range in most populations is approximately 0.4–4.0 mIU/L, although inter-laboratory and population-specific differences exist [28,29].

Guidelines from the American Thyroid Association (ATA, 2014), the European Thyroid Association (ETA, 2013), and NICE (2019) recommend that TSH interpretation should take into account patient age, comorbidity, and physiological variation. In older adults, typically above 70–80 years, mild TSH elevations up to 6–7 mIU/L may reflect age-related physiological changes related to better survival rather than actual thyroid failure [30–32]. Consequently, a more conservative diagnostic and therapeutic threshold is often advised in this population to minimize the risk of overtreatment. Conversely, persistently elevated TSH levels exceeding 10 mIU/L are generally considered indicative of clinically significant hypothyroidism, irrespective of age [7,28,30].

These diagnostic definitions and reference intervals formed the conceptual framework for this scoping review when evaluating how individual studies defined and operationalized overtreatment and undertreatment.

Over- and undertreatment

In this review, we use the terms ‘indications of overtreatment’ and ‘indications of undertreatment’ to describe patterns of clinical practice that suggest deviations from guideline-recommended management of hypothyroidism. These terms do not imply definitive clinical mismanagement but refer to behaviors, thresholds, or prescribing patterns that are interpreted in the literature as suggestive of excessive or insufficient dose.

Overdiagnosis is defined as the initiation of treatment based on insufficient verification or in deviation from current clinical guidelines (e.g. starting treatment therapy after only a single TSH measurement or when TSH is below 5), and overtreatment is defined as cases in which, during treatment, TSH levels are excessively low and/or T4 levels are elevated. This review does not differentiate between overdiagnosis and overtreatment, and all relevant findings are categorized as overtreatment.

Undertreatment is defined as a failure to initiate treatment despite clear indications (e.g., persistently high TSH and/or low T4 levels, with or without symptoms, or when TSH is at the upper limit of the reference range and/or T4 is at the lower limit, accompanied by symptoms), and cases where TSH remains elevated and/or T4 remains low during ongoing treatment.

Data analysis

We conducted a descriptive and thematic synthesis of the included studies following the scoping review framework by Arksey and O’Malley [25]. The analysis proceeded through four structured stages to ensure transparency and replicability. First, data from each study were charted in a standardized extraction matrix capturing study characteristics, population, setting, design, and outcomes related to treatment adequacy. Second, extracted data were coded as indicating overtreatment, undertreatment, or both, using explicit criteria such as TSH thresholds or lack of biochemical confirmation. Third, coded data were compared within and across studies to identify recurring quantitative patterns (e.g., proportions overtreated/undertreated) and qualitative themes describing diagnostic or behavioural drivers. Finally, themes were iteratively reviewed by two researchers (BNS and MBB) to verify consistency and documented in synthesis tables summarising how each pattern emerged across settings. No formal meta-analysis or quality appraisal was performed, consistent with scoping review methodology and objectives.

Results

The PRISMA-ScR flowchart (Figure 1) illustrates the data selection process. The five selected databases identified 1817 studies, of which 731 duplicates were removed. Title and abstract were then screened for the remaining 1086 studies, and a further 978 were excluded, leaving 114 studies for full-text screening. In addition, six records were identified through reference list screening and citation tracking, of which one met the inclusion criteria and was included, yielding a total of 18 studies.

Selection and characteristics of sources of evidence

A total of 18 studies were included, of which 13 were registry and audit studies [19–21,33–42], four were survey-based studies [43–46], and one was mixed [47]. The registry and audit studies were conducted across the UK (n = 6), USA (n = 1), Sweden (n = 1), France (n = 1), Denmark (n = 2), Switzerland (n = 1), Singapore (n = 1) and New Zealand (n = 1). Study sample sizes ranged from 15 to 52,298 (median = 547). The survey studies represented GP perspectives from four countries: India (n = 1), the UK (n = 1), the USA (n = 2), and Spain (n = 1), with respondent counts ranging from under 20 to nearly 900. Across the studies, a total population of 103,323 was included (register and audit population = 102,127; survey population = 1,002). Of the 18 studies, two included a mixed physician population, where the results could not be separated by specialty. The percentage of GPs was 66.5% [36] and 57.4% [33]. Findings

Indications of overtreatment

Indications of overtreatment were reported in 12 of the 14 register and audit studies. Across these, the proportion of overtreated patients ranged from 9.5% to 68.6%. Several studies described early or non-evidence-based initiation of replacement therapy. Ayala et al. [33] found that 60.3% of all levothyroxine prescriptions were initiated without biochemical confirmation of hypothyroidism. Medici et al. [20] observed that 50% of patients were started on therapy after only a single TSH measurement above 5 mIU/L, with treatment thresholds decreasing over time. Similarly, Taylor et al. [19] demonstrated a population-wide shift toward earlier treatment initiation: in a cohort of over 52,000 adults, the proportion of patients starting levothyroxine with TSH <10 mIU/L increased from 37% to 52% between 2001 and 2009, and 18% initiated treatment despite normal FT4 levels (i.e. subclinical hypothyroidism). More than one quarter subsequently developed suppressed TSH (<0.4 mIU/L), indicating biochemical over-replacement [19]. Cooper et al. [35] also reported that 56.4% of patients received thyroxine despite no biochemical documentation of hypothyroidism.

In patients already receiving therapy, La Cour et al. [21] identified 3.8% (n = 558) cases of overtreatment, which occurred more frequently in females and younger individuals. In Gunasekaran et al. [38], 18% of patients had suppressed TSH levels below 0.4 mIU/L after two years of therapy, indicating overreplacement, again most commonly among younger and female patients.

These patterns suggest that overtreatment frequently results from early initiation in subclinical cases, lack of dose adjustment, and absence of individualized TSH targets during follow-up.

Indications of undertreatment

Indications of undertreatment were reported in 12 of the 14 register and audit studies. Across these, the proportion of undertreated patients ranged from 10% to 53%. Several studies highlighted inadequate initiation or follow-up of thyroid hormone therapy. Bengtsson et al. [34] reported that 24% of patients remained untreated within one year of diagnosis, and 13% had persistently elevated TSH levels. La Cour et al. [21] identified 6.4% (n = 932) undertreated patients defined by three consecutive TSH measurements exceeding 10 mIU/L (median follow-up time = 4.3 years, IQR = 2.1–7.1). Similarly, Jager et al. [39] found that 53.3% of patients received neither repeat TSH testing nor thyroid hormone replacement, while Gunasekaran et al. [38] observed that 29.5% of 3,312 treated patients had persistently elevated TSH after two years of follow-up. Several studies further reported limited use of anti-TPO antibody testing and incomplete biochemical assessment before treatment initiation [37,40]. Collectively, these findings point to insufficient biochemical verification and suboptimal follow-up as key contributors to undertreatment, particularly among older adults. Findings from survey-based studies

Several surveys indicated that a notable proportion of clinicians deferred treatment even in cases of biochemically confirmed hypothyroidism. In Surana et al. [46], 23.5% of GPs would not test for anti-TPO in a subclinical case, and 36.3-68.6% would not do repeat testing in a case of overt hypothyroidism. McDermott et al. [44] found that 56-81% of GPs would not order anti-TPO testing in cases of subclinical hypothyroidism, and 56-69% in cases of overt hypothyroidism. Diez et al. [43] reported that 22.1% of GPs would not treat patients with TSH levels between 5 and 10 mIU/L.

Collectively, these findings illustrate cautious prescribing behavior and hesitancy to initiate treatment in subclinical cases.

At the same time, surveys also revealed a tendency toward overtreatment, particularly regarding subclinical hypothyroidism. In a US-based study, Papaleontiou et al. [45] showed that 64.4% of respondents did not adjust TSH targets according to patient age. Allport et al. [47] similarly reported that 80% of GPs ‘sometimes’ prescribed thyroxine for subclinical hypothyroidism in elderly patients. The survey findings thus suggest a broad variation in clinical reasoning, with both overly conservative and overly liberal treatment practices co-existing across settings and nations. Themes and explanatory factors

Across all studies, three recurring themes emerged as potential explanations for the observed indications of over- and undertreatment. First, limited biochemical confirmation before initiating therapy was common, with several studies describing treatment decisions based on a single TSH measurement or without FT4 or antibody assessment [20,35,43,44]. Second, inadequate follow-up and monitoring were repeatedly identified, leading to both undertreatment and overtreatment [34,37–40]. Third, inconsistent application of guideline-based thresholds in clinical practice contributed to variability in treatment adequacy, particularly the use of fixed rather than age-adjusted TSH targets [21,42,45,46]. These themes were present across all studies and suggest that deviations from evidence-based practice arise from a combination of system-level and behavioral drivers rather than from isolated clinical errors. Discussion

This scoping review aimed to explore indications of over- and undertreatment of hypothyroidism in primary care settings and found consistent and widespread evidence of both. Despite the biochemical simplicity of diagnosing and monitoring thyroid dysfunction, frequent deviations from guideline-recommended thresholds and follow-up procedures were observed. Overtreatment was driven by early prescription and limited biochemical confirmation, whereas undertreatment was linked to inadequate monitoring and conservative initiation practices. Both overtreatment and undertreatment are common and largely arise in diagnostic and therapeutic grey zones, particularly in subclinical hypothyroidism, incomplete diagnostic workup, and inconsistent follow-up. Our findings suggest that managing hypothyroidism in primary care is shaped by broader systemic and contextual challenges rather than isolated clinical errors.

The variability across studies was substantial and not fully explained by study design alone. While heterogeneity in methods and populations likely contributes, the absence of consistent patterns within specific study types suggests that additional factors may influence reported findings.

This is further illustrated in Figure 2, where indications of over- and undertreatment are distributed across study designs without a consistent pattern. In particular, registry and audit studies, despite representing the largest group, showed relatively uniform distributions across indication levels. This suggests that aggregating findings across study designs without considering underlying differences may obscure important variation.

Registry data showed that up to two-thirds of new levothyroxine prescriptions occurred without a complete diagnostic workup [19–21,33]. Similar symptom-driven and precautionary prescribing patterns have been described in other areas of primary care, including proton pump inhibitor use and, in some cases, antibiotic prescribing, where diagnostic uncertainty and workflow constraints may lower the threshold for treatment initiation [48,49]. However, these comparisons should be interpreted with caution, as levothyroxine treatment is typically long-term or lifelong, in contrast to the often time-limited use of these medications. This may indicate that thyroid hormone prescribing reflects general primary care decision-making under uncertainty rather than thyroid-specific mismanagement.

Although registry-based studies may overestimate inappropriate prescribing by relying solely on biochemical data, patient-record–based studies suggest that symptom-driven decision-making may itself contribute to overtreatment. Levothyroxine was frequently initiated despite only mildly elevated TSH levels [47], and a substantial proportion of prescriptions were classified as inappropriate even among symptomatic patients [33]. Discontinuation studies further showed high rates of euthyroidism after treatment withdrawal, suggesting that symptoms initially attributed to thyroid dysfunction may reflect alternative causes or transient hypothyroidism [50,51]. This interpretation is supported by a Danish study on subclinical hypothyroidism, which found that patients with a high symptom burden frequently had substantial comorbidity, and that symptoms were often attributable to non-thyroidal conditions, particularly cardiovascular and pulmonary disease [52].

Similar patterns have been described in chronic conditions such as mild hypertension, early chronic kidney disease, and prediabetes, where diagnostic uncertainty and borderline biochemical abnormalities contribute to symptom-driven treatment decisions and potential overtreatment [53–55].

Undertreatment was similarly prevalent and clinically relevant. Across study designs, patients with clear biochemical indications, such as TSH levels ≥10 mIU/L, were frequently left untreated or insufficiently monitored. Similar therapeutic inertia has been reported in chronic disease management more broadly and is often attributed to competing clinical priorities, uncertainty regarding benefit–harm balance in older or multimorbid patients, and limited system support for follow-up [56–59]. Thus, undertreatment likely reflects not only deliberate conservatism but also structural barriers to longitudinal care.

Both overtreatment and undertreatment were amplified by incomplete diagnostic documentation. Missing baseline measurements of TSH, FT4, or thyroid antibodies were common, complicating interpretation of treatment decisions. Such gaps may predispose clinicians to symptom-based initiation while simultaneously inflating estimates of inappropriate care in retrospective analyses. Limited reporting of the interaction between biochemical findings and clinical presentation further restricts differentiation between individualized care and true deviations from best practice.

Follow-up data indicated persistent quality issues beyond treatment initiation. Suppressed TSH levels were common, suggesting delayed dose adjustment and suboptimal monitoring, while substantial proportions of patients remained biochemically hypothyroid, reflecting long monitoring intervals or slow titration. Although reliance on single laboratory measurements may exaggerate these estimates, their consistency across studies indicates genuine deficiencies in long-term management.

Methodological limitations, including retrospective data, missing information, cross-sectional measurements, and changing guideline standards, may influence the exact estimates but are unlikely to explain the deviations observed. The findings align with previous evidence showing early treatment initiation, particularly among younger adults and women, alongside persistent undertreatment in older patients and those with comorbidities, often attributed to clinical inertia and uncertainty regarding treatment benefit [11,17,24,30,60–63].

In addition to methodological heterogeneity, temporal and geographical factors may also contribute to the observed variation. Clinical guidelines for hypothyroidism have evolved over time and, in some cases, become more detailed and nuanced, particularly regarding subclinical hypothyroidism and treatment thresholds. While this increasing complexity may support more individualized care, it may also lead to variation in interpretation and implementation across clinicians and healthcare systems. Furthermore, differences in healthcare organisation, access to laboratory testing, and local clinical cultures may influence both diagnostic practices and treatment decisions. As these factors were not formally analysed in the present review, these interpretations should be considered hypothesis-generating.

Overall, observed practice patterns broadly aligned with self-reported clinical behavior, although surveys consistently indicated stronger guideline adherence than was evident in registry and observational data. This gap between intended and actual practice mirrors findings from other therapeutic areas and underscores that guideline awareness alone is insufficient to prevent unwarranted variation. Addressing gaps in diagnostic evaluation, monitoring, and follow-up may reduce avoidable complications and support more consistent, patient-centered management of hypothyroidism.

An additional factor that may contribute to variation in over- and undertreatment is the role of patient-related factors. These include patient preferences, adherence to prescribed therapy, and lay understanding of thyroid disease. While these factors were not within the scope of the present review, patient preferences may influence both the initiation and continuation of treatment, particularly in cases of mild or subclinical hypothyroidism where the benefits of therapy may be uncertain. Some patients may prefer treatment for non-specific symptoms, while others may be reluctant to initiate long-term medication without clear clinical indications.

Adherence to levothyroxine therapy also represents a potential source of variation. Even when treatment is prescribed appropriately, inconsistent use may lead to biochemical findings suggestive of undertreatment, while irregular dosing or self-adjustment may contribute to overtreatment. In addition, variation in health literacy and understanding of thyroid disease may influence help-seeking behaviour and engagement with treatment decisions. Although these factors were not systematically evaluated in the included studies, they are likely to play a meaningful role in real-world management and represent an important area for future research.

Implications for future research/practice

Our findings underscore the need for more structured diagnostic approaches, regular monitoring, and more individualised dose adjustment to reduce persistent biochemical abnormalities. As most patients with hypothyroidism are managed in primary care, efforts to address these challenges in this setting are likely to yield substantial improvements in patient outcomes at the population level.

From a research perspective, heterogeneity across the studies limits direct translation of findings into clinical practice but highlights important gaps in the delivery of hypothyroidism care. The observed variation appears to be driven less by lack of guideline awareness and more by behavioural and organisational factors. Future research should therefore focus on interventions that support clinical decision-making, such as decision-support tools that aid clearer diagnostics and follow-up. Qualitative research may further clarify how general practitioners balance guidelines, patient expectations, and clinical judgment when managing hypothyroidism, and how these processes may contribute to over- and under-treatment.

Strengths and limitations

This review synthesised evidence from diverse study designs and healthcare contexts, providing a broad overview of hypothyroidism management in real-world primary care. The inclusion of both register-based data and clinician surveys enabled triangulation between observed practice patterns and reported decision-making processes, strengthening the interpretive depth of the findings. However, several limitations must also be acknowledged. First, the review aimed to identify indications of over- and undertreatment rather than to determine their true prevalence, and the reported ranges should therefore be interpreted with caution. Second, variation in how individual studies reported biochemical measures limited the ability to fully examine specific laboratory thresholds underpinning treatment decisions. Finally, some included studies involved mixed physician populations where GP-specific data could not be separated from other specialties, potentially influencing the estimates; however, these studies reported patterns broadly consistent with those observed in primary care–focused samples, particularly regarding incomplete diagnostic work-up. Taken together, these considerations mean that the findings should be interpreted as signals of practice variability rather than definitive evidence of inappropriate care, and caution is warranted when extrapolating the magnitude of over- or undertreatment to individual clinical settings.

Conclusion

This scoping review found consistent evidence of both over- and undertreatment of hypothyroidism in primary care, primarily driven by deviations from guideline-recommended diagnostic thresholds and follow-up routines. Overtreatment frequently stemmed from early initiation at mild TSH elevations and incomplete biochemical confirmation, while undertreatment was represented by lack of treatment or insufficient follow-up. Although methodological limitations may influence the exact estimates, the convergence of findings across audits, registries, and surveys indicates that these patterns reflect real and widespread clinical behaviour. The persistent discrepancy between clinicians’ reported intentions and observed practice suggests that addressing variation in thyroid management will require attention to behavioural and organisational factors, as well as greater support for individualised decision-making to improve care for patients with hypothyroidism.
