Generation and single-cell characterization of functional megakaryocytes derived from umbilical cord blood
Authors: Yanmin He, Yue Liang, Ji He, Yu Shen, Zhipan Wu, Shanshan Pei, Faming Zhu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundGeneration of megakaryocytes (MKs) from stem cells in vitro to produce platelets (PLTs) is an appealing approach for providing an alternative source of PLTs. Understanding the transcriptomic characteristics of MKs in vitro is crucial for providing a theoretical foundation for producing functional MKs more efficiently in the future.MethodsCD34+ hematopoietic stem and progenitor cells (HSPCs) were separated from umbilical cord blood (UCB) and were induced to differentiate into MKs in vitro through a culture system. The phenotypes of these cells cultured for different durations were analyzed through flow cytometry. The mRNA expression levels of GATA1, GATA2, FOG1, NF-E2, FLI1, CD41, and CD61 at different time points were analyzed through quantitative PCR. The activation levels of PLTs in vitro and the PLTs produced in vivo were determined. The MK subpopulations were further analyzed through single-cell RNA sequencing (scRNA-seq).ResultsUCB-derived MKs exhibited typical characteristics of MKs in vivo, including morphology, polyploidy, and subcellular structure. The mRNA expression levels of GATA1, FOG1, NF-E2, FLI1, CD41, and CD61 on Days 10 and 14 were significantly greater than those on Day 4. CD62P expression on the surface of UCB-PLTs increased markedly in response to thrombin or TRAP6 stimulation. Humanized PLTs were also detected in the peripheral blood of NCG mice following the infusion of UCB-MKs. According to the results of the sc-RNAseq analysis, nine transcriptionally distinct clusters of UCB-MKs, labeled MK1-MK9, were identified, with only the MK9 population being related to immunity. The MK1–MK8 populations displayed typical MK characteristics and were the most prevalent subtypes. In addition, compared with hESCs, UCB-derived MKs exhibited a greater proportion of active-cycling MKs with strong differentiation potential.ConclusionsIn conclusion, this study describes the biological functions and transcriptomic profile of MKs derived from UCB, which will aid in the further development of more efficient systems for generating MKs in vitro and promote their application in cellular therapy.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s13287-026-05047-9.Keywords: Megakaryocytes, Platelets, Umbilical cord blood, In vitro, Single-cell RNA sequencing

Introduction

Megakaryocytes (MKs) constitute approximately 0.05%–0.1% of nucleated cells in the bone marrow and are responsible for producing platelets (PLTs) within the hematopoietic system. PLTs play crucial roles in hemostasis, coagulation, and immune-related functions [1, 2]. Currently, PLTs are obtained mainly from blood donors. However, there is a significant shortage of PLT donors because of the high demand for PLTs in clinical applications [3, 4]. Consequently, numerous scientists have endeavored to generate PLTs in vitro [5–8], a promising approach that serves as an alternative source [9, 10]. One of the primary strategies involves generating MKs in vitro, followed by platelet generation in vivo [11–14]. Therefore, it is imperative to establish efficient MK production methods in vitro, understand their biology, and ultimately develop in vivo systems to evaluate the platelet production capacity of MK products [15–19].

Since Choi et al. reported the first case in which PLTs were generated from hematopoietic stem and progenitor cells (HSPCs) in vitro [5], numerous researchers have attempted to generate MKs and PLTs from various cell types in vitro by using new methods for gene manipulation and optimization of culture conditions, cytokines, and chemical compounds [20–26]. The cell sources for in vitro MK production include hematopoietic stem cells (HSCs), induced pluripotent stem cells (iPSCs), fibroblasts, and adipose-derived cells [27–29]. Umbilical cord blood (UCB) is an important source of HSCs that are safe, harmless, and easy to store in the UCB hematopoietic stem cell bank. UCB-derived MKs are considered to be among the most promising for future clinical applications in the treatment of thrombocytopenia. However, the efficiency of MK production in vitro remains an urgent issue.

Recently, the application of single-cell RNA sequencing (sc-RNAseq) technology has improved our understanding of the MK development process, MK functional features, and the identification of the optimal path for MK generation in vitro. Several studies have been reported on the transcriptome data of MKs generated in vivo and in vitro. Zhou J et al. revealed the presence of immune-related MK subpopulations in the yolk sac, fetal liver, and adult bone marrow, in addition to thrombopoiesis-biased MKs [2]. Subsequently, Sun S et al. reported the transcriptomic profile of human bone marrow MKs and proposed a new classification for MKs in vivo, in which four distinct MK subpopulations with unique functions were identified, including active-cycling MKs, HSC niche MKs, platelet-generating MKs, and inflammatory response MKs [30]. Jing H et al. reported that the intrinsic characteristics of MKs derived from distinct cell sources, including bone marrow (BM), peripheral blood (PB), and UCB, are different [31]. However, the composition of functional subpopulations of UCB-derived MKs has not been fully defined, and the molecular signatures underlying MK differentiation remain to be further explored.

In the present study, a stable culture system derived from UCB was established, in which the cells differentiated into MKs in vitro. The biological characteristics of UCB-derived MKs and their ability to produce PLTs in vivo were subsequently analyzed. Finally, the transcriptome data of UCB-derived MKs produced by our system were analyzed and compared to previously reported data on MKs derived from human embryonic stem cells (hESC-derived MKs).

Materials and methods

Human subjects

UCB was obtained from the Umbilical Cord Blood Hematopoietic Stem Cell Bank of Zhejiang Province. Pregnant women who met the inclusion criteria and provided informed consent were eligible to donate their UCB. This project was approved by the Ethics Committee of Umbilical Cord Blood Hematopoietic Stem Cell Bank and the Blood Center of Zhejiang Province (Approval No. 2023-003).

Animals

NCG (NOD/ShiLtJGpt-Prkdcem26Cd52IL2rgem26Cd22/Gpt) mice were purchased from GemPharmatech Co., Ltd. (Nanjing, China). All animal experiments were conducted in the Laboratory of the Animal Research Center at Zhejiang Chinese Medical University, and this work is reported in line with the ARRIVE guidelines 2.0.

CD34+ cells derived from UCB were induced to differentiate into MKs in vitro

The entire process of MK differentiation in vitro was divided into two stages spanning 14 consecutive days. The cells were cultured at 37 °C in a humidified environment with a 5% CO2 atmosphere. In brief, CD34+ cells were separated and enriched from UCB using a human CD34 MicroBead Kit (Miltenyi Biotec, GmbH, Germany) following the manufacturer's instructions. The cells were then seeded at a density of 1.0–2.0 × 105/mL in a 25-cm2 flask and cultured in StemSpan serum-free expansion medium (SFEM) (Stemcell Technologies, Vancouver, BC, Canada) supplemented with 20 ng/mL interleukin-3 (IL-3), 50 ng/mL stem cell factor (SCF), 50 ng/mL interleukin-6 (IL-6), and 50 ng/mL thrombopoietin (TPO) for 7 days. All cytokines were purchased from Peprotech (NJ, USA). On Day 7, the cells were harvested, washed, and seeded with SFEM supplemented with 100 ng/mL TPO in a 25-cm2 flask for an additional 7 days. In addition, half of the medium was changed on Days 4 and 10. A schematic of the culture system is depicted in Fig. 1A. UCB from three different donors was isolated for culture, and the cells were further collected for biological detection, including cell phenotyping, morphological observation, MK ploidy, MK-related gene expression, and functional analysis in vitro. However, cells from only one of the three donors were collected on Day 14 and were used for functional assessment in vivo and for sc-RNAseq in addition to the abovementioned detection.

Analysis of the cell phenotype at different culture times by flow cytometry

The cells were collected and prepared as single-cell suspensions on Days 4, 7, 10, and 14. Following cell counting, the cells were stained with specific antibodies, including anti-human CD34-PE, anti-CD41a-APC, and anti-CD42b-BV510, in phosphate-buffered saline (PBS) at 4 °C for 30 min. The stained cells were then analyzed using a BD FACSVerse, and the data were processed using FlowJo software. All the aforementioned antibodies were purchased from BD Biosciences, and the details are listed in Table S1.

Immunofluorescence staining

On Day 14, the cultured cells were cytocentrifuged onto glass slides, fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde for 20 min at room temperature, rinsed with PBS, permeabilized with 0.5% Triton X-100, blocked with 2% bovine serum albumin (BSA) in PBS for 1 h, and incubated with anti-CD62P and α-tubulin mAbs (1:100; Abcam, Cambridge, UK) at 4 °C overnight. When necessary, Alexa Fluor 647-conjugated goat anti-rabbit IgG (H + L) and Alexa Fluor 594-conjugated goat anti-mouse IgG (H + L) (1:1000; Thermo Fisher Scientific, MA, USA) were added and incubated for 1 h at room temperature. The cells were counterstained with 4,6-diamidino-2-phenylindole (DAPI) for the visualization of cell nuclei under a Zeiss LSM 880 confocal microscope (Zeiss, Oberkochen, Germany) by using Zeiss ZEN microscope software.

MK ploidy analysis

On Day 12, 1 × 106 cultured cells were collected; stained with anti-human CD41a-APC, anti-human CD42b-BV510, and Live/Dead antibodies; fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde, permeabilized with 70% chilled ethanol, and then suspended in propidium iodide (PI)/RNase buffer. Without washing the cells, DNA ploidy in the CD41a+/CD42b+ populations was analyzed through flow cytometry. Details of these reagents are listed in Table S1.

Wright‒Giemsa staining

On Day 14, the cells were collected and sorted using a human CD61 MicroBead Kit (Miltenyi Biotec, GmbH, Germany) following the manufacturer's instructions. The sorted cells were then rinsed with PBS, cytocentrifuged onto adhesion microscope slides, stained with Wright‒Giemsa solution (Sangon Biotech, Shanghai, China), and subsequently observed. Images were captured using a microscope (Nikon, Tokyo, Japan).

Transmission electron microscopy

On Day 14, the cultured cells were collected and analyzed through electron microscopy to further observe the cell ultrastructure. The experiments were conducted in the Electron Microscope Laboratory, the Medical Research Center, Academy of Chinese Medicine, Zhejiang Chinese Medical University. The samples were processed according to a published protocol [26]. In brief, cultured cells were fixed in 2.5% glutaraldehyde and embedded in Epon. Ultrathin sections were stained with uranyl acetate and lead citrate and observed under an H7650 transmission electron microscope (Hitachi, Tokyo, Japan).

Detection of MK-related genes by fluorescence-based quantitative PCR

The specific primers for the expression of the MK-related genes (GATA1, GATA2, FOG1, NF-E2, FLI1, PF4, CD41, and CD61) and the internal reference gene (GAPDH) were synthesized by Sangon Biotech Co., Ltd. (Shanghai, China). The sequences are listed in Table 1. The cells from three donors were collected on Days 4, 7, 10, and 14, and total RNA from the cells was extracted using a QIAamp RNA Blood Mini Kit (QIAGEN, Shanghai, China). The mRNA expression levels of MK-related genes at different induction times were detected, and the results were analyzed according to the 2−△△Ct method.

Analysis of the activation level of CD62P on the cell surface by flow cytometry

To analyze the CD62P activation level, cultured PLTs were collected on Day 14, following two centrifugation steps, as previously reported [26], and were named UCB-PLTs. Concurrently, platelets in fresh peripheral blood from sodium citrate-treated donors were isolated as PB-PLTs. Both UCB-PLTs and PB-PLTs (approximately 2.0 × 107/mL) were resuspended in 200 µL of PBS and stimulated with 2 U/mL thrombin and 100 µM TRAP6, respectively, for 20 min at 37 °C. Prestimulated and nonstimulated platelets were then stained with anti-human CD41a-APC, CD42b-BV510, and CD62P-BV421 antibodies, fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde, and analyzed through flow cytometry.

Clot formation and retraction assays

UCB-PLTs and PB-PLTs (approximately 2 × 107/mL) were resuspended in 200 µL of platelet-depleted plasma in siliconized glass tubes and incubated with 2 U/mL thrombin at 37 °C for 30 min. Clusters formed were retracted after induction by thrombin. Tubes without thrombin were used as controls.

In vivo functional analysis of PLTs generated from MKs

On Day -1, six-week-old male NCG mice (n = 5) were depleted of macrophages using clodronate liposomes (Liposoma Technology, Amsterdam, Netherlands) at a dosage of 50 µg per gram of mouse weight. The cultured cells were harvested on Day 14, adjusted to a concentration of 2 × 106 cells per 100 µL, and subsequently injected through the tail vein 24 h after macrophage depletion. PBs were obtained from the mice at 30 min, 1 h, 2 h, 6 h, and 24 h post-infusion. No anaesthesia was administered during both the tail vein injection and PB collection. The mice were euthanized by CO2 inhalation at 24 h post-infusion, and their femurs and spleens were collected. Cells from the blood, BM, and spleen were harvested and analyzed for the percentage of the human CD41a+CD42b+ population through flow cytometry.

Single-cell RNA sequencing analysis

The cultured cells from one donor on Day 14 were directly subjected to the 10 × Genomics Chromium platform to produce single-cell gel beads in emulsion, followed by cDNA synthesis, amplification, and library construction using the Chromium Single-Cell 3′ Reagent Kit v3 (10 × Genomics) according to the manufacturer's instructions. The libraries were pooled and sequenced on Illumina NovaSeq 6000 (Illumina, California, USA). The primary analytical pipeline for the scRNA-seq analysis followed the recommended protocols from 10 × Genomics. The R package Seurat v5.0.0 [32] was utilized for downstream analysis. The azimuth (https://satijalab.org/azimuth/) label-transferring method was employed for cell type annotation. The developmental trajectories of the MK cells were inferred using Slingshot v2.10.0 [33]. To identify immune-related MKs (iMKs), marker genes mentioned in published data (hESC_Mk4, FL_YS_Mk6, and iMK_TFs) [2] were utilized. A heatmap of the GSEA results was generated using the function ‘plot_heatmap_for_fGSEA_all_clusters’ in SingCellaR [34]. FDR and P values less than 0.05 were considered to indicate statistical significance. The scRNA-seq data used for comparative analyses in this study were obtained from the NCBI Gene Expression Omnibus (GEO) under accession number GSE144024 [2]. The scRNA-seq data obtained from this study have also been submitted to NCBI GEO under accession number GSE317847, and the key data are shown in Tables S2 and S3, respectively.

Statistical analysis

The mRNA expression levels of MK-related genes at various time points were compared using one-way ANOVA. The CD62P activation levels were compared using Student's t test (unpaired, two-tailed). Additionally, platelet production in mice following UCB-derived MK infusion was compared using one-way ANOVA. Other analyses and visualizations were conducted using the GraphPad Prism or the R package. The mean ± SD is represented on the bar in all the figures. For all the statistical tests, a P value < 0.05 was considered to indicate statistical significance.

Results

In vitro generation of UCB-derived MKs in a two-dimensional (2D) culture system over 14 days

We initially established a 2D culture system using CD34+ HSPCs derived from UCB samples to produce MKs in vitro (Fig. 1A). As depicted in Fig. 1B, the cells exhibited slow growth during the first seven days, followed by a relatively rapid proliferation phase during the subsequent seven days. Flow cytometry analysis at various time points (Days 4, 7, 10, and 14) revealed that the percentage of CD34−CD41a+CD42b+ MKs progressively increased over time, reaching 70% ± 1.7% by Day 14 (Fig. 1C). Over time, the maturation of MKs advanced through four distinct stages of megakaryocytic maturation: progenitors (CD34+CD41−CD42b−), megakaryocyte progenitors (CD34+CD41+CD42b−), immature megakaryocytes (CD34−CD41+CD42b−), and mature megakaryocytes (CD34−CD41+CD42b+) (Fig. 1D). In summary, the process involves two main stages of MK production: an initial phase of HSPC proliferation from Day 1 to Day 7 and a second phase of MK differentiation from Day 7 to Day 14. Additional immunofluorescence staining and analysis revealed the expression of characteristic platelet proteins, such as CD62P and α-tubulin, on the cell surface and in the cytoplasm, respectively (Fig. 1E). Finally, we estimated that this 14-day protocol can yield approximately 2 × 107 MKs from 1 × 106 CD34+ HSPCs isolated from a typical UCB sample.

UCB-derived MKs with typical morphological characteristics of MKs

To initially characterize the UCB-MKs, we performed MK ploidy analysis on Day 12 using flow cytometry. As illustrated in Fig. 2A (n = 3), approximately 44.45% ± 3.28% of the CD41a+CD42b+ cells were diploid (2N), 31.2% ± 1.3% were tetraploid (4N), 15.4% ± 2.5% were octoploid (8N), and 8.95% ± 1.27% were polyploid with more than 8N. Typical diploid, tetraploid, naked MKs, and thrombocytopenic MKs were confirmed by Wright‒Giemsa staining (Fig. 2B). Finally, transmission electron microscopy (TEM) of the UCB-derived MKs harvested on Day 14 revealed typical MK features, including a demarcation membrane system (DMS), an open tubular system, granules, and mitochondria (Fig. 2C). The DMS is a crucial marker of cytoplasmic maturation and a reservoir of the cell membrane required for proplatelet formation, which is consistent with the findings of previous studies [25, 27].

UCB-derived MKs gradually express MK-related genes during maturation

To further investigate the expression of MK-related genes during culture, the mRNA expression levels of five transcription factors (GATA1, GATA2, NF-E2, FLI1, and FOG1) and three marker protein genes (PF4, CD41, and CD61) were measured through real-time quantitative PCR (RT‒qPCR) at various culture time points (Day 4, Day 7, Day 10, and Day 14). The mRNA expression levels of all the genes monitored gradually increased during the culture period (Fig. 2D). However, with the exception of GATA2, the mRNA expression levels of all the genes on Days 10 and 14 were significantly greater than those on Day 4. Specifically, on Day 14, the mRNA expression levels of GATA1, NF-E2, FLI1, and FOG1 increased significantly by 2.7-, 3.4-, 5.1-, and 2.7-fold, respectively, compared with those on Day 4. Among the three megakaryocyte marker genes (PF4, CD41, and CD61), the mRNA expression level of PF4 increased by hundreds of times on Days 10 and 14. On Day 14, the mRNA expression levels of PF4, CD41, and CD61 increased significantly (415.9-, 8.9-, and 30.5-fold, respectively) compared with the levels on Day 4.

Detection of UCB-PLT functionality in vitro

To further analyze the activation level of CD62P on the cell surface, we stimulated UCB-PLTs and PB-PLTs with thrombin and TRAP6. CD62P expression on the surface of UCB-PLTs significantly increased upon stimulation, as illustrated in Fig. 3A. However, when UCB-PLTs were stimulated with TRAP6, the relative mean fluorescence intensity (MFI) of CD62P was approximately four times greater than that without stimulation, but likely twice as high as that without stimulation by thrombin. Additionally, clot formation and retraction were induced by thrombin in UCB-PLTs and PB-PLTs, respectively. As shown in Fig. 3B, compared with platelets from peripheral blood, UCB-PLTs promoted fibrin clot formation equivalently.

Functional platelet production from UCB-derived MKs in vivo

To verify the ability of UCB-derived MKs to produce functional platelets in vivo, UCB-derived MKs were infused into NCG mice, as shown in Fig. 3C. The cultured cells were collected on Day 14 and analyzed through flow cytometry prior to injection, and the data are shown in Figure S1A. The PB of the mice was collected at various time points. Humanized platelets (0.24% ± 0.04) were detected in the PB of mice within 30 min, indicating that megakaryocytes could generate platelets and that these could appear in the blood circulation of the mice. The percentage of humanized platelets in the PB of the mice increased after 1 h (0.27% ± 0.05), was maintained at a certain level (0.23% ± 0.02) at 6 h, and significantly decreased to almost undetectable levels at 24 h (Fig. 3D). However, no humanized platelets were detected in the bone marrow or spleen, as shown in Figure S1B and S1C, respectively.

MK clustering and developmental trajectory analysis by scRNA-seq technology

To investigate the intrinsic heterogeneity and potential functional subtypes of MKs derived from UCB, we collected cells and conducted scRNA-seq using the 10 × Genomics Chromium platform on Day 14. After quality control and clustering, a total of 8,064 cells were captured, and 9 cell clusters were identified. The sample primarily consisted of MKs, with smaller populations of HSCs, megakaryocyte-erythroid progenitors (MEPs), myeloid cells, and erythroid cells, which were identified on the basis of marker gene expression (Fig. 4A, B). Within the MK subpopulation, genes encoding PF4 and proplatelet basic protein (PPBP) were specifically expressed (Fig. 4C). Additionally, the expression of erythroid Krüppel-like factor (EKLF/KLF1), glycophorin A (GYPA), and aminolevulinate synthase 2 (ALAS2) in erythrocytes (Eryths); myeloperoxidase (MPO) and colony-stimulating factor 3 receptor (CSF3R) in granulocyte‒macrophage progenitors (GMPs); and CD14 in monocytes was detected.

Single-cell transcriptomic profiling of human MKs from the embryonic yolk sac (YS) and fetal liver (FL) has been recently reported, revealing that a MK subpopulation with high enrichment of immune-associated genes has been identified. To determine whether heterogeneous subpopulations were also present in the UCB-MKs in our study, the MK subpopulation was further analyzed, and 9 transcriptionally distinct subclusters were identified (Fig. 5A). The MK1–MK8 populations exhibited typical MK characteristics and were the most common MK subtypes. Interestingly, the MK9 subset expressed NKG7 and CEBPA (Fig. 5B), which are immune-related genes. To further comprehend the features of MK9, marker genes were analyzed through gene set enrichment analysis (GSEA), which revealed that genes associated with FL_YS_MK6 and hESC_MK4 were highly enriched in MK9 compared with other MK subpopulations. Moreover, the signature scores of the iMK_TFs were calculated. Notably, the signature score of iMK_TFs was greater for MK9 than for MK8 (Fig. 5C). Thus, MK9 may represent an MK with potential immune functions. Afterward, pseudotime analysis was performed using the Slingshot algorithm, and the developmental trajectory of the MK subpopulation was obtained (Fig. 5D) according to the expression of the feature genes during different stages over time. The MK development process is continuous from MK1 to MK7 in vitro. The expression of the feature genes during different stages over time is shown in Figure S2A, and the dynamic expression of representative genes along the inferred pseudotime axis is also shown in Figure S2B.

Comparative transcriptomic analysis reveals differences in the composition of functional subpopulations between UCB- and hESC-derived MKs

The ploidy of MKs and their efficiency in platelet production vary during different developmental stages in vivo [30]. Therefore, determining the ontogenic details of MKs derived from various cell sources in vitro is essential. We analyzed previously obtained transcriptomic data from hESC-derived MKs [2]. On the basis of data from human native bone marrow [30], three functional clusters, namely, active-cycling MKs, platelet-generating MKs, and immune/inflammatory response MKs, were identified according to the functional annotation of hESC-derived MKs and UCB-MKs in our study (Fig. 6A–B). Additionally, other cell clusters that do not exhibit particularly distinct expression patterns, such as undefined MKs and HSC niche MKs, were not identified. The proportions of the four subgroups are depicted in Fig. 6C. Notably, platelet-generating MKs constitute the largest cell subpopulation. However, a significant difference was detected in the number of active-cycling MKs (35% vs. 7%), which expressed high levels of cell cycle and DNA replication-related genes, such as MKI67, TOP2A, and PCNA, consistent with their proliferative state (Fig. 6D).

The enrichment of distinct gene expression patterns among the MK functional clusters prompted us to further explore their biological characteristics through pathway analysis. Consistent with these findings, the pathways most enriched in active-cycling MKs were associated with DNA replication, cell cycle regulation, and mitotic processes, whereas platelet-generating MKs were enriched mainly in platelet activation, hemostasis, and cytoskeleton organization pathways. By contrast, immune/inflammatory response MKs were significantly enriched in inflammatory signaling, cytokine-mediated response, and myeloid leukocyte activation pathways (Fig. 6E). Moreover, the subpopulation of active-cycling MKs is produced in the early developmental stage of UCB-MKs, as mentioned above (see details in Figure S2C), indicating that compared with hESCs, UCB has stronger differentiation potential.

Discussion

In this study, UCB-derived MKs were obtained in vitro through a 14-day culture phase in traditional serum-free medium supplemented with cytokines. By combining sc-RNAseq analysis and functional assays in vitro and in vivo, we first proposed that UCB-derived MKs exhibit characteristics similar to those of MKs in vivo, including morphology, cell phenotype, DNA polyploidy, the capacity for platelet production, and transcriptomic features. Moreover, nine distinct subpopulations of MKs corresponding to three separate functional subgroups were identified.

The cell phenotypes were detected at various time points, and the production process of UCB-MKs in this study was similar to that of MKs in vivo, involving the formation of MK progenitors, immature MKs, and mature MKs. Polyploidization is a key feature of mature MKs. DNA ploidy was determined using flow cytometry, and the proportion of cells with a ploidy level greater than 4N significantly exceeded that of iMKs induced by chemical compounds. Furthermore, the transcription factors GATA1, NF-E2, FLI1, and FOG play pivotal roles in the gene expression of terminally differentiated genes, as evidenced by real-time PCR, which aligns with the results of previous reports [24]. The platelet factor 4 (PF4) protein is a small cytokine released from the alpha-granules of activated platelets, playing a role in blood clotting and inflammation [35–38]. In our study, the mRNA expression levels of PF4 significantly increased by Day 14, indicating that second-stage culture markedly promoted MK differentiation and maturation. Notably, the activation of in vitro-generated PLTs was detected only by flow cytometry. UCB-PLTs may be in a preactivated state during processing, where immunofluorescence might be a more suitable method.

In vitro generation of MKs from stem cells to produce platelets may be a possible method to develop platelet substitutes. Many researchers have focused on studying the timing and production of platelets by MKs in vitro. Figueiredo C et al. [21] successfully established a culture system that generated MKs from iPSCs in vitro over 33 days. They demonstrated that humanized platelets were detected in the PB of mice following the infusion of iPSC-derived MKs for 1 h. However, the ability of these iPSC-derived MKs to produce platelets in vivo persisted for only 4 h post-infusion. Recently, Pei X et al. reported the direct chemical reprogramming of human UCB erythroblasts to induce MKs in vitro, and the resulting MKs further produced functional platelets in vivo after 24 h [24]. Therefore, the duration required by MKs to produce platelets varies depending on the stem cell they originated from. In this study, humanized platelets were detected in the PB of mice within 30 min after the infusion of UCB-derived MKs, indicating that the platelets produced by these MKs are rapidly released into the peripheral blood circulation. Furthermore, compared with those in previous studies, the release rate of UCB-derived MKs in our study was faster.

Dynamic monitoring of platelet levels in the peripheral blood of mice revealed that the platelet count decreased within 6 h following the infusion of UCB-derived MKs and became undetectable at the 24-h time point. These findings suggest that the half-life of platelets produced from UCB-derived MKs is approximately 6 h. Interestingly, no platelet production was detected in the bone marrow or spleen of the mice 24 h post-infusion, a finding that contradicts with the results of Kumar B et al. [25]. Two potential explanations for this discrepancy could be coculture with mesenchymal stem cells (MSCs) during MK differentiation or the presence of these cells in these tissues shortly after the infusion of UCB-derived MKs. Similarly, MKs were not detected in the bone marrow or spleen. One possible reason is related to pulmonary obstruction.

The previous transcriptome data of MKs [2] revealed that the MK subpopulation with thrombopoiesis and immune features present in adult MKs from bone marrow is also present in embryonic MKs from the YS and FL or hESCs, substantiating the nontraditional functions of MKs beyond platelet production. However, immunogenic MKs also exist in different induction culture systems in vitro. Pei et al. [26] demonstrated that immune-related MKs are one of the subtypes of MKs induced by chemical reprogramming in human UCB erythroblasts. According to our sc-RNAseq data, nine transcriptionally distinct clusters of MKs labeled as MK1–MK9 were further identified. Interestingly, our study revealed that the MK9 subpopulation expressed immune-related genes (hESC_MK4 and FL_YS_MK6). Therefore, we also verified the previous findings and proved the presence of immunogenic MKs. Unfortunately, the branches of immune-related MKs are difficult to observe because of the limited number of cells.

The efficiency of MK generation varies depending on the cell source. Our study revealed that the efficiency of MK generation from UCB was significantly greater than that from hESCs, as previously reported [2] (91.81% vs. 26.37%). UCB contains numerous early-stage MKs when the composition of the MK subpopulation is compared with that of hESC-derived MKs; the data are not shown. Further functional annotation of these cell subpopulations suggested a greater proportion of active-cycling MKs, indicating that UCB retains the potential to produce MKs with continued induction. Conversely, it is speculated that hESCs may lose early megakaryocytes and rapidly undergo terminal differentiation. However, functional studies will be further implemented to verify these findings.

Notably, the molecular mechanism underlying platelet production in vitro remains to be fully understood. MK polyploidization is a critical process for MK vand subsequent PLT production. Previous research has indicated that GATA-1 is a key transcription factor involved in MK polyploidization and platelet formation [17, 39]. Muntean et al. demonstrated that GATA-1 regulates growth and polyploidization by controlling cyclin D-Cdk4 (CDK4) kinase activity [39]. Our pseudotime analysis (Figure S2) revealed that CDK4 is expressed during early stages of MK development and that CEBPZ, a CCAAT/enhancer binding protein [40], is also expressed during these early stages. These findings could prove valuable for future research efforts aimed at enhancing the yield of UCB-derived MKs through gene editing. However, several inherent issues related to current limitations in terms of platelet yield, lifespan, and functional maturity must be considered. Moreover, we will face potential challenges in assessing the quality and safety of in vitro-derived platelets in the future.

Conclusion

In summary, this study comprehensively and systematically described the biological characteristics and functions of MKs derived from UCB. The transcriptomic profile of UCB-derived MKs is conducive to a deeper understanding of their intrinsic functional features and will assist in the development of a more efficient system for generating MKs in vitro in the future.

Limitations of this study

Although typical characteristics of UCB-derived MKs were successfully obtained, the yield of PLTs is relatively low. Furthermore, the in vivo experiments conducted were limited to a single instance, which may introduce inherent limitations to the data. Additionally, the cultured mixture was directly used for scRNAseq by 10 × Genomics technology without prior sorting or purification. Given the pore size of the MKs, the purified MKs could be analyzed on a SMART-seq platform in future studies, which may be a better approach for comparing the variation between two different platforms. Finally, the transcriptomic data provided in the present study represent the results from only one donor at the culture endpoint, and future studies should expand the sample size and further analyze the data from different time points, which will help elucidate the differentiation and developmental mechanisms of MKs.
