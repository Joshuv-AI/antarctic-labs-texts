Abstract
. In collaborative settings, difficulties in sustaining a consistent pace and engagement often lead to task drift, reducing preparedness and overall effectiveness between meetings. To address this, we conducted a formative study and developed ReflecToMeet , an AI-assisted system that integrates theory-driven reflective prompts with mechanisms for sharing teammates’ reflections. Informed by ten formative interviews, the system was evaluated in a mixed-method study across three conditions: (1) deeper reflection, (2) regular reflection, and (3) control (unstructured reflection). Participants in the control condition demonstrated less deliberate thought and weaker collaboration, which led to stress and misalignment during team meetings. In contrast, structured reflection supported greater organization and steadier progress. The deeper reflection condition further facilitated confidence, teamwork, and idea generation, although it imposed a higher cognitive load. We also discuss design implications for AI agents that facilitate reflection to enhance collaboration and outline broader considerations for AI-assisted systems aimed at sustaining collaborative goals.

3.
Formative Study

3.1.
Procedure

To explore how students work together in peer-based meetings where no formal hierarchy exists, we conducted a formative study examining two central aspects of collaboration. The first focus was on momentum, task distribution, and task management between meetings, looking at how participants carried forward ideas and tracked progress while working independently. The second focus was on preparedness and collaboration around meetings, examining how participants organized themselves between sessions, initiated discussions, and navigated challenges or gaps that emerged when working as equals.

For our formative study, we conducted semi-structured interviews with 10 student participants (3 female, 7 male), aged 20 to 37 years (
M=28.9
,
SD=5.90
). Participants were recruited from various U.S. universities through email outreach and convenience sampling, and at the time of the study, all were enrolled in either undergraduate or graduate programs. All participants had prior experience completing collaborative tasks such as course projects, research papers, workshops, or poster presentations at least three times during their academic careers. The study received approval from the authors’ Institutional Review Board (IRB) office. Interviews were carried out online via Google Meet by the first author and on an average took 27.4 minutes to complete. In total, the dataset included approximately 4.7 hours of recordings and about 27,000 words of transcribed text. Each participant was compensated with USD $15. The interview questionnaire is provided in Appendix
A
.

3.2.
Data Analysis

We analyzed the interview data using grounded theory. Following grounded theory principles
(
103
;
43
)
, the first and second authors independently performed open coding, allowing themes to emerge inductively rather than being imposed from the interview guide. Through ongoing discussions and iterative refinement, they arrived at a shared thematic framework. Inter-rater reliability for the initial coding was assessed with Cohen’s Kappa, yielding a score of 0.882, which indicates strong agreement.

3.3.
Findings

From our formative study, we have derived three key themes which we have elaborated in the following part of this section.

3.3.1.
Temporal Drift and Visible Loss of Momentum

50% of the participants (N=5) described a recurring pattern in their motivation. They felt a strong boost immediately after a meeting. This motivation gradually declined until the next meeting or an approaching deadline. Several participants said the initial lift came from feeling closer to their goal after aligning with others. Over time, this feeling faded as the goal seemed further away. This pattern reflects the brief “fresh start” effect that often follows temporal landmarks
(
22
)
. One participant (F4) said,
“Immediately after a meeting my focus is high; within a couple of days it declines noticeably.”
Another (F9) noted,
“When there isn’t a deadline coming up, I tend to lose momentum.”
.

Participants (N=6) often linked their motivation to seeing “visible progress” between meetings. They stayed motivated when they could notice small steps forward. These steps could be because of their own initiatives or their teammates’. Many said they tried to track each other’s progress in chat groups. But updates often got lost in long threads. One participant said,
“Messages disappear quickly. When you can’t see what’s new, it’s easy to lose steam.”
Some participants tried tools like Jira and Trello. But they were not very enthusiastic about using them. They felt there was no strong reason to commit. F1 said,
“There’s no one enforcing it. We’re not in an office, so it’s easy to bend the rules.”
Another participant, F3, emphasized the need for personal responsibility when working with peers. They noted that without individual effort, it becomes hard to stay organized. As they explained,
“When you’re working with friends, you have to balance being chill and staying strict. But if I know my friend is making progress, it really motivates me to do my part too.”

The pattern was not universal. A few participants (N=3) said they stayed motivated without reminders. Their roles kept them in structured pipelines where progress was tracked and others relied on their output. In contrast, most participants preferred having light structure to stay on track. They found that small supports helped them stay aligned with the goal. These included a weekly summary of progress, a few timely reminders, and a brief recap to reconnect goals with actions.

3.3.2.
Pre-Meeting Readiness and Declining Confidence

Although most of the meetings involved peers and did not have immediate deadlines, participants still reported feeling a sense of dissatisfaction with the effort they put into their assigned task. This did not always result from visible underperformance, but rather from the inability to organize their thoughts in a structured way. A few (N=3) noted that they had brainstormed ideas earlier in the week or mentally rehearsed possible directions, but later forgot these insights entirely. One participant, F7, said,
“I had a great idea when I was in the bus. I thought I’d remember it. Then I couldn’t find it again when I needed it.”
Others mentioned feeling unsure about what they had actually committed to, especially when the work was loosely defined or open-ended.

As meetings approached, several participants (N=4) described a quiet pressure that made it harder to return to their assigned tasks. They often began the week with a plan, but daily routines and competing responsibilities crowded their attention. For many, the issue was not a lack of intention but the difficulty of carrying it out. Some felt that if they had small prompts or guiding questions to help them re-engage, they might have started earlier and with more clarity. F1 explained,
“The task is not huge, but I keep worrying about how to start. The more I wait, the heavier it feels.”
This often led to procrastination, with work pushed to the night before the meeting. F10 noted,
“I open my notes the night before and try to make sense of it. It feels rushed, like I am only preparing to avoid embarrassment.”
Competing priorities also made it difficult to stay on track. As F10 added,
“I want to sit and work on it, but other things keep coming up. At the end of the week, the task feels like the one I have never touched.”

Several participants emphasized that their confidence going into a meeting was shaped less by how much they had completed and more by how mentally organized they felt. If they had not recently reviewed what had changed or where the group was headed, it became harder to speak clearly about their own part. In these cases, even basic questions, such as what their contribution was meant to be, felt mentally taxing. F7 shared,
“Sometimes I just don’t know how to explain where I am with the task. It’s all in pieces in my head.”

3.3.3.
Collaboration Hurdles and Meeting Traction

Some participants (N=4) described imbalance in how work was shared. Some members carried heavier responsibilities because they did not want tasks left unfinished, while others delayed their contributions until the last moment. This unevenness was less about ability or willingness and more about habits of timing and reliance on peers. Friendship norms made it difficult to confront fairness directly, discouraging critical feedback:
“Sometimes I do more because I can’t leave it unfinished.”-F9
. F5 also talked about this issue,
“It is hard to know if everyone is putting in effort, and you do not want to bring it up when you are all friends on regular basis.”
This led to a pattern of quiet acceptance, where disparities in effort were recognized but rarely addressed.

Beyond workload distribution, participants (N=4) pointed to challenges in the flow of collaboration. Misaligned expectations about standards and styles, when not identified early, often led to misunderstandings or rework due to poor communication. As one participant (F6) explained,
“I spent hours adding details, but later my teammate said they only wanted a rough draft, so all that work went to waste.”
Under-preparedness and incorrect assumptions also disrupted progress, as teams struggled to maintain clarity during meetings, which prolonged task completion. Meeting again without coordinated progress often pushed teams into repeated brainstorming, revisiting the same points rather than moving work forward. As F6 further noted,
“When every week feels like we are having the same discussion, nothing moves ahead unless everyone is on the same page.”

Participants (N=5) also identified broader challenges that align with findings from prior research. One recurring issue was difficulty maintaining focus during meetings, particularly in remote or home-based settings where distractions are common
(
57
;
14
)
. Participants described efforts to remain engaged and avoid missing key points
(
55
)
, yet noted that conversations often drifted away from the central agenda
(
17
)
. Another concern involved the efficient capture of meeting notes, which participants viewed as essential for supporting asynchronous engagement at both individual and group levels
(
111
)
. Additionally, the phenomenon of groupthink was observed, where participants tended to converge on simpler ideas, limiting exploration and innovation. This dynamic was often reinforced by a reluctance to express disagreement or by deferring to more vocal members of the group
(
58
)
. Such dynamics reduced participation, reinforced uneven contributions, and left quieter members less engaged
(
83
;
84
)
. Prior work has also shown that in larger groups, talk time often concentrates in a few voices while others contribute less, and that frequent interruptions or context switching around meetings disrupt focus and require recovery time
(
80
)
.

3.3.4.
Summary

The findings of the formative study suggest that students’ motivation followed a temporal rhythm, often increasing immediately after meetings but declining in the absence of deadlines
(
97
)
. This pattern underscores the importance of lightweight structures that help sustain engagement across intervals. Visible progress, whether through individual steps or group advances, emerged as a critical factor for maintaining momentum. However, participants noted that such progress was often difficult to observe, making it harder for them to stay engaged and coordinate efforts effectively. Simple summaries and timely cues may therefore provide more effective continuity than reliance on complex task management systems. Readiness for meetings was shaped less by the volume of work completed and more by participants’ sense of mental organization, indicating that reflective reminders can strengthen both clarity and confidence. Collaboration was further challenged by uneven effort and misaligned expectations, which reduced productivity and often stalled progress. While broader behaviors such as procrastination and role-specific work habits extend beyond the immediate scope of our current work, the overall results highlight visible progress, timely reflection, and minimal preparatory supports as central elements for sustaining collaboration between meetings.

3.4.
Design Considerations

Based on our formative study, we propose two design considerations (DCs) to guide the development of an AI-assisted system aimed at supporting collaborative tasks. The goals focus on strengthening preparedness between meetings, thereby reducing stress and minimizing misalignment during group interactions.

3.4.1.
DC1: Provide personalized reflective prompts and summaries to support preparedness

Participants often struggled to recall earlier ideas or commitments as meetings approached, which reduced clarity and confidence. Some described forgetting insights that emerged during the week, while others noted that tasks felt heavier as they waited longer to re-engage. These lapses often led to last minute preparation and hindered meaningful contributions during meetings. Providing reflective prompts and concise summaries can help participants recall prior insights, reconnect with ongoing goals, and translate plans into concrete actions. Personalized prompts are particularly valuable, as they would encourage participants to reflect on their goals with greater sincerity and to plan more effectively for the upcoming meeting in relation to both prior discussions and the time elapsed since the last session.
DC
1
highlights the role of such prompts and summaries in strengthening preparedness and ensuring that meetings build on prior progress.

3.4.2.
DC2: Share team members’ progress to help participants remain focused and informed

A recurring challenge was the difficulty of staying aware of teammates’ progress between meetings. Without clear visibility, participants often felt uncertain about the group’s overall status, which reduced motivation and made collaboration feel uneven. In contrast, visible progress from peers was described as motivating and encouraging reciprocal effort. Beyond task updates, participants also valued understanding how team members approached the same task differently, including the level of enthusiasm or effort they were investing. Hearing such perspectives in their own words was seen as a way to strengthen accountability and increase a sense of collective responsibility. Providing updates in a clear and accessible format, as emphasized in
DC
2
, can help participants stay focused, reinforce accountability, and minimize misunderstandings about task status.

4.
ReflecToMeet

Based on the design guidelines identified in the formative study, we designed, developed, and empirically evaluated
ReflecToMeet
, a AI-assisted system that provides personalized reflective questions and shares team members’ reflective progress to enhance collaborative preparedness and task involvement between meetings.

4.1.
System Overview

ReflecToMeet
is an AI-assisted system that provides personalized reflective questions to individuals based on task context and user responses. It also shares team members’ responses to these reflective questions with one another, allowing participants to understand each other’s state of progress and individual approaches to shared tasks.
ReflecToMeet
is designed to address
DC
1
and
DC
2
, enabling us to examine whether personalized reflection and shared peer progress can enhance collaborative preparedness and increase task involvement between meetings.

4.1.1.
Theoretical Baseline of
ReflecToMeet

The reflective questions in
ReflecToMeet
are based on Kolb’s Experiential Learning Theory
(
47
)
. This theory explains learning as a continuous cycle that involves four stages:

•

Concrete Experience (CE):
Engaging in a new experience or situation.

•

Reflective Observation (RO):
Thinking back and reflecting on the experience.

•

Abstract Conceptualization (AC):
Forming theories, ideas, or generalizations from those reflections.

•

Active Experimentation (AE):
Testing those ideas in practice, which leads to new experiences.

This theory conceptualizes learning as a cyclical process of concrete experience, reflective observation, abstract conceptualization, and active experimentation, through which knowledge emerges from the transformation of experience. This framework highlights the importance of designing systems and activities that cause reflection and iteration, enabling learners to connect lived experiences with theoretical insights and apply them in practice.

We used four stages of Kolb’s theory to design reflective questions that would encourage users to think about their work in a structured way. As per our design consideration,
DC
1
, we hypothesized that reflective questions would help users recall their commitments and prepare for their tasks more effectively. These reflections first guide users to recall and reflect on what happened in the previous meeting (CE, RO). Then they ask users to interpret these experiences and identify broader insights (AC). Finally, they encourage users to apply these insights in preparing for the upcoming meeting (AE). Each reflective question is adapted to the specific task context and is informed from a summary of all prior interactions, including the user’s earlier responses. The reflective questions are supposed to appear regularly in the time interval between two meetings and are designed to connect one meeting to the next. After each new meeting, a new set of reflective questions is generated so that the cycle of reflection and preparation continues.

In line with
DC
2
, we further hypothesized that sharing responses among teammates would promote awareness of collective progress and facilitate greater task engagement. Users are notified when their peers’ responses become available in the system, allowing them to view each other’s reflections as an evolving work in progress.

4.1.2.
System Development

ReflecToMeet
was implemented as a web-based system to support structured reflection between meetings. The front-end was developed in Streamlit, providing participants with secure login and an interface for uploading meeting transcripts periodically. Uploaded transcripts were processed through GPT-4o-mini API calls, which generated meeting summaries and extracted key discussion points to serve as inputs for subsequent reflective questions. The backend used MongoDB to store user data. Nginx managed routing, WebSocket upgrades, and HTTPS enforcement across the system.

As shown in Figure
2
, the workflow begins when a user uploads a meeting transcript to
ReflecToMeet
(indicated with a dotted line). The system processes the transcript using GPT-4o-mini to create a
comprehensive meeting summary (CMS)
, a short recap, and a set of personalized reflective questions. The recap is a brief note, limited to 150 words, that reminds participants of the previous discussion and their ongoing task. The CMS is updated after each meeting to include, meeting’s state of progress, any changes, or clarifications. The reflective questions, designed around Kolb’s experiential learning cycle, guide participants in reviewing their work and preparing for the next step. Users respond to these questions, and their answers are stored for later reflections. The system then combines the CMS with the reflective responses to maintain continuity in generating reflective questions for the following day. Meeting transcript is added only on days when meetings take place, and the same cycle continues into the next interval. The following text shows a sample recap generated using the prompt shown in Appendix

.

Throughout the reflections,

4.1.3.
Interaction Workflow

While designing

, we aimed for a minimal and easy-to-navigate interface structured around two main tabs:

Reflections

Reflections

Based on this input, the system generates a set of personalized reflective questions, which the participant responds to directly within the interface. For the user study, we used two set of structured reflective questions: (1) regular, fact-based questions and (2) deeper, thought-based questions.
Regular reflections
were designed to mainly ask “what” questions that encourage participants to reconsider or review their actions. It involved brief, structured check-ins that help participants recall meeting goals, track progress, and identify immediate next steps. It emphasized consistency and accountability without adding a lot of cognitive load. In contrast,
deeper reflections
were designed to ask “how” and “why” questions that encourage thoughtfulness and deeper analysis (shown in Table

It encouraged exploring alternative approaches, connecting current tasks to broader goals, and generating new ideas to facilitate confidence, teamwork, and long-term growth, though with greater effort required. In addition, we used one set of unstructured reflective questions which are shown in Table

.

Once the user submitted their response to the reflective questions, the response appears on the
Dashboard
, alongside the question, previous responses, and reflections from team members answering the reflective question on the same day. To maintain engagement between meetings, the system sends email notifications when a new reflective question is generated or when a teammate responds, reminding participants to revisit the interface and remain connected with their own and their team’s evolving understanding of the task. 5.
Methodology: User Study

To evaluate the impact of
ReflecToMeet
on collaborative tasks, we conducted a user study in which participants used our AI-based system to complete a shared task. The primary goal of
ReflecToMeet
is not to track progress, but to support reflection on individual actions while maintaining focus on the overall objectives. It also aims to enhance self-efficacy by allowing users to stay informed about their teammates’ reflective thoughts. To support this, the system design incorporates Kolb’s experiential learning framework, introducing varying levels of reflection to assess how users perceive them and whether these differences influence their experience individually and collectively. The following section describes the user study conducted with
ReflecToMeet
in detail.

5.1.
Study Design and Setup

We conducted a user study using
ReflecToMeet
, where participants were paired to design a poster over a period of five days. We selected a task that required real collaboration over multiple days to better evaluate our system’s impact. Participants were allowed to form pairs based on their own preference. Those who did not select a partner were randomly paired. All participants were given clear instructions about the task. They were asked to hold two online meetings. The first meeting was to choose a poster topic and divide responsibilities. We provided suggested topics, such as reducing food waste, improving study habits, making online learning more engaging, or cutting down screen time, but participants were free to choose their own topics. The second meeting was held to review and finalize the poster. Each meeting was recommended to last no longer than 40 minutes. Participants were instructed to use any online meeting platform they preferred and to transcribe their meeting data using any tool into a text file to upload to
ReflecToMeet
. The entire study was conducted remotely. We instructed participants to refrain from using any LLM tools for this task. Between these two meetings, participants received daily notifications to answer reflective questions within the system. On each day, they could also view their partner’s reflections to stay informed. Communication outside the scheduled meetings was restricted to reduce unstructured interaction and isolate the effect of guided reflection. The study focused on how structured reflection shaped the individual’s collaborative experience, rather than evaluating the quality of the final poster.

5.2.
Participants and Group Assignment

We recruited 30 students for this study, including 18 male and 12 female participants, with ages ranging from 20 to 34 years (
M=26.40
,
SD=3.87
). Participants were enrolled in undergraduate or graduate programs at various universities in the United States. Recruitment was carried out through convenience and snowball sampling methods. All participants had prior experience completing collaborative academic tasks, such as course projects, research papers, workshops, or poster presentations, at least twice in their academic or professional career. We did not include participants from the formative study to avoid bias and ensure that all participants approached the task with fresh perspectives. Participants had to spent around 4 hours during the entire study in the span of 5 days. Each participant received USD $50 as compensation. The study was approved by the Institutional Review Board (IRB) of the authors’ institution.

All participants were randomly assigned to one of the three groups, each consisting of five teams or pairs. The groups differed in their exposure to reflective questions. Group 1 (G1) received regular reflection question each day. Group 2 (G2) received both regular and deeper questions designed to encourage critical and abstract reflection. Finally, Group 3 (G3), the control group, received unstructured reflection questions. The questions consisted of structured reflection grounded in Kolb’s experiential learning theory and were tailored to the evolving task context and interactions. Participants received these reflections throughout the study to gauge how different levels of reflection affected collaborative preparedness and task engagement.

5.3.
Study Timeline and Reflection Flow

In real settings, meetings may occur at different intervals such as weekly, biweekly, or monthly. For this empirical study, we limited the reflection stage to five consecutive days to balance task load, sustain participant engagement, and keep the study duration manageable. During this period, participants followed a structured five-stage plan. The approach could be extended in longer studies by introducing gaps between reflections, by spacing reflections around major milestones, or by aligning them with project deadlines. These adjustments would allow the structure to adapt to longer timelines while preserving the benefits of staged reflection. In Table

On Day #1, participants held their first meeting, and they received their reflective questions focused on reflection after this initial meeting. On Day #2, the questions were designed to check early progress and identify challenges in an early stage. Day #3 marked the midpoint of the task, and the focus shifted to aligning with teammates and reflecting on individual contributions. On Day #4, the day before the final meeting, the questions emphasized preparation by outlining the final steps and considering the actions taken so far. On Day #5, the final stage, the questions supported last-minute checks, readiness, and reflection on the overall effort. Table

5.4.
Evaluation Method

We designed a post-task feedback survey to evaluate the impact of
ReflecToMeet
on participants’ collaborative experience. The survey was grounded in the same theoretical framework that guided the design of our reflective questions, drawing on Kolb’s experiential learning cycle (Concrete Experience, Reflective Observation, Abstract Conceptualization, and Active Experimentation). Most of the questions (5-point Likert, 1 = Strongly Disagree to 5 = Strongly Agree) targeted the perceived effects of reflection at each stage: recall and presence during the task (CE), awareness and interpretation of experiences (RO), drawing connections and generalizations (AC), and planning future actions (AE). We tested deeper reflection questions for all four stages, while regular reflection questions were used for three stages (CE, RO, AE). Additional questions measured participants’ overall sense of preparedness, confidence, and perceived usefulness of the reflection process. We also included questions about the timing, frequency, and delivery method of the reflective prompts, teammate reflection awareness for G1/G2, and a few open-ended questions to capture qualitative insights on perceived benefits, design feedback, and suggestions for future use. In addition, we administered the NASA-TLX to capture perceived workload. Participants rated the six sub-scales (Mental Demand, Physical Demand, Temporal Demand, Performance, Effort, and Frustration) on 1–10 scales. These workload ratings were analyzed alongside the survey outcomes.

We piloted the survey with a small sample to ensure clarity and reasonable length; participants took approximately 10–12 minutes to complete it. Following this, we administered the final version at the end of the study. The responses were analyzed using a mixed-method approach, which is described in detail in the Results section.

6.
Result

We adopted a mixed-method analysis to evaluate the results of our study. First, we report findings from the quantitative analysis of the Likert-scale items, followed by a discussion of qualitative insights derived from participants’ responses to the open-ended questions.

6.1.8.
Summary: Quantitative Analysis

Participants across all groups found the reflective questions were helpful for recalling their daily work, as measured by the Concrete Experience (CE) scales. However, scores for both regular (CE-R) and deeper (CE-D) reflection were similar across conditions, with no statistically significant differences. This suggests that the prompts may have supported subjective memory and awareness, but did not lead to measurable variation in task recall. In Reflective Observation (RO), clearer differences appeared. For regular version (RO-R), the deeper reflection group (G2) scored higher than both the regular reflection group (G1) and the control group (G3). In contrast, for deeper version (RO-D), the regular reflection group (G1) had the highest score, even above G2. Both reflective groups outperformed the control group on this scale. These results suggest that structured reflections were effective in promoting attention to task experiences, and that regular reflection could sometimes support version as effectively as deeper reflections. In Abstract Conceptualization (AC), both G1 and G2 reported stronger pattern recognition and meaning-making compared to G3. The deeper reflection group (G2) also showed a marginal but noticeable advantage over G1, suggesting that deeper prompts provided some additional benefit in supporting conceptual insights.

In Active Experimentation (AE), no reliable group differences were found in the AE-R scale, and the internal consistency was low. This limits interpretation. In contrast, the deeper reflection group (G2) scored significantly higher than the other groups in AE-D. This finding suggests that deeper prompts were effective in supporting planning and preparation for future work. The
Overall Effect
composite also showed significant group differences. Both reflective groups scored higher than the control group on items related to preparedness, confidence, and the value of reflection, and the two reflection conditions did not differ from each other. Responses about peer reflection were positive in both G1 and G2. G1 showed more consistent agreement, while G2 responses were slightly stronger but more varied. Finally, NASA-TLX results showed no group differences in performance, effort, frustration, or overall workload. However, the control group reported higher temporal demand, while the deeper reflection group reported greater mental demand. These findings suggest that deeper reflection helped participants engage more fully with the task and plan ahead, while also requiring more cognitive effort.

Additionally, We found that 16 participants (53.3%) preferred receiving reflective questions in the evening or night, while 9 (30.0%) favored the morning and 5 (16.7%) chose the afternoon. Regarding delivery method, 12 participants (40.0%) preferred in-app notifications, followed by 7 (23.3%) who opted for email reminders, 6 (20.0%) for text message notifications, and 5 (16.7%) for calendar alerts.

6.2.
Qualitative Analysis

To gain deeper insight into participants’ experiences, we included several open-ended questions in the post-study survey. These questions asked about the usability of the interface, the usefulness of reflection in supporting task planning and collaboration, reactions to receiving two types of questions daily (for Group 2), and the influence of viewing a teammate’s reflections. Participants also described what worked or did not in their second meeting and offered suggestions for improving the reflection model. We analyzed the responses using grounded theory
(
103
;
43
)
, allowing themes to emerge inductively from the data. The first and second authors independently performed open coding, followed by iterative discussion to develop a shared thematic framework. Inter-rater reliability for the initial coding was strong (Cohen’s Kappa = 0.861), indicating a high level of agreement.

6.2.1.
Perceived Role of Reflection and Recommendations

Reflection functioned as a micro deadline that reset attention and reduced drift. Participants across groups described a brief pause that helped them review progress and choose the next step. The effect was strongest in Group 1 and Group 2, where structure and partner visibility supported steady pacing. Group 3 reported similar benefits from unstructured prompts but asked for clearer cues. As a regular reflection participant (G1), GP7 mentioned:

“When I reach the check-in, it feels like a small deadline. I pause to review what I have finished and what remains, then I choose one next step and write it down. Putting the step into words makes the work concrete, and I begin with less hesitation.” -GP7

Participants (N = 6) emphasized the importance of lightweight task support directly adjacent to the reflection prompt. Many expressed a desire for a simple checklist they could update in the same space, along with features such as a shared progress bar, filters for due items, and calendar integration. These small additions were seen as crucial for turning reflection into concrete action. Participants (N = 3) also requested easier ways to share their status updates with partners and to receive personalized reminders. These features were believed to reduce friction, clarify responsibilities, and improve coordination without introducing extra effort. One participant from the control group, CP6, added:

“I wanted a small checklist next to the prompt and a progress bar we both could see. That would’ve helped us stay aligned and know what’s left without digging through messages.” -CP6

Participants (N = 4) also reflected on the limitations of individual reflection when it was not accompanied by shared visibility. While the daily prompts supported personal consistency and helped reduce procrastination, several participants noted difficulties in coordinating with teammates, especially as the task approached completion. Without features for sharing tasks or viewing a partner’s progress, each person had to manage their work independently. This lack of awareness made it harder to align efforts and prepare shared deliverables. One participant explained that the absence of collaborative tools led to inefficiencies during the final stages of the project:

“The prompts helped me organize my work by guiding my daily reflections and keeping me consistent, which reduced procrastination. However, they lacked features for collaboration and planning. My tasks were invisible to my teammates, creating a drawback in coordination, especially on the final day when we needed to merge our work smoothly.” -CP2

Participants (N = 4) highlighted moments of cognitive fatigue and expressed the need for greater control over their reflective experience. While deeper prompts were often seen as valuable for encouraging insight, they could feel burdensome, particularly on days when participants were balancing multiple commitments. Several described a sense of overwhelm when responding to such prompts under time pressure. To address this, they recommended features that would allow them to modulate the depth of their reflection depending on context. One commonly requested feature was a private notes option (N = 3), enabling users to record sensitive blockers or incomplete thoughts without having to share them fully with a partner. Instead, they could choose to share a brief summary or status update, preserving transparency while maintaining autonomy.

6.2.2.
Perceptions of Fact-Based and Thought-Based Reflections

As Group 2 received both fact-based and thought-based reflection prompts, their responses offer insight into how this dual-question format shaped their experience. Most participants (N = 7) appreciated the combination, noting that it offered a balance between maintaining task focus and encouraging deeper insight. Fact-based prompts were considered helpful for clarifying goals, tracking progress, and reducing procrastination. Because they were easy to answer, participants found them effective for sustaining momentum across the week.

“The factual question helped me remember my progress and keep procrastination in check… The thought-provoking one helped me see the difficulties of the task more clearly… Overall, the approach worked well by covering both aspects.” -DP5

In contrast, thought-based prompts required more cognitive effort but supported sense-making by helping participants identify blockers, question assumptions, and reassess plans. Many participants (N = 5) noted that while these prompts were valuable, they were better suited for key moments such as midpoints or preparation before meetings, rather than for everyday use. This contrast led to a common preference for a mixed rhythm, with regular fact-based prompts to support continuity and occasional thought-based prompts to encourage reflection. As another participant explained:

“On busy days, the deeper prompt felt a bit heavy, though it often led to useful reflection when I had the time.” -DP7

This structure helped participants stay organized without becoming overwhelmed, aligning their daily task flow with opportunities for deeper learning.

6.2.3.
Impact of Accessing Partner Reflections on Collaboration

Participants emphasized that access to partner reflections played a key role in shaping how they coordinated tasks and shared responsibility throughout the collaboration. In Groups 1 and 2, even brief updates were often enough to understand what had been completed, what remained, and how to adjust individual plans accordingly. This kind of lightweight visibility supported day-to-day coordination, reduced redundant work, and clarified ownership of tasks. It also offered a subtle sense of mutual accountability. Several participants (N = 6) noted that knowing their reflections would be seen by a partner encouraged them to contribute consistently and maintain a steady pace.

Participants also noted that visibility into partner reflections influenced team engagement. A few participants (N = 3) were surprised by the enthusiasm of quieter or more introverted partners, which became apparent only through written reflections. These individuals were more likely to contribute when the system provided explicit, low-pressure invitations to share their thoughts. Such prompts helped balance participation and made collaboration feel more inclusive. Additionally, after reading a partner’s update, participants often adjusted their plans, reduced overlapping work, and clarified task ownership. Even short entries were sufficient to align roles and facilitate smooth handoffs. As RP3 reflected:

“The reflection questions kept me focused. They reminded me of what I had done and what was still pending. Knowing I had to report my progress helped me stay accountable and reduced procrastination. Seeing my partner’s responses also clarified our roles, so we could plan more effectively together. It made the whole process feel more organized.” -RP3

In Group 3, where participants (N = 6) did not have access to their partner’s reflections, many (N = 5) described a lingering sense of uncertainty in their collaboration. Group projects are rarely seamless; team members often bring differing levels of motivation, communication habits, and availability. Some participants (N = 3) checked in regularly, while others were absent for long periods, creating gaps in coordination. In this context, participants were not simply seeking task updates. They expressed a desire to understand how their partner was approaching the work, what challenges they were facing, and whether they remained actively engaged. Several (N = 4) believed that access to partner reflections could have revealed misalignments early, reduced unintentional duplication of effort, and facilitated timely support when a teammate appeared to be struggling.

“The most useful thing I could notice by reading my partner’s responses would be their progress, challenges, and unfinished tasks. This insight would help me align my work with theirs, avoid duplication, and provide support where needed. It could also be surprising to see differences in how we approach the same task, which might give me new strategies.” -CP3

6.2.4.
From Alignment to Friction

Participants described the second meeting as a key point where they had to bring together individual work and prepare their final output. Although all teams completed the task, participants reported different experiences in terms of clarity, pacing, and coordination. These differences appeared to reflect whether and how they engaged with the reflection support provided during the task. Since the meeting transcripts contained timestamps, we analyzed the duration of second sessions across all three groups. Group 3 held the longest meetings, averaging 36.7 minutes, compared to 25.1 minutes in Group 1 and 29.6 minutes in Group 2.

In Group 1, participants (N = 5) frequently described the meeting as efficient and focused. The use of factual or regular prompts helped them keep track of their individual contributions, which made it easier to enter the meeting with shared awareness of progress. Most reported that there was little confusion and that the conversation centered on wrapping up final tasks. RP8 reflected on the experience, saying,

“The second meeting felt like tying up loose ends. We had already written most things down during the week, so it was just a matter of checking off what was left.” -RP8

Group 2 participants (N = 6) described a different kind of meeting. They said the inclusion of deeper reflection prompted more discussion and branched out, which sometimes extended the meeting but also helped clarify decisions. The conversation often turned to why certain choices had been made, and how best to merge the work. DP4 shared,

“We had to talk through some decisions we made separately. It took time, but it helped us spot gaps and explain our reasoning before merging.” -DP4

Several participants (N = 4) viewed the longer discussions as useful, especially when they led to a more consistent and coherent final product. A few (N = 2) noted that the reflections helped them revisit earlier decisions or spot misalignments that would have otherwise gone unnoticed.

Group 3 participants (N = 5) reported greater difficulty. Since they did not receive structured reflection or have access to their partner’s updates, many (N = 4) said they entered the meeting without knowing what their teammate had completed. Several (N = 3) described spending the first part of the meeting catching up and redistributing tasks. CP10 noted,

“We spent the first half just figuring out what each of us had done. If I had seen their progress earlier, we could have finished the poster faster.” -CP10

Although some participants in Group 3 (N = 2) were still able to finish the task without major issues, many described the meeting as reactive rather than strategic. The lack of early visibility made it harder to align, and final integration required more effort than expected.

6.2.5.
Summary: Qualitative Analysis

Reflection acted as a micro-deadline that helped participants pause, review progress, and decide on next steps. This effect was strongest in Groups 1 and 2, where structure and partner visibility supported steady pacing, while Group 3 reported similar benefits but asked for clearer cues. Participants emphasized the need for lightweight task support, such as checklists, progress bars, and reminders, to turn reflection into concrete action and reduce coordination burden. Fact-based prompts were valued for sustaining momentum and reducing procrastination, whereas thought-based prompts encouraged deeper insight but were seen as more useful at midpoints or before meetings rather than every day. Access to partner reflections improved collaboration, accountability, and inclusivity, while the lack of visibility in Group 3 led to uncertainty, duplicated work, and longer meetings. Participants also noted cognitive fatigue from deeper prompts on busy days and recommended more flexibility, including private notes and adjustable depth, to balance personal autonomy with collaborative transparency.

7.
Discussion

Our findings show that reflective cues supported participants in recalling daily activities, as indicated by the absence of significant differences in recall-related scores (CE-R, CE-D) across groups. This suggests that both structured and unstructured reflection acted as retrieval cues that stabilized memory but did not change how participants described their work, consistent with prior findings that reflection often produces descriptive accounts rather than “deeper reframing”
(
19
)
. Clearer differences emerged on interpretive dimensions: deeper reflection enhanced noticing on simpler items, while regular reflection sometimes matched or exceeded it on more demanding cues. The effectiveness of reflective questions thus depended as much on timing and workload as on depth
(
34
)
, with deeper questions burdensome under workload but valuable at natural pauses. Both reflective groups outperformed the control in spotting patterns and making sense of past work, comparable to the recap tools that transform raw notes into usable summaries
(
114
;
90
)
. The strongest benefits of deeper reflection appeared in planning for future work, aligning with research on self-regulation, intentions, and the motivational effects of temporal landmarks
(
22
)
. Deeper reflection encourages more thorough observation of a topic, which can be especially valuable for tasks that unfold over a longer period. In contrast, its benefits are harder to realize in short-term projects, where the added cognitive effort may outweigh the gains. However, it is important to note that this user study was conducted in a compressed time-frame, where all groups had to respond to reflective questions regularly, which naturally felt more cognitively burdensome. Overall, these findings reinforce Kolb’s experiential learning model, which progresses from concrete experience to reflective observation, abstract conceptualization, and preparation for action. In the following section, we discuss the various aspects of the design implications of
ReflecToMeet
:

7.1.
Time, Personalization and Frequency

As per our findings from the user study, timing, personalization, and frequency emerged as central factors shaping how participants engaged with reflection. Reflection were seen as useful when they remained brief and factual, supporting quick recall without interrupting ongoing work. Deeper reflection, by contrast, carried more weight which led to cognitive load. Probably, deeper reflections are best received at a certain interval, like mid-cycle or just before meetings, when participants naturally would pause to consolidate progress. This suggests that reflection is not only about what is asked but when it is asked, and at what level of cognitive demand
(
75
;
50
)
. Yet this insight surfaces a tricky tension: at what point does a “helpful reminder” turn into a distraction rather than a prompt for reflection? Because reflection is a personal and internal cognitive process, it is appropriate that the individual makes the ultimate decision. Moreover, personalization is shaped not only by contextual factors but also by the person’s own schedule
(
19
;
61
;
49
)
. Allowing participants to set their own reflection windows and delivery channels (for example, evening reminders or calendar notifications) will increase flexibility and compliance. However, while personal choice supports autonomy, it also risks undermining team rhythm.

While participants in our study chose to do steady daily engagement, in real life scenario there could be different circumstance where someone may lean toward bursts of concentrated work. Designing for both styles suggests the value of features like weekly syntheses that allow “burst workers” to stay aligned without daily entries. Yet, in accommodating both rhythms, systems risk implicitly privileging one productivity ethic over another, framing reflection either as a discipline of consistency or as a flexible, on-demand resource
(
70
;
9
)
. A similar tension appeared when managing mental effort. Participants valued features like adjusting question depth or saving answers to finish later, since these made reflection easier. Yet too much structure can feel controlling. For example, if every prompt always asks “why” or if sample answers push users to copy a certain style, the tool risks telling people how to think instead of letting them reflect in their own way.

Sustaining engagement over time required variety. Participants appreciated when regular and deeper reflections felt personalized and when there was a day-long gap between two reflections. They also suggested adjusting the frequency of reflection based on how much they responded. These strategies may reduce fatigue and keep reflection fresh. Yet adaptive reflection raises concerns. Does adjusting frequency based on behavior truly respect individual choice, or could it quietly encourage avoidance or limit opportunities for growth? This question is especially relevant in academic and peer groups, where participation is voluntary but contributions also carry social weight. Another issue is that when responding to reflections feels demanding, skipping them can also create pressure since absence itself becomes noticeable. Participants emphasized the need for a gentle way back after missed days, such as a single catch-up summary that eased guilt and lowered the effort to re-engage. While this approach reduces friction, it raises an important question: should missed reflections be viewed as a sign of overload, suggesting the system should ease off, or as a lapse that requires correction? The way absence is addressed strongly influences whether reflection is experienced as supportive or as an imposed obligation. These findings suggest that systems like
ReflecToMeet
must balance support with flexibility, allowing reflection to remain both effective and self-directed.

7.2.
From Reflection to Accountability

Participants noted the value of visibility when it focused on practical coordination between meetings. Seeing which tasks were in motion after a meeting, where blockers had emerged, and how work depended on others helped them plan their next steps for the following session. They also suggested using metrics such as scoreboards or streaks to quantify or visualize progress. Although this might make tracking easier on a regular basis, it can also create pressure to perform rather than collaborate, shifting the purpose of reflection from genuine progress to mere appearances. The challenge is to provide just enough visibility to coordinate without encouraging performative reporting. One participant (RP9) noted that their teammate was not very engaged or consistent in responding to reflective questions on time. As a result, they suggested a feature that would allow writing a short note such as “waiting for response from X,” to appear in the shared meeting recap, so that partners could understand why progress might be slowing. When combined with gentle reciprocity nudges, such context could invite updates without placing blame and help sustain coordination. At the same time, if a partner consistently under-participates, visibility may facilitate empathy by clarifying their situation; otherwise, it may instead create frustration and lead others to disengage. Moreover, making progress visible does not ensure accountability. If someone deliberately misrepresents their progress and this becomes evident in the next meeting, it can erode trust in the system and reduce team members’ motivation to engage. Requiring periodic progress reports can help mitigate this risk by making it more difficult to sustain false claims until everyone has to face consequence.

Participants appreciated when their reflections were converted into simple summaries that specified what needed to be done and who would take responsibility before the next meeting. For instance, if someone wrote “I need to finish the introduction,”
ReflecToMeet
generated a recap that identified the action and the person responsible which then served as a planning anchor in the following meeting. This process made it easier to move from ideas to concrete tasks and created a clearer sense of accountability across meetings. However, there is a risk that if the system generates too much of the plan, participants may feel they are only following instructions rather than making their own decisions. A feature that allows users to override or edit the system’s summary would therefore be more appropriate.
ReflecToMeet
was designed and evaluated in settings without a formal hierarchy. In teams with designated leaders, the system would need to present a clear overview of risks, delays, and overall progress while protecting the privacy of individual reflections. Designing such a system without compromising personal space is challenging, since reflection is inherently personal and cannot be fully controlled
(
29
;
87
)
. Moreover, excessive visibility can create a sense of surveillance. If participants believe that every word is being monitored, they may withhold honest reflections, undermining the value of the practice itself.

Participants emphasized that privacy is central to reflective practice because entries often contain tentative ideas, uncertainty, or frustration that are not intended for others and may not yet be ready to share in the next meeting. A prudent default is to keep all entries private and allow users to promote selected items to partner or team visibility only when they are ready to surface them in the upcoming discussion. For example, a participant might keep a personal note about difficulty managing time private but share a progress update such as “finished draft of methods” with the group. This design treats privacy as a contextual gradient rather than a binary setting, which improves legibility and reduces the risk of accidental sharing
(
23
;
20
)
. Without such clarity, accidental sharing can erode trust in systems such as
ReflecToMeet
. Similarly, when
ReflecToMeet
is used by instructors or supervisors, strong transparency is essential. If responsiveness or the perceived quality of reflections is used for grading, it can undermine reflection, which benefits from independence and spontaneity. Systems should therefore provide only aggregate views and trends, such as overall progress or recurring blockers, and keep raw reflections private unless the author explicitly opts to share them. This approach prevents reflective writing from becoming part of formal evaluation and reduces bias in assessment.

Participants suggested adding voice recording features to reduce the need for typing long responses between meetings. Not everyone is comfortable with writing; some individuals prefer speaking, sketching, or leaving brief audio notes as they prepare for or reflect on meetings. Supporting multiple modalities such as text, audio, or simple sketches can make reflection more accessible and inclusive. For example, one participant mentioned recording a 30-second voice note while commuting, which felt more natural than composing a written entry. By offering diverse input options, systems can avoid privileging one form of expression over others. Beyond modality, accessibility must also account for users with different physical, cognitive, and situational needs. Reflection tools should be designed to accommodate this diversity, ensuring that all users can participate comfortably. Reflections should be delivered in ways that align with users’ cognitive load, providing both ease of response and a sense of autonomy in the reflective process.

7.3.
Generalizability of
ReflecToMeet

Our study was conducted over a fixed five-day period, during which participants held two meetings and responded to daily reflection designed using
Kolb’s Experiential Learning Model

(
47
)
. Each day’s prompt targeted a different stage of Kolb’s cycle, helping participants recall recent actions, interpret their experiences, and prepare for the next session. This tightly sequenced schedule worked because the interval between meetings was short, allowing participants to sustain attention and benefit from daily reflection without the process feeling disconnected from their ongoing work. However, real-world meetings often occur on looser cycles such as every seven, ten, or fifteen days. Extending
ReflecToMeet
to these contexts would require recalibrating both the frequency and intensity of reflection. If the same five-day structure were applied unchanged across a fifteen-day gap, participants would face ten unanchored days, likely experiencing drift or forgetting what had been discussed, while asking for fifteen daily reflections would be excessive and could create fatigue and reduce engagement.

One way to address this is to stretch the Kolb-based structure across longer intervals by adjusting frequency. In a seven-day interval, the system could retain near-daily check-ins but reduce their cognitive load through short and low-effort reflection for quick status and planning, while inserting one deeper reflection around midweek to encourage conceptual reframing. In a ten-day interval, reflections could appear every two to three days, alternating between brief recaps of progress and occasional deeper questions that synthesize work completed so far. In a fifteen-day interval, the system could adopt a layered structure with early lightweight reflection on days three to five to keep momentum, mid-cycle deeper reflections on days seven to ten to revisit assumptions, and pre-meeting reflection on days thirteen to fourteen to consolidate what has been done and plan what will be discussed (shown in Table
5
). This approach preserves the pedagogical intent of Kolb’s model by cycling through experience, reflection, conceptualization, and planning while spacing it to match longer project rhythms.
ReflecToMeet
could implement this adaptively by using meeting dates as anchors and automatically rescaling the reflection schedule between them.

Beyond rescheduling, longer intervals would also require stronger context persistence. When gaps stretch to two weeks or more, participants are more likely to forget prior decisions or lose narrative continuity. To support this,
ReflecToMeet
would need to generate cumulative summaries that integrate earlier reflections and meeting notes so each prompt reactivates shared context before asking users to contribute. These adaptations could also support broader deployments beyond our study setting. In classroom projects spanning several weeks, reflection intensity could ramp up before milestones, while in research labs or distributed professional teams, low-frequency lightweight reflections could maintain alignment across long cycles. In mentorship or training programs, deep reflective reflections could appear only at key developmental checkpoints, separated by long intervals of lighter progress-oriented reflections. Overall, while our five-day structure demonstrated that daily reflection can sustain short-term momentum, generalizing
ReflecToMeet
requires decoupling the reflection frequency from fixed calendar days and tying it instead to the temporal distance between meetings. This would allow the system to remain effective across diverse meeting rhythms, preserving both continuity and cognitive balance as intervals lengthen.

8.
Limitations and Future Work

Though
ReflecToMeet
has shown that it can facilitate better collaborative preparedness through its reflection-on-action based cues, it is not without limitations. First, this study primarily relied on self-reported measures of collaboration quality and engagement, and did not evaluate the quality of the posters produced by each group. As a result, it remains unclear whether improvements in preparedness directly translated into higher-quality outcomes. Future studies will include independent assessments of final artifacts to triangulate perceived and actual performance. Second, while responding to reflection prompts requires cognitive effort and time to process, this study was conducted over a compressed five-day span to observe effects within a controlled time-frame. Such rapid cycles may have amplified cognitive load and may not reflect how participants would engage with reflection over longer intervals. In future work, we plan to examine how adaptive scheduling and varied reflection frequency influence engagement and outcomes across longer projects. Third, the reflective structure used in
ReflecToMeet
was based on
Kolb’s Experiential Learning Model
. While this model helped structure reflection in our study, it has faced criticism for oversimplifying learning processes. More recent work, such as
Morris’s experiential learning framework
, offers simplified and context-sensitive adaptations that may better suit diverse learners
(
71
)
. In future iterations, we aim to incorporate revised versions of Kolb’s model and explore alternative frameworks to account for the variability of learning styles and contexts. Finally, this study was conducted with small, peer-based student teams working on structured tasks in a non-hierarchical setting. This limits the generalizability of our findings to other contexts. Larger groups often involve more diverse dynamics, and hierarchical teams may introduce power asymmetries that influence how reflection is perceived and enacted. Future work will evaluate
ReflecToMeet
in settings with more heterogeneous team structures, larger group sizes, and different organizational cultures to examine its scalability and robustness.

9.
Conclusion

In collaborative settings, one of the most challenging moments occurs during the long intervals between meetings, when attention, motivation, and accountability often drift. While prior research has explored AI-assisted support before, during, and after meetings, these gaps remain underexplored even though they represent a critical period for sustaining collaboration. To address this gap, we first conducted a formative interview study with students to identify the challenges they face in maintaining progress during these intervals. Drawing on these findings, we designed an AI-assisted reflection system named
ReflecToMeet
that integrates personalized reflective prompts with shared awareness of teammates’ reflections. Our five-day study with three collaborative groups in different settings showed that
ReflecToMeet
improved several aspects of collaborative preparedness: it helped participants stay engaged, reduced drift, and turned reflection into small, manageable checkpoints. We also present several design implications based on these findings. Overall, our work shows how AI-guided reflection can support collaboration in a way that feels helpful rather than intrusive. By extending reflection-on-action into the intervals between meetings,
ReflecToMeet
helps groups remain engaged and better prepared for future collaboration.
