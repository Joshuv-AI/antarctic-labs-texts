On Formulas and Fractional Exponents for Umbral Operators
Kei Beauduin

Abstract
This study presents a new formula for umbral operators which provides three key insights. First, it clarifies a connection between umbral calculus and iteration theory. Second, it paves the way for a definition of fractional exponents of umbral operators. And lastly, its proof synthesizes a multitude of existing operational calculus results that demonstrates a new level of effectiveness in the field. We demonstrate its application through a new and natural extension of the Laguerre polynomials.

There has been some notable research on

In

2
Operational calculus preliminaries

Let
\Hom(V,W)
denote the set of
\mathbb{C}
-linear maps between two vector spaces
V
and
W
, and
\End(V):=\Hom(V,V)
be the set of endomorphism over

respectively denote the set of polynomials and formal power series in the variable

2.1
Pincherle derivative

The
Pincherle derivative

U^{\prime}

[
27
,
29
,
1
]
of an operator
U
is defined as

U^{\prime}:=U\text{x}-\text{x}U.

(2)

The Pincherle derivative is
\mathbb{C}\left\llbracket\text{x}\right\rrbracket
-linear and, for any power series
g
,
g(D)^{\prime}=g^{\prime}(D)
. In other words, for shift-invariant operators, the Pincherle derivative acts like the usual derivative, but with respect to
D
.
Equation

2
extends to the
n
-th Pincherle derivative as follows
[
27
]
:

U^{(n)}=\sum_{k=0}^{n}\binom{n}{k}(-\text{x})^{n-k}U\text{x}^{k},

(3)

which is readily shown through induction, and which can be inverted to the equation

U\text{x}^{n}=\sum_{k=0}^{n}\binom{n}{k}\text{x}^{n-k}U^{(k)}.

(4)

2.2
Generalized exponentiation

We will employ the
generalized exponentiation
operation for operators
U,V
, introduced in
[
3
]
and defined as

U^{V}:=\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}(U-1)^{n}\binom{V}{n},

(5)

where
\binom{x}{n}:=\frac{(x)_{n}}{n!}
represents the
generalized binomial coefficient
, and
(x)_{n}:=x(x-1)\cdots(x-n+1)
denotes the
falling factorial
. It’s important to note that in general

U^{V}\neq\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\binom{V}{n}(U-1)^{n},

(6)

as operators do not always commute. In
[
3
, Prop. 2.2]
the following identity for exponentiation was established:

\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\frac{1}{n!}U^{n}V^{n}=(e^{U})^{V}.

(7)

Again, note that
(e^{U})^{V}=e^{UV}
only when
U
and
V
commute. This identity enables us to express, in a compact form, a type of series that arises frequently in operational calculus.

2.3
Generating function of an operator

For any operator
U
, we associate a sequence of polynomials
(U_{n}(x))_{n\geq 0}
defined by
U_{n}(x):=Ux^{n}
. Their generating function
\G_{U}
is given by

\G_{U}(x,t):=\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\frac{U_{n}(x)}{n!}t^{n}.

(8)

A sequence of polynomial
(p_{n}(x))_{n\geq 0}
for which the degree of
p_{n}(x)
is
n
, for all nonnegative integers
n
, is called a
polynomial sequence
denoted
\{p_{n}(x)\}_{n\geq 0}
. For these sequences, it is possible to associate an invertible operator
p
such that
px^{n}=p_{n}(x)
.

2.4
Umbral calculus

Let
f
be a compositionally invertible formal power series, meaning
f(0)=0
and
f^{\prime}(0)\neq 0
. It can be expressed as

f(t)=\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\frac{t^{n}}{n!}a_{n},\quad a_{1}\neq 0.

(9)

Such functions enable the definition of a sequence of polynomials
(\phi_{n}(x))_{n\geq 0}
via a generating function:

e^{xf(t)}=\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\frac{t^{n}}{n!}\phi_{n}(x).

(10)

They are characterized by the properties that
\{\phi_{n}(x)\}_{n\geq 0}
is a polynomial sequence,
\phi_{0}(x)=1
,
\phi_{n}(0)=0
for
n>0
and the existence of a
delta operator

Q
such that for all
n>0
,
Q\phi_{n}(x)=n\phi_{n-1}(x)
.
Q
is, in fact, unique and satisfy

Q=f^{-1}(D).

(11)

The associated operator
\phi
is called an
umbral operator
. Since it evidently satisfies
\G_{\phi}(x,t)=e^{xf(t)}
, we say that
\phi
is generated by
f
.

3.3
Pincherle’s contributions

The expansion theorem provides a formula for expanding shift-invariant operators in terms of the derivative
D
. When considering non shift-invariant operators, given that the multiplication operator
x
is the simplest of them, an inquiry arises as to whether an analogous expansion in terms of
x
and
D
exists, and, if so, the specific formulation of such an expansion. These questions were, in fact, addressed by Pincherle in 1895
[
27
]
(see also
[
26
, Chap. II.II]
and
[
28
, Chap. 6]
). His demonstration relies on the following theorem, which extends the Leibniz rule to arbitrary operators using the Pincherle derivative.

Theorem 3.1

(Pincherle’s formula)
.

If
U
is an operator and
p
is a formal power series, then

Up(\text{x})=\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\frac{p^{(k)}(\text{x})U^{(k)}}{k!}.

(20)

The general Leibniz rule corresponds to the case
U=D^{n}
. This case extends easily to operators
U
of the form
q(D)
, a fact known since 1848
[
20
]
, but
equation

20
exhibits a strikingly broader level of generality.

Proof
.

Let
(a_{n})_{n\geq 0}
be the coefficients of
p
and
q
be a polynomial. Then, using
equation

4
, we compute

\displaystyle Up(\text{x})q(x)=\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}a_{n}Ux^{n}q(x)=\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}a_{n}\sum_{k=0}^{n}\binom{n}{k}\text{x}^{n-k}U^{(k)}q(x)

\displaystyle={}

\displaystyle\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\frac{1}{k!}\left\lparen\sum_{n=k}^{\infty}a_{n}(n)_{k}\text{x}^{n-k}\right\rparen U^{(k)}q(x)=\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\frac{p^{(k)}(\text{x})}{k!}U^{(k)}q(x).

Corollary 3.1

(Pincherle)
.

If
U
is an operator, then

U=\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\left\lparen\sum_{j=0}^{k}\binom{k}{j}(-\text{x})^{k-j}U_{j}(\text{x})\right\rparen\frac{D^{k}}{k!}.

(21)

Remark 2
.

This formula, also due to Pincherle
[

]
, establishes that

and

\End(\mathbb{C}[x])=\mathbb{C}[\text{x}]\left\llbracket D\right\rrbracket.

An analogous result holds for our set of operators:

\Hom(\mathbb{C}[x],\mathbb{C}\left\llbracket x\right\rrbracket)=\mathbb{C}\left\llbracket\text{x}\right\rrbracket\left\llbracket D\right\rrbracket.

(23)

Since
[D,\text{x}]=1

and

represents a completion of the Weyl algebra, and indeed the largest one we can consider. It is important to note, however, that not all such operators can be extended to

4
A new expression for umbral operators

We now aim to express umbral operators in their simplest possible form:

\phi=e^{\text{x}V(D)}=1+\text{x}V(D)+\frac{1}{2}(\text{x}V(D))^{2}+\ldots.

(30)

The existence of such a representation was anticipated by Zeilberger
[
32
, §4.2]
. Although his argument was not fully rigorous, it can be adapted to provide a proof of the existence of
V
.

Proposition 4.1
.

There exists a formal power series
V
such that
V(0)=0
and
\phi=e^{\text{x}V(D)}
.

Proof
.

By
Corollary

3.1
, there exists a sequence of formal power series
(p_{k}(x))_{k\geq 0}
such that

L:=\log\phi=\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}p_{k}(\text{x})D^{k}.

Our goal is to show that
p_{0}(x)=0
and that for
k>0
,
p_{k}(x)
is linear. Operationally, the binomial-type property reads
\phi_{x+y}=\phi_{x}\phi_{y}
in
\mathbb{C}[x+y]

[
1
, Prop. 2.3]
, where the subscript indicates the variable on which
\phi
acts. Since
L_{x}
and
L_{y}
commute, translating this identity to

yields

e^{L_{x+y}}=e^{L_{x}}e^{L_{y}}=e^{L_{x}+L_{y}},

and therefore
L_{x+y}=L_{x}+L_{y}
. We now apply this identity to the monomial
(x+y)^{n}
for
n\geq 0
:

\sum_{k=0}^{n}(p_{k}(x+y)-p_{k}(x)-p_{k}(y))\frac{n!}{(n-k)!}(x+y)^{n-k}=0.

By induction, we conclude that for every nonnegative
k
,
p_{k}(x+y)=p_{k}(x)+p_{k}(y)
, which implies the existence of a sequence
(a_{k})_{k\geq 0}
of complex numbers such that
p_{k}(x)=a_{k}x
. Defining
V(t)=\sum_{k\geq 0}a_{k}t^{k}
, we obtain
\phi=e^{\text{x}V(D)}
. Finally, for
\phi x^{n}
to remain a polynomial, we must have
a_{0}=0
, or equivalently
V(0)=0
.

Proposition

4.1

4.1
The

In

Theorem 4.1

There exists a transform

•

•

For all operators

\mathfrak{L}(UV)=\mathfrak{L}(V)\mathfrak{L}(U)

•

\mathfrak{L}(D)=\text{x}

In particular,
\mathfrak{L}^{-1}=\mathfrak{L}
, and for any power series

This transform enables the conversion of operator identities into forms that are otherwise difficult to prove.

This result follows from

\displaystyle\mathfrak{L}(C_{f})

\displaystyle=\mathfrak{L}((e^{f(\text{x})-\text{x}})^{D})=\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\frac{1}{k!}\mathfrak{L}(D^{k})\mathfrak{L}((f(\text{x})-\text{x})^{k})

\displaystyle=\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\frac{1}{k!}\text{x}^{k}(f(D)-D)^{k}=(e^{\text{x}})^{f(D)-D}=\phi.

4.2
Iteration theory and proof of the new formula

The notion of
fractional iteration
, i.e., non-integral compositional iterates of a formal power series can be defined uniquely (see
[
22
,
15
,
16
]
) for a power series
g
such that
g^{1}:=g
and for all
r,s\in\mathbb{C}
,
g^{r}\circ g^{s}=g^{r+s}
. This is the main object of study in
iteration theory
. The
iterative logarithm

\itlog
is the natural analog of the ordinary logarithm in the context where the composition serves as the multiplication. For example, it satisfies
\itlog(g^{s})=s\itlog(g)
and is defined by the equation

\itlog(g):=\left.\frac{\mathrm{d}g^{s}}{\mathrm{d}s}\right|_{s=0}.

(32)

Bourlet was the first to apply the composition operator to the iteration theory
[
6
]
. Écalle later reintroduced it and showed the following formula
[
14
,
17
,
15
,
16
]
.

Proposition 4.2
.

For an invertible formal power series
g
and
s\in\mathbb{C}

C_{g^{s}}=e^{s\itlog(g)(\text{x})D}.

(33)

We follow Labelle’s proof
[
22
]
.

Proof
.

For some power series
p
, first we compute via the chain rule

\begin{gathered}\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}s}p(g^{s}(x))=\left.\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}s}p(g^{s+r}(x))\right|_{r=0}=\left.\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}r}p(g^{s+r}(x))\right|_{r=0}=\left.\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}r}p(g^{s}(g^{r}(x)))\right|_{r=0}\\
=\left.\frac{\mathrm{d}g^{r}(x)}{\mathrm{d}r}\right|_{r=0}\frac{\mathrm{d}p(g^{s}(x))}{\mathrm{d}x}=\itlog(g)(x)\frac{\mathrm{d}p(g^{s}(x))}{\mathrm{d}x}=\itlog(g)(\text{x})Dp(g^{s}(x)).\end{gathered}

This constitutes a differential equation in
s
with solution

p(g^{s}(x))=e^{s\itlog(g)(\text{x})D}p(x),

hence
equation

33
.

This directly leads us to our main result.

Formula 6
.

\phi=e^{\text{x}\itlog(f)(D)}.

(34)

Proof
.

We simply combine the
Lemma

4.1
with
Proposition

4.2
:

\phi=\mathfrak{L}(C_{f})=\mathfrak{L}(e^{\itlog(f)(\text{x})D})=e^{\text{x}\itlog(f)(D)}.

Example 4.1
.

If
f(t)=\lambda t
, then
f^{s}(t)=\lambda^{s}t
and

\itlog(f)(t)=\left.\frac{\mathrm{d}\lambda^{s}t}{\mathrm{d}s}\right|_{s=0}=\log(\lambda)t.

Therefore, with
6
, we find once again that
\mathcal{S}^{\left\lparen\lambda\right\rparen}=\lambda^{\text{x}D}
.

4.3
Converse of the theorem

We have expressed
V
in terms of
f
, but the reverse is also possible: given some
V
, we can find
f
such that
e^{\text{x}V}
is an umbral operator associated to
f
.

Our first ingredient is the well-known operational form of Taylor’s theorem due to Lagrange in 1772
[
23
]
. For a polynomial
p
, If
E^{a}p(x):=p(x+a)
, then

E^{a}=e^{aD}.

(35)

This identity was extended by Reverend Graves in 1850
[
19
]
.

Proposition 4.3
.

For a formal power series
V
, if
h
is a function such that
h^{\prime}(x)=1/V(t)
,
g(t):=h^{-1}(h(t)+1)
is a formal power series satisfying
g^{s}(t)=h^{-1}(h(t)+s)
, for all
s\in\mathbb{C}
, and
\itlog(g)=V
. In addition,

e^{sV(\text{x})D}=C_{g^{s}}.

(36)

Combining
Propositions

3.1
and
4.3
gives
e^{V(\text{x})D}=(e^{g(\text{x})-\text{x}})^{D}
, a result can also be found through a theorem due to Viskov
[
31
]
(see
[
3
, Cor. 5.2]
).

Proof
.

The functions
G(s,x):=h^{-1}(h(x)+s)
satisfy the functional equation
G(s,G(r,x))=G(s+r,x)
. Moreover,
\left.\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}s}G(s,x)\right|_{s=0}=V(x)
, so with similar argument as in the proof of
Proposition

4.3
, we find that
G(s,x)=e^{sV(\text{x})D}x
. Because
V
is a formal power series, so is
g(x)=G(1,x)
. By uniqueness of the fractional iterates of the formal power series
g
, it follows that
g^{s}(x)=G(s,x)
and therefore
\itlog(g)=V
.

Let
p
be a polynomial and
P(x)=p(h^{-1}(x))
, then by the chain rule
D_{x}=h^{\prime}(\text{x})D_{h(x)}
and
equation

35

\displaystyle e^{v(\text{x})Dt}p(x)

\displaystyle=e^{sv(\text{x})h^{\prime}(\text{x})D_{h}}P(h)=E_{h}^{s}P(h)=P(h+s)

\displaystyle=p(h^{-1}(h(x)+s))=C_{g^{s}}p(x).

Corollary 4.1
.

For
V
a shift-invariant operator, if
h
is a function such that
h^{\prime}(t)=1/V(t)
, then for all
s\in\mathbb{C}

e^{s\text{x}V(D)}=(e^{\text{x}})^{h^{-1}(h(D)+s)-D}.

In particular, if
V(0)=0
,
e^{s\text{x}V(D)}
is the umbral operator generated by
g^{s}(t):=h^{-1}(h(t)+s)
.

Proof
.

We combine
Propositions

3.1
and
4.3
and apply
\mathfrak{L}
:

\displaystyle\mathfrak{L}(e^{sV(\text{x})D})

\displaystyle=\mathfrak{L}((e^{h^{-1}(h(\text{x})+s)-\text{x}})^{D}),

\displaystyle e^{s\mathfrak{L}(V(\text{x})D)}

\displaystyle=(e^{\mathfrak{L}(D)})^{\mathfrak{L}(h^{-1}(h(\text{x})+s)-\text{x})},

\displaystyle e^{s\text{x}V(D)}

\displaystyle=(e^{\text{x}})^{h^{-1}(h(D)+s)-D}.

If
V(0)=0
,
e^{s\text{x}v(D)}
is an endomorphism over the polynomials and since
e^{s\text{x}V(D)}=\mathfrak{L}(C_{g^{s}})
, by
lemma

4.1
, it is an umbral operator generated by
g^{s}(t)
.

5
Fractional exponents of umbral operators

In light of
6
, there is a straightforward way of defining the fractional exponent of an umbral operator:

\phi^{s}:=e^{s\text{x}\itlog(f)(D)}.

(37)

Since
s\itlog(f)=\itlog(f^{s})
,
\phi^{s}
is generated by the formal power series
f^{s}
. This is precisely the definition adopted in
[
2
]
. It stands in contrast with an alternative definition based on the binomial series, which carries convergence issues.

In
[
2
]
we derived several formulas for the coefficients of
f^{s}(t)^{k}
, which in turn give the coefficients of
\phi^{s}x^{n}=:\phi^{s}_{n}(x)
. Among other formulas, letting
q:=f^{\prime}(0)
, the coefficient
\genfrac{[}{]}{0.0pt}{}{n}{k}_{\phi^{s}}
of
x^{k}
in the polynomial
\phi^{s}_{n}(x)
is given by
[
2
, Thm. 7]

\genfrac{[}{]}{0.0pt}{}{n}{k}_{\phi^{s}}=\sum_{p=0}^{n-k}\genfrac{[}{]}{0.0pt}{}{n}{k}_{\phi^{p}}\binom{s}{p}_{\mkern-7.0muq}\binom{n-k-s}{n-k-p}_{\mkern-7.0muq}q^{(n-p)(s-p)},

(38)

where we have used the
q
-binomial coefficient
. There, we also derived an expansion for the iterative logarithm of
f
. Its coefficients are given by
\left.\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}s}\genfrac{[}{]}{0.0pt}{}{n}{1}_{\phi^{s}}\right|_{s=0}
, which can be interpreted as the coefficient
\genfrac{[}{]}{0.0pt}{}{n}{1}_{\log\phi}
, since
\log\phi=\text{x}\itlog(f)(D)
. This logarithm corresponds to the infinitesimal generator of the one-parameter group
(\phi^{s})_{s\in\mathbb{C}}
, which satisfies

\phi^{s+t}=\phi^{s}\phi^{t}.

(39)

In addition, the delta operator
Q^{[s]}:=f^{-s}(D)
associated with
\phi^{s}
satisfies

Q^{[t]}\phi^{s}=\phi^{s}Q^{[t-s]},

(40)

for all
s,t\in\mathbb{C}
, which extends
[
1
, Prop. 2.8]
. Furthermore,
(Q^{[s]})_{s\in\mathbb{C}}
itself forms a one-parameter group under the law

Q^{[s+t]}=Q^{[s]}\diamond Q^{[t]}:=(f^{-s}\circ f^{-t})(D),

(41)

with infinitesimal generator
-\itlog(f)(D)
. Thus, the preceding results provide a complete answer to Rota’s third problem
[
29
, Sec. 14]
, now in the broader case where
f^{\prime}(0)
need not be
1
.

6
Example: degenerate Laguerre polynomials

We will follow the construction of
Corollary

4.1
with
V(t)=-t^{p+1}
, for some integer
p\geq 0
. Let
L_{p}:=e^{-\text{x}D^{p+1}}
. According to
Example

3.1
,
L_{0}=\mathcal{S}^{\left\lparen 1/e\right\rparen}
, so from now on we only consider the cases
p>0
. Let

h(t):=\int_{-\infty}^{t}\frac{\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}x}{V(x)}=\frac{1}{pt^{p}},

so that

f^{s}(t):=h^{-1}(h(t)+s)=\sqrt[-p]{p\left\lparen\frac{1}{pt^{p}}+s\right\rparen}=\sqrt[-p]{\frac{1+spt^{p}}{t^{p}}}=\frac{t}{\sqrt[p]{1+spt^{p}}}.

(42)

The delta operator is therefore given by
\Psi_{p}:=f^{-1}(D)=D/\sqrt[p]{1-pD^{p}}
. The case
p=1
is associated with the
Laguerre polynomials

[
1
, Sec. 6.5]
.

We will refer
L_{p,n}(x):=(L_{p})_{n}(x)
as the
degenerate Laguerre polynomials
and define their
associated
version by
L^{\left\lparen\alpha\right\rparen}_{p}:=(1-pD^{p})^{\frac{\alpha}{p}}L_{p}
. They are an example of a
cross-sequence
, so according to
[
1
, Prop. 3.6]
, for all
\alpha,\beta
.

L^{\left\lparen\alpha+\beta\right\rparen}_{p,n}(x+y)=\sum_{k=0}^{n}\binom{n}{k}L^{\left\lparen\alpha\right\rparen}_{p,k}(x)L^{\left\lparen\beta\right\rparen}_{p,n-k}(y),

(43)

and their generating function is given by
[
1
, Thm. 4.2]

\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}L^{\left\lparen\alpha\right\rparen}_{p,n}(x)\frac{t^{n}}{n!}=\frac{1}{(\sqrt[p]{1+pt^{p}})^{\alpha}}\exp\left\lparen\frac{xt}{\sqrt[p]{1+pt^{p}}}\right\rparen.

(44)

\Psi_{p}^{\prime}=(1-pD^{p})^{-\frac{1}{p}-1}
, thus, with the transfer formula
[
1
, Thm. 3.4]
we have

L_{p,n}(x)=\Psi_{p}^{\prime}(\sqrt[p]{1-pD^{p}})^{n+1}x^{n}=(1-pD^{p})^{\frac{n}{p}-1}x^{n},

therefore

\displaystyle L^{\left\lparen\alpha\right\rparen}_{p,n}(x)

\displaystyle=(1-pD^{p})^{\frac{n+\alpha}{p}-1}x^{n}=\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\binom{\frac{n+\alpha}{p}-1}{k}(-pD^{p})^{k}x^{n}

\displaystyle=\sum_{k=0}^{\left\lfloor n/p\right\rfloor}\binom{\frac{n+\alpha}{p}-1}{k}\frac{n!(-p)^{k}}{(n-pk)!}x^{n-pk}.

(45)

The following identity holds:

L_{p}^{(\alpha)}=\exp(-\text{x}D^{p+1}-\alpha D^{p}).

(46)

The case
p=1
and
\alpha=1
, corresponding to a different convention for the Laguerre polynomials, was discovered by Dattoli and Torre
[
11
]
(see also
[
12
,
10
]
). Dattoli and Migliorati later extended this result to arbitrary
\alpha

[
9
, eq. (33) with
f(x)=x^{n}
and
y=1
]
.
Equation

46
then follows quickly from the
Berry identity

[
4
]
: since the
commutator

[D^{p},\text{x}D^{p+1}]=p(D^{p})^{2}
, it is a consequence of
[
3
, Cor. 3.4 and Prop. 2.4]
that

\exp(-\text{x}D^{p+1}-\alpha D^{p})=((1-pD^{p})^{1/p})^{\alpha}e^{-\text{x}D^{p+1}}=L^{\left\lparen\alpha\right\rparen}_{p}.

The degenerate associated Laguerre polynomials
L^{\left\lparen\alpha\right\rparen}_{p,n}(x)
satisfy the following differential equation

xpF^{(p+1)}+\alpha pF^{(p)}-xF^{\prime}+nF=0,

(47)

which follows from a computation similar to that of
[
1
, eq. (59)]
.

The Laguerre polynomials also serve as a good example for real exponents. Using the expression for
f^{s}(t)
from
equation

42
, we have
\Psi_{p}^{[s]}=D/\sqrt[p]{1-spD^{p}}
, and a similar calculation gives:

L^{s}_{p,n}(x)=\sum_{k=0}^{\left\lfloor n/p\right\rfloor}\binom{\frac{n}{p}-1}{k}\frac{n!(-sp)^{k}}{(n-pk)!}x^{n-pk}.

(48)

For
p=1
, this formula essentially reduces to the bivariate Laguerre polynomials of Dattoli and Torre
[
11
]
, where
s
is the second variable.
