Post-Craniotomy Headache: Current Concepts and Emerging Procedural Therapies
Authors: Sarah A. Friedman, Arathi Nandyala
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Purpose of ReviewPost-craniotomy headache (PCH) is a common complication following neurosurgery, yet there remains limited consensus regarding the mechanism, classification, and optimal management. This review summarizes the current literature and highlights recent trends in procedural therapies.Recent FindingsPCH occurs in approximately 60–70% of patients following craniotomy, with nearly 25% progression to persistent PCH. Identified risk factors include younger age, female sex, pre-existing headache disorders, and longer operative duration. Some studies further characterize PCH into migrainous, tension-type, cervicogenic, or neuropathic phenotypes, however classification remains inconsistent across the literature. Pharmacological treatment largely extrapolates from migraine and neuropathic pain management, though emerging evidence in the last few years supports the potential role of onabotulinumtoxinA and nerve blocks.SummaryCurrent evidence supports the use of pharmacological, nonpharmacological, and procedural interventions to treat PCH. Future research should prioritize a phenotype-driven approach, evaluation of standardized multi-modal protocols, as well as novel therapies, including calcitonin gene-related peptide (CGRP)-targeted agents.

Introduction

Headache onset following a craniotomy is a well-described post-surgical complication, initially recognized as a consequence of vestibular schwannoma resection in the 1980s [1]. A majority of these headaches resolve within one month, however a subset persists. The International Classification of Headache Disorders, 3rd Edition (ICHD-3), recognizes two distinct diagnoses: acute headache attributed to craniotomy and persistent headache attributed to craniotomy. The acute phenotype is defined as a headache of less than 3 months duration (whether it resolved within 3 months or 3 months have not passed since its onset), while the persistent phenotype is diagnosed if the headache has lasted greater than 3 months. Both diagnoses require that a surgical craniotomy has been performed, the headache onset occurred within 7 days of (1) the craniotomy, (2) regaining consciousness following the craniotomy, or (3) the discontinuation of medications impairing ability to accurately sense or report symptoms following the craniotomy, as well as not better accounted for by another ICHD-3 diagnosis [2]. About 1 in 4 patients with a diagnosis of acute headache attributed to craniotomy experience ongoing headache that later meets criteria for persistent headache attributed to craniotomy [2]. Recommendations have also been made to consider extending the onset to greater than 7 days, as there is a population of patients who develop a delayed post-craniotomy headache (PCH) [3].

Literature and clinical expertise suggest that PCH can have varying phenotypes including migrainous, tension-type, neuralgiform, and cervicogenic, though there are no formal recommendations to designate as such [4–7]. Recognition, evaluation, and appropriate management of this disorder is crucial as PCH can be incapacitating and interfere with daily functioning and mood [8]. Management of persistent PCH has historically relied on neuropathic pain agents. However, emerging case reports and case series have shown encouraging responses to various protocols using onabotulinumtoxinA (BoNT/A) and nerve blocks, which will be highlighted in this review.

Methods

This is a narrative review of literature focused on publications from 2021 to 2026, while incorporating seminal earlier studies on epidemiology, pathophysiology, and management. It includes case reports, case series, systematic reviews, and other literature concerning acute and persistent pain management after craniotomy. The data were collected by doing a search of PubMed and a manual search of all pertinent references in the literature. The keywords used were post-craniotomy headache, persistent post-craniotomy headache, and post-craniotomy pain.

Epidemiology

Early descriptions of PCH were primarily in patients who underwent acoustic neuroma, now termed vestibular schwannoma, resection [1]. With increasing awareness of PCH, the reported incidence of PCH has varied as definitions and criteria have changed. Recent reports based on current ICHD-3 criteria found the incidence of PCH to be approximately 60–70% of patients undergoing craniotomy [3, 9]. While nearly 70% of patients developed acute PCH, 34.4% went on to develop persistent PCH [9]. When stratified by surgical indication, 66% of patients who underwent microvascular decompression, 17% of patients who underwent tumor resection, and 50% of patients who underwent suboccipital craniectomy developed PCH [10]. Risk factors for the development of PCH included younger age, female sex, preoperative headaches, microvascular decompression surgery, facial spasms, muscle dissection, neural damage, patient positioning, surgery lasting greater than 4 h, and craniectomy rather than craniotomy or cranioplasty [9–12].

Etiologies

There have been a multitude of theorized etiologies of PCH. The leading hypothesis is that the development of adhesions between the dura and subcutaneous tissue leads to tension on the dura, a pain-sensitive structure [8]. Other implicated structures include scalp muscles, soft tissue, the periosteum of the skull, and trigeminal nerves. Irritation of these structures may be due to direct mechanical irritation or aseptic inflammation [8, 13]. Some PCH may be caused more directly by nerve entrapment and neuroma formation within the craniotomy scar itself [14]. A recent body of literature suggests that pneumocephalus may be an underrecognized contributor to PCH as well [15–16]. While most hypotheses suggest a peripheral etiology of PCH, direct injury to the brain due to an exaggerated stress response or central sensitization due to higher cortical dysfunction may contribute particularly to the transformation from acute to persistent PCH [17–18]. Psychological contributors (e.g., premorbid anxiety), sleep dysfunction, and pre-existing migraine biology have also been implicated in the development of persistent PCH [8, 11].

Phenotype

PCH is a heterogeneous category and can be present in various phenotypes including migrainous, tension-type, neuropathic, cervicogenic, CSF-pressure related, and a mixed phenotype. This further classification can be clinically useful when considering the suspected mechanism and management options (Table 1). For example, craniotomy could act as a trigger in patients with a genetic predisposition to migraine and the mechanism may similarly be trigeminovascular activation. This would lend itself to the use of classic migraine preventatives and BoNT/A. Tension-type headache (TTH) remains poorly understood but the same acute and preventative agents utilized for TTH can be employed for the tension-type phenotype of PCH including non-steroidal anti-inflammatory drugs (NSAIDs), tricyclic antidepressants (TCA), topiramate, mirtazapine, and physical therapy [4–5, 8]. Many cases of PCH are neuralgiform in nature due to mechanisms described earlier in this review and these patients may preferentially benefit from gabapentinoids and consideration of nerve blocks. Cervicogenic PCH may be related to patient positioning during surgery (particularly in cases lasting more than 5 h and in those with pre-existing cervical disease) causing irritation of peripheral nerves and muscle tissue or direct transection of these structures based on surgical approach. Physical therapy may be considered depending on the postoperative safety of cervical manipulation. Cervical nerve blocks have also been shown to be of use in these cases [7, 19]. Cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) leaks are a known complication of neurosurgical procedures and can also lead to headache following craniotomy, however these should be thought of and treated differently than PCH. If a patient experiences a clearly positional headache that resolves (or nearly resolves) with lying flat and worsens when standing, this should raise suspicion for a CSF leak or intracranial hypotension and be treated as such with caffeine, fluids, and empiric blood patch if needed [20].

Treatment for Post-Craniotomy Headache

Acute PCH Treatment

Pharmacological Treatment

Pharmacological treatment has long been the mainstay of treatment for PCH. Acute inpatient management tends to rely on acetaminophen, NSAIDs with a focus on cyclooxygenase (COX)-2 inhibition, combination acetaminophen-butalbital-caffeine, and opioids [21]. Acetaminophen and NSAIDs are generally effective at reducing pain in the acute period [22]. When opioids are indicated, tramadol is the favored agent [23]. Opioids and barbiturates should be confined to the acute setting and avoided in the long term given lack of established efficacy and risk of dependence. Two studies evaluated the effect of gabapentinoids (gabapentin and pregabalin) on acute pain at 24 and 48 h and found that pre-administration improved pain outcomes in the acute setting [22]. Other adjunct pharmacological agents have been studied including sumatriptan and ketamine with some support for their use [24–25].

Intraoperative Nerve Blocks

Scalp nerve blocks performed during the operative case have been shown to decrease the need for post-operative analgesia and pain at 6 to 24 h. These blocks utilize either bupivacaine, lidocaine, ropivacaine, or levobupivacaine and are performed along the incisional line or throughout the scalp flap [21–22]. While there are no reports of lidocaine associated systemic toxicity (LAST) with intraoperative nerve blocks for this indication, injection of local anesthetics near procedural sites and in vascular regions is a risk factor for the development of LAST and can lead to central nervous system and cardiovascular toxicity [26].

Persistent PCH Treatment

Pharmacological Treatment

The treatment of persistent PCH has borrowed pharmacologic methods from other headache disorders including migraine, TTH, and neuralgias. However, all reports of pharmacologic treatment for persistent PCH are anecdotal as there is a lack of clinical trials focusing on PCH. Treatment for the chronic form of PCH has included TCAs, verapamil, and antiseizure medications including valproic acid, lamotrigine, topiramate, gabapentin, and carbamazepine [8, 11, 27–28]. Memantine could also be considered for long-term therapy given the use of other N-methyl-D-aspartate (NMDA) receptor-based therapies (ketamine and dextromethorphan) for acute treatment [25, 29]. Notably the newest class of headache medication, the calcitonin gene-related peptide (CGRP) antagonists, have not been studied for PCH. Their use in this disorder is promising as they have changed the landscape of headache medicine and there is evidence of increased CGRP following craniotomy in animal models [30]

OnabotulinumtoxinA

The majority of recent literature has focused on the use of BoNT/A for PCH. The mechanism of BoNT/A treatment for headache is by preventing peripheral nerves from releasing pain-inducing neuropeptides including CGRP, pituitary adenylate cyclase-activating polypeptide (PACAP), and glutamate [31].

Published case reports and case series used variable BoNT/A protocols and the patient cohorts were heterogenous in terms of surgical procedure and headache presentation (Table 2). The majority of protocols utilized peri-incisional injections, while one group explored the use of additional BoNT/A along irritated cranial suture lines. Irritated cranial suture lines were determined based on the prior surgical procedure and discomfort experienced on physical examination [32].

The dose of BoNT/A varied significantly across cases with a range of 10–200 units (U), though most reported doses were between 50 and 100 U [32–37]. The majority of patients received multiple cycles of BoNT/A (though many at intervals greater than the standard 12 weeks) with the longest duration of 9 years of treatment [35]. There were no reported adverse events, though potential risks of BoNT/A include localized pain, infection, muscle weakness, and cosmetic asymmetry. A subset of patients had comorbid chronic migraine and received BoNT/A treatment for migraine either previously or concurrently [35–36]. Outcomes were overwhelmingly positive with all patients experiencing improvement in at least one of the following domains: overall pain, intensity, pain-free days, headache frequency, or quality of life. Ultimately 6 of 21 patients (28.6%) in these six reports achieved pain freedom (Table 2) [32–37].

There are further reports of the use of BoNT/A in post-traumatic headache, which may provide additional support for its use in PCH. A study of 40 patients, all of which were military veterans, showed improvement in weekly headache days at 16 weeks compared to controls treated with a saline protocol. Those treated with BoNT/A showed a decrease of 2.24 headache days per week compared to 1.28 in the control group [38].

The mechanistic overlap between PCH and migraine suggests that those with a migrainous phenotype may be best primed for BoNT/A treatment, however the majority of published literature does not clearly delineate PCH phenotype. Further, the lack of standardized injection paradigms and small sample sizes limits conclusions and highlights the need for prospective phenotype-driven studies.

Cervical Nerve Blocks

Two studies evaluated the effect of cervical nerve blocks in patients with a cervicogenic phenotype of PCH. Huang et al. performed cervical nerve blocks in 39 patients who had a suboptimal response to medical management. Their pain scores improved from 7.49 to 2.0. An additional 29 patients underwent a cervical nerve block without having received prior medical management and their pain improved from 9.0 to 1.0. There were no reported adverse effects from the procedure [19]. Meng et al. performed cervical nerve blocks (with 2% lidocaine or 1% bupivacaine) and found that 31 of 32 patients experienced significant pain improvement. They performed these as diagnostic nerve blocks to differentiate between cervicogenic headache due to positioning during surgery and PCH [7]. Neither study explicitly stated which cervical nerves were targeted.

Nonpharmacological Treatment

There is evidence to support the use of nonpharmacological treatments in the management of PCH. These include transcutaneous electrical nerve stimulation (TENS), acupuncture, radiofrequency, cryoablations, and physical therapy [6]. Additionally, a study of inhaled peppermint oil showed a reduction in pain scores at 48 h when compared to scalp nerve blocks, though the combination of the two showed the most benefit [39].

Discussion

The development of PCH is a common adverse event following neurosurgical procedures and there is limited research on the most effective approach to treatment, particularly for the persistent form of PCH. There is a role for both pharmacological treatments (particularly TCAs, gabapentinoids, and NSAIDs), nonpharmacological treatments, and recent studies additionally support the use of BoNT/A and nerve blocks [6–8, 11, 19, 27–28, 32–37]. We suggest that clinician choice of treatment strategies may be optimized by treating based on headache phenotype.

There are limitations to this review. There is a predominance of retrospective studies with small sample sizes, varied surgical populations, inconsistent diagnostic definitions, and a lack of procedural protocols and standardized outcome measures. We propose that future studies should focus on phenotype-based protocols, a multimodal approach, and prospective trials and registries. It will be important to evaluate CGRP-based therapies for this disorder and the development of standardized BoNT/A paradigms.

Conclusion

While the current evidence provides support for several treatment modalities of PCH, an optimal protocol has not been elucidated. Further prospective studies are needed to explore the use of CGRP-based therapies and standardized procedural paradigms.
