Epidemiological investigation and patterns of antimicrobial use in multidrug-resistant bacteria at a tertiary hospital: a retrospective cohort study
Authors: Man Wang, Ying Cao, Jian-Hong Zhang, Sheng-Nan Ma, Yu Wang, Ting Miao, Wei Xiao, Qiang Fu
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectiveTo analyse trends and characteristics of multidrug-resistant (MDR) bacteria over the past 7 years, in relation to patterns of antimicrobial use, to inform rational antimicrobial use and strengthen hospital infection control measures.DesignRetrospective cohort study.SettingA large teaching hospital in Tianjin, a major metropolitan city in northern China.ParticipantsA total of 190 352 inpatients aged >18 years, admitted between 1 January 2017 and 31 December 2023, were included. Patients were eligible if they had complete clinical data and met the five quality control indicators for multidrug-resistant bacteria (QC-MRB), defined in the ‘Hospital Infection Surveillance Specifications’ (WS/T 312–2023). Patients with MDR infections before admission or those with duplicate results from multiple specimens were excluded.ResultsStatistical analysis revealed a detection rate of 12.11% for the five QC-MRB and an incidence rate of 0.20%. The findings also indicated an upward trend in the detection and incidence rates of carbapenem-resistant Enterobacteriaceae, despite a relatively stable rate of antimicrobial use over 7 years. A positive correlation was observed between the incidence of MDR bacteria and the intensity of antimicrobial use within the hospital setting.ConclusionsThe hospital’s bacterial data align with national trends. It established an interdisciplinary management framework for clinical data analysis and prediction of antimicrobial resistance. This approach enhances infection control measures and supports the rational use of antimicrobials.

Introduction

Multidrug resistance (MDR) has become a major global public health concern,1 responsible for approximately 700 000 deaths annually. By 2050, this figure is projected to rise to 10 million deaths per year. In addition to the substantial loss of lives, MDR imposes significant economic burdens.2 The Infectious Diseases Society of America (IDSA) has identified MDR as one of the most urgent global health challenges.3

MDR infections are prevalent in hospital settings, posing severe clinical and therapeutic challenges. In China, the prevalence of bacterial MDR ranges from 30% to 50%.4 Furthermore, nearly 8% of hospitalised patients acquire nosocomial infections, and approximately 20% of these may be caused by MDR.5 6 Research shows that hospital-acquired infections caused by MDR microorganisms are associated with increased mortality, higher readmission rates, greater emergency department utilisation and healthcare costs compared with infections caused by susceptible strains.

This study aimed to analyse MDR cases over the past 7 years to elucidate their trends and characteristics, to inform rational antimicrobial use and strengthen hospital infection control measures.

Methods

Study design and setting

A retrospective analysis was conducted on a sample of 1 90 352 patients admitted to a tertiary Grade A general hospital in northern China between 1 January 2017 and 31 December 2023.

Study site and justification

The study was conducted at a large teaching hospital in Tianjin, a major city in northern China with a reported population of 13.64 million as of 2023. The hospital is affiliated with Tianjin Medical University, one of the leading medical universities in the region, and is situated within the municipal limits.

Sample size and sampling technique

This study employed a non-probability purposive sampling method to retrospectively select 190 352 clinical records that met the eligibility criteria and were available over the 7-year study period.

Study population and selection criteria

Data on patients with bacterial infections were retrospectively retrieved from the hospital’s laboratory information system (LIS). The dataset included demographic information, identified bacterial species, detection methods and antimicrobial sensitivity test results. Inclusion criteria were as follows: (1) patients aged >18 years, (2) patients with complete clinical data and (3) cases relevant to the five quality control indicators for multidrug-resistant bacteria (QC-MRB), as outlined in the ‘Hospital Infection Surveillance Specifications’ WS/T 312–2023. These indicators include carbapenem-resistant Enterobacteriaceae (CRE), with specific emphasis on carbapenem-resistant Klebsiella pneumoniae (CRKP) and carbapenem-resistant Escherichia coli (CREC); carbapenem-resistant Acinetobacter baumannii (CRAB); carbapenem-resistant Pseudomonas aeruginosa (CRPA); methicillin-resistant Staphylococcus aureus (MRSA) and vancomycin-resistant Enterococcus (VRE). Exclusion criteria included (1) patients with MDR infection before admission and (2) cases where only the first result of multiple examinations for the same patient was retained.

Data on antimicrobial usage rates and intensity were collected retrospectively via the hospital’s Pharmacy Information System. Additionally, information on nosocomial infection incidence and other related indicators was obtained from the hospital’s Infection Control Information System. All variables were compiled monthly to construct a comprehensive database.

Variables of interest

The primary outcome variables were CRE (with particular emphasis on CRKP and CREC), CRAB, CRPA, MRSA and VRE. Key predictor variables included demographic factors such as age, sex, specimen type, disease category and ward type.

Specimen collection, isolation and identification

The required data were retrospectively retrieved from medical records in the hospital’s LIS. These records were based on microbiological procedures for specimen collection and processing, which were conducted following established standard operating procedures.7 Antimicrobial susceptibility tests were performed according to the guidelines of the Clinical and Laboratory Standards Institute (CLSI).8

Multidrug resistance

Bacterial isolates were classified as MDR if they were resistant to at least three classes of first-line antimicrobial agents.9

Quality assurance, data processing and statistical analysis

Reliability of the study findings was ensured through adherence to standard microbiological practices, as guided by the American Society for Microbiology and CLSI protocols. For quality control, the following reference strains were used: S. aureus (ATCC 25923), E. coli (ATCC 25922), K. pneumoniae (ATCC 700603) and P. aeruginosa (ATCC 27853), provided by the Clinical Inspection Centre of the National Health and Wellness Commission.

Data were coded, entered and analysed using the SPSS software, V. 26 (SPSS Inc., Chicago, Illinois, USA). Descriptive statistics were presented as frequencies and percentages. Continuous data were summarised using standard descriptive statistics. Frequency (%) tables were generated for categorical variables. Observational indicators included the annual distribution of the five QC-MRB strains, annual trends in the number of infected patients, distribution of cases across hospital departments, detection rate, incidence rate and the rate and intensity of antimicrobial drug use.

Patient and public involvement

No patients were directly involved in this retrospective study. The analysis was based solely on secondary data obtained through routine microbiological procedures.

Results

Annual trend and distribution of MDR by department

From 1 January 2017 to 31 December 2023, 190 352 inpatient cases were recorded, of which 389 MDR infections occurred hospital-wide, and 2250 strains of MDR bacteria were detected across the five QC-MRB strains. A heat map was generated to show the distribution of detected MDR strains by year and clinical department. The number of CRE strains detected tended to increase over the years, with a slope of the linear regression line >1 (15.54). The slope of the linear regression line for the total number of isolates across all five QC-MRB strains was 5.96. Notably, no VRE strains were detected during the study period. The remaining strains exhibited either a stable or decreasing trend over time. For MRSA, the slope of the linear fits was 0.71; for CRAB, it was −1.5. A slope of −0.00 was also observed, likely indicating a flat trend. The slope for CRPA was −8.79. Figure 1B shows that hospital-acquired CRE cases increased annually, with a slope coefficient >1 (1.82). The remaining MDR cases demonstrated a decreasing trend. Figure 1C shows that the departments in which MDR strains were most frequently detected, in order, were intensive care, respiratory care, neurosurgery and emergency general medicine.

Detection rate of five QC-MRB

During the study period, 18 578 pathogenic bacteria were detected in the laboratory, of which 2250 (12.11%) were MDR strains. MRSA (13.33%–31.37%) and CRAB (24.73%–40.63%) had the highest detection rate, followed by CRPA (13.49%%–28.34%), CRE (0.47%%–7.08%) and VRE (0%). The overall detection rates of the five QC-MRBs were CRE (2.84%), MRSA (21.85%), VRE (0%), CRAB (35.33%) and CRPA (20.98%) (figure 2, left panel).

Incidence of five QC-MRB

A total of 1 90 352 patients were admitted during the study period. Among these, 389 patients (0.08%–0.61%) developed hospital-acquired MDR infections. The highest frequencies of resistant organisms were observed for MRSA (0.00%%–0.16%), CRAB (0.03%%–0.29%) and CRPA (0.01%%–0.15%), followed by VRE (0%) and CRE (0.00%–0.04%) (figure 2, right panel).

Discussion

In this 7-year study, the detection rate of drug-resistant bacteria in our hospital was 12.11%, and the incidence rate was 0.20%, both lower than those reported by the China Antimicrobial Resistance Surveillance System (CHINET). Among the identified drug-resistant bacteria, MRSA and CRAB exhibited the highest detection and incidence rates, consistent with findings from the 2023 CHINET report.

CRE incidence peaked in 2021 and declined thereafter. The incidences of MRSA, CRAB and CRPA also decreased over time, while VRE remained at 0% throughout the study period. These trends reflect the hospital’s response to national infection control policies and the implementation of targeted measures. A surveillance system was established, and key areas for MDR prevention and control were identified. Overall, the antimicrobial resistance situation in the hospital appears to be stable and gradually improving. Continued monitoring of CRE is essential, as it plays a central role in MDR control. CRE prevalence varies globally; it spreads primarily via contact and can result in either infection or colonisation.1016 A 2021 review reported a 43% attributable mortality rate for CRE infections.17

Hospitalised patients with carbapenemase-producing bacteria should be placed under contact precautions.18 Emphasis must be placed on infection control practices, including rigorous hand hygiene and prudent antibiotic use, to limit the spread of drug-resistant bacteria. In addition, endoscopes must be properly cleaned and sterilised, and structural upgrades to sinks and toilets should be undertaken to reduce environmental contamination risks.19 In departments with high CRE incidence, over-the-counter antibiotic use should be restricted, and the use of broad-spectrum antibiotics in livestock should be reduced. Wastewater should be separated from public water supplies to prevent CRE dissemination. Environmental monitoring can help assess and interrupt potential CRE transmission pathways.20

The study found that MDR bacteria were most prevalent in intensive care units (ICUs) and respiratory units, with common infections including bloodstream infections, pneumonia, urinary tract infections and catheter-associated infections.2123 MDR infections significantly worsen patient outcomes, particularly in ICU settings.24 Infections caused by CRKP are associated with a higher mortality rate than those caused by sensitive strains.25 Predictors of non-carbapenemase-producing CRE infection include recent ICU admission, chronic skin ulcers and antibiotic exposure.26 While recent ICU admission and chronic ulcers are non-modifiable risk factors, antibiotic exposure is modifiable.15 27 Other risk factors for carbapenemase-producing infections include prior colonisation, trauma, diabetes, malignancy, mechanical ventilation, catheter use and residence in long-term care facilities.2833 Older adults in long-term care are at high risk of MDR infections due to physiological decline, multiple comorbidities and reduced immune function.32 33 Catheter use increases infection risk by breaching physiological defences and facilitating MDR bacterial invasion.34 35

During the study period, the inpatient antimicrobial use rate showed a slight decline, while antimicrobial use intensity increased marginally. Compared with the 2024 China report, our stewardship data show a modest increase of 4.74% and 6.61% in use intensity relative to 2023. In 2023, the national inpatient use rate was 33.4%, with an intensity of 42.7 DDDs per 100 patient-days. Although the incidence of multiresistant bacteria declined, a global standard for this indicator has not been established.

The misuse of antimicrobials contributes to resistance and disrupts the normal flora. Concurrent use of broad-spectrum drugs results in greater selective pressure, perpetuating a cycle of resistance development.36 Additionally, the use of specific antibiotics may increase the risk of bacterial colonisation.37 Optimal treatment options for CRE infections remain unclear, with few effective antibiotics available. In such cases, consultation with experts in MDR infections is recommended.

Our tertiary care hospital has implemented a collaborative strategy for managing drug-resistant bacteria and antimicrobial use, with preliminary results indicating positive outcomes. Future work will involve using surveillance data to develop a predictive module for identifying and tracking drug-resistant bacteria. The objective is to establish an effective management and collaboration framework to optimise clinical antimicrobial use and prevent the spread of MDR pathogens. A multidisciplinary team will be formed, focusing on high-risk departments and patients at increased risk of MDR infections. This team will be responsible for monitoring, early warning, prevention and control of drug-resistant bacteria within healthcare environments.

Limitations

This study is limited by its retrospective design, which relies on existing records. The detection rate for VRE remained consistently at 0%, making it challenging to exclude the influence of confounding factors or to determine whether incomplete data may have introduced bias. National VRE detection rates for E. faecalis and E. faecium have been reported to range from 1.2% to 2.5% and 3.0% to 5.8%, respectively, with higher rates in southern provinces and lower rates in tertiary medical institutions. Future research should aim to enhance VRE testing protocols, extend monitoring and minimise potential sources of bias. Additionally, the study lacks timely analysis of the distribution of pathogenic bacteria across departments, trend data on antimicrobial use, and a comprehensive assessment of infection prevention and control measures. These gaps may contribute to delays in response and management. Despite these limitations, the findings underscore the need for real-time surveillance of drug-resistant bacteria and antimicrobial use.

Conclusion

This study highlights a correlation between the implementation of prevention and control strategies for drug-resistant bacteria, the rational utilisation of antimicrobials and the observed detection and incidence rates within healthcare institutions. To strengthen antimicrobial stewardship, a four-phase strategy was adopted: restricted use, optimised selection, alternative therapies, and continuous monitoring. First, strict control over antibiotic usage was enforced to prevent misuse. Second, optimal drug selection was applied based on clinical necessity. Third, alternative treatment options were explored. Finally, a comprehensive monitoring system was established. Based on local epidemiological data and supported by the 2023 IDSA guidelines, reversing antimicrobial resistance is recognised as a long-term endeavour.

Furthermore, the core value of predictive models in clinical surveillance lies in their capacity to proactively prevent hospital-acquired infections and improve the accuracy of prevention through interdisciplinary collaboration. At the national level, these models enhance diagnostic efficiency, promote the rational use of antimicrobial agents and reinforce public health policies and national security frameworks. Globally, they contribute to reducing disease burdens and economic costs, addressing challenges posed by climate change and sustainable development and promoting global public health governance. Antimicrobial resistance prediction models not only strengthen national public health defences but also help mitigate global health crises through international cooperation, serving as strategic tools for safeguarding both human and ecological health.
