Extending the Gini Index to Higher Dimensions via Whitening Processes
Gennaro Auricchio, Paolo Giudici, Giuseppe Toscani

Abstract
Measuring the degree of inequality expressed by a multivariate statistical distribution is a challenging problem, which appears in many fields of science and engineering. In this paper, we propose to extend the well known univariate Gini coefficient to multivariate distributions, by maintaining most of its properties. Our extension is based on the application of whitening processes that possess the property of scale stability.



Keywords.
Multivariate inequality measures, Gini index, whitening process, Mahalanobis distance

1
Introduction

The Lorenz curve
[
Lor05
]
and the Gini index
[
Gin14
,
Gin21
]
are still the most important tools to measure the inequality (mutual variability) expressed by a statistical distribution, such as the distribution of income or wealth in a country
[
Eli18
,
GCC14
,
GCIC16
,
Tos22
]
. However, they are univariate instruments, so that, for a given random
n
-dimensional vector
X
of scalar components
X_{i}
,
i=1,2,\dots,n
, they are suitable to analyze the variables
X_{i}
individually, ignoring the dependence structure they have as components of
X
. In reason of their importance in economical applications, there had been several efforts to extend the notions of Lorenz curve and Gini index to the multivariate case. The earliest approach, by means of methods of differential geometry, is due to Taguchi
[
Tag72a
,
Tag72b
]
. Further suggestions came by Arnold
[
Arn12
]
, Arnold and Sarabia
[
AS18
]
, Gajdos and Weymark
[
GW05
]
, Grothe, Kächele and Schmid
[
GKS22
]
, Koshevoy and Mosler
[
KM96
,
KM97
]
, and Sarabia and Jorda
[
SJ20
]
. Unfortunately, as outlined in
[
AS18
]
, all these multivariate extensions are essentially determined by elegant mathematical considerations but often lack applicability and interpretability.
In addition, these proposals do not possess some of the fundamental properties which are required to inequality measures, properties satisfied by the univariate Gini index.

In a recent paper
[
GRT24
]
, the possibility of measuring inequality of multidimensional statistical distributions by resorting to Fourier transform
[
ACG+20
,
ACGZ23
,
Tos22
]
, has been investigated.
There, one of the key properties that a multivariate Gini index should possess has been identified in the
scaling invariance
property on components
[
HR09
]
, which
is essential when trying to recover the value of the inequality index in a
multivariate phenomenon composed by different quantities, possibly measured in different
unit of measurement.

By resorting to the Mahalanobis distance
[
Mah18
]
, in place of the Euclidean distance, in
[
GRT24
]
a new version of the multivariate Gini coefficient satisfying the the scaling invariance property was proposed and studied.
Furthermore, it was shown that, for multivariate Gaussian distributions, the value of the proposed multivariate Gini index is related to the coefficient of variation introduced by Voinov and Nikulin
[
VN96
]
, as it does for the univariate case.
Owing to the fact that Mahalanobis distance is closely related to the process of
whitening
of a random vector, we will here extend the methods in
[
GRT24
]
, leading
to some generalizations of the Mahalanobis distance.
Among them, we will extract one which is particularly well suited to define a new multivariate Gini index, which appears easy-to-handle and interpret.

Whitening is a linear transformation which, given a random
n
-dimensional vector
{\textbf{X}}=(X_{1},\dots,X_{n})^{T}
, of mean value
{{\textbf{m}}}=(m_{1},\dots,m_{n})^{T}
and covariance matrix
\Sigma
, returns a new random vector
{\textbf{X}}^{*}
whose entries are orthonormal, that is the variance of each
X^{*}_{i}
is
1
and
the covariance of any
X^{*}_{i}
and
X^{*}_{j}
is null, whenever
i\neq j
.
Considering that orthonormality among random variables greatly simplifies multivariate data analysis, both from a computational and a statistical standpoint, whitening is a critically important tool, most often employed in pre-processing.
In essence, whitening is a generalization of standardisation, a
transformation
that is carried out by:

{{\textbf{X}}}^{*}=V^{-1/2}{{\textbf{X}}}

(1)

where the diagonal matrix
V=diag(var(X_{1}),var(X_{2}),\dots,var(X_{n}))
contains the variances of
X_{i}
,
i=1,2,\dots,n
.
This results in a new random vector, namely
{\textbf{X}}^{*}
, whose components have unitary variance, that is
var(X^{*}_{i})=1
, for every
i=1,2,\dots,n
.
Notice, however, that this transformation does not remove any correlation that the original entries of the vector
X
possess.
As we shall see, most whitening procedures lack the
scale stable
property, which ensures that the whitened random vector remains unchanged if the components of the original random vector
X
are scaled by a positive quantity.
This property is essential to obtain a multivariate inequality index that is scale invariant.

The content of this paper is as follows. In Section 2 we describe in details the whitening process. Furthermore, we discuss the properties which are important in connection with a good definition of a multivariate inequality index. Then, in view of applications, in Section 3, we introduce the new multivariate Gini-type index, by discussing its main properties. Section 4 presents an application of the multivariate index to the study of market economic inequality.

2
The Whitening Process

In what follows, we denote with
\mathcal{P}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
the set of
n
dimensional random vectors.
Since every random vector is identified by its associated probability distribution, with a slight abuse of notation, we use the random vector
X
and its associated probability measure
\mu
interchangeably.
Moreover, we denote with
\mathcal{P}_{Id}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
the subset of
\mathcal{P}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
containing the random vectors whose covariance matrix is the identity matrix.
In its most generic form, a
whitening process
is a map that, given a
n
-dimensional random vector
X
, of positive mean
m
, covariance matrix
\Sigma
, and characterized by a probability measure
\mu
, returns a new
n
-dimensional random vector whose covariance matrix is the identity, that is
\mathcal{S}:\mathcal{P}(\mathbb{R}^{n})\to\mathcal{P}_{Id}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
.
We say that a whitening process
\mathcal{S}
is linear if, for any given
{\textbf{X}}\in\mathcal{P}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
, there exists a
n\times n
square matrix that depends on
X
through its distribution, namely
W_{\mu}
, such that

{{\textbf{X}}}^{*}=\mathcal{S}({\textbf{X}})=W_{\mu}{{\textbf{X}}}.

(2)

The matrix
W_{\mu}
is also known as whitening matrix (associated with
\mathcal{S}
)
[
KLS18
]
.
If the covariance matrix of
X
, namely
\Sigma
, is invertible, then the whitening matrix in (
2
) must satisfy the identity
W_{\mu}\Sigma W_{\mu}^{T}=I
and thus
W_{\mu}(\Sigma W_{\mu}^{T}W_{\mu})=W_{\mu}
, which boils down to

W_{\mu}^{T}W_{\mu}=\Sigma^{-1}.

(3)

We remark that, given a
n
-dimensional random vector
X
whose covariance matrix is
\Sigma
, condition (
3
) does not determine uniquely the linear application that sends
X
to a whitened vector.
Indeed, the identity (
3
) does not fully identify
W_{\mu}
but allows for rotational freedom.
For example, given a whitening matrix
W_{\mu}
, any
\widetilde{W}_{\mu}
of the form

\widetilde{W}_{\mu}=ZW_{\mu},

is a whitening matrix as long as
Z
is an orthogonal matrix,
i.e.

Z^{T}Z=I
, since

(\widetilde{W}_{\mu})^{T}\widetilde{W}_{\mu}=W_{\mu}^{T}Z^{T}ZW_{\mu}=W_{\mu}^{T}W_{\mu}=\Sigma^{-1},

hence
\widetilde{W}_{\mu}
satisfies (
3
) regardless of the choice of
Z
.
Consequentially, there are multiple ways to whiten a random vector, even if we restrict our attention to linear whitening processes
[
LZ98
]
.

The
Zero-phase Components Analysis
(ZCA) whitening transformation, also known as Mahalanobis whitening
[
Mah18
]
, is characterized by the matrix

W_{\mu}^{Maha}=\Sigma^{-1/2}.

(4)

Note that, since
\Sigma
is symmetric and positive definite, we can decompose it as follows

\Sigma=Z\Theta Z^{T},

where
Z
is the eigenmatrix associated with
\Sigma
, and
\Theta
is the diagonal matrix whose diagonal contains the positive eigenvalues of
\Sigma
.
Since
\Theta
is diagonal and its diagonal contains only positive values, we have that
\Sigma=(\Theta^{{1}/{2}}Z)^{T}(\Theta^{{1}/{2}}Z)
and, therefore

\Sigma^{-1/2}=Z^{T}\Theta^{-1/2}Z.

(5)

Example 1
.

Let
X
be a
n
-dimensional Gaussian random vector, of mean
m
and positive definite
n\times n
covariance matrix
\Sigma
, with associated probability density function

f_{\textbf{X}}({{\textbf{x}}})=\frac{1}{(2\pi)^{n/2}(\det\Sigma^{-1})^{1/2}}\exp\left\{-\frac{1}{2}({\textbf{x}}-{\textbf{m}})^{T}\Sigma^{-1}({\textbf{x}}-{\textbf{m}})\right\},

Since the whitening process returns a new
n
-dimensional Gaussian random vector
{{\textbf{X}}}^{*}
of the same dimension
n
and with unit diagonal
white
covariance, the components of
{{\textbf{X}}}^{*}
are uncorrelated. In the Gaussian case, this is equivalent to independence, since we can express the joint probability density function as a product of the marginals. Hence

{\textbf{X}}^{*}
is a
n
-dimensional Gaussian random vector, of mean
{\textbf{m}}^{*}={W_{\mu}{\textbf{m}}}
and unit diagonal covariance, with independent components. Indeed

\displaystyle f_{{\textbf{X}}^{*}}({{\textbf{x}}})

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{(2\pi)^{n/2}}\exp\left\{-\frac{1}{2}({\textbf{x}}-{\textbf{m}}^{*})^{T}({\textbf{x}}-{\textbf{m}}^{*})\right\}=\prod_{i=1}^{n}\frac{1}{(2\pi)^{1/2}}\exp\left\{-\frac{1}{2}(x_{i}-m_{i}^{*})^{2}\right\}.

When it comes to measure the inequality of a probability distribution, not all whitening processes are, however, equal.
For example, only a few of the known whitening processes are
Scale Stable
, i.e. such that the random vector
{\textbf{X}}^{*}
obtained via the whitening process does not change if we multiply one or more entries of the pre-whitening vector
X
by a positive constant.

Definition 1

(Scale Stability)
.

A whitening process
\mathcal{S}
is said to be
Scale Stable
if

\mathcal{S}({{\textbf{X}}})=\mathcal{S}(Q{{\textbf{X}}}),

for every random vector
X
and for any diagonal matrix
Q=diag(q_{1},q_{2},\dots,q_{n})
whose diagonal elements are positive constants.

Scale Stability is an essential property for any sparsity index whose definition relies on whitened data, as it is connected to the scale invariance of the index itself.
For this reason, we now show that linear
Scale Stable
whitening processes always exist and identify two of them.

The Choleski Whitening

Cholesky whitening
is a process based on the Cholesky factorization of a definite positive matrix.
In this case the whitening matrix is

W_{\mu}^{Chol}=L^{T},

(6)

where
L
is the unique lower triangular matrix with positive diagonal values that satisfies (
3
).
Owing to the triangular structure of
W_{\mu}^{Chol}
, the whitening process it induces is Scale Stable, as the following result shows.

Theorem 1
.

The Choleski whitening process is Scale Stable.

Proof.

Let
Q=diag(q_{1},q_{2},\dots,q_{n})
be a diagonal matrix such that
q_{i}>0
.
Given a random vector
X
, let us denote with
\Sigma
its covariance matrix; then, we have that the covariance matrix of
{\textbf{Y}}=Q{\textbf{X}}
is
Q\Sigma Q
.

Let
L
be the Choleski factorization of
\Sigma^{-1}
, i.e.
\Sigma^{-1}=L^{T}L
.
It is easy to see that the inverse matrix of
Q\Sigma Q
is
Q^{-1}\Sigma^{-1}Q^{-1}
. Hence we have

(Q\Sigma Q)^{-1}=(LQ^{-1})^{T}(LQ^{-1}).

(7)

Since
L
is lower triangular,
LQ^{-1}
is lower triangular as well, since the
i
-th row of
LQ^{-1}
is equal to the
i
-th row of
L
multiplied by
q_{i}^{-1}
.
Owing to the uniqueness of the Choleski factorization, we conclude that
LQ^{-1}
is the Choleski factorization associated to
(Q\Sigma Q)^{-1}
.
Finally, notice that

LQ^{-1}(Q{{\textbf{X}}})=L{{\textbf{X}}},

which concludes the proof.
∎

The correlation Whitening

The correlation whitening, also known as
Zero-Components Analysis
(ZCA-cor) employs a whitening matrix which derives from the correlation matrix.
In this case, given a random vector
X
, the whitening matrix is defined as:

W_{\mu}^{ZCA-cor}=P^{-1/2}V^{-1/2},

(8)

where
P
is the correlation matrix of
X
, and
V
is the diagonal matrix introduced in (
1
).
Again, notice that
P^{-\frac{1}{2}}
in (
8
) is not defined uniquely, thus there are multiple ZCA-cor matrices associated with the same
{\textbf{X}}\in\mathcal{P}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
.
Since the correlation matrix
P
is scale invariant, it is easy to prove that the ZCA-cor whitening process is Scale Stable.

Theorem 2
.

The ZCA-cor whitening process is Scale Stable, regardless of how the square root of
P^{-1}
is selected.

Proof.

Without loss of generality, we show this result for a specific square root of
P^{-1}
as our proof can be generalized to any square root of
P^{-1}
.
Let
Q=diag(q_{1},q_{2},\dots,q_{n})
be a diagonal matrix such that
q_{i}>0
.
Given a random vector
X
, let us denote with
\Sigma
its covariance matrix; then, we have that the covariance matrix of
{\textbf{Y}}=Q{\textbf{X}}
is
Q\Sigma Q
.

Let
P
be the correlation matrix associated with
X
.
It is easy to see that
P
is definite positive and symmetric.
We then decompose
P
as
P=O^{T}\Lambda O
, where
\Lambda
is a diagonal matrix containing the eigenvalues of
P
and
O
is the matrix containing the eigenvectors associated with
P
.
Notice that
\Sigma=V^{\frac{1}{2}}PV^{\frac{1}{2}}
, where
V=diag(var(X_{1}),var(X_{2}),\dots,var(X_{n}))
.
Moreover, the correlation matrix
P
is scale invariant, thus the correlation matrix induced by
Q{\textbf{X}}
is still
P
.
Let us now define
\Lambda^{-\frac{1}{2}}O^{T}V^{-\frac{1}{2}}
and consider
{{\textbf{X}}}_{*}=\Lambda^{-\frac{1}{2}}O^{T}V^{-\frac{1}{2}}{\textbf{X}}
. It is easy to see that the covariance matrix induced by
{{\textbf{X}}}_{*}
is the identity matrix.
Let us now consider
{{\textbf{Y}}}=Q{\textbf{X}}
.
The variance of each
Y_{i}
is equal to
q_{i}^{2}
times the variance of
X_{i}
, that is
var(Y_{i})=q_{i}^{2}var(X_{i})
.
Since the correlation matrix of
Y
is the same as the correlation matrix of
X
, and since we have that the ZCA-cor whitening matrix induced by
Y
is
\Lambda^{-\frac{1}{2}}O^{T}V_{Q}^{-\frac{1}{2}}
, where
V_{Q}=diag(q_{1}^{2}var(X_{1}),q_{2}^{2}var(X_{2}),\dots,q_{n}^{2}var(X_{n}))=QVQ
, we infer that

\Lambda^{-\frac{1}{2}}O^{T}V_{Q}^{-\frac{1}{2}}{\textbf{Y}}=\Lambda^{-\frac{1}{2}}O^{T}V^{-\frac{1}{2}}Q^{-1}{\textbf{Y}}=\Lambda^{-\frac{1}{2}}O^{T}V^{-\frac{1}{2}}{\textbf{X}},

which concludes the proof for the ZCA-cor whitening.
∎

Counter example

We remark that we not all the whitening processes are Scale Stable.
Consider for example the Principal Components Analysis (PCA) whitening, a well known statistical pre-processing method, whose whitening matrix is defined as

W^{PCA}=\Theta^{-1/2}Z^{T},

(9)

where
\Theta
is the diagonal matrix containing the eigenvalues of the covariance matrix
\Sigma
, and
Z
the corresponding (orthogonal) eigenvector matrix (e.g.
[
Fri87
]
).
The PCA transformation first rotates the variables using the eigenvector matrix of
\Sigma
.
This results in orthogonal components, but with different variances.
To obtain whitened components, the rotated variables are then scaled by the square root of the eigenvalues via the matrix
\Theta^{-1/2}
.
Note that, due to the sign ambiguity of the eigenvectors
Z
, the PCA whitening matrix given by (
9
) is not unique.
However, adjusting the column signs in
Z
such that elements on the diagonal of
\Theta
are positive: all diagonal elements positive, results in a unique PCA whitening transformation with positive diagonal cross-covariance and cross-correlation.

Notice that this procedure is different from the one defining the ZCA whitening process, since the ZCA first scales the entries, then rotates the variables, and then scales the variables according to the eigenvalues of the correlation matrix.
Despite its similarities with the ZCA, the PCA whitening is not Scale Stable, as the next example shows.

Let us consider a Gaussian random vector
X
, and let us set
\nu
its probability measure. Its mean is
m_{\textbf{X}}=(1,1)
and covariance matrix is

\Sigma_{\nu}=\begin{pmatrix}4&-2\\
-2&3\end{pmatrix}.

Since the eigenvalues of
\Sigma_{\nu}
are
\theta_{1}=5.56
and
\theta_{2}=1.44
and their associated eigenvectors are
v_{1}=(0.78,0.61)
and
v_{2}=(-0.61,0.78)
, respectively, from (
9
) we infer that

W^{PCA}_{\nu}=\begin{pmatrix}\frac{1}{\sqrt{5.56}}&0\\
0&\frac{1}{\sqrt{1.44}}\end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix}0.78&-0.61\\
0.61&0.78\end{pmatrix}=\begin{pmatrix}0.33&-0.26\\
0.51&0.66\end{pmatrix}.

Therefore, the random vector
W_{\nu}^{PCA}{\textbf{X}}
is a Gaussian vector whose covariance is the identity matrix and its mean is
W_{\nu}^{PCA}m_{\textbf{X}}=(0.07,1.17)
.

Let us now consider the Gaussian random vector
{\textbf{Y}}=(2X_{1},X_{2})
, that is the random vector obtained by multiplying the first entry of
X
by two. Let us denote by
\tilde{\nu}
its probability measure.
It is easy to see that
Y
is still a Gaussian random vector, whose mean is
m_{\textbf{Y}}=(2,1)
and whose covariance matrix is

\Sigma_{\tilde{\nu}}=\begin{pmatrix}16&-4\\
-4&3\end{pmatrix}.

In this case, the eigenvalues of
\Sigma_{\tilde{\nu}}
are
\eta_{1}=17.13
and
\eta_{2}=1.87
and their associated eigenvector are
w_{1}=(0.96,0.27)
and
w_{2}=(-0.27,0.96)
, respectively.
In particular, the PCA whitening matrix associated with
Y
is

W^{PCA}_{\tilde{\nu}}=\begin{pmatrix}0.23&-0.06\\
0.20&0.71\end{pmatrix}.

Therefore, the random vector
W^{PCA}_{\tilde{\nu}}{\textbf{Y}}
follows a Gaussian distribution whose covariance matrix is the identity and whose mean is
W^{PCA}_{\tilde{\nu}}m_{\textbf{Y}}=(0.40,1.11)
.
We then conclude that
W^{PCA}_{\nu}{\textbf{X}}\neq W^{PCA}_{\tilde{\nu}}{\textbf{Y}}
so that the PCA whitening is not Scale Stable.

The
p
-Mahanolobis Metrics

Given a
n
-dimensional random vector
X
, we denote with
{{\textbf{m}}}=(m_{1},m_{2},\dots,m_{n})^{T}
its mean and with
\Sigma_{\mu}
its positive definite
n\times n
covariance matrix.
The Mahalanobis metric is then defined as

{\textbf{m}}_{2}({{\textbf{X}}})=\sqrt{{{\textbf{m}}}^{T}\Sigma^{-1}_{\mu}{{\textbf{m}}}}=\sqrt{(W_{\mu}{{\textbf{m}}})^{T}(W_{\mu}{{\textbf{m}}})}=||W_{\mu}{{\textbf{m}}}||_{2},

(10)

where
W_{\mu}
is any whitening matrix.
Since for any whitening matrix
W_{\mu}
we have that

{\textbf{m}}^{T}W_{\mu}^{T}W_{\mu}{\textbf{m}}={\textbf{m}}^{T}\Sigma^{-1}_{\mu}{\textbf{m}}

the Euclidean norm of
W_{\mu}{\textbf{m}}
does not depend on
W_{\mu}
.
This property, however, is lost if we consider other norms of the vector
W_{\mu}{\textbf{m}}
.
In this case, the choice of the whitening matrix
W_{\mu}
affects the value of the norm, and it may not be Scale Stable.
To overcome this issue it suffices to consider a Scale Stable whitening.

Definition 2

(The
l_{p}
Mahalanobis norm)
.

Let
\mathcal{S}
be a
Scale Stable
whitening procedure and let
X
be a random vector.
For any
p\geq 1
, we define the
l_{p}
-Mahalanobis norm induced by
\mathcal{S}
of
X
as follows

M_{p}({{\textbf{X}}})=||\mathbb{E}[\mathcal{S}({{\textbf{X}}})]||_{p},

(11)

where
\mathbb{E}[\mathcal{S}({\textbf{X}})]
is the vector containing the mean of
\mathcal{S}({{\textbf{X}}})
.

From a constructive viewpoint, note that, owing to Theorem
1
and
2
, both Choleski and correlation whitening processes allow us to define a generalized Mahanolobis norm that is scale invariant, i.e.

M_{p}({{\textbf{X}}})=M_{p}(Q{{\textbf{X}}}),

for every diagonal matrix
Q
whose diagonal contains strictly positive values.

3
A new multivariate Gini-type index

In this section, we show how to define a scaling invariant Gini index for multivariate distributions using the
l_{p}
-Mahalanobis norm introduced in Definition
2
.
From now on we consider only the correlation whitening, as it allows to define a whitening process that, given a random non-negative vector
X
, returns a whitened vector that is also non-negative.
As we will see, the key to obtain such property is to select a suitable
P^{-\frac{1}{2}}
in equation (
8
).

Definition 3
.

For any
X
random vector, let
W_{\mu}^{ZCA-cor}
be the correlation whitening process associated with
X
.
Then, we define

G_{p}({\textbf{X}})=\frac{1}{2M_{p}({\textbf{X}})}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}\times\mathbb{R}^{n}}||W^{ZCA-cor}_{\mu}({\textbf{x}}-{\textbf{y}})||_{p}\mu(d{\textbf{x}})\mu(d{\textbf{y}}),

(12)

where, for every
p\geq 1
,
M_{p}({\textbf{X}})
is the Mahalanobis metric (see Definition
2
).

Depending on the whitening matrix, i.e. on the function
{\textbf{X}}\to W_{\mu}^{ZCA-cor}
that maps a random vector into its whitening matrix, the index
G_{p}
satisfies the defining properties of an inequality index.
For example, if the components of
X
are non-negative, the whitened vector
W_{\mu}^{ZCA-cor}{\textbf{X}}
might not be non-negative, depending on how we select
P^{-\frac{1}{2}}
in (
8
).
We now characterize a way to associate any
X
to a square root of
P^{-1}
such that
W_{\mu}^{ZCA-cor}{\textbf{X}}
is non-negative if and only if
X
is non-negative.

Theorem 3
.

Given
X
a random vector whose correlation matrix
P
is invertible, let
P^{-1}
denote the inverse of
P
.
Moreover, let us decompose
P^{-1}
as
P^{-1}=O\Lambda O^{T}
where
O
is the matrix containing the eigenvectors and
\Lambda
is the diagonal matrix containing the eigenvalues of
P^{-1}
.
Finally, let
V
be the diagonal matrix containing the variances of
X
.
Then, the random vector

O\Lambda^{-\frac{1}{2}}O^{T}V^{-\frac{1}{2}}{\textbf{X}}

(13)

is non-negative if and only if
X
is non-negative.

Proof.

Let us consider
P^{-\frac{1}{2}}=O\Lambda^{-\frac{1}{2}}O^{T}
, where
O
is the orthonormal matrix containing all the eigenvectors of
P^{-1}
and let
\Lambda^{-1}=diag(\frac{1}{\lambda_{1}},\frac{1}{\lambda_{2}},\dots,\frac{1}{\lambda_{n}})
, where
\lambda_{i}
are the eigenvalues of
P
.
If
X
is a non-negative random vector then
O\Lambda^{-\frac{1}{2}}O^{T}V^{-\frac{1}{2}}{\textbf{X}}
is also non-negative, since

1.

if
X
is non-negative, then
V^{-\frac{1}{2}}{\textbf{X}}
is non-negative;

2.

the rotation
O^{T}
maps the set
\mathbb{R}_{+}^{n}:=\{{\textbf{x}}\;\text{s.t.}\;x_{i}\geq 0\}
to
O^{T}(\mathbb{R}^{n}_{+})
;

3.

the linear application induced by
\Lambda^{-\frac{1}{2}}
maps
O^{T}(\mathbb{R}^{n}_{+})
to itself;

4.

the rotation
O
maps
O^{T}(\mathbb{R}^{n}_{+})
back to
\mathbb{R}_{+}^{n}
, which concludes the proof.

∎

Leveraging on Theorem
3
, given a random vector
X
, from now on we will consider the correlation whitening process induced by
P^{-\frac{1}{2}}=O\Lambda^{-\frac{1}{2}}O^{T}
, and set

W^{ZCA}_{\mu}=O\Lambda^{-\frac{1}{2}}O^{T}V^{-\frac{1}{2}}.

(14)

Note that, if
X
is a random vector whose covariance matrix is the identity, then
W^{ZCA}_{\mu}=Id
.
We will then consider the family of multidimensional Gini indices induced by
W^{ZCA}_{\mu}
, so that

G_{p}({\textbf{X}})=\frac{1}{2M_{p}({\textbf{X}})}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}\times\mathbb{R}^{n}}||W^{ZCA}_{\mu}({\textbf{x}}-{\textbf{y}})||_{p}\mu(d{\textbf{x}})\mu(d{\textbf{y}}),

When
p=1
, the latter identity becomes particularly interesting as, in this case, we can express
G_{1}
as a convex combination of the one dimensional Gini indexes, which we denote with
G
, of the components of the vector
{\textbf{X}}^{*}=W^{ZCA}_{\mu}{\textbf{X}}
.

Definition 4

(
l_{1}
Gini Index, ZCA)
.

Let
X
be a random vector whose mean vector is
{{\textbf{m}}}=(m_{1},m_{2},\dots,m_{n})^{T}
and whose covariance matrix
\Sigma
is positive definite.
Moreover, we denote with
\mu
the probability measure associated with
X
.
We define

G_{1}({\textbf{X}})=\frac{1}{2\sum_{i=1}^{n}|(W_{\mu}^{ZCA}{\textbf{m}})_{i}|}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}\times\mathbb{R}^{n}}\sum_{i=1}^{n}|(W_{\mu}^{ZCA}({\textbf{x}}-{\textbf{y}}))_{i}|\mu(d{\textbf{x}})\mu(d{\textbf{y}}).

Theorem 4
.

Let
X
be a random vector of mean
{{\textbf{m}}}=(m_{1},m_{2},\dots,m_{n})^{T}
and positive definite
n\times n
covariance matrix
\Sigma
.
Then, we have

G_{1}({\textbf{X}})=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\frac{|m_{i}^{*}|}{\sum_{i=1}^{n}|m_{i}^{*}|}G\big((W_{\mu}^{ZCA}{\textbf{X}})_{i}\big)

(15)

where
m_{i}^{*}=(W_{\mu}^{ZCA}{\textbf{m}})_{i}
and
G(X_{i}^{*})
is the one dimensional Gini index of the
i
-th component of
{\textbf{X}}^{*}
.
Furthermore, if the components of
X
are non negative, we have that

0\leq G_{1}({\textbf{X}})\leq 1.

Equation (
15
) is the most important result of this paper, and establishes that the higher dimensional Gini index induced by the
l_{1}
Mahalanobis norm is a convex combination of the
1
-dimensional Gini indexes of the random variable
{\textbf{X}}^{*}=W^{ZCA}_{\mu}{\textbf{X}}
.

Proof.

First, notice that the mean vector of
{\textbf{X}}^{*}
is, by definition,
{\textbf{m}}^{*}:=W_{\mu}^{ZCA}{\textbf{m}}
, so that

\sum_{i=1}^{n}|m_{i}^{*}|=\sum_{i=1}^{n}|(W^{ZCA}_{\mu}{\textbf{m}})_{i}|.

By definition of
G_{1}
, we have that

G_{1}(X_{i}^{*})=\frac{1}{2|m^{*}_{i}|}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}\times\mathbb{R}^{n}}|x_{i}^{*}-y_{i}^{*}|(N_{i})_{\#}\mu(d{\textbf{x}}_{*})(N_{i})_{\#}\mu(d{\textbf{y}}_{*})

where
\mu
is the probability measure associated with
X
and
N:\mathbb{R}^{n}\to\mathbb{R}^{n}
defined as
N_{i}:{\textbf{x}}\to(W_{\mu}^{ZCA}{\textbf{x}})_{i}
.
By a change of variables, we have that

G_{1}(X_{i}^{*})=\frac{1}{2|m_{i}^{*}|}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}\times\mathbb{R}^{n}}|(W_{\mu}^{ZCA}({\textbf{x}}-{\textbf{y}}))_{i}|\mu(d{\textbf{x}})\mu(d{\textbf{y}}).

By plugging the value of
G_{1}(X_{i}^{*})
in the right hand-side of (
15
), we retrieve identity (
15
).

To conclude, notice that
G(X^{*}_{i})\in[0,1]
since
W_{\mu}^{ZCA}{\textbf{X}}
is a non-negative random vector hence
G_{1}({\textbf{X}})\in[0,1]
since
G_{1}({\textbf{X}})
is a convex combination of values in
[0,1]
.
∎

It is insightful to interpret the result presented in Theorem
4
in the context of what a whitening procedure does in to the multivariate data.
When analysing a multidimensional statistical distribution, derived from a set of data, the measured quantities are typically interdependent.
Consequently, the inequality expressed by the distribution, which measures how far apart are the individual multidimensional observations from each other, cannot be assessed simply as a function of the one-dimensional Gini indexes, each applied to a different one-dimensional component of the distribution.
However, by whitening the data, we can express the same inequality as a function of the standard one-dimensional Gini indexes, applied to the whitened one-dimensional components.

Indeed, Theorem
4
indicates a natural method to combine the one-dimensional Gini indices by means of a convex combination. The weights of the combination depend on the relative importance of the mean of the
whitened components.
Specifically, the weight assigned to the Gini index of the
i
-th
component of
{\textbf{X}}^{*}
is proportional to its mean value, normalised by the sum of all mean values.
We formalize the properties of the
G_{1}
inequality measure in the following Corollary.

Corollary 1
.

Let
X
be a random vector of mean
{{\textbf{m}}}=(m_{1},m_{2},\dots,m_{n})^{T}
and positive definite
n\times n
covariance matrix
\Sigma_{\mu}
.
Then following properties hold

1.

For any
\epsilon>0
, there exists a random variable
{\textbf{X}}_{\epsilon}
such that
G_{1}({\textbf{X}}_{\epsilon})\leq\epsilon
.

2.

For any
\epsilon>0
, there exists a random variable
{\textbf{X}}_{\epsilon}
such that
G_{1}({\textbf{X}}_{\epsilon})\geq 1-\epsilon
.

3.

The
G_{1}
is Scale Invariant, that is
G_{1}(\mathcal{X})=G_{1}(\mathcal{X}_{Q})
for any diagonal matrix
Q=diag(q_{1},q_{2},\dots,q_{n})
with
q_{j}>0
, and satisfies the Rising Tide property, that is

G_{1}({\textbf{X}}+{\textbf{c}})\leq G_{1}({\textbf{X}})

for any positive vector
{\textbf{c}}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}_{+}
.

4.

Let us assume that
|m_{1}^{*}|
is much larger than
\sum_{i=2}^{n}|m_{i}^{*}|
.
Then,

\lim_{|(m_{1})_{*}|\to\infty}G_{1}({\textbf{X}})=G(X_{1}^{*})

that is
G_{1}({\textbf{X}})\sim G(X_{1}^{*})
.
This property tells us that if one of the uncorrelated component of
{\textbf{X}}^{*}
dominates the others meanwise, then the overall inequality is mostly determined by its inequality.

Proof.

We divide the proof into four parts.

Proof of Point (
1
).

Let
\epsilon>0
be fixed.
Consider
{\textbf{X}}=(X_{1},X_{2},\dots,X_{n})
be a random vector whose components are independent and identically distributed. Moreover, assume that each
X_{i}
is distributed as follows

X_{i}=\begin{cases}0\quad\quad\quad&\textrm{with probability}\quad p=\frac{1}{2}\\
2\quad\quad\quad&\textrm{with probability}\quad p=\frac{1}{2}.\end{cases}

It is easy to check that
\mathbb{E}[X_{i}]=1
,
Var(X_{i})=1
for every
i=1,\dots,n
, and, by construction,
Cor(X_{i},X_{j})=0
if
i\neq j
, therefore
{\textbf{X}}^{*}=W^{ZCA}_{\mu}{\textbf{X}}={\textbf{X}}
.
Since each component
{\textbf{X}}^{*}
are identically distributed, formula (
15
) boils down to

G_{1}({\textbf{X}})=G(X_{1})=\frac{2}{2\mathbb{E}[X_{i}]}=1.

Given
M>0
let us define
{\textbf{X}}_{M}=(X_{1}+M,X_{2}+M,\dots,X_{n}+M)
.
By the same argument used above, we have that
{\textbf{X}}_{M}^{*}={\textbf{X}}_{M}
and that

G_{1}({\textbf{X}}_{M})=G(X_{1}+M)=\frac{1}{(1+M)},

It is then easy to see that if
M>\frac{1}{2\epsilon}
, then
G_{1}({\textbf{X}}_{M})\leq\epsilon
.

Proof of Point (
2
).

Let
\epsilon>0
be fixed.
Consider
{\textbf{X}}=(X_{1},X_{2},\dots,X_{n})
be a random vector whose components are independent and identically distributed. Moreover, assume that each
X_{i}
is distributed as follows

X_{i}=\begin{cases}0\quad\quad\quad&\textrm{with probability}\quad 1-p\\
\frac{1}{\sqrt{p}(1-p)}\quad\quad\quad&\textrm{with probability}\quad p,\end{cases}

where
p\in(0,1)
.
It is easy to see that, for every
p\in(0,1)
, the covariance matrix of
X
is the identity matrix, thus
{\textbf{X}}^{*}=W^{ZCA}_{\mu}{\textbf{X}}={\textbf{X}}
.
Moreover, we have that
\mathbb{E}[X_{i}]=\frac{\sqrt{p}}{1-p}
and thus

G_{1}({\textbf{X}})=G(X_{1})=\frac{1-p}{2\sqrt{p}}\frac{2p(1-p)}{\sqrt{p}(1-p)}=1-p.

In particular, if
p\leq\epsilon
, we have
G_{1}({\textbf{X}})\geq 1-\epsilon
.

Proof of Point (
3
).

The scale invariance follows directly from Theorem
2
.
Let us now consider the rising tide property.
Let
c
be a vector whose components are positive, that is
{\textbf{c}}=(c_{1},c_{2},\dots,c_{n})
, with
c_{i}\geq 0
.
Since
X
and
{\textbf{X}}+{\textbf{c}}
have the same covariance matrix, it follows that
W^{ZCA}_{\mu}=W^{ZCA}_{{\textbf{X}}+{\textbf{c}}}
and that
\mathbb{E}[{\textbf{X}}+{\textbf{c}}]=\mathbb{E}[{\textbf{X}}]+{\textbf{c}}
.
In particular, we have that

W^{ZCA}_{{\textbf{X}}+{\textbf{c}}}(\mathbb{E}[{\textbf{X}}+{\textbf{c}}])=W^{ZCA}_{\mu}(\mathbb{E}[{\textbf{X}}]+{\textbf{c}})={\textbf{m}}^{*}+W^{ZCA}_{\mu}{\textbf{c}}

and thus

\displaystyle G_{1}(

\displaystyle{\textbf{X}}+{\textbf{c}})

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2\sum_{i=1}^{n}|({\textbf{m}}^{*}+W^{ZCA}_{\mu}{\textbf{c}})_{i}|}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}\times\mathbb{R}^{n}}\sum_{i=1}^{n}|(W^{ZCA}_{\mu}({\textbf{x}}+{\textbf{c}}-({\textbf{y}}+{\textbf{c}})))_{i}|\mu(d{\textbf{x}})\mu(d{\textbf{y}})

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2\sum_{i=1}^{n}|({\textbf{m}}^{*}+W^{ZCA}_{\mu}{\textbf{c}})_{i}|}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}\times\mathbb{R}^{n}}\sum_{i=1}^{n}|(W^{ZCA}_{\mu}({\textbf{x}}-{\textbf{y}}))_{i}|\mu(d{\textbf{x}})\mu(d{\textbf{y}})

\displaystyle\leq\frac{1}{2\sum_{i=1}^{n}|{\textbf{m}}^{*}_{i}|}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}\times\mathbb{R}^{n}}\sum_{i=1}^{n}|(W^{ZCA}_{\mu}({\textbf{x}}-{\textbf{y}}))_{i}|\mu(d{\textbf{x}})\mu(d{\textbf{y}})=G_{1}({\textbf{X}}),

where the last inequality follows from the fact that
W^{ZCA}_{\mu}
maps
\{{\textbf{x}}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}\;\text{s.t.}\;x_{i}\geq 0\}
into itself, thus
|({\textbf{m}}^{*}+W^{ZCA}_{\mu}{\textbf{c}})_{i}|=|{\textbf{m}}^{*}_{i}|+|(W^{ZCA}_{\mu}{\textbf{c}})_{i}|\geq|{\textbf{m}}^{*}_{i}|
for every
i=1,\dots,n
.

Proof of Point (
4
).

It follows from the following identity

\lim_{|(m_{1})_{*}|\to\infty}\frac{|m_{i}^{*}|}{\sum_{j=1}^{n}|m_{j}^{*}|}=\begin{cases}1\quad\quad\text{if}\quad i=1\\
0\quad\quad\text{otherwise}\end{cases}.

∎

Finally, since any affine transformation of a Gaussian random vector is a Gaussian random vector, we can explicitly express
G_{1}
as a function of the parameters of the Gaussian distribution.

Theorem 5
.

If
X
is a Gaussian random vector whose mean is non-null, that is
{\textbf{m}}\neq(0,0)
, then also
{\textbf{X}}^{*}
is a Gaussian random vector with non-null mean.
Moreover, we have that

G_{1}({\textbf{X}})=\frac{n}{\sqrt{\pi}\sum_{i=1}^{n}|(W^{ZCA}_{\mu}{\textbf{m}})_{i}|}.

Proof.

It follows from the fact that any whitened multivariate Gaussian distribution is a Gaussian distribution with independent components and whose covariance matrix is the identity.
∎

This shows that the
G_{1}
index of a Gaussian distribution is proportional to the inverse of the
\|{\textbf{m}}^{*}\|_{1}
.
However, the result in Corollary

1
does not hold, as Gaussian vectors take value on
\mathbb{R}^{n}
. In this case, Theorem
5
is to be interpreted as giving the explicit expression of a multidimensional coefficient of variation, which exhibits the same properties of that of Voinov and Nikulin
[
AHR15
,
VN96
]
, which is obtained by evaluating the multivariate Gini index of a Gaussian random vector with respect to the 2-Mahalanobis Metric.

Finally, notice that Theorem
5
can be generalized to any case in which the multivariate probability distribution allows an explicit computation of the one dimensional Gini index for its components.
