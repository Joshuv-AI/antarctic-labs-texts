Patient perspectives on knowledge gaps in hypersensitivity pneumonitis
Authors: Janani Varadarajan, Armani Edgar, Ronan O'Beirne, Tessy K Paul, Jamuna K Krishnan, Robert Kaner, Monika M Safford, Kerri Aronson
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectivesResponding to prior research findings that lack of knowledge about their disease impacted patients’ quality of life, the objective of this study was to identify and prioritise information gaps about hypersensitivity pneumonitis (HP) from the perspective of patients living with the disease.DesignThis study used the nominal group technique, a semiquantitative and qualitative method to identify stakeholder priorities.SettingVirtual group sessions were conducted in an academic medical centre in the USA.Participants21 patients diagnosed with HP participated in this study. Participants were eligible if they spoke English and had access to the internet and were excluded if they had cognitive impairment.ResultsThe patient statements were grouped into seven themes that were prioritised by participants in the following order: (1) natural history and prognosis; (2) current treatment options and therapeutic research; (3) epidemiology and aetiology of HP; (4) living well with HP; (5) origin and management of symptoms; (6) identifying and mitigating exposures and (7) methods of educating patients, clinicians and others about HP.ConclusionsThis study provides an understanding of patients’ prioritisation of knowledge gaps in HP to inform targeted educational interventions and provide avenues for future research.

Background

Hypersensitivity pneumonitis (HP) is a rare interstitial lung disease (ILD) in which an adverse immune reaction occurs in the lungs of susceptible individuals from exposure to an environmental allergen. The lung parenchyma subsequently develops inflammation and/or fibrosis that may be progressive over time,1 2 resulting in a myriad of intrusive and life-altering symptoms that substantially impact quality of life. Patients with HP were found to have lower health-related quality of life (HRQOL) than patients with other kinds of ILD.3

The distinctly severe effects of HP on quality of life can be explained in part by the fact that patients with HP are tasked with identifying and mitigating a culprit exposure as the best course of treatment.4 This need to successfully identify and avoid exposure creates complex psychosocial challenges for those living with this disease.5 Unfortunately, only about half of patients on average successfully identify their exposure, and there are limited data available on the efficacy of currently employed pharmacological treatments.4 6 7 The ambiguity posed by exposure identification coupled with the potentially progressive and incurable nature of the disease create unique challenges for patients with HP that impair their quality of life.

Our prior work has shown that patients’ profound lack of knowledge about the disease is a major driver of poor HRQOL in HP.5 Patients with ILD have voiced the need for improved access to understandable information about their disease.8 The American Thoracic Society has identified patient knowledge as a priority patient-centred endpoint,9 however, there is a lack of verified resources and well-studied interventions to improve knowledge in HP. This is a substantial gap in HP patient-centred care and supports the critical need for evidence-based interventions targeting improved patient knowledge in HP.

The biggest perceived knowledge gaps about HP among patients are currently unknown and have not been systematically identified and categorised from patient perspectives. To inform future education targets and intervention development to improve knowledge gaps, we first seek to identify the biggest questions about HP from patients’ perspectives. The objective of this study was to understand where patients with HP are currently receiving information about their disease and to identify and prioritise the information gaps about HP from the perspective of those patients who are living with the disease.

Methods

Patient and public involvement

Patients were involved in the design and conduct of this research. A patient advisory committee was consulted to develop the questions asked during the study and review and advise on the results to inform future education material development. Patient participants were also informed of the results after the study was concluded through a letter sent via mail.

Study participants

Participants over the age of 18 with a diagnosis of HP were recruited from Weill Cornell Medicine (WCM) in New York, New York, USA and University of Virginia in Charlottesville, Virginia, USA. A diagnosis of HP had to be made at an ILD centre based on multidisciplinary discussion. Participants were eligible if they were English speaking and were excluded if they had cognitive impairment as deemed by their treating physician. To allow individuals who did not have internet access to participate, research coordinators worked with interested and otherwise eligible participants who did not have reliable internet access at home to provide them with a space within the clinic.

Data collection

The nominal group technique (NGT) is a validated semiquantitative method in clinical research for obtaining group consensus and priority setting among stakeholders.10 11 Participants take turns responding to a question during a structured group session, and all responses are subsequently ranked by the group (figure 1). This method allows for the identification and prioritisation of stakeholder input, which can inform the development of a targeted education or research agenda.

Nominal group technique. Focus group sizes ranged from three to six people.

Five NGT sessions were conducted virtually using a software tool from March to August 2023. Participants joined a Zoom call and simultaneously logged onto a secure website designed to support NGT sessions. The sessions were audio recorded. All sessions were conducted by the WCM research team, and the sessions were moderated by a clinician researcher (KA) who is an experienced group moderator. A second member of the study team (AE) was responsible for recording the statements generated during the session in real time into the software. Prior to the start of the session, participants were oriented to the background and roles of the research team members present during the group session. Participants were asked to share their perspectives on the topic by answering two distinct questions that aimed to elicit the most important knowledge gaps for patients with HP. These questions were developed with input from ILD clinicians and a patient advisory committee.

Question 1: ‘What questions about your HP do you have that keep you awake at night?’

Question 2: ‘What information do you want about your HP that you cannot find?’

After a brief introduction about the study and NGT procedures, participants were asked to consider the first question. Participants were given 5 min to brainstorm and independently create a list of questions. Then, participants were each asked to share one new question from their list at a time in a round-robin fashion. Participants continued to share in this manner until there were no new questions left to share with the group. Each response was typed real time, verbatim into a list visible to all participants by a member of the study team. Once all responses were recorded, participants were given the opportunity to view the entire list of statements and correct or clarify to ensure they were correctly understood and documented by the study team. They were given a chance to edit, combine or separate questions as needed after discussing with the group.

After finalising the shared list, participants were each asked to select the three most important questions from their perspective on the NGT website. Then, they were asked to rank the three questions as first, second and third most important. Each participant had three votes available to them. Points were assigned for each response depending on how it was ranked by the participant; first choice was given 3 points, second choice was given 2 points and third choice was given 1 point. Responses that received no votes received a score of zero. The NGT software then generated the results from the voting for all participants. Participants were asked to reflect on the voting pattern and discuss any perceived inconsistencies or surprises. Then, the group moved on to the second question and repeated the same procedures outlined above.

Data analysis

As previously described the NGT is a semiquantitative technique that allows for participants to provide the statements that answer the question provided and then rank them to create a prioritised list. The software creates a ranked list of statements in real time during the session. In order to summarise the data across groups, additional qualitative content analysis was undertaken.12 13 The responses and rankings from all groups were combined into master lists, with a separate list for question 1 and question 2. One member of the research (JV) team identified identical statements within each list that could be combined (eg, a question that was raised in more than one group session), and another member (KA) reviewed this list to come to a consensus. The generated statements were then organised into themes based on their similarities.14 15 The framework of themes was developed inductively without an a priori framework. This was done separately by JV and KA and inconsistencies were discussed. Any differences were discussed with additional team members and resolved to come to a consensus. All individual statements from each group and the theme they fall into are provided in online supplemental file.

Results

Participant characteristics

21 participants with an HP diagnosis completed surveys and attended the NGT interview sessions. All 21 provided input regarding their questions, however, due to technical issues, only 20 participants completed the rankings of questions posed during the NGT session. The NGT sessions had a mean duration of 90 min Participants had a mean age of 69.5 and 81% were women, 28% of participants had a high-school degree or lower, 33.4% of participants had an associate or bachelor’s degree and 38.1% of participants had a master’s/professional degree. 85.7% of participants identified as white, 9.5% of participants identified as black, and 4.8% of participants identified as ‘other’. No participants identified as Hispanic or Latino ethnicity.

When asked about current modalities of receiving education about HP, most patients (40%) stated that their preferred method of education about their disease was at a visit with their healthcare provider, and the second most favoured method was virtual education classes (35%). For patients who used the internet, the majority used search engines (57%), with some patients using specific websites (28%) and others using online discussion threads (14%) (table 1).

Identified themes and ranking

A total of 20 participants completed rankings during NGT sessions. Since there were 20 participants and each participant had 3 votes, there were 60 total votes available. There were 6 points available to each participant, resulting in a total of 120 points available for all participants (each participant could assign 3 points to their first choice, 2 points to their second choice and 1 point to their third choice). Question 1 yielded 60 unique responses and question 2 yielded 51 unique responses across all 5 NGT sessions. The top-ranked representative statements from question 1 and 2 are included in tables2 3, respectively. The recorded questions were then grouped into the seven major themes, namely: (1) natural history and prognosis; (2) current treatment options and therapeutic research; (3) epidemiology and aetiology of HP; (4) living well with HP; (5) origin and management of symptoms; (6) identifying and mitigating exposures and (7) methods of educating patients, clinicians and others about HP. Most questions posed by patients fell under the themes of natural history and prognosis, current treatment options and therapeutic research, and epidemiology and aetiology of HP (figure 2). Though the same themes were identified in both questions, the responses within each theme and the rank order of the themes varied between questions 1 and 2 (figure 3).

Questions that fell into the theme ‘natural history and prognosis’ received 33% of total points (33.3% of total votes) for Q1 and 24% of total points (20% of total votes) for Q2. These questions focused on life expectancy, the possibility of recovery and what progression looks like in HP.

Questions that fell into the theme ‘current treatment options and therapeutic research’ received 14% of total points (13.3% of total votes) for Q1 and 31% of total points (31.6% of total votes) for Q2. This category included questions about the currently available medications for HP, alternative remedies, and the ‘standard’ treatment protocol for HP.

Questions that fell into the theme ‘epidemiology and aetiology’ received 16% of total points for Q1 (16.6% of total votes) and 19% of total points for Q2 (23.3% of total votes). These questions focused on the possible causes of HP as well as background information about HP, terminology of HP versus ILD versus pulmonary fibrosis, and how HP may relate to other conditions like autoimmune disease.

Questions that fell into the theme ‘living well with HP’ received 15% of total points (13.3% of total votes) for Q1 and 16% of total points (13.3% of total votes) for Q2. This category included questions about lifestyle habits that could help manage HP symptoms, managing the need for oxygen and lifestyle habits that may potentially slow the progression of HP.

Questions that fell into the theme ‘origin and management of symptoms’ received 13% of total points (13.3% of total votes) for Q1 and 4% of total points (3.3% of total votes) for Q2. This theme covered questions about oxygen requirements as they relate to symptom management and managing breathlessness.

Questions that fell into the theme ‘identifying and mitigating exposures’ received 8% of total points for Q1 (8% of total votes) and 0.8% of total points for Q2 (1.6% of total votes). These questions addressed awareness about causal antigens and exposures and knowing whether there is ongoing exposure as well as logistics of exposure remediation, including finding reputable home inspection professionals and making changes to one’s home and environment.

Questions that fell into the theme ‘methods of educating patients, clinicians and others about HP’ received 1.6% of total points (1.6% of total votes) for Q1 and 5% of total points (6.6% of total votes) for Q2. These questions focused on how to educate doctors about HP, how to educate family and friends about HP, and how to find reputable information about HP.

Discussion

This study used the NGT to elicit the top questions about HP from the perspective of patients living with the disease. Our findings reveal the questions that are most important to patients and elucidate areas that require further attention from clinicians and researchers. Seven key themes emerged from the group interviews, highlighting stakeholder-identified areas that can be targeted to reduce knowledge gaps that are important to patients. Many patients’ concerns centre around a lack of understanding about prognostication, aetiology and exposures, treatment options, and how to live well with HP.

A highly prioritised theme was current treatment options and therapeutic research. Participants expressed confusion about what the treatment protocol was for HP. This is an area of limited clarity for clinicians as well, as there are currently no clinical practice guidelines on pharmacological treatment for HP. While corticosteroids and immunosuppressants continue to be used for HP, this is mostly based on small studies and observational data, and there is no set guidance about the dosage, duration and long-term efficacy of these medications due to a lack of robust clinical trials.7 16 17 It may be challenging for clinicians to communicate this uncertainty effectively to patients.18 The only Food and Drug Administration (FDA)-approved treatment for patients who have fibrotic HP is nintedanib, which has only been shown to slow the progression of the forced vital capacity in those with progressive fibrotic disease.19 The scarcity of effective medications and treatment guidelines points to the urgent need to prioritise the conduct of clinical trials on pharmacologic treatments for HP and their outcomes. Participants also had questions about whether there were alternatives to steroid medications, including herbal medications or supplements. Given the severe side effects of steroids that often have a significant impact on patients’ quality of life, alternatives that have limited side effects need to be investigated.

Additional questions that emerged from the NGT sessions concerned the epidemiology and aetiology of HP and the identification and mitigation of exposures. Many participants were worried about not knowing the cause of their HP, as this leads to a lack of clarity on what steps they could take to improve their health given that recovery hinges on avoiding one’s exposure. Questions in the epidemiology and aetiology theme reflected the nebulous aetiology of HP, with a top question being ‘Why does it keep coming back even after I remediated my home, and have been on medication?’ and ‘What is the cause of my HP? If I don’t know do I live in a bubble?’ Questions categorised under the identification and mitigation of exposures were more focused on practical measures like ‘How often should I be having a mold inspection?’ The prevalence of these concerns reinforces the need for better methods of antigen identification and exposure mitigation.20 Moreover, considering the expensive and elaborate procedures required for exposure avoidance and mitigation, more accessible and practical methods are needed, as well as improved guidance for clinicians to counsel their patients on this topic.21 Questions about the nomenclature of HP versus ILD versus pulmonary fibrosis were also asked, categorised under epidemiology and aetiology. One participant wanted to know, ‘How different is HP from interstitial lung disease? Is it a death sentence?’ and others also expressed being devastated at the life expectancy of a few years that came up when they looked up their ILD diagnosis on the Internet. More clarity about the differences between the various aetiologies of ILD, risk of progression and overall survival is likely important to help convey appropriate expectations to patients when they are diagnosed.

Another major theme was about living well with HP. Participants expressed a desire to know more about steps they could take on their own to improve their health, concerning topics like exercise and symptom management. More lifestyle-focused education is likely crucial to empowering patients with HP to feel more in control of their health, especially as they are faced with so much uncertainty about prognosis and treatment. A major barrier to lifestyle education is that there is little time to devote to this kind of education in an office visit, which lasts about 21 min on average.22 Other resources that can be deployed outside of an office visit such as online education or group education classes have been shown to be a successful method of education in IPF and chronic obstructive pulmonary disease (COPD) and offer promising models for intervention development in HP and ILD.23 24 Currently, pulmonary rehabilitation is a major intervention used in ILD and provides education on improving their lifestyle with regards to oxygen use, physical functionality and more.25 However, a cited problem has been that these pulmonary rehabilitation programmes do not provide specific education for ILD, suggesting that there is even less HP-specific education.26 The findings from this study can help inform the development of more HP-specific educational interventions.

Our findings suggest that the majority of patients rely on internet search engines for information. The preference for search engines for health-related inquiries has been confirmed by other reports.27 28 As internet search engines can often yield unreliable information, these findings suggest the necessity for not only developing high-quality educational resources but for making them more accessible and known to patients. The internet has been documented as a poor source of patient-centred information for ILD due to incomplete and outdated content.29 30 Still, there are a few helpful online patient resources from reputed academic institutions and foundations on HP.31 Aside from the standard overview of the disease and its treatments, some resources go a step further to empower patients to take control of their health. Notably, the CHEST Foundation and American Lung Association websites include information to prepare patients for their visit with the physician, including a list of questions they can ask their doctor.32 33 Academic institutions have also put forth effort in this domain. For example, UCSF Health features lifestyle-focused education and has a nutrition manual for ILD, including tips for managing the side effects of prednisone.34 More such comprehensive and vetted resources that address patients’ specific questions regarding topics such as exposure remediation, treatment options and lifestyle are needed. These resources can also be transparent in addressing how many questions about exposure identification and avoidance remain unanswered and provide information on areas of active investigation that are aimed at helping to answer these questions for patients and clinicians.

The two questions posed during the NGT sessions elicited slightly different answers from participants. The first question, ‘What questions about your HP do you have that keep you awake at night?’ was intended to identify the most critical questions that cause participants distress and uncertainty. This question evoked more existential responses and more questions related to prognostication, with some of the top voted questions being ‘Is this going to kill me?’ and ‘Am I going to get off this oxygen?’. In contrast, the second question, ‘What information do you want about your HP that you cannot find?’ generated more practical, concrete questions that can be answered with evidence such as ‘What are the recognised stages of progression of HP?’ and ‘What can I do to be on less oxygen?’.

Strengths of this study include recruitment of participants from more than one centre, and the diversity of educational levels among participants. This study also had limitations. Due to the low number of available participants given the rare nature of HP, and recruitment from tertiary referral centres, our cohort did not include many men and many participants who identified as black race and/or Hispanic ethnicity. While the cohort included participants with diverse educational levels, a significant number of participants had advanced degrees, which may make these results less generalisable towards the overall population. About half of the participants were on supplemental oxygen, a marker for more severe disease, which may have informed the prioritisation of the questions in this study, such as the high prioritisation of questions about oxygen use. The results of this study may thus be less generalisable towards patients with milder forms of the disease.
