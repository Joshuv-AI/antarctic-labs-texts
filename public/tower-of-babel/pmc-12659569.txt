Measurement of three‐dimensional facial changes after orthodontic extraction: a retrospective longitudinal study
Authors: Weiqiang Wu, Yu Wang, Wenhsuan Lu, Jiale Yan, Jade Teng, Lanxi Nie, Yao Tang, Bing Han, Si Chen, Yuning Zhang, Wei Liang, Xiaomo Liu, Yunfan Zhang
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundThis study aimed to assess the three-dimensional facial morphological changes and acquire quantitative relationship between facial landmarks and zygion point to analyze the overall facial soft tissue impact of extraction and non-extraction orthodontic treatment.MethodsA total of 21 extraction (19.5 ± 9.2 years) and 21 non-extraction (26.2 ± 9.1 years) versus consecutive patients were enrolled. The pretreatment and posttreatment 3D facial scans were captured using the FaceSCAN3D optical system. After establishing the coordinate system and manually placing landmark, fourteen linear and twelve angular measurements were obtained. Additionally, sixteen projection distances between landmarks and the Zygion point were measured. Color-coded displacement maps were conducted through surface registration for visualization and qualitatively assessed facial changes.ResultsIn extraction group, inter-cheek width (2.60 ± 5.64 mm, P = 0.050) and mouth width (1.92 ± 3.89 mm, P = 0.040) increased, while upper lip fullness (-0.88 ± 1.11 mm, P = 0.003), lower lip fullness(-0.55 ± 1.21 mm, P = 0.050), and Li-N’-Pg’ angle (-1.55 ± 1.09°, P < 0.001) decreased (T1-T0). As for quantitative measurements, the backward movement of landmarks in paranasal area (bilateral Al [nasal ala]: -1.14 ± 1.84 mm and -1.14 ± 1.47 mm, P = 0.010 and P = 0.002, respectively; bilateral Subal [subnasal ala]: -1.23 ± 2.18 mm and -1.25 ± 1.46 mm, P = 0.018 and P = 0.001, respectively) was observed. In the cheek area, significant changes were found in: left Ck-i [cheek-inner]: -1.40 ± 2.18 mm, P = 0.008; right Ck-m (cheek-middle): -2.32 ± 2.71 mm, P = 0.001), right Ck-o [cheek-outer]: -2.65 ± 4.05 mm, P = 0.007). In the lip area, changes were noted in: (bilateral Chp [chresta philtri]: P < 0.001, bilateral Ch [cheilion]: P < 0.001, Ls (labrale superius]: P = 0.002, Stm [stomion]: P < 0.001, and Li [labrale inferius]: P = 0.002. All changes observed were measured relative to the Zygion point in extraction group.ConclusionsThis study reveals the effects of extraction and non-extraction treatments on the lip, cheek and paranasal areas. Significant differences were found in the linear and angular measurements. The lower lip retraction was more pronounced in the extraction group. A new quantitative method for measuring facial landmarks was established. Inward and downward movement of facial landmarks was observed after extraction treatment.Trial registrationThe protocol of this study (PKUSSIRB-202392145) was registered at the Chinese Clinical Trial Registry (Chictr.org.cn) with the identifier ChiCTR2000034288 (01/07/2020). Background

Facial aesthetics play a considerable role in social behavior. With growing aesthetic awareness, more people are seeking orthodontic treatment. Both orthodontists and patients have placed increasing emphasis on changes in facial soft tissue [1].

Orthodontic extraction treatment is commonly used to achieve a more harmonious and attractive facial profile [2]. Numerous studies have used two-dimensional (2D) imaging to evaluate facial changes after extraction in patients with facial protrusion, but their conclusions remain inconsistent [3]. It is commonly believed that extraction tends to flatten the face, while non-extraction patients results in a fuller appearance [4]. However, these two-dimensional studies focused primarily on sagittal changes, and lacked the capacity to evaluate specific anatomical regions [5]. Orthodontists and patients are increasingly interested in understanding the three-dimensional (3D) changes at various soft tissue anatomical points following extraction treatment [6].

With the emergence of stereo-photography technology, 3D facial scanning has emerged as an accurate, non-invasive, and rapid tool for evaluating soft tissue changes. This technology allows orthodontists to measure distance, angle, volume on 3D images using computer software [7]. The capture time is about 1.5 ms, allowing patients to remain still and minimizing motion artifacts [8].

Influenced by social media, some adult patients have expressed dissatisfaction with post-treatment facial changes, such as hollow paranasal region, protruding cheekbones, and sunken temples, which were often referred to as “braces face” or “bracket face” [9, 10]. Previous three-dimensional studies mainly focused on directional change of soft tissue, especially in the lips region, where orthodontic treatment changes are most significant [5, 10–12]. However, patients are often more concerned about the magnitude of changes. Many desire only improvements around their lips without adverse changes in other areas that might disrupt overall facial harmony. Some studies also focused on quantifying changes in various anatomical face regions, but the research samples were mainly limited to adult females and used varying measurement methods [6, 9]. In this study, a novel approach was used. A stable anatomical landmark was chosen and quantitative relationships between this point and other facial landmarks were measured. We aim to better understand how facial landmarks change during orthodontic treatment and provide meaningful reference for orthodontists and patients.

Therefore, the aim of the present study was to assess the 3D facial morphological changes following extraction and non-extraction orthodontic treatment. This study also explores the quantitative relationship between facial landmarks and Zygion points to analyze the overall facial soft tissue impact. The null hypothesis was that no soft tissue changes would occur after either extraction or non-extraction orthodontic treatments, and that both treatments have the same impact on the soft tissues as analyzed by 3D facial scans.

Methods

Study sample

This was a retrospective longitudinal study approved by the Institutional Review Board of Peking University School and Hospital of Stomatology (PKUSSIRB-202392145). Consecutive patients were enrolled between August 2020 and July 2024 during their initial visit to the Department of Orthodontics, Peking University, School and Hospital of Stomatology, Beijing, China. Written informed consent was provided to patients or their legal guardian before participating in the study.

Inclusion criteria were as follows: (1) Chinese individual aged 14 to 35 years at the beginning of the treatment; (2) ANB angle between −1.3° and 6.7° (i.e. no severe skeletal deformities); (3) the treatment group include patients treated with four premolars extracted and average anterior tooth retraction greater than 2 mm (measured as the distance from the upper and lower incisor points to the GALL line on the lateral cephalogram), meanwhile the patients in control group were treated without extraction; (4) overall good health; and (5) body mass index (BMI) within 18 to 24 kg/m2.

Exclusion criteria were as follows: (1) previously received orthodontic treatment (excluding early treatment); (2) interproximal enamel reduction performed; (3) severe facial asymmetry deformity (defined as horizontal chin deviation greater than 3 mm); (4) congenital deformities or syndromes; (5) defects in dentition (except cases where the space of missing teeth was closed, which have not been excluded but considered as extraction cases); (6) combined orthodontic and orthognathic treatment.

Sample size calculation

The sample size was calculated before recruitment using the PASS software (Power Analysis and Sample Size, version 15.0; NCSS Corp., USA). The nasolabial angle was chosen as the primary outcome variable. Based on a previous research by Rongo et al.[12], the standard deviation of the nasolabial angle was approximately 7°. The mean difference between the treatment group and the control group was 5°. Thus, at least 21 patients per group were needed in this study (α = 0.05 and 1-β = 0.9). 42 patients were enrolled in total, including 21 in the extraction group (treatment group) and 21 in the non-extraction group (control group).

Orthodontic treatment

All patients were treated using labial fixed appliances (0.022 × 0.028-inch bracket slot, Roth prescription; Xinya, Hangzhou, China) for a minimum of 12 months. No auxiliary anchorage devices, such as Headgear, J hook, intraoral arch, Nance arch, or transverse palatal arch were used during treatment. For patients in the extraction group, either a sequential or en-masse retraction method was chosen according to corresponding clinical technique used to close the extraction spaces.

Acquisition of 3D facial scans

Three-dimensional facial scans were acquired using the 3D optical FaceSCAN3D system (3D-Shape, Erlangen, Germany) (Fig. 1a). All patients underwent facial scans at two time points: before orthodontic treatment (T0) and after bracket removal (T1). The position of the 3D scanning system and patient was fixed at both T0 and T1. Fig. 1Obtaining 3D facial scan images. a. FaceSCAN3D scanning system (b). Preview of scanned 3D facial image

Obtaining 3D facial scan images. a. FaceSCAN3D scanning system (b). Preview of scanned 3D facial image

All scans were performed by a trained PhD student (W.Q.W) under standardized conditions. Patients were instructed to sit in a natural head position approximately 300 cm from the scanner. Makeup and accessories were not allowed. Scans were taken in intercuspal position (ICP), and subjects were asked to maintain the sound of ‘mm’ (as in the pronunciation of “Emma”) throughout the scan to capture the lips at rest [13]. Three dimensional facial images were obtained through machine scanning and previewed using the corresponding software (Fig. 1b).

Coordinate system establishment

The coordinate system was established following the method described by Shen et al. [11] The 3D scans were imported into the software Geomagic Control X 2023 (3D Systems, Rock Hill, South Carolina). The origin of the coordinate system where all three planes intersect was set at the midpoint of the inner canthi of the eyes.

The X axis (XZ plane) was defined to include bilateral pupils and soft tissue nasion. The Y axis (XY plane) was the vertical plane, which contained the N-subnasal (Sn) line and equally divided the facial structure. The Z axis (XZ plane) was set perpendicular to the first two axes (Fig. 2).Fig. 2Schematic diagram of the coordinate system: red: X-axis, green: Y-axis, blue: Z-axis

Quantitative analysis for movement of landmarks

Previous studies have found that the zygonion (Zy) point in the malar region is a relatively stable landmark during orthodontic treatment in the absence of growth [9, 10, 17, 18]. In this study, t he relative distances between sixteen landmarks (Al, Subal, Chp, Ch, Ck-i, Ck-m, Ck-o, Go, N’, Prn, Sn, Ls, Stm, Li, B’, Pg’) and Zy point were measured for quantitative analysis. Using the coordinate system, the projection distance between each point and the three planes (i.e. XY, XZ, and YZ planes) passing through Zy point were recorded. For bilateral landmarks, the projection distance between the Zy point and the landmark on the corresponding side was measured. For landmarks in the midline, the average of the projected distances between the bilateral Zy points and the corresponding landmarks on each side was calculated. Superimposition of 3D facial images

3D images were imported into the software Geomagic Studio 2013 (3D Systems, Rock Hill, South Carolina) for superimposition. Images were manually registered, with the T0 image chosen as the reference image and T1 images as the floating image. The forehead, nasal bridge, and malar areas were manually selected as registration reference areas for pre- and post-treatment 3D facial images superimposition [19]. The range of selected areas are shown in Fig. 6. Following region selection, the surface registration function of the software was used to automatically register the T0 and T1 images. After region registration, color-coded displacement maps were generated to visualize soft tissue displacement along the direction of the surface normal (Fig. 6). Qualitative and quantitative analysis of the distance between T0 and T1 images were implemented by means of absolute color mapping. Green indicated that the soft tissue change did not exceed the allowable deviation level. Blue or cyan indicated that the soft tissue shape became flat or depressed with a negative reading. Red, orange or yellow indicated a more convex soft tissue profile with a positive reading. Statistical analysis

Statistical analyses were computed using the SPSS software (Statistical Package for Social Sciences, version 23.0; IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA). The Shapiro–Wilk test was used to assess the normality of the data from 3D facial scans. The significance level was set at P < 0.05. The Levene’s test was used to verify the homogeneity of variance before and after treatment. A paired t-test was performed to evaluate facial changes after orthodontic treatment for both groups. An independent t-test was carried out to compare the treating group and control group.

Intra-observer and inter-observer reliability

All measurements were repeated by three operators (WWQ, LWX, and LXM). To assess intra-observer reliability, one operator re-measured 20 randomly selected 3D scans after an interval of more than two weeks. Both intra-observer and inter-observer reliability were assessed using the 2-way absolute agreement intraclass correlation coefficient (ICC) based on the mean of measurements. An ICC value ≥ 0.80 was considered acceptable for measurement reliability.

Results

Sample description

A total of 42 patients (15 males, 27 females; mean age: 22.9 years ± 9.6 years) were included in the study. The extraction group consisted of 21 patients (7 males, 14 females; mean age: 19.5 years ± 9.2 years) Among them, 18 patients underwent four premolar extractions, 1 patient had two upper premolar extractions, 2 patients had premolar and deciduous teeth extractions. The non-extraction group also included 21 patients (8 males, 13 females; mean age: 26.2 years ± 9.1 years). Sample description and details are shown in Table 2.

Error of the method

Intraclass correlation coefficient (ICC) was calculated for each measurement respectively. For landmark placement, all ICC values exceeded 0.95 (Table S1 and S3), indicating a good consistency. The gonion points showed the lowest consistency among the landmarks.

For linear and angular measurements, all ICC values met the acceptability threshold above 0.80. For intra-observer reliability (Table S2), the lowest consistency in linear measurements was observed in: cupid’s bow width (average ICC values of 0.867, 0.823, and 0.873 for both sides of three operators) and lower lip fullness (average ICC values of 0.861, 0.881, and 0.866 for both sides of three operators). Meanwhile the lowest consistency in angle measurements was observed in: Ls-N '- Sn (average ICC values of 0.822, 0.894, and 0.858 of three operators) and Ls-N'—Li (average ICC values of 0.898, 0.911, and 0.824 of three operators). For inter-observer reliability (Table S4), the poorest consistency in linear measurements was observed in: cupid’s bow width (average ICC values of 0.942) and lower lip fullness (average ICC values of 0.955), On the other hand, the lowest consistency in angle measurements was observed in: Ls-N '- Sn (average ICC values of 0.957) and Ls-N'—Li (average ICC values of 0.976).

Discussion

In order to accurately evaluate the soft tissue changes caused by growth or treatment, superimposition of three-dimensional facial scans should rely on reference points or areas that are remain morphologically stable over time[6]. Previous research has identified the f orehead, nasal bridge, and malar areas as anatomically stable area, which formed the basis for the superimposition in this study. In addition, surface-based registration technology has been shown to offer greater reliability than landmark-based technology[19–21]. Hence, we use forehead, nasal bridge, and malar areas as regions of interest (ROI) to superimpose 3D images. Furthermore, several stable anatomical features have drawn the attention of researchers. Many studies have found that the soft tissue of the malar area remains largely unchanged during orthodontic treatment. The zygion point also maintained a stable position on the face [9, 10, 17]. Based on these findings, we choose this stable point as the reference for subsequent quantitative measurements.

When establishing the coordinate system, accuracy and repeatability were prioritized. Inspired by the research of Shen et al. [11], landmarks with small measurement errors (bilateral pupils, soft tissue nasion, and subnasal) were first considered. Meanwhile, the direction of the coordinate system was established to align with typical clinical and social contexts, making the results more applicable to clinical diagnosis and treatment [22]. The sagittal direction was also consistent with the SN plane, which helps explain growth-related changes in patients with development potential [23].

Previous 3D studies have focused on changes in the lip soft tissue. Shen et al. [11] found that the philtrum width, mouth height and lower vermilion area decreased, while the mouth width and upper vermilion area remained stable after the extraction treatment. Similarly, Liu et al. [5] found that the lip vermilion height decreased after extraction orthodontic treatment, which is consistent with our findings. However, they also confirmed that the vermilion morphology remained unchanged after non-extraction treatment, which differs from our measurements. In our cases, the lower lip height decreased in the non-extraction group, although the difference is smaller compared to the extraction group. Additionally, the mouth width, the cupid’s bow width and the upper lip height increased in the non-extraction group, which is related to the upper lip protrusion and the reduced lip tension. A decrease in the right lower lip angle in the non-extraction group further revealed the change in lip morphology.

Extraction treatment also appeared to affect profile angle. In Rongo et al.’s [12] study, the extraction group showed a more significant reduction in nasolabial angle. But in our study, this angle shows no significant difference between the two groups. Rongo et al. [12] also observed reductions in both Ls-N’-Pg’ and Li-N’-Pg’ after extraction treatment. However, in our study, only Li-N’-Pg’ (P < 0.001) became smaller after extraction treatment. Ls-N’-Pg’ (P = 0.51) showed no significant difference. This indicates that extraction has a greater effect on lower lip retraction within our samples.

This study is one of the few to examine multi-regional changes of lower facial soft tissue after orthodontic treatment. The phenomenon known as “bracket face” does exist during orthodontic procedures. In popular belief, extraction orthodontic treatment is often associate with a flatter facial feature, while non-extraction patients does not experience significant changes in their appearance [4]. However, with our limited samples, some adults do experience cheek depression after orthodontic treatment, whether with or without extraction (Fig. 8). Gao et al. [9] found no significant change in the paranasal and buccal area during extraction treatment. Hou et al.’s [10] 3D geometric morphometric study revealed invaginated buccal and temporal region during alignment stage. Similarly, our study found that depressions occurred in the paranasal and buccal areas after orthodontic treatment. The landmarks Al, Subal and Ck-i/m/o, have shifted backwards in the sagittal direction relative to Zy point. Additionally, in the X-axis direction, cheek width increased in the extraction group, indicating remodeling of soft tissue around Ck-m point.

The occurrence of “bracket face” often raises concerns among both orthodontists and patients. Many patients hope to improve the profile without experiencing facial depression [24]. Many factors may increase the possibility of developing “bracket face”. While the potential adverse effects of extraction on facial contour have long been a shared concern [25], our study also identified man cases of "bracket face” in the non-extraction group [25]. Jin et al. [26] identified female patients over the age of 30 as being at higher risk. In our sample, about 1/4 to 1/3 of participants were over 30 years old, which leads to changes in soft tissue landmarks in the paranasal and cheek areas. In addition, Jin et al. [26] also believed that the change in vertical height may be related to the depression of the lower cheek area. We found the facial height increased after both extraction and non-extraction treatment, which preliminarily supports this hypothesis. Although inward changes were observed in color-coded displacement maps our study did not conduct quantitative measurements on temporal areas. We focused primarily on the middle and lower parts of face, as these areas are more susceptible to the effects of orthodontic treatment [27]. Simultaneously, both clinicians and patients are particularly concerned about changes in these areas [28].

We also found vertical changes in facial landmarks. Specifically, in the extraction group, the bilateral Al points showed significant downward movement along the Y-axis relative to the Zy point in the extraction group. To our knowledge, this is currently the first study on the vertical change of this landmark point. In addition, we investigated changes in the buccal area. Qiao et al. [6] measured evenly distributed points within the target buccal area and found that the mean Y-axis displacement showed an upward trend after extraction. However, in our study, we focused on special anatomical landmarks in this area. We found that Ck-i points moved downwards in the extraction group. Baek et al. [29] also studied the change in soft tissue cheek point using CBCT data in both extraction and non-extraction patients. They found that cheek point moved downwards in retraction group under 30 years old, However, their defined cheek point was located at the lower edge of the zygomatic arch, which is higher than the landmarks used in our study.

Effective vertical control plays an important role in improving facial profile and has always been a major focus for orthodontists [30]. Shafique et al. [31] found that tooth extraction may lead to an increase in vertical dimension. In our study, higher facial heights were observed during orthodontic treatment for both groups. However, Guo et al.’s [32] study suggests that with the use of additional anchorage for vertical control, vertical dimensions could be significantly reduced. Hans et al. [33] reported no increase in mandibular vertical height when using standard edgewise or Tweed edgewise treatment with extraction of four first molars. Gao et al. [9] also found no significant change in the facial vertical height in the extraction group. At the same time, we found that the Pg’ point moved downward in extraction group during treatment. Compared with Rongo et al.’s [12] study, we observed a higher increment in downward shift of Pg’ points, which may be related to different proportions of growing individuals. However, current evidence cannot indicate that extraction or non-extraction treatment will significantly alter the vertical dimensions [34].

We observed bilateral differences in certain measurements during orthodontic treatment, which is a phenomenon that has also appeared in other studies [5, 11, 12]. Asymmetry is very common in orthodontic treatment and minor asymmetry is difficult for visual detection [35]. Recently, there is an increase in patients' willingness to improve asymmetry. In some cases, asymmetry may be reduced after orthodontic treatment [36, 37]. In the sagittal directions, the cheek area near the lips shows significant backward movement on the left side, while no such change was observed on the right side. However, in the area farther from the lips, the opposite result was observed. These asymmetric changes may be related to differences in bilateral occlusion, choice of extraction, and treatment plans. Meanwhile, some studies suggested symmetric extraction patterns will cause greater changes in profile, whereas asymmetric patterns may reduce the risk of facial flattening [38, 39]. In future studies, CBCT imaging will be essential to detect the remodeling of hard tissues and their impact on corresponding soft tissues [40].

Due to the limited sample size, subgroup analysis could not be performed in this study. Wysong et al. [41, 42] discovered different patterns of facial soft tissue loss during aging in male and female through magnetic resonance imaging. The findings emphasize the importance of conducting subgroup analysis in future. In addition, the soft tissue analysis of extraction cases did not consider the skeletal pattern. Previous studies have shown that Class II and Class III patients exhibit different patterns of soft tissue change after orthodontic treatment [40]. Furthermore, the strength of anchorage, incisor inclination change and the initial crowding degree should also be taken into consideration.

This study only included Chinese individuals to avoid differences in orthodontic effects across different races. However, this also limits the generalizability of the conclusions. Future studies should incorporate multiple centers and include diverse populations to further validate the results.

Another limitation of this study is the wide age range of samples. Growth may arise unpredictable changes in hard and soft tissue, making it difficult to distinguish treatment effects from natural development [43]. Seven underage patients were included in this study, including five females (ages at T0 were 14, 14, 14, 15, and 16 respectively) and two male (both ages at T0 were 16). Among the 7 adolescents, there were no Class III skeletal patients. Two 14-year-old females, one 15-year-old female, and one 16-year-old male underwent extraction treatment, while the remaining patients underwent non-extraction treatment. In Rongo et al.’s [12] study, three subjects under the age of 18 were also included and had irrelevant influence on results. We believe that a small number of teens have a relatively small impact on the overall results. Nevertheless, facial soft tissue may undergo age-related changes as individuals grow older. The broad age range and the imbalance in age distribution between the extracted and non-extracted groups may have introduced confounding effects.

Furthermore, this study only used three-dimensional facial scans to study the effectiveness of orthodontic treatment. Although it is currently the gold standard for facial soft tissue analysis of three-dimensional studies [44], it is impossible to explore the mechanisms that cause soft tissue changes without the assistance of cone-beam computed tomography (CBCT). To address this limitation, our ongoing prospective cohort study is currently collecting more subjects who received both CBCT and 3D facial scan simultaneously to explore the correlation between soft and hard tissue changes. Long term follow-up with large samples is necessary to clarify the causes of facial soft tissue changes.
