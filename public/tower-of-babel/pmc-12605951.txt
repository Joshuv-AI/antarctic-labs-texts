Successful Treatment of Post‐SARS‐CoV‐2 Infection Reactive Arthritis With Secukinumab: A Case Report and Literature Review
Authors: Qi Zhao, Tianyu Yao, Danyue He, Jie Hu, Yulin Wang, Ruyi Wang, Yiyang Hu, Chengyu Li, Jia Cai, Xiangning Li, Lili Tang, Xing Fan
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTThis case describes a rare occurrence of post‐SARS‐CoV‐2 infection reactive arthritis with psoriasiform lesions. The patient failed to respond to conventional therapies but responded well to secukinumab, underscoring that it may be a valuable treatment for refractory post‐SARS‐CoV‐2 infection reactive arthritis when conventional therapies fail.

Introduction

Reactive arthritis (ReA) typically presents as an aseptic oligoarthritis that develops within 1–6 weeks after an antecedent extra‐articular infection, most commonly associated with gastrointestinal or genitourinary infection [1]. Although less frequently reported in the literature, infections of the respiratory tract have also been identified as potential triggers for ReA, broadening the spectrum of causative pathogens in this condition.

Coronavirus Disease 2019 (COVID‐19), caused by Severe Acute Respiratory Syndrome Coronavirus 2 (SARS‐CoV‐2), is a pandemic‐associated illness with a diverse clinical presentation, ranging from mild respiratory symptoms to multisystemic inflammatory syndromes. Emerging evidence suggests that post‐viral ReA may occur as a complication following SARS‐CoV‐2 infection. This form of ReA is defined by sterile joint inflammation occurring after viral infections, without evidence of direct microbial invasion into the affected joints. Management of ReA predominantly relies on nonsteroidal anti‐inflammatory drugs (NSAIDs) and corticosteroids as first‐line therapeutic agents to alleviate inflammation and symptoms. However, biologic therapies in ReA remain sparsely reported in the current literature [2, 3, 4].

Case History

A 28‐year‐old Chinese female presented to our hospital on August 16, 2024, with complaints of rash, joint pain, fever, and general malaise. Her medical history revealed that she had previously been hospitalized 6 days earlier due to bilateral lower limb weakness. During the prior hospitalization, her rash had progressively worsened and a nucleic acid test for SARS‐CoV‐2 returned positive. Consequently, she was referred to our institution for further evaluation and management. On admission, she reported morning stiffness lasting less than 1 h. Additional symptoms were documented, including the persistence of joint pain and systemic malaise, which were exacerbating her functional limitations. Her past medical history was significant for a prior episode of uveitis 6 years ago, which was managed with cyclosporine; immunosuppressive therapy had been discontinued 1 year prior to the current presentation. Additionally, the patient reported a recent left hip trauma sustained in July.

On physical examination, her vital signs were stable. Dermatological assessment demonstrated widespread erythematous papules and plaques with overlying silvery‐white scales, distributed over the scalp, face, trunk, and extremities. Hair tufting was noted; however, nail involvement was absent. The musculoskeletal examination revealed marked swelling in both knees and right ankles, and a reduced range of motion in the left hip, culminating in functional impairment of the lower limbs.

Investigation and Treatment

Laboratory investigations revealed markers of systemic inflammation, including a white blood cell (WBC) count of 11.20 × 109/L, C‐reactive protein (CRP) of 91.20 mg/L, erythrocyte sedimentation rate (ESR) of 128 mm/h, and interleukin‐6 (IL‐6) level of 64.80 pg/mL. SARS‐CoV‐2 testing by nucleic acid amplification was positive at the time of admission. Genetic testing for HLA‐B27 was negative. Epstein–Barr virus (EBV), rheumatoid factor (RF) and antinuclear antibody (ANA) tests were all negative. Comprehensive immunological evaluation revealed no abnormalities.

Serological screening for acute respiratory tract infections, including IgG and IgM antibodies against Chlamydia pneumoniae and Mycoplasma pneumoniae yielded negative results. Due to ongoing menstruation at the time of admission, a urinary test for Chlamydia trachomatis was not performed.

Arthrocentesis was not performed, as the patient's joint effusions were minimal and not overtly apparent or clinically significant according to musculoskeletal examination and MRI findings, which revealed only a negligible volume of synovial fluid.

Lower extremity vascular Doppler ultrasonography demonstrated normal venous flow and excluded the presence of deep vein thrombosis. Radiographic evaluation of the affected joints revealed no evidence of bone erosion or destructive changes (Figure 1). MRI of the lower limbs identified bilateral knee joint effusions, a left knee meniscal injury, and bilateral ankle tenosynovitis manifested by tendon sheath effusions and peritendinous soft tissue edema (Figure 2).

Histopathological examination of the skin specimen revealed psoriasiform dermatitis, characterized by marked parakeratosis, pronounced acanthosis, and superficial dermal infiltration by lymphocytes (Figure 3).

During hospitalization, the patient developed persistent fever, for which ibuprofen and hydrocortisone sodium succinate were initially administered. However, these interventions did not result in symptomatic improvement. Empirical intravenous antibiotic therapy was subsequently initiated with cefoperazone (4 g/day, August 16–19, 2024) and later with piperacillin‐tazobactam (9 g/day, August 19–26, 2024), but there was no appreciable clinical response. Methylprednisolone was also administered intravenously at a dose of 40 mg/day from August 26 to 28, 2024, again without notable improvement in fever or inflammatory symptoms.

On August 28, 2024, a subcutaneous injection of secukinumab (300 mg) was given. Within 12 h of secukinumab administration, the patient's body temperature normalized. After 48 h post‐injection, there was a substantial reduction in joint swelling and pain, with the visual analog scale (VAS) score for pain decreasing from 6 to 2. Significant improvement in cutaneous lesions was observed within 72 h. One week following secukinumab administration, laboratory analyses demonstrated normalization of inflammatory markers (CRP < 0.5 mg/L). Outcome and Follow‐Up

Following hospital discharge, the patient was maintained on secukinumab therapy (300 mg administered subcutaneously every 4 weeks). During the course of follow‐up, a trial of adalimumab was initiated. However, this was associated with localized injection site reactions—characterized by erythema and swelling, as well as recurrence of psoriasiform cutaneous lesions. Consequently, adalimumab was discontinued and secukinumab treatment was reinstated. In January 2025, the patient experienced a recurrence of uveitis, which responded favorably to surgical intervention. At the most recent follow‐up in June 2025, the patient remained asymptomatic, with full restoration of joint function, as indicated by a Health Assessment Questionnaire‐Disability Index (HAQ‐DI) score of 0. No evidence of disease recurrence was observed at that time.

Discussion

ReA is defined as a sterile inflammatory arthritis that typically occurs secondary to gastrointestinal, genitourinary, or, less commonly, respiratory tract infections. The majority of research to date has focused on urogenital and gastrointestinal pathogens as etiological agents. A historically recognized subset, referred to as “Reiter's Syndrome”, is characterized by the clinical triad of arthritis, urethritis, and conjunctivitis. This syndrome was first described by German physician Hans Reiter in 1916, and the term “Reiter's Arthritis” was later formalized in 1964 to highlight the condition's underlying mechanism as an immune‐mediated response to preceding infection. However, due to ethical concerns regarding Hans Reiter's involvement in war crimes during World War II, the medical community has since shifted towards the use of the term “Reactive Arthritis” in both clinical practice and academic contexts [5]. Currently, there are no universally accepted diagnostic criteria for ReA, underscoring the need for ongoing review of the historical evolution of the concept and existing clinical guidelines. A final diagnosis of post‐SARS‐CoV‐2 infection ReA was established in this patient. A thorough differential diagnosis was conducted and systematically excluded on the basis of clinical presentation, laboratory parameters, and imaging findings.

Septic arthritis was considered but excluded due to the absence of monoarticular involvement, pronounced fever, or severe localized joint inflammation. No purulent synovial effusion was present, blood cultures remained negative, and, due to minimal joint effusion on imaging, synovial fluid aspiration was not performed. There were no clinical features suggestive of septic arthritis.

Acute rheumatic fever was ruled out, as the patient's antistreptolysin O (ASO) titer was negative, and the arthropathy lacked the typical migratory pattern associated with ARF. Additionally, there was no recent history of streptococcal pharyngitis. Crystal‐induced arthritis was excluded given normal serum uric acid levels, the absence of acute monoarthritis or involvement of the first metatarsophalangeal joint, and no evidence of monosodium on microscopic evaluation. Psoriatic arthritis (PsA) was considered due to the presence of psoriasiform skin lesions. However, histopathological examination of the skin failed to demonstrate diagnostic features of PsA, such as Munro microabscesses. Furthermore, there was no personal or family history of psoriasis, and radiographic findings were not consistent with PsA. Ankylosing spondylitis was excluded as the patient exhibited no axial symptoms, such as inflammatory back pain. Furthermore, MRI of the sacroiliac joints demonstrated no evidence of sacroiliitis or structural abnormalities indicative of axial spondylarthritis. Viral arthritis due to common etiological agents was also considered and ruled out. Serological tests for hepatitis B virus, hepatitis C virus, and human immunodeficiency virus (HIV) were negative. The diagnosis of post‐SARS‐CoV‐2 infection ReA was further supported by the temporal relationship with recent COVID‐19 infection and the exclusion of other viral pathogens.

The diagnosis was based on the constellation of clinical manifestations, laboratory and imaging findings, as well as the systematic exclusion of alternative etiologies. However, certain diagnostic limitations merit acknowledgment. Due to minimal synovial effusion in the knee and ankle joints, synovial fluid aspiration and culture could not be performed, which restricts the ability to unequivocally exclude concurrent viral arthritis. Nevertheless, the overall clinical scenario is most consistent with ReA [11, 12, 13].

This case represents a noteworthy instance of ReA temporally associated with SARS‐CoV‐2 infection and underscores the diagnostic and management challenges inherent to post‐SARS‐CoV‐2 infection ReA. Since the COVID‐19 pandemic, numerous case reports have described ReA in the context of SARS‐CoV‐2 infection. Although current evidence remains low in quality, these reports suggest that SARS‐CoV‐2 may be a novel etiological trigger for ReA, highlighting the need for further systematic investigation. A comprehensive systematic review conducted by Namakin et al. analyzed case reports and found that ReA symptoms typically present within one to 6 weeks following SARS‐CoV‐2 infection [11], with the majority of cases occurring in middle‐aged adults. Extra‐articular manifestations, particularly dermatological findings such as urticaria and erythema, were also frequently reported. Additionally, Migliorini et al. observed considerable heterogeneity in the clinical presentation of post‐COVID‐19 ReA, indicating a broad spectrum of phenotypic variance.

In contrast to the patterns described in previous reports, the patient in this case exhibited a distinctive psoriasiform skin eruption, a manifestation that is exceedingly rare in the existing literature. This unique cutaneous presentation further underscores the clinical relevance and novelty of this case [12].

Several pathogenic mechanisms have been proposed to underlie the clinical manifestations observed in post‐SARS‐CoV‐2 infection ReA, including molecular mimicry which may facilitate cross‐reactive immune responses between viral and host antigens, cytokine dysregulation notably involving IL‐6 and tumor necrosis factor‐alpha (TNF‐α), contributing to a hyperinflammatory state, and disruption of the gut–joint axis, possibly mediated by SARS‐CoV‐2‐induced alterations in intestinal microbiota (dysbiosis) [14]. The present case provides a clinical example consistent with these evolving pathogenic concepts.

Compared to previously reported cases, this case demonstrates several distinctive features. Atypical Cutaneous Manifestations: The development of psoriasiform skin lesions represents a significant deviation from the classical presentation of ReA, which is primarily characterized by articular involvement. While dermatologic findings such as purpura, urticaria, and erythema have been described in cases of SARS‐CoV‐2‐associated ReA, psoriasiform eruptions remain rare. To our knowledge, only isolated cases have been described psoriasis‐like eruptions following SARS‐CoV‐2 infection in the literature. This observation broadens the recognized spectrum of cutaneous involvement in post‐viral ReA and underscores the need for heightened clinical awareness of such atypical presentations.HLA‐B27‐Negative Phenotype: Whereas HLA‐B27 positivity is observed in more than 50% of ReA cases—and is especially prevalent in patients with axial spondyloarthritis—this patient was HLA‐B27 negative. This observation aligns with emerging evidence suggesting that HLA‐B27‐negative individuals may experience a milder disease course and a more favorable prognosis, as reflected in the absence of disease recurrence at three‐month follow‐up in this case. In contrast, HLA‐B27 positivity is generally associated with an increased risk of developing ReA, more severe manifestations, and a higher likelihood of chronicity.Refractory Disease Course and Therapeutic Response: The patient exhibited clinical features consistent with refractory disease, including lack of response to corticosteroids, progressive symptomatology, and a pattern of symmetrical polyarthritis involving both knees and the right ankle, despite the absence of radiographic evidence of bone destruction. Notably, administration of secukinumab, an IL‐17A inhibitor, resulted in rapid and substantial symptom improvement (approximately 70% reduction in disease activity within 48 h). This case highlights the potential role of biologic agents—particularly IL‐17A inhibitors—in the management of refractory cases of ReA unresponsive to conventional therapies.

TNF‐α inhibitors, such as adalimumab, have demonstrated efficacy in treating refractory ReA [15]. However, in our case, adalimumab failed to achieve clinical efficacy, which suggests that not all patients respond immediately to adalimumab, and we think their use frequently necessitates long‐term administration. In contrast, secukinumab, a fully human monoclonal antibody that targets interleukin‐17A (IL‐17A), has established efficacy in psoriatic arthritis and ankylosing spondylitis and may offer therapeutic benefit in cases of ReA where IL‐17‐mediated inflammation is predominant. Mechanistically, IL‐17A promotes synovitis, facilitates bone resorption (in part via RANKL activation), and contributes to gut–joint axis dysregulation [16]. Therefore, IL‐17A inhibition represents a targeted approach for select cases of ReA. Consequently, the therapeutic success observed in this patient underscores IL‐17A inhibition as a rational, targeted strategy for ReA, especially in circumstances where TNF‐α proves ineffective.

Escalation to biologic therapy is warranted when NSAIDs and glucocorticoids fail to control symptoms. In the present case, rapid and marked clinical improvement following secukinumab administration aligns with prior reports of efficacy in refractory ReA. Furthermore, the presence of psoriasiform skin lesions provided a specific rationale for the selection of IL‐17A inhibitor therapy over TNF‐α inhibition, as IL‐17 blockade has demonstrated superior efficacy for cutaneous manifestations of this nature.

To date, the majority of the literature on post‐SARS‐CoV‐2 infection ReA has focused on conventional therapies, predominantly NSAIDs and corticosteroids. This report describes a case of refractory post‐SARS‐CoV‐2 infection ReA successfully treated with a biologic agent, thereby offering a novel therapeutic perspective for the management of this condition.

Conclusion

These findings suggest that secukinumab may represent a safe and effective therapeutic option for patients with refractory ReA following SARS‐CoV‐2 infection. In the present case, rapid and substantial resolution of both articular and cutaneous manifestations was observed following IL‐17A inhibition. However, larger prospective studies are essential to further evaluate the efficacy and safety of secukinumab and to inform evidence‐based treatment protocols for post‐SARS‐CoV‐2 infection ReA.
