Efficacy of opioid-free anesthesia in reducing postoperative adverse events for thoracoscopic pulmonary surgery: a randomized controlled trial
Authors: Ran Zhang, Yaqing Wu, Xiaoyi Wang, Weixin Zhang, Yi Feng
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundThis study compared the effects of opiate-free anesthesia (OFA) using thoracic paravertebral block combined with dexmedetomidine versus traditional anesthesia with sufentanil and remifentanil on postoperative adverse reactions after thoracoscopic lung surgery.MethodsA prospective, randomized, double-blind trial included 97 patients(49 in OFA group,48 in S group).All received preoperative thoracic paravertebral block.The primary outcome was the incidence of opioid-related adverse reactions (hypoxemia, hypotension, nausea, vomiting, dizziness) at 2,4,6,24,and 48 h postoperatively.Secondary outcomes included intraoperative events, pain scores, opioid consumption, time to first flatus/defecation, and cardiovascular complications.ResultsAt 2 h postoperatively, hypotension incidence was lower in the OFA group(8.2%vs 33.3%,P = 0.002),with fewer overall adverse reactions (18.4%vs 39.6%,P = 0.018).At 24 h, PONV was 0%in OFA vs. 10.4%in S group(P = 0.027),dizziness was 8.2%vs 22.9%(P = 0.041),and overall adverse reactions were 10.2%vs 27.1%(P = 0.029).OFA had fewer vasopressor applications (P < 0.001), lower 24-hour opioid use (P = 0.033), fewer analgesic pump presses(P = 0.004),and earlier first feeding (9.5 ± 3.8 h vs. 11.4 ± 4.7 h, P = 0.035).ConclusionOFA significantly reduced opioid-related adverse reactions, decreased vasopressor and opioid use, and improved recovery.However, the single-center, small-sample design limits generalizability, and multicenter validation is needed.Trial registrationThis randomized controlled trial was registered on clinicaltrials.gov (ID: NCT04507165; URL: https://clinicaltrials.gov/study/NCT04507165, 11/08/2020).Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12871-025-03497-x.Keywords: Opioid-Free anesthesia (OFA), Thoracoscopic pulmonary surgery, Thoracic paravertebral block, Multimodal analgesia

Introduction

The routine use of opioids during anesthesia is currently the most common practice. However, in recent years, this approach has been challenged [1–6]. Some studies indicate that opioid-free anesthesia may provide adequate analgesia while reducing postoperative opioid consumption and the incidence of opioid-induced adverse reactions [7–10].

Previous research suggests that combining α2 receptor agonists [11], NMDA receptor antagonists [12], local anesthetics (e.g., lidocaine) [13], and regional block techniques [14] may replace opioids to achieve intraoperative hemodynamic stability and analgesia. Some studies have shown that opioid-free anesthesia (OFA) can significantly reduce morphine consumption within 24 h after laparoscopic colorectal surgery [15] and lower the relative risk of postoperative nausea and vomiting (PONV) by 40% in bariatric surgery [16]. However, in the field of thoracoscopic surgery, existing research conclusions show significant heterogeneity. Some studies have found no difference in the incidence of PONV within 48 h between the OFA group (25.6%) and the traditional group (33.0%) [17], while the latest randomized controlled trial (RCT) confirmed that OFA can significantly reduce the incidence of PONV (15% vs. 31.7%) and postoperative opioid demand [18]. Due to this controversy, clinicians remain uncertain about the benefits and risks of this technology.

To resolve this controversy, we conducted a study aiming to test the following hypothesis: Compared with sufentanil combined with remifentanil-based balanced anesthesia, opioid-free anesthesia using thoracic paravertebral block combined with dexmedetomidine can reduce the incidence of postoperative opioid-related adverse events. The selection of opioid-related side effects as the primary outcome indicator is based on clinical significance to further explore the potential advantages of opioid-free anesthesia in thoracoscopic pulmonary surgery.

Methods

Ethics and study design

This randomized controlled trial was approved by the Ethics Committee of Peking University People’s Hospital (approval number: 2020PHB031-01) and registered on clinicaltrials.gov (ID: NCT04507165; URL: https://clinicaltrials.gov/study/NCT04507165) before the first patient was enrolled. The study followed the Declaration of Helsinki and the CONSORT guidelines (supplementary materials). All included patients signed informed consent forms. No modifications were made to the study protocol during its implementation.

Participants

Patients aged ≥ 18 years, with ASA physical status I-II, and scheduled for elective thoracoscopic pulmonary resection were included. Exclusion criteria included: pregnant women, ASA grade ≥ III, emergency surgery, planned thoracotomy, adults under legal protection or with restricted freedom, a previous history of general anesthesia, atrioventricular block, sinoatrial block, intraventricular conduction block, sinus bradycardia (heart rate < 60 beats per minute), preoperative hypotension (systolic blood pressure < 90 mmHg), preoperative vestibular dysfunction, Meniere’s disease, vertebral artery stenosis, cerebrovascular disease, NSAIDs contraindications, and a history of allergy to anesthetic drugs.

Randomization and blinding

An independent researcher (XWM), not involved in patient recruitment, coordination, data collection, or outcome assessment, created a random sequence using SPSS 25.0 software. The randomization results were hidden in sequentially numbered sealed opaque envelopes. Shortly before anesthesia induction, a researcher (NS), unaware of the randomization process, opened the envelopes and assigned patients to either the opioid-free anesthesia group (OFA group) or the opioid-based control group (S group) according to the predetermined randomization sequence. The anesthesiologist was informed of the study drugs, while the surgeons and other medical team members were not. Except for the different drugs, all patients received standardized intraoperative management and monitoring. Participants, clinical doctors other than the anesthesiologist, and researchers responsible for patient recruitment and outcome assessment were completely unaware of group allocation. Assessors had no access to anesthesia records and did not participate in patient care.

Anesthesia and study interventions

Preoperative preparation

All selected patients underwent thoracic paravertebral block preoperatively, with the block site selected at T3 and T7 on the surgical side. Patients were positioned on their side with the affected side up. A low-frequency convex array probe was used, and an out-of-plane puncture technique was applied. After the puncture needle entered the paravertebral space, 1 ml of normal saline was injected to observe subpleural pressure, followed by 15 ml of 0.5% ropivacaine in each paravertebral space. Additionally, serratus anterior block was performed at the level of the fourth rib on the affected side along the mid-axillary line, using 0.5% ropivacaine at a dose of 10 ml. Fifteen minutes later, the block range was measured using the ice cube method.

Anesthesia protocol

In the operating room, in addition to monitoring the electrocardiogram, pulse oximetry (SpO2), invasive arterial blood pressure, and anesthetic end-tidal concentration, the bispectral index (BIS; Medtronic, Minneapolis, MN, USA) was used to monitor the depth of sedation in patients. The anesthesia induction and maintenance protocols for the two groups of patients are shown in Table 1. Both groups of patients underwent double-lumen tracheal intubation under the guidance of a video laryngoscope after successful induction.

Anesthetic induction and maintenance protocol

In the OFA group, if blood pressure increased (more than 30% of the mean arterial pressure after induction) or heart rate increased (more than 30% of the heart rate after induction) during skin incision, lidocaine was administered intravenously at 1.5 mg/kg and continuously infused at 1.5 mg/kg/h. Both groups of patients returned to the anesthesia recovery room with tracheal intubation for anesthesia awakening and assessment.

Prophylaxis for postoperative nausea and vomiting and multimodal analgesia

Both groups of participants received intravenous injection of tropisetron 5 mg after anesthesia induction to prevent postoperative nausea and vomiting (PONV). For participants with severe PONV (three or more vomiting episodes), additional intravenous injection of droperidol 1 mg could be administered as postoperative emergency antiemetic treatment. Postoperative multimodal analgesia regimen: Postoperative analgesia in both groups was primarily based on NSAIDs. The NSAID selected was flurbiprofen ester (200 mg/day); opioids were used as rescue analgesics, with oxycodone chosen. When postoperative breakthrough pain (NRS > 4) occurred, patients self-administered oxycodone 1 mg, with intervals of 5 min allowed for additional doses, limited to 10 mg per hour. If pain relief was still inadequate, patients were given oral Talwin (oxycodone 5 mg + acetaminophen 375 mg) or intramuscular injection of fentanyl 0.05 mg/morphine 5 mg [19].

Research outcomes

Primary outcomes

The total incidence of opioid-related adverse reactions at 2 h, 4 h, 6 h, 24 h, and 48 h postoperatively was recorded, with hypoxemia, hypotension, nausea and vomiting, and dizziness as primary indicators of adverse reactions. Adverse reactions were recorded following the one-event principle, meaning that if a patient experienced any of the above adverse reactions, it was counted as one event, and if multiple adverse reactions occurred simultaneously, it was also counted as one event. The incidence of adverse reactions was calculated as the number of adverse reactions divided by the total number of participants. These indicators were followed up by blinded assessors.

Postoperative hypotension was defined as systolic blood pressure below 20% of baseline or mean arterial pressure below 60 mmHg (baseline blood pressure was defined as the average of blood pressure measurements over three days after admission).

Postoperative hypoxemia was defined as the need for oxygen therapy to maintain oxygen saturation above 95% within 48 h after surgery. The duration of oxygen therapy was also recorded.

Postoperative nausea and vomiting were defined as nausea and vomiting graded ≥ I (nausea and vomiting grading: Grade 0, no nausea or vomiting; Grade I, mild nausea, no vomiting; Grade II, mild nausea, 1–2 vomiting episodes/day; Grade III, moderate nausea, 3–5 vomiting episodes/day; Grade IV, severe nausea, > 5 vomiting episodes/day) within 48 h after surgery.

Secondary outcomes

The incidence of intraoperative arrhythmias and the probability of vasopressor use; pain conditions at 2 h, 4 h, 6 h, 24 h, and 48 h postextubation (assessed using the NRS score for both activity and rest); postoperative opioid consumption at 24 h and 48 h (all postoperative opioid use was recorded in detail and converted to equivalent doses as follows: 5 mg oral oxycodone is equivalent to 2 mg intravenous oxycodone, 0.1 mg injectable fentanyl is equivalent to 5 mg oxycodone, and 10 mg intravenous morphine is equivalent to 6 mg oxycodone); time to postoperative flatus and defecation; incidence of postoperative cardiovascular complications.

All of the above data were recorded in detail by blinded assessors during postoperative follow-up and entered into the anesthesia clinical information system for storage.

Statistical analysis

A pre-experiment revealed that the overall incidence of hypoxemia, hypotension, nausea and vomiting, and dizziness within 24 h after conventional anesthesia was approximately 35%, while the incidence of these adverse reactions after opioid-free anesthesia was 9%. To achieve 80% statistical power (α = 0.05), the calculated sample size was 48 cases per group (PASS 15; NCSS, LLC, Kaysville, UT, USA). Considering a dropout rate, the sample size was increased to 50 cases per group (total 100 cases).

The distribution of data was determined using the Shapiro-Wilk test and expressed as mean (standard deviation), median (interquartile range), or frequency (%). For continuous data, if it followed a normal distribution, independent sample t-tests were used for intergroup comparison. If the data did not follow a normal distribution, the Kruskal-Wallis H test was used for intergroup comparison. For count data, chi-square tests were used for intergroup comparison, and for ordinal data, the Kruskal-Wallis H test was used. No interim analysis or imputation of missing data was performed. Data were analyzed using SPSS software (version 25.0; IBM SPSS, Chicago, IL, USA). A two-sided P-value less than 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

Between September 2020 and June 2022, a total of 747 patients were screened, of which 100 were randomized, and ultimately 97 patients (48 in the S group and 49 in the OFA group) were included in the analysis (Fig. 1).

Primary outcomes

At 2 h postoperatively, there were no significant differences between the OFA group and the S group in the incidences of hypoxemia, postoperative nausea and vomiting (PONV), and dizziness. However, the incidence of hypotension was significantly lower in the OFA group than in the S group (8.2% vs. 33.3%, P = 0.002), and the overall incidence of adverse reactions was also significantly lower in the OFA group (18.4% vs. 39.6%, P = 0.018).

At 4 h postoperatively, no statistically significant differences were observed between the two groups in the incidences of hypoxemia, PONV, dizziness, and hypotension, nor in the overall incidence of adverse reactions (14.3% vs. 27.1%, P = 0.095).

At 6 h postoperatively, there were no significant differences between the OFA group and the S group in the incidences of hypoxemia, PONV, dizziness, and hypotension, and the overall incidence of adverse reactions also showed no statistical difference between the two groups (6.1% vs. 14.6%, P = 0.150).

At 24 h postoperatively, no significant differences were found between the OFA group and the S group in the incidences of hypoxemia and hypotension. Notably, no PONV cases occurred in the OFA group, while 5 patients in the S group developed PONV (incidence: 10.4%, 5/48), showing a significant difference between the two groups (P = 0.027). Additionally, the incidence of dizziness was significantly lower in the OFA group than in the S group (8.2%, 4/49 vs. 22.9%, 11/48, P = 0.041). Correspondingly, the overall incidence of adverse reactions was significantly lower in the OFA group (10.2%, 5/49 vs. 27.1%, 13/48, P = 0.029).

At 48 h postoperatively, there were no statistically significant differences between the two groups in the incidences of hypoxemia, PONV, dizziness, and hypotension, and the overall incidence of adverse reactions also did not differ significantly (4.1% vs. 4.2%, P = 0.684) (Table 4).

Secondary outcomes

There were no statistically significant differences between the two groups in the incidence of intraoperative arrhythmias, postoperative NRS scores (rest and movement) at 24 and 48 h, postoperative opioid consumption, postoperative rescue analgesic application rate, time to first postoperative activity, time to first postoperative flatus, and time to first postoperative water intake. No cases of intraoperative awareness occurred in either group. The OFA group had fewer intraoperative vasopressor applications (0[0,1] vs. 3[1,4], P < 0.001), lower postoperative opioid consumption at 24 h (1[0,2] vs. 2[0,4], P = 0.033), and fewer postoperative analgesic pump presses at 24 h (1[0,2] vs. 2[0,4], P = 0.004). Additionally, the OFA group had an earlier time to first postoperative feeding (9.5 ± 3.8 h vs. 11.4 ± 4.7 h, P = 0.035) (Table 5).

Discussion

This study is a prospective, randomized, double-blind study aimed at comparing the differences in postoperative adverse reactions and related clinical indicators between opioid-free anesthesia based on thoracic paravertebral block combined with dexmedetomidine and opioid-based anesthesia based on thoracic paravertebral block. The results showed that at 2 h postoperatively, the incidence of hypotension was significantly lower in the OFA group than in the S group, and the overall incidence of adverse reactions was also lower. At 24 h postoperatively, the incidence of PONV, dizziness, and overall adverse reactions were lower in the OFA group. In terms of secondary objectives, the OFA group had fewer intraoperative vasopressor applications, lower postoperative opioid consumption and analgesic pump presses at 24 h, and an earlier time to first postoperative feeding. These results indicate that the OFA group has certain advantages in controlling some postoperative adverse reactions and in postoperative recovery.

This study demonstrated that the overall incidence of adverse reactions within 24 h after surgery in the OFA group was significantly reduced (10.2% vs. 27.1%, P = 0.029), which is highly consistent with the latest evidence in the field of thoracoscopic surgery. A meta-analysis by D’Amico et al. on thoracic surgery indicated that OFA can reduce the risk of postoperative complications by 24% (RR = 0.76, 95% CI 0.65–0.89), with an absolute risk reduction of 15.3% for postoperative nausea and vomiting (PONV) [7]. Notably, in this study, the incidence of PONV within 24 h after surgery in the OFA group was reduced to 0%, which was significantly better than that in the traditional opioid group (10.4%, P = 0.027). This finding is in line with that of Yan et al., who reported that in 168 cases of thoracoscopic lobectomy, the incidence of PONV was 15% in the OFA group versus 31.7% in the traditional group (OR = 0.38) [20]. This difference may be attributed to the fact that the OFA regimen completely avoids the activation of µ-receptors, thereby blocking the neural pathways that trigger the vomiting reflex.

Postoperative hypoxemia may be influenced by non-opioid factors (e.g., postoperative atelectasis or impaired respiratory mechanics after thoracoscopy). Although the OFA group showed a numerically lower hypoxemia incidence at 2–6 h postoperatively (as seen in Table 4), this difference was not statistically significant, suggesting that opioids may play a limited role in hypoxemia after thoracoscopic surgery compared to other factors.

Despite no significant difference in postoperative NRS scores at rest and during movement between the two groups, the OFA group showed a 50% reduction in postoperative opioid consumption at 24 h (1[0,2] vs. 2[0,4] mg, P = 0.033), which is consistent with the conclusions of a systematic review by Salomé et al., who pointed out that OFA can reduce morphine-equivalent consumption by 4.02 mg within 48 h (95%CI 1.73–6.30) [10]. More importantly, in this study, the number of postoperative analgesic pump presses in the OFA group was reduced by 50% (1[0,2] vs. 2[0,4], P = 0.004), suggesting that the synergistic effect of regional blocks and non-opioid drugs may improve analgesia by inhibiting central sensitization. A study by An et al. found that thoracic paravertebral block combined with dexmedetomidine can reduce the expression of c-Fos protein in the spinal dorsal horn by 64% (P = 0.003), supporting the clinical observations of this study from a molecular perspective [21].

This study found that compared to opioid-based anesthesia (S Group), opioid-free anesthesia (OFA group) significantly reduced the frequency of intraoperative vasopressor use (P = 0.001) and lowered the incidence of hypotension at 2 h postoperatively. This result may be related to the synergistic hemodynamic suppression effect between opioids and thoracic paravertebral block (TPVB). Opioids (such as remifentanil) mediate central sympathetic inhibition via µ-opioid receptors, reducing peripheral vascular resistance and myocardial contractility. Additionally, some opioids can induce mast cell release of histamine, further dilating peripheral blood vessels [22]. TPVB, by blocking the thoracic sympathetic chain, causes vasodilation in the blocked area, which may exacerbate hypotension induced by opioids [23]. The OFA regimen, by substituting opioids with dexmedetomidine (an α2 receptor agonist), leverages its α2-mediated mild vasoconstrictive effect to partially counteract the sympathetic inhibition caused by TPVB, thereby maintaining intraoperative hemodynamic stability [24]. Furthermore, dexmedetomidine regulates blood pressure by inhibiting sympathetic activity rather than through direct vasodilation, avoiding opioid-related histamine release and myocardial depression [25]. In the postoperative phase, the reduced incidence of hypotension in the OFA group may be associated with the elimination of residual opioid effects; for example, sufentanil has an elimination half-life of approximately 2.7 h, and studies have confirmed that remifentanil metabolites can still affect vascular tone via central mechanisms [22]. In summary, OFA avoids the synergistic hypotensive effects of opioids and TPVB, it provides a more optimized anesthetic option for thoracoscopic surgery.

The OFA group had an earlier time to first postoperative feeding by 1.9 h (9.5 ± 3.8 h vs. 11.4 ± 4.7 h, P = 0.035), a result superior to that reported by Yan X et al. in thoracoscopic pulmonary resection (OFA group recovery time shortened by 1.2 h). Possible mechanisms include: (1) Opioid receptor-independent intestinal neuromodulation, with dexmedetomidine enhancing cholinergic activity in the myenteric plexus (ACh release increased by 38%, P = 0.02); (2) Regional blocks reduce postoperative IL-6 levels (reduction by 52%, P < 0.01), improving intestinal microcirculation. These findings are highly consistent with the core concepts of ERAS, suggesting that OFA may become a key component of accelerated recovery pathways.

The single-center design and sample size limitation (n = 97) of this study may affect the generalizability of the results, and multicenter RCTs are needed for validation. Additionally, this study did not collect data on chronic pain outcomes at 3 or 6 months postoperatively, nor did it follow up on long-term patient outcomes (such as tumor recurrence rates or 5-year survival rates), which should be included in future studies. Furthermore, caution should be exercised regarding the risks of specific OFA regimens, such as severe bradycardia events reported by Beloeil H et al., indicating the need for an individualized drug monitoring system [11].

In summary, this study demonstrates that opioid-free anesthesia (thoracic paravertebral block combined with dexmedetomidine) significantly reduces the incidence of postoperative adverse reactions compared to traditional opioid-based anesthesia (thoracic paravertebral block combined with opioids), reduces intraoperative vasopressor use and postoperative opioid consumption, and accelerates postoperative recovery. These advantages stem from dual regulation of vomiting reflex pathways, stable hemodynamic mechanisms, and promotion of gastrointestinal function recovery, supporting its role as a safe and effective accelerated recovery strategy. The study is limited by its single-center, small-sample design and requires further multicenter validation.
