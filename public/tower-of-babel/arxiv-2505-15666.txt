Shubnikov-de Haas Oscillations in 2D PtSe 2 \text{PtSe}_{2} : A fermiological Charge Carrier Investigation Preprint: APS/123-QED
Julian Max Salchegger, Rajdeep Adhikari, Bogdan Faina, Alberta Bonanni

Abstract
High magnetic field and low temperature transport is carried out in order to characterize the charge carriers of PtSe 2 \text{PtSe}_{2} . In particular, the Shubnikov-de Haas oscillations arising at applied magnetic field strengths ≳ 4.5 ​ T \gtrsim 4.5\,\text{T} are found to occur exclusively in plane and emerge at a layer thickness of ≈ 18 ​ nm \approx 18\,\text{nm} , increasing in amplitude and decreasing in frequency for thinner PtSe 2 \text{PtSe}_{2} flakes. Moreover, the quantum transport time, Berry phase, Dingle temperature and cyclotron mass of the charge carriers are ascertained. The emergence of weak antilocalization (WAL) lies in contrast to the presence of magnetic moments from Pt vacancies. An explanation is provided on how WAL and the Kondo effect can be observed within the same material. Detailed information about the charge carriers and transport phenomena in PtSe 2 \text{PtSe}_{2} is obtained, which is relevant for the design of prospective spintronic and orbitronic devices and for the realization of orbital Hall effect-based architectures.



I
Introduction

The van der Waals layered
transition-metal dichalcogenide (TMD)
with type-II Dirac cones
Huang et al. 2016
;
Zhang et al. 2017

\text{PtSe}_{2}
has been proposed for usage in electronic
Yim et al. 2018
;
Yim et al. 2016
;
Zhao et al. 2017
and spintronic
Jo et al. 2022
devices and, more recently, for the detection of the
orbital Hall effect (OHE)

Bhowal and Satpathy 2020
;
Sahu et al. 2024
. Unlike the conventional Hall effect, the
OHE
does not require an applied magnetic field to emerge: The orbital momentum of the charge carriers couples to an applied electric field, resulting in the generation of a transversal orbital current. By injecting the orbital current into a material with high
spin orbit coupling (SOC)
, or
e.g.
through Rashba
SOC

Ding et al. 2022
, the orbital current can be converted to a spin current. Harnessing orbital currents promises applications in spintronic
Gupta et al. 2025
and orbitronic
Wang et al. 2023
;
Wang et al. 2024
devices. Additionally, the
OHE
may account for the discrepancy between observed and theoretical magnitudes of the spin Hall effect in materials such as Pt
Tanaka and Kontani 2010
.
\text{PtSe}_{2}
is predicted to host significant orbital currents
Sahu et al. 2024
and offers the advantage of inherent
SOC
, due to the heavy Pt atoms. Understanding the nature of the charge carriers present in
\text{PtSe}_{2}
, as well as its electronic structure, is a fundamental step towards the experimental realization of an orbital Hall device.

Here, high magnetic field and low temperature transport studies are carried out to investigate the charge carriers of
\text{PtSe}_{2}
flakes. In particular, the
Shubnikov-de Haas (SdH)
oscillations, arising in both the longitudinal and transverse resistance as the applied magnetic field exceeds
\sim 4\,\,\text{{T}}
, are analyzed. The oscillations are closely linked to the orbital motion of the charge carriers and to their Fermi surface, allowing for the determination of key characteristics
Fu et al. 2018
;
Dai et al. 2024
;
Busch et al. 2018
, such as the cyclotron mass, the quantum scattering time, the mean free path of the charge carriers and the Berry phase.

The spectral analysis of the oscillations points at the charge transport being dominated by a single electron-like band. The quantum scattering time
\tau
and the cyclotron mass
m_{c}
are determined together with the Berry phase
\Phi_{B}
. The thickness-dependence of the extremal Fermi surface cross-sectional area indicates that the bulk limit of the electronic structure establishes only for thicknesses
t>20\,\text{{nm}}
. Using the angular dispersion of the
SdH
oscillations, insights into the Fermi surface shape are gained.

In a previous work
Salchegger et al. 2024
, we demonstrated that
\text{PtSe}_{2}
flakes exfoliated from a bulk crystal contain Pt vacancies, resulting in a Kondo effect. Here, a further analysis reveals that the vacancy concentration is not uniform since the Kondo effect varies across samples. A reduced number of Pt vacancies leads to the emergence of
WAL
in the non-local resistance measurements, heralding
SOC
in the samples.

being the source-drain current. The samples show
n(2\,\text{K})\sim(2\times 10^{20})\,\text{cm}^{-3}
. Exemplary plots of
V_{\text{H}}(H)
are presented in the Supplemental Material
Sup
Fig.
S5
for samples F and X1.

III.2
Shubnikov-de Haas oscillations

The nonlocal longitudinal resistivity
\rho_{xx}

\bm{j}

\rho_{xy}
(cognate with

\bm{j}

\rho_{xx}(H)

2\,\text{{K}}

\bm{j}

\rho_{xx}^{(\text{lin.})}
which originates from the Hall voltage. The longitudinal resistance
\rho_{xx}-\rho_{xx}^{(\text{lin.})}
shows an approximately quadratic dependence on
H
.

For
|\mu_{0}H|\gtrsim 4.5\,\text{{T}}
,
SdH
oscillations emerge, displayed in the upper panel of Fig.
2

2\,\text{{K}}
shows a quasi-linear dependence
\rho_{xy}\propto-H
, depicted in the inset to lower panel of Fig.
2

|\mu_{0}H|\gtrsim 4.5\,\text{{T}}
,
SdH
oscillations emerge, provided in the lower panel of Fig.
2
.

The origin of the
SdH
oscillations is the Landau-quantization of the electronic states, which modulates the
density of states (DOS)
at the Fermi level when varying
\bm{H}
. A local maximum in
\rho_{xx}(H)
corresponds to a
Landau level (LL)
coinciding with the Fermi level, as the increased number of available states (compared to no
LL
coinciding with the Fermi level) enhances the electron-electron scattering. A similar mechanism also affects
\rho_{xy}
, in which case the modulation of the
DOS
at the Fermi level alters the charge carrier density
n
and thus the Hall voltage
\rho_{xy}\cong V_{\text{H}}\propto\frac{1}{n}
shows a minimum when a
LL
coincides with the Fermi level.

Let
d\in\{xx,xy\}\equiv\{\text{longitudinal},\text{transversal}\}
denote the measurement direction and
\widetilde{\rho_{d}}
be the oscillatory part of
\rho_{d}
, such that
\rho_{d}=\rho_{d}^{\text{(bg.)}}+\widetilde{\rho_{d}}
, where
\rho_{d}^{\text{(bg.)}}
is a smooth background. Then,
\widetilde{\rho_{d}}(H,T)
follows from the
Lifshitz-Kosevich (L-K)
relation
Lifshitz and Kosevich 1956
;
Shoenberg 1984
:

\displaystyle\widetilde{\rho_{d}}=

\displaystyle AT\left(\frac{5}{2}\left(\frac{H}{2\mathfrak{F}}\right)^{\frac{1}{2}}+\frac{3}{2}\left(\frac{H}{2\mathfrak{F}}\right)\right)

(1)

\displaystyle\times

\displaystyle\frac{\exp(-x\frac{NM}{H})\cos(\pi M)}{\sinh(T\frac{NM}{H})}\cos\left(2\pi\left(\frac{\mathfrak{F}}{H}+\phi\right)\right),

where
A
is an amplitude prefactor,
\phi
the phase of the oscillations,
x
the Dingle temperature,
M=\frac{m_{c}}{m_{0}}
the relative electron cyclotron mass (
m_{c}
is the electron cyclotron mass and
m_{0}
the free electron rest mass) and
N=2\pi^{2}\frac{m_{0}k_{\text{B}}}{e\hbar}
a constant (
\hbar
is the reduced Planck constant,
e
the elementary charge and
k_{\text{B}}
the Boltzmann constant). Finally,
\mathfrak{F}
is the frequency of the oscillations (periodic in
\frac{1}{H}
), which is linked to
S_{\text{F}}
, the extremal cross-sectional area of the Fermi surface normal to
\bm{H}
,
via

\mathfrak{F}=\frac{\hbar}{2\pi e}S_{\text{F}}
. The adaptation of the
L-K
formula given in Ref.
Shoenberg 1984
is described in Appendix B.
The experimental value of
\widetilde{\rho_{d}}
is obtained by subtracting a polynomial background from
\rho_{d}
. Eq.
1
is augmented with a second-order polynomial,

\widetilde{\rho_{d}}\rightarrow\widetilde{\rho_{d}}+A^{(0)}+A^{\text{(}{lin.})}H+A^{\text{(}{quad.})}H^{2},

(2)

to account for any remaining background, and the fitting of
\widetilde{\rho_{d}}
as a function of applied magnetic field of sample F at
2\,\text{K}
with Eq.
2
is reported in Fig.
3
.

(and minima to half-integers). Therefore, in the conductivity

\displaystyle\Phi_{\text{B}}=2\pi\left(\frac{1}{2}-\phi\pm\delta\right)\mod 2\pi.

(3)

The phase shift

In order to obtain the quantum transport time
\tau
, the extrema of
\widetilde{\rho_{d}}
as a function of applied magnetic field, where
\frac{\partial^{2}\widetilde{\rho_{d}}}{\partial H^{2}}=0
, are investigated. At these points, the

\tau
can be expressed as:

\tau=\left(\frac{e}{-m_{c}\pi}\frac{\partial}{\partial\frac{1}{H}}\ln{\mathfrak{G}_{d}}\right)^{-1},

(5)

with
\mathfrak{G}_{d}\equiv\frac{\widetilde{\rho_{d}}}{\rho_{d}^{\text{(bg.)}}}\sinh{\left(NM\frac{T}{H}\right)}\frac{1}{\sqrt{H}}
and
\rho_{d}^{\text{(bg.)}}
the smooth background of
\rho_{d}
. A derivation is given in Appendix C.
\tau
can be extracted from the slope of
\ln\mathfrak{G}_{d}
over
\frac{1}{H}
, leaving only

as an unknown parameter. Since

. A linear fit of

III.3
Weak antilocalization

The local conductance is obtained by measuring the voltage difference directly between source and drain. Around
H=0
, the samples show a conductance peak, reported in Fig.
12
as conductance over applied magnetic field for sample F at
2\,\text{K}
. Similar plots are obtained for samples X1 and X2, as given in Fig.
S13
in the Supplemental Material
Sup
. In this two-terminal configuration, the measured conductance is affected by both the
\text{PtSe}_{2}
film and by the (metallic) Pt contacts.

The origin of the conductance peak is assigned to the
SOC
of the charge carriers within the
12\,\text{nm}
thin Pt contacts leading to
WAL
: Without external magnetic field, the
time-reversal symmetry (TRS)
is preserved and the
SOC
triggers a destructive interference between the backscattering paths with their time-reversed counterparts. The applied magnetic field breaks the
TRS
, reducing the destructive backscattering interference, and thus leading to a decrease in conductance.

In the non-local conductance, sample X1 shows
WAL
, depicted in Fig.
13
as conductance over applied magnetic field at
1.7\,\text{K}
. The
WAL
remains resolvable until
T\approx 10\,\text{K}
(Fig.
S14
in the Supplemental Material
Sup
).

It is proposed that this non-local
WAL
originates from the
SOC
in the
\text{PtSe}_{2}
: The Pt vacancies in
\text{PtSe}_{2}
contribute uncompensated magnetic moments which manifest
via
a Kondo effect
Salchegger et al. 2024
– it is inferred that the magnetic moments also suppress the
SOC
inside
\text{PtSe}_{2}
. The vanishing of the Kondo effect in sample X1, correlated with the emergence of non-local
WAL
, suggests that the Pt vacancy concentration varies within in the bulk crystal. The Kondo effect elaborated in Appendix A, and the
resistance-over-temperature-curve (R-T)
data of samples X1 and X2 are compared. Alternatively, the Pt/
\text{PtSe}_{2}
junction may play a significant role since it can affect the charge distribution at the Pt sites and the Pt coordination number
Wang et al. 2021
.

The
WAL
data is understood within the (2D)
Hikami-Larkin-Nagaoka (HLN)
model
Hikami et al. 1980
;
Sultana et al. 2018
:

\Delta\sigma_{\text{WAL}}^{\text{(2D)}}\propto-\frac{\alpha e^{2}}{\pi h}\left(\ln\left(\frac{B_{\phi}}{H}\right)-\Psi\left(\frac{1}{2}+\frac{B_{\phi}}{H}\right)\right),

(7)

where the magnetic field length is
B_{\phi}=\frac{h}{8e\pi Hl_{\phi}}
(
l_{\phi}
is the phase coherence length),
\Psi
is the digamma function and
\alpha\in\left[-\frac{3}{2},-\frac{1}{2}\right]
is a coefficient. Alternatively, a 3D extension of the
HLN
model can be employed as in Refs.
Nakamura et al. 2020
;
Salawu et al. 2022
, where it is also used to model a Dirac system:

\displaystyle\Delta\sigma_{\text{WAL}}^{\text{(3D)}}

\displaystyle=A_{\text{WAL}}\sqrt{\frac{H}{f}}

(8)

\displaystyle\times\Bigg[2\zeta\left(\frac{1}{2},\frac{1}{2}+\frac{f}{H\,l_{0}^{2}}\right)

\displaystyle+\zeta\left(\frac{1}{2},\frac{1}{2}+\frac{f}{H\,l_{\phi}^{2}}\right)

\displaystyle-3\zeta\left(\frac{1}{2},\frac{1}{2}+4\frac{f}{H\,l_{\text{SO}}^{2}}+\frac{f}{H\,l_{\phi}^{2}}\right)\Bigg],

where
A_{\text{WAL}}
is the magnitude of the conductance peak,
\zeta
the Hurwitz zeta function and
f=\frac{\hbar}{4e}=\frac{\Phi_{0}}{8\pi}
a constant. There are four length scales involved: (i) The mean free path length
l_{0}
, (ii) the phase coherence length
l_{\phi}
, (iii) the spin orbit scattering length
l_{\text{SO}}
and (iv) the magnetic length
l_{H}=\sqrt{\frac{f}{H}}
. To fit the
WAL
in a region
|\mu_{0}H|<2\,\,\text{{T}}
, expressions for the orbital magnetoconductance and the Hall intermix are added. The
L-K
term modeling the
SdH
oscillations is omitted, given that its magnitude is negligible at
|\mu_{0}H|<2\,\,\text{{T}}
. The total conductance as a function of applied magnetic field is modeled as the
WAL
conductance taking place in parallel (additive) to the orbital magnetoconductance:

\displaystyle\sigma(H)

\displaystyle=\Delta\sigma_{\text{WAL}}^{(i)}

(9)

\displaystyle+\left(\frac{1}{\sigma_{0}}+A_{\text{MR}}\,|(H+H_{\text{lag}})|^{s}+kH)\right)^{-1},

with
\sigma_{0}
the zero-field conductance,
A_{\text{MR}}
the
orbital magnetoresistance (OMR)
magnitude and
s
the scaling exponent thereof. Since the highest considered temperature of
15\,\text{K}
still lies in the range in which the system is in the Fermi liquid regime (
\approx 80\,\text{K}
),
s=2
is chosen. The parameter
H_{\text{lag}}
compensates for the lag between requested magnetic field and applied magnetic field, which is about
\pm 16\,\text{{mT}}
when sweeping at
8\frac{\text{mT}}{\text{s}}
, while
k
models the already mentioned Hall intermix. Details about the evaluation of Eq.
9
and implementation of the least-squares fit are provided in Sec. C in the Supplemental Material
Sup
.

Both the 2D model and the 3D model are applied to the local conductivity of sample F at
2\,\text{K}
(Fig.
12
). The 3D model captures the peak shape more precisely. The obtained length scales are compared in Table
S4
in the Supplemental Material
Sup
. The data is comparable to the
WAL
exhibited by Pt thin films reported in Ref.
Jana et al. 2023
.

IV
Conclusions

Here, key aspects of the charge carriers in
\text{PtSe}_{2}
are extracted
via
implementation of an analytic
L-K
framework. Under application of a magnetic field,
SdH
oscillations, observed in the resistance measurements, occur in plane. This provides key information about the charge carriers, such as the Berry phase
\Phi_{\text{B}}=(1.01\pm 0.05)\pi
, the cyclotron mass
m_{c}=(0.321\pm 0.008)\,m_{0}
, in-plane mobility
\mu=(1200\pm 100)\frac{\text{cm}^{2}}{\text{V s}}
and quantum scattering time
\tau=[(0.22\pm 0.02)\times 10^{-12}]\,\text{s}
. A spectral analysis of the oscillations yields a single species of electrons as the prevalent charge carriers with
\mathfrak{F}=(194.19\pm 0.18)\,\text{T${}^{-1}$}
, equating to a Fermi surface cross-section
S_{\text{F}}\approx 0.0185\,\text{Å${}^{-2}$}
for
t=11\,\text{nm}
.
\mathfrak{F}
is reduced as the thickness is lowered, suggesting a thickness-dependence of the band structure for
8\,\text{nm}<t<20\,\text{nm}
and potentially presenting a compelling tuning parameter for the precise electronic properties of
\text{PtSe}_{2}
flakes and thin films. The Pt vacancies, inherently present in the grown bulk crystals, are found to play a crucial role, as they provide uncompensated magnetic moments which establish a Kondo effect but suppress the
SOC
leading to a
WAL
. While the local
WAL
is better understood with the 3D
HLN
model and originates from the Pt contacts, the non-local conductance peak also fits the 2D
HLN
model, consistent with the 2D confinement observed from the
SdH
oscillations. It is concluded, that the non-local
WAL
heralds substantial inherent
SOC
in
\text{PtSe}_{2}
. The employed bulk crystals provide long-range order larger than the physical measurement area and contain Pt vacancies from growth, prompting a comparative investigation to directly grown
\text{PtSe}_{2}
structures. The adaptation of the
L-K
formalism is valid for a single charge carrier species with 2D oscillations. The results motivate magnetotransport studies in highly stoichiometric
\text{PtSe}_{2}
flakes and
\text{PtSe}_{2}
/ferromagnetic heterostructures, in order to fully harness the
SOC
inherent in the material and demonstrate the capability of
\text{PtSe}_{2}
in orbitronic and orbital Hall-based devices.

Appendix A: Exposition of Kondo effect

Although samples X1 and X2 are exfoliated from bulk crystal batch 2, their
R-T
behaviour is qualitatively different: The nonlocal resistance over temperature curves (
field-warming (FW)
and
zero-field-warming (ZFW)
) of sample X1 are given in Fig.
15
and the
field-cooling (FC)
and
zero-field-cooling (ZFC)
curves of sample X2 are provided in Fig.
15
. The expression
(\theta,\psi)
describes the orientation of the
SH
in relation to the applied magnetic field:
\psi
is the angle, by which the
SH
is rotated with respect to axis
ψ
, which is normal to
\bm{H}
;
\theta
is the angle rotated about the axis
θ
, which is normal to axis
ψ
and encloses an angle
(\pi/2+\psi)
with
\bm{H}
. The angles and their respective axes are sketched in the right panel of Fig.
15
. The curves for sample X1 are distinguished mainly by a temperature-independent offset, which is caused by the
OMR
. The
OMR
is strongest when the applied magnetic field is oriented along the sample surface normal, corresponding to an orientation
(0,0)
. In the
(90,0)
orientation, the applied magnetic field lies in plane and normal to the source-drain direction. The resulting
OMR
is reduced due to the charge carriers being confined to the sample thickness of
17\,\text{nm}
. Considering the structural anisotropy of
\text{PtSe}_{2}
, the out-of-plane mobility can be assumed to be lower than the in-plane value, which can also account for the reduced in-plane
OMR
. In the
(90,-90)
orientation, the applied magnetic field lies in plane and parallel to the source-drain direction. Almost no
OMR
is observed, since the Lorentz force is zero. The origin of the additional resistance for
T\gtrsim 35\,\text{K}
is unclear. The
ZFW
shows the lowest resistance because no
OMR
applies. As
T\to 0
, no increase in the resistance
R
is observed.
This is in contrast to all other considered samples which display a global minimum resistance temperature of
\approx 15\,\text{K}
and an increase in resistance as the temperature is lowered below
T\lesssim 15\,\text{K}
. This property of samples A, B and C is discussed in a previous work
Salchegger et al. 2024
and can be gleaned in Fig.
15
for sample X2. The anomalous increase in resistance as
T\to 0
is mediated by Pt vacancies, which contribute an uncompensated spin when located in the top- or bottom-most layers. At or below the Kondo temperature
T_{\text{K}}
, this results in a Kondo effect: The charge carriers scatter at the vacancy spin
via
a double antiferromagnetic spin exchange, resulting in an increase in resistance as the temperature is lowered further.

Appendix B: Adaptation of the Lifshitz-Kosevich formula

The oscillatory part of the thermodynamic potential of the electrons due to quantum oscillations
\widetilde{\Omega}
is given by
Lifshitz and Kosevich 1956
;
Shoenberg 1984
:

\displaystyle\widetilde{\Omega}

\displaystyle=\left(\frac{e}{2\pi c\hbar}\right)^{3/2}\frac{2kTH^{3/2}V}{(S_{\text{F}}^{\prime\prime})^{1/2}}\sum_{p=1}^{\infty}\frac{\exp(-2\pi^{2}pkx/(\beta H))\cos(\frac{1}{2}p\pi g\frac{m}{m_{0}})}{p^{3/2}\sinh(2\pi^{2}pkT/(\beta H))}\cos\left(2\pi p\left(\frac{\mathfrak{F}}{H}-\frac{1}{2}\right)\pm\frac{\pi}{4}\right)

(10)

\displaystyle\propto H^{3/2}\sum_{p=1}^{\infty}R_{D}R_{T}R_{S}\mathfrak{O}

(11)

with
c
the speed of light,
\hbar
the reduced Planck quantum,
k
the Boltzmann constant,
V
the sample volume,
p
the harmonic number,
x
the Dingle temperature,
\beta=\frac{e\hbar}{m}
,
m
the electron cyclotron mass,
g
the Landé g-factor,
m_{0}
the electron rest mass and
\mathfrak{F}
the oscillation frequency. The extremal cross-sectional area of the Fermi surface perpendicular to
\bm{H}
is
S_{\text{F}}
and
\kappa
is the component of
\bm{k}
parallel to
\bm{H}
. The parameter
S_{\text{F}}^{\prime\prime}
describes the curvature of
S_{\text{F}}
with respect to
\kappa
:

S_{\text{F}}^{\prime\prime}=\frac{\text{d}^{2}S_{\text{F}}}{\text{d}\kappa^{2}},\quad\kappa=\frac{\bm{k}\cdot\bm{H}}{|\bm{H}|}.

(12)

When considering the conductivity oscillations
\widetilde{\sigma}
instead of
\widetilde{\Omega}
, the same expressions for the Dingle damping
R_{D}=\exp(-2\pi^{2}pkx/(\beta H))
, the thermal smearing
R_{T}=T\sinh(2\pi^{2}pkT/(\beta H))^{-1}
and the spin phase interference
R_{S}=\cos(\frac{1}{2}p\pi g\frac{m}{m_{0}})
are applicable. The conductivity does however scale differently under application of a magnetic field:
\widetilde{\sigma}
is related to the variation of the density of states at the Fermi energy
\widetilde{\mathscr{D}}

Shoenberg 1984
:

\frac{5}{2}\frac{\widetilde{\mathscr{D}}}{\mathscr{D}_{0}}+\frac{3}{2}\left(\frac{\widetilde{\mathscr{D}}}{\mathscr{D}_{0}}\right)^{2},

(13)

where
\mathscr{D}_{0}
is the steady density of states at the Fermi energy (without applied magnetic field). The ratio
\frac{\widetilde{\mathscr{D}}}{\mathscr{D}_{0}}
relates to the applied magnetic field as:

\displaystyle\frac{\widetilde{\mathscr{D}}}{\mathscr{D}_{0}}

\displaystyle=\left(\frac{H}{2\mathfrak{F}}\right)^{1/2},

(14)

\displaystyle\frac{\widetilde{\sigma}}{\sigma}

\displaystyle\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mathrel{\rotatebox[origin]{180.0}{$\Lsh\,$}}}}{{\propto}}\left(\frac{5}{2}\left(\frac{H}{2\mathfrak{F}}\right)^{\frac{1}{2}}+\frac{3}{2}\left(\frac{H}{2\mathfrak{F}}\right)\right).

(15)

The higher harmonic numbers
p>1
are omitted because they are not observed in the considered samples (a single peak is obtained in the
FFT
). The expression of the phase of the oscillations inside
\cos\left(2\pi\left(\frac{\mathfrak{F}}{H}-\frac{1}{2}\right)\pm\frac{\pi}{4}\right)
is obtained for a 3D case, wherein the offset
\delta=\frac{\pi}{4}
results from an integration over
\kappa

Shoenberg 1984
. This offset is zero in the 2D case. The phase offset
\phi=\frac{1}{2}
is expanded to also consider a potentially non-zero the Berry curvature
\Phi_{\text{B}}
Busch et al. 2018
:

\displaystyle 2\pi\left(\frac{\mathfrak{F}}{H}-\frac{1}{2}\right)\pm\frac{\pi}{4}\equiv 2\pi\left(\frac{\mathfrak{F}}{H}-\phi\right),

(16)

\displaystyle\phi=\left(\frac{1}{2}-\frac{\Phi_{\text{B}}}{2\pi}\pm\delta\right)\mod 1,\quad\delta^{\text{(2D)}}=0.

(17)

\displaystyle\iff\Phi_{\text{B}}=2\pi\left(\frac{1}{2}-\phi\right)\mod 2\pi

(18)

Thus, for
\phi=\frac{1}{2}
the Berry phase is zero while for
\phi=0
the Berry phase is
\pm\pi
. The expression for the oscillation for the 2D case is
\cos\left(2\pi\left(\frac{\mathfrak{F}}{H}+\phi\right)\right)
.
Eq.
11
is adapted to describe the oscillatory part of
\rho_{d}
by keeping the behavior
\propto R_{D}R_{T}R_{S}\mathfrak{O}^{\text{(2D)}}
, while also considering that the origin of
\widetilde{\sigma}
(and hence
\widetilde{\rho_{d}}
) lies in the modulation of
\mathscr{D}
:

\displaystyle\widetilde{\rho}_{d}

\displaystyle=AR_{D}R_{T}R_{S}\mathfrak{O}\left(\frac{5}{2}\frac{\widetilde{\mathscr{D}}}{\mathscr{D}_{0}}+\frac{3}{2}\left(\frac{\widetilde{\mathscr{D}}}{\mathscr{D}_{0}}\right)^{2}\right)

(19)

\displaystyle\widetilde{\rho}_{d}

\displaystyle\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mathrel{\rotatebox[origin]{180.0}{$\Lsh\,$}}}}{{=}}AT\left(\frac{5}{2}\left(\frac{H}{2\mathfrak{F}}\right)^{\frac{1}{2}}+\frac{3}{2}\left(\frac{H}{2\mathfrak{F}}\right)\right)\frac{\exp\left(-x\frac{NM}{H}\right)\cos(\pi M)}{\sinh\left(T\frac{NM}{H}\right)}\cos\left(2\pi\left(\frac{\mathfrak{F}}{H}+\phi\right)\right).

(20)

To account for nonlocal transport measurements, the amplitude
A
is introduced. Several constants are included into
A
. The parameter
M=\frac{m_{c}}{m_{0}}
is the fermion cyclotron mass ratio. The value of
N=2\pi^{2}\frac{k\,m_{0}}{\hbar\,e}\approx 14.693\,\frac{\text{K}}{\text{T}}
is determined by substituting the values for the constants.

Appendix C: Extraction of the quantum transport time from the Lifshitz-Kosevich formula

The quantum transport time
\tau
is related to the Dingle temperature
via

x=\frac{\hbar}{2\pi k\tau}
, and is extracted from Eq.
20
: At a local extrema
\frac{\partial^{2}\widetilde{\rho_{d}}(H)}{\partial H^{2}}=0
, the phase factor
|\mathfrak{O}^{\text{(2D)}}|=1
. A relative oscillation magnitude
\frac{\widetilde{\rho_{d}}}{\rho_{d}^{\text{(bg.)}}}
is introduced to obtain a dimensionless expression (the value of
A
changes accordingly). Let
\mathscr{H}=\frac{5}{2}\left(\frac{H}{2\mathfrak{F}}\right)^{\frac{1}{2}}+\frac{3}{2}\left(\frac{H}{2\mathfrak{F}}\right)
be the magnetic field dependence of the oscillation amplitude. This yields:

\frac{\widetilde{\rho_{d}}}{\rho_{d}^{\text{(bg.)}}}=AT\mathscr{H}\frac{\exp(-x\frac{NM}{H})\cos(\pi M)}{\sinh(T\frac{NM}{H})},

(21)

which is solved for
x
:

\displaystyle\iff\frac{\widetilde{\rho_{d}}}{\rho_{d}^{\text{(bg.)}}}\sinh\left(T\frac{NM}{H}\right)\mathscr{H}^{-1}\frac{1}{AT\cos(\pi M)}

\displaystyle\propto\exp\left(-x\frac{NM}{H}\right)

(22)

\displaystyle\iff\ln\left(\frac{\widetilde{\rho_{d}}}{\rho_{d}^{\text{(bg.)}}}\sinh\left(T\frac{NM}{H}\right)\mathscr{H}^{-1}\right)-\ln(AT\cos(\pi M))

\displaystyle\propto-x\frac{NM}{H},

(23)

and taking the derivative in
\frac{1}{H}
gives:

\displaystyle\frac{\partial}{\partial\frac{1}{H}}\ln\left(\frac{\widetilde{\rho_{d}}}{\rho_{d}^{\text{(bg.)}}}\sinh\left(T\frac{NM}{H}\right)\mathscr{H}^{-1}\right)

\displaystyle=-xNM.

(24)

Now putting
x=\frac{\hbar}{2\pi k\tau}
and re-substituting
N=2\pi^{2}\frac{k\,m_{0}}{\hbar e}
the right-hand term becomes:

xNM=\frac{\pi\,m_{c}}{e\,\tau}.

(25)

On the left-hand side, the expression inside the logarithm is denoted by:

\mathfrak{G}_{d}\equiv\frac{\widetilde{\rho_{d}}}{\rho_{d}^{\text{(bg.)}}}\sinh{\left(NM\frac{T}{H}\right)}\mathscr{H}^{-1}.

(26)

The expression for
\tau
becomes:

\tau=\left(\frac{e}{-m_{c}\pi}\frac{\partial}{\partial\frac{1}{H}}\ln{\mathfrak{G}_{d}}\right)^{-1}.

(27)

The second term in
\mathscr{H}
can be neglected for
\mathfrak{F}\gg H\Rightarrow\left(\frac{H}{2\mathfrak{F}}\right)^{\frac{1}{2}}\gg\left(\frac{H}{2\mathfrak{F}}\right)
, yielding a simplified expression for
\mathfrak{G}_{d}
:

L-K
formula and, by extension, the data is periodic in
\frac{1}{H}
. Thus, to facilitate the
FFT
, evenly spaced data in
\propto\frac{1}{H}
is needed. This is achieved by resampling the data as
R(\frac{1}{H},T=\text{const.})
into bins of
2\cdot 10^{-4}\frac{1}{\text{T}}
. The resampling procedure gives empty bins as
H\rightarrow 0
, which would limit the support of the
FFT
and, in turn, result in a low frequency resolution. To remedy this, values for empty bins are linearly interpolated.

V.2
Estimation of the fermion cyclotron radius

In order to substantiate the 2D character of the
SdH
oscillations, the fermion cyclotron radius
r_{c}
(in real space) is estimated by assuming that
S_{\text{F}}
is the area of a circular Fermi surface cross section:

\displaystyle r_{c}=\frac{\hbar k_{\text{F}}}{\mu_{0}He}.

(S1)

The Fermi wavevector
k_{\text{F}}
is related to
S_{\text{F}}
:

\displaystyle k_{\text{F}}=\sqrt{\frac{S_{\text{F}}}{\pi}},\quad r_{c}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mathrel{\rotatebox[origin]{180.0}{$\Lsh\,$}}}}{{=}}\sqrt{\frac{2\hbar}{e}}\frac{\sqrt{\mathfrak{F}}}{\mu_{0}H}.

(S2)

The smallest attainable value of
r_{c}
in this work is achieved with
\mu_{0}H=7\,\text{T}
and
\mathfrak{F}=193\,\text{T}
. The resulting cyclotron radius is
r_{c}\approx 72\,\text{nm}
and the diameter is
d_{c}\approx 144\,\text{nm}
. This is significantly larger than the thicknesses of the considered samples, which range from
8\,\text{nm}
to
26\,\text{nm}
. Therefore, the obtained values of
\mathfrak{F}
cannot result from oscillations around an in-plane axis.

V.3
Implementation of the weak antilocalization fitting

The implementation of the Hurwitz
\zeta
function
\zeta\left(s,a\right)=\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}(n+a)^{-s}
is challenging, due to the fact, that it cannot be evaluated analytically, since the sum
\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}(n+a)^{-s}
diverges to
+\infty
for
s=\frac{1}{2}
and
a\geq\frac{1}{2}
. Mathematica
Inc. 2019
calculates the sum
via
analytic continuation and is used to generate
1000
precomputed values for
s=\frac{1}{2}
and
a\in[0.1;1000]
(logarithmically spaced). The fitting function interpolates between these precomputed values to evaluate
\zeta
. The least-squares fit of the
WAL
is hindered by the fact that the
WAL
peak occurs only over a limited range of
H
and the sum of squares is not strongly enough affected by an improper
WAL
peak fit. This is remedied by assigning lower error
\text{err}_{\sigma}
to the data points around
H=0
:

\text{err}_{\sigma}(H)\propto\frac{1}{1+\frac{W}{(W\,H)^{2}+1}},\quad W\in\mathbb{R}_{+}

(S3)

The error is related to the weighting
w
of the data points during the least-squares fit
via
:

\displaystyle w(H)=(\text{err}_{\sigma}(H))^{-2},\quad

(S4)

where
\text{err}_{\sigma}(H)
is the error in
\sigma
. The error
\text{err}_{\sigma}
and corresponding weight
w
for
W=30\,\text{T${}^{-2}$}
are depicted in Fig.
S1
as function of applied magnetic field.

Figure S1:
Assigned weight
w
(log-scale) and

as function of applied magnetic field for
W=30\,\text{T${}^{-2}$}
for usage in the least-squares fitting of data containing a
WAL
peak around
H=0
.
