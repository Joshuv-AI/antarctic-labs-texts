LDL hypercholesterolemia in children: genetic influence and response to lifestyle advice - follow-up of the Fr1dolin-trial
Authors: Stefan Arens, Ursa Sustar, Karin Lange, Thomas Danne, Tadej Battelino, Urh Groselj, Olga Kordonouri
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Background and aimsAlthough familial hypercholesterolemia (FH) is common and associated with significant cardiovascular morbidity, screening programs are scarce. Furthermore, the effectiveness of nutritional advice for parents on children’s LDL cholesterol (LDL-C) levels is unclear, especially in FH versus non-FH hypercholesterolemia.MethodsIn the Fr1dolin study, 367 of 14,513 children in Lower Saxony and Hamburg were positively screened for LDL hypercholesterolemia with an LDL-C > 135 mg/dl (> 3.5 mmol/l). The screening was considered positive if the first finding of hypercholesterolemia was confirmed in a second sample. These 367 children were invited to a follow-up examination including an extended lipid status, nutritional counseling and genetic analysis.Results289 out of 367 screen-positive children (57.4% girls) were followed up for 12 months; 191 (66.1%) underwent genetic testing. Children with a FH-causing pathogenic or likely pathogenic variant (n = 40, 20.9%) had significantly higher LDL-C levels than children without a variant or with a variant of uncertain significance (p < 0.001). After the nutritional counselling and after 6 and 12 months of follow-up, LDL-C levels did not improve significantly (median LDL-C at baseline 142 mg/dl, and 140 mg/dl at 6 and 12 months follow-up, respectively).ConclusionsA population-based screening program detects LDL-hypercholesterolemia in up to 2.5% of children, with a significant proportion of them having FH genetic variants, supporting early detection. One-time nutritional advice had little effect on LDL-C in general, but particularly in FH, highlighting on the one hand need for structured lifestyle programs and on the other hand early genetic testing in population based FH screening.

Introduction

In most people, hypercholesterolemia is multifactorial and highly caused by an unhealthy lifestyle and therefore only becomes apparent at an older age, whereas children with familial hypercholesterolemia (FH) already have an elevated LDL cholesterol level from birth. Heterozygous FH is the most common congenital metabolic disease and is associated with significantly increased cardiovascular morbidity even in young adulthood [1, 2]. The incidence of FH is usually given as 1:200 to 1:300 depending on the population; however, in Europe more than 95% of people affected are unaware of their own condition [1–4].

The morbidity and mortality associated with FH can be reduced to the level of that of the general population by identifying those affected at an early stage and starting treatment in good time as a child [1, 5]. Nevertheless, there are almost no comprehensive screening programs for early detection worldwide; as today only Slovenia has a population-wide universal screening program in childhood [6, 7].

In Germany, the current guidelines recommend that every child, regardless of family history of cardiovascular disease, should undergo a one-time cholesterol test; the optimal age for screening is around 5 years [8]. However, this recommendation is only implemented to a very limited extent, as the statutory health insurance funds only cover cholesterol testing in adolescence and only if there is a positive family history of early cardiovascular events [9].

In cases of proven hypercholesterolemia, the guidelines suggest lifestyle intervention as the first therapeutic measure, regardless of the type of hypercholesterolemia [8]. However, there is insufficient evidence to prove the effectiveness of lifestyle interventions in FH [10].

The Fr1dolin trial was a feasibility study for the early detection of FH and type 1 diabetes at population level [11]. Collecting these data on LDL hypercholesterolemia at pre-school age should help to overcome the limitations of a universal screening program for FH in Germany. The aim of this one-year follow-up study was to evaluate whether nutritional advice as offered under the current conditions of the health care system shows therapeutic success and leads to lower LDL-C levels. Furthermore, a better classification of the etiopathogenesis of LDL hypercholesterolemia was pursued by performing molecular genetic testing in children with positive screening results.

Methods

The design of the Fr1dolin trial and key aspects have already been published by our research group [11, 12].

Screening

In summary, families with children aged 2 to 6 years were offered voluntary screening for FH and pre-symptomatic type 1 diabetes. The results relating to type 1 diabetes are not reported in this paper. With the consent of at least one parent or guardian, a capillary blood sample of 200–300 µl was taken during mandatory check-ups or during any other visit to one of the 178 participating pediatric practices in Lower Saxony or Hamburg, and the samples were sent by post or by laboratory transport to the central screening laboratory at the children’s hospital AUF DER BULT in Hannover. In addition, the families were asked to complete a one-page questionnaire including questions about demographic data of the parents, the presence of cholesterol elevation and/or the presence of early cardiovascular disease (e.g. heart attack, stroke or bypass surgery) in the relatives; early cardiovascular event was defined in this context as before the age of 55 for men and before the age of 65 for women.

LDL cholesterol was not calculated according to the Friedewald formula, but measured directly. Technical details of the LDL cholesterol measurement in this screening program are described elsewhere [12]. LDL cholesterol values ≥135 mg/dl (3.5 mmol/l) were checked with a second blood sample (Screening 2), also with direct measurement of LDL-C; if both values were ≥135 mg/dl, the families were asked to present their child at the study center or at one of the 13 cooperating pediatric lipid centers in Lower Saxony or Hamburg.

Extended lipid analysis, genetic testing and follow-up

Extended diagnostics were carried out at the pediatric lipid center in fasting conditions including the measurement of total, LDL and HDL cholesterol, triglycerides, apolipoprotein B, lipoprotein(a) as well as blood count, creatinine, transaminases, glucose and urine status.

Parents received a standardized one-hour counselling on nutrition for hypercholesterolemia from a certified nutritionist and printed educational material focused on hypercholesterolemia in childhood. This included also counselling on optimal physical activity. Parents learned about the basics of fat metabolism and the principles of a child-friendly nutrition in accordance with the recommendations of the national pediatric societies. The recommendations are based on the guidelines of pediatric medical associations [8] and are tailored to the individual patient and their family. Details on the content of the nutritional counseling are listed in the appendix.

In order to evaluate the success of these measures and to further control hypercholesterolemia, the families were asked to present their children for a follow-up examination after 6 and 12 months.

Furthermore, the families were offered molecular genetic testing for FH-associated variants. EDTA blood (2–5 ml) was taken for genetic testing and frozen at -18 °C. The frozen samples were transported on dry ice to the laboratory of the University Children’s Hospital, Ljubljana, Slovenia, where next generation sequencing (NGS) was used as described previously [6]. Briefly, genomic DNA was isolated from whole blood sample according to the established laboratory protocols using FlexiGene isolation kit (Qiagen, Germany). Samples for NGS for detection of coding and promoter regions of ABCA1, ABCG5, ABCG8, ALMS1, APOA1, APOA5, APOB, APOC2, APOC3, APOE, CREB3L3, GPIHBP1, LDLR, LDLRAP1, LIPA, LMF1, LPL, and PCSK9 genes were prepared using custom designed xGen® Lockdown® NGS Probes (Integrated DNA Technologies Inc., USA). Samples were sequenced on MiSeq sequencer with MiSeq Reagent kit v3 (both Illumina, USA) following the manufacturer’s protocol. The bioinformatics analysis of genomic data was provided locally, at the Institute of Special Laboratory Diagnostics, University Children’s Hospital Ljubljana, following GATK recommendations and guidelines of best analytical practice (Broad Institute, USA). The guidelines and standards of American College of Medical Genetics and Genomics and the Association for Molecular Pathology were followed to classify the variants according to their pathogenicity [13]. Single nucleotide variants and small duplications/deletions with potentially disease-causing effect were confirmed by targeted Sanger DNA sequencing to reduce the possibility of false positive results.

Statistical analysis

Children with pathogenic variant in the genetic analysis and children with likely pathogenic variant were summed up in one group. In an additional analysis children were categorized in three groups according to their lipoprotein (a) level, which is considered as an additional cardiovascular risk (CVR) factor: <30 mg/dl (normal), 30–50 mg/dl (borderline elevated CVR) and > 50 mg/dl (elevated CVR) [14].

Descriptive statistics of the study cohort were presented. Numbers and percentages or median and interquartile range [IQR] were given. Statistical analysis was performed using the statistical software IBM SPSS Statistics Version 27.0. Differences between groups were analyzed using the Friedman test for related samples and the Kruskal-Wallis test for independent samples. A statistically significant difference was assumed with a two-sided level of p < 0.05 for each test.

Ethic vote

All procedures performed in studies involving human participants were in accordance with the ethical standards of the Ethics Committee of Hannover Medical School (MHH, Number of Approval 7089/20.01.2016 and 27.09.2018) and with the Helsinki declaration and its latest revisions or comparable ethical standards.

Results

From 14,513 of the 15,009 screened children LDL cholesterol levels were obtained, in the remaining 496 children LDL cholesterol could not be measured due to small sample volume. 367 of the 14,513 children (2,5%) had twice a LDL cholesterol of ≥135 mg/dl (3.5 mmol/l). These children were invited to participate in the follow-up program. A total of 289 (78.7%) children were included in the Fr1dolin follow-up study and their data are presented here. Genetic tests searching for a monogenetic cause of FH were carried out in 191 children (66.1%). Figure 1 shows a flowchart detailing the study procedure.

In 161 (55.1%) children, relatives with hypercholesterolemia were reported, predominantly the parents: in 57 (19.7%) children the fathers had elevated cholesterol, in 41 (14.2%) children the mothers and in 9 (3.1%) children both parents. Only a small proportion of siblings or 2nd or 3rd degree relatives were reported to have elevated cholesterol.

Relatives with premature cardiovascular disease were reported in 108 (37.4%) children, but predominantly in 2nd and 3rd degree relatives and only rarely in the parents; overall, only 11 (3.8%) children had a parent already affected by premature cardiovascular disease.

Clinical abnormalities indicating hypercholesterolemia, such as xanthelasma or xanthomas, were not found in any of the 289 children.

Median LDL-C was 147 [139–160, IQR] mg/dl at the Fr1dolin screening and 142 [124–160] mg/dl at the baseline examination of the follow-up, indicating stable conditions. Total cholesterol was 206 [185–227] mg/dl, HDL cholesterol 53 [44–62] mg/dl, triglyceride 58 [47–77] mg/dl, lipoprotein(a) 10.7 [5.4–48.2] mg/dl, and apolipoprotein B 1.08 [0.92–1.24] g/l.

At or shortly after the baseline examination, 241 (83.4%) children and their families received a structured nutritional counselling. Remaining 48 (16.6%) children did not receive any dietary intervention due to various circumstantial reasons, including restrictions in regard to the Covid-19 pandemic. In terms of sociodemographic and clinical criteria, there was no difference between the groups with and without nutritional counseling.

In 191 children (66.1%), genetic testing was performed after written consent was obtained from at least one parent. Variants in the genes coding for FH were found in 40 (20.9%) children: 23 (12.0%) had a pathogenic and 17 (8.9%) a likely pathogenic variant. As a result, 40 children were diagnosed with genetically confirmed FH which would give a prevalence of 0.28% of the entire cohort, which is similar to that reported worldwide. But as only 66.1% of children with hypercholesterolemia underwent genetic testing this may be an underestimate. Further 40 (20.9%) children with possible FH were identified with a VUS, in 111 (58.1%) children no variant was found in the investigated genetic panel. All variants found were heterozygous; no homozygous or biallelic form of FH was detected. A table listing the pathogenic and likely pathogenic variants identified is included in the Appendix (Table 3).

Of the 40 children with genetically confirmed FH, 25 (62.5%) showed a variant in the LDLR gene, 11 (27.5%) in APOB, and none in PCSK9. Four (10.0%) children had a variant in another FH-causing gene. The 40 children with a VUS were divided into 14 (35.0%) LDLR, 15 (37.5%) APOB, 4 (10.0%) PCSK9, and 7 (17.5%) others.

Follow-up examinations were carried out after 6 months in 175 (60.6%) children and after 12 months in 107 (37.0%). The course of LDL-C levels during the 12 months of follow-up is presented in Table 2. In the total cohort, there was no significant change in LDL-C levels during follow-up (p = 0.855).

Discussion

The Fr1dolin trial was the first population-based screening study for FH in preschool children in Germany. The study clearly showed that screening at this age is feasible and effective [12].

Nutritional counselling

In the present evaluation, which included preschool children with LDL hypercholesterolemia detected with the screening and subsequently followed-up over 12 months, a specific nutritional advice to their parents did not lead to a significant reduction in LDL-C levels; there were no significant effects on the level of LDL-C over the 12 months following dietary advice and recommendations for optimal physical activity.

The value of lifestyle intervention in heterozygous FH is sometimes controversially discussed in the literature [16]. Although insufficient data is available, the prevailing view is that lifestyle modifications can only marginally influence LDL-C in cases of genetic hypercholesterolemia. However, Fahed et al. were able to show that a healthy lifestyle, based on diet, exercise, non-smoking status and absence of obesity, also reduces the risk of coronary heart disease in adults with an FH variant compared to variant carriers with a lifestyle considered unhealthy [17].

In contrast, however, there are hardly any studies on the influence of lifestyle modifications on heterozygous FH in childhood. Capra et al. were able to show in 115 Italian children and adolescents with FH that a change in diet over a period of one year led to a statistically significant but only slight reduction in cholesterol and LDL-C [18]. Rodriguez-Borjabad et al. found that in a sample of Spanish and Norwegian children with heterozygous FH, dietary fibre intake was inversely correlated with the level of apoB-containing lipoproteins (mainly LDL-C), but there was no significant influence of diet on the overall lipid profile [19]. A similar association between diet and lipid levels was reported by Torvik et al. in 54 Norwegian children and adolescents with FH, in whom saturated fat intake correlated positively with LDL-C and apolipoprotein B [20]. In a small subgroup of 10 children not on statins or plant sterols, LDL-C decreased significantly (median − 22%) after dietary counselling. Unlike our one-time intervention, however, this counselling was repeated at every clinic visit over a median of two years, and the children were considerably older than our preschool cohort. This difference in intervention intensity and duration may explain the discrepant results and further supports the need for repeated, structured lifestyle programs rather than a single counselling session. Individual studies investigated the effects of specific nutritional interventions in childhood with varying results [21, 22].

In contrast, to our knowledge, the present study is one of the first to systematically investigate the effect of a one-time standardised dietary counselling on LDL-C levels in children with LDL hypercholesterolemia as early as preschool age, additionally comparing the cohorts with or without FH causing genetic variants. It is plausible that a one-time knowledge transfer was not enough to fundamentally change the complex everyday life of a family with a preschool child, but this approach reflects real-world conditions rather than a complex intervention that encompasses all areas of the child’s life, including home, kindergarten, etc. There is therefore still insufficient evidence that a lifestyle intervention in early childhood leads to a relevant reduction in LDL-C in children with heterozygous FH. To our opinion, an extended lifestyle intervention program for families of affected children could be evaluated. It should consist of modules on healthy eating and cooking, optimization of physical activity as well as behavior therapeutic elements in order to modify the whole family’s lifestyle.

Screening

Only 11 of the 289 children had a parent with early-onset cardiovascular disease; for the vast majority of the remaining 278 children, therefore, hypercholesterolemia would not have been diagnosed so early. This underlines very clearly the need for population-based screening.

Furthermore, the early identification of affected children is not only of crucial importance for the children themselves; through a reverse cascade screening program, which should logically follow on from this, other affected family members can also be identified and referred for treatment.

Genetics

In terms of genetic status, the Fr1dolin study showed significantly higher LDL-C levels in children with pathogenic or likely pathogenic variants compared to children without evidence of a variant or to children with a VUS. This difference persisted over the entire follow-up and remained significant. The result showed that in preschool children with significant hypercholesterolemia, the genetic status was essentially responsible for the level of LDL cholesterol. This result is consistent with current international experience, which has been demonstrated in numerous studies, but predominantly in children across the age spectrum up to 18 years. Only few studies exist that provide evidence of this even at pre-school age [3, 23–25].

However, it also seems logical that genetic variants can have a greater influence in a cohort in which lifestyle and dietary habits have not yet been able to develop such a resounding influence due to the young age.

Only the children without a genetic FH variant showed in the 12-month follow up a difference in LDL cholesterol levels which, although statistically significant, was only marginal. It remains possible that this difference in the absence of a genetic variant shows that LDL-C can be influenced by a lifestyle intervention, but the differences in LDL-C levels are so small that the clinical relevance could be questioned.

Although children with VUS in the present study showed similar LDL-C levels on average to children without variants, these results must always be assessed in the light of the current classification of genetic findings; with increasing overall examination numbers, individual VUS are categorized as pathogenic or likely pathogenic [26, 27]. In few individual children in our cohort, cascade screening in the family also showed that a variant originally categorized as VUS must be regarded as FH-causing.

The LDL-C values in the present cohort differ significantly between the groups according to the genetic variant class, however, they still show a slight overlap, so that the LDL-C values alone cannot be used to differentiate between heterozygous FH and non-FH-hypercholesterolemia with sufficient certainty. These results thus support the approach of including genetic diagnostics as early as possible in the diagnostic process for childhood LDL hypercholesterolemia, as neither the absolute level of LDL-C nor clinical risk scores can accurately reflect the probability of FH. This was recently shown for the most used clinical scores (Simon-Broome criteria, MEDPED score and an EAS guideline derived score) [28]. The importance of genetics in the diagnostic process of possible FH is underpinned by the fact that pathogenic FH variant triggers significant cardiovascular morbidity even at lower LDL-C levels [29].

Another advantage of early implementation of genetic testing in screening for FH is the fact that knowledge of genetic findings of FH leads to better concordance with therapy [30]. Among other aspects, this question will be addressed in the study “Vroni” and “Vroni in the North”, in cooperation with the German Heart Centre Munich, in which genetic testing is implemented earlier in the diagnostic process [31].

Lipoprotein(a)

In the present study, 23.6% of the children with LDL hypercholesterolemia had also significantly elevated Lp(a) levels above 50 mg/dl. This is largely consistent with the observations in large population-based studies [14]. As expected, the Lp(a) value in the present cohort had no statistically detectable influence on LDL-C levels, either at baseline or at follow-up. Nevertheless, Lp(a) is considered to be an additional risk factor, especially in children without a disease-causing variant [32].

Limitations

The Fr1dolin follow-up study was limited by incomplete confirmatory sampling and follow-up participation. Because Fr1dolin screened simultaneously for diabetes-specific antibodies and LDL cholesterol, families and pediatricians may have prioritized diabetes findings over an initially elevated LDL-C result. Follow-up participation was further reduced by COVID-19 restrictions on outpatient care in 2020–2022 and by insufficient communication of its importance by the participating pediatricians. Some families of children aged 2–5 years may have considered prompt follow-up unnecessary because pharmacotherapy could not begin before age 6, while others may have underestimated cardiovascular risk in apparently healthy children.

Another limitation is the limited tracking of adherence to the recommended lifestyle changes.

Genetic analyses were only available in 66.1% of the children concerned. Problems during the Covid-19 pandemic also played a decisive role here, as genetic analyses could only be offered with great delay at times due to capacity constraints. In addition, some families refused genetic testing on principle, mainly out of fear of negative consequences, such as rejection by life insurance companies and the like.

Conclusions

The follow-up of the Fr1dolin trial clearly shows that the level of LDL-C is significantly higher at preschool age in the presence of a genetic variant for FH, enabling an early detection of FH. Standardized one-time nutritional advice alone cannot significantly influence LDL cholesterol levels in this group of children. There might be a need for extended structured programs for lifestyle modification for families with young affected children. The results of this study support feasibility for the introduction of population-based screening for FH and emphasize the need for early implementation of genetic testing in the diagnostic procedures.
