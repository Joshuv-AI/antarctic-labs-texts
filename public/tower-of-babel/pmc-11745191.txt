Hidradenitis Suppurativa and Peripheral Ulcerative Keratitis
Authors: Indira Acharya, Mohamed I Jalloh, Caroline D Trevisan, Christopher J Haas
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractThe co-occurrence of inflammatory eye disease and hidradenitis suppurativa (HS) is uncommon; ocular involvement typically manifests as anterior uveitis. In this report, we present a unique case of peripheral ulcerative keratitis (PUK) where both infectious and rheumatological workup was negative, leading us to consider the possibility of an association between this condition and severe HS. As far as we are aware, there have only been six documented cases of PUK associated with HS.Keywords: Inflammatory eye disease, Hidradenitis suppurativa (HS), Anterior uveitis, Peripheral ulcerative keratitis (PUK)

Introduction

HS is an immune-mediated, chronic, and debilitating disease characterized by frequent relapses.1 It exerts a detrimental impact on the patient’s quality of life, imposing significant social, emotional, and psychological burdens.1 Additionally, HS is a rare cause of inflammatory eye disease with anterior uveitis being the most common manifestation, followed by episcleritis/scleritis and various forms of keratitis (such as interstitial keratitis, mooren-type ulceration, and PUK).2 PUK is typically characterized by the clinical triad of rapidly advancing, crescent-shaped region of thinning in the outer cornea, an erosion in the epithelium, and an inflammatory infiltration in the cornea.3 Lack of recognition and delayed diagnosis of PUK in association with HS, deprive many patients of receiving timely and appropriate treatment, resulting in permanent vision loss.4 Therefore, it is crucial to raise awareness about HS associated clinical features, risk factors, and potential comorbidities, including inflammatory eye disease to ensure timely management and prevent debilitating consequences.4

Case presentation

A man in his mid 50’s presented to the emergency department due to a week-long increase in bilateral eye pain and blurred vision. The pain was described as burning, 8/10 in intensity, with associated radiation to the forehead. It was accompanied by excessive lacrimation and increased eye pain upon eye movement and exposure to light. There were no noted alleviating factors. Furthermore, he consistently experienced blurry vision in both eyes, with a significant loss of visual acuity leading to seeing only the silhouette of objects.

During the past week, the patient also reported dysuria and increased pain and discharge from his right groin and bilateral buttock HS lesions. He denied experiencing dryness of the eyes or mouth, joint or muscle pain, joint swelling, oral or mucosal lesions, excessive hair loss, fatigue, shortness of breath, fever, chills, cough, mucopurulent eye discharge, excessive nasal or eye itchiness, sneezing, nasal congestion, weakness, dizziness, oral sores, new rashes, diarrhea, or Raynaud’s phenomenon. There was no recent travel, exposure to toxins, or insect bites prior to the onset of symptoms. He is a current smoker with a 14 packs year history; he denied using recreational drugs or alcohol. Family history was positive for HS in the patient’s mother and sister.

Medical history was notable for hypertension, gout, and a 20-year history of HS with multiple past incisions and drainages. The patient also suffered a left corneal ulceration, diagnosed two years prior and was managed with tobramycin/dexamethasone eyedrops for a suspected infectious etiology. Eight months later he also developed a right corneal perforation, presumably secondary to scratching the eye with the same hand used to scratch an HS ulcer, as corneal culture revealed scant diphtheroid infection, though diphtheroid species are considered a nonpathogenic contaminant of the human eye. The perforation was sealed with glue and did not require corneal transplant as the glued area maintained effective closure. There was also concern about inflammatory eye disease due to the right corneal haze, telangiectasias, and scaly erythema of the right eyelid. However, the immunological workup was negative, and dermatologists considered it to be consistent with dermatitis. He received oral doxycycline, erythromycin, and cyclosporine eyedrops with recommendations to follow up with ophthalmology outpatient, but was not seen for 2 years.

On most recent presentation the patient was noted to have slight sinus tachycardia (110 beats per minute) but was normotensive with a preserved respiratory rate and oxygen saturation on ambient air. Physical examination revealed a well-appearing man with actively discharging hyperkeratotic nodules, draining tracts, and fibrotic scarring in the right groin. Multiple patchy scattered fistulous tracts with dermal changes were observed in the bilateral gluteal region extending to the anal canal, indicative of Hurley stage III HS (Fig. 1). Ophthalmology evaluation revealed significantly reduced visual acuity in both eyes, with the left eye being worse (right eye: 20/200, left eye: hand motion). Bilateral conjunctival injection, diffuse corneal neovascularization, and peripheral ulceration were observed in the right eye (Fig. 2).

Initial laboratory diagnostics (Table 1) showed elevated acute phase reactants with a negative infectious workup including Chlamydia, Neisseria, HIV, HSV, Syphilis, Hepatitis C virus, tuberculosis and Lyme. Reactive arthritis was considered but ruled out due to the absence of joint pain and negative urinalysis and HLAB27 test. Behcet’s disease was in our differential but did not meet international diagnostic criteria.5 Ocular sarcoidosis was ruled out given absence of intraocular signs suggestive of the diagnosis - keratic precipitate, iris nodules, vitreous opacities, chorio-retinal lesions - that was further supported by a normal ACE level, normal liver enzymes, and an unremarkable chest radiograph.

Distinguishing between cutaneous manifestations of Crohn’s disease and perianal HS lesions can be challenging. Perianal ulcerative lesions with endoanal fistulas involving the anal sphincter, along with gastrointestinal symptoms, suggest Crohn’s disease.4 On the other hand, HS is more likely when intertriginous areas are involved. A cutaneous manifestation of Crohn’s disease was considered in our patient, but he denied relevant symptoms (diarrhea, abdominal pain, blood in stool or recent weight loss) and a screening colonoscopy performed three years prior was unremarkable. Lupus and rheumatoid arthritis were unlikely causes of his peripheral ulcerative keratitis due to the absence of associated clinical features (skin rash, new hair loss, arthralgia, oral lesions, morning stiffness) and negative serologic work-up.

Hailey-Hailey disease, also known as benign familial pemphigus, shares similarities with HS, as it also causes painful recurrent lesions in intertriginous areas, however, it is characterized by erythematous vesicular plaques that can crust and form painful cracks.4 The presence of chronic scarred wounds accompanied by discharging sinus tracts instead of blisters with sores were inconsistent with Hailey-Hailey disease.

Initial treatment for PUK included erythromycin ophthalmic ointment four times a day in both eyes, cyclopentolate 1% eyedrops thrice daily in both eyes, oral ibuprofen 600 mg three times daily, and oral Valacyclovir 500 mg thrice daily for possible HSV-related keratitis. For HS, he was started on oral doxycycline 100 mg twice daily, oral Metformin 500 mg once a day, and oral zinc gluconate 90 mg once a day. Despite 5 days of medical therapy, the patient continued to experience excessive discharge from his HS lesions as well as ongoing conjunctival injection, irritation, and poor visual acuity. Additional laboratory diagnostics (HSV IgM, chlamydia, gonorrhea, syphilis) were negative and valacyclovir was discontinued. Prednisolone acetate 1% eyedrops twice daily in both eyes was prescribed in the setting of an unlikely infectious etiology.

By day eight, the patient showed slight improvement in the discharge from HS lesions as well as his bilateral conjunctival injection. Furthermore, he consistently experienced blurry vision in both eyes, with a significant loss of visual acuity, able to see only the silhouette of objects. Ophthalmology re-evaluation showed slight improvement of his vision with associated reduction in the noted conjunctival irritation. Due to partial improvement in his eye symptoms with the use of topical steroids, the ocular findings were thought to be due to an ongoing autoimmune process potentially related to the underlying, poorly controlled HS. He started a tapering dose of oral prednisone. On the third day of steroid therapy, his visual issues and conjunctival injection resolved and there was only a scanty amount discharge from the HS. The patient was discharged on tobramycin-dexamethasone eyedrops, Artificial Tears® and erythromycin ointment for his ocular manifestation, and oral doxycycline for HS. He was discharged with plans to follow up in the rheumatology clinic for initiation of steroid sparing maintenance immunosuppression with Adalimumab.

Discussion

HS, also referred to as acne inversa or Verneuil’s disease, is a complex inflammatory condition that impacts roughly 1% of the general population.1 In the United States, its reported prevalence is around 0.10%.1,6 HS presents as tender nodules, abscesses, sinus tracts or fistulas, with the subsequent development of extensive scarring, primarily in skin folds such as the axillary, inguinal, gluteal, inframammary and perianal regions.1,7 It predominantly affects females, with a ratio of 3:1.4

The underlying etiology of HS appears to be multifactorial - genetic susceptibility, positive family history, obesity, hormonal factors, tight clothing, tobacco smoking, certain medications (such as oral contraceptives and lithium), and other underlying conditions like acne conglobata, pilonidal sinus, Crohn’s disease and α-1-antitrypsin deficiency.6 The pathophysiology involves blockage of hair follicles in the folliculo-pilosebaceous units, followed by follicular rupture and activation of both innate and adaptive immune systems, ultimately leading to the development of HS lesions.1 Studies indicate involvement of Th17 cells and increased expression of interleukin-17 (IL-17), tumor necrosis factor, and IL-1β.1,4 Additionally, a minority of patients with HS have been found to carry genetic mutations in gamma-secretase genes, which predispose to abnormal epidermal proliferation and differentiation. 4 In our case, the patient had a positive family history and a history of tobacco smoking, which may have contributed to his predisposition to developing HS.

The diagnosis of HS relies solely on clinical presentation - recurring episodes, multiple affected areas, and scarring.4,7 Regular blood tests typically show no abnormalities, but inflammatory markers like leukocytes, ESR, and CRP may be elevated during active inflammation or in severe cases.4 Biopsy is not mandatory for diagnosis, but it may be useful to rule out squamous cell carcinoma in severe HS cases with uncertain diagnoses.8 In our case, the absence of gastrointestinal symptoms did not support a diagnosis of cutaneous manifestation of Crohn’s disease, and the presence of chronic scarred wounds with discharging sinuses rather than vesicular lesions did not align with Hailey-Hailey disease. Although rare, it is worth briefly assessing the patient’s ocular history and symptoms during routine visits to identify those with HS who may require evaluation by an ophthalmologist.

While HS is often associated with spondyloarthropathies, pyoderma gangrenosum (PG), and inflammatory bowel disease,1,4 the presence of inflammatory eye disease is uncommon,7 with a higher prevalence observed among females and individuals of African American descent.9 PUK is an uncommon condition, with estimated occurrence rates ranging from 0.2 to 3 individuals per million annually.3 In a study involving 20 patients with both HS and inflammatory eye disease, it was observed that 35% of these patients did not have any other concomitant chronic systemic or autoimmune diseases. 2 This finding supports the hypothesis of a shared underlying mechanism between inflammatory eye disease and HS.2 What makes this case unique is the absence of concurrent autoimmune or rheumatologic conditions, leading us to consider HS as the probable cause of his ophthalmic manifestation. Adding to its uniqueness, the patient is a male, contrasting with the typical presentation of the condition.

Treating HS with PUK is challenging due to a limited understanding of its underlying mechanisms and the recurrent nature of the disease.4 Treatment depends on the number, type, distribution, location of lesions, and any associated risk factors or comorbidities. Current guidelines suggest a comprehensive approach involving various strategies. Adjuvant therapies like pain management, smoking cessation, weight loss, superinfection treatment, hygiene practices, and topical wound dressings are recommended.4 Additionally, topical and systemic agents such as antibiotics, antiinflammatory drugs, and biologics, as well as surgical interventions like excisional and laser surgeries, may be employed.4

Although the rationale for antimicrobial therapy in HS is not well-established, it is believed to have anti-inflammatory effects. Tetracycline is the preferred systemic antibiotic for skin lesions, while rifampicin plus Clindamycin is recommended for non-responsive cases.10 In cases of inflammatory eye disease associated with HS, topical steroids are typically the first-line treatment. Immunosuppressive therapies such as anti-TNF-α therapy and adalimumab are necessary, especially for patients who do not achieve remission with topical treatments. 2,7 In a few instances, Cyclophosphamide has demonstrated efficacy in reducing the recurrence of PUK in cases associated with HS.8,11 Achieving complete resolution of HS is uncommon, especially in severe cases.7,12 Furthermore, there is currently no research available that explains the long-term outcome of ocular involvement in HS.

Conclusion

Physicians need to be knowledgeable about the connection between HS and inflammatory eye disease, which could be a result of immune dysregulation. Immunosuppressants like anti-TNF-α agents are essential for individual with HS who do not attain remission with topical therapies.

Additional research is necessary to evaluate the long-term progression and outcomes associated with ocular involvement in individuals with HS. Likewise, there is a necessity for further studies to explore effective treatment approaches specifically tailored for ocular involvement in HS.
