Mechanically resilient 3D lattices with soft–hard bicontinuous nanostructures via phase-separated interpenetrating composites
Authors: Na Ye Jang, Seo Rim Park, Seok Kim, Young Tae Cho
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractAchieving simultaneously high strength and toughness in lightweight architected materials remains a fundamental challenge because of the inherent tradeoff between these properties. Herein, we propose a novel strategy for fabricating robust 3D lattice structures by integrating photopolymerization-induced phase separation with the formation of an interpenetrating phase composite (IPC). This approach enables the creation of bicontinuous nanostructures composed of a soft acrylate phase and a hard epoxy phase, which are uniformly distributed within a porous lattice fabricated via digital light processing 3D printing. The resulting soft–hard interface microstructures exhibit remarkable mechanical enhancements. Compared with those of solid structures with the same geometry, the compressive stiffness, strength, and toughness are improved by 35%, 12%, and 47%, respectively. In addition, these structures exhibit excellent fatigue resistance under cyclic loading due to efficient load transfer and energy dissipation through the multiscale bicontinuous network. This scalable and versatile platform not only overcomes the key limitations of traditional IPC fabrication methods but also opens new avenues for designing lightweight, damage-tolerant metamaterials. Introduction

The demand for materials and composites that combine lightweight structural efficiency with high mechanical strength continues to grow across industries such as aerospace, automotive, and biomedicine1–6. For these applications, balancing mechanical performance—specifically strength and toughness—with significant weight reduction is crucial in material selection and engineering design7–10. One promising approach is the use of bioinspired structural materials that incorporate multiscale hierarchical architectures by embedding synthetic materials into lightweight porous architectures11–13. Bioinspired hierarchical structures reduce density while enhancing impact resistance and mechanical stability, primarily via energy dissipation facilitated by interconnected pore networks14–19. Among them, engineered lattice structures have gained prominence owing to their optimized mechanical properties, structural repeatability, and geometric versatility20–22. They can be precisely designed via advanced manufacturing technologies and have considerable potential as next-generation lightweight, high-performance materials.

To improve the mechanical performance of these materials, recent studies have focused on selectively infiltrating functional resins into porous rigid materials to form interpenetrating composites (IPCs) with efficient ductile–hard interfaces23,24. These interfaces are tightly coupled and form a continuous network within the material structure, exploiting the complementary mechanical properties of each phase25,26. Previous studies have focused mainly on improving strength and toughness by filling macroscopic open-cell structures such as lattice structures27,28 or infiltrating porous scaffolds based on particles composed of metals or ceramics29–33 with resin, but these approaches have failed to form robust ductile‒hard interfaces from the nanoscale to the microscale. Poor interfacial quality results in poor mechanical synergy between the constituent phases. Moreover, conventional IPC fabrication methods typically use high-temperature curing34 or high-pressure infiltration16,35, which limits design flexibility and scalability. Therefore, a scalable, efficient, and flexible strategy that preserves a bicontinuous, multiscale soft–hard interface is critically needed26.

This study develops and evaluates a new approach for creating strong and tough 3D-printed architectural materials with a bicontinuous nanostructured soft–hard interface24,25,36. Our fabrication strategy combines (1) digital light processing (DLP)-based 3D printing to fabricate microarchitectural lattices; (2) photopolymerization-induced phase separation (PIPS) to generate bicontinuous nanoporous structures serving as the soft phase37; and (3) capillary-driven epoxy infiltration to form a rigid, interpenetrating hard phase (Fig. 1). This synergistic combination allows strong interfacial bonding between the soft and hard phases, as well as a uniform phase distribution within the 3D-printed microarchitectures. The mechanical performance of the epoxy-infiltrated 3D-printed microarchitecture was evaluated through compression and cyclic fatigue testing. The results indicated significant increases in strength and toughness compared with those of conventional epoxy-coated 3D-printed structures produced via dip-coating38–40. This study demonstrates an efficient method for the scalable fabrication of mechanically resilient and lightweight metamaterials suitable for a wide range of high-performance applications.

Results and discussion

Internal microstructures of porous and solid architectures

Figure 2 presents scanning electron microscopy (SEM) images of the porous 3D-printed microarchitecture (P) and solid 3D-printed structure (S) before and after epoxy infiltration for comparison. Figures 2(a) and (b) show the porous architecture in the as-printed (P) and epoxy-infiltrated (P/E) states, respectively. In the optical image in Fig. 2(a-i), the printed geometry remains well-defined. The low-magnification SEM image in Fig. 2(a-ii) reveals distinct layer boundaries of the printed resin and fine surface textures. The high-magnification SEM image of the cross-section (Fig. 2(a-iii)) confirms the formation of a continuously interconnected porous network with pore sizes ranging from 300 to 500 nm, characteristic of PIPS-derived structures. In contrast, Fig. 2(b-iii) shows uniform epoxy filling throughout the porous network, indicating successful deep infiltration. This self-directed flow of the resin was achieved via capillary action, enabling the formation of an IPC.

Structural analysis of porous (P) and solid (S) architectures before and after epoxy immersion: (a) P, (b) P/E, (c) S, and (d) S/E. Each column shows (i) optical, (ii) surface SEM, and (iii) cross-sectional SEM images of the struts.

Figures 2(c) and (d) show images of the solid structures fabricated under identical conditions in the as-printed (S) and epoxy-coated (S/E) states, respectively. The cross-sectional SEM image (Fig. 2(c-iii)) confirms a dense, nonporous interior, whereas the surface image displays a smooth, featureless morphology. In Fig. 2(d-iii), no significant resin infiltration is observed, even after immersion in the epoxy resin, owing to the absence of internal porosity. These physical changes in the porous microarchitecture and solid structure are presented in Fig. S1.

In contrast to the solid structure, the porous architecture enables deep resin infiltration and uniform phase distribution. This structural difference directly impacts mechanical behavior, as demonstrated by subsequent mechanical performance tests24,33. These results confirm the effectiveness of combining PIPS-based phase separation with IPC formation strategies to enhance the multiscale interfacial architecture41. The comparative pore-size characteristics of PEG 200 and PEG 400 are presented in the Supplementary Information (Fig. S2).

Effect of epoxy immersion time on mechanical performance

Figure 3 shows the mechanical performance evaluation of the porous (P) and solid (S) 3D-printed structures as a function of the epoxy immersion time (0, 2, and 8 h), as evaluated via compression testing. The maximum immersion time was set to 8 h—the longest possible duration for which the epoxy remained stable at the ambient temperature. Figures 3(a) and 3(b) show the stress–strain curves of the porous and solid structures, respectively. For the porous architecture (P, Fig. 3(a)), increasing the immersion time significantly improved the mechanical performance. The ultimate compressive strength increased from 317 kPa (0 h) to 1279 kPa (2 h), reaching 2146 kPa after 8 h. The elastic modulus also increased significantly. These enhancements indicated effective epoxy infiltration, which improved the load distribution through the porous network. In contrast, the solid structure (S, Fig. 3(b)) exhibited only moderate improvement. The ultimate compressive strength increased from 744 kPa (0 h) to 1145 kPa (2 h), reaching 1537 kPa after 8 h. This was due to the external epoxy coating, which remained on the surface without penetrating the solid structure, only strengthening the outer layer but not reinforcing the inner part.

Compressive stress–strain curves of (a) porous and (b) solid architectures at different epoxy immersion times and corresponding comparisons of the strength, modulus, and toughness for (c) porous and (d) solid architectures.

Figures 3(c) and (d) show the compressive strength, elastic modulus, and toughness for P/E and S/E as a function of immersion time. For P/E, the compressive strength increased more than fourfold after 2 h of infiltration and 6.7-fold after 8 h of infiltration compared with the non-infiltrated state. The elastic modulus increased by a factor of approximately 12 and the toughness increased by a factor of > 3.8 after 8 h, indicating a significant increase in the energy-absorption capacity of the entire structure. In contrast, for S/E (Fig. 3(d)), over the same immersion duration, the compressive strength approximately doubled from 744 to 1537 kPa, the elastic modulus increased from 10.6 to 31.9 MPa, and the toughness increased from 525 to 781 kJ/m3. These results experimentally confirm that the bicontinuous porous 3D-printed microarchitecture formed via PIPS facilitates deep epoxy infiltration, enabling the formation of a robust soft–hard interface. The significant mechanical performance improvements in P/E compared to those in S/E highlight the critical role of the structural design of porous 3D-printed microarchitectures in optimizing infiltration pathways and mechanical reinforcement. These findings demonstrate that porous microarchitectures formed via PIPS offer a more effective strategy for enhancing mechanical performance than simple surface coatings on solid structures.

Comparison of compression performance: effect of structural design

Figure 4 presents a comparative analysis of the compressive mechanical performance between porous (P) and solid (S) 3D-printed microarchitectures before and after epoxy immersion. In contrast to the previous section, which examined variations in immersion time, this analysis focuses on structural design as the key differentiator in soft–hard interface formation and mechanical strengthening.

Compressive stress–strain curves of (a) porous (P) and solid (S) architectures before epoxy immersion and (b) epoxy-infilled (P/E) and epoxy-coated (S/E) architectures after epoxy immersion. Comparison of the (c) strength, (d) Young’s modulus, and (e) toughness.

Figure 4(a) shows the compressive stress–strain behavior of the 3D-printed structures. P exhibited low stress levels and continuous ductile behavior, whereas S exhibited a relatively high initial modulus with nonlinear behavior under increasing load. This behavior is attributed to the characteristics of the densely filled internal structure of S, which promotes stress localization and leads to mechanical instability under compression. Figure 4(b) presents the compressive stress–strain curves of P/E and S/E. In the case of P/E, epoxy infiltrated the porous 3D-printed microarchitecture formed by PIPS and transformed it into an IPC that significantly enhanced the load distribution. In contrast, S/E exhibited limited performance gains owing to epoxy remaining on the exterior surface without permeating the internal matrix. Quantitative analysis confirmed this difference. After 8 h of epoxy immersion, approximately 16.6% of the total volume of the porous structure was filled with epoxy, which corresponded to approximately 33% of the actual pore space when considering the porosity (~ 50%). In contrast, the solid structure contained only approximately 6.4% epoxy relative to its total volume. This disparity in infiltration rates explains the differences in mechanical performance observed between P/E and S/E (see Table S1 in the Supplementary Information). Figures 4(c)–(e) show the improvements in mechanical performance. Compared with S/E, P/E exhibited increases of approximately 46%, 12%, and 47% in compressive strength, elastic modulus, and toughness, respectively. These gains resulted from the continuous, multiscale soft–hard interface formed inside the structure, which effectively transferred loads and absorbed energy13,41. Similar improvements were observed for other lattice geometries, as shown in Figs. S3 (Kelvin) and S4 (diamond)42.

A stress-drop phenomenon after the elastic region was observed for both P and S43. In the case of P/E, the brittle characteristics of the epoxy resin infiltrating into the PIPS-formed pores reduced the ductility of the entire structure, increasing the sensitivity to local fracture or rupture.

A similar stress drop was observed in S/E, despite the absence of resin infiltration into the structure. This behavior is attributed to localized structural failure caused by load concentration in specific regions of the dense, nonporous structure43.

The differences in mechanical properties are clearly illustrated in the compression-test images in Fig. 5 (i) and the corresponding DIC strain maps (equivalent von Mises strain) in Fig. 5 (ii). In P/E, as compression increased, the deformation was distributed uniformly throughout the structure, and the structural shape remained relatively stable. Moreover, S/E exhibited a sudden collapse due to load localization, with pronounced asymmetric shear failure initiating at the bottom. These results align with the limited improvement in mechanical performance and behavioral characteristics shown in Fig. 4 and demonstrate that a simple surface coating is insufficient to ensure mechanical stability. Resin infiltration into an internal pore network is significantly more effective for enhancing strength, stiffness, toughness, and compression resistance. Further observations of local deformation depending on strut thickness gradients are presented in Fig. S5.

Compression images of porous and solid lattices at 0%, 15%, and 25% strain: (a) porous (P), (b) epoxy-infiltrated porous (P/E), (c) solid (S), and (d) epoxy-coated solid (S/E); (i) optical images, (ii) strain maps. (All scale bars represent 10 mm.).

Fatigue behavior under repeated loading

Figure 6 shows the fatigue mechanical behavior of the epoxy resin-impregnated P/E and S/E samples under cyclic compressive loading. The stress–strain curves and variations in the mechanical properties, specifically the compressive strength and elastic modulus, were analyzed over 500 repeated compression cycles. As indicated by the stress–strain curves (Figs. 6(a) and (b)), both structures exhibited typical fatigue behavior, with the stress level gradually declining as the number of cycles increased owing to damage accumulation inside the structure.

Fatigue behavior of epoxy-infilled (P/E) and epoxy-coated (S/E) lattices under cyclic loading. (a, b) Stress–strain response of (a) P/E and (b) S/E structures. (c, d) Evolution of the (c) compressive strength and (d) Young’s modulus over 500 cycles.

Notably, P/E (Fig. 6(a)) maintained higher stress levels than S/E (Fig. 6(b)), and the slope of the stress–strain curve decreased more gradually with an increasing number of cycles. Figure 6(c) shows the changes in compressive strength over 500 cycles. The P/E ratio exhibited relatively stable behavior, with the strength decreasing from 1800 to 1235 kPa—a reduction of 31%. In contrast, S/E demonstrated relatively low fatigue resistance, with the strength decreasing sharply from 1500 to 830 kPa—a reduction of 45%. Similar trends were observed for the elastic modulus (Fig. 6(d)): it gradually decreased from 35 to 27 MPa (22% reduction) for P/E, whereas it exhibited a steeper decrease from 31 to 17 MPa (45% reduction) for S/E.

These results demonstrate that P/E effectively maintains a bicontinuous soft–hard interface and enables efficient damage dispersion under repeated loading. In contrast, S/E, with a simple epoxy coating, lacks internal load-transfer pathways, leading to localized stress concentrations and accelerated mechanical degradation. The experimental findings confirm that P/E, with its integrated soft–hard interface, represents a robust design strategy for achieving enhanced mechanical durability and stability under both short-term compressive loading and long-term cyclic fatigue conditions44.

Conclusion

To balance lightweight characteristics with high mechanical performance—a key requirement in various industrial fields—this study proposed a novel design strategy for bioinspired hierarchical microstructures. We developed a microarchitecture featuring precisely engineered soft–hard interfaces by integrating PIPS and IPC formation techniques within a DLP-based 3D-printing platform. Through capillary-driven infiltration of epoxy resin into the bicontinuous nanoporous architecture formed via PIPS, we fabricated a bicontinuous soft–hard phase composite with strong interfacial bonding between the soft and hard phases. Experimental results demonstrated that this architecture offers significantly improved structural integrity and mechanical performance compared with conventional surface-coated solid structures.

Furthermore, DLP-based 3D printing provides high design flexibility and scalability, making it a promising platform for fabricating complex geometries and customized material architectures. In addition to mechanical enhancements, this strategy holds potential for multifunctionality, enabling the integration of electrical and thermal functionalities through the incorporation of diverse functional materials such as conductive fillers, magnetic functional particles, and polymer-derived ceramics45,46.

In summary, this study presents a practical and efficient approach for fabricating hierarchical composites with soft–hard interfaces. The proposed method can be utilized to design and manufacture next-generation lightweight, high-performance structural materials for applications in extremely challenging conditions, such as in the aerospace, biomedical, and transportation industries.

However, this study is limited to a specific material system and microstructural scale, and the long-term mechanical durability under periodic environmental stresses requires further investigation. Future research will focus on expanding the material platforms, optimizing infiltration, and validating multifunctionality under real-world operational conditions.

Finally, the strength–density relationship and energy-absorption capacity of the proposed PIPS-based IPC lattice structures were compared with those of existing architected materials and IPCs using an Ashby plot, and their superior performance was clearly demonstrated27–29,35,47,48 (Fig. 7).

Methods

Fabrication of porous microarchitecture: DLP 3D printing via PIPS

A porous lattice architecture was fabricated via 405-nm-wavelength DLP-based 3D printing (Fig. 1(a); DLP 3D Printer, Carima Co., Ltd., Korea) at an irradiation intensity of 5.58 mW/cm2. The photocurable resin consisted of a 1:1 ratio of polyurethane acrylate monomer (CUTK05B, Carima Co., Ltd., Korea) and polyethylene glycol 300 (PEG 300, Tokyo Chemical Industry Co., Ltd., Japan), which were stirred at 300 rpm for 1 h to ensure homogeneity49. The mixture was loaded into the resin tank of the printer, and structures were printed with a 100-µm layer thickness and 8 s of exposure per layer. The PIPS phenomenon occurred during printing under ultraviolet (UV) light, creating a bicontinuous structure50–53. In this study, polyurethane acrylate resin was selected because of its rapid UV-curing capability and excellent flexibility and ductility, which facilitated the formation of an effective soft network during subsequent epoxy infiltration. Printed samples were immersed in isopropyl alcohol (IPA) for 6 h to remove unreacted PEG, forming a microporous network structure (Figs. 1(b) and 1(c)). The washed 3D-printed microarchitectures were immediately immersed in the epoxy resin for infiltration (Figs. 1(b) and 1(d)). Their mechanical properties under different drying conditions after IPA washing are presented in Figs. S6 and S7.

Epoxy resin infiltration and curing

Epoxy resin and a hardener (EB-107LP-2, EpoxySet, USA)38–40 were used to infiltrate the nanoporous architecture. A low-viscosity epoxy resin was selected to increase the pore infiltration efficiency54. This epoxy resin, owing to its low viscosity, was able to deeply infiltrate the porous acrylate network and distribute uniformly; after curing, it provided high stiffness and strength, functioning as a stable supporting framework for the composite. The epoxy and hardener were mixed at a 100:35 mass ratio and stirred at 200 rpm for 1 h to achieve a homogeneous solution. The IPA-filled structures were immersed in this epoxy solution for 8 h, during which the IPA gradually diffused out while the epoxy infiltrated the porous network through capillary action. After epoxy immersion, the specimens were placed vertically at room temperature for approximately 1 h to allow the excess epoxy accumulated on the surface to drain naturally, resulting in only a thin and uniform epoxy coating layer remaining on the surface. The structures were then cured at 80 °C for 3 h to form a soft–hard interpenetrating network (Fig. 1(d)). The properties of the epoxy resin are presented in Table S2.

Fabrication of solid structures

For comparison, solid structures were fabricated under identical printing and postprocessing conditions using polyurethane-acrylate-based monomers (CUTK05B, Carima Co., Ltd., Korea), with PEG excluded from the resin formulation.

Microstructural analysis (SEM imaging)

A low-voltage scanning electron microscope (LV-SEM, Merlin Compact, Carl Zeiss AG, Germany) was used to analyze the surface and cross-sectional microstructures. Before imaging, a 30-nm-thick Pt layer was deposited on the samples via an ion sputter coater to ensure conductivity. The observations were performed at an accelerating voltage of 3 kV to examine the pore morphology, distribution, and epoxy infiltration throughout the surface and cross-sections. The internal pore network and epoxy distribution within the porous microarchitecture were qualitatively compared and evaluated.

Compression tests

Compression tests were conducted to evaluate the mechanical performance of the fabricated structures. The tests were performed using a universal material testing machine (UTM, Z2.5, ZwickRoell GmbH & Co. KG, Germany) with a 2.5-kN load capacity. The samples were compressed at a speed of 2 mm/min to a nominal strain of 70%. The nominal stress–strain curve was derived from load–displacement data, with stress calculated as the applied force divided by the initial cross-sectional area and strain calculated as the displacement divided by the initial specimen height.

Fatigue tests

Fatigue tests were performed under cyclic compressive loading using the same testing machine. The samples were subjected to 500 cycles at a constant speed of 8 mm/min, with compression limited to a nominal strain of 5% within the linear elastic range. The changes in mechanical performance due to cumulative fatigue damage were monitored and analyzed across cycles.
