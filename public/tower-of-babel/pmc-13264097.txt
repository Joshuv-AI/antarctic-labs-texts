Variation in LINE-1 ORF1p expression across histologic and demographic subgroups in non-small cell lung cancers
Authors: Yingshan Wang, Can Li, Kimberly Nguyen, Reid Powell, Muralidhar Kondapaneni, Kenneth S. Ramos
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Highlights•Significant variations in LINE-1 ORF1p expression exist across tumor subtype, race, and age.•LINE-1 ORF1p is higher in lung squamous cell carcinomas than in lung adenocarcinomas and in African American patients compared with Caucasian American patients.•LINE-1 ORF1p expression in tumors increases with age, peaking between 70 and 80 years of age.•Profiling LINE-1 expression may advance precision-based approaches for optimizing clinical management of these malignancies.•Novel interaction between LINE-1 ORF1p and macrophages provides new insights into the tumor immune microenvironment in NSCLC and highlights potential avenues for future therapeutic interventions.

Introduction

Non-small cell lung cancer (NSCLC) remains the leading cause of cancer-related mortality in the United States and worldwide [1,2]. A high degree of variability in lung cancer profiles poses significant challenges for diagnosis, detection, and treatment [3]. Several factors contribute to the heterogeneity of NSCLC, including regional and histological differences between its two major subtypes, namely, lung squamous cell carcinoma (LSQCC) and lung adenocarcinoma (LUAD) [2,4], as well as considerable variation in phenotypic expression among LUAD tumors [5]. Beyond histological variation, demographic factors such as race, sex, and age further shape NSCLC heterogeneity. For example, African American (AA) patients experience a higher disease burden, are less likely to be diagnosed at an early stage, and have higher cancer-related mortality compared to other racial and ethnic groups [[6], [7], [8]]. Survival disparities are also evident, with AA patients having lower 5-year survival rates compared to Caucasian American (CA) patients (16%vs. 19%) [9]. Sex-specific differences add further complexity, with women more likely to develop LUAD, while men are more often diagnosed with LSQCC [10,11]. Additionally, women appear to have greater susceptibility to smoking-associated NSCLC compared to men [10].

Long Interspersed Element-1 (LINE-1) retroelements, a family of non-long terminal repeat (LTR) transposable elements, have been strongly implicated in NSCLC [12,13]. LINE-1 represents the only autonomous transposable element family that remains active in the human genome. Approximately 500,000 LINE-1 copies are present, with most either truncated or carrying internal rearrangements or mutations that impair function [14,15]. Intact LINE-1 elements are ∼6 kb in length and encode two proteins: ORF1p, an RNA-binding protein, and ORF2p, a multifunctional protein with endonuclease and reverse transcriptase (RT) activities. Both proteins are essential for LINE-1 replication and for mobilization of non-autonomous retrotransposons [16]. In most somatic cells, LINE-1 activity is suppressed through epigenetic mechanisms, including DNA methylation and histone modifications [[17], [18], [19]]. However, de-repression of LINE-1 contributes to genomic instability and cancer initiation and progression [[20], [21], [22]]. LINE-1 insertions can result in gene activation or inactivation, chromosomal rearrangements, pseudogene formation, and chimeric transcript generation, all of which have been implicated in tumorigenesis [23]. Notably, expression of LINE-1-encoded proteins—particularly ORF1p—is a hallmark of multiple aggressive malignancies [22,24], whereas ORF2p expression is often undetectable due to deficits in translational efficiency [25,26].

Multiple studies, including ours, have focused on developing methodologies for detecting LINE-1 ORF1p as a diagnostic biomarker, such as ultrasensitive digital immunoassays and analysis of LINE-1 analytes in extracellular vesicles [27,28]. LINE-1 hypomethylation has been linked to invasive growth, advanced disease stage, and specific clinicopathologic features of NSCLC, including histology, p53 status, and smoking history [29,30]. In addition, LINE-1 activity influences NSCLC tumorigenesis through effects on cell metabolism, inflammation, and extracellular matrix remodeling [[31], [32], [33], [34], [35]]. LINE-1 proteins may also function as tumor-associated antigens, as NSCLC patients exhibit significantly higher IgG titers against ORF1p and ORF2p compared to healthy individuals [36]. Collectively, these findings underscore the central role of LINE-1 in NSCLC biology.

Despite these insights, the relationship between LINE-1 ORF1p expression and NSCLC clinicopathologic features remains incompletely defined. In this study, we used immunohistochemistry (IHC) and multiplex immunofluorescence (IF) to evaluate LINE-1 ORF1p expression in NSCLC tumors and matched adjacent non-tumor tissues, integrating these patterns with tumor histology and patient demographics (sex, race, age). We found that ORF1p expression was significantly elevated in NSCLC tumors, particularly LSQCC, compared to non-tumor tissues. Expression was also higher in AA compared to CA patients, and in older males compared to females. Quantitative IF demonstrated strong co-expression of ORF1p with CD68+ macrophages along with their enrichment in the immediate spatial vicinity of tumor cells with high ORF1p expression, suggesting potential regulation by immune infiltration. Together, these results highlight the influence of tumor subtype, race, age, and macrophage presence on LINE-1 ORF1p expression and identify ORF1p as a relevant biomarker for the evaluation of NSCLC heterogeneity.

Immunohistochemistry (IHC) analysis

The mouse anti-LINE-1 ORF1p (#MABC1152, Sigma-Aldrich, Burlington, MA) was used at a dilution of 1:500 [28]. Tissue slides were deparaffinized in xylene and subsequently rehydrated through graded alcohol solutions (100%, 96%, 70%, 50%, 30%). Antigen retrieval was achieved by heat-mediated treatment in citrate buffer (pH 6.0) for 20 min. Slides were washed with PBS and incubated with 2.5% normal goat serum (#ZG0723, Vector Laboratories) for 20 min to block nonspecific binding sites. Primary antibody was applied, and tissue sections incubated at 4 °C overnight. Samples were then treated with ImmPRESS® HRP Goat Anti-Mouse IgG Polymer Reagent (# MP-7452, Vector Laboratories) for 30 min at room temperature. Images were obtained using a Nikon Ds-Ri2 color camera using 40 × 0.95 NA or 10 × 0.45 NA lenses. For each patient sample, ten randomly selected 40x high-power fields were analysed using Image J to quantify LINE-1 ORF1p staining intensity based on color deconvolution. The background signal subtracted from threshold was set at 0–85 and the mean grey value recorded as the intensity of LINE-1 ORF1p. All ten values per sample were included in the statistical analysis to ensure robust assessment of intratumoral heterogeneity. Quantified data were reported as mean ± standard error of mean (S.E.M.), and statistical significance determined using an unpaired t-test with a two-sided significance level of 0.05. Power analysis was conducted a priori to determine the appropriate sample size for robust statistical analyses. Randomization was implemented when allocating samples to ensure an unbiased distribution of experimental units. Blinding was implemented during data collection and analysis to minimize bias.

Immunofluorescence

Tissue slides were deparaffinized in xylene and rehydrated through graded alcohol (100%, 95%, 70%). Endogenous peroxidase activity was quenched in a bath of 95% ethanol and 3% hydrogen peroxide for 20 min. Antigen retrieval was achieved by heating in citrate-based antigen unmasking buffer (# H-3300, Vector Laboratories) for 10 min. Nonspecific background fluorescence was decreased following a 0.05% trypsin digestion at 37 °C for 20 min. Permeabilization of samples was achieved by incubation with 0.5% Tween-20 in PBS for 5 min. Subsequently, the samples were blocked with 5% BSA for 1 h at room temperature prior to incubation overnight with primary antibodies (dilution of 1:200) at 4 °C. Samples were then incubated with a combination of Alexa Fluor 488 goat anti-rabbit (Thermo Fisher Scientific Cat# A-11,008) and Alexa Fluor Plus 594 goat anti-mouse IgG (H+L) (Thermo Fisher Scientific Cat# A32742) as secondary antibodies (dilution of 1:2000) for 1 h at room temperature. Primary antibodies used included mouse anti-LINE-1 ORF1 (Sigma-Aldrich Cat# MABC1152), rabbit anti-CD3 (#ab16669, Abcam), rabbit anti-CD15 (#ab135377, Abcam), rabbit anti-CD68 (#D4B9C, Cell Signaling Technology).

Image analysis

Image analysis was performed using a custom workflow developed using the advanced imaging and integration libraries of Biovia Pipeline Pilot (2023 Server Edition, Dassault Systems). Images were first read into the environment using the bioformats image importer which converted raw nd2 files into individual 16-bit tiff images. The “nuclei” CellPose model (https://doi.org/10.1038/s41592–020–01018-x) was implemented to segment nuclear regions using the default (auto) diameter, a flow threshold of 0.2, and a cell probability of -2. An expert reviewer visually assessed nuclear segmentation quality. Region growing was performed using the nuclear masks as seeds to quantify the peri‑nuclear/cell area. Intensity metrics were collected from each labeled region, and results were exported in CSV format for downstream processing.

Cellular neighborhood enrichment analysis

ORF1p-high cells (top 25% ORF1p intensity in Chan2) and ORF1p-low cells (bottom 25%) were identified in each section to compute the fractional composition of each immune marker-positive population (top 25% intensity in Chan1) within three concentric neighborhood radii: 30, 50, and 100 µm. An enrichment ratio of immune marker-positive cells in ORF1p-high neighborhoods divided by the fraction in ORF1p-low neighborhoods was then calculated per sample, with an enrichment ratio > 1 indicating preferential co-localization with ORF1p-high cells. The Mann-Whitney U test was used to compare fractional composition across the tumor samples examined.

Spatial autocorrelation

Moran's I is a global spatial autocorrelation statistic that quantifies whether a continuous variable (per-cell ORF1p intensity) is spatially clustered (I > 0), randomly distributed (I ≈ 0), or dispersed (I < 0). Moran’s I was computed for each tumor section using k = 8 nearest-neighbor spatial weights (row-standardized), implemented via the libpysal/esda Python libraries with 499 permutations for significance testing. To assess co-expression relationships and to determine if the tissue microenvironmental context of each immune population influenced the degree to which ORF1p-high cells formed organized spatial clusters, we calculated Moran’s I for each cell type solely using ORF1p (Chan2) intensity values and cell coordinates and without reference to the immune marker channel.

Results

LINE-1 ORF1p expression is elevated in NSCLC, especially in LSQCC

We evaluated LINE-1 ORF1p expression in 76 tissue specimens, including tumors from 43 NSCLC patients and their matched adjacent non-tumor tissues. IHC staining revealed clear differences in ORF1p expression between tumor and non-tumor samples. Representative images are shown in Fig. 1, Fig. 2, demonstrating stronger ORF1p staining in tumors compared to paired non-tumor tissues across both histologic subtypes. Notably, LSQCC tissues displayed markedly higher staining intensity relative to LUAD tissues. Quantitative analysis of IHC intensity (Fig. 3) confirmed these observations. ORF1p expression was significantly elevated in NSCLC tumors compared to adjacent non-tumor tissues (Fig. 3A, ****p < 0.0001). Subgroup analysis revealed that this increase was driven primarily by the LSQCC subtype, where tumor tissues showed significantly higher ORF1p expression compared to matched non-tumor tissues (Fig. 3B, ****p < 0.0001). In contrast, LUAD samples exhibited a similar trend, but the difference did not reach statistical significance (Fig. 3C). Direct comparison between tumor subtypes further demonstrated that ORF1p levels were substantially higher in LSQCC compared to LUAD (Fig. 3D, ****p < 0.0001). Stage-specific analyses also revealed consistently elevated ORF1p expression in LSQCC tumors across early- and mid-stage disease (Fig. 3E). Together, these results demonstrate that LINE-1 ORF1p is highly expressed in NSCLC tumors relative to non-tumor lung, with the strongest upregulation observed in LSQCC. LINE-1 ORF1p expression is significantly higher in AA

We next examined whether LINE-1 ORF1p expression varied by race. Across the entire NSCLC cohort, ORF1p intensity was significantly higher in AA patients compared to CA patients (Fig. 4A, ****p < 0.0001). These racial differences were consistent across tumor stages, with AAs exhibiting markedly higher ORF1p expression (Fig. 4B). Subtype-specific analyses demonstrated significantly elevated ORF1p expression among AA patients across demographic subsets (Fig. 4C-H), indicating a robust association between race and LINE-1 ORF1p expression. LINE-1 ORF1p expression differs by age in NSCLC tumor tissues

To evaluate the impact of age on LINE-1 activity, patients were stratified into younger (<75 years) and older (≥75 years) groups. Across the entire NSCLC cohort, no significant difference in ORF1p expression was observed between the two age groups (Fig. 5A). Stage-specific analyses, however, revealed significantly higher ORF1p levels in younger patients at stage I (***p < 0.001) and stage II (**p < 0.01), with no difference at stage III (Fig. 5B). Subtype analyses showed no significant differences in LSQCC (Fig. 5C), while LUAD tumors displayed significantly higher ORF1p expression in younger patients compared to older patients (*p < 0.05; Fig. 5D). When stratified by race and sex, no significant age-related differences were detected in either CA (Fig. 5E) or AA (Fig. 5F) patients, or in females (Fig. 5G) or males (Fig. 5H). Correlation analysis of age with ORF1p intensity provided further insight showing that in non-tumor tissues LINE-1 ORF1p expression exhibited a significant negative correlation with increasing age (R² = 0.0718, p = 2.1 × 10⁻⁷), whereas in tumor tissues no such correlation was observed (R² = 0.0057, p = 0.1174; Fig. 5I). Interestingly, tumor samples consistently showed elevated ORF1p expression across all ages, with the highest expression concentrated between 70 and 80 years of age. Differential LINE-1 ORF1p expression between male and female NSCLC patients

We next compared LINE-1 ORF1p expression between male and female NSCLC patients. Overall, males exhibited significantly higher ORF1p expression than females (Fig. 6A, p < 0.01). Stage-specific analyses revealed elevated ORF1p expression in males at stage I, whereas no differences were observed in stages II or III (Fig. 6B). Tumour subtype analyses revealed no significant sex differences, though ORF1p levels trended higher in males with LSQCC and in females with LUAD (Figs. 6C–D). When stratified by race, CA males expressed significantly higher ORF1p than females (Fig. 6E, p < 0.0001), whereas AA patients showed no sex-based differences, with a slight male predominance (Fig. 6F). Age-stratified analyses revealed no significant differences in younger patients, although males trended higher (Fig. 6G). In contrast, older patients (≥75 years) demonstrated significantly higher ORF1p expression in males compared to females (Fig. 6H, p < 0.0001). Together, these results indicate that sex-based differences in LINE-1 ORF1p expression are context-dependent, with higher expression in males, a pattern most evident at early tumor stages in CA patients, and among older individuals. LINE-1 ORF1 and immune markers in NSCLC tumor tissues

LINE-1 negatively correlates with several immune pathways, with lesser abundance of LINE-1 analytes seen in tumors showing high immune activity [13,30]. To further investigate this relationship in NSCLC, we performed multiplex immunofluorescence staining to assess LINE-1 ORF1p co-expression with immune cell markers. Representative images from LSQCC and LUAD tumors (Fig. 7) demonstrate overlapping expression of ORF1p (red) with CD3 (T cells), CD15 (neutrophils), and CD68 (macrophages) (green), with nuclei counterstained with DAPI (blue). While some co-expression was observed with CD3+ T cells and CD15+ neutrophils, the strongest overlap occurred with CD68+ macrophages, particularly in LSQCC tumors. Quantitative co-expression analysis confirmed these findings (Fig. 8). Correlation plots revealed minimal overlap between ORF1p and CD3 or CD15, but a marked positive correlation between ORF1p and CD68 expression. Notably, a substantial proportion of tumor cells exhibited dual positivity for ORF1p and CD68, supporting macrophages as a key immune population associated with LINE-1 activity in NSCLC. Cellular neighborhood enrichment and spatial autocorrelation analyses

CD68⁺ macrophages were consistently enriched in ORF1p-high cell neighborhoods across all tumor samples examined and among all three spatial radii tested (Figure S1). The average enrichment ratio for CD68+ cells was 1.51 ± 0.57 at 30 µm, declining to 1.30 ± 0.38 at 50 µm and 1.11 ± 0.25 at 100 µm, reflecting proximity-dependent enrichment. Critically, CD68+ cells showed the lowest inter-sample variance among all three immune cell populations at every radius tested, indicating that enrichment was reproducible across patients and not driven by individual outliers. In contrast, CD15⁺ cells showed no enrichment at any radius (ratio ≈ 1.06 ± 0.14), while CD3⁺ T cells showed a nominally higher ratio at 30 µm (2.92 ± 2.20) but with substantially greater variance, nearly a four-fold higher standard deviation than CD68+ cells, driven primarily by two small sections. Marked reductions in CD3+ signals were seen at larger radii.

ORF1p-high cells were non-randomly clustered in space, with ORF1p expression spatially autocorrelated across all tumor samples (Figure S2). Strikingly, the degree of ORF1p spatial autocorrelation was highest in CD68-stained tissue sections, with a mean Moran's I value of 0.237 ± 0.069 (significant in all samples, p < 0.01). This was followed by CD3-stained sections (I = 0.157 ± 0.072, all significant) and CD15-stained sections (I = 0.097 ± 0.091). A rank order CD68 > CD3 > CD15 was consistent across all individual samples, indicating that ORF1p expression forms the tightest and most spatially organized clusters in sections where macrophages are most prevalent. These relationships are consistent with a model in which macrophage-rich tumor microenvironments either promote spatially organized LINE-1 ORF1p expression or are preferentially recruited to ORF1p-high tumor cell niches. Because Moran's I did not rely on the immune marker channel, these results are free from co-expression confounding and proximity-based relationships seen with multi-channel findings confirm that CD68⁺ cells are the most reliably enriched immune population in the immediate spatial vicinity of ORF1p-high tumor cells, consistent with the co-expression findings shown in Fig. 8.

Discussion

NSCLC remains the leading cause of cancer-related mortality worldwide, with a notably poor 5-year survival rate of approximately 15%, largely due to late-stage diagnosis when the disease has already metastasized [37,38]. The biological and clinical heterogeneity of NSCLC significantly contributes to this poor prognosis. NSCLC encompasses a diverse group of lung cancers, including adenocarcinoma, squamous cell carcinoma, and large cell carcinoma, each with distinct molecular drivers, histological patterns, and clinical behaviors. This large degree of heterogeneity complicates the development of universal diagnostic and therapeutic strategies. By integrating IHC, multiplex IF, and clinicopathologic data, we demonstrate that LINE-1 ORF1p expression is not only elevated in tumors compared to adjacent non-tumor tissues, but also varies systematically by histological subtype, race, sex, and age. Importantly, we show that ORF1p co-expresses with macrophage infiltration, implicating LINE-1 in shaping the tumor immune microenvironment. These findings highlight LINE-1 as both a contributor to NSCLC pathogenesis and a potential context-specific biomarker for disease stratification.

Our analysis confirmed significantly higher ORF1p expression in NSCLC tumors compared to adjacent non-tumor lung tissues, consistent with prior reports that LINE-1 is normally epigenetically silenced but reactivated in malignancy [[17], [18], [19], [20], [21], [22]]. LINE-1 hypomethylation in lung cancer has been linked to aggressiveness, invasion, and poor prognosis [29,30], with derepression driving genomic instability through insertional mutagenesis, DNA breaks, and chromatin remodelling [20,21,23]. We further observed consistently higher ORF1p in LSQCC than in LUAD, echoing prior studies showing stronger LINE-1 hypomethylation in squamous tumors [29]. This likely reflects underlying biology, with LUAD driven by frequent oncogenic mutations (e.g., EGFR, KRAS, ALK) and higher heterogeneity, and LSQCC exhibiting marked chromosomal instability, smoking-associated mutational load, and widespread epigenetic dysregulation [2,4,5]. Thus, elevated ORF1p in LSQCC may stem from its instability phenotype and reliance on DNA damage–associated pathways, highlighting a potential histology-specific vulnerability.

The most striking results of our study were the markedly higher LINE-1 expression in AA patients compared to CA patients. This notable difference likely reflects a convergence of genetic, epigenetic, environmental and/or lifestyle factors that collectively amplify LINE-1 activity in AA. At the genetic level, population-specific LINE-1 source elements provide a compelling mechanistic basis. Previous studies have shown that specific LINE-1 source elements from Chromosome 17 and Chromosome 14 are restricted to populations from Africa or African ancestry [39], suggesting that AA individuals may harbor a greater number of transcriptionally competent, retrotransposition-competent LINE-1 loci compared to CA individuals. At the epigenetic level, global LINE-1 hypomethylation, the primary mechanism of LINE-1 derepression, has been shown to vary across racial and ethnic groups. Zhu et al. demonstrated that African American individuals exhibit significantly lower LINE-1 methylation levels compared to non-Hispanic whites, establishing LINE-1 methylation as an epigenetic marker with relevance to cancer risk [40]. Furthermore, AA patients may face disproportionate exposure to environmental carcinogens, including polycyclic aromatic hydrocarbons (PAHs) such as benzo(a)pyrene through occupational, residential, and dietary pathways. Our laboratory has previously demonstrated that benzo(a)pyrene exposure in vivo directly reactivates LINE-1 retrotransposon activity through epigenetic reprogramming mechanisms [41], providing a plausible environmental pathway through which patterned exposures could drive differential ORF1p expression in AA patients. Understanding the role of population-specific LINE-1 retroelements is therefore crucial for the development of targeted diagnostic tools and personalized therapeutic strategies. Our laboratory and others continue aggressive efforts to evaluate the use of LINE-1 analytes as biomarkers for risk stratification and treatment optimization of NSCLC patients [28,41], which could ultimately lead to improved outcomes in populations that bear a disproportionate disease burden.

We observed a positive correlation between LINE-1 ORF1p expression and age in NSCLC tumors, with the highest levels detected in patients between 70 and 80 years of age. This pattern suggests that LINE-1 activity increases with age in NSCLC tumors, potentially driving tumorigenesis through mechanisms such as genomic instability and heterochromatin loss. The absence of a positive correlation in non-tumor samples indicates that regulatory mechanisms, such as DNA methylation, may continue to more effectively suppress LINE-1 activity in non-malignant tissues [17]. Although prior studies have not demonstrated a statistically significant relationship between chronological age and LINE-1 methylation [42], work by Bollati et al. and Iacopetta et al. described a decreasing tendency in LINE-1 methylation with age, which might contribute to increased LINE-1 expression in older tumor samples [43,44]. Age-related changes, including alterations in DNA repair efficacy and inflammatory processes like the senescence-associated secretory phenotype, may further enhance LINE-1 activity, promoting a pro-tumorigenic environment [45].

Our analysis revealed significantly higher LINE-1 ORF1p expression in male NSCLC patients across the overall cohort, with the most pronounced differences observed in CA and older (≥75 years) subgroups. These findings add nuance to an area where existing literature has been inconsistent. Our previous work reported sex-specific differences in the exosomal loading of LINE-1 analytes in female and male NSCLCs, and the work of others shows sex-specific differences in LINE-1 methylation and expression, with males generally exhibiting higher levels of LINE-1 hypermethylation and lower LINE-1 levels compared to females [46,47]. Conversely, several studies investigating global LINE-1 methylation failed to detect significant differences between males and females [[48], [49], [50]]. The inconsistent findings across studies may be attributed to differences in detection methodology, inherent sensitivity of the techniques used, variations in the tissues studied, or differences in the size and other demographic factors of the study samples. When stratified by histology, our results align with epidemiologic observations that LUAD is more common in women while LSQCC predominates in men [10,11]. The elevated LINE-1 ORF1p levels observed in male patients, particularly among CA and in older age groups, may reflect hormonal influences, genetic predispositions, or lifestyle factors that impact the regulation of LINE-1. Together, our collective findings suggest that sex-specific regulatory mechanisms may influence LINE-1 expression and methylation across different contexts and biological compartments.

A novel contribution of our study is the demonstration of LINE-1 ORF1p co-expression with immune markers in NSCLC tissues. While modest overlap was seen with CD3+ T cells and CD15+ neutrophils, the strongest correlation was observed with CD68+ macrophages. Quantitative analysis confirmed macrophages as the immune population most strongly associated with LINE-1 expression. This finding is significant given the established role of tumor-associated macrophages (TAMs) in promoting NSCLC progression through angiogenesis, extracellular matrix remodeling, and suppression of anti-tumor immunity [51]. Previous studies have reported an inverse correlation between LINE-1 expression and immune activity [21], suggesting that LINE-1 may modulate tumor–immune interactions. Our data refine this view by identifying macrophages as a key cellular node in the LINE-1-immune interaction in NSCLC. The mechanistic basis of this relationship is likely bidirectional (Fig. 9). On one hand, LINE-1-derived nucleic acids, particularly single-stranded RNA and cytoplasmic DNA intermediates generated during retrotransposition, can function as damage-associated molecular patterns (DAMPs), activating innate immune sensors including cGAS–STING, thereby triggering interferon signaling pathways [52,53]. Macrophages, as professional innate immune sentinels, are uniquely positioned to respond to such signals. Chronic or dysregulated activation of these pathways within the tumor microenvironment may paradoxically drive macrophage polarization toward an immunosuppressive M2-like or tumor-associated macrophage (TAM) phenotype, thereby facilitating immune evasion and tumor progression [51]. On the other hand, macrophage-derived inflammatory mediators may themselves promote LINE-1 de-repression. Pro-inflammatory cytokines such as TNF-α, IL-6, and IL-1β secreted by activated macrophages have been shown to reduce DNA methyltransferase activity and disrupt heterochromatin maintenance, thereby relieving epigenetic repression of LINE-1 elements [35]. This creates a potential feed-forward loop in which LINE-1 activation and macrophage infiltration mutually reinforce one another within the tumor microenvironment. Supporting this concept, prior work has demonstrated that LINE-1 activity negatively correlates with immune pathway activity at the transcriptomic level, with lesser abundance of LINE-1 analytes observed in tumors exhibiting high immune infiltration [13][30]. Our findings add important protein-level spatial evidence suggesting that within macrophage-rich tumor regions, this suppressive relationship may be reversed or overridden by the immunosuppressive TAM phenotype [51]. Additionally, ORF1p itself, as an RNA-binding protein, may directly interact with immune regulatory RNA species within macrophages, influencing post-transcriptional gene regulation in ways that alter macrophage function. Whether ORF1p is expressed by macrophages themselves, by neighboring tumor cells, or is transferred intercellularly via extracellular vesicles remains an open and important question [34]. Our multiplex immunofluorescence data demonstrate dual ORF1p/CD68 positivity at the single-cell level, suggesting that at least a subset of macrophages within the tumor microenvironment directly express ORF1p, though whether this reflects active LINE-1 retrotransposition or intercellular transfer warrants further investigation. Several limitations of this study warrant acknowledgment. Although a priori power analysis was performed to guide the overall study design, certain subgroup analyses, including LSQCC stratified by tumor stage, and race- and sex-stratified comparisons, involve small sample sizes that may limit statistical power and the generalizability of those specific findings. These constraints are inherent to the retrospective, single-institution nature of the cohort and the relative rarity of certain NSCLC subtypes within it. Accordingly, the subgroup-level findings should be interpreted with appropriate caution and regarded as hypothesis-generating rather than definitive. Validation of key observations, particularly the race-based differences in LINE-1 ORF1p expression and the association with macrophage infiltration in larger, independent cohorts, or through mining of publicly available datasets such as TCGA is warranted and will be a priority for future studies. A recent RNA-Seq study by our group of 121 NSCLC cell lines independently confirmed that LSQCC exhibit higher LINE-1 transcript expression compared to other NSCLC subtypes, providing orthogonal support for a key finding in the present study.

We acknowledge that the current study does not include longitudinal clinical outcome data such as overall survival, disease-free survival, or treatment response, and therefore the prognostic or predictive value of ORF1p expression cannot be formally determined from the present dataset. Similarly, we have not defined clinical ORF1p expression cutoff thresholds or performed AUC-based comparison with existing NSCLC biomarkers such as PD-L1 or EGFR mutation status. These limitations define the boundaries of the current findings as hypothesis-generating rather than clinically actionable, and these critical validation steps are planned for subsequent phases of this research program.

The clinical translation of LINE-1 ORF1p as a biomarker for NSCLC will require advances on two parallel fronts: standardization of detection platforms and prospective validation in well-powered clinical cohorts. The IHC protocol employed in this study represents a reproducible foundation for clinical IHC standardization. Establishment of a formal ORF1p H-score or quantitative intensity scoring system, analogous to scoring frameworks used for PD-L1 and HER2 in clinical oncology, would be a critical next step, defining clinically actionable thresholds (e.g., ORF1p-high vs. ORF1p-low) validated for inter-laboratory reproducibility across multiple pathology centers. Beyond tissue-based IHC, liquid biopsy represents a particularly promising avenue for non-invasive ORF1p detection and longitudinal monitoring. LINE-1 analytes, including ORF1p protein and LINE-1-containing extracellular vesicles are detectable in blood and other biofluids [28], and ultrasensitive digital immunoassay platforms such as Single Molecule Array (Simoa) technology have been applied to detect circulating ORF1p as a multi-cancer biomarker with high specificity [27]. Integration of circulating ORF1p with established liquid biopsy analytes including circulating tumor DNA (ctDNA) and circulating tumor cells, could further enhance diagnostic sensitivity and specificity for NSCLC detection and stratification.

In summary, our findings reveal that LINE-1 ORF1p expression in NSCLC varies significantly by tumor histology, race, sex, and age, with the strongest associations in LSQCC and AA patients. These differences may underlie key aspects of NSCLC heterogeneity and contribute to patient-specific differences in disease outcomes. Future studies should clarify the mechanisms driving these patterns and assess whether LINE-1 ORF1p can be leveraged as a biomarker for patient stratification as well as a novel therapeutic target in NSCLC.
