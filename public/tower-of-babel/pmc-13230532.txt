Chitosan-EDTA-cellulose functionalized with β-cyclodextrin as a pH-sensitive bio-based nanocarrier for curcumin drug delivery
Authors: Negin Rostami, Mohammad G. Dekamin, Mohammad Reza Naimi-Jamal
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
In this work, chitosan/cellulose/β-cyclodextrin nanostructure was synthesized by cross-linking chitosan and cellulose with EDTA followed by bridging with ethylenediamine-β-cyclodextrin. The chitosan/cellulose/β-cyclodextrin bio-based nanostructure is a novel drug delivery system that has been developed for the delivery of hydrophobic drugs. The chitosan/cellulose/β-cyclodextrin nanostructure was characterized by EDX, XRD, FESEM, TGA, FT-IR, in vitro drug loading and release study. The drug efficiency of the curcumin inclusion complex with chitosan/cellulose/β-cyclodextrin was higher compared to chitosan-EDTA-cellulose and increased with increasing β-cyclodextrin fragments. The curcumin release behavior from chitosan-EDTA-cellulose and chitosan/cellulose/β-cyclodextrin nanostructures was evaluated at two pHs of 5.5 (acidic extracellular microenvironment of tumors) and 7.4 (typical physiological pH of most body tissues and fluids) in 72 h, which was better fitted with the Korsmeyer’s–Peppas kinetic model. The cytotoxicity of curcumin-loaded chitosan/cellulose/β-cyclodextrin was less than free curcumin. It appeared that the presence of β-cyclodextrin appreciably reduced the burst release observed in chitosan-EDTA-cellulose network. Finally, chitosan/cellulose/β-cyclodextrin was obtained with nanoparticle size of about 56.07 ± 12.11 nm, zeta potential range of 31.26 ± 0.09 to 47.98 ± 0.11 mV, and encapsulation efficiency of 86.0 ± 0.17%. The outcomes indicated that the chitosan/cellulose/β-cyclodextrin nanostructure is applicable for the parenteral injection delivery of the curcumin anticancer drug.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-026-45290-x.

Introduction

A drug delivery system (DDS) is specified as a device that authorizes the ingress of a therapeutic substance into the organs[1]. The examinations for new drug delivery systems represent one of the research areas[2]. There are diverse patterns of drug delivery systems, among which dendrimer nanocarriers[3], silica materials[4,5], carbon nanomaterials[6], magnetic nanoparticles[7], metal organic frameworks[8] and polymeric nanomaterials[9] can be mentioned[10]. Nanoscale materials have an excellent potential as drug carriers due to their small sizes and unique physicochemical and biological properties[11,12]. Polymeric drug delivery systems could not only protect drugs from degradation and control the drug release kinetic to lengthen the drug’s effective therapeutic duration, but also could ensure the biocompatibility, biodegradability, and low immunogenicity over the synthetic ones[13,14]. Additionally, polymeric systems offer a versatile platform for pH-sensitive drug delivery due to their tunable chemical properties[15]. In the context of oncology, pH-sensitive polymers are particularly effective because they can exploit the biological gradient between healthy tissue (usually has a physiological pH approximately 7.4) and the acidic tumor microenvironment (pH approximately 6.5—6.8)[16,17]. By incorporating pH-sensitive functional groups, these polymers can undergo structural transitions such as swelling, dissociation, or solubility changes specifically at the tumor site[18]. This ensures that the drug loaded on nanocarrier is protected during systemic circulation and selectively released within the intracellular compartments such as endosomes (pH approximately 5.5)[19]. Recently, natural polymers such as alginate[20], chitosan[21,22], cellulose[23], polysaccharides[24], Poly(glycolic acid)[25], poly(lactic acid)[26], and hyaluronic acid[27] have attracted considerable research interest for drug delivery applications. Among them, chitosan and cellulose are widely studied in pharmaceutical science for drug delivery applications due to their unique properties[28]. Chitosan (CS) is a biopolymer obtained from the deacetylation of chitin and can easily be found as a by-product of the fishing industry in insect, crustacean, and some fungal exoskeletons, as well as being biodegradable and non-toxic[29,30]. Chitosan has versatile reactive sites, such as amino and hydroxyl groups, for chemical and physical modifications to acquire additional functional properties or improve its characteristics[12,31]. Cellulose (Cell) is a significant raw material used for the preparation of biodegradable, biocompatible, and non-toxic particles in life sciences[32,33]. These properties of cellulose as a biopolymer gained attention in the drug delivery system over synthetic polymers[34]. While significant progress has been made in the chemical modification of polysaccharides for pharmaceutical applications, developing facile methods for preparing polysaccharide-based nanostructures for controlled drug release remains a challenging and important area of research.

Host–guest chemistry is a branch of chemistry that focuses on the interactions between a host molecule and a guest molecule[35]. The binding between the host and guest can be reversible or irreversible, and it is driven by various intermolecular forces such as hydrogen bonding[36], electrostatic interactions[37], van der Waals forces[38], hydrophobic interactions[39], and π-π stacking[40]. They can include macrocycles, such as cyclodextrins[41], crown ethers[42], calixarenes[43], and cucurbiturils[44], as well as proteins[45], enzymesp[46], and synthetic receptors[47]. Host–guest chemistry has numerous applications in various fields, including drug delivery[48], supramolecular catalysis[49], sensor design[50], materials science[51] and separation and purification[52]. Host–guest chemistry involving cyclodextrins is a fascinating area of research and application[53,54]. Cyclodextrins (CDs) are cyclic oligosaccharides that are named according to the number of glucopyranose units and variously named as α-, β- or γ-cyclodextrin (respectively 6,7and 8)[55,56]. Cyclodextrins have a unique structure that gives them the ability to form inclusion complexes with various guest molecules[57,58]. This structure consists of a hydrophobic cavity formed by the glucose units and a hydrophilic exterior due to the presence of hydroxyl groups[59]. The hydrophobic cavity provides a favorable environment for encapsulating guest molecules, while the hydrophilic exterior allows for solubility in aqueous solutions[60,61]. In host–guest chemistry, cyclodextrins can act as hosts by encapsulating guest molecules within their cavities[62,63].

Curcumin (Cur), as a natural polyphenolic compound, has many biological properties such as anti-cancer[64,65], antioxidant[66], antimicrobial[67], anti-parasitic[68], anti-inflammatory[69] and wound healing[70]. Despite these biological properties, curcumin has very low solubility in water and poor intestinal absorption, which reduces its bioavailability and medicinal effectiveness[71,72]. Therefore, to increase the solubility and medicinal activities, various nanocarrier formulations such as CS/AG/g-C3N4/Cur[73], MSNs[74], FA-PRCMCs@CUR[75], CSpbCD-cur[76], LF-EGCG-HA[77], PAA/CMC/HNT/CUR[78], Cur-AMs[79] , etc. have been described[78,80]. However, a critical analysis of these systems reveals several persistent challenges that constitute a research gap[81]. Certain synthetic or inorganic components may raise long-term concerns regarding biodegradability, which limits their clinical application[82]. As well, highly functionalized carriers often require elaborate, costly, or non-green synthesis methods[83]. Low drug loading and high carrier-to-drug ratios in some carriers demonstrated a limited capacity for the drug, requiring larger doses of the carrier itself for a therapeutic effect[84]. Furthermore, systems based on simple electrostatic interactions may exhibit premature drug release, compromise therapeutic efficacy, and increase systemic toxicity[85]. Therefore, there is a compelling need for novel nanocarrier systems that combine enhanced biocompatibility with pH-sensitive release using a simple and scalable synthesis[86].

This research aimed to create a novel polymeric drug delivery system that explores the synergistic potential of biomaterials to design and engineer a novel chitosan/cellulose/β-cyclodextrin nanostructure for drug delivery applications (Fig. 1). This system leverages the benefits of both CS and Cell to enhance the pH-responsiveness and bioavailability. Additionally, it incorporates β-cyclodextrin to facilitate host–guest drug delivery and increase the load of curcumin. Experimental

Materials and methods

Chitosan (MW = 190—310 kDa, medium molecular weight, 75–85% deacetylated, supplied by Across company), cellulose microcrystalline for thin-layer chromatography (provided by Merck company), and ethylenediaminetetraacetic acid (EDTA, MW = 292.24 g/mol) were used for the preparation of the CS-EDTA-Cell network. β‐cyclodextrin (β-CD, 98%), 4-toluenesulfonyl chloride (TsCl), ethylene diamine (EDA, MW = 60.1 g/mol), and 1-ethyl-3-(3-dimethylaminopropyl)carbodiimide (EDCI) are commercially available and purchased from usual sources (Sigma Aldrich and Merck). 3–(4,5-Dimethyl-thiazol-2-yl)-2,5-diphenyltetrazolium bromide (MTT) and curcumin (MW = 368.37), as a hydrophobic drug, were purchased from E-Merck Products. Elemental analysis and mapping of the nanostructures were provided by EDX analysis, which was recorded by TESCAN4992. The technique operates by exciting the sample with an electron beam and detecting the unique characteristic X-rays emitted by each element to provide both qualitative and quantitative composition data. The FT-IR spectra were recorded in the range of 400–4000 cm−1 using the 1720-X PerkinElmer spectrometer by using KBr pellets. This analysis was crucial for identifying the functional groups and chemical bonds present in the materials, confirming successful synthesis by tracking characteristic peak changes. The morphology and size of the synthesized nanocomposite were studied by FESEM using MIRA2 and MIRA3 TESCAN instruments. FESEM creates high-resolution images by scanning a sample with a focused electron beam and detecting secondary and backscattered electrons. Samples are coated with gold on an aluminum stub to prevent charging. Thermogravimetric Analysis of the prepared nanocomposite was obtained by the Bahr company STA 504. This technique measures a nanocomposite’s mass change from ambient temperature to about 800 °C at a rate of 10 °C per minute in air atmosphere to determine thermal stability, decomposition temperature, and the quantity of components based on their characteristic weight loss. The XRD measurements were recorded with the Rigaku Ultima IV to determine the crystallographic structure. In X-ray diffraction, a sharp pattern recorded over a 2θ range (typically 5°- 80°) indicates a crystalline structure, while a broad halo signifies an amorphous nature. The hydrogel was frozen using a freeze dryer model FD-10V, Pishtaz Engineering Co. UV–Vis spectrophotometry (Shimadzu UV-2550PC) was used to find the maximum absorbance wavelength of curcumin and to determine its concentration. The zeta potential of nanocarriers and drug-loaded nanocarriers was determined using a Particlemetrix Zeta-Sizer analyzer. In this technique, the electrical potential at a particle’s shear plane serves as a key indicator of colloidal stability (with large positive or negative values signifying stable dispersion).

Preparation of CS-EDTA-Cell network

The CS-EDTA-Cell network was prepared following a previous report.[87]. At first, 0.266 g of chitosan was mixed in a 1% v/v solution of acetic acid (1 ml of acetic acid and 99 ml of distilled water) for 30 min at room temperature. Then, 0.132 g of cellulose was added and stirred for 1 h until the hydrogel was completely uniform. Subsequently, 1.6 g of EDTA dianhydride[88] as a crosslinker was dispersed in 5 ml of methanol and added drop by drop to the hydrogel. Meanwhile, 0.14 g of EDCI, a reagent used for activating carboxylic acids to facilitate the formation of new bonds (amide and ester bonds), was added to the reaction vessel, and the mixture of CS-EDTA-Cell hydrogel was stirred for 24 h at room temperature. The produced hydrogel was filtered and mixed with EtOH under stirring for 4 h. The extra EDTA was eliminated by washing the Hydrogel with an excess of 0.1 M NaOH, deionized water, 0.1 M HCl, and deionized water, respectively. Eventually, the swollen hydrogel was frozen and dried in a freeze-dryer under vacuum at − 42 °C for 24 h.

Preparation of OTs-β‐CD (mono‑6‑(p‑tosylsulfonyl)‑6‑deoxy‑β‑cyclodextrin)

Β-cyclodextrin (β-CD) has a lot of OH groups that must be functionalized with amino group via two steps by reference to the previous literature[89]. The first step is tosylation, and the second is amination[90]. In brief, β-cyclodextrin (5.0 g) was dissolved in deionized water and stirred at 0–4 °C in an ice and cold-water bath, and NaOH (3 ml, 6 M) was added drop by drop until the solution was entirely clear. Then, 4-toluenesulfonyl chloride (TsCl, 1.5 g) was dissolved in acetonitrile (1 ml) was added dropwise over 5 min and reacted under vigorous stirring for 2 h. The solution containing tosylated β-CD (OTs-β-CD) was neutralized to pH 6–7 by using HCl (6 M). The white solid product was obtained by filtration and was recrystallized from hot water and dried for 6 h at room temperature. (Yield: 55%).

Preparation of ethylenediamine-β-cyclodextrin (EDA-β-cyclodextrin)

At this step, prepared OTs-β-CD (0.32 g) was dissolved in 2.1 ml of DMF and added to 3 ml of ethylenediamine in a 25 ml flask at 40 °C for 24 h. Eventually, by adding 40 ml of acetone, an orange sediment appeared. The precipitate was filtered by filter paper, washed with fresh acetone twice, and dried in a vacuum oven.

Preparation of chitosan/cellulose/β-cyclodextrin nanostructure

Method I: CS-EDTA-Cell (1.00 g) was poured into a 100 ml flask, and 50 ml of acetone was added to it, and stirred for 30 min until CS-EDTA-Cell was completely dispersed in acetone. Then, EDA-β-cyclodextrin (1.00 g) with EDCI (0.14 g) was added to the mixture and stirred at room temperature for 24 h. Finally, CS/Cell/β-CD synthesized by Method I (M I) nanostructure was filtered with filter paper and dried in an oven at 50 °C.

Method II: CS-EDTA-Cell (1.00 g) was poured into a 100 ml flask, and 50 ml of acetone was added to it, and stirred for 30 min until CS-EDTA-Cell was completely dispersed in acetone. Then, EDA-β-cyclodextrin (2.00 g) with EDCI (0.14 g) were added to the mixture and stirred at room temperature for 24 h. Finally, CS/Cell/β-CD synthesized by Method II (M II) nanostructure was filtered with filter paper and dried in an oven at 50 °C.

In vitro curcumin loading

Different amounts of CS/Cell//β-CD (MI) (0.01g, 0.05g, and 0.1g) were dispersed into 20 ml of curcumin solution (250 µg/ml, absolute EtOH) at room temperature for 24 h. CS-EDTA-Cell and CS/Cell/β-CD (M II) were also loaded with curcumin under the same conditions for comparison, and the results of three repetitions are given in Table S1. Cur‐loaded nanocarriers were separated from the solution using a filter paper, washed with distilled water, and dried overnight at 30 °C and named Cur-CS/Cell/β-CD (M I), Cur-CS/Cell/β-CD (M II), and Cur-CS-EDTA-Cell. The supernatant was collected to calculate drug loading efficiency. Unbound curcumin concentration was determined by UV–Vis standard curve, according to the measurement of a series of concentrations of curcumin solutions (12.5, 25, 40, 50, 80, and 100 µg/ml), at λ = 428 nm, as shown in Fig. S2. In vitro curcumin release study

To investigate the practicability of the prepared nanostructure as hydrophobic anticancer drug carriers, the in vitro release behavior under simulated physiological conditions (pH = 7.4) and in an acidic environment (pH = 5.5) at 37 °C was performed. The curcumin release profile was compared to the Cur-CS/Cell/β-CD (M II), Cur-CS/Cell/β-CD (M I) inclusion complex, and Cur-CS-EDTA-Cell nanostructure. The speed of stirring was kept at 500 rpm. At a certain intervening period of time, 1 ml of the release medium was withdrawn and replaced with an equal volume of fresh PBS buffer to keep the final volume constant. These samples were then determined for curcumin content at 428 nm by a UV–Vis spectrophotometer. A series of solutions (1 − 25 µg/ml) in PBS buffer solution (pH = 7.4 and 5.5, Tween 80) was prepared in a 25 ml volumetric flask. Then, the calibration curve was drawn and calculated in pH = 7.4 and 5.5 using a UV–Vis spectrophotometer in these concentrations (Fig. S2). These procedures were repeated 3 times, and the average curcumin concentration was calculated using the standard curves, and the drug release curve was plotted.

The surface charge of nanoparticles determines cellular uptake, biodistribution, and interaction with biological environments[105]. Among the characteristics of nanoparticles, zeta potential is related to surface charge, and it could influence the behavior and stability of the nanoparticles, making its measurement necessary to investigate the possible interactions of nanoparticles and biological systems[106]. Additionally, positively charged nanoparticles present higher non-phagocytic cellular uptake percentages than neutral and negatively charged nanoparticles[107]. The stability and degradation profile of the chitosan/cellulose/β-cyclodextrin nanostructures are crucial for drug delivery application, particularly in sustained release or environmental remediation. Specifically, the CS/Cell/β-CD (M II) and Cur-CS/Cell/β-CD (M II) were incubated at two different pH conditions (pH = 7.4 and pH = 5.5) at 37 ℃, and their structural integrity was monitored via zeta potential to quantify surface charge and electrostatic repulsion over time, over a period of 4 days. The results showed excellent colloidal stability for 3 days in pH = 7.4, but significant degradation have been observed thereafter (Fig. 7b). In PBS (pH = 7.4), the both nanostructures maintained a stable zeta potential (29.42 ± 0.18 mV for CS/Cell/β-CD (M II) and 46.14 ± 0.37 mV for Cur-CS/Cell/β-CD (M II)) in 3 days, indicating excellent colloidal stability due to hydrogen bonding and strong electrostatic interactions within the polymeric network. After 3 days, a significant decrease in zeta potential were observed, suggesting early stages of degradation or aggregation. In acidic medium (pH = 5.5), the both nanostructures showed a more rapid decrease in zeta potential, becoming unstable within 48 h. This accelerated destabilization is likely due to the protonation of the amine groups in chitosan, which leads to polymer chain swelling, demonstrating the pH-responsive nature of the chitosan component. In summary, the CS/Cell/β-CD (M II) and Cur-CS/Cell/β-CD (M II) nanostructures exhibit excellent stability under neutral physiological conditions for up to 3 days, sufficient for many intended applications. Their destabilization at lower pH highlights a pH-responsive feature that could be advantageous for drug release in acidic environments (e.g., tumor microenvironment).

Investigation of curcumin loading

Evaluation of the Cur release kinetic models from the nanocarriers

The profile of the Cur releases from the nanocarriers in different mediums was studied by fitting the obtained pure data with four common release kinetic equations. The established plots and results obtained respectively are presented in Fig. S8, S9 ,S10, S10, S11, S12 and 13 and Table 2. From the comparison of the obtained correlation coefficients (R2) for each release kinetic model, it is understood that in both release medium the release kinetics are best fitted with the Higuchi and Korsmeyer-Peppas kinetic model. The Higuchi model fit suggests that diffusion plays a significant role in the release process, characteristic of drug transport from an insoluble matrix. The fit to the Korsmeyer-Peppas model further elucidates the underlying mechanism through the determination of the release exponent (n). The calculated n values for chitosan/cellulose/β-cyclodextrin nanostructures (n = 0.79—0.85) fall between 0.43 and 0.85 (for spherical carriers). In addition, this range also demonstrated the non-Fickian behavior of Cur release in both mediums. This non-Fickian transport indicates that the release is governed by a coupled mechanism involving both drug diffusion through the polymer matrix and polymer chain relaxation/swelling upon contact with the dissolution medium. Specifically, the ingress of the medium causes the nanocarrier matrix to swell, creating larger channels that facilitate Cur diffusion, which is the definition of a non-Fickian process. Conclusion

In summary, CS/Cell/β-CD (M I) and CS/Cell/β-CD (M II) nanocarriers were prepared and their performance for new drug delivery system was investigated and compared. The data analysis shows that increasing the amount of β‐cyclodextrin in the nanocomposite structure increases the effect of the host–guest complex and ultimately increases the amount of drug loading. In vitro, the assays demonstrated that CS/Cell/β-CD (M II) nanocarrier showed the best result with 86.0 ± 0.17% of drug adsorption and favorable slow-release at pH 7.4 and 5.5 under phosphate buffer solution. Physicochemical characterization techniques, such as FT-IR spectroscopy, FESEM, XRD, TGA and EDX confirmed the successful synthesis of CS/Cell/β-CD (M I) and CS/Cell/β-CD (M II) and the encapsulation of curcumin within the nanocarriers. The prepared chitosan/cellulose/β-cyclodextrin nanocarrier presented the controlled release profile for the Cur which fitted the Korsmeyer-Peppas kinetics model. Furthermore, the kinetics study revealed the non-Fickian behavior of Cur release in both studied mediums. The cell biocompatibility of nanocarriers was determined by MTT assay. According to the results, the nanocarriers have negligible toxicity at different concentrations and studied durations and it can be concluded that any cell death resulting from the curcumin loaded nanocarriers will be due to the release of the drug.
