Melanocortin-4 Receptor Gene Variants and Weight Change Following Switch to Integrase Inhibitor-Based Antiretroviral Therapy
Authors: Todd Hulgan, Kristine M Erlandson, Yuki Bradford, Katherine Tassiopoulos, Kunling Wu, Sara H Bares, Todd T Brown, Jordan E Lake, Michael Leonard, Grace A McComsey, Marylyn D Ritchie, Paul E Sax, John R Koethe, David W Haas
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractGiven the melanocortin-4 receptor's (MC4R) importance in obesity, we examined associations between MC4R gene variants and weight following a switch to integrase inhibitor-containing antiretroviral therapy among participants in Advancing Clinical Therapeutics Globally studies. Among 529 persons with HIV (median age: 50 years, 29% non-Hispanic Black, 22% female), 62% were overweight or obese at integrase strand transfer inhibitor switch, and 62% switched to raltegravir. In adjusted linear models, three variants were associated (P < .05) with weight change after switch in all participants or in prespecified subgroups. These exploratory findings are consistent with the role of melanocortins in appetite and weight and suggest a neuroendocrine contribution to integrase inhibitor-related weight gain.

BACKGROUND

Metabolic adverse effects have been recognized with antiretroviral therapy (ART) for decades. With the use of integrase strand transfer inhibitor (INSTI)-based ART, overt adverse effects such as dyslipidemia are less common. However, INSTI use has been associated with greater weight gain in persons with HIV (PWH) initiating or switching therapy in both observational studies and analyses of clinical trial data [1–6]. There are wide variations and extreme outliers in observed weight gain, suggesting host genetic factors may be involved. Studies of genetic risk for weight gain after switching to INSTI have found that CYP2B6 poor metabolizer status was associated with greater weight gain after switching from efavirenz- to INSTI-based ART [7, 8]. Further investigation in one of these populations also identified an association with mitochondrial genetic variation and weight change after INSTI switch [9]. Other studies of ART-naïve persons initiating dolutegravir (DTG)-containing regimens reported associations with genetic variants, including CYP2B6 metabolizer status, and weight gain [10, 11].

Melanocortins in the hypothalamus, activated by leptin, are associated with energy homeostasis, caloric intake, insulin sensitivity, and obesity [12]. Melanocortin-4 receptor (MC4R) function specifically affects appetite and body weight regulation [13]. Variants in the MC4R gene and the surrounding region on chromosome 18 have consistently been associated with obesity-related phenotypes across various human populations [14–30]. More than 25 variants within ± 200 kB of MC4R have been associated with obesity-related traits at genome-wide statistical significance (P < 5.0 × 10−8). Of note, MC4R gene variants have also been associated with antipsychotic drug-related weight gain [24, 31], supporting a pharmacogenetic role for this gene. Melanocortin signaling has been postulated as a mechanism of ART-associated weight gain [32], as discussed in a combined clinical trial analysis [5, 33]. Finally, a report from the European Medicines Agency found that DTG inhibited the binding of melanocortin-stimulating hormone (MSH) to MC4R by 64% when used in concentrations equivalent to clinical maximum concentration [34].

We hypothesized that MC4R variants would be associated with weight change following the switch to INSTI-based ART, with loss-of-function variants associated with greater weight gain, and gain-of-function variants associated with less weight gain.

METHODS

The study population included participants in Advancing Clinical Therapeutics Globally (ACTG) studies A5001 and A5322. Participants in A5001 initiated ART as part of ACTG clinical trials between 1997 and 2011 and were followed until 2013 [35]. A subset of A5001 participants at least 40 years of age transitioned to A5322 for continued observation through 2021 [36]. Participants who switched from any non-INSTI ART to INSTI-based ART, had available MC4R variant information from genome-wide genotyping, and had at least one weight measurement available within 2 years before and after INSTI switch were included. All participants provided written informed consent for the use of clinical and genetic data in future analyses.

Genome-wide genotyping and imputation were performed using multiple genotyping platforms across multiple ACTG trial populations as described elsewhere [37]. Variants with a call rate <95% or a minor allele frequency (MAF) < 5% were excluded. Within the MC4R ± 50 kB region, 507 variants passed quality control (QC), including 20 that were targeted due to prior reports of association with weight-related phenotypes in persons without HIV (Supplementary Table 1). Six MC4R variants were not included because of MAF <5% or low imputation quality. A total of 693 817 variants that passed QC were included in exploratory genome-wide analyses.

The primary outcome was within-person weight change up to 2 years post-switch in the overall population, allowing participants to serve as their own controls for estimation of background age-related weight change. Associations between variants and weight change were tested by linear regression models and included covariates of age, sex, ancestry (first five principal components), and body mass index (BMI), CD4+ T cell count, and HIV-1 RNA <200 copies/mL at switch. Additional analyses included stratification by self-reported race/ethnicity, sex, and BMI category (any BMI up to 24.9; 25–30 [overweight]; >30 kg/m2 [obese]).

For the a priori-determined 20 MCR4 variants with previously reported genome-wide associations at P < 1.0 × 10−15, we did not correct for multiple comparisons. For other MC4R variants, Bonferroni estimates were used to correct for multiple comparisons based on the total number of variants analyzed (0.05/507 = corrected P < 1 × 10−6). In addition to the above analyses focused on MC4R variants, we also explored genome-wide associations across all genes and chromosomes. The Bonferroni correction was P < 5 × 10−8. Gene loci with nominal genome-wide associations (P < 1 × 10−6) were compared with genes previously reported to be associated with obesity-related phenotypes in the genome-wide association study (GWAS) catalog [38].

RESULTS

A total of 529 individuals from A5001/A5322 with available genetic data were included in analyses. Median age was 50 years at first INSTI switch, 29% were self-reported non-Hispanic Black race/ethnicity, and 22% had female sex assigned at birth (Table 1). The median BMI at switch was 26.5 kg/m2, and 36% and 26% were overweight and obese, respectively. Sixty-seven percent had plasma HIV-1 RNA level <200 copies/mL at switch, and median CD4+ T cells were 483 cells/mm3. ART prior to switch included a protease inhibitor in 73%, a non-nucleoside reverse transcriptase inhibitor in 31%, and tenofovir disoproxil fumarate (TDF) in 69%. INSTIs used after switch were raltegravir (62%), dolutegravir (21%), and elvitegravir (17%). Bictegravir and tenofovir alafenamide (TAF) use was infrequent (<1% and 9%, respectively) due to the time frame of inclusion. During a median follow-up of time of 59 (IQR: 59–65) weeks after switch, the overall median weight change was +1.30 kg (IQR: −1.20, 4.0); median change was similar in non-Hispanic White (+1.30 kg) and Black (+1.25 kg) and among men (+1.30 kg) and women (+0.90 kg).

In or within 50 kB of MC4R, a total of 20 variants previously reported to be associated with weight gain or obesity were available and analyzed. Of these, three were statistically significantly associated with weight change after INSTI switch in the overall group and/or a prespecified subgroup (Table 2). Most notably, rs11873305 was significantly associated with weight gain in the overall (P = .01) population and in the male (P = .004) and White, non-Hispanic (P = .02) subgroups. Additional targeted MC4R variants associated with weight change after INSTI switch included rs489693 and rs12970134, both of which were associated with a weight decrease in females after INSTI switch (P = .03 and .05, respectively). Neither were associated with decreased weight in the overall population (P = .09 for both), and there were no significant associations observed in other subgroups.

None of the non-targeted MC4R gene variants were associated with weight change at a Bonferroni-corrected P-value. In exploratory GWAS analyses, no variant met genome-wide significance in the overall population (Supplementary Figure). Twelve variants from seven gene loci were associated with weight change after INSTI switch at P < 1 × 10−6 in the overall population (Supplementary Table 3). An additional 271 variants met this p-value threshold in various subgroups (Supplementary Table 3).

DISCUSSION

In this exploratory analysis of a diverse population of PWH switching from non-INSTI- to INSTI-based ART, median weight change was just over a 1 kg increase and was associated with three variants in MC4R that have been previously associated with weight and/or obesity risk in persons without HIV. Variant rs11873305 was associated with weight gain in the overall population of PWH switching to INSTI-based ART, and in male and White, non-Hispanic subgroups. This variant has been associated with higher BMI in European ancestry populations [39, 40]. Interestingly, two variants, rs489693 and rs12970134 were associated with decreased weight after INSTI switch in females. These variants have previously been associated with antipsychotic drug-induced weight gain [24, 41, 42] and obesity risk [43], respectively, providing biologic plausibility for the role of MC4R gene variants in drug-associated weight changes. We did not find significant associations between weight change and other MC4R variants previously associated with BMI in persons of European ancestry without HIV [25]. Different results may be due to different populations and the differences between evaluating inherent genetic risk and risk of medication-associated weight changes. While the overall median weight gain was modest (1.3 kg), 25% of the population gained 4 kg or more, which is 5% of the overall median body weight of 80 kg. Recent data in PWH over the first 48 weeks of ART demonstrated significant increases in serum total and low-density lipoprotein (LDL) cholesterol with each 1 kg weight increase [44]. An increase in weight of >5%–10% was associated with a 56% increase in metabolic syndrome in the same study. While these data were not from persons switching ART, it suggests even modest weight gains can have important metabolic impacts.

This is the first study focused on MC4R gene variants and weight changes after INSTI switch in PWH. A recent in vitro study examined the effects of INSTI on MC4R function in models of ligand binding [45]. In the biochemical assay, all commercially available INSTIs demonstrated >50% MC4R binding at a fixed 100 µM concentration. In a cell-based assay, no agonistic or antagonistic effects were observed at physiologic INSTI concentrations (unbound Cmax). IC50 values were unable to be determined at maximum solubility dose concentrations. While suggesting that MC4R binding is not a primary mechanism of weight gain, this in vitro study cannot account for functional genetic variation in vivo.

Given the known role of MC4R in metabolic and appetite regulation, functional variants could impact weight via INSTI inhibition of MSH action or by modulating MC4R expression. MC4R is predominantly expressed in the brain, and experimental disruption of the receptor or gene can induce hyperphagia and obesity, while activation of the receptor or gain-of-function mutations suppresses food intake and decreases weight [46]. MC4R mutations associated with weight gain typically cause partial or complete loss of function. Antipsychotic medications may reduce MC4R expression in the brain [47], and MC4R variants associated with weight gain on antipsychotic medications likely further modulate the effect of the drug on expression.

While exploratory GWAS did not identify associations at Bonferroni-corrected p-value cutoffs, there were nominal associations in genes previously reported to be associated with obesity (Supplementary Table 3) [48–51]. These included variants in long intergenic non-coding (LINC) RNA on chromosome 1 (LINC01677) in the overall population, and two variants in the KIAA1217 loci identified in the Hispanic subgroup. Other loci upstream or downstream of variants nominally associated with weight change have also been associated with obesity phenotypes in populations without HIV (QKI, COL22A1, and NYAP2).

Strengths of this prospective cohort analysis include the well-defined ART exposure history and reliable weight and covariate measures. While the relatively large population of persons of African ancestry allowed for stratified analyses, the diversity of the overall population may have limited our results. We attempted to mitigate this by including ancestry-informed principal components in analyses. Limitations of this exploratory analysis also included a relatively small sample size, especially within ancestry-stratified subgroups and for GWAS. While TAF use has been associated with weight gain independent of INSTI exposure [52], recent evidence suggests a neutral weight effect of TAF relative to a weight suppressive effect of TDF [53]. Given the time period during which switches occurred, fewer than 10% of participants had TAF in their first INSTI-containing regimen, and the majority continued TDF, limiting our ability to address this question. The infrequent use of TAF does, however, allow our analysis to focus more specifically on weight gain on INSTIs. Similarly, given the study period, bictegravir use was very infrequent but has also been associated with weight gain similar to dolutegravir. While viral suppression was adjusted for in analyses, the inclusion of a mixed population of persons suppressed and those not at the time of switch may also limit generalizability to contemporary populations. Additionally, other medications and lifestyle-related factors related to weight change (e.g., diet and physical activity) could not be fully accounted for in these exploratory analyses. Adjustment for BMI at the time of switch might have captured the effects of some but not all of these unmeasured factors, and whether associations with variants in MC4R or other genes would make these true confounders is unknown.

These exploratory results add to a body of literature defining the genetics of weight gain on INSTIs, but at present do not provide sufficient evidence for clinicians to use genetic screening to stratify PWH at risk for marked weight gain with INSTI-based ART. Future studies should expand on these findings and include more persons treated with the frequently prescribed combination of bictegravir with TAF. Validating MC4R as a locus of metabolic risk in PWH could explain some of the variability in weight change on ART, identify those at higher risk for weight gain on INSTIs, and provide a new therapeutic target for the MC4R agonists already in development as novel obesity treatments [54].
