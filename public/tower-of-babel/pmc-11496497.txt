A fingerprint dictionary processing approach in indoor localization system based on wi-fi
Authors: Junhua Yang, Yuan Wang, Wei Cheng, Yang Liu, Jingyu Lu, Jin Wu, Santuan Qin, Gang Han
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractIndoor localization using Wi-Fi fingerprinting based on Received Signal Strength (RSS) has gained widespread attention due to its immunity to external factors and ability to penetrate obstacles. The localization process involves an offline phase for building a radio map and an online phase for matching location queries. Existing matching algorithms often prioritize enhancing online phase accuracy, overlooking the importance of offline data preprocessing, which can negatively impact overall performance. This study introduces a novel approach called Fingerprint Dictionary Preprocessing (FDP) that employs Convolutional Dictionary Learning (CDL) to process radio map data. CDL learns a set of kernels capturing site characteristics, representing RSS values from Access Points (APs) in a sparse manner. The proposed FDP system compresses data through feature learning, reducing storage requirements for data transmission. In the online phase, CDL is utilized for assisting matching fingerprints against the learned dictionary, accurately locating users. The contributions of the FDP system presenting a cost-effective and practical solution for indoor localization, addressing the challenges associated with large data collection and multi-dimensional data requirements, making it a promising approach for real-world applications. We conducted experiments in two real indoor environments, and the results indicated that the proposed FDP system, whether applied to the original radio map or the preprocessed fingerprint database, led to improved localization accuracy and reduced localization time.Subject terms: Information technology, Computer science

Introduction

Location awareness plays a key role in the Internet of Things (IoT), which is manifested in many ways, such as smart device positioning, autonomous driving, robot wayfinding, intelligent infrastructure, health monitoring, intelligent transportation system, unmanned workshop, and smart home 1. Due to the frequent impact of line-of-sight (LoS) and non-line-of-sight (NLoS) switching, the indoor localization mode is not consistent with the outdoor positioning mode with Global Navigation Satellite System (GNSS) 2. Indoor localization-based services, meanwhile, are playing a growing role in office buildings, shopping malls, airports, stadiums, hospitals, homes, etc3,4.

Wireless Fidelity (Wi-Fi)-based localization technologies are considered a suitable alternative to GNSS due to the explosion of smart wireless IoT infrastructure and devices, especially for the indoor environment 5. Wi-Fi technology is not affected by external factors such as light, humidity, temperature, etc.6–9, and it can penetrate obstacles well within a certain frequency band. Indoor localization based on Wi-Fi, which is provided with the above advantages, has been widely studied in numerous fields, especially fingerprinting techniques based on Received Signal Strength (RSS) 6–8 and Channel State Information (CSI) 9. However, due to the large amount of collected data and the high requirements of multi-dimensional data on hardware and software, it is challenging to use CSI in real environments. In contrast, the cost-effective and practical advantages of RSS data make it more widely used.

Indoor localization based on fingerprints method is divided into two phases, which are the offline phase and the online phase 10. In the offline phase, site surveyors use a smartphone to record RF fingerprints from Access Points (APs) within a region of interest. Thereafter, a radio map is built up at the server, in which each fingerprint is associated with the corresponding Reference Points (RPs). In the online phase, a user submits a location query with the current fingerprint, then the server responds by matching the query fingerprint with candidate RPs in the radio map. The matching algorithm plays a crucial role in the online phase as it directly influences the level of localization accuracy.

A variety of matching algorithms based on minimizing the squared fitting errors (under the assumption that the errors follow Gaussian distribution), including k-nearest neighbor (kNN) based on Euclidean distance 11, Naive Bayes (NB) 12, Gaussian process regression (GPR) 13, k-means (k-Means) clustering algorithm 14, Artificial Neural Network (ANN)15, Support Vector Machine (SVM) 16, etc.17 have been proposed in the literature. These algorithms are generally selected based on the initial radio map and frequently prioritize enhancing the localization accuracy in the online phase, resulting in the noise signal in the initial data being brought into the online phase. Overlooking the preprocessing phase in the offline phase can have a detrimental effect on the overall performance of the localization process, as it plays a crucial role in determining the accuracy of the online phase by preparing the fingerprint data for analysis.

In this paper, we employed Convolutional Dictionary Learning (CDL) to process the fingerprint data of each RP in radio map to generate a dictionary that captures the characteristic feature of the site. CDL is a technique used in signal processing and image analysis, which involves learning a set of kernels from input signals 18–20. It is an extension of dictionary learning, which aims to represent a signal as a linear combination of kernels from a pre-defined dictionary. The proposed scheme is named as Fingerprint Dictionary Preprocessing (FDP), which relies on CDL to improve the expressiveness and interpretability of data in radio map. In this scheme, a set of convolutional kernels are learned at each RP that can be used to sparsely represent RSS values from all detectable APs in the offline phase. When positioning is required, the learned kernels are typically optimized to minimize the reconstruction error of user’s fingerprint data, subject to a sparsity constraint that encourages the use of as few kernels as possible in the representation.

In the online phase, CDL is used for fingerprint matching in localization process. Through the learning process, we establish a carefully designed dictionary containing features of Wi-Fi signals. To be specific, we employ dictionary coding techniques to match the target fingerprint against elements in the dictionary. Dictionary coding, based on sparse representation, represents the target fingerprint as a linear combination of selected elements in the dictionary, efficiently modeling complex signals. This matching approach allows us to identify the correlation between the target fingerprint and dictionary elements, enabling us to find the optimal match and achieve precise indoor positioning. The entire process, driven by CDL learning and matching, enhances the adaptability of the localization system to complex indoor environments, providing users with reliable positioning services. Considering Wi-Fi factors such as RSS, spatial distribution, and variability, we can achieve high-precision indoor positioning in dynamically changing environments, offering users an improved localization experience.

The contributions of this study are summarized as follows:

1)The use of CDL can be employed for preprocessing the initial radio map, which allows for the compression of data through feature learning, resulting in a reduction of storage space necessary for data transmission. Different from the traditional method of using Sparse Representation only to characterize fingerprint database, in order to obtain accurate location estimation, this paper also deduced how to use kernel function CDL to optimize fingerprint database to match location.

2)There are a lot of interference data in the actual fingerprint of the location target. In this paper, the CDL method is proposed to process and optimize the single target fingerprint. In the localization phase, the user data is input into the dictionary learned by the FDP scheme to assist in finding the user’s corresponding location, which enhances the matching algorithm.

3)In view of the RSS value changing with time in the real environment, the radio map needs to be updated in time to correct the RP error. FDP system can not only operate on the initial radio map, but also perform on the classic radio map upgrade models and bring about the improvement of localization accuracy, such as LAAFU21, RecNet 22, Supreme 23 and AIPS 10.

The remainder of this paper is organized as follows. In Section II, we introduce system model and problem on indoor localization schemes from the perspective of fingerprint based, and CDL methods. In Section III, we introduce algorithm derivations of FDP indoor localization system. In Section IV, we present two experimental deployments and performance analysis. In Section V, conclusive remarks are presented.

System model and problem formulation

In an indoor environment, the RSS values for a fixed AP at the same location can be influenced by various factors. These factors include the distance between the AP and the receiver, physical obstructions like walls and furniture, interference from other electronic devices, reflection and refraction of the wireless signal, signal propagation characteristics such as fading and diffraction, and the orientation of the AP’s antennas. These influences result in varying recorded numerical results over time. Figure 1. records the change of the RSS value of a fixed location point for two APs in the 2.4 GHz and 5 GHz frequency band within 10 h. The locations of the two APs are consistent, and the location of the receiving device is also fixed. Whether it is the 2.4 GHz signal in Fig. 1(a) or the 5 GHz signal in Fig. 1(b), there will be a sudden change, which mainly depends on the complex indoor radio frequency propagation environment.

Therefore, when analyzing and interpreting the received signal strength data in an indoor setting, it is important to consider these factors and their impact on the recorded values. CDL can be used for anomaly detection and anomalous data processing in databases. By learning the convolutional dictionary of normal data, new data in the database can be reconstructed and compared to detect abnormal data. In the Offline Phase, the process begins with the collection of RSS data from APs at various RPs within the environment. This initial radio map serves as the foundational data set for further processing. The next step involves applying CSC with Orthogonal Matching (Algorithm 1) to this initial radio map, which helps generate a sparse representation of the data. This representation is crucial as it captures the essential features of the radio signals while discarding noise and redundancy. Following this, the sparse coefficients are updated using a Shrinkage Operator (Algorithm 2), refining the sparse representation further. This step ensures that the sparse model accurately represents the signal environment. The process then updates the dictionary and kernel based on these refined sparse coefficients, resulting in an updated radio map. This map, which now contains enhanced and concise information about the signal landscape, is stored and will be used for future localization tasks.

In the Online Phase, the target device collects real-time RSS data from the surrounding APs. This real-time data is then converted into fingerprint data, representing the target’s current signal environment. To prepare this data for comparison with the stored radio map, it undergoes a feature representation process using a method known as CDL which could stand for Convolutional Dictionary Learning or another relevant algorithm. This step translates the fingerprint data into a comparable feature space. The resulting feature representation of the target device is then matched against the updated radio map using a matching algorithm. This algorithm identifies the most likely location of the target by comparing its current signal features with those in the stored radio map. Finally, the identified location is displayed on a map, providing an accurate and real-time localization of the target device within the indoor environment.

Dictionary update for radio map

In early research on CSC, classical fixed bases, such as wavelet and discrete cosine transform, were commonly employed to represent signals 24,25. While possessing favorable properties, such as sparsity and multi-scale representation capability, these fixed bases may not adapt well to different APs and RPs as they are pre-designed and cannot be optimized through training. The development of machine learning and deep learning technologies has led to the emergence of learned dictionaries as a mainstream method for CSC. Compared to fixed bases, learned dictionaries can better adapt to different alterations in radio map and can be optimized through training to improve the performance. Typically, dictionary learning algorithms are used to learn an optimal set of bases from a training dataset such that signals can be represented using as few bases as possible, achieving the goal of CSC. Therefore, learned dictionaries can usually provide better performance and higher representation capacity.

Classical optimization learning tools including algorithmic development with greedy approach26,27 and augmented Lagrangian method28,29 can be employed due to CSC is a convex problem. The current algorithms are designed to run on datasets of limited size, which is not compatible with the multiple APs in one RP and multiple RPs in radio map. Hence, it is necessary to enhance the CDL solution approach to accommodate fingerprint datasets that are much larger than what is presently feasible, thereby enhancing efficiency.

γ is the sparsity regularization parameter, which is used to control the sparsity of the sparse representation. A larger γ value will force higher sparsity, causing many sparse coefficients to approach zero, thereby reducing the complexity and storage requirements of the model, but may lead to oversimplification and loss of important information. A smaller ρ value will allow more non-zero sparse coefficients, thereby retaining more information, but may lead to overfitting. Therefore, choosing a suitable γ value requires maintaining sparsity while minimizing the loss of important signal information.

η is a regularization parameter in the dictionary update process, which is mainly used to control the stability and convergence speed of the dictionary update. A larger η value may cause the dictionary update speed to be too slow, thereby prolonging the training time, but it helps to ensure the stability and convergence of the dictionary update. A smaller η value will speed up the dictionary update speed, but may cause instability and difficulty in converging to the optimal solution. Therefore, the choice of η needs to ensure the update speed without affecting the overall convergence.

In the indoor localization system of this paper, the number of training times required for each RP depends on the number of RPs N and the number of APs A. For the RSS-based indoor positioning system of this paper, the number of training times for each RP is not uniform, ranging from 60 to 500. This ensures that the model can learn enough environmental features and signal patterns, and can strike a balance between computational efficiency and positioning accuracy.

In terms of computational complexity, the CDL-based method involves an iterative process of sparse coding and dictionary updating. The relevant parameters included in this paper are the number of RPs N, the number of APs A, the length of the kernel F, and the number of learned convolution kernels K. The computational complexity is mainly reflected in the following two aspects, the complexity of sparse coding O(N⋅A⋅K⋅F) and the complexity of dictionary updating O(N⋅K⋅F2). Therefore, the overall computational complexity of the algorithm is: O(N⋅A⋅K⋅F + N⋅K⋅F2).

These computational complexities can be adjusted according to specific parameters and experimental environment. In order to obtain a more accurate value, it is necessary to conduct experiments in a real environment and record the calculation time and number of training iterations.

Experiments and results

The proposed FDP-based localization system has been implemented in real indoor environments. We performed experiments utilizing two separate datasets, specifically Testing Environment 1 and Testing Environment 2. The first dataset is gathered at an office room, while the second dataset formed inside a library building. In Testing Environment 1, consider a network situated in an area measuring 8.5 m × 12.6 m. This network consists of four APs positioned at the corners of the rectangular area. Additionally, there are six distinct Test Points (TPs) randomly distributed within the network area. Within this indoor environment, a total of 150 RPs is uniformly distributed and each RP has the ability to receive fingerprint information transmitted by four APs. In a discriminate manner, Testing Environment 2 consists of 348 RPs, with the majority of these RPs having 30 RSS samples. Additionally, there are 46 TPs in Testing Environment 2, each is randomly selected within the region. We examine the offline and test phases of algorithms on a laptop in Testing Environment 1, and then transport the position information to the user device in the real-world scenario. The operation process of Testing Environment 2 is basically similar to that of Testing Environment 1, the difference is that we put the training data and FDP system on a server, which is mainly the reason for the large amount of data in scenario 2.

After calculating the average of the measurement values, the quality of the RSS data can be reliably guaranteed. Initial radio map of testing environment 1.

Since each reference point is measured 60 times in the experiment and the average is taken to calculate the final RSS value, in such a scenario, the T value needs to be at least equivalent to the number of measurements of a single reference point, and generally needs to be higher to ensure the stability and accuracy of the data. Therefore, we fixed the T value at 80 in the experiment, which can ensure sufficient iterations to improve the accuracy of the model without increasing too much computational burden.

The use of CDL for preprocessing the initial radio map allows for the compression of data. Our experimental results demonstrate a significant reduction in storage space due to CDL preprocessing. Specifically, the initial fingerprint library is 128.4 KB, and after compression it is 36.07 KB, the storage space required for the compressed data was reduced by 28.1%. In addition, the compressed data after using traditional Sparse Representation is 81.35 KB, and the compression ratio is 63.36%. These results validate our claim that the use of CDL for preprocessing the initial radio map leads to efficient data compression, resulting in substantial savings in storage space.

In our pursuit of selecting the most suitable classification algorithm, we adopted a comprehensive approach, employing four distinct methodologies: ANN 15, NB 12, kNN 11, and SVM 16. Each of these methodologies was tasked with the critical objectives of matching and localizing within both the initial radio map and the enhanced dictionary. To facilitate clear documentation and differentiation, we designated the four algorithms employed for matching and localization using the initial radio map as IRM-ANN, IRM-NB, IRM-kNN, and IRM-SVM. Similarly, the four algorithms utilized for matching and positioning within the framework of the FDP method proposed in this paper are referred to as FDP-ANN, FDP-NB, FDP-kNN, and FDP-SVM. This design allows comparison for each matching algorithm. The initial radio map does not undergo additional denoising or other preprocessing during the FDP operation. It is noteworthy that the kNN algorithm incorporated in our study was configured with a k value of 4. This specific parameter value was determined through rigorous experimentation conducted in scenario 1, ensuring its optimal performance and relevance to our classification tasks.

Conclusion

In this paper, we present an enhanced FDP approach by incorporating the CDL algorithm into the initial radio map of location fingerprints. This novel structure enables comprehensive exploration of interrelationships between the fingerprint database RPs and optimizes the handling of subsequent changes and updates to fingerprint data. Comparative analyses against the initial radio map demonstrate that the CDL-based FDP approach significantly improves localization accuracy, indicating the efficacy of environmental learning in enhancing localization performance. Furthermore, the proposed FDP method’s transferability across different environmental settings is tested, and the results reveal that it achieves the best localization performance. In a small-scale indoor testing environment, FDP applied directly to the initial radio map yields an average localization accuracy improvement of 34.98%. Moreover, in a large-scale indoor testing environment, the FDP system, when applied after four classical processing algorithms, leads to a remarkable 41.48% enhancement in localization accuracy with reduced localization time.

While FDP system demonstrates significant improvements in localization accuracy and efficiency, it is not without limitations. One notable constraint is the dependency on a sufficiently large and representative dataset for effective kernel learning, which may not be feasible in all environments. Additionally, the computational complexity associated with CDL could pose challenges for real-time applications, especially in dynamic settings. Future research should focus on optimizing the algorithm for faster processing times and exploring adaptive techniques that allow for continuous updates to the radio map, enhancing robustness against environmental changes. Moreover, investigating the integration of multi-modal data sources could further improve localization accuracy and reliability in varied indoor scenarios.
