Disentangling shifting demographic and treatment effects on years of life lost to cancer in Denmark
Authors: Ólafur Birgir Davíðsson, Emelie Hamotal Curovic-Rotbain, Ragnar Pétur Kristjánsson, Jojo Biel-Nielsen Dietz, Signe Holst Søegaard, Mikkel Werling, Fie Stegenborg Andersen, Susanne Dalton, Carsten Utoft Niemann, Mads Melbye, Søren Friis, Henrik Hjalgrim, Klaus Rostgaard
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Cancer is increasingly relevant to ageing societies, yet progress in cancer control is difficult to assess when population years of life lost (YLL) to cancer appear stable. Using Danish registers, we followed 4.26 million residents aged ≥50 years (1980–2019) and estimated YLL under observed and counterfactual scenarios, separating demography and incidence from post-diagnosis outcomes. From the 1980s to the 2010s, YLL per individual changed little (men 2.3 to 2.1; women 2.5 to 2.2), while YLL per patient fell markedly (men 8.5 to 5.2; women 9.3 to 5.8). With 2010s incidence and background mortality but 1980s management, YLL per individual would have been 4.2 (men) and 3.9 (women), showing that cancer control averted about half of the life-years otherwise lost. YLL reductions were largest for localized and regional disease (~60 and ~40%) and modest for distant stage (~15%), highlighting persistent losses in advanced cancer and growing survivorship-care needs.

Introduction

Western populations are aging rapidly, and life expectancy at older ages continues to rise1. Among age-related diseases, cancer stands out as a major obstacle to healthy longevity: its incidence increases steeply with age, and it remains one of the leading causes of death and morbidity in older adults worldwide2,3. In Nordic countries, current estimates suggest that nearly one in two individuals will be diagnosed with cancer during their lifetime, up from roughly one in three in the 1980s4,5. Over the same period, cancer prevention, diagnostics, and treatment have improved substantially, extending survival for many patients, while demographic ageing and rising cancer incidence have pushed the burden of cancer upwards6. These opposing forces yield a mixed and sometimes contradictory picture of progress, making it difficult to judge how effective cancer control has been in preserving remaining life-years in ageing populations and where current outcomes stand relative to past decades.

Declines in age-standardized cancer mortality since the late 1980s are largely attributed to reductions in smoking, the introduction of screening programs, and advances in systemic therapies and supportive care2,6–8. However, progress is not uniform. Lung cancer remains a leading cause of premature mortality, and poor-prognosis cancers such as pancreatic cancer contribute an increasing share of cancer deaths9,10. By contrast, cancers with long-standing screening programs (such as cervix uteri, breast, colon, and prostate cancer) or major therapeutic breakthroughs (such as several hematological malignancies and subsets of lung, breast, testicular, and prostate cancer) have experienced marked improvements in prognosis8,11–16.

To make sense of these long-term and sometimes divergent trends, we summarize cancer burden in terms of years of life lost (YLL), an intuitive measure that expresses how many years of expected remaining life are lost to cancer deaths at different ages17–21. By comparing observed YLL with estimated YLL in counterfactual scenarios where survival patterns are held at earlier levels while demographic structure and cancer incidence reflect later decades, we quantify how much additional cancer burden would have occurred in the absence of improvements in cancer control, and how much of that burden has been averted. This approach builds on seminal work by Olshansky and colleagues, who noted in 1990 that eliminating all cancer mortality at the time would yield only modest gains in life expectancy, underscoring the need to understand how improvements in cancer control translate into life-years preserved20.

In this study, we address these questions in Denmark, using nationwide register data to characterize how cancer affects remaining life expectancy in an ageing population22. We also present traditional metrics such as incidence rates and 5-year survival, and examine how they have evolved over the past four decades. Given Denmark’s universal, tax-financed health system, which provides largely free care at the point of use and is explicitly founded on the principle of free and equal access to healthcare, socio-economic differences in basic access to diagnosis and treatment are comparatively limited. Observed temporal changes therefore likely reflect genuine shifts in disease risk and management rather than widening inequities. Within this framework, we address a simple but fundamental question for an ageing population: given demographic ageing and rising cancer incidence, how much have advances in prevention, diagnostics, and treatment reduced or averted population-level YLL to cancer?

Results

Full descriptive statistics of the study cohort are provided in Supplementary Table 1. In brief, the analysis cohort comprised 4,261,340 unique individuals, observed from January 1, 1980, to December 31, 2019, aged 50 years or older (2,184,878 women; 2,076,462 men), of whom 2,254,426 (52.9%) died during the study period. The number of cases observed in each cancer group is further detailed in Supplementary Table 1. Supplementary Fig. 1 presents the distributions of age at diagnosis in each cancer group analyzed.

Survival and incidence

Overall, 5-year survival after cancer diagnosis improved substantially, from 20.4% for men and 34.1% for women diagnosed in 1980 to 56.7 and 62.2%, respectively, for those diagnosed in 2014. Similarly, in the same period, 5-year survival by cancer stage at diagnosis showed marked improvement: for cancers detected at a localized stage, survival increased from 50.7 to 71.7%; for regional-stage cancers, from 21.4 to 57.4%; and for cancers with distant metastases, from 3.5 to 14.7% (Fig. 1A, B). The marked decline in localized-stage survival and corresponding increase in regional-stage survival around 2003 is a register artifact: mandatory TNM staging was introduced in Denmark in 2004, shifting some cases between categories23. Age-adjusted cancer incidence by stage showed clear, consistent trends over the study period. In 1980, the overall incidence was 11.1 diagnoses per 1000 person-years, with 33.5% of cancers detected at a localized stage, 26.9% at a regional stage, 24.5% with distant metastases, and 15.1% uncategorizable. By 2019, applying the same 1980 age distribution, incidence had increased to 14.1 diagnoses per 1000 person-years (a 27.0% increase), with 58.1% localized, 16.9% regional, 13.2% distant, and 11.8% uncategorizable. Thus, the increase in localized-stage diagnoses likely reflects both a true rise in incidence and a shift toward earlier-stage detection of cancers that would previously have presented at more advanced stages. (Fig. 1C).

We quantified YLL in two complementary ways: per individual (expected YLL for a randomly selected 50-year-old) and per patient (expected YLL conditional on a future cancer diagnosis, averaged over the age at diagnosis distribution implied by contemporary incidence). If post-diagnosis outcomes in the 2010s had remained at 1980s levels, cancer burden would have been substantially higher (Fig. 2). Under this counterfactual, YLL per individual would have risen to 4.2 years in men and 3.9 years in women, and YLL per patient to 10.2 and 10.3 years, respectively. The gap between observed and counterfactual estimates quantifies the life-years preserved by improvements in cancer management over the past four decades.

Stratifying YLL by stage at diagnosis (localized, regional, distant metastasis) showed that, in the 1980s, 50-year-olds were expected to lose 0.6 years of life to localized cancer, 0.7 years to regional cancer, and 0.9 years to cancers with distant metastases. By the 2010s, these values were 0.7, 0.5, and 1.1 years, respectively, indicating broadly stable overall YLL with a shift towards a larger contribution from distant-stage disease. In counterfactual analyses, assuming that post-diagnosis outcomes had remained at 1980s levels, expected YLL in the 2010s would have been 1.9 years for localized cancers, 0.8 years for regional cancers, and 1.3 years for distant cancers. Comparing these counterfactual estimates with observed YLL suggests that modern management has reduced YLL by roughly 60% for localized cancers, 40% for regional cancers, and 15% for distant cancers. (Fig. 2).

Site-specific analyses showed marked heterogeneity in YLL reductions among cancer patients (Fig. 2). In the 2010s, YLL per cancer patient remained highest for cancers typically diagnosed at an advanced stage or with limited curative options (such as pancreatic, lung, liver, and esophageal cancers), and lowest for cancers with effective screening or treatment (including prostate, breast, and melanoma). Counterfactual estimates indicated that, for most sites, modern diagnostics and treatment have substantially reduced YLL compared with 1980s standards, particularly for breast, colorectal and hematological malignancies.

Discussion

Using nationwide Danish register data and a counterfactual YLL framework, we quantified how improvements in cancer control have shaped cancer burden over four decades. At the individual level, YLL from age 50 years appears nearly stable, changing from 2.3 years in men and 2.5 years in women in the 1980s to 2.1 and 2.2 years in the 2010s. This stability is misleading, as under a counterfactual in which post-diagnosis outcomes in the 2010s remained at 1980s levels, unconditioned YLL would have risen to 4.2 years for men and 3.9 years for women. Thus, contemporary cancer control has prevented roughly half of the additional life-years lost that would otherwise have accrued from population ageing and rising incidence. Our findings thus extend the original insight of Olshansky et al. 199020, from a static thought experiment about eliminating cancer mortality to a dynamic evaluation of how improvements in cancer care have continually offset the effects of demographic ageing and increasing incidence.

Stage-specific counterfactual contrasts indicate the largest reductions in YLL for localized cancers, smaller reductions for regional disease, and only modest improvement for cancers with distant metastasis. In parallel, the stage distribution shifted toward localized diagnosis. Together, a greater share of cancers detected before metastasis and improved outcomes in early-stage disease explain how substantial progress can coexist with broadly stable population-level YLL, while also highlighting that remaining losses are increasingly concentrated in advanced disease.

Progress also varied markedly by cancer type. Cancers with established screening and/or effective treatment contribute the largest reductions in population-level YLL, reflecting large cumulative gains where incidence is high. For screen-detected cancers, however, incidence and survival are not independent: screening can inflate both (as in prostate cancer) or remove indolent cases while concentrating aggressive interval cancers (as in cervix uteri), so counterfactual scenarios combining contemporary incidence with 1980s survival should be interpreted cautiously for these sites, and the estimated life-years preserved may partly reflect lead-time and stage-migration artifacts. By contrast, poor-prognosis cancers, particularly lung and pancreatic cancer, continue to account for substantial loss of life-years per patient, with limited improvement over time. A key concern is female lung cancer, where rising risk has been accompanied by increased population-level YLL, underscoring the continued importance of prevention alongside therapeutic advances.

Consistent with Fig. 4A, cancers with high contemporary risk, many of which are screen-detectable and/or highly treatable, show the greatest reductions in YLL per individual, reflecting large cumulative impact at the population level. These gains also appear at the patient level for several common cancers: under 1980s treatment standards, breast cancer was estimated to cost over eight life-years per patient compared with fewer than three years with modern management, and prostate cancer declined from nearly nine years to just under two. In contrast, cancers with persistently poor prognosis, including pancreatic, liver, esophageal, and stomach cancers, continue to result in more than ten life-years lost per patient, with only limited gains over the study period. Head and neck cancers were the main exception, with increasing YLL over time, plausibly reflecting shifts in case-mix as lip cancers (with relatively favorable prognosis) became a much smaller fraction of diagnoses.

A downstream implication echoing the conclusions of multiple other studies, is that cancer survivorship will become an increasingly important domain for ageing societies. As more patients live longer after diagnosis, the population living with a prior cancer diagnosis will grow, increasing demand for survivorship-oriented care that addresses late effects, comorbidity, and functional outcomes24–27. Additionally, alongside improvements in cancer control, cancer-related spending in Europe has risen sharply, with drug costs accounting for a growing share of the total burden28,29. High launch prices for novel cancer medicines add further cost pressure30, and global projections suggest that the economic burden of cancer will continue to increase in the coming decades31, raising concerns about affordability and unequal access to innovation across settings32.

Together, these trends strengthen the case for evaluating cancer control strategies in value terms (e.g., life-years preserved and quality-adjusted outcomes), while prioritizing scalable prevention and early detection where they can avert substantial YLL at relatively low cost. Finally, as continued progress increasingly relies on costly innovation, such value-based evaluation becomes even more important for comparing investments in novel therapies against prevention and early detection. Methodologically, our cause-elimination YLL calculations hold background mortality fixed across scenarios, which can underestimate absolute YLL for common causes of death; however, the counterfactual contrasts still provide a conservative summary of life-years preserved by improvements in cancer management. Additionally, because cancer patients may carry a higher burden of shared risk factors (e.g., smoking, obesity) than comparable cancer-free individuals, some excess mortality attributed to cancer may reflect pre-existing competing risks.

Methods

We followed the STROBE reporting guidelines for cohort studies; a completed STROBE checklist is provided in the Supplementary Information. This study was conducted in accordance with the principles of the Declaration of Helsinki. Under the Danish Act on Research Ethics Review of Health Research Projects (Komitéloven), §14(2), studies based exclusively on register data without human biological material do not require approval from a Health Research Ethics Committee, and individual informed consent is therefore not required. The project was recorded in the Danish Cancer Society’s register of research activities (reference number 2021-CDRC-0033). Data access was granted by the Danish Health Data Authority and Statistics Denmark.

Study cohort and follow-up

We constructed a dynamic cohort of all individuals recorded in the Danish Civil Registration System (DCRS) who were aged 50 years or later at any time between January 1, 1980, and December 31, 2019. Information on vital status and date of death was obtained from the CRS33. For each individual, the start of follow-up was marked by the latest of: (i) their 50th birthday, (ii) January 1, 1980, or (iii) the date of first immigration to Denmark. End of follow-up was marked by the earliest of: (i) date of death, (ii) date of emigration, or (iii) December 31, 2019. Individuals contributed person-time only while resident in Denmark. This yielded the study population and follow-up time used for all analyses.

Cancer case ascertainment, cancer type, and staging

Incident cancer cases were identified from the Danish Cancer Register. Cancer types were classified using ICD7 and ICD10 codes according to categories defined by Danish Health Authorities with minor aggregations34. (Supplementary Table 3).

For each diagnosis, we extracted information on “extent of disease”, TNM classification (where available from 2004 onwards) and, for lymphomas, Ann Arbor stage. A standardized hierarchical algorithm was applied to define stage at diagnosis, classifying diagnoses into four mutually exclusive categories: localized disease, regional spread, distant metastases, and unknown stage. Cases were assigned the most advanced stage compatible with the available data according to prespecified rules.

Descriptive analyses

We first summarized demographic characteristics of the cohort using counts, proportions and medians with interquartile ranges. Age-standardized cancer incidence rates per 1000 person-years were calculated by sex, cancer type and stage at diagnosis using direct standardization to the 1980 Danish population (in 5-year age groups from age 50 years upwards). For each cancer type and stage, we estimated 5-year overall survival from the date of first diagnosis using the Kaplan–Meier method, censoring at emigration or end of follow-up, and stratified results by sex, cancer type, and stage at diagnosis.

This follow-up structure provided the input for all subsequent modeling steps.

We estimated background mortality using Poisson regression35. For each sex and calendar decade (1980–1989, 1990–1999, 2000–2009, and 2010–2019), we selected split-person-time records and modeled the number of deaths in each cell as a Poisson outcome with the log of person-years observed as offset. We included a flexible function of attained age (via smoothing splines) to capture patterns of mortality by age and allowed the mortality to differ by calendar decade.

From these models, we obtained smoothed estimates of the background mortality rate as a function of age, stratified by sex and calendar decade. These estimates were then evaluated on a fine age grid from 50 to 120 years. For ages above 100, we assumed that the background mortality rate was constant and equal to the estimated rate at age 100.

Age-specific incidence rates for each cancer site and stage were estimated using similar Poisson models. For each sex, cancer stage, and calendar decade, we selected person-time during which individuals were at risk of a first diagnosis of that cancer and modeled the number of incident cases in each year-age cell as a Poisson outcome with the log of person-years as offset. As for mortality, we used flexible functions of age (and, where needed, calendar year) to obtain smooth incidence curves by age for each decade. These models yielded smoothed age-specific incidence rates for each combination of sex, cancer site, stage, and decade.

To describe mortality after cancer diagnosis, we adopted an excess-hazard approach. For each sex, cancer site and stage, we used the split-person-time data for individuals with a prior diagnosis of that cancer. Within strata defined by attained age, time since diagnosis, calendar decade and sex, we counted deaths and person-years. For each such stratum, we also computed the expected number of deaths given the background mortality rate estimated above, by multiplying the background rate by the corresponding observed person-years.

We then fitted Poisson models in which the observed number of deaths were expressed as the sum of expected deaths (under background mortality) and an excess component attributable to the cancer36. The excess mortality rate was modeled as a function of time since diagnosis and allowed to vary by sex, cancer site, stage and calendar decade of diagnosis. From these models, we obtained estimates of the excess mortality rate over time since diagnosis for each cancer site/stage, sex and decade. For the time since diagnosis beyond 15 years, we assumed that the excess mortality rate remained constant at the level estimated for ≥15 years in order to stabilize estimates and account for limited data availability.

Together, the background mortality, cancer incidence, and post-diagnosis excess mortality rates provide the building blocks for the years of life lost calculations.

Two YLL frameworks are predominantly used in the literature. The first, death-based YLL, quantifies, for each cancer death at age a, the shortfall between a and the expected remaining life expectancy at a in the absence of cancer, then sums over cancer deaths. This approach is often predicated on accurate cause-of-death registration. The second is diagnosis-anchored, cause-elimination YLL that asks, conditional on a cancer diagnosis at age a, how many years would be preserved if cancer conferred no excess mortality from diagnosis onward (i.e., survival followed background mortality only), with other risks unchanged; akin to scenario where cancer is instantaneously, permanently, and ubiquitously cured at diagnosis (e.g., via perfect surgery or treatment), with no subsequent adverse effects.

We adopt the latter framework since it is the only framework amenable to scenario-based counterfactual modeling. It also has the benefit of circumventing biases from cause-of-death attribution in quantifying life-years preserved after diagnosis. We estimate YLL in two complementary ways: (i) at the population level, defined as the expected life-years lost to cancer per individual (evaluated at baseline age 50) and (ii) among cancer patients, defined as the average loss conditional on diagnosis.

All quantities of interest were derived from the above-described mortality- and incidence rate using a flexible methodology that could accommodate scenario-based simulations. We calculated sex-specific mortality rates across age categories (a = 50, 51, 52, …, 120 years) and time since diagnosis (t = 0, 1, 2, …, 14, ≥15 years). In intermediate steps, rates were also stratified by calendar period. To stabilize estimates and account for limited data availability, the excess mortality rate beyond 15 years after a cancer diagnosis was assumed constant, as was the background mortality rate for individuals aged 100 years and older.

The relationship between age and mortality in the general population was estimated using Poisson regression, where the log mortality rate was smoothed using a spline with 10 degrees of freedom35. The parameterization of these estimates does not allow for a straightforward expression of statistical uncertainty. While extracting precise error estimates would be computationally cumbersome, the potential error in these counterfactual calculations is negligible. Therefore, results are presented without uncertainty intervals.
