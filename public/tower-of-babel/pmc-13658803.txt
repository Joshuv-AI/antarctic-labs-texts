New thinking for the next generation of antimalarials
Authors: Hannah Asiki, Jason Hlozek, Kathryn J Wicht, John G Woodland, Kelly Chibale
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Despite decades of intensive research and substantial clinical gains, malaria remains a major global health challenge exacerbated by the continued emergence of drug-resistant strains of the causative agent, Plasmodium parasites. Encouragingly, recent advances in functional genomics, chemical biology and computational science are reshaping antimalarial drug discovery. In this review, we examine the discovery and development of next-generation antimalarials, including advances in phenotypic and target-based screening, omics-enabled target discovery and emerging therapeutic modalities such as long-acting agents, targeted covalent inhibitors and host-directed therapies. We further evaluate the opportunities and limitations of drug repurposing and discuss how artificial intelligence and data-driven approaches are reshaping target identification, compound optimisation and clinical development. Finally, we argue that future success will depend not only on scientific innovation but also on interdisciplinary collaboration, equitable partnerships, open science and the development of accessible therapies tailored to malaria-endemic populations.

Introduction: why do we still need new antimalarials?

Despite considerable progress over the past two decades, malaria remains a major global health burden and a persistent challenge for molecular medicine. The most recent estimates indicate that there were approximately 282 million malaria cases and 610,000 deaths worldwide in 2024, with the vast majority occurring in sub-Saharan Africa and disproportionately affecting children under 5 years of age (WHO, 2025). Although more than two billion cases and over 12 million deaths have been averted since 2000, progress has stalled in recent years, with case numbers increasing and incidence rising since 2015. This stagnation reflects a convergence of biological, environmental and systemic pressures, including climate change and population growth, as well as the concerning emergence and spread of antimalarial drug resistance.

Artemisinin-based combination therapies (ACTs), the cornerstone of current malaria treatment, are increasingly threatened by resistance. Alarmingly, artemisinin partial resistance has now been confirmed or suspected in multiple countries in Africa, accompanied by the declining efficacy of partner drugs (WHO, 2025). Historically, the spread of resistance to earlier antimalarials such as chloroquine and sulfadoxine-pyrimethamine (SP) led to dramatic increases in malaria mortality, underscoring the fragility of current therapeutic gains. The continued evolution of Plasmodium parasites, combined with widespread drug pressure, raises the realistic prospect that current frontline therapies may become ineffective within the foreseeable future.

The contemporary antimalarial pipeline (Fig. 1) should be viewed against a remarkable transformation in malaria drug discovery over the past two to three decades. At the turn of the century, the pipeline of genuinely novel antimalarial medicines was sparse, reflecting limited commercial incentives and declining pharmaceutical-sector investment in diseases predominantly affecting low- and middle-income countries. The establishment of the Medicines for Malaria Venture (MMV) in 1999 and subsequent growth of public-private and philanthropic investment helped to change this landscape, creating a coordinated discovery and development network spanning academia, industry and endemic-country partners. This investment, together with advances in parasite culturing methods, high-throughput phenotypic screening and molecular technologies, contributed to what has been described as a renaissance in antimalarial drug development, generating new chemical classes and mechanisms of action (MoAs) alongside improved combinations of existing medicines (Anthony et al, 2012; Flannery et al, 2013; Hooft van Huijsduijnen et al, 2019).Figure 1An illustration of the evolving antimalarial drug discovery and development landscape.Thousands to millions of compounds are typically employed in phenotypic or target-based high-throughput screening; these are eventually whittled down to dozens of compounds that undergo lead optimisation—with only a handful making it all the way through the clinic. However, a strong pipeline does not diminish the need for continued innovation. Antimalarial discovery and development remain lengthy and vulnerable to attrition (Wouters et al, 2020), while parasite resistance can progressively erode the therapeutic lifetime of otherwise highly effective medicines. Moreover, the ambition for new antimalarials has expanded considerably beyond replacing existing blood-stage treatments. Contemporary target candidate and product profiles reflect these broader ambitions, prioritising novel MoAs, high barriers to resistance, activity across multiple parasite life cycle stages, prevention of transmission and relapse, and simplified or long-acting regimens suitable for treatment and chemoprotection (Burrows et al, 2017). Together with the identification and validation of new molecular targets, the increasingly ambitious profiles are now driving the development of next-generation antimalarial strategies.

On the other hand, drug repurposing offers an additional complementary approach, leveraging existing pharmacological knowledge to accelerate development timelines and reduce costs. However, its success depends on overcoming challenges related to dosing, pharmacokinetics and target relevance. Finally, the integration of artificial intelligence (AI) and data-driven methodologies is beginning to reshape the antimalarial drug pipeline, enabling more efficient target identification, compound optimisation and clinical trial design. Together, these approaches point toward a more integrated and multidisciplinary paradigm for antimalarial drug discovery to outpace resistance and reinvigorate global malaria control efforts.

An overview of the biological toolkit for developing next-generation antimalarials

Life cycle complexity of Plasmodium and implications for drug discovery

Plasmodium parasites have a complex life cycle from the human host to the mosquito vector and back again. The five human-infecting Plasmodium species share similar life cycles, exemplified by P. falciparum.

Transmission begins when a female Anopheles mosquito injects sporozoites into the skin, which migrate via the blood to the liver. Crossing Kupffer cells, they invade hepatocytes and undergo asexual replication for about a week, forming schizonts that release ~20,000 merozoites into circulation (Frevert, 2004; Wirth and Pradel, 2012). These rapidly invade erythrocytes, initiating the 48-h blood cycle responsible for malaria symptoms (Crutcher and Hoffman, 1996). Parasites progress from ring forms to trophozoites and schizonts, fuelled by haemoglobin degradation and producing haemozoin crystals (malaria pigment) (Francis et al, 1997). Merozoites egress from schizonts, perpetuating parasitaemia (Wirth and Pradel, 2012). A fraction of merozoites undergo gametocytogenesis, producing mature crescent-shaped gametocytes after roughly 10 days (Bousema and Drakeley, 2011). These circulate in the blood for ~7 days where they can be ingested by a mosquito, mature into gametes, undergo fertilisation and form motile ookinetes (Angrisano et al, 2012). Penetrating the gut wall, ookinetes develop into oocysts that generate sporozoites, which migrate to salivary glands, completing the cycle (Ouologuem et al, 2023).

Given the extensive biological transformation of the parasite as it progresses through the stages, drug discovery for malaria is inherently multifaceted, wide-ranging and defined by the specific stages being targeted. Historically, most drugs have targeted the asexual blood stage (ABS) which causes clinical symptoms and is easiest to study in the laboratory (Swearingen and Lindner, 2018). However, with global efforts toward elimination, new drugs must overcome resistance, simplify dosing and provide longer-lasting protection. This shift has expanded the diversity of compounds in development, guided by target candidate and target product profiles (TCPs and TPPs) (Siqueira-Neto et al, 2023). For case management, combinations are sought that treat blood-stage infection (TCP-1), prevent relapse (TCP-3) and reduce transmission (TCP-5). For chemoprotection during outbreaks, TCP-4—activity against hepatic schizonts—is vital, and is often paired with TCP-1 to suppress new infections. Additionally, endectocides (TCP-6), which kill mosquitoes feeding on treated individuals, offer a novel transmission-blocking strategy. Together, these approaches highlight the need for therapies that address both disease treatment and malaria eradication.

Traditional approaches to antimalarial drug discovery

Antimalarial drug discovery has traditionally relied on phenotypic screening, in which compounds are tested directly on whole parasites without prior knowledge of their molecular targets (Antonova-Koch et al, 2018; Guiguemde et al, 2010). This method has been highly effective because it measures functional outcomes in parasite culture and can reveal complex biology, including novel MoAs (Hovlid and Winzeler, 2016). Once a promising compound is identified, unravelling its precise MoA and pinpointing the drug target is often difficult, time-consuming and resource-intensive, although this knowledge is not required for regulatory approval (Swinney, 2013). Notably, ultra-high-throughput screening (HTS) campaigns against extensive compound libraries have previously been conducted with ABS parasites (Gamo et al, 2010), while the capacity to screen against other Plasmodium life cycle stages is orders of magnitude lower (Hovlid and Winzeler, 2016).

In contrast, target-based drug development begins by selecting a specific protein or pathway believed to be essential for parasite survival. Compounds are then screened or optimised to act against this target (Guerra and Winzeler, 2022). For a protein to be considered “druggable”, it must contain a binding pocket suitable for small molecules or antibodies, differ sufficiently from human proteins to ensure selectivity, and preferably be expressed across multiple parasite life cycle stages (Arendse et al, 2021). Ideally, it should also resist mutation under drug pressure to minimise the risk of resistance. This approach can also involve multi-target strategies in which inhibiting more than one protein produces synergistic effects or creates a “resistance trap”, making it harder for the parasite to adapt (Stokes et al, 2019a; Wicht et al, 2020).

Despite these theoretical advantages, translating target-based inhibitors into effective whole-cell activity has proven challenging. Progress has been slow, as optimising pharmacological properties and ensuring clinical relevance remains a complex and risky endeavour (Hovlid and Winzeler, 2016). This contrast between phenotypic and target-based approaches underscores both the promise and the difficulties of modern antimalarial drug discovery. Nevertheless, the identification of antimalarial drug targets has largely followed phenotypic screening, where compounds with strong activity against parasites are first discovered and their MoAs are then investigated afterwards (Godinez-Macias et al, 2025b).

An important classical approach used to retrospectively determine the target of existing antimalarials is a genetic cross, whereby male and female gametes fuse inside the mosquito gut, undergoing meiosis and gene mixing (Vendrely et al, 2020; Wellems et al, 1990). The resulting progeny are then isolated and analysed to map specific genes linked to survival, drug resistance and potential target pathways. However, the execution of genetic crosses in P. falciparum is technically challenging and resource-intensive; therefore, only four experimental crosses were completed during the first four decades of P. falciparum genetics research (Button-Simons et al, 2021; Vaughan et al, 2015). However, recent advances using humanised mouse models have enabled several additional crosses, facilitating resistance trait mapping for drugs such as piperaquine and quinine (Kanai et al, 2026; Kane et al, 2024).

In the context of current drug development, one of the most effective strategies for uncovering targets is in vitro evolution and whole-genome analysis (IVEWGA) (Luth et al, 2018). In this approach, parasites are cultured under drug pressure until resistant strains emerge. These resistant parasites are cloned, sequenced and compared to the original sensitive strain to identify mutations such as single-nucleotide variants or copy number variants (Ng and Fidock, 2019). Typically, these mutations occur in genes encoding the target of the drug, which can then be validated using tools such as CRISPR/Cas9 (Lee et al, 2019). This unbiased method is considered the gold standard for target discovery and has been central to the Malaria Drug Accelerator (MalDA) consortium, leading to the identification of several key targets (Forte et al, 2021a).

Examples include the Plasmodium P-type cation ATPase, PfATP4 (target of cipargamin or KAE609); dihydroorotate dehydrogenase, PfDHODH (DSM265); translation elongation factor 2, eEF2 (DDD107498); acetyl-CoA synthetase, PfACS (MMV019721 and MMV084978) and phosphatidylinositol 4-kinase, PfPI4K (MMV390048)—all essential for parasite survival across different stages (Luth et al, 2018). IVEWGA has also revealed Plasmodium aminoacyl-tRNA synthetases (aaRSs) as critical targets (Xie et al, 2023). These enzymes drive protein synthesis in multiple parasite organelles. Inhibitors such as halofuginone (prolyl-tRNA synthetase), cladosporin (lysyl-tRNA synthetase) and benzoxaboroles (leucyl-tRNA synthetase) show potent activity, although none has advanced to clinical trials due to challenges with stability, bioavailability and selectivity (Khan, 2016).

Despite its success, IVEWGA can be time-intensive, and some compounds are “irresistible”, meaning that they fail to generate resistant strains. Such compounds may act on multiple targets or non-mutable pathways, making them highly valuable as they are less prone to resistance. To study these, researchers employ complementary methods, including biochemical assays (e.g., haemozoin formation inhibition), fluorescence microscopy and testing against conditional knockdown lines (Langeveld et al, 2025). Additionally, omics-based approaches such as proteomics and transcriptomics have expanded target discovery (Cowell and Winzeler, 2019; Mohammed et al, 2024; Painter et al, 2018); these approaches will be discussed later in this review.

A variety of chemoproteomics methods have been useful in determining drug targets (Challis et al, 2022; Lu et al, 2021b). For instance, covalent immobilisation and pull-down experiments helped validate PfPI4K as the target of MMV390048 (Paquet et al, 2017b) and to identify purvalanol B as a casein kinase 1 binder (Knockaert et al, 2000). Activity-based protein profiling (ABPP) was employed to identify the essential α/β serine hydrolase monoacylglycerol lipase (PfMAGLLP) as the target of a salinipostin A (Yoo et al, 2020). Furthermore, a cellular thermal shift assay coupled with mass spectrometry (MS-CETSA) studies revealed that quinine, one of the oldest antimalarials, binds to the purine nucleoside phosphorylase (PfPNP) (Dziekan et al, 2019).

Biochemical assays have also been useful in supporting target identification studies. For example, the PfArk1 (aurora-related kinase 1) inhibitors hesperidin (Langeveld et al, 2025) and CHIR-124 (Wicht et al, 2025), the cGMP-dependent protein kinase (PfPKG) inhibitor ML10 (Baker et al, 2017) and the PfPI4K inhibitors AZD0156 (Woodland et al, 2025), MMV390048 (Paquet et al, 2017b) and KDU691 (McNamara et al, 2013) were all shown to bind to recombinantly expressed versions of these enzymes. Together, these strategies highlight the evolving toolkit for antimalarial drug discovery. While phenotypic screening remains the foundation, integrating genomic, biochemical and proteomic methods has broadened the landscape of validated targets, offering new opportunities to combat resistance and discover new therapeutic strategies.

However, the criteria for next-generation therapies are stringent. Since no fully protective vaccine exists, chemoprevention remains essential for vulnerable groups, including travellers, pregnant women and children under the age of five (Greenwood et al, 2021). Long-acting oral and injectable drugs are expected to form the backbone of chemoprevention, addressing current barriers such as frequent dosing, limited options in early pregnancy and challenges for women living with HIV. Ideally, these drugs should also target multiple parasite life cycle stages (Braunack-Mayer et al, 2026; El Gaaloul et al, 2024). Drug development must also consider tropical climate conditions, ensuring chemical stability in heat and humidity. Because children are the primary population affected, formulations must be child-friendly, with taste-masking and dispersibility, alongside rigorous pharmacovigilance to account for genetic variations in drug metabolism (Siqueira-Neto et al, 2023). Seasonal malaria chemoprevention (SMC) is particularly critical, yet resistance to sulfadoxine-pyrimethamine-amodiaquine necessitates new alternatives. Effective SMC drugs should combine high potency, safety, long half-lives and complementary MoAs to enable monthly dosing (Plowe, 2022).

Unlike diseases predominantly afflicting wealthier nations, antimalarial drug development faces unique challenges: high costs, limited commercial returns and reliance on public procurement through national malaria control programmes and agencies such as the Global Fund (Hanefeld, 2014). These realities underscore the need for sustained innovation and global collaboration to deliver safe, effective and accessible therapies.

Can drug repurposing help to deliver new antimalarials?

Drug repurposing is a strategy in which existing medicines are used to treat diseases for which they were not originally designed, sometimes involving further structural modification of the compound (drug repositioning). One of the biggest benefits of this approach is a significant reduction in the drug development timeframe and an associated reduction in cost, as the safety and tolerability profile of the active component is often already well established. Repurposing and repositioning are enticing approaches for diseases such as malaria, in which drug development is not commercially attractive. The potential for faster regulatory approval and implementation, therefore, offers an alternative path to traditional de novo drug discovery.

Approaches to drug repurposing

Phenotypic screening

Identifying suitable drugs for repurposing is a key step in the success of this approach, which has often employed HTS of compound libraries for activity against Plasmodium. As described above, traditional screening has focused on phenotypic responses, as this provides an unbiased way to identify compounds with varied targets acting on different life cycle stages of the parasite. Most phenotypic-response assays have been developed against the ABS, which was the basis for the MMV “boxes”—compounds prioritised by MMV for the development of new antimalarials (Adam et al, 2024; Spangenberg et al, 2013).

However, it is important not to overlook other parasite life cycle stages in HTS campaigns seeking new chemical matter to reposition. Gametocytes are an important bottleneck for transmission to the mosquito vector, and blocking this has been identified as a key target area (Burrows et al, 2017). New screens are continually being developed (D’Alessandro et al, 2016; Delves et al, 2018; Lucantoni et al, 2016; Miguel-Blanco et al, 2015; Paonessa et al, 2022; Plouffe David et al, 2016), including incorporating a mouse model for in vivo monitoring of transmission blocking (Brancucci et al, 2025). A recently developed transgenic Ookluc line has enabled HTS for compounds that inhibit parasite fertilisation, the initial step within the mosquito vector, which provides a new tool for compound identification (Calit et al, 2023).

Key chemical matter has also been identified from screening for in vivo activity against Plasmodium within the mosquito (Probst et al, 2025), which contributes to malaria control. Screening against liver stages has often focused on the liver schizonts and early hypnozoites (Maher et al, 2021a; Maher et al, 2021b; Nguyen et al, 2024; Roth et al, 2018), but recently the first HTS of dormant liver-stage hypnozoites has been established (Maher et al, 2025), which has been missing from the routine drug discovery pipeline and contributes towards finding a radical cure, i.e. complete removal of dormant parasites alongside ABS parasites to prevent disease relapse.

Drug target repurposing

In addition to repurposing existing drug compounds, drug targets themselves can also be repurposed. This target repurposing approach has been especially evident with kinase targets originally identified in cancer research, which are now being investigated in Plasmodium (Younis et al, 2012). The PfPI4K inhibitor MMV390048 progressed to Phase IIa clinical trials (Mohammed et al, 2023; Paquet et al, 2017a), although the development of this compound was discontinued due to embryofoetal toxicity in rats (Demarta-Gatsi et al, 2022). In another target repurposing example, the anticancer compound sapanisertib (MLN0128), originally developed to target the human mTOR kinase, has demonstrated excellent activity across multiple parasite stages as a dual PfPI4K and PfPKG inhibitor (Arendse et al, 2022), with drug repositioning also being pursued (Gachuhi et al, 2025). Another success story is imatinib, an FDA-approved tyrosine kinase inhibitor that was recently discovered to block the egress of Plasmodium from erythrocytes (Kesely et al, 2016) and has been used in a clinical trial to enhance antimalarial efficacy when combined with artemisinin (Chien et al, 2021).

Target-based screening

A complementary strategy for drug repurposing is target-based screening, mentioned briefly above, which seeks to identify compounds active against validated Plasmodium targets, with subsequent confirmation through phenotypic validation. A list of priority targets has been released by MalDA, based on chemical and genetic validation, propensity to generate resistance, druggability, assay availability and structural information (Forte et al, 2021b). Although there is still a need to develop high-throughput screens specific to many of these targets, a few examples do exist. Screening for inhibitors of the mitochondrial enzyme Plasmodium dihydroorotate dehydrogenase (DHODH) identified compound DSM265 after structural optimisation (Baldwin et al, 2005; Margaret and Pradipsinh, 2010; Phillips et al, 2015), although this target has since been deprioritised as resistance has been found to arise relatively easily (Forte et al, 2021b). For kinase targets, a PKG HTS in combination with Kinobead studies has led to the discovery of a promising multi-stage targeting scaffold (Penzo et al, 2019), while five Plasmodium kinases were screened against a large library which demonstrated the capacity to selectively target Plasmodium kinases over mammalian ones (Crowther et al, 2016).

Combining multiple screening strategies can be a powerful way to more rapidly prioritise candidates for repurposing, particularly by leveraging computational methods to dock and screen compounds in silico against known targets or pathways. Target-specific computational screens have been developed for compounds targeting chemically-validated kinases (Godara et al, 2024), the haemozoin formation pathway via β-haematin formation (de Sousa et al, 2020), and DHODH (Pavadai et al, 2016), among others.

At this juncture, it is noteworthy that the potential for a drug to be repurposed requires demonstrating that a predicted efficacious dose for the new indication is within the already-established clinical safety and tolerability profile of the drug to be repurposed. This was recently demonstrated for malaria using a human dose prediction approach based on integrating PK-PD modelling with physiologically-based pharmacokinetics modelling to investigate the potential to repurpose an anticancer human kinase inhibitor (Redhi et al, 2026).

Challenges of drug repurposing

Although drug repurposing is an appealing route to discovering new antimalarials, challenges remain. Despite open-access initiatives led by organisations such as MMV and collaborations across public and private sectors sharing compound identities, intellectual property restrictions are still in place for certain structures, with examples where companies patent whole compound series even if they are later discarded (Chesbrough and Chen, 2013). This disincentivises exploration of known chemical entities and was reported as the second most common barrier to repurposing after financial constraints (Krishnamurthy et al, 2022). Additionally, there can be restrictions to data sharing, meaning that abandoned projects and their corresponding results remain unpublished, restricting knowledge-sharing (Rogawski and Federoff, 2011). Another challenge is that the regulatory pipeline for repurposed drugs is less well defined than for novel compounds, introducing additional financial risk (Ivanov et al, 2024; Recino et al, 2025). Even after a suitable drug has been identified for repurposing, it must still undergo randomised clinical trials for malaria which can be logistically challenging, costly and time-consuming, particularly in the resource-limited settings with the highest burden of malaria (Espinoza, 2025). Although these events conspire to limit drug repurposing and repositioning efforts, a few successful examples, such as the kinase inhibitors discussed above, highlight the potential of this approach to uncover new antimalarial medicines.

New frontiers and other promising approaches for next-generation antimalarials

The current landscape of drug discovery is increasingly shaped by modern omics-driven approaches, and malaria research is no exception. These cutting-edge technologies enable probing of fundamental Plasmodium biology to identify new drug targets. Recently, 540 novel, druggable targets were prioritised following inspection of the full P. falciparum genome to identify proteins essential for the ABS parasites with evidence of small molecule binding (Godinez-Macias et al, 2025a). The annotation used in this process is freely available at https://pftargetbrowser.org/ and downstream scoring by MalDA experts has put forward 29 potential new targets, with five particularly highlighted: aspartate transcarbamoylase (ATCase), topoisomerase I (TopoI), DNA gyrase subunit B (GyrB), glucose-6-phosphate dehydrogenase-6-phosphogluconolactonase (GluPho) and bromodomain protein 1 (BDP1). These can serve as a starting point for target-based drug discovery and it is likely that these will prove to be invaluable.

Omics approaches to accelerate antimalarial drug discovery

Genomics

Omics approaches (genomics, transcriptomics, proteomics and metabolomics) have utility in elucidating the underlying MoA of active compounds, an important aspect of drug characterisation, and have been extensively reviewed (Carolino and Winzeler, 2020; Lu et al, 2021a; Okombo et al, 2021; Yu et al, 2021). A recent in-depth study combined genetic information from 724 P. falciparum resistance cell lines, uncovering novel resistance genes and enabling the identification of multi-drug resistance markers (Luth et al, 2024). This dataset contributes a starting point for prioritising irresistible chemical matter and unravelling the key mutations in genomics experiments for MoA deconvolution.

Genomic overexpression techniques involve the use of cell libraries engineered to systematically overexpress potential targets, in which overexpression can confer resistance under drug pressure if the target is directly engaged by the compound. Such libraries have been successfully applied to MoA deconvolution in other parasitic diseases such as leishmaniasis and trypanosomiasis (Begolo et al, 2014; Gazanion et al, 2016). Until recently, this has not been possible for malaria owing to the difficulty in large-scale genetic manipulation of Plasmodium, but the advancement in P. knowlesi transfection efficiency coupled with barcode sequencing and artificial chromosomes has enabled a cell overexpression library to be developed and validated with a PI4K inhibitor (Bower-Lepts et al, 2024). This tool is a novel addition to the target discovery pipeline, and it is likely to expedite the identification of MoAs of phenotypically discovered compounds in the future.

Transcriptomics and proteomics

The Plasmodium transcriptome can also contain valuable information about expression levels following drug perturbation, and this has been recently investigated with known antimalarial compounds which revealed distinct clustering dependent on compound MoA (Ishii et al, 2025). In one example, the Plasmodium transcriptome was screened to identify essential, multi-stage genes and their associated targets, after which appropriate chemical matter was identified; this helps to prioritise compounds for drug repurposing (Borba et al, 2023). Single-cell transcriptomics can similarly be applied to identify crucial proteins and predict likely drug structures to engage these (Choudhuri and Ghosh, 2024). As briefly mentioned above, a Plasmodium-specific cellular thermal shift assay (CETSA) has been developed to show definitive target engagement by detecting shifts in protein melting temperature upon target engagement, enabling proteome-wide profiling (Dziekan et al, 2020) and which has recently been improved (Pazicky et al, 2026).

Compound resistance generation

As resistance remains a concern for antimalarial drug development, it is important to consider the likelihood of resistance emerging for new scaffolds. This is well established by checking for minimum inoculum of resistance for promising new compounds, in the hope that in vitro resistance is not easily generated, although this does not always translate well to the field (Duffey et al, 2021). It is possible also to interrogate the target, demonstrated by the proof-of-concept study ResMAP, which mutated every amino acid around the binding pocket of lysyl-tRNA synthetase (KRS) to investigate resistance likelihood (Wall et al, 2024). One can envision applying this to other targets to identify key amino acid mutations conferring resistance, which in turn can guide compound design to circumvent these mechanisms.

Morphology-based techniques for mode of action elucidation

Morphology-first approaches, in which changes in cell morphology are correlated to MoAs, are an upcoming trend in drug discovery and can also help predict toxicity. In mammalian cells, a cell painting approach was able to identify morphological changes upon drug perturbation across large compound screens, employing machine learning analysis (Wolff et al, 2025). A similar project is currently being undertaken in Plasmodium through a partnership between MMV, the University of Dundee and LPIXEL (Venture, 2025). Powerful combinations of cell painting with existing transcriptomics data can enable predictions of novel compounds in the absence of additional costly new transcriptomics data (Ha et al, 2025); it remains to be seen if this can be applied to Plasmodium.

New modalities in drug discovery

Host-directed therapies

While traditional drug discovery has primarily focused on small molecules acting on Plasmodium protein targets, there are new modalities emerging that merit discussion. One such approach is host-directed therapy (HDT), in which compounds act on host rather than parasite targets. This can be especially valuable when pathways essential for Plasmodium development, such as invasion of erythrocytes and/or hepatocytes, are disrupted. Its application is confounded by the fact that different Plasmodium species use distinct receptors for host cell invasion (Manzoni et al, 2017) and there are marked differences in host susceptibility to invasion (Ebel et al, 2021), which perhaps accounts for the fact that this approach is yet to yield candidates for clinical development. Modulation of the immune response is an alternative HDT. In this context, the anti-inflammatory drug ruxolitinib has shown limited success when repurposed for acute malaria infection to suppress the immune response, potentially boosting immunity in future infections (Webster et al, 2025).

Targeted covalent inhibitors

A second new modality for antimalarials is targeted covalent inhibitors (TCIs); these are compounds in which the active component attaches covalently to an amino acid residue in the drug target, in contrast to traditional drugs that typically rely on weak, noncovalent binding kinetics to drive target engagement. This approach has potential advantages of better selectivity, longer duration of action and therefore lower resistance risk and lower required dosing, among others (Singh et al, 2011). Nine TCI drugs have been FDA-approved in the last decade for cancer treatment (Dhillon, 2023; Singh, 2022), and although no TCIs are in clinical trials yet for malaria, discovery efforts are ongoing. The proteasome has historically been a challenging target to drug, but was successfully targeted with TCIs, which demonstrated the expected benefit of decreased propensity for resistance generation (Stokes et al, 2019b). Brettell et al. further achieved target engagement with TCIs designed against cyclin-dependent-like protein kinase-3 (CLK3), providing the first proof-of-concept for covalent kinase inhibition in Plasmodium (Brettell et al, 2025; Brettell et al, 2024). In addition, CRISPR-based technologies can be applied to finding targetable amino acid residues, potentially driving interest in the TCI modality (Benns et al, 2022).

Long-acting injectables

In a push towards longer-lasting protection, long-acting injectables (LAIs) are an alternative modality. These are desirable because they can provide sustained prophylaxis by effectively behaving as a chemical vaccine. LAIs may also help reduce malaria transmission (Macintyre et al, 2018). Several compounds have been developed as LAIs, including MMV371, a prodrug of atovaquone already shown to provide 3 months of protection that is entering Phase Ib clinical trials (ClinicalTrials.gov; Gupta et al, 2024), and ELQ-331, a prodrug of ELQ-300/MMV055 (Karunakaran et al, 2024; Smilkstein et al, 2019).

Monoclonal antibodies

Monoclonal antibodies (mABs) are another modality for long-lasting protection (Aleshnick et al, 2022). Advantages of mABs include their high specificity, which makes them amenable to combination therapy, and likely favourable safety profiles over traditional small molecules. However, one current limitation of mABs is their high cost. For malaria, a key consideration is the complex life cycle of Plasmodium in comparison to the viral diseases for which mABs have been highly successful. An ideal candidate would act on the pre-erythrocytic stages, as achieving protection at this point is generally more feasible than attaining sterile clearance of blood stage parasites. One example is CIS43LS, a monoclonal antibody in Phase II clinical trials that targets the circumsporozoite protein (CSP), key for sporozoite invasion of hepatocytes (Skinner et al, 2025). CSP has attracted significant attention because the truncated form is a component of the newly approved RTS,S/AS01 and R21/Matrix-M vaccines (Singer et al, 2024). Another sporozoite protein of interest is thrombospondin-related adhesive protein (TRAP), although using TRAP combined with the RTS,S vaccine did not show an additional benefit (Kester et al, 2014). In the erythrocytic stages, reticulocyte-binding protein homologue 5 (RH5) has emerged as the leading target due to its essentiality to merozoite invasion of erythrocytes, with anti-PfRH5 showing promise in monkeys (Douglas et al, 2019).

Chimeric molecules

Finally, among the most promising innovations are chimeric molecules, particularly proteolysis targeting chimaeras (PROTACs), which harness cellular proteolytic machinery to degrade essential proteins (Birkholtz et al, 2026). Unlike traditional small molecules, these agents can act on previously “undruggable” targets, reduce required therapeutic concentrations and slow resistance development (Ng et al, 2017).

Precision medicine

The highest malaria burden is in sub-Saharan Africa, which has the most genetically diverse populations yet remains comparatively under-characterised. This presents a particular challenge for precision and/or stratified medicine in which patient genetics, environmental circumstances and lifestyle should be factored into treatment decisions. Limited understanding, along with high costs and practical constraints, still remain to be overcome before precision and/or stratified medicine can become a reality for malaria (Okeke and Ksouri, 2026).

Combination therapies

Combination therapies form the cornerstone of the current frontline drugs and, as already mentioned earlier, usually involve combining fast-acting artemisinin with a slower-acting partner drug to form ACTs (WHO, 2023). However, the emergence of partial resistance to ACT regimens has prompted consideration of adding a third drug, forming triple ACTs (TACTs) as a strategy to delay resistance development. These regimens are already being evaluated in trials across Asia and Africa (van der Pluijm et al, 2020). Mathematical modelling has been applied to assess the benefits of TACTs, as the long-term evolutionary dynamics of resistance cannot be assessed with clinical trials. These indicate that early implementation of TACTs, especially in Africa, where artemisinin resistance is relatively low, could have a marked reduction in treatment failure rates (Nguyen et al, 2023).

Clinical development and dose optimisation

The development of a next-generation antimalarial does not end with the identification of an efficacious molecule or even regulatory approval. Selection of an appropriate dose and formulation across the populations in which the drug will ultimately be deployed is equally important, particularly because malaria disproportionately affects young children and pregnant women, in whom PK can differ substantially from that of non-pregnant adults. Historically, PK data in these populations have often been limited at the time dosing recommendations were established. For example, subsequent population PK analysis of sulfadoxine-pyrimethamine (Fansidar) showed that children aged 2–5 years had approximately half the drug exposure observed in adults after adjustment for dose, suggesting that contemporary paediatric dosing recommendations were suboptimal (Barnes et al, 2006). For tafenoquine, modelling of pre-registration trial data indicated a clear dose-response relationship and suggested that the licensed 300 mg adult dose achieves substantially less than the maximal obtainable hypnozoiticidal effect, although the safety and efficacy of higher doses require prospective evaluation (Watson et al, 2022). Thus, clinical pharmacology should be considered a continuum extending from early development through Phase I–III trials to post-marketing surveillance and dose optimisation (Fig. 1). For next-generation antimalarials, early inclusion of children, pregnant women and other priority populations in appropriately designed PK/PD studies will be critical to ensure that innovative medicines translate into optimal real-world efficacy.

AI and data-driven innovation in antimalarial drug discovery

Artificial intelligence (AI) is increasingly being applied within the drug discovery pipeline, enabling the integration of heterogeneous datasets to enable analysis and prospective predictions which may not be readily apparent (Ferreira and Carneiro, 2025). Indeed, the number of clinical candidates that have employed AI during their development continues to grow (Jayatunga et al, 2024). Frequent areas of application include improving target identification, compound optimisation and clinical development. Here, we highlight several common areas of application with relevant examples.

AI-enabled target identification and validation

As described above, a central challenge at the start of the drug discovery process is the identification of novel drug targets within complex biological systems which can unlock new treatment options. Multi-modal deep learning models, such as the PandaOmics platform (Kamya et al, 2024), can integrate knowledge from multi-omics datasets and gene interaction networks to prioritise genes associated with disease (Pun et al, 2023). In addition, large-language models (LLMs), such as Google’s Co-Scientist and OpenAI’s Deep Research (Moons et al, 2025), can efficiently mine large bodies of scientific literature and identify unrecognised connections to support novel target identification and assist with target deconvolution for compounds with unknown MoAs. Computer vision models provide an alternative approach for target deconvolution by comparing cellular phenotypes induced by novel compounds to reference profiles of compounds with known MoAs (Chandrasekaran et al, 2021). Following target identification, tools such as AlphaFold can predict the structure of identified protein targets and facilitate downstream identification of druggable pockets for which generative AI methods can then propose chemical starting points by optimising chemical structures against predicted binding interactions (Adams et al, 2025).

AI-aided compound discovery and optimisation

Quantitative structure-activity relationship (QSAR) models learn relationships between chemical structure and various biochemical parameters to efficiently guide multi-parameter compound optimisation (Turon et al, 2023; Zhang et al, 2025). Complementary approaches assess synthetic feasibility and propose optimised synthetic routes (Tu et al, 2025) while ligand-based generative models, such as the REINVENT package (Loeffler et al, 2024), can explore novel chemical space. Though AI tools typically require technical expertise to use, platforms such as the Ersilia Model Hub (Turon, 2024) and Drug Discovery 4 Global Health (DD4GH) (Nasto, 2025) are lowering barriers to adoption by providing accessible, no-code implementations of these tools.

AI in clinical design and implementation

Clinical trials represent the final and most costly stage of the drug development pipeline. While the application of AI for an antimalarial clinical trial has not yet been reported, AI methods are increasingly being applied in other therapeutic areas such as oncology (Askin et al, 2023). As AI approaches are often transferable between disease areas, examples from other disease areas can provide a foundation for potentially improving the design of future antimalarial clinical trials. The most frequent application is to support patient recruitment (Askin et al, 2023) by integrating digital clinical records with unstructured medical data to identify eligible patients more efficiently (Beck et al, 2020). AI is also leveraged to optimise trial design, including cohort composition and endpoint selection to improve prediction success (Aliper et al, 2023) and to maximise the statistical power of trial findings. Additional applications include data analysis of trial results and real-time monitoring of participants to predict patient dropout to enable early intervention.

As indicated above, a significant challenge in conducting clinical trials for antimalarial treatment is the high genetic diversity of populations in endemic regions. Variation in pharmacogenes can lead to substantial differences in drug response (Haga, 2024; Zack et al, 2025). This remains acutely understudied in Africa, where over 60% of malaria clinical trials occur in populations possessing the greatest genetic diversity of any continent (Gomez et al, 2014; Takata et al, 2020). To address this, a collaboration between the H3D Centre at the University of Cape Town and the Ersilia Open Source Initiative analysed data from the PharmGKB database to identify variably expressed pharmacogenes in African populations and identified drug-gene pairings for common drugs used to treat malaria and tuberculosis. In that work, machine learning (ML) and AI were coupled to pharmacometrics modelling to hypothesise pharmacogenes of potential clinical interest, with the goal to propose dose adjustments for better treatment outcomes (Turon et al, 2025).

Challenges for AI integration into antimalarial drug discovery

Despite these advances, applications of AI in antimalarial drug discovery remain more limited than in other disease areas (Leung et al, 2026; Odah, 2025). Key challenges include data scarcity, complex parasite biology, limited research resources and the need for context-specific deployment. Insufficient high-quality training data, a common limitation in resource-constrained settings, reduces model performance and generalisability beyond the training data. Meanwhile, mechanisms to connect data silos and provide incentives for sharing data have been explored in the Global North. For example, the malaria inhibitor prediction (MAIP) platform was developed through standardised model training across partners with subsequent model sharing for mutual benefit from a broader chemical space without exposing proprietary data (Bosc et al, 2021). Alternatively, federated learning approaches enable securely pooling data through a trusted third-party platform to facilitate model training with a broader domain of applicability while preserving data privacy and benefit sharing across contributors (Napoli et al, 2025).

Overall, AI and data-driven approaches provide a powerful framework for accelerating antimalarial drug discovery. However, their impact will depend on investment in data generation, improved data sharing infrastructure and careful integration into experimental workflows to translate into real-world benefit for the costly research and development process. Mutually-beneficial data-sharing methods should be encouraged in antimalarial research to unlock the field’s full potential and bridge the divides between established and emerging research centres.

Conclusion: What will a successful next-generation antimalarial look like?

The future of antimalarial drug development will depend not only on discovering new compounds but also on rethinking how antimalarial therapies are identified, optimised and deployed. As discussed throughout this review, the next generation of antimalarials must move beyond the historical focus on rapid clearance of ABS parasites toward integrated, multi-stage interventions capable of supporting malaria elimination. A successful next-generation antimalarial will therefore likely combine several key characteristics: activity across multiple parasite life cycle stages, a high barrier to resistance, compatibility with long-acting or simplified dosing strategies, and affordability and relevance for widespread implementation in endemic regions.

Ultimately, effective malaria medicines require fixed-dose drug combinations to prevent resistance and enhance compliance. However, discovery begins with single compounds before identifying optimal partners. Advances in parasite biology and target discovery are already reshaping the field. The expansion of genomic, transcriptomic, proteomic and chemoproteomic toolkits has dramatically increased the number of validated and potentially druggable targets in Plasmodium parasites. Approaches such as IVEWGA, CRISPR-based validation, CETSA profiling, overexpression libraries and morphology-based phenotypic screening are accelerating MoA deconvolution and helping to identify compounds with reduced resistance liability. Importantly, these approaches are broadening the therapeutic landscape beyond conventional small molecules, enabling the exploration of emerging modalities including TCIs, HDTs, PROTACs, mABs and LAI chemoprotection. Collectively, these developments signal a transition from empirical discovery toward increasingly mechanism-informed and systems-level antimalarial development.

At the same time, the review highlights that no single discovery strategy is likely to be sufficient in isolation. Phenotypic screening remains a cornerstone of antimalarial discovery because of its ability to identify biologically active compounds in unbiased cellular contexts, yet target-based approaches provide critical mechanistic insight and opportunities for rational optimisation. Increasingly, the most effective paradigm is likely to emerge from synergy between experimental and computational approaches. AI and machine learning are beginning to augment nearly every stage of the discovery pipeline; however, AI is not a substitute for experimental biology. Rather, its value lies in integrating complex datasets, identifying hidden relationships and guiding iterative cycles of empirical validation. In malaria research specifically, the impact of AI will depend heavily on the generation of high-quality datasets, context-specific modelling and closer integration with wet-lab workflows.

Drug repurposing similarly represents a complementary rather than a standalone solution. As discussed above, repurposed compounds and targets—including kinase inhibitors originally developed for oncology—have demonstrated the potential to accelerate discovery timelines and uncover novel mechanisms of action. Yet repurposing also illustrates many of the broader challenges facing antimalarial development, including pharmacokinetic incompatibility, toxicity concerns, intellectual property barriers and uncertain regulatory pathways. Consequently, the future pipeline will likely depend on balancing repurposing strategies with continued investment in genuinely novel chemical matter and first-in-class mechanisms.

Finally, scientific innovation alone will not determine success. Malaria disproportionately affects populations in low- and middle-income countries where financial return on drug development remains limited. As a result, sustained progress will require strong public–private partnerships, open-science initiatives and globally coordinated funding mechanisms. Organisations such as MMV and collaborative consortia such as MalDA demonstrate the power of precompetitive collaboration, shared compound libraries and collective target prioritisation. Expanding equitable access to data, infrastructure and leadership opportunities—particularly for researchers and institutions in endemic regions—will be essential both ethically and scientifically. The increasing use of AI further reinforces the importance of equitable data sharing and representation, particularly given the underrepresentation of African populations in pharmacogenomic and clinical datasets.

Ultimately, a successful next-generation antimalarial is unlikely to be defined by a single “breakthrough” molecule. Rather, it will emerge from an interdisciplinary and globally- connected innovation ecosystem that integrates molecular parasitology, medicinal chemistry, computational biology, clinical pharmacology and so on. Such therapies must not only overcome biological resistance but must also be deployable under real-world conditions: chemically stable, child-friendly, cost-effective and accessible to the communities that need them most. Achieving this vision will require balancing technological innovation with practical implementation and global equity. If these challenges can be met, the next generation of antimalarials may not simply sustain malaria control but help enable humanity’s long-standing goal of malaria elimination.
