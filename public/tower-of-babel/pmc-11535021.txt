Statistical analysis of the association between El Niño and the biological carbon pump in the East Sea (Japan Sea)
Authors: Geunsoo Jang, Seunghyun Hong, Janghun Oh, Young-Il Kim, Minkyoung Kim, Hyojung Lee
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
Understanding the impacts of climate change on oceanic carbon cycling is important from a carbon sequestration perspective. A sediment trap study focused on the biological carbon pump system in the Ulleung Basin (UB) in the southwestern part of the East Sea (Japan Sea) was conducted from 2011 to 2017. Particulate organic carbon (POC) flux significantly increased by 37, 56, and 43% from 2014 to 2016 during the El Niño phase. We examined data related to water current variability, such as sea surface height, current velocity, and eddy frequency to understand their roles in particle transport. In addition, a Martin curve was employed to analyze the rate of vertical attenuation of POC flux in the UB. Current variability could be the most important factor influencing the increase in sinking-particle flux during the El Niño phase.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-024-74818-2.

Introduction

The biological carbon pump (BCP) is one of the main mechanisms of the oceanic carbon pump system. It involves a series of biogeochemical processes associated with carbon sequestration1. In brief, primary production (PP) in the upper layers of the ocean water column fixes inorganic carbon into biomass. These particles, including particulate organic carbon (POC), start to sink into the deep ocean interior, undergo remineralization, leading to attenuation with depth, and are stored in deep-sea sediments2. Accordingly, the sinking POC flux is primarily affected by the seasonal variation of PP. In addition to this, aeolian deposition, riverine inputs, sediment resuspension, and lateral transport can affect the sinking POC flux3–7. Mesoscale dynamics such as eddies, and episodic typhoons can also affect the POC flux8–10. Climate changes, such as global warming, potentially directly and indirectly impact the oceanic uptake of atmospheric CO2; therefore, understanding its dynamics and controlling factors is important11. Previous studies revealed that El Niño can be linked to global atmospheric changes triggered by fluctuations in sea surface temperatures (SST)12.

Additionally, variations in sea surface wind patterns and ocean water depth may influence the occurrence of El Niño13. Furthermore, significant climate changes can persist even after the disappearance of El Niño14. El Niño has different characteristics depending on where it occurs15, and it has different impacts depending on the ocean14,16–20 and the stage of El Niño21,22.

Sediment traps have been widely used to collect sinking particles in the ocean interior to better understand BCP23,24. However, long-term sediment trap studies are globally restricted due to their operation and maintenance difficulties. Therefore, statistical methods are crucial in Earth and Ocean Sciences for addressing the challenges posed by limitations in field observations, difficulties in data collection, and the often-encountered issue of data scarcity25–27.

The East Sea (Japan Sea, hereafter EJS) is a semi-enclosed marginal sea located east of the Korean Peninsula, surrounded by Russia and the Japanese Islands. Physical and biogeochemical changes in the EJS are known to occur faster than those in the adjacent Pacific; therefore, long-term and multidisciplinary approaches to understanding changing biogeochemical parameters are necessary28. Continuous sediment trap deployment has been conducted in the Ulleung Basin (UB) in the southwestern part of the EJS since 201129,30. Kim et al.28 examined the 1-year sinking-particle flux at 1000 m and 2000 m depths in the UB from 2011 to 2012, showing a bimodal POC flux with the highest flux in spring, reflecting variations in net PP. The following study by Kim et al.29 expanded the idea and deployed sediment traps at 500 m, 1000 m, and 2000 m depths to collect sinking-particle samples from 2011 to 2017.

Interestingly, the sinking POC fluxes in the UB significantly increased during the El Niño phase (2014–2016)29. The increase in POC flux was interpreted in conjunction with factors such as PP, aeolian deposition, and the Oceanic Niño Index (ONI). In particular, mesoscale eddies and their role in transporting resuspended sediments have been proposed. However, the important factors have not been clearly described, and we recognize the need to investigate these aspects further by assessing the statistical analyses in our study. Further research and data analyses are required to better understand the relative contributions of these factors to the increased particle flux in the UB.

In the present study, we aimed to analyze the impact of two factors on the increase in POC flux in the UB 2014 to 2016: (i) the association with the 2015/16 El Niño and (ii) enhanced particle transport resulting from current variability on increased sinking POC fluxes in the UB. Elevated SST during El Niño can increase surface productivity, which results in increased sinking POC. In addition, the increase in sinking POC flux also represents an increase in export efficiency (e.g., the POC/PP ratio). Surface current variability can partly explain the influences of mesoscale physical dynamics on sinking particles. We categorized the periods into El Niño and non-El Niño phases and performed a thorough statistical analysis to explore the factors behind the increase in particle flux. Our comprehensive statistical analysis identified the most influential factor contributing to the increase in particle flux during the El Niño phase. We anticipate that this interdisciplinary analysis has the potential to provide valuable insights into the interpretation of results in the field of Earth and Ocean Sciences.

Results

Association between the impact of the SST and El Niño phase

We examined the impact of SST increases on POC flux by dividing the dataset into El Niño and non-El Niño phases. Association between the impact of the surface current variability and the El Niño phase

We investigated the impact of current variability in surface water during the El Niño phase on sinking POC flux. Figure 3 compares the monthly SSH, VEL, ACE, and CE averages between the El Niño and non-El Niño phases. Figure 3B and D show that eddy current velocity (m/s) and amplitude (m) were somewhat higher during the El Niño phase, from January to March and June to August. When comparing the El Niño and non-El Niño phases, the frequency (event/month) of eddies did not exhibit statistically significant differences. Although no significant differences were observed in the frequency of CE and ACE between El Niño and non-El Niño periods, our investigation revealed that the average amplitude of ACE was consistently higher during El Niño events. This finding suggests that the intensity of these eddies, rather than their mere occurrence, may play a critical role in modulating the observed patterns of POC flux. We applied the SARIMAX model to analyze whether ENSO influences the time-series patterns of various oceanographic physical variables. While the Mann-Whitney U test provided an initial assessment of the differences in monthly distributions of these variables between El Niño and non-El Niño phases, it was not thorough enough to definitively determine variations in their time-series patterns. The SARIMAX model included a dummy variable for El Niño phases to assess its significance, aiming to statistically ascertain if the time-series patterns of oceanographic variables change during the El Niño phase, as shown in Table S2. As a result, we found significant differences in the time-series patterns of the ACE amplitude and speed average variables between El Niño and non-El Niño periods.

Discussion

Kim et al.29 observed increased sinking POC flux during the El Niño phase in the UB and proposed the potential effect of mesoscale eddies. Other studies have shown that mesoscale eddies can affect the seasonal variability of BCP systems17,33,34. Gaube et al.33 found that nonlinear mesoscale eddies can enhance Chl-a and phytoplankton biomass by up to 100% through Ekman pumping, which impacts the PP and marine ecosystems. Dufois et al.34 found that eddies in the South Indian Ocean affect the surface Chl-a during winter by seasonal adjustment of the mixed layer depth by eddies. The effects of El Niño on sinking-particle flux in the UB are poorly understood, whereas those in the Pacific have been relatively well studied35–37. A continuous, long-term observational study is necessary to better understand the association of the BCP with climate change in the UB. With oceanic sample collection and observations, it is beneficial to adapt mathematical and statistical analyses to compensate for data deficiencies.

This study investigated the factors responsible for the notable increase in sinking POC flux during the El Niño phase from 2014 to 2016. The increase in POC flux indicates (i) an increase in PP and/or (ii) an increase in carbon export efficiency, and/or (iii) carbon transport from elsewhere. Substantial increases in Chl-a and PP were observed during the El Niño phase, particularly in April and August. To address this, we compared SST changes between the El Niño and non-El Niño phases to clarify the influence of the increase in POC flux. As a result, it became apparent that the upsurge in sinking POC flux during El Niño could not be solely attributed to SST changes when comparing the El Niño and non-El Niño phases. This finding suggests that a more intricate interplay of factors is involved, warranting further research.

Additionally, we investigated the impact of current variability during El Niño phases on sinking POC flux. We compared the monthly SSH, VEL, and eddies (ACE and CE) averages between El Niño and non-El Niño phases. Although most variables did not exhibit distinct time-series patterns during El Niño, the current velocity showed a notable increase, particularly from January to March and June to August, with significant variations from February to March. Current variability can influence the sinking POC flux dynamics, particularly in current velocity and eddy amplitude. Using the Mann-Whitney U test, we found that the differences observed might be due to factors other than increased SST during El Niño.

Additionally, by applying the SARIMAX model to assess the impact of ENSO on time-series patterns of various oceanographic physical variables, we identified significant differences in the time-series patterns, specifically in the amplitude and speed average variables of ACE, between El Niño and non-El Niño phases; this suggests that the amplitude of ACE was greater during El Niño phase than in non-El Niño phase. This increase in ACE could be a key factor contributing to the lateral transport of particles, as inferred from the analysis of eddy data.

We utilized the Martin curve to predict the estimated anomaly regarding the mass of POC transported and compare the POC attenuation rates during the El Niño and non-El Niño phases to further evaluate the extent of current variability. We found that the laterally transported particles at 1000 m and 2000 m were notably higher during the El Niño phases. As a result, we statistically analyzed the data to demonstrate that lateral particle transport is a more likely significant factor influencing the increase in POC flux compared to the rise in SST during the El Niño phase, aligning closely with previous suggestions in existing research30. This comparative analysis allowed us to thoroughly explore the factors contributing to the increase in the sinking POC flux and the significance of lateral transport.

Limitations of the study

Despite new efforts to combine oceanography and statistics, several limitations remain. Herein, we present the limitations and potential future studies. First, although our study thoroughly analyzed various factors related to the increase in sinking POC flux, including SST, SSH, VEL, and eddies, it is important to acknowledge that additional factors may be considered.

Second, relevant data are lacking. This study reused published sinking particle data, and the sampling period was sufficiently short to cover long-term variations. Further deployment of mooring lines with sediment traps and current meters is necessary to gain insights into the relationship between ENSO and its effect on the BCP. Biologically, mesoscale eddies can supply nutrients and modulate light availability in the surface water, thereby enhancing PP38. To thoroughly understand this process, combined research cruises to collect water samples and measure nutrients in depth are necessary. The current study has limitations as it did not encompass all potential contributing elements. The study site in the East Sea (Japan Sea) exhibits complex physical dynamics, making it challenging to trace particle sources using the surface current data alone due to the absence of a current meter deployment in this mooring line. To address this, various biogeochemical tracers have been utilized to understand the sources and trajectories of sinking particles30,31,39. Regardless, it is essential to recognize that this research augmented its analysis by supplementing oceanographic data, such as velocity and eddy data, and employing mathematical and statistical approaches to quantitatively compare the POC attenuation levels. These additional statistical analyses add significance to our investigation of the abrupt increases in sinking POC flux.

Third, we did not compare the POC flux during several El Niño phases in various regions. Studies about El Niño show that it has different characteristics depending on where it occurs15 and has different impacts depending on the ocean14,16–20 and the stage of El Niño21. In addition, few studies link El Niño to sinking particles. The previous research in the subtropical Pacific region36,37 highlighted the significant impact of ENSO events. However, the sampling periods for these studies span from 2003 to 2013, which do not coincide with our sampling periods.

Furthermore, we conducted a Martin curve fitting process with data from specific depths (500 m, 1000 m, and 2000 m). Data from a broader range of depths could have provided more extensive insights, which could be further solved by collecting high-resolution in-depth water with large-volume in situ pumps at the same site. Furthermore, to ensure data consistency across all three depths, we limited our fitting to five distinct periods: \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:{T}_{1}$$\end{document} to \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:{T}_{5}$$\end{document} and accordingly, the seasonal variations in sinking particle fluxes and compositions that might affect the sinking particle decomposition could not be fully considered. Despite these limitations, we obtained sufficient results to examine the differences in sinking POC flux attenuation between El Niño and non-El Niño phases.

In conclusion, our study demonstrates the complex nature of the sinking POC dynamics in the UB, particularly during El Niño events. Although SST alone does not explain the observed increase in POC flux, factors such as current variability in the surface water and the influence of eddies appear to play a significant role. Martin curve analysis further substantiated the influence of El Niño on the attenuation of sinking POC flux. The importance of our study lies in the comprehensive examination of these factors and their combined impact on the sinking POC flux. This reanalysis proposes the necessity of multidisciplinary approaches in ocean and earth sciences with a lack of available data. These findings contribute to a better understanding of the intricate processes governing carbon sequestration in the ocean, with implications for climate change and ocean carbon cycling studies. It can also help us comprehend how climate change impacts oceanic carbon flux, providing valuable data for future circulation change predictions.

Methods

Regional setting

The sinking-particle data used in this study were obtained from Kim et al. (2020). In brief, sinking particles intercepted by bottom-tethered sediment traps deployed on a mooring at three depths (500 m, 1000 m, and 2000 m; ∼2370 m total water depth) between 2011 and 2017 at station ES (37.63°N, 131.36°E) in UB (Fig. 7). Further details on the sampling method are described in Kim et al.29. The PP and Chl-a in this study obtained from the previous study30. The PP was estimated using the Vertically Generalized Productivity Model (VGPM)38 and the optimal carbon fixation rate31,40. For the statistical analysis, we utilized PP and the Chl-a concentration. Although the existence of the subsurface chlorophyll maximum (SCM) may increase the uncertainty in the satellite-derived NPP, in the EJS, the Moderate-Resolution Imaging Spectroradiometer (MODIS)-derived PP41,42 is relatively consistent with in situ measurements of PP43. Therefore, we decided to use the satellite data to enlarge the number of data points for a more robust statistical analysis. The ONI represents the 3-month moving average of the monthly SST anomalies in the Niño 3.4 region, a monitoring area for El Niño/La Niña events44. It serves as an indicator of the El Niño phase or the non-El Niño phase. We extracted oceanic physical data, including SST, SSH, and VEL, from Mercator Ocean’s45 analysis from July 1, 2011, to September 30, 2017. These data were collected within the geographical bounds of latitude 33.75°–38.25°N and longitude 125.25°–134°E (Fig. 7). We calculated the averages of these variables in the vicinity of the four specified areas marked A, B, C, and D in Fig. 7, originating from a specific location marked with an asterisk. This specific location served as the mooring site for obtaining sample data. Thus, we selected three oceanic physical variables–SSH, SST, and VEL–obtained from the Copernicus Marine Service45. We obtained the frequency and magnitude of mesoscale eddies using eddy tracking data in Aviso + to match the spatial and temporal domains of the oceanic physical data46. Eddies are detected using Absolute Dynamic Topography maps generated using the most advanced altimeter satellites. Materials and methods

Analysis of two factors: increased POC flux during El Niño

The ONI was used to classify the El Niño events, which indicates the influence of El Niño-Southern Oscillation (ENSO) on the Pacific climate. According to the Climate Prediction Center (CPC), El Niño is defined when the ONI exceeds 0.5 for five consecutive months44. To clarify the difference in POC flux between El Niño and non-El Niño phases, we defined the El Niño phase when the ONI was greater than zero (i.e., ONI > 0), which was also used in Lee et al.47. In addition, the Pacific Decadal Oscillation (PDO) also plays a significant role, especially in the North Pacific (north of 20°N). ONI is a better indicator to indicate EI Niño events than PDO even though the UB is not located in the equatorial Pacific region, where the influence effect of the EI Niño expected. We especially use the ONI as the high sinking particle flux periods match the super EI Niño phase well. There are some studies about the relationships between East Korea Warm Current and the EI Niño48–50.

According to the redefined definition of ONI and PDO, the period from April 2014 to May 2016 was the El Niño phase, as shown in Figure S1. We compared whether there were statistically significant differences between the El Niño and non-El Niño phases in terms of weekly or monthly averages of the variables.

Two factors have been proposed to explain the increased particle flux: one factor is the impact of the SST on PP, which is associated with the 2015/16 El Niño. The other factor is enhanced particle transport because of the current variability. Figure 8 shows the outline of the statistical analysis to explore the impact of two factors. Regarding the first factor, we examined surface productivity data, including Chl-a and PP, to investigate the productivity surge attributed to elevated SST during El Niño. Our analysis included data related to surface productivity, such as SST, Chl-a, and PP32,51. We compared the amounts of PP and the sinking POC during the El Niño and non-El Niño phases. For the second factor, we analyzed data related to current variability, including variables such as SSH, current velocity (VEL), and eddy occurrence frequency (Eddy), aiming to elucidate the potential causes of the heightened sinking POC flux. Furthermore, we employed the Martin curve to assess the differences in sinking POC flux attenuation during the El Niño and non-El Niño phases. These two factors collectively explain the increased particle flux from 2014 to 2016.Fig. 8Graphical abstract of two factors leading to an increase in POC flux. Vertical POC attenuation using Martin curve

It has been proposed that sinking POC in the UB is transported via dynamic sources, especially by using biogeochemical tracers such as radiocarbon values of POC29,30. With multidisciplinary specialties in the statistical analysis of various oceanic datasets, we analyzed several datasets and found that the sinking POC flux was more pronounced based on two primary factors.

We used the Martin curve as a mathematical model to re-examine these resuspended and/or laterally transported particles and quantitatively analyze the variability of the vertical POC flux in the UB. The Martin curve uses a power law to describe the POC flux attenuation2,3. Several studies have employed the Martin curve to investigate biogeochemical fluxes, particularly the PP and POC52,53. Cael et al.52 introduced a probabilistic approach, revealing that the probability distributions of two significant biogeochemical fluxes, the POC flux, and marine PP, follow a log-normal distribution. Furthermore, they demonstrated a statistically significant linear relationship between the two physical quantities53.

Efforts have been made to mathematically describe the relationship between the POC flux according to the water depth. Various models have been proposed, including those using exponential functions54, extended models such as the ballast model55, and models employing rational functions56. We employed the best-known and traditional model, the Martin curve2,57.
