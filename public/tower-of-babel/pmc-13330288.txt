Integrative single-cell and spatial multi-Omics analyses identify NETs-driven colon cancer subtypes with distinct metabolic features and prognostic implications
Authors: Xiao-Long Tang, Yi He, Wen-Yu Luan, Kai-Zhen Xu, Zheng Zhang, Zhen-Xi Xu, Yu-Hui Shang, Wen-Jian Hu, Mao-Yin Shan, Jian Gan, Ya-Bo Wang, Yan-Dong Miao
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundColon cancer remains one of the leading causes of cancer-related deaths worldwide and is associated with high rates of recurrence and metastasis despite advances in therapy. The tumor microenvironment (TME), characterized by complex immune and stromal interactions, plays a pivotal role in tumor progression and treatment resistance. Neutrophil extracellular traps (NETs), an emerging component of the TME, have been implicated in promoting tumor invasion, metastasis, and immune evasion. However, their specific role in colon cancer remains unclear.MethodsComprehensive transcriptomic analyses were conducted using datasets from The Cancer Genome Atlas (TCGA) and Gene Expression Omnibus (GEO) to identify NETs-associated genes involved in colon cancer. Single-cell RNA sequencing (scRNA-seq) was utilized to assess cellular heterogeneity, and spatial transcriptomics mapped NETs activity and cell–cell interactions within the TME. A prognostic model was constructed using multivariate Cox and LASSO regression analyses based on key NETs-related genes. Model performance was validated using internal and external cohorts. Additionally, colocalization analysis between TUBB2A eQTL signals and colorectal cancer overall survival GWAS effects was performed to investigate potential genetic correlations.ResultsTwo distinct NETs-related molecular subtypes of colon cancer were identified, differing in immune composition, metabolic activity, and gene expression profiles. NETs-high malignant cells demonstrated metabolic reprogramming involving oxidative phosphorylation and cellular respiration, contributing to immune escape and therapeutic resistance. A four-gene prognostic signature (ARRDC1, TUBB2A, DUSP5, and SLC2A3) was developed and showed moderate prognostic discrimination across internal and external cohorts, indicating potential value for risk stratification but limited predictive accuracy at the current stage. Colocalization analysis revealed a modest negative correlation between TUBB2A expression and overall survival and cancer-specific survival, suggesting that increased TUBB2A expression may be associated with adverse clinical outcomes.ConclusionsThis study reveals that NETs-associated genes play crucial roles in colon cancer progression, immune modulation, and metabolic reprogramming. The identified four-gene signature may serve as a candidate biomarker for risk stratification in colon cancer, although further validation is needed.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12967-026-08227-6.Keywords: Colon cancer, Neutrophil extracellular traps, Tumor microenvironment, Single-cell RNA sequencing, Spatial transcriptomics, TUBB2A, Biomarkers

Introduction

Colon cancer is one of the most common malignancies worldwide, and its increasing incidence and mortality impose substantial clinical and economic burdens on both patients and healthcare systems [1]. This burden is further amplified by the high cost of treatment, which often involves surgery, chemotherapy, and long-term surveillance, thereby placing considerable pressure on healthcare resources. Although advances in surgery, chemotherapy, immunotherapy, and targeted therapy have improved patient care [2], their overall effectiveness remains limited by recurrence, treatment resistance, and toxicity. These limitations highlight the need to clarify the molecular mechanisms underlying colon cancer and to identify actionable biomarkers that can improve diagnosis, prognosis, and therapeutic outcomes. These challenges are driven, at least in part, by pronounced tumor heterogeneity, adaptive metabolic rewiring, persistent inflammatory signaling, and the establishment of an immunosuppressive tumor microenvironment (TME), all of which may contribute to treatment resistance, recurrence, and therapy-related failure [3, 4].

The complex pathogenesis of colon cancer is characterized by the involvement of multiple cell types and various intricate signaling pathways [3], which collectively necessitate the application of advanced research methodologies to effectively elucidate the TME and the critical intercellular interactions that occur within it [4]. Recent research has begun to elucidate the complex molecular landscape of colon cancer, revealing the critical role of the TME in disease progression [5]. Among the emerging components of this microenvironment, neutrophil extracellular traps (NETs) have attracted increasing attention. NETs are web-like structures composed of chromatin DNA decorated with histones, proteases, and antimicrobial proteins, which are released by activated neutrophils during NETosis and can influence tumor progression through inflammatory amplification and immune modulation [6]. Recent studies have highlighted the pivotal role of NETs in promoting tumor cell metastasis, particularly in colon caccer [7–9].

In colon cancer specifically, NETs have been implicated in circulating tumor cell capture, enhanced tumor–endothelial adhesion, inflammatory amplification, recurrence-related processes, and reduced therapeutic responsiveness [10–15]. These observations suggest that NETs may represent an important mechanistic link between tumor progression and microenvironmental remodeling in colon cancer [9, 16]. However, the precise molecular features of NETs-related malignant states and their spatial and cellular interactions within the colon cancer microenvironment remain incompletely understood. Therefore, high-resolution multi-omics approaches, particularly single-cell RNA sequencing and spatial transcriptomics, may provide valuable insight into the cellular heterogeneity and intercellular communication networks associated with NETs-related tumor biology.

Single-cell RNA sequencing (scRNA-seq) has become a powerful tool for cancer research because it enables high-resolution analysis of cellular heterogeneity and the immune landscape in colon cancer [17]. Single-cell technologies have substantially advanced understanding of colon cancer, particularly tumor heterogeneity and the TME [18]. For example, scRNA-seq has been used to identify distinct subpopulations of cancer stem cells in colorectal cancer (CRC) with gene expression profiles linked to metastasis and treatment resistance [19]. Studies have also implicated the immune microenvironment in CRC progression, showing that regulatory T cells (Tregs) and tumor-associated macrophages (TAMs) promote immune evasion and tumor growth [20]. In addition, scRNA-seq has illuminated mechanisms of drug resistance in CRC by revealing targetable cellular pathways [21]. Beyond cell-type identification, scRNA-seq enables comprehensive interrogation of signaling networks and transcriptional programs associated with NET formation. These analyses clarify how neutrophils drive CRC progression through inflammation, tissue remodeling, and immune suppression, refining understanding of the TME and its clinical implications.

Spatial transcriptomics complements scRNA-seq by adding spatial context to gene-expression data, revealing cell–cell interactions within native tissue architecture [22]. The integration of scRNA-seq data with spatial transcriptomics has also emerged as a powerful approach to map the TME, providing a comprehensive view of how cellular interactions and spatial organization influence CRC biology [23]. This innovative technique empowers researchers to visualize, in detail, the distribution of NETs and tumor-associated neutrophils (TANs) directly within tumor tissues. It also elucidates their spatial relationships with immune and tumor cells, thereby deepening insight into the TME [24]. Within the TME, NETs are associated with greater tumor aggressiveness and metastatic propensity, suggesting that these structures shield tumor cells from immune surveillance [25]. Accordingly, understanding the spatial dynamics of neutrophil activation and NET formation in CRC can inform the development of therapies that modulate immune responses and enhance antitumor immunity.

In this context, our study focuses on the exploration of NET-associated genes and their implications in colon cancer progression. We integrate bulk RNA-seq, single-cell RNA-seq (scRNA-seq), and spatial transcriptomics to delineate the molecular features of colon cancer samples. Leveraging public datasets from The Cancer Genome Atlas (TCGA) and the Gene Expression Omnibus (GEO), we systematically profile NET-related gene expression in colon cancer. This multimodal strategy provides a comprehensive view of the TME and enables identification of biomarker candidates associated with patient prognosis.

Materials and methods

Download and processing of conventional transcriptome data

All data used in this study were publicly available and freely accessible, primarily from the TCGA (https://portal.gdc.cancer.gov/) and the GEO (https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/geo/) databases. The genome-wide expression profile data in TPM format, clinical data, copy number variation (CNV) data, and single nucleotide variant (SNV) data predicted using the “VarScan2 Variant Aggregation and Masking” tool for colon adenocarcinoma (COAD) were downloaded from the TCGA database using the R package “TCGAbiolinks” (version 2.25.0) [26]. Expression profile data and clinical data from the GEO database were retrieved using the R package “GEOquery” (version 2.25.0). For this study, TCGA-COAD and GSE17537 datasets were included. The TCGA-COAD dataset (n = 524) comprises 483 tumor samples and 41 normal control samples, with 419 COAD tumor samples containing survival information used for constructing the prognostic model.

The external GEO validation cohort was selected according to the following criteria [1]: the dataset contained colon cancer tissue samples [2]; both gene expression data and survival information were publicly available [3]; the platform annotation was clear and the data were compatible with downstream preprocessing and validation analyses; and [4] the sample size and clinical annotation quality were sufficient for external prognostic validation. Datasets were excluded if they lacked survival data, included non-comparable sample types, had unclear platform annotations, or did not provide sufficient information for risk-model validation. Based on these criteria, GSE17537, sequenced using the GPL570 [HG-U133_Plus_2] Affymetrix Human Genome U133 Plus 2.0 Array platform, was selected as the external validation cohort. This dataset includes 55 colon cancer tissue samples, all of which have complete survival information, and was used to evaluate the generalizability of the prognostic model.

This study complies with the data access policies of each database and is primarily based on publicly available datasets, thus requiring no additional ethical review. The NETs-related gene set was obtained from published literature [27], and the full gene list is provided in Table S2. This literature-derived signature was used as a surrogate marker of NETs-related biological activity rather than a direct measurement of NET formation.

Download and processing of single-cell transcriptome sequencing data

The GEO database contains extensive single-cell sequencing data. In this study, the colon cancer single-cell RNA sequencing dataset GSE132257 was obtained from GEO. This dataset includes single-cell sequencing data for five colon cancer tumor samples (SMC13T.A1, SMC13T.A1.F, SMC171.T.BEL, SMC171.T.SGI, SMC171.T. SING) and five normal tissue samples (SMC13N.A1, SMC13N.A1.F, SMC171.N.BEL, SMC171.N.SGI, SMC171.N.SING). The single-cell transcriptome data were imported using the “Seurat” package (version 4.2.0) in the R [28].

Low-quality cells and genes were filtered according to the following criteria: genes detected in fewer than five cells were excluded; cells with fewer than 200 or more than 2,000 expressed genes were removed; cells with UMI counts greater than 7,500 were excluded; and cells with mitochondrial gene proportions exceeding 20% were discarded. After quality control, the data were normalized using the NormalizeData function in Seurat. Highly variable genes were then identified based on the relationship between average expression and dispersion.

Principal component analysis (PCA) was subsequently performed, and significant principal components (PCs) were used for graph-based clustering. Batch effects across samples were corrected using the Harmony method. Cell clustering was conducted using the FindClusters function, which applies a shared nearest neighbor (SNN) modularity optimization algorithm. Using the top 20 PCs and a resolution of 0.5, 15 clusters were identified. Dimensionality reduction for visualization was then performed with the RunTSNE function, and cell clusters were displayed according to t-SNE-1 and t-SNE-2 coordinates.

To identify differentially expressed genes (DEGs) across cell populations, the FindAllMarkers function was applied to the normalized expression matrix using Seurat default parameters. Cell clusters were then annotated based on established cell-type-specific marker genes (Table S1) [29], and the proportions of different cell types were subsequently quantified and compared.

Identification of malignant cells by copy number variation analysis

To identify malignant epithelial cells within tumor tissues, we applied InferCNV (version 1.10.1) to epithelial-cell subsets and inferred large-scale CNV patterns by comparing tumor-derived epithelial cells with reference epithelial cells from normal tissues. Clusters with relatively higher CNV scores were considered putative malignant epithelial-cell populations. To further support this annotation, we additionally examined the expression of established colorectal epithelial/tumor-associated markers, including EPCAM and KRT20, within the high-CNV clusters. This combined strategy was used to strengthen malignant-cell annotation, although it does not fully replace orthogonal experimental validation.

Identification and analysis of NETs activity in malignant cells

To further analyze malignant cell populations, we performed refined reclustering analysis at a resolution of 0.2, constructing clusters based on 10 principal components (PCs) and ultimately identifying 10 cell clusters. To characterize NETs-related transcriptional states in these malignant cells, we used a literature-derived NETs-related gene set (Table S2) [27]. Using the R package “AUCell” (version 1.16.0), we calculated an AUCell score for each malignant cell based on this gene set. Malignant cells with relatively higher AUCell scores were defined as NETs-high malignant cells, whereas those with lower scores were defined as NETs-low malignant cells. It should be noted that the AUCell score was used to reflect a NETs-associated transcriptional program rather than to directly measure NET formation or experimentally confirmed NETosis in epithelial malignant cells.

To further investigate the molecular differences between the two categories of malignant cells, we conducted differential gene expression analysis using the “FindAllMarkers” function. With the thresholds set at p < 0.05 and |log2 fold change| >0.25, a set of significantly differentially expressed genes was identified. Gene Ontology (GO) analysis was performed across three domains—Biological Process (BP), Molecular Function (MF), and Cellular Component (CC)—to clarify the functional roles of these genes in malignant cells [30]. In parallel, Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) pathway analysis identified significant alterations in metabolism-related pathways associated with the differentially expressed genes, providing further insight into metabolic reprogramming and potential therapeutic targets in malignant cells [31].

Identification and analysis of NETs subtypes

To identify potential molecular subtypes based on NETs-related gene expression in colon cancer, unsupervised consensus clustering was performed using the ConsensusClusterPlus R package (version 1.58.0). This approach enabled classification of TCGA-COAD samples according to the expression patterns of a predefined set of NET-related genes. By optimizing the clustering parameters, the most stable clustering solution was selected, and colon cancer samples were stratified into distinct NET-related subtypes.

To further characterize the biological differences between NETs subtypes, differential expression analysis was conducted using the limma package (version 3.50.3). Gene expression profiles were compared across the identified subtypes to detect significantly differentially expressed genes, which were considered potential molecular markers of each NET-related subtype. To further explore the biological functions and pathways associated with these genes, Gene Ontology (GO) enrichment analysis was performed, providing insight into the molecular mechanisms underlying the NET-related subtypes.

Deconvolution of cell types

To infer cell-type composition in bulk transcriptomic samples using single-cell data, single-sample Gene Set Enrichment Analysis (ssGSEA) was applied.ssGSEA estimates the relative enrichment of predefined gene sets within individual samples by calculating an enrichment score based on the coordinated expression of genes in each set [32]. In this study, the top 20 differentially expressed genes for each cell type as signature gene sets. ssGSEA was then performed on TCGA-COAD bulk transcriptomic data to generate enrichment scores representing the relative abundance of different cell types in each sample. By comparing cell-type scores across NET-related subtypes, differences in cellular composition among samples were assessed. Correlation coefficients and corresponding p values between cell types were subsequently calculated, and the results were visualized using the ggcorrplot package (version 0.1.4.999) to generate a correlation heatmap.

Identification of prognosis-associated cell types

To identify cell types significantly associated with patient prognosis, overall survival (OS) time, survival status, and cell abundance scores were integrated for downstream analysis. A univariate Cox proportional hazards model was applied to statistically analyze the abundance scores of each cell type. By calculating the Cox regression coefficient, hazard ratio (HR), and its 95% confidence interval (CI) for each cell type, we quantified the impact of different cell types on patient prognosis. During statistical testing, a significance threshold of p < 0.05 was set, indicating that a specific cell type’s abundance was statistically significantly associated with patient prognosis.

Construction and validation of the prognostic model

To develop a prognostic model while minimizing information leakage, the 419 TCGA-COAD tumor samples with overall survival information were first randomly divided into an internal training cohort (70%, n = 290) and an internal validation cohort (30%, n = 129). Based on the 289 differentially expressed genes identified from the preceding analyses, all feature-selection procedures for prognostic modeling were performed exclusively in the training cohort. Specifically, univariate Cox regression analysis was applied to these 289 candidate genes in the training cohort to evaluate their associations with overall survival (OS), and genes with p ≤ 0.05 were retained for subsequent analysis. The least absolute shrinkage and selection operator (LASSO) Cox regression model was then implemented using the R package “glmnet” (version 3.50.0) [33] to further refine the candidate genes and construct the prognostic model. The median risk score in the training cohort was used as the cutoff value to stratify patients into low-risk and high-risk groups, and the same cutoff was subsequently applied to the internal validation cohort and the external validation cohort. Kaplan-Meier (KM) survival analysis was performed to evaluate prognostic differences between the two groups, and statistical significance was assessed using the log-rank test. To evaluate the predictive performance of the model, time-dependent receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curves were generated, and the area under the curve (AUC) was calculated. In addition, decision curve analysis and calibration curves were used to further assess the clinical utility and predictive consistency of the model. Finally, the prognostic model was further evaluated in an external validation cohort (GSE17537) to examine its generalizability in colon cancer patients. Given the relatively limited sample size and the use of a single external dataset, the validation results were interpreted cautiously.

Expression analysis of prognostic genes

A comprehensive analysis was performed for the prognostic genes that form the core of the NET-related prognostic model. Differences in the expression patterns of these genes were further examined across NET-related subtypes, colon cancer tissues, and normal tissues. Particular attention was given to the correlations between prognostic genes and specific cell types, especially their associations with NET-low malignant cells. To further validate and extend these findings, single-gene Gene Set Enrichment Analysis (GSEA) was conducted for each prognostic gene. This analysis provided additional insight into the biological pathways and processes associated with individual genes, as well as their potential contributions to patient prognosis. Finally, to explore their potential clinical relevance, the Human Protein Atlas (HPA) database was queried to assess the protein expression levels of these prognostic genes in colon cancer tissues.

eQTL–GWAS colocalization analysis of TUBB2A

We investigated whether genetic regulation of Tubulin Beta 2A (TUBB2A) expression colocalizes with colorectal-cancer overall-survival (OS) and cancer-specific survival (CSS) using data from the SUMMER database [34]. Colocalization was assessed with the coloc R package and evidence for a shared causal variant was defined as PP.H4 > 0.70. As a complementary check, we regressed GWAS effect sizes (β) on eQTL β after LD pruning (r2 < 0.8) and reported Pearson’s r with 95% CI. For visualization, we plotted eQTL β (x-axis) versus GWAS β (y-axis); each point is a SNP with 95% CIs, the lead cis-eQTL is highlighted, and a dashed linear fit is overlaid. Plots were generated in R (4.3.1) with ggplot2.

Download and processing of spatial transcriptomics sequencing data

In this study, we obtained the spatial transcriptomics dataset GSE225857 for colon cancer patients from the GEO database. This dataset includes four primary tumor tissue samples, and we selected the sample GSM7058757 for analysis. According to the dataset annotation, GSM7058757 was generated using the 10x Genomics Visium spatial transcriptomics platform. The R package “Seurat” (version 4.0.5) was used to perform data normalization, dimensionality reduction, clustering, and UMAP visualization. The dataset was first normalized using the “SCTransform” function, followed by principal component analysis (PCA) and clustering. Based on the unsupervised clustering results, the sample was divided into distinct spatial clusters for downstream analysis.

Mapping of cell types in spatial transcriptomics data

To infer the distribution of cell-type signatures across spatial regions, we used the Multimodal Intersection Analysis (MIA) method to integrate spatial transcriptomics and single-cell RNA-sequencing data [35]. Specifically, marker genes for each spatial cluster and each cell type were first identified, and the overlap between these marker sets was then evaluated using a hypergeometric test to estimate enrichment. This strategy allowed us to infer the relative regional enrichment of specific cell-type signatures within spatial domains. Because the 10x Visium platform captures transcripts from multiple cells within each spot, the MIA results were interpreted as region-level enrichment patterns rather than true single-cell localization.

Cell-cell communication analysis

Cell–cell communication analysis was performed to evaluate ligand–receptor interactions among different cell types and to identify the signaling pathways involved in intercellular communication [36]. To characterize these interactions, we used CellChat to infer both incoming and outgoing communication patterns for each cell type, quantify signaling pathway activity, and estimate the information flow or communication probability associated with each pathway [37]. In this study, intercellular communication analysis was conducted on the single-cell dataset GSE132257 using the CellChat R package (version 1.1.3). This approach enabled identification of the communication strength of different signaling pathways, and representative pathways were selected for further visualization. All analyses were performed using the default parameters of the software, with p ≤ 0.05 considered statistically significant. To control for multiple testing, p values were adjusted using the Benjamini–Hochberg (BH) method, thereby improving the robustness of the results.

Drug sensitivity analysis

Based on half-maximal inhibitory concentration (IC50) data and corresponding gene expression data downloaded from the Genomics of Drug Sensitivity in Cancer database (GDSC, https://www.cancerrxgene.org/) [38], we predicted the potential drug sensitivity of colon cancer patients using the R package oncoPredict (version 0.2) [39]. The Kruskal–Wallis test was used to compare differences in predicted drug response among NETs subtypes, and the Wilcoxon rank-sum test was used to evaluate differences between high- and low-expression groups of prognostic genes. In addition, Spearman correlation analysis was performed to assess the continuous association between prognostic-gene expression and predicted drug response values for the candidate drugs.

Molecular docking analysis

Molecular docking was performed to evaluate the binding modes of the five targeted agents (Axitinib, Olaparib, Niraparib, Buparlisib, and Savolitinib) with their corresponding protein structures. Docking analysis was conducted using CB-Dock, an online platform that automatically identifies potential binding cavities and performs blind docking based on the AutoDock Vina algorithm. Protein structures were obtained from the Protein Data Bank, and ligand structures were downloaded from PubChem. All proteins and ligands were processed using the default preparation steps in CB-Dock. The top-ranked docking pose with the lowest predicted binding energy was selected for visualization and interaction analysis using PyMOL.

Immune checkpoint-related analysis

A total of 79 immune checkpoint genes were collected from published studies (Table S3) [40], among which 68 were available in the expression dataset (Table S4). Correlation analyses were then performed between prognostic genes and immune checkpoint genes to explore potential regulatory relationships and synergistic interactions, thereby providing further insight into the mechanisms underlying tumor immune evasion. In addition, to characterize immune checkpoint expression under different immune states, the expression patterns of immune checkpoint genes were compared across NET-related subtypes.

Immunotherapy response analysis

To systematically evaluate differential patient responses to immunotherapy, Tumor Immune Dysfunction and Exclusion analysis (TIDE)analysis was performed [41]. TIDE evaluates tumor immune dysfunction and T-cell exclusion within the tumor microenvironment. For each patient, three scores were calculated [1]: a dysfunction score, reflecting the degree of immune dysfunction within the tumor microenvironment [2]; an exclusion score, indicating the extent to which immune cells are prevented from infiltrating the tumor; and [3] an overall TIDE score, integrating both dysfunction and exclusion to characterize the immune state. To further investigate the relationships among immune features, prognosis, and predicted immunotherapy response, dysfunction, exclusion, and TIDE scores were compared between high-risk and low-risk patient groups, with the aim of identifying differences in the immune microenvironment across risk categories.

Pan-cancer analysis

To determine the broader role of prognostic genes, we performed a pan-cancer analysis to examine their expression patterns, prognostic associations, and immune infiltration relationships across multiple TCGA cancer types. Using the R package TCGAbiolinks (version 2.25.0), we downloaded expression and clinical data for 33 cancer types from the TCGA database. Differential expression of prognostic genes between tumor and normal samples within each cancer type was assessed using the Wilcoxon test. Univariate Cox regression analysis was performed to evaluate the prognostic relevance of prognostic gene expression in individual cancer types. Immune infiltration scores for all tumor samples were computed using the ssGSEA algorithm, and their correlations with prognostic gene expression levels were assessed. Because this pan-cancer analysis involved multiple TCGA projects, the results were interpreted as descriptive and exploratory rather than as strictly comparable cross-project quantitative estimates. Statistical analysis

All statistical analyses were conducted using R software (version 4.1.2). Spearman’s correlation test was used to evaluate the correlation between two parameters. Wilcoxon rank-sum test was used for comparisons between two groups. Kruskal-Wallis test was used to compare differences among three or more groups. A two-sided p-value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

Single-cell sequencing reveals the heterogeneity of colon cancer cell populations

To characterize the cellular composition of colon cancer, we analyzed the single-cell RNA sequencing dataset GSE132257. After quality control evaluation, a total of 23,268 cells were identified from the single-cell transcriptome data. We then applied a clustering algorithm, which divided these cells into 15 distinct clusters (Fig. 2A). By analyzing the gene expression characteristics of each cluster and integrating known cell-type-specific biomarkers, we successfully annotated the cell types (Fig. 2B–C). Based on this analysis, we identified six major cell types: Epithelial cells, T cells, B cells, Myeloid cells, Mast cells, and Stromal cells. Furthermore, we compared the relative proportions of these major cell types between colon cancer and normal tissues, revealing significant heterogeneity in cell composition between the two groups (Fig. 2D, Table S5).

Identification of malignant cells and NETs activity

Since colon cancer originates from epithelial cells, we analyzed CNVs in epithelial cells from the single-cell gene expression data to distinguish malignant and non-malignant cells. We performed reclustering analysis of epithelial cells, selecting Cluster 4, 7, and 8 as reference cells (Fig. 3A–B). Using an unsupervised clustering technique, we classified cells into high CNV and low CNV groups and found that Cluster 0, 1, 2, 3, 5, 6, 9, and 10 exhibited significantly higher CNV scores compared to other clusters (Fig. 3C), suggesting that these clusters represented putative malignant epithelial cells. To further validate this annotation, we examined the expression of established colorectal epithelial/tumor-associated markers within these high-CNV clusters. The results showed that epithelial cell adhesion molecule (EPCAM) and keratin 20 (KRT20) were expressed in the high-CNV cell populations, supporting their epithelial lineage and tumor-related identity. In addition, cluster of differentiation 44 (CD44), aldehyde dehydrogenase 1 family member a1 (ALDH1A1), marker of proliferation ki-67 (MKI67), and topoisomerase (dna) II alpha (TOP2A) were also detected, further supporting their malignant epithelial features (Fig. 3D). Therefore, these clusters were annotated as putative malignant epithelial cells supported by both CNV patterns and marker-gene expression, rather than being defined solely on the basis of CNV scores.

Next, we conducted reclustering analysis of malignant cells (Fig. 3E). Among the malignant clusters, seurat_cluster6 exhibited the highest AUCell score for the literature-derived NETs-related gene set (Fig. 3F) and was therefore defined as the NETs-high malignant cell cluster, whereas the remaining malignant clusters were grouped as NETs-low malignant cells. To further investigate molecular differences between NETs-high malignant cells and other malignant cells, we identified 1,895 DEGs, which showed statistically significant differences between the two groups (Fig. 3G, p < 0.05, |Log2 fold change| >0.25, Table S6). To explore the biological functions associated with these DEGs in NETs-high malignant cells, we performed GO term enrichment analysis (Fig. 3H, Table S7) and KEGG pathway enrichment analysis (Fig. 3I, Table S8). The enriched GO terms included electron transport chain, cellular respiration, and aerobic respiration in the biological process category; mitochondrial inner membrane, mitochondrial protein-containing complex, and respirasome in the cellular component category; and electron transfer activity, oxidoreduction-driven active transmembrane transporter activity, and ribonucleoprotein complex binding in the molecular function category. The KEGG analysis revealed significant enrichment in pathways such as Prion disease, Parkinson disease, and Chemical carcinogenesis–reactive oxygen species. These findings suggest that NETs-high malignant cells exhibit distinct metabolic and mitochondrial characteristics, which may contribute to tumor progression and immune evasion.

Identification of NETs subtypes

Based on bulk transcriptome data from 419 colon cancer patients, we classified colon cancer into two NETs-related subtypes according to the expression profiles of NETs-associated genes: Cluster 1 (n = 200) and Cluster 2 (n = 219) (Fig. 4A–B). To further investigate the molecular differences between these subtypes, we conducted a DEG analysis using the criteria of p < 0.05 and |log2 fold change| >0.5. After excluding 6 non-comparative genes (NO), we identified 3,565 DEGs with significantly different expression levels between the subtypes (Fig. 4C–D, Table S20).

KEGG pathway enrichment analysis indicated that these DEGs were significantly enriched in pathways related to hematopoietic cell lineage, chemokine signaling pathway, and cell adhesion molecules (Fig. 4F, Table S22). These results suggest that the two NETs-related subtypes differ not only in transcriptional profiles, but also in immune-cell regulation, inflammatory signaling, and extracellular-matrix-related programs, implying potentially distinct microenvironmental states and biological behaviors.

Construction of the prognostic model

By intersecting three sets of DEGs [1]: DEGs between NETs-high malignant cells and other cells [2], DEGs between colon cancer and normal controls (Table S13), and [3] DEGs between colon cancer NETs subtypes, we identified a total of 289 overlapping genes (Table S14). These genes were considered NETs-related candidate genes associated with malignant transformation in colon cancer. To construct the prognostic model under a clearer training-validation framework, the 419 TCGA colon cancer samples with survival information were first randomly divided into a training cohort (n = 290) and a validation cohort (n = 129) at a 7:3 ratio. Based on the 289 candidate genes, univariate Cox regression analysis was performed exclusively in the training cohort to screen for genes significantly associated with overall survival, followed by LASSO regression analysis to remove redundant variables. As a result, four genes closely associated with colon cancer prognosis were retained in the final model: arrestin domain containing 1(ARRDC1), TUBB2A, dual specificity phosphatase 5 (DUSP5), and solute carrier family 2 member 3 (SLC2A3) Figure 6A-B). Additionally, the GSE17537 dataset (n = 55) was used as an external validation cohort. The coefficients of these four genes were incorporated into the following risk score formula: Risk Score = 0.414 × Expression (ARRDC1) + 0.168 × Expression (TUBB2A) + 0.08 × Expression (DUSP5) + 0.157 × Expression (SLC2A3). The detailed coefficients of these four genes indicated in Table S15.

Using the median risk score in the training cohort as the cutoff value, patients were classified into high-risk and low-risk groups, and the same cutoff was subsequently applied to the validation cohort and the external validation cohort. Kaplan-Meier survival analysis showed that patients in the high-risk group had significantly worse overall survival than those in the low-risk group (p < 0.05, Fig. 6C–E). To further evaluate the predictive performance of the model, time-dependent ROC curves were generated. In the training cohort, the AUC values were 0.629, 0.627, and 0.649 at 1, 3, and 5 years, respectively. In the validation cohort, the corresponding AUC values were 0.768, 0.758, and 0.685 (Fig. 6F–H). Overall, these results indicate that the four-gene signature has modest discriminative ability for prognostic stratification in colon cancer. Notably, the internal validation cohort showed somewhat higher AUC values than the training cohort, which may reflect random variation introduced by sample splitting, limited cohort size, or underlying heterogeneity in clinical characteristics between the two subsets, rather than a stable improvement in model performance. In addition, because external validation was performed in only one GEO cohort with a relatively limited sample size, the generalizability of the model remains preliminary. Therefore, the predictive performance of this model should be interpreted cautiously and requires further validation in larger and more independent cohorts.

Immune-related analysis

To explore the potential regulatory relationship between prognostic genes and immune checkpoints, we conducted a correlation analysis of four prognostic genes and 68 immune checkpoints. The results revealed that several prognostic genes, including TUBB2A, DUSP5, and SLC2A3, showed significant positive correlations with a wide range of immune-related molecules, such as members of the tumor necrosis factor receptor family, cell surface membrane proteins, and molecules involved in regulating immune responses. This suggests that these genes may positively regulate immune responses. In contrast, ARRDC1 exhibited negative correlations with most immune checkpoints, implying that they may suppress immune activity (Figure S6A). Furthermore, we observed significant differential expression of the 68 immune checkpoints across NETs subtypes (Figure S6B, Figure S7–S8). When evaluating immune therapy response, we calculated the Dysfunction score, Exclusion score, and integrated TIDE score across NETs subtypes and high/low-risk groups (Table S31), all of which revealed significant group differences (Figure S6C). These results suggest that different risk groups may exhibit distinct immune dysfunction and exclusion patterns, which could be associated with heterogeneous immune microenvironment states. However, because these findings are based solely on TIDE analysis, they should be interpreted as exploratory predictions rather than direct evidence of clinical response to immunotherapy. Further validation in independent cohorts with real immunotherapy outcome data is required.

Discussion

In the present study, we integrated bulk transcriptomic data, single-cell RNA sequencing, and spatial transcriptomics to characterize NETs-related heterogeneity in colon adenocarcinoma and to explore its potential biological and clinical implications. By combining these complementary datasets, we identified a NETs-high malignant-cell state with distinct metabolic features, defined two NETs-related molecular subtypes of colon cancer, and developed a four-gene prognostic signature consisting of ARRDC1, TUBB2A, DUSP5, and SLC2A3. Collectively, these findings suggest that NETs-related transcriptional programs may be associated with coordinated remodeling of tumor metabolism, immune interactions, and prognostic heterogeneity in colon cancer.

A major finding of this study is the identification of a NETs-high malignant-cell population with prominent metabolic and mitochondrial characteristics. Compared with other malignant-cell states, NETs-high malignant cells showed enrichment of pathways related to cellular respiration, oxidative phosphorylation, mitochondrial inner-membrane function, and reactive oxygen species-associated processes. These observations suggest that NETs-related malignant states may be supported not only by inflammatory signaling but also by metabolic reprogramming. In colon cancer, such adaptation may help malignant cells survive under microenvironmental stress, maintain proliferative activity, and contribute to immune evasion. Increasing evidence indicates that metabolic rewiring is a key feature of tumor progression and therapeutic resistance, particularly in the context of inflammatory and immunosuppressive microenvironments [42, 43]. Our findings therefore support a model in which NETs-associated biology is coupled to a metabolically active and potentially more aggressive malignant-cell phenotype.

The identification of two distinct NETs-related subtypes further underscores the biological heterogeneity of colon cancer. Differential-expression and enrichment analyses indicated that these subtypes differed not only in transcriptional profiles but also in leukocyte activation, antigen binding, chemokine signaling, hematopoietic-cell-lineage programs, and cell-adhesion-related pathways. These findings suggest that NETs-related subtype heterogeneity may reflect broader differences in immune regulation, inflammatory communication, and stromal interaction. Leukocyte activation is closely associated with the tumor immune microenvironment, and NETs, as part of the inflammatory response, may contribute to tumor growth, dissemination, and immune escape by modulating leukocyte activity within the tumor microenvironment [44–49]. In addition, the enrichment of hematopoietic cell lineage and chemokine signaling pathways supports the possibility that the two subtypes differ in immune-cell composition, recruitment, and intercellular communication [50]. The enrichment of cell-adhesion molecules also suggests potential differences in cell-cell interaction, extracellular matrix remodeling, and invasive behavior, which may influence immune exclusion, metastatic potential, and treatment response [51]. Taken together, these observations indicate that NETs-related subtype heterogeneity is not limited to transcriptomic variation alone, but may also involve coordinated remodeling of malignant cells and the surrounding microenvironment.

Our single-cell analysis also provided a refined view of the cellular architecture of the colon cancer microenvironment. T cells, B cells, and myeloid cells emerged as major components of the tumor immune landscape, highlighting the complexity of immune involvement in colon cancer. T cells, particularly cytotoxic CD8+ T cells, are central mediators of anti-tumor immunity, but their functional status may be impaired by immune checkpoint activation and chronic antigen exposure, ultimately leading to T-cell exhaustion [52–55]. B cells may exert both anti-tumor and pro-tumor functions depending on their activation state and cytokine milieu [56, 57]. Myeloid cells, including macrophages and dendritic cells, are also critical regulators of the tumor microenvironment, and tumor-associated macrophages often promote tumor growth, metastasis, and immune suppression [17, 58–60]. In our study, the coexistence of malignant-cell heterogeneity with changes in immune-cell composition supports the concept that NETs-associated programs may influence, or be influenced by, a dynamic and potentially immunosuppressive tumor microenvironment. This interpretation is consistent with the increasingly recognized role of NETs in shaping inflammatory and immune-excluded tumor states, although the current study does not establish direct causality.

Although InferCNV is a widely used approach for identifying malignant epithelial cells in scRNA-seq data, CNV-based inference alone may misclassify highly proliferative normal epithelial cells or fail to capture tumor cells with near-diploid genomes. In this study, malignant epithelial-cell annotation was therefore supported not only by elevated CNV patterns but also by the expression of established epithelial/tumor-associated markers, including EPCAM and KRT20. In addition, markers related to tumor stemness and proliferation, such as CD44, ALDH1A1, MKI67, and TOP2A, provided further supportive evidence for the malignant epithelial features of these high-CNV clusters. Nevertheless, these results should be interpreted as supportive evidence for malignant-cell annotation rather than definitive proof of malignant identity.

An additional strength of this work is the integration of cell–cell communication analysis with spatial transcriptomic information. Our CellChat results, together with ligand–receptor expression patterns and regional spatial enrichment, suggested that MIF-related signaling may represent a candidate communication axis between NETs-high malignant cells and surrounding immune cells. In particular, NETs-high malignant cells appeared to be major putative senders of MIF-related signals, whereas B cells, myeloid cells, mast cells, and T cells were among the main predicted recipients. These observations are broadly consistent with previous studies implicating MIF in tumor-associated immunoregulation [61–63].

The expression patterns of MIF and its receptors, including CD74, CXCR4, and CD44, were also compatible with the predicted communication pattern, raising the possibility that MIF-mediated signaling contributes to immune microenvironment remodeling in colon cancer. Given that tumor immune escape is a critical process in tumor progression [64, 65], such a communication axis may represent one mechanism through which malignant cells shape local immune dysfunction.

At the same time, the interpretation of the MIF axis requires caution. The present evidence is based on computational inference and transcriptomic patterns rather than on direct functional validation. Therefore, the proposed role of MIF signaling in immune escape or immune exclusion should be regarded as a hypothesis generated from integrated multi-omics analyses rather than as a confirmed mechanism. In addition, receptor heterogeneity may lead to distinct downstream consequences. For example, CD74, a high-affinity receptor for MIF, has been implicated in immunosuppressive signaling through NF-κB-related pathways [66, 67], whereas co-expression of CXCR4 suggests a possible interaction with chemokine programs relevant to immune-cell trafficking and dysfunction [68]. CD44, which is also involved in extracellular matrix interactions, may further influence immune-cell positioning and stromal remodeling within the tumor microenvironment [69, 70]. Although the spatial distribution pattern observed in this study is compatible with an immune-excluded phenotype [71], this interpretation remains inferential. Moreover, because the spatial transcriptomics data were generated using the 10x Genomics Visium platform, which captures transcripts at spot-level rather than true single-cell resolution, the MIA-based mapping results should be interpreted as regional enrichment patterns of cell-type signatures rather than precise single-cell colocalization. Future studies will be needed to validate the MIF–CD74/CXCR4/CD44 axis using co-culture systems, perturbation assays, cytokine or protein quantification, and higher-resolution spatial profiling.

The prognostic model developed in this study provides another clinically relevant dimension of the NETs-related framework. The four-gene signature derived from overlapping NETs-related candidate genes showed statistically significant survival stratification ability across the TCGA training cohort, the internal validation cohort, and an external GEO cohort. These findings suggest that the signature may have value as a candidate tool for risk stratification in colon cancer. Gene-expression-based models have shown promise in prognostic assessment in colon cancer and other malignancies [72], and our results are generally in line with this broader literature. However, the predictive performance of the current model should be considered moderate rather than definitive. We used the median risk score in the training cohort as a prespecified cutoff because it is simple, reproducible, and less prone to overfitting than data-driven optimization of the cutpoint. The somewhat higher AUC values in the internal validation cohort than in the training cohort should be interpreted cautiously, as they may reflect random variation related to sample splitting, limited cohort size, and clinical heterogeneity between subsets rather than a stable improvement in model performance. In addition, because external validation was performed in only one GEO cohort with a relatively limited sample size, the generalizability of the model remains preliminary. Therefore, this four-gene model should currently be viewed as a hypothesis-generating prognostic framework rather than a mature clinical prediction tool.

Importantly, the biological interpretation of the high-risk and low-risk groups should also be considered in the context of NETs-related processes. Because the four-gene prognostic signature was derived from the intersection of differentially expressed genes associated with NETs-high malignant cells, tumor-normal differences, and NETs-related molecular subtypes, the resulting risk stratification likely reflects, at least in part, a NETs-associated biological background. In particular, the enriched pathways identified in NETs-high malignant cells and NETs-related subtypes, including oxidative stress, mitochondrial metabolism, leukocyte activation, chemokine signaling, and cell adhesion, are broadly consistent with inflammatory and immune-remodeling processes linked to NETosis. Therefore, the high-risk group may represent a tumor state more closely associated with NETs-related metabolic and immune dysregulation, although this interpretation remains inferential and requires further validation.

From a biological perspective, the four genes in the prognostic signature may reflect complementary aspects of NETs-related tumor biology. ARRDC1, which has been implicated in membrane trafficking, extracellular vesicle biogenesis, and inflammatory signaling, may be involved in regulating tumor-associated communication and stress-responsive signaling [73–77]. TUBB2A, a β-tubulin family member involved in microtubule dynamics, may reflect cytoskeletal remodeling, tumor-cell plasticity, and immune-associated phenotypes; previous studies have suggested that TUBB2A may be related to impaired T-cell recruitment and poor outcomes in cancer [78]. DUSP5, a dual-specificity phosphatase regulating MAPK/ERK signaling, may act as a signaling node linking malignant-cell adaptation to inflammatory and proliferative cues [79]. SLC2A3, which encodes the high-affinity glucose transporter GLUT3, may connect the signature to metabolic reprogramming and glucose competition within an inflammatory tumor niche, consistent with prior evidence linking SLC2A3 to colorectal cancer progression, EMT-related features, and immune-associated signatures [80, 81]. Among these genes, TUBB2A and SLC2A3 appear particularly relevant to the central theme of this study because they link NETs-related malignant states to cytoskeletal dynamics, immune-associated features, and altered glucose metabolism. Nevertheless, these interpretations remain inferential and should not be regarded as direct functional conclusions.

The colocalization analysis of TUBB2A adds an additional layer of support to its potential relevance in colon cancer. The observed negative relationship between TUBB2A eQTL effects and colorectal cancer survival-related GWAS signals suggests that genetic regulation of TUBB2A expression may be linked to adverse clinical outcomes. Although this finding does not prove causality, it strengthens the rationale for prioritizing TUBB2A in future mechanistic studies. More broadly, the integration of transcriptomic, prognostic, and genetic evidence may help identify candidate biomarkers with greater biological plausibility than expression-based associations alone.

Our exploratory drug-sensitivity and molecular-docking analyses provide preliminary clues regarding the potential therapeutic relevance of NETs-related tumor states, but these findings should be interpreted conservatively. The observed associations between prognostic-gene expression and predicted drug response may reflect broader differences in cellular state, metabolic adaptation, signaling regulation, or tumor–microenvironment interactions rather than direct drug-sensitivity mechanisms. In line with previous computational drug-discovery studies, such in silico prediction frameworks can be useful for prioritizing candidate compounds and target relationships, but they should be regarded as hypothesis-generating rather than functionally definitive [82, 83]. Similarly, the docking results only provide exploratory structural support for possible ligand–target interactions. Because more rigorous validation procedures, such as redocking, RMSD-based evaluation, and molecular dynamics simulation, were not performed, these findings should not be interpreted as evidence of stable binding or pharmacological efficacy [82].

The correlation patterns between the prognostic genes and immune checkpoint molecules suggest possible links between these genes and the immune microenvironment, but they should not be overinterpreted as direct regulatory relationships. Likewise, the TIDE analysis in this study provides only an indirect computational estimate of potential immunotherapy responsiveness based on transcriptomic features related to immune dysfunction and exclusion. Therefore, the observed differences between risk groups should not be interpreted as direct evidence of clinical benefit from immune checkpoint blockade. Instead, these findings should be regarded as hypothesis-generating and require validation in independent cohorts with actual immunotherapy response data. More broadly, predictive modeling and integrative in silico frameworks have increasingly been used to generate biological hypotheses from complex multi-omics data, although such predictions still require careful interpretation and downstream validation [84].

To further explore the broader relevance of the above prognostic genes, we performed a pan-cancer analysis. The results suggested that the four prognostic genes exhibited distinct expression patterns, prognostic associations, and immune-related correlations across different tumor contexts. However, because cross-project TCGA comparisons may be influenced by technical differences, sample composition, and other sources of heterogeneity, these findings should be interpreted as exploratory observations rather than definitive cross-cancer biological conclusions.

Limitations

Several limitations of this study should be acknowledged. First, the NETs-related score used in malignant cells was derived directly from a literature-based gene set and therefore reflects a NETs-associated transcriptional program rather than direct measurement of NET formation or NETosis. This gene set was used as a proxy for NETs-related transcriptional activity, but it has not been experimentally validated in epithelial malignant cells. Second, malignant epithelial-cell annotation relied on inferred CNV patterns together with tumor-associated marker expression, which strengthens the inference but does not replace orthogonal experimental validation. Third, the single-cell analysis was based on a single cohort, and additional independent scRNA-seq datasets would further strengthen the robustness of the cell-state annotations. Fourth, the spatial transcriptomic analyses were based on spot-level resolution and should be interpreted as regional enrichment rather than precise single-cell localization. Fifth, the prognostic model was validated in only one external cohort with a relatively limited sample size, and its predictive accuracy remains moderate. Finally, the immune-response, drug-sensitivity, docking, and pan-cancer analyses are computational and exploratory, and none of these findings should be overinterpreted as direct evidence of therapeutic response or mechanism.

Overall, our findings support the concept that NETs-related biology is linked to malignant-cell heterogeneity, metabolic rewiring, immune communication, and prognostic diversity in colon cancer. These results provide a basis for further investigation into how NETs-associated programs may contribute to tumor progression and how they might be leveraged for biomarker development and translational research.

Conclusion

In conclusion, this study provides an integrated multi-omics framework for understanding the role of NETs-related biology in colon cancer. By combining bulk transcriptomics, single-cell RNA sequencing, and spatial transcriptomics, we identified a NETs-high malignant-cell state with distinct metabolic features, defined two NETs-related molecular subtypes, and developed a four-gene prognostic signature consisting of ARRDC1, TUBB2A, DUSP5, and SLC2A3. These findings suggest that NETs-associated transcriptional programs may be linked to metabolic rewiring, immune microenvironment remodeling, and prognostic heterogeneity in colon cancer. In addition, the predicted MIF-related communication axis and the four-gene signature may represent candidate features of potential biological and translational relevance. Nevertheless, these findings remain primarily based on computational and transcriptomic analyses and require further validation in larger independent cohorts and dedicated functional experiments. Overall, this study provides a foundation for future investigation of NETs-related mechanisms and biomarker development in colon cancer.
