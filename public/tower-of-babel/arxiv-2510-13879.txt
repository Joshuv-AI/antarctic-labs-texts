Catch Your Breath: Adaptive Computation for Self-Paced Sequence Production
Alexandre Galashov, Matt Jones, Rosemary Ke, Yuan Cao, Vaishnavh Nagarajan, Michael C. Mozer

Abstract
We explore a class of supervised training objectives that allow a language model to dynamically and autonomously scale the number of compute steps used for each input token. For any token, the model can request additional compute steps by emitting a <don’t know> output. If the model is granted a delay, a specialized <pause> token is inserted at the next input step, providing the model with additional compute resources to generate an output. The model can request multiple pauses. To train the model to use <don’t know> outputs judiciously and to calibrate its uncertainty, we frame the selection of each output token as a sequential-decision problem with a time cost. We refer to the class of methods as Catch Your Breath losses and we study three methods in this class: CYB-AP frames the model’s task as anytime prediction, where an output may be required at any step and accuracy is discounted over time; CYB-VA is a variational approach that aims to maximize prediction accuracy subject to a specified distribution over stopping times; and CYB-DP imposes a penalty based on a computational budget. Through fine-tuning experiments, we determine a specific form of the loss that performs best. To cast the performance improvement in intuitive terms, a baseline (no pause) model needs 3 × \times as much training data to match the CYB loss, and a model with pauses and a cross-entropy loss requires 2 × \times as much data. We find that the CYB model requests additional steps when doing so improves accuracy, and the model adapts its processing time to token-level complexity and context. For example, it often pauses after plural nouns like patients and challenges but never pauses after the first token of contracted words like wasn and didn , and it shows high variability for ambiguous tokens like won , which could function as either a verb or part of a contraction.

Introduction

The transformer architecture
(
Vaswani et al., 2018
)
can be scaled along three distinct dimensions:
dataset size, model size, and amount of computation. Rapid progress in foundational models
was initially achieved by focusing on increasing the size of data sets and models. Recently,
the field’s focus has shifted to computational scaling—varying the amount of computation time
to produce a response. Most of this effort involves collecting multiple samples from a model or extending
reasoning chains via prompting
(
Zelikman et al., 2022
;
Wei et al., 2023
;
Yao et al., 2023
, e.g.,)
or enabling latent thought

(
Phan et al., 2023
;
Pfau et al., 2024
;
Hao et al., 2024
;
Ruan et al., 2025
, e.g.,)
. However, benefits of computational scaling can also be attained
by increasing the resources devoted to individual tokens, such as dynamically varying the model depth

(
Raposo et al., 2024
)
or the number of layer iterations at inference time
(
Chen et al., 2025
;
Geiping et al., 2025
)
.

In this article, we explore an approach to dynamically scaling the number of compute steps for
individual tokens by training the model to selectively request additional compute. As the
input is processed step by step, the model is given the option of emitting a special
<don’t know>
token which requests a delay. If the delay is granted by the data loader, a
<pause>
token
is inserted in the input stream
(
Goyal et al., 2024
)
, holding off on the arrival of further input
and allowing the model an additional forward pass to process the sequence to that point
(Figure
1
).
The model can produce the
<don’t know>
response multiple times in a row, though we design training objectives
to ensure that the model will not abuse this privilege.

1.1
How People Read

The inspiration for this research comes from studies of human reading. When gaze is
tracked as an individual reads, inter-saccade durations are highly
nonuniform. Readers fixate longer when processing demands rise, e.g.,
at points where information can be integrated across phrases, where inferences can be made
at the end of sentences, and where low-frequency terms occur
(
Just and Carpenter, 1980
)
.
Figure
2
shows fixation order within a sentence for a human
reader as well as the fixation duration. Note that short function words are skipped
or quickly glanced over. When an uncommon interpretation of a word or sentence is
required, readers pause. For instance, when idioms that typically have a figurative
meaning (e.g., ‘break the ice’) are used in a literal way, processing time slows
at the point where the disambiguating information is provided
(
Arnon and Lavidor, 2023
)
.
Similarly, readers pause when anomalous or unexpected information is provided,
including attributes and spatial position of a character or object
(
Stewart et al., 2009
)
.
These various results suggest that people integrate information in an ongoing manner,
where each incoming word and clause is processed with respect to prior context,
and reading time is governed by the computational demands of online evaluative processes.

In humans, the ongoing, word-by-word integration processes might be described
as
microinference
, in contrast to
macroinference
, which involves
solving complex reasoning and planning problems. In AI, past research on pause tokens
has focused on their utility for macroinference
(
Goyal et al., 2024
;
Herel and Mikolov, 2023
;
Pfau et al., 2024
;
Kim et al., 2025
)
.
Our present work is aimed at emphasizing the role of delays for microinference,
motivated by the need to integrate and interpret information in an ongoing fashion.
One important clue that this focus will be productive comes from a study
which found that GPT-2 attention patterns could predict human reading times,
in particular measures of the dispersion of attention and the change in attention
patterns across time steps
(
Oh and Schuler, 2022
)
.

Catch Your Breath

In this section, we describe our approach of dynamically scaling the number of compute steps for individual tokens. We refer reader to Table
1
for notation glossary.

In past research involving pause tokens (see Section
3
for more details), each input token
t
is granted a certain number of steps,
{W}_{t}\in\{1,2,...{W^{\textit{max}}}\}
, to produce an output, which involves inserting
{W}_{t}-1
pauses in the input stream.
With causal masking, the model can glean no information about the number of upcoming pauses.
Consequently, the model must be prepared to respond at each step where these additional pause tokens are inserted, leading to greedy
optimization even when additional compute time is allocated.

Because
{W}_{t}
is externally selected, the model cannot anticipate where
and how many pauses will occur. From the model’s perspective,
{W}_{t}
is thus a random variable.
If during training,
{W}_{t}
is drawn consistently in an iid manner from a prior,
{W}_{t}\sim\text{Cat}(\boldsymbol{{\omega}})
, where
\boldsymbol{{\omega}}=(\omega_{1},\ldots,\omega_{{W^{\textit{max}}}})
with
\omega_{i}=Pr(W_{t}=i)
, the model’s use of pause tokens should be facilitated.
However, pause steps might be better utilized if the model itself can control their insertion.

{W}
. (We drop the subscript

(2)

The expected discounted accuracy obtained by the model in the CYB decision task is
\mathbb{E}_{i\sim\Pr({S}|\boldsymbol{d},\boldsymbol{{\omega}})}\left[\gamma_{i}t_{i}\right]
,
which can be turned into a negative log likelihood loss:

\ell_{\text{CYB}}(\boldsymbol{t},\boldsymbol{d};~\boldsymbol{\omega},\boldsymbol{\gamma})=-\log\mathbb{E}_{i\sim\Pr({S}|\boldsymbol{d},\boldsymbol{{\omega}})}\left[\gamma_{i}t_{i}\right].

(3)

The loss depends on model outputs
\boldsymbol{t}
and
\boldsymbol{d}
and on hyperparameters

\boldsymbol{{\omega}}
, the distribution over number of steps allotted for
a token, and
\boldsymbol{\gamma}
, the vector of step-wise discount factors.
The loss is equivalent to the standard cross-entropy loss
with the additional twist that responses can be read out at any step
i\in\{1,...,{W^{\textit{max}}}\}
and
accuracy at response step
i
is discounted by
\gamma_{i}
. The expectation in Equation

{W^{\textit{max}}}
steps to compute a mean of
\gamma_{i}t_{i}
weighted by the probability in Equation
2
.

In earlier work with pause tokens
(
Goyal et al., 2024
)
, a cross-entropy loss was used at the final pause step,
which is a specific instantiation of our loss with
<don’t know>
fixed

for
i\in\{1,...,{W^{\textit{max}}}-1\}
), reward undiscounted (i.e.,
\gamma_{i}=1
for

i\in\{1,...,{W^{\textit{max}}}\}
), and no early stopping (i.e.,
\omega_{i}=0
for
i<{W^{\textit{max}}}
).

When an LLM is augmented to output
<don’t know>
, Equation
3
can
be used to train both the
<don’t know>
probabilities,
\{d_{i}\}
, and the next-token distribution, via
\{t_{i}\}
.
However, this loss won’t necessarily encourage the model to use compute to good end. For example, if reward is
undiscounted (i.e.,
\gamma_{i}=1
) and delays are always granted (
{\omega}_{i}=0
for
i<{W^{\textit{max}}}
),
the same reward is obtained whether a given response is produced on step 1 or on step
{W^{\textit{max}}}
. The model has
no incentive to adhere to a computational budget and calibrate its use of
<don’t know>
to situations where
additional time would improve the response.

We thus propose three instantiations of the CYB loss that promote sensitivity of pause requests to model performance,
aiming to obtain a
speed-accuracy trade off
, i.e., performance that improves monotonically with additional compute.
First, CYB-AP tries to ensure
anytime prediction
accuracy matched to a world stop-time distribution.
Second, CYB-VA is a
variational approach
that allows the model to internalize a stop-time distribution as a prior.
Third, CYB-DP imposes a
distributional penalty
on the number of computation steps.

2.1
Anytime Prediction (CYB-AP)

Anytime prediction requires that a model be prepared with a response at every computation step. To optimize for anytime
prediction, the loss must be sensitive to the model output at each step. Equation
3
has this property given
a stop-time distribution,
\boldsymbol{{\omega}}
, with nonzero probability at each step, i.e.,
{\omega}_{i}>0
for all
i
.
To minimize this loss, the model should learn to request additional compute only when discounted accuracy at step
i+1
is
no lower than on step
i
:
\gamma_{i+1}t_{i+1}\geq\gamma_{i}t_{i}
. With a monotonicity constraint on the discount factor,

\gamma_{i+1}\leq\gamma_{i}
, the model should learn to calibrate its
<don’t know>
probability to its discounted accuracy.
We can impose a linear cost per step with
\gamma_{i}=1-(i-1)(1-\gamma_{0})
or exponential with
\gamma_{i}=\gamma_{0}^{i-1}
for a given value of
\gamma_{0}
.
We refer to the CYB loss with these constraints on
\boldsymbol{{\omega}}
and
\boldsymbol{\gamma}
as CYB-AP, for anytime
prediction.

By incorporating the externally imposed stop-time distribution
\boldsymbol{{\omega}}
, CYB-AP has an upper limit on
the expected number of compute steps, independent of model behavior: the model can reduce the number of compute
steps but it cannot increase them. In the two approaches we describe next, the externally imposed upper limit is eliminated

(equivalently,
\widetilde{\omega}=0

model to adhere to a desired stop-time distribution on its own. We distinguish this distribution to be internalized
from
\boldsymbol{{\omega}}
using the notation
\boldsymbol{\rho}
.

2.2
Variational Approach (CYB-VA)

We wish to train the model to produce a desired distribution
\boldsymbol{\rho}
and to
minimize the loss with respect to this distribution, i.e.,

\ell_{\text{CYB}\rho}=-\log\mathbb{E}_{i\sim\text{Cat}(\boldsymbol{\rho})}\left[\gamma_{i}t_{i}\right].

However, this loss does not depend on
\boldsymbol{d}
and will thus fail to
train the
<don’t know>
output, let alone train it to be consistent with
\boldsymbol{\rho}
.
To achieve this goal, we take a variational approach and train the model using the ELBO.
Treating the model’s likelihood at step
i
as the penalized value
\Pr[{\rm target}|S=i]=\gamma_{i}t_{i}
, the ELBO is
the right hand side of the inequality:

\begin{split}\log\mathbb{E}_{i\sim\text{Cat}(\boldsymbol{\rho})}\left[\gamma_{i}t_{i}\right]&\geq\mathbb{E}_{i\sim\text{Cat}({\boldsymbol{s}})}\log\left[\gamma_{i}t_{i}\right]-D_{\text{KL}}(\boldsymbol{s}~||~{\boldsymbol{\rho}})\end{split}

where we assume as before that externally induced stops are eliminated.
The
<don’t know>
distribution,
\boldsymbol{d}
, is incorporated via the stopping time distribution
\boldsymbol{s}\equiv\Pr(S|\boldsymbol{d})
(Equation
2
).
We then define a negative ELBO loss,

\ell_{\text{CYB-VA}}=-\mathbb{E}_{i\sim\text{Cat}({\boldsymbol{s}})}\log\left[\gamma_{i}t_{i}\right]+D_{\text{KL}}(\boldsymbol{s}~||~{\boldsymbol{\rho}})~.

As an upper bound on our desired loss function,
\ell_{\text{CYB}\rho}
,
minimizing
\ell_{\text{CYB-VA}}
trains the model’s predictions (
\boldsymbol{t}
) by optimizing the expected discounted likelihood under
\boldsymbol{s}
.
It also trains the model’s stopping time distribution (
\boldsymbol{s}
) toward the posterior, following the optimization view of Bayesian inference
(
Zellner, 1988
, e.g.,)
.
We can determine the equilibrium self-stop distribution by taking the gradient of the variational loss,

\nabla_{\boldsymbol{s}}~\ell_{\text{CYB-VA}}=-\log\boldsymbol{\gamma}-\log\boldsymbol{t}-\log\boldsymbol{\rho}+\log\boldsymbol{s}+\boldsymbol{1}~.

Introducing a Lagrange multiplier for the constraint
\sum_{i}s_{i}=1
yields the optimality condition
\log\boldsymbol{s}=\log\boldsymbol{\gamma}+\log\boldsymbol{t}+\log\boldsymbol{\rho}-(\lambda+1)\boldsymbol{1}
,
resulting in
s_{i}\propto\rho_{i}\gamma_{i}t_{i}
.
The model will thus learn to stop at times when both the stopping prior and its discounted accuracy are high.
This analysis also indicates that the prior,
\boldsymbol{\rho}
, and the discount factors,
\boldsymbol{\gamma}
, are
redundant in CYB-VA. We thus assume
\gamma_{i}=1
in all experiments with CYB-VA. CYB-VA thus has hyperparameters

\boldsymbol{\rho}
, in addition to specifying
{W^{\textit{max}}}
, the upper limit on the number of steps.

2.3
Distributional Penalty (CYB-DP)

A final approach is to optimize for our original loss,
\ell_{\text{CYB}}
(Equation
3
), and impose a penalty based on the deviation of
the model’s desired and observed stop-time distributions:

\ell_{\text{CYB-DP}}=\ell_{\text{CYB}}+\alpha~D_{\text{KL}}(\boldsymbol{\rho}~||~\boldsymbol{s})~,

where
\alpha
specifies a penalty on the distribution mismatch.
Note that the direction of the KL terms is reversed for
\ell_{\text{CYB-DP}}
versus
\ell_{\text{CYB-VA}}
, in order to strongly penalize stopping times that are outside the support of the prior. (And we wish to allow the prior to have zero values.)
CYB-DP has hyperparameters
\boldsymbol{\rho}
and
\alpha
, in addition to specifying
{W^{\textit{max}}}
, the upper limit on
the number of steps.

3
Related research

Pause tokens.

Herel and Mikolov, 2023
proposed the idea of special ‘thinking’ input tokens and
Goyal et al., 2024
independently explored
‘pause’ tokens. In
Goyal et al., 2024
, a fixed number of pauses were inserted into
the text stream by the LLM controller. During pretraining, blocks of
{W}
tokens were inserted at uniformly random positions.
When pauses are inserted, model outputs for the
M-1
steps leading up to the final pause token are ignored and
excluded from the usual next-token-prediction cross-entropy loss. During fine tuning, where a prefix sequence is given
along with a target sequence,
M^{\prime}
pauses were appended to the prefix, giving the model additional compute
steps before its response.
Goyal et al., 2024
found consistent benefits in downstream-task accuracy only when pauses
were included in both pretraining and fine tuning stages. Although this approach appears to be an alternative to
chain-of-thought methods, pauses may be related given that substituting meaningless filler tokens for the chain of
thought still yields benefits
(
Pfau et al., 2024
)
. In all of these works, long delays are introduced between
a prefix and a model’s response, intended to support explicit reasoning about and calculation of the response—an
alternative to chain of thought. In contrast, our motivation is to allow short delays in processing any token, with
the aim of facilitating integration of information in prefix tokens as well as in choosing any token in the response.
Similar in spirit to our proposal, also focused on improving next-token prediction, is a method that involves
generating reasoning traces for individual tokens
(
Dong et al., 2025
)
. The computational cost
of our technique is significantly lower.

Anytime prediction and adaptive computation.

In the anytime-prediction literature, models are tasked with making a series of responses that improve over time or
with additional compute. Such models exhibit a
speed-accuracy
trade off in which a coarse answer appears quickly
but is refined with additional computation. In some schemes, the model is rewarded for getting the right response in
as few steps as possible
(
Iuzzolino et al., 2021
, e.g.,)
. Anytime prediction is related to adaptive computation-time for
recurrent networks
(
Graves, 2016
)
.

Adaptive depth models.
Extending adaptive computation-time to
transformers, methods have been proposed in which computation is dynamically allocated by token, effectively making the model deeper for hard tokens
(
Bae et al., 2025
;
Raposo et al., 2024
)
. The allocation is performed by a learned router, trained on the standard loss, rather than our approach which is based on a well-formed model of computation with an integrated loss that considers speed-accuracy trade offs.

<don’t know>
outputs.

The
<don’t know>
output is related to classification with absention (or rejection)
(
Bartlett and Wegkamp, 2008
;
Liu et al., 2019
;
Hendrickx et al., 2024
)
.
Two recent articles introduce heuristic notions of “I don’t know” to determine where pause tokens should be inserted.

Cohen et al., 2024
include a
<don’t know>
output from the LLM and introduce a
heuristic training objective that shifts probability to the
<don’t know>
output when the
model predicts incorrectly. Their approach applies multiple heuristics to prevent
the degenerate solution of always outputting
<don’t know>
. By integrating the
<don’t know>
option into
our sequential decision task, our approach does not suffer such degenerate solutions.

Kim et al., 2025
insert pause tokens when output entropy is high. This heuristic approach
cannot distinguish aleatoric from epistemic uncertainty, which the CYB loss can in principle infer.

4
Implementation decisions

4.1
Position encoding with pause sequences

To encode an input sequence, each token is assigned a position index which is used by positional embedding methods such as RoPE
(
Su et al., 2023
)
.
Treating
<pause>
inputs like any other token will cause them to shift the position indices of following tokens.
This approach was used by
Goyal et al., 2024
, but when the number of pause tokens is variable, the shifts are irregular and unpredictable
to the model. Consequently, we use RoPE but assign
<pause>
tokens the same index as the immediately preceding non-
<pause>
token.
This choice also seems to facilitate the introduction of pause tokens during fine tuning in a model that was trained without them.
To ensure that the model can selectively index a specific pause token, we encode each pause in the sequence with a unique
token code:
<pause1>
,
<pause2>
, etc. In addition, one could consider modulating query and key computations to be
sensitive to the pause index, e.g., by incorporating a pause-specific offset, as in
\boldsymbol{k}=\boldsymbol{W}^{(k)}(\boldsymbol{z}+\boldsymbol{v}_{p})
for embedding
\boldsymbol{z}
and pause index offset
p
with
\boldsymbol{v}_{p}
as a learned offset.

4.2
Where and how many pauses are permitted?

We propose two distinct recipes for training with the CYB losses.
Both require that we specify the maximum number of pause steps allowed,
{W^{\textit{max}}}
.

4.2.1
Recipe 1: Constant number of pauses

The first recipe, which we use in the experiments we report below, inserts
{W^{\textit{max}}}
pauses
in the input stream after each real input token during training. The transformer steps are run in parallel with
causal masking. The pause steps may or may not be used by the model, e.g., if at some step
i
the model
is certain (
d_{i}\to 0
) or the world enforces a stop (
\widetilde{\omega}_{i}\to 1
), the outputs at subsequent pauses
will not contribute to the loss.

During inference, when the transformer might be run autoregressively, we match the use of pauses at train time,
i.e., always inserting
{W^{\textit{max}}}
pauses after each real input token. Because an input token and the pauses that
follow can be processed in parallel, there is no computational cost to inserting the maximum number of pauses in the stream for every
token. Rather, the potential cost comes from the pauses cluttering the context window. Even when
{W^{\textit{max}}}

pauses are inserted at both training and inference, and even when the model is not pressured to reply quickly (e.g.,

\gamma_{i}=1
for all
i
), the model must still learn to use the
<don’t know>
output to calibrate itself and determine when to read out.

4.2.2
Recipe 2: Variable number of pauses

The second recipe involves a variable number of pause steps at both training and inference.
The CYB losses can accommodate variability in the number of pauses following a token at training. No modification to the loss
needs be made, but the
<don’t know>
probability must be forced to zero on the last pause, or in response to an input token itself when
there are no pauses. Rather than computing the expectation over world stopping times, we can sample a stopping time for each
token and insert that number of pause steps in the input stream. One might also envision training methods that insert
more pauses where they are likely to be most useful, e.g., where thinking might be required. (In the results section,
we present evidence that pause requests by the model are token- and context-specific.)

During inference, the transformer might be run autoregressively, and we can again sample from the world stop-time distribution to
determine the number of pause steps, and sample from the model’s
<don’t know>
confidence to determine which of the allowed steps to read
out from.

4.3
Representing
<don’t know>

With regard to implementation,
d_{i}
could be produced by a separate logistic output unit. Alternatively, an unused
token
\delta
from the standard set of output alternatives could be repurposed to represent
<don’t know>
. In the latter case, the
model’s output distribution,
y
, must be renormalized:

\hat{y}_{ij}=\begin{cases}\frac{y_{ij}}{1-d_{i,\delta}}&\text{if }j\neq\delta\\
0&\text{otherwise}\end{cases}

Methodology

We evaluate the performance of our approach using the following setting. Starting from a pretrained Gemma1-2B
(
Team et al., 2024
)
model, we perform fine tuning (tail patching) on a subset of C4 dataset
(
Raffel et al., 2020
)
. We consider the sequences of 2048 input tokens which we expand to a length 8192 by inserting three pause tokens following each original token. Unused input indices were repurposed to serve as pause tokens. An unused output token was
repurposed as the
<don’t know>
token.

Because the pretrained model was never rewarded for assigning probability mass to the
<don’t know>
token,
we wanted to ensure that the model actually had the potential to output
<don’t know>
when being fined tuned
with the CYB loss. To achieve this objective, model posteriors were renormalized to reflect a
high prior for
<don’t know>
. If
\boldsymbol{\psi}
is the prior distribution over tokens
during pretraining and
\boldsymbol{\psi}^{\prime}
is the prior we wish to be reflected in the
model posteriors, we add
\log(\boldsymbol{\psi}^{\prime}/\boldsymbol{\psi})
to the model logit vector.
With uniform pretraining priors, i.e.,
\psi_{i}=1/|V|
with
V
being the vocabulary,
we set
\psi^{\prime}_{\textsc{dk}}
for the
<don’t know>
and
\psi^{\prime}_{i}=(1-\psi^{\prime}_{\textsc{dk}})/(|V|-1)
for all
other indices
i
. In the reported experiments, we use
\psi^{\prime}_{\textsc{dk}}=0.9
.
We found the models trained better with
\psi^{\prime}_{\textsc{dk}}\geq 0.9
.

Baselines.

In addition to variants of the CYB loss, we tested a

baseline
model which was fine tuned without the inclusion of pause tokens.
A context window of 2048 is used for the baseline in order to match the number of non-
<pause>
tokens in the CYB models.
We also tested Goyal et al.’s “Think Before You Speak” or
TBYS
method, which includes pause tokens but is trained
by the ordinary cross-entropy loss. For TBYS, we used the position coding scheme used in the CYB models, even though
the original TBYS model treated pauses like any other token for the purpose of position coding. (The original TBYS model also
does not encode the first, second, etc. pause in sequence distinctly from the others.) We considered TBYS approach with
1
,
2
and
3
additional pause tokens added after every real token in the original sequence of length
2048
, making thus the expanded sequence lengths to be
4096
,
6144
and
8192
correspondingly.

Training regime.

To conduct our experiments, we finetuned the base model on a subset of
6.4
millions of sequences of
C4
dataset, with a batch size of
256
, using a total of
25k
iterations. We use "concatenate then split" packing algorithm. We use a cosine learning rate decay with a linear warmup sweeping over maximum value of a learning rate in a range
[10^{-2},5\times 10^{-3},10^{-3},5\times 10^{-4},5\times 10^{-5},10^{-5},10^{-6}]
and dividing this learning by a dimension of a model (
2048
). The minimum value of learning rate is given by a maximum value multiplied by
10^{-2}
. For the evaluation, we use 160K sequences (59.8M tokens) from a validation set of C4.

Additional training data regimes.

Besides the original training regime, we ran a subset of methods (baselines and CYB-AP) using
2\times
and
3\times
more training data as well as using
2
epochs on the original dataset set. For evaluation we still use the same subset of
160k
sequences from a validation set of
C4
.

Discussion

In this article, we introduced a family of losses that enable foundation models to set their own pace of generation. We examined three variants of the
Catch Your Breath
(CYB) loss family and converged on one particular setting that results in models that dynamically request additional computation steps when it will benefit output quality. The CYB losses reflect a synergy between two important research threads in machine learning, one focused on developing adaptive-computation techniques for LLMs and another on leveraging non-content control tokens in the input stream (here,
<pause>
tokens). The synergy produces greater benefit from
<pause>
tokens relative to earlier approaches that were based on the standard cross-entropy loss; and the synergy provides a more practical means of adapting computation time than dynamic-depth approaches.

The CYB loss obtains an explicit measure of uncertainty via the
<don’t know>
output. This
uncertainty is epistemic, meaning that the model has not yet acquired sufficient information. Ordinarily, epistemic uncertainty refers to limitations of

training
, but in the case of CYB, it refers to limitations of

computation
: the model is uncertain because it has not yet performed
sufficient compute operations.

We proposed two distinct recipes for training and inference with CYB losses, but
we focused on the recipe in which a fixed number of pauses are inserted after each
token during both training and inference. The alternative, in which pauses are
inserted only when the model requests them, requires running an LLM autoregressively.
It would thus make most sense to introduce variable pauses at the post-training
(RL) alignment stage where autoregressive updating is performed. Beyond these
two recipes, other possibilities exist, such as developing strategies to predict
which tokens are likely to most benefit and inserting pauses selectively.

One direction of future work is to better understand how model’s utilize the additional
pause steps. From one perspective, the model’s input during these pause steps is
nearly identical to that of the initial step. To compute different features during
each delay step, the model must index on the embedding that is associated with
each pause index. One might consider facilitating the model’s use of pauses by
conditioning some of the attention-head weights on the pause index, providing
the model with a more direct means of pause-conditioned compute modulation.
