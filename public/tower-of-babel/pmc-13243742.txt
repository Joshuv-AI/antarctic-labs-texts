Diet-induced obesity exacerbates and prolongs postoperative pain via peripheral DRG transcriptional changes in rats
Authors: Norika Katagiri, Kanta Kido, Shigekazu Sugino, Daisuke Konno, Jun Suzuki, Masanori Yamauchi, Yukie Nitta, Takuro Sanuki
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundObesity is a known risk factor for the increased severity and persistence of postoperative pain; however, the underlying mechanisms remain incompletely understood. The primary objective of this study was to elucidate the peripheral mechanisms underlying obesity-related exacerbation and prolongation of postoperative pain. A comparative transcriptomic analysis of the spinal cord was performed as a secondary exploratory assessment.MethodsMale Wistar rats were fed a high-fat diet (HFD) or standard diet (SD) to induce obesity. Postoperative pain was modeled using the plantar incision (Brennan) model. Pain-related behaviors including spontaneous pain, mechanical withdrawal thresholds, and thermal withdrawal latencies were evaluated longitudinally. Electrophysiological recordings were performed to assess the mechanical sensitivity of primary afferent fibers in the tibial nerve–plantar skin preparation. Transcriptome analyses were conducted on the dorsal root ganglia (DRG) and spinal cord on postoperative day 4 to identify the differentially expressed genes (DEGs) associated with obesity-related pain modulation.ResultsRats fed an HFD exhibited increased body weight and body fat percentage compared with SD-fed rats. Following surgery, the HFD-fed rats showed significantly higher spontaneous pain scores and prolonged mechanical hypersensitivity than the SD-fed rats, whereas preoperative pain sensitivity did not differ between the groups. Electrophysiological analysis revealed no significant differences in the mechanical thresholds or stimulus–response relationship of mechanosensitive C-fibers between the groups. Transcriptome analysis identified 19 DEGs in the DRG of HFD-fed rats; however, no genes in the spinal cord dorsal horn met the statistical threshold for differential expression under the present analytical conditions. Pathway analysis indicated that the DRG DEGs were primarily associated with endocrine disorders and lipid metabolism.ConclusionDiet-induced obesity exacerbated and prolonged postoperative pain in rats. This effect may be associated with peripheral molecular adaptations in primary sensory neurons, including changes in DRG gene expression related to metabolic and endocrine pathways. In contrast, no genes met the statistical threshold for differential expression in the spinal cord dorsal horn on postoperative day 4 under the present analytical conditions.

INTRODUCTION

In recent decades, the global prevalence of obesity has markedly increased, driven in part by prolonged life expectancy, and now exceeds 30% of the world’s population [1]. Obesity substantially increases the risk of metabolic, cardiovascular, neurological, and musculoskeletal disorders, and its prevalence has nearly tripled over the past 30 years [2]. Consequently, opportunities for clinical encounters with obese patients have increased, and various perioperative management challenges, such as impaired respiratory function and difficulty in determining optimal drug dosages, have become increasingly apparent [34].

Obesity is not only a major cause of lifestyle-related diseases, including diabetes mellitus, dyslipidemia, and hypertension, but is also associated with the development of cardiovascular disease, cerebrovascular disease, and cancer [5]. In addition, excessive body weight imposes mechanical stress on the musculoskeletal system, contributing to tissue degeneration and chronic inflammation [6]. With respect to pain, previous studies have reported that individuals with obesity exhibit lower pain thresholds, indicating increased pain sensitivity [78]. The association between obesity and pain is complex and likely involves a combination of mechanical, inflammatory, and psychosocial factors [9].

Surgical procedures inevitably involve tissue injury and are often accompanied by pain. Inadequately managed postoperative pain can delay mobilization and hospital discharge and is associated with an increased risk of respiratory and cardiovascular complications [10]. Obesity has been identified as a significant risk factor for more severe and persistent postoperative pain [1112]. Clinical studies have demonstrated that obese patients report greater postoperative pain following thoracic, orthopedic, or orthodontic procedures than non-obese patients [131415]. This heightened pain experience can lead to increased opioid consumption, which carries its own risks, particularly in obese individuals, who are more susceptible to opioid-related respiratory depression and may benefit from non-opioid analgesic strategies [16].

Several basic studies have suggested cellular and molecular mechanisms by which obesity exacerbates postoperative pain. Diet-induced obesity has been shown to prolong mechanical hypersensitivity after surgery, and alterations in neurotrophic factor expression (e.g., BDNF, NGF) have been reported in peripheral nerves and the spinal cord of obese animals [1718]. However, these studies focused on a limited number of molecular targets, and the comprehensive mechanisms underlying the obesity-related exacerbation and persistence of postoperative pain remain unclear. The interplay between the peripheral and central nervous systems in this context is particularly underexplored. Therefore, the primary objective of this study was to elucidate the peripheral mechanisms underlying obesity-associated postoperative pain. A comparative analysis of the spinal cord dorsal horn transcriptome was included as a secondary exploratory component to evaluate whether central transcriptional changes were detectable on postoperative day 4.

METHODS

1. Animals

All experimental procedures were approved by the Animal Care and Use Committees of Kanagawa Dental University and Tohoku University (Approval No. 2019-035, 2020-006, and 2021MdA-022) and were conducted in accordance with institutional guidelines based on the National Institutes of Health Guide for the Care and Use of Laboratory Animals. Male Wistar rats aged 4 (n = 16) or 9 weeks (n = 56) were purchased from a commercial supplier (CLEA Japan, Inc., Tokyo, Japan). Animals were housed in a temperature- and humidity-controlled environment (23 ± 2℃, 55 ± 10% humidity) on a 12-hour light/dark cycle, with free access to food and water. Upon arrival, the rats were acclimated for 1 week before being randomly assigned to receive either a high-fat diet (HFD; D12451, 45% kcal from fat; Research Diets, New Brunswick, NJ, USA) or a standard diet (SD; MF, Oriental Yeast Co., Tokyo, Japan). This study was reported in accordance with the ARRIVE guidelines.

2. Postoperative pain model

Postoperative pain was induced using the plantar incision model described by Brennan et al. [19]. The rats were anesthetized with 3% sevoflurane in oxygen. The plantar surface of the right hind paw was sterilized with 70% ethanol. A 10-millimeter longitudinal incision was made through the skin and fascia, starting 5 mm from the heel. The flexor digitorum brevis muscle was elevated and stretched using curved forceps. The wound was then closed with two mattress sutures using 5-0 nylon, and gentamicin ointment was applied.

3. Experimental design

Body weight was measured weekly to confirm the induction of obesity. Body fat percentage was measured at week 8 of diet feeding using EchoMRI (EchoMRI-900, Echo Medical Systems, Houston, TX, USA), and blood glucose levels were measured at the time of surgery using a glucometer (Accu-Chek, Roche, Basel, Switzerland). Two independent cohorts were included. The first cohort (4-week-old rats fed for 8 weeks) was used to confirm obesity induction, including body composition analysis. The second cohort (9-week-old rats) underwent plantar incision surgery after 5 weeks on the assigned diet to ensure the development of obesity-related phenotypes while maintaining a comparable age at surgery. Pain-related behaviors were assessed preoperatively (baseline), 3 h after surgery, and on postoperative days 1, 2, 4, 7, 10, 14, 18, and 21. For electrophysiological and transcriptome analyses, separate cohorts were euthanized on postoperative day 4.

4. Pain-related behavioral analysis

All behavioral tests were conducted by an investigator who was blinded to the dietary group assignments. Spontaneous pain was assessed using a guarding pain score, where 0 = the paw was completely flat on the floor, 1 = the paw was slightly elevated or turned on its side, and 2 = the paw was fully elevated. Observations were made for 1 min and repeated 12 times at 5-minute intervals, with the total score summed. Mechanical withdrawal thresholds were assessed using an electronic von Frey anesthesiometer (IITC Life Science Inc., Woodland Hills, CA, USA). The force (g) applied to the plantar surface near the incision that elicited a paw withdrawal response was recorded. Measurements were repeated three times at 5-minute intervals, and the mean value was calculated. Thermal withdrawal latencies were assessed by applying a radiant heat source to the plantar surface (Plantar Test, Ugo Basile, Gemonio, Italy) with a cutoff time of 20 s to prevent tissue damage. The radiant heat intensity was determined in preliminary experiments to produce baseline withdrawal latencies of approximately 10–15 s and was kept constant throughout the experiment. As the manufacturer did not provide calibrated physical power output values for the device, the instrument setting was defined operationally based on the baseline withdrawal latency and applied consistently across all experiments.

5. Electrophysiological recording

On postoperative day 4, the rats were euthanized, and the tibial nerve–plantar skin preparation was dissected [20]. The preparation was placed in a chamber perfused with synthetic interstitial fluid (SIF) saturated with 95% O2 and 5% CO2. The SIF contained (in mM): 107 NaCl, 26.2 NaHCO3, 9.64 sodium gluconate, 5.5 glucose, 7.6 sucrose, 3.48 KCl, 1.67 NaH2PO4, 1.53 CaCl2, and 0.69 MgSO4. The temperature was maintained at 32 ± 0.5℃. Single-fiber recordings were made from fine filaments of the tibial nerve. Mechanosensitive primary afferent fibers were identified based on their response to mechanical stimulation of their receptive fields. Conduction velocity was calculated, and fibers with a velocity ≤ 2.5 m/s were classified as C-fibers. Mechanical thresholds were determined using von Frey filaments, and evoked action potentials in response to suprathreshold stimulation (5-120 mN) delivered by a servomotor were recorded and analyzed using Spike2 software (Cambridge Electronic Design, Cambridge, UK).

6. Transcriptome analysis

On postoperative day 4, ipsilateral lumbar (L4–L6) dorsal root ganglia (DRG) and the corresponding spinal cord dorsal horn were rapidly dissected. Total RNA was extracted using the RNeasy Mini Kit (Qiagen, Hilden, Germany). RNA quality and integrity were assessed using a NanoDrop spectrophotometer (Thermo Fisher, Waltham, MA, USA) and a BioAnalyzer (Agilent Technologies, Santa Clara, CA, USA), respectively, ensuring an A260/A280 ratio > 1.9 and an RNA integrity number (RIN) > 7.0. cDNA libraries were prepared using the TruSeq® Stranded mRNA Library Prep Kit (Illumina, San Diego, CA, USA). Sequencing was performed on a NovaSeq 6000 platform (Illumina) to generate 150 bp paired-end reads. The reads were mapped to the rat reference genome (RGSC 6.0/rn6) using HISAT2 [21]. Differentially expressed genes (DEGs) were identified using DESeq2 [22] with a false discovery rate (FDR) adjusted p-value (q-value) < 0.05. Pathway analysis of the DEGs was conducted using Ingenuity Pathway Analysis (IPA; Qiagen) [23]. Given the sample size (n = 4 per group), transcriptome analysis was considered exploratory.

7. Statistical analysis

Data are presented as mean ± standard error of the mean (SEM). Statistical analyses were performed using GraphPad Prism 9 (GraphPad Software, San Diego, CA, USA). Body weight and behavioral data over time were analyzed using a two-way repeated-measures analysis of variance (ANOVA) followed by Sidak's post hoc test. Body fat percentage and electrophysiological data were analyzed using unpaired t-tests. Statistical significance was set at P < 0.05.

RESULTS

1. High-fat diet induces an obese phenotype in rats

After 5 weeks of diet feeding, rats fed the HFD exhibited significantly greater body weight than the SD-fed controls (P < 0.001, Fig. 1A). Body fat percentage measured at week 8 was significantly higher in the HFD group (46.2 ± 4.0%) than in the control group (37.6 ± 6.3%; P = 0.002, Fig. 1B). Blood glucose levels at the time of surgery did not differ significantly between groups (Fig. 1C).

2. Obesity exacerbates and prolongs postoperative pain behaviors

Before surgery, there were no differences in baseline pain sensitivity between the HFD and SD groups. Following plantar incision, HFD-fed rats showed significantly higher guarding pain scores on postoperative days 1 (P < 0.0001) and 2 (P < 0.0001) than the SD group (Fig. 2A). Mechanical hypersensitivity was observed in both the groups. Two-way repeated measures ANOVA revealed a significant diet × time interaction (P < 0.0001), indicating prolonged mechanical hypersensitivity in the HFD group. While the SD group recovered to near-baseline mechanical thresholds by day 7, the HFD group's thresholds remained significantly lower until day 21 (P < 0.0001, Fig. 2B). There were no significant differences in the thermal withdrawal latencies between the groups at most time points, except for minor differences on days 14 and 21 (Fig. 2C).

3. Peripheral C-fiber sensitization is not markedly altered in obese rats

To investigate the peripheral neural basis for enhanced pain behavior in HFD-fed rats, we performed ex vivo electrophysiological recordings of tibial nerve–innervating C-fiber afferents on postoperative day 4. Representative recordings showed typical stimulus-dependent firing in response to graded mechanical stimulation Fig. 3A). However, there were no statistically significant differences between the HFD and SD groups in stimulus–response relationship (Fig. 3B) or mechanical activation thresholds (Fig. 3C). These findings indicated that differences in mechanosensitive C-fiber properties were not detectable at this time point.

4. Transcriptome analysis reveals DRG-specific gene expression changes

To explore the molecular mechanisms underlying the prolonged pain phenotype, we performed RNA sequencing of the ipsilateral DRG and spinal cord dorsal horn of HFD and SD rats on postoperative day 4. In the spinal cord, no genes met the FDR < 0.05 threshold for differential expression under the present analytical conditions on postoperative day 4. In contrast, the DRG of HFD-fed rats exhibited a distinct transcriptional signature, with 19 genes identified as being significantly differentially expressed, compared with the SD group (Table 1).

Pathway analysis of these 19 DEGs using IPA revealed significant enrichment of genes related to metabolic and endocrine functions. The top canonical pathways identified were associated with "endocrine system disorders," "gastrointestinal disease," "metabolic disease," and "lipid metabolism" (Table 2). Genes such as Rps6, Fkbp5, Zbtb16, Tnc, Serpine1, and Acer2 were key contributors to these pathways, suggesting that metabolic dysregulation within primary sensory neurons is a key feature of the obesity-related pain phenotype.

DISCUSSION

In this study, we demonstrated that diet-induced obesity exacerbates and prolongs postoperative pain in a rat model of plantar incision. Our comprehensive analysis, combining behavioral, electrophysiological, and transcriptomic approaches, suggests that peripheral mechanisms may contribute substantially during the early postoperative period. The behavioral analyses revealed a significantly delayed recovery from postoperative mechanical hypersensitivity in obese rats. This is consistent with clinical observations of more severe and persistent pain in obese patients undergoing surgery [131424].

Interestingly, our electrophysiological findings did not show significant differences in the mechanical sensitivity or firing properties of primary afferent C-fibers between obese and non-obese rats on postoperative day 4. These findings indicated that differences in the intrinsic excitability of mechanosensitive C-fibers were not detectable at this time point. The mechanisms could be more complex, possibly involving other fiber types (e.g., Aδ-fibers), alterations in the local inflammatory milieu of the DRG washed out in our ex vivo preparation, or changes in neuronal properties that manifest at later time points during the recovery period [25].

The most striking finding of our study was the distinct molecular signature observed in the DRG of HFD-fed rats on postoperative day 4. While the spinal cord dorsal horn transcriptome showed no genes passing the FDR < 0.05 threshold, we identified 19 DEGs in the DRG, with enrichment of pathways related to metabolic and endocrine functions. These data suggest that obesity-related postoperative pain may be associated with peripheral molecular adaptations in primary sensory neurons, at least at this postoperative time point. Neuroinflammation is increasingly being recognized as a key contributor to chronic and widespread pain [26]. Previous transcriptomic work has reported changes in gene expression in the skin, muscle, and DRG after plantar incision in rats [27].

Pathway analysis of the DRG DEGs provides critical insights. The enrichment of genes involved in "lipid metabolism" and " endocrine system disorders" (Table 2) directly links the metabolic state of obesity to sensory neuron function. For instance, Rps6 (ribosomal protein S6), which was highly upregulated, is a downstream target of the mTORC1 pathway, and its kinase S6K1 has been implicated in inflammatory pain and protein synthesis-dependent sensitization [28]. Altered expression of Rps6 may reflect changes in translational activity in sensory neurons; however, functional validation is required to determine its precise role in postoperative pain modulation. Other identified genes such as Fkbp5 (a stress-responsive protein), Serpine1 (an inflammatory mediator), and Zbtb16 (also known as PLZF, which is involved in metabolism and inflammation) further support the hypothesis that a pro-inflammatory and metabolically dysregulated state within the DRG contributes to the enhanced pain phenotype [29]. These peripheral changes may lead to a state of heightened neuronal irritability or altered neuroimmune interactions within the ganglion, thus contributing to the prolonged pain state [26].

The absence of significant hyperglycemia suggests that the observed pain phenotype is not attributable to an overt diabetic pathology at this stage. This study has several limitations. First, this study did not directly assess obesity-related comorbidities such as insulin resistance or systemic inflammatory markers (e.g., cytokines), which could be important contributing factors. Second, the transcriptomic and electrophysiological analyses were performed at a single time point (day 4). A time-course analysis would provide a more dynamic view of the molecular and cellular changes. Since only male rats were included in this study, sex-dependent mechanisms were not evaluated. Finally, although our data point to a peripheral mechanism, we cannot completely exclude subtle or later-onset central changes that have not been captured by our methods. Future studies should explore the specific functional roles of the identified DEGs using genetic or pharmacological tools and investigate the temporal dynamics of both peripheral and central changes throughout the recovery period.

In conclusion, diet-induced obesity exacerbated and prolonged postoperative pain behaviors in rats. On postoperative day 4, DRG-specific transcriptional changes enriched for metabolic and endocrine pathways were observed, whereas no genes met the FDR < 0.05 threshold for differential expression in the spinal cord dorsal horn.
