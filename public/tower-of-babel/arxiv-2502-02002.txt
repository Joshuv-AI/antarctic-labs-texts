The Ball-Proximal (=“Broximal”) Point Method: a New Algorithm, Convergence Theory, and Applications
Kaja Gruntkowska, Hanmin Li, Aadi Rane ⋆, Peter Richtárik

Abstract
Non-smooth and non-convex global optimization poses significant challenges across various applications, where standard gradient-based methods often struggle. We propose the Ball-Proximal Point Method , Broximal Point Method , or Ball Point Method ( BPM ) for short – a novel algorithmic framework inspired by the classical Proximal Point Method ( PPM ) ( Rockafellar 1976 ) , which, as we show, sheds new light on several foundational optimization paradigms and phenomena, including non-convex and non-smooth optimization, acceleration, smoothing, adaptive stepsize selection, and trust-region methods. At the core of BPM lies the ball-proximal (“broximal”) operator, which arises from the classical proximal operator by replacing the quadratic distance penalty by a ball constraint. Surprisingly, and in sharp contrast with the sublinear rate of PPM in the nonsmooth convex regime, we prove that BPM converges linearly and in a finite number of steps in the same regime. Furthermore, by introducing the concept of ball-convexity, we prove that BPM retains the same global convergence guarantees under weaker assumptions, making it a powerful tool for a broader class of potentially non-convex optimization problems. Just like PPM plays the role of a conceptual method inspiring the development of practically efficient algorithms and algorithmic elements, e.g., gradient descent, adaptive step sizes, acceleration ( Ahn & Sra 2020 ) , and “W” in AdamW ( Zhuang et al. 2022 ) , we believe that BPM should be understood in the same manner: as a blueprint and inspiration for further development.



1
Introduction

The minimization of non-convex functions is a fundamental challenge across many fields, including machine learning, optimization, applied mathematics, signal processing and operations research.
Solving such problems is integral to most machine learning algorithms arising in both training and inference, where non-convex objectives or constraints are often necessary to capture complex prediction tasks.

1.1
Global nonconvex optimization

In this paper, we propose a new meta-algorithm (see
Section

1.3
) capable of finding
global
minimizers for a specific (new) class of non-convex functions.
In particular, we introduce an algorithmic framework designed to solve the (potentially non-convex) optimization problem

\displaystyle\min_{x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}}f(x),

(1)

where
f:\mathbb{R}^{d}\mapsto\mathbb{R}\cup\{+\infty\}
is assumed to be proper (which means that the set
{\rm dom}f:=\{x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}:f(x)<+\infty\}
is nonempty), closed, and have at least one minimizer.
We let
\mathcal{X}_{f}
be the set of all minimizers of
f
, and
f_{\star}:=\min_{x}f(x)
.

1.2
The ball-proximal operator

A key inspiration for our method stems from the well-known Proximal Point Method (
PPM
)
(
Rockafellar 1976
)
, which iteratively adds a quadratic penalty term to the objective (see Section
3
for more details) and solves a modified subproblem at each step.
Building on this idea, we introduce the
ball-proximal (“broximal”) operator
:

Definition 1.1

(Ball-Proximal Operator)
.

The
ball-proximal (“broximal”) operator
with radius
t>0
associated with a function
f:\mathbb{R}^{d}\mapsto\mathbb{R}\cup\{+\infty\}
is given by

\displaystyle\textstyle\textnormal{{brox}}^{t}_{f}(x):=\argmin\limits_{z\in B_{t}(x)}f(z),

(2)

where
B_{t}(x):=\left\{z\in\mathbb{R}^{d}:\left\|z-x\right\|\leq t\right\}
and
\left\|\cdot\right\|
is the standard Euclidean norm.

According to this definition, for a given input point
x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
,
\textnormal{{brox}}^{t}_{f}(x)
returns the minimizer(s) of
f
within the ball of radius
t
centered at
x
.

1.3
The Ball-Proximal Point Method

With the above definition in place, we turn to introducing our basic method, aiming to solve problem (
1
), which we refer to as the
Ball-Proximal Point Method
,
Broximal Point Method
, or simply
Ball Point Method
(
BPM
):

\displaystyle\textstyle\boxed{x_{k+1}\in\textnormal{{brox}}^{t_{k}}_{f}(x_{k})}

(
BPM
)

Similarly to the classical
PPM
, at each iteration
BPM
solves an auxiliary optimization problem – in this case, minimizing
f
over a ball centered at
x_{k}
of radius
t_{k}>0
.
The use of “
\in
” instead of “
=
” reflects the fact that
\textnormal{{brox}}^{t}_{f}(x_{k})
may not in general be a singleton (unless further assumptions are made).
In such cases, the algorithm allows the flexibility of selecting from the set of minimizers.
Notably, when this radius is large enough, i.e.,
t_{0}\geq\left\|x_{0}-x_{\star}\right\|
(where
x_{0}
is a starting point and
x_{\star}
is an optimal point), then
x_{\star}\in\textnormal{{brox}}^{t_{0}}_{f}(x_{0})
and the algorithm finds a global solution in
1
step.

1.4
Summary of contributions

Our key contributions are summarized as follows:

1.

New oracle: broximal operator.
Inspired by the classical proximal operator, we introduce the
ball-proximal (=broximal) operator
(
Section

1.2
) mapping input points to minimizers of the objective function within a localized region (a ball) centered at the input. The broximal operator enjoys several useful properties.
In certain scenarios (e.g., when
f
is convex or satisfies Assumption
A.1
), the operator is single-valued and firmly nonexpansive on
\{x:\textnormal{{brox}}^{t}_{f}(x)\not\subseteq\mathcal{X}_{f}\}

E.3
), and the minimizer is guaranteed to lie on the boundary of the ball (

and

For a full discussion of the relevant properties, see Appendices

and

. We relegate these and other auxiliary results related to the broximal operator to the appendix since in the main body of the paper we decided to focus on more high-level results, such as convergence and connections to existing works, phenomena and fields.

2.

New abstract method: Broximal Point Method.

We propose the
Broximal Point Method
(
BPM
), a novel abstract yet immensely powerful algorithmic framework (
Section

1.3
).

is inherently linked to several existing methods, and admits multiple reformulations, summarized in
Table

1
, with detailed discussions in the subsequent sections. If

1.5
Comments on practical utility

Since each step of
BPM
is itself an optimization task, it can be very challenging.
The method is therefore best understood as an
abstract procedural framework
under the broximal operator oracle, offering a foundation for a class of algorithms with elegant
global convergence guarantees
under weak assumptions.
While the
BPM
scheme may not be directly implementable, it functions as a conceptual “master” method, providing a high-level algorithmic structure that should guide the development of practical variants aiming to approximate the idealized trajectory of
BPM
’s iterates.

For example, one may
approximate
the broximal operator of
f
by
(i) the broximal operator of a suitably chosen
approximation
(e.g., linearization) of
f
(see
Section

3.2
and
Section

4
), or by (ii)
approximate
minimization of
f
over the ball by running some iterative subroutine (e.g., sampling), or
(iii) both.

This paradigm mirrors the approach used in various fields.
The simplest example is the already mentioned classical proximal operator, which is expensive to evaluate
(
Beck & Teboulle 2012
)
, yet inspires the development of practical methods
(
Parikh & Boyd 2014
)
. Another instance is Stochastic Differential Equations, where exact solutions are often unavailable, necessitating the use of numerical approximations for practical implementation
(
Kloeden & Platen 1992
)
.

2
BPM and Non-convex Optimization

The study of non-convex optimization has a rich history, with recent advances driven largely by its role in training deep neural networks.
Unlike convex problems, where global minimizers can be efficiently found
(
Nemirovski & Yudin 1983
;
Nemirovski & Nesterov 1985
;
Nesterov 2003
;
Bubeck et al. 2015
)
, solving non-convex problems is generally NP-hard
(
Murty & Kabadi 1987
)
.
This difficulty arises from the complex landscape of non-convex functions, which can have many local minima and saddle points that can trap optimization algorithms
(
Dauphin et al. 2014
;
Jin et al. 2021
)
.
Consequently, much of the research in this area has shifted focus from global optimization to more attainable goals, such as finding stationary points or local minima. However, local minima can often be far from optimal when compared to global solutions
(
Kleinberg et al. 2018
)
.

One of the key strategies to address these challenges is incorporating stochasticity into gradient-based methods
(
Kleinberg et al. 2018
;
Zhou et al. 2019a
;
Jin et al. 2021
)
, with algorithms like Stochastic Gradient Descent (
SGD
) and its variants being particularly popular for this application.

Some non-convex problems allow global optimization by exploiting structural properties of the objective function.
Examples include one-layer neural networks, where all local minima are guaranteed to be global
(
Feizi et al. 2017
;
Haeffele & Vidal 2017
)
.
It is also known that under additional assumptions,
SGD
can converge to a global minimum for linear networks
(
Danilova et al. 2022
;
Shin 2022
)
and sufficiently wide over-parameterized networks
(
Allen-Zhu et al. 2019
)
.
Beyond neural networks, classes of non-convex functions, such as weakly-quasi-convex functions and those satisfying the Polyak-Łojasiewicz condition, enjoy global convergence guarantees – with sublinear and linear rates, respectively
(
Hinder et al. 2019
;
Garrigos & Gower 2023
)
.
Local minima are also globally optimal for certain non-convex low-rank matrix problems, including matrix sensing, matrix completion, and robust PCA
(
Ge et al. 2017
)
.

New approach to non-convex optimization.

Recent research has made significant progress in non-convex optimization, providing theoretical guarantees for convergence to stationary points and, in some cases, even global minima.
However, the field remains relatively under-explored, with substantial room for innovation.
To extend this line of research, we consider a new approach that goes beyond merely finding stationary points, focusing on methods capable of escaping local minima.
By design,
BPM
demonstrates this ability if the radius
t_{k}
is chosen large enough, as illustrated in a simple experiment (
Figure

).

).
Additionally, numerical experiments (
Appendix

Sharpness-aware minimization.

“Idealized” sharpness-aware minimization (
SAM
) performs the iteration

x_{k+1}=x_{k}-\eta_{k}\nabla f(z_{k+1}),

(3)

where
z_{k+1}:=\arg\max_{z\in B_{\rho}(x_{k})}f(z)
, which is typically approximated through linearization by
\tilde{z}_{k+1}:=\arg\max_{z\in B_{\rho}(x_{k})}\left\{f(x_{k})+\langle\nabla f(x_{k}),z-x_{k}\rangle\right\}=x_{k}+\rho\tfrac{\nabla f(x_{k})}{\|\nabla f(x_{k})\|}

(
Foret et al. 2021
)
. This leads to the practical
SAM
method

x_{k+1}=x_{k}-\eta_{k}\nabla f\left(x_{k}+\rho\tfrac{\nabla f(x_{k})}{\|\nabla f(x_{k})\|}\right).

Note that (
3
) is structurally similar to the
\|
PPM
\|
reformulation of
BPM
(see
Table

1
); the key difference being that
z_{k+1}
in
SAM
arises from
maximizing

f
over a ball, while
BPM
employs
minimization
. We believe that exploring these similarities may lead to novel insights about
SAM
.

3
BPM and Non-smooth Optimization

Standard gradient-based methods heavily depend on the smoothness of the objective function. However, many real-world problems lack this property, making non-smooth optimization a critical challenge arising in a wide range of applications, such as sparse learning, robust regression, and deep learning
(
Shamir & Zhang 2013
;
Zhang et al. 2019
)
. A classic example is the support vector machine problem, where using the standard hinge loss makes the objective function non-smooth.
From a practical perspective, even when the objective is smooth, its smoothness constant – commonly used to determine hyperparameters like the step size – is often unknown.
From a theoretical standpoint, the analysis of non-smooth problems typically differs significantly from the smooth case, requiring different tools and techniques.
To effectively address non-smooth problems, several strategies have been developed.
Two notable approaches in this domain are proximal-type updates and normalized gradient methods, both of which reveal a profound connection to the framework we propose.

3.1
Proximal Point Method.

Proximal algorithms
(
Rockafellar 1976
)
are a cornerstone of optimization.
They are powerful since their convergence does not rely on the smoothness of the objective function
(
Richtárik et al. 2024
)
.
This property makes them especially attractive for deep learning applications, where loss functions often lack smoothness
(
Zhang et al. 2019
)
.
Central to these methods is the
proximal operator
, defined as

\displaystyle\textstyle\textnormal{{prox}}_{f}(x):=\underset{z\in\mathbb{R}^{d}}{\arg\min}\left\{f(z)+\frac{1}{2}\cdot\left\|z-x\right\|^{2}\right\},

where
f:\mathbb{R}^{d}\to\mathbb{R}\cup\{+\infty\}
is an extended real-valued function.
It is known that if
f
is a proper, closed and convex function, then
\textnormal{{prox}}_{f}(x)
is a singleton for all
x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}

(
Bauschke et al. 2011
;
Beck 2017
)
. Furthermore, if
f
is differentiable, then for any
\gamma>0
the proximal operator satisfies the equivalence

\displaystyle\textstyle z=\textnormal{{prox}}_{\gamma f}(x)\quad\iff\quad z+\gamma\nabla f(z)=x.

(4)

The simplest proximal algorithm is the Proximal Point Method (
PPM
)
(
Moreau 1965
;
Martinet 1970
)
.
Originally introduced to address problems involving variational inequalities,
PPM
has since been adapted to stochastic settings to address the challenges of large-scale optimization. This led to the development of Stochastic Proximal Point Methods (
SPPM
), which have been extensively studied and refined over time
(
Bertsekas 2011
;
Khaled & Jin 2022
;
Anyszka et al. 2024
;
Li & Richtárik 2024
)
.

In its simplest form, the update rule of
PPM
is

\displaystyle\textstyle x_{k+1}=\textnormal{{prox}}_{\gamma_{k}f}(x_{k})

(
PPM
)

for some step size
\gamma_{k}>0
.
Using the equivalence given in (
4
), the above expression can be rewritten as

\displaystyle\textstyle x_{k+1}=x_{k}-\gamma_{k}\nabla f(x_{k+1}).

(5)

While similar in form to
GD
, a key distinction lies in the implicit nature of the update: the gradient is evaluated at the
new
iterate

x_{k+1}
.
This implicitness provides enhanced stability in practice
(
Ryu & Boyd 2016
)
.

As shown in the following lemma,
BPM
and
PPM
share a deep connection that goes beyond their similar names.

Lemma 3.1
.

Let
f:\mathbb{R}^{d}\to\mathbb{R}
be a differentiable convex function, and let
x_{k+1}=\textnormal{{brox}}^{t_{k}}_{f}(x_{k})
be the iterates of
BPM
. Provided that
x_{k+1}
is not optimal,

\displaystyle\textstyle\boxed{x_{k+1}=\textnormal{{prox}}_{\frac{t_{k}}{\left\|\nabla f(x_{k+1})\right\|}f}(x_{k})}

Consequently, for differentiable objectives, the update rule of
BPM
becomes

\displaystyle\textstyle\boxed{x_{k+1}=x_{k}-\tfrac{t_{k}}{\left\|\nabla f(x_{k+1})\right\|}\cdot\nabla f(x_{k+1})}

(see
Theorem

4.1
), which can be interpreted as
normalized

PPM
(
\|
PPM
\|
).
Hence, computing broximal operator is equivalent to evaluating the proximal operator with a carefully chosen, adaptive step size.
Alternatively, computing the proximal operator of a convex function can be viewed as finding the point on the sphere of radius
t=\gamma\left\|\nabla f(\textnormal{{prox}}_{\gamma f}(x))\right\|
centered at
x
that minimizes the function value (Lemma
F.1
).
Building on this reformulation, the
PPM
update rule can be expressed as

\displaystyle\textstyle x_{k+1}=\textnormal{{prox}}_{\gamma_{k}f}(x_{k})=\underset{z\in B_{t_{k}}(x_{k})}{\arg\min}f(z),

where
t_{k}=\gamma_{k}\left\|\nabla f(\textnormal{{prox}}_{\gamma_{k}f}(x_{k}))\right\|
.
This highlights the motivation for
BPM
, which originates from using alternative choices for the radius sequence
\left\{t_{k}\right\}_{k\geq 0}
.

3.2
Normalized gradient descent

Normalized Gradient Descent (
\|
GD
\|
) is another popular approach for non-smooth optimization, particularly useful when gradient norms provide little information about the appropriate choice of the step size.
Originally introduced by
Nesterov 1984
for differentiable quasi-convex objectives, it was later extended by
Kiwiel 2001
to include upper semi-continuous quasi-convex functions, and analyzed in the stochastic setting by
Hazan et al. 2015
.
To illustrate the intuition behind it, consider the simple example
f(x)=|x|
.
In this case, the gradient norm is
1
everywhere except the optimum, offering no guidance on the right choice of the step size.
Normalization addresses this issue by removing the influence of the gradient norm, while preserving the descent direction (negative gradient).
Beyond handling non-smoothness,
\|
GD
\|
has demonstrated superior performance in non-convex settings, escaping saddle points more effectively than standard
GD

(
Murray et al. 2019
)
.

Although normalization is intuitively justified, a rigorous explanation for its use has been missing.
It is well-established that applying
PPM
to the linear approximation of
f
at the current iterate yields the update rule of
GD
.
However, an analogous result connecting
\|
GD
\|
to a principled framework has yet to be established.
Interestingly, such a result can be derived by adopting a similar approach, replacing
PPM
with
BPM
.

Theorem 3.2
.

Define
f_{k}(z):=f(x_{k})+\left\langle\nabla f(x_{k}),z-x_{k}\right\rangle
and let
x_{k+1}=\textnormal{{brox}}^{t_{k}}_{f_{k}}(x_{k})
be the iterates of
BPM
applied to the first-order approximation of
f
at the current iterate.
Then, the update rule is equivalent to

\displaystyle\textstyle x_{k+1}=x_{k}-\frac{t_{k}}{\left\|\nabla f(x_{k})\right\|}\cdot\nabla f(x_{k}).

(6)

Just as
GD
naturally arises from
PPM
, we show that
\|
GD
\|
follows directly from the mechanics of
BPM
.
This establishes normalization as an intrinsic property of the broximal operator, rather than a mere heuristic, providing a robust theoretical foundation for
\|
GD
\|
and validating
BPM
’s design and applications.

4
BPM and Acceleration

When the objective
f
belongs to a certain function class (including convex functions, and defined in Section
A
), the result in
Lemma

3.1
still holds, allowing for the derivation of an explicit update rule for
BPM
.

Theorem 4.1
.

Let
f:\mathbb{R}^{d}\to\mathbb{R}
be a differentiable function satisfying
A.1
. Let
x_{k+1}=\textnormal{{brox}}^{t_{k}}_{f}(x_{k})
be the iterates of
BPM
. Provided that
x_{k+1}
is not optimal,

\displaystyle\textstyle x_{k+1}=x_{k}-\frac{t_{k}}{\left\|\nabla f(x_{k+1})\right\|}\cdot\nabla f(x_{k+1}).

(7)

The first observation is the similarity between (
6
) and (
7
).
However, the above update rule is
doubly implicit
, as both the gradient and the effective step size depend on the next iterate
x_{k+1}
.
A similar doubly implicit structure arises in
p
-th order proximal point methods
(
Nesterov 2023
)
.
In particular, the
p
-th order proximal operator is defined as

\displaystyle\textstyle\textnormal{{prox}}^{p}_{\gamma f}(x):=\underset{z\in\mathbb{R}^{d}}{\arg\min}\left\{\gamma f(z)+\frac{1}{(p+1)}\cdot\left\|z-x\right\|^{p+1}\right\}.

The corresponding
p
-th order Proximal Point Method (
PPM
p
) iterates

\displaystyle\textstyle x_{k+1}=\textnormal{{prox}}^{p}_{\gamma f}(x_{k}),

(
PPM
p
)

which can be reformulated (see
Theorem

G.1
) as

\displaystyle\textstyle x_{k+1}=x_{k}-\left(\frac{\gamma}{\left\|\nabla f(x_{k+1})\right\|^{p-1}}\right)^{\nicefrac{{1}}{{p}}}\cdot\nabla f(x_{k+1}).

(8)

A notable feature of higher-order proximal methods is their accelerated convergence rate of
\mathcal{O}(\nicefrac{{1}}{{K^{p}}})
for convex objective functions.

BPM
can achieve the same accelerated rate by carefully selecting the radius
t_{k}
of the ball.
Specifically, choosing
t_{k}=\left(\gamma\left\|\nabla f(x_{k+1})\right\|\right)^{1/p}
leads to the update rule

\displaystyle\textstyle x_{k+1}=\textnormal{{brox}}^{t_{k}}_{f}(x)\overset{\eqref{lemma:brox_prox}}{=}x_{k}-\frac{t_{k}}{\left\|\nabla f(x_{k+1})\right\|}\cdot\nabla f(x_{k+1}),

which aligns with that of
PPM
p
in (
8
), enabling
BPM
to inherit the favorable convergence properties of higher-order proximal methods (
Theorem

G.2
).

BPM
can also achieve acceleration in the classical Nesterov sense, drawing on the work of
Ahn & Sra 2020
, who interpret the Accelerated Gradient Method (
AGM
) as an approximation of
PPM
.
Specifically, let
\{y_{k}\}_{k\geq 0}
be an auxiliary sequence, define
l_{y}(x):=f(y)+\left\langle\nabla f(y),x-y\right\rangle
,
u_{y}(x):=f(y)+\left\langle\nabla f(y),x-y\right\rangle+\frac{L}{2}\left\|x-y\right\|^{2}
, and fix
x_{0}=y_{0}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
.
Now, consider the algorithm

\displaystyle\begin{split}\textstyle x_{k+1}&=\textnormal{{brox}}^{t^{x}_{k+1}}_{l_{y_{k}}}(x_{k}),\\
\textstyle y_{k+1}&=\textnormal{{brox}}^{t^{y}_{k+1}}_{u_{y_{k}}}(x_{k+1}),\end{split}

(
A-BPM
)

where

\displaystyle t^{x}_{k+1}

\displaystyle:=\textstyle\frac{k+1}{2L}\left\|\nabla l_{y_{k}}(\textnormal{{prox}}_{\left(\nicefrac{{k+1}}{{2L}}\right)l_{y_{k}}}(x_{k}))\right\|,

\displaystyle t^{y}_{k+1}

\displaystyle:=\textstyle\frac{k+1}{2L}\left\|\nabla u_{y_{k}}(\textnormal{{prox}}_{\left(\nicefrac{{k+1}}{{2L}}\right)u_{y_{k}}}(x_{k+1}))\right\|.

The convergence guarantee of the method is characterized in the theorem below.

Theorem 4.2
.

Let
f:\mathbb{R}^{d}\to\mathbb{R}
be convex and
L
–smooth. Then the iterates of
A-BPM
satisfy
f(x_{K})-f_{\star}\leq\frac{2Ld_{0}^{2}}{K(K+1)}
.

Consequently, we recover the well-known
\mathcal{O}\left(\nicefrac{{1}}{{K^{2}}}\right)
accelerated convergence rate of
AGM
.

5
BPM and Smoothing

The proximal operator is closely related to the Moreau envelope
(
Moreau 1965
)
, also known as the Moreau-Yosida regularization.
It is well-established that running proximal algorithms on the original objective
f
is equivalent to applying gradient methods to its Moreau envelope
(
Ryu & Boyd 2016
)
.
A key observation is that the algorithm’s effectiveness is preserved, as the minima of the original objective and the Moreau envelope coincide
(
Planiden & Wang 2016
;
Planiden & Wang 2019
;
Li et al. 2024a
)
.
The Moreau envelope has applications beyond proximal minimization algorithms, finding use in areas such as personalized federated learning
(
Dinh et al. 2020
)
and meta-learning
(
Mishchenko et al. 2023
)
.

5.1
Ball envelope and normalized gradient descent

Analogously, we define a concept of an envelope function associated with the ball-proximal operator.

Definition 5.1

(Ball envelope)
.

The
ball envelope
with radius
t>0
of
f:\mathbb{R}^{d}\mapsto\mathbb{R}\cup\{+\infty\}
is given by

\displaystyle\textstyle{N}^{t}_{f}\left(x\right):=\min\limits_{z\in B_{t}(x)}f(z).

(9)

The ball envelope has several interesting properties and enables theoretical insights into the behavior of the broximal operator and its applications.
One noteworthy observation is the relationship between the sets of minimizers of
f
and its ball envelope.
Specifically, it turns out that
\mathcal{X}_{N}=\left\{x:\textnormal{dist}\left(x,\mathcal{X}_{f}\right)\leq t\right\}=\mathcal{X}_{f}+B_{t}(0)
, where
\mathcal{X}_{f}
and
\mathcal{X}_{N}
are the sets of minimizers of
f
and
N_{f}^{t}
, respectively (further details can be found in
Section

H.1
).
This key observation enables us to interpret
BPM
applied to
f
as
GD
on the ball envelope
N^{t}_{f}
, analogous to the interpretation of
PPM
on
f
as
GD
on the Moreau envelope (which is known for its “smoothing” properties).

As in the standard proximal setting, the result requires a smoothness assumption.
Recall that a differentiable function
f:\mathbb{R}^{d}\mapsto\mathbb{R}
is
L
–smooth if

\displaystyle\textstyle f(x)-f(y)-\left\langle\nabla f(y),x-y\right\rangle\leq\frac{L}{2}\left\|x-y\right\|^{2}\,\forall x,y\in\mathbb{R}^{d}.

With the assumptions set, we can state the equivalence result.

Theorem 5.2
.

Let
f:\mathbb{R}^{d}\to\mathbb{R}
be convex and
L
–smooth, and let
x_{k+1}=\textnormal{{brox}}^{t_{k}}_{f}(x_{k})
be the iterates of
BPM
. Provided that
x_{k+1}
is not optimal,

\displaystyle\textstyle\boxed{x_{k+1}=x_{k}-\tfrac{t_{k}}{\|\nabla N^{t_{k}}_{f}(x_{k})\|}\cdot\nabla{N}^{t_{k}}_{f}\left(x_{k}\right)}

(10)

Therefore,
BPM
can be viewed as normalized gradient descent (
\|
GD
\|
) on the ball envelope.

6
BPM and Adaptive Step Sizes

In gradient-based methods, selecting an appropriate step size is a notoriously challenging problem: choosing a step size that is too small results in slow convergence, while a step size that is too large risks divergence.
This challenge has driven significant research into the development of adaptive methods that adjust the learning rate dynamically based on algorithm history
(
Polyak 1987
;
Bach & Levy 2019
;
Malitsky & Mishchenko 2019
;
Horváth et al. 2022
;
Yang & Ma 2023
)
. However, all of these algorithms come with inherent limitations and trade-offs.

A similar dilemma arises in trust-region methods
(
Conn et al. 2000
;
Nocedal & Wright 2006
)
.
If the trust-region radius is too small, progress is slow; if it is too large, the model function may fail to approximate the objective accurately, potentially resulting in an increase in the function value at the next iterate
x_{k+1}
.
To address this, trust-region methods typically rely on heuristic rules to modify the radius, adjusting it up or down based on predefined criteria.

Proximal methods offer a different approach. They can, in theory, achieve convergence in a single step if the step size
\gamma
is sufficiently large
(
Güler 1991
)
. However, this advantage hinges on the assumption that the proximal operator is computationally easy to evaluate.
In practice, each proximal step involves solving a nested optimization problem, which is often computationally expensive, making proximal methods more conceptual than practical for many applications.

BPM
preserves the desirable properties of proximal methods while facing similar computational challenges.
Similar to
PPM
– which, as shown in Lemma
3.1
, is a special case of
BPM
– it retains the ability to converge in a single step provided that a sufficiently large step size
t
is used (
Section

8
). However, just like
PPM
, it inherits the drawback that solving the local optimization subproblem can be computationally challenging in general.

To gain a clearer insight into the step size sequence generated by
BPM
, let us examine the setting where
f
is differentiable. As demonstrated in
Theorem

4.1
, the algorithm can then be interpreted as normalized
PPM
with the step size
\frac{t_{k}}{\left\|\nabla f(x_{k+1})\right\|}
. In the smooth setting,
BPM
can be further expressed as
GD
on the ball envelope with the adaptive step size given by

\displaystyle\textstyle\gamma_{k,t}^{E}:=\frac{t_{k}}{\left\|\nabla N^{t}_{f}\left(x_{k}\right)\right\|}\overset{\eqref{lemma:N3}}{=}\frac{t_{k}}{\left\|\nabla f(x_{k+1})\right\|}

(11)

(
Theorem

5.2
).
Unlike traditional methods, where step sizes decrease over time
(
Robbins & Monro 1951
)
, the step size sequence of
BPM
is
increasing
even if the radius
t_{k}
is fixed across iterations (i.e.,
t_{k}\equiv t>0
; see
Theorem

8.1
(v)).
To better understand the implications, we compare the step size
\gamma_{k,t}^{E}
in (
11
) with the classic Polyak step size
(
Polyak 1987
)
. The Polyak step size for
GD
applied to the ball envelope is defined as

\displaystyle\textstyle\gamma_{k}^{P}:=\frac{{N}^{t_{k}}_{f}\left(x_{k}\right)-{N}^{t_{k}}_{f}\left(x_{\star}\right)}{\left\|\nabla{N}^{t_{k}}_{f}\left(x_{k}\right)\right\|^{2}}\overset{\eqref{lemma:N3},\eqref{lemma:N4}}{=}\frac{f(x_{k+1})-f_{\star}}{\left\|\nabla f(x_{k+1})\right\|^{2}},

(12)

which corresponds to the Polyak step size for the original objective
f
evaluated at the next iterate.

Comparing (
11
) and (
12
), we see that the relationship between
\gamma_{k,t}^{E}
and
\gamma_{k}^{P}
is determined by the interplay between
t_{k}
and
\nicefrac{{(f(x_{k+1})-f_{\star})}}{{\left\|\nabla f(x_{k+1})\right\|}}
. In particular, choosing
t_{k}=\nicefrac{{(f(x_{k+1})-f_{\star})}}{{\left\|\nabla f(x_{k+1})\right\|}}
recovers the Polyak step size exactly.
Furthermore, when
f
is
L
–smooth and
\mu
–strongly convex, the Polyak step size is uniformly bounded above and below by

\displaystyle\textstyle\frac{1}{2L}\leq\frac{f(x_{k+1})-f_{\star}}{\left\|\nabla f(x_{k+1})\right\|^{2}}\leq\frac{1}{2\mu}.

Under the same conditions, we also have

\displaystyle\textstyle\frac{t_{k}}{L\left\|x_{k+1}-x_{\star}\right\|}\leq\frac{t_{k}}{\left\|\nabla f(x_{k+1})\right\|}\leq\frac{t_{k}}{\mu\left\|x_{k+1}-x_{\star}\right\|}.

These bounds coincide for
t_{k}=\nicefrac{{\left\|x_{k+1}-x_{\star}\right\|}}{{2}}
.
On the other hand, if the step size
t_{k}\equiv t
is kept constant,
BPM
can initially take smaller steps, but as the iterates approach the solution, the lower bound on
\gamma_{k,t}^{E}
increases and can eventually surpass the upper bound associated with the Polyak step size, resulting in
BPM
taking larger steps.

7
BPM and Trust Region Methods

Trust region methods represent another complementary connection to our work.
These methods trace their origins to
Levenberg 1944
, who introduced a modified Gauss-Newton method to solve nonlinear least squares problems.
Their widespread recognition followed the influential work of
Marquardt 1963
.
At their core, trust region methods minimize the function
f
by iteratively approximating it within a neighborhood around the current iterate, referred to as the
trust region
, using a model
m_{k}(x)
(e.g., a quadratic approximation)
(
Conn et al. 2000
)
.
The trust region is typically defined as
B_{t_{k}}(x_{k}):=\left\{z\in\mathbb{R}^{d}:\left\|z-x_{k}\right\|\leq t_{k}\right\}
, where
t_{k}
is the
trust-region radius
.
1
1

1

Alternatively, more complex and problem-specific trust regions, like ellipsoidal or box-shaped ones, could be used.

The next iterate is determined by solving the constrained optimization problem

\displaystyle\textstyle x_{k+1}=\underset{z\in B_{t_{k}}(x_{k})}{\arg\min}m_{k}(x_{k}),

after which the radius is adjusted, and the process is repeated.
While intuitive and reasonable, this approach is not conceptually aligned with the abstraction level of
GD
, which can be interpreted as minimization of a quadratic upper bound on
f
, applied without constraints.
Why, then, should such constraints arise in trust region methods?

BPM
provides a new perspective, elevating trust region methods to a more principled framework.
It functions as the
PPM
of trust region methods, naturally explaining the emergence of neighborhoods in the optimization process.
Traditional trust region methods rely on
approximate
models and introduce constraints to compensate for their limitations – since local approximations of
f
become less reliable farther from
x_{k}
, the trust-region radius must be continuously adjusted to maintain accuracy. Within this framework,
t_{k}
acts as a control mechanism for approximation quality.
In contrast,
BPM
can be considered a “supercharged” version of these methods, where the model is assumed to be perfect, eliminating the need for radius adjustment when optimizing directly on
f
.
By interpreting
BPM
as a “master” trust region method, we can unify the two approaches at a higher level of abstraction.
In this context, trust regions emerge naturally – not as a heuristic, but as an inherent component of the optimization process.

Although this paper does not focus on trust region methods explicitly,
BPM
serves as a conceptual bridge to that field.
By presenting a globally convergent framework without approximation, our approach lays the groundwork for advancing the theory and practice of trust region methods.

8
Convergence Theory: Convex Case

We first analyze the algorithm assuming that the objective function is convex.
2
2

2

Extension to the nonconvex regime is presented in
Appendix

A
.

In this setting, the broximal operator has several favorable properties.
For example,
\textnormal{{brox}}^{t}_{f}(x)
is always a singleton and lies on the boundary of
B_{t}(x)
unless
\textnormal{{brox}}^{t}_{f}(x)\subseteq\mathcal{X}_{f}
, meaning that the algorithm has reached the set of global minimizers.
In other words, at each iteration,
BPM
moves from
x_{k}
to a new point
x_{k+1}
located on the boundary of
B_{t_{k}}(x_{k})
, effectively traveling a distance of
t_{k}
at each step (possibly except for the very last iteration). Thus, we sometimes refer to the radius
t_{k}
as the
step size
. Let
d_{k}:=\left\|x_{k}-x_{\star}\right\|
.

The following theorem presents the main results.

Theorem 8.1
.

Assume that
f:\mathbb{R}^{d}\mapsto\mathbb{R}\cup\{+\infty\}
is proper, closed and convex, and let
\{x_{k}\}_{k\geq 0}
be the iterates of
BPM
run with any sequence of positive radii
\{t_{k}\}_{k\geq 0}
, where
x_{0}\in{\rm dom}f
. Then

(i)

If
\mathcal{X}_{f}\cap B_{t_{k}}(x_{k})\neq\emptyset
, then
x_{k+1}
is optimal.

(ii)

If
\mathcal{X}_{f}\cap B_{t_{k}}(x_{k})=\emptyset
, then
\left\|x_{k+1}-x_{k}\right\|=t_{k}
. Moreover, for any
x_{\star}\in\mathcal{X}_{f}
, we have

\displaystyle\left\|x_{k+1}-x_{\star}\right\|^{2}\leq\left\|x_{k}-x_{\star}\right\|-t_{k}^{2},

\displaystyle\textnormal{dist}^{2}(x_{k+1},\mathcal{X}_{f})\leq\textnormal{dist}^{2}(x_{k},\mathcal{X}_{f})-t_{k}^{2}.

(iii)

If
\sum_{k=0}^{K-1}t_{k}^{2}\geq\textnormal{dist}^{2}(x_{0},\mathcal{X}_{f})
, then
x_{K}\in\mathcal{X}_{f}
.

(iv)

For any
k\geq 0
,

\displaystyle\textstyle f(x_{k+1})-f_{\star}\leq\left(1+\frac{t_{k}}{\left\|x_{k+1}-x_{\star}\right\|}\right)^{-1}\left(f(x_{k})-f_{\star}\right).

(v)

If
f
is differentiable, then
\left\|\nabla f(x_{k+1})\right\|\leq\left\|\nabla f(x_{k})\right\|
for all
k\geq 0
, and

\displaystyle\textstyle\sum_{k=0}^{K-1}\left(\frac{t_{k}}{\sum_{k=0}^{K-1}t_{k}}\left\|\nabla f(x_{k+1})\right\|\right)\leq\frac{f(x_{0})-f_{\star}}{\sum_{k=0}^{K-1}t_{k}}.

Proof sketch.

The complete proof of
Theorem

8.1
is presented in
Appendix

D
. Here, we provide a brief sketch of its final part to emphasize the main ideas underlying the argument.
Let us consider some iteration
k
such that
x_{k+1}\not\in\mathcal{X}_{f}
(otherwise, the problem is solved in
1
step).
We start the proof by invoking
Theorem

D.2
, stating that

\displaystyle f(y)-f(u)\geq c_{t}(x)\left\langle x-u,y-u\right\rangle

(13)

for some
c_{t}(x)\geq 0
,
u\in\textnormal{{brox}}^{t}_{f}(x)
and all
y\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
.
Substituting
y=x=x_{k}
and
t=t_{k}
, we can bound
f(x_{k+1})-f_{\star}
by

f(x_{k})-f_{\star}-c_{t_{k}}(x_{k})\left\|x_{k+1}-x_{k}\right\|^{2}.

Next, applying the same inequality with
x=x_{k}
,
y=x_{\star}\in\mathcal{X}_{f}
, and using the Cauchy-Schwarz inequality, we obtain

\displaystyle f(x_{k+1})-f_{\star}\leq c_{t_{k}}(x_{k})\left\|x_{k}-x_{k+1}\right\|\left\|x_{k+1}-x_{\star}\right\|.

Since
x_{k+1}\not\in\mathcal{X}_{f}
, it follows that

\displaystyle\textstyle\left(f(x_{k+1})-f_{\star}\right)\frac{\left\|x_{k}-x_{k+1}\right\|}{\left\|x_{k+1}-x_{\star}\right\|}\leq c_{t_{k}}(x_{k})\left\|x_{k}-x_{k+1}\right\|^{2}.

Applying this bound and using the fact that
\left\|x_{k}-x_{k+1}\right\|=t_{k}
, we rearrange the terms to obtain (iv).
∎

Corollary 8.2
.

Let the assumptions of
Theorem

8.1
hold. Then, for any
K\geq 1
, the iterates of
BPM
satisfy

\displaystyle\textstyle f(x_{K})-f_{\star}\leq\prod\limits_{k=0}^{K-1}\left(1+\frac{t_{k}}{d_{0}}\right)^{-1}\left(f(x_{0})-f_{\star}\right).

(14)

Several important observations are in order:

X
\bullet

Large step sizes travel far.
Note that
Theorem

8.1
holds without any upper bound on the radii. Therefore,
BPM
converges even after a single iteration provided that the radius
t_{0}
is large enough:
t_{0}\geq\textnormal{dist}(x_{0},\mathcal{X}_{f})
.

X

\bullet

Finite convergence.
If we fix the radii sequence to be constant, i.e., if
t_{k}\equiv t>0
, then
(iii)
implies convergence to the
exact optimum
in a
finite number of steps
. Indeed,
Kt^{2}=\sum_{k=0}^{K-1}t^{2}\geq\textnormal{dist}^{2}(x_{0},\mathcal{X}_{f})
holds for
K=\left\lceil\nicefrac{{\textnormal{dist}^{2}(x_{0},\mathcal{X}_{f})}}{{t^{2}}}\right\rceil.
This is in stark contrast to proximal methods, which never reach the exact solution.

X

\bullet

Linear convergence without smoothness nor strong convexity
. Surprisingly, inequality (
14
) posits linear convergence of
BPM
without assuming smoothness nor strong convexity (or any relaxation thereof, such as the PŁ condition, which normally leads to linear convergence
(
Karimi et al. 2016
)
).

Remark 8.3
.

Under the assumptions of
Theorem

8.1
, the iterates of
BPM
with a fixed step size
t_{k}\equiv t>0
satisfy

\displaystyle\textstyle f(x_{K})-f_{\star}\leq\frac{2d_{0}}{2d_{0}+t}\cdot\frac{d_{0}^{2}}{2Kt^{2}}\left(f(x_{0})-f_{\star}\right)

(see
Theorem

D.3
).
This bound outperforms (
14
) when
K
is small and
t
is large (
K\in\{1,2,3\}
and
t\approx\left\|x_{0}-x_{\star}\right\|
).
However, such a choice of
t

Convergence Theory: Beyond Convexity

Convex geometry offers valuable insights into the properties of the objective function, enabling the design of efficient algorithms for finding globally optimal solutions. Consequently, many optimization methods rely on the convexity assumption to provide theoretical guarantees. However, many problems of practical interest involve functions that fail to be convex, while still retaining certain structural similarities to convex functions
(
Kleinberg et al. 2018
;
Hardt et al. 2018
;
Zhou et al. 2019b
)
. This motivates the search for broader function classes for which theoretical convergence results can still be provided.

In this section, we introduce
ball-convexity
, a relaxed notion that extends standard convexity while maintaining sufficient structure to enable theoretical analysis. We demonstrate how this property preserves key inequalities used in our proofs, allowing us to extend the convergence guarantees of
BPM
beyond the convex regime.

The proof of
Theorem

8.1
heavily relies on inequality (
13
). It turns out that such an inequality holds beyond the convex case, and the method can be analyzed based exclusively on this weaker condition. Motivated by this, we introduce the following assumption:

Assumption A.1

(
B_{t}
–convexity)
.

A proper function
f:\mathbb{R}^{d}\to\mathbb{R}\cup\{+\infty\}
is said to be
B_{t}
–convex
if there exists a function
c_{t}:\mathbb{R}^{d}\to\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}
such that for all
x\in{\rm dom}f
,

\displaystyle f(y)\geq f(u)+c_{t}(x)\left\langle x-u,y-u\right\rangle

(15)

for any
u\in\textnormal{{brox}}^{t}_{f}(x)
and for all
y\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
.

When referencing
A.1
without specifying a particular value of
t
, we refer to it as
ball-convexity
.
While inequality (
15
) always holds for convex
f
(see
Theorem

D.2
), ball-convexity extends beyond traditional convexity, as demonstrated in the following example.

Example 1
.

Consider the function
f:\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R}
defined via

\displaystyle f(x)=\begin{cases}-x-1&x\leq-1\\
x+1&-1<x\leq 0\\
-x+1&0<x\leq 1\\
x-1&x>1.\end{cases}

The function is clearly non-convex.
However, taking
t=1
for simplicity, one can show that

\displaystyle\textnormal{{brox}}^{t}_{f}(x)=\begin{cases}x+1&x<-2\\
\{-1\}&-2\leq x<0\\
\{-1,1\}&x=0\\
\{1\}&0<x\leq 2\\
x-1&x>2\end{cases}

and Assumption
A.1
holds with

\displaystyle c_{t}(x)=\begin{cases}1&|x|>2,\\
0&|x|\leq 2.\end{cases}

Furthermore, the example illustrates that for functions satisfying
A.1
, the set of global minima may not be a singleton, and it need not be connected. Additionally, the mapping
x\mapsto\textnormal{{brox}}^{t}_{f}(x)
is not necessarily single-valued on the set
\{x:\textnormal{{brox}}^{t}_{f}(x)\subseteq\mathcal{X}_{f}\}
.

The class of ball-convex functions is broader than that of convex functions, yet the broximal operator preserves all its desirable properties for this extended family of objectives. Notably, the convergence guarantees in
Theorem

8.1
and
Remark

8.3
hold unchanged.
Interestingly,
BPM
retains the linear convergence rate under even weaker assumptions (see
Section

E.1
). However, this comes at the cost of the broximal operator losing some of its favorable properties.

The formal statements and proofs of these results can be found in
Appendix

E
, where we provide more information about the function class defined by
A.1
, analyze the properties of the broximal operator, and establish the convergence guarantees.

.
Then

\displaystyle\left\langle\nabla\phi(a)-\nabla\phi(b),c-d\right\rangle=D_{\phi}\left(c,b\right)+D_{\phi}\left(d,a\right)-D_{\phi}\left(c,a\right)-D_{\phi}\left(d,b\right).

Lemma C.4

(Subdifferential of indicator function)
.

The subdifferential of an indicator function of a set
\mathcal{X}\neq\emptyset
at a point
y\in\mathcal{X}
is

\displaystyle\partial\delta_{\mathcal{X}}(y)=\mathcal{N}_{\mathcal{X}}(y):=\left\{g\in\mathbb{R}^{d}:\left\langle g,z-y\right\rangle\leq 0\,\forall z\in\mathcal{X}\right\},

where
\mathcal{N}_{\mathcal{X}}(y)
is the normal cone of
\mathcal{X}
at
y
.

Proof.

For
y\notin\mathcal{X}
,
\partial\delta_{\mathcal{X}}(y)=\emptyset
. When
y\in\mathcal{X}
, by definition of subdifferential,
g\in\partial\delta_{\mathcal{X}}(y)
if and only if

\displaystyle\delta_{\mathcal{X}}(z)\geq\delta_{\mathcal{X}}(y)+\left\langle g,z-y\right\rangle\qquad\forall z\in\mathcal{X},

which is equivalent to
\left\langle g,z-y\right\rangle\leq 0
for all
z\in\mathcal{X}
.
∎

Lemma C.5

(Normal cone of the indicator function of a ball)
.

The normal cone of a ball
B_{t}(x)
is

\displaystyle\mathcal{N}_{B_{t}(x)}(y)=\begin{cases}\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}(y-x)&\left\|x-y\right\|=t,\\
\{0\}&\left\|x-y\right\|<t,\\
\emptyset&\left\|x-y\right\|>t.\end{cases}

Proof.

For
y\notin B_{t}(x)
,
\mathcal{N}_{B_{t}(x)}(y)=\emptyset
.
Now, let
y\in B_{t}(x)
. Then

\displaystyle g\in\partial\delta_{B_{t}(x)}(y)\qquad

\displaystyle\overset{\eqref{lemma:subfiff_id}}{\iff}\qquad\left\langle g,z-y\right\rangle\leq 0\quad\forall z\in B_{t}(x)

\displaystyle\iff\qquad\left\langle g,z\right\rangle\leq\left\langle g,y\right\rangle\quad\forall z\in B_{t}(x)

\displaystyle\iff\qquad\sup_{z:\left\|z-x\right\|\leq t}\left\langle g,z\right\rangle\leq\left\langle g,y\right\rangle

\displaystyle\iff\qquad\sup_{z:\left\|\frac{z-x}{t}\right\|\leq 1}\left\langle g,\frac{z-x}{t}\right\rangle\leq\left\langle g,\frac{y-x}{t}\right\rangle

\displaystyle\iff\qquad\sup_{z:\left\|w\right\|\leq 1}\left\langle g,w\right\rangle\leq\left\langle g,\frac{y-x}{t}\right\rangle

\displaystyle\iff\qquad\left\|g\right\|\leq\frac{\left\langle g,y-x\right\rangle}{t}.

On the other hand, Cauchy-Schwarz inequality gives

\displaystyle t\left\|g\right\|\leq\left\langle g,y-x\right\rangle\leq\left\|g\right\|\left\|y-x\right\|,

(17)

meaning that

\displaystyle 0\leq\left\|g\right\|\left(\left\|y-x\right\|-t\right).

Since
y\in B_{t}(x)
,
\left\|y-x\right\|-t\leq 0
, and hence we must have
\left\|y-x\right\|=t
or
\left\|g\right\|=0
. In the former case, when
y
lies on the boundary of
B_{t}(x)
, (
17
) says that the Cauchy-Schwarz inequality is an equality, implying that
g
and
y-x
are linearly dependent.
Otherwise, when
\left\|y-x\right\|<t
, we get
g=0
, which finishes the proof.
∎

Before proving the convergence guarantee, we first introduce some useful preliminary results. The first result establishes that the proximity operator of a proper, closed and convex function is well-defined.

Theorem D.1

(First brox theorem)
.

Let
f:\mathbb{R}^{d}\mapsto\mathbb{R}\cup\{+\infty\}
is proper, closed and convex and choose
x\in{\rm dom}f
. Then

(i)

\textnormal{{brox}}^{t}_{f}(x)\neq\emptyset
. Moreover, if
B_{t}(x)\cap\mathcal{X}_{f}\neq\emptyset
, then
\textnormal{{brox}}^{t}_{f}(x)
is a nonempty subset of
\mathcal{X}_{f}
.

(ii)

If
B_{t}(x)\cap\mathcal{X}_{f}=\emptyset
, then
\textnormal{{brox}}^{t}_{f}(x)
is a singleton lying on the boundary of
B_{t}(x)
.

Proof.

(i)

The broximal operator is minimizing a proper, closed and convex function over a closed set
B_{t}(x)
.
Hence, by the Weierstrass Theorem,
f
is lower bounded on
B_{t}(x)
and attains its minimal value, proving that
\textnormal{{brox}}^{t}_{f}(x)\neq\emptyset
.
It follows that if
B_{t}(x)\cap\mathcal{X}_{f}\neq\emptyset
, then
\textnormal{{brox}}^{t}_{f}(x)\subseteq\mathcal{X}_{f}
is nonempty.

(ii)

Let

.
Then
z_{\star}
is a minimizer of the function

\displaystyle A^{t}_{x}(z):=f(z)+\delta_{B_{t}(x)}(z),

where

\delta_{\mathcal{X}}(z):=\begin{cases}0&z\in\mathcal{X}\\
+\infty&z\notin\mathcal{X}\end{cases}

is the indicator function of the set
\mathcal{X}\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{d}
.
Suppose that
z_{\star}\in\interior B_{t}(x)
and consider the line segment connecting
z_{\star}
and any global minimizer
x_{\star}\notin B_{t}(x)
of
f
.
Obviously it intersects with
\bdry B_{t}(x)
at some point
z_{\lambda}=\lambda z_{\star}+\left(1-\lambda\right)x_{\star}
, where
\lambda\in(0,1)
.
Using convexity of
f
, we know that

f(z_{\lambda})\leq\left(1-\lambda\right)f(x_{\star})+\lambda f(z_{\star})<f(z_{\star}),

(18)

where the last inequality holds because
f(x_{\star})<f(z_{\star})
, which is true because
x_{\star}\in\mathcal{X}_{f}
,
z_{\star}\in B_{t}(x)
and
\mathcal{X}_{f}\cap B_{t}(x)=\emptyset
.

Equation

18
clearly contradicts the assumption that
z_{\star}
is a minimizer of
A^{t}_{x}(z)
, as
A^{t}_{x}(z_{\lambda})<A^{t}_{x}(z_{\star})
.
Thus, we must have
z_{\star}\in\bdry B_{t}(x)
.

Now, assume that
\textnormal{{brox}}^{t}_{f}(x)
is not a singleton and there exist
z_{\star,1},z_{\star,2}\in\textnormal{{brox}}^{t}_{f}(x)
. Then, by the argument above,
z_{\star,1},z_{\star,2}\in\bdry B_{t}(x)
, and due to the convexity of
f
, all points on the line segment connecting
z_{\star,1}
and
z_{\star,2}
are also minimizers of
A^{t}_{x}(z)
.
However, this contradicts the fact that no minimizers of
A^{t}_{x}(z)
lie within
\interior B_{t}(x)
.
Therefore,
\textnormal{{brox}}^{t}_{f}(x)
must be a singleton.

∎

The second part of

(Second brox theorem)

is also a global minimizer of
f
.

∎

The next proposition states that, similar to the convex case, under
A.1
, the iterates of
BPM
are uniquely determined until they reach the optimal solution set.

Proposition E.2
.

Let
A.1
hold. If
B_{t}(x)\cap\mathcal{X}_{f}=\emptyset
, then the mapping
x\mapsto\textnormal{{brox}}^{t}_{f}(x)
is single-valued.

Proof.

Fix
x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
and let
x_{1},x_{2}\in\textnormal{{brox}}^{t}_{f}(x)
. Then, the defining property (
15
) gives

\displaystyle f(y)\geq f(x_{1})+c_{t}(x)\left\langle x-x_{1},y-x_{1}\right\rangle

and

\displaystyle f(y)\geq f(x_{2})+c_{t}(x)\left\langle x-x_{2},y-x_{2}\right\rangle

for any
y\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
. Taking
y=x_{2}
in the first inequality and
y=x_{2}
in the second inequality and adding the two, we get

\displaystyle 0\geq c_{t}(x)\left\langle x-x_{1},x_{2}-x_{1}\right\rangle+c_{t}(x)\left\langle x-x_{2},x_{1}-x_{2}\right\rangle=c_{t}(x)\left\|x_{1}-x_{2}\right\|^{2}.

Now, by assumption,
B_{t}(x)\cap\mathcal{X}_{f}=\emptyset
, so
\textnormal{{brox}}^{t}_{f}(x)\not\subseteq\mathcal{X}_{f}
. Hence, Theorem
E.1
gives
c_{t}(x)>0
, implying that
x_{1}=x_{2}
.
∎

Proposition E.3
.

Let
A.1
hold. Then, the mapping
x\mapsto\textnormal{{brox}}^{t}_{f}(x)
is firmly nonexpansive on the set
\{x:\textnormal{{brox}}^{t}_{f}(x)\not\subseteq\mathcal{X}_{f}\}
.

Remark E.4
.

Note that
x\mapsto\textnormal{{brox}}^{t}_{f}(x)
cannot be firmly nonexpansive on the whole space. Indeed, if that was the case, then the mapping would be quasinonexpansive, and hence
\textnormal{Fix}(\textnormal{brox}_{f}^{t})
would be convex (see Proposition 4.22 by
Bauschke et al. 2011
). Meanwhile, by Theorem
E.1
, we have
\textnormal{Fix}(\textnormal{brox}_{f}^{t})=\mathcal{X}_{f}
, and
\mathcal{X}_{f}
is not necessarily convex, as shown in
Example

1
.

Proof of
Proposition

E.3
.

Fix
x_{1},x_{2}\in{\rm dom}f
and let
u_{1}\in\textnormal{{brox}}^{t}_{f}(x_{1})
and
u_{2}\in\textnormal{{brox}}^{t}_{f}(x_{2})
. Then, the defining property (
15
) gives

\displaystyle f(y)\geq f(u_{1})+c_{t}(x_{1})\left\langle x_{1}-u_{1},y-u_{1}\right\rangle

and

\displaystyle f(y)\geq f(u_{2})+c_{t}(x_{2})\left\langle x_{2}-u_{2},y-u_{2}\right\rangle

for any
y\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
. Taking
y=u_{2}
in the first inequality and
y=u_{2}
in the second inequality and adding the two, we get

\displaystyle 0

\displaystyle\geq c_{t}(x_{1})\left\langle x_{1}-u_{1},u_{2}-u_{1}\right\rangle+c_{t}(x_{2})\left\langle x_{2}-u_{2},u_{1}-u_{2}\right\rangle

\displaystyle\geq\min\left\{c_{t}(x_{1}),c_{t}(x_{2})\right\}\left\langle x_{1}-u_{1}-x_{2}+u_{2},u_{2}-u_{1}\right\rangle.

Since by assumption
\textnormal{{brox}}^{t}_{f}(x_{1}),\textnormal{{brox}}^{t}_{f}(x_{2})\not\subseteq\mathcal{X}_{f}
, Theorem
E.1
gives
c_{t}(x_{1}),c_{t}(x_{2})>0
, and hence

\displaystyle\left\|u_{1}-u_{2}\right\|^{2}\leq\left\langle x_{1}-x_{2},u_{1}-u_{2}\right\rangle.

The result follows from Proposition
4.4
by
Bauschke et al. 2011
.
∎

Lemma E.5
.

Let
A.1
hold and let
x\in{\rm dom}f
be such that
\textnormal{dist}(x,\mathcal{X}_{f})>t
. Then

\displaystyle 0<\left\langle x-u,u-x_{\star}\right\rangle

for any
x_{\star}\in\mathcal{X}_{f}
, where
u\in\textnormal{{brox}}^{t}_{f}(x)
.

Proof.

First,
\textnormal{dist}(x,\mathcal{X}_{f})>t
means that
u\not\in\mathcal{X}_{f}
, and hence
c_{t}(x)>0
(see part
(i)
of Theorem
E.1
). Now, letting
y=x_{\star}
in (
15
), we obtain

\displaystyle f_{\star}\geq f(u)+c_{t}(x)\left\langle x-u,x_{\star}-u\right\rangle,

and consequently

\displaystyle 0<f(u)-f_{\star}\leq c_{t}(x)\left\langle x-u,u-x_{\star}\right\rangle.

Dividing by
c_{t}(x)>0
proves the claim.
∎

Proposition E.6
.

Let Assumption
A.1
hold and let
z_{1},\ldots,z_{n}\in\mathcal{X}_{f}
. Define
z(\lambda):=\lambda_{1}z_{1}+\ldots+\lambda_{n}z_{n}
, where
\lambda:=\{(\lambda_{1},\ldots,\lambda_{n})\in[0,1]:\sum_{i=1}^{n}\lambda_{i}=1\}
. Then
u\in\mathcal{X}_{f}
for any
u\in\textnormal{{brox}}^{t}_{f}(z(\lambda))
.

Remark E.7
.

As shown in
Example

1
, the solution set need not be connected for Assumption
A.1
to hold. However, as proven in Proposition
E.6
, any point in the convex hull of
\mathcal{X}_{f}
must be at a distance of at most
t
from the solution set.

Proof of
Proposition

E.6
.

Assume that
c_{t}(z(\lambda))>0
and let
u\in\textnormal{{brox}}^{t}_{f}(z(\lambda))
. Then, by Theorem
E.1
, we have
\textnormal{dist}(z(\lambda),\mathcal{X}_{f})>t
, and hence by Lemma
E.5

\displaystyle 0>\left\langle z(\lambda)-u,z_{i}-u\right\rangle\qquad\forall i\in[n].

Multiplying the
i
th inequality by
\lambda_{i}
and adding them up, we get

\displaystyle 0>\left\langle z(\lambda)-u,\lambda_{1}z_{1}+\ldots+\lambda_{n}z_{n}-u\right\rangle=\left\|z(\lambda)-u\right\|^{2},

which is a contradiction. Thus,
c_{t}(z(\lambda))=0
, and Theorem
E.1
shows that
u\in\mathcal{X}_{f}
.
∎

The next two propositions say that under Assumption
A.1
, the radius
t
must be large enough for the iterates to be able to move to a point with a strictly smaller function value.

Proposition E.8
.

Let Assumption
A.1
hold and let
x\in{\rm dom}f\backslash\mathcal{X}_{f}
. Then, there exists
\bar{x}\in B_{t}(x)
such that
f(\bar{x})<f(x)
.

Proof.

If there existed
x\not\in\mathcal{X}_{f}
such that
f(x)\leq f(\bar{x})
for all
\bar{x}\in B_{t}(x)
, by definition of broximal operator, we would have
x\in\textnormal{{brox}}^{t}_{f}(x)
. But then Theorem
E.1
would imply that
x\in\mathcal{X}_{f}
, which is a contradiction.
∎

Proposition E.9
.

Let Assumption
A.1
hold and let
x\in{\rm dom}f\backslash\mathcal{X}_{f}
. Then, for all
u\in\textnormal{{brox}}^{t}_{f}(x)
we have
f(u)<f(x)
and
\textnormal{dist}(u,\mathcal{X}_{f})<\textnormal{dist}(x,\mathcal{X}_{f})
.

Proof.

By definition of broximal operator, we have
f(u)\leq f(x)
. Since by Theorem
E.1
equality can hold if and only if
x\in\mathcal{X}_{f}
, the inequality must be strict, proving the first part.

Now, fix any
x\in{\rm dom}f\backslash\mathcal{X}_{f}
. By the reasoning above, we know that there exists
u\neq x
such that
u\in\textnormal{{brox}}^{t}_{f}(x)
. If
\textnormal{dist}(x,\mathcal{X}_{f})\leq t
, then
\textnormal{{brox}}^{t}_{f}(x)\subseteq\mathcal{X}_{f}
, so
\textnormal{dist}(u,\mathcal{X}_{f})=0
and the claim holds.
Otherwise, if
\textnormal{dist}(x,\mathcal{X}_{f})>t
, then
Lemma

E.5
says that

\displaystyle 0>\left\langle x-u,x_{\star}-u\right\rangle\overset{\eqref{fact:1:3pointidentity}}{=}\frac{1}{2}\left(\left\|x-u\right\|^{2}-\left\|x-x_{\star}\right\|^{2}+\left\|u-x_{\star}\right\|^{2}\right)

for all
x_{\star}\in\mathcal{X}_{f}
. It follows that

\displaystyle\left\|u-x_{\star}\right\|^{2}<\left\|x-x_{\star}\right\|^{2}-\left\|x-u\right\|^{2}<\left\|x-x_{\star}\right\|^{2},

and taking infimum over
x_{\star}\in\mathcal{X}_{f}
gives
\textnormal{dist}(u,\mathcal{X}_{f})<\textnormal{dist}(x,\mathcal{X}_{f})
as needed.
∎

Next, similar to convex functions, ball-convex functions guarantee that the steps taken by
BPM
are of length
t
, as long as the algorithm has not reached the set of global minima.

Proposition E.10
.

Suppose that
f
is continuous and Assumption
A.1
holds. Let
x\in{\rm dom}f
be such that
B_{t}(x)\cap\mathcal{X}_{f}=\emptyset
. Then
\left\|x-u\right\|=t
, where
u=\textnormal{{brox}}^{t}_{f}(x)
.

Proof.

Suppose that there exists
x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
such that
u=\textnormal{{brox}}^{t}_{f}(x)\not\in\mathcal{X}_{f}
and
\left\|x-u\right\|<t
. According to Proposition
E.2
,
u
is the unique strict minimizer of
f
over the ball
B_{t}(x)
.
Since
u\not\in\mathcal{X}_{f}
, by Proposition
E.8
, there exists
u^{\prime}\in\textnormal{{brox}}^{t}_{f}(u)
such that
f(u^{\prime})<f(u)
.
Next,
u\in\interior B_{t}(x)
implies that
f(z)>f(u)
for all boundary points
z\in B_{t}(x)\backslash\interior B_{t}(x)
.
Furthermore, by single-valuedness of
\textnormal{{brox}}^{t}_{f}(\cdot)
(Proposition
E.2
),
L_{f}(u)\cap B_{t}(x)=\{u\}
, where
L_{f}(u):=\left\{x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}:f(x)=f(u)\right\}
.
By continuity of
f
and Intermediate Value Theorem, for any path connecting
u
and
u^{\prime}
, there must be a point along the path where
f(\cdot)
equals
f(u)
.
Hence,
L_{f}(u)
forms a closed loop surrounding
B_{t}(x)
.
Let us denote the union of
L_{f}(u)
and the region it surrounds by
L_{f}^{\geq}(u)
.
Now,
f(z)\geq f(u)
for all
z\in B_{t}(x)
,
f(z)>f(u)
for all
z\in\interior(L_{f}^{\geq}(u))\backslash B_{t}(x)
and
f(z)=f(u)
for all
z\in L_{f}(u)
.

Consider the balls with centers lying on the line connecting
x
and
u
. Since
\left\|u-u^{\prime}\right\|\leq t
and
f(u^{\prime})<f(u)
, there exists
\bar{u}\in[u,u^{\prime}]\cap L_{f}(u)
.
Then
\left\|u-\bar{u}\right\|<t
, and hence there exists a ball
B_{t}(\bar{z})
, where
\bar{z}\in(x,u)
, that is contained in
L_{f}^{\geq}(u)
, is tangent to
L_{f}(u)
, and contains
u
.
But now,
f(y)\geq f(u)
for all
y\in B_{t}(\bar{z})
and
f(z)=f(u)
, so
z,u\in\textnormal{{brox}}^{t}_{f}(\bar{z})
, while
z,u\not\in\mathcal{X}_{f}
, contradicting the single-valuedness of broximal operator. This contradiction completes the proof.
∎

The following bounds on
c_{t}(x)
, derived directly from inequality (
15
), play a key role in establishing the convergence result.

Corollary E.11
.

Let
A.1
hold. Then

\displaystyle c_{t}(x)\left\|x-u\right\|^{2}\leq f(x)-f(u)

for all
x\in{\rm dom}f
, where
u\in\textnormal{{brox}}^{t}_{f}(x)
.

Remark E.12
.

In particular, as long as
x_{k}\not\in\mathcal{X}_{f}
(meaning that
x_{k}\neq x_{k+1}
), the iterates of
BPM
satisfy

\displaystyle c_{k}(x_{k})\leq\frac{f(x_{k})-f(x_{k+1})}{\left\|x_{k}-x_{k+1}\right\|^{2}}.

Otherwise, if
x_{k}=x_{k+1}
, then
x_{k}
is a global minimizer and
c_{k}(x_{k})=0
by Theorem
E.1
.

Proof of
Corollary

E.11
.

The result follows by letting
y=x
in inequality (
15
).
∎

Corollary E.13
.

Let Assumption
A.1
hold. Then, for any
x\in{\rm dom}f\backslash\mathcal{X}_{f}

\displaystyle c_{t}(x)\geq\frac{f(u)-f_{\star}}{\left\|u-x\right\|\left\|u-x_{\star}\right\|}

(27)

for any
u\in\textnormal{{brox}}^{t}_{f}(x)
and
x_{\star}\in\mathcal{X}_{f}
such that
u\neq x_{\star}
.

Remark E.14
.

In particular, as long as
x_{k}\not\in\mathcal{X}_{f}
and
x_{k+1}\neq x_{\star}
, the iterates of
BPM
satisfy

\displaystyle c_{t}(x_{k})\geq\frac{f(x_{k+1})-f_{\star}}{\left\|x_{k+1}-x_{k}\right\|\left\|x_{k+1}-x_{\star}\right\|}.

Proof of
Corollary

E.13
.

Taking
y=x_{\star}\in\mathcal{X}_{f}
in (
15
), we get

\displaystyle f_{\star}\geq f(u)+c_{t}(x)\left\langle x-u,x_{\star}-u\right\rangle\geq f(u)-c_{t}(x)\left\|x-u\right\|\left\|x_{\star}-u\right\|,

where the second inequality follows from Cauchy-Schwarz inequality. Rearranging gives the result.
∎

Corollary E.15
.

Let Assumption
A.1
hold and choose
x\in{\rm dom}f
.
If
B_{t}(x)\cap\mathcal{X}_{f}=\emptyset
, then
\textnormal{{brox}}^{t}_{f}(x)
is a singleton and

\displaystyle\left\|u-w\right\|c_{t}(u)\leq\left\|x-u\right\|c_{t}(x),

for all
w\in\textnormal{{brox}}^{t}_{f}(u)
, where
u=\textnormal{{brox}}^{t}_{f}(x)
.

Remark E.16
.

In particular, the iterates of
BPM
satisfy

\displaystyle c_{t}(x_{k+1})\left\|x_{k+1}-x_{k+2}\right\|\leq c_{t}(x_{k})\left\|x_{k}-x_{k+1}\right\|.

Hence, an immediate consequence of
Corollary

E.15
and
Proposition

E.10
is that the constants
c_{t}(x_{k})
generated by
BPM
form a non-increasing sequence.

Proof of
Corollary

E.15
.

Let
x\in{\rm dom}f
be such that
B_{t}(x)\cap\mathcal{X}_{f}=\emptyset
.
Then, by
Proposition

E.2
,
\textnormal{{brox}}^{t}_{f}(x)
is a singleton, so let us denote
u=\textnormal{{brox}}^{t}_{f}(x)
and choose any
w\in\textnormal{{brox}}^{t}_{f}(u)
.
From
Corollary

E.11
, we have

\displaystyle c_{t}(u)\left\|u-w\right\|^{2}\leq f(u)-f(w),

and using Assumption
A.1
with
y=w
, we can write

\displaystyle f(w)\geq f(u)+c_{t}(x)\left\langle x-u,w-u\right\rangle.

Hence, applying the Cauchy-Schwarz inequality

\displaystyle c_{t}(u)\left\|u-w\right\|^{2}\leq f(u)-f(w)\leq c_{t}(x)\left\langle x-u,u-w\right\rangle\leq c_{t}(x)\left\|x-u\right\|\left\|u-w\right\|.

Since
B_{t}(x)\cap\mathcal{X}_{f}=\emptyset
, it follows that
u\not\in\mathcal{X}_{f}
, so
u\neq w
by
Theorem

E.1
.
Dividing by
\left\|u-w\right\|
yields the result.
∎

The results above do not require
f
to be differentiable. Under this additional assumption, a closed-form expression for
c_{t}(x)
can be derived.

Lemma E.17
.

Let
f
be a differentiable function satisfying
A.1
. Then, for any
x\in{\rm dom}f

\displaystyle c_{t}(x)=\frac{\left\|\nabla f(u)\right\|}{t},

where
u\in\textnormal{{brox}}^{t}_{f}(x)
.

It follows that when
f
is differentiable,
c_{t}(x_{k})
in
BPM
is well-defined and equals

\displaystyle c_{t}(x_{k})=\frac{\left\|\nabla f(x_{k+1})\right\|}{t}.

Proof.

Suppose first that
u\not\in\mathcal{X}_{f}
. Then, by Proposition
E.10
, we have
\left\|x-u\right\|=t
, and the optimality condition states that

\displaystyle c_{t}(x)(x-u)=\nabla f(u)

for some
c_{t}(x)\geq 0
(
Theorem

D.2
). Taking norms, we get

\displaystyle\left\|\nabla f(u)\right\|=c_{t}(x)\left\|x-u\right\|=c_{t}(x)t

as required.

The conclusion holds trivially when
u\in\mathcal{X}_{f}
, as both sides are equal to
0
(
Theorem

E.1
).
∎

Building on the results above, we can establish convergence guarantees that are fully analogous to those in the convex setting.

Theorem E.18
.

Assume
f:\mathbb{R}^{d}\mapsto\mathbb{R}\cup\{+\infty\}
is proper, closed and satisfies
A.1
, and let
\{x_{k}\}_{k\geq 0}
be the iterates of
BPM
run with any sequence of positive radii
\{t_{k}\}_{k\geq 0}
, where
x_{0}\in{\rm dom}f
. Then

\displaystyle\textstyle f(x_{K})-f_{\star}\leq\prod\limits_{k=0}^{K-1}\left(1+\frac{t_{k}}{d_{k+1}}\right)^{-1}\left(f(x_{0})-f_{\star}\right),

where
d_{k}:=\left\|x_{k}-x_{\star}\right\|
and
x_{\star}\in\mathcal{X}_{f}
.
Moreover, if
\sum_{k=0}^{K-1}t_{k}^{2}\geq\textnormal{dist}^{2}(x_{0},\mathcal{X}_{f})
, then
x_{K}\in\mathcal{X}_{f}
.

Proof.

The proof closely mirrors that of
Theorem

8.1
, with the primary difference being the starting point. Here, we begin with the defining property from Assumption
A.1
, using
y=x_{\star}\in\mathcal{X}_{f}
, which leads to

\displaystyle f(x_{k+1})-f_{\star}

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle-c_{t}(x_{k})\left\langle x_{k}-x_{k+1},x_{\star}-x_{k+1}\right\rangle.

The remainder of the argument proceeds exactly as in the proof of
Theorem

8.1
.
∎

Theorem E.19
.

Assume
f:\mathbb{R}^{d}\mapsto\mathbb{R}\cup\{+\infty\}
is proper, closed and satisfies
A.1
, and choose
x_{0}\in{\rm dom}f
. Then, for any
K\geq 1
, the iterates of
BPM
run with
t_{k}\equiv t>0
satisfy

\displaystyle\textstyle f(x_{K})-f_{\star}\leq\frac{2d_{0}}{2d_{0}+t}\cdot\frac{d_{0}^{2}}{2Kt^{2}}\left(f(x_{0})-f_{\star}\right).

Proof.

The proof is again entirely analogous to the one presented for the convex case in
Theorem

D.3
.
∎

E.1
Linear convergence under weaker assumption

In fact, we can establish a linear convergence rate without relying on the property that the solution of the local optimization problem lies on the boundary of the ball. Specifically, consider the
k
th iteration of
BPM
with
t_{k}\equiv t>0
. For the algorithm to continue progressing, there must exist
x_{k+1}\in B_{t}(x_{k})
such that
f(x_{k+1})<f(x_{k})
. Similarly, there must exist
x_{k+2}\in B_{t}(x_{k+1})
satisfying
f(x_{k+1})<f(x_{k+2})
(unless
x_{k+1}\in\mathcal{X}_{f}
). Consequently,
x_{k+2}\not\in B_{t}(x_{k})
(since otherwise, the algorithm would transition directly from
x_{k}
to
x_{k+2}
, skipping
x_{k+1}
). This implies that
\left\|x_{k}-x_{k+2}\right\|>t
, meaning that every
second
iterate is separated by a distance of at least
t
.

Building on this observation, let us consider the following weaker assumption as a replacement for
A.1
:

Assumption E.20

(Weak
B_{t}
–convexity)
.

A proper function
f:\mathbb{R}^{d}\to\mathbb{R}\cup\{+\infty\}
is said to be
weakly
B_{t}
–convex
if there exists an optimal point
x_{\star}\in\mathcal{X}_{f}
such that for all
x\in{\rm dom}f
there exists a function
c:\mathbb{R}^{d}\to\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}
such that

\displaystyle f(u)-f_{\star}

\displaystyle\leq c_{t}(x)\left\langle x-u,u-x_{\star}\right\rangle,

(28)

\displaystyle f(x)-f(u)

\displaystyle\geq c_{t}(x)\left\|x-u\right\|^{2}

(29)

for any
u\in\textnormal{{brox}}^{t}_{f}(x)
.

Remark E.21
.

Clearly, there always exist
t>0
such that the function
f
is (weakly)
B_{t}
–convex on a bounded domain (which is sufficient for our application since the iterates of
BPM
remain bounded, as shown in
Proposition

E.9
). Indeed, for
t
large enough, we have
\textnormal{{brox}}^{t}_{f}(x)\subseteq\mathcal{X}_{f}
for all
x
, and both inequalities hold with
c_{t}(x)=0
. However, in practice, the radius
t
can often be chosen much smaller.

Remark E.22
.

The results in
Theorem

E.1
,
Lemma

E.5
,
Proposition

E.6
,
Proposition

E.8
,
Proposition

E.9
,
Corollary

E.11
and
Corollary

E.13
still hold when
E.20
is used instead of
A.1
.

The convergence result under
E.20
is analogous to the one in Theorems
8.1
and
E.18
, with the difference that the exponent is halved, since only every second step, rather than every iteration, is separated by a distance of
t
.

Theorem E.23
.

Assume
f:\mathbb{R}^{d}\mapsto\mathbb{R}\cup\{+\infty\}
is proper, closed and satisfies
E.20
, and choose
x_{0}\in{\rm dom}f
. Then for any
K\geq 1
, the iterates of
BPM
satisfy

\displaystyle\textstyle f(x_{K})-f_{\star}\leq\left(1+\frac{t}{d_{0}}\right)^{-\left\lceil\frac{K-1}{2}\right\rceil}\left(f(x_{0})-f_{\star}\right).

(30)

where
d_{0}:=\left\|x_{0}-x_{\star}\right\|
and
x_{\star}\in\mathcal{X}_{f}
.

Proof.

Consider some iteration
k
such that
x_{k+1}\not\in\mathcal{X}_{f}
(otherwise, the problem is solved in
1
step) and let
x_{\star}\in\mathcal{X}_{f}
be the optimal point for which
E.20
holds.
We again proceed similarly to the proof of
Theorem

8.1
. Recall the inequality (
21
), which states that

\displaystyle f(x_{k+1})-f_{\star}\leq f(x_{k})-f_{\star}-\left(f(x_{k+1})-f_{\star}\right)\frac{\left\|x_{k}-x_{k+1}\right\|}{\left\|x_{k+1}-x_{\star}\right\|}.

Rearranging, we obtain

\displaystyle f(x_{k+1})-f_{\star}\leq\left(1+\frac{\left\|x_{k}-x_{k+1}\right\|}{\left\|x_{0}-x_{\star}\right\|}\right)^{-1}\left(f(x_{k})-f_{\star}\right),

(31)

and applying the bound iteratively gives

\displaystyle f(x_{K})-f_{\star}\leq\left(1+\frac{\left\|x_{K-1}-x_{K}\right\|}{\left\|x_{0}-x_{\star}\right\|}\right)^{-1}\left(1+\frac{\left\|x_{K-2}-x_{K-1}\right\|}{\left\|x_{0}-x_{\star}\right\|}\right)^{-1}\ldots\left(1+\frac{\left\|x_{0}-x_{1}\right\|}{\left\|x_{0}-x_{\star}\right\|}\right)^{-1}\left(f(x_{0})-f_{\star}\right).

(32)

Now, for any
k\geq 0
, we have

Each iteration of

.
In practice, finding an exact solution is often infeasible.
To address this, we propose an implementable modification.

Suppose that
f
is convex and differentiable. Then, the broximal operator can be expressed as

\displaystyle\textnormal{{brox}}^{t}_{f}(x)=x+\argmin_{\left\|u\right\|\leq t}f(x+u)\overset{\eqref{thm:1stbroxthm}}{=}x+\argmin_{\left\|u\right\|=t}f(x+u),

which can be approximated as

\displaystyle\textnormal{{brox}}^{t}_{f}(x)=x+\argmin_{\left\|u\right\|=t}f(x+u)\approx x+\argmin_{\left\|u\right\|=t}\left\{f(x)+\left\langle\nabla f(x),u\right\rangle\right\}=x+\argmin_{\left\|u\right\|=t}\left\langle\nabla f(x),u\right\rangle.

Now, by Cauchy-Schwarz inequality, we find that
\left\langle\nabla f(x),u\right\rangle\geq-\left\|\nabla f(x)\right\|\left\|u\right\|=-\left\|\nabla f(x)\right\|t
, with equality achieved when

. Hence

\displaystyle\textnormal{{brox}}^{t}_{f}(x)\approx x-t\frac{\nabla f(x)}{\left\|\nabla f(x)\right\|}.

Building on this idea, we propose an approximate version of

directly, we minimize its linear approximation at the current iterate

\displaystyle\argmin\limits_{z\in B_{t_{k}}(x_{k})}\left\{f_{k}(z):=f(x_{k})+\left\langle\nabla f(x_{k}),z-x_{k}\right\rangle\right\},

(36)

resulting in the update rule

\displaystyle x_{k+1}=\textnormal{{brox}}^{t_{k}}_{f_{k}}(x_{k}).

(Linearized
BPM

Proof.

Proof.

We start with the decomposition

\displaystyle\left\|x_{k+1}-x_{\star}\right\|^{2}=\left\|x_{k}-t\frac{\nabla f(x_{k})}{\left\|\nabla f(x_{k})\right\|}-x_{\star}\right\|^{2}=\left\|x_{k}-x_{\star}\right\|^{2}-2t\frac{\left\langle x_{k}-x_{\star},\nabla f(x_{k})\right\rangle}{\left\|\nabla f(x_{k})\right\|}+t^{2}.

Appendix H

H.1
Properties of Ball Envelope

The concept of the Moreau envelope
(
Moreau 1965
)
has been employed in many recent studies to analyze the (Stochastic) Proximal Point Method (
(S)PPM
).
This is due to the property that solving the proximal minimization problem for the original objective function
f
is equivalent to applying gradient-based methods to the envelope objective
(
Ryu & Boyd 2016
;
Li et al. 2024a
;
Li & Richtárik 2024
)
.
In this section, we elaborate on the topic introduced in
Section

5
and demonstrate that a similar analysis can be conducted for broximal algorithms.

Following the introduction of the
ball envelope
in
Definition

5.1
, we proceed to derive and analyze its key properties. First, the ball envelope offers a lower bound for the associated function
f
.

Lemma H.1
.

Let
f:\mathbb{R}^{d}\mapsto\mathbb{R}
. Then

\displaystyle{N}^{t}_{f}\left(x\right)\leq f(x)

for any
x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
.

Proof.

The proof of the lemma is immediate once we notice that
{N}^{t}_{f}\left(x\right)=\min_{z\in B_{t}(x)}f(z)\leq f(x)
.
∎

Similar to the Moreau envelope, the ball envelope can be expressed as an infimal convolution of two functions.

Definition H.2

(Infimal convolution)
.

The
infimal convolution
of two proper functions
f,g:\mathbb{R}^{d}\mapsto\mathbb{R}\cup\left\{+\infty\right\}
is the function
\left(f\square g\right):\mathbb{R}^{d}\mapsto\mathbb{R}\cup\left\{\pm\infty\right\}
defined by

\left(f\square g\right)(x)=\min_{z\in\mathbb{R}^{d}}\left\{f(z)+g(x-z)\right\}.

Using the definition of the ball envelope, we obtain

\displaystyle{N}^{t}_{f}\left(x\right)

\displaystyle=\min\left\{f(z):\left\|z-x\right\|\leq t\right\}

\displaystyle=\min_{z\in\mathbb{R}^{d}}\left\{f(z)+\delta_{B_{t}(x)}(z)\right\}

\displaystyle=\min_{z\in\mathbb{R}^{d}}\left\{f(z)+\delta_{B_{t}(0)}(x-z)\right\}=f\square\delta_{B_{t}(0)}

\displaystyle=\min_{u\in\mathbb{R}^{d}}\left\{\delta_{B_{t}(0)}(u)+f(x-u)\right\}=\delta_{B_{t}(0)}\square f.

The next two lemmas are consequences of the above reformulation.

Lemma H.3
.

Let
f:\mathbb{R}^{d}\mapsto\mathbb{R}\cup\{+\infty\}
be proper, closed and convex.
Then
{N}^{t}_{f}\left(x\right)
is convex.

Proof.

We have already shown that
N^{t}_{f}=\delta_{B_{t}(0)}\square f
, where
\delta_{B_{t}(0)}
is a proper convex function and
f
is a real-valued convex function.
Hence, according to Theorem 2.19 of
Beck 2017
,
N^{t}_{f}
is convex.
∎

Lemma H.4
.

Let
f:\mathbb{R}^{d}\mapsto\mathbb{R}
be convex and
L
–smooth.
Then
{N}^{t}_{f}\left(x\right)
is
L
–smooth and

\displaystyle\nabla{N}^{t}_{f}\left(x\right)=\nabla f(u)

for any
x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
and
u\in\textnormal{{brox}}^{t}_{f}(x)

Proof.

We know that
N^{t}_{f}=\delta_{B_{t}(0)}\square f
, where
\delta_{B_{t}(0)}:\mathbb{R}^{d}\mapsto\mathbb{R}\cup\left\{+\infty\right\}
is proper, closed and convex.
Since
f
is convex and
L
–smooth, and the function
\delta_{B_{t}(0)}\square f
is real valued, using Theorem
5.30
of
Beck 2017
, we know that
N^{t}_{f}=\delta_{B_{t}(0)}\square f
is
L
–smooth, and for any
x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
and
u
that minimizes

\displaystyle\delta_{B_{t}(0)}(u)+f(x-u),

we have

\displaystyle\nabla{N}^{t}_{f}\left(x\right)=\nabla f(x-u).

This means that
x-u=z
minimizes
{N}^{t}_{f}\left(x\right)
, and hence

\displaystyle\nabla{N}^{t}_{f}\left(x\right)=\nabla f(x-(x-z))=\nabla f(z).

∎

Unlike the Moreau envelope, which shares the same set of minimizers as the original objective
f
, the ball envelope does not preserve this property. Nevertheless, there exist a certain relationship between the two sets of minimizers.

Lemma H.5
.

Consider
f:\mathbb{R}^{d}\mapsto\mathbb{R}
and denote the sets of minimizers of
f
and

as
\mathcal{X}_{f}
and

, respectively.
Then

\displaystyle\mathcal{X}_{N}=\left\{x:\textnormal{dist}\left(x,\mathcal{X}_{f}\right)\leq t\right\}=\mathcal{X}_{f}+B_{t}(0),

Proof.

Let us pick any

. Then

\displaystyle{N}^{t}_{f}\left(x_{f}\right)\overset{\eqref{lemma:N1}}{\leq}f(x_{f})=\inf f=\inf N^{t}_{f},

which implies that

.
Now, we prove that
\mathcal{X}_{N}=\{x:\textnormal{dist}\left(x,\mathcal{X}_{f}\right)\leq t\}

First, for every
x_{N}\in\{x:\textnormal{dist}\left(x,\mathcal{X}_{f}\right)\leq t\}

f
, leading to variability across different client functions.
To reflect this dependency, we denote the constant associated with iterate
x_{k}
and function
f_{\xi_{k}}
as
c_{t}(x_{k},\xi_{k})
.

Lemma I.1

(Projection)
.

Let
k\geq 0
be an iteration of
SBPM
such that
B_{t}(x_{k})\cap\mathcal{X}_{f_{\xi_{k}}}\neq\emptyset
.
Then

\displaystyle x_{k+1}=\Pi\left(x_{k},\textnormal{{brox}}^{t}_{f_{\xi_{k}}}(x_{k})\right)=\Pi\left(x_{k},B_{t}(x_{k})\cap\mathcal{X}_{f_{\xi_{k}}}\right)=\Pi\left(x_{k},\mathcal{X}_{f_{\xi_{k}}}\right).

Proof.

First, suppose that
B_{t}(x_{k})\cap\mathcal{X}_{f_{\xi_{k}}}\neq\emptyset
. Using the definition of
\textnormal{{brox}}^{t}_{f_{\xi_{k}}}(x_{k})
, it is obvious that

\displaystyle\textnormal{{brox}}^{t}_{f_{\xi_{k}}}(x_{k})=B_{t}(x_{k})\cap\mathcal{X}_{f_{\xi_{k}}}.

Now, assume that
x^{\prime}_{k+1}:=\Pi(x_{k},\mathcal{X}_{f_{\xi_{k}}})\neq x_{k+1}
. Then
x^{\prime}_{k+1}\notin\textnormal{{brox}}^{t}_{f_{\xi_{k}}}(x_{k})
, since otherwise one would have
\Pi(x_{k},\textnormal{{brox}}^{t}_{f_{\xi_{k}}}(x_{k}))=x^{\prime}_{k+1}
, in which case
x_{k+1}=x^{\prime}_{k+1}
.
However, if
x^{\prime}_{k+1}\notin\textnormal{{brox}}^{t}_{f_{\xi_{k}}}(x_{k})
, then
\left\|x_{k+1}-x_{k}\right\|\leq t<\left\|x^{\prime}_{k+1}-x_{k}\right\|
.
Since
x_{k+1}\in\mathcal{X}_{f_{\xi_{k}}}
, this contradicts the fact that
x^{\prime}_{k+1}
is a projection.
∎

The above lemma allows us to rewrite
SBPM
as

\displaystyle x_{k+1}=\begin{cases}\textnormal{{brox}}^{t}_{f_{\xi_{k}}}(x_{k})&\text{ if $B_{t}(x_{k})\cap\mathcal{X}_{f_{\xi_{k}}}=\emptyset$,}\\
\Pi\left(x_{k},\mathcal{X}_{f_{\xi_{k}}}\right)&\text{ otherwise}.\end{cases}

Note that when
B_{t}(x_{k})\cap\mathcal{X}_{f_{\xi}}\neq\emptyset
, we have
\Pi(x_{k},\mathcal{X}_{f_{\xi_{k}}})\in\textnormal{{brox}}^{t}_{f_{\xi_{k}}}(x_{k})
, and hence many existing tools developed for the single-node case remain applicable in the distributed setting.

The extra projection enables us to establish additional properties that guarantee convergence of the method.

Lemma I.2

(Descent lemma I)
.

Let each local objective function
f_{i}:\mathbb{R}^{d}\mapsto\mathbb{R}
be convex and
L_{i}
–smooth.
Then, the iterates of
SBPM
satisfy

\displaystyle-c_{t}(x_{k},\xi_{k})\left\langle x_{k+1}-x_{\star},x_{k}-x_{k+1}\right\rangle\leq\left(f_{\xi_{k}}(x_{\star})-f_{\xi_{k}}\left(\Pi\left(x_{k},\textnormal{{brox}}^{t}_{f_{\xi_{k}}}(x_{k})\right)\right)\right),

where
x_{\star}
is any minimizer of
f_{\xi_{k}}
.

Proof.

According to
Theorem

D.2
, we have

\displaystyle c_{t}(x_{k},\xi_{k})\left(x_{k}-x_{k+1}\right)=\nabla f_{\xi_{k}}(x_{k+1}).

Therefore, by convexity of
f_{\xi_{k}}
,

\displaystyle-c_{t}(x_{k},\xi_{k})\left\langle x_{k+1}-x_{\star},x_{k}-x_{k+1}\right\rangle

\displaystyle=\left\langle x_{\star}-x_{k+1},\nabla f_{\xi_{k}}(x_{k+1})\right\rangle

\displaystyle\leq f_{\xi_{k}}(x_{\star})-f_{\xi_{k}}(x_{k+1})

\displaystyle=f_{\xi_{k}}(x_{\star})-f_{\xi_{k}}\left(\Pi\left(x_{k},\textnormal{{brox}}^{t}_{f_{\xi_{k}}}(x_{k})\right)\right)

as needed.
∎

Lemma I.3

(Descent lemma II)
.

Let each local objective function
f_{i}:\mathbb{R}^{d}\mapsto\mathbb{R}
be convex and
L_{i}
–smooth.
Then, the iterates of
SBPM
satisfy

\displaystyle\left\|x_{k}-x_{k+1}\right\|^{2}\geq\frac{1}{\frac{L_{\xi_{k}}}{2}+c_{t}(x_{k},\xi_{k})}\left(f_{\xi_{k}}(x_{k})-f_{\xi_{k}}(x_{k+1})\right).

Proof.

Since
f_{\xi_{k}}
is
L_{\xi_{k}}
–smooth, we have

\displaystyle f_{\xi_{k}}(x_{k})-f_{\xi_{k}}(x_{k+1})-\left\langle\nabla f_{\xi_{k}}(x_{k+1}),x_{k}-x_{k+1}\right\rangle\leq\frac{L_{\xi_{k}}}{2}\left\|x_{k}-x_{k+1}\right\|^{2}.

Next, by
Theorem

D.2
, it holds that

\displaystyle\left\langle\nabla f_{\xi_{k}}(x_{k+1}),x_{k}-x_{k+1}\right\rangle=c_{t}(x_{k},\xi_{k})\left\|x_{k}-x_{k+1}\right\|^{2},

which implies

\displaystyle f_{\xi_{k}}(x_{k})-f_{\xi_{k}}(x_{k+1})\leq\left(\frac{L_{\xi_{k}}}{2}+c_{t}(x_{k},\xi_{k})\right)\left\|x_{k}-x_{k+1}\right\|^{2}.

Rearranging the terms gives the result.
∎

Lemma I.4

(Descent lemma III)
.

Let each local objective function
f_{i}:\mathbb{R}^{d}\mapsto\mathbb{R}
be convex and
L_{i}
–smooth.
Then, the iterates of
SBPM
satisfy

\displaystyle\left\|x_{k+1}-x_{\star}\right\|^{2}\leq\left\|x_{k}-x_{\star}\right\|^{2}-\frac{1}{\frac{L_{\xi_{k}}}{2}+c_{t}\left(x_{k},\xi_{k}\right)}\left(f_{\xi_{k}}\left(x_{k}\right)-f_{\xi_{k}}\left(x_{\star}\right)\right),

where
x_{\star}
is any minimizer of
f_{\xi_{k}}
.

Proof.

We start with the simple decomposition

\displaystyle\left\|x_{k+1}-x_{\star}\right\|^{2}=\left\|x_{k}-x_{\star}\right\|^{2}-\left\|x_{k}-x_{k+1}\right\|^{2}-2\left\langle x_{k+1}-x_{\star},x_{k}-x_{k+1}\right\rangle.

(40)

Now, let us consider two cases.

Case 1:
c_{t}\left(x_{k},\xi_{k}\right)>0
.

In this case, combining
Lemma

I.2
and
Lemma

I.3
gives

\displaystyle\left\|x_{k+1}-x_{\star}\right\|^{2}\leq\left\|x_{k}-x_{\star}\right\|^{2}-\frac{1}{\frac{L_{\xi_{k}}}{2}+c_{t}(x_{k},\xi_{k})}\left(f_{\xi_{k}}(x_{k})-f_{\xi_{k}}(x_{k+1})\right)-\frac{2}{c_{t}(x_{k},\xi_{k})}\left(f_{\xi_{k}}(x_{k+1})-f_{\xi_{k}}\left(x_{\star}\right)\right).

Now, notice that

\displaystyle\min\left\{\frac{2}{c_{t}\left(x_{k},\xi_{k}\right)},\frac{1}{\frac{L_{\xi_{k}}}{2}+c_{t}\left(x_{k},\xi_{k}\right)}\right\}=\frac{1}{\frac{L_{\xi_{k}}}{2}+c_{t}\left(x_{k},\xi_{k}\right)},

and by the definition of broximal operator, it holds that

\displaystyle f_{\xi_{k}}(x_{k})-f_{\xi_{k}}(x_{k+1})\geq 0.

Moreover, since
x_{\star}
is a minimizer of
f_{\xi_{k}}
, it is obvious that

\displaystyle f_{\xi_{k}}(x_{k+1})-f_{\xi_{k}}(x_{\star})\geq 0.

Combining the above inequalities gives

\displaystyle\left\|x_{k+1}-x_{\star}\right\|^{2}\leq\left\|x_{k}-x_{\star}\right\|^{2}-\frac{1}{\frac{L_{\xi_{k}}}{2}+c_{t}\left(x_{k},\xi_{k}\right)}\left(f_{\xi_{k}}\left(x_{k}\right)-f_{\xi_{k}}\left(x_{\star}\right)\right).

Case 2:
c_{t}\left(x_{k},\xi_{k}\right)=0
.

The condition
c_{t}(x_{k},\xi_{k})=0
implies that
x_{k+1}\in\mathcal{X}_{f_{\xi_{k}}}
(
Theorem

E.1
), so
x_{k+1}=\Pi(x_{k},\textnormal{{brox}}^{t}_{f_{\xi_{k}}}(x_{k}))\in\mathcal{X}_{f_{\xi_{k}}}
.
By
Lemma

I.1
, we know that
x_{k+1}=\Pi(x_{k},\mathcal{X}_{f_{\xi_{k}}})
, which implies that
\left\langle x_{k+1}-x_{\star},x_{k}-x_{k+1}\right\rangle\geq 0
by the second projection theorem (Theorem 6.14 of
(
Beck 2017
)
).
Hence, using
Lemma

I.3
, inequality (
40
) simplifies to

\displaystyle\left\|x_{k+1}-x_{\star}\right\|^{2}

\displaystyle\leq\left\|x_{k}-x_{\star}\right\|^{2}-\frac{1}{\frac{L_{\xi_{k}}}{2}+c_{t}(x_{k},\xi_{k})}\left(f_{\xi_{k}}(x_{k})-f_{\xi_{k}}(x_{k+1})\right)

\displaystyle=\left\|x_{k}-x_{\star}\right\|^{2}-\frac{1}{\frac{L_{\xi_{k}}}{2}+c_{t}\left(x_{k},\xi_{k}\right)}\left(f_{\xi_{k}}\left(x_{k}\right)-f_{\xi_{k}}\left(x_{\star}\right)\right),

which finishes the proof.
∎

Finally, one can prove the following convergence guarantee:

Theorem I.5
.

Let each local objective function
f_{i}:\mathbb{R}^{d}\mapsto\mathbb{R}
be convex and
L_{i}
–smooth, and assume that there exists
x_{\star}
such that
\nabla f_{i}(x_{\star})=0
for all
i\in[n]
.
Then, for any
K\geq 1
, the iterates of
SBPM
with
t_{k}\equiv t>0
satisfy

\displaystyle{\rm\mathbb{E}}\left[f(\tilde{x}_{K})\right]-f_{\star}\leq L_{\max}\left(1+\frac{\left\|x_{0}-x_{\star}\right\|^{2}}{t^{2}}\right)\cdot\frac{\left\|x_{0}-x_{\star}\right\|^{2}}{2K}.

where
\tilde{x}_{K}
is chosen uniformly at random from the first
K
iterates
\left\{x_{0},\ldots,x_{K-1}\right\}
and
L_{\max}:=\max_{i\in[n]}L_{i}
.

Remark I.6

(Semi-adaptivity)
.

An observation from
Theorem

I.5
is that the algorithm converges regardless of the step size
t
.
Notably, similar to
SPPM
, smoothness is not required to determine the step size.
A smaller
t
results in a slower convergence rate, but it simplifies the local subproblems, making them easier to solve.
Conversely, a larger
t
improves the convergence rate, but increases the complexity of each subproblem, thereby requiring more local computation.

Proof of
Theorem

I.5
.

Let
x_{\star}
be a common minimizer of all client functions.
We start with the inequality from
Lemma

I.4

\displaystyle\left\|x_{k+1}-x_{\star}\right\|^{2}\leq\left\|x_{k}-x_{\star}\right\|^{2}-\frac{1}{\frac{L_{\xi_{k}}}{2}+c_{t}\left(x_{k},\xi_{k}\right)}\left(f_{\xi_{k}}\left(x_{k}\right)-f_{\xi_{k}}\left(x_{\star}\right)\right).

Taking expectation conditional on
x_{k}
, we have

\displaystyle{\mathbb{E}}\left[\left.\left\|x_{k+1}-x_{\star}\right\|^{2}\right|x_{k}\right]

\displaystyle\leq\left\|x_{k}-x_{\star}\right\|^{2}-\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\frac{1}{\frac{L_{i}}{2}+c_{t}(x_{k},i)}\left(f_{i}(x_{k})-f_{i}\left(x_{\star}\right)\right).

We can further simplify the recursion as

\displaystyle{\mathbb{E}}\left[\left.\left\|x_{k+1}-x_{\star}\right\|^{2}\right|x_{k}\right]\leq\left\|x_{k}-x_{\star}\right\|^{2}-\frac{1}{\frac{L_{\max}}{2}+c^{\max}_{t}\left(x_{k}\right)}\left(f(x_{k})-f(x_{\star})\right),

where
c^{\max}_{t}\left(x_{k}\right)=\max_{i\in[n]}c_{t}(x_{k},i)
.
Taking expectation again and using the tower property gives

\displaystyle{\rm\mathbb{E}}\left[\left\|x_{k+1}-x_{\star}\right\|^{2}\right]\leq{\rm\mathbb{E}}\left[\left\|x_{k}-x_{\star}\right\|^{2}\right]-{\rm\mathbb{E}}\left[\frac{1}{\frac{L_{\max}}{2}+c^{\max}_{t}\left(x_{k}\right)}\left(f(x_{k})-f(x_{\star})\right)\right],

and hence, unrolling the recurrence,

\displaystyle\sum_{k=0}^{K-1}\frac{1}{\frac{L_{\max}}{2}+c^{\max}_{t}\left(x_{k}\right)}{\rm\mathbb{E}}\left[f(x_{k})-f_{\star}\right]\leq\left\|x_{0}-x_{\star}\right\|^{2}.

Denoting
c_{\max}=\sup_{k\in\left\{0,1,\ldots,K-1\right\}}c^{\max}_{t}\left(x_{k}\right)
, we obtain

\displaystyle{\rm\mathbb{E}}\left[f(\tilde{x}_{K})\right]-f_{\star}\leq\left(\frac{L_{\max}}{2}+c_{\max}\right)\cdot\frac{\left\|x_{0}-x_{\star}\right\|^{2}}{K},

(41)

where
\tilde{x}_{K}
is sampled randomly from the first
K
iterates
\left\{x_{0},x_{1},\ldots,x_{K-1}\right\}
.

Now, we proceed to obtain an upper bound on
c_{\max}
.
Consider some client function
f_{i}
,
i\in[n]
. Since, by definition,
c_{t}(x_{k},i)\geq 0
, it suffices to consider the case
c_{t}(x_{k},i)\neq 0
. From
Theorem

D.2
we know that
\Pi\left(x_{k},\textnormal{{brox}}^{t}_{f_{i}}(x_{k})\right)\notin\mathcal{X}_{f_{i}}
, so
\left\|x_{k}-\Pi\left(x_{k},\textnormal{{brox}}^{t}_{f_{i}}(x_{k})\right)\right\|=t
(
Proposition

E.10
). Using
Corollary

E.11
, we can deduce that

\displaystyle c_{t}(x_{k},i)\leq\frac{f_{i}(x_{k})-f_{i}\left(\Pi\left(x_{k},\textnormal{{brox}}^{t}_{f_{i}}(x_{k})\right)\right)}{\left\|x_{k}-\Pi\left(x_{k},\textnormal{{brox}}^{t}_{f_{i}}(x_{k})\right)\right\|^{2}}=\frac{f_{i}(x_{k})-f_{i}\left(\Pi\left(x_{k},\textnormal{{brox}}^{t}_{f_{i}}(x_{k})\right)\right)}{t^{2}}\leq\frac{f_{i}(x_{k})-f_{i}(x_{\star})}{t^{2}}.

Using the
L_{i}
–smoothness of
f_{i}
, we get

\displaystyle c_{t}(x_{k},i)\leq\frac{L_{i}\left\|x_{k}-x_{\star}\right\|^{2}}{2t^{2}},

and consequently

\displaystyle c^{\max}_{t}\left(x_{k}\right)\leq\frac{L_{\max}\left\|x_{k}-x_{\star}\right\|^{2}}{2t^{2}}.

Now, since

\displaystyle\left\|x_{k+1}-x_{\star}\right\|^{2}=\left\|x_{k}-x_{\star}\right\|^{2}-\left\|x_{k}-x_{k+1}\right\|^{2}-2\left\langle x_{k+1}-x_{\star},x_{k}-x_{k+1}\right\rangle,

and by
Lemma

I.2

\displaystyle\left\langle x_{k+1}-x_{\star},x_{k}-x_{k+1}\right\rangle\geq 0,

it follows that
\left\|x_{k+1}-x_{\star}\right\|^{2}\leq\left\|x_{k}-x_{\star}\right\|^{2}
.
As a result, we have

\displaystyle c^{\max}_{t}\left(x_{k}\right)\leq\frac{L_{\max}\left\|x_{0}-x_{\star}\right\|^{2}}{2t^{2}}.

Plugging this back to (
41
) gives

\displaystyle{\rm\mathbb{E}}\left[f(\tilde{x}_{K})\right]-f_{\star}\leq L_{\max}\left(1+\frac{\left\|x_{0}-x_{\star}\right\|^{2}}{t^{2}}\right)\cdot\frac{\left\|x_{0}-x_{\star}\right\|^{2}}{2K}.

∎

Appendix J
Bregman Broximal Point Method

A natural extension of
BPM
is to replace the ball constraint with a more general one.
In this section, we propose a generalization based on the Bregman divergence.

Definition J.1

(Bregman divergence)
.

Let
h:\mathbb{R}^{d}\mapsto\mathbb{R}
be a continuously differentiable function.
The
Bregman divergence
between two points
x,y\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
associated with
h
is the mapping
\mathbb{R}^{d}\times\mathbb{R}^{d}\to\mathbb{R}
defined as

\displaystyle D_{h}\left(x,y\right)=h(x)-h(y)-\left\langle\nabla h(y),x-y\right\rangle.

The Bregman divergence can be intuitively understood by fixing a point
x_{0}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
and interpreting
D_{h}\left(x,x_{0}\right)
as the difference between the function
h
and its linear approximation at
x_{0}
, evaluated at
x
.
When
h(x)=\left\|x\right\|^{2}
, the Bregman divergence simplifies to
D_{h}\left(x,y\right)=\left\|x-y\right\|^{2}=D_{h}\left(y,x\right)
.
In general, however, the Bregman divergence is not symmetric.

In this section, we address the minimization problem (
1
), assuming that
f:\mathbb{R}^{d}\to\mathbb{R}
is a differentiable convex function.
We propose the following algorithm:

\displaystyle x_{k+1}\in\textnormal{{brox}}^{t}_{f,h}\left(x_{k}\right):=\argmin_{z\in\mathbb{R}^{d}}\left\{f(z):D_{h}\left(z,x_{k}\right)\leq t^{2}\right\}.

(
BregBPM
)

We refer to
\textnormal{{brox}}^{t}_{f,h}\left(\cdot\right)
as the
Bregman Broximal Operator
, and name the corresponding algorithm the
Bregman Ball-Proximal Method
(
BregBPM
).

At each iteration,
BregBPM
minimizes
f
within a localized region around
x_{k}
, defined by the constraint
\mathcal{H}_{k}:=\{z:D_{h}\left(z,x_{k}\right)\leq t^{2}\}
. This translates to solving the constrained optimization problem

\displaystyle\min\limits_{z\in\mathcal{H}_{k}}f(z)\quad\Leftrightarrow\quad\min\limits_{z\in\mathbb{R}^{d}}f(z)+\delta_{\mathcal{H}_{k}}(z).

In this case, the optimality condition states that

\displaystyle 0\in\partial\left(f+\delta_{\mathcal{H}_{k}}\right)(x_{k+1}).

Since
f
is differentiable and convex, and the indicator function of a closed convex set is proper, closed and convex, according to
C.2
we have

\displaystyle 0\in\nabla f(x_{k+1})+\partial\delta_{\mathcal{H}_{k}}(x_{k+1}),

which implies

\displaystyle-\nabla f(x_{k+1})\in\partial\delta_{\mathcal{H}_{k}}(x_{k+1}).

To proceed with the analysis, we first establish several essential results.
For analytical convenience, we assume that
h
is strictly convex, thus ensuring that
D_{h}\left(x,y\right)
is strictly convex with respect to its first argument, as established in
Lemma

J.2
.

Lemma J.2
.

Let
h:\mathbb{R}^{d}\mapsto\mathbb{R}
be a continuously differentiable and strictly convex function.
Then, for any fixed
y\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
, the Bregman divergence
D_{h}\left(x,y\right)
is strictly convex with respect to
x
.

Proof.

For any two distinct points
x_{1},x_{2}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
and
\lambda\in(0,1)
, we have

\displaystyle D_{h}\left(\lambda x_{1}+\left(1-\lambda\right)x_{2},y\right)

\displaystyle=h\left(\lambda x_{1}+\left(1-\lambda\right)x_{2}\right)-h(y)-\lambda\left\langle\nabla h(y),x_{1}\right\rangle-\left(1-\lambda\right)\left\langle\nabla h(y),x_{2}\right\rangle

\displaystyle<\lambda\left(h(x_{1})-h(y)-\left\langle\nabla h(y),x_{1}\right\rangle\right)+\left(1-\lambda\right)\left(h(x_{2})-h(y)-\left\langle\nabla h(y),x_{2}\right\rangle\right)

\displaystyle=\lambda D_{h}\left(x_{1},y\right)+\left(1-\lambda\right)D_{h}\left(x_{2},y\right)

as needed.
∎

The following lemma demonstrates that strict convexity ensures that the Bregman broximal operator is single-valued, possibly except for the last iteration of the algorithm.

Lemma J.3
.

Let
h:\mathbb{R}^{d}\mapsto\mathbb{R}
be a continuously differentiable and strictly convex function.
If
\textnormal{{brox}}^{t}_{f,h}\left(x\right)\not\subseteq\mathcal{X}_{f}
, then the mapping
x\mapsto\textnormal{{brox}}^{t}_{f,h}\left(x\right)
is single-valued and
u=\textnormal{{brox}}^{t}_{f,h}\left(x\right)\in\bdry\mathcal{H}
, where
\mathcal{H}:=\left\{z:D_{h}\left(z,x\right)\leq t^{2}\right\}
.

Remark J.4
.

For
z\in\bdry\mathcal{H}_{k}
, we always have
D_{h}\left(z,x_{k}\right)=t^{2}
, which means that
\bdry\mathcal{H}_{k}
is a level set of
D_{h}\left(z,x_{k}\right)
.

Proof.

Suppose that there exists
u\in\textnormal{{brox}}^{t}_{f,h}\left(x\right)
such that
u\in\interior\mathcal{H}
and take any
x_{\star}\in\mathcal{X}_{f}
. Since
\mathcal{H}\cap\mathcal{X}_{f}=\emptyset
, we have
x_{\star}\notin\mathcal{H}
.
Hence, the line segment connecting
x_{\star}
and
u
must intersect
\bdry\mathcal{H}
at a point
\tilde{u}:=\lambda u+\left(1-\lambda\right)x_{\star}
for some
\lambda\in(0,1)
.
Using strict convexity of
f
, we obtain

\displaystyle f(\tilde{u})<\lambda f(u)+(1-\lambda)f(x_{\star})<f(u),

which contradicts the fact that
u
minimizes
f
on
\mathcal{H}
.
As a result,
u
must lie on the boundary of
\mathcal{H}
.

Now, suppose that there exist two distinct points
u_{1},u_{2}\in\textnormal{{brox}}^{t}_{f,h}\left(x\right)
.
The strict convexity of
D_{h}\left(z,x\right)
in its first argument guarantees that
\mathcal{H}
is strictly convex as well.
Hence, the line segment connecting
u_{1}
and
u_{2}
lies in the interior of
\mathcal{H}
, and for any
\alpha\in(0,1)

\displaystyle f\left(\alpha u_{1}+\left(1-\alpha\right)u_{2}\right)\leq\alpha f(u_{1})+\left(1-\alpha\right)f(u_{2})=f(u_{1})=f(u_{2}),

which implies that
\alpha u_{1}+(1-\alpha)u_{2}\in\interior\mathcal{H}
is also a minimizer of
f
on
\mathcal{H}
. This contradicts the fact that a minimizer must lie on the boundary.
∎

Lemma J.5
.

Let
\phi:\mathbb{R}^{d}\mapsto\mathbb{R}
be a differentiable convex function,
c>\inf\phi
be a constant, and denote
\mathcal{C}=\left\{x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}:\phi(x)\leq c\right\}
.
Then for any
x\in\bdry\mathcal{C}
, it holds that

\displaystyle\mathcal{N}_{\mathcal{C}}(x)=\left\{\lambda\nabla\phi(x),\lambda\geq 0\right\}.

Proof.

Let
z\in\bdry\mathcal{C}
and denote

\displaystyle\mathcal{H}(z)

\displaystyle:=\left\{y\in\mathbb{R}^{d}:\phi(z)+\left\langle\nabla\phi(z),y-z\right\rangle=\phi(z)\right\}

\displaystyle=\left\{y\in\mathbb{R}^{d}:\left\langle\nabla\phi(z),y-z\right\rangle=0\right\}.

Then,
\mathcal{H}(z)
is a supporting hyperplane of the convex set
\mathcal{C}
, and
\nabla\phi(x)
is a normal vector to this hyperplane.
Now, recall the definition of the normal cone

\displaystyle\mathcal{N}_{\mathcal{C}}(x)=\left\{y\in\mathbb{R}^{d}:\left\langle y,z-x\right\rangle\leq 0\quad\forall z\in\mathcal{C}\right\}.

For any
z\in\mathcal{C}
, using convexity, we have

\displaystyle\phi(z)\geq\phi(x)+\left\langle\nabla\phi(x),z-x\right\rangle,

which indicates that

\displaystyle\left\langle\lambda\nabla\phi(x),z-x\right\rangle\leq 0\quad\forall\lambda\geq 0,

for all
z\in\mathcal{C}
, implying that
\lambda\nabla\phi(x)\in\mathcal{N}_{\mathcal{C}}(x)
.

Now, assume that there exists
v\in\mathcal{N}_{\mathcal{C}}(x)\neq\lambda\nabla\phi(x)
for any
\lambda\geq 0
.
Since
\nabla\phi(x)\neq 0
and
v\neq 0
, there exists
h\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
such that

\displaystyle\left\langle\nabla\phi(x),h\right\rangle<0\text{ and }\left\langle v,h\right\rangle>0.

Let
\varepsilon>0
and consider a point
x+\varepsilon h
. Since
f(x)
is differentiable, for
\varepsilon
small enough, we have

\displaystyle\phi(x+\varepsilon h)=\phi(x)+\varepsilon\left\langle\nabla\phi(x),h\right\rangle+r(\varepsilon h),

where
r(\varepsilon h)
satisfies
\lim_{\varepsilon\rightarrow 0}\frac{r(\varepsilon h)}{\varepsilon\cdot\left\|h\right\|}=0
.
Therefore,
\phi(x+\varepsilon h)<\phi(x)
, and hence
x+\varepsilon h\in\mathcal{C}
.
However, we also have

\displaystyle\left\langle v,x+\varepsilon h-x\right\rangle=\varepsilon\left\langle v,h\right\rangle>0,

so
v\notin\mathcal{N}_{\mathcal{C}}(x)
.
This contradiction shows that there are no directions other than
\lambda\nabla\phi(x)
,
\lambda\geq 0
in
\mathcal{N}_{\mathcal{C}}(x)
.
∎

The following corollary is a direct consequence of
Lemma

J.5
:

Corollary J.6
.

Let
f:\mathbb{R}^{d}\mapsto\mathbb{R}
be a differentiable convex function and
h:\mathbb{R}^{d}\mapsto\mathbb{R}
be a continuously differentiable strictly convex function. Then

\displaystyle\partial\delta_{\mathcal{H}_{k}}(x_{k})=\left\{\lambda\left(\nabla h(x_{k+1})-\nabla h(x_{k})\right):\lambda\geq 0\right\},

where
\{x_{k}\}_{k\geq 0}
are the iterates generated by
BregBPM
.
Hence, there exists a function
c_{t,h}:\mathbb{R}^{d}\to\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}
such that

\displaystyle\nabla f(x_{k+1})=c_{t,h}(x_{k})\left(\nabla h(x_{k})-\nabla h(x_{k+1})\right).

Remark J.7
.

A similar result applies when
f:\mathbb{R}^{d}\mapsto\mathbb{R}\cup\left\{+\infty\right\}
. In this case, the subdifferential is given by

\displaystyle\partial f(x_{k+1})=\left\{\lambda\left(\nabla h(x_{k})-\nabla h(x_{k+1})\right):\lambda\geq 0\right\}.

In both scenarios, convexity ensures that

\displaystyle f(y)\geq f(x_{k+1})+c_{t,h}(x_{k})\left\langle\nabla h(x_{k})-\nabla h(x_{k+1}),y-x_{k+1}\right\rangle

(42)

for some
c_{t,h}(x_{k})\geq 0
and any
y\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
.
Consequently,
c_{t,h}(x_{k})
can be bounded above as follows:

\displaystyle c_{t,h}(x_{k})\leq\frac{f(x_{k})-f(x_{k+1})}{\left\langle\nabla h(x_{k})-\nabla h(x_{k+1}),x_{k}-x_{k+1}\right\rangle}\leq\frac{f(x_{0})-f(x_{\star})}{D_{h}\left(x_{k},x_{k+1}\right)+D_{h}\left(x_{k+1},x_{k}\right)}\leq\frac{f(x_{0})-f(x_{\star})}{t^{2}}.

(43)

Proof of
Corollary

J.6
.

Using Example
3.5
of
(
Beck 2017
)
, we know that
\delta_{\mathcal{H}_{k}}(x_{k+1})=\mathcal{N}_{\mathcal{H}_{k}}(x_{k+1})
.
By
Lemma

J.3
,
\textnormal{{brox}}^{t}_{f,h}\left(x_{k}\right)
is a singleton and
x_{k+1}=\textnormal{{brox}}^{t}_{f,h}\left(x_{k}\right)\in\bdry\mathcal{H}_{k}
.
Next, invoking
Lemma

J.5
, the Bregman divergence
D_{h}\left(z,x_{k}\right)
is differentiable and convex in its first argument, with
\nabla_{z}D_{h}\left(z,x_{k}\right)=\nabla h(z)-\nabla h(x_{k+1})
.
Hence,

\displaystyle\delta_{\mathcal{H}_{k}}(x_{k+1})=\mathcal{N}_{\mathcal{H}_{k}}(x_{k})=\left\{\lambda\left(\nabla h(x_{k+1})-\nabla h(x_{k})\right):\lambda\geq 0\right\}.

Since by the optimality condition
-\nabla f(x_{k+1})\in\delta_{\mathcal{H}_{k}}(x_{k+1})
, we conclude that there exists
c_{t,h}(x_{k})\geq 0
such that

\displaystyle\nabla f(x_{k+1})=c_{t,h}(x_{k})\left(\nabla h(x_{k})-\nabla h(x_{k+1})\right).

∎

Equipped with the necessary analytical tools, we now derive the convergence guarantee for
BregBPM
.

Theorem J.8
.

Let
f:\mathbb{R}^{d}\mapsto\mathbb{R}\cup\left\{+\infty\right\}
be proper, closed and convex, and
h:\mathbb{R}^{d}\mapsto\mathbb{R}
be continuously differentiable and strictly convex.
Then, for any
K\geq 1
, the iterates of
BregBPM
satisfy

\displaystyle f(x_{K})-f(x_{\star})\leq\frac{\left(f(x_{0})-f(x_{\star})\right)D_{h}\left(x_{\star},x_{0}\right)}{Kt^{2}}.

Proof.

Let us consider some iteration
k
such that
x_{k+1}\not\in\mathcal{X}_{f}
.
Taking
y=x_{\star}\in\mathcal{X}_{f}
in (
42
) and rearranging the terms, we have

\displaystyle f(x_{k+1})-f_{\star}\leq c_{t,h}(x_{k})\left\langle\nabla h(x_{k})-\nabla h(x_{k+1}),x_{k+1}-x_{\star}\right\rangle.

Now, using the four point identity (
C.3
) gives

\displaystyle f(x_{k+1})-f_{\star}

\displaystyle\leq c_{t,h}(x_{k})\left(D_{h}\left(x_{k+1},x_{k+1}\right)+D_{h}\left(x_{\star},x_{k}\right)-D_{h}\left(x_{k+1},x_{k}\right)-D_{h}\left(x_{\star},x_{k+1}\right)\right)

\displaystyle=c_{t,h}(x_{k})\left(D_{h}\left(x_{\star},x_{k}\right)-D_{h}\left(x_{k+1},x_{k}\right)-D_{h}\left(x_{\star},x_{k+1}\right)\right).

Rearranging, we have

\displaystyle f(x_{k+1})-f_{\star}\leq f(x_{k+1})-f_{\star}+c_{t,h}(x_{k})D_{h}\left(x_{k+1},x_{k}\right)\leq c_{t,h}(x_{k})\left(D_{h}\left(x_{\star},x_{k}\right)-D_{h}\left(x_{\star},x_{k+1}\right)\right),

and hence, applying the bound in (
43
) gives

\displaystyle f(x_{k+1})-f_{\star}\leq\frac{f(x_{0})-f_{\star}}{t^{2}}\left(D_{h}\left(x_{\star},x_{k}\right)-D_{h}\left(x_{\star},x_{k+1}\right)\right).

Finally, averaging over
k\in\{0,1,\ldots,K-1\}
and noticing that the function values are decreasing, we obtain

\displaystyle f(x_{K})-f_{\star}\leq\frac{1}{K}\sum_{k=0}^{K-1}f(x_{k+1})-f_{\star}\leq\frac{\left(f(x_{0})-f_{\star}\right)D_{h}\left(x_{\star},x_{0}\right)}{Kt^{2}}.

∎

Remark J.9
.

By following the same steps, one can establish a convergence guarantee for a general proper, closed and convex function
f:\mathbb{R}^{d}\to\mathbb{R}\cup\left\{+\infty\right\}
.

Remark J.10
.

For
h=\left\|\cdot\right\|^{2}
, the convergence guarantee becomes

\displaystyle f(x_{K})-f(x_{\star})\leq\frac{\left(f(x_{0})-f(x_{\star})\right)\left\|x_{0}-x_{\star}\right\|^{2}}{2Kt^{2}},

which matches the result from
Theorem

D.3
up to a constant factor.
The discrepancy arises due to the asymmetry of the Bregman divergence.
