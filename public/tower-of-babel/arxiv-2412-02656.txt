Geometric bound on structure factor
Yugo Onishi, Alexander Avdoshkin, Liang Fu

Abstract
We show that a quadratic form of quantum geometric tensor in k k -space sets a bound on the q 4 q^{4} term in the static structure factor S ⁡ ( q ) S(q) at small 𝒒 {\bf\it q} . Bands that saturate this bound satisfy a condition similar to Laplace’s equation, leading us to refer to them as harmonic bands . We provide examples of harmonic bands in one- and two-dimensional systems, including (higher) Landau levels. The geometric bound further leads to a topological bound on the q 4 q^{4} term, which is saturated only when the band geometry satisfies the trace condition and, additionally, the quantum geometric tensor is uniform in k k -space. We speculate that these bounds taken together provide a useful guide for identifying Chern bands that favor (Abelian or non-Abelian) fractional Chern insulators.



Introduction. —
The static structure factor is an important physical observable that characterizes density correlations in ground states of electronic systems. While the Bragg peaks in the structure factor have been used to identify periodic crystal structures and charge density wave orders, the structure factor away from reciprocal lattice vectors also encodes rich information about the electronic structure and physical responses of the system. For example, the structure factor is related to the density responses through the fluctuation-dissipation theorem
[
1
]
. In interacting systems, it determines the interaction energy of the ground state, and also offers a way to estimate the excitation energy, as shown by Feynman for liquid Helium systems
[
2
]
. A related approach based on the projected structure factor was developed to study the collective modes in fractional quantum Hall systems
[
3
]
.

Recently, an exact general relation between the structure factor and the many-body quantum geometry was established
[
4
]
, enabling direct measurement of the ground state quantum geometry via X-ray scattering and electron loss spectroscopy
[
5
]
.
Specifically, for insulating states in reduced dimensions or with short-range interaction, the static structure factor at leading order in the wavevector
q
is directly related to the many-body quantum metric.
The many-body quantum metric is also closely related to polarization fluctuation
[
6
]
, localization of electron wavefunction
[
7
]
, the spread of Wannier functions
[
8
]
, and the Bloch band geometry in noninteracting band insulators. Furthermore, this geometric perspective naturally leads to universal bounds on physical observables in topological phases
[
9

4
,
10
,
11
]
.

In this work, we explore the relation between quantum geometry and the static structure factor
S({\bf\it q})
of band insulators beyond the leading order in
q

Specifically, we show that the isotropic part of
S({\bf\it q})
at fourth order in

This establishes a connection between the structure factor and fluctuations of quantum geometric tensor in
k

Interestingly, we find that this geometric bound is saturated when the band geometry satisfies a certain “harmonic” condition. Examples of harmonic bands in one- and two-dimensional systems, including higher Landau levels, are provided.

This geometric bound allows us to derive a topological bound on

Our geometric and topological bounds on

\displaystyle\equiv\frac{1}{N_{e}}\expectationvalue{{n}_{\vec{q}}{n}_{-\vec{q}}}

Atomic insulators. —

To gain intuition about the structure factor of band insulators at small
q
, let us start with a simple example of an array of atoms forming a
d
-dimensional cubic
lattice. When the lattice constant
a
is large enough, we can neglect the hopping between the atoms and the system is an atomic insulator. Its energy eigenstates are given by the localized orbitals of individual atoms, denoted as
\phi_{n}({\bf\it r}-{\bf\it R})
, with
{\bf\it R}
the position of an atom and
n
the label for each atomic orbital. When each atom has one electron occupying
n=0
orbital, the structure factor at
{\bf\it q}
away from the reciprocal lattice vectors is given by (see SM for the derivation)

\displaystyle S({\bf\it q})

\displaystyle=1-\absolutevalue{\expval{e^{-i\vec{q}\vdot\vec{r}}}_0}^{2}

(2)

where
\expectationvalue{\dots}_{0}=\int\differential{r}(\dots)\absolutevalue{\phi_0(\vec{r})}^{2}
is the expectation value in
n=0
orbital.
The quantity
\expectationvalue{e^{-i\vec{q}\vdot\vec{r}}}_{0}
contains all the moments of
{\bf\it r}
for the probability distribution
p({\bf\it r})=\absolutevalue{\phi_0(\vec{r})}^{2}
. Thus, the structure factor completely captures electron density distribution within an atom.

At small
{\bf\it q}
, we expand
S({\bf\it q})
in
{\bf\it q}
and obtain

\displaystyle S({\bf\it q})

\displaystyle=\expectationvalue{(\vec{q}\vdot\vec{r})^2}_{0}-\frac{2}{4!}\quantity(\expval{(\vec{q}\vdot\vec{r})^4}_0 + 3\expval{(\vec{q}\vdot\vec{r})^2}_0^2)+\dots

\displaystyle=K_{\alpha\beta}\frac{\bar{q}_{\alpha}\bar{q}_{\beta}}{2\pi}+\dots,

(3)

where
\bar{q}_{\alpha}=q_{\alpha}a
is the dimensionless wavevector.
Here, we have chosen the origin such that
\expectationvalue{\vec{r}}_{0}=0
, and
K
is a rank-2 tensor given by

\displaystyle K_{\alpha\beta}

\displaystyle=2\pi\frac{\expectationvalue{r_{\alpha}r_{\beta}}_{0}}{a^{2}},

(4)

which represents the position fluctuation of an electron within the atom.

Although
q^{4}
term is more complicated, its isotropic component is fairly simple. We define the isotropic component of
S({\bf\it q})
as
\bar{S}(q)=\int_{\absolutevalue{\vec{q}}=q}\differential{\Omega}S({\bf\it q})/A_{d}
, where
d
is the spatial dimension,
\int\differential\Omega
is integration over solid angle, and
A_{d}
is the total solid angle in
d
-dimensions. We expand
\bar{S}(q)
in
q
as

\displaystyle\bar{S}(q)

\displaystyle=\frac{S_{2}}{2!}\frac{\bar{q}^{2}}{2\pi}-\frac{S_{4}}{4!}\quantity(\frac{\bar{q}^2}{2\pi})^{2}+\dots,

(5)

where
S_{2}
and
S_{4}
are given by

\displaystyle S_{2}

\displaystyle=\frac{4\pi}{da^{2}}\expectationvalue{r^2}_{0}=\frac{2}{d}\tr K,

(6)

\displaystyle S_{4}

\displaystyle=\frac{12}{d(d+2)}\quantity((\tr K)^2 + \tr(K^2))

\displaystyle\quad+\frac{6(2\pi)^{2}}{d(d+2)a^{4}}\expectationvalue{(r^2-\expval{r^2})^2}_{0}.

(7)

Here and hereafter,
\tr
represents the sum over the spatial indices
x,y,\dots
.
In probability theory, the fourth moment, such as
\expectationvalue{x^4}

S_{4}

kurtosis
from now on.
Since the last term in
S_{4}
is semi-positive, we see a lower bound on
S_{4}
determined solely by
the quadratic coefficients of structure factor,
K

\displaystyle S_{4}\geq\frac{12}{d(d+2)}\quantity((\tr K)^2 + \tr(K^2)).

(8)

Notably, the bound on
S_{4}
is set by the square of
K

K
is related to quantum metric
[
20
]
, this observation suggests a relation between
S_{4}
and the quadratic form of quantum geometry.

In the rest of this work, we will present a generalization of Eq. (

S(q)
at
q^{4}
order has a lower bound given by the quadratic form of quantum geometry in
k

General theory. —
Consider a
d
-dimensional non-interacting band insulator. The static structure factor at finite small
{\bf\it q}
is given by
[
4
]

\displaystyle S({\bf\it q})

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\nu}\int[dk]\Tr[P({\bf\it k})(P({\bf\it k})-P({\bf\it k}+{\bf\it q}))]

(9)

where
P({\bf\it k})=\sum_{n=1}^{\nu}\outerproduct{u_n(\vec{k})}{u_n(\vec{k})}
is the projector onto the occupied bands at wavevector
{\bf\it k}
with the
cell-periodic
Bloch wavefunction of
n
-th band
\ket{u_n(\vec{k})}
and the number of occupied bands
\nu
.
\int[dk]=\int_{\rm BZ}V_{\rm uc}d^{d}k/(2\pi)^{d}
is the integral over the Brillouin zone normalized by the unit cell volume
V_{\rm uc}
. We can confirm
S({\bf\it q}\to\infty)=1
since
\lim_{{\bf\it q}\to\infty}\Tr[P({\bf\it k})P({\bf\it k}+{\bf\it q})]=0
and
\Tr[P({\bf\it k})^{2}]=\Tr[P({\bf\it k})]=\nu
.
For simplicity, we first consider cases with
\nu=1
so that the electron density
n
is
n=1/V_{\rm uc}
, before addressing the general cases.

As shown by Eq. (
9
), the static structure factor of band insulators is determined solely by the Bloch wavefunction of occupied bands, suggesting a connection between the structure factor and quantum geometry. The connection becomes clear by expanding
S({\bf\it q})
in powers of
q
:

\displaystyle S({\bf\it q})

\displaystyle=K_{\alpha\beta}\frac{\bar{q}_{\alpha}\bar{q}_{\beta}}{2\pi}+\dots,

(10)

where
\bar{q}_{\alpha}\equiv q_{\alpha}/n^{1/d}=q_{\alpha}a
is the dimensionless wavevector with
a\equiv V_{\rm uc}^{1/d}
.
The quadratic coefficient
K
, recently termed the quantum weight,
is directly given by the quantum metric of the occupied bands
[
9
]

\displaystyle K_{\alpha\beta}

\displaystyle=2\pi\int[\differential{k}]g_{\alpha\beta},

(11)

where the quantum metric
g=\real Q
is the real part of the quantum geometric tensor defined as

\displaystyle Q_{\alpha\beta}=\frac{1}{a^{2}}\matrixelement{\partial_{\alpha}u}{(1-P)}{\partial_{\beta}u}=\frac{1}{a^{2}}\innerproduct{D_{\alpha}u}{D_{\beta}u}.

(12)

Here,
D_{\alpha}=\partial_{\alpha}+iA_{\alpha}
is the covariant derivative with
A_{\alpha}=\matrixelement{u}{i\partial_{\alpha}}{u}
the (Abelian) Berry connection. We define
Q
with normalization
1/a^{2}
so that quantum geometric quantities are dimensionless.

In the special case of atomic insulators, the cell-periodic Bloch wavefunction
\ket{u_n(\vec{k})}=\sum_{{\bf\it R}}\exp(i \vec{k} \cdot(\vec{x}-\vec{R}) )\ket{\phi_n(\vec{R})}
is a linear combination of atomic orbitals. Then, the structure factor reduces to the expression Eq. (
2
) and the quantum metric tensor
g_{\alpha\beta}({\bf\it k})
becomes a constant given by the expectation value of quadrupole moment
r_{\alpha}r_{\beta}
within an atom as shown in
Eq. (
4
)
[
21
]
.

Our focus in this work is on higher-order terms in the small-
q
expansion of
S({\bf\it q})
. In particular, we consider the
q
-expansion of its isotropic part:

\displaystyle\bar{S}(q)

\displaystyle\equiv\int_{{\bf\it q}=q}\frac{\differential{\Omega}}{A_{d}}S({\bf\it q})=\frac{S_{2}}{2!}\frac{\bar{q}^{2}}{2\pi}-\frac{S_{4}}{4!}\quantity(\frac{\bar{q}^2}{2\pi})^{2}+\dots,

(13)

where
S_{2}=\tr K
and
S_{4}
is the next leading order term of our interest. We can express
S_{4}
in a simple form:

\displaystyle S_{4}

\displaystyle=\frac{3(2\pi)^{2}}{d(d+2)a^{4}}\int[\differential{k}]\Tr[(\nabla^{2}P)^{2}].

(14)

We further rewrite
S_{4}
with the Bloch wavefunctions as

\displaystyle S_{4}

\displaystyle=\frac{12(2\pi)^{2}}{d(d+2)}\int[\differential{k}]\quantity((\tr Q)^2 + \tr(Q^2))

\displaystyle\quad+\frac{6(2\pi)^{2}}{d(d+2)a^{4}}\int[\differential{k}]\matrixelement{D^2 u}{(1-P)}{D^2 u}.

(15)

Eq. (
15
) is the generalization of Eq. (
7
) to arbitrary band insulators with one occupied band.
It reduces to Eq. (
7
) for atomic insulators since the quantum metric is uniform in
{\bf\it k}
-space and the Berry curvature vanishes.

Noting that the last term is semi-positive as
\matrixelement{D^2 u}{(1-P)}{D^2 u}=\norm{(1-P)\ket{D^2 u}}^{2}\geq 0
and using
\tr Q=\tr g
and
\tr(Q^2)=\tr(g^2)-\tr(\Omega^2)/4
, we obtain a bound for
S_{4}
in terms of the quantum geometric tensor:

\displaystyle S_{4}

\displaystyle\geq\frac{12(2\pi)^{2}}{d(d+2)}\int[\differential{k}]\quantity((\tr Q)^2 + \tr(Q^2))

(16)

\displaystyle=\frac{12(2\pi)^{2}}{d(d+2)}\int[\differential{k}]\quantity((\tr g)^2 + \tr(g^2) - \frac{\tr(\Omega^2)}{4}).

(17)

Eq. (
17
) is the first main result of this work. Importantly, the bound on
S_{4}
is set by the quadratic form of the quantum geometric tensor
Q
in
k
-space. This should be contrasted with
S_{2}
, which involves only the integral of quantum metric.
We also note that the general geometric bound (
17
) involves the Berry curvature, which is absent in the atomic insulator example discussed above.

The geometric bound (
17
) is saturated when the last term in Eq. (
15
) vanishes, or equivalently,

\displaystyle(1-P)\ket{D^2 u}=0.

(18)

When the system has time reversal and inversion symmetry, the Berry connection
{\bf\it A}
vanishes and
D_{\alpha}=\partial_{\alpha}
. In this case, the saturation condition (
18
) becomes

(1-P)\ket{\nabla^2 u}=0
.
This condition can be viewed as a generalized form of Laplace’s equation with the factor
1-P
. Therefore, we shall call a band satisfying Eq. (
18
) a
harmonic band
, analogous to harmonic functions that satisfy the regular Laplace’s equation. When the filled band is harmonic, the geometric bound (
17
) is saturated.

Finally, we note that the last term in Eq. (
15
),
\matrixelement{D^2 u}{(1-P)}{D^2 u}
, cannot be written in terms of the quantum metric and Berry curvature. Instead, it represents a new kind of geometry contained in the structure factor. A detailed study will be reported elsewhere
[
22
]
.

Two-band model example. —

To illustrate our geometric bound, we consider a two-band model with the lower band fully occupied. The Hamiltonian can be written as

H=-E({\bf\it k})\hat{{\bf\it n}}_{{\bf\it k}}\dotproduct{\bf\it\sigma}
, where
{\bf\it\sigma}=(\sigma_{x},\sigma_{y},\sigma_{z})
are the Pauli matrices,
E({\bf\it k})>0
determines the band dispersion, and
\hat{{\bf\it n}}_{{\bf\it k}}
is a unit vector. The projector for the lower band is
P({\bf\it k})=(1+\hat{{\bf\it n}}_{{\bf\it k}}\dotproduct{\bf\it\sigma})/2
, and thus the structure factor is
S({\bf\it q})=(1/2)\int[\differential{k}]\hat{{\bf\it n}}_{{\bf\it k}}\dotproduct(\hat{{\bf\it n}}_{{\bf\it k}}-\hat{{\bf\it n}}_{{\bf\it k}+{\bf\it q}})
, and in particular
S_{4}
is given by

\displaystyle S_{4}

\displaystyle=\frac{3(2\pi)^{2}}{2d(d+2)a^{4}}\int[\differential{k}](\nabla^{2}\hat{{\bf\it n}})^{2}

(19)

On the other hand, the quantum metric
g_{\alpha\beta}
and the Berry curvature
\Omega_{\alpha\beta}
are given by
g_{\alpha\beta}=(4a^{2})^{-1}(\partial_{\alpha}\hat{{\bf\it n}}_{{\bf\it k}})\dotproduct(\partial_{\beta}\hat{{\bf\it n}}_{{\bf\it k}})
and
\Omega_{\alpha\beta}=-(2a^{2})^{-1}\hat{{\bf\it n}}\dotproduct(\partial_{\alpha}\hat{{\bf\it n}}\crossproduct\partial_{\beta}\hat{{\bf\it n}})
, and thus the geometric bound (
17
) is given by

\displaystyle S_{4}\geq\frac{6\pi^{2}}{d(d+2)a^{4}}\int[\differential k]\quantity(\vec{n}\vdot\nabla^2\vec{n})^{2}.

(20)

We can verify the bound with an inequality
(\nabla^{2}\hat{{\bf\it n}})^{2}\geq(\hat{{\bf\it n}}\dotproduct\nabla^{2}\hat{{\bf\it n}})^{2}
. The bound is saturated when
\nabla^{2}\hat{{\bf\it n}}\parallel\hat{{\bf\it n}}
.

One simple example saturating the geometric bound is a one-dimensional two-band system where
\hat{{\bf\it n}}=(\cos\theta,\sin\theta,0)
with
\theta=mk_{x}a
, the lattice constant
a
, and an integer
m
. In this case, the Berry curvature vanishes, and the quantum metric is uniform over the Brillouin zone, given by
g_{xx}=m^{2}/4
. Then
S_{4}=2\pi^{2}m^{4}
, saturating the geometric bound.
This two-band model with
m=1
is realized in a one-dimensional tight-binding model with two orbitals per site, labeled
\alpha=a,b
. Denoting the annihilation operator for orbital
\alpha
at
n
-th site by
{c}_{n,\alpha}
, the Hamiltonian in real space is given by

H=\sum_{n}\quantity(t \cre{c}_{n+1,a}\ani{c}_{n, b} + \text{h.c.})
.

It is worth noting that the harmonic condition in one-dimensional two-band systems has a geometric interpretation.
The harmonic condition in one dimension reduces to
(\partial_{x}^{2}\hat{{\bf\it n}})_{\perp}=0
, with
(\dots)_{\perp}
denoting the component perpendicular to
\hat{{\bf\it n}}
. This is equivalent to that a curve specified by
\hat{{\bf\it n}}
is geodesic on the sphere.

Generalization to multiband cases. —

We now generalize the above discussion to systems with multiple occupied bands. To this end, we introduce the non-Abelian quantum geometric tensor as
[
23
]

\displaystyle\mathcal{Q}_{\alpha\beta}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{a^{2}}(\partial_{\alpha}P)(1-P)(\partial_{\beta}P),

(21)

where
P
is the projection operator for all occupied bands.
\mathcal{Q}_{\alpha\beta}
is a matrix whose
(i,j)
component for occupied bands
i,j
is given by
\mathcal{Q}^{ij}_{\alpha\beta}=a^{-2}\matrixelement{\partial_{\alpha}u_i}{(1-P)}{\partial_{\beta}u_j}
.
Note that the trace of
\mathcal{Q}_{\alpha\beta}
over the band indices,
Q_{\alpha\beta}\equiv\Tr\mathcal{Q}_{\alpha\beta}
, yields the Abelian quantum geometric tensor for the Slater determinant state composed of all occupied states at
{\bf\it k}
,
\ket{\Psi_{\vec{k}}}=|u_{{\bf\it k}1}\dots u_{{\bf\it k}\nu}|

[
21
]
.

With the non-Abelian quantum geometric tensor (
21
), we can find the geometric bound on
S_{4}
for general band insulators (see Supplemental Material for the derivation)

\displaystyle S_{4}

\displaystyle\geq\frac{12(2\pi)^{2}\nu^{4/d-1}}{d(d+2)}\int[\differential{k}]\Tr[\mathcal{Q}_{\alpha\alpha}\mathcal{Q}_{\beta\beta}+\mathcal{Q}_{\alpha\beta}\mathcal{Q}_{\beta\alpha}],

(22)

where
\nu
is the number of occupied bands and the repeated spatial indices
\alpha,\beta=x,y
are summed over. Note that
\bar{q}=qn^{-1/d}
used to define
S_{4}
in Eq. (
13
) is not equal to
qa
when
\nu\neq 1
. The condition to saturate the bound (
22
) is given by

\displaystyle(1-P)(\nabla^{2}P)P=0.

(23)

This is the generalization of the harmonic condition (
18
) to multiband cases. When
\nu=1
, Eq. (
22
), (
23
) reduce to Eq. (
17
), (
18
) respectively.

Importantly, the integrand in Eq. (
22
), which involves a matrix product of two
\mathcal{Q}
, cannot be reduced to the Abelian quantum geometric tensor
\Tr\mathcal{Q}_{\alpha\beta}
. Rather, it is related to a Finsler metric, which measures distance between the points of the Grassmannian manifold associated with the occupied states
\{u_{{\bf\it k}1},\dots,u_{{\bf\it k}\nu}\}

[
24
]
.

A simple example of multiband cases saturating the geometric bound is given by an extension of the above one-dimensional tight-binding model to two dimensions. Consider a two-dimensional square lattice with lattice constant
a
and each site having four orbitals,
\alpha=1,2,3,4
. The hopping is finite only between orbital
1
at site
{\bf\it R}
and
3
at site
{\bf\it R}+{\bf\it a}_{y}
, or between
2
at site
{\bf\it R}
and
4
at site
{\bf\it R}+{\bf\it a}_{x}
, with
{\bf\it a}_{x(y)}
a vector with length
a
in the
x(y)
direction. The Hamiltonian is given by

H=\sum_{{\bf\it R}}(t_{y}{c}^{\dagger}_{1}({\bf\it R}){c}_{3}({\bf\it R}+{\bf\it a}_{y})+t_{x}{c}^{\dagger}_{2}({\bf\it R}){c}_{4}({\bf\it R}+{\bf\it a}_{x})+\mathrm{h.c.})
,
where
{c}_{\alpha}({\bf\it R})
is the annihilation operator for orbital
\alpha
at site
{\bf\it R}=(na,ma)
. In this case, the ground state with two electrons per site (
\nu=2
) is given by the collection of the independent bonding orbitals each formed by two orbitals on adjacent sites.

By calculating the quantum geometric tensor directly for this simple model, we obtain the bound on
S_{4}
given by the formula Eq. (
22
),

S_{4}\geq 3\pi^{2}/4
.
On the other hand, the static structure factor of this system in real space is given by

S({\bf\it R}_{1},{\bf\it R}_{2})=\expectationvalue{n(\vec{R}_1)n(\vec{R}_2)}-\expectationvalue{n(\vec{R}_1)}\expectationvalue{n(\vec{R}_2)}=(1/4)\sum_{{\bf\it d}}(\delta_{{\bf\it R}_{1},{\bf\it R}_{2}}-\delta_{{\bf\it R}_{1},{\bf\it R}_{2}+{\bf\it d}})

where
{\bf\it d}=\pm{\bf\it a}_{x},\pm{\bf\it a}_{y}
are the vectors connecting the adjacent sites. Then the Fourier transform of
S({\bf\it R}_{1},{\bf\it R}_{2})

gives the static structure factor
S({\bf\it q})=(2-\cos q_{x}a-\cos q_{y}a)/4
which yields
S_{4}=3\pi^{2}/4
, saturating the geometric bound.

S_{4}
bound and quantum geometry fluctuations —

The geometric bound on
S_{4}
further implies a lower bound in terms of quantum weight and Chern number. To express this relation, we introduce the average of Abelian quantum geometric tensor in
k
-space as
\bar{Q}_{\alpha\beta}\equiv 2\pi\nu^{-1+2/d}\int[\differential{k}]Q_{\alpha\beta}
. Its real part is the quantum weight
K=\real\bar{Q}
, while the imaginary part yields the Chern number in two dimensions as
C=-2\imaginary\bar{Q}_{xy}
.
We can show that the lower bound (
22
), which is given by the quadratic form of quantum geometric tensor
Q
, can itself be bounded with the average
\bar{Q}
using Cauchy-Schwartz inequality (see SM for details)

\displaystyle S_{4}

\displaystyle\geq\frac{12}{d(d+2)}\quantity((\tr\bar{Q})^2 + \tr(\bar{Q}^2))

(24)

\displaystyle\geq\frac{3(d+1)}{d+2}S_{2}^{2}+\frac{6}{d(d+2)}C_{\alpha}^{2}

(25)

where
C_{\alpha}=\epsilon_{\alpha\beta\gamma}C_{\beta\gamma}/2
.
C_{z}
is the Chern number
C
in
d=2
and
C_{\alpha}=0
in
d=1
. The sum over
\alpha=1,\dots,d
is implied.
The second inequality follows from relations
\tr(\bar{Q}^2)=\tr(K^2)+C^{2}_{\alpha}/2
,
\tr(K^2)\geq(\tr K)^{2}/d
and
S_{2}=2\tr K/d
. Eq. (
24
) is the second main result of this work. It relates
q^{4}
term of the structure factor to the leading order in the structure factor and the Chern number.
It is a direct generalization of Eq. (
8
), and applies to general band insulators with arbitrary
\nu
. Notably, the lower bound includes a contribution from the Chern number, which was absent in the atomic insulator example but is nonzero in Chern insulators.

For two-dimensional Chern insulators,
\tr K\geq\absolutevalue{C}
holds
[
12
,
25
]
, and thus
S_{2}\geq\absolutevalue{C}
follows. Hence, we find a topological bound on
S_{4}
:

\displaystyle S_{4}

\displaystyle\geq 3C^{2}.

(26)

Therefore,
S_{4}
has a universal lower bound determined by topology as
S_{2}
does in two dimensions.

For
\nu=1
, the bound (
24
) is saturated only when the band is harmonic
and
the quantum geometric tensor
Q_{\alpha\beta}
is constant in
k
-space. Therefore, the saturation of the topological bound (
26
) also requires uniform band geometry. The deviation from these bounds thus quantifies fluctuations of the quantum geometry in
k
-space.

Landau level example. —

Notably, both the geometric bound (
17
) and topological bound (
26
) are saturated for the lowest Landau level. For the Landau level with filling factor
\nu=1
, the structure factor is isotropic, given by

\displaystyle S({\bf\it q})

\displaystyle=1-e^{-q^{2}l^{2}/2}=\frac{q^{2}l^{2}}{2}-\frac{q^{4}l^{4}}{8}+\dots

is the magnetic length. With the carrier density

S_{4}=3
.
Using the quantum metric
g_{\alpha\beta}=\delta_{\alpha\beta}/(4\pi)
and Berry curvature
\Omega_{xy}=1/(2\pi)
, it is easy to see the lowest Landau level saturates both the geometric and topological bounds.

For the filling factor
\nu=2
,
the Landau level saturates the geometric bound (

26
) (see SM for details).
In general, to saturate the topological bound (
26
) for multiple bands occupied, all the non-Abelian quantum geometric tensor
\mathcal{Q}_{\alpha\beta}
needs to be proportional to identity.

Note that the static structure factor of Landau levels we discuss here should be distinguished from the guiding-center structure factor (or the projected structure factor), defined as the correlation function of the projected density operator onto the lowest Landau level. It is known that the guiding-center structure factor has a lower bound (Haldane bound) in
fractional
quantum Hall states
[

]
. However, by definition it vanishes identically at
integer
fillings. In contrast, our bound is for the
full
static structure factor, which contains a wealth of information about band geometry as we have shown.

So far, we have considered the structure factor for band insulators associated with the entire set of occupied bands. More generally, we can consider the band-resolved structure factor, associated only with a single band
\ket{u_{n\vec{k}}}
, by replacing the projector onto the occupied bands
P
with
P_{n}=\outerproduct{u_{n\vec{k}}}{u_{n\vec{k}}}
. This band-resolved structure factor also has a geometric bound (
17

\ket{u_n(\vec{k})}

For example, the band-resolved

and
S_{4}
for
n
-th Landau level saturate the bound (
25
) for any

(see SM for details), thus they are all harmonic. This contrasts with the trace condition

13
,
14
,
15
,
16

17
,
18

19
]
, which is satisfied only by the lowest Landau level.

In general, bands satisfying the trace condition are always harmonic, but the converse is not true. To see this, we rewrite the trace condition
\tr g=\absolutevalue{\Omega_{xy}}
as (see Ref.
[
13
,
14
,
15
]
and SM for details)

\displaystyle(D_{x}\pm iD_{y})\ket{u}=0.

(28)

The harmonic band is thus a broader class than the Chern band satisfying the trace condition.

Since harmonic Chern bands can be viewed as generalizations of (lowest and higher) Landau levels, the closeness of
S_{4}
to the geometric bound (
25

We also note that the topological bound (
26

As we discussed earlier, it further requires the band geometry to be uniform in
k
-space.
To illustrate this point, we consider two-dimensional Dirac fermions in a periodic magnetic field

However, strong spatial modulation of the magnetic field results in a highly inhomogeneous local density of states, which should lead to Wigner crystal rather than fractional Chern insulator under realistic Coulomb interaction. This example suggests the limitations of the trace condition in distinguishing “ideal” Chern bands and the

Conclusion. —
We have studied the
q^{4}
term in the small
q
expansion of the structure factor
S({\bf\it q})
and showed that it obeys a geometric bound determined by the quadratic form of the quantum geometric tensor in
k
-space. This geometric bound further implies the relation between the fourth-order and second-order terms of
S({\bf\it q})
, leading to a topological bound. The deviation from the topological bound quantifies band geometry fluctuations.

Our results highlight the significance of band geometry fluctuations in
k
-space. We showed that the
q^{4}
term in the structure factor, directly measurable by scattering experiments, captures the
k
-space distribution of the quantum geometric tensor through its quadratic form. It would be interesting to see if higher-order terms in
S(q)
can further capture more detailed information on
k
-space distribution of band geometry, and how it can affect other physical quantities.

Furthermore, our new bounds naturally guide us to the idealized systems that saturate the bounds. We see studying such idealized systems as a useful reference for understanding realistic models. This approach can be made more rigorous if one could quantify the deviation from the harmonic limit in a geometrically natural way. We plan to develop this idea in future work.

Appendix C
Trace condition

The trace condition
\tr g=\absolutevalue{\Omega_{xy}}
is equivalent to that the Bloch wavefunction
\ket{u_{\vec{k}}}
satisfies one of the following conditions:

\displaystyle(D_{x}\pm iD_{y})\ket{u}=0.

(84)

Mathematically equivalent expressions are found in Ref.
[
13
,
14
,
15
]
. Here we provide the proof for completeness.

First we note that when
\tr g=\pm{\Omega_{xy}}
, an inequality
\tr g\geq 2\sqrt{\det g}\geq\absolutevalue{\Omega_{xy}}
is saturated, which implies that
\tr g=2\sqrt{\det g}
. This means that
g
is proportional to identity:
g_{\alpha\beta}=g_{xx}\delta_{\alpha\beta}
. Therefore, the trace condition is equivalent to a condition
g_{xx}=g_{yy}=\pm{\Omega_{xy}}/2
, and the quantum geometric tensor
Q
takes the form
Q_{\alpha\beta}=g_{xx}(\delta_{\alpha\beta}\pm i\epsilon_{\alpha\beta})
, with
\epsilon_{\alpha\beta}
an antisymmetric tensor.
Then the Bloch wavefunction satisfies the following conditions:

\displaystyle\innerproduct{D_x u}{D_x u}=\innerproduct{D_y u}{D_y u}=\pm i\innerproduct{D_x u}{D_y u}.

(85)

This condition implies that
\ket{D_x u}
and
\ket{D_y u}
are parallel and have the same length, and are related to each other by a factor of
\pm i
. Therefore, it follows that

\displaystyle\ket{D_x u}=\pm i\ket{D_y u}

(86)

which yields Eq. (
84
).

We also note that Eq. (
84
) can be written as
(1-P)(\partial_{x}\pm i\partial_{y})P=0
with the projector
P=\outerproduct{u}{u}
. This expression appears in Ref.
[
15
]
.

As discussed in the main text, when a band is ideal and satisfies Eq. (
84
), the band is also harmonic. This can be seen by multiplying
(1-P)(D_{x}\mp iD_{y})
to Eq. (
84
) and use
\commutator{D_x}{D_y}=i(\partial_{x}A_{y}-\partial_{y}A_{x})=\Omega_{xy}
to obtain

\displaystyle(1-P)(D_{x}^{2}+D_{y}^{2}\mp\Omega_{xy})\ket{u}=(1-P)(D_{x}^{2}+D_{y}^{2})\ket{u}=0.

(87)

This is nothing but the condition for a band to be harmonic.
