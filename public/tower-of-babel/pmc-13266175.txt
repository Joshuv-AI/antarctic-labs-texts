Association of shoulder girdle function and glenohumeral joint alignment with pain during throwing in cases of shoulder posterosuperior impingement
Authors: Takuya Aso, Masaki Tamura, Takuya Maeda, Tomoyuki Takahashi, Kanji Furuya, Naoya Nishinaka
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundNo study has compared shoulder girdle function or glenohumeral joint alignment during shoulder abduction and external rotation in baseball players with posterosuperior impingement (PSI) with and without pitching-related shoulder pain. This study aimed to clarify factors associated with shoulder pain during throwing in players with PSI.MethodsThis retrospective observational study enrolled 53 professional or nonprofessional baseball players who visited our orthopedic department for a checkup. The upper thoracic movement, scapular upward rotation angle, humeral elevation angle, glenohumeral joint horizontal abduction angle in the shoulder abduction/external rotation position, and the presence/absence of shoulder pain during throwing were examined. The presence/absence of PSI was assessed using oblique axial magnetic resonance images during shoulder abduction and external rotation. Data were compared between the pain and no-pain groups. Factors associated with shoulder pain during throwing were identified using logistic regression analysis.ResultsAll participants had PSI findings on magnetic resonance images (pain group, n = 34; no-pain group, n = 19). The pain group showed lesser upper thoracic movement than no-pain group (P = .001). The factors associated with shoulder pain during throwing included decreased upper thoracic movement (odds ratio: 0.82, P = .002) and increased horizontal abduction of the glenohumeral joint in the abduction–external rotation position (odds ratio: 1.10, P = .045).ConclusionAmong baseball players with PSI findings, decreased upper thoracic movement may be associated with shoulder pain during throwing, whereas the association with increased glenohumeral horizontal abduction is more suggestive and should be interpreted cautiously.Keywords: Baseball, Posterosuperior impingement, Shoulder pain, Thoracic elevation, Glenohumeral joint alignment, Throwing

Posterosuperior impingement (PSI) is one of the most common pathologies of shoulder injury due to throwing.27 PSI occurs when the humeral head, posterior superior part of the glenoid fossa, and labrum collide during the late cocking to acceleration phase of the pitch, resulting in the rotator cuff being pinched between them.7,27 In overhead athletes, PSI can cause shoulder pain and instability during pitching, significantly affecting pitching performance.2,17,20 Horizontal hyperabduction of the glenohumeral joint is a risk factor for PSI development.27 Furthermore, the contact pressure on the posterior–superior part of the glenoid fossa increases when the glenohumeral joint is in a horizontal hyperabduction position.11,12 Therefore, to reduce the load on the shoulder joint during pitching, it is important to control horizontal hyperabduction of the glenohumeral joint after the late cocking phase.

To reduce the load on the shoulder joint during pitching, coordinated function of the glenohumeral joint, thoracic spine, and scapular is required.10,12,16,18,28 In particular, thoracic elevation mobility in the antigravity direction and scapular external rotation, posterior tilt, and upward rotation functions are important in preventing shoulder injury due to throwing, including PSI.10,13,16,18,28 On the other hand, even if magnetic resonance (MR) images or ultrasound images suggest PSI, this does not necessarily mean that overhead sports athletes experience shoulder pain when throwing.8,25 In our clinical experience, players with PSI findings but no shoulder pain during pitching have good shoulder girdle function, whereas players with shoulder pain often have poor shoulder girdle function. However, to the best of our knowledge, no study has compared shoulder girdle function or glenohumeral joint alignment in the abduction/external rotation position between players with PSI findings who do and do not experience shoulder joint pain during pitching. Clarifying these functional differences may provide crucial insights to guide physiotherapy intervention for players with PSI findings and shoulder pain during pitching.

This study aimed to clarify factors associated with shoulder pain during throwing in players with PSI findings. The hypothesis was that decreased shoulder girdle function and an increase in the horizontal abduction angle of the glenohumeral joint during shoulder abduction and external rotation would be associated with for shoulder pain during throwing.

Materials and methods

Participants

Using a database, we conducted a retrospective observational study and included participants with access to patient records. This study enrolled 53 male baseball players who visited our Orthopedic Department for a checkup between December 2019 and December 2024. Of these, 31 were nonprofessional baseball players (high school baseball players: n = 11; collegiate baseball players: n = 14; recreational baseball players: n = 6), and 22 were professional baseball players. All nonprofessional baseball players visited our hospital for examination and treatment of shoulder pain during throwing, while all professional baseball players visited our hospital for a medical checkup. Players who (1) had pain when raising their arms, (2) were unable to perform maximum arm elevation, and (3) had no PSI findings on MR image evaluation were excluded from this study. The Research Ethics Committee of Showa Medical University approved the study protocol (study number: 2024-259-A). In addition, after approval from the ethics committee, information about the study was made public, and participants were guaranteed the opportunity to refuse participation in the study.

Questionnaire

Questionnaires were distributed at the time of the medical examination or checkup. The questionnaire included information on height, weight, age, throwing upper limb, position, presence or absence of shoulder pain during throwing, location of shoulder pain (posterior, superior, anterior), and key temporal throwing events in which shoulder pain occurs (wind-up phase; the period from the initiation of movement until maximum lift of the stride leg: early cocking phase; the interval from maximum lift of the stride leg to stride foot contact: late cocking phase; the period between stride foot contact and maximum shoulder external rotation: arm acceleration phase; interval from maximum external rotation to ball release: follow-through phase; and period after ball release until completion of motion).

Evaluation of posterosuperior impingement

A doctor specializing in shoulder joint surgery (NN or KF) diagnosed PSI using images taken at the initial consultation with a 1.5- or 3.0-T magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) (MAGNETOM; Siemens Healthineers Co., Ltd., Tokyo, Japan). The images were taken with shoulder abduction and external rotation5,6 with the following imaging conditions (minimum–maximum): fast spin-echo; repetition time, 2,460-4,060 ms; field of view, 160-220 mm; echo time, 93.5-103.0 ms; and slice thickness, 3.0-4.0 mm. T2-weighted images or short T1 inversion recovery images were used for evaluation. All nonprofessional baseball players underwent MR arthrography, whereas professional baseball players did not because they did not wish to undergo this examination. Despite this difference in imaging modality, PSI was diagnosed using identical imaging criteria across all participants. Specifically, PSI was defined as contact between the rotator cuff and the posterior labrum accompanied by tissue changes on oblique axial images obtained in the abducted/external rotation position, regardless of whether MRI or MR arthrography was used (Fig. 1).5,6 MR arthrography was performed solely to enhance intra-articular visualization when clinically indicated, and the diagnostic criteria themselves did not differ between groups.

Evaluation of PSI. Contact and damage to the rotator cuff and posterior labrum (marked with a circle) was defined as PSI. (A) T2 weighted images. (B) Short T1 inversion recovery image. PSI, posterosuperior impingement.

Shoulder girdle function

The primary measurement items in this study were the thoracic elevation mobility, scapular upward rotation angle, and glenohumeral joint alignment. The secondary measurement item was the humeral elevation angle (Table I). The image analysis software used in this study was Synapse 4.1.0 (FUJIFILM Medical Systems Co., Ltd., Tokyo, Japan), a medical imaging workstation for manually measuring distances and angles on plain x-ray and MR images. This software does not have the functionality to automatically analyze scapular or thoracic movement, and all measurements in this study were performed manually by the examiner using the measurement tools within the software. Therefore, measurement accuracy depends on the examiner's measurement reproducibility, not on the accuracy of the software's automatic analysis. In this study, images taken at the initial consultation were analyzed.

The thoracic elevation mobility was measured using straightforward frontal radiographs of the upper extremities in a drooped position and at maximum scapular-plane elevation mobility.1,15 The image analysis software was utilized to determine the distance between the midpoints of the lines connecting the upper borders of the left and right first thoracic vertebral arch roots, as well as the upper borders of the left and right proximal clavicles (Fig. 2). The difference between the upper-extremity drooping position and maximum upper-extremity elevation of the scapular plane was calculated and subsequently used to represent the upper thoracic mobility. For the simple radiography, the patient sat upright, and the x-ray tube assembly was irradiated horizontally, focusing on the upper end of the sternum. The patient was later radiographed again with their upper extremities elevated without altering the x-ray tube assembly position. In addition, the participants maintained an anterior pelvic tilt during the simple radiographs to avoid excessive head anteversion, ensuring that the pelvic and cervical spine alignment did not influence the measurements.

Measurement of the upper thoracic mobility, scapular upward rotation angle, and humeral elevation angle. Upper thoracic mobility (①-②) was measured using a plain X-ray (frontal view) with the arms in the drooped (A) or elevated position (B). The scapular upward rotation angle (③) and humeral elevation angle (④) were measured using a plain X-ray (frontal view) with the arms in the elevated position (B).

The scapular upward rotation angle and humeral elevation angle were measured using plain x-ray frontal images of the upper limb at maximum elevation (Fig. 2).24 The limb on the throwing side was measured. The scapular upward rotation angle was measured as the angle between the line segment connecting the upper and lower edges of the scapular joint and the line perpendicular to the bottom edge of the image. The humeral elevation angle was measured as the angle between the long axis of the humerus at maximum scapular-plane elevation and the line perpendicular to the bottom edge of the image.1,24 This measurement aimed to examine if the angle of humeral elevation influences thoracic elevation mobility.

Glenohumeral joint alignment was evaluated by measuring the horizontal abduction angle of the glenohumeral joint using oblique axial MR images with the shoulder abducted and externally rotated (Fig. 3).22 In this study, the image showing the maximum transverse diameter of the glenoid was used for analysis. The horizontal abduction angle of the glenohumeral joint was measured as the angle between the line perpendicular to the glenoid and the long axis of the humerus.

Measurement of the horizontal abduction angle of the glenohumeral joint with shoulder abduction and external rotation The horizontal abduction angle of the glenohumeral joint was measured as the angle between the line perpendicular to the glenoid and the long axis of the humerus.

The intraexaminer reliability intraclass correlation coefficient (ICC (1, 1) = 0.991) for thoracic elevation measurements was based on the results of a previous study conducted by this research team.15

Statistical analysis

Statistical analysis was performed using JMP Student edition (Statistical Analysis System Institute Inc., Cary, USA). For the statistical analysis, players were divided into those who had shoulder pain during throwing (pain group) and those who did not have shoulder pain (no-pain group). If a player was able to pitch but was in pain, they were assigned to the pain group. To confirm measurement reproducibility, intra-rater reliability for scapular upward rotation, humeral elevation, and glenohumeral horizontal abduction was assessed using ICC(1,1) with 95% confidence intervals. The normality of each dataset was examined using the Shapiro–Wilk test. If the data were normally distributed, Levene test was used to check for homogeneity of variance. The Student t-test or Mann–Whitney U test was used to compare data between the two groups, as appropriate. In addition, Fisher exact test was used to compare the pitching sides between the two groups. When using the Student t-test, the 95% confidence intervals and effect size (Cohen d) were calculated. When using the Mann–Whitney U test, the effect size (r) was calculated. The effect size (Cohen d) was defined as 0.20 ≤ d < 0.50, small effect; 0.50 ≤ d < 0.80, medium effect; and d ≥ 0.80, large effect.3 The effect size when using the Mann–Whitney U test was defined as 0.10 ≤ r < 0.30, small effect; 0.30 ≤ r < 0.50, medium effect; and r ≥ 0.50, large effect.3 In addition, post hoc power (1−β) analysis was performed using the G∗Power 3.1.9.7 program (Heinrich-Heine Universität Düsseldorf, Düsseldorf, Germany). Multiple logistic regression analysis was also performed. The presence or absence of pain during throwing was the objective variable, and items with a P value <.20 in the two-group comparison of measurement items (upper thoracic mobility, scapular upward rotation angle, humerus elevation angle, or glenohumeral horizontal abduction angle) were included as explanatory variables.19 Odds ratios with 95% confidence intervals were reported. Model assumptions were evaluated within the limitations of the available software. Model fit was assessed using the model chi-square test, the area under the receiver operating characteristic curve, and the lack-of-fit test. Multicollinearity among explanatory variables was examined using pairwise Pearson correlation coefficients, with correlation values below 0.80 considered to indicate no substantial multicollinearity.26 Because advanced diagnostic tools (eg, influence statistics or detailed residual analyses) were not available in the software environment, model assumptions were evaluated using these practical indicators. The cutoff value of the items associated with pain during throwing was calculated using the Youden index. The significance level was set at P < .05.

Results

In this study, none of the players experienced pain when their upper limbs were fully elevated; therefore, none of the players were excluded (mean age: 22.5 ± 4.7 years; height: 176.0 ± 6.2 cm; body mass: 77.1 ± 10.2 kg; throwing side: 35 right, 18 left; position: 41 pitcher, 4 catcher, and 8 fielder) (Table II). All players had PSI findings on MR image evaluation. Of them, 34 were in the pain group and 19 were in the no-pain group (Fig. 4). The pain group consisted of 31 nonprofessional baseball players and three professional baseball players, while the no-pain group only contained professional baseball players. All patients in the pain group had pain in the posterior shoulder joint from the late cocking phase to ball release when throwing.

Discussion

PSI is a typical shoulder injury due to throwing.27 Reduced thoracic movement and upward scapular rotation during pitching, as well as excessive horizontal shoulder abduction, are associated with its onset.10,12,16,18 In this study, players with shoulder pain during throwing had less upper thoracic movement than players without shoulder pain. In addition, logistic regression analysis revealed that decreased upper thoracic movement and an increased glenohumeral horizontal abduction angle were associated with shoulder pain during throwing.

However, the association with shoulder horizontal abduction should be interpreted more cautiously, as its role is less certain and may be influenced by measurement and confounding limitations. In this study, all patients had PSI findings on MR images. Therefore, athletes who maintain greater thoracic elevation mobility or exhibit smaller glenohumeral horizontal abduction angles in the abducted–external rotation position may be more likely to present without shoulder pain, although these characteristics may also reflect differences in athlete level rather than symptomatic status. Given this possibility, it is important to consider the influence of group composition when interpreting the findings.

In our cohort, almost all nonprofessional players presented with shoulder pain, whereas all professional players visited our department for a routine medical checkup. As a result, the pain group consisted predominantly of nonprofessional athletes, and the no-pain group consisted exclusively of professional athletes. This imbalance was not attributable to the exclusion criteria, but rather reflects the characteristics of the available database: asymptomatic nonprofessional players rarely undergo medical evaluation, and no symptomatic professional players presented during the study period. Therefore, the cohort is not population-based and does not represent the full clinical spectrum of baseball players with or without shoulder pain. These differences in competitive level, physical conditioning, and training background may have influenced the observed associations, and the results should be interpreted with caution.

The thoracic position is reported to be closely related to scapular movement and glenohumeral joint movement.13,28 Yoshimi et al28 reported that when thoracic movement is restricted and maximum external rotation is performed with shoulder abduction, the scapular posterior tilt is restricted. Miyakoshi et al13 reported that when comparing scapular alignment during shoulder abduction and external rotation between thoracic flexion and thoracic extension, the scapular posterior tilt and external rotation angle were smaller in the thoracic flexion position. Suzuki et al21 showed that the scapular anteversion and glenohumeral joint horizontal abduction angles were larger during shoulder abduction and external rotation in thoracic flexion. These reports support the known kinematic relationship that when thoracic elevation is restricted during arm elevation, scapular posterior tilt and external rotation are restricted, resulting in the glenohumeral joint being more likely to be displaced into horizontal abduction. In addition, Mihata et al10 found that increasing the angle of scapular internal rotation in the shoulder abduction/external rotation position increases the contact pressure on the posterior superior glenohumeral joint. In this study, players with PSI findings and shoulder pain during throwing had limited upper thoracic mobility when raising the upper limb. In addition, all players in the pain group had shoulder pain from the late cocking phase to ball release. In other words, players in the pain group had poor thoracic elevation movement after the late cocking phase during throwing, which may be associated with increased loading on the posterior superior glenohumeral joint. Therefore, a decrease in upper thoracic movement may be associated with shoulder pain during throwing.

Furthermore, the maximum shoulder external rotation angle during the late cocking phase is related to overhead athletes' performance.9 Matsuo et al9 used a three-dimensional motion analysis device to compare players with fast and slow ball speeds and reported that players with faster ball speeds had a greater maximum shoulder external rotation angle during the late cocking phase. In addition, the maximum shoulder external rotation during the late cocking phase is a complex movement composed of glenohumeral joint external rotation, scapular posterior tilt, and thoracic extension.14 Therefore, fully utilizing scapular posterior tilt and thoracic extension to ensure maximum external rotation without pain allows for efficient energy transfer during the acceleration phase, which may ultimately lead to the maintenance and improvement of overhead athletes' performance.

In this study, an increase in the horizontal abduction angle of the glenohumeral joint in the abduction and external rotation position was associated with the presence of shoulder pain during throwing. Mihata et al11 investigated the relationship between the horizontal abduction angle of the glenohumeral joint and the contact pressure at the posterior superior part of the shoulder joint and showed that the contact pressure increases as the horizontal abduction angle of the glenohumeral joint increases. Similarly, when comparing the glenohumeral joint contact pressure between normal shoulders and shoulders with an anterior laxity capsule, they reported that the contact pressure was greater in shoulders with an anterior laxity capsule.12 In addition, Funakoshi et al4 reported that in PSI cases, the anterior components of the shoulder joint, including the middle glenohumeral ligament, are often disrupted. A possible factor contributing to increased glenohumeral horizontal abduction during the late cocking phase of pitching is a lack of scapular motion.16 Nomura et al16 used a three-dimensional motion analysis device to examine the relationship between the glenohumeral horizontal abduction angle and the scapular external rotation angle at maximum shoulder external rotation during the late cocking phase. They reported that players with a smaller scapular external rotation angle had a greater glenohumeral horizontal abduction angle. Based on these findings, it is possible that the players with pain were unable to fully exert scapular external rotation due to reduced thoracic elevation mobility, resulting in excessive glenohumeral horizontal abduction as a compensation. This movement pattern may be associated with laxity in the anterior shoulder structures, which may in turn be related to a shoulder joint alignment prone to deviation toward horizontal abduction and increased posterior–superior load on the shoulder joint during throwing. Therefore, we speculate that there may be an association between the horizontal abduction angle of the glenohumeral joint during shoulder abduction and external rotation and shoulder pain during throwing.

This study has some limitations. First, the sample size was relatively small (n = 53), which limited the statistical power of several analyses. Post hoc analysis revealed that the power (1-β) for upper thoracic movement was 0.31, and although a significant difference was observed, the low power suggests that the estimate may be unstable. In contrast, no significant difference was observed in glenohumeral horizontal abduction angle, for which the effect size was small (0.18) and the power was only 0.09, raising the possibility of a type II error. In addition, although there was no significant difference in the horizontal abduction angle of the glenohumeral joint in the abduction–external rotation position between the two groups, logistic regression analysis identified this variable as being associated with the presence of shoulder pain. This discrepancy may reflect residual confounding and insufficient power in the two-group comparison. Moreover, because the number of participants without pain was limited, the logistic regression model may be at risk of overfitting despite acceptable model fit indices. Therefore, the regression results should be interpreted with caution, and future studies with larger sample sizes are needed to validate these findings. Second, all nonprofessional baseball players underwent MRI and MR arthrography examinations to determine the presence or absence of PSI, but professional baseball players only underwent MRI. It has been reported that MR arthrography examination is superior to MRI for diagnosing intra-articular lesions.23 Although identical diagnostic criteria for PSI were applied across all participants based on rotator cuff–posterior labrum contact and associated tissue changes on oblique axial images in the abducted/external rotation position, the difference in imaging modality may have influenced lesion detectability and characterization. This introduces the possibility of classification bias, as PSI findings may not have been identified with equal sensitivity in both groups. Nonprofessional players underwent arthrography because they presented with shoulder pain and required detailed intra-articular evaluation, whereas professional players declined arthrography due to its invasiveness. Because this was a retrospective study, the specific reasons for refusal could not be confirmed. Subgroup analysis by imaging modality was not feasible because imaging modality and competitive level were completely confounded. Future studies should adopt a standardized imaging protocol to ensure diagnostic consistency. Third, pain was treated as a binary variable, and detailed clinical characterization was not available. Because this was a retrospective study using a database, we were unable to collect sufficient clinical information that may affect the occurrence of pain, such as number of pitch, duration of shoulder joint pain, characteristics of throwing motion, and clinical evaluation of shoulder girdle function. The absence of these data limits our ability to interpret the imaging-based associations and raises the possibility of residual confounding. As a result, the findings of this study should not be considered supportive of the proposition that thoracic mobility or glenohumeral alignment alone can predict shoulder pain.

While there are limitations to this study, one of its strengths is that it clarified that a decrease in upper thoracic movement and an increase in the horizontal abduction angle of the glenohumeral joint in the abduction and external rotation position were associated with shoulder pain during throwing. Because these items can be easily evaluated using images, they may help characterize athletes who present with shoulder pain during throwing and may provide useful information for education and guidance aimed at reducing shoulder symptoms in overhead athletes.

Conclusion

The aim of this study was to clarify factors associated with shoulder pain during throwing in players with PSI. Among baseball players with PSI, those with shoulder pain during throwing had less upper thoracic movement than players without shoulder pain. Overall, decreased upper thoracic movement appears to be clearly associated with shoulder pain during throwing, whereas the association between increased glenohumeral horizontal abduction in the abducted/external rotation position and shoulder pain is more suggestive and should be interpreted cautiously due to measurement and confounding limitations.
