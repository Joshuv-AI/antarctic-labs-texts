Partnerships and practices: a scoping review of engaging older adults living with frailty in the research process
Authors: Erin Davis, Ishaan Cheema, Chantelle Zimmer, Lindsay Morrison, Caitlin McClurg, Jayna Holroyd-Leduc
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundFrailty is a multidimensional condition that increases vulnerability to poor health outcomes, yet older adults living with frailty are often underrepresented in research or involved only in limited ways. Engaging people with lived experience can improve the relevance, acceptability, and impact of research, but little is known about the involvement of older adults living with frailty across the research process. This scoping review explored how engagement is practiced, the roles older adults play at different stages of research, and the effectiveness of these approaches.MethodThe review followed the methodological guidelines of the Joanna Briggs Institute. Five databases and Google Scholar were searched for articles published in English between 2005 and 2025. Eligible studies included those that described when and how older adults living with frailty were involved in the research process. Two reviewers independently screened titles, abstracts, and full texts to select articles and extracted data using a structured template. Data were analyzed to categorize engagement approaches, assess levels of engagement across research stages, and evaluate the effectiveness of these approaches.ResultsTen studies met the inclusion criteria. Most used participatory and action-oriented, co-design and human-centred design, patient and public involvement and priority-setting, or dialogic and capacity-building approaches. Engagement was strongest during the early stages of research. Older adults living with frailty frequently contributed to identifying priorities, shaping study aims, refining tools and interventions, and participating in collaborative workshops. Across studies, involvement was described as meaningful and personally empowering, contributing to increased confidence, social connection, and a sense of being valued. Engagement also produced tangible research outcomes. However, involvement declined substantially in later stages. Only a few studies engaged older adults in interpreting data, reviewing results, co-presenting findings, and contributing to implementation efforts, and no study provided shared decision-making authority across the full research cycle.ConclusionThis review shows that engaging older adults living with frailty in the research process is both feasible and valuable. To advance the field, future work must extend engagement beyond early stages by fostering continuity, transparency, and shared decision-making through flexible practices and explicit attention to power and governance.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s40900-026-00952-y.

Background

As the global population continues to age, it has become increasingly vital for research to reflect the distinct needs, experiences, and priorities of older adults, particularly those living with frailty. Frailty, characterized by physical, cognitive, and social vulnerabilities, places older individuals at heightened risk for adverse health outcomes, diminished quality of life, and greater dependence on health care and social support systems [1]. Recent global estimates indicate that approximately 17% of community-dwelling older adults experience multidimensional frailty, reflecting vulnerabilities across various domains [2]. Prevalence is substantially higher in assisted living, where an estimated 52% of residents are affected by frailty [3].

Despite the high prevalence of frailty and the relevance of their lived experience, older adults living with frailty remain underrepresented in research, both as participants and partners [4–6]. Multiple barriers contribute to this underrepresentation. For research participants, restrictive eligibility criteria often exclude older adults based on advanced age, multimorbidity, or cognitive impairment, limiting opportunities for involvement from the outset [6]. Among those who are eligible, participation may be further constrained by study designs that lack appropriate accommodations, such as adapted consent materials, flexible scheduling, and transportation or mobility supports [4–6]. In addition, commonly used measurement approaches may not adequately capture outcomes that are meaningful or relevant to older adults [7]. Older adults living with frailty also face barriers to involvement as research partners, including assumptions about their capacity to engage in research activities and a lack of structures and supports that facilitate sustained participation in research planning and decision-making [4–6]. Together, these barriers limit opportunities for meaningful involvement and reduce the relevance, applicability, and impact of research findings for this population.

Existing evidence highlights that engaging people with lived experience early in the research process can enhance ethical rigour, improve the quality of research outcomes, and facilitate more effective translation of findings into practice [8, 9]. In this review, engagement refers to the involvement of older adults living with frailty as active contributors to the research process, rather than solely as research participants [10]. This includes a range of collaborative approaches through which these individuals help shape research priorities, study design, data collection and analysis, dissemination, and the implementation of findings. We use the term engagement as an overarching concept to encompass diverse approaches that involve community members in research, including participatory, co-design, patient and public involvement, and related approaches. Here, community refers to who is engaged rather than the setting in which engagement occurs.

Over the past decade, community engagement in research has evolved, with growing efforts to better understand the processes, outcomes, and impacts of involving people with relevant lived, caregiving, or professional experience as partners throughout the research cycle. For example, initiatives such as the Patient-Centered Outcomes Research Institute have contributed to the development of the science of engagement by evaluating how the involvement of community members and other non-academic partners influences research design, conduct, and relevance [11]. Early involvement helps ensure that the specific priorities and needs of older adults are addressed, while simultaneously fostering trust, ethical accountability, and a sense of ownership over the research process [9]. Engaging people with lived experience during the planning and design phases has been shown to produce interventions that are more relevant, contextually appropriate, and responsive to the real-world challenges they encounter [8]. Conversely, late or limited engagement reduces the ability of people to influence research priorities and methodologies, potentially resulting in outcomes that are misaligned with their lived experiences and practical needs.

At present, there is no clear guidance regarding the most effective methods for engaging older adults living with frailty throughout the research process. A scoping review is appropriate for this study because it allows for a broad examination of the literature to map existing approaches and identify strengths and limitations in engagement practices. This review will focus on the timing and nature of involvement of older adults living with frailty, investigating when and how they are engaged across different stages of the research process. By exploring these dimensions, the review will help us understand the impact of current engagement practices on the experiences of those involved and the research outcomes, supporting the development of recommendations for more meaningful, systematic, and impactful involvement. Ultimately, this study aims to advance research practices that are well-planned, inclusive, and responsive to the priorities of older adults living with frailty.

Accordingly, this review is guided by three research questions: [1] What community engagement approaches are currently being used in research involving older adults living with frailty [2]? How are older adults living with frailty contributing to the research process? and [3] How effective are these approaches in engaging older adults living with frailty?

Method

Design

A scoping review was conducted to systematically identify and synthesize evidence on engagement practices involving older adults living with frailty. The review followed the methodological framework described by Arksey and O’Malley [12] and refined by Levac et al. [13], operationalized through the Joanna Briggs Institute guidance for scoping reviews. Reporting adheres to the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses Extension for Scoping Reviews (PRISMA-ScR) and incorporates relevant elements of the PRISMA 2020 checklist to ensure transparency and reproducibility [14, 15]. The protocol was developed collaboratively by a multidisciplinary team affiliated with the University of Calgary’s Centre on Aging and was prospectively registered with the Open Science Framework on March 4, 2025. The review was initially part of a broader project and conceived as a rapid review. However, as the project evolved and a scoping review was deemed more appropriate based on the preliminary search, the methodology was revised prior to study selection to better align with the project aims. A new registration was created to provide a public record consistent with the methods implemented (registration DOI: 10.17605/OSF.IO/BH82T).

Identification of relevant studies

A comprehensive search strategy was designed with support from a health sciences research librarian to ensure breadth and methodological rigour. The search followed a three-step process. First, a preliminary search of MEDLINE and CINAHL was conducted to identify relevant keywords and index terms. These terms were then incorporated into a comprehensive search strategy, which was adapted for each database to account for indexing differences and syntax. Finally, the reference lists of included studies were hand-searched to identify any additional publications not captured through the database search.

MEDLINE (Ovid), CINAHL, Embase, Scopus, and PsycINFO were searched from January 2005 to February 2025, and were supplemented by a Google Scholar search (first 10 pages) to capture additional peer-reviewed studies not indexed in the core databases. The search combined controlled vocabulary (e.g., medical subject heading terms) and free-text keywords related to older adults, frailty, and community engagement approaches. The full search strategy for each database, including Boolean operators (e.g., AND, OR) and applied filters (e.g., English language and publication date limits), is presented in Appendix 1.

Study selection

Eligibility criteria

Our inclusion criteria were based on the population, concept, context framework: Population—older adults (55 years or older) living with frailty; Concept—engagement of older adults in the research process; Context—community, health, and social care settings. Studies were eligible for inclusion if they involved older adults living with frailty aged 55 years or older. This age threshold was chosen to account for the earlier onset of frailty that can occur among individuals with chronic health conditions or those experiencing socioeconomic disadvantages [1]. A formal or clinical diagnosis of frailty was not required; studies were included if frailty was clinically assessed, identified through validated screening tools, self-reported, or described by researchers based on indicators of vulnerability. Frailty was understood broadly as a state of increased susceptibility to adverse outcomes following physical, cognitive, emotional, or social stressors [16]. Within this broad conceptualization, physical frailty often reflects reduced strength, endurance, or mobility; cognitive frailty refers to situations where physical vulnerability co-occurs with measurable cognitive decline [17, 18]; and social frailty reflects limited social participation, reduced access to support, or experiences of isolation or loneliness [19, 20].

Eligible study designs included experimental, quasi-experimental, observational, and qualitative studies, as well as systematic and scoping reviews that aligned with the research questions and provided sufficient detail about the included studies to support data extraction. Narrative reviews were excluded because they generally summarize findings at a higher level and do not report the methodological or population-specific information required for this review. Studies needed to describe when and how older adults were engaged in the research process, such as during problem identification, study design, data collection, analysis, or dissemination, and had to use community engagement approaches, such as participatory, co-design, or patient-oriented research. Only peer-reviewed studies published in English within the past 20 years were included, as the early-mid 2000s mark the beginning of modern community engagement approaches (e.g. Community-Based Participatory Research (CBPR), Participatory Action Research (PAR), Patient and Public Involvement (PPI)) [21–23] and the widespread adoption of standardized frailty models such as the Frailty Phenotype and Frailty Index [24, 25].

Selection process

All citations retrieved from the search were imported into Covidence systematic review software [26], and duplicates were automatically removed. Two reviewers (ED and IC) conducted all three stages of screening. The process began with a calibration exercise in which both reviewers independently screened a pilot set of 20 articles to ensure consistent application of the eligibility criteria. Following calibration, the two reviewers independently completed both the title and abstract screening and the full-text review phases. Any discrepancies identified at either stage were discussed between the two reviewers, and unresolved disagreements were referred to other members of the research team (CZ, LM, and JHL) for a final decision. The reference lists of all studies included after the full-text review were then hand-searched to ensure comprehensive coverage. Additional articles identified underwent full-text screening by the two primary reviewers (ED and IC). An overview of the screening and selection process, including reasons for exclusion at the full-text stage, is presented in the PRISMA flow diagram (see Fig. 1).

Data extraction

Data were extracted independently by two members of the research team (ED and IC) using a customized data extraction template within Covidence. Extracted information included study details, characteristics of older adults living with frailty, study design, engagement practices, and outcomes relevant to the study’s methodology and the involvement of older adults (see Supplementary File). The extraction template was piloted on three studies to ensure that the fields captured the necessary information and to refine instructions for consistent data entry. Any discrepancies between ED and IC were resolved through discussion and, when needed, consultation with other members of the research team (CZ, LM, and JHL).

At the completion of data extraction, several studies contained unclear or incomplete descriptions of their engagement methods or reported outcomes. Following best practice guidance recommending author contact in cases of missing or unclear information in evidence syntheses [27, 28], a member of the research team (ED) contacted the corresponding authors of these studies to request clarification. Of the nine authors contacted, six responded and provided additional detail that allowed the team to confirm or refine extracted information. These clarifications were incorporated into the final dataset to enhance the completeness and accuracy of the review’s findings.

Data analysis and synthesis

Data analysis was conducted separately for each research question using methods appropriate for the extracted data. All analyses were completed after data extraction was finalized and author clarifications were incorporated. Analyses for Research Questions 1 and 3 followed a similar process due to their reliance on narrative descriptions of engagement.

A directed content analysis was conducted for Research Question 1 to identify engagement approaches. One member of the research team (ED) reviewed the extracted data and developed an initial coding framework based on descriptions of engagement methods reported in the included studies. Codes were iteratively refined through comparison across studies and discussion with IC, CZ, and JHL. Related codes were then grouped into higher-order categories representing distinct engagement approaches (i.e., participatory and action-oriented, co-design and human-centred design, PPI and priority-setting, and dialogic and capacity-building).

To address Research Question 2, the Participatory Research Choice Points Framework developed by Vaughn and Jacquez [10] was used to understand when and how older adults living with frailty were engaged in the research process. This framework conceptualizes research as a series of choice points—decision moments at each stage in the research process (i.e., partner, design, collect, analyze, disseminate, act)—where the research team determines the degree (i.e., inform, consult, involve, collaborate, empower) of stakeholder involvement (see Table 1). Higher levels of engagement confer greater stakeholder power and influence over research priorities, processes, and outcomes. Two members of the research team (ED and IC) independently reviewed the extracted data from each study to classify the level of involvement of older adults living with frailty at each research stage. For example, studies where older adults were informed of research outcomes were classified as inform, whereas those where older adults co-developed research questions, methods, or contributed to data interpretation were classified as involve or collaborate. Discrepancies were discussed to reach consensus, and any remaining disagreements were resolved by consulting CZ and JHL to ensure consistent and accurate classifications.

Finally, a thematic analysis was conducted for Research Question 3 to assess the effectiveness of engagement approaches. Because no studies directly evaluated engagement effectiveness, we interpreted effectiveness broadly using descriptions of partnership and engagement processes, the experiences of older adults living with frailty, and reported research outcomes. ED initially coded the data inductively to identify patterns related to these areas. Codes were organized into a working coding framework and iteratively refined through discussion with IC, CZ, and JHL. Related codes were then grouped into broader themes that captured common patterns across studies. The themes were subsequently interpreted using two complementary perspectives. First, themes and supporting data were compared with each study’s stated goals and authors’ descriptions of success to determine perceived effectiveness. Second, themes were considered in relation to the participatory principles and decision points outlined in the Participatory Research Choice Points Framework [10] to assess methodological alignment. This process enabled consideration of effectiveness across multiple dimensions.

Results

Search yield

The database and Google Scholar searches yielded 1,546 records, of which 1,067 remained after duplicates were removed. Following title and abstract screening and full-text review, 10 studies met the inclusion criteria (see Fig. 1). Citation searching identified eight additional articles. Only one of these articles was retained; it was a companion publication to an included study and was used to supplement data extraction. As a result, 11 articles informed data extraction across the 10 included studies, with one study reported in two publications (Bindels et al., 2014, 2015) [29, 30].

Study characteristics

The 10 included peer-reviewed studies were published between 2014 and 2024, covering the past decade of research. Studies were conducted across six countries, predominantly in Europe, where participatory and co-design approaches to frailty research have been more extensively applied. These included the Netherlands (n = 3), Ireland (n = 2), United Kingdom (n = 1), Norway (n = 1), and Spain (n = 1). Two studies from Canada (n = 2) provided the only non-European perspective, modestly broadening the geographical and contextual scope of the evidence base.

The 10 studies employed either qualitative (n = 8) or mixed-method designs (n = 2). They were operationalized through a range of community engagement approaches, including participatory and action-oriented (n = 3), co-design and human-centred design (n = 3), PPI and priority-setting (n = 2), and dialogic and capacity-building approaches (n = 2). All studies aimed to co-develop, evaluate, or improve care programs, technologies, and decision-making frameworks in collaboration with older adults living with frailty. The studies clustered around four primary areas: service and care pathways (e.g., frailty care pathways, deprescribing, shared decision-making frameworks) (n = 5), technology and innovation (e.g., digital monitoring systems, co-created assistive technologies) (n = 2), research governance and priority setting for frailty-related topics (n = 2), and social connection and isolation (n = 1).

People involved in the studies were generally aged 65 years and older; however, one study included adults as young as 55. Frailty was commonly self-identified, although several studies used validated indicators. Many studies included both frail and pre-frail individuals, reflecting the continuum of vulnerability within aging populations. Across the studies, most involved participants who self-identified as living with frailty without specifying a subtype (n = 7). In contrast, some studies explicitly focused on individuals with physical frailty (n = 3) or social frailty (n = 1), and one study applied a multidimensional frailty measure. No studies focused specifically on cognitive frailty. Sample sizes were typically small, ranging from 2 to 30, but were often expanded to include family caregivers, advocates, and health care professionals, providing multi-stakeholder perspectives. For this review, analyses were focused on the involvement and experiences of older adults living with frailty. Where findings were reported separately, only data relating to older adults living with frailty were extracted and analyzed; where findings were reported collectively, results were interpreted with this focus in mind.

Research question 1

Across the 10 included studies, most adopted participatory or co-design approaches that actively involved older adults living with frailty throughout the research process, while a smaller number focused on large-scale priority setting or framework development. Collectively, these approaches demonstrate an evolving commitment to meaningful engagement, emphasizing iterative feedback, shared decision-making, and responsiveness to people with lived experience.

Participatory and action-oriented approaches

Three studies adopted PAR or action-oriented approaches emphasizing sustained collaboration and reflexivity. Bindels et al. [29, 30] used a responsive evaluation model in which older adults acted as co-researchers through monthly meetings, joint interviews, and co-analysis sessions exploring social connectedness and quality of life. Scheffelaar et al. [31] conducted a multi-year participatory case study with older adults, individuals with mental health conditions, and people with intellectual disabilities to improve long-term care participation tools, using co-researcher training, structured team meetings, and shared data interpretation. MacLeod et al. [32] integrated PAR as a methodology with an integrated knowledge translation approach to address social isolation during the COVID-19 pandemic, involving older adults and caregivers from rural and long-term care contexts in taskforce meetings, virtual discussions, and knowledge exchange events. Collectively, these studies exemplify participatory engagement in which older adults influenced research questions, data interpretation, and dissemination activities.

Co-design and human-centred design

Three studies implemented structured co-design frameworks that positioned older adults as partners in intervention development. O’Donnell et al. [33] employed a participatory co-design process grounded in service design principles to capture lived experience and translate it into improved communication and discharge practices. The approach incorporated filmed narrative interviews, empathy mapping, and joint redesign workshops with older adults who had recent hospital admissions, including individuals identified as living with frailty. The process redirected improvement priorities toward patient dignity and interpersonal communication. Silcock et al. [34] used experience-based co-design integrated with the Medical Research Council framework to co-develop a deprescribing intervention in primary care, combining a trigger film with iterative workshops involving older adults, caregivers, and clinicians. Moral et al. [35] applied co-creation and human-centred design principles through 18 iterative workshops to co-develop a digital frailty monitoring tool. Older adults engaged alongside caregivers and professionals in sessions that incorporated simplified visuals, paced facilitation, and iterative feedback cycles. These co-design studies collectively emphasized user-centred insight, iterative refinement, and joint decision-making across design stages.

PPI and priority-setting approaches

Two studies focused on broad collaboration and consultation to shape frailty-related priorities. Bethell et al. [36] coordinated a nationwide James Lind Alliance priority-setting partnership involving older adults, caregivers, and clinicians through surveys, workshops, and consensus meetings to identify the top national research priorities for frailty. Travers et al. [37] implemented a staged PPI approach to co-design an exercise and nutrition intervention for frailty reversal. Engagement comprised Socratic discussion groups, individual telephone interviews, and online workshops during the pandemic. Older adults engaged in the project represented a continuum from non-frail to pre-frail and frail, aligning with the project’s preventive and restorative aims. Together, these studies illustrate consultative and collaborative engagement models that informed research design and enhanced intervention feasibility.

Dialogic and capacity-building approaches

Two studies emphasized dialogue and mutual learning rather than co-design of interventions. Lund et al. [38] employed a Responsible Research and Innovation (RRI) framework, conducting dialogue cafés in assisted living facilities to explore residents’ experiences with assistive technology. The cafés directly involved older residents, while the advisory group included next-of-kin representatives and user organisations. van de Pol et al. [39] used qualitative inquiry to co-develop a shared decision-making teaching framework for health professionals. Older adults and clinicians helped to identify core competencies, communication needs, and strategies for integrating shared decision-making into frailty care. These studies extend the concept of engagement beyond co-production to include ethical reflection, education, and capacity building within care systems.

Research question 2

Applying the Participatory Research Choice Points Framework across the 10 studies revealed clear stage-based patterns in how older adults living with frailty were engaged throughout the research process [10], as shown in Fig. 2. Engagement was strongest in the early stages, particularly during the partner and design phases, where older adults most frequently contributed through co-design activities, experiential insights, shared agenda setting, and the shaping of study materials or priorities. Engagement declined progressively across later stages, with far fewer studies involving older adults in data analysis, dissemination, and implementation activities, and no study reaching the empower level at any stage. Across all studies, older adults contributed meaningfully to developing research aims, refining processes, and providing experiential expertise; however, decision-making authority remained with researchers, limiting structural empowerment. Partner stage

The partner stage involves establishing collaborations, defining shared priorities, and determining how decision-making will be distributed between researchers and individuals with lived experience [10]. Six studies were classified as collaborate [29–34, 36], one as involve [35], two as consult [37, 38], and one as inform [39]. At this stage, older adults contributed by identifying research priorities, sharing experiential concerns, and shaping the initial direction of projects. Co-researcher models [29–32] and co-design partnerships [33–35, 37] offered the deepest involvement, while the national priority-setting work by Bethell et al. [36] extended older adult influence into broader research agendas. Overall, partnership activities reflected meaningful input into agenda setting but seldom involved negotiating shared authority, limiting opportunities for structural empowerment at this early stage.

Design stage

The design stage focuses on creating the research plan, selecting or adapting methods, developing tools or materials, and ensuring that the study reflects the values and needs of people with lived experience [10]. Seven studies were classified as collaborate [29–35, 37], two as involve [36, 38], and one as consult [39]. Older adults contributed to shaping study procedures, developing intervention components, refining communication strategies, and ensuring materials were acceptable and relevant. Co-design and human-centred design approaches were most prominent at this stage, using activities such as iterative workshops, empathy mapping, trigger films, and virtual taskforce meetings to integrate lived experience into the study design. Taken together, the design stage demonstrated the most consistent and meaningful collaboration, underscoring that co-design remains the primary entry point for deep engagement in frailty research.

Collect stage

The collect stage refers to how data are gathered and the extent to which individuals with lived experience participate in shaping data collection procedures and facilitation methods [10]. Most studies were classified as involve at this stage, with fewer as collaborate [29–31, 35] and one as consult [39]. Contributions included offering narrative accounts, engaging in dialogue cafés, providing iterative feedback, testing or refining prototypes, and co-facilitating aspects of data collection. Engagement centred on lived experience and tool refinement rather than decision-making. Overall, older adults played an active role in shaping data collection procedures, though their engagement typically remained advisory rather than collaborative.

Analyze stage

The analyze stage reflects involvement in analyzing data, interpreting findings, shaping meaning-making processes, and validating themes [10]. Levels of engagement declined markedly during analysis. Most studies were classified as consult, with only three achieving the collaborate level [29, 30, 32, 33] and two the involve level [31, 34]. Older adults contributed to interpretation by reviewing preliminary findings, sharing personal narratives that informed meaning-making, validating themes, and participating in reflective dialogues. However, analytic authority consistently remained with researchers, and co-analysis was limited to studies using PAR or responsive evaluation. The decline in engagement highlights the continued dominance of researcher-held interpretive power and the challenges of maintaining collaboration during the analytic decision-making process.

Disseminate stage

The disseminate stage involves deciding how findings are shared, contributing to knowledge translation strategies, and co-presenting or co-authoring outputs [10]. Engagement varied widely. Four studies were classified as collaborate [29–33], two as consult [36, 38], and four as inform [34, 35, 37, 39]. Older adults contributed by advising on knowledge exchange strategies, reviewing outputs, co-authoring materials, and co-presenting findings. However, in many studies, dissemination primarily involved receiving results rather than shaping how findings were communicated. Reporting practices were inconsistent across studies. These patterns indicate that, while some projects engaged older adults meaningfully in dissemination, most limited their involvement to consultation or information sharing.

Act stage

The act stage refers to how study findings are implemented, translated into practice, or used to inform systems or services, and the extent to which individuals with lived experience participate in this process [10]. This stage was the least consistently reported. Four studies did not provide sufficient detail for classification, and follow-up requests for clarification from the authors were unsuccessful. Among those that did report, engagement ranged from collaborate [31, 32] to involve [33], consult [34], and inform [37, 39]. Contributions included supporting community-level implementation, advising on the adaptation of tools or frameworks, and participating in practice-oriented discussions. No study transferred decision-making authority to older adults, and engagement at this stage primarily reflected consultation or information sharing. Overall, engagement in implementation activities was limited, underscoring the challenges of extending partnership roles into later phases of research and practice.

Research question 3

The effectiveness of the approaches used across the 10 studies is captured in three themes: (i) older adult and researcher experiences, (ii) mechanisms supporting success and strategies for overcoming challenges, and (iii) research outcomes and impact. These themes build on the process-level results from Research Question 2 by highlighting the experiences of older adults living with frailty and researchers, as well as the outcomes resulting from the engagement of individuals with lived experience in the research process.

Older adult and researcher experiences

Across nearly all studies, engagement enhanced purpose, identity, and self-worth among people with lived experience. Involvement was consistently described as affirming and socially meaningful, with individuals reporting that they felt “listened to,” “useful,” and “valued.” In Bindels et al. [29, 30], older adults acting as co-researchers expressed pride in their contributions and described renewed confidence in their abilities. Scheffelaar et al. [31] similarly observed emotional enrichment and a sense of connection among co-researchers in long-term care settings. Travers et al. [37] and Silcock et al. [34] also reported positive experiences through active involvement in exercise, nutrition, and deprescribing interventions.

In several studies, co-learning reinforced these positive experiences. Older adults gained research literacy, self-efficacy, and a sense of belonging through ongoing engagement, while researchers deepened their understanding of frailty as a lived and relational experience [29–31]. These experiences were often strengthened through iterative feedback cycles that adapted materials to sensory, cognitive, and communication needs [32, 34, 35], enhancing older adults’ comfort and confidence throughout the process. Although they frequently described feeling personally empowered—citing increased confidence, validation, and renewed purpose—analysis using the Participatory Research Choice Points Framework showed that structural empowerment did not occur. Structural empowerment requires that people with lived experience hold decision-making authority throughout different stages of the research. In all studies, these forms of authority remained with the researchers. Thus, empowerment was experienced at the personal and relational levels but did not extend to shared governance within the research process.

Mechanisms supporting success and strategies for overcoming challenges

Several mechanisms supported effective engagement of older adults living with frailty across the studies. Relational trust and high-quality communication were central. Studies such as Bindels et al. [29, 30], O’Donnell et al. [33], van de Pol et al. [39], and MacLeod et al. [32] reported facilitation strategies grounded in empathy, reciprocity, and patience. Dialogue-based approaches—including storytelling, filmed narratives, reflective workshops, and dialogue cafés—helped bridge power gaps and foster shared understanding between people with lived experience and professionals. In Lund et al. [38] and Moral et al. [35], iterative testing and feedback cycles supported digital and interpersonal trust, demonstrating how relational facilitation could support participation even in technology-oriented projects.

Iterative co-learning processes also acted as a mechanism for effective engagement. Several studies embedded ongoing cycles of reflection and adaptation that allowed researchers to refine study materials and activities in response to older adults’ input. These adjustments—such as simplified visuals, paced facilitation, shorter sessions, and flexible formats—helped create environments that supported comfort, inclusion, and sustained involvement [32, 34, 35]. These co-learning processes aligned closely with the Choice Points Framework’s emphasis on reflexivity and responsiveness and contributed to maintaining engagement across multiple stages.

Despite these strengths, there were some engagement challenges. Multiple studies identified representation bias, noting that participants were often healthier, more educated, and less frail than the populations most affected by frailty [36, 39]. Health fluctuations, cognitive changes, and time demands further constrained continuity in studies involving long-term or multi-stage participation [31, 32]. Digital inequities also emerged, particularly in studies involving virtual participation or digital tool development, limiting involvement among those lacking technological confidence or resources [35, 38]. These challenges contributed to declining engagement in later research stages—particularly analysis, dissemination, and implementation.

Technology-related engagement had a distinct set of mechanisms and challenges. Travers et al. [37] found that phone and online sessions during the COVID-19 pandemic reduced fatigue and transportation barriers, allowing broader participation. MacLeod et al. [32] similarly demonstrated that virtual PAR could facilitate timely community collaboration. In Moral et al. [35] and Lund et al.’s [38] studies, older adults directly influenced the design of digital health tools, enhancing usability and acceptability. At the same time, these studies highlighted that digital formats may exclude individuals with limited connectivity or digital literacy. As a result, technology offered meaningful opportunities for accessibility and adaptation but also introduced new inequities that require careful consideration.

Research outcomes and impact

Several studies demonstrated that meaningful collaboration translated into tangible outcomes. Bethell et al. [36] successfully established a national top 10 frailty research agenda through a James Lind Alliance priority-setting partnership, embedding older adult and caregiver perspectives in health research priorities. MacLeod et al. [32] generated actionable recommendations on social isolation that were adopted by community taskforces, reflecting practical knowledge translation. Moral et al. [35] co-developed a functional frailty monitoring prototype, while Silcock et al. [34] co-designed deprescribing tools later integrated into clinical practice. Travers et al. [37] achieved readiness for a randomized controlled trial through iterative co-design. In these examples, researchers reported strong perceived success, and framework analysis confirmed moderate to high alignment across design, analysis, and act phases. However, the locus of decision-making typically remained institutional, reinforcing that tangible outputs do not necessarily equate to governance by older adults living with frailty.

Discussion

This review examined the engagement of older adults living with frailty in the research process, as well as the impact of these engagement practices on the experiences of those involved and the research outcomes. By synthesizing frailty-specific studies and applying a framework sensitive to structural power dynamics and decision-making authority, the review identifies not only when engagement occurs, but also how involvement is influenced by frailty-related realities and institutional norms. Our findings showed that engagement was consistently strongest during the partner and design stages, then declined across the collect, analyze, disseminate, and act stages. This “front-loading” pattern is not unique to frailty research; it reflects a well-documented trend across community engagement research where early-stage collaboration is more easily integrated into conventional research processes than later-stage activities [21, 40–42].

Applying the Participatory Research Choice Points Framework helped clarify these patterns by making the distribution of decision-making visible across stages of the research process. In community-engaged and participatory research fields such as CBPR, disability research, Indigenous health research, and mental health research, the decline in later-stage power sharing is often due to institutional processes, academic reward structures, and methodological norms [40, 41, 43–46]. Within these traditions, engagement is understood not as a single technique but as an ongoing negotiation shaped by trust, resources, and governance structures (21,23,43). In practice, however, analysis and implementation frequently remain with researchers, both because they demand specialized expertise and because institutional access is unevenly distributed between academic teams and those with lived experience [40–42]. This helps explain why engagement is often concentrated in the design stage—where contributions can be integrated through workshops and iterative feedback—while analytic authority and implementation control more often remain researcher-held.

Many studies demonstrated collaboration and high relational quality, though none reached the empower level. This distinction is important because engagement in research is often judged by its activities (e.g., advisory input, workshops) rather than by who ultimately holds decision-making authority. The Participatory Research Choice Points Framework made it possible to see that collaboration can occur without co-leadership and that meaningful engagement experiences for older adults living with frailty do not necessarily translate into shared governance. This finding is particularly relevant to ongoing debates across community engagement approaches, including co-design and PPI, where consultation and collaboration may be presented as partnership despite decision-making authority remaining with researchers or institutions [41, 47, 48]. In contrast, empower-level engagement would involve older adults living with frailty holding substantial influence over governance and key decisions, such as serving as co-applicants on grants, participating in steering committees with shared voting authority, co-leading data interpretation and dissemination activities, or determining how findings are implemented and evaluated. Although such approaches were not identified in the included studies, they represent important aspirations for advancing shared leadership and accountability in frailty research.

Relational practices—including trust-building, dialogue, reciprocity, and skilled facilitation—underpinned meaningful engagement across the included studies. This is consistent with participatory literature emphasizing that partnership ethics are enacted through ongoing practices of respect, mutual learning, and attention to power within the day-to-day dynamics of interaction [21, 23, 49–52]. Older adults reported benefits related to purpose, identity, and self-worth, underscoring the importance of recognition and belonging, particularly because frailty can be associated with social loss or marginalization [4]. These findings highlight the need to distinguish between relational empowerment (feeling heard, respected, and valued) and structural empowerment (holding authority over decisions, resources, and outputs). For frailty research, this distinction is ethically and epistemically important because it prevents positive relational experiences from being mistaken for equitable power sharing and reinforces that effective involvement depends on transparent governance, clear communication, and context-sensitive facilitation rather than a single “best” engagement model [42, 53, 54].

The studies included in this review also demonstrated that frailty itself should not be viewed as a barrier to meaningful engagement. Older adults living with frailty made substantial contributions to agenda setting, intervention refinement, and the interpretation of care experiences. This aligns with frailty literature emphasizing heterogeneity and context—frailty indicates increased vulnerability, not incapacity [16, 17]—and with broader guidance on inclusion in health and social care research, which highlights how exclusion often reflects assumptions embedded in recruitment, eligibility, and study design rather than individuals’ willingness or ability to contribute [5, 6]. At the same time, the older adults represented in the included studies were often those able to engage as research partners in advisory groups, co-design activities, workshops, and interviews. Individuals experiencing more severe frailty, significant cognitive impairment, profound social isolation, or unstable health may have been less likely to be recruited into these partnership roles. This raises important questions about whose perspectives are represented in frailty research and highlights the need to address structural barriers to research partnership, including recruitment and partnership formation practices, accessibility and accommodation needs, communication approaches, caregiver support, and assumptions about capacity to contribute. Greater attention to these barriers is important not only for equity and inclusion but also for ensuring that research findings reflect the diversity of experiences and perspectives of older adults living with frailty.

Documented accounts from older adults living with frailty show that engagement can feel like stepping “into” the research world when processes are respectful, appropriately paced, and relationally supportive [4]. However, the realities of aging and vulnerability must also be considered. Health fluctuations, fatigue, mobility limitations, and multimorbidity can make sustained involvement more challenging over extended periods [55, 56]. These realities do not render later-stage involvement inappropriate; rather, they underscore the need for flexible roles and accessible ways of contributing that do not depend on constant availability or impose a high procedural burden [6, 53, 54]. Together, these findings challenge deficit-oriented research cultures that treat frailty as a reason to shield people from research and instead support the design of structures and processes that make participation feasible, ethical, and non-extractive for older adults living with frailty. This planning should begin early in the research process, particularly during funding applications, to ensure adequate resources for partner compensation, accessibility accommodations and supports, and the additional time needed for meaningful engagement.

Pragmatic adaptations made in the studies were repeatedly linked to engagement quality; flexible formats, shorter sessions, simplified visuals, and iterative feedback cycles helped accommodate sensory changes, fatigue, and varying comfort [34, 35]. These adaptations reflect best practice recommendations and involvement guidance that emphasize accessibility, training/support, and clarity around roles and expectations [6, 9]. Framing engagement as relational and adaptive also supports a more ethical approach, in which participation is designed around dignity, comfort, and sustainable contribution rather than maximizing demands. As a result of meaningful engagement, several studies produced tangible outputs, including co-developed tools, redesigned pathways, improved communication processes, and national priority setting [32–37]. However, these improvements did not necessarily entail shared decision-making authority; governance gaps remained visible in later-stage activities. This review highlights that advancing engagement requires not simply increasing involvement but investing in the conditions that make later-stage participation possible, including clearer governance agreements, resources to support shared leadership in analysis and dissemination, and implementation planning that includes older adults in decisions about how change is enacted and evaluated [40–42]. Feasible strategies may include training and supporting older adults living with frailty to contribute to data analysis through structured data interpretation sessions, collaborative theme validation, and decisions regarding which findings are prioritized and how they are communicated. Shared leadership may also be strengthened by engaging older adults in developing dissemination strategies, selecting target audiences, co-authoring outputs, participating in presentations, and contributing to decisions about how findings are implemented and evaluated. Such contributions can support shared decision-making in later research stages while recognizing that engagement may vary according to individuals’ interests, preferences, and capacities.

Overall, the evidence suggests that frailty research has moved beyond tokenistic consultation in early stages, yet still rarely achieves continuity of power sharing across the research cycle. Advancing the field will require attention to both structure and relationship: relational excellence without structural authority risks reproducing benevolent hierarchies, while structural intent without relational practice risks extractive participation. Consistent with community engagement guidance and reporting standards [9, 57–60], future work would benefit from (i) explicit governance arrangements and role descriptions that support co-leadership where feasible; (ii) clearer reporting of who made decisions at each stage of the research process, how older adults living with frailty were involved in those decisions, and the extent of shared decision-making; and (iii) adaptable participation options that allow people with frailty to contribute meaningfully across stages without requiring constant, high-intensity involvement. In doing so, frailty research can better align engagement principles with the realities of aging, vulnerability, and health systems—and shift from episodic involvement toward sustained, ethically grounded partnership.

Recommendations

Future research engaging older adults living with frailty should involve them early in agenda setting and study design, while avoiding assumptions that greater intensity or continuity of participation is always desirable. Researchers must respect older adults’ preferences, limits, and choices about when and how they engage [4, 34, 36]. Engagement should be flexible and adaptive to accommodate fluctuating health, energy, and capacity, including shorter or paced sessions, multiple participation formats (e.g., in-person, telephone, virtual), and opportunities for episodic involvement [29–32, 37]. Engagement should be supported through skilled facilitation grounded in trust, empathy, and reciprocity, alongside accessible materials, clear communication, and iterative feedback processes that address sensory, cognitive, and digital needs [33, 35, 38]. Across the full research cycle, engagement practices should be strengthened through explicit attention to power and governance, including clear articulation of decision-making roles, feasible opportunities for shared leadership (e.g., co-analysis, co-authorship, community-led implementation), and transparent communication about the limits of shared authority [41, 47–51]. Finally, engagement activities, roles, and decision-making processes should be reported consistently and transparently using established guidance to support comparability, accountability, and ethical practice in engagement-focused frailty research [9, 57, 59].

Study strengths and limitations

This scoping review has several limitations. Older adults living with frailty were not involved in the conduct of this review; therefore, the interpretation of findings and the development of recommendations were not informed by direct input from people with lived experience. In addition, recruitment and frailty identification strategies varied across studies and often relied on existing community and healthcare networks, service-based recruitment, and self-identification. These approaches may have influenced which older adults living with frailty were represented in this review, potentially underrepresenting those who were more socially isolated, disconnected from services, or unwilling to identify as frail. The review also required studies to explicitly identify participants as living with frailty through a clinical diagnosis, self-report, validated screening tool, or other recognized indicators of frailty to ensure consistency in study selection and that included studies focused on this population. Consequently, studies involving populations likely to experience frailty may have been excluded if frailty was not explicitly identified or assessed, which may have contributed to the small number of included studies and the limited geographic representation.

Most studies were conducted in European contexts, potentially limiting the generalizability of findings to regions with different health systems or cultural perspectives on aging. Further, engagement activities were often reported briefly or embedded within broader methodological descriptions, making it difficult to assess the depth and influence of involvement across research stages. Variation in reporting and the absence of standardized engagement measures also prevented quantitative comparison of engagement quality and outcomes.

Despite these limitations, the review was strengthened by its rigorous and transparent methodology. Screening and data extraction were completed by multiple reviewers to enhance consistency and reduce bias. Applying the Participatory Research Choice Points Framework added conceptual depth by enabling analysis of how engagement occurred across stages and how decision-making was distributed, rather than simply noting whether engagement was present. The inclusion of diverse study designs across multiple contexts supported a broad and contextualized synthesis. Finally, when reporting was unclear, study authors were contacted for clarification, reducing reliance on published descriptions alone and providing additional insight into engagement processes not always available in manuscripts.

Conclusion

This scoping review demonstrates that meaningful engagement of older adults living with frailty in the research process is both feasible and valuable, particularly during partnership formation and study design. When supported through participatory and action-oriented, co-design and human-centred design, PPI and priority-setting, and dialogic and capacity-building approaches, older adults contributed lived experience that enhanced research relevance and acceptability, reinforcing that frailty itself is not a barrier to engagement. However, engagement consistently declined across studies in later research stages, with decision-making authority remaining largely with researchers. This pattern reflects broader structural and methodological constraints within community engagement research rather than limitations of older adults living with frailty. Future research should therefore move beyond early-stage involvement toward continuity, transparency, and shared decision-making across the research process, using flexible methods and explicit attention to power and governance to sustain meaningful engagement.
