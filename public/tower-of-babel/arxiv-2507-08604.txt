Abstract
We present the results of optical identifications for Very Large Array Sky Survey (VLASS) radio sources employing the Ultraviolet Near Infrared Optical Northern Survey (UNIONS). A cross-match between UNIONS and VLASS Epoch 2 catalogs yields 146,212 146,212 radio galaxies down to r = 24.5 r=24.5 mag over a wide area of ∼ 4,200 ​ deg 2 \sim 4,200\mathrm{\ deg^{2}} in the northern hemisphere. We perform g − g- dropout selections and find ≳ 200 \gtrsim 200 sources at z ∼ 4 z\sim 4 optimistically. Of 63,019 sources with valid photometric redshifts, 8,692 are at z photo ≥ 1 {z}_{\mathrm{photo}}\geq 1 and 1,171 are at z photo ≥ 2 {z}_{\mathrm{photo}}\geq 2 . Based on spectral luminosities at 1.4 and 3 GHz using the valid UNIONS photo- z , we identify ∼ 49,000 \sim 49,000 radio-loud AGNs (RLAGNs) with L 1.4 ​ GHz > 10 24 ​ W ​ Hz − 1 {L}_{\mathrm{1.4GHz}}>10^{24}\mathrm{\ W\ Hz^{-1}} , and all radio galaxies at z ≥ 1 z\geq 1 are RL. Adopting radio loudness instead, 138,266 out of 146,212 UNIONS-VLASS radio galaxies are RL. By virtue of the wide area and medium depth provided by UNIONS, our catalog greatly increases the number counts of RLAGNs at z > 1 z>1 . We further cross-match the UNIONS-VLASS catalog with LOw-Frequency ARray Two-metre Sky Survey (LoTSS) at 144 MHz and Faint Images of the Radio Sky at Twenty-cm (FIRST) at 1.4 GHz, yielding 101,671 UNIONS-VLASS-LoTSS, 79,638 -FIRST, and 64,672 -LoTSS-FIRST sources, respectively. This multifrequency radio dataset reveals sources of various spectral shapes, including the steep spectrum of aged populations, the peaked spectrum of young populations, and the upturned spectrum that might be associated with transient sources. The UNIONS-VLASS radio galaxy candidates will be covered by the Euclid wide survey, bringing about legacy values to benefit multi-faceted studies related to radio galaxies and beyond.

I
Introduction

Supermassive black holes (SMBHs) are believed to ubiquitously reside in the center of most galaxies
(
Kormendy & Ho 2013
;
McConnell & Ma 2013
)
, and a tight correlation between the mass of a SMBH and the properties of its host galaxy has been observed since the 1990s
(
Magorrian et al. 1998
;
Ferrarese & Merritt 2000
;
Gebhardt et al. 2000
;
Tremaine et al. 2002
)
.
The tight correlation implies that SMBHs grow in tandem with the parameters of their host galaxies, so-called “co-evolution”.
How SMBHs co-evolve with their host galaxies and how they impact their host galaxy evolution are long-standing questions in modern astronomy.

One key phenomenon to shape such co-evolution is called AGN feedback.
Such a feedback can lead to the outflowing and/or heating of the cold gas, suppressing the host galaxy star formation and self-regulating the growth of the SMBH
(
Fabian 2012
;
Alexander & Hickox 2012
;
Bischetti et al. 2022
;
Harrison & Ramos Almeida 2024
;
Talbot et al. 2024
;
Huško et al. 2025
)
, although a compression of cold gas that enhances the star formation has also been observed
(
Shin et al. 2019
;
Duncan et al. 2023
)
.
One mechanism of AGN feedback is through the coupling between the host galaxy ISM and the relativistic plasma, which is known as radio jets, launched from the central SMBH.
This radio/jet-mode feedback is considered the primary mode of AGN feedback in radio-loud active galactic nuclei (RLAGNs).

RLAGNs are often selected via the observed radio loudness
\mathcal{R}_{\mathrm{obs}}(={F}_{\mathrm{radio}}/{F}_{\mathrm{optical}}>10)
defined as the ratio of radio-to-optical flux densities
(
Kellermann et al. 1989
;
Balokovic et al. 2012
)
,
or via the 1.4 GHz spectral luminosity
{L}_{\mathrm{1.4GHz}}\gtrsim 10^{24}\mathrm{\ W\ Hz^{-1}}
;
Peacock et al. 1986
;
Condon 1989
;
Tadhunter 2016
;
Padovani et al. 2017
).
In the local Universe, RLAGNs are found to preferentially reside in early-type galaxies correlated with high stellar masses, low star formation rates, and radiatively inefficient AGNs (
Best et al. 2005
;
Heckman & Best 2014
;
Gürkan et al. 2015
;
Pierce et al. 2022
; however, see also
Ramos Almeida et al. 2012
;
Pierce et al. 2023
).
At higher redshifts (
z>1
), there is an increasing number of RLAGNs found to be radiatively efficient
(
Merloni & Heinz 2008
;
Ichikawa et al. 2021
, Eddington ratio
{\lambda}_{\mathrm{Edd}}\gtrsim 3\times 10^{-2}
;)

and their host galaxies can be star-forming or even starburst galaxies
(
Drouart et al. 2014
;
Zhu et al. 2023
;
Zhong et al. 2024
)
.
These high-redshift radio galaxies (HzRGs) at
z>1
are found tightly correlated with galaxy mergers
(
Chiaberge et al. 2015
;
Zhong et al. 2023
;
Heckman et al. 2024
)
, suggestive of merger-triggered star formation and launching of radio jets.
Therefore, RLAGNs across cosmic time, especially HzRGs, are valuable laboratories to study the interplay between the host galaxy evolution, SMBH growth, and radio jets, as well as the jet-accretion disc connections.

The rareness of RLAGNs (
10-20\%
among all AGN populations;
Kellermann et al. 1989
;
Urry & Padovani 1995
;
Ivezić et al. 2002
;
Chiaberge & Marconi 2011
) requires large surveys to increase the sample size to statistically understand RLAGNs, their host galaxies, and the connections in between.
Over the past decades, following the groundbreaking Cambridge Catalogue of Radio Sources
(
Kellermann et al. 1969
, e.g.,)
, many radio sky surveys have been conducted centered around
\sim 1
GHz, including the National Radio Astronomy Observatory (NRAO) Very Large Array (VLA) Sky Survey
(
Condon et al. 1998
, NVSS; 1.4 GHz;)
and VLA Faint Images of the Radio Sky at Twenty-cm
(
Becker et al. 1995
, FIRST; 1.4 GHz;)
, and Australian Square Kilometre Array Pathfinder (ASKAP) Evolutionary Map of the Universe
(
Norris et al. 2011
, EMU; 944 MHz;)
and Rapid ASKAP Continuum Survey
(
McConnell et al. 2020
, RACS;
\sim 900-1600
MHz;)
.
The vast sky coverages (
\mathrm{>10,000\ deg^{2}}
) of these surveys offer great opportunities to build up an unbiased sample to elevate the statistical importance of studies related to RLAGNs.

Wide-area optical surveys are necessary to identify the optical counterparts of radio sources and determine their redshifts, either photometrically or spectroscopically.
Using the Sloan Digital Sky Survey (SDSS;
York et al. 2000
),
Ivezić et al. 2002
and
Helfand et al. 2015
identified the optical counterparts for the FIRST radio core sources at 1.4 GHz, adopting a positional cross-match between the two catalogs.
Due to the shallow optical sensitivity of SDSS
r
-band imaging (
r<22.2
mag), the fraction of matches (
{f}_{\mathrm{matches}}
) is only
\sim 30\%
, and a significant fraction of optically faint host galaxies may have been missed.
Using the Subaru Hyper Suprime-Cam (HSC) Subaru Strategic Program
(
Aihara et al. 2018
, SSP;)
,
Yamashita et al. 2018
reaches
{f}_{\mathrm{matches}}=50.6\%
optimistically for FIRST sources in the HSC field thanks to the depth of HSC reaching a limiting magnitude of 25.9 in
i-
band.
Despite an improvement in associating radio sources with faint objects, elevating the fairness of the radio galaxy catalog, the survey area is
\mathrm{1,400\ deg^{2}}
such that the catalog size is limited.

Other than the 1.4 GHz continuum, radio galaxy catalogs have been constructed centering at 3 GHz and 144 MHz.
Based on the largest sample of uniformly selected and spectrally confirmed quasars provided by SDSS,
Arnaudova et al. 2024
identified 3,697 RL and
111,132 RQ sources in LOw-Frequency ARray (LOFAR) observations at 144 MHz via the radio loudness dichotomy.
With 384 h observations of the upgraded
Karl G. Jansky
VLA, the VLA-COSMOS 3 GHz Large Project
(
Smolčić et al. 2017
)
identifies 10,830 radio sources in the COSMOS field.
This project is equipped with a sensitivity better than that of FIRST by almost two orders of magnitude, greatly exploring the RQ populations.
These catalogs are advantageous explorations in the faint end of radio galaxies; however, they do not significantly expand the number counts of RLAGNs.

To improve both the sample size of RLAGNs and the unbiasedness of host galaxy populations,
we search for UNIONS
(
Gwyn et al. 2025
)
optical counterparts for VLASS
(
Lacy et al. 2020
)
radio sources at 3 GHz.

UNIONS reaches a depth of 24.7 mag in
r-
band and covers an extremely wide area of
\sim 5,000\mathrm{\ deg^{2}}
that fully overlaps with VLASS, bridging the gap between the shallow imaging of SDSS and the deep but slightly limited sky coverage of HSC-SSP.
VLASS is the state-of-the-art radio survey operated at 3 GHz in terms of spatial resolution, sensitivity, and total survey area.
The cross-match between these two catalogs will produce one of the largest RLAGN catalogs covering a wide redshift range.
Furthermore, both catalogs are ongoing projects, ensuring a great scalability of the UNIONS-VLASS catalog.

In this paper, we use radio galaxies for sources cross-matched between the UNIONS optical and VLASS radio catalogs, and define HzRGs as radio galaxies at
z>1
.
This paper is organized as follows.
In Section
II
, we introduce the optical and radio source catalogs used to build the radio galaxy catalog.
The cross-matches between the catalogs and the statistical results of our radio galaxy catalog are presented in Section
III
.
We examined the optical and radio number counts of UNIONS-VLASS radio galaxies in Section
IV
, and we further presented the selection of RLAGNs based on the observed radio loudness and spectral luminosity.
We show radio galaxy classifications using multifrequency radio datasets and discuss the high-
z
sources based on the dropout technique in Section
V
.
A summary is presented in Section
VI
.
Throughout this paper, we assume a
\mathrm{\Lambda CDM}
cosmology with
\Omega_{m}=0.309
,
\Omega_{\Lambda}=0.691
, and
H_{0}=\mathrm{67.7\ km\ s^{-1}\ Mpc^{-1}}

(
Planck Collaboration et al. 2016
)
.

II
The Data

This exploration for radio galaxies is established on the basis of angular separation-based cross-match between the VLASS for radio sources at 3 GHz and UNIONS for optical counterparts.
The cross-matched UNIONS-VLASS catalog is set as our reference and further cross-matched with the FIRST 1.4 GHz and LoTSS 144 MHz radio source catalogs.
In Figure
1
, we summarize the flowchart that illustrates the criteria applied to each survey to filter out valid sources for the cross-match and to exclude invalid sources in the resultant cross-matches.

II.1
UNIONS

An elevation of the statistical importance of our radio galaxies catalog requires a wide (
>1000
deg
2
) and deep (
r>22
mag) optical survey.
The Ultraviolet Near-Infrared Optical Northern Survey (UNIONS;
Gwyn et al. 2025
), as a science collaboration between wide imaging surveys that cover the northern hemisphere, provides an excellent opportunity to abundantly discover the optical counterparts of radio sources.
The UNIONS integrates multiband (five broadbands,
u
,
g
,
r
,
i
,
z
) observations from Canada-France-Hawaii Telescope/Canada-France Imaging Survey (CFHT/CFIS,

g
), The Panoramic Survey Telescope & Rapid Response System (Pan-STARRS,
i
), and Subaru/Wide Imaging with Subaru HSC of the Euclid Sky (WISHES,
z

The main UNIONS footprint is located at a declination (DEC.)
>30^{\circ}
and
a galactic latitude of
|b|>25^{\circ}
, enclosing a total area of
4,861\mathrm{\ deg^{2}}
.
Extended
u-
and
z-
(WISHES+) observations are being conducted to cover the sky area at
\mathrm{DEC.}<30^{\circ}
, elevating the sky coverage of UNIONS.
Albeit UNIONS is motivated by the
Euclid
wide survey
(
Euclid Collaboration et al. 2022
)
, efforts have been devoted to maintaining UNIONS as an independent survey with scientific values beyond what will be brought about by the
Euclid
.

The
r-
band observation of CFHT/CFIS has an excellent median seeing of
0.69\arcsec
and reaches a surface brightness limit of 28.4
\mathrm{mag/arcsec^{2}}
for extended sources.
It is set as the reference catalog by which the elliptical aperture is defined for the forced photometry applied to all five UNIONS bands.
Since the broadband observations of UNIONS are conducted under multiple telescopes, the Gaussian Aperture and Photometry (
\mathtt{GAaP}
;
Kuijken et al. 2015
) is adopted to produce Gaussianized images for raw imaging data collected from observations at different instruments.
The forced photometry with Gaussian-weighted, elliptical apertures can then be properly used to perform homogeneous multiband photometric measurements simultaneously.
The depth of UNIONS is characterized by the
10\sigma
point source depth measured through a
2.0\arcsec
aperture.
The current median depth of UNIONS is
[u,g,r,i,{z}_{\mathrm{PS}},{z}_{\mathrm{HSC}}]=[23.7,24.5,24.2,23.8,23.3,23.3]
, to be compared with the original goal of
[u,g,r,i,z]=[23.6,24.5,23.9,23.6,23.4]

(
Gwyn et al. 2025
)
.

In this work, we use the latest internal 5-band data release UNIONS5000 (as of May 2025;
\mathrm{UNIONS5000}_{ugriz}
hereafter) that includes
\sim 20,600
tiles with a total processed survey area of
\sim 4,200\mathrm{\ deg^{2}}
, covering both north (NGC) and south Galactic caps (SGC).

\mathrm{UNIONS5000}_{ugriz}
contains photometric measurements using forced photometry adopting an aperture size of
\mathrm{APER}=0.7\arcsec
and
\mathrm{APER}=1.0\arcsec
, and an additional one with the optimal aperture
\mathrm{APER_{opt}}
.
In most cases, the optimal one is equal to the smallest possible aperture of
\mathrm{APER}=0.7\arcsec
.
In the case where the measurement adopting
\mathrm{APER}=0.7\arcsec
fails, the optimal photometric measurement is then that using
\mathrm{APER}=1.0\arcsec
(see also
Kuijken et al. 2019
).
We always use the optimal photometry for the data presented in this work.

II.2
VLASS

The VLASS (Very Large Array Sky Survey;
Lacy et al. 2020
) observes the entire sky at a declination
>-40
degrees, covering a total area of 33,885
\mathrm{deg^{2}}
.
The observation frequency ranges
2-4
GHz,
with a central (reference) frequency of 3 GHz.
In baseline configurations in B and BnA to compensate for the projection effect, an angular resolution of
2.5\arcsec
has been reached.

The total survey is divided into three epochs, which have been completed in October 2024, plus a further half-epoch observation.

Plus the single-epoch rms sensitivity (root mean square error

(
de Gasperin et al. 2021

.

Therefore, VLASS is ideal for serving as our reference radio source catalog.

VLASS data release delivers quick look (QL) and single epoch (SE) images.
QL images are products of the rapid-CLEAN process with a cell size of
1\arcsec
.
SE images undergo deeper cleaning and self-calibration and reach a better cell size of
0.6\arcsec
.
However, only an area of 1,000
\mathrm{deg^{2}}
is available in SE, we therefore only use QL products for the cross-match.
We use the epoch 2 catalog (VLASS2 hereafter) since astrometric errors and underestimates of flux densities were previously present in epoch 1, and the final release of epoch 3 is unsettled.

There are
\sim 3
M radio sources detected in VLASS2.
Prior to cross-matching, we adopted the following criteria to filter the VLASS2 catalog following the VLASS catalog user guide (see also
Gordon et al. 2021
)

•

\mathtt{E\_RA\&E\_DEC<2.5^{\prime\prime}}
: astrometric uncertainties in both RA and DEC should be smaller than the angular resolution of
2.5\arcsec
;

•

\mathtt{Duplicate\_flag<2}
: the source has no duplicated or has a preferred version of the duplicated counterpart within
2\arcsec
search radius;

•

\mathtt{Quality\_flag==(0|4)}
: the source is unflagged or its peak flux density is higher than the integrated one (
\mathtt{Peak\_flux>Total\_flux}
);

•

\mathtt{S\_code!=E}
: the source can be fitted with a single-Gaussian or multi-Gaussian components;

plus
\mathtt{NN\_dist}
, which the angular separation between the target source and its nearest neighbor in VLASS2, is larger than
30^{\prime\prime}
(see §
III.1
for the discussion).
These criteria reduce the number of sources in “clean” VLASS2 to 1,380,707.
The VLASS2 catalog also includes the column
\mathtt{P\_sidelobe}
, which means the probability of a source being a sidelobe but misclassified as a real source.
As recommended in the VLASS Catalog User Guide,
\mathtt{Quality\_flag==0}
that indicates
\mathtt{P\_sidelobe<0.1}
is preferred, and the estimated contamination rate is 0.39%.
In the cleanest criterion,
\mathtt{P\_sidelobe<0.05}
is applied, and the estimated contamination rate is 0.12%.
For the final “clean” sample of 146,212 UNIONS-VLASS sources cross-matched (see §
III.3
for details), adopting these two criteria results in a difference of 1,766 (
\sim 1.2\%
) sources, which is statistically insignificant.
In the following of this work, without the loss of totality of our catalog, we present results adopting
\mathtt{P\_sidelobe<0.1}
.

II.4
LoTSS

To understand the evolution of radio galaxies, the spectral shape plays a crucial role, which requires multifrequency observations to construct the synchrotron spectral energy distribution (SED).
MHz-observations carried out by LOw-Frequency ARray are ideal supplements to our GHz-VLASS and FIRST data.
The LoTSS (LOFAR Two-metre Sky Survey;
Shimwell et al. 2017
;
Shimwell et al. 2022

120-168

The sky coverage has two separate regions that span 4,178 and 1,457
\mathrm{deg^{2}}
respectively.
The full bandwidth Stokes I continuum imaging of LoTSS yields images at a spatial resolution of
6.0\arcsec
, centered at a frequency of 144 MHz.
The median detection sensitivity of the survey is 89
\mathrm{\mu Jy\ beam^{-1}}
with a typical positional uncertainty smaller than
0.2\arcsec
.
In this work, we use the latest and widest coverage catalog, LoTSS DR2
(
Shimwell et al. 2022
)
, which contains 4,396,228 radio sources derived from integrated intensity maps in Stokes I polarization.

III.1
\mathtt{NN\_dist}
Cut

Radio galaxies with low radio luminosities often have their jets confined by the ISM and rapidly disrupted
(
Fanaroff & Riley 1974
;
Turner & Shabala 2015
)
.
As a result, the peak surface brightness can be close to the host galaxy.
The nearest neighbour matching is thus a straightforward and efficient approach for identifying the optical counterpart of a radio source
(
Williams et al. 2019
)
.
On the other hand, powerful radio jets can rapidly overcome the confinement of the ISM of their host galaxies without suffering significant disruption
(
Mukherjee et al. 2016
)
.
Thereby, they often propagate far from their host galaxies and end in the terminal shocks known as radio hotspots, where the observed radio surface brightness peaks
(
Turner & Shabala 2023
)
.
Identifying the optical counterparts of such radio sources requires a larger search radius, which, in turn, inevitably increases the likelihood of contamination by unrelated sources
(
Banfield et al. 2015
;
Gordon et al. 2023
)
.

To statistically avoid erroneous associations attributed to large-scale, complex radio structures, we perform a cross-match between UNIONS and the VLASS2 catalog – adopting the criteria present in
Gordon et al. 2021
– choosing a search radius of
6^{\prime\prime}
, which is
\sim 550,000
sources.
The angular separation between UNIONS-VLASS versus
\mathtt{NN\_dist}
is shown in Figure
2
.
It is clear that the number of cross-matched sources shows a bimodal distribution along
\mathtt{NN\_dist}
.
Sources cross-matched with
\mathtt{NN\_dist<30^{\prime\prime}}
tend to have large angular separations between UNIONS and VLASS centroids.
We randomly select 36 VLASS sources with
\mathtt{NN\_dist<10^{\prime\prime}}
and show their QL images, overlaid with VLASS and UNIONS source centroids, in Figure
18
.
For these radio sources, they often show complex and extended radio morphology, including core-jet and double-jet structures.
VLASS could capture the centroid of the lobe or radio hotspot, naturally begetting the large offsets between UNIONS and VLASS.
We, therefore, choose a
\mathtt{NN\_dist}
cut of
30^{\prime\prime}
to avoid false positive associations between UNIONS-VLASS arising from evolved radio structures that can extend well beyond host galaxy scale and overlap with other galaxies.
This cut can remove a large fraction of bright, evolved radio galaxies at low-
z
.
Consequently, we see that UNIONS-VLASS RGs are absent from the bright end (
{F}_{\mathrm{3GHz}}>3000
mJy; Figure
9
) and could be biased toward compact, young radio AGN populations (Figures
15
–
16
; see §
V.3
for discussion).

III.2
Contamination and Completeness

Since the galaxies are randomly distributed throughout the whole sky, the one-to-one correspondence between the radio and optical sources could be a false positive.
To statistically circumvent such contamination, we estimate the cleanness and completeness of the catalog in the following way.

We first estimate the number density of galaxies in each UNIONS tile lying within the VLASS field.
This gives a maximum number density of
{n}_{\mathrm{den,max}}=81.1
per
\mathrm{arcmin^{2}}
and a median value of
{n}_{\mathrm{den,med}}=36.1
per
\mathrm{arcmin^{2}}
(leftmost panel of Figure
3
).
We perform an angular separation-based cross-match, choosing a search radius of
2.5\arcsec
.
We remove optical counterparts without valid photometry in any of
ugriz
and multiple optical counterparts corresponding to a single radio source.
The total number of clean UNIONS-VLASS radio-optical pairs is then
{N}_{\mathrm{tot}}
, and the separation between each pair is defined as
{r}_{\mathrm{sep}}
.
Within the search radius of
r_{s}
, the number of sources with
{r}_{\mathrm{sep}}\leq{r}_{\mathrm{s}}
is
{N}_{\mathrm{s}}
, and these sources occupy a total area of
{A}_{\mathrm{s}}=\pi{r}_{\mathrm{s}}^{2}{N}_{\mathrm{s}}

\mathrm{arcsec^{2}}
.
The completeness of UNIONS-VLASS is then represented by
{N}_{\mathrm{s}}/{N}_{\mathrm{tot}}
and the maximum is 100%.
The number of optical sources adjacent to radio sources by coincidence is
{N}_{\mathrm{rand}}={A}_{\mathrm{s}}{n}_{\mathrm{den}}
.
The cleanness is then calculated by
({N}_{\mathrm{tot}}-{N}_{\mathrm{rand}})/{N}_{\mathrm{tot}}
.
The
{r}_{\mathrm{s}}
at which the cleanness intersects the completeness represents a compromise between the totality of the catalog and the contamination control (middle panel of Figure
3
).
With
{n}_{\mathrm{den,med}}=36.1
per
\mathrm{arcmin^{2}}
, the contaminated cross-matches arising from chance coincidence is 7.5%, approaching an estimated completeness of 92.7% at
{r}_{\mathrm{s}}\sim 1.6\arcsec
.
When
{n}_{\mathrm{den,max}}=81.1
per
\mathrm{arcmin^{2}}
is adopted,
{r}_{\mathrm{s}}\sim 1.2\arcsec
is preferred, and the contamination rate is 9.1% while the totality of the catalog is 89.3%.

Without excluding optical counterparts associated with a single radio source, we show the differential distribution of the separation between UNIONS and VLASS sources (rightmost panel of Figure
3
).
At
{r}_{\mathrm{s}}>1.5\arcsec
, the increase in the search radius is accompanied by an increase in the number of optical counterpart counts in the annulus, suggesting these optical counterparts are likely to be coincident associations rather than real matches.
The threshold of
{r}_{\mathrm{s}}\sim 1.5\arcsec
is consistent with the completeness-cleanness trade-off of
1.6\arcsec
adopting a median galaxy number density of
36.1
per
\mathrm{arcmin^{2}}
.

This value is also comparable to
Gordon et al. 2023
, who found that the probability that an AllWISE soruce is the true positive counterpart of an unresolved VLASS radio source reaches 80% at a positional uncertainty of
1.8\arcsec
.

III.3
Clean UNIONS-VLASS Radio Galaxies

Choosing an angular separation of
\Delta\theta=\sqrt{(\Delta\alpha\cos\delta)^{2}+(\Delta\delta)^{2}}\leq 1.5\arcsec
as the search radius, our cross-match between the UNIONS and VLASS Epoch 2 catalogs immediately yields 164,169 optical counterparts of radio sources.

This search radius represents a trade-off between the completeness of the catalog and a statistical exclusion of optical sources coincidentally associated with radio sources, which is described in §
III.2
.

To further make a clean radio galaxy catalog, we first eliminate optical sources without valid photometry in
r-
band since the UNIONS
\mathtt{GAaP}
catalog is constructed upon the successful CFHT
r-
band detections.
This reduces the number of optical sources to 157,346.
Out of these sources, there are 135,671 UNIONS sources uniquely associated with VLASS sources, and they are flagged with
\mathtt{0}
in the column
\mathtt{flag\_multi\_opt}
.

If the radio source has more than one optical counterpart, we always choose the nearest one as the real match.
This yields 10,541 radio sources that have multiple optical sources within a search radius of
1.5\arcsec
, and they are flagged with
\mathtt{1}
in the column
\mathtt{flag\_multi\_opt}
.

Because of the general trend of being compact for our UNIONS-VLASS RGs (see §
IV.1
and §
V.1
), this nearest match method could be valid in most cases, as jets and jet-induced shocks have not traveled far away from the host galaxy.
However, it should be noted that this does not fully rule out optical counterparts by chance overlaps.
Especially in cases where the RGs have developed jet-lobe structures that can result in large offsets between radio-emitting regions and host galaxies.
Consequently, the centroid of the radio emission captured by VLASS may be the radio hotspot and appears to be closer to another galaxy (up to approximately
4\%
based on
\sim 1,500

Euclid
-VLASS RGs; Zhong et al. in prep.).

These multiple optical counterparts are also considered merging or interacting galaxy candidates, and they will be investigated in detail in future work.

The results of the cross-match are summarized in Table
1
.
Our angular separation-based cross-match generated 146,212 VLASS radio sources matched to UNIONS optical counterparts.
Defining the fraction of matched VLASS sources in the area that overlaps with the UNIONS as the fraction of matches
({f}_{\mathrm{matches}})
, the corresponding value is 0.594, which is the maximum to be considered.
By virtue of the higher sensitivity and wide sky coverage provided by the UNIONS, this rate is higher than those in the previous studies cross-matching FIRST with SDSS (
\sim 30\%
;
Ivezić et al. 2002
;
Helfand et al. 2015
).
This fraction of matches also exceeds the cross-matching FIRST with Subaru HSC (
\sim 47\%
;
Yamashita et al. 2018
), even HSC optical number counts share a similar trend as the UNIONS (see
IV.2
for further discussions).
This could be explained by a combination of several factors, including a higher sensitivity of VLASS over FIRST and a larger search radius (
1.5\arcsec
) of UNIONS-VLASS than
1\arcsec
of HSC-FIRST.

In summary, our cross-match between UNIONS and VLASS catalogs yields 146,212 radio galaxies.
This “clean” UNIONS-VLASS radio galaxy catalog is the final sample to be presented in this work.

III.4
UNIONS-VLASS Astrometry

This vast radio galaxy catalog offers an excellent opportunity to investigate the systematic astrometric offsets between UNIONS and VLASS catalogs.
The offsets along R.A. and DEC. are residuals of a VLASS position minus a UNIONS position:
\Delta\alpha=\mathrm{\alpha_{VLASS}-\alpha_{UNIONS}}
and
\Delta\delta=\mathrm{\delta_{VLASS}-\delta_{UNIONS}}
.
However, our cross-match method selects associated pairs based on the angular separation (
\Delta\theta
) between radio and optical sources.
Because the positional difference along the R.A. on the celestial sphere has a declination dependence, this calculation has to be corrected for the projection effect and is given by
\Delta\theta=\sqrt{({\Delta\alpha}_{\mathrm{corr}})^{2}+(\Delta\delta)^{2}}
,
{\Delta\alpha}_{\mathrm{corr}}=(\mathrm{\alpha_{VLASS}-\alpha_{UNIONS}})\times\cos({\delta}_{\mathrm{UNIONS}})
.
Therefore, for
\Delta\alpha=\mathrm{\alpha_{VLASS}-\alpha_{UNIONS}}
, the value can exceed the search radius of
1.5\arcsec
, and such an excess becomes more severe for sources at high declinations, as shown in the left panel of Figure
19
.

To make a fair comparison of the astrometric accuracy in R.A. with other catalogs adopting positional cross-match that simply calculates the residuals between coordinates, we then make a subsample of “clean” UNIONS-VLASS sources.
This subsample contains 140,065 UNIONS-VLASS sources that have their optical counterparts lying within a circle of a projected radius
1.5\arcsec
on the sky and centered on the radio source.
This reduces the catalog size by
\sim 4.2\%
, which is statistically insignificant.
The distributions of
\Delta\alpha
and
\Delta\delta
of these sources are shown by orange histograms in Figure
4
.

The standard deviations of 140,065 UNIONS-VLASS sources whose
\sqrt{\Delta\alpha^{2}+\Delta\delta^{2}}\leq 1.5\arcsec
are
0.44\arcsec
in R.A. and
0.29\arcsec
in DEC.
On the other hand, for 146,212 “clean” UNIONS-VLASS sources, the offset along the R.A. retards to
0.61\arcsec
while the change in the offset along DEC. is negligible.
These offsets are much worse than the positional uncertainties (20 mas) of the CFHT
u-
and
r-
band imaging surveys employed by UNIONS
(
Gwyn et al. 2025
)
.
For the VLASS catalog, by comparing the Epoch 1 catalog with Gaia DR2, the VLASS component positional accuracy is
\sim 0.5\arcsec
at
\mathrm{DEC.>-20\ deg^{2}}
.
Therefore, the current standard deviations of the positional errors for our UNIONS-VLASS sources are thus reasonable compared to the entire VLASS survey, suggesting that the astrometric offsets between optical and radio pairs are attributed to the intrinsic uncertainties of VLASS QL images.

In VLASS Epoch 1, the positional error peaks at
0.0\arcsec
in R.A. and peaks at
-0.25\arcsec
in DEC.
Our UNIONS-VLASS sources based on Epoch 2 observations show a similar trend in R.A. with an improved astrometric accuracy in DEC. (
{\Delta\delta}_{\mathrm{peak}}\sim 0.0\arcsec
) compared to VLASS Epoch 1.
However, there is no significant improvement in the typical astrometry of
\sim 0.5\arcsec
, which is much worse than that of FIRST (
0.2\arcsec
).
This is more of a problem of the rapid CLEAN procedure adopted by VLASS QL products.
More accurate astrometry can be expected when Epoch 3 and epoch-concatenated data are delivered, as well as for the SE images.

III.5
Cross-matches between Surveys

Once the unique optical counterpart catalog of VLASS sources (146,212 “UNIONS-VLASS Radio Galaxies” in Figure
1
) is constructed, we then perform additional cross-match with the VLA/FIRST 1.4 GHz survey and LOFAR/LoTSS 144 MHz survey.
We search for FIRST 1.4 GHz radio counterparts around our UNIONS-VLASS radio galaxies by requiring the angular separation between a pair of VLASS and FIRST sources to be within a
2.5\arcsec
radius.
This results in a total of 85,060 matched sources.
We then remove the sources containing possible sidelobes, using the sidelobe probability
\mathtt{P(S)}
presented in the FIRST catalog.
We choose
\mathtt{0.00<P(S)<0.05}
as the threshold, by which 76.3% of sources are considered true positives, and this results in 79,638 radio sources identified in UNIONS, VLASS, and FIRST.

We search for LoTSS 144 MHz radio counterparts for the UNIONS-VLASS radio galaxies by applying a positional cross-match of
<2.5\arcsec
radius, consistent with the VLASS beam size.
This cross-match results in 101,671 radio galaxies identified in UNIONS, VLASS, and LoTSS.
Finally, there are 64,672 UNIONS-VLASS-FIRST-LoTSS radio galaxies.

III.6.3
Extended Radio Sources

The morphology of radio emission, such as its compactness, presumably originating from radio jets, provides key information about the evolution of RLAGNs.
To distinguish between compact (unresolved) and extended (resolved) sources, we make use of the ratio of the total radio flux density (
{F}_{\mathrm{int}}
) to the peak radio flux density (
{F}_{\mathrm{peak}}
), a widely adopted method
(
Ivezić et al. 2002
;
Bondi et al. 2003
;
Schinnerer et al. 2007
, e.g.)
.
This ratio directly measures the extension of a radio source, and for unresolved or marginally resolved sources,
{F}_{\mathrm{int}}={F}_{\mathrm{peak}}

(
Bondi et al. 2008
, e.g.,)
.
Extended and resolved sources have
{F}_{\mathrm{int}}>{F}_{\mathrm{peak}}
whereas the background noise could lower the total flux density.
Therefore, we plot
{F}_{\mathrm{int}}/{F}_{\mathrm{peak}}
as a function of the signal-to-noise ratio
\mathrm{S/N}\ (={F}_{\mathrm{peak}}/\mathrm{rms})
in Figure
7
for 146,212 “clean” UNIONS-VLASS sources (blue crosses) and those (79,638) with FIRST detections (orange dots).

Values of
{F}_{\mathrm{int}}<{F}_{\mathrm{peak}}
then a result of the measurement uncertainties probably arising from the background noises that could lower
{F}_{\mathrm{int}}

(
Bondi et al. 2003
, e.g.,)
.

To select extended sources, we first determined a lower envelope that encloses 99% of the UNIONS-VLASS sources with
{F}_{\mathrm{int}}<{F}_{\mathrm{peak}}
, as indicated by the dashed cyan curve.
We then mirrored it above the horizontal line defined by
{F}_{\mathrm{int}}/{F}_{\mathrm{peak}}=1
, and this upper envelope (solid cyan curve) is represented as:

{F}_{\mathrm{int}}/{F}_{\mathrm{peak}}=1+8.6\times\left({{F}_{\mathrm{peak}}/\mathrm{rms}}\right)^{-1}.

(1)

Adopting these criteria, 29,862 (
\sim 20\%
) of our UNIONS-VLASS sources are extended.

We performed a similar evaluation for sources detected in FIRST where the FIRST S/N is defined as
({F}_{\mathrm{peak}}-0.25)/\rm{rms}
to correct for the CLEAN bias
(
Helfand et al. 2015
)
, and the corresponding upper envelope is defined by
{F}_{\mathrm{int}}/{F}_{\mathrm{peak}}=1+7.9\times\left[\left({{F}_{\mathrm{peak}}-0.25}\right)/\mathrm{rms}\right]^{-1}
, which is in general agreement with
{F}_{\mathrm{int}}/{F}_{\mathrm{peak}}=1+6.5\times\left({{F}_{\mathrm{peak}}/\mathrm{rms}}\right)^{-1}
calculated for the HSC-FIRST catalog
(
Yamashita et al. 2018
)
.

Alternatively, thanks to the higher sensitivity and better resolution, the VLA-COSMOS 3 GHz survey
(
Smolčić et al. 2017
)
determined the envelope by a 95% enclosure of the sources below the
{F}_{\mathrm{int}}={F}_{\mathrm{peak}}
line.
Adopting this criterion, the upper envelope is

{F}_{\mathrm{int}}/{F}_{\mathrm{peak}}=1+3.7\times\left({{F}_{\mathrm{peak}}/\mathrm{rms}}\right)^{-1},

(2)

and 63,203 (
\sim 43\%
) of our UNIONS-VLASS sources are extended.
In this work, we adopt Eq. (1) as the threshold considering the similar beam sizes and sensitivities between VLASS and FIRST, and extended radio sources are labeled with
\mathtt{1}
in column
\mathtt{extended}
.
Examples of extended and compact radio sources are shown in Figure
8
and they are marked on the
{F}_{\mathrm{int}}/{F}_{\mathrm{peak}}-\mathrm{S/N}
plane (Figure
7
).

•

The global amplitude of the scaled “all” VLASS is elevated, whereas the slope becomes flatter.

These results clearly reflect the complexity of spectral indices of real radio sources that they cannot always be described by a simple power-law distribution (see §
V.1
for details).

IV.3
Radio-Loud AGNs

IV.3.1
Radio Loudness

To separate RLAGNs from radio-quiet (RQ) populations, the observed radio loudness
\mathcal{R}_{\mathrm{obs}}
is often used.
A widely adopted quantification of
\mathcal{R}_{\mathrm{obs}}
is defined as the radio-to-optical flux ratio where no
k-
corrections are applied:

\log\mathcal{R}_{\mathrm{obs}}=\log\left({{F}_{\mathrm{radio}}/{F}_{\mathrm{optical}}}\right)=0.4(m-t),

(5)

where
m
is one of the optical magnitudes and
t
is the AB radio magnitudes in Eq. (3).
Although the
i-
band photometry is commonly chosen for
{F}_{\mathrm{optical}}
, the fact that UNIONS5000 is built upon the
r-
band detections ensures a hundred percent completeness of
\mathcal{R}_{\mathrm{obs}}
for our catalog.
We thus use the
r-
band photometry instead.
The median difference between
r-
and
i-
band photometry, as shown in the left panel of Figure
22
, is 0.44 in both UNIONS5000 and UNIONS2000 data releases.
Therefore, statistically, the choice of
{r}_{\mathrm{ABmag}}
has insignificant impacts on the final classifications.
For
{F}_{\mathrm{radio}}
, we use the 1.4 GHz flux density.
The 1.4 GHz flux densities of UNIONS-VLASS sources with FIRST detection are calculated using FIRST flux densities.
Those not detected in FIRST have their VLASS 3 GHz flux densities scaled to those at 1.4 GHz using
{\alpha}_{\mathrm{3GHz}}^{\mathrm{1.4GHz}}=-0.7
.
RLAGNs are then selected with
\log\mathcal{R}_{\mathrm{obs}}>1
.
In this manner, for 146,212 sources in the “clean” UNIONS-VLASS catalog, 138,266 (
\sim 94.6\%
) are RL and 7,946 (
\sim 5.4\%
) are RQ populations.

Discussions

V.1
Radio Color-color Diagram

The spectral shape of the synchrotron radiation and the projected linear sizes of the synchrotron-radiating regions offer implications for the evolution stage and environment of an RLAGN
(
Miley & De Breuck 2008
;
Murgia et al. 2011
;
Gasperin et al. 2018
;
Hardcastle & Croston 2020
;
Zhong et al. 2023
)
.

To characterize the spectral shapes of our UNIONS-VLASS sources, we show the radio color-color diagram (or spectral index map) between
{\alpha}_{\mathrm{FIRST=1400MHz}}^{\mathrm{LoTSS=144Mhz}}
and
{\alpha}_{\mathrm{VLASS=3000MHz}}^{\mathrm{FIRST=1400Mhz}}
in Figure
14
, which includes 30,091 sources detected in VLASS, FIRST, and LoTSS with
\mathtt{alpha\_\{frequency1\}\_\{frequency2\}\_err}<0.2
.

The expected spectral shapes covering 144 MHz to 3 GHz are indicated by the orange lines and can be divided into four quadrants (labeled Q
1-4
).

Sources located in quadrant 1 have an upturned spectrum, which could be explained by a burst of synchrotron radiation subsequent to the FIRST observations conducted mostly in the 1990s, or the inhomogeneity of the radio regions observed
(
Condon & Ransom 2016
)
.
Sources located in quadrant 2 have an inverted spectrum.
One possible explanation is the sudden increase in the radio brightness.
More commonly, it is due to the fact that synchrotron self-absorption
(
Tingay & de Kool 2003
;
Nyland et al. 2020
, SSA; e.g.,)
dominates synchrotron radiation.
Sources located in quadrant 3 have the most commonly observed power-law distribution of the non-thermal bremsstrahlung.
These sources may have a steepening of the spectral index, forming the curved power-law spectrum as a result of synchrotron losses and/or inverse-Compton losses
(
Dermer & Menon 2009
;
Blandford et al. 2019
)
.
Sources located in quadrant 4 have a peaked frequency (
{\nu}_{\mathrm{p}}
) inasmuch an equilibrium between the brightness and kinetic temperatures of the synchrotron-radiating electrons
(
Longair 2011
)
.
The corresponding radio galaxies could be GHz-peaked (
0.5\leq{\nu}_{\mathrm{p}}\leq 5
GHz) or high-frequency-peaked (
{\nu}_{\mathrm{p}}>5
GHz) sources depending on their redshifts, which comprises the so-called Peaked-Spectrum (PS) sources that are young radio galaxies (see
O’Dea & Saikia 2021
and references therein).

Sources with inverted spectrum between 144 MHz and 3 GHz (in quadrant 2) may also have peaked/turnover frequencies above 3 GHz, making them PS sources.

V.2
High-
z
Candidates with
z\geq 3

As introduced in §
V.1
, the USS characterized by
\alpha<-1.3
is an efficient technique to select HzRGs.
However, in addition to these USS sources, many HzRGs have been discovered with relatively flat spectral index (e.g.,
{\alpha}_{\mathrm{1.4GHz}}^{\mathrm{325MHz}}=-0.75
for J163912.11+405236.5 at
z=4.88
,
Jarvis et al. 2009
;
{\alpha}_{\mathrm{1.4GHz}}^{\mathrm{325MHz}}=-1.1
for HSC J083913.17+011308.1 at
z=4.72
,
Yamashita et al. 2020
; see also
Miley & De Breuck 2008
).
As a consequence, a sole reliance on USS could lead to a bias in the statistical physical properties of the HzRGs since the diverse nature could be over-represented by USS.

An alternative approach to select HzRGs, which can circumvent the selection bias arising from the uncertain real spectral index distributions, is the Lyman break technique commonly adopted in the search for high-
z
galaxies.
The effectiveness of this technique in discovering HzRGs has been proven by
Capetti et al. 2025
, who identified 39
g-
dropout HzRGs expected to be at
3<z<4.5
over an area of
\sim 2,000\mathrm{\ deg^{2}}
, and by
Yamamoto et al. 2025
, who constructed a photometric sample of 146 HzRGs at
z\sim 4
within a total survey area of
\sim 560\mathrm{\ deg^{2}}
.

The Lyman break color selection technique selects sources that exhibit a clear Lyman break and blue UV continuum in their optical SEDs covered by broadband (
ugriz
) photometry.
Following
Hildebrandt et al. 2009
that have used a similar filter set,
g-
dropouts (
3<z<5
) are selected according to

\displaystyle g-r>1.0,

(6)

\displaystyle r-i<1.0,

\displaystyle g-r>1.5(r-i)+0.8,

and
r-
dropouts (
4<z<6
) are extracted adopting

\displaystyle r-i>1.2,

(7)

\displaystyle i-z<0.7,

\displaystyle r-i>1.5(i-z)+1.0.

In each dropout selection, three adjacent bands have to be used.
We therefore only select dropout candidates if the photometric measurements of these bands are not flagged and have valid AB magnitudes (see §
III.3
).
Additionally, for
g-
dropouts, the observation can barely detect fluxes prior to the Lyman break, thus the
u-
band should be fainter than the detection limit with
\mathtt{MAG\_GAAP\_u==99}
, that is, there is no successful photometric measurement of the corresponding source.
Likely, this criterion of
u-
band for
g-
dropouts should be applied to both
u-
and
g-
bands for
r-
dropouts.
Moreover, to ensure these high-
z
candidates are real detections, we further require their
{i}_{\mathrm{ABmag}}
to be brighter than the
10\sigma
detection limit 23.8.
Furthermore, to avoid foreground contaminants, we apply a magnitude cut of
{i}_{\mathrm{ABmag}}\geq 23
.

We show
g-
and
r-
dropout candidates in the upper and lower panels of Figure
17
, respectively.
The above criteria produce 4,049 valid sources for the
r-
dropouts, and only 12 of them can be at
z\sim 5
, which is reasonable considering the limited depth of UNIONS.
Of
{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}63,019}
UNIONS-VLASS sources with reliable BPZ photo-
z
, there are only 2 at
z>4.5
, comparable with the
r-
dropout selections.
The number of
g-
dropout-selected sources is
477
, which exceeds the number counts of
53
expected from reliable
3.5<{z}_{\mathrm{photo}}<4.5
estimates by almost one order of magnitude.

This potential excess in the number counts of
g-
dropouts – if they are not bona fide sources at
z\sim 4
– could be explained by the contamination of foreground sources such as red elliptical galaxies at intermediate redshifts, whose
{D}_{\mathrm{4000}}
break due to the absorption of old stellar populations mimic the Lyman break feature.
The photometric errors of these foreground contaminations satisfy the selection criteria of the
g-
dropout
(
Ono et al. 2018
, e.g.,)
.
For Subaru HSC, which has comparable optical number counts and similar efficient broadband wavelengths to those of UNIONS,
Ono et al. 2018
found the contamination rate in the
i-
band magnitude bin of 22.5 can reach
\sim 68\%
and decreases to
\sim 41\%
at
{i}_{\mathrm{ABmag}}=23.1
and to
\sim 35\%
at
{i}_{\mathrm{ABmag}}=23.7
for
g-
dropouts.
Therefore, even if we only select UNIONS galaxies at the faint end of
i-
band (
{i}_{\mathrm{AB}}>23
), the contamination problem can still persist.
Similarly, for
r-
dropouts,
Ono et al. 2018
reported contaminate rates of approximately
23\%
at
{z}_{\mathrm{ABmag}}=22.3
and around
59\%
at
{z}_{\mathrm{ABmag}}=23.5
, implying that merely
<5

r-
dropouts could be true positives if applying these estimates to UNIONS-VLASS.
Nonetheless, under the optimistic assumption of HSC-level contamination rates, our UNIONS-VLASS radio galaxy catalog is expected to yield more than 200 HzRGs at
z\sim 4
, offering strong potential to significantly enhance the statistical robustness of studies on HzRGs.

All in all, these expectations for high-
z
RLAGNs are subject to the reliability of the photometry of UNIONS.
A deeper investigation into the HzRGs will be conducted in future work using spectral energy distribution fittings combining IR data (Zhong et al. in preparation).

V.3
Caveat to the Catalog

The effect of applying
\mathtt{NN\_dist>30^{\prime\prime}}
to VLASS2 is clearly reflected in the projected linear size, that is, the majority of our RGs are compact (
{l}_{\mathrm{s}}\leq 20
kpc), which means our UNIONS-VLASS RGs have the tendency to be young and evolving populations.
This also leads to a potential bias of our HzRG populations.
As they are often selected by the USS feature in previous studies, there are only a few of them showing USS at
z>2
amongst
15,906
UNIONS-VLASS-FIRST sources with valid
{\alpha}_{\mathrm{VLASS}}^{\mathrm{FIRST}}
.
This global trend of being compact for our RGs is more a result of the limited search radius of
1.8^{\prime\prime}
,
As shown in Figure
2
, in
\mathtt{NN\_dist<30^{\prime\prime}}
, there is no concentration of radio sources with small offsets (
\leq 1^{\prime\prime}
) from their optical counterparts.
To maintain the current cleanliness of UNIONS-VLASS catalog, even if
\mathtt{NN\_dist<30^{\prime\prime}}
is not adopted, we do not expect a significant increase in the number counts of bright and extended radio sources.
We, thereby, remind the readers to be cautious about the intrinsic and unresolved bias arising from the design of this work.
Nonetheless, the vast size of this catalog still ensures statistical studies of radio AGNs across various evolution stages, offering valuable insights into the connections between radio AGNs and their host galaxies.

VI
Summary

We presented the results of searching for the optical counterparts of radio sources using the grand UNIONS catalog and the VLASS catalog.
This marks the first step of the ongoing project, UNIONS-VLASS radio galaxies in the Euclid wide survey area (UNVEIL).
The current angular separation-based cross-match between UNIONS and VLASS found 146,212 radio galaxies/quasars spanning a wide area of
\sim 4,200\ \mathrm{deg^{2}}
in the northern and southern Galactic caps.
The fraction of matches reaches 0.594, beyond the previous FIRST 1.4 GHz radio galaxy catalog based on the SDSS and Subaru HSC catalogs.

Setting UNIONS-VLASS as the reference catalog, we further performed cross-match with FIRST 1.4 GHz and LoTSS 144 MHz catalogs, yielding 79,638 UNIONS-VLASS-FIRST, 101,671 UNIONS-VLASS-LoTSS, and 64,672 UNIONS-VLASS-FIRST-LoTSS radio galaxies, respectively.
We identified radio-loud AGNs based on the observed radio loudness (
\mathcal{R}_{\mathrm{obs}}
) defined as the ratio of 1.4 GHz radio to
r-
band optical flux densities.
Of 146,212 “clean” UNIONS-VLASS radio galaxies, 138,266 (
\sim 94.6\%
) are RL and 7,496 (
\sim 5.4\%
) are RQ populations.
Alternatively, RLAGNs can be selected by their 1.4 spectral luminosities
{L}_{\mathrm{1.4GHz}}>10^{24}\mathrm{\ W\ Hz^{-1}}
.
Under this condition, at
z\gtrsim 1
, both VLASS and FIRST cannot detect any RQ populations.
RLAGNs selections based on
\mathcal{R}_{\mathrm{obs}}
and
{L}_{\mathrm{1.4GHz}}
are common for 49,012 out of 63,019 sources with valid UNIONS photometric redshift estimates.

We presented radio color-color (
{\alpha}_{\mathrm{1400MHz}}^{\mathrm{144MHz}}-{\alpha}_{\mathrm{3000MHz}}^{\mathrm{1400MHz}}
) based on LoTSS, FIRST, and VLASS observations, and further plotted spectral indices as a function of redshift and the projected linear size of the synchrotron-emitting volume.
We found a great diversity of radio classifications, including Compact-Steep-Spectrum and Peaked-Spectrum sources that are likely young radio galaxies, Ultra-Steep-Spectrum sources which may have significantly evolved, and inverted and upturned spectrum that may be linked to transient sources.
There are
\sim 10,000
additional radio galaxies corresponding to more than one optical counterpart.
These sources are merger candidates that host radio galaxies.
The radio classifications/RLAGN evolution and merger-RLAGN connections will be further studied in future works.

The UNIONS internal data release used for this work does not cover the full UNIONS footprint in both NGC and SGC.
In addition, UNIONS is an ongoing survey that will eventually cover the entire northern hemisphere.
Moreover, the VLASS Epoch 3 observation has just been completed, and the concatenated catalog reaching a great depth has not been released.
Therefore, our UNIONS-VLASS catalog has great potential for expansion.
The full UNIONS footprint will be covered by the
Euclid
wide survey, and this work serves as a valuable pavement for the future
Euclid
radio galaxy catalog.
Our UNIONS-VLASS radio galaxy catalog will be observed in the Subaru PFS filler program.
By virtue of the broad spectroscopic coverage (
\sim 3000-20000\AA
) and imaging data, the abundance of RLAGNs in the UNIONS-VLASS catalog will profoundly facilitate multifaceted statistical studies of radio galaxies and beyond.

Appendix D
Radio-loud AGNs

Star formation can contribute to the non-thermal emission, and rigorously
\log\mathcal{R}_{\mathrm{obs}}>2
may be adopted to exclude star-forming/starburst galaxies that mimic RLAGNs
(
Chiaberge & Marconi 2011
, e.g.,)
.
This reduces our RLAGNs to 109,901 (
\sim 77.0\%
).
The spectral luminosity of free-free emission attributed to star formation can be quantified by
(
Condon 1992
)
:

\left({\frac{{L}_{\mathrm{NT}}}{\mathrm{\ W\ Hz^{-1}}}}\right)\sim 5.5\times 10^{20}\left({\frac{\nu}{\mathrm{GHz}}}\right)^{-0.1}\left[\frac{\mathrm{SFR(\mathit{{M}_{\mathrm{star}}}\geq\mathrm{M_{\odot}})}}{\mathrm{M_{\odot}}\ \rm yr^{-1}}\right],

(D1)

and the non-thermal emission related to SF activities follows

\left({\frac{{L}_{\mathrm{NT}}}{\mathrm{\ W\ Hz^{-1}}}}\right)\sim 5.3\times 10^{21}\left({\frac{\nu}{\mathrm{GHz}}}\right)^{-0\alpha}\left[\frac{\mathrm{SFR(\mathit{{M}_{\mathrm{star}}}\geq\mathrm{M_{\odot}})}}{\mathrm{M_{\odot}}\ \rm yr^{-1}}\right].

(D2)

Assuming an upper limit of
\mathrm{SFR}=300\ \mathrm{M_{\odot}}\ \rm yr^{-1}
for star-forming main sequence galaxies at
z\gtrsim 2

(
Popesso et al. 2023
)
, the corresponding luminosities are
{L}_{\mathrm{T,SF}}\sim 1.5\times 10^{23}\mathrm{\ W\ Hz^{-1}}
and
{L}_{\mathrm{NT,SF}}\sim 1.3\times 10^{24}\mathrm{\ W\ Hz^{-1}}
.
In Figure
13
for
{L}_{\mathrm{1.4GHz}}
and
{L}_{\mathrm{3GHz}}
as a function of
z
, this
{L}_{\mathrm{NT,SF}}
only selects radio galaxies at
z\lesssim 1
where the host galaxies should have much smaller values of SFR compared to high-
z
sources even if they are rarely observed starburst systems.
The real contributions of star-forming activities to the observed synchrotron radiation should be insignificant (
\lesssim 1.5\times 10^{22}\mathrm{\ W\ Hz^{-1}}
assuming
\mathrm{SFR}=40\ \mathrm{M_{\odot}}\ \rm yr^{-1}
at
z\sim 0.5
).
Therefore,
\mathcal{R}_{\mathrm{obs}}>1
can be safely adopted to select RLAGNs for the UNIONS-VLASS sample.

We see correlations between
{L}_{\mathrm{1.4GHz}}-\mathcal{R}_{\mathrm{obs}}
and
\mathcal{R}_{\mathrm{obs}}-{z}_{\mathrm{photo}}
, and these correlations are likely a propagation of the correlation between
{i}_{\mathrm{ABmag}}-{z}_{\mathrm{photo}}
shown in the left panel of Figure
21
that reflects the cosmological surface brightness dimming.
As a result, high-
z
sources have higher possibilities of being classified as RLAGNs because of their optical faintness.
In the right panel of Figure
21
, we plot absolute radio magnitude
M_{t}
as a function of the observed radio loudness
\mathcal{R}_{\mathrm{obs}}
, where
M_{t}
is defined as
(
Ivezić et al. 2002
)

M_{t}=-2.5\log\left({\frac{{L}_{\mathrm{radio}}}{{L}_{\mathrm{AB}}}}\right).

(D3)

{L}_{\mathrm{radio}}
is the specific or spectral radio luminosity, and
{L}_{\mathrm{AB}}=4.345\times 10^{13}\mathrm{\ W\ Hz^{-1}}
is the specific luminosity of a source whose specific flux
is 3631 Jy at a distance of 10 pc.
Normally,
M_{t}=-25.9
is chosen as the RL/RQ demarcation, corresponding to
{L}_{\mathrm{1.4GHz}}\sim 10^{24}{h}_{\mathrm{50}}^{\mathrm{-2}}\mathrm{\ W\ Hz^{-1}}
.
For 146,212 UNIONS-VLASS sources with valid
r-
band photometry to calculate
\mathcal{R}_{\mathrm{obs}}
, the two thresholds to select RLAGNs are the same for 83.3%.

r-
