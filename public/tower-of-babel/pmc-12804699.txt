IL-21 promotes the anti-tumor effect of anti-CD47 chimeric antigen receptor macrophages in ovarian cancer
Authors: Yizhao Chen, Xiangling Zhu, Yu Chen, Zhaoyi Yang, Zhaolei Shen, Mengchen Chen, Chong Liu, Yuanyuan Zhou, Huihui Wang, Mengjuan Zhu, Jiaqi Qiu, Jinhua Huang, Xintong Han, Wei Wei, Li Ruilin, Wenming Hong, Jiajie Tu
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Macrophages play a key role in immunity against solid tumors. However, their development and clinical applications are limited by their difficult-to-transfect nature, low proliferative capacity, and easily changing polarization states. The combination of chimeric antigen receptor (CAR) technology with macrophages to form chimeric antigen receptor macrophages (CAR-Ms) is an emerging strategy for adoptive cell therapy. In our previous study, we confirmed that anti-CD47 CAR-Ms have potential for ovarian cancer treatment. Here, we demonstrated that the introduction of IL-21 significantly increases the tumor-suppressive effect of anti-CD47 CAR-Ms against ovarian cancer. Specifically, IL-21-modified CAR-Ms with second-generation CARs targeting CD47 showed potent tumor cell-killing activity, both in vitro and in vivo, through direct and indirect pathways (direct phagocytosis and activation of cytotoxic T lymphocytes). In addition, an IL-21 modification significantly enhanced the tumor microenvironmental regulation of immunosuppression mediated by anti-CD47 CAR-Ms in vivo, thereby improving their therapeutic efficacy in a mouse model of ovarian cancer, without any obvious adverse effects. Taken together, these results suggest that anti-CD47 CAR-Ms combined with IL-21 is a promising treatment strategy for ovarian cancer.

Introduction

Chimeric antigen receptor (CAR)-T cell cancer immune cell therapy has been successful for the treatment of B cell leukemia/lymphoma and other hematologic malignancies, and continuous progress is being made in the treatment of other diseases1. However, although therapeutic cell therapies have been designed to precisely target solid tumor cell antigens, relevant studies that have achieved clear clinical improvements are rare2,3, and breakthroughs in immune cell therapy for solid tumors are still lacking4. To date, challenges such as the immunosuppressive tumor microenvironment (TME), limited infiltration of anti-cancer immune cells, off-target effects, antigen evasion, and intratumoral heterogeneity have been identified as significant barriers to successfully targeting solid tumors using immune cell therapy4,5.

Macrophages, terminally differentiated monocytic phagocytic cells, have gained prominence in the field of solid tumor immunotherapy owing to their phagocytic ability and important role in bridging adaptive and innate immunity6. Accumulating evidence confirms that tumor-associated macrophages (TAMs) are abundantly present in solid tumor tissues, are reprogrammed to an alternatively activated M2 phenotype, and exert a tumorigenic effect in cancer by suppressing the anti-tumor immune response7. Thus, the introduction of pro-inflammatory or M1-like macrophages represents a viable strategy for improving the solid TME8. Over the past several years, considerable efforts have been made to target endogenous macrophages and TAMs. Further, recent technological advances in genetically engineered macrophages have facilitated their improved utilization as therapeutic cells for adoptive transfer.

Our previous study validated the feasibility of using anti-CD47 CAR-macrophages (CAR-Ms) and demonstrated their efficacy in treating ovarian cancer9. However, the structure of the first-generation CAR-Ms was generated based on CAR-T cells10. Therefore, designing a new macrophage-specific CAR that confers phagocytic and M1-polarization capabilities to CAR-Ms will help with solid cancer treatment. IL-21 is a widely expressed cytokine that regulates the functions of B cells, T cells, dendritic cells, and NK cells through IL-21R11. In recent years, IL-21R was also found to be expressed on the surfaces of monocytes/macrophages, and it is regulated by IL-21, which promotes phagocytosis, enhances antigen presentation, and affects the M1 polarization of TAMs12–14. Considering the beneficial effects of IL-21 on the immune system, including promoting the proliferation and activation of CD8+ T cells and NK cells, inhibiting angiogenesis15 and angiostatic activity16, and promoting macrophage phagocytosis and antigen presentation17, the use of IL-21 to modify CAR-Ms targeting solid tumors is a worthwhile approach.

We then validated the effects of IL-21 on macrophage phagocytosis (Fig. S2A). To evaluate the effect of IL-21 on the phagocytic capacity of anti-CD47 CAR-Ms, two CD47-expressing ovarian cancer cell lines, SKOV3 and A2780, were co-cultured with the CAR-Ms. Flow cytometry results showed a significant increase in the phagocytosis of the two CD47+ ovarian cancer cell lines co-cultured with anti-CD47 CAR-Ms (IL-21) (Fig. 1D, E and Fig. S3A), and similar results were obtained using primary macrophage-derived CAR-Ms (Fig. S3B). Simultaneously, the survival rate of SKOV3 and A2780 ovarian cancer cells was significantly reduced after co-culture with anti-CD47 CAR-Ms, and the presence of IL-21 decreased the survival of ovarian cancer cells (Fig. 1F, G and Fig. S2B), suggesting that anti-CD47 CAR-Ms (IL-21) exert a strong anti-tumor effect in vitro.

IL-21 augments the promoting effect of anti-CD47 CAR-Ms on the anti-tumor capacity of cytotoxic T lymphocytes (CTLs) in vitro

Previously, we confirmed the positive effects of anti-CD47 CAR-Ms on CTLs and evaluated the interactions among anti-CD47 CAR-Ms (IL-21), CTLs, and ovarian tumor cells. Macrophages can be affected by the TME owing to their phenotypic plasticity, and mechanisms to resist the effect of the immunosuppressive TME on the M2 polarization of CAR-Ms to maintain the anti-tumor activity of CAR-Ms comprise an important issue to achieve CAR-M adoptive therapy18. We first examined whether IL-21 can affect the polarization of CAR-Ms; after co-culturing anti-CD47 CAR-Ms with SKOV3 or A2780 cells, flow cytometry results showed that the expression of CD86, an M1 marker, was upregulated on anti-CD47 CAR-Ms (IL-21) (Fig. 2A). Meanwhile, CD206 expression was largely unchanged (Fig. 2B). Moreover, cytokine analysis of co-culture supernatants revealed that IL-21 promoted IFN-γ secretion from anti-CD47 CAR-Ms (Fig. S4A–D). Antigen presentation is an important mechanism for macrophages to communicate with T cells, and we tested whether CAR-Ms could present phagocytosed tumor cell debris to T cells as antigens. The flow cytometry results showed that both anti-CD47 CAR-Ms and anti-CD47 CAR-Ms (IL-21) promoted the differentiation of CD3+ T cells into CTLs after co-culture with SKOV3 or A2780 cells, whereas the presence of IL-21 further promoted CTL activation (Fig. 2C and Fig. S4E). Simultaneously, CD40L expression on the surface of CD3+ T cells was upregulated, suggesting positive communication between CAR-Ms and CD3+ T cells (Fig. 2K and Fig. S4F).

CTLs mainly attack tumor cells through the secretion of anti-tumor cytokines, and thus, we tested the secretion of several key cytokines, including GzmB, RPF1, IL-2, IFN-γ, TNF-α, and LT-α, in the co-culture system. ELISA results demonstrated that the levels of these cytokines were significantly increased in the co-culture system comprising CAR-Ms, CD3+ T cells, and SKOV3 or A2780 cells and were further upregulated in the presence of IL-21 (Fig. 2D–I). Fas/FasL-induced apoptosis is another important mechanism by which CTLs kill tumor cells. FasL expression on the surfaces of CD3+ T cells was induced by co-culture with anti-CD47 CAR-Ms, whereas the upregulation of FasL expression on the surfaces of anti-CD47 CAR-Ms (IL-21) was more pronounced (Fig. 2J). Overall, these results imply that IL-21 promotes pro-inflammatory phenotypic switching in anti-CD47 CAR-Ms, which induces antigen presentation to enhance CTL activation and anti-tumor functions (Fig. 2L).

IL-21 promotes the anti-tumor effects of anti-CD47 CAR-Ms in vivo

The effects of IL-21 co-expression on the in vivo anti-tumor effects of anti-CD47 CAR-Ms were evaluated using two models. The first model comprised the subcutaneous implantation of a mixture of SKOV3 cells and CAR-Ms into nude mice (Fig. 3A). The inhibitory effect of anti-CD47 CAR-Ms was more evident compared to that in the control group, and the presence of IL-21 amplified this (Fig. 3B–D). Consistent with this result, the proportion of Ki67+ cells in the tumor tissue was lower in the anti-CD47 CAR-M (IL-21) group (Fig. 3E). Safety assessment of anti-CD47 CAR-Ms (IL-21)

To evaluate the safety of CAR-Ms for immunotherapy, these cells were injected subcutaneously into wild-type C57 mice, and toxicity was monitored, including changes in body weight, CAR-M infiltration, and histopathological changes in major organs (Fig. S5A). In vitro, CAR-M phagocytosis, upon co-culture with HEK293T cells, showed no significant differences (Fig. S5E), consistent with the results of animal experiments showing no significant changes in body weights (Fig. S5B). The infiltration of CAR-Ms was detected in the liver, spleen, lungs, and kidneys (Fig. S5D), and H&E staining of these tissues showed no significant abnormalities in these organs (Fig. S5C). These results suggest that anti-CD47 CAR-M treatment is safe, and no significant CAR-M-related toxicity was observed in our study.

Discussion

CAR-Ms have been identified as a novel anti-tumor adoptive cell therapy based on the tumor-homing effect of macrophages, forming a functional network targeting solid tumors through CAR-mediated antigen-specific phagocytosis, followed by antigen presentation and interference with the immunosuppressive TME19. Clinical trials testing CAR-Ms for solid tumor treatment have yielded preliminary results and provided evidence of this. However, an important issue is maintaining the anti-tumor activity of CAR-Ms and the inflammatory TME. The significance of cytokines for CAR immune cell therapy, including the regulation of immune cell survival, activation, proliferation, and enhancement of anti-tumor activity, has been demonstrated in several studies, and commonly used cytokines include interleukin and IFN-γ, among others20,21. However, in CAR-M-related studies, there have been a few attempts to modify the CAR structure with cytokines. Han et al.22 used IFN-γ to modify the CAR structure, which promoted the M1 polarization of CAR-Ms and maintained the inflammatory TME. However, there have been no attempts to co-express interleukins to enhance CAR-M functions and improve the TME.

The gamma chain family of cytokine receptors is one of the most widely studied cytokines, and it includes IL-2, IL-4, IL-7, IL-9, IL-15, and IL-21, which together promote the development and differentiation of various immune cell populations23. IL-21 is a multipotent cytokine that regulates various immune cells by promoting the proliferation and activation of T cells, driving the differentiation of B cells into memory cells, and enhancing the activity of NK cells24. IL-21 is an important prophagocytic cytokine that promotes antigen presentation and matrix metalloproteinase expression in macrophages12,17. In addition, IL-21 has lower toxicity compared to other γ-family cytokines25. Based on this, we explored the feasibility of combination therapy using IL-21 and CAR-M cells.

We found that IL-21 promoted the phagocytosis of macrophages through the AKT and ERK signaling pathways and that anti-CD47 CAR-Ms (IL-21) showed enhanced anti-tumor activity in vitro, exhibited induction of the M1 phenotype, and promoted CTL activation. Previously, we demonstrated that CAR-Ms could induce an inflammatory TME in a humanized mouse model and promote a CD8+ T cell response. In this study, the introduction of IL-21 further enhanced CAR-M survival and sustained tumor inhibition in tumor-bearing mice; it also enhanced remodeling of the TME mediated by anti-CD47 CAR-Ms, and IL-21 secreted by CAR-Ms promoted T cell activation through STAT signaling. Moreover, IL-21 improved the anti-tumor function of CAR-Ms in tumor-bearing mice without causing the excessive expansion of CAR-Ms after tumor clearance. Therefore, IL-21 co-expression can directly promote the adaptability of CAR-Ms, successfully preserve the survival rate of effector cells in vivo, assist in tumor suppression, and provide a preclinical experimental platform for CAR-mediated immunotherapy. This study provides the latest results based on the modification of CAR-Ms using the interleukin family, suggesting an important application of cytokine modifications to maintain and enhance CAR-M activity.

However, although the IL-21 modification enhanced the activity of CAR-Ms, this was validated using only immunodeficient and humanized mouse models. This model cannot simulate the complex human tissue microenvironment to accurately evaluate the interactions between CAR-Ms and immune cells and the metabolic characteristics of CAR-Ms. Furthermore, limited by the shorter period used for the Hu-PBMC model, we were unable to assess the long-term effects of IL-21-modified CAR-Ms on the TME.

As cell sources, with gene delivery methods, targeting, efficacy, and safety being validated or alternatives becoming available, CAR-Ms have entered a new era of clinical research26. Accordingly, one clinical study on the first adoptive CAR-M therapy, CT-0508 (NCT04660929), has made progress. Of the 14 treated patients, four patients achieved stable disease, and of note, all four were highly HER2-positive, whereas five patients in whom HER2 was moderately expressed experienced disease progression27. Researchers are now faced with the problem of developing more efficient and safer CAR-Ms, including the development of non-viral vectors, the metabolic reprogramming of CAR-Ms, and novel CAR structural designs, and assessing the feasibility of combination therapy28,29. Moreover, CAR-M technology has been tested in preclinical studies on non-tumor diseases, such as fibrosis and drug-resistant bacterial infections and has shown effectiveness using animal models, achieving disease remission or cure, through the elimination of specific pathogens or target cells30–32. These research advances indicate that CAR-M technology has great potential and broad application prospects for the treatment of various diseases33.

Materials and methods

Cell lines

The ovarian cancer cell lines SKOV3 and A2780, human monocyte cell line THP-1, and human embryonic kidney cell line HEK293T were purchased from Procell Life Science & Technology. SKOV3 cells were cultured in McCoy’s 5A medium containing 10% FBS and 1% penicillin/streptomycin. A2780 cells were cultured in RPMI1640 medium containing 10% FBS and 1% penicillin/streptomycin, and HEK293T cells were cultured in DMEM containing 10% FBS and 1% penicillin/streptomycin; THP-1 cells were cultured in RPMI1640 medium containing 10% FBS. All cells are cultured in a constant-temperature incubator containing 5% carbon dioxide at 37 °C. Identity of each cell line was confirmed by STR profiling, and regular testing was conducted to ensure they were free from mycoplasma contamination.

Isolation of primary T cells and monocytes

Peripheral blood from healthy volunteers was collected, diluted 1:1 with phosphate-buffered saline (PBS), and spread on the upper layer of the human lymphocyte separation solution. Peripheral blood mononuclear cells (PBMCs) were separated via density gradient centrifugation. After PBMCs were successfully isolated, CD3+ T cells were labeled, sorted with CD3 magnetic beads (130-097-043, Miltenyi Biotec), and cultured in RPMI1640 medium containing 10% FBS. Similarly, monocytes were isolated from PBMCs using CD14 immunomagnetic beads. The isolated monocytes were cultured in RPMI1640 medium containing 10% FBS and 10 ng/ml GM-CSF(300-03, PeproTech). All experiments were approved by the Biomedical Ethics Committee of Anhui Medical University, and informed consent was obtained from all human participants (Grant No. 81250167). All ethical regulations relevant to human research participants were followed.

FACS-based phagocytosis assay

SKOV3 and A2780 cells were labeled with CellTracker Red CMTPX (C34552; Invitrogen) and co-cultured with GFP-expressing CAR-Ms (THP-1-M cells or primary macrophages) at a 1:1 ratio. After 4 h, cells were collected, centrifuged at 1200 rpm for 5 min, and resuspended in 200 μl of PBS for subsequent flow cytometric and imaging flow cytometric analyses.

In vitro cytotoxicity assay

SKOV3 or A2780 cells were co-cultured with GFP-expressing CAR THP-1-derived macrophages (THP-1Ms) at a 1:1 ratio (untransfected THP-1Ms were added to ensure the same number and proportion of CAR-Ms), and after 48 h, whole cells were collected, centrifuged at 1200 rpm for 5 min, resuspended in 200 µl of PBS, and tested via flow cytometry, with FITC-negative cells representing surviving tumor cells.

Phenotypic analysis of CAR-Ms

SKOV3 or A2780 cells were co-cultured with CAR THP-1Ms at a 1:1 ratio; cells were collected after 48 h, followed by labeling with anti-human CD86 (374207, BioLegend) and anti-human CD206 (321125, BioLegend) antibodies, incubation at 4 °C for 30 min, centrifugation at 1200 rpm for 5 min, and the resuspension of cells in 200 μl of PBS. Flow cytometry was then performed.

Evaluation of macrophage-dependent activation of CD8+ T cells

SKOV3 or A2780 cells were co-cultured with CAR THP-1Ms at a 1:1 ratio for 4 h, and sorted CD3+ T cells were added to the co-culture system for 48 h. After co-culture, T cells were collected, labeled with anti-human CD8 (344747, BioLegend), anti-human IFN-γ (2331102, Invitrogen), anti-human CD40L (310824, BioLegend), and anti-human FasL (306421, BioLegend) antibodies, and incubated at 4 °C for 30 min. The sample was then centrifuged at 1200 rpm for 5 min, and cells were resuspended in 200 μl of PBS for subsequent flow cytometry.

Cytokine analysis

The anti-CD47 CAR-M (IL-21) cell culture supernatant was collected, and the concentration of IL-21 (1112102, Dakewe Biotech) was determined according to the manufacturer’s instructions. The tumor cell, CAR-M, and CD3+ T cell co-culture system supernatants were collected, and cytokine analysis was performed using ELISA kits, including those for GzmB (ELH-GZMB, RayBio), PRF1 (EK16250, SABbiotech), IFN-γ (ELH-IFNg, RayBio), IL-2 (ELH-IL-2, RayBio), TNF-α (ELH-TNFα, RayBio), and LT-α (EK17004, SABbiotech). The results were analyzed using a microplate reader (Tecan, Infnite M1000 PRO).

RNA-seq

Four groups of samples were tested for RNA-seq, namely Skov3 cells stimulated with or without IL-21, anti-CD47 CAR-Ms, and anti-CD47 CAR-Ms (IL-21). RNA was extracted according to standard methods, and the RNA integrity was assessed. Sequencing was performed by Baixin Biotechnology Co., Ltd. (Shenzhen, China). Sequencing and analysis were performed according to the standard RNA-seq protocol provided by Baixin Biotechnology Co., Ltd.

scRNA-seq

The samples for scRNA-seq analysis in this study were derived from the subcutaneous tumor-bearing tissues of Hu-PBMC-model mice. Quantitative information was collected using the DNBelab C series platform, library construction was performed according to the number of isolated cells per sample, and sequencing was performed using the DNBSEQ platform.

In vivo tumor models

C57BL/6 mice (female, 18 ± 4 g, 5 weeks old), BALB/c nude mice (female, 18 ± 2 g, 4 weeks old), and NOD/ShiLtJGpt-Prkdcem26Cd52Il2rgem26Cd22/Gpt (NCG) mice (female, 16 ± 2 g, 4 weeks old) were purchased from Nanjing Jicui Yaokang Biotechnology Co., Ltd. They were maintained in the SPF animal room of the Institute of Clinical Pharmacology of Anhui Medical University, with SPF-grade drinking water and food provided and a 12 h light cycle. All experiments were approved by the Experimental Ethics Review Committee of Anhui Medical University (Grant No. PZ-2023-019). The committee stipulated a maximum allowable tumor volume of 2000 mm3, which was not exceeded in any experiments. We have complied with all relevant ethical regulations for animal use.

The first in vivo tumor model comprised a hybrid tumor implant model based on SKOV3 cells and CAR-Ms. Nude mice were divided into three groups and administered subcutaneous injections of SKOV3+vehicle control, SKOV3+anti-CD47 CAR-Ms, and SKOV3+anti-CD47 CAR-Ms (IL-21), and the tumor volume was recorded every 3 days. For the second in vivo tumor model, we first established a subcutaneous tumor-bearing model based on SKOV3 cells and then intravenously injected animals with CAR-Ms on the fifth day after the model was established. The tumor volumes and body weights of the mice were recorded every 2 days, with death, weakness, or a tumor volume exceeding 1500 mm3 comprising the final event.

For the tumor-bearing Hu-PBMC mouse model, we first injected NCG mice with SKOV3 cells, in the axillary arm, followed by the intravenous injection of Hu-PBMC cells. After the successful establishment of the Hu-PBMC model was confirmed, the intratumoral injection of CAR-Ms was performed on day 18 for treatment. Then mouse body weights and tumor volumes were recorded every 3 days from the establishment of the model.

Flow cytometry

For the SKOV3 cell-bearing tumor model established subcutaneously using nude mice or NCG mice, tumor tissues were finally removed, partially minced, digested into a single-cell suspension, and incubated with antibodies at 4 °C for 30 min for surface staining. These included anti-human Ki67 (350514, BioLegend), anti-human FOXP3 (320007, BioLegend), anti-human CD25 (302604, BioLegend), anti-human CD3 (300308, BioLegend), and anti-human CD8 (344747, BioLegend) antibodies. Assays were performed using a Beckman flow cytometer, and results were analyzed using CytExpert software.

Immunohistochemistry

Immunohistochemistry experiments were performed according to the standard protocol, and antigen retrieval was performed according to the requirements of the anti-PCNA (ab92552, Abcam), anti-human α-SMA (ab5694, Abcam), anti-human VEGF (ab1316, Abcam), and anti-human CD68 (ab213363, Abcam) primary antibodies.

Western blotting

First, 1 × 106 cells were collected, resuspended in 100 μl of RIPA buffer, incubated on ice for 30 min, and centrifuged at 12,000×g for 15 min. Then, the supernatant was collected, protein loading buffer was added, and the sample was boiled at 100 °C for 15 min to obtain the samples. Western blot assays were performed according to standard procedures, and the primary antibodies used in the experiments included anti-β-actin (T0022, Affinity Biosciences), anti-ERK (4695, Cell Signaling Technology), anti-p-ERK (4370, Cell Signaling Technology), anti-AKT (10176-2-AP, Proteintech), anti-p-AKT (4060 s, Cell Signaling Technology), and anti-p-Syk (AF3315, Affinity Biosciences).

Statistics and reproducibility

Student’s t-tests, one or two-way analysis of variance, and Kaplan–Meier survival curves were used for statistical analysis using GraphPad Prism 8.0. All experiments were performed independently, at least in triplicate, and the data were expressed as the mean ± standard error. For all statistical analyses in this study, significance was set at P < 0.05, which was considered indicative of a significant difference.
