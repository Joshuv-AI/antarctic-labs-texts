Enabling full localization of qubits and gates with a multi-mode coupler
Zhongyi Jiang, Simon Geisert, Sören Ihssen, Ioan M. Pop, Mohammad H. Ansari

Abstract
Tunable couplers are a key building block of superconducting quantum processors, enabling high on–off ratios for two-qubit entangling interactions. While crosstalk can be mitigated in idle mode, conventional single-mode couplers lack independent control over interactions in the one- and two-excitation manifolds, leading to unitary errors such as leakage during gate operations. Moreover, even at the nominal decoupled point, residual wavefunction delocalization persists, causing unintended qubit–qubit coupling. Here we propose a multi-mode tunable coupler that enables nonlinear control of interactions across excitation manifolds, achieving a high on–off ratio in the one-excitation manifold while suppressing coupling in the two-excitation manifold. The proposed design also realizes complete localization between qubits, providing perfect isolation at the decoupled point and opening new possibilities for scalable, high-fidelity quantum gates.



I
Introduction

Superconducting quantum information processing systems have emerged as one of the most promising platforms for realizing large-scale quantum computing. Several experiments have already demonstrated that state-of-the-art superconducting processors can outperform classical supercomputers in certain tasks
Arute et al. 2019
;
Kim et al. 2023
. The preparation of entangled states on these processors is particularly vulnerable to errors. Effective denoising requires meticulous characterization of noise sources and the development of protocols that mitigate these disturbances without modifying the quantum state. Advancements in state denoising mark one of the critical milestones toward realizing scalable and fault-tolerant quantum computing. There are deterministic protocols for addressing denoising such as quantum error correction
Bravyi et al. 2024
and error mitigation techniques, such as quantum autoencoders
Pazem and Ansari 2025
. Therefore, achieving fault-tolerant quantum computing has become the next central objective of the community
Shor 1995
;
AI et al. 2024
. To implement quantum error correction, the physical qubit and gate errors must be reduced below a certain threshold
Shor 1995
;
Fowler et al. 2012
;
Bravyi et al. 2024
;
Mohseni et al. 2025
.

Although single-qubit gate errors can already be suppressed well below
0.1\%
, sometimes reaching
0.01\%
or even lower
Rower et al. 2024
, two-qubit gate errors cannot yet be consistently reduced below
0.1\%

Li et al. 2024
;
Ding et al. 2023
. Consequently, from a hardware perspective, the design and control of high-fidelity two-qubit gates remains one of the key challenges
AI et al. 2024
;
Mohseni et al. 2025
.

In superconducting systems, the most common approach to controlling two-qubit interactions is through a tunable coupler, typically realized with a single tunable mode that mediates the interaction
Chen et al. 2014
. Such couplers enable versatile gate schemes
McKay et al. 2016
;
Yan et al. 2018
;
Liang et al. 2023
. Recent lattice Hamiltonian techniques
Xu et al. 2024
and surface-code QPU Hamiltonian
Xu et al. 2025a
show the importance of tunable couplers to suppress unwanted two-body and three-body interactions
Xu et al. 2025b
in the soft qubit decoupling regime, which allows the achievement of large on-off ratios
Chen et al. 2014
.

Despite these advantages, the single-mode tunable coupler (SMC) design suffers from intrinsic limitations. A primary issue is that qubits remain partially delocalized, even when the coupler is tuned to the nominal “interaction-off” point
Berke et al. 2022
;
Heunisch et al. 2023
. Qubit wavefunctions inevitably leak into nearby qubits, generating crosstalk and degrading gate fidelity
Lange et al. 2025
. Another limitation is the lack of independent control over qubit–qubit interactions in the one-excitation and two-excitation subspaces. Because the interaction is mediated exclusively by a single tunable mode, both subspaces are activated simultaneously during a two-qubit gate
Xu et al. 2025a
;
Xu and Ansari 2023

In practice, computationally noninteracting qubits may still couple through higher-energy, noncomputational states, giving rise to the so-called
stray couplings

;
Xu and Ansari 2021
.

To address the limitations of single-mode couplers (SMCs), a recent design introduced a multi-mode coupler that allows a qubit to couple to a left-handed metamaterial ring resonator
McBroom-Carroll et al. 2024
. Its detailed theoretical treatment and the purity of the agreement with experiment, without fine tuning, motivated this work. Here, we propose a two-mode tunable coupler (TMC) and generalize it to multiqubit-multimode architectures. Couplers with multiple modes have already been proposed and experimentally demonstrated in various contexts
Goto 2022
;
Li et al. 2024
;
Mundada et al. 2019
;
Moskalenko et al. 2021
;
McBroom-Carroll et al. 2024
;
Khoshnegar et al. 2014
. However, they don’t focus on reducing delocalization or independent coupling control. In our TMC design, we address both delocalization and independent control of interactions. The core of our TMC is two tunable modes and tunable mode-mode interaction. With fully tunable mode frequencies and coupling, qubits can be decoupled with full localization. The wavefunctions of qubits are limited to individual qubits and associated coupler modes, which we name localized decoupling. Interactions in different excitation subspaces can be switched on independently. This can be used to implement pure iSWAP operation or pure CPhase operation without interference from one another. It can also be used to implement iSWAP and CPhase simultaneously, as in continuous fSim gates
Foxen et al. 2020
;
Jiang and Ansari 2025
.

V

II
Theory of Interacting Couplers

The circuits considered in this work incorporate more than one coupling mode, and these modes can interact with each other. In particular, the coupling strength between different coupler modes is a tunable physical parameter that can be switched on or off experimentally.

We consider a system of
N
qubits coupled to
M
coupler modes. Depending on the design, the couplers may mediate all-to-all qubit interactions or be localized as lumped elements that provide pairwise qubit coupling. The general Hamiltonian of the system can be written as

\displaystyle H=H_{C}+H_{QC}+H_{Q},

\displaystyle\mathrm{and}\ \ H_{C}=\sum_{i}^{M}H_{c_{i}}+\sum_{i\neq j}^{M}H_{c_{i}c_{j}}(\lambda),

(1)

where
H_{Q}=\sum_{i=1}^{N}H_{q_{i}}
describes the qubits,
H_{C}
the coupler modes, and
H_{QC}=\sum_{i}^{N}\sum_{j=1}^{M}H_{q_{i}c_{j}}
their mutual interaction. The coupler Hamiltonian
H_{C}
is naturally separated into two parts: (i) the diagonal terms
H_{c_{i}}
, corresponding to all individual modes
c_{i}
, and (ii) the off-diagonal mode–mode interaction terms
H_{c_{i}c_{j}}(\lambda)
, representing the coupling between modes
i
and
j
, with
i,j\in\{1,2,\dots,M\}
and
\lambda
denoting a physical knob for tuning the coupling strength between the two modes. In analogy to the standard notation for qubits
Ansari 2019
;
Blais et al. 2021
, diagonal coupling terms describe the couplers on a bare-mode basis, while off-diagonal terms account for the renormalization induced by mode–mode interactions.

The qubit Hamiltonian
H_{Q}
is, in general, non-diagonal: direct qubit–qubit couplings appear as small off-diagonal terms. Although typically weak, these couplings cannot be neglected, as they shift the effective qubit–qubit interaction points
Xu et al. 2024
;
Xu et al. 2025a
. This shift can be compensated or made bigger in the presence of (tunable) couplers.
For clarity, our analytical derivations and schematic diagrams omit explicit direct qubit–qubit couplings. However, our numerical simulations fully include them, demonstrating that the proposed multi-mode coupler architecture can successfully compensate for such residual interactions.

A key feature of our model is the mode–mode coupling, denoted
H_{cc}(\lambda)
. This term plays a central role because we consider couplers whose modes are strongly hybridized and whose mode–mode interactions are tunable. In previously proposed designs, the situation is markedly different: either (i) the modes are weakly coupled, so
H_{cc}\approx 0
is negligible
Goto 2022
;
Li et al. 2024
;
Mundada et al. 2019
;
Moskalenko et al. 2021
;
McBroom-Carroll et al. 2024
, or (ii) the mode–mode coupling
H_{cc}(\lambda)
is a fixed value over a large domain of
\lambda
, thus can be removed by re-framing the Hamiltonian into a diagonal form of the coupler Hamiltonian
McBroom-Carroll et al. 2024
. In contrast, our design combines strong mode hybridization with
tunable
mode–mode coupling, making
H_{cc}
essential. As we will show, this term is also a crucial ingredient for realizing localized decoupling within the qubit network. A schematic qubit setup and the Hamiltonians are shown in FIG.
2
.

Next, we demonstrate how the coupling between qubits can be switched on or off by selectively controlling the mode–mode interactions within the coupler.

II.1
Locally Confined Qubit–Coupler Blocks

The central idea of our qubit–coupler confinement scheme is to achieve
complete decoupling
by partitioning the circuit of
N
qubits and
M
couplers/coupling modes into
N
independent subsystems, each containing a single qubit and one or more coupler modes. This confinement is supposed to suppress all interactions between blocks, not only within the computational subspace but across the entire Hilbert space. Moreover, the decoupling achieved is localized. Qubit wavefunctions are confined within its own block without any leakage to other qubits.

We show that such confinement can be realized by tuning the parameter
\lambda
in the mode–mode coupling
H_{cc}(\lambda)
. To demonstrate this, we first diagonalize the coupler Hamiltonian using a unitary transformation
\mathcal{U}_{c}
acting solely on the coupler degrees of freedom. Since the mode–mode coupling is tunable,
\mathcal{U}_{c}
itself can be externally controlled via
\lambda
, for instance, through tunable flux qubits or superconducting quantum interference devices.

After the transformation, the total Hamiltonian takes the following form:

\tilde{H}=\mathcal{U}^{\dagger}_{c}H\mathcal{U}_{c}

(2)

The operator
\mathcal{U}_{c}
depends on the physical tuning parameters
\lambda
and must act on the Hamiltonian in the
full
Hilbert space, prior to any truncation to the computational subspace. Since the transformation involves no qubit degrees of freedom, Eq. (
2
) yields the transformed total Hamiltonian

\tilde{H}=H_{Q}+\mathcal{U}^{\dagger}_{c}(\lambda)H_{C}\mathcal{U}_{c}(\lambda)+\mathcal{U}^{\dagger}_{c}(\lambda)H_{QC}(\lambda)\mathcal{U}_{c}(\lambda),

which can be written compactly as

\tilde{H}=H_{Q}+\tilde{H}_{C}(\lambda)+\tilde{H}_{QC}(\lambda).

FIG.
2
(b) illustrates this transformation in a matrix representation.

After applying the coupler transformation
\mathcal{U}c(\boldsymbol{\lambda})
, the
M
modes
\{c_{1},c_{2},\ldots,c_{M}\}\equiv\{c_{j}\}_{j=1}^{M}
are grouped into
N
disjoint subgroups
\mathcal{C}_{i}\equiv\{c_{j_{k}^{(i)}}\}_{k=1}^{m_{i}}
(one set per qubit, with
\sum_{i=1}^{N}m_{i}=M
), so that in
\tilde{H}_{QC}
each term couples one qubit only to the coupler modes of its associated subgroup. An illustrative example is shown in FIG.
3
.

(

The interaction Hamiltonian
\tilde{H}_{QC}
can be split into the following form:

\tilde{H}_{QC}(\lambda)=\sum_{i=1}^{N}H_{Q_{i}\mathcal{C}i}(\lambda)

(3)

where
H_{Q_{i}\mathcal{C}i}(\lambda)=\sum_{j_{i}\in\mathcal{C}i}H_{q_{i}c_{j_{i}}}(\lambda)
is constructed from operators of qubit
i
and the coupler modes of the corresponding group
\mathcal{C}_{i}
.

The diagonalized coupling parts of the Hamiltonian in Eq. (
2
), i.e.
\tilde{H}_{C}
, can be grouped accordingly into the modes associated with the subgroups
\mathcal{C}_{i}
so that

\tilde{H}_{C}(\lambda)=\sum_{i=1}^{N}H_{\mathcal{C}_{i}}(\lambda)

(4)

with
H_{\mathcal{C}_{i}}(\lambda)\equiv\sum_{j_{i}\in\mathcal{C}_{i}}H_{c_{j_{i}}}(\lambda)
. Thus, the full Hamiltonian can be rewritten as a sum of
N
commuting Hamiltonians supported on disjoint qubit–coupler groups.

The two decompositions in Eqs. (
3
) and (
4
) help to decompose the full Hamiltonian into the qubit-coupling blocks

\tilde{H}=\sum_{i=1}^{N}H_{i}(\lambda),

(5)

with
H_{i}(\lambda)=H_{q_{i}}+H_{Q_{i}\mathcal{C}i}(\lambda)+H_{\mathcal{C}_{i}}(\lambda)
. For notational simplicity, we have removed the tilde symbol throughout these decompositions. This construction partitions the system into
N
mutually disjoint, non-interacting subspaces, each consisting of a single qubit together with its associated coupler modes. The eigenstates are consequently renormalized by the parameters defined within their respective subgroup elements. In other words, a qubit that is in a subgroup with the coupler modes
c_{1}
and
c_{2}
acquires a renormalized frequency which is determined solely by these two modes; other qubits and modes do not influence this shift. This makes it clear that we are invoking a physical regrouping into genuinely disjoint subunits. The qubit eigenstates are obviously localized in each subspace, thereby achieving localized decoupling. The process is illustrated in FIG.

.

II.2
Overlap Method

In order to evaluate effective couplings between two states (qubit-qubit, qubit-readout, etc.) in the non-perturbative regime, we propose a numerical method based on matching eigenvectors and wavefunction delocalization
Heunisch et al. 2023
. This method is more consistent with our goal to minimize delocalization compared to standard methods such as the least action method
Magesan and Gambetta 2020
.

The common way to calculate effective couplings between two qubits usually employs a transformation to rotate away higher levels and coupler degrees of freedom. An effective Hamiltonian only consisting of the two qubits of interest can be derived from the transformation. The effective couplings can then be read out from the effective Hamiltonian.

The effective Hamiltonian approach, although commonly adopted by the community, has some flaws. One of the issues is that the effective couplings derived from this approach are frame-dependent. The transformation rotates the original Hamiltonian into a different frame and the effective couplings are not between the original two qubits but the two qubits in the dressed basis in the new frame. As one can choose different transformations to derive the effective Hamiltonian, the effective couplings derived from those transformations will be different. There is no consensus on how to choose the transformation or the frame. Importantly, when we consider delocalization, the choice of transformation/frame is crucial.

Localization in circuit QED is defined with respect to a local frame, where flux and charge variables are associated with a single node or branch, rather than superpositions across multiple locations. Delocalization and localization are thus measured in this local frame. However, the transformation used to derive the effective Hamiltonian—and in particular to extract the coupling—may redefine the frame in a nonlocal way, thereby introducing delocalization. As a result, decoupling and delocalization may occur simultaneously, since coupling strength and localization are defined in different frames. One might argue that, within the perturbative regime, different choices of transformation should yield equivalent results up to higher-order corrections. Yet, when targeting very high gate fidelities (99.9%, 99.99%, or beyond), even these small discrepancies can accumulate and become a significant source of error.

To address the ambiguity in choosing the frame, we propose our localization-compatible method for estimating the qubit-qubit coupling at the operational level. Consider a circuit of qubits and couplers in which
N
qubits are coupled via
M
coupling modes. Such a system contains a set of qubit-coupled-mixed eigenstates, which represent
N
dressed eigenstates for the qubits,
\{\ket{\psi_1},\cdots,\ket{\psi_N}\}
. In this case the dressed Hamiltonian is diagonal in this basis and denoted by
\tilde{H}_{D}
with
\tilde{}
denoting the interaction being in place and
D
denoting the diagonal basis, i.e.
\tilde{H}_{D}\ket{\psi_i}=\tilde{E}_{i}\ket{\psi_i}
where
\tilde{E}_{i}
denotes the corresponding energy of
\ket{\psi_i}
.

Moreover, we eliminate all coupling modes from the circuit, qubits are considered non-interacting and the set of non-interacting states are called the qubit bare states
\{\ket{\varphi_1},\cdots,\ket{\varphi_N}\}
.

Once the dressed eigenbasis has been identified, it is important to recognize that information about interaction strengths is encoded not only in the eigenvalues
\{\tilde{E}_{i}\}
, but also in the structure of the associated dressed eigenstates
\{|\psi_{i}\rangle\}
. These eigenstates reflect how bare (localized) states are hybridized through interactions, and hence encode both delocalization patterns and effective coupling strengths.

II.2.1
Validity Domain

To proceed, we aim to define a well-posed one-to-one mapping between the bare states
\{|\varphi_{i}\rangle\}
and dressed states
\{|\psi_{i}\rangle\}
, under the key assumption that hybridization does not significantly mix multiple bare states. In other words, we assume that each dressed state is dominantly supported on a single bare state, such that:

|\langle\varphi_{i}|\psi_{j}\rangle|^{2}\gg|\langle\varphi_{k}|\psi_{j}\rangle|^{2},\quad\forall k\neq i.

(6)

This assumption ensures that the overlaps form a near-permutation matrix. More formally, we define the mapping via:

|\psi_{i}\rangle\equiv\arg\max_{|\psi\rangle}|\langle\varphi_{i}|\psi\rangle|,\quad|\psi\rangle\in\{\text{eigenstates of }\tilde{H}_{D}\},

(7)

with the constraint that this maximum is unique and non-degenerate. That is, we require:

\exists!\ |\psi_{i}\rangle\text{ such that }|\langle\varphi_{i}|\psi_{i}\rangle|^{2}=\max_{|\psi\rangle}|\langle\varphi_{i}|\psi\rangle|^{2}>\epsilon,

(8)

for some threshold
\epsilon\in(0.5,1)
to ensure the dominance of the overlap. This threshold defines the ‘validity domain’ of the method.

If the condition above is not satisfied—i.e., if a dressed state exhibits near-equal support on multiple bare states—the mapping becomes ill-defined. This typically occurs in the strong coupling regime or near accidental degeneracies, where the dressed eigenstates take the form:

|\psi_{i}\rangle\approx\sum_{j=1}^{m}c_{ij}|\varphi_{j}\rangle,\quad\text{with }|c_{ij}|^{2}\approx\frac{1}{m},

(9)

with
m
being the number of significantly mixed bare states. In this case, the system cannot be interpreted as weakly dressed versions of localized qubits, and the overlap method loses interpretability, and the states should be assigned manually.

, which is optimally unitarized to obtain

Therefore, the applicability of this approach is restricted to systems satisfying the following conditions:

1.

Spectral separation: The bare states
\{|\varphi_{i}\rangle\}
must be spectrally well-separated such that mixing due to near-degenerate perturbations is suppressed.

2.

Weak-to-intermediate hybridization: The interaction strength
J
, in general, should obey
J\ll\Delta
, where
\Delta
is the minimum energy gap between target states and their nearest neighbors in the spectrum. The exact threshold, however, depends on the choice of
\epsilon
. When
\epsilon
is chosen close to
0.5
, the condition can be relaxed to
J\sim\Delta
.

3.

Localized dressing: The support of each dressed state on the bare basis should be sharply peaked around one index. That is, the dressed state’s participation ratio
\mathrm{PR}
—defined as inverse purity—should be close to 1:

\text{PR}(|\psi_{i}\rangle)=\left(\sum_{j}|\langle\varphi_{j}|\psi_{i}\rangle|^{4}\right)^{-1}\approx 1.

(10)

II.2.2
Overlap-Based Construction of the Effective Hamiltonian

As discussed above the method begins with the identification of a reduced subspace
\mathcal{H}_{m}=\text{span}\{|\varphi_{1}\rangle,\ldots,|\varphi_{m}\rangle\}
consisting of
m
localized bare states of interest. The full system Hamiltonian
H
is numerically diagonalized, yielding a set of dressed eigenstates
\{|\psi_{i}\rangle\}
and in the previous section we discussed how to associate dressed eigenstates with bare states by a one-to-one mapping, which is physically meaningful under the assumption that coupling is not strong enough to induce widespread hybridization across multiple bare states. When this condition holds, the overlaps
\langle\varphi_{i}|\psi_{j}\rangle
are sharply peaked, and a unique assignment is possible.

Next, we define the reduced diagonalization matrix
U_{r}
by projecting the dressed states
\{|\psi_{i}\rangle\}
onto the bare subspace:

(U_{r})_{ij}=\langle\varphi_{j}|\psi_{i}\rangle,\quad i,j\in\{1,\ldots,m\}.

(11)

Since
U_{r}
is generally not unitary—due to leakage from the subspace or truncation effects—we solve an orthogonal Procrustes problem to find the unitary matrix
U
that optimally approximates
U_{r}
in Frobenius norm
Rebentrost et al. 2018
:

U=\arg\min_{U\in\mathcal{U}(m)}\|U_{r}-U\|_{F}^{2}.

(12)

This has a well-known closed-form solution: letting
U_{r}=W\Sigma V^{\dagger}
be the singular value decomposition of
U_{r}
, the minimizing unitary is

U=WV^{\dagger}.

(13)

The effective Hamiltonian within the reduced subspace is then constructed by rotating the diagonal matrix of dressed energies into the bare basis:

H_{\text{eff}}=U\,\mathrm{diag}(\tilde{E}_{1},\ldots,\tilde{E}_{m})\,U^{\dagger}.

(14)

The off-diagonal elements of
H_{\text{eff}}
yield effective coupling strengths between bare states:

J^{\text{eff}}_{ij}=\langle\varphi_{j}|H_{\text{eff}}|\varphi_{i}\rangle.

(15)

II.2.3
Block-wise Application and Spatial Locality

The method naturally generalizes to large systems by applying it block-wise over localized regions. For example, in circuit QED architectures with modular qubit-coupler units, the full Hamiltonian can be approximately decomposed into quasi-independent blocks:

H_{\text{full}}\approx\bigoplus_{\text{blocks}}H_{\text{eff}}^{(\text{block})},

(16)

where each
H_{\text{eff}}^{(\text{block})}
is constructed independently via the overlap method in a local subspace. This modular approach significantly reduces computational complexity and respects the underlying connectivity of the hardware.

The resulting effective Hamiltonian satisfies several key properties:

1.

Spectral Consistency: The eigenvalue differences within the effective Hamiltonian preserve those from the full system:

\tilde{E}_{i}-\tilde{E}_{j}=E_{i}^{\text{eff}}-E_{j}^{\text{eff}}.

2.

Minimal Basis Distortion: The transformation
U
is the closest unitary to the bare-dressed rotation matrix
U_{r}
, minimizing frame mismatch and preserving localization structure.

3.

Delocalization Sensitivity: In the case where each dressed state is fully localized (i.e.,
|\psi_{i}\rangle=|\varphi_{i}\rangle
), the overlap matrix becomes identity, and the resulting coupling vanishes:
J^{\text{eff}}_{ij}=0
for
i\neq j
.

II.3
Modifying Qubit-Qubit Coupling via Mode-Mode Interaction
\lambda

We now examine whether the same tunable parameter
\lambda
, previously used to decouple idle qubits, can be repurposed to enhance qubit-qubit interactions during gate operation.

Consider a system composed of multiple qubits and associated coupler modes. Initially, the Hamiltonian factorizes into decoupled components:

H=\sum_{i}H_{i},\quad[H_{i},H_{j}]=0,

(17)

where
H_{i}
includes the Hamiltonian of qubit
Q_{i}
and its local coupler modes.

To activate coupling between two qubits (e.g.,
Q_{1}
and
Q_{2}
), we introduce mode-mode interactions between their respective coupler modes:

H=H_{12}+\sum_{k\geq 3}H_{k},

(18)

with

H_{12}=H_{q_{1}}+H_{q_{2}}+\sum_{i,j\in\mathcal{C}_{1}\cup\mathcal{C}_{2}}H_{c_{i}c_{j}}(\lambda),

(19)

where
\mathcal{C}_{1}
and
\mathcal{C}_{2}
denote the sets of coupler modes attached to
Q_{1}
and
Q_{2}
, respectively, and
H_{c_{k}c_{l}}(\lambda)
encodes the mode-mode interactions tunable via
\lambda
.
This configuration forms a local interacting block comprising
Q_{1}
,
Q_{2}
, and their associated coupler modes. The rest of the system remains dynamically decoupled.

Expanding
H_{12}
, we separate direct and indirect interaction terms:

\begin{split}H_{12}=&\sum_{i=1}^{2}\left(H_{q_{i}}+H_{Q_{i},\mathcal{C}_{i}}\right)\\
&+\sum_{i,j\in\mathcal{C}_{1}\cup\mathcal{C}_{2}}H_{c_{i}c_{j}}(\lambda),\end{split}

(20)

where
H_{Q_{i},\mathcal{C}_{i}}
is the coupling between
Q_{i}
and its local coupler modes
\mathcal{C}_{i}
.

We now diagonalize the coupled-mode Hamiltonian using a unitary transformation
\mathcal{U}_{c}
such that:

\mathcal{U}_{c}^{\dagger}(\lambda)\left(\sum_{i,j}H_{c_{i}c_{j}}(\lambda)\right)\mathcal{U}_{c}(\lambda)=\sum_{k}H_{c_{k}}^{\text{diag}}.

In this new frame, the coupler modes are de-hybridized, therefore when the transformation is applied on the mode-qubit interaction, it results in modified interaction strength with the orthonormal modes:

H_{12}=\sum_{i=1}^{2}H_{q_{i}}+\sum_{k}\left(H_{q_{1}c_{k}}(\lambda)+H_{q_{2}c_{k}}(\lambda)+H_{c_{k}}^{\text{diag}}\right),

(21)

where
k
runs over the diagonalized hybrid modes.

This frame reveals that both qubits are coupled to shared coupler modes, enabling indirect qubit-qubit interaction. The strength of this interaction depends sensitively on
\lambda
through the structure of the hybrid modes and their coupling amplitudes.

Estimate via Schrieffer–Wolff Transformation

To quantify this enhancement, consider a simplified model where
Q_{1}
and
Q_{2}
couple to
m
identical harmonic modes with strength
g_{0}
. In the diagonal frame, the Hamiltonian reads
Blais et al. 2021
:

\begin{split}H_{12}&=-\frac{\omega_{q1}}{2}\sigma_{1}^{z}-\frac{\omega_{q2}}{2}\sigma_{2}^{z}+\sum_{k=1}^{m}\omega_{k}c_{k}^{\dagger}c_{k}\\
&\quad+g_{0}\sum_{k=1}^{m}\left(\sigma_{1}^{x}+\sigma_{2}^{x}\right)\otimes(c_{k}+c_{k}^{\dagger}),\end{split}

(22)

where
\sigma^{x}=\sigma^{+}+\sigma^{-}
denotes transverse coupling.

Applying a second-order Schrieffer–Wolff transformation to eliminate the coupler degrees of freedom yields the effective two-qubit Hamiltonian:

H_{\text{eff}}=-\frac{\omega_{q1}}{2}\sigma_{1}^{z}-\frac{\omega_{q2}}{2}\sigma_{2}^{z}+J\left(\sigma_{1}^{+}\sigma_{2}^{-}+\sigma_{1}^{-}\sigma_{2}^{+}\right),

(23)

with the exchange coupling:

J=\frac{g_{0}^{2}}{2}\sum_{k=1}^{m}\left(\frac{1}{\omega_{q1}-\omega_{k}}+\frac{1}{\omega_{q2}-\omega_{k}}\right).

(24)

Assuming similar detunings
\omega_{q1}\approx\omega_{q2}
and approximately degenerate coupler modes
\omega_{k}\approx\omega_{c}
, this simplifies to:

J\approx m\cdot\frac{g_{0}^{2}}{\Delta},\quad\Delta=\omega_{q}-\omega_{c}.

Thus, the effective coupling strength scales linearly with the number of hybrid modes
m
, showing that interaction strength can be amplified by mode sharing—a mechanism tunable via
\lambda
.

This analysis highlights a dual utility of the parameter
\lambda
: it can be tuned to minimize residual couplings during idle, and also to boost inter-qubit coupling on demand during gate operation by activating constructive mode-mediated interactions.

III
Full Localization: Ideal Decoupling

We demonstrate how to achieve complete decoupling between two transmons by controlling the interaction strength between a two-mode coupler and following the general recipe outlined in Sec.
II
. A circuit-level derivation starting from the Lagrangian is sketched in Appendix
A
.

We consider a circuit with two qubits
Q_{a}
and
Q_{b}
, where they are coupled to a two-mode coupler with modes
c_{1}
and
c_{2}
. The coupler Hamiltonian part of the system
H_{C}=\sum_{i=1,2}\omega_{i}c_{i}^{\dagger}c_{i}-\lambda(c_{1}-c_{1}^{\dagger})(c_{2}-c_{2}^{\dagger})
can be fully diagonalized (after applying the rotating wave approximation eliminating doubly excited/de-excited states) via a Bogoliubov transformation
Blais et al. 2021
;
Boissonneault et al. 2008
;
Bocko et al. 1997
:

U_{C}=e^{\Lambda(c_{1}^{\dagger}c_{2}-c_{1}c_{2}^{\dagger})}.

(25)

This transforms the mode operators as:

\begin{split}U_{C}^{\dagger}c_{1}U_{C}&=\cos\Lambda\,c_{1}+\sin\Lambda\,c_{2},\\
U_{C}^{\dagger}c_{2}U_{C}&=\cos\Lambda\,c_{2}-\sin\Lambda\,c_{1}.\end{split}

(26)

Choosing the mixing angle
\Lambda=\frac{1}{2}\arctan(2\lambda/\Delta_{12})
, with
\Delta_{12}=\omega_{2}-\omega_{1}
, diagonalizes the coupler Hamiltonian:

U_{C}^{\dagger}H_{C}U_{C}=\tilde{\omega}_{1}c_{1}^{\dagger}c_{1}+\tilde{\omega}_{2}c_{2}^{\dagger}c_{2},

(27)

with

\displaystyle\tilde{\omega}_{1,2}=\frac{1}{2}\left(\omega_{1}+\omega_{2}\mp\sqrt{\Delta_{12}^{2}+4\lambda^{2}}\right).

(28)

Under this transformation, the qubit-coupler interaction Hamiltonian
\sum_{i=a,b}\sum_{k=1,2}g_{ik}\left(q_{i}^{\dagger}c_{k}+H.c.\right)
becomes:

\begin{split}\tilde{H}_{\text{int}}=U_{C}^{\dagger}H_{\text{int}}U_{C}&=\sum_{i=1}^{2}\sum_{k=1}^{2}\tilde{g}_{ik}\left(q_{i}^{\dagger}c_{k}+q_{i}c_{k}^{\dagger}\right),\end{split}

(29)

where
q_{1}=a
,
q_{2}=b
are the annihilation operators of the two transmons, and the transformed couplings
\tilde{g}_{ij}
are given by:

\begin{pmatrix}\tilde{g}_{a1}\\
\tilde{g}_{a2}\end{pmatrix}=R(\Lambda)\begin{pmatrix}g_{a1}\\
g_{a2}\end{pmatrix},\quad\begin{pmatrix}\tilde{g}_{b1}\\
\tilde{g}_{b2}\end{pmatrix}=R(\Lambda)\begin{pmatrix}g_{b1}\\
g_{b2}\end{pmatrix},

(30)

with the rotation matrix:

R(\Lambda)=\begin{pmatrix}\cos\Lambda&-\sin\Lambda\\
\sin\Lambda&\cos\Lambda\end{pmatrix}.

(31)

III.1
Condition for Full Decoupling

To fully decouple the two qubits from one another, we need to impose a condition on the two coupling mode interaction strength. This requires the following condition:

\tilde{g}_{a2}=0,\quad\text{and}\quad\tilde{g}_{b1}=0.

This leads to the condition:

\tan\Lambda=-\frac{g_{a2}}{g_{a1}},\quad\text{and}\quad\tan\Lambda=\frac{g_{b1}}{g_{b2}}.

(32)

Using
\Lambda=\frac{1}{2}\arctan(2\lambda/\Delta_{12})
, and assuming symmetric coupling ratios
g_{a2}=-\alpha g_{a1}
and
g_{b1}=\alpha g_{b2}
, the condition simplifies to:

\frac{\lambda}{\Delta_{12}}=\frac{\alpha}{1-\alpha^{2}}.

(33)

III.2
Resulting Decoupled Hamiltonian

With this condition, the interaction Hamiltonian reduces to:

\tilde{H}_{\text{int}}=\frac{1}{\cos\Lambda}\left[g_{a1}(a^{\dagger}c_{1}+ac_{1}^{\dagger})+g_{b2}(b^{\dagger}c_{2}+bc_{2}^{\dagger})\right],

(34)

and the full system separates into two uncoupled subsystems:

\begin{split}\tilde{H}=U_{C}^{\dagger}HU_{C}&=\omega_{a}a^{\dagger}a+\frac{\delta_{a}}{2}(a^{\dagger}a-1)a^{\dagger}a+\tilde{\omega}_{1}c_{1}^{\dagger}c_{1}+\frac{g_{a1}}{\cos\Lambda}(a^{\dagger}c_{1}+ac_{1}^{\dagger})\\
&+\omega_{b}b^{\dagger}b+\frac{\delta_{b}}{2}(b^{\dagger}b-1)b^{\dagger}b+\tilde{\omega}_{2}c_{2}^{\dagger}c_{2}+\frac{g_{b2}}{\cos\Lambda}(b^{\dagger}c_{2}+bc_{2}^{\dagger}).\end{split}

(35)

Each transmon couples exclusively to a single hybridized mode. The absence of any shared coupling path guarantees strict decoupling between the two qubits, realizing ideal localization in the diagonalized frame.

IV
Selective Control of Effective Coupling in Excitation Manifolds and two-qubit gates

A key advantage of two-mode couplers is their ability to generate distinct effective
J

Of particular relevance is the ability to engineer configurations where the coupling in the one-excitation manifold is strong (e.g., for gate operations), while the coupling in the two-excitation manifold is suppressed, or vice versa. In this section, we show analytical derivations and numerical simulations to demonstrate the selective control of couplings.

The coupler mode hybridization will mediate qubit interactions corresponding to the associated coupler modes.

VI
Summary

We present a systematic theoretical framework for the design and control of multi-mode couplers in superconducting quantum processors. In contrast to conventional single-mode couplers, our approach enables selective engineering of interactions across different excitation manifolds, thereby offering new routes to suppress leakage and unwanted unitary errors during gate operations. As a concrete demonstration, we analyze in detail a two-transmon system coupled through a two-mode tunable coupler. Numerical simulations show that this architecture can realize a high-fidelity iSWAP gate by exploiting independent control of one- and two-excitation subspaces. Furthermore, we provide explicit examples of lumped-element circuit designs that illustrate the practical feasibility of our proposal. Beyond demonstrating a specific gate, our results highlight how multi-mode couplers can combine flexibility, strong tunability, and complete qubit localization at the decoupled point. We believe this work lays the groundwork for the next generation of high-performance couplers in scalable quantum processing units.

Appendix A
Derivation of decoupling with the two-mode coupler using the Lagrangian method

Let’s assume the system Lagrangian reads:

\begin{split}&L=L_{T}+L_{C}+L_{int}\\
&L_{C}=\frac{1}{2}\dot{\vec{\phi}}^{T}_{c}C\dot{\vec{\phi}}_{c}-\frac{1}{2}\vec{\phi}^{T}_{c}\frac{1}{L}\vec{\phi}_{c}\\
&L_{int}=\begin{pmatrix}\dot{\phi}_{a}&\dot{\phi}_{b}\end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix}C_{a1}&C_{a2}\\
C_{b1}&C_{b2}\\
\end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix}\dot{\phi}_{1}\\
\dot{\phi}_{2}\end{pmatrix}\end{split}

(38)

The transmon Lagrangian is not explicitly written out because it does not contain any coupler degree of freedom. The two coupler modes are labeled as
\phi_{1}
and
\phi_{2}
and the transmon modes are
\phi_{a}
and
\phi_{b}
. We have assumed that the transmons are capacitively coupled to the coupler.

We start by finding the normal modes of the coupler via a transformation
\eta=A\phi_{c}
. This transformation can be found using different methods. We present one of them. We first introduce an intermediate transformation
x=\sqrt{C}\phi_{c}
. Under this transformation, the coupler Lagrangian becomes:

\displaystyle L_{C}=\frac{1}{2}\dot{\vec{x}}^{T}\dot{\vec{x}}-\frac{1}{2}\vec{x}^{T}C^{-1/2}\frac{1}{L}C^{-1/2}\vec{x}

(39)

The normal modes are found by diagonalizing the matrix
C^{-1/2}\frac{1}{L}C^{-1/2}
with another transformation
\eta=Dx
:
C^{-1/2}\frac{1}{L}C^{-1/2}\eta=\omega^{2}\eta
. After the transformations, the coupler Lagrangian contains two uncoupled modes:

\displaystyle L_{C}=\frac{1}{2}\dot{\vec{\eta}}^{T}\dot{\vec{\eta}}-\frac{1}{2}\vec{\eta}^{T}\begin{pmatrix}\omega_{1}^{2}&0\\
0&\omega_{2}^{2}\\
\end{pmatrix}\vec{\eta}

(40)

The total transformation is:

\eta=A\phi

(41)

where
A=D\sqrt{C}
.

The interaction Lagrangian is also transformed:

L_{int}=\begin{pmatrix}\dot{\phi}_{a}&\dot{\phi}_{b}\end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix}\tilde{C}_{a1}&\tilde{C}_{a2}\\
\tilde{C}_{b1}&\tilde{C}_{b2}\\
\end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix}\dot{\eta}_{1}\\
\dot{\eta}_{2}\end{pmatrix}

(42)

The new coupling capacitance is related to the old capacitance as:

\begin{pmatrix}\tilde{C}_{a1}&\tilde{C}_{a2}\\
\tilde{C}_{b1}&\tilde{C}_{b2}\\
\end{pmatrix}=\begin{pmatrix}C_{a1}&C_{a2}\\
C_{b1}&C_{b2}\\
\end{pmatrix}A^{-1}

(43)

We have shown that the coupling capacitance seen by the transmons is a function of the coupler circuit parameters (
C
and
L
). By tuning the coupler parameters, the transformation matrix
A
is changed. We therefore are effectively tuning the coupling between the transmons and the coupler modes. In reality, it is more natural to assume fixed capacitance and tunable inductance. We thus can control transmon-coupler coupling by tuning the inductance in the coupler. Decoupling can be achieved when:

\tilde{C}_{a1}=\tilde{C}_{b2}=0\text{ or }\tilde{C}_{a2}=\tilde{C}_{b1}=0

(44)

In either case, the whole system is split into two subsystems that each contains one transmon and one coupler mode and there is no interaction between the two subsystems.

Appendix B
Applying the overlap J method to the two-mode coupler

We apply our overlap method to calculate the effective coupling
J
between
|01\rangle
and
|10\rangle
.
We assume that the full Hamiltonian
H
can be numerically diagonalized and

|01\rangle
and
|10\rangle
are well separated from other states so that they can be associated with new eigenstates
|\alpha\rangle
and
|\beta\rangle
respectively. The corresponding eigenvalues are
\omega_{\alpha}
and
\omega_{\beta}
.
|\alpha\rangle
and
|\beta\rangle
can be numerically found by:

\begin{split}&|\alpha\rangle=\arg\max|\langle 01|\psi\rangle|,|\psi\rangle\in\{\text{eigenvectors of }H\}\\
&|\beta\rangle=\arg\max|\langle 10|\psi\rangle|,|\psi\rangle\in\{\text{eigenvectors of }H\}\end{split}

(45)

We then project eigenstates
|\alpha\rangle
and
|\beta\rangle
onto the subspace spanned by
|a\rangle
and
|b\rangle
to get a reduced diagonalization matrix
U_{r}
:

U_{r}=\begin{pmatrix}\langle 01|\alpha\rangle&\langle 01|\beta\rangle\\
\langle 10|\alpha\rangle&\langle 10|\beta\rangle\\
\end{pmatrix}

(46)

The effective coupling strength
J
is found by requiring it to produce the closest diagonalization matrix to
U_{r}
. To proceed, we write down the general 2 by 2 unitary transformation
U
:

\begin{split}U=&e^{i(r_{x}\sigma_{x}+r_{y}\sigma_{y}+r_{z}\sigma_{z})}\\
=&e^{i\theta(n_{x}\sigma_{x}+n_{y}\sigma_{y}+n_{z}\sigma_{z})}\\
=&\cos(\theta)+i\sin(\theta)(n_{x}\sigma_{x}+n_{y}\sigma_{y}+n_{z}\sigma_{z})\\
=&\begin{pmatrix}\cos(\theta)+i\sin(\theta)n_{z}&\sin(\theta)(in_{x}+n_{y})\\
\sin(\theta)(in_{x}-n_{y})&\cos(\theta)-i\sin(\theta)n_{z}\\
\end{pmatrix}\end{split}

(47)

As usual, we have defined:
\theta=\sqrt{r_{x}^{2}+r_{y}^{2}+r_{z}^{2}},n_{x}=\frac{r_{x}}{\theta},n_{y}=\frac{r_{y}}{\theta}
and
n_{z}=\frac{r_{z}}{\theta}
.
r_{x},r_{y}
and
r_{z}
are determined by minimizing the distance between the reduced diagonalization matrix
U_{r}
and
U
:

r_{x},r_{y},r_{z}=\arg\min|U_{r}-U|

(48)

The effective Hamiltonian
H_{eff}
can be calculated from
U,\omega_{\alpha}
and
\omega_{\beta}
as:

\begin{split}H_{\mathrm{eff}}=&U(-\frac{\omega_{\alpha}-\omega_{\beta}}{2}\sigma_{z})U^{\dagger}\\
=&-\frac{\omega_{\alpha}-\omega_{\beta}}{2}U\sigma_{z}U^{\dagger}\\
=&-\frac{\omega_{\alpha}-\omega_{\beta}}{2}[(\cos^{2}(\theta)+(-n_{x}^{2}-n_{y}^{2}+n_{z}^{2})\sin^{2}(\theta))\sigma_{z}+n_{x}(\sin(2\theta)+2\sin^{2}(\theta)n_{z})\sigma_{x}\\
&+n_{y}(-\sin(2\theta)+2\sin^{2}(\theta)n_{z})\sigma_{y}]\end{split}

(49)

J^{\mathrm{eff}}
between
|01\rangle
and
|10\rangle
can now be read from the effective Hamiltonian:

\begin{split}J^{\mathrm{eff}}=&-\frac{\omega_{\alpha}-\omega_{\beta}}{2}(n_{x}(\sin 2\theta+2n_{z}\sin^{2}\theta)\\
&\pm in_{y}(-\sin 2\theta+2n_{z}\sin^{2}\theta))\end{split}

(50)

Assuming
n_{y}=n_{z}=0
and
\theta\rightarrow 0
, we can get the perturbative expansion:

\displaystyle J^{\mathrm{eff}}=-(\omega_{\alpha}-\omega_{\beta})\sin(\theta)

(51)

Eq. (
51
) is used in numerical simulation for the off-mode.

Appendix C
Coupler circuit designs

In this section, we present analytical derivations for the two circuit designs shown in FIG.
8
.

C.1
The left-handed design

After neglecting the coupling capacitances to the qubits (readouts), the Lagrangian of the circuit shown in FIG.
8
(a) can be found as:

\begin{split}L=&\frac{(c_{1}+c_{3}+4c_{2})}{2}\dot{\varphi}^{2}_{+}+\frac{(c_{1}/4+c_{3}/4)}{2}\dot{\varphi}_{-}^{2}+(c_{1}/2-c_{3}/2)\dot{\varphi}_{+}\dot{\varphi}_{-}\\
&-\frac{(l_{3}/\Sigma+l_{1}/\Sigma+4l_{2}/\Sigma)}{2}\varphi_{+}^{2}-\frac{(l_{3}/\Sigma+l_{1}/\Sigma)}{2}\varphi_{-}^{2}-\frac{(l_{3}/\Sigma-l_{1}/\Sigma)}{2}\varphi_{+}\varphi_{-}\end{split}

(52)

where we have defined:

\begin{split}\varphi_{+}&=\frac{\varphi_{1}+\varphi_{2}}{2},\varphi_{-}=\varphi_{1}-\varphi_{2}\\
\Sigma&=l_{1}l_{2}+l_{1}l_{3}+l_{2}l_{3}\end{split}

(53)

For simplicity, we require
c_{1}=c_{3}=c
and define
l=(l_{1}+l_{3})/2
,
\Delta l=(l_{1}-l_{3})/2

The resulting Lagrangian is

L=\frac{c_{+}}{2}\dot{\varphi}_{+}^{2}+\frac{c_{-}}{2}\dot{\varphi}_{-}^{2}-\frac{1}{2l_{+}}\varphi_{+}^{2}-\frac{1}{2l_{-}}\varphi_{-}^{2}+\frac{\Delta l}{\Sigma}\varphi_{+}\varphi_{-}

(54)

with

c_{+}=2c+4c_{2}\text{ },\text{ }c_{-}=c/2\text{ },\text{ }l_{+}=l/2\text{ },\text{ }l_{-}=2l+4l_{2}

(55)

Defining the canonical variable
q_{k}
:

\begin{split}q_{k}&=\frac{\partial L}{\partial\dot{\varphi}_{k}}\\
&=c_{k}\dot{\varphi}_{k}\end{split}

(56)

The Hamiltonian reads:

\begin{split}H=&\Sigma q_{k}\varphi_{k}-L\\
=&\frac{q_{+}^{2}}{2c_{+}}+\frac{q_{-}^{2}}{2c_{-}}+\frac{\varphi_{+}^{2}}{2l_{+}}+\frac{\varphi_{-}^{2}}{2l_{-}}-\frac{\Delta l}{\Sigma}\varphi_{+}\varphi_{-}\end{split}

(57)

From standard circuit QED we know:

\begin{split}q_{k}=&i\sqrt{\frac{\hbar}{2}\sqrt{\frac{c_{k}}{l_{k}}}}(a_{k}^{\dagger}-a_{k})\\
\varphi_{k}=&\sqrt{\frac{\hbar}{2}\sqrt{\frac{l_{k}}{c_{k}}}}(a_{k}^{\dagger}+a_{k})\end{split}

(58)

Using
\omega_{k}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{l_{k}c_{k}}}
, the frequencies can be calculated:

\omega_{+}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{l(c+2c_{2})}}\text{ , }\omega_{-}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{(l+2l_{2})c}}

(59)

The circuit Hamiltonian is

\begin{split}H/\hbar=&\omega_{+}a^{\dagger}_{+}a_{+}+\omega_{-}a^{\dagger}_{-}a_{-}\\
&-\frac{\Delta l}{2\Sigma}(\frac{l_{+}l_{-}}{c_{+}c_{-}})^{1/4}(a_{+}^{\dagger}+a_{+})(a^{\dagger}_{-}+a_{-})\end{split}

(60)

Defining
g_{12}=-\frac{\Delta l}{2\Sigma}(\frac{l_{+}l_{-}}{c_{+}c_{-}})^{1/4}
and using the rotating-wave approximation we get:

H/\hbar=\omega_{+}a^{\dagger}_{+}a_{+}+\omega_{-}a^{\dagger}_{-}a_{-}+g_{12}(a_{+}^{\dagger}a_{-}+a_{+}a^{\dagger}_{-})

(61)

We therefore have:

\begin{split}\frac{g_{12}}{\Delta_{12}}=&\frac{g_{12}}{\omega_{-}-\omega_{+}}\\
=&\frac{\Delta l}{2\Sigma}\frac{(l_{+}l_{-})^{3/4}(c_{+}c_{-})^{1/4}}{\sqrt{l_{-}c_{-}}-\sqrt{l_{+}c_{+}}}\end{split}

(62)

To calculate the right-hand-side of Eq. (
33
), we define a parameter
\beta
:

\beta=\frac{g_{a-}g_{b-}}{g_{a+}g_{b+}}

(63)

where
g_{a+},g_{a-}.g_{b+},g_{b-}
are couplings between qubit
a
and mode +, qubit
a
and mode -, qubit
b
and mode + and qubit
b
and mode - respectively. The condition
-g_{a-}/g_{a+}=g_{b+}/g_{b-}
is translated to
\beta=-1
. To calculate the parameter
\beta
, we need to include capacitances between qubits
a,b
and the coupler
c_{a}
and
c_{b}
. This gives two new coupling terms in the Lagrangian:

c_{a}(\dot{\varphi}_{0}-\dot{\varphi}_{a})^{2}\text{ and }c_{b}(\dot{\varphi}_{0}+\dot{\varphi}_{1}+\dot{\varphi}_{2}-\dot{\varphi}_{b})^{2}

(64)

\varphi_{0}
is not an independent variable. It can be written as:

\varphi_{0}=-\frac{l_{1}l_{2}+l_{1}l_{3}}{\Sigma}\varphi_{1}-\frac{l_{1}l_{2}}{\Sigma}\varphi_{2}

(65)

In the +/- basis, we have:

\begin{split}&\varphi_{0}=-\frac{2l_{1}l_{2}+l_{1}l_{3}}{\Sigma}\varphi_{+}-\frac{l_{1}l_{3}}{2\Sigma}\varphi_{-}\\
&\varphi_{0}+\varphi_{1}+\varphi_{2}=\frac{l_{1}l_{3}+2l_{2}l_{3}}{\Sigma}\varphi_{+}-\frac{l_{1}l_{3}}{2\Sigma}\varphi_{-}\end{split}

(66)

For our parameters, it is

\begin{split}\varphi_{0}=&-(1+\frac{2l_{2}\Delta l}{2ll_{2}+l^{2}-\Delta l^{2}})\varphi_{+}-\frac{l^{2}-\Delta l^{2}}{2(2ll_{2}+l^{2}-\Delta l^{2})}\varphi_{-}\\
\varphi_{0}+\varphi_{1}+\varphi_{2}=&(1-\frac{2l_{2}\Delta l}{2ll_{2}+l^{2}-\Delta l^{2}})\varphi_{+}-\frac{l^{2}-\Delta l^{2}}{2(2ll_{2}+l^{2}-\Delta l^{2})}\varphi_{-}\end{split}

(67)

Now we deal with the coupling term
c_{a}(\dot{\varphi}_{0}-\dot{\varphi}_{a})^{2}
.

Expanding it, we get three terms:

c_{a}(\dot{\varphi}_{0}-\dot{\varphi}_{a})^{2}=c_{a}\dot{\varphi}_{0}^{2}-2c_{a}\dot{\varphi}_{0}\dot{\varphi}_{a}+c_{a}\dot{\varphi}_{a}^{2}

(68)

The last term gives a correction to the qubit/readout energy. The middle term gives the coupling between the coupler mode and qubit/readout. The first term is a bit problematic. It generates a new mode in the coupler because it introduces
\dot{\varphi}_{0}
into the Lagrangian, which was absent before. We made use of the absence of
\dot{\varphi}_{0}
to eliminate
\varphi_{0}
and get Eq.
65
. So when adding this coupling term, the circuit holds three modes instead of two. There are three ways of dealing with it:
1. Studying the three-mode circuit.
2. Change circuit design so there are still only two modes after introducing coupling.
3. Tuning the parameters in a way the new mode’s contribution can be minimized.

We will discuss solution 2 in the next section. Here we assume solution 3 is possible. This can be done by pushing the mode to a high frequency such that the mode is effectively frozen.

Therefore we only keep the middle term for our analysis:

\begin{split}2c_{a}\dot{\varphi}_{0}\dot{\varphi}_{a}&=2c_{a}(-(1+\frac{2l_{2}\Delta l}{2ll_{2}+l^{2}-\Delta l^{2}})\dot{\varphi}_{+}-\frac{l^{2}-\Delta l^{2}}{2(2ll_{2}+l^{2}-\Delta l^{2})}\dot{\varphi}_{-})\dot{\varphi}_{a}\\
&=-2c_{a}(1+\frac{2l_{2}\Delta l}{2ll_{2}+l^{2}-\Delta l^{2}})\dot{\varphi}_{+}\dot{\varphi}_{a}-c_{a}\frac{l^{2}-\Delta l^{2}}{(2ll_{2}+l^{2}-\Delta l^{2})}\dot{\varphi}_{-}\dot{\varphi}_{a}\end{split}

(69)

For any mode
k
, using Eq.(
56
) and Eq.(
58
), we have:

\begin{split}\dot{\varphi}_{k}&=\frac{n_{k}}{c_{k}}\\
&=i\sqrt{\frac{\omega_{k}}{2c_{k}}}(a_{k}^{\dagger}-a_{k})\end{split}

(70)

Now we can use it to calculate the couplings:

\begin{split}&2c_{a}\dot{\varphi}_{0}\dot{\varphi}_{a}=c_{a}(1+\frac{2l_{2}\Delta l}{2ll_{2}+l^{2}-\Delta l^{2}})\sqrt{\frac{\omega_{+}\omega_{a}}{c_{+}c_{qa}}}(a_{+}^{\dagger}-a_{+})(a_{a}^{\dagger}-a_{a})\\
&+c_{a}\frac{l^{2}-\Delta l^{2}}{2(2ll_{2}+l^{2}-\Delta l^{2})}\sqrt{\frac{\omega_{-}\omega_{a}}{c_{-}c_{qa}}}(a_{-}^{\dagger}-a_{-})(a_{a}^{\dagger}-a_{a})\end{split}

(71)

Note that
c_{qa}
is not the coupling capacitance
c_{a}
but the capacitance of qubit
a
.

We can then write down the equations for the
g
couplings:

\begin{split}g_{a+}&=c_{a}(1+\frac{2l_{2}\Delta l}{2ll_{2}+l^{2}-\Delta l^{2}})\sqrt{\frac{\omega_{+}\omega_{a}}{c_{+}c_{qa}}}\\
g_{a-}&=c_{a}\frac{l^{2}-\Delta l^{2}}{2(2ll_{2}+l^{2}-\Delta l^{2})}\sqrt{\frac{\omega_{-}\omega_{a}}{c_{-}c_{qa}}}\end{split}

(72)

Similarly we can derive the couplings between qubit
b
and the coupler:

\begin{split}g_{b+}&=-c_{b}(1-\frac{2l_{2}\Delta l}{2ll_{2}+l^{2}-\Delta l^{2}})\sqrt{\frac{\omega_{+}\omega_{b}}{c_{+}c_{b}}}\\
g_{b-}&=c_{b}\frac{l^{2}-\Delta l^{2}}{2(2ll_{2}+l^{2}-\Delta l^{2})}\sqrt{\frac{\omega_{-}\omega_{b}}{c_{-}c_{b}}}\end{split}

(73)

we can evaluate
\beta
:

\begin{split}\beta=&\frac{g_{a-}g_{b-}}{g_{a+}g_{b+}}\\
=&-\frac{(l^{2}-\Delta l^{2})c_{+}}{4(l+2l_{2}+\Delta l)(l+2l_{2}-\Delta l)c_{-}}\end{split}

(74)

Using Eq.
55
we get:

\beta=-\frac{l^{2}-\Delta l^{2}}{(l+2l_{2}+\Delta l)(l+2l_{2}-\Delta l)}\frac{c+2c_{2}}{c}

(75)

Recall that in Eq.(
32
),
\alpha=\frac{g_{b+}}{g_{b-}}
. Therefore,

\alpha=-\frac{l+\Delta l+2l_{2}}{l+\Delta l}\sqrt{\frac{\omega_{+}c}{\omega_{-}(c+2c_{2})}}

(76)

Requiring
\beta=1
we get:

l_{2}=\sqrt{kl^{2}-(k-1)\Delta l^{2}}-l

(77)

with
k=\frac{c+2c_{2}}{c}

We solve Eq.(
33
) by doing a 2D scan of
c_{2}
and
l
. We use parameters:

\begin{split}c_{1}=c_{3}=&30\text{ fF, and }l=30\text{ nH}\end{split}

(78)

The result is shown in FIG.
9
.

C.2
The right-handed design

The Lagrangian of the circuit in FIG.
8
(b) can be written as:

\begin{split}L=&\frac{c_{1}}{2}\dot{\varphi}_{0}^{2}+\frac{c_{2}}{2}(\dot{\varphi}_{0}+\dot{\varphi}_{1})^{2}+\frac{c_{3}}{2}(\dot{\varphi}_{0}+\dot{\varphi}_{1}+\dot{\varphi}_{2})^{2}\\
&-\frac{\varphi_{1}^{2}}{2l_{1}}-\frac{\varphi_{2}^{2}}{2l_{3}}-\frac{(\varphi_{1}+\varphi_{2})^{2}}{2l_{2}}\end{split}

(79)

where without loss of generality we have assumed
\varphi_{ext}=0
.

Note that
\varphi_{0}
doesn’t appear in the potential energy. We have therefore:

\frac{d}{dt}\frac{\partial L}{\partial\dot{\varphi}_{0}}=0

(80)

\dot{\varphi}_{0}
can then be eliminated:

\dot{\varphi}_{0}=\frac{C}{\Sigma}-\frac{c_{2}+c_{3}}{\Sigma}\dot{\varphi}_{1}-\frac{c_{3}}{\Sigma}\dot{\varphi}_{2}

(81)

with
\Sigma=c_{1}+c_{2}+c_{3}
and
C
being an arbitrary constant.

Using Eq.
81
we can simplify the Lagrangian up to a constant:

\begin{split}L=&\frac{1}{2}\frac{4c_{1}c_{3}+c_{1}c_{2}+c_{2}c_{3}}{\Sigma}\dot{\varphi}_{+}^{2}+\frac{1}{2}\frac{c_{1}c_{2}+c_{2}c_{3}}{4\Sigma}\dot{\varphi}_{-}^{2}+\frac{1}{2}\frac{c_{1}c_{2}-c_{2}c_{3}}{\Sigma}\dot{\varphi}_{+}\dot{\varphi}_{-}\\
&-\frac{1}{2}(\frac{1}{l_{1}}+\frac{1}{l_{3}}+\frac{4}{l_{2}})\varphi_{+}^{2}-\frac{1}{2}(\frac{1}{4l_{1}}+\frac{1}{4l_{3}})\varphi_{-}^{2}-\frac{1}{2}(\frac{1}{l_{1}}-\frac{1}{l_{3}})\varphi_{+}\varphi_{-}\end{split}

(82)

Introduce the +/- modes:

\varphi_{+}=\frac{\varphi_{1}+\varphi_{2}}{2}\text{ and }\varphi_{-}=\varphi_{1}-\varphi_{2}

(83)

After defining
c_{1}=c_{3}=c
,
\frac{2}{l}=\frac{1}{l_{1}}+\frac{1}{l_{3}}
, and
\frac{2}{\Delta l}=\frac{1}{l_{1}}-\frac{1}{l_{3}}
, the Lagrangian is simplified to:

L=\frac{c_{+}}{2}\dot{\varphi}_{+}^{2}+\frac{c_{-}}{2}\dot{\varphi}_{-}^{2}-\frac{1}{2L_{+}}\varphi_{+}^{2}-\frac{1}{2L_{-}}\varphi_{-}^{2}-\frac{1}{\Delta l}\varphi_{+}\varphi_{-}

(84)

where

c_{+}=2c
,
c_{-}=\frac{cc_{2}}{2(2c+c_{2})}
,

\frac{1}{L_{+}}=\frac{2}{l}+\frac{4}{l_{2}}
and
\frac{1}{L_{-}}=\frac{1}{2l}

The frequencies can thus be calculated:

\begin{split}\omega_{+}&=\frac{1}{\sqrt{lc}}\sqrt{1+2\frac{l}{l_{2}}}\\
\omega_{-}&=\frac{1}{\sqrt{lc}}\sqrt{1+2\frac{c}{c_{2}}}\end{split}

(85)

To calculate
\beta
we need to decompose
\dot{\varphi}_{0}
and
\dot{\varphi}_{0}+\dot{\varphi}_{1}+\dot{\varphi}_{2}
into
a^{\dagger}_{+}
,
a_{+}
,
a^{\dagger}_{-}
and
a_{-}
. We first decompose
\dot{\varphi}_{0}
and
\dot{\varphi}_{0}+\dot{\varphi}_{1}+\dot{\varphi}_{2}
into
\dot{\varphi}_{+}
and
\dot{\varphi}_{-}
:

\begin{split}\dot{\varphi}_{0}&=-\dot{\varphi}_{+}-\frac{1}{2}\frac{c_{2}}{2c+c_{2}}\dot{\varphi}_{-}\\
\dot{\varphi}_{0}+\dot{\varphi}_{1}+\dot{\varphi}_{2}&=\dot{\varphi}_{+}-\frac{1}{2}\frac{c_{2}}{2c+c_{2}}\dot{\varphi}_{-}\end{split}

(86)

where we have set
C=0
.

Following the same way as in Section
C.1
we can derive the
g
couplings:

\begin{split}g_{+a}&=c_{a}\sqrt{\frac{\omega_{+}\omega_{a}}{c_{+}c_{qa}}}\\
g_{-a}&=c_{a}\frac{1}{2}\frac{c_{2}}{2c+c_{2}}\sqrt{\frac{\omega_{-}\omega_{a}}{c_{-}c_{qa}}}\\
g_{+b}&=-c_{b}\sqrt{\frac{\omega_{+}\omega_{a}}{c_{+}c_{qa}}}\\
g_{-b}&=c_{b}\frac{1}{2}\frac{c_{2}}{2c+c_{2}}\sqrt{\frac{\omega_{-}\omega_{a}}{c_{-}c_{qa}}}\end{split}

(87)

\beta,\alpha
and
\frac{g_{12}}{\Delta_{12}}
can then be solved similarly as in the left-hand case.
