Gradient Free Deep Reinforcement Learning With TabPFN
David Schiff, Ofir Lindenbaum, Yonathan Efroni

Abstract
Gradient-based optimization is fundamental to most modern deep reinforcement learning (RL) algorithms; however, it introduces significant sensitivity to hyperparameters, unstable training dynamics, and high computational costs. We propose TabPFN-RL a novel gradient-free deep RL framework that re-purposes the meta-trained transformer TabPFN as a Q-function approximator. Originally developed for tabular classification, TabPFN is a transformer pre-trained on millions of synthetic datasets to perform inference on new unseen datasets via in-context learning. Given an in-context dataset of sample–label pairs and new unlabeled data, it predicts the most likely labels in a single forward pass, without gradient updates or task-specific fine-tuning. We use TabPFN to predict Q values using inference only, thereby eliminating the need for back-propagation at both training and inference. To cope with the model’s fixed context budget, we design a high-reward episode gate that retains only the top 5% of trajectories. Empirical evaluations on the Gymnasium classic-control suite demonstrate that TabPFN-RL matches or surpasses Deep Q Network on CartPole-v1, MountainCar-v0, and Acrobot-v1, without applying gradient descent or any extensive hyperparameter tuning. We discuss the theoretical aspects of how bootstrapped targets and non-stationary visitation distributions violate the independence assumptions encoded in TabPFN ’s prior, yet the model retains a surprising generalization capacity. We further formalize the intrinsic context size limit of in-context RL algorithms and propose principled truncation strategies that enable continual learning when the context is full. Our results establish prior-fitted networks such as TabPFN as a viable foundation for fast and computationally efficient RL, opening new directions for gradient-free RL with large pre-trained transformers.



Introduction

RL has achieved remarkable success across domains, from game playing
(
Mnih et al. 2013
)
to robotics
(
Haarnoja et al. 2024
)
. These advances are primarily driven by gradient-based methods, which exploit the differentiability of neural networks to optimize policies or value functions via stochastic gradient descent
(
Battash, Wolf, and Lindenbaum 2024
)
. While effective at enabling end-to-end learning and long-term credit assignment, such methods are often sample-inefficient and sensitive to hyperparameters, posing challenges in sparse-reward and offline settings.

In particular, RL algorithms typically require careful tuning of numerous hyperparameters—including optimizer choice, learning rate, and network architecture—which can significantly impact performance
(
Adkins, Bowling, and White 2024
)
. Even different runs of the same algorithm with varying hyperparameters may yield drastically different results, necessitating extensive search procedures and substantial computational resources.

Popular deep RL algorithms, such as Deep Q-Networks (DQN)
(
Mnih et al. 2013
)
and Proximal Policy Optimization (PPO)
(
Schulman et al. 2017b
)
, exemplify these challenges. DQN, which approximates the Q-function using a neural network and relies on temporal difference updates, is particularly susceptible to instability due to the combination of bootstrapping, function approximation, and off-policy learning. This often necessitates the introduction of additional mechanisms, such as target networks and experience replay buffers, to stabilize training, which in turn introduces new hyperparameters and design choices. On the other hand, PPO, a widely used policy gradient method, improves stability through clipped surrogate objectives but remains fundamentally on-policy, which limits sample efficiency. Furthermore, both DQN and PPO assume that the policy or value function is differentiable with respect to its parameters, making them inapplicable in domains involving non-differentiable components or discrete structures. These caveats, including sensitivity to noisy gradients, reliance on differentiability, and the complexity of stabilizing training dynamics, motivate the exploration of gradient-free RL algorithms. By avoiding gradient-based optimization altogether, such methods offer the potential for greater robustness, especially in sparse, non-smooth, or highly stochastic environments.

Recent progress in large language models (LLMs) has demonstrated that transformers can perform a wide range of tasks through in-context learning (ICL)
(
Garg et al. 2023
)
, eliminating the need for gradient-based fine-tuning. In natural language processing, models like GPT-4 demonstrate the ability to solve diverse problems purely by conditioning on task demonstrations in the input sequence. The ability to learn in context without any gradient-based training could also be implemented for RL. Instead of training task-specific RL agents from scratch via gradient descent, we explore the conceptual possibility of a foundation model for RL that can perform RL through ICL, using past transitions to predict optimal behavior. Our work explores this direction through the use of
TabPFN
a transformer trained for ICL over tabular classification/regression problems, which is repurposed here as a Q-function approximator that requires no gradient-based updates during interaction. This opens the door to a novel framework for gradient-free deep RL, where policy improvement occurs purely through ICL.

Several approaches have explored RL without gradient-based updates. Evolution Strategies have demonstrated competitive performance on benchmarks like Atari, albeit at high computational cost due to population-based optimization
(
Salimans et al. 2017
)
. Other methods, such as KNN-based Q-learning, replace neural networks with instance-based estimators but rely on problematic assumptions about state similarity
(
Martín H., de Lope, and Maravall 2009
)
. More recently, meta-RL and ICL have emerged as powerful alternatives to traditional RL. Works like RL
2

(
Duan et al. 2016
)
and Algorithm Distillation
(
Laskin, Srinivas, and Abbeel 2022
)
utilize recurrent or transformer-based models to learn RL algorithms that adapt solely via context, without requiring parameter updates at test time. However, the success of both models has been evaluated mainly on discrete state spaces or a simple visual maze environment. OmniRL
(
Wang et al. 2025
)
enhances this approach by training a large transformer on a variety of procedurally generated tasks, which facilitates broader generalization across RL settings. However, OmniRL has only demonstrated success in discrete state spaces. To our knowledge, all ICL RL methods do not address the issue of continual learning when the context is full. This is a problem for all ICL RL methods, as their ability to learn is limited by the size of their contexts.

TabPFN-RL
is a supervised, meta-trained Transformer that performs ICL for small tabular datasets and infers the most likely labels using inference only. Given a context of input–output pairs
\mathcal{D}_{\text{train}}
, it directly outputs the distribution over the possible labels
q_{\theta}(y\mid x_{\text{test}},\mathcal{D}_{\text{train}})
for a test point, without requiring any gradient-based updates at test time. Trained on millions of synthetic supervised tasks sampled from a data-generating prior,
TabPFN
effectively learns a meta-algorithm capable of inferring labels or target values from small, structured datasets in a single forward pass.

In this study, we propose a new approach to gradient-free RL by utilizing
TabPFN
as an ICL RL model. Specifically, we study whether
TabPFN
’s strong inductive bias, acquired through pre-training on millions of synthetic tabular datasets, can be used for RL without relying on gradient-based updates. This method paves the way for faster, data-efficient RL. Our results demonstrate that
TabPFN-RL
can be effectively applied to RL and is competitive with widely used RL methods, such as DQN
(
Mnih et al. 2013
)
, in Gymnasium’s classic control environments
(
Towers et al. 2024
)
.

Our contributions are as follows: (1) We develop a framework that uses
TabPFN
as a
Q(s,a)
function approximator in online RL. (2) We empirically evaluate our method on standard Gymnasium environments, demonstrating its effectiveness in RL. (3) We discuss the theoretical limitations of in-context RL algorithms due to their limited context size. (4) We offer solutions to context size limitations and evaluate them empirically. (5) We explore the theory of a Prior Fitted RL Learning agent and outline directions for future work in this field.

Background

Reinforcement Learning

Preliminaries

RL formalizes sequential decision-making as a Markov Decision Process (MDP), defined by the tuple
(\mathcal{S},\mathcal{A},P,R,\gamma)
. Here,
\mathcal{S}
is the state space,
\mathcal{A}
is the action space,
P(s^{\prime}\mid s,a)
is the transition probability of moving to state
s^{\prime}
from state
s
by taking action
a
,
R(s,a)
is the reward function, and
\gamma\in[0,1)
is the discount factor.

The goal of an RL agent is to learn a policy
\pi(a\mid s)
that maximizes the expected cumulative discounted reward:

\mathbb{E}_{\pi}\left[\sum_{t=0}^{\infty}\gamma^{t}R(s_{t},a_{t})\right].

Approaches to learn an approximation of the optimal policy include methods such as Deep-Q Networks (DQN)
(
Mnih et al. 2013
)
. DQN relies heavily on gradient-based optimization.

In this work, we explore an alternative approach that entirely avoids gradient-based updates. We propose a gradient-free Q-learning framework using
TabPFN
as a Q-function approximator, leveraging its meta-learned prior and efficient inference capabilities.

Offline Reinforcement Learning and Fitted Q Iteration

Offline Reinforcement Learning (Offline RL) aims to learn optimal policies from a fixed dataset of previously collected interactions, without further access to the environment. This setting is particularly relevant in safety-critical or expensive domains where online exploration is impractical or risky. Standard RL methods, which assume active environment interaction, often fail in this regime due to distributional shift and overestimation of value functions outside the data distribution.

A common and effective approach in Offline RL is
Fitted Q Iteration
(FQI). FQI is a batch-mode variant of Q-learning that performs value iteration using supervised regression. Given a dataset
\mathcal{D}=\{(s_{i},a_{i},r_{i},s^{\prime}_{i})\}_{i=1}^{N}
of transitions, FQI initializes a Q-function
Q_{0}
and iteratively performs the following update:

\displaystyle y_{i}^{(k)}=r_{i}+\gamma\max_{a^{\prime}}Q_{k-1}(s^{\prime}_{i},a^{\prime})\quad,

(1)

for each transition
(s_{i},a_{i},r_{i},s^{\prime}_{i})
, and

\displaystyle Q_{k}=\arg\min_{f\in\mathcal{F}}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\left(f(s_{i},a_{i})-y_{i}^{(k)}\right)^{2},

(2)

where
\mathcal{F}
is a function class (for example, neural networks, decision trees, or, in our case,
TabPFN
). Each iteration fits a new Q-function to the updated Bellman targets using supervised regression, avoiding the need for online updates or bootstrapping within trajectories.

FQI has been demonstrated to be stable and effective in offline settings, particularly when combined with regularization or conservative value estimation techniques to mitigate extrapolation errors. In this work, we build upon FQI by proposing a gradient-free variant that utilizes
TabPFN
as the Q-function approximator, and therefore framing FQI as an ICL problem. This allows us to retain the benefits of offline RL while leveraging TabPFN’s inductive bias and meta-learned prior for fast and reliable generalization.

TabPFN: Prior-Data Fitted Networks

TabPFN
is a transformer-based architecture designed for fast and flexible inference on tabular data. Unlike other neural network models such as
(
Yang, Lindenbaum, and Kluger 2022
;
Svirsky and Lindenbaum 2024
)
, which train separate models for each task,
TabPFN
performs ICL by conditioning on a small training dataset and predicting labels for test points in a single forward pass. It is trained on a synthetic distribution of millions of small tabular classification and regression tasks, which enables it to learn a strong prior over common data-generating processes.

Concretely,
TabPFN
is a transformer
f_{\theta}
trained to map an input sequence containing both training examples and unlabeled samples to label predictions. Each training example
(x_{j},y_{j})
is embedded as a token
t_{j}=\text{Embed}(x_{j},y_{j})
, and each unlabeled input
x_{\text{test}}
is embedded as a token
q=\text{Embed}(x_{\text{test}})
. The full input to the model is then a sequence
S=[t_{1},\ldots,t_{n},q_{1},\ldots,q_{m}]
. Self-attention layers allow unlabeled tokens
q_{k}
to attend to the training tokens
\{t_{j}\}
, enabling them to condition on the training set and extract the relevant mapping from inputs to outputs. During training, synthetic datasets
\mathcal{D}=\{(x_{j},y_{j})\}
are sampled, split into
\mathcal{D}_{\text{train}}
and
\mathcal{D}_{\text{test}}
, and the model parameters
\theta
are optimized by minimizing the cross-entropy loss
\mathcal{L}(\theta)=\sum_{(x,y)\in\mathcal{D}_{\text{test}}}\ell(f_{\theta}(\mathcal{D}_{\text{train}},x),y)
, where
\ell
is the standard classification loss. Once trained,
f_{\theta}
does not require gradient updates or refitting: for a new dataset
\mathcal{D}_{\text{real}}
and queries
x_{\text{test}}
, predictions are obtained simply by concatenating training and query tokens into a sequence and running a single forward pass through the transformer, i.e.,
\hat{y}_{\text{test}}=f_{\theta}(\mathcal{D}_{\text{real}},x_{\text{test}})
. For regression tasks,
TabPFN
is trained similarly, except that continuous outputs are represented by using discrete buckets, which are transformed into a single prediction using a weighted average.

Fitting and Training TabPFN

We define the term ”fitting”
TabPFN
as simply changing the in-context data, and therefore, no explicit training is done when
TabPFN
is fitted. This is, in contrast to actually training
TabPFN
which was trained using gradient updates, as explained previously. For all intents and purposes, when we use the term ”fit” in this paper, we refer to updating the in-context labeled samples for which
TabPFN
will base its inference on.

A study by
Hoo et al. 2025
utilizes
TabPFN
for time-series data. This variant competes effectively with specialized forecasting models, despite the fact that it has never been exposed to real sequences during its meta-training. The idea of using
TabPFN
for time series has sparked our interest in investigating its potential applications in other fields.

Related Work

Transformers in Reinforcement Learning.

Offline-RL formulations, such as the Decision Transformer, frame decision-making as conditional sequence modeling and achieve state-of-the-art results on Atari and OpenAI Gym benchmarks without explicitly learning a value function. They condition the model to predict the sequence of actions given states and the future returns. This method uniquely uses transformers for RL but requires backpropagation and, therefore, gradients
(
Chen et al. 2021
)
.

The Trajectory Transformer re-frames offline RL as a sequence modeling problem, treating entire trajectories as sequences to be modeled by a Transformer. Instead of learning value functions, policies, or dynamics models separately, it models the joint distribution over states, actions, rewards, and optionally returns-to-go, and uses beam search to plan. This approach requires training the Trajectory Transformer on these sequences, and therefore, it also necessitates the use of gradients
(
Janner, Li, and Levine 2021
)
.

Gradient-Free Reinforcement Learning.

Derivative-free policy search has long provided an alternative to back-propagation.
Modern evolutionary algorithms scale to thousands of CPU (central processing unit) cores and achieve competitive scores on RL environments such as Atari, despite noisy gradients
(
Salimans et al. 2017
)
, although these evolutionary strategies are computationally intensive.
The KNN-TD Online RL algorithm utilizes K-Nearest Neighbors to approximate the Q-function, employing a similar approach to Tabular RL by updating the estimated Q-values based on the nearest neighbors. This approach is also computationally intensive and assumes that the state space and the Q function have a linear relationship, i.e., the closer a state and action are under Euclidean distance, the more similar their Q values are. This assumption is invalid in complex, nonlinear environments and is addressed by employing a more capable function approximation, such as deep neural networks
(
Martín H., de Lope, and Maravall 2009
)
.
Our
TabPFN
RL algorithm departs from these by leveraging a meta-learned prior to fit Q-values, eliminating the need for backpropagation or computationally intensive evolutionary search methods.

In-Context and Meta Reinforcement Learning.

Meta-RL aims to learn agents that can rapidly adapt to new tasks. Some works, such as RL
2

(
Duan et al. 2016
)
, introduce a meta RL framework that embeds an RL algorithm within a recurrent neural network, enabling the agent to learn how to learn from experience. The model, typically an LSTM (Long Short-Term Memory)
(
Hochreiter and Schmidhuber 1997
)
, receives as input the current observation, the previous action, the reward, and the done flag, and outputs the following action. It is trained across a distribution of tasks using TRPO (Trust Region Policy Optimization)
(
Schulman et al. 2017a
)
.
At test time, the recurrent policy generalizes to new tasks by leveraging its hidden state to track past interactions and rewards, allowing it to explore and exploit effectively without gradient-based updates. This approach, often referred to as RL
2
, is one of the earliest examples of meta-RL and a precursor to more recent in-context RL models.

Algorithm Distillation showed that a Transformer can learn an RL algorithm. Given a context of past transitions and learning history, it can produce actions that mimic the behavior of different RL algorithms. The transformer can then learn to improve its policy in a new environment by updating its context with new interactions with the environment without any gradient updates
(
Laskin, Srinivas, and Abbeel 2022
)
.

Another similar work proposes a powerful Transformer-based in-context RL agent trained through a large-scale meta-learning process across procedurally generated tasks. The training regime utilizes an artificial environment generator to produce a vast and diverse set of strictly discrete MDPs with randomized transitions and reward structures. Rather than learning a single policy, OmniRL
(
Wang et al. 2025
)
is trained to model causal sequences of interaction histories and adapt behavior by conditioning on context alone.
Our method similarly performs inference entirely in context. Still, it focuses on learning a Q-function via an ICL tabular prior fitted foundation model rather than a monolithic sequence policy.

Method

TabPFN
is used for inference on small tabular datasets. Its prior can be used for many tasks it has not explicitly been trained on, such as time series.
(
Hoo et al. 2025
)

TabPFN
can be used as a Q network, by predicting Q values of states and actions using in-context inference based on a small curated in-context dataset.
Our approach treats fitted Q iteration (FQI) as a pure inference problem by using
TabPFN
as a meta-trained regressor over state–action pairs. We utilize
TabPFN
as the model backbone in the Fitted Q Iteration (FQI) algorithm, leveraging its predictive power and a powerful prior trained on synthetic tabular data to predict the Q values in each FQI iteration. We refer to this combination of
TabPFN
and FQI as
TabPFN-FQI
.

TabPFN-RL and Task-Prior Mismatch

During meta-training,
TabPFN
has access to multiple datasets

\mathcal{D}_{\!\text{train}}=\{(x_{j},y_{j})\}_{j=1}^{M}

whose rows are
i.i.d.
: once a latent task

t\sim p(t)
is fixed, every pair
(x_{j},y_{j})
is sampled
independently from the same distribution
p(x,y\mid t)
.
This independence assumption is baked into the learned prior, and the network’s posterior predictive distribution

q_{\theta}(y\mid x,D_{\!\text{train}})

implicitly expects future data to share that property. This property is violated by the fact that each episode contains transitions which are not i.i.d., because transition
t_{i+1}
is dependent on
t_{i}
.

Furthermore, in FQI, the supervision signal is fundamentally different.
For each transition
(s_{t},a_{t},r_{t},s_{t+1})
we fit

x_{t}\;=\;(s_{t},a_{t}),\qquad y_{t}\;=\;r_{t}+\gamma\max_{a^{\prime}}Q_{k-1}(s_{t+1},a^{\prime}).

The label
y_{t}
is
bootstrapped
: it depends on the

previous
approximation
Q_{k-1}
.
Because
\varepsilon_{t}=y_{t}-\mathbb{E}[y_{t}\mid s_{t},a_{t}]
now contains the
unknown Bellman error of
Q_{k-1}
. The noise is neither independent across samples nor identically
distributed.
TabPFN
has never been trained on such self-referential targets, so its prior offers no guidance on how to treat this additional, model-induced uncertainty.

A second deviation arises from the way data is collected.
States and actions are generated by an evolving policy
\pi_{k}
;
as
\pi_{k}
improves, the empirical distribution over
(s,a)
drifts.
Formally, if
\mu_{k}
denotes the state–action visitation measure under
\pi_{k}
, then
\mu_{k+1}\neq\mu_{k}
, so the dataset at iteration
k+1
is drawn from a different distribution than the one used at iteration
k
.
This
distribution shift
violates the core i.i.d. assumption under which TabPFN’s posterior predictive is calibrated.

Taken together, the presence of bootstrapped labels and the
policy-induced data shift means that the statistical environment encountered in RL lies well outside the prior support learned from i.i.d. tabular tasks.
As we witness, the fact that
TabPFN
yields competitive Q-estimates is therefore surprising and suggests that transformer-based ICL has a greater generalization capability than what the model itself was explicitly trained on.

Theoretical Limitations of In Context Learning RL Methods

Let
M
be an in-context learning RL architecture (for instance, OmniRL or Algorithm Distillation
Wang et al. 2025
;
Laskin, Srinivas, and Abbeel 2022
) with a fixed context budget
B
conditioned on a multiset of at most
B
transitions

\mathcal{S}_{k}=\bigl\{(s_{i},a_{i},r_{i},s_{i}^{\prime})\bigr\}_{i=1}^{|\mathcal{S}_{k}|},\qquad|\mathcal{S}_{k}|\leq B,

at an ICL step
k
. Clearly,
M
stops learning once
|\mathcal{S}_{k}|=B
because the context budget has been reached and no new information can be added into the context. This is a limiting factor for all ICL RL algorithms. We can propose a way to continue learning even when
|\mathcal{S}_{k}|=B
by truncating the context by removing uninformative episodes or transitions.

Removing episodes when context is full

Uninformative episodes or transitions are defined differently per the ICL method. In a more abstract sense, a set
\mathcal{S^{\prime}}
of uninformative transitions or episodes are those such that the policy or value function approximated by model
M
conditioned on context
\mathcal{S}_{k}\setminus\mathcal{S^{\prime}}
is approximately equal to the model conditioned on
\mathcal{S}_{k}
alone. Thus, the remaining context budget, after subtraction, can be used to improve the policy or value network. We investigate methods for truncating the context, or more formally, finding a set
\mathcal{S^{\prime}}
such that we can continue learning even though our context budget is up.

Continual learning with a full context

The problem of finding a subset of transitions
\mathcal{S^{\prime}}
that can be removed from the context and still retain the performance of model
M
is a challenging one and has been discussed by
(
Yang et al. 2025
)
.
As far as we know, no existing ICL method for RL has addressed the problem of what to do when the context is full, other than taking the latest transitions that can fit into the context within the budget
B
. The problem with this method is that removing older episodes to make “space” for new ones could remove significant information from the context. A simple example of important information removal from the context is an episode with a high-reward trajectory, which we would want to keep in the context so that our model can exploit the information about achieving such a high reward. We offer three different approaches to continual learning, even when the context is full. We compare these methods against a ”Stale Context” (
S
in Figure
2
) baseline, which means that we run the model without updating the context when it has reached
B
transitions. Another baseline we compare it to is to keep the latest
B
transitions. Empirically, we find that our methods outperform the baselines and show potential for In-Context RL, which is not limited by the context size, but rather by the algorithm’s context truncation method for retaining useful transitions.

Context‐Truncation Operators

Let
\mathcal{S}_{k}

M
be
\mathcal{E}=\{(s_{t},a_{t},r_{t})\}_{t=1}^{M}
.
We implement four replacement heuristics for maintaining

\mathcal{S}_{k}
once it is full:

Latest Trajectories (L)

This is a pure first-in/first-out strategy that
appends

\mathcal{E}
and, if
|\mathcal{S}_{k}|>B
, discards the oldest
|\mathcal{S}_{k}|-B
transitions. It keeps exactly the
B
most recent transitions and thus performs no value- or diversity-based filtering.

Naive De-duplication (ND)
.

We build a lower-triangular pairwise distance matrix over all pairs of
x_{i}=(s_{i},\mathrm{onehot}(a_{i}))
that are in context
\mathcal{S}_{k}
. Such that the distance is

D_{ij}=\lVert x_{i}-x_{j}\rVert_{2}
for all stored transitions, we then identify, for each transition
x_{i}
, its closest neighbor
\hat{\jmath}(i)=\arg\min_{j<i}D_{ij}
, and mark the transition with the smaller row index in each closest pair for removal. We choose the
M
transitions
x_{i}
with the smallest distance to their neighbor, removing one element from each pair of near-duplicates. Intuitively, we remove duplicate transitions from the context by finding near-duplicate pairs and removing one of them.

Embeddings De-duplication (ED)
.

Identical logic to the naive variant, but redundancy is computed in the model’s representation space. Let
z(\cdot)
map a transition
x_{i}
to its transformer encoder representations. We then run the Naive Deduplication algorithm on
z(x_{i})
. This representation-aware pruning preserves a more diverse set of semantics than raw state–action coordinates.

Reward Variance (RV)
.

We maintain two disjoint partitions of
\mathcal{S}_{k}
—a
good
set

\mathcal{G}
and a
bad
set
\mathcal{W}
—each targeting

B/2
transitions. These partitions are defined by total episodic return. When

\mathcal{E}
arrives, we compare its return
R(\mathcal{E})
with empirical

5
-th and
95
-th percentiles of
\mathcal{G}
and
\mathcal{W}
: (R1) If
R(\mathcal{E})
exceeds the 95-th percentile of
\mathcal{G}
, evict the smallest-return member of
\mathcal{G}
and add
\mathcal{E}
to
\mathcal{G}
. (R2) If
R(\mathcal{E})
falls below the 5-th percentile of
\mathcal{W}
, evict the largest-return member of
\mathcal{W}
and add
\mathcal{E}
to
\mathcal{W}
. (R3) Otherwise, no replacement occurs.
This heuristic promotes a balanced mixture of high-reward exemplars and challenging, low-reward episodes, generating richer learning signals than recency or geometric diversity alone.

Discussion

Our experiments demonstrate that

TabPFN
can serve as a surprisingly strong
Q
–function approximator even though its meta-training prior was never exposed to RL targets or bootstrapped labels. On the classic control suite,
TabPFN-RL
matches or surpasses a gradient-based Deep Q Network (DQN) while completely sidestepping back-propagation. This finding suggests that the inductive bias learned from millions of synthetic tabular tasks transfers, at least partially, to the structure of Bellman targets.

There are several important considerations to keep in mind. First, the evaluation domains are low-dimensional and dense, especially after reward shaping. It is still uncertain how well this approach will scale to high-dimensional visual inputs or scenarios with truly sparse rewards, such as in the game Montezuma’s Revenge. Second, performance is sensitive to the selection of hyperparameter values. Poor choices can lead to excessive use of the fixed context budget
B
due to redundant or low-signal transitions, resulting in plateaued learning. Third, while the training process does not rely on gradients, the cost of inference increases quadratically with the context size. This is because all transitions must be processed by the transformer at every step. For large
\ B
, this can outweigh the savings from omitting back-propagation. Finally, the current implementation relies on reward shaping, raising questions about how the model would work in a sparse reward environment without any additional fine-tuning to the
TabPFN
network on synthetic data that aligns with the data distribution of sparse RL environments.

From a theoretical perspective, our study reveals that our in-context RL algorithm violates several assumptions underlying the
TabPFN
model. Specifically, the labels are bootstrapped, the transitions within an episode are not i.i.d., and the data distribution shifts as the policy improves. Nevertheless, the model demonstrates sufficient flexibility to adapt in context. This resilience suggests a broader capability of prior-fitted networks and ICL to extrapolate beyond their formal prior support, an effect that deserves further analysis.

Future Work

The encouraging results from classic control suggest several potential extensions:

(1) Scaling to high-dimensional tasks: Further empirical evaluations are needed to test
TabPFN-RL
in higher-dimensional settings. Applying
TabPFN-RL
to visual domains (e.g., Atari, DM Control) will require a learned state encoder whose outputs remain compatible with the tabular prior. One challenge in such settings is the computational cost of self-attention: as the input dimensionality grows, the attention mechanism incurs a quadratic runtime complexity (O(
N^{2}
)). Potential solutions for this include compressing states into smaller dimensions or using linearized attention solutions
(
Katharopoulos et al. 2020
)
.

(2) Prior alignment for RL: A principled approach to reducing the prior mismatch is to extend meta-training to incorporate bootstrapped targets and non-stationary visitation distributions. Synthetic Markov chains with known dynamics can act as a controlled curriculum, gradually introducing Bellman noise and distribution shifts.

(3) Learned context compression: Our hand-designed truncation operators (
L
,
DN
,
DE
,
RV
) achieve continual learning but still rely on heuristics. By training a secondary network to compress contextual information and feed it back as a compressed representation, we could potentially surpass the performance of current heuristic methods while adhering to a fixed budget
B
. Another alternative is to use a latent memory-based ICL method, which would be based on architectures like RWKV or Mamba, which can work significantly better with longer sequences.

(4) Integration with policy-gradient methods: The current work concentrates on value-based RL. A logical extension of this research is to utilize
TabPFN
within an actor-critic framework, employing it both as an actor and a critic. This could be achieved by treating
TabPFN
as a value network, where its value approximations for each state serve as input to a
TabPFN
policy network, along with the state itself. This approach resembles the methodology presented in
(
Chen et al. 2021
)
. Exploring these approaches will help determine whether prior-fitted transformers can genuinely offer a new paradigm for efficient and scalable RL.

Implementation Details

Code is implemented in
Python 3.9.21
using the
TabPFN
library version 2.0.9 and stable-baselines3 version 2.6.0.
For our comparison with DQN on the gymnasium classic control
(
Towers et al. 2024
)
environments, we ran 10 different seeds for each environment. We ran
TabPFN-RL
with a different
\varepsilon
for each environment: 0.95, 0.7, 0.7, along with an initial set of random transitions, to fit the context size of 200, 200, and 128 for Acrobot-v1, MountainCar-v0, and CartPole-v1, respectively. The final
\varepsilon
value for each environment was 0.1 with an
\varepsilon
decay of 0.9955, 0.99, 0.99. For the
TabPFN-FQI
Algorithm, the number of iterations was 60 while the
\gamma
was 0.99 for all environments. The final hyperparameters we used yielded these results. However, we cannot guarantee that these are the optimal results, and there may be considerable room for improvement. Before the first FQI iteration, we perturb every reward with an infinitesimal uniform noise,

r\leftarrow r+\epsilon,\;\epsilon\sim\mathcal{U}(0,10^{-4})
.
This guarantees a non-zero standard deviation after TabPFN’s internal
z
-score normalization. Our baseline, DQN,
Q(s,a)
, is approximated by a neural network trained using temporal-difference (TD) learning:

Q(s,a)\leftarrow Q(s,a)+\alpha\left(r+\gamma\max_{a^{\prime}}Q(s^{\prime},a^{\prime})-Q(s,a)\right),

where
\alpha
is the learning rate.
We set DQN with the following hyperparameters for the learning rate of
5\times 10^{-4}
, initial exploration rate
\varepsilon_{\text{init}}=0.95
decaying to
\varepsilon_{\text{final}}=0.1
. A discount factor
\gamma=0.99
, batch size of 64, target network update interval of 500 steps, one gradient step per update, and training frequency set to 1 (i.e., training occurs at every step).
We compared the performance of DQN and
TabPFN-RL
with the following reward shaping functions.
For Acrobot-v1, we define a shaped reward for Acrobot by computing the vertical height of the end-effector (tip of the second link) based on the angular state:

r_{\text{shaped}}=-\cos(\theta_{1})-\cos(\theta_{1}+\theta_{2}).

This encourages the agent to raise the tip of the pendulum, promoting upright configurations. We assume both links have unit length.
For MountainCar-v0, we use a dense reward that combines progress and velocity:

r_{\text{shaped}}=\frac{\text{x}-x_{\text{min}}}{-x_{min}+x_{max}}+c\cdot|\text{v}|-1.

Where
x_{min}=-1.2
and
x_{max}=0.6
, and these define the environment borders, while
v
is the velocity of the car. We use
c
as a constant to encourage velocity and define
c=10
.
This formulation encourages forward motion and penalizes stagnation, helping the agent build momentum toward the goal.
For CartPole, we define a shaped reward based on normalized deviations from the center and upright position:

r_{\text{shaped}}=2-\frac{|\text{x}|}{x_{\text{max}}}-\frac{|\theta|}{\theta_{\text{max}}},

where
x_{\text{max}}=2.4
and
\theta_{\text{max}}=12^{\circ}
. This encourages the pole to stay near vertical and the cart not to move out of the environment’s bounds.

All experiments were conducted using an NVIDIA A100 GPU server with an Intel(R) Xeon(R) Gold 6338 CPU at 2.00 GHz.
