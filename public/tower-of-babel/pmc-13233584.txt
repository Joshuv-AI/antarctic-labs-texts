Intramedullary screw fixation of ballistic metacarpal and phalanx fractures
Authors: Kanad Ghosh, Larissa Wietlisbach, Lyndsay Kandi, Hope Xu, Ronak Patel, Jignesh Unadkat
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractIntroductionBallistic hand fractures present a complex problem for surgeons. Traditionally, these have been treated with K-wires, necessitating prolonged immobilization. While fixation of hand fractures using intramedullary (IM) screws has been shown to have excellent outcomes in blunt trauma, their use in ballistic fractures has not been well studied. Here, we report our early outcomes of fixation of ballistic hand fractures with IM screws.MethodsOur retrospective study reviewed adult patients from March 2023 to August 2024 treated for ballistic hand fractures with IM screws. Demographic data, operative details, complications, and return to motion were recorded.ResultsThirty-two patients were included. The average age was 31.6 ± 14.0 years old, with the majority (29/32, 87.9%) being male. Most injuries involved the metacarpal and proximal phalanx, and 24/32 patients (75.0%) had intraarticular involvement. The average operative time was 75.9 min, including time required for other concurrent hand procedures in 19/32 (59.4%) of patients.The mean time from surgery to initiation of range of motion was 11.1 days, with 12/32 patients (37.5%) allowed to start range of motion immediately post-operatively. Time to full range of motion was 30.2 days and time to weightbearing was 51.6 days. Four patients (13.3%) had unplanned re-operations, all of which were due to proximal screw migration.ConclusionOur study demonstrates the feasibility of IM screw fixation of ballistic hand fractures. Our complication rate was minimal, and patients were able to safely return to normal activities of daily living at an accelerated pace.Keywords: Intramedullary screw fixation, Metacarpal, Phalanx, Hand fracture

Introduction

Firearm injuries cause significant morbidity and mortality in the United States, with over 48,000 deaths reported by the Center of Disease Control in 2022.1,2 These injuries tend to occur in a predominantly younger, male population, and often present with wounds in several anatomic areas.3,4 Injury to the upper extremity presents a particularly challenging problem due to a greater zone of injury, more significant degree of comminution, and associated neurovascular damage seen in low-velocity ballistics present in the civilian population.5, 6, 7 Ballistic fracture fixation in the hands specifically have derived management principles from wartime injuries and are traditionally treated with operative debridement and Kirschner wire (K-wire), plating, or external fixation.8,9 However, this method of bony fixation requires prolonged immobilization and can lead to significant stiffness and infection.10, 11, 12

Intramedullary (IM) screw fixation has been used extensively in long bone fractures and, expounding on principles introduced by Foucher in the 1970s, has become increasingly more common in fractures of the phalanges and metacarpals.13, 14, 15 IM screws offer the patient rigid fracture fixation and allows early range of motion with a faster return to work.16,17 However, their use thus far is often limited to transverse or short oblique fractures and prior work has cautioned against use in severe comminution due to possible bone shortening and fracture compression if using variable pitch screws.18 In this study, we report our institution’s experience in the treatment of ballistic metacarpal and phalanx fractures with IM screw fixation.

Methods

An Institutional Review Board (IRB) approved retrospective study was conducted according to STROBE guidelines examining the medical records of patients who presented to our institution’s Level 1 Trauma Center from March 2023 to June 2024 and were treated by the senior author. Included patients were over the age of 16 years and presented with gunshot wounds (GSWs) to the hand with associated metacarpal or phalangeal fractures; patients with injuries in addition to GSWs to the hand were included as well. Patients were triaged according to clinical status and stability on which outpatient treatment of fractures versus inpatient admission was predicated. Patients chosen for IM screw fixation had maintained at least 1 cm of uninjured bone proximal and distal to the fracture site. Patients were followed-up in an outpatient setting with repeat imaging during subsequent visits. Patient demographic data, hand injury patterns and additional injuries, operative intervention, time to initiation of range of motion and weightbearing, and complications were recorded.

Operative management

Patients presenting with GSWs to the hand were seen by the on-call Plastic Surgery team in the Emergency Department, at which time the wound was irrigated and closed at bedside. Patients were splinted with a volar resting or ulnar gutter splint depending on the location of injury. If patients had other injuries requiring urgent or emergent intervention, this was coordinated on a case-by-case basis with Trauma Surgery and other services. Patients requiring admission were placed on antibiotic coverage if indicated by other injuries; they were not placed on antibiotics for hand fracture alone, as long as the fracture was not grossly contaminated and was amenable to irrigation and closure in the Emergency Department. If the patient was otherwise stable and cleared from all other medical teams, they were allowed to be discharged and return for the operation in an ambulatory setting.

Patients with phalanx and metacarpal fractures were given a dose of intravenous antibiotics within 30 min of the case start time. A 0.045-inch K-wire was placed retrograde in the IM canal of the digit under fluoroscopic guidance. A small longitudinal incision was made at the interface of the K-wire and skin, after which a drill was guided over the K-wire and drilled until reaching the proximal cortex. Screw length was measured to ensure both ends of the screw were in contact with subchondral bone and an appropriate diameter non-variable pitch IM screw (Acumed, Colson Medical LLC, Hillsboro, Oregon) was chosen based on available screw diameters from the Acumed system. Metacarpal screw lengths were typically 4.5 mm for all digits except the ring finger, which typically required a smaller diameter 3.6 mm screw. All phalangeal screws were 2.0 mm in diameter and length was similarly chosen to ensure both ends of the screw were in contact with subchondral bone,

Following this, any other indicated hand procedures were performed, when applicable. At the end of the case, stability of the fracture was determined by passive range of motion under live fluoroscopy. If there was concern for instability, patients were placed in a volar resting splint. Otherwise, they were placed in a soft dressing and advised to start gentle active range of motion. Patients were scheduled for follow up at 2-weeks at which time splints were removed, if applicable. Formal hand therapy was prescribed to all patients at their first follow-up. Patients were then scheduled to be seen at 4-weeks, 6-weeks, and 3 months to monitor healing with clinical exam and X-ray (Figures 1 and 2).

Results

Thirty-two patients were included in our study with a mean follow up time of 55.2 ± 38.1 days; three patients were lost to follow-up. The average age was 31.6 ± 14.0 years old, with the majority (29/32, 87.9%) being male (Table 1). None of the included patients had any significant chronic past medical history. Mean time to surgery was 3.9 days. Nearly half of the included patients (15/32, 46.9%) had injury to other areas in addition to hand fracture, with the vast majority attributed to assault. Most injuries were sustained to the metacarpal and proximal phalanx, and the majority (24/32, 75.0%) had intraarticular involvement. The average operative time was 75.9 min, though this included time required for other concurrent hand procedures in 19/32 (59.4%) of patients (Table 2).

Discussion

Ballistic injury to the upper extremity presents a host of unique challenges. These injuries are especially salient in younger, male populations as evidenced by a large epidemiological study conducted by Hooper et al. in 2021 and supported by our data in which almost 90% of patients were male with a mean age of 32 years old.19 Standard trauma guidelines should be followed for assessment of these patients to exclude any life-threatening injuries, after which a focused hand exam to determine acute vascular compromise, functional and neurologic deficits, and bony involvement is conducted. The presence of fractures after GSWs increases the potential for possible neurovascular damage and threatened limb or digits if not assessed acutely.20

Bony fixation in the setting of comminuted ballistic fractures has traditionally relied on percutaneous pinning and a period of immobilization, risking possible infection, soft tissue tethering, and stiffness of the affected digit.3,21, 22, 23, 24 In a retrospective study by Hsu et al., the group found complications in 14% of all pins including superficial and deep infections, as well as fractures through the pin tracks, pin migration, and pin loosening.23 The use of external fixation has also been reported, though this method can preclude full stability in these injuries.21 Open reduction with plating can be technically challenging in the setting of significant comminution and risks stiffness, periosteal stripping, plate prominence, and tendon rupture. Hall and colleagues, noting some of these same limitations, reported on the use of intramedullary rigid fixation in the context of ballistic hand fractures; this was done in conjunction with proximal and distal locking mechanisms to maintain rotational control as well as bone grafting.15,25 In their 2006 study of ten ballistic metacarpal fractures treated with locked intramedullary nail with bone grafting, the authors found almost complete range of motion at the metacarpophalangeal joint (MPJ), no malrotation, and no infection.

In our study, we report our experience in the use of a single non-variable pitch headless intramedullary screw for rigid fixation, allowing early range of motion and faster return to weightbearing for patients. This was done without bone grafting or the need for a locking mechanism on the screws. Additionally, our study is the first to report on the fixation of both ballistic metacarpal and phalangeal fractures with IM screws. Our group has found that, provided adequate bony purchase proximal and distal to the fracture site, the intramedullary screw can sufficiently maintain rotational position of the digit and allow bony consolidation around it without the need for bone grafting.

The incidence of ballistic hand fracture are associated with a significantly increased risk of tendon and nerve injury, which in turn leads to higher risk of more long-term disability.26 Though more than half (59.4%) of included patients in this study required concomitant hand procedures, there were no instances of vascular compromise or concern for ischemic digit. The majority of concomitant procedures were extensor tendon repairs, followed by joint fusions which were separate from ballistic fracture treated with IM fixation. Concern for acute carpal tunnel was noted in two patients, and ulnar nerve decompression with Guyon canal release was noted in one patient. Despite mean operative time of over an hour, the time for the bony fixation portion of the case was significantly less – a mean of 44.9 min when only accounting for patients undergoing fracture fixation without concomitant hand procedures, 32.1 min per screw when accounting for the mean of 1.3 screws per patient in those undergoing bony fixation alone.

The treatment of ballistic injuries as open or closed fractures remains controversial and may be predicated on factors such as velocity of injury and contamination of the wound bed on presentation.27, 28, 29 Though there are advocates for surgical debridement and antibiotics for all low-velocity GSWs,30,31 other prospective studies and systematic reviews have found that this may not be necessary.32,33 Fracture due to GSWs in our cohort was not by itself an indication for debridement beyond what was done in the Emergency Department to clean any grossly devitalized tissue, irrigate and close the wound, and immobilize. Despite this, there were no instances of superficial or deeper space infection within our patient cohort.

Four patients from our studied cohort required unplanned reoperation due to screw migration. As mentioned previously, post-operative splinting was determined at the end of the procedure with live fluoroscopic examination of the repaired fracture. Furthermore, patients were placed on strict non-weightbearing precautions post-operatively, regardless of splint use. While patient factors and adherence to instructions may have been a factor in these cases, we do believe IM fixation in ballistic injuries is associated with a higher reoperation rate than other methods of fixation, especially in significantly comminuted fractures such as the ones displayed by the patients with screw migration. In order to mitigate this risk after our experience, we opt to use screws that are shorter by 2 mm subchondral and will use transverse mini-screw to compress the base of intraarticular fractures to provide additional stability. In the future, the use of bone graft may reduce the risk of migration – we believe bone grafting may be a helpful adjunct in the setting of significant comminution but should be evaluated on a case-by-case basis based on degree of comminution. The indications and timing for bone grafting in these clinical situations will be the topic of future study. Though the time to full-weightbearing for the entire cohort was a mean of 51.6 weeks, this decreased to 41.2 weeks when excluding patients requiring reoperation.

The main limitation of our study in addition to its retrospective nature is lack of objective range of motion data. This can be attributed to poor follow up along with inconsistent documentation in medical records and remains a major area of improvement for future studies. While all patients were prescribed occupational therapy when allowed to begin passive and active range of motion, rate of attendance was often low. Furthermore, there were patients lost to follow up before their final appointment, which decreases the accuracy of time to full-weightbearing. Future studies using larger cohorts and prospectively maintained range of motion data, as well as a comparative study of traditional Kirschner wire fixation versus IM screw fixation of ballistic hand fractures, may continue to improve management of these complex injuries.
