Comparative analysis of the outcomes of craniotomy and endoscopic resection for recurrent craniopharyngioma: a single institution study
Authors: Haiyang Wu, Tianzhi Zhao, Wei Zhang, Liang Mi, Yan Qu, Yingxi Wu
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundCraniopharyngioma consistently represents one of the most challenging diseases encountered by neurosurgeons and has a high recurrence rate. Currently, there is still no consensus on the surgical approach for recurrent craniopharyngioma. The purpose of this study was to compare the surgical outcomes and prognoses of endoscopic endonasal surgery (EES) with those of transcranial surgery (TCS) for recurrent craniopharyngioma.MethodsA retrospective study was conducted on patients with craniopharyngioma who underwent surgical resection at Tangdu Hospital, Air Force Medical University, between January 2013 and December 2023. Patients who did not undergo surgery or who underwent primary surgery were excluded. Patients were separated into two groups based on the surgical approach. Specifically, one group underwent EES, and the other cohort underwent TCS. Patient demographic data, presenting symptoms, postoperative complications, and prognostic data were collected and analyzed. Student’s t test and the chi-square test were used to evaluate differences in patient demographics, tumor characteristics, and surgical outcomes between the two cohorts. A Kaplan-Meier curve was used to compare progression-free survival (PFS) and overall survival (OS) after reoperation between the two groups.ResultsA total of 399 patients who were diagnosed with craniopharyngioma were identified, 91 of whom had recurrent craniopharyngioma and met the inclusion criteria. Among these patients, 57 patients underwent TCS, whereas 34 patients received EES. Postoperatively, comparisons of the extent of tumor resection, intraoperative blood transfusion volume, postoperative hospital stay length, incidence of diabetes insipidus, and in-hospital mortality rate revealed no statistically significant differences. However, the EES group exhibited a greater incidence of postoperative cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) leakage than did the TCS group (14.71% vs. 0%,respectively; p = 0.006). Conversely, the EES group exhibited a greater rate of improvement in visual field defects (82.35% vs. 42.11%, p = 0.001), a shorter operative time (3.99 ± 1.97 hours vs. 4.86 ± 1.85 hours, p = 0.037), and a lower incidence of new onset of hypopituitarism (14.71% vs. 36.84%, p = 0.031). The average follow-up duration was 33.5 months (ranging from 4 to 51 months). There were no statistically significant differences observed in the PFS or OS between the TCS and EES groups.ConclusionsBoth EES and TCS are effective methods for the resection of recurrent craniopharyngioma. Although the degree of resection, PFS and OS after the second surgery were comparable between the two surgical approaches, the EES group demonstrated several advantages compared with the TCS group, although a high risk of CSF leakage was noted with EES. Therefore, the endoscopic transnasal approach is a safe and effective surgical approach for the removal of recurrent craniopharyngioma, achieving equivalent resection outcomes while minimizing complications.Keywords: Recurrent craniopharyngioma, Endonasal surgery, Transcranial surgery, Complication, Prognosis

Background

Craniopharyngiomas are midline suprasellar brain tumors that are typically classified as being histologically benign (WHO grade I) [1]. It originates from squamous epithelial remnants of Rathke’s capsule [2]. The incidence of craniopharyngioma is approximately 0.2 per 100,000, accounting for 2–5% of all central nervous system tumors [1, 3, 4]. Despite their benign histological appearance, craniopharyngiomas exhibit biologically aggressive behavior. These tumors frequently extend into the suprasellar skull base and are commonly located posterior to the optic chiasm and adjacent to critical neurovascular structures. This anatomical complexity results in challenges regarding the treatment of these tumors for neurosurgeons [5].

Even after gross total resection (GTR), the recurrence rates for craniopharyngiomas range from 5% to 57% [6, 7]. Long-term follow-up indicates recurrence rates ranging from 20% to 40% and 10-year survival rates ranging from 65% to 100% [8–13]. Recurrent craniopharyngioma may involve tumor recurrence or the progression of a residual tumor and is generally more challenging to surgically manage compared with primary cases [12, 14]. Moreover, the mortality rate associated with surgeries for recurrent tumors is higher than that associated with primary surgeries [6].

Factors contributing to the increased mortality rate in recurrent craniopharyngioma include tumor adherence to surgical scars, brain retraction, disruption of original intracranial structures, subtotal resection (STR), and increased complication rates [14, 15]. Emerging evidence suggests that successful reoperation can provide adequate symptomatic relief, with complication and survival rates being comparable to those of primary resection [16–18]. Other studies have highlighted the feasibility of the safe resection of recurrent tumors and the establishment of surgery as the treatment of choice for recurrent craniopharyngioma [19–22].

Traditionally, transcranial surgery (TCS) has been the standard procedure for removing craniopharyngiomas; however, endoscopic endonasal surgery (EES) has gained popularity in recent years. It is widely recognized that no single approach to skull base surgery is without risk. Numerous studies have compared the advantages and disadvantages of TCS and EES in primary resections; however, a consensus regarding the benefits of TCS versus EES in secondary resections is still lacking. Therefore, this study aimed to retrospectively evaluate the outcomes of TCS and EES in recurrent craniopharyngioma.

Methods

Study populations and programs

This retrospective study identified all patients with recurrent craniopharyngioma who underwent surgical resection via either EES or TCS at the Tangdu Hospital of Air Force Medical University from January 2013 to December 2023. The inclusion criterion included patients who underwent reoperation with pathologically confirmed craniopharyngioma. Patients were excluded if they had undergone only one surgery, had incomplete imaging data, or were lost to follow-up. For patients who underwent more than two surgeries, only the data related to the second surgery were analyzed.

The data that were collected for each patient included demographics, presenting symptoms, hormonal status, neuroradiological findings, visual field examinations, previous treatments, laboratory results, extent of resection, complication rates, survival prognosis and follow-up information. Patients were categorized into two groups based on the utilized surgical approach: those who underwent TCS and those who underwent EES.

Tumor volume was assessed via preoperative and postoperative MRI scans by using a line measurement tool available in our picture archiving and communication system. The tumor volume was calculated via the following formula: tumor volume = (A × B × C)/2, where A, B, and C represent the tumor dimensions in three orthogonal planes. Although this method assumes a spherical shape for the lesion, it provides a reasonable estimate of tumor volume and serves as a suitable measure of the extent of resection. The tumor volumes were recorded in cubic centimeters. Additional tumor characteristics, including consistency (solid vs. cystic), were documented based on the surgeon’s operative notes. The tumor location was classified according to the classification scheme proposed by Kassam et al. [23], whereby tumors were categorized into three types. Specifically, lesions located primarily anterior to the stalk were classified as Type I, lesions located intrinsic to the stalk were designated as Type II, and lesions located posterior to the stalk were classified as Type III. We also classified the location of each tumor based on the relationship between the tumor and the diaphragma sellae. Pathological findings were documented, wherein craniopharyngiomas were classified as either the adamantinomatous subtype or the papillary subtype. Intraoperative conditions and postoperative complications, including operation time, blood loss, transfusion requirements, incidence of diabetes insipidus, and cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) leakage, were recorded for each procedure. The extent of resection was categorized on postoperative MRI images according to the amount of the removed tumor as follows: (1) GTR was defined as no residual lesions or residual calcifications; (2) STR was defined as the resection rate of the tumor being ≥ 90%; and (3) partial resection (PR) was defined as the resection rate of the tumor being < 90%. The visual fields of all of the patients before and after the operation were routinely evaluated by a neuro-ophthalmologist.

Prognostic data for each patient were also retrieved, including visual field status, incidence of recurrence, endocrine assessment data, progression-free survival (PFS), overall survival (OS) and duration until the last follow-up. PFS was defined as the time from reoperation to documented tumor growth on MRI, encompassing both residual disease growth and recurrence. For patients without progression or recurrence, PFS corresponded to the interval from the initial surgery to the last follow-up. Additionally, mortality and OS rates were recorded during follow-up. We also evaluated quality of life by assessing postoperative visual deficits, obesity, and cognitive decline following surgical treatment. These outcomes were measured using the Karnofsky Performance Status (KPS) scale and an evaluation of each patient’s living conditions.

Statistical analysis

Descriptive statistics were reported for all of the patients and for cohorts categorized by treatment group. Continuous variables are summarized as the means and medians, whereas categorical variables are presented as frequencies (percentages). Where appropriate, univariate comparisons were performed via Student’s t tests, nonparametric rank-sum tests, chi-square tests, and Fisher’s exact tests. PFS and OS for patients treated with EES and TCS were estimated via the Kaplan-Meier method, and the curves were compared via the log-rank test. P values were two-tailed, and statistical significance was set at p < 0.05. All of the statistical analyses were conducted via R software (version 4.1.2; R Foundation for Statistical Computing; https://www.r-project.org/).

Results

Baseline characteristics of the population

A total of 399 patients who were diagnosed with craniopharyngioma were identified in the electronic medical management system in our hospital between January 2013 and December 2023. Among these 399 patients, 91 met the inclusion criteria of the study. Among the 91 patients who underwent reoperation, 34 (37.36%) patients underwent EES, whereas 57 (62.64%) patients underwent TCS (Fig. 1).

Flow diagram demonstrating case identification and exclusion to determine the final number of surgical groups

No statistically significant differences were observed between the EES and TCS groups in terms of average age (33.65 ± 17.38 years vs. 34.61 ± 17.27 years, p = 0.797) or sex distribution (17 males vs. 33 males, p = 0.464). In the EES group, 9 (26.47%) patients experienced recurrence after initial GTR, whereas in the TCS group, 16 (28.07%) patients experienced recurrence following initial GTR; however, there was no statistically significant difference observed between the two groups (p = 0.534). Compared with the EES group, patients who underwent TCS did not show a significantly higher likelihood of undergoing Gamma Knife surgery after the initial operation (21.05% vs. 8.82%; p = 0.155).The mean tumor diameters were similar between the two groups (EES: 3.04 cm vs. TCS: 3.56 cm, p = 0.128); furthermore, no statistically significant difference was detected in terms of tumor volume between the two groups (EES: 8.90 cm³ vs. TCS: 9.39 cm³, p = 0.484). There were no significant differences observed in the consistency of the tumor, the tumor pathology, or the tumor location (according to both location classification schemes). Preoperatively, visual deficits were the most common symptom (87.91%), followed by endocrine disorders (72.53%), headaches (36.26%), and neurological symptoms (14.29%). Preoperative symptoms were comparable between the TCS and EES groups, with no significant differences being observed. Comparison of surgical outcomes between the EES group and the TCS group

Table 2 shows the surgical outcomes of the patients in the 2 groups. Across the entire cohort, 31 (54.39%) patients in the TCS group achieved GTR, and 24 (70.59%) patients in the EES group achieved GTR. Although the GTR rate was greater in the EES group than in the TCS group, the rates of GTR did not significantly differ between the two groups (p = 0.069). In our cohort, intraoperative ICA injury occurred in 1 patient (1.75%) in the TCS group and in 1 patient (2.94%) in the EES group. In both cases, ICA injury occurred as a small tear during tumor dissection. The injuries were properly managed with gelatin sponge compression, electrocoagulation, and muscle wrapping, and these injuries did not lead to serious consequences. Moreover, ICA injury rates did not significantly differ between the EES and TCS groups (p = 1.0). In terms of the surgical parameters, the operation time was significantly shorter in the EES group than in the TCS group (p = 0.037). Furthermore, intraoperative blood loss was significantly lower in the EES group than in the TCS group (p = 0.025), although there was no significant difference observed in the requirement for blood transfusion between the two groups (p = 0.500). The average postoperative hospitalization duration was 13.06 ± 7.60 days for the EES group and 12.16 ± 5.34 days for the TCS group, with no statistically significant difference being noted (P = 0.509). Additionally, 2 patients in the EES group and 8 patients in the TCS group exhibited normal preoperative and postoperative visual acuity. Visual improvement was observed in 28 patients (82.35%) in the EES group and 24 patients (42.11%) in the TCS group, with a significant difference (p = 0.001) being observed between the two groups. A minority of patients required postoperative radiotherapy, including 2 patients (5.88%) in the EES group and 8 patients (14.04%) in the TCS group, with no significant difference being observed between the groups. Case examples are shown in Figs. 2 and 3. Furthermore, we compared the preoperative and postoperative BMIs of the two groups and observed that the postoperative BMI of the two groups was significantly greater than the preoperative baseline BMI (p < 0.01); however, there was no significant difference observed between the two groups after the operation (p = 0.600).

Comparison of complication rates between the EES group and the TCS group

Table 3 presents the incidence rates of specific complications (including endocrine dysfunction, CSF leakage, central nervous system infections, and death) in the TCS and EES groups. Diabetes insipidus was the most frequently observed complication, with similar incidences being observed between the EES and TCS groups (76.47% vs. 77.19%, respectively; p = 1.0). New onset of hypopituitarism was noted in 5 patients (14.71%) in the EES group and in 21 patients (36.84%) in the TCS group, and this difference was statistically significant (p = 0.031). CSF leakage occurred exclusively in the EES group, affecting 5 patients (14.71%), which was significantly greater than that in the TCS group (p = 0.006). Postoperative meningitis occurred in 5 patients (14.71%) in the EES group and in 11 patients (19.30%) in the TCS group, with no statistically significant difference being observed (p = 0.777). No significant differences were observed in the rates of other complications based on the number of patients affected between the two groups. The results indicated that the TCS group appeared to have a higher incidence of complications; however, there was no statistically significant difference in the overall complication rates between the two groups(p = 0.166). During hospitalization, 4 patients (7.02%) died, with all of these patients originating from the TCS group; however, no significant difference in the mortality rates was observed between the groups. Comparison of PFS and OS after reoperation between the two cohorts

The average follow-up time for the EES group was 59.19 months (95% CI: 49.20–69.19), and that for the TCS group was 62.34 months (95% CI: 46.79–77.90) (p = 0.198). During the follow-up period, recurrence occurred in 11 patients (32.35%) in the EES group and in 21 patients (39.62%) in the TCS group. There was no statistically significant difference observed in recurrence rates between the two groups (Table 2). The median PFS times for the EES group and TCS group were 64 months (95% CI: 42.85–85.15) and 72 months (95% CI: 10.98–133.02), respectively. The average survival time for the EES group was 78.85 months (95% CI: 68.51–89.19), and that for the TCS group was 105.91 months (95% CI: 92.67–119.15). The 5-year PFS for the EES group was 52.20%, and that for the TCS group was 64.0%. Moreover, the 5-year OS for the EES group was 91.20%, and that for the TCS group was 85.80%. At the last follow-up, 4 patients in the EES group and 5 patients in the TCS group had died; furthermore, there was no statistically significant difference observed in PFS or OS after reoperation between the groups (Fig. 4).

Postoperative quality of life and recurrence management

At the final follow-up, the KPS scores were comparable between the EES and TCS groups (81.11 ± 10.5 vs. 76.86 ± 8.83, respectively; p = 0.620). Notably, in the EES group, 51.85% (14/27) of the patients recovered from preillness occupational or educational activities, whereas this proportion was 56.86% (29/51) in the TCS group. Three patients in the EES group required complete life support, whereas four patients in the TCS group required such support.

The detailed treatment for recurrence primarily consisted of repeat surgery and radiotherapy. In the EES group, six patients underwent one additional operation, two patients underwent two additional operations, and one patient underwent five additional operations. In the TCS group, eight patients underwent one additional operation, one patient underwent two additional operations, and one patient required three additional operations. All of the patients experiencing recurrence received radiotherapy as part of their treatment regimen.

Discussion

The contemporary paradigm for managing craniopharyngioma goes beyond GTR and prioritizes quality of life, thereby demonstrating a significant challenge regarding the development of effective surgical strategies. Historically, previous surgical techniques have heavily relied on transcranial approaches, which are constrained by the optical limitations of the microscope. EES overcomes the limitations of the microscope by facilitating a linear surgical pathway and providing an extensive field of view with direct visualization of the skull base. The introduction of expanded EES has further broadened its indications for treating craniopharyngioma [24–30]. Moreover, this approach minimizes the need for extensive manipulation of brain tissue and neurovascular structures, thereby enhancing visualization of critical anatomical features such as the pituitary gland, pituitary stalk, inferior surface of the optic chiasm, and hypothalamus [26, 29, 31–34]. Additionally, compared with traditional transcranial approaches, EES exhibits a higher rate of GTR, improved visual outcomes, and a tendency toward reduced recurrence [33, 35]. Furthermore, several studies have suggested the use of this technique to manage recurrent or residual craniopharyngioma [36, 37]. Therefore, we retrospectively analyzed the surgical outcomes and complications of craniotomy versus endoscopic surgical resection of recurrent craniopharyngioma.

Current treatment strategies for craniopharyngioma remain contentious, with these strategies ranging from radical surgical approaches aimed at complete resection to more conservative strategies that involve subtotal resection followed by radiotherapy. However, when feasible (particularly in cases where hypothalamic integrity can be preserved), the achievement of safe GTR remains the primary goal of treatment, as it is associated with the highest rates of recurrence-free survival [2]. It is generally accepted that intrasellar tumors can be approached via the endoscopic endonasal approach; for most third-ventricle tumors, the transcranial approach is preferred. For sellar-intrasellar and suprasellar tumors, the surgical approach should be tailored based on specific tumor characteristics (e.g., lateral extension, relationship with the optic chiasm, etc.).A previous study reported that complete resection was achieved in more than one-third of recurrent craniopharyngioma patients, regardless of the utilized surgical technique [38]. This outcome is notably more promising compared to findings from earlier studies [37, 39]. A literature review revealed that the GTR rate following transcranial surgical approaches for recurrent craniopharyngioma varies significantly (ranging from 0% to 56%) and is often less than 25% [6, 10, 20, 40]. In contrast, the GTR rates for endoscopic transnasal transsphenoidal surgery range from 40.9% to 83.3%, with most studies reporting rates above 80% [5, 17, 36, 41, 42]. Overall, the GTR rate for reoperations performed via the endoscopic endonasal approach is substantially higher than that for transcranial reoperations. Furthermore, a previous study comparing the outcomes of initial surgeries and reoperations for recurrent craniopharyngioma revealed that the surgical results for reoperation via the endoscopic endonasal approach were comparable to those of the first procedure, thereby demonstrating either a similar or improved total resection rate, as well as comparable or reduced complication rates [17]. In our study, total resection was achieved in 54.39% of the patients in the TCS group and in 70.59% of those in the EES group. These findings are generally consistent with the results of previous studies; however, no statistically significant difference was observed between the two surgical approaches. This lack of difference may be due to advancements in anatomical knowledge and the evolution of various transcranial endoscopic techniques. Although previous studies have demonstrated a significant association between EES and shorter postoperative hospital stay [43–45], our study did not observe this finding. Furthermore, we observed that, compared with TCS, EES significantly reduced intraoperative bleeding, thus potentially lowering the risk of excessive bleeding.

Visual dysfunction is the most common symptom associated with recurrent craniopharyngioma, and the improvement of visual field function is a primary objective of surgical interventions. For both adults and children with primary craniopharyngioma, the rate of visual improvement is generally greater with the endoscopic endonasal approach than with the transcranial approach [33, 46, 47]. The endoscopic endonasal approach facilitates direct decompression of visual structures, thereby enhancing visualization and preserving the superior hypophyseal artery [48, 49]. This technique is also associated with better postoperative visual outcomes [33, 35, 47, 50]. Studies have indicated that 75% to 89% of patients with preoperative visual impairments experience normalization of vision after endoscopic surgery [29, 30, 47, 51]. In our study, the rate of visual acuity improvement was 82.35% for EES, which significantly exceeded that of TCS. Therefore, for recurrent craniopharyngioma, EES is also superior to the TCS approach for improving visual function.

A major challenge in managing craniopharyngioma involves the preservation of endocrine function. Postoperative deterioration of anterior pituitary function, including deficiencies in adrenocorticotropic hormone, growth hormone, thyroid-stimulating hormone, and gonadotropins, occurs in 39% to 100% of patients; additionally, posterior pituitary dysfunction, which is characterized by arginine-vasopressin deficiency, affects 25% to 86% of patients [2]. In a study by Turel et al. [37], 44% of the patients in their cohort developed new endocrine insufficiencies after repeat surgery, whereas other studies have reported rates as low as 14% [38]. Moreover, diabetes insipidus has been observed as a major complication, with an incidence of 41% in the EES group compared with 62% in the TCS group [52]. Although a large systematic review and meta-analysis revealed similar endocrine outcomes between endoscopic and transcranial approaches [53], other studies have suggested that EES may better preserve endocrine function [54, 55]. In our study, the incidence of new-onset endocrine insufficiency was significantly greater in the TCS group than in the EES group, thus indicating a potential advantage of EES in preserving pituitary function.

CSF leakage is a well-recognized risk factor regarding complications after EES. Although the risk of CSF leakage is increased after EES, this outcome is expected, due to the fact that skull base surgeries inherently increase this risk [56]. Recent studies have demonstrated that the use of a vascularized nasal septal flap significantly reduces the incidence of CSF leakage [30, 57]. To minimize CSF leakage, multilayer reconstruction using abdominal fat and/or broad fascia patches (when feasible) combined with a vascularized flap and sealing materials is the most commonly used technique. This approach has been well documented in the literature [58–62]. Furthermore, surgical series using this technique have reported a reduction in the rate of postoperative CSF leakage from 23% to 4% [26, 29, 51, 60, 63–65].

A meta-analysis conducted by Komotar et al. [33] in 2012 reported a postoperative seizure rate of 8.5%, a hemorrhage rate of 2.0%, and a perioperative mortality rate of 3.2% for TCS patients. In comparison, the EES group exhibited a postoperative seizure rate of 0%, a hemorrhage rate of 3.2%, and a perioperative mortality rate of 1.9%. A more recent national retrospective study conducted by Lin et al. [45] in 2017 analyzed outcomes in 314 patients with craniopharyngiomas who were treated in the United States in 2003, 2006, and 2009 and further highlighted the advantages of EES; specifically, compared with TCS, EES was associated with a lower incidence of postoperative stroke (2% vs. 5%), a lower incidence of seizures (0% vs. 12%), and lower mortality (0% vs. 1%). In our study, the rates of single complications (such as bleeding, cerebral infarction, hydrocephalus, epilepsy, and pneumonia) were very low. However, the results showed that the overall incidence of negative outcomes was lower in the EES group(82.35% for EES and 92.98% for TCS).This result is mainly due to the fact that the TCS method causes greater damage to brain tissue and neurovascular structures compared to the EES method, especially regarding operations that are performed near the hypothalamus or pituitary gland. Moreover, prolonged operation time can also increase the incidence of TCS infection.

In our study, the recurrence rate following surgery for recurrent craniopharyngioma was comparable between the two groups. Specifically, the EES group demonstrated a recurrence rate of 32.35%, whereas the TCS group demonstrated a rate of 39.62% (p > 0.05). These ratios are consistent with previous research reports [37, 38, 66]. Similar recurrence rates for both endoscopic and transcranial approaches have also been documented [33, 53], in addition to comparable PFS rates [67]. Thus, the surgical approach does not appear to significantly influence recurrence rates. Currently, studies on recurrent craniopharyngioma possess several limitations, such as small numbers of cases and short follow-up durations. Therefore, very few studies have reported the 5-year survival rate after surgery for patients with recurrent craniopharyngioma. Data from a recent report indicate that the 5-year survival rate after reoperation is 81.10%, which is consistent with our findings (91.20% for EES and 85.80% for TCS).

Tumor recurrence poses a significant challenge for surgeons. However, the incorporation of radiosurgical techniques alongside surgical intervention is gradually being adopted for the treatment of residual or recurrent tumors, which aligns with the trend toward the use of less aggressive surgical approaches. The data suggest that subtotal resection combined with adjuvant radiotherapy is not inferior to total GTR in terms of disease control [68–70] and may help to mitigate the adverse effects associated with aggressive resection while reducing the risk of injury to vital structures [71, 72]. Nevertheless, resection remains the primary treatment modality (whether combined with adjuvant radiotherapy or performed alone) [73].

In terms of quality of life, hypothalamic injury, pituitary injury, optic nerve injury, and other complications (including sleep-rhythm disturbances, hormone replacement therapy, and visual impairments) play central roles in the development of postoperative quality-of-life decline. In a previous study [74], 75% patients regularly attended school without significant social restrictions; however, our results were significantly lower than this previously reported value. A reasonable explanation for this scenario involves the uncertainty caused by the second surgery. The fear of recurrence and concerns about future health also affect quality of life after craniopharyngioma surgery. Lan Zhang et al. [75] conducted a questionnaire survey of 152 patients and reported that patients exhibited a high degree of fear of disease recurrence, as well as anxiety and depression symptoms. The relevant factor for these observations includes incomplete resection, and patients may view frequent consultations as a reminder of the risk of recurrence.

The present study has several limitations. First, its retrospective nature may have introduced biases inherent to the study design. Second, the small sample size (particularly the limited number of recurrence cases) may have affected the statistical power and generalizability of the findings. Third, selection bias may have occurred due to the specific characteristics of the study population. Future studies should aim to address these limitations via prospective, larger-scale investigations with more rigorous methodologies to provide more reliable and robust evidence.

Conclusions

Our study demonstrated that recurrent craniopharyngioma can be successfully resected via either EES or TCS, depending on the individual condition of the patient. Although the prognoses of these two methods are similar, EES exhibits several distinct advantages compared to TCS. Therefore, when considering the prognoses of patients and the management of complications, EES can be used as the preferred treatment option for recurrent craniopharyngioma. Further prospective, multi-institutional collaborations will be needed to power studies that are capable of fully evaluating which approach is most appropriate for recurrent craniopharyngioma.
