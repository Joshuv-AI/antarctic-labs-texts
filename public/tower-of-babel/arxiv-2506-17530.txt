Abstract
Orthogonal Frequency Division Multiplexing (OFDM) is the foundational waveform in current 5G deployments due to its robustness in quasi-static channels and efficient spectrum use. However, in high-mobility scenarios, OFDM suffers from inter-carrier interference (ICI), and its reliance on dense pilot patterns and cyclic prefixes significantly reduces spectral efficiency. In this work, we propose Deep-OFDM: a learnable modulation framework that augments traditional OFDM by incorporating neural parameterization. Instead of mapping each symbol to a fixed resource element, Deep-OFDM spreads information across the OFDM grid using a convolutional neural network modulator. This modulator is jointly optimized with a neural receiver through end-to-end training, enabling the system to adapt to time-varying channels without relying on explicit channel estimation. Deep-OFDM outperforms conventional OFDM when paired with neural receiver baselines, particularly in pilot-sparse and pilotless regimes, achieving substantial gains in BLER and goodput, especially at high Doppler frequencies. In the pilotless setting, the neural modulator learns a low-rank structure that resembles a superimposed pilot, effectively enabling reliable communication without explicit overhead. Deep-OFDM demonstrates significant improvements in BLER and goodput at high Doppler frequencies across various scenarios, including MIMO systems. Comprehensive ablation studies quantify the role of nonlinear activations and characterize performance-complexity trade-offs. These results highlight the potential of transmitter–receiver co-design for robust, resource-efficient communication, paving the way for AI-native physical layer designs in next-generation wireless systems.



I
Introduction

Orthogonal frequency-division multiplexing (OFDM) is the dominant waveform in 4G and 5G networks. It transforms a multipath fading channel into a set of parallel flat-fading subchannels, enabling simple equalization and efficient use of the spectrum. As a result, OFDM delivers strong average-case performance in quasi-static channels, making it well-suited for current 5G deployments.

However, this performance degrades significantly in high-mobility environments, where the wireless channel varies rapidly in both time and frequency. In such settings (eg, vehicular communications and high-speed rail scenarios), Doppler spread destroys subcarrier orthogonality, causing inter-carrier interference and rendering the standard one-tap equalizer ineffective
[
1
]
. Moreover, pilot-aided channel estimation becomes unreliable as coherence time shrinks, and pilot symbols must be injected more frequently to track the channel.

To compensate for these effects, conventional systems resort to increasing pilot density and extending the cyclic prefix (CP). While these measures help maintain performance, they reduce spectral efficiency: pilots consume time-frequency resources that could otherwise carry data, and the CP occupies a growing fraction of the OFDM symbol, particularly at high carrier frequencies or with fewer subcarriers, where the absolute delay spread remains fixed but symbol durations shrink. These issues are particularly severe in uplink scenarios, where the transmit SNR is low and efficient resource usage is crucial.

These limitations are expected to intensify with the demands of next-generation systems. Proposed 6G standards call for ultra-reliable, low-latency communication at data rates up to 1 Tbps, and support for mobility up to 1000 km/h
[
2
,
3
]
. Meeting these goals will require methods that are fundamentally more robust to rapid channel variation and that can deliver high performance even under sparse pilot conditions. While recent efforts have proposed new waveform designs, such as OTFS
[
4
]
, to better handle channel dynamics, we take a complementary approach in this work.

Designing new waveforms is challenging, as it requires balancing spectral efficiency, robustness, and implementation feasibility. OFDM represents a carefully engineered tradeoff across these axes, and entirely replacing it risks losing critical system-level advantages. Instead, we adopt a hybrid approach known as neural augmentation, building on the OFDM framework while addressing its limitations using learning-based techniques. This paradigm has shown promising results in areas like source and channel coding
[
5
,
6
,

,

,

,

,
11
]
.

Following this principle, we retain the OFDM structure but learn a neural modulation scheme tailored to time-varying channels. Recent work has shown that neural receivers can replace traditional OFDM receivers, achieving competitive performance without relying on explicit channel estimation
[
12
,
13
,
14
]
. These data-driven receivers are not restricted to conventional constellations and can adapt to arbitrary modulation patterns, often with improved robustness in time-varying conditions.

Previous work has indicated gains in transmitter co-design with neural receivers, including learning better constellations, and combining this with novel piloting schemes like Superimposed pilots (SIP)
[
15
,
16
,
17
]
. In our work, we propose Deep-OFDM : a two-dimensional time-frequency spreading scheme implemented via a learnable neural modulator. Unlike full joint coded modulation approaches, which learn both coding and modulation end-to-end
[
18
,
19
,
20
,
21
]
but face scalability issues, Deep-OFDM retains the practical modularity of standard systems by fixing LDPC coding and decoding. This hybrid strategy captures many of the robustness benefits of joint optimization, while remaining scalable and easy to integrate into existing OFDM pipelines.

Our focus is on high-mobility, doubly selective channels, where conventional OFDM-based systems experience significant degradation due to channel aging, inter-carrier interference, and limited coherence time. In this regime, traditional receivers rely heavily on dense pilot insertion to maintain performance, but exhibit diminishing returns as mobility increases. We observe that neural modulation, by introducing learnable structure across time and frequency, provides the most significant performance gains under pilot-sparse conditions, where classical estimation techniques become unreliable. For instance, we show that Deep-OFDM outperforms a neural receiver baseline with a learnable constellation by over 3 dB in BLER at 60 m/s, and achieves higher goodput than pilot-assisted systems, even when operating
without any explicit pilots
. These findings highlight the importance of transmitter-side adaptation in rapidly time-varying channels, and motivate our end-to-end design.

Our main contributions are summarized as follows:

•

We propose Deep-OFDM, a neural modulation scheme that performs learnable time-frequency spreading, explicitly optimized to align with the inductive biases of CNN-based neural receivers. This design yields substantial performance gains in doubly-selective channels.

•

We characterize the interplay between pilot density and neural modulation. Our results show that Deep-OFDM achieves its most considerable improvements under
pilot-sparse

•

We demonstrate that Deep-OFDM maintains high performance even in the

•

We demonstrate through systematic analysis that Deep-OFDM learns implicit pilot structures and exploits time-frequency diversity via convolutional mixing.

•

We conduct comprehensive ablation studies to systematically evaluate the role of nonlinear activation functions in modulation schemes and quantify the performance-complexity trade-offs across varying model complexities.

II-A

System Model

We consider a single-input single-output (SISO) communication system. At the transmitter, the message bit stream
\mathbf{b}\in\{0,1\}^{K}
, is encoded using a rate
\frac{K}{N}
LDPC code, producing a codeword
\mathbf{c}\in\{0,1\}^{N}
. The encoded sequence is mapped to complex symbols using either a standard
2^{m}
-QAM constellation or a learned constellation, resulting in the modulated symbols
\mathbf{s}\in\mathbb{C}^{N}
.

These symbols are transmitted over a 5G NR-compliant OFDM system with
n_{S}
subcarriers and
n_{T}=14
OFDM symbols per slot. The frequency-domain symbols are reshaped into a time-frequency grid
\mathbf{X}\in\mathbb{C}^{n_{S}\times n_{T}}
and transformed to the time domain using an inverse FFT, followed by cyclic prefix (CP) insertion.

We assume a time-varying multipath channel modeled using the clustered delay line (CDL) model
[
22
]
. The UE moves at speed
u
m/s, inducing a maximum Doppler frequency given by
f_{d}=\frac{uf_{c}}{c}
, where
f_{c}
is the carrier frequency, and
c
is the speed of light.
At the receiver, after CP removal and FFT, the received time-frequency grid
\mathbf{Y}\in\mathbb{C}^{n_{S}\times n_{T}}
can be modeled as:

\mathbf{Y}=\mathbf{H}\mathbf{X}+\mathbf{W}

(1)

where
\mathbf{X}\in\mathbb{C}^{n_{S}\times n_{T}}
is the transmitted symbols in the time-frequency grid,
\mathbf{X}\in\mathbb{C}^{n_{S}\times n_{S}}
is the efective channel matrix, and
\mathbf{W}\in\mathbb{C}^{n_{S}\times n_{T}}
is AWGN noise.

In low-mobility settings,
\mathbf{H}
is approximately diagonal, but in high-mobility environments, the channel varies within an OFDM symbol duration, breaking the orthogonality between subcarriers. This results in inter-carrier interference (ICI), where
\mathbf{H}
becomes a full matrix with non-zero off-diagonal elements. The magnitude of these off-diagonal elements increases with mobility, significantly degrading the performance of conventional equalization techniques.

II-B

Baselines

With perfect channel state information (CSI), the Linear Minimum Mean Square Error (LMMSE) equalizer is given by:

\mathbf{W}_{\text{LMMSE},t}=\mathbf{H}_{t}^{H}\left(\mathbf{H}_{t}\mathbf{H}_{t}^{H}+\sigma_{w}^{2}\mathbf{I}\right)^{-1}

(2)

In practice, the channel
\mathbf{H}_{t}
is unknown and must be estimated from pilot symbols. The standard least-squares (LS) estimate of the diagonal of
\mathbf{H}_{t}
at pilot subcarriers is:

\hat{\mathbf{H}}_{t,\mathcal{P}}=\text{diag}\left(\frac{\mathbf{Y}_{t,\mathcal{P}}}{\mathbf{X}_{t,\mathcal{P}}}\right)

(3)

where
\mathcal{P}_{t}\subset\{1,\dots,n_{S}\}
denotes the set of pilot subcarrier indices in the
t
-th OFDM symbol, and
\mathbf{X}_{t,\mathcal{P}},\mathbf{Y}_{t,\mathcal{P}}\in\mathbb{C}^{|\mathcal{P}_{t}|}
are the transmitted and received pilot symbols, respectively.

Channel estimates at non-pilot subcarriers are obtained through interpolation over
\hat{\mathbf{H}}_{t,\mathcal{P}}
.

This approach performs adequately in low-mobility scenarios but suffers from several limitations in high-mobility environments:
1) Violation of the time-invariance assumption within an OFDM symbol, 2) Inability to capture ICI (off-diagonal terms of
\mathbf{H}_{t}
), 3) Standard interpolation methods cannot accurately capture rapid channel variations.

III
Deep-OFDM: Architecture and Training

III-A

Neural modulation and receivers

Neural network-based receivers (NRx) have emerged as promising alternatives to traditional signal processing methods
[
13
,
23
,
16
,
24
]
. These approaches unify channel estimation, interpolation, and demapping into a single trainable model. The NRx takes the received OFDM frame
\mathbf{Y}
, possibly along with pilots, and directly predicts the transmitted bits
\hat{\mathbf{b}}
. Such receivers have demonstrated strong robustness in dynamic channels, particularly in pilot-sparse scenarios. Moreover, recent work has demonstrated the practical feasibility of such systems through the real-time deployment of a 5G standard-compliant NRx, incorporating both hardware and model optimizations
[
14
]
.

The availability of such trainable receiver modules opens the door for end-to-end system optimization. Specifically, in this work, we focus on mitigating the effects of mobility on OFDM systems by joint optimization of the transmitter and neural receiver, as depicted in Figure
1
. Prior work has explored Tx-Rx co-design in this setting, including constellation shaping and super-imposed pilot design
[
15
,
16
,
17
]
.

These methods maintain a one-to-one mapping between transmit symbols and resource elements (REs). In contrast, our method leverages the inductive bias of CNN-based neural receivers (NRx), which process local neighborhoods of subcarriers and time slots. To align with this structure, we introduce Deep-OFDM - a modulation scheme that relaxes the symbol-to-RE mapping by spreading information across the time-frequency grid using a CNN-based modulator. This learned spreading introduces controlled redundancy and enhances time-frequency diversity, significantly improving robustness in high-mobility channels.

III-B

Deep-OFDM Architecture

In conventional OFDM, the
n_{S}\times n_{T}
resource grid is populated with the modulated symbols with one symbol per RE. In contrast, Deep-OFDM uses a CNN encoder to spread the modulated symbols across time and frequency, enabling learned redundancy. For simplicity, we assume all REs are available for data/pilot (i.e., no DC/guard carriers), to isolate the effect of neural modulation. We impose an average power constraint per OFDM frame to ensure parity with conventional systems and initially restrict our study to the SISO setting.

Neural Receiver.

We adopt a CNN-based architecture for the neural receiver (NRx), closely following the design introduced in
[
16
]
. The core building block is a
ResidualBlock
, which contains two convolutional layers. To enhance parameter efficiency without compromising performance, we replace the standard
Conv2D
layers with
SeparableConv2D
, as illustrated in Fig.
2(b)
. The overall architecture comprises an input convolution layer, followed by five residual blocks, and an output layer that directly predicts the log-likelihood ratios (LLRs) of the transmitted bits, as shown in Fig.
3
. The input to the NRx consists of the received OFDM frame (i.e., the complex-valued time-frequency grid after FFT and CP removal), along with the positions and values of any known pilot symbols if present. This setup enables the receiver to flexibly operate in pilot-rich, sparse-pilot, or fully pilotless scenarios.
In contrast to traditional receivers, which employ separate modules for channel estimation, equalization, and demapping, the NRx performs all these tasks jointly through a single unified neural module. This end-to-end formulation unifies the traditionally disjoint processing stages into a single learnable module, maintaining adaptability across diverse channel and pilot configurations. Importantly, it enables the discovery of improved transceiver strategies through joint transmitter–receiver optimization.

Neural Modulator.
To enable time-frequency diversity at the transmitter, we design the neural modulator as a convolutional neural network (CNN). CNNs are a natural fit for this purpose, as their spatial structure enables the model to effectively couple nearby time-frequency components, potentially enhancing robustness against mobility-induced impairments, such as inter-carrier interference (ICI). This constellation is optimized jointly with the modulator and receiver as part of the end-to-end training process. Training.

We jointly optimize the neural modulator and neural receiver by minimizing an end-to-end loss function. Our objective is to maximize the mutual information between the transmitted bit sequence and the received log-likelihood ratios (LLRs). However, direct maximization of mutual information is intractable in general settings, especially under unknown or learned channel models. As a practical surrogate, we minimize the binary cross-entropy (BCE) loss between the transmitted bits and the predicted LLRs at the receiver output.

We express this surrogate in terms of the effective rate, and define the loss as

\mathcal{L}{\mathrm{rate}}=-\left(1-\frac{\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{BCE}}}{\ln 2}\right)=\frac{\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{BCE}}}{\ln 2}-1.

(4)

This formulation measures the deviation from ideal bitwise mutual information (i.e., 1 bit per binary symbol), and is minimized when the predicted LLRs perfectly match the transmitted bits.

We present simulation results in Sec.
IV
: our approach is particularly applicable in scenarios where pilot-based channel estimation is ineffective, such as in very high mobility.

Evaluation setup.

We conduct extensive experiments to evaluate our proposed neural modulation approach against multiple baselines. Our simulation environment follows the system model introduced in Section

To reduce training complexity, we omit LDPC encoding/decoding during training and reintroduce a rate-1/2 LDPC code (blocklength equal to the total number of resource elements in the OFDM grid) only at inference. Simulations are implemented using the GPU-accelerated Sionna framework

To ensure a fair comparison, we allocate an identical total parameter budget to both our end-to-end neural link and the QAM + neural receiver (NRx) baseline. In this way, any performance differences arise from how complexity is apportioned between transmitter and receiver, rather than from an overall parameter increase. We evaluate performance at three mobility regimes - low speeds (
10

22

40

88

60

132

60

Under high mobility, the wireless channel varies significantly between consecutive symbols, necessitating a large number of pilot symbols for reliable channel estimation. However, increasing pilot density reduces spectral efficiency, presenting a fundamental tradeoff. This motivates the need for designs that perform reliably under limited pilot overhead. To study this tradeoff, we evaluate system performance under three pilot configurations: (i) a dense setting with two pilot symbols (2P) inserted at the third and twelfth OFDM symbols, (ii) a sparse single-pilot (1P) setting with a single pilot at the third symbol, and (iii) a fully pilotless configuration.

IV-A

Pilot-rich: Deep-OFDM offers no additional gains

We begin by examining the performance of Deep-OFDM in a moderately pilot-rich regime, where two pilot symbols are inserted at the third and twelfth OFDM symbols (2P). This configuration provides sufficiently frequent channel state updates to the receiver and serves as a standard baseline for pilot-aided communication. The 2P results in Figure
4
show that Deep-OFDM performs almost identically to the neural receiver equipped with geometric shaping (NRx+GS), across all evaluated mobility levels.

While this may seem surprising, given that Deep-OFDM incorporates a learnable neural modulator with additional expressiveness, the result is consistent with the fact that, under moderate pilot density, the receiver has sufficient information to estimate the channel accurately. In this regime, performance is primarily driven by the neural receiver’s capacity to exploit pilot observations and adapt to residual channel variations. Since the pilots already provide reliable CSI, transmitter-side enhancements such as time-frequency mixing contribute little beyond what the receiver can already achieve. As a result, Deep-OFDM and NRx+GS converge to similar performance, indicating that the transmitter’s role becomes secondary when CSI is already well-resolved. This observation suggests that the primary benefits of Deep-OFDM are not evident in pilot-rich settings, where conventional receiver-side adaptation is already robust and dominates the overall system behavior.

IV-B

Pilot-sparse: Deep-OFDM excels at high mobility.

We now consider more challenging pilot-sparse setting, in which only a single pilot symbol is inserted (1P) at the third OFDM symbol. This configuration drastically limits the receiver’s ability to estimate and track the time-varying channel, especially at high mobility. Unlike the 2P setting, where pilot observations are sufficiently frequent to mitigate channel aging, the 1P setting requires the receiver to extrapolate CSI far beyond the pilot’s temporal vicinity. This setup more closely reflects real-world constraints in low-overhead or latency-sensitive communication systems. Figure
4
presents BLER performance under varying mobility.

Traditional approaches such as LMMSE+LS rely on explicit pilot-based estimation, which becomes increasingly inaccurate as the channel evolves between pilot and data symbols. This degradation is especially acute when pilots are sparse, as in the 1P setting, and results in significant performance loss even at moderate speeds.
In contrast, the neural receiver implicitly learns to track or interpolate channel variations across symbols, offering a form of learned channel extrapolation that remains effective well beyond the pilot’s temporal support. To further improve robustness, we incorporate geometric shaping (GS), the joint optimization of the constellation mapping alongside the receiver. Prior work has demonstrated that GS can yield notable gains in both AWGN and fading environments
[
15
,
16
,
17
]
. In our implementation, GS is applied by initializing the constellation with a standard QAM layout and treating the constellation points as trainable parameters during end-to-end training. While this setup performs well at low to moderate Doppler speeds, its performance deteriorates significantly at higher mobility—for instance, at 60 m/s.

Beyond geometric shaping, Deep-OFDM introduces additional degrees of freedom across both time and frequency dimensions, enabling the system to better cope with mobility-induced distortions. While the performance gains at low Doppler shifts (e.g., 10 m/s) are modest, the advantages of neural modulation become increasingly pronounced as Doppler frequencies rise. For instance, at higher speeds (e.g., 60 m/s), neural modulation yields a substantial improvement in both BLER and spectral efficiency (Figure
4
). Notably, the performance gap between Deep-OFDM and NRx is more pronounced under the single-pilot setting than under the two-pilot case, highlighting the value of learned modulation when CSI is scarce.

To quantify overall system efficiency, we report the achieved goodput, defined as

\text{Goodput}=R\cdot\rho\cdot(1-\text{BLER}),

(5)

where

R
denotes the transmission rate in bits per channel use, and

\rho
is the fraction of symbols allocated to data (excluding pilots). Goodput captures the effective throughput after accounting for pilot overhead and decoding errors. When more time-frequency slots are occupied by pilots, as in traditional orthogonal pilot schemes,
\rho
decreases, directly reducing goodput even if BLER remains unchanged. Additionally, for fixed modulation order and channel conditions, lower coding rates result in a smaller
R
, constraining goodput despite potential improvements in reliability.

The difficulty of reliable channel estimation at high Doppler is underscored by the observation that the standalone NRx achieves nearly the same goodput at 60 m/s with one pilot (1P) as with two pilots (2P). This stagnation indicates that simply increasing pilot density does not sufficiently mitigate mobility impairments. In contrast, our end-to-end neural link achieves significantly higher goodput than both NRx-1P and NRx-2P, despite maintaining the same pilot overhead as NRx-1P. These results highlight the efficacy of transmitter-side adaptation in challenging channel conditions.

Finally, despite operating under the same parameter budget, Deep-OFDM consistently outperforms the QAM + NRx baseline across all mobility levels. This highlights a key architectural insight: jointly optimizing transmitter and receiver yields better robustness than concentrating model capacity entirely at the receiver. While prior work has shown that large neural receivers alone can achieve robustness
[
16
]
, our design achieves similar or better performance with far fewer parameters.

IV-C

Deep-OFDM enables pilotless communication.

We now evaluate the performance of neural modulation in the complete absence of pilot signaling. Operating without pilots is particularly challenging in wireless systems, as conventional coherent receivers rely heavily on pilot symbols for channel estimation and synchronization. Without this reference information, traditional techniques such as least-squares or LMMSE estimation break down, rendering standard OFDM pipelines ineffective. Even learned receivers such as NRx fail to generalize in this setting, as they are designed to consume explicitly labeled pilot positions as part of their input. Despite these challenges, pilotless communication is highly desirable in practical systems, especially in ultra-low latency, short-packet, or bandwidth-constrained applications, where dedicating time-frequency resources to pilots can significantly degrade throughput.

One approach to this problem is the use of superimposed pilots (SIP)
[
16
]
, where reference signals are embedded within the same resource elements used for data transmission. In this scheme, a BPSK-modulated pilot matrix
P\in{\pm 1}^{N\times M}
is defined, and an energy allocation matrix A is learned.

\sqrt{1-A}\cdot X+\sqrt{A}\cdot P
is transmitted. Unlike orthogonal pilot schemes, which reserve dedicated symbols or subcarriers, SIP entangles pilots and data across the full frame, requiring the receiver to jointly disentangle channel effects and recover the transmitted bits. This approach offers a pilotless-like behavior while still embedding a learnable structure.

In contrast, our Deep-OFDM system is trained and evaluated in a fully pilotless configuration: the neural modulator operates over the entire time-frequency grid without any reserved pilot symbols or pilot-specific channels. No explicit reference structure is present during training; instead, the system learns to implicitly encode features that facilitate robust demodulation and channel compensation. This setup more closely mimics true unsupervised signaling and pushes the burden of channel adaptivity entirely onto the learned waveform. We compare pilotless Deep-OFDM with SIP and LMMSE using perfect CSI. Figure
5
demonstrates the robustness of our neural modulation approach across different mobility scenarios. Within the training range (0–40 m/s), SIP and pilotless modulation deliver similar reliability and goodput. However, Deep-OFDM exhibits superior generalization to unseen channel conditions; indeed, when the relative speed between the UE and the base station is 60 m/s, beyond the training distribution, neural modulation outperforms SIP.

Remarkably, despite using no pilots, the pilotless Deep-OFDM system matches the performance of a GS+NRx system with two pilots, achieving a
14\%
increase in goodput.
In addition to performance, Deep-OFDM also offers architectural flexibility. Owing to its convolutional structure, a model trained on one OFDM frame size naturally generalizes to configurations with different numbers of subcarriers. In contrast, SIP requires a separately trained pilot pattern for each OFDM frame configuration. We analyze the mechanism of the achieved pilotless communication in Section
V

IV-D

Performance of Deep-OFDM under MIMO

We evaluate the Deep-OFDM framework in a downlink MIMO configuration with four transmit antennas at the base station and a single receive antenna at the UE. The system employs zero-forcing equalization with perfect knowledge of only the diagonal elements of the frequency-domain channel matrix
\mathbf{H}_{f}
. In contrast, the off-diagonal elements, which capture ICI, remain unknown. We refer to this as
incomplete CSI
. This assumption reflects practical limitations where estimating the full
N\times N
frequency-domain channel matrix scales as
\mathcal{O}(N^{2})
, making complete CSI acquisition computationally prohibitive for large subcarrier counts in high-mobility scenarios. We maintain the modulation operation exclusively across the time and frequency dimensions, avoiding modulation across the antenna dimension to isolate modulation gains from MIMO precoding effects.

Fig.
6
presents results for a UE velocity of 60 m/s, which introduces significant time-domain channel variations within each OFDM symbol. Under these conditions, the incomplete CSI assumption severely degrades conventional OFDM performance due to uncompensated ICI from off-diagonal channel coupling. Deep-OFDM maintains consistent gains over traditional OFDM with a neural receiver (NRx) despite identical model complexity, demonstrating its ability to implicitly learn and compensate for the missing channel correlation structure through its neural network architecture.

V-B

Time-Frequency mixing

In the pilotless communication scenario discussed previously, we demonstrated that Deep-OFDM without pilots achieves a BLER performance comparable to traditional OFDM communication systems using two pilot symbols. We attributed this ability to the emergence of implicit pilots learned by the neural modulator.
This subsection quantifies the additional gains achieved via the time-frequency diversity provided by Deep-OFDM’s convolutional structure, beyond the implicit pilot mechanism.

Learning SIP along with Deep-OFDM.

We now investigate the combination of Deep-OFDM with explicit SIP. As detailed in Section
IV-C
, the SIP technique explicitly computes a linear combination of input data symbols and a predetermined pilot sequence to embed pilot information directly within the transmitted data symbols. Instead of using a conventionally modulated symbol matrix
X
, we use the output of the Deep-OFDM modulator, denoted
X_{m}
, as the data-bearing signal. The transmitted signal is thus formed as:

\tilde{X}=\sqrt{1-A}\cdot X_{m}+\sqrt{A}\cdot P

where
P\in\{\pm 1\}^{N\times M}
is a fixed BPSK-modulated pilot matrix, and
A\in[0,1]^{N\times M}
is a learnable energy allocation matrix.

This formulation allows us to combine the structured pilot design of SIP with the expressive power of Deep-OFDM. As demonstrated in Figure
9
, the Deep-OFDM + SIP system achieves significantly better performance than SIP alone. The performance gains of Deep-OFDM can thus be attributed to two complementary mechanisms: (1) the learning of implicit pilot-like features embedded in the Deep-OFDM signal, and (2) the exploitation of time-frequency diversity enabled by the convolutional architecture. Together, these effects enable the model to learn redundancy and structure tailored for time-varying Doppler-OFDM channels.
This experiment demonstrates that the structure of SIP remains valuable and that time-frequency mixing further enhances its effectiveness.

Interpreting the time-frequency diversity.

To better understand the source of time-frequency diversity, we consider a simplified modulation architecture. Specifically, we design a modulator that consists of an
m
-QAM mapper followed by a single-layer linear convolutional neural network with a
5\times 5
kernel, and batch normalization. This setup allows us to isolate and interpret the structure learned by the convolutional kernel.

Although this simplified architecture has reduced expressivity compared to the full Deep-OFDM system, it still achieves non-trivial performance in the pilotless setting. The network operates on I/Q symbols produced by the
m
-QAM mapper and outputs a transformed I/Q signal. Since there are 2 input and 2 output channels, the convolutional layer learns a total of 4 distinct
5\times 5
kernels: one for each input-output pairing (
I\to I,I\to Q,Q\to I,Q\to Q
). We visualize these in Figure
11
, where we show the normalized energy of the learned kernel weights.

Interestingly, we observe that the "diagonal" kernels, those corresponding to
I\to I
and
Q\to Q
, exhibit significant energy in the off-center positions. Specifically, only 42% and 67% of the total energy in the
I\to I
and
Q\to Q
kernels, respectively, is concentrated at the center of the kernel. This indicates that the learned filters are not localized in time and frequency but instead exploit a broader receptive field. This is direct evidence of time-frequency mixing: the modulator learns to distribute information across both time and frequency dimensions rather than treating each time-frequency bin independently.

While the single convolutional layer can learn basic time-frequency mixing patterns, its limited depth restricts its capacity to capture more complex dependencies. In contrast, the full Deep-OFDM architecture, with its deeper convolutional stack, is significantly more expressive. As shown in Figure

To further probe the importance of spatial structure, we consider a modified version of the Deep-OFDM modulator in which all convolutional kernels are replaced with 1×1 kernels. This architectural change effectively removes the network’s ability to mix across time and frequency, constraining it to point-wise transformations.

The results, also presented in Figure
10
, show a clear degradation in performance when using 1×1 kernels. Unlike the 5×5 kernels that learn to mix and aggregate information across neighboring time-frequency bins, 1×1 kernels treat each bin independently and are unable to exploit spatial correlations. These findings confirm that the spatial receptive field provided by larger convolutional kernels and the resulting time-frequency mixing patterns revealed in our kernel visualizations are fundamental to the robustness and generalization ability of Deep-OFDM under high-mobility conditions.

Probing the role of time-frequency structure.

To further demonstrate the robustness of Deep-OFDM’s time-frequency structure exploitation, we perform a series of controlled experiments that perturb the T-F grid during training or evaluation.

First, we introduce random
masking
in the time-frequency grid during training: a fixed subset of RE columns or rows are zeroed out or blocked in each training iteration, forcing the model to learn representations that are robust to missing local structure. Interestingly, this yields performance nearly identical to standard training, as demonstrated in Figure

Second, to test whether locality in the time-frequency grid is essential to Deep-OFDM’s performance, we apply a fixed random permutation to the 2D resource grid. This permutation scrambles the spatial arrangement of REs before modulation, and the inverse is applied before decoding, ensuring the information content is preserved while disrupting spatial locality. As observed in Figure
13
, performance degrades significantly. This is expected: Doppler-induced distortion tends to occur across adjacent subcarriers, and the neural receiver is a convolutional architecture with a fixed local receptive field. Permuting the grid destroys locality, breaking the spatial correlations the receiver relies on. Hence, for Deep-OFDM to perform well, the modulator must learn a representation that is
matched to the inductive bias
of the receiver.

Together, these experiments reinforce that Deep-OFDM learns to exploit local structure in the time-frequency domain, not just for redundancy, but in a way that is compatible with the convolutional receiver.

Ablation studies

This section provides detailed ablation studies to understand the key components contributing to Deep-OFDM’s performance.

VI-A

Neural modulation complexity

In practical deployment scenarios, computational constraints at the transmitter may limit the complexity of neural modulators that can be implemented in real-time. To understand the trade-off between network size and performance, we systematically evaluate Deep-OFDM with reduced architectural complexity.

2\times 36

\{16,32\}

As shown in Figure

In contrast, reducing the number of ResNet blocks (i.e., reducing network depth) leads to noticeably larger performance drops, underscoring that depth is more critical than width in maintaining modulation quality.

This observation is consistent with findings from the neural network pruning literature, which suggest that network width is often less critical than depth for achieving strong representational power
[

. In our setting, the relatively high robustness to width reduction implies that Deep-OFDM’s performance is driven more by its ability to compose expressive transformations over layers, rather than by sheer filter count. This supports the feasibility of deploying lightweight neural modulators in resource-constrained transmitters without significantly sacrificing reliability.

VI-B

Effect of Non-linearities

To better understand the role of non-linearities in Deep-OFDM, we study a linear variant of the architecture in which all ReLU activations are removed. That is, we retain the same convolutional structure and batch normalization (BN) as described in Section
III-B

Prior work has shown that linear convolutional networks can retain some expressive power, particularly when batch normalization is present
[
28

29

In our experiments, we observe a clear degradation in performance when ReLUs are removed from Deep-OFDM, indicating that non-linear transformations are essential for the modulator to learn sufficiently expressive representations.

VI-C

Evaluating different code lengths and rates

This configuration provided a natural baseline for assessing system-level throughput/goodput, as it ensures that the entire OFDM frame is utilized efficiently without the need for code block segmentation or padding.

VII
Conclusion and Remarks

In this work, we introduced Deep-OFDM, a learnable neural modulation scheme that enhances OFDM by performing trainable time-frequency spreading of transmit symbols, explicitly designed to align with the inductive biases of CNN-based receivers. Through joint end-to-end optimization of a low-complexity neural modulator and receiver, Deep-OFDM achieves substantial performance gains in doubly-selective channels, with the most significant improvements manifesting under pilot-sparse and pilotless communication scenarios. Remarkably, the proposed scheme maintains high performance even in completely pilotless configurations with only modest degradation compared to pilot-assisted systems, thereby substantially improving spectral efficiency across various scenarios, including MIMO antenna systems. Our systematic analysis reveals that Deep-OFDM learns implicit pilot structures and exploits time-frequency diversity through convolutional mixing, effectively embedding channel estimation functionality within the modulation process itself. Through comprehensive ablation studies, we quantified the role of nonlinear activation functions in neural modulation and characterized the performance-complexity trade-offs across varying model architectures, with analysis of the learned time-frequency mixing patterns providing valuable insights into the system’s robust performance. The demonstrated ability of Deep-OFDM to reduce pilot overhead while maintaining reliable communication performance opens promising avenues for future research. Key directions include developing analytical approximations for learned behaviors
[
30
,
31
]
, further reducing transmitter-side complexity through quantized low-bit neural networks
[
32
]
, and investigating generalization capabilities across diverse channel distributions via multi-objective optimization frameworks. These advances are crucial steps toward the practical deployment of neural modulation schemes, where computational efficiency, analytical tractability, and robust performance across varying operating conditions remain paramount considerations for real-world wireless communication systems.
