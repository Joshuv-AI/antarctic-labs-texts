Five values for COVID-19 vaccine acceptance described by racial and ethnic minoritized caregivers
Authors: Sonal Jain, Rohan Dayal, Jennifer Sikov, Imme Kobayashi, Meera Savage, Valeria Ladino, Emelie Poston, J Krystel Loubeau, Nadia Garriga-Cerni, Jessica Kabrt, Andrea E Spencer
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackground and objectives:Persistent racial and ethnic disparities in child COVID-19 vaccination rates in the US suggest that vaccinated racial and ethnic minoritized caregivers have hesitated to vaccinate their children. To address these disparities, we sought to understand perspectives on the COVID-19 vaccine among racial and ethnic minoritized caregivers of school-aged children.Methods:We recruited English, Spanish, and Haitian Creole-speaking caregivers of children ages 5–11 years-old seen for pediatric care at a safety-net hospital. We surveyed caregivers on vaccination status, demographics, and conducted semi-structured interviews regarding vaccine perception. We analyzed interviews using thematic analysis with grounded theory principles.Results:We interviewed 20 caregivers (62% Non-Hispanic Black, 29% Hispanic). Caregivers had all received at least one vaccine dose and 62% of children were vaccinated against COVID-19. Caregivers discussed five core values used to appraise the COVID-19 vaccine: Safety, Knowledge, Trust, Humanity, and Autonomy. These five core values were used in decision-making about vaccination; ongoing appraisal of the vaccine after their own vaccination; and in consideration of the vaccine for their children.Conclusions:We conclude that public health campaigns attuned to these core values may be more accepting to families and help to reduce perpetuation of healthcare mistrust and persistent health disparities among racial and ethnic minoritized families.

Introduction

The coronavirus disease 2019 (COVID-19) pandemic caused by the severe acute respiratory syndrome coronavirus 2 (SARS-CoV-2) had an estimated mortality rate of 14.83 million deaths worldwide in 2020 and 2021 [1]. On December 11, 2020, the US Food and Drug Administration granted an emergency-use authorization for the country’s first COVID-19 vaccine for adults [2]. Within 7 months, nearly 80% of the US population had received at least one dose, and within the first year, these vaccines had prevented an estimated 14.4 million deaths globally [3]. On October 29th, 2021, children over 5 years old became eligible for the Pfizer-BioNTech COVID-19 vaccine under emergency use authorization [4].

Despite this success, the COVID-19 vaccine roll-out faced skepticism and concern about worsening health disparities. Racial and ethnic minoritized individuals in the US, who were more likely to be hospitalized and die from COVID-19 compared to White individuals [5], were also more vaccine hesitant and less likely to receive the vaccine. [6–8] Although these disparities have decreased over time among adults [9], by May 2023, only about one-third (32.9%) of children aged 5–11 had been vaccinated, [10] with particularly low rates among Black and Hispanic populations (8% and 21.7%, respectively). [11] Public health efforts to improve vaccination rates and reduce disparities in adults have not been as effective in children, who experienced more difficulty accessing vaccination, communication barriers, and hesitancy around vaccination [12]. Parental decision making around childhood COVID-19 vaccination may also be shaped by the perception that children are at lower risk of severe COVID-19 outcomes, reducing the perceived urgency of vaccination and contributing to hesitancy [13]. As of 2025–2026, U.S. guidance recommends COVID-19 vaccination for children through shared clinical decision-making between caregivers and healthcare providers, rather than a routine requirement, highlighting the ongoing importance of understanding factors that influence vaccine acceptance and persistent disparities among minoritized families. Additionally, understanding the experiences of minoritized families with the COVID-19 vaccine and their perspectives on policies that led to improved vaccine uptake among adults will better inform future equitable vaccination efforts.

We sought to understand vaccine acceptance, hesitancy, and decision-making by interviewing minoritized caregivers of school-aged children. Through this research, we aim to develop a framework for targeted public health strategies that promote equitable vaccine distribution and acceptance in these communities.

Methods

Recruitment and setting

We recruited participants from a cohort that previously participated in a longitudinal survey study on child mental health during COVID-19 [14]. The sample consisted of English, Spanish, and Haitian Creole-speaking caregivers of children aged 5–11 from a large, urban, academic, safety-net hospital and its affiliated health centers between September 2019 and February 2020. These participants were invited to participate in this qualitative study if they completed the final survey and agreed to be re-contacted for future research. The Boston University Medical Campus Institutional Review Board approved this research.

Researcher identities and reflexivity

Our reporting follows the consolidated criteria for reporting qualitative research (COREQ) framework (supplemental file 1). The Principal Investigator (PI) was a resident psychiatrist (SJ) mentored by an attending child and adolescent psychiatrist and mixed methods researcher (AS). Other researchers included a resident psychiatrist (JK), graduate-level research assistants in public health (IK and JKL) and psychology (JS), and undergraduate research assistants (RD, VL, EP, MS, NGC). No study team clinicians (AS, SJ, JK) had known clinical relationships with participants.

Enrollment and study procedures

Research staff completed informed consent via videoconferencing or telephone and collected electronic signatures through REDCap (Research Electronic Data Capture) [15]. Participants completed a survey on vaccination status and demographics [16] (supplemental files 2 and 3) and participated in a semi-structured in-depth interview (supplemental file 4) that assessed perspectives on the COVID-19 vaccine, vaccine experiences, culture and background, and vaccine information. Research staff (JS, RD, VL, EP) and the PI (SJ) conducted in-depth interviews. Participants received a $20 gift card. Enrollment of participants continued until the point of thematic saturation, i.e., when no new themes emerged.

Data analysis

We used R version 4.2.1 [17] with the “tidyverse” package [18] to summarize quantitative data. We analyzed qualitative data via thematic analysis [19]. Interviews were recorded, transcribed, translated, verified, and de-identified prior to analysis (RD, SJ, VL, EP). We developed the initial codebook via collaborative reflexivity: two authors independently reviewed the first eight transcripts prior to reviewing during team discussions (AS, SJ, RD, JS, EP, IK, MS, NGC). Two coders (SJ and RD) coded all 20 interviews with NVivo 11 software [20]. We calculated intercoder reliability (ICR) with Cohen’s Kappa coefficient [21]. Initially, interviews were double-coded until coders maintained kappa>80%. Remaining interviews were coded independently, with every third interview double-coded to ensure kappa>80%. While maintaining coding reliability is not necessary for thematic analysis, we used this strategy to support coders’ full use of the codebook, facilitate discussions about the meaning of the codes, and deepen understanding of the codes. We developed themes as a group and used axial coding to discuss relationships between themes and finalize analyses.

Thematic analysis

Caregivers discussed five core values (Fig. 1) that contributed to their perception of the COVID-19 vaccine: Safety, Knowledge, Trust, Humanity, and Autonomy. Themes were conceptualized as core value domains that shaped vaccine decision-making, while factors such as prior experiences, misinformation, and adverse effects were understood as influencing these domains rather than constituting separate themes. Participants described how these intersecting values were influenced by personal factors such as background, identity, self-preservation, and family protection; as well as external factors such as information sources and historical and current experiences with health systems, COVID-19, and vaccination.

Many caregivers described weighing the safety of the vaccine vs. contracting COVID-19 and decided to vaccinate to protect themselves and their family against COVID-19.

Knowledge

Caregivers relied on background knowledge about vaccines plus information they learned over time about the COVID-19 vaccine (Table 3B). They described how their background and life experiences contributed to vaccine acceptance.

When we were born in [country], we all got vaccinated as children and that’s what makes me know that the vaccines do work... growing up in [country] compared to coming here, we have a...very low percentage of children having these diseases ...So now when the COVID comes out and they’re saying this vaccine is gonna help protect you, I believe it.

Caregivers sought information from trusted sources about vaccine safety to make vaccination decisions.

At first, I didn’t want it...I was just listening to everybody telling me it was bad or whatever. And then when I caught COVID...I had to go to the hospital because I couldn’t breathe...I was like, ‘Oh, I don’t want to feel this again.’ So then, I did my own research on the vaccine. I was like, oh, well, [what] people were telling me was not true. So then, I ended up getting vaccinated myself.

Caregivers valued data on vaccine effectiveness and safety but were skeptical about safety for children while they knew studies were ongoing. Caregivers were less likely to tolerate inconsistent or uncertain information for their children than for themselves.

I had reservations because it was still in the study, and I believe that like they didn’t have enough data to kind of say like this is safe for all children and as soon as they say that it was not safe for certain ages, two weeks later they said it’s safe for certain ages and the mix of information made me unsure about giving it to [my child]. So that’s why we didn’t give it to him. Of course, I had to take it to stay [employed]...but I have less to lose than my son, who’s growing, So I would rather be the guinea pig than him.

Trust

Caregivers assessed trust by appraising whether institutions were out to harm or help them, which was influenced by cultural context and historical racism (Table 3C). Many participants described baseline distrust of medical research and health professionals due to past harms and historical wrongs. This extended to concerns of being experimented upon, intentionally infected, or not offered lifesaving treatments.

Yeah, there’s just been a lot of things that have happened in the past like the Tuskegee trials... I think that people felt the same way, like ‘hey, this is something we don’t know about the COVID vaccine. Let’s take this population, which is like minorities, and let’s get more information about it’...I think that there is a cure for HIV and AIDS, but...it’s a gimmick in the government [and] in the health sectors. It’s a money-making business. If everybody gets cured from HIV, are the doctors, the clinics, the medical facilities, are they gonna make money? No.

Some described how their identity as individuals of low socioeconomic status impacted their trust and decreased their autonomy.

The poor people are going to be the ones...they go after because...you depend on the government...they’re gonna...take this away if you don’t do [what they say]. So I just felt like this vaccine, this COVID is something like that......I don’t think it came from any animal. I think it was created...with an agenda, but who am I? I’m one of the poor people, so I have to go along with it whether I like it or not because I’m going to suffer, my kids going to suffer.

Increased exposure to medical information from trusted sources, including the recommendation to vaccinate from trusted healthcare providers, increased vaccine acceptance for caregivers and children.

The doctor talked to me well, he told me it was safe and all that. And he recommended me to do it, that we should give him the shot. Since he is going to school too, in person, and there are more children there...that’s when I decided to give him the vaccine.

Caregivers described increased trust related to cultural or familial acceptance of vaccination or specific vaccine brands.

Humanity

Caregivers emphasized the importance of humanity in communication about the vaccine, including respect and care for them as an individual person (Table 3D). Feeling unheard or disrespected was a deterrent to vaccine acceptance for caregivers and their children.

I was at the doctor’s office and they asked me, did I want to give the vaccine [to my son]...I said, no...there’s not enough data...so I’m not comfortable with that. And then, of course, the script came out well, you know, if you you’re vaccinated, blah, blah, blah, the things that we are supposed to say to hesitant person...after hearing my concerns, I felt like I wasn’t heard ... I was so annoyed with that.

Alternatively, caregivers who felt heard and treated with respect felt more inclined to accept the vaccine.

I...recently changed my doctor... and he was more attentive about my concerns...I asked him “Am I healthy enough to get the shot?” He was looking at my chart, because...I have a bunch of allergies and...he said, “You’re totally healthy enough to take the shot.” That was the most honest thing I heard.

Some caregivers felt disrespected because of their racial, ethnic, or socioeconomic background.

I’m not rich enough...that’s how I always looking at it...In my little world, my little opinion, it doesn’t matter I mean, it matters to my population...but they don’t care about what the hell I think.

Caregivers described the importance of balancing individual concerns and nuanced decision-making with the public health priority to increase vaccination rates.

The way it was implemented is the issue because it’s good for you to care about other people who are immunocompromised...But at the same time, the rollout was in such a way that people felt they had no other choice. Like even if they spoke to their doctor, their doctor was completely dismissive of their concerns because they wanted everyone to get their vaccine.

Autonomy

Caregivers overwhelmingly emphasized how they valued autonomy in decision-making about the COVID-19 vaccine (Table 3E).

I just believe it’s their own body and they are in the right position to decide if they will take it or not.

Caregivers resented loss of autonomy related to vaccine mandates.

I think that’s what most people fear of losing their job, which is not right. They were forcefully being demanded that they have to take it. That is not right. That is taking away people’s rights, and that is what the government has to stand liable for taking away people’s rights.

Caregivers frequently described the importance of being able to decide what is best for their family.

I don’t care what happens in the community. And I know that’s horrible to say, but it’s like, what is good for me is not good for another person. So, I don’t insert myself into other people’s problems. I focus on me, and my community is just me and my children.

Caregivers shared the importance of empowering their child to exercise their own autonomy about the vaccine.

All my kids ever since they were able to talk...I’ve always given them a choice....I’ve always let them have a say...It’s always been if you don’t feel like it then you can tell me so and yeah, we discussed it at home...He was proud...he got a shot and shocked me.

Discussion

We describe a new framework including five core values – Safety, Knowledge, Trust, Humanity, Autonomy - that influenced racial and ethnic minoritized caregivers’ perspectives on the COVID-19 vaccine. When these core values were upheld, caregivers expressed greater confidence in the vaccine. If these values were threatened, caregivers expressed less confidence in the vaccine, regretted getting vaccinated, or delayed vaccinating their children. We propose that public health campaigns attuned to these core values will be more accepting to racial and ethnic minoritized families in the US. Our findings corroborate previous research that describes safety and mistrust as contributors to vaccine hesitancy [22,23], and that threatening autonomy with vaccine mandates could cause public discontent including psychological reactance (i.e., how individuals resist or oppose actions perceived as threats to their freedom of choice) and anti-vaccination activism [24,25]. Our data mirrors this phenomenon, as caregivers expressed resentment when autonomy was constrained.

Although we had a sample of vaccinated caregivers, caregivers described vaccine hesitancy even after vaccination. Research on vaccine hesitancy often overlooks these “hesitant adopters.” [26,27] One study found that 60% of recently vaccinated Americans – more commonly Black than White Americans – expressed vaccine hesitancy [26]. Post-vaccination hesitancy has implications for future decision-making about vaccines, including for children. In our study, 40% of vaccinated caregivers had not vaccinated their children, and US child COVID-19 vaccination rates are substantially under adult rates [28]. Post-vaccination hesitancy may reflect and reinforce mistrust of healthcare institutions by minoritized individuals.

While some concerns related to Safety, Knowledge, Trust, Humanity, and Autonomy may also be experienced by non-minority caregivers, our data suggests that these values are particularly shaped and amplified by experiences of systemic racism among racial and ethnic minoritized families. Historical harms, inequities in healthcare access, and cultural mistrust influenced participants’ vaccine decision-making in ways that differ from non-minority caregivers (Table 3). As a result of systemic racism, people of color have been historically treated inhumanely and unethically, had their autonomy undermined by White people in positions of power, been restricted from knowledge needed to make informed decisions, had their safety compromised, and developed a deep mistrust of healthcare and systems of power. Public health policies that do not align with these values could perpetuate systemic racism and increase mistrust in public health systems. Conversely, policies implemented according to these values could not only be more equitable and effective but also could build trust in public health systems.

To improve alignment with these values, we must end economic and social inequities created by systemic racism by investing in communities of color to create conditions that promote health. Vaccination efforts enacted within an inequitable society could perpetuate inequities that go beyond disparities in percent vaccinated. To combat the systemic racism that underlies mistrust in public health system and fuels inequities, communities of color should be resourced and empowered to lead public health efforts [29]. Public health campaigns that are led by communities strengthen community autonomy, demonstrate empathy and respect for the community (humanity), improve trust due to local leadership, and can effectively disseminate accurate knowledge.

Our study has some limitations. We recruited caregivers from one longitudinal study at one safety-net hospital system, limiting generalizability. Participants were drawn from a cohort who had agreed to be re-contacted for research. While this may introduce some degree of selection bias, the sample provides valuable insights into vaccine decision-making among racial and ethnic minoritized caregivers. Our sample included only vaccinated caregivers; while this is a limitation, it also contributes a unique perspective to the literature on vaccine hesitancy. Additionally, single-site qualitative research can have inherent limitations. While the findings provide valuable insights into vaccine decision-making, they may not fully capture the perspectives of all racial and ethnic minoritized caregivers or those in different geographic or healthcare settings. Despite these limitations, strengths of our study include recruitment of a diverse sample of racial and ethnic minoritized caregivers and the use of rigorous qualitative methods to develop a theory of vaccine appraisal that could be tested in other settings and inform future public health efforts. Future research should replicate this study in other settings, ideally across multiple sites, to triangulate these findings. Furthermore, additional studies incorporating mixed methods with a larger sample size could extend these findings by helping clarify how these values related to specific vaccine decisions. Such studies should also encompass both routine and novel childhood and adolescent immunizations to understand which values may be specific to COVID-19 vaccination, and which extend to childhood vaccination in general.

Conclusion

This study gives insight into COVID-19 vaccine perspectives among racial and ethnic minoritized families and provides a framework that could guide equitable public health strategies. We posit that public health campaigns that take these values into account would also help to address health inequities rooted in systemic racism.
