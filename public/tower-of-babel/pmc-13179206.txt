The effect of bite planes on deepbite correction with clear aligners
Authors: Bingguo Kou, Yimeng Xu, Qiong Liu, Zexuan Yang, Yue Hui, Siyu Lan, Wenmin Ye, Qian Liu, Zexu Gu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ObjectivesDeep overbite correction with clear aligners faces challenges including decreased anterior intrusion efficiency and spontaneous molar intrusion. Bite planes are often incorporated to address these issues, but their effects remain debated. This study aimed to investigate the impact of bite planes on anterior intrusion efficiency and posterior displacement during a specific deep overbite correction phase using clear aligners.Materials and methodsThis retrospective study included 36 adult patients with deep overbite who were treated with the clear aligner (Smartee® system) divided into a bite plane group (n = 15) and a control group (n = 21). Pre- (T1) and post-intrusion (T2) digital models were superimposed to measure tooth displacement and subsequently calculate intrusion accuracy of anterior teeth. Comparisons of tooth displacement and intrusion accuracy between the two groups were performed using an independent t-test or a Mann-Whitney U test, intragroup vertical changes of posterior teeth were analyzed by one-way analysis of variance, and Pearson correlation was used to assess the relationship between molar intrusion and treatment duration.ResultsNo significant difference was found in anterior intrusion accuracy between the bite plane group (18.8% ± 24.7%) and the control group (18.4% ± 21.6%). The bucco-lingual movement of teeth where bite planes were designed was not significantly affected. Premolars in both groups exhibited extrusion ranging from 0.0 to 0.5 mm, with no significant intergroup difference. Molars in the control group exhibited 0.1–0.2 mm of spontaneous intrusion (P<0.01), which showed a positive correlation with treatment duration. The maxillary molar intrusion correlated moderately with the number of aligners (r = 0.49, P<0.0001), while the correlation was weaker in the mandible (r = 0.33, P = 0.0031). In contrast, the bite plane group showed no spontaneous molar intrusion but rather slight extrusion of 0.1–0.2 mm, with no correlation with treatment duration.ConclusionBite planes in clear aligners do not enhance anterior intrusion efficiency or affect labiolingual movement but effectively prevent spontaneous molar intrusion caused by the “posterior bite-block effect,” thereby helping maintain vertical molar position during deep overbite correction.Clinical relevanceThe bite planes in clear aligners are less effective in inducing posterior teeth extrusion and the associated increase in vertical facial height compared to those in fixed appliances. However, they do not affect the intrusion and labiolingual movement of anterior teeth, and prevent spontaneous intrusion of molars, making them a recommended design for deepbite correction in both normodivergent and hypodivergent patients.

Introduction

Deep overbite is a common type of malocclusion with a high prevalence among population. A meta-analysis indicated that although the reported prevalence rates vary significantly across studies due to differences in methodology and ethnic groups, the overall prevalence was approximately 21% [1, 2]. Deep overbite not only affects aesthetic appearance [3], but may also cause functional impairments including palatal mucosal trauma, insufficient space for restorations, and accelerated occlusal wear [4, 5]. Common therapeutic measures for correcting deep overbite include anterior intrusion, posterior extrusion, or their combination [6, 7]. In particularly severe skeletal cases, treatment may even necessitate combined orthodontic-orthopedic intervention or orthodontic-orthognathic surgery, depending on the patient’s age and growth potential [8, 9].

With the advancement of clear aligner (CA) technology, its application has evolved from initially treating mild malocclusions and simple alignment issues to addressing various complex malocclusions, achieving diverse types of tooth movements [10]. However, significant differences exist between CAs and fixed appliances in the treatment of deep overbite. As removable appliances, CAs prevent tooth eruption by covering the occlusal surfaces of the upper and lower dentition. Studies have revealed that during CA therapy, spontaneous molar intrusion—a phenomenon that may occur in both maxillary and mandibular molars—may compromise the predictability of deep overbite correction, warranting particular attention in the management of such cases [11–14]. Moreover, CAs demonstrate limited efficiency in achieving anterior intrusion [15–17]. The relative intrusion accuracy of mandibular anterior teeth was approximately 65% and 45% in adolescent and adult patients, respectively, with the amount of intrusion achieved for mandibular incisors ranging from 1 to 2 mm [15]. The accuracy was even lower for maxillary anterior teeth, with central incisors achieving only 13.3% of the planned intrusion [16]. Due to the inherent characteristics of CAs—exhibiting spontaneous molar intrusion and decreased efficiency in anterior tooth intrusion—vertical control in deep overbite correction poses greater challenges compared to fixed appliances. Overall, the actual reduction in deep overbite achieved with CAs ranges only from 27% to 49% of the planned reduction [16, 18, 19]. The correction of deep overbite is primarily achieved through relative intrusion of the upper and lower anterior teeth, while posterior extrusion contributes minimally and may even impede the process.

To address the limitations of CAs in deep overbite correction, clinicians, researchers, and manufacturers have proposed various strategies to enhance their efficacy. These include implementing over-correction in anterior intrusion design to compensate for inadequate intrusion efficiency [14], optimizing attachment design [20], combining aligners with temporary anchorage devices (TADs) to facilitate anterior intrusion [21, 22], and utilizing bite plane (BP) (e.g., Invisalign’s bite ramp) to assist in bite opening. The BPs in CAs are designed to mimic the morphology and function of traditional bite plates or bite turbos used in fixed appliances, aiming to perform similar functions in deep overbite correction. However, due to the occlusal coverage on both dental arches by the aligners—which impedes tooth eruption—along with the material properties of the aligners, their influence on tooth movement in the upper and lower dental arches differs significantly from that of conventional appliances. Current researches on BPs in CAs remain divided regarding their effectiveness in deep overbite correction. Some studies suggest that BPs can significantly enhance the efficiency and effectiveness of deep overbite treatment, recommending their use in CA therapy for deep overbite cases [19, 23, 24]. However, other studies indicate no significant difference in deep overbite improvement with or without BPs [18, 25, 26]. Furthermore, the aforementioned studies exhibit methodological limitations for various reasons. First, the use of lateral cephalogram superimposition for evaluating tooth position changes demonstrates suboptimal sensitivity in detecting positional changes in three-dimensional space, while being limited to assessing only the central incisors and first molars. Comprehensive evaluation of all teeth within the dental arches requires three-dimensional models (such as CBCT or digital models) to enhance both accuracy and comprehensiveness of measurements. Second, most studies analyzed treatment changes either before and after treatment or following the first series of aligners, during which multiple types of tooth movement typically occur simultaneously across most or all teeth in both arches, introducing multiple confounding factors. Third, the majority of research has focused primarily on evaluating the effectiveness of BPs on deep overbite correction, without directly comparing the differential effects on tooth movement between cases with and without BPs within the same study.

Therefore, this study tested the null hypothesis that BPs do not significantly affect anterior intrusion efficiency or the vertical position of posterior anchorage teeth during deep overbite correction with CAs. By superimposing three-dimensional digital models obtained before and after a specific anterior intrusion phase, we compared outcomes between cases with and without BPs, thereby clarifying their roles in deep overbite correction with CAs.

Method

The study protocol was approved by the Medical Ethics Committee of the Fourth Military Medical University (Approval No. KQ-YJ-2025-198). This retrospective study included patients with deep overbite who were treated with the Smartee® system (Shanghai Smartee Medical Equipment Co., Ltd., Shanghai, China) at the Department of Orthodontics, School of Stomatology, Fourth Military Medical University, between 2017 and 2024. All treatments were performed by a professor specializing in CA research and clinical practice who has over 30 years of orthodontic experience and has been using CAs clinically since 2004. The strategy for managing deep overbite cases can be summarized through three key characteristics. First, horizontal rectangular attachments are designed on the first and second premolars, while molar attachments—including their presence and type—are determined according to crown length and retention requirements. If BPs were utilized, they were placed on canines, incisors, or both based on the anterior overjet to ensure contact with the lower anterior teeth. Second, over-correction was incorporated for anterior deep overbite. Typically, the anterior overbite was designed to an edge-to-edge or a 1–2 mm open bite, based on the initial severity of the deep overbite. Third, the treatment process was generally divided into three phases: initial alignment, correction of deep overbite through intrusion of the upper and/or lower anterior teeth as planned, and final occlusal detailing. This study examined the dental changes occurring before (T1) and after (T2) the phase involving intrusion of the upper and lower anterior teeth to correct deep overbite.

Patients meeting the following criteria were included in the study: (1) Adult patients requiring correction of deep overbite through anterior intrusion; (2) Complete permanent dentition with fully erupted second molars in occlusion, absent of missing teeth or morphological dental anomalies; (3) Absence of crowding in the anterior teeth, and no IPR needed; (4) Included anterior teeth with prescribed intrusion ≥ 1 mm and bucco-lingual movement < 1 mm; (5) Treatment performed exclusively with CAs without additional auxiliaries, and proper seating of aligners throughout the observed phase; (6) No use of class II elastics; (7) Availability of pre-treatment lateral cephalograms and accurate digital models before (T1) and after (T2) the intrusion phase; (8) Good compliance with aligner wear (≥ 20 h daily) and timely replacement every 7–10 days. Cases not meeting the inclusion criteria or presenting with any of the following conditions were excluded: (1) Morphological abnormalities of the hard palate or palatal rugae due to cleft palate, trauma, or other causes; (2) Periodontitis patients with moderate or severe gingival recession; (3) Systemic diseases or conditions affecting tooth movement; (4) History of previous orthodontic treatment.

Based on the established inclusion and exclusion criteria, a total of 36 cases were collected, comprising 15 in the BP group and 21 in the control group. Previous studies have reported spontaneous molar intrusion with CAs, showing intrusion of maxillary and mandibular first molars by 0.98 ± 0.54 mm (mean ± SD) and 0.84 ± 0.29 mm, respectively [11]. In contrast, another study demonstrated that when utilizing bite ramps for deep overbite correction, U6-PP showed elongation of 0.35 ± 0.57 mm while L6-MP elongated by 0.58 ± 0.76 mm [24]. Based on the means and standard deviations from these two studies, sample size calculation was performed using PASS software (PASS 2021, NCSS, LLC) with a significance level (α) of 0.05 and power (β) of 0.9. The minimum required sample size was determined to be 18 cases (9 per group).

Pretreatment lateral cephalograms of subjects from both groups were collected to measure and analyze baseline data, while digital models obtained before (T1) and after (T2) the tooth intrusion phase were used to assess positional changes of the teeth in both groups. The procedure for evaluating tooth positional changes and anterior intrusion efficiency from T1 to T2 primarily included: digital model superimposition, establishment of a three-dimensional coordinate system, selection of dental measurement landmarks, and calculation of tooth displacement and intrusion efficiency.

Maxillary digital models were superimposed using the palatal vault as a stable reference area to align T1 and T2 models [27]. The T1 and T2 digital model files in .stl format were imported into ProPlan CMF 3.0 software (Materialise, Belgium). The same palatal vault region was selected as the reference area for model superimposition (Fig. 1A and B) [27]. The superimposed models were exported simultaneously and imported into Geomagic Studio 2013 (Geomagic Inc., USA) to verify the reliability of the superimposition (Fig. 1D). A larger area of predominantly green color-coded zones across the palatal rugae and vault, particularly when symmetrically distributed bilaterally, indicated a higher degree of correspondence between the two models. Only T1 and T2 models meeting this criterion were used for subsequent measurements. Mandibular digital models were superimposed using the best-fit alignment method to register the T1 and T2 models [28]. The mandibular T1 and T2 models were imported into Geomagic Studio 2013 software, where the best-fit alignment function was utilized to superimpose the models. The reliability of the mandibular superimposition was verified using the same method as described for the maxillary models. Only the mandibular T1 and T2 models meeting the reliability criteria were used for subsequent measurement and analysis.

The reliably superimposed maxillary and mandibular T1 and T2 models were used to establish respective coordinate systems in Geomagic Studio 2013 software, facilitating measurement of tooth displacement within the dentition from T1 to T2. Using the T1 model as the reference, customized three-dimensional coordinate systems were defined for measuring the maxillary and mandibular dental arches. The maxillary coordinate system was established with its origin at the mesio-incisal point of the most labially inclined maxillary central incisor on the T1 model. The X-axis was defined by the line connecting the mesiobuccal cusps of the two first molars, with its positive direction oriented from the right to the left first molar. The XZ plane was constructed using the distobuccal cusps of both maxillary second molars and the lowest point of the mesio-incisal angle of one central incisor, creating a plane approximately parallel to the occlusal plane. The Y-axis was oriented perpendicular to the occlusal plane in an inferior direction, while the Z-axis extended sagittally from anterior to posterior (Fig. 2A). The mandibular coordinate system was established in a similar manner to the maxillary system (Fig. 2B).

Selection of dental measurement landmarks. The landmarks for measuring tooth displacement should accurately represent the overall displacement of each tooth. Therefore, multiple landmarks were selected on each tooth for positioning and measurement, and these points were sufficiently dispersed to minimize measurement error. When selecting landmarks, characteristic locations were identified through observation of dental anatomical morphology, typically including cusp tips, incisal angles, and prominent ridges on the tooth surface, while avoiding areas with poorly defined anatomical features. In this study, for posterior teeth, four points were selected from cusp tips or marginal ridges; for anterior teeth, three dispersed points were chosen along the incisal edge (Fig. 3). The coordinates of the selected landmarks were imported into an Excel spreadsheet. The mean coordinate values of all landmarks for each tooth were calculated within Excel to represent the tooth’s positional coordinates. The mean values from the T1 model were recorded as X1, Y1, Z1, while those from the T2 model were recorded as X2, Y2, Z2. All the above measurements were performed by a single investigator (Y.X) in a blinded manner.

Tooth displacements along the X-, Y-, and Z-axes between the T1 and T2 models were calculated as DX = X₂ − X₁, DY = Y₂ − Y₁, and DZ = Z₂ − Z₁. DY represents the displacement of the tooth in the vertical direction, denoted as ΔIE; DZ represents the buccal-lingual displacement of the central incisor, denoted as ΔBL. In addition to magnitude of tooth displacement based on the absolute values of the calculated differences, the direction of movement in the vertical and bucco-lingual dimensions should also be determined according to the coordinate axis orientation and the positive or negative values of DX, DY, and DZ. The planned vertical displacement (ΔIE’) and bucco-lingual displacement (ΔBL’) for this treatment stage are evaluated in the Smartcheck software (functionally analogous to Invisalign’s ClinCheck). The accuracy of anterior intrusion was defined as the ratio of actual to planned intrusion, ΔIE/ΔIE’. The discrepancy in bucco-lingual displacement of anterior teeth is calculated as ΔBL’ - ΔBL.

To ensure the accuracy of tooth displacement measurements, a random selection of 12 cases (6 from the control group and 6 from the BP group) was remeasured by the same investigator (Y.X) under blinded conditions after one month, and assessed the reliability between the two measurements.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analysis of the data was performed using SPSS (Version 26.0; IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA) and statistical graphs were generated with GraphPad Prism (Version 9.0; GraphPad Software, San Diego, CA, USA). The reliability of repeated tooth displacement measurements along the X-, Y-, and Z-axes was assessed using a single-rater intraclass correlation coefficient (ICC) model. Gender differences between two groups were evaluated using Fisher’s exact test. The normality of quantitative data was assessed using the Shapiro-Wilk test. For baseline characteristics, intrusion efficiency of homologous anterior teeth, and vertical positional changes of homologous posterior teeth which followed a normal distribution, the independent samples t-test was employed to compare differences between the two groups. For data which violated the normality assumption, the Mann-Whitney U test was used to assess intergroup differences. Bland-Altman plots were constructed to visualize the agreement between actual and planned buccal-lingual displacements of anterior teeth for both groups. One-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) was employed to compare the changes in vertical displacement among the first premolar, second premolar, first molar, and second molar within each group. Pearson linear correlation analysis was performed separately for maxillary and mandibular posterior teeth to examine the relationship between vertical displacement changes and the number of aligners in both groups, with scatter plots generated and linear regression analysis conducted. A p-value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Result

The intraclass correlation coefficients (ICC) of repeated tooth displacement measurements along the X-, Y-, and Z-axes were 0.928, 0.933, and 0.915, respectively. These results demonstrate high measurement agreement. Baseline characteristics of the two groups before treatment (Table 1) showed no statistically significant differences in gender, age, cephalometric measurements in the sagittal and vertical dimensions, inclination of the upper and lower central incisors, overbite, or overjet. Moreover, no statistically significant differences were observed between the two groups regarding the planned displacement of anterior teeth in the vertical and bucco-lingual dimensions, treatment duration, or number of aligners (Table 2). The baseline characteristics of the two cohorts were comparable.

The intrusion accuracy of anterior teeth was 18.4% ± 21.6% in the control group and 18.8% ± 24.7% in the BP group, with no statistically significant difference between them. The values for the three subgroups—maxillary incisors, mandibular incisors, and canines—were all close to the overall levels. Furthermore, at the subgroup level, the presence or absence of BPs demonstrated no statistically significant differences in the intrusion accuracy of maxillary incisors, mandibular incisors, or canines (Table 3).

Both groups were planned with minor bucco-lingual tooth movement. Bland-Altman plots were used to evaluate the effect of BPs on the agreement between planned and actual bucco-lingual tooth movement (Fig. 4). The results demonstrated minimal differences between the actual and planned bucco-lingual displacements across the three subgroups—maxillary incisors, mandibular incisors, and canines—with mean differences ranging from 0 to 0.37 mm.

One-way ANOVA was performed to compare the vertical changes of posterior teeth within each group (Fig. 5). The results revealed that in the control group, the maxillary first and second premolars exhibited slight extrusion, while the first and second molars demonstrated minor spontaneous intrusion. The vertical positional changes of mandibular posterior teeth were largely consistent with those observed in the maxilla, except that the mandibular first molars exhibited no intrusion (ΔIE = 0.0 ± 0.2 mm), indicating essentially stable vertical positions (Table 4). Compared with the control group, subjects in the BP group exhibited similar extrusion tendencies in both maxillary and mandibular premolars. However, the molars showed no spontaneous intrusion but rather slight extrusion. Independent samples t-tests were conducted to compare vertical positional changes of homologous posterior teeth between the two groups (Table 4). The results demonstrated that premolars in both arches exhibited extrusion ranging from 0 to 0.5 mm in both groups, with no statistically significant intergroup differences observed. Furthermore, the vertical changes displayed two distinct characteristics: greater extrusion magnitude in first premolars compared to second premolars, and greater extrusion in mandibular premolars compared to maxillary premolars. In contrast, the two groups exhibited completely opposite trends in molar changes. The control group demonstrated spontaneous intrusion of 0–0.2 mm in both maxillary and mandibular molars, while the BP group showed extrusion of 0–0.2 mm. Statistically significant differences were observed in the vertical positional changes of both first and second molars between the two groups.

Discussion

Deep overbite poses a significant challenge in CA therapy. The integration of BPs into CAs offers a promising approach for such cases during bite opening. While previous studies have investigated the effects of BPs on deep overbite or molars in CA treatment, most have included cases with pre- and post-treatment records or those following the first series of aligners, using lateral cephalogram superimposition methods to evaluate changes in overbite or molar position [18, 19, 24–26]. Furthermore, those studies have only validated the therapeutic efficacy of BPs in managing deep overbite with CA therapy, overlooking their impact on specific tooth movement in vertical dimension. Notably, since orthodontists strived to correct deep overbite to a normal level to achieve satisfactory treatment outcomes in clinical practice, the previous studies found no significant difference in the therapeutic efficacy of BPs for deep overbite during CA therapy [18, 25, 26]. These studies may present limitations including confounding factors, incomplete evaluation, and limited accuracy in elucidating the role of BPs in CA therapy. Our study employed a stage-specific design, focusing exclusively on the intrusion phase instead of the entire treatment period, to improve measurement accuracy and reduce confounding factors.

In CA therapy for deep overbite, intrusion of anterior teeth serves as a primary treatment approach for overbite reduction. Previous studies on the accuracy of anterior intrusion have reported approximately 40% in adult patients [17]. Substantial variations in intrusion efficiency exist across different studies due to heterogeneous study populations and methodologies [15–17]. In the present study, the intrusion accuracy of anterior teeth was 18.8% ± 24.7% in BP group and 18.4% ± 21.6% in control group. These values are lower than those reported in previous studies, which is primarily attributed to the specific tooth movement patterns included in this research. Anterior intrusion accompanied by retraction tends to cause extrusion rather than intrusion, thereby substantially compromising the intrusion accuracy [29]. The decreased anterior intrusion efficiency observed in both groups is likely attributable to the actual tooth retraction and its concomitant extrusion frequently exceeding the prescribed values [22], despite including only cases with less than 1 mm of retraction.

Our findings indicate that anterior intrusion efficiency did not differ significantly between the two groups at both the overall and subgroup levels. Although the incorporation of BPs in CAs led to reduced aligner coverage on teeth, this did not compromise anterior intrusion efficiency. The underlying reasons may involve two aspects. Firstly, during intrusion of anterior teeth, the intrusive forces are primarily concentrated on the incisal ridges and lingual fossae [30]. The compressive stresses are distributed by the adjacent zones of the BPs. Secondly, the aligner material remains uninterrupted, thereby maintaining its structural integrity and intrusive forces on anterior teeth. In addition, Bland-Altman plots of bucco-lingual movement in anterior teeth demonstrated that the actual displacements in both groups fluctuated within a mean difference of 0–0.37 mm from the planned values, indicating no significant influence of the BP on labio-lingual tooth movement. Overall, this study found that the incorporation of BPs did not significantly affect the efficiency of tooth intrusion or labio-lingual movement, although it reduced aligner coverage on the teeth.

The in vitro experimental findings by Liu et al. [31] demonstrated that intrusion of mandibular anterior teeth generated extrusive counterforces on the bilateral first and second premolars, which is consistent with the findings of the present study. In the control group of this study, the mandibular first and second premolars exhibited extrusion of 0.5 mm and 0.3 mm, respectively, with no significant difference between them (Fig. 5B). This indicates that during mandibular anterior intrusion, both the first and second premolars serve as crucial anchorage teeth, jointly counteracting the reactive forces of intrusion. The maxillary first and second premolars in the control group exhibited an extrusion trend similar to their mandibular counterparts. Notably, the maxillary premolars exhibited less extrusion. This can be explained by the distribution of included canines: 82.8% were mandibular, and only 5 of 29 (17.2%) were maxillary. Thus, the reduced anchorage demand during maxillary anterior intrusion resulted in less extrusion of the maxillary premolars compared to their mandibular counterparts. The BP group exhibited consistent movement trends in maxillary and mandibular premolars compared to the control group. No statistically significant differences were identified in the vertical displacements of homologous premolars between the two groups (Table 4). These findings suggest that anchorage tooth selection during anterior intrusion should consider both the number of teeth to be intruded and their periodontal ligament area. For simultaneous anterior intrusion, at least the bilateral first and second premolars should serve as anchorage, equipped with large, sharp-edged attachments (e.g., horizontal rectangular) to prevent aligner dislodgement under reactive extrusive forces [32]. When only incisor intrusion is planned, anchorage demand on the second premolars decreases.

During CA therapy, the “posterior bite-block effect” of aligners often leads to spontaneous intrusion of molars [11–14]. A similar phenomenon was observed in the control group of this study (Fig. 5). The greater molar intrusion observed in the maxilla compared to the mandible—potentially attributable to differences in bone density between the two jaws—was consistent with the findings reported by Moradinejad et al. [13]. In the present study, intrusion occurred only in the molars of the control group, while the premolars exhibited extrusion despite being subjected to occlusal forces. This suggests that the intrusive force generated by the “posterior bite-block effect” of CAs is a continuous light force, which can be entirely counteracted by the opposing extrusive force. It has been documented that the interocclusal space at rest is approximately 3 mm in 87% of individuals [33]. Wearing aligners reduces the interocclusal distance due to the appliance’s thickness, thereby inducing unintentional and frequent light posterior contacts. This sustained light intrusive force ultimately leads to posterior intrusion, which may constitute the primary mechanism of the “posterior bite-block effect” in CA therapy.

The trend of vertical positional changes in posterior teeth correlates with tooth location. The first premolars experienced the greatest extrusive force, which was sufficient to overcome the intrusion induced by the “bite-block effect”. The second premolars exhibited a similar pattern, with a lesser magnitude of extrusion. In contrast, the reactive force from anterior intrusion became negligible upon reaching the molar region. Consequently, the light intrusive force generated by the “posterior bite-block effect” predominated the vertical positional changes of the first and second molars, resulting in spontaneous intrusive movement. Overall, during anterior intrusion, the “posterior bite-block effect” exhibits a progressively increasing influence from the middle to the posterior dental segments.

Furthermore, spontaneous molar intrusion positively correlates with treatment duration within a certain range (Fig. 6). The discrepancy between this finding and previous studies [11, 13] may be primarily attributed to inconsistencies in the treatment stages of the included cases. The study by Moradinejad et al. [13] analyzed cases from pre- and post-treatment stages, with participants using an average of over 60 aligners. As intrusion of the upper and lower posterior teeth progressed, the interocclusal space at rest with aligners gradually increased, leading to a reduction in both the frequency and magnitude of posterior contacts. Consequently, the potential and tendency for spontaneous posterior intrusion progressively diminished. Thus, no positive correlation between treatment duration and magnitude of spontaneous molar intrusion was demonstrated. In the present study, the control group was selected from a single stage involving tooth intrusion within the first series of aligners. This approach prevented the loss of the trend and potential for spontaneous posterior intrusion during the observation period, thereby revealing the time-related nature of the “posterior bite-block effect”. Although the control group in this study exhibited spontaneous molar intrusion of only 0–0.3 mm during the shorter intrusion phase—an amount considered clinically insignificant—it is important to note that the degree of molar intrusion increases with treatment duration and can exceed the clinically significant threshold of 0.5 mm. Moreover, previous studies utilizing pre- and post-treatment comparisons have confirmed that the cumulative molar intrusion observed post-treatment is indeed clinically significant [11–13].

Despite confining occlusal forces to the anterior segment, the bite plane did not enhance anterior intrusion.The underlying reason may be that current BPs in CAs universally feature a hollow structural design. Light occlusal contact forces can cause minor deformation or even collapse of the material in the BP regions. Consequently, the energy intended for dental intrusion is absorbed by this hollow structure and fails to be transmitted to the anterior teeth. We hypothesize that solid BPs enhance anterior intrusion efficiency by more effectively transmitting occlusal forces, though further studies are needed to confirm this.

In terms of morphology and function, the BPs of CAs are designed to mimic the bite plates or bite turbos used in conventional fixed appliances. Previous studies [34, 35] have demonstrated bite plates or bite turbos in fixed appliances induce vertical changes including labial tipping and intrusion of the central incisors, extrusion of the first molars, accompanied by a reduction in overbite and an increase in vertical facial height. In our study, the vertical effects induced by the BPs of CAs differed from those produced by bite plates or bite turbos in fixed appliances. First, the BPs of CAs did not facilitate intrusion of the anterior teeth. Therefore, anterior intrusion was primarily achieved through the active intrusive force of the aligners. Secondly, the BPs in CAs did not effectively promote molar extrusion. Although the maxillary and mandibular molars in this study exhibited a mean extrusion of 0.1–0.2 mm, this trend did not progress with increased treatment duration. This vertical change can be considered clinically insignificant, as the teeth merely maintained their original position without meaningful extrusion. The limited capacity of BPs in CAs to extrude posterior teeth thus results in inferior skeletal effects compared to fixed appliances in the treatment of skeletal deep overbite [24]. Nevertheless, the current BPs in CAs remain a recommended design for deep overbite correction in both normodivergent and hypodivergent patients, as they effectively mitigate the undesired posterior intrusion associated with the “bite-block effect”.

Based on the findings regarding the effects of BPs, this study nevertheless has the following limitations. First, as a retrospective study, although specific stages (before and after intrusion rather than the entire treatment period) were selected to minimize the influence of other tooth movements, the inherent risk of confounding factors could not be entirely eliminated. Second, the anterior teeth were permitted up to 1 mm of labiolingual movement during intrusion, which may have affected intrusion efficiency. However, this reflects clinical reality, as pure intrusion without any labiolingual movement is rarely required in practice. Third, due to the limited sample size, subgroup analyses were not conducted for maxillary versus mandibular canines or for BPs placed on different positions (canines versus incisors). Whether the effects of BPs differ between maxillary and mandibular canines, or according to their positioning, remains unknown. Further studies are warranted to establish optimal design strategies for BPs.
