Existence and Calculation of Optimal Equilibria on Overlapping Generations Economies
Leandro Lyra Braga Dognini

Abstract
A well-known feature of overlapping generations economies is that the First Welfare Theorem fails and equilibrium may be inefficient. The Cass 1972 criterion furnishes a necessary and sufficient condition for efficiency, but does not address the matter of existence of efficient equilibria, and Cass et al. 1979 provide nonexistence examples. I develop an algorithm based on successive approximations of a nonstationary, consumption-loan, prone to savings, overlapping generations economy with finite-lived heterogeneous agents to find elements of its set of equilibria as the limit of nested compact sets. These compact sets are the result of a backward calculation through equilibrium equations that departs from the set of Pareto optimal equilibria of well-behaved tail economies. The equilibria calculated through this algorithm satisfy the Cass 1972 criterion and are used to derive the existence results on efficient equilibria.



Keywords
: Economic Dynamics, Transition Paths, Equilibrium Calculation Algorithm, Computable General Equilibrium, Consumption-loan Economies.

JEL
: D51, D58.

1.  Introduction

Overlapping generations economies
are the workhorse for several applications of general equilibrium theory. However, their double infinity of households and dated commodities
(
Shell 1971
)
results in a striking difference from classical general equilibrium economies
(
Arrow & Debreu 1954
)
and its positive and normative characteristics.

In overlapping generations economies, not only equilibrium can be locally indeterminate (e.g.,
Kehoe & Levine 1984
;
Kehoe & Levine 1984a
;
Kehoe & Levine 1985
;
Kehoe & Levine 1990
;
Muller & Woodford 1988
;
Kehoe et al. 1989
;
Kehoe et al. 1991
;
Burke 1990
), but also the First Welfare theorem is not valid and, therefore, equilibrium can be inefficient (e.g.,
Samuelson 1958
;
Cass & Yaari 1966
;
Shell 1971
). This article focuses on this second feature of these economies.

Following
Cass 1972
, the literature has derived necessary and sufficient conditions to characterize efficient equilibria (e.g.,
Balasko & Shell 1980
;
Okuno & Zilcha 1980
;
Benveniste 1986
;
Geanakoplos & Polemarchakis 1991
;
Aiyagari 1992
) that are mainly based on the asymptotic behavior of equilibrium price sequences, in the form of

\displaystyle\sum^{+\infty}_{t=1}\frac{1}{\|p_{t}\|}=+\infty.

(1)

However, such results do not address the matter of existence of a Pareto optimal equilibrium (i.e., if there is any equilibrium that satisfies (
1
)) and nonexistence examples were given by
Cass et al. 1979
.

When dealing with this matter of existence, the literature on efficient equilibria has relied on the assumptions of stationary (e.g.,
Okuno & Zilcha 1983
;
Benveniste & Cass 1986
;
Aiyagari & Peled 1991
;
Duc & Ghiglino 1998
) or nearly stationary economies (e.g.,
Burke 1995
), or on the possibility of wealth transfers (e.g.,
Okuno & Zilcha 1980
;
Balasko & Shell 1981
;
Burke 1987
), and a comprehensive discussion on this topic was written by
Burke 1995
.

In order to analyze nonstationary economies, the literature has dealt with this possible lack of efficient equilibria by assuming the existence of a durable dividend-paying asset (e.g., land), since it rules out inefficiency and reestablishes the First Welfare theorem (e.g.,
Muller & Woodford 1988
,
Mas-Colell et al. 1995
,
İmrohoroğlu et al. 1999
;
Demange 2002
). Also, an alternative path has been to assume the existence of infinite-lived households or, equivalently, the possibility of bequests (e.g.,
Barro 1974
;
Wilson 1981
;
Kehoe & Levine 1985
;
Muller & Woodford 1988
;
Aiyagari 1988
).

Considering this literature overview, the first contribution of this article is to provide existence results (Theorems
12
and
14
) of efficient equilibria on consumption-loan, nonstationary overlapping generations economies with heterogeneous finite-lived households and without durable dividend-paying assets. The two main assumptions of these theorems are that: (i) the
Cass 1972
criterion is valid; and (ii) the economy has a natural propensity to savings, which is defined as a
prone to savings economy
.

The second contribution is to show that, in consumption-loan overlapping generations economies representing long-standing economic activity, it is adequate to explicitly model the younger period of life of generation
G_{0}
at
t=0
and to define the equilibrium set by: (i) imposing market clearing for all periods
t\geq 1
; and (ii) limiting aggregate demand of generation
G_{0}
at
t=0
to values that are compatible with bounds over demographic and productivity dynamics.

This explicit modeling of the younger period of life of generation
G_{0}
is a fundamental departure from classical overlapping generations economies, for which the inception of the economy is assumed to occur in the first period
t=1
(e.g.
Balasko & Shell 1980
;
Okuno & Zilcha 1980
;
Kehoe & Levine 1985
;
Aiyagari 1992
). In particular, this modeling perspective allows one to endogenize the savings or borrowing decisions of households from generation
G_{0}
without recurring to a
regressum ad infinitum
setup (e.g.,
Gale 1973
) and to obtain the previously mentioned existence results.

The third contribution is to demonstrate that, in overlapping generations economies representing long-standing economic activity, the appropriate definition of Pareto optimality to evaluate equilibrium allocations is one for which aggregate resources are only
implicitly
defined by the respective allocation and, not,
explicitly
carried out in the definition itself.

The fourth contribution is to highlight that efficiency conditions written in the form of (
1
) can be naturally related to the dynamic of real savings per capita, and, on prone to savings economies, nonvanishing real savings per capita fully characterize efficient equilibria (Proposition
7
).

The last contribution of this article relates to the actual calculation of Pareto optimal equilibria on nonstationary overlapping generations models and, particularly, to the calculation of efficient transition paths. In the proof of the existence results previously mentioned, it is shown that a Pareto optimal equilibrium exists by taking successive approximations of the reference economy by a sequence of economies that coincide with it up to a finite, but arbitrarily large, time period.

This sequence of auxiliary economies is called a
finitely replicating sequence
. In particular, one may construct finitely replicating sequences by taking arbitrarily large sections of the reference economy and then properly choosing well-behaved
tail economies
.

In this setting, Theorem
13
in
Section 4
provides a backward calculation algorithm to identify Pareto optimal equilibria through a finitely replicating sequence. The algorithm is based on the fact that, by departing from the well-known set of Pareto optimal equilibria of the tail economies, one may use equilibrium equations to “move” backwards and correctly approach at least one efficient equilibrium of the reference economy.

This last contribution is, therefore, directly related to the computation literature on overlapping generations (e.g.,
Auerbach & Kotlikoff 1987
;
İmrohoroğlu et al. 1999
).

After this
Introduction
, the outline of the article is as follows.
Section 2
builds the overlapping generations economy
\mathcal{E}
and
Section 3
states the main definitions of the article (e.g., of the subset of Pareto optimal equilibria and of prone to savings economies).
Section 3
also derives several results with respect to the set of equilibria of
\mathcal{E}
.
Section 4
, then, focuses on the backward calculation algorithm and the existence results.

Finally, a brief discussion of the results and the motivation of this article is presented in the
Conclusion
and all the proofs are stated in the
Appendix
.

m^{h}\in\mathbb{R}
,
h\in G^{3}_{0}
, from the economic activity that occurred in the past.

Although past overlaps are not explicitly modeled, Definition
1
requires that the generation
G_{0}
consumption bundle is derived from competitive prices
(p_{0},p_{1})
that are compatible with Assumptions
1
and
3
.

But if economies
\mathcal{E}
and
\mathcal{E}^{3}
are related, why not work directly with
\mathcal{E}^{3}
instead of
\mathcal{E}
? First, because
\mathcal{E}^{3}
was derived from
\mathcal{E}
– it is a sort of “chopped” part of it. Second, because
\mathcal{E}
explicitly models the fundamentals that allow households, including those born in period
t=0
, to become borrowers or savers. It can be seen as a way to make the savings or borrowing decisions of all generations endogenous, without recurring to a
regressum ad infinitum
setup (e.g.,
Gale 1973
).

Third, because the fundamental question that is being answered when dealing with the economy
\mathcal{E}
is: What equilibrium prices are consistent with long-standing economic activity? Or, stated otherwise, if economic activity started long ago and, therefore, we are not at the inception of the economy, what prices are compatible with equilibrium under perfect foresight?

One could also argue that it is possible to define an economy
\mathcal{E}^{3}
by arbitrarily choosing the number of households born in period
t=0
, along with their utility functions
\tilde{u}^{h}(\cdot)
and the money or debt holdings
m^{h}\in\mathbb{R}
. So why not proceed in this manner and study the set of equilibria of this economy? The main reason is technical. If
m^{h}<0
, for some
h\in G^{3}_{0}
, the budget set becomes empty at some prices, and, therefore, the equilibrium existence is not guaranteed. If
m^{h}>0
, for all
h\in G^{3}_{0}
, then this is again the second type of classical economies.

Why then did the classical models not adopt the economic setup of
\mathcal{E}
and the definition of the equilibrium set
\mathcal{H}
? The answer to this question involves the well-understanding of two points. First, the classical models were not looking for results as the ones in this article (e.g., Theorem
13
), in which one needs to take conveniently chosen overlapping generations economies (which will later be called
tail economies
by Definition
6
) and use them to “replace” all generations born after a given period in a reference economy. For this sort of replacement to be possible, the older generation that is also alive at
t=0
in this conveniently chosen tail economy cannot be explicitly modeled.

Second, because the Pareto optimal definition for consumption-loan economies commonly assumes some concept of
feasibility
(e.g.,
Okuno & Zilcha 1980
), which presumes fixed aggregate resources for the economy. However, when looking at the allocations implied by
\mathcal{H}
, aggregate resources are only well defined for
t\geq 1
, in which case they are given by
\sum_{h\in G_{t-1}}e^{h}_{t}+\sum_{h\in G_{t}}e^{h}_{t}
.

In order also to have well-defined aggregate resources in
t=0
and, consequently, to adopt a concept of feasibility, one could impose a market clearing equation in
t=0
,
\sum_{h\in G_{0}}x^{h}(p_{0},p_{1})=\sum_{h\in G_{0}}e^{h}_{0}
. But then we would obtain precisely the classical model of an economy without money (for the coincidence to be full, one would also need to relabel periods
t=0
and
t=1
as the initial period).

One may notice, however, that the concept of Pareto optimality can be stated by looking solely at the allocation (e.g.,
Balasko & Shell 1980
), without any explicit reference to matters of feasibility. In this case, aggregate resources are implicitly defined by the allocation itself, allowing one to use the Pareto optimality concept to characterize elements of
\mathcal{H}
. This leads us to the following definition.

Definition 2
.

The
subset of Pareto optimal equilibria

\mathcal{H}^{PO}\subseteq\mathcal{H}
is

\displaystyle\mathcal{H}^{PO}=\{(p_{0},p_{1},\ldots)\in\mathcal{H}\mid\nexists\{y^{h}\}_{h\geq 1},y^{h}\in\mathbb{R}^{L_{t}+L_{t+1}}_{+},h\in G_{t},t\geq 0,\textrm{ such that }

\displaystyle\sum_{t\geq 0}\sum_{h\in G_{t}}y^{h}=\sum_{t\geq 0}\sum_{h\in G_{t}}x^{h}(p_{t},p_{t+1})\textrm{ and }u^{h}(y^{h})\geq u^{h}(x^{h}(p_{t},p_{t+1})),

\displaystyle\textrm{for }h\in G_{t},t\geq 0,\textrm{with at least one strict inequality}\}.

Definition
2
identifies the subset of
\mathcal{H}
for which there is no possibility of redistribution of aggregate resources leading to a Pareto improvement. Furthermore, due to Definition
1
, aggregate resources implicitly defined by elements of
\mathcal{H}
can vary only at time
t=0
.

There is a fundamental remark about the subset of Pareto optimal equilibria
\mathcal{H}^{PO}
. Notice that for
p\in\mathcal{H}
, it is always possible to build another overlapping generations economy
\mathcal{E}_{p}
, with the same households from
\mathcal{E}
, but with endowments given by
e^{h}_{p}=x^{h}(p_{t},p_{t+1})
,
h\in G_{t}
,
t\geq 0
. In this economy
\mathcal{E}_{p}
, the new Walrasian demand of household
h\in G_{t}
,
t\geq 0
, is given by

\displaystyle x^{h}_{p}(q_{t},q_{t+1})=\textrm{argmax}_{c\in\mathbb{R}^{L_{t}+L_{t+1}}_{+}}

\displaystyle u^{h}(c)

s.t.

\displaystyle(q_{t},q_{t+1})\cdot(c-e^{h}_{p})\leq 0.

for
(q_{t},q_{t+1})\in\mathbb{R}^{L_{t}+L_{t+1}}_{++}
, and one can straightforwardly see that
x^{h}_{p}(p_{t},p_{t+1})=e^{h}_{p}
. In particular,
\sum_{t\geq 0}\sum_{h\in G_{t}}x^{h}_{p}(p_{t},p_{t+1})-e^{h}_{p}=0
and, therefore,
p\in\mathbb{R}^{\infty}_{++}
is an equilibrium of the economy
\mathcal{E}_{p}
, with market clearing satisfied at all periods, including
t=0
.

But then, by relabeling periods
t=0
and
t=1
as the initial period, one must notice that
\mathcal{E}_{p}
is actually the classical setup of an overlapping generations economy (e.g.,
Balasko & Shell 1980
;
Balasko & Shell 1981
,
Cass et al. 1979
,
Okuno & Zilcha 1980
,
Kehoe & Levine 1985
and
Aiyagari 1992
). Also,
p\in\mathcal{H}^{PO}
if, and only if,
p
is a Pareto optimal equilibrium of
\mathcal{E}_{p}
.

Then, if the
Cass 1972
criterion can be applied to characterize the set of Pareto optimal equilibria of
\mathcal{E}_{p}
, the following equivalence holds

\displaystyle p\in\mathcal{H}^{PO}\iff\sum^{+\infty}_{t=0}\frac{1}{H_{t}\|p_{t}\|}=+\infty,

and, if this reasoning can be applied for all
p\in\mathcal{H}
, then

\displaystyle\mathcal{H}^{PO}=\biggr\{(p_{0},p_{1},\ldots)\in\mathcal{H}\mid\sum^{+\infty}_{t=0}\frac{1}{H_{t}\|p_{t}\|}=+\infty\biggr\}.

This leads to the following assumption.

Assumption 4

(
Cass 1972
criterion)
.

Let
p\in\mathcal{H}
. Then,
p\in\mathcal{H}^{PO}
if, and only if,

\displaystyle\sum^{+\infty}_{t=0}\frac{1}{H_{t}\|p_{t}\|}=+\infty.

As described in the
Introduction
, following
Cass 1972
,
Balasko & Shell 1980
;
Okuno & Zilcha 1980
;
Benveniste 1986
;
Geanakoplos & Polemarchakis 1991
derived conditions under which Assumption
4
can be sustained (e.g., uniform bounds on the Gaussian curvature of the indifference curves
(
Balasko & Shell 1980
, 296)
and bounded coefficients of smoothness and strictness
(
Okuno & Zilcha 1980
, 803)
).

Since
\mathcal{H}
is seen as the set of equilibria that represent long-standing economic activity, one may directly notice, under Assumption
4
, that
\mathcal{H}^{PO}
can be regarded as the set of equilibria that represent long-standing efficient economic activity, with
\mathcal{E}
being just part of a “larger classical economy” whose inception was in period
t=T
,
T\leq-1
.

This is because, in such larger classical economy, competitive markets under perfect foresight yield a Pareto optimal equilibrium through prices
(p_{-T},\ldots,p_{-1},p)\in\mathbb{R}^{\infty}_{++}
, and, due to the
Cass 1972
criterion,
\sum^{\infty}_{t=-T}(H_{t}\|p_{t}\|)^{-1}=+\infty
. Due to the equivalence

\displaystyle\sum^{\infty}_{t=-T}\frac{1}{H_{t}\|p_{t}\|}=+\infty\iff\sum^{\infty}_{t=0}\frac{1}{H_{t}\|p_{t}\|}=+\infty,

every efficient equilibrium of the larger economy provides an element of
\mathcal{H}^{PO}
. Since no specific form of the larger economy is given,
\mathcal{H}^{PO}
contains the projection of the sets of efficient equilibria of all possible larger classical economies that satisfy Assumptions
1
–
4
. One can also notice that every element of
\mathcal{H}^{PO}
lies within an efficient equilibrium of some larger classical economy that satisfies Assumptions
1
–
4
.

Before moving on to the results of this section, the following definition of short-sighted equilibrium for the economy

is needed.

Definition 3
.

The

\displaystyle\mathcal{V}_{j}=\biggr\{p\in\mathbb{R}^{\sum^{j+1}_{i=0}L_{i}}_{++}\mid(p_{0},p_{1})\in\mathcal{K}_{0}\times\mathcal{K}^{0}_{1},p\in\mathcal{Z}_{j},(p_{j}/p_{j1},p_{j+1}/p_{j1})\in\mathcal{K}^{0}_{j}\times\mathcal{K}^{0}_{j+1}\biggr\}.

Definition

focuses only on “short-term” market clearing, limited to a finite number of periods. Furthermore, prices

and

that the generations on the edges face must be compatible with past and future overlaps according to Assumptions

and

. For the next lemma, let
\mathcal{V}^{\infty}_{j}=\mathcal{V}_{j}\times\prod^{\infty}_{t=j+2}\mathcal{K}_{t}

Lemma 1
.

Under Assumptions
1
–
3
, for all

, the extension to
\mathbb{R}^{\infty}_{++}
of the set of j-sighted equilibria,

, is non-empty and compact, with
\mathcal{V}^{\infty}_{j+1}\subseteq\mathcal{V}^{\infty}_{j}\subseteq\prod^{\infty}_{t=0}\mathcal{K}_{t}

Lemma

states that
\{\mathcal{V}^{\infty}_{j}\}_{j\geq 1}
is a nested sequence of non-empty compact sets. Let
\lim_{j\rightarrow\infty}\mathcal{V}^{\infty}_{j}=\bigcap_{j\geq 1}\mathcal{V}^{\infty}_{j}
. The next result clarifies the relation between the sequence

and the set of equilibria

.

Theorem 2
.

Under Assumptions
1
–

\mathcal{H}\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{\infty}_{++}
is non-empty and compact, with
\mathcal{H}=\lim_{j\rightarrow\infty}\mathcal{V}^{\infty}_{j}

In order to better characterize the subset of Pareto optimal equilibria
\mathcal{H}^{PO}\subset\mathcal{H}
, yet another definition is needed.

Definition 4
.

The
real savings

s^{h}:\mathbb{R}^{L_{t}+L_{t+1}}_{++}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
of household
h\in G_{t}
,
t\geq 0
, is

\displaystyle s^{h}(p_{t},p_{t+1})=\frac{p_{t}}{\|p_{t}\|}\cdot(e^{h}_{t}-x^{h}_{t}(p_{t},p_{t+1})).

Definition
4
can be understood through the households budget constraint. Due to nonsatiation of the preferences of
h\in G_{t}
,
t\geq 0
, the budget constraint holds with equality and, therefore, all resources not consumed when young
p_{t}\cdot(e^{h}_{t}-x^{h}_{t}(p_{t},p_{t+1}))>0
are destined for old-age consumption
p_{t+1}\cdot(x^{h}_{t+1}(p_{t},p_{t+1})-e^{h}_{t+1})>0
. This description assumes a positive savings level, but one could have zero or negative savings depending on the price levels.

The next remark is that to obtain a real savings measure, a reference basket of commodities at time
t
must be defined since
p_{t}\cdot(e^{h}_{t}-x^{h}_{t}(p_{t},p_{t+1}))
is homogeneous of degree one and varies with the price level. A possibility is the basket with one unity of all existing commodities
(1,\ldots,1)\in\mathbb{R}^{L_{t}}
, which costs
\sum^{L_{t}}_{i=1}p_{ti}=\|p_{t}\|
. Dividing
p_{t}\cdot(e^{h}_{t}-x^{h}_{t}(p_{t},p_{t+1}))
by
\|p_{t}\|
yields the desired measure of real savings. The next result gives a first relation between real savings and the subset of Pareto optimal equilibria
\mathcal{H}^{PO}
.

Lemma 3
.

Under Assumption
4
, if
p\in\mathcal{H}/\mathcal{H}^{PO}
, then
\lim_{t\rightarrow\infty}\sum_{h\in G_{t}}s^{h}(p_{t},p_{t+1})/H_{t}=0
.

Lemma
3
states that an inefficient equilibrium depresses average real savings generation after generation, leading to an economic scenario in which real interperiod wealth transfers become each time smaller for subsequent generations, and intraperiod trade or autarchy is all that remains in the long run. In order to find conditions under which this depressed real savings dynamic fully characterizes inefficient equilibria, I restrict the analysis to economies in which positive average real savings can be considered a natural outcome.

Definition 5
.

The economy
\mathcal{E}
is
prone to savings
if there are
\varepsilon>0
,
\delta>0
such that, for
t\geq 0
,

\displaystyle\sum_{h\in G_{t}}\frac{s^{h}(p_{t},p_{t+1})}{H_{t}}\leq\delta\implies\frac{\|p_{t}\|}{\|p_{t+1}\|}\leq\frac{\alpha_{t}}{1+\varepsilon},

for all
p\in\mathcal{H}
.

Definition
5
uses the set of equilibria
\mathcal{H}
to define a prone to savings economy. However, if our objective is, precisely, to calculate efficient equilibria, wouldn’t this be a contradiction? If we already know the set
\mathcal{H}
, would it not be better to check the
Cass 1972
criterion?

The answer is no in both cases. First, because, although Definition
5
uses the set
\mathcal{H}
to define a prone to savings economy, one does not need to know
\mathcal{H}
to demonstrate that an economy is prone to savings. It is sufficient to demonstrate that the implication in Definition
5
is satisfied in a set
\Omega\supseteq\mathcal{H}
and this is done, for example, in Proposition
4
.

Second, since evaluating whether an infinite series diverge requires the calculation of an infinite number of terms, applying the
Cass 1972
criterion is not a viable option, in general. Our main goal, therefore, is to build an algorithm that directly approximates efficient equilibria. I will discuss this in more detail in
Section 4
.

The economy
\mathcal{E}
is prone to savings if, in equilibrium, a low average of real savings of generation
G_{t}
,
t\geq 0
, implies a low real return rate relative to the population growth rate. Conversely, if, in equilibrium, the real return rate is sufficiently high, then so will the real savings average. This is because the utility maximization problem of household
h\in G_{t}
can be written as

\displaystyle\max_{c\in\mathbb{R}^{L_{t}+L_{t+1}}_{+}}

\displaystyle u^{h}(c)

s.t.

\displaystyle\frac{p_{t}}{\|p_{t}\|}\cdot(c_{t}-e^{h}_{t})+s=0

\displaystyle\frac{p_{t+1}}{\|p_{t+1}\|}\cdot(c_{t+1}-e^{h}_{t+1})\leq\frac{\|p_{t}\|}{\|p_{t+1}\|}s=r_{t}s,

with
r_{t}=\|p_{t}\|/\|p_{t+1}\|
representing the real return rate on savings of generation
G_{t}
.

Since
s^{h}(\cdot)
is homogeneous of degree zero and
p\in\mathcal{H}
due to Definition
5
, we can assume, up to normalization of the first coordinate, that
(p_{t},p_{t+1})\in\mathcal{K}^{0}_{t}\times\mathcal{K}^{0}_{t+1}
. The definition of
\mathcal{K}^{0}_{t}\times\mathcal{K}^{0}_{t+1}
implies that

\displaystyle\sigma^{2}=\frac{\|(\sigma,\ldots,\sigma)\|}{\|(\sigma^{-1},\ldots,\sigma^{-1})\|}\leq\frac{\|p_{t}\|}{\|p_{t+1}\|}=r_{t}\leq\frac{\|(\sigma^{-1},\ldots,\sigma^{-1})\|}{\|(\sigma,\ldots,\sigma)\|}=\sigma^{-2}.

Also, if
(p_{t},p_{t+1})\in\mathcal{K}^{0}_{t}\times\mathcal{K}^{0}_{t+1}
,
q_{t}=p_{t}/\|p_{t}\|
satisfies

\displaystyle q_{t}\geq\frac{(\sigma,\ldots,\sigma)}{\|(\sigma^{-1},\ldots,\sigma^{-1})\|}\geq\biggr(\frac{\sigma^{2}}{L_{t}},\ldots,\frac{\sigma^{2}}{L_{t}}\biggr),

with
\|q_{t}\|=1
. An analogous relation holds for
q_{t+1}=p_{t+1}/\|p_{t+1}\|
. Define
\Delta_{t}(\sigma)=\{q_{t}\in\mathbb{R}^{L_{t}}_{++}\mid\|q_{t}\|=1\textrm{ and }q_{t}\geq(\sigma^{2}/L_{t},\ldots,\sigma^{2}/L_{t})\}
and, to simplify notation, write
\Delta_{t}=\Delta_{t}(\sigma)
. Define
\Delta_{t+1}
analogously and, for
r_{t}\in[\sigma^{2},\sigma^{-2}]
, let
f_{r_{t}}:\Delta_{t}\times\Delta_{t+1}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
be given by

\displaystyle f_{r_{t}}(q_{t},q_{t+1})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{h\in G_{t}}\frac{s^{h}(r_{t}q_{t},q_{t+1})}{H_{t}}.

Therefore,
f_{r_{t}}
indicates the average real savings rate of generation
G_{t}
when the real return rate is
r_{t}
and the spot real prices are
q_{t}
and
q_{t+1}
. One may notice that

\displaystyle f_{r_{t}}(q_{t},q_{t+1})=\sum_{h\in G_{t}}\frac{q_{t}\cdot(e^{h}_{t}-x^{h}_{t}(r_{t}q_{t},q_{t+1}))}{H_{t}}\leq\sum_{h\in G_{t}}\frac{q_{t}\cdot e^{h}_{t}}{H_{t}}\leq\sum_{h\in G_{t}}\frac{\|e^{h}_{t}\|_{\infty}}{H_{t}}\leq e_{\max},

and, by an analogous argument, Walras’ law implies that
r_{t}f_{r_{t}}(q_{t},q_{t+1})\geq-e_{\max}
. Furthermore, let
g_{r_{t}}:\Delta_{t}\times\Delta_{t+1}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^{2}
be given by
g_{r_{t}}(q_{t},q_{t+1})=(f_{r_{t}}(q_{t},q_{t+1}),r_{t}f_{r_{t}}(q_{t},q_{t+1}))
, and notice that the coordinates of
g_{r_{t}}(q_{t},q_{t+1})\in\mathbb{R}^{2}

g_{r_{t}}
is continuous and
\Delta_{t}\times\Delta_{t+1}
is compact and connected,
g_{r_{t}}(\Delta_{t}\times\Delta_{t+1})
is compact and connected. Since all connected and compact one-dimensional sets are compact intervals,
g_{r_{t}}(\Delta_{t}\times\Delta_{t+1})=\{(y,r_{t}y))\in\mathbb{R}^{2}\mid y\in[a_{r_{t}},b_{r_{t}}]\}
, for some
a_{r_{t}}\leq b_{r_{t}}
.

Also,
\mathcal{H}
is compact by Theorem
2
and therefore the set
\mathcal{C}_{r_{t}}=\{(q_{t},q_{t+1})\in\Delta_{t}\times\Delta_{t+1}\mid(r_{t}q_{t},q_{t+1})=(p_{t},p_{t+1}),\textrm{ for some }p\in\mathcal{H}\}
is again compact, although it may be empty. Finally, the fact that

is a compact subset of

can be used to derive a graphical representation of Definition

We can infer from
Figure 1
that high demographic growth rates tend to favor the emergence of prone to savings economies. We can also guess that households’ preferences that value old-age consumption high and skewed endowment distribution toward the younger periods of life are both situations that favor the emergence of prone to savings economies, since larger real return rates then tend to move the set
g_{r_{t}}(\mathcal{C}_{r_{t}})

Therefore, a prone to savings economy can be thought as one in which generations have, given the demographic dynamic, a meaningful preference for old age consumption or a sufficiently skewed endowment distribution towards the younger periods of life. In these economies, there is a natural propensity to save, and the real savings level becomes a signal on whether the terms of trade between younger and older periods of life are unfavorable, since unnaturally low savings could only be achieved through unfavorable real return rates.

The next result formalizes this intuitive description by giving a sufficient condition for the economy
\mathcal{E}
to be prone to savings, when utility functions are constant elasticity of substitution (CES) ones and homogeneous within each generation.

Proposition 4
.

Suppose there are
(\lambda_{t},\mu_{t+1})\in\mathbb{R}^{L_{t}+L_{t+1}}_{++}

\displaystyle u^{h}(c_{t},c_{t+1})=\biggr(\sum^{L_{t}}_{i=1}\lambda_{ti}c^{\rho_{t}}_{ti}+\sum^{L_{t+1}}_{i=1}\mu_{(t+1)i}c^{\rho_{t}}_{(t+1)i}\biggr)^{1/\rho_{t}}.

Also, let
\gamma_{t}\in(0,1)

\displaystyle\gamma_{t}=\frac{\sum^{L_{t}}_{i=1}\lambda^{\eta_{t}}_{ti}}{\sum^{L_{t}}_{i=1}\lambda^{\eta_{t}}_{ti}+\sigma^{2(\eta_{t}-1)}\sum^{L_{t+1}}_{i=1}\mu^{\eta_{t}}_{(t+1)i}}<1,

\displaystyle\inf_{t\geq 0}\biggr\{(1-\gamma_{t})\min_{1\leq i\leq L_{t}}e^{t}_{ti}-\gamma_{t}\|e^{t}_{t+1}\|_{\infty}\alpha^{-1}_{t}\biggr\}>0,

(2)

Clearly, depending on the overlapping generations economy, solving the equilibrium equations after period

However, it seems reasonable to guess that, under natural circumstances, what will happen in a few years from now is more relevant for today’s economy than what will happen in a few decades, and what will happen in a few decades is more relevant than what will happen in a century from now.

This reasoning leads us to guess that replacing generations that live in the far future may have a small effect on today’s economic variables we are interested in and, also, can make it easier to solve the infinite equilibrium equations remaining after the start of the “far future”.

5.  Concluding remarks

From a theoretical perspective, this article provides conditions under which the set of Pareto optimal equilibria of a nonstationary consumption-loan overlapping generations economy is non-empty. Namely, the
Cass 1972
criterion must be valid and the economy prone to savings, a condition that is related to sufficiently high demographic growth rates, skewed endowment distributions toward younger periods of life, and meaningfully valued consumption on older periods of life.

The article also provides a modeling setup for overlapping generations economies that represent long-term economic activity, and Theorem
13
can be related to an interpretation of equilibria for which the perfect foresight hypothesis is relaxed.

Due to its macroeconomic implications, a result from
Section 3
that deserves attention is Proposition
7
, showing that the dynamic of real per capita savings completely characterizes efficient equilibria.

From an applied perspective, this article shows that an adequate way to calculate efficient equilibria on nonstationary overlapping generations economies is by choosing convenient tail economies, with well-known equilibria sets, and then using equilibrium equations to move backward. This algorithm provides a clear step-by-step direction for solving equilibrium equations and identifying efficient equilibria.

Finally, the specific economic problem that motivated the results of this article should be clearly stated. Worldwide plummeting fertility rates are leading to a fast demographic transition, and therefore pay-as-you-go social security systems are under high pressure. The optimal design of these systems can be done through overlapping generations models and, specifically, through nonstationary consumption-loan ones with heterogeneous households. However, this design requires the calculation of at least one efficient equilibrium.
