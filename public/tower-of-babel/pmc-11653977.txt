Comparative study of open elbow arthrolysis with and without hinge external fixation for the treatment of post-traumatic elbow stiffness
Authors: Changhao Yang, Jinye Dong, Fanxiao Liu, Xuehui Zhao, Jiajun Xu, Zhanchuan Yu, Shun Lu, Weicheng Xu, Lianxin Li, Jinlei Dong
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
BackgroundThe present study aimed to compare the functional outcomes of hinged external fixators and non-external fixation in open elbow arthrolysis (OEA) for post-traumatic elbow stiffness (PTES) and to evaluate their applicability and limitations in patients with posttraumatic elbow stiffness.MethodsThe clinical data of patients with PTES treated with OEA at our hospital between March 2015 and June 2022 were retrospectively analyzed. The assessed variables were the operation time, intraoperative blood loss volume, duration of hospitalization, and treatment costs. The elbow range of motion (ROM), Mayo Elbow Performance Score (MEPS), shortened disabilities of the arm, shoulder and hand questionnaire (Q-DASH) score, Oxford Elbow score (OES), Broberg and Morrey score (BMS), visual analog scale (VAS) for pain, and complications such as pin tract infection and heterotopic ossification were compared between patients with and without hinge external fixation. We also compared the baseline characteristics and functional outcomes of patients with and without hinge external fixation.ResultsA total of 156 patients (48 patients with hinged external fixators and 108 patients without external fixators) diagnosed with PTES were included in this study. The mean follow-up was 15.8 ± 3.6 months. Compared with patients without external fixators, those with external fixators showed significantly greater improvements in elbow flexion and extension ROM (59.6° ± 26.1° vs. 46.2° ± 26.2°, p = 0.004) but had a significantly longer duration of hospitalization (p < 0.001), significantly longer operation time (p < 0.001), significantly higher treatment costs (p < 0.001), and significantly greater intraoperative blood loss volume (p < 0.001). There were no significant differences between the two groups in terms of improvement in elbow rotation ROM, MEPS score, VAS score for pain, OES score, Q-DASH score, BMS score, or incidence of complications.ConclusionThe use of a hinged external fixator in open arthrolysis for posttraumatic elbow stiffness may result in short-term improvements in flexion-extension range of motion but is accompanied by increased blood loss, longer operative time, extended hospitalization, and higher costs. Further studies are needed to confirm these findings.Clinical trial numberNot applicable.

Introduction

The elbow joint is a complex structure that serves as an important connection between the shoulder and hand. The elbow plays a crucial role in various daily activities, such as eating, work, and hygiene, as well as in controlling the stability and fine movements of the hand [1, 2]. Mobility loss is more likely to occur after experiencing trauma to the elbow [3]. Elbow stiffness pertains to the restriction of movement and impairment of elbow joint functionality, which may arise from traumatic or nontraumatic factors. Elbow stiffness is characterized by limited flexion, extension, and rotation of the elbow [4, 5]. ROM of the elbow is 30–130°, which is sufficient for the performance of most routine daily tasks [6]. With the increasing use of devices such as smartphones and keyboards, modern daily activities require a broader range of elbow movements [5, 7]. Addressing elbow stiffness across varying levels of severity is critical to ensuring patients regain optimal functionality in their daily lives. However, the use of devices such as mobile phones, computer mice, and keyboards in modern tasks may necessitate a wide range of elbow movements [5]. Consequently, the decline in the activity and functionality of the elbow joint to a degree that interferes with everyday tasks indicates the presence of elbow joint stiffness. On the basis of the angles of extension and flexion, elbow stiffness can be divided into four levels: extremely severe (≤ 30°), severe (31–60°), moderate (61–90°), and mild (> 90°)6.

The most common cause of elbow stiffness is trauma, although there are other causes, such as heterotopic ossification (HO) and joint capsule contracture [8]. Trauma can result in damage to soft tissues, including muscles, which may be irreversible [3]. Therefore, it is crucial to focus on the treatment and rehabilitation of posttraumatic elbow stiffness (PTES). Owing to the complex anatomical structure of the elbow, the need for precise movements, and the frequent recurrence of elbow stiffness, arthroscopic surgery may not always yield satisfactory results. Therefore, when nonsurgical treatments fail to provide adequate pain relief or restore normal ROM, open elbow joint release surgery (open elbow arthrolysis; OEA) has emerged as a reliable option that is widely used to address elbow stiffness [9]. While OEA is a widely used surgical method to treat elbow stiffness, the addition of a hinged external fixator provides the unique advantage of maintaining joint stability while facilitating elbow movement during recovery [10, 11]. However, its application remains controversial due to the risk of complications and increased costs [12, 13]. This study evaluates these trade-offs by systematically analyzing the effectiveness of hinged external fixators in comparison to non-external fixation for PTES treatment. To ensure the stability of the elbow joint and achieve complete recovery of elbow ROM, surgeons typically apply a hinged external fixator for several weeks following OEA. The hinged external fixator differs from the traditional static fixator in that it not only ensures fixation but also allows for elbow movement. This facilitates the healing and recovery of soft tissues and enhances the stability of the elbow joint [14]. However, the use of hinged external fixators also carries the risk of complications, including infection at the pinhole site and injury to the radial nerve. These complications lead to increased expenses and greatly impact an individual’s quality of life and ability to regain normal function at a later stage [12]. Ruan et al. [15] conducted a retrospective study on 15 patients with posttraumatic elbow stiffness (ROM 0°), concluding that open arthrolysis combined with a monolateral hinged external fixator significantly improves range of motion and functional outcomes. However, the study had certain limitations, including a small sample size and its focus solely on patients with severe posttraumatic elbow stiffness, which may restrict the generalizability of its findings to a broader range of clinical scenarios. Xiao et al. [4] conducted a retrospective study on 49 patients with severe elbow stiffness, concluding that arthrolysis without external fixation is a safe and effective treatment with fewer complications compared to cases with external fixation. However, similar to Ruan et al.‘s study, this research was limited by its focus exclusively on patients with severe elbow stiffness, which may restrict the applicability of its findings to patients with different types of elbow stiffness. Compared to previous studies that primarily focused on severe elbow stiffness, our study aims to include patients with varying degrees of posttraumatic elbow stiffness and a relatively larger sample size, with the hope of providing a more comprehensive exploration of the functional outcomes of hinged external fixators versus non-external fixation in open elbow arthrolysis. The primary objective of this study is to compare the functional outcomes and clinical effectiveness of hinged external fixators versus nonhinged external fixation in OEA for PTES treatment. Additionally, the study aims to assess the applicability and limitations of these techniques for patients with posttraumatic elbow stiffness.

Materials and methods

Sample allocation

The selection of patients was based on strict inclusion and exclusion criteria (detailed in Sect. 2.2) to ensure homogeneity of the study population. Furthermore, the following measures were taken to minimize potential bias: all data were independently collected by a single trained researcher to ensure consistency; postoperative functional assessments were conducted by the same attending physician to reduce measurement error; and detailed comparisons of preoperative baseline characteristics between the groups revealed no significant differences.

Sample collection

Data from patients with PTES treated at our trauma center between March 2015 and June 2022 were retrospectively collected. A total of 387 patients underwent OEA at our trauma center. Among these patients, 156 met the inclusion and exclusion criteria and were included in the study cohort. The study was approved by the ethics committee of our hospital, and informed consent was obtained from each patient.

Inclusion and exclusion criteria

The inclusion criteria were as follows: [16] (1) aged between 18 and 60 years; (2) had a diagnosis of PTES on the basis of medical history, physical examination, and imaging, such as radiography, CT, or MRI; (3) had unilateral or bilateral injury; and (4) had a follow-up of more than 12 months.

The exclusion criteria were as follows: (1) age younger than 18 years or older than 60 years; (2) rheumatoid joint inflammation or bone tumors in the elbow; (3) burns or central nervous system damage; (4) incomplete clinical data; (5) a nonspecified chief surgeon; and (6) a history of mental illness. The patients were then divided into those who received hinged external fixation (group A) and those who did not receive hinged external fixation (group B) (Fig. 1).

Surgical technique

Patients were informed preoperatively about the potential necessity and importance of using a hinged external fixator due to elbow joint instability, as well as the possible inconvenience and additional costs associated with the fixator. Patients consented to its use. The patients underwent surgery either under general anesthesia or brachial plexus anesthesia. Patients were placed in the supine position, and a sterile air tourniquet was applied. All surgeries were performed by two experienced surgeons who were well trained in elbow joint release. The combined medial and lateral approach was used for OEA in 115 patients, whereas the posterior median approach was used in 41 patients. The choice of surgical approach and sequence was based on the location of previous surgical incision scars and the presence of HO. No significant difference in the selection of surgical approach was observed between the two patient groups (p > 0.05, Table 1).

In the combined medial‒lateral approach, the elbow joint was first accessed through the medial approach. The ulnar nerve was identified and protected at the medial edge of the triceps brachii. The triceps tendon was incised and reflected to the lateral side, exposing the posterior aspect of the elbow joint. The ectopic ossification and posterior capsule of the posteromedial medial collateral ligament (MCL) were resected while preserving the anterior bundle. If there was still obstruction on the posterior side, olecranon fossa plasty and olecranon tip resection were performed sequentially. The lateral approach was used to release the anterior part of the elbow joint, perform lateral column surgery, incise part of the extensor digitorum digitalis pacemaker, and expose the anterolateral part of the joint while preserving the lateral ulnar collateral ligament (LUCL). The surgeon then removed the HO and performed capsulotomy and arthrolysis of the coronal fossa and humeroradial joint. If there was bony blockage on the posterolateral side, the surgeon made an incision along the lateral edge of the triceps brachii to expose the lateral ulnohumeral joint, removed the ectopic ossification, and released the posterolateral joint capsule.

In the posterior midline approach, the first step was to create a single posterior midline incision. The skin was then freed along the incision on both sides under the deep fascia. Next, both sides of the triceps brachii muscle were exposed and incised. The ulnar nerve was located and isolated in the ulnar nerve groove behind the medial epicondyle of the humerus and was protected by a rubber strip. The triceps brachii muscle was completely separated and elevated to expose the olecranon fossa. The posterior wall of the joint capsule was sharply separated, and any fibrous tissue and free bone fragments in the olecranon fossa were removed. The bone surface where the bone was removed was sealed with bone wax. The triceps tendon and skin were retracted to expose the lateral side of the elbow joint, and then an appropriate anterior release was performed from the muscle space above.

Throughout each step of the surgical procedure, the surgeon checked the ROM to determine the effectiveness of the surgical release and to determine the next steps. The surgery was considered complete when satisfactory joint ROM (flexion and extension of 0°–140°, pronation and supination exceeding 50°) was achieved. Lateral stress tests and lateral fulcrum displacement tests were performed to evaluate the stability of the elbow joint. The ulnar nerve was then moved anteriorly to reduce the risk of postoperative ulnar nerve palsy. The use of the hinged fixator during surgery was determined based on the degree of elbow joint instability [17–19]. After the rotation axis of the elbow joint was determined, the upper and lower parts of the external fixator were placed on the bones on both sides of the elbow joint. Once satisfactory ROM was confirmed, the fascia and subcutaneous tissue were closed in layers, a drainage tube was placed, and the incision was closed.

Postoperative management

All patients used an analgesic pump for 3 days postoperatively to alleviate postoperative pain and facilitate postoperative exercise. The closed negative pressure drainage was retained until the drainage volume was less than 20 ml within a 24-hour period. The patients were administered one oral celecoxib capsule (0.2 g) twice daily for 6 weeks and then gradually discontinued antibiotic usage on the basis of their individual circumstances.

Postoperative rehabilitation is typically divided into three stages: early (within 2 weeks postoperatively), mid-term (3–6 weeks postoperatively), and late (after 6 weeks postoperatively). During the early stage, patients perform active exercises followed by passive exercises. The movements were gentle, and forcefulness was strictly avoided to prevent secondary injuries such as fractures. The clinicians observed the condition of the affected limb, including any swelling in the forearm or hand, as well as any neurological symptoms such as numbness in the fingers. If any of these symptoms occurred, the fixation was released immediately. In the middle-term stage, exercises were performed as tolerated, with a focus on active functional exercises while avoiding heavy weight-bearing or strenuous daily activities. Patients were recommended to visit the outpatient clinic of our hospital for a review at 6–8 weeks postoperatively. The decision to remove the external fixator depended on the specific conditions of recovery. In both the early and middle-term stages, patients were advised to apply an ice pack locally after exercise. In the later stage, forearm strengthening exercises were permitted. For passive rotation function exercises, patients were advised to avoid lifting heavy objects but to place the affected limb flat on a table and gradually perform small weight-bearing exercises (starting at 1 kg and slowly increasing) to increase elbow strength. Evaluation of outcomes

Patients were evaluated preoperatively and at 6 weeks and 12 months postoperatively via various methods, including in-hospital assessment, out-of-hospital phone calls or text messages, and outpatient review. All follow-up visits were conducted by the same surgeon to ensure consistency. The average follow-up was 15.8 ± 3.6 months (range 12–18 months). To objectively evaluate elbow joint function, we measured the elbow range of motion (ROM), Mayo Elbow Performance Score (MEPS) score, shortened disabilities of the arm, shoulder and hand questionnaire (Q-DASH) score, Oxford Elbow score (OES), Broberg and Morrey score (BMS), visual analog scale (VAS) for pain and recorded any postoperative complications [20–23]. Additionally, data such as operation time, blood loss, hospitalization time, and cost were collected and analyzed retrospectively. Standard anteroposterior and lateral radiographs of the elbow were obtained after surgery to evaluate the surgical effect and provide rehabilitation guidance.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analysis was conducted via SPSS 25.0 for Windows (IBM, Armonk, NY, USA). The data are presented as the proportion, mean ± standard deviation, and range, as appropriate. A paired t test was used to identify changes in the ROM, VAS score for pain, MEPS score, Q-DASH score, BMS, and OES within each group. Independent sample t tests were used to compare the functional outcomes between groups A and B preoperatively, 6 weeks postoperatively, and at the final follow-up. The chi-square test or Fisher’s exact test was used to compare the incidence of complications between the two groups. P < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

General information

Sample size estimation was conducted to ensure sufficient power for detecting meaningful differences in key outcomes. Based on a significance level of 0.05, a power of 0.80, and an expected moderate effect size (Cohen’s d = 0.5), a minimum of 64 patients per group was required. A total of 156 patients who underwent OEA for PTES from March 2015 to June 2022 were included in the study cohort. Group A consisted of 48 patients (37 men and 11 women) with an average age of 36.4 ± 11.3 years. Group B consisted of 108 patients (69 men and 39 women) with an average age of 36.8 ± 11.3 years. The original injury type did not significantly differ between the two groups and included injury types such as olecranon fracture, distal humeral fracture, terrible triad injury of the elbow, coronoid process fracture, and elbow dislocation. Furthermore, no significant differences were found between the two groups in terms of age, sex, surgical side, body mass index, or type of incision (p > 0.05, Table 1).

Discussion

PTES is a common complication after elbow trauma. The causative factors of PTES can be divided into intrinsic factors inherent in the joint structure and extrinsic factors such as HO and joint capsular contracture [9, 24–26]. Additionally, the development of PTES is reportedly affected by age, alcoholism, elbow immobilization time, and number of surgeries [27]. Currently, the most common and effective treatment for PTES is OEA [9]. Numerous studies have demonstrated that OEA effectively restores elbow function and enhances patient quality of life [28–30].

The stability of the elbow joint is dependent on several factors, including the consistency of the articular surface and the integrity of the ligaments, joint capsule, interosseous membrane, and arm and forearm muscles. The key structures involved in maintaining elbow stability are the anterior MCL, LUCL, and ulnohumeral joint [31–40]. In cases of PTES, the formation of HO and contracture of the joint capsule often result in impaired elbow function [24]. Elbow joint release surgery commonly results in partial or complete damage to the anterior bundle of the anterior MCL and the LUCL, resulting in elbow instability [41].

In recent years, many surgeons have used hinged external fixation to treat PTES, aiming to achieve elbow stability and promote healing of the injured soft tissues [10, 14]. Additionally, hinged external fixation can aid in postoperative rehabilitation exercises, create appropriate distraction of the elbow joint space, protect the articular cartilage, and promote recovery [42, 43]. Wang et al. [10] evaluated the functional outcomes of 25 patients with PTES who underwent elbow arthrolysis combined with external fixation and reported a significant increase in flexion and extension ROM from 33.4° to 97.4° at the final follow-up (mean follow-up of 16 months). Similarly, Sun et al. [11] evaluated 49 patients who underwent OEA combined with external fixation and reported an increase in flexion and extension ROM from 27° to 131° and an increase in the MEPS from 54° to 95° after 5 years of follow-up. Zhou et al. [44] evaluated the functional outcomes of 38 patients who underwent OEA combined with external fixation and reported an increase in the average ROM of the flexion‒extension arc from 27° to 126° and an increase in the ROM of rotation from 148° to 153° after a follow-up of 31 months. In our study, the flexion and extension ROMs of 48 patients with external fixators improved from 31° preoperatively to 100° at the final follow-up. Additionally, other functional indicators and pain scores also significantly improved from preoperatively to the final follow-up. The present findings are consistent with those of previous reports. These studies collectively demonstrate that the use of combined hinged external fixators leads to significant improvements in postoperative function while maintaining the stability of the elbow joint. However, in contrast, other studies have reported that the use of external fixators does not improve functional outcomes. Xiao et al. [4] concluded that there were no significant differences in functional outcomes among 49 patients with severe elbow stiffness treated with or without external fixators after an average follow-up of 28 months. Another study reported no significant difference in the change in ROM between patients treated with and without external fixators (89° versus 78°) [45]. Conversely, our study of 156 patients with PTES revealed that OEA with hinged external fixation led to a significant improvement in the ROM for flexion and extension compared with OEA without hinged external fixation.

Hinged external fixators are also associated with a range of complications, such as pinhole infection and radial nerve injury, which not only result in high costs but also significantly impact quality of life and the recovery of later functions [12]. A review of 100 consecutive patients who underwent treatment with hinged external fixators revealed that 15 patients developed minor complications (15%) and that 10 had serious complications (10%), including five cases of needle tract infection and four cases of needle loosening [12]. In our study, among the 156 patients who underwent surgery, 21 reported ulnar nerve symptoms, nine experienced elbow pain, six experienced HO recurrence, and two developed pin site infection. There were no significant differences in the incidence of complications between the two groups.

Although the stability of the elbow joint is confirmed during surgery, some surgeons still choose to use an external fixator after joint release to prevent instability during early recovery. In contrast, some surgeons believe that external fixation is not necessary for early recovery. Furthermore, surgeons must discuss the advantages and disadvantages of external fixation with patients prior to surgery. Some patients may decrease the use of external fixation because of the inconvenience and increased costs associated with the procedure. The present study aimed to compare the functional effects of external fixation after OEA versus OEA without external fixation. Both groups showed significant improvements in the flexion and extension ROM, rotation ROM, MEPS, VAS for pain, OES, Q-DASH, and BMS at the final follow-up compared with the preoperative values. Therefore, both methods achieved satisfactory functional results.

In the present study, the functional outcomes and VAS score for pain did not significantly differ between patients with and without external fixation. However, patients with external fixators showed greater improvements in elbow flexion and extension ROM than patients without external fixators did. Furthermore, patients without external fixators had significantly shorter hospitalization and operation times, lower costs, and less intraoperative bleeding than patients with external fixators. Additionally, two patients who received external fixation experienced pin tract infection. These findings confirm that OEA and hinged external fixation can improve the functional prognosis of the elbow in patients with PTES. Nonetheless, surgeons should be mindful of the potential complications associated with external fixation and consider all the factors when making treatment decisions.

Hinged external fixation offers several clinical benefits, including improved elbow stability, protection of soft tissue healing, and enhanced functional recovery. These advantages are particularly relevant in cases of postoperative instability, as supported by both our findings and prior studies [19, 46, 47]. However, the use of hinged external fixators is associated with certain drawbacks, such as longer operation time, greater intraoperative blood loss, higher hospitalization costs, and complications like pin tract infections and nerve injuries [12]. Based on existing literature and the results of the present study, we believe that hinged external fixation may be considered for patients with elbow joint instability. These findings align with prior research emphasizing the selective application of external fixation in cases with instability [18, 19]. In the present study, patients with hinged external fixators demonstrated greater improvement in flexion-extension ROM compared to those without fixators. However, this benefit was accompanied by longer hospitalization time, increased treatment costs, and greater blood loss. These results highlight the need for careful evaluation of clinical indications to balance the benefits and potential drawbacks of hinged external fixation. Surgeons should tailor their treatment approach to the specific clinical characteristics of each patient.

The present study had several limitations. First, the study design was retrospective, which introduces the possibility of selection bias. Second, while the sample size of this study was comparable to or even larger than that of similar studies, it may still not be sufficient to detect significant differences between groups. The estimated sample size required 64 patients per group, but the final cohort was smaller, potentially limiting the study’s statistical power. Third, elbow ROM measurements by the same doctor and surgeries by two different surgeons could introduce detection and procedural bias. Fourth, the relatively short follow-up period restricts the ability to assess long-term outcomes, such as sustained functional improvement and potential late complications. Future prospective studies with extended follow-up durations are needed to comprehensively evaluate the durability of these outcomes. Additionally, the small number of complications observed hindered statistical analysis of their relationship with risk factors, and adherence monitoring during rehabilitation was not systematically assessed. While functional outcomes were the primary focus, patient-centered factors such as quality of life and satisfaction remain critical and should be incorporated into future research. Prospective studies with larger sample sizes, extended follow-up durations, and validated tools to assess both functional and patient-centered outcomes are needed to provide a more comprehensive understanding of hinged external fixators in PTES management.

Conclusions

This study suggests that while the application of a hinged external fixator in open arthrolysis for posttraumatic elbow stiffness may result in short-term improvements in flexion-extension range of motion, it is also accompanied by greater blood loss, prolonged operative time, extended hospitalization, and increased healthcare costs. Further studies with larger sample sizes and longer follow-up periods are needed to validate these findings.
