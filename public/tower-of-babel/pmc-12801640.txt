Stereotactic re-irradiation for lymph node recurrence of breast cancer
Authors: Zied Fessi, Salomé Bonnier, Kaoutar Lodyga, Jennifer Wallet, Maël Barthoulot, Chloé Delerue, Pauline Lemoine, Paul Archer, Marie Dworczak, David Pasquier
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Background and purposeBetween 5% and 20% of patients with breast cancer (BC) experience locoregional recurrence (LRR). Managing LRR in previously irradiated areas is particularly challenging. We evaluated the toxicity and efficacy of stereotactic body re-irradiation in this context.Materials and methodsThis was a single-center retrospective study conducted between January 2009 and December 2022. It included patients who underwent stereotactic re-irradiation for LRR of BC. We assessed toxicity, local control (LC), progression-free survival (PFS), and overall survival (OS).ResultsEighteen patients were included. The median dose of the initial radiotherapy (RT) was 60 Gy (range: 50–68 Gy). Recurrences occurred in level I-II nodes (n = 10), internal mammary nodes (IMN; n = 4), level III (n = 2), and level IV (n = 2). The median interval between the two irradiations was 11 years (range: 1–24 years). The most commonly used stereotactic re-irradiation regimens were 6 × 6 Gy and 3 × 9 Gy. Median D50% to the GTV and PTV was 36.9 Gy (32–41.8 Gy) and 35.7 (30.2–40.6 Gy), respectively. The D0.035 cc to the brachial plexus was 11.2 Gy (range, 4–33.6 Gy). With a median follow-up of 4.5 years from the start of SRT (IQR: 2.0‒7.5), no radiation-induced brachial plexopathy was observed. The cumulative incidence of locoregional recurrence was 56.4% (95%CI: 30.8–75.6) at 2 years. The 5-year overall survival (OS) rate was 75.5% (95CI: 39.7–91.8), with most deaths attributed to distant metastases.ConclusionStereotactic re-irradiation is a well-tolerated treatment option for LRR in breast cancer. Particular care should be taken to minimize the dose to the brachial plexus. While local control is acceptable, the risk of further regional recurrence remains high.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12885-025-15418-9.

Introduction

Lymph node recurrence in breast cancer (BC) is associated with poor prognosis and a higher risk of metastatic recurrence [1]. While the literature often addresses both local and nodal recurrences in the same entity, locoregional recurrences (LRRs) are rarely isolated and are frequently associated with metastatic disease. The 10-year incidence of isolated lymph node recurrence ranges from 0.1% to 5%, and that of overall LRR ranges from 5% to 20% [2–4]. These rates are influenced by factors such as disease stage, type of initial treatment (surgical approach to the tumor and axilla, with or without associated regional node radiotherapy), and molecular subtype [4–7]. However, the incidence of LRR when associated with metastatic disease remains poorly described. The axillary and supraclavicular regions are the most frequently affected [2, 3].

Management of LRR may involve surgery, radiotherapy, chemotherapy, targeted therapy, and more recently, immunotherapy [8–10]. Radiotherapy (RT) remains a cornerstone of locoregional management in BC‒routinely administered after lumpectomy and in high-risk cases after radical mastectomy. In cases of LRR, a second course of RT may be administered alone or combined with surgery.

The ESTRO consensus classifies re-irradiation (re-RT) into two types: Type 1 re-irradiation involves geometric overlap with prior irradiated volume, while Type 2 involves no overlap. Despite advances, concerns about cumulative dose toxicity persist, especially when recurrence occurs within or near the previously irradiated field [11].

While several studies have examined breast re-irradiation in the context of local relapse [12–15], data on re-RT for nodal relapse are limited. Treating nodal relapse is particularly challenging due to the proximity of critical structures such as the brachial plexus, heart, and lungs. RT has advanced significantly, with stereotactic body radiotherapy (SBRT) transforming the treatment of various tumor sites and enabling re-RT in selected cases. SBRT has proven effective in managing bone and brain metastases, and in prostate and lung cancers [16–19]. In breast cancer, SBRT has shown promise for metastatic disease, but data on its use in LRR are scarce [18, 20].

The Oscar Lambret Center (COL), one of the first facilities in France to adopt SBRT, has explored this innovative modality. Here, we present our findings on the feasibility, toxicity profile, and clinical outcomes of SBRT for nodal recurrence in BC.

Materials and methods

This retrospective study was conducted at the Oscar Lambret Center in Lille, France. Inclusion criteria were: (1) age > 18 years; (2) Prior treatment for BC, including RT to the breast or chest wall, with or without regional lymph node irradiation‒regardless of whether the first RT was performed at the Oscar Lambret Center‒as long as treatment plans or descriptive reports on target volumes and doses (for 2-D RT) were available; (3) diagnosis of local and/or regional lymph node recurrence confirmed by imaging and/or biopsy. Re-irradiated sites were not always the initial recurrence; prior locoregional or metastatic relapses could have occurred; and (4) recurrence presenting as either isolated LRR or metastatic disease. All cases are discussed in a tumour board meeting. The indication for surgery is considered when histological confirmation is required to assess tumour markers, provided that surgery is feasible. SBRT was selected in all cases due to the anatomical proximity of the recurrence to previously irradiated regions, thereby classifying it as type 1 re-RT.

A 2 mm slice CT scan was used for contouring and planning. Breath-controlled devices were not used. The use of fiducial markers was evaluated on a case-by-case basis by radiation oncologists and dosimetrists, and markers were implanted by an interventional radiologist when required.

The gross tumor volume (GTV) was delineated on the planning CT. Rigid or deformable registration with FDG-PET/CT was performed when necessary. A planning target volume (PTV) was defined as a 1–2 mm geometric expansion of the GTV. No clinical target volume (CTV) margins were added. For OARs, we delineated the heart, lungs, ribs, skin, and brachial plexus. For the brachial plexus, we used the RTOG atlas. In some cases, it was difficult to apply this atlas to our planning CT, so we performed fusions with contrast-enhanced CT or MRI when available. Treatment was delivered using the CyberKnife system. Organ at risk (OAR) constraints and target volume objectives followed the department’s internal protocol. A representative case is shown in Fig. 1.

For the brachial plexus, D0.1 cc, D0.035, and D2% were evaluated. Dosimetric parameters for target volumes included GTV and PTV in cc, and D50%, D98%, and D2% in accordance with ICRU 91 guidelines [21].

The equivalent dose in 2 Gy fractions (EQD2, in Gy2) was calculated using an α/β ratio of 3. The cumulative dose was defined as the total dose received by an OAR across both RT courses. For patients initially treated with 2D techniques, the maximum OAR dose during the first irradiation series was assumed to be equal to the prescribed dose.

Acute and late toxicities were assessed using the Common Terminology Criteria for Adverse Events (CTCAE) version 5.0. The primary endpoint was the occurrence of toxicity greater than grade 2. Secondary endpoints included locoregional recurrence-free survival (LRFS), progression-free survival (PFS), and overall survival (OS).

LRFS was defined as the time interval from the start of SBRT to the date of local or regional recurrence, including that of the irradiated lesion. PFS was defined as the interval from the start of SRT to the date of recurrence or death from any cause. Patients who survived without recurrence were censored at the date of last contact. OS was defined as the time from the start of SRT to death from any cause. Patients who were alive at the time of analysis were censored at their last follow-up date.

The median follow-up period from the start of SBRT was estimated using Schemper’s method (inverse Kaplan–Meier).

PFS and OS curves were estimated using the Kaplan–Meier method.

Due to the small sample size, the results are presented as a range rather than as an IQR.

Data were collected from hospital records without any patient interaction. The Commission for Clinical Studies (Institutional Review Board) of the Oscar Lambret Center approved the study (CEC-2023-023) and confirmed that independent ethics committee approval was not required. The study complies with the Reference Methodology MR004 of the French National Commission on Informatics and Freedoms (CNIL “Commission Nationale Informatique et Liberté”). All patients were informed, and none objected to the use of their medical data for research purposes.

All methods were carried out in accordance with relevant guidelines and regulations. Written non opposition was obtained from all patients included in the study in accordance with the applicable regulations.

This protocol was approved by the Clinical Trials Committee of the Oscar Lambret Center, reference CEC-2023-023. Due to the retrospective nature of the study, Centre O. Lambret Review Board waived the need of obtaining specific informed consent.

Results

Between January 2009 and December 2022, 18 patients met the final inclusion criteria.

At initial treatment

Patient characteristics at the time of initial treatment are presented in the Supplementary Table. Two patients (11%) were male. The right side was affected in 11 out of 18 patients. Two-thirds had regional lymph node involvement, and only one patient had metastatic disease at diagnosis. A lumpectomy was performed in ten patients (55.6%), and axillary dissection in 15 (83%).

Systemic treatment with chemotherapy and/or hormonal therapy was administered to 17 (94%) and 15 (83%) patients, respectively. In the first radiotherapy course, the median dose‒including the tumor bed boost‒was 60 Gy (range: 50‒68), delivered over a median of 30 fractions (25‒38).

When regional lymph node irradiation was performed, the median dose was 50.2 Gy (50–56) in 1.8–2 Gy per fraction.

Levels III and IV lymph nodes were irradiated in ten patients (62.5%), and the internal mammary chain in 11 patients (69%). Only one patient received irradiation to the axillary area. No residual toxicity was reported.

Recurrence characteristics

Eighteen cases of LRR were identified, including four with synchronous metastatic recurrence. Levels I and II were the most common recurrence sites. The internal mammary node was involved in four cases. Recurrence distribution is summarized in Fig. 2.

The median time interval between initial treatment and recurrence was 6.1 years [0.6‒14.1].

Systemic therapy was initiated prior to re-RT. Chemotherapy was administered to eight patients, with a median of three prior lines and maintenance in 62.5%. Endocrine therapy was used in 13 patients (72.2%), with a median of two lines before radiotherapy and maintenance in 92.3% of cases. The mean and median time between any type of first recurrence of disease and the time of intervention with re-irradiation was 3.83 years and 3 (0–12) years respectively.

The median time interval between the two courses of radiation was 11.1 years [1‒24.4]. Ten patients (59%) required fiducial placement. The most commonly used dose/fraction regimen was 36 Gy in six fractions. Other regimens included 27 Gy in three fractions, 30 Gy in five fractions, and 36 Gy in nine fractions. The median treatment duration was 13 days [4–22].

For target volumes, the median GTV in cc was 7.2 [0.8‒65.6], with a corresponding median PTV of 18.6 [3.6‒95]. Median D98%, D50%, and D2% doses to the PTV are presented in Table 1.

Toxicity analysis

No acute toxicities were observed. One patient had brachial pain due to tumor invasion of the plexus before re-RT, which did not worsen during follow-up after SBRT. No radiation-induced plexitis was reported. Grade 2 skin fibrosis was observed in one patient.

Survival analysis after SRT

The median follow-up after the start of stereotactic re-RT was 4.5 years (IQR, 2.0–7.5).

At the end of the follow-up, five patients had no recurrence. For local control, we observed four recurrences of irradiated lesions, all associated with distant metastasis.

Regarding regional control, 10 cases of non-irradiated lymph node recurrence were observed, almost all of which were associated with metastatic recurrence. The cumulative incidence of LRR was 50% (95%CI: 26.9–70.1) at 1 year, 56.4% (95%CI: 30.8–75.6) at 2 years, and 65.9% (95%CI: 36.2–84.2) at 5 years (Fig. 3).

The median PFS was 10.6 months. PFS rates at 1, 2, and 3 years were 44.4% (95%CI: 21.6–65.1); 38.1% (95%CI: 16.6–59.6); and 19.1% (95%CI: 3.6–43.6), respectively (Fig. 4).

At the end of the follow-up period, six deaths were noted, of which five were related to metastatic disease. One patient died without any recurrence.

The median survival was 5.6 years. Overall survival at 3, 5, and 7 years was 86.3% (95%CI: 54.7–96.5); 75.5% (95%CI: 39.7–91.8); and 30.2% (95%CI: 4.7–62.6), respectively (Fig. 4).

Discussion

To the best of our knowledge, this series represents the largest study to date evaluating the tolerance and feasibility of stereotactic re-RT for nodal LRR in BC. Our findings support the feasibility of SBRT for nodal relapse, with a favorable safety profile and effective local control of irradiated lesions. However, the rate of new regional recurrence remains high.

Popescu et al. (2021) published a case report describing re-RT 18 years after the initial treatment for bilateral BC [22]. The first course involved bilateral 2D RT with a dose of 40 Gy in 16 fractions, including Levels III and IV. Recurrence occurred in the second left intercostal space (IMN), where the initial estimated dose was only 20%‒40% of the prescribed dose. Re-RT was delivered using a linear accelerator with a step-and-shoot technique, administering 40 Gy to the 80% isodose of the PTV in five fractions, with one fraction every other day. No toxicity or further recurrence was observed.

Rice et al. published a case report of a woman with metastatic left BC who was treated with chemotherapy, surgery, and RT to the chest wall and regional lymph nodes (levels I–IV), receiving a total dose of 50 Gy delivered in 25 fractions [23]. The patient subsequently developed recurrence in the internal mammary chain, which was managed with stereotactic radiotherapy, delivering 55 Gy in 11 fractions guided by daily cone-beam computed tomography (CBCT). Six months after treatment, the patient remained responsive. The cumulative cardiac dose and associated toxicities were not reported.

Fattahi et al. reported on a cohort of patients who underwent re-RT for BC [12]. A total of 72 patients were included, of whom 19 (26%) received lymph node irradiation alone. Thirty-four patients had significant residual disease at the time of re-RT. The target volumes were extensive and included the breast or chest wall, with or without regional lymph nodes. Proton therapy was administered in 20 patients. The most commonly prescribed regimens were conventional fractionation at 50 Gy (65% of cases) and hypofractionated schedules delivering 40.05 Gy (2.25–7.3 Gy per fraction) in 29% of cases. The median equivalent dose in 2 Gy fractions (EQD2), including boost doses, was 103.54 Gy (range: 95.04–109.62 Gy). The two-year LRFS rates were 74.6% in the overall cohort, 57.1% in patients treated with curative intent but with residual disease, and 50.7% in those treated with palliative intent or residual disease. In our study, this rate was 43.6%, although one-third of patients were already metastatic at the time of re-RT. Grade 2 late toxicities were predominantly cutaneous and included fibrosis, necrosis, and telangiectasia, along with two cases of brachial plexopathy. In both cases, the plexopathy was attributed to tumor invasion rather than radiation. No radiation-induced brachial plexopathy was observed in our cohort.

A recent study examined breast or chest wall re-RT after local recurrence in 28 patients [24]. Radiotherapy was normofractionated in 93% of cases, with a median dose of 48 Gy. The irradiation fields were extensive and included the breast/chest wall with or without lymph nodes (irradiated in seven patients). The median EQD2 to the brachial plexus was 99 Gy [90.6–114.3]. Reported toxicities were limited to the skin, and no neurological toxicities were observed.

In our study, the median D0.035 cc EQD2 for the brachial plexus was 11.3 Gy [2.9–57.6]. Because the dosimetric data from the initial irradiation were incomplete, we assumed that the brachial plexus had previously received the entire prescribed dose to the nodal areas.

The limitations of this study include its retrospective design and small sample size. Additionally, reproducing prior irradiations remains challenging. Several patients were treated during their initial course at external centers, sometimes using outdated irradiation techniques. Moreover, changes in morphology due to weight loss or gain, or alterations in patient positioning, can affect image fusion and, consequently, the calculation of cumulative doses.

Conclusion

SBRT appears to be a well-tolerated re-RT modality for LRR in BC. Extreme caution should be exercised regarding the dose delivered to the brachial plexus. Although local control is satisfactory, the incidence of new regional recurrences remains high.
