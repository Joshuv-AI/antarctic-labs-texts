Early inhaled isoflurane sedation in neurocritical patients with invasive intracranial pressure monitoring: the NEURO-CONDA randomized pilot trial
Authors: Cristina Murcia-Gubianas, Marina Vilà-Rivas, Laia Tarré Ferré, Cristina Fuster Bertolin, Patricia Sebastián Cernuda, María Luisa Palomanes Espadalé, Joan Martinez Sancho, Carol Lorencio
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractPurposeEvidence on inhaled sedation in neurocritically ill patients remains limited, mainly due to concerns about potential increases in intracranial pressure (ICP). We aimed to evaluate the efficacy and safety of early isoflurane sedation compared with propofol in invasively monitored neurocritical patients without intracranial hypertension.MethodsNEURO-CONDA is a phase IV, randomized, open-label, parallel-group pilot trial conducted in a tertiary ICU (May 2024–October 2025). Adult neurocritical patients requiring ICP monitoring and without intracranial hypertension were randomized to receive either propofol or isoflurane. The primary endpoints were efficacy (assessed with RASS and BIS™) and safety, defined as the occurrence of serious adverse drug reactions (ADRs), including sustained ICP elevation or cerebral perfusion pressure (CPP) < 60 mmHg requiring increased vasopressor support. Nociception (NOL®) and other systemic parameters were recorded for up to 72 h. Analyses were performed according to the intention-to-treat principle.ResultsThirty patients were included (15 per group), and baseline characteristics were comparable; 19 had traumatic brain injury. Sedation efficacy was 100% in both groups throughout the study period. No serious ADRs occurred. ICP and CPP remained stable throughout the study period without requiring higher doses of vasopressors in the isoflurane sedated group.ConclusionsIn this randomized pilot trial, early sedation with isoflurane was feasible and achieved reliable deep sedation without compromising intracranial or cerebral perfusion parameters in invasively monitored neurocritical patients without intracranial hypertension. Larger randomized studies are warranted to confirm these findings and assess long-term neurological outcomes.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s13054-026-06101-x.Keywords: Isoflurane, Neurocritical care, Intracranial pressure, Multimodal monitoring

Introduction

Sedation and analgesia are essential components of the management of critically ill patients, enabling control of pain, anxiety, and agitation, as well as adequate adaptation to mechanical ventilation (MV). In neurocritical patients, sedation is more complex, as it must contribute to the control of intracranial pressure (ICP), preserve cerebral perfusion, decrease cerebral metabolic demand, and prevent secondary insults that could worsen the primary brain injury (e.g., seizures or cortical spreading depression (CSD)) [1–4]. Traditionally, intravenous sedation with propofol or benzodiazepines has been the predominant strategy in intensive care units (ICUs). However, prolonged administration is associated with several limitations, including metabolite accumulation, tolerance and tachyphylaxis, prolonged time to awakening, increased risk of delirium, hypertriglyceridemia and propofol-related infusion syndrome (PRIS). These drawbacks have driven the search for alternative strategies. Inhalation sedation with isoflurane, widely used in surgical settings, has recently been introduced in ICUs, primarily in Europe [5]. Its pharmacodynamic and pharmacokinetic characteristics could offer advantages in the sedation of patients with acute brain injury. These include minimal renal and hepatic metabolism, shorter time to cognitive recovery, the absence of tachyphylaxis, tolerance, and withdrawal, the ability to promote spontaneous ventilation by facilitating respiratory drive, the potential opioid-sparing and muscle-relaxant effect, and other neuroprotective properties under investigation (anticonvulsant, preconditioning to cerebral hypoxia) [6].

However, its use in neurocritical care remains limited, mainly due to concerns about increased ICP and decreased cerebral perfusion pressure (CPP) related to its vasodilatory effects [7]. Consequently, evidence in this population is still scarce.

Methods

The study was approved by the Research Ethics Committee for Medicinal Products of our center (CEIm 2024.011) and the Spanish Agency for Medicines and Health Products (AEMPS -CTIS 2024–512077-27–00) and was conducted in accordance with the principles of the Declaration of Helsinki. Given the clinical status of the included patients, informed consent was obtained from their legal representatives.

Study design and population

This is a phase IV, randomized, open-label, parallel-group pilot trial that included neurocritical patients with traumatic brain injury (TBI), stroke and intracranial hemorrhage. The recruitment period spanned from May 2024 to October 2025. Patients underwent an inclusion process (90% of them enrolled within the first 6 h following the initial computed tomography (CT) scan confirming brain injury) followed by randomization, and each enrolled patient was assigned to one of two treatment arms (allocation ratio 1:1): a control group receiving propofol or an experimental group receiving isoflurane via the Sedaconda ACD-S® device (Sedana Medical). Data were collected at six time points (0, 1 12, 24, 48 and 72 h). Patients over 18 years who, due to their clinical condition and the criteria of their attending physician, required ICP monitoring and whose ICP value was within the normal range (< 20 mmHg) were included. Patients with intracranial hypertension, initial values of regional cerebral oxygen saturation (rSO2) < 50%, severe hypoxemia (pO2 < 60 mmHg), high inspiratory oxygen fraction (FiO2) requirements (> 0.6) and/or high positive end-expiratory pressure (PEEP) values (> 10cmH2O), shock with vasoactive support with norepinephrine > 0.5 mcg/kg/min and/or lactate > 2.5 mmol/L, pregnant patients, patients with any contraindication to isoflurane administration, patients at the end of life or admitted for donation purposes, and those with refusal of informed consent by family member/legal representative were excluded (Fig. 1).

Sedation protocol

Sedation was dynamically adjusted throughout the study period to achieve and maintain the predefined target. This management was guided by continuous multimodal monitoring [8] and individualized according to each patient’s clinical condition, particularly considering the vulnerability of the injured brain [9].

Objectives

The primary objective was to evaluate the efficacy and safety of isoflurane compared to propofol. Efficacy was defined as the sedative’s ability to achieve the target level of sedation. Safety was defined by the percentage of serious adverse drug reactions (ADRs) detected in each group: increased intracranial pressure (ICP) > 25 mmHg for > 5 min, ICP > 20 mmHg for 30 min or unresponsive to standard treatment, cerebral perfusion pressure (CPP) < 60 mmHg requiring vasopressor support > 20% of basal doses, or rSO2 < 50% for 30 min or a decrease > 20% from baseline. These criteria were used to discontinue the sedative therapy under study.

Secondary objectives were to assess analgesic status using data provided by NOL® (Medasense), record various complementary neuromonitoring parameters, and perform exhaustive monitoring of respiratory and hemodynamic parameters as well as the need for vasoactive support throughout the various study time points.

Demographic and systemic variables

Baseline variables collected included demographic data, comorbidity (Charlson Comorbidity Index (CCI)), and severity (Simplified Acute Physiology Score (SAPS II)), as well as hospital admission characteristics (days of MV, length of stay, and mortality), neurological diagnosis at admission, and initial Glasgow Coma Scale (GCS) score. In addition, vital signs, vasopressor doses, laboratory and respiratory variables were recorded at each time point.

Monitoring of sedation and nociception

In all patients, the degree of sedation was monitored using the Bispectral Index System (BIS™, Medtronic) and the Richmond Agitation-Sedation Scale (RASS), both standard monitoring methods used in cases of deep sedation in the ICU. For patients sedated with isoflurane, the level of expired gas was also monitored using a specific gas analyzer (Phillips). Nociception was assessed using the Nociception Level Index (NOL®) device with the PMD200 platform (Medasense).

Neuromonitoring

Neuromonitoring included continuous ICP measurement using a parenchymal sensor (Raumedic) and calculation of CPP (CPP = Mean Arterial Pressure (MAP)—ICP). The ICP and CPP values at 0 h control were obtained immediately after catheter placement, considering these values as the patient’s baseline. Additionally, rSO2 was monitored using an INVOS 7100 monitor with bifrontal near-infrared spectroscopy (NIRS) technology (Medtronic), as well as various parameters assessed by transcranial Doppler (TCD) (GE Healthcare). As described in Fig. 1, baseline ICP and rSO2 values were determining factors for the patient’s inclusion in the trial. Following inclusion, one or other sedative was initiated depending on the randomization process.

Statistical analysis and sample size calculation

Statistical analyses were conducted under the intention-to-treat principle, including all randomized patients according to their assigned group regardless of protocol deviations. Continuous variables were assessed for normality using graphical methods and the Shapiro–Wilk test, and summarized as mean (SD) or median (IQR) as appropriate; categorical variables were expressed as counts and percentages. Baseline comparability between the isoflurane and propofol groups was evaluated using Student’s t-test or Mann–Whitney U test for continuous variables, and chi-square or Fisher’s exact test for categorical variables. Given the pilot nature of the study, these comparisons were considered descriptive and no adjustment for multiple testing was performed. An exploratory post hoc subgroup analysis was conducted in patients with TBI following the same analytical approach. All tests were two-sided with a significance level of 0.05. Analyses were performed using RStudio (version 4.5.2). No formal sample size calculation was conducted [10]; however, the sample size (15 patients per group) aligns with general recommendations for pilot studies 24–40 patients in total [11, 12].

Results

Study population

Thirty patients were included, reaching the planned sample size for this pilot study. Baseline demographic, clinical, and severity characteristics are presented in Table 1 and were comparable between groups, with no clinically relevant differences in key variables. The most frequent admission diagnosis was TBI with 10 patients in the isoflurane and 9 patients in the control group. It was therefore decided to conduct a post hoc subgroup analysis of these patients.

Efficacy and safety

The sedation target set by the attending physician during the study period was deep sedation. Efficacy was 100%, with no differences in RASS and BIS™ values obtained according to the sedative (Fig. 2a and b). The mean initial isoflurane doses required were 1.89 mL/h (maximum doses were 3.55 mL/h). Exhaled gas values measured by the analyzer ranged from 0.2 to 0.5%. In all cases, the Sedaconda ACD-S® device could be positioned in the standard configuration without clinically relevant respiratory effects (Supplementary Table 2). The average initial propofol doses required were 1.87 mg/Kg/h (maximum doses of 2.02 mg/Kg/h) (Table 2). No adjuvant sedatives were required in either the control or experimental group during the study period.

Neuromonitoring safety parameters including ICP and CPP, remained stable throughout the study period in both groups. Lower ICP values were detected at 48 and 72 h, and higher CPP values were observed at 72 h in the isoflurane study group (Fig. 2c and d). Norepinephrine doses were not statistically different between groups and never exceeded 0.2 mcg/kg/min (Table 2). The administration of osmotic agents was not required in any of the patients. Furthermore, no patients experienced serious ADRs, nor were any hepatic [13] or renal [14] related to sedation recorded during the study period. Finally, ICU and hospital mortality rates were similar.

Nociception monitoring and other parameters

According to the established interpretation ranges, NOL® values < 10 are associated with overanalgesia, values between 10 and 25 with adequate analgesia, and values > 25 with underanalgesia [15]. At almost all of the analyzed time points, more than 70% of patients presented NOL® values < 10, with no differences between groups (Supplementary Table 1). Additional monitoring using NIRS and TCD was available for all patients (Supplementary Table 2). The ICP waveform morphology showed a similar distribution between groups, with adequate intracranial compliance (P1 > P2) in 17 patients and impaired compliance in 12 (1 missing value). The analytical evaluation and respiratory parameters are detailed in Supplementary Tables 3 and 4.

Subanalysis in TBI

In the subgroup of patients with TBI (n = 19), baseline characteristics were comparable between both groups (Supplementary Table 5). Sedation efficacy was 100% throughout the study period (Supplementary Table 6), and no significant differences were found in any of the variables studied (Supplementary Table 6 and 7). Supplementary Fig. 1 shows the evolution of ICP values over the study period in both groups. Neurological follow-up at 6 months using the Glasgow Outcome Scale–Extended (GOSE) was available in this sub-analysis, with no statistically significant differences between groups [16] (Supplementary Fig. 2).

Discussion

To our knowledge, NEURO-CONDA is the first randomized clinical trial evaluating early inhaled isoflurane sedation in neurocritical patients undergoing invasive ICP monitoring. Sedation efficacy was 100% at all time points, with no differences between groups in RASS and BIS™ values (Fig. 2). Throughout the entire trial, our findings suggest that in fragile brains, the doses of isoflurane necessary to achieve deep sedation are, especially at the beginning of it, slightly lower than the usual doses in other critical patients (mean 1.89 mL/h versus approximately 3 mL/h according to standard practice). It is clear that more studies and larger sample sizes are needed to verify this finding. We also believe that it would be of interest to incorporate more advanced sedation monitoring such as density spectral array analysis [17] or individualized BIS™ targets [18] in future studies.

Unlike previous reports, which evaluated inhaled sedation as a late switch strategy, our study focuses on the acute and most vulnerable phase of brain injury. The first noteworthy bibliographic reference appears in 2012, when Bösel et al. [19] published a study that included 19 patients undergoing MV and diagnosed with neurocritical conditions (subarachnoid hemorrhage (SAH), intracranial hemorrhage, and ischemic stroke) who underwent a switch from intravenous to inhaled sedation. The objective of this study was to evaluate different cerebral and systemic physiological parameters when changing sedation from intravenous to inhaled. They concluded that the increase in ICP values was not clinically relevant, although MAP and CPP did decrease, making multimodal neuromonitoring necessary when performing it. This study is really interesting because it links the switch from intravenous to inhaled sedation with the onset of hypotension and impaired cerebral perfusion. In our study, one notable finding is the decrease in ICP observed in the isoflurane-treated group, which reached statistical significance compared to the group undergoing intravenous sedation at 48 h (p = 0.049) and 72 h (p = 0.029) (Fig. 2). Moreover, higher CPP values were observed at 72 h in the isoflurane study group (Fig. 2) and the need for vasopressor support did not show significant differences between groups (Table 2). Patients with shock have been excluded.

Isoflurane is known to induce dose-dependent cerebral vasodilatation [20–24], suggesting that the relatively low concentrations used for ICU sedatives may limit clinically relevant intracranial effects when strict multimodal monitoring is applied. The observed reduction in ICP at 48 and 72 h time points raises the hypothesis that sustained exposure to low-dose isoflurane may induce adaptive cerebrovascular responses. In patients with preserved autoregulation, compensatory vasoconstrictive mechanisms may offset vasodilatory effects, maintaining intracranial stability. Confirmation of this hypothesis requires future studies incorporating dynamic autoregulation monitoring through pressure reactivity index (PRx) and mean flow index (Mx) [18]. Finally, we also believe that the incorporation of brain tissue oxygen partial pressure (PbtO₂) and cerebral microdialysis would be of great use to better individualize the management especially of patients with TBI.

To date, only a limited number of clinical studies [25, 26] have evaluated the use of isoflurane sedation in neurocritical ill patients. Preclinical studies [27, 28], including experimental models of SAH, suggest that isoflurane may exert anti-inflammatory effects and reduce the incidence of CSD.

Finally, we would like to highlight the sub-analysis performed in patients with TBI, representing the biggest subgroup of patients, showed results consistent with the overall population, with no evidence of ICP increase or CPP deterioration. Although the sample size was small (n = 19), these findings are noteworthy, as previous studies specifically evaluating early isoflurane sedation in neurocritical TBI patients are lacking.

Sevoflurane has also been explored in neurocritical care as an inhaled alternative to intravenous agents. However, differences in pharmacokinetics and pharmacodynamics between sevoflurane and isoflurane should be considered. Furthermore, isoflurane is the only volatile agent authorized for use in ICUs [29].

Regarding nociception monitoring, an interesting finding of our study is that most patients presented NOL® values < 10. Importantly, opioid selection and dose adjustments were left to the discretion of the treating physician, according to individual clinical judgment. It should be emphasized that the primary objective of this study was not to titrate opioid administration based on NOL index values. It is also important to consider that a substantial proportion of our cohort consisted of trauma patients, who may require higher opioid doses compared to non-traumatic populations. Incorporating objective nociception monitoring in states of deep sedation, where behavioral scales are not valid, could allow for a reduction in opioid exposure. It would also be interesting to evaluate whether the potential cost-saving effect of isoflurane is confirmed. Few studies to date validate its use in the ICU [30], and none of these studies specifically evaluated the use of NOL® monitoring in neurocritical patients.

Limitations

This study has several limitations. First, its small sample size and single-center design limit its statistical power. Second, the open-label design may introduce clinical management bias, although the use of monitored endpoints partially mitigates this effect. Patients remained deeply sedated throughout the study period, with no sedation interruptions or awakening attempts. As a result, wake-up times, ventilator weaning, and extubation outcomes were not evaluated. Finally, it was not designed to assess long-term clinical outcomes; therefore, no conclusions can be drawn regarding neurological prognosis. It is important to note that our cohort did not include patients with SAH since our hospital is not a designated center for this condition. Therefore, these results should be interpreted as physiological safety rather than evidence of clinical superiority.

Conclusions

In this randomized pilot trial, early inhaled sedation with isoflurane was feasible and associated with a safety profile comparable to that of conventional intravenous sedation in neurocritically ill patients undergoing invasive neuromonitoring. Our findings suggest that, with prior experience, strict multimodal monitoring, and appropriate patient selection, inhaled sedation can be used from early stages without compromising neurological control. This approach may facilitate more predictable neurological wake-up assessments and potentially reduce complications associated with prolonged intravenous sedation. Larger, prospective, randomized studies are needed to confirm these findings and evaluate the impact of inhaled sedation on both ICU outcomes and medium- to long-term neurological prognosis.
