Uncertainty Sets for Distributionally Robust Bandits Using Structural Equation Models
Katherine Avery, Chinmay Pendse, David Jensen

Abstract
Distributionally robust evaluation estimates the worst-case expected return over an uncertainty set of possible covariate and reward distributions, and distributionally robust learning finds a policy that maximizes that worst-case return across that uncertainty set. Unfortunately, current methods for distributionally robust evaluation and learning create overly conservative evaluations and policies. In this work, we propose a practical bandit evaluation and learning algorithm that tailors the uncertainty set to specific problems using mathematical programs constrained by structural equation models. Further, we show how conditional independence testing can be used to detect shifted variables for modeling. We find that the structural equation model (SEM) approach gives more accurate evaluations and learns lower-variance policies than traditional approaches, particularly for large shifts. Further, the SEM approach learns an optimal policy, assuming the model is sufficiently well-specified.



1
Introduction

In multi-armed bandit settings, the distribution of data can shift between the training and deployment phases, leading to inaccurate policy evaluations and decreased deployment performance.
Distributionally robust
policies can protect against distribution shift by considering the worst-case performance on an
uncertainty set
, or a set of covariate and reward distributions that are likely to be encountered during deployment
(
Si et al. 2023
)
. Ideally, the uncertainty set will contain the distributions from the deployment data but is not so large that it contains irrelevant or implausible distributions. This paper demonstrates how to improve robust evaluation and learning by using mathematical programs constrained by structural equation models (SEMs) to tailor the uncertainty set. Mathematical programming refers to both discrete and continuous constrained optimization problems. The SEM approach leads to improved evaluation and learning, particularly for larger shift sizes.

Offline policy evaluation
uses previously collected data to assess a policy’s performance, and
offline policy learning
uses that data to learn new policies. Distributionally robust offline evaluation methods attempt to find the worst-case expected reward:

\inf_{\mathbb{P}\in\mathcal{U}}\mathbb{E}[Y(\pi)]

(1)

Distributionally robust offline policy learning (Eq.
2
) finds the policy
\pi
that maximizes Eq.
1
. This formulation is standard in the distributionally robust bandit literature
(
Si et al. 2020
;
Si et al. 2023
;
Shen et al. 2024
;
Kallus et al. 2022
;
Mu et al. 2022
)
. It is useful in risk-sensitive or adversarial environments.

\arg\max_{\pi\in\Pi}\inf_{\mathbb{P}\in\mathcal{U}}\mathbb{E}[Y(\pi)]

(2)

Most current methods for evaluating and learning robust policies use ideas from the distributionally robust optimization (DRO) literature
(
Si et al. 2020
;
Si et al. 2023
;
Shen et al. 2024
;
Kallus et al. 2022
)
. These methods define an uncertainty set
\mathcal{U}
as all distributions some distance
r
away from the training distribution, where
r
can be defined using KL divergence (KLD), Wasserstein distance, etc. To differentiate between distributionally robust evaluation and learning
in general
from these specific methods, we will refer to the latter using the term
distributionally robust optimization
or DRO. A common issue with DRO methods is that
\mathcal{U}
contains distributions that are implausible or unlikely to be seen in the deployment data
(
Thams et al. 2022
;
Mu et al. 2022
)
. For example, DRO may include shifts such as negative age values or very large shifts in the rewards given covariates. DRO therefore often produces overly conservative evaluations and policies.

This work uses SEMs to create an uncertainty set with more plausible distribution shifts. SEMs model variables as additive models of the variables’ parents and a noise term, and they are widely used in economics, social science, and other fields to model causal relationships. We model shifts in the distribution of the noise terms and the coefficients and intercepts of the additive model. These shifts are encoded as constraints in a mathematical program, which can be efficiently solved using standard commercial optimizers.

We show how background knowledge and causal modeling can be used to constrain the uncertainty set, and we create a new robust evaluation and learning method using
SEM-constrained programs (SEMCPs)
. Finally, we show how to identify distribution shifts for distributionally robust evaluation and learning in practice using conditional independence tests. This allows practitioners to tailor the uncertainty set to
only
include variables that shift. We find that the method gives more accurate evaluations and learns lower-variance policies than traditional approaches, particularly for large shifts. Further, the method learns an optimal policy, assuming that the SEM is sufficiently well-specified.

1.1
Motivating Example

Alice uses multi-armed bandits to set the level of security (low, medium, or high) in a new system based on data from old systems. She decides to create a robust policy that will maximize the expected reward of the worst-case distribution of reward and covariates given background knowledge (as in Eq.
2
). She will also run a robust evaluation (as in Eq.
1
).

Suppose Alice has training data from several different systems and a set of covariates that could affect our chosen level of security (e.g., authentication requirements). For simplicity, assume that the level of security affects only two covariates: number of allowed login attempts (
X_{0}
) and the breadth of access rights (
X_{1}
). These two covariates affect the number of breaches (
X_{2}
).
Y
represents the outcome or reward, which heavily penalizes breaches and somewhat rewards user convenience. Using background knowledge and causal structure learning on the training data, Alice first learns a causal graph. Second, she uses conditional independence testing, as laid out in Section
4.1
, to determine which variables have shifted among the systems in her training data. Finally, Alice assigns structural equations to the shifted variables in each system in the training data (using a package like DoWhy’s GCM
(
Blöbaum et al. 2024
)
). Alice then applies evaluation and learning using SEMCP, as laid out in Sec.
4.2
and
4.3
. Only the structural equations for the shifted variables need to be included in the mathematical program.

If Alice does not have any more information about her new system, then she can apply the mathematical programming method directly to get a robust evaluation or policy. If she has some prior knowledge about the covariates of the new system, then she can constrain the program even further to obtain a more accurate estimate. For example, if Alice knows that the expected number of breaches is 25% greater than the average, then she can add that constraint to the mathematical program.

2
Related Work

We survey three relatively distinct areas of related work: traditional distributionally robust bandits; causal inference approaches to distributional robustness; and transportability for bandit domain adaptation. Further related work can be found in Appx.
F
.

2.1
Distributionally Robust Bandits

Traditionally, work on distributionally robust bandits uses uncertainty sets that contain all distributions some distance
r
away from the training distribution. The radius
r
of this uncertainty set can be defined using a variety of measures or metrics, such as KL-divergence, f-divergence, or Wasserstein distance
(
Si et al. 2020
;
Si et al. 2023
;
Mu et al. 2022
;
Shen et al. 2024
)
. Distributional robustness can also be combined with double robustness
(
Chernozhukov et al. 2018
)
, which is particularly useful when the behavior policy is not known
(
Kallus et al. 2022
)
. The primary downside of distributionally robust bandits is that their uncertainty sets are large, leading to overly conservative policies. This work attempts to address this problem by modeling potential distribution shifts.

Appx.
F
discusses DRO for counterfactual risk minimization and the connection between robust and pessimistic policies.

2.2
Robustness Using Causal Inference

Distributional robustness can be framed as robustness to interventions on a structural causal model (SCM)
(
Meinshausen 2018
;
Bühlmann 2020
;
Christiansen et al. 2022
)
. Essentially, a set of SCMs represent a set of plausible test distributions. For example, the worst-case loss under distribution shift can be estimated by modeling shifted variables given each setting of their causal parents
(
Thams et al. 2022
)
. This work describes a mathematical programming method that uses a class of SCMs to represent plausible test distributions.

Distributionally robust predictors are thought to rely on invariant parts of the SCM. This idea has inspired work in anchor regression, which regularizes ordinary least squares to favor invariant predictions
(
Rothenhäusler et al. 2021
)
. Environmental invariances can also be exploited for robust learning
(
Arjovsky et al. 2019
;
Magliacane et al. 2018
;
Rojas-Carulla et al. 2018
)
. For example, multi-armed bandits can be trained to depend only on invariant aspects of the environment
(
Saengkyongam et al. 2023
)
.

2.3
Transportability for Bandit Domain Adaptation

Transportability transfers causal information from one domain to another
(
Pearl and Bareinboim 2011
;
Pearl and Bareinboim 2014
)
and can be used to efficiently learn policies in a target domain given data from a source domain
(
Bellot et al. 2023
)
. For example, data collected from other environments can be used to efficiently learn a policy in an online environment (i.e. warm start the policy)
Bellot et al. 2023
. Our work focuses instead on an offline, distributionally robust formulation, represented by Eq.
1
and
2
. Rather than leveraging collected data to learn a policy in an online environment, we evaluate and learn policies over a set of possible environments.

3.2
Structural Equation Models

Structural equation models (SEMs) are used to model causal relationships. The equation model of a shifted variable
W_{i}
is of the form

f_{W_{i}}(Pa(W_{i}),\epsilon_{W_{i}})=\epsilon_{W_{i}}+\beta_{W_{i}}+\sum\limits_{z_{i}\in Pa(W_{i})}\beta_{W_{i}z_{i}}f_{z_{i}}(Pa(z_{i}),\epsilon_{z_{i}})

(4)

That is,
W_{i}
can be modeled as the sum of a noise term
\epsilon_{W_{i}}
, an intercept, and the equation model for each of
W_{i}
’s parents times a coefficient
\beta_{W_{i}z_{i}}

(
Pearl 2009
)
. Suppose that
W_{i}
is
Y
and the parents
Pa(W_{i})
are
X_{0}
and
X_{1}
. Then, the SEM for
Y
would be
\epsilon_{Y}+\beta_{Y}+\beta_{YX_{0}}f_{X_{0}}+\beta_{YX_{1}}f_{X_{1}}
.

Now, suppose that an action
A
intervenes on Y and changes its SEM. We can one-hot encode
A
as
\{A_{0},A_{1},...,A_{n}\}
.
Y
would be
A_{0}(f_{Y}(Pa(Y),\epsilon_{Y})_{0})+A_{1}(f_{Y}(Pa(Y),\epsilon_{Y})_{1})+...+A_{n}(f_{Y}(Pa(Y),\epsilon_{Y})_{n})
.

If
W_{i}
is a binary variable, it is typically modeled as the sigmoid of the SEM. That is, the SEM models the logits, rather than the variable itself. The noise term is typically dropped since direct and indirect effects do not cleanly separate into two linear terms
(
Breen et al. 2013
)
:
\text{sigmoid}(f_{W_{i}}(Pa(W_{i})))
.

3.3
Assumptions

This work makes the following assumptions, which are common in the distributionally robust bandit literature
(
Si et al. 2020
;
Mu et al. 2022
;
Kallus et al. 2022
;
Wang et al. 2024
)
:

Assumption 1
.

Bounded rewards:
Y
cannot take on an infinite value.

Assumption 2
.

Positivity:
\pi_{0}(a|x)
is non-zero for every setting of the context variables.

Assumption 3
.

Positive densities: For all conditional probability distributions of the reward
P(Y|A,X)
, the probability density
g(Y|A,X)
has a non-zero lower bound
g(Y|A,X)\geq c>0
, where
c
is some constant.

The method laid out in Sec.
4
makes assumptions necessary for creating causal models of distribution shifts. (The Taylor approximation baseline also makes these assumptions
(
Thams et al. 2022
)
.)

Assumption 4
.

Causal factorization: It is possible to factorize
\mathbb{P}(\mathbf{X},Y,A)
as a product of variables given their parents (Eq.
3
).

Assumption 5
.

Sufficiently identified parents of shifted variables: For each
W_{i}
in Eq.
3
, we assume that
Pa(W_{i})
is identified sufficiently well.

Note that we perform conditional independence testing when determining which variables belong in
\mathbf{W}
and which in
\mathbf{V}
. Then, only the shifted variables
\mathbf{W}
are modeled.

Assumption
5
is the strongest assumption made. In Sec.
6
, we violate it by providing mis-specified graphs. Fortunately, the error terms
\epsilon
in the SEMs account for some uncertainty in the causal model. Further, the uncertainty set
\mathcal{U}
can encode uncertainty about the graph. Uncertainty in the values of
\beta
allow for edge deletions and edge additions that do not create cycles since
\beta
values can be set to zero.

4
Methodology

4.1
Identifying Distribution Shifts

Before applying the mathematical programming method, we need to determine which variables shift so that they can be included in the constraints for the optimization problem. First, we will apply a causal discovery method (such as PC
(
Spirtes et al. 2000
)
) to determine the parents of each variable.

Distribution shifts are identified using conditional independence tests. To determine the conditional independence of two empirical samples, we test the independence of
S_{i}\perp\!\!\!\perp B|Pa(S_{i})
for each variable
S_{i}
in the causal graph.
B
is an indicator vector for the dataset of individual samples
(
Huang et al. 2020
;
Budhathoki et al. 2021
)
.

S_{i}
and
Pa(S_{i})
are drawn from a dataset
D
constructed from the two concatenated samples:
D=[D_{0},D_{1}]^{\top}
. The indicator vector
B
is constructed by assigning 1 for all samples from
D_{0}
and -1 for all samples from
D_{1}
.

Our experiments use a kernel-based conditional independence test
(
Zhang et al. 2011
;
Budhathoki et al. 2021
)
, though a variety of alternative methods could be employed instead.

4.3
Policy Learning using SEM-Constrained Mathematical Programs

For policy learning (Eq.
9
), we seek to find a policy
\pi
that maximizes Eq.
7
:

\arg\max\limits_{\pi\in\Pi}\min\limits_{\mathbb{P}\in\mathcal{U}_{SEM}}\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i}^{N}f_{Y}(Pa(Y),\epsilon_{Y})

(9)

To learn
\pi
, we find the worst-case distribution first and then apply policy iteration to maximize the return on that distribution. The reward function used for policy iteration is then just the equation model for
Y
with the worst-case values for the uncertain terms.
Brandfonbrener et al. 2021
and
Chen and Jiang 2019
show that offline learning using the direct method for multi-armed bandits has a convergence rate of
O(\frac{1}{\sqrt{c}}\sqrt{\frac{\log|\mathcal{Q}|}{n})}
.
c
is the constant in Assumption
2
;
n
is the number of samples; and
\mathcal{Q}
is the model class for the reward function. (See
Brandfonbrener et al. 2021
and
Chen and Jiang 2019
for details.) Note that the policy can be solved analytically for simple SEMs.

Appx.
H.2
proves that the worst-case distribution can be found before finding the policy because the actions
A
do not affect the worst-case distribution. We assume that the worst-case shift in each variable
W_{i}
can co-occur with the worst-case shift in any other variable. This allows us to choose the worst possible shift for each variable in advance, including the variables that
A
affects.

It is also worth noting that less modeling is required for learning than for evaluation since we are only interested in finding the optimal action for each context. Appx.
H.3
proves that the optimal policy is affected by only the structural equations of the intervened variables, along with the
\beta
values modifying the effect of the intervened variables. That is, when the equation for
Y
is expanded, the only terms that include intervened variables affect the policy. Assuming positivity,
A
is unaffected by distribution shifts in its ancestors. The optimal action for a given context is the same, though some contexts may be more or less frequent for different distributions. However, changes in descendants can change the optimal action to maximize
Y
.

5
Experiments

For every dataset, each distribution is assigned to either the observed set or the partially observed set. The observed (or training) set is used for training and identifying shifts, and both covariates and rewards are observed. The covariates of the partially observed (or test) set are used for identifying shifts, but the rewards are not observed. This setup is similar to that of
Mu et al. 2022
.

We compare SEMCP with DRO
(
Si et al. 2023
)
, factored DRO (fDRO)
(
Mu et al. 2022
)

7
Discussion

Overall, we found that causal-graph-based methods perform give more accurate evaluations and lower-variance policies compared to KL-ball methods. The results are more pronounced for evaluation since covariate shifts affect the evaluation more dramatically. SEMCP is more similar to KLD-based methods when the shifts are smaller since the KL ball is more likely to be a reasonable model. A major advantage of the SEM method is it finds an optimal policy given that the SEM is sufficiently well-specified, whereas KLD methods can converge to local extrema.

Generalization to out-of-distribution data requires some amount of modeling
(
Kaur et al. 2022
)
. That model could be a KL ball or Wasserstein ball with a single parameter (the radius
r
), or it could be a more complex model, as discussed in this work. If potential distribution shifts are small perturbations around the training data, then a KL ball may be a good model. However, distribution shifts are often more complex, involving shifts in only some conditional or marginal distributions.

Further, practitioners often have background knowledge about the uncertainty in their system. For example, practitioners can bound the the values taken by the variable
Age
so that no distribution shift results in negative or unrealistically large
Age
values. Background knowledge can also be more complex. Sec.
4.2
shows how to bound potential shifts based on subsets of the training data.

The primary limitation of this work is the potential for model misspecification. See Sec.
3.3
for a discussion of assumptions. Note that all the methods discussed (including DRO and fDRO) require splitting the training set and modeling the differences among the splits, either by measuring KLD or causal modeling. However, causal modeling approaches are more powerful when there are larger differences between splits. For example, if a practitioner has deployed an algorithm in two very different regions and wishes to deploy to a third, then causal modeling may be more useful.

The broader impacts of this work are the same as other improvements upon distributionally robust evaluation and learning. Robust evaluation and learning seeks to create worst-case evaluations and conservative policies, which could be useful for the safe deployment of multi-armed bandit algorithms, especially in adversarial environments where the worst case is likely. Other the other hand, conservative policies are not always appropriate and can decrease average return compared to more optimistic policies. It is therefore up to practitioners to decide the kinds of policies and evaluations that are appropriate for their problem setting.

8
Conclusion and future work

Distributionally robust evaluations estimate the worst-case performance over a set of distributions, and distributionally robust learning creates conservative policies. Robust evaluation and learning are useful for multi-armed bandit generalization. This paper seeks to create a method for robust evaluation and learning that tailors the uncertainty set to specific domains using SEMs and allows practitioners to easily encode background knowledge. Note that this work focuses on traditional minimax robustness, though future work could put a prior over the uncertainty set and use Bayesian model averaging
(
Hoeting et al. 1999
)
. In this work, we focus on the shape and size of the uncertainty set.

We find that SEMCP gives more accurate worst-case evaluations and learns low-variance, robust policies compared to traditional approaches for large shifts. Importantly, policy learning with SEMCP converges to an optimal policy if the model is well-specified, while more traditional distributionally robust policy learning approaches can converge to local maxima.

Future work includes extending the SEM method to reinforcement learning with disentangled representations. Causal modeling may be useful for robust reinforcement learning for applications such as personalization, though it would be expensive for high-dimensional settings like robotics.

Appendix C
SOS2 constraints

Special ordered sets of type 2 (SOS2) include ordered lists with two adjacent non-zero elements
[
Beale and Tomlin 1970
]
. In Sec.
4.2
, the weights
\lambda
are in SOS2. Some solvers, like AIMMS’s solver, can enforce that certain variables are in SOS2
[
Bisschop 2021
]
. Alternatively, the following constraints can be added:

\displaystyle\forall i,b_{i}\in\{0,1\}

(11a)

\displaystyle\sum\limits_{i=0}^{n-1}b_{i}\leq 2

(11b)

\displaystyle\forall i,\forall j,b_{i}+b_{j}\leq 1\text{ if }i\neq j,i\neq j+1,i\neq j-1

(11c)

\displaystyle\forall i,0\leq\lambda_{i}\leq b_{i}

(11d)

where
b
is a binary indicator variable that helps enforce SOS2. SOS2 constraints are implemented using mixed-integer programming because of the inclusion of
b
.

SOS2 constraints are often used to encode piecewise functions. Given a set of breakpoints, a point on the x-axis can be assigned to a point on the y-axis of the piecewise function by calculating a weighted average of the two breakpoints the x-axis point falls between. The weights
w
would be in SOS2.

We can use the sigmoid piecewise approximation from Sec.
4.2
as an example. Suppose our x-axis point
f_{W_{i}}(Pa(W_{i}))=-2
. Constraint
8b
becomes
0\cdot f_{W_{i},l}+\frac{5}{6}\cdot-3+\frac{1}{6}\cdot 3+0\cdot f_{W_{i},u}=f_{W_{i}}(Pa(W_{i}))=-2
. Constraint
8c
becomes
0\cdot 0+\frac{5}{6}\cdot 0.05+\frac{1}{6}\cdot 0.95+0\cdot 0=0.2
. The piecewise approximation of sigmoid(
-2
) is therefore 0.2.

Bisschop 2021
provides an overview of SOS2 constraints and piecewise functions in Chapter 7.

D.1
Taylor approximation experiment details

The Taylor approximation method was applied as in
Thams et al. 2022
. All shifted continuous variables were modeled as Gaussian distributions, and all shifted binary variables were modeled as binomial distributions.

The first step of the method involves creating a vector of shifts, referred to as
\delta
in
Thams et al. 2022
. To find the shifts in the Gaussian and binomial distributions, all the data was used. The second step of the method applies the Taylor approximation. Because this step is memory-intensive,
Thams et al. 2022
recommends downsampling the data. In this work, we found that 3000 samples was sufficient to get an accurate estimate, so 3000 samples were used for both datasets.

D.2
Structural equation experiment details

As described in Sec.
4
, continuous variables were modeled directly as structural equations, and binary variables were modeled as structural equations in the logits. The error terms for the continuous variables were all modeled as Gaussian distributions. Any shifts in the Gaussian error term
\epsilon^{0}
are modeled using mean and variance shifts:
(\epsilon^{0}+\mu)\sigma
.

Structural equation models were assigned to every distribution in the observed (training) set. These models were used to find the bounds for the optimization problem. For example,
\beta_{YX_{0}}
may be 0.05 in one model and 0.1 in another. Therefore, the bounds for
\beta_{YX_{0}}
would be
l_{\beta_{YX_{0}}}=0.05
and
u_{\beta_{YX_{0}}}=0.1
. The only constraint enforcing the maximum or minimum value of individual variables was the non-negativity constraint since that is necessary for the proofs in Appx.
H
.

For continuous variables, root nodes consist of only the error term plus the intercept, though these can be easily combined into one term. Therefore, continuous root nodes were modeled by a Gaussian.

For binary root nodes, the shift
(\epsilon^{0}+\mu)\sigma
would not have been appropriate, and modeling only shifts in the intercept would yield a root node with all the same values. Therefore, shifts were modeled using KL divergence, as in Sec.
4.2
. However, KL divergences of zero or near zero were filtered out, and the few binary root nodes in the voting dataset had a divergence of near zero.

All SEMs and bounds were found using the full dataset, though the data was downsampled for the optimization to avoid large numbers of constraints. We found that 1000 samples per node yielded accurate estimates for the synthetic and voting datasets.

D.3
DRO and fDRO experiment details

DRO and fDRO alternate between finding
\alpha
, representing the worst-case distribution, and taking policy learning steps. (See
Si et al. 2023
for details.) If
\alpha
reached an invalid value, the optimization was restarted up to 20 times.

Learning was warmstarted with a non-robust policy. For each policy learning step, a new policy was learned for 50 epochs. A new policy was learned a maximum of 500 times, though the final policy was returned if
\alpha
converged before the 500th iteration.

For fDRO, the worst-case reward shifts for each action and setting of the covariates can be cached. See Sec. 4.1.1 of
Mu et al. 2022
for details about the reward shift calculation. In this work, we round the covariate values to three decimal places for fDRO so there are fewer reward shifts that need to be calculated.

The KL divergence was calculated by finding the maximum divergence between the full training distribution (i.e., all of the training data) and each individual training distribution.

Both DRO and fDRO are run on the full dataset.

Extended related work

Counterfactual risk minimization attempts to minimize the regret of an estimator on counterfactual actions in importance sampling
[
Swaminathan and Joachims 2015
]
. Distributionally robust optimization can be used for counterfactual risk minimization. The uncertainty in the empirical distribution is formalized by creating an uncertainty set of distributions that could have generated the empirical distribution
[
Faury et al. 2020
,
Sakhi et al. 2020
]
.

Pessimistic policies
[
Jin et al. 2021
,
Swaminathan and Joachims 2015
,
Sakhi et al. 2024
]
can be thought of as equivalent to distributionally robust policies
[
Faury et al. 2020
,
Sakhi et al. 2020
]
. For example, in counterfactual risk minimization, sample-variance penalization
[
Swaminathan and Joachims 2015
]
is equivalent to distributionally robust optimization with chi-squared uncertainty sets
[
Faury et al. 2020
]
.

, Alice learns a causal graph using data and background knowledge; uses conditional independence testing to find the shifted variables; and assigns structural equations to the shifted variables in each geographic region.
