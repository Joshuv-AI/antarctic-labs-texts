Numerical stability of force-gradient integrators and their Hessian-free variants in lattice QCD simulations Journal: Elsevier, Preprint CERN-TH-2025-093
Kevin Schäfers, Jacob Finkenrath, Michael Günther, Francesco Knechtli

Abstract
We investigate the numerical stability of force-gradient integrators and their Hessian-free variants within the molecular dynamics step of the Hamiltonian Monte Carlo algorithm in lattice QCD simulations. A linear stability analysis of (Hessian-free) force-gradient integrators is conducted by investigating the harmonic oscillator as a test equation. By performing detailed stability investigations for the entire family of self-adjoint integrators with up to eleven exponentials per time step, we detect promising integrator variants that are providing a good trade-off between accuracy and numerical stability. Simulations for the two-dimensional Schwinger model demonstrate that there are no significant differences in the stability domain of a force-gradient integrator and its Hessian-free counterpart. Furthermore, lattice QCD simulations are conducted to emphasize the significance of numerical stability as a metric for evaluating the computational efficiency of integrators when applied to lattice QCD simulations.

1
Introduction

The Hamiltonian Monte Carlo (HMC) algorithm
duane1987hybrid
is a frequently employed algorithm for lattice QCD simulations.
In the molecular dynamics (MD) step of the HMC algorithm, trial configurations are generated by integrating the Hamiltonian equations of motion from an initial configuration for a fictitious time
\tau
.
In a subsequent Metropolis step
metropolis1953
, configurations are accepted or rejected by investigating the energy change
\Delta\mathcal{H}
along a trajectory.

The selection of the numerical integration scheme is crucial for an efficient and structure-preserving integration of the equations of motion.
Firstly, the numerical integration scheme must be volume-preserving and time-reversible to satisfy the detailed balance condition
duane1987hybrid
, ensuring that the Markov process converges to the correct equilibrium.
Secondly, the links are elements of the special unitary group
\mathrm{SU}(3)
, and therefore we additionally require that the integrator provides numerical approximations situated on the Lie group manifold.

1.1
Decomposition algorithms

For separable Hamiltonian systems
\mathcal{H}(\mathbf{q},\mathbf{p})=\mathcal{T}(\mathbf{p})+\mathcal{V}(\mathbf{q})
, decomposition algorithms
mclachlan2002splitting
,
omelyan2003symplectic
facilitate the derivation of explicit numerical integration schemes that adhere to the aforementioned properties. In addition to conventional splitting methods
mclachlan2002splitting
, force-gradient integrators
omelyan2003symplectic
enhance the computational process by incorporating a symplectic corrector
wisdom1996symplectic
into the momentum updates.
The correction necessitates the inclusion of the
force-gradient term
, which involves the Hessian of the potential.
Hessian-free force-gradient integrators
schäfers2024hessianfree
utilize an approximation of the force-gradient updates, effectively replacing the force-gradient term in the force-gradient step with a second evaluation of the force
-\mathcal{V}_{\mathbf{q}}(\mathbf{q})
.
The efficiency of the Hessian-free variants has been demonstrated in various large-scale lattice QCD simulations, see e.g.
Jung2017xef
,
Finkenrath2022eon
,
Finkenrath2023sjg
,
schäfers2024hessianfree
.

1.2
Tuning integrators

When tuning a numerical integration scheme for lattice QCD simulations, we aim at achieving the optimal acceptance rate of
\langle P_{\mathrm{acc}}\rangle_{\mathrm{opt}}=\exp(-1/n)
with
n
denoting the convergence order of the numerical integration scheme
TAKAISHI20006
.
For normally distributed
\Delta\mathcal{H}
, the acceptance rate corresponds to the variance
\sigma^{2}(\Delta\mathcal{H})
via

\langle P_{\mathrm{acc}}\rangle=\mathrm{erfc}\left(\sqrt{\sigma^{2}(\Delta\mathcal{H})/8}\right),

where
\mathrm{erfc}
denotes the complementary error function
KNECHTLI20033
.
Consequently, one aims at maximizing the step size
h
while satisfying

\sigma^{2}(\Delta\mathcal{H})\leq 8\cdot\mathrm{erfc}^{-1}(\langle P_{\mathrm{acc}}\rangle_{\mathrm{opt}})^{2},

(1)

i.e., we are primarily concerned with the region

10^{-2}\leq\sigma^{2}(\Delta\mathcal{H})\leq 1,

which corresponds to acceptance probabilities ranging from from 61.7% to 96%.

1.3
Numerical stability

During the tuning process, the numerical stability of the integrator assumes paramount importance. Decomposition algorithms applied to separable Hamiltonian systems are only conditionally stable, necessitating the selection of a sufficiently small step size
h
.
The primary challenge lies in identifying whether the accuracy or the numerical stability of the numerical integration scheme becomes the limiting factor.
In such instances, a superior integrator can be obtained by selecting an algorithm that is more accurate or more stable, respectively.

While the accuracy of decomposition algorithms has been extensively studied
omelyan2003symplectic
,
schäfers2024hessianfree
, the numerical stability of decomposition algorithms has only been investigated for conventional splitting methods
BlanesStability
.
Since (Hessian-free) force-gradient integrators turn out to be more efficient decomposition algorithms for increasing lattice volumes, an investigation of the numerical stability of (Hessian-free) force-gradient integrators is of particular interest.
It is commonly argued
EDWARDS1997375
,
instabilityMD
that the instability in lattice QCD can be likened to that of a collection of oscillator modes, with the onset of the instability caused by the mode of highest frequency,
\omega_{\max}
.
Based on this hypothesis, the linear stability analysis of decomposition algorithms when applied to the harmonic oscillator is sufficient to study the numerical stability for lattice QCD simulations.

The present paper contributes to providing a linear stability analysis of force-gradient integrators and their Hessian-free variants. This analysis enables the identification of accurate decomposition algorithms with a large stability domain within the family of self-adjoint algorithms.

Subsequently, Section

will undertake a linear stability analysis of force-gradient integrators by tailoring the existing linear stability analysis for conventional splitting methods

2
A brief survey of decomposition algorithms

In this section, we will briefly introduce splitting methods and (Hessian-free) force-gradient integrators for separable Hamiltonian systems of the form

\mathcal{H}(\mathbf{q},\mathbf{p})=\mathcal{T}(\mathbf{p})+\mathcal{V}(\mathbf{q})=\frac{1}{2}\mathbf{p}^{\top}\mathbf{M}\mathbf{p}+\mathcal{V}(\mathbf{q})

(2)

with symmetric and positive definite matrix
\mathbf{M}\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times d}
.
Since we will apply the integrators to the harmonic oscillator to perform the linear stability analysis, we consider the canonical case with phase space
\mathbb{R}^{2d}
here.
The formulations of the integrators when the phase space is the cotangent bundle
T^{*}\mathcal{G}
over a configuration space manifold
\mathcal{G}
that is a semi-simple matrix Lie group can be found in
A
.
For a more general formulation, we refer to
schäfers2024hessianfree
,
kennedy2013shadow
.

2.1
Hamiltonian equations of motion

In the canonical case, the vector fields of the kinetic and potential energy zero-forms are given by

\displaystyle\hat{\mathcal{T}}

\displaystyle=\sum_{i,j=1}^{d}m_{i,j}p_{j}\frac{\partial}{\partial q_{i}},

\displaystyle\hat{\mathcal{V}}

\displaystyle=\sum_{i=1}^{d}\mathcal{V}_{q_{i}}(\mathbf{q})\frac{\partial}{\partial p_{i}}.

(3)

Consequently, the equations of motion read

\displaystyle\dot{\mathbf{q}}

\displaystyle=\hat{\mathcal{T}}\mathbf{q}=\mathbf{M}\mathbf{p},

\displaystyle\dot{\mathbf{p}}

\displaystyle=-\hat{\mathcal{V}}\mathbf{p}=-\mathcal{V}_{\mathbf{q}}(\mathbf{q}).

The natural splitting
\mathcal{H}=\mathcal{T}+\mathcal{V}
yields two easily solvable Hamiltonian systems, namely

\displaystyle\begin{pmatrix}\dot{\mathbf{q}}\\
\dot{\mathbf{p}}\end{pmatrix}

\displaystyle=\begin{pmatrix}\mathbf{M}\mathbf{p}\\
\mathbf{0}\end{pmatrix},

\displaystyle\begin{pmatrix}\dot{\mathbf{q}}\\
\dot{\mathbf{p}}\end{pmatrix}

\displaystyle=\begin{pmatrix}\mathbf{0}\\
-\mathcal{V}_{\mathbf{q}}(\mathbf{q})\end{pmatrix}.

(4)

The exact flows of these two subsystems are

\displaystyle\e^{t\hat{\mathcal{T}}}\begin{pmatrix}\mathbf{q}_{0}\\
\mathbf{p}_{0}\end{pmatrix}

\displaystyle=\begin{pmatrix}\mathbf{q}_{0}+t\mathbf{M}\mathbf{p}_{0}\\
\mathbf{p}_{0}\end{pmatrix},

(5)

\displaystyle\e^{t\hat{\mathcal{V}}}\begin{pmatrix}\mathbf{q}_{0}\\
\mathbf{p}_{0}\end{pmatrix}

\displaystyle=\begin{pmatrix}\mathbf{q}_{0}\\
\mathbf{p}_{0}-t\mathcal{V}_{\mathbf{q}}(\mathbf{q}_{0})\end{pmatrix}.

(6)

In this context, we refer to (
5
) as a
position update
and to (
6
) as a
momentum update
.

2.2
Splitting methods

In light of the splitting
\mathcal{H}=\mathcal{T}+\mathcal{V}
, splitting methods
mclachlan2002splitting
compute a numerical approximation of the formal solution
\e^{{h}(\hat{\mathcal{T}}+\hat{\mathcal{V}})}
through a composition of the position updates (
5
) and momentum updates (
6
).
An
s
-stage splitting method is defined by arbitrary coefficients
a_{1},b_{1},\ldots,a_{s},b_{s}\in\mathbb{R}
and can be expressed as

\Phi_{h}=\e^{a_{1}{h}\hat{\mathcal{T}}}\e^{b_{1}{h}\hat{\mathcal{V}}}\cdots\e^{a_{s-1}{h}\hat{\mathcal{T}}}\e^{b_{s-1}{h}\hat{\mathcal{V}}}\e^{a_{s}{h}\hat{\mathcal{T}}}\e^{b_{s}{h}\hat{\mathcal{V}}}.

(7)

As a composition of symplectic maps, the resulting integrator is symplectic and thus volume-preserving.
To obtain a time-reversible integrator, it is necessary to consider self-adjoint splitting methods, i.e., splitting methods (
7
) whose coefficients satisfy for all
\ell=1,\ldots,s
either

\displaystyle a_{1}

\displaystyle=0,\ a_{\ell+1}=a_{s-\ell+1},\ \text{and }b_{\ell}=s_{s-\ell+1},

(8a)

resulting in a
velocity version
, or

\displaystyle b_{s}

\displaystyle=0,\ b_{\ell}=b_{s-\ell},\ \text{and }a_{\ell}=a_{s-\ell+1},

(8b)

resulting in a
position version
.
If the integrator coefficients satisfy

\sum_{\ell=1}^{d}a_{\ell}=\sum_{\ell=1}^{d}b_{\ell}=1,

(9)

the splitting method is consistent and, due to the inherent symmetry, of convergence order
n\geq 2
.
The order conditions for splitting methods can be determined by applying the Baker–Campbell–Hausdorff (BCH) formula
mclachlan2002splitting
.
A splitting method (
7
) is of convergence order
n
if

\Phi_{h}=\e^{{h}(\hat{\mathcal{T}}+\hat{\mathcal{V}})}+\mathcal{O}({h}^{n+1}).

For further details on the order theory and the construction of higher-order splitting methods, we refer to
mclachlan2002splitting
,
Blanes_Casas_Murua_2024
and the references therein.

2.3
Force-gradient integrators

For Hamiltonian systems of the form (
2
), splitting methods (
7
) can be enhanced by incorporating the commutator
\hat{\mathcal{C}}\coloneqq[\hat{\mathcal{V}},[\hat{\mathcal{T}},\hat{\mathcal{V}}]]
into the computational process.
Given the vector fields (
3
), the commutator solely depends on
\mathbf{q}
and reads

\hat{\mathcal{C}}=2\sum_{i,j,k=1}^{d}\mathcal{V}_{q_{k}}(\mathbf{q})m_{j,k}\mathcal{V}_{q_{i}q_{j}}(\mathbf{q})\frac{\partial}{\partial p_{i}}.

(10)

Since the term involves the gradient of the force, it is frequently referred to as the
force-gradient term
.
The incorporation of force-gradient evaluations into the momentum updates (
6
) results in force-gradient steps

\displaystyle\begin{split}&\e^{b_{\ell}{h}\hat{\mathcal{V}}+c_{\ell}{h}^{3}\hat{\mathcal{C}}}\begin{pmatrix}\mathbf{q}_{0}\\
\mathbf{p}_{0}\end{pmatrix}\\
&\qquad\coloneqq\begin{pmatrix}\mathbf{q}_{0}\\
\mathbf{p}_{0}-b_{\ell}{h}\mathcal{V}_{\mathbf{q}}(\mathbf{q}_{0})+2c_{\ell}{h}^{3}\mathcal{V}_{\mathbf{q}\mathbf{q}}(\mathbf{q}_{0})\mathbf{M}\mathcal{V}_{\mathbf{q}}(\mathbf{q}_{0})\end{pmatrix}.\end{split}

(11)

A force-gradient integrator
kennedy2009force
,
omelyan2003symplectic
with
s
stages reads

\Phi_{h}=\e^{a_{1}{h}\hat{\mathcal{T}}}\e^{b_{1}{h}\hat{\mathcal{V}}+c_{1}{h}^{3}\hat{\mathcal{C}}}\cdots\e^{a_{s}{h}\hat{\mathcal{T}}}\e^{b_{s}{h}\hat{\mathcal{V}}+c_{s}{h}^{3}\hat{\mathcal{C}}}.

(12)

A force-gradient step (
11
) involving the force-gradient term
\hat{\mathcal{C}}
may be regarded as a momentum update (
6
), applied to a Hamiltonian with modified potential

\tilde{\mathcal{V}}(\mathbf{q})=\mathcal{V}(\mathbf{q})-\frac{c_{\ell}{h}^{2}}{b_{\ell}}\mathcal{C}(\mathbf{q}),

where
\mathcal{C}(\mathbf{q})
is solved by

\frac{\partial}{\partial q_{i}}\mathcal{C}(\mathbf{q})=2\sum_{j,k=1}^{d}\mathcal{V}_{q_{k}}(\mathbf{q})m_{j,k}\mathcal{V}_{q_{i}q_{j}}(\mathbf{q})
.
Moreover, the modified potential is again Hamiltonian, i.e., the exact flow is again a symplectic map. Thus, force-gradient integrators are symplectic and, as a consequence, volume-preserving.
Self-adjoint force-gradient integrators, that is, those integrators satisfying the symmetry relations (
8
) and where the coefficients
c_{\ell}
moreover satisfy the same symmetry relations as the coefficients
b_{\ell}
, are time-reversible.
For a comprehensive analysis of the order theory of force-gradient integrators, including a detailed classification of self-adjoint integrators with
s\leq 6
stages, we refer to
omelyan2003symplectic
.

2.4
Hessian-free force-gradient integrators

If computing the force-gradient update (
11
) is significantly more expensive than computing a momentum update (
6
), or if an expression for (
10
) is not available (either analytically or in a software implementation), then
Hessian-free force-gradient integrators

schäfers2024hessianfree
represent a promising alternative.
In this approach, the force-gradient step (
11
) is approximated via

\displaystyle\begin{split}\e^{b_{\ell}{h}\hat{\mathcal{D}}({h},b_{\ell},c_{\ell})}\!\begin{pmatrix}\mathbf{q}_{0}\\
\mathbf{p}_{0}\end{pmatrix}&\coloneqq\begin{pmatrix}\mathbf{q}_{0}\\
\mathbf{p}_{0}-b_{\ell}{h}\mathcal{V}_{\mathbf{q}}\left(\mathbf{q}_{0}-\frac{2c_{\ell}{h}^{2}}{b_{\ell}}\mathbf{M}\mathcal{V}_{\mathbf{q}}(\mathbf{q}_{0})\right)\end{pmatrix}\\
&=\e^{b_{\ell}{h}\hat{\mathcal{V}}+c_{\ell}{h}^{3}\hat{\mathcal{C}}}\begin{pmatrix}\mathbf{q}_{0}\\
\mathbf{p}_{0}\end{pmatrix}+\mathcal{O}({h}^{5}),\end{split}

(13)

effectively replacing the Hessian of the potential by an additional force evaluation.
Consequently, an
s
-stage Hessian-free force-gradient integrator is given by

\Phi_{h}=\e^{a_{1}{h}\hat{\mathcal{T}}}\e^{b_{1}{h}\hat{\mathcal{D}}({h},b_{1},c_{1})}\cdots\e^{a_{s}{h}\hat{\mathcal{T}}}\e^{b_{s}{h}\hat{\mathcal{D}}({h},b_{s},c_{s})}.

(14)

The approximation of the force-gradient step does not affect the time-reversibility of the method, provided that the composition is self-adjoint.
As a composition of shears, Hessian-free force-gradient integrators are also volume-preserving. The approximated force-gradient step (
13
), however, is no longer a symplectic map, except in the special cases where either
c_{\ell}=0
or the Hessian of the potential commutes with itself when evaluated at (in general, different)
\mathbf{q}
.
A consequence of symplecticity is the preservation of a nearby shadow Hamiltonian
kennedy2013shadow
. In contrast to force-gradient integrators, the Hessian-free variants do not preserve a shadow Hamiltonian. In general, without any additional assumptions on the potential, the numerical energy will thus exhibit a drift of size
\mathcal{O}(\tau{h}^{\max\{4,n\}})
. The lack of long-time energy conservation of the Hessian-free variants may prove disadvantageous, particularly for exponentially long-time simulations. In lattice QCD, however, trajectory lengths of
\tau\approx 2
are typically considered. Therefore, the theoretical energy drift has no impact on the acceptance probability of the HMC algorithm.
A Taylor series expansion of the approximation (
13
) reveals that the additional error terms involve derivatives of the potential
\mathcal{V}
of order three and higher. It follows that for quadratic Hamiltonian functions, i.e., Hamiltonian functions (
2
) with potential energy
\mathcal{V}(\mathbf{q})=\frac{1}{2}\mathbf{q}^{\top}\mathbf{N}\mathbf{q}
, the approximated force-gradient update (
13
) is identical to the force-gradient step (
11
).
As a consequence, the two frameworks are equivalent when applied to linear ODE systems like the harmonic oscillator, i.e., their linear stability is identical.

.

To identify splitting methods, force-gradient integrators, and Hessian-free force-gradient integrators, we will use the following abbreviations.

denotes a position update (
5
),
B
a momentum update (

) with
c_{\ell}=0
,
C
a conventional force-gradient step (
11
) with
c_{\ell}\neq 0
, and
D
an approximated force-gradient step (
13
) with
c_{\ell}\neq 0
.

Without loss of generality, we will carry out a linear stability analysis for force-gradient integrators (

).

3
Linear stability of force-gradient integrators

As the exact flows of the subsystems (
4
) are not bounded, the decomposition algorithms presented in Section
2
are only conditionally stable.
To perform a numerical stability analysis, one selects a simple test problem with bounded solutions whose flow lies in the same subgroup of the group of diffeomorphisms.
In case of Hamiltonian systems, this is typically the harmonic oscillator

\ddot{y}+\omega^{2}y=0,\quad\omega>0.

(15a)

For the linear stability analysis, we consider the splitting
(q,p)=(\omega y,\dot{y})
, which gives rise to the first-order system

\begin{pmatrix}\dot{q}\\
\dot{p}\end{pmatrix}=\Bigg[\begin{pmatrix}0&\omega\\
0&0\end{pmatrix}+\begin{pmatrix}0&0\\
-\omega&0\end{pmatrix}\Bigg]\begin{pmatrix}q\\
p\end{pmatrix}=\begin{pmatrix}\omega p\\
0\end{pmatrix}+\begin{pmatrix}0\\
-\omega q\end{pmatrix}.

(15b)

The force-gradient term (
10
) for the harmonic oscillator (
15b
) reads

\hat{\mathcal{C}}=2\omega^{3}q\frac{\partial}{\partial p}
.
A decomposition algorithm will typically become unstable for
\lvert z\rvert>z_{*}
, where
z\coloneqq\omega{h}
and the parameter
z_{*}
represents the
stability threshold
of the integrator.
The objective is to determine the parameter
z_{*}
for a given force-gradient integrator by adapting the theory for splitting methods as presented in
BlanesStability
.
Starting from the initial values
(q(0),p(0))=(q_{0},p_{0})
, the exact solution to the harmonic oscillator (
15b
) at time point
{h}>0
is given by

\begin{pmatrix}q({h})\\
p({h})\end{pmatrix}=\underbrace{\begin{pmatrix}\cos(z)&\sin(z)\\
-\sin(z)&\cos(z)\end{pmatrix}}_{=\mathbf{O}(z)}\begin{pmatrix}q_{0}\\
p_{0}\end{pmatrix},\quad z\coloneqq\omega{h}.

Applying a force-gradient integrator (
12
) to the harmonic oscillator (
15b
) yields an approximation

\begin{pmatrix}q_{1}\\
p_{1}\end{pmatrix}=\mathbf{K}(z)\begin{pmatrix}q_{0}\\
p_{0}\end{pmatrix}

with
stability matrix

\displaystyle\begin{split}\mathbf{K}(z)&=\prod_{\ell=1}^{s}\begin{pmatrix}1&0\\
-b_{s+1-\ell}z+2c_{s+1-\ell}z^{3}&1\end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix}1&a_{s+1-\ell}z\\
0&1\end{pmatrix},\end{split}

(16)

which is an approximation to
\mathbf{O}(z)
.
Additionally, we define the
stability polynomial

\mathfrak{p}(z)\coloneqq\frac{1}{2}\trace(\mathbf{K}(z)).

(17)

In case of self-adjoint force-gradient integrators, the stability matrix (
16
) takes the form

\mathbf{K}(z)=\begin{pmatrix}\mathfrak{p}(z)&K_{1,2}(z)\\
K_{2,1}(z)&\mathfrak{p}(z)\end{pmatrix},

(18a)

with even stability polynomial

\mathfrak{p}(z)=1+\sum\limits_{i=1}^{2(s-1)}\bar{p}_{i}z^{2i},

(18b)

and odd polynomials

K_{1,2}(z)=\sum_{i=1}^{2s-1}k_{i}z^{2i-1},\quad K_{2,1}(z)=\sum_{i=1}^{2s}\bar{k}_{i}z^{2i-1},

(18c)

where
\bar{p}_{i},k_{i},\bar{k}_{i}
are homogeneous polynomials in the coefficients
a_{\ell},b_{\ell},c_{\ell}
.
In particular, it holds

k_{2s-2}=k_{2s-1}=0

for a velocity version (
8a
), and

\bar{k}_{2s-1}=\bar{k}_{2s}=0

for a position version (
8b
) of the force-gradient integrator (
12
).
Furthermore, for consistent schemes satisfying (
9
), it holds

\bar{p}_{1}=-\tfrac{1}{2},\quad k_{1}=1,\quad\bar{k}_{1}=-1,

resulting in
\mathfrak{p}(z)=1-z^{2}/2+\mathcal{O}(z^{4})
.

Example 1
.

Consider the force-gradient integrator BACAB

\Phi_{h}=\e^{\tfrac{{h}}{6}\hat{\mathcal{V}}}\e^{\tfrac{{h}}{2}\hat{\mathcal{T}}}\e^{\tfrac{2{h}}{3}\hat{\mathcal{V}}+\tfrac{{h}^{3}}{72}\hat{\mathcal{C}}}\e^{\tfrac{{h}}{2}\hat{\mathcal{T}}}\e^{\tfrac{{h}}{6}\hat{\mathcal{V}}}.

(19a)

and its Hessian-free variant BADAB

\Phi_{h}=\e^{\tfrac{{h}}{6}\hat{\mathcal{V}}}\e^{\tfrac{{h}}{2}\hat{\mathcal{T}}}\e^{\tfrac{2{h}}{3}\hat{\mathcal{D}}\left({h},\tfrac{2}{3},\tfrac{1}{72}\right)}\e^{\tfrac{{h}}{2}\hat{\mathcal{T}}}\e^{\tfrac{{h}}{6}\hat{\mathcal{V}}}.

The stability matrix of these two integrators is given by

\mathbf{K}(z)=\begin{pmatrix}1-\tfrac{z^{2}}{2}+\tfrac{z^{4}}{24}-\tfrac{z^{6}}{864}&z-\tfrac{z^{3}}{6}+\tfrac{z^{5}}{144}\\
-z+\tfrac{z^{3}}{6}-\tfrac{z^{5}}{108}+\tfrac{z^{7}}{5184}&1-\tfrac{z^{2}}{2}+\tfrac{z^{4}}{24}-\tfrac{z^{6}}{864}\end{pmatrix},

with stability polynomial
\mathfrak{p}(z)=1-\tfrac{z^{2}}{2}+\tfrac{z^{4}}{24}-\tfrac{z^{6}}{864}
.

The stability analysis presented in
BlanesStability
can be applied to force-gradient integrators in a straightforward manner.
We provide a brief summary of the fundamental results, tailored to the framework of force-gradient integrators.
A force-gradient integrator (
12
) is considered as stable when applied to the harmonic oscillator (
15b
) if
\mathbf{K}(z)^{i}
is bounded independently of the number of time steps
i\geq 1
.

Definition 1

(Stability interval)
.

We denote by
z_{*}
the largest non-negative real number such that
\mathbf{K}(z)
is stable for all
z\in(-z_{*},z_{*})
. We refer to
z_{*}
as the stability threshold and call
(-z_{*},z_{*})
the stability interval of
\mathbf{K}(z)
.

We moreover introduce the following characterization of stability.

Proposition 1
.

Let
\mathbf{K}(z)
denote the stability matrix of a self-adjoint force-gradient integrator (
12
) and
\mathfrak{p}(z)
its stability polynomial. Then, the following statements are equivalent:

(i)

The matrix
\mathbf{K}(z)
is stable.

(ii)

The matrix
\mathbf{K}(z)
is diagonalizable with eigenvalues of modulus one.

(iii)

It holds
\lvert\mathfrak{p}(z)\rvert\leq 1
, and
\mathbf{K}(z)
is similar to the matrix

\mathbf{S}(z)=\begin{pmatrix}\cos{\Psi(z)}&\sin{\Psi(z)}\\
-\sin{\Psi(z)}&\cos{\Psi(z)}\end{pmatrix},\,\text{where }\Psi(z)=\arccos{\mathfrak{p}(z)}.

Proof.

See
[
BlanesStability
, Proposition 2.4]
. ∎

Before determining the stability threshold
z_{*}
for a given force-gradient integrator (
12
), we define an upper bound for
z_{*}
.

Definition 2
.

Let
\mathfrak{p}(z)
be the stability polynomial of a consistent force-gradient integrator (
12
). We define

as the largest real non-negative number such that
\lvert\mathfrak{p}(z)\rvert\leq 1\;\forall z\in[0,z^{*}]
.

Remark 2
.

It is clear from the proof of Proposition 1 that
[-z^{*},z^{*}]
is the largest interval including
0
such that
\mathbf{K}(z)
has eigenvalues of modulus one and therefore
z_{*}\leq z^{*}
.

For a consistent self-adjoint force-gradient integrator (
12
), it holds
\mathfrak{p}(z)=1-\frac{z^{2}}{2}+\mathcal{O}(z^{4})
and thus
\mathfrak{p}^{\prime\prime}(0)<0
. Then,
\mathfrak{p}(z)^{2}-1\leq 0
for sufficiently small

, and in that case

is the smallest real positive zero with odd multiplicity of the polynomial
\mathfrak{p}(z)^{2}-1
.
Therefore, potential instabilities may occur within the interval
[0,z^{*}]
at the real zeros with even multiplicity of the polynomial
\mathfrak{p}(z)^{2}-1
.
The following proposition will determine the stability threshold

.

Proposition 2
.

Let
\mathbf{K}(z)
denote the stability matrix of a self-adjoint force-gradient integrator (
12
) and
\mathfrak{p}(z)

4
Detection of efficient decomposition algorithms

The integrator coefficients of decomposition algorithms can be selected to achieve the maximum attainable convergence order.
For numerous versions, the order conditions establish an under-determined system, leaving certain integrator coefficients as degrees of freedom.
Although selecting the degrees of freedom does not alter the convergence order
n
, it does influence the accuracy and the stability threshold
z_{*}
of the integrator.
A commonly employed procedure
mclachlan1995
,
omelyan2003symplectic
is to identify the integrator coefficients that minimize a given norm of the leading error coefficients.

4.1
Minimum-error methods

Given a decomposition algorithm of convergence order
n
, the integrator possesses a local truncation error of order
\mathcal{O}(h^{n+1})
, which is a linear combination of elementary differentials whose coefficients are determined by the violations in the order conditions for convergence order
n+1
.
For instance, applying a self-adjoint decomposition algorithm
\Phi_{{h}}
of convergence order
n=2
to the Hamiltonian system (
2
) yields a local truncation error of the form

\displaystyle\begin{split}\Phi_{{h}}(\mathbf{q}_{0},\mathbf{p}_{0})&-(\mathbf{q}({h}),\mathbf{p}({h}))\\
=\ &{h}^{3}\left(\alpha[\hat{\mathcal{T}},[\hat{\mathcal{T}},\hat{\mathcal{V}}]]+\beta[\hat{\mathcal{V}},[\hat{\mathcal{T}},\hat{\mathcal{V}}]]\right)+\mathcal{O}({h}^{5}),\end{split}

(21)

with leading error coefficients
\alpha,\beta\in\mathbb{R}
,
\alpha\cdot\beta\neq 0
.
Minimizing the principal error term will result in a more accurate numerical integration scheme. Since the elementary differentials are problem-specific, a common approach is to equate all elementary differentials to one and then minimize some norm of the leading error coefficients. Typically, the Euclidean norm is chosen.
For our example (
21
), this implies that we seek to minimize the expression

\sqrt{\alpha^{2}+\beta^{2}}.

(22)

For higher orders, this concept can be extended in a straightforward manner; see for example
omelyan2003symplectic
,
schäfers2024hessianfree
.
Additionally, the computational cost must be taken into account to determine which integrator is more efficient, i.e., which integrator achieves the desired accuracy at the least computational cost.
In lattice QCD, the computational cost is governed by the evaluations of the forces and the force-gradient terms.
Consequently, a common way to compare the efficiency of two decomposition algorithms of the same order
n
is given by the efficiency measure
omelyan2003symplectic
,
schäfers2024hessianfree

\mathrm{Eff}_{\mathtt{err}}^{(n)}=\frac{1}{(n_{f}+\xi n_{g})^{n}\cdot\mathrm{Err}_{n+1}},

(23)

where
n_{f}
and
n_{g}
denote the number of force evaluations and force-gradient evaluations per time step, respectively. Furthermore,
\xi>0
is a constant that quantifies the relative cost difference between force-gradient and force evaluations.
\mathrm{Err}_{n+1}
denotes a (possibly weighted) norm of the leading error coefficients, as exemplified in (
22
).
This approach has been employed to derive self-adjoint minimum-error methods with
s\leq 6
stages.
On the one hand, in
omelyan2003symplectic
, the derivation of minimum-error force-gradient integrators was conducted by selecting
\xi=2
and employing the non-weighted Euclidean norm for the principal error term.
On the other hand, in
schäfers2024hessianfree
, we conducted similar investigations for Hessian-free force-gradient integrators by setting
\xi=1
(as the force-gradient evaluation is replaced by another force evaluation) and a modified Euclidean norm that incorporates additional error terms with appropriate weights.
Both investigations include traditional splitting methods as a special case.

4.2
The relative stability threshold

The investigation of the linear stability of (Hessian-free) force-gradient integrators presents an alternative approach to identifying promising decomposition algorithms.
By investigating the stability threshold, we can determine which integrator enables stable integration at the lowest computational cost.
Consider two integrators,
\Phi_{{h}}^{\{1\}}
and
\Phi_{{h}}^{\{2\}}
, with corresponding stability thresholds,
z_{*}^{\{1\}}
and
z_{*}^{\{2\}}
, and computational costs per time step,
n_{f}^{\{1\}}+\xi n_{g}^{\{1\}}
, and
n_{f}^{\{2\}}+\xi n_{g}^{\{2\}}
, respectively.
For a given
\omega>0
, the maximum permissible step size ensuring the stability of the first integrator is
{h}_{\mathrm{max}}^{\{1\}}=z_{*}^{\{1\}}/\omega
.
To achieve the same computational cost as
\Phi^{\{1\}}_{{h}_{\mathrm{max}}^{\{1\}}}
, the second integrator must be employed with the step size

\tilde{{h}}=\frac{z_{*}^{\{1\}}}{\omega}\cdot\frac{n_{f}^{\{2\}}+\xi n_{g}^{\{2\}}}{n_{f}^{\{1\}}+\xi n_{g}^{\{1\}}}.

If
{h}_{\mathrm{max}}^{\{2\}}=z_{*}^{\{2\}}/\omega<\tilde{{h}}
,
then
\Phi_{\tilde{{h}}}^{\{2\}}
is unstable, implying that
\Phi^{\{1\}}_{{h}_{\mathrm{max}}^{\{1\}}}
enables a stable integration at a lower computational cost.
Consequently,
\Phi^{\{1\}}_{{h}}
is computationally more efficient than
\Phi^{\{2\}}_{{h}}
, provided that

\frac{z_{*}^{\{2\}}}{\omega}<\frac{z_{*}^{\{1\}}}{\omega}\cdot\frac{n_{f}^{\{2\}}+\xi n_{g}^{\{2\}}}{n_{f}^{\{1\}}+\xi n_{g}^{\{1\}}}\quad\Leftrightarrow\quad\frac{z_{*}^{\{2\}}}{n_{f}^{\{2\}}+\xi n_{g}^{\{2\}}}<\frac{z_{*}^{\{1\}}}{n_{f}^{\{1\}}+\xi n_{g}^{\{1\}}},

i.e., we seek to maximize the efficiency measure

\mathrm{Eff}_{\mathtt{stab}}=z_{*}/(n_{f}+\xi n_{g}),

(24)

which we refer to as the
relative stability threshold
.
We conducted a comprehensive analysis of all variants of self-adjoint (Hessian-free) force-gradient integrators with

stages.

4.3.4
Remarks

Although investigations in
omelyan2003symplectic
and
schäfers2024hessianfree
did not explicitly address numerical stability, it is evident that maximizing the efficiency measure
\mathrm{Eff}_{\mathtt{err}}^{(n)}
, resulting in minimum-error variants, yields promising integrator variants that provide the optimal trade-off between accuracy and numerical stability.
By incorporating the proposed relative stability threshold
\mathrm{Eff}_{\mathtt{stab}}
into these investigations, it is possible to identify efficient integrator variants that excel in both accuracy and numerical stability.

In particular, the findings highlight that the force-gradient approach is not recommended for second-order integrators. However, for convergence order
n=4
, these integrators demonstrate remarkable efficiency. For convergence order
n=6
, the family of (Hessian-free) force-gradient integrators with
s\leq 6
stages fails to yield any variant that surpasses the splitting method BABABABABABABAB (
32
). The family of (Hessian-free) force-gradient integrators with
s\geq 7
stages may potentially yield promising variants, which require further investigation.

For completeness, the definitions of all non-dominated variants that have not yet been introduced are given in
B
. Moreover,
B
provides a summary of the results for all minimum-error variants of (Hessian-free) force-gradient integrators with
s\leq 6
stages, leading to extended versions of the tables
[
omelyan2003symplectic
, Table 2]
and
[
schäfers2024hessianfree
, Table 1]
.

We will conduct numerical tests demonstrating that incorporating the relative stability threshold

Particularly, in Section

5.1
The two-dimensional Schwinger model

The linear stability analysis for force-gradient integrators and their Hessian-free variants coincides, as both frameworks are equivalent when applied to linear ODE systems, such as the harmonic oscillator (

The linear stability analysis considers the harmonic oscillator as a test equation to determine the stability threshold

6
Conclusion and outlook

In this work, we have performed a linear stability analysis for force-gradient integrators and their Hessian-free variants.
When applied to linear ODE systems, conventional force-gradient integrators and Hessian-free force-gradient integrators are equivalent, i.e., their linear stability analysis coincides.
For the family of self-adjoint integrators with
s\leq 6
stages, we performed detailed stability investigations, augmented by the accuracy investigations performed in
omelyan2003symplectic
,
schäfers2024hessianfree
.
The investigations show that the efficiency measure
\mathrm{Eff}_{\mathtt{err}}^{(n)}
is way more sensitive to alterations in the integrator coefficients than the relative stability threshold
\mathrm{Eff}_{\mathtt{stab}}
. As a consequence, the family of minimum-error methods derived in
omelyan2003symplectic
,
schäfers2024hessianfree
contains promising variants.
Incorporating the relative stability threshold
\mathrm{Eff}_{\mathtt{stab}}
allows to detect minimum-error variants that show desirable stability properties. Particularly, for convergence order
n=4
, conventional force-gradient integrators and Hessian-free force-gradient integrators turn out to be promising choices.
Numerical tests demonstrate that Hessian-free force-gradient integrators offer a more efficient computational process compared to conventional splitting methods.
Although the efficiency measure
\mathrm{Eff}_{\mathtt{err}}^{(n)}
provided insights into the accuracy of the integrators, it was insufficient to explain the superior efficiency of certain variants.
The relative stability threshold
\mathrm{Eff}_{\mathtt{stab}}
reveals that the efficiency of the integrators is strongly influenced by their numerical stability.
Furthermore, the simulations provide numerical evidence indicating no significant difference in the numerical stability of force-gradient integrators and their Hessian-free counterparts.
Overall, the stability threshold derived from investigating the harmonic oscillator emerges as a reliable criterion for lattice QCD simulations.
Already for large fermion masses, stability-enhanced variants turn out to be more efficient than more accurate but less stable variants.
In our experiments, a Hessian-free force-gradient integrator,
ABADABA
(
25b
), achieves the same acceptance probability as the most efficient conventional splitting method, BABABABABAB (
42
), while requiring less than
90\%
of the computational cost.
When increasing the lattice volume, the accuracy is expected to become more important as the numerical stability does not depend on the volume. On the other hand, for smaller fermion masses, the numerical stability becomes more important so that preference should be given to integrators with a higher value for
\mathrm{Eff}_{\mathtt{stab}}
. Depending on the particular ensemble, it has to be checked which variant provides the best possible trade-off between accuracy and numerical stability for this particular problem.

In general, the efficiency of the integrators can be further increased by the use of multiple time stepping techniques
SEXTON1992665
,
URBACH200687
.
For this purpose, we aim to provide a general framework for these
nested integrators
, including an order theory, a refined error analysis, a linear stability analysis, as well as generalizations to the efficiency measures
\mathrm{Eff}_{\mathtt{err}}^{(n)}
and
\mathrm{Eff}_{\mathtt{stab}}
.
Moreover, a next step is to enhance the efficiency measure
\mathrm{Eff}_{\mathtt{err}}^{(n)}
. Taking the standard Euclidean norm for the efficiency measure
\mathrm{Eff}_{\mathtt{err}}^{(n)}
assumes that all error terms contribute equally to the overall error. In practice, this is not necessarily the case. Deriving problem-dependent weights to obtain a weighted norm and to minimize this norm instead will provide a more appropriate measure for the computational efficiency when applied to a specific ensemble.
