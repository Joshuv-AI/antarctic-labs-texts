Identification of new QTLs for quality traits and yield using tetraploid wheat interspecific backcross inbred lines
Authors: Ilaria Marcotuli, Josè Miguel Soriano, Pasqualina Colasuonno, Sara Muciaccia, Agata Gadaleta
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundThe wild tetraploid wheat Triticum turgidum var. dicoccoides is a valuable source of genetic variation for improving disease resistance, drought tolerance, and grain protein quantity and quality in cultivated wheat. However, little is known about the genetic basis of DNA introgression from wild relatives into cultivated backgrounds. Because wild germplasm contains both favorable alleles and undesirable agronomic traits, understanding segregation and recombination patterns is essential for its effective use in breeding. This study aimed to evaluate a population of backcross inbred lines (BILs) derived from T. turgidum var. dicoccoides to identify quantitative trait loci (QTL) associated with grain quality and yield-related traits.ResultsThe BIL population was evaluated in replicated field trials for grain protein content, fiber content, and yield components. Genomic analysis characterized the distribution of introgressed segments from T. turgidum var. dicoccoides within the durum wheat genome. QTL mapping identified 24 QTLs associated with variation in grain protein (12 QTL), fiber content (5 QTL), and yield traits (7 QTL). QTLs were detected on nine chromosomes (1B, 2B, 3 A, 4 A, 4B, 5 A, 5B, 6B, and 7B), with several loci consistently expressed across environments and years, highlighting stable genomic regions controlling key traits.ConclusionsThe study demonstrates that BIL populations are effective tools for a rapid introgression of beneficial alleles from wild wheat relatives, as it is the case for the fiber content and its implication for nutritional quality. The detected QTL provide a foundation for developing near-isogenic lines (NILs) to investigate individual locus effects and facilitate the incorporation of valuable wild alleles into future wheat breeding programs.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12864-025-12323-0.Keywords: Triticum turgidum var. dicoccoides, Introgression, Quantitative trait loci, Grain protein content, Backcross inbred lines, QTL mapping

Background

Wheat is one of the most important staple crops worldwide, providing a significant portion of daily caloric and protein intake for millions of people. As the global demand for both food quantity and nutritional quality continues to grow, wheat breeding programs face the dual challenge of improving grain yield while enhancing key quality traits such as protein and fiber content. To achieve this, a deeper understanding of the genetic basis of complex traits and the incorporation of novel alleles from diverse genetic resources, including wild relatives, are essential.

Chromosome Segment Substitution Lines (CSSLs) and Backcross Inbred Lines (BILs) represent two important types of mapping populations used for QTL detection and trait dissection, yet they differ in design and application. CSSLs are developed through repeated backcrossing combined with marker-assisted selection, resulting in lines that each contain one or a few defined donor chromosome segments within an otherwise uniform recurrent parent background [1]. Collectively, a CSSL population can represent the entire donor genome in discrete, non-overlapping fragments, which greatly reduces genetic background noise and facilitates the precise detection of even minor-effect QTLs. In contrast, BILs are typically produced by backcrossing followed by selfing without stringent marker-assisted selection [2]. As a result, each BIL often harbors multiple donor segments that are randomly distributed across the genome, providing broader but less controlled genome coverage. While both populations are useful for mapping and introgression studies, CSSLs are particularly valuable for fine mapping and functional characterization of individual loci, whereas BILs are more suitable for initial QTL discovery and trait evaluation across multiple genomic regions.

The identification of desirable donors for both qualitative and quantitative traits, along with yield components, is a fundamental prerequisite for mapping complex traits in crop breeding programs. In wheat, quantitative trait loci (QTL) mapping has traditionally relied on biparental mapping populations [3, 4]. Although the utility of backcross inbred lines (BILs) in breeding programs has been well established, there are relatively few reports on the application of BILs for QTL mapping in wheat or other crops [2].

BILs, particularly when developed as introgressed or near-isogenic lines, offer stability for quantitative trait analysis and serve as powerful tools for dissecting complex traits across multiple environments. Their design allows for replicated evaluation across diverse target environments, enabling independent and simultaneous selection of lines fixed for desirable donor alleles. Moreover, the development of BIL populations without selection enables comprehensive dissection of the donor genome, allowing for the identification of superior alleles even when present in lines with low proportions of the donor background. This makes BILs especially suited for fine mapping of specific genomic regions associated with valuable QTL [2].

Wild relatives of wheat, particularly species within the Triticeae tribe, have evolved under diverse environmental conditions and thus harbor a broad spectrum of genetic variability for a wide array of traits. These include morphological characteristics, tolerance to abiotic stresses such as drought and cold, and resistance to biotic stresses like fungal diseases and insect pests. In addition, wild accessions exhibit considerable variation in grain quality attributes, including protein composition and dietary fiber content, making them valuable resources for wheat improvement [5, 6].

The main constituents of wheat grains are carbohydrates, proteins, and fibers. Among these, grain protein content (GPC) in durum wheat (Triticum turgidum L. var. durum) is a trait of considerable nutritional and technological importance. GPC is a complex quantitative trait that is often negatively correlated with grain yield, posing a significant challenge in breeding programs where both high protein content and high yield are desired [7, 8].

Cereal grains are also an excellent source of dietary fiber (DF), a critical component of human nutrition. Crude fiber, a subset of DF, plays a key role in determining grain quality. Dietary fiber encompasses a wide range of polysaccharide structures with diverse physical and chemical properties, influencing digestion and overall health. Fiber are generally classified into soluble and insoluble types based on water solubility, fermentability, and physicochemical behavior. Soluble fiber forms gel-like structures that slow gastric emptying and aid in nutrient absorption, while insoluble fiber contributes to bulk and supports gastrointestinal health. Major DF components include cell wall polysaccharides such as hemicellulose, cellulose, β-glucans, and lignin, which are further categorized as neutral detergent fiber (NDF), acid detergent fiber (ADF), and acid detergent lignin (ADL) [9].

Advancements in genotyping technologies, such as the development of the high-density wheat 90 K SNP iSelect array [10], have enhanced the resolution and efficiency of QTL mapping [2]. This array, encompassing approximately 90,000 gene-associated SNPs, provides broad genome coverage and facilitates the discovery of genetic loci linked to important agronomic and quality traits, including GPC and fiber content [8]. Building upon this resource, the 25 K wheat SNP array was developed as a reduced platform specifically designed for durum wheat, with markers optimized for the A and B genomes. This array enables cost-effective genome-wide marker analysis, supporting advances in elucidating the genetic architecture of complex traits, detecting selective sweeps associated with domestication and adaptation, and identifying novel alleles for breeding [11].In this study, a BIL population was developed by crossing the elite durum wheat cultivar Latino with Triticum turgidum var. dicoccoides accession MG29896, known for its superior agronomic traits. The primary aim of this study was to introgress alleles associated with high GPC from dicoccoides into durum wheat using the BIL approach and to identify molecular markers linked to these favorable alleles without concomitant reductions in yield. The findings of this study may contribute to the development of improved durum wheat cultivars combining superior nutritional quality and agronomic performance.

Methods

Plant materials and field trials

A population of 186 BILs was developed using a procedure similar to that of Wehrhahn and Allard [12] in order to spread the donor parent genome in a cultivated genome background. The high-yielding cultivar Latino of durum wheat was used as a recurrent parent, and the accession MG29896 of T. turgidum L. var. dicoccoides was used as donor parent because of its high GPC. From three to five successive backcrosses were made to Latino followed by four generations of self-pollination to introgress differ size fragments. No selection was applied in any generation, and plants were chosen randomly for additional backcrossing or selfing. However, some plants were lost in the subsequent generations and a total of 172 BC3F8- BC5F8 lines were obtained and multiplied to have sufficient seeds for use in replicated trials. The lines with 3 BCs (78 lines) were defined as 3BIL and those derived from 5 BCs (94 lines) as 5BIL. The resulting BILs were evaluated across three field trials to assess genetic variability and introgression of beneficial alleles from the wild parent. Introgression is defined as the stable incorporation of donor-derived chromosomal segments into the cultivated wheat genome through repeated backcrossing and selection. Such introgressed segments, delimited by molecular markers, represent the genomic regions transferred from the wild relative and provide the basis for detecting associations with phenotypic variation in target traits. The complete series of BILs and the recurrent parent Latino were evaluated for grain protein content, fiber content and grain yield components in Valenzano (Bari) in southern Italy over 3 years (2021, 2022, 2023) by using a randomized complete block design with three replications. In all the experiments, each progeny was planted with 9 g of seeds in three rows 1 m long with 30 cm between rows. During the growing season, standard cultivation practices were used. Plots were hand harvested at maturity and yield was determined as the total weight of grains produced per unit area, expressed tons per hectare (t/ha). GPC, expressed as a percentage of protein on a dry weight basis, was directly obtained on a 3 g sample of whole-meal flour using near-infrared reflectance spectroscopy.

To determine the fiber fractions, the Van Soest method [13] was employed, which sequentially isolates the neutral detergent fiber (NDF), acid detergent fiber (ADF), and acid detergent lignin (ADL) components. All reagents used were of analytical grade. The neutral detergent solution (NDS), consisting of sodium lauryl sulfate, EDTA, sodium borate, disodium phosphate, and 2-ethoxyethanol, was prepared to extract cell contents and isolate the total insoluble fiber fraction. For ADF analysis, an acid detergent solution (ADS) containing cetyltrimethylammonium bromide (CTAB) in 1 N sulfuric acid was used to remove hemicellulose, isolating cellulose and lignin. The lignin content was determined by treating the ADF residue with 72% sulfuric acid, which solubilizes cellulose, leaving lignin as the indigestible component.

Approximately 1 g of wheat flour was weighed into fiber filter bags (ANKOM system) or into glass crucibles equipped with a filtration support. The samples were digested in preheated NDS at 100 °C for 60 min with continuous agitation. The residues were washed with hot distilled water and acetone, dried at 105 °C, and weighed to determine the NDF content. The same residues were then digested with ADS under similar conditions, washed, dried, and reweighed to quantify the ADF. For ADL determination, the ADF residues were incubated in 72% sulfuric acid for 3 h at room temperature with occasional stirring. After treatment, samples were filtered, washed, oven-dried, and combusted at 550 °C in a muffle furnace for 4 h. The remaining ash was weighed, and lignin content was calculated as the difference between the ADF and the ash weight.

All fiber analyses were conducted in triplicate, and results were corrected to a dry matter basis. Fiber fractions were expressed as percentages of the original sample weight: NDF as the weight after neutral detergent treatment, ADF as the residue after acid detergent digestion, and ADL as the ash-free portion of the ADF remaining after sulfuric acid digestion. Hemicellulose content was calculated as the difference between NDF and ADF, while cellulose was determined by subtracting ADL from ADF. Procedural blanks and certified reference materials were included to ensure analytical accuracy.

Statistical analyses were carried out using JMP Pro 18 (SAS Institute Inc., Cary, NC, USA). To assess the differences between years (Valenzano 2021 and Valenzano 2022) and locations (Valenzano 2022 and Gaudiano 2022) two different analyses of one-way ANOVAs were conducted. Least squares means were calculated and compared using the t-student test. Pearson’s correlation coefficients were calculated among the evaluated traits. The significance level for statistical analyses was considered at P < 0.05.

Molecular markers analysis

DNA was extracted from leaves by applying the CTAB method [14]. Genotyping was performed by Trait Genetics (Gatersleben, Germany) using the Illumina® iSelect 15 K wheat SNP array, an optimized and reduced version of the 90 K iSELECT SNP-chip described by Wang et al. [10], which contains 13,600 highly informative gene-associated SNP markers [15]. SNPs on un-linked chromosomes were filtered out. Additionally, the genetic and physical positions of all SNPs were assigned based on a consensus map [10] and Svevo durum wheat chromosomes [16].

SNP quality control was performed using GenAlEx software [17, 18]. SNPs with a minor allele frequency (MAF) of < 1% and a call rate of >10% were excluded from the downstream analysis. Finally, the dataset of 2.885 markers was pruned for linkage disequilibrium (r2 = 0.80) using the SNP and Variation Suite (SVS) software package v.8.4.0 (Golden Helix Inc, Bozeman, MT, USA).

Map construction was performed combining the 3BIL and 5BIL to have enough genotypes for an accurate mapping and was carried out using QTL IciMapping software [19] with the Kosambi mapping function. Monomorphic and redundant markers were eliminated from the analysis. Linkage groups were defined at LOD of 3 and ordering was performed using the K-optimality (2-OptMap2) optimization algorithm for ordering markers with the Shortest nearest-neighbor (NN.) as initial route for ordering. This method chooses the best initial marker order from multiple nearest-neighbor routes. The Rippling function was used to fine-tune of the ordered chromosomes. Linkage groups were named according to the wheat chromosome nomenclature.

QTL detection was performed using the QTL IciMapping software [20], employing the Composite Interval Mapping (CIM) procedure with a permutation test of 1000 replicates for LOD threshold estimation. A bioinformatics analysis using BLAST program was carried out to identify genes associated with the biosynthesis of grain protein content, fiber content and grain yield components, utilizing SNP sequences from the CerealDB website (https://www.cerealsdb.uk.net/cerealgenomics/CerealsDB/indexNEW.php) and the SVEVO genome sequence available on the GrainGenes database (https://wheat.pw.usda.gov/GG3/). Specifically, the sequences of the SNPs located within QTL regions were used as queries in BLAST searches against the databases, and gene sequences showing the highest levels of homology were selected for further investigation.

Results

Molecular characterization

Atotal of 896 SNPs was used for map construction. The 14 chromosomes of durum wheat were identified with an average of 63 markers per chromosome ranging from 163 markers in chromosomes 6B to 28 in chromosome 2B. By genomes the SNP markers were not equally distributed with 37% of the markers mapped on genome A and 63% in genome B. The map covered a total length of 1844 cM. Chromosome 5B was the longest (241.5 cM) whereas chromosome 1 A was the shortest (51.2 cM), with an average length of chromosome of 130.2 cM (Fig. 1, supplementary file 1).

Phenotypic variation for quality traits and yield

A total of 2 parental genotypes and 172 backcross inbred lines were evaluated for agronomic and qualitative traits, including yield (t/ha), protein content (%), crude fiber (%), and fiber fractions (NDF, ADF, and ADL).

Variation in yield was observed across genotypes, ranging from a low of 1.99 t/ha (MG29896) to a high of 6.80 t/ha (3BIL-83). Among the genotypes, 3BIL-83, 3BIL-72 (6.72 t/ha), 3BIL-81 (6.55 t/ha), 3BIL-90 (6.46 t/ha), and 3BIL-39 (6.45 t/ha) demonstrated the highest yields. In contrast, several 5BIL genotypes showed comparatively lower yields, with many clustering around 4.0–4.7 t/ha (Table 3). The genotypic variance for GY was 0.111, and the trait exhibited a high heritability (0.71), suggesting that yield improvement through selection is feasible Table 3).

When comparing the two years of evaluation (Valenzano 2021 vs. Valenzano 2022), highly significant differences were observed for grain yield. The F-value was very high (2469.68) and the corresponding p-value was < 0.0001, indicating a statistically significant difference in yield between the two years with most of the variation in yield due to the difference between years rather than within-year variability (Table 5). In this case the mean difference test reported also statistically significant differences between years, with 6.5 t/ha in 2001 and 3.1 t/ha in 2022.

Protein content varied substantially, ranging from 11.23% (LATINO) to 15.65% (MG29896). Among the BIL lines, notable high protein values were recorded in 3BIL-69 (15.63%), 3BIL-48 (15.10%), and 3BIL-5 (14.75%). Several genotypes exceeded 14%, indicating potential for protein improvement. The genotypic variance for GPC was 0.251, and the heritability estimate was high (0.77), indicating strong genetic control and good prospects for selection (Table 3). The ANOVA for protein content in the BIL population showed that the effect of location was not statistically significant. F-value was 2.01 with a p-value of 0.1575, which exceeds the conventional significance threshold of 0.05 (Table 4). These findings indicate that the protein content in the BIL population was not significantly affected by the environmental differences between the tested locations.

However, a significant effect of year was observed for protein content in the Valenzano trials conducted in 2021 and 2022 (Table 5). The F-ratio was 21.30 and statistically significant (p < 0.0001), indicating a highly significant difference in protein content between the two years, and suggesting that environmental conditions among years played a substantial role in influencing protein accumulation. Mean difference test results indicated a significant difference among years with a decrease in protein content from 12.7% in 2001 to 12.3% in 2022.

Crude fiber content ranged from 1.41% (MG29896) to 3.94% (3BIL-39). Genotypes with elevated crude fiber levels included 3BIL-9 (3.11%), 3BIL-21 (3.03%), and 5BIL-2 (2.97%). The genotypic variance was 0.110, and heritability was high (0.78), demonstrating that this trait is strongly influenced by genetic factors (Table 3).

Fiber Fraction NDF values ranged from as low as 24.47% (3BIL-92) to as high as 70.78% (3BIL-48), with 3BIL-22, 3BIL-23, and 3BIL-24 also exhibiting NDF above 66%. Genotypic variance for NDF was the highest among all traits (11.73), though heritability was 0.65. This indicates that while genetic variability is substantial, environmental factors also play a significant role in the expression of NDF (Table 3). ADF content showed similar variability, with 3BIL-15 recording the highest value at 10.50%, while 3BIL-2 had the lowest (1.98%). Genotypic variance was 0.060, and heritability was 0.75, suggesting a strong genetic impact (Table 3). ADL values were highest in 3BIL-15 (6.37%), followed by 3BIL-22 (5.90%) and 3BIL-50 (4.99%). Notably low ADL values were found in genotypes such as 3BIL-89 (0.25%) and 3BIL-11 (0.98%). Genotypic variance was 0.120, and heritability was the highest among all traits (0.80), indicating strong genetic determination and a high potential for selection (Table 3).

In general, BIL genotypes displayed a broad spectrum of variation for all traits, reflecting the influence of parental diversity and transgressive segregation. Several lines, particularly from the 3BIL group, combined high yield with favorable protein and moderate fiber profiles, suggesting strong potential for selection in breeding programs.

Correlation analysis among agronomic and fiber-related traits in the backcross inbred line population revealed varying degrees of association (Table 2). Grain yield was not significantly correlated with most traits, except for a low but statistically significant positive correlation with crude fiber (r = 0.1671, p < 0.05). Protein content showed significant positive correlations with crude fiber (r = 0.4295, p < 0.001), neutral detergent fiber (NDF; r = 0.2368, p < 0.01), acid detergent fiber (ADF; r = 0.2369, p < 0.01), and acid detergent lignin (ADL; r = 0.2038, p < 0.01). Crude fiber content was positively correlated with NDF (r = 0.2229), ADF (r = 0.2739), and ADL (r = 0.2569), all statistically significant at p < 0.01. Furthermore, NDF showed positive correlations with both ADF (r = 0.6343) and ADL (r = 0.5966), while ADF and ADL were almost correlated (r = 0.9618), indicating strong interdependence among fiber fractions (Table 6).

QTL detection and candidate genes

The composite interval mapping (CIM) approach, using the BIL population, detected a total of 24 QTLs for yield, protein content and fiber content (Table 7; Fig. 4). From them 7 corresponded for yield, 12 for protein content and 5 for fiber content. QTLs were distributed along 9 chromosomes (1B, 2B, 3 A, 4 A, 4B, 5 A, 5B, 6B, and 7B). Several loci showed consistency across environments and years, indicating stable genomic regions involved in trait determination.

Grain yield

Grain yield QTL were detected in chromosomes 1B (4), 3 A (1), 4 A (1) and 6B (1) (Table 6). Two QTL were reported in the environment Valenzano 2021, one in Valenzano 2022, two in Gaudiano 2022 and finally two other QTLs were detected among environments (mean among years and locations).).

The phenotypic variance explained (PVE) by these QTLs ranged from 6.9% for QTL GY_VA01_3A to 41.2% in QTL GY_GA02_1B.1. This last QTL also reported the largest additive effect (1.05 t/ha), being the most promising QTL for yield increase in this population. The confidence interval in the genetic map covered 1.5 cM that was projected to the Svevo genome to 6.58 Mb. This hotspot harbored the candidate gene WPP domain-interacting tail-anchored protein 1, suggesting a potential role in plant development and stress response. An additional QTL (GY_GA02_1B.2) was detected at 54 cM with lower effect (LOD = 3.70).

A yield QTL on chromosome 3 A (GY_VA01_3A, 63 cM) was associated with ethylene-responsive transcription factor ERF118, indicating a regulatory role in stress adaptation or grain filling. Another yield QTL on 4 A (GY_VA01_4A, 40 cM) was associated with Calcium-transporting ATPase (Table 9), possibly linking calcium signaling with yield traits.

Protein content

Protein content QTLs were detected in chromosomes 2B (1), 4B (2), 5 A (2), 5B (3), 6B (3) and 7B (1). PVE ranged from 6.3% to 21.9%. The additive effect for these QTLs showed that alleles from MG29896 reported an increase in protein content, especially relevant for QTL GPC_VA01_6B with an increase in 1% and was linked to Calcium lipid binding protein-like (Table 8.), possibly involved in calcium-regulated protein expression.

The confidence interval for this QTL was restricted to 1 cM in the genetic map and 10.37 Mb in the genome sequence. This QTL reported the highest PVE for protein content (near 21.9%).

Chromosome 5B harbored three QTL (GPC_Mean_5B, GPC_VA02_5B, GPC_VA01_5B), all in close proximity (152–156 cM), with strong effects and overlapping confidence intervals. The Serine protease HtrA-like gene (Table 8.) in this region suggests a role in protein turnover or stress response.

Another prominent protein QTL was located on 4B at 25 cM (GPC_VA01_4B and GPC_Mean_4B), co-localizing across environments and linked to monoacylglycerol lipase (Table 7), a gene involved in lipid metabolism and potentially connected to nitrogen remobilization.

Protein QTL on 5 A (GPC_VA01_5A and GPC_Mean_5A) were associated with the coatomer subunit alpha-3-like gene and showed moderate additive effects. Similarly, a QTL on 2B (GPC_VA02_2B) was linked to Homeobox-leucine zipper family protein G (Table 8.), pointing to a transcriptional control mechanism.

Two QTL detected on 6B (GPC_Mean_6B and GPC_VA02_6B) shared proximity (130–131 cM) and were linked to glucomannan 4-beta-mannosyltransferase 4 (Table 8.), involved in hemicellulose biosynthesis, indicating a connection between cell wall metabolism and grain protein accumulation.

A minor but notable QTL on 7B (GPC_VA02_7B) was linked to zinc finger CCHC domain-containing protein 7 (Table 7), a gene potentially involved in transcriptional regulation under abiotic stress.

Fiber content

QTL analysis for fiber content revealed no significant QTLs for NDF. Two QTLs were detected for ADF and ADL in chromosomes 2B and 3 A, this last was the same for both traits. On 2B, ADF_2B and ADL_2B were both located between 35 and 44 cM, overlapping with bHLH91 G transcription factor, known for regulating cell wall-related genes. QTL ADF_3A and ADL_3A (43 cM) shared the same genomic region with annexin D5 (Table 8.), which may participate in cell expansion and structural integrity. A QTL for crude fiber (CF) was located on chromosome 5 A overlapped with protein QTL in the same region and was also associated with coatomer subunit alpha-3-like (Table 8.), indicating pleiotropic effects or tight linkage. PVE ranged from 22.8% for QTL CF_5A to 5.7% for QTL ADL_2B. ADF and ADL QTLs showed a positive additive effect from MG29896 whereas the QTL for CF reported a positive effect from Latino. This QTL showed a confidence interval of 1 cM and 1.4 Mb in the genetic and physical map respectively. The most significant QTLs for ADF (2B) and ADL (3 A) were located within a 3 cM interval in both cases and within a 17.9 Mb and 10.7 Mb respectively.

In this case the number of lines with recombinant introgressions ranged from no recombinations for QTLs GPC_VA01_6B and GPC_Mean_6B to 8 for QTLs GPC_Mean_5B, GPC_VA02_5B and GPC_VA02_7B. The average number of lines with recombinant introgression for these QTLs was 4.

Discussion

The molecular analysis of quantitative traits has advanced beyond traditional biparental QTL mapping toward approaches that capture the full spectrum of genetic variation underlying complex traits. Unlike classical QTL studies, which typically identify a limited number of loci segregating in a single population, genome-wide association studies (GWAS) exploit historical recombination in diverse germplasm panels to detect multiple loci with higher mapping resolution. By leveraging natural variation across many lines, GWAS allows more precise localization of trait-associated genomic regions, effectively improving the density and accuracy of genetic maps. Complementary approaches, such as nested association mapping (NAM) and multiparent advanced generation inter-cross (MAGIC) populations, combine the advantages of controlled crosses with broader allelic diversity, enabling high-resolution dissection of quantitative traits and the identification of minor-effect loci that may be missed in traditional QTL mapping [21–23].Backcross introgression lines libraries can accelerate the identification of genome regions containing genes of interest as only small portions of a donor genome with interest for a given trait are introgressed in a recurrent parent. When differences in performance in these lines are detected, the haplotype of donor genome is rapidly identified in a small part of the genome, concluding that genes affecting the trait are located within the introgression. The identification of recombinant lines within the introgression helps to fine map the traits of interest.

This study demonstrates the power of using backcross inbred lines (BILs) derived from crosses between elite durum wheat (Triticum turgidum L. var. durum, cv. Latino) and the wild tetraploid relative T. turgidum var. dicoccoides (accession MG29896) for dissecting the genetic basis of complex traits. Through high-resolution mapping and replicated phenotypic evaluation, we identified 24 QTLs associated with grain yield (GY), grain protein content (GPC), and fiber fractions (ADF, ADL, and crude fiber).

The use of wild relatives as donors of genetic variation represents a promising strategy to overcome the narrow genetic base of modern durum wheat cultivars. Introgression lines, particularly those developed through repeated backcrossing followed by selfing, offer a unique platform for assessing the functional effects of small, defined chromosomal regions from wild donors in an elite background. In our BIL population, introgressed segments from dicoccoides covered the entire genome, though their distribution was uneven—likely reflecting structural differences between donor and recipient genomes, linkage drag, and possibly selection against deleterious alleles during population development. In our BIL population, introgressed segments from dicoccoides covered the entire genome, though their distribution was uneven—likely reflecting structural differences between donor and recipient genomes, linkage drag, and possibly selection against deleterious alleles during population development. On average, each chromosome in the BIL population harbored ~ 4% of the donor genome. Some chromosomes (e.g., 5B, 7B) showed higher introgression rates (up to 6–9%), while others (1 A, 4B) were underrepresented. The observed differential introgression pattern is consistent with previous studies reporting recombination suppression in crosses with T. dicoccoides and other wild relatives. For instance, molecular mapping of T. dicoccoides × T. durum populations revealed uneven recombination rates and suppressed crossovers in certain chromosomal regions [24]. Similarly, structural reorganization and suppressed recombination in 5B and other chromosomes have been documented when wild emmer wheat is crossed with domesticated cultivars [25, 26]. These findings suggest that genomic regions exhibiting reduced introgression may correspond to structural rearrangements, centromeric regions, or loci under strong selection, which constrain recombination between donor and recipient genomes. Despite these constraints, the identification of recombinant BILs within QTL intervals enabled fine mapping of trait-associated regions. Recombinant lines allow partitioning of introgressed donor segments into smaller intervals, facilitating high-resolution QTL dissection and narrowing down candidate genes. This feature is particularly valuable in polyploid crops such as wheat, where genetic redundancy and homoeologous loci complicate trait dissection. The identification of 24 QTLs for yield, GPC, and fiber content underscores the robustness of the BIL approach for QTL detection. Importantly, all QTLs that enhanced GPC were derived from MG29896, consistent with its higher protein levels compared to Latino, and with previous findings from similar interspecific crosses [7, 8]. In contrast, QTLs associated with increased yield predominantly originated from the recurrent parent Latino, reflecting the typical trade-off between yield and protein content frequently observed in wheat breeding [5].

The QTLs identified in this study are largely consistent with previously reported loci. For instance, QTLs for GPC on chromosomes 5B and 6B overlapped with known loci reported in recent studies [20, 27] and with regions identified by Peleg et al. [8] and Blanco et al. [9] as harboring candidate genes related to protein biosynthesis and nitrogen metabolism. The QTL on 6B (GPC_VA01_6B) explained the highest phenotypic variance (~ 22%) and was tightly linked to a calcium lipid-binding protein gene, which could play a role in calcium-regulated protein expression. Chromosome 5B harbored a cluster of three overlapping QTLs for GPC, all associated with a serine protease HtrA-like gene, previously linked to protein turnover and stress responses [28–31].

Similarly, the strong yield QTL detected on 1B (GY_GA02_1B.1), explaining over 40% of phenotypic variance, aligns with genomic regions reported by Wang et al. [10] and Maccaferri et al. [16] using different mapping populations and SNP arrays. Other GY QTLs were detected on 3 A, 4 A, and 6B, and linked to candidate genes involved in ethylene response, calcium transport, and oxidative metabolism, respectively. These findings are in agreement with recent meta-QTL analyses that highlight consistent yield QTLs on chromosomes 1B, 3 A, and 6B in both durum and bread wheat under stress and non-stress conditions [29, 31].Several BILs with wild introgressions maintained high yield levels, suggesting that the common GPC-Yield trade-off can be broken, as also reported in recent meta-QTL and GWAS analyses. Even though yield-enhancing QTLs mostly originated from the recurrent parent Latino—as expected—the absence of significant negative correlations between wild-derived GPC or fiber QTLs and yield suggests that certain wild alleles can be introduced into elite backgrounds without detrimental agronomic effects. This supports the hypothesis that deleterious linkages in wild donors can be broken with sufficient recombination and line development. However, in this population, no significant negative correlation was found between these two traits. Lines such as 3BIL-69 and 3BIL-48 demonstrated high protein content and above-average yield, suggesting the possibility of decoupling these traits genetically—a result also echoed in recent studies aiming to overcome the GPC–yield trade-off through selection of specific QTL alleles or use of genomic selection models [30, 31].

Interestingly, some GPC QTLs were in proximity to loci controlling fiber traits (e.g., on 5 A), suggesting either pleiotropy or tight linkage—an important consideration for simultaneous trait improvement.

Novel QTLs detected for fiber components, particularly ADF and ADL on chromosomes 2B and 3 A, represent new contributions to the literature. Previous work has largely focused on total dietary fiber or crude fiber content without fine dissection into structural components. Although these traits are less frequently targeted in traditional breeding programs, they are increasingly valued for their role in human health and potential applications in bio-based products. The QTLs detected on 2B and 3 A for these traits had additive effects derived from the wild genome The co-localization of ADF and ADL QTLs with candidate genes such as bHLH91 G and annexin D5 suggests their involvement in secondary cell wall formation and lignin deposition, offering valuable targets for further functional validation.

The identification of recombination events within QTL regions is particularly noteworthy, as these recombinants are critical for fine mapping and future development of near-isogenic lines (NILs). Such NILs can be used to isolate and validate the effects of single loci, minimizing confounding from background genetic variation [5].

Interestingly, while several QTLs for GPC were tightly linked with those for fiber traits, no significant negative correlation was observed between yield and GPC or fiber content in the BIL population. This contrasts with the commonly reported antagonism between yield and protein content and suggests that certain genotypes—such as 3BIL-69 and 3BIL-48—may harbor beneficial allelic combinations that mitigate this trade-off. These lines, therefore, represent promising candidates for breeding programs aiming to enhance both nutritional quality and productivity.

To compare the QTLs detected in our study with other studies reporting QTLs for yield and quality related traits different meta-QTL analysis were used to cover the highest number of studies in durum wheat [27, 29, 31–33] (supplementary file 2). Additionally, these studies reported the position of common SNP markers in the reference genome or in the reference map [16].

Grain yield QTLs were compared with the meta-QTL analysis carried out by Arriaga et al. [29] where the authors compared the identification of QTLs under contrasting water environments. No common regions were detected among the studies for both irrigated and rainfed trials.

Several studies were used for the comparison of GPC. When compared to the meta-QTL analyses reported by Soriano et al. [31] for GPC traits, only the durumQTL5B.2 were located in a homologous position with GPC_Mean_5B and GPC_VA02_5B. However, the QTL position in both studies reported a large confidence interval probably due to the situation of these QTLs in centromeric positions, resulting in difficult for mapping comparison. These QTLs were common also with the previously reported QGpc.mgb-5B.1° from [27]. No common positions for GPC QTLs were found with the study carried out by Marcotuli et al. [32] and Roselló et al. [33]. These results support the use of the BIL derived population for the identification of new QTLs involving important traits for durum wheat breeding.

Conclusion

This study demonstrates an innovative implementation of interspecific backcross inbred lines (BILs) as a platform for precision introgression breeding in durum wheat. By integrating high-density SNP genotyping, replicated multi-environment field trials, and fine-scale QTL mapping, we applied a “smart introgression” strategy that enables the targeted transfer of beneficial alleles from Triticum turgidum ssp. dicoccoides into elite genetic backgrounds with minimal linkage drag.

Beyond validating known genomic regions, we identified novel and stable QTLs for fiber components (ADF and ADL) on chromosomes 2B and 3 A, traits rarely dissected in durum wheat and with potential relevance to both nutritional and processing quality. The discovery of environmentally consistent loci across nine chromosomes underscores the power of the BIL design for uncovering stable genetic determinants of complex quantitative traits.

These findings provide not only new biological insights into the genetic control of protein, fiber, and yield-related traits, but also a practical framework for accelerating pre-breeding and cultivar improvement through marker-assisted and genomic selection. This work thus represents a methodological and conceptual advancement toward more efficient use of wild genetic diversity in modern wheat breeding.
