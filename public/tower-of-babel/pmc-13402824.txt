Lysosome-related biomarkers in peripheral blood immune cells discriminate sepsis from SIRS
Authors: Chenglin Wang, Ling Mu, Lu Liu, Haili Li, Yingchun Hu, Defeng Yin, Hao Jiang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractThis study aimed to screen lysosome-related genes that distinguish sepsis from Systemic Inflammatory Response Syndrome (SIRS), in order to provide potential targets for the differential diagnosis of sepsis and for lysosome-targeted therapeutic strategies. Peripheral blood samples were collected from 12 SIRS patients and 20 sepsis patients for RNA sequencing and differential expression analysis. Meanwhile, lysosome-related gene sets were obtained from the Gene Ontology database. The intersection was taken between the differentially expressed genes and the lysosomal gene sets. Subsequently, Protein-Protein Interaction (PPI), Gene Ontology (GO), and Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) analyses were performed on these overlapping genes. Meta-analysis was used to screen for core genes, and their diagnostic efficacy was evaluated using Receiver Operating Characteristic (ROC) curves. Furthermore, single-cell RNA sequencing was performed to identify the immune cell types that predominantly express the core genes, facilitating the selection of appropriate cell models for subsequent experimental validation. Functional enrichment analysis revealed that these 21 overlapping genes were significantly enriched in biological pathways such as receptor metabolic process, autophagy, vacuolar transport, cellular catabolic process, and lysosomal transport. Meta-analysis identified four core genes: CD1C, RNASE6, and SNCA were significantly downregulated in the sepsis group, while DRAM1 was significantly upregulated. Diagnostic efficacy evaluation demonstrated that all four core genes possessed good discriminatory value, with AUC as follows: CD1C (0.758), DRAM1 (0.888), RNASE6 (0.737), and SNCA (0.765). Single-cell RNA sequencing analysis suggested that CD1C and RNASE6 are primarily expressed in circulating monocyte-macrophages and B cells, DRAM1 is mainly expressed in circulating monocyte-macrophages, and SNCA is predominantly expressed in circulating monocyte-macrophages and platelets. The four core genes identified in this study could serve as potential diagnostic biomarkers to distinguish sepsis from SIRS. Introduction

Systemic Inflammatory Response Syndrome (SIRS) is a systemic inflammatory state triggered by infectious or non-infectious factors. Although the definition of sepsis has evolved from “SIRS resulting from infection” in the Sepsis-1.0 criteria to the Sepsis-3.0 criteria, which places greater emphasis on “dysregulated host response and organ dysfunction“1. This shift has created new clinical dilemmas despite improved specificity. As the SIRS criteria are no longer central to the diagnosis, treatment may not be initiated promptly unless the patient has already developed organ dysfunction2. This not only signifies the abandonment of a familiar, widely used, and sensitive early warning tool but also directly contributes to the current lack of biomarkers capable of distinguishing sepsis from SIRS. Early identification and intervention (such as initiating anti-infective therapy and organ support in advance) in this high-risk population are crucial, as delays in sepsis treatment are significantly associated with increased mortality, with each hour of delay potentially increasing mortality by 7–10%3. In summary, despite ongoing advancements in diagnosis and treatment, developing specific biomarkers for the differential diagnosis and treatment of sepsis remains a critical gap in current clinical practice.

The lysosome is not merely a degradation center containing more than 50 hydrolases (with an optimal pH of 4.5–5.0.5.0), responsible for the breakdown and recycling of endocytic, phagocytic, and autophagic substrates4. It also serves as a key hub for maintaining cellular homeostasis by regulating secretion, membrane repair, signal transduction, and energy metabolism, with its function being intricately linked to various physiological and pathological processes5. Consequently, the lysosome plays a significant role in immune regulation, including the modulation of inflammation, antigen presentation, and immune cell persistence. In the context of sepsis, the activation of pan-lysosomal pathways suggests that lysosomal dysfunction is associated with the disease pathogenesis6. This notion is supported by in vivo experiments: compensatory increases in lysosomal levels in septic mouse models, along with increased liver injury and mortality observed upon functional inhibition of lysosomes, confirm their endogenous protective role7. However, under excessive stress, increased lysosomal membrane permeability can lead to the leakage of contents, triggering novel forms of cell death. For instance, both NLRP3 inflammasome activation and NET-induced macrophage pyroptosis can exacerbate the inflammatory cascade in sepsis by promoting lysosomal rupture and the release of contents such as cathepsin B8. Neutrophil Extracellular Traps (NETs) can promote inflammation in sepsis by inducing macrophage pyroptosis and facilitating cathepsin B release from ruptured lysosomes9. Given these pivotal roles, targeting the lysosome represents a highly promising therapeutic direction for modulating inflammation and metabolism.

Based on the well-established SIRS and sepsis clinical cohort and transcriptomic resource from our research group, the current study differs distinctly from our earlier work focusing on metabolic pathways and mitochondrial functional genes. To the best of our knowledge, this is the first study to screen potential molecular markers for discriminating sepsis from SIRS from the perspective of lysosome-related regulatory pathways. We further combined bioinformatic analysis and single‑cell transcriptome profiling to characterize the cellular expression distribution of core genes in peripheral blood immune cells, providing novel candidate biomarkers for differential diagnosis between the two clinical syndromes. Therefore, this study integrates RNA sequencing and bioinformatic analyses, aiming to screen key lysosome-related genes associated with the differential diagnosis from SIRS to sepsis. The goal is to provide new research directions for the differential diagnosis of sepsis and lysosome-targeted therapies, and to offer novel insights into its underlying molecular mechanisms. Flowchart of the study design. The flowchart illustrates the overall analytical strategy: (1) Collection of peripheral blood samples from SIRS and sepsis patients for RNA sequencing and identification of DEGs; (2) Intersection of DEGs with lysosome-related gene sets from the Gene Ontology database; (3) PPI, GO, and KEGG enrichment analyses of overlapping genes; (4) Screening of core genes via meta-analysis and evaluation of diagnostic efficacy using ROC curves; (5) Determination of cellular expression patterns of core genes using single-cell RNA sequencing.

Methods

Subject recruitment

This study prospectively enrolled 12 SIRS patients and 20 sepsis patients admitted to the Emergency Intensive Care Unit (EICU) of The Affiliated Hospital of Southwest Medical University between January 2019 and December 2020. The study was approved by the hospital’s ethics committee (ky2018029) and was registered as a clinical trial (ChiCTR1900021261), and the trial registration date is 04/02/2019. Written informed consent was obtained from all participants. Inclusion criteria were: (1) Sepsis group: meeting the Sepsis-3 consensus criteria: presence of a clinically confirmed acute infection plus an acute increase in the Sequential Organ Failure Assessment (SOFA) score of at least 2 points compared with the baseline admission SOFA (ΔSOFA ≥ 2)10; (2) SIRS group: meeting at least two of the established clinical criteria (abnormal body temperature, heart rate, respiratory rate/PaCO2, or white blood cell count)11; (3) Age ≥ 18 years; (4) Provision of informed consent. Exclusion criteria were: (1) Pregnancy or lactation; (2) History of mental illness; (3) Immunodeficiency or HIV positivity; (4) Chronic organ dysfunction. All SIRS patients underwent comprehensive infectious screening including clinical examination, chest imaging, urine analysis, sputum Gram stain and culture, blood culture, and procalcitonin (PCT) measurement. Patients with confirmed or highly suspected infection were excluded from the SIRS group. Only SIRS patients without evidence of infection were enrolled. This study conforms to the Declaration of Helsinki.

Blood collection and processing

Total RNA from blood was collected and stabilized using the PAXgene Blood RNA System to ensure transcriptomic data quality12. The procedure was as follows: Prior to collection, it was confirmed that the blood collection tubes were at room temperature and correctly labeled. After discarding the first tube of blood, the PAXgene tube was connected, and at least 2.5 mL of blood was drawn utilizing its vacuum, maintaining the puncture for at least 10 s. Immediately after collection, the tubes were gently inverted 8–10 times for mixing. Samples were placed upright at room temperature, then transferred to −20℃ for 24 h, and finally stored in a −80℃ ultra-low temperature freezer.

RNA extraction and sequencing

Total RNA was extracted from 500 µL of whole blood using the Trizol method according to the manufacturer’s protocol. After quality control using the Agilent 2100 Bioanalyzer, mRNA was enriched using Oligo(dT) magnetic beads. Subsequently, a library was constructed through steps including reverse transcription, double-stranded cDNA synthesis, PCR amplification, and phi29-mediated DNA nanoball preparation. Sequencing was performed on the BGIseq500 platform for 50 bp single-end reads. Raw data were filtered using SOAPnuke (v1.5.2), aligned to the reference genome using HISAT2, and gene expression levels were finally calculated using RSEM software (based on Bowtie2 alignment results)13,14.

Screening of differentially expressed genes (DEGs)

Differential expression analysis was performed using the iDEP online platform (version 0.96), a commonly used tool for genomic and transcriptomic big data analysis15. First, the platform was used for preliminary quality control and log transformation of the raw RNA sequencing data. Quality control included generating box plots and density plots to assess data homogeneity between samples and identify potential outliers. Subsequently, the DESeq2 algorithm was applied for differential expression analysis, with screening thresholds set at a False Discovery Rate (FDR) < 0.05 and an absolute fold change |FC|≥1.2.

Screening of lysosome-related genes

Lysosome-related genes were retrieved from the Gene Ontology database (http://geneontology.org/). The retrieval adopted GO term: GO:0005764 (lysosome) and all its subordinate child terms, database release version 2023-12-01, limited to Homo sapiens. After deduplication, a total of 878 lysosome-related genes were obtained. The Venny 2.1 online tool was then used to intersect this gene set with the sepsis DEGs to obtain the overlapping target genes.

PPI analysis

To assess functional associations among the proteins encoded by the lysosome-related overlapping genes, a PPI network was constructed using the STRING database online platform16. The minimum interaction confidence score was set to 0.15 to screen for interactions with medium confidence or higher. Potential key proteins occupying central positions were identified by analyzing the network topology.

GO analysis

To elucidate the potential biological functions of the overlapping genes, Gene Ontology enrichment analysis was performed using the Omicshare online platform. This analysis covered three categories: Biological Process (BP), Cellular Component (CC), and Molecular Function (MF). The top 20 most significantly enriched terms in each category were extracted, and interactive charts were generated for visualization.

KEGG analysis

Based on the Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes database17, pathway enrichment analysis for the lysosome-related overlapping genes was conducted using the Omicshare platform to reveal significantly enriched signaling pathways in the genomic context. The significance threshold was set at P < 0.05, and the top 20 most enriched pathways were selected for visualization to interpret their potential biological significance.

Meta analysis

Five publicly available gene expression datasets (GSE653518, GSE12624, GSE2875019, GSE6304220, GSE7422421 were retrieved from the Gene Expression Omnibus (GEO) database. Detailed information regarding platform, sample size, clinical grouping, study context and processing strategy for each dataset is provided in Supplementary Table 1. All datasets were standardized using a unified pipeline including background correction, log2 transformation, quantile normalization and probe annotation consolidation. Datasets with ambiguous clinical definitions or irrelevant subgroups were excluded to avoid mixing heterogeneous clinical constructs. Meta-analysis was performed to evaluate the consistent expression trend of candidate genes across cohorts with adjusted heterogeneity assessment.

ROC curve analysis

To evaluate the diagnostic efficacy of the core genes, ROC curve analysis was performed using the GSE74224 dataset21 from GEO (containing 31 SIRS and 74 sepsis patients). The Area Under the Curve (AUC) was calculated to quantify their discriminative value, with an AUC closer to 1.0 indicating higher diagnostic accuracy.

Single-cell RNA sequencing

To preliminarily explore the cellular expression distribution of core genes, we performed single‑cell RNA sequencing using peripheral blood samples from a small exploratory cohort (2 healthy controls, 1 SIRS patient, 2 sepsis patients). Given the very limited sample size, this analysis was only used for qualitative cellular localization (i.e., which cell types express the genes) and not for quantitative comparison between groups. Single-cell library construction and sequencing were performed using the 10× Genomics platform. Standard data processing was conducted using Cell Ranger and Seurat packages, including quality control, normalization, PCA, and t-SNE clustering22.

Cell type annotation and localization analysis

To define cell identities, the FindAllMarkers function in the Seurat package was used to identify marker genes for each cell cluster, followed by visualization. Cell type annotation was performed using the SingleR package and the HPCA reference dataset23; this method automatically annotates cells by calculating correlation with reference cell expression profiles. All single-cell data have been archived on the OEbiotech cloud platform.

Results

Quality control of the mRNA sequencing data from 32 samples (Sepsis = 20, SIRS = 12) showed good homogeneity between groups and no outlier samples (Fig. 2A-B). Differential expression analysis (thresholds: |FC|≥1.2, FDR < 0.05) identified a total of 314 DEGs, of which 277 were upregulated and 37 were downregulated in the SIRS group compared to the sepsis group (Fig. 2C-D). Screening of lysosome-related genes

By intersecting the set of 878 lysosome-related genes with the 314 sepsis DEGs, we successfully identified 21 key sepsis-associated lysosome-related genes (Fig. 3A).

Screening and functional characterization of lysosome-related genes. (A) Venn diagram showing the intersection of 314 sepsis-related DEGs and 878 lysosome-related genes, yielding 21 overlapping key genes associated with lysosome function and molecular distinction between sepsis and SIRS. (B) PPI network of the 21 overlapping genes (21 nodes, 49 edges). These genes are intensively involved in receptor internalization, vacuolar transport, vesicle-mediated transport, and lysosomal homeostasis, which are critical for lysosomal trafficking, degradation, and immune regulation in sepsis. (C) Expression heatmap of the 21 overlapping genes. Most genes were highly expressed in the SIRS group, whereas a small subset was upregulated in the sepsis group, indicating distinct expression patterns linked to lysosomal dysfunction in sepsis.

PPI network analysis

PPI network analysis revealed dense interactions among the 21 lysosome-related genes (49 edges, average node degree: 4.67, PPI enrichment p-value: 3.96e-12). These genes were functionally enriched in processes such as receptor internalization, vacuolar transport, vesicle-mediated transport, and lysosomal homeostasis (Fig. 3B). The expression heatmap showed that genes like RNASE6 and CD1C were specifically highly expressed in the SIRS group, while DRAM1 and TNFAIP3 were highly expressed in the sepsis group, with significant differences (P < 0.05) (Fig. 3C). The 21 overlapping genes are core lysosome‑associated molecules that participate in lysosomal transport, vesicle trafficking, autophagy, and cellular catabolic processes. Their dysregulation suggests disrupted lysosomal function in macrophages during the transition from SIRS to sepsis, which contributes to impaired pathogen clearance, excessive inflammation, and disease progression.

GO and KEGG enrichment analysis

GO and KEGG enrichment analysis (top 20 terms) revealed the core functions of the 21 overlapping genes. They were primarily involved in biological processes such as autophagy and lysosomal transport, localized to lysosomes and related membrane structures, and performed functions like molecular adapter activity. Key enriched pathways included endocytosis, ubiquitin-mediated proteolysis, and protein processing in the endoplasmic reticulum (Fig. 4A-D).

GO and KEGG enrichment analysis of the 21 overlapping lysosome-related DEGs between SIRS and sepsis groups. (A) GO BP enrichment; (B) GO CC enrichment; (C) GO MF enrichment. GO analysis was performed using the Omicshare platform with a significance threshold of P < 0.05. Genes were mainly enriched in vesicle transport, lysosomal localization, and molecular adaptor activity. (D) KEGG pathway enrichment analysis (P < 0.05). Top enriched pathways include endocytosis, ubiquitin‑mediated proteolysis, and protein processing in the endoplasmic reticulum.

Meta-analysis

Comprehensive meta-analysis based on five GEO datasets confirmed that, compared to the SIRS group, DRAM1 expression was significantly increased in the sepsis group, while the expressions of CD1C, RNASE6, and SNCA were significantly decreased (p < 0.05) (Fig. 5A-D). The analysis employed random/fixed effects models and included p-value correction.

Meta-analysis of four core lysosome-related genes in multiple independent GEO datasets. (A-D) Pooled effect sizes and expression trends of CD1C, DRAM1, RNASE6, and SNCA based on five public human sepsis transcriptome datasets (GSE6535, GSE12624, GSE28750, GSE63042, GSE74224). Random‑ or fixed‑effects models were applied as appropriate. Compared with the SIRS group, expression of CD1C, RNASE6, and SNCA was significantly decreased, while DRAM1 was significantly increased in the sepsis group (all P < 0.05).

ROC curve analysis

ROC curve analysis confirmed that all four core genes possessed good diagnostic efficacy, with AUC values as follows: CD1C (0.758), DRAM1 (0.888), RNASE6 (0.737), and SNCA (0.765) (Fig. 6A-D). The discriminative ability demonstrated by these genes highlights their significant potential as differential diagnosis biomarkers for sepsis and as new targets for research into lysosome-targeted therapies.

ROC curve analysis of the diagnostic performance of four core genes in distinguishing sepsis from SIRS. (A-D) ROC curves and AUC values of CD1C (0.758), DRAM1 (0.888), RNASE6 (0.737), and SNCA (0.765). Analysis was performed using the GSE74224 dataset (31 SIRS patients, 74 sepsis patients).

Single-cell RNA sequencing and cell type identification

After stringent quality control of the single-cell RNA sequencing data, the number of high-quality cells per sample ranged from 3,108 to 8,509. To define cell identities, we used classical cell type-specific marker genes for annotation. The marker genes used included: PTPRC for pan-immune cells, CD8A for CD8 + T cells, CD14 for circulating monocyte-macrophages, CD3E for CD4 + T cells, CD79A for B cells, FCGR3A for natural killer (NK) cells, FLT3 for dendritic cells, PPBP for platelets, and PRF1 for cytotoxic T cells. Based on the expression profiles of these marker genes, we successfully identified major cell populations including circulating monocyte-macrophages, T cells, B cells, NK cells, and platelets (Fig. 7A-I). These single-cell data and clustering results were derived from samples of healthy controls, SIRS patients, and sepsis patients combined, and were used to define major immune cell populations for subsequent core gene localization analysis.

Single-cell clustering analysis identified 9 cell subpopulations, including circulating monocyte-macrophages (clusters 3, 5), T cells (clusters 1, 2, 6, 8), B cells (cluster 7), NK cells (cluster 4), and platelets (cluster 9) (Fig. 8A-D). The core gene expression map revealed that: CD1C and RNASE6 were primarily expressed in circulating monocyte-macrophages and B cells; DRAM1 was specifically highly expressed in circulating monocyte-macrophages; SNCA showed predominant expression in circulating monocyte-macrophages and platelets (Fig. 9A-D).

Discussion

In clinical practice, the timely initiation of antimicrobial therapy is crucial for septic patients, while unnecessary antibiotic exposure should be avoided in patients with non-infectious SIRS24. Therefore, the early identification of whether SIRS patients have an infectious source is critical. However, the clinical manifestations of sepsis and SIRS often overlap, posing a significant challenge for their differentiation. In this context, biomarkers capable of reliably distinguishing sepsis from SIRS would not only help accurately identify patients requiring antimicrobial therapy but also positively impact patient outcomes; from a theoretical perspective, such markers could also serve as powerful tools for determining the presence of underlying infection in SIRS patients. The lysosome, as a key organelle dynamically regulating cellular metabolism and autophagy, plays an important role in the pathogenesis of sepsis: it influences both the regulation of inflammatory responses by autophagy and may contribute to bacterial immune evasion, making it an ideal starting point for the search for novel diagnostic markers25. These genes were mainly expressed in circulating monocyte-macrophages in peripheral blood, supporting their role as blood-based biomarkers.

Cluster of Differentiation 1 C (CD1C) is a lipid antigen-presenting molecule primarily expressed in dendritic cells and B cells. Its expression can be induced by inflammatory signals, and it localizes to lysosomes, playing a pivotal role in immune responses26. The expression changes of CD1C vary across different disease states: in systemic lupus erythematosus and non-small cell lung cancer, the number of CD1C+ dendritic cells is decreased or their function is impaired, correlating with an immunosuppressive state27; whereas in gastric cancer patients, an increased number has been observed, and in vitro studies have confirmed its ability to stimulate tumor-specific immune responses28, highlighting the complexity of its immunomodulatory functions. This study preliminarily identified CD1C as a key lysosome-related gene associated with molecular differences between sepsis and SIRS. We found that CD1C is expressed in circulating monocyte-macrophages and B cells and is involved in autophagy and vesicular transport. Based on this, we hypothesize that CD1C may play a critical role in the immunopathology of sepsis by mediating the phagocytosis of bacterial toxins, antigen presentation, and subsequent lysosomal degradation pathways in circulating monocyte-macrophages.

Damage-regulated Autophagy Modulator 1 (DRAM1) is a lysosomal membrane protein that positively regulates autophagic flux by enhancing lysosomal acidification and is a key node connecting p53/NF-κB signaling with autophagy29. DRAM1 plays a dual role in various pathophysiological processes: on one hand, its upregulated expression can restore autophagic flux, thereby protecting cardiomyocytes from ischemic stress injury30; on the other hand, it is also involved in the disruption of the intestinal mucosal barrier in inflammatory bowel disease31. Most importantly, in host defense against bacterial infection, DRAM1 positively regulates macrophage autophagy to resist intracellular pathogens by promoting the transport of mycobacteria within the phagolysosomal pathway32. Through RNA sequencing and multi-dataset meta-analysis, this study preliminarily screened DRAM1 as a notable lysosome-related gene with potential value in discriminating sepsis from SIRS. We observed significant upregulation of DRAM1 in the sepsis group, showing moderate discriminatory value for distinguishing sepsis from SIRS. Combined with its close association with autophagy in GO analysis, we speculate that DRAM1 may contribute to the molecular differences between sepsis and SIRS by regulating autophagic activity in circulating monocyte-macrophages.

Human Ribonuclease 6 (RNASE6) is an antimicrobial lysosomal-resident protein that exerts its antibacterial function through the interaction of its surface cationic residues with the negatively charged bacterial envelope33. Studies have shown that RNASE6, being a lysosomal-resident protein, is considered a protective factor in urinary tract infections34; concurrently, it has also been identified as a novel inflammatory risk factor for Alzheimer’s disease, where its overexpression may be associated with increased reactive oxygen species levels and pro-inflammatory cytokine secretion35. This study found that RNASE6 expression is downregulated in circulating monocyte-macrophages in sepsis compared with SIRS, and the gene is enriched in biological processes such as receptor-mediated endocytosis. We speculate that RNASE6 may be involved in host immune and inflammatory responses in sepsis, potentially through its association with lysosomal function and related antimicrobial molecular pathways.

α-Synuclein (SNCA) is a multifunctional protein whose homeostasis imbalance not only drives neuroinflammation36 but also plays a complex role in the regulation of autophagy37. Most notably in the field, SNCA gene mutations or overexpression are primary causes of familial Parkinson’s disease (PD), and its aberrant protein aggregation pathology is a core mechanism in PD pathogenesis38. It is important to note that cells precisely regulate the degradation of SNCA through two major mechanisms: the ubiquitin-proteasome system and the autophagy-lysosome pathway39. This study identifies SNCA as a core gene localized to lysosomes and involved in the autophagy process. Based on these findings, we speculate that in sepsis, SNCA may participate in pathogen clearance and cellular homeostasis maintenance through modulating lysosomal autophagy, and its dysregulated expression may be correlated with the molecular characteristics distinguishing sepsis from SIRS.

It should be emphasized that the present study is based merely on transcriptomic and bioinformatic analyses, lacking protein-level verification, cellular functional experiments, prospective clinical validation, and therapeutic intervention tests. By integrating RNA sequencing, bioinformatic functional enrichment, and diagnostic efficacy evaluation, we identified four core lysosome-related genes—CD1C, DRAM1, RNASE6, and SNCA—as candidate transcriptomic markers with potential discriminatory value for distinguishing sepsis from SIRS. These genes exhibit moderate diagnostic potential and are predominantly enriched in peripheral blood monocyte-lineage cells. Given the absence of further experimental confirmation at this stage, all identified genes should be considered preliminary molecular candidates rather than fully validated clinical diagnostic or therapeutic targets. This finding provides novel molecular clues for the differential diagnosis of sepsis and offers a theoretical reference for subsequent exploration of lysosome-associated pathogenesis and underlying regulatory mechanisms. Further validation including Western blot and immunofluorescence will be conducted in follow-up studies to verify the differential expression of these four genes at the protein level, and larger independent cohorts as well as in vitro and in vivo functional experiments are still needed to support their future clinical translational application.

Conclusion

This study suggests that CD1C, DRAM1, RNASE6, and SNCA form a candidate molecular signature functionally related to the monocyte-lysosome regulatory pathway. These genes show potential discriminatory value for distinguishing sepsis from SIRS, and may provide preliminary clues and theoretical implications for subsequent lysosome-associated mechanistic studies, rather than immediate clinical tool development.

Limitations

This study has several limitations that should be acknowledged. First, the sample size of the clinical cohort is relatively small (12 SIRS and 20 sepsis patients), which may limit the statistical power and generalizability of the findings. Second, the study adopts a cross-sectional design rather than a longitudinal design, so we can only support the use of these genes for differential diagnosis rather than for predicting disease progression. Third, our findings are based on transcriptomic data, and protein-level validation has not been performed to confirm expression changes. Fourth, the public gene datasets used for meta-analysis may have inherent heterogeneity in patient enrollment criteria, treatment strategies, and sequencing platforms, which could introduce variability. Fifth, the single-cell RNA sequencing analysis is exploratory with a small number of samples, serving only to locate cellular expression rather than to quantify cell proportion changes. Finally, this study is bioinformatic-based and lacks in vitro or in vivo functional validation of the four core genes. Future studies with larger cohorts, longitudinal design, protein verification, and functional experiments are warranted to confirm these findings.
