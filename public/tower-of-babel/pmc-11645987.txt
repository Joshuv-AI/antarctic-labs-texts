Using the Melosh Model of Acoustic Fluidization to Simulate Impact Crater Collapse on the Earth and Moon
Authors: A Rajšić, B C Johnson, G S Collins, H C F C Hay
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractThe formation of complex craters requires some form of transient weakening of target rocks. Acoustic fluidization is one proposed mechanism applied in many numerical simulations of large crater formation. In a companion paper, we describe implementing the Melosh model of acoustic fluidization in the iSALE shock physics code. Here, we explore the effect of Melosh model parameters on crater collapse and determine the range of parameters that reproduce observed crater depth‐to‐diameter trends on the Earth and Moon. Target viscosity in the Melosh model is proportional to the vibrational wavelength, λ, and the longevity of acoustic vibrations is ∝λQ (Q—quality factor). Our simulations show that λ affects the size of the fluidized region, its fluidity, and the magnitude of the vibrations, producing a variety of crater collapse styles. The size of the fluidized region is strongly affected by the Q. The regeneration factor, e, controls the amount of (re)generated acoustic energy and its localization. We find that a decrease in e leads to less crater collapse and that there are trade‐offs between e and Q. This trade‐off contributes to the more realistic Q values than those used in the Block model. The diffusion of vibrations in regions with high stress and strain is controlled by the scattering term, ξ. Compared to the Block model, the Melosh model results in a shallower zone of weakening in complex craters and enhanced strain localization around the crater rim. The parameter set that produces best depth‐diameter trends is λ = 0.2×impactor radius, Q = 10–50, e = 0.025–0.1, and ξ = 10–105 m2s−1.Keywords: impact cratering, crater collapse, acoustic fluidization, transient weakening

Introduction

Impact cratering is a geological process important to all solid bodies in the Solar System. An impact first excavates a bowl‐shaped transient crater that will collapse under the force of gravity. In the case of a simple crater, collapse is limited to the formation of a breccia lens, and the crater keeps its initial bowl‐shaped morphology (simple craters, depth‐to‐diameter ratio (d/D) is ∼15). For larger complex craters, the forces of gravity exceed material strength beneath and around the crater, and plastic flow causes floor collapse in which the transient crater floor is uplifted (e.g., Melosh, 1989). The small depth/diameter (d/D≤110) ratio observed in complex craters is the result of this uplift (Melosh, 1989). Morphological features of complex craters include a central peak surrounded by a flat floor and terraced rims and outer zones of faulting (e.g., Kenkmann et al., 2014). The degree of crater collapse increases for complex craters of increasing diameter, producing a peak ring.

Crater floor collapse requires that the effective rock strength beneath the crater is smaller than approximately 3 MPa and with little or no friction (McKinnon, 1978; Melosh, 1977). This fluid‐like rheology is inconsistent with laboratory measurements of rock strength. Thus, some mechanism(s) must temporarily weaken the target rock. The most widely used model of temporary rock weakening during crater collapse is the acoustic fluidization model (Melosh, 1979). This model relies on acoustic vibrations (pressure fluctuations) generated during the impact and scattered within the rock mass and fault zones. When the pressure fluctuations cause the local pressure to drop below the overburden pressure, localized sliding can occur even when shear stresses are low. The cumulative effect of these sliding events causes the entire rock mass to behave like a nonlinear fluid.

Alternative models for temporary weakening in rocks have been proposed. One of them considers thermal softening of the target (O’Keefe & Ahrens, 1993, 1999). However, this model is efficient only for large‐scale craters because shock heating affects too small a volume for crater diameters <200 km. Another model is dynamic weakening along faults, based on laboratory measurements of friction coefficient as a function of strain and strain rate (Senft & Stewart, 2009). The dynamic weakening model applies an empirical friction reduction in rapidly deforming cells during impact cratering (Senft & Stewart, 2009). Although simulations of craters ∼100 km in diameter showed that dynamic friction reduction was sufficient to explain complex crater collapse, the simulation results were resolution‐dependent, and the model was not tested in different targets or for various crater sizes. Crawford and Schultz (2013) tested a brittle, damaged model with localized thermal softening (BDL). Manual adjustment of BDL model parameters successfully reproduced complex craters on the Moon.

While these alternative weakening models had some level of success in reproducing complex crater collapse, they have not been as thoroughly tested as acoustic fluidization models. Moreover, the exact mechanism and duration of transient target weakening are unknown due to the lack of direct observations. However, recent drilling (Morgan et al., 2017) of the Chicxulub impact structure revealed evidence of the mechanisms and chronology of deformation in the peak ring rock mass (Riller et al., 2018). A transition from pervasive fracturing during transient cavity excavation to discrete formation of (ultra‐)cataclasite zones and shear faulting, followed by melt emplacement and ductile deformation, was suggested as a strong support of the acoustic fluidization mechanism. Most importantly, the (ultra‐)cataclasite zones lack evidence for shear heating and were proposed as the strain zones that (re)generate pressure fluctuations during crater collapse.

An additional advantage of the acoustic fluidization concept is that it can represent the solution for other geological processes, such as the friction reduction along seismic faults during earthquakes (Melosh, 1996) and long‐runout landslides (Collins & Melosh, 2003; Melosh, 1979). Discrete particle simulations of long‐runout landslides (Campbell et al., 1995) showed that friction reduction occurs even when fluids are absent. Campbell et al. (1995) and Johnson, Campbell, and Melosh (2016) show the low effective friction of long‐runout landslides in these simulations is caused by sliding that occurs mainly when the overburden pressure is relieved by the pressure vibrations in the slide, consistent with the acoustic fluidization theory.

The effects of acoustic fluidization have been described mathematically by two very similar models (Melosh & Ivanov, 1999). Melosh (1979) introduced the concept of acoustic fluidization to explain the failure of geologic materials under surprisingly low differential stresses in the context of seismic faulting, long‐runout landslides, and the collapse of large craters. This pioneering work presented a mathematical model to describe the evolution of acoustic energy within a deforming rock mass, which we refer to here as the Melosh model of acoustic fluidization. While the Melosh model has subsequently been applied to tectonic faulting (Melosh, 1996) and long‐runout landslides (Collins & Melosh, 2003), it has not been applied in earnest to impact cratering. Instead, many impact simulations of complex and large crater formation in recent years have used the so‐called Block model approximation of acoustic fluidization (Ivanov & Artemieva, 2002; Ivanov & Kostuchenko, 1997; Melosh & Ivanov, 1999; Wünnemann & Ivanov, 2003).

The Block model has remained the most widely adopted implementation of acoustic fluidization because impact simulations employing this model have successfully reproduced the size‐morphology progression of impact craters on the Moon and Earth (e.g., Baker et al., 2016; Collins, 2014; Wünnemann & Ivanov, 2003) as well as large‐scale structural and geophysical observations at many terrestrial craters (e.g., Collins et al., 2002; Ivanov, 2005; Ivanov, 2019; Ivanov & Artemieva, 2002; Morgan et al., 2017; Wünnemann & Lange, 2002). Melosh and Ivanov (1999) compared the relation between strain rate and applied stress in the Block and Melosh models and showed that they reproduce similar rheological behavior. However, an important distinction between these two models is that current implementations of the Block model do not include (re)generation and scattering of acoustic energy, which may be important processes in the evolution of impact‐generated pressure fluctuations and the kinematics of crater collapse (Hay et al., 2024).

In a companion paper, Hay et al. (2024) describe how the Melosh model was implemented in the iSALE shock physics code. Hay et al. (2024) show that including acoustic energy regeneration and scattering can qualitatively change how acoustic fluidization facilitates crater collapse. Here, we build on the efforts of this and other previous works (Collins & Melosh, 2003; Hay et al., 2024; Johnson, Campbell, & Melosh, 2016; Melosh, 1979) to explore the sensitivity of final crater morphology to various parameters of the Melosh model. We define the parameter space for crater formation and observe the transition in simple‐to‐complex depth‐to‐diameter transition on Earth and the Moon. The aim is to explore how acoustic fluidization affects crater collapse when regeneration and scattering of acoustic energy are considered. Our results show that varied vibrational wavelength contributes to shallower or deeper target weakening, producing contrasting complex crater collapse styles and a variety of subsurface deformations not observed in the Block model. The (re)generation and scattering factors also permit localized weakening in the target. The additional contribution of (re)generation in the acoustic fluidization model is that it contributes to more realistic Q values compared to the Block model (Bradley & Fort, 1966; Rae et al., 2017; Waters & Waters, 1981).

The Block Model and the Melosh Model of Acoustic Fluidization

A detailed mathematical description of the differences between the Block and Melosh models of acoustic fluidization is given by Melosh and Ivanov (1999) and Hay et al. (2024). Here, we briefly summarize the conceptual differences between the two models before analyzing the Melosh model and its governing parameters in detail.

The Block and Melosh models are two alternative implementations of the weakening of a rock mass by transient internal pressure oscillations or acoustic vibrations. In the context of an impact, these vibrations are generated by the impact shock wave and, potentially, the subsequent deformation of the target rocks around the crater. The vibrations cause pressure fluctuations that can locally reduce the ambient overburden pressure, reducing the resistance to slip and allowing deformation to occur. When this effect is averaged over time and space, the rock mass takes on a non‐linear fluid‐like rheology in which stronger vibrations reduce the shear resistance of the rock mass and make it more mobile. Internal dissipation and scattering reduce the amplitude of the pressure fluctuations until the rheology of the rock mass returns to normal. Schematically, this can be understood as the generation of an acoustically fluidized zone of target material surrounding the crater that shrinks in size as the crater grows and collapses until it finally disappears, returning the deformed target rocks to their unfluidized state (Figure 1).

The Block Model

The commonly adopted Block model, as implemented, for example, in iSALE (Wünnemann & Ivanov, 2003), realizes this conceptual model through the prognostic variable of vibrational velocity vvib, which represents a particle velocity attributed to the acoustic vibrations. The pressure associated with the vibrations pvib is related to the vibrational velocity by: pvib=ρcpvvib, where ρ is the density and cp is the compressional wave velocity of the rock mass. The spatio‐temporal evolution of the vibrational velocity field in the Block model is controlled by three processes: generation, advection, and dissipation. Generation of vibrational velocity by the expanding shock wave is implemented by prescribing vvib as a fraction of the peak particle velocity up,max achieved during expansion of the impact‐generated stress wave.

where Cvib is a user‐defined constant, typically taken to be 0.1 (Wünnemann & Ivanov, 2003). This approach is designed to parameterize the unresolved natural processes by which vibrations develop behind the shock wave, such as scattering and mode conversion, as the wave interacts with small‐scale heterogeneities in elastic properties within the rock mass. Advection of vibrational velocity is accounted for by converting the vibrational velocity into an equivalent specific acoustic energy E=vvib2/2 and solving the conservative transport equations for this variable along with other conserved quantities, such as mass and momentum. Finally, the dissipation of vibrational energy is accounted for by applying a simple model of exponential decay, wherein each timestep of time Δt the vibrational velocity is reduced by a factor 1−Δt/Tdec, where Tdec is a user‐defined characteristic decay time constant.

The free parameters of the Block model are the effective viscosity of the fluidized rock mass ηeff,B and the decay time of the vibrations Tdec (Cvib is also a free parameter but in practice is seldom modified from a nominal value of 0.1). While these parameters are sometimes tuned to match crater observations for a specific impact scenario, they are often chosen based on empirical scaling relationships that relate the parameters to impactor size. Numerical Modeling

We now explore numerical solutions to Equation 6 when implemented in impact simulations. We simulated crater formation using the iSALE‐2D shock physics code (https://isale‐code.github.io/), a finite‐difference shock physics code based on the SALE hydrocode (Amsden et al., 1980). iSALE includes constitutive models, fragmentation, and strength models for dilatant materials (Collins et al., 2004, 2011; Ivanov et al., 1997; Melosh et al., 1992), as well as various equations of state (EoS) and porosity and compaction models (Collins et al., 2011; Wünnemann et al., 2006). These modifications made iSALE capable of simulating hypervelocity impacts on planetary surfaces. It also includes recent modifications to implement the Melosh model of acoustic fluidization as described in a companion paper (Hay et al., 2024).

We simulate three main scenarios to explore the effect of individual acoustic fluidization parameters and important parameter combinations, such as the decay time Tdec, regeneration parameter r, and scattering parameter γ. Because our work focuses on a parameter sensitivity investigation for the Melosh model, we keep impact conditions as simple as possible. Table 1 shows the impact conditions used for each simulation. All simulations had the same gravity (9.81 ms2) and vertical impact velocity (12 kms−1). The 7.2 km radius impactor at 12 kms−1 was previously used in simulations that produced impact craters comparable in size to the Chicxulub peak ring basin (Collins et al., 2008; Rae et al., 2019). We use these impact conditions and three impactor radii (7.2, 0.72, and 0.072 km) that span two orders of magnitude to model central peak and simple crater morphologies (e.g., Pierazzo et al., 2008). Table 1 also shows expected depths and diameters for each impact scenario according to Collins et al. (2005). Target and projectile material was modeled using strength parameters for granite (Collins et al., 2004), and ANEOS‐derived equation of state (Pierazzo et al., 2008) (Table S1 in Supporting Information S1).

The physical range of parameters in the Melosh model of acoustic fluidization is poorly constrained. The least constrained is scattering diffusivity (ξ). The value used in previous works (Collins & Melosh, 2003; Hay et al., 2014) is 103 m2s−1. In our companion paper (Hay et al., 2024), we match the scattering timescale (Tscatter) to the decay time of acoustic vibrations (Tdec) to demonstrate a scenario in which scattering has an observable effect, for which ξ = 3×104 m2s−1. Here, we consider a range between 10 and 105 m2s−1.

Among varied parameters, the best constraints are given for the dissipation quality factor as it is similar to the seismic quality factor (Melosh et al., 1992). The typical value for seismic Q in crystalline rocks in the upper crust is 50–600 (Anderson et al., 1977; Bradley & Fort, 1966; Waters & Waters, 1981). Although Melosh and Ivanov (1999) and Ivanov and Artemieva (2002) proposed Q = 10–200 for the Block model to produce the expected crater size, recent work that sought to constrain acoustic fluidization parameters using observations from the West Clearwater Lake structure showed that according to the scaling of the effective Q‐value required to replicate crater observations was an order to two orders of magnitude larger (Rae et al., 2017). They suggested that the overestimation of Q results from the Block model not considering acoustic energy regeneration (Ivanov, 2021) (see Hay et al., 2024). Here, we vary Q between 10 and 400 to remain within the suggested values of Melosh and Ivanov (1999) and values of seismic Q (Table 1).

Previous studies (Collins & Melosh, 2003; Hay et al., 2014; Melosh, 1979) suggest that a reasonable assumption for vibrational wavelength is that it should be much smaller than the crater diameter and much larger than the grain size of the fragmented rock mass beneath the crater. Wünnemann and Ivanov (2003) found a good match to the size‐morphology progression of craters using the Block model if the characteristic block size, which sets an analogous length scale to the vibrations, was proportional to impactor size. Ivanov (2019) However, the maximum fragment size is ∼a/10 (Ivanov, 2019; Wünnemann & Ivanov, 2003), and most fragments are considerably smaller than this (Wiggins et al., 2019). To account for both these ideas, we therefore consider wavelength λ to be a constant fraction of the impactor radius (a), between 0.01a to 0.5a (Table 1).

The regeneration parameter (e) governs acoustic energy generation by deformation. Consequently, this parameter is expected to control the localized dynamic weakening. The iSALE shock physics code separates the full stress tensor into an isotropic and a deviatoric part. So, in the current implementation of the Melosh model in iSALE (Hay et al., 2024), regeneration of acoustic energy is also decomposed into a volumetric ev and deviatoric es contribution (Equation 5, (Hay et al., 2024)). In Hay et al. (2024), we explored the trade‐off between ev and es (within sensible bounds). We found that while the Melosh model can replicate the behavior of the Block model almost exactly with just ev es=0, regeneration of acoustic energy during crater collapse is not accounted for if the shear regeneration term is neglected. Moreover, when shear regeneration is included, the volumetric term is redundant (Figure S1 in Supporting Information S1). So here, we focus our parameter investigation on the shear regeneration coefficient es and assume ev=0. In the following, we drop the subscript and assume e≡es. Previous studies (Collins & Melosh, 2003; Hay et al., 2024) suggested e = 0.1 to be effective in converting strain energy to acoustic energy, and here, we explore a wider range of values: e = 0.025–0.1 (Table 1).

Results

Melosh Model Parameter Exploration

This study explores the effect of the parameters in the Melosh model of acoustic fluidization on crater collapse and resulting morphology. We show the relationship between vibrational wavelength (λ), quality factor (Q), (ξ), and the shear regeneration factor (e). We define a set of parameters that produces crater sizes comparable to the Block model (Hay et al., 2024) and observed craters on Earth and the Moon.

Effects of the Quality Factor, Q

We first explore the effect of the quality factor Q, which defines the rate at which vibrations dissipate. Q is inversely proportional to the fraction of vibrational energy lost per cycle. Higher Q implies less energy is lost per cycle and hence less dissipation. Besides the decay time of the acoustic vibrations, Q also controls the threshold diameter for the onset of crater collapse and regeneration (r=eQ) and scattering (γ) parameters. This implies that as Q increases, so does the decay time of the vibrations and regeneration and scattering parameters. Here, we explore a range of Q between 10 and 400 (Table S2 in Supporting Information S1) with all other acoustic fluidization parameters kept constant (λ=0.2a; e=0.1; ξ=103 m2s−1). As the viscosity of strongly fluidized debris does not depend on Q, it is constant for simulations with constant impactor radius (e.g., for mid‐sized impact scenario, a = 0.72 km: ηeff=1×108 Pa s; Table S2 in Supporting Information S1).

Figure 2 compares the vibrational velocity field and subsurface deformation during transient crater formation and collapse for the mid‐size impact scenario (a = 0.72 km) with different Q. The increase in Q strongly affects the size of the fluidized region at the time of transient crater formation, the size of the regenerated fluidized region during crater collapse, and the style of subsurface deformation produced by transient crater collapse. However, the speed of deformation is relatively unchanged. In Figure 2a is a simulation with Q = 10 (Tdec = 0.5 s; r=1; γ=0.16). The strong vibrations (vvib≥20 m/s) are generated initially upon impact (t = 1.5 s). They decay during transient crater formation (t = 16 s) and regenerate at a lower velocity (vvib∼5 m/s) at the onset of transient crater collapse (t = 30–40 s). The edge of the regenerated fluidized region extends only just beyond the crater rim and the transient crater floor. These shallow vibrations move the crater floor upward during collapse (t = 70 s). However, directly beneath the crater center is a narrow zone where the fluidized region is shallow and fails to uplift the strata. The fluidized region shrinks rapidly in size, and after 100 s, it is confined to a small region around the central peak. By 200 s, the vibrations have completely dissipated, and the final crater has formed. The final crater has a complex central peak morphology with d/D = 0.04 (D = 20.6 km; d = 0.8 km).

Increasing the decay time of acoustic vibrations by increasing Q results in a larger fluidized zone, slower decay of the fluidized region, and more deep‐seated collapse and floor uplift. In Figure 2d, in a simulation where the decay of acoustic vibrations is ∼40× longer (Tdec = 21.04 s; Q = 400), the magnitude of the generated vibrational velocity and the fluidized area are larger (vvib>20 m/s) compared to the case with shorter decay time (Q = 10). The larger zone of stronger vibrations facilitates material flow at greater depth, forming a large central uplift that rises above the preimpact surface (t = 70 s). The fluidized zone extends well below the crater floor and beyond the crater rim even after the central uplift collapses outwards (t = 100 s). The crater in the simulation with the highest Q is larger (D = 33.1 km) and shallower (d = 0.2 km) than in the case of the shortest decay time. Moreover, in this case, the uplift of the strata is greatest directly beneath the crater center and extends far beneath the crater floor. The complex final crater morphology lacks an obvious central peak, which is replaced by a broad collapsed central uplift.

The importance of acoustic energy regeneration diminishes with increasing Q. In the case of smaller Q, vibrations are lower in magnitude, do not scatter far from where they are generated, and dissipate rapidly after excavation. However, vibrations are then regenerated and sustained during rim and floor collapse (t = 30–100 s) before quickly dissipating again after the collapse (t = 200 s). On the other hand, when Q is higher (Figures 2c and 2d), late‐stage regeneration of acoustic energy is less important due to the slow dissipation and wider scattering of acoustic energy generated during shock propagation and crater growth.

Increasing Q decreases the d/D with increasing crater diameter for all Earth and Moon impact scenarios. Effects of the Regeneration Factor, e

The regeneration factor e controls the rate of acoustic energy generation by deformation and appears in the regeneration parameter r as a product with Q. In Figure 4, we compare crater collapse in simulations of the mid‐sized impact scenario (a = 0.72 km) with different e and constant Q (Figures 4b–4d). The regeneration parameter r=eQ changes from 1.25 to 5 from B to D due to the varied e. We also compare simulations with different Q but constant r (Figures 4a and 4b). All other parameters are held constant. When Q = 50 (Tdec = 2.6 s), increasing e from 0.025 to 0.1 increases the size of the fluidized region and the strength of vibrations. The larger fluidized region results in relatively deeper seated and more dramatic floor uplift (t = 70 s) and produces shallower but larger craters. When e = 0.025, the crater is 35.7% (d = 0.7 km) deeper and 16% smaller (D = 21.3 km) than the crater in a simulation with e = 0.1 (d = 0.45 km; D = 25.2 km).

In columns (b–d) Q=50, but e increases respectively, e=0.025,0.05,0.1, corresponding to eQ=1.25,2.5,5. In all simulations λ=0.2a and ξ=103 m2s−1. The origin denotes the point of impact.

Despite the increase in the size of the fluidized region with increasing e, the constant decay time in these simulations implies that the fluidized region shrinks in size at the same rate, suppressing the uplift of strata beneath the crater center in all cases. Thus, the pattern of subsurface deformation beneath each simulated crater is very similar.

In Figures 4a and 4b, we compare simulations with the same r=eQ but different Tdec, as decay time is proportional to Q but independent of e. In column A is a simulation with 2× lower Tdec = 1.3 s than the simulation in column B. The evolution of the vibrational velocity field and the collapse style in these two simulations is very similar despite the difference in decay time. The size of the fluidized zone and magnitude of the vibrations are very similar at early times, and the rate at which the fluidized zone shrinks is comparable in both simulations, regardless of decay time. In Figure 4a, the more rapid dissipation of vibrational energy from the lower Q is compensated for by the more efficient conversion of distortional energy into new vibrations (e = 0.05). This supports the idea that the regeneration parameter, rather than the decay time, is the key governing parameter in crater collapse for the Melosh model. The final crater in the simulation with a shorter decay time has the same central peak morphology and the same d/D = 0.04 (D = 20.04 km, d = 0.8 km), as simulation with a longer decay time (D = 21.3 km, d = 0.7 km). This similarity in outcomes (e.g., d/D) between simulations with constant r is also seen for a range of r (Figure 5).

Effects of Vibrational Wavelength, λ

Next, we explore the effect of vibrational wavelength on crater collapse and morphology. Figure 6 shows time frames of crater collapse and vibrational velocity field for the mid‐size impact scenario (a = 0.72 km) and different values of λ. To isolate the effect of λ, we kept Q, eQ, and ξ constant (Q = 10, eQ = 1, ξ = 103 m/s2), and explored a range of λ as a fraction of impactor size: 0.01a–0.5a.

Time series showing the effect of vibrational wavelength on crater collapse and vibrational velocity field. The origin denotes the point of impact. From (a) to (d), vibrational wavelength increases from 0.01×a to 0.5×a. All other impact and acoustic fluidization parameters were constant (a = 0.72 km, Q = 10, e = 0.1, ξ = 103m2s−1).

Although eQ is constant, λ greatly influences the evolution of the vibrational energy field and the style of crater collapse. For a given impact scenario, increasing λ has two effects through its influence on decay time Tdec and viscosity ηeff. Higher λ reduces the dissipation of vibrational energy and increases the decay time, resulting in a larger fluidized region and stronger vibrations at a given time. At the same time, increasing λ raises the effective viscosity of the fluidized rock mass, which slows the flow speed and spreads deformation more continuously over the fluidized region. This results in more distributed, less localized deformation beneath the crater.

Differences in the magnitude of the initially generated vibrations and the size of the fluidized zone are observed from the earliest time frame (t = 1.5 s). In the case of λ = 0.01a (Figure 6a), strong vibrations are almost completely dissipated at t = 16 s while the transient crater is formed. Weaker vibrations are localized around the expanding crater walls. The regenerated vibrations, during wall collapse t = 30–70 s, are weak and localize along the crater wall and within the slumping material. In this case, the low‐magnitude vibrational field and rapid dissipation result in no uplift of the crater floor; instead, the rim wall collapses along a rotational slip surface into the crater and collides in the crater center to form a central mound. The low viscosity of the collapsing rock mass results in very fast, fluid motion, and the mound overshoots the preimpact surface level (t = 70 s). Coherent mass movement of the rim also results in a terrace‐like feature near the bottom of the crater wall (t = 100 s).

On the other hand, in simulations with larger λ (Figures 6b–6d), the fluidized region is larger, and generated vibrations are stronger in magnitude at the onset of crater floor collapse (t = 30 s). The fluidized zone extends deeper in the target for higher λ, resulting in deeper‐seated collapse, more uplift of crater floor collapse, and more distributed crater rim collapse. Increasing λ also slows the rise of the central uplift at t = 70 s owing to higher viscosity. In the case of λ = 0.1a (Figure 6b), vibrations are still present around the crater rim and in the central uplift during the collapse of the central uplift. In the simulation with λ = 0.5a (Figure 6d), all vibrations cease during central uplift formation, and no overshoot of the preimpact target occurs.

While the style of crater collapse and subsurface deformation are strongly affected, λ has a relatively small and nonlinear effect on crater depth and diameter (Figure 7). For λ = 0.01a, when the collapsing material from the rim behaves most fluidly, crater rim‐to‐floor depth is the largest (d = 1.65 km), and crater diameter is the smallest (D = 18.5 km). Increasing λ results in crater depths 1.1, 0.8, and 1.2 km, and crater diameters 20.8, 20.6, 20.8 km for λ = 0.1a, 0.2a, and 0.5a, respectively. The lack of trend in the changing depth of the craters with varied λ due to its proportionality with both ηeff and Tdec. So, for the first increase in λ, the effect of the longer Tdec produces a shallower crater, but as it increases, the effect of ηeff wins over Tdec, making the crater deeper. However, in the smallest impact scenario (a = 0.072 km) λ does not exhibit a substantial change in d/D or D. In the largest impact scenario (a = 7.2 km), on the other hand, the final crater is progressively shallower with decreasing λ, while the crater diameter is very similar.

Depth‐to‐diameter ratio as a function of crater diameter for simulations with different λ: solid line (λ = 0.5a), dashed line (λ = 0.2a), dotted line (λ = 0.1a), and dash‐dotted line (λ = 0.01a). All other acoustic fluidization parameters were constant (Q = 10; ξ = 103 m/s2, es = 0.1). The measurements are included in Table S4 in Supporting Information S1.

Our results show that a shorter vibrational wavelength results in a smaller, shallower, and more transient zone of weakening, while a longer wavelength contributes to stronger vibrations and a longer‐lived and deeper zone of weakening (Figure 6). However, in the simulations just described, ηeff and Tdec varied in proportion with λ. To isolate the effect of ηeff on crater collapse, we compare two simulations of the mid‐size impact scenario (a = 0.72 km) with different λ (left: 0.5a and right: 0.01a), but we adjust Q to maintain the same Tdec and e to also maintain the same regeneration parameter r=eQ (Figure 8). Although the decay time and the regeneration parameter r are constant between these two simulations, the magnitude of the vibrational velocity field, the size of the fluidized region, and the rate at which this region shrinks in size are very different. At t = 2 s (Figure 8a) the vibrations in the case with smaller ηeff = 5.2×106 Pa.s (right) are half the magnitude of the simulations with 50× larger ηeff. During transient crater formation (t = 6 s; Figure 8b), higher magnitude vibrations (left) open the transient cavity faster, but the short‐wavelength vibrations show the first signs of localization and fault generation. At the onset of the transient crater collapse (Figure 8c), the fluidized zone extends to ∼5 km depth and ∼8 km radial distance in the high viscosity scenario. In the low effective viscosity scenario, the fluidized zone is highly localized and does not extend beyond the transient crater. The dramatically different vibrational velocity fields in each case suggest that the generation of vibrational energy is not independent of λ despite its absence from the regeneration parameter. This is likely because lowering λ reduces the viscosity of the fluidized rock mass and, hence, the driving stress required for crater collapse. The lower driving stress, in turn, reduces the distortional energy available to be converted into new vibrational energy. Thus, λ is an important independent parameter governing crater formation in the Melosh model.

Time series showing the effect of vibrational wavelength on crater collapse and vibrational velocity field. The origin denotes the point of impact. Left from the origin is a simulation with λ = 0.5a (ηeff = 2.6×108 Pa.s), and right of the origin λ = 0.01a (ηeff = 5.2×106 Pa.s). Simulations have the same eQ = 1, and the same decay time Tdec = 1.3 s.

The differences in the generation of vibrational energy between the simulations with different effective viscosities result in contrasting styles of crater collapse. In the high viscosity scenario (left), the crater floor uplifts from a greater depth (t = 50 s), and due to the larger fluidized zone, uplift is limited (t = 70 s; Figure 8e), and vibrations completely decay by t = 90 s (Figure 8f). In this case, deformation beneath the crater is distributed. Inside the rim is a wide region of subsidence, which surrounds a broad central uplift, apart from a narrow region at the crater center where uplift is suppressed. This contrasts with the collapse style of the low‐viscosity scenario, which exhibits several interesting features. The limited regeneration of vibrations at t = 30 s generates localized deformation‐faulting around the crater rim. During transient crater collapse (t = 50 s (Figure 8d)), the material from the crater floor remains in place, and material from the crater rim slumps along the transient crater walls back into the crater cavity. Low‐magnitude vibrations are regenerated within the collapsing material, and additional faults form as the ejecta blanket material is emplaced outside the rim. With low effective viscosity, rim and wall material that collapses into the crater cavity behaves very fluidly and collides in the crater center, forming a central mound that overshoots the pre‐impact surface. Ninety seconds after impact (Figure 8f), the central mound material collapses into the cavity. At the same time, more material slumps from the crater rim and regenerates additional low‐magnitude vibrations along the slip surface. Subsequently, the collapsing central mound material over‐thrusts the slumped rim material (t = 115 s (Figure 8g)). While this collapse style produces rim‐faulting, slumped rim walls and a central topographic high, as expected for a complex crater of this size (Kenkmann et al., 2014), directly beneath the crater floor, deep strata are displaced downwards, rather than upward as is commonly observed at terrestrial complex craters. When λ = 0.5a all vibrations cease and form final crater after ∼100 s (d = 1.3 km, D = 20 km). Short wavelength vibrations take longer to cease and for highly fluidized material to stop moving (t ∼ 200 s (Figure 8h)), forming smaller but deeper final crater (d = 1.5 km, D = 18 km).

Effects of Scattering Diffusivity, ξ

Lastly, we explore the effect of the scattering diffusivity, ξ, which determines the rate at which acoustic energy diffuses from areas of high acoustic energy to areas of lower acoustic energy (Equation 6). In Figure 9, we show the vibrational velocity field for three simulations where scattering diffusivity is ξ = 10 m2s−1 (Figure 9a), two orders of magnitude larger (Figure 9b) and four orders of magnitude larger ξ = 105 m2s−1 (Figure 9c). All the other acoustic fluidization parameters were constant (λ=0.2a, a=0.72 km, Q=10, and e=0.1). This spans a four‐order‐of‐magnitude variation in the scattering parameter γ and a two‐order‐of‐magnitude variation in the characteristic scattering length (l∗).

Time series showing the effect of scattering term. The origin denotes the point of impact. (a) ξ = 10 m2s−1, (b) ξ = 103 m2s−1, (c) ξ = 105 m2s−1. In all simulations λ = 0.2a, Q = 10, e = 0.1, and a = 0.72 km.

When ξ is low (Figure 9a), acoustic energy travels shorter distances before it decays, resulting in a more localized acoustic energy field generated at 1.5 s after impact. As expected, a higher ξ results in more diffusion of acoustic energy and hence less localization. During transient crater collapse (t = 40 s), vibrations are regenerated, and they are again more localized in the case of lower ξ compared to high ξ. During transient crater floor collapse (t = 70 s), regenerated vibrations traveling larger distances for larger ξ cause floor uplift to rise to 1 km height above the pre‐impact level, compared to smaller ξ where uplift rises to 2 km and above. This affects the speed at which material collapses back into the crater, where at t = 100 s all material has collapsed and forms a central peak for ξ = 105m/s2, while for smaller ξ collapse is still continuing at t = 100 s.

However, these differences in collapse style do not affect the final crater depth or diameter for the range of ξ values explored here. The final crater diameter for the highest scattering diffusivity was 20.9 km, which is only 1.8% larger than the crater from the simulation with a ξ‐value four orders of magnitude lower. The d/D was independent of ξ (d/D=0.04).

Summary of the Effects of the Melosh Model Parameters

So far, results presented here suggest that crater formation as facilitated by the Melosh model of acoustic fluidization is most sensitive to the rate of dissipation of acoustic energy (∝Q), the viscosity of the target (∝λ), and the amount of energy (re)generated from the shear (∝e). Different combinations of these three parameters can produce final craters with the expected depth‐diameter ratios for craters of appropriate size. Additionally, different parameters showed distinct effects on the morphology and subsurface structure of the craters formed by various impactor sizes.

We explored each parameter and showed that the quality factor Q strongly affects crater morphology. It affects both the volume of material fluidized by acoustic vibrations and the duration of the fluidized region. However, a degeneracy exists between Q and the regeneration factor e; approximately the same outcome occurs if the regeneration parameter r=eQ is the same. This is likely because a higher rate of acoustic energy dissipation can be compensated for by a more efficient conversion of distortional energy into new vibrations. This implies that a more realistic Q that agrees with a lower bound of seismic Q can be applied when less energy is regenerated from shear deformation. But, so far, Q and e cannot be determined uniquely.

Our exploration also suggests that the vibrational wavelength λ is an important governing parameter. Small λ (∼0.01a) produces a shallow zone of short‐lived, low‐magnitude vibrations and promotes localized deformation because vibrations are rapidly dissipated and do not scatter far from where they are generated. This results in fault development around the crater rim and rotational slumping of the rim during crater collapse. However, shorter vibrational wavelengths also suppress the uplift of the crater floor. When λ is longer (∼0.5a), the fluidized zone is larger, with stronger vibrations, and shrinks in size more gradually. This promotes more deep‐seated and distributed deformation, with broad floor uplift and rim subsidence, while localized deformation is suppressed. While a detailed comparison between subsurface deformation predicted by the simulations and that observed beneath natural craters is beyond the scope of this work, our simulations suggest that a fixed λ is probably an oversimplification. In reality, λ probably varies with depth due to the change in the fragmented material size (Wiggins et al., 2019).

To further test the ability of the Melosh model to replicate the observed and expected trends of crater depth‐diameter ratio with increasing crater diameter, we simulated additional impact scenarios using a set of Melosh model parameters that produced final craters with depth‐diameter ratios closest to the expected values. We adopt a regeneration parameter r of 1.25, with Q = 50 and es = 0.025 so that Q is approximately consistent with typical seismic Q and an intermediate vibrational wavelength (λ = 0.2a). Because scattering diffusivity does not affect final crater morphology, we adopt a nominal value of ξ = 103 m2s−1. For craters on Earth (D = 1–132 km), we use the same material models (Table S1 in Supporting Information S1) and impact velocity (12 km s−1) as our previous simulations, and consider impactor radii between 36 m, and 3.6 km. For simulations where the impactor radius was ≥0.72 km we used a crustal thickness of 30 km, and a dunite equation of state for mantle (Benz et al., 1989). These simulations also included a thermal gradient of 10 K/km and conductive lid thickness of 100 km (e.g., Rae et al., 2019).

We measure the final crater diameter and depth in each simulation and compare it to the expected depth‐to‐diameter trends of simple, transitional, central peak, and peak ring craters. Our results suggest that flat‐floored, shallow craters, with insufficient central uplift to be classified as complex craters, occur at impactor radii 110–180 m and crater diameters 3.2–5.6 km. Below this threshold diameter are craters with d/D>0.2 (simple), and above are craters with d/D≤0.05 (complex). In Figure 10, we show that this agrees with observations on Earth, where the expected simple crater threshold is at ∼ 3.2 km (Wünnemann & Ivanov, 2003). An inferred transition to peak‐ring craters in our models is defined when d/D≤0.03. This occurs at impactor sizes 0.72–0.9 km and crater diameters 21–26 km. However, we reiterate that our simulations of craters of this size do not produce a pronounced topographic peak ring, and the final crater morphology, in this case, is flat‐floored.

On the Moon, we model the target and impactor as in previous works (Johnson et al., 2018) using a 15 km s−1 impactor speed and impactor radii between 0.25 km, and 25 km. Discussion

We performed a parameter sensitivity study for the Melosh model of acoustic fluidization. We showed the effect of each of the governing parameters of the Melosh model—λ, Q, ξ, and e on the size of the (re)generated fluidized region, duration and magnitude of fluidization, and deformation localization. These different effects produce a variety of collapse styles in the complex craters. We applied explored parameters to a range of impact conditions and measured the depths and diameters of the resulting craters. According to the decreasing depth‐to‐diameter ratio (d/D) with increasing crater diameter, we classify craters as simple (d/D∼ 0.2–0.3), central peak (d/D∼ 0.04–0.1) and peak ring (d/D≤0.04). We determined parameters that reproduce the observed depth‐to‐diameter progression for Earth and Moon craters.

Our work shows major improvements in understanding the importance of nonlinear decay of acoustic vibrations for generating the subsurface deformations observed in the complex craters. However, currently, there are two major limitations in the presented model. First, while short λ contributes to the fault growth, it does not initiate the uplift of the strata beneath the crater center, and transient crater collapse is marked simply by the rim material slumping back to the crater cavity. On the other hand, longer λ initiates the uplift of the deeper strata during crater collapse, but it does not contribute to the fault growth. This suggests that enabling λ to change with depth in the target could produce a more realistic subsurface morphology of the complex craters. For example, a shorter λ would be favorable in the near‐surface regions to generate faults, but it would increase with increasing depth to contribute to the floor uplift.

Additionally, Riller et al. (2018) analyzed Chicxulub drill cores and suggested that the dominant vibrational wavelength evolves by progressive lengthening during cratering, meaning that the effective λ increases from a few meters at the end of the excavation stage to a few hundred meters by the end of the peak‐ring formation. So, if λ was allowed to vary temporally in our models, the shorter wavelength (i.e., λ = 0.01a) would allow rim‐faulting during the early cratering stages, as shown in Figure 8b. The vibrations become regenerated during transient crater collapse. At this time, λ would become longer (i.e., λ = 0.5a; Figure 8c), allowing for deeper target weakening and floor uplift. Hence, with a better understanding of acoustic fluidization and how rocks are fractured during an impact (e.g., Wiggins et al., 2019), allowing the acoustic wavelength to vary in time and space, we may produce even more realistic impact simulations.

A direct comparison of the Block and Melosh models (Hay et al., 2024) demonstrates the significance of acoustic energy regeneration in the latter. In the Block model, the vibrations decay slowly and remain until the final crater is formed (Figure 1). However, the vibrations in the Melosh model initially decay rapidly after transient crater formation. The vibrations are regenerated during the crater floor collapse (e.g., Figure 2). So, faster decay of the acoustic vibrations in the Melosh model is accommodated with the regeneration of acoustic vibrations from shear deformation. Depending on the impact size, the regeneration not only appears during the transient crater collapse but can appear in the later stages of the cratering, as well (i.e., peak ring formation: t = 150–300 s, Figure 9 in Hay et al. (2024)). The percentage of acoustic energy (re)generated in regions of large stresses and strains is managed by e. The results show that the overfluidization of the target is avoided due to the trade‐off between e and Q. The more acoustic vibrations there are (large regeneration term, e), the more rapidly these vibrations have to be dissipated to heat (lower Q), and vice‐versa. This trade‐off was also previously shown in the study that explored the effects of acoustic regeneration in long runout landslides (Collins & Melosh, 2003).

An important outcome of the eQ trade‐off is that it allows using more realistic Q values than those in the Block model. For example, by constraining the Block model parameters from the observations at the West Clearwater Lake impact structure and using scaling from Ivanov and Artemieva (2002) and Rae et al. (2017) found that Q is overestimated by an order to two orders of magnitude in the Block model. They argued that due to the lack of scattering and regeneration of acoustic energy, vibrations need to last longer (larger Q) to fluidize the target for long enough to facilitate crater collapse. Ivanov (2021) and Rae et al. (2017) (Bradley & Fort, 1966; Waters & Waters, 1981).

Additionally, it is widely observed that seismic Q shows a dramatic increase with depth (e.g., Abercrombie, 1997; Abercrombie et al., 2021; Hough et al., 1988). Considering that the major limitation of our work, at the moment, is that our simulations do not produce clear and obvious topographic peak rings, it is reasonable to assume that future models would require Q to vary with depth on top of spatially and temporally varied λ for more realistic peak ring morphology.

While none of our results (so far) can replicate all structural elements of a typical complex crater, as described above, there is clear potential for future refinement by spatially/temporally varying λ and Q. The current incorporation of the Melosh model into a shock physics code enables localization of deformation that has not been possible in the Block model (e.g., Ivanov, 2021; Senft & Stewart, 2009). Our work shows that the Melosh model can produce a wider array of deformation styles because of the nonlinear feedback of the regeneration term, which is a major development over the simplified Block model. The importance of deformation styles shown here was emphasized in the field observations of complex craters (Ivanov, 2021; Kenkmann et al., 2014; Senft & Stewart, 2009). In addition, numerical models of larger impact basins (peak ring, multiring) have shown that the location and spacing of basin rings are controlled by impactor size and pre‐impact thermal structure (Johnson, Blair, et al., 2016). None of those previous works considered strain localization due to the regeneration of acoustic vibrations. Further exploration of Melosh model parameters could strongly impact hitherto applied impact and pre‐impact conditions when modeling large basins (Rajšić & Johnson, 2024).

Conclusions

The Melosh model of acoustic fluidization offers an elegant explanation for the permanent deformation of rocks under low differential stresses in long‐runout landslides, seismic faulting, and crater collapse. In this study, we applied the Melosh model to crater formation and investigated the effect of varying the Melosh model parameters on the nature of crater collapse. We explored a range of values for vibrational wavelength (λ), quality factor (Q), (shear) regeneration factor (e), and scattering diffusivity (ξ). We showed that Melosh model parameters influence the size of the fluidized region (λ, Q), duration of the fluidization (λ, Q), collapse style (λ, eQ), localization of deformation (λ, ξ, eQ), viscosity of fluidized region (λ), and final depth‐to‐diameter ratio of craters (eQ).

Our simulations show that a shorter vibrational wavelength (λ<0.2a) causes shallow weakening and fluid‐like behavior of collapsing material that results in slumping and faulting around the crater rim. The shallow vibrations observed in the Melosh model have a laterally wider distribution that causes clear deformation localization around the crater rim, which is not observed in the Block model. However, when λ is too short, deeper strata beneath the crater center do not uplift. A longer vibrational wavelength (λ>0.2a) causes deeper target weakening and contributes to the uplift of material beneath the crater floor. While this work showed that the λ = 0.2a reproduces overall depth‐to‐diameter trends on the Moon and Earth, it is necessary to spatially and temporally vary λ to produce more realistic subsurface deformation in complex craters morphology.

Varying Q ∝Tdec alone or in combination with e strongly affected the final depth‐to‐diameter trends. When the eQ ratio was larger than 1.25, all craters, regardless of impact condition, had d/D≤0.1, which we consider diagnostic of complex craters. So, Q between 10 and 100 and e between 0.1 and 0.025 produced depth‐to‐diameter trends comparable to those created by the block model in Earth‐like and Moon‐like conditions. The values of Q that reproduced d/D trends agree with the lower bounds of seismic Q. However, considering that we did not see distinct peak ring morphology forming in simulations, it is sensible to assume that Q most likely relates to the impactor radius and that larger impactors that excavate larger basins require increased Q at depth. The scattering term (ξ) controls how fast the vibrations diffuse in the regions of large stresses and strains but does not affect d/D trends.

While a single set of Melosh model parameters is not able to replicate all aspects of crater formation, it can replicate the crater depth‐to‐diameter trends observed on rocky surfaces. Moreover, our work shows that the Melosh model accommodates a richer diversity of deformation styles than the more simplistic Block Model. Further exploration of the relationship between regenerated and scattered vibrations and spatially/temporarily varied λ and Q will contribute to reproducing detailed rim faulting and ductile central uplift in complex craters.
