Head trauma complicated with multi-site infections of Ureaplasma urealyticum and Mycoplasma hominis: a case report
Authors: Xuchun Bai, Yuchao Sang, Longyan Ke, Minyue Chen, Nanhong Su, Shaoxi Li, Xiaofeng Hong, Shumin Xie, Xinjian Lin, Xintan Chen
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundMycoplasma hominis and Ureaplasma urealyticum are genitourinary colonizers that can cause extragenital infection after trauma or surgery, yet diagnosis is frequently delayed by negative routine cultures.Case presentationA 60-year-old man developed persistent postoperative infection after decompressive craniotomy for head trauma. Routine cultures were non-informative. Guided by a stepwise protocol (targeted sampling of blood/cerebrospinal fluid (CSF)/wound and urethral reservoir), we detected M. hominis by matrix-assisted laser desorption/ionization time-of-flight mass spectrometry (MALDI-TOF MS)/culture and confirmed disseminated U. urealyticum by species-specific qPCR (multiple sites except CSF). Targeted therapy (doxycycline with levofloxacin) led to clinical resolution.ConclusionsIn ICU trauma or postoperative patients with culture-negative infection despite β-lactams, targeted testing for mollicutes, combining mycoplasma and ureaplasma culture, MALDI-TOF MS, and species-specific qPCR, can expedite diagnosis and guide effective therapy.Clinical trial numberNot applicable.Keywords: Case report, Postoperative infection, Mycoplasma hominis, Ureaplasma urealyticum

Introduction

In the realm of clinical medicine, patients with multiple traumas and head injuries constitute a demographic highly susceptible to nosocomial infections, which notably emerge as the principal cause of mortality beyond the initial three weeks post-admission [1]. Among the plethora of pathogens implicated in such infections, Mycoplasma hominis and Ureaplasma urealyticum, typically inhabitants of the human genital tract, are noteworthy [2]. While these mycoplasmas primarily colonize the genitourinary mucosa and are commonly associated with urinary tract infections [3] and adverse pregnancy outcomes [4], they can also cause extragenital infections, particularly when mucosal barriers are breached or host defenses are compromised. These include sepsis, central nervous system infections, respiratory infections, joint infections, and wound infections, often precipitated by mucosal injuries due to mechanical operations, surgeries, or trauma, alongside immune dysfunction [5]. The challenge in culturing and identifying Mycoplasma hominis and Ureaplasma urealyticum poses a significant diagnostic and therapeutic hurdle [6]. Because mycoplasma surgical-site infections are uncommon, they are not usually covered by empiric therapy. However, in selected post-traumatic or postoperative settings, particularly when infection persists despite standard coverage and cultures remain negative, targeted testing (e.g., PCR) for Mycoplasma hominis and Ureaplasma urealyticum should be considered to avoid delays in diagnosis and definitive treatment, which can prolong hospitalization and increase costs. This case report aims to delineate the stepwise diagnostic reasoning that uncovered disseminated Mycoplasma hominis and Ureaplasma urealyticum infection after head trauma and surgery, and proposes a practical, testable protocol for timely recognition and targeted management in similar ICU scenarios. The described patient developed multisite infection with Mycoplasma hominis and Ureaplasma urealyticum following head trauma, underscoring the need for heightened clinical vigilance and prompt, targeted diagnostics rather than routine empiric coverage.

Case report

A 60-year-old male patient was urgently admitted to Anxi County Hospital on Day 0, following an hour of unconsciousness due to a traumatic incident. Upon admission, his vital signs were recorded: body temperature at 36.6 ℃, heart rate at 70 beats per minute, respiratory rate at 20 breaths per minute, and blood pressure at 145/66 mm Hg. The initial examination revealed the patient’s state of clouded consciousness, with both pupils being large, round, and measuring 2.5 mm in diameter, exhibiting a sluggish response to light. Physical examination also noted a soft neck, elevated muscle tone in the limbs, active tendon reflexes, absence of pathological reflexes, and no signs of meningeal irritation. An urgent head CT scan was conducted, unveiling multiple contusions within the frontotemporal lobes, a subarachnoid hemorrhage, dilated bilateral temporo-occipital sutures, and a subcutaneous hematoma localized in the left temporo-occipital region (Fig. 1).

The patient underwent emergency surgery on Day 0, which included a bilateral fronto-temporal craniotomy for decompression, removal of bilateral frontotemporal intracerebral hematomas, and placement of a cranial pressure monitoring probe (Fig. 2). Following surgery, the patient remained under pharmacological sedation and was admitted to the intensive care unit for continuous care. The postoperative regimen encompassed multimodal sedation, therapies for edema and hemorrhage control, and antibiotic administration (ceftriaxone 2 g daily). Additionally, meticulous attention was given to correcting any water-electrolyte and acid-base imbalances.

Postoperative cranial computed tomography. Lesions are highlighted with circled ROI contours

A lung CT scan on Day 7 showed infectious inflammation in the patient’s lower left lung. Sputum culture identified Pseudomonas aeruginosa and Klebsiella pneumoniae. Consequently, ceftriaxone was discontinued, and the patient was treated with cefoperazone-sulbactam sodium (3 g every 8 h) for the infection. Despite these antibiotics, the patient’s inflammatory markers continued to rise, indicating that the infection was not adequately controlled (Fig. 3). On Day 11, cerebrospinal fluid cultured on blood agar (sample collected on Day 7) showed slow growth, with pinhead-sized colonies appearing after three days (Fig. 4A). These colonies were identified as Mycoplasma hominis (MH) using matrix-assisted laser desorption/ionization time-of-flight mass spectrometry (MALDI-TOF MS) (Fig. 4B), yielding a score of 9.433. Based on our laboratory thresholds (species-level 9.0–<9.5; possible subspecies 9.5–10.0; genus-level 6.0–<9.0; unreliable < 6.0), this value supports species-level identification. Mycoplasma hominis was then cultured on specific mycoplasma media, and after 48 h, unique “fried-egg” shaped colonies of Mycoplasma hominis were observed on the specific solid culture medium (Fig. 4C). Antibiotic susceptibility testing in liquid mycoplasma medium showed sensitivity to doxycycline, minocycline, josamycin, sparfloxacin, and spectinomycin, moderate resistance to ciprofloxacin and levofloxacin, and resistance to azithromycin, roxithromycin, clarithromycin, norfloxacin, and ofloxacin (Fig. 4D). Given the elevated white blood cell count and the possibility of intracranial infection, the patient was treated with doxycycline (0.1 g/12 h) for the infection.

Also on Day 11, the patient underwent another debridement and suturing due to insufficient healing of the head wound. At the same time, secretions from the patient’s head wound, urethral secretions, and bilateral blood samples were collected for general bacterial and mycoplasma culture, as shown in Fig. 5. The aerobic blood culture, processed on the BacT/ALERT 3D microbial detection system (bioMérieux, France), remained below the positivity threshold with a flat growth curve over 3.74 days and no instrument alarm (Fig. 5A). Given the known fastidiousness of Mollicutes in standard blood-culture systems, we performed blind subculture at 48 h from both aerobic bottles: 50 µL of bottle broth was inoculated onto routine blood agar and onto a selective mycoplasma solid medium provided in a commercial culture-based kit for Ureaplasma urealyticum and Mycoplasma hominis (Zhong Aisheng Hebei Biotechnology Co., Ltd., China), followed by incubation at 35–37 °C in 5% CO₂. Gram staining of bottle broth showed “no bacteria seen”, consistent with the wall-less nature of Mollicutes, whereas Giemsa staining revealed purple “fine sand-like” particles (Fig. 5B). On the selective mycoplasma solid medium, colonies exhibited morphology typical of Mollicutes: “fried-egg” shaped colonies indicative of Mycoplasma hominis and “sea urchin-like” colonies indicative of Ureaplasma urealyticum (UU) (Fig. 5C). Figure 5D shows positive results for mycoplasma testing in bilateral blood (MH), head wound secretions (UU, MH), and urethral secretions (UU, MH), along with corresponding antibiotic sensitivity results (yellow indicates sensitivity, red indicates resistance). Subsequent species-specific real-time PCR detected Ureaplasma urealyticum (UU) in all sampled sites except cerebrospinal fluid (Fig. 5E), supporting disseminated involvement and informing targeted management. For UU detection, we used a primer-only real-time PCR assay with forward primer 5′-GGCGGGTTTGTAAGTTTGGT-3′ and reverse primer 5′-CCCATTCAGGCCGACATTTA-3′. PCR thermocycling comprised an initial denaturation at 95 °C for 5 min, 35 cycles of 95 °C for 30 s, 60 °C for 30 s, and 72 °C for 30 s, followed by a final extension at 72 °C for 5 min and a 4 °C hold. This assay targets a UU-specific genomic region with published analytical specificity that discriminates U. urealyticum from U. parvum and other bacteria [7–9]. Due to low concentrations of doxycycline in the cerebrospinal fluid and ongoing lung infection, combined treatment with levofloxacin (0.75 g/d) was administered for the infection.

On Day 24, the wound had healed, and the relevant inflammatory markers were essentially normal (Fig. 3). However, there were still signs of infection in the lungs, so doxycycline was discontinued and replaced with piperacillin-tazobactam (4.5 g every 12 h). By Day 27, the lung inflammation was better controlled, and levofloxacin was discontinued. On Day 33, the surgical incisions at the site of the stored cranial bone flaps (bilateral frontal bones) in the upper right thigh had healed, but there was an accumulation of bloody purulent fluid that required debridement and negative pressure drainage (Fig. 6A). Microbiological examination of the extracted pus revealed the presence of Ureaplasma urealyticum (Figs. 5E and 6B). As a result, doxycycline treatment was continued (0.1 g every 12 h) to combat the mycoplasma infection. By Day 56, the patient’s neurological symptoms had significantly improved, with clear consciousness and a fair mental state. The drainage fluid had markedly decreased and the lungs were largely normal. The patient and his family requested to be discharged to return home (out of province) for the Chinese Lunar New Year. It was recommended that the patient continue negative pressure drainage at a local hospital, regularly change wound dressings, and continue oral doxycycline until the drainage ceased. The drainage tube was to be removed 3–4 days after no more drainage fluid was present, and the patient was scheduled to return to the hospital for cranial reconstruction surgery two months later.

Discussion

Mycoplasmas belong to the class Mollicutes, part of the Mycoplasmataceae family, known for their slow growth, lack of cell walls, small genomes, and limited metabolic capacities [10]. This unique structure confers resistance to β-lactam antibiotics [11] and presents challenges for cultivation due to specific nutritional requirements [12]. Although primarily colonizing the respiratory and urogenital tracts, mycoplasmas can become pathogenic under conditions that disrupt host mucosa or tissues, such as immunosuppression or surgical interventions [13]. Isolation of M. hominis or U. urealyticum from blood, sterile body fluids, or deep tissue should prompt strong suspicion for invasive disease; correlation with clinical findings and, where feasible, confirmation by species-specific PCR or repeat culture are recommended to exclude contamination and guide targeted therapy. Mycoplasmas lack a cell wall and therefore do not retain the Gram stain; they require enriched, sterol-containing media for culture, and their metabolic requirements are species-specific—M. hominis primarily catabolizes arginine, Ureaplasma spp. depend on urea hydrolysis, and several mycoplasmas (e.g., M. pneumoniae) preferentially metabolize glucose [14]. Although blood cultures rarely detect genital mycoplasmas using traditional laboratory methods, we have adjusted our blood culture detection procedures based on our recent experience with bloodstream infections. Our updated protocol routinely includes screening for mycoplasmas, particularly those originating from sterile sites [15]. Mycoplasma hominis can grow slowly growth on blood agar forming small translucent colonies [16]. These colonies can then be accurately identified using MALDI-TOF MS [17, 18], which is the standard in modern clinical microbiology. In contrast, Ureaplasma urealyticum does not grow on blood agar and is challenging to detect even in specialized mycoplasma media, often being overlooked in routine blood cultures. This oversight likely explains why bloodstream infections reported in literature frequently involve Mycoplasma hominis but seldom Ureaplasma urealyticum [15]. Multiplex PCR systems have been described and are effective for detecting genital mycoplasmas in clinical samples, providing better results than culture methods [19]. Real-time fluorescence PCR is now widely used for qualitative and quantitative detection of mycoplasmas [7, 20]. The anticoagulants in most blood culture media, like sodium polyanethol sulfonate, inhibit the growth of mycoplasmas. Additionally, except for Mycoplasma pneumoniae, most mycoplasmas do not utilize glucose and rely on the breakdown of urea, arginine, and cysteine to produce CO2, which is often insufficient for detection by instruments. However, in this reported case, without any blood culture alarms, we opted for conventional bacterial and specialized mycoplasma media, coupled with MALDI-TOF MS and real-time fluorescence PCR, successfully detecting Mycoplasma hominis and Ureaplasma urealyticum from aerobic blood cultures. Therefore, in selecting methods for pathogen detection, it is crucial to cover rare, uncommon, and atypical pathogens, enhancing the diagnostic rate of blood cultures and guiding precise clinical treatment effectively.

In recent years, factors such as antibiotic misuse, non-standard drug use, and repeated infections have contributed to increasing drug resistance in Ureaplasma urealyticum and Mycoplasma hominis, complicating standardized clinical treatments [21]. Mycoplasmas lack a cell wall, making them inherently resistant to antibiotics that target cell walls, like β-lactams and glycopeptides. However, tetracyclines, which disrupt protein synthesis, are commonly utilized to treat mycoplasma infections. Mycoplasmas also respond to quinolones, which inhibit DNA replication. Fluoroquinolones, tetracyclines, and clindamycin are known to exhibit effective in vitro activity against Mycoplasma hominis and have moderate permeability through the blood-brain barrier [22]. Despite the complexity of this case, targeted therapy with doxycycline combined with levofloxacin was initiated immediately upon identification of the pathogen and determination of antibiotic susceptibility. Two weeks later, the patient’s body temperature, wound condition, cerebrospinal fluid, and inflammation markers all returned to normal, demonstrating significant treatment efficacy. Therefore, timely and accurate etiological diagnosis, along with the implementation of effective and targeted anti-mycoplasma therapy, can significantly improve patient outcomes.

Reports of disseminated mollicute infection increasingly implicate postoperative and post-traumatic settings, with Mycoplasma hominis causing central nervous system and wound infections particularly after head injury or neurosurgery, and Ureaplasma spp. described in hematogenous, device-associated, and occasional CNS disease [23–25]. Across these series, common features include recent genitourinary manipulation or catheterization, failure of β-lactam therapy, negative routine Gram stain and culture, slow or missed growth on standard media, and subsequent identification by mycoplasma/ureaplasma culture or species-specific PCR. Three routes are typically considered for intracranial involvement—direct contamination at trauma, intraoperative introduction, or bacteremic seeding from a genitourinary reservoir—and the latter is often favored when urethral colonization coexists with sterile-site recovery and there is perioperative instrumentation [26, 27]. Given the concurrent detection of M. hominis and U. urealyticum in urethral secretions and in sterile-site specimens, the absence of urinary symptoms aside from microscopic hematuria consistent with colonization, and the timing around urinary catheterization and prolonged surgery, the most probable route was autoinoculation from a genitourinary reservoir with transient bacteremia and secondary seeding of the scalp wound and other sites. This interpretation is consistent with prior reports and supports a practical approach for similar cases. In ICU trauma or postoperative patients, consider mollicute infection when fever or localized sepsis persists despite broad β-lactam therapy, routine Gram stains and blood or wound cultures are repeatedly negative, there has been urinary catheterization or other genitourinary manipulation, or the laboratory reports atypical colony morphology or no growth despite rising inflammatory markers. We also acknowledge the possibility of exogenous transmission related to hand hygiene or device handling, particularly because Mollicutes are fastidious and may be missed by routine culture, introducing diagnostic delays during which subclinical spread can occur; accordingly, we highlight the importance of strict hand hygiene, preservation of closed-system integrity (e.g., maintaining closed suction and minimizing circuit breaks), and aseptic technique for specimen handling. Finally, retrograde contamination via drainage systems is plausible in the setting of disrupted one-way flow or compromised negative-pressure systems; prevention focuses on early removal of unnecessary drains, routine verification of vacuum integrity and one-way valves, minimizing line manipulations, and prompt replacement of any compromised tubing. Therapeutically, once mollicutes are suspected, avoid β-lactams and use tetracyclines or fluoroquinolones guided by local resistance patterns and isolate susceptibilities, consider combination therapy for central nervous system involvement, and ensure source control.

Conclusion

In ICU trauma or postoperative patients with persistent, culture-negative infection despite β-lactams, suspicion for mollicutes should prompt targeted sampling plus species-specific testing (mycoplasma and ureaplasma culture, MALDI-TOF MS, and qPCR) to guide effective non-β-lactam therapy.
