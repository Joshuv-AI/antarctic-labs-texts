Mapping surface soil organic carbon density of cultivated land using machine learning in Zhengzhou
Authors: Hengliang Guo, Jinyang Wang, Dujuan Zhang, Jian Cui, Yonghao Yuan, Haoming Bao, Mengjiao Yang, Jiahui Guo, Feng Chen, Wenge Zhou, Gang Wu, Yang Guo, Haitao Wei, Baojin Qiao, Shan Zhao
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
Research on soil organic carbon (SOC) is crucial for improving soil carbon sinks and achieving the "double-carbon" goal. This study introduces ten auxiliary variables based on the data from a 2021 land quality survey in Zhengzhou and a multi-objective regional geochemical survey. It uses geostatistical ordinary kriging (OK) interpolation, as well as classical machine learning (ML) models, including random forest (RF) and support vector machine (SVM), to map soil organic carbon density (SOCD) in the topsoil layer (0 − 20 cm) of cultivated land. It partitions the sampling data to assess the generalization capability of the machine learning models, with Zhongmu County designated as an independent test set (dataset2) and the remaining data as the training set (dataset1). The three models are trained using dataset1, and the trained machine learning models are directly applied to dataset2 to evaluate and compare their generalization performance. The distribution of SOCD and SOCS in soils of various types and textures is analyzed using the optimal interpolation method. The results indicated that: (1) The average SOC densities predicted by OK interpolation, RF, and SVM are 3.70, 3.74, and 3.63 kg/m2, with test set precisions (R2) of 0.34, 0.60, and 0.81, respectively. (2) ML achieves a significantly higher predictive precision than traditional OK interpolation. The RF model's precision is 0.21 higher than the SVM model and more precise in estimating carbon stock. (3) When applied to the dataset2, the RF model exhibited superior generalization capabilities (R2 = 0.52, MSE = 0.32) over the SVM model (R2 = 0.32, MSE = 0.45). (4) The spatial distribution of surface SOCD in the study area exhibits a decreasing gradient from west to east and from south to north. The total carbon stock in the study area is estimated at approximately 10.76 × 106t. (5) The integration of soil attribute variables, climatic variables, remote sensing data, and machine learning techniques holds significant promise for the high-precision and high-quality mapping of soil organic carbon density (SOCD) in agricultural soils.

Introduction

The soil organic carbon (SOC) reservoir is the largest carbon sink in terrestrial ecosystems, exceeding the total carbon stock of the green carbon pool and the atmospheric carbon reservoir, equivalent to twice the atmospheric carbon content (Wang et al., 2000). As a crucial component of soils, SOC is closely related to soil fertility and significantly impacts the Earth's carbon cycle and global climate change. It is both a "source" of greenhouse gases and a prominent "sink" for carbon (Beillouin et al., 2023). With global warming becoming increasingly prominent, carbon emissions have become a significant issue for countries worldwide. Currently, two primary methods exist to reduce CO2 emissions: reducing carbon emissions by controlling sources and increasing carbon sequestration by enhancing sinks. With its vast territory and large population, China finds that enhancing terrestrial ecosystem carbon sinks is the best way to reduce CO2 emissions and achieve the "double-carbon" goal (Yang et al., 2022). Farmland SOC is a vital factor affecting soil nutrients and physicochemical properties, representing a significant indicator of soil quality and fertility (Ling et al., 2015). Farmland constitutes about 10.5% of the total land area, and from 1961 to 2010, it contributed a carbon sink of 0.42 Pg C per year, demonstrating its potential for carbon sequestration (Ren et al., 2020). Different crop rotations and the uptake of crop residues can increase the amount of organic matter in the soil, improving SOC accumulation (Tiefenbacher et al., 2021). Cropland in China has the potential to store approximately 2.13 ± 0.96 kg m−2 (3.25 Pg) SOCs at a soil depth of 20 cm (Wang et al., 2023). Compared to uncultivated soils, cultivated soils, significantly impacted by human activities, can have their SOC content regulated quickly to reduce atmospheric CO2 concentrations through carbon sequestration. This strategy has gained recognition globally and has become a focal research topic.

In recent years, the number of studies on soil organic carbon stock (SOCS) and soil organic carbon density (SOCD) has grown annually, with an average growth rate of 9% (Zhou et al., 2020; Sun and Liu, 2019)to enhance the soil carbon sequestration capacity. Traditional soil carbon studies, primarily based on field samples and laboratory analysis, are labor-intensive and costly, with limited regional accessibility. They also overlooked the intricate linear relationships between soil carbon and environmental factors. However, the advent of geographical information systems (GIS) technology and the establishment of prediction models using machine learning (ML) for auxiliary variables and soil carbon have facilitated the spatial mapping of SOC. This method is increasingly used for spatial SOCD prediction and SOCS estimation. Common methods include stepwise linear regression, partial least squares discrimination analysis, random forest (RF), and support vector machine (SVM). SVM exhibits strong generalization and prediction capabilities in high-dimensional and nonlinear problems. RF is advantageous in processing multivariate nonlinear data and has shown high predictive precision (r ranging from 0.7 to 0.95) in large-scale spatial prediction of SOC content and SOCS. SOCD, a key component of soil carbon pools and a comprehensive indicator of ecosystem service functions (Du et al., 2022), directly affects SOCS. SOC's dynamic changes and density distribution have consistently attracted significant attention globally. Vos et al., (2019) estimated the SOCD in Germany to be 10.30 kg·m−2 using the soil type method and 2,515 soil samples. Emadi et al., (2020) employed the SVM model to forecast the SOC content in Iranian forest soils (R2 = 0.55). Jia et al., (2021) applied a denitrification-decomposition (DNDC) model to predict the future climate change effects on the SOCS of oasis soils. Similarly, (Zhu et al., 2022 and Wu et al., 2024) utilized the InVEST model to study carbon storage. The studies above employed various models, including Support Vector Machine (SVM), DNDC, and InVEST, to simulate soil organic carbon density, successfully predicting soil organic carbon density and carbon stock across different regions and scenarios.

In addition, the auxiliary factors utilized in various studies vary considerably. Frequently employed auxiliary factors in organic carbon research encompass topographic variables, climatic variables, remote sensing vegetation indices, and others. For example, Sreenivas et al., 2016 utilized the RF model with auxiliary variables such as climate, soil, and vegetation to simulate the distribution of SOC in India (R2 = 0.82). Liu et al., (2018) incorporated soil properties such as bulk density and soil layer thickness with environmental variables such as vegetation and climate, using the RF algorithm to simulate the SOCD of grasslands in northern China (R2 = 0.73). Wang et al., (2024) integrated derived variables from Sentinel-1, Sentinel-2, topography, and environmental factors and applied machine learning methods, including two-point machine learning, random forest (RF), and gradient-boosted regression trees (GBRT), to invert SOC content. The results showed that the two-point machine learning method achieves the highest inversion accuracy, followed by the random forest model. Yanfeng et al., (2018) employed a 30 m resolution digital elevation model (DEM) and Thematic Mapper (TM) images, using six methods, including the soil type method, RF, and OK, to predict SOCD in Nanyang, finding RF to have the highest explanatory power and predictive effectiveness for SOCD variability. Apparently, ML algorithms such as RF and SVM have the potential to improve SOCD's spatial simulation precision. However, various algorithms can adapt to different combinations of variables in varying regions, and the generalization ability of the model must be further explored.

Zhengzhou is a grain production base in China. Studies on Zhengzhou primarily focus on transforming land use patterns but rarely address the SOCD or SOCS of cultivated soil. This study focuses on the cultivated land in Zhengzhou and selects auxiliary variables such as terrain, climate, and soil property factors as inputs to the model. It investigates the distribution of surface SOCD, the estimation of SOCS, and their influencing factors using RF, SVM, and Ordinary Kriging. The generalization ability of the model is subsequently evaluated. The goal is to promote sustainable agricultural development, enhance soil structure, and ensure grain security in China, providing a scientific basis for the quality management of cultivated land and government macro decision-making. For this study, the surface SOC of cultivated land refers to the SOC in the surface layer (0–20 cm).

Materials

Study area

Zhengzhou is located at the extension of the eastern segment of the Qinling Mountains in the central and northern parts of Henan Province. It lies at the intersection of the middle and lower sections of the Yellow River and the northeast portion of the Funiu Mountains towards the Huang-Huai Plain. The area spans 7,567 square kilometers (112° 42′ –114° 14′ E; 34° 16′–34° 58′ N). Its terrain is elevated in the southwest and lower in the northeast, presenting a stepped descent. Zhengzhou is influenced by a temperate continental monsoon climate, experiences four distinctive seasons, and contains 124 rivers of various sizes. It spans across the Yellow River and Huai River basins. The geographical location of the research area is depicted in Fig. 1.Fig. 1Geographical location of the study area

Soil sampling collection

Soil samples were collected using the five-point sampling method, which involved gathering samples from the center and four corners of a 30 × 30 m rectangular area, with the five types of samples uniformly mixed as the final soil samples. Starting in March 2021, 11,382 soil samples were gathered from depths between 0 and 20 cm within the research area, and their positions were documented. During this period, the cultivated land was free of crop residue or snow cover, exposing the cultivated soil and forming a bare area. This time was ideal for creating spatial distribution maps of soil properties, facilitating the soil sample collection (Bao et al., 2021). All soil samples were sieved to remove crop roots, gravel, and other pollutants and dried indoors. The SOC content of each sample was measured using the potassium dichromate volumetric method, followed by total analysis for pH, heavy metals (Cu, Zn, Pb, Cd, Ni, Cr, and others), as well as other indicators (total N, P, Se, F, and others). The dataset from Zhongmu County was isolated and treated as an independent test dataset, named dataset2, to assess the generalization ability of the models. The remaining data were used for comparison and validation of the estimation accuracy of different models for SOCD and were named dataset1. Data sources and processing

Data sources

This study selected ten auxiliary factors closely related to the spatial distribution of SOCD, namely, N content interpolation, average annual temperature, average annual precipitation, elevation, normalized difference vegetation index (NDVI), nighttime light, slope, aspect, surface roughness, and Net primary productivity(NPP) (as shown in Fig. 3). N content interpolation is derived from performing Ordinary Kriging (OK) interpolation on the total N content of each sampling point. Evaluation criteria for OK interpolation: The mean standardized is closest to 0; the Root-Mean-Square prediction error is the smallest; the Average Mean Error is closest to the Root-Mean-Square prediction error; the Root-Mean-Square standardized prediction error is closest to 1. The mean standardized of the N interpolation model used in this study is -0.028, the Root-Mean-Square prediction error and Average Mean Error are 0.222 and 0.214, respectively, and the Root-Mean-Square standardized prediction error is 1.090. The model exhibited good fitting and is an auxiliary factor in predicting SOCD. The data on slope, aspect, and surface roughness are obtained from DEM data. Methods

The surface SOCD of the main cultivated land in Zhengzhou was the research object, with SOCD data derived from soil sampling points. Pearson's correlation analysis used candidate data on terrain, climate, remote sensing, and soil properties to identify significant influencing factors for modeling. The sampling data were partitioned into dataset1 (11,264 points) and dataset2 (118 points). Ordinary kriging (OK), random forest (RF), and support vector machine (SVM) models were developed to predict the spatial distribution of SOCD within the dataset1 coverage area using SOCD data from dataset1 and the selected modeling factors. In dataset1, 80% of the data were used for model training, and model performance was assessed using R2, MAE, and MSE on the test set. The trained machine learning models from dataset1 were subsequently applied to dataset2 to evaluate their generalization capability. The optimal model for SOCD prediction was selected based on precision results to estimate the surface SOCS of cultivated soil in Zhengzhou. Evaluation of algorithm performance

The overall precision of the three models (OK, SVM, and RF) was measured by simulating and validating SOCD using 80% of the training samples and 20% of the test samples in dataset1. The modeling and validation dataset of the OK model was automatically partitioned using the subset feature module in the Geostatistical Analyst module of ArcGIS 10.8 software, a fast and convenient program for automatic partitioning based on the percentage or absolute value of the input set. In contrast, SVM and RF performed partitioning using Python statements. The coefficient of determination, the mean square error (MSE), and the mean absolute error (MAE) were adopted to assess the fitting and generalization abilities of the model. The closer the value of R2 to 1 and the lower the values of MSE and MAE, the greater the stability of the model, the higher the precision, the more accurate the inversion and estimation of organic carbon, and the greater the likelihood of the model's excellent generalization ability and robustness. Analysis of SOCD influencing factors

The correlation bar chart between SOCD and auxiliary factors is shown in Fig. 5. In the surface soil of the main cultivated land in Zhengzhou, SOCD shows significant positive correlations with N, Se, Ni, S, Cu, Pb, and P in soil properties, increasing with their content. However, it presents negative correlations with pH and K, decreasing as pH and K increase. Regarding climate factors, SOCD in the study area indicates a positive correlation with average annual precipitation and a negative correlation with average annual temperature. SOCD content increases with rising precipitation and declining temperature. Generally, temperature and precipitation significantly influence the vegetation type of a region, indirectly affecting SOC content through their effects on crop yield and soil decomposition rate. Kabir et al., reported that average annual precipitation and temperature are primary predictors of SOC (Odebiri et al., 2020; Xu et al., 2018; Zhang et al., 2023). Terrain factors also represent the status of SOCD to some extent. SOCS shows a positive correlation with elevation and weak correlations with surface roughness and slope, while aspect exhibits no significant correlation. The surface SOCD of the main cultivated land in Zhengzhou does not have clear correlations with slope, aspect, or surface roughness. The SOCD in Zhengzhou reveals a positive correlation with NDVI and weak correlations with NPP and nighttime light. The intensity of nighttime light is not conducive to SOC accumulation. Accordingly, soil chemical properties significantly affect SOCD in the study area, followed by climate factors, while terrain factors have minor impacts. As shown in Fig. 6, feature importance in the random forest model was evaluated using SHAP values. In this plot, the horizontal axis represents SHAP values, with positive contributions on the right and negative contributions on the left. Among the features, "N" has the greatest impact on the output of the random forest model, followed by "average annual temperature." In contrast, "aspect" has the least impact. This visualization provides a clear view of how each feature contributes to the model, with "N" and average annual temperature as primary drivers of predictions, while features such as aspect show minimal influence. Discussion

Variable selection

This study selected soil properties, climate, terrain, and other factors for modeling to predict SOCD. N is the most important environmental variable for predicting SOCD, exhibiting a significant positive correlation (r = 0.792). The importance of auxiliary factors is also crucial for variable selection in the RF model. N is the primary cofactor affecting the spatial variability of SOCD in cultivated land in Zhengzhou, demonstrating a close relationship with SOCD. Although the contributions of other single factors are relatively minor, their roles in predicting spatial distribution are indispensable.

Numerous studies have confirmed the close coupling of carbon and nitrogen cycling and the significant positive correlation between N and SOC in terrestrial ecosystems (Niu et al., 2023). However, the challenge of data acquisition has limited their use in predicting SOC or related variables. Xie et al. demonstrated the feasibility of using soil factors for the spatial prediction of SOC by comparing the effects of retaining and removing the auxiliary factor N on the precision of organic matter prediction in farmland soils (Xie et al., 2018, Yuqi et al., 2021). In addition, Yuqi et al., (2021) observed a 7.95% enhancement in the coefficient of determination (R2) when soil properties were integrated into the random forest (RF) model. The most important factor affecting the spatial distribution of organic carbon in arable land was N, followed by DEM. In contrast, in this study, it was N, followed by mean annual precipitation and mean annual temperature (Fig. 8). Among terrain factors, elevation positively correlates with SOCD to a certain extent (r = 0.167). Terrain factors, such as elevation, slope, aspect, and surface roughness, indirectly affect the spatial distribution of SOCD via terrain and landform differences and are considered external factors. However, cultivated land is typically located in relatively flat areas with convenient transportation. In this study, the elevation range of cultivated land was 77–1,471 m, showing significant elevation variation, indicating elevation's influence on SOCD distribution. Using 8,637 soil samples, (Sun et al., 2021) estimated the surface SOCD in Yunnan Province to be about 4.84 kg·m−2, identifying elevation, temperature, precipitation, and the terrain humidity index as the main factors affecting the spatial variability of SOCD in Yunnan Province. Regarding auxiliary factors, research on the impact of human activities on SOC is limited. It has been proven that agricultural activities on cultivated land, such as crop rotations, irrigation, and fertilization, significantly influence the surface SOC content of soils. (Yang et al., 2024a) demonstrated that human activities and fertilization patterns affect SOC. Thus, identifying additional auxiliary variables with strong correlations to SOC and alternative factors representing human activities will be crucial for enhancing the precision of SOC prediction.

Model limitations

This study employed only two ML models, RF and SVM, to forecast SOCD, and they were compared to OK. The results showed that SVM (R2 = 0.60, MSE = 1.06, and MAE = 0.43) and RF (R2 = 0.81, MSE = 0.41, and MAE = 0.34) both had higher precision than OK (R2 = 0.34, MSE = 1.43, and MAE = 0.77). However, the applications of ML models are limited. In future predictions of SOCD, more ML algorithms, such as classification trees and artificial neural networks, should be introduced for comparative research. In addition, with the development of machine learning models, deep neural networks should also be used in the digital mapping of soil properties (Hosseinpour-Zarnaq et al., 2023, Datta et al., 2023, Wang et al., 2023). Yang Kui et al. employed multiple linear regression (MLR), SVM, RF, and long-short-term memory (LSTM) models to reverse carbon storage. The results showed that the LSTM algorithm has the best fit, with an R2 of 0.84 and an RMSE of 3.56 (Yang et al., 2024b). Tao Zhang et al. 2024 developed a topsoil soil organic carbon stock (SOCS) model using a deep learning approach, specifically the multilayer perceptron (MLP), and evaluated its performance. Deep learning offers significant advantages in the agricultural field (Attri et al., 2023). Kang et al. (2020) combined artificial neural network (ANN) with OK interpolation, finding that the predictive precision of this combination surpassed that of either method alone. The best results of the RF model in the study area were R2 = 0.81, MSE = 0.41, and MAE = 0.34. The model fitting results fell within a desirable range when considering soil property factors. However, compared to previous literature, this study does not yield significant advantages, possibly due to the small and non-concentrated cultivated area in the study region. When the trained models were directly applied to the new dataset, the R2 of the RF and SVM models decreased by 0.29 and 0.28, respectively. Whether other machine learning and deep learning models will demonstrate better generalization in regions with similar soil conditions, surpassing the performance of the two machine learning models used in this experiment, remains a topic for further investigation.

Accordingly, this study's limitations include the reliance on less accessible variables such as N, the exclusion of factors representing anthropogenic influences, and the absence of deep learning models such as neural networks. Addressing these issues can reduce the costs associated with organic carbon inversion while enhancing its accuracy. Future research should prioritize the resolution of these challenges.

Conclusion

This study introduces auxiliary variables to predict the spatial distribution of SOCD and estimate SOCS in Zhengzhou using the classical geostatistical method and two ML models. All three methods succeed in predicting the SOCD of cultivated land, but the RF model (R2 = 0.81, MSE = 0.41, and MAE = 0.34) exhibits the best performance. Among the auxiliary factors introduced, soil properties show a higher overall correlation with SOCD than terrain, remote sensing, and climate factors. Including soil property factors, especially N, helps predict SOCD more effectively. The RF model also indicates strong generalization performance within the study area. The distribution of surface SOCD of cultivated land in Zhengzhou presents a pattern of "high in the west and low in the east," representing the terrain trend of Zhengzhou. The correlation between SOCD and elevation in the study area was 0.167, likely because higher-elevation areas have better hydrothermal conditions conducive to increased SOCD. Soil type and texture influence SOCD and SOCS. The cultivated area with a soil type of Calcic Luvisols (4.74 kg/m2) and soil texture of sandy loam (4.63 kg/m2) exhibits the highest average surface SOCD.

Through the combination of multivariate and machine learning, this study achieves excellent performance in cultivated soil's organic carbon density mapping, but it also has limitations. Future research on organic carbon in cultivated soil should focus on finding auxiliary variables that can reflect human activities, followed by more use of deep learning, such as neural networks. The study's findings indicated that increasing the SOCD and SOCS of cultivated land can be achieved by regulating soil properties by promoting organic nitrogen fertilizers, returning straw to the field, and implementing crop rotations. The study of soil organic carbon in cultivated land aids in promoting the sustainable development of agriculture in China, enhancing soil structure, and offering a scientific foundation for cultivated land quality management and governmental macro-decision-making. This study is informative for predicting the SOCD and estimating the carbon stock of cultivated soils. However, it is limited by model precision and auxiliary variables, so future research should explore these findings.
