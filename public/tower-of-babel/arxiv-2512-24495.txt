Spectroscopy of Quantum Phase Slips: Visualizing Complex Real-Time Instantons
Foster Thompson, Daniel K. J. Boneß, Mark Dykman, Alex Kamenev

Abstract
Parametrically driven oscillators can emerge as a basis for the next generation of qubits. Classically, these systems exhibit two stable oscillatory states with opposite phases. Upon quantization, these states turn into a pair of closely spaced Floquet states, which can serve as the logical basis for a qubit. However, interaction with the environment induces phase-slip events which set a limit on qubit coherence. Such phase slips persist even at zero temperature due to a mechanism known as quantum activation 35 . In contrast to conventional tunneling, the quantum activation is described by a real-time instanton trajectory in the complexified phase space of the system. In this work, we show that the phase-slip rate is exponentially sensitive to weak AC perturbations. The spectrum of the system’s response – captured by the so-called logarithmic susceptibility (LS) – enables a direct observation of characteristic features of real-time instantons. Studying this spectrum suggests new means of efficient qubit control.

Fluctuations, either thermal or quantum, induce transitions between the two dynamically stable states. Since such transitions lead to a sudden change of the oscillation phase by

The corresponding phase-slip rates for both classical

Such instantons evolve in real time as opposed to imaginary-time tunneling events in equilibrium quantum mechanics. They are reminiscent of the most probable trajectories followed by a Brownian particle as it escapes from a potential well
26

\omega_{0}

It is therefore important to gain a maximally detailed information about phase slips internal dynamics to minimize the rate of their occurrence. This in turn maximizes the coherence time of qubits based on PDOs. It is also a major challenge to find a way to “visualize” a quantum real-time instanton, which would provide a direct proof of the mechanism of quantum activation.

A way of doing it is to use spectroscopy. In the present context, it amounts to applying a weak external drive (in addition to the relatively strong parametric drive) with some frequency
\omega_{\mathrm{d}}

The dependence of the phase slip rate on the spectroscopic frequency,
\omega_{\mathrm{d}}

Qualitatively, the phase-slips can be understood in the following way. In the frame rotating at twice the modulation period

When the weak spectroscopic drive is in resonance with the period-two parametric oscillations,
\omega_{\mathrm{d}}=\omega_{\mathrm{p}}
, the rotating frame motion is perturbed by a
time-independent
tilting of the double-well quasi-potential. Such a perturbation breaks the symmetry between the two oscillatory states and makes switching out of the shallow well exponentially more frequent. The corresponding change in the (logarithm of the) phase-slip rate was obtained for both classical
42

3

4

\nu=\omega(E)

\nu=0

In this work, we address spectroscopy of quantum phase-slips in a broad range of environmental temperature, from the temperatures where the thermal occupation number
n_{\mathrm{B}}
of the oscillator in the absence of modulation is zero to the classical high-temperature limit,
n_{\mathrm{B}}\gg 1
. We show that the strong enhancement of the switching rate persists for all temperatures. It exhibits a strongly pronounced frequency dependence, which serves as an experimentally detectable signature of the quantum phase-slips’ coherent dynamics.

We show that the resonant enhancement of the phase-slip rate occurs only when the spectroscopic frequency in the rotating frame is inside the frequency band of the intrawell motion,
|\nu|<\omega(E_{\mathrm{min}})
, i.e. when there exists a value of the quasi-energy inside the well for which the condition
\nu=\omega(E)
is met. There is a sharp (non-analytic as a function of
\nu
) drop of the response when
|\nu|
exceeds the largest intrawell frequency,
\omega_{\mathrm{min}}=\omega(E_{\mathrm{min}})
, corresponding to the disappearance of the resonance. However, there are also secondary sharp features in the response at
\nu=n\omega_{\mathrm{min}}
, where
n
is an integer, corresponding to resonances with higher harmonics of the oscillatory instanton trajectory, see Fig.
2
. Such higher harmonics exist due (and are highly sensitive) to the anharmonicity of the quasi-potential, Fig.
1
.
The corresponding classical (i.e. for thermal phase-slips) calculation was presented in Ref.
42
.

The technical approach we take is to develop a parallel treatment of the stochastic classical and quantum PDO based on the path integral representation of the Keldysh technique
24
. This has an advantage of treating both the classical and quantum theories on the same footing. Moreover, the classical theory constitutes a particular limiting case at the level of the quantum action. This observation provides a transparent connection between the classical and quantum regimes before any specific calculations are performed. Corrections to the switching rate
exponent
are then straightforward to obtain for both the classical and quantum theory via the computation of a certain response function known as the logarithmic susceptibility
44
.

The microscopic models for both the quantum and classical PDO are introduced in Section
II
and the corresponding path integral treatments are presented. Section
III
discusses the computation of the bare switching rate using action-angle coordinates. Previously known results for both classical
9
and quantum
35

IV

42

V

In this regime the dissipation necessarily becomes fast compared to the coherent rotation and the motion becomes overdamped, resulting in the breakdown of the resonant response. We derive the spectroscopic signatures of this regime. Concluding remarks are given in Section
VI

II
The Parametric Oscillator

We consider a single mode with a frequency
\omega_{0}
and the quartic Duffing anharmonicity
G
subject simultaneously to a parametric drive with frequency
2\omega_{\mathrm{p}}
and a weak spectroscopic signal at a different frequency,
\omega_{\mathrm{d}}
(see Appendix
D
for the relation to the notations in
35
;
3
;
4
). The classical Hamiltonian describing this system is given by

H=\frac{1}{2}\,p^{2}+\frac{1}{2}\big(\omega_{0}^{2}+A_{\mathrm{p}}\cos(2\omega_{\mathrm{p}}t)\big)x^{2}+\frac{1}{4}Gx^{4}\\
+A_{\mathrm{d}}\cos(\omega_{\mathrm{d}}t+\varphi_{\mathrm{d}})x,

(1)

where the mass of the oscillator has been absorbed into a rescaling of the momentum and thus set to unity for brevity.

When the amplitude,
A_{\mathrm{d}}
, of the second drive is small, it may be treated as a perturbation. The dynamics can be well-approximated using the rotating wave approximation in the frame of the parametric drive. This is conveniently achieved using the complex coordinate in the rotating frame

\phi=\sqrt{\frac{\omega_{\mathrm{p}}}{2}}\,(x+\text{i}p/\omega_{\mathrm{p}})\,\text{e}^{-\text{i}\omega_{\mathrm{p}}t}.

(2)

Upon dropping the fast counter-rotating terms, the Hamiltonian in the rotating frame
H_{\mathrm{RWA}}=H_{0}+H_{1}
becomes

H_{0}(\bar{\phi},\phi)=\Delta\bar{\phi}\phi+\lambda(\phi^{2}+\bar{\phi}^{2})+\frac{g}{4}(\bar{\phi}\phi)^{2};

(3a)

H_{1}(\bar{\phi},\phi;t)=\alpha(\text{e}^{\text{i}(\nu t+\varphi_{\mathrm{d}})}\phi+\text{e}^{-\text{i}(\nu t+\varphi_{\mathrm{d}})}\bar{\phi}),

(3b)

where
\alpha=A_{\mathrm{d}}/(2\sqrt{2\omega_{\mathrm{p}}})
is the normalized amplitude of the (weak) spectroscopic signal,
\Delta=(\omega_{0}^{2}-\omega_{\mathrm{p}}^{2})/2\omega_{\mathrm{p}}
is a detuning,
\lambda=A_{\mathrm{p}}/8\omega_{\mathrm{p}}
is the normalized amplitude of the (strong) parametric drive,
g=3G/2\omega_{\mathrm{p}}^{2}
is an anharmonicity, and
\nu=\omega_{\mathrm{d}}-\omega_{\mathrm{p}}
is the effective spectroscopic frequency. The complex variables
\phi
and
\bar{\phi}
form a canonical pair with the corresponding equations of motion
i\partial_{t}\phi=\partial_{\bar{\phi}}H_{\mathrm{RWA}}
and
i\partial_{t}{\bar{\phi}}=-\partial_{\phi}H_{\mathrm{RWA}}
.

The classical dynamics described by the unperturbed rotating-wave approximated Hamiltonian
H_{0}
is symmetric under the parity symmetry
\phi\to-\phi
. For a fixed quasi-energy,
E=H_{0}(\bar{\phi},\phi)
, in the laboratory frame the oscillator may have period-two states, which oscillate at frequency
\omega_{\mathrm{p}}
, modulated by
\omega(E)
. The nature of the state with the minimal quasi-energy depends on the dimensionless ratio
\Delta/2\lambda
. For
\Delta/2\lambda>1
there is a unique symmetric minimum of
H_{0}
at
\phi=0
, corresponding to the absence of the parametrically induced oscillations of the mode. For
\Delta/2\lambda<1
the
\phi=0
point becomes a saddle point and
H_{0}
acquires a double well shape
1
1

1

More precisely, we consider
|\Delta|<2\lambda
. If
\Delta
is too strongly negative,
\Delta<-2\lambda
, the point at
\phi=0
becomes a maximum of
H_{0}
and two additional saddles appear at
\phi=\pm\sqrt{-2(2\lambda+\Delta)/g}
. We do not consider the large negative detuning regime in this work.
, depicted in Fig.
1
.
The parity symmetry is spontaneously broken and the system displays bistability as there emerge two oscillatory states, which are stable in the presence of dissipation.
They are located at the minima of the function
H_{0}
, at
\phi=\pm\phi_{0}
with
\phi_{0}=\text{i}\sqrt{2(2\lambda-\Delta)/g}
and
H(\pm\phi_{0})\equiv E_{\mathrm{min}}=-(2\lambda-\Delta)^{2}/g<0
. Near the bottom of the wells, the steady states
\omega_{\mathrm{p}}
-oscillations are modulated by small-amplitude vibrations with the frequency

\omega_{\mathrm{min}}=\omega(E_{\mathrm{min}})=2\sqrt{2\lambda(2\lambda-\Delta)},

(4)

which play an important role in the PDO spectroscopy.

The nature of the classical motion described by
H_{0}
away from the fixed points depends on the value of the quasi-energy.
For
E\in[E_{\mathrm{min}},0)
all classical states are localized in one of the two wells. For positive quasi-energies,
E>0
, all states regain their symmetry and are not restricted to a single well. At
E=0
there is a separatrix separating these two regimes, which passes through the saddle point at
\phi=0
. For our purposes, it suffices to consider in detail only the intrawell motion with
E\in[E_{\mathrm{min}},0)
. Solutions of the classical Hamilton’s equations
\text{i}\partial_{t}\phi=\partial_{\bar{\phi}}H_{0}
, centered around
\phi_{0}
, are denoted with an underline,
\underline{\phi}(t;E)
.
These solutions are elliptic functions; details of their specific functional forms are presented in Appendix
A
. As elliptic functions, the solutions
\underline{\phi}(t;E)
are bi-periodic in complex plane of the time argument
t
. For all values of the system parameters and quasi-energy, there is a real-valued period
t_{1}(E)
and an additional complex period
t_{2}(E)
, so that

\underline{\phi}\big(t+t_{j}(E);E\big)=\underline{\phi}(t;E)

(5)

for
j=1,2
. The real period is the physical period of the motion in the rotating frame,
t_{1}(E)=2\pi/\omega(E)
. The complex period does not have a meaning in the classical problem, however its appearance can be traced to the fact that constant quasi-energy condition
H_{0}(\bar{\phi},\phi)=E
defines an elliptic curve in the complexified classical phase space. As a consequence of this fact, the complex period is related to the tunneling rate of a closed quantum system with the Hamiltonian
H_{0}
, for example see Refs.
21
;
20
.

The second drive,
H_{1}
, explicitly breaks the parity symmetry.
In the bistable regime this results in the modulation of the heights of the two wells. When the second drive period is exactly at half-resonance with the parametric drive,
\nu=\omega_{\mathrm{d}}-\omega_{\mathrm{p}}=0
, the problem is time-independent and the two wells are tilted so that one is shallower than the other. The direction of this tilt is dictated by the phase
\varphi_{\mathrm{d}}
between the parametric drive and the second drive.
Off resonance, the heights of the two wells oscillate in time, so that a given well is tilted to become shallower and then deeper than its counterpart over the oscillation period
2\pi/\nu
.

II.1
Action-Angle Variables

As will be discussed in detail in section
III
, the problem of computing the quantum fluctuation-induced switching rate involves identifying appropriate instanton trajectories that connect the classical fixed points. This is in general a difficult task for driven systems, because the rotating wave Hamiltonian,
H_{0}
, cannot be written as a sum of kinetic and potential energies. Thus the momentum coordinate cannot be integrated out, and so the resulting effective semiclassical phase space has large dimension.
This predicament can be avoided by identifying a coordinate system which separates degrees of freedom that evolve with different time scales. When this is possible, the faster coordinate can be effectively integrated out to provide an effective dynamics of the slow variable, thus reducing the dimensionality of the phase space. This greatly simplifies the computational challenge associated with finding the instanton trajectory.

In the present context,
the action-angle canonical coordinates,
(\phi,\bar{\phi})\to(I,\theta)
, provide such a decomposition. They are defined in a way to make the unperturbed Hamiltonian angle independent,

H_{0}(\bar{\phi},\phi)=H_{0}(I)=E(I)
, and preserve the canonical Poisson bracket between
I
and
\theta
. The corresponding Hamilton equation

\partial_{t}\theta=\partial_{I}H_{0}(I)\equiv\omega(I)

(6)

dictates
\theta=\omega(I)t
, while
I=I(E)
is an integral of motion for
H_{0}
. The action for a given quasi-energy
E
is given by the area enclosed by the trajectory
\underline{\phi}(t;E)
in the complex
\phi
-plane

I(E)=\int\limits_{0}^{t_{1}(E)}\frac{\text{d}t}{2\pi}\ \underline{\bar{\phi}}(t;E)\,\text{i}\partial_{t}\underline{\phi}(t;E).

(7)

The function
I(E)
is thus monotonic with
I(E_{\mathrm{min}})=0
and
I(E=0)=I_{\mathrm{top}}>0
, cf. Fig.
1
.
The frequency
\omega(I)
is also a monotonic function, decreasing from
\omega(0)\equiv\omega_{\mathrm{min}}>0
down to
\omega(I_{\mathrm{top}})=0
. The corresponding periodic classical trajectory may be also transformed to the action-angle representation and decomposed into the Fourier series:

\underline{\phi}(t;E)=\underline{\phi}(\theta(t);I)=\sum_{n}c_{n}(I)\,\text{e}^{-\text{i}n\theta(t)}.

(8)

The functional form of the Fourier coefficients,
c_{n}(I)
, and their asymptotic expressions are given in Appendix
A
.

II.2
Classical Stochastic Theory

The influence of an environment can be modeled by a coupling between the system and a thermal bath of linear oscillators with position coordinates
X_{u}
which oscillate with a spectrum of frequencies
\omega_{u}
. Each bath mode is taken to be in thermal equilibrium at temperature
T
(here and throughout we work with units in which
k_{\mathrm{B}}=1
). We consider a linear coupling between the system and the environment described by the interaction
H_{\mathrm{int}}=\sum_{u}c_{u}X_{u}x
. This retains the parity symmetry of the un-perturbed system
x\to-x
if it is expanded also to include the bath modes
X_{u}\to-X_{u}
.

In the standard way
8
, averaging over the bath degrees of freedom gives an effective theory described by the stochastic Langevin equation in the rotating frame and in the rotating wave approximation
with dissipation and noise. The dissipation strength is given by
\kappa=J(\omega_{\mathrm{p}})/2
where
J(\epsilon)=\pi\sum_{u}(c_{u}^{2}/\epsilon)\delta(\epsilon-\omega_{u})
is the bath spectral density. The Langevin equation reads

\text{i}\partial_{t}\phi=\partial_{\bar{\phi}}H_{\mathrm{RWA}}-\text{i}\kappa\phi+\xi(t),

(9)

where the Hamiltonian,
H_{\mathrm{RWA}}
, is given by Eq. (
3
) and
\xi(t)
is a complex Gaussian white noise with zero mean and equal variance for real and imaginary parts
\braket{\bar{\xi}(t)\xi(t^{\prime})}=(2\kappa T/\omega_{\mathrm{p}})\delta(t-t^{\prime})
and
\braket{\xi(t)\xi(t^{\prime})}=0
.

With this, one may pass to the action-angle variables by taking the time derivative of
I=I(E(\underline{\bar{\phi}},\underline{\phi}))
and then using Eq. (
9
).
This results in a Langevin equation for the action variable with a multiplicative noise,

\partial_{t}I=\mathcal{E}(I,\theta)+\bar{\eta}(I,\theta)\xi(t)+\eta(I,\theta)\bar{\xi}(t),

(10)

where the functions
\mathcal{E}=\mathcal{E}_{0}+\alpha\mathcal{E}_{1}
and
\eta
are given by

\mathcal{E}_{0}(I,\theta)=-\frac{\kappa}{\omega(I)}\,\big(\underline{\phi}\,\partial_{\phi}H_{0}+\underline{\bar{\phi}}\,\partial_{\bar{\phi}}H_{0}\big),

(11a)

\mathcal{E}_{1}(I,\theta;t)=\partial_{\theta}\underline{\phi}\,\partial_{\phi}H_{1}+\partial_{\theta}\underline{\bar{\phi}}\,\partial_{\bar{\phi}}H_{1},

(11b)

\eta(I,\theta)=\frac{\text{i}\partial_{\bar{\phi}}H_{0}}{\omega(I)}.

(11c)

The angle coordinate can be treated similarly. In the limit of weak dissipation
\kappa\ll\omega_{\mathrm{min}}
, the motion is underdamped. Therefore the action coordinate undergoes slow relaxation with a time scale set by the weak dissipation,
\kappa
, while the angle coordinate coherently rotates at a faster scale,
\omega(I)
, set by the dissipationless classical problem. The dynamics of the phase can thus be well-approximated by neglecting the effects of dissipation and fluctuations,

\partial_{t}\theta\simeq\omega(I).

(12)

This separation of time scales means the dynamics of the action can be described by averaging over the fast angle rotation.

We are interested in rare events of phase-slip transitions, i.e., switching between the classically stable states with opposite phase. To calculate the phase-slip rate
W_{\mathrm{ps}}
it is convenient to first pass to the path integral description and then use the WKB approximation. We do it here in the spirit of the Keldysh formalism
24
;
43
;
47
. We introduce the partition function as noise average of equations Eqs. (
10
) and (
12
), enforced through delta functions:

\displaystyle Z=

\displaystyle\int\text{D}\bar{\xi}\text{D}\xi\,\exp\left[-\frac{\omega_{\mathrm{p}}}{2\kappa T}\int\text{d}t\bar{\xi}\xi\right]

\displaystyle\times\int\text{D}I\text{D}\theta\,\delta\big(\partial_{t}I-\mathcal{E}-\bar{\xi}\eta-\bar{\eta}\xi\big)\delta\big(\partial_{t}\theta-\omega\big).

(13)

This can be massaged into the standard form by introducing a Lagrange multiplier field, which will be denoted
p
, to impose the delta constraint of the action coordinate, and then integrating out both the noise and the angle. Integrating out the angle leads to the replacement
\theta\to\underline{\theta}(I)=\int^{t}\text{d}t\,\omega(I)
, thus resulting in an effective theory with only two fields:

Z=\int\text{D}I\,\text{D}p\,\,\text{e}^{\text{i}S[I,p]}

(14a)

\text{i}S[I,p]=-\int\text{d}t\big(p\partial_{t}I-K(I,p)\big),

(14b)

where the standard integration contour of the auxiliary variable runs along the imaginary axis,
p\in\text{i}\mathbb{R}
.

The effective Hamiltonian,
K=K_{0}+K_{1}
, is a sum of the term
K_{0}
that describes dissipation and the term
K_{1}
, which is due to the second drive. The term
K_{0}
is obtained by averaging over the fast-rotating parts of the functions from Eq. (
11
).
This is achieved using the Fourier series representation of the classical solutions Eq. (
8
) and then using the replacement
\exp(\text{i}(n^{\prime}-n)\underline{\theta}(I))\to\delta_{nn^{\prime}}
to re-sum the resulting series. This results in the substitutions
\mathcal{E}_{0}\to-2\kappa I
and
\bar{\eta}\eta\to\Gamma(I)
, where
\Gamma(I)=\int_{0}^{2\pi}(\text{d}\theta/2\pi)\,\partial_{\theta}\underline{\bar{\phi}}(\theta;I)\,\partial_{\theta}\underline{\phi}(\theta;I)
is a function whose properties may be obtained in certain limits, see Appendix
A
. Employing Eqs. (
7
) and (
8
), one observes the following expressions through the Fourier coefficients:

I=\sum_{n}n|c_{n}(I)|^{2};\qquad\Gamma(I)=\sum_{n}n^{2}|c_{n}(I)|^{2}

(15)

The resulting unperturbed effective Hamiltonian is given by

\displaystyle\begin{split}K_{0}(I,p)&=p\,\mathcal{E}_{0}(I)+\frac{2\kappa T}{\omega_{\mathrm{p}}}\,\bar{\eta}(I)\eta(I)\,p^{2}\\
&\simeq-2\kappa pI+\frac{2\kappa T}{\omega_{\mathrm{p}}}\,\Gamma(I)\,p^{2}.\end{split}

(16)

This is the effective Hamiltonian for a Langevin equation describing the linear relaxation of the action variable,
\partial_{t}I=-2\kappa I
, under the influence of a multiplicative noise with the variance
\sim(\kappa T/\omega_{\mathrm{p}})\Gamma(I)
.

The second-drive induced term
K_{1}
does not have any fast rotating terms that can be neglected, so all Fourier modes should be kept. It has the form:

K_{1}(I,p;t)=\alpha p\sum_{n}nc_{n}(I)\text{e}^{-\text{i}(n\underline{\theta}(I)-\nu t-\varphi_{\mathrm{d}})}+\mathrm{c.c.}

(17)

where
\mathrm{c.c.}
is complex conjugate.

II.3
Quantum Theory

The Hamiltonian operator
\hat{H}
of the quantum theory is defined by adorning
x
and
p
in Eq. (
1
) with hats.
The quantum Hamiltonian in the rotating wave approximation is similarly obtained from Eq. (
3
) by replacing the complex coordinates
\phi
and
\bar{\phi}
with the bosonic creation and annihilation operators
\hat{a}
and
\hat{a}^{\dagger}
, which satisfy the canonical bosonic commutation rule
[\hat{a},\hat{a}^{\dagger}]=1
.

Like in the classical theory, the environment may be modeled by a thermal bath of linear oscillators linearly coupled to the system.
Integrating out the bath modes gives an effective dynamics of the reduced density matrix of the system described, in the rotating wave approximation, by a Lindblad equation
24
,

\displaystyle\partial_{t}\rho=

\displaystyle\hat{\vphantom{\rule{1.0pt}{5.71527pt}}\smash{\hat{\mathcal{L}}}}\rho=-\text{i}[\hat{H}_{\mathrm{RWA}},\rho]

\displaystyle+\sum_{v=\ell,\mathrm{g}}\gamma_{v}\Bigl(\hat{L}_{v}\rho\hat{L}_{v}^{\dagger}-\frac{1}{2}\{\hat{L}_{v}^{\dagger}\hat{L}_{v},\rho\}\Bigl),

(18)

where the Lindbladian superoperator
\hat{\vphantom{\rule{1.0pt}{5.71527pt}}\smash{\hat{\mathcal{L}}}}
acts on the Hilbert space of operators. There are two jump operators
\hat{L}_{\ell}=\hat{a}
and
\hat{L}_{\mathrm{g}}=\hat{a}^{\dagger}
with the corresponding rates
\gamma_{\ell}=2(n_{\mathrm{B}}+1)\kappa
and
\gamma_{\mathrm{g}}=2n_{\mathrm{B}}\kappa
.
The Bose occupation number
n_{\mathrm{B}}=1/(\text{e}^{\omega_{\mathrm{p}}/T}-1)
is the occupation number of the bath in the rotating frame given by the Bose function evaluated at
\epsilon=\omega_{\mathrm{p}}
. The coupling leads also to renormalization of the oscillator eigenfrequency, which we assume to have been incorporated into
\omega_{0}
.

The path integral description for the quantum problem is also provided by the Keldysh formalism
24
;
43
;
47
. The partition function for the system is represented as a coherent state functional integral

Z=\int\text{D}\phi^{\pm}\text{D}\bar{\phi}^{\pm}\exp(\text{i}S[\bar{\phi}^{\pm},\phi^{\pm}]),

(19)

where the action corresponding to the Lindbladian from Eq. (
II.3
) is

S[\bar{\phi}^{\pm},\phi^{\pm}]=\int\text{d}t\Big(\bar{\phi}^{+}\text{i}\partial_{t}\phi^{+}-\bar{\phi}^{-}\text{i}\partial_{t}\phi^{-}\\
-H_{\mathrm{RWA}}^{+}+H_{\mathrm{RWA}}^{-}-\text{i}\sum_{v=\ell,\mathrm{g}}\gamma_{v}D_{v}\Big),

(20)

where
H_{\mathrm{RWA}}^{\pm}=H_{\mathrm{RWA}}(\bar{\phi}^{\pm},\phi^{\pm})
and the dissipative terms are

D_{\ell}(\bar{\phi}^{\pm},\phi^{\pm})=\bar{\phi}^{-}\phi^{+}-\frac{1}{2}(\bar{\phi}^{+}\phi^{+}+\bar{\phi}^{-}\phi^{-}),

(21a)

D_{\mathrm{g}}(\bar{\phi}^{\pm},\phi^{\pm})=\bar{\phi}^{+}\phi^{-}-\frac{1}{2}(\bar{\phi}^{+}\phi^{+}+\bar{\phi}^{-}\phi^{-}).

(21b)

The action-angle coordinates may be introduced in the Keldysh language using the coordinate transformation defined by Eq. (
8
) applied for the fields on forward and backward parts of the time contour independently. The resulting set of four coordinates
I^{\pm}
and
\theta^{\pm}
can be separated into physical and auxiliary “quantum” variables with the Keldysh rotation:
I^{\pm}=I\pm I^{\mathrm{q}}/2
and
\theta^{\pm}=\theta\pm\theta^{\mathrm{q}}/2
. In total, this amounts to the coordinate transformation:

\phi^{\pm}=\sum_{n}c_{n}(I\pm I^{\mathrm{q}}/2)\text{e}^{-\text{i}n(\theta\pm\theta^{\mathrm{q}}/2)},

(22)

where, as before,
c_{n}(I)
are Fourier coefficients of the classical trajectory with the fixed action,
I
.

Like in the classical theory, for weak dissipation the fluctuations of the angle coordinate can be neglected. This is achieved by expanding to first order in the quantum action coordinate
I^{\mathrm{q}}
in the Hamiltonian, so that
H_{0}^{+}-H_{0}^{-}\simeq\partial_{I}H_{0}(I)I^{\mathrm{q}}=\omega(I)I^{\mathrm{q}}
, and dropping the
I^{\mathrm{q}}
dependence in the perturbation
H_{1}^{\pm}
and dissipative parts
D_{v}
. With this,
I^{\mathrm{q}}
appears only at linear order in the action
\sim I^{\mathrm{q}}(\partial_{t}\theta-\omega(I))
. Subsequently integrating out both
I^{\mathrm{q}}
and
\theta
thus leads to the replacement
\theta\to\underline{\theta}(I)=\int^{t}\!\text{d}t\,\omega(I)
like in the classical theory.
This defines an effective theory with only two fields, which upon defining
\theta^{\mathrm{q}}=-\text{i}p
has the same form as Eq. (
14
).

The effective Hamiltonian
K=K_{0}+K_{1}
comes from the dissipative part of the Lindbladian evolution
D_{v}
and the second drive perturbation
H_{1}
.
Averaging over the fast-rotating parts of
K_{0}
is carried out in the same way as in the classical theory. It results in the replacements
\bar{\phi}^{\mp}\phi^{\pm}\to\sum_{n}|c_{n}|^{2}\text{e}^{\mp np}
and
\bar{\phi}^{\pm}\phi^{\pm}\to\sum_{n}|c_{n}|^{2}
. The result is an effective Hamiltonian with a form matching that of reaction kinetics models

K_{0}(I,p)=\sum_{n}W_{n}(I)(\text{e}^{-np}-1).

(23)

This describes the stochastic dynamics of the action variable in which each term in the sum with
n\gtrless 0
is associated with a processes in which the quasi-energy transitions up or down by
n
quantized levels. The transitions are induced by the bath and their rates are given by:

W_{n}(I)=\gamma_{\ell}|c_{n}(I)|^{2}+\gamma_{\mathrm{g}}|c_{-n}(I)|^{2}.

(24)

There are transitions both down and
up
in quasi-energy even for
T=0
(which corresponds to
\gamma_{\mathrm{g}}=0
). The
T=0
up-transitions are due to the parametric driving, which makes the quasi-energy states to be different from the energy states of the corresponding equilibrium oscillator. This is the formal manifestation of the phenomenon known as quantum heating, in which driven-dissipative quantum systems can have finite occupation even when connected to a zero temperature bath
35
.

In this formulation, it is very clear how the classical theory can be obtained as a limit from the quantum theory. Expanding Eq. (
23
) to second order in
p
and re-summing the resulting series using Eq. (
15
) gives
K_{0}(I,p)\simeq-(\gamma_{\ell}-\gamma_{\mathrm{g}})Ip+(\gamma_{\ell}+\gamma_{\mathrm{g}})p^{2}\Gamma(I)/2
. This expansion is justified when the temperature is large,
T\gg\omega_{\mathrm{p}}
, and thus the bath occupation number
n_{\mathrm{B}}\approx T/\omega_{\mathrm{p}}
is also large,
n_{\mathrm{B}}\gg 1
. Using definitions of the rates
\gamma_{\ell}-\gamma_{\mathrm{g}}=2\kappa
and
\gamma_{\ell}+\gamma_{\mathrm{g}}=2\kappa\coth(\omega_{\mathrm{p}}/2T)\simeq 4\kappa T/\omega_{\mathrm{p}}
in the high temperature limit, one retrieves the classical effective Hamiltonian (
16
).

As in the classical case, in the term
K_{1}
that describes the second drive all Fourier modes must be kept. Using Eq. (
22
) dropping
I^{\mathrm{q}}
and with
\theta\to\underline{\theta}
we obtain

K_{1}(I,p;t)=2\alpha\sum_{n}\sinh\!\left(\frac{np}{2}\right)c_{n}(I)\,e^{-\text{i}(n\underline{\theta}(I)-\nu t-\varphi_{\mathrm{d}})}+\mathrm{c.c.}

(25)

The classical result (
17
) can again be found by expansion in the auxiliary variable
p
. Indeed, the first order expansion of the sinh function leads to Eq. (
17
).

Equations (
14
), (
23
) - (
25
) provide the full description of a weakly dissipative quantum parametric oscillator, including the effects of the spectroscopic drive,
\omega_{\mathrm{d}}
. Whereas expanding the effective Hamiltonian,
K=K_{0}+K_{1}
to the second order in
p
fully recovers the classical results, the opposite transition, from classical to quantum, is far less obvious. This is due to exponential,
\text{e}^{-np}
and
\sinh(np/2)
, functions in the quantum version. Upon quantization they represent shift operators of the action
I
by an integer
n
, reflecting the underlying Bohr-Sommerfeld quantization of the action.

III
Phase Slip Rate

We now turn to the computation of the phase slip rate. Without the spectroscopic drive the PDO respects parity symmetry
\phi\to-\phi
.
The two classical ground states
\phi=\pm\phi_{0}
spontaneously break this symmetry.
At long times the symmetry is restored by rare phase slips events, in which the system traverses from the vicinity of one of the ground states in the phase space to the vicinity of another over the quasi-energy barrier.
This results in a bimodal probability distribution over both wells of Fig.
1
at long times.

We will consider the case where the frequency detuning
|\nu|
exceeds the phase-slip rate
W_{\mathrm{ps}}
of the switching events. In this case the rates of switching between the two states are equal, so we do not distinguish from which state the switching occurs.
Within the path integral formalism, the phase-slip rate
W_{\mathrm{ps}}
can be computed using the saddle point approximation,

W_{\mathrm{ps}}\simeq C\,\text{e}^{\text{i}S_{\mathrm{inst}}}

(26)

where the prefactor
C
has only a sub-exponential dependence on the parameters and
S_{\mathrm{inst}}
is the action evaluated for an instanton trajectory that connects the classically stable state and the saddle point
\phi=0
of the classical fluctuation-free trajectory. For switching from the state
\phi_{0}
, the boundary conditions are
\phi_{\mathrm{inst}}(-\infty)\to\phi_{0}
and
\phi_{\mathrm{inst}}(\infty)\to 0
, whereas for switching from the state
-\phi_{0}
we have
\phi_{\mathrm{inst}}(-\infty)\to-\phi_{0}
.
In the weak damping limit, in the both cases the instanton is a trajectory that starts at the bottom of the well at
I=0
and activates to the top of the quasi-energy barrier,
I_{\mathrm{top}}
.
The computation of the switching rate exponent thus reduces to identifying such an instanton.

In the language of open quantum systems, these phase-slips lead to the dynamical restoration of
weak
parity symmetry in the sense of Refs.
1
;
32
.
The problem of symmetry restoration of
strong
parity symmetry was considered in
46
, which studied a
T=0
quantum PDO with nonlinear friction.
In both cases, the exponent of the instanton action and thus the phase slip rate is related to (in the present context – real-valued) dissipative gap in the spectrum of the Lindbladian super-operator.
In the classical stochastic setting, one may similarly argue that the phase slip rate characterizes the dissipative gap of the corresponding Fokker-Planck operator.

III.1
Classical Activation

The problem of identifying the instanton trajectory requires understanding the classical mechanics described by the effective Hamiltonian Eq. (
16
). Being of the standard form for Langevin processes with multiplicative noise, the activation path is a time-reversed version of the fluctuationless relaxation path. This owes to the presence of an emergent detailed balance in the classical theory of Markovian systems with one dynamical variable, despite being driven out of equilibrium.

The activation and relaxation trajectories are lines in the semiclassical phase portrait on which
K_{0}=0
. The phase portrait is shown in Fig.
3
.
The corresponding Hamilton equations are:

\partial_{t}I=-2\kappa I+\frac{4\kappa T}{\omega_{\mathrm{p}}}\,\Gamma(I)\,p,

(27a)

\partial_{t}p=2\kappa p-\frac{2\kappa T}{\omega_{\mathrm{p}}}\,\partial_{I}\Gamma(I)\,p^{2}.

(27b)

From these, one may identify three invariant lines with
K_{0}(I,p)=0
, which connect three fixed points.
The
p=0
line corresponds to the fluctuationless relaxation, along which the evolution of the action is
\partial_{t}I_{\mathrm{rel}}=-2\kappa I_{\mathrm{rel}}
. This trajectory connects the saddle point (
p=0
and
I=I_{\mathrm{top}}
) to the bottom of the well (
p=0
and
I=0
).
The
I=0
line also defines an invariant subspace, along which positive
p
flows from
p=0
to a second fixed point at
p_{*}=\omega_{\mathrm{p}}/(T\partial_{I}\Gamma(0))
.
The third line is the instanton path, which connects the fixed point at
p=p_{*}
and
I=0
to the top of the well
p=0
and
I=I_{\mathrm{top}}
, along which both coordinates are non-zero. Motion along this line describes the activation process. It is defined implicitly through

p_{\mathrm{inst}}(I_{\mathrm{inst}})=\frac{\omega_{\mathrm{p}}}{T}\frac{I_{\mathrm{inst}}}{\Gamma(I_{\mathrm{inst}})}.

(28)

Along such instanton path the action evolves according to
\partial_{t}I_{\mathrm{inst}}=2\kappa I_{\mathrm{inst}}
, cf. Eq. (
27a
), realizing the time-reversed version of the relaxation trajectory. In fact, for an underdamped thermal equilibrium system this trajectory was found by Kramers
26
by solving the Fokker-Planck equation.

Figure 3:
Phase portrait of the effective Hamiltonian
K_{0}
from Eq. (
16
) plotted with
\Delta/2\lambda=0.3
Bold blue lines show the separatrix trajectories, which are the
K_{0}=0
lines. They connect the fixed points, which are shown by dots. The instanton action Eq. (
29
) is indicated by the shaded region.

Thus, following Eq. (
26
), the phase-slip rate exponent is given by

\text{i}S_{\mathrm{inst}}=-\int\!\text{d}t\,p_{\mathrm{inst}}\partial_{t}I_{\mathrm{inst}}=-\frac{\omega_{\mathrm{p}}}{T}\int\limits_{0}^{I_{\mathrm{top}}}\!\text{d}I\,\frac{I}{\Gamma(I)}.

(29)

A closed-form analytical result is hardly possible, but it is straightforward to numerically evaluate this expression.
The parametric dependence of the instanton action can be further simplified by noting that the problem can be made dimensionless through a rescaling of the quasi-energy
E\to(2\lambda^{2}/g)\tilde{E}
and time
t\to(1/2\lambda)\tilde{t}
. With this, the action and its auxiliary partner are rescaled as
I\to(\lambda/g)\tilde{I}
and
p(I)\to\tilde{p}(\tilde{I})
. The final result is

\text{i}S_{\mathrm{inst}}=-\frac{\omega_{\mathrm{p}}\lambda}{Tg}\,R(\Delta/2\lambda)

(30)

where
R=\int\text{d}\tilde{I}\,\tilde{p}_{\mathrm{inst}}(\tilde{I})
is a dimensionless constant of order unity whose value depends only on the detuning parameter,
\Delta
.
This together with Eq. (
29
) concur with results reported in Ref.
9
.

III.2
Quantum activation

As discussed above, the quantum problem matches the classical problem in the limit of large
T
. They disagree, however, in the opposite limit. Equation (
30
) predicts that the switching rate goes to zero as
T\to 0
. In contrast, the quantum result shows that it saturates to a finite value. The finite rate of phase slips at zero temperature is driven by the quantum fluctuations of the bath and is known as quantum activation
35
.

The quantum instanton is given by the
K_{0}(I,p)=0
lines of the Keldysh Hamiltonian, Eq. (
23
).
The phase portrait is similar to the classical theory in that it consists of three of these lines connecting the fixed points, which generally have the same meaning as in the classical theory.
The
p=0
line flowing from
I=I_{\mathrm{top}}
to
I=0
describes fluctuationless relaxation, along which the time evolution may be obtained by re-summing the Fourier series of
\partial_{p}K_{0}|_{p=0}
. The result is the same as in the classical theory,
\partial_{t}I_{\mathrm{rel}}=-(\gamma_{\ell}-\gamma_{\mathrm{g}})I_{\mathrm{rel}}=-2\kappa I_{\mathrm{rel}}
.
The
I=0
line connects the bottom of the well to a finite
p
fixed point
p_{*}
with a shifted valued compared to the classical theory, an exact expression for which can be found in Appendix
B
.

The identification of the instanton line is made easier using an alternative representation for
K_{0}=\gamma_{\ell}K_{\ell}+\gamma_{\mathrm{g}}K_{\mathrm{g}}
obtained by re-summing the Fourier series,

K_{\ell,\mathrm{g}}(I)\!=\!\!\int\!\frac{\text{d}\theta}{2\pi}\big(\underline{\bar{\phi}}(\theta\pm\text{i}p/2;I)\underline{\phi}(\theta\mp\text{i}p/2;I)-\underline{\bar{\phi}}(\theta;I)\underline{\phi}(\theta;I)\big)

(31)

In this representation one may immediately identify a
K_{0}=0
line with
-\text{i}p(I)=\omega(I)\mathrm{Im}(t_{2}(I))
where
t_{2}(I)
is the complex period of the elliptic function solutions
\underline{\phi}(t;I)
. Such a choice annihilates the integrands of both
K_{\ell,\mathrm{g}}
. This determines the tunneling rate of the closed system with the Hamiltonian Eq. (
3
).
It turns out that there is always another
K_{0}=0
line along which the action accrues a smaller value. This trajectory, which corresponds to quantum activation, leads to an exponentially larger switching rate compared with the coherent tunneling process and is thus the primary mechanism responsible for phase slips.

We first consider the instanton line for zero temperature. This corresponds to
\gamma_{\mathrm{g}}=0
and thus
K_{0}=K_{\ell}
.
The instanton line must annihilate the integrand of Eq. (
31
). This turns out to be possible for
p_{\mathrm{inst}}<\omega\mathrm{Im}(t_{2})
due to a certain quasi-periodicity of the classical solutions
\underline{\phi}
, which obey the condition
\underline{\phi}(t-t_{\mathrm{Q}})=\underline{\bar{\phi}}(t)
. The quasi-period time
t_{\mathrm{Q}}(I)
is purely imaginary and always smaller in magnitude than
\mathrm{Im}(t_{2}(I))/2
; the functional form of
t_{\mathrm{Q}}
and further details are discussed in Appendix
A
. This gives the instanton implicitly as a function of action

p_{\mathrm{inst}}^{(T=0)}(I_{\mathrm{inst}})=-2\text{i}\omega(I_{\mathrm{inst}})t_{\mathrm{Q}}(I_{\mathrm{inst}}).

(32)

Feeding this back into the equation of motion for
I
gives
\partial_{t}I_{\mathrm{inst}}=\gamma_{\ell}I_{\mathrm{inst}}=2\kappa I_{\mathrm{inst}}
, which is exactly the time-reversed relaxation trajectory. This may be traced back to the fact that at
T=0
detailed balance emerges in the quantum system, similar to the classical result
35
.
The
T=0
phase portrait of
K_{0}
along with the corresponding instanton trajectories are shown in Fig.
4
.
The switching rate exponent is given by substituting this result into the action,

\text{i}S_{\mathrm{inst}}=-2\int\limits_{0}^{I_{\mathrm{top}}}\text{d}I\,\omega(I)|t_{\mathrm{Q}}(I)|\equiv-\frac{\lambda}{g}\,R^{(T=0)}(\Delta/2\lambda),

(33)

where
R^{(T=0)}
is a dimensionless constant that depends only on the dimensionless ratio
\Delta/2\lambda
.

, Eq. (

) for

(a) and

) is indicated by the shaded region. The red dashed lines show the path

and the yellow dashed lines show the

instanton. Note that this instanton trajectory diverges in panel (b) at
I=I_{\mathrm{D}}(\Delta/2\lambda)
. This is a generic feature of the
T=0
instanton when
\Delta/2\lambda\leq 0
. The singularity of
p_{\mathrm{inst}}(I)
is integrable, resulting in a finite switching rate. This singularity is no longer present for
T\to 0
because
I_{\mathrm{D}}>I_{\mathrm{F}}
for all values of
\Delta/2\lambda
.

The finite temperature problem differs in a qualitative way from the
T=0
problem in that the instanton
p_{\mathrm{inst}}
comes from a cancellation between
K_{\ell}
and
K_{\mathrm{g}}
rather than annihilating their integrands individually.
The exact form of the finite temperature instanton is not easy to deduce analytically, however it may be obtained in certain limits. The resulting phase slip time interpolates between the
T\to\infty
limit, which matches the classical result, and the
T\to 0
limit.
This limit is not always equivalent to the
T=0
result.
Such fragility
35
of the zero temperature result indicates the breakdown of the semiclassical approximation for some very small temperature scale at which a crossover between the
T=0
and
T\to 0
results occurs.

The origin of the zero temperature fragility can be traced to the series representation of the Keldysh Hamiltonian, Eq. (
23
). Following Eq. (
24
) for
T=0
,
\gamma_{\mathrm{g}}=0
and so the transition rates are
W_{n}\propto|c_{n}|^{2}
, which fall off as
\exp(-np_{<})
for
n\gg 1
and
\exp(-|n|p_{>})
for
-n\gg 1
(expressions for
p_{\gtrless}\equiv p_{\gtrless}(I;\Delta/2\lambda)
are given in Appendix
A
). Therefore the series
\sum_{n}W_{n}\exp(-np)
has an asymmetric radius of convergence
-p_{<}<p<p_{>}
. It is important that
p_{<}(I;\Delta/2\lambda)\neq p_{>}(I;\Delta/2\lambda)
in a broad range of
I
and
\Delta/2\lambda
.
For any finite
T>0
the additional factor of
|c_{-n}|^{2}
in
W_{n}
changes the radius of convergence to be symmetric,
-\mathrm{min}(p_{>},p_{<})<p<\mathrm{min}(p_{>},p_{<})
.

It turns out that in a certain region of
\Delta/2\lambda
there exists a range
I>I_{\mathrm{F}}(\Delta/2\lambda)
where the zero temperature instanton from Eq. (
32
) falls outside the reduced radius of convergence,
p_{<}(I;\Delta/2\lambda)<p^{(T=0)}_{\mathrm{inst}}(I;\Delta/2\lambda)
.
In this range the limit
T\to 0
brings the instanton to the edge of the radius of convergence, so that
p_{\mathrm{inst}}^{(T\to 0)}=p_{<}
, or in total

p_{\mathrm{inst}}^{(T\to 0)}=\begin{cases}p_{\mathrm{inst}}^{(T=0)},&I\in[0,I_{\mathrm{F}})\\
p_{<},&I\in(I_{\mathrm{F}},I_{\mathrm{top}})\end{cases}

(34)

where
I_{\mathrm{F}}
defines the value of the action above which the zero temperature result is fragile.
This piecewise form of the
T\to 0
limit of the instanton is shown in Fig.
4
.
The fragility of the zero temperature result indicates the breakdown of the semiclassical approximation for some very small temperature scale, at which a crossover between the
T=0
and
T\to 0
results occurs
35
. For a resonantly driven oscillator the crossover temperature was investigated in Ref.
23
.

IV
Logarithmic Susceptibility

We now turn to the computation of the logarithmic susceptibility (LS). In the presence of a finite
\alpha
– the spectroscopic signal amplitude – there is an exponential correction to the phase slip rate. The correction to the exponent may be computed perturbatively
S_{\mathrm{inst}}\simeq S_{\mathrm{inst}}^{(0)}+S_{\mathrm{inst}}^{(1)}
to first order in
\alpha
.

This problem was recently solved for the case when the linear drive is at half resonance with the parametric drive
\omega_{\mathrm{d}}=\omega_{\mathrm{p}}

3
;
4
. In this case
\nu=0
and so the rotating-wave approximation results in a time-independent theory of switching in a tilted double well in the phase space.
The technical approach in
3
was to work in the unperturbed action-angle variables and absorb the tilting of the double well into perturbative corrections to the Fourier coefficients in Eq. (
8
). The perturbative correction to the instanton
p_{\mathrm{inst}}=p_{\mathrm{inst}}^{(0)}+\alpha p_{\mathrm{inst}}^{(1)}
is then obtained by solving
K_{0}+K_{1}=0
for
p_{\mathrm{inst}}^{(1)}(I)
at order
\alpha
.
It results in an asymmetric correction to the phase slip rate, so that switching out of the shallower well into the deeper well becomes exponentially more likely.
The results discussed there can be extended to the regime of very small
\nu
by replacing
\varphi_{\mathrm{d}}\to\varphi_{\mathrm{d}}+\nu t
. This is valid in the adiabatic limit
\nu\ll W_{\mathrm{ps}}
, in which there are many phase-slip events over a single period of modulation of the wells.

Here, as indicated earlier, we consider the regime where
\nu=\omega_{\mathrm{d}}-\omega_{\mathrm{p}}
is large enough so that the modulation of the double well is fast compared to the phase slip rate, such that many modulation periods occur in-between successive phase slips. This is qualitatively different from the adiabatic limit. Over timescales large compared to
1/\nu
the symmetry of the problem is restored and switching rates between the wells are again symmetric. However, the spectroscopic drive still leads to an exponentially strong increase in the switching rates. Whenever the frequency
\nu
or its integer multiples resonate with the motion at the bottom of the wells this increase becomes particularly large.

On a technical level, this problem is unavoidably time-dependent and so requires an alternative approach from that used for the half resonant linear drive.
Such an approach is provided by the standard machinery for computing time-dependent perturbations to the instanton action (see Chapter 4 of
24
for a review). The first-order correction is given by evaluating the perturbing action
\text{i}S_{1}=\int\text{d}t\,K_{1}(I,p;t)
along the unperturbed instanton trajectory,

\text{i}S_{\mathrm{inst}}^{(1)}=-\min_{t_{0}}\int\text{d}t\,K_{1}\big(I_{\mathrm{inst}}(t-t_{0}),p_{\mathrm{inst}}(t-t_{0});t\big),

(35)

where the minimization is taken over the central time
t_{0}
of the bare instanton trajectory. The time-dependence of the perturbation breaks the time translation invariance of the unperturbed result, pinning the instanton center to the time where the switching event is the most likely to occur
44
.
This formula is applicable to both the classical and quantum problems, which are treated on the same footing in the action formulation. For both, this can be understood as the Melnikov theorem
24
applied over the effective phase space guaranteeing that the perturbed instanton remains on the stable manifold of the saddle point of the fluctuation-free classical motion.

Using the specific form of
K_{1}
, either the classical result from Eq. (
17
) or the quantum result from Eq. (
25
), Eq. (
35
) becomes

\text{i}S_{\mathrm{inst}}^{(1)}=-\alpha\,\underset{t_{0}}{\mathrm{min}}\left\{\int\!\text{d}t\ \chi(t-t_{0})\,e^{\text{i}(\nu t+\varphi_{\mathrm{d}})}+\mathrm{c.c.}\right\},

(36)

where, following Ref.
44
,
\chi(t)
defines the logarithmic susceptibility. The LS is essentially a response function for the switching rate exponent. Its computation for the classical and quantum problems are presented below.

IV.1
Classical Logarithmic Susceptibility

The computation of the LS for a classical PDO was originally performed in Ref.
42
.
The method employed there in the analysis of the LS in the weak damping limit is to express the LS as a sum over different harmonic contributions and argue that, for certain values of
\nu
, the resonant harmonic gives the dominant contribution, which can be found by the steepest descent. The strongest response comes from the first harmonic with
n=1
, when
\nu\lesssim\omega_{\mathrm{min}}
. When
\nu
exceeds
\omega_{\mathrm{min}}
, the external drive can no longer resonate with the dominant harmonic of the intrawell motion, causing a sharp drop off in the LS. Resonance with sub-leading harmonics with
n>1
is still possible however, but the response is weaker. Increasing
\nu
produces a series of drop-offs in the LS corresponding to
\nu
exceeding integer multiples of
\omega_{\mathrm{min}}
.
For the classical theory, this leads to resonant peaks in the LS for
\nu
near integer multiples of
\omega_{\mathrm{min}}
.

Here, we compute the LS by using the form of
K_{1}
from Eq. (
17
), which appears in the form of a series. This can be decomposed into harmonics
\chi(t)=\sum_{n}\chi_{n}(t)
. Additionally, note that Eq. (
36
) is just the Fourier transform of
\chi
, minimized over an overall phase. After shifting the time argument and absorbing the phase shift
\varphi_{\mathrm{d}}
into the minimization, the correction to the switching rate exponent becomes:

\text{i}S_{\mathrm{inst}}^{(1)}=-\alpha\,\underset{t_{0}}{\mathrm{min}}\bigg\{\text{e}^{\text{i}\nu t_{0}}\sum_{n}\chi_{n}(\nu)+\mathrm{c.c.}\bigg\},

(37)

with
\chi_{n}(\nu)
the Fourier transforms of
\chi_{n}(t)
,

\chi_{n}(\nu)=\!\int\!\text{d}t\ \text{e}^{-\text{i}(n\underline{\theta}(t)-\nu t)}\,c_{n}\big(I_{\mathrm{inst}}(t)\big)np_{\mathrm{inst}}(t),

(38)

where
\underline{\theta}(t)=\int^{t}\text{d}t\,\omega(I_{\mathrm{inst}}(t))
, while
\partial_{t}\underline{\theta}(t)=\omega(I_{\mathrm{inst}}(t))

and
\partial_{t}^{2}\underline{\theta}(t)=\partial_{I}\omega(I_{\mathrm{inst}})\partial_{t}I_{\mathrm{inst}}=2\kappa I_{\mathrm{inst}}\partial_{I}\omega(I_{\mathrm{inst}})
, where the instanton equation of motion,
\partial_{t}I_{\mathrm{inst}}=2\kappa I_{\mathrm{inst}}
was used.
The integral in Eq. (
38
) can be evaluated via the saddle point approximation, where all terms outside the exponent are treated as slow. This brings the saddle point condition
n\omega(I_{\mathrm{inst}}(t))=\nu
,
which selects a certain resonant time
t_{\mathrm{r}}<\log(I_{\mathrm{top}}/2\kappa)
, along the instanton path
I_{\mathrm{inst}}(t)=\text{e}^{2\kappa t}
and the corresponding action
I=I_{\mathrm{inst}}(t_{\mathrm{r}})
, such that
0<I<I_{\mathrm{top}}
, where the saddle point condition is satisfied. The corresponding LS, up to an overall phase factor, is given by

\chi_{n}(\nu)\simeq\frac{\omega_{\mathrm{p}}}{T}\,\sqrt{\frac{\pi\,|n|\,I}{\kappa\,|\partial_{I}\omega(I)|}}\,\frac{c_{n}(I)}{\Gamma(I)}\ \Bigg|_{n\omega(I)=\nu},

(39)

where Eq. (
28
) was employed.

Function
\omega(I)
is positive and monotonically decreasing from
\omega(0)=\omega_{\mathrm{min}}
down to
\omega(I_{\mathrm{top}})=0
. Therefore for a given value of
\nu
the saddle point condition is only satisfiable for sufficiently large values of
n
. For
(|m|-1)\omega_{\mathrm{min}}<|\nu|\leq|m|\omega_{\mathrm{min}}
, one must choose
|n|\geq|m|
.
When the saddle point condition is not met, the functions
\chi_{n}(\nu)
cannot be evaluated by a saddle point approximation and provide only small background contributions to
\chi_{n}(\nu)
.

For a given value of
\nu
, it turns out that the harmonic
\chi_{n}(\nu)
corresponding to the smallest value
n=m
, for which the saddle point condition can be satisfied, gives the dominant contribution to the LS. This is a consequence of the exponential decrease of the Fourier coefficients,
c_{n}
, with
n
, see Appendix
B
.

Because of this, a good approximation is provided by keeping only the harmonic corresponding to the smallest value of
n
satisfying the saddle point condition.
Thus, for
(n-1)\omega_{\mathrm{min}}<\nu<n\omega_{\mathrm{min}}
Eq. (
37
) results in the correction to the switching rate exponent,

\text{i}S_{\mathrm{inst}}^{(1)}\simeq 2\alpha|\chi_{n}(\nu)|,

(40)

where the minimization over
t_{0}
has eliminated the overall phase.
This expression is peaked near the upper boundary of the frequency interval
\nu\simeq n\omega_{\mathrm{min}}
, where
I\simeq 0
.

The shape of each resonant peak can be extracted from Eq. (

and expanding to leading order in
\delta\nu
. The saddle point condition for
\chi_{n}
then becomes
\omega(I)=\omega_{\mathrm{min}}-\delta\nu/|n|
.
This corresponds to early times along the instanton path and therefore small action

I\simeq\delta\nu/|n\omega_{I}|
, where

\omega_{I}\equiv\partial_{I}\omega(I)|_{I=0}=-g\,\frac{\lambda-\Delta/8}{\lambda-\Delta/2}.

(41)

This, along with the limiting forms of
\Gamma

for small

.
In particular,
\Gamma(I\to 0)\propto I
and

|\chi_{1}(\nu)|\underset{\delta\nu\ll|\omega_{I}|}{\simeq}a_{1}\frac{\omega_{\mathrm{p}}}{T}\sqrt{\frac{\pi}{\kappa|\omega_{I}|}}\,\left(1+b_{1}\frac{\delta\nu}{|\omega_{I}|}\right)\Theta(\omega_{\mathrm{min}}-\nu),

(42)

where
a_{1}

For sub-leading resonances with

|\chi_{n}(\nu)|\underset{\delta\nu\ll|\omega_{I}|}{\simeq}a_{n}\frac{\omega_{\mathrm{p}}}{T}\sqrt{\frac{\pi}{\kappa|\omega_{I}|}}\,\Bigg|\frac{\delta\nu}{n\omega_{I}}\Bigg|^{\frac{|n|-1}{2}}\Theta(|n|\omega_{\mathrm{min}}-|\nu|).

(43)

The Heaviside theta function

(for a fixed

.
This reproduces the main results of Ref.

The precise form of the constants

limiting forms of

discussed in Appendix

a_{1}=\sqrt{\frac{\omega_{\mathrm{min}}(\bar{\omega}+\omega_{\mathrm{min}})}{2\bar{\omega}^{2}}},

(44)

with
\bar{\omega}=4\lambda-\Delta
.

Interestingly, for
\Delta<0
there is a narrow region of frequencies for which the leading-order harmonic does not provide the dominant contribution to the LS. This occurs close to the frequency
\nu_{n}=n\omega(I_{\mathrm{D}})
, at which

\chi_{n}

The value of

corresponds to the point where the zero temperature quantum instanton in the absence of the spectroscopic drive diverges, see also Fig.

In the vicinity of

A significant feature of the LS is the scaling with the dissipation strength
\chi\sim\kappa^{-1/2}
. Because this result is valid in the limit of weak dissipation, this scaling provides a significant enhancement to the magnitude of the LS. As will be shown below, this scaling persists in the quantum theory at
T=0

4

This implies that the non-commensurate second drive

produces a parametrically larger response, resulting in a bigger enhancement of the phase-slip rate.
It can be understood to be a result of the resonance of the second drive with the intrawell motion.

IV.2
Quantum Logarithmic Susceptibility

Equation (
42
) predicts the LS to diverge in the limit of
T\to 0
, which would signal the breakdown of the perturbative expansion for sufficiently small

. Like the unperturbed switching rate, quantum effects curtail this divergence of the LS and give a finite result down to zero temperature.

The same methodology described above for the classical result in the weak-damping limit can be employed for the quantum problem. The leading correction to the switching rate exponent is provided by Eq. (
35
), where the Fourier transform of the harmonics
\chi_{n}
are obtained from

given by the quantum theory, Eq. (

\chi_{n}(\nu)=\!\int\!\text{d}t\text{e}^{-\text{i}(n\underline{\theta}(t)-\nu t)}c_{n}\big(I_{\mathrm{inst}}(t)\big)2\sinh\left(\frac{np_{\mathrm{inst}}(t)}{2}\right)

(45)

The integral may be evaluated via saddle point approximation, with all terms outside the exponent treated as slow,

\chi_{n}(\nu)=\frac{\sqrt{2\pi}\,c_{n}(I)\,2\sinh(np_{\mathrm{inst}}(I)/2)}{\sqrt{n\partial_{I}\omega(I)\sum_{n^{\prime}}n^{\prime}W_{n^{\prime}}(I)\text{e}^{-n^{\prime}p_{\mathrm{inst}}(I)}}}\Bigg|_{n\omega(I)=\nu},

(46)

where

is given for

by Eqs. (
32
), (
34
), see also Fig.
4
.
This gives the quantum analogue to Eq. (
39
).

Just as for the classical theory, for a given value of
\nu
the harmonic
\chi_{n}
corresponding to the smallest value of
n
for which the saddle point condition is satisfiable provides the dominant contribution to the switching rate. Despite the different form of Eq. (
46
) compared to Eq. (

\nu/\omega_{\mathrm{min}}<n<\nu/\omega_{\mathrm{min}}+1

\omega_{\mathrm{min}}

This may be obtained by putting
|\nu|=|n|\omega_{\mathrm{min}}-\delta\nu

\delta\nu
. Like in the classical problem this requires evaluating Eq. (
46

. In this limit the instanton is replaced by its value at the fixed point
p_{\mathrm{inst}}(I\to 0)=p_{*}

The sum in the denominator originates from
\partial_{t}I_{\mathrm{inst}}(I)
from the saddle point condition; for early times along the instanton path

(47a)

(47b)

(48)

with

At high temperature

V
Near Vicinity of the Bifurcation Point

Close to the bifurcation point
\Delta\to 2\lambda
, the frequency of the bottom of the well becomes small,
\omega_{\mathrm{min}}\to 0
. Once
\kappa\sim\omega_{\mathrm{min}}
the dissipation can no longer be regarded as small and the motion becomes overdamped.
When this occurs, there is no longer any coherent motion with which the external drive can resonate. The LS as a function of
\nu
has a simple monotonically decreasing form. The sharp drops near integer multiples of
\omega_{\mathrm{min}}
discussed in the previous section are absent owing to the lack of resonance.
In this regime, the evolution of the angle coordinate is no longer fast and so the action-angle variables are no longer a useful choice of coordinates and a new approach to calculating the instanton is needed.

A finite value of
\kappa
shifts the location in phase space of the classically stable attractors. The two fixed points of Eq. (
9
) in the absence of noise are
\phi=\pm\phi_{0}
, where
\phi_{0}=|\phi_{0}|\text{e}^{\text{i}\varphi}
with
|\phi_{0}|^{2}=(\sqrt{4\lambda^{2}-\kappa^{2}}-\Delta)/g

and
2\varphi=\pi-\arcsin(\kappa/2\lambda)
.
These fixed points exist so long as
\Delta<\Delta_{\mathrm{B}}
, where
\Delta_{\mathrm{B}}=\sqrt{4\lambda^{2}-\kappa^{2}}
is the critical detuning, when accounting for a finite dissipation strength.
For small
\kappa
,
\Delta_{\mathrm{B}}\simeq 2\lambda
as was used in the preceding sections.
As the dissipationless bifurcation point
\Delta/2\lambda=1
is approached, the function
H_{0}
becomes very flat along the axis parallel to the line in phase space that contains the fixed points
\pm\phi_{0}
and
0
.
This line is rotated off the imaginary axis for finite values of
\kappa
.
The real coordinate corresponding to this direction thus evolves slowly, while the coordinate corresponding to the perpendicular direction dissipates at a fast rate.
The switching rate can be well-approximated by first integrating out the fast perpendicular coordinate.

In the classical setting, this is conveniently achieved by going to the path integral description for the original Langevin equation Eq. (
9
), which is obtained by following a similar procedure to Eq. (
II.2
) for the complex coordinates
\phi
and
\bar{\phi}
. The delta functions that contain the Langevin equations for
\phi
and
\bar{\phi}
can be written as integrals over complex auxiliary fields, which will be respectively denoted
\bar{\phi}^{\mathrm{q}}
and
\phi^{\mathrm{q}}
. After integrating out the noise, the partition function is then a path integral
Z=\int\text{D}\bar{\phi}^{\alpha}\text{D}\phi^{\alpha}\exp(\text{i}S[\bar{\phi}^{\alpha},\phi^{\alpha}])
over the complex physical and auxiliary fields
\phi^{\alpha}=[\phi\ \phi^{\mathrm{q}}]
, where the action is
S=S_{0}+S_{1}
,

S_{0}[\bar{\phi}^{\alpha},\phi^{\alpha}]=\int\text{d}t\Big(\bar{\phi}^{\mathrm{q}}(\text{i}\partial_{t}-\Delta+\text{i}\kappa)\phi+\bar{\phi}(\text{i}\partial_{t}-\Delta-\text{i}\kappa)\phi^{\mathrm{q}}\\
-2\lambda(\bar{\phi}^{\mathrm{q}}\bar{\phi}+\phi^{\mathrm{q}}\phi)+\frac{2T\kappa}{\omega_{\mathrm{p}}}\,\bar{\phi}^{\mathrm{q}}\phi^{\mathrm{q}}-\frac{g}{2}(\bar{\phi}^{\mathrm{q}}\phi+\bar{\phi}\phi^{\mathrm{q}})\bar{\phi}\phi\Big),

(50a)

S_{1}[\bar{\phi}^{\alpha},\phi^{\alpha}]=-\alpha\int\text{d}t\big(\bar{\phi}^{\mathrm{q}}\text{e}^{-\text{i}(\nu t+\varphi_{\mathrm{d}})}+\phi^{\mathrm{q}}\text{e}^{\text{i}(\nu t+\varphi_{\mathrm{d}})}\big).

(50b)

The decomposition into slow and fast modes is achieved by decomposing
\phi
into a set of two real variables
\phi=\text{i}\text{e}^{-\text{i}\varphi}(X+\text{i}Y)
and
\phi^{\mathrm{q}}=\text{e}^{-\text{i}\varphi}(P_{Y}-\text{i}P_{X})
, where the integration contour of the auxiliary variables is in the imaginary direction
P_{X},P_{Y}\in\text{i}\mathbb{R}
.
The resulting unperturbed fluctuationless
P_{X}=0=P_{Y}
equations of motion for the physical fields are then

\partial_{t}X=\Big(\Delta-\Delta_{\mathrm{B}}+\frac{g}{2}(X^{2}+Y^{2})\Big)Y,

(51a)

\partial_{t}Y=-2\kappa Y-\Big(\Delta+\Delta_{\mathrm{B}}+\frac{g}{2}(X^{2}+Y^{2})\Big)X

(51b)

For
\Delta
close to
\Delta_{\mathrm{B}}
, the
X
coordinate evolves slowly while the
Y
coordinate is fast.
Therefore
Y
and
P_{Y}
can be integrated out at the linear level to obtain an effective theory for the slow variables
X
and
P_{X}
.
This is achieved by neglecting the non-linear terms and fluctuations of
Y
and keeping only the term
P_{Y}(2\kappa Y+(\Delta+\Delta_{\mathrm{B}})X)
.
Integrating out
Y
and
P_{Y}
then imposes the replacement
Y\to-(\Delta+\Delta_{\mathrm{B}})X/2\kappa
.

To make this more precise, introduce
\Delta=\Delta_{\mathrm{B}}-2\lambda\epsilon
where
\epsilon
is a small dimensionless parameter that measures distance to the bifurcation. The coordinate
Y
is fast in the limit
\epsilon\to 0
, so the resulting effective theory for
X
should keep track of each term order by order in
\epsilon
.
This is conveniently achieved by going to dimensionless coordinates
X=\sqrt{4\lambda\epsilon/g}\,Q
,
P_{X}=\sqrt{4\lambda\epsilon^{3}/g}\,P
,
t=\tilde{t}/2\lambda\epsilon
.
The final result of this procedure is a path integral over two coordinates:

Z=\int\text{D}Q\text{D}P\ \text{e}^{\text{i}S[Q,P]},

(52a)

\text{i}S[Q,P]=-\frac{8\lambda}{g}\epsilon^{2}\int\text{d}\tilde{t}\big(P\partial_{\tilde{t}}Q-K(Q,P)\big),

(52b)

where the effective Hamiltonian
K=K_{0}+K_{1}
is given to leading order in
\epsilon
by:

K_{0}(Q,P)=-P\,U^{\prime}(Q)+DP^{2},

(53a)

K_{1}(Q,P;\tilde{t})=\frac{\alpha}{4}\sqrt{\frac{g}{\lambda^{3}\epsilon^{3}}}\cos\big((\nu/2\lambda\epsilon)\tilde{t}+\varphi_{\mathrm{d}}+\varphi\big).

(53b)

Here
U(Q)=-(\Delta_{\mathrm{B}}/2\kappa)Q^{2}+(\Delta_{\mathrm{B}}\lambda^{2}/\kappa^{3})Q^{4}
and
D=\kappa T/\omega_{\mathrm{p}}\lambda
.

This is the effective Hamiltonian for a particle undergoing frictional relaxation in a 1D quartic double-well potential. As such, the activation path is given by
P_{\mathrm{inst}}(Q)=U^{\prime}(Q)/D
and the switching rate is the height of the barrier,

\text{i}S_{\mathrm{inst}}^{(0)}=-\frac{8\lambda}{g}\epsilon^{2}\!\int\limits_{Q_{\mathrm{min}}}^{0}\!\text{d}Q\ \frac{U^{\prime}(Q)}{D}=-\frac{\Delta_{\mathrm{B}}\omega_{\mathrm{p}}\epsilon^{2}}{2gT}.

(54)

The instanton solution
Q_{\mathrm{inst}}(t)
is the standard time-reversed relaxation path
Q_{\mathrm{inst}}(t)=Q_{\mathrm{rel}}(-t)
, where
\partial_{t}Q_{\mathrm{rel}}=-U^{\prime}(Q_{\mathrm{rel}})
.
It is straight forward to evaluate this directly and substitute the result into
S_{1}
and subsequently minimize over the instanton center time
t_{0}
to obtain the correction to the switching rate exponent
7

\text{i}S_{\mathrm{inst}}^{(1)}(\nu)=\frac{\alpha\omega_{\mathrm{p}}}{2T}\sqrt{\frac{\epsilon}{\pi\lambda g}}\bigg|\boldsymbol{\Gamma}\bigg(\frac{1-\text{i}\upsilon}{2}\bigg)\boldsymbol{\Gamma}\bigg(1+\frac{\text{i}\upsilon}{2}\bigg)\bigg|,

(55)

where
\boldsymbol{\Gamma}
is the gamma function and
\upsilon=\kappa\nu/2\lambda\Delta_{\mathrm{B}}\epsilon
. Higher order terms in
\epsilon
deform the shape of the potential
U(Q)
but the double-well shape is preserved, and so the problem is qualitatively unchanged.

The quantum problem near the bifurcation point can be solved using the same set of ideas.
Beginning from the Keldysh action Eq. (
22
), the action is brought to a form similar to Eq. (

-(g/8)(\bar{\phi}^{\mathrm{q}}\phi+\bar{\phi}\phi^{\mathrm{q}})\bar{\phi}^{\mathrm{q}}\phi^{\mathrm{q}}

and

and rescaling

to the dimensionless variable

matches the form of Eq. (

This leads to similar results to the classical theory, with the main difference being that the quantum result is finite down to

. The switching rate is given by Eq. (

2T/\omega_{\mathrm{p}}\to\coth(\omega_{\mathrm{p}}/2T)

\text{i}S_{\mathrm{inst}}^{(0)}=-\tanh\left(\frac{\omega_{\mathrm{p}}}{2T}\right)\frac{\Delta_{\mathrm{B}}\epsilon^{2}}{g}.

(56)

Similarly, the correction to the switching rate exponent is similar in form to Eq. (

)

\text{i}S_{\mathrm{inst}}^{(1)}(\nu)=\alpha\tanh\left(\frac{\omega_{\mathrm{p}}}{2T}\right)\sqrt{\frac{\epsilon}{\pi\lambda g}}\bigg|\boldsymbol{\Gamma}\bigg(\frac{1-\text{i}\upsilon}{2}\bigg)\boldsymbol{\Gamma}\bigg(1+\frac{\text{i}\upsilon}{2}\bigg)\bigg|

(57)

In the limit

one recovers Eq. (

This is a consequence of the general property of quantum dynamics near a bifurcation point that can be reduced to fluctuations of a single “slow” dynamical variable: since this variable commutes with itself, the only signature of the quantumness is that the noise intensity

is replaced with
(\omega_{\mathrm{p}}/2)\coth(\omega_{\mathrm{p}}/2T)
.

Like in the classical theory, higher order terms in
\epsilon
can deform the shape of the potential
U(Q)
. Unlike the classical theory, they also lead to an additional term in the effective Hamiltonian
K_{0}
that is of the form
P^{3}X
. The effect of this term is very weak near the bifurcation point and appears only at
O(\epsilon^{2})
corrections to
K_{0}
. This originates from the additional nonlinear term
(\bar{\phi}^{\mathrm{q}}\phi+\bar{\phi}\phi^{\mathrm{q}})\bar{\phi}^{\mathrm{q}}\phi^{\mathrm{q}}
in the Keldysh formulation.
Such a term cannot be reduced to an auxiliary noise field, rendering the theory inequivalent to a Langevin process. As a result, the instanton action is no longer just a height of an effective potential barrier.
This turns out not to have a big effect as the additional factor does not modify the topology of the phase portrait, and thus gives only a weak numerical modification to the phase slip rate.

VI
Conclusion

Switching between coexisting stable states in Floquet systems is a generic phenomenon. A qualitative aspect of the switching is the possibility, even for low temperatures, to go over an effective barrier rather than to tunnel. This has no analog in systems in thermal equilibrium. The real-time instanton is a trajectory in the complex phase space of the system, which is the most likely path of the corresponding quantum fluctuation. Therefore it may not be directly observed.

This paper shows that, albeit indirect, unambiguous demonstration of complex real-time instantons and their features is possible. The suggested way to do it is to use an auxiliary comparatively weak drive, in addition to the strong parametric drive that defines the Floquet dynamics. The auxiliary drive can exponentially strongly modify the switching rate, depending on its frequency. Essentially, it performs an exponentially-sensitive spectroscopy of the system dynamics as the system moves along the instanton trajectory.

The specific system studied in the paper is a parametric oscillator. In recent years, such an oscillator has been attracting an increasing attention in mesoscopic physics and quantum information. Its stable states have opposite phases, and switching between them is a phase-slip transition.

We show that the rate of phase-flip transitions is exponentially strongly modified if, in addition to the parametric modulation, the oscillator is driven by an extra field with frequency close to half the modulation frequency. If the oscillator is underdamped, the logarithm of the rate change shows sharp strongly asymmetric peaks. The peaks are almost equidistant, but have different amplitudes. The peaks manifest where the detuning of the extra drive from half the modulation frequency is close to the overtones of the frequency of weakly damped oscillator vibrations about the stable states; such vibrations occur in the rotating frame and modulate the parametrically excited vibrations in the lab frame.

The shapes and amplitudes of the spectral peaks “probe” the instanton trajectory. Their amplitudes linearly depend on the amplitude of the extra drive, this is not an Ohmic heating by an extra drive. The peaks’ hight scales as the inverse square root of the decay rate, facilitating the observation of the effect for weak damping. On the other hand, for stronger damping, the spectrum of the response to the extra drive displays a characteristic single peak.

On the technical side, the paper presents a unified approach to the problem of switching based on the Keldysh technique. The formulation naturally connects the quantum and classical analyses. It provides an explicit description of the real-time instantons for weak damping and close to bifurcation points where, unavoidably, the damping becomes effectively strong. It also explicitly describes the effect of the extra drive and makes it possible to study the spectroscopy of real-time instantons in a broad range of parameters, including a broad temperature range.

Appendix B
Limiting Forms of the Classical Solutions

Near the bottom of the well
E\to E_{\mathrm{min}}
(or equivalently
I\to 0
), the problem becomes approximately harmonic. In this limit the problem can be easily solved by writing
\phi=\phi_{0}+\delta\phi
and expanding to quadratic order in
\delta\phi
. The resulting quadratic Hamiltonian has the same form as the linear rotating wave Hamiltonian Eq. (
3
),

H_{0}\simeq E_{\mathrm{min}}+\bar{\omega}\delta\bar{\phi}\delta\phi+\frac{\Delta}{2}(\delta\phi^{2}+\delta\bar{\phi}^{2}),

(71)

where
\bar{\omega}=4\lambda-\Delta
.
The Hamiltonian is diagonal in the coordinates
\zeta=\cosh(\beta)\phi+\sinh(\beta)\bar{\phi}
, where the Bogoliubov rotation angle is given by:

\beta=\frac{1}{2}\text{arctanh}(\Delta/\bar{\omega}).

(72)

This gives
H_{0}=E_{\mathrm{min}}+\omega_{\mathrm{min}}\bar{\zeta}\zeta
, with the the frequency
\omega_{\mathrm{min}}
matching the limiting form of
\omega(I=0)
. This, together with its derivative are both finite:

\omega(I=0)\equiv\omega_{\mathrm{min}}=2\sqrt{2\lambda(2\lambda-\Delta)},

(73a)

\partial_{I}\omega(I=0)=-\frac{g(8\lambda-\Delta)}{4(2\lambda-\Delta)}.

(73b)

This is shown in the plot of
\omega(I)
in Fig.
7
.

In this limit, the real period
t_{1}
is finite and the imaginary period
t_{2}
diverges logarithmically.
The pole time
t_{\mathrm{P}}
also diverges logarithmically but with an overall prefactor of
1/4
compared to
t_{2}
. This leads to a cancellation, rendering the quasi-period time
t_{\mathrm{Q}}
finite (though without a simple closed-form expression),

t_{1}(I=0)=\frac{2\pi}{\omega_{\mathrm{min}}},

(74a)

|t_{2}(I)|\underset{I\ll\bar{I}}{\simeq}\frac{2}{\omega_{\mathrm{min}}}\big|\log(I/\bar{I})\big|\underset{\overset{I\to 0}{}}{\longrightarrow}\infty,

(74b)

|t_{P}(I)|\underset{I\ll\bar{I}}{\simeq}\frac{1}{2\omega_{\mathrm{min}}}\big|\log(I/I_{\mathrm{P}})\big|\underset{\overset{I\to 0}{}}{\longrightarrow}\infty,

(74c)

t_{\mathrm{Q}}(I)\underset{\overset{I\to 0}{}}{\longrightarrow}t_{\mathrm{Q}}(0)<\infty,

(74d)

where
\bar{I}=\omega_{\mathrm{min}}^{2}/(\lambda g\sinh(\beta)\cosh(\beta)))
and
I_{\mathrm{P}}=\omega_{\mathrm{min}}^{2}/(\lambda g\sinh^{2}(\beta))
.

In the harmonic limit the Hamiltonian written in terms of the action adopts the approximate form
H_{0}\simeq E_{\mathrm{min}}+\omega_{\mathrm{min}}I
. It follows that the first Fourier coefficients are the largest, with
c_{\pm 1}\sim\sqrt{I}
.
The exact expression in the Harmonic limit is obtained from the diagonalizing Eq. (
71
),

c_{1}(I\to 0)=\cosh(\beta)\sqrt{I},\qquad c_{-1}(I\to 0)=\sinh(\beta)\sqrt{I}.

(75)

These may be expressed in terms of the microscopic parameters explicitly using the relations

\cosh(\beta)=\sqrt{\frac{\bar{\omega}+\omega_{\mathrm{min}}}{2\omega_{\mathrm{min}}}}

(76a)

\sinh(\beta)=\frac{\Delta}{\sqrt{2\omega_{\mathrm{min}}}\sqrt{\bar{\omega}+\omega_{\mathrm{min}}}}.

(76b)

The other Fourier components come with increasing powers of
I
, which can be deduced from the limiting forms of the various periods discussed above.
Substituting the forms of the logarithmic divergences from Eq. (
74
) into Eq. (
67
) gives, up to an overall complex phase,

c_{n}(I)\underset{I\ll I_{\gtrless}}{\simeq}\frac{\omega_{\mathrm{min}}}{\sqrt{\lambda g}}(I/I_{\gtrless})^{|n|/2},

(77)

where
I_{>}=\bar{I}^{2}/I_{\mathrm{P}}
and
I_{<}=I_{\mathrm{P}}
apply for
n\gtrless 0
.

In the opposite limit the classical motion approaches the separatrix near the top of the well. This corresponds to
E\to 0^{-}
and
I\to I_{\mathrm{top}}\equiv I(E\to 0^{-})
.
In this limit the frequency goes to zero and its derivative diverges,

\omega(I)\underset{I\simeq I_{\mathrm{top}}}{\simeq}\frac{2\pi\sqrt{g\sqrt{E_{\mathrm{min}}E_{\mathrm{D}}}}}{\big|\log(1-I/I_{\mathrm{top}})\big|}\underset{\overset{I\to I_{\mathrm{top}}}{}}{\longrightarrow}0,

(78a)

\partial_{I}\omega(I)\underset{I\simeq I_{\mathrm{top}}}{\simeq}\simeq-\frac{E_{\mathrm{min}}\big(\omega(I)\big)^{2}}{E(I)\big|\log(1-I/I_{\mathrm{top}})\big|}\underset{\overset{I\to I_{\mathrm{top}}}{}}{\longrightarrow}-\infty.

(78b)

The physical period
t_{1}
diverges as the separatrix is approached. The imaginary period
t_{2}
and the quasi-period
t_{\mathrm{Q}}
both have finite limits.

t_{1}(I)\underset{E\simeq 0^{-}}{\simeq}\frac{1}{\sqrt{g\sqrt{E_{\mathrm{min}}E_{\mathrm{D}}}}}\big|\log(1-I/I_{\mathrm{top}})\big|\underset{\overset{I\to I_{\mathrm{top}}}{}}{\longrightarrow}\infty,

(79a)

\mathrm{Im}\big(t_{2}(I_{\mathrm{top}})\big)=\frac{2\pi}{\sqrt{g\sqrt{E_{\mathrm{min}}E_{\mathrm{D}}}}},

(79b)

t_{\mathrm{Q}}(I)\underset{\overset{I\to I_{\mathrm{top}}}{}}{\longrightarrow}t_{\mathrm{Q}}(I_{\mathrm{top}})<\infty.

(79c)

Similar to

Appendix E
Relation to the Hamiltonian formulation

The approach based on the Keldysh technique has much in common with the analysis of the switching rates between the states of forced vibrations, which is based on the Hamiltonian formulation
10
. The latter analysis is also related to the approach to the rare events in classical systems described by the Fokker-Planck equation, cf.
18
;
16
;
17
;
6
;
33
. A qualitative difference between the classical and quantum formulations is that, in the quantum problem, the extreme trajectory of the appropriate Hamiltonian, which describes the optimal path followed by the system in switching, i.e., the real-time instanton, is complex. Therefore it is not directly observable, and the logarithmic susceptibility is a major tool for studying its features.

To reveal the similarity of the Hamiltonian-based and Keldysh-technique based approaches, we will re-write the Hamiltonian approach
10
directly in the action-angle variables. There is no difference, in general terms, between the formulations of the problem of a resonantly driven oscillator considered in
10
and the problem of a parametrically modulated oscillator considered here, although the solutions are very different. This is rooted in the different symmetries: a resonantly driven oscillator does not have parity. Since the logarithmic susceptibility was not discussed in
10
, we will begin with the case where the only time-dependent drive is parametric modulation.

The starting point of the Hamiltonian formulation is to write the Lindblad equation for the density matrix
\rho\equiv\rho(Q_{1},Q_{2})
of a modulated oscillator in the coordinate representation. In the variables and the parameters of the 3rd column of Table
1
the equation reads

\displaystyle\partial_{\tau}\rho=-i\lambda^{-1}\mathcal{H}(Q_{1},Q_{2},-i\lambda\partial_{Q_{1}},-i\lambda\partial_{Q_{2}})\rho,

\displaystyle\mathcal{H}(Q_{1},Q_{2},P_{1},P_{2})=\hat{g}(Q_{1},P_{1})-\hat{g}(Q_{2},P_{2})

\displaystyle+\kappa\hat{\mathcal{D}}(Q_{1},Q_{2},P_{1},P_{2}).

(86)

Here,
\tau=tF_{p}/2\omega_{p}
is the dimensionless time,
\lambda
and
\kappa
are the dimensionless reduced Planck constant and the decay rate,

\displaystyle\hat{g}=\frac{1}{4}(Q^{2}+P^{2}-\mu)^{2}+\frac{1}{2}(P^{2}-Q^{2})-\frac{\mu^{2}}{4}

(87)

is the scaled RWA Hamiltonian of the oscillator in the absence of dissipation, and

\displaystyle\hat{\mathcal{D}}=-(P_{1}Q_{2}+P_{2}Q_{1})

\displaystyle-\frac{1}{2}i(2\bar{n}+1)[(Q_{1}-Q_{2})^{2}+(P_{1}+P_{2})^{2}]+i\lambda

(88)

is the operator that describes relaxation (linear damping). It corresponds to the operator
\sum\gamma_{v}D_{v}
in Eq. (
20
).

We assume that
\lambda\ll 1
. Then each “well” of the Hamiltonian
g(Q,P)\propto H_{0}
has many quasienergy levels (function
H_{0}
is plotted in Fig.
1
; as seen from Table
1
, the coordinate
Q
is
\propto\mathrm{Im}\,\phi
). For small
\lambda
, there is a separation of time scales: the dwell time inside a well, which is the reciprocal of the phase-slip rate
W_{\mathrm{ps}}
, is much longer than the relaxation time inside the well, which is
\sim\kappa^{-1}
. Then the distribution over the intrawell states is quasistationary. To find it, for a given well, and the rate of switching from this well, one can set
\partial_{\tau}\rho=0
. In the classical theory of switching, this approximation can be traced back to Kramers
26
.

The tail of the intrawell distribution and the rate
W_{\mathrm{ps}}
can be sought in the eikonal approximation by setting

\displaystyle\rho(Q_{1},Q_{2})=\exp[iS(Q_{1},Q_{2})/\lambda].

(89)

To logarithmic accuracy, function
S
can be associated with the action of a classical system with complex coordinates
Q_{1},Q_{2}

10
. The equation of motion of this system is the Hamilton-Jacobi equation

\displaystyle\mathcal{H}(Q_{1},Q_{2},\partial_{Q_{1}}S,\partial_{Q_{2}}S)=0,

(90)

where
\mathcal{H}
is a function, not an operator; the term
i\lambda
in
\mathcal{D}
should be dropped in this approximation. The condition
\mathcal{H}=0
is the condition of the stationarity of the density matrix.

For the considered occupied well, the function
\rho(Q_{1},Q_{2})
is Gaussian near the classical stable state of the oscillator in this well
Q_{1}=Q_{2}=Q_{\mathrm{st}}
, with
\partial_{Q_{1}}S=-\partial_{Q_{2}}S=P_{\mathrm{st}}
. The values of
Q_{\mathrm{st}},P_{\mathrm{st}}
are given by the equations
\partial_{Q_{j}}\mathcal{H}=\partial_{P_{j}}\mathcal{H}=0
with
j=1,2
. Respectively,
S
is quadratic in
Q_{1}-Q_{\mathrm{st}},Q_{2}-Q_{\mathrm{st}}
. It can be easily found from Eq. (
90
). One can see from the normalization condition
\int dQ_{1}dQ_{2}\,\rho(Q_{1},Q_{2})=1
that
S(Q_{\mathrm{st}},Q_{\mathrm{st}})=0
, to the leading order in
\lambda
.

The value of
S
further away from the stable state can be found by solving the Hamiltonian equations of motion

\displaystyle\dot{Q}_{j}=\partial_{P_{j}}\mathcal{H},\quad\dot{P}_{j}=-\partial_{Q_{j}}\mathcal{H},\quad j=1,2.

(91)

This is somewhat different from the Keldysh technique where the “left” and “right” variables
\phi^{+}
and
\phi^{-}
evolve in the opposite directions of time. The initial conditions for the equations of motion are
Q_{1}(\tau),Q_{2}(\tau)\to Q_{\mathrm{st}}
and
P_{1}(\tau),-P_{2}(\tau)\to P_{\mathrm{st}}
for
\tau\to-\infty
. In Eq. (
91
) and below in this section the over-dot means differentiation over
\tau
, i.e.,
\dot{X}\equiv dX/d\tau
.

As in the main text, and as it was done in
10
, we will consider the case of the small damping parameter
\kappa
, although the general formulation based on Eq. (
91
) applies for an arbitrary
\kappa
(some numerical results on solving the boundary-value problem (
91
) for a resonantly driven oscillator have been discussed in
29
). We will change from
(Q_{j},P_{j})
to the action-angle variables
(I_{j},\varphi_{j})
of dissipation-free motion. The replacement
(Q,P)\to(I,\varphi)
is described by the standard canonical transformation for the Hamiltonian function
g(Q,P)
. The variables
Q,P
are periodic in
\varphi
,

Q(I,\varphi)=\sum_{m}Q_{m}(I)e^{im\varphi},\quad P(I,\varphi)=\sum_{m}P_{m}(I)e^{im\varphi},

and we choose
Q(I,\varphi)=Q(I,-\varphi),\;P(I,\varphi)=-P(I,-\varphi)
.

In the action-angle variables, the equations of motion (
91
) read

\displaystyle\dot{I}_{j}=-\kappa\partial_{\varphi_{j}}\mathcal{D},\quad\dot{\varphi}_{j}=(-1)^{3-j}\omega(I_{j})+\kappa\partial_{I_{j}}\mathcal{D}

(92)

with
\omega(I)=\partial g/\partial I
(in
10
there was used a formulation in terms of quasienergies and phases). Importantly, the variables
I_{j},\varphi_{j}
on the trajectories (
92
) are complex. We further change to

I_{j}=I+(-1)^{3-j}I^{q}/2,\quad\varphi_{j}=(-1)^{3-j}\theta+\theta^{q}/2.

From the condition
\mathcal{H}=0
it follows that
g(I+I^{q}/2)-g(I-I^{q}/2)=-\kappa\mathcal{D}
. Therefore
I^{q}\approx-[\kappa/\omega(I)]\mathcal{D}
is small and can be disregarded when calculating
\mathcal{D}
, i.e.,
\mathcal{D}
is a function of
I\approx I_{1}\approx I_{2}
.
Then, from Eq. (
92
),
\dot{\theta}\approx\omega(I)
, whereas
|\dot{\theta}^{q}|\propto\kappa\ll\omega(I)
. For a given
I
, the function
\mathcal{D}
is periodic in
\varphi_{1,2}
, and therefore

\displaystyle\mathcal{D}(Q_{1},Q_{2},P_{1},P_{2})=\sum_{m_{1},m_{2}}\mathcal{D}_{m_{1}m_{2}}(I)e^{i(m_{1}+m_{2})\theta^{q}/2}

\displaystyle\times\exp[i(m_{1}-m_{2})\theta].

The terms
\propto\exp[i(m_{1}-m_{2})\theta]
with
m_{1}\neq m_{2}
are fast oscillating at frequency
\omega(I)
. Therefore, from Eq. (
92
),
\dot{I}^{q}=-\kappa\partial_{\theta}\mathcal{D}
is a sum of fast oscillating terms. Given that
I^{q}\to 0
for
\tau\to-\infty
, we see that
I^{q}
is not just small, but is also fast oscillating, with no components that smoothly depend on time. Then, noting that the condition
\mathcal{H}=0
implies, in particular, that there are no fast-oscillating terms in
\mathcal{H}
, we obtain

\displaystyle\sum_{m}\mathcal{D}_{mm}(I)\exp(im\theta^{q})=0.

(93)

Setting, similar to the main text,
\theta_{q}=-ip
and using the explicit form of
\mathcal{D}(Q_{1},Q_{2},P_{1},P_{2})
, we can write this equation in the form

\displaystyle\sum_{m}\left[\bar{n}|a_{m}|^{2}(1-e^{-mp})\right.

\displaystyle\left.+(\bar{n}+1)|a_{-m}|^{2}(1-e^{-mp}\right]=0,

(94)

where

a_{m}\equiv(P_{m}-iQ_{m})/\sqrt{2\lambda}=-a_{m}^{*}.

The Fourier component
a_{m}\equiv a_{m}(I)
is the matrix element of the ladder operator
a
on the semiclassical Floquet intrawell wave functions
\ket{n}
of the Hamiltonian
\hat{g}
, i.e.,
a_{m}(I_{n})=\braket{n+m|a|n}

35
.

Equation (
E
) determines the function
p\equiv p(I)
. It coincides with Eq. (
23
) of the main text once one takes into account that
a_{m}(I)=c_{-m}(I)
. In fact, this equation coincides with the balance equation for the populations
\rho_{nn}\equiv\rho(I_{n})
of the intrawell Floquet states
10
;
35
. The function
p\equiv p(I)
determines the change of the populations with the varying state number in the continuous limit,
p(I)=-\lambda d\log\rho(I)/dI
. This is seen from Eq. (
89
) for the density matrix as a function of
I
by noting that, for
\mathcal{H}=0
, the action
S
is

\displaystyle S=-\sum_{j}\int_{0}^{I}\varphi_{j}\,dI_{j}=-\int\theta\,dI^{q}+i\int_{0}^{I}p(I^{\prime})\,dI^{\prime},

\displaystyle\rho(I)=C\exp\left[-\int_{0}^{I}p(I^{\prime})\,dI^{\prime}/\lambda\right],

(95)

where we have disregarded small corrections from the integral over
dI^{q}=\dot{I}^{q}dt
(we recall that
I^{q}
is small and fast oscillating in time); we have set
S=0
at the stable state; the prefactor
C
is a smooth function of
I
.

The rate of phase-slips via quantum activation
W_{\mathrm{ps}}
is determined by the population near the top of the barrier that separates the stable vibrational states of the oscillator. This is a saddle point with
Q_{1}=Q_{2}=Q_{\mathrm{u}}
,
P_{1}=-P_{2}=P_{\mathrm{u}}
and, respectively,
I=I_{\mathrm{u}}
. For the considered weak damping

\displaystyle W_{\mathrm{ps}}=\mathrm{const}\times\exp(-R_{A}/\lambda),

\displaystyle R_{A}=\mathrm{Im}\,S(I_{\mathrm{u}})=\int_{0}^{I_{\mathrm{u}}}p(I)\,dI

(96)

This expression coincides with the result of the main text.

E.1
Logarithmic susceptibility

In the absence of an extra drive the trajectory followed in escape is described by
Q_{j}(\tau)\equiv Q_{j}(I_{j}(\tau),\varphi_{j}(\tau)
,
P_{j}(\tau)\equiv P_{j}(I_{j}(\tau),\varphi_{j}(\tau)
(
j=1,2
), where, for weak damping,
I_{1,2}(\tau)
and
\varphi_{1,2}(\tau)
are determined by Eq. (
92
) with

\displaystyle\dot{I}=2\kappa\lambda\sum_{n}|a_{n}|^{2}n\left[(\bar{n}+1)e^{pn}-\bar{n}e^{-pn}\right],

\displaystyle\dot{\theta}=\omega(I).

(97)

Here
\theta^{q}=-ip\equiv-ip(I)
is given by Eq. (
E
) and
a_{n}\equiv a_{n}(I)=(P_{n}-iQ_{n})/\sqrt{2\lambda}
. The escape trajectory is a complex real-time instanton. It starts from the dynamically stable classical oscillator state
Q_{1}=Q_{2}=Q_{\mathrm{st}}
,
P_{1}=-P_{2}=P_{\mathrm{st}}
and goes to the saddle point (unstable stationary state)
Q_{1}=Q_{2}=Q_{\mathrm{u}}
,
P_{1}=-P_{2}=P_{\mathrm{u}}
.

In the notations of Ref.
3
;
4
the perturbation from an extra drive at frequency
\omega_{d}
close to half the modulation frequency is described by adding to
\hat{g}
a term
\hat{g}^{(1)}
,

\displaystyle\hat{g}^{(1)}(Q,P;\tau)=-\alpha_{d}\left[P\cos(\nu_{d}\tau+\varphi_{d})\right.

\displaystyle\left.+Q\sin(\nu_{d}\tau+\varphi_{d})\right]

(98)

where
\nu_{d}=2\omega_{p}[\omega_{d}-(\omega_{p}/2)]/F_{p}
, in the notations of the right column of Table
1
;
\alpha_{d}
is also defined in the same column (note that
\phi_{d}
is shifted by
\pi
compared to Eq. (
1
). The corresponding term in the Hamiltonian
\mathcal{H}
is

\mathcal{H}^{(1)}(\tau)=g^{(1)}(Q_{1},P_{1};\tau)-g^{(1)}(Q_{2},-P_{2};\tau).

In terms of the Fourier components
a_{m}(I)
and the variables
\theta
and
p=i\theta^{q}
it has the form

\displaystyle\mathcal{H}^{(1)}(\tau)=

\displaystyle 2\alpha_{d}(\lambda/2)^{1/2}\sum_{m}a_{-m}e^{i\nu_{d}\tau+i\phi_{d}-im\theta}

\displaystyle\times\sinh(mp/2)-\mathrm{c.c.}

(99)

To the first order in
\alpha_{d}
the correction to the action due to the perturbation is

S^{(1)}=-\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}d\tau\,\mathcal{H}^{(1)}(\tau|\tau_{0}),

where
\mathcal{H}^{(1)}(\tau|\tau_{0})
is the Hamiltonian
\mathcal{H}^{(1)}(\tau)
calculated for the unperturbed trajectory
Q_{1,2}(\tau-\tau_{0})
,
P_{1,2}(\tau-\tau_{0})
, and one has to take the extremum of
S^{(1)}
over
\tau_{0}
. This maps the calculation on the calculation of the logarithmic susceptibility in the main text.
