Beyond CA-125: circulating cell-free DNA as the next-generation biomarker in ovarian malignancy
Authors: Gamalat A Elgedawy, Rasha A Adbelmoneum, Amira I Aldesoky, Samya S Mashal, Amany E Awad, Naglaa S Elabd, Marwa L Helal
Published: 2026

Background

Due to a lack of efficient screening techniques or reliable biomarkers with high sensitivity and specificity, ovarian cancer (OC) is a quarrelsome disease with a significant death rate. While the initial treatment response rate is satisfactory, advanced-stage OC patients continue to have a high recurrence rate, in part due to a lack of knowledge about the mechanisms underlying drug resistance [1].

As the most fatal of the gynecological cancers, OC has a higher death rate than endometrial, breast, and cervical cancers. Every year, about 180,000 patients die, and 300,000 newly identified cases of OC are reported worldwide [2].

According to the Normal Risk Ovarian Screening Study and the U.K. Collaborative Trial of Ovarian Cancer Screening, current tests like cancer antigen 125 (CA-125) can help detect early stages of ovarian cancer but do not increase survival rates, likely because they don’t identify ovarian cancer effectively enough. These evaluations pave the way for novel and more efficient strategies to find biomarker combinations for the early detection of OC, especially high-grade serous ovarian carcinoma (HGSOC), the primary cause of ovarian cancer deaths [3–5].

The gold standard for verifying the diagnosis and genetic profiling of ovarian cancer (OC) is still histopathological analysis. However, tissue biopsy is not the best option for continuous clinical monitoring because of its invasive nature. Liquid biopsy, on the other hand, provides a less invasive and economical method by identifying components of the tumor that are present (circulating) in the blood. These involve circulating tumor DNA (ctDNA), circulating tumor cells (CTCs), cell-free DNA (cfDNA), tumor-educated platelets (TEPs), and tumor-derived extracellular vesicles (TEVs), which come from either primary tumors or metastatic sites. These studied biomarkers have a great deal of promise for a number of clinical uses, such as disease monitoring, diagnosis, and treatment assessment [6].

Cell-free DNA (cfDNA) is DNA present in body fluids that originates from two primary sources: dying cells that passively release DNA, and living cells that secrete DNA actively. Understanding how cfDNA is released helps improve liquid biopsy tests used for disease monitoring, and research is ongoing to better explain these processes [7]. cfDNA released into the bloodstream carries valuable information, and the amount and genetic makeup of tumor-related cfDNA are commonly used as biomarkers to monitor cancer and guide treatment [8, 9].

Analyzing cfDNA in the blood has grown in importance in cancer research in recent years. Liquid biopsy is a non-invasive and economical substitute for conventional tissue biopsies. Early detection, tracking treatment response, determining minimal residual disease, and comprehending mechanisms of therapy resistance are just a few of the potential uses for it. The application of cfDNA analysis in gynecologic cancers is still developing, despite its validation as a clinical biomarker in a number of solid tumors [10, 11]. Research is still ongoing to determine the best clinical uses for this new technology.

Studies on gynecologic cancer have concentrated on using cfDNA analysis to identify specific genetic changes, such as BRCA1/2 mutations in ovarian cancer, and to monitor the disease and detect minimal/molecular residual disease (MRD) [12].

Researchers believe that circulating tumor cell-free DNA (cfDNA) can offer important clues about both primary and metastatic tumors, which is why many studies have focused on cfDNA across different types of cancer. Therefore, new biomarkers overcoming the limitations of CA-125 and the invasiveness of tissue biopsies and can support patient follow-up and early relapse detection are needed to improve ovarian cancer screening and diagnosis. In this regard, our goal was to investigate the possibility of using circulating cell-free DNA (cfDNA) as a non-invasive ovarian cancer biomarker.

Materials and methods

Study design and participants

This case‒control prospective study was carried out in the National Liver Institute’s Department of Clinical Biochemistry & Molecular Diagnostics, collaborated with the Departments of Clinical Pathology, Obstetrics & Gynecology and Clinical Oncology & Nuclear Medicine departments, Faculty of Medicine-Menoufia University.

This study included a total of 150 participants, divided into three groups: Group A: included fifty newly diagnosed epithelial ovarian cancer patients, confirmed by histopathology and, when indicated, immunophenotyping. These patients were recruited from the New Cases Clinic at the Clinical Oncology and Nuclear Medicine Department. Group B: Fifty patients diagnosed with benign ovarian lesions, recruited from the outpatient surgical clinic. Group C: Fifty apparently healthy, age-matched blood donors, serving as the control group. These participants were enrolled from Menoufia University Hospital during 2023.

Inclusion criteria: histopathological evidence of ovarian cancer, age ≥ 18 years, all FIGO stages, completeness of file data and ECOG performance status (PS) ≤ 2. Exclusion criteria: Presence of other malignancies elsewhere in the body, advanced comorbid conditions such as cardiac. renal, or hepatic impairment, and inflammatory conditions (acute or chronic). Furthermore, all patients who refused to take part in our study, skipped it, or lacked the required data.

Ethical considerations

The National Liver Institute’s Research Ethics Committee at Menoufia University in Egypt reviewed and approved the study (Approval Number: IRB 00466/2023), and it was carried out in compliance with the Declaration of Helsinki. Prior to enrolment, all participants gave their written informed consent, enabling the use of their blood samples and medical records for research.

Clinical evaluation and diagnostic workup

Patients diagnosed with ovarian cancer (Group A) had definitive confirmation of ovarian cancer diagnosis via histopathological confirmation. Comprehensive data were gathered for each participant, including medical history, physical examination findings, and laboratory investigation results. These data included age, complete blood count (CBC), liver & kidney function tests, and tumor marker levels including CA-125, β-human chorionic gonadotropin (β-HCG), lactate dehydrogenase (LDH), α-fetoprotein (AFP), and circulating cell-free DNA (cfDNA).

The cancer staging method for patients diagnosed with ovarian cancer is based on the American Joint Committee on Cancer (AJCC) (Tumor, Node, Metastasis) TNM Classification and the International Federation of Gynecology and Obstetrics (FIGO) classifications, 8th Edition [13].

Radiological investigations performed to evaluate potential metastatic spread of ovarian cancer included contrast-enhanced CT examinations of chest, abdomen and pelvis, as well as brain and bone scans for assessment of possible metastatic spread. In addition, a subset of OC patients had PET/CT examinations as part of a comprehensive staging evaluation before surgery.

For all participants, peripheral blood samples were collected before any therapeutic intervention. At the time of sample collection, none of the participants in the OC group received prior surgery, chemotherapy, or radiotherapy.

Treatment protocol

Following blood sample collection, all patients in the ovarian cancer group (Group A) proceeded with standard active treatment. Depending on the clinical evaluation and stage of the disease, treatment options included either primary debulking surgery (PDS) followed by adjuvant chemotherapy (ACT) or neoadjuvant chemotherapy (NACT) followed by interval debulking surgery (IDS).

All patients received platinum-based chemotherapy. Carboplatin alone or in combination with paclitaxel made up the chemotherapy regimen. Paclitaxel was administered at a dose of 175 mg/m², and carboplatin was given at an area under the curve (AUC) of 5. Depending on each patient’s response and tolerance, chemotherapy was administered every 21 days for a total of six to eight cycles.

Treatment monitoring and follow-up

Before beginning treatment, patients who received neoadjuvant chemotherapy underwent baseline investigations, including serum CA-125 measurement and imaging in the form of contrast-enhanced computed tomography (CT) of the chest, abdomen, and pelvis. To gauge treatment response and reevaluate resectability, these assessments were repeated before interval debulking surgery and after three to four cycles of chemotherapy.

Post-operative assessment for patients who underwent primary debulking surgery included contrast-enhanced CT of the chest, abdomen, and pelvis, in addition to measurement of serum CA-125 values to check early recurrence or residual disease.

Patients were monitored every two to three months after completing all scheduled treatments (chemotherapy and surgery). Physical examinations and clinical evaluations were part of the follow-up. If there were any clinical signs or symptoms that suggested a recurrence of the disease, imaging tests, especially CT scans, were carried out.

Response evaluation

The Response Evaluation Criteria in Solid Tumours (RECIST), version 1.1, was used to evaluate the tumor’s response to treatment [14]. Using the smallest sum measured during the study as the reference (including baseline if it represented the lowest value), progressive disease (PD) was defined as at least a 20% increase in the total diameters of target lesions. The sum diameters must exhibit an absolute increase of at least 5 mm in addition to the relative increase. On the other hand, the emergence of one or more new lesions was also considered to be a sign of progressive disease.

Survival analysis

Progression-free survival or PFS was calculated by the time between the diagnosis date to the documented disease progression date or the last follow-up visit, whichever came first. The overall survival or OS was defined as the time from the date of diagnosis to the date of death due to any cause or the last recorded follow-up. Patients were monitored for a maximum duration of thirty-six months.

Sample collection and processing

Each participant provided total of 7 mL of whole blood that was separated. 3 mL was placed into an EDTA tube, while 4 mL was transferred into a plain tube to prepare serum. These serum samples were centrifuged and used for tumor marker analysis and biochemical tests.

For cfDNA extraction, two rounds of centrifuging (double centrifugation) were performed to provide a high-quality plasma sample. Centrifugation was performed at 2,500 ×g for 10 min at 4 °C. Then at the end of the first round, the plasma was carefully separated. The remaining cells and cellular debris present in the plasma were removed in the second round of centrifugation, where the same conditions of the first round were followed. All resulting supernatant (plasma) samples were aliquoted and stored at -–80 °C according to their respective protocols until further analysis.

Molecular analysis

cfDNA extraction

The plasma samples collected for DNA extraction ranging from 400 to 800 µL were used for the extraction cell-free DNA (cfDNA). According to the manufacturer’s protocol using the QIAamp® UltraSens® Virus Kit (Qiagen, Hilden, Germany) the cfDNA was isolated. The extracted cfDNA was stored at a temperature of − 80 °C until further use. To guarantee uniformity, all samples were prepared under the same standardized conditions.

Real-time PCR assay

SYBR green-based real-time PCR on the 7500 Fast Real-Time PCR System (Applied Biosystems, Life Technologies, CA, USA) was performed for the quantification of cfDNA. The cycling conditions were carried out as follows: Initial denaturation at 95 °C for 10 min (the holding stage), 40 cycles of denaturation for 15 s at 95 °C, 40 cycles of annealing and extension for 1.5 min at 60 °C. A melting curve analysis was performed at the end of each run to verify the specificity of the PCR products by assessing the correlation between temperature and fluorescence intensity.

Quantification of circulating free DNA

To quantify the amount of circulating free DNA (cfDNA) in plasma, the beta-globin gene served as a reference gene. PCR reactions were performrd in a 20 µL volume comprising of 6 µL of the extracted cfDNA, 10 µL of Maxima SYBR Green Master Mix (2X), 2 µL of nuclease-free water, 0.05 µL of ROX Reference Dye, and 1 µL each of forward and reverse β-globin gene primers.

(Thermo Fisher Scientific, Baltics UAB, Lithuania)

The Primer sequences for β-globin gene are present as (GenBank Accession No. U01317): Forward: 5′-GTGCACCTGACTCCTGAGGAGA-3′, and Reverse: 5′-CCTTGATACCAACCTGCCCAG-3′.

A standard curve was generated using serial 10-fold dilutions of genomic DNA with known concentrations (100 µg/mL, 10 µg/mL, 1 µg/mL and 0.1 µg/mL) extracted from Promega (USA). The standard curves enabled absolute quantification of cfDNA when applied to each PCR assay processed from the same samples.

Statistical analysis

The data of our findings was analyzed using the software IBM SPSS Statistics, Version 20 (IBM Corporation, Armonk, NY, USA). For categorical variables, Frequency (counts) and Percentage (%) were reported. Chi-Square test was used to compare the study groups; Fisher’s Exact test was used to compare between study groups if > 20% of the expected frequency were < 5. Before analyzing continuous variables, they were assessed for normal distribution. The Shapiro-Wilk Test was used for the assessment of normality. Continuous variables that were normally distributed were reported as the range (minimum-maximum), mean standard deviation (SD) ± median (SD). For comparison of the three study groups, the One-Way ANOVA test was performed; Tukey’s Post-Hoc test was performed for pairwise comparisons. For continuous variables that were not normally distributed, the Kruskal-Wallis Test was used; Dunn’s Post-Hoc test was performed for multiple comparisons. Progression-Free Survival (PFS) and Overall Survival (OS) were analyzed using Kaplan-Meier Method; comparison of survival curves was performed using the Log-Rank Test.

The mean CA125 level was significantly higher in the Malignant group (66.20 U/mL) than in the Benign group (21.22 U/mL) or Control group (5.66 U/mL). CA125 levels were statistically different between the Malignant, Benign, and control groups (p1 < 0.003, p2 < 0.001, p3 = 0.031) and support the potential utility of CA125 to be a marker of malignancy as levels decrease from Malignant to Benign conditions to the control group.

Table 1 highlights a distinct difference in the mean circulating DNA (cfDNA) in the malignant group (11.84 ng/mL) compared to the benign (3.90 ng/mL) and control (0.23 ng/mL) groups. The ovarian cancer group had a greater standard deviation (± 6.28), suggesting more variation in the amount of DNA released per unit volume of plasma, which may be potentially linked with the tumor burden. Furthermore, with highly significant statistical differences of p1 < 0.001, p2 < 0.001, and p3 < 0.001, it is reasonable to conclude that circulating DNA, as a biomarker, can robustly distinguish malignant cases from benign and healthy controls, with a discernible gradient between the three groups.

Table 2 shows a distribution of the cases examined according to the histopathological diagnosis. Of the cases with ovarian cancer, serous carcinoma is the most common, with 35 cases (70%), suggesting it is the predominant diagnosis. Mucinous adenocarcinoma follows with 15%, while clear cell adenocarcinoma and endometrioid adenocarcinoma account for even less, with 8% and 6%, respectively. Of the benign cases examined, serous surface papilloma is the most common diagnosis, with 21 cases (42%), with mucinous cystadenoma and endometrioid cystadenoma also being significant, presenting 20% and 24%, respectively. However, serous adenofibroma was less common (14%).

Diagnostic performance (malignant vs. benign)

ROC curve analysis revealed that cfDNA had the highest AUC (0.934, p < 0.001, 95% CI: 0.881–0.986) at a cut-off > 5.38 and had 94% sensitivity and 82% specificity, suggesting it is a strong discriminatory marker for prediction of ovarian cancer in suspected cases. CA125 also has a reasonable diagnostic utility (AUC 0.846, p < 0.001, 95% CI: 0.772–0.920) at a cut-off > 25.2, having 84% sensitivity and 72% specificity. For β-HCG (AUC 0.506, p = 0.918, 95% CI: 0.392–0.620) and LDH (AUC 0.678, p = 0.002, with 95% CI: 0.570–0.786), which have poor and modest diagnostic performance, respectively, β-HCGβ was not statistically significant (p > 0.05), with LDH having limited statistical significance (cutoff > 318, 68% sensitivity, 62% specificity). α-Fetoprotein had the lowest AUC (0.615, p = 0.047), indicating limited diagnostic utility (Table 4).

Prognostic performance for late ovarian cancer stage

We noticed that cfDNA remains to be the best prognostic marker for late stage cancer with good predictive ability (AUC 0.993, p < 0.001, 95% CI: 0.978–1.0); offering 95.24% sensitivity and 93.10% specificity with a cut-off > 10.63. CA125 (AUC 0.571, p = 0.398, 95% CI: 0.409–0.732) and β-HCGβ (AUC 0.501, p = 0.992, 95% CI: 0.330–0.672) show poor prognostic performance and low sensitivity/specificity with non-significant p-values. β-HCG, α-fetoprotein, and LDH underperform, with most lacking statistical significance for prognostic late stage (Table 4).

Prognostic performance for mortality

cfDNA again exhibited the strongest prognostic performance (AUC 0.814, p = 0.001, 95% CI: 0.690–0.937), with a cut-off > 10.28, it achieved 83.33% sensitivity and 63.16% specificity, suggesting it is a reasonable predictor of mortality. β-HCG (AUC 0.734, p = 0.016, 95% CI: 0.546–0.922) performed moderately. CA125, LDH and α-fetoprotein showed limited prognostic value, with limited sensitivity and specificity (Table 4).

Discussion

One of the most prevalent cancers in women worldwide is ovarian cancer. Since a number of risk factors could contribute to the development of this illness, it is reasonable to assume that incidence rates vary globally. Among female cancers in the Arab world, ovarian cancer is the sixth highest detected and has the fourth highest death rate, with a noticeable upward trend in incidence reported in several countries, including Egypt [15].

cfDNA is made up of small DNA fragments that circulate readily in the bloodstream. A portion of cfDNA is derived from tumor cells in cancer patients and thus contains the same genetic as well as epigenetic alterations, which is known as circulating cell-free tumor DNA (liquid biopsy). cfDNA gained attention for its potential use as a non-invasive ovarian cancer biomarker [16].

Although quantification of cfDNA in ovarian cancer has been previously reported, the present study is distinguished by its evaluation of treatment-naïve patients across all FIGO stages at initial diagnosis. The main innovation is the demonstration that pretreatment cfDNA concentration is an independent predictor of both progression-free and overall survival in multivariate Cox regression models, even after adjustment for CA-125 and clinicopathological variables. These findings extend beyond prior reports by supporting the integration of cfDNA into risk stratification and personalized treatment planning.

A complete overview of the diagnostic and prognostic landscape in this study is provided by the combined analysis of the clinical, histopathological, and biomarker data from our cohort of 50 malignant and 50 benign cases. Collectively, our results highlight the significant contributions of cfDNA as a non-invasive biomarker for ovarian cancer.

The current study’s histopathological distribution shows a predominance of serous carcinoma in malignant cases and serous surface papilloma among benign cases, which is consistent with prior research that showed serous histotypes are the most common presentation in patients with ovarian cancer. Similarly, the characteristics of the disease with Stage IIB and Grade II were most common and consistent with similar levels of intermediate disease severity found in previous studies [17].

We observed that the mean cfDNA in the malignant, benign, and control groups differs significantly, with ovarian cancer having a higher standard deviation. As a biomarker, cfDNA can accurately differentiate between cancerous cases and healthy controls, showing a clear gradient between the three groups. Furthermore, according to analysis of diagnostic performance, cfDNA is the most effective marker in the differentiation of malignant vs. benign disease (AUC 0.934, p < 0.001, sensitivity 94%, specificity 82%).

Our findings are consistent with those of Zaher et al. [18] and close to those of Shao et al., who found by the ROC curve analysis that the cut-off values for cfDNA were 478.435 µg/l, with AUC, sensitivity and specificity of cfDNA being 0.917, 88.9% and 89.5%, respectively, for separating malignant from benign cases in the ovarian cancer group [19]. In addition, Kamat et al. reported that, in contrast to controls and those with benign ovarian disease, patients with ovarian carcinoma had significantly higher preoperative cfDNA levels [20]. This is in line with research demonstrating the potential of circulating tumor DNA as a non-invasive diagnostic method [21].

According to our data analysis, cfDNA continues to be a good prognostic marker for late stage (AUC 0.993), with a cut-off > 10.63, providing 95.24% sensitivity, 93.10% specificity, and good predictive ability. With a cut-off > 10.28, cfDNA once again demonstrated that it is a reasonable predictor of mortality (AUC 0.814, p = 0.001). Based on a study on cfDNA quantification for ovarian cancer screening, patients with advanced-stage ovarian cancer had significantly higher serum cfDNA levels than those with early stage (p < 0.01) [19].

CA125 demonstrated diagnostic potential (AUC 0.846, p < 0.001), consistent with its previous characterization as a biomarker for ovarian cancer [22], although it was somewhat limited in prognostic value. Antovska et al. came to the conclusion in their study that CA-125 is not a useful diagnostic test on its own [23]. As a result, numerous researchers have included additional markers to increase CA-125’s diagnostic precision. The performance of β-HCG, LDH, and α-fetoprotein was also lower, most also without prognostic value, highlighting the need for new biomarkers as an alternative to existing serum markers.

AFP, one of the fetal serum proteins in the developing embryo, is primarily produced by the liver and yolk sac, and its values are frequently elevated in cancers. For hepatocellular carcinomas (HCC), ovarian embryonal carcinoma, yolk sac tumors, and some testicular tumors, AFP is a useful diagnostic tumor marker. However, epithelial ovarian carcinoma has rarely been reported to secrete AFP [24, 25].

Correlation analysis further validates Circulating DNA’s relevance, with a strong association with the Grade (r = 0.900, p < 0.001) and the stage (r = 0.900, p < 0.001) of ovarian cancer, suggesting its reflection of tumor differentiation. Remarkably, aggressive phenotypic traits like high-grade and high-stage were associated with elevated CFDNA levels. In patients with ovarian carcinoma, elevated plasma was a better indicator of mortality than CA125 and an independent predictor of DSS (decision support system) [20]. In contrast to our findings, Shao et al. reported no discernible correlation between serum levels of cfDNA and clinicopathological characteristics, including age, ovarian cancer complications, tumor classification, or differentiation [19].

The detection of tumor-derived cfDNA using an orthotopic mouse model of OC demonstrated that cfDNA levels significantly decrease with chemotherapy and have a strong correlation with tumor weight [26]. In comparison with controls, Zachariah and colleagues found that ovarian cancer patients had higher levels of both cell-free nuclear and mitochondrial DNA; however, their cohort’s prognosis was not correlated with cfDNA levels [27].

Recent research has explored the use of cell-free DNA as a prognostic marker in solid tumors. Studies have shown that tumor progression correlates with increasing plasma DNA concentrations in non-small cell lung cancer patients. In addition, plasma DNA levels also correlate with clinical stage, lymph node metastasis, and tumor size in breast cancer [28, 29].

Cox regression analysis highlights the prognostic potential of cfDNA. In univariate models 1, it significantly predicted lower progression-free survival (PFS, HR 12.587, p < 0.001) and overall free survival (OFS, HR 6.886, p < 0.013). cfDNA’s independent prognostic value was supported by multivariate analysis, which showed that it remained independently significant (OFS HR 8.742, p = 0.034; PFS HR 10.599, p < 0.010) with an increased hazard ratio [30].

Kaplan-Meier survival curves provide a temporal perspective, with high cfDNA expression significantly worsening PFS (mean 20.40 vs. 33.0 months, p < 0.001) and OFS (mean 27.475 vs. 33.154 months, p = 0.003). Previous studies revealed that cfDNA analysis helps track treatment response and disease progression in real time. A decrease in cfDNA levels indicates a favorable response, while an increase may indicate disease progression. This principle can be applied to ovarian cancer patients, as persistent or increasing cfDNA levels can negatively impact disease progression-free and overall survival [31, 32]. Excessive cfDNA shedding is linked to aggressive tumor phenotypes, genomic instability, and immune evasion, resulting in poor survival. cfDNA serves as a proxy for tumor shedding and metastatic ability [9].

The incorporation of cfDNA into ovarian cancer management offers a promising non-invasive approach for personalized medicine, risk stratification, and early intervention. However, its clinical application is challenged by significant variability in cfDNA levels due to non-tumor-related factors such as inflammation, the requirement for highly sensitive detection methods (e.g., next-generation sequencing), and the lack of standardized cut-off values.

Conclusion

Overall, these results demonstrate that cfDNA has the potential to be a superior diagnostic and prognostic biomarker compared to CA125 and conventional markers as reflected in its strong association with grade and independent predictive power in Cox models, as well as suggesting a possible biological role in tumor progression and mortality. From a clinical perspective, our results suggest the incorporation of cfDNA into risk stratification and treatment planning.

However, several limitations should be acknowledged. First, the relatively small sample size may have limited statistical power, particularly for subgroup analyses. Second, the study was conducted at a single center, which may restrict the generalizability of the findings to other populations or healthcare settings with different patient demographics and tumor characteristics. Third, although we adjusted for several known confounders in the multivariate models, unmeasured variables such as detailed treatment history and residual disease could have influenced the results. Additionally, a single measurement of cfDNA restricts assessment of longitudinal changes in cfDNA levels and their dynamic relationship with disease progression or treatment response.

Larger, multicenter studies with longer follow-up periods are therefore essential to confirm these preliminary findings, evaluate cost-effectiveness, and determine the true clinical utility of cfDNA in ovarian cancer management.
