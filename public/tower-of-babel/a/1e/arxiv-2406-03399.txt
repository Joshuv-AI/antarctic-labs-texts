Elliptic curves over Hasse pairs
Eleni Agathocleous, Antoine Joux, Daniele Taufer

Abstract
. We call a pair of distinct prime powers ( q 1 , q 2 ) = ( p 1 a 1 , p 2 a 2 ) (q_{1},q_{2})=(p_{1}^{a_{1}},p_{2}^{a_{2}}) a Hasse pair if | q 1 − q 2 | ≤ 1 |\sqrt{q_{1}}-\sqrt{q_{2}}|\leq 1 . For such pairs, we study the relation between the set ℰ 1 \mathcal{E}_{1} of isomorphism classes of elliptic curves defined over 𝔽 q 1 \mathbb{F}_{q_{1}} with q 2 q_{2} points, and the set ℰ 2 \mathcal{E}_{2} of isomorphism classes of elliptic curves over 𝔽 q 2 \mathbb{F}_{q_{2}} with q 1 q_{1} points. When both families ℰ i \mathcal{E}_{i} contain only ordinary elliptic curves, we prove that their isogeny graphs are isomorphic. When supersingular curves are involved, we describe which curves might belong to these sets. We also show that if both the q i q_{i} ’s are odd and ℰ 1 ∪ ℰ 2 ≠ ∅ \mathcal{E}_{1}\cup\mathcal{E}_{2}\neq\emptyset , then ℰ 1 ∪ ℰ 2 \mathcal{E}_{1}\cup\mathcal{E}_{2} always contains an ordinary elliptic curve. Conversely, if q 1 q_{1} is even, then ℰ 1 ∪ ℰ 2 \mathcal{E}_{1}\cup\mathcal{E}_{2} may contain only supersingular curves precisely when q 2 q_{2} is a given power of a Fermat or a Mersenne prime. In the case of odd Hasse pairs, we could not rule out the possibility of an empty union ℰ 1 ∪ ℰ 2 \mathcal{E}_{1}\cup\mathcal{E}_{2} , but we give necessary conditions for such a case to exist. In an appendix, Moree and Sofos consider how frequently Hasse pairs occur using analytic number theory, making a connection with Andrica’s conjecture on the difference between consecutive primes.

1.
Introduction

Given an elliptic curve
E
over a finite field
\mathbb{F}_{q}
of characteristic
p
, its number of points
|E(\mathbb{F}_{q})|
is directly related with a number
t
, called the trace of
E/\mathbb{F}_{q}
, which is defined as

t=q+1-|E(\mathbb{F}_{q})|.

All elliptic curves over
\mathbb{F}_{q}
with the same number of rational points have the same trace, which we will denote by
t
.
We can then consider the set
\mathcal{E}(t)
of
\mathbb{F}_{q}
-isomorphism classes of elliptic curves
E/\mathbb{F}_{q}
with trace
t
:

\mathcal{E}(t)=\{E/\mathbb{F}_{q}\ :\ |E(\mathbb{F}_{q})|=q+1-t\}/\{\mathbb{F}_{q}\textnormal{-isomorphisms}\}.

It is of both mathematical and cryptographic interest, to study such families
\mathcal{E}(t)
over some finite field
\mathbb{F}_{q}
, when the number of points, and therefore the trace
t
, is given.
In this paper, we are interested in a ‘dual formulation’ of this problem: given two prime powers
q_{1},q_{2}
, we investigate the two sets

\mathcal{E}_{1}=\{E_{1}/\mathbb{F}_{q_{1}}\ :\ |E_{1}(\mathbb{F}_{q_{1}})|=q_{2}\}/\{\mathbb{F}_{q_{1}}\textnormal{-isomorphisms}\},

and

\mathcal{E}_{2}=\{E_{2}/\mathbb{F}_{q_{2}}\ :\ |E_{2}(\mathbb{F}_{q_{2}})|=q_{1}\}/\{\mathbb{F}_{q_{2}}\textnormal{-isomorphisms}\}.

To exclude trivial cases, we further ask that the two prime powers
q_{1},q_{2}
be different and satisfy the relation

|\sqrt{q_{1}}-\sqrt{q_{2}}|\leq 1.

When this relation holds true, we call such a pair
(q_{1},q_{2})
a
Hasse pair
.
We remark that the case
|\sqrt{q_{1}}-\sqrt{q_{2}}|>1
is degenerate: when
q_{1},q_{2}
do not belong to the Hasse interval of each other, then both sets
\mathcal{E}_{i}
are necessarily empty.

The integer
t_{1}=q_{1}+1-q_{2}
(resp.
t_{2}=q_{2}+1-q_{1}
) will be referred to as the trace of
\mathcal{E}_{1}
(resp.
\mathcal{E}_{2}
), as it equals the trace of every curve (if any) in the corresponding set.

When
E_{1}\in\mathcal{E}_{1}
and
E_{2}\in\mathcal{E}_{2}
both arise as good reductions of a global curve
E
, the pair
(q_{1},q_{2})
was called
amicable
or
2
-
aliquot cycle
for
E

[
7
,
31
]
, and the distribution of amicable primes for a given
E
was notably shown to depend on whether
E
has complex multiplication
[
16
,
25
,
31
]
.
If the
E_{i}
’s share the same endomorphism ring
\mathcal{O}
, the pair
(q_{1},q_{2})
has been referred to as
dual elliptic
associated to
\mathcal{O}

[
21
]
.
In cryptography, such pairs of curves
(E_{1},E_{2})
are called
2-cycles
, and may be employed to design recursive zk-SNARK protocols
[
5
]
. Those are particularly efficient when the considered curves are pairing-friendly, which poses strong constraints on the feasible curves
[
4
,
9
]
.
Examples of such applications appeared, among others, in
[
6
,
14
,
15
,
20
]
, and we refer the interested reader to
[
2
]
for an extensive survey on this topic.

1.1.
Novel contributions

Unlike previous related works, in this paper we do not start from a global curve. We observe instead that for a given Hasse pair, the associated traces
t_{i}
are linearly dependent (Remark
3.2
) and they have the same discriminant
\Delta
(Lemma
3.5
), which prescribes whether the corresponding elliptic curves are ordinary or supersingular (Lemma
3.10
).
Furthermore, even though the set
\mathcal{E}_{i}
may occasionally be empty, even when
(q_{1},q_{2})
is a Hasse pair, this never occurs when the
q_{i}
’s are pure primes (Lemma
3.11
); and in this case, both sets
\mathcal{E}_{i}
consist of ordinary elliptic curves, except for a few small exceptions (Remark
3.12
).

For every choice of odd Hasse pair
(p_{1}^{a_{1}},p_{2}^{a_{2}})
, at least one of the
p_{i}
’s is proved to split completely in
\mathbb{Q}(\sqrt{\Delta})
(Proposition
5.6
).

For any Hasse pair
(q_{1},q_{2})
, both the sets
\mathcal{E}_{i}
are likely to consist of ordinary elliptic curves, and in this case, the directed multigraphs of their prime degree
\mathbb{F}_{q_{i}}
-isogenies are isomorphic (Theorem
6.9
).
However, special choices of the
q_{i}
’s may lead to different configurations.
When both the
q_{i}
’s are odd, at most one of the sets
\mathcal{E}_{i}
may contain supersingular elliptic curves (Proposition
4.2
), while this fails with even characteristic in a specific list of cases involving Fermat and Mersenne primes (Theorem
4.6
).
Moreover, if
q_{1}
is even, then the existence of a curve in
\mathcal{E}_{1}
is sufficient to guarantee that
\mathcal{E}_{2}
is also non-empty (Lemma
4.4
and Theorem
4.6
).

The only open case left is
\mathcal{E}_{1}\cup\mathcal{E}_{2}=\emptyset
.
In even characteristic this can happen, but we found only one such instance, namely
(q_{1},q_{2})=(2^{8},3^{5})
.
We did not find any such a pair when both the
q_{i}
’s are odd, and we proved that such an example (if existent) should satisfy strict numerical conditions (Proposition
5.11
).

Problem 1.1
.

Characterizing Hasse pairs
(q_{1},q_{2})
such that
\mathcal{E}_{1}
and
\mathcal{E}_{2}
are both empty.
In particular, deciding if the set of such pairs is finite (or even empty, in odd characteristics).

To the best of our knowledge, Problem
1.1
is open, except for cases of very small exponents ruled out by Corollary
5.12
.
The possible configurations of sets
\mathcal{E}_{i}
discussed in the present paper are displayed by Tables
1
and
2
.

Finally, given the interesting properties that these sets of elliptic curves and their graphs exhibit, it is natural to ask, not only out of mathematical curiosity but also from a cryptographic perspective, whether there are many Hasse pairs.
This question is directly related to a long-standing conjecture known as Andrica’s conjecture, which states that the difference of the square roots of any two consecutive primes is always less than one. In Appendix
A
, Moree and Sofos discuss this conjecture in the context of prime powers, and they prove an asymptotic result regarding the number of Hasse pairs in which a given prime
p
can be a member.

1.2.
Notation

We denote the set of natural numbers by
\mathbb{N}=\{0,1,\dots\}
, and its positive subset by
\mathbb{Z}_{>0}=\{1,2,\dots\}
.
We write
p^{a}||N
when
p^{a}|N
but
p^{a+1}\nmid N
.
When the order of the elements of a pair is irrelevant, we denote it by
((q_{1},q_{2}))
to indicate
(q_{1},q_{2})
or
(q_{2},q_{1})
.

Paper organization

In Section
2
we recall a theorem of Waterhouse on valid traces, and we prove a corollary of this result regarding elliptic curves of
j
-invariant zero. In Section
3
, we define Hasse pairs and we establish several properties of its associated discriminant
\Delta
and traces
t_{1}
and
t_{2}
.
In Section

\mathcal{E}_{1}
or
\mathcal{E}_{2}

\mathbb{Q}(\sqrt{\Delta})
.
In Section

, we deal with the ordinary case and we establish the correspondence between the isogeny graphs of the elliptic curves in
\mathcal{E}_{1}
and of those in
\mathcal{E}_{2}
.

7
.
Finally, in Section

2.
Valid Traces

Let
q\in\mathbb{Z}_{>0}
be a prime power.
We consider all elliptic curves over
\mathbb{F}_{q}
with the same number of points or, equivalently, with the same trace
t\in\mathbb{Z}
.
We denote the set of
\mathbb{F}_{q}
-isomorphism classes of such curves by
\mathcal{E}(t)
.
The following theorem of Waterhouse determines when
\mathcal{E}(t)
is not empty and the type of elliptic curves it contains.

Theorem 2.1

(
[
35
, Theorem 4.1]
)
.

Let
\mathcal{E}(t)
be the set of elliptic curves of trace
t
over
\mathbb{F}_{q}=\mathbb{F}_{p^{a}}
. Then
\mathcal{E}(t)
is non-empty precisely in the following cases.

(a)

|t|\leq 2\sqrt{q}
, with
\gcd(t,q)=1
.

(b)

t=\pm 2\sqrt{q}
, and
a
is even.

(c)

t=\pm\sqrt{q}
, with
p\not\equiv 1\bmod 3
and
a
is even.

(d)

t=\pm\sqrt{p^{a+1}}
, with
p\in\{2,3\}
and
a
is odd.

(e)

t=0
, and
(\textnormal{I})

a
is odd or
(\textnormal{II})

a
is even, and
p\not\equiv 1\bmod 4
.

The elliptic curves of case (
a
) are ordinary.
The curves of case (
b
) are supersingular and have all their endomorphisms defined over
{\mathbb{F}_{q}}
, while the rest are supersingular but do not have all their endomorphisms defined over
{\mathbb{F}_{q}}
.

By
[
35
, Theorems 4.1 and 4.2]
, one can deduce the values of the
j
-invariants for some of the cases of Theorem
2.1
.
Since we have not found this result in the literature, we state and prove it here, for completeness.

Proposition 2.2
.

The elliptic curves corresponding to cases (
c
) and (
d
) in Theorem
2.1
have
j
-invariant
0
.

Proof.

Since the CM-discriminant is
\Delta=t^{2}-4q
, we have

t=\begin{cases}\pm p^{\frac{a}{2}}&\textnormal{ with }a\textnormal{ even,}\\
\pm p^{\frac{a+1}{2}}&\textnormal{ with }p\in\{2,3\},a\textnormal{ odd.}\\
\end{cases}\implies\Delta=\begin{cases}-3q&\textnormal{ in case }\eqref{W:odd1},\\
(p-4)q&\textnormal{ in case }\eqref{W:odd2}.\\
\end{cases}

The elliptic curves corresponding to the above cases, do not have all their endomorphisms defined over
\mathbb{F}_{q}
, hence by
[
35
, Theorem 4.2-(3)]
the possible endomorphism rings arising from the elliptic curves in
\mathcal{E}(t)
have conductor coprime to
q
.

In case (
c
) the possible conductors are those dividing
\sqrt{q}
, therefore the unique possible order is the maximal one, i.e. the ring of integers of
\mathbb{Q}(\sqrt{-3})
, which is the endomorphism ring of curves with
j
-invariant
0
.

Similarly, in case (
d
), if
p=2
the possible conductors are those dividing
2^{(a+1)/2}
, therefore the unique possible order is the ring of integers of
\mathbb{Q}(i)
, which is defined by curves with
j
-invariant
1728=0\in\mathbb{F}_{q}
. If
p=3
the conductors need to divide
3^{(a-1)/2}
, therefore the unique endomorphism order is the ring of integers of
\mathbb{Q}(\sqrt{-3})
, which is again defined by curves with
j
-invariant
0
.
∎

3.
Hasse pairs

Throughout the paper,
q_{1}=p_{1}^{a_{1}}
and
q_{2}=p_{2}^{a_{2}}
will denote arbitrary prime powers. Given such a pair
(q_{1},q_{2})
, we consider the (possibly empty) set of elliptic curves defined over
\mathbb{F}_{q_{1}}
with
q_{2}
rational points, and respectively the set of elliptic curves over
\mathbb{F}_{q_{2}}
with
q_{1}
points.
To avoid trivial repetitions, we consider these curves up to
\mathbb{F}_{q_{i}}
-isomorphism.

Definition 3.1
.

Let
(q_{1},q_{2})
be a pair of distinct prime powers.
For
(i,j)=((1,2))
, we define

\mathcal{E}_{i}=\{E_{i}/\mathbb{F}_{q_{i}}\ :\ |E_{i}(\mathbb{F}_{q_{i}})|=q_{j}\}/\{\mathbb{F}_{q_{i}}\textnormal{-isomorphisms}\}.

We also define the
trace

t_{i}
corresponding to such a pair as

t_{i}=q_{i}+1-q_{j}.

Remark 3.2
.

The integers
t_{i}
are the traces of the Frobenius morphism for every elliptic curve (if any) in
\mathcal{E}_{i}

[
30
, Remark V.2.6]
.
Moreover, by adding their defining equations, we immediately see that

t_{1}+t_{2}=2.

Remark 3.3
.

The results of the present paper would hold even when
q_{1}=q_{2}
. We have excluded this case from Definition
3.1
, since it leads to the trivial relations
\mathcal{E}_{1}=\mathcal{E}_{2}
and
t_{1}=t_{2}=1
.
These curves of trace
1
are often called
anomalous
curves, and are all ordinary by Theorem
2.1
(a).

Definition 3.4
.

We define the
discriminant
associated with the pair
(q_{1},q_{2})
as

\Delta=(q_{1}-q_{2})^{2}-2(q_{1}+q_{2})+1.

As we prove below, this is the maximal CM-discriminant of the curves in the sets
\mathcal{E}_{i}
, when they are not empty.
An analogous result for amicable curves was already observed in
[
31
, Proposition 3.4]
.

Lemma 3.5
.

Let
t_{1},t_{2}
and
\Delta
be defined as above. Then

t_{1}^{2}-4q_{1}=\Delta=t_{2}^{2}-4q_{2}.

Proof.

By the definition of
t_{1}
we have

t_{1}^{2}-4q_{1}=(q_{1}-q_{2})^{2}+2(q_{1}-q_{2})+1-4q_{1}=\Delta,

which is symmetric in
q_{1}
and
q_{2}
, thus it also equals
t_{2}^{2}-4q_{2}
.
∎

Hasse’s Theorem
[
30
, Theorem V.1.1]
implies that the sets
\mathcal{E}_{i}
can be non-empty if the considered
q_{i}
’s are close enough. This motivates the following definition.

Definition 3.6

(Hasse pair)
.

Let
q_{1},q_{2}
be distinct prime powers. We call the pair
(q_{1},q_{2})
a
Hasse pair
iff

|\sqrt{q_{1}}-\sqrt{q_{2}}|\leq 1.

When both
q_{1}
and
q_{2}
are odd, we will refer to it as an
odd Hasse pair
.

The following lemma links the above definition to Hasse’s theorem.

Lemma 3.7
.

Let
t_{1},t_{2}
be defined as above. The following are equivalent:

(1)

(q_{1},q_{2})
is a Hasse pair,

(2)

|t_{1}|\leq 2\sqrt{q_{1}}
,

(3)

|t_{2}|\leq 2\sqrt{q_{2}}
.

Proof.

Condition (
1
) is equivalent to

\sqrt{q_{2}}-1\leq\sqrt{q_{1}}\leq\sqrt{q_{2}}+1.

Since all the quantities are positive, this is equivalent to its squared version, namely

q_{2}-2\sqrt{q_{2}}+1\leq q_{1}\leq q_{2}+2\sqrt{q_{2}}+1.

This can be written as

-2\sqrt{q_{2}}\leq-(q_{2}-q_{1}+1)\leq 2\sqrt{q_{2}},

which is condition (
3
).
Since condition (
1
) is symmetric, the equivalence between (
1
) and (
2
) follows in the same way, by reversing the roles of
q_{1}
and
q_{2}
.
∎

An elliptic curve over
\mathbb{F}_{q_{i}}
is called
supersingular
if its trace is divisible by
p_{i}
, and
ordinary
otherwise. Since all the curves in
\mathcal{E}_{i}
(if any) have the same trace
t_{i}
, then the set
\mathcal{E}_{i}
is either empty or contains curves that have the same type (i.e. they are all ordinary or all supersingular). This motivates the following definition.

Definition 3.8
.

We call the set
\mathcal{E}_{i}

ordinary
(resp.
supersingular
) if it is non-empty and contains ordinary (resp. supersingular) elliptic curves.

Remark 3.9
.

We recall that two ordinary curves are isomorphic over
\mathbb{F}_{q}
if and only if they have the same size and the same
j
-invariant
[
10
, Proposition 14.19]
. Hence, when the set
\mathcal{E}_{i}
is ordinary, it can be identified with the set containing the
j
-invariants of all elliptic curves that belong to
\mathcal{E}_{i}
.

Lemma 3.10
.

Let
(q_{1},q_{2})
be a Hasse pair. For both
i\in\{1,2\}
, the following hold.

•

If
\gcd(\Delta,q_{i})=1
, then
\mathcal{E}_{i}
is ordinary.

•

If
\gcd(\Delta,q_{i})\neq 1
, then
\mathcal{E}_{i}
is either supersingular or empty.

Proof.

We notice that

\gcd(t_{i},q_{i})=1\iff\gcd(t_{i}^{2}-4q_{i},q_{i})=1\iff\gcd(\Delta,q_{i})=1.

All the curves of
\mathcal{E}_{i}
(if any) have trace
t_{i}
(Remark
3.2
), hence they are ordinary when
\gcd(\Delta,q_{i})=1
, and supersingular when
\gcd(\Delta,q_{i})\neq 1
.
Since
|t_{i}|\leq 2\sqrt{q_{i}}
by Lemma
3.7
, in the ordinary case the set
\mathcal{E}_{i}
is always non-empty (see Theorem
2.1
, case (
a
)).
∎

When dealing with pure primes, the sets
\mathcal{E}_{i}
are almost always comprised of ordinary curves, as shown by the next lemma.

Lemma 3.11
.

If
(p_{1},q_{2})
is a Hasse pair, with
p_{1}
prime, then
\mathcal{E}_{1}
is non-empty.
Moreover, if
\mathcal{E}_{1}
is supersingular then
q_{2}=p_{1}+1
or
p_{1}\in\{2,3\}
.

Proof.

Since
(q_{1},q_{2})
is Hasse, by Lemma
3.7
we have
|t_{1}|\leq 2\sqrt{p_{1}}<2p_{1}.

If
p_{1}\in\{2,3\}
, the integer values lying between
-2p_{1}
and
2p_{1}
are either coprime to
p_{1}
(
a
), or equal to
\pm p_{1}
(
d
), or equal to
0
(
e
).

When
p_{1}>3
, we have the tighter bound
|t_{1}|\leq 2\sqrt{p_{1}}<p_{1}
.
In this case, the integers between
-p_{1}
and
p_{1}
are either coprime to
p_{1}
(
a
) or equal to
0
(
e
).
In all such cases,
\mathcal{E}_{1}
is non-empty by Theorem
2.1
.
Furthermore, if
p_{1}>3
the only supersingular case has
t_{1}=0

which implies
q_{2}=p_{1}+1
.
∎

Remark 3.12
.

As a consequence of Lemma
3.11
, whenever
(p_{1},p_{2})
is a Hasse pair consisting of pure primes (i.e.
a_{1}=a_{2}=1
), in all but a few small cases, both sets
\mathcal{E}_{i}
are ordinary.
A straightforward verification of those cases shows that
\mathcal{E}_{1}
is actually supersingular precisely when
(p_{1},p_{2})\in\{(2,3),(2,5),(3,7)\}
.

Example 3.13
.

Since
\sqrt{7}-\sqrt{3}=0.913\dots
, then
(p_{1},p_{2})=(3,7)
is an odd Hasse pair.
Up to isomorphism, there is only one elliptic curve in
\mathcal{E}_{1}
, defined by

y^{2}=x^{3}+2x+1.

As
t_{1}=3+1-7=-3
, this curve is supersingular. Similarly, there is only one curve in
\mathcal{E}_{2}
, defined by

y^{2}=x^{3}+4.

It has trace
t_{2}=2-t_{1}=5
, hence it is ordinary. Finally, we notice that the
j
-invariant of both curves is
0
, and this is not a coincidence as we show in Proposition
4.3
.

4.
Non-ordinary cases

In this section, we discuss the relation between
\mathcal{E}_{1}
and
\mathcal{E}_{2}
when
\gcd(\Delta,q_{1}q_{2})\neq 1
, namely when at least one of these sets is either supersingular or empty.

We begin by noticing that non-ordinary cases may occur only in different characteristics.

Lemma 4.1
.

If
p_{1}=p_{2}
, then
\gcd(\Delta,q_{1}q_{2})=1
.

Proof.

If
p_{1}=p_{2}
, then
q_{i}=p_{1}^{e_{i}}
.
Therefore

\Delta=(q_{1}-q_{2})^{2}-2(q_{1}+q_{2})+1\equiv 1\bmod p_{1},

which implies
\gcd(\Delta,q_{1}q_{2})=1
.
∎

4.1.
Odd characteristics

When
q_{1}
and
q_{2}
are both odd, then so are
t_{1}
and
t_{2}
.

Proposition 4.2
.

Let
(q_{1},q_{2})
be an odd Hasse pair. If
\mathcal{E}_{1}
is supersingular, then
\mathcal{E}_{2}
is ordinary.
Thus, if
\mathcal{E}_{1}\cup\mathcal{E}_{2}
is non-empty, then it always contains an ordinary elliptic curve.

Proof.

Let
E_{1}\in\mathcal{E}_{1}
be a supersingular curve. We first consider the case
p_{1}\neq 3
.
Since
t_{1}
is odd, only case (
c
) of Theorem
2.1
can apply. Thus,
a_{1}
needs to be even and
t_{1}=\pm\sqrt{q_{1}}
, hence

\Delta=t_{1}^{2}-4q_{1}=-3q_{1}.

Since
\gcd(q_{1},q_{2})=1
by Lemma
4.1
, then
\gcd(\Delta,q_{2})\in\{1,3\}
.

Let us assume by contradiction that
p_{2}=3
, then by Remark
3.2
we get

2-t_{1}=t_{2}=3^{a_{2}}+1-p_{1}^{a_{1}},\quad\mbox{which implies}\quad p_{1}^{a_{1}}\mp p_{1}^{\frac{a_{1}}{2}}+1=3^{a_{2}}.

If
a_{2}=1
, then
q_{2}=3
and the only odd prime powers inside its Hasse interval are
5
and
7
, both with odd exponents, contradicting the parity of
a_{1}
.
If
a_{2}>1
, then
x=-p_{1}^{\frac{a_{1}}{2}}
should be a root of one of the polynomials

x^{2}\pm x+1\in\mathbb{Z}/9\mathbb{Z}[x],

which is impossible since those polynomials are both irreducible. Hence, we conclude that
p_{2}\neq 3
, which implies
\gcd(\Delta,q_{2})=1
, then
\mathcal{E}_{2}
is ordinary by Lemma
3.10
.

Let us now consider the case
p_{1}=3
.
Since
t_{1}
is odd, by Theorem
2.1
we have

t_{1}=\begin{cases}\pm 3^{\frac{a_{1}}{2}}&\textnormal{if }a_{1}\textnormal{ is even, \quad case~\eqref{W:odd1}},\\
\pm 3^{\frac{a_{1}+1}{2}}&\textnormal{if }a_{1}\textnormal{ is odd, \ \quad case~\eqref{W:odd2}},\end{cases}

which leads us to

\Delta=\begin{cases}(3^{\frac{a_{1}}{2}})^{2}-4\cdot 3^{a_{1}}=-3^{a_{1}+1}&\textnormal{if }a_{1}\textnormal{ is even,}\\
(3^{\frac{a_{1}+1}{2}})^{2}-4\cdot 3^{a_{1}}=-3^{a_{1}}&\textnormal{if }a_{1}\textnormal{ is odd.}\end{cases}

By Lemma
4.1
we have
\gcd(q_{1},q_{2})=1
, therefore
3\nmid q_{2}
. Hence,
\Delta
and
q_{2}
cannot have common factors and we conclude that
\mathcal{E}_{2}
is ordinary by Lemma
3.10
.

Thus, for odd Hasse pairs the sets
\mathcal{E}_{i}
cannot be both supersingular, then if
\mathcal{E}_{1}\cup\mathcal{E}_{2}
is not empty, then it contains an ordinary elliptic curve.
∎

Next, we show that in odd characteristics, establishing the existence of supersingular curves in
\mathcal{E}_{1}\cup\mathcal{E}_{2}
only requires testing curves with
j
-invariant
0
.

Proposition 4.3
.

Let
(q_{1},q_{2})
be an odd Hasse pair. If
E_{1}\in\mathcal{E}_{1}
is supersingular, then
j(E_{1})=0
. Furthermore, in this case
\mathcal{E}_{2}
is ordinary and it also contains an elliptic curve with
j
-invariant
0
.

Proof.

Since
t_{1}
is odd, we are in cases (
c
) or (
d
) of Theorem
2.1
, thus by Proposition
2.2
we have
j(E_{1})=0
.
Moreover, by Proposition
4.2
,
\mathcal{E}_{2}
is ordinary and, by
[
35
, Theorem 4.2 (2)]
, the maximal order always arises as the endomorphism ring of some curve in
\mathcal{E}_{2}
. We have

t_{1}=\begin{cases}\pm p_{1}^{\frac{a_{1}}{2}}&\textnormal{if }a_{1}\textnormal{ is even,}\\
\pm 3^{\frac{a_{1}+1}{2}}&\textnormal{if }a_{1}\textnormal{ is odd,}\end{cases}\implies\Delta=\begin{cases}-3p_{1}^{a_{1}}&\textnormal{if }a_{1}\textnormal{ is even,}\\
-3^{a_{1}}&\textnormal{if }a_{1}\textnormal{ is odd.}\end{cases}

We observe that the maximal order in both cases is the ring of integers of
\mathbb{Q}(\sqrt{-3})
, which is always isomorphic to the endomorphism ring of elliptic curves of
j
-invariant
0
.
∎

4.2.
Even characteristics

In this section, we discuss Hasse pairs involving even prime powers, so we will assume
p_{1}=2
.

Lemma 4.4
.

Let
(2^{a_{1}},q_{2})
be a Hasse pair. The following are equivalent:

(1)

\mathcal{E}_{1}
is ordinary,

(2)

q_{2}
is even,

(3)

(2^{a_{1}},q_{2})\in\{((2,4)),((4,8))\}
.

Proof.

(
1
)
\Rightarrow
(
2
): Since
\mathcal{E}_{1}
is ordinary, then
t_{1}
is odd, so is
t_{2}
(Remark
3.2
). Therefore
q_{2}=t_{2}-1+2^{a_{1}}
is even.

\eqref{lem:ord-ord2-ii}\Rightarrow\eqref{lem:ord-ord2-iii}
: A straightforward check shows that there are only two powers of
2
lying in the Hasse bound of each other, namely
\{2,4\}
and
\{4,8\}
.

\eqref{lem:ord-ord2-iii}\Rightarrow\eqref{lem:ord-ord2-i}
: Every
(2^{a_{1}},q_{2})\in\{((2,4)),((4,8))\}
has discriminant
\Delta=-7
, which is coprime to
2^{a_{1}}
. Thus,
\mathcal{E}_{1}
is ordinary by Lemma
3.10
.
∎

The ordinary cases of Lemma
4.4
are detailed in Examples
7.2
and
7.3
.
Except for such cases, if
\gcd(\Delta,q_{1}q_{2})\neq 1
we can assume that
q_{2}
is odd and
\mathcal{E}_{1}
is supersingular or empty.
In the supersingular case, we know from Deuring
[
12
, §8.2]
that the only possible curves in
\mathcal{E}_{1}
have
j
-invariant
0
, similarly to the odd case (Proposition
4.3
).

However, with an even characteristic one may simultaneously find supersingular curves in both
\mathcal{E}_{1}
and
\mathcal{E}_{2}
.
To determine when this is the case we need the following lemma. It is an easy instance of the theorem of Mihăilescu
[
22
]
, which proved the famous Catalan’s conjecture, but the version we need can be proved with elementary techniques.

Lemma 4.5

(
[
28
, pp. 201-203]
)
.

Let
\alpha,\beta\geq 2
be integers and let
p\in\mathbb{N}
be a prime.
If
2^{\alpha}
and
p^{\beta}
are consecutive integers, then
\alpha=3,\beta=2
and
p=3
.

Theorem 4.6
.

Let
(q_{1},q_{2})=(2^{a_{1}},p_{2}^{a_{2}})
be a Hasse pair.
If
\mathcal{E}_{1}
is supersingular of trace
t_{1}
, then precisely one of the following holds.

(1)

t_{1}=\pm 2^{\frac{a_{1}+2}{2}}
and

(q_{1},q_{2})=\begin{cases}(2^{6},3^{4}),&\textnormal{ or }\\
\big(2^{2^{n+1}},(2^{2^{n}}+1)^{2}\big),&\textnormal{ with }p_{2}=2^{2^{n}}+1\textnormal{ a Fermat prime, or }\\
\big(2^{2m},(2^{m}-1)^{2}\big),&\textnormal{ with }p_{2}=2^{m}-1\textnormal{ a Mersenne prime. }\end{cases}

(2)

t_{1}=2
and
(q_{1},q_{2})=(2^{2},3)
.

(3)

t_{1}=0
, or
a_{1}
is odd and
t_{1}=\pm 2^{\frac{a_{1}+1}{2}}
, or
a_{1}
is even,
t_{1}=\pm 2^{\frac{a_{1}}{2}}
and
p_{2}\neq 3
.

In cases (
1
) and (
2
),
\mathcal{E}_{2}
is supersingular, while in case (
3
),
\mathcal{E}_{2}
is ordinary.

Proof.

Since
\mathcal{E}_{1}
is supersingular, then
p_{2}
is odd by Lemma
4.4
and
t_{1}
is given by one of the cases (
b
)-(
e
) of Theorem
2.1
.
We examine such cases separately.

Case (
b
):
a_{1}
is even and
t_{1}=\pm 2^{\frac{a_{1}+2}{2}}
, thus
\Delta=t_{1}^{2}-4q_{1}=2^{a_{1}+2}-4\cdot 2^{a_{1}}=0
.
Since
\Delta=t_{2}^{2}-4p_{2}^{a_{2}}
by Lemma
3.5
, then
a_{2}
is also even.
As
(q_{1},q_{2})
is Hasse, this implies that
2^{\frac{a_{1}}{2}}
and
p_{2}^{\frac{a_{2}}{2}}
are consecutive integers.
By Lemma
4.5
if
\frac{a_{1}}{2},\frac{a_{2}}{2}\geq 2
there is a unique possibility, namely
(q_{1},q_{2})=(2^{6},3^{4})
, corresponding to the consecutive integers
8
and
9
.
Now consider the case where
a_{1}
or
a_{2}
is
2
.
If
a_{1}=2
, we straightforwardly verify that there are no Hasse pairs
(4,p_{2}^{a_{2}})
with
a_{2}
even.
If
a_{2}=2
, then
2^{\frac{a_{1}}{2}}
and
p_{2}
are consecutive integers, therefore
p_{2}
is either a Fermat or a Mersenne prime.
It is easy to check that the corresponding traces
t_{2}
fit into case (
b
) of Theorem
2.1
, hence in such cases
\mathcal{E}_{2}
is supersingular.

Case (
c
):
a_{1}
is even and
t_{1}=\pm 2^{\frac{a_{1}}{2}}
, then
\Delta=-3\cdot 2^{a_{1}}
.
If
p_{2}>3
then
\mathcal{E}_{2}
is ordinary by Lemma
3.10
.
Let us consider
p_{2}=3
.
By Lemma
3.5
we have

-3\cdot 2^{a_{1}}=t_{2}^{2}-4\cdot 3^{a_{2}}.

Since
-3
is not a quadratic residue modulo
9
, the above equation may have integer solutions only if
a_{2}=1
, hence
q_{2}=3
. We straightforwardly check that the unique Hasse pair
(2^{a_{1}},3)
with
a_{1}
even is
(2^{2},3)
, which gives
t_{1}=2,t_{2}=0
and therefore
\mathcal{E}_{2}
supersingular.

Cases (
d
) and (
e
): If
a_{1}
is odd and
t_{1}=\pm 2^{\frac{a_{1}+1}{2}}
, or if
t_{1}=0
, then
\Delta
is a power of
2
, hence
\mathcal{E}_{2}
is ordinary by Lemma
3.10
.
∎

Remark 4.7
.

By Proposition
4.3
and Theorem
4.6
, both sets
\mathcal{E}_{i}
can be simultaneously supersingular only if one of the considered prime powers is even, and the other one involves primes of Fermat or Mersenne.
On the other side, if the Lenstra-Pomerance-Wagstaff conjecture
[
34
]
holds true, this would imply the existence of infinitely many Mersenne primes, hence by Theorem
4.6
both sets
\mathcal{E}_{i}
are supersingular infinitely many times.

Remark 4.8
.

When both sets
\mathcal{E}_{i}
are supersingular, they are often unrelated.
For instance, for
(q_{1},q_{2})=(2^{10},31^{2})
the set
\mathcal{E}_{1}
contains one curve of
j
-invariant
0\in\mathbb{F}_{2^{10}}
, while
\mathcal{E}_{2}
contains three curves of
j
-invariants
2,4,23\in\mathbb{F}_{31^{2}}
.
Instead, in the next sections we will prove that this is never the case when both sets
\mathcal{E}_{i}
are ordinary.

5.
The splitting behavior of the primes in odd Hasse pairs

In this section, we consider odd Hasse pairs
(q_{1},q_{2})
, and therefore the traces
t_{i}
are both odd integers.

Lemma 5.1
.

Let
(q_{1},q_{2})
be an odd Hasse pair.
Then
\Delta<0
,
\Delta\equiv 1\bmod 4
and
|\Delta|
is not a perfect square.

Proof.

Since
(q_{1},q_{2})
is Hasse and
t_{1}
is odd, by Lemma
3.7
we have
t_{1}<2\sqrt{q_{1}}
, hence by Lemma
3.5
we conclude

\Delta=t_{1}^{2}-4q_{1}<0.

Since
t_{1}^{2}\equiv 1\bmod 4
, the above equation implies
\Delta\equiv 1\bmod 4
, so
|\Delta|=-\Delta\equiv-1\bmod 4
.
Therefore
|\Delta|
cannot be a square, as it is not a quadratic residue modulo
4
.
∎

We can write the discriminant
\Delta
associated with
(q_{1},q_{2})
as

\Delta=f^{2}D,

where
f
is called the
conductor
, and
D
is the squarefree part of
\Delta
.
By Lemma
5.1
, we have
D\equiv 1\bmod 4
as well, hence
D
is a fundamental discriminant.
We denote by
K
the imaginary quadratic field

K=\mathbb{Q}\big(\sqrt{\Delta}\big)=\mathbb{Q}\big(\sqrt{D}\big),

and by
\mathcal{O}_{K}
the maximal order of
K
.

Remark 5.2
.

When
(q_{1},q_{2})
is an odd Hasse pair, we observe that we never have
K=\mathbb{Q}(i)
, since
\Delta\equiv 1\bmod 4
. Therefore, in this case, the
j
-invariant
1728
cannot correspond to an elliptic curve in either set
\mathcal{E}_{i}
.

Remark 5.3
.

By Lemma
3.5
we have
\Delta=t_{i}^{2}-4q_{i}
, from which we have

q_{i}=\frac{{t_{i}}^{2}-\Delta}{4}=\textnormal{Norm}_{K/\mathbb{Q}}\left(\frac{t_{i}+\sqrt{\Delta}}{2}\right).

It shows that
q_{i}
is the norm from
K
down to
\mathbb{Q}
, of the integral element

\pi_{i}=\frac{t_{i}+\sqrt{\Delta}}{2}\in\mathcal{O}_{K}.

Furthermore, by Remark
3.2
, we obtain the relation

(1)

\pi_{i}=1-\overline{\pi_{j}},\qquad i\neq j.

Remark 5.4
.

Since
\Delta
is not a square, the polynomial
f_{i}(x)=x^{2}-t_{i}x+q_{i}\in\mathbb{Z}[x]
is irreducible for
i\in\{1,2\}
, and it is the minimal polynomial of the element
\pi_{i}\in\mathcal{O}_{K}
. In particular,
\mathbb{Z}[\pi_{i}]
are both suborders of
\mathcal{O}_{K}
.

Remark 5.5
.

The discriminant
\delta_{i}
of each order
\mathbb{Z}[\pi_{i}]
can be easily computed as

\delta_{i}=\left(\det\begin{pmatrix}1&\pi_{i}\\
1&\overline{\pi}_{i}\end{pmatrix}\right)^{2}=\Delta=f^{2}D.

Since
\pi_{1}-\pi_{2}=1-t_{2}
by Remark
3.2
, we also have
\mathbb{Z}[\pi_{1}]=\mathcal{O}_{\Delta}=\mathbb{Z}[\pi_{2}]
.

Proposition 5.6
.

Let
(q_{1},q_{2})
be an odd Hasse pair.
Then at least one of the base primes
p_{i}
splits completely in
K
.

Proof.

We equivalently show that in
\mathcal{O}_{K}
: (a) the
p_{i}
’s cannot be both inert, and (b) if
p_{i}
ramifies, then
p_{j}
splits for
j\neq i
.

Case (a): The fact that
\textnormal{Norm}_{K/\mathbb{Q}}(\pi_{i})=q_{i}=p_{i}^{a_{i}}
implies that
p_{i}
is the unique prime of
\mathcal{O}_{K}
that divides
\pi_{i}
, since
\mathcal{O}_{K}
is Dedekind and we have unique factorization of ideals.
Thus, for both
i\in\{1,2\}
there are positive integer
b_{i}\in\mathbb{Z}_{>0}
such that

(\pi_{i})=(p_{i}^{b_{i}}).

If both primes
p_{i}
were inert in
K
, by taking norms in the above equation, we would have
p_{i}^{a_{i}}=q_{i}=\textnormal{Norm}_{K/\mathbb{Q}}(\pi_{i})=p_{i}^{2b_{i}}
.
This in turn gives us that
a_{i}=2b_{i}
should be both even.
Since
q_{1}\neq q_{2}
, it would imply that

|\sqrt{q_{1}}-\sqrt{q_{2}}|=|p_{1}^{b_{1}}-p_{2}^{b_{2}}|>1,

contradicting the fact that
(q_{1},q_{2})
is Hasse.

Case (b): Assume without loss of generality that
p_{1}
ramifies in
K
.
From Case (a) we know that only one of the exponents
a_{i}
can be even.

(i) Let us first assume that
a_{1}
is odd.
Since
p_{1}
ramifies in
\mathcal{O}_{K}
, this implies that
p_{1}
should divide the fundamental discriminant
D
of
K
, and therefore the power of
p_{1}
dividing
\Delta
must be odd. We recall that in the case of odd Hasse pairs, the fundamental discriminant is not divisible by
4
and is therefore squarefree.
From the following relation

t_{1}^{2}-4q_{1}=\Delta=f^{2}D,

it follows that
p_{1}|t_{1}
.
Therefore, there exist
k_{1}\in\mathbb{Z}
and
b_{1}\in\mathbb{Z}_{>0}
such that
t_{1}=k_{1}p_{1}^{b_{1}}
, with
\gcd(k_{1},p_{1})=1
.
By considering the parity of the exponents of
p_{i}
in the above equation, we have that
2b_{1}>a_{1}
.
However, since
\Delta<0
and
p_{1}
is odd, this case can only happen when
p_{1}=3
and
|t_{1}|=3^{\frac{a_{1}+1}{2}}
.
This is case (
d
) of Theorem
2.1
, which implies that
\mathcal{E}_{1}
is supersingular.
Proposition
4.2
now implies that
\text{gcd}(\Delta,p_{2})=1
, from which we conclude that
\Delta=t_{2}^{2}-4q_{2}
is a non-zero quadratic residue modulo
p_{2}
, and so is
D
since
D\equiv(\frac{t_{2}}{f})^{2}\bmod p_{2}
. Therefore
p_{2}
must split in
K
by
[
10
, Proposition 5.16]
.

(ii) Assume now that
a_{1}
is even, so
a_{2}
is odd. Then, by Case (a)
p_{2}
cannot be inert. From Case (b-i) above, we see that
p_{2}
cannot ramify either, because this would imply that
p_{1}
splits, contradicting our assumption that
p_{1}
ramifies. Hence,
p_{2}
must split in
K
.
∎

Remark 5.7
.

We note that if
\mathcal{E}_{i}
is ordinary, then
p_{i}
splits because
\Delta\equiv t_{i}^{2}\bmod p_{i}
. The opposite, however, might not hold, since
p_{i}
may split and divide the conductor
f
of
\Delta
.
In particular, Proposition
5.6
alone does not guarantee the existence of an ordinary elliptic curve in
\mathcal{E}_{1}\cup\mathcal{E}_{2}
.

In the case of odd Hasse pairs
(p_{1},p_{2})
with
p_{1},p_{2}
pure primes, one can conclude that both of them split completely in
K
unless
(p_{1},p_{2})=((3,7))
(Example
5.8
).
However, this does not have to be true for prime powers with exponent
>1
(Example
5.9
).
Thus, the splitting of one
p_{i}
given by Proposition
5.6
is the best one can guarantee for Hasse pairs in general.

Example 5.8
.

Let us consider the Hasse pair
(3,7)
as in Example
3.13
, whose discriminant is
\Delta=-3
.
By Remarks
3.12
and
5.7
, this is the unique odd Hasse pair consisting of pure primes that are not both guaranteed to split completely.
In fact,
(3)=(\sqrt{-3})^{2}\subset\mathcal{O}_{K}
is ramified, while
(7)
splits because
\mathcal{E}_{2}
is ordinary (Remark
5.7
).

Example 5.9
.

Since
\sqrt{307}=17.521\dots
, then
(17^{2},307)
is an odd Hasse pair, whose discriminant is
\Delta=-3\cdot 17^{2}
.
As
\mathcal{E}_{2}
is ordinary by Lemma
3.10
, then
(307)
splits completely in
K
(Remark
5.7
).
On the other hand,
(17)
is prime (inert) in
K
.

For the remainder of this section, we study under which conditions
\mathcal{E}_{1}\cup\mathcal{E}_{2}
may be empty.

Definition 5.10
.

Let
\mathcal{O}
be an order with fraction field
K
.
We say that an ideal
\mathfrak{a}\subset\mathcal{O}
is
proper
whenever
\mathcal{O}=\{b\in K\ |\ b\mathfrak{a}=\mathfrak{a}\}
.
We recall that principal ideals are always proper
[
10
, p. 135]
.
A proper
\mathcal{O}
-ideal is called
primitive
if it is not of the form
d\mathfrak{a}
for some proper ideal
\mathfrak{a}
and
d\in\mathbb{Z}_{>0}
.
We will call an element
\lambda\in\mathcal{O}
primitive if
(\lambda)
is a primitive ideal.

Proposition 5.11
.

Let
(q_{1},q_{2})
be an odd Hasse pair with corresponding traces
t_{i}=k_{i}p_{i}^{b_{i}}
, where
b_{i}\in\mathbb{N}
and
\gcd(k_{i},p_{i})=1
. Then one of the sets
\mathcal{E}_{i}
is ordinary, except possibly when
b_{i}\geq 1
,
a_{i}>2b_{i}
and
p_{i}^{2b_{i}}||\Delta
for both
i\in\{1,2\}
.

Proof.

By Proposition
4.2
it is sufficient to show that if the above conditions are not satisfied, then
\mathcal{E}_{1}\cup\mathcal{E}_{2}
is non-empty. We show the result in steps.

Step 1:
If either one of the
\pi_{i}
’s is primitive, then
\mathcal{E}_{i}
is non-empty:

(i): If
\gcd(t_{i},\Delta)=1
, then
\mathcal{E}_{i}
is ordinary by Lemma
3.10
.

(ii): If
\gcd(t_{i},\Delta)>1
, since
\pi_{i}
is primitive then in particular
p_{i}
cannot divide the conductor. This implies that
p_{i}
must divide
D
and hence ramifies.
From Case(b-i) of Proposition
5.6
, this can only happen when
p_{i}=3
and
|t_{i}|=3^{\frac{a_{i}+1}{2}}
, which implies that
\mathcal{E}_{i}
is supersingular.

Step 2:
If
2b_{i}\geq a_{i}
, then
\mathcal{E}_{1}\cup\mathcal{E}_{2}
is non-empty:

We rewrite the norm equation

t_{i}^{2}-4q_{i}=p_{i}^{a_{i}}(k_{i}^{2}p_{i}^{2b_{i}-a_{i}}-4)=\Delta<0\ \Rightarrow\ k_{i}^{2}p_{i}^{2b_{i}-a_{i}}<4.

Then we must have
k_{i}=\pm 1
, and either

(i)
2b_{i}>a_{i}
, in which case we should have
p_{i}=3
and
\mathcal{E}_{i}
is supersingular by case (
d
) of Theorem
2.1
, or

(ii)
2b_{i}=a_{i}
, which implies that
2b_{j}>a_{j}
and this is case (i) right above.

Step 3:
Given Steps 1 and 2,
\mathcal{E}_{1}\cup\mathcal{E}_{2}
may be empty only if neither
\pi_{i}
is primitive and
2b_{i}<a_{i}
for both
i\in\{1,2\}
. Given the norm equation, this again implies that
p_{i}^{2b_{i}}||\Delta
for both
i\in\{1,2\}
.
∎

Corollary 5.12
.

If
a_{i}\leq 2
for some
i\in\{1,2\}
, then at least one of the sets
\mathcal{E}_{i}
is ordinary.

Proof.

If for some
i\in\{1,2\}
we have
a_{i}\leq 2
, then we cannot have
a_{i}>2b_{i}
and
b_{i}\geq 1
, hence we are not in the exceptional case of Proposition
5.11
.
∎

A necessary condition for an odd Hasse pair to satisfy the exceptional conditions of Proposition
5.11
is having positive integers
a_{i}>2b_{i}
and odd integer primes
p_{i}
such that

\begin{cases}p_{1}^{a_{1}}\equiv 1\bmod p_{2}^{b_{2}},\\
p_{2}^{a_{2}}\equiv 1\bmod p_{1}^{b_{1}},\\
\left|\sqrt{p_{1}^{a_{1}}}-\sqrt{p_{2}^{a_{2}}}\right|\leq 1.\\
\end{cases}

Even for
b_{i}=1
, we have found no evidence of such values for moderately small prime powers (
p_{i}\leq 10^{5}
and
a_{i}\leq 10^{3}
).
Moreover, large prime powers are expected to be asymptotically “very far apart”
[
26
,
27
]
, therefore finding large solutions to the above inequality seems hard.

6.
The isogeny graphs of ordinary Hasse pairs

6.1.
The graph vertices: Sets of
j
-invariants

In this section, we consider the case where both sets
\mathcal{E}_{i}
are ordinary and we will refer to the pair
(q_{1},q_{2})
as an
ordinary Hasse pair
.
In such cases,
\gcd(\Delta,q_{1}q_{2})=1
and
\Delta
is odd, therefore the fundamental discriminant
D
is squarefree.

By Lemma
4.4
there are only two non-odd ordinary Hasse pairs, up to permutation, and it may be directly verified that their isogeny graphs (as will be defined in Section
6.2
) are isomorphic (see Examples
7.2
and
7.3
).
Therefore, we can restrict to odd ordinary Hasse pairs and use our definitions and results from Section
5
.

We recall from Remark
5.5
that the order
\mathbb{Z}[\pi_{1}]=\mathcal{O}_{\Delta}=\mathbb{Z}[\pi_{2}]
is of discriminant
\Delta=f^{2}D
. For any positive divisor
g
of
f
, there corresponds an order
\mathcal{O}_{g^{2}D}
, with discriminant
g^{2}D|\Delta
and ideal class group
\textnormal{Cl}(\mathcal{O}_{g^{2}D})

[
10
, Lemma 7.2]
.
The cardinality
h_{g^{2}D}=|\textnormal{Cl}(\mathcal{O}_{g^{2}D})|
of the ideal class group is known as the class number of
\mathcal{O}_{g^{2}D}
.
We denote by
H_{g^{2}D}(x)
the Hilbert class polynomial of
\mathcal{O}_{g^{2}D}
and by
L_{g^{2}D}
the splitting field of
H_{g^{2}D}(x)
, also known as the ring class field of
\mathcal{O}_{g^{2}D}
.
Given any set of representatives
\{\mathfrak{a}_{s}\}_{1\leq s\leq h_{g^{2}D}}
for
\textnormal{Cl}(\mathcal{O}_{g^{2}D})
, the Hilbert class polynomial
H_{g^{2}D}(x)
is given by

H_{g^{2}D}(x)=\prod_{1\leq s\leq h_{g^{2}D}}\big(x-j(\mathfrak{a}_{s})\big)\in\mathbb{Z}[x].

We also have a group isomorphism
\textnormal{Cl}(\mathcal{O}_{g^{2}D})\simeq\textnormal{Gal}(L_{g^{2}D}/K)
,
which can be explicitly described as follows
[
19
, Theorem 5, Chapt. 10, §3]
:

\mathfrak{b}\mapsto\sigma_{\mathfrak{b}},\quad\textnormal{where}\quad\sigma_{\mathfrak{b}}j(\mathfrak{a})=j(\mathfrak{b}^{-1}\mathfrak{a}).

In particular, the ideal class group
\textnormal{Cl}(\mathcal{O}_{g^{2}D})
acts on the set
\{j(\mathfrak{a}_{s})\}_{1\leq s\leq h_{g^{2}D}}
freely and transitively, and therefore
L_{g^{2}D}=K\big(j(\mathfrak{a})\big)
for any
\mathfrak{a}\in\textnormal{Cl}(\mathcal{O}_{g^{2}D})
.

Lemma 6.1
.

Let
(q_{1},q_{2})
be an ordinary Hasse pair. The ideals
(\pi_{i})\subset\mathcal{O}_{K}

are proper, primitive, and
(\pi_{i})=\mathfrak{p}_{i}^{a_{i}}
, where the
\mathfrak{p}_{i}
’s are prime ideals such that
\mathfrak{p}_{i}\overline{\mathfrak{p}}_{i}=(p_{i})
and
\mathfrak{p}_{i}\neq\overline{\mathfrak{p}}_{i}
.

Proof.

Since we are in the ordinary case we must have that
\gcd(t_{i},\Delta)=1
, which implies that
p_{i}\nmid t_{i}
and
p_{i}\nmid f
for both
i\in\{1,2\}
.
As a consequence
[
35
, Lemma, §4, p. 537]
both primes
p_{i}
must split in
K
as
p_{i}=\mathfrak{p}_{i}\overline{\mathfrak{p}}_{i}
,
\mathfrak{p}_{i}\neq\overline{\mathfrak{p}}_{i}
.
Let us consider the norm equation

(q_{i})=(\pi_{i})(\overline{\pi}_{i})=\mathfrak{p}_{i}^{a_{i}}\overline{\mathfrak{p}}_{i}^{a_{i}}.

Since the norms of
\mathfrak{p}_{i}^{a_{i}}
and
(\pi_{i})
are prime to the conductor, these ideals are therefore primitive and they belong to the group of ideals with unique factorization in the order
\mathcal{O}_{\Delta}\subseteq\mathcal{O}_{K}
.
We conclude that
(\pi_{i})=\mathfrak{p}_{i}^{m_{i}}\overline{\mathfrak{p}}_{i}^{n_{i}}
, for some
m_{i},n_{i}\in\mathbb{N}
such that
m_{i}+n_{i}=a_{i}
.
Assume by way of contradiction that both
m_{i},n_{i}
are not zero. Then
p_{i}|\pi_{i}
and therefore
p_{i}|t_{i}=\pi_{i}+\overline{\pi}_{i}
, contradiction.
Therefore we have that either
m_{i}=0
or
n_{i}=0
, so up to conjugation we have

(\pi_{i})=\mathfrak{p}_{i}^{a_{i}}\ \text{in}\ \mathcal{O}_{\Delta}\subseteq\mathcal{O}_{K}
.
∎

Lemma 6.2
.

Let
(q_{1},q_{2})
be an ordinary Hasse pair.
For every
g\in\mathbb{Z}_{>0}
with
g|f
,
H_{g^{2}D}
splits completely with distinct roots in both
\mathbb{F}_{q_{i}}[x]
.
In particular,
H_{\Delta}
splits completely with distinct roots in both
\mathbb{F}_{q_{i}}[x]
.

Proof.

Let us fix primes
\mathfrak{B}_{i}
of
\mathcal{O}_{L}
above
\mathfrak{p}_{i}
.
Since our primes
p_{i}
neither ramify nor divide the conductor, then by
[
24
, Proposition I.8.3]
, the Hilbert class polynomial
H_{\Delta}
splits completely with distinct roots in both finite fields
\mathbb{F}_{p_{i}^{\iota_{i}}}
, where
\iota_{i}
is called the inertia degree and it is the degree of the residue field extension

(2)

\iota_{i}=[\mathcal{O}_{L_{\Delta}}/\mathfrak{B}_{i}:\mathcal{O}_{\Delta}/\mathfrak{p}_{i}].

Since the inertia degree is the smallest power to which the base ideal,
\mathfrak{p}_{i}
in this case, becomes principal in
\textnormal{Cl}(\mathcal{O}_{\Delta})
, and since
\mathfrak{p}_{i}^{a_{i}}=(\pi_{i})
by Lemma
6.1
, then
\iota_{i}|a_{i}
.
From the fact that
h_{g^{2}D}|h_{\Delta}
, we have that the primes
\mathfrak{p}_{i}\mathcal{O}_{g^{2}D}
must also become principal when raised to
a_{i}
, for both
i\in\{1,2\}
. Hence, by the same
[
24
, Proposition I.8.3]
,
H_{g^{2}D}
must also split completely with distinct roots over both
\mathbb{F}_{q_{i}}[x]
.
∎

For each
g|f
, we denote by
J_{g^{2}D}
the set of complex roots of the corresponding Hilbert Class Polynomial
H_{g^{2}D}
. We note that two different
H_{g^{2}D}
cannot have common roots, and
we denote by
J
the (disjoint) union of all these sets.
We similarly denote by
J_{{(g^{2}D)}_{i}}
and
J_{i}
the sets of roots of the same polynomials in
\mathbb{F}_{q_{i}}
.

Remark 6.3
.

Since
H_{g^{2}D}
splits completely by Lemma
6.2
, the cardinality of each
J_{g^{2}D}
is
h_{g^{2}D}
, i.e.

|J_{g^{2}D}|=|J_{{(g^{2}D)}_{1}}|=|J_{{(g^{2}D)}_{2}}|.

Therefore, we also have

|J|=|J_{1}|=|J_{2}|.

We also remark that, given Lemma
6.2
, the sets
J_{{(g^{2}D)}_{i}}
are already defined in
\mathbb{F}_{p_{i}^{\iota_{i}}}\hookrightarrow\mathbb{F}_{q_{i}}
.

Since we are in the ordinary case, by Remark
3.9
each set
\mathcal{E}_{i}
can be identified with the set of
j
-invariants of their elliptic curves. We note that this cannot be done in the supersingular case (Example
7.1
).
Remark
6.3
now implies that we can identify the sets
\mathcal{E}_{i}
with
J_{i}
.

Lemma 6.4
.

Let
j^{(0)}\in L
be a root of
H_{\Delta}
and
r
be a fixed square root of
\Delta
. Let also
D_{0}=\textnormal{Disc}(H_{\Delta})\in\mathbb{Z}
.
For every
j\in J
, there exists
h_{j}(x,y)\in\mathbb{Z}[x,y]
such that

j=\frac{h_{j}(j^{(0)},r)}{2\,D_{0}}.

Moreover, if
p
is an odd prime such that
H_{\Delta}
does not have repeated roots over
\bar{\mathbb{F}}_{p}
, then
\gcd(2\,D_{0},p)=1
.

Proof.

For every order
\mathcal{O}_{\Delta}\subseteq\mathcal{O}\subseteq\mathcal{O}_{K}
and every
j\in J
, we have
j\in L_{g^{2}D}\subseteq L=K(j^{(0)})

[
19
, Theorem 10.6]
and
H_{\mathcal{O}}(j)=0
.
Since
H_{\mathcal{O}}
is a monic integral polynomial, then
j\in\mathcal{O}_{L}
.
By classical properties of simple separable extensions
[
24
, Lemma I.2.9]
, we have

\mathcal{O}_{L}\subseteq D_{0}^{-1}\mathcal{O}_{K}[j^{(0)}]\subseteq(2D_{0})^{-1}\mathbb{Z}[j^{(0)},r].

Thus, every
j\in\mathcal{O}_{L}
can be written as
\frac{h_{j}(j^{(0)},r)}{2D_{0}}
for some
h_{j}(x)\in\mathbb{Z}[x,y]
.
Moreover, since
H_{\Delta}
is the minimal polynomial of
j^{(0)}\in L
over
\mathbb{Q}

[
29
, Theorem II.4.3]
, we have

D_{0}=\pm\textnormal{Disc}(H_{\Delta})=\pm\textnormal{Res}_{x}\big(H_{\Delta}(x),H_{\Delta}^{\prime}(x)\big)\in\mathbb{Z}.

Then
H_{\Delta}
has no repeated roots in
\bar{\mathbb{F}}_{p}
precisely when
D_{0}=\pm\textnormal{Res}_{x}\big(H_{\Delta}(x),H_{\Delta}^{\prime}(x)\big)\in\bar{\mathbb{F}}_{p}^{*}
, i.e. when
D_{0}
is coprime to
p
.
∎

Remark 6.5
.

We fix once and for all a square root
r
of
\Delta\in\mathbb{C}
, and an element
j^{(0)}\in J_{\Delta}
, which generates
L_{\Delta}
over
K
.
Now, given Remark
6.3
and Lemma
6.4
, any fixed choice of a square root
r_{i}
of
\Delta\in\mathbb{F}_{p_{i}^{\iota_{i}}}
and
j^{(0)}_{i}\in J_{(\Delta)_{i}}
, induces an explicit bijection

\Pi_{i}:J\to J_{i}\subseteq\mathbb{F}_{p_{i}^{\iota_{i}}},\quad\frac{h_{j}(j^{(0)},r)}{2D_{0}}\mapsto\frac{h_{j}(j^{(0)}_{i},r_{i})}{2D_{0}}.

By embedding
\mathbb{F}_{p_{i}^{\iota_{i}}}
into
\mathbb{F}_{q_{i}}
, this extends to a bijection
\Pi_{i}:J\to J_{i}\subseteq\mathbb{F}_{q_{i}}
, which we denote in the same way. We notice that the above choices of
r_{i}
and
j^{(0)}_{i}
are associated with a fixed choice of primes
\mathfrak{P}_{i}\subset\mathcal{O}_{L}
and
\mathfrak{p}_{i}\subset\mathcal{O}_{K}
such that
\mathfrak{P}_{i}|\mathfrak{p}_{i}|p_{i}
, for both
i\in\{1,2\}
.

6.2.
The Isogeny Graphs

Definition 6.6
.

Let
\textnormal{B}\subset\mathbb{N}
be a finite set of integer primes, and
\mathcal{E}
be a set of elliptic curves defined over a finite field
\Bbbk
, up to
\Bbbk
-isomorphism.
We define the
B
-isogeny graph
of
\mathcal{E}
, denoted by
G_{\textnormal{B}}(\mathcal{E})
, as the directed colored multigraph, whose vertices are the elements in
\mathcal{E}
, and the edges are cyclic
\Bbbk
-isogenies between them.
The color of any edge corresponds to the degree of the isogeny, which is some prime in
B
.

Remark 6.7
.

When drawing isogeny graphs we use the convention that an undirected edge corresponds to one edge in each direction.
We recall that
j=1728
can appear in an ordinary
G_{\textnormal{B}}(\mathcal{E})
only in even characteristic (so it equals
j=0
) by Remark
5.2
.
Since every isogeny has a dual isogeny
[
30
, Section III.6]
, and
\textnormal{Aut}(E)=\{\pm\textnormal{id}\}
for every elliptic curve
E
with
j(E)\neq 0,1728

[
30
, Section III.10]
, our graphs may be thought of as undirected, with the only possible exception of isogenies from
j=0
.

Lemma 6.8
.

Let
(q_{1},q_{2})
be an ordinary Hasse pair.
For both
i\in\{1,2\}
, the graphs
G_{\{p_{i}\}}(\mathcal{E}_{1})
and
G_{\{p_{i}\}}(\mathcal{E}_{2})
are isomorphic and consist of
h_{\Delta}/\iota_{i}
cycles of length
\iota_{i}
(as defined in (
2
)).

Proof.

Since the relation is symmetric, we can assume without loss of generality that
i=2
.
We can also restrict to vertices contained in
J_{(\Delta)_{1}}
and
J_{(\Delta)_{2}}
, because all the
p_{2}
-isogenies are horizontal since
(q_{1},q_{2})
is ordinary.
We compute the eigenvalues of the
\pi_{i}
’s by examining the splitting of their minimal polynomials
f_{i}(x)\bmod p_{2}
. Given Remark
5.4
, a simple computation shows that

f_{1}(x)=f_{2}(-x+1).

We compute the eigenvalues
\{\mu_{i},\nu_{i}\}
of each
\pi_{i}
as the roots of

\displaystyle f_{2}(x)

\displaystyle\equiv x(x-t_{2})\bmod p_{2},

\displaystyle f_{1}(x)=f_{2}(-x+1)

\displaystyle\equiv(x-1)\big(x-(1-t_{2})\big)\bmod p_{2}.

For ordinary elliptic curves in characteristic
p_{2}
, there are only two isogenies of degree
p_{2}
: the Frobenius isogeny, which is inseparable, and its dual, called the
Verschiebung
, which is separable.
The Frobenius isogeny creates cycles of length equal to the degree of the minimal extension field where the elliptic curve is defined, which in this case is
\iota_{2}
as in (
2
). Therefore, the number of cycles in this case is
h_{\Delta}/\iota_{2}
.

We now look at the eigenvalues
\lambda_{1}=1
and
\mu_{1}=1-t_{2}
of
f_{1}
.
We see that
\mu_{1}\not\equiv 0\bmod p_{2}
since otherwise, from the equation
q_{1}=q_{2}+1-t_{2}
, we would have that
p_{2}|q_{1}
, which is not possible for odd Hasse pairs.
Furthermore,
\lambda_{1}\not\equiv\mu_{1}\bmod p_{2}
, as
t_{2}\equiv 0\bmod p_{2}
would lead to supersingular curves.
Since we have two distinct eigenvalues, this implies that
p_{2}
is an Elkies prime and we have
h_{\Delta}/\iota_{2}
cycles of degree-
p_{2}
isogenies in this case as well (
[
32
, Theorem 7]
).
Thus,
G_{\{p_{2}\}}(\mathcal{E}_{1})
and
G_{\{p_{2}\}}(\mathcal{E}_{2})
have the same structure.
∎

Theorem 6.9
.

Let
(q_{1},q_{2})
be an ordinary Hasse pair and
B\subset\mathbb{N}
be a finite set of primes.
Then
G_{\textnormal{B}}(\mathcal{E}_{1})
is isomorphic to
G_{\textnormal{B}}(\mathcal{E}_{2})
.

Proof.

Let
G_{\textnormal{B}}(\Delta)
be the directed colored multigraph (in characteristic zero) whose vertices are the elements of
J
and whose edges
\phi:j_{1}\to j_{2}
represent isogenies of prime color
\deg\phi=\ell
between curves with
j
-invariants
j_{1}
and
j_{2}
, for any
\ell\in B
.

By adapting
[
29
, Proposition II.4.4]
to the context of Remark
6.5
, for both
i\in\{1,2\}
we have an injection

\textnormal{Hom}(j_{1},j_{2})\to\textnormal{Hom}\big(\Pi_{i}(j_{1}),\Pi_{i}(j_{2})\big),

which preserves the degree of the isogenies.
Therefore, there is an isomorphic copy of
G_{\textnormal{B}}(\Delta)
over both the
\mathbb{F}_{p_{i}^{\iota_{i}}}
’s.
Since the isogenies, as well as the
j
-invariants involved, are defined over
\mathbb{F}_{p_{i}^{\iota_{i}}}
, they are also defined over
\mathbb{F}_{q_{i}}
, therefore the maps
\Pi_{i}
are isomorphic graph embeddings of
G_{\textnormal{B}}(\Delta)
into the
G_{\textnormal{B}}(\mathcal{E}_{i})
’s.

Since all the considered curves are ordinary, the structure of the isogeny graphs
G_{\textnormal{B}}(\Delta)
,
G_{\textnormal{B}}(\mathcal{E}_{1})
, and
G_{\textnormal{B}}(\mathcal{E}_{2})
only depends on
\Delta
.
Therefore, the number and type of isogenies (horizontal or vertical) from curves with
j
-invariant different from
0
are the same in both characteristics (by
[
32
, Theorem 7]
and
[
17
, Proposition 23]
) for isogenies of degree
l\not\in\{p_{1},p_{2}\}
.
By Lemma
6.8
, the same holds for isogenies of degree
p_{1}
and
p_{2}
.

Finally, either none or both sets
\mathcal{E}_{i}
contain a curve
E^{0}_{i}
with
j(E^{0}_{i})=0
, as this occurs if and only if the squarefree part of
\Delta
is
D=-3
.
If such curves exist, then we have

\textnormal{End}_{\mathbb{F}_{p_{1}}}(E^{0}_{1})\simeq\textnormal{End}_{\mathbb{F}_{p_{2}}}(E^{0}_{2})\simeq\mathbb{Z}\left[\frac{1+\sqrt{-3}}{2}\right].

Since a
j
-invariant zero curve over
\mathbb{F}_{p_{i}^{a_{i}}}
is ordinary if and only if
p_{i}\equiv 1\bmod 3
, then
\sqrt{-3}
is defined over both the
\mathbb{F}_{p_{i}}
’s (and consequently over the
\mathbb{F}_{q_{i}}
’s).
Thus, in both the
G_{\textnormal{B}}(\mathcal{E}_{i})
’s, if we have any descending
l
-isogenies
0\to j\neq 0
, then there are precisely three distinct
l
-isogenies from
0
to
j
.
Moreover, in this case, both the isogeny graphs have exactly
(\frac{\Delta}{l})+1
isogenies of degree
l
from
0
to itself.
∎

Furthermore, in the last section we observed that, regardless of the characteristic, when
\mathcal{E}_{1}
and
\mathcal{E}_{2}
are both ordinary, we have a perfect correspondence between their isogeny graphs.
This relation is effective only on paper at the moment, as we lack an efficient way of passing from one isogeny graph to the other.
Performing such correspondence efficiently, i.e. without passing through the construction of huge Hilbert class polynomials in characteristic zero, is regarded as an ambitious task, which could lead to interesting cryptographic applications.

Finally, similarly to how aliquot cycles extend the idea of amicable primes

Appendix A
Hasse pairs and Andrica’s conjecture

by Pieter Moree and Efthymios Sofos

Recall that a pair of prime
powers
(q_{1},q_{2})

is called a
Hasse pair
if

(4)

\mid\sqrt{q_{1}}-\sqrt{q_{2}}\mid\leq 1.

If both
q_{1}
and
q_{2}
are odd, then the
pair is called an
odd Hasse pair
.
Using Lemma
4.5
it is easy to see that if
\sqrt{q_{1}}-\sqrt{q_{2}}=1
, then
necessarily
q_{1}=81
and
q_{2}=64
, thus
replacing
\leq 1
by
<1
is almost equivalent.

It is natural to ask if there are many Hasse pairs.
In this direction we contribute the following
result.

Theorem A.1
.

Fix any
\epsilon>0
. There exists a constant
c(\epsilon)
such that
for every prime
p<x
and real number
x\geq 2
, with at most
c(\epsilon)\,x^{3/5+\epsilon}

exceptions, there are at least
\frac{1}{2}\sqrt{p}\,(\log p)^{-1}
Hasse pairs

(p,\ell)
with
\ell
a prime.

We will apply some ideas from
analytic number theory to this question. Since the number of primes up
to
x
grows asymptotically as
x/\log x
and the number of
prime powers
p^{m}
with
m\geq 2
only as
\sqrt{x}
, which is

\leq c(\epsilon)\,x^{3/5+\epsilon}
for every
x
sufficiently large, we see
that Theorem
A.1
implies the following corollary.

Corollary A.2
.

Theorem
A.1
also holds true if instead of primes we consider prime powers.

Thus typically a large prime (power) occurs as member of a
large number

of Hasse pairs.
However, we cannot exclude the existence of a prime power
that
never
occurs as a member of a Hasse pair.

Conjecture 1
.

Every prime power is member of some Hasse pair.

Every odd prime power is member of some odd Hasse pair.

In Section
A.2
we show that this conjecture is intimately
connected with Andrica’s conjecture.
Research on that conjecture then shows that Conjecture
1
is
certainly true for all prime powers
<10^{19}
.
Let
p_{1}=2,p_{2}=3,\ldots
be the sequence of
consecutive primes.

Proposition A.3
.

If Andrica’s conjecture that
(p_{j},p_{j+1})
is always a Hasse pair is
true, then so is Conjecture
1
.

Proof.

If
q
is a prime power, we let
p_{j}
be the largest prime
\leq q
.
Then
(q,p_{j+1})
is a Hasse pair by Andrica’s conjecture.
If
q
is odd, then
(q,p_{j+1})
is an odd Hasse pair. ∎

A.1.
Proof of Theorem
A.1

Theorem
A.1
is in essence a corollary of the following
deep result due to Heath-Brown
[
13
]
.

Theorem A.4

(Heath-Brown
[
13
]
)
.

For any fixed
\epsilon>0
the measure of the set of
y\in[0,x]
such that

\max_{0\leq h\leq\sqrt{y}}\left|\pi(y+h)-\pi(y)-\int_{y}^{y+h}\frac{\mathrm{d}t}{\log t}\right|\geq\frac{\sqrt{y}}{(\log y)(\log\log y)}

is
O_{\epsilon}(x^{3/5+\epsilon})
.

Proof of Theorem
A.1
:
Let
B_{x}
be the set of primes
p\in[2,x]

such that the interval
(p,p+\sqrt{p})
contains
\leq\sqrt{p}/(2\log p)
primes.
Our plan is to show that for any
p\in B_{x}\cap[2,x-1]
the interval

(p,p+1)
contributes towards the measure in Theorem
A.4
, from which we can directly
deduce that the number of elements in
B_{x}
is
c(\epsilon)\,x^{3/5+\epsilon}
.

For any
p\in B_{x}
and
y\in(p,p+1)

we have that the number of primes in the interval

(y,y+\sqrt{p})
is at most
2
plus the number of primes in the interval
(p,p+\sqrt{p})
, which by
assumption is
\leq 2+\sqrt{p}/(2\log p)
.
Now for all sufficiently large
p
by trivial calculus this is

\leq\sqrt{p}/(\log 2p)-\sqrt{p+1}/(\log{(p+1)\log\log(p+1)})

and by the monotonicity of the log function this is

\leq\int_{y}^{y+\sqrt{p}}\frac{\mathrm{d}t}{\log t}-\frac{\sqrt{p+1}}{\log(p+1)\log\log(p+1)},

but since

y<p+1
,
this is

\leq\int_{y}^{y+\sqrt{p}}\frac{\mathrm{d}t}{\log t}-\frac{\sqrt{y}}{\log y\log\log y}.

Now taking
h=\sqrt{p}
we note that
h\leq\sqrt{y}

and hence we have shown that if
p\in B_{x}
, then every
y
in the
interval
(p,p+1)
violates the inequality in Theorem
A.4
.
\square

A.2.
Andrica’s conjecture

Let
d_{n}:=p_{n+1}-p_{n}
be the
n
th prime gap.
Over the centuries many conjectures involving

d_{n}
have been made. Here we are interested in a conjecture
made in 1986 by Dorin Andrica
[
1
]
.

Conjecture 2

(Andrica
[
1
]
)
.

The inequality

(5)

\sqrt{p_{n+1}}-\sqrt{p_{n}}<1

holds for all
n
.

A lot of numerical work on large gaps has
been done.
This can be used to infer that
Andrica’s conjecture holds for
p_{n}\leq 2\cdot 2^{63}\approx 1.8\cdot 10^{19}
; see Visser
[
33
]
.

An equivalent formulation of Andrica’s conjecture is that

(6)

d_{n}<2\sqrt{p_{n}}+1

holds for all
n
. It seems to be far out of reach, as under RH the best
result is due to Cramér
[
11
]
who showed in
already 1920 that
d_{n}=O(\sqrt{p_{n}}\log p_{n})
. More explicitly,
Carneiro et al.
[
8
]
showed under RH that
d_{n}\leq\frac{22}{25}\sqrt{p_{n}}\log p_{n}

for every
p_{n}>3
. Unconditionally Baker et
al.
[
3
]
showed that
d_{n}<p_{n}^{0.525}
for all
n
sufficiently
large.

Heath-Brown used Theorem
A.4
to obtain the
following result on large gaps between consecutive primes,
which is a culmination of an enormous amount of research done over
many years.

Theorem A.5

(Heath-Brown
[
13
]
)
.

We have

\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}p_{n}\leq x\\
p_{n+1}-p_{n}\geq\sqrt{p_{n}}\end{subarray}}(p_{n+1}-p_{n})\ll_{\epsilon}x^{3/5+\epsilon}.

For a table of earlier (larger) exponents than
3/5
achieved and their authors, see Moree
[
23
]
.

The authors (with Kosyak and Zhang)
[
18
]
came across the quantity
on the left hand side in Theorem
A.5
as essentially being the number of failures up to
x
for a
method of construction they have for producing cyclotomic polynomials

\Phi_{n}(x)
having maximum coefficient
h
, with
h\leq x
.
If a slightly stronger variant of Andrica’s conjecture holds true, then they showed
that
every
natural number occurs as maximum coefficient of some cyclotomic polynomial.
For a more
leisurely account, see Moree
[
23
]
.

A.3.
Andrica’s conjecture for denser sequences

In our context a slightly weaker version of Andrica’s conjecture comes
up, namely:

Conjecture 3

(Andrica for prime powers)
.

Let
q_{1},q_{2},\ldots
be the consecutive prime powers.
The inequality

(7)

\sqrt{q_{n+1}}-\sqrt{q_{n}}<1

holds for all
n
.

Since the number of primes up to
x
grows asymptotically as
x/\log x

and the number of
prime powers
p^{m}
with
m\geq 2
as
\sqrt{x}
, this modified version
is, unfortunately, not that much weaker.
One could hope that if one replaces the sequence of prime numbers by a
substantially ‘fatter’ sequence, one might be
able to prove the analogue of Andrica’s conjecture. Finally, we present
an example showing that this hope is not unjustified.

A positive integer
n
is
said to be
practical
if every positive integer
\leq n

is a subset-sum of the positive divisors of

n
.
Weingartner
[
36
]

showed that there is a constant

c=1.33607\ldots
such that the number of
practical numbers
\leq x
grows as
cx/\log x
.
The analogue of Andrica’s conjecture for the practical numbers is known
to be true, see
Wu
[
37
]
.
