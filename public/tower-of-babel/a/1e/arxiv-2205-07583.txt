A least-squares Galerkin approach to gradient recovery for Hamilton-Jacobi-Bellman equation with Cordes coefficients
Omar Lakkis, Amireh Mousavi

Abstract
. We propose a conforming finite element method to approximate the strong solution of the second-order Hamilton-Jacobi-Bellman equation with Dirichlet boundary conditions and coefficients that satisfy the Cordes condition. We show the convergence of the continuum semismooth Newton method for the fully nonlinear Hamilton-Jacobi-Bellman equation. Using this linearization approach for the equation yields a recursive sequence of linear elliptic boundary value problems (BVPs) in nondivergence form. We numerically solve these BVPs using the least-squares gradient recovery method proposed by Lakkis and Mousavi 2021 . We offer an optimal-rate a priori and a posteriori error bounds for the approximation. The a posteriori error estimators are used to drive an adaptive refinement procedure. We close with computer experiments on both uniform and adaptive meshes to reconcile the theoretical findings.



Keywords:

Hamilton–Jacobi–Bellman equations, Cordes coefficients, semismooth
Newton linearization, least-squares approach, gradient recovery,
optimal a priori error bound, a posteriori error bound, adaptive
refinement

1.
Introduction

We develop a Galerkin least-squares numerical method to approximate a function
u:\varOmega\to\mathbb{R}
that satisfies the following elliptic Dirichlet boundary value problem (BVP) associated to the Hamilton--Jacobi--Bellman (HJB) partial differential equation (PDE)

(1.1)

\sup_{\alpha\in\mathcal{A}}\left({\mathcal{{L}}^{\alpha}u-f^{\alpha}}\right)=0\text{ in }\varOmega\text{ and }\left.u\right|_{\partial\varOmega}=r.

Here and throughout
\varOmega
is a bounded convex domain in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
,

d\in\mathbb{N}
(typically
d=2,3
),
\mathcal{A}
is a compact metric
space called the
(admissible) control set
,

r\in\operatorname{H}^{3/2}(\partial\varOmega)
. For each admissible

control

\alpha
in
\mathcal{A}
, the corresponding forcing term

f^{\alpha}
is a member of
\operatorname{L}_{2}(\varOmega)
and the elliptic operator
\mathcal{{L}}^{\alpha}
is
defined by a triple of functions
\left({\boldsymbol{{A}}^{\alpha},\boldsymbol{b}^{\alpha},c^{\alpha}}\right):\varOmega\to\mathbb{R}^{d\times d}\times\mathbb{R}^{d}\times\mathbb{R}
and

(1.2)

\mathcal{{L}}^{\alpha}v:=\boldsymbol{{A}}^{\alpha}\!:\!\operatorname{D}\!^{2}v+\boldsymbol{b}^{\alpha}\cdot\nabla v-c^{\alpha}v\text{ for each }v\in\operatorname{H}^{2}(\varOmega).

Here,
\nabla\phi
and
\operatorname{D}\!^{2}\phi
respectively indicate the gradient
and Hessian of a function
\phi
, and
\boldsymbol{{M}}\!:\!\boldsymbol{{N}}:=\operatorname{tra}\left({{\boldsymbol{{M}}}^{\intercal}\boldsymbol{{N}}}\right)
defines the Frobenius
product for two equally sized matrices
\boldsymbol{{M}},\boldsymbol{{N}}\in\mathbb{R}^{m\times n}
,
m,n\in\mathbb{N}
. Additionally, if
m=n
,
\operatorname{tra}\boldsymbol{{M}}

denotes
\boldsymbol{{M}}
’s trace, i.e., the sum of its (possibly repeated)
eigenvalues.

Excepting special situations, e.g., when the control set
\mathcal{A}

is a singleton, equation (
1.1
) is not linear
in
u
and both its mathematical and computational analysis must be
approached as a fully nonlinear elliptic equation.

The HJB equation (
1.2
) was introduced in the context of

dynamic programming
developed by

Bellman 1957
;
Bellman 2010
for optimal control
models. The second order equation considered here appears in optimal
control problems with stochastic processes; we refer to

Fleming and Soner 2006a
and the references therein as a source of
information on the modeling and analysis.

Fully nonlinear PDEs, including the HJB equations, of which a special
case is a reformulation of the Monge–Ampère equation

Lions 1984
;
Krylov 2001
, play an essential role in many fields of
natural and social sciences as well as technology. The practical
relevance motivates further the search for practical numerical
methods. The nonvariational
nature of fully nonlinear PDEs forms a challenge to their numerical
approximation via standard Galerkin methods, making finite differences
a natural first resort; but the flexibility
that Galerkin methods offer in terms of geometry approximation
and powerful adaptive mesh refinement techniques makes seeking
such methods worthwhile.

One of the main difficulties in seeking solutions to general fully
nonlinear PDEs, is the lack of classical solutions, which necessitates
often the investigation of solutions in a weak or generalized sense.
Since the structure of such PDEs precludes a natural variational
formulation the direct application of a weak solution in
\operatorname{H}^{1}(\varOmega)

is not practical. The natural approach to weak solutions, instead,
traced by
Crandall and Lions 1983
for first order equations and
extended to second order equations by
Lions 1983a
;
Lions 1983b
, relies on
the concept of
viscosity solution
, rooted in the theory of
vanishing viscosity methods in fluid dynamics intiated by

Hopf 1950
. Independent developments to weak
solutions for fully nonlinear eqautions and HJB can be traced also to

Aleksandrov 1961
who
underscored the importance of the
maximum principle
for
viscosity solutions and subsequent work connecting to stochastic
control by

Krylov 1972
;
Krylov 1979
.
This avenue has since led to a flurry in the theory of fully nonlinear
problems, often in connection with the theory of stochastic control

(
Caffarelli and Cabré 1995
;
Fleming and
Soner 2006b
;
Krylov 2009
;
Krylov 2018
)
.

From a numerical perspective, a milestone was reached with the seminal
result of
Barles and Souganidis 1991
, demonstrating that consistency,
stability, and monotonicity of an approximating scheme guarantee the
convergence of the approximate solution to the exact viscosity
solution. The importance of the Barles–Souganidis theorem
is vindicated by the extensive literature built upon it

(
Barles and Jakobsen 2005
;
Oberman 2006
;
Debrabant and Jakobsen 2013
;
Feng and Jensen 2017
)
.
The finite difference, including semi-Lagrangian or carefully designed
wide-stencil methods are well suited in approximating a viscosity
solution, as they preserve the maximum principle and consistency

(
Motzkin and Wasow 1953
;
Kuo and Trudinger 1992
;
Bonnans and Zidani 2003
;
Froese and Oberman 2011
)
.
Moreover, certain Galerkin methods, such as
\mathbb{P}^{1}
on meshes that
satisfy the maximum mrinciple, have been shown to converge to the
viscosity solution, as demonstrated by
(
Jensen and Smears 2013
;
Nochetto and Zhang 2018
;
Salgado and Zhang 2019
)
.

While besides stability and consistency, monotonicity-based
discretization methods are guaranteed to converge, providing this
property in discretizations is not always immediately obvious and may
preserve only in specific cases.

To ease the strict requirement of discretization monotonicity,

Smears and Süli 2014
explored the HJB equation under the Cordes condition
on the coefficients of the ellipic operators
\mathcal{{L}}^{\alpha}

(
Cordes 1959
)
. They introduced and analyzed
a finite element approximation that converges without the need to
maintain monotonicity.
In this approach, the underlying PDE is reformulated into a

second-order binonlinear form
, i.e., a form in two
variables which is nonlinear in at least one of its arguments, which
they then discretize employing a discontinuous Galerkin finite element
scheme.

The provided convergence analysis relies on the strong monotonicity of
the binonlinear form, considered as a generalization of coercivity for
nonlinear operators, but as a functional rather than in
discretization.

In
Gallistl and Süli 2019
, a similar convergence argument is employed, but
with a distinction: the HJB equation is treated as a variational
binonlinear form problem, utilizing a conforming finite element
method. The fundamental analytical tool in this work is also the
strong monotonicity of the binonlinear form.

There are two strategies to deal with nonlinear PDEs such as the HJB
equation. In the first strategy, the nonlinear problem is discretized
and the resulting nonlinear finite-dimensional system is linearized
with a nonlinear solver such as Newton’s method. Many computational
methods for approximating the solution of the HJB equation follow this
approach
(
Oberman 2006
;
Smears and Süli 2014
;
Gallistl and Süli 2019
)
.
In this scenario, if either the discretization is monotone or, in the
functional setting, the binonlinear form is strongly monotone, the
error analysis of the method becomes possible. Furthermore, strong
monotonicity establishes the framework for applying the Browder-Minty
theorem to demonstrate the well-posedness of solutions to nonlinear
equations.
It is however not always possible to achieve a monotone discretization
or a strongly monotone binonlinear form for a fully nonlinear
problem. To the best of our knowledge, all methods that provide an
approximate solution and offer a satisfactory convergence analysis for
the HJB equation follow this strategy by somehow enforcing
monotonicity.

In a second strategy, which we pursue here, the nonlinear PDE is first
linearized, for example, by using Newton’s method in the appropriate
infinite-dimensional space, into an iterative sequence of linear PDEs
in nondivergence form at the continuum level. Subsequently, these
linear PDEs are discretized. By following this strategy, the
convergence rate of the nonlinear solver is independent of the
discrete space parameters, such as the meshsize or polynomial degree
in Galerkin methods. Furthermore any numerical method applicable to
linear problems in nondivergence form can be extended to fully
nonlinear problems, such as HJB equation we study herein. An instance
of the second strategy is

Lakkis and Pryer 2011
;
Lakkis and Pryer 2013
, who derived a
computational method for linear elliptic problems in nondivergence
form used to solve the linearized iteration for a class of fully
nonlinear problems in which the nonlinearity is
algebraic
, i.e., a nonlinearity that can be written
without resorting to
\sup_{\alpha}
operations with infinitely many

\alpha
s (e.g., the Monge–Ampère).

In this paper, we adopt the strategy of
first-linearize-then-discretize. In the linearization step we
establish the Newton differentiability of the HJB operator from

\operatorname{H}^{2}(\varOmega)
to
\operatorname{L}_{2}(\varOmega)
, subject to a uniform convergence of controls
Assumption
3.6
.

While
Smears and Süli 2014
have demonstrated this concept for the operator
from
{\smash{\operatorname{W}}}^{2}_{s}(\varOmega;\mathcal{{T}})
to
\operatorname{L}_{l}(\varOmega)
, where
1\leqslant l<s\leqslant\infty
, these spaces are mesh-dependent and encompass only finite
element spaces (not
\operatorname{H}^{2}(\varOmega)\rightarrow\operatorname{L}_{2}(\varOmega)
).

The Newton differentiability of the HJB operator allows us to extend
the classical Newton linearization to the HJB operator, even when it
is not necessarily Fréchet differentiable. This extended method is
referred to as the
semismooth Newton
linearization

(
Hintermüller 2010
;
Ito and Kunisch 2008
)
. With this
approach, the solution of the nonlinear HJB equation is realized as
the limit of a recursive sequence of solutions to linear problems in
nondivergence form. We complete the linearization theory by
demonstrating the superlinear convergence rate of this recursive
method.

We also introduce the algorithmic form of the resulting recursive
method, known as
Howard’s algorithm
or
policy iteration
,
which, to the best of our knowledge, is presented here for the first
time in the infinite dimensional setting for HJB PDEs.

At each iteration, this algorithm updates a space-dependent function,
denoted as
q
and taking values in the control set
\mathcal{A}
. This
function
q
determines the linear operator
\mathcal{{L}}^{q_{n}}
as seen
in (
3.24
). Consequently, Howard’s
algorithm yields a sequence of control-seeking parts and nondivergence
form PDE solvers, essentially constituting a parametrized
linearization procedure.

In the context of connecting fully nonlinear and linear problems, the
bridge is formed by nondivergence form linear operators. To discretize
PDEs in this scenario, we employ a least-squares Galerkin gradient
recovery method. This approach offers a straightforward means of
dealing with linear equations in nondivergence form. It can be
interpreted as a mixed finite element technique and provides a
convenient framework for deriving a posteriori error estimates and
corresponding adaptive methods, as demonstrated in

Lakkis and Mousavi 2021
.

The least-squares approach enables us to replace any constraint required
to ensure the problem is well-posed with an additional term in the quadratic
(least-squares) form. This flexibility to work in a general space is a
significant advantage, as constructing finite element approximations
that exactly satisfy conditions like rotational-free or vanishing
tangential trace can be challenging.

The rest of our article is arranged as follows: in §
2
,
we clarify the problem assumptions and the existence theory concerning
to the well-posedness of the strong solution for the HJB equation.

In §
3
, we introduce the concept of
Newton differentiability for operators and illustrate its application
to the HJB operator in the continuous setting, particularly from

\operatorname{H}^{2}(\varOmega)
to
\operatorname{L}_{2}(\varOmega)
. This is done under the assumption of
uniform convergence on the policy operators, as outlined in
Assumption
3.6
. We also
discuss the use of the semismooth Newton method for linearizing the
HJB equation. Subsequently, we present the algorithmic expression of
the linearization procedure in the form of Howard’s algorithm, which
involves solving a second-order elliptic equation in nondivergence
form. In §
4
, we provide a review of the
least-squares Galerkin approach, which includes gradient recovery as
proposed in
Lakkis and Mousavi 2021
. This
method is employed to solve linear elliptic PDEs in nondivergence
form, and we also revisit the key error bounds associated with it. In
§
5
, we return to the nonlinear HJB equation
and discuss the error analysis of the approximation, which includes
both the associated a priori and a posteriori error estimates. Owing
to the a posteriori error bound, we design error indicators to be
employed in an adaptive refinement strategy. Additionally, we outline
the algorithms used during the implementation phase to approximate the
solution of the HJB equation. In §
6
we
present two numerical tests that validate the theoretical results.

Semismooth Newton method

3.1.

3.2.
Lemma (state-to-policy operator is well defined and continuous)

For any

\mathcal{N}[v]
is nonempty.
Moreover, if

\lim_{j\rightarrow\infty}\underset{\begin{subarray}{c}q\in\mathcal{N}[v]\end{subarray}}{\inf}d_{\mathcal{A}}(q_{j},q)=0\text{ a.e. in }\varOmega,

with reminder that

Proof.
To prove that
\mathcal{N}[v]
is nonempty, we refer to Theorem 10 of

For almost every

, we prove (

Suppose that there exist a sequence
\left({v_{j}}\right)_{{{j}\in{\mathbb{N}}}}
convergent to
v

\operatorname{H}^{2}(\varOmega)\cap\operatorname{H}^{1}_{0}(\varOmega)
, a subsequence

\left({q_{j}}\right)_{{{j}\in{\mathbb{N}}}}
,

and a real
\bar{\varepsilon}>0
such that

(3.4)

d_{\mathcal{A}}(q_{j}(\boldsymbol{x}),q(\boldsymbol{x}))>\bar{\varepsilon}\quad\text{for all }j\in\mathbb{N}\text{ and for all }q\in\mathcal{N}[v].

It means that

q_{j}(\boldsymbol{x})\in\mathcal{A}\smallsetminus\underset{\begin{subarray}{c}q\in\mathcal{N}[v]\end{subarray}}{\bigcup}\operatorname{B}_{{\bar{\varepsilon}}}^{\mathcal{A}}\!\left({{q(\boldsymbol{x})}}\right)

where

(3.5)

\operatorname{B}_{{\bar{\varepsilon}}}^{\mathcal{A}}\!\left({{q(\boldsymbol{x})}}\right):=\left\{\alpha\in\mathcal{A}\left|\penalty\ d_{\mathcal{A}}(\alpha,q(\boldsymbol{x}))<\bar{\varepsilon}\right\}\right..

Since

\mathcal{A}\smallsetminus\underset{\begin{subarray}{c}q\in\mathcal{N}[v]\end{subarray}}{\bigcup}\operatorname{B}_{{\bar{\varepsilon}}}^{\mathcal{A}}\!\left({{q(\boldsymbol{x})}}\right)

is compact, there exists a subsequence, which we pass without changing notation,
such that

q_{j}(\boldsymbol{x})\rightarrow\tilde{\alpha}\in\mathcal{A}\smallsetminus\underset{\begin{subarray}{c}q\in\mathcal{N}[v]\end{subarray}}{\bigcup}\operatorname{B}_{{\bar{\varepsilon}}}^{\mathcal{A}}\!\left({{q(\boldsymbol{x})}}\right)

On the other hand,

4.
A least-squares Galerkin gradient recovery for linear

We review here the

This enables us to derive a

in Theorem

4.1.
Least-squares Galerkin for linear nondivergence form PDE

Consider the second order elliptic linear equation in nondivergence form

(4.1)

But dealing with such sizeable regular function space (

Because of this, we propose to consider an alternative equivalent problem in which its solution resides in a weaker space. To facilitate this, we introduce some notations.

We denote the outer normal to

We inset the function space

(4.3)

(4.4)

\left\|{(\varphi,\boldsymbol{\psi})}\right\|_{\operatorname{H}^{1}(\varOmega)}^{2}:=\left\|{\varphi}\right\|_{\operatorname{H}^{1}(\varOmega)}^{2}+\left\|{\boldsymbol{\psi}}\right\|_{\operatorname{H}^{1}(\varOmega)}^{2}\text{ for each }(\varphi,\boldsymbol{\psi})\in\operatorname{H}^{1}(\varOmega)\times\operatorname{H}^{1}\left({\varOmega;\mathbb{R}^{d}}\right).

\star
stands for one of the arithmetic, Euclidean-scalar, or Frobenius inner product in
\mathbb{R}
,
\mathbb{R}^{d}
, or
\mathbb{R}^{d\times d}
respectively. The notations
a\wedge b
,
a\vee b
respectively indicate the minimum and maximum of two values
a,b
.

By considering

0\leqslant\theta\leqslant 1
, we define the linear operator

(4.6)

\begin{gathered}\mathcal{{M}}_{\theta}:\operatorname{H}^{1}(\varOmega)\times\operatorname{H}^{1}\left({\varOmega;\mathbb{R}^{d}}\right)\rightarrow\operatorname{L}_{2}(\varOmega)\\
(\varphi,\boldsymbol{\psi})\mapsto\boldsymbol{{A}}\!:\!\operatorname{D}\!\boldsymbol{\psi}+\boldsymbol{b}\cdot(\theta\boldsymbol{\psi}+(1-\theta)\nabla\varphi)-c\varphi=:\mathcal{{M}}_{\theta}(\varphi,\boldsymbol{\psi}).\end{gathered}

Initially, we assume that the boundary condition is zero, i.e.,
\left.u\right|_{\partial\varOmega}=0
.
In this regard, we introduce the following quadratic functional on
\operatorname{H}^{1}_{0}(\varOmega)\times\operatorname{H}^{1}(\varOmega;\mathbb{R}^{d})

(4.7)

E_{\theta}(\varphi,\boldsymbol{\psi}):=\left\|{\nabla\varphi-\boldsymbol{\psi}}\right\|_{\operatorname{L}_{2}(\varOmega)}^{2}+\left\|{\nabla\!\times\!\boldsymbol{\psi}}\right\|_{\operatorname{L}_{2}(\varOmega)}^{2}+\left\|{\operatorname{\textbf{t}}_{\varOmega}\boldsymbol{\psi}}\right\|_{\operatorname{L}_{2}(\partial\varOmega)}^{2}+\left\|{\mathcal{{M}}_{\theta}(\varphi,\boldsymbol{\psi})-f}\right\|_{\operatorname{L}_{2}(\varOmega)}^{2}

where
\nabla\!\times\!\boldsymbol{\psi}
indicates curl of
\boldsymbol{\psi}
. We then deal with the convex minimization problem of finding a unique pair of the form

(4.8)

(u,\boldsymbol{g})=\underset{\begin{subarray}{c}(\varphi,\boldsymbol{\psi})\in\operatorname{H}^{1}_{0}(\varOmega)\times\operatorname{H}^{1}(\varOmega;\mathbb{R}^{d})\end{subarray}}{\operatorname{argmin}\nolimits}E_{\theta}(\varphi,\boldsymbol{\psi}).

The key point is that the problem of finding the strong solution
u
to (
4.1
) with
\left.u\right|_{\partial\varOmega}=0
and solving the minimization problem (
4.8
) are equivalent and
\boldsymbol{g}=\nabla u
holds as a consequence of (
4.8
). In the rest of the paper,
\boldsymbol{g}
will be synonymous with
\nabla u
.
The Euler–Lagrange equation of the minimization problem (
4.8
) includes in finding
(u,\boldsymbol{g})\in\operatorname{H}^{1}_{0}(\varOmega)\times\operatorname{H}^{1}(\varOmega;\mathbb{R}^{d})
such that

(4.9)

\left\langle{\nabla u-\boldsymbol{g},\nabla\varphi-\boldsymbol{\psi}}\right\rangle+\left\langle{\nabla\!\times\!\boldsymbol{g},\nabla\!\times\!\boldsymbol{\psi}}\right\rangle+\left\langle{\operatorname{\textbf{t}}_{\varOmega}\boldsymbol{g},\operatorname{\textbf{t}}_{\varOmega}\boldsymbol{\psi}}\right\rangle_{\partial\varOmega}\\
+\left\langle{\mathcal{{M}}_{\theta}(u,\boldsymbol{g}),\mathcal{{M}}_{\theta}(\varphi,\boldsymbol{\psi})}\right\rangle=\left\langle{f,\mathcal{{M}}_{\theta}(\varphi,\boldsymbol{\psi})}\right\rangle\text{ for each }(\varphi,\boldsymbol{\psi})\in\operatorname{H}^{1}_{0}(\varOmega)\times\operatorname{H}^{1}(\varOmega;\mathbb{R}^{d}).

Consistent with (
4.9
), we define the symmetric
bilinear form

a_{\theta}:{\left(\operatorname{H}^{1}_{0}(\varOmega)\times\operatorname{H}^{1}(\varOmega;\mathbb{R}^{d})\right)}^{2}\to\mathbb{R}

by

(4.10)

a_{\theta}(\varphi,\boldsymbol{\psi};\varphi^{\prime},\boldsymbol{\psi}^{\prime}):=\left\langle{\nabla\varphi-\boldsymbol{\psi},\nabla\varphi^{\prime}-\boldsymbol{\psi}^{\prime}}\right\rangle+\left\langle{\nabla\!\times\!\boldsymbol{\psi},\nabla\!\times\!\boldsymbol{\psi}^{\prime}}\right\rangle\\
+\left\langle{\operatorname{\textbf{t}}_{\varOmega}\boldsymbol{\psi},\operatorname{\textbf{t}}_{\varOmega}\boldsymbol{\psi}^{\prime}}\right\rangle_{\partial\varOmega}+\left\langle{\mathcal{{M}}_{\theta}(\varphi,\boldsymbol{\psi}),\mathcal{{M}}_{\theta}(\varphi^{\prime},\boldsymbol{\psi}^{\prime})}\right\rangle.

To establish the well-posedness of (
4.9
) using the Lax–Milgram theorem, it suffices to demonstrate the coercivity and continuity of the bilinear form
a_{\theta}
. We address this matter through the following results.

4.2.
Lemma (Maxwell’s inequality)

For a convex
\varOmega
, every function
\boldsymbol{\psi}\in\operatorname{H}^{1}(\varOmega;\mathbb{R}^{d})
satisfies

(4.11)

\left\|{\operatorname{D}\!\boldsymbol{\psi}}\right\|_{\operatorname{L}_{2}(\varOmega)}^{2}\leqslant\left\|{\nabla{}\kern-1.0pt\cdot\boldsymbol{\psi}}\right\|_{\operatorname{L}_{2}(\varOmega)}^{2}+\left\|{\nabla\!\times\!\boldsymbol{\psi}}\right\|_{\operatorname{L}_{2}(\varOmega)}^{2}\\
+2\left\langle{\operatorname{\textbf{t}}_{\varOmega}\boldsymbol{\psi},\operatorname{D}\!\left[{\boldsymbol{\psi}\cdot\operatorname{\mathbf{n}}_{\varOmega}}\right]-\partial_{\operatorname{\mathbf{n}}_{\varOmega}}(\boldsymbol{\psi}\cdot\operatorname{\mathbf{n}}_{\varOmega})\operatorname{\mathbf{n}}_{\varOmega}}\right\rangle_{\partial\varOmega}.

See
Costabel and Dauge 1999
for more details.
The bound (
4.11
) for
\boldsymbol{\psi}\in\mathscr{{W}}
known as
Maxwell’s inequality, which is

(4.12)

\left\|{\operatorname{D}\!\boldsymbol{\psi}}\right\|_{\operatorname{L}_{2}(\varOmega)}^{2}\leqslant\left\|{\nabla{}\kern-1.0pt\cdot\boldsymbol{\psi}}\right\|_{\operatorname{L}_{2}(\varOmega)}^{2}+\left\|{\nabla\!\times\!\boldsymbol{\psi}}\right\|_{\operatorname{L}_{2}(\varOmega)}^{2}.

4.3.
Lemma (generalized Maxwell’s inequality)

Let
\varOmega
be a convex domain. There exists
C_{\ref{ineq:general-maxwell}}>0

such that for any
\boldsymbol{\psi}\in\operatorname{H}^{1}(\varOmega;\mathbb{R}^{d})
,

(4.13)

\left\|{\operatorname{D}\!\boldsymbol{\psi}}\right\|_{\operatorname{L}_{2}(\varOmega)}^{2}\leqslant C_{\ref{ineq:general-maxwell}}\left({\left\|{\nabla{}\kern-1.0pt\cdot\boldsymbol{\psi}}\right\|_{\operatorname{L}_{2}(\varOmega)}^{2}+\left\|{\nabla\!\times\!\boldsymbol{\psi}}\right\|_{\operatorname{L}_{2}(\varOmega)}^{2}+\left\|{\operatorname{\textbf{t}}_{\varOmega}\boldsymbol{\psi}}\right\|_{\operatorname{L}_{2}(\partial\varOmega)}^{2}}\right).

Proof.
We argue by contradiction. Suppose inequality (
4.13
) fails,
then for any
n\in\mathbb{N}
there is
\boldsymbol{\psi}_{n}\in\operatorname{H}^{1}(\varOmega;\mathbb{R}^{d})

such that

(4.14)

\left\|{\nabla{}\kern-1.0pt\cdot\boldsymbol{\psi}_{n}}\right\|_{\operatorname{L}_{2}(\varOmega)}^{2}+\left\|{\nabla\!\times\!\boldsymbol{\psi}_{n}}\right\|_{\operatorname{L}_{2}(\varOmega)}^{2}+\left\|{\operatorname{\textbf{t}}_{\varOmega}\boldsymbol{\psi}_{n}}\right\|_{\operatorname{L}_{2}(\partial\varOmega)}^{2}<\dfrac{1}{n}\left\|{\operatorname{D}\!\boldsymbol{\psi}_{n}}\right\|_{\operatorname{L}_{2}(\varOmega)}^{2}.

Without loss of generality, assume
\left\|{\operatorname{D}\!\boldsymbol{\psi}_{n}}\right\|_{\operatorname{L}_{2}(\varOmega)}=1

for all
n\in\mathbb{N}
.
Then (
4.14
) implies that

\left\|{\nabla{}\kern-1.0pt\cdot\boldsymbol{\psi}_{n}}\right\|_{\operatorname{L}_{2}(\varOmega)}
,

\left\|{\nabla\!\times\!\boldsymbol{\psi}_{n}}\right\|_{\operatorname{L}_{2}(\varOmega)}
and

\left\|{\operatorname{\textbf{t}}_{\varOmega}\boldsymbol{\psi}_{n}}\right\|_{\operatorname{L}_{2}(\partial\varOmega)}

all converge to zero as
n\to\infty
.
On the other hand, from (
4.11
) and by applying
Cauchy-Schwarz inequality, for all
n\in\mathbb{N}
we have

(4.15)

\left\|{\operatorname{D}\!\boldsymbol{\psi}_{n}}\right\|_{\operatorname{L}_{2}(\varOmega)}^{2}\leqslant\left\|{\nabla{}\kern-1.0pt\cdot\boldsymbol{\psi}_{n}}\right\|_{\operatorname{L}_{2}(\varOmega)}^{2}+\left\|{\nabla\!\times\!\boldsymbol{\psi}_{n}}\right\|_{\operatorname{L}_{2}(\varOmega)}^{2}\\
+2\left\langle{\operatorname{\textbf{t}}_{\varOmega}\boldsymbol{\psi}_{n},\operatorname{D}\!(\boldsymbol{\psi}_{n}\cdot\operatorname{\mathbf{n}}_{\varOmega})-\partial_{\operatorname{\mathbf{n}}_{\varOmega}}(\boldsymbol{\psi}_{n}\cdot\operatorname{\mathbf{n}}_{\varOmega})\operatorname{\mathbf{n}}_{\varOmega}}\right\rangle_{\partial\varOmega}\leqslant\left\|{\nabla{}\kern-1.0pt\cdot\boldsymbol{\psi}_{n}}\right\|_{\operatorname{L}_{2}(\varOmega)}^{2}\\
+\left\|{\nabla\!\times\!\boldsymbol{\psi}_{n}}\right\|_{\operatorname{L}_{2}(\varOmega)}^{2}+2\left\|{\operatorname{\textbf{t}}_{\varOmega}\boldsymbol{\psi}_{n}}\right\|_{\operatorname{L}_{2}(\partial\varOmega)}\left\|{\operatorname{D}\!\left[{\boldsymbol{\psi}_{n}\cdot\operatorname{\mathbf{n}}_{\varOmega}}\right]-\partial_{\operatorname{\mathbf{n}}_{\varOmega}}(\boldsymbol{\psi}_{n}\cdot\operatorname{\mathbf{n}}_{\varOmega})\operatorname{\mathbf{n}}_{\varOmega}}\right\|_{\operatorname{L}_{2}(\partial\varOmega)}.

By taking the limit of the both sides of the above inequality, when
n\rightarrow\infty
, we get

(4.16)

1=\lim_{n\rightarrow\infty}\left\|{\operatorname{D}\!\boldsymbol{\psi}_{n}}\right\|_{\operatorname{L}_{2}(\varOmega)}^{2}\leqslant\lim_{n\rightarrow\infty}\Big(\left\|{\nabla{}\kern-1.0pt\cdot\boldsymbol{\psi}_{n}}\right\|_{\operatorname{L}_{2}(\varOmega)}^{2}+\left\|{\nabla\!\times\!\boldsymbol{\psi}_{n}}\right\|_{\operatorname{L}_{2}(\varOmega)}^{2}\\
+2\left\|{\operatorname{\textbf{t}}_{\varOmega}\boldsymbol{\psi}_{n}}\right\|_{\operatorname{L}_{2}(\partial\varOmega)}\left\|{\operatorname{D}\!(\boldsymbol{\psi}_{n}\cdot\operatorname{\mathbf{n}}_{\varOmega})-\partial_{\operatorname{\mathbf{n}}_{\varOmega}}\left[{\boldsymbol{\psi}_{n}\cdot\operatorname{\mathbf{n}}_{\varOmega}}\right]\operatorname{\mathbf{n}}_{\varOmega}}\right\|_{\operatorname{L}_{2}(\partial\varOmega)}\Big)=0,

which it is a contradiction.
∎

4.8.
An equivalent problem to the equation with nonzero boundary

If the boundary condition of (
4.1
) is nonhomogeneous,
i.e.,
\left.u\right|_{\partial\varOmega}=r\neq 0
for some
r\in\operatorname{H}^{3/2}(\partial\varOmega)
,
the functional
E_{\theta}
is replaced by the extended functional
\tilde{E_{\theta}}
as

(4.21)

\begin{gathered}\tilde{E_{\theta}}:\operatorname{H}^{1}(\varOmega)\times\operatorname{H}^{1}(\varOmega;\mathbb{R}^{d})\to\mathbb{R}\\
\begin{aligned} \tilde{E_{\theta}}(\varphi,\boldsymbol{\psi}):=\left\|{\nabla\varphi-\boldsymbol{\psi}}\right\|_{\operatorname{L}_{2}(\varOmega)}^{2}&+\left\|{\nabla\!\times\!\boldsymbol{\psi}}\right\|_{\operatorname{L}_{2}(\varOmega)}^{2}+\left\|{\mathcal{{M}}_{\theta}(\varphi,\boldsymbol{\psi})-f}\right\|_{\operatorname{L}_{2}(\varOmega)}^{2}\\
+\left\|{\varphi-r}\right\|_{\operatorname{L}_{2}(\partial\varOmega)}^{2}&+\left\|{\operatorname{\textbf{t}}_{\varOmega}(\boldsymbol{\psi}-\nabla r)}\right\|_{\operatorname{L}_{2}(\partial\varOmega)}^{2}\end{aligned}\end{gathered}

and we then consider the Euler–Lagrange equation of the minimization problem

(4.22)

(u,\boldsymbol{g})=\underset{\begin{subarray}{c}(\varphi,\boldsymbol{\psi})\in\operatorname{H}^{1}(\varOmega)\times\operatorname{H}^{1}(\varOmega;\mathbb{R}^{d})\end{subarray}}{\operatorname{argmin}\nolimits}\tilde{E_{\theta}}(\varphi,\boldsymbol{\psi}).

Indeed, we find
(u,\boldsymbol{g})\in\operatorname{H}^{1}(\varOmega)\times\operatorname{H}^{1}(\varOmega;\mathbb{R}^{d})
such that,

(4.23)

\left\langle{\nabla u-\boldsymbol{g},\nabla\varphi-\boldsymbol{\psi}}\right\rangle+\left\langle{\nabla\!\times\!\boldsymbol{g},\nabla\!\times\!\boldsymbol{\psi}}\right\rangle+\left\langle{\mathcal{{M}}_{\theta}(u,\boldsymbol{g}),\mathcal{{M}}_{\theta}(\varphi,\boldsymbol{\psi})}\right\rangle+\left\langle{u,\varphi}\right\rangle_{\partial\varOmega}\\
+\left\langle{\operatorname{\textbf{t}}_{\varOmega}\boldsymbol{g},\operatorname{\textbf{t}}_{\varOmega}\boldsymbol{\psi}}\right\rangle_{\partial\varOmega}=\left\langle{f,\mathcal{{M}}_{\theta}(\varphi,\boldsymbol{\psi})}\right\rangle+\left\langle{r,\varphi}\right\rangle_{\partial\varOmega}+\left\langle{\operatorname{\textbf{t}}_{\varOmega}\nabla r,\operatorname{\textbf{t}}_{\varOmega}\boldsymbol{\psi}}\right\rangle_{\partial\varOmega}\\
\text{ for each }(\varphi,\boldsymbol{\psi})\in\operatorname{H}^{1}(\varOmega)\times\operatorname{H}^{1}(\varOmega;\mathbb{R}^{d}).

The well-posedness of the resulting problem can be demonstrated using a similar argument to the proof of Theorem
4.7
. Accordingly, we define the bilinear form

(4.24)

\begin{gathered}\tilde{a}_{\theta}:{\operatorname{H}^{1}(\varOmega)\times\operatorname{H}^{1}(\varOmega;\mathbb{R}^{d})}^{2}\to\mathbb{R}\\
\tilde{a}_{\theta}(\varphi,\boldsymbol{\psi};\varphi^{\prime},\boldsymbol{\psi}^{\prime}):=a_{\theta}(\varphi,\boldsymbol{\psi};\varphi^{\prime},\boldsymbol{\psi}^{\prime})+\left\langle{\varphi,\varphi^{\prime}}\right\rangle_{\partial\varOmega}.\end{gathered}

4.9.
Theorem (coercivity and continuity of
\tilde{a}_{\theta}
)

Under the assumption of Lemma
4.7
, there exist

C_{\textup{\ref{ineq:coercivity-inhomogen}{{}}}},C_{\textup{\ref{ineq:continuity-inhomogen}{{}}}}>0

such that for any

(\varphi,\boldsymbol{\psi}),(\varphi^{\prime},\boldsymbol{\psi}^{\prime})\in\operatorname{H}^{1}(\varOmega)\times\operatorname{H}^{1}(\varOmega;\mathbb{R}^{d})

(4.25)

\tilde{a}_{\theta}(\varphi,\boldsymbol{\psi};\varphi,\boldsymbol{\psi})\geqslant C_{\textup{\ref{ineq:coercivity-inhomogen}{{}}}}\left\|{(\varphi,\boldsymbol{\psi})}\right\|_{\operatorname{H}^{1}(\varOmega)}^{2}

(4.26)

\left|\tilde{a}_{\theta}(\varphi,\boldsymbol{\psi};\varphi^{\prime},\boldsymbol{\psi}^{\prime})\right|\leqslant C_{\textup{\ref{ineq:continuity-inhomogen}{{}}}}\left\|{(\varphi,\boldsymbol{\psi})}\right\|_{\operatorname{H}^{1}(\varOmega)}\left\|{(\varphi^{\prime},\boldsymbol{\psi}^{\prime})}\right\|_{\operatorname{H}^{1}(\varOmega)}

Proof.
Using Poincaré’s inequality: there exists
C_{\ref{ineq:Poincare}}>0
such that for any
(\varphi,\boldsymbol{\psi})\in\operatorname{H}^{1}(\varOmega)\times\operatorname{H}^{1}(\varOmega;\mathbb{R}^{d})
we have

(4.27)

\begin{gathered}\left\|{\nabla\varphi}\right\|_{\operatorname{L}_{2}(\varOmega)}^{2}+\left\|{\varphi}\right\|_{\operatorname{L}_{2}(\partial\varOmega)}^{2}\geqslant C_{\ref{ineq:Poincare}}\left\|{\varphi}\right\|_{\operatorname{H}^{1}(\varOmega)}^{2},\\
\left\|{\operatorname{D}\!\boldsymbol{\psi}}\right\|_{\operatorname{L}_{2}(\varOmega)}^{2}+\left\|{\operatorname{\textbf{t}}_{\varOmega}\boldsymbol{\psi}}\right\|_{\operatorname{L}_{2}(\partial\varOmega)}^{2}\geqslant C_{\ref{ineq:Poincare}}\left\|{\boldsymbol{\psi}}\right\|_{\operatorname{H}^{1}(\varOmega)}^{2},\end{gathered}

the argument is analogous to what is presented in the proof of Theorem
4.7
.
∎

4.10.
Galerkin finite element discretization

Consider
\mathfrak{T}
as a collection of shape-regular conforming
simplicial parititions (also known as triangulations) of

\varOmega
into simplices. Given
\mathcal{{T}}\in\mathfrak{T}
, for each

K\in\mathcal{{T}}
, let

and write

Having a curved boundary

prevents
\underset{\begin{subarray}{c}K\in\mathcal{{T}}\end{subarray}}{\bigcup}K
of coinciding with
\varOmega
. In this case, one can approximate sections of
\partial\varOmega

Consider the following Galerkin finite
element spaces

(4.28)

\displaystyle\mathbb{{U}}{}:=\mathbb{P}^{k}\left({\mathcal{{T}}}\right)\cap\operatorname{H}^{1}_{0}(\varOmega),\quad\tilde{\mathbb{{U}}{}}:=\mathbb{P}^{k}\left({\mathcal{{T}}}\right)\cap\operatorname{H}^{1}(\varOmega),\quad\mathbb{{G}}{}:=\mathbb{P}^{k}\left({\mathcal{{T}};\mathbb{R}^{d}}\right)\cap\operatorname{H}^{1}(\varOmega;\mathbb{R}^{d}).

By applying these Galerkin finite element spaces, the discrete problem corresponding to
zero boundary condition finds
(\mathsf{u}_{\mathbb{{U}}{}},\boldsymbol{\mathsf{g}}{}_{\mathbb{{G}}{}})\in\mathbb{{U}}{}\times\mathbb{{G}}{}

such that

(4.29)

a_{\theta}(\mathsf{u}_{\mathbb{{U}}{}},\boldsymbol{\mathsf{g}}{}_{\mathbb{{G}}{}};\varphi,\boldsymbol{\psi})=\left\langle{f,\mathcal{{M}}_{\theta}(\varphi,\boldsymbol{\psi})}\right\rangle\text{ for each }(\varphi,\boldsymbol{\psi})\in\mathbb{{U}}{}\times\mathbb{{G}}{}

and the discrete problem corresponding to
nonzero boundary condition finds

(\mathsf{u}_{\tilde{\mathbb{{U}}{}}},\boldsymbol{\mathsf{g}}{}_{\mathbb{{G}}{}})\in\tilde{\mathbb{{U}}{}}\times\mathbb{{G}}{}

such that

(4.30)

\tilde{a}_{\theta}(\mathsf{u}_{\tilde{\mathbb{{U}}{}}},\boldsymbol{\mathsf{g}}{}_{\mathbb{{G}}{}};\varphi,\boldsymbol{\psi})=\left\langle{f,\mathcal{{M}}_{\theta}(\varphi,\boldsymbol{\psi})}\right\rangle+\left\langle{r,\varphi}\right\rangle_{\partial\varOmega}+\left\langle{\operatorname{\textbf{t}}_{\varOmega}\nabla r,\operatorname{\textbf{t}}_{\varOmega}\boldsymbol{\psi}}\right\rangle_{\partial\varOmega}\text{ for each }(\varphi,\boldsymbol{\psi})\in\tilde{\mathbb{{U}}{}}\times\mathbb{{G}}{}.

and

\theta
is implicit in the notation

Mixed formulation of HJB problem

To introduce the mixed formulation corresponding to
HJB problem (
2.6

we define the mixed HJB operator as following

(5.4)

\begin{gathered}\hat{\mathcal{{F}}}:\operatorname{H}^{1}(\varOmega)\times\operatorname{H}^{1}(\varOmega;\mathbb{R}^{d})\to\operatorname{L}_{2}(\varOmega)\\
\begin{aligned} \hat{\mathcal{{F}}}(\varphi,\boldsymbol{\psi}):=\mathcal{{M}}_{\theta}^{q}(\varphi,\boldsymbol{\psi})-f^{q}\text{ such that }q\in\tilde{\mathcal{N}}(\varphi,\boldsymbol{\psi}),\end{aligned}\end{gathered}

where similar to (

\theta

Through adapting the proof of Theorem
3.9

to operator

(\varphi,\boldsymbol{\psi})\in\operatorname{H}^{1}(\varOmega)\times\operatorname{H}^{1}(\varOmega;\mathbb{R}^{d})

\operatorname{\mathfrak{D}}\!{\hat{\mathcal{{F}}}}[(\varphi,\boldsymbol{\psi})]:=\left\{\mathcal{{M}}_{\theta}^{q}:=\boldsymbol{{A}}^{q}\!:\!\operatorname{D}\!\penalty\ +\boldsymbol{b}^{q}\cdot(\theta+(1-\theta)\nabla\penalty\ )-c^{q}\left|\penalty\ q\in\tilde{\mathcal{N}}(\varphi,\boldsymbol{\psi})\right\}\right.

at

Based on the least-squares idea applied for the linearized problem, we introduce the following quadratic functional

\begin{gathered}\hat{E_{\theta}}:\operatorname{H}^{1}_{0}(\varOmega)\times\operatorname{H}^{1}(\varOmega;\mathbb{R}^{d})\to\mathbb{R}\\
\begin{aligned} \hat{E_{\theta}}(\varphi,\boldsymbol{\psi}):=\left\|{\nabla\varphi-\boldsymbol{\psi}}\right\|_{\operatorname{L}_{2}(\varOmega)}^{2}+\left\|{\nabla\!\times\!\boldsymbol{\psi}}\right\|_{\operatorname{L}_{2}(\varOmega)}^{2}+\left\|{\operatorname{\textbf{t}}_{\varOmega}(\boldsymbol{\psi})}\right\|_{\operatorname{L}_{2}(\partial\varOmega)}^{2}+\left\|{\mathcal{{M}}_{\theta}^{q}(\varphi,\boldsymbol{\psi})-f^{q}}\right\|_{\operatorname{L}_{2}(\varOmega)}^{2}\end{aligned}\end{gathered}

in which
q\in\tilde{\mathcal{N}}(\varphi,\boldsymbol{\psi})

(u,\boldsymbol{g})\in\operatorname{H}^{1}_{0}(\varOmega)\times\operatorname{H}^{1}(\varOmega;\mathbb{R}^{d})

such that

(5.7)

(u,\boldsymbol{g})=\underset{\begin{subarray}{c}(\varphi,\boldsymbol{\psi})\in\operatorname{H}^{1}_{0}(\varOmega)\times\operatorname{H}^{1}(\varOmega;\mathbb{R}^{d})\end{subarray}}{\operatorname{argmin}\nolimits}\hat{E_{\theta}}(\varphi,\boldsymbol{\psi}).

Obviously,

By applying Newton derivative (

)
finds

5.3.
Remark

When discretizing the HJB equation using the Galerkin finite element method, the steps of discretization and linearization are computationally commutative. This means that whether we apply the Galerkin method first to enter a finite-dimensional function space and then perform linearization, or if we linearize first and then use finite element discretization, the outcomes are equivalent.

7.
Conclusion

This study efficiently and practically approximated the strong
solution of the fully nonlinear HJB equation in two steps.
First, we linearized the nonlinear HJB equation using the semismooth
Newton method. We demonstrated the Newton differentiability of the HJB
operator from
\operatorname{H}^{2}(\varOmega)
to
\operatorname{L}_{2}(\varOmega)
without requiring
ellipticity or the Cordes condition. This suggests that this
linearization method can be applied to approximate the strong solution
of a well-posed HJB equation under less stringent conditions.
Through semismooth Newton linearization, the fully nonlinear HJB
problem transforms into a recursive linear problem in nondivergence
form, with its strong solution converging to the strong solution of
the HJB equation with a superlinear rate.

With appropriate modifications, one can extend Newton
differentiability and the semismooth Newton linearization to nonconvex
Isaacs equations.

In the second step of the approximation, we discretized the recursive
linear problem in each iteration using a mixed finite element method, employing a least-squares approach with gradient recovery.
A notable advantage of the least-squares approach is the ability to enforce constraints on the unknowns by incorporating square terms into the cost functional.
However, each discretization that works for the linear equation in nondivergence form can also be applied.

Additionally, we have conducted an error analysis of the approximation, obtaining both a priori and a posteriori error bounds. The a priori error bound serves to demonstrate the convergence of the discretization, while the a posteriori error bound facilitates the application of adaptive refinement procedures.
