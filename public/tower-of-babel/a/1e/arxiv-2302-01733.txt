Abstract
A private information retrieval (PIR) scheme allows a client to retrieve a data item x i x_{i} among n n items x 1 , x 2 , … , x n x_{1},x_{2},\ldots,x_{n} from k k servers, without revealing what i i is even when t < k t<k servers collude and try to learn i i . Such a PIR scheme is said to be t t -private . A PIR scheme is v v -verifiable if the client can verify the correctness of the retrieved x i x_{i} even when v ≤ k v\leq k servers collude and try to fool the client by sending manipulated data. Most of the previous works in the literature on PIR assumed that v < k v<k , leaving the case of all-colluding servers open. We propose a generic construction that combines a linear map commitment (LMC) and an arbitrary linear PIR scheme to produce a k k -verifiable PIR scheme, termed a committed PIR scheme. Such a scheme guarantees that even in the worst scenario, when all servers are under the control of an attacker, although the privacy is unavoidably lost, the client won’t be fooled into accepting an incorrect x i x_{i} . We demonstrate the practicality of our proposal by implementing the committed PIR schemes based on the Lai-Malavolta LMC and three well-known PIR schemes using the GMP library and blst , the current fastest C library for elliptic curve pairings.

Download Rate.

Another approach to reduce the communication cost is to optimize the download cost, assuming that the data items are of large size and hence the upload cost will be overshadowed by the download cost (see, e.g. Sun and Jafar

More precisely, one can aim for maximizing the

Computation Cost
. The computation cost of a PIR scheme typically consists of the computation time required by the client in generating the request and in recovering the desired data

2.4
Committed Private Information Retrieval

Apart from the large amount of research aiming for optimizing the upload or the download costs of a PIR scheme, there have also been a number of proposals in the literature that seek to extend the basic setting of the PIR problem (see, e.g.

In the scope of this work, we are interested in the

A

3.2
A Generic Construction of Com-PIR

We now introduce a generic construction that combines an
n
-dimensional LMC and a linear
k
-server
m\times n
-dimensional PIR to produce a
k
-verifiable Com-PIR (see Fig.

for an illustration). The construction first applies a cryptographic hash function
h^{*}(\cdot)
followed by a modulo operation to each column of the database
{\bm{x}}
to achieve
h_{j}=h^{*}(x_{j})\pmod{p}
, where
x_{j}
denotes the
j
th column of
{\bm{x}}\in\mathbb{F}^{m\times n}=\mathbb{Z}_{p}^{m\times n}
. It then applies an LMC to the vector
{\bm{h}}=(h_{1},h_{2},\ldots,h_{n})\in\mathbb{F}^{n}
. The client performs PIR requests for
both

x_{i}
and
h_{i}
. As the correctness of the received

is guaranteed by the LMC, the verification
h_{i}\overset{?}{=}h(\hat{x}_{i})
is reliable.

, which in turn is taken modulo

. This only reduces the security of SHA3-256 by at most one bit. The modulo operation is necessary to turn the hashes into valid input to the LMC. The LMC computation is carried out over the

.

•

•

as

. Next, it converts

. Then, it verifies

by running

. Otherwise, it calls

and returns

Note that in the generic construction above, the PIR scheme is applied to
{\bm{x}}\in\mathbb{Z}_{p}^{m\times n}

\mathbb{Z}_{p}^{n}
as in Definition

The communication and computation costs of a Com-PIR scheme based on the generic construction can be calculated easily based on the costs of the underlying PIR and LMC.
Note that the LMC doesn’t depend on

,

Lemma 1
(Correctness/Privacy)

The Com-PIR constructed by the generic construction is correct and
t
-private if the underlying LMC scheme is correct and the underlying PIR scheme is both correct and
t
-private.

Proof.

The correctness of the constructed Com-PIR scheme can be proved in a straightforward manner, implied directly from the correctness of the underlying LMC and PIR schemes. The privacy of the Com-PIR follows from the privacy of the underlying PIR scheme because the queries sent from the client are identical to those in the original PIR scheme.

-server

Lemma 2
(Verifiability)

Let

and

. Then the
k

-dimensional Com-PIR using the Lai-Malavolta LMC is
k
-verifiable in the generic bilinear group model.

and the number of servers
k
.

Comparison of the three Com-PIR schemes.
We compare these schemes based on their communication and computation complexities (Table
1
).

•

LM-CKGS
: this scheme has the
lowest computation time
among the three for both servers and client. The reason is that each server only performs cheap field additions for the data part and generates LMC witness for a single linear combination of hashes. The client performs one LMC verification per server.

•

LM-WY
: although having the
lowest upload cost
, the computation cost of this scheme is the highest among the three.
The reason is that its running time also depends on
\ell\in O(n^{1/d})
. See Appendix C for more details.

•

LM-BE
: this scheme achieves the
optimal download rate
and has computation cost lying in between the other two. Computation-wise, the smaller the difference
k-t
, the lower the running time of both servers and client. The reason is that the LMC has to run not on
n
but on
(k-t)n
hashes.

6
Conclusions

We proposed a modular approach to combine a linear map commitment and a linear PIR scheme to achieve a
k
-verifiable PIR scheme, which guarantees that the client will never accept wrong data even in the extreme case when all servers are malicious. By applying the commitment scheme on hashes of data rather than on data themselves, the construction is reasonably practical, taking less than one second to privately retrieve 3MB of data from a database of size 3GB. A drawback of our approach is that the commitment scheme may incur a significant computation overhead on top of the PIR scheme if the database consists of a large number of small-sized items.

Appendix A: Verifiability Proof of Com-PIR

For simplicity, we consider one linear combination of
{\bm{x}}
in the definition below.

Definition 10
(Function Binding for LMC)

[
13
]

An LMC over
\mathbb{F}
is function binding if for any PPT adversary
{\mathcal{A}}
, any positive integer
n\in{\sf{poly}}(\lambda)
, there exists a negligible function
\varepsilon(\lambda)
such that

{\sf{Pr}}\left[\begin{array}[]{c|c}y\in\mathbb{F}&\omega\mbox{ }{}{\leftarrow}\vbox{\hbox{\tiny\$}}{}\mbox{ }\{0,1\}^{\lambda}\\
\mbox{ }{\sf{Verify}}(C,{\bm{c}},y,w)=1&\mbox{ }{\sf{pp}}\leftarrow{\sf{Setup}}(1^{\lambda},n;\omega)\\
\not\exists{\bm{x}}\in\mathbb{F}^{n}\text{ s.t. }\sum_{j\in[n]}c_{j}x_{j}=y&(C,{\bm{c}},y,w)\leftarrow{\mathcal{A}}({\sf{pp}})\\
\end{array}\right]\leq\varepsilon(\lambda).

Lemma 3
(
[
13
]
)

Let
n\in{\sf{poly}}(\lambda)
and
1/p\in{\sf{negl}}(\lambda)
. Then Lai-Malavolta LMC is function binding in the generic bilinear group model.

Proof.
[Proof of Lemma
2
]
According to the generic construction, the adversary wins the security experiment, i.e.,
{\sf{EXP}}_{{\mathcal{A}},\Pi}(k,m,n,{\bm{x}},i,B)=1
, if and only if the challenger extracts
\hat{x}_{i}\notin\{x_{i},\perp\}
. This happens only when one of the following two independent events occur: either
\hat{h}_{i}=h_{i}
, i.e., the adversary finds a hash collision
h(\hat{x}_{i})=h(x_{i})=h_{i}
, or
\hat{h}_{i}\neq h_{i}
but the adversary manages to fool
\text{LMC}.{\sf{Verify}}()
with at least one wrong linear combination of
h_{i}
’s. Therefore,

\begin{split}&{\sf{Pr}}[{\sf{EXP}}_{{\mathcal{A}},\Pi}(k,m,n,{\bm{x}},i,B)=1]={\sf{Pr}}[\hat{x}_{i}\neq x_{i}\wedge h(\hat{x}_{i})=h(x_{i})]\\
&+{\sf{Pr}}[\text{At least one linear combination is wrong but still passes LMC.{\sf{Verify}}()}]\\
&\leq\varepsilon_{1}(\lambda)+\varepsilon_{2}(\lambda)\in{\sf{negl}}(\lambda),\vskip-5.0pt\end{split}

as both events happen with probabilities negligible in
\lambda
assuming that
1/p
is negligible in
\lambda
and that the security is considered under the generic bilinear group model (Lemma
3
).

Appendix B: WY and BE PIR Schemes

Woodruff-Yekhanin (WY) Scheme
(
[
17
]
).
This is a linear
k
-server
n
-dimensi-onal PIR scheme working over an arbitrary finite field
{\mathbb{F}}
. Let
1\leq t<k
and
d=\lfloor(2k-1)/t\rfloor
. Let
\ell\in O\big(n^{1/d}\big)
be the smallest integer satisfying
\binom{\ell}{d}\geq n
and
E\colon[n]\to\mathbb{F}^{\ell}
a 1-to-1 mapping that maps an index
j\in[n]
to a vector in
\mathbb{F}^{\ell}
of Hamming weight
d
. Each
{\bm{x}}\in\mathbb{F}^{n}
is encoded by a multivariate polynomial
F_{{\bm{x}}}({\bm{z}})
, where
{\bm{z}}=(z_{1},\ldots,z_{\ell})
, defined as follows.

F_{{\bm{x}}}({\bm{z}})\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mbox{\tiny$\triangle$}}}{{=}}\sum_{j\in[n]}x_{j}\prod_{u\in[\ell]\colon E(j)_{u}=1}z_{u}.\vskip-5.0pt

Then,
\deg(F)=d
and
x_{i}=F_{{\bm{x}}}(E(i))
. Fix
k
distinct elements
\{\beta_{j}\}_{j\in[k]}\subseteq\mathbb{F}_{p}^{*}
.

•

\big(\{q_{j}\}_{j\in[k]},{\sf{aux}}\big)\leftarrow{\sf{QueriesGen}}(n,k,i)
: The algorithm picks
t
random vectors
\{v^{(s)}\}_{s\in[t]}\subseteq\mathbb{F}^{\ell}
and outputs
q_{j}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mbox{\tiny$\triangle$}}}{{=}}E(i)+\sum_{s\in[t]}\beta_{j}^{s}v_{s}
and
{\sf{aux}}=\{v^{(s)}\}_{s\in[t]}
.

•

a_{j}\leftarrow{\sf{AnswerGen}}({\bm{x}},q_{j})
: The algorithm computes
a_{j}=(a_{j,0},a_{j,1},\ldots,a_{j,\ell})
, where
a_{j,0}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mbox{\tiny$\triangle$}}}{{=}}F_{{\bm{x}}}(q_{j})
and
a_{j,u}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mbox{\tiny$\triangle$}}}{{=}}\frac{\partial F_{{\bm{x}}}}{z_{u}}\rvert_{q_{j}}
,
u\in[\ell]
.

•

\{x_{i}\}\leftarrow{\sf{Extract}}\big(n,i,\{a_{j}\}_{j\in[k]},{\sf{aux}}\big)
: The algorithm reconstructs the polynomial
f(y)\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mbox{\tiny$\triangle$}}}{{=}}F_{{\bm{x}}}\big(E(i)+\sum_{s\in[t]}y^{s}v^{(s)}\big)
and outputs
f(0)=F_{{\bm{x}}}(E(i))=x_{i}
. The reconstruction of
f
is possible because
\deg(f)\leq dt\leq 2k-1
while
2k
linear combinations of its coefficients, namely,
\{f(\beta_{j}),f^{\prime}(\beta_{j})\}_{j\in[k]}
, can be extracted from the answers
\{a_{j}\}_{j\in[k]}
and
{\sf{aux}}=\{v^{(s)}\}_{s\in[t]}
as follows. For
j\in[k]
,
f(\beta_{j})=F_{{\bm{x}}}(q_{j})=a_{j,0}
, and

f^{\prime}(\beta_{j})\hskip-2.0pt=\hskip-2.0pt\hskip-2.0pt\sum_{u\in[\ell]}\hskip-2.0pt\frac{\partial F}{\partial z_{u}}\bigg\rvert_{q_{j}}\hskip-2.0pt\frac{\partial}{\partial y}\bigg(\hskip-2.0ptE(i)_{u}\hskip-1.0pt+\hskip-2.0pt\sum_{s\in[t]}\hskip-2.0pty^{s}v^{(s)}_{u}\hskip-2.0pt\bigg)\hskip-2.0pt\bigg\rvert_{\beta_{j}}\hskip-5.0pt=\hskip-4.0pt\sum_{u\in[\ell]}\hskip-2.0pta_{j,u}\frac{\partial}{\partial y}\bigg(\hskip-2.0ptE(i)_{u}+\hskip-2.0pt\sum_{s\in[t]}y^{s}v^{(s)}_{u}\hskip-2.0pt\bigg)\hskip-2.0pt\bigg\rvert_{\beta_{j}}\hskip-3.0pt.\vskip-5.0pt

Note that each server generates
\ell\in O(n^{1/d})
linear combinations of
{\bm{x}}
. Although in Algorithm 1 we let the LMC verify just one linear combination of
{\bm{x}}
for simplicity, in its original form, Lai-Malavolta LMC can verify multiple linear combinations using a single witness.

Bitar-El Rouayheb (BE) Scheme
(
[
18
]
).
This scheme works slightly different from the previous ones in that the client retrieves a fixed block of
k-t
components of
{\bm{x}}
instead of a single component. All definitions of a PIR scheme can be generalized to this block form in a straightforward manner. Let
{\bm{x}}=(x_{1},\ldots,x_{(k-t)n})\in\mathbb{F}^{(k-t)n}
and assume the client wants to retrieve the
i
th block
\big(x_{(i-1)(k-t)+1},\ldots,x_{i(k-t)}\big)
for some
i\in[n]
.

•

\big(\{q_{j}\}_{j\in[k]},{\sf{aux}}\big)\leftarrow{\sf{QueriesGen}}(n,k,i)
: The algorithm picks a
k\times k
Vandermonde matrix
\bm{V}=\big(\beta_{a}^{b-1}\big)_{a,b\in[k]}
, where
\{\beta_{s}\}_{s\in[t]}
is a set of
k
distinct elements in
{\mathbb{F}}
. It also picks
t
random vectors
\{v^{(s)}\}_{s\in[t]}\subseteq\mathbb{F}^{(k-t)n}
and set the queries to be the rows of the matrix
{\bm{Q}}=\bm{V}{\bm{M}}
given as follows.

{\bm{Q}}=\begin{pmatrix}q_{1}\\
\hline\cr q_{2}\\
\hline\cr\vdots\\
\hline\cr q_{k}\end{pmatrix}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mbox{\tiny$\triangle$}}}{{=}}\bm{V}{\bm{M}}=\bm{V}\begin{pmatrix}v^{(1)}\\
\hline\cr\vdots\\
\hline\cr v^{(t)}\\
\hline\cr{\bm{e}}_{(i-1)(k-t)+1}\\
\hline\cr\vdots\\
\hline\cr{\bm{e}}_{i(k-t)}\end{pmatrix}.

•

a_{j}\leftarrow{\sf{AnswerGen}}({\bm{x}},q_{j})
: The algorithm outputs
a_{j}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mbox{\tiny$\triangle$}}}{{=}}q_{j}\cdot{\bm{x}}
.

•

\{x_{i}\}\leftarrow{\sf{Extract}}\big(n,i,\{a_{j}\}_{j\in[k]},{\sf{aux}}\big)
: The algorithm calculates
\bm{V}^{-1}{\bm{Q}}{\bm{x}}={\bm{M}}{\bm{x}}
, which gives
\big(x_{(i-1)(k-t)+1},\ldots,x_{i(k-t)}\big)
.

The
t
-privacy is guaranteed because any set of
t
queries has
t
random vectors
\{v^{(s)}\}_{s\in[t]}
well mixed (the submatrix of
{\bm{Q}}
formed by any
t
rows and the first
t
columns is always invertible) and hence appears completely random.
