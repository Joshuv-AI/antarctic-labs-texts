Theory and Application of Augmented Dimensional Analysis
Dan Jonsson

Abstract
. We present an innovative approach to dimensional analysis, referred to as augmented dimensional analysis and based on a representation theorem for complete quantity functions with a scaling-covariant scalar representation. This new theorem, grounded in a purely algebraic theory of quantity spaces, allows the classical π \pi theorem to be restated in an explicit and precise form and its prerequisites to be clarified and relaxed. Augmented dimensional analysis, in contrast to classical dimensional analysis, is guaranteed to take into account all relations among the quantities involved. Several examples are given to show that the information thus gained, together with symmetry assumptions, can lead to new or stronger results. We also explore the connection between dimensional analysis and matroid theory, elucidating combinatorial aspects of dimensional analysis. It is emphasized that dimensional analysis rests on a principle of covariance.



1.
Introduction

In classical dimensional analysis, the main result is the so-called

\pi
theorem, with a long history featuring contributions by Fourier

[
10
]
, Vaschy
[
27
]
, Federman
[

[

(1.1)

expressing a relationship among quantities, into a more informative

This can be done by representing the unknown function
\phi
as a
product of the form
\prod_{j=1}^{r}\nolimits\!x{}_{j}^{K_{j}}\,\psi

where

than

t_{0}=\prod_{j=1}^{r}\nolimits\!x{}_{j}^{K_{j}}\,\psi\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\pi_{1},\ldots,\pi_{n-r}}}\right)\qquad\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(1\leq r\leq n}}\right),

In classical dimensional analysis,
\phi
is a real function, and

are measures of physical quantities. Various
assumptions pertaining to the functions
\phi
and
\psi

and the parameters

have been made in connection
with the development of the theory of dimensional analysis.

(1)

Proofs of the

(a)

Those that translate the fact that

(b)

Those that are made for internal mathematical reasons. For example,

\pi
theorem have involved assumptions that
\phi

is continuous or differentiable or analytic
[
11
, pp. 67–71]
;
such assumptions may relate more to availability of mathematical notions
and techniques than to what is “physically meaningful”.

(2)

It is usually assumed that
t_{i}>0
. Some authors consider quantities
to be positive by nature, but there are also formal reasons for this
assumption: it is required in some proofs of the

(3)

The exponents

The emergence of quantity calculus

,

12

(i)

The idea that

(iv)

There may be more than one partition

2.
Preliminaries

2.1.
Quantity spaces and free abelian groups of dimensions

In this article, we we will use the formalization of quantities and
dimensions presented and developed in some detail in
[
17
]
.
For the sake of completeness, we briefly review some definitions and
results from
[
17
]
here.

A
scalable monoid
over a ring
R
is a monoid
Q
equipped
with an
R
-action
\cdot
on
Q
,

R\times Q\rightarrow Q,\qquad\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\alpha,q}}\right)\mapsto\alpha\cdot q,

compatible with multiplication in
Q
. For any
\alpha,\beta\in R

and
q,q^{\prime}\in Q
, we have
1\cdot q=q
,
\alpha\cdot\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\beta\cdot q}}\right)=\alpha\beta\cdot q

and
\alpha\cdot qq^{\prime}=\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\alpha\cdot q}}\right)q^{\prime}=q\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\alpha\cdot q^{\prime}}}\right)
;
as a consequence,
\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\alpha\cdot q}}\right)\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\beta\cdot q^{\prime}}}\right)=\alpha\beta\cdot qq^{\prime}
.
We denote the identity element of
Q
by
1_{\!Q}
, and set
q^{0}=1_{\!Q}

for any
q\in Q
. An element
q\in Q
may have an inverse
q^{-1}\in Q

such that
qq^{-1}=q^{-1}q=1_{\!Q}
.

A
finite

(quantity-space) basis
for a commutative scalable
monoid
Q
over a field
\mathbb{K}
is a set
E_{Q}=\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\{e_{1},\ldots,e_{m}}}\right\}

of invertible elements of
Q
such that every
q\in Q
has a unique
expansion

q=\mu_{E_{Q}}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(q}}\right)\cdot\prod_{j=1}^{m}\nolimits e_{j}^{K_{j}},

where
\mu_{E_{Q}}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(q}}\right)\in\mathbb{K}
and
K_{j}\in\mathbb{Z}
.
A
(finitely generated) quantity space

Q
is a commutative
scalable monoid over a field, such that there exists a finite basis
for
Q
. The elements of a quantity space are called
quantities
.
We may think of
\mu_{E_{Q}}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(q}}\right)
as the measure of
q

relative to the derived unit
\prod_{j=1}^{m}\nolimits e_{j}^{K_{j}}

in a coherent system of units, and indirectly relative to the base
units in
E_{Q}
.

The relation
\sim
on
Q
defined by
q\sim q^{\prime}
if and only if

\alpha\cdot q=\beta\cdot q^{\prime}
for some
\alpha,\beta\in\mathbb{K}

is a congruence on
Q
. The corresponding equivalence classes are
called
dimensions
;
\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[q}}\right]
is the dimension that
contains
q
. We have
\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[\lambda\cdot q}}\right]=\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[q}}\right]

for any
\lambda\in\mathbb{K},q\in Q
.

The set of all dimensions in
Q
, denoted
Q/{\sim}
, is a finitely
generated free abelian group with multiplication defined by
\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[q}}\right]\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[q^{\prime}}}\right]=\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[qq^{\prime}}}\right]

and identity
\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[1_{Q}}}\right]
. Hence,
\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[q^{k}}}\right]=\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[q}}\right]^{k}

for any
q\in Q
and any integer
k
when
q
is invertible, or
any non-negative integer otherwise.

The quantities in a dimension
\mathsf{C}\in Q/{\sim}
form a one-dimensional
vector space over
\mathbb{K}
with addition inherited from
\mathbb{K}

[
17
, Definition 2.35]
and a unique zero vector or
zero

quantity

0_{\mathsf{C}}\neq 1_{Q}
. While
0_{\mathsf{C}}q=0_{\mathsf{C}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[q}}\right]}

for every
q\in Q
, the product of non-zero quantities is a non-zero
quantity. A quantity is invertible if and only if it is non-zero,
and any non-zero
u\in\mathsf{C}
is a
unit quantity
for
\mathsf{C}
,
meaning that for every
q\in\mathsf{C}
there is a unique
\mu\in\mathbb{K}

for
u
such that
q=\mu\cdot u
, where
\mu=0
if and only if

q=0_{\mathsf{C}}
.

2.2.
Motivation and preview

Dimensional analysis is based on the principle that when quantities
are represented by scalars relative to a choice of units, so that
a given
relation
between quantities is represented by a particular
relation between scalars relative to a choice of units, that relation
between scalars must hold for any choice of units. For example, if

q
and
q^{\prime}
are quantities,
\mu_{E}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(q}}\right)
and
\mu_{E}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(q^{\prime}}}\right)

are scalars representing
q
and
q^{\prime}
, respectively, relative to
a basis
E
, and
\mu_{E}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(q}}\right)=\mu_{E}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(q^{\prime}}}\right)

represents the relation
q=q^{\prime}
relative to
E
then
\mu_{E^{\prime}}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(q}}\right)=\mu_{E^{\prime}}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(q^{\prime}}}\right)

for any choice of basis
E^{\prime}
. Below, we define relations between
quantities by equations of the form
q_{0}=\Phi\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(q_{1}.\ldots.q_{n}}}\right)
,
where
\Phi
is a quantity function, and relations between scalars
by equations of the form
\mu_{E}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(q_{0}}}\right)=\phi\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\mu_{E}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(q_{1}}}\right),\ldots,\mu_{E}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(q_{n}}}\right)}}\right)
,
where
\phi
is a scalar function. If
\phi
conforms with the representation
principle just described, it is said to be a “covariant scalar representation”
of
\Phi
(Section
2.4
).

In Section
2.3
, we consider several closely related notions
shown in the table below:

One may question whether these are distinctions that need to be emphasized,
but it turns out that they are quite important. In particular, these
distinctions serve a proof strategy often used in mathematics: results
are proved in one domain in order to be transported to another one,
exploiting the fact that the first domain is better understood or
allows simpler arguments.

In a free abelian group
G
, every maximal independent set of elements
has the same number of elements, the rank
r
of
G
. In particular,
every basis
\mathsf{E}
for
G
has
r
elements. Thus, every

\mathsf{D}\in G
has an expansion
\mathsf{D}=\prod_{i=1}^{r}\mathsf{E}_{i}^{K_{i}}
,
where
\mathsf{E}_{i}\in\mathsf{E}
for
i=1,\ldots,r
and
K_{i}

are unique integers. However, not every maximal independent set
\mathcal{E}=\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\{\mathcal{E}_{1},\ldots,\mathcal{E}_{r}}}\right\}

of
r
elements of
G
is a basis; we only have that
\mathsf{D}^{K}=\prod_{i=1}^{r}\mathcal{E}{}_{i}^{K_{i}}

with unique integers
K,K_{i}
such that
K>0
and
\gcd\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(K,K_{1},\ldots,K_{r}}}\right)=1

(Section
2.3
). A maximal independent subset of
G
is a
“prebasis” for
G
; this is a basis if and only if
K=1
.

In classical dimensional analysis, one considers a free abelian group

G
given by a fixed basis such as
\mathsf{E}_{G}=\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\{\mathsf{L},\mathsf{T},\mathsf{M}}}\right\}

(length, time and mass). In the present approach, we start instead
from a free abelian group
G
without a choice of basis and a quantity
function
\Phi:\mathsf{C}_{1}\times\cdots\times\mathsf{C}_{n}\rightarrow\mathsf{C}_{0}^{\mathfrak{K}}
,
where
\mathsf{C}_{i}\in G
. We then look for those subsets
\mathcal{E}

of
\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\{\mathsf{C}_{1},\ldots.\mathsf{C}_{n}}}\right\}
which
are prebases for the free abelian group
G^{\prime}\subseteq G
generated
by
\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\{\mathsf{C}_{0},\ldots,\mathsf{C}_{n}}}\right\}
. At least
one such
\mathcal{E}
must exist for dimensional analysis to be
possible, but more than one may be found. Each such prebasis may or
may not be a basis for
G^{\prime}
.

It is easiest to prove an analogue of the
\pi
theorem –
a representation theorem for
\Phi
– in the case when

\mathcal{E}
is a basis for
G^{\prime}
. In Section
3
, we first
prove a representation theorem that concerns this special case (Theorem

3.1
), and then generalize to the case when
\mathcal{E}

is a prebasis (Theorem
3.3
) by means of Lemma
3.2
.

Informally, a dimension is a quantity that has “forgotten its size”,
so quantities and dimensions are largely equivalent, but as mathematical
objects dimensions are better understood, being elements of free abelian
groups. Thus, in Section
2.3
some notions are first developed
for dimensions and then carried over to quantities; the main “transport
theorem” is Lemma
2.2
. To prove Theorem
3.1
,
we use the covariance principle described above, properties of
\mu_{E}

and of free abelian groups, and Lemma
2.2
.

2.3.
Quantity functions with associated prebases and bases

A
quantity function
on a quantity space

is a function
of the form

(2.1)

\Phi:\mathsf{C}_{1}\times\cdots\times\mathsf{C}_{n}\rightarrow\mathsf{C}_{0}^{\mathfrak{K}},\qquad\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(q_{1},\ldots,q_{n}}}\right)\mapsto q_{0}^{\mathfrak{K}}\qquad(n\geq 0,\mathsf{C}_{i}\in Q/{\sim}),

where
\mathfrak{K}
is a positive integer. Equation (

)
now takes the form
q_{0}^{\mathfrak{K}}=\Phi\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(q_{1},\ldots,q_{n}}}\right)
,
where

are quantities. It is easy to show that

q_{0}^{\mathfrak{K}}\in\mathsf{C}_{0}^{\mathfrak{K}}
implies
q_{0}\in\mathsf{C}_{0}

as

The dimensions
\mathsf{D}_{1},\ldots,\mathsf{D}_{m}\in Q/{\sim}

or quantities

when there are integers

A

.

Similarly, a
prebasis
(of quantities) for
\Phi
is a set
of non-zero independent quantities

such that for

there are integer sequences

such that

(2.3)

\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[q_{i}^{k_{i}^{\prime}}}}\right]=\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[\prod_{j=1}^{r}\nolimits e_{j}^{k_{ij}^{\prime}}}}\right]\qquad\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(k_{i}^{\prime}\neq 0}}\right).

It can be shown

]

of

such that

and

\mathsf{E}=\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\{\mathsf{E}_{1},\ldots,\mathsf{E}_{r}}}\right\}

Lemma 2.1
.

Let

\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(Q/{\sim}}}\right)^{\prime}

of

is free abelian, and the number of elements in any prebasis of dimensions

Proof (sketch).

Any maximal independent subset of

\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(Q/{\sim}}}\right)^{\prime}
. This proves the assertion
since
\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(Q/{\sim}}}\right)^{\prime}
, a subgroup of

, is also

∎

Lemma 2.2

Let
\mathsf{E}
be a basis of dimensions for
\Phi:\mathsf{C}_{1}\times\cdots\times\mathsf{C}_{n}\rightarrow\mathsf{C}_{0}^{\mathfrak{K}}
,

E=\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\{e_{1},\ldots,e_{r}}}\right\}\simeq\mathsf{E}
a basis for

\Phi

q_{i}\in\mathsf{C}_{i}
. If
\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[q_{i}}}\right]=\prod_{j=1}^{r}\nolimits\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[e_{i}}}\right]^{K_{ij}}

then

has a unique expansion
q_{i}=\mu\cdot\prod_{j=1}^{r}e_{j}^{K_{ij}}

Proof.

Since
q_{i}\in\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[\prod_{j=1}^{r}e_{j}^{K_{ij}}}}\right]
and
\prod_{j=1}^{r}\nolimits e_{j}^{K_{ij}}\neq 0_{\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[q_{i}}}\right]}
,
there is a unique
\mu\in\mathbb{K}
for
\prod_{j=1}^{r}e_{j}^{K_{ij}}

such that
q_{i}=\mu\cdot\prod_{j=1}^{r}e_{j}^{K_{ij}}
. Also, if

\mu^{\prime}\cdot\prod_{j=1}^{r}e_{j}^{k_{ij}}=\mu\cdot\prod_{j=1}^{r}e_{j}^{K_{ij}}

then
\prod_{j=1}^{r}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[e_{j}}}\right]^{k_{ij}}=\prod_{j=1}^{r}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[e_{j}}}\right]^{K_{ij}}
,
so
\prod_{j=1}^{r}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[e_{j}}}\right]^{k_{ij}-K_{ij}}=\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[1_{Q}}}\right],

so
k_{ij}=K_{ij}
for
j=1,\ldots,r
since
\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[e_{1}}}\right],\ldots,\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[e_{r}}}\right]

are independent, so
\mu^{\prime}=\mu
.
∎

Let
Q^{\prime}
be the subspace of
Q
generated by all non-zero
q_{0},\ldots,q_{n}
,
where
q_{i}\in\mathsf{C}_{i}
. Since
e_{1},\ldots,e_{r}
are independent
and generate
Q^{\prime}
by Lemma
2.2
, every
q\in Q^{\prime}
has a
unique expansion of the form
q=\mu_{E}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(q}}\right)\cdot\prod_{j=1}^{r}e_{j}^{K_{j}}

in terms of the “local basis”
E
. For any
E
,
\mu_{E}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(qq^{\prime}}}\right)=\mu_{E}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(q}}\right)\mu_{E}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(q^{\prime}}}\right)

for any
q,q^{\prime}\in Q^{\prime}
, and
\mu_{E}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(q^{-1}}}\right)=\mu_{E}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(q}}\right)^{-1}

for any invertible
q\in Q^{\prime}

[
17
]
. If
q\in\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[1_{Q}}}\right]

then
q=\mu_{E}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(q}}\right)\cdot\prod_{i=1}^{n}e_{i}^{0}
, so in
that case
\mu_{E}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(q}}\right)
does not depend on
E
.

2.4.
Covariant scalar representations

The “physically meaningful” quantity functions of interest in
augmented dimensional analysis have scalar representations that do
not depend on a choice of basis. Specifically, a
covariant scalar
representation
of a complete quantity function
\Phi
of the form
(
2.1
) is a function
\phi:\mathbb{K}^{n}\rightarrow\mathbb{K}

such that

(2.4)

\mu_{E}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\Phi\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(q_{1},\ldots,q_{n}}}\right)}}\right)=\phi\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\mu_{E}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(q_{1}}}\right),\ldots,\mu_{E}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(q_{n}}}\right)}}\right)

for any basis
E
for
\Phi
and any
q_{1},\ldots,q_{n}
.

If
E
is only allowed to be any basis such that
E\simeq\mathsf{E}
,
where
\mathsf{E}
is a fixed basis of dimensions, then
\phi

is said to be a
scaling-covariant
scalar representation. A
covariant scalar representation is obviously scaling-covariant.

Note that the identity map
\mathrm{id}:\mathbb{K}\rightarrow\mathbb{K}

is a covariant scalar representation of the identity map
\mathrm{Id}:\mathsf{C}\rightarrow\mathsf{C}

since
\mu_{E}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\mathrm{Id}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(q}}\right)}}\right)=\mathrm{id}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\mu_{E}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(q}}\right)}}\right)

for all
q
and
E
. Also, if
\phi_{1}
is a covariant scalar
representation of
\Phi_{1}:\mathsf{C}_{1}\rightarrow\mathsf{D}_{1}
,
and
\phi_{2}
of
\Phi_{2}:\mathsf{C_{2}}\rightarrow\mathsf{D}_{2}
,
then
\phi_{1}\phi_{2}
is a covariant scalar representation of
\Phi_{1}\Phi_{2}

since

\mu_{E}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\Phi_{1}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(q_{1}}}\right)\Phi_{2}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(q_{2}}}\right)}}\right)=\mu_{E}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\Phi_{1}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(q_{1}}}\right)}}\right)\mu_{E}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\Phi_{2}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(q_{2}}}\right)}}\right)=\phi_{1}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\mu_{E}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(q_{1}}}\right)}}\right)\phi_{2}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\mu_{E}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(q_{2}}}\right)}}\right)

for all
q_{1},q_{2}
and
E
, and if
\phi
is a covariant scalar
representation of
\Phi:\mathsf{D}_{1}\times\cdots\times\mathsf{D}_{n}\rightarrow\mathsf{D}_{0}

and
\omega_{1},\ldots,\omega_{n}
are covariant scalar representations
of
\text{$\Omega$}_{1}:\mathsf{C}_{1}\rightarrow\mathsf{D}_{1},\ldots,\text{$\Omega$}_{n}:\mathsf{C}_{n}\rightarrow\mathsf{D}_{n}
,
respectively, then
\phi\circ\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\omega_{1},\ldots,\omega_{n}}}\right)

is a scalar representation of
\Phi\circ\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\Omega_{1},\ldots,\Omega_{n}}}\right)

since

\mu_{E}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\Phi\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\Omega_{1}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(q_{1}}}\right),\ldots,\Omega_{n}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(q_{n}}}\right)}}\right)}}\right)=\phi\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\mu_{E}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\Omega_{1}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(q_{1}}}\right)}}\right),\ldots,\mu_{E}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\Omega_{n}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(q_{n}}}\right)}}\right)}}\right)=\phi\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\omega_{1}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\mu_{E}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(q_{1}}}\right)}}\right),\ldots,\omega_{n}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\mu_{E}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(q_{n}}}\right)}}\right)}}\right)

for all
q_{1},\ldots,q_{n}
and
E
. These results apply to scaling-covariant
scalar representations as well.

Note that a complete quantity function need not
have a covariant scalar representation.

Example 2.3
.

Consider a quantity function

\Phi_{u}:\mathsf{C}\rightarrow\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[1_{Q}}}\right],\qquad\Phi_{u}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\lambda\cdot u}}\right)\mapsto\lambda\cdot 1_{Q}\qquad\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(0_{\mathsf{C}}\neq u\in\mathsf{C},\mathsf{C}\neq\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[1_{Q}}}\right]}}\right).

has a basis of dimensions
\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\{\mathsf{C}}}\right\}

since

and
\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[1_{Q}}}\right]=\mathsf{\mathsf{C}^{0}}
.
Let us show that

\mu_{\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\{u}}\right\}}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\Phi_{u}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(u}}\right)}}\right)\neq\phi\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\mu_{\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\{u}}\right\}}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(u}}\right)}}\right)\quad\mathrm{or}\quad\mu_{\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\{2\cdot u}}\right\}}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\Phi_{u}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(2\cdot u}}\right)}}\right)\neq\phi\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\mu_{\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\{2\cdot u}}\right\}}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(2\cdot u}}\right)}}\right).

We have

since

, but
\mu_{\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\{2\cdot u}}\right\}}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\Phi_{u}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(2\cdot u}}\right)}}\right)=2

since
\mu_{\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\{u}}\right\}}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\Phi_{u}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(2\cdot u}}\right)}}\right)=2

and
\Phi_{u}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(x}}\right)\in\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[1_{Q}}}\right]
. However,
\phi\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\mu_{\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\{u}}\right\}}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(u}}\right)}}\right)=\phi\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\mu_{\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\{2\cdot u}}\right\}}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(2\cdot u}}\right)}}\right)=\phi\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(1}}\right)

Conversely, if

has a scaling-covariant scalar representation then

is a constant
function, as shown in Example

.

3.
Representation theorems

Given a quantity function
\Phi:\mathsf{C}_{1}\times\cdots\times\mathsf{C}_{n}\rightarrow\mathsf{C}_{0}^{\mathfrak{K}}

and a permutation
\sigma
of
\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\{1,\ldots,n}}\right\}
one
can construct a new quantity function
\Phi^{\prime}:\mathsf{C}_{\sigma\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(1}}\right)}\times\cdots\times\mathsf{C}_{\sigma\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(n}}\right)}\rightarrow\mathsf{C}_{0}^{\mathfrak{K}}

by setting
\Phi^{\prime}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(q_{\sigma\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(1}}\right)},\ldots,q_{\sigma\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(n}}\right)}}}\right)=\Phi\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(q_{1},\ldots,q_{n}}}\right)
.
One may in particular reorder the arguments so that the elements of
a certain prebasis for
\Phi
come first in
\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(q_{\sigma\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(1}}\right)},\ldots,q_{\sigma\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(n}}\right)}}}\right)
;
we call this a
prebasis reordering
of
\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(q_{1},\ldots,q_{n}}}\right)
.
The results in this section apply to a quantity function obtained,
if necessary, by a prebasis reordering of arguments.

Theorem 3.1

(special representation theorem)
.

Let
Q
be a quantity space, let

\Phi:\mathsf{E}_{1}\times\cdots\times\mathsf{E}_{r}\times\mathsf{D}_{1}\times\cdots\times\mathsf{D}_{n-r}\rightarrow\mathsf{D}_{0},\qquad\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(x_{1},\ldots,x_{r}}}\right)\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(y_{1},\ldots,y_{n-r}}}\right)\mapsto y_{0}\qquad\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(r,n-r\geq 0}}\right)

be a complete quantity function on
Q
and let
\mathsf{E}=\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\{\mathsf{E}_{1},\ldots,\mathsf{E}_{r}}}\right\}

be a basis of dimensions for
\Phi
with associated minimal exponents

1,K_{ij}
such that
\mathsf{D}_{i}=\prod_{j=1}^{r}\mathsf{E}_{j}^{K_{ij}}

for
i=0,\ldots,n-r
. If
\Phi
has a scaling-covariant scalar representation,
then there exists a quantity function of
n-r
arguments

\Psi:\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[1_{Q}}}\right]\times\cdots\times\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[1_{Q}}}\right]\rightarrow\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[1_{Q}}}\right]

such that if

are non-zero then

(3.1)

\pi_{0}=\Psi\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\pi_{1},\ldots,\pi_{n-r}}}\right),

where
\pi_{i}=y_{i}\prod_{j=1}^{r}\nolimits x_{j}^{-K_{ij}}
for

i=0,\ldots,n-r
, or equivalently

(3.2)

\Phi\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(x_{1},\ldots,x_{r}}}\right)\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(y_{1},\ldots,y_{n-r}}}\right)=y_{0}=\prod_{j=1}^{r}\nolimits\!x_{j}^{K_{0j}}\,\Psi\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\pi_{1},\ldots,\pi_{n-r}}}\right).

Proof.

Fix
X=\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\{x_{1},\ldots,x_{r}}}\right\}
such that
X\simeq\mathsf{E}
.

Any
E=\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\{e_{1},\ldots,e_{r}}}\right\}
such that
E\simeq\mathsf{E}

is a basis for
\Phi
, and
x_{i}\in\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[x_{i}}}\right]=\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[e_{i}}}\right]

so by Lemma
2.2
there are unique expansions relative to

E
,

(3.3)

x_{i}=\mu_{E}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(x_{i}}}\right)\cdot e_{i}\qquad\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(i=1,\ldots,r}}\right),\qquad\qquad y_{i}=\mu_{E}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(y_{i}}}\right)\cdot\prod_{j=1}^{r}\nolimits e_{j}^{K_{ij}}\qquad\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(i=0,\ldots,n-r}}\right).

Set

(3.4)

\breve{y}{}_{i}=\prod_{j=1}^{r}\nolimits x_{j}^{K_{ij}}\hskip 18.49988pt\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(i=0,\ldots,n-r}}\right)

so that
\breve{y}{}_{i}\neq 0_{\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[\breve{y}{}_{i}}}\right]}
since

x_{j}\neq 0_{\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[x_{j}}}\right]}
for
j=1,\ldots r
. Then

\pi_{i}=y_{i}\breve{y}_{i}^{-1}
by definition, and (
3.3
)
and (
3.4
) gives

\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[y_{i}}}\right]=\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[\mu_{E}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(y_{i}}}\right)\cdot\prod_{j=1}^{r}\nolimits e_{j}^{K_{ij}}}}\right]=\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[\prod_{j=1}^{r}\nolimits e_{j}^{K_{ij}}}}\right]=\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[\prod_{j=1}^{r}\nolimits x_{j}^{K_{ij}}}}\right]=\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[\breve{y}{}_{i}}}\right]

since
\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[x_{j}}}\right]=\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[e_{j}}}\right]
, so
\pi_{i}\in\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[y_{i}\breve{y}_{i}^{-1}}}\right]=\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[y_{i}}}\right]\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[\breve{y}_{i}}}\right]^{-1}=\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[1_{Q}}}\right]
,
so
\mu_{E}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\pi_{i}}}\right)
does not depend on
E
.

Let
\mathbf{q}
denote the sequence of quantities
\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(x_{1},\ldots,x_{r}}}\right)\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(y_{1},\ldots,y_{n-r}}}\right)
,
and let
\mu_{E}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\mathbf{q}}}\right)
be the sequence of scalars

\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\mu_{E}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(x_{1}}}\right),\ldots,\mu_{E}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(x_{r}}}\right)}}\right)\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\mu_{E}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(y_{1}}}\right),\ldots,\mu_{E}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(y_{n-r}}}\right)}}\right)
.
By definition,
y_{0}=\Phi\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\mathbf{q}}}\right)
and by assumption
there is a function
\phi:\mathbb{K}^{n}\rightarrow\mathbb{K}
such
that
\mu_{E}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\Phi\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\mathbf{q}}}\right)}}\right)=\phi\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\mu_{E}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\mathbf{q}}}\right)}}\right)

for any
\mathbf{q}
and
E\simeq\mathsf{E}
. Also,
\mu_{E}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\breve{y}_{0}}}\right)\neq 0

since
\breve{y}{}_{0}\neq 0_{\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[\breve{y}{}_{0}}}\right]}
, and

\mu_{E}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\prod_{j=1}^{r}x_{j}^{K_{0j}}}}\right)=\prod_{j=1}^{r}\mu_{E}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(x_{j}}}\right)^{K_{0j}}
.
There is thus a function
\varphi:\mathbb{K}^{n}\rightarrow\mathbb{K}

such that for any
\mathbf{q}
and
E\simeq\mathsf{E}
we have

\displaystyle\mu_{E}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\pi_{0}}}\right)=\mu_{E}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(y_{0}\breve{y}_{0}^{-1}}}\right)=\frac{\mu_{E}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\Phi\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\mathbf{q}}}\right)}}\right)}{\mu_{E}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\breve{y}_{0}}}\right)}=\frac{\phi\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\mu_{E}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\mathbf{q}}}\right)}}\right)}{\prod_{j=1}^{r}\mu_{E}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(x_{j}}}\right)^{K_{0j}}}=\varphi\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\mu_{E}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\mathbf{q}}}\right)}}\right).

Furthermore, as
\mu_{E}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\pi_{i}}}\right)=\mu_{E}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(y_{i}}}\right)/\prod_{j=1}^{r}\mu_{E}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(x_{j}}}\right)^{K_{ij}}

for

there is a function

\displaystyle\omega:\mu_{E}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\mathbf{q}}}\right)\>\mapsto\>\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\mu_{E}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(x_{1}}}\right),\ldots,\mu_{E}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(x_{r}}}\right)}}\right)\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\mu_{E}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\pi_{1}}}\right),\ldots,\mu_{E}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\pi_{n-r}}}\right)}}\right),

and
\omega
is bijective since conversely
\mu_{E}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(y_{i}}}\right)=\mu_{E}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\pi_{i}}}\right)\prod_{j=1}^{r}\mu_{E}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(x_{j}}}\right)^{K_{ij}}

for

. Hence, there is a function
\Gamma=\varphi\circ\omega^{-1}:\mathbb{K}^{n}\rightarrow\mathbb{K}

such that

\mu_{E}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\pi_{0}}}\right)=\varphi\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\mu_{E}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\mathbf{q}}}\right)}}\right)=\Gamma\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\mu_{E}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(x_{1}}}\right),\ldots,\mu_{E}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(x_{r}}}\right)}}\right)\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\mu_{E}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\pi_{1}}}\right),\ldots,\mu_{E}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\pi_{n-r}}}\right)}}\right).

Note that we can set
E=X
since
X\simeq\mathsf{E}
. There is thus
a function
\psi:\mathbb{K}^{n-r}\rightarrow\mathbb{K}
such that

(3.5)

\displaystyle\mu_{X}\!\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\pi_{0}}}\right)=\Gamma\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(1,\ldots,1}}\right)\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\mu_{X}\!\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\pi_{1}}}\right),\ldots,\mu_{X}\!\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\pi_{n-r}}}\right)}}\right)=\psi\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\mu_{X}\!\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\pi_{1}}}\right),\ldots,\mu_{X}\!\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\pi_{n-r}}}\right)}}\right)

since
x_{j}=1\cdot x_{j}
so that
\mu_{X}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(x_{j}}}\right)=1

for
j=1,\ldots,r
.

To complete the proof, we convert the scalar function
\psi
, obtained
from the quantity function
\Phi
, back into a quantity function

\Psi
. As
\mu_{E}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\pi_{i}}}\right)
does not depend on

,
we can define a quantity function of
n-r
arguments

\Psi:\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[1_{Q}}}\right]\times\cdots\times\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[1_{Q}}}\right]\rightarrow\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[1_{Q}}}\right],

which depends only on
\psi
, by setting

\Psi\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\mu_{X}\!\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\pi_{1}}}\right)\cdot 1_{Q},\ldots,\mu_{X}\!\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\pi_{n-r}}}\right)\cdot 1_{Q}}}\right)=\psi\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\mu_{X}\!\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\pi_{1}}}\right),\ldots,\mu_{X}\!\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\pi_{n-r}}}\right)}}\right)\cdot 1_{Q},

so that, by (
3.5
),

(3.6)

\mu_{X}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\pi_{0}}}\right)\cdot 1_{Q}=\Psi\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\mu_{X}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\pi_{1}}}\right)\cdot 1_{Q},\ldots,\mu_{X}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\pi_{n-r}}}\right)\cdot 1_{Q}}}\right).

Recall that
\pi_{i}=\mu_{X}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\pi_{i}}}\right)\cdot 1_{Q}
is the
unique expansion of

relative to

,
so we can rewrite (

We have thus derived (
3.1
), or equivalently (
3.2
),
proving the theorem.
∎

As
\breve{y}{}_{0}\neq 0_{\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[\breve{y}{}_{0}}}\right]}
,

\text{$\Phi\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(x_{1},\ldots,x_{r}}}\right)\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(y_{1},\ldots,y_{n-r}}}\right)$}=\breve{y}_{0}\Psi\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\pi{}_{1},\ldots,\pi{}_{n-r}}}\right)=\breve{y}_{0}\Psi^{\prime}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\pi{}_{1},\ldots,\pi{}_{n-r}}}\right)

implies

, and
in view of Theorem

\Phi\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(q_{1},\ldots,q_{n}}}\right)=0_{\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[y_{0}}}\right]}
if and
only if
\Psi\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\pi{}_{1},\ldots,\pi{}_{n-r}}}\right)=0_{\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[1_{Q}}}\right]}
,
as in Buckingham’s original
\pi
theorem
[

]
.

Lemma 3.2
.

Let
Q
be a quantity space, let

\Phi:\mathsf{E}_{1}\times\cdots\times\mathsf{E}_{r}\times\mathsf{D}_{1}\times\cdots\times\mathsf{D}_{n-r}\rightarrow\mathsf{D_{0}^{\mathfrak{K}}},\qquad\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(x_{1},\ldots,x_{r}}}\right)\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(y_{1},\ldots,y_{n-r}}}\right)\mapsto y_{0}^{\mathfrak{K}}\qquad\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(r,n-r\geq 0}}\right),

where
y_{0}\in\mathsf{D}_{0}
and
\mathfrak{K}>0
, be a precomplete
quantity function on
Q
and let
\mathsf{E}=\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\{\mathsf{E}_{1},\ldots,\mathsf{E}_{r}}}\right\}

be a prebasis of dimensions for
\Phi
with associated minimal exponents

K_{i},K_{ij}

such that
\mathsf{D}_{i}^{K_{i}}=\prod_{j=1}^{r}\mathsf{E}_{j}^{K_{ij}}

for
i=0,\ldots,n-r
. If
\mathfrak{K}=K_{0}
and there exists a
bijection of arguments

\chi:\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(x_{1},\ldots,x_{r}}}\right)\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(y_{1},\ldots,y_{n-r}}}\right)\mapsto\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(x_{1},\ldots,x_{r}}}\right)\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(y_{1}^{K_{1}},\ldots,y_{n-r}^{K_{n-r}}}}\right)\qquad\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(x_{i}\in\mathsf{E}_{i},y_{i}\in\mathsf{D}_{i}}}\right),

then there exists a complete quantity function

\Phi^{*}:\mathsf{E}_{1}\times\cdots\times\mathsf{E}_{r}\times\mathsf{D}_{1}^{K_{1}}\times\cdots\times\mathsf{D}_{n-r}^{K_{n-r}}\rightarrow\mathsf{D}_{0}^{\mathfrak{K}},\qquad\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(x_{1},\ldots,x_{r}}}\right)\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(y_{1}^{K_{1}},\ldots,y_{n-r}^{K_{n-r}}}}\right)\mapsto y_{0}^{\mathfrak{K}},

with
\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\{\mathsf{E}_{1},\ldots,\mathsf{E}_{r}}}\right\}
a basis
of dimensions and such that

(3.7)

\Phi^{*}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(x_{1},\ldots,x_{r}}}\right)\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(y_{1}^{K_{1}},\ldots,y_{n-r}^{K_{n-r}}}}\right)=\Phi\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(x_{1},\ldots,x_{r}}}\right)\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(y_{1},\ldots,y_{n-r}}}\right).

Proof.

Note that if
y_{i}\in\mathsf{D}_{i}
then
\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[y_{i}}}\right]=\mathsf{D}_{i}
,
so

.
If we set
\Phi^{*}=\Phi\circ\chi^{-1}
then (
3.7
) holds,
and
\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\{\mathsf{E}_{1},\ldots,\mathsf{E}_{r}}}\right\}
is a
basis of dimensions for
\Phi^{*}
since
\mathsf{E}_{i}=\prod_{j=1}^{r}\nolimits\!\mathsf{E}_{j}^{\delta_{ij}}

for

,
\mathsf{D}_{i}^{K_{i}}=\prod_{j=1}^{r}\mathsf{E}_{j}^{K_{ij}}

for

, and
\mathsf{D}_{0}^{\mathfrak{K}}=\prod_{j=1}^{r}\mathsf{E}_{j}^{K_{0j}}
.
∎

If
\mathfrak{K}=K_{0}
we say that the choice of

,
or the corresponding equation, is

, and a precomplete

. By first applying Lemma

to

Theorem 3.3

(general representation theorem)
.

Let
Q
be a quantity space, let

\Phi:\mathsf{E}_{1}\times\cdots\times\mathsf{E}_{r}\times\mathsf{D}_{1}\times\cdots\times\mathsf{D}_{n-r}\rightarrow\mathsf{D_{0}^{\mathfrak{K}}},\qquad\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(x_{1},\ldots,x_{r}}}\right)\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(y_{1},\ldots,y_{n-r}}}\right)\mapsto y_{0}^{\mathfrak{K}}\qquad\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(r,n-r\geq 0}}\right),

where
y_{0}\in\mathsf{D}_{0}
and
\mathfrak{K}>0
, be a precomplete
quantity function on
Q
and let
\mathsf{E}=\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\{\mathsf{E}_{1},\ldots,\mathsf{E}_{r}}}\right\}

be a prebasis of dimensions for
\Phi
with associated minimal exponents

K_{i},K_{ij}

such that
\mathsf{D}_{i}^{K_{i}}=\prod_{j=1}^{r}\mathsf{E}_{j}^{K_{ij}}

for
i=0,\ldots,n-r
. If
\Phi
is consistent and portable then
there exists a complete quantity function

\Phi^{*}:\mathsf{E}_{1}\times\cdots\times\mathsf{E}_{r}\times\mathsf{D}_{1}^{K_{1}}\times\cdots\times\mathsf{D}_{n-r}^{K_{n-r}}\rightarrow\mathsf{D}_{0}^{\mathfrak{K}},\qquad\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(x_{1},\ldots,x_{r}}}\right)\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(y_{1}^{K_{1}},\ldots,y_{n-r}^{K_{n-r}}}}\right)\mapsto y_{0}^{\mathfrak{K}},

with
\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\{\mathsf{E}_{1},\ldots,\mathsf{E}_{r}}}\right\}
a basis
of dimensions and such that

(3.8)

\Phi^{*}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(x_{1},\ldots,x_{r}}}\right)\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(y_{1}^{K_{1}},\ldots,y_{n-r}^{K_{n-r}}}}\right)=\Phi\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(x_{1},\ldots,x_{r}}}\right)\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(y_{1},\ldots,y_{n-r}}}\right),

and if
\Phi^{*}

has a scaling-covariant scalar representation,
then there exists a quantity function of
n-r
arguments

\Psi:\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[1_{Q}}}\right]\times\cdots\times\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[1_{Q}}}\right]\rightarrow\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[1_{Q}}}\right]

such that if
x_{1},\ldots,x_{r}
are non-zero then

(3.9)

\displaystyle\pi_{0}=\Psi\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\pi_{1},\ldots,\pi_{n-r}}}\right),

where
\pi_{i}=y_{i}^{K_{i}}\prod_{j=1}^{r}\nolimits x_{j}^{-K_{ij}}

for
i=0,\ldots,n-r
, or equivalently

(3.10)

\Phi\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(x_{1},\ldots,x_{r}}}\right)\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(y_{1},\ldots,y_{n-r}}}\right)=y_{0}^{\mathfrak{K}}=\prod_{j=1}^{r}\nolimits\!x_{j}^{K_{0j}}\,\Psi\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\pi_{1},\ldots,\pi_{n-r}}}\right).

Each of the following conditions guarantees the existence of a bijection

\chi
in Lemma
3.2
:

(1)

n=r
.

(2)

K_{i}=1
for
i=1,\ldots,n-r
.

(3)

The restriction of
\chi
to
\mathsf{D}_{i}
is bijective for
i=1,\ldots,n-r
;
this generalizes (2).

In proofs of scalar versions of the
\pi
theorem it is generally
assumed that
\mu_{E}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(q_{i}}}\right)\geq 0
for all
q_{i}
. If

\mathbb{K}=\mathbb{C}
, we need not assume this to prevent roots
of negative numbers from appearing in equations, but another reason
for assuming that
\mu_{E}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(y_{i}}}\right)\geq 0
is that then we
can let
\chi^{-1}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\mu_{E}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(y_{i}}}\right)^{n}}}\right)
be
the unique non-negative
n
th root of
\mu_{E}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(q_{i}}}\right)^{n}
.
In a version of the
\pi
theorem for an ordered quantity space
\mathfrak{Q}
,
where the order is given by a choice of basis for
\mathfrak{Q}

[
24
]
, one could similarly assume that
y_{i}\geq 0_{\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[y_{i}}}\right]}

for all
y_{i}
. However,
\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(x}}\right)\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(y}}\right)\mapsto\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(x}}\right)\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(y^{2}}}\right)
,
for example, is a bijection also if we require that
y\leq 0_{\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[y}}\right]}
,
so a universal positive sign requirement is too strict and not quite
to the point.

4.
Doing dimensional analysis

Let
\Phi
be a quantity function on
Q
, and consider the corresponding
equation

(4.1)

q_{0}^{\mathfrak{K}}=\Phi\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(q_{1},\ldots,q_{n}}}\right)\qquad\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(n\geq 0}}\right),

where
\mathfrak{K}
is an initially unspecified positive integer,
Recall that the purpose of augmented dimensional analysis is to represent
(
4.1
) by a definite system of equations of the form

q_{0}^{\mathfrak{K}}=\prod_{j=1}^{r}\nolimits x_{j}^{K_{j}}\,\Psi\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\pi_{1},\ldots,\pi_{n-r}}}\right),

where
\pi_{i}=y_{i}^{K_{i}}\prod_{j=1}^{r}x_{j}^{-K_{ij}}
and
r,n-r\geq 0
.
Based on the results in Section
3
, we describe in some
detail below when and how this can be done.

4.1.
Systems of quantity equations

If
\Phi
is precomplete then we may use one or more prebasis reorderings

\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(x_{\theta,1},\ldots,x_{\theta,r}}}\right)\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(y_{\theta,1},\ldots.y_{\theta,n-r}}}\right)

of
\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(q_{1},\ldots,q_{n}}}\right)
such that
\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\{\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[x_{\theta,1}}}\right],\ldots,\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[x_{\theta,r}}}\right]}}\right\}

is a prebasis of dimensions for
\Phi
and
E_{\theta}=\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\{x_{\theta,1},\ldots,x_{\theta,r}}}\right\}

a prebasis for
\Phi
whenever all
x_{\theta,j}
are non-zero quantities.
Thus, we obtain
\Theta
equations

(4.2)

\begin{cases}y_{0}^{\mathfrak{K_{\theta}}}=\Phi_{\theta}^{\prime}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(x_{\theta,1},\ldots,x_{\theta,r}}}\right)\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(y_{\theta,1},\ldots,y_{\theta,n-r}}}\right)&\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\theta=1,\ldots,\Theta}}\right),\end{cases}

where
y_{0}=q_{0}
and
\Phi_{\theta}^{\prime}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(x_{\theta,1},\ldots,x_{\theta,r}}}\right)\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(y_{\theta,1},\ldots,y_{\theta,n-r}}}\right)=\Phi\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(q_{1},\ldots,q_{n}}}\right)

for all
\theta
. For each
E_{\theta}
we have associated minimal
exponents
K_{\theta,i},K_{\theta,ij}
such that

(4.3)

\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[y_{\theta,i}}}\right]^{K_{\theta,i}}=\prod_{j=1}^{r}\nolimits\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[x_{\theta,j}}}\right]^{K_{\theta,ij}}\quad\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(i=0,\ldots,n-r}}\right).

We obtain
\Theta
consistent equations of the form (
4.2
)
by setting
\mathfrak{K}_{\theta}=K_{\theta,0}
for all
\theta
.
If each
\Phi_{\theta}^{\prime}
is portable as well, meaning that there
are bijections

\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(x_{\theta,1},\ldots,x_{\theta,r}}}\right)\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(y_{\theta,1},\ldots,y_{\theta,n-r}}}\right)\mapsto\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(x_{\theta,1},\ldots,x_{\theta,r}}}\right)\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(y_{\theta,1}^{K_{\theta,1}},\ldots,y_{\theta,n-r}^{K_{\theta,n-r}}}}\right)\quad\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\theta=1,\ldots,\Theta}}\right),

then there are
\Theta
equations with complete functions
\Phi_{\theta}^{*}
,

(4.4)

\begin{cases}y_{0}^{\mathfrak{K_{\theta}}}=\Phi_{\theta}^{*}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(x_{\theta,1},\ldots,x_{\theta,r}}}\right)\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(y_{\theta,1}^{K_{\theta,1}},\ldots,y_{\theta,n-r}^{K_{\theta,n-r}}}}\right)&\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\theta=1,\ldots,\Theta}}\right),\end{cases}

where
\Phi_{\theta}^{*}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(x_{\theta,1},\ldots,x_{\theta,r}}}\right)\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(y_{\theta,1}^{K_{\theta,1}},\ldots,y_{\theta,n-r}^{K_{\theta,n-r}}}}\right)=\Phi_{\theta}^{\prime}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(x_{\theta,1},\ldots,x_{\theta,r}}}\right)\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(y_{\theta,1},\ldots,y_{\theta,n-r}}}\right)

for all
\theta
.

If each
\Phi_{\theta}^{*}
has a scaling-covariant scalar representation
then we obtain
\Theta
equations

(4.5)

\begin{cases}y_{0}^{\mathfrak{K}_{\theta}}=\prod_{j=1}^{r}x_{\theta,j}^{K_{\theta,0j}}\,\Psi_{\theta}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\pi_{\theta,1},\ldots,\pi_{\theta,n-r}}}\right)&\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\theta=1,\ldots,\Theta}}\right),\end{cases}

where
\prod_{j=1}^{r}x_{\theta,j}^{K_{\theta,0j}}\,\Psi_{\theta}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\pi_{\theta,1},\ldots,\pi_{\theta,n-r}}}\right)=\Phi_{\theta}^{*}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(x_{\theta,1},\ldots,x_{\theta,r}}}\right)\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(y_{\theta,1}^{K_{\theta,1}},\ldots,y_{\theta,n-r}^{K_{\theta,n-r}}}}\right)

for all
\theta
.

Finally, since
\Psi_{\theta}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\pi_{\theta,1},\ldots,\pi_{\theta,n-r}}}\right)\in\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[1_{Q}}}\right]

for all
\theta
we obtain from (
4.5
)
\Theta
equations

(4.6)

\begin{cases}y_{0}^{\mathfrak{K}}=y_{0}^{C_{\theta}\mathfrak{K}_{\theta}}=\prod_{j=1}^{r}\nolimits x_{\theta,j}^{C_{\theta}K_{\theta,0j}}\,\Psi_{\theta}^{C_{\theta}}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\pi_{\theta,1},\ldots,\pi_{\theta,n-r}}}\right)&\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\theta=1,\ldots,\Theta}}\right),\end{cases}

where
C_{\theta}=\mathrm{lcm}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\mathfrak{K}_{1},\ldots,\mathfrak{K}_{\Theta}}}\right)/\mathfrak{K}{}_{\theta}
,

\mathfrak{K}=\mathrm{lcm}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\mathfrak{K}_{1},\ldots,\mathfrak{K}_{\Theta}}}\right)

and
\Psi_{\theta}^{C_{\theta}}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\pi_{\theta,1},\ldots,\pi_{\theta,n-r}}}\right)\in\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[1_{Q}}}\right]
.

Note that we can derive (
4.5
) and (
4.6
) from
(
4.1
) by the method described here only if
\Phi
is precomplete,
all
\Phi_{\theta}^{\prime}
are portable and all
\Phi_{\theta}^{*}
have
scaling-covariant scalar representations, although we can obtain an
incomplete result if
\Phi
is precomplete and there is at least
one
\theta
such that
\Phi_{\theta}^{\prime}
is portable and
\Phi_{\theta}^{*}

has a scaling-covariant scalar representation.

4.2.
Starting from a dimensional matrix

The minimal exponents
K_{\theta,i},K_{\theta,ij}
required in (
4.3
)
depend on the structure of the subgroup
\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(Q/{\sim}}}\right)^{\prime}

of
Q/{\sim}
generated by
\mathsf{C}_{0},\ldots,\mathsf{C}_{n}
.
As described below, it is usually most convenient to derive the structure
of
\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(Q/{\sim}}}\right)^{\prime}
, and indirectly the minimal exponents,
from the structure of
Q/{\sim}
by choosing a basis for
Q/{\sim}
.

Let
\Phi:\mathsf{C}_{1}\times\cdots\times\mathsf{C}_{n}\rightarrow\mathsf{C_{0}^{\mathfrak{K}}}

be a precomplete quantity function on
Q
, and fix a basis
\mathsf{E}=\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\{\mathsf{E}_{1},\ldots,\mathsf{E}_{m}}}\right\}

for
Q/{\sim}
. Each
\mathsf{C}_{i}
is associated with a unique
column vector
\varepsilon_{i}=\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\varepsilon{}_{i1},\ldots,\varepsilon_{im}}}\right)^{\!\mathrm{T}}

of integer exponents
\varepsilon_{i\ell}
such that
\mathsf{C}_{i}=\prod_{\ell=1}^{m}\mathsf{E}_{\ell}^{\varepsilon_{i\ell}}
.
The
dimensional matrix
for
Q
and
\Phi
relative to
\mathsf{E}

is

(4.7)

\begin{array}[]{ccccccc}&\mathsf{C}_{0}&\mathsf{C}_{1}&\cdots&\mathsf{C}_{i}&\cdots&\mathsf{C}_{n}\\
\mathsf{E}_{1}&\varepsilon_{01}&\varepsilon_{11}&\cdots&\varepsilon_{i1}&\cdots&\varepsilon_{n1}\\
\vdots&\vdots&\vdots&&\vdots&&\vdots\\
\mathsf{E}_{m}&\varepsilon_{0m}&\varepsilon_{1m}&\cdots&\varepsilon_{im}&\cdots&\varepsilon_{nm}\end{array}.

For
j=1,\ldots,s
, let
\overline{\varepsilon}_{j}=\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\overline{\varepsilon}{}_{j1},\ldots,\overline{\varepsilon}{}_{jm}}}\right)^{\!\mathrm{T}}

be the column vector associated with
\mathsf{\overline{C}}_{j}\in\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\{\mathsf{C}_{0},\ldots,\mathsf{C}_{n}}}\right\}
.
It is clear from the construction of the dimensional matrix that each

\overline{\mathsf{C}}_{j}
is represented by
\overline{\varepsilon}_{j}

relative to
\mathsf{E}
in such a way that
\prod_{j=1}^{s}\mathsf{\overline{C}}_{j}^{k_{j}}=\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[1_{Q}}}\right]

if and only if
\sum_{j=1}^{s}k_{j}\overline{\varepsilon}_{j}=\boldsymbol{0}^{m}
,
where
\boldsymbol{0}^{m}
is a column vector with
m
zeros. In
other words,
k_{1},\ldots,k_{s}
satisfy the system of equations

(4.8)

\begin{cases}\sum_{j=1}^{s}\nolimits\overline{\varepsilon}{}_{j\ell}k_{j}=0&\qquad\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\ell=1,\ldots,m}}\right)\end{cases}

Hence, independence of dimensions is equivalent to linear independence
of columns in an associated dimensional matrix, so the rank of the
matrix is equal to the rank of the corresponding
\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(Q/{\sim}}}\right)^{\prime}

and to the number of elements in any prebasis of dimensions for
\Phi

by Lemma
2.1
.

Considering the functions
\Phi_{\theta}^{\prime}:\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(x_{\theta,1},\ldots,x_{\theta,r}}}\right)\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(y_{\theta,1},\ldots,y_{\theta,n-r}}}\right)\mapsto y_{0}^{\mathfrak{K_{\theta}}}

in (
4.2
) it also follows from the equivalence of
\prod_{j=1}^{s}\mathsf{\overline{C}}_{j}^{k_{j}}=\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[1_{Q}}}\right]

and equations (
4.8
) that, for
\theta=1,\ldots,\Theta
,
the sequences of minimal exponents
K_{\theta,i},K_{\theta,i1},\ldots,K_{\theta,ir}

in (
4.3
) are obtained from the sets of sequences of integers

k_{\theta,i},k_{\theta,i1},\ldots,k_{\theta,ir}
that satisfy corresponding
systems of equations,

(4.9)

\begin{cases}\eta_{0\ell}k_{\theta,0}=\sum_{j=1}^{r}\nolimits\xi{}_{\theta,j\ell}k_{\theta,0j}&\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\ell=1,\ldots,m}}\right),\end{cases}

(4.10)

\begin{cases}\eta{}_{\theta,i\ell}k_{\theta,i}=\sum_{j=1}^{r}\nolimits\xi{}_{\theta,j\ell}k_{\theta,ij}&\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\ell=1,\ldots,m}}\right)\qquad\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(i=1,\ldots,n-r}}\right),\end{cases}

where
\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\eta_{01},\ldots,\eta_{0m}}}\right)
,
\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\eta{}_{\theta,i1},\ldots,\eta_{\theta,im}}}\right)

and
\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\xi_{\theta,j1},\ldots,\xi_{\theta,jm}}}\right)
are the

m
-tuples of exponents associated with
\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[y_{0}}}\right]
,
\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[y_{\theta,i}}}\right]

and
\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[x_{\theta,j}}}\right]
, respectively, relative to
\mathsf{E}
.

Example 4.1
.

Consider the equation
q_{0}^{\mathfrak{K}}=\Phi\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(q_{1},q_{2},q_{3}}}\right)

and the corresponding dimensional matrices

\begin{array}[]{ccccc}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(A}}\right)&\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[q_{0}}}\right]&\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[q_{1}}}\right]&\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[q_{2}}}\right]&\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[q_{3}}}\right]\\
\mathsf{E}_{1}&4&1&2&1\\
\mathsf{E}_{2}&2&0&0&1\end{array},

\begin{array}[]{ccccc}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(B}}\right)&\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[y_{0}}}\right]&\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[x_{1,1}}}\right]&\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[x_{1,2}}}\right]&\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[y_{1,1}}}\right]\\
\mathsf{E}_{1}&4&1&1&2\\
\mathsf{E}_{2}&2&0&1&0\end{array},\qquad\begin{array}[]{ccccc}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(C}}\right)&\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[y_{0}}}\right]&\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[x_{2,1}}}\right]&\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[x_{2,2}}}\right]&\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[y_{2,1}}}\right]\\
\mathsf{E}_{1}&4&2&1&1\\
\mathsf{E}_{2}&2&0&1&0\end{array},

where
\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(B}}\right)
and
\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(C}}\right)
are obtained from
\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(A}}\right)

by the prebasis reorderings
\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(q_{1},q_{2},q_{3}}}\right)\mapsto\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(q_{1},q_{3},q_{2}}}\right)=\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(x_{1,1},x_{1,2}}}\right)\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(y_{1,1}}}\right)

and
\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(q_{1},q_{2},q_{3}}}\right)\mapsto\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(q_{2},q_{3},q_{1}}}\right)=\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(x_{2,1},x_{2,2}}}\right)\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(y_{2,1}}}\right)
,
respectively. From each one of
\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(B}}\right)
and
\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(C}}\right)
,
we obtain two systems of two equations each,

\begin{array}[]{cc}\begin{cases}4k_{1,0}=1k_{1,01}+1k_{1,02}\\
2k_{1,0}=0k_{1,01}+1k_{1,02}\end{cases}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(B_{0}}}\right),&\begin{cases}2k_{1,1}=1k_{1,11}+1k_{1,12}\\
0k_{1,1}=0k_{1,11}+1k_{1,12}\end{cases}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(B_{1}}}\right),\end{array}

\begin{array}[]{cc}\begin{cases}4k_{2,0}=2k_{2,01}+1k_{2,02}\\
2k_{2,0}=0k_{2,01}+1k_{2,02}\end{cases}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(C_{0}}}\right),&\begin{cases}1k_{2,1}=2k_{2,11}+1k_{2,12}\\
0k_{2,1}=0k_{2,11}+1k_{2,12}\end{cases}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(C_{1}}}\right).\end{array}

The minimal exponents in (
4.3
), obtained by solving these
systems of equations, are

\displaystyle\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(K_{1,0},K_{1,01},K_{1,02}}}\right)=\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(1,2,2}}\right)\quad\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(B_{0}}}\right),\qquad\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(K_{1,1},K_{1,11},K_{1,12}}}\right)=\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(1,2,0}}\right)\quad\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(B_{1}}}\right),

\displaystyle\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(K_{2,0},K_{2,01},K_{2,02}}}\right)=\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(1,1,2}}\right)\quad\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(C_{0}}}\right),\qquad\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(K_{2,1},K_{2,11},K_{2,12}}}\right)=\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(2,1,0}}\right)\quad\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(C_{1}}}\right).

Setting
\mathfrak{K}=K_{1,0}=K_{2,0}=1
,
q_{0}^{\mathfrak{K}}=\Phi\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(q_{1},q_{2},q_{3}}}\right)

thus has the representations
y_{0}=x_{1,1}^{2}x_{1,2}^{2}\,\Psi_{1}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(y_{1,1}/x_{1,1}^{2}}}\right)

and
y_{0}=x_{2,1}x_{2,2}^{2}\,\Psi_{2}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(y_{2,1}^{2}/x_{2,1}}}\right)
,
or alternatively
q_{0}=q_{1}^{2}q_{3}^{2}\,\Psi_{1}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(q_{2}/q_{1}^{2}}}\right)

and
q_{0}=q_{2}q_{3}^{2}\,\Psi_{2}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(q_{1}^{2}/q_{2}}}\right)
,
provided that the required scaling-covariant scalar representations
exist and
\Phi_{2}^{\prime}
is portable although

.

Remark 4.2
.

It is known from the practice of dimensional analysis that a change
of the basis
\mathsf{E}
for a dimensional matrix may lead to a
change of the minimal exponents associated with its variables. This
can only happen, however, if the change of basis is tied to a change
of quantity space. The conceptual reason for this is that a change
of basis for
Q/{\sim}
, where
Q
is a fixed quantity space, does
not affect the dependencies in
Q/{\sim}
or its subgroup
\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(Q/{\sim}}}\right)^{\prime}
.

Example 4.3
.

Consider a (right, circular) cone. The slant height
H

of the cone (that is, the length of the line segment from the periphery
of its base to its apex) is determined by the area of its base
a

and its height
h
,

H^{\mathfrak{K}}=\Phi\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(a,h}}\right).

The dimensional matrix for
\Phi
is simply

\begin{array}[]{cccc}&\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[H}}\right]&\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[a}}\right]&\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[h}}\right]\\
\mathsf{L}&1&2&1\end{array}.

\Phi
is precomplete and has prebases of dimensions
\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\{\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[a}}\right]}}\right\}

and
\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\{\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[h}}\right]}}\right\}
, so the system of equations
of the form (
4.2
) is

\begin{cases}H^{\mathfrak{K}_{1}}=\Phi_{1}^{\prime}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(a}}\right)\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(h}}\right)&\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\mathrm{for}\;\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\{\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[a}}\right]}}\right\}}}\right),\\
H^{\mathfrak{K}_{2}}=\Phi_{2}^{\prime}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(h}}\right)\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(a}}\right)&\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\mathrm{for}\;\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\{\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[h}}\right]}}\right\}}}\right),\end{cases}

and the minimal exponents in (
4.3
) are
\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(K_{1,0},K_{1,01}}}\right)=\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(2,1}}\right)
,

\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(K_{1,1},K_{1,11}}}\right)=\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(2,1}}\right)
,
\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(K_{2,0},K_{2,01}}}\right)=\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(1,1}}\right)

and
\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(K_{2,1},K_{2,11}}}\right)=\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(1,2}}\right)
. Thus, setting

\mathfrak{K}_{1}=2
and
\mathfrak{K}_{2}=1
both equations become
consistent, and
\Phi_{2}
is portable because
K_{2,1}=1
, so if

\text{$h>0_{\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[h}}\right]}$ so that }\Phi_{1}^{\prime}
is portable
as well then

\begin{cases}H^{2}=\Phi_{1}^{*}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(a}}\right)\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(h^{2}}}\right),\\
H=\Phi_{2}^{*}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(h}}\right)\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(a}}\right).\end{cases}

Hence, if
\Phi_{1}^{*}
and
\Phi_{2}^{*}
have scaling-covariant
scalar representations then we obtain

\begin{cases}H^{2}=a\Psi_{1}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(h^{2}/a}}\right),\\
H=h\Psi_{2}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(a/h^{2}}}\right),\end{cases}

and thus

\begin{cases}H^{2}=a\Psi_{1}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(h^{2}/a}}\right),&\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(1}}\right)\\
H^{2}=h^{2}\Psi_{2}^{2}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(a/h^{2}}}\right),&\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(2}}\right)\end{cases}

where
\Psi_{2}^{2}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(a/h^{2}}}\right)=\Psi_{2}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(a/h^{2}}}\right)^{2}
.
Note that
a=\pi r^{2}
, where
r
is the radius of the circular
periphery of the base, so
\Phi\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(a,h}}\right)=H^{2}=r^{2}+h^{2}=a/\pi+h^{2}
.
The same function is obtained by substituting
\Psi_{1}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(x}}\right)=1_{Q}/\pi+x

in (1) or
\Psi_{2}^{2}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(x}}\right)=1_{Q}+x/\pi
in (2).

5.
Applications of dimensional analysis

We start with two theoretically interesting examples of dimensional
analysis without dimensional matrices, and then move on to a little
more familiar applications with dimensional matrices as inputs. Only
quantity spaces over
\mathbb{R}
will be considered in the examples
below.

Example 5.1
.

By convention,
\prod_{j=1}^{0}e_{j}^{k_{j}}=1_{Q}
and
\prod_{j=1}^{0}\mathsf{C}_{i}^{k_{j}}=\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[1_{Q}}}\right]
.
In particular,
\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[1_{Q}}}\right]=\prod_{j=1}^{0}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[1_{Q}}}\right]^{k_{j}}
,
so
\emptyset
is (vacuously) the unique prebasis of dimensions for
any quantity function of the form

\Phi:\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[1_{Q}}}\right]\times\cdots\times\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[1_{Q}}}\right]\rightarrow\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[1_{Q}}}\right],\qquad\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(q_{1},\ldots,q_{n}}}\right)\mapsto q_{0}.

Thus,
\Theta=1
and the sequences of minimal exponents
\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(K_{1,0},K_{1,0j}}}\right),\ldots,\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(K_{1,n},K_{1,nj}}}\right)

associated with
\emptyset
are all just the sequence
\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(1}}\right)
,
so
\Phi
is complete with basis of dimensions
\emptyset
, and
we can apply Theorem
3.1
directly. The unique expansion
of
\Phi\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(q_{1},\ldots,q_{n}}}\right)
relative to
\emptyset

is
\Phi\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(q_{1},\ldots,q_{n}}}\right)=\mu_{\emptyset}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\Phi\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(q_{1},\ldots,q_{n}}}\right)}}\right)\cdot 1_{Q},

so
\phi
defined by

\phi\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\mu_{\emptyset}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(q_{1}}}\right),\ldots,\mu_{\emptyset}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(q_{n}}}\right)}}\right)\cdot 1_{Q}=\Phi\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(q_{1},\ldots,q_{n}}}\right)

is a covariant scalar representation of
\Phi
since
\phi\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\mu_{\emptyset}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(q_{1}}}\right),\ldots,\mu_{\emptyset}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(q_{n}}}\right)}}\right)\cdot 1_{Q}=\mu_{\emptyset}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\Phi\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(q_{1},\ldots,q_{n}}}\right)}}\right)\cdot 1_{Q}

and
1_{Q}
is a unit quantity for
\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[1_{Q}}}\right]
. Hence,

q_{0}=1_{Q}\Psi\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(q_{1}/1_{Q},\ldots,q_{n}/1_{Q}}}\right)=\Psi\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(q_{1},\ldots,q_{n}}}\right),

so
\Phi=1_{Q}\Psi=\Psi
. We conclude that it is possible but pointless
to use dimensional analysis if all quantity variables range over
\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[1_{Q}}}\right]
.

Example 5.2
.

Consider a quantity function
\Phi
of the form

(5.1)

\Phi:\mathsf{C}_{1}\rightarrow\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[1_{Q}}}\right],\qquad q_{1}\mapsto q_{0},

where
\mathsf{C}_{1}\neq\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[1_{Q}}}\right]
, As
\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[1_{Q}}}\right]^{1}=\mathsf{C}_{1}^{0}
,

\Phi
is complete with basis of dimensions
\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\{\mathsf{C}_{1}}}\right\}
,
and we can apply Theorem
3.1
directly.

Hence, if
\Phi
has a scaling-covariant scalar representation then

q_{0}=q_{1}^{0}\Psi\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(}}\right)=1_{Q}\Psi\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(}}\right)=k\in\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[1_{Q}}}\right],

so
\Phi
is a constant function. Had
\Phi
not admitted a scaling-covariant
scalar representation then nothing could have been said about how

\Phi\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(q_{1}}}\right)
depends on
q_{1}
, so we obtain specific
information about
\Phi
from this assumption. A non-constant function
of the form (
5.1
), complete but without a scaling-covariant
scalar representation, was defined in Example
2.3
.

In the examples below, we assume that every
\Phi_{i}^{*}
has a
scaling-covariant scalar representation, meaning that every
\Phi_{i}^{*}

is “physically meaningful”.

Example 5.3
.

Assume that the period of oscillation
t
of a pendulum
is determined by its length
\ell
, the mass of the bob
m
, the
amplitude of the oscillation
\theta
(an angle) and the constant
of gravity
g
, that is,

t^{\mathfrak{K}}=\Phi\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\ell,m,\theta,g}}\right).

The dependencies among the corresponding dimensions are given by the
dimensional matrix

\begin{array}[]{cccccc}&\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[t}}\right]&\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[\ell}}\right]&\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[m}}\right]&\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[\theta}}\right]&\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[g}}\right]\\
\mathsf{L}&0&1&0&0&1\\
\mathsf{T}&1&0&0&0&-2\\
\mathsf{M}&0&0&1&0&0\end{array}.

The unique prebasis of dimensions for
\Phi
is
\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\{\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[\ell}}\right],\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[m}}\right],\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[g}}\right]}}\right\}
,
and from the minimal exponents calculated as in Example
4.1

we obtain

\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[t}}\right]^{2}=\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[\ell}}\right]\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[m}}\right]^{0}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[g}}\right]^{-1},\quad\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[\theta}}\right]=\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[\ell}}\right]^{0}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[m}}\right]^{0}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[g}}\right]^{0}.

Thus,

t^{2}=\Phi^{\prime}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\ell,m,g}}\right)\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\theta}}\right)

is a consistent equation with a portable function, so we also have

t^{2}=\Phi^{*}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\ell,m,g}}\right)\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\theta}}\right),

and hence

(5.2)

t^{2}=\ell m^{0}g^{-1}\Psi\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\theta/\ell^{0}m^{0}g^{0}}}\right)=\ell g^{-1}\Psi\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\theta}}\right).

Consider a covariant scalar representation
\phi
of
\Phi
and
let
\boldsymbol{t}
,
\boldsymbol{\ell}
,
\boldsymbol{m}
,
\boldsymbol{g}
,

\boldsymbol{g}^{-1}
and
\boldsymbol{\theta}
denote the scalars

\mu_{E}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(t}}\right)
,
\mu_{E}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\ell}}\right)
,
\mu_{E}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(m}}\right)
,

\mu_{E}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(g}}\right)
,
\mu_{E}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(g^{-1}}}\right)
and
\mu_{E}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\theta}}\right)
,
respectively. Then
\boldsymbol{t}^{2}=\boldsymbol{\ell}\boldsymbol{g}^{-1}\psi\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\boldsymbol{\theta}}}\right)
,
where
\psi
is the covariant scalar representation of
\Psi:\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[1_{Q}}}\right]\rightarrow\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[1_{Q}}}\right]

(recall Example
5.1
). If
\boldsymbol{\ell},\boldsymbol{g}>0

so that
\boldsymbol{\ell g}^{-1}>0
then
\psi\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\boldsymbol{\theta}}}\right)\geq 0

since
\boldsymbol{t}^{2}\geq 0
, so
\boldsymbol{t}=\sqrt{\boldsymbol{\ell}\boldsymbol{g}^{-1}}\sqrt{\psi\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\boldsymbol{\theta}}}\right)}\geq 0
.
If we require
\phi
to be defined for
\theta=0
and set
\boldsymbol{t}_{0}=\sqrt{\phi\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\boldsymbol{\ell},\boldsymbol{m},0,\boldsymbol{g}}}\right)}=0

then
\sqrt{\psi\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(0}}\right)}=0
since
\sqrt{\phi\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\boldsymbol{\ell},\boldsymbol{m},0,\boldsymbol{g}}}\right)}=\sqrt{\boldsymbol{\ell}\boldsymbol{g}^{-1}}\sqrt{\psi\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(0}}\right)}
,
but it is known that
\sqrt{\psi\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\boldsymbol{\theta}}}\right)}\rightarrow 2\pi

as
\boldsymbol{\theta}\rightarrow 0

[
25
]
, so
\boldsymbol{t}\approx 2\pi\sqrt{\boldsymbol{\ell}\boldsymbol{g}^{-1}}

for small
\theta\neq 0
. We note that the functions appearing in
dimensional analysis need not be continuous.

Remark 5.4
.

If
q\in\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[1_{Q}}}\right]
then we may define
\sqrt[n]{q}
as

\sqrt[n]{\mu_{E}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(q}}\right)}\cdot 1_{Q}
(if
\mu_{E}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(q}}\right)\geq 0)
,

e^{q}
as
e^{\mu_{E}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(q}}\right)}\cdot 1_{Q}
and so on, since

\mu_{E}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(q}}\right)
does not depend on a basis
E
. Conversely,
if
q\notin\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[1_{Q}}}\right]
then
\sqrt[n]{q}\in Q
only if
q=x^{n}

for some
x\in Q
, where
n
is a positive integer, since in a monoid

x^{n}
is defined in terms of repeated multiplication. In Example

5.3
,
\Psi\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\theta}}\right)\in\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[1_{Q}}}\right]
,
\mu_{E}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\Psi\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\theta}}\right)}}\right)=\psi\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\mu_{E}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\theta}}\right)}}\right)\geq 0

and
\ell g^{-1}=\mu_{E}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\ell g^{-1}}}\right)\cdot t^{2}=\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\sqrt{\mu_{E}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\ell g^{-1}}}\right)}\cdot t}}\right)^{2}

by Lemma
2.2
and since
\mu_{E}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\ell}}\right)\mu_{E}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(g^{-1}}}\right)>0
,
so there are indeed quantities
\sqrt{\Psi\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\theta}}\right)},\sqrt{\ell g^{-1}}\in Q
.

Example 5.5
.

Let
\mathfrak{A}
and
\mathfrak{B}
be two bodies of
mass
a
and
b
, respectively, let
c
be the combined mass of

\mathfrak{A}
and
\mathfrak{B}
, and assume that we have

c^{\mathfrak{K}}=\Phi(a,b).

The simple dimensional matrix is

\begin{array}[]{cccc}&\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[c}}\right]&\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[a}}\right]&\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[b}}\right]\\
\mathsf{M}&1&1&1\end{array}.

Thus,
\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\{\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[a}}\right]}}\right\}
and
\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\{\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[b}}\right]}}\right\}

are the two prebases of dimensions for
\Phi
, and from the minimal
exponents calculated from the two corresponding dimensional matrices
we obtain

\begin{cases}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[c}}\right]=\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[a}}\right],\quad\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[b}}\right]=\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[a}}\right]&\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\mathrm{for}\;\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\{\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[a}}\right]}}\right\}}}\right),\\
\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[c}}\right]=\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[b}}\right],\quad\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[a}}\right]=\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[b}}\right]&\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\mathrm{for}\;\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\{\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[b}}\right]}}\right\}}}\right).\end{cases}

Hence,

\begin{cases}c=\Phi_{1}^{\prime}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(a}}\right)\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(b}}\right)\\
c=\Phi_{2}^{\prime}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(b}}\right)\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(a}}\right)\end{cases}

are consistent equations with portable functions, so we have

\begin{cases}c=\Phi_{1}^{*}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(a}}\right)\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(b}}\right)=a\,\Psi_{1}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(b/a}}\right),&(M1)\\
c=\Phi_{2}^{*}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(b}}\right)\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(a}}\right)=b\,\Psi_{2}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(a/b}}\right).&(M2)\end{cases}

Thus, for
a,b\neq 0_{\mathsf{M}}
we have
\Phi\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(a,b}}\right)=a\,\Psi_{1}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(b/a}}\right)

by (
M1
) and
\Phi\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(b,a}}\right)=a\,\Psi_{2}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(b/a}}\right)

by (
M2
), so if we assume by symmetry that
\Phi(a,b)=\Phi(b,a)

then
\Psi_{1}=\Psi_{2}=\Psi
. Hence,
a\,\Psi\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(b/a}}\right)=b\,\Psi\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(a/b}}\right)
,
so setting
x=b/a
we obtain a functional equation of the form
\Psi\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(x}}\right)=F\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(x,\Psi}}\right)
,
namely

\Psi\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(x}}\right)=x\,\Psi\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(x^{-1}}}\right).

This equation has solutions of the form

\Psi(x)=k\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(1_{Q}+x}}\right)\qquad\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(k,x\in\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[1_{Q}}}\right]}}\right),

unique under natural analyticity conditions (see Appendix A), so

c=a\,\Psi\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(b/a}}\right)=ak\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(1_{Q}+b/a}}\right)=k\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(a+b}}\right)\qquad\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(a\neq 0_{\mathsf{M}}}}\right).

If we assume that
\Phi(a,0_{\mathsf{M}})=a
for all non-zero
a\in\mathsf{M}

then
a=a\Psi(0_{\mathsf{\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[1_{Q}}}\right]}})=ak\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(1_{Q}+0_{\mathsf{\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[1_{Q}}}\right]}}}}\right)=ak
,
so
k=1_{Q}
, so we obtain
c=a+b
as one might expect. If also

\Phi(0_{\mathsf{M}},0_{\mathsf{M}})=0_{\mathsf{M}}
then

c=a+b

for all
a,b\in\mathsf{M}
(taking care also of hypothetical negative
masses).

Example 5.6
.

Consider a right triangle with sides
\mathfrak{A}
,

\mathfrak{B}
and
\mathfrak{C}
, where the right angle is the
one between
\mathfrak{A}
and
\mathfrak{B}
. Let
a
,
b
and

c
be the lengths of
\mathfrak{A}
,
\mathfrak{B}
and
\mathfrak{C}
,
respectively, and
A
,
B
and
C
the areas of the squares whose
sides are
\mathfrak{A}
,
\mathfrak{B}
and
\mathfrak{C}
, respectively.

We first want to find a function
\Phi
such that
C=\Phi\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(A,B}}\right)
.
Assuming that
\Phi\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(A,B}}\right)=\Phi\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(B,A}}\right)
and reasoning
as in Example
5.5
, we again derive the functional equation
\Psi\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(x}}\right)=x\,\Psi\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(x^{-1}}}\right)
.
Substituting the analytical solution
\Psi\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(x}}\right)=k\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(1_{Q}+x}}\right)
,
where
x=B/A
, in
\Phi\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(A,B}}\right)=A\Psi\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(B/A}}\right)
we
obtain
\Phi\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(A,B}}\right)=k\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(A+B}}\right)
, and it is clear
that
\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(A+B}}\right)/C\rightarrow 1_{Q}
as
B\rightarrow 0_{\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[B}}\right]}
,
so
k=1_{Q}
. Thus, we have
C=A+B
, Pythagoras’ theorem.

In the same way, we could “prove” that
c=a+b
, but this is obviously
not true. Note, however, that
\Psi\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(x}}\right)=k\sqrt{1_{Q}+x^{2}}
,
where
x=b/a
, is also a solution of
\Psi\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(x}}\right)=x\,\Psi\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(x^{-1}}}\right)
,
albeit not an analytical one. Substituting this solution in
\Phi\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(a,b}}\right)=a\Psi\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(b/a}}\right)

we obtain
\Phi\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(a,b}}\right)=k\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\sqrt{a^{2}+b^{2}}}}\right)
,
and finally
c=\sqrt{a^{2}+b^{2}}
, Pythagoras’ theorem in another
form.

This example shows that a solution
\Psi
of the functional equation
cannot be discarded just because it is non-analytical. Thus, the non-uniqueness
of
\Psi
may be a concern, but it is often obvious which
\Psi

to choose. For example, in the right triangle case we obviously have

c<a+b
. Presumably, in most cases of interest
\Psi
is analytical,
and thus unique, and this will be assumed in the next two examples.

Example 5.7

(based on a problem in Buckingham
[
18
]
, pp. 358–359)
.

It is assumed that the energy density
u
at a certain point is determined
by the strengths
E
and
H
there of an electric field
\boldsymbol{\mathbf{E}}

and a magnetic
\mathbf{H}
-field, respectively, as well as the permittivity

\epsilon
and permeability
\mu
of the medium, that is,

(5.3)

u^{\mathfrak{K}}=\Phi\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(E,H,\epsilon,\mu}}\right).

The corresponding dimensional matrix is

\begin{array}[]{cccccc}&\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[u}}\right]&\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[E}}\right]&\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[H}}\right]&\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[\epsilon}}\right]&\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[\mu}}\right]\\
\mathsf{L}&-1&1&-1&-3&1\\
\mathsf{T}&-2&-3&0&4&-2\\
\mathsf{M}&1&1&0&-1&1\\
\mathsf{I}&0&-1&1&2&-2\end{array}.

It is easy to verify that the dimensional matrix has rank 3 and that
there are four prebases of dimensions for
\Phi
, namely
\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\{\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[E}}\right],\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[\epsilon}}\right],\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[\mu}}\right]}}\right\}
,

\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\{\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[H}}\right],\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[\epsilon}}\right],\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[\mu}}\right]}}\right\}

\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\{\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[E}}\right],\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[H}}\right],\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[\epsilon}}\right]}}\right\}

and
\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\{\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[E}}\right],\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[H}}\right],\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[\mu}}\right]}}\right\}
.
From the minimal exponents we obtain

\begin{cases}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[u}}\right]=\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[E}}\right]^{2}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[\epsilon}}\right],\quad\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[H}}\right]^{2}=\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[E}}\right]^{2}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[\epsilon}}\right]\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[\mu}}\right]^{-1}&\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\mathrm{for}\;\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\{\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[E}}\right],\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[\epsilon}}\right],\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[\mu}}\right]}}\right\}}}\right),\\
\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[u}}\right]=\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[H}}\right]^{2}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[\mu}}\right],\quad\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[E}}\right]^{2}=\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[H}}\right]^{2}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[\epsilon}}\right]^{-1}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[\mu}}\right]&\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\mathrm{for}\;\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\{\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[H}}\right],\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[\epsilon}}\right],\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[\mu}}\right]}}\right\}}}\right),\\
\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[u}}\right]=\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[E}}\right]^{2}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[\epsilon}}\right],\quad\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[\mu}}\right]=\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[E}}\right]^{2}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[H}}\right]^{-2}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[\epsilon}}\right],&\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\mathrm{for}\;\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\{\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[E}}\right],\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[H}}\right],\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[\epsilon}}\right]}}\right\}}}\right),\\
\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[u}}\right]=\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[H}}\right]^{2}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[\mu}}\right],\quad\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[\epsilon}}\right]=\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[H}}\right]^{2}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[\epsilon}}\right]^{-1}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[\mu}}\right],&\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\mathrm{for}\;\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\{\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[E}}\right],\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[H}}\right],\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[\mu}}\right]}}\right\}}}\right),\end{cases}

giving the set of consistent equations,

\begin{cases}u=\Phi_{1}^{\prime}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(E,\epsilon,\mu}}\right)\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(H}}\right),\\
u=\Phi_{2}^{\prime}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(H,\epsilon,\mu}}\right)\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(E}}\right),\\
u=\Phi_{3}^{\prime}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(E,H,\epsilon}}\right)\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\mu}}\right),\\
u=\Phi_{4}^{\prime}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(E,H,\mu}}\right)\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\epsilon}}\right),\end{cases}

where at least
\Phi_{3}^{\prime}
and
\Phi_{4}^{\prime}
are portable. If
\Phi_{1}^{\prime}

and
\Phi_{2}^{\prime}
are portable as well, we obtain

(5.4)

\begin{cases}u=\Phi_{1}^{*}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(E,\epsilon,\mu}}\right)\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(H^{2}}}\right)=E^{2}\epsilon\,\Psi_{1}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(H^{2}/\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(E^{2}\epsilon\mu^{-1}}}\right)}}\right),\\
u=\Phi_{2}^{*}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(H,\epsilon,\mu}}\right)\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(E^{2}}}\right)=H^{2}\mu\,\Psi_{2}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(E^{2}/\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(H^{2}\epsilon^{-1}\mu}}\right)}}\right),\\
u=\Phi_{3}^{*}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(E,H,\epsilon}}\right)\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\mu}}\right)=E^{2}\epsilon\,\Psi_{3}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\mu/\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(E^{2}H^{-2}\epsilon}}\right)}}\right),\\
u=\Phi_{4}^{*}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(E,H,\mu}}\right)\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\epsilon}}\right)=H^{2}\mu\,\Psi_{4}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\epsilon/\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(E^{-2}H^{2}\mu}}\right)}}\right).\end{cases}

We note that

and

in these equations, so we can set
\varPhi\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(E^{\prime},H^{\prime}}}\right)=\Phi\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(E,H,\epsilon,\mu}}\right)
.
Also,
\Psi_{1}=\Psi_{3}
and
\Psi_{2}=\Psi_{4}
, so it suffices
to consider
\Psi_{3}
and
\Psi_{4}
, corresponding to the manifestly
portable functions
\Phi_{3}^{\prime}
and
\Phi_{4}^{\prime}
. Thus, we can reduce
(
5.4
) to

(5.5)

\begin{cases}u=E^{\prime}\,\varPsi_{3}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(H^{\prime}/E^{\prime}}}\right),&(EM1),\\
u=H^{\prime}\,\varPsi_{4}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(E^{\prime}/H^{\prime}}}\right).&(EM2).\end{cases}

Although he considers only the sets
\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\{E,\epsilon,\mu}}\right\}

and
\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\{H,\epsilon,\mu}}\right\}
of “fundamental” quantities,
Buckingham, too, finds the representations in (

),
writing them as
u=\epsilon E^{2}\,\varphi_{1}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\frac{\mu H^{2}}{\epsilon E^{2}}}}\right)

and
u=\mu H^{2}\,\varphi_{2}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\frac{\epsilon E^{2}}{\mu H^{2}}}}\right)
,
respectively
[
18
, p. 359]
. He then remarks:

Assuming that the complete formula is

we have
\varphi_{1}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(x}}\right)=\varphi_{2}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(x}}\right)=\frac{1+x}{8\pi}.

However, the purpose of dimensional analysis in this case is not to
derive
\varphi_{1}
and
\varphi_{2}
from the sought-after function

\phi
such that
u=\phi\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(E^{\prime},H^{\prime}}}\right)
but to do the opposite,
so let us reverse Buckingham’s inference, proceeding as in Example

5.5
.

We have
\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[u}}\right]=\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[E^{\prime}}}\right]=\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[H^{\prime}}}\right]\neq\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[1_{Q}}}\right]

and if we assume for symmetry reasons that

and set
x=H^{\prime}/E^{\prime}
then we obtain the functional equation
\varPsi\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(x}}\right)=x\,\varPsi\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(x^{-1}}}\right)

as before. Thus,
\varPsi_{3}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(x}}\right)=\varPsi_{4}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(x}}\right)=\varPsi\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(x}}\right)=k\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(1_{Q}+x}}\right)
,
where

, and substitution in
\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(EM1}}\right)

or
\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(EM2}}\right)
gives

or
u=k\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\epsilon E^{2}+\mu^{-1}B^{2}}}\right)
if
B=\mu H
.

Working with examples, Buckingham thus recognised in
[
18
]

that there may be more than one way of representing
\Phi
. However,
he dismissed this observation by asserting that the representation
with
\varphi_{1}
is “equivalent” to that with
\varphi_{2}

[
18
, p. 359, 362]
. While this equivalence is not obvious
from Buckingham’s presentation,
\varPsi_{3}

\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\phi_{1}}}\right)

and
\varPsi_{4}

\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\phi_{2}}}\right)
are in fact equal, but
on the assumption that
\varPhi\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(E^{\prime},H^{\prime}}}\right)=\varPhi\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(H^{\prime},E^{\prime}}}\right)
.

Example 5.8

(based on a problem in Bridgman
[
4
]
, pp. 5–8)
.

Let two bodies
\mathfrak{B}
and
\mathfrak{b}
with
masses
M
and
m
revolve around each other in empty space under
influence of their mutual gravitational attraction, as in the classical
two-body problem. Let
t
denote the period of revolution and

the mean distance between
\mathfrak{B}
and
\mathfrak{b}
(or
another characteristic distance). One might want to find out how
t

depends on
M
,
m
and
d
,
that is,

t^{\mathfrak{K}}=\Phi_{0}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(M,m,d}}\right),

but the associated dimensional matrix

\begin{array}[]{ccccc}&\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[t}}\right]&\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[M}}\right]&\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[m}}\right]&\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[d}}\right]\\
\mathsf{L}&0&0&0&1\\
\mathsf{T}&1&0&0&0\\
\mathsf{M}&0&1&1&0\end{array}

shows that
\Phi_{0}
is not precomplete; there is no prebasis of
dimensions for
\Phi_{0}
.

Bridgman suggests that

does also depend on
the gravitational constant
G
,
that is,

t^{\mathfrak{K}}=\Phi\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(M,m,d,G}}\right).

giving the dimensional matrix

\begin{array}[]{cccccc}&\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[t}}\right]&\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[M}}\right]&\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[m}}\right]&\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[d}}\right]&\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[G}}\right]\\
\mathsf{L}&0&0&0&1&3\\
\mathsf{T}&1&0&0&0&-2\\
\mathsf{M}&0&1&1&0&-1\end{array}.

Then
\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\{\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[M}}\right],\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[d}}\right],\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[G}}\right]}}\right\}

and
\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\{\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[m}}\right],\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[d}}\right],\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[G}}\right]}}\right\}

are the two prebases of dimensions for
\Phi
, and the minimal exponents
yield

\begin{cases}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[t}}\right]^{2}=\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[M}}\right]^{-1}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[d}}\right]^{3}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[G}}\right]^{-1},\quad\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[m}}\right]=\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[M}}\right]&\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\mathrm{for}\;\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\{\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[M}}\right],\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[d}}\right],\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[G}}\right]}}\right\}}}\right),\\
\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[t}}\right]^{2}=\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[m}}\right]^{-1}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[d}}\right]^{3}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[G}}\right]^{-1},\quad\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[M}}\right]=\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[m}}\right]&\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\mathrm{for}\;\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\{\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[m}}\right],\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[d}}\right],\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[G}}\right]}}\right\}}}\right),\end{cases}

so

\begin{cases}t^{2}=\Phi_{1}^{\prime}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(M,d,g}}\right)\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(m}}\right)\\

are consistent equations with portable functions. Hence, we have

\begin{cases}t^{2}=\Phi_{1}^{*}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(M,d,G}}\right)\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(m}}\right)=M^{-1}d^{3}G^{-1}\,\Psi_{1}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(m/M}}\right),&(K1)\\
t^{2}=\Phi_{2}^{*}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(m,d,G}}\right)\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(M}}\right)=m^{-1}d^{3}G^{-1}\,\Psi_{2}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(M/m}}\right).&(K2)\end{cases}

Combining
\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(K1}}\right)
and
\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(K2}}\right)
we obtain

,
and assuming for symmetry reasons that
\Phi\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(M,m,d,G}}\right)=\Phi\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(m,M,d,G}}\right)

this gives
M^{-1}\Psi_{1}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(m/M}}\right)=M^{-1}\Psi_{2}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(m/M}}\right)
.
This implies that
\Psi_{1}=\Psi_{2}=\Psi
, so setting

we
obtain the functional equation

\Psi\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(x}}\right)=x^{-1}\,\Psi\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(x^{-1}}}\right).

This functional equation has solutions of the form

\Psi(x)=k\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(1_{Q}+x}}\right)^{-1}\qquad\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(k,x\in\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[1_{Q}}}\right]}}\right),

unique under natural analyticity conditions (see Appendix A). Substituting
this in (
K1
) or (
K2
) gives

t^{2}=kd^{3}G^{-1}(M+m)^{-1}.

Here,
k
and

are constants, so if several planets
\mathfrak{b}_{i}

orbit the sun and

for every planet mass
m_{i}

then, approximately,

regardless of relative
planetary masses. This is Kepler’s third law of planetary motion.
(The mutual gravitational attraction of planets can be disregarded
since
m_{i}\ll M
.)

As before, (
5.6
) can be interpreted both as a quantity
equation and as a scalar equation, and assuming that
t,d,G,M,m>0

the latter can also be written as

(5.7)

t=k\sqrt{d^{3}G^{-1}(M+m)^{-1}}\qquad\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(k\in\mathbb{R}}}\right).

It is worth pointing out that Bridgman
[
4
, p. 8]
considered
only one equation, namely

t=\nicefrac{{r^{\frac{3}{2}}}}{{\,G^{\frac{1}{2}}m_{2}^{\frac{1}{2}}}}\,\phi\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\frac{m_{2}}{m_{1}}}}\right).

This corresponds to (
K1
) with
r=d
,
m_{1}=m
,
m_{2}=M
and

\phi\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(x}}\right)^{2}=\Psi_{1}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(x^{-1}}}\right)
. Unlike Buckingham
before him, Bridgman did not reflect on the possibility that the original
function could have more than one representation, and apparently none
of them reflected on whether it was possible to obtain a stronger
result by combining dimensional analysis with a symmetry assumption.

Remark 5.9
.

The symmetry assumptions in Examples
5.5
–
5.8

can be applied to
\Psi_{1}
and
\Psi_{2}
separately. In Example

5.5
, for example. if
\Phi\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(a,b}}\right)=a\,\Psi_{1}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(b/a}}\right)

and
\Phi\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(a,b}}\right)=\Phi\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(b,a}}\right)
then
a\,\Psi_{1}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(b/a}}\right)=b\,\Psi_{1}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(a/b}}\right)
,
and we obtain the functional equation
\Psi_{1}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(x}}\right)=x\,\Psi_{1}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(x^{-1}}}\right)

with the solutions
\Psi_{1}(x)=k_{1}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(1_{Q}+x}}\right)
. Similarly,
combining
\Psi_{2}
with
\Phi\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(a,b}}\right)=\Phi\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(b,a}}\right)

we obtain
\Psi_{2}(x)=k_{2}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(1_{Q}+x}}\right)
. However, recall
that the symmetry assumption also allows us to conclude that
\Psi_{1}=\Psi_{2}
,
implying that
k_{1}=k_{2}
.

Conversely, we may heuristically look for a symmetry of
\Phi
that
makes it possible to conclude that
\Psi_{1}=\Psi_{2}
. For example,
in Example
5.8
it is not difficult to see that the symmetry

\Phi\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(M,m,d,G}}\right)=\Phi\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(m,M,d,G}}\right)
leads to the
conclusion that
\Psi_{1}=\Psi_{2}
. The symmetry of
\Phi
can
in turn be derived from an assumption that “all motion is relative”
so that a description of the two-body system in which
\mathfrak{B}

is at rest and
\mathfrak{b}
revolves around
\mathfrak{B}
is
equivalent to a description in which
\mathfrak{b}
is at rest and

\mathfrak{B}
revolves around
\mathfrak{b}
. Thus, we derive (
5.6
),
an empirically falsifiable generalization, from a relativity principle
together with the assumption that
M,m,d,G
determine
t
and assumptions
about the dimensions of these variables. By falsifying (
5.6
)
we would falsify at least one postulate from which it was derived,
but by verifying (
5.6
) we provide support for the postulates,
including a relative motion assumption implying the symmetry of
\Phi
.

6.
Dimensional analysis and matroid theory

Recall that a (finite) matroid is a finite set
\mathcal{E}
equipped
with a set
\mathcal{I}
of subsets of
\mathcal{E}
, said to be

independent
sets.
\mathcal{I}
is required to satisfy certain
conditions so as to generalize the notion of (linear) independence
of columns in a matrix, vectors in a vector space, elements of a free
abelian group etc. Those subsets of
\mathcal{E}
which are not independent
are said to be
dependent
. In matroid theory, a maximal independent
subset of
\mathcal{E}
is called a
basis
, and a minimal dependent
subset of
\mathcal{E}
is called a
circuit
. A subset
C

of
\mathcal{E}
such that
C\setminus\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\{e}}\right\}
is a basis
for some
e\in C
is called a
pseudocircuit
in this article.
Every matroid basis contains the same number
r
of elements of
\mathcal{E}
,
so every pseudocircuit is a set of
r+1
elements with a subset that
is a basis.

An integer matrix
D
with labelled columns

\begin{array}[]{ccc}t_{1}&\cdots&t_{p}\\
\varepsilon_{11}&\cdots&\varepsilon_{1p}\\
\vdots&\ddots&\vdots\\
\varepsilon_{m1}&\cdots&\varepsilon_{mp}\end{array}

can be interpreted as a matroid with
\mathcal{E}=\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\{t_{1},\ldots,t_{p}}}\right\}

and the

independent subsets of
\mathcal{E}
corresponding
to the sets of linearly independent associated columns in
D
. A
maximal independent subset of
\mathcal{E}
corresponds to a maximal
independent set of columns in
D
, so
r
is equal to the rank of

D
.

D
can also be interpreted in several ways as a dimensional matrix
associated with a quantity space
Q
, a quantity function on
Q

and a basis for
Q/
\!\sim
,

\begin{array}[]{cccc}&\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[t_{1}}}\right]&\cdots&\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[t_{p}}}\right]\\
\mathsf{E}_{1}&\varepsilon_{11}&\cdots&\varepsilon_{1p}\\
&\vdots&\ddots&\vdots\\
\mathsf{E}_{m}&\varepsilon_{m1}&\cdots&\varepsilon_{mp}\end{array}.

As elaborated below, a matroid basis corresponds to a prebasis for
a quantity function in augmented dimensional analysis and to a group
of “repeating variables
”
in certain formulations of dimensional
analysis (see, e.g.,
[
28
,
21
]
), while a pseudocircuit corresponds
to a
\pi
-monomial in augmented dimensional analysis and a certain
kind of “dimensionless group” or
“
\pi
-group
”

in classical dimensional analysis.

Example 6.1
.

Consider the set
\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\{a,b,c}}\right\}
with the matroid structure
given by the matrix

\begin{array}[]{ccc}a&b&c\\
1&1&1\\
0&1&1\end{array}.

Here,
\emptyset
,
\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\{a}}\right\}
,
\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\{b}}\right\}
,

\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\{c}}\right\}
,
\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\{a,b}}\right\}
and
\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\{a,c}}\right\}

are independent sets,
\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\{a,b}}\right\}
and
\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\{a,c}}\right\}

are bases,
\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\{a,b,c}}\right\}
is a pseudocircuit but not a
circuit, and
\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\{b,c}}\right\}
is a circuit but not a pseudocircuit.

For every pseudocircuit
\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\{s_{1},\ldots,s_{r+1}}}\right\}\subseteq\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\{t_{1},\ldots,t_{p}}}\right\}
,
there are integers
K_{j}
, unique up to sign, that define a pair
of
\pi
-monomials
of the form
s_{1}^{K_{1}}\cdots s_{r+1}^{K_{r+1}}

such that

(6.1)

\displaystyle\prod_{j=1}^{r+1}\nolimits\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[s_{j}}}\right]^{K_{j}}=\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[1_{Q}}}\right],\qquad\mathrm{or\;equivalently}\qquad\sum\nolimits_{j=1}^{r+1}K_{j}\varepsilon_{\ell j}=0\quad\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\ell=1,\ldots,m}}\right),

(6.2)

\displaystyle\exists j:K_{j}\neq 0,\qquad\mathrm{gcd}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(K_{1},\ldots,K_{r+1}}}\right)=1.

The uniqueness of the integers
K_{j}
follows from the rank-nullity
theorem for free
\mathbb{Z}
-modules, according to which the kernel
of an
m\times\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(r+1}}\right)
integer matrix of rank
r
has rank
1; see also
[
16
]
.

Note that a monomial
t_{1}^{K_{1}}\cdots t_{p}^{K_{p}}
satisfying

\prod_{j=1}^{p}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[t_{j}}}\right]^{K_{j}}=\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[1_{Q}}}\right]
instead
of (
6.1
) need not be a
\pi
-monomial, and that the proof
of Theorem
3.1
requires that the variables
\pi_{0},\ldots,\pi_{n-r}

are
\pi
-monomials, in that context written as
y_{i}^{K_{i}}\prod_{j=1}^{r}\nolimits t_{j}^{-K_{ij}}

with
K_{i}>0
.

Example 6.2
.

For the matrix

\begin{array}[]{ccc}a&b&c\\
1&2&1\end{array},

there are three pseudocircuits
\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\{a,b}}\right\},\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\{a,c}}\right\},\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\{b,c}}\right\}

with associated
\pi
-monomial pairs
\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\{a^{2}b^{-1},a^{-2}b}}\right\}
,

\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\{ac^{-1},a^{-1}c}}\right\}
and
\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\{bc^{-2},b^{-1}c^{2}}}\right\}
.
Although
ab^{-1}c,a^{-1}bc^{-1}\in\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[1_{Q}}}\right]
as well, these
invariant monomials or “dimensionless groups” are not appropriate
for dimensional analysis. In this case, augmented dimensional analysis
yields, for example, the equation
a=c\Psi\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(b/c^{2}}}\right)
,
but not
a=kbc^{-1}
as obtained by setting
\Psi\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(x}}\right)=kx
.
Only if
\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\{a,b,c}}\right\}
is a pseudocircuit does the fact
that
ab^{-1}c\in\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[1_{Q}}}\right]
for quantity variables
a,b,c

imply that there is a constant
k
such that
ab^{-1}c=k\in\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[1_{Q}}}\right]

(compare Example
4.3
).

Furthermore,
t_{1}^{K_{1}}\cdots t_{p}^{K_{p}}
can be identified
with an element
\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(K_{1},\ldots,K_{p}}}\right)
of
\mathbb{Z}^{p}

as a free
\mathbb{Z}
-module, making the set of monomials satisfying

\prod_{j=1}^{p}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[t_{j}}}\right]^{K_{j}}=\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[1_{Q}}}\right]
a
free submodule
\mathcal{N}
of
\mathbb{Z}^{p}
, that is, a free
abelian group. It can be shown that a prebasis (maximal independent
subset) of
\mathcal{N}
can be formed by selecting
p-r

\pi
-monomials
from as many
\pi
-monomial pairs. For example,
ab^{-1}c=\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(a^{2}b^{-1}}}\right)\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(ac^{-1}}}\right)^{-1}=\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(ac^{-1}}}\right)\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(b^{-1}c^{2}}}\right)

\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\{a^{2}b^{-1},ac^{-1}}}\right\}
,

\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\{ac^{-1},b^{-1}c^{2}}}\right\}

\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\{a^{2}b^{-1},b^{-1}c^{2}}}\right\}

\mathcal{N}
.
Thus,
ab^{-1}c
and
a^{-1}bc^{-1}
are redundant as well as inappropriate
for dimensional analysis, as are all invariant monomials except
\pi
-monomials.

Remark 6.4
.

There is an ambivalence in classical dimensional analysis regarding
multiple-equation results. Augmented dimensional analysis yields one
equation for every prebasis for a quantity function. Similarly, in
some formulations of mainly classical dimensional analysis groups
of “repeating variables” are distinguished; each such group is
just a prebasis for a quantity function in the present approach. However,
when it has not been overlooked or ignored, the fact that dimensional
analysis may yield more than one possible equation has often been
regarded as a non-uniqueness problem, dealt with by arbitrarily choosing
one equation or by introducing pragmatic rules to single out the “right”
equation
[
28
, p. 300]
,
[
21
, p, 2]
. By contrast, augmented
dimensional analysis generates a system of simultaneous equations,
all of which can be retained. The guiding principle is that equations
that contain the same information should be merged into one equation,
but if an equation cannot be derived from the other equations (if
any) then it should be retained, since otherwise useful information
could be lost.

7.
Concluding remarks

We have seen that dimensional analysis is simply a mathematical technique
that can be used to apply and test certain theories that admit mathematical
formalizations. This technique belongs to a big family of approaches
that are based on the principle of covariance
[
3
,
20
]
: a relation
between scalars representing a certain relation between fundamental
objects relative to a reference frame must continue to hold when the
frame is changed to another one of the same kind. The scalars may
change, but they change in tandem. In physics, the reference frames
are often inertial frames in an affine space; in dimensional analysis,
the reference frames are bases for a quantity space, and the fundamental
objects are quantities. A “physically meaningful” complete quantity
function is precisely one with a covariant scalar representation.
While it is an important principle, underlying classical dimensional
analysis in terms of scalars, that a function which is a covariant
scalar representation of a quantity function must have a special form,
a quantity function which admits a covariant scalar representation
must also have a special form – see the proof of Theorem

3.1
and Examples
2.3
and
5.2
. This principle
of quantity-measure duality presupposes a clear distinction between
quantities and their scalar representations, elaborated in quantity
calculus
[
17
]
.

Theorem
3.1
applies to scaling-covariant scalar representations
of quantity functions, not to covariant scalar representations in
general, so the premise that the quantity function considered is “physically
meaningful” in the sense of possessing the latter kind of scalar
representation is not fully used. It is not immediately obvious how
representation theorems where covariance under a more general form
of basis change is assumed should be formulated and proved, and if
they would produce stronger results. We leave these questions as open
problems.

Classical dimensional analysis can be formulated without reference
to products of quantities by relying on a
\pi
theorem expressed
in terms of “dimensionless” products
p_{i}
of measures of quantities;
these are products which are invariant under a scaling of fundamental
units of measurement. In augmented dimensional analysis, the “dimensionless”
products are instead
\pi
-monomials
\pi_{i}\in\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[1_{Q}}}\right]
,
products of quantities corresponding to pseudocircuits. This approach
requires more background, but also leads to a deeper understanding
of dimensional analysis. A system with a set of quantities, a basis
of quantities and corresponding measures of quantities is completely
analogous to a system with a set of vectors, a basis of vectors and
corresponding tuples of vector coordinates, or a system with a set
of tensors, a basis of tensors (often vectors) and corresponding arrays
of tensor components. Nowadays, vectors and tensors are seldom identified
or confused with their scalar representations, and a coordinate-free
approach is widely embraced in linear and multilinear algebra. It
may be time to make this transition also in dimensional analysis.
