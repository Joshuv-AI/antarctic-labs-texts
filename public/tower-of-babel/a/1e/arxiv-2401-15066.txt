Efficient High-Dimensional Entangled State Analyzer with Linear Optics
Niv Bharos, Liubov Markovich, Johannes Borregaard

Abstract
The use of higher-dimensional photonic encodings (qudits) instead of two-dimensional encodings (qubits) can improve the loss tolerance and reduce the computational resources of photonic-based quantum information processing. To harness this potential, efficient schemes for entangling operations such as the high-dimensional generalization of a linear optics Bell measurement will be required. We show how an efficient high-dimensional entangled state analyzer can be implemented with linear optics and auxiliary photonic states. The Schmidt rank of the auxiliary state in our protocol scales only linearly with the dimensions of the input states instead of more than exponentially, as in previous proposals. In addition, we outline how the state can be generated deterministically from a single quantum emitter coupled to a small qubit processor. Our protocol thus outlines an experimentally feasible route for efficient, high-dimensional entangled state analyzers with linear optics.



I
Introduction

Encoding and transmitting quantum information with photons is at the heart of both quantum networking
Kimble 2008
and photonic-based quantum computing
Slussarenko and Pryde 2019
. In the former, photonic qubits can be used to entangle distant stationary qubit systems
Moehring et al. 2007
;
Hensen et al. 2015
;
van Leent et al. 2022
, while in the latter, multi-photon entangled states can serve as a resource for universal quantum computing with linear optics
Browne and Rudolph 2005
;
Istrati et al. 2020
. Both approaches take advantage of the excellent coherence properties of single photons and the easy manipulation of photonic qubits through linear optics elements such as phase shifters and beam splitters.

There are multiple ways of encoding quantum information into photons
Knill et al. 2001
. Conventionally, two orthogonal modes, such as the polarization of a photon, are used to encode a qubit. However, standard encodings in the time-bin basis, frequency, or spatial degrees of freedom are not limited to only two dimensions and can easily be extended to high-dimensional encodings where a single photon encodes more than one qubit of information
Knall et al. 2022
;
Borghi et al. 2023
. A
d
-dimensional photonic state is referred to as a qudit and can encode
\lfloor\text{log}_{2}{(d)}\rfloor
qubits of information.

Photonic qudits can have a number of advantages compared to photonic qubits. Recent work has shown how qudit-mediated entanglement distribution can lower the quantum memory requirements for near-term hardware
Lo Piparo et al. 2019
;
Zheng et al. 2022
;
Xie et al. 2021
;
Zhou et al. 2023
and enable more error-robust quantum key distribution
Yang et al. 2021
;
Erhard et al. 2020
;
Cerf et al. 2002
;
Bechmann-Pasquinucci and Peres 2000
;
Bell et al. 2022
. For photonic-based quantum computing, qudit encodings can provide means for more efficient algorithms with reduced circuit depth
Zhou et al. 2003
;
Imany et al. 2019
;
Paesani et al. 2021
and lower the resource requirements for simulation of high-dimensional gauge theories
Meth et al. 2023
and optimization problems
Karácsony et al. 2023
. More fundamentally, qudit entanglement can exhibit stronger non-classical correlations than qubit entanglement
Kaszlikowski et al. 2000
;
Collins et al. 2002
.

The crux of all photonic-based quantum information processing is the ability to perform entangling operations between photonic systems. For photonic qubits, this can be obtained with linear optics through probabilistic Bell measurements
Calsamiglia and Lütkenhaus 2001
. Notably, the use of auxiliary photonic states and quantum teleportation can enable near-deterministic entangling operations without the requirement of any photonic non-linearity
Knill et al. 2001
;
Kok et al. 2007
. In order to harness the full potential of photonic qudits, similar high-dimensional techniques have to be devised. However, devising a scheme for a linear-optics, high-dimensional entangled state analyzer (ESA) with efficient success rates has proven to be difficult and remains an outstanding problem in the field.

It is known that auxiliary photonic states are necessary to perform an ESA
Calsamiglia 2002
. Protocols with non-entangled auxiliary states exhibit an exponentially decreasing success probability as the dimension grows
Luo et al. 2019
which severely limits their feasibility. The use of entangled auxiliary states allows, in principle, for efficient operation where the success probability only decreases quadratically with the dimension
Zhang et al. 2019
. The protocol in Ref.
Zhang et al. 2019
, however, requires the Schmidt rank of the auxiliary state to increase faster than exponentially with the dimension of the input states. Generating such highly complex states is challenging, and known generation protocols only succeed with exponentially decreasing success probability
Zhang et al. 2019
.

In this paper, we introduce a novel protocol for linear optics ESA in even dimensions, that circumvents the exponential scalings of previous proposals. Specifically, we outline how an efficient
d
-dimensional ESA with success probability decreasing as
2/d^{2}
can be achieved using an auxiliary entangled photonic state with Schmidt rank scaling only as
d/2
. Furthermore, we outline how the auxiliary states can be generated deterministically from a single quantum emitter coupled to a small qubit processor with
\log{(d)}
qubits. As such, our protocol significantly relaxes the experimental requirements for a high-dimensional ESA and outlines a feasible route where quantum emitters are used to generate the resource states in an efficient manner.

II
High-Dimensional Entangled State Analyzer

We will consider time-bin encoded qudits, where the presence of a single photon in different time slots, or ‘bins’, is used to encode quantum information. For example, a single photon in the first time-bin corresponds to the state
\ket{0}
, in the second time-bin corresponds to the state
\ket{1}
, and so on, such that a
d
-dimensional qudit state can be expressed in the basis
\{\ket{0},\ldots,\ket{d-1}\}
. Although we focus on the time-bin encoding, our scheme is compatible with other photonic qudit encodings such as spatial or frequency encoding. The implementations of the necessary operations of our scheme that we describe here, such as the quantum Fourier transform (QFT) and the generation of the auxiliary state, are however, specific for time-bin encoding. We leave it to future work to design the implementations for other encodings.

The objective of the ESA is to measure two photonic qudits
\ket{a}
and
\ket{b}
encoded in
d
time-bins in an entangled state basis consisting of orthogonal, maximally entangled states with Schmidt rank
d
. The two-dimensional Bell state measurement has two successful detection patterns that each correspond to a Bell state projection.
When our protocol succeeds, it in general projects
\ket{a}
and
\ket{b}
on to an entangled state with Schmidt rank
d
of the form:

\begin{split}\ket{\Psi}_{m}=\sum_{k=0}^{d-1}&\lambda_{k}\ket{k}_{a}\ket{(k+m)\;\textrm{mod d}}_{b},\\
&\text{where}\;\;|\lambda_{k}|^{2}=\frac{1}{d}.\end{split}

(1)

Here
m\in\{1,\ldots,d-1\}
and the phases in front of each mode will in general be determined by the detection pattern.

A schematic overview of an ESA of two photonic input qudits following our protocol is shown in Fig.
1
. The ESA uses
d-2
auxiliary spatial modes labeled as
x_{0},x_{1},\dots,x_{d-3}
. Each auxiliary mode contains a single photonic qudit. As shown in Ref.
Calsamiglia 2002
, performing an ESA in
d
dimensions requires
d
initial particles. As there already are two initial photons
a
and
b
, we use the minimal number of auxiliary photons for the ESA. We mix the input photons with a QFT of the spatial modes. This
d
-dimensional QFT can be achieved with a sequence of beam splitters and phase shifters
Reck et al. 1994
;
Barak and Ben-Aryeh 2007
.

d-2

\ket{\textrm{aux}}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{d/2}}\sum_{j=0}^{\frac{d}{2}-1}\ket{\textrm{aux}_{j}}

(2)

where
|\text{aux}_{j}\rangle=\ket{a_{j,0}a_{j,1}\ldots\;a_{j,d-3}}=\ket{a_{j,0}}\otimes\ket{a_{j,1}}\otimes\ldots\;\otimes\ket{a_{j,d-3}}
. Here
|a_{j,k}\rangle
with
a_{j,k}\in[0,d-1]
corresponds to a photon in time-bin
a_{j,k}
in spatial mode
k
.

To ensure that each spatial mode in
\ket{\textrm{aux}_{j}}
contains photons in different time-bins, we enforce the constraint
(a_{j,0},a_{j,1},\dots,a_{j,d-3},y_{j},z_{j})\in P[0,1,...,d-1]
i.e., that they correspond to a permutation
P
of all the
d
time-bins. Since there are only
d-2
auxiliary modes, there will be two time-bins
y_{j},z_{j}
that do not appear in a state
\ket{\textrm{aux}_{j}}
. Furthermore, we enforce that the two time-bins that are excluded in one state
\ket{\textrm{aux}_{j}}
are not equal to the two time-bins excluded from any other state
\ket{\textrm{aux}_{i}}
, i.e.,
y_{i}\neq y_{j}\;\land\;z_{i}\neq z_{j}\;\land\;y_{i}\neq z_{j},\quad\forall\;i,j
. As a consequence of the constraints, the auxiliary state is an entangled state with Schmidt rank
d/2
.

The freedom in choosing the sets
\{y_{j},z_{j}\}
for the auxiliary state corresponds to choosing what type of states the measurements will project onto. We can, for example, choose
z_{j}=(y_{j}+i)\;\text{mod}\;d,\;\;\forall i\in\{1,\ldots d-1\}
. For a specific
i
, a successful ESA corresponds to a measurement of photons
a
and
b
in a state from Eq. (
1
) with
m=i
where the phases in
\lambda_{k}
are determined by the specific detection pattern.

III
Preparation of the Auxiliary state

The use of auxiliary photonic states of the form in Eq. (
2
) is key to having an efficient ESA where the success probability only decreases quadratically with the dimension.
In the four-dimensional ESA example, we can prepare the auxiliary state with one quantum emitter in a cavity and one optical switch. The setup is shown in Fig.

\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}\left(\ket{0}_{\text{s}}\ket{\text{vv}}+\ket{1}_{\text{s}}\ket{0\text{v}}\right).

\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}\left(\ket{0}_{\text{s}}\ket{\text{vv}}+\ket{1}_{\text{s}}\ket{01}\right).

\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}\left(\ket{0}_{\text{s}}\ket{01}+\ket{1}_{\text{s}}\ket{23}\right)

X

\ket{+}/\ket{-}
), the prepared state will be
\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}(\ket{01}\pm\ket{23})

The above procedure can be generalized to prepare the auxiliary states for arbitrary even dimensions. With a control register that contains
\lceil\log_{2}\left(\frac{d}{2}\right)\rceil
qubits, multi-qubit controlled gates and optical switches, we can create the required state with
d-2
photonic qudits and Schmidt rank
\frac{d}{2}
in the following way. The control qubits are prepared in an equal superposition of
d/2
different states. By applying a series of multi-qubit controlled operations, the state of the spin qubit is flipped controlled on a specific term in the superposition of control qubits allowing for the emission of a photon conditioned on the state of the qubit register. The optical switches are used to route the photons to the correct spatial modes. Thus, each of the
d/2
superposition states of the qubit register corresponds to one mode in the superposition of the auxiliary multi-qudit state. We refer the reader to Supplemental Material D for further details.

IV
Conclusion

In summary, we have proposed an efficient linear-optics implementation of a high-dimensional entangled state analyzer in even dimensions. Our scheme uses the minimal number of required auxiliary photons and has a success probability that decreases only quadratically with the dimension. Importantly, we achieve this with auxiliary states with a Schmidt rank that increases only linearly with the dimension and we outline how the states can be generated deterministically with a single quantum emitter coupled to a small (logarithmic in dimension) qubit register.

Our work significantly relaxes the experimental requirement for a high-dimensional ESA and points to the use of quantum emitters for the efficient preparation of auxiliary states for linear-optics, high-dimensional photonics quantum processing. As outlined in this paper, the auxiliary state of a four-dimensional ESA succeeding with probability 1/8 can be generated from a single quantum emitter, paving the way for near-term experimental demonstrations using neutral atoms
Thomas et al. 2022
or solid-state emitters
Knall et al. 2022
;
Meng et al. 2023
. We note that the auxiliary state of the four-dimensional ESA could also be prepared from an SPDC source in a probabilistic manner using a delay line for one of the emitted photons. While such an approach does not scale to higher dimensions, it may be relevant for near-term experiments with no access to a quantum emitter. While the success probability of our scheme is on par with other, more complicated schemes in the literature, we are not ruling out that even better performance could potentially be obtained in the future. Investigation of this as well as the extension of our scheme to odd dimensions is left for future work.

IV.2
High-Dimensional Entanglement Swapping

High-dimensional entanglement swapping aims to swap the entanglement of photonic qudit a (b) with Alice’s (Bob’s) system to an entangled state with Schmidt rank
S=d
from Eq. (
1
) shared by Alice and Bob’s systems. Alice and Bob can hold a qudit of dimension
d
, in which case entanglement swapping is equivalent to generating an entangled state with Schmidt rank
d
between the two qudits. More generally, the qudits that end up in an entangled state can be entangled with other qudits, for example in a graph state in which case we are fusing two high-dimensional graph states.

In high-dimensional entanglement swapping, the ESA is applied to photons
a
and
b
that are entangled with the qudits from Alice and Bob, respectively
A
and
B
, in the following way:

\ket{\psi}_{ABab}=\frac{1}{d}\sum_{i=0}^{d-1}\ket{i}_{a}\ket{i}_{A}\otimes\sum_{j=0}^{d-1}\ket{k}_{b}\ket{k}_{B}\;\;.

(19)

The projection of a successful ESA after applying the local unitary from Eq. (
17
) applied to the initial state Eq. (
19
) results in the following state shared by Alice and Bob’s qudits.

\footnotesize\begin{split}\ket{\Psi}_{AB}=\frac{1}{d\sqrt{d^{d}}\cdot\sqrt{\frac{d}{2}}}\sum_{j=0}^{\frac{d}{2}-1}\ket{y_{j}}_{A}\ket{z_{j}}_{B}+\ket{z_{j}}\ket{y_{j}}_{B}.\end{split}

(20)

For a specific choice of the auxiliary state, for example
z_{j}=(y_{j}+i)\;\text{mod}\;d\;\;\forall\;\;i\in\{1,\ldots d-1\}
, the output state is:

\footnotesize\begin{split}\ket{\Psi}_{AB}=\frac{1}{d\sqrt{d^{d}}\cdot\sqrt{\frac{d}{2}}}\sum_{j=0}^{\frac{d}{2}-1}&\ket{y_{j}}_{A}\ket{(y_{j}+i)\;\text{mod}\;d}_{B}\\
+&\ket{(y_{j}+i)\;\text{mod}\;d}\ket{y_{j}}_{B}.\end{split}

(21)

By applying a different local unitary operation, any entangled state with Schmidt rank
d
can be generated. The probability of measuring one successful detection pattern is:

\left|\bra{\Psi}_{m}\ket{\psi}_{AB}\right|^{2}=\left|\frac{1}{\sqrt{d}}\cdot\frac{1}{d\sqrt{d^{d}}\sqrt{\frac{d}{2}}}\cdot d\right|^{2}=\frac{2}{d^{2}\cdot d^{d}}.

(22)

As long as all photons are measured in different time-bins, the combination of output ports is irrelevant. Thus, each photon can be detected in
d
output ports, and the protocol will succeed. There are
d
photons and
d
photon detectors, thus,
d^{d}
detection patterns lead to success. The total success probability is a polynomial function of dimension:

p_{\textrm{ES}}=\left|\bra{P}_{ab}\ket{\psi}_{ABab}\right|^{2}\cdot d^{d}=\frac{2}{d^{2}}\;\;.

(23)

IV.3
High-Dimensional Quantum Teleportation

Figure 5:

In related work
Zhang et al. 2019
;
Luo et al. 2019
, high-dimensional quantum teleportation is the focus of the ESA. To fairly compare our protocol, we consider our protocol for high-dimensional quantum teleportation in even dimensions. We show an overview of the scheme in Fig.
5
. Photon
a
is prepared in the
d
-dimensional state to be teleported.

\ket{a}=\sum_{i=0}^{d-1}\alpha_{i}\ket{i}_{a}.

(24)

Photon
b_{0}
is prepared in an entangled state with qudit
b_{1}
in the following way:

\ket{b_{0}b_{1}}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{d}}\sum_{k=0}^{d-1}\ket{k}_{b_{0}}\ket{k}_{b_{1}}.

(25)

We rewrite the input state:

\footnotesize\begin{split}&\ket{ab_{0}b_{1}}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{d}}\sum_{i=0}^{d-1}\alpha_{i}\ket{i}_{a}\otimes\sum_{k=0}^{d-1}\ket{k}_{b_{0}}\ket{k}_{b_{1}}\\
&=\frac{1}{\sqrt{d}}\bigg[(\alpha_{0}\ket{00}+\alpha_{1}\ket{10}+\ldots+\alpha_{d-1}\ket{(d-1)\;0})_{ab_{0}}\otimes\ket{0}_{b_{1}}\\
&+(\alpha_{0}\ket{01}+\alpha_{1}\ket{11}+\ldots+\alpha_{d-1}\ket{(d-1)\;1})_{ab_{0}}\otimes\ket{1}_{b_{1}}\\
&+\ldots\\
&+(\alpha_{0}\ket{0(d-1)}+\alpha_{1}\ket{1(d-1)}+\ldots+\alpha_{d-1}\ket{(d-1)(d-1)})_{ab_{0}}\\
&\hskip 8.50012pt\otimes\ket{(d-1)}_{b_{1}}\bigg].\end{split}

(26)

By applying the projection from Eq. (
18
), we see that the only terms that have a non-zero contribution to the state of photon
b_{1}
are terms where
i=z_{j}\;\land\;k=y_{j}
and the other way around,
i=y_{j}\;\land\;k=z_{j}
. The state of qubit
b_{1}
is now:

\begin{split}\ket{b_{1}}=\bra{P}_{ab_{0}}\ket{ab_{0}b_{1}}=\frac{1}{d\sqrt{\frac{d^{d}}{2}}}\sum_{j=0}^{\frac{d}{2}-1}\alpha_{z_{j}}\ket{y_{j}}+\alpha_{y_{j}}\ket{z_{j}}.\end{split}

(27)

Which is equal to the state to be teleported from Eq. (
24
) up to the following unitary operation on Bob’s side.

U=\sum_{j=0}^{\frac{d}{2}-1}\ket{z_{j}}\bra{y_{j}}+\ket{y_{j}}\bra{z_{j}}

(28)

Thus, we have successfully transported the state from
a
to Bob’s qudit
b_{1}
:

U\ket{b_{1}}=\sum_{j=0}^{\frac{d}{2}-1}\alpha_{z_{j}}\ket{z_{j}}+\alpha_{y_{j}}\ket{y_{j}}=\sum_{i=0}^{d-1}\alpha_{i}\ket{i}

(29)

with the total success probability of
d
-dimensional quantum teleportation:

p_{\text{tel}}=|\braket{a|b_{1}}|^{2}\cdot d^{d}=\frac{2}{d^{2}},

(30)

which is equal to the success rates for high-dimensional entanglement swapping.

IV.4
Auxiliary Photon State

In this section, we show how the auxiliary state can be prepared deterministically from a single quantum emitter coupled to a small qubit processor. Our generation scheme requires
2^{\lceil\log_{2}\left(d-2\right)\rceil}-1
optical switches, one single quantum emitter, and a
\lceil\log_{2}\left(\frac{d}{2}\right)\rceil
qubit register capable of performing multi-qubit controlled CNOT gates on the emitter system. A schematic of the setup is shown in Fig.
4
. In
d
dimensions, the state of the
d-2
auxiliary photons from Eq. (
2
) is an equal superposition of
\frac{d}{2}
modes. We propose a way to create auxiliary states of the form:

\small\begin{split}\ket{\text{aux}}&=\frac{1}{\sqrt{d/2}}\sum_{j=0}^{\frac{d}{2}-1}\ket{\textrm{aux}_{j}}\\
&=\frac{1}{\sqrt{d/2}}\bigg(\ket{a_{0}b_{0}a_{1}b_{1}\;\ldots\;a_{\frac{d}{2}-2}b_{\frac{d}{2}-2}}\\
&+\ket{a_{\frac{d}{2}-1}b_{\frac{d}{2}-1}a_{0}b_{0}\;\ldots\;a_{\frac{d}{2}-3}b_{\frac{d}{2}-3}}+\;\ldots\\
&+\ket{a_{1}b_{1}a_{2}b_{2}\;\ldots\;a_{\frac{d}{2}-1}b_{\frac{d}{2}-1}}\bigg)_{x_{0},x_{1},\ldots,x_{d-3}},\\
\text{where}\;&b_{i}=a_{i}+1\;\;\land\;\;a_{i}\neq a_{j}\hskip 9.24994pt\forall\hskip 9.24994pti\neq j.\end{split}

(31)

Where
a_{i}\in\{0,1,\ldots d-1\}
. We use groups of two photons
a
and
b
with
b_{i}
in one time-bin later than
a_{i}
. In each consecutive mode
\ket{\text{aux}_{j+1}}
the time-bins of the previous mode
\ket{\text{aux}}_{j}
are rotated clockwise, which ensures that in each mode of the auxiliary superposition two unique time-bins are excluded.

There is a control register that consists of
m=\lceil\log_{2}\left(\frac{d}{2}\right)\rceil
qubits and one quantum emitter. The quantum emitter has the energy level structure as shown in Fig.
3
with two energy levels in the ground state, where one of the levels
\ket{0}_{\text{s}}
(the bright state) can be optically excited to an excited state. This excited state will decay back to the bright state through the emission of a single photon. The dark state
\ket{0}_{\text{s}}
cannot access the excited state. Quantum systems that can be used as quantum emitters are, for example, neutral atoms
Thomas et al. 2022
or solid-state emitters
Knall et al. 2022
;
Meng et al. 2023
.

The
m
qubits in the control register
\ket{c}
start in an equal superposition of all possible modes and the quantum emitter
\ket{\text{s}}
is initialized in the dark mode:

\small\begin{split}\frac{1}{\sqrt{\frac{d}{2}}}\left(\ket{c_{0}}+\ket{c_{1}}+\ldots+\ket{c_{\frac{d}{2}-1}}\right)\otimes\ket{0}_{\text{s}}.\end{split}

(32)

With
\ket{c_{j}}
the state of the control qubits and
\{\ket{c_{0}},\ket{c_{1}},\ldots,\ket{c_{\frac{d}{2}-1}}\}
spanning a basis, for example the computational basis. In the protocol, the following operations are applied to flip and excite the quantum emitter twice controlled on one mode of the control register.

1.

Flip the quantum emitter controlled on one of the modes in the superposition of control qubits such that it is in the bright state.

2.

Excite the quantum emitter to emit a photon in time-bin
a_{i}
.

3.

Excite the quantum emitter time
\Delta t
later to emit a photon in time-bin
b_{i}=a_{i}+1
.

4.

Execute step
1
again to flip the quantum emitter back to the original (dark) state.

This results in the emission of two photons in successive time-bins that are routed to the correct spatial mode and delayed according to that spatial mode. From here on ’emitting two photons’ refers to the procedure above. Now we have all the elements to build up the state of the auxiliary photons. Time-bins
a_{0}
and
b_{0}
occur in
\frac{d}{2}-1
modes in Eq. (
31
), so we start by creating these time-bins in all modes. We are free to choose the time-bins of these photons as
a_{0}=0
and
b_{0}=1
.

First, we emit photons
a_{0}
and
b_{0}
controlled on mode
\ket{c_{0}}
and route them to spatial modes
x_{0}
and
x_{1}
, respectively. The state is:

\begin{split}\sqrt{\frac{1}{d/2}}\bigg(&\ket{c_{0}}\ket{01\text{vv}\ldots\text{v}}+\ket{c_{1}}\ket{\text{vv}\ldots\text{v}}+\;\ldots\;\\
&+\ket{c_{\frac{d}{2}-1}}\ket{\text{vv}\ldots\text{v}}\bigg)\otimes\ket{1}_{\text{s}}.\end{split}

(33)

So far, we only have photons in two spatial modes, correlated to one term of the control qubits, and no photons (vacuum ’v’) in all other modes. We emit two photons in time-bins
2
and
3
controlled on
\ket{c_{1}}
and route them to spatial modes
x_{2}
and
x_{3}
, respectively. We continue to emit photons in consecutive time-bins and route them to the correct spatial modes until we create all photons
a_{0}
and
b_{0}
from Eq. (
31
):

\footnotesize\begin{split}&\sqrt{\frac{1}{d/2}}\bigg(\ket{c_{0}}\ket{01\text{vv}\ldots\text{v}}+\ket{c_{1}}\ket{\text{vv}23\text{vv}\ldots\text{v}}+\ket{c_{2}}\ket{\text{vvvv}45\text{vv}\ldots\text{v}}\\
&+\ldots\;+\ket{c_{\frac{d}{2}-2}}\ket{\text{vv}\ldots\;\text{v}(d-4)(d-3)}+\ket{c_{\frac{d}{2}-1}}\ket{\text{vv}\ldots\text{v}}\bigg)\otimes\ket{1}_{\text{s}}.\end{split}

(34)

However, since the photons are emitted consecutively, their time-bins are not equal. The delay lines ensure temporal alignment. By taking into account the delay lines as indicated in Fig.
4

\footnotesize\begin{split}\sqrt{\frac{1}{d/2}}&\bigg(\ket{c_{0}}\ket{(d-4)(d-3)\text{vv}\ldots\text{v}}\\

(35)

(d-4)\rightarrow\;0
and
(d-3)\rightarrow\;1
and now we have created photons in time-bins
0
and
1

\footnotesize\begin{split}\sqrt{\frac{1}{d/2}}\bigg(&\ket{c_{0}}\ket{01\text{vv}\ldots\text{v}}+\ket{c_{1}}\ket{\text{vv}01\text{vv}\ldots\text{v}}\\
&+\ket{c_{2}}\ket{\text{vvvv}01\text{vv}\ldots\text{v}}+\ldots\;\\
