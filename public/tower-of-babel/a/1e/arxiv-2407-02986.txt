Abstract
In this paper we propose and analyse a new formulation and pointwise divergence-free mixed finite element methods for the numerical approximation of Darcy–Brinkman equations in vorticity–velocity–pressure form, coupled with a transport equation for thermal energy with viscous dissipative effect and mixed Navier-type boundary conditions. The solvability analysis of the continuous and discrete problems is significantly more involved than usual as it hinges on Banach spaces needed to properly control the advective and dissipative terms in the non-isothermal energy balance equation. We proceed by decoupling the set of equations and use the Banach fixed-point theorem in combination with the abstract theory for perturbed saddle-point problems. Some of the necessary estimates are straightforward modifications of well-known results, while other technical tools require a more elaborated analysis. The velocity is approximated by Raviart–Thomas elements, the vorticity uses Nédélec spaces of the first kind, the pressure is approximated by piecewise polynomials, and the temperature by continuous and piecewise polynomials of one degree higher than pressure. Special care is needed to establish discrete inf-sup conditions since the curl of the discrete vorticity is not necessarily contained in the discrete velocity space, therefore suggesting to use two different Raviart–Thomas interpolants. A discrete fixed-point argument is used to show well-posedness of the Galerkin scheme. Error estimates in appropriate norms are derived, and a few representative numerical examples in 2D and 3D and with mixed boundary conditions are provided.



Key words
: Flow–transport coupling; Highly permeable porous media; Vorticity-based formulation; Mixed finite element methods; Analysis in Banach spaces; Viscous dissipation.

1
Introduction

Scope.

The interplay of vorticity and viscous dissipation in natural convection within high permeability porous media results in intricate flow and thermal dynamics. Vorticity enhances mixing and improves heat transfer efficiency, while viscous dissipation raises the local temperature, potentially altering the overall thermal gradient that drives convection. To accurately predict the system performance and design effective solutions, these interactions need to be thoroughly understood and appropriately modelled. We consider the numerical analysis of the coupled Brinkman equations with temperature including a viscous dissipation term. In fluid saturated porous domains with relatively large permeabilities (as in the regime where Brinkman equations hold), viscous dissipation effects within the fluid are typically non-negligible as discussed in
[
21
]
. In the case of clear fluid, the viscous dissipation term typically includes a nonlinearity in the form of the velocity scaled by its own Laplacian. In contrast, the type of viscous dissipation used here (and more appropriate for flow in porous media) has a nonlinear term depending on the square filtration velocity modulus and it represents the power per unit volume generated by the dissipation (see also
[
10
]
). Some of the common features of viscous dissipation (acting as an internal heat generation mechanism) include the onset of unstable temperature gradients and secondary buoyant flow patterns that are not induced by external thermal forcing terms.

The formulation of incompressible viscous flow equations using vorticity (or microrotation), velocity and pressure has been used and analysed extensively in, e.g.,
[
1
,
2
,
8
,
7
,
11
,
17
,
19
,
22
,
14
,
28
,
31
,
37
,
43
,
44
]
. Later on, models for coupled advection-diffusion equations and vorticity-based viscous flow formulations have been introduced in
[
6
,
34
]
(see also the recent contribution
[
24
]
). For the analysis in this case one needs to require higher regularity of the Brinkman filtration velocity, for example. One can also follow
[
12
]
(designed for the coupling of thermal energy equation and Darcy flow in mixed form) treating the advective term with a duality argument and invoking continuity and compactness of suitably chosen operators, and requiring further that the concentration-dependent permeability is Lipschitz continuous and uniformly bounded away from zero. Alternatively to those approaches, here we follow the series of works
[
26
,
15
,
18
,
25
]
(see also the references therein) where the functional structure of the problem is generalised to Banach spaces. This strategy has a number of advantages, such as relaxing the regularity assumptions and not needing to include augmented Lagrangian terms, but it exhibits a more involved functional structure. This approach requires to find suitable Banach spaces that would allow us to show boundedness of all linear and nonlinear forms. We herein work with vorticity in the space
\mathbf{H}(\mathop{\mathbf{curl}}\nolimits_{s},\Omega)
(vector fields in
\mathbf{L}^{2}(\Omega)
whose curl is in
\mathbf{L}^{s}(\Omega)
) and velocity in the space
\mathbf{H}^{r}(\mathop{\mathrm{div}}\nolimits,\Omega)
(vector fields in
\mathbf{L}^{r}(\Omega)
whose divergence is in
\mathrm{L}^{2}(\Omega)
), with
\frac{1}{r}+\frac{1}{s}=1
.

We use a fixed-point approach to separate the Brinkman and viscous dissipated heat equations, and the functional framework for the Brinkman equations requires the analysis of a perturbed saddle-point problem. The required inf-sup conditions necessitate non-standard regularity properties of auxiliary boundary value problems not typically available in the literature. For vector potentials it is possible to use the elegant theory from the series of papers
[
3
,
4
,
5
]
(see also
[
38
]
), which holds for any Lebesque exponent larger than one. However, the assumptions that lead to that theory do not apply to our case since the two sub-boundaries in our domain meet. Therefore we appeal to the works in
[
35
,
36
]
and use a new auxiliary problem in the range of admissible Lebesgue exponents.

Another distinctive feature of the present paper appears at the discrete level. As a consequence again of the functional structure, one of the issues is that the curl of the discrete vorticity is not in the same space as the discrete velocity. We then need to define two Raviart–Thomas type interpolators and use commutativity properties in the
\mathbf{H}(\mathop{\mathbf{curl}}\nolimits)
space associated with vorticity and the extra Raviart–Thomas space. The approach used here might be of benefit for the analysis of other systems with similar Banach structure.

2
Model problem and its weak formulation

2.1
Natural convection with viscous dissipation

Let us consider a simply connected bounded and Lipschitz domain
\Omega\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}
, with
d\in\{2,3\}
occupied by an incompressible fluid in a non-isothermal regime and moving within a fully saturated porous media.
The domain boundary
\partial\Omega

is partitioned into disjoint sub-boundaries where slip and tangential vorticity trace conditions are imposed
\partial\Omega:=\overline{\Gamma}\cup\overline{\Sigma}
,
\overline{\Gamma}\cap\overline{\Sigma}=\emptyset
, and it is assumed for sake of simplicity that both sub-boundaries are non-empty
|\Gamma|\cdot|\Sigma|>0
.
For a sufficiently smooth external body force
\boldsymbol{f}:\Omega\to\mathbb{R}^{d}
and external heat source
g:\Omega\to\mathbb{R}
, we consider the following form of the equations of steady natural convection in their velocity–pressure–temperature form and using dimensional units (see, for example,
[
20
]
but here not taking into account the Rayleigh dissipation)

(2.1a)

\displaystyle\frac{\mu}{\kappa}\boldsymbol{u}-\mu^{\prime}\boldsymbol{\Delta}\boldsymbol{u}+\nabla p

\displaystyle=\rho\boldsymbol{f}(T)

\displaystyle\quad\text{in $\Omega$},

(2.1b)

\displaystyle\mathop{\mathrm{div}}\nolimits\boldsymbol{u}

\displaystyle=0

\displaystyle\quad\text{in $\Omega$},

(2.1c)

\displaystyle\sigma_{0}T+\boldsymbol{u}\cdot\nabla T-\alpha\Delta T

\displaystyle=g+\frac{\mu}{\kappa c^{\prime}\rho}\boldsymbol{u}\cdot\boldsymbol{u}

\displaystyle\quad\text{in $\Omega$},

stating the balance of linear momentum and of mass, and the balance of energy including the viscous dissipation function on the right-hand side, which is proportional to the square modulus of the seepage velocity (the kinetic energy, see
[
41
]
).
While viscous dissipation is a significant factor in driving convection inside the fluid-saturated porous medium, fluid phase and solid phase of the porous medium are assumed to be in local thermal equilibrium. In high-permeability porous media, the flow behaviour becomes more complex due to the intensified interaction between the fluid and the porous matrix. Such an interaction is significantly affected by, e.g., vorticity patterns and viscous dissipation, which are crucial in shaping the convection patterns and thermal distribution within the medium.

We also consider the following set of boundary conditions representing zero tangential traces of vorticity and slip velocity (the so-called Navier boundary condition) together with insulated boundaries on the component
\Gamma
; while non-tangential flow velocity, vanishing pressure, and fixed temperature are prescribed on the remainder of the boundary:

(2.1d)

\displaystyle\mathop{\mathbf{curl}}\nolimits\boldsymbol{u}\times\boldsymbol{n}=\boldsymbol{0},\quad\boldsymbol{u}\cdot\boldsymbol{n}=0\quad\text{and}\quad\alpha\nabla T\cdot\boldsymbol{n}=0

\displaystyle\quad\text{on $\Gamma$},

(2.1e)

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{u}\times\boldsymbol{n}=\boldsymbol{0},\quad p=0\quad\text{and}\quad T=0

\displaystyle\quad\text{on $\Sigma$}.

The model coefficients are the fluid mass density
\rho
, the dynamic viscosity
\mu
, the effective dynamic viscosity in the porous layer
\mu^{\prime}
, the permeability of the porous medium
\kappa
, specific heat capacity per unit mass of the fluid
c^{\prime}
, heat capacity ratio
\sigma_{0}
, and the thermal diffusivity
\alpha
. The linear Oberbeck–Boussinesq approximation is considered, and the exerted force due to changes in temperature is linearly temperature-dependent

\boldsymbol{f}(T)=-\beta(T-T_{0})\boldsymbol{g},

with
\beta
a positive constant (thermal expansion coefficient),
T_{0}
a reference temperature, and
\boldsymbol{g}
gravity.

Problem (
2.1
) can be equivalently set in terms of velocity, vorticity, pressure, and temperature. For this we rewrite the momentum and energy balance equations using the rescaled vorticity vector

\boldsymbol{\omega}:=\sqrt{\mu^{\prime}}\mathop{\mathbf{curl}}\nolimits\boldsymbol{u},

and then use the vector identity

\mathop{\mathbf{curl}}\nolimits\mathop{\mathbf{curl}}\nolimits\boldsymbol{u}=-\boldsymbol{\Delta}\boldsymbol{u}+\nabla(\mathop{\mathrm{div}}\nolimits\boldsymbol{u}),

together with the incompressibility constraint and the fact that the apparent viscosity is assumed constant, which leads to the following set of equations

(2.2a)

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\omega}-\sqrt{\mu^{\prime}}\mathop{\mathbf{curl}}\nolimits\boldsymbol{u}

\displaystyle=\boldsymbol{0}

\displaystyle\quad\text{in $\Omega$},

(2.2b)

\displaystyle\frac{\mu}{\kappa}\boldsymbol{u}+\sqrt{\mu^{\prime}}\mathop{\mathbf{curl}}\nolimits\boldsymbol{\omega}+\nabla p

\displaystyle=\rho\boldsymbol{f}(T)

\displaystyle\quad\text{in $\Omega$},

(2.2c)

\displaystyle\mathop{\mathrm{div}}\nolimits\boldsymbol{u}

\displaystyle=0

\displaystyle\quad\text{in $\Omega$},

(2.2d)

\displaystyle\sigma_{0}T+\boldsymbol{u}\cdot\nabla T-\alpha\Delta T

\displaystyle=g+\frac{\mu}{\kappa c^{\prime}\rho}|\boldsymbol{u}|^{2}

\displaystyle\quad\text{in $\Omega$},

and the compatible vorticity trace boundary condition (the first relation in (
2.1d
)) reads now
\boldsymbol{\omega}\times\boldsymbol{n}=\boldsymbol{0}
on
\Gamma
.

2.2
Weak formulation

We start by considering the real numbers
r>1,s>1
and we recall the definition of the following functional spaces

\displaystyle\mathbf{H}(\mathop{\mathbf{curl}}\nolimits_{s},\Omega)=\left\{\boldsymbol{\zeta}\in\mathbf{L}^{2}(\Omega):\mathop{\mathbf{curl}}\nolimits\boldsymbol{\zeta}\in\mathbf{L}^{s}(\Omega)\right\},\qquad\mathbf{H}^{r}(\mathop{\mathrm{div}}\nolimits,\Omega)=\left\{\boldsymbol{v}\in\mathbf{L}^{r}(\Omega):\mathop{\mathrm{div}}\nolimits\boldsymbol{v}\in\mathrm{L}^{2}(\Omega)\right\},

\displaystyle\mathrm{H}^{1}(\Omega)=\{\psi\in\mathrm{L}^{2}(\Omega):\nabla\psi\in\mathbf{L}^{2}(\Omega)\},

equipped with the following norms

\displaystyle\left\|\boldsymbol{\zeta}\right\|_{\mathop{\mathbf{curl}}\nolimits_{s},\Omega}:=\left\|\boldsymbol{\zeta}\right\|_{0,\Omega}+\left\|\mathop{\mathbf{curl}}\nolimits\boldsymbol{\zeta}\right\|_{\mathbf{L}^{s}(\Omega)},\qquad\left\|\boldsymbol{v}\right\|_{r,\mathop{\mathrm{div}}\nolimits,\Omega}:=\left\|\boldsymbol{v}\right\|_{\mathbf{L}^{r}(\Omega)}+\left\|\mathop{\mathrm{div}}\nolimits\boldsymbol{v}\right\|_{0,\Omega},

(2.3)

\displaystyle\left\|S\right\|^{2}_{1,\Omega}:=\left\|S\right\|^{2}_{0,\Omega}+\left\|\nabla S\right\|^{2}_{0,\Omega},

respectively. The first two spaces are Banach and the third space is Hilbert with respect to the respective graph topology.
In view of the boundary conditions, we also define the following closed subspaces

\displaystyle\mathbf{H}_{\star}(\mathop{\mathbf{curl}}\nolimits_{s},\Omega)

\displaystyle:=\{\boldsymbol{\zeta}\in\mathbf{H}(\mathop{\mathbf{curl}}\nolimits_{s},\Omega):\boldsymbol{\gamma}_{\boldsymbol{\tau}}\boldsymbol{\zeta}=\boldsymbol{0}\text{ on }\Gamma\},

\displaystyle\mathbf{H}^{r}_{\star}(\mathop{\mathrm{div}}\nolimits,\Omega)

\displaystyle:=\{\boldsymbol{v}\in\mathbf{H}^{r}(\mathop{\mathrm{div}}\nolimits,\Omega):\gamma_{\boldsymbol{n}}\boldsymbol{v}=0\text{ on }\Gamma\},

\displaystyle\mathrm{H}^{1}_{\star}(\Omega)

\displaystyle:=\{S\in\mathrm{H}^{1}(\Omega):\gamma S=0\text{ on }\Sigma\},

where the boundary specification is to be understood in the sense of traces restricted to sub-boundaries.
Note that if
\boldsymbol{\zeta}\in\mathbf{H}(\mathop{\mathbf{curl}}\nolimits_{s},\Omega)
then its tangential component
\boldsymbol{\gamma}_{\boldsymbol{\tau}}\boldsymbol{\zeta}
is defined in the trace space
\mathbf{W}^{-1/s,s}(\partial\Omega)
and if
\boldsymbol{v}\in\mathbf{H}^{r}(\mathop{\mathrm{div}}\nolimits,\Omega)
then its normal component
\gamma_{\boldsymbol{n}}\boldsymbol{v}
is defined in
\mathrm{W}^{-1/r,r}(\partial\Omega)
.

We proceed to multiply the momentum balance, constitutive, mass, and energy equations by suitable test functions and to integrate by parts over the domain. Note that for the divergence-based terms we use the following form of the Gauss formula conveniently extended to the case of Banach spaces (see, e.g.,
[
16
, Section 2.2]
):

\int_{\Omega}\boldsymbol{v}\cdot\nabla q=-\int_{\Omega}\mathop{\mathrm{div}}\nolimits\boldsymbol{v}\,q+\langle\gamma_{\boldsymbol{n}}\boldsymbol{v},\gamma q\rangle\qquad\forall\boldsymbol{v}\in\mathbf{H}^{t}(\mathop{\mathrm{div}}\nolimits,\Omega),q\in\mathrm{W}^{1,t^{\prime}}(\Omega),

where
\langle\cdot,\cdot\rangle
denotes the duality product between
\mathrm{W}^{-1/t,t}(\partial\Omega)
and
\mathrm{W}^{1/t,t^{\prime}}(\partial\Omega)
, with
1<t<\infty
. Similarly,
for curl-based terms we use a generalisation of
[
27
, Theorem 2.11]
(see, e.g.,
[
4
, Section 2]
):

\int_{\Omega}\mathop{\mathbf{curl}}\nolimits\boldsymbol{v}\cdot\boldsymbol{\zeta}=\int_{\Omega}\boldsymbol{v}\cdot\mathop{\mathbf{curl}}\nolimits\boldsymbol{\zeta}+\langle\boldsymbol{\gamma}_{\boldsymbol{\tau}}\boldsymbol{\zeta},\boldsymbol{\gamma}\boldsymbol{v}\rangle\qquad\forall\boldsymbol{\zeta}\in\mathbf{H}(\mathop{\mathbf{curl}}\nolimits_{t},\Omega),\boldsymbol{v}\in\mathbf{W}^{1,t^{\prime}}(\Omega),

where
\langle\cdot,\cdot\rangle
denotes the pairing between
\mathbf{W}^{-1/t,t}(\partial\Omega)
and its dual
\mathbf{W}^{1/t,t^{\prime}}(\partial\Omega)
.

We then arrive at the following weak formulation for (
2.2
): for given
\boldsymbol{g}\in\mathbf{L}^{\infty}(\Omega)
and
g\in L^{2}(\Omega)
, find
(\boldsymbol{\omega},\boldsymbol{u},p,T)\in\mathbf{H}_{\star}(\mathop{\mathbf{curl}}\nolimits_{s},\Omega)\times\mathbf{H}^{r}_{\star}(\mathop{\mathrm{div}}\nolimits,\Omega)\times\mathrm{L}^{2}(\Omega)\times\mathrm{H}_{\star}^{1}(\Omega)
such that

(2.4a)

\displaystyle\int_{\Omega}\boldsymbol{\omega}\cdot\boldsymbol{\zeta}-\sqrt{\mu^{\prime}}\int_{\Omega}\boldsymbol{u}\cdot\mathop{\mathbf{curl}}\nolimits\boldsymbol{\zeta}

\displaystyle=0\qquad\forall\boldsymbol{\zeta}\in{\mathbf{H}_{\star}(\mathop{\mathbf{curl}}\nolimits_{s},\Omega)},

(2.4b)

\displaystyle-\sqrt{\mu^{\prime}}\int_{\Omega}\mathop{\mathbf{curl}}\nolimits\boldsymbol{\omega}\cdot\boldsymbol{v}-\frac{\mu}{\kappa}\int_{\Omega}\boldsymbol{u}\cdot\boldsymbol{v}+\int_{\Omega}p\mathop{\mathrm{div}}\nolimits\boldsymbol{v}

\displaystyle=-\rho\int_{\Omega}\boldsymbol{f}(T)\cdot\boldsymbol{v}\qquad\forall\boldsymbol{v}\in{\mathbf{H}^{r}_{\star}(\mathop{\mathrm{div}}\nolimits,\Omega)},

(2.4c)

\displaystyle\int_{\Omega}q\mathop{\mathrm{div}}\nolimits\boldsymbol{u}

\displaystyle=0\qquad\forall q\in{\mathrm{L}^{2}(\Omega)},

(2.4d)

\displaystyle\sigma_{0}\int_{\Omega}T\,S+\int_{\Omega}(\boldsymbol{u}\cdot\nabla T)\,S+\alpha\int_{\Omega}\nabla T\cdot\nabla S

\displaystyle=\int_{\Omega}g\,S+\frac{\mu}{\kappa c^{\prime}\rho}\int_{\Omega}|\boldsymbol{u}|^{2}\,S\qquad\forall S\in{\mathrm{H}^{1}_{\star}(\Omega)},

where we have also used the boundary conditions (
2.1d
)–(
2.1e
).

For
\boldsymbol{\omega},\boldsymbol{\zeta}\in\mathbf{H}_{\star}(\mathop{\mathbf{curl}}\nolimits_{s},\Omega)
,
\boldsymbol{u},\boldsymbol{v}\in\mathbf{H}^{r}_{\star}(\mathop{\mathrm{div}}\nolimits,\Omega)
,
p,q\in\mathrm{L}^{2}(\Omega)
,
T,S\in\mathrm{H}^{1}_{\star}(\Omega)
, we define the following bilinear and trilinear forms, as well as the (affine,linear)-form
F
and linear functional
G
:

\displaystyle a_{1}(\boldsymbol{\omega},\boldsymbol{\zeta}):=\int_{\Omega}\boldsymbol{\omega}\cdot\boldsymbol{\zeta},\qquad b_{1}(\boldsymbol{\zeta},\boldsymbol{v}):=-\sqrt{\mu^{\prime}}\int_{\Omega}\mathop{\mathbf{curl}}\nolimits\boldsymbol{\zeta}\cdot\boldsymbol{v},\qquad b_{2}(\boldsymbol{v},q):=\int_{\Omega}q\mathop{\mathrm{div}}\nolimits\boldsymbol{v},

\displaystyle a_{2}(\boldsymbol{u},\boldsymbol{v}):=\frac{\mu}{\kappa}\int_{\Omega}\boldsymbol{u}\cdot\boldsymbol{v},\quad a_{3}(T,S):=\sigma_{0}\int_{\Omega}T\,S+\alpha\int_{\Omega}\nabla T\cdot\nabla S,\quad c_{1}(\boldsymbol{v};T,S):=\int_{\Omega}(\boldsymbol{v}\cdot\nabla T)S,

\displaystyle c_{2}(\boldsymbol{u},\boldsymbol{v};S):=\frac{\mu}{\kappa c^{\prime}\rho}\int_{\Omega}(\boldsymbol{u}\cdot\boldsymbol{v})\,S,\qquad F(S;\boldsymbol{v}):=\rho\beta\int_{\Omega}(S-T_{0})\boldsymbol{g}\cdot\boldsymbol{v},\qquad G(S):=\int_{\Omega}g\,S,

and denote by

\mathbf{B}_{i}
and
\mathbf{B}_{i}^{*}
the operators induced by the bilinear form
b_{i}(\cdot,\cdot)
,
i=1,2
:

\displaystyle\mathbf{B}_{1}

\displaystyle:\mathbf{H}_{\star}(\mathop{\mathbf{curl}}\nolimits_{s},\Omega)\to[\mathbf{H}_{\star}^{r}(\mathop{\mathrm{div}}\nolimits,\Omega)]^{\prime},\;\langle\mathbf{B}_{1}(\boldsymbol{\zeta}),\boldsymbol{v}\rangle:=b_{1}(\boldsymbol{\zeta},\boldsymbol{v}),\;\mathbf{B}_{1}^{*}:\mathbf{H}_{\star}^{r}(\mathop{\mathrm{div}}\nolimits,\Omega)\to[\mathbf{H}_{\star}(\mathop{\mathbf{curl}}\nolimits_{s},\Omega)]^{\prime},

\displaystyle\mathbf{B}_{2}

\displaystyle:\mathbf{H}^{r}_{\star}(\mathop{\mathrm{div}}\nolimits,\Omega)\to[\mathrm{L}^{2}(\Omega)]^{\prime},\quad\langle\mathbf{B}_{2}(\boldsymbol{v}),q\rangle:=b_{2}(\boldsymbol{v},q),\quad\mathbf{B}_{2}^{*}:\mathrm{L}^{2}(\Omega)\to[\mathbf{H}^{r}_{\star}(\mathop{\mathrm{div}}\nolimits,\Omega)]^{\prime}.

The system (
2.4
) is rewritten as follows: find the tuple
(\boldsymbol{\omega},\boldsymbol{u},p,T)\in\mathbf{H}_{\star}(\mathop{\mathbf{curl}}\nolimits_{s},\Omega)\times\mathbf{H}^{r}_{\star}(\mathop{\mathrm{div}}\nolimits,\Omega)\times\mathrm{L}^{2}(\Omega)\times\mathrm{H}_{\star}^{1}(\Omega)
such that

(2.5a)

\displaystyle a_{1}(\boldsymbol{\omega},\boldsymbol{\zeta})+b_{1}(\boldsymbol{\zeta},\boldsymbol{u})

\displaystyle=\;0

\displaystyle\qquad\forall\boldsymbol{\zeta}\in{\mathbf{H}_{\star}(\mathop{\mathbf{curl}}\nolimits_{s},\Omega)},

(2.5b)

\displaystyle b_{1}(\boldsymbol{\omega},\boldsymbol{v})-a_{2}(\boldsymbol{u},\boldsymbol{v})+b_{2}(\boldsymbol{v},p)

\displaystyle=\;F(T;\boldsymbol{v})

\displaystyle\qquad\forall\boldsymbol{v}\in{\mathbf{H}^{r}_{\star}(\mathop{\mathrm{div}}\nolimits,\Omega)},

(2.5c)

\displaystyle b_{2}(\boldsymbol{u},q)

\displaystyle=\;0

\displaystyle\qquad\forall q\in{\mathrm{L}^{2}(\Omega)},

(2.5d)

\displaystyle a_{3}(T,S)+c_{1}(\boldsymbol{u};T,S)-c_{2}(\boldsymbol{u},\boldsymbol{u};S)

\displaystyle=\;G(S)

\displaystyle\qquad\forall S\in{\mathrm{H}_{\star}^{1}(\Omega)}.

3
Unique solvability analysis of the continuous formulation

The well-posedness analysis of (
2.5
) shall use Banach fixed-point theory. To do so we will separate the Darcy–Brinkman equations (
2.5a
)–(
2.5c
) (and will consider a reduced problem in the Kernel of
\mathbf{B}_{2}
), from the energy equation (

).

3.1
Preliminaries

Given an arbitrary
t\in(1,\infty)
, consider for each
\boldsymbol{z}\in\mathbf{L}^{t}(\Omega)
the function

(3.1)

\mathcal{J}_{t}(\boldsymbol{z})\,:=\,\left\{\begin{array}[]{cl}\displaystyle|\boldsymbol{z}|^{t-2}\,\boldsymbol{z}&\quad\hbox{if}\,\,\boldsymbol{z}\not=\mathbf{0}\,,\\[4.30554pt]
\displaystyle\mathbf{0}&\quad\hbox{otherwise}.\end{array}\right.

The following two technical results from
[
25
]
provide a useful pairing property, and an appropriate relationship between divergence-free vector fields, respectively. The second result is here adapted to the case of mixed boundary conditions.

Lemma 3.1
.

Let
t,\,t^{\prime}\in(1,\infty)
be such that
\frac{1}{t}+\frac{1}{t^{\prime}}=1
. Then, for each
\boldsymbol{z}\in\mathbf{L}^{t}(\Omega)

there hold

(3.2a)

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{w}\,:=\,\mathcal{J}_{t}(\boldsymbol{z})\,\in\,\mathbf{L}^{t^{\prime}}(\Omega)\,,\qquad\boldsymbol{z}\,=\,\mathcal{J}_{t^{\prime}}(\boldsymbol{w})\,,{\quad\hbox{and}\quad}

(3.2b)

\displaystyle\int_{\Omega}\boldsymbol{z}\cdot\boldsymbol{w}=\|\boldsymbol{z}\|^{t}_{\mathbf{L}^{t}(\Omega)}=\|\boldsymbol{w}\|^{t^{\prime}}_{\mathbf{L}^{t^{\prime}}(\Omega)}=\|\boldsymbol{z}\|_{\mathbf{L}^{t}(\Omega)}\,\|\boldsymbol{w}\|_{\mathbf{L}^{t^{\prime}}(\Omega)},

and therefore
\mathcal{J}_{t}:\mathbf{L}^{t}(\Omega)\to\mathbf{L}^{t^{\prime}}(\Omega)

and
\mathcal{J}_{t^{\prime}}:\mathbf{L}^{t^{\prime}}(\Omega)\to\mathbf{L}^{t}(\Omega)
are bijective and inverse to each other.

Lemma 3.2
.

Let
t,\,t^{\prime}\in(1,\infty)
be such that
\frac{1}{t}+\frac{1}{t^{\prime}}=1
. Then, there exists a linear
and bounded operator
\mathcal{D}_{t^{\prime}}:\mathbf{L}^{t^{\prime}}(\Omega)\to\mathbf{L}^{t^{\prime}}(\Omega)
satisfying

(3.3)

\mathop{\mathrm{div}}\nolimits(\mathcal{D}_{t^{\prime}}(\boldsymbol{w}))=0\quad\text{in}\quad\Omega\quad\text{and}\quad\mathcal{D}_{t^{\prime}}(\boldsymbol{w})\cdot\boldsymbol{n}=0\quad\text{on}\quad\Gamma,\qquad\forall\,\boldsymbol{w}\in\mathbf{L}^{t^{\prime}}(\Omega)\,.

Moreover, for each
\boldsymbol{z}\in\mathbf{L}^{t}(\Omega)
such that
\mathop{\mathrm{div}}\nolimits(\boldsymbol{z})=0
in
\Omega

and
\boldsymbol{z}\cdot\boldsymbol{n}=0
on
\Gamma
, there holds

(3.4)

\int_{\Omega}\boldsymbol{z}\cdot\mathcal{D}_{t^{\prime}}(\boldsymbol{w})=\int_{\Omega}\boldsymbol{z}\cdot\boldsymbol{w}\qquad\forall\,\boldsymbol{w}\in\mathbf{L}^{t^{\prime}}(\Omega).

Let us now present, in a form which is convenient for our analysis, the following result regarding integrability of vector potentials in the case of mixed boundary conditions.

Lemma 3.3
.

If
\boldsymbol{f}\in\mathbf{L}^{s}(\Omega)
with
\mathop{\mathrm{div}}\nolimits\boldsymbol{f}=0
and
(\boldsymbol{f}\cdot\boldsymbol{n})|_{\Gamma}=0
, then there exists a unique
\boldsymbol{z}\in\mathbf{W}^{1,s}(\Omega)
solution to

\displaystyle\mathop{\mathbf{curl}}\nolimits\boldsymbol{z}=\boldsymbol{f}\quad\text{and}\quad\mathop{\mathrm{div}}\nolimits\boldsymbol{z}

\displaystyle=0\quad\text{in }\Omega,

(3.5)

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{z}\cdot\boldsymbol{n}

\displaystyle=0\quad\text{on }\Sigma,

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{z}\times\boldsymbol{n}

\displaystyle=\boldsymbol{0}\quad\text{on }\Gamma,

which satisfies

(3.6)

\|\mathop{\mathbf{curl}}\nolimits\boldsymbol{z}\|_{\mathbf{L}^{s}(\Omega)}\lesssim\|\boldsymbol{f}\|_{\mathbf{L}^{s}(\Omega)}.

We stress that a proof of a similar result is found in
[
4
, Theorem 4.21]
, stating that if the domain is as in
[
4
, Section 2]
(but in particular, this requires that
\Gamma
and
\Sigma
do not actually meet) and if it is furthermore of class
C^{2,1}
, then the result holds for
1<s<\infty
. Another similar result is given in
[
30

Theorem 3.1
.

Let
X,Y
be reflexive Banach spaces and consider bounded bilinear forms
a:X\times X\to\mathbb{R}
,
b:X\times Y\to\mathbb{R}
, and
c:Y\times Y\to\mathbb{R}
(with boundedness constants
\|a\|,\|b\|
, and
\|c\|
, respectively). Let
\mathbf{B}:X\to Y^{\prime}
be a bounded linear map induced by
b(\cdot,\cdot)
and let
X_{0}
denote its null space. Assume that
\mathrm{Ker}(\mathbf{B}^{*})=\{0\}
and further suppose that

1.

a(\cdot,\cdot)
and
c(\cdot,\cdot)
are symmetric and semi-positive definite over
X
and
Y
, respectively,

2.

there exists
\tilde{\alpha}>0
such that

\sup_{\tau\in X_{0}\setminus\{0\}}\frac{a(\sigma_{0},\tau)}{\|\tau\|_{X}}\geq\tilde{\alpha}\|\sigma_{0}\|_{X}\qquad\forall\sigma_{0}\in X_{0},

3.

and there exists
\tilde{\beta}>0
such that

\sup_{\tau\in X\setminus\{0\}}\frac{b(\tau,v)}{\|\tau\|_{X}}\geq\tilde{\beta}\|v\|_{Y}\qquad\forall v\in Y.

Then, for each
(f,g)\in X^{\prime}\times Y^{\prime}
there exists a unique
(\sigma,u)\in X\times Y
solution to the following perturbed saddle-point problem

\displaystyle a(\sigma,\tau)+b(\tau,u)

\displaystyle=\;f(\tau)\qquad\forall\tau\in X,

\displaystyle b(\sigma,v)-c(u,v)

\displaystyle=\;g(v)\qquad\forall v\in Y.

Moreover, the solution satisfies the stability bound

\|(\sigma,u)\|_{X\times Y}\lesssim\|f\|_{X^{\prime}}+\|g\|_{Y^{\prime}},

where the hidden constant depends only on
\|a\|,\|c\|,\tilde{\alpha}
, and
\tilde{\beta}
.

4
Galerkin scheme and well-posedness of the discrete problem

Here we derive a discrete formulation and show, under appropriate assumptions of the bilinear forms and finite dimensional spaces, that the discrete problem has a unique solution.

Let
\mathcal{T}_{h}

\tilde{\mathcal{C}}_{2}\leq C_{\text{Sob}}C_{s_{1}}\rho\beta\left\|\boldsymbol{g}\right\|_{\mathbf{L}^{\infty}(\Omega)}R+\max(\sigma_{0},\alpha)=:\mathcal{C}_{2}.

This culminates in the following error estimate for the temperature field,

\left\|e_{T}\right\|\leq\min(\sigma_{0}^{-1},\alpha^{-1})\,\mathcal{C}_{1}\left\|\boldsymbol{e}_{\boldsymbol{u}}\right\|_{r,\mathop{\mathrm{div}}\nolimits,\Omega}+(1+\min(\sigma_{0}^{-1},\alpha^{-1})\,\mathcal{C}_{2})\left\|\chi_{T}\right\|_{1,\Omega},

for which we readily conclude with the following total error estimate

\displaystyle\left\|\boldsymbol{e}_{\boldsymbol{\omega}}\right\|_{\mathop{\mathbf{curl}}\nolimits_{s},\Omega}

\displaystyle+\left\|\boldsymbol{e}_{\boldsymbol{u}}\right\|_{r,\mathop{\mathrm{div}}\nolimits,\Omega}+\left\|e_{p}\right\|_{0,\Omega}+\left\|e_{T}\right\|_{1,\Omega}

\displaystyle\leq(1+\max(C_{s_{1}},\beta_{2}^{-1})\left\|\mathcal{A}\right\|)\left\|(\boldsymbol{\chi}_{\boldsymbol{\omega}},\boldsymbol{\chi}_{\boldsymbol{u}},\chi_{p})\right\|

\displaystyle\quad+(1+\max(C_{s_{1}},\beta_{2}^{-1})C_{\Omega,r}\rho\beta\left\|\boldsymbol{g}\right\|_{\mathbf{L}^{\infty}(\Omega)})(1+\min(\sigma_{0}^{-1},\alpha^{-1})\mathcal{C}_{2})\left\|\chi_{T}\right\|_{1,\Omega}

\displaystyle\quad+(1+\max(C_{s_{1}},\beta_{2}^{-1})C_{\Omega,r}\rho\beta\left\|\boldsymbol{g}\right\|_{\mathbf{L}^{\infty}(\Omega)})\min(\sigma_{0}^{-1},\alpha^{-1})\mathcal{C}_{1}\left\|\boldsymbol{e}_{\boldsymbol{u}}\right\|_{r,\mathop{\mathrm{div}}\nolimits,\Omega}.

Given the assumption on parameters, subtracting the error term in velocity
\boldsymbol{e}_{\boldsymbol{u}}
and dividing throughout yields the claimed result, once the infimum of
((\tilde{\boldsymbol{\omega}}_{h},\tilde{\boldsymbol{u}}_{h},\tilde{p}_{h}),\tilde{T}_{h})
in
\mathfrak{U}_{h}\times\mathrm{Y}_{h}
is taken.

\square

Verification of general hypotheses

Inf-sup condition
(A1)
.

Consider
\boldsymbol{v}_{h}\in\mathbf{V}_{0,h}
. We can readily construct
\mathcal{D}_{s}[\mathcal{J}_{r}(\boldsymbol{v}_{h})]\in\mathbf{L}^{s}(\Omega)
. By construction this function has zero divergence. Then, guided by the arguments used in the first part of the proof of Lemma
3.4
we can find the unique
\boldsymbol{z}\in\mathbf{H}_{\star}(\mathop{\mathbf{curl}}\nolimits_{s},\Omega)
such that

(5.9)

\mathop{\mathbf{curl}}\nolimits\boldsymbol{z}=\mathcal{D}_{s}[\mathcal{J}_{r}(\boldsymbol{v}_{h})],\quad\mathop{\mathrm{div}}\nolimits\boldsymbol{z}=0,

and

(5.10)

\|\boldsymbol{z}\|_{\mathop{\mathbf{curl}}\nolimits_{s},\Omega}\leq\|\boldsymbol{z}\|_{\mathbf{W}^{1,s}(\Omega)}\lesssim\|\mathcal{D}_{s}[\mathcal{J}_{r}(\boldsymbol{v}_{h})]\|_{\mathbf{L}^{s}(\Omega)},

where the hidden constant only depends on the domain.

We now define
\tilde{\boldsymbol{z}}_{h}
as the Nédélec interpolant of the
\boldsymbol{z}
found above

\tilde{\boldsymbol{z}}_{h}:=\mathcal{I}^{\mathrm{N}}_{h}\boldsymbol{z}\in\mathbf{Z}_{h},

and use the commutativity of the Nédélec and Raviart–Thomas interpolation (
5.7a
) to write

(5.11)

\mathop{\mathbf{curl}}\nolimits\tilde{\boldsymbol{z}}_{h}=\mathop{\mathbf{curl}}\nolimits\mathcal{I}^{\mathrm{N}}_{h}\boldsymbol{z}=\mathcal{I}^{\mathrm{RT}{,s}}_{h}(\mathop{\mathbf{curl}}\nolimits\boldsymbol{z})=\mathcal{I}^{\mathrm{RT}{,s}}_{h}(\mathcal{D}_{s}[\mathcal{J}_{r}(\boldsymbol{v}_{h})])={\mathcal{D}_{s}[\mathcal{J}_{r}(\boldsymbol{v}_{h})]},

where we have employed (
5.9
) and the fact that
\mathcal{D}_{s}[\mathcal{J}_{r}(\boldsymbol{v}_{h})]
is in
\mathbf{V}^{t}
as well as in
\mathbf{V}_{h,s}
.

Note also that since
\boldsymbol{z}\in\mathbf{L}^{2}(\Omega)
(cf. proof of Lemma
3.4
), then
\tilde{\boldsymbol{z}}_{h}
is also in
\mathbf{L}^{2}(\Omega)
. Then, using triangle inequality, the bound (
5.10
), the continuity of the Nédélec interpolator (
5.7b
), and (
5.11
), we can write

(5.12)

\|\tilde{\boldsymbol{z}}_{h}\|_{\mathop{\mathbf{curl}}\nolimits_{s},\Omega}\leq\|\mathcal{I}^{\mathrm{N}}_{h}\boldsymbol{z}\|_{\mathbf{L}^{2}(\Omega)}+\|\mathop{\mathbf{curl}}\nolimits\mathcal{I}^{\mathrm{N}}_{h}\boldsymbol{z}\|_{\mathbf{L}^{s}(\Omega)}\lesssim\|\mathcal{D}_{s}[\mathcal{J}_{r}(\boldsymbol{v}_{h})]\|_{\mathbf{L}^{s}(\Omega)}.

Therefore, using the definition of the bilinear form
b_{1}(\cdot,\cdot)
, relation (
5.11
), and the bound (
5.12
), we can derive the inf-sup condition as follows

\displaystyle\sup_{\boldsymbol{\zeta}_{h}\in\mathbf{Z}_{h}\setminus\{\boldsymbol{0}\}}\frac{b_{1}(\boldsymbol{\zeta}_{h},\boldsymbol{v}_{h})}{\|\boldsymbol{\zeta}_{h}\|_{\mathop{\mathbf{curl}}\nolimits_{s},\Omega}}

\displaystyle\geq\frac{\int_{\Omega}\mathop{\mathbf{curl}}\nolimits\tilde{\boldsymbol{z}}_{h}\cdot\boldsymbol{v}_{h}}{\|\tilde{\boldsymbol{z}}_{h}\|_{\mathop{\mathbf{curl}}\nolimits_{s},\Omega}}=\frac{\int_{\Omega}\mathcal{I}^{\mathrm{RT}{,s}}_{h}(\mathcal{D}_{s}[\mathcal{J}_{r}(\boldsymbol{v}_{h})])\cdot\boldsymbol{v}_{h}}{\|\tilde{\boldsymbol{z}}_{h}\|_{\mathop{\mathbf{curl}}\nolimits_{s},\Omega}}

\displaystyle=\frac{\int_{\Omega}{\mathcal{D}_{s}[\mathcal{J}_{r}(\boldsymbol{v}_{h})]}\cdot\boldsymbol{v}_{h}}{\|\tilde{\boldsymbol{z}}_{h}\|_{\mathop{\mathbf{curl}}\nolimits_{s},\Omega}}=\frac{\|{\mathcal{D}_{s}[\mathcal{J}_{r}(\boldsymbol{v}_{h})]}\|_{\mathbf{L}^{s}(\Omega)}\|\boldsymbol{v}_{h}\|_{\mathbf{L}^{r}(\Omega)}}{\|\tilde{\boldsymbol{z}}_{h}\|_{\mathop{\mathbf{curl}}\nolimits_{s},\Omega}}

\displaystyle\gtrsim\|\boldsymbol{v}_{h}\|_{\mathbf{L}^{r}(\Omega)}\qquad\forall\boldsymbol{v}_{h}\in\mathbf{V}_{0,h},

where we have also used (
3.4
). The discrete inf-sup constant is independent of
h
, but depends on the continuous dependence on data of the auxiliary problem and on the continuity bound of the Nédélec interpolator.

Non-isothermal through-flow in a porous channel

For our second example we model the flow of cold water through a channel with five hot cylinders. The design of the test aims at heating the liquid and observing buoyancy effects as well. The channel has length
L=1.7
and height
H=1
(adimensional units). The cylinders have radii of approximately 0.1 and are slightly unsymmetrically distributed. The flow enters the channel from the left segment and exits on the right end.
For this test we consider a time-dependent model (adding the terms
\partial_{t}\boldsymbol{u}
to the momentum balance and
\partial_{t}T
to the thermal balance), and adopt a simple backward Euler time discretisation with constant time step
\Delta t=0.005
and run the simulation until the final time
t=1
. We follow a similar flow configuration as in, e.g.,
[
40
, Chapter 15]
and take the following values for the remaining parameters
\nu=0.001
,
\boldsymbol{g}=(0,-9.8)^{\tt t}
,
\rho=1
,
\mu=10^{-4}
,
K=1
,
\mu^{\prime}=10^{-3}
,
\beta=c^{\prime}=1
,
\alpha=0.1
. For the thermal equation we set a prescribed cold temperature on the inlet
T_{\mathrm{cold}}=10
, hot at the cylinders’ surface
T_{\mathrm{hot}}=50
, and consider zero-flux boundary conditions at the horizontal walls and at the outlet. For the flow problem we
impose a parabolic velocity profile and a compatible vorticity on the inlet

\boldsymbol{u}_{\mathrm{in}}=(1.5\arctan(40y(1-y)),0)^{\tt t},\qquad\omega_{\mathrm{in}}=-60\sqrt{\nu}\frac{2y-1}{1600y^{2}(y-1)^{2}+1},

slip velocity condition at the walls and cylinders,
and on the outlet assume zero pressure
p_{\mathrm{out}}=0

\sqrt{\mu^{\prime}}\langle\boldsymbol{u}_{h}\times\boldsymbol{n},\boldsymbol{\zeta}_{h}\rangle_{\mathrm{out}}.

7
Concluding remarks

Studying natural convection in highly permeable porous media considering vorticity and viscous dissipation has the potential to advance our understanding of complex fluid systems and improving various industrial processes. These multiphysics models include fluid dynamics, heat transfer, and porous media mechanisms. We prove that the governing equations are well-posed using the Banach fixed-point theory and perturbed saddle-point theory in Banach spaces. The discrete problem is shown to be well-posed and the analysis requires two Raviart–Thomas interpolators to match the regularity requirements of the continuous problem. We have presented numerical tests that confirm the properties of the proposed numerical methods.
It still remains to tackle the time-dependent version of the problem, a mixed formulation for the thermal energy equation (to provide energy conservation as well), and to analyse the functional structure of the set of equations in the case of a second viscous dissipation term in the energy equation.
