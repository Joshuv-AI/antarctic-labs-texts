Automorphism groups of cocompact CAT(0) cube complexes and simplicity
Tobias Hartnick, Merlin Incerti–Medici

Abstract
. We provide a systematic description of the automorphism groups of specially cocompact CAT(0) \CAT cube complexes. We show that these groups are topologically finitely generated, present a method to explicitly obtain generating sets, and prove a dichotomy on their size. Furthermore, we show that, under some extra assumptions, the normal subgroup known as Aut + \text{Aut}^{+} is simple, non-discrete, and tdlc. In particular, we obtain a new class of simple, non-discrete, tdlc groups that are accessible to further study. Finally, we study the relative size of Aut + < Aut \text{Aut}^{+}<\text{Aut} , providing a sufficient condition for its closure to be finite index and presenting a common example where it is not even cocompact.



1.
Introduction

J. Tits showed in
[
Tit70
]
that the (inversion-free) automorphism group of a regular tree with valence at least three is locally compact and uncountable, and that the subgroup generated by edge-fixators is simple. Following up on these ideas, Haglund and Paulin studied several types of negatively curved complexes in
[
HP98
]
, including some negatively curved buildings, certain Coxeter systems, and polyhedral complexes that admit a geometric action by a hyperbolic group. They showed that, similarly, the automorphism groups of these complexes are locally compact and uncountable. Furthermore, they generalised the group generated by edge-fixators to a group denoted
\text{Aut}^{+}
and showed that, under the right assumptions, it is simple and has finite index. For right-angled buildings, the study of the automorphism group has yielded similar results and more
[
Cap14
,
DMS19
,
BDM21
,
BDM23
,
BF23
]
and in the case of trees,
[
MV12
]
has expanded on several results regarding subgroups of the automorphism group.

In this paper, we consider the automorphism groups
\text{Aut}(X)
of
\CAT
cube complexes that are universal coverings of compact, non-positively curved special cube complexes. Particular instances of such cube complexes appear already in the work of Haglund and Paulin. Since then, automorphisms of
\CAT
cube complexes have been studied implicitly in the work on automorphisms of right-angled buildings mentioned above and in
[
Laz18
]
, where Lazarovich studied regular
\CAT
cube complexes (that is, all vertices have the same link) and in particular provided a method to determine simplicity of certain subgroups of
\text{Aut}(X)
.

We will show that, for these cube complexes,
\text{Aut}(X)
satisfies both analogous and distinct properties to the cases studied by Tits, Haglund, Paulin, and Caprace. Specifically, we will show that these automorphism groups are topologically finitely generated and we present a characterisation as to when they are uncountable. Furthermore, we will show that, under some mild assumptions, the subgroup
\text{Aut}^{+}(X)
(see Section
1.3
) is a simple, non-discrete tdlc (totally disconnected and locally compact) group and we will study the size of
\text{Aut}^{+}(X)
in
\text{Aut}(X)
.

An additional motivation for our work arises as follows: Let
S
be a compact, non-positively curved special cube complex,
\Gamma
its fundamental group and
X
its universal covering. In this case,
X
is a
\CAT
cube complex and
\Gamma
acts cubically, freely, properly, and cocompactly on
X
. Such group actions have attracted significant attention over the last two decades, leading to significant results in group theory and topology
[
NR98
,
SW05
,
Wri12
,
BW12
,
Ago13
]
. Since
\Gamma
acts cubically, properly, and cocompactly and
X
is locally finite,
\Gamma
embeds as a uniform lattice in
\text{Aut}(X)
. A systematic understanding of
\text{Aut}(X)
may thus allow us to study cubulable groups using the theory of lattices in locally compact groups.

1.1.
The key tool: admissible edge labelings

Our description of
\text{Aut}(X)
relies on the choice of a good edge labeling on
X
. Let
\vec{\mathcal{E}}(X)
denote the set of oriented edges in
X
and let
\vec{\mathcal{E}}(X)_{v}
denote the set of outgoing edges at a vertex
v
. Our labelings are going to be as follows:

Definition 1
.

Let
\Sigma_{0}
be a finite set with a fixpoint free involution
\cdot^{-1}:\Sigma_{0}\rightarrow\Sigma_{0}
. A map
\ell:\vec{\mathcal{E}}(X)\rightarrow\Sigma_{0}
is called an
admissible edge labeling
, if the following hold:

(A1)

If
e,e^{\prime}
are in the same parallel class (as oriented edges), then
\ell(e)=\ell(e^{\prime})
. If
e,e^{\prime}
are the same geometric edge with opposite orientation, then
\ell(e^{\prime})=\ell(e)^{-1}
.

(A2)

The restriction
\ell|_{\vec{\mathcal{E}}(X)_{v}}
is injective for every
v\in X^{(0)}
.

(A3)

For all
v,w\in X^{(0)}
,
e_{1},e_{2}\in\vec{\mathcal{E}}(X)_{v}
,
e^{\prime}_{1},e^{\prime}_{2}\in\vec{\mathcal{E}}(X)_{w}
, we have the following: If
\ell(e_{1})=\ell(e^{\prime}_{1})
and
\ell(e_{2})=\ell(e^{\prime}_{2})
, then
e_{1}
and
e_{2}
span a square if and only if
e^{\prime}_{1}
and
e^{\prime}_{2}
span a square.

An admissible edge labeling is called
\Gamma
-invariant
, if for all
e\in\vec{\mathcal{E}}(X)
and all
g\in\Gamma
, we have
\ell(g(e))=\ell(e)
.

Example
.

The Salvetti complex of a right-angled Artin group admits an admissible edge labeling which is invariant under the action of the given right-angled Artin group. The labeling is the one coming from the Cayley-graph of the standard presentation of the right-angled Artin group, where oriented edges are labeled by the standard generators and their inverses.

It turns out that the existence of
\Gamma
-invariant admissible edge labelings is closely related to the existence of special cocompact actions. This is the content of our first result, which characterises the existence of
\Gamma
-invariant admissible edge labelings.

Theorem A
.

Let
S
be a compact, non-positively curved special cube complex,
\Gamma
its fundamental group, and
X
its universal covering. Then
X
admits a
\Gamma
-invariant admissible edge labeling.

Conversely, let
X
be a locally finite
\CAT
cube complex and
\Gamma
a group acting cubically and cocompactly on
X
such that there exists a
\Gamma
-invariant admissible edge labeling. Then the quotient
{\mathchoice{\raisebox{3.41666pt}{$\displaystyle{X}$}\mkern-5.0mu\diagup\mkern-4.0mu\raisebox{-3.41666pt}{$\displaystyle{\Gamma}$}}{\raisebox{3.41666pt}{$\textstyle{X}$}\mkern-5.0mu\diagup\mkern-4.0mu\raisebox{-3.41666pt}{$\textstyle{\Gamma}$}}{\raisebox{2.39166pt}{$\scriptstyle{X}$}\mkern-5.0mu\diagup\mkern-4.0mu\raisebox{-2.39166pt}{$\scriptstyle{\Gamma}$}}{\raisebox{1.70833pt}{$\scriptscriptstyle{X}$}\mkern-5.0mu\diagup\mkern-4.0mu\raisebox{-1.6994pt}{$\scriptscriptstyle{\Gamma}$}}}
is a compact, non-positively curved special cube complex,
\Gamma
acts freely on
X
, and
X
is the universal covering of
{\mathchoice{\raisebox{3.41666pt}{$\displaystyle{X}$}\mkern-5.0mu\diagup\mkern-4.0mu\raisebox{-3.41666pt}{$\displaystyle{\Gamma}$}}{\raisebox{3.41666pt}{$\textstyle{X}$}\mkern-5.0mu\diagup\mkern-4.0mu\raisebox{-3.41666pt}{$\textstyle{\Gamma}$}}{\raisebox{2.39166pt}{$\scriptstyle{X}$}\mkern-5.0mu\diagup\mkern-4.0mu\raisebox{-2.39166pt}{$\scriptstyle{\Gamma}$}}{\raisebox{1.70833pt}{$\scriptscriptstyle{X}$}\mkern-5.0mu\diagup\mkern-4.0mu\raisebox{-1.6994pt}{$\scriptscriptstyle{\Gamma}$}}}
.

Given an admissible edge labeling, we can also label oriented diagonals. Namely, we label an oriented diagonal by the collection of labels of the corresponding oriented edges (see figure

). This expands our alphabet
\Sigma_{0}
to an alphabet

Notation
.

Given a vertex
v\in X^{(0)}
, we write
\Sigma_{v}\subset\Sigma
for the set of labels that the outgoing edges and diagonals at
v
are labeled by. We write
\Sigma_{0,v}:=\Sigma_{v}\cap\Sigma_{0}
for the labels of the outgoing edges at
v
.

Furthermore, if
s,t\in\Sigma_{0,v}
are the labels of two edges
e,e^{\prime}\in\vec{\mathcal{E}}(X)_{v}
, then we say that
s
and
t

commute
if and only if
e
and
e^{\prime}
span a square in
X
. We write
[s,t]=1
if they commute and
[s,t]\neq 1
, if they do not. (See Definition
2.3
and Lemma
2.4
as to why this does not depend on the vertex
v
and Proposition
2.18
for a justification of this terminology and notation.)

Having labeled oriented edges and diagonals, we can use these labels to identify concatenations of oriented edges and diagonals (we call such concatenations
cube paths
) with words over the alphabet
\Sigma
together with a starting vertex. In particular, if we restrict to the cube paths that start at a fixed vertex
o
, we obtain a language
\mathcal{L}_{o}^{cube}
over the alphabet
\Sigma
. Given two words
v,w\in\mathcal{L}_{o}^{cube}
, we say they are
equivalent
if and only if their corresponding cube paths, starting at
o
, have the same endpoint and we write
v\equiv_{o}w
. Each equivalence class under this equivalence relation corresponds to the set of all cube paths from
o
to a particular vertex in
X^{(0)}
. In particular, the empty word
\epsilon
represents the vertex
o
. We use this to identify vertices in
X
with
\equiv_{o}
-equivalence classes in
\mathcal{L}_{o}^{cube}
.

1.2.
Topological generators and the size of
\text{Aut}(X)

Let
S
,
X
, and
\Gamma
be as in Theorem
A
. We always consider
\text{Aut}(X)
equipped with the compact-open topology. By a series of standard arguments, local finiteness of
X
implies that
\text{Aut}(X)
is second countable and locally compact. Furthermore, since every element of
\text{Aut}(X)
preserves vertices in
X
and the set of vertices is discrete,
\text{Aut}(X)
is totally disconnected.

Let
\ell
be a
\Gamma
-invariant admissible edge labeling on
X
. In order to state our main results, we need to establish a few more definitions.

Definition 2
.

Let
\gamma
be a concatenation of edges and diagonals, starting at
o
, and let
v\in\mathcal{L}_{o}^{cube}
be the word spelt out by
\gamma
. We say
v
is
reduced
, if
\gamma
does not cross any hyperplane more than once. We call
v

reducible
, if it is not reduced.

Every vertex
v\in X^{(0)}
admits a (usually not unique) cube path from
o
to
v
whose word is reduced.

Definition 3
.

Let
v\in\mathcal{L}_{o}^{cube}
represent a vertex and let
\sigma:\Sigma_{v}\rightarrow\Sigma
be an injective map. We call
\sigma
a
label-morphism
, if it satisfies the following:

•

\sigma(\Sigma_{0,v})\subset\Sigma_{0}
.

•

\forall s,t\in\Sigma_{0,v}:[s,t]=1\Leftrightarrow[\sigma(s),\sigma(t)]=1
.

•

\forall\alpha=\{s_{1},\dots,s_{n}\}\in\Sigma_{v}:\quad\sigma(\alpha)=\{\sigma(s_{1}),\dots,\sigma(s_{n})\}.

Since label-morphisms are uniquely determined by their restriction to
\Sigma_{0,v}
, we frequently write
\sigma:\Sigma_{0,v}\rightarrow\Sigma_{0}
.

Convention
.

If
\alpha=\{s_{1},\dots,s_{n}\}\in\Sigma
, we write
\sigma(\alpha)=\alpha
if
\sigma
fixes the set
\alpha
pointwise. We write
\sigma(\{\alpha\})=\{\alpha\}
, if
\sigma
preserves the set
\alpha
, although it may not fix its elements pointwise.

Definition 4
.

Given a reduced word
v\in\mathcal{L}_{o}^{cube}\setminus\{\epsilon\}
and a label-morphism
\sigma:\Sigma_{0,v}\rightarrow\Sigma_{0}
, we say that
\sigma
is
compatible with
v
if and only if for all
\alpha\in\Sigma_{v}
such that
v\alpha
is reducible and for all
\beta\in\Sigma_{v}
such that
[\alpha,\beta]=1
, we have
\sigma(\alpha)=\alpha
and
\sigma(\beta)=\beta
.

Let
o\in X^{(0)}
. We denote
G_{o}:=\Stab_{\text{Aut}(X)}(o)
. For any
g\in G_{o}
and any
v\in X^{(0)}
,
g
induces a bijection

\prescript{}{v\cdot}{\sigma(g)}:\Sigma_{v}\rightarrow\Sigma_{g(v)}

which sends the label of an outgoing edge
e
at
v
to the label of the edge
g(e)\in\vec{\mathcal{E}}(X)_{g(v)}
. Equivalently,
\prescript{}{v\cdot}{\sigma(g)}
is defined by the equation

g(v\alpha)=g(v)\prescript{}{v\cdot}{\sigma(g)}(\alpha)\quad\forall\alpha\in\Sigma_{v}.

The maps
(\prescript{}{v\cdot}{\sigma(g)})_{v\in X^{(0)}}
form what we will call the
portrait of
g
. Portraits will be of significant importance throughout the paper.

Definition 5
.

Let
v\in\mathcal{L}_{o}^{cube}
represent a vertex other than
o
. Let
\hat{h}_{1},\dots,\hat{h}_{n}
be the collection of hyperplanes that intersect an edge incident to
v
and that separate
o
from
v
. Let
h_{1},\dots,h_{n}
denote the corresponding halfspaces containing
v
.

Let
w\in\mathcal{L}_{o}^{cube}
represent a vertex. We say
w
is of
Type 1 relative to
(o,v)
if
w
is not contained in
h_{1}\cap\dots\cap h_{n}
. We say
w
is of
Type 2 relative to
(o,v)
if
w
is contained in
h_{1}\cap\dots\cap h_{n}
and contained in the carrier of
\hat{h}_{i}
for some
i
. It is of
Type 3 relative to
(o,v)
otherwise. (See Definition
5.3
for an equivalent, but formally more useful definition.)

Definition 6
.

Let
\sigma:\Sigma_{0,o}\rightarrow\Sigma_{0}
be a label-morphism. We say that
\sigma
appears at
o
in
G_{o}
, if there exists some isometry
g\in G_{o}
such that
\prescript{}{\epsilon\cdot}{\sigma(g)}=\sigma
.

Let
v\in\mathcal{L}_{o}^{cube}
represent a vertex other than
o
and let
\sigma:\Sigma_{0,v}\rightarrow\Sigma_{0}
be a label-morphism. We say that
\sigma
appears at
v
in
G_{o}
if there exists some isometry
g\in G_{o}
such that
\prescript{}{v\cdot}{\sigma(g)}=\sigma
and
g
fixes all vertices of Type 1 and 2 relative to
(o,v)
.

Example
.

Suppose
X
is a regular tree,
\ell
an admissible edge labeling on
X
,
o
a vertex in
X
, and let
v\in\mathcal{L}_{o}^{cube}
represent a vertex other than
o
. We define
X_{v}
to be the subtree containing all the vertices whose unique geodesic to
o
contains
v
.

In this situation,
v
is of Type 2 relative to
(o,v)
and the vertices of Type 1 relative to
(o,v)
are exactly those that are not contained in
X_{v}
. If
\sigma:\Sigma_{0,v}\rightarrow\Sigma_{0}
is a label-morphism, then it is compatible with
v
if and only if it fixes the label of the only edge at
v
that is not contained in
X_{v}
. Furthermore, the label-morphism
\sigma
appears at
v
in
G_{o}
if and only if there exists an automorphism
g
of

that fixes all vertices that are not in
X_{v}
. (Readers familiar with rooted automorphisms of regular trees will quickly observe that every label-morphism that is compatible with
v
also appears at
v
in
G_{o}
.)

As can be seen from the example above, the last few definitions heuristically mean the following: A label-morphism appears at
v
in
G_{o}
, if there exists an element in
G_{o}
that performs the permutation
\sigma
on the edges emanating from
v
and fixes all vertices that are not ‘behind
v
’ from the vantage point of

. A label-morphism
\sigma
is compatible with
v
if it satisfies the basic requirements needed in order to appear at
v
in
G_{o}
. The reasons for these definitions will become clear throughout the paper.

Theorem B
.

Suppose that for every reduced
v\in\mathcal{L}_{o}^{cube}\setminus\{\epsilon\}
, every
\sigma
that is compatible with
v
also appears at
v
in
G_{o}
. Then there exists a finitely generated dense subgroup of
\text{Aut}(X)
.

Remark
.

As we will see, the assumption stated in Theorem
B
is satisfied if the action of
\Gamma
on
X
is transitive on vertices. If the action of
\Gamma
is not vertex-transitive, but cocompact, we can still find a finite set
V\subset\mathcal{L}_{o}^{cube}\setminus\{\epsilon\}
such that we only need to require for all
v\in V
that every
\sigma
compatible with
v
also appears at
v
in
G_{o}
(see Lemma
5.14
and Theorem
5.15
). It seems likely that, with the development of some additional tools to understand
\mathcal{L}_{o}^{cube}
in the non-vertex-transitive case, this assumption can either be described more concretely or removed.

We can get a more explicit version of Theorem
B
if we assume the action of
\Gamma
to be transitive on vertices. In that case, every letter in
\Sigma_{0}
appears at every vertex of
X
, that is
\Sigma_{0,v}=\Sigma_{0}
for every vertex
v
, and our label-morphisms become bijective maps
\sigma:\Sigma_{0}\rightarrow\Sigma_{0}
. In this case, we call these maps
label-isomorphisms
. We also highlight that, if the action of
\Gamma
is vertex-transitive, then one can easily show that
\Gamma
is a right-angled Artin group and
X
its Salvetti complex (see Remark
5.16
for details).

If
\Gamma
acts vertex-transitively on
X
, then we can do the following: For any vertex
o\in X^{(0)}
, any reduced word
v\in\mathcal{L}_{o}^{cube}
, and any label-isomorphism
\sigma
that is compatible with
v
, we can construct an element
A_{v,\sigma}\in G_{o}
such that
\prescript{}{v\cdot}{A_{v,\sigma}}=\sigma
and
A_{v,\sigma}
fixes all vertices in
X
that are of Type 1 or 2 relative to
(o,v)
. These automorphisms allow us to build a topologically generating set in the sense of the following Theorem.

Theorem C
.

Suppose
\Gamma
acts vertex-transitively on
X
. Let
S
be a finite generating set of
\Gamma
such that
S^{-1}=S
. The set

S\cup\{A_{o,\sigma}|\sigma\text{ a label-isomorphism}\}\cup\{A_{\alpha,\sigma}|\alpha\in\Sigma,\sigma\text{ compatible with }\alpha\}

generates a dense subgroup of
\text{Aut}(X)
.

We now turn to the size of the automorphism group. The size of
\text{Aut}(X)
depends significantly on the ‘fractally self-repeating nature’ of
\mathcal{L}_{o}^{cube}
. Since
\text{Aut}(X)
is a second countable, locally compact group, it is non-discrete if and only if it is uncountable. (One direction of this equivalence uses Baire’s category theorem, the other simply follows from second countability of
\text{Aut}(X)
.) Thus, the size of
\text{Aut}(X)
can be characterised in terms of whether it is discrete or not.

Theorem D
.

Suppose there exists some
o\in X^{(0)}
such that for all reduced
v\in\mathcal{L}_{o}^{cube}\setminus\{\epsilon\}
, every
\sigma
that is compatible with
v
also appears at
v
in
G_{o}
. Then exactly one of the following holds:

(1)

\text{Aut}(X)
is finitely generated. Furthermore, all vertex-stabilizers are finite.

(2)

\text{Aut}(X)
is non-discrete.

In particular, the vertex-stabilizers are either uncountable or finite.

In the vertex-transitive case, there is a characterisation when
\text{Aut}(X)
is non-discrete entirely in terms of
\Gamma
. This characterisation has been proven independently in
[
Tay17
]
. Unfortunately, this work seems to be unpublished and is difficult to get a hand on, so we provide the result including our proof using the technology developed in this paper.

Theorem E

(cf.
[
Tay17
]
)
.

Suppose the action
\Gamma\curvearrowright X
is vertex-transitive. Then the following are equivalent:

(1)

The automorphism-group
\text{Aut}(X)
is non-discrete.

(2)

There exist some distinct
s,t\in\Sigma_{0}
such that
s\neq t^{-1}
and
[s,t]\neq 1
.

(3)

\Gamma
is not free abelian.

1.3.
The subgroup
\text{Aut}^{+}(X)
and simplicity

Our remaining results require some additional assumptions. A
\CAT
cube complex
X
is called
reducible
, if it is isomorphic to a non-trivial product. It is called
irreducible
, if it is not reducible. We say
X
is
essential
, if every halfspace
h
in
X
contains points that are arbitrarily far away from its bounding hyperplane
\hat{h}
. A group
G<\text{Aut}(X)
is called
elementary
, if its limit set
\Lambda(G)
has at most two points. It is called
non-elementary
, if it is not elementary. We will require that
X
is irreducible and essential and that
\text{Aut}(X)
is non-elementary. Note that, if
\text{Aut}(X)
is non-elementary, then
X
cannot be isometric to the real line.

We define
\text{Aut}^{+}(X)
to be the subgroup generated by all elements in
\text{Aut}(X)
that fix some halfspace of
X
pointwise. We emphasize that we do not take the closure and
\text{Aut}^{+}(X)
may not be closed in general. One easily checks that
\text{Aut}^{+}(X)
is a normal subgroup of
\text{Aut}(X)
, because conjugation sends halfspace-fixators to halfspace-fixators. Thus, this group is bound to appear when studying questions of simplicity in the automorphism group.

Remark
.

Both the literature on automorphisms of right-angled buildings (e.g.
[
Cap14
,
DMSS18
,
DMS19
,
BDM21
,
BDM23
]
) and the literature on automorphisms of
\CAT
cube complexes (e.g.
[
Laz18
]
) have a group denoted
\text{Aut}^{+}(X)
. However, the definitions of this group in these two bodies of literature are distinct and likely do not coincide outside of some special cases. Both notions of
\text{Aut}^{+}(X)
are generalisations of the definition given by Tits
[
Tit70
]
, who studied the case where
X
is a tree. It is worth noting that
\text{Aut}^{+}(X)
enjoys similar properties in both cases, although some properties are significantly harder to prove in one context than the other. Throughout this paper, we work with the definition given above, which is taken from
[
Laz18
]
and is commonly used for automorphisms of
\CAT
cube complexes.

In
[
Laz18
, Appendix A]
, Lazarovich provides a sufficient condition for
\text{Aut}^{+}(X)
to be simple. We can combine his work with our results on non-discreteness to prove the following theorem.

Theorem F
.

Suppose
X
is irreducible and essential, suppose
\text{Aut}(X)
is non-elementary, and suppose that the action
\Gamma\curvearrowright X
is vertex-transitive.

Suppose that for every pair
s,t\in\Sigma_{0}
such that
[s,t]=1
, there exists
r\in\Sigma_{0}\setminus\{s^{-1},t^{-1}\}
such that
[s,r]\neq 1
and
[t,r]\neq 1
. Then
\text{Aut}^{+}(X)
is a non-discrete, simple, totally disconnected group.

Remark
.

Suppose
\Gamma
and
X
are as in the theorem above. Using Theorem
A
, one easily checks that, because
\Gamma
acts vertex-transitively on
X
, there exists a finite graph
L
such that
\Gamma
is the right-angled Artin group induced by
L
and
X
is its corresponding Salvetti complex (cf. Remark
5.16
). One can now reformulate the condition on labels required in Theorem
F
as follows: Suppose that for every edge
e
in
L
there exists a vertex that is not adjacent to either endpoint of
e
. Then
\text{Aut}^{+}(X)
is non-discrete, simple, and totally disconnected. (Note that
L
cannot be a single isolated vertex, as
\text{Aut}(X)
is assumed to be non-elementary.)

One of the reasons why simple, non-discrete, tdlc groups are of interest is their relevance in developing a structure theory of tdlc groups (see
[
CRW17a
,
CRW17b
]
for an in-depth discussion of current developments on this subject). Since
\text{Aut}(X)
is tdlc whenever
X
is locally finite and its non-discreteness can be understood in terms of Theorems
D
and
E
, the groups we are studying are an interesting class of examples to consider in the context of such a structure theory. Theorem
F
tells us that, under some extra conditions,
\text{Aut}^{+}(X)
is a simple, non-discrete, totally disconnected subgroup of
\text{Aut}(X)
. However, it is not a-priori obvious that
\text{Aut}^{+}(X)
is locally compact. The following result adresses this.

Theorem G
.

Suppose that the action
\Gamma\curvearrowright X
is vertex-transitive and suppose there exists some
s\in\Sigma_{0}
such that for any two distinct
t,r\in\Sigma_{0}
such that
[s,t]=[s,r]=1
, we have either
t=r^{-1}
or
[t,r]=1
. Then
\text{Aut}^{+}(X)
is locally compact.

Remark
.

Let
L
be a finite graph,
\Gamma
its induced right-angled Artin group, and
X
the corresponding Salvetti complex. The condition required from
\Sigma_{0}
in the theorem above can again be formulated as a property of
L
. Namely,
\text{Aut}^{+}(X)
is locally compact if there exists a vertex
v
in
L
such that all vertices adjacent to
v
are adjacent to each other. In particular, if
L
contains an isolated vertex, or a vertex of degree one, then
\text{Aut}^{+}(X)
is locally compact.

While this provides a sufficient condition for
\text{Aut}^{+}(X)
to be locally compact, it seems likely that one can obtain local compactness results for more examples by further enhancing the methods developed in this paper.

With
\text{Aut}^{+}(X)\triangleleft\text{Aut}(X)
being a simple subgroup, a naturally arising question is how big
\text{Aut}^{+}(X)
is inside
\text{Aut}(X)
. Lazarovich showed in
[

]
that
\text{Aut}^{+}(X)
has finite index in
\text{Aut}(X)
in some specific cases. We obtain a sufficient condition for the closure of
\text{Aut}^{+}(X)
to have finite index in
\text{Aut}(X)
.

Definition 7
.

Let
L
be a graph. We say
L
is flexibly vertex-transitive, if for all vertices

in
L
, there exists a vertex
u
and a graph-automorphism
\varphi\in\text{Aut}(L)
such that
\varphi
fixes
u
and all its neighbours and
\varphi(v)=w
.

See section

for a discussion of flexible vertex-transitivity. For now, we simply point out that flexible vertex-transitivity is a very strong condition that is rarely satisfied.

Since we assume that
\Gamma
acts vertex-transitively on

, the links at the vertices of

are all canonically isomorphic. We denote by
\mathrm{lk}(X)
the link at any vertex of
X
.

Theorem H
.

Suppose
\Gamma
acts vertex-transitively on
X
and suppose the
1
-skeleton of
\mathrm{lk}(X)
is flexibly vertex-transitive. Then
\overline{\text{Aut}^{+}(X)}
has finite index in
\text{Aut}(X)
.

This provides us with a sufficient condition for the closure of
\text{Aut}^{+}(X)
to be large in
\text{Aut}(X)
. On the other hand, we can present a relatively basic example in which
\overline{\text{Aut}^{+}(X)}
is non-trivial, even uncountable and non-discrete, yet not even cocompact in
\text{Aut}(X)
. This answers a question by Haglund. Our example is as follows: Let
\Gamma_{CK}
be the right-angled Artin group defined by

\Gamma_{CK}:=\langle a,b,c,d|[a,b]=[b,c]=[c,d]=1\rangle.

Let

be the Salvetti-complex of

Theorem I
.

The quotient
{\mathchoice{\raisebox{3.75pt}{$\displaystyle{\text{Aut}(X_{CK})}$}\mkern-5.0mu\diagup\mkern-4.0mu\raisebox{-2.83888pt}{$\displaystyle{\overline{\text{Aut}^{+}(X_{CK})}}$}}{\raisebox{3.75pt}{$\textstyle{\text{Aut}(X_{CK})}$}\mkern-5.0mu\diagup\mkern-4.0mu\raisebox{-2.83888pt}{$\textstyle{\overline{\text{Aut}^{+}(X_{CK})}}$}}{\raisebox{2.625pt}{$\scriptstyle{\text{Aut}(X_{CK})}$}\mkern-5.0mu\diagup\mkern-4.0mu\raisebox{-2.83888pt}{$\scriptstyle{\overline{\text{Aut}^{+}(X_{CK})}}$}}{\raisebox{1.875pt}{$\scriptscriptstyle{\text{Aut}(X_{CK})}$}\mkern-5.0mu\diagup\mkern-4.0mu\raisebox{-2.83888pt}{$\scriptscriptstyle{\overline{\text{Aut}^{+}(X_{CK})}}$}}}
is not compact, while
\text{Aut}^{+}(X_{CK})

Remark
.

We point out that

does not satisfy the condition on commuting pairs

required in Theorem
F
. However, this condition is only required to prove that
\text{Aut}(X_{CK})
acts faithfully on

1.4.
About the proofs

Key to our proofs is our ability to describe the stabilizer subgroups
G_{o}
and generate them by elements that enjoy useful properties. We do so by noticing that
G_{o}
canonically embeds into the automorphism group of a particular rooted tree.

The language
\mathcal{L}_{o}^{cube}
carries the structure of a prefix tree (because this language is regular, see Proposition
2.15
). That is, we obtain a rooted tree
T_{o}^{cube}
with root
\epsilon
, whose vertices are given by
\mathcal{L}_{o}^{cube}
such that
v
,
w
are connected by an edge if and only if
w=v\alpha
for some
\alpha
in
\Sigma
. (This is called the
path language tree
and it is a standard construction that can be done for any regular language.) Since any automorphism
g\in G_{o}
fixes
o
and sends cube paths to cube paths, it induces a rooted tree-automorphism on
T_{o}^{cube}
. This provides us with a canonical embedding
G_{o}<\text{Aut}(T_{o}^{cube})
(see Lemma
3.1
).

Our key technical result is a description of
G_{o}
as a subgroup of
\text{Aut}(T_{o}^{cube})
. For this, we use that rooted tree-automorphisms can be described in terms of a family of bijections.

Definition 8
.

Let
\mathcal{L}
be a regular language over the alphabet
\Sigma
. For all
v\in\mathcal{L}
, we denote

\Sigma(v):=\{\alpha\in\Sigma|v\alpha\in\mathcal{L}\}.

A portrait in a language
\mathcal{L}
is a family of maps
(\prescript{}{v\cdot}{\sigma})_{v\in\mathcal{L}}
such that
\prescript{}{v\cdot}{\sigma}:\Sigma(v)\rightarrow\Sigma
is injective for every
v\in\mathcal{L}
. For any portrait, we define the map
\sigma:\mathcal{L}\rightarrow\Sigma^{*}
by

\sigma(\alpha_{1}\dots\alpha_{n}):=\prescript{}{\epsilon\cdot}{\sigma}(\alpha_{1})\dots\prescript{}{\alpha_{1}\dots\alpha_{i-1}\cdot}{\sigma}(\alpha_{i})\dots\prescript{}{\alpha_{1}\dots\alpha_{n-1}\cdot}{\sigma}(\alpha_{n}).

Denote the path language tree corresponding to
\mathcal{L}
by
T
. We say a portrait
defines an automorphism on
T
if
\sigma
is the vertex-map of a rooted automorphism on
T
. (Since rooted automorphisms on
T
are uniquely determined by what they do on vertices,
\sigma
determines the entire automorphism.) We will discuss in section
2.5
how to check whether a portrait defines an automorphism on
T
.

Given an element
g\in G_{o}
, its portrait
(\prescript{}{v\cdot}{\sigma(g)})_{v\in V(T_{o}^{cube})}
defined earlier is exactly the portrait in
\mathcal{L}_{o}^{cube}
that recovers
g
as an element in
\text{Aut}(T_{o}^{cube})
. Thus, this notion of a portrait on a tree is compatible with the notion of portrait we defined previously for elements
g\in G_{o}
.

Definition 9
.

A portrait in the language
\mathcal{L}_{o}^{cube}
is called
admissible
if the following two conditions hold:

•

For every pair
v,w\in\mathcal{L}_{o}^{cube}
such that
v\equiv_{o}w
, we have
\prescript{}{v\cdot}{\sigma}=\prescript{}{w\cdot}{\sigma}
.

•

For every
v\in\mathcal{L}_{o}^{cube}
, the restriction of
\prescript{}{v\cdot}{\sigma}
to
\Sigma_{0,v}
is a label-morphism and
\prescript{}{v\cdot}{\sigma}
is the canonical extension of that label-morphism to
\Sigma_{v}
.

Definition 10
.

Suppose
(\sigma_{v})_{v\in\mathcal{L}_{o}^{cube}}
is an admissible portrait that defines an automorphism on
T_{o}^{cube}
. We define the following two properties that this family may satisfy:

(par)

For all
v\in\mathcal{L}_{o}^{cube}
, for all non-isolated
s\in\Sigma_{0,v}
, and for all
\beta\in\Sigma_{v}
such that
[\beta,s]=1
, we have

\prescript{}{v\cdot}{\sigma(g)}(\beta)=\prescript{}{vs\cdot}{\sigma(g)}(\beta).

(inv)

For all
\alpha_{1}\dots\alpha_{n}\in\mathcal{L}_{o}^{cube}
and all
s\in\alpha_{n}
, we have

\prescript{}{\alpha_{1}\dots\alpha_{n}\cdot}{\sigma(g)}(s^{-1})=\prescript{}{\alpha_{1}\dots\tilde{\alpha}_{n}\cdot}{\sigma(g)}(s)^{-1},

where
\tilde{\alpha}_{n}:=\alpha_{n}\setminus\{s\}
. (If
\tilde{\alpha}_{n}
is empty, then
\alpha_{1}\dots\tilde{\alpha}_{n}=\alpha_{1}\dots\alpha_{n-1}
.)

Theorem J
.

Let
o\in X^{(0)}
and let
(\prescript{}{v\cdot}{\sigma})_{v\in\mathcal{L}_{o}^{cube}}
be a portrait in
\mathcal{L}_{o}^{cube}
. There exists some
g\in G_{o}
such that
(\prescript{}{v\cdot}{\sigma})_{v\in\mathcal{L}_{o}^{cube}}
is the portrait of
g
if and only if
(\prescript{}{v\cdot}{\sigma})_{v\in\mathcal{L}_{o}^{cube}}
is admissible, defines an automorphism on
T_{o}^{cube}
, and satisfies
par
and
inv
.

The characterisation of stabilizer elements as portraits that are admissible, define an automorphism on
T_{o}^{cube}
, and satisfy
par
and
inv
, allows us to work and reason with the local behaviour of automorphisms. In addition, it enables us to explicitly construct useful stabilizer elements and do computations with them. This is the key to most of our results.

The remainder of the paper is structured as follows. In section
2
, we establish all the necessary knowledge on rooted tree-automorphisms,
\CAT
cube complexes, and their edge labelings. In particular, we discuss properties of the language
\mathcal{L}_{o}^{cube}
and define the language
\mathcal{L}_{o}
induced by normal cube paths. In section 3, we discuss the stabilizer subgroups

and prove Theorem

. In section

, we prove Theorem
A
. In section

, we produce generators of dense subgroups of the stabilizer subgroups

and use them to produce finitely generated dense subgroups of
\text{Aut}(X)
, proving Theorems
B
and
C
. In section
6
, we prove Theorems
D
and
E
. In section
7
, we move to the subgroup
\text{Aut}^{+}(X)
, prove its simplicity, and study its size in
\text{Aut}(X)
, which yields Theorems

,
G
,

, and
I
.

2.
Encodings of
\mathrm{CAT(0)}
cube complexes

Throughout this article we are going to use the following notation:

•

X
is a locally finite
\CAT
cube complex with vertex set
X^{(0)}
and
o\in X^{(0)}
is a basepoint.

•

Automorphisms of
X
are cubical isometries from
X
to itself and we denote the group of automorphisms on
X
by
\text{Aut}(X)
.

•

We denote by
\mathfrak{h}(X)
the set of halfspaces in
X
and by
\widehat{\mathfrak{h}}(X)
the set of hyperplanes. Given
h\in\mathfrak{h}(X)
we denote by
\widehat{h}
the bounding hyperplane and by
h^{c}
the opposite halfspace. Given a cube
C
we denote by
\widehat{\mathfrak{h}}(C)
the set of hyperplanes which intersect
C
. We say that
\widehat{h}_{1},\widehat{h}_{2}\in\widehat{\mathfrak{h}}(X)

intersect transversally
and write
\widehat{h}_{1}\pitchfork\widehat{h}_{2}
if they intersect and are not equal.

•

We denote by
\vec{\mathcal{D}}(X)
the set of
oriented diagonals
of cubes in
X
. If
v
and
w
are opposite vertices on a cube
C
, then we denote by
(v,C,w)
the oriented diagonal of
C
from
v
to
w
and refer to
C
as its
underlying cube
. There is a fixpoint free involution on
\vec{\mathcal{D}}
given by
(v,C,w)^{-1}:=(w,C,v)
. We refer to the corresponding orbit
[v,C,w]
as a
diagonal
and denote the set of diagonals by

\mathcal{D}(X)
.

•

\vec{\mathcal{E}}(X)\subset\vec{\mathcal{D}}(X)
denotes the subset of oriented edges (i.e. oriented diagonals of
1
-dimensional cubes) and
\mathcal{E}(X)\subset\mathcal{D}(X)
denotes the corresponding subset of geometric edges. Given an oriented edge
e=(v,C,w)
we denote by
h(e)
the unique halfspace containing
w
, but not
v
. We then denote by
\widehat{h}(e)
the corresponding hyperplane. If
[e]
is the corresponding geometric edge, then we set

\widehat{h}([e]):=\widehat{h}(e)=\widehat{h}(e^{-1}).

•

Two oriented edges
e,e^{\prime}
are
parallel
, if
h(e)=h(e^{\prime})
. We call the resulting equivalence classes
oriented parallel classes
. Oriented parallel classes are in 1–1 correspondence with
\mathfrak{h}(X)
and – for our purposes – they are a useful generalisation of halfspaces to general cube complexes.

•

If
S
denotes a cube complex, we abuse notation and denote the set of oriented parallel classes by
\mathfrak{h}(S)
. Similarly, we write
h(e)
for the oriented parallel class containing an oriented edge
e
and
h^{c}
for the oriented parallel class obtained by reversing the orientation of all elements of
h\in\mathfrak{h}(S)
.

•

We denote by
\Gamma_{\mathcal{D}}(X)
the graph with vertex set
X^{(0)}
and oriented edge set
\vec{\mathcal{D}}(X)
with source and target map given by
s(v,C,w)=v
and
t(v,C,w)=w
. There is a natural immersion
|\Gamma_{\mathcal{D}}(X)|\looparrowright X
obtained by sending each edge in
|\Gamma_{\mathcal{D}}(X)|
to the corresponding diagonal in
X
.

•

A path in
\Gamma_{\mathcal{D}}(X)
is called a
cube path
in
X
; if all underlying cubes are
1
-dimensional, then it is called an
edge path
. We denote by
D(X)
and
E(X)
the collections of all cube paths and edge path respectively. We consider
\vec{\mathcal{D}}(X)
and
\vec{\mathcal{E}}(X)
as subsets of
D(X)
by considering oriented diagonals as cube paths of length one.

•

Using the immersion
|\Gamma_{\mathcal{D}}(X)|\looparrowright X
, cube paths correspond to curves in
X
which move exclusively along edges and diagonals, and edge paths correspond to curves in the
1
-skeleton of
X
. It thus makes sense to say that a cube path intersects a hyperplane in
X
.

•

We abbreviate a cube path
((v,C_{1},v_{1}),\dots,(v_{n-1},C_{n},w))\in D(X)
by
(v,C_{1},\dots,C_{n},w)
and refer to
(C_{1},\dots,C_{n})
as the
underlying sequence of cubes
.
We then denote by
\widehat{\mathfrak{h}}(v,C_{1},\dots,C_{n},w)
the set of hyperplanes which intersect at least one of the cubes
C_{1},\dots,C_{n}
.

•

If
v\in X^{(0)}
is a vertex, then we denote by
D(X)_{v}
the collection of all cube paths emanating from
v
. The subsets
E(X)_{v}
,
\vec{\mathcal{D}}(X)_{v}
,
\vec{\mathcal{E}}(X)_{v}
are defined accordingly.

2.1.
Labelings of
\mathrm{CAT(0)}
cube complexes

Let
\Sigma_{0}
be a finite set with a fixpoint free involution
s\mapsto s^{-1}
.

Definition 2.1
.

Let
S
be a cube complex. A map
\ell:\mathfrak{h}(S)\to\Sigma_{0}
is
an
oriented parallel class labeling
of
S
if

\ell(h^{c})=\ell(h)^{-1}\quad\text{for all }h\in\mathfrak{h}(S).

If
S
is
\CAT
, we also call this a
half-space labeling
. Either way, the
induced edge labeling
is defined as

\ell:\vec{\mathcal{E}}(S)\to\Sigma_{0},\quad\ell(e):=\ell(h(e)).

We will be interested in a special class of induced edge labelings. To define these we introduce the following notion:

Definition 2.2
.

We say that two geometric edges
[e],[e^{\prime}]

span a square
if they have precisely one endpoint in common and if

\widehat{h}([e])\pitchfork\widehat{h}([e^{\prime}])
. We say that two oriented edges span a square if the underlying geometric edges span a square.

Definition 2.3
.

A map
\ell:\vec{\mathcal{E}}(S)\to\Sigma_{0}
is called an
admissible edge labeling
if the following hold:

(A1)

\ell
is induced from an oriented parallel class labeling.

(A2)

\ell|_{\vec{\mathcal{E}}(S)_{v}}
is injective for every
v\in S^{(0)}
.

(A3)

For all
v,w\in S^{(0)}
,
e_{1},e_{2}\in\vec{\mathcal{E}}(S)_{v}
, and
e^{\prime}_{1},e^{\prime}_{2}\in\vec{\mathcal{E}}(S)_{w}
, the following holds: If
\ell(e_{1})=\ell(e_{1}^{\prime})
and
\ell(e_{2})=\ell(e_{2}^{\prime})
, then
e_{1}
and
e_{2}
span a square if and only if
e_{1}^{\prime}
and
e_{2}^{\prime}
span a square.

We then refer to the pair
(S,\ell)
as a
\Sigma_{0}
-labeled cube complex
.

If a group
\Gamma
acts by automorphisms on
S
, then an admissible edge labeling is called
\Gamma
-invariant
if
\ell(g\cdot e)=\ell(e)
for all
e\in\vec{\mathcal{E}}(S)
.

We want to give a sufficient condition for the existence of an admissible labeling. As we will see later, this sufficient condition is also necessary. To describe this, we need the notion of special cube complexes introduced by Haglund and Wise in
[
HW07
]
. We use the terminology from
[
Wis21
, Chapter 6.b]
regarding special cube complexes.

Lemma 2.4
.

Let
X
be the universal covering of a compact, non-positively curved special cube complex
S
with fundamental group
\Gamma
. Then there exists a
\Gamma
-invariant admissible edge labeling on
X
.

Proof.

Our strategy is to put a labeling on the oriented parallel classes in
S
which lifts to an admissible labeling on
X
. As the lift of a labeling in
S
, this will be
\Gamma
-invariant.

Since
S
is compact, we can choose a sufficiently large finite set
\Sigma_{0}
and an involution
\cdot^{-1}
on
\Sigma_{0}
such that there exists an injective map
\ell:\mathfrak{h}(S)\rightarrow\Sigma_{0}
satisfying
\ell(h^{c})=\ell(h)^{-1}
for all
h\in\mathfrak{h}(S)
. Since all hyperplanes in
S
are two-sided (that is, every hyperplane induces exactly two oriented parallel classes),
\cdot^{-1}
is fixpoint free. We denote the induced edge labeling by
\ell
as well and the lift of
\ell
to
X
by
\tilde{\ell}
. By construction
\tilde{\ell}
satisfies (A1), as
\ell
lifts both as a labeling of oriented parallel classes and as an edge labeling and the two lifts are compatible with each other. We are left to show that
\tilde{\ell}
satisfies (A2) and (A3).

We first show that, if
\ell
satisfies (A2) and (A3), then so does
\tilde{\ell}
. For (A2) this is obvious, as it is a local property and
X
is a covering of
S
. For (A3), suppose we have vertices
v,w\in X^{(0)}
,
e_{1},e_{2}\in\vec{\mathcal{E}}(X)_{v}
, and
e^{\prime}_{1},e^{\prime}_{2}\in\vec{\mathcal{E}}(X)_{w}
such that
\tilde{\ell}(e_{1})=\tilde{\ell}(e^{\prime}_{1})
and
\tilde{\ell}(e_{2})=\tilde{\ell}(e^{\prime}_{2})
. Denoting the projection map by
p:X\rightarrow S
, this implies that
\ell(p(e_{1}))=\ell(p(e^{\prime}_{1}))
and
\ell(p(e_{2}))=\ell(p(e^{\prime}_{2}))
. Suppose that
e_{1},e_{2}
span a square, while
e^{\prime}_{1}
and
e^{\prime}_{2}
do not. Since
p
is a covering map, this implies that
p(e_{1}),p(e_{2})
span a square, while
p(e^{\prime}_{1}),p(e^{\prime}_{2})
do not. We see that, if
\tilde{\ell}
does not satisfy (A3), neither does
\ell
. Therefore, it is sufficient to show that
\ell
satisfies (A2) and (A3) to conclude that
\tilde{\ell}
is admissible.

We first show (A2). Let
v\in S^{(0)}
and suppose there exist edges
e_{1},e_{2}\in\vec{\mathcal{E}}(X)_{v}
such that
\ell(e_{1})=\ell(e_{2})
. Since
\ell
is injective on oriented parallel classes, we conclude that
h(e_{1})=h(e_{2})
. This means that the hyperplane
\hat{h}(e_{1})
self-osculates which contradicts the assumption that
S
is special. We conclude that
\ell
satisfies (A2).

We are left to show (A3). Let
v,w\in S^{(0)}
,
e_{1},e_{2}\in\vec{\mathcal{E}}(X)_{v}
, and
e^{\prime}_{1},e^{\prime}_{2}\in\vec{\mathcal{E}}(X)_{w}
such that
\ell(e_{1})=\ell(e^{\prime}_{1})
and
\ell(e_{2})=\ell(e^{\prime}_{2})
. Since
\ell
is injective on halfspaces by assumption, this implies that
e_{1}
and
e^{\prime}_{1}
cross the same hyperplane in the same direction and analogously for
e_{2}
and
e^{\prime}_{2}
. Let
\hat{h}=\hat{h}(e_{1})
and
\hat{k}=\hat{h}(e_{2})
and suppose that
e_{1},e_{2}
span a square, while
e^{\prime}_{1}
and
e^{\prime}_{2}
do not. This means that
\hat{h}
and
\hat{k}
interosculate, which is ruled out by the fact that
S
is special. We conclude that
\ell
satisfies (A3). This proves that its lift
\tilde{\ell}
is a
\Gamma
-invariant admissible edge labeling.
∎

Remark 2.10

Proposition 2.15
.

Suppose a subgroup
\Gamma<\text{Aut}(X)
acts cocompactly on

and let
\ell

\Gamma
-invariant admissible edge labeling. Then the language
\mathcal{L}^{\mathrm{cube}}_{o}

Proof.

We define an equivalence relation on

as follows: If

are two vertices, then

and

are equivalent, if

. We denote by

Now let

be the following finite automaton: The underlying alphabet is

, the set of states is given by

defined as follows:

At a state
\Gamma v

\alpha\in\Sigma_{v}
is accepted (the set
\Sigma_{v}
does not depend on the choice of representative, as
\Sigma_{gv}=\Sigma_{v}
for all

is the equivalence class of the endpoint of the diagonal with label

emanating at

One easily checks that this automaton accepts all words in

is accepted by

and thus there exists a diagonal

emanating from

This proposition explains the terminology of commuting labels. The next Lemma generalises a standard result about edge paths in

Definition 2.19
.

Let
\alpha_{1}\dots\alpha_{n}\in\mathcal{L}_{o}
. A pair
\alpha_{i},\alpha_{j}
(with
i<j
)
contains an innermost cancellation
if and only if there exists a hyperplane that is crossed by
\alpha_{i}
and
\alpha_{j}
in opposite directions and the path-segments
\alpha_{i}\dots\alpha_{j-1}
and
\alpha_{i+1}\dots\alpha_{j}
both do not cross any hyperplane twice.

Remark 2.20
.

Any cube path
\gamma
that crosses some hyperplane more than once contains an innermost cancellation. One may start with any hyperplane
\hat{h}
that is crossed twice by
\gamma
. If it does not produce an innermost cancellation, there has to be another hyperplane
\hat{h}_{new}
that crosses
\gamma
twice on a segment of
\gamma
between two places where
\gamma
crosses
\hat{h}
. (One of the two crossings of
\hat{h}_{new}
may happen at the same place where
\gamma
crosses
\hat{h}
.) We replace
\hat{h}
by
\hat{h}_{new}
. After finitely many iterations, this yields an innermost cancellation.

If
\alpha_{i},\alpha_{j}
contains an innermost cancellation, then there exists a labeling pair
s,s^{-1}\in\Sigma_{0}
that corresponds to the hyperplane being crossed by
\alpha_{i}
and
\alpha_{j}
. In particular,
s^{-1}\in\alpha_{i}
and
s\in\alpha_{j}
(or vice-versa).

.

Let
\alpha_{1}\dots\alpha_{n}\in\mathcal{L}_{o}

Definition 2.22

(Niblo-Reeves)
.

A cube path
(v,C_{1},\dots,C_{n},w)
is
normal
if it does not cross any hyperplane more than once and if for all
i\in\{1,\dots,n-1\}
there exists no hyperplane
\widehat{h}\in\widehat{\mathfrak{h}}(C_{i+1})
which intersects all hyperplanes in
\widehat{\mathfrak{h}}(C_{i})
transversally.

The indicated cube path is not a normal cube path. However, the reverse path from

to

is a normal cube path.

Remark 2.23
.

Niblo and Reeves’ original definition of normal cube paths in
[
NR98
, Section 3]
does not require that the path crosses every hyperplane at most once. Instead of this condition, they require that for all
1\leq i\leq n-1
,
\widehat{\mathfrak{h}}(C_{i})\cap\widehat{\mathfrak{h}}(C_{i+1})=\emptyset
and then they implicitly show that their definition is equivalent to the one given above.

The set of normal cube paths in

will be denoted by
N(X)\subset D(X)
and for

we define
N(X)_{o}:=N(X)\cap D(X)_{o}
.
We define the

\mathcal{L}_{o}:=\ell(N(X)_{o})\subset\mathcal{L}^{\mathrm{cube}}_{o}.

Remark 2.24
.

Given a word
\alpha_{1}\dots\alpha_{n}\in\Sigma^{*}
, one can easily verify from the definition, together with Remark
2.23
, that

\alpha_{1}\dots\alpha_{n}\in\mathcal{L}_{o}\Leftrightarrow\forall 1\leq i\leq n-1,\forall s\in\alpha_{i+1}:[\alpha_{i},s]\neq 1\text{ and }s^{-1}\notin\alpha_{i}.

Lemma 2.25

(Niblo–Reeves,
[
NR98
]
)
.

Suppose there is a subgroup
\Gamma<\text{Aut}(X)
that acts cocompactly on
X
and let
\ell
be a
\Gamma
-invariant admissible edge-labeling. The language
\mathcal{L}_{o}
of normal cube paths is regular, i.e. it is the language recognized by a finite automaton.

Remark 2.26

(Normalization)
.

By
[
NR98
]
, every cube path is
\equiv
-equivalent to a unique normal cube path. This shows that we can identify
N(X)_{o}
with
X^{(0)}
and hence obtain a bijective encoding
\mathcal{L}_{o}\to X^{(0)}
. Moreover, there are
normalization operations

\mathrm{norm}:D(X)_{o}\to N(X)_{o}\quad\text{and}\quad\mathrm{norm}:\mathcal{L}^{\mathrm{cube}}_{o}\to\mathcal{L}_{o},

which map every cube path (or word in the cube path language) to the unique
\equiv_{o}
-equivalent normal cube path (or word in the normal cube path language).
This allows us to define a partial multiplication on
\bigcup_{o\in X^{(0)}}\mathcal{L}_{o}
as follows:

\forall o\in X^{(0)},v_{1}\in\mathcal{L}_{o},v_{2}\in\mathcal{L}_{v_{1}}:v_{1}\ast v_{2}:=\mathrm{norm}(v_{1}v_{2})

This partial multiplication is associative and defines a groupoid.

To describe the partial multiplication explicitly, it suffices to compute
v\ast s
for
s\in\Sigma_{0,v}
. For this we need the following observation:

Lemma 2.27

(Double crossing)
.

Let
v=\alpha_{1}\dots\alpha_{n}\in\mathcal{L}_{o}
and
s\in\Sigma_{0,v}
. Suppose
vs
crosses some hyperplane more than once. Then there exists some
k\in\{1,\dots,n\}
such that
s^{-1}\in\alpha_{k}
and
[s,\alpha_{i}]=1
for all
i>k
.

Proof.

Since
v
is a normal cube path, the multi-crossing in
vs
cannot occur within
v
. We conclude that the only hyperplane that can be crossed more than once by
vs
is the hyperplane that is crossed in the last step, when passing through the edge labeled by
s
. We denote this hyperplane by
\hat{h}(s)
. If
vs
crosses
\hat{h}(s)
more than once, then
v
has to cross the hyperplane
\hat{h}(s)
. Since
v
is a normal cube path, it can cross
\hat{h}(s)
exactly once and in opposite direction from the crossing in the last edge of
vs
. We conclude that
s^{-1}\in\alpha_{k}
for some
k
.

By construction,
\alpha_{k},s
is an innermost cancellation. By Lemma
2.21
, we conclude that for every

and every

,

(see also figure

). This proves the Lemma.

.

(4)

Suppose

and let
k\in\{1,\dots,n\}
be the least integer such that
[s,\alpha_{i}]=1
for all
i\geq k
. If
s^{-1}\notin\alpha_{k-1}
, then
v\ast s=\alpha_{1}\dots\tilde{\alpha}_{k}\dots\alpha_{n}
, where
\tilde{\alpha}_{k}:=\alpha_{k}\cup\{s\}
.

Accordingly, the word length of
v\ast s
over
\Sigma
can be
n-1
,
n
, or
n+1
.

Proof.

Let
\hat{h}(s)
denote the hyperplane crossed by the path
s\in\mathcal{L}_{v}^{cube}
. In statement (1), we suppose that
[s,\alpha_{n}]\neq 1
and
s^{-1}\notin\alpha_{n}
. This implies that
vs
cannot cross any hyperplanes twice (otherwise there would be a contradiction to Lemma
2.27
). Furthermore,
vs
satisfies the second condition required of normal cube paths, because
\alpha_{1}\dots\alpha_{n}
does so and
[s,\alpha_{n}]\neq 1
. Thus,
vs
is a normal cube path.

In statement (2), the path
vs
crosses the hyperplane
\hat{h}(s)
twice, once in the segment labeled
\alpha_{n}
and once in the segment labeled
s
. Using canceling of labels, we see that

\alpha_{1}\dots\alpha_{n}s\equiv_{o}\alpha_{1}\dots\alpha_{n-1}\tilde{\alpha}_{n},

where
\tilde{\alpha}_{n}=\alpha_{n}\setminus\{s^{-1}\}
. The path
\alpha_{1}\dots\tilde{\alpha}_{n}
crosses the same hyperplanes as
\alpha_{1}\dots\alpha_{n}
, except for
\hat{h}(s)
. Furthermore, it crosses every hyperplane at most once and it is a normal cube path, as we still have for every
t\in\tilde{\alpha}_{n}\subset\alpha_{n}
that
[t,\alpha_{n-1}]\neq 1
. Thus, it is the unique normal cube path equivalent to
vs
.

In statement (3), the path
vs
crosses
\hat{h}(s)
twice, once in the segment labeled
\alpha_{k-1}
and once in the segment labeled
s
. Since
[s,\alpha_{i}]=1
for every
i\geq k
and
s^{-1}\in\alpha_{k-1}
, we know that

\alpha_{1}\dots\alpha_{n}s\equiv\alpha_{1}\dots\alpha_{k-1}s\alpha_{k}\dots\alpha_{n}\equiv\alpha_{1}\dots\tilde{\alpha}_{k-1}\dots\alpha_{n},

where
\tilde{\alpha}_{k-1}:=\alpha_{k-1}\setminus\{s^{-1}\}
. The path
\alpha_{1}\dots\tilde{\alpha}_{k-1}\dots\alpha_{n}
crosses the same hyperplanes as
v
, except for
\hat{h}(s)
, and it crosses them exactly once. In order to show that
\alpha_{1}\dots\tilde{\alpha}_{k-1}\dots\alpha_{n}
is still a normal cube path, we are left to show that for every
t\in\alpha_{k}
,
[t,\tilde{\alpha}_{k-1}]\neq 1
. Since we know that
[s,\alpha_{k}]=1
, we have that
[s,t]=1
. On the other hand,
[t,\alpha_{k-1}]\neq 1
, as we started out with the normal cube path
v
. Therefore, there has to be some element in
\tilde{\alpha}_{k-1}
that does not commute with
t
. We conclude that
\alpha_{1}\dots\tilde{\alpha}_{k-1}\dots\alpha_{n}
is a normal cube path.

In statement (4), the path
vs
cannot cross any hyperplane twice, as that would require
s^{-1}\in\alpha_{k-1}
by Lemma
2.27
. Putting
\tilde{\alpha}_{k}:=\alpha_{k}\cup\{s\}
, we obtain that the path
\alpha_{1}\dots\tilde{\alpha}_{k}\dots\alpha_{n}
does not cross any hyperplane more than once. We are left to show that for every
t\in\tilde{\alpha}_{k}
,
[t,\alpha_{k-1}]\neq 1
. For
t\in\alpha_{k}
, this is true because
v
is a normal cube path. For
t=s
, this holds because statement (4) supposes that
[s,\alpha_{k-1}]\neq 1
. This proves the final statement and the Lemma.

∎

2.5.
Regular languages and rooted automorphisms of trees

For the remainder of the paper, it will be useful to understand the geometric structure intrinsically present in a regular language and how one can describe automorphisms of this geometric structure. Our main reference for this is
[
HIM23
]
. Here, we present the relation between regular languages and self-similar trees and the description of automorphisms as portraits as developed in said reference to the extent that we need in this paper.

Let
\mathcal{L}
be a regular language over an alphabet
\Sigma
, that is,
\mathcal{L}
is precisely the language accepted by a finite automaton. This language has an associated path language tree
T
, which is a rooted tree, whose vertices are the elements of
\mathcal{L}
(with the empty word
\epsilon
as the root). Given two vertices
v,w\in\mathcal{L}
, there is an edge from
v
to
w
in
T
if and only if there exists some
\alpha\in\Sigma
such that
w=v\alpha
. We can label the edges of
T
by elements of
\Sigma
by labeling the edge from
v
to
v\alpha
by
\alpha
. A rooted tree is called
self-similar
if it is the path language tree of a regular language.

Notation 2.29
.

By Proposition
2.15
, there is a self-similar tree associated to the language
\mathcal{L}_{o}^{cube}
. We denote this tree by
T_{o}^{cube}
. Similarly, Lemma
2.25
implies that there is a self-similar tree associated to the language
\mathcal{L}_{o}
, which we denote by
T_{o}
. We write
V(T_{o}^{cube})=\mathcal{L}_{o}^{cube}
and
V(T_{o})=\mathcal{L}_{o}
for the vertices of these trees.

Definition 2.30
.

Let
\mathcal{L}
be a regular language over the alphabet
\Sigma
and
T
the associated path language tree. For all
v\in\mathcal{L}
, we denote

\Sigma(v):=\{\alpha\in\Sigma|v\alpha\in\mathcal{L}\}.

A portrait in a language
\mathcal{L}
is a family of maps
(\prescript{}{v\cdot}{\sigma})_{v\in\mathcal{L}}
such that
\prescript{}{v\cdot}{\sigma}:\Sigma(v)\rightarrow\Sigma
is injective for every
v\in\mathcal{L}
. For any portrait, we define the map
\sigma:\mathcal{L}\rightarrow\Sigma^{*}
by

\sigma(\alpha_{1}\dots\alpha_{n}):=\prescript{}{\epsilon\cdot}{\sigma}(\alpha_{1})\dots\prescript{}{\alpha_{1}\dots\alpha_{i-1}\cdot}{\sigma}(\alpha_{i})\dots\prescript{}{\alpha_{1}\dots\alpha_{n-1}\cdot}{\sigma}(\alpha_{n}).

We say a portrait
defines an automorphism on
T
if
\sigma
is the vertex-map of a rooted automorphism on
T
. (Since rooted automorphisms on
T
are uniquely determined by what they do on vertices,
\sigma
determines the entire automorphism.)

Remark 2.31
.

Given an element
\sigma\in\text{Aut}(T)
, we define a map

\prescript{}{v\cdot}{\sigma}:\Sigma(v)\rightarrow\Sigma(\sigma(v))

by the equation

\forall\alpha\in\Sigma_{v}:\quad\sigma(v\alpha)=\sigma(v)\prescript{}{v\cdot}{\sigma}(\alpha).

As was shown in
[
HIM23
, Construction 3.3]
, the collection
(\prescript{}{v\cdot}{\sigma})_{v\in V(T)}
is a portrait and recovers the automorphism
\sigma
via the formula given in Definition
2.30
.

Remark 2.32
.

By
[
HIM23
, Theorem 3.6]
, a portrait defines an automorphism on
T
if and only if
\sigma(\mathcal{L})\subset\mathcal{L}
and, for every
v\in\mathcal{L}
,
\prescript{}{v\cdot}{\sigma}(\Sigma(v))\subset\Sigma(\sigma(v))
. By
[
HIM23
, Remark 3.8]
, this condition can be checked by verifying inductively over the length of
v
that
\prescript{}{v\cdot}{\sigma}(\Sigma(v))\subset\Sigma(\sigma(v))
for every
v\in\mathcal{L}
.

Remark 2.33
.

One easily checks from the equation in Remark
2.31
that the composition of automorphisms on
T
satisfies the following formula, when written in portraits:

(1)

\forall\sigma,\tau\in\text{Aut}(T):\prescript{}{v\cdot}{(\sigma\circ\tau)}=\prescript{}{\tau(v)\cdot}{\sigma}\circ\prescript{}{v\cdot}{\tau}.

Notation 2.34
.

For the language
\mathcal{L}_{o}^{cube}
, we have already introduced the special notation
\Sigma_{v}
for the set
\Sigma(v)
introduced in Definition
2.30
. For the language
\mathcal{L}_{o}
, we write
\Sigma_{v}^{norm}
for its set
\Sigma(v)
and
\Sigma_{0,v}^{norm}:=\Sigma_{v}^{norm}\cap\Sigma_{0}
.

This allows us to describe elements of
\text{Aut}(T_{o}^{cube})
and
\text{Aut}(T_{o})
in terms of the corresponding portraits, which we will make use of throughout the rest of the paper.

3.
Stabilizer elements of
\text{Aut}(X)
as tree automorphisms

Let
X
be a locally finite
\CAT
cube complex,
\Gamma
a group acting cubically and cocompactly on
X
, and
\ell
a
\Gamma
-invariant admissible edge labeling on
X
. We fix a vertex
o\in X^{(0)}
. Our goal in this section is to find a description of the stabilizer subgroup

G_{o}:=\{g\in\text{Aut}(X)|g(o)=o\}.

We start by embedding
G_{o}
in the group of rooted automorphisms of the trees
T_{o}
and
T_{o}^{cube}
as follows: For
T_{o}
, we note that vertices of
T_{o}
correspond to normal cube paths in
X
that start at
o
(see Remark
2.26
). Since the action of
G_{o}
on
X
preserves cube paths that start at
o
, it induces an action on the vertices of
T_{o}
that fixes the root
\epsilon
.

For
T_{o}^{cube}
, we observe that vertices of
T_{o}^{cube}
correspond to elements in
D(X)_{o}
(see Remark
2.16
). Since the action of
G_{o}
on
X
preserves cube paths that start at
o
, it induces an action on the vertices of
T_{o}^{cube}
that fixes the root
\epsilon
.

Lemma 3.1
.

Let
o\in X^{(0)}
. The actions
G_{o}\curvearrowright V(T_{o})
,
G_{o}\curvearrowright V(T_{o}^{cube})
described above are actions by rooted automorphisms. In particular,
G_{o}
canonically embeds into
\text{Aut}(T_{o})
and
\text{Aut}(T_{o}^{cube})
.

Proof.

We present the proof for
T_{o}
. The proof for
T_{o}^{cube}
is analogous. Let
g\in G_{o}
and consider its action on
V(T_{o})
. Clearly, this action fixes the root, as
g
fixes the vertex
o
. To prove that
G_{o}
acts by rooted automorphisms, we need to show that the action of
g
on
T_{o}
preserves edges.

Let
e
be an edge from
v
to
w
in
T_{o}
, i.e.
v=\alpha_{1}\dots\alpha_{n}
and
w=\alpha_{1}\dots\alpha_{n+1}
are two normal cube paths in
X
. Since the action of
g
on
X
is a cubical isometry that fixes
o
, it sends normal cube paths starting at
o
to normal cube paths (of the same length) that start at
o
. In particular,
g
sends
\alpha_{1}\dots\alpha_{n+1}
to a normal cube path
\alpha^{\prime}_{1}\dots\alpha^{\prime}_{n+1}
, which implies that
g(v)=\alpha^{\prime}_{1}\dots\alpha^{\prime}_{n}
and
g(w)=\alpha^{\prime}_{1}\dots\alpha^{\prime}_{n+1}
. Therefore,
g(w)=g(v)\alpha^{\prime}_{n+1}
and there is an edge from
g(v)
to
g(w)
in
T_{o}
.

Applying the same argument to
g^{-1}\in G_{o}
shows that, if there is an edge from
g(v)
to
g(w)
in
T_{o}
, then there is an edge from
v
to
w
in
T_{o}
. Thus,
g
acts on
T_{o}
as a rooted tree-automorphism.

Since the action of
g
on both
X
and
T_{o}
is determined by the action on the respective vertices, which are canonically bijective, we immediately see that this map is injective and a homomorphism.
∎

The Lemma above tells us that, for every vertex
o\in X^{(0)}
, we may think of
G_{o}
as a subgroup of
\text{Aut}(T_{o})
and
\text{Aut}(T_{o}^{cube})
. We can thus use the theory developed in
[
HIM23
, Section 3]
to describe elements of
G_{o}
in terms of a portrait. We will define the portrait of
g
explicitly and then show that it is indeed a portrait in the sense of Definition
2.30
.

Definition 3.2
.

Let
g\in G_{o}
and
v\in V(T_{o}^{cube})
. We define

\prescript{}{v\cdot}{\sigma(g)}:\Sigma_{v}\rightarrow\Sigma

s\mapsto\ell\circ g\circ\ell|_{\vec{\mathcal{D}}(X)_{v}}^{-1}(s).

Equivalently, the map
\prescript{}{v\cdot}{\sigma(g)}
is defined to send the label
s
of a diagonal
d
that is outgoing at
v
to the label of the edge
g(d)
. Equivalently,
\prescript{}{v\cdot}{\sigma(g)}
is defined as the unique map satisfying the following equation:

\forall\alpha\in\Sigma_{v}:g(v\alpha)=g(v)\prescript{}{v\cdot}{\sigma(g)}(\alpha).

We define the
portrait of
g
to be the family
(\prescript{}{v\cdot}{\sigma(g)})_{v\in V(T_{o}^{cube})}
.

Remark 3.3
.

Following Definition
2.30
, we obtain a map
\sigma(g):\mathcal{L}_{o}^{cube}\rightarrow\mathcal{L}_{o}^{cube}
defined by

\sigma(g)(\alpha_{1}\dots\alpha_{n}):=\prescript{}{\epsilon\cdot}{\sigma(g)}(\alpha_{1})\dots\prescript{}{\alpha_{1}\dots\alpha_{i}\cdot}{\sigma(g)}(\alpha_{i+1})\dots\prescript{}{\alpha_{1}\dots\alpha_{n-1}\cdot}{\sigma(g)}(\alpha_{n}).

One easily verifies that this map satisfies the formula

\forall\alpha_{1}\dots\alpha_{n}\in\mathcal{L}_{o}^{cube}:g(\alpha_{1}\dots\alpha_{n})=\sigma(g)(\alpha_{1}\dots\alpha_{n}).

We recall that, by the definition of a labeling, the restrictions
\ell|_{\vec{\mathcal{D}}(X)_{v}}
and
\ell|_{\vec{\mathcal{D}}(X)_{g(v)}}
are injective and
\ell(\vec{\mathcal{D}}(X)_{v})=\Sigma_{v}
. Therefore,
\prescript{}{v\cdot}{\sigma(g)}
is injective, making the portrait of
g
a portrait in
\mathcal{L}_{o}^{cube}
in the sense of Definition
2.30
.

3.1.
Label-morphisms

Given
g\in G_{o}
, the portrait of
g
satisfies much better properties than a general portrait in
\mathcal{L}_{o}^{cube}
. In this subsection, we introduce the necessary terminology to discuss these properties. To do so, we define a suitable notion of morphism on the set
\Sigma
.

Definition 3.4
.

A map
\sigma:\Sigma_{0,v}\rightarrow\Sigma_{0}
is called a
label-morphism
if it is injective and satisfies the following condition:

\forall s,t\in\Sigma_{0,v}:[s,t]=1\Leftrightarrow[\sigma(s),\sigma(t)]=1.

If
\Sigma_{0,v}=\Sigma_{0}
for all
v
, then label-morphisms are bijective and we call them
label-isomorphisms
.

Remark 3.5
.

A label-morphism admits a canonical extension
\sigma:\Sigma_{v}\rightarrow\Sigma
defined by

\sigma(\{s_{1},\dots,s_{n}\}):=\{\sigma(s_{1}),\dots,\sigma(s_{n})\}.

Since label-morphisms preserve commutation, the expression above sends elements of
\Sigma_{v}
to elements of
\Sigma
. Furthermore, the size of a label does not change, since label-morphisms are injective. We always denote this canonical extension by the same symbol as the initial label-morphism.

Warning
.

If
\beta\in\Sigma
, then
\beta\subset\Sigma_{0}
and thus
\beta
can be thought of as an element or a subset of
\Sigma
. In order to distinguish between
\beta
as an element and
\beta
as a subset, we write
\{\beta\}
whenever we speak of
\beta
as a subset of
\Sigma
, and
\beta
when we speak of it as an element of
\Sigma
. Under this convention, we have that, for a label-morphism
\sigma
, the expression

\sigma(\{\beta\})=\{\beta\}

means that
\sigma
preserves the subset
\{\beta\}\subset\Sigma_{0}
, while we write

\sigma(\beta)=\beta:\Leftrightarrow\forall s\in\beta:\sigma(s)=s.

We are rarely going to use the expression
\sigma(\{\beta\})
, but
\sigma(\beta)
and in particular
\sigma(\beta)=\beta
will occur frequently.

Remark 3.6
.

We emphasize that, in general, a label-morphism does not satisfy the equation

\sigma(s^{-1})=\sigma(s)^{-1}.

An easy way to produce a counterexample is to work with a cube complex that has a link containing more than two isolated vertices. However, even non-isolated labels do not necessarily satisfy the above equation. (Consider for example the right-angled Artin group induced by a square and set
X
to be the universal cover of its Salvetti complex. Study the automorphism-group of its link.)

Definition 3.7
.

A portrait
(\prescript{}{v\cdot}{\sigma})_{v\in V(T_{o}^{cube})}
in the language
\mathcal{L}_{o}^{cube}
is called
admissible
, if the following two conditions hold:

•

For every pair
v,w\in\mathcal{L}_{o}^{cube}
such that
v\equiv w
, we have
\prescript{}{v\cdot}{\sigma}=\prescript{}{w\cdot}{\sigma}
.

•

For every
v\in\mathcal{L}_{o}^{cube}
,
\prescript{}{v\cdot}{\sigma}|_{\Sigma_{0,v}}:\Sigma_{0,v}\rightarrow\Sigma_{0}
is a label-morphism and
\prescript{}{v\cdot}{\sigma}
is its canonical extension.

We frequently identify the maps
\prescript{}{v\cdot}{\sigma},\prescript{}{w\cdot}{\sigma}
for equivalent words. Furthermore, since
\prescript{}{v\cdot}{\sigma}
is determined by its restriction to
\Sigma_{0,v}
, we view
(\prescript{}{v\cdot}{\sigma})_{v\in V(T_{o})}
as a collection of maps
\prescript{}{v\cdot}{\sigma}:\Sigma_{0,v}\rightarrow\Sigma_{0}
.

Definition 3.8
.

Let
(\prescript{}{v\cdot}{\sigma})_{v\in\mathcal{L}_{o}^{cube}}
be an admissible portrait that defines an automorphism on
T_{o}^{cube}
. We define the following two properties that this family may satisfy:

(par)

For all
v\in\mathcal{L}_{o}^{cube}
, for all non-isolated
s\in\Sigma_{0,v}
, and for all
\beta\in\Sigma_{v}
such that
[\beta,s]=1
, we have

\prescript{}{v\cdot}{\sigma(g)}(\beta)=\prescript{}{vs\cdot}{\sigma(g)}(\beta).

(inv)

For all
\alpha_{1}\dots\alpha_{n}\in\mathcal{L}_{o}^{cube}
and all
s\in\alpha_{n}
, we have

\prescript{}{\alpha_{1}\dots\alpha_{n}\cdot}{\sigma(g)}(s^{-1})=\prescript{}{\alpha_{1}\dots\tilde{\alpha}_{n}\cdot}{\sigma(g)}(s)^{-1},

where
\tilde{\alpha}_{n}:=\alpha_{n}\setminus\{s\}
. (If
\tilde{\alpha}_{n}
is empty, then
\alpha_{1}\dots\tilde{\alpha}_{n}=\alpha_{1}\dots\alpha_{n-1}
.)

3.2.
Proof of Theorem
J

The main result of this section is a characterisation of the portraits in

and

that are obtained from elements in

.

Theorem 3.9
.

Let

. The following statements hold:

(1)

Let
(\prescript{}{v\cdot}{\sigma})_{v\in V(T_{o}^{cube})}

By

Proof.

Let

(\prescript{}{v\cdot}{\sigma(g)})_{v\in V(T_{o}^{cube})}
.

Now let

.
∎

Lemma 3.11
.

Let
g\in G_{o}
. Then the family

and

.

Let
g\in G_{o}

(\prescript{}{v\cdot}{\sigma(g)})_{v\in V(T_{o}^{cube})}

The purpose of portraits is to encode automorphisms of their underlying tree. This requires that portraits send children of a vertex

Lemma 3.12
.

Suppose
(\prescript{}{v\cdot}{\sigma})_{v\in V(T_{o}^{cube})}

and all

\prescript{}{\alpha_{1}\dots\alpha_{n-1}\cdot}{\sigma}(s)=\prescript{}{\alpha_{1}\dots\alpha_{n}\cdot}{\sigma}(s),

\prescript{}{\alpha_{1}\dots\alpha_{n-1}\cdot}{\sigma}(\alpha_{n})^{-1}=\prescript{}{\alpha_{1}\dots\alpha_{n}\cdot}{\sigma}(\alpha_{n}^{-1})

.

The first equation is simply a repeated application of property

. The second equation follows, if we can show for every

that

Lemma 3.13
.

Let
(\prescript{}{v\cdot}{\sigma})_{v\in V(T_{o}^{cube})}
be an admissible portrait that defines an automorphism on
T_{o}^{cube}
and satisfies
par
and
inv
. Then, for every
v\in\mathcal{L}_{o}
,
\prescript{}{v\cdot}{\sigma}
restricts to a bijection
\Sigma_{v}^{norm}\rightarrow\Sigma_{\sigma(v)}^{norm}
. In particular, the map

\sigma^{norm}:\mathcal{L}_{o}\rightarrow\mathcal{L}_{o}

\sigma^{norm}(\alpha_{1}\dots\alpha_{n}):=\prescript{}{\epsilon\cdot}{\sigma}(\alpha_{1})\dots\prescript{}{\alpha_{1}\dots\alpha_{i-1}\cdot}{\sigma}(\alpha_{i})\dots\prescript{}{\alpha_{1}\dots\alpha_{n-1}\cdot}{\sigma}(\alpha_{n})

is well-defined and defines an automorphism on
T_{o}
.

Proof.

Let
v=\alpha_{1}\dots\alpha_{n}\in\mathcal{L}_{o}=V(T_{o})
. We denote
v_{-}:=\alpha_{1}\dots\alpha_{n-1}
and
v_{end}:=\alpha_{n}
. By
[
HIM23
, Theorem 3.6]
, we know that
\prescript{}{v\cdot}{\sigma}
is in fact a bijection
\Sigma_{0,v}\rightarrow\Sigma_{0,\sigma(v)}
. We prove the Lemma by showing that
\prescript{}{v\cdot}{\sigma}
restricts to a bijection between the complements of
\Sigma_{v}^{norm}
and
\Sigma_{\sigma(v)}^{norm}
. We know from Remark
2.24
that

\begin{split}\Sigma_{v}^{norm}&=\Sigma_{v}\setminus(\{\alpha\in\Sigma_{v}|\alpha\cap v_{end}^{-1}\neq\emptyset\}\cup\{\alpha\in\Sigma_{v}|\exists s\in\alpha:[s,v_{end}]=1\}),\\
\Sigma_{\sigma(v)}^{norm}&=\Sigma_{\sigma(v)}\setminus(\{\alpha\in\Sigma_{\sigma(v)}|\alpha\cap\prescript{}{v_{-}\cdot}{\sigma}(v_{end})^{-1}\neq\emptyset\}\\
&\qquad\qquad\qquad\cup\{\alpha\in\Sigma_{\sigma(v)}|\exists s\in\alpha:[s,\prescript{}{v_{-}\cdot}{\sigma}(v_{end})]=1\}).\end{split}

Let
\alpha\in\Sigma_{v}
such that there exists
s\in\alpha\cap v_{end}^{-1}
. By Lemma
3.12
, we have

\prescript{}{v\cdot}{\sigma}(v_{end}^{-1})=\prescript{}{v_{-}\cdot}{\sigma}(v_{end})^{-1}

and thus
\prescript{}{v\cdot}{\sigma}(s)\in\prescript{}{v\cdot}{\sigma}(\alpha\cap v_{end}^{-1})=\prescript{}{v\cdot}{\sigma}(\alpha)\cap\prescript{}{v_{-}\cdot}{\sigma}(v_{end})^{-1}
. We conclude that
\alpha\cap v_{end}^{-1}\neq\emptyset
if and only if
\prescript{}{v\cdot}{\sigma}(\alpha)\cap\prescript{}{v_{-}\cdot}{\sigma}(v_{end})^{-1}\neq\emptyset
.

Now suppose
\alpha\in\Sigma_{v}
such that there exists
s\in\alpha
satisfying
[s,v_{end}]=1
. Since all
\prescript{}{v\cdot}{\sigma}
are label-isomorphisms, this holds if and only if

[\prescript{}{v_{-}\cdot}{\sigma}(s),\prescript{}{v_{-}\cdot}{\sigma}(v_{end})]=1.

Since
[s,v_{end}]=1
, property
par
implies that
\prescript{}{v_{-}\cdot}{\sigma}(s)=\prescript{}{v\cdot}{\sigma}(s)
and thus the commutation above is equivalent to

[\prescript{}{v\cdot}{\sigma}(s),\prescript{}{v_{-}\cdot}{\sigma}(v_{end})]=1.

We conclude that there exists
s\in\alpha
such that
[s,v_{end}]=1
if and only if there exists
s^{\prime}\in\prescript{}{v\cdot}{\sigma}(\alpha)
such that
[s^{\prime},\prescript{}{v_{-}\cdot}{\sigma}(v_{end})]=1
(with
s^{\prime}=\prescript{}{v\cdot}{\sigma}(s)
). Thus,
\prescript{}{v\cdot}{\sigma}
sends the complement of
\Sigma_{v}^{norm}
bijectively to
\Sigma_{\sigma(v)}^{norm}
.

Lemma
3.10
and Lemma

tell us that the map that sends

to its portrait
(\prescript{}{v\cdot}{\sigma(g)})_{v\in V(T_{o})}
sends

into the collection of admissible portraits that define an automorphism on

and that satisfy the properties

and

. To prove the first statement of Theorem

Proposition 3.14
.

Let
(\prescript{}{v\cdot}{\sigma})_{v\in V(T_{o}^{cube})}
be an admissible portrait that defines an automorphism on
T_{o}^{cube}
and satisfies

and

. Then, for any two

if and only if

.

The proof of this proposition is built on the following two lemmata.

Lemma 3.15
.

Let
(\prescript{}{v\cdot}{\sigma})_{v\in V(T_{o}^{cube})}
be an admissible portrait that defines an automorphism on
T_{o}^{cube}
and satisfies
par
. Then, for any
v\in\mathcal{L}_{o}^{cube}
and any
\alpha=\{s_{1},\dots,s_{n}\}\in\Sigma_{v}
, we have

\prescript{}{v\cdot}{\sigma}(\alpha)\equiv_{\sigma(v)}\prescript{}{v\cdot}{\sigma}(s_{1})\dots\prescript{}{v\cdot}{\sigma}(s_{n})=\prescript{}{v\cdot}{\sigma}(s_{1})\dots\prescript{}{vs_{1}\dots s_{n-1}\cdot}{\sigma}(s_{n}).

Proof.

Since
\prescript{}{v\cdot}{\sigma}
is a label-morphism for every

, we know that

\prescript{}{v\cdot}{\sigma}(\alpha)=\{\prescript{}{v\cdot}{\sigma}(s_{1}),\dots,\prescript{}{v\cdot}{\sigma}(s_{n})\}.

Since any directed diagonal
\{s_{1},\dots,s_{n}\}
has the same start and end point as the edge-path

, we obtain the equivalence

\prescript{}{v\cdot}{\sigma}(\alpha)\equiv_{\sigma(v)}\prescript{}{v\cdot}{\sigma}(s_{1})\dots\prescript{}{v\cdot}{\sigma}(s_{n}).

The second equation is a consequence of
par
.
∎

The other result we need is a well-known fact about edge-paths in
\CAT
cube complexes. For convenience of use, we formulate it using our labelings.

Lemma 3.16
.

Consider two edge-paths in
X
that both start at a vertex
o
and end at a vertex
p
. Let
v,w\in\mathcal{L}_{o}^{cube}
be the words corresponding to these edge-paths under the labeling
\ell
. Then
v
can be transformed into
w
by finitely many applications of the following two moves:

u_{1}u_{2}\equiv_{o}u_{1}ss^{-1}u_{2}

u_{1}stu_{2}\equiv_{o}u_{1}tsu_{2}.

Proof of Proposition
3.14
.

We introduce the following auxiliary notation for this proof. Given a word
u_{1}u_{2}\in\mathcal{L}_{o}^{cube}
, where
u_{2}=\alpha_{n+1}\dots\alpha_{n+m}
, we write

\prescript{}{u_{1}\cdot}{\sigma}(u_{2}):=\prescript{}{u_{1}\cdot}{\sigma}(\alpha_{n+1})\dots\prescript{}{u_{1}\alpha_{n+1}\dots\alpha_{n+m-1}\cdot}{\sigma}(\alpha_{n+m}).

Admissibility of
(\prescript{}{v\cdot}{\sigma})_{v\in V(T_{o}^{cube})}
immediately implies that, if
u_{1}\equiv u^{\prime}_{1}
, then
\prescript{}{u_{1}\cdot}{\sigma}(u_{2})=\prescript{}{u^{\prime}_{1}\cdot}{\sigma}(u_{2})
.

Let
v\in\mathcal{L}_{o}^{cube}
. By Lemma
3.15
, we can build an edge-path
v^{\prime}\equiv_{o}v
such that
\sigma(v)\equiv\sigma(v^{\prime})
. It is thus sufficient to check that
v\equiv w
if and only if
\sigma(v)\equiv\sigma(w)
for edge-paths. By Lemma
3.16
, we only need to check the cases, where
v
and
w
differ by one of the two moves above.

``\Rightarrow"
: Suppose
v\equiv w
and there are
u_{1},s,u_{2}
such that

v=u_{1}ss^{-1}u_{2},\quad w=u_{1}u_{2}.

Since
(\prescript{}{v\cdot}{\sigma})_{v\in V(T_{o}^{cube})}
is admissible and
u_{1}\equiv u_{1}ss^{-1}
, we know that

\prescript{}{u_{1}\cdot}{\sigma}(u_{2})=\prescript{}{u_{1}ss^{-1}\cdot}{\sigma}(u_{2}).

Furthermore, property
inv
implies that

\prescript{}{u_{1}\cdot}{\sigma}(s)=\prescript{}{u_{1}s\cdot}{\sigma}(s^{-1})^{-1}.

We compute

\begin{split}\sigma(v)&=\sigma(u_{1}ss^{-1}u_{2})\\
&=\sigma(u_{1})\prescript{}{u_{1}\cdot}{\sigma}(s)\prescript{}{u_{1}s\cdot}{\sigma}(s^{-1})\prescript{}{u_{1}ss^{-1}\cdot}{\sigma}(u_{2})\\
&=\sigma(u_{1})\prescript{}{u_{1}\cdot}{\sigma}(u_{2})\\
&=\sigma(w).\end{split}

Thus
\sigma(v)\equiv\sigma(w)
.

Now suppose
v\equiv w
and suppose there are
u_{1},s,t,u_{2}
such that
[s,t]=1
and

v=u_{1}stu_{2},\quad w=u_{1}tsu_{2}.

Using admissibility again, we obtain

\prescript{}{u_{1}st\cdot}{\sigma}(u_{2})=\prescript{}{u_{1}ts\cdot}{\sigma}(u_{2})

and, since
[s,t]=1
,
[\prescript{}{u_{1}\cdot}{\sigma}(s),\prescript{}{u_{1}\cdot}{\sigma}(t)]=1
. This, combined with
par
, implies that

\prescript{}{u_{1}\cdot}{\sigma}(s)=\prescript{}{u_{1}t\cdot}{\sigma}(s),

\prescript{}{u_{1}\cdot}{\sigma}(t)=\prescript{}{u_{1}s\cdot}{\sigma}(t).

We thus compute

\begin{split}\sigma(v)&=\sigma(u_{1}stu_{2})\\
&=\sigma(u_{1})\prescript{}{u_{1}\cdot}{\sigma}(s)\prescript{}{u_{1}s\cdot}{\sigma}(t)\prescript{}{u_{1}st\cdot}{\sigma}(u_{2})\\
&\equiv\sigma(u_{1})\prescript{}{u_{1}\cdot}{\sigma}(t)\prescript{}{u_{1}t\cdot}{\sigma}(s)\prescript{}{u_{1}ts\cdot}{\sigma}(u_{2})\\
&=\sigma(u_{1}tsu_{2})\\
&=\sigma(w).\end{split}

``\Leftarrow"
: We are left to show the converse, that is
\sigma(v)\equiv\sigma(w)\Rightarrow v\equiv w
. One could show this by arguing that the inverse of
(\prescript{}{v\cdot}{\sigma})_{v\in V(T_{o}^{cube})}
in the group
\text{Aut}(T_{o}^{cube})
is also admissible and satisfies
par
and
inv
. This requires dealing with the encoding of inverses in portraits, which becomes cumbersome in notation. Instead, we simply prove the converse directly.

Suppose
\sigma(v)\equiv\sigma(w)
and there are
u^{\prime}_{1},s^{\prime},u^{\prime}_{2}
such that

\sigma(v)=u^{\prime}_{1}s^{\prime}s^{\prime-1}u^{\prime}_{2},\quad\sigma(w)=u^{\prime}_{1}u^{\prime}_{2}.

Since
(\prescript{}{v\cdot}{\sigma})_{v\in V(T_{o}^{cube})}
defines an automorphism on
T_{o}^{cube}
, we obtain the following:

•

There exists
u_{1}\in\mathcal{L}_{o}^{cube}
such that
\sigma(u_{1})=u^{\prime}_{1}
.

•

There exists
s\in\Sigma_{0,u_{1}}
such that
\prescript{}{u_{1}\cdot}{\sigma}(s)=s^{\prime}
.

•

There exists
u_{2}\in\mathcal{L}_{u_{1}}^{cube}
such that
\prescript{}{u_{1}\cdot}{\sigma}(u_{2})=u^{\prime}_{2}
. (The decomposition of
\sigma(w)
into
u^{\prime}_{1}
and
u^{\prime}_{2}
induces a decomposition of the path
w
into
u_{1}
and a second path segment. This second path-segment is
u_{2}
.)

Since
(\prescript{}{v\cdot}{\sigma})_{v\in V(T_{o}^{cube})}
is admissible, we know that

\prescript{}{u_{1}\cdot}{\sigma}(u_{2})=\prescript{}{u_{1}ss^{-1}\cdot}{\sigma}(u_{2}).

By
inv
, we have that

\prescript{}{u_{1}\cdot}{\sigma}(s)=\prescript{}{u_{1}s\cdot}{\sigma}(s^{-1})^{-1}.

We thus compute

\begin{split}\sigma(u_{1}ss^{-1}u_{2})&=\sigma(u_{1})\prescript{}{u_{1}\cdot}{\sigma}(s)\prescript{}{u_{1}s\cdot}{\sigma}(s^{-1})\prescript{}{u_{1}ss^{-1}\cdot}{\sigma}(u_{2})\\
&=u^{\prime}_{1}s^{\prime}s^{\prime-1}u^{\prime}_{2}\\
&=\sigma(v)\end{split}

Since
\sigma
is bijective, this implies that
v=u_{1}ss^{-1}u_{2}
, while
w=u_{1}u_{2}
. Thus,
v\equiv w
.

Suppose now
\sigma(v)\equiv\sigma(w)
and there are
u^{\prime}_{1},s^{\prime},t^{\prime},u^{\prime}_{2}
such that
[s^{\prime},t^{\prime}]=1
and

\sigma(v)=u^{\prime}_{1}s^{\prime}t^{\prime}u^{\prime}_{2},\quad\sigma(w)=u^{\prime}_{1}t^{\prime}s^{\prime}u^{\prime}_{2}.

Since
(\prescript{}{v\cdot}{\sigma})_{v\in V(T_{o}^{cube})}
defines an automorphism on
T_{o}^{cube}
, the decomposition of the path
\sigma(v)
into
u^{\prime}_{1}
,
s^{\prime}
,
t^{\prime}
,
u^{\prime}_{2}
induces a decomposition of the path

with the following properties:

•

There exists
u_{1}\in\mathcal{L}_{o}^{cube}
such that
\sigma(u_{1})=u^{\prime}_{1}
.

•

There exists
s\in\Sigma_{0,u_{1}}
such that
\prescript{}{u_{1}\cdot}{\sigma}(s)=s^{\prime}
.

•

There exists
t\in\Sigma_{0,u_{1}s}
such that
\prescript{}{u_{1}s\cdot}{\sigma}(t)=t^{\prime}
.

•

There exists
u_{2}\in\mathcal{L}_{u_{1}st}^{cube}
such that
\prescript{}{u_{1}st\cdot}{\sigma}(u_{2})=u^{\prime}_{2}
.

Since
\prescript{}{u_{1}\cdot}{\sigma}
is a label-morphism and

, we conclude that

[s,t]=[\prescript{}{u_{1}\cdot}{\sigma}(s),\prescript{}{u_{1}\cdot}{\sigma}(t)]=[s^{\prime},t^{\prime}]=1.

In particular,

\prescript{}{u_{1}ts\cdot}{\sigma}(u_{2})=\prescript{}{u_{1}st\cdot}{\sigma}(u_{2})=u^{\prime}_{2}.

and

, while

.

This shows that

\sigma(v)\equiv\prescript{}{\epsilon\cdot}{\sigma}(\alpha_{1})\dots\prescript{}{\alpha_{1}\dots\alpha_{i-1}\cdot}{\sigma}(\alpha_{i})\dots\prescript{}{\alpha_{1}\dots\alpha_{n-1}\cdot}{\sigma}(\alpha_{n}),

``\Leftarrow"

∎

Proof of Theorem

.

The first statement is the combined result of Lemma

3.11
, and
3.17

∎

4.
Special actions and edge labelings

In this section, we present a characterisation of compact special cube complexes in terms of actions on
\CAT
cube complexes and edge labelings.

Lemma 4.1
.

Suppose there exists a
\Gamma
-invariant admissible edge labeling on
X
. Then
\Gamma
acts freely on
X
.

Proof.

Let
\ell
be a
\Gamma
-invariant admissible edge labeling on
X
. We start by showing that
\Gamma
acts freely on
X^{(0)}
. Let
g\in\Gamma
such that
g
fixes a vertex
o\in X^{(0)}
. Then
g\in G_{o}
and we can identify
g
with its portrait
(\prescript{}{v\cdot}{\sigma(g)})_{v\in V(T_{o}^{cube})}
. By definition of the portrait,
\prescript{}{v\cdot}{\sigma(g)}(s)
is the label of
g(e)
, where
e
is the unique outgoing edge at
v
labeled
s
. Since
\ell
is
\Gamma
-invariant,
g
has to preserve the labeling of oriented edges. Therefore,
\prescript{}{v\cdot}{\sigma(g)}=\text{id}
for every
v\in\mathcal{L}_{o}
and, by admissibility, for every
v\in\mathcal{L}_{o}^{cube}
. Since
g
is uniquely characterised by its portrait, this implies that
g=\text{Id}_{X}
. Thus,
\Gamma
acts freely on
X^{(0)}
.

Now suppose
g
does not fix any vertices, but fixes a point
p\in X
. Let
C(p)
be the lowest-dimensional cube that contains
p
. Without loss of generality, we may assume that
p
is chosen such that
C(p)
has minimal dimension among all fixed points of
g
. Since
g
fixes
p
, it has to preserve
C(p)
and we can look at the restriction of
g
to
C(p)
. This restriction has to send the edges of
C(p)
to the edges of
C(p)
while preserving the labeling
\ell
. Let
s_{1}^{\pm 1},\dots,s_{n}^{\pm 1}
be the labels appearing on the edges of
C(p)
. Since every vertex of
C(p)
is uniquely determined by an orientation of all the half-spaces in

, every vertex

in

is uniquely determined by a choice

. Since

has to preserve the labeling, it can only preserve

if it fixes all vertices of

. This contradicts our assumption and we conclude that every

that has a fixed point also fixes a vertex. Since
\Gamma
acts freely on

, this implies that
\Gamma
acts freely on
X
.
∎

Theorem 4.2
.

Suppose
\Gamma<\text{Aut}(X)
acts cocompactly on

and suppose there exists a
\Gamma
-invariant admissible edge-labeling on

{\mathchoice{\raisebox{3.41666pt}{$\displaystyle{X}$}\mkern-5.0mu\diagup\mkern-4.0mu\raisebox{-3.41666pt}{$\displaystyle{\Gamma}$}}{\raisebox{3.41666pt}{$\textstyle{X}$}\mkern-5.0mu\diagup\mkern-4.0mu\raisebox{-3.41666pt}{$\textstyle{\Gamma}$}}{\raisebox{2.39166pt}{$\scriptstyle{X}$}\mkern-5.0mu\diagup\mkern-4.0mu\raisebox{-2.39166pt}{$\scriptstyle{\Gamma}$}}{\raisebox{1.70833pt}{$\scriptscriptstyle{X}$}\mkern-5.0mu\diagup\mkern-4.0mu\raisebox{-1.6994pt}{$\scriptscriptstyle{\Gamma}$}}}

{\mathchoice{\raisebox{3.41666pt}{$\displaystyle{X}$}\mkern-5.0mu\diagup\mkern-4.0mu\raisebox{-3.41666pt}{$\displaystyle{\Gamma}$}}{\raisebox{3.41666pt}{$\textstyle{X}$}\mkern-5.0mu\diagup\mkern-4.0mu\raisebox{-3.41666pt}{$\textstyle{\Gamma}$}}{\raisebox{2.39166pt}{$\scriptstyle{X}$}\mkern-5.0mu\diagup\mkern-4.0mu\raisebox{-2.39166pt}{$\scriptstyle{\Gamma}$}}{\raisebox{1.70833pt}{$\scriptscriptstyle{X}$}\mkern-5.0mu\diagup\mkern-4.0mu\raisebox{-1.6994pt}{$\scriptscriptstyle{\Gamma}$}}}

Let
\tilde{\ell}
be a
\Gamma
-invariant admissible edge labeling on

In order to show that

in

Suppose there exists a hyperplane
\hat{h}
in

that self-osculates. Then we find a vertex in

with two outgoing edges

that have the same label. This lifts to a vertex in

Suppose there exist two hyperplanes

in

that interosculate. We find two vertices

,

with outgoing edges

,

and

and

\ell(e_{1})=\ell(e^{\prime}_{1})

. This lifts to two vertices
\overline{v}

\overline{w}
and edges
\overline{e_{1}},\overline{e_{2}}\in\vec{\mathcal{E}}(X)_{\overline{v}}
,

{\mathchoice{\raisebox{3.41666pt}{$\displaystyle{X}$}\mkern-5.0mu\diagup\mkern-4.0mu\raisebox{-3.41666pt}{$\displaystyle{\Gamma}$}}{\raisebox{3.41666pt}{$\textstyle{X}$}\mkern-5.0mu\diagup\mkern-4.0mu\raisebox{-3.41666pt}{$\textstyle{\Gamma}$}}{\raisebox{2.39166pt}{$\scriptstyle{X}$}\mkern-5.0mu\diagup\mkern-4.0mu\raisebox{-2.39166pt}{$\scriptstyle{\Gamma}$}}{\raisebox{1.70833pt}{$\scriptscriptstyle{X}$}\mkern-5.0mu\diagup\mkern-4.0mu\raisebox{-1.6994pt}{$\scriptscriptstyle{\Gamma}$}}}

∎

Lemma

, Lemma

, and Theorem

.

Definition 4.3
.

Let

such that
\mathcal{L}_{o}^{cube}=\mathcal{L}_{v}^{cube}
. This means that the concatenation of words

\mathcal{L}_{o}^{cube}\rightarrow\mathcal{L}_{o}^{cube},\quad w\mapsto vw

is well-defined. It induces a map on the vertices of

We call the map

One immediately sees that two vertices

and

Lemma 4.4
.

Let

be a compact, non-positively curved special cube complex,

its fundamental group, and

its universal covering. Let

be an admissible edge labeling on

. Then the action

Choose

and let

be its projection in

. Viewing

as the fundamental group with basepoint

, every element of

can be represented by an edge path

in the

-skeleton of

that starts and ends at

. Let

be the unique lift of
\bar{\gamma}
to that starts at

on

and any

∎

By Lemma

5.
Topologically generating set

Throughout this section, we assume
X
to be a locally finite
\CAT
cube complex and
\Gamma<\text{Aut}(X)
a subgroup whose action on
X
is cocompact. Suppose there exists a
\Gamma
-invariant admissible edge labeling
\ell:\vec{\mathcal{E}}(X)\rightarrow\Sigma_{0}
. By our results from section
4
, we know that
\Gamma
acts freely and by translations.

Definition 5.1
.

Let
o\in X^{(0)}
. Given two words
w_{1},w_{2}\in\mathcal{L}_{o}^{cube}
, we say that
w_{1}
and
w_{2}

commute
if all letters in
w_{1}
commute with all letters in
w_{2}
and we write
[w_{1},w_{2}]=1
.

A word
w\in\mathcal{L}_{o}^{cube}
is called
reducible
if its corresponding cube path with starting point
o
crosses some hyperplane more than once. (Equivalently, there exists a letter
s\in\Sigma_{0}
and a word
u
such that
[s,u]=1
and
\alpha u\beta^{-1}
is a subword in
w
, where
s\in\alpha
and
s^{-1}\in\beta
.) A word is called
reduced
, if it is not reducible.

Note that every normal cube path induces a reduced word, that is the language
\mathcal{L}_{o}
consists entirely of reduced words. Furthermore, every cube path that starts at
o
is
\equiv_{o}
-equivalent to an edge path that does not cross any hyperplane more than once. These edge paths are exactly the shortest paths in the
1
-skeleton of
X
from
o
to the endpoint of the initial cube path. In the notation of
\mathcal{L}_{o}^{cube}
, this means that for every word
w\in\mathcal{L}_{o}^{cube}
there exists a reduced word
w^{\prime}\in\mathcal{L}_{o}^{cube}\cap\Sigma_{0}^{*}
such that
w\equiv_{o}w^{\prime}
.

Our goal in this section is to construct a family of elements in
\text{Aut}(T_{o}^{cube})
that generates a dense subgroup of
G_{o}=\Stab_{\text{Aut}(X)}(o)
. We will first give an abstract argument that provides us with such a family and use this to prove Theorem
B
. After that, we will explicitly construct a topologically generating family of
G_{o}
under the assumption that
\Gamma
acts vertex-transitively on
X
. This will yield a proof of Theorem
C
.

5.1.
Generating a dense subgroup of the stabilizer

Fix a basepoint
o\in X^{(0)}
. For every
v\in\mathcal{L}_{o}
, we will find a finite set of isometries in
G_{o}
that addresses ‘everything that can happen at
v
’. We need to distinguish between the case
v=\epsilon
and
v\neq\epsilon
.

Definition/Construction 5.2

(Generators at
o
)
.

Let
\sigma:\Sigma_{0,o}\rightarrow\Sigma_{0}
be a label-morphism. We say that
\sigma
appears at
o
in
G_{o}
if there exists some isometry
g\in G_{o}
such that
\prescript{}{\epsilon\cdot}{\sigma(g)}=\sigma
. For every label-morphism
\sigma
that appears at
o
in
G_{o}
, we choose one such isometry and denote it by
A_{\epsilon,\sigma}
.

To introduce a choice of generators at
v
for
v\neq\epsilon
, we need some preparation. First, we sort all vertices
w\in\mathcal{L}_{o}
into three distinct types relative to
o
and
v
.

Definition 5.3
.

Let
v\in\mathcal{L}_{o}\setminus\{\epsilon\}
and
w\in\mathcal{L}_{o}
. Since every cube path is
\equiv
-equivalent to a reduced cube path, we can choose a, usually not unique, reduced word
w^{\prime}\in\mathcal{L}_{v}^{cube}
such that
w^{\prime}\equiv_{v}v^{-1}w
. We obtain the equivalence

w\equiv vv^{-1}w\equiv vw^{\prime}.

We now distinguish between three types:

Type 1:

vw^{\prime}
is reducible.

Type 2:

vw^{\prime}
is reduced and there exists
\alpha\in\Sigma
such that
v\alpha
is reducible and
[\alpha,w^{\prime}]=1
.

Type 3:

vw^{\prime}
is reduced and for all
\alpha\in\Sigma
such that
v\alpha
is reducible,
[\alpha,w^{\prime}]\neq 1
.

We say that
w
is
of Type 1, 2, or 3 relative to
(o,v)
respectively.

Remark 5.4
.

We highlight the following points:

•

The type of
w
does not depend on the choice of
w^{\prime}
. Since every choice of
w^{\prime}
crosses the same hyperplanes, reducibility of
vw^{\prime}
is independent of
w^{\prime}
and all choices of
w^{\prime}
commute with the same letters
\alpha\in\Sigma
.

•

The vertex
v
is of Type 2 relative to
(o,v)
. (Following Definition
5.1
, the empty word commutes with everything.) The vertex
o
is of Type 1 relative to
(o,v)
. In particular, swapping
o
and
v
in the pair
(o,v)
changes the Type of vertices.

•

If
w
is of Type 3, then
d_{\ell^{1}}(o,w)>d_{\ell^{1}}(o,v)
, as
w\equiv vw^{\prime}
, where
vw^{\prime}
is reduced and
w^{\prime}\neq\epsilon
.

For every
v\in\mathcal{L}_{o}\setminus\{\epsilon\}
, our goal is to find elements
A_{v,\sigma}\in G_{o}
that fix all vertices of Type 1 and 2. To do so, we make use of the following definitions.

Definition 5.5
.

Given a vertex

and a label-morphism
\sigma:\Sigma_{0,v}\rightarrow\Sigma_{0}
, we say that
\sigma
is

if and only if for all

such that

is reducible and for all

such that

, we have

and
\sigma(\beta)=\beta
.

Definition/Construction 5.6

(Generators at
v
)
.

Let

and
\sigma:\Sigma_{0,v}\rightarrow\Sigma_{0}
a label-morphism. We say that
\sigma
appears at
v
in
G_{o}
if there exists some isometry

such that
\prescript{}{v\cdot}{\sigma(g)}=\sigma
and

fixes all vertices of Type 1 and 2 relative to

. For every label-morphism

that appears at

in

, we choose one such isometry and denote it by

.

Remark 5.7
.

One easily checks that, if

Remark 5.9
.

We will show in section

Proof.

Recall that we have a canonical identification of the sets

{\mathchoice{\raisebox{4.49501pt}{$\displaystyle{\mathcal{L}_{o}^{cube}}$}\mkern-5.0mu\diagup\mkern-4.0mu\raisebox{-2.31873pt}{$\displaystyle{\equiv_{o}}$}}{\raisebox{4.24501pt}{$\textstyle{\mathcal{L}_{o}^{cube}}$}\mkern-5.0mu\diagup\mkern-4.0mu\raisebox{-2.31873pt}{$\textstyle{\equiv_{o}}$}}{\raisebox{3.24501pt}{$\scriptstyle{\mathcal{L}_{o}^{cube}}$}\mkern-5.0mu\diagup\mkern-4.0mu\raisebox{-1.72855pt}{$\scriptstyle{\equiv_{o}}$}}{\raisebox{2.45833pt}{$\scriptscriptstyle{\mathcal{L}_{o}^{cube}}$}\mkern-5.0mu\diagup\mkern-4.0mu\raisebox{-1.287pt}{$\scriptscriptstyle{\equiv_{o}}$}}}
, and
\mathcal{L}_{o}
(see Remark

).

Step 1: Preparing for induction.

Let

. By Lemma

We continue by induction over the

-distance to

(2)

is well-defined and fixes all vertices in

Step 2: For every

In Constructions

in

. By assumption, every

that is compatible with

in

. Thus, it suffices to show that

is compatible with

for all

\ell^{1}
-distance

from

. In other words, we need to show that, for every

such that

is reducible and for every

such that

, we have
\sigma_{v}(\alpha)=\alpha
and
\sigma_{v}(\beta)=\beta

So let

Since

Let

s^{\prime}=\prescript{}{u_{1}\cdot}{\sigma(g_{n})}(s^{\prime})=\prescript{}{u_{1}\alpha^{\prime}u_{2}s\cdot}{\sigma(g_{n})}(s^{\prime})=\sigma_{v^{\prime}}(s^{\prime}),

(3)

to the set

does not depend on the ordering in the product. Note that this set is in fact preserved under the product, since we are multiplying automorphisms that fix

We are left to show that the map

Now suppose

g_{n}(w)=g_{n}(v)\prescript{}{v\cdot}{\sigma(g_{n})}(s)=v\sigma_{v}(s),

A_{v,\sigma_{v}}(vs)=A_{v,\sigma_{v}}(v)\prescript{}{v\cdot}{A_{v,\sigma_{v}}}(s)=v\sigma_{v}(s),

\forall v^{\prime}\neq v:A_{v^{\prime},\sigma_{v^{\prime}}}(vs)=vs\text{ by Step 3},

\forall v^{\prime}\neq v:A_{v^{\prime},\sigma_{v^{\prime}}}(v\sigma(s))=v\sigma_{v}(s)\text{ by Step 4}.

This construction implies that, for any

5.2.
Generating a dense subgroup of
\text{Aut}(X)

In this subsection, we prove Theorem
B
. Our strategy is to conjugate elements
A_{v,\sigma}
by elements of
\Gamma
and show that these conjugates have useful properties. Recall from section
4
that
\Gamma
acts on
X
by translations. We start by introducing some terminology and establishing some properties of the interaction between translations and elements of
\{A_{v,\sigma}\}_{v,\sigma}
.

Definition 5.10
.

Let
v
be a reduced word in
\mathcal{L}_{o}^{cube}
. We define the
reductions of
v
to be the elements of the set

\mathrm{red}(v):=\{\alpha\in\Sigma_{v}|v\alpha\text{ is reducible}\}.

Let
v,v^{\prime}
be two reduced words in
\mathcal{L}_{o}^{cube}
. We say
v
and
v^{\prime}

have the same reductions
if
\mathrm{red}(v)=\mathrm{red}(v^{\prime})
and we write
v\sim v^{\prime}
. This defines an equivalence-relation on the set of reduced words in
\mathcal{L}_{o}^{cube}
.

Note that the empty word
\epsilon
forms its own, somewhat special equivalence class, which is why we remove it from some of the upcoming statements and treat it separately.

Lemma 5.11
.

Suppose two reduced words
v,v^{\prime}
in
\mathcal{L}_{o}^{cube}
have the same reductions, and
\sigma:\Sigma_{0,v}\rightarrow\Sigma_{0}
is a label-morphism.

(1)

If
s,t\in\Sigma_{0,v}
such that
vs
and
vt
are reducible, then
[s,t]=1
.

(2)

If
s\in\Sigma_{0,v}
such that
vs
is reducible and
s^{-1}\in\Sigma_{0,v}
, then
vs^{-1}
is reduced.

(3)

\sigma
is compatible with

if and only if it is compatible with
v^{\prime}
.

Proof.

(1) Let
s\neq t\in\Sigma_{0}
such that
vs
and
vt
are both reducible. The path
v
thus crosses the hyperplanes
\hat{h}(s)
and
\hat{h}(t)
. Let
w
be a reduced edge path such that
v\equiv w
. Since
v
and
w
cross the same hyperplanes,
w
crosses
\hat{h}(s)
and
\hat{h}(t)
as well. Since
w
is an edge path, it crosses one of them before the other and there lies a vertex on
w
between the two crossings. Without loss of generality,
w
crosses
\hat{h}(s)
first and let
u
be a vertex on
w
after crossing
\hat{h}(s)
and before crossing
\hat{h}(t)

now lie in four different connected components of

. Thus
\hat{h}(s)
and
\hat{h}(t)
intersect transversally. Since

, they appear at the same vertex and thus
[s,t]=1
.

(2) Suppose
s\in\Sigma_{0}
such that
vs
is reducible. If
vs^{-1}
is reducible, (1) implies that
[s,s^{-1}]=1
, which is a contradiction to Example
2.11
.

(3) Suppose
\sigma
is compatible with
v
. Let
\alpha,\beta\in\Sigma_{v^{\prime}}
such that
v^{\prime}\alpha
is reducible and
[\alpha,\beta]=1
. Since

and

have the same reductions, we conclude that
\alpha,\beta\in\Sigma_{v}
and

is reducible. Because
\sigma
is compatible with

, this implies that
\sigma(\alpha)=\alpha
and
\sigma(\beta)=\beta
. Therefore, we obtain compatibility with
v^{\prime}
.
∎

Lemma 5.12
.

Let
u,v\in\mathcal{L}_{o}\setminus\{\epsilon\}
such that
\mathcal{L}_{o}^{cube}=\mathcal{L}_{u}^{cube}
and let
v^{\prime}
be a reduced word
\equiv_{o}
-equivalent to

and

have the same reductions. Then, for every

, we have that the type of

relative to
(o,v)
is the same as the type of
L_{u}(w)
relative to
(o,v^{\prime})
.

Proof.

We write
w\equiv vw^{\prime}
such that
w^{\prime}
is reduced and obtain that
L_{u}(w)\equiv uvw^{\prime}\equiv v^{\prime}w^{\prime}
. Since
v
and
v^{\prime}
have the same reductions,
vw^{\prime}
is reduced if and only if
v^{\prime}w^{\prime}
is reduced. This implies that

is of Type 1 relative to

if and only if
uw
is of Type 1 relative to
(o,uv)
. Now suppose

are reduced. We have that

. Since

and

have the same reductions, this is equivalent to the existence of

. We conclude that

is of Type 2 relative to

∎

Lemma 5.13
.

Let
u,v\in\mathcal{L}_{o}\setminus\{\epsilon\}
such that
\mathcal{L}_{o}^{cube}=\mathcal{L}_{u}^{cube}
and such that

and

. (In particular,

\prescript{}{uv\cdot}{\sigma(L_{u}\circ A_{v,\sigma}\circ L_{u}^{-1})}=\prescript{}{v\cdot}{\sigma(A_{v,\sigma})}.

The last equation follows immediately from the fact that for every

∎

Lemma 5.14
.

Suppose the action of
\Gamma
on

is cocompact and by translations. Then there exists a finite subset

, such that for every
w\in\mathcal{L}_{o}\setminus\{\epsilon\}
, there exists some

and

\mathcal{L}_{o}^{cube}=\mathcal{L}_{u}^{cube}
,

•

•

To every

∎

Theorem 5.15

\Gamma
acts cocompactly on

and a
\Gamma

. Let

We start by introducing the elements of our finite set of generators. Let

. Furthermore, choose a representative of every orbit of

is equal to

Let

be a finite subset with the properties stated in Lemma

such that, for every

\sigma
that is compatible with

also appears at

according to Construction

according to Construction
5.6
. For every

\{A_{w,\sigma}|w\in\mathcal{L}_{o},\sigma\text{ appears at $w$ in $G_{o}$}\}

generates a dense subgroup of

5.3.
An explicit set of generators in the vertex-transitive case

As before, consider a locally finite
\CAT

is cocompact and a
\Gamma
-invariant admissible edge-labeling

Notation 5.17
.

Let
w=\beta_{1}\dots\beta_{m}\in\mathcal{L}_{o}
. We denote

•

w_{-}:=\beta_{1}\dots\beta_{m-1},

•

w_{end}:=\beta_{m}.

Furthermore, given a label-isomorphism
\sigma:\Sigma_{0}\rightarrow\Sigma_{0}
and
\beta\in\Sigma
, we introduce the map

\tau(\sigma,\beta):\Sigma_{0}\rightarrow\Sigma_{0},

\tau(\sigma,\beta):=\left(\prod_{s\in\beta}\left(\sigma(s^{-1}),\sigma(s)^{-1}\right)\right)\circ\sigma,

where
(a,b)
denotes the permuation that swaps the elements
a
and
b
. Since
\beta\in\Sigma
, and
\sigma
is a label-isomorphism, the sets
\{\sigma(s^{-1}),\sigma(s)^{-1}\}
are pairwise disjoint for distinct
s
in
\beta
(because if
[s,t]=1
, then
\sigma(s^{-1})
,
\sigma(s)^{-1}
commute with
\sigma(t^{-1})
,
\sigma(t)^{-1}
and are thus not equal). Therefore, these permutations commute and we do not need to specify the order in the product. In addition,
\tau(\sigma,\beta)
preserves commutation of elements in
\Sigma_{0}
, because
\sigma
does and
\sigma(s^{-1})
,
\sigma(s)^{-1}
commute with the same elements. Thus,
\tau(\sigma,\beta)
is a label-isomorphism as well. We additionally define
\tau(\sigma,\emptyset):=\sigma
.

Remark 5.18
.

One immediately verifies from the definition that, if for all
s\in\beta\cup\beta^{-1}
,
\sigma(s)=s
, then
\tau(\sigma,\beta)=\sigma
.

We will make use of the following two formulae for
\tau(\sigma,\beta)
.

Lemma 5.19
.

Let
\sigma
be a label-isomorphism,
\beta,\beta^{\prime}\in\Sigma
. Suppose
\sigma(\beta^{\prime})=\beta^{\prime}
and
\sigma(\beta^{\prime-1})=\beta^{\prime-1}
. Then

\tau(\sigma,\beta)(\beta^{\prime\pm 1})=\beta^{\prime\pm 1}=\sigma(\beta^{\prime\pm 1}).

Proof.

Let
t\in\beta^{\prime}\cup\beta^{\prime-1}
and thus
\sigma(t)=t
. Suppose
t=\sigma(s^{-1})
for some
s\in\beta
. Then

s^{-1}=\sigma^{-1}(t)=t.

Thus,
s\in\beta^{\prime}\cup\beta^{\prime-1}
and we conclude that

\sigma(s^{-1})=s^{-1},\text{ and }\sigma(s)^{-1}=s^{-1}.

Suppose now that
t=\sigma(s)^{-1}
for some
s\in\beta
. Then

s=\sigma^{-1}(t^{-1})=t^{-1},

as
t^{-1}\in\beta^{\prime}\cup\beta^{\prime-1}
. Again, we conclude that
s\in\beta^{\prime}\cup\beta^{\prime-1}
and thus

\sigma(s^{-1})=s^{-1},\text{ and }\sigma(s)^{-1}=s^{-1}.

It follows that

\begin{split}\tau(\sigma,\beta)(t)&=\left(\prod_{s\in\beta}(\sigma(s^{-1}),\sigma(s)^{-1})\right)\circ\sigma(t)\\
&=\left(\prod_{s\in\beta\cap(\beta^{\prime}\cup\beta^{\prime-1})}(s^{-1},s^{-1})\right)\circ\sigma(t)=t.\end{split}

∎

Lemma 5.20
.

Let
\sigma,\tilde{\sigma}
be two label-isomorphisms,
\beta,\tilde{\beta}\in\Sigma\cup\{\emptyset\}
, and
s\in\Sigma_{0}
such that the following properties hold:

•

\tilde{\beta}=\beta
or
\tilde{\beta}=\beta\cup\{s\}
.

•

[s,\beta]=1
or
\beta=\emptyset
.

•

For all
t\in\Sigma_{0}
such that
[t,s]=1
, we have
\sigma(t)=\tilde{\sigma}(t)
. (In particular,
\sigma(\beta)=\tilde{\sigma}(\beta)
.)

Then,
\tau(\sigma,\beta)(t)=\tau(\tilde{\sigma},\tilde{\beta})(t)
for every
t\in\Sigma_{0}
that satisfies
[t,s]=1
.

Proof.

We prove the Lemma in the case where
\tilde{\beta}=\beta\cup\{s\}
and observe at the end how the proof changes for
\tilde{\beta}=\beta
. Let
t\in\Sigma_{0}
. By definition, we have

\tau(\sigma,\beta)(t)=\prod_{r\in\beta}(\sigma(r^{-1}),\sigma(r)^{-1})\left(\sigma(t)\right),

\tau(\tilde{\sigma},\tilde{\beta})(t)=\left(\prod_{r\in\beta}(\tilde{\sigma}(r^{-1}),\tilde{\sigma}(r)^{-1})\right)\circ(\tilde{\sigma}(s^{-1}),\tilde{\sigma}(s)^{-1})\left(\tilde{\sigma}(t)\right),

where we use the fact that, for all
r\in\tilde{\beta}
, the permutations
(\tilde{\sigma}(r^{-1}),\tilde{\sigma}(r)^{-1})
commute with each other in order to place
(\tilde{\sigma}(s^{-1}),\tilde{\sigma}(s)^{-1})
to the right of the product. Using again that all the permutations in the product and
(\tilde{\sigma}(s^{-1}),\tilde{\sigma}(s)^{-1})
permute pairwise disjoint subsets of
\Sigma_{0}
, we see that both
\sigma(t)
and
\tilde{\sigma}(t)
are changed by at most one of the permutations.

Let
t\in\Sigma_{0}
such that
[t,s]=1
. Since
\sigma(t)=\tilde{\sigma}(t)
for all such
t
and
[\beta,s]=1
, we conclude that we can rewrite the second expression above as

\tau(\tilde{\sigma},\tilde{\beta})(t)=\prod_{r\in\beta}(\sigma(r^{-1}),\sigma(r)^{-1})\circ(\tilde{\sigma}(s^{-1}),\tilde{\sigma}(s)^{-1})\left(\sigma(t)\right).

This expression differs from
\tau(\sigma,\beta)(t)
if and only if
\sigma(t)\in\{\tilde{\sigma}(s^{-1}),\tilde{\sigma}(s)^{-1}\}
. Since
\tilde{\sigma}
is a label-isomorphism and
\sigma(t)=\tilde{\sigma}(t)
, this can only be the case if
[t,s]\neq 1
. We conclude that, whenever
[t,s]=1
, we have

\tau(\sigma,\beta)(t)=\tau(\tilde{\sigma},\tilde{\beta})(t).

In the case where
\tilde{\beta}=\beta
, the only thing that changes in the computations above is that the permutation
(\tilde{\sigma}(s^{-1}),\tilde{\sigma}(s)^{-1})
vanishes. As we have seen, this term does not interact with any

that satisfies
[t,s]=1
. Thus the same argument goes through and we conclude that

\tau(\sigma,\beta)(t)=\tau(\tilde{\sigma},\tilde{\beta})(t).

∎

6.
Size of stabilizer subgroups and
\text{Aut}(X)

So far, we have established a family of elements in
\text{Aut}(X)
which can be used to topologically generate stabilizers and the full automorphism group respectively. These generating sets can give us some information about the size of both the stabilizer subgroups and the full automorphism group. Before discussing these results, we recall from Section
1
that
\text{Aut}(X)
is second countable and locally compact. Since
G_{o}
is an open and closed subgroup (because
X^{(0)}
is discrete in
X
), it inherits both second countability and local compactness. We also recall from section
1.2
that second countable, locally compact groups are uncountable if and only if they are non-discrete. In this section, we prove the following two results.

Theorem 6.1
.

Let
X
be a locally finite
\CAT
cube complex,
\Gamma<\text{Aut}(X)
such that the action
\Gamma\curvearrowright X
is cocompact, and
\ell
a
\Gamma
-invariant admissible edge labeling. Suppose there exists an
o\in X^{(0)}
such that for all
v\in\mathcal{L}_{o}\setminus\{\epsilon\}
, every
\sigma
that is compatible with
v
also appears at
v
in
G_{o}
. We have the following dichotomy:

(1)

\text{Aut}(X)
is finitely generated and all its vertex-stabilizers are finite.

(2)

\text{Aut}(X)
is non-discrete.

In particular, the vertex-stabilizers are either all uncountable or all finite.

Theorem 6.2
.

Let
X
be a locally finite
\CAT
cube complex,
\Gamma<\text{Aut}(X)
such that the action
\Gamma\curvearrowright X
is vertex-transitive, and
\ell
a
\Gamma
-invariant admissible edge labeling. Then the following are equivalent:

(1)

The automorphism-group
\text{Aut}(X)
is non-discrete.

(2)

There exist some distinct
s,t\in\Sigma_{0}
such that
s\neq t^{-1}
and
[s,t]\neq 1
.

(3)

\Gamma
is not free abelian.

Remark 6.3
.

We point out that, if
\text{Aut}(X)
is discrete, then any open subgroup has to be discrete, and if
\text{Aut}(X)
is non-discrete, then any open subgroup has to be non-discrete, as it has to contain an open neighbourhood of the identity, which will be non-discrete. In particular, any open subgroup is discrete if and only if the ambient group is. Since all vertex-stabilizers are open subgroups in compact-open topology, the dichotomy in the first theorem implies that all vertex-stabilizers are either uncountable or finite, while the second theorem implicitly characterize when one (and thus all) vertex-stabilizers are non-discrete and uncountable.

Key to the proof of both theorems is the following question:

Question
.

Let
o,v\in X^{(0)}
. Is there a non-trivial label-isomorphism
\sigma:\Sigma_{0,v}\rightarrow\Sigma_{0,v}
that appears at
v
in
G_{o}
?

As we will see, there are many cases where a non-trivial label-isomorphism
\sigma
exists for every pair
o,v\in X^{(0)}
. However, the following example illustrates that this can fail.

Example 6.4
.

Let
X
be the order-5 square tiling of the hyperbolic plane, that is the tiling of the plane by squares such that every vertex has exactly five incident squares. This is the cubulation of a surface group and the universal covering of a compact special cube complex. Thus, we can find a suitable labeling on this cubulation to describe the group
G_{o}
for some vertex
o\in X^{(0)}
. However, one easily checks that for any
o
,
G_{o}\cong D_{5}
, where
D_{5}
denotes the fifth dihedral group, as the stabilizer subgroup of
o
is exactly the group of isometries on the boundary of the union of the five squares incident to
o
.

The issue here is that, whenever we choose a vertex
v\in X^{(0)}\setminus\{o\}
, the only permutation that is compatible with
v
is the identity, regardless of the choice of admissible labeling. As such, we conclude that, if an element
g\in G_{o}
fixes the five edges incident to
o
, then
g
has to be the identity on all of
X
. This implies that every
g\in G_{o}
is determined by its action on the edges incident to
o
and there are only finitely many permutations of these edges. For the generating family we produced in Constructions
5.21
and
5.23
, this phenomenon materializes in that there are only finitely many
A_{v,\sigma}
that are non-trivial, specifically there are ten such elements, all of the form
A_{\epsilon,\sigma}
for some
\sigma
.

We emphasize that this is not a counterexample to Theorem

By Corollary A.4 in

Proof.

\forall t\in C(s)\cup\{s\}:\quad\prescript{}{\epsilon\cdot}{\sigma(g)}(t)=t.

If

It follows by induction that, for every word

Let

7.3.

\text{Aut}(X)

.

Remark 7.17
.

It seems likely that this non-compactness phenomenon can be reproduced in many other examples. Notably, the Salvetti complex of the right-angled Artin group

\Gamma:=\langle a,b,c|[a,b]=1\rangle,

a cube complex that is known as the ‘tree of flats’, likely also has non-cocompact
\text{Aut}^{+}(X)
. The structure of
X_{CK}
is particularly convenient to produce a formal proof, because any automorphism in
X_{CK}
that fixes a halfspace can only fix edge labels, or send them to their inverses. However, the geometric phenomena exploited in the proof are likely to persist in many other examples.
