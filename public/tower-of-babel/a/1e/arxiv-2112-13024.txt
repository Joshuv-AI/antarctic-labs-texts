On the mutual visibility in Cartesian products and triangle-free graphs
Serafino Cicerone, Gabriele Di Stefano, Sandi Klavžar

Abstract
Given a graph G = ( V ⁡ ( G ) , E ⁡ ( G ) ) G=(V(G),E(G)) and a set P ⊆ V ⁡ ( G ) P\subseteq V(G) , the following concepts have been recently introduced: ( i ) (i) two elements of P P are mutually visible if there is a shortest path between them without further elements of P P ; ( i ​ i ) (ii) P P is a mutual-visibility set if its elements are pairwise mutually visible; ( i ​ i ​ i ) (iii) the mutual-visibility number of G G is the size of any largest mutual-visibility set. In this work we continue to investigate about these concepts. We first focus on mutual-visibility in Cartesian products. For this purpose, too, we introduce and investigate independent mutual-visibility sets. In the very special case of the Cartesian product of two complete graphs the problem is shown to be equivalent to the well-known Zarenkiewicz’s problem. We also characterize the triangle-free graphs with the mutual-visibility number equal to 3 3 .

Introduction

Let
G=(V(G),E(G))
be a connected, undirected graph and
X\subseteq V(G)
a subset of the vertices of
G
. Two vertices of
X
are
mutually visible
if there exists a shortest path (also called
geodesic
) between them without further vertices in
X
.
X
is a
mutual-visibility set
if its vertices are pairwise mutually visible. If
X\subseteq V(G)
and
x,y\in X
, then we say that
x
and
y
are
X
-visible
, if there exists a shortest
x,y
-path
P
such that
V(P)\cap X=\{x,y\}
. The size of a largest mutual-visibility set is the
mutual-visibility number
of
G
, and it is denoted by
\mu(G)
.

In
[
8
]
, the author started the study about this invariant and the mutual-visibility sets in some classes of graphs, after showing that the problem of finding a mutual-visibility set with a size larger than a given number is NP-complete.
There are different motivations for addressing this problem. The first comes from the role that mutual visibility plays in problems arising in the context of distributed computing by mobile entities. Another derives from communication problems in computer/social networks, where vertices of a network in mutual visibility may represent entities that want to efficiently send data in such a way that the exchanged messages do not pass through other entities.

Related work.

The past two decades have seen rapid growth and development of the field of distributed computing by mobile entities
[
12
]
, whose aim is the study of the computational and complexity issues arising in systems of decentralized computational entities operating either in the Euclidean plane or in some discrete environment (e.g., a graph). In both settings, the research concern is on determining what tasks can be performed by the entities, under what conditions, and at what cost. One of such basic task is called
Mutual Visibility
and has been introduced in
[
7
]
under the assumption that three collinear entities are not mutually visible: given an arbitrary initial configuration of
n
entities located at distinct positions, they autonomously arrange themselves in a configuration in which each entity is in a distinct position and from which it can see all other entities.

Starting with this initial work, many papers have addressed the same problem (e.g., see
[
2
,
4
,
28
]
). Later, similar visibility problems were considered in different contexts, where the entities are “fat entities” modeled as disks in the Euclidean plane (e.g., see
[
29
]
) or are points on a grid based terrain and their movements are restricted only along grid lines (e.g., see
[
1
]
).

However, questions about sets of points and their mutual visibility in the Euclidean plane have been investigated since the end of XIX century. In
[
10
]
Dudeney posed the still open no-three-in-line problem: find the maximum number of points that
can be placed in an
n\times n
grid so that no three points lie on a line.
Motivated by the Dudeney’s problem, in
[
22
]
the
General Position
problem in graphs was introduced. A couple of years earlier and in a different language, an equivalent problem was posed in
[
6
]
. A subset
S
of vertices in a graph
G
is a
general position set
if no triple of vertices from
S
lie on a common geodesic in
G
. The
General Position
problem is to find a largest general position set of
G
, the order of such a set is the
general position number

{\rm gp}(G)
of
G
. Since its introduction, the general position number has been studied for several graph classes (e.g., grid networks
[
23
]
, cographs and bipartite graphs
[
3
]
, graph classes with large general position number
[
31
]
, Cartesian products of graphs
[
18
,
19
,
32
,
33
]
, and Kneser graphs
[
15
,
27
]
).

The difference between a general position set
S
and a mutual-visibility set
X
is that two vertices are in
X
if there
exists
a shortest path between them with no further vertex in
X
, whereas two vertices are in
S
if for
every
shortest path between them no further vertex is in
S
. The two concepts are intrinsically different, but also closely related, since the vertices of a general position set are in mutual visibility.

Outline and results.

In the rest of the introduction, we provide basic definitions and notation. In the next section, independent mutual-visibility sets are introduced and a couple of related results proved. We use these sets in Section
3
to bound the mutual-visibilty number of Cartesian product graphs. Then we prove that if a maximum mutual-visibility set of a graph
G
is independent, then
\mu(K_{k}\,\square\,G)=k\cdot\mu(G)
. For the very special case of
\mu(K_{m}\,\square\,K_{n})
we show that its computation is equivalent to a special case of Zarenkiewicz’s problem, which is a notorious open problem. The main result is given in Section
4
, where we prove that if
G
is a connected, triangle-free graph, then
\mu(G)=3
if and only if
G
is a tree with three leaves or a so-called frog graph.

Basic definitions.

Since two vertices from different components of a graph are not mutually visible, all graphs in the paper are connected unless stated otherwise.

For a natural number
n
, we set
[n]=\{1,\ldots,n\}
. Given a graph
G
,
V(G)
and
E(G)
are used to denote its vertex set and its edge set, respectively. The order of
G
, that is
|V(G)|
, is denoted by
n(G)
. The distance function
d_{G}
on a graph
G
is the usual shortest-path distance. The
diameter

{\rm diam}(G)
of
G
is the maximum distance between pairs of vertices of the graph.

If
X\subseteq V(G)
, then
G[X]
denotes the subgraph of
G
induced by
X
. We denote by
P_{k}
any induced path with
k\geq 1
vertices, by
C_{k}
any chordless cycle with
k\geq 3
vertices, and by
K_{k}
the clique with
k\geq 1
vertices. Given a cycle
C
, two vertices
u,v
of
C
are
antipodal
if
d_{C}(u,v)=\left\lfloor\frac{n(C)}{2}\right\rfloor
.

An independent set is a set of vertices of
G
, no two of which are adjacent. The cardinality of a largest independent set is the
independence number

\alpha(G)
of
G
.

A subgraph
G^{\prime}
of a graph
G
is
isometric
, if for every two vertices of
G^{\prime}
, the distance between them in
G^{\prime}
equals the distance in
G
. The subgraph
G^{\prime}
is
convex
, if for every two vertices of
G^{\prime}
, every shortest path in
G
between them lies completely in
G^{\prime}
. Clearly, each convex subgraph is isometric.

2
Independent mutual-visibility number

In this section we introduce independent mutual-visibility sets. They will turn out to be useful in the next section, but we believe that they may also be of independent interest.

Let
X
be a mutual-visibility set of a graph
G
. If
G[X]
is edgeless, then we say that
X
is an
independent mutual-visibility set
. The
independent mutual-visibility number

\mu_{i}(G)
of
G
is the size of a largest independent mutual-visibility set. Clearly, if
G
is an arbitrary graph, then

\mu_{i}(G)\leq\min\{\mu(G),\alpha(G)\}\,.

(1)

If
G
has small diameter, then we have the equality, more precisely, the following holds.

Lemma 2.1

If
{\rm diam}(G)\leq 3
, then
\mu_{i}(G)=\alpha(G)
.

Proof.
If
{\rm diam}(G)=1
, then
G
is a complete graph and hence
\mu_{i}(G)=1=\alpha(G)
. Suppose next that
{\rm diam}(G)\in\{2,3\}
and let
X
be an arbitrary independent set of
G
. We claim that
X
is also an independent mutual-visibility set. Let
x,y
be arbitrary vertices from
X
and let
P
be a shortest
x,y
-path. Since
X
is an independent set,
P
is of length
2
or
3
. But then, again using the fact that
X
is independent,
V(P)\cap X=\{x,y\}
. This means that
x
and
y
are
X
-visible and consequently
\mu_{i}(G)\geq\alpha(G)
. The argument is completed by applying (
1
).

\square

Lemma
2.1
need not hold for graphs
G
with
{\rm diam}(G)\geq 4
. For instance,
\mu_{i}(P_{5})=2
and
\alpha(P_{5})=3
.

In view of Lemma
2.1
we are interested in the graphs
G
for which
\mu(G)=\mu_{i}(G)
holds. Additional reasons for this interest will be given in Section
3
.

As the first example note that
\mu(C_{k})=3=\mu_{i}(C_{k})
holds for each
k\geq 6
. From
[
8
, Corollary 4.3]
we know that if
L
is the set of leaves of a tree
T
, then
L
is a mutual-visibility set and
\mu(T)=|L|
. Hence, if
n(T)\geq 3
, then

\mu(T)=|L|=\mu_{i}(T)\,.

(2)

The
corona

G\circ H
of disjoint graphs
G
and
H
was introduced in
[
13
]
as the graph obtained from the disjoint union of
G
and
n(G)
copies of
H
by joining the
i^{\rm th}
vertex of
G
to every vertex in the
i^{\rm th}
copy of
H
. This graph operation has so far been explored from many aspects, see
[
9
,
14
,
21
]
. Here we add the following result which in particular yields a large class of graphs with
\mu=\mu_{i}
.

Proposition 2.2

If
G
is a (connected) graph with
n(G)\geq 2
, and
H
is an arbitrary graph, then

\mu(G\circ H)=n(G)n(H)\,.

Moreover, for
k\geq 1
,
\mu(G\circ\overline{K}_{k})=k\cdot n(G)=\mu_{i}(G\circ\overline{K}_{k})
.

Proof.
Let
V(G)=\{v_{1},\ldots,v_{n(G)}\}
, and let
H_{i}
be the copy of
H
whose vertices are in
G\circ H
adjacent to
v_{i}
,
i\in[n(G)]
. Then
(G\circ H)[V(H_{i})\cup\{v_{i}\}]
is the join of the one vertex graph
K_{1}
and the graph
H_{i}
.
If
H_{i}
is not complete, we can see it directly or deduce from
[
8
, Corollary 4.10]
that

\mu((G\circ H)[V(H_{i})])=n(H)
. Hence, no matter whether
H
is complete or not,
\cup_{i=1}^{n(G)}V(H_{i})
is a mutual-visibility set of
G\circ H
and consequently
\mu(G\circ H)\geq n(G)n(H)
.

For the reversed inequality assume on the contrary that
\mu(G\circ H)>n(G)n(H)
. Let
X
be an arbitrary mutual-visibility set of
G\circ H
of order
\mu(G\circ H)
. Then
X
necessarily contains at least one vertex of
G
. That is, setting
k=|X\cap V(G)|
, we have
k\geq 1
. Note that if
v_{i}\in X
, then, since
n(G)\geq 2
, we have
V(H_{i})\cap X=\emptyset
. It follows that
|X|\leq k+(n(G)-k)n(H)=n(G)n(H)-k(n(H)-1)\leq n(G)n(H)
. This contradiction implies that
\mu(G\circ H)\leq n(G)n(H)
.

The second assertion of the theorem follows from the already proved assertion and the fact that the set
\cup_{i=1}^{n(G)}V(H_{i})
is independent when
H=\overline{K}_{k}
.

\square

We point out that in Proposition
2.2
the graph
H
need not be connected because
G\circ H
is connected no matter whether
H
is connected or not.

3
Mutual-visibility in Cartesian products

In this section we first give an upper and a lower bound on the mutual-visibility number of general Cartesian product graphs, where the latter bound is expressed in terms of the independent mutual-visibility number. Then we prove that if
\mu_{i}(G)=\mu(G)
, then
\mu(K_{k}\,\square\,G)=k\cdot\mu(G)
. In the second part we reduce the problem of computing
\mu(K_{m}\,\square\,K_{n})
to the well-known Zarenkiewitz’s problem
[
17
,
25
,
34
,
35
]
. This implies that to compute
\mu(G\,\square\,H)
is an intrinsically difficult problem.

Recall that the
Cartesian product

G\,\square\,H
of graphs
G
and
H
has the vertex set
V(G)\times V(H)
and the edge set
E(G\,\square\,H)=\{(g,h)(g^{\prime},h^{\prime}):\ gg^{\prime}\in E(G)\mbox{ and }h=h^{\prime},\mbox{ or, }g=g^{\prime}\mbox{ and }hh^{\prime}\in E(H)\}
. If
(g,h)\in V(G\,\square\,H)
, then the
G
-layer

G^{h}
through the vertex
(g,h)
is the subgraph of
G\,\square\,H
induced by the vertices
\{(g^{\prime},h):\ g^{\prime}\in V(G)\}
. Similarly, the
H
-layer

{}^{g}\!H
through
(g,h)
is the subgraph of
G\,\square\,H
induced by the vertices
\{(g,h^{\prime}):\ h^{\prime}\in V(H)\}
. Recall further the following known result. (For more information on the Cartesian product we refer to the book
[
16
]
.)

Lemma 3.1

If
G
and
H
are graphs and
(g,h),(g^{\prime},h^{\prime})\in V(G\,\square\,H)
, then

d_{G\,\square\,H}((g,h),(g^{\prime},h^{\prime}))=d_{G}(g,g^{\prime})+\,d_{H}(h,h^{\prime})\,.

Our first result on Cartesian products reads as follows.

Theorem 3.2

If
G
and
H
are graphs, then the following holds.

\displaystyle(i)

\displaystyle\quad\mu(G\,\square\,H)\leq\min\{\mu(G)n(H),\mu(H)n(G)\}\,,

\displaystyle(ii)

\displaystyle\quad\mu(G\,\square\,H)\geq\max\{\mu(G)\mu_{i}(H),\mu(H)\mu_{i}(G)\}\,.

Proof.
(i) If
h\in V(H)
, then the
G
-layer
G^{h}
is a convex subgraph of
G\,\square\,H
, hence each pair of vertices from
V(G^{h})
can only be mutually visible inside the layer. Applying this fact to each vertex of
H
we get that
\mu(G\,\square\,H)\leq n(H)\mu(G)
, cf.
[
8
, Lemma 2.3]
. Analogously we see that
\mu(G\,\square\,H)\leq n(G)\mu(H)
also holds.

(ii) Let
X_{G}
be a mutual-visibility set of
G
of size
\mu(G)
, and let
X_{H}
be an independent mutual-visibility set of
H
of size
\mu_{i}(H)
. We claim that
X=X_{G}\times X_{H}
is a mutual-visibility set of
G\,\square\,H
.

Let
x=(g,h)
and
y=(g^{\prime},h^{\prime})
be different vertices of
X
. We need to show
x
and
y
are
X
-visible in
G\,\square\,H
. Suppose first that
h=h^{\prime}
. Then
x
and
y
both lie in the
G
-layer
G^{h}
. Since
G^{h}
is a convex subgraph of
G\,\square\,H
isomorphic to
G
, and
X_{G}
is a mutual-visibility set of
G
, the vertices
x
and
y
are
X
-visible in
G\,\square\,H
. In the rest we may thus assume that
h\neq h^{\prime}
.

Since
X_{G}
is a mutual-visibility set of
G
, there exists a shortest
g,g^{\prime}
-path
P_{G}
such that
V(P_{G})\cap X_{G}=\{g,g^{\prime}\}
. (In the case when
g=g^{\prime}
, the path
P_{G}
consists of a single vertex
g=g^{\prime}
.) Further, since
X_{H}
is a mutual-visibility set of
H
, there exists a shortest
h,h^{\prime}
-path
P_{H}
such that
V(P_{H})\cap X_{H}=\{h,h^{\prime}\}
. Let
h^{\prime\prime}
be the neighbor of
h
on
P_{H}
. Since
X_{H}
is independent we infer that
h^{\prime\prime}\neq h^{\prime}
. Consider now the
x,y
-path
P_{G\,\square\,H}
defined as follows:
P_{G\,\square\,H}
starts with the edge
(g,h)(g,h^{\prime\prime})
, then continues along the copy of
P_{G}
in the layer
G^{h^{\prime\prime}}
, and ends with the copy of the
h^{\prime\prime},h^{\prime}
-subpath of
P_{H}
in the layer
{}^{g^{\prime}}\!H
. (Note that if
g=g^{\prime}
, then
P_{G\,\square\,H}
is simply the copy of
P_{H}
in the layer
{}^{g}\!H
.) Using Lemma
3.1
it follows that
P_{G\,\square\,H}
is a shortest
x,y
-path in
G\,\square\,H
. Moreover, since
V(P_{G\,\square\,H})\cap X=\{x,y\}
we conclude that
X
is a mutual-visibility set. Hence
\mu(G\,\square\,H)\geq\mu(G)\mu_{i}(H)
. By the commutativity of the Cartesian product we also have
\mu(G\,\square\,H)\geq\mu(H)\mu_{i}(G)
and the result follows.

\square

Theorem
3.2
(ii) together with (
2
) yields the following consequence which should be compared with the main theorem of
[
33
]
asserting that
{\rm gp}(T_{1}\,\square\,T_{2})={\rm gp}(T_{1})+{\rm gp}(T_{2})
holds for trees
T_{1}
and
T_{2}
of order at least
3
.

Corollary 3.3

If
T_{1}
and
T_{2}
are trees of order at least
3
, then
\mu(T_{1}\,\square\,T_{2})\geq\mu(T_{1})\mu(T_{2})
.

The bound of Theorem
3.2
(i) is sharp. For instance, it was proved in
[
8
, Theorem 4.6]
that if
r>3
and
s>3
, then
\mu(P_{r}\,\square\,P_{s})=2\cdot\min\{r,s\}
. Moreover, the two bounds of Theorem
3.2
can coincide. We demonstrate this by the next result in which we determine the mutual-visibility number of a large class of Cartesian product graphs.

Theorem 3.4

If
k\geq 2
and
G
is a graph with
\mu(G)=\mu_{i}(G)
, then

\mu(K_{k}\,\square\,G)=k\cdot\mu(G)\,.

Proof.
Since
\mu(K_{k})=k
, Theorem
3.2
(i) yields

\mu(K_{k}\,\square\,G)\leq n(K_{k})\cdot\mu(G)=k\cdot\mu(G)\,.

On the other hand, using Theorem
3.2
(ii) and the assumption
\mu(G)=\mu_{i}(G)
, we get

\mu(K_{k}\,\square\,G)\geq\mu(K_{k})\cdot\mu_{i}(G)=k\cdot\mu(G)

and we are done.

\square

3.1
Reduction of
\mu(K_{m}\,\square\,K_{n})
to Zarankievicz’s problem

The following lemma is the key for the reduction from the title.

Lemma 3.5

Let
r,s\geq 2
and let
X\subseteq V(K_{r}\,\square\,K_{s})
. Then
X
is a mutual-visibility set of
K_{r}\,\square\,K_{s}
if and only if
|X\cap V(C)|\leq 3
holds for each induced
4
-cycle
C
of
K_{r}\,\square\,K_{s}
.

Proof.
Let
V(K_{k})=[k]
and set
G=K_{r}\,\square\,K_{s}
. Suppose first that
X
is a mutual-visibility set of
G
. Consider an arbitrary induced
4
-cycle
C
of
G
. Then
C
has consecutive vertices of the form
(i,k),(j,k),(j,\ell),(i,\ell)
, where
i\neq j
and
k\neq\ell
. In particular,
C
is a convex subgraph of
G
and hence
|X\cap V(C_{4})|\leq 3
must hold.

Conversely, let
X\subseteq V(K_{r}\,\square\,K_{s})
be such that
|X\cap V(C)|\leq 3
holds for each induced
4
-cycle
C
of
G
. Consider arbitrary vertices
x=(i,k)
and
y=(j,\ell)
of
X
. If either
i=j
or
k=\ell
, then
x
and
y
lie either in a common
K_{s}
-layer or in a common
K_{r}
-layer. In either case,
x
and
y
are adjacent and hence
X
-visible. Suppose next that
i\neq j
and
k\neq\ell
. Then the vertices
x
and
y
lie in the convex
4
-cycle
C
with the consecutive vertices
(i,k),(j,k),(j,\ell),(i,\ell)
. Since
|X\cap C|\leq 3
, we may without loss of generality assume that
(j,k)\notin X
. But then the path
x=(i,k),(j,k),(j,\ell)=y
demonstrates that
x
and
y
are
X
-visible. Hence we can conclude that
X
is a mutual-visibility set of
G
.

\square

Each induced
4
-cycle
C
of
K_{r}\,\square\,K_{s}
can be written as
K_{2}\,\square\,K^{\prime}_{2}
, where
K_{2}
is the projection of
C
onto
G
and
K_{2}^{\prime}
is the projection of
C
onto
H
. One often says that such a
4
-cycle is a
Cartesian square
. With this terminology in hand, Lemma
3.5
asserts that the problem of computing
\mu(K_{r}\,\square\,K_{s})
is to find a largest subset of vertices
X
, so that
X
intersects each Cartesian square in at most three vertices. This brings us to the following:

Definition 3.6
(Zarankiewicz’s Problem)

Let
m
,
n
,
s
, and
t
be given positive integers. Determine
z(m,n;s,t)
the maximum number of
1
s that an
m\times n
binary matrix can have provided that it contains no constant
s\times t
submatrix of
1
s.

From Lemma
3.5
we deduce:

Corollary 3.7

If
m,n\geq 2
, then
\mu(K_{m}\,\square\,K_{n})=z(m,n;2,2)
.

Values
z(m,n;s,t)
have been extensively studied, cf.
[
34
]
, but even the values
z(m,n;2,2)
are not known. We recall the following most relevant results that can be found in
[
34
]
, the first two as Theorems 13.2.19. and Theorem 13.2.21.

Theorem 3.8

[
20
, Kővári–-Sós-Turán, 1954]

For
s,t>1
,

z(m,n;s,t)<(s-1)^{1/t}(n-t+1)m^{1-1/t}+(t-1)m\,.

Corollary
3.7
together with Theorem
3.8
for the case
s=t=2
yields:

\mu(K_{m}\,\square\,K_{n})=z(m,n;2,2)<(n-1)\sqrt{m}+m=(n-1)\sqrt{m}+m\,.

Theorem 3.9

[
5
,
11
, Brown, 1966; Erdős-Rényi-Sós, 1966]

When n is sufficiently large,

n^{3/2}-n^{4/3}\leq z(n,n;2,2)\leq\frac{1}{4}n(1+\sqrt{4n-3})\,.

Theorem 3.10

[
34
, Theorem 13.2.24.]

If
\alpha=\frac{s-1}{st-1}
and
\beta=\frac{t-1}{st-1}
,
then

z(m,n;s,t)\geq\left\lfloor\left(1-\frac{1}{s!\,t!}\right)m^{1-\alpha}n^{1-\beta}\right\rfloor\,.

We note in passing that the usual construction leading to this lower bound(s) is to use projective planes. In the special case of interest to us, Corollary
3.7
together with Theorem
3.10
yields:

\mu(K_{m}\,\square\,K_{n})=z(m,n;2,2)\geq\left\lfloor\frac{3}{4}m^{2/3}n^{2/3}\right\rfloor\,.

•

Lemma 4.1

If
G^{\prime}
is an isometric subgraph of
G
, then
\mu(G)\geq\mu(G^{\prime})
.

Proof.

Let
X
be a mutual-visibility set of
G^{\prime}
. For each
u,v\in X
there is a shortest
u,v
-path
P
in
G^{\prime}
such that
X\cap V(P)=\{u,v\}
. Since
P
is a shortest
u,v
-path also in
G
, then
u,v
are mutually visible also in
G
. Hence
X
is also a mutual-visibility set of
G
and thus
\mu(G)\geq\mu(G^{\prime})
.

\square

Since a convex subgraph is an isometric, Lemma
4.1
extends
[
8
, Lemma 2.2]
which is stated for convex subgraphs. We also need the following fact based on
\Delta(G)
, the maximum degree of a vertex in a graph

Lemma 4.2

[

If

\mu(G)\geq\Delta(G)
.

(

, respectively) with an external vertex of

(
P_{s}
, respectively). See Fig.

and

are called

of the frog graph. Notice that each cycle graph is also a frog graph (it occurs when

Lemma 4.3

If the graph
H
is a subgraph of a triangle-free graph
G
, then
\mu(G)\geq 4
.

Proof.

Consider a subgraph
H
of
G
and let
X=\{x,y,w,z\}
be the set containing the four pendant vertices of
H
as described in Fig.
2
.

We claim that
X
is a mutual-visibility set of
G
. As
G
is triangle-free, the
x,y
-path of length
2
in
H
is a shortest path in
G
and hence
x
and
y
are
X
-visible in
G
. Symmetrically,
w
and
z
are
X
-visible in
G
. It remains to show that each pair of vertices at distance
3
in

is
X
-visible in
G
. It suffices to consider the pair
x,z
because the argument for the other three such pairs is analogous. Clearly,
d_{G}(x,z)\leq d_{H}(x,z)=3
. There is nothing to prove if
d_{G}(x,z)=1
. Suppose
d_{G}(x,z)=2
. If
x
and

are not
X
-visible in

, each common neighbor of

and

must be from
\{y,w\}
, but this is not possible as

is triangle-free. Finally, if
d_{G}(x,z)=3
, then

and

are clearly
X
-visible in
G
. This proves the claim which in turn implies that
\mu(G)\geq 4
.

\square

All is now ready for our main result.

Theorem 4.4

Let
G
be a connected, triangle-free graph. Then
\mu(G)=3
if and only if
G
is a tree with three leaves or a frog graph.

Proof.

(
\Leftarrow
) If
G
is a tree with three leaves, then
\mu(G)=3
as proved in
[
8
, Corollary 4.3]
. If
G
is a frog graph, then let
C
be its cycle,
P_{r}
and
P_{s}
its attached paths, and
v^{r},v^{s}
the corresponding junction vertices. Since
C
is a convex subgraph of
G
(and hence also isometric), Lemma
4.1
gives
\mu(G)\geq\mu(C)
. This implies
\mu(G)\geq 3
since
\mu(C)=3
for any cycle (cf.
[
8
, Lemma 2.8]
). Assume now that there exists in
G
a mutual-visibility set
X
with
|X|\geq 4
. Notice first that
|P_{r}\cap X|\leq 1
and
|P_{s}\cap X|\leq 1
must hold. Indeed, if, say,
P_{r}\cap X=\{u_{1},u_{2}\}
and
u_{3}\in(P_{s}\cup C)\cap X
, then either
u_{3}
and
u_{1}
or
u_{3}
and
u_{2}
are not
X
-visible. Notice also that
X
cannot be completely contained in
C
. If
P_{r}\cap X=\{u_{1}\}
, let
u_{2},u_{3}\in X
be the vertices of
C
closest to
v^{r}
. Consider the two
v^{r},v^{s}
-paths of
C
:
u_{2}
and
u_{3}
must belong to the same path, otherwise
u_{1}
and any additional vertex
u_{4}\in X
are not
X
-visible. But, if
u_{2}
and
u_{3}
belong to the same path, then since
v^{r}
and
v^{s}
are antipodal,
u_{1},u_{2},u_{3}
are not pairwise
X
-visible. Then
\mu(G)\leq 3
.

(
\Rightarrow
) Since
\mu(G)=3
, Lemma
4.2
implies that
\Delta(G)\leq 3
. If
\Delta(G)=2
, then
G
cannot be a path as we would have
\mu(G)\leq 2
. In the rest we may assume that
\Delta(G)=3
. We analyze different cases according to the number of cycles in
G
.

Assume that
G
has no cycles. Then
G
is a tree with
\Delta(G)=3
and
\mu(G)=3
. Moreover,
G
must have exactly three leaves, for otherwise
\mu(G)\geq 4
would hold.

Assume that
G
has a single cycle
C
. We show that
G
is a frog graph. Recall that
\Delta(G)=3
and consider first the case in which
G
contains two vertices
u,v
with degree
3
and at least one of them (say
u
) is not on
C
.
In case
v
is on
C
, assume without loss of generality that
v
is the vertex of
C
with degree 3 closest to
u
.
Then, the two neighbors of
u
and the two neighbors of
v
which do not lie on a shortest
u,v
-path, form a mutual-visibility set of
G
, a contradiction with
\mu(G)=3
. Hence, if in
G
there are vertices with degree
3
, they all are on
C
. Consider the case in which there are at least three such vertices. Then at least two of them (say
u,v
) are not antipodal and hence there exists a mutual-visibility set of
G
containing the four vertices adjacent to
u
and
v
not belonging to the shortest
u,v
-path. Again a contradiction with
\mu(G)=3
. Two cases remain: (1) if
C
has a single vertex
u
such that
\deg(u)=3
, then
G
is a frog graph; (2) if
C
has two vertices
u,v
with degree
3
, they must be antipodal (otherwise we get the previous contradiction) and hence
G
is again a frog graph.

Assume finally that
G
has more than one cycle. To complete the proof of the theorem we are going to show that
\mu(G)\geq 4
. In the first subcase suppose that
G
contains two vertex-disjoint cycles
C^{\prime}
and
C^{\prime\prime}
. Let
u^{\prime}\in V(C^{\prime})
and
u^{\prime\prime}\in V(C^{\prime\prime})
be selected such that
d_{G}(u^{\prime},u^{\prime\prime})
is smallest possible. Then, having in mind that
G
is triangle-free, the four vertices in
(N(u^{\prime})\cap C^{\prime})\cup(N(u^{\prime\prime})\cap C^{\prime\prime})
are pairwise mutual-visible. Observe next that if
C^{\prime}
and
C^{\prime\prime}
are cycles of
G
, then
|V(C^{\prime})\cap V(C^{\prime\prime})|=1
is not possible because
\Delta(G)=3
. If two cycles share one single edge
uv
, then
G
contains
H
and Lemma
4.3
yields
\mu(G)\geq 4
.

It remains to analyze the case in which each pair of cycles of
G
shares more than one edge. Select a pair of cycles
C^{\prime}
,
C^{\prime\prime}
such that
n(C^{\prime})+n(C^{\prime\prime})
is as small as possible, and assume without loss of generality that
n(C^{\prime})\leq n(C^{\prime\prime})
. Then, both
C^{\prime}
and
C^{\prime\prime}
are isometric subgraphs of
G
. According to the minimality of
n(C^{\prime})+n(C^{\prime\prime})
, it follows that
C^{\prime}\cap C^{\prime\prime}=P_{n}
, where
n\geq 3
by the case assumption.

Consider the subgraph
F=G[V(C^{\prime})\cup V(C^{\prime\prime})]
and let
u,v
be the end-vertices of
P_{n}
. Then
\deg_{F}(u)=\deg_{F}(v)=3
and
u
and
v
are connected by three paths in
F
. We may without loss of generality assume that
P_{n}
is a shortest among these three
u,v
-paths. Let
P_{l}
and
P_{m}
be the other two paths such that
l\geq m\geq n
. Then
C^{\prime}=G[V(P_{n})\cup V(P_{m})]
and
C^{\prime\prime}=G[V(P_{n})\cup V(P_{l})]
.

Let
x,y,z
be three vertices such that:

•

P_{l}=(u,\ldots,z,\ldots,v)
, where

z
divides
P_{l}
in the
u,z
-subpath of length
d\geq 1
and the
z,v
-subpath of length
l-d-1
;

•

P_{n}=(u,x,\ldots,v)
, where

x
divides
P_{n}
in the
u,x
-subpath of length
1
and the
x,v
-subpath of length
n-2
;

•

P_{m}=(u,\ldots,y,\ldots,v)
, where

y
divides
P_{m}
in the
u,y
-subpath of length
d^{\prime}\geq 1
and the
y,v
-subpath of length

.

We set now a condition for the position of
z
on

such that
x,v,z
are mutually visible in
C^{\prime\prime}
. To this aim, the following conditions must hold:

•

(l-1-d)\leq(1+d)+(n-2)
;

•

(1+d)\leq(l-1-d)+(n-2)
;

•

(n-2)\leq(l-1-d)+(1+d)
.

The last condition corresponds to
l\geq n-2
, which holds by hypothesis. The first two conditions yield the following constraints about

:

l-n\leq 2d\leq l+n-4\,.

(3)

This implies that when the length
d
of the
u,z
-subpath fulfills (
3
),
x,v,z
are mutually visible in
C^{\prime\prime}
. Analogously in
C^{\prime}
, by letting
d^{\prime}
fulfilling the following constraints,

m-n\leq 2d^{\prime}\leq m+n-4,

(4)

then we get that
x,v,y
are mutually visible in
C^{\prime}
.

We now check whether (
3
) and (
4
) are sufficient to have that
X=\{v,x,y,z\}
is a mutually visible set of
F
. To ensure this property, we need to prove the following: (i)
y
does not obstruct the visibility of
z
and
v
; (ii)
z
does not obstruct the visibility of
y
and
v
;
(iii) both
x
and
v
do not obstruct the visibity of
y
and
z
, that is, the
y,z
-path passing through
u
and with length
d+d^{\prime}
is a shortest path in
F
.

Concerning proving (i), it corresponds to show that
(l-1-d)\leq d+d^{\prime}+(m-1-d^{\prime})
. The latter is equivalent to
2d\geq l-m
, which is guaranteed by (
3
). Similarly, (ii) corresponds to show that
(m-1-d^{\prime})\leq d+d^{\prime}+(l-1-d)
; this is equivalent to
2d^{\prime}\geq m-l
which trivially holds since
l\geq m\geq n
.

Concerning proving (iii), it corresponds to show that all the following relationships hold:

•

d+d^{\prime}\leq(m-1-d^{\prime})+(n-2)+(1+d)
.
This relationship holds by (
4
).

•

d+d^{\prime}\leq(d^{\prime}+1)+(n-2)+(l-1-d)
.
This relationship holds by (
3
).

•

d+d^{\prime}\leq(m-1-d^{\prime})+(l-1-d)
.
It corresponds to
2d+2d^{\prime}\leq m+l-2=(m+n-4)+(l-n+2)
. Since
2d^{\prime}\leq m+n-4
by (
4
), if we overwrite (
3
) by further imposing

l-n\leq 2d\leq l-n+2\,,

(5)

then we finally get that

is a mutually visible set of

.

In the rest of the proof we select
d
and
d^{\prime}
such that they fulfill (
4
) and (
5
), respectively, and fix them. In this way the vertices
z
and
y
are uniquely defined.

Assume now that
X
is not a mutual-visibility set of
G
. The only possibility for this is due to some path
P_{t}
that connects a vertex in
P_{l}
to a vertex in
P_{m}
. It can be easily observed that this occurs when
P_{t}
connects
z
and
y
and two vertices in
X
are not mutually visible, that is when one of the following pairs is not mutually visible:

(a)

y
and
v
(that is,
z
obstructs the visibility of
y
and
v
);

(b)

z
and
v
(that is,
y
obstructs the visibility of
z
and
v
);

(c)

z
and
x
(that is,
y
obstructs the visibility of
z
and
x
);

(d)

y
and
x
(that is,
z
obstructs the visibility of
y
and
x
).

Assume that
P_{t}
has no vertex in common with the
y,z
-path passing on
u
in
F
. We now prove that, in each of the above cases, the cycle
C^{\prime\prime\prime}
formed by
P_{t}
, by the
y,u
-subpath of
P_{m}
of length
d^{\prime}
, and by the
u,z
-subpath of
P_{l}
of length
d
has order less than
n(C^{\prime\prime})
, that is

n(C^{\prime\prime\prime})=(t-1)+d+d^{\prime}<n(C^{\prime\prime})=l+n-2<l+n
,
which is a contradiction with the minimality of
n(C^{\prime})+n(C^{\prime\prime})
. Notice that the latter is equivalent to

t<l+n-d-d^{\prime}+1.

(6)

Consider the case
(a)
, in which
z
obstructs the visibility of
y
and
v
. In this case, the length
t-1
of the
y,z
-path
P_{t}
must guarantee
(t-1)+(l-1-d)<m-1-d^{\prime}
, that is:

t<m+d-d^{\prime}-l+1.

(7)

According to (
7
), to prove (
6
) it is enough to show that the following holds:

l+n-d-d^{\prime}+1\geq m+d-d^{\prime}-l+1.

Notice that this inequality is equivalent to
2d\leq n+2l-m
,
which trivially holds by using (
5
).

Consider
(b)
. In this case, the length
t-1
of the
y,z
-path
P_{t}
must guarantee
(t-1)+(m-1-d^{\prime})<l-1-d
. By using the same approach as in case
(a)
, this relationship still leads to prove that
n(C^{\prime\prime\prime})<n(C^{\prime\prime})
. In fact, it is equivalent to showing that
2d^{\prime}\leq m+n
, which is trivially implied by (
4
).

Consider
(c)
. In this case, the length
t-1
of the
y,z
-path
P_{t}
must guarantee
(t-1)+d^{\prime}+1<d+1
. Proving
n(C^{\prime\prime\prime})<n(C^{\prime\prime})
is equivalent to showing that
2d\leq l+n
, which is trivially implied by (

).

Consider
(d)
. In this case, the length
t-1
of the
y,z
-path
P_{t}
must guarantee
(t-1)+d+1<d^{\prime}+1
. Proving
n(C^{\prime\prime\prime})<n(C^{\prime\prime})
is equivalent to showing that
2d^{\prime}\leq l+n
, which is implied by (
4
) and by the assumption

.

This proves the claim that
X
is a mutual-visibility set of

when

has no vertex in common with the
y,z
-path

passing on

in

. Assume now that

shares some vertex with such a path

.

\square

5
Concluding remarks

As a continuation of the present investigation, it would in particular be desirable to characterize the trees for which the equality holds in Corollary
3.3
, and to characterize all graphs
G
with
\mu(G)=3
.

As pointed out in the introduction, mutual-visibility sets are conceptually similar to general position sets. Hence it would be interesting to investigate graphs
G
with
{\rm gp}(G)=\mu(G)
, where
{\rm gp}(G)
is the general position number of
G
. For instance, the equality holds in geodetic graphs, that is, graphs with the property that each pair of vertices is connected by a unique shortest path, cf.
[
24
,
26
,
30
]
.

Note that the Petersen graph is geodetic. In this respect it would be of interest to consider

\mu(G)
for graph
G
with
{\rm diam}(G)=2
.
