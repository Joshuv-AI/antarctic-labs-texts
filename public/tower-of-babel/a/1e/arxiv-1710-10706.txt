Disjunctive Bases: Normal Forms and Model Theory for Modal Logics
Sebastian Enqvist \rsuper a, Yde Venema \rsuper b

Abstract
. We present the concept of a disjunctive basis as a generic framework for normal forms in modal logic based on coalgebra. Disjunctive bases were defined in previous work on completeness for modal fixpoint logics, where they played a central role in the proof of a generic completeness theorem for coalgebraic mu-calculi. Believing the concept has a much wider significance, here we investigate it more thoroughly in its own right. We show that the presence of a disjunctive basis at the “one-step” level entails a number of good properties for a coalgebraic mu-calculus, in particular, a simulation theorem showing that every alternating automaton can be transformed into an equivalent nondeterministic one. Based on this, we prove a Lyndon theorem for the full fixpoint logic, its fixpoint-free fragment and its one-step fragment, and a Uniform Interpolation result, for both the full mu-calculus and its fixpoint-free fragment. We also raise the questions, when a disjunctive basis exists, and how disjunctive bases are related to Moss’ coalgebraic “nabla” modalities. Nabla formulas provide disjunctive bases for many coalgebraic modal logics, but there are cases where disjunctive bases give useful normal forms even when nabla formulas fail to do so, our prime example being graded modal logic. We also show that disjunctive bases are preserved by forming sums, products and compositions of coalgebraic modal logics, providing tools for modular construction of modal logics admitting disjunctive bases. Finally, we consider the problem of giving a category-theoretic formulation of disjunctive bases, and provide a partial solution. This is a corrected version of the paper https://arxiv.org/abs/1710.10706 published originally on 26/3, 2019.

1.
Introduction

The topic of this paper connects modal
\mu
-calculi, coalgebra and automata.
The connection between the modal
\mu
-calculus, as introduced by
Kozen
[
20
]
, and automata running on infinite objects, is
standard
[
15
]
.
Many of the most fundamental results about the modal
\mu
-calculus have been
proved by making use of this connection, including
completeness of Kozen’s axiom system
[
37
]
,
and model theoretic results like expressive completeness
[
19
]
,
uniform interpolation and a Lyndon theorem
[
6
]
.

The standard modal
\mu
-calculus was generalized to generic, coalgebraic modal

\mu
-calculi
[
35
]
, of which the modal basis was provided by Moss’
original coalgebraic modality
[
27
]
, now known as the
nabla

modality.
From a meta-logical perspective, Moss’ nabla logics and their fixpoint
extensions are wonderfully well-behaved.
For example, a generic completeness theorem for nabla logics by a uniform system
of axioms was established
[
21
]
, and this was recently extended
to the fixpoint extension of the finitary Moss logic
[
11
]
.
Most importantly, the automata corresponding to the fixpoint extension of Moss’
finitary nabla logic always enjoy a
simulation theorem
, allowing
arbitrary coalgebraic automata to be simulated by
non-deterministic
ones;
this goes back to the work of Janin & Walukiewicz on

\mu
-automata
[
18
]
.
The simulation theorem provides a very strong normal form for these logics, and
plays an important role in the proofs of several results for coalgebraic
fixpoint logics.

The downside of this approach is that the nabla modality is rather non-standard,
and understanding what concrete formulas actually say is not always easy.
For this reason, another approach to coalgebraic modal logic has become popular,
based on so called
predicate liftings
.
This approach, going back to the work of Pattinson
[
31
]
,
provides a much more familiar syntax in concrete applications, but can still be
elegantly formulated at the level of generality and abstraction that makes the
coalgebraic approach to modal logic attractive in the first place.
1
1

1

For a comparison between the two approaches, see
[
24
]
.

Coalgebraic
\mu
-calculi have also been developed as extensions of the
predicate liftings based languages
[
3
]
, and the resulting logics
are very well behaved: for example, good complexity results were
obtained in op. cit.
Again, the connection between formulas and automata can be formulated in this
setting
[
13
]
, but a central piece is now missing: so far, no
simulation theorem has been established for logics based on predicate liftings.
In fact, it is not trivial even to define what a non-deterministic automaton

is
in this setting.

This problem turned up in recent work
[
8
]
, by ourselves together
with Seifan, where we extended our earlier completeness result for Moss-style
fixpoint logics
[
11
]
to the predicate liftings setting.
Our solution was to introduce the concept of a
disjunctive basis
, which
formalizes in a compact way the minimal requirements that a collection of
predicate liftings
\Lambda
must meet in order for the class of corresponding

\Lambda
-automata to admit a simulation theorem.
Our aim in the present paper is to follow up on this conceptual contribution,
which we believe is of much wider significance besides providing a tool to
prove completeness results.

Exemplifying this, we shall explore some of the applications of our coalgebraic
simulation theorem.
Some of these transfer known results for nabla based fixpoint logics to the
predicate liftings setting; for example, we show that a linear-size model
property holds for our non-deterministic automata (or “disjunctive” automata
as we will call them), following
[
35
]
.
We also show that uniform interpolation results hold for coalgebraic fixpoint
logics in the presence of a disjunctive basis, which was proved for the
Moss-style languages in
[
26
]
.
We prove a Lyndon theorem for coalgebraic fixpoint logics, generalizing
a result for the standard modal
\mu
-calculus proved in
[
6
]
: a
formula is monotone in one of its variables if and only if it is equivalent to
one in which the variable appears positively.
We also prove an explicitly
one-step
version of this last result, which
we believe has some practical interest for modal fixpoint logics:
It is used to show that, given an expressively complete set of monotone
predicate liftings, its associated
\mu
-calculus has the same expressive power
as the full
\mu
-calculus based on the collection of all monotone predicate
liftings.
Finally, we show that the sum, product and composition of two modal logics that
admit disjunctive bases also admit a disjunctive basis, thus providing a tool
for modular construction of logics to which our results apply.

Next to proving these results, we compare the notion of a disjunctive basis to
the nabla based approach to coalgebraic fixpoint logics.
The connection will be highlighted in Section
8
where we discuss
disjunctive predicate liftings via the Yoneda lemma: here the Barr lifting of
the ambient functor (on which the semantics of nabla modalities are based) comes
into the picture naturally.
However, this is not to say that disjunctive bases are just “nablas in disguise”: it is a fundamental concept, formulated specifically to suit the approach to coalgebraic modal logics via predicate liftings, rather than nabla based languages. Furthermore, in cases where these two approaches to coalgebraic logics are not equivalent, disjunctive bases may be available even when nabla formulas fail to provide them.
We shall see that there are several concrete and natural examples of this.
A particularly interesting specimen is
graded modal logic
, which adds
counting modalities to standard modal logic.
While we will see that the standard nabla formulas of this language do not
provide a disjunctive basis, nevertheless a disjunctive basis for graded modal
logic does exist.

Correction of earlier version:

This paper is a corrected version of the paper
https://arxiv.org/abs/1710.10706
published
originally on 26/3, 2019. That version included a Janin-Walukiewicz style characterization theorem for coalgebraic

\mu
-calculi admitting a slightly stronger form of disjunctive bases called
uniform disjunctive bases
, claiming that such
\mu
-calculi are expressively complete for the corresponding coalgebraic monadic second-order logics modulo bisimulation invariance (see
[
10
,
9
]
). The proof of this result was incorrect. We currently do not know whether the result holds, and we leave this as an open question for future research. We shall briefly explain the error here.

The argument given in the original paper defined a construction that provides, for every
\mathsf{T}
-model
(\mathbb{S},s)
, a
pre-image
f_{\mathbb{S}}:(\mathbb{S}_{*},s_{*})\to(\mathbb{S},s)
such that any disjunctive
\Lambda
-automaton
\mathbb{A}
accepts
(\mathbb{S},s)
iff
it strongly accepts
(\mathbb{S}_{*},s_{*})
. An inductively defined translation
t
was then given, mapping each formula in the monadic second-order language
\mathtt{MSO}_{\Lambda}(\mathtt{X})
to the language
\mu\mathtt{ML}_{\Lambda}(\mathtt{X})
. The key claim about this translation was that, for any formula
\varphi
, and any pointed
\mathsf{T}
-model
(\mathbb{S},s)
:

\mathbb{S}_{*},s_{*}\Vdash\varphi\text{ iff }\mathbb{S},s\Vdash t(\varphi).

The proof of this claim was by induction on the complexity of the formula
\varphi
, the crucial step being the case for an existentially quantified formula
\exists p.\alpha
, where the translation is defined by
t(\exists p.\alpha):=\widetilde{\exists}p.t(\alpha)
. (Here
\widetilde{\exists}p
is a bisimulation quantifier, defined as in Section
6
.) The problem with proving the required equivalence for this case lies in the direction from left to right. One could try to reason as follows: if
\mathbb{S}_{*},s_{*}\Vdash\exists p.\alpha
, then the model
\mathbb{S}_{*}
can be extended with a value
Z
for
p
such that we have
\mathbb{S}_{*}[p\mapsto Z],s_{*}\Vdash\alpha
, where
\mathbb{S}_{*}[p\mapsto Z]
is the extended model. If we could then conclude that also
\mathbb{S}_{*}[p\mapsto Z],s_{*}\Vdash t(\alpha)
, then we would have
\mathbb{S},s\Vdash\widetilde{\exists}p.t(\varphi)
by the semantics of the bisimulation quantifier, and the argument would be done. The missing step needed to prove this is to show that the model
(\mathbb{S}_{*}[p\mapsto Z],s_{*})
is a pre-image, i.e. to find a model
(\mathbb{S}^{\prime},s^{\prime})
such that
(\mathbb{S}_{*}[p\mapsto Z],s_{*})=(\mathbb{S}^{\prime}_{*},s^{\prime}_{*})
; we could then argue that
\mathbb{S}^{\prime},s^{\prime}\Vdash t(\alpha)
(by the induction hypothesis), hence
\mathbb{S}_{*}[p\mapsto Z],s_{*}\Vdash t(\alpha)
(since
t(\alpha)
is a formula of
\mu\mathtt{ML}_{\Lambda}(\mathtt{X})
and therefore bisimulation invariant). This looks plausible at first sight, since the model
\mathbb{S}_{*}[p\mapsto Z]
is “almost” a pre-image of
\mathbb{S}
, aside from the added value for the variable
p
. Unfortunately, the model
\mathbb{S}_{*}[p\mapsto Z]
does not have to be a pre-image of any model, in general. Without going into a detailed counter-example, the reason is that typically the pre-image construction will create many bisimilar copies of states in a model, where each copy will satisfy the same propositional variables. So even though the model
\mathbb{S}_{*}
is certainly a pre-image, the value of
p
may prevent the extended model
\mathbb{S}_{*}[p\mapsto Z]
from being a pre-image – it might not contain enough copies of the states satisfying
p
.

2.
Preliminaries

2.1.
Basics of coalgebraic logic

We assume that the reader is familiar with coalgebra, coalgebraic modal logic
and the basic theory of automata operating on infinite objects.
The aim of this section is merely to fix some definitions and notations on notions

In an appendix to this paper we provide some basic definitions related to the

(1)

For the least fixpoint operator we apply the standard description of least

2.2.
Examples

There are many well-known examples of modal logics that can be presented as
coalgebraic modal logics where the modalities correspond to predicate liftings
for the relevant functor. We shall not attempt to provide a complete list here,
but we provide a few basic examples that will be helpful in what follows.

Next-time modality

Probably the simplest example of a non-trivial modality that can be described as
a predicate lifting is the “next-time” operator of linear temporal logic.
The natural way to present models of
\mathtt{LTL}
coalgebraically is to take
the coalgebraic type functor to be
\mathsf{Id}
, the identity functor on the category
of sets.
Coalgebras
(S,\sigma)
for this functor just provide maps
\sigma:S\to S
,
which can be thought of as providing the “next-state” function for a discrete
linear flow of time.

The identity functor has a unary predicate lifting
\raisebox{1.29167pt}{$\scriptstyle\bigcirc$}:\breve{\mathsf{P}}\to\breve{\mathsf{P}}
:
the identity natural transformation defined by
\raisebox{1.29167pt}{$\scriptstyle\bigcirc$}_{S}:Z\mapsto Z
for

Z\subseteq S
. A little thought shows that the evaluation of formulas

\raisebox{1.29167pt}{$\scriptstyle\bigcirc$}\varphi
in a model turns out as expected:

\mathbb{S},s\Vdash\raisebox{1.29167pt}{$\scriptstyle\bigcirc$}\varphi\text{ iff }\mathbb{S},\sigma(s)\Vdash\varphi.

The lifting
\scriptstyle\bigcirc
is monotone and dual to itself, so
\Sigma_{\mathsf{Id}}=\{\raisebox{1.29167pt}{$\scriptstyle\bigcirc$}\}
is a modal signature for the identity functor.
The language
\mu\mathtt{ML}_{\Lambda}
induced by this signature is known as the

linear-time
\mu
-calculus
.

Basic modal logic

Kripke frames for modal logic are coalgebras for the covariant powerset functor

\mathsf{P}
, or rather can be represented equivalently as such: a binary relation

R\subseteq S\times S
of a frame
(S,R)
can be identified with the map

R[-]:S\to\mathsf{P}S
sending each point in
S
to its set of
R
-successors.
The usual Kripkean modalities
\Box
and
\Diamond
come out as predicate liftings
for
\mathsf{P}
, by setting
\Box_{S}(Z)=\{Z^{\prime}\subseteq S\mid Z^{\prime}\subseteq Z\}

and
\Diamond_{S}(Z)=\{Z^{\prime}\subseteq S\mid Z\cap Z^{\prime}\neq\varnothing\}
.
Unfolding the definitions we see that the naturality condition for the box
modality
\Box
says that for all
f:S\to S^{\prime}
and
Z\subseteq S
,

Z^{\prime}\subseteq S^{\prime}
, we have:

f[Z]\subseteq Z^{\prime}\;\Longleftrightarrow\;Z\subseteq f^{-1}[Z^{\prime}].

This is the familiar adjunction between direct and inverse image.
The semantics of modal formulas comes out as expected: given a Kripke model

(S,R,V)
represented as a
\mathsf{P}
-model
\mathbb{S}
, we have
\mathbb{S},s\Vdash\Box\varphi
if and only if
\mathbb{S},t\Vdash\varphi
for every
R
-successor
t
of

s
.
For the signature
\Sigma_{\mathsf{P}}=\{\Box,\Diamond\}
, the language

\mu\mathtt{ML}_{\Sigma_{\mathsf{P}}}
is the (standard) modal
\mu
-calculus.

Monotone modal logic

Monotone modal logic generalizes normal modal logic by dropping the constraint
that the box modality should commute with conjunctions:
\Box(\varphi\wedge\psi)\Leftrightarrow\Box\varphi\wedge\Box\psi
.
Semantics for monotone modal logic is given by coalgebras for the monotone
neighborhood functor
\mathsf{M}
, which is the sub-functor of the functor
\breve{\mathsf{P}}\circ\breve{\mathsf{P}}
defined by setting:

\mathsf{M}S=\{F\subseteq\breve{\mathsf{P}}S\mid Z\in F\;\&\;Z\subseteq Z^{\prime}\;\Rightarrow\;Z^{\prime}\in F\}

The lifting
\Box:\breve{\mathsf{P}}\to\breve{\mathsf{P}}\circ\mathsf{M}
is defined by setting
F\in\Box_{S}(Z)
iff
Z\in F
.
The dual lifting
\Diamond
is defined by setting
F\in\Diamond_{S}(Z)
iff,
for all
Z^{\prime}\in F
,
Z\cap Z^{\prime}\neq\varnothing
.

Monotone modal logic can be seen as a “base logic” for modal logics without
distribution of the box over conjunctions, with varying interpretations, much
as the modal logic
\mathbf{K}
can be seen as the most basic normal modal logic.
Examples where such modalities appear are alternating-time temporal
logic
[
1
]
and Parikh’s dynamic game logic
[
30
]
.
For
\Sigma_{\mathsf{M}}=\{\Box,\Diamond\}
, the language
\mu\mathtt{ML}_{\Sigma_{\mathsf{M}}}
is known
as the
monotone
\mu
-calculus
.
It stands in a similar relationship to alternating-time logic and game logic as
the modal
\mu
-calculus does to
\mathtt{CTL}
and
\mathtt{PDL}
.

Graded modal logic

Graded modal logic extends basic modal logic with
counting modalities

\Diamond^{k}\varphi
and
\Box^{k}\varphi
.
These modalities, interpreted on Kripke models, are interpreted as “at least

k
successors satisfy
\varphi
” and “there are less than
k
successors that
do not satisfy
\varphi
”.
It is often convenient to use a slight generalization of Kripke semantics, where
successors of a state are assigned “weights” from
\omega
.
Such models are based on
\mathsf{B}
-coalgebras, where the “bags” functor
\mathsf{B}

assigns to a set
S
the set
\mathsf{B}S
of maps
f:S\to\omega
such that

f(s)=0
for all but finitely many
s\in S
.
Given a map
h:S\to S^{\prime}
and
f\in\mathsf{B}S
, the map
f^{\prime}=\mathsf{B}h(f)
is
defined by:

f^{\prime}(s^{\prime})=\sum_{h(s)=s^{\prime}}f(s)

The functor
\mathsf{B}
comes with an infinite supply of predicate liftings

\underline{k}
and
\overline{k}
— one pair for each
k\in\omega
— given by:

\begin{array}[]{lll}\underline{k}_{S}:&U\mapsto\big\{\sigma\in\mathsf{B}S\mid\sum_{u\in U}\sigma(u)\geq k\big\}\\[2.84526pt]
\overline{k}_{S}:&U\mapsto\{\sigma\in\mathsf{B}S\mid\sum_{u\not\in U}\sigma(u)<k\big\}.\end{array}

Over Kripke models, which can be identified with
\mathsf{B}

that the formulas

For
\Sigma_{\mathsf{B}}=\{\underline{k}\mid k\in\omega\}\cup\{\overline{k}\mid k\in\omega\}

-calculus
.

2.3.
One-step logic and one-step models

A pivotal role in our approach is filled by the
one-step versions
of coalgebraic
logics. The one-step perspective on coalgebraic modal logics, developed by a number of authors over several papers
[
4
,
31
,
33
,
34
]
, has been key to proving some central results about such logics and their fixpoint extensions. In particular, it is instrumental to the theory of coalgebraic automata. Indeed our main contribution here — the concept of disjunctive bases — takes place on the one-step level.
We do not assume that the reader is familiar with the framework of one-step logics, and give a self-contained introduction here.

We begin with a formal definition.

Definition 2.5
.

Given a signature
\Lambda
and a set
A
of variables, we define the set

\mathtt{Bool}(A)
of
boolean formulas
over
A
and the set
\mathtt{1ML}_{\Lambda}(A)

of
one-step
\Lambda

\displaystyle\mathtt{Bool}(A)\ni\pi

\displaystyle\mathrel{::=}

\displaystyle a\mid\bot\mid\top\mid\pi\vee\pi\mid\pi\wedge\pi\mid\neg\pi

\displaystyle\mathtt{1ML}_{\Lambda}(A)\ni\alpha

\displaystyle\mathrel{::=}

\displaystyle\heartsuit_{\lambda}\overline{\pi}\mid\bot\mid\top\mid\alpha\vee\alpha\mid\alpha\wedge\alpha\mid\neg\alpha

where
a\in A
,
\lambda\in\Lambda
and
\overline{\pi}=(\pi_{1},\ldots,\pi_{n})
is
a tuple of formulas in
\mathtt{Bool}(A)
of the same length as the arity of

We will denote the positive (negation-free) fragments of
\mathtt{Bool}(A)
and

\mathtt{1ML}_{\Lambda}(A)
as, respectively,
\mathtt{Latt}(A)
and
\mathtt{1ML}_{\Lambda}^{+}(A)

\displaystyle\mathtt{Latt}(A)\ni\pi

\displaystyle\mathrel{::=}

\displaystyle a\mid\bot\mid\top\mid\pi\vee\pi\mid\pi\wedge\pi

\displaystyle\mathtt{1ML}_{\Lambda}^{+}(A)\ni\alpha

\displaystyle\mathrel{::=}

\displaystyle\heartsuit_{\lambda}\overline{\pi}\mid\bot\mid\top\mid\alpha\vee\alpha\mid\alpha\wedge\alpha

We shall often make use of substitutions: given a finite set

,
let
\vee_{A}:\mathsf{P}A\to\mathtt{Latt}(A)
be the map sending

to
\bigvee B
, and
let
\wedge_{A}:\mathsf{P}A\to\mathtt{Latt}(A)
be the map sending
B
to
\bigwedge B
,
and given sets

defined by mapping

.

We need a number of properties of modal signatures, formulated in terms of the corresponding one-step logics. The first is fairly standard: expressive completeness of a modal signature
\Lambda
means that every modality that makes sense for the functor — formally, every predicate lifting — can be expressed in terms of modalities in
\Lambda
.

Definition 2.6
.

A monotone modal signature
\Lambda
for
\mathsf{T}
is

if,
for every
n
-place predicate lifting
\lambda
(not necessarily in
\Lambda
) and for
all variables
a_{1},\ldots,a_{n}
there is a formula
\alpha\in\mathtt{1ML}_{\Lambda}(\{a_{1},\ldots,a_{n}\})
which is equivalent to
\heartsuit_{\lambda}\overline{a}
.

We will also be interested in the following variant of expressive completeness:

Definition 2.7
.

We say that
\Lambda
is
Lyndon complete
if, for every
monotone

n
-place predicate lifting
\lambda
and variables
a_{1},\ldots,a_{n}
, there is
a
positive
formula
\alpha\in\mathtt{1ML}_{\Lambda}^{+}(\{a_{1},\ldots,a_{n}\})

to
\heartsuit_{\lambda}\overline{a}
.

We now turn to the semantics of one-step formulas, using so-called
one-step models
.

Definition 2.8
.

A
one-step
\mathsf{T}
-frame
is a pair
(S,\sigma)
with
\sigma\in\mathsf{T}S
,
i.e., an object in the category

\mathsf{T}
.
Similarly a

-model

(S,\sigma,m)
such that

is a one-step
\mathsf{T}
-frame and

.

A
morphism

is a morphism in

\mathcal{E}(\mathsf{T})
, that is, a map from
S
to
S^{\prime}
such that
\mathsf{T}f(\sigma)=\sigma^{\prime}
.
The map
f
is said to be a
morphism of one-step models

f:(S,\sigma,m)\to(S^{\prime},\sigma^{\prime},m^{\prime})
if, in addition,
m=m^{\prime}\circ f
.

Given a one-step model
(S,\sigma,m)
, we define the
0
-step interpretation

[\![\pi]\!]^{0}_{m}\subseteq S
of
\pi\in\mathtt{Bool}(A)
by the obvious induction:

[\![a]\!]^{0}_{m}\mathrel{:=}\{v\in S\mid a\in m(v)\}
,
[\![\top]\!]^{0}_{m}\mathrel{:=}S
,

[\![\bot]\!]^{0}_{m}\mathrel{:=}\varnothing
, while we use standard clauses for

and
\neg
.
Similarly, the

[\![\alpha]\!]^{1}_{m}

[\![\heartsuit_{\lambda}(\pi_{1},\ldots,\pi_{n})]\!]^{1}_{m}\mathrel{:=}\lambda_{S}([\![\pi_{1}]\!]^{0}_{m},\ldots,[\![\pi_{n}]\!]^{0}_{m})
,
and again standard clauses apply to
\bot,\top,\wedge,\vee
and
\neg
.
Given a one-step model

Notions like one-step satisfiability, validity and equivalence are defined and
denoted in the obvious way; in particular, we use
\equiv^{1}
to denote the

For future reference we mention the following two results, the first of which
states that the truth of one-step formulas is invariant under one-step
morphisms.

Proposition 1
.

Let
f:(S^{\prime},\sigma^{\prime},m^{\prime})\to(S,\sigma,m)
be a morphism of one-step models over

.
Then for every formula
\alpha\in\mathtt{1ML}_{\Lambda}(A)
we have

S^{\prime},\sigma^{\prime},m^{\prime}\Vdash^{1}\alpha\text{ iff }S,\sigma,m\Vdash^{1}\alpha.

The second proposition is a standard observation about the semantic counterpart
of the syntactic notion of substitution.

Proposition 2
.

Let
(S,\sigma,m)
be a one-step model over
A
, and let
\sigma:B\to\mathtt{Bool}(A)
be
a substitution.
Then for every formula
\alpha\in\mathtt{1ML}_{\Lambda}(B)
we have

S,\sigma,m_{\sigma}\Vdash^{1}\alpha\text{ iff }S,\sigma,m\Vdash^{1}\alpha[\sigma],

where

is the

-marking given by

.

For readers unfamiliar with the theory of infinite games, we provide some of

Definition 2.1

A

.

3.
Disjunctive formulas and disjunctive bases

3.1.
Disjunctive formulas

In this section, we present the main conceptual contribution of the paper, and
define disjunctive bases. We then consider a number of examples.

As a first step, we begin by presenting disjunctive
formulas
, originally
introduced in
[
8
]
, as a class of one-step formulas for a given
modal signature characterized by a model-theoretic property, expressed in terms
of the one-step semantics.

Definition 3.1
.

A one-step formula
\alpha\in\mathtt{1ML}_{\Lambda}^{+}(A)
is called
disjunctive

if for every one-step model
(S,\sigma,m)
such that
S,\sigma,m\Vdash^{1}\alpha
there is
a one-step frame
(S^{\prime},\sigma^{\prime})
together with a one-step frame morphism
f:(S^{\prime},\sigma^{\prime})\to(S,\sigma)
and a marking
m^{\prime}:S^{\prime}\to\mathsf{P}A
, such that:

(1)

S^{\prime},\sigma^{\prime},m^{\prime}\Vdash^{1}\alpha
;

(2)

m^{\prime}(s^{\prime})\subseteq m(f(s^{\prime}))
, for all
s^{\prime}\in S^{\prime}
;

(3)

|m^{\prime}(s^{\prime})|\leq 1
, for all
s^{\prime}\in S^{\prime}
.

We sometimes refer to the one-step frame
(S^{\prime},\sigma^{\prime})
together with the map
f

as a
cover
of
(S,\sigma)
, and to the one-step model
(S^{\prime},\sigma^{\prime},m^{\prime})
together
with the map
f
as a
dividing cover
of
(S,\sigma,m)
for
\alpha
.

The intuition behind disjunctive formulas is that, in a certain sense, they
never “force” two distinct propositional variables to be true together, i.e.
any one-step model for a disjunctive formula
\delta
in
\mathtt{1ML}_{\Lambda}^{+}(A)
can be
transformed into one in which every point satisfies at most one propositional
variable from
A
.
Moreover, “transformed into” here does not just mean “replaced by”: we
cannot arbitrarily change the one-step model, the output of the construction
must be closely related to the one-step model that we started with.

A trivial example of a disjunctive formula is
\raisebox{1.29167pt}{$\scriptstyle\bigcirc$}a
for
a\in A
, where
we recall that
\scriptstyle\bigcirc
was the next-time modality viewed as a predicate
lifting for the identity functor
\mathsf{Id}
.
A one-step model for this functor is a triple
S,s,m
consisting of a set
S
,
an element
s\in S
and a marking
m:S\to\mathsf{P}A
. Then
S,s,m\Vdash^{1}\raisebox{1.29167pt}{$\scriptstyle\bigcirc$}a
if, and only if,
a\in m(s)
.
But then, no elements in
S
besides
s
are relevant to the evaluation of

\raisebox{1.29167pt}{$\scriptstyle\bigcirc$}a
, and for
s
we can just forget about all other variables: set

m^{\prime}(s)=\{a\}
and
m^{\prime}(v)=\varnothing
for all
s\in S\setminus\{v\}
.
We have
S,s,m^{\prime}\Vdash^{1}\raisebox{1.29167pt}{$\scriptstyle\bigcirc$}a
,
m^{\prime}(v)\subseteq m(v)
and
|m^{\prime}(v)|\leq 1
for all
v\in S
.

For an example of a one-step formula that is
not
disjunctive, consider

\Box a\land\Diamond b
(where
\Diamond
and
\Box
are the standard modalities for
Kripke structures, i.e., coalgebras for the power set functor
\mathsf{P}
).
Observe that a one-step model for this functor is a triple
(S,\sigma,m)
with

\sigma\subseteq S
.
It should be obvious that for the formula
\Box a\land\Diamond b
to hold at such
a structure,
\sigma
needs to have an element
s
where
b
holds, while at the
same time
every
element of
\sigma
, including
s
, must satisfy
a
.
There is no escape here: we can only have
(S,\sigma,m)\Vdash^{1}\Box a\land\Diamond b

if there is an element
s
making
both

a
and
b
true.

This is very different if we consider
the typical disjunctive formulas for basic modal logic, which are of the
form:

\Diamond a_{1}\wedge\dots\wedge\Diamond a_{n}\wedge\Box(a_{1}\vee\dots\vee a_{n})

This is often abbreviated as
\nabla\{a_{1},\dots,a_{n}\}
.
The operator
\nabla
is known as the
cover modality
, or sometimes
“nabla modality”.
The formula
\nabla B
says about a one-step model
(S,\sigma,m)
for
\mathsf{P}
,
that the following “back-and-forth” conditions hold: for all
s\in\sigma

there is some
a\in B
with
a\in m(s)
, and conversely, for every
a\in B

there is some
s\in\sigma
with
a\in m(s)
.
These formulas are indeed disjunctive, but less trivially so than the next-time
formulas.
For example, consider the formula
\nabla\{a,b,c\}
, which is true in a
one-step model
(S,s,m)
with
S=\{u,v\}
,
m(u)=\{a,b\}
and
m(v)=\{b,c\}
.
But there is no way to simply shrink the marking
m
to a dividing marking

m^{\prime}
so that
(S,s,m)\Vdash^{1}\nabla\{a,b,c\}

there are too many pigeons and too few pigeon holes — so the obvious solution
is to make more pigeon holes!
One way to do this is to “split” the points in
\{u,v\}

so that we make room for each variable to be witnessed at a separate point.
More formally, let
S^{\prime}=\{(u,a),(u,b),(v,b),(v,c)\}
and define
m^{\prime}:S^{\prime}\to\{a,b,c\}
via the projection on
\{a,b,c\}
, i.e.,
m^{\prime}(u,a)=\{a\}
,
etc.
This one-step model satisfies
\nabla\{a,b,c\}
and it has the obvious covering
map

\pi_{S}
on
S
.

The cover modality was arguably the starting point of coalgebraic modal logic.
In the seminal paper
[
27
]
, Moss defined nabla modalities for all
functors that preserve weak pullback squares, generalizing the cover modality.
The idea is to apply the coalgebraic type functor
\mathsf{T}
to sets of formulas

\Psi
and form new formulas from objects
\Gamma
in
\mathsf{T}\Psi
.
That is,
\nabla\Gamma
counts as a formula if
\Psi
is a set of formulas
and
\Gamma\in\mathsf{T}\Psi
.
The semantics is given in terms of the “Barr extension”
\overline{\mathsf{T}}
of
the functor
\mathsf{T}
, which is a relation lifting defined for
R\subseteq X\times A
by setting:

\overline{\mathsf{T}}R=\{(\xi,\alpha)\in\mathsf{T}X\times\mathsf{T}A\mid\exists\rho\in\mathsf{T}R:\;\mathsf{T}\pi_{X}(\rho)=\xi\;\&\;\mathsf{T}\pi_{A}(\rho)=\alpha\}

We then evaluate the nabla modality by applying this relation lifting to the
satisfaction relation:

\mathbb{S},s\Vdash\nabla\Gamma\text{ iff }(\sigma(s),\Gamma)\in{\overline{\mathsf{T}}({\Vdash})}.

In terms of one-step formulas, we would count
\nabla\Gamma
as a one-step
formula with variables in
A
, for all
\Gamma\in\mathsf{T}\mathtt{Bool}(A)
.
In particular, formulas of the form
\nabla\Gamma
for
\Gamma\in\mathsf{T}A

count as one-step formulas.

These formulas are in fact disjunctive:

Proposition 3
.

Let
\mathsf{T}
be a set functor that preserves weak pullbacks.
Then every formula of the form
\nabla\Gamma
, where
\Gamma\in\mathsf{T}A
, is disjunctive.

To see this, suppose that a one-step model
X,\xi,m
satisfies the formula

\nabla\Gamma
.
This means that
(\xi,\Gamma)\in\overline{\mathsf{T}}R
, where
R={\Vdash^{0}}\cap(X\times A)
is defined by
(u,a)\in R
iff
a\in m(u)
.
By definition of the Barr extension, this means we find some
\rho\in\mathsf{T}R

such that
\mathsf{T}\pi_{A}(\rho)=\Gamma
and
\mathsf{T}\pi_{X}(\rho)=\xi
.
But then we have our covering one-step model right in front of us already: the
triple
(R,\rho,\eta\circ\pi_{A})
is a one-step model where
\eta:A\to\mathsf{P}A
is the singleton map
a\mapsto\{a\}
, i.e., the unit of the powerset
monad.
The covering map is just the projection
\pi_{X}:R\to X
.
It is a simple exercise to check that
(R,\rho,\eta\circ\pi_{A})\Vdash^{1}\nabla\Gamma
, and clearly
|\eta(\pi_{A}(u,a))|=1
and
\eta(\pi_{A}(u,a))\subseteq m(u)
for all
(u,a)\in R
.
So we see that disjunctiveness of the nabla modalities is not incidental: it is
hardwired into their semantics.

3.2.
Disjunctive bases

We now turn to a discussion of the key notion of this paper, viz., the
disjunctive bases originating with
[
8
]
.
For their definition, we recall the substitutions
\wedge_{A}:B\mapsto\bigwedge B

and
\theta_{A,B}:(a,b)\mapsto a\land b
defined in the Preliminaries.
It will also be convenient to introduce the abbrevation

A\mathbin{\vtop{\halign{#\cr$\cup$\cr\hfil\raise 1.80832pt\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle\times$}\hfil\cr}}}B:=(A\times B)\cup A\cup B

for any two sets
A,B
.

Definition 3.2
.

Let
\mathtt{D}
be an assignment of a set of positive one-step formulas

\mathtt{D}(A)\subseteq\mathtt{1ML}_{\Lambda}^{+}(A)
for all sets of variables
A
.
Then
\mathtt{D}
is called a
disjunctive basis
for
\Lambda
if each
formula in
\mathtt{D}(A)
is disjunctive, and the following conditions
hold:

(1)

\mathtt{D}(A)
contains
\top
and is closed under finite disjunctions (in particular, also
\bot=\bigvee\varnothing\in\mathtt{D}(A)
).

(2)

\mathtt{D}
is
distributive over
\Lambda
: for every one-step formula of the form
\heartsuit_{\lambda}\overline{\pi}
in
\mathtt{1ML}_{\Lambda}^{+}(A)
there is a formula
\delta\in\mathtt{D}(\mathsf{P}(A))
such that
\heartsuit_{\lambda}\overline{\pi}\equiv^{1}\delta[\land_{A}]
.

(3)

\mathtt{D}

admits a binary distributive law
: for any two formulas
\alpha\in\mathtt{D}(A)
and
\beta\in\mathtt{D}(B)
, there is a formula
\gamma\in\mathtt{D}(A\mathbin{\vtop{\halign{#\cr$\cup$\cr\hfil\raise 1.80832pt\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle\times$}\hfil\cr}}}B)
such that
\alpha\wedge\beta\equiv^{1}\gamma[\theta_{A,B}]
.

The key property of disjunctive bases is captured by the following normal form
theorem, which is easy to derive from the definition.

Proposition 4
.

Suppose
\mathtt{D}
is a disjunctive basis for
\Lambda
.
Then for every one-step formula
\alpha\in\mathtt{1ML}_{\Lambda}^{+}(A)
there is a formula

\delta\in\mathtt{D}(\mathsf{P}(A))
such that
\alpha\equiv^{1}\delta[\land_{A}]
.

Note that the requirement
\top\in\mathtt{D}(A)
is needed for this proposition to
hold: we have
\top\in\mathtt{1ML}_{\Lambda}^{+}(A)
, so for the proposition to be true there
has to be a formula
\delta\in\mathtt{D}(\mathsf{P}(A))
such that
\top\equiv^{1}\delta[\land_{A}]
.
Since
\top\in\mathtt{D}(\mathsf{P}(A))
we can take
\delta=\top
— without having

\top\in\mathtt{D}(\mathsf{P}(A))
there would be no guarantee in general that an
appropriate formula
\delta
can be found.

We have seen that nabla formulas are disjunctive, and we shall soon see that
they provide disjunctive bases for many modal signatures.
In order for the approach of constructing disjunctive bases via nabla formulas
to work however, it is necessary that the ambient functor
\mathsf{T}
preserves weak
pullbacks.
This is one sense in which disjunctive bases generalize nabla formulas: we shall
soon see an example of a functor which does not preserve weak pullbacks, but
does have a signature admitting a disjunctive basis.
However, we want to stress that the main motivation behind disjunctive bases is
not to “get by without weak pullback preservation” — in fact, most natural
examples of disjunctive bases we can think of apply to functors that do
preserve weak pullbacks.
Rather, the point is that the existence of a disjunctive basis is a property of

modal signatures
, not of functors.
The choice of a modal signature for a functor is not arbitrary, it can
substantially affect the properties of the modal languages that we get.
In particular, even for functors that do preserve weak pullbacks, we can have
natural choices of modal signatures for which the nabla formulas associated
with the functor do not provide a disjunctive basis.
Typically, this will happen when the modal signature is (a) infinite, and (b)
not expressively complete.
An example of this situation that will be given special attention is graded
modal logic, to be presented in the next subsection.
But there are much simpler cases, as we shall see.

3.3.
Examples

In this subsection we present a number of examples of modal signatures admitting
disjunctive bases, and one example of a modal signature that provably does not
admit a disjunctive basis.

3.3.1.
Disjunctive bases for weak pullback preserving functors

In the previous section we noted that disjunctive formulas generalize the Moss

In many interesting cases this suffices to find a disjunctive basis.

3.3.2.
A simple example

The simplest non-trivial example of a disjunctive basis we can think of, that
is not a special case of the nabla-based approach of the previous paragraph,
is the following.
Consider the functor
\mathsf{T}=\Sigma\times\mathsf{Id}
, where
\Sigma
is a
countably infinite alphabet. For this functor,
\mathsf{T}
-coalgebras are (up to
unfolding) just
\Sigma
-streams, or infinite words for which the alphabet is
contained in
\Sigma
.
They can be viewed as triples
(S,\sigma_{1},\sigma_{2})
where
\sigma_{1}:S\to\Sigma
and
\sigma_{2}:S\to S
.
A simple and reasonably natural modal signature
\Lambda
for this functor is the
following: we have one nullary modality
!l
for each
l\in\Sigma
with the
interpretation:
\mathbb{S},s\Vdash{!l}
iff
\sigma_{1}(s)=l
, and we have a single
one-place modality
\scriptstyle\bigcirc
with the interpretation
\mathbb{S},s\Vdash\raisebox{1.29167pt}{$\scriptstyle\bigcirc$}\varphi

iff
\mathbb{S},\sigma_{2}(s)\Vdash\varphi
.
We can easily describe this language as a modal signature
\Lambda
for the
functor
\mathsf{T}
, and it is not hard to show that
\Lambda
has a disjunctive
basis consisting of the formulas
!l
for
l\in\Sigma
and
\raisebox{1.29167pt}{$\scriptstyle\bigcirc$}a
for

a\in A
.

The one-step nabla formulas for
\mathsf{T}
, which are of the form
\nabla(l,a)
, with

(S,(l^{\prime},s),m)\Vdash^{1}\nabla(l,a)
iff
l^{\prime}=l
and
s\Vdash^{0}a
, can easily
be expressed as
\Lambda
-formulas by writing
\nabla(l,a)\mathrel{:=}{!l}\land\raisebox{1.29167pt}{$\scriptstyle\bigcirc$}a
.
However, these formulas do not form a disjunctive basis for
\Lambda
. To see why, just consider the set of variables
A=\{a\}
. The property of distributivity of disjunctive bases would then require that the formula
\raisebox{1.29167pt}{$\scriptstyle\bigcirc$}a
should be equivalent to a finite disjunction of formulas of the form:

{!l}\land\raisebox{1.29167pt}{$\scriptstyle\bigcirc$}a

where
l\in\Sigma
. But the formula
\raisebox{1.29167pt}{$\scriptstyle\bigcirc$}a
corresponds to an infinite disjunction:

\bigvee\{{!l}\land\raisebox{1.29167pt}{$\scriptstyle\bigcirc$}a\mid l\in\Sigma\}

It is fairly obvious that this is not equivalent to any finitary disjunction of the required shape.
It is not hard to come up with other, similar examples.

3.3.3.
Graded modal logic

A much more involved example of a modal logic that admits a disjunctive basis
which cannot be reduced to nabla formulas, is graded modal logic.
The bag functor (defined in Section
2.2
) does preserve weak pullbacks, and so its Moss modalities are
disjunctive, and so the set of all monotone liftings for
\mathsf{B}

disjunctive basis as an instance of Proposition

Note, however, that the latter proposition does not apply to graded modal logic,
since its signature

It was observed in

the formula

\Box^{n+1-|J|}(\bigvee_{j\in J}a_{j}\vee b).

Now, if
s\in S
satisfies either
b
or some
a_{j}
for
j\in J
, then by the
construction of
\mathbb{G}
we have
s\in E[H]
.
We now see that
|S\setminus E[H]|\leq n-|J|
. Hence we get:

|E[H]|\geq|S|-(n-|J|)=|S|-n+|J|.

But note that
H=J\cup(H\cap K)
, so that we find

|H|\leq|J|+|H\cap K|\leq|J|+|K|=|J|+(|S|-n),

From these two inequalities it is immediate that
|H|\leq|E[H]|
, as
required.

\blacktriangleleft

Now consider a matching
M
that covers
V_{1}
.
Since the size of the set
V_{1}
is the same as that of
V_{2}
, any matching
M

of
\mathbb{G}
that covers
V_{1}
is (the graph of) a bijection between these two sets.
Furthermore, it easily follows that such an

M
restricts to a bijection between
I
and a subset

\{s_{1},\dots,s_{n}\}
of
S
such that
a_{i}\in m(s_{i})
for each
i\in I
, and
that
b\in m(t)
for each
t\notin\{u_{1},\dots,u_{n}\}
.
Hence
\nabla(\overline{a}{;}b)
is true in
(S,\sigma,m)
, as required.

∎

In light of this proposition, we shall continue to use the notation

\nabla(\overline{a}{;}B)
for the equivalent formula in
\mathtt{1ML}^{+}_{\Sigma_{\mathsf{B}}}(A)

provided in the proof.

Definition 3.5
.

We define
\mathtt{D}_{\mathsf{B}}(A)
by the following grammar:

\delta::=\top\mid\nabla(\overline{a}{;}B)\mid\delta\vee\delta

where
\overline{a}
is a tuple of elements from
A
and
B\subseteq A
.

Theorem 7
.

The assignment
\mathtt{D}_{\mathsf{B}}
provides a disjunctive basis for the signature

\Sigma_{\mathsf{B}}
.

Proof 3.6
.

It remains to prove that
\mathtt{D}_{\mathsf{B}}
is distributive over
\Sigma_{\mathsf{B}}
, and admits
a binary distributive law.
For the first part, consider the formula
\Diamond^{k}\pi\in\mathtt{1ML}^{+}_{\Sigma_{\mathsf{B}}}(A)
.
Note that
\varnothing
is a variable in
\mathsf{P}A
and that
\bigvee\{\varnothing\}[\wedge_{A}]=\bigwedge\varnothing=\top
.
With this in mind, it is not hard to see that
\Diamond^{k}\pi
can be rewritten
equivalently as:

\Diamond^{k}\pi=\bigvee\{\nabla(B_{1},\dots,B_{k}{;}\{\varnothing\})\mid\bigwedge B_{i}\vDash\pi,\text{ for each }i\}[\wedge_{A}]

Here,
\vDash
denotes propositional consequence between formulas in
\mathtt{Bool}(A)
.
Next, consider the formula
\Box^{k}\pi\in\mathtt{1ML}^{+}_{\Sigma_{\mathsf{B}}}(A)
. Keeping in mind that
\bigwedge\varnothing=\top
, the reader can verify that this is equivalent to:

\bigvee_{m<k}\nabla(\underbrace{\varnothing,\dots,\varnothing}_{m\text{ times}}{;}\{B\subseteq A\mid\bigwedge B\vDash\pi\})[\wedge_{A}]

To establish the binary distributive law, let
\delta\in\mathtt{D}_{\mathsf{B}}(A)
and
\delta^{\prime}\in\mathtt{D}_{\mathsf{B}}(B)
. Then
\delta
is of the form
\alpha_{1}\vee\dots\vee\alpha_{n}
and
\delta^{\prime}
is of the form
\beta_{1}\vee\dots\vee\beta_{m}
, where each
\alpha_{i}
is either equal to
\top
or of the form
\nabla(\overline{a}{;}A^{\prime})
for some
\overline{a},A^{\prime}
and each
\beta_{j}
is either equal to
\top
or of the form
\nabla(\overline{b}{;}B^{\prime})
for some
\overline{b},B^{\prime}
. By distributing the conjunction over disjunctions, we can rewrite the formula
\delta\wedge\delta^{\prime}
as an equivalent disjunction of formulas
\alpha_{i}\wedge\beta_{j}
for
1\leq i\leq n
and
1\leq j\leq m
. So it suffices to show that the required distributive law holds for conjunctions of this shape.

In the case where
\alpha_{i}=\beta_{j}=\top
, we have
\alpha_{i}\wedge\beta_{j}\equiv^{1}\top
. But
\top\in\mathtt{D}(A\mathbin{\vtop{\halign{#\cr$\cup$\cr\hfil\raise 1.80832pt\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle\times$}\hfil\cr}}}B)
and
\top=\top[\theta_{A,B}]
, so this case is done. If one and only one of
\alpha_{i},\beta_{j}
is equal to
\top
, say
\alpha_{i}=\nabla(\overline{a}{;}A^{\prime})
and
\beta_{j}=\top
, then:

\alpha_{i}\wedge\beta_{j}\equiv^{1}\nabla(\overline{a}{;}A^{\prime})\in\mathtt{D}(A)\subseteq\mathtt{D}(A\mathbin{\vtop{\halign{#\cr$\cup$\cr\hfil\raise 1.80832pt\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle\times$}\hfil\cr}}}B).

But
\nabla(\overline{a}{;}A^{\prime})[\theta_{A,B}]=\nabla(\overline{a}{;}A^{\prime})
, so we are done in this case as well.

Finally, the interesting case is where
\alpha_{i}
is of the form
\nabla(a_{1},\dots,a_{k}{;}A^{\prime})
and
\beta_{j}
is of the form
\nabla(b_{1},\dots,b_{l}{;}B^{\prime})
. For this case, we need some definitions: we fix the formulas
\alpha_{i}=\nabla(a_{1},\dots,a_{k}{;}A^{\prime})
and
\beta_{j}=\nabla(b_{1},\dots,b_{l}{;}B^{\prime})
. Let an
overlap record
be a subset
O\subseteq\{1,\dots,k\}\times\{1,\dots,l\}
such that
O
is the graph of a bijection from some subset of
\{1,\dots,k\}
onto some subset of
\{1,\dots,l\}
. We denote the set of such overlap records by
\mathcal{O}
. Given an overlap record
O
, an
O
-pair
is a pair
(c_{1},c_{2})
of functions
c_{1}:\{1,\dots,k\}\setminus\pi_{1}[O]\to B^{\prime}
and
c_{2}:\{1,\dots,l\}\setminus\pi_{2}[O]\to A^{\prime}
. Finally, a
case description
is a triple
(O,c_{1},c_{2})
where
O
is an overlap record and
(c_{1},c_{2})
is an
O
-pair. Note that there are finitely many case descriptions.

Given a case description
(O,c_{1},c_{2})
, let
\{1,\dots,k\}\setminus\pi_{1}[O]=\{p_{1},\dots,p_{s}\}
, let
\{1,\dots,l\}\setminus\pi_{2}[O]=\{q_{1},\dots,q_{t}\}
, and let
\vec{O}
be a list of the pairs in
O
in some arbitrary order. Now define the formula
\chi(O,c_{1},c_{2})
to be:

\nabla((a_{p_{1}},c_{1}(p_{1})),\dots,(a_{p_{s}},c_{1}(p_{s})),(b_{q_{1}},c_{2}(q_{1})),\dots,(b_{q_{t}},c_{2}(q_{t})),\vec{O}\;{;}\;A^{\prime}\times B^{\prime})

This is a formula in
\mathtt{D}_{\mathsf{B}}(A\mathbin{\vtop{\halign{#\cr$\cup$\cr\hfil\raise 1.80832pt\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle\times$}\hfil\cr}}}B)
, in fact it is a formula in
\mathtt{D}_{\mathsf{B}}(A\times B)
.
It is a fairly straightforward task to verify that the formula
\alpha_{i}\wedge\beta_{j}
is equivalent to the disjunction:

\bigvee\{\chi(O,c_{1},c_{2})\mid(O,c_{1},c_{2})\text{ is a case description}\}

We leave this as an exercise for the reader.

3.3.4.
An example without weak pullback preservation

Here is an example of a functor that does not preserve weak pullbacks, but still has a natural modal signature that admits a disjunctive basis.
Let
\mathsf{F}
be the subfunctor of
\mathsf{P}^{2}
given by setting
\mathsf{F}X
to be the set of pairs
(Y,Z)\in{(\mathsf{P}X)}^{2}
such that at least one of the sets
Y,Z
is finite. That is:

\mathsf{F}X=(\mathsf{P}_{\omega}X\times\mathsf{P}X)\cup(\mathsf{P}X\times\mathsf{P}_{\omega}X).

This is a well defined subfunctor of
\mathsf{P}^{2}
since the direct image of a finite subset
Y\subseteq X
under any given map
f:X\to X^{\prime}
is finite.

\mathsf{F}
does not preserve weak pullbacks. To see why, consider the constant map
c:\omega\to\{0\}
, and consider the following two objects in
\mathsf{F}\omega
:
\alpha=(\{0\},\omega)
and
\beta=(\omega,\{0\})
. Clearly,
\mathsf{F}c(\alpha)=\mathsf{F}c(\beta)=(\{0\},\{0\})
. But given that
(P,p_{1},p_{2})
is the pullback of the diagram:
\omega\longrightarrow\{0\}\longleftarrow\omega
where both maps are equal to
c
, if
\mathsf{F}
preserves weak pullbacks then there should be some pair
\chi=(Y,Z)\in\mathsf{F}P
such that
\mathsf{F}p_{1}(\chi)=\alpha
and
\mathsf{F}p_{2}(\chi)=\beta
. But then
p_{1}[Z]=\omega
and likewise
p_{2}[Y]=\omega
.
So both
Y
and
Z
would have to be infinite, contradicting our definition of
\mathsf{F}
.

Consider the modal signature consisting of the usual labelled diamond modalities
\Diamond^{0}
and
\Diamond^{1}
, quantifying over the left and right set in a pair
(Y,Z)\in\mathsf{F}X
respectively, and their dual box modalities. Then we can define corresponding (finitary) nablas
\nabla^{0}
and
\nabla^{1}
in the usual way. Then, finite disjunctions of
\top
and conjunctions of the form
\nabla^{0}A_{0}\wedge\nabla^{1}A_{1}
for finite sets
A_{0},A_{1}
will provide a disjunctive basis for this signature. The reason that the formulas
\nabla^{0}A_{0}\wedge\nabla^{1}A_{1}
are still disjunctive is this: given a one-step model
(X,(Y,Z),m)
satisfying the formula
\nabla^{0}A_{0}\wedge\nabla^{1}A_{1}
, where
(Y,Z)\in\mathsf{F}X
and
m
is a marking, we can construct an appropriate one-step frame morphism
f:(X^{\prime},(Y^{\prime},Z^{\prime}))\to(X,(Y,Z))
and a marking
m^{\prime}
on
X^{\prime}
as follows. Take:

\displaystyle X^{\prime}

\displaystyle:=

\displaystyle(X\times A_{0})\cup(X\times A_{1}),

\displaystyle Y^{\prime}

\displaystyle:=

\displaystyle\{(u,a)\in Y\times A_{0}\mid a\in m(u)\},

\displaystyle Z^{\prime}

\displaystyle:=

\displaystyle\{(u,a)\in Z\times A_{1}\mid a\in m(u)\}.

The map
f
is of course just the projection to
X
, and the marking
m^{\prime}
is defined by
m^{\prime}(u,a)=\{a\}
. The crucial point is that since one of
Y,Z
is finite, and since
A_{0},A_{1}
are both finite sets, one of the sets
Y^{\prime},Z^{\prime}
is finite too, so
(Y^{\prime},Z^{\prime})\in\mathsf{F}X^{\prime}
as required. The proof that the formula
\nabla^{0}A_{0}\wedge\nabla^{1}A_{1}
also holds in the one-step model
(X^{\prime},(Y^{\prime},Z^{\prime}),m^{\prime})
is routine.

3.3.5.
Monotone modal logic: a signature without disjunctive basis

Finally, we provide an example of a signature that does not admit any
disjunctive basis:

Proposition 8
.

The signature
\Sigma_{\mathsf{M}}
consisting of the box- and diamond liftings for
\mathsf{M}

does not have a disjunctive basis.

Proof 3.7
.

Let
L
be the standard relation lifting for the monotone neighborhood functor.
Given two one-step models
X,\xi,m
and
X^{\prime},\xi^{\prime},m^{\prime}
over a set of variables
A
,
we write
u\preceq u^{\prime}
if
m(u)\subseteq m^{\prime}(u^{\prime})
for
u\in X
and
u^{\prime}\in X^{\prime}
, and we say that
X^{\prime},\xi^{\prime},m^{\prime}

simulates

X,\xi,m
if
(\xi,\xi^{\prime})\in L(\preceq)
.
A straightforward proof will verify the following claim.

Claim
1
.

If
X^{\prime},\xi^{\prime},m^{\prime}
simulates
X,\xi,m
then for every one-step formula
\alpha\in\mathtt{1ML}_{\Lambda}^{+}(A)
,
X,\xi,m\Vdash^{1}\alpha
implies
X^{\prime},\xi^{\prime},m^{\prime}\Vdash^{1}\alpha
.

Given a set
A
, let
\eta_{A}:A\to\mathsf{P}{A}
denote the map given by the unit of
the powerset monad, i.e., it is the singleton map
\eta_{A}:a\mapsto\{a\}
.
Furthermore, recall that
\land_{A}
is the subsitution mapping
B\in\mathsf{P}A

to
\bigwedge B
.

Claim 9
.

Let
\alpha
be any one-step formula in
\mathtt{1ML}_{\Lambda}(\mathsf{P}A)
and let
(X,\xi,m)

be a one-step model with
m:X\to\mathsf{P}{A}
.
Consider the map
\eta_{\mathsf{P}A}:\mathsf{P}A\to\mathsf{P}\mathsf{P}A
, so that
\eta_{\mathsf{P}A}\circ m
is a marking of
X
with variables from
\mathsf{P}A
.

(1)

If
X,\xi,\eta_{\mathsf{P}A}\circ m\Vdash^{1}\alpha
then
X,\xi,m\Vdash^{1}\alpha[\land_{A}]
.

(2)

If
X,\xi,m\Vdash^{1}\alpha[\land_{A}]
and the empty set does not appear as
a variable in
\alpha
, and furthermore
m(u)
is a singleton for each
u\in X
,
then
X,\xi,\eta_{\mathsf{P}A}\circ m\Vdash^{1}\alpha
.

Proof of
Claim

For the first part of the proposition, it suffices to note that

[\![B]\!]^{1}_{\eta_{\mathsf{P}A}\circ m}\subseteq[\![\bigwedge B]\!]^{1}_{m}
for each

B\in\mathsf{P}A
, and the result then follows by monotonicity of the predicate
lifting corresponding to the one-step formula
\alpha
.

For the second part, it suffices to note that under the additional constraint
that
m(u)
is a singleton for each
u\in X
and the empty set does not appear
as a variable in
\alpha
, we have
[\![\bigwedge B]\!]^{1}_{m}\subseteq[\![B]\!]^{1}_{\eta_{\mathsf{P}A}\circ m}
for each
B\in\mathsf{P}A
that appears as a
variable in
\alpha
.
To prove this, suppose that
u\in[\![\bigwedge B]\!]^{1}_{m}
.
Since
B
appears in
\alpha
it is non-empty, and since
m(u)
is a singleton,
say
m(u)=\{b\}
, it follows that we must in fact have
B=\{b\}
. Hence:

B\in\{\{b\}\}=\{m(u)\}=\eta_{\mathsf{P}A}(m(u))

so
u\in[\![B]\!]^{1}_{\eta_{\mathsf{P}A}\circ m}
as required.

\blacktriangleleft

Now, let
A=\{a,b,c\}
and consider the formula
\psi=\nabla\{\{a,b\},\{c\}\}
.
If
\mathtt{1ML}_{\Lambda}
admits a disjunctive basis, then there is a disjunctive formula

\delta
in
\mathtt{1ML}_{\Lambda}(\mathsf{P}A)
such that
\psi=\delta[\land_{A}]
.

So suppose
\delta\in\mathtt{1ML}_{\Lambda}(\mathsf{P}A)
is disjunctive, and suppose that

\psi=\delta[\land_{A}]
.
We may in fact assume w.l.o.g. that the empty set does not appear as a variable
in
\delta
, since otherwise we just use instead the formula
\delta[\top/\varnothing]
,
which is still disjunctive (this is easy to prove).
We have
\delta[\top/\varnothing][\land_{A}]=\delta[\land_{A}]
since
\land_{A}(\varnothing)=\bigwedge\varnothing=\top
.

With this in mind, consider the one-step model
X,\xi,m
where
X=\{x_{1},x_{2},x_{3}\}
,
\xi=\{\{x_{1},x_{2}\},\{x_{3}\},X\}
and
m(x_{1})=\{a\}
,
m(x_{2})=\{b\}

and
m(x_{3})=\{c\}
.
It is easy to see that
X,\xi,m\Vdash^{1}\psi
, so by assumption
X,\xi,m\Vdash^{1}\delta[\land_{A}]
.
But since the marking
m
maps every element of
X
to a singleton, item 2 of
Claim
9
gives us that
X,\xi,\eta_{\mathsf{P}A}\circ m\Vdash^{1}\delta
.

Now, define a new one-step model
X,\xi,h
where as before
X=\{x_{1},x_{2},x_{3}\}

and
\xi=\{\{x_{1},x_{2}\},\{x_{3}\},X\}
but where the marking
h:X\to\mathsf{P}\mathsf{P}A
(with respect to variables in
\mathsf{P}A
) is defined by setting
h(x_{1})=\{\{a\}\}
,
h(x_{2})=\{\{a\},\{c\}\}
and
h(x_{3})=\{\{c\}\}
.
It is a matter of simple verification to check that
X,\xi,h
in fact simulates

X,\xi,\eta_{\mathsf{P}A}\circ m
, so by Proposition
1
we get

X,\xi,h\Vdash^{1}\delta
.

Since
\delta
is disjunctive, there should be a one-step model
X^{\prime},\xi^{\prime},h^{\prime}
and
a map
f:X^{\prime}\to X
such that:

X^{\prime},\xi^{\prime},h^{\prime}\Vdash^{1}\delta
,

\mathsf{M}f(\xi^{\prime})=\xi
,

h^{\prime}(u)\subseteq h(f(u))
for all
u\in X^{\prime}
and

h^{\prime}(u)
is at most a singleton for each
u\in X^{\prime}
.
By monotonicity of
\delta
we can in fact assume w.l.o.g. that
h^{\prime}(u)
is
precisely a singleton for each
u\in X^{\prime}
: if
h^{\prime}(u)=\varnothing
, just pick some
element
e
of
h(f(u))
(since
h(v)
is non-empty for each
v\in X
) and set

h^{\prime}(u)=\{e\}
.
The resulting marking still satisfies all the conditions above.

But this means that we can define a marking
n:X^{\prime}\to\mathsf{P}{A}
by taking each

n(u)
for
u\in X^{\prime}
to be the unique
B\subseteq A
such that
h^{\prime}(u)=\{B\}
.
Clearly,
h^{\prime}=\eta_{\mathsf{P}A}\circ n
, so by the first part of
Claim
9
, we get
X^{\prime},\xi^{\prime},n\Vdash^{1}\delta[\land_{A}]
, hence

X^{\prime},\xi^{\prime},n\Vdash^{1}\psi
, i.e.
X^{\prime},\xi^{\prime},n\Vdash^{1}\nabla\{\{a,b\},\{c\}\}
.
But from the definition of the marking
h
, the condition that
h^{\prime}(u)\subseteq h(f(u))
for all
u\in X^{\prime}
and from the definition of
n
it is clear that, for
all
u\in X^{\prime}
, we have
n(u)=\{a\}
or
n(u)=\{c\}
.
So to finally reach our desired contradiction, it suffices to prove the
following.

Claim 10
.

Let
X,\xi,m
be any one-step model such that
X,\xi,m\Vdash^{1}\nabla\{\{a,b\},\{c\}\}
.
Then either there is some
u\in X
with
\{a,c\}\subseteq m(u)
, or there is some

u\in X
with
b\in m(u)
.

Proof of
Claim

Suppose there is no
u\in X
with
b\in m(u)
.
Then there is some set
Z\in\xi
such that every
v\in Z
satisfies
a
.
Furthermore there must be some
B\in\xi
such that every
l\in B
is
satisfied by some member of
Z
.
The only choice possible for this is
\{c\}
, hence some member of
Z
must
satisfy both
a
and
c
.

\blacktriangleleft

This finishes the proof of Proposition
8
.

Interestingly, we may “repair” the failure of admitting a disjunctive basis
by moving to the
supported companion functor

\underline{\mathsf{M}}
.
This functor is defined as the subfunctor of
\mathsf{P}\times\mathsf{M}
, given (on
objects) by
\underline{\mathsf{M}}S\mathrel{:=}\{(U,\sigma)\in\mathsf{P}S\times\mathsf{M}S\mid U\text{ supports }\sigma\}
, where the
notion of support is defined in the standard way, relative to an arbitrary set functor
\mathsf{T}
, as follows:

Definition 3.8
.

Given
\xi\in\mathsf{T}X
, a
support
of
\xi
is a subset
X^{\prime}\subseteq X

such that there is some
\xi^{\prime}\in\mathsf{T}X^{\prime}
with
\mathsf{T}\iota(\xi^{\prime})=\xi
,
where
\iota:X^{\prime}\to X
is the inclusion map.

If we add to the signature
\Sigma_{\mathsf{M}}
of monotone modal logic a box and
diamond modality accessing the support, we obtain a signature which does admit
a disjunctive basis. This is connected with Proposition
5
and the fact that the functor
\underline{\mathsf{M}}
preserves weak pullbacks, which was proved in
[
8
]
.
The supported companion functor features prominently in our completeness proof
(with Seifan) for the monotone
\mu
-calculus
[
8
]
.

4.
Disjunctive automata and simulation

We now introduce disjunctive automata, which serve as a coalgebraic
generalization of non-deterministic automata for the modal
\mu
-calculus.
We refer to the sections
2.5
and
2.4
for background on,
respectively, automata and their connection with
\mu
-calculi, and the
infinite games in which their semantics is formulated.

Definition 4.1
.

A
(\Lambda,\mathtt{X})
-automaton
\mathbb{A}=(A,\Theta,\Omega,a_{I})
is said to be

disjunctive
if
\Theta(c,a)
is disjunctive, for all colors
c\in\mathsf{P}\mathtt{X}
and all states
a\in A
.

Given a disjunctive basis
\mathtt{D}
, we say that
\mathbb{A}
is a
\mathtt{D}
-automaton if

\Theta(c,a)\in\mathtt{D}(A)
, for all colors
c\in\mathsf{P}\mathtt{X}
and all states
a\in A
.

Definition 4.2
.

Let
\mathbb{A}
be a
\Lambda
-automaton and let
(\mathbb{S},s_{I})
be a pointed

\mathsf{T}
-model.
A strategy
f
for
\exists
in
\mathcal{A}(\mathbb{A},\mathbb{S})@(a,s)
is
dividing
if for
every
t
in
\mathbb{S}
there is at most one state
b
in
\mathbb{A}
such that the
position
(b,t)
is
f
-reachable
(i.e., occurs in some
f
-guided
match).
We say that

\mathbb{A}

strongly accepts

(\mathbb{S},s_{I})
, notation:

\mathbb{S},s_{I}\Vdash_{s}\mathbb{A}
if
\exists
has a dividing winning strategy
in the game
\mathcal{A}(\mathbb{A},\mathbb{S})@(a_{I},s_{I})
.

Disjunctive automata are very well behaved.
For instance, the following observation states a
linear-size
model
property.
This observation goes back to Janin & Walukiewicz
[
18
]
, who
proved the linear-size model property for so-called disjunctive formulas of
the modal
\mu
-calculus.
Their result was generalised to the coalgebraic setting of
\nabla
-based
automata by Kupke & Venema
[
22
]
.

Theorem 11
.

Let
\mathbb{A}=(A,\Theta,a_{I},\Omega)
be a disjunctive automaton for a set functor

\mathsf{T}
.
If
\mathbb{A}
accepts some pointed
\mathsf{T}
-model, then it accepts one of which the
carrier
S
satisfies
S\subseteq A
.

The main property of disjunctive automata, which we will use throughout the
remainder of this paper, is the following.

Proposition 12
.

Let
\mathbb{A}
be a disjunctive
\Lambda
-automaton.
Then any pointed
\mathsf{T}
-model which is accepted by
\mathbb{A}
has a pre-image
model which is strongly accepted by
\mathbb{A}
.

Proof 4.3
.

Let
\mathbb{S}=(S,\sigma,V)
be a pointed
\mathsf{T}
-model, let
s_{I}\in S
, and let

f
be a winning strategy for
\exists
in the acceptance game
\mathcal{A}\mathrel{:=}\mathcal{A}(\mathbb{A},\mathbb{S})@(a_{I},s_{I})
.
By Fact
2.3
we may without loss of generality assume that
f
is
positional.

Theorem 13

5.
Lyndon theorems

Lyndon’s classical theorem in model theory provides a syntactic characterization
of a semantic property, showing that a formula is

in a predicate

if and only if it is equivalent to a formula in which

occurs only

positively

A version of this result for the modal
\mu
-calculus was proved by d’Agostino
and Hollenberg in
[
6
]
.
Here, we show that their result holds for any
\mu
-calculus based on a
signature that admits a disjunctive basis.

We first turn to the one-step version of the Lyndon Theorem, for which we need
the following definition; we also recall the substitutions
\land_{A}
and

defined in Section

Definition 5.1
.

A
propositional
A
-type
is a subset of
A
.
For
B\subseteq A
and
a\in A
, the formulas
\tau_{B}
and
\tau_{B}^{a+}
are
defined by:

\begin{array}[]{lll}\tau_{B}&:=&\bigwedge B\wedge\bigwedge\{\neg a\mid a\in A\setminus B\}\\
\tau_{B}^{a+}&:=&\bigwedge B\wedge\bigwedge\{\neg b\mid b\in A\setminus(B\cup\{a\})\}\end{array}

We let
\tau
and
\tau^{a+}
denote the maps

and
B\mapsto\tau_{B}^{a+}

Proposition 14
.

Suppose

admits a disjunctive basis. Then for any formula

\mathtt{1ML}_{\Lambda}(A)
there is a one-step equivalent formula of the form

\delta[\vee_{\mathsf{P}A}][\tau]

\delta\in\mathtt{D}(\mathsf{P}\mathsf{P}A)

Proof 5.2
.

We first check that everything is correctly typed: note that we have

\vee_{\mathsf{P}A}:\mathsf{P}\mathsf{P}A\to\mathtt{Bool}(\mathsf{P}A)

\delta[\vee_{\mathsf{P}A}]\in\mathtt{1ML}_{\Lambda}(\mathsf{P}A)

\tau_{\mathsf{P}A}:\mathsf{P}{A}\to\mathtt{Bool}(A)

So

For the normal form proof, first note that we can use boolean duals of the
modal operators to push negations down to the zero-step level.
Putting the resulting formula in disjunctive normal form, we obtain a
disjunction of formulas of the form

\heartsuit_{\lambda_{1}}\overline{\pi_{1}}\wedge\dots\wedge\heartsuit_{\lambda_{k}}\overline{\pi_{k}}
,
where all the
\pi

Repeatedly applying the distributivity of

over

Now, just apply propositional logic to rewrite each formula

as a
disjunction of formulas in

, and we are done.

A useful corollary to this theorem is that, given an expressively complete set

Generally, we have no guarantee that expressive completeness entails Lyndon

p\in V_{U}^{\flat}(s)

S,\sigma(s),m_{s}\Vdash^{1}\Theta(a_{s},V^{\flat}(s))
by definition of the valuation
V_{U}
. Furthermore, since
V^{\flat}(s)=V_{U}^{\flat}(s)
, we get
S,\sigma(s),m_{s}\Vdash^{1}\Theta(a_{s},V_{U}^{\flat}(s))
.

Hence, (
7
) holds in all three possible cases.
Finally, it is straightforward to derive from (
7
) that
f
itself is
a (dividing) winning strategy for
\exists
in the acceptance game
\mathcal{A}(\mathbb{A},\mathbb{S})
initialized at
(a_{I},s_{I})
.

Remark 5.6
.

Observe that as a corollary of Theorem
17
and the decidability of the
satisfiability problem of
\mu\mathtt{ML}_{\Lambda}

[
3
]
, it is decidable whether
a given formula
\varphi\in\mu\mathtt{ML}_{\Lambda}
is monotone in
p
.

6.
Uniform Interpolation

Uniform interpolation is a very strong form of the interpolation theorem, first
proved for the modal
\mu
-calculus in
[
6
]
.
It was later generalized to coalgebraic modal logics in
[
26
]
.
However, the proof crucially relies on non-deterministic automata, and for that
reason the generalization in
[
26
]
is stated for nabla-based
languages.
With a simulation theorem for predicate liftings based automata in place, we can
prove the uniform interpolation theorem for a large class of
\mu
-calculi based
on predicate liftings.

Definition 6.1
.

Given a formula
\varphi\in\mu\mathtt{ML}_{\Lambda}
, we let
\mathtt{X}_{\varphi}
denote the set of
proposition letters occurring in
\varphi
.
Given a set
\mathtt{X}
of proposition letters and a single proposition letter
p
,
it may be convenient to denote the set
\mathtt{X}\cup\{p\}
as
\mathtt{X}p
.

Definition 6.2
.

A logic
\mathcal{L}
with semantic consequence relation
\models
is said to
have the property of
uniform interpolation
if, for any formula
\varphi\in\mathcal{L}
and any set
\mathtt{X}\subseteq\mathtt{X}_{\varphi}
of proposition
letters, there is a formula
\varphi_{\mathtt{X}}\in\mathcal{L}(\mathtt{X})
,
effectively constructible from
\varphi
, such that

\varphi\models\psi\text{ iff }\varphi_{\mathtt{X}}\models\psi,

(8)

for every formula
\psi\in\mathcal{L}
such that
\mathtt{X}_{\varphi}\cap\mathtt{X}_{\psi}\subseteq\mathtt{X}
.

To see why this property is called uniform
interpolation
, it is not hard
to prove that, if
\varphi\models\psi
, with
\mathtt{X}_{\varphi}\cap\mathtt{X}_{\psi}\subseteq\mathtt{X}
, then the formula
\varphi_{\mathtt{X}}
is indeed an interpolant in the
sense that
\varphi\models\varphi_{\mathtt{X}}\models\psi
and

\mathtt{X}_{\varphi_{\mathtt{X}}}\subseteq\mathtt{X}_{\varphi}\cap\mathtt{X}_{\psi}
.

Theorem 18

(Uniform Interpolation)
.

Let
\Lambda
be a monotone modal signature for the set functor
\mathsf{T}
and
assume that
\Lambda
has a disjunctive basis.
Then both logics
\mathtt{ML}_{\Lambda}
and
\mu\mathtt{ML}_{\Lambda}
enjoy the property of uniform
interpolation.

Following D’Agostino & Hollenberg
[
6
]
, we prove
Theorem
18
by automata-theoretic means.
The key proposition in our proof is Proposition
19
below, which refers
to the following construction on disjunctive automata.

Definition 6.3
.

Let
\mathtt{X}
be a set of proposition letters not containing the letter
p
.
Given a disjunctive
(\Lambda,\mathtt{X}p)
-automaton
\mathbb{A}=(A,\Theta,\Omega,a_{I})
, we
define the map
\Theta^{\exists p}:A\times\mathsf{P}\mathtt{X}\to\mathtt{D}(A)
by

\Theta^{{\exists}p}(a,c)\mathrel{:=}\Theta(a,c)\lor\Theta(a,c\cup\{p\}),

and we let
\mathbb{A}^{{\exists}p}
denote the
(\Lambda,\mathtt{X})
-automaton

(A,\Theta^{{\exists}p},\Omega,a_{I})
.

The following proposition shows that the operation
{(\cdot)}^{{\exists}p}

behaves like an
existential quantifier
, but with a twist: the automaton

\mathbb{A}^{{\exists}p}
accepts a pointed coalgebra model
(\mathbb{S},s_{I})
iff
for some subset
P
of
some preimage model
(\mathbb{S}^{\prime},s^{\prime}_{I})
, the
model
(\mathbb{S}^{\prime}[p\mapsto P],s^{\prime}_{I})
is accepted by
\mathbb{A}
.

Proposition 19
.

Let
\mathtt{X}\subseteq\mathtt{Y}
be sets of proposition letters, both not containing the
letter
p
.
Then for any disjunctive
(\Lambda,\mathtt{X}p)
-automaton
\mathbb{A}
and any pointed

\mathsf{T}
-model
(\mathbb{S},s_{I})
over
\mathtt{Y}
:

\mathbb{S},s_{I}\Vdash\mathbb{A}^{{\exists}p}\text{ iff }\mathbb{S}^{\prime},s^{\prime}_{I}\Vdash_{s}\mathbb{A}\text{ for some $\mathtt{Y}p$-model $(S^{\prime},s^{\prime}_{I})$ such
that }\mathbb{S}^{\prime}\!\!\upharpoonright_{\mathtt{Y}}\,,s^{\prime}_{I}\mathrel{\mathchoice{\raisebox{1.29167pt}{$\,\underline{\makebox[7.15547pt]{$\rightarrow$}}\,$}}{\raisebox{1.29167pt}{$\,\underline{\makebox[7.15547pt]{$\rightarrow$}}\,$}}{\raisebox{0.86108pt}{$\,\underline{\makebox[5.11108pt]{\scriptsize$\rightarrow$}}\,$}}{\raisebox{0.86108pt}{$\,\underline{\makebox[5.11108pt]{\scriptsize$\rightarrow$}}\,$}}}\mathbb{S},s_{I}.

(9)

Proof 6.4
.

We only prove the direction from left to right, leaving the other (easier)
direction as an exercise to the reader.
For notational convenience we assume that
\mathtt{X}=\mathtt{Y}
.

By Proposition
12
it suffices to assume that
(\mathbb{S},s_{I})
is

strongly
accepted by
\mathbb{A}^{{\exists}p}
and find a subset
U
of

S
for which we can prove that
\mathbb{S}[p\mapsto U],s_{I}\Vdash_{s}\mathbb{A}
.
So let
f
be a dividing winning strategy for
\exists
in

\mathcal{A}(\mathbb{A}^{{\exists}p},\mathbb{S})@(a_{I},s_{I})
witnessing that
\mathbb{S},s_{I}\Vdash_{s}\mathbb{A}^{{\exists}p}
.
Call a point
s\in S

f
-accessible
if there is a state
a\in A
such
that the position
(a,s)
is
f
-reachable; since this state is unique by the
assumption of strong acceptance we may denote it as
a_{s}
.
Clearly any position of the form
(a_{s},s)
is winning for
\exists
, and hence
by legitimacy of
f
it holds in particular that

S,\sigma(s),m_{s}\Vdash^{1}\Theta^{{\exists}p}(a_{s},V^{\flat}(s)),

where
m_{s}:S\to\mathsf{P}A
denotes the marking selected by
f
at position

(a_{s},s)
.
Recalling that
\Theta^{{\exists}p}(a_{s},V^{\flat}(s))=\Theta(a_{s},V^{\flat}(s))\lor\Theta(a_{s},V^{\flat}(s)\cup\{p\})
, we define

U\mathrel{:=}\{s\in S\mid s\text{ is $f$-accessible and }S,\sigma(s),m_{s}\not\Vdash^{1}\Theta(a_{s},V^{\flat}(s))\}.

By this we ensure that, for all
f
-accessible points
s
:

\displaystyle s\not\in U

\displaystyle\text{ implies }S,\sigma(s),m_{s}\Vdash^{1}\Theta(a_{s},V^{\flat}(s))

(10)

\displaystyle\text{ while }s\in U

\displaystyle\text{ implies }S,\sigma(s),m_{s}\Vdash^{1}\Theta(a_{s},V^{\flat}(s)\cup\{p\})

(11)

Now consider the valuation
V_{U}\mathrel{:=}V[p\mapsto U]
, and observe that by
this definition we have
V_{U}^{\flat}(s)=V^{\flat}(s)
if
s\not\in U
while

V_{U}^{\flat}(s)=V^{\flat}(s)\cup\{p\}
if
s\in U
.
Combining this with (
10
) and (
11
) we find that

S,\sigma(s),m_{s}\Vdash^{1}\Theta(a_{s},V_{U}^{\flat})

whenever
s
is
f
-accessible.
In other words,
f
provides a legitimate move
m_{s}
in

\mathcal{A}(\mathbb{A},\mathbb{S})@(a_{s},s)
at any position of the form
(a_{s},s)
.
From this it is straightforward to derive that
f
itself is a (dividing)
winning strategy for
\exists
in
\mathcal{A}(\mathbb{A},\mathbb{S}[p\mapsto U])@(a_{I},s_{I})
,
and so we obtain that
\mathbb{S}[p\mapsto U],s_{I}\Vdash_{s}\mathbb{A}
as required.

The remaining part of the argument follows by a fairly standard argument going
back to D’Agostino & Hollenberg
[
6
]
(see also Marti et
alii
[
26
]
) — a minor difference being that our quantification
operation
{(\cdot)}^{\exists p}
refers to pre-images rather than to bisimilar
models.

Proposition 20
.

Given any proposition letter
p
, there is a map
\widetilde{\exists}p
on

\mu\mathtt{ML}_{\Lambda}
, restricting to
\mathtt{ML}_{\Lambda}
, such that
\mathtt{X}_{\widetilde{\exists}p.\varphi}=\mathtt{X}_{\varphi}\setminus\{p\}
and, for every pointed
(\mathbb{S},s_{I})
over a
set
\mathtt{Y}\supseteq\mathtt{X}_{\varphi}
with
p\not\in\mathtt{Y}
:

\mathbb{S},s_{I}\Vdash\widetilde{\exists}p.\varphi\text{ iff }\mathbb{S}^{\prime},s^{\prime}_{I}\Vdash\varphi\text{ for some $\mathtt{Y}p$-model $(S^{\prime},s^{\prime}_{I})$ such
that }\mathbb{S}^{\prime}\!\!\upharpoonright_{\mathtt{Y}}\,,s^{\prime}_{I}\mathrel{\mathchoice{\raisebox{1.29167pt}{$\,\underline{\makebox[7.15547pt]{$\rightarrow$}}\,$}}{\raisebox{1.29167pt}{$\,\underline{\makebox[7.15547pt]{$\rightarrow$}}\,$}}{\raisebox{0.86108pt}{$\,\underline{\makebox[5.11108pt]{\scriptsize$\rightarrow$}}\,$}}{\raisebox{0.86108pt}{$\,\underline{\makebox[5.11108pt]{\scriptsize$\rightarrow$}}\,$}}}\mathbb{S},s_{I}.

(12)

Proof 6.5
.

Straightforward by the equivalence between formulas and
\Lambda
-automata, the
Simulation Theorem, and Proposition
19
.

With
p_{1},\ldots,p_{n}
enumerating the proposition letters in

\mathtt{X}_{\varphi}\setminus\mathtt{X}
, set

\varphi_{\mathtt{X}}\mathrel{:=}\widetilde{\exists}p_{1}\widetilde{\exists}p_{2}\cdots\widetilde{\exists}p_{n}.\varphi.

Then a relatively routine exercise shows that
\varphi\models\psi
iff

\varphi_{\mathtt{X}}\models\psi
, for all formulas
\psi\in\mu\mathtt{ML}_{\Lambda}
such that

\mathtt{X}_{\varphi}\cap\mathtt{X}_{\psi}\subseteq\mathtt{X}
.
For some detail, first assume that
\varphi\models\psi
, and take an arbitrary
pointed model
(\mathbb{S}_{0},s_{0})
over some set
\mathtt{Y}\supseteq\mathtt{X}_{\psi}\cup\mathtt{X}
such that
\mathtt{Y}\cap\{p_{1},\ldots,p_{n}\}=\varnothing
and

\mathbb{S}_{0},s_{0}\Vdash\varphi_{\mathtt{X}}
.
Then successive applications of Proposition
20
provide,
for

, pointed

over

such that

(\mathbb{S}_{i},s_{i})\Vdash\widetilde{\exists}p_{i+1}\cdots\widetilde{\exists}p_{n}.\varphi
and

\mathbb{S}_{i+1}\!\!\upharpoonright_{(\mathtt{Y}\cup\{p_{1},\ldots,p_{i}\})}\,,s_{i+1}\mathrel{\mathchoice{\raisebox{1.29167pt}{$\,\underline{\makebox[6.99997pt]{$\rightarrow$}}\,$}}{\raisebox{1.29167pt}{$\,\underline{\makebox[6.99997pt]{$\rightarrow$}}\,$}}{\raisebox{0.86108pt}{$\,\underline{\makebox[5.0pt]{\scriptsize$\rightarrow$}}\,$}}{\raisebox{0.86108pt}{$\,\underline{\makebox[5.0pt]{\scriptsize$\rightarrow$}}\,$}}}\mathbb{S}_{i},s_{i}
, for all

.
Thus in particular we have
\mathbb{S}_{n},s_{n}\Vdash\varphi

\mathbb{S}_{n}\!\!\upharpoonright_{\mathtt{Y}}\,,s_{n}\mathrel{\mathchoice{\raisebox{1.29167pt}{$\,\underline{\makebox[6.99997pt]{$\rightarrow$}}\,$}}{\raisebox{1.29167pt}{$\,\underline{\makebox[6.99997pt]{$\rightarrow$}}\,$}}{\raisebox{0.86108pt}{$\,\underline{\makebox[5.0pt]{\scriptsize$\rightarrow$}}\,$}}{\raisebox{0.86108pt}{$\,\underline{\makebox[5.0pt]{\scriptsize$\rightarrow$}}\,$}}}\mathbb{S}_{0},s_{0}

\mathrel{\mathchoice{\raisebox{1.29167pt}{$\,\underline{\makebox[6.99997pt]{$\rightarrow$}}\,$}}{\raisebox{1.29167pt}{$\,\underline{\makebox[6.99997pt]{$\rightarrow$}}\,$}}{\raisebox{0.86108pt}{$\,\underline{\makebox[5.0pt]{\scriptsize$\rightarrow$}}\,$}}{\raisebox{0.86108pt}{$\,\underline{\makebox[5.0pt]{\scriptsize$\rightarrow$}}\,$}}}

Finally, it is not difficult to verify that

is fixpoint-free if

.

7.
Disjunctive bases for combined modal logics

An important topic in modal logic concerns methods to construct complex logics
from simpler ones, in such a way that desirable metalogical properties transfer
to a combined logic from its components.
For an overview of this field, see
[
23
]
.
This thread has also been picked up in coalgebraic modal logic: in a particularly
interesting paper
[
5
]
, Cîrstea & Pattinson provide generic
methods to obtain, from modal signatures
for two set functors
\mathsf{T}_{1}
and
\mathsf{T}_{2}
, modal signatures for the
coproduct, product, and composition of
\mathsf{T}_{1}
and
\mathsf{T}_{2}
.
Since the verification that a particular modal logic has a disjunctive basis can
be quite non-trivial — as witnessed here by the case of graded modal logic —
it will be useful to have some methods available that guarantee the existence of
a disjunctive basis being preserved by Cîrstea & Pattinson’s modular
constructions of signatures.
In fact, we will show that disjunctive bases can be constructed in a modular
fashion as well: in each of the cases of coproduct, product and composition we
will give an explicit construction of a disjunctive basis for the combined
signature, based on disjunctive bases for the composing functors.

7.1.
Coproduct

Let
\mathsf{T}=\mathsf{T}_{1}+\mathsf{T}_{2}
be the coproduct of the set functors

\mathsf{T}_{1}
and
\mathsf{T}_{2}
.
For
i=1,2
we will use
\kappa_{i}
to denote the natural
transformation
\kappa_{i}:\mathsf{T}_{i}\Rightarrow\mathsf{T}
that instantiates to
the coproduct insertion map
{(\kappa_{i})}_{S}:\mathsf{T}_{i}S\to\mathsf{T}S
, for every
set
S
.
Now suppose that we have been given signatures for
\mathsf{T}_{1}
and
\mathsf{T}_{2}
,
respectively.
Following Cîrstea & Pattinson
[
5
]
, we define the
combined signature
\Lambda_{1}\oplus\Lambda_{2}
for
\mathsf{T}
as follows.

Definition 7.1
.

Where
\Lambda_{1}
and
\Lambda_{2}
are monotone modal signatures for
\mathsf{T}_{1}
and

\mathsf{T}_{2}
respectively, we define

\Lambda_{1}\oplus\Lambda_{2}\mathrel{:=}\{\kappa_{i}\circ\lambda\mid\lambda\in\Lambda_{i}\}.

In the syntax we shall write
\raisebox{1.29167pt}{$\scriptstyle\bigcirc$}_{i,\lambda}
rather than

\heartsuit_{\kappa_{i}\circ\lambda}
.

We leave it for the reader to check that
\Lambda_{1}\oplus\Lambda_{2}
is indeed a
collection of monotone predicate liftings for
\mathsf{T}
.
The meaning of the
\Lambda_{1}\oplus\Lambda_{2}
-modalities in an arbitrary

\mathsf{T}
-coalgebra
\mathbb{S}
is given as follows:

\mathbb{S},s\Vdash\raisebox{1.29167pt}{$\scriptstyle\bigcirc$}_{i,\lambda}(\varphi_{1},\ldots,\varphi_{n})\text{ iff }\begin{array}[]{l}\sigma(s)={(\kappa_{i})}_{S}(\sigma^{i})\text{ for some }\sigma^{i}\in\mathsf{T}_{i}S\\
\text{with }\sigma^{i}\in\lambda_{S}([\![\varphi_{1}]\!]^{\mathbb{S}},\ldots,[\![\varphi_{n}]\!]^{\mathbb{S}}).\end{array}

(13)

Note that the subscript
i
in
\raisebox{1.29167pt}{$\scriptstyle\bigcirc$}_{i,\lambda}
works as a
tag
indicating to
which part of the coproduct
\mathsf{T}S=\mathsf{T}_{1}S+\mathsf{T}_{2}S
the unfolding

\sigma(s)
of the state
s
is situated.

The result on the existence of disjunctive bases that we want to prove is the
following.

Theorem 21
.

Suppose both signatures
\Lambda_{1}
and
\Lambda_{2}
admit a disjunctive basis.
Then so does
\Lambda_{1}\oplus\Lambda_{2}
.

We start with giving a disjunctive basis for the combined signature.

Definition 7.2
.

Fix a set
A=\{a_{1},\ldots,a_{n}\}
.
Given a one-step formula
\alpha
in the language
\mathtt{1ML}_{\Lambda_{i}}(A)
, we let

\alpha^{i}\in\mathtt{1ML}_{\Lambda_{1}\oplus\Lambda_{2}}(A)
denote the result of
replacing every occurrence of a modality
\heartsuit_{\lambda}
with
\raisebox{1.29167pt}{$\scriptstyle\bigcirc$}_{i,\lambda}
.
Define

(\mathtt{D}_{1}\oplus\mathtt{D}_{2})(A)\mathrel{:=}\{\delta_{1}^{1}\lor\delta_{2}^{2}\in\mathtt{1ML}_{\Lambda_{1}\oplus\Lambda_{2}}(A)\mid\delta_{i}\in\mathtt{D}_{i}(A)\}.

where
\mathtt{D}_{1}
and
\mathtt{D}_{2}
are disjunctive bases for
\Lambda_{1}
and
\Lambda_{2}
,
respectively.

It remains to show that
\mathtt{D}_{1}\oplus\mathtt{D}_{2}
is a disjunctive basis for the
signature
\Lambda_{1}\oplus\Lambda_{2}
.

We first show that
\mathtt{D}_{1}\oplus\mathtt{D}_{2}(A)
consists of disjunctive formulas.
For this purpose we fix a set
A
and an arbitrary formula in

\mathtt{D}_{1}\oplus\mathtt{D}_{2}(A)
, say,
\delta^{1}
such that
\delta\in\mathtt{D}_{1}(A)
.
Let
(S,\sigma,m)
be an arbitrary one-step
\mathsf{T}
-model such that
S,\sigma,m\Vdash^{1}\delta^{1}
, and make a case distinction.
If
\sigma={(\kappa_{2})}_{S}(\sigma^{2})
for some
\sigma^{2}\in\mathsf{T}_{2}S
then
a straightforward induction will show that
S,\sigma,m^{\prime}\Vdash^{1}\delta^{1}
,
where
m^{\prime}(s)\mathrel{:=}\varnothing
for every
s\in S
.

If, on the other hand,
\sigma={(\kappa_{1})}_{S}(\sigma^{1})
for some
\sigma^{1}\in\mathsf{T}_{1}S
then a routine inductive proof will reveal that the one-step

\mathsf{T}_{1}
-model
(S,\sigma^{1},m)
satisfies
\delta
.
By disjunctiveness of
\mathtt{D}_{1}
we then obtain a separating cover for

(S,\sigma^{1},m)
, consisting of a one-step
\mathsf{T}_{1}
-model
(S^{\prime},\sigma^{\prime},m^{\prime})
and
a map
f:S^{\prime}\to S
.
It is then easy to verify that the one-step
\mathsf{T}
-model
(S^{\prime},\kappa_{1}(\sigma^{\prime}),m^{\prime})
, together with the same map
f
, is a separating cover for the one-step

\mathsf{T}
-model
(S,\sigma,m)
.

It is left to check that
\mathtt{D}_{1}\oplus\mathtt{D}_{2}(A)
satisfies the closure
conditions of disjunctive bases.
We leave it to the reader to verify that (modulo equivalence) the set

\mathtt{D}_{1}\oplus\mathtt{D}_{2}(A)
is closed under taking disjunctions, and contains the
formula
\top
.
For condition (2), take a formula of the form
\raisebox{1.29167pt}{$\scriptstyle\bigcirc$}_{i,\lambda}\overline{\pi}
, where

\lambda
is a predicate lifting in
\Lambda_{i}
; without loss of generality assume

i=1
.
Then by assumption there is a formula
\delta\in\mathtt{D}_{1}(\mathsf{P}A)
such that

\heartsuit_{\lambda}\overline{\pi}\equiv^{1}\delta[\wedge_{A}]
.
It is straightforward to verify that
\alpha\equiv^{1}\beta
implies
\alpha^{j}\equiv^{1}\beta^{j}
, for any pair of formulas
\alpha,\beta\in\mathtt{1ML}_{\Lambda_{j}}^{+}(A)
.
But then it is immediate that
\raisebox{1.29167pt}{$\scriptstyle\bigcirc$}_{1,\lambda}\overline{\pi}\equiv^{1}\delta^{1}[\wedge_{A}]\equiv^{1}(\delta^{1}\lor\bot)[\wedge_{A}]
; clearly this suffices, since
\delta^{1}\lor\bot\in\mathtt{D}_{1}\oplus\mathtt{D}_{2}(\mathsf{P}A)
.

Finally, for condition (3), consider the conjunction of two formulas in the sets

\mathtt{D}_{1}\oplus\mathtt{D}_{2}(A)
and
\mathtt{D}_{1}\oplus\mathtt{D}_{2}(B)
, respectively.
Using the distributive law of conjunctions over disjunctions, we may rewrite
this conjunction into an equivalent disjunction of formulas of the form
\alpha^{i}\land\beta^{j}
,
where
\alpha\in\mathtt{D}_{i}(A)
and
\beta\in\mathtt{D}_{j}(B)
for some
\{i,j\}\subseteq\{1,2\}
.
Clearly then it suffices to show that each conjunction of the latter form can be
rewritten into the required shape.
We distinguish two cases.

If
i=j
, then since
\mathtt{D}_{i}
is a disjunctive basis for
\Lambda_{i}
, there is
a formula
\gamma^{i}\in\mathtt{D}_{i}(A\mathbin{\vtop{\halign{#\cr$\cup$\cr\hfil\raise 1.80832pt\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle\times$}\hfil\cr}}}B)
such that
\alpha\land\beta\equiv^{1}\gamma^{i}[\theta_{A,B}]
.
It is then straightforward to verify that
\alpha^{i}\land\beta^{i}\equiv^{1}\gamma^{i}[\theta_{A,B}]\equiv^{1}(\gamma^{i}\lor\bot)[\theta_{A,B}]
.

If, on the other hand,
i
and
j
are distinct, then we may without loss of
generality assume that
i=1
and
j=2
.
We claim that in fact for
any
pair of formulas
\alpha\in\mathtt{1ML}^{+}_{\Lambda_{1}}(A)
and
\beta\in\mathtt{1ML}^{+}_{\Lambda_{2}}(B)
(i.e, we do not need to
assume that
\alpha\in\mathtt{D}_{1}(A)
or
\beta\in\mathtt{D}_{2}(B)
), the conjunction

\alpha^{1}\land\beta^{2}
is equivalent to a formula from the set
\{\top,\bot,\alpha^{1},\beta^{2}\}
.
The key observation in the proof of this claim is that if
\alpha=\heartsuit_{\lambda}\overline{\pi}
and
\beta=\heartsuit_{\eta}\overline{\rho}
, then
\alpha^{1}\land\beta^{2}\equiv\bot
— this easily follows from the observation about tagging that we
made just after (
13
).
Finally, note that
\{\top,\bot,\alpha^{1},\beta^{2}\}\subseteq\mathtt{D}_{1}\oplus\mathtt{D}_{2}(A\cup B)
.
This means that every formula
\gamma
in this set belongs to
\mathtt{D}_{1}\oplus\mathtt{D}_{2}(A\mathbin{\vtop{\halign{#\cr$\cup$\cr\hfil\raise 1.80832pt\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle\times$}\hfil\cr}}}B)
, and in addition satisfies that
\gamma[\theta_{A,B}]=\gamma
.
But then clearly the claim suffices to find a formula
\gamma\in\mathtt{D}_{1}\oplus\mathtt{D}_{2}(A\mathbin{\vtop{\halign{#\cr$\cup$\cr\hfil\raise 1.80832pt\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle\times$}\hfil\cr}}}B)
such that
\alpha^{1}\land\beta^{2}\equiv^{1}\gamma[\theta_{A,B}]
, as required.

∎

7.2.
Product

Given two functors
\mathsf{T}_{1},\mathsf{T}_{2}
and modal signatures
\Lambda_{1},\Lambda_{2}

for these functors respectively, we construct a new modal signature

\Lambda_{1}\otimes\Lambda_{2}
that contains the modalities of both
\Lambda_{1}
and
\Lambda_{2}
. We want to interpret this combined language on coalgebras that can be seen simultaneously as both
\mathsf{T}_{1}
- and
\mathsf{T}_{2}
-coalgebras, and the natural choice is to form the product of the two functors and consider
\mathsf{T}_{1}\times\mathsf{T}_{2}
-coalgebras.

Definition 7.3
.

B^{\prime}
that without loss of generality we may
take to be disjoint.

\mathtt{D}_{1}
being a disjunctive basis yields a formula
\beta_{1}\in\mathtt{D}(A^{\prime}\mathbin{\vtop{\halign{#\cr$\cup$\cr\hfil\raise 1.80832pt\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle\times$}\hfil\cr}}}B^{\prime})
such that
\gamma_{1}\land\delta_{1}\equiv^{1}\beta_{1}[\theta_{A^{\prime},B^{\prime}}]
.
By the disjointness of
A^{\prime}
and
B^{\prime}
we then have

\gamma^{\prime}\land\delta^{\prime}\equiv^{1}\beta_{1}[\theta_{A^{\prime},B^{\prime}}][\sigma][\tau].

(18)

Now consider an arbitrary pair
(a^{\prime},b^{\prime})\in A^{\prime}\times B^{\prime}
; since
\mathtt{D}_{2}

is a disjunctive basis there is a formula
\alpha_{a^{\prime},b^{\prime}}\in\mathtt{D}_{2}(A\times B)

such that
\sigma_{a^{\prime}}\land\tau_{b^{\prime}}\equiv^{1}\alpha_{a^{\prime},b^{\prime}}[\theta_{A,B}]
.
Define the following substitution
\sigma\mathbin{\vtop{\halign{#\cr$\cup$\cr\hfil\raise 1.80832pt\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle\times$}\hfil\cr}}}\tau:A^{\prime}\mathbin{\vtop{\halign{#\cr$\cup$\cr\hfil\raise 1.80832pt\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle\times$}\hfil\cr}}}B^{\prime}\to\ D_{2}(A^{\prime}\mathbin{\vtop{\halign{#\cr$\cup$\cr\hfil\raise 1.80832pt\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle\times$}\hfil\cr}}}B^{\prime})
:

\sigma\mathbin{\vtop{\halign{#\cr$\cup$\cr\hfil\raise 1.80832pt\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle\times$}\hfil\cr}}}\tau(d)\mathrel{:=}\begin{cases}\sigma(d)&\text{ if }d\in A^{\prime}\\
\tau(d)&\text{ if }d\in B^{\prime}\\
\alpha_{a^{\prime},b^{\prime}}&\text{ if }d=(a^{\prime},b^{\prime})\in A^{\prime}\times B^{\prime}\end{cases}

It then follows from the definitions that the substitutions

[\theta_{A^{\prime},B^{\prime}}][\sigma][\tau]
and
[\sigma\mathbin{\vtop{\halign{#\cr$\cup$\cr\hfil\raise 1.80832pt\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle\times$}\hfil\cr}}}\tau][\theta_{A,B}]
produce
one-step equivalent formulas, so that combining this with (
18
) we
obtain that

\gamma^{\prime}\land\delta^{\prime}\equiv^{1}\beta_{1}[\sigma\mathbin{\vtop{\halign{#\cr$\cup$\cr\hfil\raise 1.80832pt\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle\times$}\hfil\cr}}}\tau][\theta_{A,B}].

This suffices, since obviously we have that
\beta_{1}[\sigma\mathbin{\vtop{\halign{#\cr$\cup$\cr\hfil\raise 1.80832pt\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle\times$}\hfil\cr}}}\tau]\in\mathtt{D}_{1}\circledcirc\mathtt{D}_{2}(A\mathbin{\vtop{\halign{#\cr$\cup$\cr\hfil\raise 1.80832pt\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle\times$}\hfil\cr}}}B)
.

∎

8.
Yoneda representation of disjunctive liftings

It is a well known fact in coalgebraic modal logic that predicate liftings have
a neat representation via an application of the Yoneda lemma.
This was explored by Schröder in
[
32
]
, where it was used among
other things to prove a characterization theorem for the monotone predicate
liftings.
Here, we apply the same idea to disjunctive liftings. We shall be working with a slightly generalized notion of predicate lifting here, taking a predicate lifting over a finite set of variables
A
to be a natural transformation
\lambda:\breve{\mathsf{P}}^{A}\to\breve{\mathsf{P}}\circ\mathsf{T}
. Clearly, one-step formulas in
\mathtt{1ML}_{\Lambda}(A)
can then be viewed as predicate liftings over
A
.

Definition 8.1
.

Let
\lambda:\breve{\mathsf{P}}^{A}\to\breve{\mathsf{P}}\circ\mathsf{T}
be a predicate lifting over
variables
A=\{a_{1},\dots,a_{n}\}
.
The
Yoneda representation

y(\lambda)
of
\lambda
is the subset

\lambda_{\mathsf{P}A}(\mathsf{true}_{a_{1}},\dots,\mathsf{true}_{a_{n}})\in\mathsf{P}\mathsf{T}\mathsf{P}A

where
\mathsf{true}_{a_{i}}=\{B\subseteq A\mid a_{i}\in B\}
.
We shall write simply
\lambda\subseteq\mathsf{T}\mathsf{P}A
instead of
y(\lambda)
.

Definition 8.2
.

Given a set
A
, let
A^{\top}
be the set
A\cup\{\top\}
.
Let
\epsilon_{A}\subseteq A^{\top}\times\mathsf{P}A
be the relation defined by

a\epsilon_{A}B
iff
a\in B
, and
\top\epsilon_{A}B
for all
B\subseteq A
.
Let
\eta_{A}:A^{\top}\to\mathsf{P}A
be defined by
\eta_{A}(a)=\{a\}
, and

\eta_{A}(\top)=\varnothing
.

In the remainder of this section we assume familiarity with the Barr relation
lifting
\overline{\mathsf{T}}
associated with a functor
\mathsf{T}
; see
[
21
]

for the definition and some basic properties.

Definition 8.3
.

A predicate lifting
\lambda\subseteq\mathsf{T}\mathsf{P}A
is said to be

divisible
if, for all
\alpha\in\lambda
there is some
\beta\in\mathsf{T}A^{\top}
such that

(\beta,\alpha)\in\overline{\mathsf{T}}(\epsilon_{A})
and

\mathsf{T}\eta_{A}(\beta)\in\lambda
.

Proposition 26
.

Any disjunctive lifting over
A
is divisible, and if
\mathsf{T}
preserves weak
pullbacks the disjunctive liftings over
A
are precisely the divisible ones.

Proof 8.4
.

Suppose
\lambda\subseteq\mathsf{T}\mathsf{P}A
is disjunctive, and pick
\alpha\in\lambda
.
Then
\mathsf{P}A,\alpha,\mathsf{id}_{\mathsf{P}A}\Vdash^{1}\lambda
, so since

\lambda
is disjunctive there are some one-step model
(X,\xi,m)
and
map
f:X\to\mathsf{P}A
with
m:X\to\mathsf{P}A
,

m(u)\subseteq f(u)
for all
u\in X
,
\mathsf{T}f(\xi)=\alpha
, and

|m(u)|\leq 1
for all
u\in X
.
We define a map
g:X\to A^{\top}
by setting
g:u\mapsto\top
if
m(u)=\varnothing
,
g:u\mapsto a
if
m(u)=\{a\}
.
We tuple the maps
f,g
to get a map
\langle f,g\rangle:X\to A^{\top}\times\mathsf{P}A
.
In fact, since
m(u)\subseteq f(u)
for all
u\in X
, we have
\langle f,g\rangle:X\to\epsilon_{A}
. Let
\pi_{1}:\epsilon_{A}\to A^{\top}
and
\pi_{2}:\epsilon_{A}\to\mathsf{P}A
be the projection maps.
We have the following diagram, in which the two triangles and the outer edges
commute (i.e.,
m=\eta_{A}\circ g
).

Now apply
\mathsf{T}
to this diagram and define
\beta\in\mathsf{T}A^{\top}
to be

\mathsf{T}(\pi_{1}\circ\langle f,g\rangle)(\xi)=\mathsf{T}g(\xi)
.
First, we have
(\beta,\alpha)\in\overline{\mathsf{T}}(\epsilon_{A})
, witnessed by

\mathsf{T}(\langle f,g\rangle)(\xi)\in\mathsf{T}\epsilon_{A}
.
We claim that
\mathsf{T}\eta_{A}(\beta)\in\lambda
.
But since
X,\xi,m\Vdash^{1}\lambda
and
m=\eta_{A}\circ g
, naturality of

\lambda
applied to the map
g:X\to A^{\top}
, gives
A^{\top},\beta,\eta_{A}\Vdash^{1}\lambda
.
Another naturality argument, applied to

\eta_{A}:(A^{\top},\beta,\eta_{A})\to(\mathsf{P}A,\mathsf{T}\eta_{A}(\beta),\mathsf{id}_{\mathsf{P}A})

gives
\mathsf{P}A,\mathsf{T}\eta_{A}(\beta),\mathsf{id}_{\mathsf{P}A}\Vdash^{1}\lambda
, i.e.,

\mathsf{T}\eta_{A}(\beta)\in\lambda
.

For the converse direction, under the assumption that
\mathsf{T}
preserves weak
pullbacks, suppose that
\lambda
is divisible, and suppose
X,\xi,m\Vdash^{1}\lambda
. We get
\mathsf{T}m(\xi)\in\lambda
and so we find some
\beta\in\mathsf{T}A^{\top}
with
\beta(\overline{\mathsf{T}}\epsilon_{A})\mathsf{T}m(\xi)
and

\mathsf{T}\eta_{A}(\beta)\in\lambda
.
Pick some
\beta^{\prime}\in\mathsf{T}\epsilon_{A}
with
\mathsf{T}\pi_{2}(\beta^{\prime})=\mathsf{T}m(\xi)

and
\mathsf{T}\pi_{1}(\beta^{\prime})=\beta
.
Let
R,g_{1},g_{2}
be the pullback of the diagram

, shown in the diagram:

.
The map

on

\eta_{A}\circ\pi_{1}\circ g_{2}

It is now routine to check that

, and
|m^{\prime}(u)|\leq 1
and

for all

, so we are done.

For the moment, we leave the question open, whether a similar characterization
of disjunctive predicate liftings can be proved without weak pullback
preservation.
We also leave it as an open problem to characterize the functors that admit
a disjunctive basis.
