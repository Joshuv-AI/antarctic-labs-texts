Abstract
We investigate the semigroup of integer points inside a convex cone. We extend classical results in integer linear programming to integer conic programming. We show that the semigroup associated with nonpolyhedral cones can sometimes have a notion of finite generating set with the help of a group action. We show this is true for the cone of positive semidefinite matrices (PSD) and the second-order cone (SOC). Both cones have a finite generating set of integer points, similar in spirit to Hilbert bases, under the action of a finitely generated group. Introduction

A semigroup
S
is a subset of
\mathbb{Z}^{N}
that contains
\mathbf{0}
and is closed under addition. Given a convex cone
C\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{N}
, the integer points
S_{C}\mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}=C\cap\mathbb{Z}^{N}
form a semigroup which we will call the
conical semigroup
of
C
. In particular, given any compact convex body
K\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{n}
, the integer points
\text{cone}(K\times\{1\})\cap\mathbb{Z}^{n+1}
form a conical semigroup. Conical semigroups appear not just in optimization
Aliev et al. 2018
;
Berndt et al. 2023
, but also in algebra and number theory
Aliev et al. 2022
;
Aliev et al. 2017
. Given a convex cone
C\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{N}
for
N\geq 1
, we say a subset
B\subseteq S_{C}
is an
integral generating set
of
S_{C}
if for any
s\in S_{C}
there exist
b_{1},\dots,b_{m}\in B
and
c_{1},\dots,c_{m}\in\mathbb{Z}_{\geq 0}
such that
s=\sum_{i=1}^{m}c_{i}b_{i}
, for some
m\geq 1
. Furthermore, we call
B
a
conical Hilbert basis
if
B
is an inclusion-minimal integral generating set.

When the defining cone
C
is rational, polyhedral, and pointed, there is abundant literature on the topic. It is well-known that we have a unique finite Hilbert basis in this case
De Loera et al. 2013
;
Schrijver 1998
. Historically, Hilbert bases have been fundamental in the theory and algorithms of combinatorial optimization. For example, determining if a rational system
A\mathbf{x}\leq\mathbf{b}
is totally dual integral (TDI) is equivalent to checking if, for every face
F
of the polyhedron
P\mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}=\{\mathbf{x}\mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}A\mathbf{x}\leq\mathbf{b}\}
, the rows of
A
which are active in a face
F
form a Hilbert basis for cone(
F
)
Schrijver 1998
.

It is natural to ask, what properties transfer from rational polyhedral cones to arbitrary convex cones? The question we will consider is
do we preserve any notion of finiteness in generating sets for semigroups when we relax the polyhedral condition and instead consider general conical semigroups? Are there Hilbert bases for general cones?

Unlike for polyhedra, prior work on understanding the lattice points of general convex cones is very sparse. For instance, when is the convex hull of the lattice points a closed set? This has been investigated in
Dey and Morán R. 2013
.
In
Hemmecke and
Weismantel 2007
the authors presented conditions for when a set of lattice points have a
finite
integral generating set, partially emulating polyhedra. Unfortunately, this is rarely the case. Our paper discusses finite generation for conical semigroups and partially extends the polyhedral cone theory of Hilbert bases to nonpolyhedral convex cones.

Our first contribution to the subject is to introduce a group action, allowing for finite generation with the help of the
group. Our main results will show that with a group action,
the semigroups of the cone of positive semidefinite matrices and
(some of) the second-order cone are in fact finitely generated. Both cones play a key role in modern optimization
Barvinok 2002
;
Ben-Tal and
Nemirovski 2001
. We also discuss
some applications of our nonpolyhedral point of view.

In what follows, we denote
\mathrm{GL}(N,\mathbb{Z})\mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}=\{U\in\mathbb{Z}^{N\times N}\mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}~|\det(U)|=1\}
.
Here is our new notion of finite generation for conical semigroups.

Definition 1
.

Given a conical semigroup
S_{C}\subset\mathbb{Z}^{N}
, we call it
(R,G)
-finitely generated if there is a finite subset
R\subseteq S_{C}
and a finitely generated subgroup
G\subseteq\mathrm{GL}(N,\mathbb{Z})
acting on
C
linearly such that

1.

both the cone
C
and the semigroup
S_{C}
are invariant under the group action, i.e.,
G\cdot C=C
and
G\cdot S_{C}=S_{C}
, and

2.

every element
s\in S_{C}
can be represented as

s=\displaystyle\sum_{i\in K}\lambda_{i}g_{i}\cdot r_{i}

for some
r_{i}\in R
,
g_{i}\in G
, and
\lambda_{i}\in\mathbb{Z}_{\geq 0}
,
i\in K
, where
K
is a finite index set.

When
C
is a (pointed) rational polyhedral cone, then the conical semigroup
S_{C}=C\cap\mathbb{Z}^{N}
is
(R,G)
-finitely generated if we take
R
to be its Hilbert basis, and
G
to be the trivial group
\{I_{N}\}
of the identity action. Similarly, note that if
S_{C}
is an
(R,G)
-finitely generated semigroup, then
\cup_{r\in R}(G\cdot r)
is an integral generating set of
S_{C}
, which is a superset of a conical Hilbert basis. We call
R
the set of
roots
of
S_{C}
, and
\cup_{r\in R}(G\cdot r)
the set of generators for
S_{C}
.

While a nonpolyhedral cone cannot be finitely generated in the usual sense,
using an infinite (yet finitely generated) group
G
allows us to extend our understanding beyond the polyhedral case. Because the possibly infinite generators for
S_{C}
can be obtained by group action
G
on a finite set

and
G
is finitely generated, this allows for the possibility of algorithmic methods.
The well-known Krein-Milman theorem states that any point in a closed pointed cone
C
can be generated by extreme rays, denoted by
\operatorname{ext}(C)

Barvinok 2002
. When we restrict to the conical semigroup
S_{C}
and nonnegative integer combinations, the primitive integer points on the extreme rays of
C
must be contained in the set of generators of
S_{C}
, where an integer point

is

if
\mathrm{gcd}(x_{1},\dots,x_{N})=1

on the extreme rays of

, denoted by
\operatorname{ext}(S_{C})\mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}=\{y\in S_{C}\mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}y\in\operatorname{ext}(C)\cap\mathbb{Z}^{N}\}
.
However, as in the polyhedral case, the generators will often include extra nonextreme boundary points or even interior points. We provide the following definition of
sporadic points
which are points that cannot have an extreme point subtracted from them and still remain within the cone.

Definition 2
.

We say a point
x\in S_{C}=C\cap\mathbb{Z}^{N}
is sporadic if there does not exist
y\in\operatorname{ext}(S_{C})
such that
x-y\in S_{C}
.

If

is sporadic, then

cannot be written as an integer conical combination of extreme points (even though it can be written as a real combination of them). From the definition of sporadic points, we know that all points
x\in S_{C}
can be written as an integer conical combination of primitive extreme points and one sporadic point. To show that a semigroup is
(R,G)
-finitely generated, it is sufficient to show that the set of primitive extreme points and sporadic points are finite or can be obtained from a finitely generated group
G
that acts on a finite set of roots,
R
.

In this work, we are mainly interested in the positive semidefinite cone (PSD) and the second-order cone (SOC).

What is the significance of these results beyond their connections to classical geometry of numbers, lattices, and number theory? (See e.g.,
Kaveh and

The first application regards the notion of
Chvátal-Gomory cuts
which is useful in

methods for integer programming. How much of this can be extended to conic integer programming? Given a linear map
\mathcal{A}\mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}\mathbb{R}^{m}\to\mathbb{R}^{N}

\mathrm{LCI}_{C}(\mathbf{c},\mathcal{A})\mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}=\{\mathbf{x}\in\mathbb{R}^{m}\mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}\mathbf{c}-\mathcal{A}(\mathbf{x})\in C\}

where
\mathbf{c}\in\mathbb{Z}^{N}
and

When

is the cone of positive semidefinite matrices in

and

\mathcal{A}(\mathbf{x})=\sum_{i=1}^{m}x_{i}A_{i}

for some matrices

.
This is known as a

and defines a

An important concept for LCI is called total dual integrality (TDI), which has been well-known for polyhedral cones
C

;

We use

Definition 3

An LCI system

\displaystyle\min\quad

\displaystyle y(\mathbf{c})\quad\mathrm{s.t.}\quad\mathcal{A}^{*}(y)=\mathbf{b},\ y\in C^{*},

whenever feasible, has an integer optimal solution

To approximate the convex hull of

\mathrm{CG}\text{-}\mathrm{cl}(Z)\mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}=\bigcap_{\begin{subarray}{c}(\mathbf{u},v)\in\mathbb{Z}^{m}\times\mathbb{R}\mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}\\
S\subseteq\{\mathbf{x}\mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}\mathbf{u}^{\mathsf{T}}\mathbf{x}\leq v\}\end{subarray}}\left\{\mathbf{x}\in\mathbb{R}^{m}\mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}\mathbf{u}^{\mathsf{T}}\mathbf{x}\leq\lfloor v\rfloor\right\}.

(1)

Now take any linear function

Then, a CG cut can be generated by

Conversely, if the conical semigroup

Suppose

The final application has to do with classical notions of integer rank
Cook et al. 1986

Just like the notion of (real) rank of a linear system allows us to bound the number of nonzero entries in a solution of a linear system, we want to know how many elements are needed to decompose any element of a conical semigroup as a linear combination of generators with nonnegative integer coefficients. Suppose that our conical semigroup
S_{C}=C\cap\mathbb{Z}^{N}
has an integer generating set

s\in S_{C}
, there exist integer generators

The minimum number

(ICR) of

is the ICR of the conical semigroup
S_{C}
or the cone

The proof is almost identical to the popular polyhedral result in

Theorem 4

Let

be an arbitrary pointed convex cone and
S_{C}\mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}=C\cap\mathbb{Z}^{N}

Then
\mathrm{ICR}(S_{C})\leq 2N-2

2
The Positive Semidefinite (PSD) Cone

Let
\mathcal{S}^{n}(\mathbb{Z})
(resp.
\mathcal{S}^{n}(\mathbb{R})
) denote the set of
n\times n
symmetric matrices of integer (resp. real) entries.
For a matrix
X\in\mathcal{S}^{n}(\mathbb{Z})
, we say that
X
is PSD (denoted as
X\succeq 0
) if and only if it is so when regarded as a real matrix
X\in\mathcal{S}^{n}(\mathbb{R})
. We denote
\mathcal{S}^{n}_{+}(\mathbb{Z})
as the set of integer PSD matrices.

The group
\mathrm{GL}(n,\mathbb{Z})
embeds into
\mathrm{GL}(N,\mathbb{Z})
as follows.
Given a matrix
U\in\mathrm{GL}(n,\mathbb{Z})
and any
X\in\mathcal{S}^{n}(\mathbb{Z})
, we define the action
U\cdot X\mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}=UXU^{\mathsf{T}}
.
This action is a linear map and takes integer points in
\mathbb{Z}^{N}
to integer points, and thus can be represented by the multiplication with a matrix in
\mathrm{GL}(N,\mathbb{Z})
.

It is well-known that this group
\mathrm{GL}(n,\mathbb{Z})
is finitely generated by three matrices
Trott 1962
:

\begin{bmatrix}0&1&0&\dots&0&0\\
0&0&1&\dots&0&0\\
&&&\dots&&\\
0&0&0&\dots&0&1\\
1&0&0&\dots&0&0\\
\end{bmatrix},\begin{bmatrix}1&0&0&\dots&0&0\\
1&1&0&\dots&0&0\\
&&&\dots&&\\
0&0&0&\dots&1&0\\
0&0&0&\dots&0&1\\
\end{bmatrix},\begin{bmatrix}0&1&0&\dots&0&0\\
1&0&0&\dots&0&0\\
&&&\dots&&\\
0&0&0&\dots&1&0\\
0&0&0&\dots&0&1\\
\end{bmatrix}.

For simplicity, we keep the notation
U\cdot X
for the action of
U\in\mathrm{GL}(n,\mathbb{Z})
on
X
without explicitly specifying its representation in
\mathrm{GL}(N,\mathbb{Z})
.

2.1
Lemmas for
n\leq 5
and

The following
integer rank-1 decomposition
for PSD integer matrices is studied in

We recast their arguments with a modern geometric perspective, and use it to extend the notion of

-finite generation to the PSD cone.

Lemma 5
.

If
n\leq 5
, then for any
X\in\mathcal{S}_{+}^{n}(\mathbb{Z})
, we can find a finite index set
K
and vectors
\mathbf{x}_{i}\in\mathbb{Z}^{n}
,
i\in K
such that

X=\sum_{i\in K}\mathbf{x}_{i}\mathbf{x}_{i}^{\mathsf{T}}.

(2)

To restate Definition
2
in the PSD case, we say an integer matrix
X\in\mathcal{S}^{n}(\mathbb{Z})
is
sporadic
if there does not exist
\mathbf{x}\in\mathbb{Z}^{n}\setminus\{\mathbf{0}\}
such that
X-\mathbf{x}\mathbf{x}^{\mathsf{T}}\succeq 0
.
Lemma

is equivalent to the fact that there is no sporadic point in
\mathcal{S}_{+}^{n}(\mathbb{Z})
when

Proposition 6
.

There is no sporadic point in
\mathcal{S}_{+}^{n}(\mathbb{Z})
if and only if every positive semidefinite integer matrix in
\mathcal{S}_{+}^{n}(\mathbb{Z})
has an integer rank-1 decomposition.

Proof.

If there is no sporadic point in
\mathcal{S}_{+}^{n}(\mathbb{Z})
, then for every
Y\in\mathcal{S}_{+}^{n}(\mathbb{Z})
, there exists
\mathbf{x}\in\mathbb{Z}^{n}\setminus\{\mathbf{0}\}
such that
Y-\mathbf{x}\mathbf{x}^{\mathsf{T}}\succeq 0
.
For
X\in\mathcal{S}_{+}^{n}(\mathbb{Z})
, we do the following procedure for
X_{0}\mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}=X
(with index
i
initialized to 1).

1.

Take any
\mathbf{x}_{i}\in\mathbb{Z}^{n}\setminus\{\mathbf{0}\}
such that
X_{i}\mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}=X_{i-1}-\mathbf{x}_{i}\mathbf{x}_{i}^{\mathsf{T}}\succeq 0
.

2.

If
X=0
, then we have found an integer rank-1 decomposition
X=\sum_{j=1}^{i}\mathbf{x}_{j}\mathbf{x}_{j}^{\mathsf{T}}
;
otherwise set the index
i\leftarrow i+1
and go back to step 1.

To see that the procedure terminates in finitely many steps, note that the diagonal of
\mathbf{x}_{i}\mathbf{x}_{i}^{\mathsf{T}}
contains at least 1 nonzero entry because
\mathbf{x}_{i}\neq 0
.
Thus the trace
\mathrm{tr}(X_{i})\leq\mathrm{tr}(X_{i-1})-1
for any
i\geq 1
because the entries are integers.
The procedure can repeat no more than
\mathrm{tr}(X)
times as
\mathrm{tr}(X_{i})\geq 0
.

If every
X\in\mathcal{S}_{+}^{n}(\mathbb{Z})
has an integer rank-1 decomposition
X=\sum_{i\in K}\mathbf{x}_{i}\mathbf{x}_{i}^{\mathsf{T}}
, then any of
\mathbf{x}_{i},i\in K
satisfies the requirement
X-\mathbf{x}_{i}\mathbf{x}_{i}^{\mathsf{T}}\succeq 0
.
∎

For any matrix
X\in\mathcal{S}^{n}(\mathbb{R})
, we can define a convex set
K(X)\mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}=\{\mathbf{x}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}\mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}X-\mathbf{x}\mathbf{x}^{\mathsf{T}}\succeq 0\}
.
Since
X-\mathbf{x}\mathbf{x}^{\mathsf{T}}\succeq 0
if and only if, for any
\mathbf{v}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
,
|\mathbf{v}^{\mathsf{T}}\mathbf{x}|^{2}\leq\mathbf{v}^{\mathsf{T}}X\mathbf{v}
, we see that
K(X)
is a compact convex set that is symmetric about the origin.
This provides another equivalent formulation of the integer rank-1 decomposition.

Proposition 7
.

For
X\in\mathcal{S}_{+}^{n}(\mathbb{Z})
,

is sporadic if and only if
K(X)\cap\mathbb{Z}^{n}=\{\mathbf{0}\}
.

This provides a geometric perspective to our problem.
Note that the set
K(X)
is an ellipsoid
(that may fail to be full-dimensional)

Proposition 8

Suppose

Proof.

As

for some

for

. In particular,

. Denote

.
Without loss of generality, we can assume that

, otherwise, we can just consider the submatrix corresponding to the nonzero

Suppose that there exists some

. Then
a_{i}a_{j}\notin\mathbb{Q}

for all

or
a_{i}\notin\mathbb{Q}
for any
i
.

If

for all

, then the result holds with

and

.

If
a_{i}\notin\mathbb{Q}
for all

is not a square.
Because

, we have

for some integer

. Suppose that

are all the prime factors in the decompositions of

,
i\in[n]

is even for all

or
\alpha_{\ell}^{i}
is odd for all

. Let

and

. We have

is a square, thus

for

, where

.

3.1
Sporadic Points of

In this section, we will begin by restating the definition of sporadic points in the case of

and offer two partial characterizations of sporadic elements of

.

Definition 5
.

We call a point
\mathbf{s}\in T_{n}
sporadic if there is no integral point
\mathbf{p}
such that
\langle\mathbf{p},\mathbf{p}\rangle=0
and
\mathbf{s}-\mathbf{p}\in T_{n}
.

Just as the group
G
takes elements of
\partial T_{n}
to
\partial T_{n}
, the group
G
will take sporadic elements to sporadic elements. This closure ensures that our action by
G
on the semigroup

is well-defined.

Lemma 15
.

Let
\mathbf{s}\in T_{n}
.

1.

Then,
A_{n}^{+}\mathbf{s}
and
(A_{n}^{+})^{-1}\mathbf{s}
are both in
T_{n}
.

2.

If
\mathbf{s}
is sporadic, then
A_{n}^{+}\mathbf{s}
and
(A_{n}^{+})^{-1}\mathbf{s}
are both sporadic.

Proof.

The first claim follows by simply checking the required inequalities directly. For the second claim, we proceed by contradiction. Suppose
\mathbf{s}
is sporadic but
(A_{n}^{+})\mathbf{s}
is not. Then, there is some point
\mathbf{p}\in T_{n}
such that
\langle\mathbf{p},\mathbf{p}\rangle=0
and
(A_{n}^{+})\mathbf{s}-\mathbf{p}\in T_{n}
. However, we would then have that

(A_{n}^{+})^{-1}(A_{n}^{+}\mathbf{s}-\mathbf{p})=\mathbf{s}-(A_{n}^{+})^{-1}\mathbf{p}\in T_{n}.

As
\mathbf{p}\in\partial T_{n}
,
(A_{n}^{+})^{-1}\mathbf{p}\in\partial T_{n}
, which is a contradiction with
\mathbf{s}
being sporadic. The case of
(A_{n}^{+})^{-1}\mathbf{s}
being sporadic follows the same argument.
∎

can be written as the sum of two sporadic points with smaller heights:

(2,2,2,2,2,2,2,1,6)=(1,1,1,1,1,1,1,0,3)+(1,1,1,1,1,1,0,0,3).

We can similarly decompose
(2,2,2,2,2,2,2,2,1,6)
and
(2,2,2,2,2,2,2,1,0,6)
for
n=10
. In this sense, these sporadic points fail to be minimal. Thus, if we remove them from the set of roots
R
, our semigroup
S
remains
(R,G)
-finitely generated. However, when we remove these point from our root sets, our decomposition in equality (
12
) requires modification and we must allow for multiple sporadic points in the expression.

Remark 3
.

Lastly, it is worth noting that inequality (
11
) would fail in dimensions larger than 10. Thus, this line of argumentation would fail to produce results for
n>10
.

We can use Theorem
20
to recover a partial converse of Lemma
17
.

Corollary 21
.

Let
3\leq n<7
and fix
\mathbf{s}\in T_{n}
. If
\mathbf{s}
is a primitive sporadic, then
\langle\mathbf{s},\mathbf{s}\rangle=-1
.

Proof.

Let
\mathbf{s}\in T_{n}
be sporadic. Using theorem
20
, we can express
\mathbf{s}
as

\mathbf{s}=G^{\prime}(0,\dots,0,1)^{\mathsf{T}}

for
G^{\prime}\in G
. As
\langle G^{\prime}\mathbf{s},G^{\prime}\mathbf{s}\rangle=\langle\mathbf{s},\mathbf{s}\rangle
for all
G^{\prime}\in G
, we see that

\langle\mathbf{s},\mathbf{s}\rangle=\langle G^{\prime}(0,\dots,0,1)^{\mathsf{T}},G^{\prime}(0,\dots,0,1)^{\mathsf{T}}\rangle=\langle(0,\dots,0,1)^{\mathsf{T}},(0,\dots,0,1)^{\mathsf{T}}\rangle=-1.

∎

In particular, this converse fails to hold in dimensions
\geq 7
as we have
\mathbf{r}\in R
such that
\langle\mathbf{r},\mathbf{r}\rangle\neq-1
.

4
Not All Conical Semigroups are
(R,G)
-Finitely Generated

The next example shows that not every conical semigroup is
(R,G)
-finitely generated.

Example 1
.

Let
C\mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}=\{(x,y)\in\mathbb{R}^{2}\mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}0\leq y\leq\alpha x\}
be a cone generated by the vectors
v_{1}\mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}=(1,0)
and
v_{2}\mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}=(1,\alpha)
, where
\alpha>0
is an irrational number.
We claim that the conical semigroup
S_{C}\mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}=C\cap\mathbb{Z}^{2}
is not
(R,G)
-finitely generated for any finitely generated group
G
acting linearly on
C
and any finite set
R\subset S_{C}
.

It suffices to show that any
g\in G
that acts linearly on
C
and restricts to
S_{C}
must be the identity. Specifically, if
g
is such that
g(c_{1}u_{1}+c_{2}u_{2})=c_{1}g(u_{1})+c_{2}g(u_{2})
for any
u_{1},u_{2}\in\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}
and
u_{1},u_{2}\in C
, and
g(S_{C})\subseteq S_{C}
, then
g=I
.

To see this, note that
C
is simplicial as
v_{1}
and
v_{2}
are linearly independent, so any vector
v\in C
can be written uniquely as a linear combination of
v_{1}
and
v_{2}
.
Thus any
linear action by
g
on
C
can be represented by a matrix

U_{g}=\begin{bmatrix}u_{11}&u_{12}\\
u_{21}&u_{22}\end{bmatrix}\in\mathbb{R}^{2\times 2}.

The determinant
\det{U_{g}}=u_{11}u_{22}-u_{21}u_{12}
, which is nonzero due to the definition of the group action
g
, and
U_{g}^{-1}=\dfrac{1}{\det{U_{g}}}\begin{bmatrix}u_{22}&-u_{12}\\
-u_{21}&u_{11}\end{bmatrix}
.
Since
v_{1}=(1,0)\in S_{C}
, we must have

U_{g}v_{1}=\begin{bmatrix}u_{11}\\
u_{21}\end{bmatrix}\in S_{C},

which implies that
u_{11},u_{21}\in\mathbb{Z}
and

0\leq u_{21}\leq\alpha u_{11}.

(13)

Let
w\mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}=(\lceil 1/\alpha\rceil,1)\in S_{C}
, we must have

U_{g}{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}w}=\begin{bmatrix}\lceil 1/\alpha\rceil u_{11}+u_{12}\\
\lceil 1/\alpha\rceil u_{21}+u_{22}\end{bmatrix}\in S_{C},

which implies that
u_{12},u_{22}\in\mathbb{Z}
.
At this point, we see that
U_{g}\in\mathbb{Z}^{2\times 2}
.
Now take a sequence of vectors
w_{k}\mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}=(x_{k},y_{k})\in S_{C}
such that
\lim_{k\to\infty}\dfrac{y_{k}}{x_{k}}=\alpha
, the existence of which is guaranteed by the Dirichlet’s approximation theorem.
Since
U_{g}w_{k}\in S_{C}
, we must have
0\leq u_{21}x_{k}+u_{22}y_{k}\leq\alpha(u_{11}x_{k}+u_{12}y_{k})
, which in turn gives

0\leq u_{21}+\alpha u_{22}\leq\alpha(u_{11}+\alpha u_{12})

(14)

by taking
k\to\infty
.
Moreover, since

U_{g}^{-1}v_{1}=\frac{1}{\det{U_{g}}}\begin{bmatrix}u_{22}\\
-u_{21}\end{bmatrix}\in S_{C},

we must have

0\leq(\det{U_{g}})^{-1}(-u_{21})\leq\alpha(\det{U_{g}})^{-1}u_{22},

(15)

which implies that

\dfrac{1}{\det{U_{g}}}(\alpha u_{22}+u_{21})\geq 0
.
By (
14
),
u_{21}+\alpha u_{22}\geq 0
, and the inequality is strict because of the irrationality of
\alpha
.
Consequently, (
15
) shows that
\det{U_{g}}>0
.
Thus (
15
) further implies that
u_{21}\leq 0
, so
u_{21}=0
by (
13
).
Now consider

U_{g}^{-1}w_{k}=\dfrac{1}{\det{U_{g}}}\begin{bmatrix}u_{22}x_{k}-u_{12}y_{k}\\
u_{11}y_{k}\end{bmatrix}\in S_{C},

so again by taking
k\to\infty
, we have
0\leq\alpha u_{11}\leq\alpha(u_{22}-\alpha u_{12})
.
Together with (
14
), which becomes
u_{22}\leq u_{11}+\alpha u_{12}
, we see that
u_{22}=u_{11}+\alpha u_{12}
.
Again using the irrationality of
\alpha
, we must have
u_{12}=0
, and consequently
u_{11}=u_{22}
.
Finally, note that
U_{g}^{-1}v_{1}=\begin{bmatrix}1/u_{11}\\
0\end{bmatrix}\in S_{C}
, so
1/u_{11}\in\mathbb{Z}
and thus
u_{11}=\pm 1
.
From (
13
), we conclude that
u_{11}=u_{22}=1
, and
U_{g}=\begin{bmatrix}1&0\\
0&1\end{bmatrix}
is the identity matrix.
Since the generator
v_{2}
is not a rational vector, we know that
S_{C}
itself is not finitely generated, so it is not
(R,G)
-finitely generated either.

5
Applications: Total Dual Integrality, Chvátal-Gomory Closures, and Integer Rank

To prove Theorems
3
and
4
, we use the notation
\mathbb{R}^{\oplus I}
(or simply
\mathbb{R}^{I}
) to denote an
\mathbb{R}
-vector space for a possibly infinite index set
I
, where each vector
(a_{i})_{i\in I}\in\mathbb{R}^{I}
has all but finitely many zero coordinates
a_{i}=0
.

Proof for Theorem
3
.

The containment
\subseteq
follows from definition.
To see the other containment, take any halfspace
H\mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}=\{\mathbf{x}\in\mathbb{R}^{m}\mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}{\color[rgb]{0,0,1}\mathbf{u}}^{\mathsf{T}}\mathbf{x}\leq v\}
for some
(\mathbf{u},v)\in\mathbb{Z}^{m}\times\mathbb{R}
such that
C\subseteq H
.
Then by the full dimensionality of
C
, we have

\displaystyle v\geq

\displaystyle\sup_{x}\left\{\mathbf{u}^{\mathsf{T}}\mathbf{x}\mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}\mathbf{c}-\mathcal{A}(\mathbf{x})\in C\right\}=\inf_{y}\left\{y(\mathbf{c})\mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}\mathcal{A}^{*}(y)=\mathbf{u},\ y\in C^{*}\right\}.

Using the TDI assumption, the infimum is attained by some
y^{*}\in C^{*}\cap\mathbb{Z}^{N}
.
As
S_{C^{*}}
is
(R,G)
-finitely generated,
r_{1},\dots,r_{k}\in R
,
g_{1},\dots,g_{k}\in G
, and
\lambda_{1},\dots,\lambda_{k}\in\mathbb{Z}_{\geq 0}
such that

y^{*}=\sum_{j=1}^{k}\lambda_{j}(g_{j}\cdot r_{j}),

for some
k\geq 1
.
Consequently, we have

\lfloor v\rfloor\geq\lfloor y^{*}(\mathbf{c})\rfloor=\biggl\lfloor\sum_{j=1}^{k}\lambda_{j}(g_{j}\cdot r_{j})^{\mathsf{T}}\mathbf{c}\biggr\rfloor\geq\sum_{j=1}^{k}\lambda_{j}\lfloor(g_{j}\cdot r_{j})^{\mathsf{T}}\mathbf{c}\rfloor.

Also as a consequence of the
(R,G)
-finite generation of
S_{C^{*}}
, determining
\inf_{y}\left\{y(\mathbf{c})\mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}\mathcal{A}^{*}(y)=\mathbf{u},\ y\in C^{*}\right\}
is equivalent to solving the following (semi-infinite) linear optimization problem:

\displaystyle\inf_{\lambda}\quad

\displaystyle\sum_{r\in R,g\in G}\lfloor(g\cdot r)^{\mathsf{T}}\mathbf{c}\rfloor\lambda_{r,g}

\displaystyle\mathrm{s.t.}\quad

\displaystyle\mathcal{A}^{*}\biggl(\sum_{r\in R,g\in G}\lambda_{g,r}(g\cdot r)\biggr)=\mathbf{u},

\displaystyle\lambda\in\mathbb{R}^{\oplus{\{(g,r)\mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}~r\in R,g\in G\}}}_{\geq 0}.

Note that
(\lambda_{1},\dots,\lambda_{k})
is a feasible solution to this problem. By weak duality of the semi-infinite optimization problem, we have

\sum_{j=1}^{k}\lambda_{j}\lfloor(g_{j}\cdot r_{j})^{\mathsf{T}}\mathbf{c}\rfloor\geq\sup_{x}\left\{\mathbf{u}^{\mathsf{T}}\mathbf{x}\mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}\mathcal{A}^{*}(g\cdot r)^{\mathsf{T}}\mathbf{x}\leq\lfloor(g\cdot r)^{\mathsf{T}}\mathbf{c}\rfloor,\ \forall\,r\in R,g\in G\right\}.

Therefore, the inequality
\mathbf{u}^{\mathsf{T}}\mathbf{x}\leq\lfloor v\rfloor
is implied by the inequalities
(g\cdot r)^{\mathsf{T}}\mathcal{A}(\mathbf{x})=\mathcal{A}^{*}(g\cdot r)^{\mathsf{T}}\mathbf{x}\leq\lfloor(g\cdot r)^{\mathsf{T}}\mathbf{c}\rfloor
for
r\in R,g\in G
.
Since the halfspace
H
is arbitrary, we conclude that

\mathrm{CG}\text{-}\mathrm{cl}(Z)\supseteq\left\{\mathbf{x}\in\mathbb{R}^{m}\mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}(g\cdot r)^{\mathsf{T}}\mathcal{A}(\mathbf{x})\leq\lfloor(g\cdot r)^{\mathsf{T}}\mathbf{c}\rfloor,\quad\forall\,r\in R,g\in G\right\}.\qed

Proof for Theorem
4
.

Suppose
B=\{b_{i}\}_{i\in I}\subset S_{C}
for some possibly infinite index set
I
is an integer generating set for
S_{C}
.

Consider a semi-infinite linear optimization problem

\displaystyle\max

\displaystyle\sum_{i\in I}\lambda_{i}

(16)

\displaystyle\mathrm{s.t.}

\displaystyle\sum_{i\in I}\lambda_{i}b_{i}=s,

\displaystyle(\lambda_{i})_{i\in I}\in\mathbb{R}^{\oplus I}_{\geq 0}.

Since
C
is pointed,
B
satisfies the “opposite sign condition,” meaning that whenever
\sum_{i\in I}\mu_{i}b_{i}=0
for some nonzero
(\mu_{i})_{i\in I}\in\mathbb{R}^{\oplus I}
, we have
\mu_{i}<0<\mu_{j}
for some
i,j\in I
.
Thus by
(
Charnes et al. 1963
, Theorem 2)
, we know that (
16
) has an extreme point solution, denoted as
(\lambda^{*}_{i})_{i\in I}
with
J\mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}=\{i\in I\mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}\lambda^{*}_{i}>0\}
.
By
(
Charnes et al. 1963
, Theorem 1)
, the vectors
\{\lambda_{i}\}_{i\in J}\subset S_{C}
associated with the extreme point solution must be linearly independent.
Thus
|J|\leq N
.

For each
i\in J
, let
z_{i}\mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}=\lfloor\lambda_{i}^{*}\rfloor
and
y_{i}\mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}=\lambda_{i}^{*}-z_{i}
.
We claim that
\sum_{i\in J}y_{i}<N-1
.
Given this claim, the theorem is proved as follows.
The vector
s^{\prime}\mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}=s-\sum_{i\in J}z_{i}b_{i}\in C\cap\mathbb{Z}^{N}=S_{C}
can be written as integer combination of
B
by definition, that is, there exists an index set
J^{\prime}\subset I
with
b^{\prime}_{i}\in B
,
\lambda^{\prime}_{i}\in\mathbb{Z}_{\geq 1}
for each
i\in J^{\prime}
such that
s^{\prime}=\sum_{i\in J^{\prime}}\lambda^{\prime}_{i}b^{\prime}_{i}
.
This implies that

s=s^{\prime}+\sum_{i\in J}z_{i}b_{i}=\sum_{i\in J}z_{i}b_{i}+\sum_{j\in J^{\prime}}\lambda^{\prime}_{i}b^{\prime}_{i}.

We see that
\sum_{i\in J}z_{i}+\sum_{j\in J^{\prime}}\lambda^{\prime}_{i}\leq\sum_{i\in J}\lambda^{*}_{i}
by the optimality of
(\lambda^{*}_{i})_{i\in I}
.
Consequently,
\sum_{j\in J^{\prime}}\lambda^{\prime}_{j}\leq\sum_{i\in J}y_{i}<N-1
, and thus
s
can be written as an integer sum of at most
|J|+N-2\leq 2N-2
generators from
B
.

It remains to prove the claim,
\sum_{i\in J}y_{i}<N-1
.
Note that if
|J|\leq N-1
this is trivially true because
y_{i}<1
by definition.
So we may assume that
|J|=N
and denote
J=\{1,\dots,N\}
without loss of generality.
Moreover, if the convex hull
V\mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}=\mathrm{conv}\{b_{1},\dots,b_{N}\}
has a nonempty intersection with
B
, say
b_{N+1}\in V\cap B
with
b_{N+1}=\sum_{i=1}^{N}\gamma_{i}b_{i}
for some
0<\gamma_{1},\dots,\gamma_{N}<1
,
\sum_{i=1}^{N}\gamma_{i}=1
, then we can write
s
as

s=\epsilon b_{N+1}+\sum_{i=1}^{N}(\lambda_{i}^{*}-\epsilon\gamma_{i})b_{i},

where
\epsilon\mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}=\frac{\lambda_{\iota}^{*}}{\gamma_{\iota}}
for some
\iota\in\mathrm{argmin}\{\frac{\lambda_{i}^{*}}{\gamma_{i}}\mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}i=1,\dots,N\}
.
This shows that
(\mu^{*}_{i})_{i\in I}
with
\mu_{i}^{*}\mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}=\lambda^{*}_{i}-\epsilon\gamma_{i}
for each
i\in J\setminus\{\iota\}
,
\mu_{N+1}^{*}\mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}=\epsilon
, and
\mu_{i}^{*}=0
for any
i\notin J_{1}\mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}=J\cup\{N+1\}\setminus\{\iota\}
, is also an optimal solution to (
16
).
Thus by replacing
J
with
J_{1}
, we can assume that the intersection
V\cap B=\varnothing
.
Under this assumption, let
s^{\prime\prime}\mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}=\sum_{i=1}^{N}(1-y_{i})b_{i}=\sum_{i=1}^{N}b_{i}-s^{\prime}\in C\cap\mathbb{Z}^{N}
.
Again by the definition of
B
, we can write
s^{\prime\prime}=\sum_{j\in J^{\prime\prime}}\lambda_{j}^{\prime\prime}b_{j}^{\prime\prime}
, for some finite subset
J^{\prime\prime}\subset I
,
b_{j}^{\prime\prime}\in B
and
\lambda^{\prime\prime}_{j}\in\mathbb{Z}_{\geq 1}
for each
j\in J^{\prime\prime}
.
Note that

s=\delta s^{\prime\prime}+\sum_{i\in J}(\lambda_{i}^{*}-\delta(1-y_{i}))b_{i}=\delta\sum_{i\in J^{\prime\prime}}\lambda_{i}^{\prime\prime}b_{i}+\sum_{i\in J}(\lambda_{i}^{*}-\delta(1-y_{i}))b_{i},

for some sufficiently small
\delta>0
,
so by the optimality of
(\lambda_{i}^{*})_{i\in I}
, we must have
1\leq\sum_{i\in J^{\prime\prime}}\lambda_{i}^{\prime\prime}\leq\sum_{i\in J}(1-y_{i})
.
If
\sum_{i=1}^{N}y_{i}\geq N-1
, then this implies that
\sum_{i\in J}(1-y_{i})=1
, which is a contradiction with our assumption
V\cap B=\varnothing
.
Thus we must have
\sum_{i=1}^{N}y_{i}<N-1
.
∎

Remark 4
.

If we apply the theorem to the case
S_{C}=\mathcal{S}_{+}^{n}(\mathbb{Z})
, then
N=\dim{\mathcal{S}^{n}(\mathbb{R})}=\binom{n+1}{2}
.
The bound on the ICR in this case is
2N-2=n^{2}+n-2
, which grows
quadratically
with
n
as opposed to the linear growth in the case of the usual Carathéodory rank of positive semidefinite matrices.
If we apply the theorem to the case
T_{n}=\mathrm{SOC}(n)\cap\mathbb{Z}^{n}
, then
N=\dim{\mathrm{SOC}(n)}=n
.
The ICR in this case is
2N-2=2n-2
.
