Abstract
. This work investigates topological chaos for homeomorphisms of the open annulus, introducing a new set of sufficient conditions based on points with distinct rotation numbers and their topological relation to invariant continua. These conditions allow us to formulate classic methods for verifying annular chaos in a finitely verifiable version supported on basic properties of the map. The results pave the way for simple computer-assisted proofs of chaos in a wide range of annular maps, including many well–known examples, and we present these proofs for some analytic families, demonstrating the effectiveness of the method. On the theoretical side, one of the consequences of the established conditions permits the proof of a folkloric conjecture about the relation between topological entropy and rotation sets.



1.
Introduction

In the study of dynamical systems, the perception that there are various natural phenomena that can be modeled in a simple way and yet behave in a rather unpredictable way dates back to the beginning of the last century. This emergence of “chaotic” behavior, which eventually led to the development of the mathematical notion of
chaos
, first became apparent in two qualitatively very different settings: The first one inside Hamiltonian dynamics, as a result of the pioneering work of Poincaré on
the three-body problem
; the second appeared in dissipative systems associated with periodically forced second-order o.d.e. arising from electric circuits, such as the

the Van der Pol equation
.

The study of these unpredictability and the development of the concept of chaos was one of the driving forces in Dynamical systems in the last century, leading to the establishment of formal notions and also the comprehension of some well-know paradigmatic mathematical examples. From a topological perspective, probably the better notion of chaotic dynamical system is that of a system having positive topological entropy, which is used in this work.

However, despite the wide advancements in the field, a significant challenge remains: determining whether a specific dynamical system exhibits positive topological entropy, particularly in the absence of global topological conditions. This issue plagues many important classical examples and consequently, current theoretical frameworks for chaos struggle to translate directly to proofs for specific systems. In practice, rigorous proofs of the existence of chaos are often available only for a relatively small set of families, and the parameter ranges where such proofs work is often extremely narrow. Moreover, the classical techniques are hard to be adapted to specific maps, and hence cannot be used in a systematic way. References like
[
14
,
15
,
44
,
29
,
30
,
61
,
26
]
exemplify these limitations. In contrast, in several areas of contemporary science there is a plethora of articles concluding that certain systems are chaotic after numerical simulations, without any rigorous proof. The study of the
double physical pendulum
serves as a notorious example of these situations.

In this work we deal with
annular homeomorphisms
, that is, homeomorphisms of the two-dimensional open annulus. The study of these maps is of well- known relevance, since several relevant models in with two degrees of freedom have global Poincaré sections that are annuli. In particular, this is the case for some restrictions of the
3
-body problem as well as the above mentioned Van der Pol Equation, and many others in various fields of science. These dynamics can often be displayed as a parameterized family of maps which contain an initial
integrable
model with non-trivial rotation sets, and it was the study of these systems that led Birkhoff, continuing Poincaré’s work, to implement the systematic study of annular discrete dynamics with non-trivial rotational behavior.
Since then, the theory has been developed in several different branches and here we consider the topological one. Let us list some of the most used methods in the study of topological entropy for annular homeomorphisms.

•

Melinkov integral.
This method was already used by Poincaré,
and it shows positive entropy by giving sufficient conditions which imply that a saddle homoclinic connection of an integrable
system becomes a transversal homoclinic intersection when the parameters of the system
vary. It is usually the case that it can only be implemented for parameters
very close
to those of integrable cases. See for instance the nice survey
[
30
]
.

•

Hyperbolic sets and cone fields.
The aim in this case is to find non-trivial hyperbolic sets and is based on the construction of fitted cone fields or, equivalently,
of fitted quadratic-forms. This method been used in renown families in the Hamiltonian and dissipative settings (see for instance
[
12
,
61
]
), and usually, in the dissipative setting, it works only in situations where the dissipation is very strong. For instance, it is often the case in the dissipative context that one tries to make use of the theory of one-dimensional chaotic dynamic, and this approach requires that the Jacobian of the maps are close to zero.

•

Nielsen-Thurston Theory.
In the absence of a rich surface topology, as is the case in the annulus, this method requires the existence of a certain configuration of periodic orbits, so that once removed, the remaining dynamics is isotopic to a pseudo-Anosov map. These family of periodic points can be be characterized with
braids
(see for instance
[
8
,
46
]
). The limiting feature for the implementation of this approach in our context is the following: even in the presence of large rotation sets, the topological entropy of these systems can be arbitrarily small (see for instance
[
54
]
). This means that the periodic orbits needed in order to apply the method could have an arbitrarily large period, and be difficult to detect.

•

Generic dynamics.
In contrast with the study of specific examples, there is a collection of results where positive entropy is shown to hold in a residual sets of area-preserving smooth diffeomorphisms, see
[
58
,
55
]
and recently
[
41
]
. For example, a generic
C^{\infty}
perturbation of the time one of the Hamiltonian dynamics associated with the simple pendulum is clearly chaotic. Note that this does not replace the Melinkov integral method since the families we are interested in are usually in the complement of such residual sets.

•

Numerical evidence.
There is a vast literature about numerical evidence of chaos for different meaningful systems inside and outside mathematics. Nevertheless rigorous proofs are extremely rare.

The main results are of topological nature, but we also reach statements inside metric contexts

Let

Given an open connected region

. We say that

In particular, an accessible point

2.2.
Dynamical Preliminaries

The set of maps
f:\mathbb{A}\to\mathbb{A}
that are homeomorphisms in the isotopy class
of the identity is denoted by
\textrm{Homeo}_{0}(\mathbb{A})
. The lifts of any element

f\in\textrm{Homeo}_{0}(\mathbb{A})
is a family of orientation preserving homeomorphisms
in
\mathbb{R}^{2}
verifying that
F(x+(1,0))=F(x)+(1,0)
for all
x\in\mathbb{R}^{2}
, where
F

is any of such lifts.
The difference of any two of these lifts is given by an integer horizontal
translation. Fixing one of them, say
F
, permits to associate the rotation set

\rho(F)=\left\{\lim_{i}\frac{\textrm{pr}_{1}(F^{n_{i}}(x_{i})-x_{i})}{n_{i}}:\,n_{i}\nearrow+\infty,\,x_{i}\in\mathbb{R}^{2}\right\}

Changing the lift of
F
modifies the rotation set by a translation by some integer.
It is not difficult to prove that

\rho(F)
is always a closed interval (possibly unbounded).

When we have a compact invariant set
X\subset\mathbb{A}
for an element
f\in\textrm{Homeo}_{0}(\mathbb{A})

it is possible to define the rotation set relative to
X
for any lift
F
of
f
by

\rho(X,F)=\left\{\lim_{i}\frac{\textrm{pr}_{1}(F^{n_{i}}(x_{i})-x_{i})}{n_{i}}:\,n_{i}\nearrow+\infty,\,\pi(x_{i})\in X\right\}

This kind of sets are also found in the literature denoted by
\rho_{X}(F)
. Whenever
X
is an annular continuum, it holds that
\rho(X,F)
is a compact interval. In the case where
X
is a singleton
X=\{x\}
, we also denote

\rho(X,F)=\rho(x,F)
. Note that in this situation,
x
is a fixed point for
f
and hence

\rho(x,F)\in\mathbb{Z}
. We say that two fixed points
x_{0},x_{1}
of
f

have different rotation vectors
whenever
\rho(x_{0},F)\neq\rho(x_{1},F)
for a lift
F
of
f
. This last property does not depend upon the choice of lift
F
of
f
. We call rotational difference of
x_{0},x_{1}

to the integer number

\rho=\rho(x_{1},F)-\rho(x_{0},F)

which we will usually consider positive.

Given an annular region
U\subset\mathbb{A}
which is invariant by a homeomorphism
f:\mathbb{A}\to\mathbb{A}
we say that a point
x\in\partial U

is
dynamically virtually accesible
, d.v.a in short, whenever
it is virtually accessible and some support
K
of a ray
r
containing
x

is
f
-invariant.

Let us introduce a definition that appears in the first of ours main statements.

annular quasi-attractor
and
annular quasi-repeller
. For a homeomorphism

f:\mathbb{A}\to\mathbb{A}
, an
annular quasi attractor
is an invariant annular continuum
\mathcal{A}
so that there exists a neighborhood
V
of
\mathcal{A}
verifying that for any essential simple curve

\gamma\subset V\setminus\mathcal{A}
it holds that:

(i)

The set of accumulation points of
(f^{n}(\gamma))_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
contains an essential subset of
\mathcal{A}
,

(ii)

The set of accumulation points of
(f^{-n}(\gamma))_{n\in\mathbb{N}}

contains no essential continua of
\mathcal{A}
.

An
annular quasi-repeller
for
f
is an annular quasi-attractor for
f^{-1}
.

Remark 2.1
.

If the set of non-wandering points of
f
is the whole annulus, then
f
cannot have an annular quasi-attractor. Indeed, assume that
\mathcal{A}
an annular quasi-attractor for a map

, and let
\gamma
be an essential curve in the complement of
\mathcal{A}
whose forward iterates accumulate on a point

but whose backwards iterates do not accumulate on

. Assume, with no loss of generality, that
\mathcal{A}
lies in
\mathcal{U}^{-}(\gamma)
. The fact that the backwards iterates of

do not accumulate on

is invariant, implies there exists a neighborhood

of

that lies in
\mathcal{U}^{-}(f^{-n}(\gamma))

show that there exist a first integer

(1)

The numerics shown here were calculated for maps that are variations of the Standard Family (S.F.). The S.F.

\mathbb{A}
which lift

\mathbb{T}^{2}

Proposition 9.1
.

Given any
f_{\textbf{h},\textbf{v}}
as above, then either every point is non-wandering or
f_{\textbf{h},\textbf{v}}
has a rotational horseshoe.

Proof.

There are two cases to consider. If

f_{\textbf{h},\textbf{v}}
lifts a map of
\mathbb{T}^{2}
homotopic to the identity that preserves Lebesgue measure, and satisfies the hypotheses of Proposition
8.1

h(x+1)=h(x)+p

p

f_{\textbf{h},\textbf{v}}
lifts an homeomorphism of
\mathbb{T}^{2}

[

1

[
4
