On the Engulfment of Antifreeze Proteins by Ice
Aniket U. Thosar, Yusheng Cai, Sean M. Marks, Zachariah Vicars, Jeongmoon Choi, Akash Pallath, Amish J. Patel

Abstract
Antifreeze proteins (AFPs) are remarkable biomolecules that suppress ice formation at trace concentrations. To inhibit ice growth, AFPs must not only bind to ice crystals, but also resist engulfment by ice. The highest supercooling, Δ ​ T ∗ \Delta T^{*} , for which AFPs are able to resist engulfment is widely believed to scale as the inverse of the separation, L L , between bound AFPs, whereas its dependence on the molecular characteristics of the AFP remains poorly understood. By using specialized molecular simulations and interfacial thermodynamics, here we show that in contrast with conventional wisdom, Δ ​ T ∗ \Delta T^{*} scales as L − 2 L^{-2} and not as L − 1 L^{-1} . We further show that Δ ​ T ∗ \Delta T^{*} is proportional to AFP size and that diverse naturally occurring AFPs are optimal at resisting engulfment by ice. By facilitating the development of AFP structure-function relationships, we hope that our findings will pave the way for the rational design of novel AFPs.

Free Energetics of AFP Engulfment by Ice

To understand how AFPs resist engulfment by ice,
we first study the spruce budworm AFP (sbwAFP)
Tyshenko et al. 1997
;
Leinala et al. 2002
.
For hyperactive AFPs, such as sbwAFP, which bind to all ice planes
Scotter et al. 2006
,
AFP engulfment results in rapid and uncontrolled ice growth
Basu et al. 2015
.
Thus, the supercooling,
\Delta T^{*}
, at which a hyperactive AFP is spontaneously engulfed by ice,
also provides a quantitative measure of its thermal hysteresis activity
Drori et al. 2014
;
Naullage et al. 2018
.
To characterize sbwAFP engulfment by ice and its dependence on supercooling,
\Delta T
,
we estimate the free energetics,
G(\lambda;\Delta T)
, of observing
\lambda
ice-like waters
in the system
for a wide range of
\Delta T
-values from 4 K to 20 K.
A simulation snapshot of sbwAFP molecules bound to the ice-water interface, and separated by roughly 6 nm, is shown in Figure
1
A, and
a detailed description of the simulation setup and parameters as well as the enhanced sampling techniques used to estimate
G(\lambda;\Delta T)
is provided in the
SI
.

As shown in Figure
1
B,
G(\lambda;\Delta T)
displays a local minimum
at
\lambda\approx 2000
corresponding to the AFP bound to ice.
For low supercooling, the bound (low
\lambda
) and engulfed (high
\lambda
) states are separated by large free energetic barriers,
G_{\rm barr}
, which arrest ice growth and localize the system in its bound state.
However, as
\Delta T
is increased, and the thermodynamic driving force for ice growth increases, the engulfment barriers decrease (Figure
1
B).
Above a critical supercooling,
\Delta T^{*}\approx 18
K, the engulfment barrier,
G_{\rm barr}
, vanishes altogether,
making it possible for ice to spontaneously engulf the AFP.
We note that the precise supercooling at which engulfment occurs must depend
not only on the engulfment barrier, but also the pre-exponential constant and the observation timescale
Farag and Peters 2023
;
however, given the sensitive dependence of the engulfment barriers on
\Delta T
(Figure
1
C),
here we approximate
\Delta T^{*}
to be supercooling at which the barrier vanishes.
Indeed, using non-equilibrium simulations quenched to different temperatures, we find that ice growth is arrested for
\Delta T<\Delta T^{*}
, whereas AFP engulfment is observed for
\Delta T>\Delta T^{*}
(Figure S5).

To understand how AFP engulfment barriers vary with supercooling,
we recognize that
G_{\rm barr}
is strongly correlated with
g_{\rm m}
,
the maximum value of the slope,
g_{\lambda}\equiv dG/d\lambda
,
which quantifies the the resistance to engulfment.
As
\Delta T
is increased, both
G_{\rm barr}
and
g_{\rm m}
decrease until they vanish at
\Delta T^{*}
(Figure
1
C).
The dependence of
g_{\rm m}
on
\Delta T
is remarkably simple,
and can be described well by the linear relationship,
g_{\rm m}(\Delta T)=-m_{g}\Delta T+c_{g}
, so that
\Delta T^{*}=c_{g}/m_{g}
.
This key finding raises a number of interesting questions:
(i) why is
g_{\rm m}
a linear function of
\Delta T
;
(ii) is this trend expected for all separations,
L
, and for different AFPs; and if so,
(iii) how do
m_{g}
and
c_{g}
, and correspondingly
\Delta T^{*}=c_{g}/m_{g}
, depend on AFP structure and separation?

Shape of the Ice-Water Interface

To answer the questions raised above,
we make use of macroscopic interfacial thermodynamics
Gennes et al. 2004
;
Remsing et al. 2015
;
Prakash et al. 2016
,
which provides the following expression for
the free energy,
G_{\rm th}
, of a system
that has an ice-water interface profile,
h(x,y)
, and

\lambda
ice-like waters at a supercooling,
\Delta T
:

G_{\rm th}([h],\lambda;\Delta T)=-\Delta\mu\rho V+\gamma A+\Delta\gamma A_{\rm nbs}+\mathcal{L}_{\lambda}(\lambda-\rho V),

(1)

where
\Delta\mu\propto\Delta T
is the chemical potential difference between liquid water and ice;

\rho
and
V
are the density and volume of ice, respectively;

\gamma
and
A
are the ice-water interface tension and area, respectively;

\Delta\gamma
is the difference between the NBS-ice and NBS-water surface tensions,
and quantifies NBS ice-phobicity;

A_{\rm nbs}
is the area of the NBS in contact with ice; and

\mathcal{L}_{\lambda}
is the Lagrange multiplier that constrains
\rho V
to
\lambda
(Figure
2
A).
Because the values of
V
and
A
depend on the ice-water interface profile,
the corresponding free energy,
G_{\rm th}
, is a functional of
h(x,y)
.
Equation
1
highlights that the engulfment free energetics stem from a complex interplay between the thermodynamic driving force,
\Delta\mu
, which favors ice growth,
the ice-water interface tension,
\gamma
, which seeks to minimize the interfacial area,
and the ice-phobicity of the NBS,
\Delta\gamma
, which disfavors engulfment.

To understand the implications of this interplay, we recognize that the
mean-field
ice-water interface,
which minimizes
G_{\rm th}([h],\lambda;\Delta T)
,
must be a constant mean curvature surface,
i.e., the sum of its principal curvatures must be the same for all
(x,y)
,
and that
the magnitude of the mean curvature,
\kappa_{\lambda}
,
must be equal to
(\rho/2\gamma)(g_{\lambda}+\Delta\mu)
,
as shown in the
SI
.
Indeed, the average interface profile,
\bar{h}_{\lambda}(x,y)
, obtained from our simulations,
for a partially engulfed AFP with
\lambda\approx 3200
,
is not flat, but has a negative mean curvature, as shown in Figure
2
B.
The average interface profiles and mean curvature maps for a wide range of
\lambda
-values
are included in the
SI
(Figure S9),
along with the detailed procedure for obtaining them from our simulations.
In Figure
2
C, we plot the magnitude of the interfacial curvature,
\kappa_{\lambda}
,
as a function of
\lambda
, and find that it displays a maximum;

\kappa_{\lambda}
increases from zero at
\lambda_{\rm b}\approx 1600
(bound state at
\Delta T=0
),
reaches its maximum,
\kappa_{\rm m}=0.22\pm 0.02
nm
-1
at
\lambda_{\rm m}\approx 2900
,
then decreases to eventually vanish at
\lambda\approx 5700
(engulfed state).
In Figure
2
C, we also compare the curvature obtained from the simulated interface profiles,
\kappa_{\lambda}^{\rm int}
, to the corresponding theoretical prediction,
\kappa_{\lambda}^{\rm th}\equiv(\rho/2\gamma)(g_{\lambda}+\Delta\mu)
, and find good agreement between the two;
here,
g_{\lambda}
was estimated using the simulated free energies,
G(\lambda;\Delta T)
(Figure
1
B).

The close relationship between interfacial curvature and the resistance to engulfment,

\kappa_{\lambda}=(\rho/2\gamma)(g_{\lambda}+\Delta\mu)
,
implies that the maximum resistance to engulfment,
g_{\rm m}
, ought to be determined by
the maximum interfacial curvature,
\kappa_{\rm m}
, that the AFP NBS can sustain through:

g_{\rm m}=-\Delta\mu+\bigg(\frac{2\gamma}{\rho}\bigg)\kappa_{\rm m}.

(2)

Importantly, Equation
2
sheds light on the linear
relationship,
g_{\rm m}(\Delta T)=-m_{g}\Delta T+c_{g}
,
which was observed in Figure
1
C.
In particular, because
\Delta\mu\approx(\Delta h/T_{\rm eq})\Delta T
, where
\Delta h
is the enthalpy of melting,
and
(2\gamma/\rho)\kappa_{\rm m}
is largely independent of
\Delta T
, as shown in the
SI
,
Equation
2
not only explains the
linear dependence of
g_{\rm m}
on
\Delta T
,
but also sheds light on the physical significance of the fit parameters,
suggesting that
m_{g}=\Delta h/T_{\rm eq}
and
c_{g}=(2\gamma/\rho)\kappa_{\rm m}
.
Indeed, the values of
m_{g}
and
c_{g}
, obtained in Figure
1
C,
are in good agreement with our estimates of
\Delta h/T_{\rm eq}
and
(2\gamma/\rho)\kappa_{\rm m}
, as shown in the
SI
.

By using macroscopic theory, we are thus able to answer the questions posed at the end of the previous section, and recognize that:
(i) the linear dependence of
g_{\rm m}
on
\Delta T
stems from Equation
2
and
\Delta\mu\propto\Delta T
;
(ii) we expect this trend for all AFPs and separations; and
(iii) we expect
m_{g}\approx\Delta h/T_{\rm eq}
to be a constant, and

c_{g}
to be influenced by AFP structure and separation through
the maximum interfacial curvature,
\kappa_{\rm m}
, that pinned AFPs are able to sustain.
Moreover, because
g_{\rm m}
vanishes at the critical supercooling,
\Delta T^{*}
, these insights into
m_{g}
and
c_{g}
result in the following expression for
\Delta T^{*}
:

\Delta T^{*}=\frac{c_{g}}{m_{g}}=\frac{(2\gamma/\rho)\kappa_{\rm m}}{\Delta h/T_{\rm eq}}=2\zeta\kappa_{\rm m},

(3)

where
\zeta\equiv\gamma T_{\rm eq}/\rho\Delta h
is a physicochemical property of water,
and for the water model we use,
\zeta=37.6
K-nm
Abascal et al. 2005
;
Espinosa et al. 2016
.
By plugging this value of
\zeta

and

\kappa_{\rm m}=0.22\pm 0.02
nm
-1
(Figure
2
C)
into Equation
3
,
we obtain
\Delta T^{*}=16.5\pm 1.5
, which agrees well with our estimate of
\Delta T^{*}\approx 18
K (Figure
1
C).
We note that Equation
3
has the form of a Gibbs-Thomson equation,
i.e., it relates the supercooling,
\Delta T^{*}
, at which AFP engulfment occurs,
to the maximum interfacial curvature,
\kappa_{\rm m}
, that pinned AFPs can sustain.

Given the importance of
\kappa_{\rm m}
in determining
\Delta T^{*}
, we now
turn our attention to how the molecular characteristics of the NBS
and the separation,
L
, between bound AFPs
influence their ability to sustain highly curved ice-water interfaces.
Underpinned by the assumption that the ice-water interface has a regular shape
(e.g., spherical cap) whose radius scales as
L
,
it has been suggested that the interfacial curvature,
\kappa_{\rm m}
,
ought to vary inversely with
L
, i.e.,
\kappa_{\rm m}\propto L^{-1}
.
When combined with a Gibbs-Thompson equation (e.g., Equation
3
),
this assumption suggests the widely believed scaling:
\Delta T^{*}\propto L^{-1}

Raymond and DeVries 1977
;
Knight and Wierzbicki 2001
;
Kristiansen and Zachariassen 2005
;
Nada and Furukawa 2012
;
Drori et al. 2015
;
Bianco et al. 2020
;
Kuiper et al. 2015
;
Midya and Bandyopadhyay 2018
;
Kozuch et al. 2018
;
Naullage et al. 2018
;
Gerhauser and Gaukel 2021
.
To test this assumption and interrogate whether the ice-water interfaces observed in our simulations (Figures
2
B and S9)
have regular shapes,
we plot the ratio of the Gaussian curvature field,
\mathcal{K}_{\lambda}(x,y)
, to the square of the mean curvature,
\kappa_{\lambda}^{2}
,
in Figures
2
D and S9.
If the interface were part of a sphere or a cylinder, the ratio ought to be 1 or 0, respectively, for all
(x,y)
.
In contrast, we find that the interfaces have widely varying Gaussian curvatures that take on
both positive and negative values.
These findings highlight that AFP-pinned ice-water interfaces are not associated a unique radius of curvature, but have complex shapes,
thereby calling into question the assumption that
\kappa\propto L^{-1}
,
and correspondingly, the widely believed scaling,
\Delta T^{*}\propto L^{-1}
.

How Interfacial Curvature Scales with AFP Separation

How does the maximum interfacial curvature,
\kappa_{\rm m}
, sustained by bound AFPs,
depend on their separation and on the molecular characteristics of the NBS?
To answer this question, we first recognize that the mean curvature
at any point on the ice-water interface,
(x,y)\in\mathcal{I}
,
is related to the divergence of
\hat{n}(x,y)
, the unit vector normal to the interface (Figure
3
A),
through
2\kappa_{\rm m}=\nabla\cdot\hat{n}
.
Then, by using the divergence theorem,
which equates the surface integral of
\nabla\cdot\hat{n}
to
the flux of
\hat{n}
leaving the surface,
we get:
\iint_{\mathcal{I}}\nabla\cdot\hat{n}\penalty\ dxdy=\oint_{\partial\mathcal{I}}\hat{n}\cdot\hat{m}\penalty\ ds
,
where
ds
is the differential line element along the three phase contact line,
\partial\mathcal{I}
,
and
\hat{m}
is the unit vector normal to
\partial\mathcal{I}
(Figure
3
A).
Given that the ice-water interface is a constant mean curvature surface,
i.e.,
\kappa_{\rm m}
is independent of
(x,y)
, the surface integral
simplifies to

2\kappa_{\rm m}(L_{x}L_{y}-a_{\rm m})
, where
a_{\rm m}
is the area enclosed by three phase contact line,
\partial\mathcal{I}
.
Furthermore, because
\hat{n}
and
\hat{m}
are unit vectors,
the line integral
\oint_{\partial\mathcal{I}}\hat{n}\cdot\hat{m}\penalty\ ds
is
bounded by the perimeter of contact line,
p_{\rm m}\equiv\oint_{\partial\mathcal{I}}ds
,
prompting us to define
\eta_{\rm m}\equiv\oint_{\partial\mathcal{I}}\hat{n}\cdot\hat{m}\penalty\ ds/p_{\rm m}
,
such that
0\leq|\eta_{\rm m}|\leq 1
.
We note that
\eta_{\rm m}
depends solely on the molecular characteristics of the NBS;

\hat{m}
depends on its shape,
whereas
\hat{n}
depends on both its geometry and its chemistry (through
\Delta\gamma
).
Using
\eta_{\rm m}
, the divergence theorem can then be written as:
2\kappa_{\rm m}(L_{x}L_{y}-a_{\rm m})=\eta_{\rm m}p_{\rm m}
.
From a physical standpoint, this equation represents a balance between the net upward force that the curved interface exerts on the contact line and the downward force that the ice-phobic NBS exerts on
\partial\mathcal{I}
.
Upon rearranging, we obtain:

\kappa_{\rm m}=\frac{\eta_{\rm m}p_{\rm m}}{2(L^{2}-a_{\rm m})},

(4)

where
L\equiv\sqrt{L_{x}L_{y}}
is the geometric mean of
L_{x}
and
L_{y}
.
Equation
4
highlights that
0\leq\eta_{\rm m}\leq 1
quantifies the ability
of the NBS to pin the contact line and sustain high interfacial curvatures.
Equation
4
also highlights that
\kappa_{\rm m}
is proportional to AFP size, as quantified by
p_{\rm m}
.
Importantly, Equation
4
predicts that for sufficiently large separations (i.e.,
L^{2}\gg a_{\rm m}
),

\kappa_{\rm m}
scales as
L^{-2}
.

Sander and Tkachenko 2004
;
Farag and Peters 2023
.
The best fit to Equation
4
also enables us to estimate
\eta_{\rm m}
.
Interestingly, our estimate of
\eta_{\rm m}
for sbwAFP is close to its upper bound,
i.e.,
\eta_{\rm m}\approx 1
, suggesting that the NBS of sbwAFP is particularly adept
at pinning the contact line and sustaining highly curved ice-water interfaces.
We note that in the limit of
\eta_{\rm m}\to 1
, the interface normal near the contact line
becomes perpendicular to the direction of ice growth (Figure
3
B, inset)
Kristiansen and Zachariassen 2005
.

Collectively, these findings enable us to address how AFP structure and separation influence
\Delta T^{*}
.
In particular, by combining equations
3
with
4
, we obtain:

\boxed{\Delta T^{*}=\zeta\cdot\frac{\eta_{\rm m}p_{\rm m}}{L^{2}-a_{\rm m}}}.

(5)

Equation
5
highlights that
\Delta T^{*}
increases with AFP size (as quantified by
p_{\rm m}
)
and that in contrast with the widely-held belief,

\Delta T^{*}
scales as
L^{-2}
(for
L^{2}\gg a_{\rm m}
) and not
L^{-1}

Raymond and DeVries 1977
;
Knight and Wierzbicki 2001
;
Kristiansen and Zachariassen 2005
;
Nada and Furukawa 2012
;
Drori et al. 2015
;
Bianco et al. 2020
;
Kuiper et al. 2015
;
Midya and Bandyopadhyay 2018
;
Kozuch et al. 2018
;
Naullage et al. 2018
;
Gerhauser and Gaukel 2021
.
We also find that the NBS of sbwAFP is optimal at resisting engulfment by ice.
To what extent do these interesting insights translate to other AFPs?
How effective are other ice-binding proteins, obtained from different organisms, at resisting engulfment by ice?
Finally, how sensitive is the
\eta_{\rm m}
of an AFP to mutations on its NBS?

Diverse AFPs Excel at Resisting Engulfment

To interrogate the effectiveness of different naturally-occurring AFPs at resisting engulfment by ice,
we now estimate
\eta_{\rm m}
for three more AFPs, which are expressed by a variety of organisms
and span a wide range of activities:

Tm
AFP, a hyperactive insect AFP from the
Tenebrio molitor
beetle (Figure
4
A);
opAFP, a moderately active fish AFP from ocean pout (Figure
4
B); and

Lp
AFP, a hypoactive grass AFP from
Lolium perenne
(Figure
4
C).
For each of these diverse AFPs, we estimate the maximum interfacial curvature,
\kappa_{\rm m}
,
for four different AFP separations, and fit
\kappa_{\rm m}
vs
L^{-2}
to Equation
4
(Figures
4
A-C).
Interestingly, we find that
\eta_{\rm m}>0.8
for all the AFPs, suggesting that these naturally-evolved proteins
are proficient at resisting engulfment by ice.
A description of these simulations and the corresponding analysis is included in the
SI
(Figures S11 – S13).

The ability of an AFP to bind ice is remarkably sensitive to the chemical template presented by its IBS
Kondo et al. 2012
;
Garnham et al. 2008
;
Garnham et al. 2010
;
Graether et al. 2000
.
Is the effectiveness of an AFP at resisting engulfment by ice similarly sensitive to NBS chemistry or is the shape of the NBS more important in determining
\eta_{\rm m}
?
To answer this question, we introduce mutations into the NBS of the wild-type sbwAFP.
In particular, because diverse AFPs have been observed to
feature charged residues on their NBS
Bar Dolev et al. 2016
,
we interrogate their importance by mutating all 13 charged residues (8 positive, 5 negative)
on the sbwAFP NBS into non-polar alanines (Figure
4
D).
Although these mutations drastically change the NBS chemistry,
they do not appreciably alter the folded structure of the AFP, as shown in the
SI
(Figure S14).
Interestingly, we find that the
\eta_{\rm m}
of the mutant sbwAFP (
\eta_{\rm m}=0.8\pm 0.04
)
is only somewhat lower than that of the wild-type (
\eta_{\rm m}=0.95\pm 0.05
),
such that the mutant with its non-polar NBS remains proficient at pinning the ice-water interface (Figure
4
D).
Thus, although the charged residues on the sbwAFP NBS may have functional relevance (e.g., to enhance solubility), our findings suggest that they are not critical to resisting AFP engulfment by ice.
A detailed description of these calculations is included in the
SI
(Figure S15).

The relative insensitivity of
\eta_{\rm m}
to mutations on the sbwAFP NBS is consistent with
recent work by Marks et al.
Marks et al. 2023
, who showed that
both non-polar surfaces and polar surfaces, which are not lattice-matched with ice, can be ice-phobic.
Our finding that mutations on the NBS do not drastically alter
\eta_{\rm m}
, and thereby
\Delta T^{*}
,
also lends support to the practice of uncovering the AFP IBS
by identifying mutants that significantly suppress
\Delta T^{*}

Kondo et al. 2012
;
Garnham et al. 2008
;
Garnham et al. 2010
;
Graether et al. 2000
.
Finally, we note that in introducing numerous charge-to-alanine mutations, we dramatically alter the chemistry of the sbwAFP NBS, but leave its shape largely unchanged.
Our findings thus suggest that the shape of the NBS may be more important in conferring engulfment resistance to the AFP than the presence of charged residues.

Conclusions and Outlook

To inhibit the growth of ice crystals, antifreeze proteins (AFPs) must bind to ice,
then resist engulfment by ice
Raymond and DeVries 1977
.
The highest supercooling,
\Delta T^{*}
, for which AFPs are able to resist engulfment,
thus determines the range of temperatures over which they are active
Drori et al. 2014
.
To understand how
\Delta T^{*}
depends on the separation,
L
,
between bound AFPs and on their molecular characteristics,
here we characterize the free energetics of AFP engulfment using specialized molecular simulations.
We find that at low supercooling,
\Delta T
, engulfment is impeded by large free energy barriers,
which decrease as
\Delta T
increases, and vanish above a critical supercooling,
\Delta T^{*}
,
resulting in spontaneous engulfment.
By using interfacial thermodynamics, we then illustrate how
engulfment barriers stem from a competition between
the driving force for ice growth and
the aversion of the AFP non-binding side (NBS) for ice,
which results in the ice-water interface being pinned to the NBS
and developing a (constant) mean curvature.
We further show that the critical supercooling,
\Delta T^{*}
, is proportional to
the highest interfacial curvature,
\kappa_{\rm m}
, that bound AFPs can sustain,
and that the ice-water interface adopts complex shapes
with regions of both positive and negative Gaussian curvatures.
By using principles of differential geometry,
we then relate
\kappa_{\rm m}
to the separation,
L
, between bound AFPs,
and show that:
\Delta T^{*}\approx\zeta\eta_{\rm m}p_{\rm m}L^{-2}
,
where

\zeta
is a physico-chemical property of water,

p_{\rm m}
is the girth of the AFP, and

0\leq\eta_{\rm m}\leq 1
quantifies the effectiveness of the NBS at resisting engulfment by ice.

Interestingly, we find that
\eta_{\rm m}
is close to its optimal value, i.e.,
\eta_{\rm m}\approx 1
,
for naturally-occurring AFPs obtained from a wide variety of organisms.
We also find that to be proficient at sustaining highly curved ice-water interfaces
and resisting engulfment by ice,
an AFP must be able to pin the interface to be nearly perpendicular to the far-field interface
Kristiansen and Zachariassen 2005
.
By mutating numerous charged residues to non-polar alanines,
we further find that
\eta_{\rm m}
is relatively insensitive to
the presence of charged residues on the sbwAFP NBS.
This finding suggests that the shape of the NBS
may be more important than its chemistry in determining
\eta_{\rm m}
.
We also find that
\Delta T^{*}
is proportional to NBS size,
p_{\rm m}
, suggesting that protein engineering approaches,
which do not alter the IBS, but enhance the size of the NBS,
can nevertheless increase
\Delta T^{*}
by providing enhanced resistance to engulfment.
Indeed, AFPs conjugated with bulky green-fluorescence protein tags have been observed to have higher thermal hysteresis (TH) activities than the corresponding non-tagged variants
DeLuca et al. 1998
;
Pertaya et al. 2007
;
Drori et al. 2014
.

In contrast with the widely-held belief that
\Delta T^{*}\sim L^{-1}
,
here we show that
\Delta T^{*}\sim L^{-2}
, i.e.,
\Delta T^{*}
is a more sensitive function of
L
.
By combining non-equilibrium fluorescence experiments with a reaction-diffusion model,
Thosar et al.
Thosar et al. 2023
recently showed that the accumulation of AFPs at the ice-water interface
Drori et al. 2014
;
Kamat et al. 2021
,
and thereby the average separation,
L
, between bound AFPs,
is not limited by the diffusion of AFPs,
but by the slow kinetics of AFP binding to ice
Marshall et al. 2004
;
Meister et al. 2018
.
Collectively, these findings suggest that the TH activity of an AFP ought to be
sensitive to small changes in its IBS that influence its binding to ice and thereby
L
.
Indeed, by using protein engineering techniques to enlarge the IBS of
Gr
AFP,
Davies and co-workers were able to substantially increase its TH activity
Scholl and Davies 2023
;
conversely, random mutations on the IBS often lead to a complete loss of TH activity
Kondo et al. 2012
;
Graether et al. 2000
.

Our findings also suggest that organizing bound AFPs into ordered arrays may result in greater TH activities.
In particular, because
\Delta T^{*}\sim L^{-2}
, the first engulfment events must occur in regions with the highest separations;
such engulfment events further increase separation and are thus self-propagating
Kristiansen and Zachariassen 2005
;
Hansen-Goos et al. 2014
;
Farag and Peters 2023
.
Consequently, TH activity is not dictated by the average AFP separation,
L_{\rm avg}
,
but by the highest AFP separation,
L_{\rm max}
,
i.e., TH
\sim L_{\rm max}^{-2}\sim\alpha^{-2}L_{\rm avg}^{-2}\sim\alpha^{-2}\theta
,
where
\alpha\equiv L_{\rm max}/L_{\rm avg}
and
\theta
is the surface concentration of bound AFPs.
Thus, for a given
\theta
, the more heterogeneous the distribution of AFP separations (higher
\alpha
),
the lower the TH activity (by a factor of
\alpha^{2}
).
Indeed, the
\Delta T^{*}
-values of hyperactive AFPs obtained using simulations,
which feature a periodic array of bound AFPs (i.e.,
\alpha\to 1
),
tend to be substantially larger than the corresponding
experimentally-measured TH activities.
For example, we find that
\Delta T^{*}=7.9
K for
Tm
AFP molecules separated by
L=7.7
nm,
whereas Drori et al. report a TH activity of
0.52
K for
Tm
AFP molecules with an average AFP separation of
7.9
nm
Drori et al. 2015
, suggesting
\alpha\approx 3.8
.
Uncovering novel experimental protocols and/or AFP designs that organize bound AFPs (and lower
\alpha
) may thus offer a promising route to substantially greater TH activities.

Although the supercooling,
\Delta T^{*}
, at which an AFP is engulfed by ice
is most closely related to the TH activity of hyperactive AFPs, such as sbwAFP or
Tm
AFP (which bind all ice planes),
it also informs the TH activity of moderately active AFPs, such as opAFP (which do not bind the basal plane)
Celik et al. 2013
;
Drori et al. 2014
.
In particular, moderately active AFPs must not only bind to the prism (and/or pyramidal) planes,
and arrest their growth, but also progressively shrink the exposed basal plane
Knight and DeVries 2009
;
thus, for moderately active AFPs, resisting engulfment is necessary, but not sufficient to arrest ice growth.
Interestingly, most AFPs are also potent ice recrystallization inhibitors
and resisting engulfment is also a prerequisite for recrystallization inhibition
Budke et al. 2014
;
Bachtiger et al. 2021
;
Tas et al. 2023
.
Thus, resisting engulfment by ice plays a central role in diverse AFP functions;
by elucidating how
\Delta T^{*}
depends on the separation between bound AFPs
and on their molecular characteristics (Equation
5
),
we hope that our findings will help uncover the corresponding structure-function relationships.
We also hope that the conceptual and methodological framework introduced here will find use in understanding crystal growth inhibition in other systems, such as gas hydrates
Storr et al. 2004
,
and in designing AFP-like inhibitors for a wide variety of crystals
Rimer et al. 2010
;
Shtukenberg et al. 2017
.
