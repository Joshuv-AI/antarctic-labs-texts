q ​ POTS q\texttt{POTS} : Efficient Batch Multiobjective Bayesian Optimization via Pareto Optimal Thompson Sampling Accepted to the 28th International Conference on Artificial Intelligence and Statistics (AISTATS) 2025
Ashwin Renganathan, Kade Carlson

Abstract
Classical evolutionary approaches for multiobjective optimization are quite accurate but incur a lot of queries to the objectives; this can be prohibitive when objectives are expensive oracles. A sample-efficient approach to solving multiobjective optimization is via Gaussian process (GP) surrogates and Bayesian optimization (BO). Multiobjective Bayesian optimization (MOBO) involves the construction of an acquisition function which is optimized to acquire new observation candidates sequentially. This “inner” optimization can be hard due to various reasons: acquisition functions being nonconvex, nondifferentiable and/or unavailable in analytical form; batch sampling usually exacerbates these problems and the success of MOBO heavily relies on this inner optimization. This, ultimately, affects their sample efficiency. To overcome these challenges, we propose a Thompson sampling (TS) based approach ( q ​ POTS q\texttt{POTS} ). Whereas TS chooses candidates according to the probability that they are optimal, q ​ POTS q\texttt{POTS} chooses candidates according to the probability that they are Pareto optimal. Instead of a hard acquisition function optimization, q ​ POTS q\texttt{POTS}~ solves a cheap multiobjective optimization on the GP posteriors with evolutionary approaches. This way we get the best of both worlds: accuracy of evolutionary approaches and sample-efficiency of MOBO. New candidates are chosen on the posterior GP Pareto frontier according to a maximin distance criterion. q ​ POTS q\texttt{POTS}~ is endowed with theoretical guarantees, a natural exploration-exploitation trade-off, and superior empirical performance.



1
Introduction

Mathematically, we consider the simultaneous constrained optimization of
K
objectives

\begin{split}\max_{\mathbf{x}\in\boldsymbol{\mathcal{X}}}~&\{f_{1}(\mathbf{x}),\ldots,f_{K}(\mathbf{x})\}\\
\textnormal{s.t.}~&c_{i}(\mathbf{x})=0,~i\in\mathcal{E}\\
~&c_{i}(\mathbf{x})\geq 0,~i\in\mathcal{I}\end{split}

(1)

where
\mathbf{x}\in\boldsymbol{\mathcal{X}}\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}
is the design variable,
f_{k}:\boldsymbol{\mathcal{X}}\rightarrow\mathbb{R},~\forall k=1,\ldots,K
, are expensive zeroth-order oracles (i.e., no derivative information).
c_{i}:\boldsymbol{\mathcal{X}}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
are nonlinear constraints, also expensive zeroth-order oracles, where
\mathcal{E}
and
\mathcal{I}
are the sets of indices of equality and inequality constraints, respectively.
\boldsymbol{\mathcal{X}}
is the domain of
f
and
c
. We are interested in identifying a
Pareto
optimal set of solutions, which “Pareto dominates” all other points. Let
\boldsymbol{f(\mathbf{x})}=[f_{1}(\mathbf{x}),\ldots,f_{K}(\mathbf{x})]^{\top}
; when a solution
\boldsymbol{f(\mathbf{x})}
Pareto dominates another solution
\boldsymbol{f(\mathbf{x}^{\prime})}
, then
f_{k}(\mathbf{x})\geq f_{k}(\mathbf{x}^{\prime}),~\forall k=1,\ldots,K
and
\exists k\in[K]
such that
f_{k}(\mathbf{x})>f_{k}(\mathbf{x}^{\prime})
. We write Pareto dominance as
\boldsymbol{f(\mathbf{x})}\succ\boldsymbol{f(\mathbf{x}^{\prime})}
. The set
\mathcal{Y}^{*}=\{\boldsymbol{f(\mathbf{x})}~:~\nexists\mathbf{x}^{\prime}\in\boldsymbol{\mathcal{X}}:\boldsymbol{f(\mathbf{x}^{\prime})\succ\boldsymbol{f(\mathbf{x})}}\}
is called the
Pareto frontier
and the set
\boldsymbol{\mathcal{X}}^{*}=\{\mathbf{x}\in\boldsymbol{\mathcal{X}}~:~\boldsymbol{f(\mathbf{x})}\in\mathcal{Y}^{*}\}
is called the
Pareto set
.

We are interested in sample-efficient (that is, minimal queries to the oracles) approaches to solving our problem. Evolutionary algorithms (EA) such as the nondominated sorting genetic algorithm (NSGA)
Deb et al. 2000
;
Deb et al. 2002
are a popular choice for solving multiobjective problems (see
Zitzler et al. 2000
for a review). However, EA based approaches are known to be not sample efficient, which could be prohibitive in real-world applications with expensive oracles. A common approach to solving this problem under such circumstances is to use Bayesian optimization (BO) with Gaussian process (GP) surrogate models for each objective. The idea is to fit a GP model for the objectives using some initial observations
\boldsymbol{\mathcal{D}}^{i}_{n}=\{(\mathbf{x}_{j},y_{j}),~j=1,\ldots,n\}
(where we denote
y_{j}=f_{i}(\mathbf{x}_{j})
) and construct an appropriate
acquisition
function that quantifies the utility of a candidate point
\mathbf{x}
. Then, an inner optimization problem is solved to choose new point(s) that maximize the acquisition function. The surrogate models are updated with observations at the new points, and this process is repeated until a suitable convergence criterion is met. We provide a formal introduction in
Section

2.1
. BO is very popular in the single objective (
K=1
) case; for instance, the expected improvement (EI)
Jones et al. 1998
and probability of improvement (PI)
Mockus et al. 1978
;
Jones 2001
quantify the probabilistic improvement offered by a candidate point over the “best” observed point so far. The GP upper confidence bound (UCB)
Srinivas et al. 2009
makes a conservative estimate of the maximum using the current GP estimate of the function. Other acquisition functions, for
K=1
, include entropy-based
Wang and Jegelka 2017
;
Hernández-Lobato et al. 2014
, knowledge gradient (KG)
Frazier et al. 2008
;
Wu and Frazier 2016
;
Wu and Frazier 2019
, and stepwise uncertainty reduction (SUR)
Picheny 2014
;
Chevalier et al. 2014
. Multiobjective Bayesian optimization (MOBO) extends BO to the multiobjective setting; we review some past work in what follows.

1.1
Related work

Naturally, one is tempted to extend the ideas from single objective BO to the multiobjective setting.

One of the most common approaches in MOBO are the ones based on scalarizations; that is the
K
objectives are scalarized into one enabling the use of single objective optimization methods. For instance, the multiobjective efficient global optimization (ParEGO)
Knowles 2006

scalarizes the
K
objectives using randomly drawn weights, which is then treated like a single-objective using EI. Multiobjective evolutionary algorithm based on decomposition (MOEA/D-EGO)
Zhang et al. 2009
extends ParEGO to the batch setting using multiple random scalarizations and a genetic algorithm
Zhou et al. 2012
to optimize these scalarizations in parallel. Other extensions of ParEGO to the batch setting (qParEGO) and its noisy variant, qNParEGO, have also been proposed
Daulton et al. 2021
.

Paria et al. 2020
do a similar “Chebyshev” scalarization on the objectives but use Thompson sampling (TS) to optimize the scalarized objective.
Zhang and Golovin 2020
propose to use a hypervolume scalarization with the property that the expected value of the scalarization over a specific distribution of weights is equivalent to the hypervolume indicator. The authors propose a upper confidence bound algorithm using randomly sampled weights, but provide a very limited empirical evaluation. Scalarizations have the drawback that they are not sample efficient and often fail to capture disconnected and non-convex/concave Pareto frontiers.

Outside of scalarizations, the most common approach in MOBO uses the hypervolume indicator to construct an acquisition function that quantifies the improvement in hypervolume. This usually results in a nonconvex optimization problem that is not straightforward to solve.
The EI acquisition function can be extended to the multiobjective setting via the expected hypervolume improvement (EHVI)
Couckuyt et al. 2014
;
Emmerich 2008
; gradient based extensions of the EHVI also exist
Yang et al. 2019b
;
Daulton et al. 2020a
. Similarly, the SUR criterion extended to multiobjective problems
Picheny 2015
is another example.
Recent efforts
Daulton et al. 2020b
;
Daulton et al. 2021
extend the EHVI idea to sample a batch of acquisitions
(q>1)
for parallel evaluation and with gradient computation for efficient optimization. However, the stochasticity of the acquisition functions and the associated difficulty in their optimization, particularly as
q
increases, remains.

Recognizing the non-convexity of HVI based acquisition functions, several methods emerged which make ad hoc changes that better balance exploration and exploitation.
Diversity-Guided Efficient Multiobjective Optimization (DGEMO)
Konakovic Lukovic et al. 2020
is a recent method for batch MOBO that improves diversity of the chosen points by solving a local optimization problem to maximize hypervolume improvement. Although DGEMO scales well to large batch sizes, it does not account for noisy observations and its ability to account for constraints is unknown.
Pareto active learning (PAL)
Zuluaga et al. 2013
seeks to classify each candidate point in terms of their probability of being Pareto optimal or not, depending upon the GP posterior uncertainty. This approach presents another difficult acquisition function optimization that is handled via a discretized
\boldsymbol{\mathcal{X}}
in
Zuluaga et al. 2013
.

Instead of computing hypervolume improvement, the entropy-based approaches compute the uncertainty reduction in the hypervolume due to new candidates. At the time of writing this manuscript, these approaches are considered state of the art, and primarily include predictive entropy search (PESMO)
Hernández-Lobato et al.
and max-value entropy search (MESMO)
Belakaria et al. 2019
. MESMO improves upon PESMO in terms of scalability and computational cost however, the acquisition function is still not available in closed form and requires sample approximations. Pareto frontier entropy search (PFES)
Suzuki et al. 2020
propose a closed-form entropy criterion for MOBO, but they are restricted to stationary GP kernels, via random Fourier features (RFFs). Additionally, joint entropy search multiobjective optimization (JESMO)
Hvarfner et al. 2022
;
Tu et al. 2022
which considers the joint information gain for
the optimal set of inputs and outputs, still depends on approximations to the acquisition function.

The use of Thompson sampling (TS) in MOBO is relatively underexplored to the best of our knowledge. Existing methods still depend on the classical approaches—scalarization and hypervolume improvement—and, thus, inherit their limitations. For instance, the
Thompson sampling efficient multiobjective
optimization (TSEMO)
Bradford et al. 2018
considers the Pareto set identified from posterior GP samples, which are then evaluated for maximum hypervolume improvement. The Thompson sampling with Tcheybchev scalarizations (TS-TCH), likewise, depends on scalarizing objective functions. Multiobjective regionalized Bayesian optimization (MORBO)
Daulton et al. 2022
combines a trust-region strategy with MOBO to achieve scalability in higher dimensions, where they use TS to identify local candidates; however they still use the hypervolume improvement to identify the candidates and their ability to handle constraints is unknown.

Existing limitations and our contributions.

In summary, all of the existing approaches suffer from one or more of the following limitations. (i) Leading to a difficult inner optimization problem that can rarely be solved efficiently and accurately with off-the-shelf optimizers, which negatively impacts their sample efficiency (ii) inability to handle constraints, (iii) inability or difficulty handling batch acquisitions, and (iv) inability to handle noisy objectives .

Our contributions are focused on the following key parameters (i) sample efficiency in both the sequential and batch setting, (ii) ability to handle constraints, (iii) ability to handle noisy objectives, and (iv) ease of implementation. We show that our proposed method addresses all of these parameters while convincingly outperforming the state of the art.
Our central idea is inspired from TS in single-objective optimization; whereas TS selects candidate points according to the probability that they are optimal, our approach selects points according to the probability that they are Pareto optimal. Therefore, we call our method Pareto optimal Thompson sampling or
q\texttt{POTS}
, where the
q
stands for “batch” acquisition.
q\texttt{POTS}
involves solving a
cheap multiobjective optimization problem on the GP posterior sample paths. Our acquisitions are sampled from the computed Pareto sets of the continuous GP posterior sample paths such that they maximize the minimum distance to previously observed points—thereby enabling diversity of candidates and well balancing exploration with exploitation. A very useful consequence of our approach is that selecting a batch of
q
acquisitions costs the same as the sequential acquisitions. Overall, simplicity of our methodology is our biggest strength.

Methodology

2.1
Single objective Bayesian optimization

We place a GP prior on the oracle
f(\mathbf{x})\sim\mathcal{GP}(0,k(\mathbf{x},\cdot))
, where
k(\cdot,\cdot):\boldsymbol{\mathcal{X}}\times\boldsymbol{\mathcal{X}}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}_{+}
is a covariance function (a.k.a.,
kernel
).
We denote the observations from the oracle as
y_{i}=f(\mathbf{x}_{i})+\epsilon_{i},~i=1,\ldots,n
, where we assume that
\epsilon_{i}
is a zero-mean Gaussian with unknown variance
\tau^{2}
. We begin by fitting a posterior GP (parametrized by hyperparameters
\boldsymbol{\Omega}
) for the observations
\boldsymbol{\mathcal{D}}_{n}=\{\mathbf{x}_{i},y_{i},\tau^{2}\},~i=1,\ldots,n
, which gives the conditional aposteriori distribution
Rasmussen and Williams 2006
:

\begin{split}Y(\mathbf{x})|\boldsymbol{\mathcal{D}}_{n},\boldsymbol{\Omega}&\sim\mathcal{GP}(\mu_{n}(\mathbf{x}),{\sigma}^{2}_{n}(\mathbf{x})),\\
\mu_{n}(\mathbf{x})&=\mathbf{k}_{n}^{\top}[\mathbf{K}_{n}+\tau^{2}\mathbf{I}]^{-1}\mathbf{y}_{n}\\
\sigma^{2}_{n}(\mathbf{x})&=k(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{x})-\mathbf{k}_{n}^{\top}[\mathbf{K}_{n}+\tau^{2}\mathbf{I}]^{-1}\mathbf{k}_{n},\end{split}

(2)

where
\mathbf{k}_{n}\equiv k(\mathbf{x},X_{n})
is a vector of covariances between
\mathbf{x}
and all observed points in
\boldsymbol{\mathcal{D}}_{n}
,
\mathbf{K}_{n}\equiv k(X_{n},X_{n})
is a sample covariance matrix of observed points in
\boldsymbol{\mathcal{D}}_{n}
,
\mathbf{I}
is the identity matrix, and
\mathbf{y}_{n}
is the vector of all observations in
\boldsymbol{\mathcal{D}}_{n}
. We also denote by
X_{n}=[\mathbf{x}_{1},\ldots,\mathbf{x}_{n}]^{\top}\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times d}
the observation sites. BO seeks to make sequential decisions based on a probabilistic utility function constructed out of the posterior GP
u(\mathbf{x})=u(Y(\mathbf{x})|\boldsymbol{\mathcal{D}}_{n})
.
Sequential decisions are the result of an “inner” optimization subproblem that optimizes an acquisition function, typically, of the form
\alpha(\mathbf{x})=\mathbb{E}_{Y|\boldsymbol{\mathcal{D}}_{n}}[u(Y(\mathbf{x}))]
.

An approach that circumvents the acquisition function construction is Thompson sampling (TS)
Thompson 1933
.
TS proposes making a decision according to the probability that it is optimal in some sense
Russo and Van Roy 2014
. In (single objective) optimization, points are chosen according to the distribution
p_{\mathbf{x}^{*}}(\mathbf{x})
, where
\mathbf{x}^{*}
is the optimizer of the objective function.
In the context of BO, this can be expressed as

\begin{split}p_{\mathbf{x}^{*}}(\mathbf{x})=&\int p_{\mathbf{x}^{*}}(\mathbf{x}|Y)p(Y|\boldsymbol{\mathcal{D}}_{n})dY\\
=&\int\delta(\mathbf{x}-arg\max_{\mathbf{x}\in\boldsymbol{\mathcal{X}}}Y(\mathbf{x}))p(Y|\boldsymbol{\mathcal{D}}_{n})dY,\end{split}

(3)

where
\delta
is the Dirac delta function. Sampling from
p_{\mathbf{x}^{*}}(\mathbf{x})
is equivalent to finding the maximizer of a sample path
Y(\cdot,\omega)
, where
\omega
is the random parameter; therefore, in TS for BO, the acquisition function is simply the posterior sample path
\alpha\equiv Y
. Our proposed approach builds on TS which we present next.

2.2
Multiobjective Bayesian optimization

We first fit
K
independent posterior GP models:
\mathcal{M}_{1},\ldots,\mathcal{M}_{K}
for the
K
objectives, with observations
\boldsymbol{\mathcal{D}}^{1:K}_{n}\triangleq\{\boldsymbol{\mathcal{D}}_{n}^{1},\ldots,\boldsymbol{\mathcal{D}}_{n}^{K}\}
.
In the multiobjective setting, acquisition functions are typically defined in terms of a hypervolume (HV) indicator. HV of a Pareto frontier
\mathcal{Y}^{*}
is the
K-
dimensional Lebesgue measure
\lambda
of the region dominated by
\mathcal{Y}^{*}
and bounded from below by a reference point
\mathbf{r}\in\mathbb{R}^{K}
. That is,
HV(\mathcal{Y}^{*}|\mathbf{r})=\lambda_{K}(\bigcup_{\nu\in\mathcal{Y}^{*}}[\mathbf{r},\nu])
, where
[\mathbf{r},\nu]
denotes the hyper-rectangle bounded by vertices
\mathbf{r}
and
\nu
. EHVI
Couckuyt et al. 2014
;
Emmerich 2008
computes the expectation (with respect to the posterior GP) of the improvement in HV due to a candidate point. Our approach, however, does not involve the computation of HV but is a direct extension of
Equation

3
to the multiobjective setting.

2.3
q
POTS:
Batch Pareto optimal Thompson sampling for MOBO

Inspired from TS,
q\texttt{POTS}
chooses points according to the probability that they are Pareto optimal. That is, we choose
\mathbf{x}
according to the probability

\begin{split}p_{\boldsymbol{\mathcal{X}}^{*}}(\mathbf{x})=\int&\delta\left(\mathbf{x}-arg\max_{\mathbf{x}\in\boldsymbol{\mathcal{X}}}~\{Y_{1}(\mathbf{x}),\ldots,Y_{K}(\mathbf{x})\}\right)\\
&p(Y_{1}|\boldsymbol{\mathcal{D}}^{1}_{n})\ldots p(Y_{K}|\boldsymbol{\mathcal{D}}^{K}_{n})dY_{1}\ldots dY_{K},\end{split}

(4)

where the integral is over the support of the joint distribution of all posterior GPs. In practice, this is equivalent to drawing sample paths from the
K
posterior GPs
Y_{i}(\cdot,\omega),~i=1,\ldots,K
, and choosing the next point(s) from its Pareto set
\mathbf{x}_{n+1}\in\argmax_{\mathbf{x}\in\boldsymbol{\mathcal{X}}}\{Y_{1}(\mathbf{x},\omega),\ldots,Y_{K}(\mathbf{x},\omega)\}
. We use the “reparametrization trick” to sample from the posterior (again we exclude notations for individual objectives):
Y(\cdot,\omega)=\mu_{n}(\cdot)+{\mathbf{\Sigma}_{n}}^{1/2}(\cdot)\times Z(\omega)
, where
Z
is a standard multivariate normal random variable. Then, we solve the following cheap multiobjective optimization problem

X^{*}=\argmax_{\mathbf{x}\in\boldsymbol{\mathcal{X}}}\{Y_{1}(\mathbf{x},\omega),\ldots,Y_{K}(\mathbf{x},\omega)\},

(5)

that can be solved using any method; we choose evolutionary approaches, e.g., the NSGA-II
Deb et al. 2002
, so that we keep the best of both worlds: accuracy of evolutionary approaches and sample efficiency of MOBO.
The next batch of
q
points is then chosen as
\mathbf{x}_{n+1:q}\in X^{*}.

That is, we select points from the predicted Pareto set of the sample paths of each posterior GP.
In principle, any acquisition chosen from
X^{*}
is Pareto optimal per the drawn sample paths. However, to promote diversity of candidates, we ensure that the acquisition
\mathbf{x}_{n+1}
has the largest
maximin distance

Johnson et al. 1990
;
Sun et al. 2019
to the previously observed points. This way, there is an automatic balance between exploration and exploitation–see
Figure

5
for an illustration.
Let
\gamma(\cdot,\cdot):\mathbb{R}^{d}\times\mathbb{R}^{d}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}_{+}
denote the Euclidean distance between two points in
\boldsymbol{\mathcal{X}}
. Then, we pick a batch of
q
new acquisitions according to the following sequential maximin optimization problems

\begin{split}\mathbf{x}_{n+1}=&\argmax_{{\mathbf{x}}^{*}\in X^{*}}\min_{\mathbf{x}_{i}\in X_{n}}\gamma({\mathbf{x}}^{*},\mathbf{x}_{i})\\
\mathbf{x}_{n+2}=&\argmax_{{\mathbf{x}}^{*}\in X^{*}}\min_{\mathbf{x}_{i}\in X_{n}\bigcup\mathbf{x}_{n+1}}\gamma({\mathbf{x}}^{*},\mathbf{x}_{i})\\
\vdots&\\
\mathbf{x}_{n+q}=&\argmax_{{\mathbf{x}}^{*}\in X^{*}}\min_{\mathbf{x}_{i}\in X_{n}\bigcup\{\mathbf{x}_{n+1},\ldots,\mathbf{x}_{n+q-1}\}}\gamma({\mathbf{x}}^{*},\mathbf{x}_{i}).\end{split}

(6)

Equation

6
is a seemingly hard optimization problem, but can be solved quite easily and efficiently. This is because, in practice, the solution of (
5
) returned by an evolutionary algorithm based optimizer is a point set with finite cardinality. Therefore, what we end up with is the computation of an
N^{*}\times n
pairwise distance matrix (
N^{*}=|X^{*}|
) and rank-ordering them to according to their column-wise minima. An ascending sort of the resulting vector allows for selecting the
q
batch acquisition as the last
q
elements of the sorted vector.

2.4
Extension to constrained problems

Our method seamlessly extends to constrained multiobjective problems and the key step is explained as follows. Let there be a total of
C
constraints
\{c_{1},\ldots,c_{C}\}
, and
\mathcal{I}\cup\mathcal{E}=[C]
. Further, let
Y_{k+i\in\mathcal{I}}
and
Y_{k+i\in\mathcal{E}}
denote the posterior GP of the
i
th inequality constraint and equality constraint functions, respectively. This way, we fit a total of
K+C
posterior GPs
Y_{1},\ldots,Y_{K},Y_{K+1},\ldots,Y_{K+C}
.
For all
i\in[C]
, we define the constrained
q\texttt{POTS}
to choose points according to

\begin{split}p_{\boldsymbol{\mathcal{X}}^{*}}(\mathbf{x})=\int\delta&\left(\mathbf{x}-\argmax_{\mathbf{x}\in\boldsymbol{\mathcal{X}}}\left[\{Y_{1}(\mathbf{x}),\ldots,Y_{K}(\mathbf{x})\}\times\right.\right.\\
&\left.\left.\mathbbm{1}_{\{\bigcap_{i}Y_{k+i\in\mathcal{E}}(\mathbf{x})=0\}}\times\mathbbm{1}_{\{\bigcap_{i}Y_{k+i\in\mathcal{I}}(\mathbf{x})\geq 0\}}\right]\right)\\
&p(Y_{1}|\boldsymbol{\mathcal{D}}_{n})\ldots p(Y_{K}|\boldsymbol{\mathcal{D}}_{n})dY_{1}\ldots dY_{K},\end{split}

(7)

where
\mathbbm{1}_{\bigcap_{i}Y_{k+i\in\mathcal{I}}(\mathbf{x})\geq 0}
and
\mathbbm{1}_{\bigcap_{i}Y_{k+i\in\mathcal{I}}(\mathbf{x})=0}
are the indicator functions for joint feasibility with inequality and equality constraints, respectively, that take a value of
1
when feasible and
0
otherwise. This
is equivalent to drawing samples from the GP posteriors, filtering them out by the indicator functions, and finding a Pareto optimal solution set per the posterior GP sample paths.

\begin{split}X^{*}=\argmax_{\mathbf{x}\in\boldsymbol{\mathcal{X}}}&\left[\{Y_{1}(\mathbf{x},\omega),\ldots,Y_{K}(\mathbf{x},\omega)\}\times\right.\\
&\left.\mathbbm{1}_{\{\bigcap_{i}Y_{k+i\in\mathcal{E}}(\mathbf{x})=0\}}\times\mathbbm{1}_{\{\bigcap_{i}Y_{k+i\in\mathcal{I}}(\mathbf{x})\geq 0\}}\right],\end{split}

(8)

Then,
q
maximin candidates are chosen similar to what is done in the unconstrained case. This way, handling constraints in
q\texttt{POTS}
fits seamlessly into the unconstrained framework with minimal changes.
An illustration of the constrained Branin-Currin test case is shown in
Figure

2
; notice that
q\texttt{POTS}
is able to choose an acquisition within the feasible region.

what if
X^{*}=
(that is, an empty set) or
|X^{*}|<q
?
. This is particularly likely when the problem has one or more equality constraints. In such situations,
q\texttt{POTS}
simply does not make any acquisition and, instead, solves

and tries again. This process is repeated until
q\geq|X^{*}|
. As long as the population size in NSGA-II is large enough, in principle, this approach should eventually result in

. However, in the authors’ experience so far, this issue has never occurred and

is always non-empty in the first attempt.

2.5
Nyström approximation on active candidate set

We illustrate how our approach can improve scalability in high dimensions with the Nyström approximation with GP posteriors.
We begin by writing the reparametrization technique for a GP posterior:

\begin{split}Y(X)=&\mu(X)+\left[k(X,X_{n})k(X_{n},X_{n})^{-1}k(X_{n},X)\right]^{1/2}Z\\
=&\mu(X)+\Sigma(X)^{1/2}Z,\end{split}

where
\Sigma(X)=k(X,X_{n})k(X_{n},X_{n})^{-1}k(X_{n},X)
is an
N\times N
posterior covariance matrix, that we also write as
\Sigma_{NN}
; the main computational bottleneck is in computing the square-root of
\Sigma(X)
which costs
\mathcal{O}(N^{3})
. We now choose a subset
X_{m}\subset X
,
m\ll N
, to write the following, augmented
(N+m)\times(N+m)
matrix

\Sigma_{(N+m)(N+m)}=\begin{bmatrix}\Sigma_{mm}&\Sigma_{mN}\\
\Sigma_{Nm}&\Sigma_{NN}\end{bmatrix},

where
\Sigma_{mm}
is the intersection of the
m
rows and
m
columns of
\Sigma_{NN}
corresponding to
X_{m}
, and
\Sigma_{mN}=\Sigma_{Nm}^{\top}
are the
m
rows of
\Sigma_{NN}
corresponding to
X_{m}
. Then, we have that

\begin{split}\Sigma(X)=&\Sigma_{NN}\approx\Sigma_{mN}\Sigma_{mm}^{-1}\Sigma_{mN}^{\top}\\
=&\left(\Sigma_{mN}\Sigma_{mm}^{-1/2}\right)\left(\Sigma_{mN}\Sigma_{mm}^{-1/2}\right)^{\top}.\end{split}

(9)

Therefore, we have the Nyström approximation of the posterior covariance matrix square root as
\Sigma(X)^{1/2}\approx\left(\Sigma_{mN}\Sigma_{mm}^{-1/2}\right).

The approximation in
Equation

9

m\times m
matrix square root which costs
\mathcal{O}(m^{3})

is much better than
\mathcal{O}(N^{3})
, provided
m\ll N
. The choice of
X_{m}
is an open research question; in this work, we choose them to be the subset of nondominated points in
\{X_{n},\mathbf{y}_{n}\}
which works uniformly well in practice.
We provide more illustration in the supplementary material.

We now provide theoretical guarantees on
q\texttt{POTS}
. We show that the absolute difference between
f_{i}
and its GP posterior mean,
\forall\mathbf{x}\in\boldsymbol{\mathcal{X}}
, is asymptotically consistent. Then, we show that the predicted Pareto frontier asymptotically converges to the true Pareto frontier. Our proofs depend on two key assumptions. First, we place regularity assumptions on
f_{i},~\forall i
. Then, we assume that our inner (multiobjective) optimization solution, via evolutionary techniques, is solved exactly. We now proceed to present the key theoretical results.

Assumption 3.1

(
Exact knowledge of
X^{*}
).

We assume that the inner multiobjective optimization problem
\argmax_{\mathbf{x}\in\boldsymbol{\mathcal{X}}}\{Y_{1}(\mathbf{x},\omega),\ldots,Y_{K}(\mathbf{x},\omega)\},
is solved exactly and the true
X^{*}
is known.

Assumption
3.1
is quite reasonable in practice since evolutionary algorithms come with tuning parameters e.g., population size and number of generations, which can be made arbitrarily high to improve accuracy of
X^{*}

and

.

Assumption 3.2

(

We assume that the sample paths drawn from the GP prior on
f

\nu=5/2

(
Ghosal and Roy 2006
, Theorem 5)
. Then, the following holds (for

\mathbb{P}r\left[|f(\mathbf{x})-f(\mathbf{x}^{\prime})|>L\|\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{x}^{\prime}\|\right]\leq e^{-L^{2}/2}.

.

Let Assumption
3.2
hold. Let
\{\mathbf{x}_{i}\},~i=1,\ldots,n
be a sequence of points selected via the

\lim_{n\rightarrow\infty}~\sup_{\mathbf{x}\in\boldsymbol{\mathcal{X}}}\sigma^{2}_{n}(\mathbf{x})=0

and Assumption

. Let
\tilde{\boldsymbol{\mathcal{X}}}

5
Conclusion

We present
q\texttt{POTS}
, a sample efficient method to solve expensive constrained multiobjective optimization problems. Classical evolutionary approaches are accurate but not sample efficient. Bayesian optimization is, in general, sample-efficient, but its success relies heavily on solving a difficult inner optimization problem. Our proposed
q\texttt{POTS}
relies on sampling from the posterior GPs, and solving a cheap multiobjective optimization on the posterior sample paths with classical evolutionary algorithms such as NSGA-II. This way, we bridge the gap between classical EA’s and MOBO and thus, in a sense, get the best of both worlds.
q\texttt{POTS}
naturally balances exploration and exploitation, can handle constraints and noisy objectives, and can efficiently handle batch acquisitions; no other existing method has all these features and still outperform
q\texttt{POTS}
to the best of our knowledge. Scaling up to higher dimensions is achieved through a Nyström approximation which significantly speeds up the method without any practical loss of accuracy. Crucially, our approach freely applies to any covariance kernel (unlike RFF for stationary kernels).

Looking into the future, one avenue of interest is marrying
q\texttt{POTS}
with nonstationary GPs, such as deep GPs
Damianou and Lawrence 2013
and input warping
Snoek et al. 2014
, which would open doors for more applications where nonstationarity in the output space is expected to be strong. Additionally, we also intend to explore the use of multitask GPs
Swersky et al. 2013
to exploit potential underlying correlations between the objectives and constraints. We anticipate that
q\texttt{POTS}
would require no structural changes to accommodate nonstationary and multitask GPs.

Lemma 6.3
.

Let Assumption 3.2 hold. Let
\{\mathbf{x}_{i}\},~i=1,\ldots,n
be a sequence of points selected via the
q\texttt{POTS}
method according to Eq. (6) with
q=1
. Then,
\lim_{n\rightarrow\infty}~\sup_{\mathbf{x}\in\boldsymbol{\mathcal{X}}}\sigma^{2}_{n}(\mathbf{x})=0
.

Proof.

First of all, note that
q\texttt{POTS}
chooses new acquisitions according to the maximin distance criterion (Eq. (6)). Therefore, unless the posterior uncertainty is
0
almost everywhere, the same acquisition is never chosen more than once.

W.l.o.g, let
k(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{x})=1
. Recall that the posterior variance of the GP is given by

\sigma_{n}^{2}(\mathbf{x})=k(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{x})-\mathbf{k}_{n}^{\top}\mathbf{K}_{n}^{-1}\mathbf{k}_{n}.

Note that as
n\rightarrow\infty
, then
\mathbf{k}_{n}\rightarrow\mathbf{K}^{:,i}_{n}
, where by
\mathbf{K}^{:,i}_{n}
we mean the
i
th column of
\mathbf{K}_{n}
,
i\in[n]
. This is because, when the
f
has been observed at infinitely many points
\{\mathbf{x}_{i}\}_{i=1}^{\infty}
and recall that
X_{n}
is the row stack of all of these points, then
\mathbf{k}_{n}=k(\mathbf{x},X_{n})
represents the covariance between a candidate point
\mathbf{x}
and all previous observation sites
X_{n}
. When
n=\infty
,
\mathbf{x}
is likely very close to one of the points
\mathbf{x}_{i}\in\{\mathbf{x}_{i}\}_{i=1}^{\infty}
and hence
k(\mathbf{x},X_{n})\rightarrow k(\mathbf{x}_{i},X_{n}),~i\in[n]
. And therefore
\mathbf{k}_{n}^{\top}\mathbf{K}_{n}^{-1}=\mathbf{e}_{i}
, where
\mathbf{e}_{i}
is an
n
-vector with
1
at the
i
th location and
0
elsewhere. Then

\lim_{n\rightarrow\infty}\sigma_{n}^{2}(\mathbf{x})=1-\mathbf{K}^{:,i~\top}_{n}\mathbf{e}_{i}=1-k(\mathbf{x}_{i},\mathbf{x}_{i})=0.

∎

Using the aforementioned assumptions and
Lemma

3.3
, we first show that the vector of posterior GPs
\mathbf{Y}=[Y_{1},\ldots,Y_{K}]^{\top}
converges to the vector of true objectives
\mathbf{f}=[f_{1},\ldots,f_{K}]^{\top}
with high probability.

Theorem 6.4

(

Let
\delta\in(0,1)
and Assumption
3.2
hold with
L^{2}=2\log\left(\frac{2}{\delta}\right)
. Further, let
g
be some affine function that operates on
f
. Let
\tilde{\boldsymbol{\mathcal{X}}}
denote a discretization of
\boldsymbol{\mathcal{X}}
with
|\tilde{\boldsymbol{\mathcal{X}}}|<\infty
, where we evaluate the oracle
f

\epsilon\geq 0
, and
a_{n}=\sqrt{2\log(b_{n}|\tilde{\boldsymbol{\mathcal{X}}}|)/\delta}

\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}1/b_{n}=1

\mathbb{P}r\left[\forall\mathbf{x}\in\boldsymbol{\mathcal{X}},~\lim_{n\rightarrow\infty}~\|\mathbf{f}(\mathbf{x})-\mathbf{Y}^{n-1}(\mathbf{x})\|_{\infty}\leq\epsilon\right]>1-\delta.

Proof of Theorem 3.4.

We first present a simple identity that bounds the probability that a Gaussian random variable is greater than some value. Given
r\sim\mathcal{N}(0,1)
and
c>0
, consider the following:

\begin{split}\mathbb{P}r(r>c)=&\int_{c}^{\infty}\frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi}}e^{-r^{2}/2}dr\\
=&e^{-c^{2}/2}\int_{c}^{\infty}\frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi}}e^{-(r-c)^{2}/2}e^{-c(r-c)}dr\\
\leq&e^{-c^{2}/2}\int_{c}^{\infty}\frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi}}e^{-(r-c)^{2}/2}dr\leq\frac{1}{2}e^{-c^{2}/2},\end{split}

(10)

where the second line is due to the fact that
0\leq e^{-c(r-c)}\leq 1
for
r\geq c
and
c>0
and the last line from the fact that the integrand is the density of
\mathcal{N}(c,1)
. Using this result and the prior assumption that the unknown function
f
has a GP prior, we state, with
c=a_{n}
and a fixed
\mathbf{x}_{i}\in\tilde{X}
,

\begin{split}\mathbb{P}r\left[|f(\mathbf{x}_{i})-\mu_{n-1}(\mathbf{x}_{i})|>a_{n}\sigma_{n-1}(\mathbf{x}_{i})\right]\leq&\frac{1}{2}e^{-a_{n}^{2}/2}=\frac{\delta}{2b_{n}|\tilde{\mathcal{X}}|}.\end{split}

(11)

Applying the union bound
\forall i
, we get

\begin{split}\mathbb{P}r\bigcup_{i=1}^{|\tilde{\mathcal{X}}|}&|f(\mathbf{x}_{i})-\mu_{n-1}(\mathbf{x}_{i})|\\
&>a_{n}\sigma_{n-1}(\mathbf{x}_{i})\\
&\leq\sum_{i=1}^{|\tilde{\mathcal{X}}|}\mathbb{P}r\left[|f(\mathbf{x}_{i})-\mu_{n-1}(\mathbf{x}_{i})|>a_{n}\sigma_{n-1}(\mathbf{x}_{i})\right]\\
&\leq\sum_{i=1}^{|\tilde{\mathcal{X}}|}\frac{\delta}{2b_{n}|\tilde{\mathcal{X}}|}=\frac{\delta}{2b_{n}}.\end{split}

(12)

Now we apply the union bound
\forall n
:

\begin{split}&\mathbb{P}r\left[\forall\tilde{\mathcal{X}}\in\tilde{\mathcal{X}},~\bigcup_{n=1}^{\infty}|f(\tilde{\mathcal{X}})-\mu_{n-1}(\tilde{\mathcal{X}})|>a_{n}\sigma_{n-1}(\tilde{\mathcal{X}})\right]\\
&\leq\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\frac{\delta}{2b_{n}}=\frac{\delta}{2}.\\
\end{split}

(13)

We would like to extend the proof to a general

. For some
\mathbf{x}\in\boldsymbol{\mathcal{X}}

\tilde{\mathcal{X}}
denote a point

(14)

Combining (
13
) and (
14

\begin{split}&\mathbb{P}r\left[\forall\mathbf{x}\in\boldsymbol{\mathcal{X}},\forall n,~|f(\mathbf{x})-\mu_{n-1}(\tilde{\mathbf{x}})|\leq\right.\\
&\left.a_{n}\sigma_{n-1}(\mathbf{x})+\sqrt{2\log\left(\frac{2}{\delta}\right)}\|\mathbf{x}-\tilde{\mathbf{x}}\|\right]>1-\delta.\end{split}

(15)

From Lemma 3.2, the
\lim_{n\rightarrow\infty}\sup_{\mathbf{x}\in\boldsymbol{\mathcal{X}}}\sigma_{n-1}(\mathbf{x})=0

\mathbb{P}r\left[\forall\mathbf{x}\in\boldsymbol{\mathcal{X}},\lim_{n\rightarrow\infty}|f(\mathbf{x})-\mu_{n-1}(\tilde{\mathbf{x}})|\leq\epsilon\right]>1-\delta,

where

\begin{split}\mathbb{P}r\left[\forall\mathbf{x}\in\boldsymbol{\mathcal{X}},\lim_{n\rightarrow\infty}|f(\mathbf{x})-Y^{n-1}(\tilde{\mathbf{x}})+\sigma_{n-1}(\mathbf{x})z|\leq\epsilon\right]&\\
>1-\delta\\
\mathbb{P}r\left[\forall\mathbf{x}\in\boldsymbol{\mathcal{X}},\lim_{n\rightarrow\infty}|f(\mathbf{x})-Y^{n-1}(\tilde{\mathbf{x}})|\leq\epsilon\right]&\\
>1-\delta,\end{split}
