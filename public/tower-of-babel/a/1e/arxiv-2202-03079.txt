Call-by-Value Solvability and Multi Types
Beniamino Accattoli, Giulio Guerrieri

Abstract
This paper provides a characterization of call-by-value solvability using call-by-value multi types. Our work is based on Accattoli and Paolini’s characterization of call-by-value solvable terms as those terminating with respect to the solving strategy of the value substitution calculus , a refinement of Plotkin’s call-by-value λ \lambda -calculus. Here we show that the solving strategy terminates on a term t t if and only if t t is typable in a certain way in the multi type system induced by Ehrhard’s call-by-value relational semantics . Moreover, we show how to extract from the type system exact bounds on the length of the solving evaluation and on the size of its normal form, adapting de Carvalho’s technique for call-by-name.



I
Introduction

Plotkin’s call-by-value
\lambda
-calculus
[
55
]
is at
the heart of
programming languages such as OCaml and proof assistants such as Coq. In the study of programming
languages, call-by-value (CbV) evaluation is usually
weak
, that is, it does not reduce under
abstractions, and terms are assumed to be
closed
,
i.e.
, without free variables.
These constraints give rise to an elegant framework—we call it
Closed CbV
, following

[
8
]
.

It often happens, however, that one needs to go beyond Closed CbV by
considering
Strong CbV
, which is the extended setting where reduction under abstractions is allowed
and terms may be open, or the intermediate framework of
Open CbV
, where evaluation is weak
but terms are not necessarily closed.
The need arises, most notably, when describing the implementation model of Coq, as done by Grégoire and Leroy

[
37
]
, to realize the essential conversion test for dependent types. Other
motivations lie in the study of bisimulations by Lassen

[
48
]
, partial evaluation
[
41
]
, or various topics
of a semantical or logical nature, recalled below.

Naïve Extension of CbV.
In call-by-name (CbN) turning to open terms or strong
evaluation is harmless because CbN does not impose any special form to the arguments of

\beta
-redexes.
On the contrary, turning to Open or Strong CbV is delicate. While some fundamental properties such
as confluence and standardization hold also in such cases, as showed by Plotkin’s himself
[
55
]
,
others—typically of a semantical nature—break as soon as one considers open terms.

The problems of Strong CbV can be traced back to Plotkin’s seminal paper,
where he points out the
incompleteness of CbV with respect to CPS translations, an issue later solved with categorical
tools by Moggi
[
51
]
. This led to a number of studies, among others

[
60
,
61
,
50
,
23
,
30
,
39
]
, that
introduced many proposals of
improved calculi for CbV.

The relationship with denotational semantics is also problematic, as first shown by Paolini and Ronchi della Rocca

[
54
,
53
,
58
]
. There are two subtle
points:

1.

Solvability
: the adaptation of the notion of solvability—roughly, a form of
meaningfulness for terms—to CbV;

2.

Adequacy
: denotational semantics that are

adequate
for Closed CbV
[
1
,
31
,
40
,
57
]
are no longer adequate for the extended settings. Roughly,
there are terms that are semantically divergent, that is, with trivial semantics (or unsolvable),
while they are normal forms with respect to Plotkin’s rules, and so are expected to have non-trivial semantics (and be solvable).

Both semantical issues of Open/Strong CbV have been addressed in the literature, relying of linear logic tools. Adequacy is studied at length by Accattoli and Guerrieri in
[
9
,
11
]
, thus we here focus on solvability.

Call-by-Value Solvability

About solvability, Accattoli and Paolini
[
12
]
characterize operationally solvable terms using a calculus isomorphic to the proof-net CbV representation of
\lambda
-calculus, the
value substitution calculus
(shortened to VSC).
Namely, they introduce a
solving evaluation strategy
(called
stratified-weak
in
[
12
]
) in the VSC that terminates if and only if the term is solvable—notably their strategy requires
strong
evaluation.
This is akin to what happens in CbN, where solvable term are those for which head evaluation terminates. A key point is that
such a characterization of CbV solvability is impossible in Plotkin’s original formulation of CbV.

We would like to mention that the literature contains also a detailed study of CbV solvability due to Garcia-Perez and Nogueira
[
34
]
based on a different approach, as they stick to Plotkin’s call-by-value
\lambda
-calculus.

Here we continue Accattoli and Paolini’s study of CbV solvability, providing two contributions:

1.

Types and solvability
: we characterize CbV solvability using
multi types
, a variant of intersection types surveyed below. Namely, we prove that a term is CbV solvable if and only if it is typable in a certain way;

2.

Bounds from types
: we show how to extract, from a certain class of typing derivations, the number of steps taken by the solving strategy on a solvable term, together with the size of the solving normal form.

Before giving more details about our results, we recall multi types and their use for extracting operational bounds.

Multi Types

Intersection types are one of the standard tools to study
\lambda
-calculi, mainly used to characterize termination properties—classical references are Coppo and Dezani
[
21
,
22
]
, Pottinger
[
56
]
, and Krivine
[
47
]
. In contrast to other type systems, they do not provide a logical interpretation, at least not as smoothly as for simple or polymorphic types—see Ronchi Della Rocca and Roversi’s
[
59
]
or Bono, Venneri, and Bettini’s
[
16
]
for details. They are better understood, in fact, as syntactic presentations of denotational semantics: they are invariant under evaluation and type all and only the terminating terms, thus naturally providing an adequate denotational model.

Intersection types are a flexible tool that can
be formulated in various ways. A flavour that emerged
in the last 10 years is that of
non-idempotent
intersection
types, where the
intersection
A\cap A
is not equivalent to
A
. They were first considered by Gardner
[
35
]
, and then Kfoury
[
46
]
, Neergaard and Mairson
[
52
]
, and de Carvalho
[
25
,
27
]
provided a first wave of works abut them—a survey can be
found in Bucciarelli, Kesner, and Ventura’s
[
19
]
. Non-idempotent intersections can be seen as multisets, which is why, to ease the
language, we prefer to call them
multi types
rather than

non-idempotent intersection types
.

Multi types retain the denotational character of intersection types, and they actually refine it along two correlated lines. First, taking types with multiplicities gives rise to a
quantitative
approach, that reflects
resource consumption in the evaluation of terms. Second, such a quantitative feature turns out to coincide exactly with the one at work in linear logic.
Some care is needed here: multi types do not correspond to linear logic formulas, rather to the relational denotational semantics of linear logic (two seminal references for such a semantic are Girard’s
[
36
]
and Bucciarelli and Ehrhard’s
[
17
]
; see also
[
26
,
38
]
)—similarly to intersection types, they provide a denotational rather than a logical interpretation.

De Carvalho’s Bounds from Multi Types

An insightful use of multi types is de Carvalho’s connection between the size of types and the size of normal forms, and between the size of type derivations and evaluation lengths for the CbN
\lambda
-calculus
[
27
]
. He shows how from a certain class of typing derivations one can extract
exact
bounds about the length of evaluations and the size of the normal form of a term, according to various notions of evaluation. In particular, he shows how to do it for
head
reduction, which in CbN is the strategy characterizing solvability, that is, for which terminating terms coincide with solvable terms.

\lambda
-calculus, including call-by-value

Open CbV and Multi Types

Of all these studies, the relevant one for this paper is Accattoli and Guerrieri’s study of Open CbV
[
9
]
, which rests on Ehrhard’s CbV relational semantics
[
32
]
reformulated as multi types. They show that the evaluation of a term
t
terminates in Open CbV if and only if
t
is typable with CbV multi types, and that in that case
t
is typable with the empty multi set, that they note
\mathbf{0}
. Moreover, they show how to extract bounds to evaluation lengths and to the size of normal forms from type derivations, and characterize those type derivations that give exact bounds.

Contribution 1: Qualitative Characterization of Solvability

Here, we study the solving strategy which is an extension of evaluation in Open CbV. Namely, it is a strong strategy that iterates Open CbV under abstractions, but not all abstractions, only under
head
abstractions. We study it via Ehrhard’s CbV multi type system, as in
[
9
]
. Since the strategy
extends
open evaluation, the terms that are terminating for the solving strategy—that is, the solvable terms—cannot be characterized simply as the typable ones.

The typical example is
\lambda x.\Omega
, which is normal for open evaluation but unsolvable (and thus diverging for the solving strategy). It is typable with
\mathbf{0}
, and only with
\mathbf{0}
. One is then tempted to characterize solvable terms as those ones typable with a type different from
\mathbf{0}
. Unfortunately, things are slightly subtler, as
\lambda y.\lambda x.\Omega
is also normal for open evaluation but unsolvable, and it is typable with
\mathbf{0}
and, for instance, also with
[{\mathsf{M}}\multimap\mathbf{0}]
. We then define a notion of
solvable
multi type, which is a multi type that is not
\mathbf{0}
and that contains types that (recursively) do not have
\mathbf{0}
on the right of
\multimap
. Our result is that a term is CbV solvable if and only if it is typable with a solvable CbV multi type.

Our notion of solvable type is not new, as in fact the idea appears in the literature in other papers using intersection types to study CbV solvability
[
54
,
42
]
. Those type systems, however, are defective, and the characterizations that they claim are not in fact proved because their systems do not verify subject reduction (for
[
54
]
subject expansion also fails), as we detail in Appendix
A
. Therefore, a first contribution of this work is to recast their idea in a setting where it fully works.

Contribution 2: Quantitative Characterization of Solvability

The second contribution of this work is that we exploit the quantitative character of multi types and extract bounds from type derivations, building over de Carvalho’s technique. First, we show that every solvable derivations provides bounds to the length of the solving evaluation and to the size of the solving normal form. Second, we characterize the solvable derivations that provide exact bounds. This last part requires to introduce two refinements of solvable types, as solvable type derivations are in general too permissive with respect to quantitative bounds.

On the one hand, we need to ensure that sub-terms evaluated by the solving strategy are typed
exactly
once (with multi types a sub-term can naturally be typed many times). Such a constraint gives rise to
unitary solvable types
. On the other hand, we need to ensure that sub-terms not touched by the solving strategy are
not
typed. This is obtained by constraining types appearing in the typing context and on the left of
\multimap
, and gives rise to the notion of
inertly solvable types
. We then prove that when a solvable type derivation is both unitary and inert—which we shall refer to as being
precisely solvable
—then it captures
exactly
the evaluation length and the size of the normal form for the solving strategy.

Such a quantitative study of CbV solvability is the first one in the literature, and the described refinements of solvable types are also new.

Reasonable Cost Models

In
[
3
]
, Accattoli
et al
. prove that the number of
\beta
steps of a strategy of the VSC, namely the
external
one, is a reasonable time cost model for CbV (where
reasonable
means polynomially related to the time cost model of Turing machines). The solving strategy studied here is a sub-strategy of the external one, and so the result transfers. Thus, we obtain that multi types provide quantitative bounds that are meaningful from a computational complexity point of view.

II
Value Substitution Calculus

Here we present the
value substitution calculus
(VSC for short) introduced by Accattoli and Paolini
[
12
]

The VSC is a
\lambda

In VSC,
\beta

\beta
-redexes, because only values

A let-expression is formulated as an

(

in
t
.
All along the

contexts
,
i.e.
terms with a hole, noted
\langle\cdot\rangle
. For now, we need
substitution contexts

L
, which are simply lists of
ES
. The grammars are:

\begin{array}[]{rrl}\textsc{Values }&v&\Coloneqq x\mid\lambda x.t\\
\textsc{Terms }&t,u,s&\Coloneqq v\mid tu\mid t[x{\shortleftarrow}u]\\
\textsc{Substitution Ctxs }&L&\Coloneqq\langle\cdot\rangle\mid L[x{\shortleftarrow}t]\end{array}

The set of free variables of term
t
is denoted by
{\sf fv}(t)
.
Plugging a term

in a context
C
is noted
C\langle t\rangle
, possibly capturing variables.
An
answer
is a term of the shape

, where
v
is a value (

an abstraction) and
L
is a substitution context.
We use

for the capture-avoiding substitution of

t
for each free occurrence of
x
in

There are two kinds of rewrite rules, both work
at a distance
, that is, up to a substitution context.

\begin{array}[]{rr@{\ }l@{\ }l}\textsc{Multiplicative rule}&L\langle\lambda x.t\rangle u&\mapsto_{\mathsf{m}}&L\langle t[x{\shortleftarrow}u]\rangle\\
\textsc{Exponential rule}&t[x{\shortleftarrow}L\langle v\rangle]&\mapsto_{{\mathsf{e}}}&L\langle t\{x{\shortleftarrow}v\}\rangle\end{array}

We shall consider three fragments of the VSC.
They all contain the terms of VSC, they differ only in the choice of evaluation contexts for the rewrite rules.

III
Call-by-Value Solvability and The Solving Strategy

In the
\lambda
-calculus, the notion of solvability identifies somehow “meaningful” terms.
This notion is well studied in the CbN
\lambda
-calculus, with an elegant theory, see Barendregt
[
14
]
.
In CbV, an elegant theory of solvability is still missing because of issues first observed by Ronchi Della Rocca and Paolini
[
54
,
58
]
(a survey is in
[
34
]
), amounting to intrinsic limitations of Plotkin’s original CbV
\lambda
-calculus
[
55
]
. Accattoli and Paolini showed that instead the VSC is a good framework for CbV solvability
[
12
]
.

Since the definition of solvability depends on the calculus and its evaluation, we give a parametric definition.

Definition III.1

(Solvability)
.

Let
X
be a calculus.
A term
t
in
X
is
X
-solvable
if there are terms
u_{1},\dots,u_{k}
and variables
x_{1},\dots,x_{h}
, with
h,k\geq 0
, such that

(\lambda x_{1}\dots\lambda x_{h}.t)u_{1}\dots u_{k}

X
-evaluates to
{\mathsf{I}}\coloneqq\lambda x.x
.

The Solving VSC

Accattoli and Paolini
[
12
]
characterize operationally VSC-solvability: a term
t
is VSC-solvable if and only if
t

\mathsf{s}
-normalizes, for a suitable definition of solving reduction
\rightarrow_{\mathsf{s}}
in between the full one
\rightarrow_{\mathsf{vsc}}
and the open one
\rightarrow_{{\mathsf{o}}}
, that is, restricting
\rightarrow_{\mathsf{vsc}}
but extending
\rightarrow_{{\mathsf{o}}}
, given below.
The
Solving
VSC

is the VSC endowed with the

\rightarrow_{\mathsf{s}}
that we now define.
It is obtained by extending the open rewriting rules under head
abstractions only, via the notion of

.

\begin{aligned} \textsc{Solving ctxs}&&S&\Coloneqq O\mid\lambda x.S\mid St\mid S[x{\shortleftarrow}t]\end{aligned}

Solving rewrite rules:

t\rightarrow_{{\mathsf{o}}a}t^{\prime}
S\langle t\rangle\rightarrow_{\mathsf{s}a}S\langle t^{\prime}\rangle

(
a\in\{\mathsf{m},{\mathsf{e}}\}
)

\begin{array}[]{cccc}\textsc{Solving reduction}:&\rightarrow_{\mathsf{s}}\,\coloneqq\,\rightarrow_{\mathsf{s}\mathsf{m}}\cup\rightarrow_{\mathsf{s}{\mathsf{e}}}\end{array}

For instance, because of the extension under head abstractions,

\lambda x.({\mathsf{I}}{\mathsf{I}})\rightarrow_{\mathsf{s}\mathsf{m}}\lambda x.(z[z{\shortleftarrow}{\mathsf{I}}])\rightarrow_{\mathsf{s}{\mathsf{e}}}\lambda x.{\mathsf{I}}

But reduction under non-head abstractions is forbidden:

y(\lambda x.({\mathsf{I}}{\mathsf{I}}))\not\rightarrow_{\mathsf{s}\mathsf{m}}y(\lambda x.(z[z{\shortleftarrow}{\mathsf{I}}]))

The solving strategy captures the fact that
t\coloneqq\lambda x.\Omega

The qualitative characterization of solvable terms with multi types is fairly simple: they are those terms typable with a

Precisely Solvable Multi Types

The quantitative properties of solvable terms rest on two orthogonal predicates (see
Figure

The

exactly
once. Solvable types guarantee that each such step is counted, but it might be counted more than once. The constraint amounts to
asking that the topmost and right-hand multisets are singletons. This is the key requirement for obtaining that in the statement of subject reduction the general size of the derivation decreases by exactly one at each multiplicative step.

The
inert
predicate instead ensures that the type derivation does not type sub-terms that are not accessible to the solving strategy. Typing such sub-terms is harmless for a qualitative study, but it is problematic for a quantitative one, as it does introduces a mismatch between the size of solving normal forms and the size of their type derivations. The constraint amounts to asking that the left-hand multisets are inert.

Solvable types that are both unitary and inert are called
precise
, and provide exact bounds.

Solvable correctness claims that all terms typable with a solvable type

are

-normalizing, and the multiplicative size of the derivation bounds the number of
\rightarrow_{\mathsf{s}\mathsf{m}}
