Reinforcing a Philosophy: Littlewood–Paley theory for the moment curve over generic local fields
Kevin Hughes

Abstract
. Using the Girard–Newton formulae, I give a simple proof of isotropic square function estimates for extension operators along the moment curve in generic local fields. Using Bezout’s Theorem and the Implicit Function Theorem, I give an alternate, sharper proof for real or complex non-degenerate, polynomial curves.



1.
Introduction

The purpose of this paper is to expose two simple arguments for square function estimates of the moment curve by utilizing the number theory paradigm:

\texttt{\color[rgb]{0.75,0,0.25} Special subvarieties dictate the analysis}.

This paradigm will be investigated in the context of proving a square function estimate for the moment curve over generic local fields.
My hope is to provide some context to recent works in harmonic analysis and relate them to common, elementary ideas in analytic number theory. Stating the results requires a brief set-up taken from
[
1
]
.

In this paper, let
K
denote a (one-dimensional) local field; its non-trivial topology is determined by the metric associated to a fixed absolute value
|\cdot|_{K}:K\to\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}
.
On
\mathbb{R}
, this will be the usual absolute value. On
\mathbb{C}
, we choose our absolute value so that
|x+iy|_{\mathbb{C}}=\max\{|x|,|y|\}
where the latter is the usual absolute value on
\mathbb{R}
.
In the results below,
K
will be fixed; so, I usually suppress the dependence on
K
in future notations. For
n\in\mathbb{N}
, extend the metric to
K^{n}
by defining
|{\boldsymbol{x}}|=\max\{|x_{1}|,\dots,|x_{n}|\}
for
{\boldsymbol{x}}=(x_{1},\dots,x_{n})\in K^{n}
.
Always assume that the dimension
n
is at least two.

When
K
is a non-Archimedean local field, let
\mathfrak{o}:=\{x:|x|\leq 1\}
denote its ring of integers and
\mathfrak{p}:=\{x:|x|<1\}
denote its maximal ideal.
The image of
|\cdot|_{K}
is isomorphic to
\mathbb{Z}
as an abelian group and
K
comes with a uniformizing element, say
\pi
, generating this group.
When
K=\mathbb{R}
, let
\mathfrak{o}=[0,1]
, and when
K=\mathbb{C}
, let
\mathfrak{o}:=\{x+y\sqrt{-1}:x,y\in[0,1]\}
.

For a non-Archimedean local field
K
, define its scales as
\mathcal{R}(K):=\{|\pi|^{s}:s\in\mathbb{Z}_{\geq 0}\}
. For a scale
\delta\in\mathcal{R}(K)
, let
\mathcal{P}_{\delta}(K)
denote the partition of
\mathfrak{o}
into closed (and also open) balls of radius
\delta
; that is,
\mathcal{P}_{\delta}:=\{(i+\mathfrak{p}^{s})_{i\in\mathfrak{o}/\mathfrak{p}^{s}}\}
for some
s\in\mathbb{N}
. Over
\mathbb{R}
, define its scales as
\mathcal{R}(\mathbb{R}):=\{R^{-1}:R\in\mathbb{N}\}
, and for each
\delta
in
\mathcal{R}(\mathbb{R})
, define the partition

\mathcal{P}_{\delta}(\mathbb{R}):=\{[j\delta,(j+1)\delta):i=0,\dots,\delta^{-1}-1\}.

Over
\mathbb{C}
, define its scales as
\mathcal{R}(\mathbb{C}):=\{R^{-1}:R\in\mathbb{N}\}
, and for each
\delta
in
\mathcal{R}(\mathbb{C})
, define the partition

\mathcal{P}_{\delta}(\mathbb{C}):=\{[j\delta,(j+1)\delta)+i[k\delta,(k+1)\delta):j,k=0,\dots,\delta^{-1}-1\}.

For non-Archimedean fields, we fix a non-trivial additive character
e({\cdot})
such that
e({\mathfrak{o}})=1
and
e({1/\pi})\neq 1
.
On
\mathbb{R}
,
e({t}):=e^{-2\pi it}
denotes the usual character, and on
\mathbb{C}
,
e({z}):=e^{-2\pi i{\Tr_{\mathbb{C}/\mathbb{R}}(z)}/2}=e^{-2\pi i{x}}
for
z=x+iy\in\mathbb{C}
with
x,y\in\mathbb{R}
and
i:=\sqrt{-1}
. (This is the usual character when interpreting
\mathbb{C}
as
\mathbb{R}^{2}
in the usual way. And over
\mathbb{R}
and
\mathbb{C}
,
\pi
denotes the standard mathematical constant.)
On each local field, the Haar measure
{\,{\rm d}}{\xi}
on
K
is normalized so that the measure of
\mathfrak{o}
is 1.
For a function
f
, write its Fourier transform as
\widehat{f}
.

Throughout, assume that the functions
f
are compactly supported, measurable functions. For a fixed local field
K
, a measurable set
I
in
K
and a curve
\gamma:I\to K^{n}
, define the
extension operator along
\gamma
over
I
as

E_{I}f({\boldsymbol{x}}):=\int_{I}f(\xi)e({\gamma(\xi)\cdot{\boldsymbol{x}}}){\,{\rm d}}{\xi}\quad\text{for points}\quad{\boldsymbol{x}}\in K^{n}.

For
\delta\in\mathcal{R}(K)
, define the
square function along
\gamma
at scale
\delta
as

S_{\delta}f({\boldsymbol{x}}):=\Big(\sum_{J\in\mathcal{P}_{\delta}}|E_{J}f({\boldsymbol{x}})|^{2}\Big)^{1/2}.

Suppose that
w:K^{n}\to K
is a reasonable function. For each
{\boldsymbol{c}}\in K^{n}
and each
R\in K\setminus\{0\}
, define

w_{{\boldsymbol{c}},|R|}({\boldsymbol{x}}):=w\big(\frac{{\boldsymbol{x}}-{\boldsymbol{c}}}{|R|}\big)\quad\text{for}\quad{\boldsymbol{x}}\in K^{n}.

I will also write this as
w_{B}
when
B
is the associated box with center
{\boldsymbol{c}}
and sidelengths
|R|
.
Finally, define the constant

{{\rm H}}_{\gamma}(w):=\sup_{\delta\in\mathcal{R}(K)}\sup_{{\boldsymbol{c}}\in K^{n}}\sup_{f\neq 0}\|E_{\mathfrak{o}}f\|_{L^{2n}(w_{{\boldsymbol{c}},\delta^{-1}})}\big/\|S_{\delta}f\|_{L^{2n}(w_{{\boldsymbol{c}},\delta^{-1}})}.

Suppose that
w:\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R}
is a Schwartz function which is non-negative, at least 1 on the unit interval
[0,1]
and for which
\widehat{w}
is supported on
[-1,1]
and non-negative.
We define the
n

w
as
W({\boldsymbol{x}}):=w(x_{1})\cdots w(x_{n})
for
{\boldsymbol{x}}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
. Then
W
is at least 1 on the cube
[0,1]^{n}
while
\widehat{W}
is supported on the cube
[-1,1]^{n}
.
On
\mathbb{C}
, define our function
W({\boldsymbol{x}}+i{\boldsymbol{y}})
to be
W({\boldsymbol{x}})W({\boldsymbol{y}})
for
{\boldsymbol{x}},{\boldsymbol{y}}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
.
Finally, on a non-Archimedean field
K
, we define
W:={\bf 1}_{\{{\boldsymbol{z}}\in K^{n}:|{\boldsymbol{z}}|\leq 1\}}
. Then
W
is supported on and is 1 on the cube
\mathfrak{o}^{n}
while
\widehat{W}
is supported, and also 1, on the cube
\mathfrak{o}^{n}
.

For a non-Archimedean local field
K
, define the constants
C_{K}:=1
. For
K=\mathbb{R}
, define the constant
C_{\mathbb{R}}:=7
. For
K=\mathbb{C}
, define the constant
C_{\mathbb{C}}:=7^{2}
.
we can finally state the theorems.

Theorem 1
.

Let
K
be a local field and
\gamma(T):=(T,T^{2},\dots,T^{n})
. Assume that the characteristic of
K
is either 0 or greater than
n
.
We have the inequality
{{\rm H}}_{\gamma}(W)\leq(C_{K}n)^{1/2}
.
In other words, for each scale
\delta\in\mathcal{R}(K)
and
{\boldsymbol{c}}
in
K^{n}
, we have the inequality

\|E_{\mathfrak{o}}f\|_{L^{2n}(W_{{\boldsymbol{c}},\delta^{-n}})}\leq(C_{K}\cdot n)^{1/2}\;\|S_{\delta}f\|_{L^{2n}(W_{{\boldsymbol{c}},\delta^{-n}})}.

(1)

The bound (
1
) is close to sharp.
Indeed, Stirling’s Approximation
n!\sim\sqrt{2\pi n}(n/e)^{n}
and the following lower bound implies that (
1
) is asymptotically off by a constant.

Theorem 2
.

Fix
n\geq 2
.
For each local field
K
and polynomial curve
\gamma\in K[T]^{n}
,

{{\rm H}}_{\gamma}(W)\geq(n!)^{1/2n}.

(2)

The problem of finding sharp constants and extremizers for square function estimates appears interesting.
Inspired by
[
19
,
12
]
, I give two sharper, more general versions of Theorem
1
for Archimedean fields using calculus and classical algebraic geometry.
Define
\eta_{K}
to be 1 if
K
is
\mathbb{R}
and 2 if
K
is
\mathbb{C}
.
For a curve
\gamma:\mathfrak{o}\to K^{n}
, define its Lipschitz norm

\ell(\gamma)=\sup_{i=1,\dots,n}\sup_{s,t\in\mathfrak{o}}\frac{|\gamma_{i}(t)-\gamma_{i}(s)|}{|t-s|}.

Observe that
\ell(\gamma)
is finite when
\gamma
is polynomial and
\mathfrak{o}
is compact.

Theorem 3
.

Let
K
be
\mathbb{R}
or
\mathbb{C}
and
\gamma\in K[T]^{n}
be a non-degenerate polynomial curve.
We have the inequality
{{\rm H}}_{\gamma}(W)\leq\big(2\lceil\ell(\gamma)\rceil+1\big)^{\eta_{K}/2}\big(\prod_{i=1}^{n}\deg(\gamma_{i})\big)^{1/2n}
.

When
\gamma
is the moment curve, the proof of Theorem
3
demonstrates that we have ‘strong diagonal behavior’. For more general non-degenerate curves, any off-diagonal behavior is constrained by Bezout’s theorem.
These give the special subvarieties under-pinning square function estimates.

Over
\mathbb{R}
, there are much sharper bounds for fewnomials.

Theorem 4
.

Let
\gamma
be a non-degenerate, polynomial curve in
\mathbb{R}^{n}
such that the total number of monomials appearing in
\gamma
is
M
.
We have the inequality

{{\rm H}}_{\gamma}(W)\leq\big(2\lceil\ell(\gamma)\rceil+1\big)^{1/2}\big(2^{M(M-1)/2}(n+1)^{M}\big)^{1/2n}.

With only a minor modification, the proofs of Theorem
3
and
4
generalize to non-degenerate Pfaff curves in
\mathbb{R}^{n}
. The statement is more technical than its proof, so I refer the reader to
[
9
,
10
]
to extract what is needed.

In the form described above,
[
4
]
proved Theorem
1
for
real
non-degenerate curves; their result is far more general at the cost of an inexplicit constant. A new proof for Theorem
1
when
n=2
was given in
[
1
]
.
Subsequently,
[
6
]
proved the bound
{{\rm H}}_{\gamma}(W)^{2n}\leq n!
for non-Archimedean fields of sufficiently large characteristic.
Combined with Theorem
2
, we see that
{{\rm H}}_{\gamma}(W)=(n!)^{1/2n}
for the moment curve over non-Archimedean local fields of characteristic 0 or characteristic exceeding
n
.

The arguments proving Theorems

After consulting with experts, I believe the proofs of Theorems

I now give a concise overview of some antecedents to these results.

Historically, the main interest in such square function estimates arises from exponential sum estimates in analytic number theory; specifically, Vinogradov’s mean value theorems and Waring’s problem where the main choice of functions for input into (

[

and
[

, Chapter 4, Section 2]
.
Linnik subsequently developed a version of Vinogradov’s methods for local fields; see
[
11
, pages 71-72]
.

In harmonic analysis, related results appeared later.
A Cantor–Lebesgue theorem for the circle appeared in
[
2
]
, followed by a
\ell^{2}\to L^{4}
-discrete restriction estimate for the circle in
[
20
]
.
For more general functions,
[

]
attributes a version of Theorem

to
[

]

]
when

Uniting all of these works is the aforementioned paradigm from number theory. My use of ‘analysis’ there does not refer to any mathematical field, but to quantitative considerations of problems related to certain equations. This paradigm has long played a salient role in number theory and is prominent in analytic number theory through Manin’s conjecture and the circle method.
In harmonic analysis, this paradigm is understated despite its long-standing presence therein.

Let me take a moment to describe how this paradigm arises in the aforementioned works.
In Vinogradov’s pigeon-hole lemma and
[

In

In

In

In all cases, this information is drawn out through a reduction using orthogonality and multilinearity considerations.

3.
Reduction to special subvarieties

Fix a local field
K
and a curve
\gamma:\mathfrak{o}\to K^{n}
.
For each scale
\delta\in\mathcal{R}(K)
, each
\epsilon>0
and each
n
-tuple
{\boldsymbol{I}}
in
\mathcal{P}_{\delta}^{n}
, define
\mathcal{S}(\delta,{\boldsymbol{I}};\epsilon)
to be the set of
n
-tuples
{\boldsymbol{J}}\in\mathcal{P}_{\delta}^{n}
such that

|\sum_{i=1}^{n}\big(\gamma(t_{i})-\gamma(s_{i})\big)|\leq\epsilon

(3)

for some
{\boldsymbol{s}}\in{\boldsymbol{I}}
and some
{\boldsymbol{t}}\in{\boldsymbol{J}}
.
In other words,

\mathcal{S}(\delta,{\boldsymbol{I}};\epsilon):=\big\{{\boldsymbol{J}}\in\mathcal{P}_{\delta}^{n}:\text{ there exist points }{\boldsymbol{s}}\in{\boldsymbol{I}},{\boldsymbol{t}}\in{\boldsymbol{J}}\text{ satisfying }\eqref{close_points}\big\}.

Define
{{\rm S}}_{\gamma}:=\max_{\delta\in\mathcal{R}}\max_{{\boldsymbol{I}}\in\mathcal{P}_{\delta}^{n}}|\mathcal{S}(\delta,{\boldsymbol{I}};\delta^{n})|
.

Lemma 5
.

Let
K
be a local field,
n\geq 2
and
\gamma\in K[T]^{n}
be a curve in
K^{n}
.
Then

{{\rm H}}_{\gamma}(W)\leq({{\rm S}}_{\gamma})^{1/2n}.

(4)

Proof.

Fix
n\geq 2
.
Fix a local field
K
.
Fix a scale
\delta\in\mathcal{R}(K)
.
Let
B
be a box of sidelengths
\delta^{-n}
in
K^{n}
. Without loss of generality, it suffices to take
B
centered at the origin.
Write
E_{\mathfrak{o}}=\sum_{I\in\mathcal{P}_{\delta}}E_{I}
by the linearity of integration. Fubini’s theorem implies that

\displaystyle\|E_{\mathfrak{o}}f\|_{L^{2n}(W_{B})}^{2n}

\displaystyle=\sum_{{\boldsymbol{I}}\in\mathcal{P}_{\delta}^{n}}\sum_{{\boldsymbol{J}}\in\mathcal{P}_{\delta}^{n}}\int_{K^{n}}\big(\prod_{i=1}^{n}E_{I_{i}}f({\boldsymbol{x}})\big)\big(\prod_{j=1}^{n}\overline{E_{J_{j}}f({\boldsymbol{x}})}\big)W_{B}({\boldsymbol{x}}){\,{\rm d}}{{\boldsymbol{x}}}.

Fourier inversion and the properties of
W_{B}
imply that

\displaystyle\|E_{\mathfrak{o}}f\|_{L^{2n}(W_{B})}^{2n}

\displaystyle=\sum_{{\boldsymbol{I}}\in\mathcal{P}_{\delta}^{n}}\sum_{{\boldsymbol{J}}\in\mathcal{S}(\delta,{\boldsymbol{I}};\delta^{n})}\int\big(\prod_{i=1}^{n}E_{I_{i}}f\big)\big(\prod_{j=1}^{n}\overline{E_{J_{j}}f}\big)W_{B}.

For more details of this orthogonality, see
[
4
, Section 6]
.

Applying Fubini’s theorem once more as well as the Cauchy–Schwarz inequality on the sum over
{\boldsymbol{I}}\in\mathcal{P}_{\delta}^{n}
, we deduce that

\displaystyle\|E_{\mathfrak{o}}f\|_{L^{2n}(W_{B})}^{2n}

\displaystyle=\int\sum_{{\boldsymbol{I}}\in\mathcal{P}_{\delta}^{n}}\big(\prod_{i=1}^{n}E_{I_{i}}f\big)\big(\sum_{{\boldsymbol{J}}\in\mathcal{S}(\delta,{\boldsymbol{I}};\delta^{n})}\prod_{j=1}^{n}\overline{E_{J_{j}}f}\big)W_{B}

\displaystyle\leq\int\Big(\sum_{{\boldsymbol{I}}\in\mathcal{P}_{\delta}^{n}}|\prod_{i=1}^{n}E_{I_{i}}f|^{2}\Big)^{1/2}\Big(\sum_{{\boldsymbol{I}}\in\mathcal{P}_{\delta}^{n}}|\sum_{{\boldsymbol{J}}\in\mathcal{S}(\delta,{\boldsymbol{I}};\delta^{n})}\prod_{j=1}^{n}\overline{E_{J_{j}}f}|^{2}\Big)^{1/2}W_{B}.

Applying the Cauchy–Schwarz inequality on the inner sum over
{\boldsymbol{J}}\in\mathcal{S}(\delta,{\boldsymbol{I}};\delta^{n})
and the triangle inequality on the second outer sum over
{\boldsymbol{I}}\in\mathcal{P}_{\delta}^{n}
, we deduce that

\displaystyle\|E_{\mathfrak{o}}f\|_{L^{2n}(W_{B})}^{2n}

\displaystyle\leq\int\Big(S_{\delta}f\Big)^{n}\Big(\sum_{{\boldsymbol{I}}\in\mathcal{P}_{\delta}^{n}}|\mathcal{S}(\delta,{\boldsymbol{I}};\delta^{n})|\sum_{{\boldsymbol{J}}\in\mathcal{S}(\delta,{\boldsymbol{I}};\delta^{n})}|\prod_{j=1}^{n}{E_{J_{j}}f}|^{2}\Big)^{1/2}W_{B}

\displaystyle\leq{{\rm S}}_{\gamma}^{1/2}\int\Big(S_{\delta}f\Big)^{n}\Big(\sum_{{\boldsymbol{I}}\in\mathcal{P}_{\delta}^{n}}\sum_{{\boldsymbol{J}}\in\mathcal{S}(\delta,{\boldsymbol{I}};\delta^{n})}|\prod_{j=1}^{n}{E_{J_{j}}f}|^{2}\Big)^{1/2}W_{B}.

By the symmetry of the inequalities (
3
):
{\boldsymbol{J}}\in\mathcal{S}(\delta,{\boldsymbol{I}};\delta^{n})
if and only if
{\boldsymbol{I}}\in\mathcal{S}(\delta,{\boldsymbol{J}};\delta^{n})
.
Use this symmetry to invert the double sum and apply the triangle inequality on the innermost summand over
{\boldsymbol{I}}\in\mathcal{S}(\delta,{\boldsymbol{J}};\delta^{n})
to deduce that

\displaystyle\|E_{\mathfrak{o}}f\|_{L^{2n}(W_{B})}^{2n}

\displaystyle\leq{{\rm S}}_{\gamma}^{1/2}\int\Big(S_{\delta}f\Big)^{n}\Big(\sum_{{\boldsymbol{J}}\in\mathcal{P}_{\delta}^{n}}\sum_{{\boldsymbol{I}}\in\mathcal{S}(\delta,{\boldsymbol{J}};\delta^{n})}|\prod_{j=1}^{n}{E_{J_{j}}f}|^{2}\Big)^{1/2}W_{B}

\displaystyle\leq{{\rm S}}_{\gamma}\int\Big(S_{\delta}f\Big)^{n}\Big(\sum_{{\boldsymbol{J}}\in\mathcal{P}_{\delta}^{n}}|\prod_{j=1}^{n}{E_{J_{j}}f}|^{2}\Big)^{1/2}W_{B}={{\rm S}}_{\gamma}\|S_{\delta}f\|_{L^{2n}(W_{B})}^{2n}.

Taking
2n^{\textrm{th}}
-roots on both sides of the inequality completes the proof.
∎

4.
Diagonal behavior of the Vinogradov system

Our main estimate uniformly bounds the size of
\mathcal{S}(\delta,{\boldsymbol{I}};\delta^{n})
which implies that
{{\rm S}}_{\gamma}
is finite.
Via Lemma

Proposition 6
.

Let
\gamma:=(T,T^{2},\dots,T^{n})
be the moment curve in a fixed local field
K
of characteristic 0 or greater than
n
.
We have the bound

{{\rm S}}_{\gamma}\leq(C_{K}\cdot n)^{n}.

(5)

See
[
4
, Proposition 1.3]
,
[
1
, Proposition 3.1]
and
[

Compare with classical versions of this proposition such as its original form due to Vinogradov exposed in
[
8
, Vinogradov’s ‘pigeon-hole lemma’ on page 12]
,
[
18
, pages 37–44]
,
[
7

[

.
Linnik developed a
p

[

[

Let me take a moment to describe the idea underlying Proposition
6
.
Suppose that
{\boldsymbol{s}}
and
{\boldsymbol{t}}
are points in
K^{n}
.
If
{\boldsymbol{t}}
is a permutation of
{\boldsymbol{s}}
, then this pair of points is a solution to (
3
) with
\epsilon=0
.
It transpires that the converse is true.
To see this converse, define the elementary symmetric polynomials
\sigma_{j}(X_{1},\dots,X_{n})
to be
\sum_{i_{1}<i_{2}<\cdots<i_{j}}X_{i_{1}}\cdots X_{i_{j}}
for
j\in\mathbb{N}
and recall the Girard–Newton equations:

(-1)^{j-1}j\sigma_{j}(X_{1},\dots,X_{n})=\sum_{i=0}^{j-1}(-1)^{i}\big(X_{1}^{j-i}+\cdots+X_{n}^{j-i}\big)\sigma_{i}(X_{1},\dots,X_{n}).

(6)

The Girard–Newton equations imply that for each coordinate
t_{j}
, there exists an
s_{i}=t_{j}
.
The special subvarieties discussed previously are these ‘almost diagonal’ varieties given by
t_{j}=s_{i}
for each
j
and some
i
.
Fixing a point
{\boldsymbol{s}}
, the number of such points
{\boldsymbol{t}}
(and equivalently special subvarieties) is at most
n^{n}
.
The critical insight underlying Proposition
6
is that a fattened version of this argument continues to hold for
\mathcal{S}(\delta,{\boldsymbol{I}};\delta^{n})
.

Going further, the Girard–Newton equations (
6
) and the Fundamental Theorem of Algebra imply that permutations are the only solutions. Obtaining a fattened version of this ‘strong diagonal property’ was an essential feature in
[
6
]
; the strong diagonal property took the form: if
{\boldsymbol{J}}\in\mathcal{S}(\delta,{\boldsymbol{I}},\delta^{n})
, then
{\boldsymbol{J}}
is a permutation of
{\boldsymbol{I}}
.
We will see this strong diagonal behavior again in Proposition
7
below.

It suffices to show the bound

For

G({\boldsymbol{s}};X):=\prod_{i=1}^{n}(X-s_{i})

If
{\boldsymbol{s}}\in{\boldsymbol{I}}

|G({\boldsymbol{t}};x)-G({\boldsymbol{s}};x)|\leq\delta^{n}\text{ for all }x\in\mathfrak{o}.

Taking

|G({\boldsymbol{s}};t_{j})|\leq\delta^{n}

The pigeonhole principle implies that there exists an

In this section, I indicate how to prove Theorems

6.
On Superorthogonality

In this section I assume that the reader is familiar with
[
5
]
, and I rephrase some terminology from therein.
Suppose that
(f_{j_{1}},\dots,f_{j_{2n}})
is a tuple of functions from a finite collection of functions
\{f_{j}\}_{j\in J}
.
Call a
2n
-tuple of functions
(f_{j_{1}},\dots,f_{j_{2n}})
‘

-superorthogonal’ if

\int f_{j_{1}}\overline{f_{j_{2}}}\cdots f_{j_{2n-1}}\overline{f_{j_{2n}}}=0.

(11)

Define the ensuing ‘Types’ as subcollections of
J^{2n}
satisfying the following properties:

•

Type I*:
If
(j_{1},j_{3},\dots,j_{2n-1})
is not a permutation of
(j_{2},j_{4},\dots,j_{2n})
, then
(f_{j_{1}},\dots,f_{j_{2n}})
is
2n
-superorthogonal.

•

Type I:
If some value
j_{k}
appears an odd number of times in the
2n
-tuple
(j_{1},\dots,j_{2n})
, then
(f_{j_{1}},\dots,f_{j_{2n}})
is
2n
-superorthogonal.

•

Type II:
If some value
j_{k}
appears exactly once in the
2n
-tuple
(j_{1},\dots,j_{2n})
, then
(f_{j_{1}},\dots,f_{j_{2n}})
is
2n
-superorthogonal.

•

Type III:
If some value
j_{k}
appears exactly once and is strictly greater than all other values appearing in the
2n
-tuple
(j_{1},\dots,j_{2n})
, then
(f_{j_{1}},\dots,f_{j_{2n}})
is
2n
-superorthogonal.

•

Type IV:
If all values of the
2n
-tuple
(j_{1},\dots,j_{2n})
are distinct, then
(f_{j_{1}},\dots,f_{j_{2n}})
is
2n
-superorthogonal.

Each successive Type implies the next in the sense that if a subcollection is Type I*, then it is also Type I, and so on.

For this section, take
\gamma:=(T,T^{2},\dots,T^{n})
to be the moment curve. Since the moment curve is non-degenerate and has degree
n
, the proof of Proposition
7
reveals that
\mathcal{S}(\delta,{\boldsymbol{I}};\delta^{n})
is the set of permutations of
{\boldsymbol{I}}
along with their neighbors.
This gives ‘Type I
∗

[
13

and
[
5
, Section 6]
. The ‘almost’ refers to the neighbors.

In contrast, the proof of Theorem
1
allows for tuples which are not Type I*, I, II, or III. To be precise, let us consider superorthogonality for 6-tuples of an extension operator for the moment curve
(T,T^{2},T^{3})
.
Let
f

Let us regard

In other words, I do not prove that (
