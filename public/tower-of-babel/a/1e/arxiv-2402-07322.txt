Luofeng Liao, Columbia University Christian Kroer, Columbia University Sergei Leonenkov, Ads Online Experimentation, Meta Okke Schrijvers, Central Applied Science, Meta Liang Shi, Central Applied Science, Meta Nicolas Stier-Moses, Central Applied Science, Meta Congshan Zhang, Central Applied Science, Meta

Abstract
. Online A/B testing is widely used in the internet industry to inform decisions on new feature roll-outs. For online marketplaces (such as advertising markets), standard approaches to A/B testing may lead to biased results when buyers operate under a budget constraint, as budget consumption in one arm of the experiment impacts performance of the other arm. To counteract this interference, one can use a budget-split design where the budget constraint operates on a per-arm basis and each arm receives an equal fraction of the budget, leading to “budget-controlled A/B testing.” Despite clear advantages of budget-controlled A/B testing, performance degrades when budget are split too small, limiting the overall throughput of such systems. In this paper, we propose a parallel budget-controlled A/B testing design where we use market segmentation to identify submarkets in the larger market, and we run parallel experiments on each submarket. Our contributions are as follows: First, we introduce and demonstrate the effectiveness of the parallel budget-controlled A/B test design with submarkets in a large online marketplace environment. Second, we formally define market interference in first-price auction markets leveraging the first price pacing equilibrium (FPPE) framework which has been proposed to model autobidding in repeated auctions. Third, we propose a debiased surrogate that eliminates the first-order bias of FPPE, drawing upon the principles of sensitivity analysis in mathematical programs. Fourth, we derive a plug-in estimator for the surrogate and establish its asymptotic normality. Fifth, we provide an estimation procedure for submarket parallel budget-controlled A/B tests. Finally, we present numerical examples on semi-synthetic data, confirming that the debiasing technique achieves the desired coverage properties.



1.
Introduction

Online A/B testing, or
experimentation
, is widely used in the internet industry. Major tech companies conduct a large number of A/B tests annually to inform decisions related to ads delivery, online auction mechanisms, user interface components, new feature rollouts, infrastructure improvements, and many other aspects of their products and services.

In the context of online auctions in marketplaces, where buyers bid for sellers’ items or advertisers bid for users’ impressions, budget-controlled A/B testing has gained popularity and is supported by companies like LinkedIn, Google, and Meta
(
Basse et al. 2016
;
Liu et al. 2021
)
. The primary advantage of budget control in A/B testing is that it eliminates interference between the A and B groups that could otherwise bias the results: In a traditional A/B test, the budget of a buyer is distributed across all auctions, involving users from both A and B groups. This couples the behavior of the buyer across the groups; for example, increased expenditure under B might decrease expenditure in A, causing interference. However, in a budget-controlled A/B test, each buyer spends half of their budget on users from the A group and the other half on users from the B group, creating two separate but mirrored markets, and thus removing the interference caused by a shared budget constraint. This experiment type, therefore, is commonly used to test various ads delivery changes, such as pacing algorithms (see Section
2.1
).

Despite clear advantages of budget-controlled A/B testing, companies are extremely constrained by the number of such experiments they can run: buyers (advertisers) can only be charged once for the same item (impression), it is thus not feasible to create additional experimentation throughput by reshuffling users between the A and B groups. An alternative idea is to create more budget splits so more treatments can be tested at the same time. However, this will lower the budget-per-group substantially, which could lead to different equilibrium outcomes and may disproportionately affect smaller buyers. In addition, it poses challenges with respect to statistical power, in view of the fact that tech companies primarily focus on down-funnel conversion events which are difficult to measure effects on to begin with. In fact, the lack of bandwidth for budget-controlled A/B tests has become one of the major bottlenecks in experimentation for the internet industry.

A third approach to increase throughput is market segmentation. If the overall market can be divided into several relatively isolated submarkets, budget-controlled A/B tests can be conducted in parallel within these submarkets. However, this method also presents some challenges. First, submarkets are rarely completely isolated; certain items may attract buyers from multiple submarkets, resulting in interference across
submarkets when conducting tests in parallel. Second, submarkets differ in terms of buyer (and user) compositions, which might cause the local treatment effect estimates being not representative of the global treatment effect where all buyers are included in the market. The second challenge is relatively easy to address in practice by imposing balancing constraints in the clustering algorithm used to define submarkets, while the first challenge is more fundamentally important and requires deeper theoretical understanding.

1.1.
Parallel A/B Testing in Practice

In this section we describe the real-world problem of A/B testing with congestion that we wish to model, and our proposed solution of parallel A/B tests in carefully balanced submarkets; the formal model appears in
Section

2
and
Section

3
. Along with the description of the real-world problem, we will also discuss which aspects of it will be or will not be captured by our theoretical model.

We start by describing the market environment. Assume that there is a set of
n
advertisers, and each advertiser
i
has a budget
b_{i}
. Whenever a user shows up on the platform an
impression opportunity
occurs, and an auction is conducted in order to determine which subset of ads will be shown to the user. Each advertiser
i
has some stated value
v_{i}(\theta)
of being shown to a particular impression opportunity
\theta
. This may not be their true valuation; in practice it may be their stated willingness to pay for a click, multiplied by an estimated click-through-rate for the pair (
i
,
\theta
). The advertiser submits a bid which is determined based on
v_{i}(\theta)
, as well as the expenditure of the advertiser so far. For example, in
multiplicative pacing

(
Balseiro et al. 2017
;
Conitzer et al. 2022b
;
Conitzer et al. 2022a
)
, the platform adaptively learns a
pacing multiplier

\beta_{i}\in[0,1]
such that the bid is formed as
\beta_{i}v_{i}(\theta)
. The budget-management system then adaptively controls
\beta_{i}
over time, in order to ensure the correct rate of budget expenditure on behalf of the advertiser.
The auction format for an impression opportunity is typically some generalization of the first or second-price auction, though the real-world auctions are not fully captured by any simple-to-describe auction model from the literature. For example, in the setting of inserting ads into a news feed, multiple ads may be allowed to be shown, but the exact number may depend on the size of each ad, the size of other content in the feed, and so forth.

In our theoretical model, we will simplify the auction format in three respects:
first, we will assume that each impression opportunity allows exactly one ad to be shown. This is a common assumption in the literature studying both online learning for budget management
(
Borgs et al. 2007
;
Balseiro and Gur 2019
)
as well as in the literature on equilibrium outcomes under budget management
(
Balseiro et al. 2017
;
Conitzer et al. 2022b
;
Conitzer et al. 2022a
;
Aggarwal et al. 2019
;
Balseiro et al. 2023
)
.
Thirdly, we assume that each individual auction is a
first-price auction
, meaning that the highest bidder wins, and pays their bid. Internet advertising uses both first and second-price auctions, depending on the particular context
(
Balseiro et al. 2023
;
Goke et al. 2021
)
, though generalizations of the second-price auction are arguably still more common, in particular for sponsored search and feed auctions. We focus on first-price auctions because the equilibrium outcomes under budget management satisfy two crucial properties from the perspective of statistical inference: they are unique, and the equilibrium is captured by a convex program
(
Conitzer et al. 2022a
)
.
In contrast, equilibria under budget management in second-price auctions may not be unique
(
Conitzer et al. 2022b
)
, and they are PPAD-hard to compute
(
Chen et al. 2023
)
. It is an interesting future research direction to figure out how to develop results for the second-price setting, though we expect it to be much harder.
We will also abstract away the dynamic temporal aspects of budget management: instead of the pacing multipliers being learned over time, we will assume that what we observe is the set of pacing multipliers in equilibrium. Secondly, in the real world, advertisers may use a variety of budget management algorithms beyond multiplicative pacing. For example, Google also offers
probabilistic throttling
, where bids are not modified, and instead the expenditure is managed by independently flipping a coin for each auction, in order to determine whether to bid at all
(
Balseiro et al. 2017
;
Chen et al. 2021
;
Karande et al. 2013
)
.
We assume that every advertiser uses multiplicative pacing in order to manage budgets.
Budget-management equilibria in first-price auctions with probabilistic throttling are known to also be unique and computable
(
Chen et al. 2021
)
, so it is possible that similar results could be obtained for that setting, though it is not known whether those equilibria are captured by a convex mathematical program, which is a crucial fact for our technical approach.

Now that we have discussed budget management, we describe the A/B testing problem. Suppose that a platform wants to run
K
A/B tests, which may affect, e.g., the valuations that advertisers have for impression slots, revenue, etc.
Naively performing the A/B test on a per-impression or per-user basis and then simply running the market as usual will not work, because the budget management system means that the A and B users will be affected by each other through the budget coupling.
As mentioned in the introduction, a possible option is to perform budget splitting: create
K+1
copies of each advertiser (corresponding to each test, as well as a holdout “A” control copy), randomize users into one of the
K+1
markets, and run separate budget management for each copy of the
K+1
advertisers. As mentioned earlier, this approach runs into problems because small advertisers end up with prohibitively-small budgets, and there is a large negative impact on statistical power because some of the events measured by the A/B tests might be far downstream of an impression.

Instead, our paper considers the following parallel A/B test paradigm based on market segmentation.
We first define a bipartite graph between advertisers and users based on targeting criteria. Subsequently, we cluster the advertisers into
K
clusters, where the objective is to minimize the sum of weighted edges between clusters subject to traffic balancing constraints to make the resulting clusters as similar to the whole market as possible. The edge weight between a pair of advertisers is the number of impressions (or users) where they are both within the top-
k
bids. The choice of
k
is a parameter that must be chosen based on experience with the specific application setting.
If the clustering achieves a small objective function value, then each cluster is a mostly isolated submarket, in the sense that each user will mostly receive bids from advertisers in a single cluster.
Then, we run an A/B test within each of the
K
submarkets.
Every user is randomly assigned to either “A” or “B” in each submarket.
Note that the same user can be in “A” in submarket 1 and “B” in submarket 2. This means that all the A/B tests must be mutually compatible; in practice this means that our approach is best suited for A/B tests that apply to advertisers, as opposed to, e.g., user-side user interface changes.
If the interaction between different submarkets is not too strong, then we expect that hypothesis testing in a submarket is indicative of the true treatment effect within that submarket.
Secondly, if each submarket is representative of the overall market, then such a hypothesis test is also indicative of the true treatment effect across the whole market.

In our theoretical model we use a coarser notion of contamination between a pair of submarkets than the top-
k
measure that we use in practice. In our theoretical model, we measure the number of items with
any
bids from buyers from separate submarkets.
In practice, this measure is too coarse (and often very large). It is an interesting follow-up question to come up with stronger theoretical results that allow for finer-grained measures of contamination.
Secondly, our theoretical model will assume that what we are interested in is the treatment effect within a submarket. This implicitly assumes that each submarket is representative of the overall market.

2.
Theory overview

2.1.
Related Work

Pacing equilibrium.

Pacing is a budget management strategy where bids are scaled down by a multiplicative factor in order to smooth out and control spending.
In the first-price setting,

Borgs et al. 2007
study first price auctions with budget constraints in a perturbed model, where the limit prices converge to those of an FPPE.
Building on that work,
Conitzer et al. 2022a
introduce the FPPE framework to model autobidding in repeated auctions,
and discover several properties of FPPE such as shill-proofness monotonicity properties, and a close relationship between FPPE and the quasilinear Fisher market equilibrium
(
Chen et al. 2007
;
Cole et al. 2017
)
.

Gao and Kroer 2022
propose an infinite-dimensional variant of the quasilinear Fisher market, which lays the probability foundations of the current paper.

Gao et al. 2021
and
Liao et al. 2022
study online computation of the infinite-dimensional Fisher market equilibrium, and we utilize their methods to compute equilibria.
In the second-price setting, a variety of models have been proposed for modeling the outcome of budget management:

Balseiro et al. 2015
study budget-management in second-price auctions through a fluid mean-field approximation;

Balseiro and Gur 2019
investigate adaptive pacing strategy from buyers’ perspective in a stochastic continuous setting; and

Balseiro et al. 2021b
study several budget smoothing methods including multiplicative pacing in a stochastic context.
A second-price analogue of FPPE was explored by
Conitzer et al. 2022b
.
Modeling statistical inference in the second-price setting is most likely harder, due to equilibrium multiplicity issues
(
Conitzer et al. 2022b
)
, and hardness of even computing equilibria
(
Chen et al. 2023
)
.

Recently, a series of papers (e.g.,
Aggarwal et al. 2019
;
Deng et al. 2021
;
Balseiro et al. 2021a
) investigated an “autobidding” model for value-maximizing bidders, who aim to maximize their value subject to budget or return-on-spend constraints, which may include buying individual ad impressions that have negative utility. Several extensions provide results for more general settings, such as the quality of equilibria for generalized rationality models for bidders
(
Babaioff et al. 2021
)
, non-uniform bidding strategies
(
Deng et al. 2023
)
, and liquid welfare guarantees when budget-constrained buyers fail to reach equilibrium
(
Gaitonde et al. 2023
)
.
Statistical inference for parallel A/B testing under such models represent an interesting future direction of work.

Interference in A/B Testing for Auction Markets.
Experimentation under interference has been extensively studied in the past decade within the context of social networks
(
Ugander et al. 2013
;
Aronow and Samii 2017
;
Eckles et al. 2017
;
Athey et al. 2018
;
Li and Wager 2022
)
and two-sided marketplaces
(
Zigler and Papadogeorgou 2018
;
Wager and Xu 2021
)
. Under interference, the outcome of an experiment unit, either a user in a social network or a buyer in an online marketplace, can be affected by the treatment status of other units in the networks, violating the Stable Unit Treatment Values Assumption (SUTVA) assumption commonly employed in analyzing online A/B tests
(
Rosenbaum 2007
)
. To take into account this type of interference, novel designs and analysis approaches have been proposed, e.g., cluster randomization
(
Ugander et al. 2013
;
Karrer et al. 2021
)
, regression-based estimators
(
Chin 2019
)
, and bipartite analysis
(
Harshaw et al. 2021
)
. A/B testing in the presence of seller-side budget constraints has been discussed by
Basse et al. 2016
and
Liu et al. 2021
where the budget-splitting design spans the full market.
However, most of the above works do not consider the particular type of interference caused by competitive equilibrium effects.
Recent work has also addressed equilibrium effects
(
Wager and Xu 2021
;
Liao et al. 2023
;
Liao and Kroer 2024
)
. Most relevant to our results,
Liao and Kroer 2023
consider A/B testing via budget splitting in Fisher markets and FPPE. However, none of these works consider the effects caused by parallel A/B tests that interfere with each other.
Our method builds on top of the budget-splitting design; our approach additionally segments the full market into submarkets, where different submarkets receive different treatment. In the formal sections of our paper we assume that the submarket structure is known. In practice, the submarkets may not be known;
Rolnick et al. 2019
and
Viviano et al. 2023
present methods to identify submarkets through clustering approaches that aim to limit the interference between clusters.

{\small\widehat{\textsf{{FPPE}}}}(b,v,\sigma,\gamma)
, is the unique tuple

(\beta,p)\in[0,1]^{n}\times{\mathbb{R}}^{t}_{+}

such that there exist
x_{i}^{\tau}\in[0,1]
:

•

(First-price) For all
\tau
,
{p^{\tau}}=\max_{i}{\beta_{i}}{v_{i}^{\tau}}
. For all
i
and
\tau
,
{x_{i}^{\tau}}>0
implies
{\beta_{i}}{v_{i}^{\tau}}=\max_{k}\beta_{k}v_{k}^{\tau}
.

•

(Supply and budget feasible) For all
i
,
\sigma{\sum_{\tau=1}^{t}}{x_{i}^{\tau}}{p^{\tau}}\leq b_{i}
. For all
\tau
,
{\sum_{i=1}^{n}}{x_{i}^{\tau}}\leq 1
.
(Market clearing) For all
\tau
,
{p^{\tau}}>0
implies
{\sum_{i=1}^{n}}{x_{i}^{\tau}}=1
.

•

(No unnecessary pacing) For all
i
,
\sigma{\sum_{\tau=1}^{t}}{x_{i}^{\tau}}{p^{\tau}}<b_{i}
implies
{\beta_{i}}=1
.

In
Liao and Kroer 2023
, it is shown that if
\gamma
consists of
t
i.i.d. draws from distribution
s
, and one takes
\sigma=1/t
, then the pacing multiplier in
{\small\widehat{\textsf{{FPPE}}}}(b,v,1/t,\gamma)
converges to the pacing multiplier in
{\small\mathsf{{FPPE}}}(b,v,s,\Theta)
in probability.
Also note that the
{\small\widehat{\textsf{{FPPE}}}}(tb,v,1,\gamma)
converges to the same limit FPPE
{\small\mathsf{{FPPE}}}(b,v,s,\Theta)
because the pacing multiplier of a finite FPPE does not change when budgets and supplies are multiplied by the same scalar.

2.2.3.
The Eisenberg-Gale Program

Both the limit FPPE and the finite FPPE have convex program characterizations
(
Chen et al. 2007
;
Conitzer et al. 2022a
;
Gao and Kroer 2022
)
.
We define the dual Eisenberg-Gale (EG) objective for a single item
\theta
as

(1)

\displaystyle F(\theta,\beta)=f(\beta,\theta)-{\sum_{i=1}^{n}}b_{i}\log\beta_{i}\;,\quad f(\beta,\theta)=\max_{i\in[n]}\beta_{i}v_{i}(\theta)\;.

The population and sample (dual) EG objectives are then defined as

(2)

\displaystyle H(\beta)={\mathbb{E}}[F(\theta,\beta)]\;,\quad H_{t}(\beta)=\frac{1}{t}{\sum_{\tau=1}^{t}}F({\theta^{\tau}},\beta)\;.

We say
{\mathscr{H}}
is the Hessian of market
{\mathscr{M}}
when
{\mathscr{H}}=\nabla^{2}\int F(\theta,\beta)s\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\theta|_{\beta=\beta^{*}}
.

The equilibrium pacing multipliers
\beta^{*}
in
{\small\mathsf{{FPPE}}}(b,v,s,\Theta)
can be recovered through
the population dual EG program

(3)

\displaystyle\hskip-5.69046pt\beta^{*}=\argmin_{\beta\in(0,1]^{n}}H(\beta)\;.

The pacing multiplier
\beta^{*}
is the unique solution to
Eq.

3
.
Let
\beta^{\gamma}
be the equilibrium pacing multiplier in
{\small\widehat{\textsf{{FPPE}}}}(b,v,1/t,\gamma)
. Then
\beta^{\gamma}
solves the sample analogue of
Eq.

3
:

(4)

\displaystyle\beta^{\gamma}=\argmin_{\beta\in(0,1]^{n}}H_{t}(\beta)\;.

Let us briefly comment on the differential structure of
f
, since it plays a role in later sections.
The function
f(\beta,\theta)
is a convex function of
\beta
and its subdifferential
\partial_{\beta}f(\beta,\theta)
is the convex hull of
\{v_{i}e_{i}:\text{index $i$ such that ${\beta_{i}}{v_{i}(\theta)}=\max_{k}{\beta_{k}}v_{k}(\theta)$}\}
, with
e_{i}
being the base vector in
{\mathbb{R}^{n}}
.
When
\max_{i}{\beta_{i}}v_{i}(\theta)
is attained by a unique
i^{*}
, the function
f(\cdot,\theta)
is differentiable. In that case, all entries of
\nabla_{\beta}f(\beta,\theta)
are zero expect that the
i^{*}
-th entry is filled with the value
v_{i^{*}}(\theta)
.

3.
Setup: Interference as Contamination

In this section, we discuss how to estimate market equilibria when there is contamination in the supply. Then, we show that interference from other markets can be viewed as a form of contamination, and so the problem of removing interference bias can be analyzed via our contamination framework.

3.1.
Contaminated First-Price Pacing Equilibrium

We assume that we are in the same FPPE setting as before:
there are
n
buyers, each with budget
b_{i}
,
and an item set
\Theta
. However, now we assume that
\Theta
is partitioned into
\Theta_{\mathsf{good}}
and
\Theta_{\mathsf{bad}}
.
Let
s
be supported on
\Theta_{\mathsf{good}}
and
s^{\prime}
on
\Theta_{\mathsf{bad}}
.
We define the
\alpha
-contaminated market and the uncontaminated market by

\displaystyle{\mathscr{M}}_{\alpha}={\small\mathsf{{FPPE}}}(b,v,s_{\alpha},\Theta)\;,\quad{\mathscr{M}}_{0}={\small\mathsf{{FPPE}}}(b,v,s,\Theta)\;,

where
s_{\alpha}=\alpha s^{\prime}+(1-\alpha)s
.
Our goal is to perform inference about FPPE properties in the limit FPPE with the item set
\Theta_{\mathsf{good}}
and supply
s
. However, we are given access to finite FPPEs sampled from
s_{\alpha}
instead.
In particular, let
\gamma
be
t
i.i.d. draws from
s_{\alpha}
and let

\displaystyle\widehat{\mathscr{M}}_{\alpha}={\small\widehat{\textsf{{FPPE}}}}(b,v,1/t,\gamma)\;.

We assume knowledge of
\alpha
throughout the paper.
In practice, this can often be estimated from historical data; in the parallel A/B test setting, this can be estimated directly from the sampled set of items, since we know which items are good and bad in that setting.
Let
\beta^{*}
and
\beta^{*}_{\alpha}
be the limit pacing multipliers in
{\mathscr{M}}_{0}
and
{\mathscr{M}}_{\alpha}
, respectively. Let
\beta^{\gamma}_{\alpha}
be the pacing multipliers in the sampled market
\widehat{\mathscr{M}}_{\alpha}
and let
H_{\alpha,t}(\beta)=\frac{1}{t}{\sum_{\tau=1}^{t}}F({\theta^{\tau}},\beta)
be the sample EG objective.

Now, if we wanted to make inferences about
{\mathscr{M}}_{\alpha}
then we could use existing statistical inference theory on how to use data in a finite FPPE
\widehat{\mathscr{M}}_{\alpha}
to make inferences about the limit FPPE
{\mathscr{M}}_{\alpha}
.
In recent work of
Liao and Kroer 2023
, it is shown that for an
influence function

D_{\alpha}:\Theta\to{\mathbb{R}}
(to be defined in
Theorem

3
), the asymptotic distribution of
\beta_{\alpha}^{\gamma}
around
\beta_{\alpha}^{*}
satisfies

\displaystyle\sqrt{t}(\beta^{\gamma}_{\alpha}-\beta^{*}_{\alpha})=\frac{1}{\sqrt{t}}{\sum_{\tau=1}^{t}}(D_{\alpha}({\theta^{\tau}})-{\mathbb{E}}_{\alpha}[D_{\alpha}(\theta)])+o_{p}(1)\;,

where
\{{\theta^{\tau}}\}
is the set of items sampled in
\gamma
.
Our central research question can now be formulated as follows:

How can we use the data in the finite contaminated market
\widehat{\mathscr{M}}_{\alpha}
to make inferences about the uncontaminated limit market
{\mathscr{M}}_{0}
?

3.2.
Interference as Contamination

Now we show how the contamination model from the previous section can be used to model interference.
Consider
K
separated auction markets, which together form a global market.
In the global market there are
n
buyers, each with budget
b_{i}
,
and an item set
\Theta
, partitioned into
\Theta_{\mathsf{good}}
and
\Theta_{\mathsf{bad}}
.
Let
C_{1},\dots,C_{K}
be a partition of the buyers,

\Theta_{1},\dots,\Theta_{K}
be a partition of the good item set
\Theta_{\mathsf{good}}
,
and
s_{1},\dots s_{K}
be a set of supply functions, supported on
\Theta_{1},\dots,\Theta_{K}
respectively.
The
k
-th submarket
consists of buyers in
C_{k}
, the item set
\Theta_{k}
and supply
s_{k}
.
Let
s=\frac{1}{K}\sum_{k}s_{k}
be the average mixture and
s^{\prime}
be a supply supported on
\Theta_{\mathsf{bad}}
.
Let the contaminated supply be
s_{\alpha}=\alpha s^{\prime}+(1-\alpha)s
.

By imposing structure on

\Theta_{\mathsf{good}}
and
\Theta_{\mathsf{bad}}

the contamination model can capture interference among auction markets.
We assume that submarkets are separated, which models the ideal case where there is no interference.
A buyer
i\in C_{k}
is only interested in items from the submarket he belongs to:
v_{i}(\theta)=0
for
\theta\in\Theta_{k^{\prime}},k^{\prime}\neq k
.
Next, we let
\Theta_{\mathsf{bad}}
represent items
that cause outbound edges from submarkets; see the green edges in
Fig.

3
left panel. An item is referred to as
bad
if it has positive values for buyers from at least two different submarkets. Formally,

\theta\in\Theta_{\mathsf{bad}}
if there exist
i\in C_{k}
,
j\in C_{k^{\prime}}
,
k\neq k^{\prime}
, such that
v_{i}(\theta)>0
and
v_{j}(\theta)>0
.
Combining these assumptions, we have that a buyer
i
from submarket
k
has positive values
only
for items from the sets
\Theta_{k}
and possibly
\Theta_{\mathsf{bad}}
.
Now we have fully specified a contaminated market setup: we wish to make inferences on the market consisting of only
\Theta_{\mathsf{good}}
(which is really
K
fully separate submarkets), but we observe an actual market containing items from
\Theta_{\mathsf{good}}\cup\Theta_{\mathsf{bad}}
.
With this setup, we can use the results developed in the following section to model interference in parallel submarkets.

In
Fig.

3
we present an example of interference among
K=3
submarkets.
The market of interest is the perfectly separated market (right).
The reason is, in parallel A/B testing, submarkets are created in a way that each resembles the global market, and when a submarket receives a treatment, the observed quantities in that submarket, such as revenues and social welfare, are considered surrogates for the treatment effect in the global market.
However, in practice we only observe the interfered finite market (left), which converges to the interfered limit market (middle). In
Section

5
we show how to analyze parallel A/B testing using this framework.

4.
Main Results

In this section, we develop a methodology for making inferences about, e.g., pacing multipliers or revenue in the uncontaminated limit FPPE.
We introduce a surrogate for pacing multipliers, based on the notion of directional derivatives, and establish its debiasing property in
Section

4.1
. Then, we focus on estimating this surrogate quantity in
Section

4.2
, and develop asymptotic normality results in
Section

4.3
.
Secondly, we consider estimating revenue. Revenue can be thought of as a smooth function of pacing multipliers, and we discuss debiased estimation and inference of revenue based on our pacing multiplier results in
Section

4.4
.

Before we proceed we briefly discuss two baseline approaches to the problem; see
Table

2
for an overview.
The first method is doing nothing, i.e., treating quantities in the contaminated finite market
\widehat{\mathscr{M}}_{\alpha}
as estimators of those in
{\mathscr{M}}_{0}
.
This approach suffers from a significant level of bias, roughly of order
\Theta(\alpha)
, and the bias does not vanish as the number of items increases.
The second method is that, since we know whether an item comes from
the contamination supply
s^{\prime}
, we could throw away the market equilibrium
\widehat{\mathscr{M}}_{\alpha}
,
collect the good items, and compute a finite market equilibrium formed by the good items via solving the EG program in
Eq.

. This approach has a vanishing bias but is computationally heavy, especially in real-world advertising platforms where there are millions of buyers.
Our proposed approach reduces bias to the order
o(\alpha)
and does not require solving large-scale EG programs, by utilizing the equilibrium structure of the observed market
\widehat{\mathscr{M}}_{\alpha}
.

4.1.
A Debiased Surrogate for Pacing Multipliers

If we view
\beta^{*}_{\alpha}
as a function of the level of contamination
\alpha
, then one can imagine that under sufficient regularity conditions, the pacing multipliers in the perfectly separated market,
\beta^{*}_{0}
, can be approximated by some form of Taylor expansion of
\alpha\mapsto\beta^{*}_{\alpha}
at
\alpha
.
This can be made rigorous by the notion of directional derivatives. We define

(5)

\displaystyle\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\beta^{*}(\alpha)=\lim_{\delta\to 0^{+}}\frac{\beta^{*}_{\alpha-\delta}-\beta^{*}_{\alpha}}{\delta}\;.

if the limit exists.
We will show that
\beta^{*}_{\alpha}+\alpha\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\beta^{*}(\alpha)
serves as a good approximation to
\beta^{*}
.

We now show that the directional derivative
\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\beta^{*}(\alpha)
has a closed-form expression under certain regularity conditions (the conditions are given in
Theorem

1
; the full proof is given in the appendix). We need a few notations for this expression.
Define

\Delta_{\alpha}=\int\nabla f(\theta,\beta^{*}_{\alpha})(s-s^{\prime})\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\theta.

Let
{\mathscr{H}}_{\alpha}=\nabla_{\beta\beta}^{2}\int F(\theta,\beta^{*}_{\alpha})s_{\alpha}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\theta
be the Hessian matrix in the market
{\mathscr{M}}_{\alpha}
and
P_{\alpha}={\operatorname*{Diag}}(1(\beta^{*}_{\alpha,i}<1))

regularity conditions,

(6)

\displaystyle\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\beta^{*}(\alpha)=-(P_{\alpha}{\mathscr{H}}_{\alpha}P_{\alpha})^{\dagger}\Delta_{\alpha}\;.

We present a heuristic argument for
Eq.

6
here; for a rigorous treatment we need a perturbation theory result which is given in
Lemma

1
in the appendix.
Define the contaminated EG objective
H_{\alpha}(\beta)=\int F(\beta,\theta)s_{\alpha}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\theta
. Then
{\mathscr{H}}_{\alpha}=\nabla^{2}H_{\alpha}(\beta^{*}_{\alpha})
. The map
(\beta,\alpha)\mapsto H_{\alpha}(\beta)
has gradient
[\nabla H(\beta^{*}),\int F(\beta^{*}_{\alpha},\theta)(s^{\prime}-s)\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\theta]^{\scriptscriptstyle\mathsf{T}}
and Hessian
[{\mathscr{H}}_{\alpha},-\Delta_{\alpha};-\Delta_{\alpha}^{\scriptscriptstyle\mathsf{T}},0]
at
(\beta^{*}_{\alpha},\alpha)
.
Then we have the following quadratic approximation of
H_{\alpha-\delta}(\beta)

\displaystyle H_{\alpha-\delta}(\beta)\approx H_{\alpha}(\beta^{*}_{\alpha})+\begin{pmatrix}\beta-\beta^{*}_{\alpha}\\
-\delta\end{pmatrix}^{\scriptscriptstyle\mathsf{T}}\begin{pmatrix}\nabla H_{\alpha}(\beta^{*}_{\alpha})\\
\int F(\beta^{*}_{\alpha},\theta)(s^{\prime}-s)\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\theta\end{pmatrix}+\frac{1}{2}\begin{pmatrix}\beta-\beta^{*}\\
-\delta\end{pmatrix}^{\scriptscriptstyle\mathsf{T}}\begin{pmatrix}{\mathscr{H}}_{\alpha}&-\Delta_{\alpha}\\
-\Delta_{\alpha}^{\scriptscriptstyle\mathsf{T}}&0\end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix}\beta-\beta^{*}\\
-\delta\end{pmatrix}\;.

And so

\displaystyle\beta^{*}_{\alpha-\delta}=\argmin_{\beta\in(0,1]^{n}}H_{\alpha-\delta}(\beta)\approx\argmin_{\beta\in(0,1]^{n}}(\nabla H_{\alpha}(\beta^{*}_{\alpha})+\delta\Delta_{\alpha})^{\scriptscriptstyle\mathsf{T}}\beta+\tfrac{1}{2}(\beta-\beta^{*}_{\alpha})^{\scriptscriptstyle\mathsf{T}}{\mathscr{H}}_{\alpha}(\beta-\beta^{*}_{\alpha})\;,

where we dropped terms that do not involve
\beta
.

Next we need two observations. For
\delta
small enough, we have
\beta^{*}_{\alpha-\delta,i}=1
if
\beta^{*}_{\alpha,i}=1
.
Intuitively, buyers in the market
{\mathscr{M}}_{\alpha}
with
\beta^{*}_{\alpha,i}=1
have some nonzero leftover budget, which acts as a buffer to small changes in supply, and thus their pacing multipliers remain one.
Next, we also note
\beta\mapsto\nabla H_{\alpha}(\beta^{*}_{\alpha})^{\scriptscriptstyle\mathsf{T}}\beta
is constant on the set
\{\beta:{\beta_{i}}=1\text{ if }\beta^{*}_{\alpha,i}=1\}
by complementary slackness.
Then we further simplify and obtain

\displaystyle\beta^{*}_{\alpha-\delta}\approx\argmin_{{\beta_{i}}=1\text{ if }\beta^{*}_{\alpha,i}=1}\delta\Delta_{\alpha}^{\scriptscriptstyle\mathsf{T}}\beta+\tfrac{1}{2}(\beta-\beta^{*}_{\alpha})^{\scriptscriptstyle\mathsf{T}}{\mathscr{H}}_{\alpha}(\beta-\beta^{*}_{\alpha})\;.

The right-hand side is just a linearly constrained quadratic optimization problem, which admits a closed-form solution
\beta^{*}_{\alpha}-\delta(P_{\alpha}{\mathscr{H}}_{\alpha}P_{\alpha})^{\dagger}\Delta_{\alpha}
.
Now we define a debiased pacing multiplier

(7)

\displaystyle\widetilde{\beta}^{*}=\beta^{*}_{\alpha}+\alpha\cdot(-(P_{\alpha}{\mathscr{H}}_{\alpha}P_{\alpha})^{\dagger}\Delta_{\alpha})\;.

Theorem 1 (Analysis of Bias).

Suppose that in the market
{\mathscr{M}}_{0}
conditions

and

Then the directional derivative

•

The Hessian
. For simplicity in our theoretical results, we will simply assume a generic Hessian estimator

4.3.
Asymptotic Normality and Inference

We present two asymptotic normality results.
In the first result, we require a
stronger condition on the Hessian error rate
\eta_{t}
.
In particular, as will be shown in
Theorem

3
, the rate condition
\eta_{t}=o(1/\sqrt{t})
is sufficient for normality.
One could use a separate large historical dataset to obtain a good estimate of the Hessian matrix.
In the second result, we
impose an additional condition on market structure which simplifies the Hessian expression and facilitates efficient Hessian estimation.
To describe the additional market structure, we define the gap between the highest and the second-highest bid for an item
\theta
under pacing
\beta
by

\displaystyle{\mathsf{{bidgap}}}(\beta,\theta)=\max\{{\beta_{i}}{v_{i}(\theta)}\}-\operatorname{secondmax}\{{\beta_{i}}{v_{i}(\theta)}\}\;,

where
\operatorname{secondmax}
is the second-highest entry
potentially equal to the highest; e.g.,
\operatornamewithlimits{secondmax}([1,1,2])=1
.
When there is a tie for an item
\theta
under pacing
\beta
, we have
{\mathsf{{bidgap}}}(\beta,\theta)=0
.
When there is no tie for an item
\theta
, the gap
{\mathsf{{bidgap}}}(\beta,\theta)
is strictly positive.

For any
g:\Theta\to{\mathbb{R}}
, let
{\mathbb{E}}_{\alpha}[g]=\int gs_{\alpha}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\theta
and
{\operatorname{Cov}}_{\alpha}(g)={\mathbb{E}}_{\alpha}[(g-{\mathbb{E}}_{\alpha}[g])(g-{\mathbb{E}}_{\alpha}[g])^{\scriptscriptstyle\mathsf{T}}]
.
Recall
\widetilde{\beta}^{*}
is the debiased surrogate in
Eq.

7
and
\widehat{\beta}
is its estimator defined in
Eq.

8
.

We need to introduce a few more notations to describe the normality results. First, let

\displaystyle D_{\alpha}=-(P_{\alpha}{\mathscr{H}}_{\alpha}P_{\alpha})^{\dagger}\nabla f({\cdot},\beta^{*}_{\alpha})\;,

As mentioned previously, the pacing multipliers in the contaminated market converge to the limit counterpart and have the representation

\displaystyle\sqrt{t}(\beta^{\gamma}_{\alpha}-\beta^{*}_{\alpha})=\frac{1}{\sqrt{t}}{\sum_{\tau=1}^{t}}(D_{\alpha}({\theta^{\tau}})-{\mathbb{E}}_{\alpha}[D_{\alpha}({\theta^{\tau}})])+o_{p}(1)\;.

In the statistics literature, the function
D_{\alpha}({\cdot})-{\mathbb{E}}[D_{\alpha}]
is called the influence function
(
van der Vaart 2000
)
.
For our debiased estimators, we need the following (uncentered) influence functions.

\displaystyle IF_{1}(\theta)=\big(\tfrac{1}{1-\alpha}1_{\Theta_{\mathsf{good}}}(\theta)\big)D_{\alpha}(\theta)\;,

\displaystyle IF_{2}(\theta)=IF_{1}(\theta)-2\alpha{\operatorname*{Diag}}(\beta^{*}_{\alpha,i}\Delta_{\alpha,i}b_{i}^{{-1}})D_{\alpha}(\theta)\;.

Parallel A/B Test under Interference

In this section, we use the theory from the previous sections to formulate a statistical inference theory for parallel A/B tests under interference.

6.2.
Semi-synthetic experiments

We perform semi-synthetic experiments almost in the same way as with fully synthetic ones. The difference is that values are sampled from historical bidding data, making the budget and value distributions heavy-tailed. For each auction, we sample a given number of advertisers with their per-impression bids. 7.
Conclusion

We have proposed a practical experimental design for performing concurrent A/B tests in large-scale ad auction markets, using a submarket clustering approach, and showed that in production experiments, this submarket clustering approach leads to strong sign consistency performance, as compared to A/B testing on the full market, while allowing significantly-higher A/B test throughput.
In order to model the potential for interference between submarket A/B tests, we introduced a theoretical model of statistical inference in first-price pacing equilibrium problems, under settings with supply contamination. We showed how one can perform statistical inference in such a setting using a debiased estimation procedure, and studied the statistical properties of this procedure.
We then showed how our model of statistical inference in FPPE with contamination can be used to model the submarket clustering parallel A/B test design, and gave theoretical guarantees on performance.
Finally, we presented numerical experiments on fully synthetic and semi-synthetic data derived from Meta ad auctions. The experiments showed that our proposed debiased estimator achieves smaller biases and its statistical coverage on realistic data is generally in line with the predictions from our theory.
