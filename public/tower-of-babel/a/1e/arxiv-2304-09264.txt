1.
Introduction and motivation

The problem of finding all integer solutions of the Diophantine equation

x^{2}+7=2^{n}

is a classical one. In fact, the above equation is the famous Ramanujan-Nagell equation. Ramanujan conjectured that the equation has only five solutions corresponding to
n=3,4,5,7,15

[
8
]
. This was proved by Nagell in
[
6
]
. Today, by a Ramanujan-Nagell equation we understand a general equation of the form

x^{2}+D=aQ^{n},

where
a,Q,D\in\mathbb{Z}
are fixed. The theory of such equations is quite well developed and we have many explicit results concerning form of solutions of particular equations or the number of integer solutions (see for example
[
2
,
3
]
and references given therein). It is clear that one can consider even more general equation of the form

(1)

f(x)=aQ^{n},

where
f
is a given polynomial with rational coefficients and
a,Q\in\mathbb{Z}
are fixed. We know that if
f
has at least two roots in
\overline{\mathbb{Q}}
, then (
1
) has only finitely many solutions in integers
x,n
. In fact, even if we allow
Q
to vary, then, under mild conditions on
f
, the equation (
1
) has only finitely many solutions in integers
x,Q,n
. More on this line of research, or to be more precise, the realm of this type of Diophantine equations, can be found in the monograph
[
9
]
.

Let us observe that the result concerning the solvability of (
1
), where
a,Q
are fixed, can be phrased in a different way. More precisely, let us consider the set

\mathcal{G}(a,Q)=\{aQ^{i}:\;i\in\mathbb{N}\},

where
a,Q\in\mathbb{Q},aQ(Q^{2}-1)\neq 0
, i.e., an infinite set of values of geometric progression with initial term equal to
a
and the quotient equal to
Q
, and the set
\mathcal{W}_{f}=\{f(x):\;x\in\mathbb{Z}\}
- the set of values of the polynomial
f
. In other words, the equation (
1
) is solvable in integers if and only if
\mathcal{G}(a,Q)\cap\mathcal{W}_{f}
is non-empty. From our discussion it follows that if
f\in\mathbb{Q}[x]
, then the only possibility for
\mathcal{G}(a,Q)\cap\mathcal{W}_{f}
to be infinite is when
f
is a power of a linear polynomial. Moreover, if
f
is linear polynomial then it is not difficult to construct infinitely many pairs
a,Q\in\mathbb{Z}
such that the stronger condition
\mathcal{G}(a,Q)\subset\mathcal{W}_{f}
holds. Indeed, let
f(x)=Ax+B
, where
A,B
are fixed and observe that for each
t,T\in\mathbb{Z}
we have
\mathcal{G}(f(t),AT+1)\subset\mathcal{W}_{f}
. This is a consequence of the identity
f(v_{n})=f(t)(AT+1)^{n}
, where
v_{0}=f(t)
and for
n\geq 1
we have
v_{n}=(AT+1)v_{n-1}+BT
.

Let us change a perspective and consider polynomials with more variables. Then, it is possible that
\mathcal{G}(a,Q)
is contained in the set of values of
f
even if the degree of
f
is
\geq 2
. For example, if
p\equiv 1\mkern 4.0mu({\operator@font mod}\mkern 6.0mu4)
is a prime number, then the set
\mathcal{G}(1,p)=\{p^{n}:\;n\in\mathbb{N}\}
is contained in the set of values of the polynomial
x^{2}+y^{2}
. This simple observation motivated us to consider, for a given
f\in\mathbb{Q}(x,y)
, the set

\mathcal{V}_{f}=\{f(u,v)\in\mathbb{Q}:\;u,v\in\mathbb{Q}\;\mbox{for which}\;f(u,v)\;\mbox{determined}\}

and study the following general problem.

Problem 1.1
.

Let
f\in\mathbb{Q}(x,y)
be given. Does there exist rational numbers
a,Q
such that
\mathcal{G}(a,Q)\subset\mathcal{V}_{f}
?

Let us note that the above problem in certain cases can be seen as a variant of a well studied problem. More precisely, if
f\in\mathbb{Z}[x,y]
is a homogenous form of degree
d\geq 3
, then for given
a,Q\in\mathbb{Z}
the equation
f(x,y)=aQ^{i}
is a special case of a so called Thue-Mahler equation. It particular, it has only finitely many solutions
(x,y,i)
in co-prime integers
x,y
and
i\in\mathbb{N}
. This was proved by Mahler in
[
5
]
(generalizing an earlier result of Thue
[
10
]
concerning the equation
f(x,y)=A
, where
A\in\mathbb{Z}\setminus\{0\}
is fixed). However, we do not assume that
x,y
are integers. This assumption makes the problem more interesting and, in certain cases, allows to use the techniques typically applied in Diophantine problems where there is no real restriction between rational and integral solutions (like in case of homogenous problems).

Let us describe the content of the paper in some details. In Section
2
we present several classes of rational functions for which Problem
1.1
has a solution. We also introduce a distinction between solutions of Problem
1.1
in the case of weighted homogenous functions of degree
d
. In Section
3
we give some observations concerning Problem
1.1
for the rational function
f(x,y)=(y^{2}-x^{3})/x
and shows connections with the existence of rational points on certain elliptic curve (Theorem
3.1
). As a consequence of our investigations, we prove that for each
a\in\{1,\ldots,10^{3}\}
such that the elliptic curve
y^{2}=x^{3}+ax
has positive rank, there are infinitely many values of
Q\in\mathbb{Q}
such that
Q
is not a square and
\mathcal{G}(a,Q)\subset\mathcal{V}_{f}
. Finally, in the last section we consider Problem
1.1
for the rational function
f(x,y)=(y^{2}-x^{3})/x
from a computational point of view. We present results of various computer experiments and formulate several questions, problems and conjectures which may stimulate further research .

During our computational experiments we extensively used two computational packages: the latest version of PARI/GP
[
7
]
(in case of rank computations)
[
7
]
and Mathematica 13.2 (for symbolic computations and data manipulation)
[
13
]
.

2.
Basic results

In this section we present certain classes of rational functions such that for each element
f
of an appropriate class there are values
a,Q\in\mathbb{Q}
such that
\mathcal{G}(a,Q)\subset\mathcal{V}_{f}
.

Theorem 2.1
.

Let
g_{1},g_{2},h_{1},h_{2}\in\mathbb{Q}(y)
and suppose that
g_{1}h_{2}\neq h_{1}g_{2}
in
\mathbb{Q}(y)
. Let

f(x,y)=\frac{xg_{1}(y)+g_{2}(y)}{xh_{1}(y)+h_{2}(y)}.

Then, for any
a\in\mathbb{Q}\setminus\{0\}
and
Q\neq\pm 1
we have
\mathcal{G}(a,Q)\subset\mathcal{V}_{f}
.

Proof.

Let
a,Q
be given. For given
i\in\mathbb{N}
we need to find rational numbers
x_{i},y_{i}
such that
f(x_{i},y_{i})=aQ^{i}
for
i=0,1,\ldots
. From our assumptions, for any
i\in\mathbb{N}
, we can chose
y_{i}\in\mathbb{Q}
such that
aQ^{i}h_{1}(y_{i})-g_{1}(y_{i})\neq 0
. Then, it is enough to take

x_{i}=\frac{g_{2}(y_{i})-aQ^{i}h_{2}(y_{i})}{aQ^{i}h_{1}(y_{i})-g_{1}(y_{i})}

and note that
f(x_{i},y_{i})=aQ^{i}
for
i\in\mathbb{N}
.
∎

Theorem 2.2
.

Let
f(x,y)=ux^{2}+vxy+wy^{2}\in\mathbb{Q}[x,y]\setminus\{0\}
. There are infinitely many pairs
a,Q\in\mathbb{Q}
such that
\mathcal{G}(a,Q)\subset\mathcal{V}_{f}
is infinite.

Proof.

Multiplying
f
by
4u
we get that
4uf(x,y)=(2ux+vy)^{2}+(4uw-v^{2})y^{2}
. We substitute
a=4ua^{\prime}
and write
X=2ux+vy,Y=y,d=4uw-v^{2}
. Thus, the problem of finding
a,Q\in\mathbb{Q}
such that
\mathcal{G}(a,Q)\subset\mathcal{V}_{f}
is equivalent with the problem of finding
a^{\prime},Q\in\mathbb{Q}
such that
\mathcal{G}(a^{\prime},Q)\subset\mathcal{V}_{F}
, where
F(x,y)=x^{2}+dy^{2}
. To get the result we take
a^{\prime}=r^{2}+ds^{2},Q=u^{2}+dv^{2}
, where
r,s,u,v\in\mathbb{Q}
are chosen in such a way that
rsuvF(r,s)F(u,v)\neq 0
. Then, noting that the form
F
is multiplicative, we get the result.
∎

Theorem 2.3
.

Let
f(x,y)=\frac{f_{1}(x,y)}{f_{2}(x,y)}
, where
f_{1},f_{2}\in\mathbb{Z}[x,y]
are homogenous forms of degrees
d_{1},d_{2}
, respectively. Let us assume that
|d_{1}-d_{2}|=1
. Then, for any
Q\in\mathbb{Q}\setminus\{-1,0,1\}
we have
\mathcal{G}(a,Q)\subset\mathcal{V}_{f}
if and only
a=f(u,v)
for some
u,v\in\mathbb{Q}
.

Proof.

Without loss of generality we can assume that
d_{1}-d_{2}=1
. If
\mathcal{G}(a,Q)\subset\mathcal{V}_{f}
, then for some
u,v\in\mathbb{Q}
we have
a=f(u,v)
. To get the implication in the other side, we take
x_{i}=uQ^{i},y_{i}=vQ^{i}
, where
u,v
are rational parameters. Then we have

f(x_{i},y_{i})=\frac{f_{1}(uQ^{i},vQ^{i})}{f_{2}(uQ^{i},vQ^{i})}=\frac{Q^{d_{1}i}f_{1}(u,v)}{Q^{d_{2}i}f_{2}(u,v)}=Q^{i}f(u,v).

Thus, if
a=f(u,v)
for some
u,v\in\mathbb{Q}
such that
f(u,v)\neq 0
we have
f(x_{i},y_{i})=aQ^{i}
for each
i\in\mathbb{N}
. Consequently
\mathcal{G}(a,Q)\subset\mathcal{V}_{f}
.
∎

Remark 2.4
.

Theorem
2.3
is a special (easily) provable part of the following general observation. Suppose that
f\in\mathbb{Q}(x,y)
is weighted homogeneous of degree
d
, i.e., there is a pair
(w_{1},w_{2})
of integers, called weights, with such a property that for a variable
\lambda
, we have
f(\lambda^{w_{1}}x,\lambda^{w_{2}}y)=\lambda^{d}f(x,y)
. Thus, to find
a,Q\in\mathbb{Q}
such that
\mathcal{G}(a,Q)\subset\mathcal{V}_{f}
it is enough to prove that we have
\{a,aQ,\ldots,aQ^{d-1}\}\subset\mathcal{V}_{f}
. This is clear, if
f(x_{i},y_{i})=aQ^{i}
for
i=0,1,\ldots,d-1
, then
f(x_{i}Q^{w_{1}j},y_{i}Q^{w_{2}j})=aQ^{i+dj}
for
j\in\mathbb{Z}
and thus
\mathcal{G}(a,Q)\subset\mathcal{V}_{f}
.

Note that a rational function
f
is homogenous of degree
d
if and only if is weighted homogenous of degree
d
with weights
(w_{1},w_{2})=(1,1)
.

Example 2.5
.

Let
f(x,y)=x^{3}+y^{3}
. We find that
\mathcal{G}(13797,2)\subset\mathcal{V}_{f}
which follows from observation made in Remark
2.4
and the equalities

f(-3,24)=13797,\quad f(-13,31)=2\cdot 13797,\quad f(-33,45)=2^{2}\cdot 13797.

Let us also note that the problem of finding
a,Q
such that
\mathcal{G}(a,Q)\subset\mathcal{V}_{f}
can be related to the Problem
1.1
with different rational function
f
. Indeed, let us recall that the curve
C:\;x^{3}+y^{3}=A
is birationally equivalent with the elliptic curve in Weierstrass form
E:\;y^{2}=x^{3}-432A^{2}
. We have the maps

\displaystyle\varphi:

\displaystyle\;C\ni(x,y)\mapsto\left(\frac{36A+y}{6x},\frac{36A-y}{6x}\right)\in E,

\displaystyle\psi:

\displaystyle\;E\ni(x,y)\mapsto\left(\frac{12A}{x+y},\frac{36A(y-x)}{x+y}\right)\in C.

In other words, if
g(x,y)=y^{2}-x^{3}
, then we have

\mathcal{G}(a,Q)\subset\mathcal{V}_{f}\Longleftrightarrow\mathcal{G}(-432a^{2},Q^{2})\subset\mathcal{V}_{g}.

Let us consider
a=6,Q=7
, i.e., for
i=0,1,2
we consider the curve
E_{i}:\;y^{2}=x^{3}-432\cdot 6^{2}\cdot 7^{2i}
. One can check that the point
S_{i}
lies on the curve
E_{i}
for
i=0,1,2
, where

S_{0}=(28,80),\;S_{1}=(172,2080),\;S_{2}=(2353,113975).

Then, we have
x_{i}^{3}+y_{i}^{3}=6\cdot 7^{i}
for the corresponding points
P_{i}=\psi(S_{i})=(x_{i},y_{i})
, where

P_{0}=\left(\frac{37}{21},\frac{17}{21}\right),\;P_{1}=\left(\frac{449}{129},-\frac{71}{129}\right),\;P_{2}=\left(\frac{124559}{14118},-\frac{103391}{14118}\right).

As a consequence we get the following

Corollary 2.6
.

Let
\mathcal{C}=\{x^{3}+y^{3}:\;x,y\in\mathbb{Z}\}\subset\mathbb{Z}
. The set
\mathcal{C}
contains arbitrarily long geometric progressions.

Proof.

Let
n\in\mathbb{N}_{+}
be given and
f(x,y)=x^{3}+y^{3}
. We proved that the set
\mathcal{G}(6,7)\subset\mathcal{V}_{f}
. Let
x_{i},y_{i}\in\mathbb{Q}\setminus\{0\}
satisfy
f(x_{i},y_{i})=6\cdot 7^{i}
for
i\in\mathbb{N}
. Let
D_{n}
be a non-zero integer such that
D_{n}\prod_{i=0}^{n}(x_{i}y_{i})\in\mathbb{Z}
. Then the numbers
X_{i}=D_{n}x_{i},Y_{i}=D_{n}y_{i}
are integers and
we have

f(X_{i},Y_{i})=6D_{n}^{3}7^{i}\quad\mbox{for}\quad i=0,1,\ldots,n,

i.e., the set
\mathcal{C}
contains geometric progression
\{6D_{n}^{3}7^{i}:\;i=0,\ldots,n\}
and the result follows.
∎

In the light of the proof of Corollary
2.6
on can ask the following

Question 2.7
.

Do there exist integers
a,Q
such that for each
i\in\mathbb{N}
there is a co-prime solution in integers
x,y
of the Diophantine equation
x^{3}+y^{3}=aQ^{i}
?

We should also note that in the case of
f\in\mathbb{Q}(x,y)
which is weighted homogenous of degree
d
with weights
(w_{1},w_{2})
, the Problem
1.1
has a trivial solution. Indeed, for each
Q\in\mathbb{Q},Q(Q^{2}-1)\neq 0
, and any
u,v\in\mathbb{Q}
such that
f(u,v)\neq 0
, we have
\mathcal{G}(f(u,v),Q^{d})\subset\mathcal{V}_{f}
. This is simple consequence of the identity

f(uQ^{iw_{1}},vQ^{iw_{2}})=f(u,v)Q^{id}.

Thus, in case of weighted homogenous rational function
f
of degree
d
, we need to consider various notions of solutions of Problem
1.1
.

Definition 2.8
.

Let
f\in\mathbb{Q}(x,y)
be weighted homogenous of degree
d
. We say that Problem
1.1
has

(1)

a trivial solution
(a,Q)
if and only if
Q
is a
d
-th power of a rational number and
\mathcal{G}(a,Q)\subset\mathcal{V}_{f}
;

(2)

a non-trivial solution
(a,Q)
of level
e
if and only if
e|d,e<d,Q
is
e
-th power of a rational number, there is at least one prime
q
such that the
q
-adic valuation of
Q
is equal to
e
(or
-e
), and
\mathcal{G}(a,Q)\subset\mathcal{V}_{f}
;

(3)

a proper solution
(a,Q)
if and only if
(a,Q)
is a non-trivial solution of level
1
.

We already observed that for each weighted homogenous rational function
f
, Problem
1.1
has a trivial solution. On the other hand, for each
f
from the class of homogenous rational functions of degree 1, we know, via Theorem
2.3
, that Problem
1.1
has a proper solution. The same is true for the function
f(x,y)=x^{3}+y^{3}
. In the next section we will investigate the existence and properties of proper solutions of Problem
1.1
for
f(x,y)=(y^{2}-x^{3})/x
. However, before we do that we note the following.

Theorem 2.9
.

Let
d\in\mathbb{N}
be odd.

(1)

Let
f(x,y)=(y^{2}-x^{d})/x
. For each
Q\in\mathbb{Q}\setminus\{-1\}
which is not a square, there are infinitely many values of
a\in\mathbb{Z}
such that
(a,Q^{d-1})
is a non-trivial solution of level
d-1
of Problem
1.1
.

(2)

Let
f(x,y)=y^{2}-x^{d}
. For each
Q\in\mathbb{Q}\setminus\{-1\}
which is not a square, there are infinitely many values of
a\in\mathbb{Z}
such that
(a,Q^{d})
is a non-trivial solution of level
d
of Problem
1.1
.

Proof.

We start with the case
f(x,y)=(y^{2}-x^{d})/x
. In this case
f
is weighted homogenous of degree
2(d-1)
with weights
(2,d)
.
Let us note that to get the statement
\mathcal{G}(a,Q^{d-1})\subset\mathcal{V}_{f}
it is enough to prove that there exist rational solutions of the system of equations

f(x_{0},y_{0})=a,\quad f(x_{1},y_{1})=aQ^{d-1}

in variables
a,x_{i},y_{i},i=0,1
. Equivalently, by eliminating
a
, we consider the equation

f(x_{1},y_{1})=f(x_{0},y_{0})Q^{d-1}.

It can be easily solved via the substitution
x_{i}=p_{i}T,y_{i}=q_{i}T^{m}
, where
m=(d-1)/2,p_{i},q_{i}
for
i=0,1
are rational variables and we look for an appropriate specialization of
T
. Indeed, after the substitution, our equation reduces to the equation (in one variable
T
) of the form

T^{d}[p_{0}p_{1}(p_{1}^{d-1}-p_{0}^{d-1}Q^{d-1})T+p_{1}q_{0}^{2}Q^{d-1}-p_{0}q_{1}^{2}]=0.

Solving the equation defined by the second factor, we get an appropriate value of
T
, together with the corresponding value of
a
in the following form

T=\frac{p_{0}q_{1}^{2}-p_{1}q_{0}^{2}Q^{d-1}}{p_{0}p_{1}(p_{1}^{d-1}-p_{0}^{d-1}Q^{d-1})},\quad a=f(x_{0},y_{0})=f(p_{0}T,q_{0}T^{m})=\frac{T^{d-2}(q_{0}^{2}-p_{0}^{d}T)}{p_{0}}.

As a consequence of our reasoning we see that by taking
X_{i}=x_{i\mkern 4.0mu({\operator@font mod}\mkern 6.0mu2)}Q^{2\lfloor\frac{i}{2}\rfloor},Y_{i}=x_{i\mkern 4.0mu({\operator@font mod}\mkern 6.0mu2)}Q^{d\lfloor\frac{i}{2}\rfloor}
we get
f(X_{i},Y_{i})=aQ^{(d-1)i}
for
i\in\mathbb{N}
, and thus
\mathcal{G}(a,Q^{d-1})\subset\mathcal{V}_{f}
. If
Q
is not a square then our solution is non-trivial.

To get the statement for
f(x,y)=y^{2}-x^{d}
we note that
f
is weighted homogenous of degree
2d
with weights
(2,d)
. It is clear that to prove that
\mathcal{G}(a,Q^{d})\subset\mathcal{V}_{f}
it is enough to solve the system

f(x_{0},y_{0})=a,\quad f(x_{1},y_{1})=aQ^{d}.

or equivalently, the equation
f(x_{1},y_{1})=f(x_{0},y_{0})Q^{d}
. Using the same substitutions and the same reasoning as in the case of the function
(y^{2}-x^{d})/x
we get the corresponding values of
T
and
a
in the following form

T=\frac{q_{0}^{2}-Q^{d}q_{1}^{2}}{p_{0}^{d}-Q^{d}p_{1}^{d}},\quad a=f(x_{0},y_{0})=f(p_{0}T,q_{0}T^{m})=T^{d-1}(q_{0}^{2}-p_{0}^{d}T).

∎

3.
Geometric progressions in the set
\mathcal{V}_{f}
for
f(x,y)=\frac{y^{2}-x^{3}}{x}
and related elliptic curves

In the previous section we obtained some general result concerning Problem
1.1
. Now we concentrate on a specific rational function. Let us put

f(x,y)=\frac{y^{2}-x^{3}}{x}.

We know that
f
is weighted-homogenous of degree 4 with weights
(2,3)
and from the first part of Theorem
2.9
, for each
Q\in\mathbb{Q}
which is not a square we have
\mathcal{G}(a,Q^{2})\subset\mathcal{V}_{f}
non-trivially. Before we will go on, let us note that without loss of generality one can assume that
a,Q\in\mathbb{Z}
. Indeed, if
a=p/q,Q=u/v
, then by multiplication of the equality
y^{2}=x^{3}+aQ^{i}x
by
q^{6}v^{6i}
we easily get the equivalence

\mathcal{G}(a,Q)\subset\mathcal{V}_{f}\;\Longleftrightarrow\;\mathcal{G}(pq^{3},uv^{3})\subset\mathcal{V}_{f}.

Moreover, let us note that for any
t\in\mathbb{Q}
we have:
(a,Q)
is a solution of Problem
1.1
if and only if
(at^{4},Q)
is a solution of Problem
1.1
.

Although the choice of
f
may be seen arbitrary, it is not. This follows from the fact that for any given
A\neq 0
the curve
f(x,y)=A
is an elliptic curve, and the existence of
a,Q
such that
\mathcal{G}(a,Q)\subset\mathcal{V}_{f}
is equivalent with the property that for each
i\in\mathbb{N}
, the elliptic curve

E_{i}(a,Q):\;y^{2}=x^{3}+aQ^{i}x

contains rational point with
x\neq 0
. Actually, in the sequel we require that the curve has a positive rank. This connection with elliptic curves suggest several interesting questions.

It is not difficult to find an example of rational numbers
a,Q
such that
\mathcal{G}(a,Q)
is infinite and
\mathcal{G}(a,Q)\subset\mathcal{V}_{f}
. Indeed, let us take
Q=2
and
a=47
. We thus consider elliptic curves
E_{i}(47,2):\;y^{2}=x^{3}+47\cdot 2^{i}x
. One can compute the rank of
E_{i}(47,2)
and the set of generators of infinite order. We collect the computed data in the table below.

.

As a consequence we get that
\mathcal{G}(47,2)\subset\mathcal{V}_{f}
. In the light of this result one can consider related problem. More precisely, let
a\in\mathbb{Q}\setminus\{0\}
be given and assume that the elliptic curve
E:\;y^{2}=x^{3}+ax
has a positive rank. Is it possible to find a
Q\in\mathbb{Z}\setminus\{-1,0,1\}
which is fourth power-free and the elliptic curve
E_{i}(a,Q)
has positive rank for
i=0,1,2,3
? We prove the following:

Theorem 3.1
.

Let
a\in\mathbb{Z}\setminus\{0\}
and suppose that the elliptic curve
E:\;y^{2}=x^{3}+ax
has a positive rank. Let us consider the elliptic curve

\mathcal{E}_{a}(p,v):\;Y^{2}=X(X-f_{1}(a,p,v))(X-f_{2}(a,p,v))=:F(X),

where

\displaystyle f_{1}

\displaystyle=ap^{3}(p^{2}+a)^{3}v^{4},

\displaystyle f_{2}

\displaystyle=ap(p^{2}+a)((p^{2}+a)pv^{2}-1)((p^{2}+a)pv^{2}+1).

If there are
p,v\in\mathbb{Q}
such that
\mathcal{E}_{a}(p,v)
has a positive rank, then there are infinitely many values of fourth power-free integers
Q
which are not squares and such that
\mathcal{G}(a,Q)\subset\mathcal{V}_{f}
.

Proof.

It is clear that we are interested in finding solutions of the following system of Diophantine equations

(2)

\frac{y_{1}^{2}-x_{1}^{3}}{ax_{1}}=Q,\quad\frac{y_{2}^{2}-x_{2}^{3}}{ax_{2}}=Q^{2},\quad\frac{y_{3}^{2}-x_{3}^{3}}{ax_{3}}=Q^{3}

in variables
x_{i},y_{i},i=1,2,3
and
Q
. To find a solution we make the following substitution

(3)

x_{1}=v^{2},\;y_{1}=y,\quad x_{2}=pQ,\;y_{2}=qQ^{2},\quad x_{3}=rQ^{2},\;y_{3}=sQ^{3}

which reduces the system (
2
) to the system

(4)

Q=g_{1}(v,y)=g_{2}(p,q)=g_{3}(r,s),

where

g_{1}(v,y)=\frac{y^{2}-v^{6}}{av^{2}},\quad g_{2}(p,q)=\frac{p^{3}+ap}{q^{2}},\quad g_{3}(r,s)=\frac{ar}{s^{2}-r^{3}}.

We take
r=ap(p^{2}+a)
and observe that the equation
g_{2}(p,q)=g_{3}(r,s)
defines a quadric in variables
(q,s)
which contains rational points at infinity and can be parameterized in the following way

q=-\frac{a^{3}p^{3}u^{2}-a^{3}-3a^{2}p^{2}-3ap^{4}-p^{6}}{2au},\quad s=\frac{a^{3}p^{3}u^{2}+a^{3}+3a^{2}p^{2}+3ap^{4}+p^{6}}{2u}.

With
q,r,s
chosen in this way we left with one equation
g_{1}(v,y)=g_{2}(p,q(p,u))
. If we treat
p,v
as a rational independent variables, then this equation defines the curve in the plane
(u,Y)
, over the field
\mathbb{Q}(p,v)
, of the form

\displaystyle\mathcal{C}_{a}:\;(

\displaystyle a^{3}p^{3}u^{2}-(p^{2}+a)^{3})^{2}y^{2}=

\displaystyle a^{6}p^{6}v^{6}u^{4}-2a^{3}p(p^{2}+a)(p^{2}(p^{2}+a)^{2}v^{4}-2)v^{2}u^{2}+(p^{2}+a)^{6}v^{6}=:F_{a}(p,v,u).

As a consequence of our reasoning, we see that each point
(u,Y)
lying on the curve
\mathcal{C}_{a}
, together with corresponding values of
q,r,s
defined above, lead to the value of
Q
in the following form

Q=Q_{a}(p,u)=\frac{4a^{2}p(p^{2}+a)u^{2}}{(a^{3}p^{3}u^{2}-(p^{2}+a)^{3})^{2}}.

The genus of the curve
\mathcal{C}_{a}
is equal to 1. Moreover,
\mathcal{C}_{a}
contains
\mathbb{Q}(p,v)
-rational point
(0,(p^{2}+a)^{3}v^{3})
. Thus, the curve
\mathcal{C}_{a}
is birationally equivalent with a curve given by an appropriate Weierstrass equation. In fact,
\mathcal{C}_{a}
is birationally equivalent with the curve
\mathcal{E}_{a}:\;Y^{2}=X(X-f_{1}(p,v))(X-f_{2}(p,v))
from the statement of our theorem, via an appropriate invertible map
\varphi:\;\mathcal{C}_{a}\ni(u,Y)\mapsto(X,Y)\in\mathcal{E}_{a}
. We do not present the form of the map because it is quite complicated (but can be easily computed using standard method of finding birational model of a quartic curve of the form
y^{2}=Au^{4}+Bu^{3}+Cu^{2}+Du+q^{2}
with rational point
(0,q)
, see
[
11
, Theorem 2.17]
).

Let us observe that the discriminant of
F_{a}
with respect to
u
is given by

D_{a}(p,v)=\operatorname{Disc}_{u}(F_{a}(p,v,u))=2^{12}a^{18}p^{10}(a+p^{2})^{10}v^{20}(p^{2}(p^{2}+a)^{2}v^{4}-1)^{2}.

Because we are interested in values of
p=p_{0},v=v_{0}
such that
p_{0}(p_{0}^{2}+a)
is not a square it is clear that
D_{a}(p_{0},v_{0})\neq 0
and the corresponding curve
\mathcal{C}_{a}(p_{0},v_{0})
is non-singular. This is also true for the curve
\mathcal{E}_{a}(p_{0},v_{0})
.

If now
p_{0},v_{0}\in\mathbb{Q}
are chosen in such a way, that
f_{1}(p_{0},v_{0})f_{2}(p_{0},v_{0})\neq 0
and the elliptic curve
\mathcal{E}_{a}(p_{0},v_{0})
obtained from
\mathcal{E}_{a}
after specialization
p=p_{0},v=v_{0}
, has infinitely many rational points, then we get infinitely many rational values of
Q
. However, to finish the proof we need to show that if
\mathcal{E}_{a}(p_{0},v_{0})
has infinitely many rational points, then we get infinitely many essentially different values of
Q
. More precisely, we prove that there is an infinite set
\mathcal{U}\subset\mathbb{Q}
such that for each
u^{\prime},u^{\prime\prime}\in\mathcal{U}
the quotient
Q_{a}(p_{0},u^{\prime})/Q_{a}(p_{0},u^{\prime\prime})
is not a fourth power. We will construct the sequence of finite sets
(\mathcal{U}_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
recursively in the following way. Let us fix a point
P_{0}=(X_{0}^{\prime},Y_{0}^{\prime})
of infinite order on
\mathcal{E}_{a}(p_{0},v_{0})
and write
(u_{n},Y_{n})=\varphi^{-1}(nP_{0})\in\mathcal{C}_{a}(p_{0},v_{0})(\mathbb{Q})
. Let us note that the point
(u,Y)\in\mathcal{C}_{a}(p_{0},v_{0})
such that
Q_{a}(p_{0},u)/Q_{a}(p_{0},u_{0})
is a fourth power, lie on the variety

\mathcal{W}_{a}(u_{0}):\;Q_{a}(p_{0},u)=Q_{a}(p_{0},u_{0})T^{4},\quad Y^{2}=F_{a}(p_{0},v_{0},u),

which, as an intersection of two surfaces, defines a curve over
\mathbb{Q}
. One can check that the genus of
\mathcal{W}_{a}(u_{0})
is equal to 5, and thus there are only finitely many
u
’s which give a rational point. Thus, there is a positive integer
N_{0}
such that for all
N\geq N_{0}
the quotient
Q_{a}(p_{0},u_{n})/Q_{a}(p_{0},u_{0})
is not a fourth power. We thus take
\mathcal{U}_{0}=\{u_{0}^{\prime},u_{1}^{\prime}\}
, where
u_{0}^{\prime}=u_{0},u_{1}^{\prime}=u_{N_{0}}
. Let us suppose that we constructed the set
U_{n-1}=\{u_{0}^{\prime},u_{1}^{\prime},\ldots,u_{n}^{\prime}\}\subset\{u_{i}:\;i\in\mathbb{N}\}
with required properties, i.e., for each
i,j\in\{0,\ldots,n\},i\neq j
the quotient
Q_{a}(p_{0},v_{0},u_{i}^{\prime})/Q_{a}(p_{0},v_{0},u_{j}^{\prime})
is not a fourth power. To find
u_{n+1}^{\prime}
we note that for each
i\in\{0,\ldots,n\}
the curve
\mathcal{W}_{a}(u_{i})
has only finitely many rational solutions
(u,T,Y)
. Thus, there is an integer
N_{n+1}
such that for all
n\geq N_{n+1}
there is no rational numbers
T,Y
such that
(u_{n},T,Y)
lies on
\mathcal{W}_{a}(u_{i})
for
i=0,\ldots,n
. As a consequence of our reasoning we can take
u_{n+1}^{\prime}=u_{N_{n+1}}
and put
\mathcal{U}_{n+1}=\mathcal{U}_{n}\cup\{u_{n+1}^{\prime}\}
. Our reasoning shows the existence of an increasing sequence
(\mathcal{U}_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
of sets with cardinality
\#\mathcal{U}_{n}=n+1
, each having a required property. To finish the proof, it is enough to take
\mathcal{U}=\lim_{n\rightarrow+\infty}\mathcal{U}_{n}
.
∎

\mathcal{C}^{\prime}_{Q}:\;\begin{cases}\begin{array}[]{lll}p_{1}p_{2}Q(p_{1}^{2}Q-p_{2}^{2})q_{0}^{2}+p_{0}p_{2}(p_{2}^{2}-p_{0}^{2}Q^{2})q_{1}^{2}+p_{0}p_{1}(p_{0}^{2}Q-p_{1}^{2})q_{2}^{2}&=&0,\\

Computational observations, questions and conjectures

In this section we collect results of our computations and formulate some questions and conjectures which may stimulate further research. To compute ranks of several thousands of elliptic curves we used very fast procedure
ellrank
implemented in PARI/GP
[
7

. The function
ellrank
attempts to compute the rank of a given elliptic curve
E
. The function returns the vector

is between
r
and
R
(both included),

is related to the Tate-Shafarevitch group, and
L
is a list of independent, non-torsion rational points on the curve. To init the curve we need to use procedure

gp> E=ellinit([47,0]);

[1, 1, 0, [[289/25, 5712/125]]]

which computes the pair

is an analytic rank of an elliptic curve and

the value of

gp> [0, 7.4090738601929029389865884843731744616]

Thus, invoking Kolyvagin’s
[
4
]
together with Wiles’
[
12
]
result on modularity of elliptic curves over
\mathbb{Q}
, we know that the rank of

is equal to 0.

p_{0}=\frac{3}{4},\quad p_{1}=\frac{1}{4Q},\quad q_{0}=3(3Q^{2}-1)(3Q^{2}+1)u,\quad q_{1}=(1-3Q^{2})(3Q^{2}+1)v.

Then, the corresponding value of
a
is given by

a=a(u,v)=768Q^{3}(3Q^{2}-1)^{2}(3Q^{2}+1)^{2}(3Qu^{2}-v^{2})(3Q^{3}v^{2}-u^{2}).

Now, if we fix
u\in\mathbb{N}
, then for each integer

v\in\left(\frac{1}{Q\sqrt{3Q}}u,\sqrt{3Q}u\right)

we have
a(u,v)>0
. It is possible that for some
u,v\in\mathbb{N}_{+}
the value
a(u,v)
is a fourth power. However, the number of such possibilities is finite because the (projective) curve
z^{4}=a(u,v)
is of genus 3, and has only finitely many primitive solutions. As a consequence we get that

C_{Q^{2}}(x)>c(Q^{2})x^{1/4}

for some computable constant
c(Q^{2})
. However, this bound is far weaker than our expectations.

Question 4.4
.

Let
N\in\mathbb{N}_{+}
be given. Is the set
\bigcap_{Q=2}^{N}C_{Q}
non-empty?

We expect that the answer on the question above is YES. On the other hand, we have the following:

Proposition 4.5
.

We have
\bigcap_{Q=2}^{\infty}C_{Q}=\emptyset
.

Proof.

Let us suppose that the statement is not true and take
a\in\bigcap_{Q=2}^{\infty}C_{Q}
. Without loss of generality we can assume that
a
is fourth power-free. Write
a=q_{1}q_{2}^{2}q_{3}^{3}
, where
q_{1},q_{2},q_{3}
are square-free pairwise co-prime integers. Let us take
Q=q_{1}^{3}q_{2}^{2}q_{3}
and observe that
a\not\in C_{Q}
. Indeed, we note that the curve
E_{1}(a,Q):\;y^{2}=x^{3}+aQx
is isomorphic over
\mathbb{Q}
with the curve
E:\;y^{2}=x^{3}+x
. However, the rank of
E(\mathbb{Q})
is zero and the same is true for
E_{1}(a,Q)(\mathbb{Q})
- a contradiction.
∎
