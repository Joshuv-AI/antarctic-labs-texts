Better Fair than Sorry: Adversarial Missing Sensitive Value Imputation for Fair GNNs
Debolina Halder Lina, Arlei Silva

Abstract
Graph Neural Networks (GNNs) have achieved state-of-the-art results in many relevant tasks where decisions might disproportionately impact specific communities. However, existing work on fair GNNs often assumes that either protected attributes are fully observed or that the missing protected attribute imputation is fair. In practice, biases in the imputation will propagate to the model outcomes, leading them to overestimate the fairness of their predictions. We address this challenge by proposing Better Fair than Sorry (BFtS), a fair missing data imputation model for protected attributes. The key design principle behind BFtS is that imputations should approximate the worst-case scenario for fairness—i.e. when optimizing fairness is the hardest. We implement this idea using a 3-player adversarial scheme where two adversaries collaborate against a GNN-based classifier, and the classifier minimizes the maximum bias. Experiments using synthetic and real datasets show that BFtS often achieves a better fairness × \times accuracy trade-off than existing alternatives.

Introduction

With the increasing popularity of machine learning models for high-stakes decision-making, it has become a consensus that these models carry implicit biases, and biases should be addressed to improve the fairness of algorithmic decisions

Angwin et al. 2022
and Apple credit card

Existing work in fair machine learning has mostly focused on tabular, image, and text data. However, in several applications, data can be naturally modeled as graphs (or networks), representing different objects, their relationships, and attributes. Graph Neural Networks (GNNs) have achieved state-of-the-art results in graph machine learning tasks, including node classification, link prediction, and graph classification
Gilmer et al. 2017
;
Kipf and Welling 2017
. For instance, in the case of link prediction in a professional network, such as LinkedIn, which is a recruiting and networking tool, link recommendations should not be biased against protected groups
Fabbri et al. 2020
;
Current et al. 2022
. However, guaranteeing fairness in graph data is a challenge due to correlations in the connections caused by homophily and influence.

Another challenge for fair machine learning is the pervasiveness of missing data
Little and Rubin 2019
. This is particularly critical because fair algorithms often require knowledge of sensitive attributes that are more likely to be missing due to biases in the collection process or privacy concerns. As an example, the absence of data in census surveys and health surveys is correlated with sensitive attributes such as gender, age, race, etc.
O’Hare and O’Hare 2019
;
Weber et al. 2021
. Missing data is also a concern in networked data, especially when tracking the transmission of diseases
Ghani et al. 1998

As a consequence, many fair machine learning algorithms require the use of missing imputation methods to mitigate their biases, and biases in imputed values might be inadvertently propagated to the final decisions
Mansoor et al. 2022
.

Missing Data Imputation and fairness.
Missing data can be imputed using unconditional mean, reconstruction, preferential attachment, autoencoders, etc.

;

Fairness with missing protected attributes.
These studies are the ones most related to ours. One extreme assumption is the complete unavailability of sensitive information.
Hashimoto et al. 2018
takes into account the worst-case distribution across a given group size, and

In

Problem Definition and Preliminaries

In this section, we formalize the problem addressed in this paper and introduce the Graph Neural Network model, which is our base model for node classification. For simplicity, we only consider binary-sensitive attributes. By modifying the loss function in Eq.

We consider the fair node classification task, where the goal is to learn a classifier

Sensitive attributes missing completely at random (MCR) at a high rate:

The missingness process can be modeled by a Bernoulli distribution

with
\beta
close to
1

is expected to have a high error rate. Thus, the bias

can be underestimated by the fair classification model.

Adversarily missing sensitive attributes:

The missingness process is generated by an adversary that manipulates

to artificially decrease the fairness loss
\mathcal{L}_{A}
. As an example, the adversary might select missing values to flip the predictions of
s_{v}
for nodes that are both in the protected group and the undesired class (
y=0
). These scenarios are particularly relevant in graphs as they can exploit structural graph properties (see Sec.
4.1
).

As an independent imputation cannot be trusted in these scenarios, we propose an interactive (3-player) worst-case imputation model for fair classification described in Sec.
3
.

3
Fairness-Aware Adversarial Missing Data Imputation for Graphs

We introduce a graph learning framework, which we call BFtS (Better Fair than Sorry), that allows fair GNN training in a setting with missing sensitive data. BFtS applies a 3-player adversarial learning scheme, where the fair GNN is trained jointly with two adversaries—one to predict sensitive attributes based on GNN embeddings and another to impute missing values that minimize fairness. As a result, fairness assessments by the GNN are based on the worst-case imputed values generated by the adversarial imputation.

3.1
Proposed Model (BFtS)

Our goal is to learn a fair and accurate GNN
\hat{y}=f_{C}(\mathcal{G},\mathcal{X})
for node classification with missing sensitive values. The proposed solution is model-agnostic and based on adversarial learning. It formulates the imputation model as a second adversary of the GNN.

Figure
3
depicts the flow diagram of the proposed model. The model has three primary components: a missing sensitive attribute imputation GNN
f_{I}
, a node classification GNN
f_{C}
, and a sensitive attribute Deep Neural Net (DNN) prediction
f_{A}
.
f_{C}
takes
\mathcal{X}
and
\mathcal{G}
as inputs and predicts the node labels.
f_{A}
is an adversarial neural network that attempts to estimate the sensitive information from the final layer representations of
f_{C}
to assess the bias. More specifically, it
f_{C}
is assumed to be biased if the adversary
f_{A}
can accurately predict the sensitive attribute information from the representations of
f_{C}
. To train
f_{C}
, we need to know the values of the missing sensitive attributes.
f_{I}
predicts the missing sensitive attributes. It takes
\mathcal{X}
and
\mathcal{G}
as inputs and generates the missing sensitive attributes
\hat{si}
.
f_{I}
aims to generate sensitive values that minimize the fairness of
f_{C}
. Therefore, it works as a second adversary to the model.

We will formalize the architecture of the players, define their loss functions, and describe the training procedure for our approach in the next section.

3.1.1
Player architectures

This section describes the architecture of each player in BFtS.

GNN classifier

:

Node classification model
\hat{y}_{v}=f_{C}(x_{v},\mathcal{G})
implemented using a GNN. We assume that

does not apply sensitive attributes but uses other attributes in

that might be correlated with sensitive ones. The goal of

Sensitive attribute predictor (Adversary 1)

Neural network that applies representations

to predict sensitive attributes as
\hat{sa}_{v}=f_{A}(\mathbf{h}_{v})
.

Missing data imputation GNN (Adversary 2)

Predicts missing sensitive attributes as

. However, besides being accurate,

plays the role of an adversary to

by predicting values that maximize the accuracy of
f_{A}
.

3.1.2
Loss Functions

Here, we describe the loss functions applied in our approach.

Node Classification Loss

Let
\hat{y}=f_{C}(X,G)
be the predictions of
f_{C}
. We apply the cross-entropy loss to
f_{C}
’s predictions as follows:

\mathcal{L}_{C}=-\frac{1}{|\mathcal{V_{L}}|}\sum_{v\in\mathcal{V_{L}}}\hat{y}_{v}log(\hat{y}_{v})+(1-\hat{y}_{v})log(1-\hat{y}_{v}).

(1)

Sensitive Attribute Imputation Loss

Because sensitive attributes tend to be imbalanced in real datasets,
f_{I}
applies the Label-Distribution-Aware Margin (LDAM) loss
Cao et al. 2019
for classification. Let
\hat{si}=f_{I}(X,G)
be the one-hot encoded predictions and
\hat{si}^{s}_{v}
be the output of node
v

\mathcal{L}_{I}=\frac{1}{|\mathcal{V_{S}}|}\sum_{v=1}^{|\mathcal{V}_{S}|}-log\frac{e^{\hat{si}^{s}_{v}-\Delta^{s}}}{e^{\hat{si}^{s}_{v}-\Delta^{s}}+\sum_{k\neq s}e^{\hat{si}^{k}_{v}}}.

(2)

where
\Delta^{j}=C/n_{j}^{\frac{1}{4}}
and
j\in\{0,1\}
.

Here

is a constant independent of the sensitive attribute
s
, and

is the number of samples that belong to
s=j
. The LDAM loss is a weighted version of the negative log-likelihood loss. Intuitively, since
\Delta^{j}
is larger for smaller values of

, it ensures a higher margin for the smaller classes.

Furthermore, BFtS can generate fair outputs when very little or no sensitive information is provided. When no or very little sensitive information is available, (

can impute the sensitive values from the ground truth label

Compared to the model

trained with LDAM loss with limited sensitive information of

5
Conclusion

We present BFtS, a 3-player adversarial learning framework for the imputation of missing sensitive attributes that produce challenging values for graph-based fairness. Our theoretical analysis showed that our approach is more robust to convergence challenges during adversarial training than existing alternatives. We evaluate BFtS using synthetic and real datasets. Our experimental results provide strong evidence that our approach achieves a better fairness
\times
accuracy trade-off than the baselines.

Our work opens several venues for future research. We will investigate methods for estimating the expected fairness based on the uncertainty over missing values. We are also interested in fair graph machine learning under missing links.

Simulation
: We simulate a synthetic graph consisting of
1000
nodes based on the stochastic block model with blocks of sizes
600
and

The protected attribute

s\!\sim\!Bernoulli(p)

and
s\!\sim\!Bernoulli(1-p)
otherwise. The probability
p=0.5

p

1

so that the dataset is unfair. Each node has 20 attributes, of which 8 are considered noisy. The noisy attributes are simulated as

Bail

Jordan and Freiburger 2015
: Contains 18,876 nodes representing people who committed a criminal offense and were granted bail by US state courts between 1990 and 2009. Defendants are linked based on similarities in their demographics and criminal histories. The objective is to categorize defendants as likely to post bail vs. no bail. No-bail defendants are expected to be more likely to commit violent crimes if released. Race information is considered a protected attribute. Features include education, marital status, and profession.

German

Hofmann 1994
: The dataset consists of 1000 nodes and 20 features (e.g., credit amount, savings, profession) representing clients at a German bank. Labels are credit decisions for a person (good or bad credit). The protected attribute is gender. The goal is to predict who should receive credit.

NBA

Dai and Wang 2021
: Dataset based on a Kaggle dataset about the National Basketball Association (US). It contains 403 players as nodes connected based on their Twitter activity. Attributes represent player statistics and nationality is considered the protected attribute. The goal is to predict the players’ salaries (above/under the median).

Pokec

pokec

Baselines

Vanilla: The vanilla model is the basic GNN model trained with cross-entropy loss without any fairness intervention.

Debias
Zhang et al. 2018
: Debias is an adversarial learning method that trains a classifier and an adversary simultaneously. The adversary is trained using the softmax output of the last layer of the classifier. It does not handle missing sensitive data. Hence, we only use
\mathcal{V}_{S}\in\mathcal{V}

FairGNN
Dai and Wang 2021
: FairGNN first uses a GNN to estimate the missing sensitive attributes. It then trains another GNN-based classifier and a DNN adversary. The adversary helps to eliminate bias from the representations learned by the GNN classifier.

RNF
Du et al. 2021
: RNF imputes the missing sensitive attributes by first training the model with generalized cross entropy
Zhang and Sabuncu 2018
. It eliminates bias from the classification head. RNF uses samples with the same ground-truth label but distinct sensitive attributes and trains the classification head using their neutralized representations.

FairVGNN
Wang et al. 2022
: FairVGNN learns fair node representations via automatically identifying and masking sensitive-correlated features. It requires complete sensitive information, so we use the same imputation method as FairGNN for the missing values.

FairSIN
Yang et al. 2024
:
FairSIN learns fair representations by adding additional fairness-facilitating features. It also requires complete sensitive information, so we use the same independent imputation method as FairGNN for the missing values.

Theorem 1

Proof.

From Eq. 7, the min-max objective of BFtS is:

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{\mathbf{h}\sim p(\mathbf{h}|\hat{s}=1)}

\displaystyle[\log(f_{A}(\mathbf{h}))]+\mathbb{E}_{\mathbf{h}\sim p(\mathbf{h}|\hat{s}=0)}[\log(1-f_{A}(\mathbf{h}))]

\displaystyle=\sum_{h}p(\mathbf{h}|\hat{s}=1)\log(f_{A}(\mathbf{h}))+

\displaystyle\sum_{h}p(\mathbf{h}|\hat{s}=0)\log(1-f_{A}(\mathbf{h}))

If we fix
\theta_{C}
and
\theta_{I}
, then the optimal
f_{A}^{*}
is
\frac{p(\mathbf{h}|\hat{s}=1)}{p(\mathbf{h}|\hat{s}=1)+p(\mathbf{h}|\hat{s}=0)}.

By substituting the optimal
f_{A}^{*}
in Eq. 7 we get:

\displaystyle\min_{\theta_{C}}\max_{\theta_{I}}~

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{\mathbf{h}\sim p(\mathbf{h}|\hat{s}=1)}[\log\frac{p(\mathbf{h}|\hat{s}=1)}{p(\mathbf{h}|\hat{s}=1)+p(\mathbf{h}|\hat{s}=0)}]+

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{\mathbf{h}\sim p(\mathbf{h}|\hat{s}=0)}[\log(1-\frac{p(\mathbf{h}|\hat{s}=1)}{p(\mathbf{h}|\hat{s}=1)+p(\mathbf{h}|\hat{s}=0)})]

We further simplify the objective function and get:

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{\mathbf{h}\sim p(\mathbf{h}|\hat{s}=1)}

\displaystyle[\log\frac{p(\mathbf{h}|\hat{s}=1)}{p(\mathbf{h}|\hat{s}=1)+p(\mathbf{h}|\hat{s}=0)}]+

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{\mathbf{h}\sim p(\mathbf{h}|\hat{s}=0)}[\log\frac{p(\mathbf{h}|\hat{s}=0)}{p(\mathbf{h}|\hat{s}=1)+p(\mathbf{h}|\hat{s}=0)}]

\displaystyle=\sum_{h}p(\mathbf{h}|

\displaystyle\hat{s}=1)\log\frac{p(\mathbf{h}|\hat{s}=1)}{p(\mathbf{h}|\hat{s}=1)+p(\mathbf{h}|\hat{s}=0)}+

\displaystyle\sum_{h}p(\mathbf{h}|\hat{s}=0)\log\frac{p(\mathbf{h}|\hat{s}=0)}{p(\mathbf{h}|\hat{s}=1)+p(\mathbf{h}|\hat{s}=0)}

\displaystyle=-\log 4

\displaystyle+2JS(p(\mathbf{h}|\hat{s}=1);p(\mathbf{h}|\hat{s}=0))

By removing the constants, we can further simplify the min-max optimization with optimal
f_{A}^{*}
to:

\min_{\theta_{C}}\max_{\theta_{I}}JS(p(\mathbf{h}|\hat{s}=1);p(\mathbf{h}|\hat{s}=0))

The optimal
f_{I}^{*}
thereby maximizes the following objective:

JS(p(\mathbf{h}|\hat{s}=1);p(\mathbf{h}|\hat{s}=0))

Since
\hat{y}=\sigma(\mathbf{h.w})
,
f^{*}_{I}
generates imputation so that

\min_{\theta_{C}}\sup_{\hat{s}}JS(p(\hat{y}|\hat{s}=1);p(\hat{y}|\hat{s}=0))

The supremum value will make the two probabilities maximally different. Therefore, for the optimal
f^{*}_{I}
we get,

\min_{\theta_{C}}\sup_{\hat{s}}|p(\hat{y}|\hat{s}=1)-p(\hat{y}|\hat{s}=0)|

Let
\Delta DP_{f_{C},f_{I}}=|p(\hat{y}=1|\hat{s}=1)-p(\hat{y}=1|\hat{s}=0)|
, then, the optimal
f^{*}_{I}
generates the worst-case (minimum fairness) imputation so that

\Delta DP_{f_{C}.f^{*}_{I}}\geq\Delta DP_{f_{C},f^{*}_{I}}

∎

Theorem 2

Proof.

From Theorem 1, we get,

\min_{\theta_{C}}\sup_{\hat{s}}|p(\hat{y}|\hat{s}=1)-p(\hat{y}|\hat{s}=0)|

Let us assume that there can be
m
different
\hat{s}
that produce sensitive attributes
\hat{s}_{1},\hat{s}_{2},...,\hat{s}_{m}
. If we let
D_{i}
be the uniform distribution over each
s_{i}
, then BFtS minimizes the
|p(\hat{y}|\hat{s}=1)-p(\hat{y}|\hat{s}=0)|
in the worst case over these different distributions
D_{i}
. Therefore,
f^{*}_{C}
is a minimax estimator. The objective of Eq. 7 with optimal
f^{*}A,f^{*}I
and
f^{*}_{C}
becomes:

\inf_{\hat{y}}\sup_{\hat{s}}|p(\hat{y}|\hat{s}=1)-p(\hat{y}|\hat{s}=0)|

As
f_{C}
is a minimax estimator, the maximal
\Delta DP
of
f_{C}
is minimum amongst all estimators of
s

∎

At the global minima of
f_{C}
,
|p(\hat{y}|\hat{s}=1)-p(\hat{y}|\hat{s}=0)|
will be minimum. Its minimum value is
0
. Therefore,
p(\mathbf{h}|\hat{s}=1)-p(\mathbf{h}|\hat{s}=0)=0
.
The optimal
f^{*}_{C}
achieves demographic parity for the worst case.

Corollary 1

Proof.

If
s^{\prime}
is a separate imputation independent of
f_{C}
and
f_{A}
, then the min max game according to
Dai and Wang 2021
is the following:

\begin{split}\min_{\theta_{C}}\max_{\theta_{A}}~&\mathbb{E}_{\mathbf{h}\sim p(\mathbf{h}|s^{\prime}=1)}[\log f_{A}(\mathbf{h})]\\
&+\mathbb{E}_{\mathbf{h}\sim p(\mathbf{h}|s^{\prime}=0)}[\log(1-f_{A}(\mathbf{h}))].\end{split}

With optimal
f^{*}_{A}
, the optimization problem simplifies to-

\min_{\theta_{C}}-\log 4+JS(p(\mathbf{h}|s^{\prime}=1);p(\mathbf{h}|s^{\prime}=0)).

With independent imputation
s^{\prime}
, the adversary
f_{A}
tries to approximate the lower bound of Jensen Shannon Divergence
Weng 2019
. In BFtS, the adversarial imputation
f_{I}
tries to approximate the upper bound of the JS divergence, and the classifier
f_{C}
tries to minimize the upper bound on the JS divergence provided by the adversarial imputation (see Proposition 1). Therefore,

\displaystyle JS((p(\mathbf{h}|s^{\prime}\!=\!1);p(\mathbf{h}|s^{\prime}\!=\!0)))\!\leq\!JS(p(\mathbf{h}|\hat{s}\!=\!1);p(\mathbf{h}|\hat{s}\!=\!0)).

∎

Convergence of adversarial learning for three players vs two players with independent imputation

Let us consider a scenario when the independent missing sensitive value imputation
Dai and Wang 2021
fails to converge. There are three possible reasons for the convergence to fail:

1.

s^{\prime}
is always
1

2.

s^{\prime}
is always
0

3.

s^{\prime}
is uniform, i.e.
p(\mathbf{h}|s^{\prime}=1)=p(\mathbf{h}|s^{\prime}=0)

In scenario
1
and
2
the supports of
p(\mathbf{h}|s^{\prime}=1)
and
p(\mathbf{h}|s^{\prime}=0)
are disjoint. According to
Dai and Wang 2021
, the optimization for the classifier with an optimal adversary is

\min_{\theta_{C}}-\log 4+2JS(p(\mathbf{h}|s^{\prime}=1);p(\mathbf{h}|s^{\prime}=0))

As the supports of
p(\mathbf{h}|s^{\prime}=1)
and
p(\mathbf{h}|s^{\prime}=0)
are disjoint,
JS(p(\mathbf{h}|s^{\prime}=1);p(\mathbf{h}|s^{\prime}=0))
is always
0
. The gradient of the JS divergence vanishes, and the classifier gets no useful gradient information—it will minimize a constant function. This results in extremely slow training of the node classifier, and it may not converge.

In scenario 3,
\hat{s}
is uniform. Therefore,
p(\mathbf{h}|s^{\prime}=1)
will be equal to
p(\mathbf{h}|s^{\prime}=0)
which results in
JS(p(\mathbf{h}|s^{\prime}=1);p(\mathbf{h}|s^{\prime}=0))
being equal to
0
. Moreover, the classifier may assign all training samples to a single class and yet have an adversary that may not be able to distinguish the sensitive attributes of the samples as
\hat{s}
is random. If the classifier assigns all samples to the majority class, it will achieve low classification loss
L_{C}
along with minimum
L_{A}
. The training of the classifier may get stuck in this local minima (see Eq. 4). This phenomenon is similar to the mode collapse of GANs
Thanh-Tung and Tran 2020
.

Based on Corollary 1, our approach is more robust to the convergence issues described above.

Complexity analysis

BFtS model consists of two GNNs and one DNN. If we consider GCN for training the GNN, the complexity is
\mathcal{O}(L|V|F^{2}+L|E|F)
, where
L
is the number of layers and
F
is the nodes in each layer (We assume that the number of nodes in each layer is the same)

. The complexity is polynomial, and therefore, BFtS is scalable. The complexity of FairGNN
Dai and Wang 2021
is the same as the complexity of BFtS. FairGNN makes an additional forward pass to the trained sensitive attribute imputation network. Therefore, the empirical running time of FairGNN is higher than BFtS as described in the next section.

Ethical Considerations

Some of the ethical considerations related to our work include privacy, fairness, sustainability, and transparency. Our work is motivated by fairness and privacy. More specifically, missing sensitive values might be because users do not want to disclose their private data. Sustainability and transparency are not the focus of our work. However, we believe that existing methods for interpretable machine learning can improve the transparency of our model. The datasets used in our method are all public information and do not disclose private information from users.
