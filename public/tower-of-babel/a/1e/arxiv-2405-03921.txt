Abstract

1.
Introduction

The geometric flow is one of the most powerful tools for understanding the structure of Riemannian manifolds.
In particular, the Yamabe flow is one of the central fields of the theory and has developed rapidly (cf.
[
2
]
,
[
3
]
). Gradient Yamabe solitons are self-similar solutions of the Yamabe flow and expected to be a singularity model.
Therefore, the classification problem is one of the most important ones. To classify gradient Yamabe solitons, there are many studies with curvature assumptions and locally conformally flat conditions (cf.
[
10
]
,
[
8
]
,
[
6
]
and
[
13
]
). These studies give affirmative partial answers to the Yamabe soliton version of the Perelman conjecture:
Under what conditions do nontrivial complete gradient shrinking, steady, and expanding Yamabe solitons with positive (scalar) curvature or with bounded (scalar) curvature become rotationally symmetric? In particular, what natural geometric assumptions, beyond positive or bounded (scalar) curvature, are required?
C. He resolved for steady case (cf.
[
12
]
).
For shrinking case, the author resolved the conjecture (cf.
[
14
]
).

In order to gain a deeper understanding of gradient Yamabe solitons, we consider complete gradient Yamabe solitons without assuming any curvature assumptions.
In this paper, we completely classify nontrivial non-flat three-dimensional complete gradient Yamabe solitons.
We also provide examples of complete expanding gradient Yamabe solitons, which do not appear in the steady and shrinking cases.

The classification of two-dimensional complete gradient Ricci solitons was previously studied by Bernstein and Mettler
[
1
]
, whose approach relies on specific two-dimensional Killing vector fields to reduce the problem to an ordinary differential equation.

In this paper, we provide an essentially different proof from the broader perspective of Yamabe solitons. Instead of using Killing vector fields, we determine the warped product structure from a more general framework. This approach allows us to describe the geometric properties more explicitly.
While expanding solitons in
[
1
]
are classified mainly by their asymptotic behaviors, our theorem provides explicit bounds for the Gaussian curvature
K
using the soliton constant
\lambda
, proving its strict monotonicity and specific ranges such as
\frac{\lambda}{2}<K<0
, or
0<K\leq\frac{1+\lambda}{2}
, or
\frac{1+\lambda}{2}\leq K<0
.

Remark 1.1
.

The original Perelman conjecture
[
15
]
is that any three-dimensional complete noncompact
\kappa
-noncollapsed steady gradient Ricci soliton with positive curvature is rotationally symmetric, which was proven by S. Brendle
[
4
]
(see also
[
5
]
)
.

2.
Preliminary

An
n
-dimensional Riemannian manifold
(M^{n},g)
is called a gradient Yamabe soliton if there exists a smooth function
F
on
M
and a constant
\lambda\in\mathbb{R}
, such that

\nabla\nabla F=(R-\lambda)g,

where
\nabla\nabla F
is the Hessian of
F
, and
R
is the scalar curvature of
M
.
If
F
is constant, then
M
is called trivial.
If
\lambda>0
,
\lambda=0
, or
\lambda<0
, then the Yamabe soliton is called shrinking, steady, or expanding, respectively.

Cao, Sun, and Zhang showed the structure theorem for Yamabe solitons (see also
[
17
]
and
[
8
]
).

Theorem 2.1

(
[
17
]
,
[
6
]
,
[
8
]
)
.

Let
(M,g,F)
be a nontrivial, complete, gradient Yamabe soliton.
Then
(M,g)
is a complete warped product manifold and must take one of the two forms:

(1)

a warped product manifold

([0,+\infty),dr^{2})\times_{{F^{\prime}(r)}^{2}}(\mathbb{S}^{n-1},{\bar{g}}_{S})
,
where
\bar{g}_{S}
is the round metric on
\mathbb{S}^{n-1},
or

(2)

a warped product manifold

(\mathbb{R},dr^{2})\times_{{F^{\prime}(r)}^{2}}\left(N^{n-1},\bar{g}\right),

where the scalar curvature
\bar{R}
of
N
is constant. If the scalar curvature of
M
is nonnegative, then
\bar{R}>0
or
R=\bar{R}=0.

Remark 2.2
.

(1)

For a conformal soliton, that is, a Riemannian manifold
(M,g,F,\varphi)
that satisfies the condition
\nabla\nabla F=\varphi g
, where
\varphi\in C^{\infty}(M)
, Tashiro
[
17
]
provided the structure theorem, and Catino, Mantegazza, and Mazzieri also provided the structure theorem in
[
8
]

(
see also
[
13
]
).

Such manifolds were also studied by Cheeger and Colding
[
9
]
.

(2)

It is shown that
F
depends only on
r
, and in Case (2) of Theorem
2.1
, without loss of generality, we can assume that
\rho(r)=F^{\prime}(r)>0
on
\mathbb{R}
(cf.
[
6
]
see also
[
13
]
).

The Riemannian curvature tensor is defined by

R(X,Y)Z=-\nabla_{X}\nabla_{Y}Z+\nabla_{Y}\nabla_{X}Z+\nabla_{[X,Y]}Z,

for
X,Y,Z\in\mathfrak{X}(M)
, where
\nabla
is the Levi-Civita connection of
M
. The Ricci tensor
R_{ij}
is defined by

R_{ij}=R_{ipjp},
where
R_{ijk\ell}=g(R({\partial_{i},\partial_{j}})\partial_{k},\partial_{\ell}).

The following lemma (cf. Lemma 2.4
[
14
]
) will play a fundamental role in the proof of our results later.

Lemma 2.3
.

Let
(M,g,F,\lambda)
be a nontrivial, complete, gradient Yamabe soliton such that

M=\mathbb{R}\times N^{n-1}
and
g=dr^{2}+{{F^{\prime}(r)}^{2}}\bar{g}
.
If
R\geq\lambda
(resp.
R\leq\lambda
) on
M
, then
R>\lambda
or
R\equiv\lambda
(resp.
R<\lambda
or
R\equiv\lambda
) on
M
.

3.
Classification of three-dimensional complete shrinking gradient Yamabe solitons

In this section, we classify nontrivial, nonflat, three-dimensional complete shrinking gradient Yamabe solitons. In particular, we show that any nontrivial, nonflat, three-dimensional complete shrinking gradient Yamabe soliton is rotationally symmetric.

Theorem 3.1
.

Let
(M,g,F,\lambda)
be a nontrivial, nonflat, three-dimensional complete shrinking gradient Yamabe soliton.
Then
(M,g,F,\lambda)
is either

(1)

([0,+\infty),dr^{2})\times_{|\nabla F|}(\mathbb{S}^{2},{\bar{g}}_{S})
, or

(2)

(\mathbb{R},dr^{2})\times_{|\nabla F|}(\mathbb{S}^{2},{\bar{g}}_{S}).

Proof.

Case (2) of Theorem
2.1
.

In this case, for
a,b,c,d=2,3,\cdots,n,
the curvature tensors are obtained as follows:

(3.1)

\displaystyle R_{1a1b}

\displaystyle=-F^{\prime}F^{\prime\prime\prime}{\bar{g}}_{ab},\quad R_{1abc}=0,

\displaystyle R_{abcd}

\displaystyle=(F^{\prime})^{2}{\bar{R}}_{abcd}+(F^{\prime}F^{\prime\prime})^{2}(\bar{g}_{ad}\bar{g}_{bc}-\bar{g}_{ac}\bar{g}_{bd}),

\displaystyle R_{11}=

\displaystyle-(n-1)\frac{F^{\prime\prime\prime}}{F^{\prime}},\quad R_{1a}=0,

\displaystyle R_{ab}=

\displaystyle\bar{R}_{ab}-((n-2)(F^{\prime\prime})^{2}+F^{\prime}F^{\prime\prime\prime})\bar{g}_{ab},

(3.2)

\displaystyle R=(F^{\prime})^{-2}\bar{R}-(n-1)(n-2)\Big(\frac{F^{\prime\prime}}{F^{\prime}}\Big)^{2}-2(n-1)\frac{F^{\prime\prime\prime}}{F^{\prime}},

where the curvature tensors with bar are the curvature tensors of
(N,\bar{g})
.

By (
3.2
) and the soliton equation
\rho^{\prime}=R-\lambda
, one has

(3.3)

\rho^{2}\rho^{\prime}+\lambda\rho^{2}+2\rho^{\prime 2}+4\rho\rho^{\prime\prime}-\bar{R}=0,

where
\rho(r)=F^{\prime}(r)
.
Assume that
R\geq 0
on
\mathbb{R}
, that is,
\rho^{\prime}\geq-\lambda
. By (
3.3
), we have

\rho^{\prime\prime}\leq\frac{\bar{R}-2\rho^{\prime 2}}{4\rho}.

If
\bar{R}\leq 0
, then the positive function
\rho
is concave. Hence,
\rho
is constant. By (
3.3
), we have
\bar{R}=\lambda\rho^{2}>0,
which is a contradiction. Therefore,
\bar{R}
is positive.

Therefore, we only need to consider the other case, that is,
\rho^{\prime}<-\lambda
on some interval
(r_{1},r_{2})
.
If
r_{2}=+\infty
, then
\rho=0
at some point, which cannot occur because
\rho>0
. Hence,
r_{2}<+\infty
, that is,
\rho^{\prime}=-\lambda
at
r_{2}.
By (
3.3
), we have
\rho^{\prime\prime}=\frac{\bar{R}-2\lambda^{2}}{4\rho}
at
r_{2}
.
If
\bar{R}\leq 0
, then
\rho^{\prime\prime}=\frac{\bar{R}-2\lambda^{2}}{4\rho}<0
at
r_{2}
, which cannot occur because
\rho^{\prime}<-\lambda
on
(r_{1},r_{2})
.
Therefore,
\bar{R}
is positive.
∎

4.
Classification of three-dimensional complete steady gradient Yamabe solitons

In this section, we classify three-dimensional complete steady gradient Yamabe solitons. In particular, we show that any nontrivial, nonflat, three-dimensional complete steady gradient Yamabe soliton is rotationally symmetric.

Theorem 4.1
.

Let
(M,g,F)
be a nontrivial, nonflat, three-dimensional complete steady gradient Yamabe soliton.
Then
(M,g,F)
is one of the following:

(1)

([0,+\infty),dr^{2})\times_{|\nabla F|}(\mathbb{S}^{2},{\bar{g}}_{S})
, or

(2)

(\mathbb{R},dr^{2})\times_{|\nabla F|}(\mathbb{S}^{2},{\bar{g}}_{S})
. In this case, there exists at least one point such that
R=0
.

Proof.

Case (2) of Theorem
2.1
.

Since
(N^{2},{\overline{g}})
is a two-dimensional manifold,

(4.1)

\displaystyle{\overline{R}}_{abcd}

\displaystyle=-\frac{\bar{R}}{2}(\bar{g}_{ad}\bar{g}_{bc}-\bar{g}_{ac}\bar{g}_{bd}).

By (
3.2
) and the soliton equation
R=\rho^{\prime}
, one has

(4.2)

\rho^{2}\rho^{\prime}+2\rho^{\prime 2}+4\rho\rho^{\prime\prime}=\bar{R},

where
\rho(r)=F^{\prime}(r).

By differentiating both sides of (
4.2
), we have

(4.3)

2\rho\rho^{\prime 2}+\rho^{2}\rho^{\prime\prime}+8\rho^{\prime}\rho^{\prime\prime}+4\rho\rho^{\prime\prime\prime}=0.

Assume that
\rho^{\prime}\geq 0
on
\mathbb{R}
. If
\rho^{\prime\prime}\leq 0
on
\mathbb{R}
, then the positive function
\rho
is concave. Hence,
\rho
is constant. By (
4.2
),
\bar{R}=0
. By (
4.1
) and (
3.1
),
M
is flat.
Therefore,
\rho^{\prime\prime}>0
on some interval
(r_{1},r_{2})
.

If
r_{2}=+\infty
, since
\rho^{\prime}\geq 0
,
\rho
tends to infinity as
r\nearrow+\infty
.
Hence, the left-hand side of (
4.2
) tends to infinity as
r\nearrow+\infty
, which cannot occur.

Assume that
r_{2}<+\infty
, that is,
\rho^{\prime\prime}>0
on
(r_{1},r_{2})
and
\rho^{\prime\prime}=0
at
r_{2}.

By (
4.3
), we have
\rho^{\prime\prime\prime}<0
at
r_{2}
.
Hence, one has
\rho^{\prime\prime}<0
on
(r_{2},r_{3})
for some
r_{3}
. By the same argument,
\rho^{\prime}
is monotonically decreasing on
(r_{2},+\infty)
. Without loss of generality, we can assume that the point
r_{2}
is the first critical point of
\rho^{\prime}
.
Since
\rho^{\prime}\geq 0
and
\rho^{\prime\prime}>0
on
(-\infty,r_{2})
, there exists
r_{0}\in(-\infty,r_{2})
such that
\rho^{\prime\prime\prime}=0
. However, this contradicts (
4.3
).

Therefore, we have
\rho^{\prime}<0
on some open interval
(r_{1},r_{2})
.

Assume that
r_{2}=+\infty
, that is,
\rho^{\prime}<0
on
(r_{1},+\infty)
.
If
\rho^{\prime\prime}<0
on
(r_{1},+\infty)
, we have a contradiction, because
\rho>0
.

Assume that there exists
\tilde{r}\in(r_{1},+\infty)
such that
\rho^{\prime\prime}(\tilde{r})=0
. By (
4.3
), we have
\rho^{\prime\prime\prime}(\tilde{r})<0
. Iterating this argument, we see that
\rho^{\prime}
is weakly decreasing on
(\tilde{r},+\infty)
. Hence, one has
\rho^{\prime}<-\beta^{2}
for some nonzero constant
\beta
.
This is a contradiction, because
\rho>0
.

Hence,
\rho^{\prime\prime}>0
on
(r_{1},+\infty)
.
Thus, one has
\rho^{\prime}(r)\nearrow\alpha(\leq 0)
for some constant
\alpha
as
r\nearrow+\infty.
If
\alpha<0
, then we have a contradiction, because
\rho>0
. Hence, one has
\rho^{\prime}\nearrow 0
as
r\nearrow+\infty.

By (
4.2
),
\rho^{\prime\prime}
converges to some constant
c
or tends to
+\infty
as
r\nearrow+\infty
. Since
\rho>0
and
\rho^{\prime}<0
on
(r_{1},+\infty)
, the second case cannot occur, and we have the first case with
c=0
, that is,
\rho^{\prime\prime}\searrow 0
as
r\nearrow+\infty.

By (
4.2
),
\bar{R}
must be
0
.
Assume that
(r_{-2},r_{-1})
is some maximal interval such that
\rho^{\prime}>0
.
By (
4.2
), we have
\rho^{\prime\prime}<0
on
(r_{-2},r_{-1})
. If
\rho^{\prime\prime}=0
at some point, then by (
4.2
), we have
\rho^{\prime}\leq 0
at this point, which means that
r_{-2}
must be
-\infty
. However, since
\rho>0
, this cannot occur.
Hence, we have
\rho^{\prime}\leq 0
on
\mathbb{R}
. Therefore,
R\leq 0
on
M.

Assume that
r_{2}<+\infty
, that is,
\rho^{\prime}=0
at
r_{2}
.

Case 1:
\bar{R}>0
. In this case,

\rho^{\prime\prime}=\frac{\bar{R}}{4\rho}>0,

at
r_{2}
, and hence

\rho^{\prime}>0~~\text{on}~~(r_{2},r_{3}),~~~\text{for some}~~r_{3}.

If
\rho^{\prime}=0
at
r_{3}
, then
\rho^{\prime\prime}>0
at
r_{3}
. Hence,
\rho
is monotonically increasing on
(r_{2},+\infty)
.
If
\rho^{\prime}=0
at
r_{1}
, then
\rho^{\prime\prime}>0
at
r_{1}
, which cannot occur.
Therefore,
\rho^{\prime}<0
on
(-\infty,r_{2})
.

Case 2:
\bar{R}=0
. By the same argument as above, we have
\rho^{\prime}\leq 0
on
\mathbb{R}
.
The equation (
4.2
) becomes

4\rho\rho^{\prime\prime}+2(\rho^{\prime})^{2}+\rho^{2}\rho^{\prime}=0.

This is equivalent to

\frac{d}{dr}\left(2\rho^{1/2}\rho^{\prime}+\frac{1}{5}\rho^{5/2}\right)=0.

Integrating this with respect to
r
yields

2\rho^{1/2}\rho^{\prime}+\frac{1}{5}\rho^{5/2}=C,

where
C
is a constant of integration. Hence, we obtain

\rho^{\prime}=-\frac{1}{10}\rho^{2}+\frac{C}{2}\rho^{-1/2}.

We now consider the sign of the constant
C
.

Case 2-(a):
C\leq 0
.

Since
\rho>0
, we have
C\rho^{-1/2}\leq 0
. Thus, the equation implies

\rho^{\prime}\leq-\frac{1}{10}\rho^{2}.

Dividing by
\rho^{2}
, we obtain
-\frac{\rho^{\prime}}{\rho^{2}}\geq\frac{1}{10}
.
Integrating this inequality from
r
to
r_{0}
(where
r<r_{0}
), we find

\frac{1}{\rho(r_{0})}-\frac{1}{\rho(r)}\geq\frac{1}{10}(r_{0}-r).

Taking the limit as
r\searrow-\infty
, the right-hand side diverges to
+\infty
. However, since
\rho(r)>0
, the left-hand side is strictly bounded from above by
\frac{1}{\rho(r_{0})}
. This is a contradiction.

Case 2-(b):
C>0
.

By the condition
\rho^{\prime}\leq 0
on
\mathbb{R}
, we have

-\frac{1}{10}\rho^{2}+\frac{C}{2}\rho^{-1/2}\leq 0,

which implies

\rho(r)\geq(5C)^{2/5}>0.

This shows that
\rho(r)
is bounded from below by a positive constant.
Since
\rho(r)
is a monotonically decreasing function,
\rho^{\prime}\nearrow 0
and
\rho\searrow\alpha\geq(5C)^{2/5}
as
r\nearrow+\infty
.
Substituting this into the equation
\rho^{\prime}=-\frac{1}{10}\rho^{2}+\frac{C}{2}\rho^{-1/2}
, we find that the limit must be
\alpha=(5C)^{2/5}
.

Next, we consider the limit as
r\searrow-\infty
.
Because
\rho(r)
is monotonically decreasing, as
r\searrow-\infty
,
\rho(r)
either converges to a finite limit or tends to
+\infty
.
If
\rho(r)
converges to a finite limit, by the same argument,
\rho^{\prime}(r)\nearrow 0
as
r\searrow-\infty
and the limit must also be
(5C)^{2/5}
.
Since
\rho
is a monotonic function,
\rho
is constant and
M
is flat, which contradicts the assumption that the soliton is nonflat.

Thus,
\rho(r)
must tend to
+\infty
as
r\searrow-\infty
.
As
\rho\nearrow+\infty
, the term
C\rho^{-1/2}
tends to
0
.
Therefore, there exists a point
r_{1}\in\mathbb{R}
such that for all
r<r_{1}
, we have
\frac{C}{2}\rho^{-1/2}<\frac{1}{20}\rho^{2}
.
For
r<r_{1}
, the differential equation yields

\rho^{\prime}=-\frac{1}{10}\rho^{2}+\frac{C}{2}\rho^{-1/2}<-\frac{1}{10}\rho^{2}+\frac{1}{20}\rho^{2}=-\frac{1}{20}\rho^{2},

which yields

-\frac{\rho^{\prime}}{\rho^{2}}>\frac{1}{20}.

Integrating this inequality from
r
to
r_{1}
(where
r<r_{1}
), we have

\int_{r}^{r_{1}}\left(-\frac{\rho^{\prime}(s)}{\rho(s)^{2}}\right)ds>\int_{r}^{r_{1}}\frac{1}{20}ds,

which gives

\frac{1}{\rho(r_{1})}-\frac{1}{\rho(r)}>\frac{1}{20}(r_{1}-r).

Taking the limit as
r\searrow-\infty
, the right-hand side tends to
+\infty
.
However, the left-hand side is strictly bounded by
\frac{1}{\rho(r_{1})}
. This is a contradiction.

Case 3:
\bar{R}<0
. In this case,
\rho^{\prime\prime}<0
at
r_{2}
. Hence,
\rho^{\prime}<0
on
(r_{2},r_{3})
for some
r_{3}
.
If
r_{3}=+\infty,
by the same argument as above, one has
\bar{R}=0
, which is a contradiction. Hence,
r_{3}<+\infty
, that is,
\rho^{\prime}=0
at
r_{3}
, which means that there exists
\tilde{r}_{2}\in(r_{2},r_{3})
such that
\rho^{\prime\prime}=0
. By (
4.3
),
\rho^{\prime\prime\prime}<0
at
\tilde{r}_{2}
, which cannot occur.
∎

5.
Classification of three-dimensional complete expanding gradient Yamabe solitons

In the study of Ricci solitons, much less is understood about the expanding case compared to the shrinking or steady cases.
A similar phenomenon appears to occur for Yamabe solitons as well.
However, as will become evident in the two-dimensional case discussed later, there are numerous expanding Yamabe solitons, making them an interesting subject from a geometric standpoint.
In this section, we classify nontrivial, nonflat, three-dimensional complete expanding gradient Yamabe solitons.

Theorem 5.1
.

Let
(M,g,F,\lambda)
be a nontrivial, nonflat, three-dimensional complete expanding gradient Yamabe soliton.
Then
(M,g,F,\lambda)
is one of the following:

(1)

([0,+\infty),dr^{2})\times_{|\nabla F|}(\mathbb{S}^{2},{\bar{g}}_{S})
, or

(2)

(\mathbb{R},dr^{2})\times_{|\nabla F|}(\mathbb{S}^{2},{\bar{g}}_{S}),
or

(3)

(\mathbb{R},dr^{2})\times_{|\nabla F|}\mathbb{E}^{2}
with negative scalar curvature, or

(4)

(\mathbb{R},dr^{2})\times_{|\nabla F|}(\mathbb{H}^{2},{\bar{g}}_{H})
with negative scalar curvature,

where
\mathbb{E}^{2}
is the
2
-dimensional Euclidean space, and
(\mathbb{H}^{2},{\bar{g}}_{H})
is a
2
-dimensional hyperbolic space.

Proof.

Case (2) of Theorem
2.1
.

By (
3.2
) and the soliton equation
\rho^{\prime}=R-\lambda
, one has

(5.1)

\rho^{2}R+2(R-\lambda)^{2}+4\rho R^{\prime}-\bar{R}=0.

By differentiating both sides of (
5.1
), we have

(5.2)

2\rho(R-\lambda)R+\rho^{2}R^{\prime}+8(R-\lambda)R^{\prime}+4\rho R^{\prime\prime}=0.

We consider the case
\bar{R}\leq 0
.
Assume that
R>0
on some open interval
(r_{1},r_{2})
. In this case, by (
5.1
), one has
R^{\prime}<0
on
(r_{1},r_{2})
.
If
R=0
at
r_{1}
, then by (
5.1
), one has
R^{\prime}<0
at
r_{1}
, which is a contradiction. Hence,
R>0
and
R^{\prime}<0
on
(-\infty,r_{2})
. Therefore,
\rho
is a strictly monotonically increasing concave function on
(-\infty,r_{2})
. However, since
\rho
is positive, we have a contradiction. Therefore, we have
R(r)\leq 0
on
\mathbb{R}.

Assume that
R=0
at some point
r_{0}
. By (
5.1
),
one has
R^{\prime}<0
at
r_{0}
. By (
5.2
), one has
R^{\prime\prime}>0
at
r_{0}
, which cannot occur. Therefore, we have
R<0
on
\mathbb{R}.

∎

We provide examples of (4) in Theorem
5.1
. For
n
-dimensional complete expanding gradient Yamabe solitons, an elementary argument yields interesting examples that do not appear in the steady and shrinking cases.

Example
.

Let
(N^{n-1},~\bar{g})
be an
(n-1)
-dimensional complete Riemannian manifold with constant negative scalar curvature
\bar{R}
. Then, for any
\alpha\in\mathbb{R}
,
(M,g,F,\lambda)=(\mathbb{R}\times N^{n-1},dr^{2}+\frac{\bar{R}}{\lambda}\bar{g},\sqrt{\frac{\bar{R}}{\lambda}}r+\alpha,\lambda)
is an
n
-dimensional complete expanding gradient Yamabe soliton with
R=\lambda.

In particular,
(M^{3},g,F,\lambda)=(\mathbb{R}\times\mathbb{H}^{2},dr^{2}+\frac{\bar{R}}{\lambda}{\bar{g}}_{H},\sqrt{\frac{\bar{R}}{\lambda}}r+\alpha,\lambda)
is a 3-dimensional complete expanding gradient Yamabe soliton with
R=\lambda
, where
(\mathbb{H}^{2},{\bar{g}}_{H})
is a hyperbolic surface.

6.
Classification of two-dimensional complete gradient Yamabe solitons

In this section, we provide a proof for the classification of nontrivial, nonflat, two-dimensional complete gradient Yamabe solitons.

Theorem 6.1
.

Let
(M^{2},g,F,\lambda)
be a nontrivial, nonflat, two-dimensional complete gradient Yamabe soliton. Then exactly one of the following statements holds:

Steady case:

The manifold
(M^{2},g,F,\lambda)
is Hamilton’s cigar soliton.

Shrinking case:

There are no nontrivial, nonflat, two-dimensional complete gradient Yamabe solitons.

Expanding case:

The manifold
(M^{2},g,F,\lambda)
is one of the following.

(1)

A warped product manifold
(\mathbb{R}\times N^{1},g=dr^{2}+F^{\prime}(r)^{2}d\theta^{2},F,\lambda)
. The Gaussian curvature
K
of
\mathbb{R}\times N^{1}
depends only on
r
, is strictly increasing, and satisfies
\frac{\lambda}{2}<K<0
. Furthermore,
M
is asymptotically flat and
K\searrow\frac{\lambda}{2}
as
r\searrow-\infty.

(2)

A warped product manifold
([0,+\infty)\times\mathbb{S}^{1},g=dr^{2}+F^{\prime}(r)^{2}d\theta^{2},F,\lambda)
with
\lambda>-1
,
F^{\prime}(0)=0
, and
F^{\prime\prime}(0)=1
. The Gaussian curvature
K
of
[0,+\infty)\times\mathbb{S}^{1}
depends only on
r
, is strictly decreasing, and satisfies
0<K\leq\frac{1+\lambda}{2}
. Furthermore,
M
is asymptotically flat.

(3)

A warped product manifold
([0,+\infty)\times\mathbb{S}^{1},g=dr^{2}+F^{\prime}(r)^{2}d\theta^{2},F,\lambda)
with
\lambda<-1
,
F^{\prime}(0)=0
, and
F^{\prime\prime}(0)=1
. The Gaussian curvature
K
of
[0,+\infty)\times\mathbb{S}^{1}
depends only on
r
, is strictly increasing, and satisfies
\frac{1+\lambda}{2}\leq K<0
. Furthermore,
M
is asymptotically flat.

Proof.

By the soliton equation, one has

F^{\prime\prime}(r)=R-\lambda.

By an elementary fact of the curvature of a warped product, one has

R=-2\frac{F^{\prime\prime\prime}}{F^{\prime}}.

Combining these equations, we have

(6.1)

2\rho^{\prime\prime}+\rho\rho^{\prime}+\lambda\rho=0,

where we denote
\rho(r):=F^{\prime}(r).

By differentiating both sides of (
6.1
), we have

(6.2)

2\rho^{\prime\prime\prime}+\rho^{\prime 2}+\rho\rho^{\prime\prime}+\lambda\rho^{\prime}=0.

The equation (
6.1
) is equivalent to

2R^{\prime}+\rho R=0.

We show that
R>0
or
R<0
on
\mathbb{R}
(resp.
[0,+\infty)
).
Assume that there exists
\tilde{r}\in\mathbb{R}
(resp.
\tilde{r}\in[0,+\infty)
) such that
R(\tilde{r})=0
.
Set
A(r)=\int_{\tilde{r}}^{r}\frac{\rho(s)}{2}ds.

Since

\frac{d}{dr}(e^{A(r)}R(r))=e^{A(r)}\left(\frac{1}{2}\rho(r)R(r)+R^{\prime}(r)\right)=0,

we see that
e^{A(r)}R(r)
is constant, say
c
. Thus,
R(r)=ce^{-A(r)}.
Since
A(\tilde{r})=0
, we have
c=R(\tilde{r})=0
. Therefore, we have
R\equiv 0
on
\mathbb{R}
(resp.
[0,+\infty)
). Hence,
M
is flat.

Case (2) of Theorem
2.1
.

Case 1
\lambda=0
.

In this case, it is easy to see that the solutions to (
6.1
) are

\rho(r)=2c_{1}^{2}\tanh\left\{\frac{1}{2}(c_{1}^{2}r+c_{1}^{2}c_{2})\right\},

where
c_{1}
and
c_{2}
are constants. However, this contradicts the assumption that
\rho(r)>0
on
\mathbb{R}
.

Case 2
\lambda>0
.

(I) When
R>0
. By (
6.1
), one has
\rho^{\prime\prime}<0
. Therefore,
\rho
is a positive concave function, which cannot occur.

(II) When
R<0
. We have
\rho^{\prime}<-\lambda
. Since
\rho>0
, we have a contradiction.

Case 3
\lambda<0
.

(I) When
R>0
. By (
6.1
), one has
\rho^{\prime\prime}<0
. Therefore,
\rho
is a positive concave function, which cannot occur.

(II) When
R<0
. By (
6.1
),
\rho^{\prime\prime}>0
. Hence, there exists at most one
r_{0}\in\mathbb{R}
such that
\rho^{\prime}(r_{0})=0
.

(II)-(a) Assume that there exists
r_{0}\in\mathbb{R}\cup\{+\infty\}
such that
\rho^{\prime}<0
on
(-\infty,r_{0})
.
(This covers both the case where
\rho^{\prime}(r_{0})=0
for some finite
r_{0}
, and the case where
\rho^{\prime}<0
on all of
\mathbb{R}
.)
Because
\rho
is positive, strictly decreasing, and convex on
(-\infty,r_{0})
, we have
\rho(r)\nearrow+\infty
as
r\searrow-\infty
.
By (
6.1
), we have
2\rho^{\prime\prime}=-\rho\rho^{\prime}-\lambda\rho
. Since
\lambda<0
and
\rho>0
, we obtain the strict inequality

2\rho^{\prime\prime}>-\rho\rho^{\prime}.

Integrating this inequality from
r
to
r_{1}
(where
r<r_{1}<r_{0}
), we obtain

2\rho^{\prime}(r_{1})-2\rho^{\prime}(r)>-\frac{1}{2}\rho(r_{1})^{2}+\frac{1}{2}\rho(r)^{2}.

Thus, we have

-2\rho^{\prime}(r)>\frac{1}{2}\rho(r)^{2}-C,

where
C=\frac{1}{2}\rho(r_{1})^{2}+2\rho^{\prime}(r_{1})
is a constant.
Since
\rho(r)\nearrow+\infty
as
r\searrow-\infty
, for
r
sufficiently negative (say,
r<r_{2}<r_{1}
), we have
\frac{1}{2}\rho(r)^{2}-C>\frac{1}{4}\rho(r)^{2}>0
.
Thus, we get
-2\rho^{\prime}(r)>\frac{1}{4}\rho(r)^{2}
, which can be rewritten as

-\frac{\rho^{\prime}(r)}{\rho(r)^{2}}>\frac{1}{8}.

Integrating this inequality from
r
to
r_{2}
yields

\frac{1}{\rho(r_{2})}-\frac{1}{\rho(r)}>\frac{1}{8}(r_{2}-r).

Letting
r\searrow-\infty
, we have a contradiction.

(II)-(b) Assume that
\rho^{\prime}>0
on
\mathbb{R}
.
Since
\rho^{\prime}>0
and
\rho^{\prime\prime}>0
, we have
\rho\nearrow+\infty
as
r\nearrow+\infty.
We show
\rho^{\prime}\nearrow-\lambda
as
r\nearrow+\infty.
If
\rho^{\prime}\nearrow c(<-\lambda)
as
r\nearrow+\infty
, then by (
6.1
) one has
\rho^{\prime\prime}\nearrow+\infty
as
r\nearrow+\infty
.
Since
\rho^{\prime}<-\lambda
, we have a contradiction.
Hence, we obtain
\rho^{\prime}\nearrow-\lambda
as
r\nearrow+\infty
. Therefore,
M
is asymptotically flat.

Set
s:=-r
. Then, we have
\dot{\rho}(s)=-\rho^{\prime}(r)<0
and
\ddot{\rho}(s)>0

(s\in\mathbb{R}).

Since
\lambda<\dot{\rho}<0
and
\ddot{\rho}>0
, we have
\dot{\rho}\nearrow\beta
as
s\nearrow+\infty
for some constant
\beta\leq 0
. If
\beta\not=0
, then since
\rho>0
, we have a contradiction. Hence, we have
\dot{\rho}\nearrow 0
as
s\nearrow+\infty
. Therefore, we have
\rho^{\prime}\searrow 0
as
r\searrow-\infty
, that is,
R\searrow\lambda
as
r\searrow-\infty.

Case (1) of Theorem
2.1
.

In this case, we have two cases. (A)
\rho>0
on
(0,+\infty)
,
\rho(0)=0
,
\rho^{\prime}(0)=1
, and
\rho^{(\text{even})}(0)=0
, or (B)
\rho<0
on
(0,+\infty)
,
\rho(0)=0
,
\rho^{\prime}(0)=-1
, and
\rho^{(\text{even})}(0)=0
(cf. Sections 1.4.4 and 4.3.4 in
[
16
]
)

Case 1
\lambda=0
.

(A)
\rho>0
on
(0,+\infty)
.

In this case, it is easy to see that the solutions to (
6.1
) are

\rho(r)=2c_{1}^{2}\tanh\left\{\frac{1}{2}(c_{1}^{2}r+c_{1}^{2}c_{2})\right\},

where
c_{1}
and
c_{2}
are constants.
Since
\rho(0)=0,
one has

c_{1}=0~\text{or}~c_{2}=0.

If
c_{1}=0
, then one has
\rho(r)\equiv 0
, which is a contradiction.

If
c_{2}=0
, then one has

F(r)=4\log\left(\cosh\left(\frac{c_{1}^{2}}{2}r\right)\right)+C,

where
C
is a constant. Since
\rho^{\prime}(0)=1
, we have
c_{1}^{2}=1
and

F(r)=4\log\left(\cosh\left(\frac{r}{2}\right)\right)+C.

It is Hamilton’s cigar soliton.

By the same argument, Case (B) cannot occur.

Case 2
\lambda>0
.

(A)
\rho>0
on
(0,+\infty)
.

(A)-(I) When
R>0
. Since
\rho^{\prime}>-\lambda
and
\rho^{\prime\prime}<0
,
\rho^{\prime}\searrow\alpha
as
r\nearrow+\infty
, for some constant
\alpha
. Assume that
\alpha<0
. Since
\rho>0
, we have a contradiction. Hence,
\alpha\geq 0
. Therefore,
\rho^{\prime}>0
on
(0,+\infty)
.

Assume that
\rho\nearrow c^{2}
as
r\nearrow+\infty
for some constant
c
. Then one has
\rho^{\prime}\searrow 0
as
r\nearrow+\infty
.
Hence, by (
6.1
),
\rho^{\prime\prime}
tends to
-\frac{\lambda}{2}c^{2}
as
r\nearrow+\infty
. Since
\rho^{\prime}>0
, we have a contradiction. Therefore,
\rho\nearrow+\infty
as
r\nearrow+\infty.

By (
6.1
),
\rho^{\prime\prime}\searrow-\infty
as
r\nearrow+\infty
, which is a contradiction, because
\rho^{\prime}>0
on
(0,+\infty)
.

(A)-(II) When
R<0
. Since
\rho^{\prime}<-\lambda
and
\rho>0
, we have a contradiction.

(B)
\rho<0
on
(0,+\infty)
.

(B)-(I) When
R>0
.
Since
\rho^{\prime}>-\lambda
and (
6.1
), we have
\rho^{\prime\prime}>0
on
(0,+\infty)
. Hence,
\rho^{\prime}
is monotonically increasing. Since
\rho<0
, there exists no point in
(0,+\infty)
at which
\rho^{\prime}=0
. Thus,
\rho^{\prime}<0
on
(0,+\infty)
. By (
6.2
),
\rho^{\prime\prime\prime}>0
on
(0,+\infty)
, which yields contradiction, because
\rho<0
on
(0,+\infty)
.

(B)-(II) When
R<0
. Since
\rho^{\prime}<-\lambda<0
and (
6.1
), we have
\rho^{\prime\prime}<0
on
(0,+\infty)
. Hence,
\rho^{\prime}
is monotonically decreasing and
\rho\searrow-\infty
as
r\nearrow+\infty
. By (
6.2
), we also have
\rho^{\prime\prime\prime}<0
on
(0,+\infty)
, which yields
\rho^{\prime}\searrow-\infty
as
r\nearrow+\infty
. By (
6.1
), we have

2R^{\prime}+\rho R=0.

From this,
R<0
, and
\rho^{\prime}<0
, we have

2(R^{\prime}-\lambda(\ln(-R))^{\prime})=-\frac{1}{2}(\rho^{2})^{\prime}.

Integrating both sides, we obtain

2R-2\lambda\ln(-R)=-\frac{1}{2}\rho^{2}+C,

where
C
is a constant. Since
-R\nearrow+\infty
as
r\nearrow+\infty
, for sufficiently large
\tilde{r}
, we have

\ln(-R)<-R,

on
(\tilde{r},+\infty)
, which yields

2R+2\lambda R<-\frac{1}{2}\rho^{2}+C,

on
(\tilde{r},+\infty)
. Since
R=\rho^{\prime}+\lambda
, the inequality is equivalent to

\rho^{\prime}<-\frac{1}{4(1+\lambda)}\rho^{2}+C,

on
(\tilde{r},+\infty)
. Since
-\rho^{2}\searrow-\infty
, for sufficiently large
\tilde{r}_{1}>\tilde{r}
, we have

\rho^{\prime}<-\frac{1}{100(1+\lambda)}\rho^{2},

on
(\tilde{r}_{1},+\infty)
. Hence, we have

(-\rho^{-1})^{\prime}<-\frac{1}{100(1+\lambda)},

on
(\tilde{r}_{1},+\infty)
. Integrating both sides, we obtain

-\rho^{-1}(r)+\rho^{-1}(\tilde{r}_{1})<-\frac{1}{100(1+\lambda)}(r-\tilde{r}_{1}).

Taking
r\nearrow+\infty
, we have a contradiction.

Case 3
\lambda<0
.

(A)
\rho>0
on
(0,+\infty)
.

(A)-(I) When
R>0
. We have
\rho^{\prime}>-\lambda>0
. By
(
6.1
)
, we have
\rho^{\prime\prime}<0
.
Note that
\rho(0)=0
and
\rho^{\prime}(0)=1
.
Hence,
-1<\lambda
must be satisfied. By integrating both sides of
\rho^{\prime}>-\lambda,
one has

-\lambda r<\rho(r)
. Therefore,
\rho\nearrow+\infty
as
r\nearrow+\infty
. Since
\rho^{\prime}>-\lambda
and
\rho^{\prime\prime}<0
, we have
\rho^{\prime}\searrow\alpha(\geq-\lambda)
as
r\nearrow+\infty
for some constant
\alpha
. If
\alpha\not=-\lambda
, by (
6.1
), we have that
\rho^{\prime\prime}
tends to
-\infty
as
r\nearrow+\infty
, which is a contradiction, because
\rho^{\prime}>-\lambda
. Therefore,
\rho^{\prime}\searrow-\lambda
as
r\nearrow+\infty
, that is,
R\searrow 0
as
r\nearrow+\infty
.

(A)-(II) When
R<0
. We have
\rho^{\prime}<-\lambda
. By (
6.1
), we have
\rho^{\prime\prime}>0
. Since
\rho^{\prime}(0)=1
, we have
\rho^{\prime}>1
on
(0,+\infty)
. Hence,
-1>\lambda
must be satisfied.
Since
\rho
is a monotonically increasing convex function,
\rho\nearrow+\infty
as
r\nearrow+\infty
.
If
\rho^{\prime}\nearrow c(<-\lambda)
as
r\nearrow+\infty
, then by (
6.1
), one has
\rho^{\prime\prime}\nearrow+\infty
as
r\nearrow+\infty
. Since
\rho^{\prime}<-\lambda
, we have a contradiction.
Therefore,
\rho^{\prime}\nearrow-\lambda
as
r\nearrow+\infty
, and
M
is asymptotically flat.

(B)
\rho<0
on
(0,+\infty)
.

(B)-(I) When
R>0
. In this case,
\rho^{\prime}>-\lambda>0
, which yields a contradiction, because
\rho^{\prime}(0)=-1
.

(B)-(II) When
R<0
. We have
\rho^{\prime}<-\lambda
. By (
6.1
), we have
\rho^{\prime\prime}<0
. Since
\rho^{\prime}(0)=-1
,
\rho^{\prime}<-1
holds. Integrating both sides of (
6.1
) from
0
to
r
yields

2\rho^{\prime}+2+\frac{1}{2}\rho^{2}+\int_{0}^{r}\lambda\rho(s)ds=0.

Since
\lambda\rho>0
on
(0,+\infty)
, we have

2\rho^{\prime}+\frac{1}{2}\rho^{2}<0.

Hence, one has

(-\rho^{-1})^{\prime}<-\frac{1}{4}.

Integrating this inequality from
\tilde{r}(>0)
to
r
, we have

-\rho^{-1}(r)+\rho^{-1}(\tilde{r})<-\frac{1}{4}(r-\tilde{r}),

which yields a contradiction, because
\rho<0
on
(0,+\infty)
.
∎

.

For two-dimensional solitons, Theorem
6.1
solves many problems (see for example Page 51 of

]
).
