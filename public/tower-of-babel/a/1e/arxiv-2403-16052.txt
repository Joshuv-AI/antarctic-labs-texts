Abstract
Let A A be a Banach algebra with a right identity u u such that u ​ A uA is commutative and semisimple. In this paper, we investigate symmetric bi-derivations of A A and detremine their range. 1
Introduction

Let
A
be a Banach algebra.
A mapping
D:A\times A\rightarrow A
is called a
symmetric bi-linear
mapping if
D(a,b)=D(b,a)
,

D(\alpha a,b)=\alpha D(a,b)\quad\text{and}\quad D(a+b,c)=D(a,c)+D(b,c).

for all
a,b,c\in A
and
\alpha\in\mathbb{C}
. Also,

D
is called a
symmetric Jordan bi-derivation
if

D
is symmetric bi-linear and

D(a^{2},b)=D(a,b)a+aD(a,b)

for all
a,b\in A
.
Furthermore, if for every
a,b,c\in A
, we have

D(ab,c)=D(a,c)b+aD(b,c),

then
D
is called a
symmetric bi-derivation
.
Clearly, every Jordan bi-derivation is a bi-derivation.
But, the converse is not true, in general.
The mapping
f:A\rightarrow A
defined by

f(a)=D(a,a)

is called the
trace of

D
.

Let
T:A\rightarrow A
be a mapping and
k\in\mathbb{N}
. Then
T
is called

k
-centralizing
(
resp.
k
-skew centralizing
)
if for every
a\in A
,

[T(a),a^{k}]:=T(a)a^{k}-a^{k}T(a)\,\,\,(\text{resp. }\langle T(a),a^{k}\rangle=T(a)a^{k}+a^{k}T(a))

is an element of
Z(A)
, the set of all
a\in A
such that
ax=xa
for all
x\in A
. In particular, if for every
a\in A

[T(a),a^{k}]=0,\,\,\,(\text{resp.}\langle T(a),a^{k}\rangle=0).

then
T
is called
k
-commuting
(resp.
k
-skew commuting
).

Maksa
[
7
,
8
]
introduced and studied symmetric bi-derivations.
Some authors continued this investigation
[
2
,
4
,
13
,
15
,
16
,
17
]
.
For example, vukman
[
16
]
showed that if

D:R\times R\rightarrow R
is a symmetric bi-derivation on a non commutative prime ring of characteristic note two and there
R
such that for every
r\in R
,

[[f(r),r],r]\in Z(R),

then
D
is zero.

In this paper, we always assume that
A
is a Banach algebra with a right identity
u
such that
uA
is commutative and semisimple. We prove that every symmetric bi-derivation of
A
maps
A
into
\hbox{ran}(A)
. We also show that if the trace of a symmetric bi-derivation of
A
is
k
-centralizing, then the symmetric bi-derivation is zero. Finally, we establish that every symmetric Jordan bi-derivation of
A
is a symmetric bi-derivation.

2
Main Results

Before we give the first our result, let us recall that the right annihilator of
A
is denoted by
\hbox{ran}(A)
and it is the set of all
r\in A
such that
ar=0
for all
a\in A
. Note that
\hbox{ran}(A)
is a subset of the radical of
A
.

Theorem 2.1
.

Let
D:A\times A\rightarrow A
be a symmetric bi-derivation. Then the range of
D
is contained into
\emph{ran}(A)
.

Proof.

First, note that the range of
D
is equal to

\cup_{m\in A}\phi_{m}(A)
,
where

\phi_{m}(n)=D(m,n)

for all

n\in A
.
But
\phi_{m}
is a derivation on
A
and so

\phi_{m}(A)\subseteq\hbox{ran}(A)
; see
[
10
]
.
This proves the theorem.
∎

Remark 2.2
.

Let
\mathcal{A}
be a Banach algebra such that
\frac{\mathcal{A}}{\hbox{ran}(A)}
is commutative and semisimple.
Then a similar argument to the proof of Theorem 2.1 shows that the range of any symmetric bi-derivation
D:\mathcal{A}\times\mathcal{A}\rightarrow\mathcal{A}
is contained into

\hbox{ran}(\mathcal{A})
.

Theorem 2.3
.

Let
D:A\times A\rightarrow A
be a symmetric bi-derivation and
f
be the trace of
D
.
Then the following assertions are equivalent.

(a)
There exists
k\in\mathbb{N}
such that
f
is
k
-commuting.

(b)
There exists
k\in\mathbb{N}
such that
f
is
k
-centralizing.

(c)
There exists
k\in\mathbb{N}
such that
f
is
k
-skew commuting.

(d)
There exists
k\in\mathbb{N}
such that
f
is
k
-skew centralizing.

(e)

D=0
.

Proof.

Let
m\in A
and
k\in\mathbb{N}
.
It follows from Theorem 2.1 that

\displaystyle[D(m,m),m^{k}]

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle D(m,m)m^{k}-m^{k}D(m,m)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle D(m,m)m^{k}+m^{k}D(m,m)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\langle D(m,m),m^{k}\rangle.

Thus

[f(m),m^{k}]=\langle f(m),m^{k}\rangle.

So the statements (a)-(d) are equivalent.
Assume now that
f
is
k
-commuting.
Then

(2.1)

\displaystyle f(m)m^{k}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle D(m,m)m^{k}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle D(m,m)m^{k}-m^{k}D(m,m)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle[D(m,m),m^{k}]

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle[f(m),m^{k}]

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle 0

for all
m\in A
.
Hence
f(u)=0
. We also have

\displaystyle f(m+u)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle D(m+u,m+u)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle D(m,m)+2D(m,u)+D(u,u)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle f(m)+2D(m,u)

for all
m\in A
. This together with (2.1) shows that

\displaystyle 0

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle f(m+u)(m+u)^{k}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle(f(m)+2D(m,u))(m+u)^{k}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle(f(m)+2D(m,u))\bigg(\sum_{j=0}^{k-1}\binom{k-1}{j}m^{k-j}+\sum_{j=0}^{n-1}\binom{n-1}{j}um^{k-j-1}\bigg)

for all
m\in A
. This implies that

(2.2)

\displaystyle\sum_{j=1}^{k}\binom{k}{j}f(m)m^{k-j}+2\sum_{j=0}^{k}\binom{k}{j}D(m,u)m^{k-j}=0.

Put

\displaystyle E_{1}(m)=\sum_{\tiny{\begin{array}[]{c}j\,even\\
j=2\end{array}}}^{k}\binom{k}{j}f(m)m^{k-j},\quad E_{2}(m)=2\sum_{\tiny{\begin{array}[]{c}j\,even\\
j=0\end{array}}}^{k}\binom{k}{j}D(m,u)m^{k-j},

\displaystyle O_{1}(m)=\sum_{\tiny{\begin{array}[]{c}j\,odd\\
j=1\end{array}}}^{k}\binom{k}{j}f(m)m^{k-j},\quad O_{2}(m)=2\sum_{\tiny{\begin{array}[]{c}j\,odd\\
j=1\end{array}}}\binom{k}{j}D(m,u)m^{k-j}.

It follows from (2.2) that

(2.5)

E_{1}(m)+E_{2}(m)+O_{1}(m)+O_{2}(m)=0

for all
m\in A
.
If we replace
m
by
-m
in (2.3), then

E_{1}(m)-E_{2}(m)-O_{1}(m)+O_{2}(m)=0

Hence

(2.6)

E_{1}(m)+O_{2}(m)=0

and so

(2.7)

O_{1}(m)+E_{2}(m)=0.

Now, let
k
be even. Then by (2.5), we have

\displaystyle 0

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle O_{1}(m)+E_{2}(m)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{\tiny{\begin{array}[]{c}j\,odd\\
j=1\end{array}}}^{k-1}\binom{k}{j}f(m)m^{k-j}+2\sum_{\tiny{\begin{array}[]{c}j\,even\\
j=0\end{array}}}^{k-2}\binom{k}{j}D(m,u)m^{k-j}+2D(m,u).

Thus for every
r\in\hbox{ran}(A)
, we obtain

(2.9)

D(r,u)=0,

because

f(r)r^{k-j}=D(r,u)r^{k-j}=0.

Since
m-um\in\hbox{ran}(A)
, it follows that

\displaystyle 0

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle D(m-um,u)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle D(m,u)-D(u,u)m-uD(m,u)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle D(m,u)-f(u)m

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle D(m,u)

for all
m\in A
.
Hence
O_{2}=0
and thus

(2.10)

\sum_{\tiny{\begin{array}[]{c}j\,even\\
j=2\end{array}}}^{k}\binom{k}{j}f(m)m^{k-j}=0.

If
i
is even and
2\leq i\leq k-2
, then by
(2.7), we have

\displaystyle 0

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{\tiny{\begin{array}[]{c}j\,even\\
j=2\end{array}}}^{k}\binom{k}{j}f(m)m^{k+i-j}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{\tiny{\begin{array}[]{c}j\,even\\

\displaystyle\sum_{\tiny{\begin{array}[]{c}j\,even\\
j=i+2\end{array}}}^{k}\binom{k}{j}f(m)m^{k+i-j}.

For

.
Hence

\sum_{\tiny{\begin{array}[]{c}j\,even\\
j=4\end{array}}}^{k}\binom{k}{j}f(m)m^{k-j}=0\quad\hbox{ and}\quad\sum_{\tiny{\begin{array}[]{c}j\,even\\

and therefore
D=0
.
Similarly, if
k

D=0
.
∎

Theorem 2.4
.

Let
D:A\times A\rightarrow A
be a symmetric Jordan bi-derivation.
Then
D
is a symmetric bi-derivation.

Proof.

Let
m\in A
and
\phi_{m}
be the function defined as the proof of Theorem 2.1. Then for every
n\in A
, we have

\displaystyle\phi_{m}(n^{2})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle D(m,n^{2})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle D(m,n)n+nD(m,n)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\phi_{m}(n)n+n\phi_{m}(n).

Thus
\phi_{m}
is a Jordan derivation on
A
for all
m\in A
.
Hence,
\phi_{m}
is a derivation on
A
, see Theorem 2.2 of
[
10
]
. Therefore,

\displaystyle D(mk,n)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\phi_{n}(mk)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\phi_{n}(m)k+m\phi_{n}(k)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle D(m,n)k+mD(k,n)

for all
m,n,k\in A
.
That is,
D
is a symmetric bi-derivation.
∎

As an immediate consequence of Theorems 2.1 and 2.4 we have the following result.

Corollary 2.5
.

Let
{\cal A}
be a unital commutative semisimple Banach algebra. Then the zero map is the only symmetric bi-derivation on
{\cal A}
.
