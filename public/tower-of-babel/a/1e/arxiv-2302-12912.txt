A generalized conditional gradient method for multiobjective composite optimization problems
P. B. Assunção, O. P. Ferreira, L. F. Prudente

Abstract




Abstract:

This article deals with multiobjective composite optimization problems that consist of simultaneously minimizing several objective functions, each of which is composed of a combination of smooth and non-smooth functions. To tackle these problems, we propose a generalized version of the conditional gradient method, also known as Frank-Wolfe method. The method is analyzed with three step size strategies, including Armijo-type, adaptive, and diminishing step sizes. We establish asymptotic convergence properties and iteration-complexity bounds, with and without convexity assumptions on the objective functions. Numerical experiments illustrating the practical behavior of the methods are presented.

Keywords:
Conditional gradient method; Frank-Wolfe method; multiobjective optimization; Pareto optimality; constrained optimization problem.

Introduction

Multiobjective optimization problems typically involve the simultaneous minimization of multiple and conflicting objectives.
A solution to the problem leads to a set of alternatives with different trade-offs between the objectives.
In fact, in this scenario, we use the concept of
Pareto optimality
to caracterize a solution.
In summary, a point is called
Pareto optimal
if, with respect to this point, none of the objective functions can be improved without degrading another.
One strategy for computing Pareto points that has become very popular consists of extending methods for scalar-valued optimization to vector-value optimization, rather than using scalarization approaches
[
26
]
.
To the best of our knowledge, this strategy was coined in the work
[
19
]
that proposed the steepest descent methods for unconstrained multiobjective optimization.
Since of then, new properties related to this method have been discovered and several variants of it have been considered, see for example
[
7
,
21
,
24
,
25
,
28
,
29
,
43
]
.
In recent years, there has been a significant increase in the number of papers addressing concepts, techniques, and methods for multiobjective optimization, see for example
[
1
,
9
,
13
,
14
,
18
,
20
,
27
,
40
,
48
,
52
,
55
,
57
]
.

In the present paper, we consider
multiobjective composite optimization problems
, where the objective function
F\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}\mathbb{R}^{n}\to(\mathbb{R}\cup\{+\infty\})^{m}
, given by
F(x)\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}=(f_{1}(x),\ldots,f_{m}(x))
, has the following special separable structure:

f_{j}(x)\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}=g_{j}(x)+h_{j}(x),\quad\forall j=1,\ldots,m,

where, for each
j=1,\ldots,m
,
g_{j}\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}\mathbb{R}^{n}\to\mathbb{R}\cup\{+\infty\}
is proper, convex, and lower semicontinuous, and
h_{j}\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}\mathbb{R}^{n}\to\mathbb{R}
is continuously differentiable.
We define
G\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}\mathbb{R}^{n}\to(\mathbb{R}\cup\{+\infty\})^{m}
by
G(x)\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}=(g_{1}(x),\ldots,g_{m}(x))
and
H\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}\mathbb{R}^{n}\to\mathbb{R}^{m}
by
H(x)\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}=(h_{1}(x),\ldots,h_{m}(x))
, and denote this problem by

\displaystyle\operatorname{min}_{x\in{\mathbb{R}^{n}}}{F(x)\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}=G(x)+H(x)}.

(1.1)

An important instance of (
1.1
) is obtained when
G
is the indicator function (in a vector sense) of a given set
{\cal C}\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}
, i.e., for all
j=1,\ldots,m
,
g_{j}(x)=0
for all
x\in{\cal{C}}
and
g_{j}(x)=+\infty
otherwise.
In this case, (
1.1
) merges into the following constrained multiobjective optimization problem

\displaystyle\operatorname{min}_{x\in{\cal{C}}}{H(x)}.

(1.2)

Furthermore, as discussed in
[
53
]
, the separable structure in (
1.1
) can be used to model robust multiobjective optimization problems, which are problems that include uncertain parameters and the optimization process is considered under the worst scenario.

As far as we know,
[
11
]
was the first paper to deal with problem (
1.1
), where a forward–backward proximal point type algorithm was studied.
In
[
53
]
, a proximal gradient method to solve problem (
1.1
) was proposed, see also
[
8
,
54
]
.
More recently, some Newton-type approaches were considered in
[
2
,
45
]
.
It is worth mentioning that a version of the
conditional gradient method
also known as
Frank-Wolfe algorithm
, see
[
23
,
38
]
, to solve (
1.2
) was proposed and analyzed in
[
3
]
. However, a generalized version of this method to solve (
1.1
) has not yet been considered.

In this paper, a multiobjective version of the scalar generalized conditional gradient method
[
4
,
12
,
49
]
to solve problem (
1.1
) is proposed.
The method is analyzed with three step size strategies, including Armijo-type, adaptive, and diminishing step sizes.
Asymptotic convergence properties and iteration-complexity bounds with and without convexity assumptions on the objective functions are stablished.
Numerical experiments on some robust multiobjective optimization problems illustrating the practical behavior of the method are presented, and comparisons with the proximal gradient method
[
53
]
are discussed.

The organization of this paper is as follows. In Section
2
, some notations, definitions, and auxiliary results used throughout of the paper are presented. Section
3
presents the assumptions on the considered multiobjective composite optimization problem need to our analysis. Moreover, we introduce the gap function associated to problem (
1.1
) and study its main properties. In Section
4
, we introduce a generalization of the conditional gradient method for solving problem (
1.1
). We will also study asymptotic convergence properties and iteration-complexity bounds for the generated sequence by the proposed method. Numerical experiments are presented in Section
5
. Finally, some conclusions are given in Section
6
.

Preliminaries

In this section, we present some notations, definitions, and results used throughout the paper. We denote
{\mathbb{N}}=\{0,1,2,\ldots\}
and
{\mathbb{N}^{*}}=\{1,2,3,\ldots\}
.

\mathbb{R}
,
\mathbb{R}_{+}
, and
\mathbb{R}_{++}
are the set of real numbers, the set of nonnegative real numbers, and the set of positive real numbers, respectively.
We define
\overline{\mathbb{R}}\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}=\mathbb{R}\cup\left\{+\infty\right\}
.
Likewise,
\overline{\mathbb{R}}^{m}\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}=(\mathbb{R}\cup\left\{+\infty\right\})^{m}
.
Let
{\cal J}\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}=\{1,\ldots,m\}
,
{\mathbb{R}}^{m}_{+}\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}=\{u\in{\mathbb{R}}^{m}\mid u_{j}\geq 0,\forall j\in{\cal J}\}
, and
{\mathbb{R}}^{m}_{++}=\{u\in{\mathbb{R}}^{m}\mid u_{j}>0,\forall j\in{\cal J}\}
. For
u,v\in{\mathbb{R}}^{m}
,
v\succeq u
(or
u\preceq v
) means that
v-u\in{\mathbb{R}}^{m}_{+}
and
v\succ u
(or
u\prec v
) means that
v-u\in{\mathbb{R}}^{m}_{++}
.
The symbol
\langle\cdot,\cdot\rangle
is the usual inner product in
\mathbb{R}^{n}
and
\|\cdot\|
denotes the Euclidean norm.
Let
{\cal C}\subset{\mathbb{R}}^{n}
be a convex set. If
{\cal C}
is compact, its
diameter
is the finite number
\operatorname{diam}({\cal C})\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}=\operatorname{max}\left\{\|x-y\|\mid\forall x,y\in{\cal C}\right\}
.
If
\mathbb{K}=\{k_{1},k_{2},\ldots\}\subseteq\mathbb{N}
, with
k_{j}<k_{j+1}
for all
j\in\mathbb{N}
, then we denote
\mathbb{K}\displaystyle\mathop{\subset}_{\infty}\mathbb{N}
.
The notation
\sigma(t)\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}=o(t)
for
t\in\mathbb{R}/\{0\}
means that
\lim_{t\to 0}\sigma(t)/t=0
.

The effective domain of
\psi\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}\mathbb{R}^{n}\to\overline{\mathbb{R}}
is defined as
\mathrm{dom}(\psi)\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}=\left\{x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}\mid\psi(x)<+\infty\right\}.
The function
\psi\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}\mathbb{R}^{n}\to\overline{\mathbb{R}}
is said to be
Lipschitz continuous
with constants
L>0
on
{\cal C}\subset\mathrm{dom}(\psi)
whenever
|\psi(x)-\psi(y)|\leq L\|x-y\|
, for all
x,y\in{\cal C}.

Let
\psi\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}\mathbb{R}^{n}\to\overline{\mathbb{R}}
be a convex function. The
directional derivative
of
\psi
at
x\in\mathrm{dom}(\psi)
in the direction
d\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
is given by
\psi^{\prime}(x;d)\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}=\lim_{\alpha\to 0^{+}}({\psi(x+\alpha d)-\psi(x)})/{\alpha}
. When
\psi
is differentiable at
x\in\mathrm{int}(\mathrm{dom}(\psi))
, we can show that
\psi^{\prime}(x;d)=\left\langle\nabla\psi(x),d\right\rangle
.
The next lemma is a well-known result in convex analysis whose proof can be found in
[
10
, Section 4.1]
.

Lemma 1
.

Let
\psi\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}\mathbb{R}^{n}\to\overline{\mathbb{R}}
be a convex function. Then, the function
\lambda\mapsto(\psi(x+\lambda d)-\psi(x))/\lambda,
is non-decreasing in
(0,+\infty)
. In particular, for all
\lambda\in(0,1]
, we have
(\psi(x+\lambda d)-\psi(x))/{\lambda}\leq\psi(x+d)-\psi(x)
. Consequently,
\psi^{\prime}(x;d)\leq\psi(x+d)-\psi(x).

Definition 1
.

A function
\psi\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}\mathbb{R}^{n}\to\overline{\mathbb{R}}
is upper semicontinuous (u.s.c) at
x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
if, for any sequence
(x^{k})_{k\in\mathbb{N}}
converging to
x
,
\lim\sup_{k\to\infty}\psi(x^{k})\leq\psi(x)
. Likewise,
\psi
is said to be lower semicontinuous (l.s.c) at
x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
whenever
-\psi
is (u.s.c) at
x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}

or, equivalently, if
\lim\inf_{k\to\infty}\psi(x^{k})\geq\psi(x)
.
We say that
\psi
is upper semicontinuous (resp. lower semicontinuous) if it is upper (resp. lower) semicontinuous at every point of its domain.

Let
\Psi\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}\mathbb{R}^{n}\to\overline{\mathbb{R}}^{m}
be a vector-valued function with
\Psi(x)\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}=(\psi_{1}(x),\dots,\psi_{m}(x))
and consider the problem

\displaystyle\operatorname{min}_{x\in{\mathbb{R}^{n}}}{\Psi(x)}.

(2.1)

The
effective domain
of
\Psi
is denoted by
\mathrm{dom}(\Psi)\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}=\left\{x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}\mid\psi_{j}(x)<+\infty,~\forall j\in{\cal J}\right\}
.
A point
x^{*}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
is called a
Pareto optimal point
of (
2.1
) if there exists no other
x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
such that
F(x)\preceq F(x^{*})
and
F(x)\neq F(x^{*})
. In turn,
x^{*}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
is called a
weakly Pareto optimal point
of (
2.1
), if there exists no other
x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
such that
F(x)\prec F(x^{*})
. A
necessary optimality condition
for problem (
2.1
) at a point
{\bar{x}}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
is given by

\operatorname{max}_{j\in{\cal J}}\psi^{\prime}_{j}({\bar{x}},d)\geq 0,\quad\forall~d\in\mathbb{R}^{n}.

(2.2)

A point
\bar{x}\in\mathrm{dom}(\Psi)
satisfying (
2.2
) is called a
Pareto critical point
or a
stationary point
of problem (
2.1
). Given
x,y\in\mathrm{dom}(\Psi)
, it is said that
x
dominates
y
when
\Psi(y)-\Psi(x)\in\mathbb{R}^{m}_{+}\setminus\{0\}
.
The function
\Psi
is said to be
convex
on
{\cal C}
if

\Psi\left(\lambda x+(1-\lambda)y\right)\preceq\lambda\Psi(x)+(1-\lambda)\psi(y)
, for all
x,y\in{\cal C}
, and all
\lambda\in[0,1],

or equivalently, if each component
\psi_{j}
of
\Psi
,
j\in{\cal J}
, is a convex function on
{\cal C}
. We recall that for a differentiable convex function
\Psi
on
{\cal C}
we have
\psi_{j}(y)-\psi_{j}(x)\geq\left\langle\nabla\psi_{j}(x),~y-x\right\rangle
, for all
x,y\in{\cal C}
with
x\in\mathrm{int}(\mathrm{dom}(\Psi))
, for all
j\in\cal{J}.

Next lemma shows that, in the convex case, the concepts of stationarity and weak Pareto optimality are equivalent, see
[
28
]
.

Lemma 2
.

If
\Psi
is convex and
\bar{x}
is a Pareto critical point, then
\bar{x}
is a weakly Pareto optimal point of problem (
1.1
).

The next two lemma will be important for the convergence rate results.

Lemma 3
.

[
46

Let
\left(a_{k}\right)_{k\in\mathbb{N}}
be a nonnegative sequence of real numbers, if
\Gamma a_{k}^{2}\leq a_{k}-a_{k+1}
for some
\Gamma>0
and for any

, then
a_{\ell}\leq{a_{0}}/({1+\ell\Gamma a_{0}})<{1}/({\Gamma\ell})
.

Lemma 4
.

[

Let
\left(a_{k}\right)_{k\in\mathbb{N}}

3
The multiobjetive composite optimization problem

Throughout our presentation, we assume that
F\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}=(f_{1},\ldots,f_{m})
, where
f_{j}\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}=h_{j}+g_{j}
for all
j\in{\cal J}\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}=\{1,2,\ldots,m\}
, satisfies the following three conditions:

(A1)

The function
h_{j}
is differentiable, for all
j\in{\cal J}
;

(A2)

The function
g_{j}
is proper, convex, and lower semicontinuous, for all
j\in{\cal J}
;

(A3)

\mathrm{dom}(G)\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}=\left\{x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}\mid g_{j}(x)<+\infty,~j=1,2,\ldots,m\right\}
is convex and compact.

Since we are assuming that
\mathrm{dom}(G)
is compact, for future reference we take
\Omega>0
satisfying

\Omega\geq\operatorname{diam}\left(\mathrm{dom}(G)\right).

(3.1)

We also consider the following three additional assumptions, which will be considered only when explicitly stated
.

(A4)

The function
g_{j}
is Lipschitz continuous with constant
L{g_{j}}>0
in
\mathrm{dom}(g_{j})
, for all
j\in{\cal J}
;

(A5)

The gradient
\nabla h_{j}
is Lipschitz continuous with constants
L_{j}>0
, for all
j\in{\cal J}
, and

L\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}=\operatorname{max}\{L_{j}\mid j\in{\cal J}\};

(A6)

The function
h_{j}
, for all
j\in\cal{J}
, is convex.

Before presenting the method to solve problem (
1.1
), we first need to study a gap function associated with this problem, which will play an important role in this work. This study will be made in next section.

3.1
The gap function

This section is devoted to study the
gap function

\theta\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}\mathrm{dom}(G)\to\mathbb{R}
associated to problem (
1.1
), defined by

\theta(x)\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}=\displaystyle\operatorname{min}_{u\in{\mathbb{R}^{n}}}\operatorname{max}_{j\in{\cal J}}\left(g_{j}(u)-g_{j}(x)+\left\langle\nabla h_{j}(x),u-x\right\rangle\right).

(3.2)

As we prove below, the gap function
\theta(\cdot)
will serve as a stopping criterion for the algorithm presented in the next section.
We observe that, if the components of function
G
are the indicator function of a set
{\cal C}
, the gap function
\theta(\cdot)
in (
3.2
) becomes the one presented in
[
3
]
.

Clearly, for each
x\in\mathrm{dom}(G)
, the gap function
\theta(x)
is the optimum value of the optimization problem

\displaystyle\operatorname{min}_{u\in\mathbb{R}^{n}}\operatorname{max}_{j\in\cal{J}}\left(g_{j}(u)-g_{j}(x)+\left\langle\nabla h_{j}(x),u-x\right\rangle\right).

(3.3)

It follows from assumptions (A1)–(A3) that problem (
3.3
) has a solution (possibly not unique) and it belongs to
\mathrm{dom}(G)
. Thus, we use the notation
p(x)\in\mathrm{dom}(G)
when referring to a solution of problem (
3.3
), i.e.,

p(x)\in\arg\displaystyle\operatorname{min}_{u\in{\mathbb{R}^{n}}}\operatorname{max}_{j\in\cal{J}}\left(g_{j}(u)-g_{j}(x)+\left\langle\nabla h_{j}(x),u-x\right\rangle\right).

(3.4)

Therefore, combining (
3.2
) and (
3.4
), we conclude that

\theta(x)=\operatorname{max}_{j\in{\cal J}}\left(g_{j}(p(x))-g_{j}(x)+\left\langle\nabla h_{j}(x),p(x)-x\right\rangle\right),\quad\forall~x\in\mathrm{dom}(G).

(3.5)

To simplify the notations, for each
x\in\mathrm{dom}(G)
and
p(x)
as in (
3.4
), we set

d(x)\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}=p(x)-x.

In the following lemma, we show that
\theta(\cdot)
can in fact be seen as a gap function for problem (
1.1
).

Lemma 5
.

Let
\theta\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}\mathrm{dom}(G)\to\mathbb{R}
be defined as in (
3.2
). Then

(i)

\theta(x)\leq 0
, for all
x\in\mathrm{dom}(G)
;

(ii)

\theta(x)=0
if, and only if,
x
is a Pareto critical point of problem (
1.1
);

(iii)

\theta(x)
is upper semicontinuous.

Proof.

Consider
(i)
and let
x\in\mathrm{dom}(G)
. The definition of
\theta(\cdot)
in (
3.2
) implies

\displaystyle\theta(x)\leq\operatorname{max}_{j\in{\cal J}}\left(g_{j}(u)-g_{j}(x)+\left\langle\nabla h_{j}(x),u-x\right\rangle\right),\quad\forall u\in\mathbb{R}^{n}.

(3.6)

Thus, letting
u=x
in the previous inequality, we conclude that
\theta(x)\leq 0
, which proves
(i)
. To prove item
(ii)
, we first assume that
x
is a Pareto critical point of problem (
1.1
). Therefore, by (
2.2
), we obtain

\operatorname{max}_{j\in\cal{J}}f_{j}^{\prime}(x;d)\geq 0,\quad\forall d\in\mathbb{R}^{n}.

(3.7)

Let
d\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
be arbitrary. Using (A1) and (A2), we have
f_{j}^{\prime}(x;d)=g_{j}^{\prime}(x;d)+\left\langle\nabla h_{j}(x),d\right\rangle
. Thus, it follows from (
3.7
) that
\operatorname{max}_{j\in{\cal J}}\{g_{j}^{\prime}(x;d)+\left\langle\nabla h_{j}(x),d\right\rangle\}\geq 0
, for all
d\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
. Hence, by Lemma
1
, we conclude that
\operatorname{max}_{j\in{\cal J}}\left\{g_{j}(x+d)-g_{j}(x)+\left\langle\nabla h_{j}(x),d\right\rangle\right\}\geq 0
. In particular, letting
d=p(x)-x
, we have

\operatorname{max}_{j\in{\cal J}}\left(g_{j}(p(x))-g_{j}(x)+\left\langle\nabla h_{j}(x),p(x)-x\right\rangle\right)\geq 0.

Thus, using (
3.5
), we conclude that
\theta(x)\geq 0
which, together with item
(i)
, gives
\theta(x)=0
.
Reciprocally, now we assume that
\theta(x)=0
. Thus, as in (
3.6
), we obtain

\operatorname{max}_{j\in{\cal J}}\left\{g_{j}(u)-g_{j}(x)+\left\langle\nabla h_{j}(x),u-x\right\rangle\right\}\geq 0,\quad\forall u\in\mathbb{R}^{n}.

In particular, letting
u=x+\alpha d
, for
\alpha>0
and
d\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
, we conclude that

\operatorname{max}_{j\in{\cal J}}\Big(\frac{g_{j}(x+\alpha d)-g_{j}(x)}{\alpha}+\left\langle\nabla h_{j}(x),d\right\rangle\Big)\geq 0,\quad\forall\alpha>0,~\forall d\in\mathbb{R}^{n}.

Since the maximum function is continuous and
g_{j}
has directional derivative at
x\in\mathrm{dom}(G)
, we can take limit as
\alpha
goes to
0
in the last inequality to conclude that
\operatorname{max}_{j\in{\cal J}}\{g_{j}^{\prime}(x,d)+\left\langle\nabla h_{j}(x),d\right\rangle\}\geq 0
, for all
d\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
. Therefore, (
2.2
) holds and thus
x
is a Pareto critical point of problem (
1.1
).
We proceed to prove item
(iii)
. Let
x\in\mathrm{dom}(G)
and consider a sequence
(x^{k})_{k\in\mathbb{N}}
such that
\lim_{k\to\infty}x^{k}=x
. Since
p(x)\in\mathrm{dom}(G)
, by (
3.2
), we have

\theta(x^{k})\leq\operatorname{max}_{j\in\cal{J}}\big(g_{j}(p(x))-g_{j}(x^{k})+\big\langle\nabla h_{j}(x^{k}),p(x)-x^{k}\big\rangle\big).

Using the continuity of the maximum function and taking the upper limit in the last inequality, we have

\lim\sup_{k\to\infty}\theta(x^{k})\leq\operatorname{max}_{j\in\cal{J}}\big(g_{j}(p(x))+\lim\sup_{k\to\infty}\big(-g_{j}(x^{k})\big)+\big\langle\nabla h_{j}(x),p(x)-x\big\rangle\big).

(3.8)

On the other hand, considering that
g_{j}
is lower semicontinuous in its effective domain, we obtain
\lim\sup_{k\to\infty}\left[-g_{j}(x^{k})\right]\leq-g_{j}(x)
. Therefore, combining this inequality with (
3.8
) and (
3.5
), we have
\lim\sup_{k\to\infty}\theta(x^{k})\leq\theta(x),
which concludes the proof.
∎

Hereafter, we denote:

e\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}=(1,\ldots,1)^{T}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{m}.

When there is no confusion, we will also use letter
e
to denote the column vector of ones with an alternative dimension.
In the following lemma, we present the counterpart of
[
3
, Lemma 1]
for
F
being a composite function as defined in (
1.1
). Note that we assume that only the second component of
F
has coordinates with Lipschitz gradients.

Lemma 6
.

Assume that
F
satisfies (A5).
Let
x\in\mathrm{dom}(G)
and
\lambda\in[0,1]
. Then

F(x+\lambda[p(x)-x])\preceq F(x)+\Big(\lambda\theta(x)+\frac{L}{2}\|p(x)-x\|^{2}\lambda^{2}\Big)e.

(3.9)

Proof.

Let
j\in{\cal J}
. Since
h_{j}
has gradient Lipschitz continuous with constant
L_{j}
,
x\in\mathrm{dom}(G)
and
\lambda\in[0,1]
, we have

f_{j}(x+\lambda[p(x)-x])\leq g_{j}((1-\lambda)x+\lambda g_{j}(p(x))+h_{j}(x)+\lambda\langle\nabla h_{j}(x),(p(x)-x)\rangle+\frac{L_{j}}{2}\|p(x)-x\|^{2}\lambda^{2}.

Considering that
g_{j}

Since the last inequality holds for all

In this section, we introduce a generalization of the conditional gradient method, also known as Frank-Wolfe algorithm, to solve multiobjective composite optimization problems.
We will also study asymptotic convergence properties and iteration-complexity bounds for the sequence generated by this method.
The analysis is carried out with three different step size strategies, namely, Armijo type, adaptive and diminishing step sizes.

evaluations of gradients
\nabla h_{1},\ldots,\nabla h_{m}
.

Proof.

The proof follows from the combination of Corollaries

Remark 2
.

Assume that
F
satisfies (A4)–(A6). Moreover assume that
\lim_{k\to\infty}F(x^{k})=F(x^{*})
, for some
x^{*}\in\mathrm{dom}(G)
, and take
\Omega>0
satisfying (
3.1
). Then, for all

, the following inequality holds

\displaystyle\operatorname{min}_{j\in\cal{J}}\left(f_{j}(x^{k})-f_{j}(x^{*})\right)\leq\frac{1}{\zeta\gamma}\frac{1}{k},

(4.19)

where
\gamma
is given in (
4.16
).
Indeed, since
\lambda_{k}
satisfies the Armijo step size rule, we have

Thus, the last inequality, together with Lemma
9
, implies

\displaystyle\operatorname{min}_{j\in\cal{J}}\left(f_{j}(x^{k+1})-f_{j}(x^{*})\right)\leq\displaystyle\operatorname{min}_{j\in\cal{J}}\left(f_{j}(x^{k})-f_{j}(x^{*})\right)-\zeta\gamma\theta(x^{k})^{2}.

(4.20)

On the other hand, using the convexity of
h_{j}
, for all
j\in\cal{J}
, we conclude that

f_{j}(x^{*})-f_{j}(x^{k})=g_{j}(x^{*})-g_{j}(x^{k})+h_{j}(x^{*})-h_{j}(x^{k})\geq g_{j}(x^{*})-g_{j}(x^{k})+\big\langle\nabla h_{j}(x^{k}),x^{*}-x^{k}\big\rangle,

Since
\left(F(x^{k})\right)_{k\in\mathbb{N}}
is decreasing monotone and
\lim_{k\to\infty}F(x^{k})=F(x^{*})
,
we have
F(x^{*})\preceq F(x^{k})
, for all
k\in\mathbb{N}
. Thus, the last inequality implies that

0\geq f_{j}(x^{*})-f_{j}(x^{k})\geq g_{j}(x^{*})-g_{j}(x^{k})+\langle\nabla h_{j}(x^{k}),x^{*}-x^{k}\rangle,\quad\forall j\in\cal{J}.

Taking maximum in the least inequality and using the definition of
\theta(x^{k})
in (
4.2
), we conclude that

0\geq\operatorname{max}_{j\in\cal{J}}\left(f_{j}(x^{*})-f_{j}(x^{k})\right)\geq\operatorname{max}_{j\in\cal{J}}\left(g_{j}(x^{*})-g_{j}(x^{k})+\langle\nabla h_{j}(x^{k}),x^{*}-x^{k}\rangle\right)\geq\theta(x^{k}),

which implies that
0\geq-\displaystyle\operatorname{min}_{j\in\cal{J}}\{f_{j}(x^{k})-f_{j}(x^{*})\}\geq\theta(x^{k})

0\leq\Big(\displaystyle\operatorname{min}_{j\in\cal{J}}\big(f_{j}(x^{k})-f_{j}(x^{*})\big)\Big)^{2}\leq\theta(x^{k})^{2}.

The combination of the last inequality with (

\zeta\gamma\Big(\displaystyle\operatorname{min}_{j\in\cal{J}}\big(f_{j}(x^{k})-f_{j}(x^{*})\big)\Big)^{2}\leq\displaystyle\operatorname{min}_{j\in\cal{J}}\big(f_{j}(x^{k})-f_{j}(x^{*})\big)-\displaystyle\operatorname{min}_{j\in\cal{J}}\big(f_{j}(x^{k+1})-f_{j}(x^{*})\big),

for all

, with
a_{k}=\displaystyle\operatorname{min}_{j\in\cal{J}}\left(f_{j}(x^{k})-f_{j}(x^{*})\right)

4.2
Convergence analysis using adaptive and diminishing step sizes

The purpose of this section is to analyze the sequence
(x^{k})_{k\in\mathbb{N}}
generated by Algorithm
1
with adaptive and diminishing step sizes. We begin by showing that, in particular, if
(x^{k})_{k\in\mathbb{N}}
is generated by Algorithm
1
with the adaptive step size (
4.4
), then
(F(x^{k}))_{k\in\mathbb{N}}
is a nonincreasing sequence.

Lemma 14
.

Assume that
F
satisfies (A5). Let
(x^{k})_{k\in\mathbb{N}}
be generated by Algorithm
1
with the adaptive step size (
4.4
). Then

F(x^{k+1})-F(x^{k})\preceq-\frac{1}{2}\displaystyle\operatorname{min}\left\{|\theta(x^{k})|,\frac{\theta(x^{k})^{2}}{L\Omega^{2}}\right\}e,\quad\forall k\in\mathbb{N},

(4.21)

where
\Omega>0
is given in (
3.1
). As a consequence,
(F(x^{k}))_{k\in\mathbb{N}}
is a nonincreasing sequence.

Proof.

Let us analyze the two possibilities for
\lambda_{k}
defined in (
4.4
). First, assume that
\lambda_{k}=1
. In this case, using (
4.4
), we have
L\|p(x^{k})-x^{k}\|^{2}\leq|\theta(x^{k})|
. Thus, taking into account (A5), we apply Lemma
6
with
\lambda=1
and
x=x^{k}
to obtain

F(p(x^{k}))\preceq F(x^{k})+\Big(\theta(x^{k})+\frac{L}{2}\|p(x^{k})-x^{k}\|^{2}\Big)e\preceq F(x^{k})+\Big(\theta(x^{k})+\frac{1}{2}|\theta(x^{k})|\Big)e.

(4.22)

Due to
\lambda_{k}=1
, it follows from (
4.3
) that
x^{k+1}=p(x^{k})
. Therefore, since
|\theta(x^{k})|=-\theta(x^{k})
, we conclude from (
4.22
) that

F(x^{k+1})\preceq F(x^{k})-\frac{1}{2}|\theta(x^{k})|e.

(4.23)

Now, assume that
\lambda_{k}=-\theta(x^{k})/(L\|p(x^{k})-x^{k}\|^{2})
. Thus, applying Lemma
6
with
\lambda=\lambda_{k}
and
x=x^{k}
, and considering (
4.3
), we obtain

F(x^{k+1})\preceq F(x^{k})+\left(\lambda_{k}\theta(x^{k})+\frac{L}{2}\|p(x^{k})-x^{k}\|^{2}\lambda_{k}^{2}\right)e=F(x^{k})-\frac{\theta(x^{k})^{2}}{2L\|p(x^{k})-x^{k}\|^{2}}e.

(4.24)

Therefore, the combination of (
4.23
) and (
4.24
) yields

F(x^{k+1})\preceq F(x^{k})-\frac{1}{2}\displaystyle\operatorname{min}\left\{|\theta(x^{k})|,\frac{\theta(x^{k})^{2}}{L\|p(x^{k})-x^{k}\|^{2}}\right\}e.

Since
\Omega\geq\operatorname{diam}(\mathrm{dom}(G))
, the last inequality implies that (
4.21
) holds.
∎

For state the next result, we consider constants
f_{j^{*}}(x^{0})
and
f_{j^{*}}^{\inf}
as in (
4.17
).

Proposition 15
.

Assume that
F
satisfies (A5). Let
(x^{k})_{k\in\mathbb{N}}
be generated by Algorithm
1
with the adaptive step size (
4.4
). Then

(i)

\lim_{k\to\infty}\theta(x^{k})=0
;

(ii)

for every
N\in\mathbb{N}
, there holds

\displaystyle\operatorname{min}\left\{|\theta(x^{k})|\mid k=0,1,\ldots,N-1\right\}\leq\operatorname{max}\left\{\frac{2}{N}\left(f_{j^{*}}(x^{0})-f_{j^{*}}^{\inf}\right),\Omega\sqrt{\frac{2L}{N}\left(f_{j^{*}}(x^{0})-f_{j^{*}}^{\inf}\right)}\right\}.

Proof.

Lemma
14
implies that

0<\displaystyle\operatorname{min}\left\{|\theta(x^{k})|,\frac{\theta(x^{k})^{2}}{L\Omega^{2}}\right\}e\leq 2\left(F(x^{k})-F(x^{k+1})\right),\quad\forall k\in\mathbb{N}.

(4.25)

As in the proof of Lemma
10
, it follows that
(F(x^{k}))_{k\in\mathbb{N}}
converges. Thus, taking limits as
k
goes to infinity on (
4.25
), we obtain
(i)
.
Next we proceed to prove
(ii)
. By summing both sides of the second inequality in (
4.25
) for
k=0,1,\ldots,N-1
, and taking into account the definition of
f_{j^{*}}(x^{0})
and

, we obtain

\sum_{k=0}^{N-1}\displaystyle\operatorname{min}\left\{|\theta(x^{k})|,\frac{\theta(x^{k})^{2}}{L\Omega^{2}}\right\}\leq 2\left(f_{j^{*}}(x^{0})-f_{j^{*}}^{\inf}\right).

Therefore, we have

\displaystyle\operatorname{min}\left\{\displaystyle\operatorname{min}\left\{|\theta(x^{k})|,\frac{\theta(x^{k})^{2}}{L\Omega^{2}}\right\}\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}~k=0,1,\ldots,N-1\right\}\leq\frac{2}{N}\left(f_{j^{*}}(x^{0})-f_{j^{*}}^{\inf}\right),

.
∎

.

Theorem 17
.

Assume that
F
satisfies (A5)–(A6). Let
(x^{k})_{k\in\mathbb{N}}
generated by Algorithm
1
with
\lambda_{k}
satisfying the adaptive or the diminishing step size, i.e., (

) or (

\displaystyle\operatorname{min}_{\ell\in\left\{\lfloor\frac{k}{2}\rfloor+2,\cdots,k\right\}}|\theta(x^{\ell})|\leq\frac{8L\Omega^{2}}{k-2},\quad k=3,4,\ldots.

We first claim that

F(x^{k+1})\preceq F(x^{k})+\Big(\beta_{k}\theta(x^{k})+\frac{L}{2}\|p(x^{k})-x^{k}\|^{2}\beta_{k}^{2}\Big)e,

(4.26)

where

. Indeed, by applying Lemma
6
with

and
\lambda=\lambda_{k}
, we have

F(x^{k}+\lambda_{k}[p(x^{k})-x^{k}])\preceq F(x^{k})+\Big(\lambda_{k}\theta(x^{k})+\frac{L}{2}\|p(x^{k})-x^{k}\|^{2}\lambda_{k}^{2}\Big)e.

(4.27)

If
\lambda_{k}
is the diminishing step size given in (
4.5
), then (
4.26
) and (
4.27
) trivially coincide.
We now assume that
\lambda_{k}
is the adaptive step size given in (
4.4
). Since
\beta_{k}\in(0,1]
, it follows from (
4.4
) that

\lambda_{k}\theta(x^{k})+(L/2)\|p(x^{k})-x^{k}\|^{2}\lambda_{k}^{2}\leq\beta_{k}\theta(x^{k})+(L/2)\|p(x^{k})-x^{k}\|^{2}\beta_{k}^{2}

4.26
).

Now, by (

\displaystyle\operatorname{min}_{j\in\cal{J}}\big(f_{j}(x^{k+1})-f_{j}(x^{*})\big)\leq\displaystyle\operatorname{min}_{j\in\cal{J}}\big(f_{j}(x^{k})-f_{j}(x^{*})\big)+\beta_{k}\theta(x^{k})+\frac{L}{2}\Omega^{2}\beta_{k}^{2},\quad\forall n\in\mathbb{N}.

(4.28)

On the other hand, due to

0\geq f_{j}(x^{*})-f_{j}(x^{k})\geq\big\langle\nabla h_{j}(x^{k}),x^{*}-x^{k}\big\rangle+g_{j}(x^{*})-g_{j}(x^{k}),\quad k\in\mathbb{N}.

Thus, taking the maximum and using the optimality of

in (

5
Numerical experiments

This section presents some numerical experiments in order to illustrate the applicability of our approach. For this aim, we compare:

•

the Generalized Conditional Gradient method (Algorithm
1
);

•

the Proximal Gradient method proposed in
[
53
]
.

We implemented both methods using the Armijo step size strategy with parameters
\zeta=10^{-4}
,
\omega_{1}=0.05
, and
\omega_{2}=0.95
.
Without attempting to go into details, we remark that the Armijo line search was coded based on quadratic polynomial interpolations of the coordinate functions, see
[
41
]
for line search strategies in the vector optimization setting.
The main difference between the two considered methods consists of the subproblem to be solved to calculate the search direction.
While for the Generalized Conditional Gradient method the subproblem is given in (
4.1
), in the Proximal Gradient method the search direction in iteration
k
is defined by
d_{PG}(x^{k})\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}=p_{PG}(x^{k})-x^{k}
, where

p_{PG}(x^{k})\in\arg\displaystyle\operatorname{min}_{u\in{\mathbb{R}^{n}}}\operatorname{max}_{j\in{\cal J}}\big(g_{j}(u)-g_{j}(x^{k})+\big\langle\nabla h_{j}(x^{k}),u-x^{k}\big\rangle+\frac{\mu}{2}\|u-x^{k}\|^{2}\big),

(5.1)

and
\mu>0
is an algorithmic parameter. In our experiments, we set
\mu\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}=1
. In this case, when
G(x)\equiv 0
,
(
5.1
) reduces to the classical steepest descent approach proposed in
[
19
]
.
We denote the optimal value of problem (
5.1
) by
\theta_{PG}(x^{k})
.
As in Lemma
5
,
\theta_{PG}(\cdot)
can be used to characterize Pareto critical points, see
[
53
]
.
In order to standardize the stopping criteria, all runs were stopped at an iterate
x^{k}
declaring convergence if

\frac{\|x^{k}-x^{k-1}\|_{\infty}}{\operatorname{max}\{1,\|x^{k-1}\|_{\infty}\}}\leq 10^{-4}\quad\mbox{and}\quad\mathinner{\!\bigl\lvert\theta_{\textrm{PG}}(x^{k})\bigr\rvert}\leq 10^{-4}.

(5.2)

The first criterion in (
5.2
) seeks to detect the convergence of the sequence
\{x^{k}\}
, while the second guarantees to stop at an
approximately
Pareto critical point. For Algorithm
1
, we only calculate
\theta_{\textrm{PG}}(x^{k})
when the first criterion in (
5.2
) is satisfied. We also consider a stopping criterion related to failures: the maximum number of allowed iterations was set to 200.
The codes are written in Matlab and are freely available at
https://github.com/lfprudente/CompositeMOPCondG
.

The set of test problems is related to robust multiobjective optimization.

Solving the subproblems:

We first note that a solution of the subproblem in (
4.1
) can be calculated by solving for
\tau\in\mathbb{R}
and
u\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
the following constrained problem

\begin{array}[]{cl}\displaystyle\operatorname{min}_{\tau,u}&\tau\\
\mbox{s.t.}&g_{j}(u)-g_{j}(x^{k})+\langle\nabla h_{j}(x^{k}),u-x^{k}\rangle\leq\tau,\quad\forall j\in{\cal J},\\
&lb\preceq u\preceq ub.\end{array}

(5.6)

However, since
g_{j}(\cdot)
in (
5.3
)–(
5.4
) is non-differentiable, the inequalities in (
5.6
) are difficult to deal with.
On the other hand, if we define
A_{j}\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}=[B_{j};-B_{j}]\in\mathbb{R}^{2n\times n}
and
b_{j}\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}=\delta e\in\mathbb{R}^{2n}
, then (
5.3
)–(
5.4
) can be rewritten as

\begin{array}[]{cl}\operatorname{max}_{z}&\langle x,z\rangle\\
\mbox{s.t.}&A_{j}z\preceq b_{j},\\
\end{array}

(5.7)

for which the dual problem is given by

\begin{array}[]{cl}\displaystyle\operatorname{min}_{w}&\langle b_{j},w\rangle\\
\mbox{s.t.}&A_{j}^{\top}w=x,\\
&w\succeq 0.\end{array}

By using duality theory, it follows that (
5.6
) (and thus (
4.1
)) is equivalent to the following linear programming problem

\begin{array}[]{cl}\displaystyle\operatorname{min}_{\tau,u,w_{j}}&\tau\\
\mbox{s.t.}&\langle b_{j},w\rangle-g_{j}(x^{k})+\langle\nabla h_{j}(x^{k}),u-x^{k}\rangle\leq\tau,\\
&A_{j}^{\top}w_{j}=u,\\
&w_{j}\succeq 0,\quad\forall j\in{\cal J},\\
&lb\preceq u\preceq ub.\end{array}

(5.8)

Likewise, the subproblem (
5.1
) of the Proximal Gradient method can be reformulated as the following quadratic programming problem

\begin{array}[]{cl}\displaystyle\operatorname{min}_{\tau,u,w_{j}}&\tau+\displaystyle\frac{\mu}{2}\|u-x^{k}\|^{2}\\
\mbox{s.t.}&\langle b_{j},w\rangle-g_{j}(x^{k})+\langle\nabla h_{j}(x^{k}),u-x^{k}\rangle\leq\tau,\\
&A_{j}^{\top}w_{j}=u,\\
&w_{j}\succeq 0,\quad\forall j\in{\cal J},\\
&lb\preceq u\preceq ub,\end{array}

(5.9)

for details see
[
53
, Section 5.2 (a)]
. In our codes, we use a simplex-dual method (
linprog
routine) to solve (
5.7
) and (
5.8
), and an interior point method (
quadprog
routine) to solve (
5.9
).

5.2
Pareto frontiers

In multiobjective optimization, we are mainly interested in estimating the Pareto frontier of a given problem.
A commonly used strategy for this task is to run an algorithm from several starting points and collect the efficient points found.
Thus, given a test problem, we run each algorithm for 2 minutes obtaining an approximation of the Pareto frontier.
We compare the results using the well-known
Purity
and (
\Gamma
and
\Delta
)
Spread
metrics.
In summary, given a problem, the Purity metric measures the ability of an algorithm to find points on the Pareto frontier, while a Spread metric measures the ability to obtain well-distributed points along the Pareto frontier.
For a careful discussion of these metrics and their uses along with performance profiles, see
[
14
]
.
The results in Figure
2
show that no significant differences are notice for the three metrics.
This suggests that the Conditional Gradient method is competitive with the Proximal Gradient method in terms of obtaining
good
approximations of the Pareto frontier.

6
Conclusions

This paper extends the generalized conditional gradient method for multiobjective composite optimization problems.
Our analysis was carried out with and without convexity and Lipschitz assumptions on the smooth component of the objective functions and considering different step size strategies.
The numerical results suggests that the proposed method is competitive with the Proximal Gradient method recently introduced in
[
53
]
, in terms of computational efficiency and ability to generate Pareto frontiers properly.
It would be interesting to extend the results of the present paper for composite vector optimization problem, i.e., when the partial order is induced by other underlying cones instead of the non-negative orthant.
