Higher Rank Relations for the Askey–Wilson and 𝒒 \boldsymbol{q} -Bannai–Ito Algebra
Hadewijch DE CLERCQ

Abstract
The higher rank Askey–Wilson algebra was recently constructed in the n n -fold tensor product of U q ​ ( 𝔰 ​ 𝔩 2 ) U_{q}(\mathfrak{sl}_{2}) . In this paper we prove a class of identities inside this algebra, which generalize the defining relations of the rank one Askey–Wilson algebra. We extend the known construction algorithm by several equivalent methods, using a novel coaction. These allow to simplify calculations significantly. At the same time, this provides a proof of the corresponding relations for the higher rank q q -Bannai–Ito algebra.

Introduction

The Askey–Wilson algebra was introduced in
[
33
]
as an algebraic foundation for the bispectral problem of the Askey–Wilson orthogonal polynomials
[
22
]
. More precisely, Zhedanov defined this algebra by generators and relations, which turned out to be satisfied when realizing the generators as the Askey–Wilson
q
-difference operator on the one hand, and the multiplication with the variable on the other. A central extension, which allows a
\mathbb{Z}_{3}
-symmetric presentation, was defined and studied by Terwilliger
[
29
]
. He calls this central extension the universal Askey–Wilson algebra. We will denote it by
{\rm AW}(3)
.

The irreducible finite-dimensional representations of the Askey–Wilson algebra have been classified by Leonard pairs
[
26
,
31
]
and a similar classification for the universal Askey–Wilson algebra
{\rm AW}(3)
has appeared in
[
18
]
.
Further applications arise in the theory of special functions
[
4
,
24
]
, tridiagonal and Leonard pairs
[
25
,
28
,
32
]
, superintegrable quantum systems
[
3
]
and the reflection equation
[
2
]
. Its use to quantum mechanics is further emphasized by the identification of the Askey–Wilson algebra as a quotient of the
q
-Onsager algebra
[
27
]
, which originates from statistical mechanics. This connection was later extended to the universal Askey–Wilson algebra
[
30
]
. Furthermore, an explicit homomorphism from the original Askey–Wilson algebra to the double affine Hecke algebra of type
\big(C_{1}^{\vee},C_{1}\big)
has been constructed in
[
21
,
23
]
. Recently, connections with
q
-Higgs algebras
[
13
]
and Howe dual pairs
[
14
]
have been obtained.

The original Askey–Wilson algebra
[
33
]
was realized inside the quantum group
U_{q}(\mathfrak{sl}_{2})
in
[
17
]
. A different realization in the three-fold tensor product of
U_{q}(\mathfrak{sl}_{2})
was given in
[
16
]
, and was later extended to the universal Askey–Wilson algebra
{\rm AW}(3)
of
[
29
]
in
[
19
]
. In the latter reference,
{\rm AW}(3)
is embedded in the threefold tensor product of
U_{q}(\mathfrak{sl}_{2})
: if one denotes by
\Lambda
the quadratic Casimir element of
U_{q}(\mathfrak{sl}_{2})
and by
\Delta
its coproduct, and defines

\displaystyle\Lambda_{\{1\}}=\Lambda\otimes 1\otimes 1,\qquad\Lambda_{\{2\}}=1\otimes\Lambda\otimes 1,\qquad\Lambda_{\{3\}}=1\otimes 1\otimes\Lambda,

\displaystyle\Lambda_{\{1,2\}}=\Delta(\Lambda)\otimes 1,\qquad\Lambda_{\{2,3\}}=1\otimes\Delta(\Lambda),\qquad\Lambda_{\{1,2,3\}}=(1\otimes\Delta)\Delta(\Lambda),

(1.1)

then these elements generate the universal Askey–Wilson algebra. Indeed, they satisfy the
q
-commutation relations

\displaystyle[\Lambda_{\{1,2\}},\Lambda_{\{2,3\}}]_{q}=\big(q^{-2}-q^{2}\big)\Lambda_{\{1,3\}}+\big(q-q^{-1}\big)\big(\Lambda_{\{2\}}\Lambda_{\{1,2,3\}}+\Lambda_{\{1\}}\Lambda_{\{3\}}\big),

(1.2)

\displaystyle[\Lambda_{\{2,3\}},\Lambda_{\{1,3\}}]_{q}=\big(q^{-2}-q^{2}\big)\Lambda_{\{1,2\}}+\big(q-q^{-1}\big)\big(\Lambda_{\{3\}}\Lambda_{\{1,2,3\}}+\Lambda_{\{1\}}\Lambda_{\{2\}}\big),

(1.3)

\displaystyle[\Lambda_{\{1,3\}},\Lambda_{\{1,2\}}]_{q}=\big(q^{-2}-q^{2}\big)\Lambda_{\{2,3\}}+\big(q-q^{-1}\big)\big(\Lambda_{\{1\}}\Lambda_{\{1,2,3\}}+\Lambda_{\{2\}}\Lambda_{\{3\}}\big),

(1.4)

where
\Lambda_{\{1,3\}}
is defined through relation (
1.2
), and
\Lambda_{\{1,2,3\}}
and all
\Lambda_{\{i\}}
are central. This coincides with the presentation for
{\rm AW}(3)
given in
[
29
]
. This illustrates that there exists an algebra homomorphism from
{\rm AW}(3)
to
U_{q}(\mathfrak{sl}_{2})^{\otimes 3}
and this map turns out to be injective, as shown in
[
19
, Theorem 4.8]
.

In
[
8
]
this approach was generalized to
n
-fold tensor products for arbitrary
n
, which leads to an extension of the universal Askey–Wilson algebra to higher rank, denoted
{\rm AW}(n)
. The same algorithm allows to construct a higher rank extension of the
q
-Bannai–Ito algebra, which is isomorphic to
{\rm AW}(3)
under a transformation
q\to-q^{2}
and allows a similar embedding in
\mathfrak{osp}_{q}(1|2)^{\otimes 3}

[

Such higher rank algebras are motivated by their role as symmetry algebras for superintegrable quantum systems of higher dimension. This has been confirmed in the limiting case
q=1

[
9
,
10
,
11
]
, and later also for general
q

[
8
]
. In both cases, the Hamiltonians under consideration are built from Dunkl operators with
\mathbb{Z}_{2}^{n}
symmetry
[
12
]
, possibly
q
-deformed
[
5
]
. Moreover, these higher rank algebras allow to extend known connections with orthogonal polynomials to multiple variables. This was achieved in
[
7
]
for the
q
-Bannai–Ito algebra. To be concrete, an action of the higher rank
q
-Bannai–Ito algebra on an abstract vector space was considered, leading to various orthonormal bases for this space. The overlap coefficients between such bases turned out to be multivariable
(-q)
-Racah polynomials, the truncated analogs of Askey–Wilson polynomials. This has allowed to construct a realization of the higher rank
q
-Bannai–Ito algebra with Iliev’s
q
-difference operators
[
20
]
, which have thereby obtained an algebraic interpretation.

The construction of
{\rm AW}(n)
is rather intricate: in
[
8
]
we have outlined an algorithm which repeatedly applies the
U_{q}(\mathfrak{sl}_{2})
-coproduct
\Delta
and a coaction
\tau
to the Casimir element
\Lambda
, in a specific order. This way we construct, as an extension of (
1.2
)–(
1.4
), an element
\Lambda_{A}\in U_{q}(\mathfrak{sl}_{2})^{\otimes n}
for each
A\subseteq\{1,\dots,n\}
. In this paper we rephrase this extension algorithm in more accessible notation and provide alternative construction methods for the elements
\Lambda_{A}
which use a novel coaction. This new approach is of major use to derive algebraic identities in
{\rm AW}(n)
, as we showcase in Theorems
3.1
and
3.2
by significantly generalizing the algebraic relations given in
[
8
]
. The main achievement of this paper is hence a general criterion for two generators
\Lambda_{A}
and
\Lambda_{B}
of
{\rm AW}(n)
to commute or to satisfy a relation of the form

\displaystyle[\Lambda_{A},\Lambda_{B}]_{q}=\big(q^{-2}-q^{2}\big)\Lambda_{(A\cup B)\setminus(A\cap B)}+\big(q-q^{-1}\big)\big(\Lambda_{A\cap B}\Lambda_{A\cup B}+\Lambda_{A\setminus(A\cap B)}\Lambda_{B\setminus(A\cap B)}\big).\!\!\!

(1.5)

More precisely, we show in Theorem
3.1
that

[\Lambda_{A},\Lambda_{B}]=0\qquad\mathrm{if}\quad B\subseteq A,

and in Theorem
3.2
we prove that the relation (
1.5
) is satisfied for

\displaystyle A=A_{1}\cup A_{2}\cup A_{4},\qquad

\displaystyle B=A_{2}\cup A_{3},

\displaystyle A=A_{2}\cup A_{3},\qquad

\displaystyle B=A_{1}\cup A_{3}\cup A_{4},

\displaystyle A=A_{1}\cup A_{3}\cup A_{4},\qquad

\displaystyle B=A_{1}\cup A_{2}\cup A_{4},

(1.6)

where
A_{1},A_{2},A_{3},A_{4}\subseteq\{1,\dots,n\}
are such that for each
i\in\{1,2,3\}
one has either
\max(A_{i})<\min(A_{i+1})
or
A_{i}=\varnothing
or
A_{i+1}=\varnothing
.

It is not clear at this point whether these relations define the algebra
{\rm AW}(n)
abstractly, or, in case the answer turns to be negative, which supplementary relations should be added in order to attain this purpose. However, calculations with computer algebra packages suggest that the condition (
1.6
) describes the most general situation for the relations (
1.5
) to be satisfied.

Our methods are elementary and intrinsic: they are independent of the expressions for the coactions and the
U_{q}(\mathfrak{sl}_{2})
-Casimir
\Lambda
, and only recur to natural algebraic properties like coassociativity and the cotensor product property. As a consequence, the results of this paper are equally applicable to the higher rank
q
-Bannai–Ito algebra of
[
8
]
, without any modification.

The paper is organized as follows. In Section
2
we construct the higher rank Askey–Wilson algebra
{\rm AW}(n)
as a subalgebra of
U_{q}(\mathfrak{sl}_{2})^{\otimes n}
through different extension processes, which we prove to be equivalent. Section
3
lists the main results of this paper and explains the general strategy of proof. Consequently, in Sections
4
and
5

6
and
7
, where we prove Theorems
3.1
and
3.2
. Finally in Section

q
-Bannai–Ito algebra as a subalgebra of
\mathfrak{osp}_{q}(1|2)^{\otimes n}
. We state two explicit theorems describing their algebraic relations.

2
Defining the higher rank generators

Throughout this paper, we will work with the quantum group
U_{q}(\mathfrak{sl}_{2})
, which can be presented as the associative algebra over a field
\mathbb{K}
with generators
E
,
F
,
K
and
K^{-1}
, and relations

KK^{-1}=K^{-1}K=1,\qquad KE=q^{2}EK,\qquad KF=q^{-2}FK,\qquad[E,F]=\frac{K-K^{-1}}{q-q^{-1}},

where
q
is a fixed parameter in
\mathbb{K}
, assumed not to be a root of unity. A Casimir element, which commutes with all elements of
U_{q}(\mathfrak{sl}_{2})
, is given by

\displaystyle\Lambda=\big(q-q^{-1}\big)^{2}EF+q^{-1}K+qK^{-1}.

(2.1)

The quantum group
U_{q}(\mathfrak{sl}_{2})
has the structure of a bialgebra: it is equipped with a coproduct
\Delta\colon U_{q}(\mathfrak{sl}_{2})\to U_{q}(\mathfrak{sl}_{2})^{\otimes 2}
, which satisfies the coassociativity property
(1\otimes\Delta)\Delta=(\Delta\otimes 1)\Delta
, and a counit
\epsilon\colon U_{q}(\mathfrak{sl}_{2})\to\mathbb{K}
satisfying
(1\otimes\epsilon)\Delta=(\epsilon\otimes 1)\Delta=1
, where
1
denotes the identity mapping on
U_{q}(\mathfrak{sl}_{2})
. Explicitly, they are given by

\displaystyle\Delta(E)=E\otimes 1+K\otimes E,\qquad\!\Delta(F)=F\otimes K^{-1}+1\otimes F,\qquad\!\Delta\big(K^{\pm 1}\big)=K^{\pm 1}\otimes K^{\pm 1},\!\!

(2.2)

\displaystyle\epsilon(E)=\epsilon(F)=0,\qquad\epsilon(K)=\epsilon\big(K^{-1}\big)=1.

A binary operation we will often use is the so-called
q
-commutator. For
X,Y\in U_{q}(\mathfrak{sl}_{2})
we write

[X,Y]_{q}=qXY-q^{-1}YX.

For
i
and
j
natural numbers with
i\leq j
, we will write
[i;j]
to denote the discrete interval
\{i,i+1,\dots,j-1,j\}
. If we consider disjoint unions of discrete intervals, often denoted by
[i_{1};j_{1}]\cup[i_{2};j_{2}]\cup\dots\cup[i_{k};j_{k}]
, it is always understood that
i_{\ell}\leq j_{\ell}<i_{\ell+1}-1
for all
\ell
. Note that this implies that
i_{k}\geq i_{1}+2k-2
. Moreover, if
B
is any set of natural numbers and
a\in\mathbb{N}
, we will write
B-a
for the set
\{b-a\colon b\in B\}
.

2.1
Coideals and comodules

In
[
8
]
we have introduced the following
U_{q}(\mathfrak{sl}_{2})
-subalgebra.

Definition 2.1
.

We denote by
\mathcal{I}_{R}
the subalgebra of
U_{q}(\mathfrak{sl}_{2})
generated by
EK^{-1}
,
F
,
K^{-1}
and
\Lambda
, and we define the algebra morphism
\tau_{R}\colon\mathcal{I}_{R}\to U_{q}(\mathfrak{sl}_{2})\otimes\mathcal{I}_{R}
through its action on the generators:

\displaystyle\tau_{R}\big(EK^{-1}\big)=K^{-1}\otimes EK^{-1},

\displaystyle\tau_{R}(F)=K\otimes F-q^{-3}\big(q-q^{-1}\big)^{2}F^{2}K\otimes EK^{-1}+q^{-1}\big(q+q^{-1}\big)FK\otimes K^{-1}-q^{-1}FK\otimes\Lambda,

\displaystyle\tau_{R}\big(K^{-1}\big)=1\otimes K^{-1}-q^{-1}\big(q-q^{-1}\big)^{2}F\otimes EK^{-1},

\displaystyle\tau_{R}(\Lambda)=1\otimes\Lambda.

It is readily checked that these definitions comply with the algebra relations in
\mathcal{I}_{R}
. The following important observation was made in
[
8
, Proposition 3]
.

Proposition 2.1
.

The algebra
\mathcal{I}_{R}
is a left coideal subalgebra of
U_{q}(\mathfrak{sl}_{2})
and a left
U_{q}(\mathfrak{sl}_{2})
-comodule with coaction
\tau_{R}
. This means that
\Delta(\mathcal{I}_{R})\subset U_{q}(\mathfrak{sl}_{2})\otimes\mathcal{I}_{R}
and that one has

\displaystyle(1\otimes\tau_{R})\tau_{R}=(\Delta\otimes 1)\tau_{R},

(2.3)

\displaystyle(\epsilon\otimes 1)\tau_{R}=1.

An interpretation of the coaction
\tau_{R}
in terms of the universal
R
-matrix for
U_{q}(\mathfrak{sl}_{2})
was recently given in
[
6
]
. This coaction was constructed so as to satisfy the identity

\displaystyle\Lambda_{\{1,3\}}=(1\otimes\tau_{R})\Delta(\Lambda),

(2.4)

with
\Lambda_{\{1,3\}}
defined through (
1.1
) and (
1.2
). A similar mapping
\tau_{L}
can be constructed by demanding that

\displaystyle\Lambda_{\{1,3\}}=(\tau_{L}\otimes 1)\Delta(\Lambda).

(2.5)

This suggests the following definition.

Definition 2.2
.

We denote by
\mathcal{I}_{L}
the subalgebra of
U_{q}(\mathfrak{sl}_{2})
generated by
E
,
FK
,
K
and
\Lambda
, and we define the algebra morphism
\tau_{L}\colon\mathcal{I}_{L}\to\mathcal{I}_{L}\otimes U_{q}(\mathfrak{sl}_{2})
through its action on the generators:

\displaystyle\tau_{L}(E)=E\otimes K,

\displaystyle\tau_{L}(FK)=FK\otimes K^{-1}-q^{-1}\big(q-q^{-1}\big)^{2}E\otimes F^{2}K+q\big(q+q^{-1}\big)K\otimes F-q\Lambda\otimes F,

\displaystyle\tau_{L}(K)=K\otimes 1-q^{-1}\big(q-q^{-1}\big)^{2}E\otimes FK,

\displaystyle\tau_{L}(\Lambda)=\Lambda\otimes 1.

This subalgebra behaves in a similar fashion.

Proposition 2.2
.

The algebra
\mathcal{I}_{L}
is a right coideal subalgebra of
U_{q}(\mathfrak{sl}_{2})
and a right
U_{q}(\mathfrak{sl}_{2})
-comodule with coaction
\tau_{L}
.

Proof.

It suffices to check explicitly on each of the generators that

1)

\mathcal{I}_{L}
is a right coideal of
U_{q}(\mathfrak{sl}_{2})
:
\Delta(\mathcal{I}_{L})\subset\mathcal{I}_{L}\otimes U_{q}(\mathfrak{sl}_{2})
,

2)

\tau_{L}
is a right coaction: it preserves the algebra relations in
\mathcal{I}_{L}
and satisfies

\displaystyle(\tau_{L}\otimes 1)\tau_{L}=(1\otimes\Delta)\tau_{L},

(2.6)

\displaystyle(1\otimes\epsilon)\tau_{L}=1.

∎

It is readily checked that the element
\Delta(\Lambda)
lies in
\mathcal{I}_{L}\otimes\mathcal{I}_{R}
. Indeed, it follows immediately from (
2.1
) and (
2.2
) that one has

\Delta(\Lambda)=\Lambda\otimes K^{-1}+K\otimes\Lambda-\big(q+q^{-1}\big)K\otimes K^{-1}+\big(q-q^{-1}\big)^{2}\big(E\otimes F+q^{-2}FK\otimes EK^{-1}\big).

In the language of category theory
[
1
]
, the equality of (
2.4
) and (
2.5
) can be phrased as follows.

Corollary 2.1
.

The element
\Delta(\Lambda)\in\mathcal{I}_{L}\otimes\mathcal{I}_{R}
, with
\Lambda
defined in (
2.1
), belongs to the cotensor product of the coideal comodule subalgebras
\mathcal{I}_{L}
and
\mathcal{I}_{R}
of
U_{q}(\mathfrak{sl}_{2})
:

\displaystyle(1\otimes\tau_{R})\Delta(\Lambda)=(\tau_{L}\otimes 1)\Delta(\Lambda).

(2.7)

2.2
The right extension process

Our goal in this section will be to associate to each set
A\subseteq[1;n]
an element
\Lambda_{A}\in U_{q}(\mathfrak{sl}_{2})^{\otimes n}
, which will serve as a generator for the higher rank Askey–Wilson algebra
{\rm AW}(n)
. For the empty set, this will simply be the scalar

\displaystyle\Lambda_{\varnothing}=q+q^{-1}.

(2.8)

For general
A
, a construction algorithm was given in
[
8
]
. We will repeat it here in a more accessible notation.

Definition 2.3
.

For any set
A=\{a_{1},\dots,a_{m}\}\subseteq[1;n]
, ordered such that
a_{i}<a_{i+1}
for all
i
, we define
\Lambda_{A}\in U_{q}(\mathfrak{sl}_{2})^{\otimes n}
by

\Lambda_{A}=1^{\otimes(a_{1}-1)}\otimes\left(\overrightarrow{\prod_{i=2}^{m}}\,\mu^{A}_{i}\right)(\Lambda)\otimes 1^{\otimes(n-a_{m})},

with

\displaystyle\mu_{i}^{A}=\left(\overrightarrow{\prod_{\ell=a_{i-1}-a_{1}+1}^{a_{i}-a_{1}-1}}\big(1^{\otimes\ell}\otimes\tau_{R}\big)\right)\big(1^{\otimes(a_{i-1}-a_{1})}\otimes\Delta\big),

(2.9)

where it is understood that the term between brackets in (
2.9
) is absent if
a_{i}=a_{i-1}+1
.

Example 2.1
.

If
n=9
and
A=\{2,4,5,8\}
, then we have

\Lambda_{\{2,4,5,8\}}=1\otimes\big(\mu_{4}^{\{2,4,5,8\}}\mu_{3}^{\{2,4,5,8\}}\mu_{2}^{\{2,4,5,8\}}\big)(\Lambda)\otimes 1,

with
\mu_{2}^{\{2,4,5,8\}}=(1\otimes\tau_{R})\Delta
,
\mu_{3}^{\{2,4,5,8\}}=1^{\otimes 2}\otimes\Delta
and
\mu_{4}^{\{2,4,5,8\}}=\big(1^{\otimes 5}\otimes\tau_{R}\big)\big(1^{\otimes 4}\otimes\tau_{R}\big)\big(1^{\otimes 3}\otimes\Delta\big)
.

The rationale behind this construction is that each element of
A
, except for its minimum
a_{1}
, corresponds to an application of
\Delta
, whereas the coaction
\tau_{R}
is used to
create the gaps
between the elements of
A
. In the example above,
\tau_{R}
is applied first once and then twice, corresponding to a
hole
of size 1 between 2 and 4 and one of size 2 between 5 and 8. The improvement with respect to the notation of
[
8
]
lies in the fact that here we iterate over the elements of the set
A
rather than over all elements of
[1;n]
, such that we can avoid distinguishing several cases as in
[
8
]
.

Definition 2.4
.

The Askey–Wilson algebra of rank
n-2
, denoted
{\rm AW}(n)
, is the subalgebra of
U_{q}(\mathfrak{sl}_{2})^{\otimes n}
generated by all
\Lambda_{A}
with
A\subseteq[1;n]
.

We will refer to the algorithm described in Definition
2.3
as the right extension process, as to make the distinction with the following, alternative construction method.

2.3
The left and mixed extension processes

An alternative method to associate to each
A\subseteq[1;n]
an element of
U_{q}(\mathfrak{sl}_{2})^{\otimes n}
uses the left coideal comodule subalgebra
\mathcal{I}_{L}
and its coaction
\tau_{L}
, as introduced in Definition
2.2
.

Definition 2.5
.

For any set
A=\{a_{1},\dots,a_{m}\}\subseteq[1;n]
, ordered such that
a_{i}<a_{i+1}
for all
i
, we define
\widehat{\Lambda}_{A}\in U_{q}(\mathfrak{sl}_{2})^{\otimes n}
by

\widehat{\Lambda}_{A}=1^{\otimes(a_{1}-1)}\otimes\left(\overleftarrow{\prod_{i=1}^{m-1}}\widehat{\mu}_{i}^{A}\right)(\Lambda)\otimes 1^{\otimes(n-a_{m})},

with

\displaystyle\widehat{\mu}_{i}^{A}=\left(\overrightarrow{\prod_{\ell=a_{m}-a_{i+1}+1}^{a_{m}-a_{i}-1}}\big(\tau_{L}\otimes 1^{\otimes\ell}\big)\right)\big(\Delta\otimes 1^{\otimes(a_{m}-a_{i+1})}\big),

(2.10)

where of course the term between brackets is absent if
a_{i+1}=a_{i}+1
.

Example 2.2
.

As before we take
n=9
and
A=\{2,4,5,8\}
, and find

\widehat{\Lambda}_{\{2,4,5,8\}}=1\otimes\big(\widehat{\mu}_{1}^{\{2,4,5,8\}}\widehat{\mu}_{2}^{\{2,4,5,8\}}\widehat{\mu}_{3}^{\{2,4,5,8\}}\big)(\Lambda)\otimes 1,

with
\widehat{\mu}_{3}^{\{2,4,5,8\}}=\big(\tau_{L}\otimes 1^{\otimes 2}\big)(\tau_{L}\otimes 1)\Delta
,
\widehat{\mu}_{2}^{\{2,4,5,8\}}=\Delta\otimes 1^{\otimes 3}
and
\widehat{\mu}_{1}^{\{2,4,5,8\}}=\big(\tau_{L}\otimes 1^{\otimes 5}\big)\big(\Delta\otimes 1^{\otimes 4}\big)
.

Again, the idea is that each element of
A
but the maximum
a_{m}
corresponds to an application of
\Delta
, whereas
\tau_{L}

creates the holes
. However, as opposed to Definition
2.3
, we now run through the elements of
A
in decreasing order, from right to left. This is why we refer to the algorithm of Definition
2.5
as the left extension process.

Our first major task will be to prove the equivalence of the right and left extension processes, i.e., to show that they produce the same elements, for each set
A
. To do so, it will often be needed to switch the order of certain algebra morphisms which act on mutually disjoint tensor product positions. More precisely, if
X\in U_{q}(\mathfrak{sl}_{2})^{\otimes 2}
and
\varphi,\psi\colon U_{q}(\mathfrak{sl}_{2})\to U_{q}(\mathfrak{sl}_{2})^{\otimes 2}
, then we have the following basic property:

\displaystyle(1\otimes 1\otimes\psi)(\varphi\otimes 1)X=(\varphi\otimes 1\otimes 1)(1\otimes\psi)X.

(2.11)

Remark 2.1
.

This property also allows to replace the definitions (
2.9
) and (
2.10
) of
\mu_{i}^{A}
and
\widehat{\mu}_{i}^{A}
by certain equivalent expressions. For example,
\mu_{2}^{\{1,5\}}=\big(1^{\otimes 3}\otimes\tau_{R}\big)\big(1^{\otimes 2}\otimes\tau_{R}\big)(1\otimes\tau_{R})\Delta
. Invoking (
2.3
) and (
2.11
), we have

\displaystyle\big(1^{\otimes 2}\otimes\tau_{R}\big)(1\otimes\tau_{R})\tau_{R}=\big(1^{\otimes 2}\otimes\tau_{R}\big)(\Delta\otimes 1)\tau_{R}

\displaystyle\hphantom{\big(1^{\otimes 2}\otimes\tau_R\big)(1\otimes\tau_R)\tau_R}{}=\big(\Delta\otimes 1^{\otimes 2}\big)(1\otimes\tau_{R})\tau_{R}=\big(\Delta\otimes 1^{\otimes 2}\big)(\Delta\otimes 1)\tau_{R},

which allows to rewrite
\mu_{2}^{\{1,5\}}
, and of course this applies to any morphism of the form
\mu_{i}^{A}
with
a_{i}-a_{i-1}>2
. Similarly, one finds from (
2.6
) and (
2.11
) that

\big(\tau_{L}\otimes 1^{\otimes 2}\big)(\tau_{L}\otimes 1)\tau_{L}=\big(1^{\otimes 2}\otimes\Delta\big)(1\otimes\Delta)\tau_{L}

and its generalizations for any
\widehat{\mu}_{i}^{A}
with
a_{i+1}-a_{i}>2
.

This observation will now help us show the equivalence of both extension processes.

Proposition 2.3
.

The right and left extension processes produce exactly the same generators: for each
A\subseteq[1;n]
one has
\Lambda_{A}=\widehat{\Lambda}_{A}
.

Proof.

Without loss of generality, we can assume that
\min(A)=1
and
\max(A)=n
, since otherwise it suffices to add
1
in the remaining positions. We write
A=[1;j_{1}]\cup[i_{2};j_{2}]\cup\dots\cup[i_{m};n]
and proceed by induction on
m

follows from coassociativity:

\Lambda_{[1;n]}=\big(1^{\otimes(n-2)}\otimes\Delta\big)\cdots(1\otimes\Delta)\Delta(\Lambda)=\big(\Delta\otimes 1^{\otimes(n-2)}\big)\cdots(\Delta\otimes 1)\Delta(\Lambda)=\widehat{\Lambda}_{[1;n]}.

Suppose now the claim is true for all sets consisting of
m-1
discrete intervals, including

\widehat{A}=[1;j_{1}]\cup[i_{2};j_{2}]\cup\dots\cup[i_{m-1};j_{m-1}+1]
:
\Lambda_{\widehat{A}}=\widehat{\Lambda}_{\widehat{A}}
. The right extension process asserts

\displaystyle\Lambda_{A}=\big(1^{\otimes(n-2)}\otimes\Delta\big)\cdots\big(1^{\otimes(i_{m}-1)}\otimes\Delta\big)\big(1^{\otimes(i_{m}-2)}\otimes\tau_{R}\big)\cdots\big(1^{\otimes j_{m-1}}\otimes\tau_{R}\big)\Lambda_{\widehat{A}}.

(2.12)

On the other hand, by the left extension process we have

\displaystyle\Lambda_{\widehat{A}}=\widehat{\Lambda}_{\widehat{A}}=\big(\alpha_{j_{m-1}-1}\otimes 1^{\otimes(j_{m-1}-1)}\big)\cdots(\alpha_{1}\otimes 1)\Delta(\Lambda),

(2.13)

with each

. When combining (
2.12
) and (
2.13
), it is clear that the second tensor product position in

is left invariant by all the

. Hence by (

2.12
) through those in (
2.13
), such that

\displaystyle\Lambda_{A}=\big(\alpha_{j_{m-1}-1}\otimes 1^{\otimes(n-2)}\big)\cdots\big(\alpha_{1}\otimes 1^{\otimes(n-j_{m-1})}\big)\Lambda_{B},

(2.14)

with

\Lambda_{B}=\big(1^{\otimes(n-j_{m-1}-1)}\otimes\Delta\big)\cdots\big(1^{\otimes(i_{m}-j_{m-1})}\otimes\Delta\big)\big(1^{\otimes(i_{m}-j_{m-1}-1)}\otimes\tau_{R}\big)\cdots(1\otimes\tau_{R})\Delta(\Lambda).

By (

(1\otimes 1\otimes\tau_{R})(1\otimes\tau_{R})\Delta(\Lambda)=(\tau_{L}\otimes 1\otimes 1)(1\otimes\tau_{R})\Delta(\Lambda)=(\tau_{L}\otimes 1\otimes 1)(\tau_{L}\otimes 1)\Delta(\Lambda).

Repeating this, we find

\Lambda_{B}=\big(1^{\otimes(n-j_{m-1}-1)}\otimes\Delta\big)\cdots\big(1^{\otimes(i_{m}-j_{m-1})}\otimes\Delta\big)\big(\tau_{L}\otimes 1^{\otimes(i_{m}-j_{m-1}-1)}\big)\cdots(\tau_{L}\otimes 1)\Delta(\Lambda).

\displaystyle\Lambda_{B}=\big(\tau_{L}\otimes 1^{\otimes(n-j_{m-1}-1)}\big)\cdots\big(\tau_{L}\otimes 1^{\otimes(n-i_{m}+1)}\big)\big(\Delta\otimes 1^{\otimes(n-i_{m})}\big)\cdots(\Delta\otimes 1)\Delta(\Lambda)

\displaystyle\hphantom{\Lambda_B}{}=\widehat{\Lambda}_{\{1\}\cup[i_{m}-j_{m-1}+1;n-j_{m-1}+1]}.

Moreover, the left extension process and (

\widehat{\Lambda}_{A}=\big(\alpha_{j_{m-1}-1}\otimes 1^{\otimes(n-2)}\big)\cdots\big(\alpha_{1}\otimes 1^{\otimes(n-j_{m-1})}\big)\widehat{\Lambda}_{\{1\}\cup[i_{m}-j_{m-1}+1;n-j_{m-1}+1]}.

2.14

∎

\displaystyle[[\gamma,\delta]_{q},\beta]_{q}=[\gamma,[\delta,\beta]_{q}]_{q}\qquad\mathrm{if}\quad[\beta,\gamma]=0.

Higher rank
\boldsymbol{q}
-Bannai–Ito relations

Each of the results and proofs outlined in this paper carries over to the higher rank
q
-Bannai–Ito algebra, which is canonically isomorphic to the higher rank Askey–Wilson algebra. In this final section we will apply the methods of Section
2
to construct this higher rank extension of the
q
-Bannai–Ito algebra and we will argue why the Theorems
3.1
and
3.2
have natural
q
-Bannai–Ito analogs.

Let us start by the defining the quantum superalgebra
\mathfrak{osp}_{q}(1|2)
as the
\mathbb{Z}_{2}
-graded associative algebra over a field
\mathbb{K}
generated by elements
A_{+}
,
A_{-}
,
K
,
K^{-1}
and
P
subject to the relations

\displaystyle KA_{+}K^{-1}=q^{1/2}A_{+},\qquad KA_{-}K^{-1}=q^{-1/2}A_{-},\qquad\{A_{+},A_{-}\}=\frac{K^{2}-K^{-2}}{q^{1/2}-q^{-1/2}},

\displaystyle\{P,A_{\pm}\}=0,\qquad[P,K]=0,\qquad[P,K^{-1}]=0,\qquad KK^{-1}=K^{-1}K=1,\qquad P^{2}=1.

A Casimir element is given by

\displaystyle\Gamma^{q}=\left(-A_{+}A_{-}+\frac{q^{-1/2}K^{2}-q^{1/2}K^{-2}}{q-q^{-1}}\right)P.

Its coproduct
\Delta\colon\mathfrak{osp}_{q}(1|2)\to\mathfrak{osp}_{q}(1|2)^{\otimes 2}
and counit
\epsilon\colon\mathfrak{osp}_{q}(1|2)\to\mathbb{K}
are defined by

\displaystyle\Delta(A_{\pm})=A_{\pm}\otimes KP+K^{-1}\otimes A_{\pm},\quad\ \ \!

\displaystyle\Delta\big(K^{\pm 1}\big)=K^{\pm 1}\otimes K^{\pm 1},\quad\ \ \!

\displaystyle\Delta(P)=P\otimes P,

(8.1)

\displaystyle\epsilon(A_{\pm})=0,\qquad

\displaystyle\epsilon\big(K^{\pm 1}\big)=1,\qquad

\displaystyle\epsilon(P)=1.

We will also need the definition of the
q
-anticommutator, given by

\{X,Y\}_{q}=q^{1/2}XY+q^{-1/2}YX.

Now let
\mathcal{I}_{R}
be the subalgebra of
\mathfrak{osp}_{q}(1|2)
generated by
A_{+}K
,
A_{-}K
,
K^{2}P
and
\Gamma^{q}
and similarly write
\mathcal{I}_{L}
for the
\mathfrak{osp}_{q}(1|2)
-subalgebra generated by the elements
A_{+}K^{-1}P
,
A_{-}K^{-1}P
,
K^{-2}P
and
\Gamma^{q}
. If one defines the algebra morphism
\tau_{R}\colon\mathcal{I}_{R}\to\mathfrak{osp}_{q}(1|2)\otimes\mathcal{I}_{R}
by

\displaystyle\tau_{R}(A_{-}K)=K^{2}P\otimes A_{-}K,

\displaystyle\tau_{R}(A_{+}K)=\big(K^{-2}P\otimes A_{+}K\big)+q^{-1/2}\big(q-q^{-1}\big)\big(A_{+}^{2}P\otimes A_{-}K\big)

\displaystyle\hphantom{\tau_R(A_+K) =}{}+q^{-1/2}\big(q^{1/2}-q^{-1/2}\big)\big(A_{+}K^{-1}P\otimes K^{2}P\big)+q^{-1/2}\big(q-q^{-1}\big)\big(A_{+}K^{-1}P\otimes\Gamma^{q}\big),

\displaystyle\tau_{R}\big(K^{2}P\big)=1\otimes K^{2}P-\big(q-q^{-1}\big)(A_{+}K\otimes A_{-}K),

\displaystyle\tau_{R}\big(\Gamma^{q}\big)=1\otimes\Gamma^{q},

(8.2)

then
\mathcal{I}_{R}
is readily checked to be a left coideal subalgebra of
\mathfrak{osp}_{q}(1|2)
and a left
\mathfrak{osp}_{q}(1|2)
-comodule with coaction
\tau_{R}
. Similarly, the subalgebra
\mathcal{I}_{L}
is a right coideal subalgebra of
\mathfrak{osp}_{q}(1|2)
and a right
\mathfrak{osp}_{q}(1|2)
-comodule with coaction
\tau_{L}\colon\mathcal{I}_{L}\to\mathcal{I}_{L}\otimes\mathfrak{osp}_{q}(1|2)
defined by

\displaystyle\tau_{L}\big(A_{-}K^{-1}P\big)=A_{-}K^{-1}P\otimes K^{-2}P,

\displaystyle\tau_{L}\big(A_{+}K^{-1}P\big)=A_{+}K^{-1}P\otimes K^{2}P-q^{1/2}\big(q-q^{-1}\big)A_{-}K^{-1}P\otimes A_{+}^{2}P

\displaystyle\hphantom{\tau_L\big(A_+K^{-1}P\big) =}{}-q^{1/2}\big(q^{1/2}-q^{-1/2}\big)K^{-2}P\otimes A_{+}K-q^{1/2}\big(q-q^{-1}\big)\Gamma^{q}\otimes A_{+}K,

\displaystyle\tau_{L}\big(K^{-2}P\big)=K^{-2}P\otimes 1-\big(q-q^{-1}\big)A_{-}K^{-1}P\otimes A_{+}K^{-1}P,

\displaystyle\tau_{L}\big(\Gamma^{q}\big)=\Gamma^{q}\otimes 1.

(8.3)

In analogy to Corollary
2.1
, one can easily verify that the element

\mathcal{I}_{L}
and
\mathcal{I}_{R}

(1\otimes\tau_{R})\Delta\big(\Gamma^{q}\big)=(\tau_{L}\otimes 1)\Delta\big(\Gamma^{q}\big).

2.5

9
Conclusions and outlook

In this paper, we have presented several construction techniques for the higher rank Askey–Wilson algebra
{\rm AW}(n)
, equivalent with the algorithm given in
[
8
]
. The key observation was the existence of a novel, right coideal comodule subalgebra of
U_{q}(\mathfrak{sl}_{2})
. We have proven a large class of algebraic identities inside
{\rm AW}(n)

3.1
and
3.2

q
-Bannai–Ito algebra, which is isomorphic to
{\rm AW}(n)
.

At present, it is still an open question whether the relations obtained in Theorems
3.1
and
3.2
define the algebras
{\rm AW}(n)
abstractly. Using computer algebra packages, we have obtained all subsets
A\subseteq\{1,\dots,n\}
for which the relation (

∗

‣
3
) is satisfied, for several values of
n
, and each of them turned out to be of the form (
3.1
), (
3.2
) or (
3.3
). Hence if supplementary relations are needed to obtain a complete set of defining relations for the algebras
{\rm AW}(n)
, they will most likely not be of the form (

∗

‣
3
). Other related problems are the construction of similar algebras for more general quantum groups
U_{q}(\mathfrak{g})
and the behavior of the obtained algebras at
q
a root of unity different from 1. We plan to look into these questions in further research.
