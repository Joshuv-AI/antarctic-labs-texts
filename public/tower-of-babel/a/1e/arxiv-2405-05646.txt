Outlier-robust Kalman Filtering through Generalised Bayes
Gerardo Duran-Martin, Matias Altamirano, Alexander Y. Shestopaloff, Leandro Sánchez-Betancourt, Jeremias Knoblauch, Matt Jones, François-Xavier Briol, Kevin Murphy

Abstract
We derive a novel, provably robust, and closed-form Bayesian update rule for online filtering in state-space models in the presence of outliers and misspecified measurement models. Our method combines generalised Bayesian inference with filtering methods such as the extended and ensemble Kalman filter. We use the former to show robustness and the latter to ensure computational efficiency in the case of nonlinear models. Our method matches or outperforms other robust filtering methods (such as those based on variational Bayes) at a much lower computational cost. We show this empirically on a range of filtering problems with outlier measurements, such as object tracking, state estimation in high-dimensional chaotic systems, and online learning of neural networks.

Introduction

Probabilistic state-space models (SSMs) are widely used to address problems in
time-series forecasting, online learning, tracking problems, and signal processing.
A key challenge in SSMs is to perform online (sequential) posterior inference,
also known as Bayesian filtering.
If the model is linear and the and the dynamics are known,
then the Kalman filter (KF) algorithm is the optimal filter in terms of
minimal mean squared error
(
Morris, 1976
)
.
If the model is not linear,
approximate inference methods must be used.
Although there are a number of techniques available,
in this paper we focus
on the extended Kalman filter (EKF)

(
Sarkka & Svensson, 2023
, see e.g.,)

and the ensemble Kalman filter (EnKF)
(
Roth et al., 2017a
, see e.g.,)
,
because they both admit closed-form Bayesian updates to Gaussian posterior approximations
and scale to high dimensions.

In a Bayesian setting, the KF and KF-like methods typically use a Gaussian measurement model for computational convenience.
However, a weakness of this assumption is that extreme observations, such as outliers, are not well modelled by Gaussians, leading to model misspecification

(
Grubbs, 1969
;
Hampel et al., 2011
)
.
Moreover, in practice,
the true dynamics are often different from the one assumed by the filter.
This could be because the measurements are corrupted,
or because the user does not have access to the
measurement function.
For example, in tracking problems, sensor measurement errors record the wrong position of an object;
similarly, in economics and finance, there are instances of data providers sending
time series with erroneous values

(see e.g.
Liu, 2020
, for a survey)
.

There is a large literature that studies the filtering problem in the presence of
outliers and misspecified measurement models
1

.
Some approaches extend the state space by introducing hierarchical
priors on the measurement model.
Since closed-form estimation of the state is not generally feasible,
a factorised variational Bayes (VB) approach can be employed to
obtain a fixed-point solution

(
Ting et al., 2007
;
Agamennoni et al., 2012
;
Huang et al., 2016
;
Nurminen et al., 2015
;
Wang et al., 2018
;
Piché et al., 2012
, see e.g.,)
.
Alternatively, Huberised (E)KF-type methods are optimisation-based approaches
to filtering, which minimise the Huber loss;
see
Boncelet & Dickinson (1983)
;
Karlgaard (2015)
;
Das (2023)
and the references therein.
Recently,
Boustati et al. (2020)
proposed a particle filter (PF)
approach that uses generalised Bayes (GB)
to handle model misspecification from outliers;
this builds on work showing that GB updates
are theoretically sound and robust to outliers and model
misspecification

(
Bissiri et al., 2016
;
Jewson et al., 2018
;
Knoblauch et al., 2022
;
Fong et al., 2021
;
Jewson & Rossell, 2022
;
Husain & Knoblauch, 2022
;
Matsubara et al., 2023
;
Matsubara et al., 2022
, see, e.g.,)
.
However, these approaches can be slow, due to the need
to perform iterative optimisation per step (for VB)
(
Knoblauch et al., 2018
)
,
or the need to use a large number of samples
(for PF)
(
Boustati et al., 2020
)
.
See Appendix
A
for an overview of a number of these methods.

In this paper, we propose a novel approach that
tackles the filtering problem
in the presence of outliers and measurement
model misspecification.
Our method is based on the GB approach
where one replaces the log-likelihood of the measurement process
with a loss function.
We call our method the
weighted observation likelihood filter (WoLF)

because it uses a weighted log-likelihood as loss.
A key advantage of this choice is that we can have closed-form (conjugate) update equations
that compute an approximate Gaussian posterior.
We derive WoLF variants for the KF, the EKF, and the ensemble Kalman Filter (EnKF).

Our approach has several key advantages
over prior work:
(i) it is fast and has similar computational cost to the KF thanks to closed-form updates,
(ii) it is flexible to the form of misspecification,
(iii) it is provably robust to outliers, and
(iv) it is easy to implement and straightforward to apply to other filtering methods.
See Figure
1
for an illustrative example.

The remainder of the paper proceeds as follows:
in Section

In Section

Next, in Section

Finally, in Section

We consider a variety of filtering problems, including
2d-tracking,
state estimation in high-dimensional chaotic systems,
and online learning of neural networks.

https://github.com/gerdm/weighted-likelihood-filter

Background: Filtering in SSMs

We briefly review Kalman filtering and the main extensions we consider in this work.
In what follows,
m,d\in\mathbb{N}
are the dimensions
of the state and measurement processes, and
p(\cdot)

is used for densities.
Given an initial state
{\bm{\theta}}_{0}\in\real^{m}
, a Markovian state-space model (SSM) is defined as

\displaystyle{\bm{\theta}}_{t}

\displaystyle=f_{t}({\bm{\theta}}_{t-1})+\bm{\phi}_{t},

(1)

\displaystyle{\bm{y}}_{t}

\displaystyle=h_{t}({\bm{\theta}}_{t})+\bm{\varphi}_{t},

(2)

for
t\in\{1,\ldots,T\}
.
Here,
{\bm{\theta}}_{t}\in\real^{m}

is the (latent) state vector,

{\bm{y}}_{t}\in\real^{d}

is the (observed) measurement vector,

f_{t}:\real^{m}\to\real^{m}
is the dynamics function,

h_{t}:\real^{m}\to\real^{d}
is the measurement function,
2
2

2

In many applications, the function
h_{t}
is modulated by an exogenous feature vector
{\bm{x}}_{t}
.
See e.g., Section
4.2
.

\bm{\phi}_{t}
is a zero-mean Gaussian-distributed random vector with known covariance matrix
\mathbf{Q}_{t}
,
and

\bm{\varphi}_{t}
is any zero-mean random vector representing the measurement noise.
The meaning of the state depends on the application;
for example, it can be
the position of an object,
the state of the atmosphere,
or the weights of a neural network,
as we will see in the results section.

The filtering of an SSM consists of two steps: the predict step and the update step.
Given the result of a previous update step (i.e., the iterative posterior)

p({\bm{\theta}}_{t-1}|{\bm{y}}_{1:t-1})
, the predict step estimates the prior predictive distribution

p({\bm{\theta}}_{t}|{\bm{y}}_{1:t-1})=\int p({\bm{\theta}}_{t}|{\bm{\theta}}_{t-1})p({\bm{\theta}}_{t-1}|{\bm{y}}_{1:t-1}){\rm d}{\bm{\theta}}_{t-1},

(3)

and the update step estimates the new posterior distribution

p({\bm{\theta}}_{t}|{\bm{y}}_{1:t})\propto p({\bm{y}}_{t}|{\bm{\theta}}_{t})p({\bm{\theta}}_{t}|{\bm{y}}_{1:t-1}).

(4)

where
\propto
indicates equality up to a multiplicative normalisation constant.

2.1
The Kalman filter

Suppose the SSM is linear and Gaussian, that is,

\displaystyle{\bm{\theta}}_{t}

\displaystyle=\mathbf{F}_{t}\,{\bm{\theta}}_{t-1}+\bm{\phi}_{t},

(5)

\displaystyle{\bm{y}}_{t}

\displaystyle=\mathbf{H}_{t}\,{\bm{\theta}}_{t}+\bm{\varphi}_{t},

with
\bm{\varphi}_{t}
a zero-mean Gaussian with known covariance matrix
\mathbf{R}_{t}
,

\mathbf{F}_{t}\in\real^{m\times m}
, and
\mathbf{H}_{t}\in\real^{d\times m}
.
Then, given the initial condition
p({\bm{\theta}}_{0})={\cal N}({\bm{\theta}}_{0}\,|\,\bm{\mu}_{0},\,\bm{\Sigma}_{0})
—
a Gaussian density with known mean
\bm{\mu}_{0}\in\mathbb{R}^{m}
and covariance
\bm{\Sigma}_{0}\in\mathbb{R}^{m\times m}
—
the exact Bayesian predict and update steps are given by

\displaystyle p({\bm{\theta}}_{t}|{\bm{y}}_{1:t-1})

\displaystyle={\cal N}({\bm{\theta}}_{t}\,|\,\bm{\mu}_{t|t-1},\,\bm{\Sigma}_{t|t-1}),

(6)

\displaystyle p({\bm{\theta}}_{t}|{\bm{y}}_{1:t})

\displaystyle={\cal N}({\bm{\theta}}_{t}\,|\,\bm{\mu}_{t},\,\bm{\Sigma}_{t}),

(7)

with prior predictive mean and covariance given by

\displaystyle\bm{\mu}_{t|t-1}

\displaystyle=\mathbf{F}_{t}\,\bm{\mu}_{t-1},

(8)

\displaystyle\bm{\Sigma}_{t|t-1}

\displaystyle=\mathbf{F}_{t}\,\bm{\Sigma}_{t-1}\,\mathbf{F}_{t}^{\intercal}+\mathbf{Q}_{t},

and posterior mean and covariance given by

\displaystyle\bm{\Sigma}_{t}^{-1}

\displaystyle=\bm{\Sigma}_{t|t-1}^{-1}+\mathbf{H}_{t}^{\intercal}\,\mathbf{R}_{t}^{-1}\,\mathbf{H}_{t},

(9)

\displaystyle\mathbf{K}_{t}

\displaystyle=\bm{\Sigma}_{t}\,\mathbf{H}_{t}^{\intercal}\,\mathbf{R}_{t}^{-1},

\displaystyle\bm{\mu}_{t}

\displaystyle=\bm{\mu}_{t|t-1}+\mathbf{K}_{t}\,({\bm{y}}_{t}-\hat{\bm{y}}_{t}),

where
\hat{{\bm{y}}}_{t}=\mathbf{H}_{t}\,\bm{\mu}_{t|t-1}
is the predicted observation and

\mathbf{K}_{t}
is the Kalman gain matrix used to map
the error (residual) vector in observation space to an update in latent state space.

2.2
The Extended Kalman filter

When the state and measurement functions are non-linear,
a common approach is to introduce a Gaussian posterior density

q({\bm{\theta}}_{t}|{\bm{y}}_{1:t})={\cal N}({\bm{\theta}}_{t}\,|\,\bm{\mu}_{t},\,\bm{\Sigma}_{t})

that approximates
the posterior density
p({\bm{\theta}}_{t}|{\bm{y}}_{1:t})
through a Kullback-Leibler projection.
This is done using Gaussian approximations of the joint densities

q({\bm{\theta}}_{t},{\bm{\theta}}_{t-1}|{\bm{y}}_{1:t-1})
and

q({\bm{\theta}}_{t},{\bm{y}}_{t}|{\bm{y}}_{1:t-1})

and then performing a closed-form Bayesian update;
see Section 8.4 in
Sarkka & Svensson (2023)
for details.

The EKF is a special case of the above in which one
linearises
f_{t}
in (
1
) and
h_{t}
in (
2
),
and assumes a Gaussian measurement noise
\bm{\varphi}_{t}
.
As a consequence, the predict and update equations resemble those of the standard KF.
More precisely, the EKF
replaces the mean of the state transition density
p({\bm{\theta}}_{t}|{\bm{\theta}}_{t-1})

in (
1
) with

\mathbb{E}[{\bm{\theta}}_{t}|{\bm{\theta}}_{t-1}]\approx\mathbf{F}_{t}({\bm{\theta}}_{t-1}-\bm{\mu}_{t-1})+f_{t}(\bm{\mu}_{t-1})=:\bar{\bm{\mu}}_{t|t-1},

(10)

and the measurement mean of
p({\bm{y}}_{t}|{\bm{\theta}}_{t})

in (
2
) with

\mathbb{E}[{\bm{y}}_{t}|{\bm{\theta}}_{t}]\approx\mathbf{H}_{t}({\bm{\theta}}_{t}-\bm{\mu}_{t|t-1})+h_{t}(\bm{\mu}_{t|t-1})=:\bar{{\bm{y}}}_{t},

(11)

where

\bm{\mu}_{t|t-1}=\mathbb{E}[\bar{\bm{\mu}}_{t|t-1}|\bm{\mu}_{t-1}]=f_{t}(\bm{\mu}_{t-1})
,

\mathbf{F}_{t}
is the Jacobian of
f_{t}
evaluated at
\bm{\mu}_{t-1}
,

\mathbf{H}_{t}
is the Jacobian of
h_{t}
evaluated at
\bm{\mu}_{t|t-1}
, and

\hat{{\bm{y}}}_{t}=\mathbb{E}[\bar{{\bm{y}}}_{t}]=h_{t}(\bm{\mu}_{t|t-1})
.
The linearisation of the transition and measurement around the respective previous means allows the resulting
predict and update equations to closely resemble (
8
) and (
9
).
Therefore, the algorithm remains relatively scalable
and more efficient low-rank extensions can also be derived
(
Chang et al., 2023
;
Lambert et al., 2023
;
Cartea et al., 2023
, see e.g.)
.

2.3
The ensemble Kalman filter

The ensemble Kalman filter (EnKF)
was developed as an alternative
to the extended Kalman filter for high-dimensional and highly non-linear state dynamics

(
Evensen, 1994
;
Burgers et al., 1998
;
Roth et al., 2017a
, see e.g.,)
.
This method avoids
the need to compute Jacobians and the storage of an explicit
m\times m
posterior covariance matrix,
by instead representing the belief state
with an ensemble of
N\in\mathbb{N}
particles
\hat{{\bm{\theta}}}_{t}^{\left(i\right)}\in\real^{m}
for
i=1,\ldots,N

evolved through time.
This enables the method to scale to high-dimensional
state spaces with complex nonlinear dynamics, such as those frequently arising in data assimilation for
weather forecasting
(
Evensen, 2009
)
.
In the EnKF,
the predict step samples
(
1
) to obtain
\hat{{\bm{\theta}}}_{t|t-1}^{\left(i\right)}
.
Then, the update step samples predictions
\hat{{\bm{y}}}_{t|t-1}^{\left(i\right)}\in\real^{d}
, for each particle,
according to

\hat{{\bm{y}}}_{t|t-1}^{\left(i\right)}\sim\mathcal{N}\left(h_{t}\left(\hat{{\bm{\theta}}}_{t|t-1}^{(i)}\right),\mathbf{R}_{t}\right).

(12)

The particles are then updated according to

\hat{{\bm{\theta}}}_{t}^{\left(i\right)}=\hat{{\bm{\theta}}}_{t|t-1}^{\left(i\right)}+\bar{\mathbf{K}}_{t}\big({\bm{y}}_{t}-\hat{{\bm{y}}}_{t|t-1}^{(i)}\big),

(13)

with gain matrix
\bar{\mathbf{K}}_{t}
calculated from the ensemble.
See Appendix
D.3
for details.

3
The weighted observation likelihood filter

Our method is based on the GB
approach
where one modifies the update step in (
4
) to use a loss function

\ell_{t}:\real^{m}\to\real
in place of the negative log-likelihood of the measurement process.
This gives the generalised posterior

q({\bm{\theta}}_{t}|{\bm{y}}_{1:t})\propto\exp(-\ell_{t}({\bm{\theta}}_{t}))q({\bm{\theta}}_{t}|{\bm{y}}_{1:t-1}).

(14)

We propose to gain robustness to outliers
in observation space by taking the loss function to be the model’s negative log-likelihood scaled by a data-dependent
weighting term

\ell_{t}({\bm{\theta}}_{t})=-W^{2}({\bm{y}}_{t},\hat{{\bm{y}}}_{t})\,\log q({\bm{y}}_{t}|{\bm{\theta}}_{t}),

(15)

with

W:\real^{d}\times\real^{d}\to\real
the weighting function and

q({\bm{y}}_{t}|{\bm{\theta}}_{t})
the modelled measurement process.
We call our method the
weighted observation likelihood filter (WoLF)
.
To specify an instance of our method, ones needs to define
the likelihood
q({\bm{y}}_{t}|{\bm{\theta}}_{t})

and the weighting function
W
.
In the next subsections, we show the flexibility of WoLF
and derive weighted-likelihood-based KF, EKF, and EnKF algorithms.
Setting
W({\bm{y}}_{t},\bar{{\bm{y}}}_{t})=1
trivially recovers existing methods, but we will instead use non-constant weighting functions inspired by the work of
(
Barp et al., 2019
;
Matsubara et al., 2022
;
Altamirano et al., 2023a
;
Altamirano et al., 2023b
)
.

Our method readily extends to other nonlinear filtering algorithms.
For example,

3.3
The choice of weighting function

Weighted likelihoods have a well-established history in Bayesian inference
and have demonstrated their efficacy in improving robustness

(
Grünwald, 2012
;
Holmes & Walker, 2017
;
Grünwald & van Ommen, 2017
;
Miller & Dunson, 2018
;
Bhattacharya et al., 2019
;
Alquier & Ridgway, 2020
;
Dewaskar et al., 2023
)
.
In this context, the corresponding posteriors are often referred to as fractional, tempered, or power posteriors.
In most existing work, the determination of weights relies on heuristics
and the assigned weights remain constant across all data points
so that
W({\bm{y}}_{t},\hat{{\bm{y}}}_{t})=w\in\real
for all
t
.
In contrast, we dynamically incorporate information from the most recent observations without incurring additional computational costs
by defining the weight as a function of the current observation

{\bm{y}}_{t}

and its prediction

\hat{{\bm{y}}}_{t}=h_{t}(\bm{\mu}_{t|t-1})
,
which is based on all of the past observations.

To define the weighting function,
we take inspiration from previous work
for dealing with outliers.
In particular,
Wang et al. (2018)

proposed classifying robust filtering algorithms
into two main types:

compensation-based

algorithms,
which incorporate information from tail events into the model
in a robust way

(
Huang et al., 2016
;
Agamennoni et al., 2012
, see, e.g.,)
,
and

detect-and-reject
algorithms,
which
assume that outlier observations bear no useful information
and thus are ignored

(
Wang et al., 2018
;
Mu & Yuen, 2015
, see, e.g.,)
.
Below we show how both of these strategies can be implemented
using our WoLF method by merely changing the weighting function.

Inverse multi-quadratic weighting function:

As an example of a compensation-based method,
we follow
Altamirano et al. (2023b)

and use the Inverse Multi-Quadratic (IMQ) weighting,
which in our SSM setting is

\displaystyle W({\bm{y}}_{t},\hat{{\bm{y}}}_{t})

\displaystyle=\left(1+\frac{||{\bm{y}}_{t}-\hat{{\bm{y}}}_{t}||_{2}^{2}}{c^{2}}\right)^{-1/2},

(17)

where
c>0
is the soft threshold and
\|\cdot\|
denotes the
l_{2}
norm.
We call WoLF with IMQ weighting “WoLF-IMQ”.

Mahalanobis-based weighting function:

The
l_{2}
norm in the IMQ can be modified to account for the covariance structure of the measurement
process by replacing it with the Mahalanobis distance between
{\bm{y}}_{t}
and
\hat{{\bm{y}}}_{t}
:

\displaystyle W({\bm{y}}_{t},\hat{{\bm{y}}}_{t})

\displaystyle=\left(1+\frac{\|\mathbf{R}_{t}^{-1/2}({\bm{y}}_{t}-\hat{{\bm{y}}}_{t})\|_{2}^{2}}{c^{2}}\right)^{-1/2}.

(18)

We call WoLF with this weighting function the WoLF-MD method.
This type of weighted IMQ function has been used extensively in the kernel literature

(
Chen et al., 2019
;
Detommaso et al., 2018
;
Riabiz et al., 2022
, see e.g.)
.

Threshold Mahalanobis-based weighting function:

As an example of a detect-and-reject method,
we consider

W({\bm{y}}_{t},\hat{{\bm{y}}}_{t})=\begin{cases}1&\text{if }\|\mathbf{R}_{t}^{-1/2}({\bm{y}}_{t}-\hat{{\bm{y}}}_{t})\|_{2}^{2}\leq c\\
0&\text{otherwise}\end{cases}

(19)

with
c>0
the fixed threshold.
The weighting function (
19
) corresponds
to ignoring information from estimated measurements whose Mahalanobis distance
to the true measurement is larger than
some predefined threshold
c
.
In the linear setting, this weighting function is related to the benchmark method employed in
Ting et al. (2007)
.
We refer to WoLF with this weighting function as “WoLF-TMD”.

For
{\bm{y}}_{t}\in\real^{d}
with
d=m\gg 1
and diagonal measurement covariance

\mathbf{R}_{t}=\text{diag}\left(r_{t,1},\ldots,r_{t,d}\right)
,
the WoLF-TMD function can be modified to weight individual observations so that

W:\real^{d}\times\real^{d}\to\real^{d}
.
See Section
4.3
for an example and
Appendix
D.2

for a discussion.

The proposed weighting functions — the IMQ, the MD, and the TMD —
are defined such that
W:\real^{d}\times\real^{d}\to[0,1]

and therefore can only down-weight observations.
This means that our updates are always conservative,
i.e., our posteriors will be wider in the presence of outliers
(see Figure
1
for an example).

3.4
Theoretical properties

In this section, we prove
the outlier-robustness for WoLF-type methods.
We use the classical framework of
Huber (1981)
.
Consider measurements
{\bm{y}}_{1:t}
.
We measure the influence of a contamination
{\bm{y}}^{c}_{t}
by examining the divergence
between the posterior with the original observation
{\bm{y}}_{t}

and the posterior with the contamination
{\bm{y}}_{t}^{c}
, which is allowed to be arbitrarily large.
As a function of
{\bm{y}}_{t}^{c}
, this divergence is called the
posterior influence function
(PIF) and was studied in
Ghosh et al. (2016)
;
Matsubara et al. (2022)
;
Altamirano et al. (2023a)
;
Altamirano et al. (2023b)
. Following
Altamirano et al. (2023b)
, we consider the Kullback-Leibler (KL) divergence,
which allows us to obtain closed-form expressions for Gaussians.
The PIF is given by

\displaystyle\operatorname{PIF}({\bm{y}}_{t}^{c},{\bm{y}}_{1:t})=\operatorname{KL}\left(p({\bm{\theta}}_{t}|{\bm{y}}^{c}_{t},{\bm{y}}_{1:t-1})\|p({\bm{\theta}}_{t}|{\bm{y}}_{t},{\bm{y}}_{1:t-1})\right).

If
\sup_{{\bm{y}}_{t}^{c}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}}|\operatorname{PIF}({\bm{y}}_{t}^{c},{\bm{y}}_{1:t})|<\infty
,
then the posterior is called outlier-robust, which
indicates that as
\|{\bm{y}}_{t}-{\bm{y}}_{t}^{c}\|_{2}\to\infty
,
the contamination’s effect on the posterior is bounded.
This is the Bayesian equivalent to bias-robustness in frequentist statistics.

Theorem 3.2
.

Consider the linear Gaussian SSM (
5
) or its linearised (EKF) approximation.
The standard (E)KF posterior has an unbounded PIF and is not outlier robust.

In contrast, the generalised posterior presented in
Proposition

3.1

has bounded PIF and is, therefore, outlier robust for any weighting function
W
such that

\sup_{{\bm{y}}_{t}\in\real^{d}}W({\bm{y}}_{t},\hat{{\bm{y}}}_{t})<\infty
and

5
Conclusion

We introduced a provably robust filtering algorithm based on generalised Bayes
which we call the weighted observation likelihood filter or WoLF.
Our algorithm is as fast as the KF, has closed-form update equations, and is
straightforward to apply to various filtering methods.
The superior performance of the WoLF is shown on a wide range of filtering problems.
In contrast, alternative robust methods either have higher computational complexity than the WoLF, or
similar computational complexity but not higher performance.

Future work will investigate how to overcome the limitations of our approach. For example,
(i) not being robust to outliers in the state-process,
(ii) the assumption of a known covariance
\mathbf{R}_{t}
,
(iii) the assumption of known dynamics for the covariance
\mathbf{Q}_{t}
, and
(iv) the assumption of a unimodal posterior.

Impact statement

This paper presents work whose goal is to advance the field of Machine Learning. There are many potential societal consequences of our work, none which we feel must be specifically highlighted here.

Appendix A
Background on existing robust filters
and related methods

Robust Bayesian filtering and the minimum variance estimator dates back to
Masreliez (1975)
and
Masreliez & Martin (1977)
.
These methods propose a modified KF-recursion for the linear SSM written in terms of the score function of the measurement prior predictive.
In these early works, inference relies on Monte Carlo.

West (1981)
follows these earlier works
and proposes KF-like updates for non-normal measurement models.
That paper also studies whether several popular likelihood functions are robust in the sense of “ignoring outliers” — the analysis includes the Student-t, power exponential, Huber, logistic, and stable-law likelihoods.
For the case of Student-t likelihood with one degree of freedom (i.e., Cauchy)
and linear dynamics, the update equation for the posterior mean derived in
West (1981)
is equivalent to ours when using IMQ weights.
However, the approach taken in
West (1981)
cannot recover our TMD scheme, due to being tied to a given choice of measurement model.
Furthermore,
West (1981)
does not provide a theoretical foundation to use their approach in non-linear measurement models.

The work in
Meyr & Spies (1984)
proposes a scheme to eliminate observations that a KF procedure labels as outliers.
Their methodology relies on a “secondary decision system” which checks for discrepancies between the predicted mean and the observation,
eliminating observations with high discrepancies.
This scheme is analogous to the TMD scheme, however, it is not shown to be provably robust.

Another alternative to robustify measurement models against outliers is the work in
Agee et al. (1980)
. Their paper introduces Gaussian-mixture models for robust filtering and smoothing. Their inference method is based on particle filtering.

To the best of our knowledge, the first work that proposes robust filtering in the context of robust statistics is
Calvet et al. (2015)
.
Their robust filter follows
Masreliez & Martin (1977)
and is based on a “Huberisation” of the derivative of the log-measurement density (score function), which then they integrate.
See
Schick & Mitter (1994)
for a comprehensive review of classical robust-KF methods for linear SSMs.
Recently proposed provably-robust methods include the work by
Boustati et al. (2020)
and
Cao et al. (2022)
.

Finally, we discuss similarities and differences with the Bayesian learning rule (BLR) line of work by
Khan & Rue (2023)
.
BLR also allows the replacing of the likelihood with a loss function, but that loss depends only on the parameter and the observation, i.e., BLR uses
\ell_{t}({\bm{\theta}})=L({\bm{y}}_{t},f_{t}({\bm{\theta}}))
, with
L:\real^{d}\times\real^{d}
a loss function.
By contrast, our loss depends on the weight
W_{t}
, which in turn depends on the belief state,
via
\hat{{\bm{y}}}_{t}=f_{t}(\bm{\mu}_{t|t-1})
, which in turn is a function of all the past data, i.e.,
our loss has the form
\ell_{t}({\bm{\theta}})=L({\bm{y}}_{1:t},f_{t}({\bm{\theta}}))
.
In addition, WoLF is designed for the online inference setting, whereas BLR, at least in its standard form, is designed for offline inference.
Finally, note that the exponential family extension of BLR
Lin et al. (2019)

has also been proposed as a way to get robustness by using scale mixture posteriors.
However, this induces robustness in parameter space, whereas we focus on robustness in observation space.
In particular, the weighting term for the BLR mixture model depends on the distance in parameter space,

{\bm{\theta}}_{t}-\bm{\mu}_{t}
, whereas ours depends on the difference in observation space,
{\bm{y}}_{t}-\hat{{\bm{y}}_{t}}
.

A.1
Variational-based methods

In this section, we provide an overview of variational-Bayes (VB) robust filtering methods.
As above,
{\bm{\theta}}_{t}
is the state vector of interest and

\bm{\Psi}_{t}
are additional state parameters.
Given the SSM (
1
)
and measurement model
p({\bm{y}}_{t}|{\bm{\theta}}_{t},\bm{\Psi}_{t})
,
VB-based methods seek an approximate posterior distribution over the extended state process
\bm{\Phi}_{t}=({\bm{\theta}}_{t},\bm{\Psi}_{t})

that factorises as

q(\bm{\Phi}_{t})=\prod_{k=1}^{K}q(\bm{\Phi}_{t,k}),

(24)

with
\bm{\Phi}_{t}=(\bm{\Phi}_{t,1},\ldots,\bm{\Phi}_{t,K})
, and
K
the number of collections.
It can be shown that the log-density
q^{*}
that minimises the KL divergence
between the true posterior distribution and the variational distribution is given by

\log q^{*}(\bm{\Phi}_{t,k})=\mathbb{E}_{\neg k}\left[\log p({\bm{y}}_{t},\bm{\Phi}_{t})\right]+C,

(25)

where
C
is the normalising constant of
q^{*}
, and
the notation
\mathbb{E}_{\neg k}\left[\cdot\right]
denotes the conditional expectation given
all elements in
\bm{\Phi}_{t}
except from
\bm{\Phi}_{t,k}
.
See Section 10.1.1 in
Bishop (2006)
for details. Below, we discuss the robust VB-based filtering variants we use in the paper.

A.2
KF-IW method of
Agamennoni et al. (2012)

Agamennoni et al. (2012)
extend the state-space to be
\bm{\Psi}_{t}=({\bm{\theta}}_{t},\mathbf{R}_{t})
,
where
\mathbf{R}_{t}
is the measurement covariance.
Note that the classical KF setting,
\mathbf{R}_{t}
is known.
The SSM is of the form

\displaystyle p({\bm{\theta}}_{t}|{\bm{\theta}}_{t-1})

\displaystyle={\cal N}({\bm{\theta}}_{t}\,|\,\mathbf{F}_{t}{\bm{\theta}}_{t-1},\,\mathbf{Q}_{t}),

(26)

\displaystyle p(\mathbf{R}_{t})

\displaystyle={\cal W}^{-1}(\mathbf{R}_{t}\,|\,\nu\bm{\Lambda},\nu),

\displaystyle p({\bm{y}}_{t}|{\bm{\theta}}_{t},\mathbf{R}_{t})

\displaystyle={\cal N}({\bm{y}}_{t}\,|\,\mathbf{H}_{t}{\bm{\theta}}_{t},\,\mathbf{R}_{t}),

where
{\cal W}^{-1}(\cdot|{\bf P},\eta)

B.1

Consider the modified observation model of (
32

\displaystyle p(w_{t})

\displaystyle={\rm Gam}(w_{t}|\alpha,\beta),

(38)

\displaystyle p({\bm{y}}_{t}|\hat{{\bm{y}}}_{t})

\displaystyle=\mathcal{N}({\bm{y}}_{t}|\hat{{\bm{y}}}_{t},w_{t}^{-1}\mathbf{R}_{t}),

(39)

\alpha,\beta>0

\displaystyle p(w_{t}|{\bm{y}}_{t})

\displaystyle\propto w_{t}^{\alpha-1}e^{-\beta w_{t}}\left|w_{t}\mathbf{R}_{t}^{-1}\right|^{1/2}\exp\left(-\frac{1}{2}{\bm{e}}_{t}^{\top}w_{t}\mathbf{R}_{t}^{-1}{\bm{e}}_{t}\right)

(41)

where

W_{t}=\operatornamewithlimits{argmax}_{w_{t}\in\real^{+}}p(w_{t}|{\bm{y}}_{t})=\frac{\alpha+\frac{n_{y}}{2}-1}{\beta+\frac{1}{2}\left\|{\bm{e}}_{t}\right\|_{\mathbf{R}_{t}^{-1}}^{2}}.

(42)

For a given

and

\displaystyle\quad\beta=\frac{c^{2}}{2}\,,

(43)

where

is the number of measurements.
We obtain

\displaystyle w_{t}

\displaystyle=\left(1+\frac{\left\|{\bm{e}}_{t}\right\|_{\mathbf{R}_{t}^{-1}}^{2}}{c^{2}}\right)^{-1/2}.

(44)

This is the Mahalanobis-based IMQ weighting function (
18

loglikelihood approximation

\log p\left({\bm{y}}_{t}|\hat{{\bm{y}}}_{t}\right)\approx w_{t}^{2}\log\mathcal{N}\left({\bm{y}}_{t}|\hat{{\bm{y}}}_{t},\mathbf{R}_{t}\right).

Lemma C.1
.

Consider the linear Gaussian SSM. The standard KF posterior has an unbounded PIF and is not outlier robust.

Proof.

Let
p({\bm{\theta}}_{t}|{\bm{y}}_{t},{\bm{y}}_{1:t-1})=\mathcal{N}({\bm{\theta}}_{t}|\bm{\mu}_{t},\bm{\Sigma}_{t})
, and

p({\bm{\theta}}_{t}|{\bm{y}}^{c}_{t},{\bm{y}}_{1:t-1})=\mathcal{N}({\bm{\theta}}_{t}|\bm{\mu}_{t}^{c},\bm{\Sigma}_{t}^{c})
, the uncontaminated and contaminated standard Kalman filter posterior. Here

\displaystyle\bm{\mu}_{t}=\bm{\mu}_{t|t-1}+\mathbf{K}_{t}\left({\bm{y}}_{t}-\hat{{\bm{y}}}_{t}\right),

\displaystyle\bm{\mu}_{t}^{c}=\bm{\mu}_{t|t-1}+\mathbf{K}_{t}^{c}\left({\bm{y}}_{t}^{c}-\hat{{\bm{y}}}_{t}\right),

\displaystyle\bm{\Sigma}_{t}^{-1}=\bm{\Sigma}_{t|t-1}^{-1}+\mathbf{H}_{t}^{\intercal}\mathbf{R}_{t}^{-1}\mathbf{H}_{t},

\displaystyle(\bm{\Sigma}_{t}^{c})^{-1}=\bm{\Sigma}_{t|t-1}^{-1}+\mathbf{H}_{t}^{\intercal}\mathbf{R}_{t}^{-1}\mathbf{H}_{t}.

Here
\bm{\mu}_{t|t-1},\mathbf{K}_{t},\mathbf{H}_{t},\bm{\Sigma}^{-1}_{t|t-1},\mathbf{R}_{t}^{-1}
do not depend on the contamination
{\bm{y}}_{t}^{c}
.
It is clear that
\bm{\Sigma}_{t}^{-1}=(\bm{\Sigma}_{t}^{c})^{-1}
, hence
\mathbf{K}_{t}^{c}=\bm{\Sigma}_{t}^{c}\mathbf{H}_{t}^{\intercal}\mathbf{R}_{t}^{-1}=\bm{\Sigma}_{t}\mathbf{H}_{t}^{\intercal}\mathbf{R}_{t}^{-1}=\mathbf{K}_{t}
.
Using the fact that given two
d
-dimensional Gaussians
\mathcal{N}(\bm{\mu}_{0},\bm{\Sigma}_{0})
and
\mathcal{N}(\bm{\mu}_{1},\bm{\Sigma}_{1})
,
the KL divergence is

\text{KL}(\mathcal{N}(\bm{\mu}_{0},\bm{\Sigma}_{0})\|\mathcal{N}(\bm{\mu}_{1},\bm{\Sigma}_{1}))=\frac{1}{2}\left(\operatorname{Tr}\left(\bm{\Sigma}_{1}^{-1}\bm{\Sigma}_{0}\right)-m+\left(\bm{\mu}_{1}-\bm{\mu}_{0}\right)^{\intercal}\bm{\Sigma}_{1}^{-1}\left(\bm{\mu}_{1}-\bm{\mu}_{0}\right)+\ln\left(\frac{\det\bm{\Sigma}_{1}}{\det\bm{\Sigma}_{0}}\right)\right),

and given that
\operatorname{Tr}(\mathbf{I}_{p})=p
, we can derive the PIF as

\displaystyle\operatorname{PIF}({\bm{y}}_{t}^{c},{\bm{y}}_{1:t})

\displaystyle=\operatorname{KL}\left(p({\bm{\theta}}_{t}|{\bm{y}}^{c}_{t},{\bm{y}}_{1:t-1})\|p({\bm{\theta}}_{t}|{\bm{y}}_{t},{\bm{y}}_{1:t-1})\right)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\Bigg(\Tr\left(\bm{\Sigma}_{t}^{-1}\bm{\Sigma}_{t}\right)-m+\left(\bm{\mu}_{t}-\bm{\mu}_{t}^{c}\right)^{\intercal}\bm{\Sigma}_{t}^{-1}\left(\bm{\mu}_{t}-\bm{\mu}_{t}^{c}\right)+\ln\left(\frac{\det\bm{\Sigma}_{t}}{\det\bm{\Sigma}_{t}}\right)\Bigg)

\displaystyle=\Tr\left(\mathbf{I}_{m}\right)-m+\frac{1}{2}\left(\bm{\mu}_{t}-\bm{\mu}_{t}^{c}\right)^{\intercal}\bm{\Sigma}_{t}^{-1}\left(\bm{\mu}_{t}-\bm{\mu}_{t}^{c}\right)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\left({\bm{y}}_{t}-{\bm{y}}_{t}^{c}\right)^{\intercal}\mathbf{K}_{t}^{\intercal}\bm{\Sigma}_{t}^{-1}\mathbf{K}_{t}\left({\bm{y}}_{t}-{\bm{y}}_{t}^{c}\right).

Here,
\mathbf{K}_{t}^{\intercal}\bm{\Sigma}_{t}^{-1}\mathbf{K}_{t}
is positive definite.
To see this, first note that for every invertible matrix
B
and conformable positive definite matrix
A
,
B^{\intercal}AB
is positive definite
(
Horn & Johnson, 2012
, Chapter 7.1)
.
Since
\mathbf{K}_{t}
is a product of invertible matrices, it therefore is itself invertible.
In fact, let
{\bm{z}}\in\mathbb{R}^{m}
be a non-zero vector.
We can write
{\bm{z}}^{\intercal}B^{\intercal}AB{\bm{z}}=\tilde{{\bm{z}}}^{\intercal}A\tilde{{\bm{z}}}
, for
\tilde{{\bm{z}}}=B{\bm{z}}
.
We know that
\tilde{{\bm{z}}}=B{\bm{z}}\neq 0
, because
B
is invertible.
Then, since
A
is positive definite and
\tilde{{\bm{z}}}\in\real^{m}
,
it holds that
\tilde{{\bm{z}}}^{\intercal}A\tilde{{\bm{z}}}>0
, proving that
B^{\intercal}AB
is positive definite.
Finally, since
\bm{\Sigma}_{t}^{-1}
is positive definite, it therefore holds that
\mathbf{K}_{t}^{\intercal}\bm{\Sigma}_{t}^{-1}\mathbf{K}_{t}
is positive definite, too.

This in turn allows us to lower-bound the PIF by

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}\lambda_{\min}(\mathbf{K}_{t}^{\intercal}\bm{\Sigma}_{t}^{-1}\mathbf{K}_{t})\|{\bm{y}}_{t}-{\bm{y}}_{t}^{c}\|^{2}_{2}\leq\frac{1}{2}\left({\bm{y}}_{t}-{\bm{y}}_{t}^{c}\right)^{\intercal}\mathbf{K}_{t}^{\intercal}\bm{\Sigma}_{t}^{-1}\mathbf{K}_{t}\left({\bm{y}}_{t}-{\bm{y}}_{t}^{c}\right)=\operatorname{PIF}({\bm{y}}_{t}^{c},{\bm{y}}_{1:t}),

where we use the fact that for every positive definite matrix
A
and vector
{\bm{z}}
,
we have
\lambda_{\min}(A)\|{\bm{z}}\|^{2}\leq{\bm{z}}^{\intercal}A{\bm{z}}\leq\lambda_{\max}(A)\|{\bm{z}}\|^{2}
,
where
\lambda_{\min}(A)
and
\lambda_{\max}(A)
are the minimum and maximum eigenvalues of
A
,
respectively
(
Horn & Johnson, 2012
, Chapter 5.7)
.
Moreover, we know that
\lambda_{\min}\left(\mathbf{K}_{t}^{\intercal}\bm{\Sigma}_{t}^{-1}\mathbf{K}_{t}\right)>0
,
since
\mathbf{K}_{t}^{\intercal}\bm{\Sigma}_{t}^{-1}\mathbf{K}_{t}
is positive definite, and it does not depend on
{\bm{y}}_{t}^{c}
.
Therefore, it indeed holds that
\operatorname{PIF}({\bm{y}}_{t}^{c},{\bm{y}}_{1:t})\rightarrow+\infty
as
\|{\bm{y}}_{t}-{\bm{y}}_{t}^{c}\|^{2}_{2}\to+\infty
.
∎

Lemma C.2
.

Consider the linear Gaussian SSM. The generalised posterior presented in
Proposition

3.1
has bounded PIF and is, therefore, outlier robust for any weighting function
W
such that

\sup_{{\bm{y}}_{t}\in\mathbf{R}^{d}}W({\bm{y}}_{1:t})<\infty
and
\sup_{{\bm{y}}_{t}\in\mathbf{R}^{d}}W({\bm{y}}_{1:t})^{2}\,\|{\bm{y}}_{t}\|_{2}<\infty
.

Proof.

Let
W
be a weighting function such that

\sup_{{\bm{y}}_{t}\in\real^{d}}W({\bm{y}}_{1:t})<\infty
and
\sup_{{\bm{y}}_{t}\in\real^{d}}W({\bm{y}}_{1:t})^{2}\|{\bm{y}}_{t}\|_{2}<\infty
. Lets define
w_{t}:=W^{2}({\bm{y}}_{1:t})
, and
w_{t}^{c}:=W^{2}({\bm{y}}_{t}^{c},{\bm{y}}_{1:1-t})
.
Let
q({\bm{\theta}}_{t}|{\bm{y}}_{t},{\bm{y}}_{1:t-1})=\mathcal{N}({\bm{\theta}}_{t}|\bm{\mu}_{t},\bm{\Sigma}_{t})
, and

q({\bm{\theta}}_{t}|{\bm{y}}^{c}_{t},{\bm{y}}_{1:t-1})=\mathcal{N}({\bm{\theta}}_{t}|\bm{\mu}_{t}^{c},\bm{\Sigma}_{t}^{c})
, where

\displaystyle\bm{\mu}_{t}=\bm{\mu}_{t|t-1}+w_{t}\mathbf{K}_{t}\left({\bm{y}}_{t}-\hat{{\bm{y}}}_{t}\right),

\displaystyle\bm{\mu}_{t}^{c}=\bm{\mu}_{t|t-1}+w_{t}^{c}\mathbf{K}_{t}^{c}\left({\bm{y}}_{t}^{c}-\hat{{\bm{y}}}_{t}\right),

\displaystyle\bm{\Sigma}_{t}^{-1}=\bm{\Sigma}_{t|t-1}^{-1}+w_{t}\mathbf{H}_{t}^{\intercal}\mathbf{R}_{t}^{-1}\mathbf{H}_{t},

\displaystyle(\bm{\Sigma}_{t}^{c})^{-1}=\bm{\Sigma}_{t|t-1}^{-1}+w_{t}^{c}\mathbf{H}_{t}^{\intercal}\mathbf{R}_{t}^{-1}\mathbf{H}_{t},

as in
Algorithm

1

Then, the PIF has the form

\displaystyle\operatorname{PIF}({\bm{y}}_{t},{\bm{y}}_{1:t-1})=\frac{1}{2}\Bigg(

\displaystyle\underbrace{\Tr\left((\bm{\Sigma}_{t}^{c})^{-1}\bm{\Sigma}_{t}\right)-p}_{(1)}+\underbrace{\left(\bm{\mu}_{t}-\bm{\mu}_{t}^{c}\right)^{\intercal}(\bm{\Sigma}_{t}^{c})^{-1}\left(\bm{\mu}_{t}-\bm{\mu}_{t}^{c}\right)}_{(2)}+\underbrace{\ln\left(\frac{\det\bm{\Sigma}_{t}^{c}}{\det\bm{\Sigma}_{t}}\right)}_{(3)}\Bigg).

Now, we will get a bound for each term in the PIF. The first term can be bounded as

\displaystyle(1)

\displaystyle=\Tr\left((\bm{\Sigma}_{t}^{c})^{-1}\bm{\Sigma}_{t}\right)-m\leq\Tr\left((\bm{\Sigma}_{t}^{c})^{-1}\right)\Tr\left(\bm{\Sigma}_{t}\right)-m,

where we use the fact that for two positive semidefinite matrices
A
,
B
, it holds that
\Tr(AB)\leq\Tr(A)\Tr(B)
.
Observing that
\bm{\Sigma}_{t}
does not depend on
y_{t}^{c}
, we can now write

C_{1}=\bm{\Sigma}_{t}
, so that by using the arithmetic rules of traces, we obtain

\displaystyle(1)

\displaystyle\leq C_{1}\Tr\left(\left(\bm{\Sigma}_{t}^{c}\right)^{-1}\right)-m

\displaystyle=C_{1}\Tr\left(\bm{\Sigma}_{t|t-1}^{-1}+w_{t}^{c}\mathbf{H}_{t}^{\intercal}\mathbf{R}_{t}^{-1}\mathbf{H}_{t}\right)-m

\displaystyle=C_{1}\Tr\left(\bm{\Sigma}_{t|t-1}^{-1}\right)+C_{1}w_{t}^{c}\Tr\left(\mathbf{H}_{t}^{\intercal}\mathbf{R}_{t}^{-1}\mathbf{H}_{t}\right)-m.

Here, we use the fact that the trace of a sum is a sum of traces, and the trace of the constant times matrix is equal to the constant times trace of the matrix.
Finally, since
\sup_{{\bm{y}}_{t}^{c}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}}w_{t}^{c}\leq C_{2}<\infty
, the entire expression can be bounded by a constant

C_{3}<\infty
that does not depend on the contamination
{\bm{y}}_{t}^{c}
as

\displaystyle(1)

\displaystyle\leq C_{1}\Tr\left(\bm{\Sigma}_{t|t-1}^{-1}\right)+C_{1}C_{2}\Tr\left(\mathbf{H}_{t}^{\intercal}\mathbf{R}_{t}^{-1}\mathbf{H}_{t}\right)-m=C_{3},

where we use the fact that both traces are finite since both matrices are real-valued.
Next, we bound the second term by noting that it is the squared Mahalanobis norm of
\bm{\mu}_{t}-\bm{\mu}_{t}^{c}
with respect to
\bm{\Sigma}_{t}^{c}
, and we write it as
\|\bm{\mu}_{t}-\bm{\mu}_{t}^{c}\|_{\bm{\Sigma}_{t}^{c}}
(in particular, it then satisfies all the properties of a norm). Therefore, we apply the triangle inequality to obtain

\displaystyle(2)

\displaystyle=(\|\bm{\mu}_{t}-\bm{\mu}_{t}^{c}\|_{\bm{\Sigma}_{t}^{c}})^{2}

\displaystyle=(\|w_{t}\mathbf{K}_{t}({\bm{y}}_{t}-\hat{{\bm{y}}}_{t})-w_{t}^{c}\mathbf{K}_{t}^{c}({\bm{y}}_{t}^{c}-\hat{{\bm{y}}}_{t})\|_{\bm{\Sigma}_{t}^{c}})^{2}

\displaystyle\leq(\|w_{t}\mathbf{K}_{t}({\bm{y}}_{t}-\hat{{\bm{y}}}_{t})\|_{\bm{\Sigma}_{t}^{c}}+\|w_{t}^{c}\mathbf{K}_{t}^{c}({\bm{y}}_{t}^{c}-\hat{{\bm{y}}}_{t})\|_{\bm{\Sigma}_{t}^{c}})^{2}

\displaystyle\leq 2(\|w_{t}\mathbf{K}_{t}({\bm{y}}_{t}-\hat{{\bm{y}}}_{t})\|_{\bm{\Sigma}_{t}^{c}}^{2}+\|w_{t}^{c}\mathbf{K}_{t}^{c}({\bm{y}}_{t}^{c}-\hat{{\bm{y}}}_{t})\|_{\bm{\Sigma}_{t}^{c}}^{2}).

In the last inequality, we use the fact that, for two real numbers
a
and
b
,
it holds that
(a+b)^{2}\leq 2(a^{2}+b^{2})
.
Then, we bound each term separately,

\displaystyle 2\|w_{t}\mathbf{K}_{t}({\bm{y}}_{t}-\hat{{\bm{y}}}_{t})\|_{\bm{\Sigma}_{t}^{c}}^{2}

\displaystyle=2w_{t}^{2}({\bm{y}}_{t}-\hat{{\bm{y}}}_{t})^{\intercal}\mathbf{K}_{t}^{\intercal}(\bm{\Sigma}_{t}^{c})^{-1}\mathbf{K}_{t}({\bm{y}}_{t}-\hat{{\bm{y}}}_{t})

\displaystyle\leq 2w_{t}^{2}\lambda_{\max}\left(\mathbf{K}_{t}^{\intercal}(\bm{\Sigma}_{t}^{c})^{-1}\mathbf{K}_{t}\right)\|{\bm{y}}_{t}-\hat{{\bm{y}}}_{t}\|_{2}^{2},

where
2\lambda_{\max}\left(\mathbf{K}_{t}^{\intercal}(\bm{\Sigma}_{t}^{c})^{-1}\mathbf{K}_{t}\right)\leq C_{4}<\infty
,
since
\mathbf{K}_{t}^{\intercal}(\bm{\Sigma}_{t}^{c})^{-1}\mathbf{K}_{t}
is a positive definite matrix, and thus, all eigenvalues are real-valued.
This property arises from the fact that for every invertible matrix
B
and a positive definite matrix
A
,

B^{\intercal}AB
is positive definite.
Finally, we must verify that
(\bm{\Sigma}_{t}^{c})^{-1}
is positive definite.
Let
{\bm{z}}\in\real^{m}
be a non-zero vector. Then,

\displaystyle{\bm{z}}^{\intercal}\big(\bm{\Sigma}_{t}^{c}\big)^{-1}{\bm{z}}

\displaystyle={\bm{z}}^{\intercal}\big(\bm{\Sigma}_{t|t-1}^{-1}+w_{t}^{c}\mathbf{H}_{t}^{\intercal}\mathbf{R}_{t}^{-1}\mathbf{H}_{t}\big){\bm{z}}={\bm{z}}^{\intercal}\big(\bm{\Sigma}_{t|t-1}^{-1}\big){\bm{z}}+w_{t}^{c}\big({\bm{z}}^{\intercal}\mathbf{H}_{t}^{\intercal}\mathbf{R}_{t}^{-1}\mathbf{H}_{t}{\bm{z}}\big).

We know that
\bm{\Sigma}_{t|t-1}^{-1}
is positive definite, hence
{\bm{z}}^{\intercal}\big(\bm{\Sigma}_{t|t-1}^{-1}\big){\bm{z}}>0
. Moreover,
{\bm{z}}^{\intercal}\mathbf{H}_{t}^{\intercal}\mathbf{R}_{t}^{-1}\mathbf{H}_{t}{\bm{z}}>0
because
\mathbf{H}_{t}^{\intercal}\mathbf{R}_{t}^{-1}\mathbf{H}_{t}
is positive definite. Since
w_{t}^{c}=W^{2}({\bm{y}}_{t}^{c},{\bm{y}}_{1:1-t})\geq 0
for all
{\bm{y}}_{t}^{c}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
, then
w_{t}^{c}\big({\bm{z}}^{\intercal}\mathbf{H}_{t}^{\intercal}\mathbf{R}_{t}^{-1}\mathbf{H}_{t}{\bm{z}}\big)\geq 0
. Finally, combining these inequalities,
{\bm{z}}^{\intercal}\big(\bm{\Sigma}_{t}^{c}\big)^{-1}{\bm{z}}>0
. Therefore,
(\bm{\Sigma}_{t}^{c})^{-1}
is positive definite.
Therefore,

\displaystyle 2\|w_{t}\mathbf{K}_{t}({\bm{y}}_{t}-\hat{{\bm{y}}}_{t})\|_{\bm{\Sigma}_{t}^{c}}^{2}\leq C_{4}(w_{t}\|{\bm{y}}_{t}-\hat{{\bm{y}}}_{t}\|_{2})^{2}=C_{5}

since
(w_{t}\|{\bm{y}}_{t}-\hat{{\bm{y}}}_{t}\|_{2})^{2}
does not depend on the contamination. Similarly,

\displaystyle 2\|w_{t}^{c}\mathbf{K}_{t}^{c}({\bm{y}}_{t}^{c}-\hat{{\bm{y}}}_{t})\|_{\bm{\Sigma}_{t}^{c}}^{2}\leq C_{6}(w_{t}^{c}\|{\bm{y}}_{t}^{c}-\hat{{\bm{y}}}_{t}\|_{2})^{2},

where, using the same argument as before,
2\lambda_{\max}\left((\mathbf{K}_{t}^{c})^{\intercal}(\bm{\Sigma}_{t}^{c})^{-1}\mathbf{K}_{t}^{c}\right)\leq C_{6}<\infty
.
Now, since
\sup_{{\bm{y}}_{t}^{c}\in\mathbf{R}^{d}}w_{t}^{c}\|{\bm{y}}_{t}^{c}\|_{2}\leq C_{7}<\infty
, we have:

\displaystyle C_{6}(w_{t}^{c}\|{\bm{y}}_{t}^{c}-\hat{{\bm{y}}}_{t}\|_{2})^{2}

\displaystyle\leq C_{6}(w_{t}^{c}\|{\bm{y}}_{t}^{c}\|_{2}+w_{t}^{c}\|\hat{{\bm{y}}}_{t}\|_{2})^{2}\leq C_{6}(C_{7}+C_{2}\|\hat{{\bm{y}}}_{t}\|_{2})^{2}=C_{8}.

Putting it all together, we find that

\displaystyle(2)\leq C_{5}+C_{8}.

Lastly, the third and final term can be rewritten using properties of determinants as

\displaystyle(3)

\displaystyle=\ln\left(\frac{\det\bm{\Sigma}_{t}^{c}}{\det\bm{\Sigma}_{t}}\right)=\ln\left(\frac{1}{\det\bm{\Sigma}_{t}}\right)+\ln\left(\det\bm{\Sigma}_{t}^{c}\right)=\ln\left(\frac{1}{\det\bm{\Sigma}_{t}}\right)+\ln\left(\frac{1}{\det(\bm{\Sigma}_{t}^{c})^{-1}}\right).

We define
C_{9}=\ln\left(\frac{1}{\det\bm{\Sigma}_{t}}\right)
since it does not depend on the contamination, and write

\displaystyle(3)

\displaystyle=C_{9}+\ln\left(\frac{1}{\det(\bm{\Sigma}_{t}^{c})^{-1}}\right)

\displaystyle=C_{9}+\ln\left(\frac{1}{\det\left(\bm{\Sigma}_{t|t-1}^{-1}+w_{t}^{c}\mathbf{H}_{t}^{\intercal}\mathbf{R}_{t}^{-1}\mathbf{H}_{t}\right)}\right)

\displaystyle\leq C_{9}+\ln\left(\frac{1}{\det\left(\bm{\Sigma}_{t|t-1}^{-1}\right)+\det\left(w_{t}^{c}\mathbf{H}_{t}^{\intercal}\mathbf{R}_{t}^{-1}\mathbf{H}_{t}\right)}\right),

where in the last inequality, we use the fact that for two positive semidefinite matrices
A
,
B
, it also holds that
\det(A+B)\geq\det(A)+\det(B)
. Finally,

\displaystyle(3)

\displaystyle\leq C_{9}+\ln\left(\frac{1}{\det\left(\bm{\Sigma}_{t|t-1}^{-1}\right)+\det\left(w_{t}^{c}\mathbf{H}_{t}^{\intercal}\mathbf{R}_{t}^{-1}\mathbf{H}_{t}\right)}\right)

\displaystyle\leq C_{9}+\ln\left(\frac{1}{\det\left(\bm{\Sigma}_{t|t-1}^{-1}\right)}\right)=C_{10}.

Here, we use the fact that
w_{t}^{c}\mathbf{H}_{t}^{\intercal}\mathbf{R}_{t}^{-1}\mathbf{H}_{t}
is positive semidefinite, as we showed previously. Therefore
\det\left(w_{t}^{c}\mathbf{H}_{t}^{\intercal}\mathbf{R}_{t}^{-1}\mathbf{H}_{t}\right)\geq 0
. By putting the bounds for (1), (2), and (3) together, we obtain

\displaystyle\operatorname{PIF}({\bm{y}}_{t},{\bm{y}}_{1:t-1})

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\Bigg(\underbrace{\Tr\left((\bm{\Sigma}_{t}^{c})^{-1}\bm{\Sigma}_{t}\right)-d}_{(1)}+\underbrace{\left(\bm{\mu}_{t}-\bm{\mu}_{t}^{c}\right)^{\intercal}(\bm{\Sigma}_{t}^{c})^{-1}\left(\bm{\mu}_{t}-\bm{\mu}_{t}^{c}\right)}_{(2)}+\underbrace{\ln\left(\frac{\det\bm{\Sigma}_{t}^{c}}{\det\bm{\Sigma}_{t}}\right)}_{(3)}\Bigg).

\displaystyle\leq C_{3}+C_{5}+C_{8}+C_{10}<\infty.

∎

C.3
Ensemble Kalman Filter

Now, we extend
Theorem

3.2
to the ensemble Kalman filter case. There are a couple of caveats to proving robustness for this case. While we know the distribution of the particles in the case where the state-space model is linear and Gaussian, this is not always the case. Therefore, we propose studying the empirical measures defined by the particles. The problem with this is that the Kullback-Leibler divergence is not defined for empirical measures with different supports. This is why we propose using the 2-Wasserstein distance instead since it is unbounded and well-defined for empirical measures.
The 2-Wasserstein distance is defined as follows: If
P
is an empirical measure with samples
x_{1},...,x_{n}
and
Q
is an empirical measure with samples
y_{1},...,y_{n}
,

\displaystyle D_{W_{2}}(P,Q)=\inf_{\pi}\left(\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\left\|x_{i}-y_{\pi(i)}\right\|_{2}^{2}\right)^{1/2},

where the infimum is over all permutations
\pi
of
n
elements. Therefore, the PIF for the ensemble Kalman filter case has the form

\displaystyle\operatorname{PIF}({\bm{y}}_{t}^{c},{\bm{y}}_{1:t})=D_{W_{2}}\left(\mathbb{P}_{N},\mathbb{P}_{N}^{c}\right),

(54)

where
\mathbb{P}_{N}
is the empirical measure of the (non-contaminated) particles

\left\{\hat{\theta}^{(i)}_{t}=\hat{{\bm{\theta}}}_{t|t-1}^{\left(i\right)}+w_{t}\bar{\mathbf{K}}_{t}\left({\bm{y}}_{t}-\hat{{\bm{y}}}_{t|t-1}^{(i)}\right)\right\}_{i=1}^{N}
,
and
\mathbb{P}_{N}^{c}
is the empirical measure of the contaminated particles

\left\{\hat{\theta}^{(i)}_{t^{c}}=\hat{{\bm{\theta}}}_{t|t-1}^{\left(i\right)}+w_{t}^{c}\bar{\mathbf{K}}_{t}\left({\bm{y}}_{t}^{c}-\hat{{\bm{y}}}_{t|t-1}^{(i)}\right)\right\}_{i=1}^{N}
.
As in the previous definition, if
\sup_{{\bm{y}}_{t}^{c}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}}|\operatorname{PIF}({\bm{y}}_{t}^{c},{\bm{y}}_{1:t})|<\infty
,
then the posterior is called outlier-robust.

Lemma C.5
.

The generalised posterior presented in
Section

D.3

has bounded PIF (as defined in
Equation

54
) and is,
therefore, outlier robust for any weighting function
W:\real^{d\times d}\to\real
such that

\sup_{{\bm{y}}_{t}\in\mathbf{R}^{d}}W({\bm{y}}_{t},\hat{{\bm{y}}}_{t})<\infty
and

\sup_{{\bm{y}}_{t}\in\mathbf{R}^{d}}W({\bm{y}}_{t},\hat{{\bm{y}}}_{t})^{2}\,\|{\bm{y}}_{t}\|_{2}<\infty
.

Proof.

Let
W:\real^{d\times d}\to\real
be a weighting function such that

\sup_{{\bm{y}}_{t}\in\mathbf{R}^{d}}W({\bm{y}}_{t},\hat{{\bm{y}}}_{t})<\infty
and

\sup_{{\bm{y}}_{t}\in\mathbf{R}^{d}}W({\bm{y}}_{t},\hat{{\bm{y}}}_{t})^{2}\|{\bm{y}}_{t}\|_{2}<\infty
.
Define
w_{t}:=W^{2}({\bm{y}}_{t},\hat{{\bm{y}}}_{t})
, and
w_{t}^{c}:=W^{2}({\bm{y}}_{t}^{c},\hat{{\bm{y}}}_{t})
.
Then, the PIF

\displaystyle\operatorname{PIF}({\bm{y}}_{t}^{c},{\bm{y}}_{1:t})=D_{W_{2}}(\mathbb{P}_{N},\mathbb{P}_{N}^{c})=\inf_{\pi}\left(\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\left\|\hat{\theta}^{(i)}_{t}-\hat{\theta}^{(\pi(i))}_{t^{c}}\right\|_{2}^{2}\right)^{1/2},

where the infimum is over all permutations
\pi
of
N
elements,
\mathbb{P}_{N}
is the empirical measure of the particles
\left\{\hat{\theta}^{(i)}_{t}=\hat{{\bm{\theta}}}_{t|t-1}^{\left(i\right)}+w_{t}\bar{\mathbf{K}}_{t}\left({\bm{y}}_{t}-\hat{{\bm{y}}}_{t|t-1}^{(i)}\right)\right\}_{i=1}^{N}
, and
\mathbb{P}_{N}^{c}
is the empirical measure of the contaminated particles
\left\{\hat{\theta}^{(i)}_{t^{c}}=\hat{{\bm{\theta}}}_{t|t-1}^{\left(i\right)}+w_{t}^{c}\bar{\mathbf{K}}_{t}\left({\bm{y}}_{t}^{c}-\hat{{\bm{y}}}_{t|t-1}^{(i)}\right)\right\}_{i=1}^{N}
. Therefore, we know that the infimum will be smaller than considering only the identity as permutation,

\displaystyle D_{W_{2}}(\mathbb{P}_{N},\mathbb{P}_{N}^{c})\leq\left(\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\left\|\hat{\theta}^{(i)}_{t}-\hat{\theta}^{(i)}_{t^{c}}\right\|_{2}^{2}\right)^{1/2}.

Now, using the equation of the particles:

\displaystyle D_{W_{2}}(\mathbb{P}_{N},\mathbb{P}_{N}^{c})

\displaystyle\leq\left(\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\left\|\hat{{\bm{\theta}}}_{t|t-1}^{\left(i\right)}+w_{t}\bar{\mathbf{K}}_{t}\left({\bm{y}}_{t}-\hat{{\bm{y}}}_{t|t-1}^{(i)}\right)-\hat{{\bm{\theta}}}_{t|t-1}^{\left(i\right)}-w_{t}^{c}\bar{\mathbf{K}}_{t}\left({\bm{y}}_{t}^{c}-\hat{{\bm{y}}}_{t|t-1}^{(i)}\right)\right\|_{2}^{2}\right)^{1/2}

\displaystyle=\left(\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\left\|w_{t}\bar{\mathbf{K}}_{t}\left({\bm{y}}_{t}-\hat{{\bm{y}}}_{t|t-1}^{(i)}\right)-w_{t}^{c}\bar{\mathbf{K}}_{t}\left({\bm{y}}_{t}^{c}-\hat{{\bm{y}}}_{t|t-1}^{(i)}\right)\right\|_{2}^{2}\right)^{1/2}.

We use triangle inequality to obtain:

\displaystyle D_{W_{2}}(\mathbb{P}_{N},\mathbb{P}_{N}^{c})

\displaystyle\leq\left(\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\left(\left\|w_{t}\bar{\mathbf{K}}_{t}\left({\bm{y}}_{t}-\hat{{\bm{y}}}_{t|t-1}^{(i)}\right)\right\|_{2}+\left\|w_{t}^{c}\bar{\mathbf{K}}_{t}\left({\bm{y}}_{t}^{c}-\hat{{\bm{y}}}_{t|t-1}^{(i)}\right)\right\|_{2}\right)^{2}\right)^{1/2}

\displaystyle\leq\left(\frac{2}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\left\|w_{t}\bar{\mathbf{K}}_{t}\left({\bm{y}}_{t}-\hat{{\bm{y}}}_{t|t-1}^{(i)}\right)\right\|_{2}^{2}+\left\|w_{t}^{c}\bar{\mathbf{K}}_{t}\left({\bm{y}}_{t}^{c}-\hat{{\bm{y}}}_{t|t-1}^{(i)}\right)\right\|_{2}^{2}\right)^{1/2}.

Lets define
C_{11}=\sum_{i=1}^{N}\|w_{t}\bar{\mathbf{K}}_{t}\left({\bm{y}}_{t}-\hat{{\bm{y}}}_{t|t-1}^{(i)}\right)\|_{2}^{2}
, a constant that does not depend on the contamination. Therefore:

\displaystyle D_{W_{2}}(\mathbb{P}_{N},\mathbb{P}_{N}^{c})

\displaystyle\leq\left(\frac{2}{N}\left(C_{11}+\sum_{i=1}^{N}\left\|w_{t}^{c}\bar{\mathbf{K}}_{t}\left({\bm{y}}_{t}^{c}-\hat{{\bm{y}}}_{t|t-1}^{(i)}\right)\right\|_{2}^{2}\right)\right)^{1/2}.

First, we observe that
\left\|w_{t}^{c}\bar{\mathbf{K}}_{t}\left({\bm{y}}_{t}^{c}-\hat{{\bm{y}}}_{t|t-1}^{(i)}\right)\right\|_{2}^{2}=\left(w_{t}^{c}\right)^{2}\left({\bm{y}}_{t}^{c}-\hat{{\bm{y}}}_{t|t-1}^{(i)}\right)^{\intercal}\bar{\mathbf{K}}_{t}^{\intercal}\bar{\mathbf{K}}_{t}\left({\bm{y}}_{t}^{c}-\hat{{\bm{y}}}_{t|t-1}^{(i)}\right)
. Now we bound this expression using the fact that for every positive definite matrix
A
and vector
{\bm{z}}
,
we have
\lambda_{\min}(A)\|{\bm{z}}\|^{2}\leq{\bm{z}}^{\intercal}A{\bm{z}}\leq\lambda_{\max}(A)\|{\bm{z}}\|^{2}
,
where
\lambda_{\min}(A)
and
\lambda_{\max}(A)
are the minimum and maximum eigenvalues of
A
,
respectively
(
Horn & Johnson, 2012
, Chapter 5.7)
:

\displaystyle\left\|w_{t}^{c}\bar{\mathbf{K}}_{t}\left({\bm{y}}_{t}^{c}-\hat{{\bm{y}}}_{t|t-1}^{(i)}\right)\right\|_{2}^{2}\leq(w_{t}^{c})^{2}\lambda_{\max}(\bar{\mathbf{K}}_{t}^{\intercal}\bar{\mathbf{K}}_{t})\left\|{\bm{y}}_{t}^{c}-\hat{{\bm{y}}}_{t|t-1}^{(i)}\right\|_{2}^{2},

where
\lambda_{\max}\left(\bar{\mathbf{K}}_{t}^{\intercal}\bar{\mathbf{K}}_{t}\right)\leq C_{12}<\infty
,
since
\bar{\mathbf{K}}_{t}^{\intercal}\bar{\mathbf{K}}_{t}
is a positive definite matrix, and thus, all eigenvalues are real-valued. Then,

\displaystyle\left\|w_{t}^{c}\bar{\mathbf{K}}_{t}\left({\bm{y}}_{t}^{c}-\hat{{\bm{y}}}_{t|t-1}^{(i)}\right)\right\|_{2}^{2}\leq C_{12}\left(w_{t}^{c}\left\|{\bm{y}}_{t}^{c}-\hat{{\bm{y}}}_{t|t-1}^{(i)}\right\|_{2}\right)^{2}.

Now, since
\sup_{{\bm{y}}_{t}^{c}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}}w_{t}^{c}\leq C_{13}<\infty
, and
\sup_{{\bm{y}}_{t}^{c}\in\mathbf{R}^{d}}w_{t}^{c}\|{\bm{y}}_{t}^{c}\|_{2}\leq C_{14}<\infty
, we have:

\displaystyle C_{12}\left(w_{t}^{c}\left\|{\bm{y}}_{t}^{c}-\hat{{\bm{y}}}_{t|t-1}^{(i)}\right\|_{2}\right)^{2}

\displaystyle\leq C_{12}\left(w_{t}^{c}\left\|{\bm{y}}_{t}^{c}\right\|_{2}+w_{t}^{c}\left\|\hat{{\bm{y}}}_{t|t-1}^{(i)}\right\|_{2}\right)^{2}\leq C_{12}\left(C_{14}+C_{13}\left\|\hat{{\bm{y}}}_{t|t-1}^{(i)}\right\|_{2}\right)^{2}=C_{15}.

Putting it all together, we find that

\displaystyle D_{W_{2}}(\mathbb{P}_{N},\mathbb{P}_{N}^{c})

\displaystyle\leq\left(\frac{2}{N}\left(C_{11}+\sum_{i=1}^{N}C_{15}\right)\right)^{1/2}<\infty.

∎

Appendix D
Extensions of the weighted likelihood filter

Below, we discuss a number of extension to the WoLF methodology including generalisations to (i) exponential-family members, (ii) multi-output
weighting functions, and (iii) the EnKF.

D.1
Exponential family likelihoods

We extend WoLF for measurements modelled using an element of the exponential family of distributions (as
first mentioned in Section
3.2
).
Classical examples of exponential families, in addition to the Gaussian distribution, are
the Bernoulli distribution,
the Gamma distribution,
the Beta distribution, and
the Poisson distribution.
These distributions can be considered to tackle filtering problems when
the measurements are generated respectively from
a binary process,
a process that only takes values in positive real line,
a process that takes values in the interval
[0,1]
, or
a counting process.

We take the mass function of a measurement
{\bm{y}}_{t}\in B\subseteq\real^{d}
to be of the form

\displaystyle p({\bm{y}}_{t}|{\bm{\theta}}_{t})

\displaystyle=\text{expfam}({\bm{y}}_{t}|\bm{\eta}_{t})=Z^{-1}(\bm{\eta}_{t})\exp\Big(\bm{\eta}_{t}^{\intercal}T({\bm{y}}_{t})+b({\bm{y}}_{t})\Big).

(55)

with

B
the support of the measurement
{\bm{y}}_{t}
,

\bm{\eta}_{t}\in\real^{k}
the natural parameters,

A:B\to\real^{k}
the sufficient statistic function,

b:B\to\real
the base measure, and

Z:\real^{k}\to\real
the normalising function.
To simplify the notation, let
\bm{\eta}_{t}=h_{t}({\bm{\theta}}_{t})
and
{\bm{z}}_{t}=T({\bm{y}}_{t})
.
We also define the dual (moment) parameters

\bm{\lambda}_{t}=\mathbb{E}\left[{{\bm{z}}_{t}|\bm{\eta}_{t}}\right]=\nabla_{\bm{\eta}_{t}}\log Z(\bm{\eta}_{t})

(56)

and the conditional variance

\mathbf{R}_{t}=\text{Cov}[{\bm{z}}_{t}|\bm{\eta}_{t}]=\nabla_{\bm{\eta}_{t}}\bm{\lambda}_{t}=\nabla_{\bm{\eta}_{t}}^{2}\log Z(\bm{\eta}_{t}),

(57)

and we take our predictive model to output the dual parameters:

\displaystyle\bm{\lambda}_{t}=h_{t}({\bm{\theta}}_{t}).

(58)

For example, for a Gaussian likelihood with fixed observation noise
and a linear observation model as in (
5
),
we have that
A({\bm{y}}_{t})={\bm{y}}_{t}
,

\bm{\lambda}_{t}=\mathbf{H}_{t}{\bm{\theta}}_{t}
, and

\mathbf{R}_{t}
is constant.

The exponential family EKF algorithm of

Ollivier (2018)
approximates the likelihood in (
55
) with a moment-matched Gaussian,

\displaystyle q({\bm{y}}_{t}|{\bm{\theta}}_{t})=\mathcal{N}({\bm{z}}_{t}|\bar{\bm{\lambda}}_{t},\mathbf{R}_{t}),

(59)

where
\bar{\bm{\lambda}}_{t}=\mathbf{H}_{t}({\bm{\theta}}_{t}-\bar{\bm{\mu}}_{t|t-1})+h_{t}(\bar{\bm{\mu}}_{t|t-1})
, and

\bm{\mu}_{t|t-1}
is given by (
10
).
We modify this algorithm by including a weighting term on the log-likelihood to get

\displaystyle\log q({\bm{y}}_{t}|{\bm{\theta}}_{t})

\displaystyle=W_{t}({\bm{y}}_{t},\hat{{\bm{y}}}_{t})^{2}\left(-\frac{1}{2}\bar{\bm{\lambda}}_{t}^{\top}{\mathbf{R}}_{t}^{-1}\bar{\bm{\lambda}}_{t}+\bar{\bm{\lambda}}_{t}^{\top}{\mathbf{R}}_{t}^{-1}T({\bm{y}}_{t})\right)+{\rm cst},

(60)

with
{\rm cst}.
a constant term that does not depend on
{\bm{\theta}}
.
We leave the study of
W({\bm{y}}_{t},\hat{{\bm{y}}}_{t})
when modelling a non-Gaussian exponential family for future work.

D.2
Dimension-specific weighting

In this section, we provide details about the implementation
of a vector-valued weighting function
W_{t}:\real^{d}\times\real^{d}\to\real^{d}

introduced in Section
3.3
.
See Section
4.3
for an evaluation of this method.
We consider
{\bm{y}}_{t}\in\real^{d}
with
d>1
.

If the likelihood factorises as

p\left({\bm{y}}_{t}|{\bm{\theta}}_{t}\right)=\prod_{j=1}^{d}p\left(y_{t,j}|{\bm{\theta}}_{t}\right)
,
with
y_{t,j}
the
j
-th element of the
t
-th measurement,
we define the weighted log-likelihood as

\log q\left({\bm{y}}_{t}|{\bm{\theta}}_{t}\right)=\sum_{j=1}^{d}w_{t,j}^{2}\log p\left(y_{t,j}|{\bm{\theta}}_{t}\right),

(61)

where
w_{t,j}^{2}=W^{2}({\bm{y}}_{t},\hat{{\bm{y}}}_{t})_{j}
is the
j
-th entry of the vector-valued weight function.

For a Gaussian likelihood
p({\bm{y}}_{t}|{\bm{\theta}}_{t})=\mathcal{N}\left({\bm{y}}_{t}|h_{t}\left({\bm{\theta}}_{t}\right),\mathbf{R}_{t}\right)

we define the weighted likelihood as

\displaystyle q({\bm{y}}_{t}|{\bm{\theta}}_{t})

\displaystyle=\mathcal{N}\left({\bm{y}}_{t}|h_{t}\left({\bm{\theta}}_{t}\right),\bar{\mathbf{R}}_{t}\right),

(62)

\displaystyle\bar{\mathbf{R}}_{t}^{-1}

\displaystyle=\mathrm{Diag}\left({\bm{w}}_{t}\right)\mathbf{R}_{t}^{-1}\mathrm{Diag}\left({\bm{w}}_{t}\right),

(63)

which is a special case of (
61
)
when
\mathbf{R}_{t}
is diagonal.
8
8

8

\mathrm{Diag}({\bm{v}})
for
{\bm{v}}\in\real^{K}
is the
K\times K
matrix with
\mathrm{Diag}({\bm{v}})_{i,i}={\bm{v}}_{i}
and
\mathrm{Diag}({\bm{v}})_{i,j}=0
for
i\neq j
.

This expression scales the precision of each
y_{t,j}
by
w_{t,j}^{2}
while preserving correlations.
It can also be written in terms of dimension-specific weighting on the errors:

\displaystyle\log q({\bm{y}}_{t}|{\bm{\theta}}_{t})

\displaystyle=-\frac{1}{2}\tilde{E}_{t}\left({\bm{\theta}}_{t}\right)^{\intercal}\mathbf{R}_{t}^{-1}\tilde{E}_{t}\left({\bm{\theta}}_{t}\right),

(64)

\displaystyle\tilde{E}_{t}\left({\bm{\theta}}_{t}\right)

\displaystyle=\mathrm{Diag}\left({\bm{w}}_{t}\right)\left({\bm{y}}_{t}-h_{t}\left({\bm{\theta}}_{t}\right)\right).

(65)

The EKF update from the weighted loglikelihood in (
62
) mirrors the standard EKF update
with the true observation precision
\mathbf{R}_{t}^{-1}
replaced by
\bar{\mathbf{R}}_{t}^{-1}
:

\displaystyle\bm{\Sigma}_{t}^{-1}

\displaystyle=\bm{\Sigma}_{t|t-1}^{-1}+\mathbf{H}_{t}^{\top}\bar{\mathbf{R}}_{t}^{-1}\mathbf{H}_{t},

(66)

\displaystyle\bm{\mu}_{t}

\displaystyle=\bm{\mu}_{t|t-1}+\mathbf{K}_{t}({\bm{y}}_{t}-\hat{{\bm{y}}}_{t}),

(67)

\displaystyle\mathbf{K}_{t}

\displaystyle=\bm{\Sigma}_{t}\mathbf{H}_{t}^{\top}\bar{\mathbf{R}}_{t}^{-1}.

(68)

This generalises the WoLF update with scalar weight given by Proposition
3.1
.

Finally, when
w_{t,j}=0
for one or more observation dimensions,
(
61
) and (
62
)
define improper observation distributions
because the marginals on these dimensions are uniform over and the precision
\bar{\mathbf{R}}_{t}^{-1}
is singular.
Nevertheless, the joint marginal for the dimensions with positive weights,
{\bm{y}}_{t,J_{t}}
where
J_{t}=\{j:w_{t,j}>0\}
, is a proper distribution. This is all that is needed because the observations
y_{t,j}
with
w_{t,j}=0
are ignored in the update.
This can be seen in (
66
),
(
67
), and (
68
), where
as a consequence of (
63
), the
error terms and the Jacobian are both zeroed out on the zero-weighted dimensions.

D.3
Weighted ensembles

In this section, we introduce the weighted EnKF first mentioned in Section
3.2
.
We begin with a discussion of the EnKF originally referenced in Section
2.3
.

The ensemble Kalman filter:

The ensemble Kalman filter (EnKF)
(
Roth et al., 2017b
)
uses an ensemble of
N\in\mathbb{N}
particles

\{\hat{{\bm{\theta}}}_{t|t-1}^{\left(i\right)}\}_{i=1}^{N}
.
For each
i=1,\ldots,N
, the update step samples predictions
\hat{{\bm{y}}}_{t|t-1}^{\left(i\right)}

according to

\hat{{\bm{y}}}_{t|t-1}^{\left(i\right)}\sim\mathcal{N}\left(h_{t}\left(\hat{{\bm{\theta}}}_{t|t-1}^{(i)}\right),\mathbf{R}_{t}\right).

(69)

and then updates each particle according to

\hat{{\bm{\theta}}}_{t}^{\left(i\right)}=\hat{{\bm{\theta}}}_{t|t-1}^{\left(i\right)}+\bar{\mathbf{K}}_{t}\left({\bm{y}}_{t}-\hat{{\bm{y}}}_{t|t-1}^{(i)}\right),

(70)

The EnKF gain
\bar{\mathbf{K}}_{t}
is

\bar{\mathbf{K}}_{t}={\rm cov}_{i}\left[\hat{{\bm{\theta}}}_{t|t-1}^{\left(i\right)},\hat{{\bm{y}}}_{t|t-1}^{\left(i\right)}\right]\left(\mathbb{V}_{{i}}\left[{\hat{{\bm{y}}}_{t|t-1}^{\left(i\right)}}\right]\right)^{-1}.

(71)

We write
\mathbb{E}_{{i}}\left[{\cdot}\right],{\rm cov}_{i}\left[\cdot,\cdot\right],\mathbb{V}_{{i}}\left[{\cdot}\right]

to refer to the distribution over particles as indexed by
i
.

The EnKF converges to standard KF as
N\to\infty
when the likelihood is linear-Gaussian and the prior is also Gaussian,

\displaystyle p\left({\bm{y}}_{t}|{\bm{\theta}}_{t}\right)

\displaystyle=\mathcal{N}\left({\bm{y}}_{t}|\mathbf{H}_{t}{\bm{\theta}}_{t},\mathbf{R}_{t}\right),

(72)

\displaystyle\hat{{\bm{\theta}}}_{t|t-1}^{\left(i\right)}

\displaystyle\sim\mathcal{N}\left(\bm{\mu}_{t|t-1},\bm{\Sigma}_{t|t-1}\right).

(73)

Under these conditions the EnKF gain
\bar{\mathbf{K}}_{t}
matches the KF gain
\mathbf{K}_{t}
, because

\displaystyle{\rm cov}_{i}\left[\hat{{\bm{\theta}}}_{t|t-1}^{\left(i\right)},\hat{{\bm{y}}}_{t|t-1}^{\left(i\right)}\right]

\displaystyle=\bm{\Sigma}_{t|t-1}\mathbf{H}_{t}^{\top},

(74)

\displaystyle\mathbb{V}_{{i}}\left[{\hat{{\bm{y}}}_{t|t-1}^{\left(i\right)}}\right]

\displaystyle=\mathbf{H}_{t}\bm{\Sigma}_{t|t-1}\mathbf{H}_{t}^{\top}+\mathbf{R}_{t},

(75)

\displaystyle\bar{\mathbf{K}}_{t}

\displaystyle=\bm{\Sigma}_{t|t-1}\mathbf{H}_{t}^{\top}\left(\mathbf{H}_{t}\bm{\Sigma}_{t|t-1}\mathbf{H}_{t}^{\top}+\mathbf{R}_{t}\right)^{-1}

(76)

\displaystyle=\mathbf{K}_{t},

(77)

and therefore the statistics of the posterior ensemble also match
the KF update

\displaystyle\bm{\mu}_{t}=\mathbb{E}_{{i}}\left[{\hat{{\bm{\theta}}}_{t}^{\left(i\right)}}\right]

\displaystyle=\bm{\mu}_{t|t-1}+\mathbf{K}_{t}\left({\bm{y}}_{t}-\mathbf{H}_{t}\bm{\mu}_{t|t-1}\right),

(78)

\displaystyle\bm{\Sigma}_{t}=\mathbb{V}_{{i}}\left[{\hat{{\bm{\theta}}}_{t}^{\left(i\right)}}\right]

\displaystyle=\bm{\Sigma}_{t|t-1}-\mathbf{K}_{t}\mathbf{H}_{t}\bm{\Sigma}_{t|t-1}.

(79)

The weighted-likelihood EnKF:

We propose a weighted version of EnKF based on our WoLF
with dimension-specific weights and Gaussian likelihood,
where (
69
) is modified to sample particle predictions from the weighted likelihood in (
62
):

\displaystyle\hat{{\bm{y}}}_{t|t-1}^{(i)}\sim\mathcal{N}\left(h_{t}\left(\hat{{\bm{\theta}}}_{t|t-1}^{(i)}\right),\bar{\mathbf{R}}_{t}\right).

(80)

The weighted EnKF converges to the WoLF as
N\to\infty
, by the same argument for the vanilla EnKF and EKF in
(
74
) through (
79
).
Specifically, under this limit the sampling scheme in (
80
) and the update in (
70
) yield

\displaystyle\bar{\mathbf{K}}_{t}

\displaystyle=\bm{\Sigma}_{t|t-1}\mathbf{H}_{t}^{\top}\left(\mathbf{H}_{t}\bm{\Sigma}_{t|t-1}\mathbf{H}_{t}^{\top}+\bar{\mathbf{R}}_{t}\right)^{-1},

(81)

\displaystyle\bm{\mu}_{t}

\displaystyle=\bm{\mu}_{t|t-1}+\bar{\mathbf{K}}_{t}\left({\bm{y}}_{t}-\mathbf{H}_{t}\bm{\mu}_{t|t-1}\right),

(82)

\displaystyle\bm{\Sigma}_{t}

\displaystyle=\bm{\Sigma}_{t|t-1}-\bar{\mathbf{K}}_{t}\mathbf{H}_{t}\bm{\Sigma}_{t|t-1},

(83)

which matches the WoLF update in (
66
), (
67
), and (
68
).

For the case of scalar weights with
w_{t}^{2}=W^{2}({\bm{y}}_{t},\hat{{\bm{y}}}_{t})\in\{0,1\}
as in (

), the weighted EnKF can be implemented by unweighted sampling as in (

(84)

That is, the update is simply skipped when

and

For the case of vector weights
{\bm{w}}_{t}
as in

E.3
Robust EKF for online MLP regression (1d) — suplementary experiment

In this section, we consider an online
nonlinear 1d regression, with the training
data coming
either from an i.i.d. source, or a correlated source.
The latter
corresponds to a non-stationary problem

(
Cartea et al., 2023
;
Arroyo et al., 2024
;
Duran-Martin et al., 2022
, see e.g.)
.

We present a stream of observations

{\cal D}^{\text{filter}}=(y_{1},x_{1}),\ldots(y_{T},x_{T})
with

y_{t}\in\real
the measurements,

x_{t}\in\real
the exogenous variables, and

T=1500
.
The measurements and exogenous variables are sequentially sampled from the processes

y_{t}=\begin{cases}{\bm{\theta}}^{*}_{1}x_{t}-{\bm{\theta}}^{*}_{2}\cos({\bm{\theta}}^{*}_{3}x_{t}\,\pi)+{\bm{\theta}}^{*}_{4}x_{t}^{3}+V_{t}&\text{w.p.}\,1-p_{\epsilon},\\
U_{t}&\text{w.p. }\,p_{\epsilon},\end{cases}

(92)

where the parameters of the observation
model are
{\bm{\theta}}^{*}=(0.2,-10,1.0,1.0)
,
the inputs are
x_{t}\sim{\cal U}[-3,3]
,
and the noise is

V_{t}\sim{\cal N}(0,3)
,

U_{t}\sim{\cal U}[-40,40]
, and
p_{\epsilon}=0.05
.

We consider four configurations of this experiment. In each experiment
the data is either sorted by
x_{t}
value (i.e,
the exogenous variable satisfies
x_{i}<x_{j}
for all
i<j
,
representing a correlated source) or is
unsorted (representing
an i.i.d. source), and
the measurement function is either
a clean version of the true data generating process
(i.e.,
(
92
) with
p_{\epsilon}=0
and unknown coefficients
{\bm{\theta}}
), or
a neural network with unknown parameters
{\bm{\theta}}
.
Specifically, we use a multi-layered perceptron (MLP) with two hidden layers and 10 units per layer:

h({\bm{\theta}}_{t},x_{t})={\bm{w}}_{t}^{(3)}\phi\left({\bm{w}}_{t}^{(2)}\phi\left({\bm{w}}_{t}^{(1)}x_{t}+{\bm{b}}_{t}^{(1)}\right)+{\bm{b}}_{t}^{(2)}\right)+{\bm{b}}_{t}^{(3)},

(93)

with
activation function
\phi(u)=\max\{0,u\}

applied elementwise.
Thus the state vector encodes the parameters:

\displaystyle{\bm{\theta}}_{t}=({\bm{w}}_{t}^{(1)}\in\real^{10\times 1},{\bm{w}}_{t}^{(2)}\in\real^{10\times 10},{\bm{w}}_{t}^{(3)}\in\real^{1\times 10},{\bm{b}}_{t}^{(1)}\in\real^{10},{\bm{b}}_{t}^{(2)}\in\real^{10},{\bm{b}}_{t}^{(3)}\in\real)

and has size so that
{\bm{\theta}}\in\real^{141}
.
Note that in this experiment
h_{t}({\bm{\theta}})=h({\bm{\theta}},x_{t})
.
We set
Q_{t}=10^{-4}{\bf I}
,
which allows the parameters to slowly drift over time and provides some reguralisation.

For each method, we evaluate the

\text{RMedSE}=\sqrt{\text{median}\{(y_{t}-h_{t}(\bm{\mu}_{t|t-1}))^{2}\}_{t=1}^{T}}
.
The

EKF-IW
and the

EKF-B
methods
are taken with two inner iterations,
which implies that their computational complexity is twice that of the WoLF methods.
