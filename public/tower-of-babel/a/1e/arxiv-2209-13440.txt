Norms of embeddings between quadratically weighted spaces of holomorphic functions
Joe Viola

Abstract
. We consider spaces of holomorphic functions which are square-integrable against a Gaussian weight, which appear in the theory of metaplectic FBI–Bargmann transforms. We identify the operator norm of embeddings between two such spaces, by relating these embeddings to Fourier integral operators with complex phase.



1.
Introduction and main result

When performing phase-space analysis using FBI–Bargmann transforms, one is often led to consider the effect of changing the weight on a weighted space of holomorphic functions (see for instance
[
8
]
). We use the notation
\mathcal{L}(\textnormal{d}x)=\textnormal{d}\operatorname{Re}x\,\textnormal{d}\operatorname{Im}x
for Lebesgue measure on
\mathbb{C}^{n}
. With
\Phi:\mathbb{C}^{n}\to\mathbb{R}
we define the weighted
L^{2}
norm

(1)

\|f\|_{H_{\Phi}}=\left(\int_{\mathbb{C}^{n}}|f(x)|^{2}\textnormal{e}^{-4\pi\Phi(x)}\,\mathcal{L}(\textnormal{d}x)\right)^{1/2}

and the weighted space of holomorphic functions

(2)

H_{\Phi}=\left\{f\in\operatorname{Hol}(\mathbb{C}^{n})\>:\>\|f\|_{H_{\Phi}}<\infty\right\}.

The weights associated with quadratic phases are real-valued and real-quadratic, which means that
\Phi:\mathbb{C}^{n}\to\mathbb{R}
can be written in the form

(3)

\Phi(x)=\frac{1}{2}Lx\cdot\overline{x}+\frac{1}{2}\operatorname{Re}(Px\cdot x).

Here
L=2\Phi^{\prime\prime}_{\overline{x}x}
is a Hermitian matrix (called the Levi matrix) and
P=2\Phi^{\prime\prime}_{xx}
is symmetric (and gives the pluri-harmonic part of
\Phi
). The weights we consider are strictly plurisubharmonic, which in this context is equivalent to requiring that
L
is a positive definite Hermitian matrix.

It comes as no surprise that the embedding

(4)

\iota:H_{\Phi_{1}}\ni f\mapsto f\in H_{\Phi_{2}}

between two quadratically weighted spaces is bounded if and only if
\Phi_{2}\geq\Phi_{1}
. The precise value of the operator norm, however, is far from evident due to the assumption that the functions considered are holomorphic. These functions cannot concentrate arbitrarily close to the origin, and the norm ratio therefore represents a type of uncertainty principle. This is because each unitary FBI–Bargmann transform mapping to an
H_{\Phi}
space corresponds to a wave packet decomposition, and in fact finding the norm of the embedding when
\Phi_{j}(x)=\frac{1}{2}t_{j}|x|^{2}
is equivalent to finding the bottom of the spectrum of the quantum harmonic oscillator. (This is well-known; see for instance
[
1
, Thm. 4.12]
.) While the question is elementary, the author has not been able to find an elementary solution (except, with some difficulty, in dimension one,
[
18
, Thm. 1.2]
).

The question is also relevant to the study of FBI–Bargmann transforms and Fourier integral operators with complex quadratic phase, particularly when applied to non-self-adjoint operators. In 1961, V. Bargmann introducted and studied what we call here
H_{\Phi_{0}}
,
\Phi_{0}(x)=\frac{1}{2}|x|^{2}
and the Bargmann transform
\tilde{\mathfrak{B}}_{0}
(see (
55
) below)
[
3
, Eqs. (1.2), (2.3)]
. In the time since these operators have been used in many applications in partial differential equations (for example,
[
16
,
17
,
9
]
), have been studied as objects of intrinsic interest (for example,
[
11
,
5
,
10
]
), and have become part of the standard toolbox in the study of partial differential equations (for example,
[
13
,
21
,
8
]
). Motivated in part by applications to non-self-adjoint operators including subelliptic operators in kinetic theory, these operators and techniques are still the subject of active research (for example,
[
6
,
7
,
14
,
4
,
2
,
15
,
20
,
12
]
among many others). The author’s motivation in studying this problem is to better understand
H_{\Phi}
-spaces and their close links with the metaplectic semigroup (Definition
8
).

Viewing the weighted spaces
H_{\Phi_{j}},j=1,2
as being related by Fourier integral operators with complex quadratic phase and applying the calculus of these operators
[
10
]
we are able to find the operator norm of the embedding (
4
) using the spectral theory of underlying (complex) canonical transformations. In order to state the main result, we introduce the matrix

(5)

\mathbf{A}_{\Phi}=\overline{\begin{pmatrix}\textnormal{i}L&0\\
\textnormal{i}P&1\end{pmatrix}^{-1}}\begin{pmatrix}0&1\\
1&0\end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix}\textnormal{i}L&0\\
\textnormal{i}P&1\end{pmatrix},\quad L=2\Phi^{\prime\prime}_{\overline{x}x},P=2\Phi^{\prime\prime}_{xx}.

(Note that the entries
1
or
0
refer to the identity or zero matrices of size
n
-by-
n
.) This matrix is associated to the adjoint of phase-space shifts on an
H_{\Phi}
space and to positivity conditions on weighted spaces; see for example
[
8
, Sec. 1.2]
. Notice that
\overline{\mathbf{A}_{\Phi}}=\mathbf{A}_{\Phi}^{-1}
. In Section
4
below, we recall some well-known results on
\mathbf{A}_{\Phi}
.

Theorem 1
.

For
j=1,2
let
\Phi_{j}:\mathbb{C}^{n}\to\mathbb{R}
be real-quadratic with
(\Phi_{j})^{\prime\prime}_{\overline{x}x}
positive definite. Let
\mathbf{A}_{\Phi_{j}}
be as in (
5
).
Then the embedding

(6)

\iota:H_{\Phi_{1}}\to H_{\Phi_{2}},\quad\iota f=f

is bounded if and only if
\Phi_{2}\geq\Phi_{1}
on
\mathbb{C}^{n}
. In this case, there exist
\mu_{1},\dots,\mu_{n}\in(0,1]
such that, counting for multiplicity,

(7)

\operatorname{Spec}\mathbf{A}_{\Phi_{2}}^{-1}\mathbf{A}_{\Phi_{1}}=\{\mu_{j}\}_{j=1}^{n}\cup\{\mu_{j}^{-1}\}_{j=1}^{n}

and the operator norm of
\iota
is given by

(8)

\|\iota\|=\left(\frac{\det(\Phi_{1})^{\prime\prime}_{\overline{x}x}}{\det(\Phi_{2})^{\prime\prime}_{\overline{x}x}}\prod_{j=1}^{n}\mu_{j}\right)^{1/4}.

Remark 2
.

The main difference between Theorem
1
and
[
19
, Thm. 1.3]
(reproduced in Theorem
15
below) is the factor
(\det(\Phi_{1})^{\prime\prime}_{\overline{x}x}/\det(\Phi_{2})^{\prime\prime}_{\overline{x}x})^{1/4}
. This is essentially due to the fact that
\mathbb{C}^{n}
has real dimension
2n
, which one sees for instance in Proposition
9
below.

\triangle

Remark 3
.

Another way of writing (
7
) is to define the stable subspace

(9)

\displaystyle E_{s}

\displaystyle=\bigoplus_{j\>:\>\mu_{j}\in(0,1)}\ker(\mathbf{A}_{\Phi_{2}}^{-1}\mathbf{A}_{\Phi_{1}}-\mu_{j})

\displaystyle=\{X\in\mathbb{C}^{2n}\>:\>\exists C>0,\forall N\in\mathbb{N},\|(\mathbf{A}_{\Phi_{2}}^{-1}\mathbf{A}_{\Phi_{1}})^{N}X\|\leq C\textnormal{e}^{-N/C}\}.

So long as
\Phi_{2}\geq\Phi_{1}
, the product of eigenvalues in (
7
) is equal to the determinant of
\mathbf{A}_{\Phi_{2}}^{-1}\mathbf{A}_{\Phi_{1}}
restricted to
E_{s}
, giving

(10)

\|\iota\|=\left(\frac{\det(\Phi_{1})^{\prime\prime}_{\overline{x}x}}{\det(\Phi_{2})^{\prime\prime}_{\overline{x}x}}\det(\mathbf{A}_{\Phi_{2}}^{-1}\mathbf{A}_{\Phi_{1}}|_{E_{s}})\right)^{1/4}

\triangle

When we have a strict inequality
\Phi_{1}(x)<\Phi_{2}(x)
for all
x\neq 0
, one can identify the centered Gaussian witnessing the maximum of
\|f\|_{H_{\Phi_{2}}}/\|f\|_{H_{\Phi_{1}}}
again using
\mathbf{A}_{\Phi_{1}}
and
\mathbf{A}_{\Phi_{2}}
. The formula is a straightforward modification of the formula for the ground state of the Weyl quantization of a positive definite quadratic form (see for instance
[
16
, Thm. 3.5]
). If the inequality
\Phi_{1}(x)\leq\Phi_{2}(x)
is not in general strict, one can find a sequence of Gaussians maximizing
\|f\|_{H_{\Phi_{2,\varepsilon}}}/\|f\|_{H_{\Phi_{1}}}
when
\Phi_{2,\varepsilon}(x)=\Phi_{2}(x)+\frac{1}{2}\varepsilon|x|^{2}
. Because
\|f\|_{H_{\Phi_{2,\varepsilon}}}\to\|f\|_{H_{\Phi_{2}}}
as
\varepsilon\to 0^{+}
for
f\in H_{\Phi_{2}}\subseteq H_{\Phi_{1}}
by the monotone convergence theorem, the limit of the norm ratios converges to the norm of the embedding
\iota
. The sequence of Gaussians will not generally converge to an integrable Gaussian, however. This is because the case of non-strict positivity can include operators like multiplication on
L^{2}(\mathbb{R})
by
\textnormal{e}^{-x^{2}/2}
, whose operator norm of
1
is not attained for any
L^{2}(\mathbb{R})
-function.

Theorem 4
.

For
j=1,2
let
\Phi_{j}:\mathbb{C}^{n}\to\mathbb{R}
be real-quadratic with
(\Phi_{j})^{\prime\prime}_{\overline{x}x}
positive definite. Let
\mathbf{A}_{\Phi_{j}}
be as in (
5
). Suppose furthermore that
\Phi_{1}(x)<\Phi_{2}(x)
for all
x\in\mathbb{C}^{n}\backslash\{0\}
. Then
\operatorname{Spec}\mathbf{A}_{\Phi_{2}}^{-1}\mathbf{A}_{\Phi_{1}}\subseteq(0,1)\cup(1,+\infty)
and there exists
T
, a symmetric
n
-by-
n
matrix with complex entries, such that the stable subspace (
9
) of
\mathbf{A}_{\Phi_{2}}^{-1}\mathbf{A}_{\Phi_{1}}
is the graph of
T
, meaning

(11)

\{(x,Tx)\}_{x\in\mathbb{C}^{n}}=\bigoplus_{j\>:\>\mu_{j}\in(0,1)}\ker(\mathbf{A}_{\Phi_{2}}^{-1}\mathbf{A}_{\Phi_{1}}-\mu_{j}).

With
\iota
the embedding from (
6
), the Gaussian
g_{T}(x)=\exp(\pi\textnormal{i}Tx\cdot x)
witnesses the norm of
\iota
:

(12)

\|\iota\|=\frac{\|g_{T}\|_{H_{\Phi_{2}}}}{\|g_{T}\|_{H_{\Phi_{1}}}}.

Remark 5
.

Using
[
19
]
, it is straightforward to extend these results to
\Phi_{1},\Phi_{2}
real-valued polynomials of degree
2
so long as
(\Phi_{j})^{\prime\prime}_{\overline{x}x}
are positive definite and so long as the quadratic part of
\Phi_{2}
is strictly larger than the quadratic part of
\Phi_{1}
. To make this latter condition concrete, one would assume that

(13)

\liminf_{|x|\to+\infty}|x|^{-2}(\Phi_{2}(x)-\Phi_{1}(x))>0.

An extension to more general
\Phi_{j}
would certainly be interesting, but the author does not see how to obtain similarly sharp results in a broader setting.

\triangle

Notation
.

To make certain formulas more readable, we use

(14)

e(\theta)=\textnormal{e}^{2\pi\textnormal{i}\theta}

throughout, and we reiterate that

(15)

\mathcal{L}(\textnormal{d}x)=\textnormal{d}(\operatorname{Re}x)\wedge\textnormal{d}(\operatorname{Im}x)=(-2\textnormal{i})^{-n}\textnormal{d}x\wedge\textnormal{d}\overline{x}

is Lebesgue measure on
\mathbb{C}^{n}
. We also recall that a real-valued quadratic form
\Phi:\mathbb{C}^{n}\to\mathbb{R}
is strictly plurisubharmonic if and only if the Hermitian matrix
\partial_{\overline{x}}\partial_{x}\Phi>0
in the sense of positive definite matrices.

We use
\mathbb{M}_{n}(\mathbb{C})
for the space of
n
-by-
n
complex matrices. A matrix plus a scalar (such as
A+\textnormal{i}
) indicates the matrix plus the scalar times the corresponding identity matrix; the same principle applies to scalars in block matrices like (
36
). Bold-faced names for matrices (like
\mathbf{A}_{\Phi}
) are reserved for canonical transformations. We write
x\cdot y=\sum_{j=1}^{n}x_{j}y_{j}
for the dot product on
\mathbb{R}^{n}
or
\mathbb{C}^{n}
; the notation
\langle f,g\rangle
refers to a sesquilinear inner product. In particular, on
H_{\Phi}
spaces,

(16)

\langle f,g\rangle_{H_{\Phi}}=\int_{\mathbb{C}^{n}}f(x)\overline{g(x)}e(2\textnormal{i}\Phi(x))\,\mathcal{L}(\textnormal{d}x).

2.
Examples

To illustrate the main results of this article, we apply Theorem
1
to two simple examples treated in previous works.

Example 6
.

If the pluriharmonic parts of
\Phi_{1}
and
\Phi_{2}
vanish, meaning
\Phi_{j}(x)=\frac{1}{2}L_{j}x\cdot\overline{x}
for
L_{j}
Hermitian, then

(17)

\mathbf{A}_{\Phi_{2}}^{-1}\mathbf{A}_{\Phi_{1}}=\overline{\begin{pmatrix}0&\textnormal{i}\overline{L_{2}^{-1}}\\
\textnormal{i}L_{2}&0\end{pmatrix}}\begin{pmatrix}0&\textnormal{i}\overline{L_{1}^{-1}}\\
\textnormal{i}L_{1}&0\end{pmatrix}=\begin{pmatrix}L_{2}^{-1}L_{1}&0\\
0&\overline{L_{2}L_{1}^{-1}}\end{pmatrix}.

By a change of variables,
\Phi_{2}\geq\Phi_{1}
if and only if
L_{1}^{-1/2}L_{2}L_{1}^{-1/2}\geq 1
in the sense of positive semi-definite matrices, which we assume in what follows. In this case,

(18)

\operatorname{Spec}(L_{1}^{-1/2}L_{2}L_{1}^{-1/2})=\operatorname{Spec}(L_{1}^{-1}L_{2})\in[1,+\infty).

The spectrum of
\mathbf{A}_{\Phi_{2}}^{-1}\mathbf{A}_{\Phi_{1}}
lying in
(0,1]
is therefore precisely the spectrum of
L_{2}^{-1}L_{1}
, and the product of these eigenvalues (counted for multiplicity) is
\det(L_{2}^{-1}L_{1})
. By Theorem
1
,

(19)

\|\iota\|=\left(\frac{\det L_{1}}{\det L_{2}}\det(L_{2}^{-1}L_{1})\right)^{1/4}=\sqrt{\frac{\det L_{1}}{\det L_{2}}}.

This reproduces
[
1
, Cor. 4.13]
, which used a similar approach to the present work. Specifically, embeddings between weights with no pluriharmonic part are shown to be unitarily equivalent to certain quantum harmonic oscillators.

If we assume the strict inequality
\Phi_{2}>\Phi_{1}
on
\mathbb{C}^{n}\backslash\{0\}
, then
\operatorname{Spec}L_{2}^{-1}L_{1}\subseteq(0,1)
. Every eigenvector
v
of
L_{2}^{-1}L_{1}
corresponds to an eigenvector
(v,0)
of
\mathbf{A}_{\Phi_{2}}^{-1}\mathbf{A}_{\Phi_{1}}
. Therefore in Theorem
4
we have
T=0
and the maximum of the norm of
\iota
occurs on the constant functions.

\triangle

Example 7
.

In dimension one, let us write

(20)

\Phi_{2}(x)=\frac{1}{2}a|x|^{2}+\operatorname{Re}(bx^{2}),\quad a>0,b\in\mathbb{C}

and
\Phi_{1}(x)=\frac{1}{2}|x|^{2}
(which, as detailed in Example
11
below, can be obtained by some simple transformations). Because the argument of
bx^{2}
may be changed by replacing
x
with
e(\theta)x
for varying
\theta\in\mathbb{R}
, we see that
\Phi_{2}\geq\Phi_{1}
if and only if
a-|b|\geq 1
.

Using that
\mathbf{A}_{\Phi}^{-1}=\overline{\mathbf{A}_{\Phi}}
,

(21)

\mathbf{A}_{\Phi_{2}}^{-1}\mathbf{A}_{\Phi_{1}}=\begin{pmatrix}-\overline{b}/a&-\textnormal{i}/a\\
-\textnormal{i}(a-|b|^{2}/a)&-b/a\end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix}0&\textnormal{i}\\
\textnormal{i}&0\end{pmatrix}=\frac{1}{a}\begin{pmatrix}1&-\textnormal{i}\overline{b}\\
-\textnormal{i}b&a^{2}-|b|^{2}\end{pmatrix}.

Computing the eigenvalues and inserting into Theorem
1
(where
L_{2}=a
and
L_{1}=1
) gives, when
a-|b|\geq 1
,

(22)

\|\iota\|=\left(\frac{1}{2a^{2}}\left(1+a^{2}-|b|^{2}-\sqrt{(1+a^{2}-|b|^{2})^{2}-4a^{2}}\right)\right)^{1/4}.

One can check that this agrees with the formula in
[
18
, Thm. 1.2]
, which was obtained through an elementary calculus argument.

A routine computation reveals that, if
a-|b|>1
and if

(23)

\tau=-\frac{1}{2\overline{b}}\textnormal{i}\left(1-a^{2}+|b|^{2}+\sqrt{(1+a^{2}-|b|^{2})^{2}-4a^{2}}\right),

then
(1,\tau)
is an eigenvector of
\mathbf{A}_{\Phi_{2}}^{-1}\mathbf{A}_{\Phi_{1}}
with eigenvalue in
(0,1)
. Therefore it is

(24)

g_{\tau}(x)=\textnormal{e}^{\pi\textnormal{i}\tau x^{2}}

which maximizes the norm ratio for the embedding
\iota:H_{\Phi_{1}}\to H_{\Phi_{2}}
.

In the limiting case
a-|b|=1
(assuming
a>1
), one computes

(25)

\tau=\textnormal{i}\frac{b}{|b|}.

Since
|\tau|=1
,
g_{\tau}\notin H_{\Phi_{1}}
since
\Phi_{1}=\frac{1}{2}|x|^{2}
. But one can compute that for
\delta\in[0,1)
,

(26)

\frac{\|g_{\delta\tau}\|_{H_{\Phi_{2}}}}{\|g_{\delta\tau}\|_{H_{\Phi_{1}}}}=\left(\frac{4(2a+\delta-1)(1-\delta)}{4(1-\delta^{2})}\right)^{-1/4}\to a^{-1/4},\quad\delta\to 1^{-},

and
a^{-1/4}
is indeed the norm of the embedding from
H_{\Phi_{1}}
into
H_{\Phi_{2}}
in the case
a-|b|=1
.

\triangle

3.
Some metaplectic operators on weighted spaces

The proof of the Theorem is a relatively simple consequence of the theory of the metaplectic semigroup
[
9
,
11
,
5
,
10
]
. For the unfamiliar, we try to describe this theory as concisely as possible.

3.1.
Phase-space shifts and the metaplectic semigroup

For
X=(x,\xi),Y=(y,\eta)\in\mathbb{C}^{2n}
, define the symplectic form

(27)

\sigma((x,\xi),(y,\eta))=\xi\cdot y-\eta\cdot x,

where
\xi\cdot y=\sum_{j=1}^{n}\xi_{j}y_{j}
is the usual (non-Hermitian) scalar product on
\mathbb{C}^{n}
. Let
\mathcal{S}_{Y}
denote a shift in phase space defined by (where
e(\theta)=\textnormal{e}^{2\pi\textnormal{i}\theta}
)

(28)

\mathcal{S}_{(y,\eta)}f(x)=e(-\frac{1}{2}y\cdot\eta+\eta x)f(x-y).

When
Y\in\mathbb{R}^{2n}
this operator is unitary on
L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
; if
Y\in\mathbb{C}^{2n}\backslash\mathbb{R}^{2n}
one can define
\mathcal{S}_{Y}
as an unbounded operator defined on a core of wave packets
\{\mathcal{S}_{X}g_{T}\}_{X\in\mathbb{R}^{2n}}
with
g_{T}
an integrable Gaussian. Note that, when
D_{x}=(2\pi\textnormal{i})^{-1}\nabla_{x}
,

(29)

\mathcal{S}_{Y}=e(\sigma((y,\eta),(x,D_{x})))=\exp(2\pi\textnormal{i}\eta\cdot x-y\cdot\nabla_{x})

in the sense of an evolution equation on functions. Shifts compose via the rule

(30)

\mathcal{S}_{X}\mathcal{S}_{Y}=e(\frac{1}{2}\sigma(X,Y))\mathcal{S}_{X+Y},

making
\{e(\theta)\mathcal{S}_{Y}\>:\>\theta\in\mathbb{R},Y\in\mathbb{R}^{2n}\}
the Schrödinger representation of the Heisenberg group (see for example
[
5
, Ch. 1, §3]
).

A linear map
\mathbf{M}:\mathbb{C}^{2n}\to\mathbb{C}^{2n}
is said to be canonical if it preserves
\sigma
, meaning
\sigma(\mathbf{M}X,\mathbf{M}Y)=\sigma(X,Y)
for all
X,Y\in\mathbb{C}^{2n}
. A linear canonical transformation is said to be positive if

(31)

-\textnormal{i}(\sigma(\mathbf{M}X,\overline{\mathbf{M}X})-\sigma(X,\overline{X}))\geq 0,\quad\forall X\in\mathbb{C}^{2n}.

We are now able to define the metaplectic semigroup following
[
10
]
. In defining the operators in the metaplectic semigroup, we recall that when
g_{S}(x)=e(\frac{1}{2}Sx\cdot x)=\textnormal{e}^{\pi\textnormal{i}Sx\cdot x}
for
S\in\mathbb{M}_{n}(\mathbb{C})
symmetric with
\operatorname{Im}S
positive definite, the family of shifted Gaussians
\{\mathcal{S}_{X}g_{S}\}_{X\in\mathbb{R}^{2n}}
has dense span in
L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
. A bounded operator on
L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
can therefore be defined via its action on this family.

Definition 8

(The metaplectic semigroup)
.

Let

(32)

\mathbf{M}=\begin{pmatrix}A&B\\
C&D\end{pmatrix},

where
A,B,C,D\in\mathbb{M}_{n}(\mathbb{C})
, be a positive complex linear canonical transformation. An element of the metaplectic semigroup quantizing
\mathbf{M}
is a bounded operator
\mathcal{M}:L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{n})\to L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
satisfying

(1)

the Egorov relation

(33)

\mathcal{M}\mathcal{S}_{Y}=\mathcal{S}_{\mathbf{M}Y},\mathcal{M},\quad\forall Y\in\mathbb{C}^{2n},

and

(2)

for every
T\in\mathbb{M}_{n}(\mathbb{C})
symmetric with
\operatorname{Im}T
positive definite, there exists a choice of sign
\varepsilon(T)\in\{\pm 1\}
such that, writing
g_{S}(x)=e(\frac{1}{2}Sx\cdot x)
,

(34)

\mathcal{M}g_{T}=\varepsilon(T)\det(A+BT)^{-1/2}g_{T^{\prime}},\quad T^{\prime}=(C+DT)(A+BT)^{-1}.

\triangle

As a special case of (
33
) and (
34
) one has the action of
\mathcal{M}
on any wave packet
\mathcal{S}_{X}g_{S}
for
X\in\mathbb{R}^{2n}
. Once one has chosen
\varepsilon(T)
in (
34
) for a given
T
, there is a unique choice of
\varepsilon(S)
for every other
S
symmetric with positive definite imaginary part; it suffices to require that
\det(A+BS)^{1/2}\mathcal{M}g_{S}(0)\in\{\pm 1\}
be a continuous function of
S
. There are therefore exactly two operators in the metaplectic semigroup quantizing any given positive complex linear canonical transformation
\mathbf{M}
, each corresponding to a choice of sign.

The metaplectic semigroup extends the metaplectic group, which is the subset of the metaplectic semigroup quantizing real canonical transformations. (Equivalently, the metaplectic group is the subset of the metaplectic semigroup consisting of unitary operators on
L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
.)

The metaplectic semigroup is closed under composition: if
\mathcal{M}_{1}
and
\mathcal{M}_{2}
are elements of the metaplectic semigroup quantizing
\mathbf{M}_{1}
and
\mathbf{M}_{2}
, then
\mathcal{M}_{1}\mathcal{M}_{2}
is an element of the metaplectic semigroup quantizing
\mathbf{M}_{1}\mathbf{M}_{2}
. (It is a straightforward exercise to verify (
33
) and (
34
) for the composition, modulo an argument from positivity that the matrix
A+BT
remains invertible in (
34
).)

One may also characterize the metaplectic semigroup via its generators which may be taken to be changes of variables (
37
), multiplication by Gaussians (
35
) where the phase has positive semi-definite imaginary part, and exponentials of the quantum harmonic oscillator
\exp(-\pi t(x_{1}^{2}+D_{x_{1}}^{2}))
where
D_{x}=(2\pi\textnormal{i})^{-1}\nabla_{x}
and
\operatorname{Re}t\geq 0
. (We remark that this last family includes a partial Fourier transform when
t=\pi\textnormal{i}/2
.)

3.2.
The action of some operators on weighted spaces

Some metaplectic operators which allow us to pass from one weight to another are given by multiplication by Gaussians and changes of variables. For
T
a symmetric matrix, let

(35)

\mathcal{W}_{T}f(x)=e(\frac{1}{2}x\cdot Tx)f(x)

be the operator quantizing

(36)

\mathbf{W}_{T}=\begin{pmatrix}1&0\\
T&1\end{pmatrix}.

For
G\in\mathbb{M}_{n}(\mathbb{C})
invertible, let

(37)

\mathcal{V}_{G}f(x)=(\det G)^{1/2}f(Gx).

(In general, some sign considerations arise in the square root, but our application will involve
G
positive definite Hermitian where the usual square root on
(0,\infty)
can be used.) The operator
\mathcal{V}_{G}
quantizes

(38)

\mathbf{V}_{G}=\begin{pmatrix}G^{-1}&0\\
0&G^{\top}\end{pmatrix}.

We remark that it is straightfoward to verify (
33
) and (
34
) from the definitions of
\mathcal{W}_{T}
and
\mathcal{V}_{G}
.

Writing out the definitions of the relevant norms and a change of variables immediately gives the following description of the spaces on which
\mathcal{W}_{\textnormal{i}P}
and
\mathcal{V}_{G}
act; we also include the action of a phase-space shift on
H_{\Phi}
-spaces. We emphasize that the factor in front of
\mathcal{V}_{G}
which gives a
metaplectic
operator is different from the factor in front of

which gives a

operator between

spaces because the number of (real) variables for an

.

Proposition 9
.

Let
\Phi:\mathbb{C}^{n}\to\mathbb{R}
be real-quadratic with
\Phi^{\prime\prime}_{\overline{x}x}
positive definite. Let
G,T\in\mathbb{M}_{n}(\mathbb{C})
with
\det G\neq 0
and
T^{\top}=T
, and let
Y=(y,\eta)\in\mathbb{C}^{2n}
. Let

(39)

\Phi_{T}(x)=\Phi(x)+\frac{1}{2}\operatorname{Re}(x\cdot(\textnormal{i}T)x),

let

(40)

\Phi_{G}(x)=\Phi(Gx),

and let

(41)

\Phi_{Y}(x)=\Phi(x-y)+\operatorname{Im}(\frac{1}{2}y\cdot\eta-\eta\cdot x)

Recall
\mathcal{W}_{\textnormal{i}P}
,
\mathcal{V}_{G}
, and
\mathcal{S}_{Y}
from (

), (
37
), and (

). Then

(42)

\mathcal{W}_{T}:H_{\Phi}\to H_{\Phi_{T}},

(43)

\tilde{\mathcal{V}}_{G}=(\det\overline{G})^{1/2}\mathcal{V}_{G}:H_{\Phi}\to H_{\Phi_{G}},

and

(44)

\mathcal{S}_{Y}:H_{\Phi}\to H_{\Phi_{Y}}

are unitary.

Proof.

In each case, we assume that the target weight is some

(45)

\Phi_{1}(x)=\frac{1}{2}(L_{1}x\cdot\overline{x}+\operatorname{Re}(P_{1}x\cdot x))

to be determined. For the operator
\mathcal{K}
in question we then compute
\langle\mathcal{K}f,\mathcal{K}g\rangle_{H_{\Phi_{1}}}
and we find
\Phi_{1}
such that
\langle\mathcal{K}f,\mathcal{K}g\rangle_{H_{\Phi_{1}}}=\langle f,g\rangle_{H_{\Phi}}
.

First

(46)

\displaystyle\langle\mathcal{W}_{T}f,\mathcal{W}_{T}g\rangle_{H_{\Phi_{1}}}

\displaystyle=\int_{\mathbb{C}^{n}}e(\frac{1}{2}Tx\cdot x)f(x)\overline{e(\frac{1}{2}Tx\cdot x)g(x)}e(2\textnormal{i}\Phi_{1}(x))\,\mathcal{L}(\textnormal{d}x)

\displaystyle=\int_{\mathbb{C}^{n}}f(x)\overline{g(x)}e(2\textnormal{i}(\Phi_{1}(x)-\frac{1}{4}\textnormal{i}Tx\cdot x+\frac{1}{4}\textnormal{i}\overline{Tx\cdot x})\,\mathcal{L}(\textnormal{d}x).

We obtain
\Phi_{T}
from the observation

(47)

\Phi(x)=\Phi_{1}(x)-\frac{1}{4}\textnormal{i}Tx\cdot x+\frac{1}{4}\textnormal{i}\overline{Tx\cdot x}\iff\Phi_{1}(x)=\Phi_{T}(x).

As for

\displaystyle\langle\tilde{\mathcal{V}}_{G}f,\tilde{\mathcal{V}}_{G}g\rangle_{H_{\Phi_{1}}}

\displaystyle=\int_{\mathbb{C}^{n}}f(x^{\prime})\overline{g(x^{\prime})}e(2\textnormal{i}\Phi_{1}(G^{-1}x^{\prime}))\,\mathcal{L}(\textnormal{d}x^{\prime}).

We obtain
\Phi_{G}
from the observation

(49)

\Phi_{1}(G^{-1}x)=\Phi(x)\iff\Phi_{1}(x)=\Phi_{G}(x).

Finally, a similar exercise for

\displaystyle\langle\mathcal{S}_{Y}

\displaystyle f,\mathcal{S}_{Y}g\rangle_{H_{\Phi_{1}}}

4.
Adjoints on
H_{\Phi}

We turn to the study of
\mathbf{A}_{\Phi}
from (
5
) from the point of view of adjoints of shift operators on
H_{\Phi}
-spaces. We remark that the decomposition in (
5
) is not necessarily the most practical for every situation. One could certainly multiply out to obtain

(68)

\mathbf{A}_{\Phi}=\begin{pmatrix}-\overline{L^{-1}}P&\textnormal{i}\overline{L^{-1}}\\
\textnormal{i}(L-\overline{PL^{-1}}P)&-\overline{PL^{-1}}\end{pmatrix}.

Or one could maximize the use of the matrices in (
36

38
): if
\mathbf{R}(y,\eta)=\textnormal{i}(\eta,y)
(the factor of
i
makes
\mathbf{R}
canonical), then

(69)

\displaystyle\mathbf{A}_{\Phi}

\displaystyle=\mathbf{W}_{\textnormal{i}\overline{P}}\mathbf{R}\mathbf{V}_{L}^{-1}\mathbf{W}_{\textnormal{i}P}

\displaystyle=\mathbf{W}_{\textnormal{i}\overline{P}}\mathbf{V}_{\overline{L}}\mathbf{R}\mathbf{W}_{\textnormal{i}P}.

Proposition 12
.

Let
\Phi:\mathbb{C}^{n}\to\mathbb{R}
be real-quadratic with
\Phi^{\prime\prime}_{\overline{x}x}
positive definite. Let
\mathbf{A}_{\Phi}
be as in (
5
). Then the adjoint of a phase-space shift (
28
) by
Y\in\mathbb{C}^{2n}
, as an operator on
H_{\Phi}
, is

(70)

\mathcal{S}_{Y}^{*}=\mathcal{S}_{-\overline{\mathbf{A}_{\Phi}Y}}.

Proof.

As usual, let
L=2\Phi^{\prime\prime}_{\overline{x}x}
and let
P=2\Phi^{\prime\prime}_{xx}
. Let

(71)

\Psi(x,\overline{y})=\frac{1}{2}Lx\cdot\overline{y}+\frac{1}{4}Px\cdot x+\frac{1}{4}\overline{Py}\cdot\overline{y}

so that
\Phi(x)=\Psi(x,\overline{x})
and

(72)

\langle f,g\rangle_{\Phi}=\int_{\mathbb{C}^{n}}f(x)\overline{g(x)}e(2\textnormal{i}\Psi(x,\overline{x}))\,\mathcal{L}(\textnormal{d}x).

Since
\mathcal{L}(\textnormal{d}x)=(-2\textnormal{i})^{-n}\textnormal{d}x\wedge\textnormal{d}\overline{x}
, we can analyze the integral giving the
H_{\Phi}
inner product in holomorphic and anti-holomorphic coordinates. Formally, changes of variables are accomplished via contour deformation on dense sets of functions corresponding to rapidly decaying integrals.

As an example, let
\Phi(x)=\Phi_{0}(x)=\frac{1}{2}|x|^{2}
and
\Psi_{0}(x,\overline{y})=\frac{1}{2}x\cdot\overline{y}
. Fix
y\in\mathbb{C}^{n}
and let
f,g
be polynomials; we define
\overline{g}
by taking the complex conjugate of the coefficients of
g
so that
\overline{g(z)}=\overline{g}(\overline{z})
. When
x=x_{1}+\textnormal{i}x_{2}
for
(x_{1},x_{2})\in\mathbb{R}^{2n}
, we may use a contour deformation to make the change of variables
(z_{1},z_{2})=(x_{1}-y/2,x_{2}+\textnormal{i}y/2)
. We therefore have

(73)

\displaystyle\int_{\mathbb{C}^{n}}

\displaystyle f(x-y)\overline{g(x)}e(2\textnormal{i}\Psi_{0}(x,\overline{x}))\,\mathcal{L}(\textnormal{d}x)

\displaystyle=\iint_{\mathbb{R}^{2n}}f(x_{1}+\frac{1}{2}y+\textnormal{i}(x_{2}-\frac{1}{2}\textnormal{i}y))\overline{g}(x_{1}-\textnormal{i}x_{2})\textnormal{e}^{-2\pi(x_{1}^{2}+x_{2}^{2})}\,\textnormal{d}x_{1}\,\textnormal{d}x_{2}

\displaystyle=\iint_{\mathbb{R}^{2n}}f(z_{1}+\textnormal{i}z_{2})\overline{g}(z_{1}+\frac{1}{2}y-\textnormal{i}(z_{2}-\frac{1}{2}\textnormal{i}y))\textnormal{e}^{-2\pi((z_{1}+\frac{1}{2}y)^{2}+(z_{2}-\frac{1}{2}\textnormal{i}y)^{2})}\,\textnormal{d}z_{1}\,\textnormal{d}z_{2}

\displaystyle=\iint_{\mathbb{R}^{2n}}f(z_{1}+\textnormal{i}z_{2})\overline{g}(z_{1}-\textnormal{i}z_{2})\textnormal{e}^{-2\pi(z_{1}^{2}+z_{2}^{2}+(z_{1}-\textnormal{i}x_{2})\cdot y)}\,\textnormal{d}x_{1}\,\textnormal{d}x_{2}

\displaystyle=\int_{\mathbb{C}^{n}}f(x)\overline{g(x)}e(2\textnormal{i}\Psi_{0}(z+y,\overline{z})).

We return to an arbitrary shift on an
H_{\Phi}
space, applying this type of change of variables. If
Y=(y,\eta)
,

(74)

\displaystyle\langle\mathcal{S}_{Y}f,g\rangle_{H_{\Phi}}

\displaystyle=\int_{\mathbb{C}^{n}}e(-\frac{1}{2}y\cdot\eta+\eta\cdot x)f(x-y)\overline{g(x)}e(2\textnormal{i}\Psi(x,\overline{x}))\,(-2\textnormal{i})^{-n}\textnormal{d}x\wedge\textnormal{d}\overline{x}

\displaystyle=\int_{\mathbb{C}^{n}}e(\frac{1}{2}y\cdot\eta+\eta\cdot x+2\textnormal{i}\Psi(x+y,\overline{x}))f(x)\overline{g(x)}(-2\textnormal{i})^{-n}\textnormal{d}x\wedge\textnormal{d}\overline{x}.

For
z\in\mathbb{C}^{n}
fixed, as in the case of polynomials we can define
\overline{g}
by
\overline{g(x-z)}=\overline{g}(\overline{x}-\overline{z})
. This is a function of the antiholomorphic variable
\overline{x}
, so a similar computation gives, for
Z=(z,\zeta)\in\mathbb{C}^{2n}
,

(75)

\displaystyle\langle f,\mathcal{S}_{Z}g\rangle_{H_{\Phi}}

\displaystyle=\int_{\mathbb{C}^{n}}f(x)\overline{e(-\frac{1}{2}z\cdot\zeta+\zeta\cdot x)g(x-z)}e(2\textnormal{i}\Psi(x,\overline{x}))\,(-2\textnormal{i})^{-n}\textnormal{d}x\wedge\textnormal{d}\overline{x}

\displaystyle=\int_{\mathbb{C}^{n}}e(-\frac{1}{2}\overline{z}\cdot\overline{\zeta}-\overline{\zeta}\cdot\overline{x}+2\textnormal{i}\Psi(x,\overline{x}+\overline{z}))f(x)\overline{g(x)}(-2\textnormal{i})^{-n}\textnormal{d}x\wedge\textnormal{d}\overline{x}.

One computes directly

(76)

2\textnormal{i}\Psi(x+y,\overline{x})=2\textnormal{i}\Psi(x,\overline{x})+\textnormal{i}Ly\cdot\overline{x}+\textnormal{i}Py\cdot x+\frac{1}{2}\textnormal{i}Py\cdot y

and

(77)

2\textnormal{i}\Psi(x,\overline{x}+\overline{z})=2\textnormal{i}\Psi(x,\overline{x})+\textnormal{i}\overline{L}\overline{z}\cdot x+\textnormal{i}\overline{P}\overline{z}\cdot\overline{x}+\frac{1}{2}\textnormal{i}\overline{P}\overline{z}\cdot\overline{z}.

Therefore
\langle\mathcal{S}_{Y}f,g\rangle_{H_{\Phi}}=\langle f,\mathcal{S}_{Z}g\rangle_{H_{\Phi}}
if and only if (equating coefficients of
(x,\overline{x})
)

(78)

\begin{pmatrix}\textnormal{i}P&1\\
\textnormal{i}L&0\end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix}y\\
\eta\end{pmatrix}=\begin{pmatrix}\textnormal{i}\overline{L}&0\\
\textnormal{i}\overline{P}&-1\end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix}\overline{z}\\
\overline{\zeta}\end{pmatrix}

and (equating constant terms)

(79)

\frac{1}{2}y\cdot\eta+\frac{1}{2}\textnormal{i}Py\cdot y=-\frac{1}{2}\overline{z}\cdot\overline{\zeta}+\frac{1}{2}\textnormal{i}\overline{P}\overline{z}\cdot\overline{z}.

Equation (
78
) is equivalent to
Z=-\overline{\mathbf{A}_{\Phi}Y}
. Equation (
79
) is equivalent to

(80)

y\cdot(\eta+\textnormal{i}Py)=\overline{z}\cdot(\textnormal{i}\overline{P}\overline{z}-\overline{\zeta}).

When (
78
) holds, this is simply
y\cdot\textnormal{i}\overline{L}\overline{z}=\overline{z}\cdot\textnormal{i}Ly
, which is automatic because
L
is Hermitian. Therefore
Z=-\overline{\mathbf{A}_{\Phi}Y}
implies
\langle\mathcal{S}_{Y}f,g\rangle=\langle f,\mathcal{S}_{Z}g\rangle
as claimed.
∎

Using Proposition
12
, we recall some well-known facts about
\mathbf{A}_{\Phi}
.

Proposition 13
.

Let
\Phi:\mathbb{C}^{n}\to\mathbb{R}
be real-quadratic with
\Phi^{\prime\prime}_{\overline{x}x}
positive definite, and recall
\mathbf{A}_{\Phi}
from (
5
). Let

(81)

\Lambda_{\Phi}=\{(x,-2\textnormal{i}\Phi^{\prime}_{x}(x))\>:\>x\in\mathbb{C}^{n}\}.

Finally, let
\tilde{\mathfrak{B}}:L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{n})\to H_{\Phi}
be any unitary FBI–Bargmann transform quantizing
\mathbf{B}
a complex linear canonical transformation. (Such a transformation exists by (
60
); a metaplectic transformation like (
61
) would work as well.)

Then
\mathbf{B}(\mathbb{R}^{2n})=\Lambda_{\Phi}
,
\mathbf{A}_{\Phi}=\overline{\mathbf{B}}\mathbf{B}^{-1}
, and
X\mapsto\overline{\mathbf{A}_{\Phi}X}
is the unique antilinear involution preserving
\Lambda_{\Phi}
.

Proof.

For
Y\in\mathbb{C}^{2n}
, the shift
\mathcal{S}_{Y}
is unitary on
H_{\Phi}
if and only if
\tilde{\mathfrak{B}}^{-1}\mathcal{S}_{Y}\tilde{\mathfrak{B}}=\mathcal{S}_{\mathbf{B}^{-1}Y}
is unitary on
L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
. The unitary shifts on
L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
correspond to real phase-space vectors, therefore
\mathcal{S}_{Y}
is unitary on
H_{\Phi}
if and only if
\mathbf{B}^{-1}Y\in\mathbb{R}^{2n}
. In other words, the set of unitary shifts on
H_{\Phi}
is
\mathbf{B}(\mathbb{R}^{2n})
. By Corollary
10
and Proposition
12
,
\Lambda_{\Phi}\subseteq\mathbf{B}(\mathbb{R}^{2n})
. Since
-2\textnormal{i}\Phi^{\prime}_{x}(x)
is a real-linear function of
x\in\mathbb{C}^{n}
,
\Lambda_{\Phi}
as a vector space over
\mathbb{R}
has real dimension
2n
, as does
\mathbf{B}(\mathbb{R}^{2n})
. Therefore
\Lambda_{\Phi}=\mathbf{B}(\mathbb{R}^{2n})
.

As for the relation between
\mathbf{A}_{\Phi}
and
\mathbf{B}
, note that, as an operator on
H_{\Phi}
,

(82)

\mathcal{S}_{X}=\tilde{\mathfrak{B}}\tilde{\mathfrak{B}}^{-1}\mathcal{S}_{X}\tilde{\mathfrak{B}}\tilde{\mathfrak{B}}^{-1}=\tilde{\mathfrak{B}}\mathcal{S}_{\mathbf{B}^{-1}X}\tilde{\mathfrak{B}}^{-1},

where
\mathcal{S}_{-\mathbf{B}^{-1}X}
acts on
L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
. It is elementary that when
\mathcal{S}_{Y}
acts on
L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
,
\mathcal{S}_{Y}^{*}=\mathcal{S}_{-\overline{Y}}
. Therefore the adjoint of
\mathcal{S}_{X}
on
H_{\Phi}
can be computed as

(83)

\tilde{\mathfrak{B}}\mathcal{S}_{-\overline{\mathbf{B}^{-1}X}}\tilde{\mathfrak{B}}^{-1}=\mathcal{S}_{-\mathbf{B}\overline{\mathbf{B}^{-1}X}}.

Comparing with Proposition
12
gives
-\overline{\mathbf{A}_{\Phi}X}=-\mathbf{B}\overline{\mathbf{B}^{-1}X}
for all
X\in\mathbb{C}^{2n}
, so
\mathbf{A}_{\Phi}=\overline{\mathbf{B}}\mathbf{B}^{-1}
.

The map
X\mapsto\overline{\mathbf{A}_{\Phi}X}
is transparently antilinear, and we see that it is an involution using Proposition
12
and the fact that (as an operator on
H_{\Phi}
)
(\mathcal{S}_{X}^{*})^{*}=\mathcal{S}_{X}
. Uniqueness of the antilinear involution comes from the fact that
\Lambda_{\Phi}
has real dimension
2n
and
\Lambda_{\Phi}\cap\textnormal{i}\Lambda_{\Phi}=\{0\}
(which in turn comes from
\Phi^{\prime}_{x}(x)+\textnormal{i}\Phi^{\prime}_{x}(\textnormal{i}x)=\overline{Lx}
which vanishes only when
x=0
). The fact that
\Lambda_{\Phi}
is invariant under this involution follows from the fact that
X=\overline{\mathbf{A}_{\Phi}X}
if and only
\mathcal{S}_{X}^{*}=\mathcal{S}_{-X}=\mathcal{S}_{X}^{-1}
by Propostion
12
. But we began by showing that the set of unitary shifts on
H_{\Phi}
is precisely the set of shifts by elements of
\Lambda_{\Phi}
. Therefore
X\mapsto\overline{\mathbf{A}_{\Phi}X}
is the unique antilinear involution of
\mathbb{C}^{n}
preserving
\Lambda_{\Phi}
, completing the proof of the proposition.
∎

\Phi(e(\theta)x_{0})=\frac{1}{2}(Lx_{0}\cdot\overline{x_{0}}-|Px_{0}\cdot x_{0}|)\leq\Phi_{0}(e(\theta)x_{0}),

contradicting our assumption that

for all

Using (

(87)

-\textnormal{i}\sigma(\mathbf{A}_{\Phi}X,\overline{X})=\begin{pmatrix}L-\overline{PL^{-1}}P&\textnormal{i}\overline{PL^{-1}}\\
\textnormal{i}\overline{L^{-1}}P&-\overline{L^{-1}}\end{pmatrix}X\cdot\overline{X}.

Obtaining the same formula for

instead of

and

gives the following expression for the left-hand side of (

(88)

-\textnormal{i}(\sigma(\mathbf{A}_{\Phi}-\mathbf{A}_{\Phi_{0}})X,\overline{X})=\begin{pmatrix}L-1-\overline{PL^{-1}}P&\textnormal{i}\overline{PL^{-1}}\\
-\textnormal{i}\overline{L^{-1}}P&1-\overline{L^{-1}}\end{pmatrix}X\cdot\overline{X}.

For this form to be positive definite (as claimed in the case of a strict inequality), it is necessary that
(1-\overline{L^{-1}})\xi\cdot\overline{\xi}>0
for every
\xi\in\mathbb{C}^{n}\backslash\{0\}
. This is equivalent to

in the sense of positive definite matrices. We have therefore shown that if either
\Phi>\Phi_{0}
on
\mathbb{C}^{n}\backslash\{0\}
or if (

) holds strictly for

, then

.

Invertibility of

allows us to complete the square in (

): if
X=(x,\xi)
and if

(89)

\eta=-\textnormal{i}(\overline{L}-1)^{-1}Px

then

(90)

\displaystyle-\textnormal{i}(\sigma((\mathbf{A}_{\Phi}-\mathbf{A}_{\Phi_{0}})X,\overline{X})

\displaystyle=(1-\overline{L}^{-1})(\xi+\eta)\cdot(\overline{\xi}+\overline{\eta})

\displaystyle+(L-1-\overline{PL^{-1}}P-\overline{P}(\overline{L}-1)^{-1}\overline{L^{-1}}P)x\cdot\overline{x}.

Positivity of (

) is therefore equivalent to positivity of

(91)

\displaystyle(L-1

\displaystyle-\overline{P}(1+(\overline{L}-1)^{-1})\overline{L^{-1}}P)x\cdot\overline{x}

\displaystyle=(L-1)x\cdot\overline{x}-(\overline{L}-1)^{-1}Px\cdot\overline{Px}.

To simplify notation a little, we let

(92)

y=(L-1)^{1/2}x,\quad\tilde{P}=(\overline{L}-1)^{-1/2}P(L-1)^{-1/2},

so strict positivity for
X\neq 0
of (

y\neq 0
of

(93)

|y|^{2}-|\tilde{P}y|^{2}.

On the other hand,

(94)

\displaystyle 4(\Phi(x)-\Phi_{0}(x))

\displaystyle=(L-1)x\cdot\overline{x}+\operatorname{Re}(Px\cdot x)

\displaystyle=|y|^{2}+\operatorname{Re}(\tilde{P}y\cdot y)

when
y
and
\tilde{P}
are as in (

).

The reasoning of
[
4
, Eq. (2.21)–(2.23)]
shows that this is equivalent to positivity of (
93
) as follows. Positivity of (
93
) implies positivity of (
94
) by the Cauchy-Schwarz inequality. Conversely, if
\Phi>\Phi_{0}
on
\mathbb{C}^{n}\backslash\{0\}
, then multipliying
y
by
e(\theta)
in (
94
) gives that there exists
c\in[0,1)
such that

(95)

|\tilde{P}y\cdot y|\leq c|y|^{2},\quad\forall y\in\mathbb{C}^{n}.

Therefore for all
z\in\mathbb{C}^{n}
,

(96)

\displaystyle|\tilde{P}y\cdot z|

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{4}|\tilde{P}(y+z)\cdot(y+z)-\tilde{P}(y-z)\cdot(y-z)|

\displaystyle\leq\frac{1}{4}c(|y+z|^{2}+|y-z|^{2})=\frac{1}{2}c(|y|^{2}+|z|^{2}).

If neither
y
nor
z
is zero, we may replace
y
by
y\sqrt{|z|/|y|}
and
z
by
z\sqrt{|y|/|z|}
to obtain

(97)

|\tilde{P}y\cdot z|\leq c|y|\,|z|

for all

(the case

or

being trivial). Therefore

in operator norm which implies strict positivity of (

).

This completes the proof that strict positivity of (

) and of (

) are equivalent. Up to changes of variables and completing the square, this is equivalent to the statement of the proposition in the case of a strict inequality. As mentioned at the beginning, the non-strict inequality can be obtained by taking the limit as

of

5.
Proof of Theorem
1

We will now prove Theorem
1
. Our main tool is
[
19
, Thm. 1.3]
, which as mentioned therein is a straightforward consequence of
[
10
, Prop. 5.9, Prop. 5.10]
. A version suitable for our purposes is the following.

Theorem 15
.

Let
\mathcal{K}
be an element of the metaplectic semigroup (Definition
8
) quantizing a positive linear canonical transformation
\mathbf{K}
. Then there there exist
\mu_{1},\dots,\mu_{n}\in(0,1]
such that, counting for multiplicity,

(98)

\operatorname{Spec}\overline{\mathbf{K}^{-1}}\mathbf{K}=\{\mu_{j}\}_{j=1}^{n}\cup\{\mu_{j}^{-1}\}_{j=1}^{n}

and, as an operator on
L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
,

(99)

\|\mathcal{K}\|=\left(\prod_{j=1}^{n}\mu_{j}\right)^{1/4}.

Suppose that
\Phi_{2}\geq\Phi_{1}
. For
j=1,2
, let
\tilde{\mathfrak{B}}_{j}:L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{n})\to H_{\Phi_{j}}(\mathbb{C}^{n})
be unitary FBI–Bargmann transforms as in (
60
). Then for any
f\in H_{\Phi_{1}}\backslash\{0\}
,

(100)

\frac{\|f\|_{H_{\Phi_{2}}}}{\|f\|_{H_{\Phi_{1}}}}=\frac{\|\tilde{\mathfrak{B}}_{2}^{-1}f\|_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{n})}}{\|\tilde{\mathfrak{B}}_{1}^{-1}f\|_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{n})}}=\frac{\|\tilde{\mathfrak{B}}_{2}^{-1}\tilde{\mathfrak{B}}_{1}g\|_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{n})}}{\|g\|_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{n})}},

where
g=\tilde{\mathfrak{B}}_{1}^{-1}f
can be, by varying
f
, any element of
L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{n})\backslash\{0\}
.

By (
61
), the metaplectic version

(101)

\mathfrak{B}_{2}^{-1}\mathfrak{B}_{1}=\left(\frac{\det L_{1}}{\det L_{2}}\right)^{-1/4}\tilde{\mathfrak{B}}_{2}^{-1}\tilde{\mathfrak{B}}_{1}

satisfies (
34
) and (
33
) for the complex linear canonical transformation
\mathbf{B}_{2}^{-1}\mathbf{B}_{1}
. Moreover, this canonical transformation is positive by Proposition
14
(which again is just a special case of
[
4
, Thm. 1]
). Therefore
\mathfrak{B}_{2}^{-1}\mathfrak{B}_{1}
belongs to the metaplectic semigroup (Definition
8
), and by Theorem
15
, there exist
\mu_{1},\dots,\mu_{n}\in(0,1]
such that

(102)

\operatorname{Spec}\overline{(\mathbf{B}_{2}^{-1}\mathbf{B}_{1})^{-1}}\mathbf{B}_{2}^{-1}\mathbf{B}_{1}=\{\mu_{j}\}_{j=1}^{n}\cup\{\mu_{j}^{-1}\}_{j=1}^{n}

and

(103)

\|\mathfrak{B}_{2}^{-1}\mathfrak{B}_{1}\|=\left(\prod_{j=1}^{n}\mu_{j}\right)^{1/4}.

The spectrum of a matrix is unchanged by a similarity transform, so using Proposition
13
,

(104)

\operatorname{Spec}\overline{(\mathbf{B}_{2}^{-1}\mathbf{B}_{1})^{-1}}\mathbf{B}_{2}^{-1}\mathbf{B}_{1}=\operatorname{Spec}\mathbf{B}_{1}\overline{\mathbf{B}_{1}^{-1}\mathbf{B}_{2}^{-1}}\mathbf{B}_{2}^{-1}=\operatorname{Spec}\mathbf{A}_{\Phi_{1}}^{-1}\mathbf{A}_{\Phi_{2}}.

Inserting into (
103
) and using (
100
) and (
101
) gives the result of Theorem
1
when
\Phi_{2}\geq\Phi_{1}
.

Suppose now that there exists some
x_{0}\in\mathbb{C}^{n}\backslash\{0\}
such that
\Phi_{1}(x_{0})>\Phi_{2}(x_{0})
. By Example
11
we may assume without loss of generality that
\Phi_{1}(x)=\Phi_{0}(x)=\frac{1}{2}|x|^{2}
, and furthermore by scaling we may assume that
|x_{0}|=1
. Consider

(105)

h_{\delta}(x)=e(-\frac{1}{2}\textnormal{i}\delta(\overline{x_{0}}\cdot x)^{2}),\quad\delta\in[0,1).

Notice that

(106)

|h_{\delta}(x)|^{2}e(2\textnormal{i}\Phi_{0}(x))=e(2\textnormal{i}(\Phi_{0}(x)-\frac{1}{2}\delta\operatorname{Re}((x_{0}\cdot x)^{2}))=\textnormal{e}^{-2\pi(|x|^{2}-\delta\operatorname{Re}((x_{0}\cdot x)^{2}))}.

Since

(107)

\delta\operatorname{Re}((x_{0}\cdot x)^{2})\leq\delta|x_{0}|^{2}|x|^{2}=\delta|x|^{2},

when
\delta\in[0,1)
we have that
|h_{\delta}(x)|^{2}e(2\textnormal{i}\Phi_{0}(x))
is the exponential of a negative definite quadratic form which is therefore integrable. This shows that
h_{\delta}\in H_{\Phi_{0}}
.

On the other hand,

(108)

|h_{\delta}(x_{0})|^{2}e(2\textnormal{i}\Phi_{2}(x_{0}))=e(2\textnormal{i}(\Phi_{2}(x_{0})-\frac{1}{2}\delta|x_{0}|^{2}))=\textnormal{e}^{4\pi(\delta\Phi_{0}(x_{0})-\Phi_{2}(x_{0}))}.

Therefore
|h_{\delta}(x)|^{2}e(2\textnormal{i}\Phi_{2}(x))
is again the exponential of a quadratic form, but for
\delta
sufficiently close to
1
this quadratic form is not even negative semidefinite. Therefore, for
\delta
near

,

. We have exhibited (up to changes of variables in Example

taking
\Phi_{1}
to
\Phi_{0}
) an explicit element of

for which

, meaning that the embedding between these two spaces cannot be bounded.

This shows that
\Phi_{2}\geq\Phi_{1}
is a necessary condition for
\iota:H_{\Phi_{1}}\to H_{\Phi_{2}}
to be bounded. Above, we proved the formula for the norm in the case
\Phi_{2}\geq\Phi_{1}
, and the proof of Theorem
1
is therefore complete.

6.
The Gaussian witnessing the maximum norm ratio

We now prove Theorem
4
.

A straightforward consequence of
[
10
, Prop. 5.9, Prop. 5.10]
is that if
\mathcal{K}:L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{n})\to L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
is an element of the metaplectic semigroup quantizing
\mathbf{K}
a
strictly
positive complex linear canonical transformation, then
\mathcal{K}^{*}\mathcal{K}
quantizes
\overline{\mathbf{K}^{-1}}\mathbf{K}
and, for some
q(x,\xi)
a real-valued positive definite quadratic form on
\mathbb{R}^{2n}
,

(109)

\mathcal{K}^{*}\mathcal{K}=\exp(-2\pi q^{w}(x,D_{x})).

(Here
q^{w}(x,D_{x})
is the Weyl quantization of
q
.)

It is well-known that the ground state of
q^{w}(x,D_{x})
is
g_{T_{0}}(x)=e(\frac{1}{2}T_{0}x\cdot x)
where the symmetric matrix (with positive definite imaginary part)
T_{0}
is defined by

(110)

\{(x,T_{0}x)\}_{x\in\mathbb{C}^{n}}=\bigoplus_{-\textnormal{i}\lambda>0}\ker(H_{q}-\lambda).

Here,
H_{q}
is the Hamilton map of
q
, the unique matrix antisymmetric with respect to the symplectic form
\sigma
such that
q(X)=\sigma(X,H_{q}X)
. (See for instance
[
16
, Thm. 3.5]
.)

By the exact classical-quantum correspondence
[
10
, Thm. 5.12]
,
\exp(-2\pi q^{w})=e(\textnormal{i}q^{w})
is an element of the metaplectic semigroup quantizing
\exp(H_{-\textnormal{i}q})=\exp(-\textnormal{i}H_{q})
. Having chosen
q
such that
\mathcal{K}^{*}\mathcal{K}=\exp(-q^{w})
and checking that
\mathcal{K}^{*}
quantizes
\overline{\mathbf{K}^{-1}}
, we conclude that

(111)

\exp(H_{-\textnormal{i}q})=\overline{\mathbf{K}^{-1}}\mathbf{K}.

Equivalently,
\exp(\textnormal{i}H_{q})=\mathbf{K}^{-1}\overline{\mathbf{K}}
. We insert this fact into the definition of
T_{0}
, using the notation
\mu=\exp(\textnormal{i}\lambda)
:

(112)

\displaystyle\{(x,T_{0}x)\}_{x\in\mathbb{C}^{n}}

\displaystyle=\bigoplus_{-\textnormal{i}\lambda>0}\ker(\textnormal{i}H_{q}-(\textnormal{i}\lambda))

\displaystyle=\bigoplus_{-\textnormal{i}\lambda>0}\ker(\exp(\textnormal{i}H_{q})-\textnormal{e}^{\textnormal{i}\lambda})=\bigoplus_{\mu<1}\ker(\mathbf{K}^{-1}\overline{\mathbf{K}}-\mu).

We obtained the norm of the embedding from
H_{\Phi_{1}}
to
H_{\Phi_{2}}
via the observation that, when
\tilde{\mathfrak{B}}_{j}:L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{n})\to H_{\Phi_{j}}
are unitary FBI–Bargmann transforms and when
g=\tilde{\mathfrak{B}}_{1}^{-1}f
,

(113)

\frac{\|f\|_{H_{\Phi_{2}}}}{\|f\|_{H_{\Phi_{1}}}}=\frac{\|\tilde{\mathfrak{B}}_{2}^{-1}\tilde{\mathfrak{B}}_{1}g\|_{L^{2}}}{\|g\|_{L^{2}}},

when
g=\mathfrak{B}_{1}^{-1}f
. When
\Phi_{2}(x)>\Phi_{1}(x)
for all
x\neq 0
,
\tilde{\mathfrak{B}}_{2}^{-1}\tilde{\mathfrak{B}}_{1}
quantizes a strictly positive transformation following Proposition
14
. Then we may find
g_{T_{0}}
optimizing the right-hand side of (
113
) by applying the preceding discussion to
\mathbf{K}=\mathbf{B}_{2}^{-1}\mathbf{B}_{1}
.

The link between
\{(x,T_{0}x)\}
and
g_{T_{0}}(x)=e(\frac{1}{2}T_{0}x\cdot x)
comes from

(114)

g\in\operatorname{Span}\{g_{T_{0}}\}\iff\forall x\in\mathbb{C}^{n},(\mathcal{S}_{(x,T_{0}x)}-1)g=0.

We can identify
g_{T}=\tilde{\mathfrak{B}}_{1}g_{T_{0}}
up to a constant by applying
\tilde{\mathfrak{B}}_{1}
to both sides and using the Egorov relation (
33
):

(115)

(\mathcal{S}_{(x,T_{0}x)}-1)g=0\iff(\mathcal{S}_{\mathbf{B}_{1}(x,T_{0}x)}-1)\tilde{\mathfrak{B}}_{1}g=0.

Therefore we are looking for

such that

(116)

\{(x,Tx)\}_{x\in\mathbb{C}^{n}}=\{\mathbf{B}_{1}(x,T_{0}x)\}_{x\in\mathbb{C}^{n}}.

It is not automatic that

will take the form of a graph, but examining (

) we see that the standard FBI–Bargmann transform takes Gaussians

with
\operatorname{Im}T_{0}>0
to Gaussians

where
\operatorname{Im}T
is not necessarily positive definite. Examining (
60
), this continues to be true for any FBI–Bargmann transform we are considering.

We observe that

(117)

(\overline{\mathbf{K}^{-1}}\mathbf{K}-\mu)X=0\iff(\mathbf{B}_{1}\overline{\mathbf{K}^{-1}}\mathbf{K}\mathbf{B}_{1}^{-1}-\mu)\mathbf{B}_{1}X=0.

Explicitly, since

(118)

\mathbf{B}_{1}\mathbf{K}^{-1}\overline{\mathbf{K}}\mathbf{B}_{1}^{-1}=\mathbf{B}_{1}\mathbf{B}_{1}^{-1}\mathbf{B}_{2}\overline{\mathbf{B}_{2}^{-1}\mathbf{B}_{1}}\mathbf{B}_{1}^{-1}=\mathbf{A}_{\Phi_{2}}^{-1}\mathbf{A}_{\Phi_{1}}

Therefore

(119)

\{(x,Tx)\}_{x\in\mathbb{C}^{n}}=\{\mathbf{B}_{1}(x,T_{0}x)\}_{x\in\mathbb{C}^{n}}=\bigoplus_{\mu<1}\ker(\mathbf{A}_{\Phi_{2}}^{-1}\mathbf{A}_{\Phi_{1}}-\mu).

Since

\|\tilde{\mathfrak{B}}_{2}^{-1}\tilde{\mathfrak{B}}_{1}\|=\frac{\|\tilde{\mathfrak{B}}_{2}^{-1}\tilde{\mathfrak{B}}_{1}g_{T_{0}}\|_{L^{2}}}{\|g_{T_{0}}\|_{L^{2}}}

By (

(as an operator on
