Decomposing dense matrices into dense Pauli tensors
Tyson Jones

Abstract
Decomposing a matrix into a weighted sum of Pauli strings is a common chore of the quantum computer scientist, whom is not easily discouraged by exponential scaling. But beware, a naive decomposition can be cubically more expensive than necessary! In this manuscript, we derive a fixed-memory, branchless algorithm to compute the inner product between a 2 N × 2 N 2^{N}\times 2^{N} complex matrix and an N N -term Pauli tensor in 𝒪 ⁡ ( 2 N ) \mathcal{O}(2^{N}) time, by leveraging the Gray code. Our scheme permits the embarrassingly parallel decomposition of a matrix into a weighted sum of Pauli strings in 𝒪 ⁡ ( 8 N ) \mathcal{O}(8^{N}) time. We implement our algorithm in Python, hosted open-source on Github [ 1 ] , and benchmark against a recent state-of-the-art method called the “PauliComposer” [ 2 , 3 ] which has an exponentially growing memory overhead, achieving speedups in the range of 1.5 × 1.5\times to 5 × 5\times for N ≤ 7 N\leq 7 . Note that our scheme does not leverage sparsity, diagonality, Hermitivity or other properties of the input matrix which might otherwise enable optimised treatment in other methods. As such, our algorithm is well-suited to decomposition of dense, arbitrary, complex matrices which are expected dense in the Pauli basis, or for which the decomposed Pauli tensors are a priori unknown.



1
Introduction

Given a dense complex matrix
\bm{G}:\mathbb{C}^{2^{N}\times 2^{N}}
, we seek
4^{N}
coefficients
c_{i}\in\mathbb{C}
which encode
\bm{G}
as a
4^{N}
-term sum of weighted
N
-Pauli tensors;

\displaystyle\bm{G}\equiv\sum\limits_{n}^{4^{N}}c_{n}\,\bm{P}_{n}

(1)

where
\bm{P}_{n}:\mathbb{C}^{2^{N}\times 2^{N}}
are the matrices of the
N
-dimensional Pauli tensors.
For example, given
\bm{G}:\mathbb{C}^{4\times 4}
, we seek
c_{i}
satisfying

\displaystyle\bm{G}\equiv c_{0}\,\bm{\mathbb{1}}\otimes\bm{\mathbb{1}}\;+\;c_{1}\,\bm{\mathbb{1}}\otimes\bm{X}\;+\;c_{2}\,\bm{\mathbb{1}}\otimes\bm{Y}\;+\;c_{3}\,\bm{\mathbb{1}}\otimes\bm{Z}\;+\;c_{4}\,\bm{X}\otimes\bm{\mathbb{1}}\;+\;\dots\;+\;c_{15}\,\bm{Z}\otimes\bm{Z},

(2)

where the above are the standard Pauli matrices

\displaystyle\bm{\mathbb{1}}=\begin{pmatrix}1\\
&1\end{pmatrix},\;\;\;\bm{X}=\begin{pmatrix}&1\\
1\end{pmatrix},\;\;\;\bm{Y}=\begin{pmatrix}&-\mathrm{i}\\
\mathrm{i}\end{pmatrix},\;\;\;\bm{Z}=\begin{pmatrix}1\\
&-1\end{pmatrix}.

(3)

Obtaining
c_{i}
is a common task of a quantum computer scientist, who seeks to decompose a Hermitian operator - like a Hamiltonian, or a unitary operator’s generator - into a real-weighted sum of Pauli strings. This permits its expectation evaluation on a quantum computer
[
4
]
, its Trotterisation into Pauli gadgets
[
5
]
, or the sampling of variational quantities
[
6
]
.

While the coefficients satisfy

\displaystyle c_{n}=\frac{1}{2^{N}}\text{Tr}\left(\,\bm{P}_{n}\;\bm{G}\,\right),

(4)

this is an impractical form to evaluate directly, and might naively imply a cost of
\mathcal{O}(8^{N})

per

c_{n}
, or a total decomposition cost of
\mathcal{O}(32^{N})
. Even by making no assumptions about
\bm{G}
, we can find a significantly more efficient evaluation by studying
\bm{P}_{n}
and leveraging the properties of the Pauli matrices. In the next section, we derive a scheme which makes use of the fact that the Pauli matrices are diagonal or anti-diagonal, and that the elements of
\bm{P}_{n}
can be enumerated in a deliberate order so as to speedup evaluation of the trace.

2
Derivation

Because our scheme makes extensive use of indexing and bitwise algebra, we adopt an unconventional notation.
We notate the
(i,j)
-th element of a matrix
\bm{M}
in square brackets as
[\bm{M}]_{ij}
, where
i
and
j
begin at
zero
. The
t
-th bit (also indexing from zero) of a non-negative integer
i\in\mathbb{N}^{0}
is notated as
i_{[t]}
. The zero-th bit is the
rightmost
, as is the zero-th operator in a Pauli tensor. All sum notation implicitly begins at zero and ends
exclusive
, so that
\sum_{i}^{3}x_{i}=x_{0}+x_{1}+x_{2}
.

We begin by expanding Eq.
4
to a sum of
2^{2N}
terms;

\displaystyle c_{n}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2^{N}}\sum_{i}^{2^{N}}\sum_{j}^{2^{N}}\left[\bm{P}_{n}\right]_{ij}\left[\bm{G}\right]_{ji}.

(5)

Let the
n
-th Pauli tensor
P_{n}
have individual Pauli operators
\sigma_{t}^{(n)}
, with matrices
\bm{\sigma}_{t}^{(n)}:\mathbb{C}^{2\times 2}
,
such that

\displaystyle\bm{P}_{n}\equiv\bigotimes\limits_{t}^{N}\bm{\sigma}_{t}^{(n)},\;\;\;\;\;\;\;{\sigma}_{t}^{(n)}\,\in\{\mathbb{1},X,Y,Z\},

(6)

to make explicit that an element of
\bm{P}_{n}:\mathbb{C}^{2^{N}\times 2^{N}}
is given by

\displaystyle\left[\bm{P}_{n}\right]_{ij}=\prod\limits_{t}^{N}\left[\bm{\sigma}_{t}^{(n)}\right]_{i_{[t]},\,j_{[t]}}.

(7)

Such a bitwise treatment of a Pauli tensor is demonstrated in Ref.
[
7
]
, but we will here further leverage that the Pauli matrices are diagonal or anti-diagonal. As such, we will somewhat unusually label their elements as

\displaystyle\left[\bm{\sigma}_{t}^{(n)}\right]_{ab}=\beta_{ab}^{(n,t)}\,\times\begin{cases}\delta_{ab}&\sigma_{t}^{(n)}\in\{\mathbb{1},Z\}\\
1-\delta_{ab}&\sigma_{t}^{(n)}\in\{X,Y\}\end{cases},\;\;\;\;\;\;\;\;\beta_{ab}^{(n,t)}\in\{\pm 1,\pm\mathrm{i}\},

(8)

where
\delta_{ab}
is the Kronecker delta.
By substituting these forms back into
c_{n}
, we obtain

\displaystyle c_{n}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2^{N}}\sum_{i}^{2^{N}}\sum_{j}^{2^{N}}\left[\bm{G}\right]_{ji}\,\prod\limits_{t}^{N}\beta_{i_{[t]},j_{[t]}}^{(n,t)}\times\begin{cases}\delta_{i_{[t]},j_{[t]}}&\sigma_{t}^{(n)}\in\{\mathbb{1},Z\}\\
1-\delta_{i_{[t]},j_{[t]}}&\sigma_{t}^{(n)}\in\{X,Y\}\end{cases}

(9)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2^{N}}\sum_{i}^{2^{N}}\left[\bm{G}\right]_{f_{n}(i),i}\prod\limits_{t}^{N}\beta_{i_{[t]},f_{n}(i)_{[t]}}^{(n,t)},

(10)

where function
f_{n}(i)
accepts the
N
-bit integer
i
and flips the bits at indices
t
satisfying
\sigma_{t}^{(n)}\in\{X,Y\}
.
We will construct (and in our algorithm,
evaluate
) function
f_{n}
using an
N
-length bitmask
m^{(n)}
which contains a
1
at position
t
only if the
n
-th Pauli tensor
P_{n}
contains an
X
or
Y
operator at position
t
. Then

\displaystyle f_{n}(i)=i\oplus m^{(n)},\hskip 56.9055pt\therefore f_{n}(i)_{[t]}=i_{[t]}\oplus{m^{(n)}}_{[t]}\,.

(11)

These bitwise evaluations are constant time on systems with a fixed-size word, and when
N
is smaller or equal to that word size. For example, using a
32
-bit natural number bounds
N\leq 32
. Or, if we instantiated
m^{(n)}
as a
unsigned long long int
in a
C
program, evaluating
f_{n}(i)
is
\mathcal{O}(1)
for
N\leq 64
.

We finally define

\displaystyle\lambda_{i}^{(n)}=\prod\limits_{t}^{N}\beta_{i_{[t]},\,i_{[t]}\oplus{m^{(n)}}_{[t]}}^{(n,t)}\;\;\in\{\pm 1,\pm\mathrm{i}\}

(12)

so that we may express

\displaystyle c_{n}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2^{N}}\sum_{i}^{2^{N}}\lambda_{i}^{(n)}\left[\bm{G}\right]_{i\oplus m^{(n)},\,i}.

(13)

So far, we have simplified Eq.
5
to a sum of only
2^{N}
terms; a quadratic improvement. Evaluation of Eq.
13
for a given
n
still suggests
2^{N}
independent evaluations of
\lambda_{i}^{(n)}
, each a product of
N
scalars. We can further eliminate a factor
N
in runtime by replacing an ordered iteration of
i\in[0\,..\,2^{N})
with an enumeration of the
N
-bit Gray-codes
[
8
]
. Subsequent
i
, which can be calculated in a fixed number of operations
[
9
]
, then differ by a single bit (a Hamming distance of
1
), enabling a recurrence in the evaluation of
\lambda_{i}^{(n)}
across
i
.
This is by observing that only a
single
scalar
\beta^{(n,t)}_{ab}
among the
N
-term product in Eq.
12
has changed as
i\rightarrow i^{\prime}
.
Explicitly, let
i^{\prime}
differ from
i
by a single bit at position
t
. Then

\displaystyle\lambda_{i^{\prime}}^{(n)}=\lambda_{i}^{(n)}\;\beta_{i^{\prime}_{[t]},\,i^{\prime}_{[t]}\oplus{m^{(n)}}_{[t]}}^{(n,t)}\;/\;\beta_{i_{[t]},\,i_{[t]}\oplus{m^{(n)}}_{[t]}}^{(n,t)},

(14)

and ergo
\lambda_{i^{\prime}}^{(n)}
can be calculated from
\lambda_{i}^{(n)}
in a fixed number of operations.

If we wished, we could also leverage a potential recurrence in
\lambda_{i}^{(n)}
across
n
. We could enumerate the Pauli tensors
P_{n}
via the
quaternary
Gray code
[
10
]
such that
P_{n^{\prime}}
differs from the previously enumerated
P_{n}
by a single Pauli operator at position
t
. Then

\displaystyle m^{(n^{\prime})}=\begin{cases}m^{(n)},&\mathbb{1}\leftrightarrow Z\text{ or }X\leftrightarrow Y\\
m^{(n)}\oplus(1\texttt{<<}t)&\text{otherwise}\end{cases},

(15)

\displaystyle\lambda_{i}^{(n^{\prime})}=\lambda_{i}^{(n)}\;\beta_{i_{[t]},\,i_{[t]}\oplus{m^{(n^{\prime})}}_{[t]}}^{(n^{\prime},t)}\;/\;\beta_{i_{[t]},\,i_{[t]}\oplus{m^{(n)}}_{[t]}}^{(n,t)}\,.

(16)

In serial settings, this enables us to avoid the initial
\mathcal{O}(N)
calculation of
\lambda_{0}^{(n)}
for each
n\in[0\,..\,4^{N})
. This is a modest gain however, and in parallel settings, we should instead exploit the otherwise embarrassingly parallel evaluation possible of
c_{n}
across
n
.

Note that use of the above recurrences require we know the position
t
of the single bit
i^{\prime}
which has differed from the previous Gray code
i
. For an unbounded
N
, this determination costs time
\mathcal{O}(\log N)
, but with bounded
N
as enforced by our aforementioned use of a bitmask, the index
t
can be determined in constant time using a lookup table
[
9
]
, or a fixed-word bitwise calculation of
\log_{2}(i\oplus i^{\prime})

[
11
]
.

Also note that our formulation assumed we can efficiently identify which Pauli operator (of
\mathbb{1},X,Y,Z
) admits scalar
\beta^{(n,t)}_{ab}
, given the Pauli tensor index
n\in[0\,..\,4^{N})
and Pauli position index
t\in[0\,..\,N)
. This is equivalent to finding the
t
-th digit of a base-
4

N
-digit numeral, and can only be done in constant time if we again assert boundedness of
N
. We merely group together contiguous pairs of bits of
n
, with the
t
-th group encoding the flag
\in[0\,..4)
of
\sigma^{(n)}_{t}\in\{\mathbb{1},X,Y,Z\}
. Beware that this has
halved
the maximum number of Pauli tensor terms
N
to be
half
the number of bits in our natural number type.

.

.

.
For comparison, we include a slower, alternative algorithm (bottom) which also evaluates Eq.

On platforms where an unsigned integer (equal to the word size) has at least
2N
bits, the total runtime to compute a single coefficient
c_{n}
scales as
\mathcal{O}(2^{N})
, and ergo determining all
4^{N}
coefficients takes time
\mathcal{O}(8^{N})
.
When
2N
exceeds the word size, our method incurs a slowdown of factor
\mathcal{O}(N)
, although this is an unrealistic regime - with modern
64
-bit words, such a scenario would require our input complex matrix
\bm{G}:\mathbb{C}^{2^{32}\times 2^{32}}
, at
8
-bit double precision, was already
256

exbibytes
in memory. This is remarkably close to the 2011 estimate of the world’s total data storage capacity
[
12
]
.

Ignoring the cost of the input matrix, our scheme uses only a fixed memory overhead in each evaluation of
c_{n}
. This permits the determination of a
c_{n}
from a matrix
\bm{G}
which may fill almost the entirety of the system’s available memory. Serial simulation at such sizes will likely have long become untenable; however, because evaluation of
c_{n}
across

is embarrasingly parallel, our scheme is trivial to parallelise.

Indeed, because our scheme does not modify any scaling data structures, the memory overhead of multithreading
[

]
remains negligible, and we have no risk of performance degradation due to cache conflicts and false sharing
[
14
]
. It is further straightforward to distribute evaluation of
c_{n}
across a computer network
[
15
]
. Finally, because our algorithm contains no branching whatsoever, it is well-suited to GPU parallelisation
[

]
.

Benchmarking

Figure 2

Average runtime (with
3\times

coefficients) one hundred random, dense, complex
2^{N}\times 2^{N}

-Pauli tensors. The ratio of PD’s runtime to that of our method is shown in the subplot.

We compare our method to a very recent technique called the “PauliDecomposer” (PD) which was demonstrated in Ref.
[
2
]
to outperform several other state-of-the-art methods.
While the PD has an equivalent runtime complexity to our method of
\mathcal{O}(8^{N})
, it additionally requires a growing memory overhead of
\mathcal{O}(2^{N})
, in contrast to our fixed
\mathcal{O}(1)
cost.
Note that PD has optimised, asymptotically-improved edge-cases for when the input matrix is diagonal, or entirely real. Such facilities are not implemented by our method, and not investigated by our benchmarking.

We measure the runtime of both algorithms fully decomposing random complex matrices when running serially in Python v3.9.1 on a 2017 13-inch Macbook Pro, with 16 GiB RAM and a 2.5 GHz Intel Core i7.
Varying
N
from
1
to
7
, we time both algorithms decomposing one hundred random matrices. Our results are shown in Fig.
2
, and exhibit a speedup of our method over the PD by a factor
1.5\times
to
5\times
.

Our largest benchmark is of a meagre
N=7
, whereby the input double-precision matrix
\bm{G}:\mathbb{C}^{2^{7}\times 2^{7}}
is
256\,
KiB in memory, and the temporary data structures used by the PauliDecomposer are, to the best of the author’s assessment, only
2\,
KiB in memory. This is smaller than the tested platform’s L1 cache of
32\,
KB. As such, we have not tested the regime where the PauliDecomposer’s memory requirements may invoke a runtime penalty due to caching, to which our own constant-memory method is not liable. Benchmarks for larger
N
might ergo showcase greater speedup, though are presently precluded by the author’s laziness.

We note the pseudocode for PD (for
