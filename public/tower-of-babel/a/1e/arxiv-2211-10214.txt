Dispersive formalism for the nuclear structure correction δ NS \delta_{\mathrm{NS}} to the β \beta decay rate
Chien-Yeah Seng 1,2,3, Mikhail Gorchtein 4,5

Abstract
We analyze the axial γ ​ W \gamma W -box diagram for I ⁡ ( J P ) = 1 ​ ( 0 + ) I(J^{P})=1(0^{+}) nuclei and provide a dispersion representation of the nuclear-structure correction δ NS \delta_{\text{NS}} including its energy-dependent part. We also summarize useful isospin rotation formula and representations in nuclear theory that could facilitate the calculation of the parity-odd nuclear structure function F 3 ​ ( ν , Q 2 ) F_{3}(\nu,Q^{2}) . They provide a rigorous theory framework for the future, high-precision calculation of the nuclear structure correction δ NS \delta_{\text{NS}} necessary for the extraction of the Cabibbo-Kobayashi-Maskawa matrix element | V u ​ d | |V_{ud}| from superallowed nuclear β \beta decays.

Recently, a novel dispersion relation (DR) treatment was introduced to study the single-nucleon and nuclear axial
\gamma W
-box diagram
Seng et al. 2018
;
Seng et al. 2019
. In this method, the box diagram is expressed in terms of an integral over the parity-odd, spin-independent nuclear structure function
F_{3}(\nu,Q^{2})
. At large
Q^{2}
, it continues smoothly to the single-nucleon structure function so the nuclear and single-nucleon inner RC share the same asymptotic piece.

The focus, then, is on small and moderate
\{\nu,Q^{2}\}
where the two structure functions start to deviate. One may infer the dominant intermediate state contribution to
F_{3}
from the general knowledge of the nuclear absorption spectrum, as depicted in Fig.
2
. For spinless nuclei, the elastic intermediate state does not contribute to
F_{3}
due to parity. Meanwhile, the quenched coupling constants adopted in Ref.
Towner 1994
are obtained from nuclear Gamow-Teller transitions and nuclear magnetic moments, which characterize the contributions from the low-lying discrete energy levels at the lower end of Fig.
2
. This treatment disregards potentially important contributions from the quasi-elastic absorption peak, as pointed out in Ref.
Seng et al. 2019
.
Another new effect is associated with the electron energy
E_{e}
.
The
E_{e}
-dependence of the RC has long been believed negligible, based on the naive dimensional analysis: it can only enter in powers of
E_{e}/\Lambda
with
\Lambda
a relevant strong-interaction scale. For the free nucleon, the lightest choice of such a scale is
M_{\pi}
, so the energy-dependent corrections scale as
(\alpha/\pi)(E_{e}/M_{\pi})\sim 10^{-5}
, well below the current precision goal. For nuclear systems, as Ref.
Gorchtein 2019
pointed out, the nuclear excitation spectrum features a much lower characteristic scale
\Lambda_{\text{nucl}}\sim 10
MeV, comparable to the electron energy available in the decay process.

Both novel effects were estimated in the free Fermi gas model, demonstrating that they are non-negligible at the precision level of 0.01%. They tend to partially cancel each other, hardly affecting the central value of
\mathcal{F}t
. However, this cancellation is delicate and model-dependent, and each individual shift is at the level of three standard deviations in terms of the previous analysis of
\delta_{\text{NS}}

Hardy and Towner 2009
;
Hardy and Towner 2015
.
A conservative analysis resulted in inflated theory uncertainty in
\delta_{\text{NS}}

Gorchtein 2019
, currently the largest in the
|V_{ud}|_{0^{+}}
error budget
Hardy and Towner 2020
. A summary of the
\delta_{\text{NS}}
values used in global analysis
Hardy and Towner 2020
that includes the aforementioned new nuclear structure uncertainties can be found in Table
1
.

In this work we provide the fully-relativistic theory framework to study
\delta_{\text{NS}}
, based on the dispersive representation of the nuclear
\gamma W
-box diagram. In this method both the energy-independent and energy-dependent part of
\delta_{\text{NS}}
are simultaneously taken into account. To proceed further, one must compute the parity-odd nuclear structure function
F_{3}(\nu,Q^{2})
, which can be obtained from the discontinuity of nuclear matrix elements involving the nuclear Green’s function

G(\omega)=1/(H_{0}-\omega)
, with
H_{0}
the (isospin-symmetric) QCD Hamiltonian.
A recent proposal to study
\delta_{\text{C}}
with ab-initio methods also involves computing the nuclear Green’s function
Seng and Gorchtein 2022
.
A program on calculating
\delta_{\text{NS}}
and
\delta_{\text{C}}
with modern ab-initio methods will enable one to assess the nuclear structure uncertainties in
V_{ud}
in a controllable way.

-tensor coming from the lepton spinor structure.
We may further split this amplitude as
\delta\mathfrak{M}_{\gamma W}^{b}=\delta\mathfrak{M}_{\gamma W}^{b,V}+\delta\mathfrak{M}_{\gamma W}^{b,A}
which results from the same splitting in
T^{\mu\nu}
.
Since the spin-independent piece in
T^{\mu\nu}_{V}
is symmetric in the Lorentz indices, the contribution of
\delta\mathfrak{M}_{\gamma W}^{b,V}
must take the form of
\epsilon^{\mu\nu\alpha\lambda}p_{\mu}p^{\prime}_{\nu}p_{e\alpha}L_{\lambda}
, which vanishes in the forward limit. Hence, the only non-trivial piece for free nucleon and nuclei is
\delta\mathfrak{M}_{\gamma W}^{b,A}
.
To proceed, we make two more approximations in
\delta\mathfrak{M}_{\gamma W}^{b,A}
:

1.

We make a non-recoil approximation
T_{\mu\nu}^{A}(q;p^{\prime},p)\approx T_{\mu\nu}^{A}(q;p,p)\equiv T_{\mu\nu}^{A}(p,q)
. This is well-justified in the inner RC for
\beta
decays of nearly-degenerate systems.

2.

We are allowed to take
m_{e}\rightarrow 0
in the electron propagator since
\delta\mathfrak{M}_{\gamma W}^{b,A}
is free from electron mass singularities
Seng et al. 2022c
.

With these approximations,
\delta\mathfrak{M}_{\gamma W}^{b,A}
is proportional to the tree-level amplitude:

\delta\mathfrak{M}_{\gamma W}^{b,A}\approx\Box^{b}_{\gamma W}\mathfrak{M}_{0}\penalty\ ,

(25)

and the multiplicative factor is expressed in terms of the parity-odd amplitude
T_{3}
:

\Box^{b}_{\gamma W}=-e^{2}\int\frac{d^{4}q}{(2\pi)^{4}}\frac{M_{W}^{2}}{M_{W}^{2}+Q^{2}}\frac{1}{(p_{e}-q)^{2}Q^{2}}\frac{Q^{2}+M\nu\frac{p_{e}\cdot q}{p\cdot p_{e}}}{M\nu}\frac{T_{3}(\nu,Q^{2})}{f_{+}(0)}\penalty\ .

(26)

For the
(\delta\mathfrak{M}_{2}+\delta\mathfrak{M}_{\gamma W}^{a})_{\text{int}}
term, the outer correction and the Fermi function contain all IR-sensitive contributions that stem from the Born term
T^{\mu\nu}_{V,\text{B}}
. The remaining part stems from
T^{\mu\nu}_{V,\text{nB}}
and with the same approximations as before, reads,

(\delta\mathfrak{M}_{2}+\delta\mathfrak{M}_{\gamma W}^{a})_{\text{int}}^{\text{nB}}\approx\Box_{\gamma W}^{a}\mathfrak{M}_{0}\penalty\ ,

(27)

where the superscript denotes that only infrared-finite, non-Born parts are retained. Furthermore,

\displaystyle\Box_{\gamma W}^{a}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-\frac{2e^{2}}{f_{+}(0)}\eta\int\frac{d^{4}q}{(2\pi)^{4}}\frac{1}{(p_{e}-q)^{2}}\left\{\frac{-2(p_{e}\cdot q)^{2}}{p\cdot p_{e}(Q^{2})^{2}}T_{1}(\nu,Q^{2})\right.

(28)

\displaystyle\left.+\left[\frac{M(p_{e}\cdot q)^{2}}{p\cdot p_{e}\nu(Q^{2})^{2}}-\frac{2p_{e}\cdot q}{(Q^{2})^{2}}-\frac{p\cdot p_{e}}{M\nu Q^{2}}\right]T_{2}(\nu,Q^{2})\right\}\penalty\ .

Notice that this piece vanishes when
E_{e}\rightarrow 0
.
The two contributions can be put together,

\Box_{\gamma W}=\Box_{\gamma W}^{a}+\Box_{\gamma W}^{b}.

(29)

As we explained in the Introduction, the
E_{e}
-dependence in Eq.(
26
) may be discarded for a free neutron but not for nuclear systems.

The general formalism above applies to both superallowed decays and the free neutron decay. Using Eqs.(
23
)–(
29
), we express the full inner RC to superallowed nuclear
\beta
decays as

\delta\mathfrak{M}_{\text{inner}}^{\text{nucl}}=\left\{\frac{\alpha}{4\pi}\left[3\ln\frac{M_{Z}}{m_{p}}+\ln\frac{M_{Z}}{M_{W}}+\tilde{a}_{g}\right]+\frac{1}{2}\delta_{\text{HO}}^{\text{QED}}+\Box_{\gamma W}^{n}\right\}\mathfrak{M}_{0}+\left\{\Box_{\gamma W}^{\text{nucl}}(E_{e})-\Box_{\gamma W}^{n}\right\}\mathfrak{M}_{0}\penalty\ ,

(30)

where
\Box_{\gamma W}^{n}
and
\Box_{\gamma W}^{\text{nucl}}(E_{e})
refer to Eq.(
29
) for the case of neutron and nuclear
\beta
decay, respectively; the latter has a potentially non-negligible energy-dependence. The terms in the first curly brackets coincide with the inner correction to the free neutron
\beta
decay and are independent of nuclear structure,

\Delta_{R}^{V}=\frac{\alpha}{2\pi}\left[3\ln\frac{M_{Z}}{m_{p}}+\ln\frac{M_{Z}}{M_{W}}+\tilde{a}_{g}\right]+\delta_{\text{HO}}^{\text{QED}}+2\Box_{\gamma W}^{n}\penalty\ ,

(31)

where
\tilde{a}_{g}\approx-0.083
is the represents the perturbative QCD correction not coming from the
\gamma W
-box diagram, and
\delta_{\text{HO}}^{\text{QED}}\approx 0.0013
combines the resummation of the
\mathcal{O}(\alpha)
Quantum Electrodynamics (QED) logarithms and the leading
\mathcal{O}(\alpha^{2})
corrections
Czarnecki et al. 2004
.
The second curly brackets in Eq.(
30
) contain the only structure-dependent part in the inner RC, namely the difference between
\Box_{\gamma W}
on a nucleus and on a free nucleon. Averaging it over the electron energy spectrum (see Eq.(
93
)), but dropping the smaller structure-dependent corrections yields,

\delta_{\mathrm{NS}}=\frac{2\int_{m_{e}}^{E_{m}}dE_{e}|\vec{p}_{e}|E_{e}(E_{e}-E_{m})^{2}F(Z_{f},E_{e})\left[\mathfrak{Re}\Box_{\gamma W}^{\mathrm{nucl}}(E_{e})-\Box_{\gamma W}^{n}\right]}{\int_{m_{e}}^{E_{m}}dE_{e}|\vec{p}_{e}|E_{e}(E_{e}-E_{m})^{2}F(Z_{f},E_{e})}\penalty\ .

(32)

Eqs.(
31
) and (
32
) rigorously define the structure-independent and structure-dependent part of the inner RC to superallowed
\beta
decays in terms of the single-nucleon and nuclear
\gamma W
-box diagram or, conversely, the invariant amplitudes
T_{i}
for the free nucleon and nuclei.

IV
Crossing symmetry of the invariant amplitudes

The first step towards a complete understanding of the inner RC is to study the symmetry of
T_{i}(\nu,Q^{2})
under crossing
\nu\rightarrow-\nu
. This is most easily appreciated by observing the expression of
\Box_{\gamma W}
in the limit of zero electron momentum:

\Box_{\gamma W}(0)=\frac{e^{2}}{f_{+}(0)}\int\frac{d^{4}q}{(2\pi)^{4}}\frac{M_{W}^{2}}{M_{W}^{2}+Q^{2}}\frac{Q^{2}+\nu^{2}}{Q^{4}}\frac{T_{3}(\nu,Q^{2})}{M\nu}\penalty\ .

(33)

One clearly sees that only the component of
T_{3}
that is odd under
\nu\rightarrow-\nu
contributes to the integral in this limit. Once
E_{e}\neq 0
the contribution from
T_{1,2}
and the even component of
T_{3}
start to turn on. Therefore, the knowledge of the crossing symmetry of
T_{i}
is essential for the separate discussion of the energy-independent and energy-dependent part of the inner RC. Here we will summarize the main conclusions, leaving the detailed derivation to Appendix
C
.

First, if a certain combination of a product of two currents (which we label generically as
A
) leads to a definite crossing symmetry of the invariant amplitudes, then the crossing can always be described by a factor
\xi^{A}=\pm 1
, where

T_{1}^{A}(-\nu,Q^{2})=-\xi^{A}T_{1}^{A}(\nu,Q^{2})\penalty\ ,\penalty\ T_{2,3}^{A}(-\nu,Q^{2})=\xi^{A}T_{2,3}^{A}(\nu,Q^{2})\penalty\ .

(34)

The usual starting point for the crossing symmetry discussion is to write,

T_{i}(\nu,Q^{2})=T_{i}^{(0)}(\nu,Q^{2})+T_{i}^{(1)}(\nu,Q^{2})\penalty\ ,

(35)

following the decomposition
J_{\text{em}}^{\mu}=J_{\mathrm{em}}^{(0)\mu}+J_{\mathrm{em}}^{(1)\mu}
. Assuming isospin symmetry, we find that

1.

T_{i}^{(0)}
has a definite crossing symmetry
\xi^{(0)}=-1
that holds regardless of the external states;

2.

T_{i}^{(1)}(\nu,Q^{2})
, while vanishing identically for the pion due to G-parity, and having
a definite crossing symmetry
\xi^{(1)}=+1
for
I=1/2
systems, in the general case, and in particular for for
I=1
, has mixed crossing symmetry.

It is convenient to split
T_{i}^{(1)}
into two pieces,

T_{i}^{(1)}=T_{i,\text{s}}^{(1)}+T_{i,\text{a}}^{(1)}
,
defined by symmetrizing (s) and anti-symmetrizing (a) the isotriplet indices,

\displaystyle T^{\mu\nu}_{(1),\text{s}}(p,q)

\displaystyle\equiv

\displaystyle\frac{1}{8\sqrt{2}}\int d^{4}xe^{iq\cdot x}\langle\phi_{f}(p)|T\{V_{10}^{\mu}(x)(V_{1-1}^{\nu}(0)-A_{1-1}^{\nu}(0))

\displaystyle+V_{1-1}^{\mu}(x)(V_{10}^{\nu}(0)-A_{10}^{\nu}(0))\}|\phi_{i}(p)\rangle

\displaystyle T^{\mu\nu}_{(1),\text{a}}(p,q)

\displaystyle\equiv

\displaystyle\frac{1}{8\sqrt{2}}\int d^{4}xe^{iq\cdot x}\langle\phi_{f}(p)|T\{V_{10}^{\mu}(x)(V_{1-1}^{\nu}(0)-A_{1-1}^{\nu}(0))

(36)

\displaystyle-V_{1-1}^{\mu}(x)(V_{10}^{\nu}(0)-A_{10}^{\nu}(0))\}|\phi_{i}(p)\rangle

They have now definite crossing symmetry,
\xi^{(1),\text{s}}=-1
,
\xi^{(1),\text{a}}=+1
, and we can finally combine all the pieces that possess the same crossing behavior
T_{i}=T_{i,+}+T_{i,-}
,

\displaystyle T_{1,+}

\displaystyle=T_{1}^{(0)}+T_{1,\text{s}}^{(1)}\penalty\ ,\penalty\ T_{1,-}=T_{1,\text{a}}^{(1)}\penalty\ ,

(37)

\displaystyle T_{i,+}

\displaystyle=T_{i,\text{a}}^{(1)}\penalty\ ,\penalty\ T_{i,-}=T_{i}^{(0)}+T_{i,\text{s}}^{(1)}\penalty\ \penalty\ (i=2,3)\penalty\ .

(38)

The subscript
\pm
indicates that the function is even or odd under
\nu\rightarrow-\nu
.

Eq.(
38
) indicates that along with the isoscalar EM current also its isovector part contributes to the energy-independent nuclear box diagram in Eq.(
33
) through
T_{3,\text{s}}^{(1)}
. The latter is intrinsically a many-body effect: Consider, for example, the two currents
J_{\mathrm{em}}^{(1)\mu}
and
(J^{\nu})_{A}
acting on the same nucleon, then the symmetrized current product in Eq.(
36
) gives zero because
\{\tau_{-},\tau_{3}\}=0
; for the same reason it is also obvious that
T_{3,-}^{(1)}
cannot exist in the asymptotic regime, where the two currents probe a single quark. The same happens for
I=1/2
nuclei as the latter can be (roughly) viewed as a single active nucleon on top of an
I=0
inert core. On the other hand, an
I=1
nucleus can be viewed as two active nucleons on top of an inert core, so
\tau_{-}
and
\tau_{3}
can act on different nucleons (i.e. the third diagram in Fig.
1
); in this case they commute, instead of anti-commute, and give a non-zero contribution to
T_{3,-}^{(1)}
. Also, since the latter originates from the
I=2
combination of the current product, it cannot be described by a single Regge exchange in the
t
-channel (unlike
T_{3}^{(0)}
and
T_{3,+}^{(1)}
) as there is no observed
I=2
meson. Instead, it manifests itself as local counterterms in a low-energy effective field theory.
The role of
J_{\text{em}}^{(1)\mu}
in
\delta_{\text{NS}}
was discussed in Ref.
Towner 1992
, but was somehow forgotten or at least not explicitly articulated in later works.

V
Dispersive representation of the nuclear box diagram

Although Eqs.(
26
), (
28
) (and its simplified version at
E_{e}=0
, Eq.(
33
)) may already serve as a starting point, a dispersive representation of
\Box_{\gamma W}(E_{e})
will prove to be very useful. The structure functions
F_{i}
defined in Eqs.(
21
), (
22
) that appear in the dispersive integral may either be inferred from experimental data, or related to nuclear response functions that are standard objects of study with ab-initio methods.

Ref.
Gorchtein 2019
provided the first DR expression of the energy-dependent nuclear
\gamma W
-box diagram. There are a few aspects which are further improved in this work:

1.

Ref.
Gorchtein 2019
considered a forward
\bar{\nu}(p_{e})+\phi_{i}(p)\rightarrow e^{+}(p_{e})+\phi_{f}(p^{\prime})
scattering process. The relation to the actual
\beta
decay
\phi_{i}(p)\rightarrow\phi_{f}(p^{\prime})+e^{+}(p_{e})+\nu(p_{\nu})
is not immediately straightforward. In this paper we provide a derivation directly in the
\beta
decay kinematics, with no additional approximation except from the two discussed in Sec.
III
.

2.

Ref.
Gorchtein 2019
only addressed the energy dependence due to the
T_{2,3,+}
and
T_{1,-}
components, which are the only ones surviving in the picture of one active nucleon, relevant for the plane-wave Born approximation used in that Ref. Here we provide a complete formalism.

3.

We fix some typos in Ref.
Gorchtein 2019
, e.g. the sign of the (small)
T_{1,2}
contributions, and the incorrect use of the active nucleon number, instead of
f_{+}(0)
, as the normalization.

We outline the derivation of the DR of
\Box_{\gamma W}^{b}
first. Since
T_{A}^{\mu\nu}(p,q)
has no poles, it must remain regular for
\nu\rightarrow 0
, hence their dispersion representation reads,

\displaystyle iT_{3,-}(\nu,Q^{2})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle 4\nu\int_{\nu_{\text{thr}}}^{\infty}d\nu^{\prime}\frac{F_{3,-}(\nu^{\prime},Q^{2})}{\nu^{\prime 2}-\nu^{2}}

\displaystyle iT_{3,+}(\nu,Q^{2})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle 4\nu^{2}\int_{\nu_{\text{thr}}}^{\infty}d\nu^{\prime}\frac{F_{3,+}(\nu^{\prime},Q^{2})}{\nu^{\prime}(\nu^{\prime 2}-\nu^{2})}\penalty\ ,

(39)

where the full structure function
F_{3}
is split as
F_{3}=F_{3,-}+F_{3,+}
, in complete analogy to
T_{3}
. The lower integration limit is the minimum value of
\nu^{\prime}
such that
F_{3}(\nu^{\prime},Q^{2})
is non-zero. For instance, for the one-nucleon knockout one has
\nu_{\text{thr}}=Q^{2}/(2M)+\epsilon
, with
\epsilon
the removal energy.
Using this, we may rewrite Eq.(
26
) as:

\displaystyle\Box_{\gamma W}^{b}(E_{e})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-\frac{4ie^{2}}{Mf_{+}(0)}\int\frac{d^{4}q}{(2\pi)^{4}}\frac{M_{W}^{2}}{M_{W}^{2}+Q^{2}}\frac{Q^{2}+M\nu\left(\frac{\nu}{M}-\frac{\vec{p}_{e}\cdot\vec{q}}{ME_{e}}\right)}{Q^{2}[Q^{2}+2E_{e}\nu-2\vec{p}_{e}\cdot\vec{q}-i\varepsilon]}

(40)

\displaystyle\times\int_{\nu_{\text{thr}}}^{\infty}d\nu^{\prime}\left[\frac{F_{3,-}(\nu^{\prime},Q^{2})}{\nu^{\prime 2}-\nu^{2}}+\frac{\nu F_{3,+}(\nu^{\prime},Q^{2})}{\nu^{\prime}(\nu^{\prime 2}-\nu^{2})}\right]\penalty\ .

Therefore,

where

. One finds that the even piece is associated to
F_{3,-}
and the odd piece to

\displaystyle\mathfrak{Re}\Box_{\gamma W}^{b,\text{even}}(E_{e})

\displaystyle=

VI
Strategy to study
\delta_{\text{NS}}

The dispersive representation provides a useful platform to contrast the free-nucleon and nuclear
\gamma W
-box. To proceed further, we introduce the standard Bjorken variable:

Nachtmann 1973

Nachtmann 1974

M_{W}^{2}/(M_{W}^{2}+Q^{2})
was removed because the physics at
Q^{2}\sim M_{W}^{2}
is not probed. As is well-known, the asymptotic contribution to
\Box_{\gamma W}
is process-independent and cancels between
M_{3,-}^{\text{nucl}}
and
M_{3,-}^{n}
. Plugging this into Eq.(
32
) gives us a closed expression for
\delta_{\text{NS}}
. Below we discuss some aspects important for evaluating it.

Relevant region of the
Q^{2}
-integral
:

While the integral in Eq.(
52
) is insensitive to asymptotically high
Q^{2}
, we need to find out, starting from which value of
Q^{2}=Q^{2}_{\text{nucl}}
the cancellation between the nuclear and nucleon boxes is at such a level that a precise enough determination of
\delta_{\text{NS}}
can already be obtained with
Q^{2}_{\text{nucl}}
as an upper limit.
The first Nachtmann moment for a free nucleon,
M_{3,-}^{n}(1,Q^{2})
, has been studied recently as a function of
Q^{2}
using phenomenological
Seng et al. 2018
;
Seng et al. 2019
;
Hayen 2021
;
Shiells et al. 2021
and indirect lattice inputs
Seng et al. 2020a
.
It was found that by
Q^{2}\approx 2
GeV
2
the perturbative description sets in, and we can expect that

Q^{2}_{\text{nucl}}<2
GeV
2
.
A trial calculation of
M_{3,+}^{\text{nucl}}(1,Q^{2})
at
Q\sim 100-300
MeV may already provide a useful hint. As evidenced by the entries in Table
1
, even a
\sim 10
% determination of
M_{3,+}^{\text{nucl}}(1,Q^{2})
will significantly improve the precision of
\delta_{\text{NS}}
for most nuclei.

The energy-dependent corrections
:

The second line in Eq.(
52
) describes the leading
E_{e}
-dependence in
\delta_{\text{NS}}
associated with the structure functions
F_{3,+}^{\text{nucl}}
,
F_{1,+}^{\text{nucl}}
and
F_{2,-}^{\text{nucl}}
. The parity-even structure functions are analogous to
F_{1,2}^{\text{em}}
in ordinary Compton scattering that involve only the EM current and satisfy the Baldin sum rule
Baldin 1960
, which relate their moments to the nuclear electric dipole polarizability
\alpha_{E}
. With a typical estimation of the latter
Berman and Fultz 1975
, Ref.
Gorchtein 2019
deduced that the contribution of
F_{1,2}
to
\delta_{\text{NS}}
is of the order
10^{-5}
and could be neglected given our precision goal. One arrives at the same conclusion in the free Fermi gas model: the contribution from
F_{3}
is found to be an order of magnitude larger than that from
F_{1,2}
, in part due to the large nucleon isovector magnetic moment. Therefore, from now on we will focus on the parity-odd structure functions
F_{3,\pm}
.

Possible theoretical approaches
:

The primary objects of interest are now the nuclear Nachtmann moments
M_{3}^{\text{nucl}}(N,Q^{2})
which can be studied in different ways. The more straightforward approach is to compute
F_{3,\pm}(x_{B},Q^{2})
directly with ab-initio methods, from which the moments can be evaluated. One of the main challenges is to deal with the sum over all intermediate states in Eq.(
19
). This can either be done directly, e.g. using the short-time approximation for light nuclei
Pastore et al. 2020
, or indirectly such as using the Lorentz integral transform method
Efros et al. 1994
.

Alternatively, one could compute the amplitudes
T_{3,\pm}
, rather than their discontinuities
F_{3,\pm}
for
\nu>\nu_{\text{thr}}
. At low energy
|\nu|<\nu_{\text{thr}}
the invariant amplitudes permit a generic low-energy expansion of the form

\displaystyle iT_{3,-}(\nu,Q^{2})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle 4\sum_{N=0}^{\infty}\left(\frac{2M\nu}{Q^{2}}\right)^{2N+1}\tilde{M}_{3,-}(2N+1,Q^{2})\penalty\ \penalty\ ,\penalty\ \penalty\ |\nu|<\nu_{\text{thr}}

\displaystyle iT_{3,+}(\nu,Q^{2})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle 4\sum_{N=0}^{\infty}\left(\frac{2M\nu}{Q^{2}}\right)^{2N+2}\tilde{M}_{3,+}(2N+2,Q^{2})\penalty\ \penalty\ ,\penalty\ \penalty\ |\nu|<\nu_{\text{thr}}\penalty\ ,

(53)

with the “polarizabilities” expressed as
Q^{2}
-weighted Mellin moments of
F_{3}
. Note that negative powers of
Q^{2}
are cancelled by higher powers of
Q^{2}
implicitly contained in the Mellin moments.
Ref.
Gorchtein and Seng 2021
demonstrates, for the case of a single nucleon, how one could accurately reconstruct the desired Nachtmann moment using a few lowest Mellin moments. It is worthwhile exploring the convergence pattern for the case of nuclei.

VII
Isospin rotation

All the discussions above are formulated in terms of off-diagonal nuclear matrix elements of products of isospin currents. This may cause inconveniences in practical calculations:

1.

Although we have assumed exact isospin symmetry of the system, in practice ab-initio methods usually have built-in ISB effects. This may lead to mismatch between the parameters of the initial and final states, e.g. nuclear masses and excitation energies. This could be avoided if the external states were diagonal.

2.

Many existing programs of ab-initio methods were developed to study observables in electromagnetic or weak neutral current processes that involve physical, instead of isospin, nuclear current operators.

To trade off-diagonal nuclear matrix elements of isospin currents for diagonal nuclear matrix elements of physical EW currents we invoke isospin rotation by making use of the Wigner-Eckart theorem (WET) in the isospin space (see Appendix
B
); in this section we provide the relevant formulas.

First, the isospin rotation formula involving the isoscalar vector current is:

\displaystyle\langle 1,0|V_{00}^{\mu}\otimes A_{1-1}^{\nu}|1,1\rangle

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\langle 1,-1|V_{00}^{\mu}\otimes A_{1-1}^{\nu}|1,0\rangle

(54)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-3\left[\langle 1,1|J_{\mathrm{em}}^{\mu}\otimes(J_{Z}^{\nu})_{A}|1,1\rangle-\langle 1,-1|J_{\mathrm{em}}^{\mu}\otimes(J_{Z}^{\nu})_{A}|1,-1\rangle\right]\penalty\ .

Meanwhile, the formula involving the isovector vector current are:

\displaystyle\langle 1,-1|V_{10}^{\mu}\otimes A_{1-1}^{\nu}|1,0\rangle

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle 2\left[\langle 1,0|(J_{W}^{\mu})_{V}\otimes(J_{W}^{\dagger\nu})_{A}|1,0\rangle-\langle 1,-1|(J_{W}^{\mu})_{V}\otimes(J_{W}^{\dagger\nu})_{A}|1,-1\rangle\right]

\displaystyle\langle 1,0|V_{10}^{\mu}\otimes A_{1-1}^{\nu}|1,1\rangle

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle 2\left[\langle 1,1|(J_{W}^{\mu})_{V}\otimes(J_{W}^{\dagger\nu})_{A}|1,1\rangle-\langle 1,0|(J_{W}^{\mu})_{V}\otimes(J_{W}^{\dagger\nu})_{A}|1,0\rangle\right]

(55)

and

\displaystyle\langle 1,-1|V_{1-1}^{\mu}\otimes A_{10}^{\nu}|1,0\rangle

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle 2\left[\langle 1,0|(J_{W}^{\dagger\mu})_{V}\otimes(J_{W}^{\nu})_{A}|1,0\rangle-\langle 1,-1|(J_{W}^{\dagger\mu})_{V}\otimes(J_{W}^{\nu})_{A}|1,-1\rangle\right]

\displaystyle\langle 1,0|V_{1-1}^{\mu}\otimes A_{10}^{\nu}|1,1\rangle

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle 2\left[\langle 1,1|(J_{W}^{\dagger\mu})_{V}\otimes(J_{W}^{\nu})_{A}|1,1\rangle-\langle 1,0|(J_{W}^{\dagger\mu})_{V}\otimes(J_{W}^{\nu})_{A}|1,0\rangle\right]

(56)

from which the combinations
T_{3,\pm}^{(1)}
and
F_{3,\pm}^{(1)}
can be constructed.

Isospin rotation brings not only practical but also conceptual benefits.
To illustrate, here we will derive the well-known asymptotic (i.e. large-
Q^{2}
) contribution to the energy-independent axial box diagram. With its original definition, it is easy to show using the operator-product-expansion of
T\{J_{\text{em}}^{\mu}(x)(J_{W}^{\dagger\nu}(0))_{A}\}
that the asymptotic contribution gives a process-independent large EW logarithm
Sirlin 1982
:

\Box_{\gamma W}(0)=\frac{\alpha}{8\pi}\ln\frac{M_{W}^{2}}{\Lambda^{2}}+...

(57)

where
\Lambda
is a ultraviolet scale, above which QCD becomes asymptotically-free.
Here we offer an alternative derivation using the dispersive representation. We start from:

\displaystyle\mathfrak{Re}\Box_{\gamma W}(0)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{\alpha}{\pi}\int_{0}^{\infty}dQ^{2}\frac{M_{W}^{2}}{M_{W}^{2}+Q^{2}}\int_{\nu_{\text{thr}}}^{\infty}\frac{d\nu^{\prime}}{\nu^{\prime}}\frac{\nu^{\prime}+2\sqrt{\nu^{\prime 2}+Q^{2}}}{(\nu^{\prime}+\sqrt{\nu^{\prime 2}+Q^{2}})^{2}}\frac{F_{3,-}(\nu^{\prime},Q^{2})}{Mf_{+}(0)}

(58)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{3\alpha}{2\pi}\int_{\Lambda^{2}}^{\infty}\frac{dQ^{2}}{Q^{2}}\frac{M_{W}^{2}}{M_{W}^{2}+Q^{2}}\frac{1}{f_{+}(0)}\int_{0}^{1}dx_{B}F_{3}^{(0)}(x_{B})+...\penalty\ .

In the
Q^{2}>\Lambda^{2}
region the first Nachtmann moment reduces to the first Mellin moment, and only the “(0)” component in
F_{3,-}
survives as we
discussed in Sec.
IV
.

We wish to evaluate the
x_{B}
-integral using parton picture, but the fact that
F_{3}^{(0)}
is off-diagonal makes this step less straightforward, so now isospin rotation becomes very useful. For superallowed decays of
I=1
systems, we use the isospin relation in Eq.(
54
) to get:

F_{3}^{(0)}=\frac{1}{2\sqrt{2}}\left[F_{3,+1}^{\gamma Z}-F_{3,-1}^{\gamma Z}\right]\penalty\ .

(59)

Here
F_{3,\pm 1}^{\gamma Z}
are defined exactly like
F_{3}
in Eq.(
22
), except that we replace
(J^{\nu})_{A}
in
W_{A}^{\mu\nu}
by
(J_{Z}^{\nu})_{A}
, and the off-diagonal external states by diagonal states with
\{I,m_{I}\}=\{1,\pm 1\}
respectively.
At large
Q^{2}
, they could be expressed in terms of the quark distribution functions (see Ref.
Zyla et al. 2020
, but beware of the difference in overall normalization):

F_{3}^{\gamma Z}(x_{B})=\frac{1}{3}\left(u(x_{B})-\bar{u}(x_{B})\right)+\frac{1}{6}\left(d(x_{B})-\bar{d}(x_{B})\right).

(60)

A nucleus with
Z
protons and
N
neutrons thus satisfy:

\int_{0}^{1}dx_{B}F_{3}^{\gamma Z}(x_{B})=\frac{1}{3}(2Z+N)+\frac{1}{6}(Z+2N)=\frac{5}{6}Z+\frac{2}{3}N\penalty\ .

(61)

Recall that for the
m_{I}=\pm 1
state we have
Z=A/2\pm 1
and
N=A/2\mp 1
. Therefore:

\int_{0}^{1}dx_{B}F_{3}^{(0)}(x_{B})=\frac{1}{2\sqrt{2}}\int_{0}^{1}dx_{B}\left[F_{3,+1}^{\gamma Z}(x_{B})-F_{3,-1}^{\gamma Z}(x_{B})\right]=\frac{\sqrt{2}}{12}\penalty\ .

(62)

Finally, we recall that
f_{+}(0)=\sqrt{2}
for
I=1
isomultiplets, so plugging Eq.(
62
) into Eq.(
58
) and performing the
Q^{2}
-integral reproduces the large EW logarithm in Eq.(
57
).

The derivation above also highlights a potential caveat in applying the isospin relations: for
A\gg 1
, Eq.(
62
) involves a subtraction between two large numbers
\sim A
to get a number of order 1. In practical nuclear calculations, each of these two large numbers contains some ISB effects which, upon subtraction, may have an unnaturally large effect. We expect this effect to be less problematic for light nuclei.

VIII
Employing nuclear physics notations

The entire theory framework of box diagram amplitudes and the DR formalism outlined above are inherited from previous studies of single-nucleon EWRC, and thus are based on particle physics notations. Here we re-express them in terms of notations that are more familiar to the nuclear physics community.

In this section we introduce a somewhat more general notation (we did not do it at the beginning to avoid losing focus on the actual physical problem, i.e.
\beta^{+}
-decay, that we are interested in, when we discussed the basic framework). We take
J_{a}^{\mu}
as an arbitrary vector current and
J_{b}^{\mu}
as an arbitrary axial current, and define:

\displaystyle T^{\mu\nu}_{ab}(p,q)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}\int d^{4}xe^{iq\cdot x}\langle\phi_{f}(p)|T\{J_{a}^{\mu}(x)J_{b}^{\nu}(0)\}|\phi_{i}(p)\rangle=\frac{i\epsilon^{\mu\nu\alpha\beta}p_{\alpha}q_{\beta}}{2M\nu}T_{3}^{ab}(\nu,Q^{2})

\displaystyle W^{\mu\nu}_{ab}(p,q)

\displaystyle\equiv

\displaystyle\frac{1}{8\pi}\int d^{4}xe^{iq\cdot x}\langle\phi_{f}(p)|[J_{a}^{\mu}(x),J_{b}^{\nu}(0)]|\phi_{i}(p)=\frac{i\epsilon^{\mu\nu\alpha\beta}p_{\alpha}q_{\beta}}{2M\nu}F_{3}^{ab}(\nu,Q^{2})\penalty\ ,

(63)

where
\phi_{i,f}
, are spinless nuclear states (diagonal or off-diagonal) with degenerate mass
M
. Taking
J_{a}^{\mu}\rightarrow J_{\text{em}}^{\mu}
and
J_{b}^{\mu}\rightarrow(J_{W}^{\dagger\mu})_{A}
recovers the
T_{A}^{\mu\nu}
and
W_{A}^{\mu\nu}
relevant to
\beta^{+}
-decay, but we may also replace them by isospin currents or other physical EW currents to implement our previous discussions of crossing symmetry and isospin rotation formula.

VIII.1
Connection to nuclear Green’s function and nuclear response function

We will work in the target’s rest frame, i.e.
p_{0}=M
and
\nu=q_{0}
, and align
\vec{q}=\mathbf{q}\hat{e}_{z}
. Using the definition of the time-ordered product,

T[A(t)B(0)]=\Theta(t)A(t)B(0)+\Theta(-t)B(0)A(t)
, and translational symmetry,

A(x)=e^{iP\cdot x}A(0)e^{-iP\cdot x}
,
we can insert a complete set of states between the two currents in the definition of
T_{ab}^{\mu\nu}
, and perform the
t
-integration explicitly, with the result

\displaystyle T^{\mu\nu}_{ab}(p,q)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-\frac{i}{2}\int d^{3}xe^{-i\vec{q}\cdot\vec{x}}\langle\phi_{f}(p)|J_{a}^{\mu}(0,\vec{x})G(M+q_{0}+i\varepsilon)J_{b}^{\nu}(0,\vec{0})|\phi_{i}(p)\rangle

(64)

\displaystyle-\frac{i}{2}\int d^{3}xe^{-i\vec{q}\cdot\vec{x}}\langle\phi_{f}(p)|J_{b}^{\nu}(0,\vec{0})G(M-q_{0}+i\varepsilon)J_{a}^{\mu}(0,\vec{x})|\phi_{i}(p)\rangle\penalty\ .

Above, we introduced the nuclear Green’s function,

G(\omega)\equiv(H_{0}-\omega)^{-1}=\sum_{X}\frac{|X\rangle\langle X|}{E_{X}-\omega}\penalty\ ,

(65)

with
H_{0}
the nuclear Hamiltonian.

The external states in Eq.(
64
) are relativistically-normalized plane waves, and now we shall rewrite them in terms of properly-normalized states in quantum mechanics. Applying the translational symmetry once again leads to

\displaystyle T^{\mu\nu}_{ab}(p,q)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-\frac{i}{2V}\langle\phi_{f}(p)|J_{a}^{\mu}(\vec{q})G(M+q_{0}+i\varepsilon)J_{b}^{\nu}(-\vec{q})|\phi_{i}(p)\rangle

(66)

\displaystyle-\frac{i}{2V}\langle\phi_{f}(p)|J_{b}^{\nu}(-\vec{q})G(M-q_{0}+i\varepsilon)J_{a}^{\mu}(\vec{q})|\phi_{i}(p)\rangle\penalty\ ,

where we have defined the (spatial) Fourier transform of currents as

J^{\mu}(\vec{q})\equiv\int d^{3}xe^{-i\vec{q}\cdot\vec{x}}J^{\mu}(0,\vec{x})\penalty\ ,

(67)

and the volume factor

V\equiv\int d^{3}x\cdot 1=(2\pi)^{3}\delta^{(3)}(\vec{0})\penalty\ ,

(68)

with
\vec{0}
a zero vector in the momentum space.
Recalling that the plane wave states at
\vec{p}=0
are normalized as

\langle\phi(\vec{0})|\phi(\vec{0})\rangle=2M(2\pi)^{3}\delta^{(3)}(\vec{0})=2MV\penalty\ ,

(69)

we define a quantum mechanical state

|\phi\rangle\equiv\frac{1}{\sqrt{2MV}}|\phi(\vec{0})\rangle

(70)

that normalizes as
\langle\phi|\phi\rangle=1
. This allows us to write

\displaystyle T^{\mu\nu}_{ab}(p,q)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-iM\langle\phi_{f}|J_{a}^{\mu}(\vec{q})G(M+q_{0}+i\varepsilon)J_{b}^{\nu}(-\vec{q})|\phi_{i}\rangle

(71)

\displaystyle-iM\langle\phi_{f}|J_{b}^{\nu}(-\vec{q})G(M-q_{0}+i\varepsilon)J_{a}^{\mu}(\vec{q})|\phi_{i}\rangle\penalty\ .

One sees that the volume factor
V
drops out, and everything on the right-hand side is rigorously defined in quantum mechanics and is calculable in nuclear theory.
Similarly, one may express the hadronic tensor
W^{\mu\nu}_{ab}
in the way more familiar in nuclear calculations,

W^{\mu\nu}_{ab}(p,q)=\frac{M}{2}\sum_{X}\delta(q_{0}+M-E_{X})\langle\phi_{f}|J_{a}^{\mu}(\vec{q})|X\rangle\langle X|J_{b}^{\nu}(-\vec{q})|\phi_{i}\rangle\penalty\ ,

(72)

and the parity-odd structure function reads:

F_{3}^{ab}(\nu,Q^{2})=-\frac{iq_{0}}{\mathbf{q}}\left(W_{ab}^{12}(p,q)-W_{ab}^{21}(p,q)\right)\penalty\ ,

(73)

in complete analogy to the response function
R_{T^{\prime}}
in neutrino-nucleus scattering
Shen et al. 2012
;
Lovato et al. 2014
;
Lovato et al. 2018
;
Rocco 2020
.

A comment is in order. In studying the invariant amplitudes we encounter nuclear matrix elements of the form
\langle\phi_{f}|\hat{O}_{1}G(\omega)\hat{O}_{2}|\phi_{i}\rangle
, where
\hat{O}_{1,2}
are arbitrary operators. This is a classic problem in nuclear many-body calculations, and is challenging due to the difficulty to invert a large matrix
H_{0}-\omega
; fortunately, there are ways to circumvent the problem, e.g. using the Lanczos algorithm
Lanczos 1950
;
Haydock 1974
;
Marchisio et al. 2003
. Interestingly enough, the same form of nuclear matrix elements also appear in a newly-proposed strategy to compute the ISB correction
\delta_{\text{C}}

Seng and Gorchtein 2022
.
The fact that
\delta_{\text{NS}}
and
\delta_{\text{C}}
share the similar form nuclear matrix elements indicates that they could be studied simultaneously given a specific type of an ab-initio method.

VIII.2
Multipole expansion of invariant amplitudes and structure functions

The multipole expansion of nuclear EW currents is a powerful technique frequently adopted in ab-initio studies
Gazda et al. 2017
;
Acharya and Bacca 2020
;
Glick-Magid et al. 2021
. Combining with WET, it converts the problem of calculating the full current matrix elements into the calculation of reduced matrix elements which is more tractable. Here we shall implement the formalism to both the invariant amplitude and structure function for the convenience of interested readers; full details are available in Appendix
E
.

We start with the invariant amplitude
T_{3}^{ab}
. Analogous to Eq.(
73
), we cast it in an explicitly antisymmetric form:

T_{3}^{ab}(\nu,Q^{2})=-\frac{iq_{0}}{\mathbf{q}}\left(T_{ab}^{12}(p,q)-T_{ab}^{21}(p,q)\right)\penalty\ .

(74)

Since all currents are transverse (along
\hat{e}_{x}
and
\hat{e}_{y}
directions),
we may express them in terms of the
\lambda=\pm 1
components defined in Eq.(
141
):

\displaystyle T_{3}^{ab}(\nu,Q^{2})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{iq_{0}}{\mathbf{q}}M\langle\phi_{f}|\bigl\{J_{a}(\vec{q},+1)G(M+q_{0}+i\varepsilon)J_{b}(-\vec{q},-1)+

(75)

\displaystyle J_{b}(-\vec{q},-1)G(M-q_{0}+i\varepsilon)J_{a}(\vec{q},+1)-(\lambda=+1\leftrightarrow\lambda=-1)\bigr\}|\phi_{i}\rangle\penalty\ .

The multipole expansion of both currents reads

\displaystyle J_{a/b}(\vec{q},\pm 1)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-\sqrt{2\pi}\sum_{J=1}^{\infty}(-i)^{J}\sqrt{2J+1}\left(\pm T_{J\pm 1}^{a/b,\mathrm{mag}}(\mathbf{q})-T_{J\pm 1}^{a/b,\mathrm{el}}(\mathbf{q})\right)

\displaystyle J_{a/b}(-\vec{q},\pm 1)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-\sqrt{2\pi}\sum_{J^{\prime}=1}^{\infty}i^{J^{\prime}}\sqrt{2J^{\prime}+1}\left(\pm T_{J^{\prime}\pm 1}^{a/b,\mathrm{mag}}(\mathbf{q})+T_{J^{\prime}\pm 1}^{a/b,\mathrm{el}}(\mathbf{q})\right)\penalty\ ,

(76)

with the transverse electric and magnetic multipole operators defined in Eq.(
148
).

When substituting Eq.(
76
) into Eq.(
75
),

J^{\prime}=J
is required to conserve total angular momentum since
\phi_{i,f}
are spinless, and parity conservation further requires that only the combinations
(a,\mathrm{el})\otimes(b,\mathrm{mag})
and
(a,\mathrm{mag})\otimes(b,\mathrm{el})
survive (see the discussion below Eq.(
161
)). Since the multipole operators are irreducible tensors of the rotational group, we may apply WET,

\langle 0,0|T_{J+1}\otimes T_{J-1}^{\prime}|0,0\rangle=\langle 0,0|T_{J-1}\otimes T_{J+1}^{\prime}|0,0\rangle=-\langle 0,0|T_{J0}\otimes T_{J0}^{\prime}|0,0\rangle

(77)

to convert the
m_{J}
in all the multipole operators to 0. This leads to the following expression,

\displaystyle iT_{3}^{ab}(\nu,Q^{2})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle 4\pi\frac{q_{0}}{\mathbf{q}}M\sum_{J=1}^{\infty}(2J+1)\langle\phi_{f}|\bigl\{T_{J0}^{a,\mathrm{mag}}(\mathbf{q})G(M+q_{0}+i\varepsilon)T_{J0}^{b,\mathrm{el}}(\mathbf{q})

(78)

\displaystyle+T_{J0}^{a,\mathrm{el}}(\mathbf{q})G(M+q_{0}+i\varepsilon)T_{J0}^{b,\mathrm{mag}}(\mathbf{q})+T_{J0}^{b,\mathrm{mag}}(\mathbf{q})G(M-q_{0}+i\varepsilon)T_{J0}^{a,\mathrm{el}}(\mathbf{q})

\displaystyle+T_{J0}^{b,\mathrm{el}}(\mathbf{q})G(M-q_{0}+i\varepsilon)T_{J0}^{a,\mathrm{mag}}(\mathbf{q})\bigr\}|\phi_{i}\rangle\penalty\ ,

which is a useful starting point for ab-initio calculations of
T_{3}^{ab}(\nu,Q^{2})
. One may similarly derive the multipole expansion formula for
F_{3}^{ab}(\nu,Q^{2})
:

\displaystyle F_{3}^{ab}(\nu,Q^{2})

\displaystyle=2\pi\frac{q_{0}}{\mathbf{q}}M\sum_{J=1}^{\infty}(2J+1)\sum_{X}\delta(q_{0}+M-E_{X})

(79)

\displaystyle\times\big\{\langle\phi_{f}|T_{J0}^{a,\mathrm{mag}}(\mathbf{q})|X\rangle\langle X|T_{J0}^{b,\mathrm{el}}(\mathbf{q})|\phi_{i}\rangle+\langle\phi_{f}|T_{J0}^{a,\mathrm{el}}(\mathbf{q})|X\rangle\langle X|T_{J0}^{b,\mathrm{mag}}(\mathbf{q})|\phi_{i}\rangle\big\}.

IX
Summary

Unlike the ISB correction
\delta_{\text{C}}
which was studied since the late 1950s
MacDonald 1958
, the nuclear structure-dependent part of the RC in superallowed
\beta
decays only received serious attention about 30 years later
Jaus and Rasche 1990
. The quantity
\delta_{\text{NS}}
originates from the difference between the “inner” correction on a nucleus and on a free nucleon; however, historically the two were computed in completely unrelated paradigms. The nucleon
\gamma W
-box diagram was treated in a fully relativistic framework, while
\delta_{\text{NS}}
was computed with non-relativistic nuclear models. This artificial dissection may introduce additional model dependence; indeed, when the dispersive representation of the nuclear
\gamma W
-box diagram was first introduced in Refs.
Seng et al. 2019
;
Gorchtein 2019
, it became clear that the previous treatments did not properly include some the important nuclear structure corrections, which now results in an inflated theory uncertainty in
|V_{ud}|_{0^{+}}
. As the precision of the
V_{ud}
determination from other systems (especially from the free neutron decay) is gradually catching up, it is timely to perform a thorough re-analysis of
\delta_{\text{NS}}
with modern techniques.

In this article we present the theoretical framework to compute
\delta_{\text{NS}}
in full consistency with the existing treatment of the nucleus-independent RC
\Delta_{R}^{V}
. We start with the classical Sirlin representation that allows us to rigorously separate the structure-dependent inner correction from the full
\mathcal{O}(\alpha)
electroweak RC to the Fermi matrix element; the former is determined by the
E_{e}
-dependent nuclear
\gamma W
-box diagram. With this, one could unambiguously define
\delta_{\text{NS}}
as the difference between the nuclear and single-nucleon
\gamma W
-box diagram, averaged over the electron energy spectrum of the nuclear
\beta
decay.

The
\gamma W
-box diagram is given by the
q
-integral over the spin-independent invariant amplitudes
T_{i}(\nu,Q^{2})
(
i=1,2,3
) (Eqs.(
26
), (
28
)); the contribution from the parity-odd amplitude
T_{3}(\nu,Q^{2})
(i.e. the “axial” box diagram
\Box_{\gamma W}^{b}(E_{e})
) is dominant and is the main focus of this paper. The
E_{e}
-independent and leading
E_{e}
-dependent piece are associated with the odd and even crossing components
T_{3,\pm}
, respectively.
T_{3,+}
solely contains the isovector EM current, while
T_{3,-}
is more complicated. For pion and free neutron
T_{3,-}
only receives contribution from the isoscalar EM current, but for
I=1
nuclei the isovector EM current contributes through multi-nucleon interactions, as well (see Section
IV
).

Through a DR, we expressed
T_{3}
(and hence
\Box_{\gamma W}^{b}
) in terms of the structure function
F_{3}
. The dispersive representation of the nuclear box diagram is then represented in terms of the first and second Nachtmann moment of
F_{3}
(Eq.(
51
)). For the case of free nucleon where only the energy-independent part is relevant, the first Nachtmann moment
M_{3,-}^{n}(1,Q^{2})
is known to good precision using neutrino scattering data or indirect lattice inputs. Given that
M_{3,-}^{\text{nucl}}(1,Q^{2})
continues smoothly to
M_{3,-}^{n}(1,Q^{2})
at the asymptotic regime, the energy-independent contribution to
\delta_{\text{NS}}
thus comes from the difference
M_{3,-}^{\text{nucl}}(1,Q^{2})-M_{3,-}^{n}(1,Q^{2})
at low-
Q^{2}
, while the leading energy-dependent contribution comes from

M_{3,+}^{\text{nucl}}(2,Q^{2})
(Eq.(
52
)); both quantities may be computed with ab-initio methods. To facilitate such calculations, we provide a number of isospin rotation formulas (see Section
VII
) that relate various charge-changing nuclear matrix elements of isospin currents to diagonal nuclear matrix elements with physical electroweak currents, and also alert the readers to possible caveats in using these relations. Finally, we introduce standard nuclear physics notations that relate the relativistic invariant amplitudes and structure functions to the non-relativistic nuclear matrix elements of the nuclear Green’s function
G(\omega)=(H_{0}-\omega)^{-1}
(Eqs.(
71
), (
72
)).

Depending on the mass number
A
, different ab-initio methods may be applied for the calculation of
\delta_{\text{NS}}
. For instance, for light nuclei (
A\sim 10
) Green’s Function Monte Carlo
Carlson et al. 2015
;
Gandolfi et al. 2020
and no-core shell model
Barrett et al. 2013
are viable. For medium-size nuclei (
A\lesssim 40

Hagen et al. 2014
and nuclear lattice effective field theory
Lee et al. 2004
;
Borasoy et al. 2007
;
Lee 2009
;
Lähde and Meißner 2019
should be applicable. The same methods may also be used to study
\delta_{\text{C}}
through the newly-proposed strategy
Seng and Gorchtein 2022

V_{ud}
with controlled theory uncertainty, and shed new light on the Cabibbo angle anomaly and other low-energy precision tests of the Standard Model.

Appendix A
Superallowed
\beta
decay at tree level

In this Appendix we derive the tree-level partial width of superallowed nuclear
\beta
decays in a fully relativistic notation.
Since the kinematics are the same as that in the semileptonic kaon decay (
K_{\ell 3}
), all the exact formula in that process are directly applicable here
Seng et al. 2020b
;
Seng et al. 2021a
;
Seng et al. 2021b
;
Seng et al. 2022b
. The relativistic formalism makes some of the higher-order corrections, e.g. recoil corrections
Holstein 1974
, more transparent.

After summing up the lepton spins, the absolute square of the tree-level decay amplitude (see Eq.(
13
)) is given by:

|\mathfrak{M}_{0}|^{2}(y,z)=G_{F}^{2}|V_{ud}|^{2}\left\{f_{+}^{2}H(+1,+1)+2f_{+}f_{-}H(+1,-1)+f_{-}^{2}H(-1,-1)\right\}

(80)

where

\displaystyle H(+1,+1)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle 2M_{i}^{4}\left[4(1-y)(y+z-1)-4r_{f}+r_{e}(r_{f}+4y+3z-3)-r_{e}^{2}\right]

\displaystyle H(+1,-1)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle 2M_{i}^{4}r_{e}\left[3+r_{e}-r_{f}-2y-z\right]

\displaystyle H(-1,-1)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle 2M_{i}^{4}r_{e}\left[1-z+r_{f}-r_{e}\right]\penalty\ .

(81)

Here we have defined

y=\frac{2p\cdot p_{e}}{M_{i}^{2}}=\frac{2E_{e}}{M_{i}}\penalty\ ,\penalty\ z=\frac{2p\cdot p^{\prime}}{M_{i}^{2}}=2\frac{E_{f}}{M_{i}}\penalty\ ,\penalty\ r_{f}=\frac{M_{f}^{2}}{M_{i}^{2}}\penalty\ ,\penalty\ r_{e}=\frac{m_{e}^{2}}{M_{i}^{2}}\penalty\ .

(82)

The tree-level decay rate is given by:

\Gamma_{0}=\frac{M_{i}}{256\pi^{3}}\int_{\mathcal{D}_{3}}dydz|\mathfrak{M}_{0}|^{2}(y,z)\penalty\ ,

(83)

where the exact three-body phase space
\mathcal{D}_{3}
is given by

a(y)-b(y)<z<a(y)+b(y)\penalty\ ,\penalty\ 2\sqrt{r_{e}}<y<1+r_{e}-r_{f}\penalty\ ,

(84)

with

a(y)=\frac{(2-y)(1+r_{f}+r_{e}-y)}{2(1+r_{e}-y)}\penalty\ ,\penalty\ b(y)=\frac{\sqrt{y^{2}-4r_{e}}(1+r_{e}-r_{f}-y)}{2(1+r_{e}-y)}\penalty\ .

(85)

To examine the relative importance of the form factors, let us first approximate
f_{\pm}(t)\rightarrow f_{\pm}(0)
; in this case the
z
-integration can be exactly performed. Below we display both the exact result, and the leading power-expansion according to the following counting:

m_{e}\sim|\vec{p}_{e}|\sim E_{e}=\mathcal{O}(\Delta)\penalty\ .

(86)

The results are:

\displaystyle\int_{a-b}^{a+b}dzH(+1,+1)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{16M_{i}|\vec{p}_{e}|(E_{m}^{\text{exact}}-E_{e})^{2}(5E_{e}m_{e}^{2}M_{i}-4E_{e}M_{i}^{2}(2E_{e}-M_{i})-m_{e}^{4})}{(M_{i}(M_{i}-2E_{e})+m_{e}^{2})^{2}}

\displaystyle\approx

\displaystyle 64E_{e}|\vec{p}_{e}|(E_{m}-E_{e})^{2}=\mathcal{O}(\Delta^{4})

\displaystyle\int_{a-b}^{a+b}dzH(+1,-1)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{16m_{e}^{2}M_{i}|\vec{p}_{e}|(E_{m}^{\text{exact}}-E_{e})^{2}(M_{i}(2M_{i}-3E_{e})+m_{e}^{2})}{(M_{i}(M_{i}-2E_{e})+m_{e}^{2})^{2}}

\displaystyle\approx

\displaystyle\frac{32m_{e}^{2}|\vec{p}_{e}|(E_{m}-E_{e})^{2}}{M_{i}}=\mathcal{O}(\Delta^{5})

\displaystyle\int_{a-b}^{a+b}dzH(-1,-1)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{16m_{e}^{2}M_{i}|\vec{p}_{e}|(E_{m}^{\text{exact}}-E_{e})^{2}(E_{e}M_{i}-m_{e}^{2})}{(M_{i}(M_{i}-2E_{e})+m_{e}^{2})^{2}}

(87)

\displaystyle\approx

\displaystyle\frac{16E_{e}m_{e}^{2}|\vec{p}_{e}|(E_{m}-E_{e})^{2}}{M_{i}^{2}}=\mathcal{O}(\Delta^{6})\penalty\ ,

where

E_{m}^{\text{exact}}\equiv\frac{M_{i}^{2}-M_{f}^{2}+m_{e}^{2}}{2M_{i}}\penalty\ ,\penalty\ E_{m}\equiv M_{i}-M_{f}

(88)

are the exact and approximate versions of the positron end-point energy, respectively.
We see that the leading order contribution comes from
H(+1,+1)
and furthermore,
f_{-}
is ISB-suppressed. Therefore, for practical purposes it is safe to retain only the
f_{+}^{2}
term.
In the meantime,

\int_{2\sqrt{r_{e}}}^{1+r_{e}-r_{f}}dy=\frac{2}{M_{i}}\int_{m_{e}}^{E_{m}^{\text{exact}}}dE_{e}\approx\frac{2}{M_{i}}\int_{m_{e}}^{E_{m}}dE_{e}\penalty\ .

(89)

So,

\frac{M_{i}}{256\pi^{3}}\int_{\mathcal{D}_{3}}dydz|\mathfrak{M}_{0}|^{2}\approx\frac{1}{2\pi^{3}}G_{F}^{2}|V_{ud}|^{2}f_{+}^{2}(0)\int_{m_{e}}^{E_{m}}dE_{e}|\vec{p}_{e}|E_{e}(E_{m}-E_{e})^{2}\penalty\ .

(90)

The above is very a naïve treatment which misses some essential ingredient that could play significant roles especially for heavy nuclei. The most important one is the Coulomb interaction between the outgoing positron and the daughter nucleus, which is incorporated by including the Fermi function
F(Z_{f},E_{e})

Fermi 1934
. There are also nuclear shape corrections (or, in other words, the
t
-dependence in
f_{+}(t)
) and the influence of the atomic electrons; they give rise to an effective shape correction function
S(Z_{f},E_{e})
and a screening correction factor
Q(Z_{f},E_{e})
in the integral
Hardy and Towner 2005
. Finally, a recoil correction factor
Towner and Hardy 2015
:

R(E_{m})=1-\frac{3E_{m}}{M_{i}+M_{f}}

(91)

takes into account (to the desired level of precision) both the small differences between the exact and approximate result of the
z
-integral (See Eq.(
87
), and between the exact and approximate upper limit of the
E_{e}
-integral.
To conclude, we may write:

|V_{ud}|^{2}\approx\frac{2\pi^{3}m_{e}^{-5}G_{F}^{-2}f_{+}^{-2}(0)\ln 2}{ft}\penalty\ ,

(92)

where
t
is the half life, and

f=m_{e}^{-5}R(E_{m})\int_{m_{e}}^{E_{m}}dE_{e}|\vec{p}_{e}|E_{e}(E_{m}-E_{e})^{2}F(Z_{f},E_{e})S(Z_{f},E_{e})Q(Z_{f},E_{e})

(93)

is the statistical rate function.

Eq.(
92
) is not the end of the story, as there are still several SM corrections at the level
10^{-2}-10^{-4}
that must be included for a precise extraction of
V_{ud}
. They are: (1) the EWRC that are not already included in the Fermi function, and (2) The ISB correction
\delta_{\mathrm{C}}
that mainly originates from the Coulomb interaction between the protons. In particular, the EWRC is usually divided into the nuclear-structure-independent piece
\Delta_{R}^{V}
and the structure-dependent pieces
\delta_{\mathrm{R}}^{\prime}
and
\delta_{\mathrm{NS}}
. Adding all these gives the master formula in Eq.(
1
).

Appendix B
Useful theorems in SU(2)

Many derivations in this paper make use of relations between matrix elements of angular momentum or isospin eigenstates. Since both of them are described by an SU(2) group, it is useful to state here some of the relevant group theory relations for the benefits of unfamiliar readers. In fact we just need two of them:

1.

Product of irreducible tensors
: Suppose
H^{I_{1}}_{m_{I_{1}}}
and
K^{I_{2}}_{m_{I_{2}}}
are irreducible tensors of rank
I_{1}
and
I_{2}
respectively of an SU(2) group, then its product may be written as a sum of irreducible tensors:

H^{I_{1}}_{m_{I_{1}}}\otimes K^{I_{2}}_{m_{I_{2}}}=\sum_{Im_{I}}C_{I_{1}m_{I_{1}};I_{2}m_{I_{2}}}^{I_{1}I_{2};Im_{I}}T^{I}_{m_{I}}\penalty\ ,

(94)

where
C_{I_{1}m_{I_{1}};I_{2}m_{I_{2}}}^{I_{1}I_{2};Im_{I}}
are Clebsch-Gordan (CG)-coefficients.

2.

Wigner-Eckart theorem
: The matrix element of an irreducible tensor with respect to SU(2) eigenstates can be written as:

\langle I_{2},m_{I_{2}}|T^{I}_{m_{I}}|I_{1},m_{I_{1}}\rangle=C_{I_{1}m_{I_{1}};Im_{I}}^{I_{1}I;I_{2}m_{I_{2}}}\langle I_{2}||T^{I}||I_{1}\rangle\penalty\ ,

(95)

where
\langle I_{2}||T^{I}||I_{1}\rangle
is a reduced matrix element that is independent of the “magnetic quantum numbers”
m_{I_{1}}
,
m_{I}
and
m_{I_{2}}
.

Appendix C
Crossing symmetry

In this appendix we provide a detailed derivation of the crossing symmetry of
T_{i}(\nu,Q^{2})
that we presented in Sec.
IV
. The basic idea follows directly the arguments in Appendix A of Ref.
Seng et al. 2019
. We restrict ourselves to
\beta^{+}
-decays.

Our derivation starts from the following identity
Sakurai and Napolitano 2020
:

\langle\beta(p)|\hat{O}|\alpha(p)\rangle=\langle\tilde{\alpha}(\tilde{p})|\mathbb{T}\hat{O}^{\dagger}\mathbb{T}^{-1}|\tilde{\beta}(\tilde{p})\rangle\penalty\ ,

(96)

where
\hat{O}
is a linear operator,
\mathbb{T}
is the time-reversal operator, and
|\tilde{\alpha}\rangle
,
|\tilde{\beta}\rangle
are the time-reversed states of
|\alpha\rangle
,
|\beta\rangle
, with the time-reversed four-momentum
\tilde{p}^{\mu}=p_{\mu}
. Knowing that the time-reversal operation on a four-current reads:

\mathbb{T}J^{\mu}(\vec{x},t)\mathbb{T}^{-1}=J_{\mu}(\vec{x},-t)\penalty\ ,

(97)

then suppose a generic tensor of time-ordered current product is defined as:

\displaystyle T^{\mu\nu}(p,q)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}\int d^{4}xe^{iq\cdot x}\langle\phi_{f}(p)|T\{J_{a}^{\mu}(x)J_{b}^{\nu}(0)\}|\phi_{i}(p)\rangle

(98)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\left(-g^{\mu\nu}+\frac{q^{\mu}q^{\nu}}{q^{2}}\right)T_{1}(\nu,Q^{2})+\frac{\hat{p}^{\mu}\hat{p}^{\nu}}{M\nu}T_{2}(\nu,Q^{2})+\frac{i\epsilon^{\mu\nu\alpha\beta}p_{\alpha}q_{\beta}}{2M\nu}T_{3}(\nu,Q^{2})\penalty\ ,

using the time-reversal operation and the definition of the time-ordered product, we obtain:

\displaystyle T^{\mu\nu}(p,q)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}\int d^{4}xe^{-i\tilde{q}\cdot x}\langle\phi_{i}(\tilde{p})|T\{J^{a\dagger}_{\mu}(x)J^{b\dagger}_{\nu}(0)\}|\phi_{f}(\tilde{p})\rangle\penalty\ .

(99)

Comparing to the definition of
T_{A}^{\mu\nu}(p,q)
(see Eq.(
16
)), we find that at the right hand side the upper Lorentz indices are lowered, the initial and final states are interchanged, the currents are conjugated, and the momenta are time-reversed, with an extra sign change
\tilde{q}
.

Next, suppose the matrix element in Eq.(
99
) satisfies the following relation:

\langle\phi_{i}|J^{a\dagger}_{\mu}J^{b\dagger}_{\nu}|\phi_{f}\rangle=-\xi\langle\phi_{f}|J^{a}_{\mu}J^{b}_{\nu}|\phi_{i}\rangle\penalty\

(100)

where
\xi=\pm 1
, then we may plug this relation into Eq.(
99
), express the right hand side in terms of invariant amplitudes, and compare it with Eq.(
98
). This gives the following crossing relations:

T_{1}(-\nu,Q^{2})=-\xi T_{1}(\nu,Q^{2})\penalty\ ,\penalty\ T_{2,3}(-\nu,Q^{2})=\xi T_{2,3}(\nu,Q^{2})\penalty\ .

(101)

If both currents are neutral we have trivially
\xi=-1
; in flavor-changing processes the value of
\xi
must be deduced case-by-case in each isospin channel.

We start with the isoscalar EM current.
Using a general property of the CG coefficient,

C_{I,m_{I}-1;1,1}^{I,1;I,m_{I}}=-C_{I,m_{I};1,-1}^{I,1;I,m_{I}-1}\penalty\ ,

(102)

we can easily use WET in the isospin space to show that

\langle I,m_{I}|J_{\mu}^{\mathrm{em(0)}}(x)(J_{\nu}^{W}(0))_{A}|I,m_{I}-1\rangle=\langle I,m_{I}-1|J_{\mu}^{\mathrm{em(0)}}(x)(J_{\nu}^{W\dagger}(0))_{A}|I,m_{I}\rangle\penalty\ ,

(103)

which implies
\xi^{(0)}=-1
,
regardless of the isospin of the external hadronic states.

The isovector component is more complicated and produces different results for pion, nucleon (
I=1/2
systems) and
0^{+}
nuclei (
I=1
systems), which is often taken for granted in literature. Here we will study them carefully.

For the pion decay the isovector matrix element
T_{3}^{(1)}(\nu,Q^{2})
vanishes due to
G
-parity,

\langle\pi^{0}|J_{\mathrm{em}}^{(1)\mu}(J_{W}^{\dagger\nu})_{A}|\pi^{+}\rangle=0\penalty\ ,

(104)

given that

GJ_{\mathrm{em}}^{(1)\mu}G^{-1}=J_{\mathrm{em}}^{(1)\mu}\penalty\ ,\penalty\ G(J_{\nu}^{W\dagger})_{A}G^{-1}=-(J_{\nu}^{W\dagger})_{A}\penalty\ ,\penalty\ G(\pi^{\pm,0})=-\pi^{\pm,0}\penalty\ .

(105)

This argument does not apply for the nucleon and
I=1
nuclei, since they are not eigenstates of
G
-parity. To study crossing symmetry in these systems, we decompose the current product into
I=1
and
I=2
irreducible tensors by (anti)symmetrizing in the isospin indices,

J_{\mathrm{em}}^{(1)\mu}(J_{W}^{\dagger\nu})_{A}=-\frac{1}{2\sqrt{2}}V_{10}^{\mu}A_{1-1}^{\nu}=T^{1}_{-1}+T^{2}_{-1}\penalty\ ,

(106)

where

T^{1}_{-1}=-\frac{1}{4\sqrt{2}}\left(V_{10}^{\mu}A_{1-1}^{\nu}-V_{1-1}^{\mu}A_{10}^{\nu}\right)\penalty\ ,\penalty\ T^{2}_{-1}=-\frac{1}{4\sqrt{2}}\left(V_{10}^{\mu}A_{1-1}^{\nu}+V_{1-1}^{\mu}A_{10}^{\nu}\right)\penalty\ .

(107)

Similarly, we write,

J_{\mathrm{em}}^{(1)\mu}(J^{\nu}_{W})_{A}=\frac{1}{2\sqrt{2}}V_{10}^{\mu}A_{1+1}^{\nu}=T^{1}_{+1}-T^{2}_{+1}\penalty\ .

(108)

The difference in the tensor coefficients between Eq.(
106
) and (
108
) can be understood from Eq.(
94
).
To analyze crossing symmetry, we compare Eqs.(
106
) and (
108
). For
I=1/2
states the
T^{2}_{\pm 1}
matrix element is zero, whereas

\langle 1/2,-1/2|T^{1}_{-1}|1/2,+1/2\rangle=-\langle 1/2,1/2|T^{1}_{+1}|1/2,-1/2\rangle\penalty\ .

(109)

Together with Eq.(
100
), we find
\xi^{(1)}=+1
for
I=1/2
systems, in accord with Ref.
Seng et al. 2019
.

For
I=1
systems the matrix elements of both
T^{1}_{\pm 1}
and
T^{2}_{\pm 1}
are non-vanishing. In fact, using WET we find that they lead to opposite crossing symmetry between the matrix element of
J_{\mathrm{em}}^{(1)\mu}(J_{W}^{\dagger\nu})_{A}
and
J_{\mathrm{em}}^{(1)\mu}(J_{W}^{\nu})_{A}

\displaystyle\langle 1,m_{I}-1|T^{1}_{-1}|1,m_{I}\rangle

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-\langle 1,m_{I}|T^{1}_{+1}|1,m_{I}-1\rangle

\displaystyle\langle 1,m_{I}-1|T^{2}_{-1}|1,m_{I}\rangle

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\langle 1,m_{I}|(-T^{2}_{+1})|1,m_{I}-1\rangle\penalty\ .

(110)

This implies that
T_{i}^{(1)}(\nu,Q^{2})
for
I=1

\displaystyle\left.+\frac{1}{4QE_{e}}\ln\left(\frac{Q^{2}+4E_{e}Q\sqrt{1-x_{1}^{2}}+4E_{e}^{2}}{Q^{2}-4E_{e}Q\sqrt{1-x_{1}^{2}}+4E_{e}^{2}}\right)\right\}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{\alpha}{4\pi^{2}}\frac{1}{Mf_{+}(0)}\int_{0}^{\infty}\frac{dQ^{2}}{QE_{e}}\frac{M_{W}^{2}}{M_{W}^{2}+Q^{2}}\int_{\nu_{\text{thr}}}^{\infty}d\nu^{\prime}\left\{J\left(\frac{Q}{2E_{e}},\frac{\nu^{\prime}}{Q}\right)-\frac{Q}{E_{e}}I\left(\frac{Q}{2E_{e}},\frac{\nu^{\prime}}{Q}\right)\right\}F_{3,-}(\nu^{\prime},Q^{2})\penalty\ ,

where we have defined two integrals that are more complicated but still doable:

\displaystyle I(a,b)

\displaystyle\equiv

\displaystyle\int_{-1}^{1}dx\frac{x}{b^{2}+x^{2}}\left[\tan^{-1}\left(\frac{a+\sqrt{1-x^{2}}}{x}\right)-\tan^{-1}\left(\frac{a-\sqrt{1-x^{2}}}{x}\right)\right]

\displaystyle J(a,b)

\displaystyle\equiv

\displaystyle\int_{-1}^{1}dx\frac{1}{b^{2}+x^{2}}\ln\left(\frac{a^{2}+2a\sqrt{1-x^{2}}+1}{a^{2}-2a\sqrt{1-x^{2}}+1}\right)\penalty\ .

(117)

Both integrals take distinct functional forms for
a^{2}<1
and
a^{2}>1
. Their analytic results read:

I(a,b)=\Theta(1-a^{2})I^{<}(a,b)+\Theta(a^{2}-1)I^{>}(a,b)\penalty\ ,\penalty\ J(a,b)=\Theta(1-a^{2})J^{<}(a,b)+\Theta(a^{2}-1)J^{>}(a,b)\penalty\ ,

(118)

where

I^{<}(a,b)=-\pi\ln\left(\frac{4b^{2}}{2b(b+\sqrt{b^{2}+1})+1-a^{2}}\right)\penalty\ ,\penalty\ I^{>}(a,b)=-\pi\ln\left(1-\frac{1}{a^{2}(b+\sqrt{b^{2}+1})^{2}}\right)\penalty\ ,

(119)

and

J^{<}(a,b)=\frac{4\pi}{b}\tanh^{-1}\left(\frac{a}{b+\sqrt{b^{2}+1}}\right)\penalty\ ,\penalty\ J^{>}(a,b)=\frac{4\pi}{b}\tanh^{-1}\left(\frac{1}{a(b+\sqrt{b^{2}+1})}\right)\penalty\ .

(120)

Next we proceed with the odd piece. Integrating out
x_{2}
yields:

\displaystyle\mathfrak{Re}\Box_{\gamma W}^{b,\text{o,Wick}}(E_{e})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{\alpha}{\pi^{2}Mf_{+}(0)}\int_{0}^{\infty}dQ^{2}\frac{M_{W}^{2}}{M_{W}^{2}+Q^{2}}\int_{\nu_{\text{thr}}}^{\infty}d\nu^{\prime}\int_{-1}^{1}dx_{1}\frac{Q^{2}}{\nu^{\prime 2}+Q^{2}x_{1}^{2}}F_{3,+}(\nu^{\prime},Q^{2})

(121)

\displaystyle\times\left\{\frac{x_{1}}{2E_{e}\nu^{\prime}}\left[\tan^{-1}\left(\frac{Q+2E_{e}\sqrt{1-x_{1}^{2}}}{2E_{e}x_{1}}\right)-\tan^{-1}\left(\frac{Q-2E_{e}\sqrt{1-x_{1}^{2}}}{2E_{e}x_{1}}\right)\right]\right.

\displaystyle\left.+\frac{Qx_{1}^{2}}{8E_{e}^{2}\nu^{\prime}}\ln\left(\frac{Q^{2}+4E_{e}Q\sqrt{1-x_{1}^{2}}+4E_{e}^{2}}{Q^{2}-4E_{e}Q\sqrt{1-x_{1}^{2}}+4E_{e}^{2}}\right)-\frac{x_{1}^{2}\sqrt{1-x_{1}^{2}}}{E_{e}\nu^{\prime}}\right\}\penalty\ .

It may be cast in terms of the two integrals above, and two additional ones:

\displaystyle\tilde{I}(a)

\displaystyle\equiv

\displaystyle\int_{-1}^{1}dxx\left[\tan^{-1}\left(\frac{a+\sqrt{1-x^{2}}}{x}\right)-\tan^{-1}\left(\frac{a-\sqrt{1-x^{2}}}{x}\right)\right]=\lim_{b\rightarrow\infty}b^{2}I(a,b)

\displaystyle\tilde{J}(a)

\displaystyle\equiv

\displaystyle\int_{-1}^{1}dx\ln\left(\frac{a^{2}+2a\sqrt{1-x^{2}}+1}{a^{2}-2a\sqrt{1-x^{2}}+1}\right)=\lim_{b\rightarrow\infty}b^{2}J(a,b)\penalty\ .

(122)

Their analytic expressions read:

\tilde{I}(a)=\Theta(1-a^{2})\tilde{I}^{<}(a)+\Theta(a^{2}-1)\tilde{I}^{>}(a)\penalty\ ,\penalty\ \tilde{J}(a)=\Theta(1-a^{2})\tilde{J}^{<}(a)+\Theta(a^{2}-1)\tilde{J}^{>}(a)\penalty\ ,

(123)

where

\tilde{I}^{<}(a)=-\frac{\pi}{4}(a^{2}-2)\penalty\ ,\penalty\ \tilde{I}^{>}(a)=\frac{\pi}{4a^{2}}\penalty\ ,

(124)

and

\tilde{J}^{<}(a)=2\pi a\penalty\ ,\penalty\ \tilde{J}^{>}(a)=\frac{2\pi}{a}\penalty\ .

(125)

So the final result of the odd piece reads:

\displaystyle\mathfrak{Re}\Box_{\gamma W}^{b,{\mathrm{o,Wick}}}(E_{e})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{\alpha}{\pi^{2}}\frac{1}{Mf_{+}(0)}\int_{0}^{\infty}\frac{dQ^{2}}{E_{e}^{2}}\frac{M_{W}^{2}}{M_{W}^{2}+Q^{2}}\int_{\nu_{\text{thr}}}^{\infty}\frac{d\nu^{\prime}}{\nu^{\prime}}F_{3,+}(\nu^{\prime},Q^{2})\left\{\frac{E_{e}}{2}I\left(\frac{Q}{2E_{e}},\frac{\nu^{\prime}}{Q}\right)\right.

\displaystyle\left.+\frac{Q}{8}\left[-\frac{\nu^{\prime 2}}{Q^{2}}J\left(\frac{Q}{2E_{e}},\frac{\nu^{\prime}}{Q}\right)+\tilde{J}\left(\frac{Q}{2E_{e}}\right)\right]-\frac{\pi E_{e}}{2Q^{2}}\left[2\nu^{\prime}(\nu^{\prime}-\sqrt{\nu^{\prime 2}+Q^{2}})+Q^{2}\right]\right\}\penalty\ .

D.2
The residue contribution

Next we discuss the residue contribution. We first write the full box diagram as:

\displaystyle\Box_{\gamma W}^{b}(E_{e})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{4ie^{2}}{Mf_{+}(0)}\int\frac{d^{4}q}{(2\pi)^{4}}\frac{M_{W}^{2}}{M_{W}^{2}+Q^{2}}\frac{Q^{2}+\nu^{2}-\frac{\vec{p}_{e}\cdot\vec{q}}{E_{e}}\nu}{Q^{2}[(\nu-E_{e})^{2}-|\vec{p}_{e}-\vec{q}|^{2}+i\varepsilon]}

(127)

\displaystyle\times\int_{\nu_{\text{thr}}}^{\infty}d\nu^{\prime}\left[\frac{F_{3,-}(\nu^{\prime},Q^{2})}{\nu^{\prime 2}-\nu^{2}}+\frac{\nu F_{3,+}(\nu^{\prime},Q^{2})}{\nu^{\prime}(\nu^{\prime 2}-\nu^{2})}\right]\penalty\ ,

and all the complexities come from the electron propagator with non-zero
E_{e}
. It gives two poles:
\nu=E_{e}+|\vec{p}_{e}-\vec{q}|-i\varepsilon
and
\nu=E_{e}-|\vec{p}_{e}-\vec{q}|+i\varepsilon
. The first pole always stays in the fourth quadrant in the complex-
\nu
plane, which does not affect the Wick rotation. But the second pole could stay either in the first or second quadrant, depends on the sign of
E_{e}-|\vec{p}_{e}-\vec{q}|
. When
E_{e}>|\vec{p}_{e}-\vec{q}|
, it lies within the Wick rotation contour and thus gives a residue contribution to the integral.

To evaluate the residue, we first parameterize the measure of the Minkowskian loop momentum
q
as:

\int\frac{d^{4}q}{(2\pi)^{4}}=\int\frac{d^{3}q}{(2\pi)^{4}}\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}d\nu=\frac{1}{8\pi^{3}}\int_{0}^{\infty}d\mathbf{q}\mathbf{q}^{2}\int_{-1}^{1}dx\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}d\nu\penalty\ ,

(128)

where
\mathbf{q}=|\vec{q}|
and
x
is the cosine of the angle between
\vec{p}_{e}
and
\vec{q}
. Requiring a pole to exist in the first quadrant implies:

E_{e}>|\vec{p}_{e}-\vec{q}|=\sqrt{E_{e}^{2}-2E_{e}\mathbf{q}x+\mathbf{q}^{2}}\implies x>\frac{\mathbf{q}}{2E_{e}}\penalty\ ,

(129)

which in turn restricts the
\mathbf{q}
-integral within 0 and
2E_{e}
. With this, we may apply the residue theorem in the
\nu
-integration to obtain:

\displaystyle\Box_{\gamma W}^{b,\text{res}}(E_{e})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{2\alpha}{\pi Mf_{+}(0)}\int_{0}^{2E_{e}}d\mathbf{q}\mathbf{q}^{2}\int_{\frac{\mathbf{q}}{2E_{e}}}^{1}dx\frac{M_{W}^{2}}{M_{W}^{2}+Q^{2}}\frac{Q^{2}+\nu^{2}-\mathbf{q}\nu x}{Q^{2}}\frac{1}{|\vec{p}_{e}-\vec{q}|}

(130)

\displaystyle\left.\times\int_{\nu_{\text{thr}}}^{\infty}d\nu^{\prime}\left[\frac{F_{3,-}(\nu^{\prime},Q^{2})}{\nu^{\prime 2}-\nu^{2}}+\frac{\nu F_{3,+}(\nu^{\prime},Q^{2})}{\nu^{\prime}(\nu^{\prime 2}-\nu^{2})}\right]\right|_{\nu=E_{e}-|\vec{p}_{e}-\vec{q}|+i\varepsilon}\penalty\ .

The next step is to re-express the
\{\mathbf{q},x\}
integral in terms of
\{Q,x\}
. Starting with:

Q^{2}=-\nu^{2}+\mathbf{q}^{2}=-(E_{e}-|\vec{p}_{e}-\vec{q}|)^{2}+\mathbf{q}^{2}=2E_{e}(-E_{e}+\mathbf{q}x+\sqrt{E_{e}^{2}-2E_{e}\mathbf{q}x+\mathbf{q}^{2}})\penalty\ ,

(131)

we may solve for
\mathbf{q}
:

\mathbf{q}=\frac{-Q^{2}x+Q\sqrt{Q^{2}+4E_{e}^{2}(1-x^{2})}}{2E_{e}(1-x^{2})}\penalty\ ,

(132)

which also implies:

\displaystyle|\vec{p}_{e}-\vec{q}|

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{2E_{e}^{2}(1-x^{2})+Q^{2}-Qx\sqrt{Q^{2}+4E_{e}^{2}(1-x^{2})}}{2E_{e}(1-x^{2})}

\displaystyle\nu

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{-Q^{2}+Qx\sqrt{Q^{2}+4E_{e}^{2}(1-x^{2})}}{2E_{e}(1-x^{2})}+i\varepsilon\penalty\ .

(133)

Meanwhile, the integral measure and limits become:

\int_{0}^{2E_{e}}d\mathbf{q}\int_{\frac{\mathbf{q}}{2E_{e}}}^{1}dx=\int_{0}^{2E_{e}}dQ\int_{\frac{Q}{2E_{e}}}^{1}dx\frac{2|\vec{p}_{e}-\vec{q}|}{\sqrt{Q^{2}+4E_{e}^{2}(1-x^{2})}}\penalty\ ,

(134)

which can be checked numerically by integrating both sides with respect to an arbitrary integrand. With this, the real part of the residue contribution reads:

\displaystyle\mathfrak{Re}\Box_{\gamma W}^{b,\text{res}}(E_{e})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{2\alpha}{\pi Mf_{+}(0)}\int_{0}^{2E_{e}}dQ\int_{\frac{Q}{2E_{e}}}^{1}dx\frac{M_{W}^{2}}{M_{W}^{2}+Q^{2}}\frac{Q^{2}+\nu^{2}-\mathbf{q}\nu x}{Q^{2}}\frac{2\mathbf{q}^{2}}{\sqrt{Q^{2}+4E_{e}^{2}(1-x^{2})}}

(135)

\displaystyle\times\int_{\nu_{\text{thr}}}^{\infty}d\nu^{\prime}\left[F_{3,-}(\nu^{\prime},Q^{2})+\frac{\nu}{\nu^{\prime}}F_{3,+}(\nu^{\prime},Q^{2})\right]\text{Pr}\frac{1}{\nu^{\prime 2}-\nu^{2}}\penalty\ ,

where “Pr” denotes the principal-value.
Using Eqs.(
132
) and (
133
), one may again check that the term attached to
F_{3,-}
(
F_{3,+}
) is an even (odd) function of
E_{e}
. So we may similarly split
\mathfrak{Re}\Box_{\gamma W}^{b,\mathrm{res}}(E_{e})=\mathfrak{Re}\Box_{\gamma W}^{b,\mathrm{e,res}}(E_{e})+\mathfrak{Re}\Box_{\gamma W}^{b,\mathrm{o,res}}(E_{e})
.

The final step is to perform the
x
-integration in Eq.(
135
), remembering that
\mathbf{q}
and
\nu
are also functions of
x
. This is highly non-trivial, but fortunately the results can be expressed in terms of the functions we just defined in Eqs.(
119
), (
120
), (
124
) and (
125
):

\displaystyle\mathfrak{Re}\Box_{\gamma W}^{b,\mathrm{e,res}}(E_{e})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{\alpha}{4\pi^{2}}\frac{1}{Mf_{+}(0)}\int_{0}^{4E_{e}^{2}}\frac{dQ^{2}}{QE_{e}}\frac{M_{W}^{2}}{M_{W}^{2}+Q^{2}}\int_{\nu_{\text{thr}}}^{\infty}d\nu^{\prime}F_{3,-}(\nu^{\prime},Q^{2})\left\{\frac{Q}{E_{e}}I^{<}\left(\frac{Q}{2E_{e}},\frac{\nu^{\prime}}{Q}\right)\right.

\displaystyle\left.-J^{<}\left(\frac{Q}{2E_{e}},\frac{\nu^{\prime}}{Q}\right)+\mathfrak{Re}\left[-\frac{Q}{E_{e}}I^{>}\left(\frac{Q}{2E_{e}},\frac{\nu^{\prime}}{Q}\right)+J^{>}\left(\frac{Q}{2E_{e}},\frac{\nu^{\prime}}{Q}\right)\right]\right\}

\displaystyle\mathfrak{Re}\Box_{\gamma W}^{b,\mathrm{o,res}}(E_{e})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{\alpha}{\pi^{2}}\frac{1}{Mf_{+}(0)}\int_{0}^{4E_{e}^{2}}\frac{dQ^{2}}{E_{e}^{2}}\frac{M_{W}^{2}}{M_{W}^{2}+Q^{2}}\int_{\nu_{\text{thr}}}^{\infty}\frac{d\nu^{\prime}}{\nu^{\prime}}F_{3,+}(\nu^{\prime},Q^{2})

(136)

\displaystyle\times\left\{-\frac{E_{e}}{2}I^{<}\left(\frac{Q}{2E_{e}},\frac{\nu^{\prime}}{Q}\right)+\frac{\nu^{\prime 2}}{8Q}J^{<}\left(\frac{Q}{2E_{e}},\frac{\nu^{\prime}}{Q}\right)-\frac{Q}{8}\tilde{J}^{<}\left(\frac{Q}{2E_{e}}\right)\right.

\displaystyle\left.+\mathfrak{Re}\left[\frac{E_{e}}{2}I^{>}\left(\frac{Q}{2E_{e}},\frac{\nu^{\prime}}{Q}\right)-\frac{\nu^{\prime 2}}{8Q}J^{>}\left(\frac{Q}{2E_{e}},\frac{\nu^{\prime}}{Q}\right)+\frac{Q}{8}\tilde{J}^{>}\left(\frac{Q}{2E_{e}}\right)\right]\right\}\penalty\ .

These expressions can be easily understood. First of all we recall that
\mathfrak{Re}\Box_{\gamma W}^{b}(E_{e})=\mathfrak{Re}\Box_{\gamma W}^{b,\text{Wick}}(E_{e})+\mathfrak{Re}\Box_{\gamma W}^{b,\text{res}}(E_{e})
, and that the integrand of
\mathfrak{Re}\Box_{\gamma W}^{b,\text{Wick}}(E_{e})
takes two distinct forms in
Q<2E_{e}
and
Q>2E_{e}
. Since the integrand of the residue contribution survives only in
Q<2E_{e}
, one naturally expects it to “make up” the difference so that the full expression takes a unified form in both regions. We may then immediately postulate the solutions above, which can be easily verified numerically.

A final comment: The principal-value integration in Eq.(
135
) activates when
\nu=\nu^{\prime}
is possible, which happens at the point:

\lim_{x\rightarrow 1}\nu=E_{e}-\frac{Q^{2}}{4E_{e}}=\nu^{\prime}\implies E=E_{\text{min}}\equiv\frac{\nu^{\prime}+\sqrt{\nu^{\prime 2}+Q^{2}}}{2}\penalty\ .

(137)

This means, the integrand in
\mathfrak{Re}\Box_{\gamma W}^{b,\text{res}}(E_{e})
hits a singularity when
E_{e}=E_{\text{min}}
.

Appendix E
Basics of multipole expansion

In this Appendix we summarize some basic concepts of the multipole expansion of current operators which can be found in many references, e.g. Refs.
Donnelly and Walecka 1975
;
Donnelly et al. 2017
.

We start from a general current operator in the momentum space:

J^{\mu}(\vec{q})\equiv\int d^{3}xe^{-i\vec{q}\cdot\vec{x}}J^{\mu}(\vec{x})=(\rho(\vec{q}),\vec{J}(\vec{q}))\penalty\ ,

(138)

where
\rho(\vec{q})
and
\vec{J}(\vec{q})
are the charge and (spatial-) current densities respectively. For the latter, it is customary to be spanned using the three “spherical unit vectors”
\vec{\epsilon}_{\lambda}(\vec{q})
(
\lambda=0,\pm 1
) which satisfies
\vec{\epsilon}_{\lambda}(\vec{q})\cdot\vec{\epsilon}^{*}_{\lambda^{\prime}}(\vec{q})=\delta_{\lambda\lambda^{\prime}}
. The
\lambda=\pm 1
components are transverse while the
\lambda=0
component is longitudinal, i.e.

\vec{q}\cdot\vec{\epsilon}_{\pm 1}(\vec{q})=0\penalty\ ,\penalty\ \vec{q}\cdot\vec{\epsilon}_{0}(\vec{q})\neq 0\penalty\ .

(139)

In practice, one usually takes
\vec{q}
to be along the z-axis, i.e.
\vec{q}=\mathbf{q}\hat{e}_{z}
(here
\mathbf{q}\equiv|\vec{q}|
). In that case, the spherical unit vectors are given by:

\vec{\epsilon}_{\pm 1}(\mathbf{q}\hat{e}_{z})=\mp\frac{\hat{e}_{x}\pm i\hat{e}_{y}}{\sqrt{2}}\penalty\ ,\penalty\ \vec{\epsilon}_{0}(\mathbf{q}\hat{e}_{z})=\hat{e}_{z}\penalty\ .

(140)

With them we can span the spatial current as:

\vec{J}(\vec{q})=\sum_{\lambda}J(\vec{q},\lambda)\vec{\epsilon}_{\lambda}^{*}.

(141)

The starting point of multipole expansion is the following plane-wave expansion formula:

e^{-i\vec{q}\cdot\vec{x}}=4\pi\sum_{J=0}^{\infty}\sum_{m_{J}=-J}^{J}(-i)^{J}j_{J}(\mathbf{q}r)Y_{Jm_{J}}(\Omega_{q})Y_{Jm_{J}}^{*}(\Omega_{x})\penalty\ ,

(142)

where
r=|\vec{x}|
. With this we can express the charge density operator as:

\rho(\vec{q})=4\pi\sum_{Jm_{J}}(-i)^{J}Y_{Jm_{J}}^{*}(\Omega_{q})\int d^{3}xM_{J}^{m_{J}}(\mathbf{q},\vec{x})\rho(\vec{x})\penalty\ ,

(143)

where

M_{J}^{m_{J}}(\mathbf{q},\vec{x})\equiv j_{J}(\mathbf{q}r)Y_{Jm_{J}}(\Omega_{x})\penalty\ .

(144)

For the spatial current density operators we need one more step. We first define the “vector spherical harmonics” as:

\vec{Y}_{JL1}^{m_{J}}(\Omega_{x})\equiv\sum_{m_{L},\lambda}C_{Lm_{L};1\lambda}^{L1;Jm_{J}}Y_{Lm_{L}}(\Omega_{x})\vec{\epsilon}_{\lambda}\penalty\ ,

(145)

which is a rank-
J
tensor constructed from the product of a rank-
L
tensor (
Y_{Lm_{L}}
) and a rank-1 tensor (
\vec{\epsilon}_{\lambda}
). With this we can write:

J(\vec{q},\lambda)=4\pi\sum_{L,m_{L}}\sum_{Jm_{J}}(-i)^{L}Y_{Lm_{L}}^{*}(\Omega_{q})C_{Lm_{L};1\lambda}^{L1;Jm_{J}}\int d^{3}x\vec{M}_{JL}^{m_{J}}(\mathbf{q},\vec{x})\cdot\vec{J}(\vec{x})\penalty\ ,

(146)

where

\vec{M}_{JL}^{m_{J}}(\mathbf{q},\vec{x})\equiv j_{L}(\mathbf{q}r)\vec{Y}_{JL1}^{m_{J}}(\Omega_{x})\penalty\ .

(147)

Now we are ready to define the four types of multipole operators. They are:

\displaystyle\text{Coulomb}:\penalty\ M_{Jm_{J}}(\mathbf{q})

\displaystyle\equiv

\displaystyle\int d^{3}xM_{J}^{m_{J}}(\mathbf{q},\vec{x})\rho(\vec{x})\penalty\ ,\penalty\ J\geq 0

\displaystyle\text{Longitudinal}:\penalty\ L_{Jm_{J}}(\mathbf{q})

\displaystyle\equiv

\displaystyle\int d^{3}x\frac{i}{\mathbf{q}}\nabla M_{J}^{m_{J}}(\mathbf{q},\vec{x})\cdot\vec{J}(\vec{x})\penalty\ ,\penalty\ J\geq 0

\displaystyle\text{Transverse electric}:\penalty\ T_{Jm_{J}}^{\mathrm{el}}(\mathbf{q})

\displaystyle\equiv

\displaystyle\int d^{3}x\frac{1}{\mathbf{q}}(\nabla\times\vec{M}_{JJ}^{m_{J}}(\mathbf{q},\vec{x}))\cdot\vec{J}(\vec{x})\penalty\ ,J\geq 1

\displaystyle\text{Transverse magnetic}:\penalty\ T_{Jm_{J}}^{\mathrm{mag}}(\mathbf{q})

\displaystyle\equiv

\displaystyle\int d^{3}x\vec{M}_{JJ}^{m_{J}}(\mathbf{q},\vec{x})\cdot\vec{J}(\vec{x})\penalty\ ,\penalty\ J\geq 1\penalty\ .

(148)

Using the following mathematical identity:

\displaystyle e^{-i\vec{q}\cdot\vec{x}}\vec{\epsilon}_{\lambda}(\vec{q})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\left\{\begin{array}[]{ccc}\sqrt{4\pi}\sum_{J=0}^{\infty}(-i)^{J}\sqrt{2J+1}\frac{i}{\mathbf{q}}\nabla M_{J}^{0}(\mathbf{q},\vec{x})&,&\lambda=0\\
-\sqrt{2\pi}\sum_{J=1}^{\infty}(-i)^{J}\sqrt{2J+1}\left(\lambda\vec{M}_{JJ}^{\lambda}(\mathbf{q},\vec{x})-\frac{1}{\mathbf{q}}\nabla\times\vec{M}_{JJ}^{\lambda}(\mathbf{q},\vec{x})\right)&,&\lambda=\pm 1\end{array}\right.\penalty\ ,

The charge and current density operators can then be expressed as:

\displaystyle\rho(\vec{q})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sqrt{4\pi}\sum_{J=0}^{\infty}(-i)^{J}\sqrt{2J+1}M_{J0}(\mathbf{q})

\displaystyle J(\vec{q},\lambda)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\left\{\begin{array}[]{ccc}\sqrt{4\pi}\sum_{J=0}^{\infty}(-i)^{J}\sqrt{2J+1}L_{J0}(\mathbf{q})&,&\lambda=0\\
-\sqrt{2\pi}\sum_{J=1}^{\infty}(-i)^{J}\sqrt{2J+1}\left(\lambda T_{J\lambda}^{\mathrm{mag}}(\mathbf{q})-T_{J\lambda}^{\mathrm{el}}(\mathbf{q})\right)&,&\lambda=\pm 1\end{array}\right.

which is our desired multipole expansion formula.

One could also define the spatial Fourier transform with reversed momentum:

J^{\mu}(-\vec{q})\equiv\int d^{3}xe^{i\vec{q}\cdot\vec{x}}J^{\mu}(\vec{x})=(\rho(-\vec{q}),\vec{J}(-\vec{q}))\penalty\ .

(156)

Here we still take
\vec{q}=\mathbf{q}\hat{e}_{z}
, with all the definitions of
\vec{\epsilon}_{\lambda}
the same as before. In this case the charge and current densities can also be expanded in multipole as:

\displaystyle\rho(-\vec{q})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sqrt{4\pi}\sum_{J=0}^{\infty}i^{J}\sqrt{2J+1}M_{J0}(\mathbf{q})

\displaystyle J(-\vec{q},\lambda)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\left\{\begin{array}[]{ccc}-\sqrt{4\pi}\sum_{J=0}^{\infty}i^{J}\sqrt{2J+1}L_{J0}(\mathbf{q})&,&\lambda=0\\
-\sqrt{2\pi}\sum_{J=1}^{\infty}i^{J}\sqrt{2J+1}\left(\lambda T_{J\lambda}^{\mathrm{mag}}(\mathbf{q})+T_{J\lambda}^{\mathrm{el}}(\mathbf{q})\right)&,&\lambda=\pm 1\end{array}\right.\penalty\ .

Next, we know that the current could be vector or axial and could have isospin structure, see Eq.(
7
).
Incorporating these details, we may define the vector multipole operators:

M_{Jm_{J};Im_{I}}^{V}(\mathbf{q})\penalty\ ,\penalty\ L_{Jm_{J};Im_{I}}^{V}(\mathbf{q})\penalty\ ,\penalty\ T^{V,\mathrm{el}}_{Jm_{J};Im_{I}}(\mathbf{q})\penalty\ ,\penalty\ T^{V,\mathrm{mag}}_{Jm_{J};Im_{I}}(\mathbf{q})\penalty\ ,

(160)

and the axial multipole operators:

M_{Jm_{J};Im_{I}}^{A}(\mathbf{q})\penalty\ ,\penalty\ L_{Jm_{J};Im_{I}}^{A}(\mathbf{q})\penalty\ ,\penalty\ T^{A,\mathrm{el}}_{Jm_{J};Im_{I}}(\mathbf{q})\penalty\ ,\penalty\ T^{A,\mathrm{mag}}_{Jm_{J};Im_{I}}(\mathbf{q})

(161)

accordingly. Among the vector multipoles, the Coulomb, longitudinal and transverse electric operators have parity
(-1)^{J}
while the transverse magnetic operator has parity
(-1)^{J+1}
. The parity of the axial multipoles are exactly the opposite.

Finally, in most of the nuclear theory calculations it is natural to assume isospin symmetry, so the vector isospin current
V_{Im_{I}}^{\mu}
is conserved. As a consequence, the Coulomb and longitudinal multipole operators of the vector currents are not independent as far as their matrix elements are concerned:

\langle\phi_{f}|L_{Jm_{J};Im_{I}}^{V}(\mathbf{q})|\phi_{i}\rangle=-\left(\frac{E_{f}-E_{i}}{\mathbf{q}}\right)\langle\phi_{f}|M_{Jm_{J};Im_{I}}^{V}(\mathbf{q})|\phi_{i}\rangle\penalty\ .

(162)

One may use this relation to eliminate the vector longitudinal multipoles in favor of the vector Coulomb multipoles.
