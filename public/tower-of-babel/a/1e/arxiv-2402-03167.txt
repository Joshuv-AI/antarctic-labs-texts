Decentralized Bilevel Optimization: A Perspective from Transient Iteration Complexity
Boao Kong kongboao@stu.pku.edu.cnCenter for Data Science, Peking UniversityBeijing, China, Shuchen Zhushuchenzhu@stu.pku.edu.cnCenter for Data Science, Peking UniversityBeijing, ChinaSongtao Lu stlu@cse.cuhk.edu.hkDepartment of Computer Science and EngineeringThe Chinese University of Hong KongHong Kong SAR of ChinaXinmeng Huang xinmengh@sas.upenn.eduGraduate Group in Applied Mathematics and Computational ScienceUniversity of PennsylvaniaPhiladelphia, USA, Kun Yuan kunyuan@pku.edu.cnCenter for Machine Learning Research, Peking UniversityAI for Science InstituteBeijing, China

Abstract
Stochastic bilevel optimization (SBO) is becoming increasingly essential in machine learning due to its versatility in handling nested structures. To address large-scale SBO, decentralized approaches have emerged as effective paradigms in which nodes communicate with immediate neighbors without a central server, thereby improving communication efficiency and enhancing algorithmic robustness. However, most decentralized SBO algorithms focus solely on asymptotic convergence rates, overlooking transient iteration complexity—the number of iterations required before asymptotic rates dominate, which results in limited understanding of the influence of network topology, data heterogeneity, and the nested bilevel algorithmic structures. To address this issue, this paper introduces D-SOBA , a D ecentralized S tochastic O ne-loop B ilevel A lgorithm framework. D-SOBA comprises two variants: D-SOBA-SO, which incorporates second-order Hessian and Jacobian matrices, and D-SOBA-FO, which relies entirely on first-order gradients. We provide a comprehensive non-asymptotic convergence analysis and establish the transient iteration complexity of D-SOBA. This provides the first theoretical understanding of how network topology, data heterogeneity, and nested bilevel structures influence decentralized SBO. Extensive experimental results demonstrate the efficiency and theoretical advantages of D-SOBA.

Introduction

Stochastic bilevel optimization, which tackles problems with nested optimization structures, has gained growing interest. This two-level structure provides a flexible and potent framework for addressing a broad range of tasks, spanning from hyperparameter optimization
(
Bertinetto et al. 2019
;
Franceschi et al. 2018
;
Snell et al. 2017
)
to reinforcement learning
(
Hong et al. 2023
)
, adversarial learning
(
Zhang et al. 2022
)
, continue learning
(
Borsos et al. 2020
)
, and imitation learning
(
Arora et al. 2020
)
. State-of-the-art performance in these tasks is typically achieved with extremely large datasets, which necessitates efficient distributed algorithms for stochastic bilevel optimization across multiple computing nodes.

This paper considers
N
nodes connected through a given graph topology. Each node
i
privately owns an upper-level loss function
f_{i}:\mathbb{R}^{d}\times\mathbb{R}^{p}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
and a lower-level loss function
g_{i}:\mathbb{R}^{d}\times\mathbb{R}^{p}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
. The goal of all nodes is to collaboratively find a solution to the following distributed stochastic bilevel optimization problem:

\displaystyle\min_{x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}}\quad

\displaystyle\Phi(x):=f(x,y^{\star}(x)):=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}f_{i}\left(x,y^{\star}(x)\right)

(1a)

\displaystyle\mathrm{s.t.}\quad

\displaystyle y^{\star}(x):=\argmin_{y\in\mathbb{R}^{p}}\left\{g(x,y):=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}g_{i}(x,y)\right\}

(1b)

where
f_{i}
and
g_{i}
are defined as the expectation of
F(x,y;\xi_{i})
and
G(x,y;\zeta_{i})
:

\displaystyle f_{i}(x,y):=\mathbb{E}_{\xi_{i}\sim{\mathcal{D}}_{f_{i}}}[F(x,y;\xi_{i})],\quad g_{i}(x,y):=\mathbb{E}_{\zeta_{i}\sim{\mathcal{D}}_{g_{i}}}[G(x,y;\zeta_{i})].

(2)

The random variables
\xi_{i}
and
\zeta_{i}
represent data samples available at node
i
, drawn from the local upper-level distribution
{\mathcal{D}}_{f_{i}}
and the lower-level distribution
{\mathcal{D}}_{g_{i}}
, respectively. Throughout this paper, we assume that these local data distributions vary across nodes, potentially causing data heterogeneity issues during training.

1.1
Decentralized Bilevel Algorithms

Conventional distributed approaches for solving problem (
1
) typically follow a centralized paradigm
(
Huang et al. 2023
;
Tarzanagh et al. 2022
;
Yang et al. 2023b
)
, where a central server synchronizes updates across the network by computing a globally averaged upper- or lower-level stochastic gradient to update the model parameters. This global averaging step is commonly implemented using mechanisms such as Parameter Server
(
Li et al. 2014
;
Smola and Narayanamurthy 2010
)
or Ring-Allreduce
(
Gibiansky 2017
)
. However, these techniques incur substantial bandwidth costs or high latency
(
Ying et al. 2021b
, Table I)
, which severely limits the scalability of centralized bilevel optimization.

Decentralized bilevel optimization is a new paradigm to approach problem (
1
) eliminating the global averaging step, wherein each node maintains a local model updated by communicating with its immediate neighbors.
This distinctive feature results in a noteworthy reduction in communication costs compared to centralized approaches. Beyond the communication efficiency, decentralized bilevel algorithms also exhibit enhanced robustness to node and link failures, persisting in converging to the desired solution as long as the graph remains connected. In contrast, centralized bilevel algorithms inevitably break down when the central server crashes.
For these reasons, considerable research efforts,
e.g.
,
(
Chen et al. 2022
;
Chen et al. 2023a
;
Dong et al. 2023
;
Gao et al. 2023
;
Liu et al. 2022b
;
Lu et al. 2022b
;
Niu et al. 2023
;
Yang et al. 2022
;
Zhang et al. 2023a
)
, have been dedicated to developing decentralized bilevel algorithms with theoretical guarantees and empirical effectiveness.

1.2
Limitations and Open Questions

Limitations in existing literature.
Significant progress has been made in developing decentralized stochastic bilevel algorithms, with most studies focusing on their
asymptotic
convergence rates, which are achieved after a sufficiently large number of iterations. References
(
Lu et al. 2022b
;
Yang et al. 2022
)
demonstrate that decentralized bilevel algorithms attain the same asymptotic convergence rates as their centralized bilevel counterparts or decentralized single-level algorithms, highlighting their strong theoretical guarantees. However, an exclusive focus on asymptotic convergence rates presents several critical limitations:

L1.

In practical scenarios, computational constraints—such as limited time and resource budgets—often restrict the number of iterations that can be performed, preventing algorithms from reaching their asymptotic stage. It remains unknown the number of transient iterations required before the algorithm attains its asymptotic rate.

L2.

Asymptotic convergence rates offer limited insight into the impact of nested algorithmic structures, network topologies, and data heterogeneity on decentralized bilevel algorithms, as these factors are typically associated with the algorithms’ transient convergence stage rather than its asymptotic steady-state rate.

L3.

Asymptotic convergence rates are inadequate as a metric for distinguishing between various decentralized bilevel algorithms, as most existing methods share the same asymptotic convergence rate
(
Lu et al. 2022b
;
Yang et al. 2022
)
. This makes it challenging to determine which algorithm delivers superior theoretical performance.

Open questions.
To address these limitations and advance our understanding of decentralized bilevel optimization, several fundamental open questions need to be answered:

Q1.

Can we quantify the transient iteration complexity of decentralized stochastic bilevel algorithms—that is, the number of iterations needed for the asymptotic convergence rate to dominate? Furthermore, can transient iteration complexity serve as a criterion to differentiate between various decentralized bilevel algorithms?

Q2.

Can we elucidate how network topologies and data heterogeneity influence transient iteration complexity? What benefits can we achieve by enhancing network connectivity and reducing data heterogeneity?

Q3.

Can we clarify how the nested bilevel algorithmic structure affects transient iteration complexity? Does the lower-level optimization introduce substantial challenges to the non-asymptotic phase?

;
Kayaalp et al. 2022
;
Sow et al. 2022
)
.

C2.

We provide a comprehensive non-asymptotic convergence analysis and derive the transient iteration complexity for the D-SOBA framework. On one hand, we demonstrate that D-SOBA achieves an asymptotic gradient complexity of
\mathcal{O}(1/(N\epsilon^{2}))
where
\epsilon
is the desired convergence accuracy, outperforming existing results by at least a factor of
\log(1/\epsilon)
. On the other hand, the transient iteration complexity allows us to clarify the theoretical superiority of D-SOBA over other bilevel algorithms in the transient iteration regime. This addresses open question Q1. Notably, all convergence results for the D-SOBA framework extend naturally to both D-SOBA-SO and D-SOBA-FO.

C3.

Our transient iteration complexity is the
first
result in decentralized bilevel optimizaiton to quantify how network topology and data heterogeneity jointly affect the non-asymptotic convergence stage. It highlights that severe data heterogeneity exacerbates the impact of network topology, while poorly connected networks amplify the adverse effects of data heterogeneity. Neglecting either factor result in an incomplete transient-stage analysis. Additionally, we quantify the theoretical benefits achievable through enhanced network connectivity and reduced data heterogeneity, thereby addressing question Q2. All these results apply to both D-SOBA-SO and D-SOBA-FO.

C4.

Our comprehensive convergence analysis elucidates the impact of the nested bilevel algorithmic structure on transient iteration complexity. On one hand, we demonstrate that decentralized single-level and bilevel algorithms share the same asymptotic convergence rate and transient iteration complexity with respect to network topology and data heterogeneity. On the other hand, we clarify that the nested algorithmic structure significantly exacerbates the influence of the lower-level optimization’s condition number. This highlights the challenges inherent to decentralized bilevel optimization, thereby addressing open question Q3.

1.4
Related Works

Bilevel optimization.
Bilevel optimization
(
Bracken and McGill 1973
)

has shown extensive applications in operations research, signal processing, and machine learning
(
Colson et al. 2007
;
Vicente and Calamai 1994
;
Zhang et al. 2023b
)
. A central challenge in bilevel optimization is the estimation of the hypergradient
\nabla\Phi(x)
. To address this, various algorithms have emerged, leveraging approaches such as approximate implicit differentiation
(
Domke 2012
;
Ghadimi and Wang 2018
;
Grazzi et al. 2020
;
Ji et al. 2021
)
, iterative differentiation
(
Domke 2012
;
Franceschi et al. 2018
;
Grazzi et al. 2020
;
Ji et al. 2021
;
Maclaurin et al. 2015
)
, and Neumann series
(
Chen et al. 2021a
;
Hong et al. 2023
)
. However, these approaches incur additional inner-loops to estimate the lower-level solution
y^{\star}(x)
and the Hessian inverse of
g(x,y)
, resulting in extra computational overhead and worsening the iteration complexity. A recent work
(
Dagréou et al. 2022
)
develops a novel single-loop framework for stochastic bilevel optimization, in which the Hessian inverse is removed from the algorithmic structure and the lower and upper variables are updated simultaneously.
Moreover, first-order algorithms for bilevel optimization have gained an increasing focus to solve large-scale optimization tasks. An effective approach is to view the lower-level loss as a penalty and add it to the upper-level loss
(
Liu et al. 2022a
;
Kwon et al. 2023
;
Lu and Mei 2024
)
. Moreover,
Yang et al. 2023a
also uses finite-difference of stochastic gradients to evaluate Hessian/Jacobian, reducing computation and storage cost especially in large-scaled scenarios.

Decentralized optimization.
Decentralized optimization is useful in situations where the centralized control of all nodes by a single server is either practically infeasible or prohibitively expensive.
Early well-known algorithms include decentralized gradient descent
(
Nedic and Ozdaglar 2009
;
Yuan et al. 2016
)
, diffusion strategies
(
Chen and Sayed 2012
)
, dual averaging
(
Duchi et al. 2011
)
, EXTRA
(

)
, Exact-Diffusion
(
Li et al. 2019
;
Yuan et al. 2019
)
, gradient tracking
(
Di Lorenzo and Scutari 2016
;
Nedic et al. 2017
;
Xu et al. 2015
)
, and decentralized ADMM methods
(
Chang et al. 2014
;
Shi et al. 2014
)
. In the stochastic context, decentralized SGD is established in
(
Lian et al. 2017b
)
to achieve the same asymptotic linear speedup as centralized SGD. Since then, many efforts have extended decentralized SGD to directed topologies
(
Nedić and Olshevsky 2014
)
, time-varying topologies
(
Koloskova et al. 2020
;
Nedić and Olshevsky 2014
)
, and data-heterogeneous scenarios
(
Lin et al. 2021
;
Lu et al. 2019
;
Tang et al. 2018
;
Xin et al. 2020
;
Yuan et al. 2023
;
Yuan et al. 2021
)
. Lower bounds and optimal complexities are also recently established for stochastic decentralized optimization
(
Lu and De Sa 2021
;
Yuan et al. 2022

Preliminaries

2.1
Notations

For a second-order differentiable function
f:\mathbb{R}^{d}\times\mathbb{R}^{p}\to\mathbb{R}
, we denote its partial gradients as
\nabla_{1}f(x,y)\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
and
\nabla_{2}f(x,y)\in\mathbb{R}^{p}
with respect to
x
and
y
, respectively. The corresponding partial Hessians are
\nabla_{12}^{2}f(x,y)\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times p}
and
\nabla_{22}^{2}f(x,y)\in\mathbb{R}^{p\times p}
. Additionally, we use
\nabla_{x}f(x,y^{\star}(x))
to denote the gradient of
f
with respect to
x
, treating
y
as a function of
x
. We let
\|\cdot\|
denote the
\ell_{2}
norm for both vectors and matrices, and
\|\cdot\|_{F}
the Frobenius norm for matrices. The vector
\mathds{1}_{N}\in\mathbb{R}^{N}
represents an all-ones vector. For local variables
\{x_{i}^{(t)}\}_{i=1}^{N}
, the subscript
i
denotes the node index, while the superscript
t
indicates the iteration. Their average,
\sum_{i=1}^{N}x_{i}^{(t)}/N
, is denoted as
\bar{x}^{(t)}
. Finally, we write
a\lesssim b
if there exists a constant
C>0
such that
a\leq Cb
.

(x,y)\in\mathbb{R}^{d}\times\mathbb{R}^{p}
, it holds that:

\displaystyle\dfrac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\left\|\nabla_{1}f_{i}(x,y)-\nabla_{1}f(x,y)\right\|^{2}\leq b^{2},\quad\hskip 2.0pt\dfrac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\left\|\nabla_{2}f_{i}(x,y)-\nabla_{2}f(x,y)\right\|^{2}\leq b^{2};

\displaystyle\dfrac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\left\|\nabla_{2}g_{i}(x,y)-\nabla_{2}g(x,y)\right\|^{2}\leq b^{2},\quad\hskip 2.0pt\dfrac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\left\|\nabla_{12}^{2}g_{i}(x,y)-\nabla_{12}^{2}g(x,y)\right\|^{2}\leq b^{2},

\displaystyle\dfrac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\left\|\nabla_{22}^{2}g_{i}(x,y)-\nabla_{22}^{2}g(x,y)\right\|^{2}\leq b^{2}.

We also make the following standard assumption for stochastic gradients and Hessians.

Assumption 3

(
Stochasticity)
.

There exists a constant
\sigma\geq 0
such that for any given
(x,y)\in\mathbb{R}^{d}\times\mathbb{R}^{p}
and
1\leq i\leq N
:

1. the gradient oracles satisfy:

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{\xi_{i}\sim{\mathcal{D}}_{f_{i}}}[\nabla_{1}F(x,y;\xi_{i})]=\nabla_{1}f_{i}(x,y),\quad\mathbb{E}_{\xi_{i}\sim{\mathcal{D}}_{f_{i}}}\left[\left\|\nabla_{1}F(x,y;\xi_{i})-\nabla_{1}f_{i}(x,y)\right\|^{2}\right]\leq\sigma^{2},

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{\xi_{i}\sim{\mathcal{D}}_{f_{i}}}[\nabla_{2}F(x,y;\xi_{i})]=\nabla_{2}f_{i}(x,y),\quad\mathbb{E}_{\xi_{i}\sim{\mathcal{D}}_{f_{i}}}\left[\left\|\nabla_{2}F(x,y;\xi_{i})-\nabla_{2}f_{i}(x,y)\right\|^{2}\right]\leq\sigma^{2},

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{\zeta_{i}\sim{\mathcal{D}}_{g_{i}}}[\nabla_{2}G(x,y;\zeta_{i})]=\nabla_{2}g_{i}(x,y),\quad\mathbb{E}_{\zeta_{i}\sim{\mathcal{D}}_{g_{i}}}\left[\left\|\nabla_{2}G(x,y;\zeta_{i})-\nabla_{2}g_{i}(x,y)\right\|^{2}\right]\leq\sigma^{2};

2. the Hessian/Jacobian oracles satisfy:

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{\zeta_{i}\sim{\mathcal{D}}_{g_{i}}}\left[\nabla_{12}^{2}G(x,y;\zeta_{i})\right]=\nabla_{12}^{2}g_{i}(x,y),\quad\mathbb{E}_{\zeta_{i}\sim{\mathcal{D}}_{g_{i}}}\left[\left\|\nabla_{12}^{2}G(x,y;\zeta_{i})-\nabla_{12}^{2}g_{i}(x,y)\right\|^{2}\right]\leq\sigma^{2},

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{\zeta_{i}\sim{\mathcal{D}}_{g_{i}}}\left[\nabla_{22}^{2}G(x,y;\zeta_{i})\right]=\nabla_{22}^{2}g_{i}(x,y),\quad\mathbb{E}_{\zeta_{i}\sim{\mathcal{D}}_{g_{i}}}\left[\left\|\nabla_{22}^{2}G(x,y;\zeta_{i})-\nabla_{22}^{2}g_{i}(x,y)\right\|^{2}\right]\leq\sigma^{2}.

This work studies decentralized algorithms over networks of
N
nodes interconnected by a graph
\mathcal{G}
with a set of edges
\mathcal{E}
. Node
i
is connected to node
j
if
(i,j)\in\mathcal{E}
. To facilitate decentralized communication, we introduce the mixing matrix
W=[w_{i,j}]_{i,j=1}^{N}\in\mathbb{R}^{N\times N}
in which each weight
w_{i,j}
gauges information flowing from node
j
to node
i
. Furthermore, we set
w_{i,j}=0
for
(j,i)\notin\mathcal{E}
. The following assumption on the mixing matrix is widely used for decentralized algorithms
(
Koloskova et al. 2020
;
Lian et al. 2017b
;
Yuan et al. 2016
)
.

Assumption 4

(
Mixing matrix)
.

The mixing matrix
W
is doubly stochastic, i.e.,

\mathds{1}_{N}^{\top}W=\mathds{1}_{N}^{\top},\quad W\mathds{1}_{N}=\mathds{1}_{N}.

Moreover, we assume
\rho:=\left\|W-\mathds{1}_{N}\mathds{1}_{N}^{\top}/N\right\|_{2}\in[0,1)
.

Remark 0

(
spectral gap)
.

In decentralized algorithms, the quantity
1-\rho
is commonly known as the spectral gap
(
Lu and De Sa 2021
;
Yuan et al. 2023
)
of
W
, which serves as a metric for measuring the connectivity of the network topology. Notably, as
1-\rho\to 1
, it indicates that the topology is well-connected (e.g., for a fully connected graph, the mixing matrix is
W=\mathds{1}_{N}\mathds{1}_{N}^{\top}/N
with
\rho=0)
. Conversely, as
1-\rho\to 0
, it suggests that the topology is potentially sparse
(
Lian et al. 2017a
;
Ying et al. 2021a
)
.

Remark 0

(
Non-symmetric mixing matrix)
.

While Assumption
4
requires the mixing matrix
W
to be doubly stochastic, it does not need to be symmetric. This contrasts with the recent work
(
Zhu et al. 2024
)
, where the SPARKLE algorithm is proposed and analyzed under the assumption of a symmetric and doubly-stochastic mixing matrix.

3
Decentralized Stochastic One-loop Bilevel Algorithm (D-SOBA)

Major challenges of decentralized SBO.
In this section, we present our D-SOBA algorithm for decentralized SBO. The primary challenge in SBO stems from the estimation of the hypergradient
\nabla\Phi(x)
,
i.e.
,
\nabla_{x}f(x,y^{\star}(x))
, due to the implicit dependence of
y^{\star}(x)
on
x
. Under Assumption
1
and the principles of implicit function theory
(
Griewank and Walther 2008
)
,
\nabla\Phi(x)
can be expressed as:

\nabla\Phi(x)=\ \nabla_{1}f(x,y^{\star}(x))-\Big(\nabla^{2}_{12}g(x,y^{\star}(x))\ \cdot\left[\nabla^{2}_{22}g(x,y^{\star}(x))\right]^{-1}\cdot\nabla_{2}f(x,y^{\star}(x))\Big)

which is computationally expensive due to the need for inverting the partial Hessian. Moreover, the Hessian-inversion

\left[\nabla_{22}^{2}g(x,y^{\star}(x))\right]^{-1}=\left[\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\nabla_{22}^{2}\penalty\ g_{i}(x,y^{\star}(x))\right]^{-1}

cannot be easily accessed through decentralized communication even in the absence of stochastic noise affecting the estimate of
\nabla_{22}^{2}\penalty\ g_{i}(x,y^{\star}(x))
. This challenge can be partially mitigated by incorporating auxiliary inner loops to approximate the lower-level solution
y^{\star}(x)
and evaluate the Hessian inversion using the Neumann series, as demonstrated in works such as
(
Chen et al. 2022
;
Chen et al. 2023a
;
Lu et al. 2022b
;
Yang et al. 2022
)
. However, introducing these auxiliary inner loops results in sub-optimal convergence, as shown in Table
1
. Moreover, it complicates algorithmic implementation and may impose a substantial burden on communication and computation.

Centralized SOBA.
The challenge of Hessian inversion can be effectively addressed by a novel framework known as SOBA.
SOBA, initially proposed in
(
Dagréou et al. 2022
)
, is a single-node algorithm. We now extend it to solve the distributed problem (
1
) in the centralized setup. To begin with, SOBA introduces

\displaystyle z^{\star}(x)=\left[\nabla^{2}_{22}g(x,y^{\star}(x))\right]^{-1}\nabla_{2}f(x,y^{\star}(x))

(5)

which can be regarded as the solution to minimizing the following optimization problem

\displaystyle\!\frac{1}{2}z^{\top}\nabla_{22}^{2}g\left(x,y^{\star}\right)z-z^{\top}\nabla_{2}f\left(x,y^{\star}\right)\overset{\eqref{prob:general}}{=}\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\left\{\frac{1}{2}z^{\top}\nabla_{22}^{2}g_{i}\left(x,y^{\star}\right)z-z^{\top}\nabla_{2}f_{i}\left(x,y^{\star}\right)\right\},\!

(6)

where
y^{\star}
denotes
y^{\star}(x)
. It is worth noting that while
z^{\star}(x)
in (
5
) cannot be written as a finite sum across nodes, problem (
6
) involves only simple sums.

To save computations, we can approximately solve (
6
) using one-step (stochastic) gradient descent. This, combined with one-step (stochastic) gradient descent to update the upper- and lower-level variables
(x,y)
, forms the centralized single-loop framework for solving (
1
):

\displaystyle\underbrace{x^{(t+1)}=x^{(t)}-\frac{\alpha_{t}}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}D_{x,i}^{(t)}}_{\rm minimize\;\eqref{eq:upper}},\quad\underbrace{y^{(t+1)}=y^{(t)}-\frac{\beta_{t}}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}D_{y,i}^{(t)}}_{\rm minimize\;\eqref{eq:lower}},\quad\underbrace{z^{(t+1)}=z^{(t)}-\frac{\gamma_{t}}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}D_{z,i}^{(t)}}_{\rm minimize\;\eqref{eq:soba}},

(7)

where
D_{x,i}^{(t)},D_{y,i}^{(t)}
and
D_{z,i}^{(t)}
are defined as follows:

\displaystyle D^{(t)}_{x,i}(x^{(t)},y^{(t)},z^{(t)})

\displaystyle=\nabla_{1}f_{i}(x^{(t)},y^{(t)})-\nabla^{2}_{12}g_{i}(x^{(t)},y^{(t)})z^{(t)},

(8a)

\displaystyle D^{(t)}_{y,i}(x^{(t)},y^{(t)},z^{(t)})

\displaystyle=\nabla_{2}g_{i}(x^{(t)},y^{(t)}),

(8b)

\displaystyle D^{(t)}_{z,i}(x^{(t)},y^{(t)},z^{(t)})

\displaystyle=\nabla^{2}_{22}g_{i}(x^{(t)},y^{(t)})z^{(t)}-\nabla_{2}f_{i}(x^{(t)},y^{(t)}),

(8c)

and
\alpha_{t}
,
\beta_{t}
and
\gamma_{t}
are learning rates. We denote recursion (
7
) as centralized SOBA since a central server is required to collect
D_{x,i}^{(t)},D_{y,i}^{(t)}
, and
D_{z,i}^{(t)}
across the entire network, as well as update variables
x^{(t)},y^{(t)}
and
z^{(t)}
. It is noteworthy that (
7
) does not require any inner loop to approximate the lower-level solution
y^{\star}(x)
or evaluate the Hessian inversion of
g(x,y)
.

Decentralized SOBA (D-SOBA) Framework.

Inspired by
(
Chen and Sayed 2012
;
Nedic and Ozdaglar 2009
)
, we extend centralized SOBA (
7
) to decentralized setup:

\displaystyle x_{i}^{(t+1)}

\displaystyle=\sum_{j\in{\mathcal{N}}_{i}}w_{ij}(x_{j}^{(t)}-\alpha_{t}D_{x,j}^{(t)}),

(9a)

\displaystyle y_{i}^{(t+1)}

\displaystyle=\sum_{j\in{\mathcal{N}}_{i}}w_{ij}(y_{j}^{(t)}-\beta_{t}D_{y,j}^{(t)}),

(9b)

\displaystyle z_{i}^{(t+1)}

\displaystyle=\sum_{j\in{\mathcal{N}}_{i}}w_{ij}(z_{j}^{(t)}-\gamma_{t}D_{z,j}^{(t)}),

(9c)

where
x_{i}^{(t)}
,
y_{i}^{(t)}
, and
z_{i}^{(t)}
are local variables maintained by node
i
at iteration
t
, and
D_{x,i}^{(t)}
,
D_{y,i}^{(t)}
, and
D_{z,i}^{(t)}
are simplified notations for
D_{x,i}^{(t)}(x^{(t)},y^{(t)},z^{(t)})
,
D_{y,i}^{(t)}(x^{(t)},y^{(t)},z^{(t)})
, and
D_{z,i}^{(t)}(x^{(t)},y^{(t)},z^{(t)})
, respectively, as defined in (
8
). The set
\mathcal{N}_{i}
includes node
i
and all its immediate neighbors. We refer to recursions (
9
) as the D-SOBA framework, which forms the foundation of the D-SOBA-SO and D-SOBA-FO algorithms.

D-SOBA-SO Algorithm.
The quantities
D_{x,i}^{(t)}
,
D_{y,i}^{(t)}
, and
D_{z,i}^{(t)}
in the decentralized SOBA framework (
9
) involve second-order matrices such as
\nabla^{2}_{12}g_{i}(x^{(t)},y^{(t)})
and
\nabla^{2}_{22}g_{i}(x^{(t)},y^{(t)})
. When these second-order matrices are explicitly maintained in the algorithm, the resulting method is referred to as D-SOBA-SO. In stochastic settings, D-SOBA-SO samples stochastic estimates of the gradients, Jacobians, and Hessians in (
8
). In particular, suppose D-SOBA-SO independently samples a minibatch of data,
\xi^{(t)}_{i}\sim\mathcal{D}_{f_{i}}
and
\zeta^{(t)}_{i}\sim\mathcal{D}_{g_{i}}
, it approximates

\displaystyle{D}^{(t)}_{x,i}(x_{i}^{(t)},y_{i}^{(t)},z_{i}^{(t)})

\displaystyle\approx\nabla_{1}F(x_{i}^{(t)},y_{i}^{(t)};\xi^{(t)}_{i})-\nabla^{2}_{12}G(x_{i}^{(t)},y_{i}^{(t)};\zeta^{(t)}_{i})z_{i}^{(t)},

(10a)

\displaystyle D^{(t)}_{y,i}(x_{i}^{(t)},y_{i}^{(t)},z_{i}^{(t)})

\displaystyle\approx\nabla_{2}G(x_{i}^{(t)},y_{i}^{(t)};\zeta^{(t)}_{i}),

(10b)

\displaystyle D^{(t)}_{z,i}(x_{i}^{(t)},y_{i}^{(t)},z_{i}^{(t)})

\displaystyle\approx\nabla^{2}_{22}G(x_{i}^{(t)},y_{i}^{(t)};\zeta^{(t)}_{i})z_{i}^{(t)}-\nabla_{2}F(x_{i}^{(t)},y_{i}^{(t)};\xi^{(t)}_{i}).

(10c)

The detailed implementation of
D-SOBA-SO
is listed in Algorithm

x_{i}
in Algorithm

. As shown in
(
Chen et al. 2023b
)
, the moving average step enables a more stable and finer-grained direction for estimating hypergradients. This strategy plays a crucial role in reducing the order of bias from sample noise in the convergence analysis, as well as relaxing the technical assumptions. For notational simplicity in the proof, we introduce a scaling parameter
\tau
, such that the upper-level step size is given by
\tau\alpha

D-SOBA-FO Algorithm.
In high-dimensional bilevel optimization, storing and computing the second-order matrices
\nabla^{2}_{12}g_{i}(x^{(t)},y^{(t)})
and
\nabla^{2}_{22}g_{i}(x^{(t)},y^{(t)})
is highly expensive, particularly in large-scale machine learning applications, such as data reweighting in large language models
(
Pan et al. 2024
)
. Finite difference approximation is an efficient approach for estimating higher-order gradients using lower-order information
(
Allen-Zhu and Li 2018
;
Carmon et al. 2018
;
Yang et al. 2023a
)
. Here, we introduce finite difference Hessian/Jacobian-vector product approximations to D-SOBA. Given the Lipschitz continuity of
\nabla_{12}^{2}g_{i}
under Assumption
1
, we obtain the following approximation for some perturbation
\delta_{t}>0
:

\displaystyle\nabla_{1}g_{i}(x_{i}^{(t)},y_{i}^{(t)}+\delta_{t}z_{i}^{(t)})=\nabla_{1}g_{i}(x_{i}^{(t)},y_{i}^{(t)})+\delta_{t}\nabla_{12}^{2}g_{i}(x_{i}^{(t)},y_{i}^{(t)})z_{i}^{(t)}+\mathcal{O}\left(\delta_{t}^{2}\|z_{i}^{(t)}\|^{2}\right),

(11a)

\displaystyle\nabla_{1}g_{i}(x_{i}^{(t)},y_{i}^{(t)}-\delta_{t}z_{i}^{(t)})=\nabla_{1}g_{i}(x_{i}^{(t)},y_{i}^{(t)})-\delta_{t}\nabla_{12}^{2}g_{i}(x_{i}^{(t)},y_{i}^{(t)})z_{i}^{(t)}+\mathcal{O}\left(\delta_{t}^{2}\|z_{i}^{(t)}\|^{2}\right).

(11b)

Thus, the term
(2\delta_{t})^{-1}(\nabla_{1}g_{i}(x_{i}^{(t)},y_{i}^{(t)}+\delta_{t}z_{i}^{(t)})-\nabla_{1}g_{i}(x_{i}^{(t)},y_{i}^{(t)}-\delta_{t}z_{i}^{(t)}))
provides an approximation of the Jacobian-vector product
\nabla_{12}^{2}g_{i}(x_{i}^{(t)},y_{i}^{(t)})z_{i}^{(t)}
, particularly when
\delta_{t}
is small:

\displaystyle\dfrac{1}{2\delta_{t}}(\nabla_{1}g_{i}(x_{i}^{(t)},y_{i}^{(t)}+\delta_{t}z_{i}^{(t)})-\nabla_{1}g_{i}(x_{i}^{(t)},y_{i}^{(t)}-\delta_{t}z_{i}^{(t)}))

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\nabla_{12}^{2}g_{i}(x_{i}^{(t)},y_{i}^{(t)})z_{i}^{(t)}+\mathcal{O}\left(\delta_{t}\|z_{i}^{(t)}\|^{2}\right)\approx\nabla_{12}^{2}g_{i}(x_{i}^{(t)},y_{i}^{(t)})z_{i}^{(t)}.

Similarly,
\nabla_{22}^{2}g_{i}(x_{i}^{(t)},y_{i}^{(t)})z_{i}^{(t)}
can also be evaluated by first-order gradients as follows:

\displaystyle\dfrac{1}{2\delta_{t}}(\nabla_{2}g_{i}(x_{i}^{(t)},y_{i}^{(t)}+\delta_{t}z_{i}^{(t)})-\nabla_{2}g_{i}(x_{i}^{(t)},y_{i}^{(t)}-\delta_{t}z_{i}^{(t)}))

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\nabla_{22}^{2}g_{i}(x_{i}^{(t)},y_{i}^{(t)})z_{i}^{(t)}+\mathcal{O}\left(\delta_{t}\|z_{i}^{(t)}\|^{2}\right)\approx\nabla_{22}^{2}g_{i}(x_{i}^{(t)},y_{i}^{(t)})z_{i}^{(t)}.

By replacing the true gradients in the above expressions with stochastic approximations, we introduce
D-SOBA-FO
, which enables Hessian/Jacobian evaluation while requiring only first-order gradients. See Algorithm
1
for details.

4
Convergence Analysis

This section establishes the convergence guarantees for D-SOBA-SO and D-SOBA-FO. We define the notation
\mathcal{F}^{(t)}=\sigma\left[\bigcup_{\tau=0}^{t}\left(\bigcup_{i=1}^{N}\{x^{(\tau)}_{i},y^{(\tau)}_{i},z^{(\tau)}_{i},h^{(\tau)}_{i}\}\cup\{\alpha_{\tau},\beta_{\tau},\gamma_{\tau},\theta_{\tau},\delta_{\tau}\}\right)\right]
to represent the filtration of the
t
-th iteration, i.e., the
\sigma
-field generated by all elements with superscripts within the first
t
iterations. Additionally, we denote the conditional expectation with respect to
\mathcal{F}^{(t)}
as
\mathbb{E}_{t}[\cdot]:=\mathbb{E}[\cdot|\mathcal{F}^{(t)}]
. The following lemma quantifies the error introduced by the finite-difference approximation in D-SOBA-FO:

Proposition 0
.

Suppose Assumptions
1
and
3
hold. Then, the terms
p_{H,i}^{(t)}
and
p_{J,i}^{(t)}
obtained from D-SOBA-FO satisfy (see proof in Lemma
8
):

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{t}\left\|p_{H,i}^{(t)}-\mathbb{E}_{t}[p_{H,i}^{(t)}]\right\|^{2}\leq\sigma^{2}\left\|z_{i}^{(t)}\right\|^{2},\quad\left\|\mathbb{E}_{t}[p_{H,i}^{(t)}]-\nabla_{22}g_{i}(x_{i}^{(t)},y_{i}^{(t)})z_{i}^{(t)}\right\|^{2}\leq\iota^{2};

(12a)

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{t}\left\|p_{J,i}^{(t)}-\mathbb{E}_{t}[p_{J,i}^{(t)}]\right\|^{2}\leq\sigma^{2}\left\|z_{i}^{(t)}\right\|^{2},\quad\left\|\mathbb{E}_{t}[p_{J,i}^{(t)}]-\nabla_{12}g_{i}(x_{i}^{(t)},y_{i}^{(t)})z_{i}^{(t)}\right\|^{2}\leq\iota^{2},

(12b)

where
\iota^{2}=\dfrac{1}{3}L^{2}_{\nabla^{2}g}\delta_{t}^{2}\left\|z_{i}^{(t)}\right\|^{4}
.

Proposition
3
demonstrates that the error in the finite-difference Hessian/Jacobian-vector approximation is primarily dominated by sample noise when
\delta_{t}
is sufficiently small.

We then present the unified convergence results for both D-SOBA-SO and D-SOBA-FO, stated in the following theorem:

Theorem 1
.

Suppose Assumptions
1
,
2
,
3
, and
4
hold. If
\alpha=\Theta(\sqrt{N/T})
and
\tau=\Theta(\kappa^{-4})
, then there exist constants
c_{1},c_{2},c_{3}>0
such that, for all
0\leq t<T
, setting
\alpha_{t}\equiv\alpha
,
\beta_{t}\equiv c_{1}\alpha_{t}
,
\gamma_{t}\equiv c_{2}\alpha_{t}
, and
\theta_{t}\equiv c_{3}\alpha_{t}
, the iterates
\{\bar{x}^{(t)}\}_{t\geq 0}
generated by Algorithm
1
(including both D-SOBA-SO and D-SOBA-FO) satisfy (see proof in Appendix
A
):

\displaystyle\ \dfrac{1}{T}\sum_{t=0}^{T-1}\mathbb{E}\left[\left\|\nabla\Phi(\bar{x}^{(t)})\right\|^{2}\right]

\displaystyle\lesssim

\displaystyle\ \underbrace{\dfrac{\kappa^{5}}{\sqrt{NT}}}_{\mathrm{asymptotic\ rate}}+\quad\underbrace{\dfrac{\rho^{\frac{2}{3}}\kappa^{6}}{(1-\rho)^{\frac{1}{3}}T^{\frac{2}{3}}}+\dfrac{\rho^{\frac{2}{3}}b^{\frac{2}{3}}\kappa^{6}}{(1-\rho)^{\frac{2}{3}}T^{\frac{2}{3}}}+\dfrac{\rho\kappa^{6}}{(1-\rho)T}+\dfrac{\kappa^{13}}{T}+\dfrac{\kappa^{7}}{NT}}_{\mathrm{extra\ overhead}},

(13)

where
\kappa:=\max\{L_{f},L_{\nabla f},L_{\nabla g},L_{\nabla^{2}g}\}/\mu_{g}
denotes the condition number.

D-SOBA-SO and D-SOBA-FO achieve the same convergence rate.
According to Theorem
1
, D-SOBA-SO and D-SOBA-FO achieve the same convergence rate. Moreover, Proposition
3
shows that the error introduced by the finite difference approximation is negligible in the overall error, implying that first-order hyper-gradient evaluation does not substantially impact convergence. To the best of our knowledge, this is the first result demonstrating that fully first-order decentralized bilevel algorithms can achieve a convergence rate of the same order as their second-order counterparts.

Asymptotic linear speedup.
An algorithm is said to achieve
linear speedup
if the term
1/\sqrt{NT}
dominates the convergence rate
(
Koloskova et al. 2020
;
Lian et al. 2017a
)
as
T
becomes sufficiently large. In this regime, the number of iterations required to reach an
\varepsilon
-stationary solution is
1/(N\varepsilon^{2})
, decreasing linearly with the number of computing nodes. As shown in (
1
), D-SOBA eventually attains linear speedup, whereas the algorithms in
(
Chen et al. 2022
;
Chen et al. 2023a
)
achieve only a significantly slower asymptotic rate of
1/\sqrt{T}
. Furthermore, the algorithm that rely solely on first-order gradients proposed in
(
Wang et al. 2024
)
achieve a complexity of
1/(N\epsilon^{7})
, which is worse than that of the proposed D-SOBA-FO.

Transient iteration complexity.
While D-SOBA attains the asymptotic rate of
1/(NT)
as
T\to\infty
, it undergoes additional transient iterations, quantified by the extra overhead terms in (
1
), before reaching this rate. Transient iteration complexity
(
Pu et al. 2021
)
refers to the number of iterations an algorithm has to experience before reaching its asymptotic linear-speedup stage, that is, iterations
1,\cdots,T
where
T
is relatively small so that non-
NT
terms still dominate the rate. Here, the smoothness coefficients as well as the conditional number
\kappa
are viewed as constants in the transient analysis since we mainly consider the influence of
N,\rho,b
in transient complexity analysis. Transient iteration complexity measures the non-asymptotic stage in decentralized stochastic algorithms, see Fig.
1
. With the explicit asymptotic rate and extra overhead characterized in (
1
), we establish the transient iteration complexity of D-SOBA algorithms.

Corollary 0

(
transient iteration complexity)
.

Under the same assumptions as in Theorem
1
, the transient iteration complexity of D-SOBA is (Proof in Appendix
A.8
)

\displaystyle\mathcal{O}\left(\max\left\{\frac{N^{3}}{(1-\rho)^{2}},\frac{N^{3}b^{2}}{(1-\rho)^{4}}\right\}\right).

(14)

Many existing works, such as
(
Chen et al. 2022
;
Chen et al. 2023a
;
Lu et al. 2022a
;
Wang et al. 2024
)
, fail to establish the transient iteration complexity as their analysis ignores non-dominant convergence terms. Transient iteration complexity is crucial for differentiating between various decentralized bilevel algorithms. While
(

)

(

)
achieve nearly the same asymptotic complexity as D-SOBA (differing by a factor of

), their transient complexity is worse than that of D-SOBA, especially when the data heterogeneity

is small and the network topology is sparse (i.e.,

). This addresses
Open Question Q1
listed in Section
1.2
.

Joint influence of graph and heterogeneity
. To our knowledge, Corollary
4
presents the first result that
quantifies how network topology and data heterogeneity
jointly affect the non-asymptotic convergence in decentralized SBO. First, expression (
14
) reveals that a sparse topology with
1-\rho\to 0
can significantly amplify the influence of data heterogeneity
b^{2}
. Second, a large data heterogeneity
b^{2}
also exacerbates the adverse impact of sparse topologies from
{\mathcal{O}}((1-\rho)^{-2})
to
{\mathcal{O}}((1-\rho)^{-4})
. Prior to our work, no results have clarified the interplay between network topology and data heterogeneity. Furthermore, expression (
14
) quantifies the benefits that can be achieved by enhancing network connectivity and reducing data heterogeneity. For instance, if the influence of data heterogeneity
b^{2}
can be eliminated, the transient complexity can be improved from
{\mathcal{O}}((1-\rho)^{-4})
to
{\mathcal{O}}((1-\rho)^{-2})
. This addresses
Open Question Q2
and motivates recent efforts
(
Zhu et al. 2024
)
to develop more effective decentralized bilevel algorithms that mitigate the influence of data heterogeneity by leveraging advanced methods such as EXTRA

)

)

Comparison with single-level DSGD
. Our comprehensive convergence analysis enables the first detailed comparison between bilevel and single-level optimization. We find that both the asymptotic rate and transient iteration complexity of D-SOBA are identical to those of single-level decentralized SGD
(
Chen et al. 2021b
)
with respect to network size

fail to explicitly capture the influence of

6
Conclusion and Limitations

This paper introduces D-SOBA-SO and D-SOBA-FO, two single-loop decentralized SBO algorithms that achieve state-of-the-art asymptotic convergence rate and gradient/Hessian complexity under more relaxed assumptions compared to existing methods. Especially, D-SOBA-FO evaluates the Hessian/Jacobian-vector only by first-order gradients. We also clarify, for the first time, the joint influence of network topology and data heterogeneity on bilevel algorithms through an analysis of transient iteration complexity. Nevertheless, D-SOBA relies on the assumption of bounded gradient dissimilarity, which might be violated when local distribution
{\mathcal{D}}_{f_{i}}
or
{\mathcal{D}}_{g_{i}}
differs drastically across nodes.

{proof}
