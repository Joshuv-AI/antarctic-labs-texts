Regularity of the law of solutions to the stochastic heat equation with non-Lipschitz reaction term
Michael Salins and Samy Tindel

Abstract
. We prove the existence of density for the solution to the multiplicative semilinear stochastic heat equation on an unbounded spatial domain, with drift term satisfying a half-Lipschitz type condition. The methodology is based on a careful analysis of differentiability for a map defined on weighted functional spaces.



1.
Introduction

In this paper we consider a stochastic heat equation on
{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
of the form

(1)

\frac{\partial u}{\partial t}(t,x)=\frac{1}{2}\Delta u(t,x)+f(u(t,x))+\sigma(u(t,x))\,\dot{W}(t,x)\,,\qquad t\in[0,T],\,x\in{\mathbb{R}}^{d}.

In (
1
),
\Delta
denotes the Laplace operator,
\dot{W}
is a centered Gaussian noise which is white in time and whose covariance function satisfies a standard assumption called strong Dalang condition (see Assumption
2.2
below for a precise statement). The coefficient
\sigma
in (
1
) is supposed to be differentiable with bounded derivative. Our equation deviates from the standard setting for stochastic
pde
s due to the drift coefficient
f
. This coefficient is only assumed to verify a mild damping condition, that is we suppose that
f
is continuously differentiable and that
f^{\prime}
is upper bounded by a constant
\kappa\in{\mathbb{R}}
:

(2)

f^{\prime}(u)\leq\kappa,\quad\text{ for all }\quad u\in\mathbb{R}.

This condition will be referred to as half-Lipschitz in the sequel. As a motivating example, any odd degree polynomial with a negative leading coefficient such as
f(u)=-u^{3}+u
will satisfy (
2
).
Under this setting, we investigate the law of the random field mild solution to (
1
),
u(t,x)
, at a fixed time
t>0
and a fixed point in space
x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
. We prove using Malliavin calculus that the law has a density.

Stochastic
pde
s have been primarily considered for globally Lipschitz continuous coefficients
f
and
\sigma
(see e.g
[
7
,
26
]
). However, since multiple relevant physical systems involve polynomial type nonlinearities, a substantial amount of effort has been devoted to that case over the past decades. Among those contributions, one can single out the following:

(a)

The case of
x\in D
(with
D
a bounded domain in
{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
), and polynomial nonlinearities
f
with negative leading terms, has been investigated in
[
5
,
16
]
. The techniques use localization arguments based on stopping time methods and a priori bounds. The papers
[
5
,
16
]
are all handling the case of a colored noise
\dot{W}
which can accommodate stochastic integrals without a need for renormalization.

(b)

The case of a stochastic heat equation defined on an unbounded spatial domain
\mathbb{R}^{d}
with
f
satisfying
(
2
) and with at most polynomial growth was first investigated independently by Iwata
[
13
]
and Brzezniak and Peszat
[
3
]
. Unlike in the bounded domain setting, the solutions to (
1
) are unbounded in space if
\sigma
is bounded away from zero. Specifically, for any
t>0
,
{\mathbb{P}}(\sup_{x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}}|u(t,x)|=+\infty)=1
.

(c)

The variational methods of Röckner and collaborators can be applied when the perturbing noise is trace-class, so that Itô formula methods are available
[
18
,
23
,
15
]
. This theory allows for
spde
s that are not semilinear, such as porous medium equations, but precludes rough perturbations like space-time white noise.

(d)

In case of a space-time white noise
\dot{W}
(or even a spatial white noise if
x\in{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
with
d\geq 2
), renormalization tools are in order. We cannot list all the relevant contributions in this direction. Let us just mention
[
11
]
for the celebrated
kpz
equation and
[
21
]
for the
\Phi_{4}^{3}
model. Notice that most of those systems only admit an additive noise, and that the current techniques only yield local (in time) solutions.

Studies of densities for stochastic processes in non-Markovian settings have been one of the great achievements of Malliavin calculus. However, due to a methodology based on differentiation and integration by parts, Malliavin calculus results usually require smooth and bounded coefficients in differential systems like (
1
). This is certainly the case in classical references concerning stochastic
pde
s
[
2
,
19
,
20
]
or systems driven by a fractional Brownian motion
[
1
,
6
]
. A more recent trend has been to adapt the integration by parts technology to settings with little regularity or less restrictive growth assumptions. One can quote the following studies, which are close in spirit to our own contribution:

(a)

The article
[
12
]
deals with a stochastic differential equation driven by an additive Brownian motion, whose drift coefficient lies in a fractional Sobolev space of the form
W^{\gamma,p}
(with a regularity parameter
\gamma\in(0,1]
). The computations therein combine Malliavin calculus and Girsanov transform tools.

(b)

For stochastic differential equations driven by a fractional Brownian motion let us mention the paper
[
9
]
, which handles the case of a Hölder drift. This is achieved thanks to a smart limiting procedure taken on Euler schemes. More recently, the preprint
[
14
]
explores densities for a drift coefficient
f
which has linear growth and satisfies a mild damping condition. The main tools in
[
14
]
is Girsanov’s transform, again due to the fact that an equation with additive noise is considered. The density is then analyzed by importing arguments from the regularization by noise literature and investigating a functional for a fractional bridge.

(c)

In
[
16
,
17
]
the authors consider a
spde
of the form (
1
), satisfying an assumption which is similar to (
2
). The main difference between this setting and ours is twofold: first
[
16
,
17
]
focuses on spatial variables in bounded sets of
{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
, while our result is concerned with
x
in the whole space
{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
. Then
[
16
,
17
]
is restricted to coefficients
f
in (
1
) having polynomial growth, while we can reach exponential growth in the current paper. Notice that in
[
17
]
the strategy is based on a localization procedure relying on Lipschitz approximations of the drift coefficient
f
. This method is ruled out in our unbounded domain setting. Indeed, in our case the field
\{u(t,x);\,x\in{\mathbb{R}}^{d}\}
is unbounded for any fixed
t>0
, even if
f
is Lipschitz. The boundedness of
u(t,\cdot)
whenever
f
is Lipschitz was a crucial ingredient in
[
17
]
.

As one can see, our result is thus the first one establishing existence of density for a
spde
with drift whose first derivative is unbounded and that is defined on a noncompact domain. On top of this novel aspect, we believe that our method of proof is applicable to other settings. In some subsequent publication we plan to apply the techniques developed here to the renormalized frameworks mentioned above.

In future work, we also wish to remove the growth restriction on
f
. While many previous works restricted the growth rate of
f
to polynomial growth like
|f(u)|\leq C(1+|u|^{p})

[
13
,
3
,
5
,
15
]
, we allow
f
to grow as fast as
|f(u)|\leq Ke^{K|u|^{\nu}}
for any
K,\nu>0
like in
[
24
]
. The exponential growth restriction is helpful for proving that the integrals
\int_{0}^{t}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}G(t-s,x-y)f(u(s,y))dyds
in the mild solution are well-defined. These growth restrictions do not seem necessary, however, and in future work we hope to prove that the half-Lipschitz condition on
f
(
2
) along with appropriate assumptions on
\sigma
and the
\dot{W}
, is sufficient to guarantee the existence and uniqueness of mild solutions and the existence of a density. Such a generalization requires sensitive analysis of the spatial growth rates of solutions and is outside of the scope of the current manuscript.

As mentioned above, the solutions to (
1
) with
x\in{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
are unbounded and heat equations enjoy infinite propagation speed. Therefore the localization arguments that are invoked in the bounded domain case
[
16
,
17
]
cannot be applied to the unbounded domain setting. To investigate properties of unbounded solutions, many researchers have introduced a spatial weight. For example, Iwata
[
13
]
and Brzezniak and Peszat
[
3
]
used exponential weights
\sup_{x}e^{-\lambda|x|}|u(t,x)|
. The choice of exponential weights, unfortunately, introduced a polynomial growth restriction in the literature.
With this observation in mind, the first author of this paper proposed in
[
24
]
a new method to handle equations like (
1
).
Roughly speaking, in this paper and in
[
24
]
we use polynomial weights
\sup_{x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}}\frac{|u(t,x)|}{1+|x-x_{0}|^{\theta}}
for arbitrarily small
\theta>0
. This choice of weights allows to prove the main results for superlinear half-Lipschitz reaction terms that grow as fast as
|f(u)|\leq K\exp(K|u|^{\gamma})
for any
K,\gamma>0
.

In order to explain how we obtained the existence of a density for the solution to (
1
), let us give a few details about the approach in
[
24
]
.
The scheme therein basically splits the dynamics in two pieces: first a stochastic map
\mathcal{I}
defined for a random field
\varphi
by

\mathcal{I}(t,x)=\mathcal{I}^{\varphi}(t,x)=\int_{0}^{t}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}G(t-s,x-y)\varphi(s,y)W(ds\,dy)\,,

where
G(t,x):=(2\pi)^{-\frac{d}{2}}\exp(-|x|^{2}/2)
is the standard Gaussian heat kernel.
This map is properly introduced in (
98
) below. The second piece of our dynamics is a deterministic map called
\mathcal{M}
(see Definition
2.9
) given for a continuous function
z
defined on
[0,T]\times{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
as the solution of the following integral equation

(3)

\mathcal{M}(z)(t,x)=\int_{0}^{t}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}G(t-s,x-y)f(\mathcal{M}(z)(s,y))\,dyds+z(t,x).

The crucial point in
[
24
]
is that, despite the fact that
f
in (
3
) in not globally Lipschitz continuous, the map
\mathcal{M}
is globally Lipschitz continuous on weighted spaces of continuous functions on
[0,T]\times{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
. Thanks to some thorough estimates for both
\mathcal{I}
and
\mathcal{M}
and a Yosida type approximation procedure for the function
f
, one can prove existence and uniqueness for mild solutions to (
1
).
More specifically, the
mild solution
of (
1
) is defined to be a random field that solves the integral equation

\displaystyle u(t,x)=

\displaystyle\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}G(t-s,x-y)u(0,y)dy+\int_{0}^{t}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}G(t-s,x-y)f(u(s,y))dyds

(4)

\displaystyle+\int_{0}^{t}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}G(t-s,x-y)\sigma(u(s,y))W(ds\,dy).

Letting
U_{0}(t,x)=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}G(t-s,x-y)u(0,y)dy
and
\mathcal{I}^{\varphi}(t,x)
and
\mathcal{M}
be the maps defined above, equation (
1
) can be recast as

(5)

u(t,x)=\mathcal{M}\left(U_{0}+\mathcal{I}^{\sigma(u)}\right)(t,x).

The existence and uniqueness of the solution to this equation was then established in
[
24
]
via a Picard iteration scheme. Namely, we can recursively define

(6)

u_{0}(t,x)=U_{0}(t,x),\ \ \ \ \ u_{n+1}=\mathcal{M}(U_{0}+\mathcal{I}^{\sigma(u_{n})}).

By properly bounding both maps
\mathcal{I}^{\varphi}
and
\mathcal{M}
, the existence and uniqueness for equation (
1
) is proved thanks to a fixed point argument.

We can now explain our global method for the existence of density result and outline the structure of our paper.
First in Section
2
we introduce the main assumptions and recall the existence and uniqueness results for (
1
) from
[
24
]
, as well as Malliavin calculus results from
[
20
]
.
Then we proceed to prove the Malliavin differentiability of
u(t,x)
via the approximation scheme (
6
), and also by studying the Malliavin differentiability of the maps
\mathcal{I}^{\varphi}
and
\mathcal{M}
. To begin with, Section
3
proves Proposition
3.1
. This result states that if a random field
z:[0,T]\times\mathbb{R}^{d}\times\Omega\to\mathbb{R}
has the property that
z(t,x)
is Malliavin differentiable for any
(t,x)\in[0,T]\times\mathbb{R}^{d}
and additionally that
z(t,x)
and
Dz(t,x)
satisfy certain polynomial growth assumptions in the spatial variable, then the random field
\mathcal{M}(z)(t,x)
is also Malliavin differentiable for any
(t,x)\in[0,T]\times\mathbb{R}^{d}
. Furthermore, Proposition
3.1
establishes a weighted supremum norm bound which holds with probability one:

(7)

\sup_{t\in[0,T]}\sup_{x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}}\frac{|D\mathcal{M}(z)(t,x)|_{{\mathcal{H}}_{T}}}{1+|x-x_{0}|^{\theta}}\leq K\sup_{t\in[0,T]}\sup_{x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}}\frac{|Dz(t,x)|_{{\mathcal{H}}_{T}}}{1+|x-x_{0}|^{\theta}}\,,

where
\mathcal{H}_{T}
is the natural Cameron-Martin space related to our colored noise (see (
53
) below for a proper definition of the inner product in
\mathcal{H}_{T}
).

The Malliavin differentiability of the stochastic integrals
\mathcal{I}^{\varphi}
when
\varphi
is Malliavin differentiable is a standard result from the theory of Malliavin calculus (see Proposition
2.16
below). In Section
4
we prove that certain moment estimates of weighted supremum norms, that applied to stochastic integrals
\mathcal{I}^{\varphi}(t,x)
when
\varphi
is real-valued, will also hold in the case where
\varphi
is Hilbert-space valued. Specifically, we apply these results to derive estimates on the Malliavin derivatives of the stochastic integral terms.
In Section
4.2
we apply these Malliavin differentiability results about
\mathcal{I}^{\varphi}
and
\mathcal{M}
to the recursively defined Picard iteration scheme introduced in (
6
). In particular, this allows us to prove that
u_{n}(t,x)
is Malliavin differentiable for all
n\in\mathbb{N}
,
t\in[0,T]
, and
x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
. Furthermore, we prove that for any
p>0
and
T>0
,

(8)

\sup_{n}\sup_{x_{0}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}}{\mathbb{E}}\left|\sup_{t\in[0,T]}\sup_{x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}}\frac{|Du_{n}(t,x)|_{{\mathcal{H}}_{T}}}{1+|x-x_{0}|^{\theta}}\right|^{p}<+\infty\,.

In particular, these weighted supremum moment bounds guarantee that
\sup_{n}E|Du_{n}(t,x)|_{{\mathcal{H}}_{T}}^{2}
is finite for any fixed
t>0
and
x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
. The classical result
[
20
, Lemma 1.2.3]
then guarantees that
u(t,x)
is Malliavin differentiable.

Finally, in Section
5
we prove that the Malliavin derivative of the mild solution is positive almost surely, that is
{\mathbb{P}}(\|Du(t,x)\|_{{\mathcal{H}}_{T}}>0)=1
. By
[
20
, Theorem 2.1.2]
, this positivity property implies that the law of
u(t,x)
is absolutely continuous with respect to Lebesgue measure. We prove the positivity by constructing a particular family of deterministic test functions
h_{t,x,\delta}\in{\mathcal{H}}_{T}
and proving that, with probability one, the directional Malliavin derivative
D_{h_{t},x,\delta}u(t,x)
is non-negative for some small (and random)
\delta>0
. This analysis involves writing the directional derivatives in a mild form

D_{h}u(t,x)=\left<\phi_{t,x},h\right>_{{\mathcal{H}}_{T}}+A_{h}(t,x)+B_{h}(t,x)\,,

where
A_{h}(t,x)
is a Lebesgue integral and
B_{h}(t,x)
is a stochastic integral. We prove that when
\delta
is sufficiently small, the integral terms are much smaller than the leading term, implying that the directional Malliavin derivative is non-negative.

2.
Approach to existence and uniqueness

In this section we will summarize the method employed in
[
24
]
in order to solve an equation like (
1
) with a half-Lipschitz reaction term. The method is based on a fixed point argument in an appropriate weighted Hölder space. We also include a minimal set of Malliavin calculus tools necessary to carry out our main computations.

2.1.
Functional space, assumptions and existence result

We start by defining the weighted function spaces which will be used throughout the paper.

Definition 2.1
.

Let
\theta>0
be a positive parameter and let
x_{0}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
. The space
\mathcal{C}_{\theta,x_{0}}([0,T]\times\mathbb{R}^{d})
designates the set of continuous functions

(9)

\left\{z\in\mathcal{C}([0,T]\times\mathbb{R}^{d}):\lim_{|x|\to\infty}\sup_{t\in[0,T]}\frac{|z(t,x)|}{1+|x-x_{0}|^{\theta}}=0\right\}.

The space is endowed with the weighted supremum norm

(10)

|z|_{\mathcal{C}_{\theta,x_{0}}([0,T]\times\mathbb{R}^{d})}:=\sup_{t\in[0,T]}\sup_{x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}}\frac{|z(t,x)|}{1+|x-x_{0}|^{\theta}}.

For fixed
\theta
, the spaces
\mathcal{C}_{\theta,x_{0}}([0,T]\times\mathbb{R}^{d})
all coincide, but it is convenient to use different centers of the weight
x_{0}
.

Next we state the assumptions on the stochastic noise
\dot{W}
. All of the random variables below are defined on a complete probability space
(\Omega,\mathcal{F},{\mathbb{P}})
equipped with a filtration
\{\mathcal{F}_{t}:t\geq 0\}
.

Assumption 2.2
.

The noise
\dot{W}
in (
1
) is a centered Gaussian spatially homogeneous noise which is white in time. There exists a positive and positive definite function
\Lambda
such that formally

(11)

{\mathbb{E}}[\dot{W}(t,x)\dot{W}(s,y)]=\delta(t-s)\Lambda(x-y).

In the above expression,
\delta
is the Dirac measure. The Fourier transform of
\Lambda
is a measure
\mu
and we assume that there exists
\eta\in(0,1)
such that

(12)

\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\frac{1}{1+|\xi|^{2(1-\eta)}}\mu(d\xi)<+\infty.

The multiplicative noise coefficient
\sigma
in (
1
) satisfies standard differentiability and nondegeneracy assumptions.

Assumption 2.3
.

The noise coefficient
\sigma:\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R}
is differentiable and its derivative is uniformly bounded. Moreover, we assume that there exists
\alpha>0
such that

(13)

\sigma(u)\geq\alpha,\quad\text{ for all }\quad u\in\mathbb{R}.

As mentioned in the introduction, our system (
1
) departs from the standard stochastic
pde
setting due to the drift coefficient
f
. Namely we only suppose that
f
in (
1
) satisfies a half-Lipschitz condition, is differentiable, and obeys a very mild growth condition. This is summarized in the assumption below.

Assumption 2.4
.

The reaction term
f:\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R}
is continuously differentiable. Moreover, there exists
\kappa\in\mathbb{R}
such that the derivative is uniformly bounded from above

(14)

f^{\prime}(u)\leq\kappa,\quad\text{ for all }\quad u\in\mathbb{R}.

We further assume that there exist
K>0
,
\nu>0
such that

(15)

|f^{\prime}(u)|\leq K\exp\left(K|u|^{\nu}\right)

Notice that the upper bound on the first derivative (
14
) implies that
f:\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R}
is
half-Lipschitz
, meaning that for any
u_{1}>u_{2}
,

(16)

f(u_{1})-f(u_{2})\leq\kappa(u_{1}-u_{2}).

We now label a standard assumption for the initial condition
u_{0}
for our equation of interest.

Assumption 2.5
.

The initial condition for (
1
) is continuous and uniformly bounded, meaning that there exists
M>0
such that

(17)

\sup_{x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}}|u_{0}(x)|\leq M.

Equation (
1
) is solved in the so-called mild sense. Namely an adapted process
u
is said to solve (
1
) with initial condition
u_{0}
if for all
(t,x)\in[0,T]\times\mathbb{R}^{d}
we have

\displaystyle u(t,x)=

\displaystyle\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{d}}G(t,x-y)u_{0}(y)dy+\int_{0}^{t}\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{d}}G(t-s,x-y)f(u(s,y))dyds

(18)

\displaystyle+\int_{0}^{t}\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{d}}G(t-s,x-y)\sigma(u(s,y))\,W(ds\,dy)\,,

where
G(t,x)
is the heat kernel related to the Laplace operator in (
1
). In (
2.1
), the stochastic integral is interpreted in the Itô sense (see
[
7
]
for further details).
With this notion of solution and under the set of assumptions spelled out above, we now recall the main existence and uniqueness result from
[
24
]
.

Theorem 2.6

(Theorem 2.6 of
[
24
]
)
.

Suppose Assumptions
2.3
–
2.5
are satisfied. Then there exists a unique mild solution to (
1
). This solution lives in the space
C_{\theta}([0,T]\times\mathbb{R}^{d})
introduced in Definition
2.1
.

2.2.
Methodology

In this section we review the methods used to solve (
1
) in
[
24
]
. Those tools will also play a prominent role in analyzing the Malliavin derivative of the solution.

2.2.1.
Yosida approximations

A crucial ingredient in the analysis of equation (
2.1
) is based on Yosida approximations for the nonlinear forcing term
f
satisfying Assumption
2.4
. That is
for any function
f
satisfying (
14
) or (
16
) it is easily seen (see
[
24
, Proposition 2.4]
) that

(19)

f(u)=\phi(u)+\kappa u\,,

where
\phi
is non-increasing.
For
\phi:\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R}
that is non-increasing, we define the Yosida approximations for
\lambda>0
by

(20)

\phi_{\lambda}(u):=\frac{1}{\lambda}(J_{\lambda}(u)-u)\text{ where }J_{\lambda}(u)=(I-\lambda\phi)^{-1}(u).

The family
\{\phi_{\lambda}:\lambda>0\}
is intended to be a smooth approximation of
\phi
under monotonicity conditions.
We now summarize some properties of the Yosida approximations, taken from
[
8
, Appendix D]
.

Lemma 2.7
.

Let
\phi:\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R}
be a differentiable non-increasing function and let
\{\phi_{\lambda}:\lambda>0\}
be its Yosida approximations defined by (
20
). Then the following are true.

(i)

|\phi_{\lambda}(u_{1})-\phi_{\lambda}(u_{2})|\leq\frac{2}{\lambda}|u_{1}-u_{2}|
, for
u_{1},u_{2}\in{\mathbb{R}}
and all
\lambda>0
.

(ii)

|\phi_{\lambda}(u)|\leq|\phi(u)|
, for
u\in{\mathbb{R}}
and all
\lambda>0
.

(iii)

u\mapsto\phi_{\lambda}(u)
is nonincreasing, for all
\lambda>0
.

(iv)

\lim_{\lambda\to 0}\phi_{\lambda}(u)=\phi(u)
, for all
u\in{\mathbb{R}}
.

(v)

\lim_{\lambda\to 0}\phi_{\lambda}^{\prime}(u)=\phi^{\prime}(u)
, for all
u\in{\mathbb{R}}
.

These properties of Yosida approximations are easily translated into approximations for the half-Lipschitz function
f
.

Lemma 2.8
.

Let
f
satisfy Assumption
2.4
so that
f
satisfies the decomposition (
19
). Define a family
\{f_{\lambda}:\lambda>0\}
by

(21)

f_{\lambda}(u)=\phi_{\lambda}(u)+\kappa u

where
\phi_{\lambda}
are Yosida approximations of the non-increasing function
\phi
. Then
f_{\lambda}
satisfies the following properties.

(i)

|f_{\lambda}(u_{1})-f_{\lambda}(u_{2})|\leq\left(\frac{2}{\lambda}+\kappa\right)|u_{1}-u_{2}|
, for
u_{1},u_{2}\in{\mathbb{R}}
and all
\lambda>0
.

(ii)

|f_{\lambda}(u)|\leq(1+2\kappa)|f(u)|
, for
u\in{\mathbb{R}}
and all
\lambda>0
.

(iii)

(f_{\lambda}(u_{1})-f_{\lambda}(u_{2}))\,{\textnormal{sign}}(u_{1}-u_{2})\leq\kappa|u_{1}-u_{2}|
, for
u_{1},u_{2}\in{\mathbb{R}}
and all
\lambda>0
.

(iv)

\lim_{\lambda\to 0}f_{\lambda}(u)=f(u)
, for all
u\in{\mathbb{R}}
.

(v)

\lim_{\lambda\to 0}f_{\lambda}^{\prime}(u)=f^{\prime}(u)
, for all
u\in{\mathbb{R}}
.

2.2.2.
Mapping
\mathcal{M}

The second ingredient we wish to highlight in the study of (
1
) is the introduction of a functional mapping
\mathcal{M}:\mathcal{C}_{\theta,x_{0}}([0,T]\times\mathbb{R}^{d})\to\mathcal{C}_{\theta,x_{0}}([0,T]\times\mathbb{R}^{d})
.

Definition 2.9
.

For a continuous function
z\in\mathcal{C}_{\theta,x_{0}}([0,T]\times\mathbb{R}^{d})
let
\mathcal{M}(z)
be the solution to the following equation

(22)

\mathcal{M}(z)(t,x)=\int_{0}^{t}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}G(t-s,x-y)f(\mathcal{M}(z)(s,y))\,dyds+z(t,x).

Remark 2.10
.

In order to prove existence of the map
\mathcal{M}
one uses an approximating sequence
\{\mathcal{M}_{\lambda};\,\lambda>0\}
defined as in (
22
), with
f
replaced by its Yosida approximation
f_{\lambda}
given in (
20
). Then some a priori estimates on
\mathcal{M}_{\lambda}(z)
are provided in
[
24
]

(i)

2.3.
Malliavin Calculus

This section is devoted to review some elementary notions of Malliavin calculus (mostly borrowed from
[
20
]
). We first recall that our noise
\dot{W}
is a Gaussian centered field whose covariance is formally given by (
11
). One can also look at
\dot{W}
as a centered Gaussian family
\{W(\varphi);\,\varphi\in\mathcal{H}_{T}\}
, where
\mathcal{H}_{T}
denotes the Hilbert space with inner product

(53)

\left<\phi,\psi\right>_{{\mathcal{H}}_{T}}=\int_{0}^{T}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\phi(t,y_{1})\psi(t,y_{2})\Lambda(y_{1}-y_{2})\,dy_{1}dy_{2}dt.

Let
\mathcal{S}
be the set of smooth and cylindrical random variables of the form

F=g\left(W(h_{1}),\ldots,W(h_{N})\right),

where
N\geq 1
,
g\in C_{b}^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{N})
and
h_{1},\ldots,h_{N}\in\mathcal{H}_{T}
. For every
\ell\in\mathcal{H}_{T}
, the partial Malliavin derivative of
F
in the direction of
\ell
is defined for
F\in\mathcal{S}
as the random variable

(54)

D_{\ell}F=\sum_{i}^{N}\frac{\partial g}{\partial x_{i}}\left(W(h_{1}),\ldots,W(h_{N})\right)\left\langle h_{i},\ell\right\rangle_{\mathcal{H}_{T}}.

Relation (
54
) can also be seen as an equation for
\langle DF,\,\ell\rangle_{\mathcal{H}_{T}}
, where
DF
is now a
\mathcal{H}_{T}
-valued random variable.
We can iterate this procedure to define higher order derivatives
D^{k}_{\ell_{1}\cdots\ell_{k}}F
, which produces a
\mathcal{H}^{\otimes k}
-valued random variable. For any
p\geq 1
and integer
k\geq 1
, we define the Sobolev space
\mathbb{D}^{k,p}
as the closure of
\mathcal{S}
with respect to the norm

(55)

\|F\|_{k,p}^{p}=\mathbb{E}[|F|^{p}]+\sum_{i=1}^{k}\mathbb{E}\left[\|D^{i}F\|^{p}_{\mathcal{H}^{\otimes l}}\right].

If
V
is Hilbert space,
\mathbb{D}^{k,p}(V)
denotes the corresponding Sobolev space of
V
-valued random variables.

The existence of a density for
u(t,x)
, the mild solution of (
1
), is obtained by appling the following criterion borrowed from
[
20
, Theorem 2.1.2]
.

Proposition 2.14
.

Let
F
be a real-valued random variable in
\mathbb{D}^{1,p}
for some
p>1
, such that
\|DF\|_{{\mathcal{H}}_{T}}>0
with probability one. Then the law of
F
is absolutely continuous with respect to the Lebesgue measure in
\mathbb{R}
.

When proceeding by approximations, we will rely on a technical result summarized below (see
[
20
, Lemma 1.2.3]
) in order to probe Malliavin differentiability.

Proposition 2.15
.

Let
\{F_{\lambda}:\lambda>0\}
be a sequence of random variables such that

(56)

F_{\lambda}\in\mathbb{D}^{1,2}\ \text{ for all $\lambda>0$,}\quad\lim_{\lambda\to 0}{\mathbb{E}}\left[|F_{\lambda}-F|^{2}\right]=0,\quad\sup_{\lambda}{\mathbb{E}}\left[\|DF_{\lambda}\|_{{\mathcal{H}}_{T}}^{2}\right]<+\infty.

Then
F\in\mathbb{D}^{1,2}
and the sequence
\{DF_{\lambda}:\lambda>0\}
converges weakly to
DF
in
L^{2}(\Omega:{\mathcal{H}}_{T})

.

We now state a differentiation rule for stochastic integrals that will be invoked to differentiate solutions to stochastic
pde

Proposition 2.16

(Section 1.3.1 of
[
20
]
)
.

Let
X
be an adapted random field in
\mathbb{D}^{1,2}(\mathcal{H}_{T})
, and define the Itô stochastic integral

\mathcal{J}_{T}(X)=\int_{0}^{T}\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{d}}X(s,x)\,W(ds\,dy).

Then
\mathcal{J}_{T}(X)

\mathbb{D}^{1,2}
, and for any

we have

D_{h}\mathcal{J}_{T}(X)=\left\langle X,\,h\right\rangle_{\mathcal{H}_{T}}+\int_{0}^{T}\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{d}}D_{h}X(s,x)\,W(ds\,dy).

The next result gives an easy to check condition that guarantees that a stochastic convolution with the fundamental solution to the heat equation satisfies the assumptions of Proposition
2.16
.

Proposition 2.17
.

Let
X
be an adapted random field such that for any
(t,x)\in[0,T]\times\mathbb{R}^{d}

(57)

\sup_{t\in[0,T]}\sup_{x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}}{\mathbb{E}}\left[|X(t,x)|^{2}\right]<+\infty,

(58)

\sup_{t\in[0,T]}\sup_{x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}}{\mathbb{E}}\left[|DX(t,x)|_{{\mathcal{H}}_{T}}^{2}\right]<+\infty.

Let

be the fundamental solution of the heat equation. Define the stochastic convolution

(59)

\mathcal{I}(t,x)=\mathcal{I}^{X}(t,x)=\int_{0}^{t}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}G(t-s,x-y)X(s,y)W(ds\,dy).

Then for any
(t,x)\in[0,T]\times\mathbb{R}^{d}

\mathcal{I}(t,x)
is an element of
\mathbb{D}^{1,2}
, and for any

(60)

D_{h}\mathcal{I}(t,x)=\left\langle G(t-\cdot,x-\cdot)X(\cdot,\cdot),\,h\right\rangle_{\mathcal{H}_{T}}+\int_{0}^{t}\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{d}}G(t-s,x-y)D_{h}X(s,y)\,W(ds\,dy).

In the above expression

is defined to be
0
is

Proof.

To apply Proposition

\displaystyle{\mathbb{E}}|G(t-\cdot,x-\cdot)X(\cdot,\cdot)|_{{\mathcal{H}}_{T}}^{2}

\displaystyle={\mathbb{E}}\int_{0}^{t}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}G(t-s,x-y_{1})G(t-s,x-y_{2})X(s,y_{1})X(s,y_{2})\Lambda(y_{1}-y_{2})dy_{1}dy_{2}ds

(61)

\displaystyle\leq C\left(\sup_{s\in[0,t]}\sup_{y\in\mathbb{R}^{d}}{\mathbb{E}}|X(s,y)|^{2}\right)Q_{\Lambda}(t)\,

where we have set

(62)

{\mathbb{E}}|G(t-\cdot,x-\cdot)DX(\cdot,\cdot)|_{{\mathcal{H}}_{T}}^{2}={\mathbb{E}}\int_{0}^{t}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}G(t-s,x-y_{1})G(t-s,x-y_{2})\\
\times\left<DX(s,y_{1}),DX(s,y_{2})\right>_{\mathcal{H}_{T}}\Lambda(y_{1}-y_{2})dy_{1}dy_{2}ds,

and therefore

{\mathbb{E}}|G(t-\cdot,x-\cdot)DX(\cdot,\cdot)|_{{\mathcal{H}}_{T}}^{2}\leq C\left(\sup_{s\in[0,t]}\sup_{y\in\mathbb{R}^{d}}{\mathbb{E}}|DX(s,y)|_{{\mathcal{H}}_{T}}^{2}\right)Q_{\Lambda}(t).

Now

Plugging this relation into (

{\mathbb{E}}\left|\sup_{t\in[0,T]}\sup_{x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}}\frac{|Dz(t,x)|_{{\mathcal{H}}_{T}}}{1+|x-x_{0}|^{\theta}}\right|^{2}<+\infty.

Let

in (

Lemma 3.2
.

Let

{\mathbb{E}}\Bigg|\sup_{t\in[0,T]}\sup_{x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}}\frac{|Dz(t,x)|_{{\mathcal{H}}_{T}}}{1+|x-x_{0}|^{\theta}}\Bigg|^{2}<+\infty,

\sup_{t\in[0,T]}\sup_{x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}}\frac{|D[\mathcal{M}(z)(t,x)]|_{{\mathcal{H}}_{T}}}{1+|x-x_{0}|^{\theta}}\leq K\sup_{t\in[0,T]}\sup_{x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}}\frac{|Dz(t,x)|_{{\mathcal{H}}_{T}}}{1+|x-x_{0}|^{\theta}}\,,

is also the Lipschitz constant of

Proof.

We define a sequence of functions

\displaystyle m_{1}(t,x)=z(t,x)

\displaystyle m_{n+1}(t,x)=\int_{0}^{t}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}G(t-s,x-y)f(m_{n}(s,y))dyds+z(t,x).

Standard arguments based on the Lipschitz continuity of

in the

Furthermore,

is Malliavin differentiable by assumption. Then by induction and using the fact that integration and Malliavin differentiability commute,

is Malliavin differentiable for all

Notice that in order to get (

is bounded. Let

. We also set

(70)

\Phi_{n}(t):=\sup_{x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}}\sup_{s\in[0,t]}\frac{|Dm_{n}(s,x)|_{{\mathcal{H}}_{T}}}{1+|x-x_{0}|^{\theta}}.

We assumed in the statement of the lemma that

Plugging (

\sup_{n}{\mathbb{E}}|Dm_{n}(t,x)|^{2}\leq c_{T,x}\,{\mathbb{E}}\left|\sup_{r\in[0,T]}\sup_{x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}}\frac{|Dz(r,x)|}{1+|x-x_{0}|^{\theta}}\right|^{2}<+\infty.

\displaystyle\leq CM^{2}\left(1+|x-x_{0}|^{\theta}\right)^{2}\int_{0}^{t}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}G(t-s,x-y_{1})G(t-s,x-y_{2})\Lambda(y_{1}-y_{2})dy_{1}dy_{2}

\displaystyle\qquad+CM^{2}\int_{0}^{t}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}(t-s)^{\theta}G((t-s)/2,x-y_{1})G((t-s)/2,x-y_{2})\Lambda(y_{1}-y_{2})dy_{1}dy_{2}.

(iii)

Finally, the fact that
A_{h_{t_{0},x_{0},\delta}}(t_{0},x_{0})
, and
B_{h_{t_{0},x_{0},\delta}}(t_{0},x_{0})
in (
137
)–(
138
) are integrals whose integrands have
L^{p}(\Omega)
norms proportional to
Q_{\Lambda}(\delta)
, but whose interval of integration are of size
\delta
, we can show that with probability one there exists a subsequence
\delta_{k}\downarrow 0
such that

(139)

\lim_{k\to\infty}\frac{|A_{h_{t_{0},x_{0},\delta_{k}}}(t_{0},x_{0})|+|B_{h_{t_{0},x_{0},\delta_{k}}}(t_{0},x_{0})|}{Q_{\Lambda}(\delta_{k})}=0,\quad\text{ with probability one.}

Putting together the 3 items above, one concludes that with probability one, there exists a small enough (random)
\delta_{k}=\delta_{k}(\omega)
such that

(140)

D_{h_{t_{0},x_{0},\delta_{k}}}u(t_{0},x_{0})=\left<\phi_{t,x},h_{t_{0},x_{0},\delta_{k}}\right>_{{\mathcal{H}}_{T}}+A_{h_{t_{0},x_{0},\delta_{k}}}(t_{0},x_{0})+B_{h_{t_{0},x_{0},\delta_{k}}}(t_{0},x_{0})>0.

This will prove (
130
) and therefore the existence of a density. The remainder of the section is devoted to detail the 3 steps above.

5.2.
Lower and upper bounds on
\left<\phi_{t,x},h_{t_{0},x_{0},\delta}\right>_{\mathcal{H}_{T}}

In this section we give details about item
(i)
above. Our findings are summarized in the following lemma.

Lemma 5.2
.

Let
t_{0}>0,x_{0}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
. For
\delta\in[0,t_{0}]
we recall the definition of
h_{t_{0},x_{0},\delta}
in (
136
):

(141)

h_{t_{0},x_{0},\delta}(r,z):=G(t_{0}-r,x_{0}-z)\mathbbm{1}_{[t_{0}-\delta,t_{0}]}(r).

Also recall that
\phi_{t,x}
is defined by (
132
) and
Q_{\Lambda}
is introduced in (
62
)–(
64
). Then we have the following lower and upper bounds for
\left<\phi_{t,x},h_{t_{0},x_{0},\delta}\right>_{\mathcal{H}_{T}}
.
For any
T>0,t_{0}\in[0,T],x_{0}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
and
\delta\in[0,t_{0}]

(142)

\left<\phi_{t_{0},x_{0}},h_{t_{0},x_{0},\delta}\right>_{\mathcal{H}_{T}}\geq\alpha\,Q_{\Lambda}(\delta)\,,\quad\text{ with probability one}\,

where
\alpha>0
is from (
13
).
In addition, there exists
C>0
such that for any
T>0,t_{0}\in[0,T],x_{0}\in\mathbb{R}^{d},y_{0}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
, and
\delta\in[0,t_{0}]
,

(143)

\sup_{t\in[t_{0}-\delta,t_{0}]}\sup_{x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}}\frac{|\left<\phi_{t,x},h_{t_{0},x_{0},\delta}\right>_{\mathcal{H}_{T}}|}{1+|x-y_{0}|^{\theta}}\leq CQ_{\Lambda}(\delta)\left(1+\sup_{s\in[t_{0}-\delta,t_{0}]}\sup_{y\in\mathbb{R}^{d}}\frac{|u(s,y)|}{1+|y-y_{0}|^{\theta}}\right).

Furthermore, if
t\in[0,t_{0}-\delta],x_{0}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
, and
x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
,

(144)

\left<\phi_{t,x},h_{t_{0},x_{0},\delta}\right>_{\mathcal{H}_{T}}=0.

Proof.

Recall the expressions (
132
) and (
141
) for
\phi_{t,x}
and
h_{t_{0},x_{0},\delta}
as well as the definition (
53
) of the inner product in
{\mathcal{H}}_{T}
. Then it is readily checked that

\displaystyle\left<\phi_{t,x},h_{t_{0},x_{0},\delta}\right>_{{\mathcal{H}}_{T}}

(145)

\displaystyle=\int_{t_{0}-\delta}^{t}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}G(t-s,x-y_{1})\sigma(u(s,y_{1}))G(t-s,x_{0}-y_{2})\Lambda(y_{1}-y_{2})dy_{1}dy_{2}ds,

for all
t\in[t_{0}-\delta,t_{0}]
.
We can now prove (
142
) in the following way: owing to the fact that
\sigma(u(s,y_{1}))>\alpha
(see Assumption
2.3
) and thanks to the positivity of
G
and
\Lambda
we obtain

\displaystyle\left<\phi_{t_{0},x_{0}},h_{t_{0},x_{0},\delta}\right>_{{\mathcal{H}}_{T}}

(146)

\displaystyle\geq\alpha\int_{t_{0}-\delta}^{t_{0}}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}G(t_{0}-s,x_{0}-y_{1})G(t_{0}-s,x_{0}-y_{2})\Lambda(y_{1}-y_{2})dy_{1}dy_{2}ds.

The last term in the above expression is
Q_{\Lambda}(\delta)
, which was defined in (
62
). We have thus obtained

(147)

\left<\phi_{t_{0},x_{0}},h_{t_{0},x_{0},\delta}\right>_{H_{T}}\geq\alpha Q_{\Lambda}(\delta),

that is (
142
) holds true.

The proof of (
143
) is a consequence of Lemma
4.3
.
Namely start from the expression (
5.2
) for the inner product

\langle\phi_{t_{0},x_{0}},h_{t_{0},x_{0},\delta}\rangle_{{\mathcal{H}}_{T}}
. Then notice that in the right hand side of (
5.2
), the time integrand goes from
t_{0}-\delta
to
t
. By the Cauchy Schwarz inequality, we get

(148)

\left\langle\phi_{t_{0},x_{0}},h_{t_{0},x_{0},\delta}\right\rangle_{H_{T}}\leq(R_{1}(t,x))^{\frac{1}{2}}(R_{2}(t))^{\frac{1}{2}}\,,

where we have set

(149)

R_{1}(t,x)=\int_{t_{0}-\delta}^{t}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}G(t-s,x-y_{1})G(t-s,x-y_{2})\\
\times\sigma(u(s,y_{1}))\sigma(u(s,y_{2}))\Lambda(y_{1}-y_{2})dy_{1}dy_{2}

and

(150)

R_{2}(t)=\int_{t_{0}-\delta}^{t}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}G(t-s,x_{0}-y_{1})G(t-s,x_{0}-y_{2})\Lambda(y_{1}-y_{2})dy_{1}dy_{2}\,.

The term
R_{2}(t)
in (
150
) is easily bounded above by

(151)

R_{2}(t)\leq Q_{\Lambda}(\delta)\,.

In fact
R_{2}(t)
would be exactly
Q_{\Lambda}(\delta)
if we had
t=t_{0}
. The term
R_{1}(t,x)
defined by (
149
) can be bounded using a time translated modification of Lemma
4.3
with
T=\delta
(the length of the time interval
[t_{0}-\delta,t_{0}]
) and
X(t,x)=\sigma(u(t,x)
. Specifically, we get

\sup_{t\in[t_{0}-\delta,t_{0}]}\sup_{x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}}\frac{R_{1}(t,x)}{(1+|x-x_{0}|^{\theta})^{2}}\leq CQ_{\Lambda}(\delta)\left(\sup_{t\in[t_{0}-\delta,t_{0}]}\sup_{x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}}\frac{|\sigma(u(t,x))|^{2}}{(1+|x-x_{0}|^{\theta})^{2}}\right).

Since
\sigma
has linear growth, we thus end up with

(152)

\sup_{t\in[t_{0}-\delta,t_{0}]}\sup_{x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}}\frac{R_{1}(t,x)}{(1+|x-x_{0}|^{\theta})^{2}}\leq CQ_{\Lambda}(\delta)\left(1+\sup_{t\in[t_{0}-\delta,t_{0}]}\sup_{x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}}\frac{|u(t,x)|^{2}}{(1+|x-x_{0}|^{\theta})^{2}}\right).

Then (
143
) follows by plugging (
151
) and (
152
) into (
148
).

Finally, if
t\in[0,t_{0}-\delta]
then the supports of
\phi_{t,x}
and
h_{t_{0},x_{0},\delta}
are disjoint and therefore relation (
144
) follows.
∎

5.3.
Upper bounds on moments of the derivative of
\mathbf{u(t,x)}

We have seen in (
113
) that the Malliavin derivative of
u(t,x)
is bounded in the
L^{p}
-sense for all
p>1
. Equation (
123
) can even be seen as a quantitative bound on this derivative. In the current section we push forward this analysis to derive an upper bound for the moments of the weighted supremum norms

(153)

\sup_{y_{0}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}}{\mathbb{E}}\sup_{t\in[t_{0}-\delta,t_{0}]}\sup_{x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}}\left|\frac{|D_{h_{t_{0},x_{0},\delta}}u(t,x)|}{1+|x-y_{0}|^{\theta}}\right|^{p}\,,

and to show that these are proportional to
(Q_{\Lambda}(\delta))^{p}
. As mentioned in our strategy Section
5.1
, this is the contents of item
(ii)
above.
We will use (
135
) along with Theorem
2.12
to prove these upper bounds. Our main estimate is contained in the following lemma.

Lemma 5.3
.

For
\delta>0
and
t_{0}\in(\delta,T]
, define a quantity
\mathcal{Q}_{1,\delta,p}
by

(154)

\mathcal{Q}_{1,\delta,p}=\sup_{y_{0}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}}{\mathbb{E}}\left|\sup_{t\in[t_{0}-\delta,t_{0}]}\sup_{x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}}\frac{|D_{h_{t_{0},x_{0},\delta}}u(t,x)|}{1+|x-y_{0}|^{\theta}}\right|^{p}.

Then under the conditions of Theorem
5.1
, there exist a constant
C>0
, depending on
p
and
\theta
, but not
\delta
, and a parameter
\delta_{0}>0
such that for all
\delta\in(0,\delta_{0})
,

(155)

\mathcal{Q}_{1,\delta,p}\leq C(Q_{\Lambda}(\delta))^{p}.

Proof.

First we recall our upper bounds on the weighted supremum norms of the stochastic integral
B_{h_{t_{0},x_{0},\delta}}(t,x)
, where we recall that

B_{h_{t_{0},x_{0},\delta}}(t,x)
is defined by (
138
). Notice that

B_{h_{t_{0},x_{0},\delta}}(t,x)=\mathcal{I}^{\varphi}(t,x),\quad\text{with}\quad\varphi(s,y)=\sigma^{\prime}(u(s,y))D_{h_{t_{0},x_{0},\delta}}u(s,y)\,,

where the notation
\mathcal{I}^{\varphi}
comes from (
59
). If both
p>1
and
\theta>0
are sufficiently large, then inequality (
103
) guarantees that there exist
C_{\delta,p,\theta}
such that
\lim_{\delta\to 0}C_{\delta,p,\theta}=0
and such that

(156)

\sup_{y_{0}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}}{\mathbb{E}}\left|\sup_{t\in[t_{0}-\delta,t_{0}]}\sup_{x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}}\frac{|B_{h_{t_{0},x_{0},\delta}}(t,x)|}{1+|x-y_{0}|^{\theta}}\right|^{p}\leq C_{\delta,p,\theta}\,\mathcal{Q}_{1,\delta,p}\,,

where we have just defined
\mathcal{Q}_{1,\delta,p}
in (
154
).
We know that the right-hand-side of (
156
) is finite because of Theorem
4.4
.
In addition,
because
D_{h_{t_{0},x_{0},\delta}}u=\mathcal{L}(\langle\phi,\,h_{t_{0},x_{0},\delta}\rangle_{{\mathcal{H}}_{T}}+B_{h_{t_{0},x_{0},\delta}})
, Theorem
2.12
guarantees that there exists
C>0
such that for all
\delta\in[0,1]
,

(157)

\sup_{y_{0}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}}{\mathbb{E}}\left|\sup_{t\in[t_{0}-\delta,t_{0}]}\sup_{x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}}\frac{|D_{h_{t_{0},x_{0},\delta}}u(t,x)|}{1+|x-y_{0}|^{\theta}}\right|^{p}\leq C\sup_{y_{0}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}}{\mathbb{E}}\left|\sup_{t\in[t_{0}-\delta,t_{0}]}\sup_{x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}}\frac{|\left<\phi_{t,x},h_{t_{0},x_{0},\delta}\right>_{{\mathcal{H}}_{T}}|}{1+|x-y_{0}|^{\theta}}\right|^{p}\\
+C\sup_{y_{0}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}}{\mathbb{E}}\left|\sup_{t\in[t_{0}-\delta,t_{0}]}\sup_{x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}}\frac{|B_{h_{t_{0},x_{0},\delta}}(t,x)|}{1+|x-y_{0}|^{\theta}}\right|^{p}.

Then plugging (
143
) and (
156
) into the right hand side of (
157
), we get

(158)

\sup_{y_{0}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}}{\mathbb{E}}\left|\sup_{t\in[t_{0}-\delta,t_{0}]}\sup_{x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}}\frac{|D_{h_{t_{0},x_{0},\delta}}u(t,x)|}{1+|x-y_{0}|^{\theta}}\right|^{p}\leq C_{\delta,p,\theta}\mathcal{Q}_{1,\delta,p}+C(Q_{\Lambda}(\delta))^{p}\left(1+\mathcal{Q}_{2,\delta,p}\right)\,,

where we have set

(159)

\mathcal{Q}_{2,\delta,p}=\sup_{y_{0}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}}{\mathbb{E}}\left|\sup_{t\in[t_{0}-\delta,t_{0}]}\sup_{x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}}\frac{|u(t,x)|}{1+|x-y_{0}|^{\theta}}\right|^{p}\,.

Observe that as an easy consequence of (
51
), we have

\mathcal{Q}_{2,\delta,p}<+\infty
. Furthermore, recall that
\lim_{\delta\to 0}C_{\delta,p,\theta}=0
in equation (
156
). Hence there exists
\delta_{0}>0
small enough so that
C_{\delta,p,\theta}<\frac{1}{2}
. The left hand side of (
158
) being also of the form
\mathcal{Q}_{1,\delta,p}
according to our definition (
154
), for all
\delta\in(0,\delta_{0})
we obtain that there exists a constant
C
such that

(160)

\sup_{y_{0}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}}{\mathbb{E}}\left|\sup_{t\in[t_{0}-\delta,t_{0}]}\sup_{x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}}\frac{|D_{h_{t_{0},x_{0},\delta}}u(t,x)|}{1+|x-y_{0}|^{\theta}}\right|^{p}\leq C(Q_{\Lambda}(\delta))^{p}.

This proves our claim (
155
).
∎

5.4.
Almost sure upper bounds on the integrals
\mathbf{A}
and
\mathbf{B}

In this section, we will prove that with probability one,
A_{h_{t_{0},x_{0},\delta}}(t_{0},x_{0})
and
B_{h_{t_{0},x_{0},\delta}}(t_{0},x_{0})
converge to zero much faster than
Q_{\Lambda}(\delta)
as
\delta\downarrow 0
. These results, along with (
142
), will enable us to prove that with probability one there exists
\delta>0
such that
D_{h_{t_{0},x_{0},\delta}}u(t_{0},x_{0})>0
.

As a first step, we show that
{|B_{h_{t_{0},x_{0},\delta}}(t_{0},x_{0})|}/{Q_{\Lambda}(\delta)}
converges to zero in probability.

Lemma 5.4
.

Let
(t_{0},x_{0})\in(0,T]\times{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
, and recall that the term
B_{h_{t_{0},x_{0},\delta}}(t_{0},x_{0})
is defined by (
138
).
We work under the conditions of Theorem
5.1
. Then for any
{\varepsilon}>0
,

(161)

\lim_{\delta\to 0}{\mathbb{P}}\left(\frac{|B_{h_{t_{0},x_{0},\delta}}(t_{0},x_{0})|}{Q_{\Lambda}(\delta)}>{\varepsilon}\right)=0\,.

Proof.

We start by applying Chebyshev inequality in order to get

{\mathbb{P}}\left(\frac{|B_{h_{t_{0},x_{0},\delta}}(t_{0},x_{0})|}{Q_{\Lambda}(\delta)}>{\varepsilon}\right)\leq\frac{{\mathbb{E}}|B_{h_{t_{0},x_{0},\delta}}(t_{0},x_{0})|^{p}}{{\varepsilon}^{p}(Q_{\Lambda}(\delta))^{p}}\,.

Next applying successively (
156
) and (
155
) we easily get that

{\mathbb{P}}\left(\frac{|B_{h_{t_{0},x_{0},\delta}}(t_{0},x_{0})|}{Q_{\Lambda}(\delta)}>{\varepsilon}\right)\leq\frac{C_{\delta,p,\theta}}{{\varepsilon}^{p}(Q_{\Lambda}(\delta))^{p}}\mathcal{Q}_{1,\delta,p}\leq\frac{C}{{\varepsilon}^{p}}C_{\delta,p,\theta}\,,

where
C_{\delta,p,\theta}
satisfies
\lim_{\delta\to 0}C_{\delta,p,\theta}=0
. We have thus achieved (
161
), which finishes the proof.
∎

Let us state a corollary to Lemma
5.4
which will be important in our arguments towards positivity of the Malliavin derivative. Its proof derives from standard tools in probability theory and is omitted for sake of conciseness.

Corollary 5.5
.

Let the assumptions of Lemma
5.4
prevail. Then for any sequence
\delta_{k}\downarrow 0
, there exists a subsequence
\delta_{k_{i}}
on which
{|B_{h_{t_{0},x_{0},\delta_{k_{i}}}}(t_{0},x_{0})|}/{Q_{\Lambda}(\delta_{k_{i}})}
converges to zero almost surely.

The Lebesgue integral
A_{h_{t_{0},x_{0},\delta}}
is more difficult to analyze because of the presence of the
f^{\prime}(u(t,x))
term in (
137
). Assumption
2.4
limits the growth to
|f^{\prime}(u)|\leq K\exp(K|u|^{\nu})
, but because we only have moment estimates on
\sup_{t\in[0,T]}\sup_{x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}}\frac{|u(t,x)|}{1+|x-x_{0}|^{\theta}}
, there is no reason to expect to have moment estimates on
{\mathbb{E}}|f^{\prime}(u(t,x))|^{p}
. On the other hand, the bounds on
f^{\prime}(u)
guarantee that the integral defined in (
137
) is convergent with probability one as long as
\theta\nu<2
. Before controlling the size of
A_{h_{t_{0},x_{0},\delta}}
, let us thus state
an estimate on
D_{h_{t_{0},x_{0},\delta}}u(t,x)
that holds with probability one.

Lemma 5.6
.

For
\delta>0
and
(t_{0},x_{0})\in(0,T]\times{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
, let
h_{t_{0},x_{0},\delta}
be defined by (
136
). We suppose that the assumptions of Theorem
5.1
hold.
Consider the sequence
\{\delta_{k};\,k\geq 1\}
defined by
\delta_{k}=2^{-k}
. Then we have

(162)

{\mathbb{P}}\left(\lim_{k\to\infty}\sup_{t\in[t_{0}-\delta_{k},t_{0}]}\sup_{x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}}\frac{\delta_{k}^{\frac{1}{2}}|D_{h_{t_{0},x_{0},\delta_{k}}}u(t,x)|}{\left(1+|x-x_{0}|^{\theta}\right)Q_{\Lambda}(\delta_{k})}=0\right)=1.

Remark 5.7
.

Notice that there is an extra
\delta_{k}^{\frac{1}{2}}
in the numerator of the expression in (
162
). This extra factor will help all of this converge to zero.

Proof of Lemma
5.6
.

Recall that
{\delta}_{k}=2^{-k}
. Like in the proof of Lemma
5.4
, we first apply Chebyshev’s inequality. Recalling our notation (
154
) for
\mathcal{Q}_{1,\delta,p}
, we get

{\mathbb{P}}\left(\sup_{t\in[t_{0}-\delta_{k},t_{0}]}\sup_{x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}}\frac{\delta_{k}^{\frac{1}{2}}|D_{h_{t_{0},x_{0},\delta_{k}}}u(t,x)|}{(1+|x-x_{0}|^{\theta})Q_{\Lambda}(\delta_{k})}>\delta_{k}^{\frac{1}{4}}\right)\leq\frac{\delta_{k}^{p/2}\mathcal{Q}_{1,\delta,p}}{Q_{\Lambda}(\delta_{k})^{p}\delta_{k}^{p/4}}\,.

Therefore due to (
155
) we have

(163)

{\mathbb{P}}\left(\sup_{t\in[t_{0}-\delta_{k},t_{0}]}\sup_{x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}}\frac{\delta_{k}^{\frac{1}{2}}|D_{h_{t_{0},x_{0},\delta_{k}}}u(t,x)|}{(1+|x-x_{0}|^{\theta})Q_{\Lambda}(\delta_{k})}>\delta_{k}^{\frac{1}{4}}\right)\leq C\delta_{k}^{\frac{p}{4}}\leq 2^{-\frac{pk}{4}}.

By the Borel-Cantelli Lemma, with probability one there exists
K(\omega)
such that for all
k\geq K(\omega)

(164)

\sup_{t\in[t_{0}-\delta_{k},t_{0}]}\sup_{x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}}\frac{\delta_{k}^{\frac{1}{2}}|D_{h_{t_{0},x_{0},\delta_{k}}}u(t,x)|}{1+|x-x_{0}|^{\theta}Q_{\Lambda}(\delta_{k})}\leq\delta_{k}^{\frac{1}{4}},

implying that

(165)

{\mathbb{P}}\left(\lim_{k\to\infty}\sup_{t\in[t_{0}-\delta_{k},t_{0}]}\sup_{x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}}\frac{\delta_{k}^{\frac{1}{2}}|D_{h_{t_{0},x_{0},\delta_{k}}}u(t,x)|}{1+|x-x_{0}|^{\theta}Q_{\Lambda}(\delta_{k})}=0\right)=1.

This achieves the proof of (
162
).
∎

With this intermediate result on the behavior of
Du(t,x)
, we can now state a lemma estimating the integral term
A
.

Lemma 5.8
.

Recall that we have set
\delta_{k}=2^{-k}
for
k\geq 1
. The integral
A_{h_{t_{0},x_{0},\delta_{k}}}(t_{0},x_{0})
is defined by (
137
), that is

(166)

A_{h_{t_{0},x_{0},\delta_{k}}}(t_{0},x_{0})=\int_{t_{0}-\delta}^{t_{0}}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}G(t_{0}-s,x_{0}-y)f^{\prime}(u(s,y))D_{h_{t_{0},x_{0},\delta_{k}}}u(s,y)dyds.

Our assumptions are those of Theorem
5.1
.
Then we have

(167)

{\mathbb{P}}\left(\lim_{k\to\infty}\frac{|A_{h_{t_{0},x_{0},\delta_{k}}}(t_{0},x_{0})|}{Q_{\Lambda}(\delta_{k})}=0\right)=1.

Proof.

We have seen that the random variable
\mathcal{Q}_{2,\delta,p}
defined by (
159
) is such that
{\mathbb{E}}[\mathcal{Q}_{2,\delta,p}]<\infty
, as an easy consequence of (
51
). Hence with probability one there exists a (random) constant
M(\omega)
such that

(168)

\sup_{t\in[0,t_{0}]}\sup_{x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}}\frac{|u(t,x)|}{1+|x-x_{0}|^{\theta}}\leq M(\omega).

Furthermore, by possibly increasing the value of
M(\omega)
, Lemma
5.6
guarantees that choosing
\delta_{k}=2^{-k}
, with probability one we have

(169)

\sup_{t\in[t_{0}-\delta_{k},t_{0}]}\sup_{x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}}\frac{|D_{h_{t_{0},x_{0},\delta_{k}}}u(t,x)|}{1+|x-x_{0}|^{\theta}}\leq M(\omega)\frac{Q_{\Lambda}(\delta_{k})}{\delta_{k}^{\frac{1}{2}}}\text{ for all }k\in\mathbb{N}.

In this proof, we allow the value of
M(\omega)
to change from line to line as long as its value remains independent of
\delta_{k}
.

Let us turn to the estimate on
A
in (
166
). Owing to (
15
), (
168
) and (
169
), we easily obtain

|A_{h_{t_{0},x_{0},\delta_{k}}}(t_{0},x_{0})|\leq\int_{t_{0}-\delta_{k}}^{t_{0}}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}G(t_{0}-s,x_{0}-y)\exp\left(M(\omega)|x_{0}-y|^{\theta\nu}\right)\\
\times M(\omega)(1+|y-x_{0}|^{\theta})\frac{Q_{\Lambda}(\delta_{k})}{\delta_{k}^{\frac{1}{2}}}dyds.

By increasing the value of
M(\omega)
, the integrand in the above expression is bounded by

(170)

(2\pi(t_{0}-s))^{-\frac{d}{2}}M(\omega)\exp\left(-\frac{|y-x_{0}|^{2}}{2(t_{0}-s)}+M(\omega)|x_{0}-y|^{\theta\nu}\right)Q_{\Lambda}(\delta_{k})\delta_{k}^{-\frac{1}{2}}.

We now bound the term

(171)

\mathcal{R}_{\omega,\nu,\theta}\equiv-\frac{|y-x_{0}|^{2}}{2(t_{0}-s)}+M(\omega)|x_{0}-y|^{\theta\nu},

which appears in the exponent in (
170
). Namely recast this exponent as

\mathcal{R}_{\omega,\nu,\theta}=-\frac{|y-x_{0}|^{2}}{2(t_{0}-s)}+KM(\omega)(2(t_{0}-s))^{\frac{\theta\nu}{2}}\left(\frac{|x_{0}-y|}{\sqrt{2(t_{0}-s)}}\right)^{\theta\nu}\,.

Next because
\theta\nu<2
, Young’s inequality with powers
\frac{2}{\nu\theta}
and
\frac{2}{2-\nu\theta}
in the right hand side above proves that
\mathcal{R}_{\omega,\nu,\theta}
satisfies

\mathcal{R}_{\omega,\nu,\theta}\leq-\frac{|y-x_{0}|^{2}}{2(t_{0}-s)}+\frac{\theta\nu|y-x_{0}|^{2}}{4(t_{0}-s)}+\frac{(2-\nu\theta)(M(\omega))^{\frac{2}{2-\nu\theta}}(2(t_{0}-s))^{\frac{\theta\nu}{2-\nu\theta}}}{2}.

In addition, since
(t_{0}-s)<\delta_{k}\leq 1
, the third term in the above expression is uniformly bounded with respect to
s
and independent of
y
. Therefore
|A_{h_{t_{0},x_{0},\delta_{k}}}(t_{0},x_{0})|
is bounded by

|A_{h_{t_{0},x_{0},\delta_{k}}}(t_{0},x_{0})|\\
\leq M(\omega)Q_{\Lambda}(\delta_{k})\delta_{k}^{-\frac{1}{2}}\int_{t_{0}-\delta_{k}}^{t_{0}}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}(2\pi(t_{0}-s))^{-\frac{d}{2}}\exp\left(-\left(1-\frac{\theta\nu}{2}\right)\frac{|x_{0}-y|^{2}}{2(t_{0}-s)}\right)dyds.

The above integral over
\mathbb{R}^{d}
is the integral of a Gaussian density and its value does not depend on
s
. Therefore, owing to the fact that the time interval is length
\delta_{k}
, we end up with

(172)

|A_{h_{t_{0},x_{0},\delta_{k}}}(t_{0},x_{0})|\leq M(\omega)Q_{\Lambda}(\delta_{k})\delta_{k}^{\frac{1}{2}}\,,

with probability one.
This proves that

(173)

\lim_{k\to\infty}\frac{|A_{h_{t_{0},x_{0},\delta_{k}}}(t_{0},x_{0})|}{Q_{\Lambda}(\delta_{k})}=0\,,\quad\text{ with probability one.}

Our claim (
167
) is proved.
∎

Now we can establish the main result of this subsection, which is that there exists a subsequence
\delta_{k}\downarrow 0
such that with probability one, for small values of
\delta_{k}
,
|A_{h_{t_{0},x_{0},\delta_{k}}}(t_{0},x_{0})|
and
|B_{h{\delta_{k},t_{0},x_{0}}}(t_{0},x_{0})|
are much smaller than
Q_{\Lambda}(\delta_{k})
.

Proposition 5.9
.

Consider
(t_{0},x_{0})\in(0,T]\times{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
and the sequence
\{\delta_{k}=2^{-k};\,k\geq 1\}
. The function
h_{t_{0},x_{0},\delta_{k}}
is introduced in (
141
), and the terms
A,B
are respectively given by (
137
)-(
138
). We assume that the coefficients
b,\sigma
satisfy Assumptions
2.3
–
2.4
.
Then there exists a subsequence of
\delta_{k}
, still denoted
\delta_{k}
, such that

(174)

{\mathbb{P}}\left(\lim_{k\to\infty}\frac{|A_{h_{t_{0},x_{0},\delta_{k}}}(t_{0},x_{0})|+|B_{h_{t_{0},x_{0},\delta_{k}}}(t_{0},x_{0})|}{Q_{\Lambda}(\delta_{k})}=0\right)=1.

Proof.

Let
\delta_{k}
be a subsequence from Lemma
5.8
along which

(175)

{\mathbb{P}}\left(\lim_{k\to\infty}\frac{|A_{h_{t_{0},x_{0},\delta_{k}}}(t_{0},x_{0})|}{Q_{\Lambda}(\delta_{k})}=0\right)=1.

Then thanks to Corollary
5.5
, there is a subsequence of
\delta_{k}
(relabeled as
\delta_{k}
) along which

\lim_{k\to\infty}Q_{\Lambda}(\delta_{k})^{-1}|B_{h_{t_{0},x_{0},\delta_{k}}}(t_{0},x_{0})|=0
with probability one. This proves our result.
∎

5.5.
Positivity of the Malliavin derivative

In this section we prove that for any
t_{0}\in(0,T]
and
x_{0}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
we have
\|Du(t_{0},x_{0})\|_{{\mathcal{H}}_{T}}>0
almost surely.
This will allow to establish the existence of a density for the random variable
u(t_{0},x_{0})
.

Theorem 5.10
.

Let
t_{0}\in(0,T]
and
x_{0}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
. We assume that the coefficients
b,\sigma
satisfy Assumptions
2.3
–
2.4
and we consider the solution
u
to equation (
2.1
). Then the following holds true:

{\mathbb{P}}(\|Du(t_{0},x_{0})\|_{{\mathcal{H}}_{T}}>0)=1.

Proof.

Let
h_{t_{0},x_{0},\delta}
be defined by (
141
). In (
131
) we decomposed

D_{h_{t_{0},x_{0},\delta}}u(t_{0},x_{0})=\left<\phi_{t_{0},x_{0}},h_{t_{0},x_{0},\delta}\right>_{{\mathcal{H}}_{T}}+A_{h_{t_{0},x_{0},\delta}}(t_{0},x_{0})+B_{h_{t_{0},x_{0},\delta}}(t_{0},x_{0}).

Moreover, in (
142
), we proved that with probability one,

\left<\phi_{t_{0},x_{0}},h_{t_{0},x_{0},\delta}\right>_{{\mathcal{H}}_{T}}\geq\alpha Q_{\Lambda}(\delta).

By Theorem
5.9
there exists a subsequence
\delta_{k}\downarrow 0
such that

\lim_{k\to\infty}\frac{|A_{h_{t_{0},x_{0},\delta_{k}}}(t_{0},x_{0})|+|B_{h_{t_{0},x_{0},\delta_{k}}}(t_{0},x_{0})|}{Q_{\Lambda}(\delta_{k})}=0.

Therefore, with probability one, there exists a (random)
k(\omega)
such that

D_{h_{\delta_{k(\omega)},t_{0},x_{0}}}u(t_{0},x_{0})>0.

This implies that
\|Du(t_{0},x_{0})\|_{{\mathcal{H}}_{T}}>0
with probability one, because at least one of the directional derivatives is nonzero.
∎

We conclude this paper by proving our main result for the density of
u(t_{0},x_{0})
.

Proof of Theorem
5.1
.

According to Proposition
2.14
, we have to check that the random variable
F=u(t_{0},x_{0})
belongs to the Malliavin-Sobolev space
\mathbb{D}^{1,p}
, and that
{\mathbb{P}}(\|Du(t_{0},x_{0})\|_{{\mathcal{H}}_{T}}>0)=1
.
Now the fact that
u(t_{0},x_{0})\in\mathbb{D}^{1,p}
is established in Theorem
4.4
, while the condition
{\mathbb{P}}(\|Du(t_{0},x_{0})\|_{{\mathcal{H}}_{T}}>0)=1
is the contents of Theorem
5.10
. This implies that the law of
u(t_{0},x_{0})
is absolutely continuous with Lebesgue measure.
∎
