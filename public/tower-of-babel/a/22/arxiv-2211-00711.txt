Alternative polynomial-time algorithm for Bipartite Matching
Sylvain Guillemot

Abstract
If G G is a bipartite graph, Hall’s theorem [ 1 ] gives a condition for the existence of a matching of G G covering one side of the bipartition. This theorem admits a well-known algorithmic proof involving the repeated search of augmenting paths. We present here an alternative algorithm, using a game-theoretic formulation of the problem. We also show how to extend this formulation to the setting of balanced hypergraphs.



1
Introduction

Let
G=(V,E)
be a bipartite graph with bipartition
V_{1},V_{2}
. Hall’s theorem
[
1
]
states that
G
has a matching covering
V_{1}
if for each
S
subset of
V_{1}
, there are at least
|S|
vertices in
V_{2}
adjacent to
S
. This theorem can be proved and extended in a number of ways (see e.g.
[
2
,
3
,
4
,
5
,
6
]
). It has strong connections to other results about bipartite graphs such as König theorem for maximum cardinality matchings
[
7
]
and Egerváry theorem for maximum weighted matchings
[
8
]
.

A well-known algorithmic proof of Hall’s theorem relies on the notion of augmenting path, which can be traced back to
[
7
,
9
]
. This technique actually yields a direct algorithmic proof of König theorem, as well as an indirect algorithmic proof of Egerváry theorem using the Hungarian method
[
10
]
. This technique also applies to other problems more general than bipartite matching, such as in Edmonds’ algorithm for nonbipartite matching
[
11
]
, Lawler’s algorithm for matroid intersection
[
12
]
, and Gabow & Stallman’s algorithm for linear matroid parity
[
13
]
. Yet, there is one situation where this technique does not seem to apply: to compute a maximum matching of a balanced hypergraph
[
14
]
. Such a matching can be computed in polynomial time by LP techniques, due to a min-max theorem of
[
15
]
generalizing König theorem. However, finding a purely combinatorial algorithm for this problem remains an important open question in the field, which does not seem amenable to the previous technique. This led us to seek an alternative algorithm for bipartite matching, that may extend to balanced hypergraphs.

Such an alternative algorithm is presented here. It relies on a folklore game-theoretic formulation of the problem, that is a variant of Slither
[
16
]
. We consider a two-person game played on a graph
G
with a distinguished vertex
v_{0}
. Starting at
v_{0}
, each player in turn chooses a previously unchosen vertex, with the restriction that the sequence of vertices chosen forms a path in
G
; the first player unable to move loses. It follows from the results of
[
16
]
that a strategy for this game can be expressed in terms of maximum matchings on the underlying graph. We are concerned here with the case of a bipartite graph
G
, and in this case it turns out that the game has a memoryless strategy, called an
assignment
. We give a simple polynomial-time algorithm that computes an assignment for a given pair
(G,v_{0})
; we observe here that the polynomial-time termination of the algorithm is nontrivial and crucially relies on
G
being bipartite. We are also able to extend the previous game-theoretic formulation to balanced hypergraphs; the game and the notion of assignment can be adapted to this setting, although we don’t know here how to efficiently compute an assignment.

This paper is organized as follows. Section
2
deals with the case of bipartite graphs. We first define the game and the notion of assignment. We then explain how these notions provide an alternative proof of Hall’s theorem. Finally, we describe a
O(n^{3})
time algorithm computing an assignment in a graph with
n
vertices, which makes the alternative proof algorithmic. Section
3
deals with the case of balanced hypergraphs. We adapt the game and the notion of assignment to this setting, and we prove non-constructively the existence of an assignment.

A game-theoretic formulation of Hall’s theorem

2.1
Preliminaries

The graphs we consider are undirected, finite and simple. Let

A

, and for each

is a walk of support

and of length

. A

in

is a walk

such that: for

. Given

We recall Hall’s theorem below. We first need the following additional definitions.

Theorem 1

[

]
Let

be a bipartite graph with bipartition

2.2
The game
\mathcal{G}(G^{\prime},v_{0})

Let
G=(V,E)
be a bipartite graph with bipartition

. We introduce below a game played on a bipartite graph

derived from

V_{1}
translates to a winning strategy for this game. This is inspired by the Slither game of
[
16

Let

obtained from

by adding (1) a vertex

adjacent to all vertices of
V_{1}

adjacent to

. A play is a sequence
P=v_{0}v_{1}\ldots v_{k}
that is a path in
G^{\prime}
. The game starts with

. Suppose that it is the turn of Player
s\in\{1,2\}
, and that we have

with
k-1\equiv s~(2)
. Let
Z=\{v\in V^{\prime}-V(P):v_{k}v\in E^{\prime}\}
. The player loses if
Z=\emptyset
; otherwise, he chooses

, and hands over to the other player. It can be shown that Player 1 has a winning strategy for this game iff
G
has a matching covering
V_{1}
.

An
assignment
for
(G^{\prime},v_{0})
is a pair
(R,\sigma)
, where
R\subseteq V^{\prime}
,
v_{0}\in R
, and
\sigma:V^{\prime}\rightarrow V^{\prime}\cup\{\perp\}
is such that:

(C_{1})

for
v\in R
, if
\sigma(v)=u\in V^{\prime}
then

and
\sigma(u)=\perp
;

(C_{2})

for
v\in R
, if
\sigma(v)=\perp
then for every
u\in N_{G^{\prime}}(v)
we have
u\in R
and
\sigma(u)\neq\perp
;

(C_{3})

for
v\in R
, we have
|\sigma^{-1}(v)|\leq 1
, and
\sigma^{-1}(v_{0})=\emptyset
.

Informally,
(R,\sigma)
defines a strategy for playing the game

; here,

is the set of reachable positions, and a position

is

if
\sigma(v)\neq\perp
, and
losing
otherwise. The intuition behind this definition is as follows. Condition
(C_{1})
expresses that for a winning position
v
,
some
adjacent position
u
is losing; this information is provided by
u=\sigma(v)
. Condition
(C_{2})
expresses that for a losing position
v
,
every
adjacent position
u
is winning. Finally, Condition
(C_{3})
is necessary to ensure that the strategy does not loop on some vertex.

The following result explains how to obtain a winning strategy for either player, given an assignment.

Proposition 1

Suppose that we have
(R,\sigma)
assignment for
(G^{\prime},v_{0})
.

1.

If
\sigma(v_{0})\neq\perp
, Player 1 has a winning strategy in
\mathcal{G}(G^{\prime},v_{0})
.

2.

If
\sigma(v_{0})=\perp
, Player 2 has a winning strategy in
\mathcal{G}(G^{\prime},v_{0})
.

Proof 1

We only prove (1), since (2) follows by a similar argument. We maintain the following invariant: at step
k
of the game, the current play is
P=v_{0}v_{1}\ldots v_{k}
with: (a)
P
path in
G^{\prime}
, (b) for an integer
i

(0\leq i\leq k)
even, we have
v_{i}\in R,\sigma(v_{i})\neq\perp
and (
i<k\Rightarrow v_{i+1}=\sigma(v_{i})
), (c) for an integer
i
(
0\leq i\leq k
) odd, we have
v_{i}\in R,\sigma(v_{i})=\perp
. Player 1 uses the following strategy: if

with

. We show by induction on

that the invariant is preserved.

Suppose first that
k
is odd. Let
Z=\{v\in V^{\prime}-V(P):v_{k}v\in E^{\prime}\}
. If
Z=\emptyset
, Player 2 loses. Suppose that

and Player 2 chooses a vertex

Suppose now that

v_{k}\in R
and
\sigma(v_{k})\neq\perp

(C_{1})
we have

\sigma(v_{k+1})=\perp
. If we had

, we would have

, which proves Point (a) at step

.
∎

The following proposition shows how to compute a matching or an obstruction from an assignment.

Proposition 2

Suppose that we have
(R,\sigma)
assignment for
(G^{\prime},v_{0})
.

1.

If
\sigma(v_{0})\neq\perp
, we can obtain a matching of
G
covering
V_{1}
.

2.

If
\sigma(v_{0})=\perp
, we can obtain a set

such that
|N_{G}(S)|<|S|
.

Proof 2

Point 1. Let
(R,\sigma)
be an assignment for
(G^{\prime},v_{0})
such that
\sigma(v_{0})\neq\perp
. By
(C_{1})
, we have
\sigma(v_{0})=v_{1}
,
v_{1}\in R
and
\sigma(v_{1})=\perp
.
Fix a vertex
u\in V_{1}
and let
u^{\prime}=\sigma(u)
. We have
u\in R
and
u^{\prime}\neq\perp
by
(C_{2})
; we have
u^{\prime}\in R
and
uu^{\prime}\in E^{\prime}
by
(C_{1})
; finally, by
(C_{3})
it follows that these edges are pairwise disjoint edges of

. We conclude that the set

is a matching of

covering

.

Point 2. Let
(R,\sigma)
be an assignment for
(G^{\prime},v_{0})
such that
\sigma(v_{0})=\perp
. Let
S=\{u\in R\cap V_{1}:\sigma(u)=\perp\}
and

. We claim that:
|T|<|S|
.

We first show that for each
v\in T
, we have
v\in R\cap V_{2}
,
\sigma(v)\neq\perp
and
\sigma(v)\in S
. Fix
v\in T
and let
\sigma(v)=u^{\prime}
with

. By definition of

, we have

such that

. By definition of

and
\sigma(u)=\perp
.
It follows by

that

that

and
\sigma(u^{\prime})=\perp

,

and

We infer by
(C_{3})
that

, and thus

. Let

. By

∎

In the rest of this section, we present a combinatorial algorithm for computing an assignment. Formally, we prove the following.

Theorem 2

Consider a pair
(G^{\prime},v_{0})
as above, and suppose that
G^{\prime}
has
n

(G^{\prime},v_{0})

Combining this result with Proposition

2.3
Computing an assignment

We describe in this section the algorithm of Theorem
2

We first give a high-level description of the algorithm. At a given step, we have a path

Consider a step of the algorithm, where we have

. We show by induction on
k
that the invariant is preserved.

Suppose first that
k
is odd. We have
P=v_{0}e_{0}\ldots v_{k}e_{k}
path in
H^{\prime}
. Player 2 then chooses
v_{k+1},e_{k+1}
such that
P^{\prime}=Pv_{k+1}e_{k+1}
is a strong path in
H^{\prime}
. We need to show that
P^{\prime}
is compatible with
(R,\sigma)
. Points (a)-(b) hold by induction hypothesis and since
v_{k}\in R,\sigma(v_{k})=e_{k}
imply
v_{k+1}\in R
and
\sigma(v_{k+1})=\perp
. Point (c) holds by choice of
v_{k+1},e_{k+1}
.

Suppose now that
k
is even. We have
P=v_{0}e_{0}\ldots v_{k}e_{k}
path in
H^{\prime}
. Player 1 then chooses
v\in N_{H^{\prime}}(e_{k})-\{v_{k}\}
such that
\sigma(v)\neq\perp
, and plays
v_{k+1}=v
and
e_{k+1}=\sigma(v)
. Observe that this is possible: by induction hypothesis, we have
v_{k}\in R
and
\sigma(v_{k})=\perp
, and by
(C_{2})
we find such a
v
and we have
v\in R
. We need to show that
P^{\prime}=Pv_{k+1}e_{k+1}
is compatible with
(R,\sigma)
. Points (a)-(b) hold by induction hypothesis and since
v_{k+1}\in R,e_{k+1}=\sigma(v_{k+1})
. Point (c) follows from Lemma
3
.
∎

Building on Proposition
5
, we now prove the following theorem.

Theorem 4

Let
H
be a balanced hypergraph with independent transversal
U
, and let
(H^{\prime},v_{0})
constructed as above from
(H,U)
. Then:
H
has a matching covering
U
iff Player 2 has a winning strategy for
\mathcal{G}(H^{\prime},v_{0})
.

The proof of the Theorem proceeds by constructing an assignment
(R,\sigma)
for
(H^{\prime},v_{0})
in either case, whether or not
H
has a matching covering
U
. This is the purpose of Proposition
6
below.

Proposition 6

The following holds.

1.

If
H
has a matching covering
U
, we can find
(R,\sigma)
assignment for
(H^{\prime},v_{0})
such that
\sigma(v_{0})\neq\perp
.

2.

If
H
has no matching covering
U
, we can find
(R,\sigma)
assignment for
(H^{\prime},v_{0})
such that
\sigma(v_{0})=\perp
.

Proof 9
(Sketch)

Point 1. Let
M
be a matching covering
U
. We define
\sigma:V^{\prime}\rightarrow E^{\prime}\cup\{\perp\}
as follows. Consider
v\in V^{\prime}
. If
v=v_{0}
, we let
\sigma(v)=e_{0}
. If
v\in U
, we let
\sigma(v)
be the unique hyperedge of
M
incident to
v
. Otherwise, we let
\sigma(v)=\perp
. By definition, we have
\sigma(v_{0})\neq\perp
. It is easily checked that
(V^{\prime},\sigma)
is an assignment for
(H^{\prime},v_{0})
.

Point 2. Suppose that
H
has no matching covering
U
. Let
M
be a maximum matching of
H
, and let
T
be a transversal of
H
of minimum cardinality. By Theorem
3
, we have
|M|=|T|
. By the assumption, we find
r\in U
not covered by

. We define
\sigma:V^{\prime}\rightarrow E^{\prime}\cup\{\perp\}

\sigma(v_{0})=\perp

\sigma(v_{1})=f_{r}

, with points
