On K3 fibred Calabi–Yau threefolds in weighted scrolls
Geoffrey Mboya, Balázs Szendrői

Abstract
. The aim of this paper is to classify mildly singular Calabi–Yau threefolds fibred in low-degree weighted K3 surfaces and embedded as anticanonical hypersurfaces in weighted scrolls, extending results of Mullet. We also study projective degenerations, revisiting an example due to Gross and Ruan. Finally we briefly discuss the general question of embedding a projective fibration into a weighted scroll.



1.
Introduction

Let
(X,D)
be a polarized projective variety over
\mathbb{C}
: a pair consisting of a normal projective
\mathbb{C}
-variety
X
and an ample
\mathbb{Q}
-Cartier Weil divisor
D
on
X
, with associated divisorial sheaf
\mathcal{O}_{X}(D)
. We can then consider the graded
\mathbb{C}
-algebra

R=\bigoplus_{m=0}^{\infty}H^{0}(X,\mathcal{O}_{X}(mD)).

Ampleness of
D
is equivalent to an isomorphism
X\cong\operatorname{Proj}R
; in particular, the algebra
R
is finitely generated.
Choosing a set of algebra generators
r_{1},\ldots,r_{n}\in R
of positive integer weights
c_{1},\ldots,c_{n}
gives a surjection

S=\mathbb{C}[x_{1},\ldots,x_{n}]\twoheadrightarrow R

of graded algebras, and a corresponding embedding

(1)

(X,D)\hookrightarrow\left(\mathbb{P}^{n-1}[c_{1},\ldots,c_{n}],\mathcal{O}(1)\right)

into a weighted projective space. Under suitable vanishing assumptions, the dimension of the
m
-th graded piece of
R
is given by a Riemann–Roch type formula, allowing the
computation of the Hilbert series of
R
.

Miles Reid’s
graded ring method
studies classes of projective varieties of increasing complexity according to their codimension in the embedding (
1
), using the information derived from the Hilbert series. Well-studied classical examples include the “famous 95” K3 hypersurfaces in weighted projective 3-space due to Iano-Fletcher and Reid
[
14
, Sect.13.3]
and the attached families of
\mathbb{Q}
-Fano 3-folds
[
14
, Sect.16.6]
,
[
3
]
; the codimension 2 complete intersection K3 surfaces of Iano-Fletcher
[
14
, Sect.13.8]
; the codimension 3 Pfaffian K3 families
[
1
]
and others. The current state of the art is contained in the Graded Ring Database
[
9
,
5
]
.

In a paper
[
16
]
by Mullet, the first steps were taken to study a relative version of these constructions: the case of a polarized projective fibration
f\colon X\rightarrow B
over a polarized base
B
. In that paper, the following specific setup was studied:

(i)

the base
B\cong\mathbb{P}^{1}
;

(ii)

the general fibre of
f
is one of the “famous 95” list of Iano-Fletcher–Reid;

(iii)

the fibration
f\colon X\rightarrow\mathbb{P}^{1}
embeds into a weighted scroll
\pi\colon\mathbb{F}\rightarrow\mathbb{P}^{1}
as a quasi-smooth anticanonical hypersurface.

Our aim is to study this problem further, extending the study of K3 hypersurface-fibred Calabi–Yau threefolds in two ways. On the one hand, we return to the first few cases of the Iano-Fletcher–Reid list, and for these fibres relax assumption (iii),
to allow for isolated singularities along the base locus. This allows for a longer list of examples, with some new cases of potential further interest;
all our examples have projective Calabi–Yau resolutions.
On the other hand, we use the explicit realisation of some of the threefolds as hypersurfaces in scrolls to study their projective geometry including degenerations,
re-visiting from a different point of view an example due to Gross
[
12
]
, also studied by Ruan
[
20
]
.

We begin in Section
2
by recalling the definition and basic properties of weighted scrolls, and discuss their toric geometry and anticanonical hypersurfaces. Our main results are contained in Section
3
. In Theorems
3.1
and
3.8
, we classify K3-fibred Calabi–Yau threefolds with mild singularities embedded in weighted scrolls over
\mathbb{P}^{1}
, whose generic fibres are quartic, quintic or sextic weighted K3’s. We also study aspects of the projective geometry of some of our examples.

Other searches of a similar nature may also be of interest; the first author’s thesis
[
15
]
will study, and list, polarized elliptic K3 surfaces with rational double point singularities, as
well as higher codimension examples.

In the closing Section
4
, we speculate on how far the conditions (i)–(iii) can be relaxed to find interesting examples of projective fibrations, leaving detailed investigations of these questions to future work.

2.
Preliminaries on weighted scrolls

2.1.
Basics

We write this section in the generality needed for our examples, in order not to over-burden notation, following the treatment in
[
17
]
in the unweighted case. Fix an integer
n>1
, a set of positive integer weights
(b_{1},b_{2},\ldots,b_{n})
and arbitrary integer twists
(a_{1},\ldots,a_{n})
. To simplify the situation and because this is the case in all our applications, we assume from the outset that the first weight
b_{1}=1
; as usual, the remaining weights will be ordered in weakly increasing order.

Consider the action

(2)

(\lambda,\mu)\colon(t_{1},t_{2};x_{1},\ldots,x_{n})\mapsto(\lambda t_{1},\lambda t_{2};\lambda^{-a_{1}}\mu^{b_{1}}x_{1},\ldots,\lambda^{-a_{n}}\mu^{b_{n}}x_{n})

of
(\mathbb{C}^{*})^{2}
on affine space
\mathbb{C}^{2}\times\mathbb{C}^{n}
. This action preserves the open subset

U=(\mathbb{C}^{2}\setminus\{0\})\times(\mathbb{C}^{n}\setminus\{0\}).

We define the weighted scroll of type
(a_{1},\ldots,a_{n}|\,b_{1},\ldots,b_{n})
to be the quotient

\mathbb{F}(a_{1},\ldots,a_{n}|\,b_{1},\ldots,b_{n})=U/(\mathbb{C}^{*})^{2}.

with quotient map

q\colon U\to\mathbb{F}(a_{1},\ldots,a_{n}|\,b_{1},\ldots,b_{n}).

The
(\mathbb{C}^{*})^{2}
-orbits are easily checked to be closed on
U
, and it is known that
q
is a geometric quotient. The
(\mathbb{C}^{*})^{2}
-action has finite stabilizers along certain loci in
U
, leading to finite
quotient singularities on
\mathbb{F}(a_{1},\ldots,a_{n}|\,b_{1},\ldots,b_{n})
. There are no stabilisers if
b_{2}=\ldots=b_{n}=1
also.

It is clear that first projection on
U\subset\mathbb{C}^{2}\times\mathbb{C}^{n}
is compatible with taking a quotient, so we get a morphism

\pi\colon\mathbb{F}(a_{1},\ldots,a_{n}|\,b_{1},\ldots,b_{n})\to\mathbb{P}^{1}

with fibres that are all isomorphic to the weighted projective space
\mathbb{P}^{n-1}[b_{1},b_{2},\ldots.b_{n}]
.

Note that given weights
(1,b_{2},\ldots,b_{n})
, replacing a set of twists
(a_{1},\ldots,a_{n})
by the set
(a_{1}+k,a_{2}+kb_{2}\ldots,a_{n}+kb_{n})
leads to an isomorphic quotient. Thus, assuming

b_{1}=1
as we do, we may also assume
a_{1}=0
.

Algebraically, we can considering the coordinate algebra

(3)

S=\mathbb{C}[t_{1},t_{2},x_{1},\ldots,x_{n}]

of
\mathbb{C}^{2}\times\mathbb{C}^{n}
. The
(\mathbb{C}^{*})^{2}
-action translates into the bigrading on
S
that assigns degree
(1,0)
to the generators
t_{1},t_{2}
and
(-a_{i},b_{i})
to the generator
x_{i}
.
We can, and will, use the
variables
(t_{i};x_{j})
analogously to projective coordinates on (weighted) projective spaces.

Remark 2.1
.

Fix a further integer
k>1
, and another set of positive integer weights
(c_{1},\ldots c_{k})
. The action of
(\mathbb{C}^{*})^{2}
on affine space
\mathbb{C}^{k}\times\mathbb{C}^{n}
defined by

(\lambda,\mu)\colon(t_{1},\ldots,t_{k};x_{1},\ldots,x_{n})\mapsto(\lambda^{c_{1}}t_{1},\ldots,\lambda^{c_{k}}t_{k};\lambda^{-a_{1}}\mu^{b_{1}}x_{1},\ldots,\lambda^{-a_{n}}\mu^{b_{n}}x_{n})

gives rise to a weighted scroll
\mathbb{F}(c_{1},\ldots c_{k}||a_{1},\ldots,a_{n}|\,b_{1},\ldots,b_{n})=U/(\mathbb{C}^{*})^{2}
with a map

\pi\colon\mathbb{F}(c_{1},\ldots c_{k}||a_{1},\ldots,a_{n}|\,b_{1},\ldots,b_{n})\rightarrow\mathbb{P}^{k-1}[c_{1},\ldots,c_{k}],

whose fibres are weighted projective spaces
\mathbb{P}^{n-1}[b_{1},b_{2},\ldots.b_{n}]
.
We do not use this generality in the main body of the paper, but will return to this more general construction at the end.

2.2.
The toric description

Weighted scrolls are clearly toric varieties; in this section, we make this explicit. For simplicity, in this section
\mathbb{F}
denotes a scroll
\mathbb{F}(a_{1},\ldots,a_{n}|\,b_{1},\ldots,b_{n})
over

\mathbb{P}^{1}
given by twists and weights as above, with
b_{1}=1
.

Start with the exact sequence of abelian groups

(4)

0\longrightarrow\mathbb{Z}^{2}\longrightarrow\mathbb{Z}^{2+n}\longrightarrow N\longrightarrow 0,

where the first map maps the standard generators of
\mathbb{Z}^{2}
to the vectors
(1,1,-a_{1},\ldots,-a_{n})
and
(0,0,1,b_{2},\ldots,b_{n})
respectively. Note that by assuming
b_{1}=1
, we
automatically get that
N
is a free
\mathbb{Z}
-module. The standard lattice generators of
\mathbb{Z}^{2+n}
project to elements of
N
that we denote by
\sigma_{1},\sigma_{2},\rho_{1},\ldots,\rho_{n}
. Note that by our definition of
N
, they satisfy the relations

\sigma_{1}+\sigma_{2}-\sum_{j=1}^{n}a_{j}\rho_{j}=0,\ \ \ \ \sum_{j=1}^{n}b_{j}\rho_{j}=0.

So with
b_{1}=1
, we see that
\{\sigma_{2},\rho_{2},\ldots,\rho_{n}\}
is a
\mathbb{Z}
-basis of
N
.

We define a fan
\Sigma
in the
n
-dimensional real space
N_{\mathbb{R}}
by declaring the maximal dimensional cones to be

\tau_{i,j}={\rm Span}(\sigma_{i},\rho_{1},\ldots,\rho_{j-1},\rho_{j+1}\ldots,\rho_{n});

it is easy to check that this is indeed a fan.

Proposition 2.2
.

Using standard toric notation, the toric variety
X_{N,\Sigma}
is isomorphic to the scroll
\mathbb{F}=\mathbb{F}(a_{1},\ldots,a_{n}|\,b_{1},\ldots,b_{n})
defined above, whereas the bigraded algebra

S
in (
3
) becomes the Cox ring
[
7
]
of this toric variety.

Proof.

Tensoring (
4
) by
\mathbb{C}^{*}
, we get an exact sequence of tori

1\longrightarrow(\mathbb{C}^{*})^{2}\longrightarrow(\mathbb{C}^{*})^{2+n}\longrightarrow T\longrightarrow 1

mapping
(\lambda,\mu)
to
(\lambda,\lambda,\lambda^{-a_{1}}\mu^{b_{1}},\ldots,\lambda^{-a_{n}}\mu^{b_{n}})
.
This precisely describes the action of
(\mathbb{C}^{*})^{2}
on
\mathbb{C}^{2}\times\mathbb{C}^{n}
from (
2
) as a subgroup of the standard torus of
\mathbb{C}^{2}\times\mathbb{C}^{n}
. Now the description of
\mathbb{F}
above can be directly compared to the standard toric description of
X_{N,\Sigma}
from
[
13
]
, to check that everything agrees. In particular, the irrelevant ideal
[
7
]
corresponding to our chosen fan
\Sigma
is the ideal

B=\langle t_{i}x_{j}:1\leq i\leq 2,1\leq j\leq n\rangle\lhd S,

the vanishing locus
Z\subset\mathbb{C}^{2}\times\mathbb{C}^{n}
of which is the complement of our open set
U
.
∎

Let
\operatorname{Cl}(\mathbb{F})
denote the group of Weil divisors of
\mathbb{F}
. The one-dimensional cones in our fan give us divisors
D_{\sigma_{i}}=\{t_{i}=0\}
and
D_{\rho_{j}}=\{x_{j}=0\}
on
\mathbb{F}
. Let
L=[D_{\sigma_{1}}]\in\operatorname{Cl}(\mathbb{F})
and

M=[D_{\rho_{1}}]\in\operatorname{Cl}(\mathbb{F})
.

Assume that, using
b_{1}=1
, we have adjusted the twist so that
a_{1}=0
.

Proposition 2.3
.

We have

\operatorname{Cl}(\mathbb{F})\cong\mathbb{Z}L\oplus\mathbb{Z}M.

For
n,m\in Z
, we have

H^{0}(\mathbb{F},nL+mM)\cong S_{n,m},

the bidegree
(n,m)
piece of the graded algebra
S
.
The canonical class of
\mathbb{F}
is given by

(5)

K_{\mathbb{F}}=\left(-2+\sum_{j=2}^{n}a_{j}\right)L-\left(-1-\sum_{j=2}^{n}b_{j}\right)M\in\operatorname{Cl}(\mathbb{F}).

Finally,
\mathbb{F}
is
\mathbb{Q}
-factorial: every divisor on it is
\mathbb{Q}
-Cartier.

Proof.

This is all well known toric geometry. With
M=\operatorname{Hom}(N,\mathbb{Z})
, the dual of the sequence (
4
) is the sequence

0\longrightarrow M\longrightarrow\mathbb{Z}^{2+n}\longrightarrow\operatorname{Cl}(\mathbb{F})\longrightarrow 0,

with the first map given by
m\mapsto\sum_{\rho}\langle m,\rho\rangle D_{\rho}
, where the sum runs over all 1-dimensional cones in the fan
\Sigma
, and angle brackets denote the canonical pairing between
M
and
N
. So the class group of
\mathbb{F}
is free of rank 2. Also elements of
M
give relations between the classes of various torus-invariant divisors in
\operatorname{Cl}(\mathbb{F})
; explicitly these become the relations

[D_{\sigma_{1}}]-[D_{\sigma_{2}}]=0\mbox{ and for all }2\leq j\leq n,\ a_{j}[D_{\sigma_{1}}]-b_{j}[D_{\rho_{1}}]+[D_{\rho_{j}}]=0.

We see that indeed
L=[D_{\sigma_{1}}]
and
M=[D_{\rho_{1}}]
give a
\mathbb{Z}
-basis for
\operatorname{Cl}(\mathbb{F})
. The next statement is a standard part of the Cox ring package. The canonical class of
\mathbb{F}
is

K_{\mathbb{F}}=-[D_{\sigma_{1}}]-[D_{\sigma_{2}}]-\sum_{j=1}^{n}[D_{\rho_{j}}]=\left(-2+\sum_{j=2}^{n}a_{j}\right)L-\left(-1-\sum_{j=2}^{n}b_{j}\right)M

using the relations above. Finally, as all cones in
\Sigma
are simplicial,
\mathbb{F}=X_{N,\Sigma}
is
\mathbb{Q}
-factorial.
∎

2.3.
Generalities on anticanonical sections

Fix a weighted scroll

\pi\colon\mathbb{F}=\mathbb{F}(a_{1},\ldots,a_{n}|\,b_{1},\ldots,b_{n})\to\mathbb{P}^{1}.

Assume that the anticanonical system
|-K_{\mathbb{F}}|
is nonempty. Throughout this paper, we are interested in general anticanonical hypersurfaces
X\in|-K_{\mathbb{F}}|
, which
are themselves be fibered over
\mathbb{P}^{1}
. To fix notation, let

d=2-\sum_{j=1}^{n}a_{j},\ \ \ e=\sum_{j=1}^{n}b_{j}

be the negatives of the integer constants appearing in (
5
) above, so that

-K_{\mathbb{F}}=dL+eM\in\operatorname{Cl}(\mathbb{F}).

The hypersurface
X
is then defined inside
\mathbb{F}
as the zero locus of a general bihomogeneous section
f(t_{i},x_{j})\in S_{d,e}
.

We have to be more explicit about the equation
f
. Recall that
S
is bigraded, with
t_{i}
,
x_{j}
having degree
(1,0)
and
(-a_{j},b_{j})
respectively.
For an
n
-tuple of non-negative integers
{\bf q}=(q_{1},q_{2},\ldots,q_{n})
, write
{\bf q}\vdash_{w}e
to mean that
{\bf q}
is
a
(b_{1},\ldots,b_{n})
-weighted partition of
e
, in other words

\sum_{j=1}^{n}b_{j}q_{j}=e.

It is then easy to see that the polynomial
f
must have the form

f(t_{i},x_{j})=\sum_{{\bf q}\vdash_{w}e}\alpha_{{\bf q}}(t_{i})\prod_{j=1}^{n}x_{j}^{q_{j}},

where
\alpha_{{\bf q}}(t_{i})
is a homogeneous polynomial of the variables
(t_{1},t_{2})
of degree

\deg\alpha_{{\bf q}}=d+\sum_{j=1}^{n}a_{j}q_{j}=2+\sum_{j=1}^{n}(q_{j}-1)a_{j},

as long as this expression is non-negative; otherwise of course the monomial
\prod_{j=1}^{n}x_{j}^{q_{j}}
does not appear in
f
.

Recall the quotient construction
\mathbb{F}=U/(\mathbb{C}^{*})^{2}
together with the quotient map
q\colon U\to\mathbb{F}
, and let
\widetilde{X}=q^{-1}(X)\subset U
, the zero-locus of the polynomial
f
inside the set
U\subset\mathbb{A}^{2+n}
. Let
Z\subset\mathbb{F}
be a
T
-invariant closed subvariety. We call a hypersurface
X\subset\mathbb{F}

quasismooth away from
Z
, if
q^{-1}(X\setminus Z)\subset q^{-1}(\mathbb{F}\setminus Z)

is nonsingular. In this case, the only singularities of
X
away from
Z
are finite quotient singularities arising from finite stabiliser subgroups inside
(\mathbb{C}^{*})^{2}
.

We recall also that
X\subset\mathbb{F}
is
well-formed
, if the codimension of
{\rm Sing}(\mathbb{F})\cap X\subset X
is at least two.

Denote
B=\rm Bs\,|-K_{\mathbb{F}}|
the base locus of the anticanonical system of
\mathbb{F}
.

Proposition 2.4
.

(i)

The subset
B\subset\mathbb{F}
is
T
-invariant.

(ii)

The general anticanonical hypersurface
X\subset\mathbb{F}
is quasismooth away from
B
.

(iii)

If
X
is well-formed, and has Gorenstein singularities along
B
, then it is a Calabi–Yau threefold.

Proof.

(i) is in fact true more generally for any divisor class, and follows from the fact that the torus
T
acts trivially on the discrete group
\operatorname{Cl}(\mathbb{F})
.
(ii) is [Mullet, Prop. 6.7]. (iii) is a mild generalization of
[
16
, Thm.8.3]
and the proof there applies verbatim.
∎

Thus as long as the singularities of
X
are mild, it is a Calabi-Yau variety fibred over
\mathbb{P}^{1}
, with fibres
X_{t}\subset\mathbb{P}^{n-1}[b_{1},\ldots,b_{n}]
for
t\in\mathbb{P}^{1}
that are themselves Calabi–Yau hypersurfaces.
In order to find not-too-singular hypersurfaces
X\subset\mathbb{F}
, we thus need to focus on the possible singularities of
q^{-1}(B)\cap\widetilde{X}
.

3.
Mildly singular threefold families of K3 surfaces

3.1.
Families of quartic K3s

The easiest example of a K3 surface, also the first entry in the Iano-Fletcher–Reid list, is the quartic surface
S_{4}\subset\mathbb{P}^{3}
. The simplest example of a family of anticanonical Calabi–Yau hypersurfaces fibred in K3 surfaces is the anticanonical family
X\subset\mathbb{P}^{1}\times\mathbb{P}^{3}
. Mullet finds eight further scrolls containing nonsingular Calabi–Yau
hypersurfaces, which are listed in Table 1 below as familes 1-9. In this case, we find one further example; note that one member of this family was discussed from a different point of view
in
[
2

Theorem 3.1
.

There exists exactly one family of quartic
K3
-fibred anticanonical hypersurfaces
X\in|-K_{\mathbb{F}}|
with isolated singularities along the base locus
B=\rm Bs\,|-K_{\mathbb{F}}|
and quasi-smooth outside
B
: the family of anticanonical hypersurfaces

X\subset\mathbb{F}(0,0,1,2).

A general variety
X
in this family has
3

B\cong\mathbb{P}^{1}\times\mathbb{P}^{1}

Proof of Theorem
3.1
.

By complete symmetry of the weights
b_{1}=\ldots=b_{4}=1
, we can assume that the twists are
0=a_{1}\leq a_{2}\leq a_{3}\leq a_{4}
.
Denoting
\mathbb{F}=\mathbb{F}(0,a_{2},a_{3},a_{4})
as before, we have

-K_{\mathbb{F}}=(2-a_{2}-a_{3}-a_{4})L+4M\in\operatorname{Cl}(\mathbb{F}).

The equation of an anticanonical hypersurface is

f(t_{i},x_{j})=\sum_{{\bf q}\vdash 4}\alpha_{{\bf q}}(t_{i})\prod_{j=1}^{4}x_{j}^{q_{j}},

where
{\bf q}=(q_{1},\ldots,q_{4})
is a partition of
4
, and
\alpha_{{\bf q}}(t_{i})
is a homogeneous polynomial of the variables
(t_{1},t_{2})
of degree

\deg\alpha_{{\bf q}}=2+(q_{2}-1)a_{2}+(q_{3}-1)a_{3}+(q_{4}-1)a_{4},

as long as this quantity is non-negative. The degree
\deg\alpha_{{\bf q}}
increases as more of the higher indexed
x
variables appear in a monomial
\prod_{j=1}^{4}x_{j}^{q_{j}}
.

By Proposition
2.4
(i), the base locus
B=\rm Bs\,|-K_{\mathbb{F}}|
is defined by setting some of the Cox variables to
0
. Let us consider cases according to the dimension of
B
. If
\dim B=3
, then there is a fixed divisor in each member of the linear system, and so the general section is reducible. The cases where
B=\emptyset
have already been classified by Mullet. The case
\dim B=0
is not possible because of the symmetry of the
t_{1},t_{2}
variables: the toric base locus
B
cannot be an isolated point.

Assume that
\dim B=2
. This means that
f
cannot be divisible by any of the
x_{i}
variables. Looking at degrees of coefficients,
f
must have
nonzero coefficients at least for
x_{3}^{4}
and
x_{4}^{4}
, giving us the inequality

2-a_{2}+3a_{3}-a_{4}\geq 0.

This also means that the base locus must be

B=\{x_{3}=x_{4}=0\}\subset\mathbb{F},

and we cannot have any terms in
f
only involving
x_{1}
and
x_{2}
. This gives us the inequality

2+3a_{2}-a_{3}-a_{4}<0.

Let us now investigate potential singularities along the base locus. We have

{\rm Sing}(X)\cap B=\mathbb{V}\left(\frac{\partial f}{\partial x_{3}}\bigg|_{x_{3}=x_{4}=0},\frac{\partial f}{\partial x_{4}}\bigg|_{x_{3}=x_{4}=0},x_{3},x_{4}\right)

with

\frac{\partial f(t_{i},x_{j})}{\partial x_{3}}\bigg|_{x_{3}=x_{4}=0}=\alpha_{3010}(t_{1},t_{2})x_{1}^{3}+\alpha_{2110}(t_{1},t_{2})x_{1}^{2}x_{2}+\alpha_{1210}(t_{1},t_{2})x_{1}x_{2}^{2}+\alpha_{0310}(t_{1},t_{2})x_{2}^{3}

and

\frac{\partial f(t_{i},x_{j})}{\partial x_{4}}\bigg|_{x_{3}=x_{4}=0}=\alpha_{3001}(t_{1},t_{2})x_{1}^{3}+\alpha_{2101}(t_{1},t_{2})x_{1}^{2}x_{2}+\alpha_{1201}(t_{1},t_{2})x_{1}x_{2}^{2}+\alpha_{0301}(t_{1},t_{2})x_{2}^{3}.

To get isolated singularities along
B
, these two equations must give an isolated set of solutions on
B
. Once again recalling degrees, the condition for this is that the coefficients

\alpha_{0310}(t_{1},t_{2})
and
\alpha_{3001}(t_{1},t_{2})
should be nonzero, giving us the last set of inequalities

2+2a_{2}-a_{4}\geq 0\text{ and }2-a_{2}-a_{3}\geq 0.

Together with
0\leq a_{2}\leq a_{3}\leq a_{4}
, it can be checked that the only solutions to our full set of inequalities are
\mathbb{F}=\mathbb{F}(0,0,2,2)
and
\mathbb{F}=\mathbb{F}(0,0,1,2)
. The former already appeared in Mullet’s list and indeed in this case there are no solutions to the last two equations and thus
{\rm Sing}(X)\cap B=\emptyset
. In the final case, a quick degree count
shows that for general
f
, there exist three isolated solutions. A straightforward local analysis shows that the resulting singularities on
X
are ordinary double points,
and
B
is a non-Cartier Weil divisor at each singular point. It is then well known that blowing up
B
gives a small resolution of
X
.

The analysis in the case
\dim B=1
is analogous; we omit the details. We do not find any new examples beyond Mullet’s in this case.
∎

Remark 3.2
.

In families 1-2 of Table 1, the anticanonical divisor class is very ample, so by Lefschetz, the corresponding Calabi–Yau threefolds have Picard number
\rho(X)=2
.
For families 3 and 4, the anticanonical map is a semismall morphism, so the anticanonical divisor class is
lef
, in the language of
[
8
]
. Hence by [ibid, Prop.2.1.5],
Lefschetz still applies, and so again
\rho(X)=2
. The same was proved for family 6 in
[
20
, App.A]
. It seems possible that
\rho(X)=2
in families 1-8. However, in families 9-10 it is easy to see that the base locus
B
, or its
proper transform, give an extra divisor class in
X
or its resolution
Y
which cannot come from the ambient space, so the Picard number of the smooth models is at least (and
likely to be equal to)
3
.

One can also ask about the dimension of the space of complex deformations, the Hodge number
h^{2,1}
. For families 1-2, the dimension of the space
\mathcal{M}_{X\subset\mathbb{F}}
of embedded deformations is 86, respectively 118, and these values agree with the corresponding Hodge numbers. Once again, this is known to be the case also for family 8, with the dimension being also 86. However, in the other cases, especially families 9-10, the determination of this Hodge number appears more difficult.

3.2.
Projective degenerations

We look at three of the families from Table 1 in some more detail, numbered 1, 6 and 9: the families of anticanonical hypersurfaces in
\mathbb{P}^{1}\times\mathbb{P}^{3}
and the scrolls

\mathbb{F}(0,1,1,2)\cong\mathbb{P}\left(\mathcal{O}_{\mathbb{P}^{1}}(-1)\oplus\mathcal{O}_{\mathbb{P}^{1}}\oplus\mathcal{O}_{\mathbb{P}^{1}}\oplus\mathcal{O}_{\mathbb{P}^{1}}(1)\right)

and

The first two of these families has in fact appeared some time ago in the papers of Gross

with central fibre

in the general fibre

to a singular Calabi–Yau threefold

of the anticanonical system

are diffeomorphic, with Hodge numbers

, but not sympectic deformation equivalent
[
20

, so not algebraic deformation equivalent. The local deformation space of the singular threefold

, respectively

, and thus has obstructed deformation theory
[
12
, Thm.2.2]
.

General considerations

When looking for examples of projective varieties, it is natural to look in easily accessible ambient spaces. Examples of projective fibrations similarly arise in naturally fibered
ambient spaces, such as scrolls or more generally projectivized bundles; from many possible examples, we mention

4

Let

be a fibration of normal projective varieties, with
f_{*}\mathcal{O}_{X}\cong\mathcal{O}_{B}
so that
f
has connected fibres. Let
D
an ample
\mathbb{Q}
-Cartier Weil divisor on the base
X
, and
H
an ample
\mathbb{Q}
-Cartier Weil divisor on the total space
X
. Then we get a bigraded algebra

R_{X}=\bigoplus_{m,n\in\mathbb{Z}}H^{0}(X,\mathcal{O}_{X}(mf^{*}H+nD)),

containing the graded subalgebra

R_{B}=\bigoplus_{m\geq 0}^{\infty}H^{0}(B,\mathcal{O}_{B}(mH)).

The discussion of the Introduction applies to the base
B
: choosing generators
r_{1},\ldots,r_{k}
of positive weights
c_{i}
of
R_{B}
, we get a surjection

S_{B}=\mathbb{C}[t_{1},\ldots,t_{k}]\twoheadrightarrow R_{B}

of graded algebras from a free graded algebra, and thus a closed inclusion

(B,D)\hookrightarrow\left(\mathbb{P}^{k-1}[c_{1},\ldots,c_{k}],\mathcal{O}(1)\right).

Suppose that
R_{X}
is a
finitely generated
algebra over its subalgebra
R_{B}
, a kind of “Mori dream space” assumption. Choosing a set
r_{1},\ldots,r_{n}
of generators of
R_{X}
of bidegree
(-a_{j},b_{j})
over
R_{B}
, we then get
a diagram

{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces R_{X}}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces S_{B}}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces R_{B}}

of compatible surjections of bigraded algebras, where

S_{X}=\mathbb{C}[t_{1},\ldots,t_{k},x_{1},\ldots,x_{n}]

is a free bigraded algebra with generators of bidegrees
(c_{i},0)
, respectively
(-a_{j},b_{j})
.

embedding our original fibration
f\colon X\to B
into a general weighted scroll

\pi\colon\mathbb{F}=\mathbb{F}(c_{1},\ldots c_{k}||\,a_{1},\ldots,a_{n}|\,b_{1},\ldots,b_{n})\rightarrow\mathbb{P}^{k-1}[c_{i}],

of classifying and studying cases by increasing codimension, as in the absolute case.

The situation however is not so simple: the requirements of finite generation of the algebra
R_{X}
, and a surjective map
S_{X}\rightarrow R_{X}
from a free bigraded algebra, are too strong.
Even in our basic hypersurface examples
X\subset\mathbb{F}
, the natural restriction map

\bigoplus_{m,n\in\mathbb{Z}}H^{0}(\mathbb{F},\mathcal{O}_{\mathbb{F}}(mL+nM))\rightarrow\bigoplus_{m,n\in\mathbb{Z}}H^{0}(X,\mathcal{O}_{X}(mL|_{X}+nM|_{X}))

may fail to be surjective. In fact by
[
2
, Thm.3.1]
, this map is definitely
not
surjective for families 1-2 of Table 1, and we do not know whether the algebra

R_{X}=\bigoplus_{m,n\in\mathbb{Z}}H^{0}(X,\mathcal{O}_{X}(mL|_{X}+nM|_{X}))

is finitely generated in these examples. (Note that, curiously, this map is surjective for at least one member of family 10 by
[
2
, Rem.2.5]
.) What appears to be needed in general then
is a way to capture
enough
of the bigraded algebra
R_{X}
via a free bigraded algebra
to be able to describe the fibration
f\colon X\to B
, at least in favourable cases, as embedded in a weighted scroll. An alternative, explored in the recent
preprint
[
6
]
by Coughlan and Pignatelli in particular, is to study conditions under which
X
embeds into a relative weighted projective bundle over the base
B
using pushforwards of
\mathcal{O}_{X}(nD)
to the base, following on from the local (on the base) analysis of Reid
[
18
]
.
Finding general conditions under which we get embeddings of low codimension appears to us to be a question worthy of further study.
