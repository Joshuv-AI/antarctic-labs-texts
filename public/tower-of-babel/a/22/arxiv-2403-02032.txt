Tiny fluctuations of the averaging process around its degenerate steady state
Federico Sau

Abstract
We analyze nonequilibrium fluctuations of the averaging process on 𝕋 ε d \mathbb{T}_{\varepsilon}^{d} , a continuous degenerate Gibbs sampler running over the edges of the discrete d d -dimensional torus. We show that, if we start from a smooth deterministic non-flat interface, recenter, blow-up by a non-standard CLT-scaling factor θ ε = ε − ( d / 2 + 1 ) \theta_{\varepsilon}=\varepsilon^{-(d/2+1)} , and rescale diffusively, Gaussian fluctuations emerge in the limit ε → 0 \varepsilon\to 0 . These fluctuations are purely dynamical, zero at times t = 0 t=0 and t = ∞ t=\infty , and non-trivial for t ∈ ( 0 , ∞ ) t\in(0,\infty) . We fully determine the correlation matrix of the limiting noise, non-diagonal as soon as d ≥ 2 d\geq 2 . The main technical challenge in this stochastic homogenization procedure lies in a LLN for a weighted space-time average of squared discrete gradients. We accomplish this through a Poincaré inequality with respect to the underlying randomness of the edge updates, a tool from Malliavin calculus in Poisson space. This inequality, combined with sharp gradients’ second moment estimates, yields quantitative variance bounds without prior knowledge of the limiting mean. Our method avoids higher (e.g., fourth) moment bounds, which seem inaccessible with the present techniques.

Introduction, model and main results

The averaging process on a graph is a continuous-space Markov chain, which is commonly interpreted as an opinion dynamics, a distributed algorithm, or an interface moving through a randomized sequence of deterministic local updates (see, e.g.,
[
6
,
3
,
4
,
40
]
and references therein). Its dynamics goes as follows. Attach i.i.d. Poisson clocks to edges, and assign real values to vertices; at the arrival times of these clocks, update the values with their average. As time runs, the averaging process converges to a flat configuration, and one major problem in the field is that of quantifying the speed of convergence to its degenerate equilibrium in terms of characteristic features of the underlying graph
[
10
,
43
,
14
]
.

In this paper, we examine scaling limits of the averaging process on the discrete
d
-dimensional torus
\mathbb{T}_{\varepsilon}^{d}
,
d\geq 1
, subjected to a diffusive space-time rescaling. In this setting, the averaging process, say
u_{t}^{\varepsilon}\in\mathbb{R}^{\mathbb{T}_{\varepsilon}^{d}}
, evolves by replacing, at rate
\varepsilon^{-2}
for each nearest neighbor pair
x,y\in\mathbb{T}_{\varepsilon}^{d}
, the values
(u_{t^{-}}^{\varepsilon}(x),u_{t^{-}}^{\varepsilon}(y))
with

(u_{t}^{\varepsilon}(x),u_{t}^{\varepsilon}(y))\coloneqq(\tfrac{1}{2}\left(u_{t^{-}}^{\varepsilon}(x)+u_{t^{-}}^{\varepsilon}(y)\right),\tfrac{1}{2}\left(u_{t^{-}}^{\varepsilon}(x)+u_{t^{-}}^{\varepsilon}(y)\right))\;\text{.}

(1.1)

Alternatively, in the language of stochastic homogenization,
u_{t}^{\varepsilon}

is the solution to the discrete parabolic problem

\partial_{t}u_{t}^{\varepsilon}=\nabla_{*}^{\varepsilon}\cdot\left(a_{\varepsilon}(t,{\,\cdot\,})\,\nabla^{\varepsilon}u_{t^{-}}^{\varepsilon}\right)
on
\mathbb{T}_{\varepsilon}^{d}
, in which the coefficient field
a_{\varepsilon}=(a_{\varepsilon}(t,x))_{t\geq 0,\,x\in\mathbb{T}_{\varepsilon}^{d}}
is random, time-dependent, and formally given by

a_{\varepsilon}(t,x)=\frac{\varepsilon^{2}}{2}\begin{pmatrix}{\rm d}N_{t}^{\varepsilon,1}(x)&&\\
&\ddots&\\
&&{\rm d}N_{t}^{\varepsilon,d}(x)\end{pmatrix}\;\text{,}\;\;\qquad t\geq 0\;\text{,}\;\;x\in\mathbb{T}_{\varepsilon}^{d}\;\text{.}

(1.2)

Here,
{\rm d}N_{t}^{\varepsilon,i}(x)
stands for the increments of the Poisson process of intensity
\varepsilon^{-2}
attached to the pair
x
,
x+\varepsilon e_{i}\in\mathbb{T}_{\varepsilon}^{d}
. This homogenization problem is then degenerate, in the sense that the coefficient field
a_{\varepsilon}
is a linear combination of Dirac deltas and, thus, does not satisfy any ellipticity conditions: there is no
C>0
satisfying neither
a_{\varepsilon}\geq C
, nor
a_{\varepsilon}\leq C
(in the matrix sense).

The first ergodic theorems in stochastic homogenization for linear elliptic and parabolic problems date back to the seminal works of Kozlov
[
36
]
and Papanicolau and Varadhan
[
42
]
. Since then, there has been an intense activity in considerably extending these qualitative results. In fact, even quantitative features of the solutions, such as regularity and fluctuations limit theorems, are by now well understood in the uniform ellipticity context (see, e.g.,
[
2
,
34
]
,
[
28
,
19
]
, and references therein), while the only degenerate examples are limited to supercritical percolation clusters
[
1
,
15
]
, rigid inclusions
[
18
]
and log-normal coefficient fields
[
29
,
12
]
.

In this article, we move one step forward by investigating dynamical fluctuations of the random
u_{t}^{\varepsilon}
in this time-dependent degenerate setting (cf. (
1.2
)). This result is the content of Theorem
1
, which could be regarded as the functional central limit theorem (FCLT) after the (quantitative) law of large numbers (LLNs) established in
[
45
]
. As recently worked out in some examples of random walks in time-dependent random environment (see, e.g.,
[
44
,
8
]
, all concerned with LLNs of random walks’ probabilities), our FCLT also draws upon the fundamental idea that adding a mixing dynamics potentially tempers spatial degeneracy. In our case, this dynamics consists in having zero conductances which suddenly take the value
+\infty
at the occurrence of a Poisson mark. As in most degenerate instances, this mixing mechanisms plays a crucial role in recovering on large scales regularity estimates which would deterministically hold true in the uniform ellipticity setting.

Our approach is probabilistic, based on martingales, as developed by Holley and Stroock
[
31
]
and successfully exploited within the context of interacting particle systems (see, e.g., the monographs
[
20
,
35
]
). Nonetheless, in contrast with most systems studied in that realm, the averaging process
u_{t}^{\varepsilon}
has no truly random ergodic states (they all consist of deterministic flat configurations). This lack of microscopic fluctuations at equilibrium prevents us to employ methods based on relative entropy, not having a clear notion of local equilibrium to compare the law of
u_{t}^{\varepsilon}
with (see, e.g.,
[
33
,
32
]
and references therein).

We overcome this obstacle by combining the aforementioned martingale-based approach with two main ingredients, typical of infinite-dimensional stochastic analysis. On the one hand, we control a key quantity — squared discrete gradients of
u_{t}^{\varepsilon}
— by expressing its expected value as an infinite series of iterated integrals. On the other hand, inspired by a series of recent works in stochastic homogenization, e.g.,
[
21
,
16
,
17
]
, we leverage the Poisson nature of the updates by operating with tools from Malliavin calculus (in Poisson space). These are the two building blocks in the proof of our second main result, Theorem
2
, a LLN for weighted space-time averages of squared discrete gradients.
Theorem
2
is also an essential step in the characterization of the limiting Gaussian process from Theorem
1
, and reveals the smallness of the fluctuations
u_{t}^{\varepsilon}-\mathds{E}^{\varepsilon}[u_{t}^{\varepsilon}]
, as well as the non-diagonal correlation structure of the limiting driving noise (despite the form of the coefficient field
a_{\varepsilon}
in (
1.2
) with i.i.d. diagonal entries).

In conclusion, as originally proposed in
[
3
,
4
]
, the averaging process (together with a long list of companions “random averages” models, e.g.,
[
25
,
9
,
11
,
37
,
30
,
5
,
26
]
, with either site or edge updates) is a natural nonequilibrium system of stochastic moving interfaces, whose quantitative features are still largely unexplored. These include, for instance, a regularity theory and worst-case mixing times on geometric settings other than the torus. The latter problem becomes particularly interesting in light of the works
[
10
,
14
]
, which show that on the simplest examples of expander graphs the averaging process’ degenerate local update rule dramatically affects the timescales relevant to relaxation, if compared to the homogenized dynamics.
We believe the combination of Markov chains’ and stochastic homogenization techniques that we develop here to be fruitful also in this context.

All throughout the article,
\mathbb{T}^{d}\coloneqq(\mathbb{R}/\mathbb{Z})^{d}

d
-dimensional torus, while

denotes its lattice discretization with mesh size
\varepsilon\in(0,1)
. For simplicity, we always assume
\varepsilon^{-1}\in\mathbb{N}
. Moreover, with a slight abuse of notation, we often identify the set
\mathbb{T}_{\varepsilon}^{d}
with the undirected graph obtained by connecting vertices of the form
x
and

with an edge, where

denotes the canonical basis of
\mathbb{R}^{d}
. Hence, letting
\left\lvert{\,\cdot\,}\right\rvert
denote the usual Euclidean distance on

, we will refer to sites
x,y\in\mathbb{T}_{\varepsilon}^{d}
satisfying
\left\lvert x-y\right\rvert=\varepsilon
as nearest neighbor vertices. Finally, we shall implicitly imply “holds true for all
\varepsilon\in(0,1)
with
\varepsilon^{-1}\in\mathbb{N}
” whenever
\varepsilon
appears in a statement without any other indications.

1.1
Averaging process

The
averaging process
on
\mathbb{T}_{\varepsilon}^{d}
is the continuous-time Markov process
(u_{t}^{\varepsilon})_{t\geq 0}
which evolves on
\mathbb{R}^{\mathbb{T}_{\varepsilon}^{d}}
by updating, independently and at rate
\varepsilon^{-2}
, the values at nearest neighbor vertices with their average value. A possible rigorous description of the model goes through the introduction of a family of i.i.d. Poisson processes

N^{\varepsilon}=(N_{t}^{\varepsilon,i}(x))_{t\geq 0,\,x\in\mathbb{T}_{\varepsilon}^{d},\,i=1,\ldots,d}

(1.3)

of intensity
\varepsilon^{-2}
, with the arrivals of
N_{\,\cdot\,}^{\varepsilon,i}(x)
corresponding to an update among the two nearest neighbor vertices
x
and
x+\varepsilon e_{i}\in\mathbb{T}_{\varepsilon}^{d}
. We write
\mathds{P}^{\varepsilon}
and
\mathds{E}^{\varepsilon}
for the associated probability law and expectation, respectively. Hence, for every starting configuration
u_{0}^{\varepsilon}\in\mathbb{R}^{\mathbb{T}_{\varepsilon}^{d}}
,
(u_{t}^{\varepsilon})_{t\geq 0}
is defined as the unique
càdlàg
solution to the following finite system of SDEs driven by Poisson processes: for every
x\in\mathbb{T}_{\varepsilon}^{d}
and
t>0
,

\displaystyle{\rm d}u_{t}^{\varepsilon}(x)

\displaystyle=\sum_{i=1}^{d}{\rm d}N_{t}^{\varepsilon,i}(x)\left[\tfrac{1}{2}\left(u_{t^{-}}^{\varepsilon}(x)+u_{t^{-}}^{\varepsilon}(x+\varepsilon e_{i})\right)-u_{t^{-}}^{\varepsilon}(x)\right]

(1.4)

\displaystyle\quad+\sum_{i=1}^{d}{\rm d}N_{t}^{\varepsilon,i}(x-\varepsilon e_{i})\left[\tfrac{1}{2}\left(u_{t^{-}}^{\varepsilon}(x)+u_{t^{-}}^{\varepsilon}(x-\varepsilon e_{i})\right)-u_{t^{-}}^{\varepsilon}(x)\right]

(1.5)

\displaystyle=\sum_{i=1}^{d}{\rm d}N_{t}^{\varepsilon,i}(x)\left[\tfrac{1}{2}\left(u_{t^{-}}^{\varepsilon}(x+\varepsilon e_{i})-u_{t^{-}}^{\varepsilon}(x)\right)\right]

(1.6)

\displaystyle\quad+\sum_{i=1}^{d}{\rm d}N_{t}^{\varepsilon,i}(x-\varepsilon e_{i})\left[\tfrac{1}{2}\left(u_{t^{-}}^{\varepsilon}(x-\varepsilon e_{i})-u_{t^{-}}^{\varepsilon}(x)\right)\right]\;\text{,}\;\;

(1.7)

with
{\rm d}N_{t}^{\varepsilon,i}(x)=N_{t}^{\varepsilon,i}(x)-N_{t^{-}}^{\varepsilon,i}(x)
denoting the increments of the Poisson process. Further, note that the spacing
\varepsilon
in
\mathbb{T}_{\varepsilon}^{d}
and the intensity
\varepsilon^{-2}
of the Poisson processes correspond to a diffusive space-time rescaling in (
1.7
).

Since
\mathbb{T}_{\varepsilon}^{d}
is a finite graph, it readily follows that, as a strong solution to the system above,
(u_{t}^{\varepsilon})_{t\geq 0}
exists unique for all initial conditions
u_{0}^{\varepsilon}\in\mathbb{R}^{\mathbb{T}_{\varepsilon}^{d}}
, and is a Markov process. Moreover, due to the connectedness of
\mathbb{T}_{\varepsilon}^{d}
,
u_{t}^{\varepsilon}
approaches, as
t\to\infty
and for all
u_{0}^{\varepsilon}\in\mathbb{R}^{\mathbb{T}_{\varepsilon}^{d}}
, the flat profile with value
\langle u_{0}^{\varepsilon}\rangle_{\varepsilon}\in\mathbb{R}
, the spatial average of
\mathbb{T}_{\varepsilon}^{d}
,

u_{t}^{\varepsilon}(x)\xrightarrow{t\to\infty}\langle u_{0}^{\varepsilon}\rangle_{\varepsilon}\coloneqq\varepsilon^{d}\sum_{y\in\mathbb{T}_{\varepsilon}^{d}}u_{0}^{\varepsilon}(y)\;\text{,}\;\;

(1.8)

\mathds{P}^{\varepsilon}
-a.s. and uniformly over
x\in\mathbb{T}_{\varepsilon}^{d}
. In particular, we observe that, despite
u_{t}^{\varepsilon}
is, generically, a random element of
\mathbb{R}^{\mathbb{T}_{\varepsilon}^{d}}
for all times
t>0
, the corresponding unique steady state is deterministic and identically equal to
\langle u_{0}^{\varepsilon}\rangle_{\varepsilon}
. Hence, the averaging dynamics is trivial at equilibrium, registering, in particular, no fluctuations.

In this work, we analyze asymptotics as
\varepsilon\to 0
of out-of-equilibrium fluctuations for this model, which, due to the degeneracy of the steady state, lacks a non-trivial notion of
local equilibrium
— a fundamental feature in the theory of hydrodynamic limits for interacting particle systems (see, e.g.,
[
35
]
). In words, we shall find a scaling factor
\theta_{\varepsilon}\to\infty
as
\varepsilon\to 0
and a non-trivial process
(\mathcal{Y}_{t})_{t\geq 0}
describing well, in the diffusive regime as
\varepsilon\to 0
, the nonequilibrium fluctuations encoded in the centered fields

\mathcal{Y}_{t}^{\varepsilon}\coloneqq\theta_{\varepsilon}\left\{\varepsilon^{d}\sum_{x\in\mathbb{T}_{\varepsilon}^{d}}\left(u_{t}^{\varepsilon}(x)-\mathds{E}^{\varepsilon}[u_{t}^{\varepsilon}(x)]\right)\delta_{x}\right\}\;\text{,}\;\;

(1.9)

after taking spatial averages against suitable test functions. Note that we consider a deterministic initial configuration
u_{0}^{\varepsilon}\in\mathbb{R}^{\mathbb{T}_{\varepsilon}^{d}}
, so to observe only dynamical fluctuations.

Maybe surprisingly at first, it turns out that, instead of the most standard CLT-scaling
\varepsilon^{-d/2}
, the averaging process’ nonequilibrium fluctuations require an extra factor
\varepsilon^{-1}
, thus,

\theta_{\varepsilon}=\varepsilon^{-(d/2+1)}\;\text{.}

(1.10)

In this sense, fluctuations are unusually small in our context. Next to this, the limiting fluctuations
\mathcal{Y}_{t}
become Gaussian, have rather explicit space-time correlations, and satisfy
\mathcal{Y}_{0}=\lim_{t\to\infty}\mathcal{Y}_{t}=0
.

1.2
Tiny nonequilibrium fluctuations

In order to best describe the limit
\mathcal{Y}_{t}
, let us further manipulate the equations (
1.7
) defining
u_{t}^{\varepsilon}
. By adopting the standard notation, for all
g\in\mathbb{R}^{\mathbb{T}_{\varepsilon}^{d}}
,
x\in\mathbb{T}_{\varepsilon}^{d}
and
i=1,\ldots,d
,

\displaystyle\begin{aligned} \nabla^{\varepsilon,i}g(x)&\coloneqq\varepsilon^{-1}\left(g(x+\varepsilon e_{i})-g(x)\right)\;\text{,}\;\;&&\nabla^{\varepsilon}g(x)\coloneqq(\nabla^{\varepsilon,i}g(x))_{i=1,\ldots,d}\;\text{,}\;\;\\
\nabla_{*}^{\varepsilon,i}g(x)&\coloneqq\varepsilon^{-1}\left(g(x)-g(x-\varepsilon e_{i})\right)\;\text{,}\;\;&&\nabla_{*}^{\varepsilon}g(x)\coloneqq(\nabla_{*}^{\varepsilon,i}g(x))_{i=1,\ldots,d}\;\text{,}\;\;\end{aligned}

(1.11)

\Delta_{\varepsilon}g(x)\coloneqq\sum_{i=1}^{d}\nabla_{*}^{\varepsilon,i}\nabla^{\varepsilon,i}g(x)=\sum_{i=1}^{d}\varepsilon^{-2}\left(g(x+\varepsilon e_{i})+g(x-\varepsilon e_{i})-2g(x)\right)\;\text{,}\;\;

(1.12)

and letting
{\rm diag}(a)\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times d}
denote the diagonal matrix having
a=(a^{i})_{i=1,\ldots,d}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
as diagonal elements,
the system (
1.7
) reads as

\displaystyle{\rm d}u_{t}^{\varepsilon}(x)

\displaystyle=\frac{\varepsilon}{2}\sum_{i=1}^{d}\big\{{\rm d}N_{t}^{\varepsilon,i}(x){\nabla^{\varepsilon,i}u_{t^{-}}^{\varepsilon}(x)}-{\rm d}N_{t}^{\varepsilon,i}(x-\varepsilon e_{i}){\nabla^{\varepsilon,i}u_{t^{-}}^{\varepsilon}(x-\varepsilon e_{i})}\big\}

(1.13)

\displaystyle=\frac{\varepsilon^{2}}{2}\sum_{i=1}^{d}\nabla_{*}^{\varepsilon,i}\big({\rm d}N_{t}^{\varepsilon,i}\,\nabla^{\varepsilon,i}u_{t^{-}}^{\varepsilon}\big)(x)

(1.14)

\displaystyle=\frac{\varepsilon^{2}}{2}\,\nabla_{*}^{\varepsilon}\cdot\left({\rm diag}({\rm d}N_{t}^{\varepsilon,{\,\cdot\,}})\nabla^{\varepsilon}u_{t^{-}}^{\varepsilon}\right)(x)\;\text{,}\;\;

(1.15)

or, equivalently, by passing to the compensated Poisson processes

\bar{N}_{t}^{\varepsilon,i}(x)\coloneqq N_{t}^{\varepsilon,i}(x)-\varepsilon^{-2}t\;\text{,}\;\;\qquad t\geq 0\;\text{,}\;\;x\in\mathbb{T}_{\varepsilon}^{d}\;\text{,}\;\;i=1,\ldots,d\;\text{,}\;\;

(1.16)

as

{\rm d}u_{t}^{\varepsilon}(x)=\tfrac{1}{2}\Delta_{\varepsilon}u_{t}^{\varepsilon}(x)\,{\rm d}t+\tfrac{\varepsilon^{2}}{2}\nabla_{*}^{\varepsilon}\cdot\left({\rm diag}({\rm d}\bar{N}_{t}^{\varepsilon,{\,\cdot\,}})\nabla u_{t^{-}}^{\varepsilon}\right)(x)\;\text{.}

(1.17)

Hence, next to a smoothening Laplacian term
\frac{1}{2}\Delta_{\varepsilon}u_{t}^{\varepsilon}
, the stochastic dynamics is driven by an i.i.d. diagonal noise which multiplies the discrete gradients
\nabla^{\varepsilon}u_{t^{-}}^{\varepsilon}
of the random averaging process. The gradient
\nabla_{*}^{\varepsilon}
accounts for the conservative nature of the noise.

Our main task is to establish, after centering and scaling with
\theta_{\varepsilon}
, a homogenization principle for this noise term, in which two main effects equally contribute: on the one side, the random gradients
\nabla^{\varepsilon}u_{t^{-}}^{\varepsilon}
get replaced by their deterministic counterparts; on the other side, the stochasticity lost in the previous step is superseded by the appearance of a non-diagonal structure of the limiting noise. More precisely, we show that the limiting fluctuations
\mathcal{Y}_{t}
solve (in a weak sense) the following SPDE on
\mathbb{T}^{d}

{\rm d}\mathcal{Y}_{t}=\tfrac{1}{2}\Delta\mathcal{Y}_{t}-\tfrac{1}{2}\nabla\cdot\left(\xi\,\nabla u_{t}\right)\;\text{,}\;\;

(1.18)

where:

•

\nabla u_{t}
is the gradient of the deterministic solution
u_{t}
to the heat equation on
\mathbb{T}^{d}

\partial_{t}u=\tfrac{1}{2}\Delta u\;\text{;}\;\;

(1.19)

•

\xi=(\xi^{i,j})_{i,j=1,\ldots,d}
is a matrix-valued space-time white noise whose covariance is informally given, for some
\mathfrak{a}=\mathfrak{a}(d)\in(0,1]
, by

\mathds{E}\big[\xi_{t}^{i,j}(x)\xi_{s}^{k,\ell}(y)\big]=\delta_{0}(t-s)\delta_{0}(x-y)\,\mathds{1}_{i=k}\mathds{1}_{j=\ell}\left(\left(1+\mathfrak{a}\right)\mathds{1}_{i=j}+\frac{1-\mathfrak{a}}{d-1}\,\mathds{1}_{i\neq j}\right)\;\text{,}\;\;

(1.20)

for all
x,y\in\mathbb{T}^{d}
,
t,s\geq 0
, and
i,j,k,\ell=1,\ldots,d
;

•

a weak solution to (
1.18
) is meant to be a solution to the corresponding martingale problem (cf. (
1.27
), (
5.2
)).

The precise statement of this convergence result is the content of the following theorem, before which we need to introduce some notation.
For all
k\in\mathbb{N}_{0}\cup\left\{\infty\right\}
, let
\mathcal{C}^{k}(\mathbb{T}^{d})
be the space of
k
-continuous differentiable functions on
\mathbb{T}^{d}
. We write
\mathcal{C}(\mathbb{T}^{d})=\mathcal{C}^{0}(\mathbb{T}^{d})

For all
\alpha\in\mathbb{R}

(1.21)

Here,

and

H^{\alpha}(\mathbb{T}^{d})\hookrightarrow H^{\beta}(\mathbb{T}^{d})
for all
\alpha>\beta
, and

for all

\mathcal{D}([0,\infty);H^{-\alpha}(\mathbb{T}^{d}))

(resp. continuous) trajectories taking values in

1.3
A law of large numbers for the squared gradients

In order to prove Theorem
1
, we follow a probabilistic approach, taking advantage of the Markovian nature of the averaging process
u_{t}^{\varepsilon}
. In formulas, recalling (
1.17
) and that
\mathcal{Y}_{0}^{\varepsilon}=0
, this corresponds to decompose the fields
\mathcal{Y}_{t}^{\varepsilon}
in (
1.9
), when tested against a test function
f\in\mathcal{C}^{\infty}(\mathbb{T}^{d})
, as

\mathcal{Y}_{t}^{\varepsilon}(f)=\int_{0}^{t}\mathcal{Y}_{s}^{\varepsilon}(\tfrac{1}{2}\Delta_{\varepsilon}f)\,{\rm d}s+\mathcal{M}_{t}^{\varepsilon}(f)\;\text{,}\;\;\qquad t\geq 0\;\text{,}\;\;

(1.24)

where, for
\theta_{\varepsilon}=\varepsilon^{-(d/2+1)}
,

\displaystyle\mathcal{M}_{t}^{\varepsilon}(f)

\displaystyle\coloneqq\int_{0}^{t}{\theta_{\varepsilon}\,\varepsilon^{d+2}}\sum_{x\in\mathbb{T}_{\varepsilon}^{d}}\left(\left({\rm diag}({\rm d}\bar{N}_{s}^{\varepsilon,{\,\cdot\,}})\nabla u_{s^{-}}^{\varepsilon}\right)(x)\right)\cdot\left(\tfrac{1}{2}\nabla^{\varepsilon}f(x)\right)

(1.25)

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{t}\varepsilon^{d/2+1}\sum_{i=1}^{d}\sum_{x\in\mathbb{T}_{\varepsilon}^{d}}{\left({\rm d}\bar{N}_{s}^{\varepsilon,i}(x)\,\nabla^{\varepsilon,i}u_{s^{-}}^{\varepsilon}(x)\right)}\left(\tfrac{1}{2}\nabla^{\varepsilon,i}f(x)\right)\;\text{,}\;\;\qquad t\geq 0\;\text{,}\;\;

is a square-integrable martingale (with respect to the filtration generated by the process
u_{t}^{\varepsilon}
). The martingale
\mathcal{M}_{t}^{\varepsilon}(f)
has jumps and may be described through its predictable quadratic variation, referred to as
\langle\mathcal{M}^{\varepsilon}(f)\rangle_{t}
and explicitly given by

\langle\mathcal{M}^{\varepsilon}(f)\rangle_{t}=\int_{0}^{t}\varepsilon^{d}\sum_{x\in\mathbb{T}_{\varepsilon}^{d}}\sum_{i=1}^{d}\left(\nabla^{\varepsilon,i}u_{s}^{\varepsilon}(x)\right)^{2}\left(\tfrac{1}{2}\nabla^{\varepsilon,i}f(x)\right)^{2}{\rm d}s\;\text{,}\;\;\qquad t\geq 0\;\text{.}

(1.26)

This derivation uses elementary properties of the compensated Poisson processes in (
1.16
), such as
\mathds{E}[\bar{N}_{t}^{\varepsilon,i}(x)]=0
and
\langle\bar{N}^{\varepsilon,i}(x)\rangle_{t}=\varepsilon^{-2}t
, as well as their independence.

In view of the decomposition in (
1.24
), the proof of Theorem
1
boils down to proving the following three claims:

1.

the sequence
((\mathcal{Y}_{t}^{\varepsilon})_{t\geq 0})_{\varepsilon}
is tight in
\mathcal{D}([0,\infty);H^{-\alpha}(\mathbb{T}^{d}))
, for
\alpha>3+d/2
;

2.

all limiting processes
(\mathcal{Y}_{t})_{t\geq 0}
are continuous;

3.

all limiting processes
(\mathcal{Y}_{t})_{t\geq 0}
are solutions to the following martingale problem

\mathcal{Y}_{t}(f)=\int_{0}^{t}\mathcal{Y}_{s}(\tfrac{1}{2}\Delta f)\,{\rm d}s+\mathcal{M}_{t}(f)\;\text{,}\;\;\qquad t\geq 0\;\text{,}\;\;f\in\mathcal{C}^{\infty}(\mathbb{T}^{d})\;\text{,}\;\;

(1.27)

where
(\mathcal{M}_{t}(f))_{t\geq 0}
is a martingale (with respect to the filtration generated by
(\mathcal{Y}_{t})_{t\geq 0}
) whose predictable quadratic variation is deterministic and compatible with the noise structure described in (
1.18
)–(
1.20
).

By the form of the limiting martingale and standard uniqueness results due to Holley and Stroock
[
31
]
, (ii) and (iii) together would uniquely characterize the limit as the one described in Theorem
1
.

While the proofs of the steps (i) and (ii) above are not particularly problematic, establishing the third step on the identification of the limiting martingale represents the major challenge of the proof of Theorem
1
. More specifically, thanks to classical martingale convergence theorems, our main task reduces to prove convergence in probability of the corresponding predictable quadratic variations. Reading out the explicit expression of these quadratic variations from the right-hand side of (
1.26
), this means proving a weak law of large numbers for weighted space-time averages of squares of discrete gradients of
u_{t}^{\varepsilon}
. Most of our analysis is devoted to the proof of this result, which, for later reference, we now state in form of a theorem.

Theorem 2
(LLN for squared discrete gradients).

Fix
u_{0}\in\mathcal{C}^{2}(\mathbb{T}^{d})
, and let
(u_{t}^{\varepsilon})_{t\geq 0}
and
(u_{t})_{t\geq 0}
denote, respectively, the averaging process and the solution to the heat equation (
1.19
), started from
u_{0}^{\varepsilon}=u_{0}\rvert_{\mathbb{T}_{\varepsilon}^{d}}
and
u_{0}\in\mathcal{C}^{2}(\mathbb{T}^{d})
. Then, for all
g=(g^{i})\in(\mathcal{C}([0,\infty)\times\mathbb{T}^{d}))^{d}
and
t\geq 0
, we have

\mathds{E}^{\varepsilon}\big[\left(\varGamma^{\varepsilon}_{t}(g)-\varGamma_{t}(g)\right)^{2}\big]\xrightarrow{\varepsilon\to 0}0\;\text{,}\;\;

(1.28)

where

\displaystyle\varGamma_{t}^{\varepsilon}(g)

\displaystyle\coloneqq\sum_{i=1}^{d}\int_{0}^{t}\varepsilon^{d}\sum_{x\in\mathbb{T}_{\varepsilon}^{d}}\left(\nabla^{\varepsilon,i}u_{s}^{\varepsilon}(x)\right)^{2}g_{s}^{i}(x)\,{\rm d}s\;\text{,}\;\;

(1.29)

\displaystyle\varGamma_{t}(g)

\displaystyle\coloneqq\sum_{i,j=1}^{d}\left(\left(1+\mathfrak{a}\right)\mathds{1}_{i=j}+\frac{1-\mathfrak{a}}{d-1}\,\mathds{1}_{i\neq j}\right)\int_{0}^{t}\int_{\mathbb{T}^{d}}\left(\nabla^{j}u_{s}(x)\right)^{2}g_{s}^{i}(x)\,{\rm d}x{\rm d}s\;\text{,}\;\;

(1.30)

and
\mathfrak{a}=\mathfrak{a}(d)\in(0,1]
is the constant in Theorem
1
and Remark
1.1
.

1.4
Proof ideas and techniques

The starting point in the proof of the LLN in Theorem
2
is a variance estimate (Theorem
3
). We derive this by tools from Malliavin calculus in Poisson space (see, e.g.,
[
38
,
39
]
and references therein). More specifically, we will resort to one specific tool in that context: the Poincaré inequality. In words, this inequality allows to estimate the variance of a functional
F=F(N^{\varepsilon})
of the underlying Poisson process in terms of (a norm of) its derivative
DF
with respect to an infinitesimal variation of
N^{\varepsilon}
. The functional
DF
is referred to as the Malliavin derivative.

As we will show, for the averaging process,
DF
comes with a nice probabilistic dynamical interpretation in terms of an evolving discrepancy (Section
3.1
). We exploit this point of view to turn this Poincaré inequality into an effective bound. In order to achieve this, we derive some new second-moment estimates for the discrete gradients of the averaging process, and combine them with properties of the averaging process earlier developed in
[
3
,
43
,
45
]
. Such improved estimates are key in the proof of Theorem
2
, for controlling the variance of
\varGamma_{t}^{\varepsilon}(g)
, and are sharp enough to determine the limit of its mean (Theorem
4
).

1.4.1
Poincaré inequality in Poisson space

In our setting, this Poincaré inequality may be rigorously stated as follows. Recall the Poisson process
N^{\varepsilon}
in (
1.3
) of the edge updates,
which we can describe, more precisely, through its intensity measure
\gamma_{\varepsilon}
on
{\mathbb{Y}}_{\varepsilon}\coloneqq[0,\infty)\times\mathbb{T}_{\varepsilon}^{d}\times\left\{1,\ldots,d\right\}
(endowed with the corresponding product Borel
\sigma
-field), given by the product measure

\gamma_{\varepsilon}=\varepsilon^{-2}{\rm d}t\otimes\nu_{\mathbb{T}_{\varepsilon}^{d}}\otimes\nu_{\left\{1,\ldots,d\right\}}\;\text{.}

(1.31)

Here,
{\rm d}t
stands for the Lebesgue measure on
[0,\infty)
, whereas, for a discrete set
S
,
\nu_{S}
denotes the counting measure on
S
. Letting
\mathbf{N}({\mathbb{Y}}_{\varepsilon})
be the space of integer-valued
\sigma
-finite measures on
{\mathbb{Y}}_{\varepsilon}
, we then interpret
N^{\varepsilon}
as a random element in the measurable space
\mathbf{N}({\mathbb{Y}}_{\varepsilon})
, endowed with
\mathcal{N}^{\varepsilon}
, the smallest
\sigma
-field making the mappings
m\in\mathbf{N}({\mathbb{Y}}_{\varepsilon})\mapsto m(A)\in\mathbb{R}
measurable, for all measurable sets
A\subset{\mathbb{Y}}_{\varepsilon}
. Now,
\mathds{P}^{\varepsilon}
and
\mathds{E}^{\varepsilon}
stand for the law and expectation of
N^{\varepsilon}
, respectively.

Then, the
Poincaré inequality in Poisson space
reads, for every measurable function
F:\mathbf{N}({\mathbb{Y}}_{\varepsilon})\to\mathbb{R}
, as

\displaystyle{\rm Var}_{\varepsilon}(F)

\displaystyle\coloneqq\mathds{E}^{\varepsilon}\big[\big(F(N^{\varepsilon})-\mathds{E}^{\varepsilon}[F(N^{\varepsilon})]\big)^{2}\big]\leq\int_{{\mathbb{Y}}_{\varepsilon}}\mathds{E}^{\varepsilon}\big[\left(D_{(t,x,i)}F(N^{\varepsilon})\right)^{2}\big]\,{\rm d}\gamma_{\varepsilon}(t,x,i)

(1.32)

\displaystyle\qquad=\varepsilon^{-(d+2)}\int_{0}^{\infty}\varepsilon^{d}\sum_{x\in\mathbb{T}_{\varepsilon}^{d}}\sum_{i=1}^{d}\mathds{E}^{\varepsilon}\big[\left(D_{(t,x,i)}F(N^{\varepsilon})\right)^{2}\big]\,{\rm d}t\;\text{.}

(1.33)

In this formula, for all
(t,x,i)\in{\mathbb{Y}}_{\varepsilon}
,
D_{(t,x,i)}
is the difference operator defined, for all measurable
F:\mathbf{N}({\mathbb{Y}}_{\varepsilon})\to\mathbb{R}
, as

D_{(t,x,i)}F(m)\coloneqq F(m+\delta_{(t,x,i)})-F(m)\;\text{,}\;\;\qquad m\in\mathbf{N}({\mathbb{Y}}_{\varepsilon})\;\text{,}\;\;

(1.34)

with
\delta_{(t,x,i)}
denoting the Dirac delta at
(t,x,i)\in{\mathbb{Y}}_{\varepsilon}
. Roughly speaking,
D_{(t,x,i)}
plays the role of derivative at the “point”
m\in\mathbf{N}({\mathbb{Y}}_{\varepsilon})
in the “direction”
(t,x,i)\in{\mathbb{Y}}_{\varepsilon}
. In our context, if we keep in mind the interpretation of
N^{\varepsilon}
as identifying the edge updates for the averaging process,
D_{(t,x,i)}F
measures the effect on
F
of the addition of an update at time
t
between the nearest neighbors
x
and
x+\varepsilon e_{i}\in\mathbb{T}_{\varepsilon}^{d}
.

1.4.2
Application of Poincaré inequality

In proving Theorem
2
, we will apply the Poincaré inequality (
1.33
) to functionals
F
of the form
\varGamma_{t}^{\varepsilon}(g)
given as in (
1.29
), for some
t>0
,
g=(g^{i})_{i=1,\ldots,d}\in(\mathcal{C}([0,\infty)\times\mathbb{T}^{d}))^{d}
, and initial condition
u_{0}^{\varepsilon}\in\mathbb{R}^{\mathbb{T}_{\varepsilon}^{d}}
. This yields

\displaystyle\begin{aligned} {\rm Var}_{\varepsilon}(\varGamma_{t}^{\varepsilon}(g))&\leq\varepsilon^{-(d+2)}\int_{0}^{\infty}\varepsilon^{d}\sum_{x\in\mathbb{T}_{\varepsilon}^{d}}\sum_{i=1}^{d}\mathds{E}^{\varepsilon}\big[\left(D_{(s,x,i)}\varGamma_{t}^{\varepsilon}(g)\right)^{2}\big]\,{\rm d}s\\
&=\varepsilon^{-(d+2)}\int_{0}^{t}\varepsilon^{d}\sum_{x\in\mathbb{T}_{\varepsilon}^{d}}\sum_{i=1}^{d}\mathds{E}^{\varepsilon}\big[\left(D_{(s,x,i)}\varGamma_{t}^{\varepsilon}(g)\right)^{2}\big]\,{\rm d}s\;\text{,}\;\;\end{aligned}

(1.35)

where the identity uses the fact that
\varGamma_{t}^{\varepsilon}(g)
does not depend on the history after time
t
. Moreover, since
(\varGamma_{t}^{\varepsilon}(g))_{t\geq 0}
is an additive functional of
(u_{t}^{\varepsilon})_{t\geq 0}
,
D_{(s,x,i)}\varGamma_{t}^{\varepsilon}(g)
may be further simplified, for
s\leq t
, as

D_{(s,x,i)}\varGamma_{t}^{\varepsilon}(g)=\sum_{j=1}^{d}\int_{s}^{t}\varepsilon^{d}\sum_{y\in\mathbb{T}_{\varepsilon}^{d}}\left\{\left(\nabla^{\varepsilon,j}E_{(s,x,i)}u_{r}^{\varepsilon}(y)\right)^{2}-\left(\nabla^{\varepsilon,j}u_{r}^{\varepsilon}(y)\right)^{2}\right\}g_{r}^{j}(y)\,{\rm d}r\;\text{,}\;\;

(1.36)

where, for
r\geq s
,
E_{(s,x,i)}u_{r}^{\varepsilon}
is the averaging process following the edge updates
N^{\varepsilon}
, conditioned to be equal, at time
r=s
, to

y\in\mathbb{T}_{\varepsilon}^{d}\longmapsto E_{(s,x,i)}u_{s}^{\varepsilon}(y)\coloneqq\begin{cases}\tfrac{1}{2}\left(u_{s}^{\varepsilon}(x)+u_{s}^{\varepsilon}(x+\varepsilon e_{i})\right)&\text{if}\ y=x\ \text{or}\ y=x+\varepsilon e_{i}\\
u_{s}^{\varepsilon}(y)&\text{else}\;\text{.}\end{cases}

(1.37)

Analogously to
D_{(s,x,i)}
(which acts on functionals
F
), the operator
E_{(s,x,i)}
acts on
(u_{r}^{\varepsilon})_{r\geq 0}
by imposing an extra averaging-update at vertices
x
and
x+\varepsilon e_{i}\in\mathbb{T}_{\varepsilon}^{d}
at time
s\geq 0
. Furthermore, by linearity of the averaging process,
E_{(s,x,i)}u_{r}^{\varepsilon}
may be further decomposed as

E_{(s,x,i)}u_{r}^{\varepsilon}=u_{r}^{\varepsilon}+w_{r}^{\varepsilon,(s,x,i)}\;\text{,}\;\;\qquad r\geq s\;\text{,}\;\;

(1.38)

namely, a sum of the original process
u_{r}^{\varepsilon}\in\mathbb{R}^{\mathbb{T}_{\varepsilon}^{d}}
and a “discrepancy”
w_{r}^{\varepsilon,(s,x,i)}\in\mathbb{R}^{\mathbb{T}_{\varepsilon}^{d}}
, both evolving as two averaging processes with two different initializations, sharing the same edge updates. In particular, they are not independent. In view of this, we may recast an estimate of the right-hand side of (
1.35
) into a bound on the evolution of such a discrepancy, and a central part of our analysis deals with establishing precise quantitative estimates on it and its discrete gradients (see Section
3.2
for more details).

1.4.3
Ultracontractivity and gradient estimates for the averaging process

Next to the Poincaré inequality in Poisson space, our analysis builds on two functional analytic properties of the averaging flow on the discrete torus: ultracontractivity and second-moment gradient estimates.

Ultracontractivity.

By ultracontractivity we mean a bound of the following form:
for some
C=C(d)>0
,

\mathds{E}^{\varepsilon}\big[\left\lVert u_{t}^{\varepsilon}\right\rVert_{L^{2}(\mathbb{T}_{\varepsilon}^{d})}^{2}\big]\leq\langle u_{0}^{\varepsilon}\rangle_{\varepsilon}^{2}+C\left\lVert u_{0}^{\varepsilon}\right\rVert_{L^{1}(\mathbb{T}_{\varepsilon}^{d})}^{2}\big(\left(t\vee\varepsilon^{2}\right)^{-d/2}\vee 1\big)\;\text{,}\;\;\qquad t\in(0,1]\;\text{.}

(1.39)

Here,
\left\lVert{\,\cdot\,}\right\rVert_{L^{p}(\mathbb{T}_{\varepsilon}^{d})}
,
p\in[1,\infty]
, denotes the
L^{p}
-norm on
\mathbb{T}_{\varepsilon}^{d}
with respect to the uniform measure
\varepsilon^{d}
, i.e., for all
g\in\mathbb{R}^{\mathbb{T}_{\varepsilon}^{d}}
,

\left\lVert g\right\rVert_{L^{p}(\mathbb{T}_{\varepsilon}^{d})}\coloneqq\bigg(\varepsilon^{d}\sum_{x\in\mathbb{T}_{\varepsilon}^{d}}\left\lvert g(x)\right\rvert^{p}\bigg)^{\frac{1}{p}}\ \text{for}\ p\in[1,\infty)\;\text{,}\;\;\quad\left\lVert g\right\rVert_{L^{\infty}(\mathbb{T}_{\varepsilon}^{d})}\coloneqq\sup_{x\in\mathbb{T}_{\varepsilon}^{d}}\left\lvert g(x)\right\rvert\;\text{.}

(1.40)

We write
\left\langle{\,\cdot\,}\,\middle|\,{\,\cdot\,}\right\rangle_{L^{2}(\mathbb{T}_{\varepsilon}^{d})}
for the inner product in
L^{2}(\mathbb{T}_{\varepsilon}^{d})
.

Estimates of type (
1.39
) for parabolic equations are classical, dating back to Nash’s work (
[
41
]
, see also
[
27
]
), and are well-known also in the graph context (see, e.g.,
[
22
]
). For the averaging process, they were first derived only recently in
[
43
]
, and further refined on
\mathbb{T}_{\varepsilon}^{d}
, establishing the stronger inequality
[
45
, Theorem 2.1]

\mathds{E}^{\varepsilon}\big[\left\lVert u_{t}^{\varepsilon}\right\rVert_{H^{1}(\mathbb{T}_{\varepsilon}^{d})}^{2}\big]\leq C\left\lVert u_{0}^{\varepsilon}\right\rVert_{L^{1}(\mathbb{T}_{\varepsilon}^{d})}^{2}\left(t\vee\varepsilon^{2}\right)^{-(d/2+1)}\;\text{,}\;\;\qquad t\geq 0\;\text{.}

(1.41)

On the left-hand side above,
\left\lVert{\,\cdot\,}\right\rVert_{H^{1}(\mathbb{T}_{\varepsilon}^{d})}
reads,
for all
g\in\mathbb{R}^{\mathbb{T}_{\varepsilon}^{d}}
, as

\left\lVert g\right\rVert_{H^{1}(\mathbb{T}_{\varepsilon}^{d})}\coloneqq\bigg(\varepsilon^{d}\sum_{x\in\mathbb{T}_{\varepsilon}^{d}}\sum_{i=1}^{d}\left\lvert\nabla^{\varepsilon,i}g(x)\right\rvert^{2}\bigg)^{\frac{1}{2}}\;\text{.}

(1.42)

In order to see why (
1.41
) is stronger than (
1.39
), it suffices to integrate over time the following identity due to Aldous and Lanoue
[
3
, Eq. (2.7)]
:

\frac{{\rm d}}{{\rm d}t}\,\mathds{E}\big[\left\lVert u_{t}^{\varepsilon}\right\rVert_{L^{2}(\mathbb{T}_{\varepsilon}^{d})}^{2}\big]=-\frac{1}{2}\,\mathds{E}\big[\left\lVert u_{t}^{\varepsilon}\right\rVert_{H^{1}(\mathbb{T}_{\varepsilon}^{d})}^{2}\big]\;\text{,}\;\;\qquad t\geq 0\;\text{.}

(1.43)

Finally, we observe that the bounds in (
1.39
) and (
1.41
) are effective for all
t=O(1)
, as well as for very small times
t\ll 1
. We emphasize that such estimates for
t\ll 1
will play a key role in Lemma
3.3
, when proving Theorem
2
.

Gradient estimates.

The second important and new ingredient is a thorough analysis of the dynamics of discrete gradients of the averaging process on the torus on a diffusive space-time scale. This will be carried out in Sections
2
and
4
. In a nutshell, this approach goes through:

1.

first, solving explicitly the SDEs governing the evolution of
(\nabla^{\varepsilon,i}u_{t}^{\varepsilon})_{t\geq 0}\subset\mathbb{R}^{\mathbb{T}_{\varepsilon}^{d}}
(Lemma
2.1
);

2.

then, reducing asymptotics of the second moments
\mathds{E}\big[(\nabla^{\varepsilon,i}u_{t}^{\varepsilon}(x))^{2}\big]
to the analysis of infinitely-many iterated integrals (Propositions
2.1
,
4.1
and
4.2
).

This latter step is what allows us to determine the non-diagonal form of the limiting correlations (cf. (
1.20
)). Next to asymptotics, we extract some new pseudo-contractivity and small-time estimates for gradients’ second moments of independent interest (Lemmas
2.3
–
2.5
).

It is worth to mention that similar estimates were recently obtained in
[
45
, Proposition 2.2]
, aiming at capturing the correct order of magnitude of
\mathds{E}\big[(\nabla^{\varepsilon,i}u_{t}^{\varepsilon}(x))^{2}\big]
for a wider time window, including the regime
t\gg 1
as
\varepsilon\to 0
. However, while the approach in the aforementioned work only provides bounds, in the present article, we are able to determine exact asymptotics by restricting to times
t=O(1)
. Finally,
our analysis relies solely on second moments, not involving higher (e.g., fourth) moment estimates, whose control seems to be out of reach with the present techniques.

Structure of the paper

The rest of the paper is organized as follows. In Section
2

3
and
4
we prove Theorem
2
on the LLN for
\varGamma_{t}^{\varepsilon}
defined in (
1.29
). More specifically, in the former section, we prove that variances vanish as

, while in the latter one we establish the convergence of the means. Section

2
Discrete gradients

In this section, we derive a number of technical results on the random discrete gradients of
u_{t}^{\varepsilon}
on
\mathbb{T}_{\varepsilon}^{d}
, of use for the subsequent sections. Before starting, let us introduce some notation.

After recalling that
\mathcal{N}^{\varepsilon}
is the
\sigma
-field associated to
N^{\varepsilon}
(cf. Section
1.4.1
), we define
\mathcal{F}^{\varepsilon}\coloneqq\mathcal{N}^{\varepsilon}\otimes\sigma(u_{0}^{\varepsilon})
as the product
\sigma
-field associated to the Poisson process and the averaging process’ initial conditions. We write
(\mathcal{F}_{t}^{\varepsilon})_{t\geq 0}
for the corresponding filtration, with respect to which
(u_{t}^{\varepsilon})_{t\geq 0}\subset\mathbb{R}^{\mathbb{T}_{\varepsilon}^{d}}
is adapted.
In particular,
u_{0}^{\varepsilon}\in\mathbb{R}^{\mathbb{T}_{\varepsilon}^{d}}
is
\mathcal{F}_{0}^{\varepsilon}
-measurable.
Furthermore, recalling the definitions of
\nabla^{\varepsilon,i}
,
\nabla_{*}^{\varepsilon,i}
,
\nabla^{\varepsilon}
,
\nabla_{*}^{\varepsilon}
and
\Delta_{\varepsilon}
from (
1.11
)–(
1.12
), we introduce the random walk
(X_{t}^{\varepsilon})_{t\geq 0}
on
\mathbb{T}_{\varepsilon}^{d}
, with semigroup
(P_{t}^{\varepsilon})_{t\geq 0}
and infinitesimal generator
\tfrac{1}{2}\Delta_{\varepsilon}
. Moreover, we let
(p_{t}^{\varepsilon}(x,y))_{t\geq 0,\,x,y\in\mathbb{T}_{\varepsilon}^{d}}
denote the corresponding transition probabilities. In what follows, we will repeatedly use that all discrete gradients commute, namely,

\nabla^{\varepsilon,i}\nabla^{\varepsilon,j}=\nabla^{\varepsilon,j}\nabla^{\varepsilon,i}\;\text{,}\;\;\quad\nabla^{\varepsilon,i}\nabla_{*}^{\varepsilon,j}=\nabla_{*}^{\varepsilon,j}\nabla^{\varepsilon,i}\;\text{,}\;\;\quad\nabla_{*}^{\varepsilon,i}\nabla_{*}^{\varepsilon,j}=\nabla_{*}^{\varepsilon,j}\nabla_{*}^{\varepsilon,i}\;\text{,}\;\;

(2.1)

for all
i,j=1,\ldots,d
, from which we readily obtain the following well-known intertwining relations for the semigroup and the gradients on
\mathbb{T}_{\varepsilon}^{d}
:

P_{t}^{\varepsilon}\nabla^{\varepsilon,i}=\nabla^{\varepsilon,i}P_{t}^{\varepsilon}\;\text{,}\;\;\quad P_{t}^{\varepsilon}\nabla_{*}^{\varepsilon,i}=\nabla_{*}^{\varepsilon,i}P_{t}^{\varepsilon}\;\text{,}\;\;\qquad t\geq 0\;\text{,}\;\;i=1,\ldots,d\;\text{.}

(2.2)

We remark that
\Delta_{\varepsilon}
and
P_{t}^{\varepsilon}
are symmetric operators on
L^{2}(\mathbb{T}_{\varepsilon}^{d})
(cf. (
1.40
)) and that, by translation and rotational invariance of the random walk’s dynamics on
\mathbb{T}_{\varepsilon}^{d}
, we have
p_{t}^{\varepsilon}(x,y)=p_{t}^{\varepsilon}(y,x)=p_{t}^{\varepsilon}(y-x,0)=p_{t}^{\varepsilon}(x-y,0)
. Finally, in order to lighten the notation, we write
\mathds{P}=\mathds{P}^{\varepsilon}
and
\mathds{E}=\mathds{E}^{\varepsilon}
all throughout.

2.1
Stochastic dynamics of discrete gradients

As a first step, we write the random discrete gradients of
u_{t}^{\varepsilon}
in a mild-solution form. For this purpose, recall the definition of compensated Poisson processes
\bar{N}^{\varepsilon}=(\bar{N}_{t}^{\varepsilon,i}(x))_{t\geq 0,\,x\in\mathbb{T}_{\varepsilon}^{d},\,i=1,\ldots,d}
, as well as the equations (
1.17
) solved by
u_{t}^{\varepsilon}
.

Lemma 2.1
(Mild solution).

Fix
u_{0}^{\varepsilon}\in\mathbb{R}^{\mathbb{T}_{\varepsilon}^{d}}
. Then,

\mathds{P}
-a.s., we have

\nabla^{\varepsilon,i}u_{t}^{\varepsilon}(x)=P_{t}^{\varepsilon}\left(\nabla^{\varepsilon,i}u_{0}^{\varepsilon}\right)(x)+\sum_{j=1}^{d}\sum_{y\in\mathbb{T}_{\varepsilon}^{d}}\int_{0}^{t}b_{t-s}^{\varepsilon,i,j}(x,y)\,\nabla^{\varepsilon,j}u_{s^{-}}^{\varepsilon}(y)\,{\rm d}\bar{N}_{s}^{\varepsilon,j}(y)\;\text{,}\;\;

(2.3)

for all
t>0
,
x\in\mathbb{T}_{\varepsilon}^{d}
and
i=1,\ldots,d
,
where

b_{t}^{\varepsilon,{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}{i,j}}}(x,y)\coloneqq\tfrac{1}{2}\left(p_{t}^{\varepsilon}(x+\varepsilon e_{i},y)+p_{t}^{\varepsilon}(x-\varepsilon e_{j},y)-p_{t}^{\varepsilon}(x+\varepsilon e_{i}-\varepsilon e_{j},y)-p_{t}^{\varepsilon}(x,y)\right)\;\text{.}

(2.4)

Passing to the discrete gradients in (
1.17
), we obtain

\displaystyle{\rm d}(\nabla^{\varepsilon,i}u_{t}^{\varepsilon})(x)

\displaystyle=\tfrac{1}{2}\nabla^{\varepsilon,i}\Delta_{\varepsilon}u_{t}^{\varepsilon}(x)\,{\rm d}t+\tfrac{\varepsilon^{2}}{2}\nabla^{\varepsilon,i}\big(\nabla_{*}^{\varepsilon}\cdot\left({\rm diag}({\rm d}\bar{N}_{t}^{\varepsilon,{\,\cdot\,}})\nabla u_{t^{-}}^{\varepsilon}\right)\big)(x)

(2.5)

\displaystyle=\tfrac{1}{2}\Delta_{\varepsilon}(\nabla^{\varepsilon,i}u_{t}^{\varepsilon})(x)+\frac{\varepsilon^{2}}{2}\sum_{j=1}^{d}\nabla_{*}^{\varepsilon,j}\nabla^{\varepsilon,i}\big({\rm d}\bar{N}_{t}^{\varepsilon,j}\,\nabla^{\varepsilon,j}u_{t^{-}}^{\varepsilon}\big)(x)\;\text{,}\;\;

(2.6)

where for the second step we used the commutation relations (
2.1
). Note that the above finite system of SDEs (with compensated-Poisson noise) has the form
{\rm d}f_{t}=\tfrac{1}{2}\Delta_{\varepsilon}f_{t}\,{\rm d}t+F_{t}
, hence, recalling that
P_{t}^{\varepsilon}=\exp\left(\tfrac{t}{2}\Delta_{\varepsilon}\right)
,
\nabla^{\varepsilon,i}u_{t}^{\varepsilon}
admits the following explicit form,
\mathds{P}
-a.s.,

\displaystyle\nabla^{\varepsilon,i}u_{t}^{\varepsilon}{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}{(x)}}

\displaystyle=P_{t}^{\varepsilon}\big(\nabla^{\varepsilon,i}u_{0}^{\varepsilon}\big)(x)+\int_{0}^{t}P_{t-s}^{\varepsilon}\bigg(\frac{\varepsilon^{2}}{2}\sum_{j=1}^{d}\nabla_{*}^{\varepsilon,j}\nabla^{\varepsilon,i}\left({\rm d}\bar{N}_{s}^{\varepsilon,j}\,\nabla^{\varepsilon,j}u_{s^{-}}^{\varepsilon}\right)\bigg)(x)

(2.7)

\displaystyle=P_{t}^{\varepsilon}\big(\nabla^{\varepsilon,i}u_{0}^{\varepsilon}\big)(x)+\sum_{j=1}^{d}\frac{\varepsilon^{2}}{2}\int_{0}^{t}\nabla_{*}^{\varepsilon,j}\nabla^{\varepsilon,i}P_{t-s}^{\varepsilon}\big({\rm d}\bar{N}_{s}^{\varepsilon,j}\,\nabla^{\varepsilon,j}u_{s^{-}}^{\varepsilon}\big)(x)\;\text{,}\;\;

(2.8)

where the last step employs the
intertwinings in (
2.2
). The desired claim now follows by recalling (
2.4
) and observing that, for all
g\in\mathbb{R}^{\mathbb{T}_{\varepsilon}^{d}}
, we have

\displaystyle\varepsilon^{2}\,\nabla_{*}^{\varepsilon,j}\nabla^{\varepsilon,i}P_{t}^{\varepsilon}g(x)

\displaystyle=\varepsilon\left(\nabla^{\varepsilon,i}P_{t}^{\varepsilon}g(x)-\nabla^{\varepsilon,i}P_{t}^{\varepsilon}g(x-\varepsilon e_{j})\right)

(2.9)

\displaystyle={P_{t}^{\varepsilon}g(x+\varepsilon e_{i})-P_{t}^{\varepsilon}g(x)-P_{t}^{\varepsilon}g(x-\varepsilon e_{j}+\varepsilon e_{i})+P_{t}^{\varepsilon}g(x-\varepsilon e_{j})}

(2.10)

\displaystyle=\sum_{y\in\mathbb{T}_{\varepsilon}^{d}}2b_{t}^{\varepsilon,{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}{i,j}}}(x,y)\,g(y)\;\text{.}

(2.11)

This concludes the proof of the lemma.
∎

2.2
Second moments of discrete gradients

Let us iterate the expression from Lemma
2.1
to obtain formulas for the expectation of
\nabla^{\varepsilon,i}u_{t}^{\varepsilon}(x)\,\nabla^{\varepsilon,i}v_{t}^{\varepsilon}(x)
, where
(u_{t}^{\varepsilon})_{t\geq 0}
and
(v_{t}^{\varepsilon})_{t\geq 0}
are two copies of the averaging process starting from
u_{0}^{\varepsilon}
and
v_{0}^{\varepsilon}\in\mathbb{R}^{\mathbb{T}_{\varepsilon}^{d}}
, respectively, driven by the same Poisson noise. For this and subsequent results, the following quantity defined in terms of
b_{t}^{\varepsilon,i,j}(x,y)
(see (
2.4
))

q_{t}^{\varepsilon,i,j}(x,y)\coloneqq\big(\varepsilon^{-1}b_{t}^{\varepsilon,i,j}(x,y)\big)^{2}\;\text{,}\;\;\qquad t\geq 0\;\text{,}\;\;x,y\in\mathbb{T}_{\varepsilon}^{d}\;\text{,}\;\;i,j=1,\ldots,d\;\text{,}\;\;

(2.12)

will play a key role, whose main properties are collected in the next lemma and proved in
Appendix
.

Lemma 2.2
.

For all
t\geq 0
,
x,y\in\mathbb{T}_{\varepsilon}^{d}
and
i,j=1,\ldots,d
, we have

q_{t}^{\varepsilon,i,j}(x,y)=q_{t}^{\varepsilon,i,j}(y,x)\;\text{.}

(2.13)

Moreover, the following quantity

\mathcal{Q}_{\varepsilon}(t)\coloneqq\sum_{j=1}^{d}\sum_{y\in\mathbb{T}_{\varepsilon}^{d}}q_{t}^{\varepsilon,i,j}(x,y)

(2.14)

depends only on
d\geq 1
and
t\geq 0
(thus, not on
x\in\mathbb{T}_{\varepsilon}^{d}
and
i=1,\ldots,d
),
and satisfies

\int_{0}^{\infty}\mathcal{Q}_{\varepsilon}(t)\,{\rm d}t=\frac{1}{2}\;\text{.}

(2.15)

We state a notational remark.

Remark 2.1

(Notation)
.

Here and all throughout, we adopt the following standard notation: for all
t>0
and
k\in\mathbb{N}
,

[0,t]_{>}^{k}\coloneqq\left\{(s_{1},\ldots,s_{k})\in[0,t]^{k}:s_{1}>\ldots>s_{k}\right\}\;\text{,}\;\;

(2.16)

while, for all
h\in\mathcal{C}([0,t]_{>}^{k})
,

\int_{[0,t]_{>}^{k}}{\rm d}s_{1}\cdots{\rm d}s_{k}\,h({\,\cdot\,})\coloneqq\int_{0}^{t}\int_{0}^{s_{1}}\cdots\int_{0}^{s_{k-1}}h({\,\cdot\,})\,{\rm d}s_{k}\cdots{\rm d}s_{1}\;\text{.}

(2.17)

Moreover, within this context, we implicitly identify
s_{0}\coloneqq t
and, whenever
x\in\mathbb{T}_{\varepsilon}^{d}
is a fixed vertex and
i=1,\ldots,d
a fixed direction,
y_{0}\coloneqq x
and
j_{0}=i
. We employ analogous notations when replacing
0
(resp.
t
) by
-\infty
(resp.
\infty
), in
[0,t]_{>}^{k}
.

Proposition 2.1
.

For all
u_{0}^{\varepsilon},v_{0}^{\varepsilon}\in\mathbb{R}^{\mathbb{T}_{\varepsilon}^{d}}
,
t>0
,
x\in\mathbb{T}_{\varepsilon}^{d}
and
i=1,\ldots,d
, we have

\mathds{E}\big[\nabla^{\varepsilon,i}u_{t}^{\varepsilon}(x)\,\nabla^{\varepsilon,i}v_{t}^{\varepsilon}(x)\big]=\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\Pi_{t}^{\varepsilon,i,k}(u_{0}^{\varepsilon},v_{0}^{\varepsilon})(x)\;\text{,}\;\;

(2.18)

the above series being absolutely convergent.
Here, each summand is defined, for
k=0
, as

\Pi_{t}^{\varepsilon,i,0}(u_{0}^{\varepsilon},v_{0}^{\varepsilon})(x)\coloneqq P_{t}^{\varepsilon}(\nabla^{\varepsilon,i}u_{0}^{\varepsilon})(x)\,P_{t}^{\varepsilon}(\nabla^{\varepsilon,i}v_{0}^{\varepsilon})(x)\;\text{,}\;\;

(2.19)

while, for
k\geq 1
, as (identifying
s_{0}=t
,
y_{0}=x
and
j_{0}=i
, cf. Remark
2.1
)

\displaystyle\qquad\Pi_{t}^{\varepsilon,i,k}(u_{0}^{\varepsilon},v_{0}^{\varepsilon})(x)

(2.20)

\displaystyle\coloneqq\int_{[0,t]_{>}^{k}}{\rm d}s_{1}\cdots{\rm d}s_{k}\sum_{y_{1},\ldots,y_{k}\in\mathbb{T}_{\varepsilon}^{d}}\sum_{j_{1},\ldots,j_{k}=1}^{d}\left(\prod_{\ell=1}^{k}q_{s_{\ell-1}-s_{\ell}}^{\varepsilon,j_{\ell-1},j_{\ell}}(y_{\ell-1},y_{\ell})\right)\Pi_{s_{k}}^{\varepsilon,j_{k},0}(u_{0}^{\varepsilon},v_{0}^{\varepsilon})(y_{k})\;\text{,}\;\;

(2.21)

where
q^{\varepsilon}
is given in (
2.12
).
In particular, for
u_{0}^{\varepsilon}=v_{0}^{\varepsilon}
, we have

\mathds{E}\big[\big(\nabla^{\varepsilon,i}u_{t}^{\varepsilon}(x)\big)^{2}\big]=\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\Pi_{t}^{\varepsilon,i,k}(u_{0}^{\varepsilon})(x)\;\text{,}\;\;\quad\text{with}\ \Pi_{t}^{\varepsilon,i,k}(u_{0}^{\varepsilon})\coloneqq\Pi_{t}^{\varepsilon,i,k}(u_{0}^{\varepsilon},u_{0}^{\varepsilon})\;\text{.}

(2.22)

The two proofs of (
2.18
) and (
2.22
) are essentially the same; for notational convenience, let us discuss in detail the one of (
2.22
), and only quickly comment on that of (
2.18
).
Recall (
2.3
) and note that

P_{t}^{\varepsilon}\big(\nabla^{\varepsilon,i}u_{0}^{\varepsilon}\big)
is
\mathcal{F}_{0}^{\varepsilon}
-measurable, while

\mathds{E}\bigg[\int_{0}^{t}b_{t-s}^{\varepsilon,i,j}(x,y)\,\big({\rm d}\bar{N}_{s}^{\varepsilon,j}\,\nabla^{\varepsilon,j}u_{s^{-}}^{\varepsilon}\big)(y)\bigg|\mathcal{F}_{0}^{\varepsilon}\bigg]=0\;\text{,}\;\;

(2.23)

because
s\mapsto b_{t-s}^{\varepsilon,i,j}(x,y)\,\nabla^{\varepsilon,j}u_{s^{-}}^{\varepsilon}(y)
is predictable and
s\mapsto\bar{N}^{\varepsilon,j}_{s}(y)
is a martingale with respect to
(\mathcal{F}_{s}^{\varepsilon})_{s\geq 0}
. Hence,
the left-hand side of (
2.22
) reads as

\displaystyle\mathds{E}\big[\big(\nabla^{\varepsilon,i}u_{t}^{\varepsilon}(x)\big)^{2}\big]

(2.24)

\displaystyle\qquad=\left(P_{t}^{\varepsilon}(\nabla^{\varepsilon,i}u_{0}^{\varepsilon})(x)\right)^{2}+\mathds{E}\bigg[\bigg(\sum_{j=1}^{d}\sum_{y\in\mathbb{T}_{\varepsilon}^{d}}\int_{0}^{t}b_{t-s}^{\varepsilon,i,j}(x,y)\,\big({\rm d}\bar{N}_{s}^{\varepsilon,j}(y)\,\nabla^{\varepsilon,j}u_{s^{-}}^{\varepsilon}(y)\big)\bigg)^{2}\bigg]

(2.25)

\displaystyle\qquad=\left(P_{t}^{\varepsilon}(\nabla^{\varepsilon,i}u_{0}^{\varepsilon})(x)\right)^{2}+\sum_{j=1}^{d}\sum_{y\in\mathbb{T}_{\varepsilon}^{d}}\int_{0}^{t}\big(b_{t-s}^{\varepsilon,i,j}(x,y)\big)^{2}\,\varepsilon^{-2}\,\mathds{E}\big[\big(\nabla^{\varepsilon,j}u_{s}^{\varepsilon}(y)\big)^{2}\big]\,{\rm d}s

(2.26)

\displaystyle\qquad=\left(P_{t}^{\varepsilon}(\nabla^{\varepsilon,i}u_{0}^{\varepsilon})(x)\right)^{2}+\sum_{j=1}^{d}\sum_{y\in\mathbb{T}_{\varepsilon}^{d}}\int_{0}^{t}q_{t-s}^{\varepsilon,i,j}(x,y)\,\mathds{E}\big[\big(\nabla^{\varepsilon,j}u_{s}^{\varepsilon}(y)\big)^{2}\big]\,{\rm d}s\;\text{,}\;\;

(2.27)

where for the second step we used the fact that
\bar{N}^{\varepsilon}
is a family of i.i.d. martingales, each with predictable quadratic variation given by
s\mapsto\varepsilon^{-2}\,s
, whereas the third step employs the definition in (
2.12
). Hence, letting
U^{\varepsilon,i}_{t}(x)\coloneqq\mathds{E}\big[(\nabla^{\varepsilon,i}u_{t}^{\varepsilon}(x))^{2}\big]
and recalling (
2.19
), we may rewrite the above identity as

U_{t}^{\varepsilon,i}(x)=\Pi_{t}^{\varepsilon,i,{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}{0}}}(u_{0}^{\varepsilon})(x)+\sum_{j=1}^{d}\sum_{y\in\mathbb{T}_{\varepsilon}^{d}}\int_{0}^{t}q_{t-s}^{\varepsilon,i,j}(x,y)\,U_{s}^{\varepsilon,j}(y)\,{\rm d}s\;\text{,}\;\;

(2.28)

which, by non-negativity of all three functions involved, may be iterated infinitely often, yielding (
2.22
), which
a priori
may be infinite. However, recalling (
2.19
) and (
2.15
), we first obtain
, for all
k\in\mathbb{N}_{0}
,

\sup_{t\geq 0}\big\lVert\Pi_{t}^{\varepsilon,j,{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}{k}}}(u_{0}^{\varepsilon})\big\rVert_{L^{\infty}(\mathbb{T}_{\varepsilon}^{d})}\leq\big\lVert\nabla^{\varepsilon,j}u_{0}^{\varepsilon}\big\rVert_{L^{\infty}(\mathbb{T}_{\varepsilon}^{d})}^{2}{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}{2^{-k}}}<\infty\;\text{,}\;\;\qquad j=1,\ldots,d\;\text{,}\;\;

(2.29)

and, then,

\mathds{E}\big[(\nabla^{\varepsilon,i}u_{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}{t}}}^{\varepsilon}(x))^{2}\big]\leq\max_{j=1,\ldots,d}\left\lVert\nabla^{\varepsilon,j}u_{0}^{\varepsilon}\right\rVert_{L^{\infty}(\mathbb{T}_{\varepsilon}^{d})}^{2}\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}2^{-k}=2\max_{j=1,\ldots,d}\left\lVert\nabla^{\varepsilon,j}u_{0}^{\varepsilon}\right\rVert_{L^{\infty}(\mathbb{T}_{\varepsilon}^{d})}^{2}<\infty\;\text{.}

(2.30)

This proves the desired claim when
u_{0}^{\varepsilon}=v_{0}^{\varepsilon}
, i.e., (
2.22
). When
u_{0}^{\varepsilon}\neq v_{0}^{\varepsilon}
, we similarly have

\displaystyle\mathds{E}\big[\nabla^{\varepsilon,i}u_{t}^{\varepsilon}(x)\,\nabla^{\varepsilon,i}v_{t}^{\varepsilon}(x)\big]

(2.31)

\displaystyle\qquad=\Pi_{t}^{\varepsilon,i,0}(u_{0}^{\varepsilon},v_{0}^{\varepsilon})(x)+\sum_{j=1}^{d}\sum_{y\in\mathbb{T}_{\varepsilon}^{d}}\int_{0}^{t}q_{t-s}^{\varepsilon,i,j}(x,y)\,\mathds{E}\big[\nabla^{\varepsilon,i}u_{s}^{\varepsilon}(x)\,\nabla^{\varepsilon,i}v_{s}^{\varepsilon}(x)\big]\,{\rm d}s\;\text{,}\;\;

(2.32)

which, thanks to

\sup_{t\geq 0}\big\lVert\Pi_{t}^{\varepsilon,j,0}(u_{0}^{\varepsilon},v_{0}^{\varepsilon})\big\rVert_{L^{\infty}(\mathbb{T}_{\varepsilon}^{d})}\leq\big\lVert\nabla^{\varepsilon,j}u_{0}^{\varepsilon}\big\rVert_{L^{\infty}(\mathbb{T}_{\varepsilon}^{d})}\big\lVert\nabla^{\varepsilon,j}v_{0}^{\varepsilon}\big\rVert_{L^{\infty}(\mathbb{T}_{\varepsilon}^{d})}<\infty\;\text{,}\;\;\qquad j=1,\ldots,d\;\text{,}\;\;

(2.33)

and the identity in (
2.15
), may be iterated, yielding (
2.18
).
∎

2.3
Technical estimates

All lemmas in this section are required for the proof of Lemma
3.2
below. We start with the following upper bound on integrals of second moments of discrete gradients, which may be thought of as an annealed pseudo-contractivity bound in
H^{1}(\mathbb{T}_{\varepsilon}^{d})
.

Lemma 2.3
.

Recall the definition of
\left\lVert{\,\cdot\,}\right\rVert_{H^{1}(\mathbb{T}_{\varepsilon}^{d})}
from (
1.42
). Then, for all
u_{0}^{\varepsilon}\in\mathbb{R}^{\mathbb{T}_{\varepsilon}^{d}}
and
t>0
, we have

\mathds{E}\big[\left\lVert u_{t}^{\varepsilon}\right\rVert_{H^{1}(\mathbb{T}_{\varepsilon}^{d})}^{2}\big]\leq 2\left\lVert u_{0}^{\varepsilon}\right\rVert_{H^{1}(\mathbb{T}_{\varepsilon}^{d})}^{2}\;\text{.}

(2.34)

By definition of
\left\lVert{\,\cdot\,}\right\rVert_{H^{1}(\mathbb{T}_{\varepsilon}^{d})}
and Proposition
2.1
, we have

\mathds{E}\big[\left\lVert u_{t}^{\varepsilon}\right\rVert_{H^{1}(\mathbb{T}_{\varepsilon}^{d})}^{2}\big]=\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\varepsilon^{d}\sum_{x\in\mathbb{T}_{\varepsilon}^{d}}\sum_{i=1}^{d}\Pi_{t}^{\varepsilon,i,k}(u_{0}^{\varepsilon})(x)\;\text{.}

(2.35)

Hence, the desired claim in (
2.34
) follows from

\varepsilon^{d}\sum_{x\in\mathbb{T}_{\varepsilon}^{d}}\sum_{i=1}^{d}\Pi_{t}^{\varepsilon,i,k}(u_{0}^{\varepsilon})(x)\leq 2^{-k}\left\lVert u_{0}^{\varepsilon}\right\rVert_{H^{1}(\mathbb{T}_{\varepsilon}^{d})}^{2}\;\text{,}\;\;\qquad k\in\mathbb{N}_{0}\;\text{.}

(2.36)

We start with the case
k=0
. Recalling (
2.19
), we have

\displaystyle\varepsilon^{d}\sum_{x\in\mathbb{T}_{\varepsilon}^{d}}\sum_{i=1}^{d}\Pi_{t}^{\varepsilon,i,0}(u_{0}^{\varepsilon})(x)

\displaystyle=\sum_{i=1}^{d}\varepsilon^{d}\sum_{x\in\mathbb{T}_{\varepsilon}^{d}}\left(P_{t}^{\varepsilon}(\nabla^{\varepsilon,i}u_{0}^{\varepsilon})(x)\right)^{2}

(2.37)

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{i=1}^{d}\varepsilon^{d}\sum_{x\in\mathbb{T}_{\varepsilon}^{d}}P_{t}^{\varepsilon}(\nabla^{\varepsilon,i}u_{0}^{\varepsilon})^{2}(x)

(2.38)

\displaystyle=\sum_{i=1}^{d}\varepsilon^{d}\sum_{x\in\mathbb{T}_{\varepsilon}^{d}}(\nabla^{\varepsilon,i}u_{0}^{\varepsilon}(x))^{2}=\left\lVert u_{0}^{\varepsilon}\right\rVert_{H^{1}(\mathbb{T}_{\varepsilon}^{d})}^{2}\;\text{,}\;\;

(2.39)

where the second step follows by Jensen inequality, while the third step used the symmetry of
P_{t}^{\varepsilon}
in
L^{2}(\mathbb{T}_{\varepsilon}^{d})
. This proves (
2.36
) for
k=0
. For the general case
k\geq 1
, first recall (
2.20
). Then,

\displaystyle\varepsilon^{d}\sum_{x\in\mathbb{T}_{\varepsilon}^{d}}\sum_{i=1}^{d}\Pi_{t}^{\varepsilon,i,k}(u_{0}^{\varepsilon})(x)

(2.40)

\displaystyle\qquad=\int_{[0,t]_{>}^{k}}{\rm d}s_{1}\cdots{\rm d}s_{k}{\sum_{y_{0},\ldots,y_{k-1}\in\mathbb{T}_{\varepsilon}^{d}}\sum_{j_{0},\ldots,j_{k-1}=1}^{d}\left(\prod_{\ell=1}^{k}q_{s_{\ell-1}-s_{\ell}}^{\varepsilon,j_{\ell-1},j_{\ell}}(y_{\ell-1},y_{\ell})\right)}

(2.41)

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad\times\left(\varepsilon^{d}\sum_{y_{k}\in\mathbb{T}_{\varepsilon}^{d}}\sum_{j_{k}=1}^{d}\Pi_{s_{k}}^{\varepsilon,j_{k},0}(u_{0}^{\varepsilon})(y_{k})\right)

(2.42)

\displaystyle\qquad=\int_{[0,t]_{>}^{k}}{\rm d}s_{1}\cdots{\rm d}s_{k}\left(\prod_{\ell=1}^{k}\mathcal{Q}_{\varepsilon}(s_{\ell}-s_{\ell-1})\right)\left(\varepsilon^{d}\sum_{y_{k}\in\mathbb{T}_{\varepsilon}^{d}}\sum_{j_{k}=1}^{d}\Pi_{s_{k}}^{\varepsilon,j_{k},0}(u_{0}^{\varepsilon})(y_{k})\right)

(2.43)

\displaystyle\qquad\leq\left\lVert u_{0}\right\rVert_{H^{1}(\mathbb{T}_{\varepsilon}^{d})}^{2}\int_{[0,t]_{>}^{k}}{\rm d}s_{1}\cdots{\rm d}s_{k}\left(\prod_{\ell=1}^{k}\mathcal{Q}_{\varepsilon}(s_{\ell}-s_{\ell-1})\right)

(2.44)

\displaystyle\qquad\leq\left\lVert u_{0}^{\varepsilon}\right\rVert_{H^{1}(\mathbb{T}_{\varepsilon}^{d})}^{2}\left(\int_{0}^{\infty}\mathcal{Q}_{\varepsilon}(s)\,{\rm d}s\right)^{k}

(2.45)

\displaystyle\qquad=2^{-k}\left\lVert u_{0}^{\varepsilon}\right\rVert_{H^{1}(\mathbb{T}_{\varepsilon}^{d})}^{2}\;\text{,}\;\;

(2.46)

where the second step follows from (
2.13
)–(
2.14
), the third step used the inequality in (
2.39
), the fourth step used the non-negativity of
\mathcal{Q}_{\varepsilon}
, whereas the fifth step follows from (
2.15
). This proves (
2.36
) for
k\geq 1
, thus, concludes the proof of the lemma.
∎

The following lemma presents a second bound on the expectation of discrete gradients.

Lemma 2.4
.

For all
u_{0}^{\varepsilon},v_{0}^{\varepsilon}\in\mathbb{R}^{\mathbb{T}_{\varepsilon}^{d}}
and
t>0
, we have

{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}{\varepsilon^{d}\sum_{x\in\mathbb{T}_{\varepsilon}^{d}}\sum_{j=1}^{d}\left\lvert\mathds{E}\big[\nabla^{\varepsilon,j}u_{t}^{\varepsilon}(x)\,\nabla^{\varepsilon,j}v_{t}^{\varepsilon}(x)\big]\right\rvert}}\leq 2\,\big(\max_{i=1,\ldots,d}\left\lVert\nabla^{\varepsilon,i}u_{0}^{\varepsilon}\right\rVert_{L^{\infty}(\mathbb{T}_{\varepsilon}^{d})}\big)\bigg(\sum_{i=1}^{d}\left\lVert\nabla^{\varepsilon,i}v_{0}^{\varepsilon}\right\rVert_{L^{1}(\mathbb{T}_{\varepsilon}^{d})}\bigg)\;\text{.}

(2.47)

Recall (
2.19
). Since

\left\lvert\Pi_{t}^{\varepsilon,i,0}(u_{0}^{\varepsilon},v_{0}^{\varepsilon})(x)\right\rvert\leq\left\lVert\nabla^{\varepsilon,i}u_{0}\right\rVert_{L^{\infty}(\mathbb{T}_{\varepsilon}^{d})}P_{t}^{\varepsilon}\left\lvert\nabla^{\varepsilon,i}v_{0}^{\varepsilon}\right\rvert(x)\;\text{,}\;\;

(2.48)

we obtain

\displaystyle\varepsilon^{d}\sum_{x\in\mathbb{T}_{\varepsilon}^{d}}\sum_{i=1}^{d}\left\lvert\Pi_{t}^{\varepsilon,i,0}(u_{0}^{\varepsilon},v_{0}^{\varepsilon})(x)\right\rvert

\displaystyle\leq\max_{i=1,\ldots,d}\left\lVert\nabla^{\varepsilon,i}u_{0}^{\varepsilon}\right\rVert_{L^{\infty}(\mathbb{T}_{\varepsilon}^{d})}\left(\varepsilon^{d}\sum_{i=1}^{d}\sum_{x\in\mathbb{T}_{\varepsilon}^{d}}P_{t}^{\varepsilon}\left\lvert\nabla^{\varepsilon,i}v_{0}^{\varepsilon}\right\rvert(x)\right)

(2.49)

\displaystyle=\max_{i=1,\ldots,d}\left\lVert\nabla^{\varepsilon,i}u_{0}^{\varepsilon}\right\rVert_{L^{\infty}(\mathbb{T}_{\varepsilon}^{d})}\left(\sum_{i=1}^{d}\left\lVert\nabla^{\varepsilon,i}v_{0}^{\varepsilon}\right\rVert_{L^{1}(\mathbb{T}_{\varepsilon}^{d})}\right)\;\text{,}\;\;

(2.50)

where for the last step we used the invariance of
P_{t}^{\varepsilon}
, i.e.,
\langle P_{t}^{\varepsilon}g\rangle_{\varepsilon}=\langle g\rangle_{\varepsilon}
, for all
g\in\mathbb{T}_{\varepsilon}^{d}
.
Finally, recalling (
2.18
), we get

\displaystyle\varepsilon^{d}\sum_{x\in\mathbb{T}_{\varepsilon}^{d}}\sum_{i=1}^{d}\left\lvert\mathds{E}\big[\nabla^{\varepsilon,i}u_{t}^{\varepsilon}(x)\,\nabla^{\varepsilon,i}v_{t}^{\varepsilon}(x)\big]\right\rvert

(2.51)

\displaystyle\qquad\leq\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\varepsilon^{d}\sum_{x\in\mathbb{T}_{\varepsilon}^{d}}\sum_{i=1}^{d}\left\lvert\Pi_{t}^{\varepsilon,i,k}(u_{0}^{\varepsilon},v_{0}^{\varepsilon})(x)\right\rvert

(2.52)

\displaystyle\qquad\leq\max_{i=1,\ldots,k}\left\lVert\nabla^{\varepsilon,i}u_{0}^{\varepsilon}\right\rVert_{L^{\infty}(\mathbb{T}_{\varepsilon}^{d})}\left(\sum_{i=1}^{d}\left\lVert\nabla^{\varepsilon,i}v_{0}^{\varepsilon}\right\rVert_{L^{1}(\mathbb{T}_{\varepsilon}^{d})}\right)\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}2^{-k}\;\text{,}\;\;

(2.53)

where for the last step we first used (
2.50
) and then (
2.15
). This concludes the proof of the lemma.
∎

The following estimate turns out to be useful for small times and concentrated initial conditions.
In what follows, we let
B_{\sigma}(x)\subset\mathbb{T}^{d}
denote the open ball of radius
\sigma\in(0,\frac{1}{2})
around
x\in\mathbb{T}^{d}
, and exploit the following classical exit-time estimate for the random walk
(X_{t}^{\varepsilon})_{t\geq 0}
(see, e.g.,
[
23
, Eq. (4.4)]
and references therein): for all
t\in(0,1)
,
x\in\mathbb{T}_{\varepsilon}^{d}
and
\rho\in(0,\frac{1}{2})
,

\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}y\in\mathbb{T}_{\varepsilon}^{d}\\
\left\lvert x-y\right\rvert>\rho\end{subarray}}p_{t}^{\varepsilon}(x,y)\leq\mathbf{P}_{x}^{\varepsilon}\left(\sup_{s\in[0,t]}\left\lvert X_{t}^{\varepsilon}-x\right\rvert>\rho\right)\leq c_{1}\exp\left(-\frac{c_{2}\,\rho}{t^{1/2}\vee\varepsilon}\right)\;\text{,}\;\;

(2.54)

for some constants
c_{1},c_{2}>0
depending only on
d\geq 1
.

Lemma 2.5
.

Let the initial condition
u_{0}^{\varepsilon}\in\mathbb{R}^{\mathbb{T}_{\varepsilon}^{d}}
be vanishing outside
B_{\sigma}(x)
, for some
\sigma\in(0,\frac{1}{2})
and
x\in\mathbb{T}_{\varepsilon}^{d}
. Then, for all
t\in(0,1)
,
\rho\in(\sigma+\varepsilon,\frac{1}{2})
and
n\in\mathbb{N}
, we have

\sum_{i=1}^{d}\mathds{E}\big[\|\mathds{1}_{B_{\rho}^{c}(x)}\nabla^{\varepsilon,i}u_{t}^{\varepsilon}\|_{L^{2}(\mathbb{T}_{\varepsilon}^{d})}^{2}\big]\leq C\left\lVert u_{0}^{\varepsilon}\right\rVert_{L^{\infty}(\mathbb{T}_{\varepsilon}^{d})}^{2}\varepsilon^{-2}\bigg\{\varepsilon^{-2}\exp\left(-\frac{C^{\prime}(\rho-\sigma)}{n(t^{1/2}\vee\varepsilon)}\right)+2^{-n}\bigg\}\;\text{,}\;\;

(2.55)

for some constants
C,C^{\prime}>0
depending only on
d\geq 1
.

Recall (
2.22
). Then, for every integer
n\geq 1
, the left-hand side of (
2.55
) reads as

\displaystyle\sum_{i=1}^{d}\mathds{E}\big[\|\mathds{1}_{B_{\rho}^{c}(x)}\nabla^{\varepsilon,i}u_{t}^{\varepsilon}\|_{L^{2}(\mathbb{T}_{\varepsilon}^{d})}^{2}\big]=\varepsilon^{d}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}y\in\mathbb{T}_{\varepsilon}^{d}\\
\left\lvert x-y\right\rvert>\rho\end{subarray}}\sum_{i=1}^{d}\mathds{E}\big[\left(\nabla^{\varepsilon,i}u_{t}^{\varepsilon}(y)\right)^{2}\big]

(2.56)

\displaystyle\qquad=\varepsilon^{d}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}y\in\mathbb{T}_{\varepsilon}^{d}\\
\left\lvert x-y\right\rvert>\rho\end{subarray}}\sum_{i=1}^{d}\sum_{k=0}^{n}\Pi_{t}^{\varepsilon,i,k}(u_{0}^{\varepsilon})(y)+\varepsilon^{d}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}y\in\mathbb{T}_{\varepsilon}^{d}\\
\left\lvert x-y\right\rvert>\rho\end{subarray}}\sum_{i=1}^{d}\sum_{k=n+1}^{\infty}\Pi_{t}^{\varepsilon,i,k}(u_{0}^{\varepsilon})(y)

(2.57)

\displaystyle\qquad\leq\varepsilon^{d}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}y\in\mathbb{T}_{\varepsilon}^{d}\\
\left\lvert x-y\right\rvert>\rho\end{subarray}}\sum_{i=1}^{d}\sum_{k=0}^{n}\Pi_{t}^{\varepsilon,i,k}(u_{0}^{\varepsilon})(y)+\sum_{k=n+1}^{\infty}\bigg(\varepsilon^{d}\sum_{y\in\mathbb{T}_{\varepsilon}^{d}}\sum_{i=1}^{d}\Pi_{t}^{\varepsilon,i,k}(u_{0}^{\varepsilon})(y)\bigg)

(2.58)

\displaystyle\qquad\leq\varepsilon^{d}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}y\in\mathbb{T}_{\varepsilon}^{d}\\
\left\lvert x-y\right\rvert>\rho\end{subarray}}\sum_{i=1}^{d}\sum_{k=0}^{n}\Pi_{t}^{\varepsilon,i,k}(u_{0}^{\varepsilon})(y)+2^{-n}\left\lVert u_{0}^{\varepsilon}\right\rVert_{H^{1}(\mathbb{T}_{\varepsilon}^{d})}^{2}\;\text{,}\;\;

(2.59)

where the last step used (
2.36
) to bound the expression in parenthesis, and
2^{-n}=\sum_{k=n+1}^{\infty}2^{-k}
. Recall (
2.19
). By Jensen inequality,
{\rm supp}(u_{0}^{\varepsilon})\subset B_{\sigma}(x)
, and the crude bound

\max_{j=1,\ldots,d}\|\nabla^{\varepsilon,j}u_{0}^{\varepsilon}\|_{L^{\infty}(\mathbb{T}_{\varepsilon}^{d})}\leq 2\varepsilon^{-1}\|u_{0}^{\varepsilon}\|_{L^{\infty}(\mathbb{T}_{\varepsilon}^{d})}\;\text{,}\;\;

(2.60)

we get, for all
s\in(0,1)
,
j=1,\ldots,d
, and
y\in\mathbb{T}_{\varepsilon}^{d}
,

\Pi_{s}^{\varepsilon,j,0}(u_{0}^{\varepsilon})(y)\leq{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}{4}}\varepsilon^{-2}\left\lVert u_{0}^{\varepsilon}\right\rVert_{L^{\infty}(\mathbb{T}_{\varepsilon}^{d})}^{2}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}z\in\mathbb{T}_{\varepsilon}^{d}\\
\left\lvert x-z\right\rvert<\sigma+\varepsilon\end{subarray}}p_{s}^{\varepsilon}(y,z)\;\text{.}

(2.61)

Thanks to the exit-time estimate (
2.54
), we have, for
y\in\mathbb{T}_{\varepsilon}^{d}
satisfying
\left\lvert x-y\right\rvert>\sigma+\varepsilon
,

\Pi_{s}^{\varepsilon,j,0}(u_{0}^{\varepsilon})(y)\leq 2\varepsilon^{-2}\left\lVert u_{0}^{\varepsilon}\right\rVert_{L^{\infty}(\mathbb{T}_{\varepsilon}^{d})}^{2}\left\{c_{1}\exp\left(-\frac{c_{2}\left(\left\lvert x-y\right\rvert-\sigma-\varepsilon\right)}{s^{1/2}\vee\varepsilon}\right)\right\}\;\text{.}

(2.62)

As a consequence,
we obtain, for
k=0
and for some
C_{0}=C_{0}(d)>0
,

\displaystyle\varepsilon^{d}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}y\in\mathbb{T}_{\varepsilon}^{d}\\
\left\lvert x-y\right\rvert>\rho\end{subarray}}\sum_{i=1}^{d}\Pi_{t}^{\varepsilon,i,0}(u_{0}^{\varepsilon})(y)

\displaystyle\leq C_{0}\,\varepsilon^{-2}\left\lVert u_{0}^{\varepsilon}\right\rVert_{L^{\infty}(\mathbb{T}_{\varepsilon}^{d})}^{2}\exp\left(-\frac{c_{2}\left(\rho-\sigma-\varepsilon\right)}{t^{1/2}\vee\varepsilon}\right)\;\text{.}

(2.63)

For
k=1,\ldots,n
, recalling (
2.20

3.2
Proof strategy

We estimate the three terms in (

In this way, we avoid to estimate fourth moments of discrete gradients, for which we are not able to recover convergent recursive inequalities, as done for the second moments.

In this spirit, the first term
\varLambda_{1}^{\varepsilon}
is handled rather easily by squaring the time integral, applying Cauchy-Schwarz, and gain
ing

-norms of

from the time integration and Aldous-Lanoue identity (

The third term

\big(1-Ct^{-3/2}\varepsilon^{3/2}\big)^{k}\,R_{\infty}^{\varepsilon,i,j,k}\leq R_{t}^{\varepsilon,i,j,k}\;\text{,}\;\;\qquad k\leq\lfloor\varepsilon^{-1}\rfloor\;\text{.}

(4.15)

Indeed, for all
k\leq\lfloor\varepsilon^{-1}\rfloor
,
R_{t}^{\varepsilon,i,j,k}
is bounded below by

\sum_{j_{0},\ldots,j_{k}=1}^{d}\mathds{1}_{j_{0}=i}\mathds{1}_{j_{k}=j}\int_{t-\varepsilon t}^{t}{\rm d}s_{1}\,\mathcal{Q}_{\varepsilon}^{j_{0},j_{1}}({\color[rgb]{0,0,0}{t-s_{1}}})\int_{s_{1}-\varepsilon t}^{s_{1}}{\rm d}s_{2}\cdots\int_{s_{k-1}-\varepsilon t}^{s_{k-1}}{\rm d}s_{k}\,\mathcal{Q}_{\varepsilon}^{j_{k-1},j}(s_{k-1}-s_{k})\;\text{,}\;\;

(4.16)

where we used
\mathcal{Q}_{\varepsilon}^{j_{\ell-1},j_{\ell}}\geq 0
and
k\leq\lfloor\varepsilon^{-1}\rfloor
(guaranteeing that
s_{k-1}-\varepsilon t\geq 0
). Thanks to Lemma
4.2
, we estimate each nested integral as follows: for all
\ell=1,\ldots,k
,

\displaystyle\int_{s_{\ell-1}-\varepsilon t}^{s_{\ell-1}}\mathcal{Q}_{\varepsilon}^{j_{\ell-1},j_{\ell}}(s_{\ell-1}-s_{\ell})\,{\rm d}s_{\ell}

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{\infty}\mathcal{Q}_{\varepsilon}^{j_{\ell-1},j_{\ell}}(s)\,{\rm d}s-\int_{t\varepsilon}^{\infty}\mathcal{Q}_{\varepsilon}^{j_{\ell-1},j_{\ell}}(s)\,{\rm d}s

(4.17)

\displaystyle\geq\int_{0}^{\infty}\mathcal{Q}_{\varepsilon}^{j_{\ell-1},j_{\ell}}(s)\,{\rm d}s-c_{3}\,t^{-3/2}\varepsilon^{3/2}

(4.18)

\displaystyle\geq\big(1-Ct^{-3/2}\varepsilon^{3/2}\big)\int_{0}^{\infty}\mathcal{Q}_{\varepsilon}^{j_{\ell-1},j_{\ell}}(s)\,{\rm d}s\;\text{,}\;\;

(4.19)

where the first inequality used Lemma
4.2
, whereas for the last inequality we employed the non-degeneracy of the limits (
4.8
) (thus, ensuring
\liminf_{\varepsilon\to 0}\int_{0}^{\infty}\mathcal{Q}_{\varepsilon}^{i,j}(s)\,{\rm d}s>0
) and chose a sufficiently large constant
C=C(d)>0
. Inserting these bounds into (
4.16
), we obtain (
4.15
). In conclusion, by combining (
4.14
) and (
4.15
), we get

\displaystyle\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\left\lvert R_{t}^{\varepsilon,i,j,k}-R_{\infty}^{\varepsilon,i,j,k}\right\rvert

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{k=1}^{\lfloor\varepsilon^{-1}\rfloor}\left(1-\big(1-C^{\prime}t^{-3/2}\varepsilon^{3/2}\big)^{k}\right)R_{\infty}^{\varepsilon,i,j,k}+\sum_{k=\lfloor\varepsilon^{-1}\rfloor+1}^{\infty}R_{\infty}^{\varepsilon,i,j,k}

(4.20)

\displaystyle\leq{1-\big(1-C^{\prime}t^{-3/2}\varepsilon^{3/2}\big)^{\lfloor\varepsilon^{-1}\rfloor}}+2^{-\lfloor\varepsilon^{-1}\rfloor}\;\text{,}\;\;

(4.21)

where the last inequality used (
4.9
) and property (
2.15
). In conclusion, we obtain

\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\left\lvert R_{t}^{\varepsilon,i,j,k}-R_{\infty}^{\varepsilon,i,j{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}{,}}k}\right\rvert\xrightarrow{\varepsilon\to 0}0\;\text{,}\;\;

(4.22)

for all
t>0
and
i,j=1,\ldots,d
.

We now prove that, for all
i,j=1,\ldots,d
, we have

\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}R_{\infty}^{\varepsilon,i,j,k}\xrightarrow{\varepsilon\to 0}{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}{\mathfrak{r}^{i,j}}}\coloneqq\frac{1}{1-\mathfrak{b}+\mathfrak{c}}\times\begin{cases}\mathfrak{b}+\mathfrak{c}&\text{if}\ i=j\\
2\mathfrak{c}&\text{if}\ i\neq j\;\text{,}\end{cases}

(4.23)

where
\mathfrak{b},\mathfrak{c}\in(0,\frac{1}{2}]
are the limits in (
4.8
).

Recall the definition of
R_{\infty}^{\varepsilon,i,j,k}
,
k\in\mathbb{N}
, from (
4.13
).
Thanks to Lemma
4.1
,
R_{\infty}^{\varepsilon,i,j,k}
may be precisely determined by counting how many times adjacent indices in the sequence
(j_{0},j_{1},\ldots,j_{k-1},j_{k})\in\left\{1,\ldots,d\right\}^{k+1}
coincide. For this purpose, let
N(j_{0},\ldots,j_{k})\in\left\{0,1,\ldots,k\right\}
denote the number of adjacent indices with the same value, i.e.,

N(j_{0},\ldots,j_{k})\coloneqq\sum_{\ell=1}^{k}\mathds{1}_{j_{\ell-1}=j_{\ell}}\;\text{.}

(4.24)

Then, by (
4.7
), we have

R_{\infty}^{\varepsilon,i,j,k}=\sum_{j_{0},\ldots,j_{k}=1}^{d}\left(\mathfrak{b}_{\varepsilon}^{N(j_{0},\ldots,j_{k})}\mathfrak{c}_{\varepsilon}^{k-N(j_{0},\ldots,j_{k})}\right)\mathds{1}_{j_{0}=i}\mathds{1}_{j_{k}=j}\;\text{.}

(4.25)

Clearly, the above quantity depends on
i,j=1,\ldots,d
only through
\mathds{1}_{i=j}
; therefore, we may introduce the following shorthand notation: for all
k\in\mathbb{N}

F_{k}^{\varepsilon}\coloneqq R_{\infty}^{\varepsilon,i,j,k}\quad\text{if}\ i=j\;\text{,}\;\;\qquad G_{k}^{\varepsilon}\coloneqq R_{\infty}^{\varepsilon,i,j,k}\quad\text{if}\ i\neq j\;\text{.}

(4.26)

When
d\geq 2
, such quantities satisfy

F_{1}^{\varepsilon}=\mathfrak{b}_{\varepsilon}\;\text{,}\;\;\qquad G_{1}^{\varepsilon}=\mathfrak{c}_{\varepsilon}\;\text{,}\;\;

(4.27)

as well as the following one-step recursive formula: for
k\geq 2
,

\displaystyle F_{k}^{\varepsilon}

\displaystyle=\ \mathfrak{b}_{\varepsilon}\,F_{k-1}^{\varepsilon}+\left(\mathfrak{c}_{\varepsilon}+\left(d-2\right)\mathfrak{c}_{\varepsilon}\right)G_{k-1}^{\varepsilon}

\displaystyle=\ \mathfrak{b}_{\varepsilon}\,F_{k-1}^{\varepsilon}+\left(\tfrac{1}{2}-\mathfrak{b}_{\varepsilon}\right)G_{k-1}^{\varepsilon}

(4.28)

\displaystyle G_{k}^{\varepsilon}

\displaystyle=\ \mathfrak{c}_{\varepsilon}\,F_{k-1}^{\varepsilon}+\left(\mathfrak{b}_{\varepsilon}+\left(d-2\right)\mathfrak{c}_{\varepsilon}\right)G_{k-1}^{\varepsilon}

\displaystyle=\ \mathfrak{c}_{\varepsilon}\,F_{k-1}^{\varepsilon}+\left(\tfrac{1}{2}-\mathfrak{c}_{\varepsilon}\right)G_{k-1}^{\varepsilon}\;\text{,}\;\;

(4.29)

where for the second set of identities we used relation (
4.10
).
In other words, letting
M_{\varepsilon}\coloneqq\begin{pmatrix}\mathfrak{b}_{\varepsilon}&\tfrac{1}{2}-\mathfrak{b}_{\varepsilon}\\
\mathfrak{c}_{\varepsilon}&\tfrac{1}{2}-\mathfrak{c}_{\varepsilon}\end{pmatrix}
, we just obtained

\begin{pmatrix}F_{k}^{\varepsilon}\\
G_{k}^{\varepsilon}\end{pmatrix}=M_{\varepsilon}\begin{pmatrix}F_{k-1}^{\varepsilon}\\
G_{k-1}^{\varepsilon}\end{pmatrix}=\ldots=M_{\varepsilon}^{k-1}\begin{pmatrix}\mathfrak{b}_{\varepsilon}\\
\mathfrak{c}_{\varepsilon}\end{pmatrix}\;\text{,}\;\;

(4.30)

from which we get

\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}M_{\varepsilon}^{k-1}\begin{pmatrix}\mathfrak{b}_{\varepsilon}\\
\mathfrak{c}_{\varepsilon}\end{pmatrix}=\left(\mathds{I}-M_{\varepsilon}\right)^{-1}\begin{pmatrix}\mathfrak{b}_{\varepsilon}\\
\mathfrak{c}_{\varepsilon}\end{pmatrix}\;\text{,}\;\;\qquad\text{with}\ \mathds{I}\coloneqq\begin{pmatrix}1&0\\
0&1\end{pmatrix}\;\text{.}

(4.31)

In conclusion, since

\left(\mathds{I}-M_{\varepsilon}\right)^{-1}=\frac{1}{1-\mathfrak{b}_{\varepsilon}+\mathfrak{c}_{\varepsilon}}\begin{pmatrix}1+2\mathfrak{c}_{\varepsilon}&1-2\mathfrak{b}_{\varepsilon}\\
2\mathfrak{c}_{\varepsilon}&2-2\mathfrak{b}_{\varepsilon}\end{pmatrix}\;\text{,}\;\;

(4.32)

the left-hand side of (

) equals, depending on whether
i=j
or

\frac{1}{1-\mathfrak{b}_{\varepsilon}+\mathfrak{c}_{\varepsilon}}\times\begin{cases}\mathfrak{b}_{\varepsilon}+\mathfrak{c}_{\varepsilon}&\text{if}\ i=j\\
2\mathfrak{c}_{\varepsilon}&\text{if}\ i\neq j\;\text{.}\end{cases}

(4.33)

Taking

, (
4.8
) yields (
4.23
) for

. When

,
i\neq j
is not possible; hence, we have

Now, recall that
\nabla^{j}u_{t}=\nabla^{j}P_{t}u_{0}=P_{t}\nabla^{j}u_{0}
, for all

, and similarly for the
\varepsilon

{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}{\mathfrak{s}_{\varepsilon}\coloneqq}}\sup_{t\geq 0}\sup_{x\in\mathbb{T}_{\varepsilon}^{d}}\left\lvert\left(\nabla^{\varepsilon,j}P_{t}^{\varepsilon}u_{0}^{\varepsilon}(x)\right)^{2}-\left(\nabla^{j}u_{t}(x)\right)^{2}\right\rvert\xrightarrow{\varepsilon\to 0}0\;\text{,}\;\;\qquad j=1,\ldots,d\;\text{.}

(4.34)

In view of (
4.22
), (
4.23
) and (
4.34
), the claim in (
4.3
) follows by a triangle inequality. Indeed, recalling the definitions (

), (

\displaystyle\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\tilde{\Pi}_{t}^{\varepsilon,i,k}(u_{0}^{\varepsilon})(x)

\displaystyle=\sum_{j=1}^{d}\Pi_{t}^{\varepsilon,j,0}(u_{0}^{\varepsilon})(x)\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}R_{t}^{\varepsilon,i,j,k}

\displaystyle\eqref{eq:FCLT}\Longrightarrow\quad

\displaystyle\approx\sum_{j=1}^{d}\left(\nabla^{j}u_{t}(x)\right)^{2}\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}R_{t}^{\varepsilon,i,j,k}

(4.36)

\displaystyle\eqref{eq:R-t-R-infty}\Longrightarrow\quad

\displaystyle\approx\sum_{j=1}^{d}\left(\nabla^{j}u_{t}(x)\right)^{2}\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}R_{\infty}^{\varepsilon,i,j,k}

(4.37)

\displaystyle\eqref{eq:sum-R-i-j-infty}\Longrightarrow\quad

\displaystyle\approx\sum_{j=1}^{d}\left(\nabla^{j}u_{t}(x)\right)^{2}{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}{\left(\mathds{1}_{i=j}+\mathfrak{r}^{i,j}\right)}}\;\text{.}

4.3
) with

Lemma 5.1
(Second moments of fields).

For all

\displaystyle\mathds{E}\big[\mathcal{Y}_{t}^{\varepsilon}(f)^{2}\big]

\displaystyle\leq\left\lVert f\right\rVert_{{\rm Lip}(\mathbb{T}_{\varepsilon}^{d})}^{2}\left\lVert u_{0}^{\varepsilon}\right\rVert_{L^{\infty}(\mathbb{T}_{\varepsilon}^{d})}^{2}

(5.4)

\displaystyle\mathds{E}\big[\mathcal{M}_{t}^{\varepsilon}(f)^{2}\big]

\displaystyle\leq\left\lVert f\right\rVert_{{\rm Lip}(\mathbb{T}_{\varepsilon}^{d})}^{2}\left\lVert u_{0}^{\varepsilon}\right\rVert_{L^{\infty}(\mathbb{T}_{\varepsilon}^{d})}^{2}\;\text{.}

(5.5)

5.4
). By expanding the square and taking expectation, we obtain

\mathds{E}\big[\mathcal{Y}_{t}^{\varepsilon}(f)^{2}\big]=\theta_{\varepsilon}^{2}\,\varepsilon^{2d}\sum_{x,y\in\mathbb{T}_{\varepsilon}^{d}}\left\{\mathds{E}\big[u_{t}^{\varepsilon}(x)u_{t}^{\varepsilon}(y)\big]-\mathds{E}\big[u_{t}^{\varepsilon}(x)\big]\mathds{E}\big[u_{t}^{\varepsilon}(y)\big]\right\}f(x)f(y)\;\text{.}

(5.6)

Writing
u_{t}^{\varepsilon}(x)
as the mild solution of (
1.17
), we get

\displaystyle u_{t}^{\varepsilon}(x)

\displaystyle=P_{t}^{\varepsilon}u_{0}^{\varepsilon}(x)+\sum_{i=1}^{d}\frac{\varepsilon^{2}}{2}\int_{0}^{t}P_{t-s}^{\varepsilon}\nabla_{*}^{\varepsilon,i}\left({\rm d}\bar{N}_{s}^{\varepsilon,i}\,\nabla^{\varepsilon,i}u_{s^{-}}^{\varepsilon}\right)(x)

(5.7)

\displaystyle=P_{t}^{\varepsilon}u_{0}^{\varepsilon}(x)+\sum_{i=1}^{d}\frac{\varepsilon^{2}}{2}\int_{0}^{t}\sum_{z\in\mathbb{T}_{\varepsilon}^{d}}{\nabla_{*}^{\varepsilon,i}p_{t-s}^{\varepsilon}({\,\cdot\,},z)(x)}\,{\nabla^{\varepsilon,i}\,u_{s^{-}}^{\varepsilon}(z)\,{\rm d}\bar{N}_{s}^{\varepsilon,i}(z)}\;\text{.}

(5.8)

Remark that the notation
\nabla_{*}^{\varepsilon,i}p_{t-s}^{\varepsilon}({\,\cdot\,},z)(x)
is to emphasize that the discrete derivative acts on the
x
-variable.

Since
\bar{N}^{\varepsilon}=(\bar{N}_{t}^{\varepsilon,i}(x))_{t\geq 0,\,x\in\mathbb{T}_{\varepsilon}^{d},\,i=1,\ldots,d}
is a family of i.i.d. martingales satisfying
\mathds{E}[(\bar{N}_{t}^{\varepsilon,i}(x))^{2}]=\varepsilon^{-2}\,t
, we obtain

\displaystyle\mathds{E}\big[u_{t}^{\varepsilon}(x)u_{t}^{\varepsilon}(y)\big]=\mathds{E}\big[u_{t}^{\varepsilon}(x)\big]\mathds{E}\big[u_{t}^{\varepsilon}(y)\big]

(5.9)

\displaystyle\qquad+\sum_{i=1}^{d}\frac{\varepsilon^{2}}{4}\int_{0}^{t}\sum_{z\in\mathbb{T}_{\varepsilon}^{d}}\mathds{E}\big[\left(\nabla^{\varepsilon,i}u_{s}^{\varepsilon}(z)\right)^{2}\big]\left(\nabla_{*}^{\varepsilon,i}p_{t-s}^{\varepsilon}({\,\cdot\,},z)(x)\,\nabla_{*}^{\varepsilon,i}p_{t-s}^{\varepsilon}({\,\cdot\,},z)(y)\right){\rm d}s\;\text{.}

(5.10)

Inserting this identity into (
5.6
) and recalling that
\theta_{\varepsilon}=\varepsilon^{-(d/2+1)}
, we get

\displaystyle\mathds{E}\big[\mathcal{Y}_{t}^{\varepsilon}(f)^{2}\big]

\displaystyle=\frac{\varepsilon^{d}}{4}\sum_{z\in\mathbb{T}_{\varepsilon}^{d}}\sum_{i=1}^{d}\int_{0}^{t}\mathds{E}\big[\left(\nabla^{\varepsilon,i}u_{s}^{\varepsilon}(z)\right)^{2}\big]\left(\sum_{x\in\mathbb{T}_{\varepsilon}^{d}}\left(\nabla_{*}^{\varepsilon,i}p_{t-s}^{\varepsilon}({\,\cdot\,},z)(x)\right)f(x)\right)^{2}{\rm d}s

(5.11)

\displaystyle=\varepsilon^{d}\sum_{z\in\mathbb{T}_{\varepsilon}^{d}}\sum_{i=1}^{d}\int_{0}^{t}\mathds{E}\big[\left(\nabla^{\varepsilon,i}u_{s}^{\varepsilon}(z)\right)^{2}\big]\left(\tfrac{1}{2}P_{t-s}^{\varepsilon}\nabla^{\varepsilon,i}f(z)\right)^{2}{\rm d}s\;\text{.}

(5.12)

By the estimate
\|P_{t-s}^{\varepsilon}\nabla^{\varepsilon,i}f\|_{L^{\infty}(\mathbb{T}_{\varepsilon}^{d})}\leq\|\nabla^{\varepsilon,i}f\|_{L^{\infty}(\mathbb{T}_{\varepsilon}^{d})}\leq\left\lVert f\right\rVert_{{\rm Lip}(\mathbb{T}_{\varepsilon}^{d})}
,

\mathds{E}\big[\mathcal{Y}_{t}^{\varepsilon}(f)^{2}\big]\leq\frac{1}{2}\left\lVert f\right\rVert_{{\rm Lip}(\mathbb{T}_{\varepsilon}^{d})}^{2}\int_{0}^{t}\frac{1}{2}\,\mathds{E}\big[\left\lVert u_{s}^{\varepsilon}\right\rVert_{H^{1}(\mathbb{T}_{\varepsilon}^{d})}^{2}\big]{\rm d}s\leq\frac{1}{2}\left\lVert f\right\rVert_{{\rm Lip}(\mathbb{T}_{\varepsilon}^{d})}^{2}\left\lVert u_{0}^{\varepsilon}\right\rVert_{L^{2}(\mathbb{T}_{\varepsilon}^{d})}^{2}\;\text{,}\;\;

(5.13)

where the second inequality used Aldous-Lanoue identity (
1.43
).
This yields (
5.4
).

For what concerns (
5.5
), since the process in (
5.2
) is a mean-zero martingale, we get

\mathds{E}\big[\mathcal{M}_{t}^{\varepsilon}(f)^{2}\big]=\mathds{E}\big[\varGamma_{t}^{\varepsilon}(\tfrac{1}{2}\nabla^{\varepsilon}f)\big]\;\text{.}

(5.14)

Noting
\varGamma_{t}^{\varepsilon}(\frac{1}{2}\nabla f)
equals (
5.12
) with
P_{t-s}^{\varepsilon}\nabla^{\varepsilon,i}f
replaced by
\nabla^{\varepsilon,i}f
(cf. (
1.29
)), the argument leading from (
5.12
) to (
5.13
) proves (
5.5
). ∎

Lemma 5.2
(Size of jumps).

For all
f\in\mathcal{C}^{\infty}(\mathbb{T}^{d})
,
we have

\mathds{E}\big[\sup_{t\geq 0}\left\lvert\mathcal{Y}_{t}^{\varepsilon}(f)-\mathcal{Y}_{t^{-}}^{\varepsilon}(f)\right\rvert\big]\leq\varepsilon^{d/2}\left\lVert f\right\rVert_{{\rm Lip}(\mathbb{T}_{\varepsilon}^{d})}\left\lVert u_{0}^{\varepsilon}\right\rVert_{L^{\infty}(\mathbb{T}_{\varepsilon}^{d})}\;\text{.}

(5.15)

By the Poisson nature of the averaging dynamics, there is
\mathds{P}
-a.s. at most one update at the time. Suppose to observe an update over the nearest neighbor vertices
x
and
y=x+\varepsilon e_{i}\in\mathbb{T}_{\varepsilon}^{d}
at time
t\geq 0
; then,

\displaystyle\left\lvert\mathcal{Y}_{t}^{\varepsilon}(f)-\mathcal{Y}_{t^{-}}^{\varepsilon}(f)\right\rvert

(5.16)

\displaystyle\qquad=\theta_{\varepsilon}\,\varepsilon^{d}\left\lvert\tfrac{1}{2}\left(u_{t^{-}}^{\varepsilon}(x)+u_{t^{-}}(y)\right)\left(f(x)+f(y)\right)-u_{t^{-}}^{\varepsilon}(x)\,f(x)-u_{t^{-}}^{\varepsilon}(y)\,f(y)\right\rvert

(5.17)

\displaystyle\qquad=\theta_{\varepsilon}\,\varepsilon^{d}\left\lvert\tfrac{1}{2}\left(u_{t^{-}}^{\varepsilon}(x)-u_{t^{-}}^{\varepsilon}(y)\right)\left(f(x)-f(y)\right)\right\rvert

(5.18)

\displaystyle\qquad\leq\theta_{\varepsilon}\,\varepsilon^{d+1}\left\lVert f\right\rVert_{{\rm Lip}(\mathbb{T}_{\varepsilon}^{d})}\left\lVert u_{t^{-}}^{\varepsilon}\right\rVert_{L^{\infty}(\mathbb{T}_{\varepsilon}^{d})}

(5.19)

\displaystyle\qquad\leq\theta_{\varepsilon}\,\varepsilon^{d+1}\left\lVert f\right\rVert_{{\rm Lip}(\mathbb{T}_{\varepsilon}^{d})}\left\lVert u_{0}^{\varepsilon}\right\rVert_{L^{\infty}(\mathbb{T}_{\varepsilon}^{d})}\;\text{,}\;\;

(5.20)

where the first inequality used
\left\lvert f(x)-f(y)\right\rvert\leq{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}{\varepsilon}}\|f\|_{{\rm Lip}(\mathbb{T}_{\varepsilon}^{d})}
, while the second one used the monotonicity
\left\lVert u_{s}^{\varepsilon}\right\rVert_{L^{\infty}(\mathbb{T}_{\varepsilon}^{d})}\leq\left\lVert u_{0}^{\varepsilon}\right\rVert_{L^{\infty}(\mathbb{T}_{\varepsilon}^{d})}
. Recalling
\theta_{\varepsilon}=\varepsilon^{-(d/2+1)}
concludes the proof. ∎

5.2
Martingale convergence theorem

In view of the martingale decomposition in (
5.1
)–(
5.2
) and the conclusions of Theorem
2
and Lemma
5.2
, the classical martingale convergence theorem (see, e.g,
[
24
, Theorem 7.1.9, p. 339]
) applies to our case. For the reader’s convenience, we collect its consequences in the following proposition.

Proposition 5.1
.

Recall (
1.30
).
Then, for all
f\in\mathcal{C}^{\infty}(\mathbb{T}^{d})
, there exists a unique (in distribution) real-valued square-integrable continuous martingale
M^{f}=(M_{t}^{f})_{t\geq 0}
(with respect to its natural filtration) with
M_{0}^{f}=0
, predictable quadratic variation equal to
\varGamma_{t}(\tfrac{1}{2}\nabla f)
, and Gaussian independent increments. Moreover, recalling (
1.25
), (
1.26
), and (
1.29
), we have, for all
f\in\mathcal{C}^{\infty}(\mathbb{T}^{d})
,

(\mathcal{M}_{t}^{\varepsilon}(f))_{t\geq 0}\xRightarrow{\varepsilon\to 0}M_{t}^{f}\;\text{,}\;\;\qquad\text{in}\ \mathcal{D}([0,\infty);\mathbb{R})\;\text{.}

(5.21)

Since
(\varGamma_{t}(\frac{1}{2}\nabla f))_{t\geq 0}
is a continuous deterministic non-negative function,
[
24
, Theorem 7.1.1, p. 338]
proves the first assertion on the existence and uniqueness of the martingale
(M_{t}^{f})_{t\geq 0}
.

As for the claim in (
5.21
), it suffices to observe that the hypotheses of
[
24
, Theorem 7.1.4, p. 339]
, as well as conditions (1.16)–(1.19) therein, hold true in our case. Indeed, the predictable quadratic variations
(\varGamma_{t}^{\varepsilon}(\frac{1}{2}\nabla^{\varepsilon}f))_{t\geq 0}
of
(\mathcal{M}_{t}^{\varepsilon}(f))_{t\geq 0}
are continuous non-negative processes. Hence, the hypotheses and conditions (1.16) and (1.18) are fulfilled. Condition (1.17) therein follows from

\left\lvert\mathcal{Y}_{t}^{\varepsilon}(f)-\mathcal{Y}_{t^{-}}^{\varepsilon}(f)\right\rvert=\left\lvert\mathcal{M}_{t}^{\varepsilon}(f)-\mathcal{M}_{t^{-}}^{\varepsilon}(f)\right\rvert\;\text{,}\;\;

(5.22)

which holds because the drift term in (
5.1
) is continuous, and Lemma
5.2
. Finally, condition (1.19) is a consequence of the triangle inequality

\displaystyle\left\lvert\varGamma_{t}^{\varepsilon}(\tfrac{1}{2}\nabla^{\varepsilon}f)-\varGamma_{t}(\tfrac{1}{2}\nabla f)\right\rvert

\displaystyle\leq\left\lvert\varGamma_{t}^{\varepsilon}(\tfrac{1}{2}\nabla^{\varepsilon}f)-\varGamma_{t}^{\varepsilon}(\tfrac{1}{2}\nabla f)\right\rvert+\left\lvert\varGamma_{t}^{\varepsilon}(\tfrac{1}{2}\nabla f)-\varGamma_{t}(\tfrac{1}{2}\nabla f)\right\rvert

(5.23)

\displaystyle\leq\|f\|_{{\rm Lip}(\mathbb{T}_{\varepsilon}^{d})}\max_{i=1,\ldots,d}\|\nabla^{\varepsilon,i}f-\nabla^{i}f\|_{L^{\infty}(\mathbb{T}_{\varepsilon}^{d})}\int_{0}^{t}\left\lVert u_{s}^{\varepsilon}\right\rVert_{H^{1}(\mathbb{T}_{\varepsilon}^{d})}^{2}\,{\rm d}s

(5.24)

\displaystyle\qquad+\left\lvert\varGamma_{t}^{\varepsilon}(\tfrac{1}{2}\nabla f)-\varGamma_{t}(\tfrac{1}{2}\nabla f)\right\rvert\;\text{,}\;\;

(5.25)

taking expectation,
Aldous-Lanoue identity (
1.43
), and Theorem
2
. This concludes the proof of the proposition. ∎

5.3
Tightness and continuity of limits

In view of the decomposition (
5.1
), tightness and continuity of the limit points for
(\mathcal{Y}_{t}^{\varepsilon})_{t\geq 0}
in
\mathcal{D}([0,\infty);H^{-\alpha}(\mathbb{T}^{d}))
is equivalent to the same property for the corresponding drift and martingale terms.
Since we already established convergence for the martingale when tested against smooth test functions (Proposition
5.1
), the tightness proof for this term may be simplified. For this purpose, let us start by recalling from
[
7
]
a useful characterization of tightness with continuous limit points for general
càdlàg
processes.

Proposition 5.2
(Tightness & continuous limits).

Let
(\Xi,\left\lVert{\,\cdot\,}\right\rVert)
be a Banach space. A sequence of
\Xi
-valued
càdlàg
processes
((Y_{t}^{\varepsilon})_{t\geq 0})_{\varepsilon}
is tight in
\mathcal{D}([0,\infty);\Xi)
and such that all limits are continuous if and only if the following three conditions hold true: for all
T>0
and
\gamma>0
,

\lim_{\zeta\to\infty}\limsup_{\varepsilon\to 0}\mathds{P}\bigg(\sup_{t\in[0,T]}\left\lVert Y_{t}^{\varepsilon}\right\rVert>\zeta\bigg)=0\;\text{,}\;\;

(5.26)

\lim_{\delta\to 0}\limsup_{\varepsilon\to 0}\mathds{P}\bigg(\sup_{\begin{subarray}{c}s,t\in[0,T]\\
\left\lvert t-s\right\rvert<\delta\end{subarray}}\left\lVert Y_{t}^{\varepsilon}-Y_{s}^{\varepsilon}\right\rVert>\gamma\bigg)=0\;\text{,}\;\;

(5.27)

and

\lim_{\varepsilon\to 0}\mathds{P}\bigg(\sup_{t\in[0,T]}\left\lVert Y_{t}^{\varepsilon}-Y_{t^{-}}^{\varepsilon}\right\rVert>\gamma\bigg)=0\;\text{.}

(5.28)

Just combine Theorems 13.2 and 13.4 in
[
7
]
with the inequalities in (12.7)–(12.9) therein.
∎

Remark 5.1
.

Thanks to the definition of the norm
\left\lVert{\,\cdot\,}\right\rVert_{H^{-\alpha}(\mathbb{T}^{d})}
in (
1.21
), Proposition
5.2
simplifies if
(\Xi,\left\lVert{\,\cdot\,}\right\rVert)=(H^{-\alpha}(\mathbb{T}^{d}),\left\lVert{\,\cdot\,}\right\rVert_{H^{-\alpha}(\mathbb{T}^{d})})
, for some
\alpha>0
. Indeed, while the first condition in Proposition
5.2
, namely (
5.26
), remains unchanged, the second condition (
5.27
) may be replaced by

\lim_{\delta\to 0}\limsup_{\varepsilon\to 0}\mathds{P}\bigg(\sup_{\begin{subarray}{c}s,t\in[0,T]\\
\left\lvert t-s\right\rvert<\delta\end{subarray}}\left\lvert Y_{t}^{\varepsilon}(\phi_{m})-Y_{s}^{\varepsilon}(\phi_{m})\right\rvert>\gamma\bigg)=0\;\text{,}\;\;\qquad m\in\mathbb{Z}^{d}\;\text{.}

(5.29)

An analogous simplification holds for the third condition (
5.28
).

We now have all we need to prove the desired claim for the sequence
((\mathcal{Y}_{t}^{\varepsilon})_{t\geq 0})_{\varepsilon}
.

Proposition 5.3
.

The sequence
((\mathcal{Y}_{t}^{\varepsilon})_{t\geq 0})_{\varepsilon}
is tight in
\mathcal{D}([0,\infty);H^{-\alpha}(\mathbb{T}^{d}))
, for all
\alpha>3+d/2
, and all limit points are continuous.

By (
5.1
), it suffices to verify the conditions in Proposition
5.2
for the drift and martingale terms separately. Recall (
3.1
), and fix
T>0
,
\gamma>0
all throughout the proof.

We start with the drift term, for which we adopt the following shorthand notation:

\mathcal{A}_{t}^{\varepsilon}(f)\coloneqq\int_{0}^{t}\mathcal{Y}_{s}^{\varepsilon}(\tfrac{1}{2}\Delta_{\varepsilon}f)\,{\rm d}s\;\text{,}\;\;\qquad t\geq 0\;\text{,}\;\;f\in\mathcal{C}^{\infty}(\mathbb{T}^{d})\;\text{.}

(5.30)

As for the first condition, namely (
5.26
), Cauchy-Schwarz inequality yields

\displaystyle\limsup_{\varepsilon\to 0}\mathds{E}\big[\sup_{t\in[0,T]}\left\lVert\mathcal{A}_{t}^{\varepsilon}\right\rVert_{H^{-\alpha}(\mathbb{T}^{d})}^{2}\big]

\displaystyle\leq T\int_{0}^{T}\sum_{m\in\mathbb{Z}^{d}}(1+|m|^{2})^{-\alpha}\limsup_{\varepsilon\to 0}\mathds{E}\big[\left\lvert\mathcal{Y}_{s}^{\varepsilon}(\tfrac{1}{2}\Delta_{\varepsilon}\phi_{m})\right\rvert^{2}\big]{\rm d}s

(5.31)

\displaystyle\leq(2\pi)^{4}\,T^{2}\left\lVert u_{0}\right\rVert_{\infty}^{2}\sum_{m\in\mathbb{Z}^{d}}(1+|m|^{2})^{-(\alpha-3)}\;\text{,}\;\;

(5.32)

where the second inequality used (
5.4
) in Lemma
5.1
and
\limsup_{\varepsilon\to 0}\left\lVert\Delta\phi_{m}\right\rVert_{{\rm Lip}(\mathbb{T}_{\varepsilon}^{d})}^{2}\leq(2\pi)^{4}(1+|m|^{2})^{3}
. Since
\alpha>3+d/2
, the right-hand side above is finite. Therefore, Markov inequality yields (
5.26
) for the drift.
For the second condition (
5.27
), a similar argument yields, for all
m\in\mathbb{Z}^{d}
,

\limsup_{\varepsilon\to 0}\mathds{E}\big[\sup_{\begin{subarray}{c}s,t\in[0,T]\\
\left\lvert t-s\right\rvert<\delta\end{subarray}}\left\lvert\mathcal{A}_{t}^{\varepsilon}(\phi_{m})-\mathcal{A}_{s}^{\varepsilon}(\phi_{m})\right\rvert\big]\leq\delta\,T\,(2\pi)^{4}(1+|m|^{2})^{3}\left\lVert u_{0}\right\rVert_{\infty}^{2}\;\text{,}\;\;

(5.33)

which vanishes as
\delta\to 0
. By Markov inequality and Remark
5.1
, this suffices to prove (
5.27
). The third condition (
5.28
) is trivially satisfied since the drift is continuous.

Turning to the martingale term
(\mathcal{M}_{t}^{\varepsilon})_{t\geq 0}
, we have

\displaystyle\mathds{E}\big[\sup_{t\in[0,T]}\left\lVert\mathcal{M}_{t}^{\varepsilon}\right\rVert_{H^{-\alpha}(\mathbb{T}^{d})}^{2}\big]

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{m\in\mathbb{Z}^{d}}(1+|m|^{2})^{-\alpha}\,\mathds{E}\big[\sup_{t\in[0,T]}\left\lvert\mathcal{M}_{t}^{\varepsilon}(\phi_{m})\right\rvert^{2}\big]

(5.34)

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{m\in\mathbb{Z}^{d}}(1+|m|^{2})^{-\alpha}\,\mathds{E}\big[|\mathcal{M}_{T}^{\varepsilon}(\phi_{m})|^{2}\big]

(5.35)

\displaystyle\leq(2\pi)^{2}\left\lVert u_{0}\right\rVert_{\infty}^{2}\sum_{m\in\mathbb{Z}^{d}}(1+|m|^{2})^{-(\alpha-1)}\;\text{,}\;\;

(5.36)

where the second step used Doob inequality, while the third one used (
5.5
) in Lemma
5.1
. The right-hand side above is finite because
\alpha>1+d/2
. This proves the first condition (
5.26
). Now observe that Proposition
5.1
, (
5.22
), and Lemma
5.2
ensure that, for all
m\in\mathbb{Z}^{d}
, the sequence
((\mathcal{M}_{t}^{\varepsilon}(\phi_{m}))_{t\geq 0})_{\varepsilon}
satisfies all three conditions in Proposition
5.2
with
(\Xi,\left\lVert{\,\cdot\,}\right\rVert)=(\mathbb{C},\left\lvert{\,\cdot\,}\right\rvert)
. Remark
5.1
concludes the proof.
∎

5.4
Final step

We now have all we need to prove Theorem
1
. Indeed, the decomposition of
(\mathcal{Y}_{t}^{\varepsilon})_{t\geq 0}
in (
5.1
), and Propositions
5.1
and
5.3
ensure that any limit process, say
(\mathcal{Y}_{t})_{t\geq 0}
, have paths in
\mathcal{C}([0,\infty);H^{-\alpha}(\mathbb{T}^{d}))
, for all
\alpha>3+d/2
, and solve the martingale problem (
1.27
) in
\mathcal{C}([0,\infty);H^{-\alpha}(\mathbb{T}^{d}))
with
\mathcal{Y}_{0}=0
. Moreover, the martingale
(\mathcal{M}_{t})_{t\geq 0}
in (
1.27
) satisfies

(\mathcal{M}_{t}(f))_{t\geq 0}=(M_{t}^{f})_{t\geq 0}\;\text{,}\;\;\qquad f\in\mathcal{C}^{\infty}(\mathbb{T}^{d})\;\text{,}\;\;

(5.37)

where
(M_{t}^{f})_{t\geq 0}
is defined in Proposition
5.1
, and the above identity is meant in distribution.

The proof of Theorem
1
ends as soon as we show that such a limit martingale problem has a unique solution. For this purpose, we introduce

\mathcal{S}(\mathbb{T}^{d})\coloneqq\bigcap_{\alpha\in\mathbb{R}}H^{\alpha}(\mathbb{T}^{d})
and
\mathcal{S}(\mathbb{T}^{d})^{\prime}\coloneqq\bigcup_{\alpha\in\mathbb{R}}H^{\alpha}(\mathbb{T}^{d})
.
Since the embedding
H^{\alpha}(\mathbb{T}^{d})\hookrightarrow H^{\beta}(\mathbb{T}^{d})
is Hilbert-Schmidt for all
\alpha>\beta+d/2
,
(\mathcal{S}(\mathbb{T}^{d}),L^{2}(\mathbb{T}^{d}),\mathcal{S}(\mathbb{T}^{d})^{\prime})
defines a countably Hilbert nuclear triple. Hence, it suffices to establish uniqueness of solutions in the larger space
\mathcal{C}([0,\infty);\mathcal{S}(\mathbb{T}^{d})^{\prime})
. By the Gaussian nature of the problem, the latter is precisely covered by Holley-Stroock theory
[
31
]
(see also
[
35
, §11.4]
or
[
33
, Section C.5]
).

Proofs of Lemmas
2.2
,
4.1
and
4.2

In this appendix, we prove all results contained in Lemmas
2.2
,
4.1
and
4.2
concerned with the quantity first introduced in (
2.12
), which we recall here for the reader’s convenience: for all
t\geq 0
,
x,y\in\mathbb{T}_{\varepsilon}^{d}
and
i,j=1,\ldots,d
,

q_{t}^{\varepsilon,i,j}(x,y)\coloneqq\frac{\varepsilon^{-2}}{4}\big(p_{t}^{\varepsilon}(x-\varepsilon e_{j},y)+p_{t}^{\varepsilon}(x+\varepsilon e_{i},y)-p_{t}^{\varepsilon}(x+\varepsilon e_{i}-\varepsilon e_{j},y)-p_{t}^{\varepsilon}(x,y)\big)^{2}\;\text{.}

(.38)

All throughout this section, we will exploit the invariance and product structure of the random walk
(X_{t}^{\varepsilon})_{t\geq 0}
on
\mathbb{T}_{\varepsilon}^{d}
: for all
t\geq 0
and
x,y\in\mathbb{T}_{\varepsilon}^{d}
with
x=(x^{i})_{i=1,\ldots,d}
,

p_{t}^{\varepsilon}(x,y)=p_{t}^{\varepsilon}(y,x)=p_{t}^{\varepsilon}(0,x-y)\;\text{,}\;\;\qquad p_{t}^{\varepsilon}(0,x)=\prod_{i=1}^{d}\pi_{t}^{\varepsilon}(x^{i})\;\text{,}\;\;

(.39)

where
\pi_{t}^{\varepsilon}
denotes the distribution of the continuous-time random walk on
\mathbb{T}_{\varepsilon}
(i.e., one-dimensional), started from the origin and with nearest-neighbor jump rates equal to
\frac{1}{2}\varepsilon^{-2}
.

As already mentioned at the beginning of Section
4.1
, Lemma
4.1
essentially generalizes Lemma
2.2
. For this reason, we find more convenient to prove these two lemmas together.

We start by expressing
q^{\varepsilon}
in (
.38
) in terms of
\pi^{\varepsilon}
, introduced in (
.39
): for all
t\geq 0
,
x=(x^{i})_{i=1,\ldots,d}\in\mathbb{T}_{\varepsilon}^{d}
, and
i,j=1,\ldots,d
with
i\neq j
,

\displaystyle q_{t}^{\varepsilon,i,j}(0,x)

\displaystyle=\Bigg(\prod_{\begin{subarray}{c}\ell=1\\
\ell\neq i,j\end{subarray}}^{d}\pi_{t}^{\varepsilon}(x^{\ell})\Bigg)^{2}\frac{\varepsilon^{-2}}{4}\left(\pi_{t}^{\varepsilon}(x^{i}+\varepsilon)-\pi_{t}^{\varepsilon}(x^{i})\right)^{2}\left(\pi_{t}^{\varepsilon}(x^{j}-\varepsilon)-\pi_{t}^{\varepsilon}(x^{j})\right)^{2}

(.40)

\displaystyle=\Bigg(\prod_{\begin{subarray}{c}\ell=1\\
\ell\neq i,j\end{subarray}}^{d}\pi_{t}^{\varepsilon}(x^{\ell})\Bigg)^{2}4\varepsilon^{2}\left(\tfrac{1}{2}\nabla^{\varepsilon}\pi_{t}^{\varepsilon}(x^{i})\right)^{2}\left(\tfrac{1}{2}\nabla_{*}^{\varepsilon}\pi_{t}^{\varepsilon}(x^{j})\right)^{2}\;\text{,}\;\;

(.41)

while, for
i=j=1,\ldots,d
,

\displaystyle q_{t}^{\varepsilon,i,i}(0,x)

\displaystyle=\Bigg(\prod_{\begin{subarray}{c}\ell=1\\
\ell\neq i\end{subarray}}^{d}\pi_{t}^{\varepsilon}(x^{\ell})\Bigg)^{2}\frac{\varepsilon^{-2}}{4}\left(\pi_{t}^{\varepsilon}(x^{i}+\varepsilon)+\pi_{t}^{\varepsilon}(x^{i}-\varepsilon)-2\pi_{t}^{\varepsilon}(x^{i})\right)^{2}

(.42)

\displaystyle=\Bigg(\prod_{\begin{subarray}{c}\ell=1\\
\ell\neq i\end{subarray}}^{d}\pi_{t}^{\varepsilon}(x^{\ell})\Bigg)^{2}\varepsilon^{2}\left(\tfrac{1}{2}\Delta_{\varepsilon}\pi_{t}^{\varepsilon}(x^{i})\right)^{2}\;\text{,}\;\;

(.43)

where all
\varepsilon
-gradients and corresponding laplacians here are one-dimensional. Define, for all
t\geq 0
,

\displaystyle\begin{aligned} \mathcal{R}_{\varepsilon}(t)&\coloneqq\varepsilon^{-1}\left\lVert\pi_{t}^{\varepsilon}\right\rVert^{2}_{L^{2}(\mathbb{T}_{\varepsilon})}=\pi_{2t}^{\varepsilon}(0)\;\text{,}\;\;\\
\mathcal{S}_{\varepsilon}(t)&\coloneqq 2\left\lVert\tfrac{1}{2}\nabla^{\varepsilon}\pi_{t}^{\varepsilon}\right\rVert_{L^{2}(\mathbb{T}_{\varepsilon})}^{2}=2\left\lVert\tfrac{1}{2}\nabla^{\varepsilon}_{*}\pi_{t}^{\varepsilon}\right\rVert_{L^{2}(\mathbb{T}_{\varepsilon})}^{2}\;\text{,}\;\;\\
\mathcal{T}_{\varepsilon}(t)&\coloneqq\varepsilon\left\lVert\tfrac{1}{2}\Delta_{\varepsilon}\pi_{t}^{\varepsilon}\right\rVert_{L^{2}(\mathbb{T}_{\varepsilon})}^{2}\;\text{.}\end{aligned}

(.44)

Since
\Delta_{\varepsilon}=\nabla^{\varepsilon}\nabla_{*}^{\varepsilon}=\nabla_{*}^{\varepsilon}\nabla^{\varepsilon}
and
\frac{{\rm d}}{{\rm d}t}\pi_{t}^{\varepsilon}=\frac{1}{2}\Delta_{\varepsilon}\pi_{t}^{\varepsilon}
, we have

\mathcal{S}_{\varepsilon}(t)=-\frac{\varepsilon}{2}\,\mathcal{R}_{\varepsilon}^{\prime}(t)\;\text{,}\;\;\qquad\mathcal{T}_{\varepsilon}(t)=-\frac{\varepsilon}{2}\,\mathcal{S}_{\varepsilon}^{\prime}(t)\;\text{.}

(.45)

Now, summing over
x\in\mathbb{T}_{\varepsilon}^{d}
, we obtain

\mathcal{Q}_{\varepsilon}^{i,j}(t)\coloneqq\sum_{x\in\mathbb{T}_{\varepsilon}^{d}}q_{t}^{\varepsilon,i,j}(0,x)=\begin{cases}\mathcal{R}_{\varepsilon}(t)^{d-2}\,\mathcal{S}_{\varepsilon}(t)^{2}&\text{if}\ i\neq j\ (d\geq 2)\\
\mathcal{R}_{\varepsilon}(t)^{d-1}\,\mathcal{T}_{\varepsilon}(t)&\text{if}\ i=j\ (d\geq 1)\;\text{.}\end{cases}

(.46)

The form of the right-hand side above proves the first claim in Lemma
4.1
.

Further summing over
j=1,\ldots,d
, we get

\displaystyle\begin{aligned} \mathcal{Q}_{\varepsilon}(t)\coloneqq\sum_{j=1}^{d}\mathcal{Q}_{\varepsilon}^{i,j}(t)&={\mathcal{R}_{\varepsilon}(t)}^{d-1}\,\mathcal{T}_{\varepsilon}(t)+\left(d-1\right){\mathcal{R}_{\varepsilon}(t)}^{d-2}\,\mathcal{S}_{\varepsilon}(t)^{2}\\
&=-\frac{\varepsilon}{2}\big(\mathcal{R}_{\varepsilon}(t)^{d-1}\mathcal{S}_{\varepsilon}(t)\big)^{\prime}\;\text{,}\;\;\end{aligned}

(.47)

where the second step follows from (
.45
). Hence, since
\mathcal{R}_{\varepsilon}(0)=1
,
\mathcal{S}_{\varepsilon}(0)=\varepsilon^{-1}
, and
\mathcal{S}_{\varepsilon}(t)\to 0
as
t\to\infty
, we get

\int_{0}^{\infty}\mathcal{Q}_{\varepsilon}(t)\,{\rm d}t=-\frac{\varepsilon}{2}\int_{0}^{\infty}\big(\mathcal{R}_{\varepsilon}(t)^{d-1}\mathcal{S}_{\varepsilon}(t)\big)^{\prime}\,{\rm d}t=\frac{\varepsilon}{2}\big(\mathcal{R}_{\varepsilon}(0)^{d-1}\mathcal{S}_{\varepsilon}(0)\big)=\frac{1}{2}\;\text{.}

(.48)

This is precisely the main claim in Lemma
2.2
(see also its reformulation in Remark
4.1
). This settles the analysis when
d=1
, since
\mathcal{Q}_{\varepsilon}^{i,j}=\mathds{1}_{i=j=1}\,\mathcal{Q}_{\varepsilon}
only in one dimension.

In order to prove the final claim in Lemma
4.1
on

\mathfrak{b}_{\varepsilon}=\int_{0}^{\infty}\mathcal{R}_{\varepsilon}(t)^{d-1}\,\mathcal{T}_{\varepsilon}(t)\,{\rm d}t\;\text{,}\;\;\qquad\mathfrak{c}_{\varepsilon}=\int_{0}^{\infty}\mathcal{R}_{\varepsilon}(t)^{d-2}\,\mathcal{S}_{\varepsilon}(t)^{2}\,{\rm d}t\;\text{,}\;\;

(.49)

and the existence of their limits (see (
4.7
)–(
4.8
)), fix
d\geq 2
and first observe that, since
\mathcal{R}_{\varepsilon}(t)
,
\mathcal{S}_{\varepsilon}(t)
and
\mathcal{Q}_{\varepsilon}(t)
are all strictly positive for all
t\geq 0
, (
.46
) and (
.48
) ensure that
\mathfrak{b}_{\varepsilon},\mathfrak{c}_{\varepsilon}\in(0,\frac{1}{2})
. Furthermore, by the first identity in (
4.10
), it suffices to show that either
\mathfrak{b}_{\varepsilon}
or
\mathfrak{c}_{\varepsilon}
converges to a value in
(0,\frac{1}{2})
. Let us focus on
\mathfrak{c}_{\varepsilon}
.

Recall (
.44
). From Laplace inversion formulas (see, e.g.,
[
13
, Eq. (2.3)]
), we know

\mathcal{R}_{\varepsilon}(t)=\varepsilon\sum_{j=0}^{\varepsilon^{-1}-1}\exp\left\{-2\,\psi(\varepsilon j)\,\varepsilon^{-2}\,t\right\}\;\text{,}\;\;\quad\text{with}\ z\in\mathbb{T}\mapsto\psi(z)\coloneqq 1-\cos(2\pi z)\;\text{.}

(.50)

This and the first identity in (
.45
) yield

\displaystyle\mathfrak{c}_{\varepsilon}

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{\infty}\varepsilon^{d-2}\sum_{j_{1},\ldots,j_{d}=0}^{\varepsilon^{-1}-1}\psi(\varepsilon j_{1})\,\psi(\varepsilon j_{2})\exp\left\{-2\left(\psi(\varepsilon j_{1})+\ldots+\psi(\varepsilon j_{d})\right)\varepsilon^{-2}\,t\right\}{\rm d}t

(.51)

\displaystyle=\frac{\varepsilon^{d}}{2}\sum_{j_{1},\ldots,j_{d}{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}{=0}}}^{\varepsilon^{-1}-1}\frac{\psi(\varepsilon j_{1})\,\psi(\varepsilon j_{2})}{\psi(\varepsilon j_{1})+\ldots+\psi(\varepsilon j_{d})}\;\text{,}\;\;

(.52)

which converges, as
\varepsilon\to 0
, to

\mathfrak{c}\coloneqq\frac{1}{2}\int_{\mathbb{T}^{d}}\frac{\psi(x^{1})\,\psi(x^{2})}{\psi(x^{1})+\ldots+\psi(x^{d})}\,{\rm d}x\;\text{,}\;\;\quad\text{with}\ x=(x^{i})_{i=1,\ldots,d}\in\mathbb{T}^{d}\;\text{.}

(.53)

Since the above integral is strictly positive, this completes the proof of Lemma
4.1
.∎

Remark .2

(Properties of
\mathfrak{b}
and
\mathfrak{c}
)
.

The above proof also reveals the behaviors of the coefficients
\mathfrak{b}
and
\mathfrak{c}
as functions of the dimension
d\geq 1
.

1.

Monotonicity
. Since
\mathcal{R}_{\varepsilon}(t)\in(0,1)
and
\mathcal{S}_{\varepsilon}(t),\mathcal{T}_{\varepsilon}(t)>0
for all
t>0
, (
.46
) and (
.49
) state that both
\mathfrak{b}_{\varepsilon}
and
\mathfrak{c}_{\varepsilon}
— and, thus, their limits
\mathfrak{b}
and
\mathfrak{c}
, too — are strictly decreasing with
d\geq 1
.

2.

Bounds
. By (
4.10
) and
\mathfrak{b}\geq 0
, we have the upper bound
\mathfrak{c}\leq\frac{1}{2\left(d-1\right)}
. Since
\psi(z)\in[0,2]
and
\int_{\mathbb{T}}\psi(z)\,{\rm d}z=1
, the integral in (
.53
) yields the lower bound
\mathfrak{c}\geq\frac{1}{4d}
.

3.

2D
. When
d=2
, the value of the integral in (
.53
) is explicit:

\mathfrak{c}=\frac{1}{2}\int_{\mathbb{T}}\int_{\mathbb{T}}\frac{\psi(x^{1})\,\psi(x^{2})}{\psi(x^{1})+\psi(x^{2})}\,{\rm d}x^{1}{\rm d}x^{2}=\frac{1}{2}-\frac{1}{\pi}\;\text{,}\;\;\qquad\mathfrak{b}=\frac{1}{\pi}\;\text{,}\;\;

(.54)

where for the second identity we used (
4.10
).

Remark .3

(Formula for
\mathfrak{a}
)
.

By combining (
4.39
) and (
.53
), we get, for all
d\geq 2
,

\mathfrak{a}=\mathfrak{a}(d)=\frac{1-(d-2)\int_{\mathbb{T}^{d}}\frac{\psi(x^{1})\,\psi(x^{2})}{\psi(x^{1})+\ldots+\psi(x^{d})}\,{\rm d}x}{1+d\int_{\mathbb{T}^{d}}\frac{\psi(x^{1})\,\psi(x^{2})}{\psi(x^{1})+\ldots+\psi(x^{d})}\,{\rm d}x}\;\text{,}\;\;\quad\text{with}\ x=(x^{i})_{i=1,\ldots,d}\in\mathbb{T}^{d}\;\text{.}

(.55)

We conclude the appendix with the short proof of Lemma
4.2
.

By (
.47
) and arguing as for (
.48
), we have

\int_{T}^{\infty}\mathcal{Q}_{\varepsilon}(t)\,{\rm d}t=\frac{\varepsilon}{2}\,\big(\mathcal{R}_{\varepsilon}(T)^{d-1}\,\mathcal{S}_{\varepsilon}(T)\big)\leq\frac{\varepsilon}{2}\,\mathcal{S}_{\varepsilon}(T)\;\text{,}\;\;\qquad T>0\;\text{,}\;\;

(.56)

where for the inequality we used
\mathcal{R}_{\varepsilon}\leq 1
. The desired estimate in (
4.11
) is a consequence of the fact that
\mathcal{S}_{\varepsilon}(T)
is, up to normalization, the Dirichlet form of the diffusively-rescaled continuous-time random walk on
\mathbb{T}_{\varepsilon}^{n}
with
n=1
, which is well-known to have the power law behavior
T^{-(n+1/2)}=T^{-3/2}
for
T\in(\varepsilon^{2},1)
. For a simple proof of this fact, see, e.g.,
[
5
, Eq. (5.9)]
. ∎
