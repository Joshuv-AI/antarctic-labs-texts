Deletion-Contraction and the Surface Tutte Polynomial
Iain Moffatt, Maya Thompson

Abstract
In this paper we unify two families of topological Tutte polynomials. The first family is that coming from the surface Tutte polynomial, a polynomial that arises in the theory of local flows and tensions. The second family arises from the canonical Tutte polynomials of Hopf algebras. Each family includes the Las Vergnas, Bollobás–Riordan, and Krushkal polynomials. As a consequence we determine a deletion–contraction definition of the surface Tutte polynomial and recursion relations for the number of local flows and tensions in an embedded graph.

Introduction

By way of motivation, we begin with a brief discussion of the classical Tutte polynomial

T(G;x,y):=\sum_{A\subseteq E}(x-1)^{r(E)-r(A)}(y-1)^{|A|-r(A)}

(1)

of a graph
G=(V,E)
, where
r(A)
is the rank of the spanning subgraph on
A
. (See, for example,
[
4
,
7
,
8
,
11
,
24
]
for background on the Tutte polynomial.)
Tutte introduced his polynomial, initially named the dichromate, as a bivariate generalisation of both the flow and chromatic polynomials
[
22
,
23
]
.
Indeed the number of nowhere-zero
\mathbb{Z}_{k}
-flows and the number of proper
k
-colourings of
G
can be recovered from the Tutte polynomial through the evaluations
T(G;0,1-k)
and
T(G;1-k,0)
, respectively.
(See, for example,
[
11
, Section 3.4]
for definitions and details.)
This can be seen to be a consequence of the
Universality Property
of the Tutte polynomial, which gives that any graph parameter
U
that satisfies the deletion-contraction relations

U(G)=\begin{cases}x\,U(G\backslash e)&\text{if $e$ is a bridge,}\\
y\,U(G/e)&\text{if $e$ is a loop,}\\
a\,U(G\backslash e)+b\,U(G\backslash e)&\text{if $e$ is an ordinary edge,}\\
\alpha^{|V|}&\text{if $G$ is edgeless};\end{cases}

(2)

for some parameters
\alpha,a,b,x,y
, is necessarily an evaluation of the Tutte polynomial (see, for example,
[
4
,
11
]
for full details). Taking
\alpha=a=b=1
and
x
and
y
to be variables in (
2
) gives the
deletion-contraction relations
for the Tutte polynomial, and
T(G;x,y)
is uniquely defined through these relations. The importance of the Universality Property is that it shows the Tutte polynomial encapsulates all graph deletion-contraction invariants, and goes towards explaining the importance of the polynomial in mathematics, why it appears in areas such as knot theory and statistical physics, and why it captures such a wide and diverse range of graph parameters. Of particular relevance here is that deletion-contraction, flows and colourings are inextricably linked.

Notice that Equation (
2
) provides a recursive definition for the graph polynomial where the initial conditions are given by its values on edgeless graphs. In general, following common usage in the area, we say that a (topological) graph polynomial has a
full
deletion-contraction definition (or
full
recursive definition) if it is uniquely defined by a set of deletion-contraction relations together with its values on edgeless graphs. Thus edgeless graphs provide the initial conditions of the deletion-contraction recurrence relations for the polynomial.

In this paper we are interested in analogues of the Tutte polynomial for graphs embedded in
orientable
surfaces. (In subsequent sections we shall realise embedded graphs as ribbon graphs.) Considerable attention has been paid to such
topological Tutte polynomials
over recent years. Much of this recent interest grew from Bollobás and Riordan’s papers
[
5
,
6
]
, although the Las Vergnas polynomial predates it
[
19
]
.
A challenge when studying topological Tutte polynomials is that, unlike the classical case for graphs, there are many different analogues for the Tutte polynomial
[
5
,
6
,
13
,
14
,
16
,
17
,
18
,
19
,
20
]
.
Here we tie together two strands in the development of topological Tutte polynomials, and as a consequence find a graph polynomial that has a full deletion-contraction definition and that subsumes all these topological Tutte polynomials.

Initially, topological Tutte polynomials were defined through state-sum formulas analogous to (
1
). This is the case for the various polynomials introduced in
[
5
,
6
,
13
,
14
,
18
,
19
]
. A difficulty with these polynomials is that, unlike the classical Tutte polynomial, their deletion-contraction relations do not apply to all edges of graphs, and therefore do not provide a full recursive definition of the polynomial.

A systematic approach to constructing graph polynomials which have full recursive definitions was taken in
[
16
,
17
]
. There the idea was to start with some concept of deletion and contraction and, via Hopf algebras, use them to generate a graph polynomial.
The resulting polynomials, called
canonical Tutte polynomials
, have a Universality Property akin to (
2
) and deletion-contraction recurrence relations with edgeless graphs providing the initial conditions.
It was shown in
[
16
,
17
]
that there is a
canonical Tutte polynomial of graphs in pseudo-surfaces
that has a full recursive deletion-contraction definition, has a Universality Property, and contains all of Las Vergnas’, Bollobaś and Riordan’s and Krushkal’s topological Tutte polynomials as specialisations. (See
[
12
]
for a non-full “Recipe Theorem” for the Bollobaś–Riordan polynomial.)

After that work, a different approach to constructing topological Tutte polynomials was taken by Goodall et al. in
[
13
,
14
]
. Their approach to topological Tutte polynomials was motivated by Tutte’s original construction of
T(G;x,y)
as a bivariate generalisation of both the flow and chromatic polynomials.
Flows and tensions of graphs (the latter corresponding to vertex colourings)
can be extended to embedded graphs in the form of
local flows
and
local
tensions
taking values in a not necessarily Abelian group (the cyclic order of
edges around a vertex or face given by the embedding ensuring they are well
defined). Enumerating them gives rise to analogues for embedded graphs of
the chromatic and flow polynomial for graphs. The authors of
[
13
]
were able to combine these two polynomials to construct a multivariate polynomial of graphs in surfaces, called the
surface Tutte polynomial
. Just as the Tutte polynomial of a graph yields by evaluation the number of nowhere-zero flows and tensions, so the surface Tutte polynomial of an embedded graph yields by evaluation the number of nowhere-identity local flows and tensions.
However, the surface Tutte polynomial was defined through a state-sum analogous to (
1
) and it does not have a full deletion-contraction definition or Universality Property akin to Equation (
2
).

In this paper we unify the approaches of Krajewski et al.
[
16
,
17
]
and Goodall et al.
[
13
]
. We show that
all
of the above polynomials belong to a single family and can be recovered from a single graph polynomial.
We do this by extending the domain of the surface Tutte polynomial to objects we call “packaged ribbon graphs” (introduced in Section
2
).
Then in Section
3
we introduce a Tutte polynomial for packaged ribbon graphs (Definition
3.1
) and show that it has a full recursive deletion-contraction definition with initial values given by edgeless ribbon graphs (Theorem
3.3
), and that it has a Universality Property analogous to that for the classical Tutte polynomial (Theorem
3.6
).
We show that our graph polynomial specialises to both the surface Tutte polynomial of
[
13
]

and
the canonical Tutte polynomials
[
16
,
17
]
of graphs in surfaces (Theorem
4.3
). It follows that our polynomial includes
all
of the topological Tutte polynomials discussed above (see Figure
4
). Finally, in Section
5
we apply our results to give a recursive way to count the numbers of local flows and local tensions in a graph in a surface.

2
Packaged ribbon graphs

As noted above, our approach is to unify the two families of topological Tutte polynomials by extending their domain to a class of objects we call
packaged ribbon graphs
. In this section we introduce packaged ribbon graphs and operations of deletion, contraction and duality on them.
Packaged ribbon graphs consist of ribbon graphs equipped with a partition on their sets of vertices and sets of boundary components. Furthermore, each block of a partition has a weight. It is well-known that ribbon graphs describe graphs cellularly embedded in surfaces (see e.g.,
[
9
,
15
]
). Similarly, packaged ribbon graphs describe graphs (not necessarily cellularly) embedded in pseudo-surfaces.

2.1
A review of ribbon graphs

We begin with a brief overview of standard ribbon graph terminology and notation. Further details on ribbon graphs can be found in
[
9
]
, and a reader familiar with them may safely skip this subsection. Note that in this paper we assume all our ribbon graphs are orientable (in general, they can be non-orientable).

Definition 2.1
.

A
ribbon graph

\mathbb{G}=(V,E)
is an orientable surface with boundary represented by the union of a set of discs
V
, called the vertices, and another set of discs
E
, called the edges, satisfying the following constraints:

1.

A vertex and edge are either disjoint or meet in an arc.

2.

Each such arc lies on the boundary of just one vertex and one edge.

3.

Every edge contains two such arcs.

In the definition of a ribbon graph, note that the orientable surface with boundary need not be connected.

Given a ribbon graph
\mathbb{G}
, we use
v(\mathbb{G})
,
e(\mathbb{G})
,
f(\mathbb{G})
and
k(\mathbb{G})
to denote the number of vertices, edges, boundary components and connected components respectively.
A ribbon graph
\mathbb{G}
has
genus

g(\mathbb{G}):=\frac{1}{2}(2k(\mathbb{G})-f(\mathbb{G})+e(\mathbb{G})-v(\mathbb{G}))
;
rank

r(\mathbb{G}):=v(\mathbb{G})-k(\mathbb{G})
; and
nullity

n(\mathbb{G}):=e(\mathbb{G})-r(\mathbb{G})
.
Where it makes sense, we use this terminology and notation for graphs too.

Let
\mathbb{G}=(V,E)
be a ribbon graph and
e\in E
be an edge. The ribbon graph obtained by
deleting

e
is denoted by
\mathbb{G}\backslash e:=(V,E\setminus e)
(we use the convention of denoting singleton sets by the element they contain).
Similarly, the ribbon graph obtained by deleting an isolated vertex
v
(i.e. a vertex with no incident edges) is denoted by
\mathbb{G}\backslash v:=(V\setminus v,E)
. When deleting edges one after another the order in which we do so does not matter, and likewise when deleting vertices. This allows us to extend the operation of deletion to subsets of edges and subsets of vertices.
We say that a ribbon graph
\mathbb{H}
is a
ribbon subgraph
of a ribbon graph
\mathbb{G}
if it can be obtained from
\mathbb{G}
by deleting a subset of edges (possibly empty) and then deleting a subset of isolated vertices (possibly empty).
Let
\mathbb{H}=(V^{\prime},A)
be a ribbon subgraph of
\mathbb{G}=(V,E)
. We say
\mathbb{H}
is a
spanning ribbon subgraph
of
\mathbb{G}
if
V^{\prime}=V
and in this case we denote
\mathbb{H}
by
\mathbb{G}|A
. Note that
\mathbb{G}|A=\mathbb{G}\backslash A^{c}
where
A^{c}=E\setminus A
.

Definition 2.2
.

Let
\mathbb{G}=(V,E)
be a ribbon graph. Let
e\in E
and
u
and
v
be its incident vertices, which are not necessarily distinct. Then
\mathbb{G}/e
denotes the ribbon graph obtained as follows. Consider the boundary component(s) of
e\cup u\cup v
as curves on
\mathbb{G}
. For each resulting curve, attach a disc, which will form a vertex of
\mathbb{G}/e
, by identifying its boundary component with the curve. Delete
e
,
u
and
v
from the resulting complex.
We say that
\mathbb{G}/e
is obtained from
\mathbb{G}
by
contracting

e
.

For a depiction of ribbon graph contraction see Table
2
and disregard the colouring and weighting, and similarly for ribbon graph deletion and Table
1
.

The
(geometric) dual

\mathbb{G}^{*}
of a ribbon graph
\mathbb{G}=(V,E)
can be constructed in the following way.
Recall that, topologically, a ribbon graph is a surface with boundary; we cap off the holes using a set of discs, denoted by
V(\mathbb{G}^{*})
, to obtain a surface without boundary. The geometric dual of
\mathbb{G}
is the ribbon graph
\mathbb{G}^{*}=(V(\mathbb{G}^{*}),E)
. Observe that the edges of
\mathbb{G}
and
\mathbb{G}^{*}
are identical. The only change is which arcs on their boundaries meet vertices.

2.2
Packaged ribbon graphs and operations on them

We shall now introduce packaged ribbon graphs, which are our main object of study. Recall that, we assume every ribbon graph is orientable including those within packaged ribbon graphs.

Definition 2.3
.

A

is a tuple
\underline{\mathbb{G}}=(\mathbb{G},\mathcal{V},\omega_{\mathcal{V}},\mathcal{F},\omega_{\mathcal{F}})
where:

1.

\mathbb{G}=(V,E)

2.

\mathcal{F}

A packaged ribbon graph consists of a “coloured ribbon graph” as defined by Huggett and Moffatt

The

\omega_{\mathcal{F}^{*}}

At times we will abuse notation and simply denote the dual of
\underline{\mathbb{G}}=(\mathbb{G},\mathcal{V},\omega_{\mathcal{V}},\mathcal{F},\omega_{\mathcal{F}})
by
\underline{\mathbb{G}}^{*}=(\mathbb{G}^{*},\mathcal{F},\omega_{\mathcal{F}},\mathcal{V},\omega_{\mathcal{V}})

The following proposition extends the classical and well-known identity
G^{*}/e=(G\backslash e)^{*}
for a plane graph
G
and its dual
G^{*}
.

Proposition 2.7
.

Let
\underline{\mathbb{G}}=(\mathbb{G},\mathcal{V},\omega_{\mathcal{V}},\mathcal{F},\omega_{\mathcal{F}})
be a packaged ribbon graph and
e\in E
be an edge. Then

\displaystyle(\underline{\mathbb{G}}\backslash e)^{*}=\underline{\mathbb{G}}^{*}/e\hskip 24.0pt\text{and}\hskip 24.0pt(\underline{\mathbb{G}}/e)^{*}=\underline{\mathbb{G}}^{*}\backslash e.

Proof.

As duality is an involution, it suffices to only prove one of the theorem statements. We will show that
(\underline{\mathbb{G}}\backslash e)^{*}=\underline{\mathbb{G}}^{*}/e
. Denote the dual by
\underline{\mathbb{G}}^{*}=(\mathbb{G}^{*},\mathcal{F},\omega_{\mathcal{F}},\mathcal{V},\omega_{\mathcal{V}})
and let
F
be its set of boundary components. For the ribbon graph
\mathbb{G}
, it is well established (e.g., see
[
9
]
) that
(\mathbb{G}\backslash e)^{*}=\mathbb{G}^{*}/e
. We just need to check that the partitions and their weightings correctly align.

Observe that the three edge types considered in the definition of deletion within the primal correspond to the three edge types considered in the definition of contraction in the dual. That is to say, for example, that if an edge intersects one boundary component twice in the primal, then the corresponding edge in the dual will be a loop. Additionally, notice that the partitions and weightings are changed in the same manner for the same corresponding edge case of deletion and contraction. The only difference is that one affects the partition on the boundary components and the other affects the partition on the vertices. Hence, taking the dual after deleting an edge gives the same packaged ribbon graph as contracting the corresponding edge in the dual.

2.3
A topological interpretation of packaged ribbon graphs

Packaged ribbon graphs can be interpreted as graphs embedded in pseudo-surfaces that are equipped with vertex and face weights. Extending the work in
[
16
]
, in this subsection we define these embedded graphs and describe how to move between the two types of object.

We define a
pseudo-surface
to be a topological space that results from a closed surface by contracting finitely many closed paths to points. A
pinch point
is any point in a pseudo-surface that does not have a neighbourhood homeomorphic to a disc. If a pseudo-surface contains no pinch points then it is also a surface.
(Although not as commonly studied as graphs in surfaces, graphs in pseudo-surfaces are frequently studied in topological graph theory. See, for example,
[
1
,
2
,
10
,
15
,
16
]
for a taste of the area.)

We say a graph
G=(V,E)
is
embedded in a pseudo-surface
\Sigma
if
V
is a set of points in the pseudo-surface, any pinch points are contained in
V
, and
E
is a set of simple paths between the points such that two edges can only intersect at their incident vertices. Let
F
denote the set of connected regions in
\Sigma\setminus G
. Define a
vertex weighting
(resp.
region weighting
) to be a mapping
\phi:V\to\mathbb{N}_{0}
(resp.
\rho:F\to\mathbb{N}_{0}
).

Given a graph
G
embedded in a pseudo-surface
\Sigma
with vertex and region weights,
\phi
and
\rho
, obtain its corresponding packaged ribbon graph
\underline{\mathbb{G}}=(\mathbb{G},\mathcal{V},\omega_{\mathcal{V}},\mathcal{F},\omega_{\mathcal{F}})
in the following way.

1.

Form a graph embedded in a surface by deleting a small neighbourhood of each pinch point, then contracting each boundary component to form a vertex. Next form a ribbon graph
\mathbb{G}
in the usual way by taking a neighbourhood of the resulting embedded graph.

2.

For the partition
\mathcal{V}
, place two vertices in the same block if and only if they arise from the same vertex of
G
under this construction. Denote the ribbon graph’s set of boundary components by
F
. Define the partition
\mathcal{F}
on
F
such that two boundary components share a partition block if and only if they are in the same connected component of
\Sigma\setminus G
.

3.

There is one-to-one correspondence between the vertices in
G
and the blocks of
\mathcal{V}
, as well as between the connected regions of
\Sigma\setminus G
and the blocks of
\mathcal{F}
. So the weightings
\phi
and
\rho
naturally induce weightings
\omega_{\mathcal{V}}:\mathcal{V}\to\mathbb{N}_{0}
and
\omega_{\mathcal{F}}:\mathcal{F}\to\mathbb{N}_{0}
.

On the other hand, given a packaged ribbon graph
\underline{\mathbb{G}}=(\mathbb{G},\mathcal{V},\omega_{\mathcal{V}},\mathcal{F},\omega_{\mathcal{F}})
we can obtain an associated vertex and region weighted graph
G
embedded in a pseudo-surface
\hat{\Sigma}
in the following way.

1.

Cap off each boundary component of
\mathbb{G}
with a disc by identifying the boundary of the disc to the boundary component. Contract the vertex discs to points, and the edge discs to paths between these points. This results in the graph cellularly embedded in a surface that corresponds to
\mathbb{G}
in the usual way.

2.

Next, for vertices that share a block in
\mathcal{V}
, identify them together into a pinch point. (If a block has size one, the vertex contained in it is unchanged). This gives us a graph
G
embedded in a pseudo-surface
\Sigma
. For boundary components that share a block in
\mathcal{F}
, add a handle between the corresponding connected regions to connect them creating a new surface
\hat{\Sigma}
.

3.

Let
V
denote the set of vertices in
G
and
F
denote the set of connected regions in
\hat{\Sigma}\setminus G
. There is a one-to-one correspondence between the vertices in
G
and the blocks of
\mathcal{V}
, as well as between the connected regions of
\Sigma\setminus G
and the blocks of
\mathcal{F}
. So the weightings
\omega_{\mathcal{V}}
and
\omega_{\mathcal{F}}
naturally induce the vertex weighting
\phi:V\to\mathbb{N}_{0}
and region weighting
\rho:F\to\mathbb{N}_{0}
.

Figure 2
:
A graph embedded in a pseudo-surface and its corresponding packaged ribbon graph (weightings are omitted for clarity).

Observe that whilst there is a unique packaged ribbon graph arising from a given vertex and region weighted graph embedded in a pseudo-surface, the converse is not true. This can be seen through how we attach the handles.
In order to establish a one-to-one correspondence between the class of graphs embedded in pseudo-surfaces and the class of packaged ribbon graphs, we need to use the following equivalence relation from Definition 14 of
[
16
]
. We say two graphs embedded in a pseudo-surface are related by
stabilisation
if one can be obtained from the other by a finite sequence of removal and addition of handles which does not disconnect any region or coalesce any two regions, and any discs or annuli involved in adding or removing handles are disjoint from the graph. Using Theorem 15 of
[
16
]
, two graphs embedded in pseudo-surfaces with vertex and region weightings correspond to the same packaged ribbon graph if and only if they are related by stabilisation.

3
A Tutte polynomial for packaged ribbon graphs

In this section we introduce a Tutte polynomial for packaged ribbon graphs,
\boldsymbol{T}(\underline{\mathbb{G}};\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{y})
. We initially define our polynomial as a state sum (Definition
3.1
). However, in Theorem
3.3
we show that, just as with the classical Tutte polynomial, it can equivalently be defined through deletion-contraction relations. Moreover, Theorem
3.6
shows that it is a universal deletion-contraction invariant. In the next section we shall show that
\boldsymbol{T}(\underline{\mathbb{G}};\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{y})
subsumes both the surface Tutte polynomial of
[
13
]
, and the canonical topological Tutte polynomials of
[
16
,
17
]
. As a consequence we obtain a deletion-contraction relation for the surface Tutte polynomial.

Definition 3.1
.

Let
\underline{\mathbb{G}}=(\mathbb{G},\mathcal{V},\omega_{\mathcal{V}},\mathcal{F},\omega_{\mathcal{F}})
be a packaged ribbon graph. Define the
packaged surface Tutte polynomial

\boldsymbol{T}(\underline{\mathbb{G}};\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{y})
, with variables
\boldsymbol{x}=(x,x_{0},x_{1/2},x_{1},\ldots)
,
\boldsymbol{y}=(y,y_{0},y_{1/2},y_{1},\ldots)
, as follows

\boldsymbol{T}(\underline{\mathbb{G}};\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{y}):=\sum_{A\subseteq E}x^{n(G(\mathbb{G}^{*}|A^{c};\mathcal{F}))}y^{n(G(\mathbb{G}|A;\mathcal{V}))}\prod_{\begin{subarray}{c}H\text{ cpt. of }\\
G(\mathbb{G}^{*}|A^{c};\mathcal{F})\end{subarray}}x_{g(\mathbb{G}^{*},H)}\prod_{\begin{subarray}{c}K\text{ cpt. of }\\
G(\mathbb{G}|A;\mathcal{V})\end{subarray}}y_{g(\mathbb{G},K)}

where cpt. refers to connected component,

g(\mathbb{G},K):=\tfrac{1}{2}(2k(K)+e(K)-v(K)+\omega_{\mathcal{V}}(K)-f(\mathbb{G}[K])),

g(\mathbb{G}^{*},H):=\tfrac{1}{2}(2k(H)+e(H)-v(H)+\omega_{\mathcal{V}}(H)-f(\mathbb{G}^{*}[H])),

here
\omega_{\mathcal{V}}(K)=\sum_{v\in V(K)}\omega_{\mathcal{V}}([v])
, and
\omega_{\mathcal{V}}(H)
is defined similarly.

Note that although the packaged surface Tutte polynomial is defined in an unbounded number of variables, for any given
\underline{\mathbb{G}}
it is a polynomial in a finite number of variables. We note that a consequence of Theorem
4.3
is that when
\boldsymbol{T}(\underline{\mathbb{G}};\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{y})
is restricted to (non-packaged) ribbon graphs only integrally indexed variables arise.

Example 3.2
.

Consider the initial packaged ribbon graph
\underline{\mathbb{G}}
depicted in Figure
3
(i.e. a 2-cycle interlaced with a loop). Every block in the partitions has size one and every block is mapped to zero. Applying the definition of the packaged surface Tutte polynomial to this packaged ribbon graph yields the following.

\boldsymbol{T}(\underline{\mathbb{G}};\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{y})=x^{3}x_{1}y_{0}^{2}+2x^{2}x_{1}y_{0}+3xyx_{0}y_{0}+x^{2}yx_{0}y_{0}^{2}+y^{2}x_{0}y_{1}.

We defined
\boldsymbol{T}(\underline{\mathbb{G}};\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{y})
as a state-sum, however the following theorem shows that, just as with the Tutte polynomial, it can be defined through deletion-contraction relations. This is the key property for the applications of the polynomial in the next sections.

Theorem 3.3
.

Let
\underline{\mathbb{G}}=(\mathbb{G},\mathcal{V},\omega_{\mathcal{V}},\mathcal{F},\omega_{\mathcal{F}})
be a packaged ribbon graph and
e\in E
. Then

\boldsymbol{T}(\underline{\mathbb{G}};\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{y})=x^{2-\eta(e)}\boldsymbol{T}(\underline{\mathbb{G}}\backslash e;\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{y})+y^{2-\mu(e)}\boldsymbol{T}(\underline{\mathbb{G}}/e;\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{y}).

If
\mathbb{G}
has no edges, then

\boldsymbol{T}(\underline{\mathbb{G}};\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{y})=\prod_{[f]\in\mathcal{F}}x_{(1-|[f]|+\omega_{\mathcal{F}}([f]))/2}\prod_{[v]\in\mathcal{V}}y_{(1-|[v]|+\omega_{\mathcal{V}}([v]))/2}

where, for any block
[a]
of a partition,
|[a]|
denotes how many elements are in the block.

Proof.

Before we begin our analysis into how the packaged surface Tutte polynomial changes under deletion and contraction, we will simplify it by expressing it in terms of two polynomials that correspond under duality. Define the following polynomials for any packaged ribbon graph
\underline{\mathbb{G}}=(\mathbb{G},\mathcal{V},\omega_{\mathcal{V}},\mathcal{F},\omega_{\mathcal{F}})
with edge subset
A

\displaystyle\Theta(\underline{\mathbb{G}},A;\boldsymbol{x})

\displaystyle:=x^{n(G(\mathbb{G}^{*}|A^{c};\mathcal{F}))}\prod_{\begin{subarray}{c}H\text{ cpt. of }\\
G(\mathbb{G}^{*}|A^{c};\mathcal{F})\end{subarray}}x_{g(\mathbb{G}^{*},H)},

\displaystyle\Phi(\underline{\mathbb{G}},A;\boldsymbol{y})

\displaystyle:=y^{n(G(\mathbb{G}|A;\mathcal{V}))}\prod_{\begin{subarray}{c}K\text{ cpt. of }\\
G(\mathbb{G}|A;\mathcal{V})\end{subarray}}y_{g(\mathbb{G},K)}.

Consider
\Theta(\underline{\mathbb{G}}^{*},A^{c};\boldsymbol{x})
where
\underline{\mathbb{G}}^{*}=(\mathbb{G}^{*},\mathcal{F},\omega_{\mathcal{F}},\mathcal{V},\omega_{\mathcal{V}})
. As duality is an involution and
(A^{c})^{c}=E\setminus A^{c}=A
, we have

\displaystyle\Theta(\underline{\mathbb{G}}^{*},A^{c};\boldsymbol{x})=x^{n(G(\mathbb{G}|A;\mathcal{V}))}\prod_{\begin{subarray}{c}H\text{ cpt. of }\\
G(\mathbb{G}|A;\mathcal{V})\end{subarray}}x_{g(\mathbb{G},H)}=\Phi(\underline{\mathbb{G}},A;\boldsymbol{x}).

(3)

Using the above we can rewrite
\boldsymbol{T}(\underline{\mathbb{G}};\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{y})
in terms of
\Phi
as follows.

\begin{split}\boldsymbol{T}(\underline{\mathbb{G}};\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{y})&=\sum_{A\subseteq E}\Theta(\underline{\mathbb{G}},A;\boldsymbol{x})\Phi(\underline{\mathbb{G}},A;\boldsymbol{y})\\
&=\sum_{A\subseteq E\setminus e}[\Theta(\underline{\mathbb{G}},A;\boldsymbol{x})\Phi(\underline{\mathbb{G}},A;\boldsymbol{y})+\Theta(\underline{\mathbb{G}},A\cup e;\boldsymbol{x})\Phi(\underline{\mathbb{G}},A\cup e;\boldsymbol{y})]\\
&=\sum_{A\subseteq E\setminus e}\Phi(\underline{\mathbb{G}}^{*},A^{c};\boldsymbol{x})\Phi(\underline{\mathbb{G}},A;\boldsymbol{y})+\sum_{A\subseteq E\setminus e}\Phi(\underline{\mathbb{G}}^{*},(A\cup e)^{c};\boldsymbol{x})\Phi(\underline{\mathbb{G}},A\cup e;\boldsymbol{y}).\end{split}

(4)

We need to check how the packaged surface Tutte polynomial changes when we delete or contract the four different types of edges (i.e. whether
\eta(e)=1
or 2 and whether
\mu(e)=1
or 2). With this new expression for the polynomial that uses duality, it suffices to look at how
\Phi(\underline{\mathbb{G}},A;\boldsymbol{y})
changes under deletion and contraction. As
\Phi(\underline{\mathbb{G}},A;\boldsymbol{y})
is independent from the boundary component partition
\mathcal{F}
, our edge cases are only dependent on the vertex partition
\mathcal{V}
. So we only consider the two edge cases when
\mu(e)=1
and
\mu(e)=2
. For the remainder of the proof for the first half of the theorem, we assume that
A\subseteq E\setminus e
.

We consider how
\Phi(\underline{\mathbb{G}},A;\boldsymbol{y})
changes under deletion. As
A\subseteq E\setminus e
, then
\underline{\mathbb{G}}\backslash A^{c}=(\underline{\mathbb{G}}\backslash e)\backslash A^{c}
so
\mathbb{G}|A=(\mathbb{G}\backslash e)|A
and
\mathcal{V},\omega_{\mathcal{V}}
are unchanged. So in this case
\Phi(\underline{\mathbb{G}},A;\boldsymbol{y})=\Phi(\underline{\mathbb{G}}\backslash e,A;\boldsymbol{y})
.

We want to express
\Phi(\underline{\mathbb{G}},A\cup e;\boldsymbol{y})
as a polynomial of
\underline{\mathbb{G}}/e=(\mathbb{G}/e,\mathcal{V}^{\prime},\omega_{\mathcal{V}}^{\prime},\mathcal{F}^{\prime},\omega_{\mathcal{F}}^{\prime})
. Here we consider the two edge cases of
\mu(e)=2
and
\mu(e)=1
separately. Suppose
\mu(e)=2
, or equivalently
e
is incident to two vertices
u,v
where
[u]\neq[v]
. The packaging
G(\mathbb{G}/e|A;\mathcal{V}^{\prime})
has the same number of connected components, one less edge and one less vertex than the packaging
G(\mathbb{G}|(A\cup e);\mathcal{V})
. So
n(G(\mathbb{G}/e|A;\mathcal{V}^{\prime}))=n(G(\mathbb{G}|(A\cup e);\mathcal{V}))
. As the number of connected components is unchanged, there is a one-to-one correspondence between the connected components of
G(\mathbb{G}/e|A;\mathcal{V}^{\prime})
and those of
G(\mathbb{G}|(A\cup e);\mathcal{V})
. Let
K
be a connected component of
G(\mathbb{G}|(A\cup e);\mathcal{V})
and
K^{\prime}
denote its corresponding connected component of
G(\mathbb{G}/e|A;\mathcal{V}^{\prime})
. First assume
e\in E(K)
. Observe that contracting
e
in
\underline{\mathbb{G}}
before forming the packaging does not change the total weight
\omega_{\mathcal{V}}(K)
, it decreases the number of edges and the number of vertices in
K
by one, and does not change the number of boundary components. Therefore
g(\mathbb{G},K)=g(\mathbb{G}/e,K^{\prime})
. Next if
e\notin E(K)
, then contracting
e
does not alter
K
, and
g(\mathbb{G},K)=g(\mathbb{G}/e,K^{\prime})
is unaffected by contracting
e
. So
\Phi(\underline{\mathbb{G}},A\cup e;\boldsymbol{y})=\Phi(\underline{\mathbb{G}}/e,A;\boldsymbol{y})
when
\mu(e)=2
.

Now suppose
\mu(e)=1
, so either
e
is a non-loop edge whose incident vertices are in the same block of
\mathcal{V}
or
e
is a loop. We first assume the former is true, then the packaging
G(\mathbb{G}/e|A;\mathcal{V}^{\prime})
has the same number of connected components, one less edge and the same number of vertices as the packaging
G(\mathbb{G}|(A\cup e);\mathcal{V})
. So
n(G(\mathbb{G}/e|A;\mathcal{V}^{\prime}))=n(G(\mathbb{G}|(A\cup e);\mathcal{V}))-1
. As before, there is a one-to-one correspondence between the connected components of
G(\mathbb{G}/e|A;\mathcal{V}^{\prime})
and those of
G(\mathbb{G}|(A\cup e);\mathcal{V})
. Let
K
be a connected component of
G(\mathbb{G}|(A\cup e);\mathcal{V})
and
K^{\prime}
denote its corresponding connected component of
G(\mathbb{G}/e|A;\mathcal{V}^{\prime})
. First assume
e\in E(K)
. Observe that contracting
e
in
\underline{\mathbb{G}}
before forming the packaging increases the total weight
\omega_{\mathcal{V}}(K)
by one, it decreases the number of edges by one, and does not change the number of boundary components or vertices. Therefore,
g(\mathbb{G},K)=g(\mathbb{G}/e,K^{\prime})
. Next if
e\notin E(K)
, then contracting
e
does not alter
K
, and
g(\mathbb{G},K)=g(\mathbb{G}/e,K^{\prime})
. So
\Phi(\underline{\mathbb{G}},A\cup e;\boldsymbol{y})=y\Phi(\underline{\mathbb{G}}/e,A;\boldsymbol{y})
.

Finally, assume
e
is a loop. Then the packaging
G(\mathbb{G}/e|A;\mathcal{V}^{\prime})
has the same number of connected components, one less edge and the same number of vertices as the packaging
G(\mathbb{G}|(A\cup e);\mathcal{V})
. So
n(G(\mathbb{G}/e|A;\mathcal{V}^{\prime}))=n(G(\mathbb{G}|(A\cup e);\mathcal{V}))-1
. As before, there is a one-to-one correspondence between the connected components of
G(\mathbb{G}/e|A;\mathcal{V})
and those of
G(\mathbb{G}|(A\cup e);\mathcal{V})
. Let
K
be a connected component of
G(\mathbb{G}|(A\cup e);\mathcal{V})
. First assume
e\in E(K)
. Observe that contracting
e
in
\underline{\mathbb{G}}
before forming the packaging increases the total weight
\omega_{\mathcal{V}}(K)
by one, decreases the number of edges by one, and does not change the number of boundary components. Therefore,
g(\mathbb{G},K)=g(\mathbb{G}/e,K^{\prime})
. Next if
e\notin E(K)
, then
g(\mathbb{G},K)
is unaffected by contracting
e
. So
\Phi(\underline{\mathbb{G}},A\cup e;\boldsymbol{y})=y\Phi(\underline{\mathbb{G}}/e,A;\boldsymbol{y})
when
\mu(e)=1
.

All that remains to prove the first half of the theorem is expressing
\Phi(\underline{\mathbb{G}}^{*},A^{c};\boldsymbol{x})
and
\Phi(\underline{\mathbb{G}}^{*},(A\cup e)^{c};\boldsymbol{x})
in terms of
\underline{\mathbb{G}}\backslash e
and
\underline{\mathbb{G}}/e
. Recall that
A\subseteq E\setminus e
, so
e\in A^{c}
and
e\notin(A\cup e)^{c}
. Therefore by the previous case analysis

\Phi(\underline{\mathbb{G}}^{*},A^{c};\boldsymbol{x})=\begin{cases}\Phi(\underline{\mathbb{G}}^{*}/e,A^{c}\setminus e;\boldsymbol{x})&\text{ if $\mu_{\underline{\mathbb{G}}^{*}}(e)=2$},\\
x\Phi(\underline{\mathbb{G}}^{*}/e,A^{c}\setminus e;\boldsymbol{x})&\text{ if $\mu_{\underline{\mathbb{G}}^{*}}(e)=1$},\end{cases}

and
\Phi(\underline{\mathbb{G}}^{*},(A\cup e)^{c};\boldsymbol{x})=\Phi(\underline{\mathbb{G}}^{*}\backslash e,(A\cup e)^{c};\boldsymbol{x})

\underline{\mathbb{G}}^{*}/e=\underline{\mathbb{G}}\backslash e

and

Our first polynomial is the
Las Vergnas polynomial

L(\mathbb{G};x,y,z)
introduced in the 1980 paper
[
19
]
. This is a polynomial of graphs cellularly embedded in surfaces that arises as a special instance of his Tutte polynomial of matroid perspectives.
Unfortunately, the Las Vergnas polynomial did not receive the attention it deserved and it took another 20 years until the introduction of the
Bollobás–Riordan polynomial
of a ribbon graph in
[
5

,
R(\mathbb{G};x,y,z)

ribbon graph polynomial
,
R(\mathbb{G};x,y)
, which arises as a 2-variable specialisation of the Bollobás–Riordan polynomial.
The
Krushkal polynomial
,
K(\mathbb{G};x,y,a,b)
, was introduced around ten years later in
[
18
]
. This is a polynomial of graphs that are (not-necessarily cellularly) embedded in surfaces. It was shown to specialise to the Bollobás–Riordan polynomial in
[
18
]
and the Las Vergnas polynomial in
[
3
]
. All these polynomials specialise to the Tutte polynomial and in more than one way.

Of the above polynomials, only the ribbon graph polynomial has a full deletion-contraction definition akin to that for the Tutte polynomial (see the discussion around (
2
)). However, by enlarging their domains it is possible to find extensions of them that do have full deletion-contraction definitions. This was first done for the Las Vergnas polynomial in
[
10
]
by extending its domain to graphs in pseudo-surfaces; then, using the theory of
canonical Tutte polynomials
, for the Bollobás–Riordan polynomial and the Krushkal polynomial of cellularly embedded graphs in
[
10
]
by extending their domains to ribbon graphs whose vertex set is partitioned; and for the full Krushkal polynomial in
[
16
]
by extending its domain to graphs in pseudo-surfaces (see also the delta-matroid extension in
[
20
]
). This Krushkal polynomial extension is known as the
Tutte polynomial of a coloured ribbon graph
or the
Tutte polynomial of graphs in pseudo-surfaces
depending upon context.
In our notation, it is defined as follows.

Definition 4.1
.

Let
\mathbb{G}=(V,E)
be a ribbon graph with a partition
\mathcal{V}
of its vertex set and
\mathcal{F}
a partition of its set of boundary components. Then

T_{ps}(\mathbb{G};w,x,y,z):=\sum_{A\subseteq E}w^{r_{1}(E)-r_{1}(A)}x^{r_{2}(E)-r_{2}(A)}y^{r_{3}(A)}z^{r_{4}(A)},

where

\displaystyle r_{1}(A)

\displaystyle:=r(G(\mathbb{G}|A;\mathcal{V})),

\displaystyle r_{2}(A)

\displaystyle:=r(\mathbb{G}|A)+g(\mathbb{G}|A)-r(G(\mathbb{G}|A;\mathcal{V})),

\displaystyle r_{3}(A)

\displaystyle:=r(G(\mathbb{G}^{*};\mathcal{F}))-r(G(\mathbb{G}^{*}|A^{c};\mathcal{F})),

\displaystyle r_{4}(A)

\displaystyle:=|A|+r(G(\mathbb{G}^{*}|A^{c};\mathcal{F}))-r(G(\mathbb{G}^{*};\mathcal{F}))-r(\mathbb{G}|A)-g(\mathbb{G}|A).

All of the polynomials mentioned above can be recovered from
T_{ps}(\mathbb{G};w,x,y,z)
. See
[
16
]
for details.
We note two significant specialisations of this polynomial:

T_{cps}(\mathbb{G};w,x,y)
and
T_{s}(\mathbb{G};x,y,z)
. These are polynomials for graphs cellularly embedded in pseudo-surfaces, and emebdded in surfaces, respectively. They are related by duality and are extensions of the Bollobás–Riordan polynomial that have full deletion-contraction definitions.

Finally, the most recently defined topological Tutte polynomial we consider is the
surface Tutte polynomial
introduced in
[
13
]
and extended to non-orientable surfaces in
[
14
]
. This polynomial was motivated by Tutte’s introduction of the dichromate as a polynomial that counts both the number of proper
k
-colourings and the number of nowhere-zero
\mathbb{Z}_{k}
-flows in a graph. In particular, the surface Tutte polynomial counts both the local flows and local tensions in a graph cellularly embedded in a surface (this aspect of the polynomial is discussed in Section
5
).
In our notation, the surface Tutte polynomial is defined as follows.

Definition 4.2
.

Let
\mathbb{G}
be an orientable ribbon graph. Then the
surface Tutte polynomial

\mathcal{T}(\mathbb{G};\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{y})
, where
\boldsymbol{x}=(x,x_{0},x_{1},\ldots),\boldsymbol{y}=(y,y_{0},y_{1},\ldots)
, is defined as

\mathcal{T}(\mathbb{G};\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{y}):=\sum_{A\subseteq E}x^{n(\mathbb{G}^{*}|A^{c})}y^{n(\mathbb{G}|A)}\prod_{\begin{subarray}{c}\text{cpt. }\\
\mathbb{G}_{i}\text{ of }\mathbb{G}/A\end{subarray}}x_{g(\mathbb{G}_{i})}\prod_{\begin{subarray}{c}\text{cpt. }\\
\mathbb{G}_{j}\text{ of }\mathbb{G}|A\end{subarray}}y_{g(\mathbb{G}_{j})}

where
A^{c}=E\setminus A
.

The surface Tutte polynomial specialises to the Krushkal polynomial of a cellularly embedded graph and therefore to the Bollobás–Riordan, Las Vergnas and Tutte polynomial (see
[
13
]
for details). However, it does not have a known full deletion-contraction definition, and it does not specialise to
T_{ps}(\mathbb{G};w,x,y,z)
or any of the extensions of Krushkal, Bollobás–Riordan, or Las Vernas polynomials that have deletion-contraction definitions.

The following theorem shows that

of the above topological Tutte polynomials can be recovered from our polynomial

Theorem 4.3
.

Both the surface Tutte polynomial and the Tutte polynomial of graphs in pseudo-surfaces can be recovered from the Tutte polynomial of a packaged ribbon graph, as follows.

1.

The first item is immediate. For the second, when

5
Recursively counting local flows and local tensions

The surface Tutte polynomial has been shown to count the number of local flows and local tensions for a ribbon graph
[
13
]
. We now apply our results to obtain deletion-contraction relations for the numbers of local flows and local tensions.

Let
\mathbb{G}=(V,E)
be an oriented ribbon graph and
F
be its set of boundary components. Each boundary component inherits an orientation from
\mathbb{G}
. Furthermore, direct each edge of
\mathbb{G}
. Each edge thus consists of two half-edges, one of which is the head and the other the tail. If
h
is a half-edge we set

a(h):=\begin{cases}1&\text{if $h$ is the head of $e$},\\
-1&\text{if $h$ is the tail of $e$}.\end{cases}

Each edge contains two segments that are part of the boundary components of
\mathbb{G}
. We call these the
edge-sides
. If an edge is directed, then its edge-sides are directed in the same direction. For each edge, the direction of one edge-side will agree with the orientation of the boundary component it sits on, the other will disagree. For an edge-side
h
we set

b(h):=\begin{cases}1&\text{if the direction of $h$ disagrees with the boundary orientation},\\
-1&\text{if the direction of $h$ agrees with the boundary orientation}.\end{cases}

Now suppose that

\Gamma
is a finite group and
\gamma:E\to\Gamma
is a mapping that assigns a group element to each edge.
We say
\gamma
is a
local
\Gamma
-flow
if for each vertex
v
we have that

(\gamma(e_{1}))^{a(h_{1})}(\gamma(e_{2}))^{a(h_{2})}\dotsc(\gamma(e_{n}))^{a(h_{n})}=1,

where
h_{1},h_{2},...,h_{n}
are the cyclically ordered incident half-edges incident to
v
, and each half-edge
h_{i}
belongs to the edge
e_{i}
.
It is a
nowhere-identity local
\Gamma
-flow
for
\mathbb{G}
if it is a local
\Gamma
-flow and no edge is mapped to the identity element.

Similarly,
\gamma
is a
local
\Gamma
-tension
if for each boundary component
f
we have that

(\gamma(e_{1}))^{b(h_{1})}(\gamma(e_{2}))^{b(h_{2})}\dotsc(\gamma(e_{n}))^{b(h_{n})}=1,

where
h_{1},h_{2},...,h_{n}
are the cyclically ordered edge-sides met when following
f
, and each edge-side
h_{i}
belongs to the edge
e_{i}
.
It is a
nowhere-identity local
\Gamma
-tension
for
\mathbb{G}
if it is a local
\Gamma
-tension and no edge is mapped to the identity element.

We denote the number of local
\Gamma
-flows by
q_{\Gamma}^{1}(\mathbb{G})
and the number of nowhere-identity local
\Gamma
-flows by
q_{\Gamma}(\mathbb{G})
.
Similarly, we denote the number of local
\Gamma
-tensions by
p_{\Gamma}^{1}(\mathbb{G})
and the number of nowhere-identity local
\Gamma
-tensions by
p_{\Gamma}(\mathbb{G})
.

that all four of these counts are independent of the choice of edge directions for

and

\mathbb{G}
is a connected ribbon graph, and
\Gamma
is a finite group whose irreducible representations have dimensions

q^{1}_{\Gamma}(\mathbb{G})=|\Gamma|^{e(\mathbb{G})-v(\mathbb{G})}\sum_{k}n_{k}^{f(\mathbb{G})-e(\mathbb{G})+v(\mathbb{G})},

and

p_{\Gamma}^{1}(\mathbb{G})=q_{\Gamma}^{1}(\mathbb{G}^{*})

\mathbb{G}
is a connected ribbon graph, and
\Gamma
is a finite group whose irreducible representations have dimensions
n_{1},...,n_{k}

p^{1}_{\Gamma}(\mathbb{G})=|\Gamma|^{e(\mathbb{G})-f(\mathbb{G})}\sum_{k}n_{k}^{v(\mathbb{G})-e(\mathbb{G})+f(\mathbb{G})},

p_{\Gamma}(\mathbb{G})=\sum_{A\subseteq E}(-1)^{|A^{c}|}p_{\Gamma}^{1}(\mathbb{G}/A^{c}).

Lemma 5.3
.

Let
\underline{\mathbb{G}}=(\mathbb{G},\mathcal{V},\omega_{\mathcal{V}},\mathcal{F},\omega_{\mathcal{F}})

Proof.

First note that
G((\mathbb{G}^{*})^{*};\mathcal{F}^{*})

G(\mathbb{G};\mathcal{V})

is connected, and the disconnected case follows from this:

p^{1}_{\Gamma}(\underline{\mathbb{G}}^{*}_{\mathcal{F}^{*}})=\prod_{\begin{subarray}{c}H\text{ cpt. of }\\
G((\mathbb{G}^{*})^{*};\mathcal{F}^{*})\end{subarray}}p_{\Gamma}^{1}((\underline{\mathbb{G}}^{*}_{\mathcal{F}^{*}}[H])=\prod_{\begin{subarray}{c}H\text{ cpt. of }\\
G(\mathbb{G};\mathcal{V})\end{subarray}}q_{\Gamma}^{1}((\underline{\mathbb{G}}_{\mathcal{V}}[H]))=q^{1}_{\Gamma}(\underline{\mathbb{G}}_{\mathcal{V}}).

By an application of Proposition
2.7
it follows that
q_{\Gamma}(\underline{\mathbb{G}}_{\mathcal{V}})=p_{\Gamma}(\underline{\mathbb{G}}^{*}_{\mathcal{F}^{*}})
.
Finally, the identity
p_{\Gamma}(\underline{\mathbb{G}}_{\mathcal{F}})=q_{\Gamma}(\underline{\mathbb{G}}^{*}_{\mathcal{V}^{*}})
follows since duality is an involution.
∎

Proposition 5.4
.

Let
\mathbb{G}=(V,E)
be a connected orientable ribbon graph and
F
be its set of boundary components. Also, let
\underline{\mathbb{G}}
be the packaged ribbon graph
(\mathbb{G},\{\{v\}:v\in V\},0_{V},\{\{f\}:f\in F\},0_{F})
, where
0_{\cdot}
denotes the zero-map. Then,
q_{\Gamma}^{1}(\underline{\mathbb{G}}_{\mathcal{V}})=q_{\Gamma}^{1}(\mathbb{G})
,
q_{\Gamma}(\underline{\mathbb{G}}_{\mathcal{V}})=q_{\Gamma}(\mathbb{G})
,
p_{\Gamma}^{1}(\underline{\mathbb{G}}_{\mathcal{F}})=p_{\Gamma}^{1}(\mathbb{G})
, and
p_{\Gamma}(\underline{\mathbb{G}}_{\mathcal{F}})=p_{\Gamma}(\mathbb{G})
.
In particular,
q_{\Gamma}(\underline{\mathbb{G}}_{\mathcal{V}})
gives the number of nowhere-identity local
\Gamma
-flows in
\mathbb{G}
, and
p_{\Gamma}(\underline{\mathbb{G}}_{\mathcal{F}})
gives the number of nowhere-identity local
\Gamma
-tensions in
\mathbb{G}
.

Proof.

That
q_{\Gamma}^{1}(\underline{\mathbb{G}}_{\mathcal{V}})=q_{\Gamma}^{1}(\mathbb{G})
and
p_{\Gamma}^{1}(\underline{\mathbb{G}}_{\mathcal{F}})=p_{\Gamma}^{1}(\mathbb{G})
is immediate.
Since contraction does not change
\mathcal{F}
and deletion does not change
\mathcal{V}
it follows that

p_{\Gamma}(\underline{\mathbb{G}}_{\mathcal{V}})=p_{\Gamma}(\mathbb{G})
and
q_{\Gamma}(\underline{\mathbb{G}}_{\mathcal{F}})=q_{\Gamma}(\mathbb{G})
.
∎

Theorem 5.5
.

Let
\underline{\mathbb{G}}=(\mathbb{G},\mathcal{V},\omega_{\mathcal{V}},\mathcal{F},\omega_{\mathcal{F}})
be a packaged ribbon graph and
\Gamma
be a finite group whose irreducible representations have dimensions
n_{1},...,n_{k}
. Then the following identities hold.

1.

When
x=1
,
y=-|\Gamma|
,
x_{g}=1
, and
y_{g}=-\frac{1}{|\Gamma|}\sum_{i=1}^{k}n_{i}^{2-2g}
, we have

q_{\Gamma}(\underline{\mathbb{G}}_{\mathcal{V}})=(-1)^{e(\mathbb{G})-|\mathcal{V}|}\boldsymbol{T}(\underline{\mathbb{G}};\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{y}).

2.

When
x=-|\Gamma|
,
y=1
,
x_{g}=-\frac{1}{|\Gamma|}\sum_{i=1}^{k}n_{i}^{2-2g}
, and
y_{g}=1
, we have

p_{\Gamma}(\underline{\mathbb{G}}_{\mathcal{F}})=(-1)^{e(\mathbb{G})-|\mathcal{F}|}\boldsymbol{T}(\underline{\mathbb{G}};\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{y}).

Proof.

\displaystyle q_{\Gamma}(\underline{\mathbb{G}}_{\mathcal{V}})

\displaystyle=\sum_{A\subseteq E}(-1)^{|A^{c}|}\prod_{\begin{subarray}{c}K\text{ cpt. of }\\
G(\mathbb{G}|A;\mathcal{V})\end{subarray}}|\Gamma|^{e(K)-v(K)}\sum_{k}n_{k}^{f(\mathbb{G}[K])-e(K)+v(K)-\omega_{\mathcal{V}}(K)}

\displaystyle=\sum_{A\subseteq E}(-1)^{|A^{c}|}|\Gamma|^{e(\mathbb{G}|A)-v(G(\mathbb{G}|A;\mathcal{V}))}\prod_{\begin{subarray}{c}K\text{ cpt. of }\\
G(\mathbb{G};\mathcal{V})\end{subarray}}\sum_{k}n_{k}^{f(\mathbb{G}[K])-e(K)+v(K)-\omega_{\mathcal{V}}(K)}

\displaystyle=(-1)^{e(\mathbb{G})-|\mathcal{V}|}\sum_{A\subseteq E}(-|\Gamma|)^{n(G(\mathbb{G}|A;\mathcal{V}))}\prod_{\begin{subarray}{c}K\text{ cpt. of }\\
G(\mathbb{G}|A;\mathcal{V})\end{subarray}}-\frac{1}{|\Gamma|}\sum_{i=1}^{k}n_{i}^{2-2g(\mathbb{G},K)},

which is
(-1)^{e(\mathbb{G})-|\mathcal{V}|}\boldsymbol{T}(\underline{\mathbb{G}};\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{y})
when
x=1,y=-|\Gamma|,x_{g}=1,y_{g}=-\frac{1}{|\Gamma|}\sum_{i=1}^{k}n_{i}^{2-2g}
.

The result for
p_{\Gamma}(\underline{\mathbb{G}}_{\mathcal{F}})
follows from the first item by Lemma
5.3
and Theorem
3.5
.
∎

By applying Theorem
3.3
to the above specialisations, we get the following recursions for the number of nowhere-identity local flows and tensions. This is the main result of this section.

Theorem 5.6
.

Let
\underline{\mathbb{G}}=(\mathbb{G},\mathcal{V},\omega_{\mathcal{V}},\mathcal{F},\omega_{\mathcal{F}})
be a packaged ribbon graph,
e\in E(\mathbb{G})
and
\Gamma
be a finite group whose irreducible representations have dimensions
n_{1},...,n_{k}
.

1.

For any edge
e
,

q_{\Gamma}(\underline{\mathbb{G}}_{\mathcal{V}})=|\Gamma|^{2-\mu(e)}q_{\Gamma}((\underline{\mathbb{G}}/e)_{\mathcal{V}})-q_{\Gamma}((\underline{\mathbb{G}}\backslash e)_{\mathcal{V}}).

If
\underline{\mathbb{G}}
has no edges, then

q_{\Gamma}(\underline{\mathbb{G}}_{\mathcal{V}})=\prod_{[v]\in\mathcal{V}}|\Gamma|^{-1}\sum_{k}n_{k}^{1+|[v]|-\omega_{\mathcal{V}}([v])}.

2.

For any edge
e
,

p_{\Gamma}(\underline{\mathbb{G}}_{\mathcal{F}})=|\Gamma|^{2-\eta(e)}p_{\Gamma}((\underline{\mathbb{G}}\backslash e)_{\mathcal{F}})-p_{\Gamma}((\underline{\mathbb{G}}/e)_{\mathcal{F}}).

If
\underline{\mathbb{G}}
has no edges, then

p_{\Gamma}(\underline{\mathbb{G}}_{\mathcal{F}})=\prod_{[f]\in\mathcal{F}}|\Gamma|^{-1}\sum_{k}n_{k}^{1+|[f]|-\omega_{\mathcal{F}}([f])}.

Proof.

The first item follows by Theorem
5.5
with the specialisations of Theorem
3.3
, and using the fact that
v(G(\mathbb{G};\mathcal{V}))=v(G(\mathbb{G}\backslash e;\mathcal{V}))=v(G(\mathbb{G}/e;\mathcal{V}^{\prime}))
when
\mu(e)=1
, and
v(G(\mathbb{G};\mathcal{V}))=v(G(\mathbb{G}\backslash e;\mathcal{V}))=v(G(\mathbb{G}/e;\mathcal{V}^{\prime}))+1
when
\mu(e)=2
. The case when
\underline{\mathbb{G}}
has no edges follows directly from the definition of
q_{\Gamma}(\underline{\mathbb{G}}_{\mathcal{V}})
as the only edge subset of
E(\mathbb{G})
is the empty set.

By a similar argument, one can also obtain the second half of the theorem. We omit the details.
∎

One of the anonymous referees observed that the recurrences for the number of (nowhere-identity) local flows and tensions of a ribbon graph (with no packaging) can partially
be argued directly in a similar way to (nowhere-zero) flows and tensions for graphs. However, this cannot be done for all edge types which is where the lifting to packaged ribbon graphs comes
into its own.

Example 5.7
.

We will use Theorem
5.6
to find
q_{\Gamma}(\underline{\mathbb{G}}_{\mathcal{V}})
for the initial packaged ribbon graph
\underline{\mathbb{G}}=(\mathbb{G},\mathcal{V},\omega_{\mathcal{V}},\mathcal{F},\omega_{\mathcal{F}})
depicted in Figure
3
(i.e. a 2-cycle interlaced with a loop, every partition block has size one, and every block is mapped to zero). Let
\Gamma=D_{6}
be the dihedral group of order 6, so
\Gamma
has two 1-dimensional representations and one 2-dimensional representation. Using Theorem
5.6
and the deletion-contraction as depicted in Figure
3
,

\displaystyle q_{\Gamma}(\underline{\mathbb{G}}_{\mathcal{V}})

\displaystyle=-6^{-2}(2+2^{2})^{2}+2\cdot 6^{-1}(2+2^{2})-(2+2^{2})+6^{-1}(2+2^{2})^{2}-2(2+2^{2})+6(2+2^{0})

\displaystyle=-1+2-6+6-12+18=7.
