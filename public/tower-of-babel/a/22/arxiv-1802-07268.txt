Scrambling in the Quantum Lifshitz Model
Eugeniu Plamadeala, Eduardo Fradkin

Abstract
We study signatures of chaos in the quantum Lifshitz model through out-of-time ordered correlators (OTOC) of current operators. This model is a free scalar field theory with dynamical critical exponent z = 2 z=2 . It describes the quantum phase transition in 2D systems, such as quantum dimer models, between a phase with an uniform ground state to another one with a spontaneously translation invariance. At the lowest temperatures the chaotic dynamics are dominated by a marginally irrelevant operator which induces a temperature dependent stiffness term. The numerical computations of OTOC exhibit a non-zero Lyapunov exponent (LE) in a wide range of temperatures and interaction strengths. The LE (in units of temperature) is a weakly temperature-dependent function; it vanishes at weak interaction and saturates for strong interaction. The Butterfly velocity increases monotonically with interaction strength in the studied region while remaining smaller than the interaction-induced velocity/stiffness.

Introduction

Scrambling is the delocalization of initially local quantum information over the entire system. It implies that information about the initial state cannot be deduced by any local measurements of the final state. This notion is closely related the notion of quantum chaos in many-body systems
[
1
]
.
The so-called out-of-time-order correlators were invented to capture the failure of semiclassical methods in a study of superconductivity.
[
2
]

More recently it has been realized that they are a way to quantify chaos and information scrambling in black holes

[
3
,
4
,
5
]
.
Specifically, for generic local Hermitean operators
W
and
V
, the signature of chaos is a regime of exponential growth of the OTOC
C(t)\sim e^{\lambda t}
after local relaxation has occured but before the scrambling time when
C(t)\sim 1
. The exponent
\lambda
is called a “Lyapunov exponent” by analogy with the
Lyapunov exponents of classical dynamical systems:
[
6
,
7
]
in both classical and quantum cases the system looses memory
of its initial state exponentially fast. It is generally conjectured that systems with this behavior are “chaotic”.
In the classical setting this means that the system over very long times eventually explores all its available phase state with probability 1.
Although the precise definition of chaos is lacking (or rather there is an abundance of them
[
1
]
), in the quantum setting of a chaotic system it is believed that, at times long compared with the
scrambling time, the system becomes described by a density matrix and in some sense has thermalized.
[
8
]

The out-of-time ordered correlator
C(t)
(OTOC) of two hermitian operators
V
and
W
is

\displaystyle C(t)

\displaystyle=\tr\{\rho[W(t),V]^{\dagger}[W(t),V]\}

(1.1)

Numerous works have recently studied quantum chaos both at strong
[
9
,
3
]
and weak
coupling,
[
10
,
11
]
in gapped phases, in rational CFTs,
[
12
,
13
]

and in strongly disordered phases.
[
14
,
15
,
16
]

In this paper we will compute the chaos (Lyapunov) exponents
and the Butterfly velocity for a system close to a quantum critical point. In general, this is a challenging problem since most quantum
critical points are associated to a non-trivial UV fixed point of a quantum field theory. However in 2+1 dimensions there is a quantum critical theory,
the quantum Lifshitz model
[
17
]
(qLM), which is a free (compactified) scalar field theory with dynamical exponent
z=2
.
Since this fixed point theory is a free field, it is not chaotic. In spite of its simplicity, the qLM is a non trivial theory with a rich spectrum of operators,
and has been studied quite extensively.
[
17
,
18
,
19
]

One aspect of this theory that is useful in this context is that it has a marginally irrelevant operator
[
18
,
20
,
21
]

which, if included in the Lagrangian, spoils the integrability of the fixed point theory.
This is thus a controlled setting to examine the behavior of the OTOCs of this theory within a perturbative approach.

The imaginary-time action of the qLM in 2+1-dimensions is:

\displaystyle S_{0}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\int d\tau d^{2}x\left(\left(\partial_{\tau}\phi\right)^{2}+\kappa\left(\bigtriangledown^{2}\phi\right)^{2}\right)

(1.2)

There are several variations of this model: the compactified and non-compactified real scalar, as well as the complex scalar.
The leading irrelevant operator at all these critical points is the same -
\left(\bigtriangledown\phi\right)^{4}
, and for this reason we believe the physics
concerning chaos will be
qualitatively
the same. For simplicity, here we choose to study the case of the non-compactified real scalar theory.

Since this QCP is described by a quadratic action, it is integrable and cannot thermalize.
Lack of integrability is not sufficient to guarantee thermalization.
[
22
]

The
\left(\bigtriangledown\phi\right)^{4}
interaction is meant to both break integrability
[
23
]
and lead to thermalization,
although we have no proof it does this. It is marginally irrelevant at the QCP and, barring
\cos(n\phi)
terms only allowed
(but less relevant at
\kappa\ll 1
) in the compactified theory, it is the the leading perturbation to the scaling form of the action.
As with other irrelevant operators, its main effect is to shift the location of the QCP by inducing a renormalized stiffness,
the coefficient of the relevant operator
(\bigtriangledown\phi)^{2}
, whose renormalized value is tuned to zero at
T=0
(but
not
at
T>0
) as the
definition of the QCP. Thus, our approach consists of deforming
S_{0}
by the following:

\displaystyle S^{\prime}

\displaystyle=\int d\tau d^{2}x\left[r_{qc}\left(\bigtriangledown\phi\right)^{2}+\frac{u}{4}\left(\bigtriangledown\phi\right)^{4}\right]

(1.3)

where the stiffness
r_{qc}=r_{qc}(u)
is chosen such that we hit the QCP as
T\rightarrow 0
keeping the
bare value of
u
fixed. The field
\phi
is non-compact.

In order to perform a controlled calculation we choose to consider an
N
-flavor version of this model:

\displaystyle{\cal L}_{QLM,N}=\sum_{a}\frac{1}{2}\left[(\partial_{\tau}\phi_{a})^{2}+r_{qc}(\bigtriangledown\phi_{a})^{2}+\kappa(\bigtriangledown^{2}\phi_{a})^{2}\right]+\frac{u}{4N}\sum_{a,b}(\bigtriangledown\phi_{a})^{2}(\bigtriangledown\phi_{b})^{2}

(1.4)

Furthermore it is helpful to rewrite the
(\bigtriangledown\phi)^{4}
term by introducing an auxiliary field
\sigma
through a Hubbard-Stratonovich transformation:

Z_{E}=\int{\cal D}\phi e^{-S_{QLM,N}[\phi]}=\int{\cal D}\phi{\cal D}\sigma e^{-S_{N}[\phi,\sigma]}

(1.5)

In this form the (Euclidean) Lagrangian is

\displaystyle{\cal L}_{N}[\phi,\sigma]=\sum_{a}\frac{1}{2}\left[(\partial_{\tau}\phi_{a})^{2}+r_{qc}(\bigtriangledown\phi_{a})^{2}+\kappa(\bigtriangledown^{2}\phi_{a})^{2}\right]-\frac{\sigma^{2}}{4u}+\frac{\sigma}{2\sqrt{N}}\sum_{a}\left(\bigtriangledown\phi_{a}\right)^{2}

(1.6)

As discussed in Appendix
A
, at
N=\infty
we can solve the theory most naturally by introducting a new coupling:

r_{\rm eff}=\frac{\langle\sigma\rangle}{\sqrt{N}}+r_{qc}\quad\textrm{where}\quad r_{\rm eff}=\frac{uT}{4\pi\kappa}\frac{\ln\left(\frac{\sqrt{\kappa}T}{r_{\rm eff}}\right)}{1+\frac{u}{16\pi\kappa^{3/2}}\left[\ln\left(\frac{4\kappa\Lambda^{2}}{r_{\rm eff}}\right)\right]}

(1.7)

We note that the expectation value of the auxiliary field
\langle\sigma\rangle
induces a stiffness (“velocity”) for the
\phi
field (instead of a “thermal mass” as in
\phi^{4}
theory). We now discuss solutions of the equation above.

At fixed interaction strength
u
and sufficiently low temperatures the logarithm dominates the denominator and we obtain (see discussion around Eqn
1.12
)

\displaystyle r_{\rm eff}

\displaystyle\approx 4T\sqrt{\kappa}\frac{\ln\ln\left(\frac{\Lambda^{2}}{T\sqrt{\kappa}}\right)}{\ln\left(\frac{\Lambda^{2}}{T\sqrt{\kappa}}\right)}

(1.8)

which is consistent with our assumptions at low
T
. The marginality of the
(\bigtriangledown\phi)^{4}
term has induced log corrections that violate

scaling.
Note that

appears to be independent of the interaction strength

at low

\frac{u}{16\pi\kappa^{3/2}}\left[\ln\left(\frac{4\kappa\Lambda^{2}}{r_{\rm eff}}\right)\right]\gg 1
. The assumption we began with is clearly valid. We will call this the
low temperature regime
.

On the other hand at higher temperatures or small
u
the opposite is true

\displaystyle\frac{u}{16\pi\kappa^{3/2}}\left[\ln\left(\frac{4\kappa\Lambda^{2}}{r_{\rm eff}}\right)\right]\ll 1

(1.9)

and using similar methods we obtain a different saddle-point equation

\displaystyle r_{\rm eff}=\frac{uT}{4\pi\kappa}{\ln\left(\frac{\sqrt{\kappa}T}{r_{\rm eff}}\right)}\quad\textrm{with solution}\quad r_{\rm eff}=\sqrt{\kappa}T\left(\frac{u}{4\pi\kappa^{3/2}}\right)\ln\left(\frac{4\pi\kappa^{3/2}}{u}\right)

(1.10)

We call this the
weak interaction regime
. We will study both regimes simultaneously by definig
\alpha(u,T)=r_{\rm eff}(u,T)/T
. Since
\alpha
has very weak temperature dependence in both regimes, we will often treat
\alpha
as an indepedent parameter (in a wide but finite range of temperatures).

The
1/N
corrections are then captured by the following action (see
B
) where the
\lambda
field is the deviation from the saddle-point value of
\sigma

\lambda=\sigma-\langle\sigma\rangle_{N=\infty}

\displaystyle{\cal L}_{\phi,\lambda,N}=\sum_{a}\frac{1}{2}\left[(\partial_{\tau}\phi_{a})^{2}+r_{\rm eff}(\bigtriangledown\phi_{a})^{2}+\kappa(\bigtriangledown^{2}\phi_{a})^{2}\right]-\frac{\lambda^{2}}{4u}+\frac{\lambda}{2\sqrt{N}}\sum_{a}\left(\bigtriangledown\phi_{a}\right)^{2}

(1.11)

At leading order in

\phi_{a}

Therefore, the chaos (Lyapunov) exponent is at most of order

1/N

Computation of the OTOC and the Bethe-Salpeter Equation

Before we compute the OTOC,

this replacement achieves three things: it simplifies the expression, it captures the pole structure that leads to leading late-time dependence in
{\cal C}(t)
, and finally it also captures the scattering rate,
\Gamma_{\bm{p}}
, of the
\phi
fields (see discussion around Eqn.
C.2
).

Since the product of the two Green functions
{\cal G}_{R}
has an on-shell delta function condition, we choose the following ansatz

\displaystyle g(\nu;\omega,{\bm{p}})

\displaystyle=g(\nu;{\bm{p}})\delta(\omega^{2}-\epsilon_{\bm{p}}^{2})

(3.7)

This simplifies the Bethe-Salpeter equation
3.5
to the following

\displaystyle-i\nu\,g(\nu;{\bm{p}})

\displaystyle=\frac{\pi{\bm{p}}^{2}}{\epsilon_{\bm{p}}}+\frac{1}{N}\int_{{\bm{l}}}\left({\cal R}_{1}({\bm{l}},{\bm{p}})+{\cal R}_{2}({\bm{l}},{\bm{p}})-2N\Gamma_{\bm{p}}\,\delta^{(2)}({\bm{l}}-{\bm{p}})\right)g(\nu;{\bm{l}})

(3.8)

where we introduce auxiliary functions
{\cal R}_{1},{\cal R}_{2}
to compactify the expression. They are given by

\displaystyle{\cal R}_{1}({\bm{l}},{\bm{p}})

\displaystyle={\cal R}_{1,+}({\bm{l}},{\bm{p}})+{\cal R}_{1,-}({\bm{l}},{\bm{p}})

(3.9)

\displaystyle{\cal R}_{1,\pm}({\bm{l}},{\bm{p}})

\displaystyle=\frac{\left({\bm{l}}\cdot{\bm{p}}\right)^{2}}{4\epsilon_{\bm{l}}\epsilon_{\bm{p}}}{{\cal G}_{W,\lambda}(\pm\epsilon_{\bm{l}}-\epsilon_{\bm{p}},{\bm{l}}-{\bm{p}})}

(3.10)

\displaystyle{\cal R}_{2}({\bm{l}},{\bm{p}})

\displaystyle={\cal R}_{2,+}({\bm{l}},{\bm{p}})+{\cal R}_{2,-}({\bm{l}},{\bm{p}})

(3.11)

\displaystyle{\cal R}_{2,\pm}({\bm{l}},{\bm{p}})

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{4\epsilon_{\bm{l}}\epsilon_{\bm{p}}}{\cal G}_{\rm eff}\left(\pm\epsilon_{\bm{l}},\epsilon_{\bm{p}};{\bm{l}}-{\bm{p}},\frac{{\bm{l}}+{\bm{p}}}{2}\right)

(3.12)

Note that
{\cal R}_{2,\pm}({\bm{l}},{\bm{p}})
actually depends on
\nu,\kappa,u,T
in addition to
{\bm{l}},{\bm{p}}
. Similarly
{\cal R}_{1}
depends on
\kappa,u,T
. We convinced ourselves numerically that indeed
R_{1}({\bm{l}},{\bm{l}})
vanishes. Both
R_{1}({\bm{l}},{\bm{p}})
and
R_{2}({\bm{l}},{\bm{p}})
also vanish when either
{\bm{l}},{\bm{p}}\rightarrow 0
due to the factors of momentum in the numerator.

4
Lyapunov Exponent and Butterfly velocity

We note that
3.8
can be made dimensionless by the replacement
\nu\rightarrow\nu T,{\bm{p}}\rightarrow{\bm{p}}\sqrt{T},r_{\rm eff}(u,T)=\alpha(u,T)T
, with
[g]=[1/T]=2
. Then, by dimensional considerations and inspection of
r_{\rm eff}
, the Lyapunov exponent
\lambda_{L}
will take the form

\lambda_{L}=n(u,\alpha(u,T))\frac{T}{N}

(4.1)

where
n(u,\alpha(u,T))
is a dimensionless function with the following behaviors

•

In the
weak interaction regime

n(u,T)=n(u)
is temperature independent with
n(u=0)=0
(since at
u=0
the model becomes integrable).

•

In the
low temperature regime
,
n(u,T)
inherits a weak temperature dependence from
\alpha(T)
.

We solve the integral equation of Eq.
3.8
numerically. Given the eigenfunction
g_{\eta}(\nu;{\bm{p}})
of the right-hand-side integral
with eigenvalue
\eta
we obtain the following final form for
3.8
:

\displaystyle-i\nu\,g_{\eta}(\nu;{\bm{p}})

\displaystyle=\frac{\pi{\bm{p}}^{2}}{\epsilon_{\bm{p}}}+\frac{\eta}{N}g_{\eta}(\nu;{\bm{p}})

(4.2)

All the eigenvalues
\eta
we obtained numerically were complex; most had a negative real part which leads to exponential decay. We typically found only a handful of eigenvalues with a positive real part, and it is these that lead to exponential growth of chaotic correlations. An inverse Laplace transform
{\cal C}(t)=\int_{\nu}e^{-i\nu t}{\cal C}(\nu)
then gives

\displaystyle{\cal C}(t)\sim e^{\eta tT/N}

(4.3)

which allows us to identify the eigenvalue
\eta
as the dimensionless function
n(u,T)
introduced earlier in Eq.(
4.1
).

We remark that while maximal chaos is often associated with
the absence of “quasiparticles”
[
24
,
25
]
(so presumably large scattering rate
\Gamma_{p}
), in equation
3.8

the scattering rate appears to decrease the chaos exponent in an apparent contradiction. There is however no contradiction as
{\cal R}_{1}({\bm{l}},{\bm{p}})
which is present in Eq.
3.8
and contributes to chaos also contributes to
\Gamma_{\bm{p}}
(see Eq.
5.5
).

Despite its
z=2
scaling, the quantum Lifshitz critical point has apriori a velocity scale
\sqrt{\kappa\Lambda}
.
This is consistent with Lieb-Robinson type bounds that show the existence of a light cone even in non-relativistic systems.
[
26
]

This is explicitly a UV sensitive quantity. As we argued earlier a “thermal velocity”,
v=\sqrt{r(T)}\sim\sqrt{\alpha(u,T)T}
, is induced in our model close to the QCP.

There is growing evidence that OTOCs like ours propagate ballistically in systems that exhibit a non-zero chaos exponent.
[
27
,
28
]
.
Roberts and Swingle
[
29
]
have argued that the associated velocity is an effective Lieb-Robinson velocity in the IR,
and is UV insensitive (although this depends on the considered operators, see supplemental material of Ref
[
29
]
).
Multiple calculations support this conjecture
[
30
]
.

In the qLM we find numerical evidence that
C(t,{\bm{k}})
grows exponentially with an exponent

\lambda({\bm{k}})=\lambda_{L}-D_{L}{\bm{k}}^{2}+\cdots

(4.4)

where
\lambda_{L}
denotes the Lyapunov exponent.
With some additional assumptions, that will be spelled out later, it follows that

\displaystyle C(t,{\bm{x}})

\displaystyle\sim\int_{\bm{k}}e^{i{\bm{k}}\cdot{\bm{x}}}e^{\left(\lambda_{L}-D_{L}{\bm{k}}^{2}\right)t}\sim e^{\lambda_{L}t-\frac{{\bm{x}}^{2}}{4D_{L}t}}

(4.5)

and the wavefront propagates ballistically with (by definition) Butterfly velocity
v_{B}=\sqrt{4D_{L}\lambda_{L}}
.

Mirroring the discussion for
{\cal C}(t)
, for
{\bm{q}}\neq 0
we define the auxiliary function
g(\nu,{\bm{q}};\omega,{\bm{p}})

\displaystyle{\cal C}(\nu)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{N}\int\frac{d\omega}{2\pi}\int_{\bm{p}}{\bm{p}}\cdot({\bm{p}}-{\bm{q}})g(\nu,{\bm{q}};\omega,{\bm{p}})

(4.6)

and obtain the new Bethe-Salpeter equation for Type 1 ladders:

\displaystyle g(\nu,{\bm{q}};\omega,{\bm{p}})={\cal G}_{R}(\nu-\omega,{\bm{q}}-{\bm{p}}){\cal G}_{R}(\omega,{\bm{p}})

(4.7)

\displaystyle\times\left[{\bm{p}}\cdot({\bm{p}}-{\bm{q}})+\frac{1}{N}\int_{\omega^{\prime},{\bm{p}}^{\prime}}\left({\bm{p}}\cdot{\bm{p}}^{\prime}\right)({\bm{q}}-{\bm{p}})\cdot({\bm{q}}-{\bm{p}}^{\prime}){\cal G}_{W,\lambda}(\omega^{\prime}-\omega,{\bm{p}}^{\prime}-{\bm{p}})g(\nu,{\bm{q}};\omega^{\prime},{\bm{p}}^{\prime})\right]

The Type 2 rungs are included by shifting

\displaystyle\left({\bm{p}}\cdot{\bm{p}}^{\prime}\right)

\displaystyle({\bm{q}}-{\bm{p}})\cdot({\bm{q}}-{\bm{p}}^{\prime}){\cal G}_{W,\lambda}(\omega^{\prime}-\omega,{\bm{p}}^{\prime}-{\bm{p}})

(4.8)

\displaystyle\rightarrow\left({\bm{p}}\cdot{\bm{p}}^{\prime}\right)({\bm{q}}-{\bm{p}})\cdot({\bm{q}}-{\bm{p}}^{\prime}){\cal G}_{W,\lambda}(\omega^{\prime}-\omega,{\bm{p}}^{\prime}-{\bm{p}})+{\cal G}_{\rm eff}(\nu,{\bm{q}};\omega,\omega^{\prime},{\bm{p}},{\bm{p}}^{\prime})

where

\displaystyle{\cal G}_{\rm eff}(\nu,{\bm{q}};\omega,\omega^{\prime},{\bm{p}},{\bm{p}}^{\prime})=

\displaystyle\quad\int_{\omega^{\prime\prime},{\bm{p}}^{\prime\prime}}({\bm{q}}-{\bm{p}})\cdot({\bm{p}}^{\prime\prime}-{\bm{p}}){\bm{p}}\cdot({\bm{p}}^{\prime\prime}-{\bm{p}})({\bm{q}}-{\bm{p}}^{\prime})\cdot({\bm{p}}^{\prime}-{\bm{p}}^{\prime\prime})({\bm{p}}^{\prime}-{\bm{p}}^{\prime\prime})\cdot{\bm{p}}^{\prime}

\displaystyle\quad\times{\cal G}_{W}(\omega^{\prime\prime}-\omega,{\bm{p}}^{\prime\prime}-{\bm{p}}){\cal G}_{W}(\omega^{\prime}-\omega^{\prime\prime},{\bm{p}}^{\prime}-{\bm{p}}^{\prime\prime}){\cal G}_{R,\lambda}(\nu-\omega^{\prime\prime},{\bm{q}}-{\bm{p}}^{\prime\prime}){\cal G}_{R,\lambda}(\omega^{\prime\prime},{\bm{p}}^{\prime\prime})

(4.9)

A simplified expression for
{\cal G}_{\rm eff}(\nu,{\bm{q}};\omega,\omega^{\prime},{\bm{p}},{\bm{p}}^{\prime})
that is more amenable to numerical evaluation is provided in Eqn
C.3
.

Adopting the ansatz

\displaystyle g(\nu,{\bm{q}};\omega,{\bm{p}})

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2\epsilon_{\bm{p}}}\left[g_{+}({\bm{p}})\delta(\omega-\epsilon_{\bm{p}})+g_{-}({\bm{p}})\delta(\omega+\epsilon_{\bm{p}})\right]

(4.10)

we arrive at the following system of integral equations

\displaystyle\left(-i\nu+i\delta\epsilon_{\bm{q}}+2\Gamma_{\bm{p}}\right)g_{+}({\bm{p}})=

\displaystyle\frac{\pi}{\epsilon_{{\bm{q}}-{\bm{p}}}}{\bm{p}}\cdot({\bm{q}}-{\bm{p}})

(4.11)

\displaystyle+\frac{1}{N}\int_{{\bm{p}}^{\prime}}{\cal R}_{+}({\bm{p}}^{\prime},{\bm{p}})g_{+}({\bm{p}}^{\prime})+{\cal R}_{-}({\bm{p}}^{\prime},{\bm{p}})g_{-}({\bm{p}}^{\prime})

\displaystyle\left(-i\nu-i\delta\epsilon_{\bm{q}}+2\Gamma_{\bm{p}}\right)g_{-}({\bm{p}})=

\displaystyle\frac{\pi}{\epsilon_{{\bm{q}}-{\bm{p}}}}{\bm{p}}\cdot({\bm{q}}-{\bm{p}})

(4.12)

\displaystyle+\frac{1}{N}\int_{{\bm{p}}^{\prime}}{\cal R}_{-}({\bm{p}}^{\prime},{\bm{p}})g_{+}({\bm{p}}^{\prime})+{\cal R}_{+}({\bm{p}}^{\prime},{\bm{p}})g_{-}({\bm{p}}^{\prime})

The integral kernels are almost identical to the earlier versions:

\displaystyle{\cal R}_{\pm}(\nu,{\bm{q}};{\bm{p}}^{\prime},{\bm{p}})

\displaystyle=R_{1,\pm}(\nu,{\bm{q}};{\bm{p}}^{\prime},{\bm{p}})+R_{2,\pm}(\nu,{\bm{q}};{\bm{p}}^{\prime},{\bm{p}})

\displaystyle{\cal R}_{1,\pm}(\nu,{\bm{q}};{\bm{l}},{\bm{p}})

\displaystyle=\frac{({\bm{q}}-{\bm{l}})\cdot({\bm{q}}-{\bm{p}})\,\,{\bm{l}}\cdot{\bm{p}}}{4\epsilon_{\bm{l}}\epsilon_{\bm{p}}}{\cal G}_{W,\lambda}(\pm\epsilon_{\bm{l}}-\epsilon_{\bm{p}},{\bm{l}}-{\bm{p}})

(4.14)

\displaystyle{\cal R}_{2,\pm}(\nu,{\bm{q}};{\bm{l}},{\bm{p}})

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{4\epsilon_{\bm{l}}\epsilon_{\bm{p}}}{\cal G}_{\rm eff}\left(\nu,{\bm{q}};\pm\epsilon_{\bm{l}},\epsilon_{\bm{p}};{\bm{l}}-{\bm{p}},\frac{{\bm{l}}+{\bm{p}}}{2}\right)

We have made the following approximations in reaching the above expressions:
\epsilon_{{\bm{p}}^{\prime}}\epsilon_{{\bm{q}}-{\bm{p}}}\approx\epsilon_{{\bm{p}}^{\prime}}\epsilon_{\bm{p}}
,

\epsilon_{\bm{p}}-\epsilon_{{\bm{q}}-{\bm{p}}}\equiv\delta\epsilon_{\bm{q}}\approx{\bm{q}}\cdot v_{\bm{p}}
.
We also used the fact that the Wightman functions are even in frequency and rotationally invariant.
As noted in Ref
[
11
,
10
]
, Eq.
4.11
has the form of a kinetic theory Boltzmann equation.
This analogy inspires the ansatz that “particle” density at
-{\bm{p}}
is equals “hole” density at
{\bm{p}}
, that is
g_{+}(-{\bm{p}})=g_{-}({\bm{p}})
.
This finally allows us to decouple the two equations; we arrive at the following (dropping the homogeneous term) final expression:

\displaystyle\left(-i\nu+i{\bm{q}}\cdot v_{\bm{p}}\right)g_{+}({\bm{p}})=

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad\qquad\frac{1}{N}\int_{{\bm{p}}^{\prime}}\left[{\cal R}_{+}({\bm{p}}^{\prime},{\bm{p}})+{\cal R}_{-}(-{\bm{p}}^{\prime},{\bm{p}})-2N\Gamma_{{\bm{p}}}\delta^{(2)}({\bm{p}}^{\prime}-{\bm{p}})\right]g_{+}({\bm{p}}^{\prime})

(4.16)

Our identification of the Butterfly velocity following Eqn
4.5

of the equation above are weakly
{\bm{q}}
dependent.

5
Numerical Results

We now describe the solution of
3.8
and
4
. It is achieved by discretizing the momentum integral on the right hand side into a sum. This turns the integral kernel into a matrix and the function
g(\nu,{\bm{q}};{\bm{p}}^{\prime})
into a vector. We diagonalize this matrix. Using eigenfunctions we simplify to the form
4.2
(or similar for
{\bm{q}}\neq 0
). Finally an inverse Laplace transform takes us to real time.

The numerical calculations were carried out as described below for
\alpha=10^{-1},10^{-2},10^{-5}
:

•

We were able to eliminate the Dirac delta functions present in
\Im\,\Pi_{\lambda,R}(\nu,{\bm{q}})
by solving the quartic polynomial in the argument. This produced four integrals over two-dimensional momentum that nevertheless still vanish in some regions.

•

We computed
\Im\,\Pi_{\lambda,R}(\nu,{\bm{q}})
at fixed
{\bm{q}}
for many values of
\nu
, particularly densely around
\nu_{*}({\bm{q}})=\sqrt{\alpha{\bm{q}}^{2}+\kappa{\bm{q}}^{4}}
.

•

We rescaled the
\nu
coordinate of these slices by
\nu_{*}({\bm{q}})
and interpolated linearly between them (in the
{\bm{q}}
direction).

•

We performed the Kramers-Kronig transform as outlined in
D
.

•

We computed
A_{\lambda}
from
{\cal G}_{R,\lambda}
and from this obtained the
{\cal R}_{1}
functions.

•

To obtain
{\cal R}_{2}
we performed yet another momentum integral with the same delta function solution as earlier. This function depends on four independent real parameters (frequency, two momentum magnitudes, a relative angle) and therefore cannot be precomputed and stored for later fast lookup.

•

For the
{\bm{q}}=0
case we discretized the integral equation of Eq.
3.8
and turned it into an eigenvalue equation on an adaptively spaced grid of at least 60 x 60, or as fine as necessary to achieve grid spacing independence of the result.

•

For
{\bm{q}}\neq 0
due to the lack of rotational invariance of Eq.
4
we are forced to use the separate components of the momentum, and which lead to much smaller grid sizes around 25 x 25 (still leading to matrices of roughly 8000 x 8000).

The total duration of our calculations was several thousand CPU hours. All our computations were performed with
\kappa=1
. Furthermore, we chose to study OTO correlations only of current operators,
\bigtriangledown\phi
. The main result of this paper is the existence of a finite chaotic Lyapunov exponent for such operators; we express it in the form
\lambda_{L}=n(u,T)\frac{T}{N}
, where
n(u,T)
is a dimensionless function that is either weakly dependent on or completely independent of temperature. We estimate an error margin of 50% on our results for
n(u,T)
.
Recall that the ratio
\alpha=r_{\rm eff}(u,T)/T
is uniquely determined by
T,\kappa,u,\Lambda
. However, in light of its very weak dependence on temperature, we consider it an effective independent parameter in wide temperature ranges.

In the

\lambda_{L}
is weakly dependent on
\alpha
as it goes to zero, which corresponds to decreasing temperature. At large values of the interaction strength there does not appear to be a monotonic dependence of the Lyapunov exponent on

As expected chaos vanishes as

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\frac{-1}{2u}-\Pi}

This is the only place in the calculation where the interaction strength enters explicitly in this regime (the self-energy

An intuitive way to understand the saturation is to realize that as

6
Conclusions

We performed a perturbative calculation of a out-of-time-order correlator of current operators in the non-compact quantum Lifshitz model. We extracted the numerical values of the Lyapunov exponent and Butterfly velocity for a wide range of temperatures and/or interaction strengths.
Our results indicate that the
(\bigtriangledown\phi)^{4}
term is sufficient to generate chaos and is its dominant cause at sufficiently low energies. We observe that the Lyapunov exponent has a monotonic dependence on the interaction strength. In the
small temperature regime
it saturates at large values of interaction (which correspons to the non-linear sigma model version of the quantum Lifshitz). In the
weak interaction regime
the Lyapunov exponent vanishes as the interaction strength vanishes.
It is expected that for sufficiently generic interactions the Lyapunov exponent does not depend on the choice of operators in the OTO correlator. It would be interesting to verify this for our model, and for vertex operators in the compact version of qLM.

C.1
Crossed Diagrams

The first important observation comes from the Fourier space form of a Wightman correlator:

\displaystyle{\cal G}_{W}(\omega,{\bm{q}})=\frac{A(\omega,{\bm{q}})}{2\sinh\left(\frac{\beta\omega}{2}\right)}

(3.26)

We see that in real time,
{\cal G}_{W}(t,{\bm{r}})
has significant support only for
t\sim\beta^{-1}
.
Now consider a ladder diagram of type 1 with two rungs. Its value is, schematically:

\displaystyle\chi_{\rm ladder}

\displaystyle=\int s_{1}s_{2}s_{1}^{\prime}s_{2}^{\prime}{\cal G}_{R}(t-s_{1}){\cal G}_{R}(t-s_{1}^{\prime}){\cal G}_{R}(s_{1}-s_{2}){\cal G}_{R}(s_{1}^{\prime}-s_{2}^{\prime})

(3.27)

\displaystyle\times{\cal G}_{W,\lambda}(s_{1}-s_{1}^{\prime}){\cal G}_{W,\lambda}(s_{2}-s_{2}^{\prime})\ {\cal G}_{R}(s_{2}){\cal G}_{R}(s_{2}^{\prime})

The retarded Green functions impose, in particular,
\theta(s_{1}-s_{2})\theta(s_{1}^{\prime}-s_{2}^{\prime})\theta(s_{2})\theta(s_{2}^{\prime})
, while the Wightman function effectively imposes
s_{1}-s_{1}^{\prime}\approx 0,s_{2}-s_{2}^{\prime}\approx 0
. Putting them together the integration phase space is restricted to
\theta(s_{1}-s_{2})\theta(s_{2})
.

Now compare this with the case of a crossed diagram

\displaystyle\chi_{\rm crossed}

\displaystyle=\int s_{1}s_{2}s_{1}^{\prime}s_{2}^{\prime}{\cal G}_{R}(t-s_{1}){\cal G}_{R}(t-s_{1}^{\prime}){\cal G}_{R}(s_{1}-s_{2}){\cal G}_{R}(s_{1}^{\prime}-s_{2}^{\prime})

(3.28)

\displaystyle\times{\cal G}_{W,\lambda}(s_{1}-s_{2}^{\prime}){\cal G}_{W,\lambda}(s_{1}^{\prime}-s_{2})\ {\cal G}_{R}(s_{2}){\cal G}_{R}(s_{2}^{\prime})

where the constraints are
\theta(s_{1}-s_{2})\theta(s_{1}^{\prime}-s_{2}^{\prime})\theta(s_{2})\theta(s_{2}^{\prime})
, coming from the Green functions, and
s_{1}-s_{2}^{\prime}\approx 0,s_{2}-s_{1}^{\prime}\approx 0
from the Wightman function. Together they require
\theta(s_{1}-s_{2})\theta(s_{2}-s_{1})
, to within
\beta^{-1}
.

That is why we conclude that the crossed diagrams are parametrically suppressed compared to the ladder diagrams, a fact important at late times.
