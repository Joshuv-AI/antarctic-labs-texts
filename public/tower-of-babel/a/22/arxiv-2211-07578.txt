Classical shadow tomography for continuous variables quantum systems
Simon Becker, Nilanjana Datta, Ludovico Lami, Cambyse Rouzé

Abstract
In this article we develop a continuous variable (CV) shadow tomography scheme with wide ranging applications in quantum optics. Our work is motivated by the increasing experimental and technological relevance of CV systems in quantum information, quantum communication, quantum sensing, quantum simulations, quantum computing and error correction. We introduce two experimentally realisable schemes for obtaining classical shadows of CV (possibly non-Gaussian) quantum states using only randomised Gaussian unitaries and easily implementable Gaussian measurements such as homodyne and heterodyne detection. For both schemes, we show that N = 𝒪 ⁡ ( poly ⁡ ( 1 ϵ , log ⁡ ( 1 δ ) , M n r + α , log ⁡ ( m ) ) ) N=\mathcal{O}\big(\operatorname{poly}\big(\frac{1}{\epsilon},\log\big(\frac{1}{\delta}\big),M_{n}^{r+\alpha},\log(m)\big)\big) samples of an unknown m m -mode state ρ \rho suffice to learn the expected value of any r r -local polynomial in the canonical observables of degree α \alpha , both with high probability 1 − δ 1-\delta and accuracy ϵ \epsilon , as long as the state ρ \rho has moments of order n > α n>\alpha bounded by M n M_{n} . By simultaneously truncating states and operators in energy and phase space, we are able to overcome new mathematical challenges that arise due to the infinite-dimensionality of CV systems. We also provide a scheme to learn nonlinear functionals of the state, such as entropies over any small number of modes, by leveraging recent energy-constrained entropic continuity bounds. Finally, we provide numerical evidence of the efficiency of our protocols in the case of CV states of relevance in quantum information theory, including ground states of quadratic Hamiltonians of many-body systems and cat qubit states. We expect our scheme to provide good recovery in learning relevant states of 2D materials and photonic crystals.



I
Introduction

Obtaining classical descriptions of states of quantum-mechanical systems is a fundamental ingredient of quantum computing. It is useful for storing and transmitting quantum information and essential for verification and benchmarking of quantum devices.
However, the underlying quantum nature of such systems provides a huge hurdle in obtaining such a classical description: to learn anything about a quantum state one needs to measure it, but measurements in quantum mechanics are inherently destructive and furthermore probabilistic, entailing that individual measurement outcomes only give limited information about the state of the system. Consequently, in order to obtain a classical representation of a quantum state, one requires multiple identical copies of the state on which successive, appropriate (possibly adaptive) single-copy measurements can be performed.

This is what is done in the traditional method of learning an unknown quantum state, known as
quantum state tomography
. It is the process of inferring a quantum state by using suitable measurements on many identical copies of the state. There is, however, a huge practical limitation in using quantum state tomography for many-body quantum systems. This is due to the so-called “curse of dimensionality”: the number of parameters needed to fully specify the state of a quantum-mechanical system grows exponentially with the system size. The exponential number of measurements needed to infer these parameters makes quantum state tomography infeasible for large systems. Consequently, full quantum state tomography has only been realised in systems with few components, in particular, in a system of ten qubits, which too required millions of measurements. In a nutshell, obtaining a classical description of a
d
-dimensional quantum mixed state
\rho
, given many copies of it, via quantum state tomography, can be shown to require
\Omega(d^{2})
copies of the state. (More recently, it has been shown
[
1
,
2
]
that
\mathcal{O}(d^{2})
copies also suffice.) However, this number grows exponentially with the number
n
of qubits (since
d=2^{n}
), and the problem becomes rapidly intractable.

In 2018 Aaronson
[
3
]
pointed out that for certain concrete tasks, obtaining a complete classical characterisation of the quantum state is unnecessary. Instead it is often sufficient to accurately predict many useful properties of the state. This led him to propose a novel task called
shadow tomography
, the aim of which is not to learn a complete description of the unknown quantum state but instead to simultaneously estimate the outcome probabilities associated with a list of
M
two-outcome measurements,
E_{1},\ldots,E_{M}
, performed on the state, up to a desired accuracy (say,
\varepsilon
). For an unknown
d
-dimensional quantum mixed state
\rho
, this requires the prediction of
M
expectation values,
\Tr[E_{i}\rho]

i=1,2,\ldots,M
, to within an additive error
\varepsilon

sample size
) needed to make these predictions, scales
polynomially
in the system size. Moreover, to predict the
M
different expectation values, only
\mathcal{O}({\rm{polylog}}(M))
number of copies of the state are needed. In spite of this advantage with respect to the sample size, implementation of shadow tomography is impractical because it requires very expensive
quantum processing
, including exponentially long quantum circuits that act collectively across all copies of the unknown quantum state stored in a quantum memory, as well as a lot of storage and post-processing to make the desired predictions. Aaronson’s work was followed by a spate of papers, with the best result on the sample complexity of shadow tomography being obtained in
[

In 2020 Huang et al.

I-A

CV classical shadow tomography

Let us start with a brief summary of our extension of the protocol of Huang et al.
[
6
]
for obtaining a classical shadow of the quantum state of a continuous variables quantum system. Assume that multiple (say,
N
) identical copies of an unknown quantum state,
\rho
, are available, and one has (i) an ensemble
\cal{U}
of unitary operators and (ii) a quantum measurement described by the set of measurement operators
\{M_{x}\}_{x}
, satisfying
\sum_{x}M_{x}^{\dagger}M_{x}=I
, such that elements
M_{x}U
,

, describe a tomographically complete set of measurements. In Huang et al.
[
6
]

\rho
was considered to be an
n

was a measurement in the computational basis, in which case

. In a practical scheme, each ensemble

should be realisable as an efficient quantum circuit, and also have a succinct classical description.

In analogy with the discrete setting, our proposal for a
CV classical shadow tomography
contains the following three main ingredients:

1.

An
m
-mode CV system in an unknown state
\rho
.

2.

A random variable
S

\operatorname{Sp}(2m)
of
2m\times 2m

3.

A Gaussian positive operator valued measurement
\big\{\pazocal{D}(x)\psi\pazocal{D}(-x)\,\frac{d^{2m}x}{(2\pi)}\big\}_{x\in\mathbb{R}^{2m}}
, where

denotes the unitary displacement operator of phase-space parameter
x\in\mathbb{R}^{2m}
. In practice, we will consider two types of Gaussian measurements, namely homodyne detection along a random direction in phase space and heterodyne detection.

A
CV classical shadow
of the quantum state
\rho
is then created by using a randomised protocol that involves repeatedly performing the following simple steps:

1.

A symplectic matrix
S
is selected randomly from
\operatorname{Sp}(2m)
and applied to a copy of
\rho
, resulting in the unitary transformation
\rho\mapsto U_{S}\rho U_{S}^{\dagger}
.

2.

The Gaussian measurement of effect operators
M_{x}
is performed on the output of the previous step, yielding the post-measurement state
\widetilde{\rho}_{x}=\psi_{x}\coloneqq\pazocal{D}(x)\psi\pazocal{D}(-x)

\widetilde{\rho}_{x}
, implementing the transformation
\widetilde{\rho}_{x}\mapsto U_{S}^{\dagger}\widetilde{\rho}_{x}U_{S}
. Naturally, due to the measurement such transformation will not yield back the original state

3.

Averaging over
S,X
, where
X=\mathbb{R}^{2m}
is the set of measurement outcomes equipped the probability measure derived from the measurement outcomes, the counter-rotated state yields a quantum channel
\mathcal{M}
that depends on the measure
\mu

\displaystyle\hskip 0.0pt\mathcal{M}(\rho)\coloneqq{\mathbb{E}}_{S,X}\left[U_{\!S}^{{\dagger}}\,\widetilde{\rho}_{X}U_{\!S}^{\vphantom{{\dagger}}}\right].

(1)

For our choice of Gaussian measurements, the effective quantum channel
\mathcal{M}
is a simple linear bosonic channel whose action can be represented as a random displacement in phase space.

4.

Heuristically, one would like to define the
classical shadow
of
\rho
as the random operator

\displaystyle\hskip 0.0pt\hat{\rho}_{S,x}\coloneqq

\displaystyle\mathcal{M}^{-1}\!\left(U_{\!S}^{{\dagger}}\widetilde{\rho}_{x}U_{\!S}^{\vphantom{{\dagger}}}\right).

(2)

Once the nature of the protocol, and in particular the Gaussian measurement
\big\{\pazocal{D}(x)\psi\pazocal{D}(-x)\,\frac{d^{2m}x}{(2\pi)}\big\}_{x\in\mathbb{R}^{2m}}
, has been specified, the classical shadow is simply a function of the particular realisations

obtained in this round of the protocol. Importantly, it is
not
a function of the unknown state
\rho
. The information on
\rho
is at this point stored

in the probability distribution associated with the measurement outcome
x
. As a matter of fact,
\hat{\rho}_{S,x}
is an unbiased estimator of
\rho

\displaystyle\hskip 0.0pt\mathbb{E}_{S,X}\big[\hat{\rho}_{S,X}\big]

\displaystyle=\mathcal{M}^{-1}\left(\mathbb{E}_{S,X}\big[U_{\!S}^{{\dagger}}\,\widetilde{\rho}_{X}U_{\!S}^{\vphantom{{\dagger}}}\big]\right)

(3)

\displaystyle=\big(\mathcal{M}^{-1}\!\!\circ\!\mathcal{M}\big)(\rho)=\rho\,.

This is the final output of one iteration of the protocol. A purely classical description of the operator
\hat{\rho}_{S,x}
— which, we stress again, does not depend on the unknown state

Let us summarise the whole procedure. The measurement yields

Moment constraints

When trying to implement the above strategy, one faces however two issues that are specific to the continuous variables setting. First, as we will see, the quantum channel
\mathcal{M}
is in general not surjective on the space of trace class operators, which implies that the classical shadow
\hat{\rho}_{S,X}
will typically not be a trace class — and, for that matter, not even a bounded — operator. In other words, the right-hand side of (
6

\mathds{R}^{2m}
, is not the characteristic function of a quantum state. Second, the parameters
S
and
X
that need to be stored in the classical memory at each round of the protocol belong to continuous and unbounded sets.

To overcome the first problem, we focus on a suitable characterisation of the classical shadow
\hat{\rho}_{S,X}
that is well defined, namely, its characteristic function
\chi_{\hat{\rho}_{S,X}}
, and construct the operator
\hat{\rho}_{S,X}
itself as defined only on a restricted domain. To obtain effective estimates of
\rho
from the above scheme, we will further need to assume that
\rho
has controlled moments of low degree. Such an assumption allows us to show that the projection of the state
\rho
onto the finite subspace of Hilbert–Schmidt operators supported on the space of low energy Fock states is sufficient for obtaining a good enough approximation of the state via the Gaussian shadow tomography protocol.

Let us make these considerations more precise. We consider the maps

0\leq\alpha<n
and assuming that
E^{(n)}_{r}\coloneqq\max_{|A|\leq r}\tr(\rho_{A}H_{r}^{n})<\infty
for all
A
of size
|A|\leq r
, we have that for
N=\mathcal{O}\Big(\operatorname{poly}\Big(\frac{1}{\epsilon^{2}},\,(E_{r}^{(n)})^{r+\alpha},\log\big(\frac{1}{\delta}\big),\log(m)\Big)\Big)
and any region
A
of size
|A|\leq r
,

\displaystyle\|\widetilde{\sigma}^{(N)}_{A}(M)-\rho_{A}\|^{(\alpha)}_{1}\leq\epsilon

with probability at least
1-\delta
. Similarly, for
N=\mathcal{O}\Big(\operatorname{poly}\Big(\frac{1}{\epsilon^{2}},\,(E_{r}^{(n)})^{r+\alpha},\log\big(\frac{1}{\delta}\big),\log(L)\Big)\Big)
we have that for any set of
L
observables
O_{j}
on regions
A_{j}
of size at most
r
and with
\|H_{r}^{-\frac{\alpha}{2}}O_{j}H_{r}^{-\frac{\alpha}{2}}\|_{\infty}\leq 1
,

\displaystyle\max_{j}\big|\tr[O_{j}\,(\widetilde{\sigma}_{A}^{(N)}(M)-\rho_{A_{j}})]\big|\leq\epsilon

with probability at least
1-\delta
.

III
Notation and basic notions

III-A

Operators and norms

Given a separable Hilbert space
{\pazocal{H}}
, we denote by
{\pazocal{B}}({\pazocal{H}})
the space of bounded linear operators on
{\pazocal{H}}
, and by
{\pazocal{T}}_{p}({\pazocal{H}})
the
Schatten
p
-class
, which is the Banach subspace of
{\pazocal{B}}({\pazocal{H}})
formed by all bounded linear operators whose Schatten
p
-norm, defined as
\|X\|_{p}=\left(\Tr|X|^{p}\right)^{1/p}
, is finite. Henceforth, we refer to
{\pazocal{T}}_{1}({\pazocal{H}})
as the set of
trace class
operators. The set of quantum states (or density matrices), i.e. positive semi-definite operators
\rho\in{\pazocal{T}}_{1}({\pazocal{H}})
of unit trace, is denoted by
{\pazocal{D}}({\pazocal{H}})
. The Schatten
1
-norm,
\|\cdot\|_{1}
, is the trace norm, and the corresponding induced distance (e.g. between quantum states) is the trace distance. Note that the Schatten
2
-norm,
\|\cdot\|_{2}
, coincides with the
Hilbert–Schmidt norm
. We also recall that a quantum channel with input system
A
and output system
B
is any completely positive, trace-preserving (CPTP) linear map
\mathcal{N}:{\pazocal{T}}_{1}({\pazocal{H}}_{A})\to{\pazocal{T}}_{1}({\pazocal{H}}_{B})
, where
{\pazocal{H}}_{A},{\pazocal{H}}_{B}
are the Hilbert spaces corresponding to
A,B
, respectively.

If
\rho
is a quantum state with spectral decomposition
\rho=\sum_{i}p_{i}\ket{\phi_{i}}\!\!\bra{\phi_{i}}
, and
A
is a positive semi-definite operator, the
expected value
of
A
on
\rho
is defined as

\displaystyle\hskip 0.0pt\Tr[\rho A]\coloneqq\sum_{i:\,p_{i}>0}p_{i}\big\|A^{1/2}|{\phi_{i}}\rangle\big\|^{2}\in\mathbb{R}_{+}\cup\{+\infty\}\,;

(12)

here we use the convention that
\Tr[\rho A]=+\infty
if the above series diverges or if there exists an index
i
for which
p_{i}>0
and
\ket{\phi_{i}}\notin\dom\left(A^{1/2}\right)
. This definition can be extended to a generic densely defined self-adjoint operator
A
on
{\pazocal{H}}
, by considering its decomposition
A=A_{+}-A_{-}
into positive and negative parts, with
A_{\pm}
being positive semi-definite operators with mutually orthogonal supports. The operator
A
is said to have a
finite expected value on
\rho
if (i)
\ket{\phi_{i}}\in\dom\big(A_{+}^{1/2}\big)\cap\dom\big(A_{-}^{1/2}\big)
for all
i
for which
p_{i}>0
, and (ii) the two series
\sum_{i}p_{i}\big\|A_{\pm}^{1/2}\ket{\phi_{i}}\big\|^{2}
both converge. In this case, the following quantity is called the
expected value
of
A
on
\rho
:

\displaystyle\hskip 0.0pt\Tr[\rho A]\coloneqq\sum_{i:\,p_{i}>0}p_{i}\big\|A_{+}^{1/2}\ket{\phi_{i}}\big\|^{2}-\sum_{i:\,p_{i}>0}p_{i}\big\|A_{-}^{1/2}\ket{\phi_{i}}\big\|^{2}\,.

(13)

Obviously, for a pair of operators
A,B
satisfying
A\geq B
, we have that
\Tr[\rho A]\geq\Tr[\rho B]
.

Let
A
be an (unbounded) operator
A
on some Banach space
{{X}}
, with domain
\dom(A)
. Such an operator is called closed if its
graph
, that is
\left\{(\ket{x},A\ket{x});\ket{x}\in\dom(A)\right\}\subset X\times X,
is closed. The spectrum of a closed operator
A
is defined as the set
[
65
, Definition 9.16]

\spec(A)\coloneqq\left\{\lambda\in\mathbb{C}:\,\lambda I-A\text{ is not continuously invertible}\right\}.

Henceforth, we often suppress the identity operator
I
in the expression
(\lambda I-A)
for notational simplicity.
We remind the reader that the spectrum of a self-adjoint positive operator is a closed subset of the positive real half-line
[
65
, Proposition 9.20]
. Given a possibly unbounded operator
X
,
L_{X}
stands for the left multiplication by
X
:
L_{X}(Y)=XY
, whereas
R_{X}
stands for right multiplication by
X
:
R_{X}(Y)=YX
, whenever these products are well-defined.

III-B

Continuous variable quantum systems

A CV system with
m
modes is defined on the Hilbert space
{\pazocal{H}}_{m}\coloneqq L^{2}(\mathds{R}^{m})
, equipped with the multi-mode Fock basis
\{|\mathbf{n}\rangle\}_{\mathbf{n}\in\mathbb{N}^{m}}
of eigenvectors of the number operator
N_{m}
:

\displaystyle N_{m}|\mathbf{n}\rangle=\Big(\sum\nolimits_{j=1}^{m}n_{j}\Big)\,|\mathbf{n}\rangle\,.

(14)

We denote the canonical operators on each mode as
X_{j},P_{j}
(
j=1,\ldots,m
). Define the formal vector

\displaystyle\hskip 0.0ptR\coloneqq\begin{pmatrix}X_{1},\ldots,X_{n},P_{1},\ldots,P_{n}\end{pmatrix}^{\intercal}

and the symplectic form

\displaystyle\hskip 0.0pt\Omega\coloneqq\begin{pmatrix}0&\mathds{1}\\
-\mathds{1}&0\end{pmatrix}

(15)

(where all blocks are
m\times m
matrices), in terms of which the canonical commutation relations read (at least when evaluated on Schwartz functions)

\displaystyle\hskip 0.0pt[R_{j},R_{k}]=i\Omega_{jk}\,.

(16)

We also introduce also the annihilation and creation operators
a_{j}^{\dagger},a_{j}
(
j=1,\ldots,m
), defined by

\displaystyle\hskip 0.0pta_{j}\coloneqq\frac{X_{j}+iP_{j}}{\sqrt{2}}\,,\qquad a_{j}^{\dagger}\coloneqq\frac{X_{j}-iP_{j}}{\sqrt{2}}\,.

(17)

In terms of these operators, the single-mode Fock states can be constructed as

\displaystyle\hskip 0.0pt\ket{n}\coloneqq\frac{(a^{\dagger})^{n}}{\sqrt{n!}}\ket{0}\,,

(18)

with similar formulae holding for the multi-mode case. The canonical commutation relations can also be written as
[a_{j},a_{k}^{\dagger}]=\delta_{jk}
.

With a slight abuse of notation, we will often denote by the same symbol
\Omega
the symplectic form for different sets of modes. The quantum covariance matrix
V[\rho]
and mean vector
t[\rho]
associated with a generic state
\rho
are defined by

\displaystyle\hskip 0.0ptt[\rho]_{j}\coloneqq\Tr\left(\rho R_{j}\right)\,,\quad V[\rho]_{jk}\coloneqq\Tr\left(\rho\left\{R_{j}-t_{j},\,R_{k}-t_{k}\right\}\right)\,,

(19)

provided that these expressions are well defined. For an arbitrary
x\in\mathds{R}^{2m}
, we define the associated displacement operator by

\displaystyle\hskip 0.0pt\pazocal{D}(x)\coloneqq e^{-ix^{\intercal}\Omega R}\,.

(20)

Note that
\pazocal{D}(x)^{\dagger}=\pazocal{D}(x)^{-1}=\pazocal{D}(-x)
. By writing
x=x^{\prime}\oplus x^{\prime\prime}
, where
x^{\prime}\in\mathds{R}^{m}
groups together the first
m
components of
x
and
x^{\prime\prime}\in\mathds{R}^{m}
the last
m
, one can also introduce the complex vector

\displaystyle\hskip 0.0pt\alpha(x)\coloneqq\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}\left(x^{\prime}+ix^{\prime\prime}\right),

(21)

in terms of which we have that
[
66
, Eq. (3.3.30)–(3.3.31)]

\displaystyle\pazocal{D}(x)

\displaystyle=\exp\left[\sum\nolimits_{j=1}^{m}\left(\alpha_{j}(x)a_{j}^{\dagger}-\alpha_{j}(x)^{*}a_{j}\right)\right]

(22)

\displaystyle=e^{-\frac{1}{4}\|x\|^{2}}e^{\sum_{j}\alpha_{j}(x)a_{j}^{\dagger}}e^{-\sum_{j}\alpha_{j}(x)^{*}a_{j}},

(23)

where
\|x\|^{2}\coloneqq\sum_{j}x_{j}^{2}
. In dealing with continuous variable systems, one can stick to the real notation, employing real vectors
x
, or move to the complex one, which uses the complex vectors
\alpha(x)
. In this paper we will mostly follow the former convention; however, it will be useful, occasionally, to use the latter too. In general, a prompt translation between one set of conventions and the other can be obtained by means of (
21
).

Another important identity involving displacement operators is

\displaystyle\hskip 0.0pt\int\frac{d^{2m}x}{(2\pi)^{m}}\,\pazocal{D}(x)Z\pazocal{D}(-x)=I\Tr Z\,,

(24)

valid for all trace class operators
Z\in{\pazocal{T}}_{1}({\pazocal{H}}_{m})
, with the integral on the left-hand side converging in the weak sense (see
[
67
, Proposition 3.5.1]
). Using displacement operators, we can re-write (
16
) in Weyl form as

\displaystyle\hskip 0.0pt\pazocal{D}(x+y)=e^{\frac{i}{2}x^{\intercal}\Omega y}\,\pazocal{D}(x)\pazocal{D}(y)\,.

(25)

Coherent states are instead defined as

\displaystyle\hskip 0.0pt\ket{x}\coloneqq\pazocal{D}(x)\ket{0}\,,

(26)

where
\ket{0}
is the vacuum state. We can decompose
\ket{x}
mode-wise as
\ket{x}=\bigotimes_{j=1}^{m}\ket{x^{(j)}}
, where
x^{(j)}\coloneqq(x_{j},x_{m+j})^{\intercal}\in\mathds{R}^{2}
is the sub-vector of
x
obtained by picking the coordinates corresponding to the
j^{\text{th}}
coordinate and momentum, and
\ket{x^{(j)}}
is a single-mode coherent state. In general, the latter can be represented in the single-mode Fock basis according to the identity

\displaystyle\hskip 0.0pt\ket{y}

\displaystyle=e^{-\|y\|^{2}/4}\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\frac{(y_{1}+iy_{2})^{n}}{\sqrt{2^{n}\,n!}}\ket{n}

(27)

\displaystyle=e^{-\|y\|^{2}/4}\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\frac{\alpha(y)^{n}}{\sqrt{n!}}\ket{n}\,,

where
\alpha(y)\in\mathds{C}
is defined in (
21
).

For an arbitrary trace class operator
Z
, we can construct its characteristic function
\chi_{Z}:\mathds{R}^{2m}\to\mathds{C}
by

\displaystyle\hskip 0.0pt\chi_{Z}(x)\coloneqq\Tr\big[Z\,\pazocal{D}(x)\big]\,.

(28)

Characteristic functions are always bounded and furthermore continuous, because of the strong operator continuity of the mapping
x\mapsto\pazocal{D}(x)
. Moreover, in the sense of weak operator convergence it holds that

\displaystyle\hskip 0.0ptZ=\int\frac{d^{2m}x}{(2\pi)^{m}}\,\chi_{Z}(x)\,\pazocal{D}(-x)\,.

(29)

By applying (
25
) and (
27
), one can prove that

\displaystyle

\displaystyle\chi_{\ket{x}\!\bra{y}}(u)

(30)

\displaystyle=\tr[\ket{x}\!\bra{y}\pazocal{D}(u)]

\displaystyle=\braket{y|\pazocal{D}(u)|x}

\displaystyle=\braket{0|\pazocal{D}(-y)\pazocal{D}(u)\pazocal{D}(x)|0}

\displaystyle=e^{-\frac{i}{2}u^{\intercal}\Omega x}\braket{0|\pazocal{D}(-y)\pazocal{D}(u+x)|0}

\displaystyle=e^{-\frac{i}{2}u^{\intercal}\Omega x}e^{\frac{i}{2}y^{\intercal}\Omega(u+x)}\braket{0|\pazocal{D}(u+x-y)|0}

\displaystyle=e^{-\frac{i}{2}u^{\intercal}\Omega x}e^{\frac{i}{2}y^{\intercal}\Omega(u+x)}e^{-\frac{1}{4}\|u+x-y\|^{2}}.

As special cases, we conclude e.g. that

\displaystyle\hskip 0.0pt\chi_{\ket{x}\!\bra{x}}(u)

\displaystyle=e^{-\frac{1}{4}\|u\|^{2}-iu^{\intercal}\Omega x},

(31)

\displaystyle\chi_{\ket{-x}\!\bra{x}}(u)

\displaystyle=e^{-\frac{1}{4}\|u-2x\|^{2}},

\displaystyle\chi_{\ket{x}\!\bra{-x}}(u)

\displaystyle=e^{-\frac{1}{4}\|u+2x\|^{2}}.

Given two single-mode Fock states
|k\rangle,|j\rangle

[
68
]
and
x=x^{\prime}\oplus x^{\prime\prime}
:

\displaystyle\chi_{|k\rangle\langle j|}(x)=\left\{\begin{aligned} &\sqrt{\frac{k!}{j!}}e^{-\frac{\pi}{2}|\omega|^{2}}(\sqrt{\pi}\omega)^{j-k}L_{k}^{(j-k)}(\pi|\omega|^{2}),~~j\geq k\\
&\sqrt{\frac{k!}{j!}}e^{-\frac{\pi}{2}|\omega|^{2}}(-\sqrt{\pi}\omega)^{k-j}L_{j}^{(k-j)}(\pi|\omega|^{2}),~~j\leq k\,,\end{aligned}\right.

(32)

where
\omega\coloneqq-x^{\prime}+i\frac{x^{\prime\prime}}{2\pi}
. Above, the functions
L_{k}^{(j)}
are the Laguerre polynomials, defined for any two integers
k,j
as

\displaystyle L_{k}^{(j)}(x)\coloneqq\sum_{l=0}^{k}\frac{(k+j)!}{(k-l)!(j+l)!}\,\frac{(-x)^{l}}{l!}\,.

(33)

Interestingly, the correspondence between trace class operators and characteristic functions is injective — even more strikingly, it can be extended to an isometry between the space of Hilbert–Schmidt operators and that of square integrable functions
\mathds{R}^{2m}\to\mathds{C}

[
67
, Theorem 5.3.3]
. A consequence of the existence of this isometry is the quantum Plancherel theorem, which tells us that for any two trace class operators
Z,Z^{\prime}\in{\pazocal{T}}_{1}({\pazocal{H}}_{m})
,

\displaystyle\hskip 0.0pt\Tr\big[Z^{\dagger}Z^{\prime}\big]=\int\frac{d^{2m}x}{(2\pi)^{m}}\,\chi_{Z}(x)^{*}\chi_{Z^{\prime}}(x)\,.

(34)

The canonical commutation relations are invariant under so-called symplectic unitaries, constructed as follows. A
2m\times 2m
real matrix
S
such that
S\Omega S^{\intercal}=\Omega
(or equivalently
S\Omega=\Omega S^{-\intercal}
) is called a symplectic matrix. From the defining relation it can be immediately seen that any symplectic matrix must satisfy
\det S=\pm 1
; however, remarkably, it turns out that in fact all symplectic matrices have determinant
1
. To any symplectic matrix we can associate a symplectic unitary
U_{S}
acting on
{\pazocal{H}}_{m}
. This is defined by either of the following relations

\displaystyle\hskip 0.0ptU_{S}^{\dagger}RU_{S}^{\phantom{{\dagger}}}=SR\,,\qquad U_{S}^{\phantom{{\dagger}}}\pazocal{D}(x)U_{S}^{\dagger}=\pazocal{D}(Sx)\,,

(35)

where the first identity is to be understood coordinatewise:
(SR)_{j}=\sum_{k}S_{jk}R_{k}
. Note that symplectic matrices form a group, denoted as
\operatorname{Sp}(2m)
, and that the correspondence
S\mapsto U_{S}
is a group homomorphism.
In particular,

\displaystyle\hskip 0.0ptU_{S}^{\dagger}=U_{S}^{-1}=U_{S^{-1}}=U_{\Omega\,S^{\text{\raisebox{0.6pt}{$\intercal$}}}\Omega^{\text{\raisebox{0.6pt}{$\intercal$}}}}.

(36)

Also, from (
35
) we deduce that

\displaystyle\hskip 0.0ptt\left[U_{S}^{\phantom{{\dagger}}}\rho\,U_{S}^{\dagger}\right]=S\,t[\rho]\,,\qquad V\left[U_{S}^{\phantom{{\dagger}}}\rho\,U_{S}^{\dagger}\right]=S\,V[\rho]S^{\intercal}\,,

(37)

where we recall that
t[\omega]
and
V[\omega]
denote the mean vector and quantum covariance matrix of the state
\omega
as defined in (
19
). A generic Gaussian unitary is obtained as the product between a symplectic unitary and a displacement operator. States obtained by applying an arbitrary Gaussian unitary to the vacuum state
\ket{0}=\bigotimes_{j=1}^{m}\ket{0}_{j}
are called pure Gaussian states. Often times, displacements can be ignored; we will thus write an arbitrary pure Gaussian state with zero mean as

\displaystyle\hskip 0.0pt\ket{\psi}=U_{S}\ket{0}\,,

(38)

where
S\in\operatorname{Sp}(2m)
is an arbitrary symplectic matrix.

A quantum channel that will be particularly useful to us is the Gaussian white noise channel, defined for
\lambda>0
by

\displaystyle\hskip 0.0pt\mathcal{N}_{\lambda}(\cdot)\coloneqq\int\frac{d^{2m}x}{(2\pi\lambda)^{m}}\,e^{-\frac{\|x\|^{2}}{2\lambda}}\pazocal{D}(x)(\cdot)\pazocal{D}(-x)\,.

(39)

Using this formula one can show that

\displaystyle\hskip 0.0pt\mathcal{N}_{\lambda}:\chi_{Z}\longmapsto\chi_{\mathcal{N}_{\lambda}(Z)}(x)\coloneqq\chi_{Z}(x)\,e^{-\frac{\lambda}{2}\|x\|^{2}}\,.

(40)

Curiously, for
\lambda\in(0,1]
its action can be expressed alternatively as

\displaystyle\hskip 0.0pt\mathcal{N}_{\lambda}(\cdot)=\int\!\!\frac{d^{2m}x}{(2\pi\lambda)^{m}}\,\pazocal{D}(x)\,\tau_{\!\frac{1}{2\lambda}-\frac{1}{2}}^{\otimes m}\pazocal{D}(x)^{\dagger}(\cdot)\pazocal{D}(x)\,\tau_{\!\frac{1}{2\lambda}-\frac{1}{2}}^{\otimes m}\pazocal{D}(x)^{\dagger}\!,

(41)

where
\tau_{\nu}
is the single-mode thermal state with mean photon number
\nu
, given by

\displaystyle\hskip 0.0pt\tau_{\nu}=\frac{1}{\nu+1}\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\left(\frac{\nu}{\nu+1}\right)^{n}\ket{n}\!\!\bra{n}\,,

(42)

where
\ket{n}
stands for the
n^{\text{th}}
Fock state. Since we could not locate a complete proof of (
41
) in the existing literature, we provide a self-contained one in Appendix
A
. A special case of (
41
) is when
\lambda=1
, in which case
\tau_{\frac{1}{2\lambda}-\frac{1}{2}}=\ket{0}\!\!\bra{0}
(the vacuum state) and

\displaystyle\hskip 0.0pt\mathcal{N}_{1}(\cdot)=\int\frac{d^{2m}x}{(2\pi)^{m}}\,\ket{x}\!\!\bra{x}(\cdot)\ket{x}\!\!\bra{x}\,.

(43)

In terms of the real mean vector and quantum covariance matrix, for all
\lambda>0
we have that

\displaystyle\mathcal{N}_{\lambda}:\left\{\begin{array}[]{lll}V&\longmapsto&V+2\lambda I\,,\\
t&\longmapsto&t\,.\end{array}\right.

The above channel,
\mathcal{N}_{\lambda}
, is just an example within the larger class of Gaussian channels. To construct the most general Gaussian channel, take two arbitrary
2m\times 2m
real matrices
X
and
Y
such that

\displaystyle\hskip 0.0ptY+i\Omega-iX\Omega X^{\intercal}\geq 0\,;

(46)

the corresponding Gaussian channel, denoted as
\mathcal{G}_{X,Y}
, then acts as

\displaystyle\hskip 0.0pt\mathcal{G}_{X,Y}:\chi_{Z}\mapsto\chi_{\mathcal{G}_{X,Y}(Z)}(u)\coloneqq\chi_{Z}\left(\Omega^{\intercal}X^{\intercal}\Omega\,u\right)e^{-\frac{1}{4}u^{\intercal}\Omega^{\intercal}Y\Omega u}

(47)

and

\displaystyle\hskip 0.0pt\mathcal{G}_{X,Y}:\left\{\begin{array}[]{lll}V&\longmapsto&XVX^{\intercal}+Y\,,\\
t&\longmapsto&Xt\,.\end{array}\right.

(48)

It is worth observing that (
46
) implies that
Y\geq 0
is positive semi-definite.

An important class of operators on
{\pazocal{H}}_{m}
that we consider in this paper is the set of Schwartz operators
[
69
]
. They can be defined as those trace class operators whose characteristic function is a Schwartz function on
\mathbb{R}^{2m}
. We denote the set of Schwartz function as
\mathfrak{S}(\mathbb{R}^{2m})
, and that of Schwartz operators as
\mathfrak{S}({\pazocal{H}}_{m})
. In particular, we consider the set
\mathfrak{S}({\pazocal{H}}_{m})_{0}
of Schwartz operators whose characteristic functions are compactly supported.

As spaces of Schwartz functions are usually employed as test spaces in the rigorous theory of distributions, we can use the space
\mathfrak{S}({\pazocal{H}}_{m})_{0}
to formalise the definition of objects — called symbols — that would be ill-defined as operators in the traditional sense. For example, given
any
smooth function
\chi
on
\mathbb{R}^{2m}
, we can construct a symbol
\rho_{\chi}
with ‘characteristic function’
\chi
. This is defined formally as a functional
\rho_{\chi}:\mathfrak{S}({\pazocal{H}}_{m})_{0}\to\mathds{C}
acting as

\displaystyle\rho_{\chi}(Z)\coloneqq\int\frac{d^{2m}x}{(2\pi)^{m}}\,\chi(x)^{*}\chi_{Z}(x)\,.

(49)

This expression is justified by the fact that, when
\sigma\in{\pazocal{T}}_{1}({\pazocal{H}}_{m})
, we have
\rho_{\chi_{\sigma}}(Z)=\tr(\sigma Z)
by Plancherel’s theorem (
34
). The above functional extends to the whole space
\mathfrak{S}({\pazocal{H}}_{m})
whenever the function
x\mapsto\chi(x)^{*}\chi_{Z}(x)
is integrable.

III-C

Concentration inequalities

In this paper, we make use of Bernstein’s matrix inequality in order to prove that the probability that, on a well-chosen finite-dimensional subspace, the output of our shadow tomography protocol is far from the original unknown state decays exponentially fast in the number
N
of samples used to gather statistics:

Lemma 4

((Bernstein’s matrix inequality
[
70
]
))
.

Given
N
i.i.d. random matrices
X_{1},\dots,X_{N}\in\mathbb{M}_{n}(\mathbb{C})
which obey
\|X_{i}-\mathbb{E}[X_{i}]\|_{\infty}\leq R
almost surely, for some
R>0
, the following tail bound holds:

\displaystyle

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\bigg(\bigg\|\frac{1}{N}\!\sum_{i=1}^{N}\,(X_{i}\!-\!\mathbb{E}[X_{i}])\bigg\|_{\infty}\!\!\geq{\epsilon}\bigg)\leq 2n\,e^{-\frac{N\epsilon^{2}}{2\Sigma^{2}+2R\epsilon/3}},

(50)

where the constant
\Sigma
is defined as
\Sigma^{2}\coloneqq\|\mathbb{E}[X_{1}^{2}]\|_{\infty}<\infty
.

IV
Classical shadow tomography of a CV system

In this section, we will have a closer look at the shadow tomography scheme sketched in Section
I-A
. The goal of the procedure is to construct a good estimator of an unknown
m
-mode state
\rho
, by measuring as few i.i.d. copies of
\rho
as possible. To this end, we repeatedly sample symplectic matrices from
\operatorname{Sp}(2m)
according to some probability distribution
\mu
, apply the corresponding symplectic unitary
U_{S}
on one copy of
\rho
, implementing the transformation
\rho\mapsto U_{S}^{\phantom{{\dagger}}}\rho U_{S}^{\dagger}
, and subsequently perform a fixed Gaussian measurement
\big\{\psi_{x}\,\frac{d^{2m}x}{(2\pi)}\big\}_{x\in\mathbb{R}^{2m}}
on that same state. Here,
\psi_{x}=\ket{\psi_{x}}\!\!\bra{\psi_{x}}
with
\ket{\psi_{x}}\coloneqq\pazocal{D}(x)\ket{\psi}
; the Gaussian state
\ket{\psi}
, which uniquely identifies the Gaussian measurement, is a fixed parameter of the shadow tomography protocol. Without loss of generality, we can take
\ket{\psi}
to have zero mean, in which case, according to (
38
), we can introduce a symplectic matrix
T\in\operatorname{Sp}(2m)
satisfying that
U_{T}^{\dagger}\ket{0}=\ket{\psi}
. The
{\dagger}
here is immaterial, thanks to (
36
). Now, acting with a displacement operator on the left and on the right yields immediately

\displaystyle\hskip 0.0pt\ket{\psi_{x}}=\pazocal{D}(x)\ket{\psi}=U_{\!T}^{{\dagger}}\left(U_{\!T}^{\vphantom{{\dagger}}}\pazocal{D}(x)U_{\!T}^{{\dagger}}\right)\ket{0}=U_{\!T}^{{\dagger}}\ket{Tx}\,,

(51)

where the last step is due to the action of symplectic unitaries on displacement operators, see (
35
), and to the definition (
26
) of coherent states.

The measurement makes the system collapse into a random state
\widetilde{\rho}_{x}=\psi_{x}
, where
x
is distributed with probability distribution

\displaystyle\hskip 0.0ptp_{\rho}(x|S)\,d^{2m}x=\braket{\psi_{x}|U_{S}^{\phantom{{\dagger}}}\rho U_{S}^{\dagger}|\psi_{x}}\frac{d^{2m}x}{(2\pi)^{m}}\,.

(52)

When combined with the probability measure
\mu
on
\operatorname{Sp}(2m)
, this yields a joint probability distribution on
\operatorname{Sp}(2m)\times\mathbb{R}^{2m}
.

We then attempt to undo the effect of the symplectic unitary by applying
U_{S}^{\dagger}
. This amounts to the mapping
\widetilde{\rho}_{x}=\psi_{x}\mapsto U_{\!S}^{{\dagger}}\widetilde{\rho}_{x}U_{\!S}^{\vphantom{{\dagger}}}
. Once we average over the random variable
S
and the random variable
X
whore realisation we denoted with
x
, the whole process yields an effective noisy channel
\mathcal{M}
modelled as (
1
). Making this more explicit, we write the action of
\mathcal{M}
on an arbitrary trace class operator
Z\in\pazocal{T}(\pazocal{H}_{m})
as

\displaystyle\hskip 0.0pt\mathcal{M}(Z)=\int\!\!d\mu(S)\int\!\!\frac{d^{2m}x}{(2\pi)^{m}}\,\braket{\psi_{x}|U_{\!S}^{\vphantom{{\dagger}}}Z\,U_{S}^{\dagger}|\psi_{x}}\,U_{\!S}^{{\dagger}}\psi_{x}U_{\!S}^{\vphantom{{\dagger}}}\,.

(53)

Our first result allows us to express the action of
\mathcal{M}
in a form that is more easily amenable to investigation with phase space methods.

Lemma 5
.

The map
\mathcal{M}:{\pazocal{T}}_{1}({\pazocal{H}}_{m})\to{\pazocal{T}}_{1}({\pazocal{H}}_{m})
defined in (

) can be re-expressed as

\displaystyle\hskip 0.0pt\mathcal{M}(Z)=\int\!\!d\mu(S)\,\mathcal{G}_{I,\,2(TS)^{-1}(TS)^{-\intercal}}(Z)\,,

(54)

where
\mathcal{G}_{X,Y}
is the Gaussian channel defined in (
47
).
Moreover, the characteristic function of
\mathcal{M}(Z)
satisfies

\displaystyle\hskip 0.0pt\chi_{\mathcal{M}(Z)}(x)

\displaystyle=\int\!\!d\mu(S)\,\chi_{Z}(x)\,e^{-\frac{1}{2}\left\|TSx\right\|^{2}}=\chi_{Z}(x)\,f_{\mu,T}(x)\,,

(55)

where

\displaystyle\hskip 0.0ptf_{\mu,T}(x)\coloneqq\int\!\!d\mu(S)\,e^{-\frac{1}{2}\left\|TSx\right\|^{2}}.

(56)

Proof.

We start by noticing that due to (
51
) and thanks the fact that
S\mapsto U_{\!S}^{\vphantom{{\dagger}}}
is a group homomorphism, one obtains that

\displaystyle\hskip 0.0ptU_{S}^{\dagger}\ket{\psi_{x}}=U_{S}^{\dagger}U_{T}^{\dagger}\ket{Tx}=\left(U_{T}U_{S}\right)^{\dagger}\ket{Tx}=U_{TS}^{\dagger}\ket{Tx}\,.

(57)

Thus, for any trace class operator
Z\in{\pazocal{T}}_{1}({\pazocal{H}}_{m})

\displaystyle\int\!\!\frac{d^{2m}x}{(2\pi)^{m}}\,\braket{\psi_{x}|U_{\!S}^{\vphantom{{\dagger}}}ZU_{S}^{\dagger}|\psi_{x}}\,U_{\!S}^{{\dagger}}\psi_{x}U_{\!S}^{\vphantom{{\dagger}}}

\displaystyle\quad\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mathclap{\scriptsize\mbox{(i)}}}}{{=}}\int\!\!\frac{d^{2m}x}{(2\pi)^{m}}\,\braket{Tx|U_{\!TS}^{\vphantom{{\dagger}}}ZU_{\!TS}^{{\dagger}}|Tx}\,U_{\!TS}^{{\dagger}}\ket{Tx}\!\!\bra{Tx}U_{\!TS}^{\vphantom{{\dagger}}}

\displaystyle\quad\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mathclap{\scriptsize\mbox{(ii)}}}}{{=}}\int\!\!\frac{d^{2m}y}{(2\pi)^{m}}\,\braket{y|U_{\!TS}^{\vphantom{{\dagger}}}ZU_{\!TS}^{{\dagger}}|y}\,U_{\!TS}^{{\dagger}}\ket{y}\!\!\bra{y}U_{\!TS}^{\vphantom{{\dagger}}}

(58)

\displaystyle\quad=U_{\!TS}^{{\dagger}}\left(\int\!\!\frac{d^{2m}y}{(2\pi)^{m}}\,\ket{y}\!\!\bra{y}U_{\!TS}^{\vphantom{{\dagger}}}ZU_{\!TS}^{{\dagger}}\ket{y}\!\!\bra{y}\right)U_{\!TS}^{\vphantom{{\dagger}}}

\displaystyle\quad\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mathclap{\scriptsize\mbox{(iii)}}}}{{=}}U_{\!TS}^{{\dagger}}\,\mathcal{N}_{1}\left(U_{\!TS}^{\vphantom{{\dagger}}}ZU_{\!TS}^{{\dagger}}\right)U_{\!TS}^{\vphantom{{\dagger}}}

Here, in (i) we applied (
57
) twice; in (ii) we changed variable, defining
y\coloneqq Tx
, and used the fact that
T
, being symplectic, has determinant
1
; and in (iii) we employed the representation in (
43
) for the action of
\mathcal{N}_{1}
.

Now, let us compute the action of the above transformation at the level of covariance matrices. By applying (
37
) and (
III-B
), we see that

\displaystyle V\left[U_{\!TS}^{{\dagger}}\,\mathcal{N}_{1}\left(U_{\!TS}^{\vphantom{{\dagger}}}Z\,U_{\!TS}^{{\dagger}}\right)U_{\!TS}^{\vphantom{{\dagger}}}\right]

\displaystyle\qquad=(TS)^{-1}V\left[\mathcal{N}_{1}\left(U_{\!TS}^{\vphantom{{\dagger}}}Z\,U_{\!TS}^{{\dagger}}\right)\right](TS)^{-\intercal}

\displaystyle\qquad=(TS)^{-1}\left(V\left[U_{\!TS}^{\vphantom{{\dagger}}}Z\,U_{\!TS}^{{\dagger}}\right]+2I\right)(TS)^{-\intercal}

\displaystyle\qquad=(TS)^{-1}\left(TS\,V[Z](TS)^{\intercal}+2I\right)(TS)^{-\intercal}

\displaystyle\qquad=V[Z]+2(TS)^{-1}(TS)^{-\intercal}\,.

Comparing the above calculation with (
48
), we see that

\displaystyle\hskip 0.0ptU_{\!TS}^{{\dagger}}\,\mathcal{N}_{1}\left(U_{\!TS}^{\vphantom{{\dagger}}}Z\,U_{\!TS}^{{\dagger}}\right)U_{\!TS}^{\vphantom{{\dagger}}}=\mathcal{G}_{I,\,2(TS)^{-1}(TS)^{-\intercal}}(Z)\,.

(59)

Using this insight in (

) shows that

\displaystyle\hskip 0.0pt\int\!\!\frac{d^{2m}x}{(2\pi)^{m}}\,\braket{\psi_{x}|U_{\!S}^{\vphantom{{\dagger}}}ZU_{S}^{\dagger}|\psi_{x}}\,U_{\!S}^{{\dagger}}\psi_{x}U_{\!S}^{\vphantom{{\dagger}}}=\mathcal{G}_{I,\,2(TS)^{-1}(TS)^{-\intercal}}(Z)\,.

(60)

In turn, the above identity yields (
54
) upon integration in
S
with respect to the measure
\mu
.

We conclude by computing the characteristic function (
28
) of both sides of (
54
). For an arbitrary
x\in\mathbb{R}^{2m}
, we obtain that

\displaystyle\hskip 0.0pt\chi_{\mathcal{M}(Z)}(x)

\displaystyle=\int d\mu(S)\,\chi_{\mathcal{G}_{I,\,2(TS)^{-1}(TS)^{-\intercal}}(Z)}(x)

(61)

\displaystyle\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mathclap{\scriptsize\mbox{(iv)}}}}{{=}}\int d\mu(S)\,\chi_{Z}(x)e^{-\frac{1}{2}x^{\intercal}\Omega^{\intercal}(TS)^{-1}(TS)^{-\intercal}\Omega x}

\displaystyle\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mathclap{\scriptsize\mbox{(v)}}}}{{=}}\int d\mu(S)\,\chi_{Z}(x)e^{-\frac{1}{2}x^{\intercal}(TS)^{\intercal}(TS)x}

\displaystyle=\int d\mu(S)\,\chi_{Z}(x)e^{-\frac{1}{2}\left\|TSx\right\|^{2}}.

Here, (iv) follows from (
47
), and (v) is due to the fact that since
TS
is symplectic,
\Omega^{\intercal}(TS)^{-1}=(TS)^{\intercal}\Omega^{\intercal}
, or upon transposing
(TS)^{-\intercal}\Omega=\Omega(TS)
.
∎

We therefore see that the action of
\mathcal{M}
is actually very simple, amounting to a point-wise multiplication at the level of the characteristic function. Channels of this form are particular examples of so-called
linear bosonic channels
, introduced and studied by Holevo and Werner
[
8
]
. Although we will not use this observation in this work, it is worth noting that linear bosonic channels are always approximable — in the strong operator sense — by
Gaussian dilatable
channels, i.e. channels admitting a Stinespring representation in which the unitary is Gaussian and the ancilla is arbitrary
[
71
,
72
]
.

As it turns out,
\mathcal{M}
is a special type of linear bosonic channel whose action is representable as a random displacement. Namely, using (
25
) one sees that

\displaystyle\mathcal{M}(Z)=

\displaystyle\ \int\frac{d^{2m}x}{(2\pi)^{m}}\,\widetilde{f}_{\mu,T}(x)\,\pazocal{D}(x)Z\pazocal{D}(-x)\,,

(62)

\displaystyle\widetilde{f}_{\mu,T}(x)\coloneqq

\displaystyle\ \int\frac{d^{2m}u}{(2\pi)^{m}}\,f_{\mu,T}(u)\,e^{ix^{\intercal}\Omega u}=f_{\mu,T}(x)\,,

(63)

i.e.
f_{\mu,T}
coincides with its own Fourier transform. The rigorous proof of the general validity of (
63
) is deferred to Appendix
B
. In light of this discussion, it is easy to write down, at least formally, the inverse of
\mathcal{M}
, which acts as

\displaystyle\hskip 0.0pt\mathcal{M}^{-1}:\chi_{Z}\longmapsto\chi_{\mathcal{M}^{-1}(Z)}(x)\coloneqq\frac{1}{f_{\mu,T}(x)}\,\chi_{Z}(x)\,.

(64)

Note that
f_{\mu,T}(x)>0
for all
x\in\mathds{R}^{2m}
, so the above expression is always well defined. In particular,
\mathcal{M}
is injective as a linear map, and thus it is invertible on its range.

The problem, naturally, is that such a range is in general significantly smaller than the space of trace class operators (we will shortly see an example of this). This entails that the right-hand side of (
64
) is not always the characteristic function of a trace class operator. This, however, does not pose any problem since the classical shadow obtained at the output of
\mathcal{M}^{-1}
is, as the name suggests, just a classical object which we will merely use as a computational tool.

Incidentally, also
\mathcal{M}^{-1}
, just like
\mathcal{M}
, acts as a mere point-wise multiplication at the level of characteristic functions. This implies that one can also try, as done in (
62
), to represent it as an affine combination of displacement operators, i.e. by writing

\displaystyle\hskip 0.0pt\mathcal{M}^{-1}(Z)=\int\frac{d^{2m}x}{(2\pi)^{m}}\,\widetilde{g}_{\mu,T}(x)\,\pazocal{D}(x)Z\pazocal{D}(-x)\,,

(65)

where
g_{\mu,T}(x)\coloneqq\frac{1}{f_{\mu,T}(x)}
, and

\displaystyle\hskip 0.0pt\widetilde{g}_{\mu,T}(x)\coloneqq\int\frac{d^{2m}u}{(2\pi)^{m}}\,g_{\mu,T}(u)\,e^{ix^{\intercal}\Omega u}

(66)

would be its Fourier transform. The trouble, of course, is that
g_{\mu,T}
will not be absolutely integrable — and not even bounded — in general, so there is little hope to define its Fourier transform unless one appeals to the theory of distributions.

To provide a solution to this apparent issue, let us return to our original problem. We can now formally construct the classical shadow

\displaystyle\hskip 0.0pt\hat{\rho}

\displaystyle=\mathcal{M}^{-1}\left(U_{\!S}^{{\dagger}}\pazocal{D}(x)\psi\pazocal{D}(-x)U_{\!S}^{\vphantom{{\dagger}}}\right)

\displaystyle=\mathcal{M}^{-1}\left(U_{\!TS}^{{\dagger}}\ket{Tx}\!\!\bra{Tx}U_{\!TS}^{\vphantom{{\dagger}}}\right)\,.

The shadow,
\hat{\rho}
, is more rigorously defined as a functional on the set
\mathfrak{S}({\pazocal{H}}_{m})_{0}
of Schwartz operators with compactly supported characteristic functions via (
49
). Its corresponding improper characteristic function can hence be computed as follows:

\displaystyle\hskip 0.0pt\hat{\chi}(u)\equiv\chi_{\hat{\rho}}(u)

\displaystyle=\chi_{\mathcal{M}^{-1}\left(U_{\!TS}^{{\dagger}}\ket{Tx}\!\bra{Tx}U_{\!TS}^{\vphantom{{\dagger}}}\right)}(u)

(67)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{f_{\mu,T}(u)}\,\chi_{U_{\!TS}^{{\dagger}}\ket{Tx}\!\bra{Tx}U_{\!TS}^{\vphantom{{\dagger}}}}(u)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{f_{\mu,T}(u)}\,\chi_{\ket{Tx}\!\bra{Tx}}(TSu)

\displaystyle={\frac{1}{f_{\mu,T}(u)}\,e^{-\frac{1}{4}\left\|TSu\right\|^{2}-iu^{\intercal}\Omega S^{-1}x}}\,,

where in the last step we used (
31
). In other words, for any
Z\in\mathcal{S}({\pazocal{H}}_{m})
:

\displaystyle\hat{\rho}(Z)\coloneqq\int\frac{d^{2m}u}{(2\pi)^{m}}\,\hat{\chi}(u)^{*}\,\chi_{Z}(u)\,,

(68)

whenever the function
u\mapsto\hat{\chi}(u)^{*}\chi_{Z}(u)
is integrable. In what follows, we will also consider the reduced shadow over a subset
A
of
|A|=r
modes, formally given by the partial trace
``\hat{\rho}_{A}\coloneqq\tr_{A^{c}}(\hat{\rho})"
of the shadow
\hat{\rho}
. Again, we will use the characteristic function to rigorously define it: given a region
A
of
r
modes, it is defined for any
u_{A}\in\mathbb{R}^{2r}
as

\displaystyle\hat{\chi}_{A}(u_{A})\equiv\chi_{\hat{\rho}_{A}}(u_{A})\coloneqq\chi_{\hat{\rho}}(u_{A},0)\,.

(69)

In that case, we write for any
Z_{A}\in\mathfrak{S}({\pazocal{H}}_{r})
:

\displaystyle\hat{\rho}_{A}(Z_{A})\coloneqq\int\frac{d^{2r}u_{A}}{(2\pi)^{r}}\,\hat{\chi}_{A}(u_{A})^{*}\,\chi_{Z_{A}}(u_{A})\,,

(70)

whenever the function
u_{A}\mapsto\hat{\chi}_{A}(u_{A})^{*}\,\chi_{Z_{A}}(u_{A})
is integrable.

The following lemma further justifies the claim made in (
3
) that the shadow
\hat{\rho}
has
average
\rho
by construction.

Lemma 6
.

For any subset
A
of
|A|=r
modes, and all
Z_{A}\in\mathfrak{S}({\pazocal{H}}_{r})_{0}
with corresponding characteristic function
\chi_{Z_{A}}
, the random variable
\hat{\rho}_{A}(Z_{A})
defined via (
70
) is integrable and

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\big[\hat{\rho}_{A}(Z_{A})\big]\,=\tr[\rho_{A}Z_{A}]\,,

where the conditional expectation is taken with respect to the probability density function
p_{T}(S,x)\coloneqq\braket{Tx|U_{TS}^{\phantom{{\dagger}}}\rho U_{TS}^{\dagger}|Tx}
with respect to
\mu\otimes\frac{d^{2m}x}{(2\pi)^{m}}
on
\operatorname{Sp}(2m)\times\mathbb{R}^{2m}
. The result extends to
Z_{A}\in\mathfrak{S}({\pazocal{H}}_{r})
under the condition of integrability with respect to
\mu\otimes\frac{d^{2m}x}{(2\pi)^{m}}\otimes\frac{d^{2r}u}{(2\pi)^{r}}
of the function

\displaystyle\hskip 0.0pt(S,x,u)\mapsto\frac{\chi_{Z_{A}}(u)\,\chi_{\ket{Tx}\!\bra{Tx}}(TS(u,0)^{\intercal})\,p_{T}(S,x)}{f_{\mu,T}(u,0)}\,.

(71)

Proof.

We present the proof for
|A|=m
since the case
|A|=r<m
follows the exact same strategy using that
\hat{\chi}(u_{A},0)=\hat{\chi}_{A}(u_{A})
by definition. Now, on the one hand, if
Z\in\mathfrak{S}({\pazocal{H}}_{m})_{0}
then the characteristic function
\chi_{Z}
of
Z
is compactly supported; therefore, owing to the boundedness of
\chi_{Z}
we deduce that
the function

\displaystyle\hskip 0.0pt(S,x,u)\mapsto\frac{\chi_{Z}(u)\chi_{\ket{Tx}\!\bra{Tx}}(TSu)\,p_{T}(S,x)}{f_{\mu,T}(u)}

(72)

is integrable with respect to
\mu\otimes\frac{d^{2m}x}{(2\pi)^{m}}\otimes\frac{d^{2m}u}{(2\pi)^{m}}
. On the other hand, if only

such integrability is assumed by hypothesis. Therefore, in both cases thanks to Fubini’s theorem and (
67
) we have that

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\big[\hat{\rho}(Z)\big]

\displaystyle\ =\!\int\!\!\!\frac{d^{2m}u}{(2\pi)^{m}}\!\int\!\!\!d\mu(S)\!\int\!\!\!\frac{d^{2m}x}{(2\pi)^{m}}\,\chi_{Z}(u)\,\frac{\chi_{\ket{Tx}\!\bra{Tx}}(TSu)\,p_{T}(S,x)}{f_{\mu,T}(u)}

\displaystyle\ \stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mathclap{\scriptsize\mbox{(i)}}}}{{=}}\!\int\!\!\!\frac{d^{2m}u}{(2\pi)^{m}}\!\int\!\!\!d\mu(S)\,\chi_{Z}(u)\,\frac{\chi_{\mathcal{N}_{1}\left(U_{TS}^{\phantom{{\dagger}}}\rho U_{TS}^{\dagger}\right)}(TSu)}{f_{\mu,T}(u)}

\displaystyle\ \stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mathclap{\scriptsize\mbox{(ii)}}}}{{=}}\!\int\!\!\!\frac{d^{2m}u}{(2\pi)^{m}}\!\int\!\!\!d\mu(S)\,\frac{\chi_{Z}(u)\,\chi_{U_{TS}^{\phantom{{\dagger}}}\rho U_{TS}^{\dagger}}(TSu)\,e^{-\frac{1}{2}\|TSu\|^{2}}}{f_{\mu,T}(u)}

In (i) we changed variable, defining
y\coloneqq Tx
, used the fact that
T
, being symplectic, has determinant
1
, and employed the representation in (
43
); in (ii) we used (
40
); finally, in (iii) we leveraged (
35
).
∎

In this section, we have made rigorous our first intuitive notion of a CV shadow by means of its characteristic function. We have also seen that the latter reduces to the characteristic function of the original unknown state
\rho
on average when integrated against sufficiently smooth functions. The goal of the next section is to prove that under some physically relevant conditions such as energy boundedness of the state
\rho
, these integrals are enough to estimate expected values of observables with respect to the state
\rho
to high accuracy.

IV-A

Finite moments assumption

As we saw in the previous section, the shadow
\hat{\rho}
, which we formally defined through its characteristic function, is in general unbounded. In this section, we show that this issue can be fixed if we further assume that the photon number distribution of the unknown state
\rho
satisfies some moment constraints. This allows us to show that the projection of
\rho
onto a certain finite subspace of Hilbert–Schmidt operators is enough to get a sufficiently good approximation of it while running the shadow tomography protocol.

More precisely, we argue that all we need is to ensure finite rank convergence of density operators and an energy constraint on the initial state
\rho
.

Let
Z\in{\pazocal{T}}_{2}({\pazocal{H}}_{m})
be a Hilbert Schmidt operator and denote by
\chi_{Z}
its characteristic function. By density, one can find a function
\widetilde{\chi}_{Z}\in C_{c}^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{2m})
such that
\|\chi_{Z}-\widetilde{\chi}_{Z}\|_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{2m})}<\varepsilon
and
\widetilde{\chi}_{Z}
is the characteristic function of some operator
\widetilde{Z}
, i.e.
\chi_{\widetilde{Z}}=\widetilde{\chi}_{Z}
. The operator
\widetilde{Z}
is a Schwartz operator, and is in particular trace class, and clearly
\|Z-\widetilde{Z}\|_{2}<\varepsilon

[
69
]
. Thus,
\hat{\rho}(\widetilde{Z})
is now necessarily a well-defined quantity by the quantum Plancherel formula (cf. (
34
)). In particular, we have that, for any
Z\in{\pazocal{T}}_{2}({\pazocal{H}}_{m})
,

\displaystyle|\Tr(Z\rho)-\hat{\rho}(\widetilde{Z})|

\displaystyle\leq|\Tr((Z-\widetilde{Z})\rho)+|\Tr(\rho\widetilde{Z})-\hat{\rho}(\widetilde{Z})|

\displaystyle\leq\varepsilon+|\Tr(\rho\widetilde{Z})-\hat{\rho}(\widetilde{Z})|\,.

This can be used in the above context by choosing
Z=Z_{\mathbf{n}_{1}\mathbf{n}_{2}}=\ket{\mathbf{n}_{1}}\!\!\bra{\mathbf{n}_{2}}
, given two multi-mode Fock states
\ket{\mathbf{n}_{1}}\!\!\bra{\mathbf{n}_{2}}\in{\pazocal{H}}_{m}
, and defining

\mathcal{P}_{M}(T)\coloneqq\sum_{\mathbf{n}_{1},\mathbf{n}_{2}\in\{0,..,M\}^{m}}\langle\mathbf{n}_{1}|T|\mathbf{n}_{2}\rangle\,\ket{\mathbf{n}_{1}}\!\!\bra{\mathbf{n}_{2}}\equiv P_{M}TP_{M}\,.

(73)

Using the above approximation scheme, we can approximate the Schwartz operators
Z_{\mathbf{n}_{1}\mathbf{n}_{2}}
by Schwartz operators
\widetilde{Z}_{\mathbf{n}_{1}\mathbf{n}_{2}}
with smooth compactly supported characteristic functions and define an auxiliary map

\widetilde{\mathcal{P}}_{M}(T)\coloneqq\sum_{\mathbf{n}_{1},\mathbf{n}_{2}\in\{0,..,M\}^{m}}\Tr\left[\widetilde{Z}_{\mathbf{n}_{2}\mathbf{n}_{1}}T\right]\ket{\mathbf{n}_{1}}\!\!\bra{\mathbf{n}_{2}}\,.

(74)

For sake of simplicity, we will use the same notation for projected reduced states on subsets of
r<m
modes. We further assume that the characteristic function of
\widetilde{Z}_{\mathbf{n}_{1}\mathbf{n}_{2}}
is obtained from that of
\ket{\mathbf{n}_{1}}\!\!\bra{\mathbf{n}_{2}}
by point-wise multiplication by a simple compactly supported function. Namely, we set

\displaystyle\widetilde{\chi}_{\mathbf{n}_{1}\mathbf{n}_{2}}(u)\coloneqq\widetilde{\chi}_{Z_{\mathbf{n_{1}}\mathbf{n}_{2}}}(u)=\chi_{\ket{\mathbf{n}_{1}}\!\bra{\mathbf{n}_{2}}}(u)\prod_{j=1}^{m}\xi_{\eta,R}\big(u^{(j)}\big)\,,

(75)

where as usual
u^{(j)}\coloneqq(u_{j},u_{j+m})^{\intercal}\in\mathds{R}^{2}
, and, given some parameters
0<\eta<R
, the smooth function
\xi_{\eta,R}:\mathds{R}^{2}\to[0,1]
satisfies that

\displaystyle\hskip 0.0pt\xi_{\eta,R}(z)=\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}1,&\qquad\text{if $\|z\|\leq\eta$,}\\[4.30554pt]
0,&\qquad\text{if $\|z\|\geq R$.}\end{array}\right.

(76)

In particular, this allows us to evaluate
\rho_{\chi}\big(\widetilde{Z}_{\mathbf{n}_{2}\mathbf{n}_{1}}\big)
for functionals
\rho_{\chi}
defined as in (
49
).

Lemma 7
.

For all
\alpha>0
, all non-negative integers
m
(number of modes) and
M
(Fock truncation number), and all
\eta\geq 0
, it holds that

\displaystyle\left\|\mathcal{L}_{(I+N_{m})^{\alpha}}\mathcal{R}_{(I+N_{m})^{\alpha}}\left(\mathcal{P}_{M}-\widetilde{\mathcal{P}}_{{M}}\right)\right\|_{2\to 1}\leq\delta_{0}(\eta,M,\alpha,m)\,,

(77)

where the rapidly vanishing function
\delta_{0}
is defined by

\displaystyle\delta_{0}(\eta,M,\alpha,m)

(78)

\displaystyle\coloneqq(mM+1)^{2\alpha}(M+1)^{m}\,3^{mM}e^{-\frac{m}{4}\eta^{2}}\left(\sum_{p=0}^{2M}\frac{\eta^{2p}}{2^{p}p!}\right)^{m/2}.

Next, we consider an approximation of
\rho
in the following norm: given
0\leq\alpha<n
and two trace class operators
\rho,\rho^{\prime}\in{\pazocal{T}}_{1}({\pazocal{H}}_{m})
with
\tr(\rho(I+N_{m})^{n}),\tr(\rho^{\prime}(I+N_{m})^{n})<\infty
, we denote
H_{m}\coloneqq I+N_{m}
and

\displaystyle\|\rho-\rho^{\prime}\|^{(\alpha)}_{1}\coloneqq\left\|H_{m}^{\alpha/2}(\rho-\rho^{\prime})H_{m}^{\alpha/2}\right\|_{1}\,.

Similar norms were previously defined in
[
73
]
under the name of m-mode bosonic Sobolev norms.

Proposition 8
.

Let
N_{m}
be the number operator on
{\pazocal{H}}_{m}
,
H_{m}=I+N_{m}
, and
\rho
a state such that for some
n>0
we have
E_{m}^{(n)}\coloneqq\Tr[\rho\,H_{m}^{n}]<\infty
. Define
\mathcal{P}_{M}
and
\widetilde{\mathcal{P}}_{M}
as in (
73
) and (
74
), respectively. Then for any
0\leq\alpha<n

\displaystyle\left\|\rho-\mathcal{P}_{M}(\rho)\right\|_{1}^{(\alpha)}\leq 2(M+2)^{-\frac{n-\alpha}{2}}\,E_{m}^{(n)},

(79)

\displaystyle\left\|\rho-\widetilde{\mathcal{P}}_{{M}}(\rho)\right\|^{(\alpha)}_{1}\leq 2(M+2)^{-\frac{n-\alpha}{2}}\,E_{m}^{(n)}+\delta_{0}\big(\eta,M,\tfrac{\alpha}{2},m\big)\,,

(80)

where
\delta_{0}
was introduced in Lemma
7

Proof.

We use a duality argument for this proof. For any
O
such that
\big\|H_{m}^{-\alpha/2}OH_{m}^{-\alpha/2}\big\|_{\infty}\leq 1
, we find

\displaystyle\left|\tr\big[O(\rho-\mathcal{P}_{M}(\rho))\big]\right|

\displaystyle\quad=\left|\Tr\left[H_{m}^{-\frac{\alpha}{2}}OH_{m}^{-\frac{\alpha}{2}}H_{m}^{\frac{\alpha}{2}}(\rho-\mathcal{P}_{M}(\rho))H_{m}^{\frac{\alpha}{2}}\right]\right|

\displaystyle\quad\leq\left\|H_{m}^{\frac{\alpha}{2}}(\rho-\mathcal{P}_{M}(\rho))H_{m}^{\frac{\alpha}{2}}\right\|_{1}

\displaystyle\quad\leq\left\|H_{m}^{-\frac{n-\alpha}{2}}H_{m}^{\frac{n}{2}}(I-P_{M})\rho\,H_{m}^{\frac{n}{2}}H_{m}^{-\frac{n-\alpha}{2}}\right\|_{1}

\displaystyle\quad\quad+\left\|H_{m}^{-\frac{n-\alpha}{2}}H_{m}^{\frac{n}{2}}P_{M}\rho(I-P_{M})\,H_{m}^{\frac{n}{2}}H_{m}^{-\frac{n-\alpha}{2}}\right\|_{1}

\displaystyle\quad\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mathclap{\scriptsize\mbox{(i)}}}}{{\leq}}\left\|H_{m}^{-\frac{n-\alpha}{2}}(I-P_{M})\right\|_{\infty}\left\|H_{m}^{\frac{n}{2}}\rho\,H_{m}^{\frac{n}{2}}\right\|_{1}\left\|H_{m}^{-\frac{n-\alpha}{2}}\right\|_{\infty}

\displaystyle\quad\quad+\left\|H_{m}^{-\frac{n-\alpha}{2}}\right\|_{\infty}\|P_{M}\|_{\infty}\left\|H_{m}^{\frac{n}{2}}\rho\,H_{m}^{\frac{n}{2}}\right\|_{1}\left\|(I-P_{M})H_{m}^{-\frac{n-\alpha}{2}}\right\|_{\infty}

\displaystyle\quad\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mathclap{\scriptsize\mbox{(ii)}}}}{{\leq}}2(M+2)^{-\frac{n-\alpha}{2}}E_{m}^{(n)}\,.

Here, (i) comes from a repeated application of Hölder’s inequality, and we also observed that
P_{M}
and
H_{m}
commute; (ii) is because

\displaystyle\hskip 0.0pt\left\|H_{m}^{-\frac{n-\alpha}{2}}(I-P_{M})\right\|_{\infty}

\displaystyle=\left\|\sum\nolimits_{\mathbf{n}\in\mathds{N}^{m}\setminus[M]^{m}}(1+|\mathbf{n}|)^{-\frac{n-\alpha}{2}}\ket{\mathbf{n}}\!\!\bra{\mathbf{n}}\right\|_{\infty}

(81)

\displaystyle=\max_{\mathbf{n}\in\mathds{N}^{m}\setminus[M]^{m}}(1+|\mathbf{n}|)^{-\frac{n-\alpha}{2}}

\displaystyle=(2+M)^{-\frac{n-\alpha}{2}}.

This proves (
79
). Next, we have that

\displaystyle\left|\tr\left[O\,(\mathcal{P}_{M}-\widetilde{\mathcal{P}}_{{M}})(\rho)\right]\right|

\displaystyle\quad=\left|\Tr\left[H_{m}^{-\frac{\alpha}{2}}OH_{m}^{-\frac{\alpha}{2}}H_{m}^{\frac{\alpha}{2}}(\mathcal{P}_{M}(\rho)-\widetilde{\mathcal{P}}_{M}(\rho))H_{m}^{\frac{\alpha}{2}}\right]\right|

\displaystyle\quad\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mathclap{\scriptsize\mbox{(iii)}}}}{{\leq}}\left\|H_{m}^{-\frac{\alpha}{2}}OH_{m}^{-\frac{\alpha}{2}}\right\|_{\infty}\left\|H_{m}^{\frac{\alpha}{2}}\,(\mathcal{P}_{M}-\widetilde{\mathcal{P}}_{M})(\rho)H_{m}^{\frac{\alpha}{2}}\right\|_{1}

\displaystyle\quad\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mathclap{\scriptsize\mbox{(iv)}}}}{{\leq}}\delta_{0}\left(\eta,M,\tfrac{\alpha}{2},m\right),

where (iii) is again Hölder’s inequality, and (iv) comes from our assumptions together with Lemma
7
. Combining this with (
79
) yields (
80
) and concludes the proof.
∎

In order to use the approximation bounds of
8
, it remains to estimate how well the empirical average of the shadows

\displaystyle{\hat{\sigma}}^{(N)}\coloneqq\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\hat{\rho}^{(i)}\,,

(82)

where
\hat{\rho}^{(i)}
are
N
i.i.d. copies of the shadow
\hat{\rho}
constructed in (
68
), approximates
\mathcal{P}_{M}(\rho)
and
\widetilde{\mathcal{P}}_{{M}}(\rho)
depending on the choice of the Gaussian shadow tomography scheme. For this, we introduce, for any set
A
of
|A|=r
modes, the operators

\displaystyle{\sigma}^{(N)}_{A}(M)\coloneqq\sum_{\mathbf{n}_{1},\mathbf{n}_{2}\in\{0,..,M\}^{r}}\,\hat{\sigma}_{A}^{(N)}(\ket{\mathbf{n}_{2}}\!\!\bra{\mathbf{n}_{1}})\,\ket{\mathbf{n}_{1}}\!\!\bra{\mathbf{n}_{2}},

\displaystyle\widetilde{\sigma}_{A}^{(N)}(M)\coloneqq\sum_{\mathbf{n}_{1},\mathbf{n}_{2}\in\{0,..,M\}^{r}}\hat{\sigma}_{A}^{(N)}(\widetilde{Z}_{\mathbf{n}_{2}\mathbf{n}_{1}})\,\ket{\mathbf{n}_{1}}\!\!\bra{\mathbf{n}_{2}}\,.

(83)

Since both operators are supported on a finite dimensional subspace, we can resort to the matrix Bernstein inequality (
50
) in order to prove that, with high probability,
\|{\sigma}_{A}^{(N)}(M)-{\mathcal{P}}_{{M}}(\rho_{A})\|_{1},\|\widetilde{\sigma}_{A}^{(N)}(M)-\widetilde{\mathcal{P}}_{{M}}(\rho_{A})\|_{1}\leq\epsilon
for
N
large enough. In the next section, we explain in more detail how we use the matrix Bernstein inequality in the case of both the homodyne and the heterodyne detection strategies.

V
Homodyne and heterodyne shadow tomography

V-A

Local homodyne detection

Let us first consider the scenario in which one performs a homodyne detection along a random direction in phase space. Here,
m
independent random matrices
S_{1},\dots,S_{m}
are distributed uniformly (according to the Haar measure) on the intersection
\operatorname{Sp}(2)\cap\operatorname{SO}(2)
between the symplectic and the orthogonal group. It is useful to note that
\operatorname{Sp}(2)\cap\operatorname{SO}(2)\simeq\operatorname{U}(1)
, where on the right-hand side we have the unitary group of
1\times 1
matrices, so that the Haar measure on
\operatorname{Sp}(2)\cap\operatorname{SO}(2)
is essentially that on
\operatorname{U}(1)
. In this simple homodyne case the algorithm for shadow tomography is summarised as follows:

1.

A copy of
\rho
is loaded, and
m
matrices
S_{1},\dots,S_{m}\in\operatorname{U}_{1}
are drawn at random according to the Haar measure. In other words
U_{j}=R_{\theta_{j}}
for some angle
\theta_{j}\in[-\pi,\pi]
, where

\displaystyle R_{\theta}\coloneqq\begin{pmatrix}\cos\theta&-\sin\theta\\
\sin\theta&\cos\theta\end{pmatrix}\,.

2.

The rotation
U_{S}\equiv U_{S_{1}}\otimes\dots\otimes U_{S_{m}}
is applied to
\rho
, where
S=S_{1}\oplus\dots\oplus S_{m}
, obtaining the state
U_{S}\rho\,U_{S}^{\dagger}
.

3.

The output state of (2) is subjected to a homodyne measurement along the position axis, yielding the classical outcome
x=(x_{1},\dots,x_{m})^{\intercal}\in\mathbb{R}^{2m}
.

4.

We construct the classical shadow
\hat{\rho}
, which is the final output of this round of the protocol.

After several rounds have been conducted, we can process the classical shadows as we prefer. A typical method would be that of computing the empirical average
\hat{\sigma}^{(N)}
as defined in (
82
) for the above protocol. We can then use that operator e.g. for computing expected values of observables, or else reduced density operators, etc. To model homodyning in a rigorous way, let us introduce a parameter
s>0
, which we will later take to infinity, and let us set

\displaystyle\hskip 0.0ptT^{\oplus m}=T^{\oplus m}_{s}\coloneqq\begin{pmatrix}e^{-s}&0\\
0&e^{s}\end{pmatrix}^{\bigoplus m},

(84)

These choices of
\mu\sim\operatorname{Haar}(\operatorname{U}_{1})
and
T
completely determine our shadow tomographic setting. In the limit
s\to\infty
, the measurement will reproduce a homodyne measurement along the position axis of each mode. To make things more concrete, given a threshold
M\in\mathbb{N}
and
\alpha\geq 0
we define

\displaystyle\hat{\rho}_{A}(M)

\displaystyle\coloneqq\bigotimes_{j\in A}\,\Big(\sum\nolimits_{n_{1},n_{2}=0}^{M}\,\hat{\rho}_{j}(M)_{n_{1},n_{2}}\ket{n_{1}}\!\!\bra{n_{2}}\Big),

(85)

\displaystyle\hat{\rho}_{j}(M)_{n_{1},n_{2}}

\displaystyle\coloneqq\int\!\!dy\ |y|\,e^{-i(y,0)S_{j}^{-\intercal}\Omega S_{j}x_{j}}\,\chi_{\ket{n_{2}}\!\bra{n_{1}}}(S_{j}(y,0)^{\intercal})\,,

By (
32
), the absolute value of the characteristic function
\chi_{|n_{2}(j)\rangle\langle n_{1}(j)|}(S_{j}(y,0)^{T})
is upper bounded by

\displaystyle\sqrt{\frac{n_{2}(j)!}{n_{1}(j)!}}\,e^{-\frac{\pi}{2}|\omega|^{2}}(\sqrt{\pi}|\omega|)^{M_{j}-m_{j}}\,L_{m_{j}}^{(M_{j}-m_{j})}(\pi|\omega|^{2})\,,

where
\omega\coloneqq-x^{\prime}+i\frac{x^{\prime\prime}}{2\pi}
with
x=x^{\prime}\oplus x^{\prime\prime}=S_{j}(y,0)^{T}
,
m_{j}\coloneqq\min\{n_{1}(j),n_{2}(j)\}
,
M_{j}\coloneqq\max\{n_{1}(j),n_{2}(j)\}
, and where the Laguerre polynomials
L_{j}^{(k)}
were defined in (
33
). Since
\frac{|y|}{2\pi}\leq|\omega|\leq|y|
, we therefore have that
\left\|H_{r}^{\frac{\alpha}{2}}\hat{\rho}_{A}(M)H_{r}^{\frac{\alpha}{2}}\right\|_{\infty}
is almost surely bounded by

\displaystyle\Sigma^{(\alpha)}_{r}(M)

(86)

\displaystyle\coloneqq\Bigg\|\,\Bigg(\!\left((1+|\mathbf{n}_{1}|)(1+|\mathbf{n}_{2}|)\right)^{\frac{\alpha}{2}}\prod_{j=1}^{r}\,\sqrt{\frac{n_{2}(j)!}{n_{1}(j)!}}

\displaystyle\times\int\!\!dy\,|\sqrt{\pi}y|^{1+M_{j}-m_{j}}\,\,e^{-\frac{|y|^{2}}{8\pi}}\left|L_{M_{j}}^{M_{j}-m_{j}}\big(\pi|y|^{2}\big)\right|\Bigg)_{\mathbf{n}_{1},\mathbf{n}_{2}}\Bigg\|_{\infty},

where
\mathbf{n}_{1},\mathbf{n}_{2}\in\{0,\dots,M\}^{r}
, by using the simple fact that given two matrices
A=\{(a_{ij})\}_{ij}
and
B=\{(b_{ij})\}_{ij}
with
|a_{ij}|\leq b_{ij}
for all
i,j
, then
\|A\|_{\infty}\leq\|B\|_{\infty}
. This can be proved as follows. For a vector
v
with entries
v_{i}
and norm
\|v\|_{2}^{2}=\sum_{i}|v_{i}|^{2}
, define the new vector
w
with entries
w_{i}\coloneqq|v_{i}|
, so that
\|w\|^{2}=1
. Using the triangle inequality, it is straightforward to verify that
\|Av\|^{2}\leq\|Bw\|^{2}
; this entails that
\|A\|_{\infty}=\sup_{\|v\|=1}\|Av\|\leq\sup_{\|w\|=1}\|Bw\|=\|B\|_{\infty}
. Continuing, as before we then denote

\displaystyle\sigma_{A}^{(N)}(M)\coloneqq\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\,\hat{\rho}^{(i)}_{A}(M)\,,

where
\hat{\rho}^{(i)}_{A}(M)
are i.i.d. random matrices of law the one of
\hat{\rho}_{A}(M)
. With this, we are ready to state our first main result:

Theorem 9
.

With the above notation,
given
0\leq\alpha<n
such that
E^{(n)}_{r}\coloneqq\max_{|A|\leq r}\tr(\rho_{A}H_{r}^{n})<\infty
and

\displaystyle N

\displaystyle\geq\frac{(M+1)^{2r}}{3\epsilon^{2}}\Big\{24\,\Sigma^{(\alpha)}_{r}(M)^{2}+4\Big(\Sigma_{r}^{(\alpha)}(M)+E_{r}^{(\alpha)}\Big)\epsilon\Big\}

(87)

\displaystyle\times\log\left(\frac{2\big[m(M+1)\big]^{r}}{\delta}\right),

where

M=\Big\lceil\big(4E_{r}^{(n)}/\epsilon\big)^{\frac{2}{n-\alpha}}\Big\rceil,

(88)

we have that for any region
A
of size
|A|\leq r
, it holds that

\left\|\sigma_{A}^{(N)}(M)-\rho_{A}\right\|^{(\alpha)}_{1}\leq\epsilon

(89)

with probability at least
1-\delta
. Similarly, for

\displaystyle N

\displaystyle\geq\frac{(M+1)^{2r}}{3\epsilon^{2}}\Big\{24\,\Sigma^{(\alpha)}_{r}(M)^{2}+4\Big(\Sigma_{r}^{(\alpha)}(M)+E_{r}^{(\alpha)}\Big)\epsilon\Big\}

(90)

\displaystyle\times\log\left(\frac{2L(M+1)^{r}}{\delta}\right)\,,

we have that for any set of
L
observables
O_{j}
on regions
A_{j}
of size at most
r
and with
\left\|H_{r}^{-\frac{\alpha}{2}}O_{j}H_{r}^{-\frac{\alpha}{2}}\right\|_{\infty}\leq 1
,

\max_{j}\big|\tr\big[O_{j}\,(\sigma_{A_{j}}^{(N)}(M)-\rho_{A_{j}})\big]\big|\leq\epsilon

(91)

with probability at least
1-\delta
.

Remark 10
.

We essentially recover the same dependence of the number of samples in terms of the logarithm of the number of observables/total number of modes and on the exponential of the size of the regions
A
as in the qubit setting of
[
6
]
.

Proof.

The first part of the proof consists in computing the function
f_{\mu^{\otimes m},T^{\oplus m}}
defined as in (
56
) in the limit
s\to\infty
. It is given by

\displaystyle f_{\mu^{\otimes m},T^{\oplus m}}(u)

\displaystyle\coloneqq\int\!\!d\mu^{\otimes m}(S_{1},\dots,S_{m})\,e^{-\frac{1}{2}\,u^{\intercal}(T^{\oplus m}S)^{{}^{\intercal}}(T^{\oplus m}S)\,u}

\displaystyle=\prod_{j=1}^{m}f_{\mu,T}(u_{j})\,,

with
u=(u_{1},\dots,u_{m})^{T}\in\mathbb{R}^{2m}
, where for each mode
j
we have

\displaystyle\hskip 0.0ptf_{\mu,T}(u_{j})

\displaystyle\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mathclap{\scriptsize\mbox{(i)}}}}{{=}}\int_{-\pi}^{+\pi}\frac{d\theta}{2\pi}\,e^{-\frac{1}{2}u_{j}^{\intercal}R_{\theta}^{\intercal}D_{s}R_{\theta}u_{j}}

(92)

\displaystyle\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mathclap{\scriptsize\mbox{(ii)}}}}{{=}}\int_{-\pi/2}^{+\pi/2}\frac{d\theta}{\pi}\,e^{-\frac{1}{2}u_{j}^{\intercal}R_{\theta}^{\intercal}D_{s}R_{\theta}u_{j}}

\displaystyle\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mathclap{\scriptsize\mbox{(iii)}}}}{{=}}\int_{-\pi/2}^{+\pi/2}\frac{d\theta}{\pi}\,e^{-\frac{\|u_{j}\|^{2}}{2}\,e_{1}^{\intercal}R_{\theta}^{\intercal}D_{s}R_{\theta}e_{1}}

\displaystyle=\int_{-\pi/2}^{+\pi/2}\frac{d\theta}{\pi}\,e^{-\frac{\|u_{j}\|^{2}}{2}\,\left(e^{-2s}\cos^{2}\theta+e^{2s}\sin^{2}\theta\right)}

\displaystyle\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mathclap{\scriptsize\mbox{(iv)}}}}{{=}}e^{-\frac{\|u_{j}\|^{2}\cosh(2s)}{2}}I_{0}\Big(-\tfrac{\|u_{j}\|^{2}\sinh(2s)}{2}\Big)

\displaystyle=e^{-\frac{\|u_{j}\|^{2}\cosh(2s)}{2}}I_{0}\Big(\tfrac{\|u_{j}\|^{2}\sinh(2s)}{2}\Big).

Here, in (i) we introduced the matrix
D_{s}=T_{s}^{2}
; in (ii) we noted that the integrand is invariant under rotations of
\pi
, in (iii) we observed that the integral is invariant under rotations of
u_{j}
, and thus chose to compute it for
u_{j}=\|u_{j}\|e_{1}\coloneqq\|u_{j}\|(1,0)^{\intercal}
; and in (iv) we recognised the integral representation for the modified Bessel function

I_{0}(x)\coloneqq\frac{1}{\pi}\,\int_{0}^{\pi}e^{\pm x\cos\theta}d\theta\,.

Plugging this into (
67
), we have found that

\displaystyle\chi_{\hat{\rho}}(u)

\displaystyle=\frac{e^{-\frac{1}{4}\left\|T^{\oplus m}Su\right\|^{2}-iu^{\intercal}\Omega Sx}}{f_{\mu^{\otimes m},T^{\oplus m}}(u)}\,

\displaystyle=\prod_{j=1}^{m}\,\frac{e^{\frac{\|u_{j}\|^{2}\,\operatorname{cosh}(2s)}{2}}}{I_{0}\Big(\frac{\|u_{j}\|^{2}\operatorname{sinh}(2s)}{2}\Big)}e^{-\frac{1}{4}\left\|TS_{j}u_{j}\right\|^{2}-iu_{j}^{\intercal}\Omega S_{j}^{-1}x_{j}}

\displaystyle\equiv\prod_{j=1}^{m}\,\chi_{\hat{\rho}_{j}}(u_{j})\,.

In other words,
\chi_{\hat{\rho}}
is formally the characteristic function of a tensor product of forms
\hat{\rho}=\hat{\rho}_{1}\otimes\dots\otimes\hat{\rho}_{m}
.
Moreover, by the asymptotic expansion of the modified Bessel function

\displaystyle I_{0}\Big(\tfrac{\|u_{j}\|^{2}\sinh(2s)}{2}\Big)

\displaystyle=\frac{e^{\tfrac{\|u_{j}\|^{2}\sinh(2s)}{2}}}{\sqrt{\sinh(2s)\pi}\|u_{j}\|}\Big(1+\mathcal{O}((\|u_{j}\|^{2}\sinh(2s))^{-1})\Big),

we see that

\displaystyle\chi_{\hat{\rho}_{j}}(u_{j})=\frac{\sqrt{2\sinh(2s)\pi}\|u_{j}\|\,e^{\frac{\|u_{j}\|^{2}e^{-2s}}{2}}e^{-\frac{1}{4}\|T_{s}S_{j}u_{j}\|^{2}-iu_{j}^{\intercal}\Omega S_{j}^{-1}x_{j}}}{1+\mathcal{O}((\|u_{j}\|^{2}\sinh(2s))^{-1})}

which shows that
\chi_{\hat{\rho}}(u)
can be integrated against any element of the Fock basis as soon as
s>0
. Taking the distributional limit, we find

\displaystyle\chi_{\hat{\rho}_{j}}(u_{j})\mathrel{\hbox{\hskip 12.80205pt\hskip-12.80205pt\hbox{$\xrightarrow{\makebox[19.0659pt]{$\scriptstyle\mathrm{}$}}$}\hskip-12.80205pt\hskip-10.36626pt\raisebox{-5.0pt}{\hbox{$\scriptstyle\!s\to\infty\!\,$}}\hskip-10.36626pt\hskip 12.80205pt}}2\pi\|u_{j}\|\,\delta\left((S_{j}u_{j})_{2}\right)e^{-iu_{j}^{\intercal}\Omega S_{j}^{-1}x_{j}}\,.

Next, we fix a region
A
of size
|A|=r
and consider for a fixed squeezing
s

\displaystyle\hat{\rho}_{A}(s,M)\coloneqq\bigotimes_{j\in A}\,\Big(\sum_{n_{1},n_{2}=1}^{M}\,\hat{\rho}_{j}(\ket{n_{2}}\!\!\bra{n_{1}}|)\,\ket{n_{1}}\!\!\bra{n_{2}}\Big)\,.

We have from Lemma
6
that

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\big[\hat{\rho}_{A}(s,M)\big]=\mathcal{P}_{M}(\rho_{A})

which holds for any squeezing parameter
s
. Therefore, we can take the limit
s\to\infty
above. We find

\displaystyle\hat{\rho}_{A}(s,M)=\bigotimes_{j\in A}\!\bigg(\sum_{n_{1},n_{2}=1}^{M}\!\!\ket{n_{1}}\!\!\bra{n_{2}}\int\!\!\chi_{\hat{\rho}_{j}}(u_{j})\,\chi_{\ket{n_{2}}\!\bra{n_{1}}}(u_{j})\,\frac{d^{2}u_{j}}{(2\pi)^{r}}\bigg)

(93)

so that

\displaystyle\hskip 0.0pt\hat{\rho}_{A}(s,M)

\displaystyle\mathrel{\hbox{\hskip 12.80205pt\hskip-12.80205pt\hbox{$\xrightarrow{\makebox[19.0659pt]{$\scriptstyle\mathrm{}$}}$}\hskip-12.80205pt\hskip-10.36626pt\raisebox{-5.0pt}{\hbox{$\scriptstyle\!s\to\infty\!\,$}}\hskip-10.36626pt\hskip 12.80205pt}}\hat{\rho}_{A}(M)\coloneqq\bigotimes_{j\in A}\hat{\rho}_{j}(M),

(94)

\displaystyle\hat{\rho}_{j}(M)

\displaystyle\coloneqq\sum_{n_{1},n_{2}=1}^{M}\!\!\ket{n_{1}}\!\!\bra{n_{2}}

\displaystyle\times\int\!\!|y|\,e^{-i(y,0)S_{j}^{-\intercal}\Omega S_{j}x_{j}}\,\chi_{\ket{n_{2}}\!\bra{n_{1}}}(S_{j}(y,0)^{\intercal})\,dy,

with
\mathbb{E}[\hat{\rho}_{A}(M)]=\mathcal{P}_{M}(\rho_{A})
. Then, we have seen below Equation (
85
) that, almost surely,
\|H_{r}^{\frac{\alpha}{2}}\hat{\rho}_{A}(M)H_{r}^{\frac{\alpha}{2}}\|_{\infty}\leq\Sigma^{(\alpha)}_{r}(M)
. Moreover, we have that
\|H_{r}^{\frac{\alpha}{2}}\mathcal{P}_{M}(\rho_{A})H_{r}^{\frac{\alpha}{2}}\|_{\infty}\leq E_{r}^{(\alpha)}
, therefore almost surely
\|H_{r}^{\frac{\alpha}{2}}(\hat{\rho}_{A}(M)-\mathcal{P}_{M}(\rho_{A}))H_{r}^{\frac{\alpha}{2}}\|_{\infty}\leq E_{r}^{(\alpha)}+\Sigma^{(\alpha)}_{r}(M)
. Finally,
\|\mathbb{E}[(H_{r}^{\frac{\alpha}{2}}\hat{\rho}_{A}(M)H_{r}^{\frac{\alpha}{2}})^{2}]\|_{\infty}\leq\Sigma_{r}^{(\alpha)}(M)^{2}
. We can hence use the equivalence of matrix norms together with the matrix Bernstein inequality (
50
) to get

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\Big(\big\|\sigma_{A}^{(N)}(M)-\mathcal{P}_{M}(\rho_{A})\big\|^{(\alpha)}_{1}\geq{\epsilon}\Big)

\displaystyle\leq\mathbb{P}\Big(\big\|H_{r}^{\frac{\alpha}{2}}\big(\sigma_{A}^{(N)}(M)-\mathcal{P}_{M}(\rho_{A})\big)H_{r}^{\frac{\alpha}{2}}\big\|_{\infty}\geq{\epsilon}(M+1)^{-r}\Big)

\displaystyle\leq 2(M+1)^{r}\,\operatorname{exp}\left(-\frac{3N\epsilon^{2}(M+1)^{-2r}}{6\Sigma_{r}^{(\alpha)}(M)^{2}+2(\Sigma_{r}^{(\alpha)}(M)+E_{r}^{(\alpha)})\epsilon}\right).

(95)

Therefore, by a union bound, we get that

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\Big(\exists A,\,|A|\leq r:\,\big\|\sigma_{A}^{(N)}(M)-\mathcal{P}_{M}(\rho_{A})\big\|^{(\alpha)}_{1}\geq{\epsilon}\Big)

\displaystyle\leq 2\big[m(M+1)\big]^{r}

\displaystyle\quad\times\operatorname{exp}\left(-\frac{3N\epsilon^{2}(M+1)^{-2r}}{6\Sigma_{r}^{(\alpha)}(M)^{2}+2(\Sigma_{r}^{(\alpha)}(M)+E_{r}^{(\alpha)})\epsilon}\right)\,.

(96)

Next, we use Proposition
8
and choose
M=\Big\lceil\big(4E_{r}^{(n)}/\epsilon\big)^{\frac{2}{n-\alpha}}\Big\rceil
, so that
\|\mathcal{P}_{M}(\rho_{A})-\rho_{A}\|_{1}^{(\alpha)}\leq\epsilon/2
and

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\Big(\exists A,\,|A|\leq r:\,\big\|\sigma_{A}^{(N)}(M)-\rho_{A}\big\|^{(\alpha)}_{1}\geq{\epsilon}\Big)

\displaystyle\leq\mathbb{P}\Big(\exists A,\,|A|\leq r:\,\big\|\sigma_{A}^{(N)}(M)-\mathcal{P}_{M}(\rho_{A})\big\|^{(\alpha)}_{1}\geq\frac{\epsilon}{2}\Big)

\displaystyle\leq 2\big[m\big(M+1\big)\big]^{r}

\displaystyle\quad\times\operatorname{exp}\left(-\frac{3N\epsilon^{2}(M+1)^{-2r}}{24\Sigma^{(\alpha)}_{r}(M)^{2}+4(\Sigma^{(\alpha)}_{r}(M)+E_{r}^{(\alpha)})\epsilon}\right)\,.

Therefore, choosing
N
as in the statement of the theorem, we obtain that the probability that on any subset
A
of at most
r
modes
\|\sigma_{A}^{(N)}(M)-\rho_{A}\big\|^{(\alpha)}_{1}\geq{\epsilon}
is at least
1-\delta
.

The results for a fixed number of
L
observables
O_{1},\dots,O_{L}
supported on regions
A_{1},\dots,A_{L}
of size at most
r
follow after replacing the above union bounds over regions
A
by a union bound over the observables
O_{j}
:

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\Big(\exists j:\,|\tr(O_{j}\sigma_{A_{j}}^{(N)}(M))-\tr(O_{j}\,\mathcal{P}_{M}(\rho_{A_{j}}))|\geq{\epsilon}\Big)

\displaystyle\leq 2L\,\big[(M+1)\big]^{r}\,\operatorname{exp}\left(-\frac{3N\epsilon^{2}(M+1)^{-2r}}{6\Sigma_{r}^{(\alpha)}(M)^{2}+2(\Sigma_{r}^{(\alpha)}(M)+E_{r}^{(\alpha)})\epsilon}\right)

and the result follows after replacing
m^{r}
by
L
in the estimate for
N
.

∎

V-B

Local heterodyne detection

Even simpler than homodyne detection, the simplest Gaussian shadow tomographic setting is that where the measurement employed is a heterodyne detection

\displaystyle\hskip 0.0pt\left\{\frac{1}{(2\pi)^{m/2}}\ket{x}\!\!\bra{x}\right\}_{x\in\mathds{R}^{2m}}\,,

(97)

i.e.
T=I
, and all unitaries employed are passive, i.e. such that
\big[U_{\!S}^{\vphantom{{\dagger}}},\frac{1}{2}R^{\intercal}R\big]=0
. Naturally, this is the same as requiring that
S
be (not only symplectic but also) orthogonal. Since passive unitaries send coherent states to coherent states, amounting to a rotation in that space, the effective measurement being carried out on
\rho
is the same irrespectively of
S
— the only thing changing is that the outcome is rotated. Mathematically, this means that the probability distribution of the random variable
U_{\!S}^{{\dagger}}\pazocal{D}(x)\psi\pazocal{D}(-x)U_{\!S}^{\vphantom{{\dagger}}}
, where
x
is the outcome of the heterodyne detection (
97
) on
U_{\!S}^{\vphantom{{\dagger}}}\psi U_{\!S}^{{\dagger}}
, is the same as that of the random variable
\pazocal{D}(y)\psi\pazocal{D}(-y)
, where
y
is the outcome of the heterodyne on
\psi
. This means that in the shadow tomography protocol we can skip the unitary operation altogether without losing any data. In what follows we will therefore set
S=I
(deterministically) without loss of generality.

With the above simplifications, one can see that
f_{\mu,T}(u)=e^{-\frac{1}{2}\|u\|^{2}}
, so that
\mathcal{M}=\mathcal{N}_{1}
(cf. (
40
)). Therefore, the classical shadow will have improper characteristic function

\displaystyle\chi_{\hat{\rho}}(u)=e^{\frac{1}{4}\|u\|^{2}-iu^{\intercal}\Omega x}\,.

Once again, the characteristic function tensorises: given
u=(u_{1},\dots,u_{m})^{\intercal}
and
x=(x_{1},\dots,x_{m})^{\intercal}
:

\displaystyle\chi_{\hat{\rho}}(u)=\prod_{j=1}^{m}\chi_{\hat{\rho}_{j}}(u_{j})

(98)

where

\displaystyle\chi_{\hat{\rho}_{j}}(u_{j})\coloneqq e^{\frac{1}{4}\|u_{j}\|^{2}-iu_{j}^{\intercal}\Omega x_{j}}\,.

(99)

Now, if we want to use the classical shadow to compute expectation values, we can formally use Plancherel’s relation

\displaystyle\hskip 0.0pt\hat{\rho}(O)

\displaystyle=\int\frac{d^{2m}u}{(2\pi)^{m}}\,\chi_{\hat{\rho}}(u)\,\chi_{O}(u)

(100)

\displaystyle=\int\frac{d^{2m}u}{(2\pi)^{m}}\,e^{+\frac{1}{4}\|u\|^{2}-iu^{\intercal}\Omega x}\,\chi_{O}(u)\,.

In order for this to make sense, we should make sure that not only
O
is trace class (instead of bounded), but also that
\chi_{O}(u)
decays sufficiently rapidly, for instance like
\sim e^{-\frac{\lambda}{4}\|u\|^{2}}
, with
\lambda>1
. This decay is too fast — but
barely
too fast — to be useful in practice. For instance, if
O
has a finite expansion in the Fock basis then
\chi_{O}(u)\sim p(u)e^{-\frac{1}{4}\|u\|^{2}}
as
\|u\|\to\infty
, where
p(u)
is some polynomial of the entries of
u
. We get rid of the diverging Gaussian in (
100
), but not of the diverging polynomial. In order to take care of this issue, we make use of the approximations
\widetilde{Z}_{\mathbf{n}_{1}\mathbf{n}_{2}}
of the Schwartz operators
\ket{\mathbf{n}_{1}}\!\!\bra{\mathbf{n}_{2}}
as well as of the auxiliary map
\widetilde{\mathcal{P}}_{M}
and the corresponding matrices
\widetilde{\sigma}_{A}^{(N)}(M)
introduced in (
74
), resp. in (
83
). We consider the matrices

\displaystyle\hat{\rho}_{A}(M)\coloneqq\sum_{\mathbf{n}_{1},\mathbf{n}_{2}\in\{0,...,M\}^{|A|}}\,\hat{\rho}(\widetilde{Z}_{\mathbf{n}_{2}\mathbf{n}_{1}})\,\ket{\mathbf{n}_{1}}\!\!\bra{\mathbf{n}_{2}},\quad\text{where}

\displaystyle\hat{\rho}(\widetilde{Z}_{\mathbf{n}_{2}\mathbf{n}_{1}})\coloneqq\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2r}}\widetilde{\chi}_{\mathbf{n}_{2}\mathbf{n}_{1}}(u)\,e^{\frac{1}{4}\|u\|^{2}-iu^{\intercal}\Omega x}\,\frac{d^{2r}u}{(2\pi)^{r}}\,

where
\widetilde{\chi}_{\mathbf{n}_{2}\mathbf{n}_{1}}
is defined in (
75
). From Lemma
6
,
\mathbb{E}\big[\hat{\rho}_{A}(M)\big]=\widetilde{\mathcal{P}}_{M}(\rho_{A})
. For
\alpha\geq 0
,
R>0
and
r,M\in\mathbb{N}
, we introduce the matrix norm

\displaystyle\widetilde{\Sigma}^{(\alpha)}_{r}(M,R)\coloneqq\left\|K_{r}(M,R)\right\|_{\infty}\,,

where the entries of the
(M+1)^{r}\times(M+1)^{r}
matrix
K_{r}(M,R)
are defined by

\displaystyle K_{r}(M,R)_{\mathbf{n_{1}},\mathbf{n_{2}}}

\displaystyle\ \,\coloneqq\left((1\!+\!|\mathbf{n}_{1}|)(1\!+\!|\mathbf{n}_{2}|)\right)^{\frac{\alpha}{2}}\int_{|u|\leq R}\!\!|\widetilde{\chi}_{\mathbf{n}_{2}\mathbf{n}_{1}}(u)|\,e^{\frac{1}{4}\|u\|^{2}}\frac{d^{2r}u}{(2\pi)^{r}}\,,

and
\mathbf{n_{1}},\mathbf{n_{2}}\in\{0,\ldots,M\}^{r}
.
With these definitions, we can write the inequality

\displaystyle\hskip 0.0pt\left\|H_{r}^{\frac{\alpha}{2}}\hat{\rho}_{A}(M)\,H_{r}^{\frac{\alpha}{2}}\right\|_{\infty}\leq\widetilde{\Sigma}^{(\alpha)}_{r}(M,R)\,.

(101)

As before, we then denote

\displaystyle\sigma_{A}^{(N)}(M)\coloneqq\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\,\hat{\rho}^{(i)}_{A}(M)\,,

where
\hat{\rho}^{(i)}_{A}(M)
are i.i.d. random matrices of law the one of
\hat{\rho}_{A}(M)
. Our second main result is stated below:

Theorem 11
.

With the notation introduced above, given
0\leq\alpha<n
,
r\in\mathbb{N}
such that
E^{(n)}_{r}\coloneqq\max_{|A|\leq r}\tr(\rho_{A}H_{r}^{n})<\infty
, and
0<\eta<R
, set

\displaystyle\hskip 0.0ptM\!\coloneqq\min\!\Big\{

\displaystyle M^{\prime}\!\!\in\!\mathbb{N}:\eta^{2}>2M^{\prime 2}

(102)

\displaystyle 2(1\!+\!M^{\prime})^{\frac{\alpha-n}{2}}\!E_{r}^{(n)}+\delta_{0}\big(\eta,M^{\prime}\!\!,\tfrac{\alpha}{2},r\big)\leq\tfrac{\epsilon}{2}\Big\}\,,

with
\delta_{0}
defined as in Proposition
8
. Then for any

\displaystyle\hskip 0.0ptN\geq\inf_{0<\eta<R}

\displaystyle\frac{(M+1)^{2r}}{3\epsilon^{2}}\log\left(\frac{2\big[m(M+1)\big]^{r}}{\delta}\right)

(103)

\displaystyle\Big[24\,\widetilde{\Sigma}^{(\alpha)}_{r}(M\!,R)^{2}

\displaystyle+4\Big(\widetilde{\Sigma}_{r}^{(\alpha)}(M\!,R)+E_{r}^{(\alpha)}+\delta_{0}(\eta,M,\tfrac{\alpha}{2},r)\Big)\epsilon\Big]\,,

we have that for any region
A
of size
|A|\leq r
, it holds that

\displaystyle\hskip 0.0pt\left\|\sigma_{A}^{(N)}(M)-\rho_{A}\right\|^{(\alpha)}_{1}\leq\epsilon

(104)

with probability at least
1-\delta
. Similarly, for

\displaystyle\hskip 0.0ptN\geq\inf_{0<\eta<R}

\displaystyle\frac{(M+1)^{2r}}{3\epsilon^{2}}\log\left(\frac{2L(M+1)^{r}}{\delta}\right)

(105)

\displaystyle\Big[24\,\widetilde{\Sigma}^{(\alpha)}_{r}(M\!,R)^{2}

\displaystyle+4\Big(\widetilde{\Sigma}_{r}^{(\alpha)}(M\!,R)+E_{r}^{(\alpha)}+\delta_{0}(\eta,M,\tfrac{\alpha}{2},r)\Big)\epsilon\Big]\,,

we have that for any set of
L
observables
O_{j}
on regions
A_{j}
of size at most
r
and with
\left\|H_{r}^{-\frac{\alpha}{2}}O_{j}H_{r}^{-\frac{\alpha}{2}}\right\|_{\infty}\leq 1
, it holds that

\displaystyle\hskip 0.0pt\max_{j}\big|\tr\big[O_{j}\,(\sigma_{A_{j}}^{(N)}(M)-\rho_{A_{j}})\big]\big|\leq\epsilon

(106)

with probability at least
1-\delta
.

Proof.

By construction, we have almost surely that
\|H_{r}^{\frac{\alpha}{2}}\hat{\rho}_{A}(M)H_{r}^{\frac{\alpha}{2}}\|_{\infty}\leq\widetilde{\Sigma}^{(\alpha)}_{r}(M,R)
. Moreover, we have that

\displaystyle\|H_{r}^{\frac{\alpha}{2}}\widetilde{\mathcal{P}}_{M}(\rho_{A})H_{r}^{\frac{\alpha}{2}}\|_{\infty}

\displaystyle\leq\|\widetilde{\mathcal{P}}_{M}(\rho_{A})\|_{1}^{(\alpha)}

\displaystyle\leq\|(\widetilde{\mathcal{P}}_{M}-\mathcal{P}_{M})(\rho_{A})\|_{1}^{(\alpha)}+\|\mathcal{P}_{M}(\rho_{A})\|_{1}^{(\alpha)}

\displaystyle\leq\delta_{0}(\eta,M,\frac{\alpha}{2},r)+E_{r}^{(\alpha)}

for
\eta^{2}>2M^{2}
, by Lemma
7
. Therefore almost surely
\|H_{r}^{\frac{\alpha}{2}}(\hat{\rho}_{A}(M)-\widetilde{\mathcal{P}}_{M}(\rho_{A}))H_{r}^{\frac{\alpha}{2}}\|_{\infty}\leq E_{r}^{(\alpha)}+\delta_{0}(\eta,M,\frac{\alpha}{2},r)+\widetilde{\Sigma}^{(\alpha)}_{r}(M,R)
. Finally,
\|\mathbb{E}[(H_{r}^{\frac{\alpha}{2}}\hat{\rho}_{A}(M)H_{r}^{\frac{\alpha}{2}})^{2}]\|_{\infty}\leq\widetilde{\Sigma}_{r}^{(\alpha)}(M,R)^{2}
. We hence use the matrix Bernstein inequality (
50
) to get in terms of a constant
C=C(N,\epsilon,R,\alpha,r)
with

C\coloneqq\frac{3N\epsilon^{2}(M+1)^{-2r}}{6\widetilde{\Sigma}_{r}^{(\alpha)}(M,R)^{2}+2(\widetilde{\Sigma}_{r}^{(\alpha)}(M,R)+\delta_{0}(\eta,M,\frac{\alpha}{2},r)+E_{r}^{(\alpha)})\epsilon}

the estimate

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\Big(\big\|\widetilde{\sigma}_{A}^{(N)}(M)-\widetilde{\mathcal{P}}_{M}(\rho_{A})\big\|^{(\alpha)}_{1}\geq{\epsilon}\Big)

(107)

\displaystyle\leq 2(M+1)^{r}\,\operatorname{exp}\left(-C(N,\epsilon,R,\alpha,r)\right)\,.

Therefore, by a union bound, we get that

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\Big(\exists A,\,|A|\leq r:\,\big\|\widetilde{\sigma}_{A}^{(N)}(M)-\widetilde{\mathcal{P}}_{M}(\rho_{A})\big\|^{(\alpha)}_{1}\geq{\epsilon}\Big)

\displaystyle\leq 2\big[m(M+1)\big]^{r}\,\operatorname{exp}\left(-C(N,\epsilon,R,\alpha,r)\right)\,.

Next, we use Proposition
8
and choose
M=\min\{M^{\prime}\in\mathbb{N}:\,2(1+M^{\prime})^{\frac{\alpha-n}{2}}\,E_{r}^{(n)}+\delta_{0}\big(\eta,M^{\prime},\frac{\alpha}{2},r\big)\leq\frac{\epsilon}{2}\}
, so that
\|\widetilde{\mathcal{P}}_{M}(\rho_{A})-\rho_{A}\|_{1}^{(\alpha)}\leq\epsilon/2
, and

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\Big(\exists A,\,|A|\leq r:\,\big\|\widetilde{\sigma}_{A}^{(N)}(M)-\rho_{A}\big\|^{(\alpha)}_{1}\geq{\epsilon}\Big)

\displaystyle\leq\mathbb{P}\Big(\exists A,\,|A|\leq r:\,\big\|\widetilde{\sigma}_{A}^{(N)}(M)-\widetilde{\mathcal{P}}_{M}(\rho_{A})\big\|^{(\alpha)}_{1}\geq\frac{\epsilon}{2}\Big)\,.

Therefore, choosing
N
as in the statement of the theorem, we obtain that the probability that on any subset
A
of at most
r
modes
\|\widetilde{\sigma}_{A}^{(N)}(M)-\rho_{A}\big\|^{(\alpha)}_{1}\geq{\epsilon}
is at least
1-\delta
.

The results for a fixed number of
L
observables
O_{1},\dots,O_{L}
supported on regions
A_{1},\dots,A_{L}
of size at most
r
follow after replacing the above union bounds over regions
A
by a union bound over the observables
O_{j}
:

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\Big(\exists j:\,|\tr(O_{j}\widetilde{\sigma}_{A_{j}}^{(N)}(M))-\tr(O_{j}\,\widetilde{\mathcal{P}}_{M}(\rho_{A_{j}}))|\geq{\epsilon}\Big)

\displaystyle\leq 2L\,\big[(M+1)\big]^{r}\,\operatorname{exp}\left(-C(N,\epsilon,R,\alpha,r)\right)

and the result follows after replacing
m^{r}
by
L
in the estimate for
N
.

∎

V-C

Comparison to related work

In a concurrent and independent work
[
23
]
, the authors also developed a shadow tomography protocol for bosonic systems. Their framework is equivalent to ours in the homodyne and heterodyne settings. Let us consider the former in the one-mode setting: the shadows in
[
23
]
are constructed in the Fock basis by taking
\check{\rho}_{mn}\coloneqq e^{i(m-n)\theta}f_{mn}(x)
, after making a random rotation
\theta
and obtaining
x
from a measurement in the position axis. The functions
f_{mn}=f_{nm}
are the so-called pattern functions
[
38
,
74
]
and are defined using Fock state wavefunctions
\psi_{m}
(
m^{th}
energy eigenstate of the harmonic
oscillator) and
\phi_{n}
(
n^{th}
non-normalizable solution of
the Schrödinger equation of a harmonic oscillator) as

\displaystyle f_{mn}(x)\coloneqq\frac{\partial}{\partial x}(\psi_{m}(x)\phi_{n}(x)),\qquad n\geq m\,.

Then, they construct the following estimator from
N
copies of the unknown state:

\displaystyle\check{\sigma}^{(N)}_{mn}\coloneqq\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\,\check{\rho}^{(i)}_{mn}=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}e^{i(m-n)\theta_{i}}f_{mn}(x_{\theta_{i}})\,,

where the angles
\theta_{i}
are picked uniformly at random, and
x_{\theta}=x^{\prime}_{\theta}\oplus x^{\prime\prime}_{\theta}=\sqrt{2\pi}x^{\prime}\oplus\frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi}}x^{\prime\prime}
in the notations of Section
V-A
. It turns out that these pattern functions can be equivalently defined in terms of their Fourier transforms (eq. (31) of
[
75
]
, see also eqs. (20)-(21) of
[
76
]
): for
n\geq m
:

\displaystyle\widetilde{f}_{mn}(t)

\displaystyle\coloneqq\int e^{-itx}\,f_{mn}(x)\,dx

\displaystyle=\pi(-i)^{n-m}\,\sqrt{\frac{2^{m-n}m!}{n!}}\,|t|\,t^{n-m}e^{-\frac{t^{2}}{4}}\,L_{m}^{(n-m)}\Big(\frac{t^{2}}{2}\Big)\,.

Therefore
|\widetilde{f}_{mn}(x_{\theta})|
corresponds to the coordinate
\Sigma_{1}^{(0)}(M)_{mn}
for
m,n\leq M
. As observed in
[
59
]
, the pattern functions have been already studied for tomography purposes (see e.g. Lemma 7.1 in
[
61
]
). In particular,

\displaystyle\sum_{0\leq j\leq k\leq M}\|f_{kj}\|^{2}_{\infty}=\mathcal{O}(M^{7/3})\,.

These bounds can be directly used to get control over
\|\Sigma_{1}^{(0)}(M)\|_{\infty}
. That way, we recover Theorem III.1 of
[
23
]
. Slightly better bounds can be achieved using estimates in Lemma 4 of
[
76
]
.
It is also worth observing that, from the proof of Theorem
9
, one can extend these results to more realistic models of homodyning with finitely squeezed resources.

VI
Learning non-linear functionals of the states

So far, we considered properties of the quantum system which could be related to local linear functionals of the unknown state. In finite dimensions, a simple trick permits the estimation of non-linear functionals, e.g. the entropy of entanglement
[
6
,
77
]
. Here, we show that the technique developed in these works combined with recent energy-constrained continuity bounds provide us with a similar extension. For sake of conciseness, we will only consider the entropy of a reduced CV state over
r\leq m
modes of the
m
-mode state:

\displaystyle S(\rho_{A})\coloneqq-\tr(\rho_{A}\ln\rho_{A})\,,

where we denote by
A
the set of those modes, so that
|A|=r
. We also assume that the unknown state
\rho
has locally finite energy:
\tr((I+N^{(j)})\rho_{j})\coloneqq E<\infty
, where
N^{(j)}
corresponds to the number operator on mode
j
, and where
\rho_{j}
is the reduced state on mode
j\in A
. Here, we also restrict ourselves to the shadows constructed in our local homodyne detection scheme of (
V-A
), although similar conclusions can be drawn from shadows arising from a local heterodyne detection and states with higher moment constraints. Given the shadow

\sigma_{A}^{(N)}(M)
arising from the homodyne scheme of (
V-A
), and
d_{p}\in\mathbb{N}
, we denote the matrix polynomial

\displaystyle H^{(d_{p})}(\sigma_{A}^{(N)}(M))

\displaystyle\coloneqq-\tr(\sigma_{A}^{(N)}(M)-P_{M})

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad+\sum_{k=2}^{d_{p}}\frac{\tr\big[(P_{M}-\sigma_{A}^{(N)}(M))^{k}\big]}{k(k-1)}\,.

Theorem 12
.

With the notation of the previous paragraph, we have that for any
\epsilon>0
and
M\in\mathbb{N}
,

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\Big(\exists A,\,|A|\leq r:\,|S(\rho_{A})-H^{\big(\big\lceil\frac{3(M+1)^{r}}{\epsilon}\big\rceil\big)}(\sigma_{A}^{(N)}(M))|\geq\epsilon\Big)

\displaystyle\leq 2\big[m(M+1)\big]^{r}\,\operatorname{exp}\left(-\frac{3N\epsilon^{\prime 2}(M+1)^{-2r}}{6\Sigma_{r}^{(0)}(M)^{2}+2(\Sigma_{r}^{(0)}(M)+1)\epsilon^{\prime}}\right)\,,

where
\epsilon^{\prime}=\frac{\epsilon^{2}}{12(M+1)^{r}e}2^{-4(M+1)^{r}/\epsilon}
.

Proof.

By
[
78
, Theorem 3]
, we have that for all
\gamma\in[0,rE/(1+rE)]
, and any state
\rho_{A}^{\prime}
on subsystem
A
with
\|\rho_{A}-\rho_{A}^{\prime}\|_{1}\leq 2\gamma
,

\displaystyle|S(\rho_{A})-S(\rho_{A}^{\prime})|\leq h(\gamma)+rE\,h\big(\gamma/(rE)\big)\,,

(108)

where
h(.)
denotes the binary entropy. Next, we pick
\rho_{A}^{\prime}\coloneqq\mathcal{P}_{M}(\rho_{A})/\tr(\mathcal{P}_{M}(\rho_{A}))
. By Proposition
8
, we have that, assuming
1+M>4r^{2}E^{2}
,

\displaystyle\|\rho_{A}-\rho_{A}^{\prime}\|_{1}=\frac{\|\tr(\mathcal{P}_{M}(\rho_{A}))\rho_{A}-\mathcal{P}_{M}(\rho_{A})\|_{1}}{\tr(\mathcal{P}_{M}(\rho_{A}))}\,

\displaystyle\leq\frac{|\tr(\mathcal{P}_{M}(\rho_{A}))-1|+\|\rho_{A}-\mathcal{P}_{M}(\rho_{A})\|_{1}}{1-|\tr(\mathcal{P}_{M}(\rho_{A}))-1|}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{2\|\rho_{A}-\mathcal{P}_{M}(\rho_{A})\|_{1}}{1-\|\mathcal{P}_{M}(\rho_{A})-\rho_{A}\|_{1}}\,

\displaystyle\leq\frac{4{rE}}{(1+M)^{\frac{1}{2}}-2rE}\equiv 2\gamma\,.

Next, we approximate
S(\rho_{A}^{\prime})
in terms of
S(\mathcal{P}_{M}(\rho_{A}))
, where the entropy of a sub-normalised positive trace class operator
A
is defined as
S(A)\coloneqq-\tr A\ln A
: denoting
\tr(\mathcal{P}_{M}(\rho_{A}))\coloneqq\lambda\leq 1
,

\displaystyle S(\rho_{A}^{\prime})-S(\mathcal{P}_{M}(\rho_{A}))

\displaystyle=-\tr\frac{\mathcal{P}_{M}(\rho_{A})}{\lambda}\ln\frac{\mathcal{P}_{M}(\rho_{A})}{\lambda}+\tr\mathcal{P}_{M}(\rho_{A})\ln\mathcal{P}_{M}(\rho_{A})

\displaystyle=\frac{1-\lambda}{1-(1-\lambda)}\,S(\mathcal{P}_{M}(\rho_{A}))+\ln\lambda

\displaystyle\leq\frac{\|\rho_{A}-\mathcal{P}_{M}(\rho_{A})\|_{1}}{1-\|\rho_{A}-\mathcal{P}_{M}(\rho_{A})\|_{1}}\,S(\mathcal{P}_{M}(\rho_{A}))

\displaystyle\leq\gamma\,S(\mathcal{P}_{M}(\rho_{A}))

\displaystyle\leq r\ln(M+1)\,\gamma\,.

(109)

Next, we use a polynomial approximation of
S(\mathcal{P}_{M}(\rho_{A}))
as was already done in
[
77
]
:

\displaystyle H^{(d_{p})}(\mathcal{P}_{M}(\rho_{A}))

\displaystyle\coloneqq-\tr(\mathcal{P}_{M}(\rho_{A})-P_{M})+\sum_{k=2}^{d_{p}}\frac{\tr\big[(P_{M}-\mathcal{P}_{M}(\rho_{A}))^{k}\big]}{k(k-1)}

where
d_{p}
is the truncation degree which we will choose later. Since
P_{M}
projects onto a subspace of dimension
(M+1)^{r}
, a simple extension of the proof leading to
[
77
, Equation (K46)]
gives us

\displaystyle|S(\mathcal{P}_{M}(\rho_{A}))-H^{(d_{p})}(\mathcal{P}_{M}(\rho_{A}))|\leq\frac{(M+1)^{r}}{d_{p}}\,.

(110)

Now, we re-express the function
H^{(d_{p})}(\mathcal{P}_{M}(\rho_{A}))
as a linear function of
(\mathcal{P}_{M}(\rho_{A}))^{\otimes d_{p}}
:

\displaystyle H^{(d_{p})}(\mathcal{P}_{M}(\rho_{A}))

(111)

\displaystyle=-1+(M\!+\!1)^{r}+\sum_{k=2}^{d_{p}}\frac{1}{k(k-1)}\,\sum_{j=0}^{k}\binom{k}{j}\,(-1)^{j}\tr((\mathcal{P}_{M}(\rho_{A}))^{j})

\displaystyle=-1+(M\!+\!1)^{r}

\displaystyle+\sum_{j=0}^{d_{p}}\frac{1}{j!}\,\tr(S_{j}(\mathcal{P}_{M}(\rho_{A}))^{\otimes j})\,(-1)^{j}\!\!\sum_{k=\max\{2,j\}}^{d_{p}}\frac{(k\!-\!2)!}{(k\!-\!j)!}

\displaystyle\equiv-1+(M\!+\!1)^{r}+\sum_{j=0}^{d_{p}}\frac{1}{j!}\,C_{j}\,\tr(S_{j}(\mathcal{P}_{M}(\rho_{A}))^{\otimes j})\,,

where
S_{j}
is a generalised swap operator over
j
subsystems. Let us now chose
\sigma_{A}^{(N)}(M)
to be the classical shadow arising from the homodyne scheme of (
V-A
). We have, similarly to
[
77
, Lemma 11]

\displaystyle|H^{(d_{p})}(\mathcal{P}_{M}(\rho_{A}))-H^{(d_{p})}(\sigma_{A}^{(N)}(M))|

(112)

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{j=0}^{d_{p}}\,\frac{1}{j!}|C_{j}|\,\|(\mathcal{P}_{M}(\rho_{A}))^{\otimes j}-(\sigma_{A}^{(N)}(M))^{\otimes j}\|_{1}\,.

Next, we estimate the trace distances in the above summand. By an arbitrary labelling
A_{1},\dots,A_{j}
of the subsystems so that
(\mathcal{P}_{M}(\rho_{A}))^{\otimes j}\coloneqq\rho_{A_{1}}^{\prime}\otimes\dots\otimes\rho_{A_{j}}^{\prime}
and
(\sigma_{A}^{(N)}(M))^{\otimes j}\coloneqq\sigma_{A_{1}}\otimes\dots\otimes\sigma_{A_{j}}
, we have

\displaystyle\|(\mathcal{P}_{M}(\rho_{A}))^{\otimes j}-(\sigma_{A}^{(N)}(M))^{\otimes j}\|_{1}

(113)

\displaystyle=\|\rho_{A_{1}}^{\prime}\otimes\dots\otimes\rho_{A_{j}}^{\prime}-\sigma_{A_{1}}\otimes\dots\otimes\sigma_{A_{j}}\|_{1}

\displaystyle=\big\|(\rho_{A_{1}}^{\prime}-\sigma_{A_{1}})\otimes\rho_{A_{2}}^{\prime}\otimes\dots\otimes\rho_{A_{j}}^{\prime}

\displaystyle+\sigma_{A_{1}}\otimes(\rho_{A_{2}}^{\prime}\otimes\dots\otimes\rho_{A_{j}}^{\prime}-\sigma_{A_{2}}\otimes\dots\otimes\sigma_{A_{j}})\big\|_{1}

\displaystyle\leq\|\rho_{A_{1}}^{\prime}-\sigma_{A_{1}}\|_{1}\|\rho_{A_{2}}^{\prime}\|_{1}\dots\|\rho_{A_{j}}^{\prime}\|_{1}

\displaystyle+\|\sigma_{A_{1}}\|_{1}\,\big\|\rho_{A_{2}}^{\prime}\otimes\dots\otimes\rho_{A_{j}}^{\prime}-\sigma_{A_{2}}\otimes\dots\otimes\sigma_{A_{j}}\big\|_{1}

\displaystyle\leq\|\rho_{A_{1}}^{\prime}-\sigma_{A_{1}}\|_{1}

\displaystyle+(1+\|\rho_{A_{1}}^{\prime}\!-\!\sigma_{A_{1}}\|_{1})\,\big\|\rho_{A_{2}}^{\prime}\!\otimes\!\dots\!\otimes\!\rho_{A_{j}}^{\prime}-\sigma_{A_{2}}\!\otimes\!\dots\!\otimes\!\sigma_{A_{j}}\big\|_{1}.

Now, we recall that the homodyne tomography protocol provides us with the following concentration bound borrowed from (
V-A
)

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\Big(\exists A,\,|A|\leq r:\,\big\|\sigma_{A}^{(N)}(M)-\mathcal{P}_{M}(\rho_{A})\big\|_{1}\geq{\epsilon^{\prime}}\Big)

\displaystyle\leq 2\big[m(M+1)\big]^{r}\,

\displaystyle\quad\times\operatorname{exp}\left(-\frac{3N\epsilon^{\prime 2}(M+1)^{-2r}}{6\Sigma_{r}^{(0)}(M)^{2}+2(\Sigma_{r}^{(0)}(M)+1)\epsilon^{\prime}}\right)\,.

(114)

Plugging this bound into (
113
), we have that with high probability

\displaystyle\|(\rho_{A}^{\prime})^{\otimes j}-(\sigma_{A}^{(N)}(M))^{\otimes j}\|_{1}

\displaystyle\leq\epsilon^{\prime}+\big(1+\epsilon^{\prime}\big)\|(\mathcal{P}_{M}(\rho_{A}))^{\otimes j-1}-(\sigma_{A}^{(N)}(M))^{\otimes j-1}\|_{1}\,.

After iterating the procedure
j
times, we find

\displaystyle\|(\mathcal{P}_{M}(\rho_{A}))^{\otimes j}-(\sigma_{A}^{(N)}(M))^{\otimes j}\|_{1}

\displaystyle\leq\epsilon^{\prime}\sum_{i=0}^{j-1}\big(1+\epsilon^{\prime}\big)^{i}\leq\big(1+\epsilon^{\prime}\big)^{j}-1\,.

Into (
112
), we have that

\displaystyle|H^{(d_{p})}(\mathcal{P}_{M}(\rho_{A}))-H^{(d_{p})}(\sigma_{A}^{(N)}(M))|

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{j=0}^{d_{p}}\frac{1}{j!}|C_{j}|\,\Big(\Big(1+\epsilon^{\prime}\Big)^{j}-1\Big)

\displaystyle\leq\big(\big(1+\epsilon^{\prime}\big)^{d_{p}}-1\big)\,\sum_{j=0}^{d_{p}}\,\frac{|C_{j}|}{j!}

\displaystyle\leq\big(\big(1+\epsilon^{\prime}\big)^{d_{p}}-1\big)\,2^{d_{p}}\,.

(115)

Then, combining (
108
), (
109
), (
110
) and (
115
), we end up with

\displaystyle|S(\rho_{A})-H^{(d_{p})}(\sigma_{A}^{(N)}(M))|

\displaystyle\leq h(\gamma)+rEh\Big(\frac{\gamma}{rE}\Big)+2r\ln(M+1)\gamma

\displaystyle\quad+\frac{(M+1)^{r}}{d_{p}}+\big(\big(1+\epsilon^{\prime}\big)^{d_{p}}-1\big)\,2^{d_{p}}\,.

Now, the first three terms on the right-hand side above are small for
M
large enough, the fourth term is small for
d_{p}
large enough, and the last term is small for
N
large enough. The result follows after choosing
M
so that the first three terms are smaller than
\epsilon/3
,
d_{p}=\Big\lceil\frac{3(M+1)^{r}}{\epsilon}\Big\rceil
so that the fourth term is smaller than
\epsilon/3
, and finally
\epsilon^{\prime}=\frac{\epsilon^{2}}{12(M+1)^{r}e}2^{-4(M+1)^{r}/\epsilon}
so that the last term is smaller than
\epsilon/3
.
∎

VII
Discretisation scheme via quasi Monte-Carlo integration

Since the objects we are manipulating in this paper are defined on continuous metric spaces, we need to explain how to devise an efficient description
\sigma^{\operatorname{dis}}_{N}
of the estimator state
\sigma_{N}
in terms of a number of discrete parameters which scales at most polynomially with the number
m
of modes and such that
\tr(O(\sigma^{\operatorname{dis}}_{N}-\sigma_{N}))
can be controlled for the observables
O
for which we want to learn the average
\tr(\rho O)
. One natural strategy consists e.g. in approximating the coefficients
\tr(\widetilde{Z}_{\mathbf{n}_{1}\mathbf{n}_{2}}\sigma_{N})
of the matrix
\widetilde{\mathcal{P}}_{{M}}(\sigma_{N})
. Since the characteristic function of
\widetilde{Z}_{\mathbf{n}_{1}\mathbf{n}_{2}}
is compactly supported in a ball
B(0,R)
around the origin, it suffices to store its values on a net of small enough mesh.

In order to evaluate traces
\tr(\tilde{Z}_{\mathbf{n}_{1}\mathbf{n}_{2}}\sigma_{N}),
in an efficient way, we shall employ quasi Monte-Carlo techniques using the isometry (
49
).
We start by recalling that an
L^{1}
function
u:\Omega\to\mathbb{R}
is of bounded variation if

\displaystyle\operatorname{TV}(u)

\displaystyle\coloneqq\sup\left\{\int_{\Omega}u(x)\operatorname{div}(\phi(x));\phi\in C_{c}^{1}(\Omega,\mathbb{R}^{n});\|\phi\|_{\infty}\leq 1\right\}

\displaystyle\quad<\infty\,

and denote by
\operatorname{TV}(K,\mathbb{R})
the space of functions of bounded variation. In particular, for
u\in C^{1}(\Omega,\mathbb{R}^{n})
one just has

\displaystyle\operatorname{TV}(u)=\int_{\Omega}|\nabla u(x)|\ dx.

For a complex valued function to be of bounded variation, we accordingly require that both its real and imaginary part are of bounded variation.
Let
K=[-l_{j},l_{j}]^{m}
be a compact set and
\ell:[0,1]^{m}\to K
a linear map. We set
S=\{t_{k}\}_{k\in\mathbb{N}},
where
t_{k}\in[0,1]^{m}
is a
Halton sequence
, see
[
79
]
for a definition, in a pairwise prime basis
b_{1},..,b_{m}.

We then introduce for
f\in\operatorname{TV}(K,\mathbb{R})
, the numerical integral

I_{K}(f,k)=\frac{1}{k}\sum_{i=1}^{k}f(\ell(t_{i})).

It then follows that there exists a constant
C(b_{1},...,b_{d})
independent of both
f
and
N
such that, see
[
79
, Theorem
2.11
]
,

\displaystyle\Big|I_{K}(f,k)-\int_{K}f(x)\ dx\Big|

\displaystyle\leq\operatorname{TV}(f)\,C(\ell(t_{1}),...,\ell(t_{m}))\,\frac{\log(k)^{m}}{k}.

Thus, this technique allows us to approximate high-dimensional integrals with errors that depend only very mildly on the number of modes
m.
We can then apply this construction to

f(x)=\frac{\chi_{|\mathbf{n}_{1}\rangle\langle\mathbf{n}_{2}|}(x)\chi_{\sigma_{N}}(x)\prod_{j=1}^{m}\xi_{\eta,R}(x_{j})}{(2\pi)^{m}},

with
K=[-R,R]^{m}.

VIII-A

Vacuum state

We start with the simplest example
\rho=\ket{0}\!\!\bra{0}
to fix ideas. In this case
|\langle 0|\alpha\rangle|^{2}=e^{-|\alpha|^{2}/2}
.
Measurements of
\alpha
are then standard normally distributed
\alpha\sim\mathcal{N}_{\mathbb{R}^{2}}(0,1).

Considering then
N=50
experimental realisations,
\alpha_{1},...,\alpha_{N}\in\mathbb{R}^{2},
the trial quantum characteristic function is

\chi_{\sigma_{N}}(u)=\frac{e^{\frac{|u|^{2}}{4}}}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}e^{-iu^{T}\Omega\alpha_{i}}.

The function is illustrated in Figure
1
. Reduced particle characteristic functions for up to
1000
oscillators are illustrated in Figure
5
. This yields an approximation of the quantum characteristic function on compact sets around zero. Due to the exponentially increasing function
e^{\frac{|u|^{2}}{4}}
, this approximation is only local for fixed
N
. We also plot the variance of the true characteristic function
\chi
vs the reconstructed characteristic function
\tilde{\chi}
at
N
grid points
(x_{n})_{i=1}^{N}
with

in some domain
D

\mathbf{V}_{N,D}=\frac{\sum_{i=1}^{N}|\chi_{i}(x_{n})-\tilde{\chi}_{i}(x_{n})|^{2}}{\operatorname{vol}(D)N}.

VIII-B

Cat qubit states

For our second example, we consider a non-Gaussian, one-mode pure quantum state called cat state. These states are used in quantum error correction
[
21
,
24
]
. Given a coherent state
|\alpha\rangle
, we denote the cat states

\displaystyle|+\rangle_{\operatorname{Cat}}=\frac{|\alpha\rangle+|-\alpha\rangle}{\mathcal{N}_{+}},\quad|-\rangle_{\operatorname{Cat}}=\frac{|\alpha\rangle-|-\alpha\rangle}{\mathcal{N}_{-}}\,,

with normalisation constants

\displaystyle\mathcal{N}_{\pm}\coloneqq\sqrt{(\bra{\alpha}\pm\bra{-\alpha})(\ket{\alpha}\pm\ket{-\alpha})}

\displaystyle=\sqrt{2\pm(\langle\alpha|-\alpha\rangle+\langle-\alpha|\alpha\rangle)}=\sqrt{2(1\pm e^{-2|\alpha|^{2}})}.

(116)

Here,
\langle x|\pm\rangle_{\operatorname{Cat}}=\frac{\langle x|\alpha\rangle\pm\langle x|-\alpha\rangle}{\mathcal{N}_{\pm}},
where
\langle x|\alpha\rangle=e^{-\frac{|x|^{2}+|\alpha|^{2}}{4}+\frac{\alpha\bar{x}}{2}}
such that
\langle-\alpha|\alpha\rangle=\langle\alpha|-\alpha\rangle=e^{-|\alpha|^{2}}.
This way, we can define

|0\rangle_{\operatorname{Cat}}\coloneqq\frac{|+\rangle_{\operatorname{Cat}}+|-\rangle_{\operatorname{Cat}}}{\sqrt{2}}\text{ and }|1\rangle_{\operatorname{Cat}}\coloneqq\frac{|+\rangle_{\operatorname{Cat}}-|-\rangle_{\operatorname{Cat}}}{\sqrt{2}}.

For
N=100
we are able to reconstruct the characteristic function of states
\ket{0}_{\operatorname{Cat}}
and
\ket{1}_{\operatorname{Cat}}
respectively on
[-2,2]
, for
\alpha=(1,1)
, which is illustrated in Figure
2
.

The probability distribution is then

\displaystyle\big|\langle x|0\rangle_{\operatorname{Cat}}\big|^{2}=\frac{|\langle x|+\rangle|^{2}+|\langle x|-\rangle|^{2}+2\Re(\overline{\langle x|-\rangle}\langle x|+\rangle)}{2}

(117)

and

\displaystyle\big|\langle x|1\rangle_{\operatorname{Cat}}\big|^{2}=\frac{|\langle x|+\rangle|^{2}+|\langle x|-\rangle|^{2}-2\Re(\overline{\langle x|-\rangle}\langle x|+\rangle)}{2}

(118)

This implies that

\displaystyle\chi_{\ket{0}_{\operatorname{Cat}}\bra{0}_{\operatorname{Cat}}}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\left(\chi_{\ket{+}\bra{+}}+\chi_{\ket{+}\bra{-}}+\chi_{\ket{-}\bra{+}}+\chi_{\ket{-}\bra{-}}\right)

(119)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}(\frac{1}{\mathcal{N}_{+}^{2}}+\frac{1}{\mathcal{N}_{-}^{2}}\Bigg)(\chi_{\ket{\alpha}\bra{\alpha}}+\chi_{\ket{-\alpha}\bra{-\alpha}})

\displaystyle\quad+\frac{1}{2}\Bigg(\frac{1}{\mathcal{N}_{+}^{2}}-\frac{1}{\mathcal{N}_{-}^{2}}\Bigg)(\chi_{\ket{\alpha}\bra{-\alpha}}+\chi_{\ket{-\alpha}\bra{\alpha}})

\displaystyle\quad+\frac{\chi_{\ket{\alpha}\bra{\alpha}}-\chi_{\ket{-\alpha}\bra{-\alpha}}}{\mathcal{N}_{+}\mathcal{N}_{-}}

(120)

and

\displaystyle\chi_{\ket{1}_{\operatorname{Cat}}\bra{1}_{\operatorname{Cat}}}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\left(\chi_{\ket{+}\bra{+}}-\chi_{\ket{+}\bra{-}}-\chi_{\ket{-}\bra{+}}+\chi_{\ket{-}\bra{-}}\right)

(121)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\Bigg(\frac{1}{\mathcal{N}_{+}^{2}}+\frac{1}{\mathcal{N}_{-}^{2}}\Bigg)(\chi_{\ket{\alpha}\bra{\alpha}}+\chi_{\ket{-\alpha}\bra{-\alpha}})

\displaystyle\quad+\frac{1}{2}\Bigg(\frac{1}{\mathcal{N}_{+}^{2}}-\frac{1}{\mathcal{N}_{-}^{2}}\Bigg)(\chi_{\ket{\alpha}\bra{-\alpha}}+\chi_{\ket{-\alpha}\bra{\alpha}})

\displaystyle-\frac{\chi_{\ket{\alpha}\bra{\alpha}}-\chi_{\ket{-\alpha}\bra{-\alpha}}}{\mathcal{N}_{+}\mathcal{N}_{-}}.

(122)

The characteristic functions on the right hand side are explicitly given by (
31
).

VIII-C

Chain of quadratic harmonic oscillators

We consider a hermitian Hamiltonian matrix
h=\begin{pmatrix}h_{XX}&h_{XP}\\
h_{PX}&h_{PP}\end{pmatrix}

to which we associate a quadratic chain with Hamiltonian

H=\langle R,hR\rangle\text{ with }R=(X,P),

where
h_{XP}=h_{PX}=0
,
h_{PP}=\frac{\operatorname{id}}{2}
and

h_{XX}=\begin{pmatrix}1/2&-\frac{\kappa}{4}&\cdots&0&-\frac{\kappa}{4}\\
-\frac{\kappa}{4}&1/2&-\frac{\kappa}{4}&&0\\
\vdots&-\frac{\kappa}{4}&1/2&\ddots&\vdots\\
0&&\ddots&\ddots&-\frac{\kappa}{4}\\
-\frac{\kappa}{4}&0&\cdots&-\frac{\kappa}{4}&1/2\\
\end{pmatrix}

for some
\kappa\in[-1,1].
Here,
\kappa
refers to the interaction strength between neighboring oscillators: small
\kappa
means weak correlations whereas large
\kappa
means strong correlations. Let

X=h_{XX}^{-1/2}\sqrt{\sqrt{h_{XX}}h_{PP}\sqrt{h_{XX}}}h_{XX}^{-1/2}.

The ground state
|\psi\rangle
of
H
is a Gaussian state with covariance matrix given by
\gamma=\operatorname{diag}(X,X^{-1})

[
80
]
. The decay of spatial correlations is illustrated in Figure
4
. We will again use our heterodyne tomography protocol to reconstruct that decay. The distribution of the random variable arising from the heterodyne detection takes the following expression:

|\langle x|\psi\rangle|^{2}\propto e^{-x^{T}\Omega^{T}\Big(\frac{I+\gamma}{2}\Big)^{-1}\Omega x}\,.

(123)

We recall that for any two modes
i,j
, the reduced shadow characteristic function after sampling
(x_{k})_{k=1}^{N}
from the above distribution is defined as

\displaystyle\hat{\chi}(u_{i},u_{j})\coloneqq\frac{1}{N}\sum_{k=1}^{N}e^{\frac{1}{4}(\|u_{i}\|^{2}+\|u_{j}\|^{2})-i(u_{i}^{\intercal}\Omega(x_{k})_{i}+u_{j}^{\intercal}\Omega(x_{k})_{j})}\,,

where
(x_{k})_{i},(x_{k})_{j}\in\mathbb{R}^{2}
are the components of vector
x_{k}\in\mathbb{R}^{2m}
corresponding to modes
i
and
j
. The reduced characteristic functions of the marginal of
|\psi\rangle
over modes
i
and
j
takes the form

\displaystyle\chi(u_{i},u_{j})=e^{-\frac{1}{4}(u_{i},u_{j})^{\intercal}\gamma(u_{i},u_{j})}

From which the correlations coefficients corresponding to these two modes can be read-off.
