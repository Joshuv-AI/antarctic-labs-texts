Abstract
. The expected hitting time from vertex a a to vertex b b , H ⁡ ( a , b ) H(a,b) , is the expected value of the time it takes a random walk starting at a a to reach b b . In this paper, we give estimates for H ⁡ ( a , b ) H(a,b) when the distance between a a and b b is comparable to the diameter of the graph, and the graph satisfies a Harnack condition. We show that, in such cases, H ⁡ ( a , b ) H(a,b) can be estimated in terms of the volumes of balls around b b . Using our results, we estimate H ⁡ ( a , b ) H(a,b) on various graphs, such as rectangular tori, some convex traces in ℤ d \mathds{Z}^{d} , and fractal graphs. Our proofs use heat kernel estimates.

1.
Introduction

Given a Markov chain on a graph (for us here, a finite graph), there are many reasons to be interested in the random variables
\tau_{b}
, the time it takes for the Markov chain to make its first visit at the vertex
b
. An excellent introduction is in
[
LP17
]
. See also
[
AF02
]
. In the case of simple random walk on the
d
-torus
(\mathbb Z/N\mathbb Z)^{d}
, when
d
is fixed and
N
is a varying parameter, and for vertices
a,b
at distance of order
N
of each other,

H(a,b)=\mathds{E}_{a}[\tau_{b}]\asymp\left\{\begin{array}[]{cl}N^{2}&\mbox{ if }d=1,\\
N^{2}\log N&\mbox{ if }d=2,\\
N^{d}&\mbox{ if }d>2.\end{array}\right.

The goal of this work is try to explain these behaviors in geometric terms so that they can be extended beyond very specific examples such as the
(\mathbb Z/N\mathbb Z)^{d}
. For this, we use heat kernel techniques. Comparing to
[
LP17
, Proposition 10.21]
which gives the behavior of
\mathds{E}_{a}[\tau_{b}]
on
(\mathbb Z/N\mathbb Z)^{d}
in terms of the distance between
a
and
b
even when
a,b
are relatively close to each other,
our results are (mostly) limited to the case when the distance between vertices
a
and
b
is of order the diameter of the graph. However, we also provide a simple condition that imply that
\mathds{E}_{a}[\tau_{b}]\asymp\mathds{E}_{a}[\tau_{c}]

for any vertices
b,c\neq a
, as it happens for instance on
(\mathbb Z/N\mathbb Z)^{d}
when
d\geq 3
. From the point of view of heat kernel estimates, treating the small distance case in the most sensitive cases would require gradient (i.e., difference) estimates which are much more difficult to obtain than the heat kernel estimates themselves.

Our focus is to understand how the torus result cited above generalizes to a variety of other examples of a similar type, from rectangular tori
\prodop\displaylimits_{1}^{N}(\mathbb Z/a_{i}\mathbb Z)
,
a_{1}\leq\dots\leq a_{N}
, to Cayley graphs of finite nilpotent groups such as the group of
3
by
3
upper-triangular matrices with entries mod
N
and entries equal to
1
on the diagonal, to lattice traces on simple convex subsets of
\mathbb R^{2}
or
\mathbb R^{3}
, and classes of finite fractal graph such as the
N
-iteration in the construction of the Sierpinski gasket. See the examples in Section
6
. The common thread amongst all these examples is that they are all
Harnack graphs
which means that they are all amenable to sharp two-sided
heat kernel bounds
.
Here, the
heat kernel
refers to the discrete time iterated kernel of the natural random walk on the graph in question, and
heat kernel bounds
refers to estimates based on basic geometric quantities such graph distance,
d
, and volume of balls with respect to the underlying reversible probability measure
\pi
,
V(x,r)=\pi(\{y:d(x,y)\leq r))
. The definition of
Harnack graph
involves an important parameter,
\theta\geq 2
, and can be given in several equivalent forms. Here, we use a characterization based on four conditions,
(\El),(\VD),(\PI(\theta))
, and
(\CS(\theta))
, for some
\theta\geq 2
. Our main result can be stated as follows.

Theorem 1.1
.

Suppose that the Markov kernel
(\Gamma,K,\pi)
(on a finite graph
\Gamma=(\mathbb{V},E)
) satisfies
(\El),(\VD),(\PI(\theta))
, and
(\CS(\theta))
, for some
\theta\geq 2
.
Let
x,y\in\mathbb{V}
such that
d(x,y)\geq c_{0}D
for some
c_{0}>0
.
Then, there exists constants
C_{1},C_{2}>0
such that

\sumop\displaylimits_{0\leq n\leq 2D^{\theta}}\frac{C_{1}}{V(y,n^{1/\theta})}\leq H(x,y)\leq\sumop\displaylimits_{0\leq n\leq 2D^{\theta}}\frac{C_{2}}{V(y,n^{1/\theta})}.

The structure of the article is as follows. Section
2
sets notation and review key identity relying hitting times expectation to the iterated kernel of the chain. It also review deep iterated kernel estimates related to the notion of Harnack graph. Section
3
derives the key estimates for what we call the "Green function." Section
4
applies the results of the previous sections to obtain the main result of this paper, Theorem
1.1
(also Theorem
5.2
). The last section describes various examples.

2.
Background

2.1.
Random walks preliminaries

Let
\Gamma=(\mathbb{V},E)
be a finite graph where
\mathbb{V}
is the vertex set and
E\subset\mathbb{V}\times\mathbb{V}
is the symmetric edge set. We write
x\sim y
to signify that
(x,y)\in E
. We assume the graph is connected in the sense that one can join any two vertices by a path crossing edges.
Our random process of interest is an aperiodic, irreducible, and reversible Markov chain
K:\mathbb{V}\times\mathbb{V}\to\mathds{R}
with reversible probability measure
\pi:\mathbb{V}\to[0,1]
(we use the same notation for the probability measure and its probability mass function).
To relate
K
to the graph structure, we specify that
K(x,y)>0
if
x\sim y
and
K(x,y)=0
for all
x\neq y
such that
x\nsim y
. The iterated kernel is defined inductively by
K^{n}(x,y)=\sumop\displaylimits_{z}K(x,z)K^{n-1}(z,y).
Reversibility means that
\pi(x)K(x,y)=\pi(y)K(y,x)
and this implies that
\pi
is an invariant measure for
K
,
\sumop\displaylimits_{x}\pi(x)K(x,y)=\pi(y)
. We say that
K
is lazy if
K(x,x)\geq 1/2
for all
x\in\mathbb{V}
. When
K
is lazy, it is obviously aperiodic.
Let
d(x,y)
to be the graph distance on
\Gamma
, i.e. the minimal number of edges one must cross going from
x
to
y
.
The
diameter
of the graph,

D=\max_{x,y\in\mathbb{V}}d(x,y),

is one of the key geometric parameter we will use.
Note that, for all
x\neq y
and
0\leq n<d(x,y)
, we have
K^{n}(x,y)=0
.

For the remainder of this article, we will refer to these objects as the
Markov kernel
(\Gamma,K,\pi)
.
The
normalized kernel
is

k^{n}(x,y)=\frac{K^{n}(x,y)}{\pi(y)},\quad\text{for }n\geq 0.

Each Markov kernel
(\Gamma,K,\pi)
defines a
random walk
\{X_{n}\}_{n\geq 0}
driven by
K
, where
X_{0}
has an initial distribution
\nu
on
\mathbb{V}
and for all
n\geq 1
and
y\in\mathbb{V}
,

\mathds{P}_{\nu}(X_{n+1}=y)=\sumop\displaylimits_{x\in\mathbb{V}}\nu(x)K^{n}(x,y).

For any
x\in\mathbb{V}
, it is convenient to define the operators

\mathds{P}_{x}(\;\cdot\;)=\mathds{P}_{x}(\;\cdot\;|X_{0}=x)\qquad\text{and}\qquad\mathds{E}_{x}[\;\cdot\;]=\mathds{E}_{x}[\;\cdot\;|X_{0}=x].

The
hitting time of
a\in\mathbb{V}
is

\tau_{a}=\min\{n\geq 0:X_{n}=a\}.

For
a,b\in\mathbb{V}
, the
expected hitting time of
b
starting at
a
is

H(a,b)=\mathds{E}_{a}[\tau_{b}].

(2.1)

In general, this is not a symmetric function of
a,b
.
The
exit time of a set
B\subseteq\mathbb{V}
is

\mathrm{T}_{B}=\inf\{t:X_{n}\notin B\}.

(2.2)

For
x\in\mathbb{V}
and
r\geq 0
, we define the (closed) balls of radius
r
centered at
x
as

B(x,r)=\{y\in\mathbb{V}:d(x,y)\leq r\},

and its volume as
V(x,r)=\pi(B(x,r))=\sumop\displaylimits_{y\in B(x,r)}\pi(y)
.

2.2.
Discrete Laplacian, Green’s function, and expected hitting time

Definition 2.1
.

Define the
identity operator
,
I:\mathbb{V}\times\mathbb{V}
,
I(x,y)=\mathbf{1}_{\{x\}}(y)
.
The
random walk Laplacian
is
\Delta=I-K
, and the
(normalized) discrete Green’s function
is the function
\mathcal{G}:\mathbb{V}\times\mathbb{V}\to\mathds{R}
, where

\mathcal{G}(x,y)=\sumop\displaylimits_{n=0}^{\infty}(k^{n}(x,y)-1).

(2.3)

For convenience, we will refer to
\Delta
as
the Laplacian

and
\mathcal{G}
as
the Green function
.

At this point, it will useful to establish some basic facts about these operators.
Set

G(x,y)=\pi(y)\mathcal{G}(x,y)=\sumop\displaylimits_{n=0}^{\infty}(K^{n}(x,y)-\pi(y)).

First, note that
\sumop\displaylimits_{y}G(x,y)=\sumop\displaylimits_{y}\mathcal{G}(x,y)\pi(y)=0
.
Second,
\mathcal{G}
is symmetric by reversibility, but the same does not apply to
G
.
Third, when viewed as operators (or matrices) acting on functions satisfying
\pi(f)=0
, the Laplacian,
\Delta
, and the Green function,
G
, are inverse of each other. More specifically, for functions
f
such that
\pi(f)=0
,
[G\Delta]f=f
as seen from the following lemma.

Lemma 2.2
.

[G\Delta](x,y)=I(x,y)-\pi(y)

Proof.

Compute

\displaystyle[GK](x,y)

\displaystyle=\sumop\displaylimits_{z\in\mathbb{V}}\sumop\displaylimits_{n=0}^{\infty}(K^{n}(x,z)-\pi(z))K(z,y)=\sumop\displaylimits_{n=0}^{\infty}(K^{n+1}(x,y)-\pi(y))=G(x,y)-(I(x,y)-\pi(y)).

Then by the definition of the discrete Laplacian,

\displaystyle[G\Delta](x,y)

\displaystyle=G(x,y)-[GK](x,y)=G(x,y)-G(x,y)+I(x,y)-\pi(y)=I(x,y)-\pi(y).\qed

Lemma 2.3
.

Let
H:\mathbb{V}\times\mathbb{V}\to\mathds{R}
be any kernel such that
H(x,x)=0
for all
x\in\mathbb{V}
.
Then, we have

[G\Delta H](x,x)=-\sumop\displaylimits_{y\in\mathbb{V}}\pi(y)H(y,x).

Proof.

Using Lemma
2.2
, for all
x,y\in\mathbb{V}
, we have

\displaystyle H(x,y)

\displaystyle=\sumop\displaylimits_{z\in\mathbb{V}}([G\Delta](x,z)+\pi(z))H(z,y)=[G\Delta H](x,y)+\sumop\displaylimits_{z\in\mathbb{V}}\pi(z)H(z,y).

By the assumption that
H(x,x)=0
, we have

[G\Delta H](x,x)=-\sumop\displaylimits_{z\in\mathbb{V}}\pi(z)H(z,x).\qed

Proposition 2.4

(
[
AF02
, Chapter 2, Lemma 12]
)
.

Let
H(\cdot,\cdot)
be the expected hitting time function defined in (
2.1
).
Consider the random walk
\{X_{n}\}_{n\geq 0}
driven by
(\Gamma,K,\pi)
.Then for all
x,y\in\mathbb{V}
,

H(x,y)=\mathcal{G}(y,y)-\mathcal{G}(x,y).

Proof.

First, we want to compute
KH(a,b)
for all
a,b\in\mathbb{V}
. On the diagonal, we have

\displaystyle[KH](a,a)=\sumop\displaylimits_{z\in\mathbb{V}}K(a,z)H(z,a)=\mathds{E}_{a}[\tau^{+}_{a}]=\frac{1}{\pi(a)},

where
\tau^{+}_{a}=\min\{t\geq 1:X_{t}=a\}
.
The last equality follows from
K
being irreducible, see
[
Dur10
, Theorem 5.5.11]
.
When
a\neq b
, we have
X_{0}=a
and
X_{1}
is distributed according to
\mathds{1}_{a}K
, and thus, the following relation holds:

\displaystyle H(a,b)

\displaystyle=\mathds{E}[\tau_{b}|X_{0}=a,X_{1}=b]+\sumop\displaylimits_{z:z\neq b}\mathds{E}[\tau_{b}|X_{0}=a,X_{1}=z]

\displaystyle=K(a,b)+\sumop\displaylimits_{z:z\neq b}K(a,z)(1+\mathds{E}[\tau_{b}|X_{0}=z])

\displaystyle=\sumop\displaylimits_{z\in\mathbb{V}}K(a,z)+\sumop\displaylimits_{z:z\neq b}K(a,z)H(z,b)

\displaystyle=1+[KH](a,b).

Then for all
x,y\in\mathbb{V}
,

[\Delta H](x,y)=\begin{cases}1-\frac{1}{\pi(x)}&\text{if }x=y\\
1&\text{if }x\neq y.\end{cases}

(2.4)

Applying
G
to both sides and using
\sumop\displaylimits_{y}G(x,y)=0
, we have

\displaystyle[G\Delta H](x,x)

\displaystyle=\sumop\displaylimits_{y\in\mathbb{V}}G(x,y)[\Delta H](y,x)=-\frac{1}{\pi(x)}G(x,x).

Note that the hitting time (
2.1
) from a point to itself is identically zero.
Combining this with Lemma
2.3
, we have

\mathcal{G}(x,x)=\frac{G(x,x)}{\pi(x)}=\sumop\displaylimits_{y\in\mathbb{V}}\pi(y)H(y,x).

Then for all
x,y
, we can apply
G
once again to (
2.4
) to get

[G\Delta H](x,y)=\sumop\displaylimits_{z\in\mathbb{V}}G(x,z)[\Delta H](z,y)=-\frac{G(x,y)}{\pi(y)}.

Combining this with Lemma
2.2
, we get

H(x,y)=[G\Delta H](x,y)+\sumop\displaylimits_{z\in\mathbb{V}}\pi(z)H(z,y)=\frac{G(y,y)}{\pi(x)}-\frac{G(x,y)}{\pi(y)}=\mathcal{G}(y,y)-\mathcal{G}(x,y).\qed

We note that these properties are all essentially well known although they are often presented in slightly different ways.

2.3.
Remarks on periodicity and laziness

Although we have assumed aperiodicity in addition to irreducibility, the aperiodicity assumption is not essential for our purpose. Indeed, the definition of the Green function,
\mathcal{G}(x,y)=\sumop\displaylimits_{n=0}^{\infty}(k^{n}(x,y)-1)
makes sense for irreducible periodic chain as well as long as on understand it in the form

\mathcal{G}(x,y)=\sumop\displaylimits_{n=0}^{\infty}(k^{2n}(x,y)+k^{2n+1}(x,y)-2).

To see that this series converges, we use the spectral decomposition of the reversible irreducible kernel
K
with eigenvalues
\beta_{i}\in[-1,1]
,
0\leq i\leq|\mathbb{V}|-1
, arranged in non-increasing order, and associated normalized real eigenfunctions
\varphi_{i}
. Because
K
is irreducible,
\beta_{0}=1
and
\beta_{2}<1
. The chain is periodic if and only if
\beta_{|\mathbb{V}|-1}=-1
. This gives

k^{2n}(x,y)+k^{2n+1}(x,y)-2=\sumop\displaylimits_{i=1}^{|\mathbb{V}|-1}(1+\beta_{i})\beta_{i}^{2n}\varphi_{i}(x)\varphi_{i}(y).

This term decays exponentially fast because all eigenvalues equal to
-1
drop from this sum thanks to the factor
(1+\beta_{i})
.

Define

I_{\pm}(x,y)=\left\{\begin{array}[]{l}+1\mbox{ if $x$ and $y$ are in the same class},\\
-1\mbox{ if $x$ and $y$ are not in the same class}.\end{array}\right.

The statement of Lemma
2.2
need to be adjusted to

\displaystyle GK(x,y)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle I(x,y)-\lim_{n\to\infty}K^{2n+2}(x,y)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle I(x,y)-\left\{\begin{array}[]{l}\pi(y)\mbox{ if $K$ is aperiodic},\\
2\pi(y)\mbox{ if $K$ is periodic and $x,y$ are in the same class},\\
0\mbox{ if $K$ is periodic and $x,y$ are not in the same class}.\end{array}\right.

This gives the correct result whether or not
K
is aperiodic. In the periodic case, letting
\mathfrak C(x)
denote the class of
x
, the identity in Lemma (
2.3
) for an arbitrary kernel
H
satisfying
H(x,x)=0
becomes

[G\Delta H](x,x)=-2\sumop\displaylimits_{z\in\mathfrak C(x)}\pi(z)H(z,x).

(2.9)

We need to observe that for
y\notin\mathfrak C(x)
,
H(y,x)=1+\sumop\displaylimits_{z\in\mathfrak C(x)}K(y,z)H(z,x)
.
Summing over
y\notin\mathfrak C(x)
and remembering that
\pi(\mathfrak C(x))=1/2
, we obtain

\sumop\displaylimits_{y\notin\mathfrak C(x)}\pi(y)H(y,x)=\frac{1}{2}+\sumop\displaylimits_{z\in\mathfrak C(x)}\pi(z)H(z,x).

From there, one checks that the identity of Proposition
2.4
concerning the expected hitting time
H
and stating that

H(x,y)=\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}\mathcal{G}(y,y)-\mathcal{G}(x,y)&\mbox{ if $x,y$ are in the same class},\\
1+\mathcal{G}(y,y)-\mathcal{G}(x,y)&\mbox{ if $x,y$ are not in the same class},\end{array}\right.

(2.10)

continues to hold with essentially the same proof, adjusting for (
2.3
)-(
2.9
).

When computing or estimating the expected hitting times
H(a,b)
for an aperiodic reversible chain
K
, there is no loss of generality in assuming that the chain is lazy. Indeed, fix
\epsilon\in(0,1)
and define

K_{\epsilon}=\epsilon I+(1-\epsilon)K
. Using the spectral decomposition again,

\mathcal{G}_{\epsilon}(x,y)=\sumop\displaylimits_{n=0}^{+\infty}\sumop\displaylimits_{i=1}^{|\mathbb{V}|-1}\beta_{i}(\epsilon)^{n}\varphi_{i}(x)\varphi_{i}(y)=\sumop\displaylimits_{i=1}^{|\mathbb{V}|-1}\frac{1}{1-\beta_{i}(\epsilon)}\varphi_{i}(x)\varphi_{i}(y).

But
\beta_{i}(\epsilon)=\epsilon+(1-\epsilon)\beta_{i}
so that
1-\beta_{i}(\epsilon)=(1-\epsilon)(1-\beta_{i})
and

\mathcal{G}_{\epsilon}(x,y)=(1-\epsilon)^{-1}\mathcal{G}(x,y).

It follows that
H_{\epsilon}(x,y)=(1-\epsilon)^{-1}H(x,y)
(the
\epsilon
-lazy chain behaves as if slowdown by a factor of
(1-\epsilon)^{-1}
. The periodic case is a bit more subtle.

Recall that for a reversible irreducible periodic chain, their is two periodic class
A,A^{c}
with
\pi(A)=\pi(A^{c})=1/2
, and
-1
is an eigenvalue of multiplicity
1
with eigenfunction
\mathbf{1}_{A}-\mathbf{1}_{A^{c}}
. Of course
K^{2n+1}(a,b)=0
if
a,b
are in the same class and
K^{2n}(a,b)=0
if
a,b
are not in the same class. In this case, we find that

\mathcal{G}_{\epsilon}(x,y)=(1-\epsilon)^{-1}\left(\mathcal{G}(x,y)+\frac{1}{2}(\mathbf{1}_{A}(x)-\mathbf{1}_{A^{c}}(x))(\mathbf{1}_{A}(y)-\mathbf{1}_{A^{c}}(y)\right)=(1-\epsilon)^{-1}\left(\mathcal{G}(x,y)+\frac{I_{\pm}(x,y)}{2}\right)

For the expected hitting time
H_{\epsilon}(a,b)
with (
2.10
), this gives

H_{\epsilon}(a,b)=(1-\epsilon)^{-1}H(a,b).

Alternatively, this last result can be seen directly has follows. Consider the product probability space of
\mathbb{V}^{\infty}
equipped the probability
\mathds{P}_{a}
induced by the Markov kernel
K
(we can fix the starting point
a
) and
\{0,1\}^{\infty}
with the product of Bernoulli measures with parameter
1-\epsilon
.
For
\omega=(\theta,\sigma)\in\mathbb{V}^{\infty}\times\{0,1\}^{\infty}
, set
X_{k}(\omega)=\theta_{k}
for all
k\geq 0
.

N_{k}(\sigma)=\argmin_{j}\{\sumop\displaylimits_{i=0}^{j}\sigma_{i}=k\}=n.

Under
\mathds{P}_{a}
, the random variable
X_{k}
is distributed according to
K^{k}(a,\cdot)

and
(X_{k})_{0}^{\infty}
is a realization of the
K
-Markov chain.
Similarly,

Y_{n}
is distributed according to
K_{\epsilon}^{n}(a,\cdot)
and
(Y_{n})_{0}^{\infty}
is a realization of the

K_{\epsilon}
-Markov chain.
Consider

\tau_{b}(\omega)=\inf\{k:\theta_{k}=b\}
and
\tau^{*}_{b}(\omega)=\inf_{k}\{N_{k}(\sigma):\theta_{N_{k}(\sigma)}=b\}
.
By construction, on
\{\tau_{b}=k\}
,
\tau_{b}^{*}=N_{k}
and (using the known first moment of a negative-binomial)

H_{\epsilon}(a,b)=\mathds{E}_{a}(\tau_{b}^{*})=\sumop\displaylimits_{k\geq 0}\sumop\displaylimits_{n\geq k}n\mathds{P}(N_{k}=n)\mathds{P}_{a}(\tau_{b}=k)=(1-\epsilon)^{-1}\sumop\displaylimits_{k}k\mathds{P}_{a}(\tau_{b}=k)=(1-\epsilon)^{-1}H(a,b).

This shows that, as far as
H(a,b)
is concerned, we may just as well study the lazy version of the chain of interest.

2.4.
Heat kernel estimates

In this section, we recall criteria for
(\Gamma,K,\pi)
that imply the existence of good estimates for
k^{n}(x,y)
, in the sense of Theorem

2.9
.
We refer the reader to
[
Bar17
]
for detailed exposition on the topic.
The majority of our results assume that
(\Gamma,K,\pi)
satisfies the conditions

(\El),(\PI(\theta)),(\VD),
and
(\CS(\theta))
for some
\theta\geq 2
. These (well-known) conditions are described below.
The (sometimes unspecified) constants in our results will depend on
\theta
and the constants appearing in all these conditions, but they are independent of other all other parameters such as the size of
\mathbb{V}
and its diameter.

Definition 2.5
.

The Markov kernel
(\Gamma,K,\pi)
satisfies
ellipticity
(E) if there exists a constant
p_{0}
such that for all
x\sim y
,

K(x,y)\geq p_{0}.

(E)

Definition 2.6
.

The Markov kernel
(\Gamma,K,\pi)
satisfies
volume doubling
(VD) if
there exists a constant
C_{D}>0
such that for all
x\in\mathbb{V}
and
r>0
,

V(x,2r)\leq C_{D}V(x,r).

(VD)

Definition 2.7
.

The Markov kernel
(\Gamma,K,\pi)
satisfies
the Poincaré inequality
(PI
(\theta)
) if there is a constant
C_{P}>0
such that, for all
x\in\mathbb{V}
and all
r>0
,
the following statement is true:

\forall f,\quad\sumop\displaylimits_{z\in B}\left|f(z)-f_{B}\right|^{2}\pi(z)\leq C_{P}r^{\theta}\sumop\displaylimits_{\xi,\zeta\in B,\{\xi,\zeta\}\in E}|f(\xi)-f(\zeta)|^{2}K(\xi,\zeta)\pi(\xi)\text{, }

(PI)

where
B=B(x,r)
and
f_{B}=\pi(B)^{-1}\sumop\displaylimits_{B}f\pi
.

Definition 2.8
.

Fix
\theta\in[2,\infty)
. The Markov kernel
(\Gamma,K,\pi)
satisfies
the cut-off function existence property

(\operatorname{CS}(\theta))
if there are constants
C_{1},C_{2},C_{3}
and
\epsilon>0
such that, for any
x\in\mathbb{V}
and
r>0
, there exists a cut-off function
\sigma=\sigma_{x,r}
satisfying the following properties:

(a)

\sigma\geq 1
on
B(x,r/2)

(b)

\sigma\equiv 0
on
\mathbb{V}\backslash B(x,r)

(c)

For all
y,z\in\mathbb{V},|\sigma(z)-\sigma(y)|\leq C_{1}(d(z,y)/r)^{\epsilon}

(d)

For any
s\in(0,r]
and any function
f
on
B(x,2r)
,

\displaystyle\sumop\displaylimits_{z\in B(x,s)}|f(z)|^{2}\sumop\displaylimits_{y:\{z,y\}\in E}|\sigma(z)-\sigma(y)|^{2}K(z,y)\pi(z)

\displaystyle\leq C_{2}(s/r)^{2\epsilon}\left\{\sumop\displaylimits_{\begin{subarray}{c}z,y\in B(x,2s)\\
\{z,y\}\in E\end{subarray}}|f(z)-f(y)|^{2}K(z,y)\pi(z)+s^{-\theta}\sumop\displaylimits_{z\in B(x,2s)}|f(z)|^{2}\pi(z)\right\}.

Note that when
\theta=2
,
(\CS(2))
is always automatically satisfied. For other values of
\theta
, it is typically extremely difficult to verify this condition based on a description of the graph.

Theorem 2.9
.

[
BB04
]

Suppose that the Markov kernel
(\Gamma,K,\pi)
satisfies
(\El),(\VD),(\PI(\theta))
, and
(\CS(\theta))
, for some
\theta\geq 2
.
Then there exists constants
c_{1},c_{2},C_{1},C_{2}>0
such that for all
x,y\in\mathbb{V}

k^{n}(x,y)\leq\frac{C_{1}}{V\left(x,n^{1/\theta}\right)}\exp\left(-C_{2}\left(\frac{d(x,y)^{\theta}}{n}\right)^{1/(\theta-1)}\right),

and, for all
d(x,y)\leq n
,

\frac{c_{1}}{V\left(x,n^{1/\theta}\right)}\exp\left(-c_{2}\left(\frac{d(x,y)^{\theta}}{n}\right)^{1/(\theta-1)}\right)\leq k^{n}(x,y)+k^{n+1}(x,y).

The following lemma gives the exponential decay of
|k^{n}-1|
when
n
is of order greater than
D^{\theta}
.
The proof uses a standard interpolation argument, previously used in the proofs of

[
DS96
, Lemma 1.1]
and

[

, Theorem 6.4]
.

Lemma 2.10
.

Suppose that the Markov kernel
(\Gamma,K,\pi)
satisfies
(\El),(\VD),(\PI(\theta))
, and
(\CS(\theta))
, for some
\theta\geq 2
and is lazy.
Then, there exists
C_{2}
and, for any
c_{0}>0
, there exists
C_{1}
such that,
for all
n\geq c_{0}D^{\theta}
and
x,y\in\mathbb{V}
,

|k^{n}(x,y)-1|\leq C_{1}\exp(-C_{2}n/D^{\theta}),

(
C_{1}
depends on
c_{0}
, but not
C_{2}
).

Proof.

This proof relies on the language of operator norms.
For a more thorough explanation about the analysis of reversible finite Markov chains, we refer the reader to
[
SC97
]
.
Recall that the space
\ell^{p}(\pi)
is the set of functions from
\mathbb{V}
to
\mathds{R}
under the norm

||f||_{p}=\left(\sumop\displaylimits_{x\in\mathbb{V}}|f(x)|^{p}\pi(x)\right)^{1/p}
if
p\geq 1
and

||f||_{\infty}=\sup_{x\in\mathbb{V}}|f(x)|.

Given
p,q\in[1,\infty]
and
K:\ell^{p}(\pi)\to\ell^{q}(\pi)
, define

||K||_{p\to q}=\sup_{f\in\ell^{p}(\pi)}\left\{\frac{||Kf||_{q}}{||f||_{p}}\right\}.

Given this notation, we set
n_{1},n_{2}
such that
n=n_{1}+2n_{2}
and have

\displaystyle|k^{n}(x,y)-1|

\displaystyle\leq||K^{n}-\pi||_{1\to\infty}

\displaystyle\leq||K^{n_{2}}-\pi||_{1\to 2}||K^{n_{1}}-\pi||_{2\to 2}||K^{n_{2}}-\pi||_{2\to\infty}.

By reversibility of
K
, we know that

||K^{n_{2}}-\pi||_{1\to 2}=||K^{n_{2}}-\pi||_{2\to\infty}=\sqrt{\sup_{x,y\in\mathbb{V}}k^{2n_{2}}(x,y)}\leq\sqrt{\sup_{x\in\mathbb{V}}k^{2n_{2}}(x,x)}.

By Theorem
2.9
, there exists
C_{1},C_{2}>0
such that

\displaystyle k^{2n_{2}}(x,y)\leq\frac{C_{1}}{V\left(x,(2n_{2})^{1/\theta}\right)}\exp\left(-C_{2}\left(\frac{d(x,y)^{\theta}}{2n_{2}}\right)^{1/(\theta-1)}\right).

By choosing
n_{2}
to be the largest integer less than
c_{0}D^{\theta}
, the exponential term is bounded by a constant depending on
c_{0}
.
The volume doubling property (
\VD
) implies

\displaystyle\frac{1}{V\left(x,(2n_{2})^{1/\theta}\right)}\leq\frac{V(x,D)}{V\left(x,(2n_{2})^{1/\theta}\right)}\leq C_{3}\left(\frac{D}{n_{2}^{1/\theta}}\right)^{C_{4}},

for some
C_{3},C_{4}>0
.
Thus, we have

||K^{n_{2}}-\pi||_{1\to 2}||K^{n_{2}}-\pi||_{2\to\infty}\leq C_{5}\left(\frac{D}{n_{2}^{1/\theta}}\right)^{C_{4}}.

(2.11)

By (
\El
) and (
\PI(\theta)
) and the assumed laziness of
K
, there exist
C_{6},C_{7}>0
such that

||K^{n_{1}}-\pi||_{2\to 2}=(1-C_{6}/D^{\theta})^{n_{1}}\leq\exp(-C_{7}n_{1}/D^{\theta}),

Combined with (
2.11
), there exists a constant
C_{8}>0
, such that

|k^{n}(x,y)-1|\leq C_{5}\left(\frac{D}{n_{2}^{1/\theta}}\right)^{C_{4}}\exp(-C_{7}n_{1}/D^{\theta})\leq\exp(-C_{8}n/D^{\theta}).

∎

3.
Green’s function estimates

Proposition 3.1
.

Suppose that the Markov kernel
(\Gamma,K,\pi)
is lazy and satisfies
(\El),(\VD),(\PI(\theta))
, and
(\CS(\theta))
, for some
\theta\geq 2
.
Then there exists a constant
C>0
such that for all
x,y\in\mathbb{V}
,

|\mathcal{G}(x,y)|\leq\sumop\displaylimits_{d(x,y)^{\theta}\leq n\leq 2D^{\theta}}\frac{C}{V(x,n^{1/\theta})}.

(3.1)

Proof.

We use
k^{n}(x,y)+1
to upper bound the summand of (
2.3
) for
0\leq n\leq 2D^{\theta}
and break up the sum of as follows:

|\mathcal{G}(x,y)|\leq 2D^{\theta}+\sumop\displaylimits_{0\leq n<d(x,y)^{\theta}}k^{n}(x,y)+\sumop\displaylimits_{d(x,y)^{\theta}\leq n\leq 2D^{\theta}}k^{n}(x,y)+\sumop\displaylimits_{n>D^{\theta}}|k^{n}(x,y)-1|.

(3.2)

Now we will show that each of the four summands above can be bounded above by a constant multiple of the sum in (
3.1
).
First note that for all
D^{\theta}\leq n\leq 2D^{\theta}
, we have
V(x,n^{1/\theta})=1
. Thus, we have for the first summand

D^{\theta}\leq\sumop\displaylimits_{d(x,y)^{\theta}\leq n\leq 2D^{\theta}}\frac{1}{V(x,n^{1/\theta})}.

By Theorem
2.9
, we know that there exists
C_{1},C_{2}>0
such that
k^{n}(x,y)\leq u(n)v(n)
for all
n\geq d(x,y)
, where

u(t)=\frac{C_{1}}{V\left(x,t^{1/\theta}\right)}v(t)=\exp\left(-C_{2}\left(\frac{d(x,y)^{\theta}}{t}\right)^{1/(\theta-1)}\right).

For the second summand of (
3.2
), we use Lemma
A.1
and the fact that
k^{n}(x,y)=0
for
n<d(x,y)
to get

\displaystyle\sumop\displaylimits_{0\leq n<d(x,y)^{\theta}}k^{n}(x,y)\leq\sumop\displaylimits_{d(x,y)\leq n<d(x,y)^{\theta}}u(n)v(n)\leq\frac{C_{3}d(x,y)^{\theta}}{V(x,d(x,y))}.

By
(\VD)
, there exists a constant
C_{D}
such that

\displaystyle\frac{d(x,y)^{\theta}}{V(x,d(x,y))}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{C_{D}d(x,y)^{\theta}}{V(x,2d(x,y))}\leq\sumop\displaylimits_{d(x,y)^{\theta}\leq n\leq 2d(x,y)^{\theta}}\frac{C_{D}}{V(x,n^{1/\theta})},

as desired.
For the third summand of (
3.2
), we simply use that
v(t)\leq 1
to get

\sumop\displaylimits_{d(x,y)^{\theta}\leq n\leq 2D^{\theta}}k^{n}(x,y)\leq C_{1}\sumop\displaylimits_{d(x,y)^{\theta}\leq n\leq 2D^{\theta}}\frac{1}{V(x,n^{1/\theta})}.

In the range of the fourth summand of (
3.2
), we know that
V(x,n^{1/\theta})=1
.
By Lemma
2.10
there exists constants
C_{5},C_{6},C_{7}>0
such that

\sumop\displaylimits_{n>2D^{\theta}}|k^{n}(x,y)-1|\leq C_{5}\sumop\displaylimits_{n>2D^{\theta}}\exp(-C_{6}n/D^{\theta})\leq C_{5}D^{\theta}\intop\nolimits_{1}^{\infty}\exp(-C_{6}u)\;du\leq C_{7}D^{\theta}.

(3.3)

∎

Corollary 3.2
.

Suppose that the Markov kernel
(\Gamma,K,\pi)
is lazy and satisfies
(\El),(\VD),(\PI(\theta))
, and
(\CS(\theta))
, for some
\theta\geq 2
.
Let
x,y\in\mathbb{V}
such that
d(x,y)\geq c_{0}D
for some
c_{0}>0
.
Then there exists
C_{1}>0
(depending on
c_{0}
but not
x,y
) such that

|\mathcal{G}(x,y)|\leq C_{1}D^{\theta}.

Proof.

We simply apply Proposition
3.1
to get

\displaystyle|\mathcal{G}(x,y)|

\displaystyle\leq\sumop\displaylimits_{d(x,y)^{\theta}\leq n\leq 2D^{\theta}}\frac{C_{1}}{V(x,n^{1/\theta})}\leq\sumop\displaylimits_{c_{0}^{\theta}D^{\theta}\leq n\leq 2D^{\theta}}\frac{C_{1}}{V(x,n^{1/\theta})}\leq(2-c_{0}^{\theta})D^{\theta}\frac{1}{V(x,c_{0}D)}.

By Lemma
A.2
, we know that
V(x,c_{0}D)
is bounded below by a constant, which gives the desired result.
∎

Proposition 3.3
.

Suppose that the Markov kernel
(\Gamma,K,\pi)
is lazy and satisfies
(\El),(\VD),(\PI(\theta))
, and
(\CS(\theta))
, for some
\theta\geq 2
.
Then there exists
C_{1},C_{2}>0
such that for all
x\in\mathbb{V}

\mathcal{G}(x,x)\geq\sumop\displaylimits_{0\leq n\leq 2D^{\theta}}\frac{C_{1}}{V(x,n^{1/\theta})}-C_{2}D^{\theta}.

Proof.

In the sum of (
2.3
), we use the lower bound of
-|k^{n}(x,x)-1|
for
n>8D^{\theta}
, and break up the sum as follows:

\mathcal{G}(x,x)\geq-4D^{\theta}+\sumop\displaylimits_{0\leq n\leq 4D^{\theta}}k^{n}(x,x)-\sumop\displaylimits_{n>4D^{\theta}}|k^{n}(x,x)-1|.

(3.4)

For the second summand of (
3.4
), we use Theorem
2.9
with

to get

\displaystyle\sumop\displaylimits_{0\leq n\leq 4D^{\theta}}k^{n}(x,x)\geq\frac{1}{2}\sumop\displaylimits_{0\leq n\leq 2D^{\theta}}(k^{n}(x,x)+k^{n+1}(x,x))\geq\frac{1}{2}\sumop\displaylimits_{0\leq n\leq 2D^{\theta}}\frac{C_{1}}{V(x,n^{1/\theta})}.

For the third summand of (

), we use the same technique that we used for the proof of Proposition

in (

) to get

∎

4.
Exit time estimates

Lemma 4.1
.

Let
o\in\mathbb{V}
and
\{X_{t}\}_{t\geq 0}
be the random walk driven by a Markov kernel
(\Gamma,K,\pi)
with
X_{0}=o
.
For all
R>0
and
t\geq 0
,

\mathds{P}_{o}\left(\sup_{0<s\leq t}d(o,X_{s})>R\right)\leq 2\sup_{0<s\leq t}\left(\sup_{x\in B(o,R+1)}\mathds{P}_{x}(d(x,X_{s})>R/2)\right)

(4.1)

Proof.

Consider a random walk
\{X_{n}\}_{n\geq 0}
starting at
o\in\mathbb{V}
, such that
\sup_{0<s\leq t}d(o,X_{s})>R
for some
R,t\geq 0
.
Recall from (
2.2
) that the exit time of
B\subset\mathbb{V}
is
\mathrm{T}_{B}=\inf\{t:X_{n}\notin B\}.

Define the events

\displaystyle E_{1}

\displaystyle=\{T_{B(o,R)}\leq t\}=\{\exists s\leq t:d(o,X_{s})>R\}=\left\{\sup_{0\leq s\leq t}d(o,X_{s})>R\right\}

\displaystyle E_{2}

\displaystyle=\{d(o,X_{t})\leq R/2\}.

We will prove the desired inequality (
4.1
) via

\mathds{P}_{o}(E_{1})\leq\mathds{P}_{o}(E_{1}\cap E_{2})+\mathds{P}_{o}(E_{2}^{c}).

(4.2)

The first summand can be written as

\mathds{P}_{o}\left(E_{1}\cap E_{2}\right)=\sumop\displaylimits_{k=0}^{t}\mathds{P}_{o}\left(T_{B(o,R)}=k\right)\mathds{P}_{o}\left(E_{1}\cap E_{2}|T_{B(o,R)}=k\right).

(4.3)

Figure 1
.
In the event
E_{1}\cap E_{2}
, the walk exits the ball
B(o,R)
for the first time at say point
x
. Then if the walk returns to the ball
B(o,R/2)
at time
t
, it must have also exited the ball
B(x,R/2)
by then.

For a fixed
k\in[0,t]
, consider the event
E_{1}\cap E_{1}
given
T_{B(o,R)}=k
.
This means that the walk starts at
o
, reaches distance
R+1
from
o
at time
k
, and returns to the ball of
B(o,R/2)
by time
t
, see Figure
1
. By the triangle inequality, we have

R+1=d(o,X_{t})\leq d(o,X_{k})+d(X_{k},X_{t})\leq\frac{R}{2}+d(X_{t},X_{k}).

Thus, we have
d(X_{t},X_{k})>R/2
, and

\displaystyle\mathds{P}_{o}\left(E_{1}\cap E_{2}|T_{B(o,R)}=k\right)

\displaystyle\leq\sup_{x\in B(o,R+1)}\mathds{P}_{o}\left(\left.d(X_{t},x)>\frac{R}{2}\;\right|\;T_{B(o,R)}=k,X_{k}=x\right).

By the strong Markov property, for any
x\in B(o,R+1)
, we have

\displaystyle\mathds{P}_{o}\left(\left.d(X_{t},x)>\frac{R}{2}\;\right|\;T_{B(o,R)}=k,X_{k}=x\right)

\displaystyle=\mathds{P}\left(\left.d(X_{t-k},x)>\frac{R}{2}\;\right|\;X_{0}=x\right)

\displaystyle\leq\sup_{0\leq s\leq t}\mathds{P}_{x}\left(d(X_{s},x)>\frac{R}{2}\right).

Combined with (
4.3
), the above implies

\mathds{P}_{o}\left(E_{1}\cap E_{2}\right)\leq\sup_{0\leq s\leq t}\left(\sup_{x\in B(o,R+1)}\mathds{P}_{x}\left(d(X_{s},x)>\frac{R}{2}\right)\right).

(4.4)

It is also clear that

\mathds{P}_{o}(E_{2}^{c})=\mathds{P}_{o}\left(d(o,X_{t})>R/2\right)\leq\sup_{0\leq s\leq t}\left(\sup_{x\in B(o,R+1)}\mathds{P}_{x}\left(d(X_{s},x)>\frac{R}{2}\right)\right).

This, with (
4.2
) and (
4.4
), gives our desired result.
∎

Lemma 4.2
.

Suppose that the Markov kernel
(\Gamma,K,\pi)
satisfies
(\El),(\VD),(\PI(\theta))
, and
(\CS(\theta))
, for some
\theta\geq 2
.
Let
x\in\mathbb{V}
and
\{X_{n}\}_{n\geq 0}
be the canonical random walk driven by
K
with
X_{0}=x
.
Then, there exists a constant
C>0
, independent of
x
such that

\mathds{P}_{x}(d(x,X_{s})>R)\leq C\exp\left(-(R^{\theta}/s)^{\frac{1}{\theta-1}}\right),

(4.5)

for all
R,s\geq 0
.

Proof.

Note that if
R^{\theta}\leq s
, the right hand side of (
4.5
) is greater than one by choosing
C\geq 1
, so the inequality is trivially satisfied.
Thus, we solely consider the case when
R^{\theta}\geq s
.
First, the probability in (
4.5
) can be written in terms of the kernel and the normalize kernel as:

\mathds{P}_{x}(d(x,X_{s})>R)=\sumop\displaylimits_{y\in\mathbb{V}:d(x,y)>R}K^{s}(x,y)=\sumop\displaylimits_{y\in\mathbb{V}:d(x,y)>R}k^{s}(x,y)\pi(y).

By Theorem
2.9
, we have that there exists
C_{1},C_{2}>0
such that

\mathds{P}_{x}(d(x,X_{s})>R)\leq C_{1}\sumop\displaylimits_{y\in\mathbb{V}:d(x,y)>R}\frac{\pi(y)}{V\left(x,s^{1/\theta}\right)}\exp\left(-C_{2}\left(\frac{d(x,y)^{\theta}}{s}\right)^{1/(\theta-1)}\right).

(4.6)

Now, we break up the sum on the right hand side into partitions of vertices
y
such that
2^{k}R<d(x,y)\leq 2^{k+1}R
:

\displaystyle\mathds{P}_{x}(d(x,X_{s})>R)

\displaystyle\leq C_{1}\sumop\displaylimits_{k=0}^{\infty}\frac{V(x,2^{k+1}R)}{V\left(x,s^{1/\theta}\right)}\exp\left(-C_{2}\left(\frac{(2^{k}R)^{\theta}}{s}\right)^{1/(\theta-1)}\right)

\displaystyle\leq C_{3}\sumop\displaylimits_{k=0}^{\infty}\left(\frac{2^{k}R}{s^{1/\theta}}\right)^{C_{4}}\exp\left(-C_{2}\left(\frac{2^{k}R}{s^{1/\theta}}\right)^{\frac{\theta}{\theta-1}}\right),

where the last inequality is by (
\VD
).
Since
\frac{\theta}{\theta-1}>1
, there exists a constant
C_{5}>0
such that for all
x\geq 1
,

x^{C_{4}}\exp\left(-C_{2}x^{\frac{\theta}{\theta-1}}\right)\leq C_{5}\exp\left(-\frac{C_{2}x^{\frac{\theta}{\theta-1}}}{2}\right).

Recall that
R^{\theta}>s
, so we can apply the above statement to simplify (
4.6
) further:

\displaystyle\mathds{P}_{x}(d(x,X_{s})>R)

\displaystyle\leq C_{6}\sumop\displaylimits_{k=0}^{\infty}\exp\left(-C_{7}\left(\frac{2^{k}R}{s^{1/\theta}}\right)^{\frac{\theta}{\theta-1}}\right)\leq C_{6}\exp\left(-C_{7}\left(\frac{R^{\theta}}{s}\right)^{\frac{1}{\theta-1}}\right)\left(1+\sumop\displaylimits_{k=1}^{\infty}\exp\left(-C_{7}2^{k}\right)\right)

\displaystyle\leq C_{8}\exp\left(-C_{7}\left(\frac{R^{\theta}}{s}\right)^{\frac{1}{\theta-1}}\right).

∎

5.
Expected hitting time estimates

We first use the exit time estimates established in the previous section to show that the expected hitting time is at least of order
D^{\theta}
.

Lemma 5.1
.

Suppose that the Markov kernel
(\Gamma,K,\pi)
satisfies
(\El),(\VD),(\PI(\theta))
, and
(\CS(\theta))
, for some
\theta\geq 2
.
Let
x,y\in\mathbb{V}
such that
d(x,y)\geq c_{0}D
for some
c_{0}>0
.
Then, there exists
C>0
such that

H(x,y)\geq CD^{\theta}.

Proof.

Let
r=d(x,y)\geq c_{0}D
.
If the random walk starting at
x
hits
y
, it must exit the ball of radius
r/2
.
Applying Markov’s inequality, we have that

\mathds{E}_{x}[\tau_{y}]>\mathds{E}_{x}[T_{B(x,r/2)}]>t\mathds{P}_{x}[T_{B(x,r/2)}>t],

(5.1)

for all
t>0
.

In addition, by Lemma
4.2
, there exists a constant
C>0
, such that

\mathds{P}_{x}(d(x,X_{s})>r/4)\leq C\exp\left(-(r^{\theta}/s)^{\frac{1}{\theta-1}}\right),

for all
x\in\mathbb{V}
and
s\geq 0
.
We can choose a constant
C_{0}>0
such that for all
x\in\mathbb{V}
and
0\leq s\leq C_{0}r^{\theta}
, it is true that

\mathds{P}_{x}(d(x,X_{s})>r/4)<1/4.

(5.2)

Applying Lemma
4.1
with
t=C_{0}r^{\theta}
, we get

\displaystyle\mathds{P}_{x}(T_{(B(x,r/2)}\leq t)

\displaystyle=\mathds{P}_{x}\left(\sup_{0<s\leq t}d(x,X_{t})>r/2\right)\leq 2\sup_{0<s\leq t}\left(\sup_{z\in B\left(x,\frac{r}{2}+1\right)}\mathds{P}_{z}(d(z,X_{s})>r/4)\right)

\displaystyle\leq 2\sup_{0<s\leq t}\left(C\exp\left(-(r^{\theta}/s)^{\frac{1}{\theta-1}}\right)\right)\leq\frac{1}{2}\qquad\textrm{(by (\ref{eq:exit0})).}

Combined with (
5.1
), there exists
C_{1}>0
such that

\mathds{E}_{x}[\tau_{y}]\geq\frac{C_{0}r^{\theta}}{2}\geq C_{1}D^{\theta}.

∎

Theorem 5.2
.

Suppose that the Markov kernel
(\Gamma,K,\pi)
satisfies
(\El),(\VD),(\PI(\theta))
, and
(\CS(\theta))
, for some
\theta\geq 2
.
Let
x,y\in\mathbb{V}
such that
d(x,y)\geq c_{0}D
for some
c_{0}>0
.
Then, there exists constants
C_{1},C_{2}>0
such that

\sumop\displaylimits_{0\leq n\leq 2D^{\theta}}\frac{C_{1}}{V(y,n^{1/\theta})}\leq H(x,y)\leq\sumop\displaylimits_{0\leq n\leq 2D^{\theta}}\frac{C_{2}}{V(y,n^{1/\theta})}.

The upper bound is valid, in fact, for all
x,y\in\mathbb{V}
.

Proof.

Without loss of generality, we can assume that
K
is lazy. For the upper bound, Proposition
3.1

and the fact that
\mathcal{G}(\cdot,\cdot)
is symmetric (by reversibility) give

\displaystyle H(x,y)

\displaystyle\leq|\mathcal{G}(y,y)|+|\mathcal{G}(y,x)|\leq\sumop\displaylimits_{0\leq n\leq 2D^{\theta}}\frac{C_{1}}{V(y,n^{1/\theta})}+\sumop\displaylimits_{d(x,y)^{\theta}\leq n\leq 2D^{\theta}}\frac{C_{1}}{V(y,n^{1/\theta})}\leq\sumop\displaylimits_{0\leq n\leq 2D^{\theta}}\frac{2C_{1}}{V(y,n^{1/\theta})}.

This gives the desired upper bound.

For the lower bound, by Corollary
3.2
, Proposition
3.3
, and Lemma
5.1
, we have

\displaystyle H(x,y)

\displaystyle\geq\max\left\{\sumop\displaylimits_{0\leq n\leq 2D^{\theta}}\frac{C_{4}}{V(y,n^{1/\theta})}-C_{5}D^{\theta},C_{6}D^{\theta}\right\}\geq\sumop\displaylimits_{0\leq n\leq 2D^{\theta}}\frac{C_{7}}{V(y,n^{1/\theta})},

for some
C_{7}>0
.
∎

6.1.
Expected hitting times and resistances

The connection between random walks and electric networks is well known, see

Then when

6.2.
Rectangular tori

There is extensive literature on computing
H(x,y)
for various graphs. One important early result is the following theorem by Cox:

Proposition 6.1
.

[
Cox89
, Theorem 4]

Consider the simple random walk on the torus
\mathbb{Z}_{n}^{d}
. There exist constants
0<c_{d}\leq C_{d}<\infty
such that if
x
and
y
are at distance order
n
, the diameter of the torus, then

\displaystyle c_{d}n^{d}

\displaystyle\leq H(x,y)\leq C_{d}n^{d}

\displaystyle\text{ if }d\geq 3

\displaystyle c_{2}n^{2}\log(n)

\displaystyle\leq H(x,y)\leq C_{2}n^{2}\log(n)

\displaystyle\text{ if }d=2

\displaystyle c_{1}n^{2}

\displaystyle\leq H(x,y)\leq C_{1}n^{2}

\displaystyle\text{ if }d=1.

(6.1)

Early proofs of this result involve careful estimation of the spectrum of the kernel and transition probabilities. Later,
[
LP17
, Proposition 10.21]
provides a proof using the relationship of
H(x,y)
and
\mathcal{R}(x\leftrightarrow y)
.
Then the resistance is estimated from test functions constructed from Pólya urns.
The complexity of these proofs increases greatly even when we consider tori with different side lengths.

Consider a rectangular
2
-dimensional torus,
\Gamma=\mathds{Z}_{a}\times\mathds{Z}_{b}
, where
a\geq b
.
It is known that that assumptions of Theorem
5.2
is satisfied with
\theta=2
.
Let
x,y\in\Gamma
be such that
d(x,y)\asymp a
, i.e. of the order of the diameter of
\Gamma
.
Note that around
r=b
, the volume growth of balls change from quadratic to linear growth, see Figure
2
, and we have

\#B(x,r)\asymp\begin{cases}r^{2}&\text{if }r\leq b\\
b^{2}+b(r-b)&\text{if }b<r\leq a.\end{cases}

Applying these estimates to Theorem
5.2
with
\theta=2
, we get

H(x,y)\asymp ab\left(\log(b)+\frac{1}{b}(a-b)\right)\asymp\max(ab\log b,a^{2}).

(6.2)

When
a=b=n
, the above formula agrees with the known estimates (
6.1
) for the
2
-torus,
H(x,y)\asymp n^{2}\log n
. Similarly, when
a=n
and
b=1
, we have
H(x,y)\asymp n^{2}
, which matches the
d=1
case. In general, we have the following result:

Proposition 6.2
.

Fix the integer
N
(the dimension).
Consider the simple random walk on the rectangular torus

\Gamma=\mathds{Z}_{a_{1}}\times\mathds{Z}_{a_{2}}\times\dotsb\times\mathds{Z}_{a_{N}},

where
1\leq a_{1}\leq a_{2}\leq\cdots\leq a_{N}
.
Then for
x,y\in\mathbb{V}
such that
d(x,y)\asymp D
,

H(x,y)\asymp_{N}\max\left\{\prodop\displaylimits_{i=1}^{N}a_{i},a_{N}a_{N-1}\log\left(\frac{a_{N-1}}{a_{N-2}}\right),a_{N}^{2}\right\}.

(6.3)

If
N<3
, one simply uses
N=3
and set appropriate
a_{i}
’s to
1
.
Note that with
N=3
and
a_{1}=1
, the above expression reduces to (
6.2
).

Proof.

In order to apply Theorem
5.2
, we first compute the sizes of balls in
\Gamma
.
For convenience, we set
a_{0}=1
and introduce the notation

A_{[j_{1};j_{2}]}:=\prodop\displaylimits_{j=j_{1}}^{j_{2}}a_{j}.

Moreover, since the torus is vertex transient, we can set
B(r)=B(x,r)
and
V(r)=B(x,r)
for any
x\in V
.
Note that as
r
grows past
a_{i}
, the contribution to the volume in the
i^{th}
coordinate is fixed at
a_{i}
.
Thus, for any
x\in\mathbb{V}
and
0\leq i\leq N-1
, we have

A_{[0;i]}\left(\frac{r}{2}\right)^{N-i}\leq\#B(r)\leq A_{[0;i]}(2r)^{N-i},\qquad\qquad\text{for }a_{i}<r\leq a_{i+1}.

(6.4)

In particular, when
0\leq r\leq a_{N-2}
, we know that
\#B(r)\geq 2^{-N}r^{3}
.
This implies that

A_{[0;N]}\leq\sumop\displaylimits_{n=0}^{a_{N-2}^{2}}\frac{1}{V(\sqrt{n})}\leq A_{[0;N]}2^{N}\left(1+\sumop\displaylimits_{n=1}^{\infty}n^{-3/2}\right).

(6.5)

Now we establish two useful facts. First,
suppose that for a particular
i
, we have
a_{i+1}\leq 2a_{i}
, i.e.

a_{i+1}/2\leq a_{i}\leq a_{i+1}
.
In this case, (
6.4
) become

A_{[0;i+1]}\left(\frac{1}{4}\right)\left(\frac{r}{2}\right)^{N-(i+1)}\leq\#B(r)\leq A_{[0;i+1]}2(2r)^{N-(i+1)},\qquad\qquad\text{for }a_{i}<r\leq a_{i+1}.

(6.6)

In other words, if
a_{i}
and
a_{i+1}
are of comparable size, the estimate that works for
i+1
also works for
i
.

Secondly, suppose for
i<N
, we have that
a_{N}\leq ca_{i}
for some constant
c>0
. Then we have

\sumop\displaylimits_{a_{i}^{2}<n\leq a_{N}^{2}}\frac{1}{V(\sqrt{n})}\leq C_{1}(a_{N}^{2}-a_{i}^{2})\frac{A_{[0;N]}}{A_{[0;N-i]}a_{i}^{i}}\leq C_{2}a_{N}^{2}\leq C_{2}A_{[0;N]}.

(6.7)

Now, we estimate

H(x,y)\asymp\sumop\displaylimits_{n=0}^{a_{N}^{2}}\frac{1}{V(\sqrt{n})}

depending on the relationship between
a_{N-2}\leq a_{N-1}\leq a_{N}
:

Case 1 (
(a_{N}\leq 2a_{N-1})\,\&\,(a_{N-1}\leq 2a_{N-2})
) :

In this case, we know that all three of the largest sides are comparable in size. By (
6.7
), we have

\displaystyle\sumop\displaylimits_{a_{N-2}^{2}<n\leq a_{N}^{2}}\frac{1}{V(\sqrt{n})}\leq C_{3}A_{[0;N]}.

Combined with (
6.5
), we have
H(x,y)\asymp A_{[0,N]}
, which is comparable to the left hand side of (
6.9
) in this case.

Case 2 (
(a_{N}\leq 2a_{N-1})\,\&\,(a_{N-1}\geq 2a_{N-2})
) :

First, by (
6.7
), we have

\displaystyle\sumop\displaylimits_{a_{N-1}^{2}<n\leq a_{N}^{2}}\frac{1}{V(\sqrt{n})}\leq C_{4}A_{[0;N]}.

Then,

\displaystyle\sumop\displaylimits_{a_{N-2}^{2}<n\leq a_{N-1}^{2}}\frac{1}{V(\sqrt{n})}\asymp a_{N}a_{N-1}\sumop\displaylimits_{a_{N-2}^{2}<n\leq a_{N-1}^{2}}\frac{1}{n}\asymp a_{N}a_{N-1}\log\left(\frac{a_{N-1}}{a_{N-2}}\right).

Combined with (
6.5
), we have
H(x,y)\asymp A_{[0,N]}+a_{N}a_{N-1}\log\left(\frac{a_{N-1}}{a_{N-2}}\right)
, giving the desired estimate.

Case 3 (
(a_{N}\geq 2a_{N-1})\,\&\,(a_{N-1}\leq 2a_{N-2})
) :

By (
6.6
), we have

\displaystyle\sumop\displaylimits_{a_{N-2}^{2}<n\leq a_{N}^{2}}\frac{1}{V(\sqrt{n})}\asymp a_{N}\sumop\displaylimits_{a_{N-2}^{2}<n\leq a_{N}^{2}}\frac{1}{\sqrt{n}}\asymp a_{N}(a_{N}-a_{N-2})\asymp a_{N}^{2},

where the last asymptotic is by
2a_{N-2}\leq a_{N}
.
Combined with (
6.5
), we have
H(x,y)\asymp A_{[0,N]}+a_{N}^{2})
.

Case 4 (
(a_{N}\geq 2a_{N-1})\,\&\,(a_{N-1}\geq 2a_{N-2})
) :

Similar to the computation in the previous cases, we have

\sumop\displaylimits_{a_{N-2}^{2}<n\leq a_{N-1}^{2}}\frac{1}{V(\sqrt{n})}+\sumop\displaylimits_{a_{N-1}^{2}<n\leq a_{N}^{2}}\frac{1}{V(\sqrt{n})}\asymp a_{N}a_{N-1}\log\left(\frac{a_{N-1}}{a_{N-2}}\right)+a_{N}^{2}.

Combined with (
6.5
), we have the desired estimate.

6.3.
Spaces with Ahlfors regularity

Definition 6.3
.

A finite graph
\Gamma=(\mathbb{V},E)
equipped with a probability measure
\pi
satisfies

the Ahlfors regularity condition
if there exists

such that

|\mathbb{V}|V(x,r)\asymp r^{\alpha}

For such a space,

Furthermore,

\sumop\displaylimits_{n=0}^{2D^{\theta}}\frac{1}{V(x,n^{1/\theta})}\asymp D^{\alpha}\sumop\displaylimits_{n=1}^{2D^{\theta}}n^{-\frac{\alpha}{\theta}}\asymp\begin{cases}D^{\alpha}&\text{if $\alpha>\theta$},\\
D^{\theta}\log D&\text{if $\alpha=\theta$},\\
D^{\theta}&\text{if $0\leq\alpha<\theta$}.\end{cases}

(6.8)

Assume that
(\Gamma,K,\pi)
satisfies
(\El),(\VD),(\PI(\theta))
, and
(\CS(\theta))
, for some
\theta\geq 2
, and is Ahlfors regular. Then, for any two points
a,b
in
\mathbb{V}
with

, we have

H(a,b)\asymp\begin{cases}D^{\alpha}&\text{if $\alpha>\theta$},\\
D^{\theta}\log D&\text{if $\alpha=\theta$},\\
D^{\theta}&\text{if $0\leq\alpha<\theta$}.\end{cases}

6.4.
Doubling spaces with
\theta
-fast volume growth

A finite graph
(\Gamma,\pi)
has
\theta
-fast volume growth if there exists
\epsilon
such that the volume function of
(\Gamma,K,\pi)
satisfies

\frac{V(x,r)}{\pi(x)}\geq c_{0}r^{\theta+\epsilon},\;\;1/2\leq r\leq D.

In such a case,

\sumop\displaylimits_{n=0}^{2D^{\theta}}\frac{1}{V(x,n^{1/\theta})}=\frac{1}{\pi(x)}\sumop\displaylimits_{n=0}^{2D^{\theta}}\frac{\pi(x)}{V(x,n^{1/\theta})}\asymp\frac{1}{\pi(x)}.

Assume that
(\Gamma,K,\pi)
satisfies
(\El),(\VD),(\PI(\theta))
, and
(\CS(\theta))
, for some
\theta\geq 2
and has
\theta
-fast volume growth, for any two points
a,b
in
\mathbb{V}
with
d(a,b)\asymp D
, we have

H(a,b)\asymp\frac{1}{\pi(b)}.

Under these hypotheses, one can in fact estimate
H(a,b)
for all
a,b\in\mathbb{V}
, not just those pairs with
d(a,b)\asymp D
. Indeed, we have
\mathcal{G}(b,b)\asymp 1/\pi(b)
and, by Proposition
3.1
,

|\mathcal{G}(a,b)|\leq C\sumop\displaylimits_{d(a,b)^{\theta}\leq n\leq 2D^{\theta}}\frac{1}{V(b,n^{1/\theta})}\leq C^{\prime}\frac{d(a,b)^{\theta}}{V(x,d(a,b))}\leq C^{\prime\prime}\frac{1}{\pi(b)}\frac{1}{d(a,b)^{\epsilon}}.

Hence,

H(a,b)=\mathcal{G}(b,b)-\mathcal{G}(a,b)\geq\frac{1}{\pi(b)}\left(c-\frac{C^{\prime\prime}}{d(a,b)^{\epsilon}}\right).

This suffices to prove the following result.

Proposition 6.4
.

Assume

satisfies

, and
(\CS(\theta))

\theta\geq 2

6.6.
Birth-death chains

Birth-death chains are a classical example of Markov chains representing the evolution of population over time.
The state space is
\{0,1,\dotsc,N\}
and transition probabilities can be specified by the information
\{(p_{k},r_{k},q_{k})\}_{k=0}^{N}
, with
q_{0}=p_{N}=0
.
Then the kernel driving the chain is

K(i,j)=\begin{cases}p_{i}&\text{$j=i+1$}\\
q_{i}&\text{$j=i-1$}\\
r_{i}&\text{$j=i$}\\
0&\text{otherwise}.\end{cases}

The reversible measure for this chain is proportional to
\prodop\displaylimits_{0}^{N-1}(p_{i}/q_{i+1})
.
Conversely, given a positive weight function on the state space,
w_{k}
,
k\in\{0,\dots,N\}
, we can choose appropriate
p_{k},r_{k}
and
q_{k}
so that the Markov chain is reversible with reversible measure proportional to
w_{k}
,

\pi(k)=\frac{w_{k}}{\sumop\displaylimits_{i=0}^{N}w_{k}}.

For instance
\pi(i)p_{i}=\pi(i+1)q_{i+1}=\frac{1}{2}\min\{\pi(i),\pi(i+1)\}
work (this is the Metropolis chain for
\pi
with symmetric simple random walk proposal). See, e.g.,
[
Sal99
]
.

Consider the chain with weight function
w_{k}=(1+k)^{\alpha}
for some
\alpha>0
.
It is clear that this chain satisfies
(\VD)
, and from
[
Sal99
]
, this chain also satisfies
(\PI(2))
.
Using Theorem

, we compute

\displaystyle V(0,r)\asymp\left(\frac{r}{N}\right)^{1+\alpha}H(N,0)\asymp N^{1+\alpha}\sumop\displaylimits_{n=1}^{N^{2}}n^{-\frac{\alpha+1}{2}}\asymp\begin{cases}N^{\alpha+1}&\text{if $\alpha>1$},\\
N^{2}\log N&\text{if $\alpha=1$},\\
N^{2}&\text{if $0\leq\alpha<1$}.\end{cases}

However,

\displaystyle V(N,r)\asymp\frac{rN^{\alpha}}{N^{1+\alpha}}=\frac{r}{N}H(0,N)\asymp N\sumop\displaylimits_{n=1}^{N^{2}}n^{-1/2}\asymp N^{2}.

and

6.7.
Fractal graphs

In the work of
[

]
, the authors show that a large class of fractal graphs satisfies the desired criteria for Theorem

, with
\theta
being the walk dimension of the fractal.
Here we will show how to use our main theorem to compute expected hitting times on two fractal graphs. These examples are example of Ahlfors-regular graphs.

Let
\texttt{ST}_{k}
be the
k^{th}
iteration of the Sierpinski triangle.
\texttt{ST}_{1}
is the complete graph with three vertices, a triangle graph.
At the
k^{th}
iteration, we use three copies of
\texttt{ST}_{k-1}
and glue them at the corners, see Figure

.

\texttt{ST}_{k}
has size

\theta=\log 5/\log 2
.
Let
o
and
p
be two corners of
\texttt{ST}_{k}
.
It is known that

\#B(o,r)\asymp\min(r^{\alpha},3^{k}),

where
\alpha=\log 3/\log 2
.
Applying Theorem
5.2
, we get

H(o,p)\asymp 3^{k}\sumop\displaylimits_{n=1}^{2^{k\theta}}n^{-\frac{\log 3}{\log 5}}\asymp 3^{k}\left(5^{k}\right)^{1-\frac{\log 3}{\log 5}}\asymp 5^{k}=D^{\theta},\;\;\theta=\frac{\log 5}{\log 2}.

Let
\texttt{Vic}_{k}
be the
k^{th}
iteration of the Vicsek fractal graph.

\texttt{Vic}_{0}
is a tree with one root vertex with four children.
From
\texttt{Vic}_{k-1}
, the next iteration
\texttt{Vic}_{k}

\texttt{Vic}_{k-1}
joined at the corners with four more copies of
\texttt{Vic}_{k-1}
, see Figure

.

\texttt{Vic}_{k}

, diameter

, and walk dimension

.
Let

and
p
be two corners of
\texttt{Vic}_{k}
.
We know that
\texttt{Vic}_{k}
has volume growth

\#B(o,r)\asymp\min(r^{\alpha},5^{k}),

.
Our main theorem gives

H(o,p)\asymp 5^{k}\sumop\displaylimits_{n=1}^{3^{k\theta}}n^{-\frac{\log 5}{\log 3+\log 5}}\asymp 5^{k}\left((3\cdot 5)^{k}\right)^{\frac{\log 3}{\log 3+\log 5}}\asymp 15^{k}=D^{\theta},\;\;\theta=1+\frac{\log 5}{\log 3}.

). Experts on analysis on fractals know that there are

\Gamma^{\diamond}
satisfies

t_{rel}(\Gamma^{\diamond})\asymp\max_{x,y\in\mathbb{V}}H_{\Gamma}(x,y),

where
H_{\Gamma}(x,y)
is the expected hitting time from
x
to
y
for the simple random walk on
G
.

Theorem
5.2
also then gives an estimate for the relaxation time for random walks on lamplighter groups.

Corollary 6.6
.

Suppose that the simple random walk on a vertex-transitive graph
\Gamma
satisfies

(\El),(\VD),(\PI(\theta))
, and
(\CS(\theta))
, for some
\theta\geq 2
.
Then, the associated random walk on
G^{\diamond}
satisfies

t_{rel}(\Gamma^{\diamond})\asymp\max_{x\in\mathbb{V}}\sumop\displaylimits_{0\leq n\leq 2D_{\Gamma}^{\theta}}\frac{1}{V_{\Gamma}(x,n^{1/\theta})},

where
D_{\Gamma}
is the diameter of
\Gamma
, and
V_{\Gamma}
is normalized volume of balls in
\Gamma
.

Proof.

Let
(\Gamma,K,\pi)
be the Markov kernel associated with the lazy simple random walk on
\Gamma
,
and
\mathcal{G}
be the Green function for the base graph,
\Gamma
.
Note that the upper bound of Theorem
5.2
can be applied for all
x,y\in\Gamma
, which gives the upper bound directly.
For the lower bound, we can restrict to
x,y
far apart and apply Theorem
5.2
to get

t_{rel}(\Gamma^{\diamond})\geq\max_{x,y:d(x,y)=D_{\Gamma}}H_{\Gamma}(x,y)\gtrsim\max_{x\in\mathbb{V}}\sumop\displaylimits_{0\leq n\leq 2D_{\Gamma}^{\theta}}\frac{1}{V_{\Gamma}(x,n^{1/\theta})}.

∎

Appendix A
Lemmas

Lemma A.1
.

Let
C_{2}>0
,
\theta\geq 2
and the Markov chain
(\Gamma,K,\pi)
satisfies
(\VD)
.
There exists a
C_{0}>0
such that for all
x,y\in\mathbb{V}
and
t\in[d(x,y),d(x,y)^{\theta}]
,

\frac{1}{V\left(x,t^{1/\theta}\right)}\exp\left(-C_{2}\left(\frac{d(x,y)^{\theta}}{t}\right)^{1/(\theta-1)}\right)\leq\frac{C_{0}}{V\left(x,d(x,y)\right)}

Proof.

Let
A\geq 1
be the doubling constant of
\Gamma
.
Since
(\Gamma,K,\pi)
satisfies
(\VD)
, for
0\leq m\leq n\leq\infty
, we know that

\frac{V(x,n)}{V(x,m)}\leq A\left(\frac{n}{m}\right)^{\frac{\log A}{\log 2}}.

(A.1)

See
[
DSC94
, Lemma 5.1]
for a proof.
Then for all
d(x,y)\leq t\leq d(x,y)^{\theta}
,

\frac{V\left(x,t^{1/\theta}\right)}{V(x,d(x,y))}\geq\frac{1}{A}\left(\frac{t^{1/\theta}}{d(x,y)}\right)^{\frac{\log A}{\log 2}}.

(A.2)

Let
z=d(x,y)/t^{1/\theta}
.
Then, choose
C_{0}>0
large enough so that

\frac{\log A}{\log 2}\log z\leq\log C_{0}-\log A+C_{2}z^{\frac{\theta}{\theta-1}}.

for all
z\geq 1
.
We know that this is possible because the derivative of the left hand side is positive decreasing, while that of the right hand side is positive increasing.
After rearranging and taking exponential on both sides, we get

\frac{1}{A}\left(\frac{t^{1/\theta}}{d(x,y)}\right)^{\frac{\log A}{\log 2}}\geq C_{0}\exp\left(-C_{2}\left(\frac{d(x,y)^{\theta}}{t}\right)^{\frac{1}{\theta-1}}\right).

Combined with (
A.2
), this gives us the desired result.
∎

Lemma A.2
.

Let
(\Gamma,K,\pi)
be a Markov chain satisfying
(\VD)
,
x\in\mathbb{V}
and
c_{0}\in(0,1)
.
Then there exists a constant
C>0
, that depends on
c_{0}
, such that

V(x,c_{0}D)>C.

Proof.

Let
A>0
be the doubling constant of
\Gamma
.
Then, we use the same formula as in the previous lemma, (
A.1
) with
m=c_{0}D
and
n=D
:

\displaystyle\frac{1}{V(x,c_{0}D)}\leq A\left(\frac{1}{c_{0}}\right)^{\frac{\log A}{\log 2}}.

∎
