Abstract
This paper investigates functions from ℝ d \mathbb{R}^{d} to ℝ ∪ { ± ∞ } \mathbb{R}\cup\{\pm\infty\} that satisfy axioms of linearity wherever allowed by extended-value arithmetic. They have a nontrivial structure defined inductively on d d , and unlike finite linear functions, they require Ω ⁡ ( d 2 ) \Omega(d^{2}) parameters to uniquely identify. In particular they can capture vertical tangent planes to epigraphs: a function is convex if and only if it is the pointwise supremum of a family of “affine extended” functions. These results are applied to the well-known characterization of proper scoring rules for the finite-dimensional case: it is carefully and rigorously extended here to a more constructive form. In particular it is investigated when proper scoring rules can be constructed from a given convex function.



Keywords:
convex analysis, proper scoring rules, linear functions, extended reals

Introduction

The extended real number line, denoted
\overline{\mathbb{R}}=\mathbb{R}\cup\{\pm\infty\}
, is widely useful particularly in convex analysis.
But I am not aware of an answer to the question: What would it mean to have a “linear”
f:\mathbb{R}^{d}\to\overline{\mathbb{R}}
?
The extended reals have enough structure to hope for a useful answer, but differ enough from a vector space to need investigation.
A natural approach is that
f
must satisfy the usual linearity axioms of homogeneity and additivity whenever legal under extended-reals arithmetic.
Here is an example when
d=3
:

f(x,y,z)=\begin{cases}\text{if $z>0$}&\rightarrow~~\infty\\
\text{if $z<0$}&\rightarrow~~-\infty\\
\text{if $z=0$}&\rightarrow~~\begin{cases}\text{if $y>0$}&\rightarrow~~\infty\\
\text{if $y<0$}&\rightarrow~~-\infty\\
\text{if $y=0$}&\rightarrow~~x.\end{cases}\end{cases}

We will see that all
linear extended
functions (Definition
2.1
) on
\mathbb{R}^{d}
have the above inductive format, descending dimension-by-dimension until reaching a finite linear function (Proposition
2.7
).
In fact, a natural representation of this procedure is parsimonious, requiring as many as
\Omega(d^{2})
real-valued parameters to uniquely identify an extended linear function on
\mathbb{R}^{d}
(Proposition
2.8
).
This structure raises the possibility, left for future work, that linear extended functions are significantly nontrivial on infinite-dimensional spaces.

Linear extended functions arise naturally as
extended subgradients
(Definition
3.1
) of a convex function
g
.
These can be used to construct
affine extended
functions (Definition
3.4
) capturing vertical supporting hyperplanes to convex epigraphs.
Along such a hyperplane, intersected with the boundary of the domain,
g
can sometimes have the structure of an arbitrary convex function in
d-1
dimensions.
For example, the above
f
is an extended subgradient, at the point
(1,0,0)
, of the discontinuous convex function

g(x,y,z)=\begin{cases}\text{if $z>0$}&\rightarrow~~\infty\\
\text{if $z<0$}&\rightarrow~~0\\
\text{if $z=0$}&\rightarrow~~\begin{cases}\text{if $y>0$}&\rightarrow~~\infty\\
\text{if $y<0$}&\rightarrow~~0\\
\text{if $y=0$}&\rightarrow~~\frac{1}{2}x^{2}.\end{cases}\end{cases}

A function is convex if and only if it is the pointwise supremum of affine extended functions (Proposition
3.7
).
We also find it is convex if and only if it has an extended subgradient at every point where it is finite, assuming a convex effective domain (Proposition
3.10
).

The role of vertical tangents to epigraphs has certainly been noted and appreciated in convex analysis.
For example,
Rockafellar and Wets 1998
utilizes “horizon subgradients”.
However, such approaches motivated by e.g. optimization problems focus on niceness properties, e.g. benign topological properties, not explored here.
Recently,
Dudík et al. 2022
independently discover linear extended functions with an optimization and topological motivation.
Beginning with an algorithmic definition, essentially Algorithm
1
,
Dudík et al. 2022
shows that linear extended functions are characterized as being both convex and concave while vanishing at the origin.
That work also considers extended subgradients, there called
astral dual subgradients
.

Proper scoring rules.

The original motivation for this investigation was the study of scoring rules: functions
S
assigning a score
S(p,y)
to any prediction
p
(a probability distribution over outcomes) and observed outcome
y
.
This
S
is called
proper
if reporting the true distribution of the outcome maximizes expected score.
A well-known characterization states that proper scoring rules arise as and only as subgradients or “subtangents” of convex functions
(
McCarthy 1956
;
Savage 1971
;
Schervish 1989
)
.

Modern works
(
Gneiting and Raftery 2007
;
Frongillo and Kash 2021
)
generalize this characterization to allow scores of
-\infty
, corresponding to convex functions that are not subdifferentiable
1
1

1

They also consider infinite-dimensional outcome spaces, which are not treated here.
.
These works therefore replace subgradients with briefly-defined generalized “subtangents”, but they slightly sidestep the questions tackled head-on here: existence of these objects and their roles in convex analysis.
So these characterizations are not fully constructive.
In particular, it may surprise even experts that rigorous answers are not available to the following questions, given a convex function
g
:
(a)
under what conditions can one construct a proper scoring rule from it?
(b)
when is the resulting scoring rule necessarily
strictly
proper?
(c)
do the answers depend on which subgradients of
g
are used, when multiple choices are available?

Section
4
uses the machinery of extended linear functions to prove a “construction of proper scoring rules” that answers such questions for the finite-outcome case.

First, Theorem
4.11
allows predictions to range over the entire simplex.
It implies a slightly-informal claim of
Gneiting and Raftery 2007
, Section 3.1:
Any convex function on the probability simplex gives rise to a proper scoring rule (using any choices of its extended subgradients).

Furthermore, it shows that a strictly convex function can
only
give rise to strictly proper scoring rules and vice versa.
These facts are likely known by experts in the community; however, I do not know of formal claims and proofs.
This may be because, when scoring rules can be
-\infty
, proofs seem to require significant formalization and investigation of “subtangents”.
In this paper, this investigation is supplied by linear extended functions and characterizations of (strictly) convex functions as those that have (uniquely supporting) extended subgradients everywhere.

Next, Theorem
4.16
allows prediction spaces
\mathcal{P}
to be any nonempty subset of the simplex.
It sharpens the characterizations of
Gneiting and Raftery 2007
;
Frongillo and Kash 2021
, for the finite-outcome case, by showing
S
to be proper if and only if it can be constructed from extended subgradients of a convex
g
that is
interior-locally-Lipschitz
(Definition
4.14
).
It also answers the construction questions
(a)-(c)
above, e.g. showing that given such a
g
, some but not necessarily all selections of its extended subgradients give rise to proper scoring rules.
An important remaining direction is an analogous construction for truthful scoring functions on general type spaces, as studied by
Frongillo and Kash 2021
.

Preliminaries.

Functions in this work are defined on Euclidean spaces of dimension
d\geq 0
.
I ask the reader’s pardon for abusing notation slightly: I use
\mathbb{R}^{d}
refer to any such space.
For example, I may argue that a certain function exists on domain
\mathbb{R}^{d}
, then refer to that function as being defined on a given
d
-dimensional subspace of
\mathbb{R}^{d+1}
.

Let
g:\mathbb{R}^{d}\to\overline{\mathbb{R}}
.
The
effective domain

\textrm{effdom}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(g}}\right)
of
g
is
\{x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}~:~g(x)\neq\infty\}
.
The function
g
is
convex
if its
epigraph

\{(x,y)\in\mathbb{R}^{d}\times\mathbb{R}:y\geq g(x)\}
is a convex set.
Equivalently, it is convex if for all
x,x^{\prime}\in\textrm{effdom}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(g}}\right)
and all
0<\rho<1
,
g(\rho\cdot x+(1-\rho)x^{\prime})\leq\rho g(x)+(1-\rho)g(x^{\prime})
, observing that this sum may contain
-\infty
but not
+\infty
.
It is
strictly
convex on a convex set
\mathcal{P}\subseteq\textrm{effdom}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(g}}\right)
if the previous inequality is always strict for
x,x^{\prime}\in\mathcal{P}
with
x\neq x^{\prime}
.
A function
g
is
concave
if
-g
, i.e. the function
x\mapsto-g(x)
, is convex.

We say a function
h

minorizes

g
if
h(x)\leq g(x)
for all
x
.

\textrm{cl}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(A}}\right)
denotes the closure of the set
A
and
\textrm{int}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(A}}\right)
its interior.
For
a\in\overline{\mathbb{R}}
, the sign function is
\textrm{sign}(a)=1
if
a>0
,
\textrm{sign}(a)=0
if
a=0
, and
\textrm{sign}(a)=-1
if
a<0
.

The extended reals.

The extended reals,
\overline{\mathbb{R}}=\mathbb{R}\cup\{\pm\infty\}
, have the following rules of arithmetic.
For
a,b\in\overline{\mathbb{R}}
, the sum
a+b
is illegal if
\{a,b\}=\{\pm\infty\}
.
Otherwise it is legal.
We have
\infty+\infty=\infty
and analogously for
-\infty
.
For any
\alpha\in\mathbb{R}
,
\alpha+\infty=\infty
,
\alpha-\infty=-\infty
, and

\alpha\cdot\infty=\begin{cases}\infty&\alpha>0\\
0&\alpha=0\\
-\infty&\alpha<0.\end{cases}

Addition is associative and commutative as long as it is legal; multiplication of multiple reals and possibly one non-real is associative and commutative.
Multiplication by a real distributes over legal sums.

\overline{\mathbb{R}}
has the following rules of comparison:
-\infty<\beta<\infty
for every
\beta\in\mathbb{R}
.
The supremum of a subset of
\overline{\mathbb{R}}
is the least element of
\overline{\mathbb{R}}
that upper-bounds every member of the subset.
In particular,
\sup A=\infty
if
\infty\in A
or if
A
contains an unbounded-above set of reals.
The analogous facts hold for the infimum.
Finally,
\inf\emptyset=\infty
and
\sup\emptyset=-\infty
.
I will not put a topology on
\overline{\mathbb{R}}
in this work.

2
Linear extended functions

The following definition makes sense with a general real vector space
\mathcal{X}
in place of
\mathbb{R}^{d}
, but it remains to be seen which results can extend.

Definition 2.1
.

Call the function
f:\mathbb{R}^{d}\to\overline{\mathbb{R}}
a
linear extended function
if:

1.

(scaling) For all
x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
and all
\alpha\in\mathbb{R}
:
f(\alpha x)=\alpha f(x)
.

2.

(additivity) For all
x,x^{\prime}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
: If
f(x)+f(x^{\prime})
is legal, i.e.
\{f(x),f(x^{\prime})\}\neq\{\pm\infty\}
, then
f(x+x^{\prime})=f(x)+f(x^{\prime})
.

If the range of
f
is included in
\mathbb{R}
, Definition
2.1
reduces to the usual definition of a linear function.
For clarity, this paper may emphasize the distinction by calling such
f

finite linear
.

To see that this definition can be satisfied nontrivially, let
f_{1},f_{2}:\mathbb{R}^{d}\to\mathbb{R}
be finite linear and consider
f(x):=\infty\cdot f_{1}(x)+f_{2}(x)
.
With a representation
f_{1}(x)=v_{1}\cdot x
, we have

f(x)=\begin{cases}\infty&v_{1}\cdot x>0\\
-\infty&v_{1}\cdot x<0\\
f_{2}(x)&v_{1}\cdot x=0.\end{cases}

Claim 2.2
.

Any such
f
is an example of a linear extended function.

Proof.

Multiplication by a scalar distributes over the legal sum
\infty\cdot f_{1}(x)+f_{2}(x)
, so
\alpha f(x)=\infty\cdot f_{1}(\alpha x)+f_{2}(\alpha x)=f(\alpha x)
.
To obtain additivity of
f
, consider cases on the pair
(v_{1}\cdot x,~v_{1}\cdot x^{\prime})
.
If both are zero,
f(x+x^{\prime})=f_{2}(x+x^{\prime})=f_{2}(x)+f_{2}(x^{\prime})=f(x)+f(x^{\prime})
.
If they have opposite signs, then
\{f(x),f(x^{\prime})\}=\{\pm\infty\}
and the requirement is vacuous.
If one is positive and the other nonnegative, then
v_{1}\cdot(x+x^{\prime})>0
, so we have
f(x+x^{\prime})=\infty=f(x)+f(x^{\prime})
.
The remaining case, one negative and the other nonpositive, is exactly analogous.
∎

Such
f
are not all the linear extended functions, because the subspace where
v_{1}\cdot x=0
need not be entirely finite-valued.
As in the introduction’s example, it can itself be divided into infinite and neg-infinite open halfspaces, with
f
finite only on some further-reduced subspace.
We will show that all linear extended functions can be constructed in this recursive way, Algorithm
1
(heavily relying on the setting of
\mathbb{R}^{d}
).

Algorithm 1

Computing a linear extended function
f:\mathbb{R}^{d}\to\overline{\mathbb{R}}
.

Parameters:

t\in\{0,\ldots,d\}
; finite linear
\hat{f}:\mathbb{R}^{d-t}\to\mathbb{R}
; if
t\geq 1
, unit vectors
v_{1}\in\mathbb{R}^{d},\ldots,v_{t}\in\mathbb{R}^{d-t+1}

Input:

x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}

for

j=1,\ldots,t

do

if

v_{j}\cdot x>0

then

return
\infty

else

if

v_{j}\cdot x<0

then

return
-\infty

end

if

reparameterize
x
as a vector in
\mathbb{R}^{d-j}
, a member of the subspace
\{x^{\prime}~:~v_{j}\cdot x^{\prime}=0\}

end

for

return
\hat{f}(x)

First, two structural results.
Proposition
2.3
, inspired by
Dudík et al. 2022
(which uses a different construction than our axiomatic one), states that linear extended functions are exactly those that are concave, convex, and equal zero at the origin.
Lemma
2.4
gives a useful decomposition of the domain of a linear extended function.

Proposition 2.3

(c.f.
Dudík et al. 2022
)
.

f:\mathbb{R}^{d}\to\overline{\mathbb{R}}
is linear extended if and only if
f(\vec{0})=0
and
f
is both concave and convex.

Proof.

(\implies)

Let
f
be linear extended.
For any
x
, by the scaling axiom,
0=0f(x)=f(0x)=f(\vec{0})
, as required.
Now let
f(x),f(x^{\prime})<\infty
, i.e.
x,x^{\prime}
are in the effective domain of
f
, and let
\rho\in[0,1]
.
By the scaling and addition axioms, we have
\rho f(x)+(1-\rho)f(x^{\prime})=f(\rho x)+f((1-\rho)x^{\prime})=f(\rho x+(1-\rho)x^{\prime})
, proving that
f
is convex.
Note that all of the above sums are legal, because none of the terms can be
+\infty
.
Using the axioms directly,
f
is linear extended if and only if
-f
is linear extended, so
-f
is also convex, so
f
is concave as well.

(\impliedby)

Let
f
be convex and concave with
f(\vec{0})=0
.
We will repeatedly use that by convexity and concavity, for
\rho\in[0,1]
, we must have
f(\rho y+(1-\rho)y^{\prime})=\rho f(y)+(1-\rho)f(y^{\prime})
whenever the sum is legal, which in particular holds if one summand is finite.

We first show
f
satisfies the scaling axiom for nonnegative coefficients.
Let
x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
.
If
\alpha=0
, then
f(\alpha x)=f(\vec{0})=0=\alpha f(x)
.
If
\alpha\in(0,1]
, then
f(\alpha x)=f(\alpha x+(1-\alpha)\vec{0})=\alpha f(x)+(1-\alpha)f(\vec{0})=\alpha f(x)
, using convexity-concavity and observing that the sum is legal.
If
\alpha>1
, then use the previous case:
\alpha f(x)=\alpha f(\tfrac{1}{\alpha}(\alpha x))=\alpha\tfrac{1}{\alpha}f(\alpha x)=f(\alpha x)
.

Next, we show additivity.
Use positive scaling and convexity-concavity to obtain
f(x+x^{\prime})=2f(\tfrac{1}{2}x+\tfrac{1}{2}x^{\prime})=2\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\tfrac{1}{2}f(x)+\tfrac{1}{2}f(x^{\prime})}}\right)=f(x)+f(x^{\prime})
whenever the latter sum is legal.

Finally, we show scaling for negative coefficients, starting with
\alpha=-1
.
By additivity, we have
0=f(x+-x)=f(x)+f(-x)
whenever the sum is legal.
If the sum is legal, then
f(x)
is finite and
f(-x)=-f(x)
.
If the sum is illegal, then
f(x)\in\{\pm\infty\}
and
f(-x)=-f(x)
.
We conclude
f(-x)=-f(x)
for all
x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
.
Finally, for arbitrary
\alpha<0
, we have
\alpha f(x)=-(-\alpha f(x))=-f(-\alpha x)=f(-(-\alpha x))=f(\alpha x)
, where we used positive scaling and scaling by
-1
.
∎

Now we begin to investigate the structure of linear extended functions.
Recall that a subset
S
of
\mathbb{R}^{d}
is a
convex cone
if, when
x,x^{\prime}\in S
and
\alpha,\beta>0
, we have
\alpha x+\beta x^{\prime}\in S
.
A convex cone need not contain
\vec{0}
.

Lemma 2.4

(Decomposition)
.

f:\mathbb{R}^{d}\to\overline{\mathbb{R}}
is linear extended if and only if: (1) the sets
S^{+}=f^{-1}(\infty)
and
S^{-}=f^{-1}(-\infty)
are convex cones with
S^{+}=-S^{-}
, and (2) the set
F=f^{-1}(\mathbb{R})
is a subspace of
\mathbb{R}^{d}
, and (3)
f
coincides with some finite linear function
\hat{f}
on
F
.

Proof.

(\implies)
Let
f
be linear extended.
The scaling axiom implies
f(\vec{0})=0
, so
\vec{0}\in F
.
Observe that
F
is closed under scaling (by the scaling axiom) and addition (the addition axiom is never vacuous on
x,x^{\prime}\in F
).
So it is a subspace.

f
satisfies scaling and additivity (never vacuous) for all members of
F
, so it is finite linear there.
This proves (2) and (3).
Next: the scaling axiom implies that if
x\in S^{+}
then
-x\in S^{-}
, giving
S^{+}=-S^{-}
as claimed.
Now let
x,x^{\prime}\in S^{+}
and
\alpha,\beta>0
.
The scaling axiom implies
\alpha x
and
\beta x^{\prime}
are in
S^{+}
.
The additivity axiom implies
\alpha x+\beta x^{\prime}\in S^{+}
, proving it is a convex cone.
We immediately get
S^{-}=-S^{+}
is a convex cone as well, proving (1).

(\impliedby)
Suppose
F
is a subspace on which
f
is finite linear and
S^{+}=-S^{-}
is a convex cone.
We immediate obtain the useful
f(\vec{0})=0
by finite linearity on a subspace (which must contain
\vec{0}
).
We now show
f
satisfies the two axioms needed to be linear extended.

Scaling: if
x\in F
, the axiom follows from closure of
F
under scaling and finite linearity of
f
on
F
.
Else, let
\alpha\in\mathbb{R}
and
x\in S^{+}
(the case
x\in S^{-}
is exactly analogous).
If
\alpha>0
, then
\alpha x\in S^{+}
because it is a convex cone, which gives
f(\alpha x)=\alpha f(x)=\infty
.
If
\alpha=0
, then
f(\alpha x)=\alpha f(x)=0
.
If
\alpha<0
, then use that
-x\in S^{-}
by assumption, and since
S^{-}
is a cone and
-\alpha>0
, we have
(-\alpha)(-x)\in S^{-}
.
In other words,
f(\alpha x)=-\infty=\alpha f(x)
, as required.

For additivity, let
x,x^{\prime}
be given.

•

If
x,x^{\prime}\in F
, additivity follows from closure of
F
under addition and finite linearity of
f
on
F
.

•

If
x,x^{\prime}\in S^{+}
, then
x+x^{\prime}\in S^{+}
as required because it is a convex cone; analogously for
x,x^{\prime}\in S^{-}
.

•

If
x\in S^{+},x^{\prime}\in S^{-}
or vice versa, the axiom is vacuously satisfied.

The remaining case is, without loss of generality,
x\in S^{+},x^{\prime}\in F
(the proof is identical for
x\in S^{-},x^{\prime}\in F
).
We must show
x+x^{\prime}\in S^{+}
.
Because
F
is a subspace and
x^{\prime}\in F,x\not\in F
, we must have
x+x^{\prime}\not\in F
.
Now suppose for contradiction that
x+x^{\prime}\in S^{-}
.
We have
-x\in S^{-}
because
S^{-}=-S^{+}
.
Because
S^{-}
is a convex cone, it is closed under addition, so
x+x^{\prime}+(-x)=x^{\prime}\in S^{-}
, a contradiction.
So
x+x^{\prime}\in S^{+}
.
∎

So far, our results (Proposition
2.3
and Lemma
2.4
) have not actually required the domain to be finite-dimensional.
To proceed, however, we will begin to rely heavily on induction on the dimension
d
.
A useful corollary of Lemma
2.4
is the following definition:

Definition 2.5

(Rank)
.

The
rank
of a linear extended function
f:\mathbb{R}^{d}\to\overline{\mathbb{R}}
is
d-k
, where
k
is the dimension of the subspace
F=f^{-1}(\mathbb{R})
.

In other words, the rank is the number of dimensions along which
f
is vertical; we will show that it equals the (unique) number of vectors
t
required to implement
f
in Algorithm
1
.
This will also imply that Definition
2.5
coincides with the definition of
astral rank
in
Dudík et al. 2022
.

The effective domain of a linear extended function, i.e. the set
F\cup S^{-}
, at least includes an open halfspace.
If
f
has rank zero (is finite linear), the effective domain is
\mathbb{R}^{d}
; if rank one, it is a closed halfspace
\{x~:~v_{1}\cdot x\leq 0\}
; and otherwise it is neither a closed nor open set.
The effective domain is an example of a
hemispace

(
Martinez-Legaz and Singer 1988
)
, a convex set whose complement is convex.

Lemma 2.6

(Recursive definition)
.

f:\mathbb{R}^{d}\to\overline{\mathbb{R}}
is linear extended if and only if one of the following hold:

1.

f
is finite linear (this case must hold if
d=0
), or

2.

There exists a unit vector
v_{1}
and linear extended function
f_{2}
on the
d-1
dimensional subspace
\{x~:~v_{1}\cdot x=0\}
such that
f(x)=f_{2}(x)
if
x\cdot v_{1}=0
, else
f(x)=\infty\cdot\textrm{sign}(v_{1}\cdot x)
.

Proof.

(\implies)
Suppose
f
is linear extended.
The case
d=0
is immediate, as
f(\vec{0})=0
by the scaling axiom.
So let
d\geq 1
and suppose
f
is not finite linear; we show case (2) holds.
Let
S^{+}=f^{-1}(\infty)
,
S^{-}=f^{-1}(-\infty)
, and
F=f^{-1}(\mathbb{R})
.
Recall from Lemma
2.4
that
F
is a subspace, necessarily of dimension
<d
by assumption that
f
is not finite; meanwhile
S^{+}=-S^{-}
and both are convex cones.

We first claim that there is an open halfspace on which
f(x)=\infty
, i.e. included in
S^{+}
.
First,
\textrm{cl}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(S^{+}}}\right)
includes a closed halfspace: if not, the set
\textrm{cl}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(S^{+}}}\right)\cup\textrm{cl}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(S^{-}}}\right)\neq\mathbb{R}^{d}
and then its complement, an open set, would necessarily have affine dimension
d
yet would be included in
F
, a contradiction.
Now, because
S^{+}
is convex, it includes the relative interior of
\textrm{cl}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(S^{+}}}\right)
, so it includes an open halfspace.

Write this open halfspace
\{x~:~v_{1}\cdot x>0\}
for some unit vector
v_{1}
.
Because
S^{-}=-S^{+}
, we have
f(x)=-\infty
on the complement
\{x~:~v_{1}\cdot x<0\}
.
Let
f_{2}
be the restriction of
f
to the remaining set, the
d-1
dimensional subspace
\{x~:~v_{1}\cdot x=0\}
.
Because
f
satisfies the axioms of a linear extended function,
f_{2}
is a linear extended function as well.

(\impliedby)
If case (1) holds and
f
is finite linear, then it is immediately linear extended as well.
In case (2), will show that
f
satisfies the conditions of Lemma
2.4
.
First, apply Lemma
2.4
to the linear extended

Finally, characterizations of strict convexity will be useful for scoring rule applications.
We consider proper convex functions, where there exist extended subgradients and affine extended supports everywhere on

Say an affine extended function is

Lemma 3.11

(Strict convexity)
.

Let
g:\mathbb{R}^{d}\to\mathbb{R}\cup\{\infty\}
be convex, and let
\mathcal{P}\subseteq\textrm{effdom}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(g}}\right)
be a convex set.
The following are equivalent: (1)
g
is strictly convex on
\mathcal{P}
; (2) no two distinct points in
\mathcal{P}
share an extended subgradient; (3) every affine extended function supports
g
in
\mathcal{P}
at most once; (4)
g
has at each
x\in\mathcal{P}
a uniquely supporting affine extended function.

Although the differences between (2,3,4) are small, they may be useful in different scenarios.
For example, proving (4) may be the easiest way to show
g
is strictly convex, whereas (3) may be the more powerful implication of strict convexity.

Proof.

We prove a ring of contrapositives.

(\lnot 1~\implies~\lnot 4)

If
g
is convex but not strictly convex on
\mathcal{P}\subseteq\textrm{effdom}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(g}}\right)
, then there are two points
a,c\in\mathcal{P}
with
a\neq c
and there is
0<\rho<1
such that, for
b=\rho\cdot a+(1-\rho)c
,
g(b)=\rho g(a)+(1-\rho))g(c)
.
We show
g
has no uniquely supporting
h
at
b
; intuitively, tangents at
b
must be tangent at
a
and
c
as well.

Let
h
be any supporting affine extended function at

and write
h(x)=g(b)+f(x-b)
.
Because
h
is supporting,
g(c)\geq h(c)=g(b)+f(c-b)
, implying with the axioms of linear extended functions that
f(b-c)\geq g(b)-g(c)
.
Now,
b-c=\rho(a-c)=\frac{\rho}{1-\rho}(a-b)
.
So
f(b-c)=\frac{\rho}{1-\rho}f(a-b)
.
Similarly,
g(b)-g(c)=\rho(g(a)-g(c))=\frac{\rho}{1-\rho}(g(a)-g(b))
.
So
f(a-b)\geq g(a)-g(b)
, so
h(a)=g(b)+f(a-b)\geq g(a)
.
But because

supports
g
, we also have
h(a)\leq g(a)
, so
h(a)=g(a)
and
h
supports
g
at
a
.

(\lnot 4~\implies~\lnot 3)

Almost immediate:
g
has a supporting affine extended function at every point in
\mathcal{P}
because it has an extended subgradient everywhere (Proposition
3.2
).
If
\lnot 4
, then one supports
g
at two points in
\mathcal{P}
.

(\lnot 3~\implies~\lnot 2)

Suppose an affine extended
h
supports
g
at two distinct points
a,b\in\mathcal{P}
.
Using Observation
3.6
, we can write

for a unique linear extended

, so

is an extended subgradient at two distinct points.

(\lnot 2~\implies~\lnot 1)

Suppose
f
is an extended subgradient at distinct points
a,c\in\mathcal{P}
.
By definition of extended subgradient, we have
g(a)\geq g(c)+f(a-c)
and
g(c)\geq g(a)-f(a-c)
, implying
f(a-c)=g(a)-g(c)
.

Now let
b=\rho\cdot a+(1-\rho)c
for any

.
By convexity and linear extended axioms,
g(b)\geq g(a)-f(a-b)=g(a)-f((1-\rho)(a-c))=g(a)-(1-\rho)(g(a)-g(c))=\rho g(a)+(1-\rho)g(c)
.
So

is not strictly convex.
∎

The extended subdifferential.

Although its topological properties and general usefulness are unclear, we should probably not conclude without defining the
extended subdifferential
of the function
g:\mathbb{R}^{d}\to\overline{\mathbb{R}}
at
x
to be the set of extended subgradients of
g
at
x
, nonempty if
g(x)\in\mathbb{R}
.
On the interior of
\textrm{effdom}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(g}}\right)
, the extended subdifferential can only contain finite subgradients.
But note that if the effective domain has affine dimension smaller than
\mathbb{R}^{d}
, then the epigraph is contained in one of its vertical tangent supporting hyperplanes.
In this case, the relative interior, although it has finite subgradients, will have extended ones as well that take on non-finite values outside the effective domain.

4
Proper scoring rules

This section will first define scoring rules; then recall their characterization and discuss why it is not fully constructive; then use linear extended functions to prove more complete and constructive versions.
In particular, given any subset
\mathcal{P}
of a finite-dimensional probability simplex, Theorem
4.16
constructively characterizes the convex functions (or
entropies
) that give rise to a proper scoring rule.

4.1
Definitions

\mathcal{Y}
is a finite set of mutually exclusive and exhaustive outcomes, also called observations.
For a vector
v\in\mathbb{R}^{\mathcal{Y}}
, we write
v(y)
for the
y
th component of
v
.
The probability simplex on
\mathcal{Y}
is
\Delta_{\mathcal{Y}}=\{p\in\mathbb{R}^{\mathcal{Y}}~:~(\forall y)~p(y)\geq 0~,~\sum_{y\in\mathcal{Y}}p(y)=1\}
.
The
support
of
p\in\Delta_{\mathcal{Y}}
is
\textrm{Supp}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(p}}\right)=\{y:p(y)>0\}
.
The distribution with
p(y)=1
is denoted
\delta_{y}\in\Delta_{\mathcal{Y}}
.

Proper scoring rules model an expert providing a forecast
p\in\Delta_{\mathcal{Y}}
, after which
y\in\mathcal{Y}
is observed and the expert’s score is
S(p,y)
.
The expert chooses
p
to maximize expected score according to an internal belief
q\in\Delta_{\mathcal{Y}}
.
More generally, it is sometimes assumed that reports and beliefs are restricted to a subset
\mathcal{P}\subseteq\Delta_{\mathcal{Y}}
.
Two of the most well-known are the log scoring rule, where
S(p,y)=\log p(y)
; and the quadratic scoring rule, where
S(p,y)=-\|\delta_{y}-p\|_{2}^{2}
.

Definition 4.1

(Scoring rule, regular)
.

Let
\mathcal{Y}
be finite and
\mathcal{P}\subseteq\Delta_{\mathcal{Y}}
, nonempty.
A function
S:\mathcal{P}\times\mathcal{Y}\to\overline{\mathbb{R}}
is termed a
scoring rule
.
It is
regular
if
S(p,y)\neq\infty
for all
p\in\mathcal{P}
,
y\in\mathcal{Y}
.

Regular scoring rules are nice for two reasons.
First, calculating expected scores such as
\sum_{y}q(y)S(p,y)
may lead to illegal sums if
S(p,y)\in\overline{\mathbb{R}}
, but regular scores guarantee that the sum is legal (using that
q(y)\geq 0
).
Second, even if illegal sums were no problem, allowing scores of
S(p,y)=\infty
leads to strange and unexciting rules:
p
is an optimal report for any belief
q
unless
q(y)=0
.
In contrast, allowing
S(p,y)=-\infty
in some cases can be reasonable; for example, this is the case for the popular log score when
p(y)=0
, but
y
is observed.

Definition 4.2

(Expected score, strictly proper)
.

Given a regular scoring rule
S:\mathcal{P}\times\mathcal{Y}\to\mathbb{R}\cup\{-\infty\}
, the
expected score
for report
p\in\mathcal{P}
under belief
q\in\Delta_{\mathcal{Y}}
is written
S(p;q):=\sum_{y\in\mathcal{Y}}q(y)S(p,y)
.
The regular scoring rule
S
is
proper
if for all
p,q\in\mathcal{P}
with
p\neq q
,
S(p;q)\leq S(q;q)
, and it is
strictly proper
if this inequality is always strict.

The log and quadratic scoring rules mentioned above are strictly proper.
An example of a scoring rule that is not proper is
S(p,y)=p(y)
.
For more background, we refer the reader to
Gneiting and Raftery 2007
.

Why use a general definition of scoring rules if the only interesting rules are regular?
It will be useful for technical reasons: We will consider “subtangent” constructions that always yield a well-defined scoring rule, which is proper if and only if it is regular.
We can then focus on conditions under which the scoring rule is regular.
Understanding these conditions is a main contribution of Theorem
4.11
and particularly Theorem
4.16
.

Subtangent rules and entropies.

The well-known proper scoring rule characterization, discussed next, roughly states that all regular proper scoring rules can be written in the following form.

Definition 4.3

(Subtangent rule)
.

If a scoring rule
S:\mathcal{P}\times\mathcal{Y}\to\overline{\mathbb{R}}
satisfies

\displaystyle S(p,y)

\displaystyle=g(p)+f_{p}(\delta_{y}-p)

\displaystyle\forall p\in\mathcal{P},\forall y\in\mathcal{Y}

(4)

for some convex function
g:\mathbb{R}^{\mathcal{Y}}\to\mathbb{R}\cup\{\infty\}
with
\mathcal{P}\subseteq\textrm{effdom}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(g}}\right)
and some selection of its extended subgradients,
f_{p}
at each
p\in\mathcal{P}
, then call
S
a
subtangent rule (of
g
)
.

The geometric intuition is that the affine extended function
q\mapsto g(p)+f_{p}(q-p)
is a linear approximation of
g
at the point
p
.
So a subtangent rule, on prediction
p
and observation
y
, evaluates this linear approximation at
\delta_{y}
.

Observation 4.4
.

If
S
is a regular subtangent rule of
g
, then its expected score function on
\mathcal{P}
is
S(p;q)=g(p)+f_{p}(q-p)
, and in particular the expected score for truthful reporting is
S(p;p)=g(p)
.

The observation follows because, by regularity, the expected score summation is legal, and we can use the scaling and additivity axioms to move the expectation inside
f_{p}
.
This implies that
g(p)
can be viewed as a
measure of value of information
or a
generalized entropy
, where
g(p)
captures the expected score or amount of knowledge of someone with belief
p

(
Howard 1966
;
Grünwald and Dawid 2004
)
.
For example, with the log scoring rule
S(p,y)=\log p(y)
, the corresponding
g
is precisely the negation of Shannon entropy.

Subgradient rules.

There is a less-often used, but tighter version of the characterization
(
McCarthy 1956
;
Hendrickson and Buehler 1971
)
: all regular proper scoring rules can be written as
subgradient rules
of a
positively homogeneous
4
4

4

g
is positively homogeneous if
g(\alpha x)=\alpha g(x)
for all
\alpha\geq 0,x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
.
convex function
g

Definition 4.5

(Subgradient scoring rule)
.

If a scoring rule
S:\mathcal{P}\times\mathcal{Y}\to\overline{\mathbb{R}}
satisfies

\displaystyle S(p,y)

\displaystyle=f_{p}(\delta_{y})

\displaystyle\forall p\in\mathcal{P},y\in\mathcal{Y}

(5)

for some positively homogeneous convex function
g:\mathbb{R}^{\mathcal{Y}}\to\mathbb{R}\cup\{\infty\}
with
\mathcal{P}\subseteq\textrm{effdom}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(g}}\right)
and some selection of its extended subgradients,
f_{p}
at each

Observation 4.6
.

Every regular subgradient rule
S(p,y)=f_{p}(\delta_{y})
of a positively homogeneous

g
.

Proof.

We must show
f_{p}(\delta_{y})=g(p)+f_{p}(\delta_{y}-p)
for all
p\in\mathcal{P},y\in\mathcal{Y}
.
We will first show that
g(p)=f_{p}(p)
.
Using the definition of subgradient and homogeneity,
g(p)+f_{p}(p)=g(p)+f_{p}(2p-p)\leq g(2p)=2g(p)
, and since
g(p)\in\mathbb{R}
, we conclude
f_{p}(p)\leq g(p)
.
Similarly,
g(p)-f_{p}(p)=g(p)+f_{p}(\vec{0}-p)\leq g(\vec{0})=0
, so
g(p)\leq f_{p}(p)
.
Since
g(p)=f_{p}(p)\in\mathbb{R}
, we have
g(p)+f_{p}(\delta_{y}-p)=g(p)+f_{p}(\delta_{y})-f_{p}(p)=f_{p}(\delta_{y})
.
∎

A
characterization
theorem is therefore strongest when it concludes that any proper scoring rule can be written as a subgradient rule; this implies that it is also a subtangent rule.
On the other hand,
constructions
of proper scoring rules, Theorems
4.11
and
4.16
, are strongest when they are able to construct, not only subgradient rules from homogeneous convex functions, but also subtangent rules from general convex functions.
In other words, it is often more practically convenient to construct subtangent rules.

The distinction between subgradient and subtangent rules is not overly significant: given any convex
g
and subtangent rule
S
of
g
, we can define the homogeneous
g^{\prime}
to match
g
on the simplex, and scale
g
appropriately outside the simplex, i.e.
g^{\prime}(x)=\|x\|_{1}g(\tfrac{x}{\|x\|_{1}})
, and
g^{\prime}(\vec{0})=0
, where
\|x\|_{1}=\sum_{y}|x(y)|
.
Then
S
can be expressed as a subgradient rule of
g^{\prime}
.

However, the distinction has historically caused confusion.
For example, the log scoring rule
S(p,y)=\log p(y)
is typically presented as a
subtangent
rule of the negative entropy
g(p)=\sum_{y\in\mathcal{Y}}p(y)\log p(y)
(infinite if

), but it is not a

rule of that function, as pointed out by

.
But, as
Hendrickson and Buehler 1971
responds, the log score is a subgradient rule of the positively homogeneous function
g(p)=\sum_{y\in\mathcal{Y}}p(y)\log\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\tfrac{p(y)}{\|p\|_{1}}}}\right)

Prior characterizations and missing pieces

The scoring rule characterization has appeared in many forms, notably in

4.3
Tool: extended expected scores

Before proving the scoring rule characterization, we encounter a problem with the expected score function
S(p;q):=\sum_{y\in\mathcal{Y}}q(y)S(p,y)
of a regular scoring rule.
Usual characterization proofs proceed by observing that
S(p;q)
is an affine function of
q
, and that a pointwise supremum over these functions yields the convex
g(q)
from Definition
4.3
(subtangent rule).

However,
S(p;q)
is not technically an affine nor affine extended function, as it is only defined on
q\in\Delta_{\mathcal{Y}}
.
Attempting to naively extend it to
q\in\mathbb{R}^{\mathcal{Y}}
may lead to illegal sums.
7
7

7

For example, for the log score,
S(\delta_{y},y^{\prime})=0
if
y=y^{\prime}
, otherwise
-\infty
.
A simple interpretation (e.g.
Gneiting and Raftery 2007
) is
S(\delta_{y};q)=\sum_{y^{\prime}\neq y}q(y)(-\infty)
, which raises no contradiction on the simplex, but leads to illegal sums if
q
contains both positive and negative components.

Therefore, this section formalizes a key fact about regular scoring rules (Lemma
4.10
): for fixed
p
,
S(p;q)
always coincides on
\Delta_{\mathcal{Y}}
with at least one affine extended function; we call any such function an extended expected score of
S
.

Definition 4.8

(Extended expected score)
.

Let
\mathcal{Y}
be finite and
\mathcal{P}
a subset of
\Delta_{\mathcal{Y}}
.
Given a regular scoring rule
S:\mathcal{P}\times\mathcal{Y}\to\mathbb{R}\cup\{-\infty\}
, for each
p\in\mathcal{P}
, an affine extended function
h:\mathbb{R}^{\mathcal{Y}}\to\overline{\mathbb{R}}
is an
extended expected score function
of
S
at
p
if
h(\delta_{y})=S(p,y)
for all
y\in\mathcal{Y}
.

The name is justified:
h(q)
is the expected score for report
p
under belief
q
.
In fact this holds for any
q\in\Delta_{\mathcal{Y}}
, not just
q\in\mathcal{P}
.
Recall that
S(p;q):=\sum_{y\in\mathcal{Y}}q(y)S(p,y)
, Definition
4.2
.

Observation 4.9
.

If
h
is an extended expected score of a regular
S
at
p
, then for all
q\in\Delta_{\mathcal{Y}}
,
h(q)=S(p;q)
.

To prove it, write
h(q)=\beta+f(q-p)
and recall by Definition

S(p,y)=h(\delta_{y})
.
So
S(p;q)=\beta+\sum_{y}q(y)f(\delta_{y}-p)

By regularity of

\beta+f(q-p)=h(q)

Lemma 4.10

(Existence of expected scores)
.

Let
\mathcal{Y}
be finite,
\mathcal{P}\subseteq\Delta_{\mathcal{Y}}
, and

Then

Proof.

Given

.
We define

1

Set

Let

By definition,
\hat{f}
is a finite linear function.
If
t\geq 1
, let
v_{1},\dots,v_{t}=\{-\delta_{y}~:~y\in Y_{1}\}
, in any order.
By Proposition
2.7
,
S_{p}
is linear extended because it is implemented by Algorithm
1
.

To show it is an extended expected score function, we calculate
S_{p}(\delta_{y})
for any given
y\in\mathcal{Y}
.
If
y\in Y_{2}
, then
v_{j}\cdot\delta_{y}=0
for all

and
S_{p}(\delta_{y})=\hat{f}(\delta_{y})=S(p,y)
.
Otherwise,

for all

There,

4.4
Scoring rules on the simplex

This section considers
\mathcal{P}=\Delta_{\mathcal{Y}}
and uses the machinery of linear extended functions to prove a complete characterization of proper scoring rules, including their construction from any convex function and set of extended subgradients.
This arguably improves little on the state of knowledge as in
Gneiting and Raftery 2007
and folklore of the field, but formalizes some important previously-informal or unstated facts.

Theorem 4.11

(Construction of proper scoring rules)
.

Let
\mathcal{Y}
be a finite set.

1.

Let
g:\mathbb{R}^{\mathcal{Y}}\to\mathbb{R}\cup\{\infty\}
be convex with
\textrm{effdom}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(g}}\right)\supseteq\Delta_{\mathcal{Y}}
.
Then: (a) all of its subtangent rules are regular and proper, and there exists at least one; and (b) if
g
is furthermore strictly convex on
\Delta_{\mathcal{Y}}
, then all of its subtangent rules are strictly proper.

2.

Let
S:\Delta_{\mathcal{Y}}\times\mathcal{Y}\to\mathbb{R}\cup\{-\infty\}
be a regular, proper scoring rule.
Then: (a) it is a subtangent rule of some convex
g
with
\textrm{effdom}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(g}}\right)\supseteq\Delta_{\mathcal{Y}}
, uniquely defined on
\Delta_{\mathcal{Y}}
; and (b) if
S
is strictly proper, then any such
g
is strictly convex on
\Delta_{\mathcal{Y}}
; and (c)
S
is a subgradient rule of some (possibly different) unique positively homogeneous convex
g
.

Proof.

(1)
Given
g
, an arbitrary subtangent rule is defined by
S(p,y)=g(p)+f_{p}(\delta_{y}-p)
for some choice of extended subgradients
f_{p}:\mathbb{R}^{\mathcal{Y}}\to\overline{\mathbb{R}}
at each
p\in\Delta_{\mathcal{Y}}
.
Proposition
3.2
asserts that at least one choice of
f_{p}
exists for all
p\in\Delta_{\mathcal{Y}}
, so at least one such function
S
exists.
And any such
S
is a regular scoring rule (i.e. never assigns score
+\infty
) because
\textrm{effdom}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(g}}\right)\supseteq\Delta_{\mathcal{Y}}
and by definition of extended subgradient,
S(p,y)\leq g(\delta_{y})
.

To show
S
is proper: Immediately, the function
S_{p}(q)=g(p)+f_{p}(q-p)
is an extended expected score of
S
(Definition
4.8
).
It is an affine extended function supporting
g
at
p
.
So
S_{q}(q)\geq S_{p}(q)
for all
p\neq q
, so
S
is proper by definition, completing
(a)
.
Furthermore, if
g
is strictly convex on
\Delta_{\mathcal{Y}}
, then (for any choices of subgradients) each resulting
S_{p}
must support
g
at only one point in
\Delta_{\mathcal{Y}}
by Lemma
3.11
, so
S_{q}(q)>S_{p}(q)
for all
p\neq q
and
S
is strictly proper.
This proves
(b)
.

(2)

Given
S
, by Lemma
4.10
, for each
p
there exists a linear extended expected score
S_{p}
.
Define
g(q)=\sup_{p\in\Delta_{\mathcal{Y}}}S_{p}(q)
, a convex function by Proposition
3.7
.
By definition of properness,
S_{q}(q)=\max_{p}S_{p}(q)=g(q)
.
So
S_{q}
is an affine extended function supporting
g
at
q\in\textrm{effdom}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(g}}\right)
.
So (e.g. Observation
3.6
) it can be written
S_{q}(x)=g(q)+f_{q}(x-q)
for some extended subgradient
f_{q}
of
g
at
q
.
In particular,
S(p,y)=S(p;\delta_{y})=S_{p}(\delta_{y})=g(p)+f_{p}(\delta_{y}-p)
, so
S
is a subtangent rule of
g
, whose effective domain contains
\Delta_{\mathcal{Y}}
.
Furthermore, by the above, any such
g
satisfies
g(q)=S_{q}(q)=S(q;q)
, which is uniquely defined for any
q\in\Delta_{\mathcal{Y}}
.
This proves
(a)
.

Furthermore, suppose
S
is strictly proper and a subtangent rule of some
g
.
Then as above, it has affine extended expected score functions
S_{q}
at each
q
and, by strict properness,
S_{q}(p)<S_{p}(p)=g(p)
for all
p\in\Delta_{\mathcal{Y}}
,
p\neq q
.
So at each
p\in\Delta_{\mathcal{Y}}
there is a supporting affine extended function
S_{p}
that supports
g
nowhere else on
\Delta_{\mathcal{Y}}
.
By Lemma
3.11
, this is a necessary and sufficient condition for strict convexity of
g
on
\Delta_{\mathcal{Y}}
.
This proves
(b)
.

Now looking closer, recall Lemma
4.10
guarantees existence of a
linear
extended
S_{p}
.
So
S_{p}(\vec{0})=0=g(p)+f_{p}(-p)
, implying
f_{p}(p)=g(p)=S_{p}(p)
.
Then for all
x
,
S_{p}(x)=S_{p}(p)+f_{p}(x-p)
, which rearranges and uses the axioms of linearity (along with finiteness of
S_{p}(p)
) to get
S_{p}(x-p)=f_{p}(x-p)
, or
S_{p}=f_{p}
.
Since we have
S(p,y)=S_{p}(\delta_{y})=f_{p}(\delta_{y})
, we get that
S
is a subgradient rule of
g
.
Furthermore, since each
S_{p}
is linear extended,
\alpha g(q)=\alpha\sup_{p\in\Delta_{\mathcal{Y}}}S_{p}(q)=\sup_{p\in\Delta_{\mathcal{Y}}}\alpha S_{p}(q)=g(\alpha q)
, for
\alpha\geq 0
.
So
g
is positively homogeneous.
Furthermore,
g(q)
is uniquely defined on
\Delta_{\mathcal{Y}}
by
g(q)=S(q;q)
, which then uniquely defines it at any point in the positive orthant.
∎

One nontrivial example is the rule that assigns score
k
to any prediction with support size
|\mathcal{Y}|-k
, assuming the outcome
y
is in its support;
-\infty
if it is not.
This is associated with the convex function
g
taking the value
k
on the relative interior of each
|\mathcal{Y}|-k-1
dimensional face of the simplex; in particular it is zero in the interior of the simplex,
1
in the relative interior of any facet, …, and
|\mathcal{Y}|-1
at each corner
\delta_{y}
.

A generalization can be obtained from any set function
G:2^{\mathcal{Y}}\to\mathbb{R}
that is
monotone
, meaning
X\subseteq Y\implies G(X)\leq G(Y)
.
We can set
g(p):=G(\textrm{Supp}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(p}}\right))
.
In other words, the score for prediction
p
is
G(\textrm{Supp}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(p}}\right))
if the observation
y
is in
\textrm{Supp}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(p}}\right)
; the score is
-\infty
otherwise.
We utilized this construction in
Chen and Waggoner 2016
.

Of course neither of these is
strictly
proper.
A similar, strictly proper approach is to construct
g
from any strictly convex and bounded function
g_{0}
on the interior of the simplex; then on the interior of each facet, let
g
coincide with any bounded strictly convex function whose lower bound exceeds the upper bound of
g_{0}
; and so on.

Definition 4.12

(Interior-finite)
.

Say a linear extended function
f:\mathbb{R}^{\mathcal{Y}}\to\overline{\mathbb{R}}
is
p
-interior-finite
for
p\in\Delta_{\mathcal{Y}}
if its parameterization
(t,v_{1},\dots,v_{t},\hat{f})
in Algorithm
1
has: (1) for all pairs
y,y^{\prime}\in\textrm{Supp}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(p}}\right)
, that
v_{j}(y)=v_{j}(y^{\prime})
for all
j=1,\dots,t
; and (2) for all
y\not\in\textrm{Supp}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(p}}\right)
and
y^{\prime}\in\textrm{Supp}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(p}}\right)
, that
v_{j}(y)=v_{j}(y^{\prime})
for some sequence
j=1,\dots,k
, with either
k=t
or else
v_{k+1}(y)<v_{k+1}(y^{\prime})
.

If
f
is
p
-interior-finite, then property (1) gives that
f(q-p)
is finite for
q
in the face of the simplex generated by the support of
p
.
Property (2) gives that
f(q-p)
is either finite or
-\infty
at all
q\in\Delta_{\mathcal{Y}}
that are not in that face.
These are the two key properties that we will need for regular scoring rules.

Lemma 4.13

(Interior-finite implies quasi-integrable)
.

Let
p\in\Delta_{\mathcal{Y}}
.
A linear extended function
f:\mathbb{R}^{\mathcal{Y}}\to\overline{\mathbb{R}}
is
p
-interior-finite if and only if
f(\delta_{y}-p)\in\mathbb{R}\cup\{-\infty\}
for all
y\in\mathcal{Y}
.
Furthermore, in this case
f(\delta_{y}-p)\in\mathbb{R}
for any
y\in\textrm{Supp}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(p}}\right)
.

Proof.

Let
f
be a linear extended function and
v_{1},\dots,v_{t}
its unit vectors in the parameterization of Algorithm
1
.

(\implies)

Suppose
f
is
p
-interior-finite.
If it is finite everywhere, then the conclusion is immediate.
Otherwise, let
y\in\textrm{Supp}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(p}}\right)
.
Using that
v_{1}(y)=v_{1}(y^{\prime})
for any
y^{\prime}\in\textrm{Supp}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(p}}\right)
, we have

\displaystyle v_{1}\cdot(\delta_{y}-p)

\displaystyle=v_{1}(y)-v_{1}\cdot p

\displaystyle=v_{1}(y)-\sum_{y^{\prime}\in\textrm{Supp}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(p}}\right)}v_{1}(y^{\prime})p(y^{\prime})

\displaystyle=v_{1}(y)-v_{1}(y)\sum_{y^{\prime}\in\textrm{Supp}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(p}}\right)}p(y^{\prime})

\displaystyle=0.

This applies to each successive
v_{j}
, so
f(\delta_{y}-p)\in\mathbb{R}
.

Now let
y\not\in\textrm{Supp}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(p}}\right)
.
Having just shown
v_{1}\cdot(\delta_{y^{\prime}}-p)=0
for any
y^{\prime}\in\textrm{Supp}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(p}}\right)
, we have
v_{1}\cdot(\delta_{y}-p)=v_{1}\cdot(\delta_{y}-\delta_{y^{\prime}})=v_{1}(y)-v_{1}(y^{\prime})\leq 0
by assumption of
p
-interior-finite.
So either Algorithm
1
returns
-\infty
in the first iteration, or we continue to the second iteration.
The same argument applies at each iteration, so
f(\delta_{y}-p)\in\mathbb{R}\cup\{-\infty\}
.

(\impliedby)

Suppose
f(\delta_{y}-p)\in\mathbb{R}\cup\{-\infty\}
for all
y\in\mathcal{Y}
.
Again if
f
is finite everywhere, it is
p
-interior-finite, QED.
So suppose its rank is at least
1
.
We must have
v_{1}\cdot(\delta_{y}-p)\leq 0
for all
y\in\mathcal{Y}
.
This rearranges to
v_{1}(y)\leq v_{1}\cdot p
for all
y
, or
\max_{y\in\mathcal{Y}}v_{1}(y)\leq v_{1}\cdot p
.
But
p
is a probability distribution, so
v_{1}\cdot p\leq\max_{y\in\mathcal{Y}}v_{1}(y)
.
So
v_{1}\cdot p=\max_{y\in\mathcal{Y}}v_{1}(y)
.
This implies that
v_{1}(y)=v_{1}(y^{\prime})=v_{1}\cdot p
for all
y,y^{\prime}\in\textrm{Supp}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(p}}\right)
.
So
v_{1}\cdot(\delta_{y}-p)=0
if
y\in\textrm{Supp}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(p}}\right)
.
Meanwhile, if
y\not\in\textrm{Supp}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(p}}\right)
, then
v_{1}(y)\leq v_{1}\cdot p=v_{1}(y^{\prime})
for any
y^{\prime}\in\textrm{Supp}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(p}}\right)
.

This argument repeats to prove that if
y\in\textrm{Supp}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(p}}\right)
, then for all
j=1,\dots,t
,
v_{j}(y)=\max_{y^{\prime}\in\mathcal{Y}}v_{j}(y^{\prime})
and also
v_{j}\cdot(\delta_{y}-p)=0
.
So it also gives
f(\delta_{y}-p)\in\mathbb{R}
.
Meanwhile, if
y\not\in\textrm{Supp}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(p}}\right)
, then we have a sequence
v_{1}(y)=\max_{y^{\prime}\in\mathcal{Y}}v_{1}(y^{\prime})
, …,
v_{k}(y)=\max_{y^{\prime}\in\mathcal{Y}}v_{k}(y^{\prime})
, where in each case
v_{j}\cdot(\delta_{y}-p)=0
.
Then finally, either
k=t
and
f(\delta_{y}-p)\in\mathbb{R}
, or we have
v_{k+1}(y)<\max_{y^{\prime}\in\mathcal{Y}}v_{k+1}(y^{\prime})
and
f(\delta_{y}-p)=-\infty
.
∎

We can now make the key definitions for the scoring rule characterization.

Definition 4.14

(Interior-locally-Lipschitz; ILL subtangent rule)
.

For
\mathcal{P}\subseteq\Delta_{\mathcal{Y}}\cap\textrm{effdom}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(g}}\right)
, say
g:\mathbb{R}^{d}\to\mathbb{R}\cup\{\infty\}
is
\mathcal{P}
-interior-locally-Lipschitz
if
g
has at every
p\in\mathcal{P}
a
p
-interior-finite extended subgradient.
Say that a scoring rule
S:\mathcal{P}\times\mathcal{Y}\to\overline{\mathbb{R}}
is an
ILL subtangent rule of
g
if it is a subtangent rule of
g
(Definition
4.3
) where each extended subgradient
f_{p}
is
p
-interior finite.

An interior-locally-Lipschitz
g
simply has two conditions on its vertical supporting hyperplanes.
First, they cannot cut faces of the simplex whose relative interior intersects
\textrm{effdom}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(g}}\right)
; they can only contain them.
This is enforced by
v_{j}(y)=v_{j}(y^{\prime})
for
y,y^{\prime}\in\textrm{Supp}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(p}}\right)
.
In other words, the extended subgradients must be
finite
(in particular bounded, an analogy of causing
g
to be Lipschitz) relative to the affine hull of these faces.
Second, they must be oriented correctly so as not to cut that face from the rest of the simplex.
This is enforced by
v_{j}(y)\leq v_{j}(y^{\prime})
for
y\not\in\textrm{Supp}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(p}}\right)
,
y^{\prime}\in\textrm{Supp}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(p}}\right)
.

Lemma
4.13
gives the following corollary.

Corollary 4.15
.

A subtangent scoring rule
S:\mathcal{P}\times\mathcal{Y}\to\overline{\mathbb{R}}
is regular if and only if it is an ILL subtangent rule.
A convex
g:\mathbb{R}^{\mathcal{Y}}\to\mathbb{R}\cup\{\infty\}
with
\textrm{effdom}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(g}}\right)\supseteq\mathcal{P}
has a regular subtangent scoring rule if and only if
g
is
\mathcal{P}
-interior-locally-Lipschitz.

Proof.

Let
S
be a subtangent scoring rule of some
g
with
\textrm{effdom}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(g}}\right)\supseteq\mathcal{P}
, with extended subgradients
\{f_{p}:p\in\mathcal{P}\}

Recall that
S
is regular if
S(p,y)\in\mathbb{R}\cup\{-\infty\}
for all
p\in\mathcal{P}
and
y\in\mathcal{Y}
.
Because
g(p)\in\mathbb{R}
for all
p\in\mathcal{P}
, regularity is equivalent to
f_{p}(\delta_{y}-p)\in\mathbb{R}\cup\{-\infty\}
for all
p\in\mathcal{P}
and all
y\in\mathcal{Y}
.
This is equivalent to
p
-interior-local-Lipschitzness of each
f_{p}
by Lemma
4.13
, proving the first claim.
By definition,
g
is
\mathcal{P}
-interior-locally-Lipschitz if and only if it has an ILL subtangent rule, proving the second claim.
∎

Theorem 4.16

(Construction of scoring rules on
\mathcal{P}
)
.

Let
\mathcal{Y}
be a finite set and
\mathcal{P}
an arbitrary nonempty subset of
\Delta_{\mathcal{Y}}
.

1.

Let

\mathcal{P}
-interior-locally-Lipschitz with
\textrm{effdom}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(g}}\right)\supseteq\mathcal{P}

g
is regular and proper if and only if it is an ILL subtangent rule of
g
, and there exists at least one; and (b) if
g

Let
S:\mathcal{P}\times\mathcal{Y}\to\mathbb{R}\cup\{-\infty\}

Then: (a) it is an ILL subtangent rule of some convex
\mathcal{P}
-interior-locally-Lipschitz

with
\textrm{effdom}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(g}}\right)\supseteq\mathcal{P}
, uniquely defined on
\mathcal{P}
; and (b) if

is strictly proper and
\mathcal{P}

is strictly convex on
\mathcal{P}
.

Proof.

(1)
Let such a

.
We have

supports

Conversely, if

Now suppose

is strictly proper, proving

.

(2)
Let a regular proper scoring rule

It has by Lemma

at each

.
Define

Then

So

of

Next, since

for all

.
So by Lemma

There remain open directions investigating interior-locally-Lipschitz functions.
Two important cases can be immediately resolved:

•

If
\mathcal{P}\subseteq\textrm{int}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\Delta_{\mathcal{Y}}}}\right)
, then
g
is
\mathcal{P}
-interior-locally-Lipschitz if
g
is subdifferentiable on
\mathcal{P}
, i.e. has finite subgradients everywhere on
\mathcal{P}
.
This follows because every
p\in\mathcal{P}
has full support.
And in fact, it is necessary and sufficient that
g
be subdifferentiable when restricted to the affine hull of the simplex; its subgradients can still be infinite perpendicular to the simplex, e.g. of the form
f(x)=\infty\cdot(x\cdot\vec{1})+\hat{f}(x)
.

•

If
\mathcal{P}
is an open set relative to the affine hull of
\Delta_{\mathcal{Y}}
, then every convex function
g
with
\textrm{effdom}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(g}}\right)\supseteq\mathcal{P}
is
\mathcal{P}
-interior-locally-Lipschitz.
This follows from the previous case along with the fact that convex functions are subdifferentiable on open sets in their domain.

One question for future work is whether one can optimize efficiently over the space of interior-locally-Lipschitz
g
, for a given set
\mathcal{P}\subseteq\Delta_{\mathcal{Y}}
.
This could be useful in constructing proper scoring rules that optimize some objective.

Another direction is to extend these constructions to general affine type spaces as studied by
Frongillo and Kash 2021
, where the structure of the probability simplex is no longer present.
