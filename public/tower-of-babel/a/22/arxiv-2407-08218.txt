Differential Tree Automata
Rida Ait El Manssour, Vincent Cheval, Mahsa Shirmohammadi, James Worrell

Abstract
A rationally dynamically algebraic (RDA) power series is one that arises as (a component of) the solution of a system of differential equations of the form 𝒚 ′ = F ⁡ ( 𝒚 ) \bm{y}^{\prime}=F(\bm{y}) , where F F is a vector of rational functions that is defined at 𝒚 ⁡ ( 0 ) \bm{y}(0) . RDA power series subsume algebraic power series and are a proper subclass of differentially algebraic power series (those that satisfy a univariate polynomial-differential equation). We give a combinatorial characterisation of RDA power series in terms of exponential generating functions of regular languages of labelled trees. Motivated by this connection, we define the notion of a differential tree automaton. Differential tree automata generalise weighted tree automata by allowing the transition weights to be rational functions of the tree size. Our main result is that the ordinary generating functions of the formal tree series recognised by differential tree automata are exactly the differentially algebraic power series. The proof of this result establishes a general form of recurrence satisfied by the sequence of coefficients of a differentially algebraic power series, generalising Reutenauer’s matrix representation of polynomially recursive sequences. As a corollary we obtain a procedure for determining equality of differential tree automata.



I
Introduction

I-A

Combinatorial Classes, Automata, and Power Series

Many combinatorial classes admit natural representations as languages of words and trees, which can be specified using automata or grammars. This provides a method for systematically extracting generating functions for combinatorial classes from their specifications (as is also done in the theory of combinatorial species
[
1
]
) and leads to a natural correspondence between classes of automata and classes of power series. For example,
regular languages of words have rational generating functions
[
2
, Section I.4]
, while unambiguous context-free languages of words have algebraic generating functions
[
2
, Sections I.5]
.
Here, recall that for a field
\mathbb{K}
a formal power series
f\in\mathbb{K}[\![x]\!]
is
rational
if there are polynomials
P,Q\in\mathbb{K}[x]
such that
f=\frac{P}{Q}
and

f
is
algebraic
if there are polynomials

P_{0},\ldots,P_{d}\in\mathbb{K}[x]
such that
\sum_{i=0}^{d}P_{i}f^{i}=0
.

In this paper we develop the correspondence between automata, power series, and combinatorial classes to relate tree automata and
differentially algebraic power series. Our main contribution is to define the class of differential tree automata and to show that their generating functions are precisely the differentially algebraic power series.
Recall here that a formal power series
f\in\mathbb{K}[\![x]\!]
is
differentially algebraic
if it satisfies a differential equation

P(f,f^{\prime},\ldots,f^{(k)})=0

for some polynomial
P(y_{1},\ldots,y_{k})
with coefficients in
\mathbb{K}[x]
.
We also give a combinatorial characterisation of
a proper subclass of differentially algebraic power series—the so-called
rationally dynamically algebraic
(RDA) series
[
3
,
4
]
.
These arise as (components of) solutions of
systems of differential equations

\displaystyle y_{1}^{\prime}=F_{1}(y_{1},\ldots,y_{k}),\,\ldots,\,y^{\prime}_{k}=F_{k}(y_{1},\ldots,y_{k}),

(1)

where
F_{1},\ldots,F_{k}\in\mathbb{K}(x,y_{1},\ldots,y_{k})
are rational functions that are defined at
(0,y_{1}(0),\ldots,y_{k}(0))
.
In Section
V

we give a collection of rational
dynamical systems that characterise the exponential generation
functions of standard constructions of combinatorial
species.

I-B

Tree Automata and their Generating Functions

Our main results concern the notion of

differential tree automata
:
a generalisation of weighted tree automata
[
5
]
. Let
\Sigma
be a finite ranked alphabet and write

T_{\Sigma}
for the set of
\Sigma
-trees.
As with a
\mathbb{K}
-weighted automaton,
a differential tree automaton
\mathcal{A}
computes a function

[\![\mathcal{A}]\!]:T_{\Sigma}\rightarrow\mathbb{K}
.
Recall that a transition in a
\mathbb{K}
-weighted tree automaton has the form

\sigma(q_{1},\ldots,q_{\ell})\stackrel{{\scriptstyle a}}{{\longrightarrow}}q\,,

where
\sigma
is a
\ell
-ary alphabet symbol,
q_{1},\ldots,q_{\ell}
and
q
are states of the automaton,
and
a\in\mathbb{K}
is the weight of the transition.
Intuitively, the automaton
reads the symbol
\sigma
and makes a transition from the tuple of states
q_{1},\ldots,q_{\ell}
to
q
with weight
a
.
Generalising this pattern, in a differential tree automaton the weight of a transition is a rational function
R(x)
that has no positive integer poles.
When instantiated on a tree
t
, the transition has weight
R(\|t\|)
, where
\|t\|
denotes the size of
t
(only counting the number of internal nodes in
t
).

We consider two different generating functions associated with (weighted and differential) tree automata.
The
ordinary generating function
of an automaton
\mathcal{A}

is the power series
f_{\mathcal{A}}\in\mathbb{K}[\![x]\!]
defined by

\displaystyle f_{\mathcal{A}}(x)=\sum_{n\in\mathbb{N}}\,\Bigg(\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}t\in T_{\Sigma}\\
\|t\|=n\end{subarray}}[\![{\mathcal{A}}]\!](t)\Bigg)x^{n}.

(2)

A formulation of the Chomsky-Schutzenberger theorem is that
the class of algebraic power series in
\mathbb{K}[\![x]\!]
coincides with the class of ordinary generating functions of tree series recognized by

\mathbb{K}
-weighted tree automata.

We also introduce the notion of a
labelled generating function
.
A
monotonic labelling
of a tree
t\in T_{\Sigma}
of size
n
is a bijective mapping from the set of internal nodes of
t
to
the set
\{1,\ldots,n\}
such that the label of any node is greater than the label of any of its children.
Define

\lambda:T_{\Sigma}\rightarrow\mathbb{Q}
such that
\lambda(t)
is the proportion of labellings of
t\in T_{\Sigma}
that are monotonic. That is,
the number of monotonic labellings of
t
is

\lambda(t)n!
.
The labelled generating function of
\mathcal{A}
is the formal power series

\widetilde{f}_{\mathcal{A}}\in\mathbb{K}[\![x]\!]
defined by

\widetilde{f}_{\mathcal{A}}(x):=\sum_{n\in\mathbb{N}}\Bigg(\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}t\in T_{\Sigma}\\
\|t\|=n\end{subarray}}\lambda(t)[\![{\mathcal{A}}]\!](t)\Bigg)x^{n}.

(3)

I-C

Main Results

Our first result is as follows:

Theorem 1
.

The following are equivalent for a power series
g(x)=\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}a_{n}x^{n}
in
\mathbb{K}[\![x]\!]
:

1.

g
is rationally dynamically algebraic;

2.

g
is equal to the ordinary generating function of a differential tree automaton in which the rational weight of
every transition has the form
\frac{ax+b}{x}
for some
a,b\in\mathbb{K}
;

3.

g
is equal to the labelled generating function of a
\mathbb{K}
-weighted tree automaton.

From Theorem
1
it follows that every RDA power series
g\in\mathbb{Z}[\![x]\!]

can be written
g=\widetilde{f}_{\mathcal{A}}-\widetilde{f}_{\mathcal{B}}
, where
\mathcal{A}
and
\mathcal{B}
are
deterministic bottom-up tree automata.

Removing the restriction on the form of rational function in Item
2
of
Theorem

1
, we show that the ordinary generating functions of differential tree automata
are precisely the differentially algebraic series.

Theorem 2
.

The following are equivalent for a power series
g(x)=\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}a_{n}x^{n}
in
\mathbb{K}[\![x]\!]
:

1.

g
is differentially algebraic;

2.

g
is equal to the ordinary generating function
of a differential tree automaton;

3.

(a_{n})_{n=0}^{\infty}
is a component of a sequence

(\bm{b}_{n})_{n=0}^{\infty}\in\mathbb{K}^{d}
of vectors that satisfies a bilinear recurrence
with polynomial coefficients, of the form

\bm{b}_{n}=\bm{b}_{n-1}\,P(n)+\sum_{k=0}^{n-1}(\bm{b}_{k}\otimes\bm{b}_{n-k-1})Q(n)\,,

where
P\in\mathbb{K}(x)^{d\times d}
and
Q\in\mathbb{K}(x)^{d^{2}\times d}
have entries defined on positive integers.

The proof of
Theorem

2
builds on a construction of Denef and Lipschitz
[
6
]
, who showed that
the sequence of coefficients
(a_{n})_{n=0}^{\infty}
of a
differentially algebraic power series satisfies a recurrence of the form

P(n)\,a_{n}=Q_{n}(a_{0},\ldots,a_{n-1})\,,

where
P_{n}
is a polynomial whose form depends on
n
. Building on the construction of
[
6
]

we show that
(a_{n})_{n=0}^{\infty}
can be recovered as a component of vectors satisfying a recurrence of the form shown in
Item

3
of Theorem
2
.

As a consequence of
Item

2
of
Theorem

2
we obtain a decision procedure for equivalence of differential tree automata (
Theorem

5
).
We further show that the representation of differentially algebraic power series by recurrences in
Items

2
and
3

is convenient for
for computing the sum, Cauchy product,
inverse, derivative, integration, and forward and backward shift of such series (
Theorem

6
in Appendix
E
).

I-D

Related Work

The special case of RDA series in which the functions
F_{1},\ldots,F_{k}
in (
1
) are polynomials
is studied in Bergeron and Reutenauer
[
7
]
under the name
constructibly differentiably algebraic (CDA)
series.
We observe below that the classes of RDA and CDA series coincide.
A combinatorial interpretation of CDA power series via so-called
tree labelling tables
, which are an automata-like
notion on labelled non-plane trees, is given in
[
7
, Theorem 1]
.
This result is closely related to Item
3
of Theorem
1
.

A power series
f\in\mathbb{K}[\![x]\!]

is
differentially finite
(D-finite) if it satisfies a relation of the form

\sum_{i=0}^{d}P_{i}(x)f^{(i)}(x)=0

for polynomials
P_{0},\ldots,P_{d}\in\mathbb{K}[x]
. Thus differentially finite power series
are a subclass of differentially algebraic series.
It is well-known
[
8
]
that
f(x)=\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}a_{n}x^{n}
is differentially finite
if and only if its sequence

(a_{n})_{n=0}^{\infty}
of coefficients satisfies a linear recurrence with polynomial coefficients.
Reutenauer
[
9
]
shows that such sequences can equivalently be characterised by vector
recurrences corresponding to a special case of the recurrence in
Item

3
of Theorem
2
in which
R
is identically zero.
See
[
10
]
for a development of these ideas, including an exact learning algorithm for such recurrences and their automata extension.

Clemente
[
11
]
uses techniques from differential algebra to show that multiplicity equivalence of
basic parallel processes
can be decided in doubly exponential space. He also shows that commutative weighted basic parallel processes (viewed as power series) coincide with the class of CDA series, thereby obtaining a doubly exponential time procedure for deciding zeroness of CDA series.

Boreale
[
12
]
defines a notion of bisimulation for systems of ordinary differential equations, based on the Lie derivative. He gives an algorithm to compute the largest bisimulation on a given system, and shows how this can be used for minimisation and determining equivalence. As observed in
[
11
]
, this procedure decides zeroness of CDA power series; however no complexity analysis is given in
[
12
]
.

Resolving a conjecture of Castiglione and Massazza
[
13
]
,
Bostan
et al.

[
14
]
show that the multivariate power series arising as the generating functions of languages accepted by weakly-unambiguous Parikh automata are
D
-finite. This result is used to prove the inherent unambiguity of certain languages based on analytic properties of univariate
D
-finite series considered as functions.

We focus on automata in this paper. Other works consider the relationship between recurrences and generating functions on the one hand, and combinatorial classes
defined by logical formulas on the other hand
[
15
,
16
]
.

II
Overview

II-A

Tree Automata

Let
\Sigma
be a ranked alphabet, that is,
\Sigma
is a finite set of function symbols, each having a non-negative integer arity.
For
k\in\mathbb{N}
, we denote by
\Sigma_{k}
the set of symbols of arity
k
in
\Sigma
.
A (non-deterministic)
tree automaton

{\mathcal{A}}
is a tuple
(Q,\Delta,F)
, where

Q
is a finite set of states,
\Delta
is a set of transition rules of the form

\sigma(q_{1},q_{2},\ldots,q_{k})\longrightarrow q,

where
\sigma\in\Sigma
is a
k
-ary symbol and
q_{1},q_{2},\ldots,q_{k},q\in Q
are
states, and
F
is a set of accepting states
[
17
]
.

The automaton processes a tree bottom-up, starting at the leaves.
Leaf nodes are assigned states according to the rules in
\Delta
for nullary symbols. States are then propagated upwards using the transition rules, combining the states of child nodes to determine the state of their parent. The value
[\![{\mathcal{A}}]\!](t)
of a tree
t\in T_{\Sigma}
is the number of runs on
t
in which the root is labelled with state in
F
.
The
n
-th coefficient of the
ordinary generating function
power series of an automaton
{\mathcal{A}}

is the sum of
[\![{\mathcal{A}}]\!](t)
over all trees of size
n
(see (
2
)).
Similarly, the
n
-th coefficient of the
labelled generating function
power series is the number of monotonic labelled trees accepted by the automaton divided by
n!
(see (
3
)).

II-B

Weighted Tree Automata

A
\mathbb{K}
-weighted tree automaton

{\mathcal{A}}
(also called a multilinear representation
[
18
]
) is a bottom-up tree automaton
(Q,\Delta,q_{f})
, with a single final state
q_{f}\in Q
, where the transition rules in
\Delta
are equipped with a weight in
\mathbb{K}
. Transitions are denoted

\sigma(q_{1},q_{2},\ldots,q_{k})\xrightarrow{a}q,

where
\sigma\in\Sigma_{k}
,
a\in\mathbb{K}
and
q_{1},\ldots,q_{k},q\in Q
.
A run of such an automaton is a run of the underlying non-deterministic tree automaton. The weight of the run is the product of the weights
of the transitions comprising the run. The value
[\![{\mathcal{A}}]\!](t)
of word
t\in T_{\Sigma}
is the sum of the weights of all accepting runs on
t
.

When reasoning with weighted tree automata it is convenient to work with a matricial presentation in which a
\mathbb{K}
-weighted tree automaton over alphabet
\Sigma
is a pair
(d,\mu)
, where
d\in\mathbb{N}
is the dimension and
\mu
is a function with domain
\Sigma
such that
\mu(\sigma)\in\mathbb{K}^{d^{k}\times d}
is a matrix for
\sigma\in\Sigma_{k}
. The dimension corresponds to the number of states in the automaton, whereas
\mu
is the matrix representation of the set of transition rules
\Delta
with the set of states
Q=\{1,\ldots,d\}
:

\sigma(q_{1},\ldots,q_{k})\xrightarrow{a}q\in\Delta\quad\text{iff}\quad\mu(\sigma)_{(q_{1},\ldots,q_{k}),q}=a

The value
[\![{\mathcal{A}}]\!](t)
of
t\in T_{\Sigma}
is defined using the Kronecker product.
Recall that the Kronecker product of two row vectors
\bm{u}=\begin{bmatrix}u_{1}&\cdots&u_{m}\end{bmatrix}
and
\bm{v}=\begin{bmatrix}v_{1}&\cdots&v_{n}\end{bmatrix}
, denoted
\bm{u}\otimes\bm{v}
,
is defined by

\begin{bmatrix}u_{1}v_{1}&u_{1}v_{2}&\cdots&u_{m}v_{n-1}&u_{m}v_{n}\end{bmatrix}\,.

The operation is associative and the nullary Kronecker product is the
1\times 1
identity matrix.

The map
\mu
induces a function
\bm{\mu}:T_{\Sigma}\rightarrow\mathbb{K}^{1\times d}
that is defined inductively by

\displaystyle\bm{\mu}(\sigma(t_{1},\ldots,t_{k})):=(\bm{\mu}(t_{1})\otimes\cdots\otimes\bm{\mu}(t_{k}))\cdot\mu(\sigma)

for a
k
-ary symbol
\sigma
. In other words,
\bm{\mu}(t)
is given by an iterated matrix product determined by parsing the tree
t
from the leaves to the root. From the properties of the Kronecker product,
\bm{\mu}(t)
is a vector
\begin{bmatrix}w_{1}&\ldots&w_{d}\end{bmatrix}
where
w_{i}
is the sum of the weights of all runs over
t
that label the root with state
i
. Without loss of generality, we will consider that the final state
q_{f}
corresponds to index 1, meaning that
[\![{\mathcal{A}}]\!](t)=\mu(t)_{1}
. By convention, in this paper, a vector will be represented by a bold symbol, say
\bm{v}
, and its elements will be denoted
v_{1},v_{2},\ldots
.

The generating function of a weighted tree automaton, given by
Equation

2
, is an algebraic power series
[
18
, Proposition 7.2]
.
Note that two automata that represent different tree series can have the same generating function, since the latter aggregates all trees of the same size.

II-C

Differential Tree Automata

The notion of differential tree automata generalises that of weighted tree automata.
A
\mathbb{K}
-differential tree automaton
over alphabet
\Sigma
is
a pair
\mathcal{A}=(d,\mu)
, where
d\in\mathbb{N}
is the dimension and

\mu
is a map with domain
\Sigma
such that

•

\mu(\sigma)\in\mathbb{K}^{1\times d}
for
\sigma\in\Sigma_{0}
and,

•

\mu(\sigma)\in\mathbb{K}(x)^{d^{\ell}\times d}
is a matrix of rational functions, having no positive integer poles,
for
\ell\geq 1
and
\sigma\in\Sigma_{\ell}
.

The map
\mu
induces a function
\bm{\mu}:T_{\Sigma}\rightarrow\mathbb{K}^{1\times d}
, inductively defined by specifying that for all

\sigma\in\Sigma_{0}
,

\bm{\mu}(\sigma):=\mu(\sigma)
; and for all
k\geq 1
and
\sigma\in\Sigma_{k}
and
t_{0}=\sigma(t_{1},\ldots,t_{k})
,

\displaystyle\bm{\mu}(t_{0}):=(\bm{\mu}(t_{1})\otimes\cdots\otimes\bm{\mu}(t_{k}))\cdot\mu(\sigma)(\|t_{0}\|)\,.

We define the formal tree series represented by
\mathcal{A}
to be the function
[\![\mathcal{A}]\!]:T_{\Sigma}\rightarrow\mathbb{K}
defined by
[\![{\mathcal{A}}]\!](t):=\bm{\mu}(t)_{1}
.

We also introduce the notion of a generalised differential tree automaton, we will see later that this does not change the expressiveness.
To set up this definition, we first define

\mathbb{K}_{u}(x_{0},\ldots,x_{k})
to be the subring of
\mathbb{K}(x_{0},\ldots,x_{k})

consisting of rational functions of the form

\frac{P(x_{0},\ldots,x_{k})}{Q_{0}(x_{0})\cdots Q_{k}(x_{k})}

where
P\in\mathbb{K}[x_{0},\ldots,x_{k}]
is a multivariate polynomial and
Q_{i}\in\mathbb{K}[x_{i}]
for
i\in\{0,\ldots,k\}
are univariate polynomials such that
Q_{0}
has no positive integer root and
Q_{1},\ldots,Q_{k}

have no nonnegative integer root.

A generalised
\mathbb{K}
-differential tree automaton is defined as above except that for
\sigma\in\Sigma_{\ell}
with
\ell\geq 1
we now take

\mu(\sigma)
to be an element of
\mathbb{K}_{u}(x_{0},\ldots,x_{k})^{d^{k}\times d}
.
Such an automaton likewise induces a function
\bm{\mu}:T_{\Sigma}\rightarrow\mathbb{K}^{1\times d}
, which is defined as above except that now we have

\displaystyle\bm{\mu}(t_{0}):=(\bm{\mu}(t_{1})\otimes\cdots\otimes\bm{\mu}(t_{k}))\cdot\mu(\sigma)(\|t_{0}\|,\ldots,\|t_{k}\|)\,.

Proposition 1
.

For every generalised differential tree automaton
\mathcal{A}
, there exists a
differential tree automaton
\mathcal{B}
such that
[\![\mathcal{A}]\!]=[\![\mathcal{B}]\!]
.

The proof is provided in Appendix
A
. Henceforth we drop the term
generalised
when referring to differential tree automata.

The definition of the
generating function

f_{{\mathcal{A}}}
and
labelled generating function

\tilde{f}_{{\mathcal{A}}}
of a differential tree automaton
{\mathcal{A}}
is exactly as for a weighted tree automaton, namely via
Equations

2
and
3
.
In general
f_{{\mathcal{A}}}
need not be algebraic, unlike for weighted tree automata, but we will show that it is differentially algebraic.

Example 2

.

Let

(t_{n})_{n=0}^{\infty}=(0,1,2,9,\allowbreak 64,625,\ldots)

be the sequence whose general term counts the number of labelled rooted

f_{T}(x)=\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\frac{t_{n}}{n!}x^{n}

for its exponential generating function.
From the recursive definition of labelled rooted trees as a combinatorial species we have that
f_{T}=x\exp(f_{T})
.
Differentiating, we see that

is a solution of the differential equation

x(1-y)y^{\prime}=y\,.

(4)

From there, the sequence

From this recurrence relation we obtain a differential tree automaton

.

In its algebraic representation, the automaton
{\mathcal{A}}
has dimension 2 with the transition function
\mu
defined as
\mu(\sigma_{0})=\begin{bmatrix}0&1\end{bmatrix}
and

\mu(\sigma_{1})=\begin{bmatrix}0&0\\
1&0\\
\end{bmatrix}\quad\text{and}\quad\mu(\sigma_{2})=\begin{bmatrix}\bm{0}_{3\times 1}&\bm{0}_{3\times 1}\\
0&\frac{x_{2}+1}{x_{0}}\\

With a simple induction, we notice that for all

\bm{\mu}(t)=\begin{bmatrix}\frac{t_{n}}{n!}&\frac{t_{n+1}}{(n+1)!}\end{bmatrix}\,.

The equality

can also be established using combinatorial reasoning.
The set

\{t\in T_{\Sigma}:[\![{\mathcal{A}}]\!](t)\neq 0\}

comprises those trees

Every such tree

•

•

II-D

Multi-holonomic sequences

Motivated by the relation between differentially algebraic power series and recurrence relations in the previous examples, we introduce the notion of a
multi-holonomic recurrence
for a sequence of vectors
(\bm{a}_{n})_{n=0}^{\infty}
in
\mathbb{K}^{d}
.

Fix
d\in\mathbb{N}
. A sequence
(\bm{a}_{n})_{n=0}^{\infty}
of elements of
\mathbb{K}^{d}
is
multi-holonomic

if there exist
m\in\mathbb{N}
, and for all
\ell\in\{1,\ldots,m\}
a matrix of polynomials

\mu_{\ell}\in\mathbb{K}_{u}(x)^{d^{\ell}\times d}\,,

such that for all
n\geq 1
we have

\bm{a}_{n}=\,\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\ell\in\{1,\ldots,m\}\\
\bm{j}\in J_{n}(\ell)\end{subarray}}(\bm{a}_{j_{1}}\otimes\ldots\otimes\bm{a}_{j_{\ell}})\cdot\mu_{\ell}(n)\,,

(8)

where
J_{n}(\ell)
is the set of tuples
(j_{1},\ldots,j_{\ell})\in\mathbb{N}^{\ell}
such that
j_{1}+\ldots+j_{\ell}=n-1
.
We call
d\in\mathbb{N}
the
order

of the recurrence and
m
its arity. The
degree
of the recurrence is the maximum degree of the numerator and denominator over all entries of the matrices
\mu_{\ell}
.

Syntactically speaking, a multi-holonomic recurrence can be regarded a differential tree automaton over an alphabet with exactly one symbol of each arity.
The difference is that we view the recurrence as defining a sequence of vectors rather than a formal tree series.
As with automata, we introduce a generalisation of multi-holonomic recurrences that permits additional predicates on the summation indices.
Formally, a sequence
(\bm{a}_{n})_{n=0}^{\infty}
of elements of
\mathbb{K}^{d}
is
generalised multi-holonomic
if there exist a set
I\subseteq\bigcup_{\ell\geq 0}\mathbb{Z}\times\mathbb{N}^{\ell}
and for all tuples
\bm{s}=(s_{0},\ldots,s_{\ell})\in I
a matrix of polynomials

\mu_{\bm{s}}\in\mathbb{K}_{u}(x_{0},\ldots,x_{\ell})^{d^{\ell}\times d}

such that for all
n\geq 1
,

\bm{a}_{n}=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\bm{s}\in I\\
\bm{j}\in G_{n}(\bm{s})\end{subarray}}(\bm{a}_{j_{1}}\otimes\ldots\otimes\bm{a}_{j_{\ell}})\cdot\mu_{\bm{s}}(n,\bm{j})\,,

where
G_{n}(s_{0},\ldots,s_{\ell})
is the set of tuples
(j_{1},\ldots,j_{\ell})\in\mathbb{N}^{\ell}
such that
j_{1}+\ldots+j_{\ell}=n-1+s_{0}
and for all
k\in\{1,\ldots,\ell\}
,
j_{k}\leq n-1-s_{k}
.

A multi-holonomic sequence of arity
m
defined by matrices
\mu_{1},\ldots,\mu_{m}
can be seen as a generalised multi-holonomic sequence by taking the set
I=\{\bm{0}_{2},\ldots,\bm{0}_{(m+1)}\}
, with
\bm{0}_{i}
being the tuple composed of
i
integers
0
, and for all
\ell\in\{1,\ldots,m\}
,

\mu_{\bm{0}_{(\ell+1)}}(x_{0},\ldots,x_{\ell})=\mu_{\ell}(x_{0})\,.

The third main result of our paper compares these notions of multi-holonomic sequences. For this purpose,
given
d_{1}\geq d_{2}

we say that a sequence
(\bm{b}_{n})_{n=0}^{\infty}
of elements of
\mathbb{K}^{d_{1}}

recognises
a sequence
(\bm{a}_{n})_{n=0}^{\infty}
of elements of
\mathbb{K}^{d_{2}}

if
\pi(\bm{b}_{n})=\bm{a}_{n}
for all
n
, where

\pi:\mathbb{K}^{d_{1}}\rightarrow\mathbb{K}^{d_{2}}
is the projection onto the first
d_{2}
coordinates.
The following result is a useful step in the proof of
Theorem

2
.
Detailed proofs can be found in Appendix
B
.

Theorem 3
.

Let
(\bm{a}_{n})_{n=0}^{\infty}
be a sequence of elements of
\mathbb{K}^{d}
. The following statements are equivalent:

1.

a multi-holonomic sequence of arity 2 recognises
(\bm{a}_{n})_{n=0}^{\infty}
;

2.

a multi-holonomic sequence recognises
(\bm{a}_{n})_{n=0}^{\infty}
;

3.

a generalised multi-holonomic sequence recognises
(\bm{a}_{n})_{n=0}^{\infty}
.

The first step of the proof of

A power series is CDA when

The CDA power series are thus RDA power series by definition. The converse is given by the following proposition.

Proposition 2
.

The class of RDA and CDA power series coincide.

Proof sketch.

Our aim is to transform the system shown in (

we introduce new variables

Differentiating these equations leads to

z^{\prime}_{i}=-z_{i}^{2}\sum_{j=1}^{k}\frac{\partial Q_{i}}{\partial y_{j}}y^{\prime}_{j}=-z_{i}^{2}\sum_{j=1}^{k}\frac{\partial Q_{i}}{\partial y_{j}}P_{j}(y_{1},\ldots,y_{k})z_{j}\,.

But
\frac{\partial Q_{i}}{\partial y_{j}}
is also a polynomial
\mathbb{K}[x,y_{1},\ldots,y_{k}]
, which concludes the proof.
∎

Proposition 7

Let

IV-B

From Differentially Algebraic Power Series to Multi-Holonomic Sequences

To complete the proof of
Theorem

2
, it remains to show that any differentially algebraic power series is the component of a multi-holonomic sequence of arity 2, that is, the implication
1

\Rightarrow

3
in
Theorem

2
. Our proof builds on a construction of Denef and Lipschitz
[
6
]
, who showed that
the sequence of coefficients of any differentially algebraic power series
f(x)=\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}a_{n}x^{n}
satisfies a recurrence of the form:

P(n)\,a_{n}=Q_{n}(a_{0},\ldots,a_{n-1})\,,

where
Q_{n}
is a polynomial whose form depends on
n
. Our proof makes explicit this recurrence and shows that how it can be transformed into a generalised multi-holonomic sequence. The proof will then conclude by applying
Theorem

3
.

Observe that
n
-th coefficient of a power series
f(x)=\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}a_{n}x^{n}
satisfies

a_{n}=n!\,f^{(n)}(0)
.
Hence, to obtain a recurrence relation between the coefficients, we only need to obtain a recurrence relation between the derivatives of
f
evaluated at
0
.
One way to do so is to differentiate the differential polynomial
P
on which the differentially algebraic power series
f(x)
, that is, the polynomial

P(y,y^{(1)},\ldots,y^{(d)})
with coefficients in
\mathbb{K}[x]
such that

P(f(x),f^{\prime}(x),\ldots,f^{(d)}(x))=0
.
In particular, notice that

P^{\prime}=\frac{\partial P}{\partial y^{(d)}}y^{(d+1)}+R^{\prime},,

(13)

with both
R
and
\frac{\partial P}{\partial y^{(d)}}
being polynomials in
\mathbb{K}[x][y,\ldots,y^{(d)}]
, thus of order strictly smaller than
d+1
. The partial derivative of
P
by its highest-order variable, namely
\frac{\partial P}{\partial y^{(d)}}
, is called the
separant of
P
. Since
f(x)
and its derivatives form a root of
P
then they are also a root of
P^{\prime}
. Hence, instantiating
Equation

13
with
f
and its derivates evaluated at
0
leads to the following equation.

\frac{\partial P}{\partial y^{(d)}}(f(0),\ldots,f^{(d)}(0))f^{(d+1)}(0)=-R(f(0),\ldots,f^{(d)}(0))

We can see here how the recurrence takes shape as
f^{(d+1)}(0)
is expressed using an equation that depends solely on
f^{(i)}(0)
for
i\leq d
. However, the main hurdle comes from the fact that the separant can vanish at
0
.

As a first step in addressing this issue,
in the next proposition, we show that
one can always assume that
the separant of
P
is not identically zero at the solution. In other words,

\frac{\partial P(y,y^{(1)},\ldots,y^{(d)})}{y^{(d)}}(f(x),f^{\prime}(x),\ldots,f^{(d)}(x))\neq 0\,.

This properties holds if
P
has minimal order and degree among differential polynomials that annihilate
f
.

Proposition 9
.

Let
f(x)\in\mathbb{K}[\![x]\!]
be a differentially algebraic power series. There exists a differential polynomial
P\in\mathbb{K}[x][y,\ldots,y^{(d)}]
such that

\displaystyle P(f(x),\dots,f^{(d)}(x))=0,\text{ and }

\displaystyle\frac{\partial P}{\partial y^{(d)}}(f(x),\ldots,f^{(d)}(x))\neq 0\,.

Proof.

By definition of differentially algebraic power series, there exists a non-zero polynomial
P(y^{(0)},\ldots,y^{(d)})
with coefficients in
\mathbb{K}[x]
such that
P(f(x),\ldots,f^{(d)}(x))=0
. We consider such polynomial
P
with the smallest
(d,\mathrm{deg}(y^{(d)}))
(using the lexicographic ordering) where
d
is the highest order of
y
in
P
and
\deg(y^{(d)})
is the degree of
y^{(d)}
in
P
.
As the polynomial
\frac{\partial P}{\partial y^{(d)}}
is not identically zero and has either the same order and strictly smaller degree than
y^{(d)}
, or a strictly small order, by minimality of
P
in the defined lexicographic ordering, we deduce that
\frac{\partial P}{\partial y^{(d)}}(f(x),\ldots,f^{(d)}(x))\neq 0
.
∎

We may thus assume that the separant of
P
not identically zero at the solution
f
.
The core idea of the Denef and Lipschitz
[
6
]
construction consists of iteratively differentiating the polynomial
P

until the resulting differential polynomial meets certain condition (requiring that the coefficient of highest order term
never vanishes at
0
).
The number of successive differentiations depends on the separant.

Given
k\in\mathbb{N}
and differential polynomials
A\in\mathbb{K}[x][y^{(\infty)}]
, denote by
A\mod y^{(k)}
the polynomials obtained from
A
by removing all monomials of order at least
k
(all monomials that are multiple of
y^{(o)}
for some
o\geq k
).

Proposition 10
.

Let
f\in\mathbb{K}[\![x]\!]
be a power series and
P\in\mathbb{K}[x][y,\ldots,y^{(d)}]
a differential polynomial such that

P(x,f,\dots,f^{(d)})=0\quad\text{ and }\quad\frac{\partial P}{\partial y^{(d)}}(x,f,\ldots,f^{(d)})\neq 0\,.

Then there exist
m,s,N\in\mathbb{N}

and polynomial
A\in\mathbb{K}[x]
such that for all
n\geq N
we have
A(n)\neq 0
and

\displaystyle f^{(n+s+1)}(0)=\frac{1}{A(n)}\,Q_{n}(0,f(0),\ldots,f^{(n+s)}(0))

(14)

where
Q_{n}=P^{(m+n)}\bmod y^{(n+s+1)}
.

Proof sketch.

Since the separant is not identically zero, after evaluating it at
f
and its derivates, we get

\frac{\partial P}{\partial y^{(d)}}\left(f,\ldots,f^{(d)}\right)=x^{k}\cdot\sum_{i=0}^{\infty}c_{i}x^{i}

for some
k
with
c_{0}\neq 0
.
In the sequel we refer to
k
as the
vanishing order
of the separant.

By differentiating
P
to order
2k+2
, we can show that

P^{(2k+2)}=y^{(d+2k+2)}f_{d}+\cdots+y^{(d+k+2)}f_{d+k}+f_{d+k+1}

where each
f_{j}
has order at most
j
and
f_{d}=\frac{\partial P}{\partial y^{(d)}}
. Continuing to differentiate, we obtain that for all
n\in\mathbb{N}
,
P^{(2k+2+n)}
becomes

y^{(d+2k+2+n)}S_{0}(n)+\cdots+y^{(d+k+2+n)}S_{k}(n)+H

(15)

where each
S_{j}(n)
has order at most
d+j
and
H
has order at most
d+k+1+n
. Moreover, each

S_{j}(n)(0,f(0),\ldots,f^{(d+j)}(0))

can be seen as a univariate polynomial in
n
.
In particular, as we assumed that the separant has vanishing order
k
, there is at least one of these polynomials that are not identically zero. We take the smallest index
r\in\{0,\ldots,k\}
such that

-S_{r}(n)(0,f(0),\ldots,f^{(d+r)}(0))

is not identically zero and we define
A(n)
to be this polynomial.

By taking
m=2k+2
and
s=d+2k+1-r
, the restriction of
Equation

15
to monomials of order strictly lower than
s+n+1
corresponds to the polynomials

y^{(d+k+2+n-r-1)}S_{r+1}(n)+\ldots+y^{(d+k+2+n)}S_{k}(n)+H\,.

Therefore, defining, for all
n\geq N
,

\displaystyle Q_{n}:=P^{(m+n)}\mod y^{(s+n+1)}\,,

(16)

we obtain the desired result.
∎

To complete the proof of the implication
1

\Rightarrow

3
in
Theorem

2
, we show that the polynomial
Q_{n}
defined in (
16
) naturally induces a generalised multi-holonomic sequence. Indeed, using Pascal’s identity, we have for every monomial
M=y^{(i_{1})}\ldots y^{(i_{\ell})}
in
P
and for all
n\in\mathbb{N}
,

M^{(n)}=\sum_{\bm{j}\in J_{n+1}(\ell)}\binom{n}{\bm{j}}\,y^{(i_{1}+j_{1})}\cdots y^{(i_{\ell}+j_{\ell})}\,.

Computing
M^{(m+n)}
modulo
y^{(s+n+1)}
intuitively corresponds to adding guard conditions on the indices
j_{1},\ldots,j_{\ell}
. As such,
M^{(m+n)}\mod y^{(s+n+1)}
becomes

\sum_{\bm{j}\in G_{n+1}(\bm{m})}\binom{n+m}{\bm{j}}\,y^{(i_{1}+j_{1})}\cdots y^{(i_{\ell}+j_{\ell})}

with
\bm{m}=(m,i_{1}-1-s,\ldots,i_{\ell}-1-s)
. The final generalised multi-holonomic sequence is obtained by summing over all monomials and applying an additional shift by
N
since
Equation

14
holds only when
n\geq N
. The proof concludes by invoking
Theorem

3
.

and we conclude by applying
Proposition

with
(\overline{\bm{a}}_{n})_{n=0}^{\infty}
and
\gamma
.

For the next proposition, recall that
G_{n}(s_{0},\ldots,s_{\ell})
is the set of tuples
(j_{1},\ldots,j_{\ell})\in\mathbb{N}^{\ell}

Proposition 15
.

Let
(\bm{a}_{n})_{n=0}^{\infty}
be a generalised multi-holonomic sequence of elements of
\mathbb{K}^{d}
. There exists an unguarded multi-holonomic sequence
(\bm{b}_{n})_{n=0}^{\infty}
of order
D\geq d
such that for all
i\in\{1,\ldots,d\}
, for all
n\in\mathbb{N}
,
\bm{a}_{n,i}=\bm{b}_{n,i}
.

Proof.

By definition of a generalised multi-holonomic sequence, we know that
(\bm{a}_{n})_{n=0}^{\infty}
satisfies for all
n\geq 1
,

\bm{a}_{n}=\sum_{\bm{m}\in I}\;\sum_{\bm{j}\in G_{n}(\bm{m})}(\bm{a}_{j_{1}}\otimes\cdots\otimes\bm{a}_{j_{\ell}})\cdot\mu_{\bm{m}}(n,\bm{j})

with
I\subseteq\mathbb{Z}\times\mathbb{N}^{*}
. The main idea will be to create a new set
I^{\prime}
that produce the same sum but which fit the conditions of
Proposition

14
, that are only negative shift.

Let us take
M
such that

M>\max_{\bm{m}\in I}\left\{\sum_{m\in\bm{m}}|m|\right\}\,.

(24)

We will define
(\bm{b}_{n})_{n=0}^{\infty}
the sequence such that for all
n\in\mathbb{N}
,

\bm{b}_{n}=\begin{bmatrix}\bm{a}_{n}&\ldots&\bm{a}_{n+M}\end{bmatrix}

We define for all
i\in\{0,\ldots,M\}
the linear maps
\pi_{i}:\mathbb{K}^{d(M+1)}\rightarrow\mathbb{K}^{d}
for all
\bm{u}_{0},\ldots,\bm{u}_{M}\in\mathbb{K}^{d}
,

\begin{bmatrix}\bm{u}_{1}&\ldots&\bm{u}_{M}\end{bmatrix}\pi_{i}=\bm{u}_{i}

Thus, for all
n\geq 1
,

\bm{b}_{n}\pi_{M}=\sum_{\bm{m}\in I}\;\sum_{\bm{j}\in G_{n+M}(\bm{m})}(\bm{a}_{j_{1}}\otimes\cdots\otimes\bm{a}_{j_{\ell}})\cdot\mu_{\bm{m}}(n+M,\bm{j})

Let us denote by
A(n,\bm{j})
the expression
(\bm{a}_{j_{1}}\otimes\cdots\otimes\bm{a}_{j_{\ell}})\cdot\mu_{\bm{m}}(n+M,\bm{j})
.
Take
\bm{m}=(m_{0},\ldots,m_{\ell})\in I
.
In the internal sum, given
(j_{1},\ldots,j_{\ell})\in G_{n+M}(\bm{m})
, we know that
j_{1}+\ldots+j_{\ell}=n-1+M-m_{0}
and
j_{1}\leq n-1+M-m_{1}
, …,
j_{\ell}\leq n-1+M-m_{\ell}
. We want to partition the tuples of
G_{n+M}(\bm{m})
into
2^{\ell}
subsets depending on whether each coordinate
j_{s}
is either smaller or greater than
M-m_{s}
. Thus, given a subset
S\subseteq\{1,\ldots,\ell\}
, the restriction of
G_{n+M}(\bm{m})
to
S
, denoted
G_{n+M}(\bm{m})_{|S}
will corresponds to all tuples
(j_{1},\ldots,j_{\ell})\in G_{n+M}(\bm{m})
such that
j_{s}\leq M-m_{s}
if and only if
s\in S
. Therefore, we obtain:

\displaystyle\bm{b}_{n}\pi_{M}

\displaystyle=\sum_{\bm{m}\in I}\;\sum_{\bm{j}\in G_{n+M}(\bm{m})}(\bm{a}_{j_{1}}\otimes\cdots\otimes\bm{a}_{j_{\ell}})\cdot\mu_{\bm{m}}(n+M,\bm{j})

\displaystyle=\sum_{\bm{m}=(m_{0},\ldots,m_{\ell})\in I}\;\sum_{S\subseteq\{1,\ldots,\ell\}}\;\sum_{\bm{j}\in G_{n+M}(\bm{m})_{|S}}(\bm{a}_{j_{1}}\otimes\cdots\otimes\bm{a}_{j_{\ell}})\cdot\mu_{\bm{m}}(n+M,\bm{j})

\displaystyle=\sum_{\bm{m}=(m_{0},\ldots,m_{\ell})\in I}\;\sum_{S\subseteq\{1,\ldots,\ell\}}\;\sum_{\bm{j}\in G_{n+M}(\bm{m})_{|S}}A(n,\bm{j})

Let us look at one instance of
\bm{m}=(m_{0},\ldots,m_{\ell})\in I
and
S\subseteq\{1,\ldots,\ell\}
. Let us define
k_{1},\ldots,k_{\ell}
such that
k_{s}=0
when
s\in S
and
k_{s}=M-m_{s}
otherwise. By definition, the internal sum can be split as follows:

\displaystyle\sum_{\bm{j}\in G_{n+M}(\bm{m})_{|S}}A(n,\bm{j})

\displaystyle=\

\displaystyle\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\forall s\in S,j_{s}\leq M-m_{s}\\
L=\sum_{s\in S}j_{s}\end{subarray}}\;\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\sum_{s\not\in S}j_{s}=n-1+M+m_{0}-L\\
\forall s\not\in S,M-m_{s}<j_{s}\leq n+M-1-m_{s}\end{subarray}}\;A(n,\bm{j})

\displaystyle=\

\displaystyle\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\forall s\in S,j_{s}\leq M-m_{s}\\
L=\sum_{s\in S}j_{s}\end{subarray}}\;\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\sum_{s\not\in S}j_{s}=n-1+M+m_{0}-L-(\ell-|S|)M+\sum_{s\not\in S}m_{s}\\
\forall s\not\in S^{\prime},0<j_{s}\leq n-1\end{subarray}}A(n,j_{1}+k_{1},\ldots,j_{\ell}+k_{\ell})

Intuitively, we will build a new set
I^{\prime}\subseteq\mathbb{N}^{+}
with an element added for each
S=\{i_{1},\ldots,i_{k}\}\subseteq\{1,\ldots,\ell\}
and each
(j_{i_{1}},\ldots,j_{i_{k}})
with
j_{i_{1}}\leq M-m_{i_{1}},\ldots,j_{i_{k}}\leq M-m_{i_{k}}
. In particular, we will add (under some conditions described below) the element
\overline{\bm{m}}=(M+m_{0}-L-(\ell-|S|)M+\sum_{s\not\in S}m_{s})
associated to the transition matrix
\overline{\mu}_{\overline{\bm{m}}}
such that:

\displaystyle(\bm{u}_{o_{1}}\otimes\ldots\otimes\bm{u}_{o_{\epsilon}})\overline{\mu}_{\overline{\bm{m}}}(x_{0},x_{o_{1}},\ldots,x_{o_{\epsilon}})\pi_{M}

\displaystyle=(\bm{v}_{1}\otimes\ldots\otimes\bm{v}_{\ell})\mu_{\bm{m}}(x_{0}+M,c_{1},\ldots,c_{\ell})

\displaystyle(\bm{u}_{o_{1}}\otimes\ldots\otimes\bm{u}_{o_{\epsilon}})\overline{\mu}_{\overline{\bm{m}}}(x_{0},x_{o_{1}},\ldots,x_{o_{\epsilon}})\pi_{i}

\displaystyle=\bm{0}^{d}\qquad\forall i\in\{0,\ldots,M-1\}

where
\{1,\ldots,\ell\}\setminus S=\{o_{1},\ldots,o_{\varepsilon}\}
and for all
i\in\{1,\ldots,\ell\}
, if
i\in S
then
\bm{v}_{i}=\bm{a}_{j_{i}}
and
c_{i}=j_{i}
else
\bm{v}_{i}=\bm{u}_{i}\pi_{M-m_{i}}
and
c_{i}=x_{i}+M-m_{i}
.

Since
M
satisfies
Equation

24
, we deduce that
M-m_{i}>0
for all
i\in\{1,\ldots,\ell\}
and so this transition matrix is fully and correctly defined. In particular, the projections
\pi_{M-m_{i}}
exist.

It remains to show that
N=M+m_{0}-\sum_{s\in S}j_{s}-(\ell-|S|)M+\sum_{s\not\in S}m_{s}
is negative. We do a case analysis on
|S|
:

•

When
|S|=\ell
:
N=M+m_{0}-\sum_{s=1}^{m}j_{s}
. However, we know that
n\in\mathbb{N}
and by
Equation

24
, we also have
N>0
. Thus,
n\neq 1-N
which entails that the inner sum is always
0
.

•

When
|S|=\ell-1
: W.l.o.g., assume
1\not\in S
. Hence
N=m_{0}-\sum_{s=2}^{\ell}j_{s}+m_{1}
. Hence, as the innermost sum contains the condition
j_{1}\leq n-1
, the sum becomes in fact
0
if
N>0
. Hence, we will only add the tuple
\overline{\bm{m}}
to
I^{\prime}
if
N\leq 0
.

•

When
|S|<\ell-1
: Recall that by
Equation

24
,

\max_{\bm{m}\in I}\left\{\sum_{m\in\bm{m}}|m|\right\}<M\,.

which entails
N\leq 0
.

Finally, we add to
I^{\prime}
an element
\bm{m}=(0,0)
with the transition matrix
\overline{\mu}_{\overline{\bm{m}}}
such that:

\displaystyle\bm{u}\overline{\mu}_{\overline{\bm{m}}}(x_{0},x_{1})\pi_{M}

\displaystyle=\bm{0}^{d}

\displaystyle\bm{u}\overline{\mu}_{\overline{\bm{m}}}(x_{0},x_{1})\pi_{i}

\displaystyle=\bm{u}\pi_{i+1}\qquad\forall i\in\{0,\ldots,M-1\}

This last element allow to
shift
the values of
\bm{a}_{n+k}
in the vector
\bm{b}_{n}
. In other words, it will ensure that
\bm{b}_{n+1}\pi_{i-1}=\bm{b}_{n}\pi_{i}
.

By construction, we have shown that
(\bm{b}_{n})_{n=0}^{\infty}
is a generalised multi-holonomic sequence with only negative shifts and such that for all
i\in\{1,\ldots,d\}
, for all
n\in\mathbb{N}
,
\bm{a}_{n,i}=\bm{b}_{n,i}
. We conclude by applying
Proposition

14
.
∎

Proposition 16
.

Let
(\bm{a}_{n})_{n=0}^{\infty}
be a multi-holonomic sequence of elements in
\mathbb{K}^{d}
and arity
m
. There exists a multi-holonomic
(\bm{b}_{n})_{n=0}^{\infty}
of arity at most
2
such that for all
i\in\{1,\ldots,d\}
, for all
n\in\mathbb{N}
,
a_{n,i}=b_{n,i}
.

Proof.

By definition, there exist for all
\ell\in\{1,\ldots,m\}
a matrix of polynomials
\mu_{\ell}\in\mathbb{K}_{u}(x)^{d^{\ell}\times d}
such that for all
n\geq 1
,

\bm{a}_{n}=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\ell\in\{1,\ldots,m\}\\
\bm{j}\in J_{n}(\ell)\end{subarray}}(\bm{a}_{j_{1}}\otimes\ldots\otimes\bm{a}_{j_{\ell}})\cdot\mu_{\ell}(n)

(25)

Let us assume that
m>2
as otherwise the result would directly hold with
(\bm{a}_{n})_{n=0}^{\infty}=(\bm{b}_{n})_{n=0}^{\infty}

We define new sequence
(\overline{\bm{a}}_{n})_{n=0}^{\infty}
such that for all
n\in\mathbb{N}
,
\overline{\bm{a}}_{n}=\bm{a}_{n+1}
. Moreover, we define
\sum_{\ell=2}^{m-1}d^{\ell}
new sequences
c_{n,(j_{1},\ldots,j_{\ell})}
for all
j_{1},\ldots,j_{\ell}\in\{1,\ldots,d\}
and
\ell\in\{2,\ldots,m-1\}
. Let us now define the value of these new sequences. For all
(j_{1},j_{2})\in\{1,\ldots,d\}
,

c_{n,(j_{1},j_{2})}:=\sum_{k=0}^{n}a_{k,j_{1}}a_{n-k,j_{2}}=\overline{a}_{n-1,j_{1}}a_{0,j_{2}}+\sum_{k=0}^{n-1}a_{k,j_{1}}\overline{a}_{n-k-1,j_{2}}

(26)

For all
\ell\in\{3,\ldots,m\}
, for all
j_{1},\ldots,j_{\ell}\in\{1,\ldots,d\}
, for all
n\in\mathbb{N}
,

c_{n,(j_{1},\ldots,j_{\ell})}=\sum_{k=0}^{n}c_{k,(j_{1},\ldots,j_{\ell-1})}a_{n-k,j_{\ell}}=c_{n,(j_{1},\ldots,j_{\ell-1})}a_{0,j_{\ell}}+\sum_{k=0}^{n-1}c_{k,(j_{1},\ldots,j_{\ell-1})}\overline{a}_{n-k-1,j_{\ell}}

(27)

We start by showing by induction on
\ell
that for all
j_{1},\ldots,j_{\ell}\in\{1,\ldots,d\}
,

c_{n,(j_{1},\ldots,j_{\ell})}=\sum_{(k_{1},\ldots,k_{\ell})\in J_{n+1}(\ell)}a_{k_{1},j_{1}}\ldots a_{k_{\ell},j_{\ell}}

(28)

The base case
\ell=2
is given by definition of
c_{n,(j_{1},j_{2})}
. In the inductive step, we have:

\displaystyle\sum_{(k_{1},\ldots,k_{\ell})\in J_{n+1}(\ell)}a_{k_{1},j_{1}}\ldots a_{k_{\ell},j_{\ell}}

\displaystyle=\

\displaystyle\sum_{k_{1}+\ldots+k_{\ell}=n}a_{k_{1},j_{1}}\ldots a_{k_{\ell},j_{\ell}}

\displaystyle=\

\displaystyle\sum_{k=0}^{n}\ (\sum_{k_{1}+\ldots+k_{\ell-1}=k}a_{k_{1},j_{1}}\ldots a_{k_{\ell-1},j_{\ell-1}})a_{n-k,j_{\ell}}

\displaystyle=\

\displaystyle\sum_{k=0}^{n}\ c_{k,(j_{1},\ldots,j_{\ell-1})}a_{n-k,j_{\ell}}\qquad\text{by inductive hypothesis}

\displaystyle=\

\displaystyle c_{n,(j_{1},\ldots,j_{\ell})}

We define the sequence
(\bm{b}_{n})_{n=0}^{\infty}
of dimension
D=d(1+\frac{d^{\ell}-1}{d-1})
such that for all
n\in N
,

\bm{b}_{n}=\begin{bmatrix}\bm{a}_{n}&\overline{\bm{a}}_{n}&c_{n,(1,1)}&c_{n,(1,2)}&\ldots&c_{n,(d,\ldots,d)}\end{bmatrix}

To simplify access to this vector, let us define
\alpha:\{1,\ldots,d\}\rightarrow\{1,\ldots,D\}
,
\beta:\{1,\ldots,d\}\rightarrow\{1,\ldots,D\}
and
\gamma_{\ell}:\{1,\ldots,d\}^{\ell}\rightarrow\{1,\ldots,D\}
for all
\ell\in\{2,\ldots,m-1\}
such that

b_{n,\alpha(j)}=a_{n,j}\qquad b_{n,\beta(j)}=\overline{a}_{n,j}\qquad b_{n,\gamma_{\ell}(j_{1},\ldots,j_{\ell})}=c_{n,(j_{1},\ldots,j_{\ell})}

Let us now show that
\bm{b}_{n}
is a multi-holonomic sequence of arity
2
. For all
n\geq 1
, for all
j\in\{1,\ldots,D\}
, we say that
b_{n,j}
is partially multi-holonomic of arity 2 when there exists some
A_{i,j}\in\mathbb{K}_{u}(x)
and
B_{(i_{1},i_{2}),j}\in\mathbb{K}_{u}(x)
for
i,i_{1},i_{2}\in\{1,\ldots,D\}
such that

b_{n,j}=\sum_{i=1}^{D}b_{n-1,i}A_{i,j}(n)+\sum_{i_{1},i_{2}\in\{1,\ldots,D\}}\sum_{k=0}^{n-1}b_{k,i_{1}}b_{n-k-1,i_{2}}B_{(i_{1},i_{2}),j}(n)

(29)

When
b_{n,j}
is partially multi-holonomic of arity 2 for all
j\in\{1,\ldots,D\}
then we directly obtain that
\bm{b}_{n}
is a multi-holonomic sequence of arity
2
. Indeed by creating two matrices
\mu\in\mathbb{K}_{u}(x)^{D\times D}
and
\mu^{\prime}\in\mathbb{K}_{u}(x)^{D^{2}\times D}
such that

\mu_{i,j}(x)=A_{i,j}(x)\quad(i,j\in\{1,\ldots,D\})\qquad\text{and}\qquad\mu^{\prime}_{(i_{1},i_{2}),j}(x)=B_{(i_{1},i_{2}),j}(x)\quad(i_{1},i_{2},j\in\{1,\ldots,D\})

we directly obtain that

\bm{b}_{n}=\bm{b}_{n-1}\cdot\mu(n)+\sum_{k=0}^{n-1}(\bm{b}_{k}\otimes\bm{b}_{n-k-1})\cdot\mu^{\prime}(n)

which would allow us to conclude.

Therefore, let us prove that for all
j\in\{1,\ldots,D\}
,
b_{n,j}
is partially multi-holonomic of arity 2. We do a case analysis on the indices given by the function
\alpha,\beta
and
\gamma_{\ell}
for
\ell\in\{2,\ldots,m-1\}
.

•

Indices
\alpha(j)
,
j\in\{1,\ldots,d\}
: By definition of
\overline{\bm{a}_{n}}
, we know that for all
n\geq 1
, for all
j\in\{1,\ldots,d\}
,

b_{n,\alpha(j)}=a_{n,j}=\overline{a}_{n-1,j}=b_{n-1,\beta(j)}\,.

Hence the result holds.

•

We prove the result for the indices given by
\gamma_{\ell}
by induction
\ell
. In the base case
\ell=2
, we have for all
j_{1},j_{2}\in\{1,\ldots,d\}
,

\displaystyle b_{n,\gamma(j_{1},j_{2})}

\displaystyle=c_{n,(j_{1},j_{2})}

\displaystyle=\overline{a}_{n-1,j_{1}}a_{0,j_{2}}+\sum_{k=0}^{n-1}a_{k,j_{1}}\overline{a}_{n-k-1,j_{2}}\qquad\text{by \lx@cref{creftypecap\lx@tilde refnum}{eq:prop-arity2-1}}

\displaystyle=b_{n-1,\beta(j_{1})}a_{0,j_{2}}+\sum_{k=0}^{n-1}b_{k,\alpha(j_{1})}b_{n-k-1,\beta(j_{2})}\,.

Hence the result holds for the base case. In the inductive step
\ell>2
, we have

\displaystyle b_{n,\gamma(j_{1},\ldots,j_{\ell})}

\displaystyle=c_{n,(j_{1},\ldots,j_{\ell})}

\displaystyle=c_{n,(j_{1},\ldots,j_{\ell-1})}a_{0,j_{\ell}}+\sum_{k=0}^{n-1}c_{k,(j_{1},\ldots,j_{\ell-1})}\overline{a}_{n-k-1,j_{\ell}}\qquad\text{by \lx@cref{creftypecap\lx@tilde refnum}{eq:prop-arity2-2}}

\displaystyle=b_{n,\gamma_{\ell-1}(j_{1},\ldots,j_{\ell-1})}a_{0,j_{\ell}}+\sum_{k=0}^{n-1}b_{k,\gamma_{\ell-1}(j_{1},\ldots,j_{\ell-1})}b_{n-k-1,\beta(j_{\ell})}

By inductive hypothesis on
\gamma_{\ell-1}
, we know that
b_{n,\gamma_{\ell-1}(j_{1},\ldots,j_{\ell-1})}
is partially multi-holonomic of arity 2 and we can replace
b_{n,\gamma_{\ell-1}(j_{1},\ldots,j_{\ell-1})}
by the sum given in
Equation

29
. This allows us to conclude that
b_{n,\gamma(j_{1},\ldots,j_{\ell})}
is partially multi-holonomic of arity 2.

•

Indices
\beta(j)
,
j\in\{1,\ldots,d\}
: From
Equation

25
, we know that for all
n\geq 1
, for all
j\in\{1,\ldots,d\}

\displaystyle b_{n,\beta(j)}

\displaystyle=a_{n+1,j}

\displaystyle=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\ell\in\{1,\ldots,m\}\\
\bm{j}\in J_{n+1}(\ell)\end{subarray}}((\bm{a}_{j_{1}}\otimes\ldots\otimes\bm{a}_{j_{\ell}})\cdot\mu_{\ell}(n+1))_{j}

\displaystyle=(\overline{\bm{a}}_{n-1}\mu_{1}(n+1))_{j}+\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\ell\in\{2,\ldots,m\}\\
\bm{j}\in J_{n+1}(\ell)\end{subarray}}((\bm{a}_{j_{1}}\otimes\ldots\otimes\bm{a}_{j_{\ell}})\cdot\mu_{\ell}(n+1))_{j}

\displaystyle=\sum_{i=1}^{d}b_{n-1,\beta(i)}\mu_{1}(n+1)_{i,j}+\sum_{\ell\in\{2,\ldots,m\}}\sum_{i_{1},\ldots,i_{\ell}\in\{1,\ldots,d\}}\sum_{\bm{j}\in J_{n+1}(\ell)}a_{j_{1},i_{1}}\ldots a_{j_{\ell},i_{\ell}}\mu_{\ell}(n+1)_{(i_{1},\ldots,i_{\ell}),j}

\displaystyle=\sum_{i=1}^{d}b_{n-1,\beta(i)}\mu_{1}(n+1)_{i,j}+\sum_{\ell\in\{2,\ldots,m\}}\sum_{i_{1},\ldots,i_{\ell}\in\{1,\ldots,d\}}c_{n,(i_{1},\ldots,i_{\ell})}\mu_{\ell}(n+1)_{(i_{1},\ldots,i_{\ell}),j}\qquad\text{by \lx@cref{creftypecap\lx@tilde refnum}{eq:prop-arity2-4}}

\displaystyle=\sum_{i=1}^{d}b_{n-1,\beta(i)}\mu_{1}(n+1)_{i,j}+\sum_{\ell\in\{2,\ldots,m\}}\sum_{i_{1},\ldots,i_{\ell}\in\{1,\ldots,d\}}b_{n,\gamma_{\ell}(i_{1},\ldots,i_{\ell})}\mu_{\ell}(n+1)_{(i_{1},\ldots,i_{\ell}),j}\,.

Since we already proved that all
b_{n,\gamma_{\ell}(i_{1},\ldots,i_{\ell})}
are partially multi-holonomic of arity 2, we can replace each
b_{n,\gamma_{\ell}(i_{1},\ldots,i_{\ell})}
by their sum given in
Equation

29
. This allows us to conclude that
b_{n,\beta(j)}
is partially multi-holonomic of arity 2.

As we have prove that for all
n\geq 1
, for all
j\in\{1,\ldots,D\}
,
b_{n,j}
is partially multi-holonomic of arity 2, we conclude that
(\bm{b}_{n})_{n=0}^{\infty}
is a multi-holonomic sequence of arity. Moreover, by construction, for all
n\in\mathbb{N}
, for all
i\in\{1,\ldots,d\}
,
a_{n,i}=b_{n,i}
. Hence we conclude.
∎

Proposition 19
.

Let

M:=y^{(i_{1})}\cdots y^{(i_{\ell})}\in\mathbb{K}[y^{(\infty)}]
be a monomial of order
d
in
y
. Then for all

we have

M^{(n)}=\sum_{\bm{j}\in J_{n+1}(\ell)}\binom{n}{\bm{j}}\,y^{(i_{1}+j_{1})}\cdots y^{(i_{\ell}+j_{\ell})}\,,

Proof.

We prove the result by induction on
n
.
The base case is immediate. The induction step is as follows.
We have

\begin{array}[]{rcl}M^{(n+1)}&=&\displaystyle\Bigg(\,\sum_{\bm{j}\in J_{n+1}(\ell)}\binom{n}{\bm{j}}\,y^{(i_{1}+j_{1})}\cdots y^{(i_{\ell}+j_{\ell})}\Bigg)^{\prime}\quad\text{(Induction hypothesis)}\\
&=&\displaystyle\sum_{k=1}^{\ell}\,\sum_{\bm{j}\in J_{n+1}(\ell)}\binom{n}{\bm{j}}\,y^{(i_{1}+j_{1})}\cdots y^{(i_{k}+j_{k}+1)}\cdots y^{(i_{\ell}+j_{\ell})}\quad\text{(Leibnitz rule)}\\

j_{1},\ldots,j_{\ell}\in\mathbb{N},j_{k}>0\end{subarray}}\binom{n}{j_{1},\dots,j_{k}-1,\ldots,j_{\ell}}\,y^{(i_{1}+j_{1})}\cdots y^{(i_{\ell}+j_{\ell})}\end{array}

\binom{n}{j_{1},\ldots,j_{\ell}}=0
when
j_{i}<0
for some
i
, we obtain:

\begin{array}[]{rcl}M^{(n+1)}&=&\displaystyle\sum_{k=1}^{\ell}\,\sum_{\bm{j}\in J_{n+2}(\ell)}\binom{n}{j_{1},\dots,j_{k}-1,\ldots,j_{\ell}}\,y^{(i_{1}+j_{1})}\cdots y^{(i_{\ell}+j_{\ell})}\\

This completes the proof.

{\mathcal{A}}=(d,\mu)

\Sigma

{\mathcal{A}}

\Gamma

t

T_{\Sigma}

\Gamma(t)=\alpha_{k,i}(t_{1},\ldots,t_{i-1},\Gamma(t_{i}))

A simple inductive proof allows us to show that for all
t\in T_{\Sigma}

\|t\|=\|\Gamma(t)\|+1
. For this, notice that when
\|t\|=1
,
t
must be of the form

We will build the automata

\bm{\mu}^{\prime}(t)=\bm{0}_{2d\times 1}

\mu^{\prime}
as follows:

•

\mu^{\prime}(\sigma_{0})=\begin{bmatrix}\bm{0}_{d\times 1}&\mu(\sigma_{0})\end{bmatrix}

•

for all
k\in\{1,\ldots,r\}
, for all
\bm{i}\in\{1,\ldots,2d\}^{k},j\in\{1,\ldots,2d\}

\mu^{\prime}(\sigma_{k})_{\bm{i},j}=\left\{\begin{array}[]{lr}\mu(\sigma)_{\bm{i}-d\bm{1},j-d}&\text{if }\forall\ell.i_{\ell},j\in\{d+1,\ldots,2d\}\\
0&\text{otherwise}\end{array}\right.

•

for all

,
\mu^{\prime}(\alpha_{k,0})=\begin{bmatrix}\bm{\mu}(\sigma_{k}(\sigma_{0},\ldots,\sigma_{0}))&\bm{0}_{d\times 1}\end{bmatrix}

•

for all
k\in\{1,\ldots,r\}

\mu^{\prime}(\alpha_{k,\ell})_{\bm{i},j}=\left\{\begin{array}[]{l}M^{k,\ell}_{(i_{1}-d,\ldots,i_{\ell-1}-d,i_{\ell}),j}\quad\text{if }i_{1},\ldots,i_{\ell-1}\in\{d+1,\ldots,2d\},i_{\ell},j\in\{1,\ldots,d\}\\
0\hfill\text{otherwise}\end{array}\right.

. In the base case,

or

\alpha_{k,0}

\Gamma(\sigma_{k}(\sigma_{0},\ldots,\sigma_{0}))=\alpha_{k,0}

\mu^{\prime}(\alpha_{k,0})=\begin{bmatrix}\bm{\mu}(\sigma_{k}(\sigma_{0},\ldots,\sigma_{0}))&\bm{0}_{d\times 1}\end{bmatrix}

In the inductive step, we have that either

•

Case

when

•

Case

\displaystyle\mu^{\prime}(t)_{j}

\displaystyle=(\bm{\mu}(t_{1})\otimes\ldots\otimes\bm{\mu}(t_{i-1})\otimes\bm{\mu}(t^{\prime}_{i}))\cdot M^{k,i}_{j}(\|t\|)

\displaystyle=(\bm{\mu}(t_{1})\otimes\ldots\otimes\bm{\mu}(t_{i-1})\otimes\bm{\mu}(t^{\prime}_{i}))\cdot(I_{d^{i}}\otimes{\text{\Large$\otimes$}}_{\ell=i+1}^{k}\mu(\sigma_{0}))\cdot\mu(\sigma_{k})(\|t^{\prime}\|)_{j}

\displaystyle\mu^{\prime}(t)_{j}

\displaystyle=(\bm{\mu}(t_{1})\otimes\ldots\otimes\bm{\mu}(t_{i-1})\otimes\bm{\mu}(t^{\prime}_{i})\otimes\text{\Large$\otimes$}_{\ell=i+1}^{k}\mu(\sigma_{0}))\cdot\mu(\sigma_{k})(\|t^{\prime}\|)_{j}

Corollary 2
.

•

There exists a differential tree automaton
{\mathcal{A}}^{\prime}

f_{{\mathcal{A}}^{\prime}}(x)=f_{{\mathcal{A}}}^{\prime}(x)
.

•

There exists a differential tree automaton

Proof.

From
Proposition

27
, we have a

for some

30
, we have

28

Proposition 31
.

Let
{\mathcal{A}}
be a differential tree automaton. Let
f_{\mathcal{A}}(x)=\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}a_{n}x^{n}
. If
a_{0}\neq 0
then there exists a differential tree automaton
{\mathcal{A}}^{\prime}
such that
f_{{\mathcal{A}}^{\prime}}(x)=\frac{1}{f_{{\mathcal{A}}}(x)}
.

Proof.

It is well known that when
a_{0}\neq 0
,
f_{A}(x)
admits an multiplicative inverse power series
f^{-1}_{\mathcal{A}}(x)=\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}b_{n}x^{n}
where the coefficients of
f^{-1}_{\mathcal{A}}(x)
satisfy the following property:

\left\{\begin{array}[]{l}b_{0}=\frac{1}{a_{0}}\\
b_{n}=-\frac{1}{a_{0}}\sum_{i=0}^{n-1}a_{i+1}b_{n-1-i}\quad\forall n>0\end{array}\right.

Using
Proposition

30
, we first build the automaton
{\mathcal{A}}^{\prime\prime}
such that
f_{{\mathcal{A}}^{\prime\prime}}(x)=\sum^{\infty}_{n=0}a_{n+1}x^{n}
. By
Proposition

25
, we can assume that
{\mathcal{A}}^{\prime\prime}=(d,\mu)
over some alphabet
\Sigma
that is arity distinct. Let us denote
\bm{f}(x)=\sum_{n=0}\bm{c}_{n}x^{n}
the vector of power series corresponding to
{\mathcal{A}}^{\prime\prime}
, that is
\bm{c}_{n}=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}t\in T_{\Sigma}\\
\|t\|=n\end{subarray}}\mu(t)
. Notice that
\bm{c}_{n,1}=a_{n+1}
for
n\in\mathbb{N}
.

We build the automaton
{\mathcal{A}}^{\prime}=(d^{\prime},\mu^{\prime})
over
\Sigma^{\prime}=\Sigma\cup\{\beta\}
with
d^{\prime}=d+1
, and
\beta
a fresh binary function symbol and the weight function
\mu^{\prime}
such that for all
n\geq 0
,
\bm{a}^{\prime}_{n}=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}t\in T_{\Sigma^{\prime}}\\
\|t\|=n\end{subarray}}\mu^{\prime}(t)=\begin{bmatrix}b_{n}&\bm{c}_{n}\end{bmatrix}
. For that purpose, let us define
\mu^{\prime}
such that

•

\mu^{\prime}(a)=\begin{bmatrix}\frac{1}{a_{0}}&\mu(a)\end{bmatrix}
where
a
is the unique nullary function symbol in
\Sigma
(recall that
\Sigma
is arity distinct)

•

for all
k>0
, for all
\sigma\in\Sigma_{k}
, for all
\bm{i}\in\{1,\ldots,d+1\}^{k},j\in\{1,\ldots,d+1\}
,

\mu^{\prime}(\sigma)_{\bm{i},j}=\left\{\begin{array}[]{lr}\mu(\sigma)_{\bm{i-1},j-1}&\text{if }\forall\ell.i_{\ell},j\in\{2,\ldots,d+1\}\\
0&\text{otherwise }\end{array}\right.

•

\mu^{\prime}(\beta)=\begin{bmatrix}0&\bm{0}_{1\times d}\\
-\frac{1}{a_{0}}&\bm{0}_{1\times d}\\
\bm{0}_{({d^{\prime}}^{2}-2)\times 1}&\bm{0}_{({d^{\prime}}^{2}-2)\times d}\end{bmatrix}

In the inductive step
n>0
, from our inductive hypothesis, we have

\displaystyle\bm{a}^{\prime}_{n}

\displaystyle=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}k>0\\
\sigma\in\Sigma^{\prime}_{k}\end{subarray}}\;\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}n_{1},\ldots,n_{k}\in\mathbb{N}\\
n_{1}+\ldots n_{k}=n-1\end{subarray}}(\bm{a}^{\prime}_{n_{1}}\otimes\ldots\otimes\bm{a}^{\prime}_{n_{k}})\mu^{\prime}(\sigma)(n)

\displaystyle=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}k>0\\
\sigma\in\Sigma^{\prime}_{k}\end{subarray}}\;\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}n_{1},\ldots,n_{k}\in\mathbb{N}\\
n_{1}+\ldots n_{k}=n-1\end{subarray}}(\begin{bmatrix}b_{n_{1}}&\bm{c}_{n_{1}}\end{bmatrix}\otimes\ldots\otimes\begin{bmatrix}b_{n_{k}}&\bm{c}_{n_{k}}\end{bmatrix})\mu^{\prime}(\sigma)(n)

\displaystyle=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}k>0\\
\sigma\in\Sigma_{k}\end{subarray}}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}n_{1},\ldots,n_{k}\in\mathbb{N}\\
n_{1}+\ldots n_{k}=n-1\end{subarray}}(\begin{bmatrix}b_{n_{1}}&\bm{c}_{n_{1}}\end{bmatrix}\otimes\ldots\otimes\begin{bmatrix}b_{n_{k}}&\bm{c}_{n_{k}}\end{bmatrix})\mu^{\prime}(\sigma)(n)

\displaystyle\quad+\sum_{i=0}^{n-1}(\begin{bmatrix}b_{i}&\bm{c}_{i}\end{bmatrix}\otimes\begin{bmatrix}b_{n-1-i}&\bm{c}_{n-1-i}\end{bmatrix})\mu^{\prime}(\beta)(n)

Following the definition of
\mu^{\prime}(\beta)(x)
, we deduce that

(\begin{bmatrix}b_{i}&\bm{c}_{i}\end{bmatrix}\otimes\begin{bmatrix}b_{n-1-i}&\bm{c}_{n-1-i}\end{bmatrix})\mu^{\prime}(\beta)(n)=\begin{bmatrix}-\frac{1}{a_{0}}\bm{c}_{i,1}\,b_{n-1-i}&\bm{0}_{1\times d}\end{bmatrix}=\begin{bmatrix}-\frac{a_{n+1}b_{n-1-i}}{a_{0}}&\bm{0}_{1\times d}\end{bmatrix}

Moreover, by definition of
\mu^{\prime}(\sigma)(x)
for all
k>0
, for all
\sigma\in\Sigma_{k}
, we deduce that:

(\begin{bmatrix}b_{n_{1}}&\bm{c}_{n_{1}}\end{bmatrix}\otimes\ldots\otimes\begin{bmatrix}b_{n_{k}}&\bm{c}_{n_{k}}\end{bmatrix})\mu^{\prime}(\sigma)(n)=\begin{bmatrix}0&(\bm{c}_{1}\otimes\ldots\otimes\bm{c}_{k})\mu(\sigma)(n)\end{bmatrix}

We therefore obtain:

\displaystyle\bm{a}^{\prime}_{n}

\displaystyle=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}k>0\\
\sigma\in\Sigma_{k}\end{subarray}}\;\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}n_{1},\ldots,n_{k}\in\mathbb{N}\\
n_{1}+\ldots n_{k}=n-1\end{subarray}}\begin{bmatrix}0&(\bm{c}_{1}\otimes\ldots\otimes\bm{c}_{k})\mu(\sigma)(n,n_{1},\ldots,n_{k})\end{bmatrix}+\sum_{i=0}^{n-1}\begin{bmatrix}-\frac{a_{n+1}b_{n-1-i}}{a_{0}}&\bm{0}_{1\times d}\end{bmatrix}

\displaystyle=\begin{bmatrix}0&\bm{c}_{n}\end{bmatrix}+\begin{bmatrix}b_{n}&\bm{0}_{1\times d}\end{bmatrix}

\displaystyle=\begin{bmatrix}b_{n}&\bm{c}_{n}\end{bmatrix}

As for all
n\geq 0
,
\bm{a}^{\prime}_{n,1}=b_{n}
, we conclude that
f_{{\mathcal{A}}^{\prime}}(x)=f^{-1}_{{\mathcal{A}}}(x)
.
∎
