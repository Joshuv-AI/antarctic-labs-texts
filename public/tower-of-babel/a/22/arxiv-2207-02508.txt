Inhomogeneous Dirac-Bloch Equations for Graphene Interacting with Structured Light
Yaraslau Tamashevich, Marco Ornigotti

Abstract
We generalise the usual framework of Dirac-Bloch equations, used to compute the nonlinear optical response of 2D materials excited by spatially uniform optical pulses, to the case of structured light pulses. We derive the general form of Dirac-Bloch equations in the presence of a spatially inhomogeneous field, for the case of a graphene-like material. Then, as as example of application of our method, we consider explicitly the case of angular momentum carrying optical pulses interacting with graphene.

II
Low-Energy Hamiltonian for Graphene

To derive the low energy Hamiltonian for graphene in the presence of an external field, we can start from the minimally coupled crystalline Hamiltonian

\hat{H}=\int d^{3}r\hat{\psi}^{\dagger}(\textbf{r})\left[\frac{\left(\hat{\textbf{p}}-e\textbf{A}\right)^{2}}{2m}+\phi_{c}(\textbf{r})+V_{B}(\textbf{r})\right]\hat{\psi}(\textbf{r}),

(1)

where
\phi_{c}(\textbf{r})
is the Coulomb potential,
V_{B}(\textbf{r})
is the crystalline (periodic) potential,
\hat{\psi}(\textbf{r})
is the electron wavefunction operator, and
\textbf{A}(\textbf{r},t)
is the vector potential. From here henceforth, we are assuming to work in the Coulomb gauge, where
\nabla\cdot\textbf{A}=0
, and we also neglect the Coulomb interaction terms, since they don’t contribute significantly for the case of graphene
Katsnelson
. Since for our purposes it is more convenient to work with the electric field, rather than the vector potential, we can apply the Power-Zienau-Wooley transformation
Barnett and Radmore
to the above Hamiltonian, to get the usual light-matter interction in the electric dipole approximation as follows:

\hat{H}=\hat{H}_{0}+\hat{H}_{I},

(2)

where

\displaystyle\hat{H}_{0}

\displaystyle=\int\,d^{3}r\,\hat{\psi}^{\dagger}(\textbf{r})\left[\frac{\hat{\textbf{p}}^{2}}{2m}+V_{B}(\textbf{r})\right]\hat{\psi}(\textbf{r})

(3a)

\displaystyle\equiv\int\,d^{3}r\,\hat{\psi}^{\dagger}(\textbf{r})\mathcal{H}_{0}\hat{\psi}(\textbf{r}),

\displaystyle\hat{H}_{I}

\displaystyle=-e\int\,d^{3}r\,\hat{\psi}^{\dagger}(\textbf{r})\,\left[\textbf{r}\cdot\textbf{E}_{T}(\textbf{r},t)\right]\,\hat{\psi}(\textbf{r}),

(3b)

where
\textbf{E}_{T}(\textbf{r},t)=-\partial_{t}\textbf{A}(\textbf{r},t)
is the (transverse) electric field.
To calculate the graphene Hamiltonian, we use the following Bloch representation for the electron wavefunction operator

\displaystyle\hat{\psi}(\textbf{r})=\frac{1}{\sqrt{N}}\sum_{k}\sum_{n=0}^{N}e^{i\textbf{k}\cdot\textbf{R}_{n}}\Big[\phi_{A}(\textbf{r}-\textbf{R}_{n})\hat{a}_{k}

(4)

\displaystyle+\phi_{B}(\textbf{r}-\textbf{R}_{n})\hat{b}_{k}\Big],

where

Low-Energy Approximation

\xi=\pm 1

\displaystyle f(\textbf{q})

\displaystyle\simeq

\displaystyle f(\textbf{K})+\nabla_{k}f(\textbf{k})\Big|_{\textbf{K}}\textbf{q}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{3ai}{2}\left(k_{x}-i\xi\,k_{y}\right)

III
Interaction Hamiltonian

We now substitute the Bloch Ansatz given by Eq. (
4
) into the expression of the interaction Hamiltonian, i.e., the second of Eqs. (
3
). To calculate the integrals, we proceed as before, first by introducing the change of variables
\textbf{x}=\textbf{r}-\textbf{R}_{n}
, assuming only nearest neighbour coupling, such that
\textbf{R}_{\ell}=\textbf{R}_{m}-\textbf{R}_{n}
only allows for
\ell=\{0,\pm 1\}
, and we make the extra assumption that the electric field is constant over a single unit cell, i.e.,

\textbf{E}_{T}(\textbf{x}+\textbf{R}_{n},t)\simeq\,\textbf{E}_{T}(\textbf{R}_{n},t).

(12)

Notice that this approximation is consistent with previous work dealing with semiconductor Bloch equations in the presence of inhomogeneous fields
Kuklinski and Mukamel 1991

Since we are doing these calculation in the site basis, we will have to deal with both diagonal terms, proportional to
\hat{a}^{\dagger}_{k}\hat{a}_{k}
and
\hat{b}^{\dagger}_{k}\hat{b}_{k}
, and nondiagonal terms, proportional to
\hat{a}^{\dagger}_{k}\hat{b}_{k}
. The former are related to the presence of permanent electric dipoles in the system, and without loss of generality we can neglect their contribution, since graphene (and most 2D materials, in general) do not possess permanent dipoles. Therefore, we focus our attention on the nondiagonal interaction terms, which describe light-induced transitions and are regulated by the usual electric dipole moment.
The general form of the (nondiagonal) interaction Hamiltonian is then given by

\displaystyle\hat{H}_{I}(\textbf{k})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{n}e^{i(\textbf{k}-\textbf{k'})\cdot\textbf{R}_{n}}\,\Bigg[\textbf{E}_{T}(\textbf{R}_{n},t)\cdot\textbf{d}_{CV}(\textbf{k'})

(13)

\displaystyle-

\displaystyle e

\displaystyle\,f(\textbf{k'})\textbf{R}_{n}\cdot\textbf{E}_{T}(\textbf{R}_{n},t)\,\int\,\frac{d^{3}x}{N}\phi_{A}^{*}(\textbf{x})\phi_{B}(\textbf{x})\Bigg]\hat{a}^{\dagger}_{k}\hat{b}_{k^{\prime}}

where the integral in the second line is zero because
\phi_{A}(x)
and
\phi_{B}(x)
are orthogonal,

\textbf{d}_{CV}(\textbf{k})=-e\sum_{\ell}e^{i\textbf{k}\cdot\textbf{R}_{\ell}}\int\,d^{3}x\phi_{A}^{*}(\textbf{x})\,\textbf{x}\,\phi_{B}(\textbf{x}-\textbf{R}_{\ell})\\

is the (conduction-to-valence-band) transition dipole moment in reciprocal space. Notice, that while normally, with spatially homogeneous fields, the first term in the equation above would be readily calculated because
\textbf{E}_{T}(\textbf{R}_{n},t)=\textbf{E}_{T}(t)
constant in space, this is not the case here, as we are considering a general, spatially inhomogeneous field. In this case, we can introduce the angular spectrum representation for the impinging field (i.e., its Fourier transform) as

\textbf{E}_{T}(\textbf{R}_{n},t)=\sum_{q}\mathcal{E}_{T}(\textbf{q},t)e^{-i\textbf{q}\cdot\textbf{R}_{n}},

(15)

and then introduce the generalised Rabi frequency as follows

\Omega(\textbf{k},\textbf{q},t)=\frac{1}{\hbar}\mathcal{E}_{T}(\textbf{q},t)\cdot\textbf{d}_{CV}(\textbf{k}-\textbf{q}),

(16)

so that the interaction Hamiltonian above can be written as

\hat{H}_{I}(\textbf{k})=\hbar\sum_{q}\,\Omega(\textbf{k},\textbf{q},t)\hat{a}^{\dagger}_{k}\hat{b}_{k-q}.

(17)

Notice, that in order for
\Omega(\textbf{k},\textbf{q},t)
to be a bona-fide Rabi frequency, it must be a dimensionless quantity, which implicitly means that the dimension of the electric field must be
Vs/m
, which is consistent with the fact that
|\textbf{E}|=|\partial_{t}\textbf{A}|\simeq\tau A_{0}
, with
\tau
being the characteristic timescale of the impinging electric field (for an impinging pulse,
\tau
is the duration of the pulse).

We are now in the position to write down the total interaction Hamiltonian
\hat{H}_{I}
in the site-basis as follows

\hat{H}_{I}=\hbar\sum_{k,q}\left[\Omega(\textbf{k},\textbf{q},t)\hat{a}^{\dagger}_{k}\hat{b}_{k-q}+\Omega^{*}(\textbf{k},\textbf{q},t)\hat{b}^{\dagger}_{k}\hat{a}_{k-q}\right].

(18)

This is the first result of our work. Notice, how accounting for the spatial variation of the impinging electromagnetic pulse means that we are forced to introduce a nonlocal coupling between the various states in
k
space, through the operators
\hat{a}^{\dagger}_{k}\hat{b}_{k-q}
, with the (transverse) momentum of the light pulse acting as the connection between the various
k
states available in the first Brillouin zone.

The interaction Hamiltonian in Eq. (
18
) can be put in a simpler and more straightforward form, by representing the (complex) Rabi frequency in polar coordinates, such that we get

\displaystyle\hat{H}_{I}=\hbar\sum_{k,q}\left|\Omega(\textbf{k},\textbf{q},t)\right|\Big[e^{i\omega(\textbf{k},\textbf{q},t)}\hat{a}^{\dagger}_{k}\hat{b}_{k-q}

(19)

\displaystyle+e^{-i\omega(\textbf{k},\textbf{q},t)}\hat{b}^{\dagger}_{k}\hat{a}_{k-q}\Big],

where
\left|\Omega(\textbf{k},\textbf{q},t)\right|=\sqrt{\Omega_{x}^{2}(\textbf{k},\textbf{q},t)+\Omega_{y}^{2}(\textbf{k},\textbf{q},t)}
is the modulus of the Rabi frequency, and

\omega(\textbf{k},\textbf{q},t)=\text{arg}\left[\Omega(\textbf{k},\textbf{q},t)\right]=\arctan\left[\frac{\Omega_{y}(\textbf{k},\textbf{q},t)}{\Omega_{x}(\textbf{k},\textbf{q},t)}\right],

(20)

is its phase.

III.1
Conduction-to-Valence band Dipole Moment in Reciprocal Space

The explicit expression of the conduction-to-valence band dipole moment can be calculated analytically, in the electron-hole representation, using the eigenstates (in reciprocal space) of the free Hamiltonian
\hat{H}(\textbf{k})
, as detailed in Ref.
Kelardeh et al. 2015
as

\textbf{d}_{CV}(\textbf{k})=\langle\psi_{c}(\textbf{k})|e\textbf{r}|\psi_{v}(\textbf{k})\rangle,

(21)

where the conduction and valence band eigenstates are given as

\ket{\psi_{c,v}(\textbf{k})}=e^{i\textbf{k}\cdot\textbf{r}}\ket{u_{\pm}(\textbf{k})},

where the
+
(
-
) sign refers to conduction (valence) band. Substitution gives

\textbf{d}_{CV}(\textbf{k})=\frac{e}{2}\nabla_{k}\varphi(\textbf{k}).

(22)

Notice that in the vicinity of a Dirac point
\varphi(\textbf{k})\simeq\frac{\pi}{2}+\xi\arctan(k_{y}/k_{x})
, and the dipole moment becomes

\displaystyle\textbf{d}_{CV}(\textbf{k})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{e\xi}{2}\left(-\frac{k_{y}}{k_{x}^{2}+k_{y}^{2}}\hat{\textbf{x}}+\frac{k_{x}}{k_{x}^{2}+k_{y}^{2}}\hat{\textbf{y}}\right)

(23)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-\frac{e\xi}{2|\textbf{k}|}\left[\sin\varphi_{\xi}(\textbf{k})\hat{\textbf{x}}-\cos\varphi_{\xi}(\textbf{k})\hat{\textbf{y}}\right],

or, by introducing the helicity basis
\hat{\textbf{h}}_{\xi}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}\left(\hat{\textbf{x}}+i\xi\hat{\textbf{y}}\right)

Mandel and Wolf

\textbf{d}_{CV}(\textbf{K})=-\frac{ie\xi}{2\sqrt{2}|\textbf{k}|}\left(e^{-i\varphi_{\xi}(\textbf{k})}\hat{\textbf{h}}_{+}-e^{i\varphi_{\xi}(\textbf{k})}\,\hat{\textbf{h}}_{-}\right)

(24)

III.2
Interaction Hamiltonian in the Electron-Hole Representation

We can transform the interaction Hamiltonian in the electron-hole representation using the inverse of the change of basis given by Eq. (
7

\left(\begin{array}[]{c}\hat{a}_{k}\\
\hat{b}_{k}\end{array}\right)=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}\left(\begin{array}[]{cc}e^{i\frac{\varphi(\textbf{k})}{2}}&e^{i\frac{\varphi(\textbf{k})}{2}}\\

Substituting this into Eq. (

(27)

with

The form of the interaction Hamiltonian in the electron-hole representation is quite complicated, because it contains not only interband transitions (

IV
Dirac-Bloch Equations

To calculate the Dirac-Bloch equations, we first introduce the following two-body populations and polarisation

\displaystyle n_{e}(\textbf{k},\textbf{q})

\displaystyle=\langle\hat{e}^{\dagger}_{k}\hat{e}_{q}\rangle,

(28a)

\displaystyle n_{h}(\textbf{k},\textbf{q})

\displaystyle=\langle\hat{h}^{\dagger}_{-k}\hat{h}_{-q}\rangle,

(28b)

\displaystyle p(\textbf{k},\textbf{q})

\displaystyle=\langle\hat{h}_{-k}\hat{e}_{q}\rangle,

(28c)

and calculate their time derivative with the help of the Heisenberg equation. Using the anti-commutation rules
\{\hat{e}^{\dagger}_{k},\hat{e}_{k^{\prime}}\}=\delta_{k,k^{\prime}}
and
\{\hat{h}^{\dagger}_{-k},\hat{h}_{-k^{\prime}}\}=\delta_{k,k^{\prime}}
we get, after some long but straightforward algebra, the following two-body Dirac-Bloch equations

\displaystyle-i\frac{d}{dt}n_{e}(\textbf{k},\textbf{k}^{\prime})

\displaystyle=\frac{E(\textbf{k})-E(\textbf{k}^{\prime})}{\hbar}n_{e}(\textbf{k},\textbf{k}^{\prime})+\sum_{q}\Bigg\{G_{c}(k+q,q,t)n_{e}(\textbf{k}+\textbf{q},\textbf{k}^{\prime})-G_{c}(\textbf{k}^{\prime},\textbf{q},t)n_{e}(\textbf{k},\textbf{k}^{\prime}-\textbf{q})

\displaystyle-i\left[G_{s}(\textbf{k}^{\prime},\textbf{q},t)p^{*}(\textbf{k}^{\prime}-\textbf{q},\textbf{k})-G_{s}(\textbf{k},\textbf{q},t)p(\textbf{k}^{\prime},\textbf{k}-\textbf{q})\right]\Bigg\},

(29a)

\displaystyle-i\frac{d}{dt}n_{h}(\textbf{k},\textbf{k}^{\prime})

\displaystyle=\frac{E(\textbf{k})-E(\textbf{k}^{\prime})}{\hbar}n_{h}(\textbf{k},\textbf{k}^{\prime})+\sum_{q}\Bigg\{G_{c}(\textbf{k},\textbf{q},t)n_{h}(\textbf{k}-\textbf{q},\textbf{k}^{\prime})-G_{c}(\textbf{k}^{\prime}+\textbf{q},\textbf{q},t)n_{h}(\textbf{k},\textbf{k}^{\prime}+\textbf{q})

\displaystyle+\left[G_{s}^{*}(\textbf{k}^{\prime}+\textbf{q},\textbf{q},t)p^{*}(\textbf{k},\textbf{k}^{\prime}+\textbf{q})+G_{s}(\textbf{k},\textbf{q},t)p(\textbf{k}-\textbf{q},\textbf{k}^{\prime})\right]\Bigg\},

(29b)

\displaystyle-i\frac{d}{dt}p(\textbf{k},\textbf{k}^{\prime})

\displaystyle=-\frac{E(\textbf{k}^{\prime})+E(\textbf{k})}{\hbar}p(\textbf{k},\textbf{k}^{\prime})+\sum_{q}\Bigg\{-G_{c}(\textbf{k}^{\prime},\textbf{q},t)p(\textbf{k},\textbf{k}^{\prime}-\textbf{q})-G_{c}(\textbf{k}+\textbf{q},\textbf{q},t)p(\textbf{k}+\textbf{q},\textbf{k}^{\prime})

\displaystyle-iG_{s}(\textbf{k}+\textbf{q},\textbf{q},t)n_{e}(\textbf{k}+\textbf{q},\textbf{k}^{\prime})+iG_{s}(\textbf{k}^{\prime},\textbf{q},t)\left[\delta_{k,k^{\prime}-q}-n_{h}(\textbf{k}^{\prime}-\textbf{q},\textbf{k})\right]\Bigg\}

(29c)

where
E(\textbf{k})
[
E(\textbf{k}^{\prime})
] are the energy eigenvalues of valence and conduction bands in state
k
(
\textbf{k}^{\prime}
), and we have defined

\displaystyle G_{c}(\textbf{k},\textbf{q},t)

\displaystyle=\left|\Omega(\textbf{k},\textbf{q},t)\right|\cos\Phi(\textbf{k},\textbf{q},t),

(30a)

\displaystyle G_{s}(\textbf{k},\textbf{q},t)

\displaystyle=\left|\Omega(\textbf{k},\textbf{q},t)\right|\sin\Phi(\textbf{k},\textbf{q},t).

(30b)

The equations above constitute a generalisation of the usual Dirac-Bloch equations, to account for the presence of a spatially varying electromagnetic pulse. This is the second result of our work. Notice, moreover, that written in the above form, the generalised Dirac-Bloch equations have a similar form that the semiconductor Bloch equations for an inhomogeneous electric field, as given in Ref.
33
, with the difference, that we don’t consider the effect of the Coulomb potential in the specific case of graphene. Accounting for the effect of Coulomb interactions on Dirac-Bloch equations, is of importance for certain categories of 2D materials, such as TMDs, for example, and will be the subject of a future publication.

V
Current

As a final step towards building the nonlinear optical response of graphene, we need to calculate the induced (nonlinear) current. To do so, we use the usual definition of current, in terms of population and polarisation

\hat{\textbf{j}}(t)=\sum_{k^{\prime},k,q}\hat{\psi}^{\dagger}_{k^{\prime}}U^{\dagger}(\textbf{k}^{\prime})\,\nabla_{k}\,h_{I}(\textbf{k},\textbf{q})\,U(\textbf{k}-\textbf{q})\hat{\psi}_{k-q},

(31)

where, again, we work in the interaction picture, so that the
k
-dependent interaction Hamiltonian above, in the band representation, is derived directly from Eq. (
19
), i.e.,

\displaystyle h_{I}(\textbf{k},\textbf{q})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\hbar\,\bm{\sigma}\cdot\bm{\Omega},

(32)

where
\bm{\Omega}=\Omega_{x}(\textbf{k},\textbf{q},t)\hat{\textbf{x}}+\Omega_{y}(\textbf{k},\textbf{q},t)\hat{\textbf{y}}
,
\bm{\sigma}=\sigma_{x}\hat{\textbf{x}}+\sigma_{y}\hat{\textbf{y}}
,

\hat{\psi}_{k}=(\hat{e}_{k}\hskip 2.84526pt\,\hat{h}^{\dagger}_{-k})^{T}

VI.1
A Remark on the numerical model

It is worth noticing, that to obtain the result shown in Fig.
3
, we have used the expression of the current given by Eq. (
31
), which is a time-dependent only quantity, since it is the result of the integration, over the 4-dimensional reciprocal space
\{\textbf{k},\textbf{k}^{\prime}\}
, of the
k
-dependent current density. If we want to have information on the spatial variation of the current or, analogously, of the nonlinear spectrum, we would instead need to compute the spatial Fourier transform of the current density, instead of simply integrating. By doing so, we will obtain the following, nonlocal, expression for the spatially-dependent nonlinear current

\displaystyle j_{x}(\textbf{R},\textbf{R}^{\prime},t)

\displaystyle=\sum_{k,k^{\prime},q}\mathcal{J}_{x}(\textbf{k},\textbf{k}^{\prime},\textbf{q},t)e^{i(\textbf{k}\cdot\textbf{R}+\textbf{k}^{\prime}\cdot\textbf{R}^{\prime})},

(39a)

\displaystyle j_{y}(\textbf{R},\textbf{R}^{\prime},t)

\displaystyle=\sum_{k,k^{\prime},q}\mathcal{J}_{y}(\textbf{k},\textbf{k}^{\prime},\textbf{q},t)e^{i(\textbf{k}\cdot\textbf{R}+\textbf{k}^{\prime}\cdot\textbf{R}^{\prime})},

(39b)

where
\mathcal{J}_{x,y}(\textbf{k},\textbf{k}^{\prime},t)
are the expressions inside the curly brackets in Eqs. (
36
). Calculating the Fourier transforms above, however, is computationally very demanding, since the current densities
\mathcal{J}_{x,y}(\textbf{k},\textbf{k}^{\prime},t)
are defined on an 8-dimensional grid (since they are functions of three independent
k
-vectors, namely
k
,
k
’, and
q
), which evolves in time. To efficiently perform these calculations, one would probably need to solve Eqs. (
29
) in a more efficient way, optimising the code and using, for example, a supercomputer, and then be very careful in defining a big enough grid in both
k
and
\textbf{k}^{\prime}
to ensure enough resolution for the Fourier transform. Doing this, however, would require a considerable amount of time and it is outside the scope of this work.

To partly solve this issue, however, we suggest a simplification of the problem, which results in significantly reducing the computational complexity of the problem, and could be employed to make qualitative predictions on the spatial distribution of the current. To do that, we restrict the 4-dimensional
k
-space to an effective 2-dimensional one by setting
\textbf{k}=\textbf{k}^{\prime}
in Eqs. (
29
). This, essentially, means, that we restrict the domain of definition of
\textbf{k}^{\prime}
to a point, rather than a plane. To test the validity of this approximation, in Fig.
4
we again show the harmonic spectrum and notice, that we get a pretty similar, at least qualitatively, result, than the one obtained in fig.
3
using the full model. Notice, in particular, how setting
\textbf{k}=\textbf{k}^{\prime}
essentially erases any information about the even harmonics. This, again, is not surprising, since the limit
\textbf{k}=\textbf{k}^{\prime}
can be interpreted, to some extent, as the homogeneous field limit.

Under this approximation, however, we would be able to look at the qualitative spatial evolution of the current, as now the components of the spatially dependent nonlinear current are much easier to calculate, as their explicit expressions now only amount to a regular spatial Fourier transform, i.e.,

\displaystyle j_{x}(\textbf{R},t)

\displaystyle=\sum_{k,q}\mathcal{J}_{x}(\textbf{k},\textbf{q},t)e^{i\textbf{k}\cdot\textbf{R}},

(40a)

\displaystyle j_{y}(\textbf{R},t)

\displaystyle=\sum_{k,q}\mathcal{J}_{y}(\textbf{k},\textbf{q},t)e^{i\textbf{k}\cdot\textbf{R}}.

(40b)

Despite the equations above are greatly simplified, in terms of computational challenge, with respect to Eqs. (
39
), they still need quite a high resolution on the
k
-space grid, in order to be able to generate meaningful images in direct space. Although the implementation of such optimised code is out of the scope of this manuscript, we intend to work on it for a future publication, where we aim at showing the different current dynamics corresponding to different spatial profiles of the impinging pulse.

VII
Conclusion

In this work, we introduced a general framework to study the optical response of graphene under the action of spatially-varying electromagnetic pulses, i.e., when graphene interacts with structured light. In particular, we have provided detailed derivation of the spatially-varying interaction Hamiltonian, as well as the two-body population and polarisation, constituting the building blocks for the Dirac-Bloch equations. We have then calculated the expression of the induced nonlinear current and used it to test our model against known results, by calculating the nonlinear spectrum for the case of an impinging Laguerre-Gaussian optical pulse. Surprisingly, although the overall structure of the nonlinear spectrum is not changed by a spatially varying electromagnetic field, we have reported how a spatially inhomogeneous pulse can locally break the centrosymmetry of graphene and allow the generation of even harmonics. Finally, we have shown how to obtain qualitative results to study the spatial evolution of the current density.

Although in this work we have focused our attention to graphene, our results can be easily adapted to any 2D material and provide a general framework to include the effects of structured light in both the linear and nonlinear optical response of such materials.
