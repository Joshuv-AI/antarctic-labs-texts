Convergence error analysis of reflected gradient Langevin dynamics for non-convex constrained optimization
Kanji Sato, Akiko Takeda, Reiichiro Kawai, Taiji Suzuki

Abstract
Gradient Langevin dynamics and a variety of its variants have attracted increasing attention owing to their convergence towards the global optimal solution, initially in the unconstrained convex framework while recently even in convex constrained non-convex problems. In the present work, we extend those frameworks to non-convex problems on a non-convex feasible region with a global optimization algorithm built upon reflected gradient Langevin dynamics and derive its convergence rates. By effectively making use of its reflection at the boundary in combination with the probabilistic representation for the Poisson equation with the Neumann boundary condition, we present promising convergence rates, particularly faster than the existing one for convex constrained non-convex problems.



keywords
Gradient Langevin dynamics, Non-convex constrained optimization problem, Non-asymptotic analysis, Reflected diffusion, Rademacher noise

Introduction

In the present work, we are interested in the following (generally) non-convex problems over a closed set
\overline{K}\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}
, defined as

\min_{x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}}f(x)\quad\mathrm{s.t.}\quad x\in\overline{K},

(1)

where

is a non-convex function mapping from
{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
to
\mathbb{R}
and

The most common approach to such constrained optimization problems with boundaries has long been based on the projected gradient descent (PG), the procedure of which can be described as

X_{k+1}=\mathcal{P}_{K}\left(X_{k}-\eta\gradient f(X_{k})\right),

where
\mathcal{P}_{K}
represents a projection operator defined later in (
2

In general, however, PG may well get stuck at a non-global stationary point.
In order to lead the algorithm out of such a point, it has been found effective to add stochastic perturbations, boosting up
gradient Langevin dynamics (GLD) to one of the most promising algorithms
Raginsky et al. 2017
;
Welling and Teh 2011
.

A line of research on Langevin dynamics, originating from stochastic diffusion, has been focused on global optimization of non-convex problems

Chiang et al. 1987
;
Gelfand and Mitter 1991
;
Geman and Hwang 1986

Constrained non-convex problems have not been tackled until recently because of theoretical difficulties of the underlying stochastic differential equations (SDE), whose solutions cannot be shown to exist in a trivial manner

To this end, we employ the following

As such, in the present work, we present a convergence error analysis

Our contributions

In short, the present work aims at enriching the existing theoretical analysis for constrained optimization problems, especially here with (generally non-convex) smooth constraints, on the basis of the RGLD algorithm.

While a geometric ergodicity is derived in
Lamperski 2021
based on a coupling argument for the 1-Wasserstein metric on a convexity assumption of the feasible region
\overline{K}
, its time-discretization error is
\mathcal{O}((\eta\log k)^{1/4})
, slower than the unconstrained counterpart
\mathcal{O}(\eta^{1/2})
. This property is largely because PGLD is employed there with a rather rough estimate for the discretization error in terms of the convergence in mean.
Despite PGLD has a convergence guarantee in convex-constrained problems (for instance,
Bubeck et al. 2018
), it is known to induce a suboptimal rate.

To address this issue, we replace the projection step with the so-called “reflection,”
which enables one to derive better convergence rates by canceling out certain terms of the error expansion.
To avoid some theoretical difficulties incurred by the reflection step, we exploit the probabilistic representation for the Poisson equation with the Neumann boundary condition, corresponding to its continuous-time limit
Leimkuhler et al. 2023
, which results in simpler proofs with the aid of the established results on the continuous-time dynamics.
In addition, we succeed to explicitly specify the parameter dependence of the error bound when deriving the convergence rate, unlike
(
Leimkuhler et al. 2023
, Theorem 4.2)
.
Since, for instance, a large inverse temperature
\beta
needs to be set in the optimization procedure, it is of great importance to have control of those key parameters.

Our contribution is summarized as follows:

•

This is the first study to address non-convex constrained problems with a certain smoothness assumption on the feasible region.

•

We develop a GLD algorithm based upon reflection steps and the Rademacher noise, unlike projection steps and the Gaussian noise in the existing GLD algorithms.

•

The obtained convergence rate is, as summarized in Table
1
, faster than that of
Lamperski 2021
owing to a sharper discretization error with a small gap still remaining in the convergence rate for constrained and unconstrained problems.

Finally, while employing existing techniques in deriving the convergence rate, we make several technical advances as well, in relation to the reflected diffusion (for instance, canceling-out of the second-order terms of the Taylor expansion), each of which is an essential yet quite unfamiliar piece in the relevant context.

2
The algorithm: RGLD

To describe the reflected gradient Langevin dynamics (RGLD) method in detail, we first develop the notation that is used throughout the paper.
We let
\partial K
,
K^{c}
and
\overline{K}
denote the boundary, complement and closure of the set
K
, respectively.
We denote by
K_{-r}
the set of points outside
K
whose distance from
\overline{K}
is at most
r
, that is,

\displaystyle K_{-r}:=\left\{x\in K^{c}:\,\mathrm{dist}(x,K)\leq r\right\},

where
\mathrm{dist}(z,S):=\min_{y\in\overline{S}}\|y-z\|
for the point
z
and set
S
.
Let
\mathcal{C}^{n}(K)
denote the set of
n
-times continuously differentiable functions on the open set
K
and let
\mathcal{C}^{n}(\overline{K})
be the set of functions in
\mathcal{C}^{n}(K)
all of whose derivatives of order up to
n
have continuous extensions to the closure
\overline{K}
.

We let
\widetilde{\mathcal{O}}
represent the order symbol ignoring the poly-log arithmetic factors.
Namely, for sequences
\{a_{n}\}_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
and
\{b_{n}\}_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
, we mean by
a_{n}=\widetilde{\mathcal{O}}(b_{n})
that there exist positive constants
c_{1}
and
c_{2}
such that
|a_{n}|\leq c_{1}|b_{n}|\log^{c_{2}}|b_{n}|
for all
n
.
Similarly, for sequences of positive elements
\{a_{n}\}_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
and
\{b_{n}\}_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
, we write
a_{n}\preceq b_{n}
if there exists a positive constant
c
such that
a_{n}\leq cb_{n}
for all
n
, and write
a_{n}\sim b_{n}
if
a_{n}\preceq b_{n}\land a_{n}\succeq b_{n}.

Finally, we let
[n]
denote the set
\{0,1,\cdots,n-1\}
.
We say that
\xi
is a Rademacher random vector in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
if it consists of independent and identically distributed (iid) components, each of which only takes two values
\pm 1
equally likely.
For a compact subset
\overline{K}
of
\mathbb{R}^{d}
,
we define the projection
\mathcal{P}_{K}
and reflection
\mathcal{R}_{K}
, respectively, as follows:

\mathcal{P}_{K}(x):=\argmin_{y\in\overline{K}}\norm{y-x},\quad\mathcal{R}_{K}(x):=2\mathcal{P}_{K}(x)-x.

(2)

With the notation defined above, we are now ready to describe the RGLD method.
Throughout, we call the parameters
\eta
and
\beta
appearing in the algorithm, respectively, the step size and the inverse temperature parameter.

Algorithm 1

(Reflected gradient Langevin dynamics (RGLD))
.

Repeat the following procedure; for
k\in\mathbb{N}_{0}(:=\{0,1,\cdots\})
,

\begin{cases}X^{\prime}_{k+1}=X_{k}-\eta\gradient f(X_{k})+\sqrt{2\eta/\beta}\xi_{k+1},\\
X_{k+1}=\mathcal{R}_{K}\left(X^{\prime}_{k+1}\right),\end{cases}

where
X_{0}\in K
,
\eta>0
,
\beta>0
and
\{\xi_{k}\}_{k\in\mathbb{N}}
is a sequence of iid Rademacher random vectors in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
.

If
\mathcal{P}_{K}
is efficiently computable by some oracle and

\gradient f
is easily accessible by some oracle, the computation of each iteration is simple.
In this paper, we are interested in estimating how many iterations are required to obtain an
\epsilon
-optimal solution.
The reflection
\mathcal{R}_{K}
in the algorithm above (or, equivalently, the projection
\mathcal{P}_{K}
in light of the definitions (
2
)) can be uniquely determined even in cases where the domain
K
lacks convexity, provided that its boundary exhibits sufficient smoothness and the location
X_{k+1}^{\prime}
(prior to reflection) remains sufficiently close to the domain
K
after overshooting it.
Let us stress the significance of employing the bounded Rademacher noise
\{\xi_{k}\}_{k\in\mathbb{N}}
(over the unbounded Gaussian one) in maintaining the proximity of
X_{k+1}^{\prime}
to the domain when combined with sufficiently small
\eta
and/or large
\beta
.

Conventionally, the projection (that is,
\mathcal{P}_{K}
, rather than
\mathcal{R}_{K}
) has been adopted to GLD, as in the projected GLD (PGLD) algorithm, as in

Bubeck et al. 2018
;
Lamperski 2021
.
Also, the Gaussian noise has naturally and thus often been employed for approximating the diffusion term appearing in the continuous-time limit of GLD
Bossy et al. 2004
, whereas it can, rarely yet with a positive probability, produce so large outputs that the solution may jump too far away from the feasible region.
The Rademacher noise that we employ in the algorithm above is bounded and thus keeps the trajectory (before projection or reflection) close to the feasible region.
It also turns out (Appendix
8
) that the Rademacher noise here plays an essential role in achieving higher-order approximation error by canceling out the second-order term of the Taylor expansion in the course of the error analysis.

The proposed algorithm can be thought of as a time-discretization version of the continuous-time reflected gradient Langevin diffusion
\{X(t):\,t\geq 0\}
, given by

dX(t)=-\gradient f(X(t))dt+\sqrt{2/\beta}dW(t)-v(X(t))dL(t),

(3)

where
\{W(t):\,t\geq 0\}
is the standard
d
-dimensional Brownian motion,
v(\cdot)
denotes the outward pointing normal defined on the boundary
\partial K
, and
\{L(t):\,t\geq 0\}
is the boundary local time, which can be defined as
L(t):=\lim_{\epsilon\to 0}\epsilon^{-1}\int_{0}^{t}\mathbbm{1}(X(s)\in\partial\Omega_{\epsilon})ds,
with
\partial\Omega_{\epsilon}:=\{x\in\overline{K}:\,{\rm dist}(x,\partial K)\leq\epsilon\}
(which is a neighborhood inside the domain, as opposed to the extension
K_{-r}
), where the limit here is well defined almost surely as well as in quadratic mean (see, for instance,
Kawai 2015
).
The first term in (
3
) represents a drift descending along the direction of
-\gradient f
, while the second one is the diffusion term that perturbs the gradient descent by Gaussian noise.
The last term
-v(X(t))dL(t)
governs reflection
Dupuis and Ramanan 1999
;
Lions and
Sznitman 1984
;
Tanaka 1979
to keep the trajectory in the domain
K

and obviously requires a non-trivial treatment when the dynamics is discretized.

It is known
Bubeck et al. 2018
that under suitable conditions, the continuous-time RGLD (
3
) converges weakly to a unique stationary distribution, which is the Gibbs distribution with the probability density function
\pi
, given by

\pi(x):=\frac{\exp(-\beta f(x))}{\int_{K}\exp(-\beta f(y))dy},

(4)

defined on the domain
K
. The explicit expression of the Gibbs distribution here enables one to bound the discrepancy between the expectation of the objective with respect to the stationary distribution and the optimal value
Raginsky et al. 2017
.
As such, our strategy is to show a weak convergence of the discrete-time dynamics to the stationary distribution by bounding the discretization error with the aid of the probabilistic representation for the Poisson equation with the Neumann boundary condition.

Convergence Analysis

First, we prepare technical conditions on the domain

Assumption

This condition is imposed here so that an associated PDE (given shortly in (

After a smoothing operation, such as the Gaussian filter, has been applied as often done in practice, this smoothness condition is fulfilled even for non-smooth constraints appearing in the sparse estimation.

Finally, Assumption

Moreover, the

Proposition 3
.

Under Assumption

\norm{\grad f(x)-\grad f(y)}\leq M\norm{x-y}
for all

.

It is worth mentioning that, from a practical standpoint, the algorithm runs properly under Assumption

alone, which is essential for the feasibility of the algorithm.

The reflection
\mathcal{R}_{K}
in Algorithm

can be uniquely determined if the step size
\eta
is set sufficiently small, due to Assumption
2
.
For this, it suffices to show the asymptotics

\left\|\mathcal{P}_{K}(X^{\prime}_{k})-X^{\prime}_{k}\right\|=\mathcal{O}\left(\eta+\sqrt{d\eta/\beta}\right),

(5)

as follows

Bossy et al. 2004
;
Leimkuhler et al. 2023
.
First, observe that
\norm{\mathcal{P}_K(X'_k)-X'_k}
is bounded, based on the definition of the projection (
2
) and
X_{k-1}\in K
, as

\norm{\mathcal{P}_K(X'_k)-X'_k}=\min_{x\in\overline{K}}\norm{x-X'_k}\leq\norm{X_{k-1}-X'_k},

which can be further bounded as

\norm{X_{k-1}-X'_k}=\norm{- \eta\grad f(X_{k-1}) + \sqrt{2\eta/\beta}\xi_{k}}\leq\eta\norm{\grad f(X_{k-1})}+\sqrt{2\eta/\beta}\norm{\xi_k},

due to the triangle inequality.
Since the norm
\|\gradient f\|
is uniformly bounded (by a suitable constant
G
) and
\norm{\xi_k}=\sqrt{d}
, it holds that

\eta\norm{\grad f(X_{k-1})}+\sqrt{2\eta/\beta}\norm{\xi_k}\leq\eta G+\sqrt{2d\eta/\beta}=\mathcal{O}\left(\eta+\sqrt{d\eta/\beta}\right),

which ensures that
\mathcal{R}_{K}(X^{\prime}_{k})
does not overshoot the domain

, as long as the step size
\eta
is sufficiently small.
In each experiment of Section

\eta
be smaller than the smallest radius of a finite open cover of the compact domain
\overline{K}
.

3.2
Convergence rate of RGLD

Here, we turn to the main result of the present work on the convergence rate of RGLD on the basis of the geometric ergodicity of the continuous-time limit of RGLD.
The rate of its weak convergence to the unique stationary distribution is governed by the spectral gap
\lambda_{*}
, defined as
the minimum non-zero eigenvalue of
-\mathcal{A}
, where
\mathcal{A}
denotes the infinitesimal generator of the underlying dynamics, that is, for
h\in\mathcal{C}^{2}(K)
,

\mathcal{A}h(x):=\beta^{-1}\Delta h(x)-\langle\gradient f(x),\gradient h(x)\rangle,\quad x\in K,

(6)

where
\Delta
denotes the Laplace operator.
For more detail on the spectral gap, we refer the reader to, for example,
Chen and Lou 2012
;
Peng et al. 2019
as well as
(
Bakry et al. 2014
, Chapter 4)
.
We are now ready to state the main result.

Theorem 4
.

Let Assumption
2
hold, let
\eta<1
and let
\beta>1
.
Then, it holds that

\mathbb{E}\left[\min_{k\in[N]}f(X_{k})\right]-\min_{x\in\overline{K}}f(x)\preceq\frac{1}{\lambda_{*}N\eta}+\frac{\sqrt{\eta}\left(\beta\sqrt{\eta}+(\beta\eta+d)^{3/2}\right)}{\lambda_{*}}+\frac{d}{\beta}\log\beta.

In particular, for every
0<\epsilon\ll 1
, it holds that

\mathbb{E}\left[\min_{k\in[N]}f(X_{k})\right]-\min_{x\in\overline{K}}f(x)\preceq\epsilon+\frac{d\log\beta}{\beta},

(7)

provided that

\beta\succeq 1,\;\eta\preceq\min\left\{\frac{\lambda_{*}^{2}\epsilon^{2}}{d^{3}},\frac{\lambda_{*}\epsilon}{\beta}\right\},\;N\succeq\frac{1}{\lambda_{*}\epsilon\eta}\succeq\max\left\{\frac{d^{3}}{\lambda_{*}^{3}\epsilon^{3}},\frac{\beta}{\lambda_{*}^{2}\epsilon^{2}}\right\}.

Broadly speaking, the theorem claims that, by letting the step size
\eta
be sufficiently small, an
\epsilon
-sampling error is achieved after
\widetilde{\mathcal{O}}(\max\{{d^{3}}{\lambda_{*}^{-3}\epsilon^{-3}},{\beta}{\lambda_{*}^{-2}\epsilon^{-2}}\})
iterations of RGLD.
Recall that the iteration complexity for PGLD obtained in
Lamperski 2021
is given as
N=\widetilde{\mathcal{O}}(d^{4}\lambda_{*}^{-1}\epsilon^{-4})
(see Table
1
), which is not as sharp as our evaluation
N=\widetilde{\mathcal{O}}(d^{3}\lambda_{*}^{-3}\epsilon^{-3})

when the inverse temperature parameter
\beta
is sufficiently large in the order
\widetilde{\mathcal{O}}(d)
(which is indeed the situation of interest).

This improvement is mainly due to the reflection
\mathcal{R}_{K}
employed in the present work, instead of the projection
\mathcal{P}_{K}
.
With the reflection, the discretization error can be evaluated by approximating the time differentiation for the infinitesimal generator by a discrete-time differentiation with the aid of the Poisson equation with the Neumann boundary condition:

\begin{cases}\mathcal{A}u(x)=f(x)-\mathbb{E}_{\pi}f,&x\in K,\\
\langle\gradient u(x),v(x)\rangle=0,&x\in\partial K,\end{cases}

(8)

where
\mathcal{A}
is the infinitesimal generator (
6
).
In that approximation, the second-order derivatives are canceled out thanks to the symmetry between the states
X_{k}
(after reflection) and
X^{\prime}_{k}
(before reflection), yielding a sharper bound than that of PGLD.
The discretization error between the outputs
f(X_{k})
(in discrete time) and
f(X(k\eta))
(in continuous time) in PGLD is
\widetilde{\mathcal{O}}(\eta^{1/4})
in
(
Lamperski 2021
, Lemmas 5 and 6)
, not as sharp as our result.

Remark 5
.

The dependence of the spectral gap
\lambda_{*}

One may be tempted to set the spectral gap

in proportion to the exponential of the inverse temperature parameter

;

If that is really the case, finding an

\epsilon

It is necessary to set

That is, the number of iterations

5
Conclusion

In the present work, we have derived a sub-linear convergence rate of RGLD in solving smoothly constrained problems.
In addition, the convergence rate, faster than that of
Lamperski 2021
, has been obtained by employing the reflection and making use of a direct estimate of the discretization error on the basis of the Poisson equation with the Neumann boundary condition.
The order of the spectral gap remains unclear as the smallest eigenvalue problems with the Neumann boundary condition have not been studied much.
The smoothness of the boundary, which is essential in the present analysis, is not always satisfied in many applications, whereas a non-smooth domain can be made so by a suitable smoothing operation.

We close this work by highlighting some future research directions.
It is certainly ideal that the thickness of the domain can be quantified in one way or another so as to set the step size in a more objective manner.
The scope of the algorithm, in its current form, may not sufficiently accommodate desired breadth due to the smoothness conditions outlined in Assumption
2
.
It certainly holds significant importance in loosening these technical constraints to encompass more general unbounded and non-smooth constraints.
One promising avenue is to introduce a dissipativity condition
Raginsky et al. 2017
;
Xu et al. 2018
with the aid of a suitable theoretical background
Barbu and Prato 2005
, in place of the boundedness assumption of the domain.
It would also be of great interest to extend our results to the stochastic gradient oracle version.
All those would be interesting future directions of research deserving of their own separate investigation.

Related studies on GLD

GLD, in a broad sense including its stochastic variants (SGLD), has long been studied, dating back to
Geman and Hwang 1986
, from a wide variety of perspectives with a view towards many fields of application ever since, such as Bayesian learning
Welling and Teh 2011
to name just a few.
In particular, the convergence of GLD has been investigated quite intensively, for instance, that to the global minimum of the objective functions (
Chiang et al. 1987
;
Gelfand and Mitter 1991
among many others), whereas to local optima
Zhang et al. 2017
.

Built upon the established probabilistic analyses of GLD and SGLD, convergence rates to global optima are derived in
Raginsky et al. 2017
;
Vempala and Wibisono 2019
;
Xu et al. 2018
for unconstrained non-convex problems in the non-asymptotic regime.
To our knowledge, the best convergence rate up to date is can be found in
Zou et al. 2021
.
SGLD is combined with stochastic variance-reduced gradient (SVRG)
Chen et al. 2022
, while a non-smooth objective function is investigated in
Durmus et al. 2018
using the Moreau envelope.

Among many studies in this line of research, the work
Bubeck et al. 2018
can be thought of as the first development on projected gradient Langevin dynamics (PGLD) for constrained sampling.
It aims at sampling from a log-concave distribution, which translates into convex optimization in our context.
Sampling from a constrained log-concave distribution is also investigated in
Brosse et al. 2017
based on a proximal operation.
Such proximal-type algorithms have also been constructed in
Pereyra 2016
;
Salim et al. 2019
;
Salim and Richtarik 2020
.
Moreover, mirrored Langevin dynamics is proposed in
Hsieh et al. 2018
;
Zhang et al. 2020
as a way to handle constraints, in which the mirror map is employed to transform the target distribution into an unconstrained one.
Despite this approach is valid in many existing methods, it is not a trivial task to find an appropriate mirror map in practice.
The developments
Patterson and
Teh 2013
;
Wang et al. 2020
can also be considered to be related studies in the realm of constrained optimization when the feasible region forms a Riemannian manifold, such as the Stiefel or Grassmann manifold.

A GLD algorithm is proposed in
Parpas et al. 2006
for the first time with convergence to the global optimum for linearly constrained non-convex problems.
Recently, a non-asymptotic convergence rate of PGLD is derived in
Lamperski 2021
for convex constrained non-convex problems, where reflected stochastic differential equations (RSDE) play a central role in its discretization and theoretical developments, just as in our analysis.

In probability theory, the existence and uniqueness of the solution of the reflected diffusion is studied
Lions and
Sznitman 1984
;
Tanaka 1979
in the name of the Skorokhod problem.
The regularity of the solution and the existence of a unique stationary distribution of RSDEs are investigated, respectively, in
Andres 2011
;
Dupuis and Ramanan 1999
and
Banerjee and
Budhiraja 2020
;
Freidlin 1985
.
RSDEs have also been investigated from the perspective of partial differential equations, for instance, in
Barbu and Prato 2005
;
Cerrai 1998
;
Miranda 1970
.
The discretization error in path generation of RSDEs has been a key component in connecting the analysis on SDEs with the convergence rate of optimization algorithms
Cattiaux et al. 2017
;
Ding and ying Zhang 2008
;
Leimkuhler et al. 2023
.

\displaystyle=-\frac{r_{k+1}^{3}}{6}D^{3}\widetilde{u}(X_{k+1}^{\rm proj}-\alpha_{1}r_{k+1}v_{k+1}^{\rm proj})\left(v_{k+1}^{\rm proj},v_{k+1}^{\rm proj},v_{k+1}^{\rm proj}\right)

\displaystyle\qquad-\frac{r_{k+1}^{3}}{6}D^{3}\widetilde{u}(X_{k+1}^{\rm proj}+\alpha_{2}r_{k+1}v_{k+1}^{\rm proj})\left(v_{k+1}^{\rm proj},v_{k+1}^{\rm proj},v_{k+1}^{\rm proj}\right)

(12)

\displaystyle\geq-\frac{r_{k+1}^{3}}{3}\norm{D^3 \widetilde u},

where we have applied
Du_{k+1}^{\rm proj}(v_{k+1}^{\rm proj})=\langle\gradient u(X^{\rm proj}_{k+1}),v_{k+1}^{\rm proj}\rangle=0
, due to (
10
).
The last inequality holds true by the definition of the operator norm and since
\|v_{k+1}^{\rm proj}\|
only takes values in
\{0,1\}
.
In a similar manner, with a constant
\alpha_{3}
in
[0,1]
, we obtain

\displaystyle u^{\prime}_{k+1}-u_{k}

\displaystyle=\widetilde{u}\left(X_{k}-\eta\gradient f_{k}+\sqrt{\frac{2\eta}{\beta}}\xi_{k+1}\right)-u_{k}

\displaystyle=Du_{k}\left(-\eta\gradient f_{k}+\sqrt{\frac{2\eta}{\beta}}\xi_{k+1}\right)

\displaystyle\quad+\frac{1}{2}D^{2}u_{k}\left(-\eta\gradient f_{k}+\sqrt{\frac{2\eta}{\beta}}\xi_{k+1},-\eta\gradient f_{k}+\sqrt{\frac{2\eta}{\beta}}\xi_{k+1}\right)

\displaystyle\quad+\frac{1}{6}D^{3}\widetilde{u}\left(X_{k}+\alpha_{3}\left(-\eta\gradient f_{k}+\sqrt{\frac{2\eta}{\beta}}\xi_{k+1}\right)\right)\left(-\eta\gradient f_{k}+\sqrt{\frac{2\eta}{\beta}}\xi_{k+1},\right.

\displaystyle\quad\left.-\eta\gradient f_{k}+\sqrt{\frac{2\eta}{\beta}}\xi_{k+1},-\eta\gradient f_{k}+\sqrt{\frac{2\eta}{\beta}}\xi_{k+1}\right),

and

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[u^{\prime}_{k+1}-u_{k}\right]

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}\left[\eta\left(f_{k}-\mathbb{E}_{\pi}f\right)+\frac{\eta^{2}}{2}D^{2}u_{k}\left(\gradient f_{k},\gradient f_{k}\right)\right.

\displaystyle\quad\left.+\frac{1}{6}D^{3}\widetilde{u}\left(X_{k}+\alpha_{3}\left(-\eta\gradient f_{k}+\sqrt{\frac{2\eta}{\beta}}\xi_{k+1}\right)\right)\left(-\eta\gradient f_{k}+\sqrt{\frac{2\eta}{\beta}}\xi_{k+1},\right.\right.

\displaystyle\quad\left.\left.-\eta\gradient f_{k}+\sqrt{\frac{2\eta}{\beta}}\xi_{k+1},-\eta\gradient f_{k}+\sqrt{\frac{2\eta}{\beta}}\xi_{k+1}\right)\right],

(13)

due to (
6
) and
\mathbb{E}[\xi_{k+1}]=0
.
By applying (
12
) and (
13
) to the equation
\mathbb{E}[u_{k+1}^{\prime}-u_{k}]=\mathbb{E}[u_{k+1}-u_{k}]-\mathbb{E}[u_{k+1}-u_{k+1}^{\prime}]
, we obtain

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[\eta\left(f_{k}-\mathbb{E}_{\pi}f\right)+\frac{\eta^{2}}{2}D^{2}u_{k}\left(\gradient f_{k},\gradient f_{k}\right)\right.

\displaystyle\qquad\left.+\frac{1}{6}D^{3}\widetilde{u}\left(X_{k}+\alpha_{3}\left(-\eta\gradient f_{k}+\sqrt{\frac{2\eta}{\beta}}\xi_{k+1}\right)\right)\left(-\eta\gradient f_{k}+\sqrt{\frac{2\eta}{\beta}}\xi_{k+1},\right.\right.

\displaystyle\qquad\left.\left.-\eta\gradient f_{k}+\sqrt{\frac{2\eta}{\beta}}\xi_{k+1},-\eta\gradient f_{k}+\sqrt{\frac{2\eta}{\beta}}\xi_{k+1}\right)\right]

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad\leq\mathbb{E}\left[u_{k+1}-u_{k}\right]+\frac{r_{k+1}^{3}}{3}\norm{D^3 \widetilde u}.

Since
\|\xi_{k+1}\|=\sqrt{d}
and
\|\gradient f\|
is uniformly bounded over the domain
K
, we get

\mathbb{E}\left[\eta\left(f_{k}-\mathbb{E}_{\pi}f\right)\right]\leq\mathbb{E}\left[u_{k+1}-u_{k}\right]+\frac{r_{k+1}^{3}}{3}\norm{D^3 \widetilde u}+C\eta^{2}\norm{D^2 \widetilde u}+C\norm{D^3 \widetilde u}\left(\eta+\sqrt{d\eta/\beta}\right)^{3}.

(14)

To obtain the upper bounds of the right-hand side, we employ the following result, which we derive after completing the proof of Lemma
7
(Appendix
8.2
) to maintain the flow of the paper.

Lemma 10
.

Let
i\in\{0,1,2,3\}
and let
r
be the supremum of positive numbers such that the projection of all
x
in
K_{-r}
is uniquely defined.
There exists a positive constant
C
such that
\|D^{i}\widetilde{u}(x)\|\leq C\beta^{i/2}/\lambda_{*}
for all
x\in\overline{K\cup K_{-r}}
.

To continue Lemma
7
, we apply Lemma
10
to the inequality (
14
) to obtain

\mathbb{E}\left[\eta\left(f(X_{k})-\mathbb{E}_{\pi}f\right)\right]\leq\mathbb{E}\left[u(X_{k+1})-u(X_{k})\right]+\frac{C}{\lambda_{*}}\left(\beta^{3/2}r_{k+1}^{3}+\beta\eta^{2}+\beta^{3/2}\eta^{3}+(d\eta)^{3/2}\right),

which, with
r_{k+1}=\mathcal{O}(\eta+\sqrt{d\eta/\beta})
shown in (
5
), further yields

\mathbb{E}\left[\eta\left(f(X_{k})-\mathbb{E}_{\pi}f\right)\right]<\mathbb{E}\left[u(X_{k+1})-u(X_{k})\right]+\frac{C\eta^{3/2}\left(\beta\sqrt{\eta}+(\beta\eta+d\right)^{3/2})}{\lambda_{*}}.

Thus, again thanks to Lemma
10
, we get

\displaystyle\frac{1}{N}\sum_{k\in[N]}\mathbb{E}\left[f(X_{k})-\mathbb{E}_{\pi}f\right]

\displaystyle\leq\mathbb{E}\left[\frac{u(X_{N})-u(X_{0})}{N\eta}\right]+\frac{C\sqrt{\eta}\left(\beta\sqrt{\eta}+(\beta\eta+d)^{3/2}\right)}{\lambda_{*}}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{C}{\lambda_{*}N\eta}+\frac{C\sqrt{\eta}\left(\beta\sqrt{\eta}+(\beta\eta+d)^{3/2}\right)}{\lambda_{*}},

which derives Lemma
7
.

8.2
Proof of Lemma
10

Finally, we give the proof of Lemma
10
.
Towards this end, we show the following Lemma
11

on the function
\phi(t,x):=\mathbb{E}\left[f(X^{x}(t))-\mathbb{E}_{\pi}f\right]
.

Lemma 11
.

Let
i\in\{1,2,3\}
.
There exists a positive constant
C
such that

\|D^{i}\phi(t,x)\|\leq C\beta^{i/2}e^{-\lambda_{*}t}
for all
t\geq 0
and
x\in\overline{K}
.

Integrating the inequality in
t
together with
u(x)=-\int_{0}^{\infty}\phi(t,x)dt
for
x\in\overline{K}
and
extending the range of
x
from
\overline{K}
to
\overline{K\cup K_{-r}}
as discussed in the beginning of this section
lead to the claim of Lemma
10
.
Therefore, it now suffices to show Lemma
11
.
To this end, we employ the following two estimates, due to
(
Gobet 2001
, Section 3.2)
and
(
Freidlin 1985
, Lemma 3.7.3)
, respectively.
We note that the second one does not stand alone by itself for our present purpose but helps the first one in combination.

Lemma 12
.

(i)
Let
i\in\{1,2,3\}
,
g\in\mathcal{C}^{5}(\overline{K})
,
and define
\Phi(t,x):=\mathbb{E}[g(X^{x}(t))]
.
There exists a positive constant
C
such that
\|D^{i}\Phi(t,x)\|\leq C(\beta/t)^{i/2}\|g\|_{\infty}

for all
t\in(0,1]
and
x\in\overline{K}
.

(ii) There exists a positive constant
C
such that
|\phi(t,x)|\leq Ce^{-\lambda_{*}t}
for all
t\geq 0
and
x\in\overline{K}
.

To continue, fix an initial state
x
in the domain
K
and define the (random) set
S:=\{t\geq 0:\,X^{x}(t)\in\partial K\}
and the function
r(t):=\sup(S\cap[0,t])
.
For
h\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
and
t\geq 0
, let
\{Y_{t}^{h}:\,t\geq 0\}
be the solution to

Y^{h}_{t}=\begin{cases}h-\int_{0}^{t}\Delta f(X^{x}(s))Y^{h}_{s}ds,&\text{if}\>t<\inf S,\\
\mathcal{P}_{X^{x}(r(t))}(Y^{h}_{r(t)^{-}})-\int_{r(t)}^{t}\Delta f(X^{x}(s))Y^{h}_{s}ds,&\text{if}\>t\geq\inf S,\end{cases}

where
Y^{h}_{s^{-}}
denotes the left limit
\lim_{t\uparrow s^{-}}Y^{h}_{t}
and
\mathcal{P}_{x}(z):=z-\langle z,v(x)\rangle v(x)
, for
z\in\mathbb{R}^{d}.

For ease of notation, we have subscripted the (continuous) time index of the solution
\{Y_{t}^{h}:\,t\geq 0\}
(as opposed to
\{X^{x}(t):\,t\geq 0\}
).
It is known (for instance,
Andres 2011
) that
Y_{t}^{h}
is the first derivative (in probability) of
X^{x}(t)
with respect to
x
along the direction
h\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
.
Next, we denote by
\{Z_{t}^{h,k}:\,t\geq 0\}
the solution to

Z_{t}^{h,k}=\begin{cases}-\int_{0}^{t}\Delta f(X^{x}(s))Z_{s}^{h,k}+D^{3}f(X^{x}(s))(Y_{s}^{h},Y_{s}^{k})ds,&\text{if }t<\inf S,\\
-\int_{r(t)}^{t}\Delta f(X^{x}(s))Z_{s}^{h,k}+D^{3}f(X^{x}(s))(Y_{s}^{h},Y_{s}^{k})ds,&\text{if }t\geq\inf S.\end{cases}

In a similar manner to the first derivative, it is known (for instance,
Cerrai 1998
) that
Z_{t}^{h,k}
is the second derivative (in probability) of
X^{x}(t)
with respect to
x
along the directions
h
and
k
.
Finally, let
\{V_{t}^{l,m,n}:\,t\geq 0\}
denote the solution to

V_{t}^{l,m,n}=\begin{cases}-\int_{0}^{t}(\Delta f(X^{x}(s))V_{s}^{l,m,n}+D^{4}f(X^{x}(s))(Y_{s}^{l},Y_{s}^{m},Y_{s}^{n})\\
\qquad\qquad+\frac{1}{2}\sum_{\sigma\in\mathcal{S}_{3}}D^{3}f(X^{x}(s))(Y_{s}^{\sigma(l)},Z_{s}^{\sigma(m),\sigma(n)}))ds,&\text{if }t<\inf S,\\
-\int_{r(t)}^{t}(\Delta f(X^{x}(s))V_{s}^{l,m,n}+D^{4}f(X^{x}(s))(Y_{s}^{l},Y_{s}^{m},Y_{s}^{n})\\
\qquad\qquad+\frac{1}{2}\sum_{\sigma\in\mathcal{S}_{3}}D^{3}f(X^{x}(s))(Y_{s}^{\sigma(l)},Z_{s}^{\sigma(m),\sigma(n)}))ds,&\text{if }t\geq\inf S,\end{cases}

where
\mathcal{S}_{3}
denotes the set of all permutations of three elements.
Just as for the first and second derivatives,
V_{t}^{l,m,n}(x)
is the third derivative (in probability) of
X^{x}(t)
with respect to
x
along the directions
l
,
m
and
n
.

We are now ready to derive Lemma
11
with the aid of
Lemma
12
.
If
t\geq 1
, then
applying Lemma
12
(i) with
t\leftarrow 1
and
g(x)\leftarrow\phi(t-1,x)
in its statement and evaluating
\norm{g}_{\infty}
by Lemma
12
(ii) yields

\norm{D^i\E{\phi(t-1,X^x(1))}}\leq C\beta^{i/2}\mathrm{e}^{-\lambda_{*}(t-1)},

which is independent of the initial state
x
and is thus equivalent to

\norm{D^i\phi(t, x)}\leq C\beta^{i/2}\mathrm{e}^{-\lambda_{*}(t-1)}\leq C\beta^{i/2}\mathrm{e}^{-\lambda_{*}t},

which proves the claim.

As for the situation
t<1
, we start by showing that
Y_{t}
,
Z_{t}
and
V_{t}
are almost surely bounded for all
t\in(0,1]
.
For
t<\inf S
, we have

\displaystyle\norm{Y_t^h}

\displaystyle\leq\norm{h}+\int_{0}^{t}\norm{\Delta f(X^x(s)) Y_s^h}ds\leq\norm{h}+C\int_{0}^{t}\norm{Y_s^h}ds.

Then, with the aid of Gronwall’s inequality, we get

\displaystyle\norm{Y_t^h}\leq\norm{h}\exp(\int_0^t Cds)\leq\norm{h}\exp(\int_0^1 Cds)\leq\norm{h}\mathrm{e}^{C}\leq C\norm{h}.

If
t\geq\inf S
, it holds by recursion that

\displaystyle\norm{Y_t^h}\leq C\norm{\mathcal{P}_{X^x({r(t)})}(Y_{r(t)-}^h)}\leq C\norm{Y_{r(t)-}^h}\leq C\norm{h}.

As for
Z_{t}
, we obtain from the results for
Y_{t}^{h}
above that

\displaystyle\norm{Z_t^{h,k}}\leq\int_{0}^{t}C\norm{Z_s^{h,k}}+C\norm{h}\norm{k}ds\leq C\norm{h}\norm{k}+\int_{0}^{t}C\norm{Z_s^{h,k}}ds.

Again with the aid of Gronwall’s inequality, we get
\|Z_{t}^{h,k}\|\leq C\norm{h}\norm{k}
.
In a similar manner, it follows that
V_{t}^{l,m,n}\leq C\norm{l}\norm{m}\norm{n}
.
Finally, for
i\in\{1,2,3\}
, the quantity
D^{i}\phi(t,x)
can be represented, as follows:

\displaystyle D\phi(t,x).h

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}\left[Df(X^{x}(t)).Y_{t}^{h}(x)\right],

\displaystyle D^{2}\phi(t,x)\left(h,k\right)

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}\left[D^{2}f(X^{x}(t))\left(Y_{t}^{h},Y_{t}^{k}\right)+Df(X^{x}(t)).Z_{t}^{h,k}\right],

\displaystyle D^{3}\phi(t,x)\left(l,m,n\right)

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}\left[D^{3}f(X^{x}(t))\left(Y_{t}^{l},Y_{t}^{m},Y_{t}^{n}\right)+Df(X^{x}(t)).V_{t}^{l,m,n}\right]

\displaystyle\quad+\mathbb{E}\left[\frac{1}{2}\sum_{\sigma\in\mathcal{S}_{3}}D^{2}f(X^{x}(t))\left(Y_{t}^{\sigma(l)},Z_{t}^{\sigma(m),\sigma(n)}\right)\right].

Combining those with the above statement yields upper bounds:
\|D\phi(t,x).h\|\leq C\norm{h}
,
\|D^{2}\phi(t,x)\left(h,k\right)\|\leq C\norm{h}\norm{k},
and
\|D^{3}\phi(t,x)\left(l,m,n\right)\|\leq C\norm{l}\norm{m}\norm{n}
, that is,
the quantities
\|D^{i}\phi(t,x)\|
are bounded by suitable constants for all
t\in(0,1].

This yields Lemma
11
.
