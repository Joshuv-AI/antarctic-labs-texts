A family of integrable maps associated with the Volterra lattice
A.N.W. Hone, J.A.G. Roberts

Abstract
Recently Gubbiotti, Joshi, Tran and Viallet classified birational maps in four dimensions admitting two invariants (first integrals) with a particular degree structure, by considering recurrences of fourth order with a certain symmetry. The last three of the maps so obtained were shown to be Liouville integrable, in the sense of admitting a non-degenerate Poisson bracket with two first integrals in involution. Here we show how the first of these three Liouville integrable maps corresponds to genus 2 solutions of the infinite Volterra lattice, being the g = 2 g=2 case of a family of maps associated with the Stieltjes continued fraction expansion of a certain function on a hyperelliptic curve of genus g ⩾ 1 g\geqslant 1 . The continued fraction method provides explicit Hankel determinant formulae for tau functions of the solutions, together with an algebro-geometric description via a Lax representation for each member of the family, associating it with an algebraic completely integrable system. In particular, in the elliptic case ( g = 1 g=1 ), as a byproduct we obtain Hankel determinant expressions for the solutions of the Somos-5 recurrence, but different to those previously derived by Chang, Hu and Xin. By applying contraction to the Stieltjes fraction, we recover integrable maps associated with Jacobi continued fractions on hyperelliptic curves, that one of us considered previously, as well as the Miura-type transformation between the Volterra and Toda lattices.

Introduction

In classical mechanics, the study of integrable Hamiltonian systems, given by Hamiltonian vector fields with a
sufficient number of functionally independent first integrals in involution with respect to a Poisson bracket, has
a long history that goes back to the origins of calculus. It was further enriched in the latter half of the last
century by the discovery of the method of inverse scattering for solving certain Hamiltonian partial differential
equations, which gave new perspectives and new techniques for deriving finite-dimensional integrable systems
obtained as reductions of the latter. The case of discrete integrable systems, in the form of difference equations
or maps preserving a symplectic (or Poisson) structure and satisfying the conditions for a discrete analogue of
Liouville’s theorem, soon began to attract attention
[
7
,
39
,
63
]
, but it is fair to say that,
despite the fact that many examples are now known, the theory of discrete integrability is much less well
developed. For integrable birational maps in the plane, the archetypal example is provided by the QRT family of
maps
[
50
]
, whose level sets are biquadratic curves (generically, of genus one), are associated with elliptic fibrations
[
16
]
. If one imposes a requirement of subexponential degree
growth (zero algebraic entropy, in the terminology of
[
6
,
64
]
), then in two dimensions the only possibilities are
maps that preserve a pencil of genus one curves (like QRT), maps that preserve a pencil of rational curves, or
completely periodic maps
[
14
]
. An example of a quadratic map in the projective plane that preserves a pencil of cubic curves was studied in
[
45
]
.
This fits in with an observation of Veselov
[
63
]
, that for an
infinite order birational map of the plane with an algebraic invariant, the level curves can have genus at most one
(as a consequence of the Hurwitz theorem on the automorphism group of a Riemann surface).

Poisson maps in three dimensions with two first integrals, of which one is a Casimir, can be reduced to the
two-dimensional case by restricting to symplectic leaves, and the common level sets are curves, so in an
algebro-geometric setting this will typically lead to elliptic fibrations. Thus, in order to see new geometrical
features, with invariant tori of dimension greater than one, it is necessary to look to integrable maps in four
dimensions. Building on the work
[
34
]
and
[

]
, which was based on considering autonomous versions of
the fourth-order members of hierarchies of discrete Painlevé I/II equations from
[

]
, in

Gubbiotti et al. presented a classification of four-dimensional birational maps of recurrence type, that is

1.2
Outline of the paper

The purpose of this article is to give a complete description of the complex geometry of the solutions of the map defined by (
1.2
). In reaching this goal, we found that all of the structures we obtained could naturally be extended to analogous constructions
associated with a family of curves of arbitrary genus
g
(elliptic for
g=1
, hyperelliptic for

g\geqslant 2
).

Section 2 presents a set of empirical observations, numerical examples and standalone results about the (P.iv) map.
Originally, these were the specific clues that led us to uncover the geometrical structure
of the solutions of (
1.2
). To begin with,
we use a
p
-adic method to identify the singularity pattern of the solutions,
leading us to introduce a tau function
\tau_{n}
, which lifts (
1.2
) to a recurrence of order 7 with the Laurent property;
this is a Laurentification of the original map, in the sense of
[
24
]
. By considering a pattern of initial values that approaches a singularity, and substituting this set of initial data into the expressions
(
1.3
) and (
1.4
) on the level set
K_{j}=k_{j}
for
j=1,2
, in the limit that the singularity is reached we find a hyperelliptic curve of genus 2, isomorphic to the Weierstrass quintic

y^{2}=(1+\nu x+bx^{2})^{2}+4a(1+\nu x+bx^{2})x^{3}+4k_{1}x^{4}+4(k_{2}+\nu k_{1})x^{5}.

(1.8)

We also show that the tau function
\tau_{n}
satisfies a Somos-9 recurrence with coefficients that depend on
a,b,\nu
and the values of
K_{1},K_{2}
along each orbit of (
1.2

It turns out that both the singularity pattern, and the corresponding tau function substitution

1.2

which is associated with a family of elliptic curves; so this
was a strong initial hint that analogues of the (P.iv) map should exist for any genus

For enthusiasts of detective stories, the results in this section provide motivation and insight into how we made the first steps on the trail that led to the rest of the paper.
However, a reader who is not particularly fond of experimental mathematics can safely omit Section 2 on first reading, since the subsequent sections are not logically dependent on it, and are written in a more linear, deductive style.

Next, in Section 4, we introduce a family of compatible Poisson brackets for the map

2
Laurentification and tau functions for the map (P.iv)

In this section we exhibit certain phenomena displayed by the iterates of the map (P.iv), which are related to its discrete
integrability. Firstly, we describe the singularity pattern of the iterates, which is found from an empirical
p
-adic approach, and leads to the introduction of a
sequence of tau functions
\tau_{n}
for these iterates. On the one hand, these tau functions satisfy a homogeneous
recursion relation of order 7 with the Laurent property; so this is a Laurentification of (P.iv), as we state here and prove in Section
3.4
.
On the other hand, these tau functions are also shown to satisfy a Somos-9 relation, with invariants of (P.iv) as
coefficients. Secondly, by considering the limit where a solution of (P.iv) approaches
a singularity, we are led to a family of genus two curves which
will turn out to be at the core of the Stieltjes continued fractions (Section
3
) and the algebraic
integrability of (P.iv) (Section
4
).

The Laurent property is a very special feature of certain birational transformations, appearing in cluster algebras and their generalizations
[

Laurentification, that is, a lift to a higher-dimensional relation that does have the Laurent property
[

]

For some time,
singularity analysis has been used as a tool to detect integrability of maps (see
[

and references), and when the pattern of
places where the solutions have a zero or pole is sufficiently simple, this can further suggest an appropriate way to introduce tau functions and perform Laurentification
[

3
S-fractions on hyperelliptic curves and Volterra maps

In this section, for a fixed integer
g>0
, we introduce an affine space of triplets of polynomials, reminiscent of
the phase space of the Mumford system
[
43
,
60
]
and use Stieltjes continued fractions (S-fractions) to
construct a series of birational automorphisms of the affine space, indexed by
g
, which we describe in several
ways. When
g=1
we recover several known integrable maps, and for
g=2
we recover the map (P.iv), which was been the primary motivation for this study, while the maps for
g>2
appear to be new. We also give solutions in
terms of Hankel determinants of the iterates of these maps, i.e., of the corresponding recursion relations.

For a fixed
g>0
we consider the affine space

M_{g}:=\left\{({\cal P}(x),{\cal Q}(x),{\cal R}(x))\in{\mathbb{C}}[x]^{3}~\Bigl|~\begin{array}[]{ll}\deg{\cal P}(x)\leqslant g\;,&{\cal P}(0)=1\\
\deg{\cal Q}(x)\leqslant g\;,&{\cal Q}(0)=2\\
\deg{\cal R}(x)\leqslant g+1\;,&{\cal R}(0)=0\end{array}\right\}\;.

(3.1)

It is clear that
M_{g}
is an affine space of dimension
3g+1
: writing

{\cal P}(x)=1+\sum_{i=1}^{g}p_{i}x^{i}\;,\hskip 20.00003pt{\cal Q}(x)=2+\sum_{i=1}^{g}q_{i}x^{i}\;,\hskip 20.00003pt{\cal R}(x)=\sum_{i=1}^{g+1}r_{i}x^{i}\;,

(3.2)

the coefficients
p_{1},\dots,p_{g},q_{1},\dots,q_{g},r_{1},\dots,r_{g+1}
provide a natural system of linear coordinates
on
M_{g}
. We will often write an element
({\cal P},{\cal Q},{\cal R})
of
M_{g}
as a traceless
2\times 2
matrix

{\bf L}(x):=\left(\begin{array}[]{lr}{\cal P}(x)&{\cal R}(x)\\
{\cal Q}(x)&-{\cal P}(x)\end{array}\right)\;,

(3.3)

which will later serve as a Lax operator, and think of
M_{g}
as an affine space of matrices (Lax operators). It is
then natural to consider the map
\mu
defined by

\begin{array}[]{lcrcl}\mu&:&M_{g}&\to&{\mathbb{C}}[x]\\
&&{\bf L}(x)=\left(\begin{array}[]{lr}{\cal P}(x)&{\cal R}(x)\\
{\cal Q}(x)&-{\cal P}(x)\end{array}\right)&\mapsto&-\det{\bf L}(x)={\cal P}(x)^{2}+{\cal Q}(x){\cal R}(x)\;.\end{array}

(3.4)

In view of the degree constraints on the entries of
{\bf L}(x)
, the polynomial
-\det{\bf L}(x)
has degree at most

2g+1
and its constant term is 1; moreover, every such polynomial is contained in the image of
\mu
. In the
g=2

case these curves are precisely the ones encountered in Section
2
in the
singularity analysis of (P.iv), see (
1.8
), (
2.12
) and (
2.13
), which in
part motivates the choice of constraints on the polynomials
{\cal P},{\cal Q}
and
{\cal R}
.
(When
M_{g}
is endowed with a Poisson structure, as in Section
4
, then
\mu
can be viewed a momentum map.)

Throughout this section,
g>0
is fixed. In each of the following
subsections, the results and phenomena being discussed will be specialized and illustrated for the cases of
g=1
and

g=2
, when
M_{g}
has dimension 4 and 7, respectively.

3.1
Stieltjes continued fractions

We start from a hyperelliptic curve
\Gamma_{f}
, defined by an odd Weierstrass equation

\Gamma_{f}:y^{2}=f(x)\;,\hskip 10.00002pt\hbox{with}\hskip 10.00002ptf(x):=1+\sum_{j=1}^{2g+1}c_{j}x^{j}\in{\mathbb{C}}[x]\;.

(3.5)

When
f
has degree
2g+1
and has no multiple roots,
\Gamma_{f}
is non-singular and its genus is
g
, which
explains the notation used. Let
({\cal P},{\cal Q},{\cal R})
be any point in
\mu^{-1}(f)
, the fiber of
\mu
over
f
, so that

f(x)={\cal P}^{2}(x)+{\cal Q}(x){\cal R}(x)
, and the spectral curve
\Gamma_{f}
is
the characteristic equation
\det({\bf L}(x)-y\mathbf{1})=0
.
We consider on
\Gamma_{f}
the rational function, given by

F:=\frac{y+{\cal P}(x)}{{\cal Q}(x)}=\frac{{\cal R}(x)}{y-{\cal P}(x)}\;.

(3.6)

In preparation for constructing the Stieltjes continued fraction of
F
, we show in the following lemma how the
triplet
({\cal P},{\cal Q},{\cal R})
leads to another triplet
(\tilde{\cal P},\tilde{\cal Q},\tilde{\cal R})
in the same fiber of
\mu
,
under the assumption that
({\cal P},{\cal Q},{\cal R})
is
regular
, meaning that

\frac{2{\cal P}(x)-{\cal Q}(x)+{\cal R}(x)}{x}\big|_{x=0}=2p_{1}-q_{1}+r_{1}\neq 0\;.

(3.7)

Lemma 3.1
.

Given a regular triplet
({\cal P},{\cal Q},{\cal R})
in
\mu^{-1}(f)
, there exists a unique

w\in{\mathbb{C}}^{*}
and a unique triplet
(\tilde{\cal P},\tilde{\cal Q},\tilde{\cal R})
in
\mu^{-1}(f)
such that

\frac{y+{\cal P}(x)}{{\cal Q}(x)}=1-\frac{w\,x}{\frac{y+\tilde{\cal P}(x)}{\tilde{\cal Q}(x)}}\;.

(3.8)

The two triplets are related by

\tilde{\cal P}(x)={\cal Q}(x)-{\cal P}(x)\;,\hskip 10.00002pt\tilde{\cal Q}(x)=\frac{2{\cal P}(x)-{\cal Q}(x)+{\cal R}(x)}{-wx}\;,\hskip 10.00002pt\tilde{\cal R}(x)=-wx{\cal Q}(x)\;,

(3.9)

and

w=-\frac{2\,p_{1}-q_{1}+r_{1}}{2}=-\frac{\tilde{r}_{1}}{2}\;.

(3.10)

Proof.

We will constructively show that we can achieve (
3.8
) with
w\in{\mathbb{C}}^{*}
and

(\tilde{\cal P},\tilde{\cal Q},\tilde{\cal R})\in\mu^{-1}(f)
uniquely determined. Clearing the denominators in
(
3.8
) and using
y^{2}=f(x)
we get

y({\cal P}(x)+\tilde{\cal P}(x)-{\cal Q}(x))+f(x)+{\cal P}(x)\tilde{\cal P}(x)-\tilde{\cal P}(x){\cal Q}(x)+wx{\cal Q}(x)\tilde{\cal Q}(x)=0\;,

(3.11)

an equality which holds in the field of fractions of
{\mathbb{C}}[x,y]/(y^{2}-f(x))
, so that the coefficients of
y
and of

y^{0}
in (
3.11
) must be zero. The vanishing of the former coefficient gives the first equation in
(
3.9
) and guarantees
\tilde{\cal P}(0)=1
and
\deg\tilde{\cal P}\leqslant g
. The vanishing of the
y^{0}

coefficient gives

-w\,\tilde{\cal Q}(x)=\frac{f(x)+({\cal P}(x)-{\cal Q}(x))\tilde{\cal P}(x)}{x{\cal Q}(x)}=\frac{f(x)-({\cal P}(x)-{\cal Q}(x))^{2}}{x{\cal Q}(x)}=\frac{2{\cal P}(x)-{\cal Q}(x)+{\cal R}(x)}{x}\;,

(3.12)

where we have used in the last step that
f(x)={\cal P}^{2}(x)+{\cal Q}(x){\cal R}(x)
. Notice that in view of the values of the
constant terms in (
3.2
), the last numerator in (
3.12
) vanishes for
x=0
and is of degree at most

g+1
. Also, as the triplet
({\cal P},{\cal Q},{\cal R})
is assumed to be regular, the polynomial
(2{\cal P}-{\cal Q}+{\cal R})/x
does not
vanish at
x=0
, hence we can (uniquely) choose
w\in{\mathbb{C}}^{*}
such that
\tilde{\cal Q}(0)=2
. This gives the first
equality in (
3.10
) and the second equation in (
3.9
). The first equality in (
3.12
) also shows
that
f(x)-\tilde{\cal P}^{2}(x)
is divisible by
\tilde{\cal Q}(x)
, with quotient
-wx{\cal Q}(x)
; thus, if we take the third
equality in (
3.9
) to define
\tilde{\cal R}
, then
\deg\tilde{\cal R}=g+1
,
\tilde{\cal R}(0)=0
and

f(x)=\tilde{\cal P}^{2}(x)+\tilde{\cal Q}(x)\tilde{\cal R}(x)
, completing the proof that
(\tilde{\cal P},\tilde{\cal Q},\tilde{\cal R})
belongs
to
\mu^{-1}(f)
. Notice that the third equality in (
3.9
) implies the alternative formula for
w
in
(
3.10
), since
{\cal Q}(0)=2
.
∎

Applying the lemma to all regular points of the fiber
\mu^{-1}(f)
yields a rational map of the fiber to itself,
given by
({\cal P},{\cal Q},{\cal R})\mapsto(\tilde{\cal P},\tilde{\cal Q},\tilde{\cal R})
with
(\tilde{\cal P},\tilde{\cal Q},\tilde{\cal R})
given by
(
3.9
). Since the latter can also be solved rationally for
({\cal P},{\cal Q},{\cal R})
in terms of

(\tilde{\cal P},\tilde{\cal Q},\tilde{\cal R})
by using the second expression for
w
in (
3.10
), this rational map is
actually a birational automorphism of the fiber. Iterating this map starting from a triplet

({\cal P}_{0},{\cal Q}_{0},{\cal R}_{0})=({\cal P},{\cal Q},{\cal R})\in\mu^{-1}(f)
, we get an infinite sequence
({\cal P}_{n},{\cal Q}_{n},{\cal R}_{n})_{n\in{\mathbb{Z}}}
of triplets
as well as an infinite sequence
(w_{n})_{n\in{\mathbb{Z}}}
in
{\mathbb{C}}^{*}
, such that
({\cal P}_{n+1},{\cal Q}_{n+1},{\cal R}_{n+1})

and
w_{n+1}
are related to
({\cal P}_{n},{\cal Q}_{n},{\cal R}_{n})
as dictated by the lemma:

{\cal P}_{n+1}(x)={\cal Q}_{n}(x)-{\cal P}_{n}(x)\;,\hskip 10.00002pt{\cal Q}_{n+1}(x)=\frac{2{\cal P}_{n}(x)-{\cal Q}_{n}(x)+{\cal R}_{n}(x)}{-w_{n+1}x}\;,\hskip 10.00002pt{\cal R}_{n+1}(x)=-w_{n+1}x{\cal Q}_{n}(x)\;.

(3.13)

Writing, as in (
3.2
),
{\cal P}_{n}(x)=1+\sum_{i=1}^{g}p_{n,i}x^{i}
, and similarly for
{\cal Q}_{n}(x)
and
{\cal R}_{n}(x)
,
the value of
w_{n+1}
is given, according to (
3.10
), by

w_{n+1}=-\frac{2p_{n,1}-q_{n,1}+r_{n,1}}{2}=-\frac{r_{n+1,1}}{2}\;,

(3.14)

for all
n\in{\mathbb{Z}}
.

It is clear that
({\cal P}_{n},{\cal Q}_{n},{\cal R}_{n})
is obtained from
({\cal P}_{0},{\cal Q}_{0},{\cal R}_{0})
by repeating the map
n
times (or,
when
n<0
, repeating the inverse of the map
-n
times). As pointed out in the lemma, the starting triplet

({\cal P}_{0},{\cal Q}_{0},{\cal R}_{0})
must be regular in order for
({\cal P}_{1},{\cal Q}_{1},{\cal R}_{1})
and
w_{1}\in{\mathbb{C}}^{*}
to exist. But nothing
guarantees that
({\cal P}_{1},{\cal Q}_{1},{\cal R}_{1})
will also be regular, and in general it need not be so; assuming

({\cal P}_{1},{\cal Q}_{1},{\cal R}_{1})
to be a regular triplet puts an open linear condition on the coefficients of

({\cal P}_{1},{\cal Q}_{1},{\cal R}_{1})
, namely that
2p_{1,1}-q_{1,1}+r_{1,1}\neq 0
, which amounts to an open polynomial
condition on
({\cal P}_{0},{\cal Q}_{0},{\cal R}_{0})
, and for the existence of every extra term of the sequence such an extra
condition is to be added to the triplet
({\cal P}_{0},{\cal Q}_{0},{\cal R}_{0})
. However, since this amounts to a countable number of
open conditions on the latter, this means that when
({\cal P}_{0},{\cal Q}_{0},{\cal R}_{0})
is
generic
, in the sense that it
belongs to a residual subset of the fiber
\mu^{-1}(f)
, the sequence of triplets of polynomials

({\cal P}_{n},{\cal Q}_{n},{\cal R}_{n})\in\mu^{-1}(f)
and the sequence of constants
w_{n}\in{\mathbb{C}}^{*}
, both indexed by
n\in{\mathbb{Z}}
, exist.
For generic
({\cal P}_{0},{\cal Q}_{0},{\cal R}_{0})
, iterating (
3.8
) gives

F_{0}:=\frac{y+{\cal P}_{0}(x)}{{\cal Q}_{0}(x)}=1-\frac{w_{1}x}{\frac{y+{\cal P}_{1}(x)}{{\cal Q}_{1}(x)}}=\cdots=1-\cfrac{w_{1}x}{1-\cfrac{w_{2}x}{1-\cfrac{w_{3}x}{1-\cdots}}}\;,

(3.15)

yielding the
Stieltjes continued fraction
, also called
S
-fraction
, of
F_{0}
.
Similarly, each triple
({\cal P}_{n},{\cal Q}_{n},{\cal R}_{n})
,
n\in{\mathbb{Z}}
, is associated with a rational function
F_{n}
,
with a corresponding S-fraction obtained by shifting each of the indices in (
3.15
), which for
n>0
appears
on the
n
th line below the top.

3.2
Lax equation and invariants

In Section
3.1

we defined a birational automorphism of the fiber
\mu^{-1}(f)\subset M_{g}
,
where
f=f(x)
is any polynomial of degree at most
2g+1
, satisfying
f(0)=1
. This map, given by (
3.9
),
is not just defined on
\mu^{-1}(f)
, but is also as it stands a well-defined birational automorphism of
M_{g}
. In view
of its relation to the Volterra lattice (see Section
5
), we call it the
Volterra map
,
denoted
{\cal V}_{g}
; explicitly,

{\cal V}_{g}:\hskip 20.00003pt({\cal P},{\cal Q},{\cal R})\mapsto(\tilde{\cal P},\tilde{\cal Q},\tilde{\cal R}),

where the entries of the latter
are given by (
3.9
); also, we can write
{\cal V}_{g}({\cal P}_{n},{\cal Q}_{n},{\cal R}_{n})=({\cal P}_{n+1},{\cal Q}_{n+1},{\cal R}_{n+1})
for all
n\in{\mathbb{Z}}
.
For a fixed initial triplet
({\cal P}_{0},{\cal Q}_{0},{\cal R}_{0})
, the entire sequence of triplets
({\cal P}_{n},{\cal Q}_{n},{\cal R}_{n})_{n\in{\mathbb{Z}}}
in
M_{g}
is called
the
orbit
of
{\cal V}_{g}
through
({\cal P}_{0},{\cal Q}_{0},{\cal R}_{0})
. We also refer to a sequence of triplets that satisfies the recursion relations
(
3.13
) for all
n
as a
solution
.
The equations (
3.9
) for the Volterra map, as well as the recursion relations (
3.13
) for its
iterates, are easily rewritten as discrete Lax equations; this fact has many important consequences which will be
worked out in what follows.

Proposition 3.4
.

The Volterra map
{\cal V}_{g}
can be written in the compact form

{\cal V}_{g}:\hskip 20.00003pt{\bf L}(x){\bf M}(x)={\bf M}(x)\widetilde{\bf L}(x)\;,

(3.20)

where
{\bf L}(x)
is given by (
3.3
),
\widetilde{\bf L}(x)
is
{\bf L}(x)
with
{\cal P},\,{\cal Q},\,{\cal R}
replaced by

\tilde{\cal P},\,\tilde{\cal Q},\,\tilde{\cal R}
, and
{\bf M}(x):=\left(\begin{array}[]{cc}1&-wx\\
1&0\end{array}\right),

with
w
given by (
3.10
). As a consequence, the
2g+1
polynomial functions
H_{1},\dots,H_{2g+1}
on
M_{g}
,
defined by

{\cal P}(x)^{2}+{\cal Q}(x){\cal R}(x)=1+\sum_{i=1}^{2g+1}H_{i}x^{i}

(3.21)

are invariants of the Volterra map, i.e.
\tilde{H}_{i}=H_{i}
for
i=1,\dots,2g+1.

Proof.

It is easily checked by direct computation that (
3.9
) and (
3.20
) are the same set of
equations. Since the latter says that
\widetilde{\bf L}(x)
is obtained from
{\bf L}(x)
by conjugation with

{\bf M}(x)
, the spectrum of
{\bf L}(x)
is preserved, hence also all coefficients of the determinant of
{\bf L}(x)
,
i.e., the coefficients
H_{i}
of
\mu({\bf L}(x))={\cal P}(x)^{2}+{\cal Q}(x){\cal R}(x)
.
∎

Upon iterating the Volterra map, as discussed in Section
3.1
, starting from a generic triplet
{\bf L}_{0}(x)

of
M_{g}
we get a sequence of triplets

{\bf L}_{n}(x):=\left(\begin{array}[]{cr}{\cal P}_{n}(x)&{\cal R}_{n}(x)\\
{\cal Q}_{n}(x)&-{\cal P}_{n}(x)\end{array}\right)

of
M_{g}
. According to (
3.20
), a discrete Lax equation for this sequence is
given by

{\bf L}_{n}(x){\bf M}_{n}(x)={\bf M}_{n}(x){\bf L}_{n+1}(x)\;,

(3.22)

where

{\bf M}_{n}(x):=\left(\begin{array}[]{cc}1&-w_{n+1}x\\
1&0\end{array}\right)\;,\hskip 20.00003pt\hbox{with}\hskip 20.00003ptw_{n+1}=-\frac{2p_{n,1}-q_{n,1}+r_{n,1}}{2}=-\frac{r_{n+1,1}}{2}\;.

(3.23)

Example 3.5
.

When
g=1
, respectively when
g=2
, the invariants
H_{i}
can be computed from (
3.21
):

\begin{array}[]{rcl}H_{1}&=&2(p_{1}+r_{1})\;,\\
H_{2}&=&p_{1}^{2}+q_{1}r_{1}+2r_{2}\;,\\
H_{3}&=&q_{1}r_{2}\;,\end{array}\hskip 20.00003pt\hskip 20.00003pt\begin{array}[]{rcl}H_{1}&=&2(p_{1}+r_{1})\;,\\

(3.24)

The formulae on the left, which correspond to

, can be obtained from the first three formulae on the right by
setting
p_{2}=q_{2}=r_{3}=0
in them.

3.3
The Volterra map and its reductions

The invariants
H_{i}
can be used to reduce the
Volterra map to the submanifolds obtained by
fixing the values of some of these invariants. Here we will use this to express the Volterra map in terms of the
variables
w_{i}
which we introduced when constructing the S-fraction (
3.15
).

We start from the linear coordinates
p_{1},\dots,p_{g},q_{1},\dots,q_{g},r_{1},\dots,r_{g+1}
of
M_{g}
, which are identified with
p_{0,1},\dots,p_{0,g},q_{0,1},\dots,q_{0,g},r_{0,1},\dots,r_{0,g+1}
. The latter functions are used to
define recursively
p_{n,1},\dots,p_{n,g},

q_{n,1},\dots,q_{n,g},r_{n,1},\dots,r_{n,g+1}
, as well as
w_{n}
, for
all
n\in{\mathbb{Z}}
. Recall that this is done using (
3.13
) and (
3.14
).

In a first step, we will use a birational map to replace our linear coordinates for
M_{g}
by
p_{0,1},\dots,p_{0,g}

and some of its iterates
p_{n,1},\dots p_{n,g}
. To do this, we fix the value of the invariant
H_{1}=2(p_{1}+r_{1})
to
an arbitrary constant
c_{1}
. It means that we consider the hyperplane
H_{1}=c_{1}
of
M_{g}
, which denote by

M_{g}^{c_{1}}
. On it, we can take
p_{1},\dots,p_{g},q_{1},\dots,q_{g},r_{2},\dots,r_{g+1}
as linear coordinates (we left
out
r_{1}
). The invariance of
H_{1}
implies that
2(p_{n,1}+r_{n,1})=c_{1}
which, combined with
r_{n,1}=-2w_{n}

(see (
3.14
)), leads to

w_{n}=\frac{1}{2}\left(p_{n,1}-\frac{c_{1}}{2}\right)\;

(3.25)

for all
n\in{\mathbb{Z}}
. Using this and the first and last equations in (
3.13
), we can express the above variables
in terms of
p_{0,1},\dots,p_{0,g}
and their iterates:

q_{n,k}=p_{n+1,k}+p_{n,k}\;,\hskip 10.00002pt\hbox{and}\hskip 10.00002ptr_{n,k+1}=-w_{n}q_{n-1,k}=\frac{1}{2}\left(\frac{c_{1}}{2}-p_{n,1}\right)(p_{n,k}+p_{n-1,k})\;,

(3.26)

where
k=1,\dots,g
. Taking
n=0
we get

q_{0,k}=p_{1,k}+p_{0,k}\;,\hskip 10.00002pt\hbox{and}\hskip 10.00002ptr_{0,k+1}=\frac{1}{2}\left(\frac{c_{1}}{2}-p_{0,1}\right)(p_{0,k}+p_{-1,k})\;,

(3.27)

and we have expressed all coordinates of
M_{g}^{c_{1}}
in terms of the
3g
coefficients of the polynomials

{\cal P}_{-1},{\cal P}_{0}
and
{\cal P}_{1}
. It is clear that the (
3.27
) can be solved rationally for
p_{1,k}
and

p_{-1,k}
so that (
3.27
) defines a birational morphism from
M_{g}^{c_{1}}
to the space of triplets

({\cal P}_{-1},{\cal P}_{0},{\cal P}_{1})
. For later use, we also express the iterates of the Volterra map as a recursion relation
on the polynomials
{\cal P}_{n}
. To do this, we apply (
3.13
) several times to get

\displaystyle{\cal P}_{n+2}

\displaystyle={\cal Q}_{n+1}(x)-{\cal P}_{n+1}(x)=\frac{2{\cal P}_{n}(x)-{\cal Q}_{n}(x)+{\cal R}_{n}(x)}{-w_{n+1}x}-{\cal P}_{n+1}(x)

\displaystyle=-{\cal P}_{n+1}(x)+\frac{1}{w_{n+1}x}({\cal P}_{n+1}(x)-{\cal P}_{n}(x))+\frac{w_{n}}{w_{n+1}}({\cal P}_{n}(x)+{\cal P}_{n-1}(x))\;.

(3.28)

We will now go one step further and show how the above coordinates
p_{0,k},p_{1,k}
and
p_{-1,k}
can be
expressed birationally in terms of
p_{0,1}
and some of its iterates
p_{n,1}
. To do this, we do a further
reduction, namely we also fix the value of each one of the invariants
H_{2},\dots,H_{g}
to an arbitrary constant

c_{2},\dots,c_{g}
and consider the subvariety
\cap_{i=1}^{g}(H_{i}=c_{i})
of
M_{g}
, which we denote by
M_{g}^{c}
, so
c

stands now for
(c_{1},\dots,c_{g})
. Notice that this subvariety may be singular, but that does not affect the
reduction or the recursion relations. Using (
3.26
), we get the following formula for the invariants
H_{i}

in terms of

3.4
Hankel determinant solutions

The function
F_{0}
in (
3.15
) that defines the S-fraction admits a series expansion in
x
around
(0,1)\in\Gamma_{f}

that we shall use to give explicit solutions to the recurrence relation defined by the Volterra map. For a generic
point
({\cal P},{\cal Q},{\cal R})
in
\mu^{-1}(f)
, with
f
as in (
3.5
), we introduce new variables

s_{1},s_{2},\dots
by writing

1-\cfrac{w_{1}x}{1-\cfrac{w_{2}x}{1-\cfrac{w_{3}x}{1-\cdots}}}=1-\sum_{j=1}^{\infty}s_{j}x^{j}=1-S(x)\;,

(3.54)

where the latter equality defines the power series
S(x)
, which can be regarded as a generating function for the moments
s_{j}
.
The moments can be defined from the integral

s_{j}=\frac{1}{2\pi\mathrm{i}}\oint\frac{(1-F_{0})}{x^{j+1}}\,\mathrm{d}x,

for a sufficiently small contour around the point
(0,1)
on
\Gamma_{f}
, and this leads to a
linear functional (defined on polynomials in
x^{-1}
), and the connection with the classical theory of orthogonal polynomials
[
53
]
,
but we shall not pursue this further here.
In view of (
3.15
),
F_{0}=1-S(x)
in the sense that
the latter is a Taylor series expansion of the rational function
F_{0}
at
(0,1)
. It was shown by Stieltjes

[
55
]
that the variables
w_{i}
can be expressed as Hankel determinants of the
variables
s_{i}
. Precisely, he showed that

w_{n}=\frac{\Delta_{n-3}\Delta_{n}}{\Delta_{n-2}\Delta_{n-1}}\hskip 20.00003pt\mathrm{for}\,\,n\geqslant 1\;,

(3.55)

where
\Delta_{2k-1}=\det(s_{i+j-1})_{i,j=1,\ldots,k}
and
\Delta_{2k}=\det(s_{i+j})_{i,j=1,\ldots,k}
, for

k\geqslant 1
, that is,

\Delta_{2k-1}=\left|\begin{array}[]{cccc}s_{1}&s_{2}&\cdots&s_{k}\\
s_{2}&&\iddots&\vdots\\
\vdots&\iddots&&\vdots\\
s_{k}&\cdots&\cdots&s_{2k-1}\end{array}\right|\hskip 10.00002pt\hbox{and}\hskip 10.00002pt\Delta_{2k}=\left|\begin{array}[]{cccc}s_{2}&s_{3}&\cdots&s_{k+1}\\
s_{3}&&\iddots&\vdots\\
\vdots&\iddots&&\vdots\\
s_{k+1}&\cdots&\cdots&s_{2k}\end{array}\right|\;.

(3.56)

Also, by definition,
\Delta_{-2}=\Delta_{-1}=\Delta_{0}=1
. For example,
w_{1}=s_{1}
,
w_{2}=s_{2}/s_{1}
,

w_{3}=(s_{1}s_{3}-s_{2}^{2})/(s_{1}s_{2})
, and so on. It is clear from (
3.54
) that, conversely,
s_{i}
can be expressed
as a polynomial in
w_{1},\dots,w_{i}
, for example
s_{1}=w_{1}
,
s_{2}=w_{1}w_{2}
,
s_{3}=w_{1}w_{2}(w_{2}+w_{3})
, and so on.
(While these expressions can be found by expanding geometric series, a systematic method using continuants
is presented in Section
3.5
.)

Theorem 3.8
.

The terms
w_{n}
,
n\geqslant 1
, of the recurrence sequence defined by the Volterra map on
\mu^{-1}(f)
can be
written as

w_{n}=\frac{\Delta_{n-3}\Delta_{n}}{\Delta_{n-2}\Delta_{n-1}}\;,

where the entries of the Hankel matrices satisfy the recursion relation

s_{j}=\sum_{k=1}^{g}\big(p_{k}-q_{k}\big)s_{j-k}+\sum_{i=1}^{j-1}s_{i}s_{j-i}+\frac{1}{2}\sum_{k=1}^{g}q_{k}\sum_{i=1}^{j-k-1}s_{i}s_{j-k-i},\hskip 10.00002ptj\geqslant g+2\;.

(3.57)

The initial values
s_{1},s_{2},\ldots,s_{g+1}
and the coefficients for the recursion (
3.57
) are provided by a generic triple
({\cal P},{\cal Q},{\cal R})\in\mu^{-1}(f)
.

Proof.

In order to prove the recursion formula (
3.57
), we will derive a quadratic formula for
S(x)=1-F_{0}
,
introduced in (
3.54
). We use (
3.15
) to write

y=-{\cal P}(x)+F_{0}{\cal Q}(x)=-{\cal P}(x)+(1-S(x)){\cal Q}(x)\;,

3.5
The birational map
M_{g}\to{\mathbb{C}}^{3g+1}_{w}

Elaborating further on the S-fraction of

, we construct a birational map between
M_{g}

and
{\mathbb{C}}^{3g+1}_{w}
, where the latter stands for
{\mathbb{C}}^{3g+1}
equipped with
w_{1},\dots,w_{3g+1}
as affine coordinates.
We may call it the

unreduced
birational map, in view of the birational map
M_{g}^{c}\to{\mathbb{C}}^{2g+1}_{w}
which we obtained by reduction (fixing

). This unreduced map turns out to be less convenient for deriving
the Volterra map in the

coordinates, but it is nevertheless useful for obtaining abstract results
in these coordinates.

We start from the equation (
3.60
), which we can view as a linear relation for
{\cal P}(x),{\cal Q}(x)
and
{\cal R}(x)
.
It amounts to an infinite linear system of equations for the coefficients

p_{1},\dots,p_{g},q_{1},\dots,q_{g},r_{1},\dots,r_{g+1}
of these polynomials,
given in terms of the coefficients
s_{j}
of the power series

defined in (
3.54
).
We show that its solution can be expressed rationally in terms of

s_{1},\dots,s_{3g+1}
, and hence in terms of
w_{1},\dots,w_{3g+1}
, yielding the birational map. To do this, we
investigate the first
3g+1
equations only, namely the ones corresponding to the coefficient of
x^{j}
in (
3.60
).
For convenience, we set

\rho_{k}:=\sum_{i+j=k}s_{i}s_{j},

for
k\geqslant 2
, so that

S^{2}=\sum_{i+j=k}s_{i}s_{j}x^{k}=\sum_{k=2}^{\infty}\rho_{k}x^{k}
,
and then consider the terms of (
3.60
) at each order in
x
.
At leading order, the constant term cancels, and the coefficients of
x,x^{2},\dots,x^{g+1}
can be used to obtain
r_{1},\dots,r_{g+1}
in terms of
p_{1},\dots,p_{g},q_{1},\dots,q_{g},
and
s_{j}
for

, so it is sufficient to show that we can solve the coefficients of
x^{j}

{g+2}\leqslant j\leqslant{3g+1}
for

in terms of

s_{1},\dots,s_{3g+1}
. For each such

in (
3.60
) is given by (
3.57
),
which we can rewrite as

\sum_{i=1}^{g}s_{j-i}(p_{i}-q_{i})+\frac{1}{2}\sum_{i=1}^{g}\rho_{j-i}q_{i}=s_{j}-\rho_{j}\;,\hskip 20.00003pt\mathrm{for}\hskip 10.00002ptg+2\leqslant j\leqslant 3g+1\;.

We view (
3.68
) as a linear system in the
2g
variables

and

, with

\begin{pmatrix}s_{g+1}&\cdots&s_{2}&\rho_{g+1}&\cdots&\rho_{2}\\

\vdots\\

so that the matrix which governs the linear system has the following

\begin{pmatrix}T_{g}(s,g)&T_{g}(\rho,g)\\

\end{pmatrix}\hskip 10.00002pt\hbox{where}\hskip 10.00002ptT_{g}(\sigma,k):=\begin{pmatrix}\sigma_{k+1}&\sigma_{k}&\dots&\sigma_{k-g+2}\\

\rho_{i}=0
for all other values of

is the zero matrix and

is a
tridiagonal matrix, with all diagonal entries equal to
2
and all superdiagonal and subdiagonal entries equal to

\displaystyle=2\frac{s_{2}s_{4}-s_{3}^{2}-s_{2}^{3}}{s_{1}(2s_{2}^{2}-s_{1}s_{3})}=-2w_{2}\left(1+\frac{w_{3}(w_{1}-w_{4})}{w_{1}(w_{2}-w_{3})}\right)\;,

where we have used (
3.70
) to write the formulae in terms of
w_{1},\dots,w_{4}
. The first two equations
can now be solved for
r_{1}
and
r_{2}
, leading to the following expressions:

r_{1}=-2w_{3}\left(1+\frac{(w_{1}-w_{2})(w_{1}-w_{4})}{w_{1}(w_{2}-w_{3})}\right)\;,\hskip 20.00003ptr_{2}=2w_{1}w_{3}\left(1+\frac{w_{1}-w_{4}}{w_{2}-w_{3}}\right)\;.

Note also that in this case the unreduced version of the Volterra map is the birational map of
{\mathbb{C}}^{4}_{w}
defined by

\displaystyle(w_{1},w_{2},w_{3},w_{4})\mapsto(w_{2},w_{3},w_{4},w_{5})\;,

\displaystyle\frac{w_{5}w_{4}+w_{4}^{2}-w_{3}^{2}-w_{3}w_{2}}{w_{4}-w_{3}}+\frac{w_{1}w_{2}+w_{2}^{2}-w_{3}^{2}-w_{3}w_{4}}{w_{3}-w_{2}}=0\;,

(3.72)

which (after replacing each
w_{i}\to w_{i-2}
) is the relation obtained by eliminating
c_{1}
from (
3.34
).

For any
g
, we can describe an explicit algorithmic procedure for obtaining the exact expressions for the birational map between
s_{1},\ldots,s_{3g+1}

and
w_{1},\ldots,w_{3g+1}
. One way round, this is given by the Hankel determinant formula (
3.55
), but to describe the inverse map
more explicitly we must recall
how this relates to the
approximation problem originally considered by Stieltjes
[
55
]
,
as well as
other classical results on convergents of continued fractions in terms of continuants (for a concise review of the latter, see
[
17
]
).

The convergents of the S-fraction (
3.15
) are the sequence of rational functions of
x
obtained by truncating the continued fraction at some finite line
n
,
which approximate the series for
F_{0}=1-S
exactly up to and including the coefficient of
x^{n}
, that is

{\cal F}_{n}(x):=1-\cfrac{w_{1}x}{1-\cfrac{w_{2}x}{1-\cfrac{\cdots}{1-w_{n}x}}}=1-S(x)+\mathcal{O}(x^{n+1}),

(3.73)

Thus, for the first few convergents we have

{\cal F}_{0}=1,\hskip 10.00002pt{\cal F}_{1}=1-w_{1}x,\hskip 10.00002pt{\cal F}_{2}=\frac{1-(w_{1}+w_{2})x}{1-w_{2}x},\hskip 10.00002pt{\cal F}_{3}=\frac{1-(w_{1}+w_{2}+w_{3})x+w_{1}w_{3}x^{2}}{1-(w_{2}+w_{3})x},

and so on. By the usual correspondence between convergents and
2\times 2
matrices, we see that the
monodromy product over the conjugation matrices (
3.23
) appearing in the discrete Lax equation is given
by

{\bm{\Phi}}_{n+1}:={\bf M}_{1}(x){\bf M}_{2}(x)\cdots{\bf M}_{n+1}(x)=\left(\begin{array}[]{cc}{\cal K}_{n+1}(w_{1},\ldots,w_{n};x)&-w_{n}x\,{\cal K}_{n}(w_{1},\ldots,w_{n-1};x)\\
{\cal K}_{n}(w_{2},\ldots,w_{n};x)&-w_{n}x\,{\cal K}_{n-1}(w_{2},\ldots,w_{n-1};x)\end{array}\right),

(3.74)

with the
n
th convergent being the ratio of the entries in the first column, that is

{\cal F}_{n}(x)=\frac{{\cal K}_{n+1}(w_{1},\ldots,w_{n};x)}{{\cal K}_{n}(w_{2},\ldots,w_{n};x)},

where the polynomial
{\cal K}_{n}
is a continuant of size
n
, that is

{\cal K}_{n}(w_{1},\ldots,w_{n-1};x):=\left|\begin{array}[]{rrrrr}{1}&{-1}&{0}&{\dots}&{0}\\
{-w_{1}x}&{1}&{-1}&{\ddots}&{\vdots}\\
{0}&{-w_{2}x}&{1}&{\ddots}&{0}\\
{\vdots}&&{\ddots}&{\ddots}&{-1}\\
{0}&{\dots}&{0}&{-w_{n-1}x}&{1}\end{array}\right|.

From (
3.74
), the continuants are generated recursively from the linear relation
{\bm{\Phi}}_{n+1}={\bm{\Phi}}_{n}{\bf M}_{n+1}
,
starting from
{\bm{\Phi}}_{0}={\bm{1}}
(the identity matrix).
Hence, by replacing the series for
F_{0}=1-S
in (
3.73
) by its truncation at the
n
th term, then multiplying by
{\cal K}_{n}(w_{2},\ldots,w_{n};x)
on both sides,
we find the relation

\Big(1-\sum_{j=1}^{n}s_{j}x^{j}\Big){\cal K}_{n}(w_{2},\ldots,w_{n};x)={\cal K}_{n+1}(w_{1},\ldots,w_{n};x)+{\cal O}(x^{n+1}),

and comparing the coefficients of
x^{j}
for
j=1,\ldots,n
allows
s_{1},s_{2},\ldots,s_{n}
to be calculated recursively as polynomial expressions in
w_{1},\ldots,w_{n}
. For instance, when
n=4
the expressions (
3.70
) are obtained from the
numerator and denominator of

{\cal F}_{4}=\frac{1-(w_{1}+w_{2}+w_{3}+w_{4})x+(w_{1}w_{3}+w_{1}w_{4}+w_{2}w_{4})x^{2}}{1-(w_{2}+w_{3}+w_{4})x+w_{2}w_{4}x^{2}}.

Observe that these expressions are universal, in the sense that they depend only on the structure of the S-fraction, so that
each
s_{n}
is always given by the same polynomial in
w_{j}
for
1\leqslant j\leqslant n
, independent of
g
.

For
g\geqslant 2
, the corresponding formulae for the coefficients of
{\cal P}(x),{\cal Q}(x),{\cal R}(x)
in terms of

w_{1},\ldots,w_{3g+1}
become very complicated, and we do not have a compact way to write them.
However, we will not need the explicit form of these formulae in what follows, even when dealing with the
example of genus 2.

4
Discrete integrability

We show in this section that the map (P.iv) is a discrete a.c.i. system, in the sense of Definition

1.1
. To do this, we will first show that the affine space
M_{g}
(
g\geqslant 1
) of (
3.1
) which was constructed in
the previous section has the same integrability properties as the odd and even Mumford systems (see

[
43
,
60
]
): the invariants
H_{i}
of the Volterra map are in involution with respect to a large family
of compatible Poisson structures and their generic level sets are affine parts of Jacobi varieties. We then show
that the Volterra map is a Poisson map with respect to these Poisson structures and that it is a translation on the
latter complex tori. In Appendix A (Section
7
) we further show the precise relation between
the Mumford-like system, introduced here, and the even Mumford system.

4.1
Compatible Poisson structures for the Mumford-like system

We first introduce a
g+1
-dimensional family of compatible Poisson structures on
M_{g}
. The family is parametrized
by the
g+1
-dimensional vector space of polynomials
\phi\in{\mathbb{C}}[x]
of degree at most
g+1
, vanishing at
0
, so

\phi(0)=0
. For such a polynomial
\phi
, the corresponding Poisson structure is most naturally written by viewing

{\cal P},\,{\cal Q}
and
{\cal R}
as generating functions, and is defined by

\displaystyle\left\{{\cal P}(x),{\cal P}(y)\right\}^{\phi}

\displaystyle=\left\{{\cal Q}(x),{\cal Q}(y)\right\}^{\phi}=0\;,\ \left\{{\cal R}(x),{\cal R}(y)\right\}^{\phi}=\phi(y){\cal R}(x)-\phi(x){\cal R}(y)\;,

\displaystyle\left\{{\cal P}(x),{\cal Q}(y)\right\}^{\phi}

\displaystyle=\frac{\phi(x)y{\cal Q}(y)-\phi(y)x{\cal Q}(x)}{x-y}\;,\ \left\{{\cal P}(x),{\cal R}(y)\right\}^{\phi}=-y\frac{\phi(x){\cal R}(y)-\phi(y){\cal R}(x)}{x-y}\;,

(4.1)

\displaystyle\left\{{\cal Q}(x),{\cal R}(y)\right\}^{\phi}

\displaystyle=2y\frac{\phi(x){\cal P}(y)-\phi(y){\cal P}(x)}{x-y}-\phi(y){\cal Q}(x)\;.

Of course, one needs to verify that the above definition is coherent, in the sense that the right-hand side of each of these
formulae is indeed a polynomial in
x
and
y
, and also that the right-hand side does not contain any monomials

x^{i}y^{j}
which are absent in the left-hand side. For example,
\left\{{\cal P}(x),{\cal Q}(y)\right\}^{\phi}
is a polynomial in
x,y

with only non-zero coefficients of
x^{i}y^{j}
when
1\leqslant i,j\leqslant g
, while
\phi(x)y{\cal Q}(y)-\phi(y)x{\cal Q}(x)

is clearly divisible by
x-y
and the quotient has only non-zero coefficients of
x^{i}y^{j}
in the same range. The
same argument applies to the other formulae. Moreover, we need to verify that
\left\{\cdot\,,\cdot\right\}^{\phi}
is a Poisson bracket,
i.e., that it satisfies the Jacobi identity. This follows easily from the formulae (
4.1
). Let
us show for example that

\left\{{\left\{{\cal Q}(x),{\cal Q}(y)\right\}^{\phi}},{\cal R}(z)\right\}^{\phi}+\left\{\left\{{\cal Q}(y),{\cal R}(z)\right\}^{\phi},{\cal Q}(x)\right\}^{\phi}+\left\{\left\{{\cal R}(z),{\cal Q}(x)\right\}^{\phi},{\cal Q}(y)\right\}^{\phi}=0\;.

(4.2)

The first term in (
4.2
) is zero since
\left\{{\cal Q}(x),{\cal Q}(y)\right\}^{\phi}=0
. Also, by direct computation
using (
4.1
),

\displaystyle\left\{\left\{{\cal Q}(y),{\cal R}(z)\right\}^{\phi},{\cal Q}(x)\right\}^{\phi}

\displaystyle=\left\{2z\frac{\phi(y){\cal P}(z)-\phi(z){\cal P}(y)}{y-z}-\bcancel{\phi(z){\cal Q}(y)},{\cal Q}(x)\right\}^{\phi}

\displaystyle=\frac{2z\phi(y)}{y-z}\frac{\phi(z)x{\cal Q}(x)-{\phi(x)z{\cal Q}(z)}}{z-x}-\frac{2z\phi(z)}{y-z}\frac{\phi(y)x{\cal Q}(x)-\phi(x)y{\cal Q}(y)}{y-x}

\displaystyle=-\frac{2xz\phi(y)\phi(z)}{(z-x)(x-y)}{\cal Q}(x)-\frac{2yz\phi(x)\phi(z)}{(y-z)(x-y)}{\cal Q}(y)-\frac{2z^{2}\phi(x)\phi(y)}{(y-z)(z-x)}{\cal Q}(z)\;.

By skew-symmetry,
\left\{\left\{{\cal R}(z),{\cal Q}(x)\right\}^{\phi},{\cal Q}(y)\right\}^{\phi}=-\left\{\left\{{\cal Q}(x),{\cal R}(z)\right\}^{\phi},{\cal Q}(y)\right\}^{\phi}
, so that

\left\{\left\{{\cal R}(z),{\cal Q}(x)\right\}^{\phi},{\cal Q}(y)\right\}^{\phi}=\frac{2yz\phi(x)\phi(z)}{(y-z)(x-y)}{\cal Q}(y)+\frac{2xz\phi(y)\phi(z)}{(z-x)(x-y)}{\cal Q}(x)+\frac{2z^{2}\phi(x)\phi(y)}{(y-z)(z-x)}{\cal Q}(z)\;.

Summing up, we get indeed zero, which shows (
4.2
).

Notice that since
\left\{\cdot\,,\cdot\right\}^{\phi}
is a Poisson bracket for any
\phi\in{\mathbb{C}}[x]
with
\deg\phi\leqslant g+1
and

\phi(0)=0
, the Poisson brackets
\left\{\cdot\,,\cdot\right\}^{\phi}
are compatible, simply because
\left\{\cdot\,,\cdot\right\}^{\phi}+\left\{\cdot\,,\cdot\right\}^{\phi^{\prime}}=\left\{\cdot\,,\cdot\right\}^{\phi+\phi^{\prime}}
for any such polynomials
\phi,\phi^{\prime}
.

We show in the next proposition that the Volterra map
{\cal V}_{g}
is a Poisson map with respect to each one of these
Poisson brackets. Recall from (
3.9
) and (
3.10
) that
{\cal V}_{g}
is given by

\tilde{\cal P}(x)={\cal Q}(x)-{\cal P}(x)\;,\hskip 20.00003pt\tilde{\cal Q}(x)=\frac{2{\cal P}(x)-{\cal Q}(x)+{\cal R}(x)}{-wx}\;,\hskip 20.00003pt\tilde{\cal R}(x)=-wx{\cal Q}(x)\;,

(4.3)

where
w=-\frac{2\,p_{1}-q_{1}+r_{1}}{2}
.

Proposition 4.1
.

For any
\phi=\sum_{i=1}^{g+1}\phi_{i}x^{i}
, the Volterra map
{\cal V}_{g}:M_{g}\to M_{g}
is a (birational) Poisson map with
respect to
\left\{\cdot\,,\cdot\right\}^{\phi}
.

Proof.

We need to check that
{\cal V}_{g}
preserves the Poisson bracket
\left\{\cdot\,,\cdot\right\}^{\phi}
. In formulae, this means that

\left\{\tilde{\cal S}(x),\tilde{\cal T}(y)\right\}^{\phi}=\widetilde{\left\{{\cal S}(x),{\cal T}(y)\right\}^{\phi}}
, where
{\cal S}
and
{\cal T}
stand for any of
the polynomials
{\cal P},{\cal Q},{\cal R}
. To do this, it helps to first use (
4.1
) to derive the
following formulae for the Poisson brackets of
w
with the polynomials
{\cal P},{\cal Q},{\cal R}
:

\left\{w,{\cal P}(y)\right\}^{\phi}=\left\{w,{\cal Q}(y)\right\}^{\phi}=\frac{\phi_{1}}{2}{\cal Q}(y)-\frac{\phi(y)}{y}\;,\hskip 10.00002pt\left\{w,{\cal R}(y)\right\}^{\phi}=\frac{\phi(y)}{y}-w\phi(y)-\frac{\phi_{1}}{2}(2{\cal P}(y)+{\cal R}(y))\;.

(4.4)

To derive these formulae from (
4.1
), it suffices to use that
-2w
is the linear term of

2{\cal P}(x)-{\cal Q}(x)+{\cal R}(x)
. For example,

\displaystyle\left\{-2w,{\cal R}(y)\right\}^{\phi}

\displaystyle=\lim_{x\to 0}\frac{1}{x}\left\{2{\cal P}(x)-{\cal Q}(x)+{\cal R}(x),{\cal R}(y)\right\}^{\phi}

\displaystyle=\lim_{x\to 0}\frac{1}{x}\left[-2y\frac{\phi(x){\cal R}(y)-\phi(y){\cal R}(x)}{x-y}-2y\frac{\phi(x){\cal P}(y)-\phi(y){\cal P}(x)}{x-y}+\phi(y)({\cal Q}(x)+{\cal R}(x))-\phi(x){\cal R}(y)\right]

\displaystyle=\phi_{1}{\cal R}(y)-\phi(y)r_{1}+2\phi_{1}{\cal P}(y)+\phi(y)\lim_{x\to 0}\frac{2y{\cal P}(x)+(x-y){\cal Q}(x)}{x(x-y)}

\displaystyle=\phi_{1}{\cal R}(y)-\phi(y)r_{1}+2\phi_{1}{\cal P}(y)-2\frac{\phi(y)}{y}-(2p_{1}-q_{1})\phi(y)

\displaystyle={\phi_{1}}(2{\cal P}(y)+{\cal R}(y))-2\frac{\phi(y)}{y}+2w\phi(y)\;,

which proves the last formula in (
4.4
). It is now easy to prove that the Volterra map is a
Poisson map. For example,
it is clear from (
4.4
) that
\left\{{\cal P}(x)-{\cal Q}(x),w\right\}^{\phi}=0
, and so

\displaystyle\left\{\tilde{\cal P}(x),\tilde{\cal R}(y)\right\}^{\phi}

\displaystyle=\left\{{\cal Q}(x)-{\cal P}(x),-wy{\cal Q}(y)\right\}^{\phi}=wy\left\{{\cal P}(x),{\cal Q}(y)\right\}^{\phi}=wy\frac{\phi(x)y{\cal Q}(y)-\phi(y)x{\cal Q}(x)}{x-y}

\displaystyle=-y\frac{\phi(x)\tilde{\cal R}(y)-\phi(y)\tilde{\cal R}(x)}{x-y}=\widetilde{\left\{{\cal P}(x),{\cal R}(y)\right\}^{\phi}}\;.

The other verifications are done in the same way. This shows that the Volterra map is a Poisson map.
∎

We show in the next proposition that the invariants of the Volterra map, as introduced in the previous section, are in
involution with respect to any of the Poisson brackets
\left\{\cdot\,,\cdot\right\}^{\phi}
, where we recall that
\deg\phi\leqslant g+1
and

\phi(0)=0
.

Proposition 4.2
.

The
2g+1
invariants
H_{1},\dots,H_{2g+1}
of the Volterra map, defined by

{\cal P}(x)^{2}+{\cal Q}(x){\cal R}(x)=1+\sum_{i=1}^{2g+1}H_{i}x^{i}\;.

(4.5)

are in involution with respect to
\left\{\cdot\,,\cdot\right\}^{\phi}
.

Proof.

We first compute from (
4.1
)

\displaystyle\left\{{\cal P}(x),{\cal P}(y)^{2}+{\cal Q}(y){\cal R}(y)\right\}^{\phi}

\displaystyle={\cal Q}(y)\left\{{\cal P}(x),{\cal R}(y)\right\}^{\phi}+{\cal R}(y)\left\{{\cal P}(x),{\cal Q}(y)\right\}^{\phi}

\displaystyle=-y{\cal Q}(y)\frac{\cancel{\phi(x){\cal R}(y)}-\phi(y){\cal R}(x)}{x-y}+{\cal R}(y)\frac{\cancel{y\phi(x){\cal Q}(y)}-x\phi(y){\cal Q}(x)}{x-y}

\displaystyle=\phi(y)\frac{y{\cal Q}(y){\cal R}(x)-x{\cal Q}(x){\cal R}(y)}{x-y}\;,

(4.6)

and similarly

\displaystyle\left\{{\cal Q}(x),{\cal P}(y)^{2}+{\cal Q}(y){\cal R}(y)\right\}^{\phi}

\displaystyle=2\phi(y)\frac{x{\cal P}(y){\cal Q}(x)-y{\cal P}(x){\cal Q}(y)}{x-y}-\phi(y){\cal Q}(x){\cal Q}(y)\;,

(4.7)

\displaystyle\left\{{\cal R}(x),{\cal P}(y)^{2}+{\cal Q}(y){\cal R}(y)\right\}^{\phi}

\displaystyle=2x\phi(y)\frac{{\cal P}(x){\cal R}(y)-{\cal P}(y){\cal R}(x)}{x-y}+\phi(y){\cal Q}(y){\cal R}(x)\;.

(4.8)

These formulae imply

\displaystyle\left\{{\cal P}^{2}(x)+{\cal Q}(x){\cal R}(x),{\cal P}^{2}(y)+{\cal Q}(y){\cal R}(y)\right\}^{\phi}

\displaystyle=2{\cal P}(x)\left\{{\cal P}(x),{\cal P}^{2}(y)+{\cal Q}(y){\cal R}(y)\right\}^{\phi}+{\cal Q}(x)\left\{{\cal R}(x),{\cal P}^{2}(y)+{\cal Q}(y){\cal R}(y)\right\}^{\phi}+{\cal R}(x)\left\{{\cal Q}(x),{\cal P}^{2}(y)+{\cal Q}(y){\cal R}(y)\right\}^{\phi}

\displaystyle=2{\cal P}(x)\phi(y)\frac{\cancel{y{\cal Q}(y){\cal R}(x)}-\bcancel{x{\cal Q}(x){\cal R}(y)}}{x-y}+{\cal Q}(x)2x\phi(y)\frac{\bcancel{{\cal P}(x){\cal R}(y)}-\xcancel{{\cal P}(y){\cal R}(x)}}{x-y}+{\cal Q}(x)\phi(y){\cal Q}(y){\cal R}(x)

\displaystyle\hskip 20.00003pt+{\cal R}(x)2\phi(y)\frac{\xcancel{x{\cal P}(y){\cal Q}(x)}-\cancel{y{\cal P}(x){\cal Q}(y)}}{x-y}-{\cal R}(x)\phi(y){\cal Q}(x){\cal Q}(y)=0\;,

so that the invariants
H_{i}
are in involution,
\left\{H_{i},H_{j}\right\}^{\phi}=0
for
1\leqslant i,j\leqslant 2g+1
.
∎

As before, we often fix the Hamiltonians
H_{1},\dots,H_{g}
to generic values so that we actually work on a
(non-singular) subvariety of
M_{g}^{c}
, which is birational with
{\mathbb{C}}^{2g+1}
. We show in the following proposition
that
M_{g}^{c}
is a bi-Hamiltonian manifold, i.e., that it is equipped with a pencil of compatible Poisson
structures.

Proposition 4.3
.

The Hamiltonians
H_{1},\dots,H_{g}
are Casimirs of the Poisson structure
\left\{\cdot\,,\cdot\right\}^{\phi}
if and only if
\phi
is of
the form
\phi=\phi_{g}x^{g}+\phi_{g+1}x^{g+1}
. In this case,

C_{\phi}:=\sum_{i=0}^{g}(-1)^{i}\phi_{g}^{g-i}\phi_{g+1}^{i}H_{2g+1-i}

(4.9)

is also a Casimir function of
\left\{\cdot\,,\cdot\right\}^{\phi}
.

Proof.

Consider for
i=1,\dots,g
the Hamiltonian vector field
\mathrm{X}_{H_{i}}
, which is given as the coefficient of
y^{i}
in

\left\{\cdot\,,{\cal P}(y)^{2}+{\cal Q}(y){\cal R}(y)\right\}^{\phi}
, which we computed in (
4.1
) – (
4.8
). These Hamiltonians are
Casimir functions for
\left\{\cdot\,,\cdot\right\}^{\phi}
if and only if their Hamiltonian vector fields are zero, which is in turn
equivalent to the fact that the right hand sides in (
4.1
) – (
4.8
) are divisible by

y^{g+1}
. Notice that the latter right hand sides, without the factor
\phi(y)
, are divisible by
y
since

{\cal P}(0)=1,

{\cal Q}(0)=2
and
{\cal R}(0)=0
, without being divisible by
y^{2}
. Therefore, this is equivalent to
\phi(y)

being divisible by
y^{g}
, i.e., that
\phi
is of the form
\phi=\phi_{g}x^{g}+\phi_{g+1}x^{g+1}
; since

\deg\phi\leqslant g+1
, it follows that the Poisson structures which make
M_{g}^{c}
into a bi-Hamiltonian manifold
are the restrictions to
M_{g}^{c}
of the Poisson pencil
\left\{\cdot\,,\cdot\right\}^{\phi}
with
\phi(x)=\phi_{g}x^{g}+\phi_{g+1}x^{g+1}
. It is
easily shown by direct computation that
C_{\phi}
, given by (
4.9
) is a Casimir of the Poisson
pencil. For example,

\displaystyle\left\{{\cal P}(x),\sum_{i=0}^{g}(-1)^{i}\phi_{g}^{g-i}\phi_{g+1}^{i}H_{2g+1-i}\right\}^{\phi}

\displaystyle=\mathop{\textrm{Res}}\limits_{y=0}\frac{1}{y^{2g+2}}\left\{{\cal P}(x),H_{2g+1-i}y^{2g+1}\right\}^{\phi}\sum_{i=0}^{g}(-1)^{i}\phi_{g}^{g-i}\phi_{g+1}^{i}

\displaystyle=\mathop{\textrm{Res}}\limits_{y=0}\frac{1}{y^{2g+2}}\left\{{\cal P}(x),{\cal P}(y)^{2}+{\cal Q}(y){\cal R}(y)\right\}^{\phi}\sum_{i=0}^{g}(-1)^{i}\phi_{g}^{g-i}\phi_{g+1}^{i}y^{i}

\displaystyle=\mathop{\textrm{Res}}\limits_{y=0}\frac{1}{y^{2g+2}}\left[\frac{\phi(x)y{\cal Q}(y)-\phi(y)x{\cal Q}(x)}{x-y}{\cal R}(y)-y{\cal Q}(y)\frac{\phi(x){\cal R}(y)-\phi(y){\cal R}(x)}{x-y}\right]\sum_{i=0}^{g}(-1)^{i}\phi_{g}^{g-i}\phi_{g+1}^{i}y^{i}

\displaystyle=\mathop{\textrm{Res}}\limits_{y=0}\frac{1}{y^{2g+2}}\;\frac{y{\cal Q}(y){\cal R}(x)-x{\cal Q}(x){\cal R}(y)}{x-y}\sum_{i=0}^{g}(-1)^{i}\phi_{g}^{g-i}\phi_{g+1}^{i}y^{i}\phi(y)

\displaystyle=\mathop{\textrm{Res}}\limits_{y=0}\frac{1}{y^{2g+2}}\;\frac{y{\cal Q}(y){\cal R}(x)-x{\cal Q}(x){\cal R}(y)}{x-y}\left(\phi_{g}^{g+1}y^{g}+(-1)^{g}\phi_{g+1}^{g+1}y^{2g+1}\right)

\displaystyle=\phi_{g}^{g+1}\mathop{\textrm{Res}}\limits_{y=0}\frac{1}{y^{g+2}}\;\frac{y{\cal Q}(y){\cal R}(x)-x{\cal Q}(x){\cal R}(y)}{x-y}=0\;,

where we have used in the last two equalities respectively that
{\cal R}(y)
is divisible by
y
and that the
polynomial
\frac{y{\cal Q}(y){\cal R}(x)-x{\cal Q}(x){\cal R}(y)}{x-y}
has degree at most
g
in
y
.
∎

Example 4.4

4.2
The generic fibers of the momentum map

We will now give an algebro-geometric description of the generic fibers of the momentum map
\mu:M_{g}\to{\mathbb{C}}[x]
,
which we recall is given by
\mu({\cal P},{\cal Q},{\cal R})={\cal P}^{2}+{\cal Q}{\cal R}
. Precisely, we will describe the fiber over any
polynomial

of degree
2g+1
, without multiple roots, and satisfying
f(0)=1
. Notice that such
polynomials are exactly those
f
in the image of
\mu
for which

y^{2}=f(x)
defines a non-singular affine curve

\Gamma_{f}

of genus
g
; we use
\bar{\Gamma}_{f}
to denote the completion of the latter,
which can be thought of as a compact Riemann surface.

Proposition 4.6
.

Let
f
be polynomial of degree
2g+1
, without multiple roots, such that
f(0)=1
. Denote by
\Gamma_{f}
the
non-singular affine curve of genus
g
, defined by
y^{2}=f(x)
, with

\bar{\Gamma}_{f}
being its completion.
Then
\mu^{-1}(f)
is
isomorphic to an affine part of the Jacobian variety of
\bar{\Gamma}_{f}
minus three translates of the theta
divisor,

\mu^{-1}(f)\cong\Jac(\bar{\Gamma}_{f})\setminus(\Theta\cup\Theta_{+}\cup\Theta_{-})\;.

(4.13)

The
(-1)
-involution on
\Jac(\bar{\Gamma}_{f})
leaves
\Theta
invariant and permutes
\Theta_{+}
and

\Theta_{-}
.

Proof.

Let
({\cal P},{\cal Q},{\cal R})\in\mu^{-1}(f)

{\cal P}^{2}(x)+{\cal Q}(x){\cal R}(x)=f(x)\;,\hskip 10.00002pt\deg{\cal P},\deg{\cal Q},\deg{\cal R}-1\leqslant g\;,\hskip 10.00002pt({\cal P}(0),{\cal Q}(0),{\cal R}(0))=(1,2,0)\;.

(4.14)

Since

has degree
2g+1
,
\deg{\cal Q}=g
and
\deg{\cal R}=g+1
; it also implies that
\bar{\Gamma}_{f}
is obtained from

\Gamma_{f}
by adding a single point, which we denote by
\infty

\bar{\Gamma}_{f}
is
denoted by
\imath
; it fixes
\infty
and sends
(x,y)\in\Gamma_{f}
to
(x,-y)
. To
({\cal P},{\cal Q},{\cal R})
we associate a
divisor
\sum_{i=1}^{g}(x_{i},y_{i})-g\infty
on

as follows:
x_{1},x_{2},\dots,x_{g}
are the roots of
{\cal Q}(x)

and
y_{i}:={\cal P}(x_{i})
for

. It is indeed a divisor on

since for

y_{j}^{2}-f(x_{j})={\cal P}^{2}(x_{j})-({\cal P}^{2}(x_{j})+{\cal Q}(x_{j}){\cal R}(x_{j}))=0\;.

Of course,
(x_{i},y_{i})\neq\infty
for all

. Notice that when
{\cal Q}(x)
has multiple roots, say
x_{1}=\dots=x_{k}
,
then
y_{1}=y_{2}=\dots=y_{k}
. We show by contradiction that if
x_{i}
is a root of
{\cal Q}(x)
and
y_{i}=0
(so that
x_{i}

is also a root of
{\cal P}(x)
), then
x_{i}
is a simple root of
{\cal Q}(x)
. Indeed, if
x_{i}
is a multiple root of

{\cal Q}(x)
and is also a root of
{\cal P}(x)
, then
x_{i}
is a multiple root of

, so that

is not square-free, contrary to the assumptions. The upshot is that the obtained divisors are of the form

\sum_{i=1}^{g}P_{i}-g\infty

P_{i}\neq\imath(P_{j})
when

. It is

these divisors are equivalent to a divisor of the form
\sum_{i=1}^{g-1}Q_{i}-(g-1)\infty
, with
Q_{i}\in\bar{\Gamma}_{f}

for
i=1,\dots,g-1
. Since
\Jac(\bar{\Gamma}_{f})
is the group of degree zero divisors on
\bar{\Gamma}_{f}
, modulo

\mu^{-1}(f)\to\Jac(\bar{\Gamma}_{f})
, which associates to

({\cal P},{\cal Q},{\cal R})
the divisor class
\left[\sum_{i=1}^{g}P_{i}-g\infty\right]
, is injective.
This map is of course not surjective. In order to determine the image, let

\displaystyle\Theta

\displaystyle:=\left\{\left[\sum_{i=1}^{g-1}P_{i}-(g-1)\infty\right]\mid\forall i\ P_{i}\in\bar{\Gamma}_{f}\right\}\;,

\displaystyle\Theta_{+}

\displaystyle:=\left\{\left[(0,1)+\sum_{i=1}^{g-1}P_{i}-g\infty\right]\mid\forall i\ P_{i}\in\bar{\Gamma}_{f}\right\}=\Theta+[(0,1)-\infty]\;,

\displaystyle\Theta_{-}

\displaystyle:=\left\{\left[(0,-1)+\sum_{i=1}^{g-1}P_{i}-g\infty\right]\mid\forall i\ P_{i}\in\bar{\Gamma}_{f}\right\}=\Theta+[(0,-1)-\infty]\;.

The first one is the theta divisor and the other two are translates of it. Notice that
\imath(\Theta)=\Theta
and

\imath(\Theta_{+})=\Theta_{-}
, since
\imath(\infty)=\infty
. As we already said, the image contains no point of the

\left[\sum_{i=1}^{g-1}P_{i}-(g-1)\infty\right]

\Theta
. Since
P_{i}=(x_{i},y_{i})
where

x_{i}
is a root of
{\cal Q}(x)
and since
{\cal Q}(0)=2

, so that every

is
different from
(0,1)
and from
(0,-1)
; hence the image is also disjoint from
\Theta_{+}
and

\Theta_{-}
. Take now any point in
\Jac(\bar{\Gamma}_{f})\setminus(\Theta\cup\Theta_{+}\cup\Theta_{-})
. It can as above
be written uniquely as
\left[\sum_{i=1}^{g}P_{i}-g\infty\right]
with
P_{i}\notin\{\infty,(1,0),(-1,0)\}
and

P_{i}\neq\imath(P_{j})
for all
i\neq j
. When all
P_{i}=(x_{i},y_{i})
are different, there is a unique polynomial

{\cal Q}(x)
whose roots are the
x_{i}
and with
{\cal Q}(0)=2
, and there is a unique polynomial
{\cal P}(x)
of degree
g
,
with
{\cal P}(x_{i})=y_{i}
for
i=1,\dots,g
and
{\cal P}(0)=1

(x_{0},y_{0})=(0,1)

{\cal Q}(x)=2\prod_{i=1}^{g}\left(1-\frac{x}{x_{i}}\right)\;,\hskip 20.00003pt{\cal P}(x)=\sum_{i=0}^{g}y_{i}\prod_{j\neq i}\frac{x-x_{j}}{x_{i}-x_{j}}\;.

(4.15)

This also works in the limiting case when some of the
P_{i}
are the same upon adding in the definition of

a tangency condition (see
[

), which assures that

{\cal Q}(x)

of degree

\mu({\cal P},{\cal Q},{\cal R})=\left[\sum_{i=1}^{g}P_{i}-g\infty\right]

4.3
Discrete Liouville and algebraic integrability

We are now ready to show that the Volterra map
{\cal V}_{g}
is Liouville integrable on
(M_{g},\left\{\cdot\,,\cdot\right\}^{\phi})
when

\phi\neq 0
. Recall that a birational map
R
on an algebraic Poisson manifold
(M,\left\{\cdot\,,\cdot\right\})
of dimension
n
and
(Poisson) rank
2r
is said to be
Liouville integrable
when the following conditions are satisfied:

(1)

R
is a Poisson map;

(2)

R
has
n-r
functionally independent invariants in involution.

In the case at hand,
M=M_{g}
so that
n=3g+1
, and
R={\cal V}_{g}
, which we already know to be a Poisson map
(Proposition
4.1
). Also, we already have
2g+1
invariants in involution
(Proposition
4.2
). So it will be sufficient to show, in the proof which follows, that the
rank of
\left\{\cdot\,,\cdot\right\}^{\phi}
is
2g
and that the invariants
H_{1},\dots,H_{2g+1}
are functionally independent.

Proposition 4.9
.

Let
\phi\in{\mathbb{C}}[x]
be any non-zero polynomial of degree at most
g+1
, vanishing at
0
. Then the Volterra map

{\cal V}_{g}
is Liouville integrable on
(M_{g},\left\{\cdot\,,\cdot\right\}^{\phi})
.

Proof.

We first show that the components of
\mu
, which are the
2g+1
polynomial functions
H_{i}
, defined by

\mu({\cal P},{\cal Q},{\cal R})=1+\sum_{i=1}^{2g+1}H_{i}x^{i}
, are functionally independent. According to
Proposition
4.6
, the generic fiber of
\mu
(which is the generic fiber of

H_{1},\dots,H_{2g+1}
) is an open subset of the Jacobian of a curve of genus
g
, hence has dimension
g
. The dimension of the generic fiber of
\mu
is given by
\dim M_{g}-s=3g+1-s
, where
s
denotes the number of functionally independent functions in

H_{1},\dots,H_{2g+1}
. Therefore,
s=2g+1
and the components of
\mu
are functionally independent.

It remains to be shown that the rank of
\left\{\cdot\,,\cdot\right\}^{\phi}
is
2g
. Since
\dim M=3g+1
and
\left\{\cdot\,,\cdot\right\}^{\phi}
admits
2g+1

functionally independent functions in involution, the rank of
\left\{\cdot\,,\cdot\right\}^{\phi}
is at most
2g
(see

[
60
, Prop. 3.4]
); to show equality, in a neighborhood of a generic point
({\cal P},{\cal Q},{\cal R})\in M_{g}
we
take the functions
x_{1},\dots,x_{n},y_{1},\dots,y_{n}
, which we constructed in the proof of
Proposition
4.6
. Then
\left\{x_{i},x_{j}\right\}^{\phi}=\left\{y_{i},y_{j}\right\}^{\phi}=0
, since

\left\{{\cal P}(x),{\cal P}(y)\right\}^{\phi}=\left\{{\cal Q}(x),{\cal Q}(y)\right\}^{\phi}=0
. We show that the brackets
\left\{x_{i},y_{j}\right\}^{\phi}
are non-zero if
and only if
i=j
, from which it follows that the rank of
\left\{\cdot\,,\cdot\right\}^{\phi}
is indeed
2g
. To do this, we compute for

1\leqslant i\leqslant g
the Poisson bracket
\left\{y_{i},\ln{\cal Q}(y)\right\}^{\phi}
in two different ways. First, using
(
4.1
),

\left\{y_{i},\ln{\cal Q}(y)\right\}^{\phi}=\left\{{\cal P}(x_{i}),\ln{\cal Q}(y)\right\}^{\phi}=\frac{\left\{{\cal P}(x_{i}),{\cal Q}(y)\right\}^{\phi}}{{\cal Q}(y)}=\frac{y\phi(x_{i})-x_{i}\phi(y){\cal Q}(x_{i})/{\cal Q}(y)}{x_{i}-y}=y\frac{\phi(x_{i})}{x_{i}-y}\;,

(4.19)

and next, using (
4.15
),

\left\{y_{i},\ln{\cal Q}(y)\right\}^{\phi}=\sum_{j=1}^{g}\left\{y_{i},\ln(1-y/x_{j})\right\}^{\phi}=y\sum_{j=1}^{g}\frac{\left\{y_{i},x_{j}\right\}^{\phi}}{x_{j}(x_{j}-y)}\;,

(4.20)

so that

\frac{\phi(x_{i})}{x_{i}-y}=\sum_{j=1}^{g}\frac{\left\{y_{i},x_{j}\right\}^{\phi}}{x_{j}(x_{j}-y)}\;.

Since (generically) all
x_{j}
are different,
\left\{x_{j},y_{i}\right\}^{\phi}=0
when
j\neq i
, while

\left\{x_{i},y_{i}\right\}^{\phi}=-x_{i}\phi(x_{i})
, so that

\left\{x_{i},y_{j}\right\}^{\phi}=-x_{i}\phi(x_{i})\delta_{ij}\;.

(4.21)

Since
\phi\neq 0
, this shows that the rank of
\left\{\cdot\,,\cdot\right\}^{\phi}
is
2g
.
∎

We now conclude with the main result of this section, namely that the Volterra map is a
discrete
a.c.i. system
. Recall from Definition
1.1
that this means that, besides being Liouville integrable,
the generic level sets defined by the invariants are affine parts of Abelian varieties (complex algebraic tori) and
the restriction of the map to any of these Abelian varieties is a translation.

Theorem 4.10
.

The Volterra map
{\cal V}_{g}
is a
discrete a.c.i. system
on
(M_{g},\left\{\cdot\,,\cdot\right\}^{\phi})
.

Proof.

Liouville integrability was already shown in Proposition
4.9
. Let
f\in{\mathbb{C}}[x]
be a
polynomial of degree
2g+1
, without repeated roots and with
f(0)=1
. Writing
f(x)=1+\sum_{i=0}^{2g+1}c_{i}x^{i}

the common level set defined by
H_{i}=c_{i}
,

is the fiber
\mu^{-1}(f)
which was shown in

4.6

\bar{\Gamma}_{f}
, and this is indeed an
Abelian variety. It remains to be shown that the restriction of the Volterra map
{\cal V}_{g}
to

\overline{\mu^{-1}(f)}\cong\Jac(\bar{\Gamma}_{f})
is a translation; more precisely we will show that it is a
translation over
[(0,-1)-\infty]=[\infty-(0,1)]
. Let
({\cal P},{\cal Q},{\cal R})\in\mu^{-1}(f)
be a generic point (a regular
triplet) so that
(\tilde{\cal P},\tilde{\cal Q},\tilde{\cal R}):={\cal V}_{g}({\cal P},{\cal Q},{\cal R})
also belongs to
\mu^{-1}(f)
. The degree

g
divisors on
\bar{\Gamma}_{f}
corresponding to these two triplets are respectively denoted by

\mathcal{D}=\sum_{i=1}^{g}(x_{i},y_{i})
and
\tilde{\mathcal{D}}=\sum_{i=1}^{g}(\tilde{x}_{i},\tilde{y}_{i})
(so that the corresponding divisor

[\mathcal{D}-g\infty]
and

\tilde{F}

{\cal V}_{g}
on (
3.6

(
4.3

) of

\tilde{F}:=\frac{y+\tilde{\cal P}(x)}{\tilde{\cal Q}(x)}=\frac{\tilde{\cal R}(x)}{y-\tilde{\cal P}(x)}=\frac{-wx{\cal Q}(x)}{y-{\cal Q}(x)+{\cal P}(x)}\;.

(4.22)

It is clear from the last, respectively first, expression that
\tilde{F}
has a simple zero at the each of the points
(x_{i},y_{i})
and

(0,-1)
, and a simple pole at each of the points

For the other zero or pole candidates in

g+1
zeros and
g+1
poles, in accord with the fact that the degree of the divisor of a rational function is zero. The upshot is
that the divisor of zeros and poles of

(\tilde{F})=\sum_{i=1}^{g}(x_{i},y_{i})+(0,-1)-\sum_{i=1}^{g}(\tilde{x}_{i},\tilde{y}_{i})-\infty=\mathcal{D}-\tilde{\mathcal{D}}+(0,-1)-\infty\;,

which leads to the linear equivalence

and hence to

(4.23)

as was to be shown.
∎

According to Proposition

5
Continuous flows and the infinite Volterra and Toda lattices

The discrete integrable systems that we have discussed so far are naturally associated with continuous systems
which are equally integrable. More precisely, Liouville integrability
of the Hamiltonian systems associated with the Volterra maps comes for free,
and with some extra work we
show that these continuous systems are also algebraically integrable.
We further show that in the genus
g
case any solution
w_{i}(t)
of one of the
integrable Hamiltonian vector fields extends, under the action of the Volterra map,
to a sequence
\big(w_{n}(t)\big)_{n\in{\mathbb{Z}}}
that is a solution to the infinite Volterra lattice; notice that in particular,
as discussed in the introduction, this applies to
the map (P.iv). We also discuss the relation between the infinite Toda and Volterra lattices, which
explains in part how some of the results in this paper
are related to the results in
[
31
]
, and what motivated us to introduce
S-fractions and the corresponding Mumford-like
systems to study the map (P.iv) and its higher genus analogues.

5.1
Liouville and algebraic integrability

Recall that on
M_{g}
we have a family of compatible Poisson brackets
\left\{\cdot\,,\cdot\right\}^{\phi}
of rank
2g
, as well as a family
of polynomial functions
H_{1},\dots,H_{2g+1}
, where
H_{i}
is the coefficient in
x^{i}
of
{\cal P}^{2}(x)+{\cal Q}(x){\cal R}(x)
;
said differently,
H_{1},\dots,H_{2g+1}
are the components of the momentum map
\mu:M_{g}\to{\mathbb{C}}[x]
.
For the sake of clarity, and since the choice of Poisson structure is not important for what follows, we will only
consider
\phi=x^{g+1}
here, and henceforth write
\left\{\cdot\,,\cdot\right\}
for
\left\{\cdot\,,\cdot\right\}^{\phi}
. With this choice of
\phi
, (
4.1
) –
(
4.8
) become

\displaystyle\left\{{\cal P}(x),{\cal P}(y)^{2}+{\cal Q}(y){\cal R}(y)\right\}

\displaystyle=y^{g+1}\frac{y{\cal Q}(y){\cal R}(x)-x{\cal Q}(x){\cal R}(y)}{x-y}\;,

\displaystyle\left\{{\cal Q}(x),{\cal P}(y)^{2}+{\cal Q}(y){\cal R}(y)\right\}

\displaystyle=2y^{g+1}\frac{x{\cal P}(y){\cal Q}(x)-y{\cal P}(x){\cal Q}(y)}{x-y}-y^{g+1}{\cal Q}(x){\cal Q}(y)\;,

\displaystyle\left\{{\cal R}(x),{\cal P}(y)^{2}+{\cal Q}(y){\cal R}(y)\right\}

\displaystyle=2xy^{g+1}\frac{{\cal P}(x){\cal R}(y)-{\cal P}(y){\cal R}(x)}{x-y}+y^{g+1}{\cal Q}(y){\cal R}(x)\;.

As we have seen,
H_{1},\dots,H_{g}
are Casimir functions of the Poisson bracket,
as well as
H_{g+1}=C_{\phi}
(see
(
4.9
)). The vector fields
\frac{1}{2}\mathrm{X}_{H_{g+i+1}}
are denoted by
\mathrm{X}_{i}
; we will mainly be
interested in
\mathrm{X}_{1}=\frac{1}{2}\mathrm{X}_{H_{g+2}}
, which we can compute by dividing the above equations by
2y^{g+2}
and taking
the limit for
y\to 0
, so that

\dot{\cal P}(x)=\lim_{y\to 0}\frac{y{\cal Q}(y){\cal R}(x)-x{\cal Q}(x){\cal R}(y)}{2y(x-y)}=\frac{2{\cal R}(x)-x{\cal Q}(x){\cal R}^{\prime}(0)}{2x}=\frac{{\cal R}(x)}{x}-\frac{r_{1}}{2}{\cal Q}(x)\;,

(5.1)

where the dot denotes the derivative
\tfrac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}t}
,
and similarly

\displaystyle\dot{\cal Q}(x)

\displaystyle=\frac{2p_{1}-q_{1}}{2}{\cal Q}(x)-\frac{2{\cal P}(x)-{\cal Q}(x)}{x}\;,

\displaystyle\dot{\cal R}(x)

\displaystyle=r_{1}{\cal P}(x)+\frac{q_{1}-2p_{1}}{2}{\cal R}(x)-\frac{{\cal R}(x)}{x}\;.

(5.2)

Notice that the vector field
\mathrm{X}_{1}
is (non-homogeneous) quadratic.
From Proposition
4.2
, the functions
H_{j}
are in involution
with one another with respect to
\left\{\cdot\,,\cdot\right\}
, which means that the vector fields
\mathrm{X}_{i}

all commute.

Note that the Liouville integrability of this continuous system is incorporated
into the Liouville integrability of the discrete system, so the following statement
is an automatic consequence of Proposition

4.9
.

Proposition 5.1
.

The Hamiltonian system
(M_{g},\left\{\cdot\,,\cdot\right\},\mu)
is a Liouville integrable system.

We now turn to the algebraic integrability of the Mumford-like system, which is
slightly more involved in the continuous case.
Recall (for example from
[
1
, Ch. 6]
) that
(M_{g},\left\{\cdot\,,\cdot\right\},\mu)
being an a.c.i. system means that

(1)

(M_{g},\left\{\cdot\,,\cdot\right\},\mu)
is a (complex) Liouville integrable system;

(2)

The generic fiber of
\mu
is (isomorphic to) an affine part of an Abelian variety;

(3)

The integrable vector fields are holomorphic (hence constant) on these Abelian varieties.

Items (1) and (2) have been shown already, in Propositions
5.1
and

4.6
, respectively, so it only remains to address item (3).

Proposition 5.2
.

The Hamiltonian system
(M_{g},\left\{\cdot\,,\cdot\right\},\mu)
is an algebraic completely integrable system (a.c.i. system).

Proof.

We show (3) for one of the integrable vector fields; then it also holds for the other
integrable vector fields, since the latter are holomorphic on the fiber and commute with a constant vector field.

The vector field which we consider is the Hamiltonian vector field
\mathrm{X}_{1}
, given by (
5.1
) and
(
5.1
). Let
({\cal P}_{0},{\cal Q}_{0},{\cal R}_{0})
be a generic point of
M_{g}
and consider for small
|t|
the
integral curve
t\mapsto({\cal P}_{t},{\cal Q}_{t},{\cal R}_{t})
of
\mathrm{X}_{1}
, starting at (
{\cal P}_{0},{\cal Q}_{0},{\cal R}_{0})
. Let

\mathcal{D}_{t}=\sum_{i=1}^{g}(x_{i}(t),y_{i}(t))-g\infty
denote the associated divisor on the algebraic curve
\Gamma_{f}
,
defined by it; recall that
\Gamma_{f}
is given by
y^{2}=f(x)
where
f={\cal P}_{0}^{2}+{\cal Q}_{0}{\cal R}_{0}={\cal P}_{t}^{2}+{\cal Q}_{t}{\cal R}_{t}
. Since
the
x_{i}(t)
are the roots of
{\cal Q}_{t}(x)
, upon substituting
x=x_{i}(t)
in the equation (
5.1
) for

\dot{\cal Q}(x)
we get

\dot{\cal Q}(x_{i}(t))=-2\frac{{\cal P}(x_{i}(t))}{x_{i}(t)}=-2\frac{y_{i}(t)}{x_{i}(t)}\;.

However, we can also compute
\dot{\cal Q}(x_{i}(t))
directly from the explicit formula (
4.15
)
for
{\cal Q}(x)
, to wit

\dot{\cal Q}(x_{i}(t))=\frac{2\dot{x}_{i}(t)}{x_{i}(t)}\prod_{j\neq i}\left(1-\frac{x_{i}(t)}{x_{j}(t)}\right)\;.

Comparing these two expressions gives

y_{i}(t)=-{\dot{x}_{i}(t)}\prod_{j\neq i}\left(1-\frac{x_{i}(t)}{x_{j}(t)}\right)\;.

(5.3)

It follows that for
k=0,\dots,g-1
,

\sum_{i=1}^{g}\frac{x_{i}^{k}(t)\mathrm{d}x_{i}(t)}{y_{i}(t)}=-\sum_{i=1}^{g}x_{i}^{k}(t)\prod_{j\neq i}\frac{x_{j}(t)}{x_{j}(t)-x_{i}(t)}\mathrm{d}t=-\delta_{k,0}\,\mathrm{d}t\;.

(5.4)

Above we have used the following identity which is well-known in the theory of symmetric functions:

\sum_{i=1}^{g}x_{i}^{k}\prod_{j\neq i}\frac{x_{j}}{x_{j}-x_{i}}=\delta_{k,0}\;,\hskip 20.00003ptk=0,\dots,g-1;

(5.5)

the
proof of the latter follows easily from the fact that any lowest degree antisymmetric polynomial in
g
variables is, up to a
factor, the Vandermonde determinant. Integrating (
5.4
) from
0
to
t
gives

\int_{\mathcal{D}_{0}}^{\mathcal{D}_{t}}\frac{x^{k}\mathrm{d}x}{y}=-t\,\delta_{k,0}\;.

(5.6)

The left-hand side of (
5.6
) contains the differentials
x^{k}\mathrm{d}x/y
for
k=0,\dots,g-1
, which form a
basis for the holomorphic differentials on
\bar{\Gamma}_{f}
. Thus the left-hand side of (
5.6
) is the image of the divisor
\mathcal{D}_{t}-\mathcal{D}_{0}
under
the Abel map, which is (by Abel’s Theorem) an isomorphism between the
algebraic Jacobian of
\bar{\Gamma}_{f}
,
consisting of degree zero divisor classes on
\bar{\Gamma}_{f}
, and the analytic
Jacobian of
\bar{\Gamma}_{f}
, which is a complex torus, that is

\Jac(\bar{\Gamma}_{f})\cong H^{0}\left(\Omega^{1}_{\bar{\Gamma}_{f}}\right)^{*}/H_{1}(\bar{\Gamma}_{f}).

Formula (
5.6
) then says that the integral
curve of
\mathrm{X}_{1}
is a straight line in this complex torus, as was to be shown.
∎

It follows that for, generic initial conditions, the solutions to
\mathrm{X}_{1}
are meromorphic functions in
t
.

5.2
Genus
g
solutions to the infinite Volterra and Toda lattices

The infinite Volterra lattice is given by the set of equations

\dot{w}_{n}=w_{n}(w_{n+1}-w_{n-1})\;,\hskip 20.00003ptn\in{\mathbb{Z}}\;.

(5.7)

It was first considered by Kac and van Moerbeke
[
35
]
, who also studied the
N
-periodic case (
w_{N+n}=w_{n}

for all
n
). We now show that the Volterra map allows us to define, for any
g
, infinite sequences
of meromorphic functions
(w_{n}(t))_{n\in{\mathbb{Z}}}
which satisfy (
5.7
). Since these sequences are
defined from solutions of the genus
g
Mumford-like system, and hence can be written in terms of genus
g
theta
functions, we will refer to these solutions to the Volterra lattice as
genus
g
solutions
.

Let
g>0
be fixed and consider the vector field on
{\mathbb{C}}^{3g+1}_{w}
, corresponding to the vector field
\frac{1}{2}\mathrm{X}_{1}

on the genus
g
Mumford-like system, via the birational transformation constructed in Section

3.5
. For the sake of brevity, let us call this the w-system (in genus
g
). By algebraic integrability,
the vector field
\frac{1}{2}\mathrm{X}_{1}
has globally defined meromorphic solutions
w_{1}(t),\dots,w_{3g+1}(t)
, corresponding to
generic initial conditions. Using the recursion relation induced by the action of the Volterra map on
{\mathbb{C}}^{3g+1}_{w}
,
we get also globally defined meromorphic functions

w_{n}(t)
for all
n>3g+1
and all
n\leqslant 0
. Algebraic integrability further implies that the
recursion and the flow of the vector field must commute, as they both correspond to translations on the fibers of the
momentum map, which are affine parts of
g
-dimensional tori. (The fact that the map and the flow
commute is already a consequence of Liouville integrability.) It follows that all formulae only involving the
variables
w_{n}
remain valid when all indices are shifted by the same integer. In the proof of the theorem
which follows we will make extensive use of the birational transformation between the w-system and the Mumford-like
system. The triplet corresponding to a meromorphic solution
(w_{1}(t),\dots,w_{3g+1}(t))
of the w-system in genus

g
will be denoted
({\cal P}_{0}(x;t),{\cal Q}_{0}(x;t),{\cal R}_{0}(x;t))
, the index
0
being added because we will also use the
triplets
({\cal P}_{n}(x;t),{\cal Q}_{n}(x;t),{\cal R}_{n}(x;t))
, obtained from it by applying the Volterra map or its
inverse several times. Again, all formulae involving only the polynomials
{\cal P}_{n},{\cal Q}_{n},{\cal R}_{n}
,
n\in{\mathbb{Z}}
, remain valid when all
indices are shifted by the same integer, and for any
n\in{\mathbb{Z}}
,
({\cal P}_{n}(x;t),{\cal Q}_{n}(x;t),{\cal R}_{n}(x;t))
corresponds to

(w_{n+1}(t),\dots,w_{3g+n+1}(t))

under the birational map.

Theorem 5.3
.

The sequence of meromorphic functions
\big(w_{n}(t)\big)_{n\in{\mathbb{Z}}}
is a solution to the infinite Volterra lattice
(
5.7
).

Proof.

We first recall the recursion relations (
3.9
) for the triplets
({\cal P}_{n},{\cal Q}_{n},{\cal R}_{n})
, which we evaluate at
any meromorphic solution to
\mathrm{X}_{1}=\frac{1}{2}\mathrm{X}_{H_{g+2}}
:

\displaystyle{\cal P}_{n+1}(x;t)={\cal Q}_{n}(x;t)-{\cal P}_{n}(x;t)\;,\ {\cal Q}_{n+1}(x;t)=\frac{2{\cal P}_{n}(x;t)-{\cal Q}_{n}(x;t)+{\cal R}_{n}(x;t)}{-w_{n+1}(t)x}\;,

(5.8)

\displaystyle{\cal R}_{n+1}(x;t)=-w_{n+1}(t)x{\cal Q}_{n}(x;t)\;.

(5.9)

From (
5.9
), since
H_{1}=2(p_{1}+r_{1})
is a first integral, and using
p_{n+1,1}=q_{n,1}-p_{n,1}
, which
follows from the first equation in the recursion relation (
3.13
), we have

w_{n}(t)=-\frac{1}{2}r_{n,1}(t)=\frac{1}{2}p_{n,1}(t)-\frac{c_{1}}{4}\;,\hskip 10.00002pt\hbox{and}\hskip 10.00002ptr_{n,2}(t)=-w_{n}(t)q_{n-1,1}(t)\;,

(5.10)

where
c_{1}
is a constant. It follows that

\displaystyle\dot{w}_{n}(t)

\displaystyle\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\eqref{eq:w_to_p}}}{{=}}\frac{1}{2}\dot{p}_{n,1}(t)\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\eqref{eq:pdot}}}{{=}}\frac{1}{2}r_{n,2}(t)-\frac{1}{4}r_{n,1}(t)q_{n,1}(t)\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\eqref{eq:w_to_p}}}{{=}}\frac{w_{n}(t)}{2}(q_{n,1}(t)-q_{n-1,1}(t))

\displaystyle\;\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\eqref{PQrect}}}{{=}}\frac{w_{n}(t)}{2}(p_{n+1,1}(t)-p_{n-1,1}(t))\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\eqref{eq:w_to_p}}}{{=}}w_{n}(t)(w_{n+1}(t)-w_{n-1}(t))\;,

as was to be shown.
∎

Remark 5.4
.

It is fairly straightforward to modify the proof of Proposition
5.2
, and the preceding result, to all of the Hamiltonian vector fields
\mathrm{X}_{i}
, associated
with times
t_{i}
,
1\leqslant i\leqslant g
, which correspond to the first
g
flows in the Volterra lattice hierarchy.
This replaces
t
by
t_{i}
and modifies the Kronecker delta on the right-hand side of (

) to
\delta_{k,i-1}
, hence producing solutions that are meromorphic
in

.

We now apply a standard Miura-like formula, to show how a genus

solution of the Volterra lattice, given by an infinite sequence of meromorphic functions

, also leads to a corresponding solution to the
infinite Toda lattice, given by

\frac{\mathrm{d}a_{n}}{\mathrm{d}t}=a_{n}(b_{n-1}-b_{n})\;,\hskip 20.00003pt\frac{\mathrm{d}b_{n}}{\mathrm{d}t}=a_{n}-a_{n+1}\;.

(5.11)

(These are almost the same as the Flaschka variables for the Toda lattice, except that traditionally
\sqrt{a_{n}}
is used in place of
a_{n}
;
and similarly, the quantities
\sqrt{w_{n}}
are used in
[
42
]
.)

Corollary 5.5
.

Let
w_{n}(t)
,
n\in{\mathbb{Z}}
be a genus
g
meromorphic solution to the infinite Volterra lattice
(

). Upon setting, for

,

a_{j+1}:=w_{2j-1}w_{2j}\;,\hskip 20.00003ptb_{j+1}:=-w_{2j}-w_{2j+1}\;,

(5.12)

the sequence of meromorphic functions
a_{j}(t),b_{j}(t)
is a solution to the infinite Toda lattice,
while another sequence of meromorphic solutions to (
5.11
) is given for

Remark 5.7

In

the transformation (

who attributed it to Hénon. The same transformation has further been applied to connect

Moser also employed finite continued fractions in
[

]

However, it turns out that the map (

)
has a much earlier origin in the classical theory of continued fractions,
where it arises from the method of contraction for S-fractions (see J.3 in
[
55
]
, and
[

]
),
a fact that has perhaps been overlooked
in the integrable systems literature. In the next subsection, we show how the Volterra maps,
as presented in this paper, are related to the integrable maps recently constructed by one of us

).

5.3
Contraction of continued fractions

The following equivalence between a pair of continued fractions, which was introduced in
[
55
]
, is known as
contraction
:

X-\cfrac{w_{1}}{1-\cfrac{w_{2}}{X-\cfrac{w_{3}}{1-\cfrac{w_{4}}{X-\cdots}}}}=X+b_{1}-\cfrac{a_{2}}{X+b_{2}-\cfrac{a_{3}}{X+b_{3}-\cfrac{a_{4}}{X+b_{4}-\cdots}}}\;.

The form of the fraction on the left is the original way that an S-fraction was written by Stieltjes, in terms of a variable

X=x^{-1}
, while the fraction on the right is a
Jacobi continued fraction (J-fraction). To be more precise, the above equality
is an identity of continued fractions,
obtained by combining successive pairs of adjacent lines in the S-fraction into a single sequence of lines in the J-fraction,
with the coefficients
a_{j},b_{j}
on the right being related to
w_{j}
on the left by

b_{1}=-w_{1}\;,\hskip 20.00003pta_{j+1}=w_{2j-1}w_{2j}\;,\hskip 20.00003ptb_{j+1}=-w_{2j}-w_{2j+1}\;,\hskip 20.00003pt\mathrm{for}\hskip 10.00002ptj\geqslant 1\;.

(5.16)

(Within the theory of continuants, the formulae for contraction of two or more lines of a general continued fraction are presented in
[
17
]
.)
To make contact with our previous discussion,
observe that (
5.16
) reproduces the transformation (
5.12
) between
the Volterra and Toda lattices, but for indices
j\geqslant 1
only.

In order to see how contraction arises in the context of Volterra maps, we start from a hyperelliptic curve
\Gamma_{f}

of the form previously considered.
We take a fixed set of coefficients
c_{i}
, which are arbitrary except that, as usual, we assume that the polynomial

f(x)=1+\sum_{i=1}^{2g+1}c_{i}x^{i}

(5.17)

is square-free with
c_{2g+1}\neq 0
,
so that the hyperelliptic curve
\Gamma_{f}:\,y^{2}=f(x)

is smooth and has genus
g
.
In order to simplify the presentation below,
initially we make the further assumption that
c_{1}=0
.
Then setting

X=\frac{1}{x}\;,\hskip 20.00003ptY=\frac{y}{x^{g+1}}\;

(5.18)

establishes a birational isomorphism between
\Gamma_{f}
and an algebraic curve
{\cal C}

which (by completing the square) can be written in the form

{\cal C}:\,\,Y^{2}=\hat{\mathrm{F}}(X),\hskip 20.00003pt\hat{\mathrm{F}}(X)=A(X)^{2}+4R(X),

(5.19)

where
A(X)
is a monic polynomial in
X
of degree
g+1
with no term of degree
g
(so
that the right-hand side of (
5.19
) has no degree
2g+1
term), and
R
is
a polynomial of degree at most
g
in
X
, not identically zero but otherwise arbitrary; such curves are exactly the ones which were considered in

[
31
]
.

Now let
({\cal P},{\cal Q},{\cal R})=({\cal P}_{0},{\cal Q}_{0},{\cal R}_{0})\in M_{g}
be a generic triplet satisfying
{\cal P}^{2}+{\cal Q}{\cal R}=f
, in the sense discussed above (
3.15
).
As we have seen in
(
3.15
), the associated rational function on
\Gamma_{f}
, denoted

, admits the following expansion as an S-fraction:

F_{0}=\frac{y+{\cal P}_{0}(x)}{{\cal Q}_{0}(x)}=1-\cfrac{w_{1}x}{1-\cfrac{w_{2}x}{1-\cfrac{w_{3}x}{1-\cdots}}}\;.

(5.20)

Then, upon multiplying (
5.20
) by
x^{-1}=X
, rewriting the S-fraction in terms of the new spectral
parameter
X
, and applying contraction, we find

x^{-1}F_{0}=X-\cfrac{w_{1}}{1-\cfrac{w_{2}}{X-\cfrac{w_{3}}{1-\cfrac{w_{4}}{X-\cdots}}}}=X+b_{1}-\cfrac{a_{2}}{X+b_{2}-\cfrac{a_{3}}{X+b_{3}-\cfrac{a_{4}}{X+b_{4}-\cdots}}}\;,

(5.21)

where the J-fraction on the right above is defined to be the contraction of the S-fraction.
Thus, via the second equality in (
5.21
), the coefficients
a_{j},b_{j}
of the J-fraction
are specified in terms of the
w_{j}
according to (
5.16
).

We now briefly recall the construction of integrable maps associated with J-fractions, as presented in
[
31
]
.
The starting point is a rational function
Y_{0}
on an even hyperelliptic curve

of the form (
5.19
),
whose completion
\bar{{\cal C}}
includes two points
\infty_{1},\infty_{2}
at infinity. This function
has
g+1
simple poles and
g+1
simple zeros, with one pole being at the point
\infty_{1}
(where

Y\sim X^{g+1}\sim A(X)
as
X\to\infty
), and one zero being at
\infty_{2}
(where
Y\sim-X^{g+1}\sim-A(X)
),
taking the form

Y_{0}=\frac{Y+P_{0}(X)}{Q_{0}(X)}\;,

(5.22)

for polynomials
P_{0}
, of degree
g+1
with no term at
{\cal O}(X^{g})
, and
Q_{0}
, of degree
g
; and there exists another polynomial

Q_{-1}
, of degree
g
, satisfying
Y^{2}=P_{0}^{2}+Q_{0}Q_{-1}=\hat{\mathrm{F}}
. The expansion of
Y_{0}
around the point
\infty_{1}
, with
X^{-1}
as a local parameter,
can be considered as an element of
{\mathbb{C}}((X^{-1}))
, and it was shown by van der Poorten (see
[

,

) that this power series admits a J-fraction expansion of the form

Y_{0}={\alpha}_{0}+\cfrac{1}{Y_{1}}={\alpha}_{0}+\cfrac{1}{{\alpha}_{1}+\cfrac{1}{Y_{2}}}=\cdots={\alpha}_{0}+\cfrac{1}{{\alpha}_{1}+\cfrac{1}{{\alpha}_{2}+\cfrac{1}{\ddots}}}

with
{\alpha}_{n}:=\left\lfloor Y_{n}\right\rfloor
, the polynomial part of
Y_{n}
.
Furthermore, for a generic choice of such

,
Q_{0}
in (
5.22
), the polynomial parts
{\alpha}_{n}
are of degree 1 in
X
for any

,
and the recursion
Y_{n}={\alpha}_{n}+\frac{1}{Y_{n+1}}

leads to a sequence of polynomials

satisfying the same degree constraints as above, such that

Y_{n}=\frac{Y+P_{n}(X)}{Q_{n}(X)}=\frac{Q_{n-1}(X)}{Y-P_{n}(X)}\;\implies Y^{2}=\hat{\mathrm{F}}(X)=P_{n}(X)^{2}+Q_{n}(X)Q_{n-1}(X),

where the above relations extend to all

The situation for the J-fraction expansion (

5.20
), and
allows the construction of a birational dynamical system that is defined by a recursion for the polynomials

Here we refer to the dynamical system (in dimension

In order to explain the very

to
[
31
]
for a complete description.

To describe the dynamics defined by

, new variables

,

and

are introduced from

P_{n}(X)=A(X)+2d_{n}X^{g-1}+{\cal O}(X^{g-2})\;,\hskip 10.00002ptQ_{n}(X)=u_{n}\big(X^{g}-v_{n}X^{g-1}+{\cal O}(X^{g-2})\big)\;,

so that from the terms of

{Y_{0}}={\alpha}_{0}+\frac{1}{Y_{1}}={\frac{2(X+v_{0})}{u_{0}}+\cfrac{1}{\frac{2(X+v_{1})}{u_{1}}+\cfrac{1}{\frac{2(X+v_{2})}{u_{2}}-\cdots}}},

(5.27)

Then the J-fraction map

is a dynamical system
on an affine phase space
\hat{M}_{g}^{\hat{c}_{1}=0}
of dimension

, which fibers over the space of curves

of the form (

), with
each (generic) fiber being an affine part of the corresponding Jacobian variety

; and on each such fiber, the map corresponds

.
It is defined recursively by (
5.24
), in terms of the coefficients of the

, except that the prefactors

in front of each

Indeed, the constant

in (

) is arbitrary: it determines the first coefficient in the series expansion of the moment generating function

but

is fixed, then

and all the other prefactors

and

and the phase space

(which is an affine space of Lax matrices) does not include the parameter

(See (

) and (

We would now like to identify (

Proposition 5.8
.

The odd, genus

6
Conclusions and outlook

We have seen how the map (P.iv) obtained by Gubbiotti et al. can
naturally be viewed as the
g=2
member of a family of algebraically integrable maps,
defined for each
g
, that are naturally related
to the infinite Volterra lattice equation, leading to genus
g
solutions of the latter.
This begs the question as to what can be said about the other Liouville integrable maps in 4D found in
[
22
]
, namely (P.v) and (P.vi), which take the same form (
1.1
)
but for a different rational function
\mathrm{F}
. Note that (P.vi) depends on an
extra parameter compared with (P.v), which we denote here by
{\delta}
(instead of
{\delta}^{2}
in

[
22
]
). In fact, (P.v) arises from (P.vi) in the limit
{\delta}\to 0
.

So far we have made the following observations:

•

Each solution
\hat{w}_{n}
of (P.v) is mapped to a solution
w_{n}
of (P.iv)
via the transformation

w_{n}=\hat{w}_{n+1}\hat{w}_{n},

(6.1)

by suitably identifying the parameters
\nu,a,b
for (P.iv) in terms of the parameters and first integrals
for (P.v).

•

Under the flow of the Hamiltonian vector field
\tfrac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}t}

associated with one of its first integrals, the sequence
(\hat{w}_{n})_{n\in{\mathbb{Z}}}

generated by iteration of the map (P.v)
extends to a solution of the modified Volterra lattice in the form

\frac{\mathrm{d}\hat{w}_{n}}{\mathrm{d}t}=\hat{w}_{n}^{2}(\hat{w}_{n+1}-\hat{w}_{n-1}).

(6.2)

•

Each solution
\hat{w}_{n}
of (P.vi) is mapped to a pair of solutions
w_{n}^{(+)}
,

w_{n}^{(-)}
of (P.iv), via
the transformations

w_{n}^{(\pm)}=(\hat{w}_{n+1}\pm{\delta})(\hat{w}_{n}\mp{\delta}),

(6.3)

by suitably identifying the parameters
\nu,a,b
for (P.iv) in terms of the parameters and first integrals
for (P.vi).

•

Under the flow of the Hamiltonian vector field
\tfrac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}t}

associated with one of its first integrals, the sequence
(\hat{w}_{n})_{n\in{\mathbb{Z}}}

generated by iteration of the map (P.vi)
extends to a solution of the modified Volterra lattice in the form

\frac{\mathrm{d}\hat{w}_{n}}{\mathrm{d}t}=(\hat{w}_{n}^{2}-{\delta}^{2})(\hat{w}_{n+1}-\hat{w}_{n-1}).

(6.4)

The formulae (
6.1
) and (
6.3
) are the well-known expressions for the Miura transformation connecting the two forms of the modified Volterra lattice equation, given by (
6.2
) and (
6.4
), respectively,
to the Volterra lattice (
5.7
). Thus the above statements about the connections between the maps can be viewed as restrictions of a Miura
transformation to a finite-dimensional phase space. Preliminary calculations, and
the results of
[
65
]
on elliptic solutions, indicate that this picture should extend
to arbitrary genus
g
. Our initial results, including an explicit description of how both (P.v) and (P.vi) are related to (P.iv), have recently appeared in
[
32
]
.
We propose that the full description of the above observations, and their extension to genus
g
analogues
of the maps (P.v) and (P.vi), should be left as the subject of future work.

It is also worth pointing out that part of the original motivation for the work
in
[
22
]
was to consider autonomous versions of the higher order discrete
Painlevé equations from
[
12
]
, and new applications of the latter have been found very recently.
Non-autonomous analogues of the Volterra maps
{\cal V}_{g}
have been considered in the context of Hermitian matrix models
[
4
]
, where they arise as string equations, and they also appear as recursion relations for orthogonal polynomials associated with generalised
Freud weights of higher order
[
11
]
. In these applications, the algebro-geometric structure of the Volterra maps should be relevant to the asymptotic description of the oscillatory behaviour that is observed in specific parameter regimes.

Appendix A: the relation between the Mumford-like and the even Mumford system

Here we now show that the Hamiltonian system
(M_{g},\left\{\cdot\,,\cdot\right\}^{\phi},\mu)
associated with the Volterra map is birationally isomorphic
(as a Poisson isomorphism) to the even Mumford system, or more precisely to a subsystem thereof, obtained
simply by fixing the value of one of the Casimirs. Recall from

[
60
, Ch. 6]
that the even Mumford system (of genus
g
) is the Hamiltonian system

(M_{g}^{\prime},\left\{\cdot\,,\cdot\right\}^{\prime}{}^{\psi},\mu^{\prime})
, whose phase space
M_{g}^{\prime}
is the
(3g+2)
-dimensional affine space

M_{g}^{\prime}:=\left\{(U(\xi),V(\xi),W(\xi))\in{\mathbb{C}}[\xi]^{3}~\Bigl|~\begin{array}[]{lll}\deg U(\xi)=g\;,&U\hbox{ monic}\\
\deg V(\xi)<g\;,&\\
\deg W(\xi)=g+2\;,&W\hbox{ monic}\\
\end{array}\right\}\;.

(7.1)

Elements
(U(\xi),V(\xi),W(\xi))
of
M_{g}^{\prime}
are written as Lax matrices

{\bf L}^{\prime}(\xi):=\left(\begin{array}[]{lr}V(\xi)&U(\xi)\\
W(\xi)&-V(\xi)\end{array}\right)\;,\hskip 20.00003pt

whose polynomial entries have the form

U(\xi)=\xi^{g}+\sum_{i=0}^{g-1}U_{i}\xi^{i}\;,\hskip 20.00003ptV(\xi)=\sum_{i=0}^{g-1}V_{i}\xi^{i}\;,\hskip 20.00003ptW(\xi)=\xi^{g+2}+\sum_{i=0}^{g+1}W_{i}\xi^{i}\;.

The
3g+2
coefficients
W_{g+1},W_{g}
and
U_{i},V_{i},W_{i}
with
0\leqslant i<g
are used as linear coordinates on

M_{g}^{\prime}
. The momentum map
\mu^{\prime}
is given by

\begin{array}[]{lcrcl}\mu^{\prime}&:&M_{g}^{\prime}&\to&{\mathbb{C}}[\xi]\\
&&{\bf L}^{\prime}(\xi)=\left(\begin{array}[]{lr}V(\xi)&U(\xi)\\
W(\xi)&-V(\xi)\end{array}\right)&\mapsto&-\det{\bf L}^{\prime}(\xi)=V(\xi)^{2}+U(\xi)W(\xi)\;.\end{array}

It is clear that
-\det{\bf L}^{\prime}(\xi)
is monic of degree
2g+2
, so
2g+2
polynomial functions

H^{\prime}_{0},H^{\prime}_{1},\dots,H^{\prime}_{2g+1}
on
M_{g}^{\prime}
are defined by

V(\xi)^{2}+U(\xi)W(\xi)=\xi^{2g+2}+\sum_{i=0}^{2g+1}H^{\prime}_{i}\xi^{i}\;,

For any non-zero polynomial
\psi
of degree at most
g+1
, a Poisson structure of rank
2g
on
M_{g}^{\prime}
is given by

\displaystyle\left\{U(\xi),U(\eta)\right\}^{\prime}{}^{\psi}

\displaystyle=\left\{V(\xi),V(\eta)\right\}^{\prime}{}^{\psi}=0\;,

\displaystyle\left\{U(\xi),V(\eta)\right\}^{\prime}{}^{\psi}

\displaystyle=\frac{U(\xi)\psi(\eta)-U(\eta)\psi(\xi)}{\xi-\eta}\;,

\displaystyle\left\{U(\xi),W(\eta)\right\}^{\prime}{}^{\psi}

\displaystyle=-2\frac{V(\xi)\psi(\eta)-V(\eta)\psi(\xi)}{\xi-\eta}\;,

\displaystyle\left\{V(\xi),W(\eta)\right\}^{\prime}{}^{\psi}

\displaystyle=\frac{W(\xi)\psi(\eta)-W(\eta)\psi(\xi)}{\xi-\eta}-(\xi+\eta+W_{g+1}-U_{g-1})U(\xi)\psi(\eta)\;,

\displaystyle\left\{W(\xi),W(\eta)\right\}^{\prime}{}^{\psi}

\displaystyle=2(\xi+\eta+W_{g+1}-U_{g-1})\left(V(\xi)\psi(\eta)-V(\eta)\psi(\xi)\right)\;.

The polynomial functions
H^{\prime}_{i}
are functionally independent and in involution, which accounts for the Liouville
integrability of the even Mumford system. It is algebraically integrable, with the fiber of
\mu^{\prime}
over any monic
polynomial
f^{\prime}(x)
of degree
2g+2
and without multiple roots being an affine part of the Jacobian of the smooth
hyperelliptic curve defined by
\eta^{2}=f^{\prime}(\xi)
.

For the isomorphism, we consider only the Poisson structures
\left\{\cdot\,,\cdot\right\}^{\prime}{}^{\psi}
for which
\psi(0)=0
. They admit

H^{\prime}_{0}
as Casimir function, hence we can restrict
(M_{g}^{\prime},\left\{\cdot\,,\cdot\right\}^{\prime}{}^{\psi},\mu^{\prime})
to the subvariety, defined by

H^{\prime}_{0}=0
; the resulting system is denoted by
(M_{g}^{0},\left\{\cdot\,,\cdot\right\}^{0,\psi},\mu^{0})
. We show that this system is
birationally equivalent to the Mumford-like system
(M_{g},\left\{\cdot\,,\cdot\right\}^{\phi},\mu)
, where the relation between
\psi
and

\phi
will be spelled out below. To do this, we construct a biregular map
\tilde{\Psi}
and a birational map
\Psi

making the following diagram commutative:

{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces M_{g}^{0}}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces M_{g}}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces B_{g}^{0}}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces B_{g}}

\eta^{2}=\phi(\xi)
and so the divisor class
\left[\sum_{i=1}^{g}(\xi_{i},\eta_{i})-g\infty_{1}\right]
is a
point of its Jacobian. The relation between the polynomials
{\cal Q}(x)
and
U(\xi)
is clearly given by

\frac{\xi^{g}}{2}{\cal Q}(\xi^{-1})=U(\xi)\;.

Indeed, both sides of this equality are polynomials of degree
2g+2
which vanish for
\xi=\xi_{i}=x_{i}^{-1}
(which
we may assume to be different). Similarly,
{\cal P}(x)
is related to
U(\xi)
and
V(\xi)
by

\xi^{g}{\cal P}(\xi^{-1})-U(\xi)=\frac{U_{0}V(\xi)-V_{0}U(\xi)}{\xi U_{0}}\;,

because both sides of this equation are the unique polynomial in
\xi
of degree less than
g
which takes for

\xi=\xi_{i}
the value
\eta_{i}/\xi_{i}
. A formula for
{\cal R}
follows from the equations of the curves, namely

{\cal P}^{2}(\xi^{-1})+{\cal Q}(\xi^{-1}){\cal R}(\xi^{-1})=f(\xi^{-1})=\xi^{-2g-2}f^{\prime}(\xi)=\xi^{-2g-2}(V^{2}(\xi)+U(\xi)W(\xi))\;.

It is clear that this defines a birational map between
M_{g}
and
M_{g}^{0}
. Under this map,
H_{i}
corresponds to

H^{\prime}_{2g+2-i}
, for
i=1,\dots,2g+1
. To see that
\Psi
is a Poisson map, hence a birational Poisson isomorphism,
we recall that the Poisson bracket
\left\{\cdot\,,\cdot\right\}^{\prime}{}^{\psi}
is given in terms of the
\xi_{i}
and
\eta_{j}
by

\left\{\xi_{i},\eta_{j}\right\}^{\prime}{}^{\psi}=\psi(\xi_{i})\delta_{ij},
and hence

\left\{x_{i},y_{j}\right\}^{\prime}{}^{\psi}=\left\{\xi_{i}^{-1},\eta_{j}\xi^{-g-1}_{j}\right\}^{\prime}{}^{\psi}=-\xi_{i}^{-2}\xi_{j}^{-g-1}\left\{\xi_{i},\eta_{j}\right\}^{\prime}{}^{\psi}=-{\xi_{i}^{-g-3}}\psi(\xi_{i})\delta_{ij}=-{x_{i}^{g+3}}\psi(x_{i}^{-1})\delta_{ij}\;.

Compared with (
4.21
), this shows that
\Psi:(M_{g}^{0},\left\{\cdot\,,\cdot\right\}^{\prime}{}^{\psi})\to(M_{g},\left\{\cdot\,,\cdot\right\}^{\phi})
is a birational
Poisson isomorphism when taking
\phi(x)=\psi(x^{-1})x^{g+2}
; notice that
\phi
, defined by this formula, is
indeed a polynomial of degree at most
g+1
, vanishing at
0
and that we get all such polynomials
\phi
for some
appropriate polynomial
\psi
of degree at most
g+1
, vanishing at
0
.
