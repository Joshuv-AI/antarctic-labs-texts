Visible Point Partition Identities for Polylogarithms, and Parametric Euler Sums Thanks: Thanks to Professor Dr Henk Koppelaar, whose suggestions helped summarize, for this paper, some chapters of the author’s draft book.
Geoffrey B Campbell

Abstract
. We set the scene with known values and functional relations for dilogarithms, trilogarithms and polylogarithms of various orders, along with more recent Euler sum values and multidimensional computations paying homage to the three late Professors Borwein et al. . We then apply many of these sum values to tabulate some sixty new combinatorial identities for weighted partitions into Visible Point Vectors in 2D, 3D, 4D and 5D cases suggesting new n n D first hyperquadrant and hyperpyramid lattice point identities.

1.
Early history of the Dilogarithm

The dilogarithm function was first studied by Euler
[
20
]
and Landen
[
21
]
in the second half of the eighteenth century; and in the second half of the 20th century we had the books from Lewin
[
22
,
23
,
24
]
. So, the function known as the Euler dilogarithm, is defined by

(1.1)

Li_{2}(z)=\sum_{r=1}^{\infty}\frac{z^{r}}{r^{2}},\;\;|z|\leq 1.

A version extended beyond the unit circle is given by the integral

(1.2)

Li_{2}(z)=-\int_{0}^{z}\frac{\log(1-t)}{t}dt.

For
z
real and greater than unity the dilogarithm is complex. The function is transcendental, and is known to be irrational when
z
is rational.

Euler easily found functional equations and a few special values. The major functional equation results known today are:

(1.3)

Li_{2}(z)+Li_{2}(-z)=\frac{1}{2}{Li_{2}}^{2}(z),

(1.4)

Li_{2}(1-z)+Li_{2}(1-\frac{1}{z})=-\frac{1}{2}Li_{2}(z^{2}),

(1.5)

Li_{2}(z)+Li_{2}(1-z)=\frac{\pi^{2}}{6}-\log(z)\log(1-z),

(1.6)

Li_{2}(-z)-Li_{2}(1-z)+\frac{1}{2}Li_{2}(1-z^{2})=-\frac{\pi^{2}}{12}-\log(z)\log(1+z).

In the literature, probably the best known bivariate functional equation is
Abel’s equation
, easily verified by differentiation,

(1.7)

Li_{2}\left(\frac{x}{1-x}.\frac{y}{1-y}\right)=Li_{2}\left(\frac{x}{1-y}\right)+Li_{2}\left(\frac{y}{1-x}\right)-Li_{2}(x)-Li_{2}(y)-\log(1-x)\log(1-y).

This formula famously given by Abel was 20 years earlier given by Spence in the equivalent form,

(1.8)

Li_{2}\left(\frac{x}{1-x}.\frac{y}{1-y}\right)=Li_{2}\left(\frac{x}{1-y}\right)+Li_{2}\left(\frac{y}{1-x}\right)+Li_{2}\left(\frac{-x}{1-x}\right)

+Li_{2}\left(\frac{-y}{1-y}\right)+\frac{1}{2}{\log}^{2}\left(\frac{1-x}{1-y}\right).

Let
L(x)
denote the Rogers
L
-function defined in terms of the usual dilogarithm by

(1.9)

L(x)=\frac{6}{\pi^{2}}\left(Li_{2}(x)+\frac{1}{2}\log(x)\log(1-x)\right)=\frac{6}{\pi^{2}}\left(\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}\frac{x^{k}}{k^{2}}+\frac{1}{2}\log(x)\log(1-x)\right).

Then
L(x)
satisfies the functional equation

(1.10)

L(x)+L(y)=L(xy)+L\left(\frac{x(1-y)}{1-xy}\right)+L\left(\frac{y(1-x)}{1-xy}\right).

Related to this, are the
Watson identities

[
27
]
defined as follows. Let
\alpha
,
-\beta
, and
-\gamma^{-1}
be the roots of the cubic equation

(1.11)

t^{3}+2t^{2}-t-1=0,

then the Rogers
L
-function satisfies

(1.12)

L(\alpha)-L(\alpha^{2})=\frac{1}{7},

(1.13)

L(\beta)+\frac{1}{2}L(\beta^{2})=\frac{5}{7},

(1.14)

L(\gamma)+\frac{1}{2}L(\gamma^{2})=\frac{4}{7}.

A complete list of
Li_{2}(x)
which can be evaluated in closed form is given by

(1.15)

Li_{2}(-1)=-\frac{\pi^{2}}{12},

(1.16)

Li_{2}(0)=0,

(1.17)

Li_{2}(\frac{1}{2})=\frac{\pi^{2}}{12}-\frac{1}{2}(\log 2)^{2},

(1.18)

Li_{2}(1)=-\frac{\pi^{2}}{6},

(1.19)

Li_{2}(2)=-\frac{\pi^{2}}{4}-i\pi\log 2,

(1.20)

Li_{2}(-\phi)=-\frac{\pi^{2}}{10}-(\log\phi)^{2},

(1.21)

Li_{2}(-\phi^{-1})=-\frac{\pi^{2}}{15}+\frac{1}{2}(\log\phi)^{2},

(1.22)

Li_{2}(\phi^{-2})=\frac{\pi^{2}}{15}-(\log\phi)^{2},

(1.23)

Li_{2}(\phi^{-1})=\frac{\pi^{2}}{10}-(\log\phi)^{2},

where
\phi
is the golden ratio
\frac{1+\sqrt{5}}{2}
. For proof of these see Bailey et al.
[
3
]
and Borwein et al.
[
10
]
. It may seem strange that the above nine values of
Li_{2}(x)
is the full known list. Also, there are many interesting identities involving the dilogarithm function. Ramanujan gave the following:

(1.24)

Li_{2}\left(\frac{1}{3}\right)-\frac{1}{6}Li_{2}\left(\frac{1}{9}\right)=\frac{\pi^{2}}{18}-\frac{1}{6}(\log 3)^{2},

(1.25)

Li_{2}\left(-\frac{1}{2}\right)+\frac{1}{6}Li_{2}\left(\frac{1}{9}\right)=-\frac{\pi^{2}}{18}+\log 2\log 3-\frac{1}{2}(\log 2)^{2}-\frac{1}{3}(\log 3)^{2}

(1.26)

Li_{2}\left(\frac{1}{4}\right)+\frac{1}{3}Li_{2}\left(\frac{1}{9}\right)=\frac{\pi^{2}}{18}+2\log 2\log 3-2(\log 2)^{2}-\frac{2}{3}(\log 3)^{2},

(1.27)

Li_{2}\left(-\frac{1}{3}\right)-\frac{1}{3}Li_{2}\left(\frac{1}{9}\right)=-\frac{\pi^{2}}{18}+\frac{1}{6}(\log 3)^{2},

(1.28)

Li_{2}\left(-\frac{1}{8}\right)+Li_{2}\left(\frac{1}{9}\right)=-\frac{1}{2}\left(\log\left(\frac{9}{8}\right)\right)^{2}.

Proofs of these can be found in the Ramanujan Notebooks Vol IV by Berndt
[
5
]
. In 1997, Bailey et al.
[
3
]
showed that

(1.29)

\pi^{2}=36Li_{2}\left(\frac{1}{2}\right)-36Li_{2}\left(\frac{1}{4}\right)-12Li_{2}\left(\frac{1}{8}\right)+6Li_{2}\left(\frac{1}{64}\right).

Lewin
[
24
]
gives 67 dilogarithm identities known as
ladders
, and Bailey and Broadhurst
[
4
]
found the impressive additional
17 dilogarithm
identity

(1.30)

0=Li_{2}(\alpha_{1}^{-630})-2Li_{2}(\alpha_{1}^{-315})-3Li_{2}(\alpha_{1}^{-210})-10Li_{2}(\alpha_{1}^{-126})

-7Li_{2}(\alpha_{1}^{-90})+18Li_{2}(\alpha_{1}^{-35})+84Li_{2}(\alpha_{1}^{-15})+90Li_{2}(\alpha_{1}^{-14})-4Li_{2}(\alpha_{1}^{-9})

+339Li_{2}(\alpha_{1}^{-8})+45Li_{2}(\alpha_{1}^{-7})+265Li_{2}(\alpha_{1}^{-6})-273Li_{2}(\alpha_{1}^{-5})-678Li_{2}(\alpha_{1}^{-4})

-1016Li_{2}(\alpha_{1}^{-3})-744Li_{2}(\alpha_{1}^{-2})-804Li_{2}(\alpha_{1}^{-1})-22050(\log\alpha_{1})^{2}+2003\zeta(2),

\textmd{where}\;\alpha_{1}=(x^{10}+x^{9}-x^{7}-x^{6}-x^{5}-x^{4}-x^{3}+x+1)_{2}\approx 1.176280818259917506544

is the largest positive root of the polynomial in
Lehmer’s Mahler measure problem
and
\zeta(z)
is the Riemann zeta function.

The closed form particular cases are all arrived at from elementary algebraic manipulation of the functional equations, and therefore will be given also as exercises. However, in these exercises clues and prompts may be useful expedients for the student.

Functional equations for the dilogarithm are most easily proved by differentiating both sides. As such, the proof of the functional equations will be part of the exercises at the end of this paper.

In the literature, the Rogers
L
-function form used in (
1.9
) turns out to be convenient in simplifying the trilogarithm and 4th and 5th degree polylogarithm functional equations.

The polylogarithm occurs naturally in the Visible Point Vector identities, and the functional equations applied to these will bring relationships for weighted vector partitions.

2.
The Trilogarithm function

The trilogarithm
Li_{3}(z)
, sometimes also denoted
L_{3}
, is a special case of the polylogarithm
Li_{n}(z)
for
n=3
. Note that the notation
Li_{3}(x)
for the trilogarithm is unfortunately similar to that for the logarithmic integral
Li(x)
.

Functional equations for the trilogarithm include

(2.1)

Li_{3}(z)+Li_{3}(-z)=\frac{1}{4}Li_{3}(z^{2})

(2.2)

Li_{3}(-z)-Li_{3}(-z^{-1})=-\frac{1}{6}(\log z)^{3}-\frac{1}{6\pi^{2}}\log z

(2.3)

Li_{3}(z)+Li_{3}(1-z)+Li_{3}(1-z^{-1})=\zeta(3)+\frac{1}{6}(\log z)^{3}+\frac{\pi^{2}}{6}\log z-\frac{1}{2}(\log z)^{2}\log(1-z).

Analytic values for
Li_{3}(x)
include

(2.4)

Li_{3}(-1)=-\frac{3}{4}\zeta(3),

(2.5)

Li_{3}(0)=0,

(2.6)

Li_{3}\left(\frac{1}{2}\right)=\frac{7}{8}\zeta(3)-\frac{1}{12}\pi^{2}\log 2+\frac{1}{6}(\log 2)^{3},

(2.7)

Li_{3}(1)=\zeta(3),

(2.8)

Li_{3}(\phi^{-2})=\frac{4}{5}\zeta(3)-\frac{2}{15}\pi^{2}\log\phi+\frac{2}{3}(\log\phi)^{3},

where
\zeta(3)
is Apéry’s constant and
\phi
is the golden ratio
\frac{1+\sqrt{5}}{2}
.

Immediate
ad hoc
examples substituting the above trilogarithm values from (
2.4
) to (
2.8
) are available from our known derived hyperquadrant identities. The 2D, 3D, 4D and 5D hyperquadrant identities are (
2.9
), (
2.10
), (
2.11
), (
2.12
) respectively.

If
|y|<1,|z|<1,
and
s+t=1
, then

(2.9)

\prod_{\begin{subarray}{c}(a,b)=1\\
a,b\geq 1\end{subarray}}\left(\frac{1}{1-y^{a}z^{b}}\right)^{\frac{1}{a^{s}b^{t}}}=\exp\left\{\left(\sum_{j=1}^{\infty}\frac{y^{j}}{j^{s}}\right)\left(\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}\frac{z^{k}}{k^{t}}\right)\right\}.

If
|x|<1,|y|<1,|z|<1
and
s+t+u=1
, then

(2.10)

\prod_{\begin{subarray}{c}(a,b,c)=1\\
a,b,c\geq 1\end{subarray}}\left(\frac{1}{1-x^{a}y^{b}z^{c}}\right)^{\frac{1}{a^{s}b^{t}c^{u}}}=\exp\left\{\left(\sum_{i=1}^{\infty}\frac{x^{i}}{i^{s}}\right)\left(\sum_{j=1}^{\infty}\frac{y^{j}}{j^{t}}\right)\left(\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}\frac{z^{k}}{k^{u}}\right)\right\}.

If
|w|<1,|x|<1,|y|<1,|z|<1
and
r+s+t+u=1
, then

(2.11)

\prod_{\begin{subarray}{c}(a,b,c,d)=1\\
a,b,c,d\geq 1\end{subarray}}\left(\frac{1}{1-w^{a}x^{b}y^{c}z^{d}}\right)^{\frac{1}{a^{r}b^{s}c^{t}d^{u}}}=\exp\left\{\left(\sum_{h=1}^{\infty}\frac{w^{h}}{h^{r}}\right)\left(\sum_{i=1}^{\infty}\frac{x^{i}}{i^{s}}\right)\left(\sum_{j=1}^{\infty}\frac{y^{j}}{j^{t}}\right)\left(\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}\frac{z^{k}}{k^{u}}\right)\right\}.

If
|v|<1,|w|<1,|x|<1,|y|<1,|z|<1
and
q+r+s+t+u=1
, then

(2.12)

\prod_{\begin{subarray}{c}(a,b,c,d,e)=1\\
a,b,c,d,e\geq 1\end{subarray}}\left(\frac{1}{1-v^{a}w^{b}x^{c}y^{d}z^{e}}\right)^{\frac{1}{a^{q}b^{r}c^{s}d^{t}e^{u}}}

=\exp\left\{\left(\sum_{g=1}^{\infty}\frac{v^{g}}{g^{q}}\right)\left(\sum_{h=1}^{\infty}\frac{w^{h}}{h^{r}}\right)\left(\sum_{i=1}^{\infty}\frac{x^{i}}{i^{s}}\right)\left(\sum_{j=1}^{\infty}\frac{y^{j}}{j^{t}}\right)\left(\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}\frac{z^{k}}{k^{u}}\right)\right\}.

2.1.
2D Hyperquadrant VPV Cases

Equation (
2.9
) has the following three example cases using our known sums for the trilogarithm and
Li_{2}(z)

If
|z|<1

\prod_{\begin{subarray}{c}(a,b)=1\\
a,b\geq 1\end{subarray}}\left(1-(-1)^{a}z^{b}\right)^{\frac{b^{2}}{a^{3}}}=\exp\left\{\frac{3z(1+z)\zeta(3)}{4(1-z)^{3}}\right\},

by (

If

\prod_{\begin{subarray}{c}(a,b)=1\\

2.8

Li_{-2}(z)

A good way to summarize what can be deduced from the 2D VPV first quadrant identity from known dilogarithm and trilogarithm sums is to use the two identities:
If
|y|<1,|z|<1,
then

(2.16)

\prod_{\begin{subarray}{c}(a,b)=1\\
a,b\geq 1\end{subarray}}\left(\frac{1}{1-y^{a}z^{b}}\right)^{\frac{b}{a^{2}}}=\exp\left\{\frac{zLi_{2}(y)}{(1-z)^{2}}\right\}

and

(2.17)

\prod_{\begin{subarray}{c}(a,b)=1\\

2.2.

4.

The Borweins contributed to experimentally calculated values of the multidimensional Mordell-Tornheim-Witten (MTW) zeta function.

(4.1)

\omega(s_{1},...,s_{K+1})=\sum_{m_{1},...,m_{K}>0}\frac{1}{{m_{1}}^{s_{1}}{m_{2}}^{s_{2}}\ldots{m_{K}}^{s_{K}}(m_{1}+m_{2}+\ldots+m_{K})^{s_{K+1}}}

\omega(s_{1},s_{2})=\sum_{m_{1}>0}\frac{1}{{m_{1}}^{s_{1}}(m_{1})^{s_{2}}},

(4.3)

\omega(s_{1},s_{2},s_{3})=\sum_{m_{1},\,m_{2}>0}\frac{1}{{m_{1}}^{s_{1}}{m_{2}}^{s_{2}}(m_{1}+m_{2})^{s_{3}}},

(4.4)

\omega(s_{1},s_{2},s_{3},s_{4})=\sum_{m_{1},\,m_{2},\,m_{3}>0}\frac{1}{{m_{1}}^{s_{1}}{m_{2}}^{s_{2}}{m_{3}}^{s_{3}}(m_{1}+m_{2}+m_{3})^{s_{4}}},

We begin by defining various special functions which we will later use. For

\Gamma(x):=\int_{0}^{\infty}e^{-t}t^{x-1}dt,\quad\Psi(x):=\frac{\Gamma^{\prime}(x)}{\Gamma(x)}.

We note that

\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\frac{x}{n(n-x)}:=-\Psi(1-x)-\gamma,\quad\textmd{where}\quad\gamma:=\lim_{N\rightarrow\infty}\left(\sum_{n=1}^{N}\frac{1}{n}-\log N\right)

\zeta(s):=\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\frac{1}{n^{s}}\quad\Re(s)>1,

defining a double Euler sum. The two-place function (

6.8

m+1

In fact, the above definitions can be extended to power series that also conform to the radial from origin region requirement for applying more general hyperquadrant and hyperpyramid VPV theorems.

There are many well known particular cases of the Euler sums as given in (
6.8
) to (
6.15

(6.19)

(6.20)

(6.21)

(6.22)

The sums (

6.21

Equation (
6.22
) is directly applicable to the VPV hyperpyramid identity case, which may be established from methods in Campbell
[
19
]

(6.23)

\prod_{\begin{subarray}{c}\gcd(j_{1},j_{2},\ldots,j_{m+1},k)=1\\
j_{1},j_{2},\ldots,j_{m+1}<k\\
j_{1},j_{2},\ldots,j_{m+1}\geq 0;k\geq 1\end{subarray}}\left(\frac{1}{1-y^{j_{1}+j_{2}+...+j_{m+1}}z^{k}}\right)^{\frac{1}{k}}\quad\quad

\quad\quad=\exp\left[\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}\left(\frac{y}{1}+\frac{y^{2}}{2}+\cdots+\frac{y^{k}}{k}\right)^{m+1}\frac{z^{k+1}}{(k+1)^{m}}\right].

Applying our three experimentally obtained sums (

(6.25)

\prod_{\begin{subarray}{c}\gcd(j_{1},j_{2},j_{3},j_{4},k)=1\\
j_{1},j_{2},j_{3},j_{4}<k\\
j_{1},j_{2},j_{3},j_{4}\geq 1;k\geq 2\end{subarray}}\left(\frac{1}{1-y^{j_{1}+j_{2}+j_{3}+j_{4}}z^{k}}\right)^{\frac{1}{k}}=\exp\left[\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}\left(\frac{y}{1}+\frac{y^{2}}{2}+\cdots+\frac{y^{k}}{k}\right)^{4}\frac{z^{k+1}}{(k+1)^{3}}\right].

(6.26)

\prod_{\begin{subarray}{c}\gcd(j_{1},j_{2},j_{3},j_{4},j_{5},k)=1\\
