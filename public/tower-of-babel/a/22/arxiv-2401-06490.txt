Generalized Distribution Function of Relaxation Times with the Davidson-Cole Model as a Kernel
Anis Allagui ∗, Ahmed Elwakil

Abstract
In this paper we propose a generalized distribution function of relaxation times (DFRT) considering the Davidson-Cole model as an elementary process instead of the standard Debye model. The distribution function is retrieved from the inverse of the generalized Stieltjes transform expressed in terms of iterated Laplace transforms. We derive computable analytical expressions of the generalized DFRT for some of the most known normalized impedance (or admittance) models including the constant phase element, the Davidson-Cole, Havriliak-Negami and the Kohlrausch-Williams-Watts models. Keywords: Fox H H -function; distribution of relaxation time; impedance spectroscopy.



I
Introduction

When a linear system is subjected to a frequency-domain input signal
X(s)\;(s=i\omega
,
\omega
is the angular frequency) and resulting in a frequency-domain output signal
Y(s)
, the transfer function describing the dynamics of the system can be written as the ratio:

Z(s)=\frac{Y(s)}{X(s)}

(1)

If
Z(s)
corresponds to an electrical impedance, then
Y(s)
is a voltage signal and
X(s)
is a current signal. When used for treating capacitive systems with a single time constant
\tau
, the function
Z(s)
is given by:

Z_{D}(s)=R_{s}+\frac{R_{p}}{1+s\tau}

(2)

where
R_{s}
and
R_{p}
are two resistive constants (in units of Ohm).
The fraction:

Q_{D}(s)=({1+s\tau})^{-1}

(3)

is the normalized or reduced Debye model, originally used for describing the frequency-dependent complex susceptibilities of dielectrics. In Nyquist representation of real vs. imaginary parts of impedance, Eq.
2
shows a symmetric semi-circular shape, while its time response obtained by inverse Laplace transform (LT) corresponds to an exponential decay function:

z_{D}(t)=R_{s}\,\delta(t)+R_{p}\,\tau^{-1}{e^{-t/\tau}}

(4)

The convolution of the time-domain functions
z_{D}(t)
with the current
i(t)
gives the resulting voltage
v(t)
as:

v(t)=(z_{D}\circledast i)(t)=\int\limits_{-\infty}^{+\infty}z_{D}(t-\xi)i(\xi)d\xi

(5)

However, if the system under consideration is more complex than the idealized one, e.g. when the system is porous, amorphous or consists of disordered, inhomogeneous structures, the Debye model is known to be insufficient for capturing accurately its frequency behavior.
By applying the principle of superposition, and with the assumption that linearity still holds
Florsch, Camerlynck, and Revil 2012
, the impedance can be expressed by the integral:

Z_{D}^{*}(s)=R_{s}+R_{p}\int\limits_{0}^{\infty}\frac{g(\tau)}{1+s\tau}d\tau

(6)

where
g(\tau)
is a distribution function of relaxation times (DFRT).
In normalized form, we rewrite Eq.
6
as:

Q_{D}^{*}(s)=\frac{Z_{D}^{*}(s)-R_{s}}{R_{p}}=\int\limits_{0}^{\infty}\frac{g(\tau)}{1+s\tau}d\tau

(7)

With this approach it is assumed that the relaxing macroscopic system consists of a large number of subsytems, each of which, upon the application of a step input, relaxes exponentially to zero with its own single relaxation time constant
Ciucci and Chen 2015
. There have been several efforts to construct reliable and robust numerical algorithms for retrieving the DFRT of a system from its spectral response (Eq.
7
), including Fourier transform techniques
Schichlein et al. 2002
;
Boukamp 2015
, Tikhonov regularization
Gavrilyuk, Osinkin, and Bronin 2017
;
Paul et al. 2021
;
Shanbhag 2020
, Max Entropy
Hörlin 1998
;
Hörlin 1993
, genetic algorithm
Tesler et al. 2010
, etc.
Boukamp 2020
;
Effendy, Song, and Bazant 2020
;
Liu and Ciucci 2020
;
Ciucci and Chen 2015
.

In general terms, Eq.
7
should be rather viewed as:

Q_{D}^{*}(s)=\int\limits_{0}^{\infty}{g(\tau)}k({s,\tau})d\tau

(8)

where
k({s,\tau})
is the kernel of the integral. In the case of the elementary Debye response, we have
k({s,\tau})=({1+s\tau})^{-1}
.
But there are several possible extensions of the Debye model that can be considered. These include for instance the Cole-Cole (CC) model
Cole and Cole 1941
,

Q_{\text{CC}}(s)=({1+(s\tau_{\alpha})^{\alpha}})^{-1}\quad(0<\alpha\leqslant 1)

(9)

the Davidson-Cole (DC) model
Davidson and Cole 1951
,

Q_{\text{DC}}(s)={(1+s\tau_{\beta})^{-\beta}}\quad(0<\beta\leqslant 1)

(10)

and the Havriliak-Negami (HN) model
Havriliak and Negami 1966
,

Q_{\text{HN}}(s)={(1+(s\tau_{{\nu}})^{\alpha})^{-\beta}}\quad(0<\alpha,\beta\leqslant 1)

(11)

which are
capable of describing a wide range of depressed and asymmetric semi-circular shapes in the complex plane with different levels of accuracy.
In the time domain the relaxation processes corresponding to these models are non-exponential. By inverse LT
Prabhakar 1971
, the response of the CC function to a step excitation is
Garrappa, Mainardi, and Maione 2016
:

z_{\text{CC}}(t)=\tau_{\alpha}^{-1}({t}/{\tau_{\alpha}})^{\alpha-1}E_{\alpha,\alpha}^{1}\left[-(t/\tau_{\alpha})^{\alpha}\right]

(12)

where
Prabhakar 1971
;
Saxena, Mathai, and Haubold 2004

{E}_{\alpha,\beta}^{\gamma}(z):=\sum\limits_{k=0}^{\infty}\frac{(\gamma)_{k}}{\Gamma(\alpha k+\beta)}\frac{z^{k}}{k!}\quad(\alpha,\beta,\gamma\in\mathbb{C},\mathrm{Re}({\alpha})>0)

(13)

(with
(\gamma)_{k}=\gamma(\gamma+1)\ldots(\gamma+k-1)=\Gamma(\gamma+k)/\Gamma(\gamma)
) is the three-parameter Mittag-Leffler (ML) function. The response functions of the DC and HN models are
Garrappa, Mainardi, and Maione 2016
:

\displaystyle z_{\text{DC}}(t)

\displaystyle=\tau_{\beta}^{-1}({t}/{\tau_{\beta}})^{\beta-1}E_{1,\beta}^{\beta}\left[-t/\tau_{\beta}\right]

(14)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\Gamma(\beta)}\tau_{\beta}^{-1}({t}/{\tau_{\beta}})^{\beta-1}e^{-t/\tau_{\beta}}

(15)

and

z_{\text{HN}}(t)=\tau_{\nu}^{-1}({t}/{\tau_{\nu}})^{\alpha\beta-1}E_{\alpha,\alpha\beta}^{\beta}\left[-(t/\tau_{\nu})^{\alpha}\right]

(16)

respectively.

Returning to Eq.
7
, Florsch, Revil, and Camerlynck
Florsch, Revil, and Camerlynck 2014
proposed for instance the possibility of using the HN model as the kernel of the integral. This gives for the normalized impedance function:

Q^{*}_{\text{HN}}(s)=\int\limits_{0}^{\infty}\frac{g(\tau)}{(1+(s\tau_{\nu})^{\alpha})^{\beta}}d\tau\quad(0<\alpha,\gamma\leqslant 1)

(17)

The problem here is to retrieve
g(\tau)
assuming that the parameters
\alpha
and
\beta
are known. The goal is that one may want to decompose the spectral response of the system using for example the Warburg model as a basis function. This can be obtained by setting
\beta=1
and
\alpha=0.5
in Eq.
17
. In any case the choice of values for the parameters
\alpha
and
\beta
should be justified in advance based on knowledge of the underlying physics.
The authors carried out their analysis for recovering the distribution function
g(\tau)
from Eq.
17
by numerical inversion algorithms given the ill-posedness of the problem.

In the same line of thought, the purpose of this work is to present a general procedure for obtaining
analytically
the DFRT associated with an impedance model when the elementary relaxation process is taken to be the DC model (Eq.
18
) instead of the Debye model. The motivation for this generalization applies for instance to macroscopic systems of self-similar character at a microscopic level, with the DC type of response in the frequency domain
Nigmatullin and Ryabov 1997
;
Elwakil, Allagui, and Psychalinos 2021
.

II
Theory

Consider the integral transform:

Q^{*}_{\text{DC}}(s)=\int\limits_{0}^{\infty}\frac{g(\tau)}{(1+s\tau)^{p}}d\tau

(18)

which is equivalent to Eq.
8
with the kernel
k({s,\tau})=({1+s\tau})^{-p}
.
We will drop both the subscript
"\text{DC}"
and the superscript
"^{*}"
from now on for ease of notation.
Using the change of variable
\lambda=\tau^{-1}
, and the distribution function:

D(\lambda)=\lambda^{p-2}g(\lambda^{-1})

(19)

Eq.
18
is rewritten as

Q(s)=\int\limits_{0}^{\infty}\frac{D(\lambda)}{(s+\lambda)^{p}}d\lambda

(20)

which is the generalized Stieltjes transform (ST)
Bateman 1954
. With
p=1
we recover the standard ST
Allagui and Elwakil 2024
.
Following the procedure of Schwarz
Schwarz 2005
,
by using the Mellin transform of a function
f(x)
defined as
\mathcal{M}[f(x);s]=\int_{0}^{\infty}x^{s-1}f(x)dx\;(x>0)
, we rewrite the kernel
{(s+\lambda)^{-p}}
in Eq.
20
as:

{(s+\lambda)^{-p}}=\frac{1}{\Gamma(p)}\int\limits_{0}^{\infty}x^{p-1}e^{-x(s+\lambda)}dx

(21)

Plugging it in Eq.
20
makes
Q(s)
to be:

Q(s)=\frac{1}{\Gamma(p)}\int\limits_{0}^{\infty}x^{p-1}e^{-xs}\int\limits_{0}^{\infty}D(\lambda)e^{-x\lambda}d\lambda\,dx

(22)

From the definition of the LT, we recognize that:

Q(s)=\frac{1}{\Gamma(p)}\mathcal{L}\left[x^{p-1}\mathcal{L}[D(\lambda);x];s\right]

(23)

and with the inverse LT, we recover
D(\lambda)
from the iterations:

D(\lambda)=\Gamma(p)\mathcal{L}^{-1}\left[x^{1-p}\mathcal{L}^{-1}[Q(s);x];\lambda\right]

(24)

The DFRT
g(\tau)
is then obtained from Eq.
19
as:

g(\tau)=\tau^{p-2}D(\tau^{-1})

(25)

We verify that for
p=1
,
D(\lambda)=\mathcal{L}^{-1}\left[\mathcal{L}^{-1}[Q(s);x];\lambda\right]
and
g(\tau)=\tau^{-1}D(\tau^{-1})
as they should be
Macdonald and Brachman 1956
;
Allagui and Elwakil 2024
.

Constant phase element

As a first example we consider the case of the reduced impedance of the constant phase element given by:

Q_{c}(s)={(s\tau_{c})^{-\alpha}},\quad 0<\alpha\leqslant 1

(26)

where
\tau_{c}
is a positive constant. This is basically the case of the CC model in Eq.
9
when
s\tau_{\alpha}\gg 1
. Using Eq.
24
followed by Eq.
25
we obtain:

g_{c}(\tau)=\frac{\Gamma(p)}{\Gamma(\alpha)\Gamma(p-\alpha)}\tau_{c}^{-\alpha}\tau^{\alpha-1},\;\;p>\alpha

Given that for physical reasons the distribution function
g(\tau)
cannot be negative, we need to ensure the condition
0<\alpha<p<1
. Otherwise, the term
{\Gamma(p)}/[{\Gamma(\alpha)\Gamma(p-\alpha)}]

We note that for

(i.e. Debye model as a kernel in Eq.

simplifies to

g_{c}(\tau)=\pi^{-1}\sin(\alpha\pi)\tau_{c}^{-\alpha}\tau^{\alpha-1}

(28)

where we used Euler’s reflection formula for the gamma function,
{\pi}/{\sin(\pi z)}=\Gamma(z)\Gamma(1-z)
(
z\notin\mathbb{Z}
).
This means that a CPE impedance of parameters
\tau_{c}
and
\alpha
can equally be constructed from the integral:

Q_{c}(s)={(s\tau_{c})^{-\alpha}}=\frac{\tau_{c}^{-\alpha}}{\Gamma(\alpha)\Gamma(1-\alpha)}\int\limits_{0}^{\infty}\frac{\tau^{\alpha-1}}{1+s\tau}d\tau

(29)

using the Debye model as a basis function
Allagui and Elwakil 2024
, or from the integral:

Q_{c}(s)={(s\tau_{c})^{-\alpha}}=\frac{\Gamma(p)\,\tau_{c}^{-\alpha}}{\Gamma(\alpha)\Gamma(p-\alpha)}\int\limits_{0}^{\infty}\frac{\tau^{\alpha-1}}{(1+s\tau)^{p}}d\tau

(30)

using the DC model (of any parameter
p>\alpha
) as a basis function.

III.3
Havriliak–Negami model

Now we show the inversion procedure for the the case of the HN model, i.e.:

Q_{\nu}(s)={(1+(s\tau_{\nu})^{\alpha})^{-\beta}}=\int\limits_{0}^{\infty}\frac{g_{\nu}(\tau)}{(1+s\tau)^{p}}d\tau

(35)

from which the CC model can be a recovered as a special case by setting
\beta=1
.
For this example, it is more convenient to use the representation of
Q_{\nu}(s)
in terms of the Fox
H
-function, as we have shown in more detail in a separate contribution of ours
Allagui and Elwakil 2024
for the case of
k({s,\tau})=({1+s\tau})^{-1}
.
We recall that the
H
-function of order

(m,n,p,q)\in\mathbb{N}^{4}
, (
0\leqslant n\leqslant p
,
1\leqslant m\leqslant q
)
and with parameters

A_{j}\in\mathbb{R}_{+}\;(j=1,\ldots,p)
,
B_{j}\in\mathbb{R}_{+}\;(j=1,\ldots,q)
,

a_{j}\in\mathbb{C}\;(j=1,\ldots,p)
and
b_{j}\in\mathbb{C}\;(j=1,\ldots,q)

is defined for
z\in\mathbb{C},\;z\neq 0
by the contour integral
Fox 1961
;
Mathai, Saxena, and Haubold 2009
;
Kilbas 2004
;
Mathai et al. 1978
:

H^{m,n}_{p,q}\left[z|^{(a_{1},A_{1}),\ldots,(a_{p},A_{p})}_{(b_{1},B_{1}),\ldots,(b_{q},B_{q})}\right]=\frac{1}{2\pi i}\int_{L}h(s)z^{-s}ds

(36)

where the integrand
h(s)
is given by:

h(s)=\frac{\left\{\prod\limits_{j=1}^{m}\Gamma(b_{j}+B_{j}s)\right\}\left\{\prod\limits_{j=1}^{n}\Gamma(1-a_{j}-A_{j}s)\right\}}{\left\{\prod\limits_{j={m+1}}^{q}\Gamma(1-b_{j}-B_{j}s)\right\}\left\{\prod\limits_{j={n+1}}^{p}\Gamma(a_{j}+A_{j}s)\right\}}

(37)

In Eq.
36
,
z^{-s}=\exp\left[-s(\ln|z|+i\arg z)\right]
and
\arg z
is not necessarily the principal value.
The contour of integration
L
is a suitable contour separating the poles
of
\Gamma(b_{j}+B_{j}s)
(
j=1,\ldots,m
) from the poles
of
\Gamma(1-a_{j}-A_{j}s)
(
j=1,\ldots,n
).
An empty product is always interpreted as unity.
We rewrite
Q_{\nu}(s)
as

Q_{\nu}(s)=\frac{1}{\Gamma(\beta)}H^{1,1}_{1,1}\left[(s\tau_{\nu})^{\alpha}\left|\begin{array}[]{c}(1-\beta,1)\\
(0,1)\hfill\\
\end{array}\right.\right]

(38)

using the formula
Mathai, Saxena, and Haubold 2009

\displaystyle(1-z)^{-\alpha}=\frac{1}{\Gamma(\alpha)}H^{1,1}_{1,1}\left[-z\left|\begin{array}[]{c}(1-\alpha,1)\\
(0,1)\hfill\\
\end{array}\right.\right]

Knowing that the LT of an
H
-function is again an
H
-function, and is given by
Hilfer 2002
;
Glöckle and Nonnenmacher 1993
;
Mathai, Saxena, and Haubold 2009

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}

\displaystyle\left[t^{\rho-1}H_{p,q}^{m,n}\left[at^{\sigma}\left|\begin{array}[]{c}(a_{p},A_{p})\\
(b_{q},B_{q})\\
\end{array}\right.\right];u\right]

\displaystyle=u^{-\rho}H^{m,n+1}_{p+1,q}\left[au^{-\sigma}\left|\begin{array}[]{c}(1-\rho,\sigma),(a_{1},A_{1}),\ldots,(a_{p},A_{p})\\
(b_{1},B_{1}),\ldots,(b_{q},B_{q})\\
\end{array}\right.\right]

and the inverse LT is given by
Mathai, Saxena, and Haubold 2009

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}^{-1}

\displaystyle\left[u^{-\rho}H_{p,q}^{m,n}\left[au^{\sigma}\left|\begin{array}[]{c}(a_{p},A_{p})\\
(b_{q},B_{q})\\
\end{array}\right.\right];t\right]

\displaystyle=t^{\rho-1}H_{p+1,q}^{m,n}\left[at^{-\sigma}\left|\begin{array}[]{c}(a_{p},A_{p}),\ldots,(a_{1},A_{1}),(\rho,\sigma)\\
(b_{1},B_{1}),\ldots,(b_{q},B_{q})\\
\end{array}\right.\right]

we find, after employing some identities between

-functions that

;
Mathai, Saxena, and Haubold 2009

\end{array}\right.\right]

(49)

This general expression includes as special cases the DFRT for the DC and CC reduced impedances for different values of

III.4
Kohlrausch-Williams-Watts model

We conclude with the Kohlrausch-Williams-Watts (KWW) model as a fourth example of our study
Williams and Watts 1970
. The KWW model is actually introduced starting from its time-domain relaxation function, which is given in normalized form by
Garrappa, Mainardi, and Maione 2016
:

\rho_{\kappa}(t)=e^{-(t/\tau_{\kappa})^{\kappa}},\;\;t\geqslant 0,\;0<\kappa\leqslant 1

(53)

This function is also known as the stretched exponential function with an exponent
\kappa
and time constant
\tau_{\kappa}
. While the response function
z(t)
and the normalized impedance function
Q(s)
are a LT pair,
\rho_{\kappa}(t)
and
Q(s)
are related via
Garrappa, Mainardi, and Maione 2016

\rho_{\kappa}(t)=1-\mathcal{L}^{-1}[Q_{\kappa}(s)/s;t]=\mathcal{L}^{-1}[(1-Q_{\kappa}(s))/s;t]

(54)

This comes from
z(t)=-d\rho(t)/dt
.
With the
H
-function identity:

e^{-(t/\tau_{\kappa})^{\kappa}}=H^{1,0}_{0,1}\left[(t/\tau_{\kappa})^{\kappa}\left|\begin{array}[]{c}-\hfill\\
(0,1)\hfill\\
\end{array}\right.\right]

(55)

one can obtain by applying the LT (Eq.
III.3
) to both sides of Eq.

Hilfer 2002
:

Q_{\kappa}(s)=1-H^{1,1}_{1,1}\left[(s\tau_{\kappa})^{\kappa}\left|\begin{array}[]{c}(1,1)\hfill\\
(1,\kappa)\hfill\\
\end{array}\right.\right]

(56)

From Garrappa et al.

, it can be shown that the LT of the relaxation function being

is in fact the ST of its corresponding spectral function. However, in order for us to apply directly Eq.

z_{\kappa}(t)=-\frac{d\rho_{\kappa}(t)}{dt}=(\kappa/\tau_{\kappa})(t/\tau_{\kappa})^{\kappa-1}e^{-(t/\tau_{\kappa})^{\kappa}}

Applying the LT we verify that
Q_{\kappa}(s)
is also equal to:

Q_{\kappa}(s)=\kappa\,H^{1,1}_{1,1}\left[(s\tau_{\kappa})^{\kappa}\left|\begin{array}[]{c}(0,1)\hfill\\
(0,\kappa)\hfill\\
\end{array}\right.\right]

(58)

We then find from Eq.
24
and Eq.
25
:

\displaystyle g_{\kappa}(\tau)

\displaystyle={\kappa\,\Gamma(p)}{\tau^{-1}}H^{1,1}_{2,2}\left[(\tau/\tau_{\kappa})^{\kappa}\left|\begin{array}[]{c}(1,\kappa),(p,\kappa)\\
(1,1),(1,\kappa)\hfill\\
\end{array}\right.\right]

\displaystyle={\kappa\,\Gamma(p)}{\tau^{-1}}H^{1,0}_{1,1}\left[(\tau/\tau_{\kappa})^{\kappa}\left|\begin{array}[]{c}(p,\kappa)\\
(1,1)\hfill\\
\end{array}\right.\right]

Conclusion

A generalized distribution function of relaxation times based on the elementary Davidson-Cole model for treating impedance functions is presented. The inversion procedure involves the generalized Stieltjes transform, which can be executed relatively easily using the Fox’s
H
-function and its properties. We tested and validated the procedure for a few cases of well-known functions.
