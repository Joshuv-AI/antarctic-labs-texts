Communication-Constrained Bandits under Additive Gaussian Noise
Prathamesh Mayekar, Jonathan Scarlett, Vincent Y.F. Tan

Abstract
We study a distributed stochastic multi-armed bandit where a client supplies the learner with communication-constrained feedback based on the rewards for the corresponding arm pulls. In our setup, the client must encode the rewards such that the second moment of the encoded rewards is no more than P P , and this encoded reward is further corrupted by additive Gaussian noise of variance σ 2 \sigma^{2} ; the learner only has access to this corrupted reward. For this setting, we derive an information-theoretic lower bound of Ω ⁡ ( K ​ T 𝚂𝙽𝚁 ∧ 1 ) \Omega\left(\sqrt{\frac{KT}{\mathtt{SNR}\wedge 1}}\right) on the minimax regret of any scheme, where 𝚂𝙽𝚁 ≔ P σ 2 \mathtt{SNR}\coloneqq\frac{P}{\sigma^{2}} , and K K and T T are the number of arms and time horizon, respectively. Furthermore, we propose a multi-phase bandit algorithm, 𝚄𝙴 ​ - ​ 𝚄𝙲𝙱 ​ ++ \mathtt{UE}\text{-}\mathtt{UCB}\text{++} , which matches this lower bound to a minor additive factor. 𝚄𝙴 ​ - ​ 𝚄𝙲𝙱 ​ ++ \mathtt{UE}\text{-}\mathtt{UCB}\text{++} performs uniform exploration in its initial phases and then utilizes the upper confidence bound (UCB) bandit algorithm in its final phase. An interesting feature of 𝚄𝙴 ​ - ​ 𝚄𝙲𝙱 ​ ++ \mathtt{UE}\text{-}\mathtt{UCB}\text{++} is that the coarser estimates of the mean rewards formed during a uniform exploration phase help to refine the encoding protocol in the next phase, leading to more accurate mean estimates of the rewards in the subsequent phase. This positive reinforcement cycle is critical to reducing the number of uniform exploration rounds and closely matching our lower bound.

Introduction

The
stochastic multi-armed bandit
(SMAB) is a classic sequential decision-making problem with decades of research (see
[
15
]

The SMAB problem crucially captures the trade-off between exploring actions to find the optimal one or exploiting the action believed to be the best, but possibly sub optimal action, lying at the heart of many practical sequential decision-making problems. In its simplest form, the SMAB problem is a sequential game between the learner and the environment. The game is played over a finite number of rounds. In each round, the learner plays an arm from a given set of arms, and then the environment reveals a reward corresponding to the played arm. The reward for a particular arm is
iid
across rounds, with the reward distribution unknown to the learner. The learner’s goal is to maximize the expected cumulative reward over the entirety of the game.

In another direction, in modern distributed optimization frameworks such as Federated Learning
[

[
13

An emerging abstraction to understand the trade-off between communication and accuracy in distributed optimization is to understand the one-client setting, where one remote client supplies the learner with gradient updates over a rate-limited channel (see, for instance,

In most distributed learning settings, the communication from the learner to client (downlink communication) is not expensive. Theorem 4.2
.

For all bandit instances
\nu\in\mathcal{E}
, the
\mathtt{UE}\text{-}\mathtt{UCB}
algorithm along with
\mathtt{CAS}
encoding as described in Algorithm (
2
) satisfies the power constraint (
1
) at all rounds and has the following regret guarantee:

\displaystyle R_{T}(\nu,\mathtt{UE}\text{-}\mathtt{UCB},\mathtt{CAS})\leq\frac{2KB^{3}}{\mathtt{SNR}}+8KB+8\sqrt{\frac{2}{\mathtt{SNR}}+1}\sqrt{KT\ln T}.

Proof.

In the first phase, we incur regret linear regret in the time horizon. Specifically, since the time horizon for the first phase is
K\tau=K(B^{2}/\mathtt{SNR}+1)
and the gap between the mean of the optimal arm and the suboptimal arm can at most be
2B
, we have that the regret incurred in the first phase is
(KB^{2}/\mathtt{SNR}+K)\cdot 2B.
Moreover, since the encoding of rewards on the client’s side and decoding of the reward on the learner’s side in the first phase is same as in Algorithm
1
, by the same arguments, the power constraint is satisfied, and for all
t\in\{(k-1)\tau+1,\ldots,k\tau\},

\mathbb{E}\left[(\tilde{X}_{t,k}-\mu_{k})^{2}\right]\leq\frac{B^{2}}{\mathtt{SNR}}+1.

(5)

For the second phase, we will first show that the power constraint is satisfied. Towards proving this, we will bound the variance of the mean estimates formed after the first phase. We have from the description of Algorithm
2
that for all
k\in K,

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[(\hat{\mu}_{k}-\mu_{k})^{2}\right]

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\tau^{2}}\sum_{t=(k-1)\tau+1}^{k\tau}\mathbb{E}\left[(\tilde{X}_{t,k}-\mu)^{2}\right]\leq 1,

where the inequality follows from (
5
) and the fact that
\tau=\frac{B^{2}}{\mathtt{SNR}}+1.

Now, note that for any round
t
in phase 2, we have

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[(X_{t,A_{t}}-\hat{\mu}_{A_{t}})^{2}\right]

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}\left[(X_{t,A_{t}}-\mu_{A_{t}})^{2}\right]+\mathbb{E}\left[(\hat{\mu}_{A_{t}}-\mu_{A_{t}})^{2}\right]\leq 2,

where the equality follows from the independence of
X_{t,A_{t}}
and
\hat{\mu}_{A_{t}},
and the inequality follows from the fact that both
(X_{t,A_{t}}-\mu_{A_{t}})
and
(\hat{\mu}_{A_{t}}-\mu_{A_{t}})
are subgaussian with a variance factor of
1
.

Therefore, scaling the centered reward
X_{t,k}-\hat{\mu}_{k}
by
\frac{\sqrt{P}}{\sqrt{2}}
satisfies the power constraint.

The reward estimate used by the learner in phase 2 is

\displaystyle\tilde{X}_{t,A_{t}}

\displaystyle=\frac{\sqrt{2}Y_{t}}{\sqrt{P}}+\hat{\mu}_{A_{t}}

\displaystyle=X_{t,A_{t}}+\frac{\sqrt{2}Z_{t}}{\sqrt{P}}.

This implies that the centered reward estimate,
\tilde{X}_{t,A_{t}}-\mu_{A_{t}}
, is subgaussian with a variance factor of
2/\mathtt{SNR}+1
in the second phase. The proof is complete by upper bounding the regret in the second phase by using Lemma
2.2
and summing the regret in the two phases.

Concluding Remarks

The near-optimal Algorithm
3
use a highly adaptive encoding protocol, where the history of the game was used to refine the encoding in a particular round. On the other hand,
[
1
]
showed for distributed convex, Lipschitz optimization under information constraints, a fixed, non-adaptive encoding protocol gives optimal performance. This suggests an interesting distinction between encoding for distributed sequential decision-making problems and distributed optimization problems.

An interesting open problem is closing the additive
\frac{8KB\log B}{\mathtt{SNR}\wedge 1}
gap between our upper and lower bound. Furthermore, the refinement to our model where the learner-to-client communication is also over an AWGN channel and the multi-client setting are interesting future research directions.

Appendix A
Proof of Theorem
4.3
(Regret bound of
\mathtt{UE}\text{-}\mathtt{UCB}\text{++}
)

We will first show that the centered rewards transmitted by the client satisfy the power constraint. Note that the centered reward coded by the client at any round in the
\ell
th sub-phase of phase 1 is
X_{t,A_{t}}-\hat{\mu}_{\ell-1,A_{t}}
. Furthermore, the client scales the reward by a factor of
\frac{\sqrt{P}}{B_{\ell}}
. Thus, we need to show that
\mathbb{E}\left[(X_{t,A_{t}}-\hat{\mu}_{\ell-1,A_{t}})^{2}\right]\leq B^{2}_{\ell}
.
Towards proving this, we write

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[(X_{t,A_{t}}-\hat{\mu}_{\ell-1,A_{t}})^{2}\right]

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}\left[(X_{t,A_{t}}-\mu_{A_{t}})^{2}\right]+\mathbb{E}\left[(\hat{\mu}_{\ell-1,A_{t}}-\mu_{A_{t}})^{2}\right]

\displaystyle\leq 1+\mathbb{E}\left[(\hat{\mu}_{\ell-1,A_{t}}-\mu_{A_{t}})^{2}\right].

From the description of Algorithm
3
, for
k\in[K],
we have

\displaystyle\hat{\mu}_{\ell-1,k}=\frac{1}{\tau}\sum_{t=(\ell-2)K\tau+(k-1)\tau+1}^{(\ell-2)K\tau+k\tau}\left(X_{t,k}+\frac{B_{\ell-1}}{\sqrt{P}}Z_{t}\right).

Therefore, we have

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[(\hat{\mu}_{\ell,A_{t}}-\mu_{A_{t}})^{2}\right]\leq\frac{\frac{B_{\ell-1}^{2}}{\mathtt{SNR}}+1}{\tau}.

Using the preceding inequalities and definition of
B_{\ell}
in (
7
), we get

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[(X_{t,A_{t}}-\mu_{\ell-1,A_{t}})^{2}\right]\leq 1+\frac{\frac{B_{\ell-1}^{2}}{\mathtt{SNR}}+1}{\tau}=B_{\ell}^{2}.

To prove that the power constraint is satisfied in the second phase, note that the centered reward transmitted by the client in the second phase is
X_{t,A_{t}}-\hat{\mu}_{L,A_{t}}.
Furthermore, client scales the reward by a factor of
\frac{\sqrt{P}}{B_{L+1}}.
By precisely the same arguments as above, we can deduce that
\mathbb{E}\left[(X_{t,A_{t}}-\hat{\mu}_{L,A_{t}})^{2}\right]\leq B_{L+1}^{2}
.

To bound the regret, first note that we incur linear regret in phase 1 of
\mathtt{UE}\text{-}\mathtt{UCB}\text{++}
. Specifically, since the time horizon for phase 1 is
\frac{4K\log B}{\mathtt{SNR}\wedge 1}
and each round’s regret is at the most
2B
, we incur a total regret of
\frac{8KB\log B}{\mathtt{SNR}\wedge 1}
in the first phase.

To bound the regret in the second phase, note that the centered reward estimate
\tilde{X}_{t,A_{t}}-\mu_{A_{t}}
in the second phase is subgaussian with a variance factor of
\frac{B^{2}_{L+1}}{\mathtt{SNR}}+1.
Substituting the value of
\tau
from (
6
) in (
7
), we obtain

\displaystyle B^{2}_{\ell+1}

\displaystyle=\frac{\frac{B^{2}_{\ell}}{\mathtt{SNR}}+1}{\tau}+1

\displaystyle\leq\frac{B^{2}_{\ell}}{2}+\frac{\mathtt{SNR}\wedge 1}{2}+1.

Rearranging the terms, we have

\displaystyle B^{2}_{\ell+1}-2\left(\frac{\mathtt{SNR}\wedge 1}{2}+1\right)\leq\frac{B^{2}_{\ell}-2\left(\frac{\mathtt{SNR}\wedge 1}{2}+1\right)}{2}.

Recursively applying the above identity, it follows that

\displaystyle B^{2}_{L+1}-2\left(\frac{\mathtt{SNR}\wedge 1}{2}+1\right)\leq\frac{B^{2}-2\left(\frac{\mathtt{SNR}\wedge 1}{2}+1\right)}{2^{L}}.

Substituting
L=2\log B,
we get
B^{2}_{L+1}
less than
4
.

The proof is then completed by using Lemma
2.2
to bound the regret in the second phase and adding the regret from the first phase.

\mu_{i}=0
for all
i\in\{2,\ldots,K\}\setminus\{j\}.
Let
Q^{T}
be the the probability distribution of the sequence
(A_{1},Y_{1},A_{2},Y_{2},\ldots,A_{T},Y_{T})
induced by the algorithm
\pi
and encoding strategy
C
on the instance
\nu^{\prime}
.

By a simple calculation, we have

R_{T}(\nu,\pi,C)\geq\frac{T\Delta}{2}P^{T}(N_{1}(T)\leq T/2)\quad\text{and}\quad R_{T}(\nu^{\prime},\pi,C)\geq\frac{T\Delta}{2}Q^{T}(N_{1}(T)>T/2).

Combining the two inequalities, using the variational formula for total variation distance, and applying Pinsker’s inequality, we get

\displaystyle R_{T}(\nu,\pi,C)+R_{T}(\nu^{\prime},\pi,C)

\displaystyle\geq\frac{T\Delta}{2}\left(P^{T}(N_{1}(T)\leq T/2)+Q^{T}(N_{1}(T)>T/2)\right)

\displaystyle=\frac{T\Delta}{2}\left(1-(P^{T}(N_{1}(T)>T/2)-Q^{T}(N_{1}(T)>T/2))\right)

\displaystyle\geq\frac{T\Delta}{2}\left(1-\mathrm{d_{TV}}(P^{T},Q^{T})\right)

\displaystyle\geq\frac{T\Delta}{2}\left(1-\sqrt{\frac{1}{2}D(P^{T}\mid\mid Q^{T})}\right).

(10)

Note that
P^{T}
and
Q^{T}
differ only when arm
j
is played.
Recall that the dependence of
Y
on an encoding strategy
C
and reward
X
is given by (
2
). For encoding scheme
C
, used in a round
t
, denote by
C(\cdot\mid x)
the conditional distribution of
Y
given that the reward is
X=x
. Let
p_{j}\circ C
and
q_{j}\circ C
denote the distribution of
Y
when arm
j
is played at round
t
. Recalling the reward distribution for arm
j
, it follows that,

\displaystyle(p_{j}\circ C)(Y=y)

\displaystyle=\frac{C(y\mid 1)}{2}+\frac{C(y\mid-1)}{2}\quad\text{and}

\displaystyle(q_{j}\circ C)(Y=y)

\displaystyle=\frac{C(y\mid 1)}{2}+\frac{C(y\mid-1)}{2}+\Delta(C(y\mid 1)-C(y\mid-1)).

Moreover, using similar arguments as in
[
15
, Lemma 15.1]
, we can prove the following lemma.

Lemma B.1
.

D(P^{T}\mid\mid Q^{T})\leq\mathbb{E}_{P^{T}}\left[N_{j}(T)\right]\cdot\sup_{C\in\mathcal{C}}D(p_{j}\circ C\mid\mid q_{j}\circ C)

We defer the proof the Lemma to Appendix
C
and proceed with proving the lower bound. Combining Lemma
B.1
with (
9
), we have

\displaystyle D(P^{T}\mid\mid Q^{T})

\displaystyle\leq\frac{T}{K-1}\sup_{C\in\mathcal{C}}D(p_{j}\circ C\mid\mid q_{j}\circ C).

We will now use the following fact.

Fact 1
.

Recall that
d_{\chi^{2}}(p,q)\coloneqq\int\frac{(p(x)-q(x))^{2}}{q(x)}\mathrm{d}x
denotes the
\chi^{2}
distance between probability measures
p
and
q
when they are absolutely continuous with respect to the Lebesgue measure
\mathrm{d}x
. Then, it holds that

D(p\mid\mid q)\leq d_{\chi^{2}}(p,q).

Fact
1
is well known (see, for instance
[
7
]
]), but we provide a short proof in Appendix
C
for completeness.

Using this fact,
D(p_{j}\circ C\mid\mid q_{j}\circ C)
can be bounded as follows:

\displaystyle\mathrm{D}(p_{i}\circ C\mid\mid q_{i}\circ C)

\displaystyle\leq d_{\chi^{2}}(p_{i}\circ C,q_{i}\circ C)

\displaystyle=\int\frac{(p_{i}\circ C-q_{i}\circ C)^{2}}{q_{i}\circ C}\,\mathrm{d}y

\displaystyle=\Delta^{2}\int\frac{(C(y\mid 1)-C(y\mid-1))^{2}}{\frac{C(y\mid 1)}{2}+\frac{C(y\mid-1)}{2}+\Delta(C(y\mid 1)-C(y\mid-1))}\,\mathrm{d}y

Now note that for
\Delta\leq 1/4,
we have

\frac{C(y\mid 1)}{2}+\frac{C(y\mid-1)}{2}+\Delta(C(y\mid 1)-C(y\mid-1))\geq\frac{C(y\mid 1)}{4}+\frac{C(y\mid-1)}{4}.
Using this fact, we get

\displaystyle\mathrm{D}(p_{i}\circ C\mid\mid q_{i}\circ C)

\displaystyle\leq 2\Delta^{2}\int\frac{(C(y\mid 1)-C(y\mid-1))^{2}}{\frac{C(y\mid 1)}{2}+\frac{C(y\mid-1)}{2}}\,\mathrm{d}y

\displaystyle=2\Delta^{2}\left(\frac{1}{2}d_{\chi^{2}}(C(\cdot\mid 1),C(\cdot\mid 1)/2+C(\cdot\mid-1)/2)\right.

\displaystyle\hskip 85.35826pt\left.+\frac{1}{2}d_{\chi^{2}}(C(\cdot\mid-1),C(\cdot\mid 1)/2+C(\cdot\mid-1)/2)\right)

\displaystyle\leq 4\Delta^{2}\left(\frac{1}{2}\mathrm{D}(C(\cdot\mid 1)\mid\mid C(\cdot\mid 1)/2+C(\cdot\mid-1)/2)\right.

\displaystyle\hskip 85.35826pt\left.+\frac{1}{2}\mathrm{D}(C(\cdot\mid-1)\mid\mid C(\cdot\mid 1)/2+C(\cdot\mid-1)/2)\right),

where the equality is easily verified by substituting the definition
d_{\chi^{2}}
and the last inequality uses the following fact.

To proceed further, we will use the following fact.

Fact 2
.

If
\frac{\mathrm{d}P}{\mathrm{d}Q}(x)\leq c\quad\forall x\in\mathbb{R}
, then

d_{\chi^{2}}(P,Q)\leq cD(P\mid\mid Q).

Fact 2 is also known
[
8
]
, but we provide a proof in Appendix
C
for completeness.

Denote by
V
a hypothetical reward that takes values
-1
and
1
with equal probability, and by
Y
the output of the AWGN channel when
V
is further coded to
C(V)
to satisfy (
1
) and transmitted over the AWGN channel. Then,

\frac{1}{2}\mathrm{D}(C(\cdot\mid 1)\mid\mid C(\cdot\mid 1)/2+C(\cdot\mid-1)/2)+\frac{1}{2}\mathrm{D}(C(\cdot\mid-1)\mid\mid C(\cdot\mid 1)/2+C(\cdot\mid-1)/2)

is mutual information between
V
and
Y
, denoted by
I(V;Y).
To see this, note that
I(W_{1};W_{2})\coloneqq D(P_{W_{1},W_{2}}\mid\mid P_{W_{1}}\times P_{W_{2}})=\mathbb{E}_{P_{W_{1}}}\left[D(P_{W_{2}|W_{1}}\mid\mid P_{W_{2}})\right].

We have therefore shown that

\displaystyle D(P^{T}\mid\mid Q^{T})\leq\frac{4\Delta^{2}T}{K-1}\sup_{C\in\mathcal{C}}I(V;Y).

(11)

Note that
I(V;Y)\leq H(V)\leq\ln 2.
Since
V-C(V)-Y
forms a Markov chain in this order, by the data processing inequality
[
6
, Chapter 2]
, we have that

\displaystyle I(V;Y)\leq I(C(V);Y).

Then, using the fact that

can be further upper bounded by the capacity of an AWGN channel
[
6
, Chapter 9]
, we have

\displaystyle I(C(V);Y)\leq\frac{1}{2}\ln(1+\mathtt{SNR})=\frac{1}{2}\log(1+\mathtt{SNR})\cdot\ln 2.

Thus, we have shown that

\displaystyle D(P^{T}\mid\mid Q^{T})\leq\frac{4\ln 2\cdot\Delta^{2}T}{K-1}\left(\frac{1}{2}\log(1+\mathtt{SNR})\wedge 1\right).

Substituting this into (
10
), we get

\displaystyle R_{T}(\nu,\pi,C)+R_{T}(\nu^{\prime},\pi,C)\geq\frac{T\Delta}{2}\left(1-\sqrt{\frac{4\ln 2\cdot\Delta^{2}T}{K-1}\left(\frac{1}{2}\log(1+\mathtt{SNR})\wedge 1\right)}\right).

Setting
\Delta^{2}=\frac{K-1}{16\ln 2\cdot T\left(\frac{1}{2}\log(1+\mathtt{SNR})\wedge 1\right)}
completes the proof. (Note that we can only set such a
\Delta
and ensure that
\Delta\leq\frac{1}{4}
, if
T\geq\frac{K-1}{\ln 2\left(\frac{1}{2}\log(1+\mathtt{SNR})\wedge 1\right)},
which is the reason we need this condition in the statement of our lower bound.)

We will now show that
for
T\geq\frac{K-1}{\ln 2\left(\frac{1}{2}\log(1+\mathtt{SNR})\wedge 1\right)}\geq K
and for a universal constant
c,
we have

R_{T}^{\ast}(\mathtt{SNR})\geq cKB.

In fact, we show the stronger bound

R_{K}^{\ast}(\mathtt{SNR})\geq cKB.

Then, using the fact that
a\vee b\geq\frac{a+b}{2}
completes the proof.

We now choose our instance
\nu
where rewards for each arm pull are Gaussian with variance
1
and the following means:
\mu_{1}=1,

\mu_{i}=0
for all
i\in\{2,\ldots,K\}.
Once again, let
j
be the arm pulled least amount of times. Then define the alternate instance
\nu^{\prime}

and the following means:
\mu_{1}=B/2,\mu_{j}=B-1,\mu_{i}=0
for all

All the arm rewards have Gaussian distribution with the variance 1 and means stated above. Proceeding as earlier, we have

\displaystyle R_{T}(\nu,\pi,C)+R_{T}(\nu^{\prime},\pi,C)

\displaystyle\geq\frac{T\Delta}{2}\left(1-\mathrm{d_{TV}}(P^{T},Q^{T})\right)

Using the fact that

Proof of Lemma

We prove the inequality by similar arguments as in
[
15
, Lemma 15.1]
.

Recall that
H_{t}=\{A_{1},Y_{1},\ldots,A_{t},Y_{t}\}
denotes the history of the game till time
t
. Also recall that the hint
S_{t}
is a function of history of the game till time
t-1
. Therefore the encoding scheme at time
t
depends on
H_{t-1}
, so we write it as
C_{H_{t-1}}.
We additionally denote by
p_{A_{t}}
and
q_{A_{t}}
the distribution of reward
X_{t,A_{t}}
under instances
\nu
and
\nu^{\prime},
respectively. Therefore,
p_{A_{t}}\circ C_{H_{t-1}}
and
q_{A_{t}}\circ C_{H_{t-1}}
denote distribution of
Y_{t}
for an arm pull
A_{t}
and history
H_{t-1}
under instances
\nu
and
\nu^{\prime},
respectively.

Using the definition of KL divergence, we have

\displaystyle D(P^{T}\mid\mid Q^{T})

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}_{P^{T}}\left[\log\left(\frac{P^{T}(A_{1},Y_{1},\ldots,A_{T},Y_{T})}{Q^{T}(A_{1},Y_{1},\ldots,A_{T},Y_{T})}\right)\right]

\displaystyle=\sum_{t=1}^{T}\mathbb{E}_{P^{T}}\left[\log\frac{P^{T}(A_{t},Y_{t}\mid H_{t-1})}{Q^{T}(A_{t},Y_{t}\mid H_{t-1})}\right].

(12)

By the definition of the bandit algorithm
\pi,
the distribution over all actions to be played remains the same for different problem instances as long as the history is the same. Therefore,

\displaystyle\frac{P^{T}(A_{t},Y_{t}\mid H_{t-1})}{Q^{T}(A_{t},Y_{t}\mid H_{t-1})}

\displaystyle=\frac{P^{T}(A_{t}\mid H_{t-1})}{Q^{T}(A_{t}\mid H_{t-1})}\cdot\frac{P^{T}(Y_{t}\mid A_{t},H_{t-1})}{Q^{T}(Y_{t}\mid A_{t},H_{t-1})}

\displaystyle=\frac{P^{T}(Y_{t}\mid A_{t},H_{t-1})}{Q^{T}(Y_{t}\mid A_{t},H_{t-1})}.

Substituting the above identity in (
C
), we have

\displaystyle D(P^{T}\mid\mid Q^{T})

\displaystyle=\sum_{t=1}^{T}\mathbb{E}_{P^{T}}\left[\log\frac{P^{T}(Y_{t}\mid A_{t},H_{t-1})}{Q^{T}(Y_{t}\mid A_{t},H_{t-1})}\right]

\displaystyle=\sum_{t=1}^{T}\mathbb{E}_{P^{T}}\left[\mathbb{E}_{P^{T}}\left[\left.\log\frac{P^{T}(Y_{t}\mid A_{t},H_{t-1})}{Q^{T}(Y_{t}\mid A_{t},H_{t-1})}\right|A_{t},H_{t-1}\right]\right]

\displaystyle=\sum_{t=1}^{T}\mathbb{E}_{P^{T}}\left[D(p_{A_{t}}\circ C_{H_{t-1}}\mid\mid q_{A_{t}}\circ C_{H_{t-1}})\right],

where the second last inequality uses the law of iterated expectations and the final one uses the definition of KL divergence.

Since
p_{A_{t}}
and
q_{A_{t}}
differ only when arm
A_{t}=j,
we have

\displaystyle D(P^{T}\mid\mid Q^{T})

\displaystyle=\sum_{t=1}^{T}\mathbb{E}_{P^{T}}\left[\mathbf{1}_{A_{t}=j}D(p_{j}\circ C_{H_{t-1}}\mid\mid q_{j}\circ C_{H_{t-1}})\right]

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{t=1}^{T}\mathbb{E}_{P^{T}}\left[\mathbf{1}_{A_{t}=j}\sup_{C\in\mathcal{C}}D(p_{j}\circ C\mid\mid q_{j}\circ C)\right]

\displaystyle\leq\mathbb{E}_{P^{T}}{[N_{j}(T)]}\cdot\sup_{C\in\mathcal{C}}D(p_{j}\circ C\mid\mid q_{j}\circ C)

\displaystyle\leq\frac{T}{K-1}\sup_{C\in\mathcal{C}}D(p_{j}\circ C\mid\mid q_{j}\circ C).

∎

C.1
Proof of Fact
1

Using the fact that
\ln x\leq x-1
and
\int\big(p(x)-q(x)\big)\mathrm{d}x=0
, we have

\displaystyle D(p\mid\mid q)

\displaystyle=\int p(x)\ln\frac{p(x)}{q(x)}\mathrm{d}x-\int\left(p(x)-q(x)\right)\mathrm{d}x

\displaystyle\leq\int p(x)\left(\frac{p(x)}{q(x)}-1\right)\mathrm{d}x-\int\left(p(x)-q(x)\right)\mathrm{d}x

\displaystyle=\int\frac{(p(x)-q(x))^{2}}{q(x)}\mathrm{d}x

\displaystyle=d_{\chi^{2}}(p,q).

∎

C.2
Proof of Fact
2

We have

\displaystyle d_{\chi^{2}}(p,q)

\displaystyle=\int\frac{(p(x)-q(x))^{2}}{q(x)}\mathrm{d}x

\displaystyle=\int p(x)\left(\frac{p(x)}{q(x)}-1\right)\mathrm{d}x-\int\left(p(x)-q(x)\right)\mathrm{d}x

\displaystyle\leq\int p(x)\cdot\frac{p(x)}{q(x)}\cdot\ln\frac{p(x)}{q(x)}\mathrm{d}x

\displaystyle\leq c\int p(x)\ln\frac{p(x)}{q(x)}\mathrm{d}x=cD(p\mid\mid q),

where the fist inequality follows by using the fact that
\frac{x-1}{x}\leq\ln x\quad\forall x\geq 0
and
\int\left(p(x)-q(x)\right)\mathrm{d}x=0
and the second inequality follows from the fact that
\frac{\mathrm{d}P}{\mathrm{d}Q}(x)\leq c\quad\forall x\in\mathbb{R}.

∎
