Schrödinger operators with complex sparse potentials
Jean-Claude Cuenin

Abstract
. We establish quantitative upper and lower bounds for Schrödinger operators with complex potentials that satisfy some weak form of sparsity. Our first result is a quantitative version of an example, due to S. Boegli (Comm. Math. Phys., 2017, 352, 629-639), of a Schrödinger operator with eigenvalues accumulating to every point of the essential spectrum. The second result shows that the eigenvalue bounds of Frank (Bull. Lond. Math. Soc., 2011, 43, 745-750 and Trans. Amer. Math. Soc., 2018, 370, 219-240) can be improved for sparse potentials. The third result generalizes a theorem of Klaus (Ann. Inst. H. Poincaré Sect. A (N.S.), 1983, 38, 7-13) on the characterization of the essential spectrum to the multidimensional non-selfadjoint case. The fourth result shows that, in one dimension, the purely imaginary (non-sparse) step potential has unexpectedly many eigenvalues, comparable to the number of resonances. Our examples show that several known upper bounds are sharp.



1.
Introduction and main results

1.1.
Introduction

Many examples of Schrödinger operators with “strange” spectral properties involve sparse potentials. In his seminal work
[
59
]
Pearson constructed examples of real-valued potentials (on the half-line) leading to singular continuous spectrum.
The potentials consists of an infinite sequence of “bumps” of identical profile, and the separation between these bumps increases rapidly. The physical interpretation is that a quantum mechanical particle will ultimately be reflected from a bump. These ideas were further developed in several directions, see e.g.
[
62
]

[
70
]
,
[
42
]
,
[
48
]
,
[
46
]
,
[
80
]
, and the references therein. Scattering from sparse potentials in higher dimensions was studied by Molchanov and Vainberg
[
53
]
,
[
54
]
; see also
[
52
]
,
[
37
]
,
[
38
]
,
[
65
]
,
[
45
]
. The discrete spectrum for multidimensional lattice Schrödinger operators was investigated by Rozenblum and Solomyak
[
63
]
. They constructed examples of sparse potentials whose number of negative eigenvalues grows like an arbitrary given polynomial power in the large coupling limit.
In the recent work
[
5
]
Boegli constructed a complex-valued sparse potential with arbitrary small
L^{q}
norm (
q>d
) that has infinitely many non-real eigenvalues accumulating at every point of the essential spectrum.
Since the proof is based on compactness arguments, there is no quantitative bound on the rate of separation between the bumps, and hence no estimate on the pointwise decay of the potential or the accumulation rate of the eigenvalues is possible.

1.2.
A quantitative version of Boegli’s example

Our first result provides quantitative decay bounds for the example in
[
5
]
. Perhaps more importantly, the construction can be used to produce a potential together with an infinite number of eigenvalues (possibly not all of them) satisfying given upper and lower bounds on their accumulation rate. We formulate our result for the most interesting spectral region

\displaystyle\Sigma_{0}=\{z\in{\mathbb{C}}:|\mathrm{Im}\,z|\leq\epsilon_{0}\mathrm{Re}\,z\},

(1)

where
\epsilon_{0}>0
is small but fixed.

Theorem 1
.

Let
d\geq 1
,
q>d
,
\epsilon_{1},\epsilon_{2}\in(0,1],\gamma>0
, and let
(\zeta_{n})_{n}\subset\Sigma_{0}
be a sequence satisfying

\displaystyle\big(\sum_{n\in{\mathbb{N}}}|\zeta_{n}|^{\frac{d}{2}}|\mathrm{Im}\,\zeta_{n}|^{q-d}|\log^{d}|\mathrm{Im}\,\zeta_{n}/\zeta_{n}||\big)^{\frac{1}{q}}\asymp\epsilon_{1}.

(2)

Then there exists a complex sparse potential
V
such that the following hold:

a)

For each
n\in{\mathbb{N}}
there exists a discrete eigenvalue
z_{n}
of
H_{V}=-\Delta+V
which is exponentially close to
\zeta_{n}
, in the sense
|z_{n}-\zeta_{n}|\leq\exp(-|\mathrm{Im}\,\zeta_{n}|^{-\gamma})
.

b)

The potential satisfies
\|V\|_{L^{q}({\mathbb{R}}^{d})}\lesssim\epsilon_{1}
.

c)

The potential decays polynomially, i.e. there exists a positive constant
\beta=\beta(\gamma,d,q)
such that
|V(x)|\lesssim\epsilon_{2}\langle x\rangle^{-\beta}
.

Remark 1
.

(i) In particular, for any
\lambda\in(0,\infty)
there exists a sequence
(\zeta_{n})_{n}\subset\Sigma_{0}
satisfying (
2
) such that
\lim_{n\to\infty}\zeta_{n}=\lambda
. In this way one can find a sequence accumulating to every point of the essential spectrum. This yields a constructive proof of the result of Boegli
[
5
]
.

(ii) One can remove the logarithm in (
2
) at the expense of replacing the
L^{q}
norm of
V
by the “Davies–Nath norm” (see (
37
)).

(iii) We will give explicit bounds on the polynomial decay
\beta
.

(iv) Substituting the trivial lower bound
|\zeta_{n}|\geq|\mathrm{Im}\,\zeta_{n}|
into (
2
) shows
\mathrm{Im}\,\zeta_{n}\to 0
. This is the reason why we say that
z_{n}
is exponentially close to
\zeta_{n}
.

We believe that the pointwise condition c) is more natural than the
L^{q}
condition b) for the phenomenon that takes place in Theorem
1
. This is because complex analogues of classical phase space bounds, which motivate the consideration on
L^{q}
norms in the first place, lack many of the features that make them so useful for real potentials (more on that in Subsection
1.5
below). Put simply, the
L^{q}
norm does not see the separation between the bumps, while the pointwise bound does. We will nevertheless work with
L^{q}
norms since we allow the bumps to have singularities. In the case where they are bounded the pointwise decay of the whole potential can easily be estimated by comparing the
L^{\infty}
norms of the bumps to their spatial separation from the origin. In his fundamental work on non-selfadjoint Schrödinger operators, Pavlov
[
57
]
,
[
58
]
showed that the number of eigenvalues in one dimension is finite if
|V(x)|\lesssim\exp(-c|x|^{1/2})
, and that this exponential rate is best possible. This means that the potential in Theorem
1
cannot decay too fast. The
L^{q}
bound imposes no decay whatsoever, but we can at least establish polynomial decay. For recent quantitative improvements of Pavlov’s bound we refer to Borichev–Frank–Volberg
[
6
]
and Sodin
[
72
]
.

The proof of the example in
[
5
]
is based on “soft” methods like weak convergence, compact embedding and the notion of the limiting essential spectrum. In contrast, our proof uses “hard” estimates for the resolvent and the Birman-Schwinger operator, combined with tools from complex analysis such as Rouché’s theorem, Jensen’s formula and Cartan type estimates. This allows us to obtain more precise results thatn those in
[
5
]
. Rouché’s theorem and Jensen’s formula are among the most ubiquitous albeit simple tools in non-selfadjoint spectral theory, where such machinery as the variational principle or the spectral theorem is not available. In the present paper Cartan type estimates are crucial to bound a certain Fredholm determinant from below and get upper upper bounds on the norm of the resolvent. This opens the way to proving existence of eigenvalues by means of quasimode construction. We are then in a setting similar to the selfadjoint case where a quasimode of size
\epsilon
guarantees the existence of a spectral point in an
\epsilon
-neighborhood of the quasi-eigenvalue. This follows from the inequality
\|(H_{V}-z)^{-1}\|\leq 1/{\rm d}(z,\sigma(H_{V}))
, where
\sigma(H_{V})
is the spectrum. In the non-selfadjoint case the inequality may fail dramatically. This phenomenon gives rise to the notion of pseudospectrum, which we will not discuss here (see e.g. the monograph
[
19
]
). The upper bounds obtained by Cartan type estimates generally grow exponentially in
1/{\rm d}(z,\sigma(H_{V}))
. In order to beat this, we are forced to construct exponentially small quasimodes, a challenging task in all but the simplest models. The strategy is reminiscent of the proof of existence of resonances close to the real axis due to Tang–Zworski
[
77
]
and Stefanov
[
73
]
(see also the recent book by Dyatlov–Zworski
[
24
]
). Our method is perhaps closest to that of Dencker–Sjöstrand–Zworski
[
22
, Section 6]
for non-selfajoint dissipative Schrödinger operators. The difference is that we consider decaying potentials and do not assume, as these authors do, that the quasi-eigenvalue is real (see
[
22
, Proposition 6.4]
). This means that the amplification of the exponential upper bound through the maximum principle (see
[
22
, Proposition 6.2]
) is in general not possible in our case.
Another crucial difference is that we need a more quantitative version of the Cartan type estimate (Lemma
33
) as well as of the conformal transformations between the spectral region and the model domain (the unit disk). The Riemann mapping theorem is notoriously non-quantitative. Instead, we use Cayley and Schwarz–Christoffel transformations, which have previously been used in other contexts related to non-selfadjoint spectral theory, especially in connection with Lieb–Thirring type inequalities. The combination with Rouché’s theorem and the Cartan type bounds is new and leads to results with an inverse problem flavor, as in Theorem
1
.

1.3.
Magnitude bounds

The second result gives precise bounds on the magnitude of eigenvalues of Schrödinger operators with complex sparse potentials, or more generally, potentials of the form
V=\sum_{j=1}^{N}V_{j}
, where the
V_{j}
have disjoint support and separate rapidly from each other. We will call these “separating” potentials. The Schrödinger operator
H_{V}=-\Delta+V
behaves like an almost orthogonal sum, due to the rapid decoupling between the
N
“channels”. This enables us to improve upon the bounds for general complex potentials due to Frank
[
27
]
,
[
28
]
. For simplicity we state the result here for
d\geq 3
. The general case along with further refinements can be found in Subsection
3.4
. We refer to Section
2
for a more in-depth explanation of the terminology.

Theorem 2
.

Assume that
d\geq 3
and
d/2\leq q\leq(d+1)/2
. If
V
is separating at scale
\eta^{-1}
, then every eigenvalue
z
of
H_{V}
with
\mathrm{Im}\,\sqrt{z}\geq(d+1)\eta
satisfies

\displaystyle|z|^{q-\frac{d}{2}}\lesssim\sup_{j\in[N]}\|V_{j}\|_{L^{q}({\mathbb{R}}^{d})}^{q}.

(3)

If
q>(d+1)/2
, then

\displaystyle|z|^{\frac{1}{2}}{\rm d}(z,{\mathbb{R}}_{+})^{q-\frac{d+1}{2}}\lesssim\sup_{j\in[N]}\|V_{j}\|_{L^{q}({\mathbb{R}}^{d})}^{q}.

(4)

The bound (
3
) follows from (
34
) by a Birman–Schwinger argument. It could also be proved by using the eigenvalue bounds of the author
[
14
]
, which are inspired by a method of Davies and Nath
[
20
]
in one dimension. For
N=1
the estimates (
3
), (
4
) coincide with those of Frank
[
27
]
,
[
28
]
, respectively. The difference is that here
V
might decay very slowly or not at all. Nevertheless, on the
\eta^{2}
energy (spectral) scale the estimate is of the same quality as for
N=1
.

We make a short remark about the connection with the Laptev–Safronov conjecture
[
47
]
, which stipulates that

\displaystyle\sup_{V\in L^{q}({\mathbb{R}}^{d})}\sup_{z\in\sigma(-\Delta+V)\setminus{\mathbb{R}}_{+}}\frac{|z|^{q-\frac{d}{2}}}{\|V\|_{q}^{q}}<\infty\quad\mbox{for all}\quad q\in[d/2,d].

(5)

For the range
q\in[d/2,(d+1)/2]
the conjecture was proven by Frank
[
27
]
. The question whether (
5
) is true for
q\in((d+1)/2,d]
is still open.
The expectation, based on intuition from counterexamples to Fourier restriction (see e.g.
[
13
]
,
[
14
]
for more explanations) is that the conjecture is false in this range. Incidentally, Theorem
1
clearly implies the necessity of
q>d
in the conjecture, but this already follows from Bögli’s result (without the pointwise bound). In fact, a single bump of the sparse potential used in Boegli’s construction (and in Theorem
1
) already provides a counterexample. Since there seems to be some confusion about this issue we use the results of Section
7
to show necessity of the condition
q>d
. Indeed, Lemma
22
implies that for small
\epsilon>0
there is a potential
V(\epsilon)
and an eigenvalue
z(\epsilon)
such that

|z(\epsilon)|^{q-\frac{d}{2}}/\|V(\epsilon)\|_{q}^{q}\gtrsim\epsilon^{q-d}\log^{q}(1/\epsilon)
.
The example (a complex step potential) is simple but, quite amazingly, generic enough to show optimality of several estimates in the literature (see
[
14
]
). In one dimension, the step potential can be tuned to essentially saturate any of the known magnitude bounds. For example, the last inequality also shows that the Davies–Nath bound
[
20
]
is sharp and, since
\mathrm{Im}\,z(\epsilon)\asymp\epsilon
, that Frank’s bound
[
28
, Theorem 1.1]

is sharp up to a logarithm. In Subsection
1.5
we will show how the complex step potential also implies sharpness of another bound in
[
28
]
.

1.4.
A generalization of Klaus’ theorem

The following is a generalization of a result due to Klaus
[
43
]
on the characterization of the essential spectrum. The generalization is twofold: First, we admit complex potentials and second, we prove it for any dimension (whereas Klaus only proved the one-dimensional case). In this introduction we again focus on the case
d\geq 3
, but the statement is valid in
d=1,2
for
q
in the range (
18
).

Theorem 3

(Klaus’ theorem
[
43
]
for complex potentials)
.

Assume that
d\geq 3
and
d/2\leq q\leq(d+1)/2
. If
V
is a separating potential and
\sup_{j\in[N]}\|V_{j}\|_{L^{q}({\mathbb{R}}^{d})}<\infty
, then

\displaystyle\sigma_{\rm e}(H)=[0,\infty)\cup S,

where
S
is the set of all
z\in{\mathbb{C}}\setminus[0,\infty)
such that there exist infinite sequences
(i_{n})_{n},(z_{n})_{n}
with
z_{n}\in\sigma(H_{V_{i_{n}}})
,
i_{n}\to\infty
and
z_{n}\to z
as
n\to\infty
.

An alternative proof (also in one dimension) of Klaus’ theorem can be found in
[
16
]
. The role of Theorem
3
in this paper will be an auxiliary one, and we will only use it to argue that the essential spectrum is invariant under the perturbations we consider in Theorem
1
. Although our proof follows the general strategy of
[
43
]
it is still worth emphasizing that some parts of it require somewhat novel techniques.

1.5.
Weyl’s law and locality

Recently, Boegli and Štampach
[
4
]
disproved a conjecture by Demuth, Hansmann and Katriel
[
21
]
for one-dimensional Schrödinger operators with complex potentials by establishing a lower bound on a certain Riesz means of eigenvalues.
More precisely, consider
H_{\alpha V}
,
where
V=\mathrm{i}\mathbf{1}_{[-1,1]}
is a purely imaginary step potential and
\alpha
is a large semiclassical parameter. Boegli and Štampach prove that, for any
p\geq 1
,

\displaystyle\alpha^{-p}\sum_{z\in\sigma_{d}(H_{\alpha V})}\frac{(\mathrm{Im}\,z)^{p}}{|z|^{1/2}}\geq C_{p}\log\alpha.

(6)

The interesting feature of this bound is that it shows a logarithmic violation of Weyl’s law. To recall Weyl’s law, consider a
self-adjoint
operator, with a smooth real-valued potential. Note that if we set
h=1/\sqrt{\alpha}
, then
\alpha^{-1}H_{\alpha V}
takes the form of a semiclassical Schrödinger operator,
-h^{2}\partial_{x}^{2}+V(x)
. Semiclassical asymptotics (Weyl’s law) yield, for a suitable class of functions
f
,

\displaystyle\Tr f(H_{\alpha V})=\frac{\sqrt{\alpha}}{2\pi}\big(\int f(\alpha(\xi^{2}+V(x))){\rm d}x{\rm d}\xi+o(1)\big)

(7)

as
\alpha\to\infty
. In particular, if
f
is homogeneous of degree
\gamma
, then

\displaystyle\lim_{\alpha\to\infty}\alpha^{-1/2-\gamma}\Tr f(H_{\alpha V})=(2\pi)^{-1}\int f(\xi^{2}+V(x)){\rm d}x{\rm d}\xi.

(8)

For
f(\lambda):=\lambda_{-}^{\gamma}
and
\gamma\geq 1/2
(since we are considering
d=1
) the Lieb-Thirring inequality

\displaystyle\sum_{\lambda\in\sigma_{\rm d}(H_{\alpha V})}\lambda_{-}^{\gamma}\leq C_{\gamma}\alpha^{1/2+\gamma}\int V_{-}(x)^{1/2+\gamma}{\rm d}x

(9)

captures the semiclassical behavior (
8
), but is valid for any
\alpha>0
, not only asymptotically. Returning to the complex potential
V=\mathrm{i}\mathbf{1}_{[-1,1]}
and noticing that
f(z):=\frac{(\mathrm{Im}\,z)^{p}}{|z|^{1/2}}
is homogeneous of degree
\gamma=p-1/2
, we observe that (
6
) implies that the formal analogue of (
8
) cannot hold, i.e. that

\displaystyle\liminf_{\alpha\to\infty}\alpha^{-1/2-\gamma}\sum_{z\in\sigma_{d}(H_{\alpha V})}\frac{(\mathrm{Im}\,z)^{1/2+\gamma}}{|z|^{1/2}}=\infty,

hence violating Weyl’s law (
8
). The comparison with Weyl’s law is formal because
f(H_{\alpha})
does not make sense in general for a non-normal operator. However, (
6
) also shows that the complex analogue of the Lieb-Thirring inequality (
9
),

\displaystyle\sum_{z\in\sigma_{d}(H_{\alpha V})}\frac{(\mathrm{Im}\,z)^{1/2+\gamma}}{|z|^{1/2}}\leq C_{\gamma}^{\prime}\alpha^{1/2+\gamma}\int V_{-}(x)^{1/2+\gamma}{\rm d}x,

(10)

cannot be true, thus disproving the conjectured bound in
[
21
]
.

For a non-selfadjoint (pseudo)-differential operator with analytic symbol and in one dimension the eigenvalues typically lie on a complex curve, hence violating Weyl’s law (in terms of complex phase space). Small random perturbations typically restore the Weyl law (in real phase space). The literature on the subject is vast and we merely refer the interested reader to the book of Sjöstrand
[
71
]
for an overview of recent developments. In contrast, a classical result of Markus and Macaev
[
51
]
implies that Weyl’s law holds for the real part of the eigenvalues, provided the non-selfadjoint perturbation is small.

A slightly different view on the same phenomenon (violation of Weyl’s law) is connected with the notion of “locality”. In the semiclassical limit, in the self-adjoint case, each state occupies a volume of
(2\pi/\sqrt{\alpha})
in phase space
T^{*}{\mathbb{R}}
.
Indeed, with
f(\lambda):=\mathbf{1}_{(-\infty,-E]}(H_{\alpha V})
, (
7
) yields

\displaystyle N(H_{\alpha V};(-\infty,-E])=\sqrt{\alpha}\,\mathrm{Vol}(S_{E,V})(1+o(1)),

where
N(H;\Sigma)
denotes the number of eigenvalues of an operator
H
in a set
\Sigma
and
S_{E,V}:=\{(x,\xi)\in T^{*}{\mathbb{R}}:\xi^{2}+V(x)\leq-E\}
is the relevant part of phase space. If we consider a sum of disjoint bumps
V=\sum_{j=1}^{N}V_{j}
, say with
V_{j}(x)=W(x-x_{j})
, then
S_{E,V}=N\,S_{E,W}
, and hence

\displaystyle\frac{N(H_{\alpha V};(-\infty,-E])}{N(H_{\alpha W};(-\infty,-E])}=N(1+o(1)).

This means that in the semiclassical limit each bump is responsible for an equal number of eigenvalues. In particular, two distinct realizations of
V
as a sum of bumps have the same number of eigenvalues, as long as the bumps are disjoint. This feature of locality is also captured by the Lieb-Thirring inequalities since the bound involves the integral linearly.

Our third result shows that this kind of locality can be violated in the non-selfadjoint case. We adopt the same notation
N(H;\Sigma)
for the number of eigenvalues of
H
in
\Sigma
as in the self-adjoint case, but emphasize that these are counted according to their
algebraic
multiplicity.
We consider one sparse one non-sparse (or non-separating) realization of
V
and denote these by
V_{\rm s}
and
V_{\rm n}
, respectively. For simplicity, we will consider the same potential as in
[
4
]
, i.e. the purely imaginary step potential
W=\mathrm{i}|W_{0}|\mathbf{1}_{[-R_{0},R_{0}]}
of size
|W_{0}|
and width
R_{0}
. For simplicity we fix these scales to be of order one.
Then
V_{\rm n}
is the single well of width
R=NR_{0}
, while
V_{\rm s}
is a sum of
N
disjoint wells
W(x-x_{j})
of width
R_{0}
. We will fix the coupling strength
\alpha
and consider the limit
N\to\infty
. For the non-sparse operator this is in fact still a semiclassical limit, as can easily be seen by rescaling.

Theorem 4
.

Let
d=1
,
N\gg 1
and consider the rectangular set

\displaystyle\Sigma:=\{z\in{\mathbb{C}}:C^{-1}\frac{N^{2}}{\log^{2}N}\leq\mathrm{Re}\,z\leq C\frac{N^{2}}{\log^{2}N},\,C^{-1}\leq\mathrm{Im}\,z\leq C\},

where
C
is a large constant.
Then we have

\displaystyle N(H_{V_{\rm n}};\Sigma)\gtrsim\frac{N^{2}}{\log N}.

(11)

Moreover, there exists a sequence
(x_{j})_{j}
such that

\displaystyle N(H_{V_{\rm s}};\Sigma)\lesssim N.

(12)

The estimates in
[
4
]
would be sufficient to prove a lower bound of size
N^{2-\epsilon}
in (
11
). Their argument proceeds by approximating the characteristic eigenvalue equation and finding the roots of this equation in an asymptotic regime.
They did not prove that the original equation has nearby roots. This can be done e.g. by a contraction mapping argument
[
75
]
. We will give a proof using Rouché’s theorem. Note that, by power counting (dimensional analysis), the constants in (
11
), (
12
) only depend on the dimensionless quantity
|W_{0}|^{1/2}R_{0}
.

In the selfadjoint case, i.e. when
W
is replaced by
|W_{0}|\mathbf{1}_{[-R_{0},R_{0}]}
, the number of negative eigenvalues of
V_{\rm n}
is of order
N
, in agreement with semiclassics. Also note that, since in one dimension each
H_{V_{j}}
has at least one negative eigenvalue, we have
N(H_{V_{\rm s}};{\mathbb{R}}_{-})\asymp N
in this case. Hence the left hand sides of (
11
) and (
12
) are equal in magnitude, which may be seen as a manifestation of locality. However, this locality is violated if one takes into account not only eigenvalues but also resonances. Zworski
[
81
]
proved that, for a compactly supported, bounded, complex potential, the number
n(r)
of resonances
\lambda_{j}^{2}
in a disk
|\lambda_{j}|\leq r
asymptotically satisfies

\displaystyle n(r)=\frac{2\,\mathrm{|ch\,supp(V)}|}{\pi}\,r(1+o_{V}(1))

(13)

as
r\to\infty
. Moreover, for any
\epsilon_{0}>0
, the number of resonances in
|\lambda_{j}|\leq r
but outside the sector
\Sigma_{0}
(see (
1
)) is
o(r)
. This and the fact that eigenvalue bounds outside
\Sigma_{0}
are “trivial” (in the sense that they can be proved by the same standard estimates as for real potentials, with the provisio that the constants blow up as the implicit constant in (
1
) becomes small) motivates us to often restrict attention to the spectral set
\Sigma_{0}
.
The result (
13
) was obtained earlier by Regge
[
61
]
in some special cases. Different proofs were given by Froese
[
31
]
and Simon
[
68
]
. Froese’s proof also works for complex potentials.
Note that eigenvalues are included in the definition of resonances. Formula (
13
) can be seen as a Weyl law for resonances but is
nonlocal
as it includes the convex hull of the support of the potential. An obvious corollary of Zworski’s formula is that the right hand side of (
13
) is an upper bound for the number of eigenvalues
\lambda_{j}^{2}
in the disk
|\lambda_{j}|\leq r
(resonances in the upper half plane). For the potential
V_{\rm n}
, taking
r\asymp N/\log N
and observing that
\mathrm{ch\,supp(V_{\rm n})}\asymp N
, we find that the leading term in (
13
) is of order
N^{2}/\log N
, in agreement with the lower bound (
11
). Note, however, that the asymptotics are not uniform, i.e. the error may depend on
N
(recall that
V_{\rm n}
depends on
N
). Korotyaev
[
44
, Theorem 1.1]
proved an upper bound which yields

\displaystyle n(r)\leq\frac{8N}{\pi\log 2}r+\mathcal{O}(N\log N)

for
r\asymp N/\log N
. Hence, (
11
) shows that, at the scale considered here,
a substantial fraction of resonances are actual eigenvalues
. In the special case of a step potential Stepanenko
[
76
]
proved that the total number of eigenvalues is bounded by
N^{2}/\log N
. The lower bound (
11
) shows that this is sharp up to constants; this was observed independently by Stepanenko
[
75
]
.

In dimension
d\geq 3
it may of course happen that all the
H_{V_{j}}
, and thus also
H_{V_{\rm s}}
, have no eigenvalues at all if
|W_{0}|^{1/2}R_{0}
is small (by the CLR bound), while
H_{V_{\rm n}}
has of the order
N^{d}
eigenvalues. This is not the kind of phenomenon that takes place in Theorem
4
. Indeed, there we allow
|W_{0}|^{1/2}R_{0}
to be of unit size. We also note that in higher dimensions the results on the asymptotics of the resonance counting function are weaker, and there are example of complex potentials with no resonances at all (see Christiansen
[
8
]
). On the other hand, Christiansen
[
7
]
and Christiansen–Hislop
[
9
]
,
[
10
]
proved that
n(r)
has maximal growth rate
r^{d}
for “most” potentials in certain families.

A final comment regarding the implications of Theorem
4
, also related to locality (or the lack thereof), concerns a general observation on Lieb–Thirring type inequalities for complex potentials. It is a fact that all known upper bounds for eigenvalue sums have a superlinear dependence on
N
. For example, in
d=1
,
[
28
, Theorem 1.3]
yields the bound

\displaystyle\sum_{j}{\rm d}(z_{j},{\mathbb{R}}_{+})^{a}\lesssim\left(\int_{{\mathbb{R}}}|V|^{b}{\rm d}x\right)^{c}

(14)

with
(a,b,c)=(1,1,2)
. The lower bound (
11
) shows that the power
c
cannot be decreased, while preserving the homogeneity condition
-2a=(-2b+1)c
. Indeed,

\displaystyle\sum_{j}{\rm d}(z_{j},{\mathbb{R}}_{+})^{a}\geq\sum_{z_{j}\in\Sigma}{\rm d}(z_{j},{\mathbb{R}}_{+})^{a}\gtrsim\frac{|W_{0}|^{a}N^{2}}{\log N},

while

\displaystyle\left(\int_{{\mathbb{R}}}|V_{\rm n}|^{b}{\rm d}x\right)^{c}=(2NR_{0}|W_{0}|^{b})^{c},

so that the ratio of the first to the second is bounded below by
(|W_{0}|^{1/2}R_{0})^{-c}\frac{N^{2-c}}{\log N}
, which tends to infinity as
N\to\infty
, for every

. On the other hand, for sufficient rapid separation of the bumps in

, we can prove Frank’s bound (

) with a linear dependence on

, at least locally in the spectrum.

Theorem 5
.

Let
V\in\ell^{2}L^{1}({\mathbb{R}})
and
N\gg 1

For any
\eta>0
there exists a sequence
(x_{j})_{j=1}^{N}
,
x_{j}=x_{j}(\eta,\|V\|_{\ell^{2}L^{1}})

\displaystyle\sum_{\mathrm{Im}\,\sqrt{z_{j}}>(d+1)\eta}{\rm d}(z_{j},{\mathbb{R}}_{+})\lesssim N\sum_{j=1}^{N}\left(\int_{{\mathbb{R}}}|V_{j}|{\rm d}x\right)^{2}

(15)

Our methods could be used to prove similar generalizations of the higher-dimensional results of

2.
Definitions and preliminaries

2.1.
Separating and sparse potentials

We consider sparse potentials of the form

\displaystyle V(x)=V_{j}(x),\quad x\in\Omega_{j},

(16)

where
j\in[N]=\{1,2,\ldots,N\}
,
N\in{\mathbb{N}}\cup\{\infty\}
, and
\Omega_{j}\subset{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
are mutually disjoint (not necessarily bounded) sets. We then set

\displaystyle L_{j}:={\rm d}(\Omega_{j},\cup_{i\in[N]}\Omega_{i}\setminus\Omega_{j}).

(17)

We assume that
V_{j}\in\ell^{p}L^{q}
, where the norms are defined by

\displaystyle\|V\|_{\ell^{p}L^{q}}:=\big(\sum_{j\in[N]}\|V_{j}\|_{L^{q}({\mathbb{R}}^{d})}^{p}\big)^{1/p}

for
p\in[1,\infty)
and
\|V\|_{\ell^{\infty}L^{q}}:=\sup_{j\in[N]}\|V_{j}\|_{L^{q}({\mathbb{R}}^{d})}
. Here
q
will be in the range

\displaystyle q\in[1,\infty]\quad\mbox{if }d=1,\quad q\in(1,\infty]\quad\mbox{if }d=2,\quad q\in[\tfrac{d}{2},\infty]\quad\mbox{if }d\geq 3.

(18)

In particular, we have
\|V\|_{L^{q}}=\|V\|_{\ell^{q}L^{q}}
. We sometimes write
V=V(L)
or
V=V(L,\Omega)
to emphasize the dependence of
V
on the sequences
L=(L_{j})_{j=1}^{N}
or
\Omega=(\Omega)_{j=1}^{N}
.

Definition 1
.

We say that
V=V(L)
is
separating
if

\displaystyle\mathrm{sep}(L,\eta):=\sum_{j\in[N]}\exp(-\eta L_{j})<\infty

for every
\eta>0
. We call
V

separating at scale

\eta^{-1}
if
\mathrm{sep}(L,\eta)\leq 1
. We say that
V
is
strongly separating
if
\mathrm{sep}(L,\delta\eta)\lesssim_{\delta}\mathrm{sep}(L,\eta)
for every
\delta,\eta>0
.

The constant
\mathrm{sep}(L,\eta)
only depends on
L
and not on
V
itself. We will sometimes abuse terminology and call the sequence
L
separating. Note that since
\eta L_{j}
is dimensionless,
\eta
has the dimension of inverse length.
We shall always assume that the sequence
L
is increasing.

Definition 2
.

We say that
V=V(L,\Omega)
is
sparse
if it is separating, the supports
\Omega_{n}
are bounded,
and
\lim_{n\to\infty}\mathrm{diam}(\Omega_{n})/L_{n}=0
.

Most of our results hold for separating potentials. The strong separation condition is convenient and facilitates some of the proofs.
Typical examples of strongly separating sequences are (
\eta\ll\eta_{0}
):

a)

It
\eta_{0}L_{k}\gtrsim k^{\alpha}
for
\alpha>0
, then
\mathrm{sep}(L,\eta)\lesssim(\eta/\eta_{0})^{-1/\alpha}
.

b)

If
\eta_{0}L_{k}\gtrsim\exp(k)
, then
\mathrm{sep}(L,\eta)\lesssim\log(\eta_{0}/\eta)
.

c)

If
\eta_{0}L_{k}\gtrsim\exp(\exp(k))
, then
\mathrm{sep}(L,\eta)\lesssim\log\log(\eta_{0}/\eta)
.

See Subsection
8.2
for a proof.
The explicit example used to prove Theorem
1
turns out to be sparse. Note that, by the disjoint supports, the definition (
16
) is equivalent to
V=\sum_{j=1}^{N}V_{j}
.

2.2.
Comparison with a direct sum

In Section
5
we will compare the two operators

\displaystyle H_{V}=-\Delta+V,\quad H_{\rm diag}=\bigoplus_{j\in[N]}(-\Delta+V_{j}).

(19)

Note that the point spectrum (eigenvalues) of
H_{\rm diag}
,

\displaystyle\sigma_{\rm p}(H_{\rm diag})=\bigcup_{j=1}^{N}\sigma_{\rm p}(H_{V_{j}})=\bigcup_{j=1}^{N}\sigma_{\rm p}(H_{W_{j}}),

(20)

is independent of the sequence
L
. We will consider
\sigma_{\rm p}(H_{W_{j}})
as part of the data and seek to prove lower bounds on
L_{j}
or
|x_{i}-x_{j}|
that imply that
\sigma_{\rm p}(H_{V})
is close to
\sigma_{\rm p}(H_{\rm diag})
. In fact, we will consider a subset of the point spectrum, the discrete spectrum. We will consider the
V_{j}
as given only
up to translations
, i.e. we stipulate that

\displaystyle V_{j}(x)=W_{j}(x-x_{j}),

(21)

where
W_{j}\in\ell^{p}L^{q}
contains the origin in its support.
Using the triangle inequality, it is easy to see that
|x_{i}-x_{j}|\geq L_{j}
for
i\neq j
, and therefore

\displaystyle\sup_{i\in[N]}\sum_{j\in[N]\setminus\{i\}}\exp(-\eta|x_{i}-x_{j}|)\leq\mathrm{sep}(L,\eta).

Straightforward arguments also show that

\displaystyle L_{i}\geq|x_{i}-x_{j}|-\mathrm{diam}(\Omega_{i})-\mathrm{diam}(\Omega_{j})

(22)

for all
j\in[N]\setminus\{i\}
. In particular, for sparse potentials,

\displaystyle L_{i}(1+o(1))\geq\sup_{j<i}|x_{i}-x_{j}|.

(23)

Note that
\mathrm{diam}(\Omega_{j})=\mathrm{diam}(\supp W_{j})
is part of the data. We can thus obtain a lower bound for
|x_{i}-x_{j}|
in terms of
L_{j}
and vice versa. For this reason we restrict attention to
L_{j}
here.

2.3.
Truncations

For technical reaons, it will turn out to be convenient to consider finite truncations. For
n\in[N]
we define

\displaystyle V^{(n)}

\displaystyle:=\sum_{j\in[n]}V_{j},\quad H^{(n)}:=-\Delta+V^{(n)}.

(24)

2.4.
Abstract Birman-Schwinger principle

We mostly disregard operator theoretic discussions here and refer e.g. to
[
28
]
for the (standard) definition of
H_{V_{j}}
as
m
-sectorial operators. The rigorous definition of
H_{V}
is a bit more subtle since
V
need not be decaying. However, a classical construction of Kato
[
40

produces a closed extension
H_{V}
of
-\Delta+V
via a Birman-Schwinger type argument. This approach works as soon as one can find point
z_{0}
in the resolvent set of
H_{0}=-\Delta
at which the Birman-Schwinger operator

\displaystyle BS_{V}(z):=|V|^{1/2}(-\Delta^{2}-z)^{-1}V^{1/2}

(25)

has norm less than one. Such bounds are provided by Lemma

, but to avoid technicalities it is useful to think of the potential as being bounded by a large cutoff (and all of the bounds will be independent of that cutoff).
By iterating the second resolvent identity,

\displaystyle R_{V}(z)=R_{0}(z)-R_{0}(z)VR(z),

it is then easy to see that

\displaystyle R_{V}(z)-R_{0}(z)=-R_{0}(z)V^{1/2}(I+BS_{V}(z))^{-1}|V|^{1/2}R_{0}(z).

(26)

For more background on the abstract Birman-Schwinger principle in a nonselfadjoint setting we refer to
[
32
]
,
[
28
]
,
[
3
]
,
[
36
]
.

2.5.
The essential and discrete spectrum

We briefly recall some facts about the essential and discrete spectrum of a closed operator
H
. There are several inequivalent definitions of essential spectrum for non-selfadjoint operators (but these all coincide for Schrödinger operators with decaying potentials
[
28
, Appendix B]
). We use the following standard definition.

\displaystyle\sigma_{\rm e}(H):=\{z\in{\mathbb{C}}:H-z\mbox{ is not a Fredholm operator}\}.

The discrete spectrum is defined as

\sigma_{\rm d}(H):=\{z\in{\mathbb{C}}:z\mbox{ is an isolated eigenvalue of $H$ of finite multiplicity}\}.

Note that, if
H
is not selfadjoint, then, in general,
\sigma(H)
is not the disjoint union of
\sigma_{\rm e}(H)
and
\sigma_{\rm d}(H)
. However, by
[
33
, Theorem XVII.2.1]
, if every connected component of
{\mathbb{C}}\setminus\sigma_{\rm e}(H)
contains points of
\rho(H)
, then

\displaystyle\sigma(H)\setminus\sigma_{\rm e}(H)=\sigma_{\rm d}(H).

(27)

In the situations we consider here (
27
) will always be true for
H=H_{V}
and
H_{\rm diag}
. In fact, Corollary
11
tells us that

\sigma_{\rm e}(H)=[0,\infty)
, just as for decaying potentials.

In this section we consider
H_{V}
as a perturbation of
-\Delta
. We will thus only make assumptions about
V
and not about
H_{\rm diag}
.

3.1.
Birman–Schwinger analysis

Since the
V_{j}
have mutually disjoint supports, we can write the Birman-Schwinger operator (
25
) as

\displaystyle BS_{V}(z)=\sum_{i=1}^{N}BS_{ii}(z)+\sum_{i\neq j}BS_{ij}(z),

where

\displaystyle BS_{ij}(z)=|V_{i}|^{1/2}R_{0}(z)V_{j}^{1/2}.

The first term is unitarily equivalent to the orthogonal sum

\displaystyle BS_{\rm diag}(z):=\bigoplus_{i=1}^{N}BS_{ii}(z).

on the Hilbert space
\mathcal{H}\simeq\bigoplus_{i=1}^{N}\mathcal{H}_{i}
, where
\mathcal{H}=L^{2}({\mathbb{R}}^{d})
,
\mathcal{H}_{i}=L^{2}(\Omega_{i})
. By abuse of notation we will always identify these two Hilbert spaces and the corresponding operators. The off-diagonal contribution is

\displaystyle BS_{\rm off}(z):=\sum_{i\neq j}BS_{ij}(z).

In the following we will use the notation

\begin{split}\omega_{q}(z):=\begin{cases}|z|^{\frac{d}{2q}-1}\quad&\mbox{if }q\leq q_{d},\\
|z|^{-\frac{1}{2q}}{\rm d}(z,{\mathbb{R}}_{+})^{\frac{q_{d}}{q}-1}\quad&\mbox{if }q\geq q_{d},\end{cases}\end{split}

(28)

where
q_{d}=(d+1)/2
. Note that for
z\in\Sigma_{0}
(see (
1
)) we have
{\rm d}(z,{\mathbb{R}}_{+})=|\mathrm{Im}\,z|
. We use the abbreviation

\displaystyle s(L,z):=\mathrm{sep}(L,\mathrm{Im}\,\sqrt{z}/(d+1))

(29)

and set
\alpha(q):=2\max(q,q_{d})
.

Lemma 6
.

Assume
q
is in the range (
18
) and
p\in[\alpha(q),\infty]
. Then the following hold.

(i)

For any
i\in[N]
,

\displaystyle\|BS_{ii}(z)\|_{\alpha(q)}\lesssim\omega_{q}(z)\|V_{i}\|_{L^{q}}.

(30)

(ii)

For any
i,j\in[N]
,
i\neq j
,

\displaystyle\|BS_{ij}(z)\|_{\alpha(q)}\lesssim\exp(-\tfrac{2}{d+1}\mathrm{Im}\,\sqrt{z}\,{\rm d}(\Omega_{i},\Omega_{j}))\omega_{q}(z)\|V_{i}\|_{L^{q}}^{1/2}\|V_{j}\|_{L^{q}}^{1/2}.

(31)

(iii)

The diagonal part satisfies

\displaystyle\|BS_{\rm diag}(z)\|_{p}\lesssim\omega_{q}(z)\|V\|_{\ell^{p}L^{q}}.

(32)

(iv)

The off-diagonal part satisfies

\displaystyle\|BS_{\rm off}(z)\|_{\alpha(q)}\lesssim s(L,z)^{2}\omega_{q}(z)\|V\|_{\ell^{\infty}L^{q}},

(33)

(v)

The full Birman–Schwinger operator satisfies

\displaystyle\|BS_{V}(z)\|_{p}\lesssim\omega_{q}(z)\langle s(L,z)\rangle^{2}\|V\|_{\ell^{p}L^{q}}.

(34)

Proof.

It follows from

\displaystyle\|BS_{\rm diag}(z)\|_{p}=\big(\sum_{i\in[N]}\|BS_{ii}(z)\|_{p}^{p}\big)^{1/p}

that (iii) is a consequence of (i). In view of the embeddings

\mathfrak{S}^{p_{1}}\subset\mathfrak{S}^{p_{2}}
,
\ell^{p_{1}}\subset\ell^{p_{2}}
for
p_{1}\leq p_{2}
, (v) follows from (iii) and (iv). Moreover, (iv) follows from (ii) and the triangle inequality, using the estimate
{\rm d}(\Omega_{i},\Omega_{j})\geq\frac{1}{2}L_{i}+\frac{1}{2}L_{j}
to sum the double series.
The estimate (i) is the same as for the
N=1
case and follows from known results in the literature: For
q\leq q_{d}
, from
[
30
, Theorem 12]
for
d\geq 3
, from
[
12
, Theorem 4.1]
for
d=2
and from
[
1
, Theorem 4]
for
d=1
, for
q\geq q_{d}
and
d\geq 1
from
[
28
, Proposition 2.1]
. The bound (ii) is proved by complex interpolation as in
[
30
, Theorem 12]
in the case
q\leq q_{d}
and in
[
28
, Proposition 2.1]
for
q\geq q_{d}
. The only difference is that we include the (second) exponential in the pointwise bound

\displaystyle|(-\Delta-z)^{-(a+\mathrm{i}t)}(x-y)|\leq C_{1}{\rm e}^{C_{2}t^{2}}{\rm e}^{-\mathrm{Im}\,\sqrt{z}|x-y|}|x-y|^{-\frac{d+1}{2}+a}

(35)

for
a\in[(d-1)/2,(d+1)/2]
and
d\geq 2
, see e.g.
[
49
, (2.5)]
. For
d=1
one can use the explicit formula for the resolvent kernel.
∎

Remark 2
.

Using the results of
[
14
]
(or
[
20
]
in one dimension) in the proof of Lemma

we could replace the bounds (

), (

) by the following. For

and

\displaystyle\|BS_{ij}(z)\|_{\alpha(q)}\lesssim\exp(-\tfrac{2}{d+1}\mathrm{Im}\,\sqrt{z}\,{\rm d}(\Omega_{i},\Omega_{j}))|z|^{\frac{d}{2q}-1}F_{V_{i},q}^{1/2}(\mathrm{Im}\,\sqrt{z})F_{V_{j},q}^{1/2}(\mathrm{Im}\,\sqrt{z}),

(36)

where

is the “Davies–Nath norm”

\displaystyle F_{V,q}(s):=\left(\sup_{y\in{\mathbb{R}}^{d}}\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{d}}|V(x)|^{q}\exp(-s|x-y|){\rm d}x\right)^{\frac{1}{q}}.

(37)

This implies the bounds

\displaystyle\|BS_{\rm diag}(z)\|_{p}

\displaystyle\lesssim|z|^{\frac{d}{2q}-1}\sup_{i\in[N]}F_{V_{i},q},

(38)

Under the assumptions of Lemma

3.3.
Proof of Klaus’ theorem

The proof is an adaptation of Klaus’ original argument, which is based on the Birman-Schwinger principle. In the non-selfadjoint setting one may use e.g.
[
19
, Lemma 11.2.1]
as a substitute for Weyl sequences. This yields an easy proof of the inclusion
\sigma_{\rm e}(H_{V})\supset[0,\infty)\cup S
. For the converse inclusion we prove an analogue of
[
43
, Proposition 2.3]
.

Proposition 10
.

For
V\in\ell^{\infty}L^{q}
and
z\in{\mathbb{C}}\setminus[0,\infty)
we have

\displaystyle\sigma(BS_{\rm diag}(z))=\overline{\bigcup_{i}\sigma(BS_{ii}(z))}.

Proof.

Only the inclusion
\subset
is nontrivial. The resolvent set of a direct sum of bounded operators
A=\bigoplus_{i}A_{i}
is known to be

\displaystyle\rho(A)=\{\lambda\in\bigcap_{i}\rho(A_{i}):\sup_{i}\|(A_{i}-\lambda)^{-1}\|<\infty\}.

We set
A_{i}=BS_{ii}(z)
, whence
A=BS_{\rm diag}(z)
. Assume that
\lambda\in{\mathbb{C}}\setminus\overline{\bigcup_{i}\sigma(BS_{ii}(z))}
. This clearly implies
\lambda\in\bigcap_{i}\rho(A_{i})
. It remains to prove that
\sup_{i}\|(A_{i}-\lambda)^{-1}\|<\infty
. By assumption, there exists
\delta>0
such that
{\rm d}(\lambda,\sigma(A_{i}))\geq\delta
. By
[
2
, Theorem 4.1]
,

\displaystyle\|(A_{i}-\lambda)^{-1}\|\leq\delta^{-1}\exp(a\|A_{i}\|_{{\alpha}}^{{\alpha}}\delta^{-{\alpha}}+b),

where
\alpha=\alpha(q)
and
a=a(q)
,
b=b(q)
are constants. Since, by (
32
),
\|A_{i}\|_{\alpha}\lesssim\|V\|_{\ell^{\infty}L^{q}}
,
it follows that
\sup_{i}\|(A_{i}-\lambda)^{-1}\|<\infty
.
∎

With Proposition
10
in hand is immediate that
[
43
, Lemma 2.4]
(
K_{i}=A_{i}
in our notation) holds in the generality needed here. The Schatten bound (
33
) provides a substitute for the compactness arguments
[
43
, Proposition 2.1-2.2]
. For the remainder of the proof one can follow the arguments in
[
43
]
verbatim.

Corollary 11
.

If
V\in\ell^{p}L^{q}
with
q
in the range (
18
) and
p<\infty
, then we have
S=\emptyset
, i.e.
\sigma_{\rm e}(H_{V})=[0,\infty)
. The same holds for
H_{\rm diag}
.

Proof.

By (
34
), we have that
\lim_{t\to\infty}\|BS_{V}(\mathrm{i}t)\|=0
, which means that the inverse exists as a bounded operator for
z=\mathrm{i}t
and
t\gg 1
. Corollary
7
implies that
R_{0}(z)V^{1/2}
is compact, whence the resolvent difference (
26
) is compact. The claim now follows by Weyl’s theorem
[
25
, Theorem IX.2.4]
.
∎

3.4.
Magnitude bounds

The following universal bounds generalize those of Theorem

Theorem 12
.

Let

\displaystyle\omega_{q}(z)^{-1}\langle s(L,z)\rangle^{-2}\lesssim\|V\|_{\ell^{\infty}L^{q}}

(43)

Remark 3
.

(i)
In the case of a single “bump” (

]
for

\displaystyle|\mathrm{Im}\,z|^{1-\frac{q_{d}}{q}}\lesssim(\mathrm{Re}\,z)^{-\frac{1}{2q}}.

In the case

on the right. If

\displaystyle|\mathrm{Im}\,z|^{1-\frac{q_{d}}{q}+\frac{2}{\alpha}}\lesssim(\mathrm{Re}\,z)^{-\frac{1}{2q}+\frac{1}{\alpha}}\eta_{0}^{-\frac{2}{\alpha}},

see Example a) after Definition

. Hence, for sufficiently large

(depending on

and

) the exponent of

remains positive, while that of

5.2.

In the following we establish criteria on the sequence

that guarantee proximity of

Let us fist discuss some standard facts from perturbation theory (
[

(and no other point of the spectrum) once in the counterclockwise direction. Then

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2\pi\mathrm{i}}\Tr\int_{\Gamma}(\zeta-H)^{-1}{\rm d}\zeta=n.

(64)

Here we consider a finite system of eigenvalues of

in some compact subset

. By Corollary

We then have

\displaystyle\delta_{0}(\Sigma):=\min\{{\rm d}(\zeta,\sigma(H_{\rm diag})\setminus\{\zeta\}):\zeta\in\Sigma\cap\sigma(H_{\rm diag})\}>0.

(65)

For

we set

\displaystyle U_{\delta}:=\bigcup_{\zeta\in\Sigma\cap\sigma(H_{\rm diag})}D(\zeta,\delta),\quad\Gamma_{\delta}:=\partial U_{\delta}.

(66)

In general it is hard to determine

, but we still have

for generic

.
The norm resolvent convergence (Proposition

we will give an alternative proof using the argument principle.

Let us first state our assumptions for the remainder of this section.

\displaystyle\|(H_{V}-\zeta_{j})\psi_{j}\|\leq\varepsilon_{j}.

Remark 4
.

Lemma
21
could be seen as a quantitative version of Lemma 2 in
[
5
]
.

Proof.

Let
\psi_{j}
be the eigenfunctions of

corresponding to
\zeta_{j}
, i.e.
(H_{V_{j}}-\zeta_{j})\psi_{j}=0
. For

we make the following (stronger) induction hypothesis

\displaystyle\|(H^{(n)}-\zeta_{j})\psi_{j}\|\leq\varepsilon_{j}\big(1-\frac{1}{\log\langle n\rangle}\big),\quad j\in[n]

(78)

(recall (

) for the definition of

).
The base case

is true by assumption.
Assume now that

holds. By the exponential decay (

),

\displaystyle\|(H^{(n)}-\zeta_{n})\psi_{n}\|

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{j=1}^{n-1}\|V_{j}\psi_{n}\|\leq nC_{q}a_{n}^{-d/\widetilde{q}}\exp(-c_{0}\eta_{n}\,L_{n})\sup_{j\in[n]}\|V_{j}\|_{L^{\widetilde{q}}},

where we have set
\eta_{n}:=\mathrm{Im}\,\sqrt{\zeta_{n}}
.
Moreover, by induction hypothesis, for

\displaystyle\|(H^{(n)}-\zeta_{j})\psi_{j}\|

\displaystyle\leq\|(H^{(n-1)}-\zeta_{j})\psi_{j}\|+\|V_{n}\psi_{j}\|

\displaystyle\leq\varepsilon_{j}\big(1-\frac{1}{\log\langle n-1\rangle}\big)+C_{q}a_{j}^{-d/\widetilde{q}}\exp(-c_{0}\eta_{j}\,L_{n})\|V_{n}\|_{L^{\widetilde{q}}}.

P(n)
would hold if
L_{n}
satisfied the estimates

\displaystyle nC_{q}a_{n}^{-d/\widetilde{q}}\exp(-c_{0}\eta_{n}\,L_{n})\sup_{j\in[n]}\|V_{j}\|_{L^{\widetilde{q}}}\leq\varepsilon_{n},

(79)

\displaystyle C_{q}a_{j}^{-d/\widetilde{q}}\exp(-c_{0}\eta_{j}\,L_{n})\|V_{n}\|_{L^{\widetilde{q}}}\leq\varepsilon_{j}\big(\frac{1}{\log\langle n-1\rangle}-\frac{1}{\log\langle n\rangle}\big),

(80)

for

By the mean value theorem

and it is easy to check that (
79
)–(
80
) are satisfied for the choice (
77
). This completes the induction step. For

) with

Since

is separating,

\displaystyle\lim_{n\to\infty}\|(H_{V}-H^{(n)})\psi_{j}\|^{2}

\displaystyle=\lim_{n\to\infty}\|(V-V^{(n)})\psi_{j}\|^{2}=\lim_{n\to\infty}\sum_{k=n+1}^{\infty}\|V_{k}\psi_{j}\|^{2}

\displaystyle\leq a_{j}^{-d/\widetilde{q}}\|V\|_{\ell^{\infty}L^{\widetilde{q}}}^{2}\lim_{n\to\infty}\sum_{k=n+1}^{\infty}\exp(-2\eta_{j}L_{k})=0.

Together with (

6.4.
Exponential decay

We prove that the exponential decay bound (
76
) holds for a class of compactly supported potentials that will be relevant in the next section. The important point here is that the constant
C
in (
85
) is independent of

.

Lemma 23
.

Assume that
\supp W\subset B(0,R)
and
\zeta\in[0,\infty)
,
\mathrm{Im}\,\sqrt{\zeta}\leq\tfrac{1}{2}R^{-1}\log R
,
|\zeta|^{1/2}\geq KR^{-1}
for a large absolute constant
K
. Assume that
\psi
is a normalized eigenfunction of
H_{W}
with eigenvalue
\zeta
. Then there exists an absolute constant
C=C(d)
such that for
|x|>R
,

\displaystyle|\psi(x)|\leq C|\zeta|^{1/4}|x|^{-\frac{d-1}{2}}\exp(-\mathrm{Im}\,\sqrt{\zeta}|x|).

(85)

Proof.

Since
\psi
is normalized in
L^{2}
it has units
l^{-d/2}
. By homogeneity, we may thus assume that
|\zeta|=1
. Since
\psi
solves the Helmholtz equation

\displaystyle-\Delta\psi(x)=\kappa^{2}\psi(x)

for
|x|>R
and
\kappa^{2}=\zeta
, we have (see e.g.
[
79
, Chapter 1, Section 2]
)

\displaystyle\psi(x)=A|x|^{-\nu}H^{(1)}_{\nu}(\kappa|x|)

in this region, where
H^{(1)}_{\nu}
is the Hankel function,
\nu=(d-2)/2
and
A=A(d,W)
is a normalization constant. By the well-known asymptotics of the Hankel function at infinity,

\displaystyle\psi(x)=Ac_{d}|x|^{-\frac{d-1}{2}}\exp(-\mathrm{Im}\,\kappa|x|)(1+\mathcal{O}(|x|^{-1})).

This would imply (
85
) if we could show that
A
has an upper bound independent of
W
. Since
\psi
is normalized,

\displaystyle A^{2}c_{d}^{2}\int_{|x|>R}|x|^{-(d-1)}\exp(-2\mathrm{Im}\,\kappa|x|)(1+\mathcal{O}(|x|^{-1})){\rm d}x\leq\|\psi\|^{2}=1.

For sufficiently large
K
we estimate the integral from below by

\displaystyle(1-\mathcal{O}(K^{-1}))\int_{R}^{2R}\exp(-2\mathrm{Im}\,\kappa r){\rm d}r\geq(1-\mathcal{O}(K^{-1}))R\exp(-\log R)\geq\frac{1}{4},

which proves that
A\leq 2/c_{d}
.
∎

Corollary 24
.

Assume that
V_{j}(x)=W_{j}(x-x_{j})
and that the assumptions of Lemma
23
are satisfied for
W_{j},\zeta,\psi_{j},R_{j}
. Then (

) holds for any

and with

\displaystyle a_{j}^{-d/\widetilde{q}}\lesssim|\zeta_{j}|^{1/4}\Big(\frac{L_{j}}{\mathrm{Im}\,\sqrt{\zeta_{j}}}\Big)^{-\frac{d-1}{2}(\frac{1}{2}-\frac{1}{\widetilde{q}})}.

Proof.

Let
\tfrac{1}{\widetilde{q}}+\tfrac{1}{r}=\tfrac{1}{2}
. By Hölder,

\displaystyle\|V_{i}\psi_{j}\|\leq\|V_{i}\|_{L^{\widetilde{q}}}\|\psi_{j}\|_{L^{r}(\Omega_{i})}

and by (

),

\displaystyle\|\psi_{j}\|_{L^{r}(\Omega_{i})}\lesssim|\zeta_{j}|^{1/4}\big(\int_{\Omega_{i}}(|x-x_{j}|^{-\frac{d-1}{2}}\exp(-\mathrm{Im}\,\sqrt{\zeta_{j}}\,|x-x_{j}|))^{r}{\rm d}x\big)^{1/r}.

Since

\displaystyle\|\psi_{j}\|_{L^{r}(\Omega_{i})}\lesssim|\zeta_{j}|^{1/4}\Big(\frac{L_{j}}{\mathrm{Im}\,\sqrt{\zeta_{j}}}\Big)^{-\frac{d-1}{2r}}.

The claim follows.

7.
Complex step potential

In this section we will establish precise estimates for eigenvalues of the sperically symmetric complex step potential
V=V_{0}\mathbf{1}_{B(0,R)}
, where
V_{0}\in{\mathbb{C}}
and
R>0
. The bound state problem for
V_{0}<0
and
d=1,3
is treated in virtually any quantum mechanics textbook (see e.g. Problem 25 and Problem 63 in
[
26
]
).
We adopt the notation

\displaystyle\chi=\sqrt{E},\quad\kappa=\sqrt{\chi^{2}-V_{0}}.

(106)

Here
E\in{\mathbb{C}}
is the eigenvalue parameter, i.e. we consider the stationary Schrödinger equation

\displaystyle-\Delta\psi+(V-E)\psi=0,

(107)

which becomes
-\Delta\psi-\kappa^{2}\psi=0

inside the step and

-\Delta\psi-\chi^{2}\psi=0

outside the step.

7.1.
One dimension

We start with one-dimensional case. The solution space to (
107
) then splits into even and odd functions, while in higher dimensions it splits into functions with definite angular momentum
\ell
. We consider odd functions as these also provide a solution for the case
d=3
and
\ell=0
(
s
-waves). The standard procedure to solving the square well problem reduces the task to finding zeros of the nonlinear scalar function
F(V_{0},\kappa):=\mathrm{i}\chi-\kappa\cot(\kappa R)
, where
\chi=\sqrt{\kappa^{2}+V_{0}}
by (
106
). A complete study of all the complex poles of this equation was initiated by Nussenzveig
[
56
]
for
V_{0}\in{\mathbb{R}}\setminus\{0\}
. Subsequent articles in the physics literature
[
39
]
,
[
18
]
,
[
17
]
,
[
35
]
investigated the case of complex potentials. The solution
\kappa=\kappa(V_{0})
is not single-valued as there are branch points where
\partial F/\partial\chi=0
. The viewpoint endorsed by
[
35
]
is to regard the equation
F(V_{0},\kappa)=0
as the definition of a Riemann surface. This approach treats the complex variables
\kappa
and
V_{0}
on equal footing. In fact, it is easy to see that one can always use
\kappa
as a coordinate, i.e. one can solve for
V_{0}
,

\displaystyle V_{0}=-\kappa^{2}\sec^{2}(\kappa R).

(108)

For the purpose of the construction of the sparse potential in Subsection
6.5
we do not need to solve for
\kappa
. Instead, we pick
\kappa
first and then define
V_{0}
by (
108
). To get an eigenvalue (i.e. a resonance on the physical sheet) we simply need to take care of the condition
\mathrm{Im}\,\chi>0
, i.e.

\displaystyle\mathrm{Re}\,(\kappa R\cot(\kappa R))>0.

(109)

We are only interested in complex eigenvalues
E
with
|E|\asymp 1
(the general case can be obtained by scaling). We will try to make
V_{0}
in (
108
) small, i.e. we postulate that
V_{0}=\epsilon\widetilde{V}_{0}
, where
\epsilon>0
is a small parameter and
\widetilde{V}_{0}\in{\mathbb{C}}
is of unit size. By (
106
) this implies that
|\kappa|\asymp 1
, and (
108
) then reveals that
|\sin^{2}(\kappa R)|\asymp\epsilon^{-1}
, which means that

\displaystyle{\rm e}^{2\mathrm{Im}\,\kappa R}\asymp\epsilon^{-1},\quad{\rm e}^{-2\mathrm{Im}\,\kappa R}\asymp\epsilon.

(110)

Since we are free to choose
\kappa
,
set
\kappa=\pm 1+\mathrm{i}\epsilon\sigma
with
\sigma>0
, which will yield an eigenvalue with
\mathrm{Re}\,E=1+\mathcal{O}(\epsilon)
and
|\mathrm{Im}\,E|=\mathcal{O}(\epsilon)
.
Going back to (
110
) we see that we must have

\displaystyle R=\frac{1}{2\sigma\epsilon}\log\frac{C}{\epsilon}

(111)

for some constant
C
. It is quickly checked that this is consistent with the bound of Abramov et al.
[
1
]
since
\|V\|_{L^{1}}\gtrsim\log\frac{1}{\epsilon}
and
|E|\asymp 1
. In view of (
110
) we may write
{\rm e}^{2\mathrm{i}\kappa R}=\epsilon u
, where
u=C{\rm e}^{2\mathrm{i}\mathrm{Re}\,\kappa R}
. Using the Taylor approximation

\displaystyle\sec^{2}(\kappa R)=-4\epsilon u(1+2\epsilon u+\mathcal{O}(\epsilon^{2}))

(112)

we obtain from (
108
) that

\displaystyle V_{0}=4\epsilon u(\mathrm{Re}\,\kappa)^{2}+\mathcal{O}(\epsilon^{2}),

(113)

which provides the desired smallness
|V_{0}|=\mathcal{O}(\epsilon)
. As already mentioned, we need to make sure that (
109
) holds. Using the Taylor approximation

\displaystyle\cot(\kappa R)=\mathrm{i}(1+2\epsilon u+\mathcal{O}(\epsilon^{2})),

we find that (
109
) holds if

\displaystyle-(\mathrm{Re}\,\kappa)(\mathrm{Im}\,u)-(\mathrm{Im}\,\kappa)(\mathrm{Re}\,u)>0

(114)

In particular, for
u\in\mathrm{i}{\mathbb{R}}_{+}
, we find that (
114
) forces us to choose
\mathrm{Re}\,\kappa=-1
. Adopting this choice for
u
, it is then easy to check that we get an eigenvalue with
\mathrm{Re}\,E=1+\mathcal{O}(\epsilon)
and
\mathrm{Im}\,E=\epsilon(4|u|-2\sigma)+\mathcal{O}(\epsilon^{2})
as desired. By simple scaling arguments this proves the one-dimensional case of Lemma
22
. We observe that the result is consistent with the trivial numerical range bound
\mathrm{Im}\,E\leq\mathrm{Im}\,V_{0}
; in fact, by choosing
\sigma
small,
E
can be taken arbitrarily close to the boundary of the numerical range
\mathrm{Im}\,z=4\epsilon|u|
, up to errors of order
\epsilon^{2}
.

Before we conclude the one-dimensional case we note that the same result could have been obtained with an even wavefunction, in which case
\sec
replaced by
\csc
in (
108
) and
\cot
is replaced by
\tan
in (
109
). The Taylor approximations

\displaystyle\csc^{2}(\kappa R)=4\epsilon u(1+\mathcal{O}(\epsilon)),\quad\tan(\kappa R)=\mathrm{i}(1-2\epsilon u+\mathcal{O}(\epsilon^{2})),

(115)

and the freedom to choose the signs and the imaginary part of
u
yields a proof of Lemma
22
using odd solutions.

7.2.
Higher dimensions

By symmetry reductions we are led to consider the radial Schrödinger equation

\displaystyle(-\partial_{r}^{2}-\frac{d-1}{r}\partial_{r}+\frac{\ell(\ell+d-2)}{r^{2}}+V(r)-E)\psi_{\ell}(r)=0.

It can be shown (see e.g.
[
10
, (5.12)]
) that an eigenvalue
E
corresponds to a zero of the function (Wronskian)

\displaystyle F(V_{0},\kappa):=\kappa J_{\nu}^{\prime}(\kappa R)H^{(1)}_{\nu}(\chi R)-\chi J_{\nu}(\kappa R)H^{(1)^{\prime}}_{\nu}(\chi R),

(116)

where
\nu=\ell+\frac{d-2}{2}
. We recall that
\chi,\kappa,E,V_{0}
are related by (
106
). Computations of resonances for spherically symmetric potentials can be
found in
[
55
]
,
[
82
]
,
[
74
]
,
[
10
]
. The last three papers use uniform asymptotic expansion of Bessel functions for large order. Here we only consider
s
-waves, i.e.
\ell=0
. Then we have the asymptotics

\displaystyle J_{\nu}(z)

\displaystyle=\big(\frac{2}{\pi z}\big)^{1/2}\cos(z-\frac{\pi\nu}{2}-\frac{\pi}{4})(1+\mathcal{O}(|z|^{-1})),

(117)

\displaystyle H^{(1)}_{\nu}(z)

\displaystyle=\big(\frac{2}{\pi z}\big)^{1/2}\exp(\mathrm{i}z-\mathrm{i}\frac{\pi\nu}{2}-\mathrm{i}\frac{\pi}{4})(1+\mathcal{O}(|z|^{-1})),

(118)

\displaystyle J_{\nu}^{\prime}(z)

\displaystyle=-\big(\frac{2}{\pi z}\big)^{1/2}\sin(z-\frac{\pi\nu}{2}-\frac{\pi}{4})(1+\mathcal{O}(|z|^{-1})),

(119)

\displaystyle H^{(1)^{\prime}}_{\nu}(z)

\displaystyle=\mathrm{i}\big(\frac{2}{\pi z}\big)^{1/2}\exp(\mathrm{i}z-\mathrm{i}\frac{\pi\nu}{2}-\mathrm{i}\frac{\pi}{4})(1+\mathcal{O}(|z|^{-1}).

(120)

With the same choice of
\kappa
as in the one-dimensional case and with
u\in{\mathbb{C}}
such that
{\rm e}^{2\mathrm{i}(\kappa R-\frac{\pi\nu}{2}-\frac{\pi}{4})}=\epsilon u
, we then obtain that the zeros of
F(V_{0},\kappa)
coincide with the zeros of a function

\displaystyle\kappa\sin(\omega(\kappa R))-\mathrm{i}\chi\cos(\omega(\kappa R))+\mathcal{O}(R^{-1}),

(121)

where
\omega(\kappa R)=\kappa R-\frac{\pi\nu}{2}-\frac{\pi}{4}
and the
\kappa
-derivative of the error term is
\mathcal{O}(1)
. Recall that
\chi=\chi(V_{0},\kappa)
is given by (
106
). The zeros of the function without the error term are found exactly as in the one-dimensional case and can be parametrized by
\kappa
, v.i.z.

\displaystyle V_{0}=-\kappa^{2}\csc^{2}(\omega(\kappa R)).

(122)

This follows by dividing the above expression by
\cos(\omega(\kappa R))
which has no zeros since
\mathrm{Im}\,\kappa>0
. Since
|\cos(\omega(\kappa R))^{-1}|=\mathcal{O}(\epsilon^{1/2})
we get from (
111
) that the error after dividing is
\mathcal{O}(\epsilon^{3/2})
, i.e. we are looking for the zeros of a function

\displaystyle\widetilde{F}(V_{0},\kappa)=V_{0}+\kappa^{2}\csc^{2}(\omega(\kappa R))+\mathcal{O}(\epsilon^{3/2}),

(123)

where the derivative of the error is
\mathcal{O}(\epsilon^{1/2})
.
The implicit function theorem thus yields
\partial\widetilde{F}(V_{0},\kappa)/\partial V_{0}=1+\mathcal{O}(\epsilon^{1/2})
, which means that we can solve
\widetilde{F}(V_{0},\kappa)
for
V_{0}
, and the solution satisfies (
122
) up to errors
\mathcal{O}(\epsilon^{3/2})
. Hence we obtain that
|V_{0}|=\mathcal{O}(\epsilon)
as before.

Proof of Theorem
4

We return to one dimension. We first prove the upper bound (
12
). Since
\sqrt{|V_{0}|}R_{0}
is of order one, the bound in
[
29
]
yields that the total number of eigenvalues of
H_{V_{j}}
is also of order one. By Proposition
17
(it is clear that the assumption on the norm of
V
can be dropped), given
N\gg 1
, we can find
L=L(N)
such that
H_{\rm s}
has the same number of eigenvalues in
\Sigma=\Sigma(N)
as
H_{\rm diag}
, which is just the
N
-fold orthogonal sum of the
H_{V_{j}}
and hence has less than
\mathcal{O}(N)
eigenvalues by the first part of the argument.

To prove the lower bound (
11
) we return to the formulas (
108
), (
109
), but we now fix
V_{0}=\mathrm{i}
. We also set
R=NR_{0}
and
R_{0}\asymp 1
, so that the dimensionless parameter
\sqrt{|V_{0}|}R
is of size
N
. We first solve an approximate equation and then use Rouché’s theorem to show that the exact equation (
108
) has solutions close to the approximate ones. Finally, we use (
109
) to check that we have found a pole on the physical plane (i.e. an eigenvalue). The approximate equation is
G_{1}(\kappa)=0
, where

\displaystyle G_{1}(\kappa):=V_{0}-4\kappa^{2}{\rm e}^{2\mathrm{i}\kappa R},

and the approximation will be valid in the regime
\mathrm{Im}\,\kappa R\gg 1
. Since
G_{1}
can be factored,

\displaystyle G_{1}(\kappa)=(\sqrt{V_{0}}-2\kappa{\rm e}^{\mathrm{i}\kappa R})(\sqrt{V_{0}}+2\kappa{\rm e}^{\mathrm{i}\kappa R}),

we only look for zeros of the first factor.
These zeros
\kappa_{n}
are expressed by means of the Lambert
W
function,

\displaystyle\kappa_{n}R=-\mathrm{i}W_{n}(\mathrm{i}\sqrt{V_{0}}R/2),

where
n\in{\mathbb{Z}}
and
W_{n}
are the branches of the Lambert
W
function. According to in
[
11
, (4.19)]
the asymptotic expansion of
W_{n}(z)
as
|z|\to\infty
is

\displaystyle W_{n}(z)=\log(z)+2\pi\mathrm{i}n-\log(\log(z)+2\pi\mathrm{i}n)+\mathcal{O}(\frac{\log(\log(z)+2\pi\mathrm{i}n)}{\log(z)+2\pi\mathrm{i}n}),

(124)

where
\log
is the principal branch of the logarithm on the slit plane with the negative real axis as branch cut. For
z=\mathrm{i}\sqrt{V_{0}}R/2
this gives

\displaystyle\kappa_{n}R=2\pi n-\mathrm{i}\log(\mathrm{i}\sqrt{V_{0}}R/2)+\mathrm{i}\log(\log(\mathrm{i}\sqrt{V_{0}}R/2)+2\pi\mathrm{i}n)+E_{n}(V_{0},R),

(125)

where, for
N\gg 1
, the error satisfies
|E_{n}(V_{0},R)|\lesssim\log(\log N+|n|)/(\log N+|n|)
, where we recalled that
\sqrt{|V_{0}|}R\asymp N
. For the assumption
\mathrm{Im}\,\kappa R\gg 1
made before to be consistent with the formula for
\kappa_{n}
we require

\displaystyle\mathrm{Re}\,\log\left(\frac{\log(\mathrm{i}\sqrt{V_{0}}R/2)+2\pi\mathrm{i}n}{\mathrm{i}\sqrt{V_{0}}R/2}\right)\gg 1\iff\left|\frac{\log(\mathrm{i}\sqrt{V_{0}}R/2)+2\pi\mathrm{i}n}{\mathrm{i}\sqrt{V_{0}}R/2}\right|\gg 1.

Since
N\gg 1
we can neglect the logarithm in the second expression and deduce the condition
|n|\gg N
, which we will assume henceforth. This gives us the error bound
|E_{n}(V_{0},R)|\lesssim\log|n|/|n|
, which implies that

\displaystyle\mathrm{Im}\,\kappa_{n}R\gtrsim\log\frac{|n|}{N},

(126)

in agreement with the assumption
\mathrm{Im}\,\kappa R\gg 1
. We also obtain the more precise formulas

\displaystyle\mathrm{Re}\,\kappa_{n}R=2\pi n+\frac{5\pi}{4}+\mathcal{O}(\log|n|/|n|),\quad\mathrm{Im}\,\kappa_{n}R=\log\frac{|n|}{N}+\mathcal{O}(1),

(127)

where we assumed that
n<0
. To justify this assumption, we recall from the discussion at the end of Subsection
7.1
that (
113
) and (
114
), together with (
127
) and the assumption
V_{0}=\mathrm{i}
made at the beginning of this subsection, imply that
n
must be negative.

Having found the large zeros of
G_{1}(\kappa)
we proceed to find those of

\displaystyle G_{2}(\kappa):=V_{0}+\kappa^{2}\sec^{2}(\kappa R),

which determines the eigenvalues of the step potential (see the beginning of Subsection
7.1
). We define
\epsilon_{n}:=\exp(-2\mathrm{Im}\,\kappa_{n}R)
, so that
{\rm e}^{2\mathrm{i}\kappa_{n}R}=\epsilon_{n}u_{n}
for some
u_{n}
on the unit circle. Note that, by (
127
),
\epsilon_{n}=\mathcal{O}(1)\big(\frac{N}{|n|}\big)^{2}
. Using (
112
) with
\epsilon=\epsilon_{n}
,
u=u_{n}
, we estimate, for
\widetilde{\epsilon}_{n}\ll 1
,

\displaystyle\sup_{|\kappa-\kappa_{n}|=\widetilde{\epsilon}_{n}}|G_{2}(\kappa)-G_{1}(\kappa)|=\mathcal{O}(n^{2}\epsilon_{n}^{2}).

(128)

Moreover, for
|\kappa-\kappa_{n}|=\widetilde{\epsilon}_{n}
we have

\displaystyle|G_{1}^{\prime}(\kappa)|

\displaystyle\gtrsim|\kappa|^{2}R{\rm e}^{-2\mathrm{Im}\,\kappa R}\gtrsim Nn^{2}\epsilon_{n},

\displaystyle|G_{1}^{\prime\prime}(\kappa)|

\displaystyle\lesssim|\kappa|^{2}R^{2}{\rm e}^{-2\mathrm{Im}\,\kappa R}\lesssim N^{2}n^{2}\epsilon_{n}.

Using
G_{1}(\kappa_{n})=0
and Taylor expanding, it follows that

\displaystyle|G_{1}(\kappa)|\gtrsim Nn^{2}\epsilon_{n}\widetilde{\epsilon}_{n}+\mathcal{O}(N^{2}n^{2}\epsilon_{n}\widetilde{\epsilon}_{n}^{2}).

(129)

For this to be meaningful we must of course assume
\widetilde{\epsilon}_{n}\ll 1/N
, which we do. Then we have
|G_{1}(\kappa)|\gtrsim Nn^{2}\epsilon_{n}\widetilde{\epsilon}_{n}
. Comparing this with (
128
) we see that

\displaystyle\sup_{|\kappa-\kappa_{n}|=\widetilde{\epsilon}_{n}}|G_{1}(\kappa)|^{-1}|G_{2}(\kappa)-G_{1}(\kappa)|<1,

provided
\widetilde{\epsilon}_{n}\gg\epsilon_{n}/N
. Adopting the choice
\widetilde{\epsilon}_{n}=C\frac{N}{n^{2}}
, where
C
is a large constant, we see that there exists a zero
\widetilde{\kappa}_{n}\in D(\kappa_{n},C\frac{N}{n^{2}})
of
G_{2}
. By the smallness of
N/n^{2}
, it follows that
\widetilde{\kappa}_{n}
also satisfies (
127
). We drop the tilde, i.e. we now denote the zeros of
G_{2}
by
\kappa_{n}
. Summarizing what we have done so far, we have found infinitely many resonances
\kappa_{n}
,
|n|\gg N
, of of the step potential satisfying (
127
). The last step is to check which of the resonances lie on the physical sheet, i.e. are actual eigenvalues. For this we need to check condition (
109
). By (
127
),

\displaystyle\cot(\kappa_{n}R)=-\mathrm{i}\big(1+\mathrm{i}\big(\frac{N}{|n|}\big)^{2}{\rm e}^{\mathcal{O}(1)+\mathrm{i}\mathcal{O}(\log|n|/|n|)}+\mathcal{O}(\big(\frac{N}{|n|}\big)^{4})\big),

\displaystyle\mathrm{Re}\,(\kappa_{n}R\cot(\kappa_{n}R))=2\pi\frac{N^{2}}{n}{\rm e}^{\mathcal{O}(1)}(1+\mathcal{O}(\log^{2}|n|/|n|))+\log\frac{|n|}{N}(1+\mathcal{O}(\log|n|/|n|)).

Hence, recalling that
n<0
, the condition (
109
) is nonvoid and is satisfied whenever
|n|\log\frac{|n|}{N}\ll N^{2}
; this holds for
|n|\ll N^{2}/\log N
. Recalling (
106
) we obtain the complex energies
E=E_{n}
,

\displaystyle\mathrm{Re}\,E_{n}\asymp\frac{n^{2}}{N^{2}},\quad\mathrm{Im}\,E_{n}\asymp\frac{|n|}{N^{2}}\log\frac{|n|}{N},

and those energies with
c\frac{N^{2}}{\log N}\leq|n|\leq C\frac{N^{2}}{\log N}
lie in the rectangle
\Sigma
(see Theorem
4
).
This completes the proof of the lower bound (
11
).

8.
Technical tools

8.1.
Lower bounds on moduli of holomorphic functions

We collect some well known results about the modulus of holomorphic functions away from zeros, based on Cartan’s bound for polynomials (see e.g.
[
50
]
).

Let
U_{1}\Subset U_{2}\Subset{\mathbb{C}}
, where
U_{2}
is simply connected. Assume that
f
is holomorphic in a neighborhood of
U_{2}
and
\zeta_{2}\in U_{2}
. Let
z_{1},z_{2}\ldots,z_{n}
, be the zeros of
f
in
U_{2}
. Define

\displaystyle Z_{f,\delta,U_{2}}:=\bigcup_{j=1}^{n}D(z_{j},\delta).

The following version can be found in
[
24
, Appendix D]
.

Lemma 32
.

There exists a constant
C=C(U_{1},U_{2},z_{0})
such that for any sufficiently small
\delta>0
,

\displaystyle\log|f(z)|\geq-C\log\frac{1}{\delta}\big(\log|f(z)|-\log\max_{z\in U_{2}}|f(\zeta_{2})|\big)

for all
z\in U_{1}\setminus Z_{f,\delta,U_{2}}
.

We need also use a more precise version, where
f
is holomorphic in a neighborhood of
U_{3}
,

\displaystyle U_{j}=D(0,r_{j}),\quad j=1,2,3,

(130)

with
r_{1}<r_{2}<r_{3}
and
\zeta_{2}=0
.

Lemma 33
.

Assume (
130
). Then there exists an absolute constant
C
such that for any sufficiently small
\delta>0
,

\displaystyle\log|f(z)|\geq-C\log\frac{1}{\delta}\big((r_{2}-r_{1})^{-1}+\log^{-1}\big(\frac{r_{3}}{r_{2}}\big)\big)\big(\log\max_{|z|=r_{3}}|f(z)|-\log|f(\zeta_{2})|\big)

for all
z\in U_{1}\setminus Z_{f,\delta r_{2},U_{2}}
.

Proof.

The proof is a straightforward adaptation of
[
50
, Chapter 1, Theorem 11]
, but we include it for completeness.
In the following,
r_{1},r_{2},r_{3}
play the roles of
R,2R,2{\rm e}R
in
[
50
]
. Without loss of generality we may and will assume that
\zeta_{2}=0
and that
f(0)=1
; otherwise we could replace
f(z)
by
\frac{f(z+\zeta_{2})}{f(\zeta_{2})}
. Consider the function

\displaystyle\varphi(z):=\frac{(-r_{2})^{n}}{z_{1}z_{2}\ldots z_{n}}\prod_{k=1}^{n}\frac{r_{2}(z-z_{k})}{r_{2}^{2}-\overline{z_{k}}z}.

We recall that
z_{1},z_{2},\ldots,z_{n}
are the zeros of
f
in
D(0,r_{2})
. Observe that
\varphi(0)=1
and

\displaystyle\varphi(r_{2}{\rm e}^{\mathrm{i}\theta})=\frac{r_{2}^{n}}{|z_{1}z_{2}\ldots z_{n}|}

for
\theta\in{\mathbb{R}}
. The function

\displaystyle\Psi(z):=\frac{f(z)}{\varphi(z)}

has no zeros in
D(0,r_{2})
and satisfies
\psi(0)=1
; therefore, by Carathéodory’s theorem
[
50
, Theorem 9]
, for
|z|\leq r_{1}
,

\displaystyle\log|\psi(z)|

\displaystyle\geq-\frac{2r_{1}}{r_{2}-r_{1}}\big(\log\max_{|z|=r_{2}}|f(z)|+\log\frac{r_{2}^{n}}{|z_{1}z_{2}\ldots z_{n}|}\big)

\displaystyle\geq-\frac{2r_{1}}{r_{2}-r_{1}}\log\max_{|z|=r_{2}}|f(z)|.

To estimate
\varphi
from below for
|z|\leq r_{1}
we use

\displaystyle\prod_{k=1}^{n}|r_{2}^{2}-\overline{z_{k}}z|

\displaystyle<(2r_{2}^{2})^{n},

\displaystyle\prod_{k=1}^{n}|r_{2}(z-z_{k})|

\displaystyle>\left(\frac{\delta r_{2}}{{\rm e}}\right)^{n}r_{2}^{n},\quad z\notin Z_{f,2\delta r_{2},U_{2}}.

The second inequality follows from Cartan’s estimate
[
50
, Theorem 10]
. We thus obtain the lower bound

\displaystyle|\varphi(z)|>(2r_{2}^{2})^{-n}\left(\frac{\delta r_{2}}{{\rm e}}\right)^{n}\frac{r_{2}^{2n}}{|z_{1}z_{2}\ldots z_{n}|}>\left(\frac{\delta}{2{\rm e}}\right)^{n},\quad z\notin Z_{f,2\delta r_{2},U_{2}}.

By Jensen’s formula
[
50
, Lemma 4]
, since
f(0)=1
,

\displaystyle n\leq\log^{-1}\big(\frac{r_{3}}{r_{2}}\big)\log\max_{|z|=r_{3}}|f(z)|,

and consequently

\displaystyle\log|\varphi(z)|>\log^{-1}\big(\frac{r_{3}}{r_{2}}\big)\log\max_{|z|=r_{3}}|f(z)|\log\left(\frac{\delta}{2{\rm e}}\right),\quad z\notin Z_{f,2\delta r_{2},U_{2}}.

Together with the lower bound for
\log|\psi|
this leads to the claimed estimate, upon redefining
\delta
and absorbing an error into the constant
C
.
∎

Next we state a version of Lemma
33
for “wedges” of the form

\displaystyle W(\varphi,\theta;r,R):=\{z\in{\mathbb{C}}\setminus[0,\infty):\arg(z)\in(2\varphi,2\theta),\,|z|\in(r^{2},R^{2})\}

(131)

and
W(\varphi,\theta;R):=W(\varphi,\theta;R,\infty)
, where
0\leq\varphi<\theta\leq\pi
. In the following we fix
0\leq\varphi_{3}<\varphi_{2}<\varphi_{1}<\theta_{1}<\theta_{2}<\theta_{3}\leq\pi
and
0<r_{3}<r_{2}<r_{1}<R_{1}<R_{2}
, and define

\displaystyle U_{1}:=W(\varphi_{1},\theta_{1};r_{1},R_{1}),\quad U_{2}:=W(\varphi_{2},\theta_{2};r_{2},R_{2}),\quad U_{3}:=W(\varphi_{3},\theta_{3};r_{3}).

Lemma 34
.

Assume
f
is a bounded holomorphic function on
U_{3}
and that

\displaystyle r_{3}\ll r_{2},\!\!\!\!\quad\frac{{\rm d}(\partial U_{2},\partial U_{3})}{{\rm d}(\partial U_{1},\partial U_{3})}\ll\left(\frac{r_{2}}{R_{2}}\right)^{\frac{2\pi}{\theta_{3}-\varphi_{3}}+2},\!\!\!\!\quad\frac{{\rm d}(\partial U_{1},\partial U_{3})}{(\theta_{3}-\varphi_{3})R_{2}^{2}}\left(\frac{r_{2}}{R_{2}}\right)^{\frac{2\pi}{\theta_{3}-\varphi_{3}}}\ll 1.

(132)

Then there exists an absolute constant
C
such that for any
\zeta_{2}\in U_{2}
and any sufficiently small
\delta>0
,

\displaystyle\log|f(z)|\geq-C\frac{R_{2}^{2}}{{\rm d}(\partial U_{2},\partial U_{3})}\left(\frac{R_{2}}{r_{2}}\right)^{\frac{2\pi}{\theta_{3}-\varphi_{3}}}\log\frac{1}{\delta}\big(\log\max_{z\in U_{3}}|f(z)|-\log|f(\zeta_{2})|\big)

(133)

for all
z\in U_{1}\setminus Z_{f,\delta,U_{2}}
.

Remark 9
.

The constant
C
only depends on the implicit constants in (
132
). Hence, one can optimize the inequality with respect to
\zeta_{2}
, subject to the conditions above.

Proof.

We map
U_{3}
conformally onto the unit disk, using a composition of the following conformal maps (where, by abuse of notation, we denote the variable and the map by the same letter):

i)

U_{3}\to\kappa(U_{3})\subset\mathbb{H}
,
\kappa(z):=\sqrt{z}
;

ii)

\kappa(U_{3})\to S:=\{\sigma\in{\mathbb{C}}:0<\mathrm{Im}\,\sigma<\pi,\,\mathrm{Re}\,\sigma>0\}
,

\displaystyle\sigma(\kappa):=\log({\rm e}^{-\frac{\mathrm{i}\pi\varphi_{3}}{\theta_{3}-\varphi_{3}}}(\kappa/r_{3})^{\frac{\pi}{\theta_{3}-\varphi_{3}}}),

where we select the principal branch of the logarithm on
{\mathbb{C}}\setminus[0,-\mathrm{i}\,\infty)
;

iii)

The Schwarz-Christoffel transformation
S\to\mathbb{H}
,
\tau(\sigma):=\cosh(\sigma)
.

iv)

The Möbius transformation
\mathbb{H}\to D(0,1)
,
w(\tau):=\frac{\tau-\tau_{2}}{\tau+\tau_{2}}
, where
\tau_{2}:=\tau(\sigma(\sqrt{\zeta_{2}}))
.

The choice of
\tau_{2}
has been made in such a way that
w(z)=0
if
z=\zeta_{2}
. Here we again abuse notation and write
w(z)=w(\tau(\sigma(\sqrt{z})))
. Note that

\displaystyle\tau(\sigma(\kappa))=\frac{1}{2}(\alpha(\kappa)+\alpha(\kappa)^{-1}),\quad\alpha(\kappa):={\rm e}^{-\frac{\mathrm{i}\pi\varphi_{3}}{\theta_{3}-\varphi_{3}}}(\kappa/r_{3})^{\frac{\pi}{\theta_{3}-\varphi_{3}}}.

(134)

By distortion bounds
[
60
, Cor. 1.4]
,

\displaystyle\left|\frac{{\rm d}w(z)}{{\rm d}z}\right|{\rm d}(z,U_{3})\leq 1-|w(z)|^{2}\leq 4\left|\frac{{\rm d}w(z)}{{\rm d}z}\right|{\rm d}(z,U_{3}).

(135)

We compute the differential of
w
at
z\in U_{2}
by the chain rule,

\displaystyle\left|\frac{{\rm d}w}{{\rm d}z}\right|=\left|\frac{{\rm d}w}{{\rm d}\tau}\frac{{\rm d}\tau}{{\rm d}\sigma}\frac{{\rm d}\sigma}{{\rm d}\kappa}\frac{{\rm d}\kappa}{{\rm d}z}\right|=\frac{\pi|\tau_{2}||\sinh(\sigma)|}{(\theta_{3}-\varphi_{3})|z|(\tau+\tau_{2})^{2}}

\displaystyle=\frac{\pi}{(\theta_{3}-\varphi_{3})|z|}\frac{|\alpha(\kappa_{2})+\alpha(\kappa_{2})^{-1}||\alpha(\kappa)+\alpha(\kappa)^{-1}|}{|\alpha(\kappa_{2})+\alpha(\kappa_{2})^{-1}+\alpha(\kappa)+\alpha(\kappa)^{-1}|^{2}}.

Since
|\kappa|\geq r_{2}\gg r_{3}
, we have
|\alpha(\kappa)|\gg 1
, whence

\displaystyle\frac{|\alpha(\kappa_{2})+\alpha(\kappa_{2})^{-1}||\alpha(\kappa)+\alpha(\kappa)^{-1}|}{|\alpha(\kappa_{2})+\alpha(\kappa_{2})^{-1}+\alpha(\kappa)+\alpha(\kappa)^{-1}|^{2}}\asymp\frac{|\alpha(\kappa_{2})||\alpha(\kappa)|}{(|\alpha(\kappa_{2})|+|\alpha(\kappa)|)^{2}},

which, in view of
|\alpha(\kappa)|=(|\kappa|/r_{3})^{\frac{\pi}{\theta_{3}-\varphi_{3}}}
, leads to

\displaystyle\frac{1}{(\theta_{3}-\varphi_{3})R_{2}^{2}}\left(\frac{r_{2}}{R_{2}}\right)^{\frac{2\pi}{\theta_{3}-\varphi_{3}}}\lesssim\left|\frac{{\rm d}w}{{\rm d}z}\right|\lesssim\frac{1}{(\theta_{3}-\varphi_{3})r_{2}^{2}}

for
z\in U_{2}
. Denoting the numbers
r_{j}
in Lemma
33
by
\rho_{j}
instead (with
\rho_{3}=1
), we then find, using (
135
),

\displaystyle 1-\rho_{2}

\displaystyle\gtrsim\frac{{\rm d}(\partial U_{2},\partial U_{3})}{(\theta_{3}-\varphi_{3})R_{2}^{2}}\left(\frac{r_{2}}{R_{2}}\right)^{\frac{2\pi}{\theta_{3}-\varphi_{3}}},

\displaystyle\rho_{2}-\rho_{1}

\displaystyle\gtrsim\frac{{\rm d}(\partial U_{1},\partial U_{3})}{(\theta_{3}-\varphi_{3})R_{2}^{2}}\left(\frac{r_{2}}{R_{2}}\right)^{\frac{2\pi}{\theta_{3}-\varphi_{3}}}-\frac{{\rm d}(\partial U_{2},\partial U_{3})}{(\theta_{3}-\varphi_{3})r_{2}^{2}}\gtrsim\frac{{\rm d}(\partial U_{1},\partial U_{3})}{(\theta_{3}-\varphi_{3})R_{2}^{2}}\left(\frac{r_{2}}{R_{2}}\right)^{\frac{2\pi}{\theta_{3}-\varphi_{3}}},

where in the second line we used the triangle inequality and the second inequality in (

). By the third inequality in (

) we can Taylor expand

\displaystyle\log(\frac{1}{\rho_{2}})=-\log(1-(1-\rho_{2}))\asymp 1-\rho_{2}.

Lemma

now yields the claim.
∎

8.2.
Distribution function

For
s>0
, we define

\displaystyle h_{L}(s)=|\{k\in[N]:\eta_{0}L_{k}\leq 1/s\}|\in{\mathbb{Z}}_{+},

where
\eta_{0}^{-1}
is an arbitrary length scale. Note that
h_{L}
is decreasing and tends to infinity as
s\to 0
.
In fact,
h_{L}
is the distribution function of the sequence
(\eta_{0}L_{k})^{-1}
. Since we assume that
L_{k}
is increasing, we also have

\displaystyle h_{L}(s)=\min\{k\in{\mathbb{Z}}_{+}:\eta_{0}L_{k+1}>1/s\}.

We will show that, under the assumption

\displaystyle\exists\lambda\in(0,1)\quad\mbox{such that}\quad\limsup_{s\to 0+}\frac{h_{L}(\lambda s)}{{\rm e}\,h_{L}(s)}<1,

(136)

the potential
V(L)
is strongly separating in the sense of Definition
2
.

Proposition 35
.

Assume (
136
). Then

\displaystyle\mathrm{sep}(L,\eta)\lesssim\exp(-\eta L_{1})\langle h_{L}(\eta/\eta_{0})\rangle.

(137)

In particular, this implies that the examples in Subsection
2.1
are strongly separating:

a)

If
\eta_{0}L_{k}\gtrsim k^{\alpha}
for
\alpha>0
, then
h_{L}(s)\lesssim s^{-1/\alpha}
.

b)

If
\eta_{0}L_{k}\gtrsim\exp(k)
, then
h_{L}(s)\lesssim\log(1/s)
.

c)

If
\eta_{0}L_{k}\gtrsim\exp(\exp(k))
, then
h_{L}(s)\lesssim\log\log(1/s)
.

Lemma 36
.

Assume (
136
). Then for any
\delta>0
and for all
s>0
,

\displaystyle\langle h_{L}(\delta s)\rangle\lesssim_{\delta}\langle h_{L}(s)\rangle.

Proof.

We may restrict our attention to the case
\delta<1
as the case
\delta\geq 1
is trivial. By (
136
) there exist
\lambda\in(0,1)
and
s_{0}>0
such that

\displaystyle h_{L}(\lambda s)<{\rm e}\,h_{L}(s)

(138)

holds for all
s\in(0,s_{0}]
. Now let
n
be the smallest integer such that
\lambda^{n}\leq\delta
. Iterating (
138
)
n
times, we get

\displaystyle h_{L}(\delta s)<{\rm e}^{n}h_{L}(s)

for all
s\in(0,s_{0}]
. For
s>s_{0}
, the inequality holds trivially.
∎

Proof of Proposition
35
.

Without loss of generality we may assume that
\eta=\eta_{0}
.
We first consider the case
\eta L_{1}\leq 1
, and hence
h_{L}(1)\geq 1
.
Then

\displaystyle\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}\exp(-\eta L_{k})\leq{h_{L}(1)}\exp(-\eta L_{1})+\sum_{k=h_{L}(1)}^{\infty}\exp(-\eta L_{k}).

It remains to show that the second term is bounded by the right hand side of (
137
). To this end, we decompose the sum into dyadic intervals
I_{j}=[h_{L}(2^{-j}),h_{L}(2^{-j-1})]
,
j\in{\mathbb{Z}}_{+}
. Then

\displaystyle\sum_{k\in I_{j}}\exp(-\eta L_{k})\leq\exp(-2^{j})h_{L}(2^{-j-1}).

Summing over
j
and using Cauchy’s condensation test yields

\displaystyle\sum_{k=h_{L}(1)}^{\infty}\exp(-\eta L_{k})\lesssim\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\exp(-n)h_{L}(\tfrac{1}{2n}).

(139)

By the quotient test, the series converges provided that

\displaystyle\limsup_{n\to\infty}\frac{h_{L}(\lambda_{n}\tfrac{1}{2n})}{{\rm e}\,h_{L}(\tfrac{1}{2n})}<1,

(140)

where
\lambda_{n}=\tfrac{n}{n+1}
.
But this follows from assumption (
136
). Indeed, since
\lambda_{n}\to 1
, we have
\lambda<\lambda_{n}
for large
n
and hence
h_{L}(\lambda_{n}\tfrac{1}{2n})\leq h_{L}(\lambda\tfrac{1}{2n})
. The series (
139
) is thus bounded by
\langle h_{L}(1)\rangle
, where we have used Lemma
36
with
\delta=1/2
. This proves (
137
) in the case
\eta L_{1}\leq 1
. The case
\eta L_{1}>1
is similar, but (
139
) is bounded by
\exp(-n_{0})h_{L}(\tfrac{1}{2n_{0}})
, where
n_{0}
is the least integer such that
h_{L}(\tfrac{1}{2n_{0}})\geq 1
, i.e.
n_{0}=\lceil\tfrac{\eta L_{1}}{2}\rceil
. Another application of Lemma
36
completes the proof.
∎
