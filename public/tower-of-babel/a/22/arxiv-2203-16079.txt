Volkov-Pankratov states in a 2d material: excited states of a structural soliton
S. Theil 1, R. Gupta 2, F. Wullschläger 3, B. Meyer 3, S. Sharma 4, S. Shallcross 4

Abstract
We show that partial dislocations, defects that naturally arise in bilayer graphene, host an analogue of the mass inversion Volkov-Pankratov (VP) states, the spectrum of excited states at a topologically non-trivial interface. In contrast to the dislocation states arising from the change in valley Chern index, such states (i) exist both with and without an interlayer bias, i.e. for metallic as well as insulating bulk and (ii) have distinct electron and hole bound states, whose number is proportional to the dislocation width. Recently discovered at heterojunctions of 3d topological insulators, their existence at the partial dislocations of a 2d material opens a rich structural playground for their investigation, and we demonstrate that the dislocation type, i.e. screw or edge, as well as the dislocation width, both play a decisive role in the creation of a topological spectrum of exited states.

Pure screw partial dislocations
: For a partial dislocation aligned along the
y
(i.e armchair) direction the Burgers vector
{\bf b}_{2}

For this geometry reflection symmetry about the dislocation line ensures that all interlayer potentials are real valued (a statement proved in the SI), and this allows for an analytical treatment of the emergence of Volkov-Pankratov states, which we now describe. Introducing a second set of

H=\tau_{0}\otimes\sigma_{x}p_{x}+\tau_{z}\otimes\sigma_{y}p_{y}+\tau_{z}\otimes\sigma_{0}\Delta+\tau_{x}\otimes S(x)

7

\phi_{\pm}=\ket{\pm}_{\sigma}\otimes\ket{\mp}_{\tau}e^{-\int_{0}^{x}S_{z}(x^{\prime})\mathrm{d}x^{\prime}}

(8)

\sigma_{z}\ket{\pm}_{\sigma}=\pm\ket{\pm}_{\sigma}

We now consider a mixed edge and screw partial dislocation, given by
{\bf b}_{1}=(1/2,1/\sqrt{3})a

In contrast to the pure screw partial dislocation this geometry generates complex dislocation potentials, for which the analytical treatment given for the pure screw partial with real potentials evidently does not hold. It is thus an open question as to whether VP states exist in this more complex potential landscape of the mixed character partial dislocation.
As can be seen in panels (a-d) of Fig.

Upon switching on an interlayer bias the VP spectrum splits with

p_{y}<0

Discussion
: We have shown that partial dislocations in bilayer graphene host a rich physics of Volkov-Pankratov mass inversion states. Two remarkable differences exist in this 2d material manifestation of VP states as compared their previous observation in 3d materials. (i) While in 3d topological heterojunctions the presence of a gapped bulk is essential, in bilayer graphene VP states are more general, being found at stacking boundaries for both metallic as well as insulating bulk. (ii) The VP states are sensitive to the details of the partial dislocation structure, with dramatically different VP spectra found for pure screw versus mixed edge/screw dislocations.
In minimally twisted bilayer graphene, which relaxes to an ordered network of pure screw partial dislocations, these states will constitute a second
“Volkov-Pankratov network”
of 1d states in addition to the
“helical network”
generated by the spatially changing Chern index. However, our calculations show that the critical dislocation width for the onset of VP states (
\sim 4
nm) is comparable to the dislocation width calculated for a relaxed
0.1^{\circ}
twist bilayer, suggesting they represent a marginal case for this material. In contrast, we find that partial dislocations of mixed edge/screw character represent a case where the typical dislocation widths easily exceeds the critical width for the observation of VP states.
Taken together with their ease of study in a 2d material, which can be set in contrast to the less accessible VP spectrum that occurs at the interface of two 3d materials, then bilayer graphene would appear to represent perhaps the perfect material for the investigation and manipulation of Volkov-Pankratov states.

I.1
Continuum formalism

We employ for our continuum calculations the methodology described in Ref.
Rost et al. 2019
. The idea of this approach is to consider a high symmetry system, lattice vectors
\bm{\mathrm{a}}_{i}
, basis vectors
\bm{\mathsf{\nu}}_{\alpha}
and reciprocal lattice vectors
\bm{\mathrm{G}}_{i}
, to which a deformation is applied by changing the value of the tight-binding hopping function. The general two-centre tight-binding Hamiltonian for this situation then reads

H_{TB}=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\alpha\beta\\
{\bf r}\bm{\delta}\end{subarray}}t_{\alpha\beta}({\bf r},\bm{\delta})c^{\dagger}_{\beta{\bf r}+\bm{\delta}}c_{\alpha{\bf r}}

(11)

where
\alpha
and
\beta
are combined atomic indices of the high symmetry system, i.e. layer index, basis atom index, angular momentum, and spin. The vector
\bm{\mathrm{r}}
sums over all lattice sites having basis vector
\bm{\mathsf{\nu}}_{\alpha}
, with
\bm{\mathsf{\delta}}
the hopping vector from
\bm{\mathrm{r}}
to some distant site
\bm{\mathrm{r}}+\bm{\mathsf{\delta}}
having basis vector
\bm{\mathsf{\nu}}_{\beta}
. The amplitude of the hopping matrix elements between these sites is given by
t_{\alpha\beta}({\bf r},\bm{\delta})
which for the purely high symmetry system (i.e., no deformation) would reduce to
t^{(0)}_{\alpha\beta}(\bm{\mathsf{\delta}})
. The
\bm{\mathrm{r}}
dependence thus encodes the spatial variation of hopping introduced by an applied deformation.

As shown in Ref.
Rost et al. 2019
for any deformation field this Hamiltonian exactly maps onto the following continuum Hamiltonian

[H({\bf r},\bm{\mathrm{p}})]_{\alpha\beta}=\frac{1}{A_{\mathrm{UC}}}\sum_{\bm{\mathrm{G}}_{j}}[M_{j}]_{\alpha\beta}\>\eta_{\alpha\beta}({\bf r},\bm{\mathrm{K}}_{j}+\bm{\mathrm{p}})

(12)

where the sum is over the reciprocal lattice vectors
\bm{\mathrm{G}}_{j}
of the underlying high symmetry system, and
{\bf K}_{j}={\bf K}_{0}+{\bf G}_{j}
with
{\bf K}_{0}
some conveniently chosen reference momentum, which for bilayer graphene is one of the six K points.
A_{UC}
is the area of the real space unit cell of the high symmetry structure. The function
\eta_{\alpha\beta}({\bf r},{\bf q})
is the mixed space Fourier transform of the hopping function
t_{\alpha\beta}({\bf r},\bm{\delta})
defined in Eq. (
11
),

\eta_{\alpha\beta}({\bf r},{\bf q})=\int d\bm{\delta}\,e^{i{\bf q}.\bm{\delta}}t_{\alpha\beta}({\bf r},\bm{\delta})

(13)

Note the absence of any formal restriction on the spatial dependence of the change in hopping function matrix elements due to deformation. The deformation may thus both be perturbative, for example in the case of strain fields applied to each layer, as well as highly non-perturbative as is the case for interlayer deformations such as the introduction of twist faults or dislocations into a pristine AB stacked lattice as is considered here.
Finally, the
“M matrices”
are given by

[M_{j}]_{\alpha\beta}=e^{\mathrm{i}\bm{\mathrm{G}}_{j}\cdot(\bm{\nu}_{\alpha}-\bm{\nu}_{\beta})}

(14)

and these, since they combine the real space basis vectors
\bm{\mathrm{\nu}}
with the reciprocal lattice vectors
\bm{\mathrm{G}}_{j}
of the high symmetry lattice this expression, contain the full information of the lattice and basis of the high symmetry system.
For further details we refer the reader to Ref.
Rost et al. 2019
as well as to several recent applications of the method: to minimally twisted bilayer graphene
Fleischmann et al. 2020
, partial dislocation networks
Kisslinger et al. 2015
;
Shallcross et al. 2017
;
Weckbecker et al. 2019
, and in-plane deformation fields
gupta19
;
gupta19a
.

For the case of interlayer deformations we consider a deformation field
\bm{\mathrm{u}}_{i}(\bm{\mathrm{r}})
applied to each layer
i
, with the difference
\Delta\bm{\mathrm{u}}(\bm{\mathrm{r}})=\bm{\mathrm{u}}_{2}(\bm{\mathrm{r}})-\bm{\mathrm{u}}_{1}(\bm{\mathrm{r}})
then describing the change in local stacking at each point
\bm{\mathrm{r}}
. The interlayer coupling blocks can also be obtained from Eq. (
12
) and, as shown by Rost
et al.
, the required Fourier transform Eq. (
13
) can be taken exactly to yield a general form for the interlayer interaction
Rost et al. 2019
given by

[S(\bm{\mathrm{r}},\bm{\mathrm{p}})]_{\alpha\beta}=\frac{1}{A_{\mathrm{UC}}}\sum_{j}[M_{j}]_{\alpha\beta}\>e^{-\mathrm{i}\Delta\bm{\mathrm{u}}(\bm{\mathrm{r}})\cdot\bm{\mathrm{G}}_{j}}\>\hat{t}^{(0)}(\bm{\mathrm{K}}_{j}+\bm{\mathrm{p}})

(15)

where
\hat{t}^{(0)}(\bm{\mathrm{q}})=\int d\bm{\delta}\,e^{i{\bf q}.\bm{\delta}}t^{(0)}_{\alpha\beta}(\bm{\delta})
is the Fourier transform of the hopping envelope function of the high symmetry lattice.
The full Hamiltonian of the bilayer system is thus given by

H=\begin{pmatrix}H^{(1)}(\bm{\mathrm{r}},\bm{\mathrm{p}})&S(\bm{\mathrm{r}},\bm{\mathrm{p}})\\
S^{\dagger}(\bm{\mathrm{r}},\bm{\mathrm{p}})&H^{(2)}(\bm{\mathrm{r}},\bm{\mathrm{p}})\end{pmatrix}

(16)

For investigating dislocations in bilayer graphene we make a number of simplifying assumptions to this general expression. Firstly, we neglect the momentum dependence of the interlayer interaction, and thus this is given by

[S(\bm{\mathrm{r}})]_{\alpha\beta}=\frac{1}{A_{\mathrm{UC}}}\sum_{j}[M_{j}]_{\alpha\beta}\>e^{-\mathrm{i}\Delta\bm{\mathrm{u}}(\bm{\mathrm{r}})\cdot\bm{\mathrm{G}}_{j}}\>\hat{t}^{(0)}(\bm{\mathrm{K}}_{j})

(17)

directly relating the local misregistry of the underlying high symmetry bilayer introduced by the deformation field
\Delta\bm{\mathrm{u}}(\bm{\mathrm{r}})
to the
2\times 2
interlayer interaction. As shown in Ref.
Rost et al. 2019
, the momentum dependence of Eq.
15
, while crucial for certain materials such as phosphorene, plays only a minor role in the interlayer interaction in graphene. We thus have to solve the Hamiltonian Secondly, for the diagonal blocks we (i) retain only linear order in
\bm{\mathrm{p}}
, i.e. we employ the Dirac-Weyl approximation, and (ii) we neglect the effect of in-plane strain fields on the two graphene layers. As shown in Ref.
Fleischmann et al. 2020
in bilayer systems the change in the electronic structure is dominated by the interlayer deformation terms, and to a good approximation these terms can be ignored.

The
C_{3}
symmetry of graphene demands that each star of the translation group of momentum boosts encoded in the above equation is described by the same 3
“M matrices”
:

M_{0}=\begin{pmatrix}1&1\\
1&1\end{pmatrix},\qquad M_{\pm}=\begin{pmatrix}1&e^{\pm 2\pi\mathrm{i}/3}\\
e^{\mp 2\pi\mathrm{i}/3}&1\end{pmatrix}

(18)

For this reason the interlayer interaction can be expressed as a sum of 3 distinct parts, with the most natural way to decompose the interlayer interaction in terms of the three stacking potentials associated with the high symmetry structures AB, BA and AA stacking, which have off-diagonal matrix structure
\sigma_{+}
,
\sigma_{-}
, and
\sigma_{0}
respectively.

To evaluate the lattice sums in the expressions above we must specify the lattice geometry of the high symmetry system and we consider here a standard lattice geometry
\bm{\mathrm{a}}_{1}=(0,1)a
,
\bm{\mathrm{a}}_{2}=(\frac{1}{2},\frac{\sqrt{3}}{2})a
with basis vectors
\bm{\nu}_{1}=\bm{\mathrm{0}}
,
\bm{\nu}_{2}=(1,1/\sqrt{3})a
,
\bm{\nu}_{3}=\bm{\mathrm{0}}
, and
\bm{\nu}_{4}=(1/2,1/(2\sqrt{3})
, where basis vectors 1 and 2 constitute the first layer basis and 3 and 4 the second layer basis. Using these vectors in Eq. (
12
) the diagonal blocks of the Hamiltonian are
v_{F}\sigma.{\bf p}
for the first layer and
v_{F}\sigma^{\ast}.{\bf p}
for the second layer.

In this work we consider straight, and not wandering, partial dislocations. The electronic structure problem is then effectively a 1d problem and we can write our final Hamiltonian as

H(x)=\begin{pmatrix}(\sigma_{x}p_{x}+\sigma_{y}p_{y})+\Delta&S(x)\\
S^{\dagger}(x)&(\sigma_{x}p_{x}-\sigma_{y}p_{y})-\Delta\end{pmatrix}

(19)

where we have used the fact that we consider straight, not wandering, partial dislocation systems we which we align in the
y

t_{\perp}
and
l_{\perp}=\hbar v_{\mathrm{F}}/t_{\perp}
respectively. The interlayer bias due to an applied layer perpendicular field

II
Analytical solution for mass inversion bound states

II.1
Model

We consider semi-infinite extended regions of AB and BA stacked bilayer graphene connected by a boundary along the armchair direction at
x=0
. A continuous lattice implies that across this boundary the bilayer misregistry should change continuously by a partial Burgers vector. The Hamiltonian for this system is

H=\begin{pmatrix}v_{\mathrm{F}}\bm{\mathrm{\sigma}}\cdot\bm{\mathrm{p}}+\Delta&t_{\perp}S(x)\\
t_{\perp}S^{\dagger}(x)&v_{\mathrm{F}}\bm{\mathrm{\sigma}}^{*}\cdot\bm{\mathrm{p}}-\Delta\end{pmatrix}=\begin{pmatrix}v_{\mathrm{F}}(\sigma_{x}p_{x}+\sigma_{y}p_{y})+\Delta&t_{\perp}S(x)\\
t_{\perp}S^{\dagger}(x)&v_{\mathrm{F}}(\sigma_{x}p_{x}-\sigma_{y}p_{y})-\Delta\end{pmatrix}.

(23)

with the stacking potentials in the coupling blocks given by

S(x)=\begin{pmatrix}V_{\mathrm{AB}}(x)&V_{\mathrm{AA}}(x)\\
V_{\mathrm{AA}}(x)&V_{\mathrm{BA}}(x)\end{pmatrix}.

(24)

Note that we take the interlayer potentials to be momentum independent. By measuring energies and lengths in terms of the constants
t_{\perp}
and
l_{\perp}=\hbar v_{\mathrm{F}}/t_{\perp}
respectively we can non-dimensionalize this Hamiltonian.

We now rewrite
S(x)
in terms of three new potentials
S_{0}
,
S_{x}
and
S_{z}

\displaystyle S(x)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sigma_{0}S_{0}+\sigma_{x}S_{x}+\sigma_{z}S_{z}

(25)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{\sigma_{0}}{2}(V_{\mathrm{AB}}+V_{\mathrm{BA}})+\sigma_{x}V_{\mathrm{AA}}+\frac{\sigma_{z}}{2}(V_{\mathrm{AB}}-V_{\mathrm{BA}}).

where
S_{x}
,
S_{0}
, and
S_{z}
represent the contribution to the interlayer potential from, respectively, AA stacking, the average of AB and BA stacking, and the difference of AB and BA stacking.

In Fig.
6
we show these potentials for a pure screw (left hand panel) and a mixed partial dislocation (right hand panel). For the pure screw partial dislocation we show the potentials obtained from values of the tight-binding constant
B_{\perp}
that sets the decay length of the interlayer interaction. As can be seen, the interlayer potentials are quite sensitive to the form of the tight-binding interaction. For a sufficiently fast decay of the interlayer interaction (the full lines in panel (1) of Fig.
6
) the only hopping allowed is between two atoms directly on top of each other. As a consequence during the transition region from AB to BA stacking the interlayer interaction becomes substantially weakened at the the core of the dislocation, as can be seen from the pronounced dip in the stacking average potential
S_{0}
. The AA component of the interlayer interaction,
S_{x}
, remains zero throughout the transition. For a slower decay of the interaction, on the other hand, one finds that the stacking average potential remains nearly unchanged with the AA potential finite throughout the transition region from AB to BA and showing a maxima at the dislocation core. Interestingly, although the
S_{x}
- and
S_{0}
-potentials depend strongly on the range of the interlayer hopping, the stacking difference potential
S_{z}
is quite insensitive to it.

One can also note that for the pure screw partial dislocations shown in panel (1) of Fig.
6
the interlayer potentials are all real. For screw dislocations aligned along the high symmetry armchair directions of the lattice this is always so, and using the general result for the interlayer potential given by Eq.
15
it is easy to show this is related to the mirror symmetry that the lattice possesses about the dislocation core.

For the lattice geometry
\bm{\mathrm{a}}_{1}=(0,1)a
,
\bm{\mathrm{a}}_{2}=(\frac{1}{2},\frac{\sqrt{3}}{2})a
; there are three armchair directions in the
\hat{\bm{\mathrm{y}}}
direction and
\hat{\bm{\mathrm{y}}}
rotated by
\pm 2\pi/3
. The reciprocal lattice vectors thus have three lines of mirror symmetry: the
k_{x}
line and this line rotated by
\pm 2\pi/3
. Consider the mirror symmetry through the
k_{x}
line, then every
\bm{\mathrm{G}}_{j}
vector has a partner with
k_{y}
component of opposite sign. For a
\Delta\bm{\mathrm{u}}(x)=\bm{\mathrm{f}}(x)\hat{\bm{\mathrm{y}}}
form of the deformation field, i.e. one acting in the armchair
\hat{\bm{\mathrm{y}}}
direction then terms in the lattice sum always come in complex conjugate pairs: the dot product in the phase
e^{-\mathrm{i}\Delta\bm{\mathrm{u}}(\bm{\mathrm{r}})\cdot\bm{\mathrm{G}}_{j}}
ensures the mirror symmetry related
k_{y}
components survive while the
k_{x}
component vanishes identically. As the coefficients of these phases are identical for elements of each star, this proves the reality of the interlayer potentials for this case, and similarly for the other two armchair directions.

II.2
Zero mode

For real potentials, we can decompose the Hamiltonian into the tensor product

H=\tau_{0}\otimes\sigma_{x}p_{x}+\tau_{3}\otimes\sigma_{y}p_{y}+\tau_{3}\otimes\sigma_{0}\Delta+\tau_{x}\otimes S(x).

(26)

If we now perform a unitary rotation in layer space

U_{1}=R_{y}(\pi/2)\otimes\sigma_{0}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}\begin{pmatrix}1&-1\\
1&1\end{pmatrix}\otimes\sigma_{0}

(27)

that sends
\tau_{z}\rightarrow-\tau_{x}
,
\tau_{x}\rightarrow\tau_{z}
and
\tau_{y}\rightarrow\tau_{y}
we find

H_{1}=U_{1}^{\dagger}HU_{1}=\begin{pmatrix}\sigma_{x}p_{x}+S(x)&-\sigma_{y}p_{y}-\Delta\\
-\sigma_{y}p_{y}-\Delta&\sigma_{x}p_{x}-S(x)\end{pmatrix}

(28)

We next apply to
H_{1}
the gauge transformation
U_{2}

U_{2}=\begin{pmatrix}\Phi_{-}&0\\
0&\Phi_{+}\end{pmatrix}\otimes\sigma_{0}

(29)

where
\Phi
is given by

\Phi_{\pm}(x)=e^{\pm\mathrm{i}\int_{0}^{x}S_{x}(x^{\prime})\mathrm{d}x^{\prime}}

(30)

The derivative in the
x
-momentum will bring down a factor of
\pm S_{x}
and together with the
\sigma_{x}
matrix from the momentum this will cancel the corresponding term in
S(x)
.
After these manipulations the Hamiltonian takes the form

H_{2}=\begin{pmatrix}\sigma_{x}p_{x}+\sigma_{0}S_{0}+\sigma_{z}S_{z}&-(\sigma_{y}p_{y}+\Delta)\Phi_{-}^{2}\\
-(\sigma_{y}p_{y}+\Delta)\Phi_{+}^{2}&\sigma_{x}p_{x}-\sigma_{0}S_{0}-\sigma_{z}S_{z}\end{pmatrix}

(31)

We now simplify the Hamiltonian by setting
p_{y}
and the interlayer bias potential
\Delta
to zero, decoupling the two diagonal blocks. The average stacking has a value close to
1/2
across the dislocation, representing therefore simply an energy shift
\pm 1/2
between the two decoupled blocks. Neglecting this constant shift in energy we find

H_{2}^{\prime}=\begin{pmatrix}\sigma_{x}p_{x}+\sigma_{z}S_{z}&0\\
0&\sigma_{x}p_{x}-\sigma_{z}S_{z}\end{pmatrix}

(32)

If we now apply another unitary rotation, this time in sublattice space,

U_{3}=\tau_{0}\otimes\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}\begin{pmatrix}1&-\mathrm{i}\\
-\mathrm{i}&1\end{pmatrix}

(33)

we send
\sigma_{z}\rightarrow\sigma_{y}
finding

H_{3}=\begin{pmatrix}h_{+}&0\\
0&h_{-}\end{pmatrix}:=\begin{pmatrix}\sigma_{x}p_{x}+\sigma_{y}S_{z}&0\\
0&\sigma_{x}p_{x}-\sigma_{y}S_{z}\end{pmatrix}

(34)

The zero energy eigenvector of this Hamiltonian is

\ket{\phi}=\begin{pmatrix}\ket{\phi_+}\\
\ket{\phi_-}\end{pmatrix}

(35)

where
\phi_{\pm}
are given by

\displaystyle\ket{\phi_+}

\displaystyle=e^{-\int_{0}^{x}S_{z}(x^{\prime})\mathrm{d}x^{\prime}}\ket{-}

(36)

\displaystyle\ket{\phi_-}

\displaystyle=e^{-\int_{0}^{x}S_{z}(x^{\prime})\mathrm{d}x^{\prime}}\ket{+}

(37)

\ket{\pm}
is the eigenvector of
\sigma_{z}
with eigenvalue
\pm
. It is easy to check that they satisfy the equation
h_{\pm}\phi_{\pm}=0
. The only condition
S_{z}
must fulfil is that for the wave function to be normalizable the stacking difference potential must asymptotically change sign across the partial dislocation line.

II.3
Full Spectrum

To determine the full spectrum we employ the trick of squaring the Hamiltonian.
After squaring
H_{3}
we find

H_{3}^{2}=\begin{pmatrix}h_{+}^{2}&0\\
0&h_{-}^{2}\end{pmatrix}=\begin{pmatrix}p_{x}^{2}+S_{z}^{2}+\partial_{x}S_{z}&0&0&0\\
0&p_{x}^{2}+S_{z}^{2}-\partial_{x}S_{z}&0&0\\
0&0&p_{x}^{2}+S_{z}^{2}-\partial_{x}S_{z}\\
0&0&0&p_{x}^{2}+S_{z}^{2}+\partial_{x}S_{z}\end{pmatrix}

(38)

We now choose a hyperbolic functional form
\frac{1}{2}\tanh\left(\frac{x}{2l}\right)
for
S_{z}(x)
. The corresponding Schrödinger equation for
h_{+}^{2}
gives us

\left[-\partial_{x}^{2}+\frac{1}{4}\tanh^{2}\left(\frac{x}{2l}\right)+\sigma_{z}\frac{1}{4l}\sech^{2}\left(\frac{x}{2l}\right)\right]\Phi=E^{2}\Phi

(39)

where
l
is the length of the dislocation (measured in units of
l_{\perp}
). After rescaling
x:=\frac{x}{2l}
and rearranging terms we find

\left[-\partial_{x}^{2}-l(l-\sigma_{z})\sech^{2}x\right]\Phi=(4E^{2}l^{2}-l^{2})\Phi.

(40)

We recognise the second term on the left hand side as the Pöschl-Teller potential

V(x)=-\lambda(\lambda+1)\sech^{2}(x)

(41)

for
\lambda=l
and
\lambda=l-1
.
The eigenvalues of the Pöchl-Teller Hamiltonian are
\epsilon=-m^{2}=4E^{2}l^{2}-l^{2}
and this immediately gives us the spectrum of the unsquared Hamiltonian

E=\pm\frac{\sqrt{l^{2}-m^{2}}}{2l}

(42)

for integer
m
and
l
.

The solutions to the Pöschl-Teller potential are just the associated Legendre polynomials of the hyperbolic tangent function.

\Phi=P^{m}_{l}(\tanh x)=\frac{(-1)^{m}}{2^{l}l!}(1-\tanh^{2}x)^{\nicefrac{{m}}{{2}}}\frac{\mathrm{d}^{m+l}}{\mathrm{d}^{m+l}(\tanh x)}(\tanh^{2}x-1)^{l}

(43)

and we can now use these solutions of the squared Hamiltonian to construct the solutions of the original unsquared Hamiltonian. It turns out that the off-diagonal entries of
h_{+}
and
h_{-}

h_{+}=\begin{pmatrix}0&-\mathrm{i}\partial_{x}-\frac{\mathrm{i}}{2}\tanh\left(\frac{x}{2l}\right)\\
-\mathrm{i}\partial_{x}+\frac{\mathrm{i}}{2}\tanh\left(\frac{x}{2l}\right)&0\end{pmatrix}:=\begin{pmatrix}0&\mathrm{i}a\\
\mathrm{i}a^{\dagger}&0\end{pmatrix}

(44)

act as lowering and raising operators for the associated Legendre polynomials

\displaystyle aP_{l}^{m}

\displaystyle=-\frac{l+m}{2l}P_{l-1}^{m}(\tanh(x/2l))

(45)

\displaystyle a^{\dagger}P_{l-1}^{m}

\displaystyle=\frac{l-m}{2l}P_{l}^{m}(\tanh(x/2l))

(46)

and so we make the Ansatz

\Psi=\begin{pmatrix}c_{+}P_{l-1}^{m}\\
c_{-}P_{l}^{m}\end{pmatrix}

(47)

for the eigenvectors of
h_{+}
. The coefficients can easily be obtained from the eigenvalue equation giving us the eigenvectors corresponding to energies
E
:

\Psi_{l}^{m}(\tanh(x/2l))=\frac{1}{N}\begin{pmatrix}\sqrt{l+m}P_{l-1}^{m}\\
\pm\mathrm{i}\sqrt{l-m}P_{l}^{m}\end{pmatrix}

(48)

This expression, however, holds only for finite energies. If we set
m=l
, i.e.
E=0
, the second component vanishes due to the square root factor while in the first component we get
P_{l-1}^{l}
, which is also zero. This results from the fact that in the derivation of the spectrum we relied, in using Eq.
46
, on the fact that
l\neq m
. We thus now instead use the fact that
a\Psi_{l}^{l}=0
to build the ground state

\Psi_{0}=\frac{1}{N}\begin{pmatrix}0\\
P_{l}^{l}\end{pmatrix}

(49)

We can confirm this by inserting
\frac{1}{2}\tan\left(\frac{x}{2l}\right)
into the general expression for the zero mode (Eq.
37
) yielding

\Psi_{0}=\frac{1}{N}e^{-\frac{1}{2}\int_{0}^{x}\tanh(x'/2l)\mathrm{d}x^{\prime}}\ket{-}=\frac{1}{N}\frac{1}{\cosh^{l}(x/2l)}\ket{-}=\frac{1}{N}\begin{pmatrix}0\\
P_{l}^{l}\end{pmatrix}

(50)

We have thus found that at a dislocation between AB and BA stacking there exists, in addition to the valley Hall edge states, a series of of mass inversion states localised on the edges, the Volkov-Pankratov states. Their energies lie inside the valence and conduction bands while their number depends on the width of the dislocation. For each
l
there are
2\lfloor l\rfloor+1
bound states.

. Close to
