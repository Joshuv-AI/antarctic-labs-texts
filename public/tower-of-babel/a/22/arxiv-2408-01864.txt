On the central ball in a translation invariant involutive field Thanks: Key words and phrases: lattice points, parabolic-taxicab distance, parabolic-taxicab ball, translation-invariant-involutive operator, partition with parabolas
Cristian Cobeli, Aaditya Raghavan, Alexandru Zaharescu

Abstract
. The iterated composition of two operators, both of which are involutions and translation invariant, partitions the set of lattice points in the plane into an infinite sequence of discrete parabolas. Each such parabola contains an associated stairway-like path connecting certain points on it, induced by the alternating application of the aforementioned operators. Any two lattice points in the plane can be connected by paths along the square grid composed of steps either on these stairways or towards taxicab neighbors. This leads to the notion of the parabolic-taxicab distance between two lattice points, obtained as the minimum number of steps of this kind needed to reach one point from the other. In this paper, we describe patterns generated by points on paths of bounded parabolic-taxicab length and provide a complete description of the balls centered at the origin. In particular, we prove an earlier conjecture on the area of these balls.

1.
Introduction

Inspired by the Ducci game
[
5
,
4
]
and the Proth-Gilbreath procedure
(see
[
11
,
2
]
and the references therein),
which involves higher-order differences calculated recursively,
in a tessellation problem
[
3
]
regarding covering the plane with integers,
a particular type of operators that are linear in all their components
are proven to be useful.
Following
[
8
]
, their two-dimensional version is defined as follows:
for any
(x,y)\in\mathbb{Z}^{2}
let

\displaystyle L^{\prime}(x,y)

\displaystyle=(-x+2y+1,y),\ \text{ and }\ L^{\prime\prime}(x,y)=(x,2x-y+1).

Among operators of this type that are linear in both variables
and have arbitrary coefficients,
L^{\prime}
and
L^{\prime\prime}
are the only ones
that are both involutions and translation invariant.
Starting with an arbitrary point
P\in\mathbb{Z}^{2}
,
the repeated application of
L^{\prime}
and
L^{\prime\prime}

generates a sequence of points, which are the nodes of
a stairway-like path situated alternately on the branches of a parabola.
These parabolas are parallel and the plane
\mathbb{Z}^{2}
is completely covered by them.

In order to be able to jump from one parabola to another,
let
M^{\prime},M^{\prime\prime},M^{\prime\prime\prime},M^{iv}
be the operators that provide movement
on the grid in all directions towards the nearest neighbors:

\displaystyle M^{\prime}(x,y)

\displaystyle=(x+1,y),

\displaystyle M^{\prime\prime}(x,y)

\displaystyle=(x-1,y),

\displaystyle M^{\prime\prime\prime}(x,y)

\displaystyle=(x,y+1),

\displaystyle M^{iv}(x,y)

\displaystyle=(x,y-1).

These four operators lead to the taxicab geometry initiated in 1952
by Menger
[
10
]

(see Çolakoğlu
[
9
]
and the references therein
for more recent developments on a generalization of the taxicab distance).
From a geometric perspective, we now have two ways of moving in
\mathbb{Z}^{2}
:
one fast, following the steps of the stairways, and the other slow,
moving only towards the nearest points in the grid.
Then, by combining in any order the operators from

\mathcal{L}\cup\mathcal{M}
,
where
\mathcal{L}=\{L^{\prime},L^{\prime\prime}\}
and
\mathcal{M}=\{M^{\prime},M^{\prime\prime},M^{\prime\prime\prime},M^{iv}\}
,
we see that there are infinitely many paths in
\mathbb{Z}^{2}

from one point to another.
Considering each step to have a length of
1
,
whether it is on a parabola or towards a nearby neighbor point,
we obtain the
parabolic-taxicab distance

\operatorname{d_{pc}}
,
which is defined as the number of steps
of the shortest path connecting two points.
Thus, for every
P,Q\in\mathbb{Z}^{2}
, let

\displaystyle\operatorname{d_{pc}}(P,Q)

\displaystyle:=\min\{n\in\mathbb{Z}_{\geq 0}:F_{1},\dots,F_{n}\in\{\mathrm{id}\}\cup\mathcal{L}\cup\mathcal{M},\ F_{1}\circ\cdots\circ F_{n}(P)=Q\}.

Upon observing that
\operatorname{d_{pc}}
satisfies the axioms of a distance,
for every point
C\in\mathbb{Z}^{2}
and every non-negative integer
r
,
we can define the
parabolic-taxicab closed ball

with center
C
and radius
r

as the set of lattice points that are
at a distance at most
r
from
C
, that is,

\mathscr{B}_{\mathrm{pc}}(C,r):=\big\{X\in\mathbb{Z}^{2}:\operatorname{d_{pc}}(C,X)\leq r\big\}.

Then, the
perimeter
or the
boundary
of

\mathscr{B}_{\mathrm{pc}}(C,r)
is

\displaystyle\partial\mathscr{B}_{\mathrm{pc}}(C,r)

\displaystyle:=\{(x,y)\in\mathbb{Z}^{2}:\operatorname{d_{pc}}(C,X)=r\}.

A representation of these objects can be seen in Figure

A tally on the small balls with center

Our aim here is to explore the atypical implications
of measuring distances with
\operatorname{d_{pc}}
.
A series of tests with small radii show that parity plays an essential role
in how the circles
\partial\mathscr{B}_{\mathrm{pc}}(O,r)

with
0\leq r\leq R
add up in successive layers
to form a complete ball
\mathscr{B}_{\mathrm{pc}}(O,R)
.
For instance, we noted that
\partial\mathscr{B}_{\mathrm{pc}}(O,r)
contains points
on the diagonal
y=x
if and only if
r
is even.
Furthermore, we observed that
\partial\mathscr{B}_{\mathrm{pc}}(O,r)

does not contain points on the lines
y=x+|r-1|
and also,
alternatively, not on the parallel lines of integer

-intercept
that are increasingly closer to the diagonal
y=x
.
Since the number of points added at step

is as large as

,
when the parity shifts from one radius to the next, it causes the balls to develop
a pulsating characteristic as their radius increases.
These preliminary remarks
indeed prove to be true for every
r\geq 0
,
and they are part of the complete characterization of
\mathscr{B}_{\mathrm{pc}}(O,r)
,
as described in the following theorem.

Theorem 1
.

Let
r\geq 0
and let
c
be integers.
Let
\mathcal{S}(r,c)
be the set that contains the first coordinate of
all points situated on both the boundary of
\mathscr{B}_{\mathrm{pc}}(O,r)

and the line
y=x+c
.
We denote the negative part of this set by
\mathcal{S}_{\raisebox{-1.0pt}{\scalebox{1.1}{-}}}(r,c)=\mathcal{S}(r,c)\cap\mathbb{Z}_{<0}
.

I.

If
r
and
c
have opposite parity, or
|c|>r
, then
\mathcal{S}(r,c)=\emptyset
.

II.

Suppose
r
and
c
have the same parity
and let
k
be an integer defined by
|c|=r-2k
.
Then
\mathcal{S}(r,c)
and
\mathcal{S}_{\raisebox{-1.0pt}{\scalebox{1.1}{-}}}(r,c)
are translations of intervals of
integers, which can be explicitly expressed as follows:

(
a
)

If
|c|<r
and
c\equiv r\pmod{2}
, then:

(1)

\mathcal{S}(r,c)\cap\mathbb{Z}_{\geq 0}=\begin{cases}\left[\scalebox{0.81}{$\dbinom{r-1-k}{2}$}+k,\scalebox{0.81}{$\dbinom{r-k}{2}$}+k\right]\cap\mathbb{Z},&\text{ if }0\leq c\leq r-2;\\[10.0pt]
\left[\scalebox{0.81}{$\dbinom{r-k}{2}$}+1,\scalebox{0.81}{$\dbinom{r-k+1}{2}$}\right]\cap\mathbb{Z},&\text{ if }-(r-2)\leq c<0.\end{cases}

(
b
)

If
-r+2\leq c\leq 0
, then:

(2)

\mathcal{S}_{\raisebox{-1.0pt}{\scalebox{1.1}{-}}}(r,c)=\begin{cases}\big([k-r,-1]\cap\mathbb{Z}\big)+c(k-1)-\scalebox{0.81}{$\dbinom{k}{2}$},&\text{if }k\equiv 1\pmod{2};\\[8.0pt]
\big([0,r-k-1]\cap\mathbb{Z}\big)+ck-\scalebox{0.81}{$\dbinom{k+1}{2}$},&\text{if }k\equiv 0\pmod{2},\end{cases}

(
c
)

If
0<c\leq r-2
, then:

(3)

\mathcal{S}_{\raisebox{-1.0pt}{\scalebox{1.1}{-}}}(r,c)=\begin{cases}\big([0,r-k-1]\cap\mathbb{Z}\big)-c(k+1)-\scalebox{0.81}{$\dbinom{k+1}{2}$},&\text{if }k\equiv 1\pmod{2};\\[8.0pt]
\big([k-r,-1]\cap\mathbb{Z}\big)-ck-\scalebox{0.81}{$\dbinom{k}{2}$},&\text{if }k\equiv 0\pmod{2}.\end{cases}

(
d
)

Lastly, we have:

(4)

\begin{split}\mathcal{S}(r,r)&=\left[-r,\scalebox{0.81}{$\dbinom{r}{2}$}\right]\cap\mathbb{Z},\ \text{ and }\ \mathcal{S}(r,-r)=\left[0,\scalebox{0.81}{$\dbinom{r+1}{2}$}\right]\cap\mathbb{Z}.\end{split}

Next, as a result, we obtain the area
\#\mathscr{B}_{\mathrm{pc}}(O,r)

and the perimeter
\#\partial\mathscr{B}_{\mathrm{pc}}(O,r)
.

Theorem 2
.

Let
r\geq 0
be an integer.
Then the number of points on the boundary of the
closed parabolic-taxicab ball of radius
r
and center
O=(0,0)
is

(5)

\#\partial\mathcal{B}(O,r)=\frac{1}{2}\big(5r^{2}-r\big)-\left\lceil\frac{r-1}{2}\right\rceil+\left\lceil\frac{r}{r+1}\right\rceil+1,

and the number of points on the entire closed ball is

(6)

\#\mathscr{B}_{\mathrm{pc}}(O,r)=\mbox{\small$\displaystyle\frac{1}{12}$}\big(10r^{3}+9r^{2}+23r\big)+\frac{1}{2}\left\lceil\frac{r}{2}\right\rceil+1.

Theorem
2
proves, in particular, the unfolded formula for the area of the parabolic-taxicab ball centered at the origin as conjectured in
[
8
]
.

Problem 2.1
.

Let

(i)

What is the parabolic-taxicab diameter of

(ii)

Show that there are no chords

Short arguments proving part
(ii)
and part
(iii)
above (the latter in the affirmative) were communicated to the authors by Omer Cantor. We present them below.

For
(ii)
, consider the graph
G
on
\mathbb{Z}^{2}
in which two distinct
vertices
(a_{1},b_{1})
and
(a_{2},b_{2})

are adjacent if one can reach
one of the points from the other by a single application
of

, or

. More explicitly, the edges of

are of the form
\left\{(m,k),(n,k)\right\}

or
\left\{(k,m),(k,n)\right\}
, where

or (without loss of generality,
assuming
m\geq n
)
m-n=1
.
Then, for each edge in
G
, we have
m\not\equiv n\pmod{2}
when represented in the aforementioned form. In particular, if two vertices
(a_{1},b_{1})
and
(a_{2},b_{2})
are adjacent, then
(a_{1}+b_{1})\not\equiv(a_{2}+b_{2})\pmod{2}
. Thus, any closed walk in
G
has even length, and so, for points

, the sum
\operatorname{d_{pc}}(A,B)+\operatorname{d_{pc}}(B,C)+\operatorname{d_{pc}}(A,C)
is even. It follows that if

, then
\operatorname{d_{pc}}(A,B)
is even.

2.2.3.

Remarks on the symmetry

Let ‘
\sim
’ be the operator that makes the associations:

\widetilde{L^{\prime}}=L^{\prime\prime}
;
\widetilde{L^{\prime\prime}}=L^{\prime}
;

\widetilde{M^{\prime}}=M^{\prime\prime\prime}
;
\widetilde{M^{\prime\prime\prime}}=M^{\prime}
;

\widetilde{M^{\prime\prime}}=M^{iv}
;
\widetilde{M^{iv}}=M^{\prime\prime}
.
This pairwise symmetry of the operators in
\mathcal{L}\cup\mathcal{M}=\{L^{\prime},L^{\prime\prime},M^{\prime},M^{\prime\prime},M^{\prime\prime\prime},M^{iv}\}

causes, geometrically, the points transformed by their compositions
to mirror across the first diagonal.
To be exact, if
F_{1},\dots,F_{n}\in\mathcal{L}\cup\mathcal{M}
, then for every
(a,b)\in\mathbb{Z}^{2}

the midpoint of the segment with endpoints

F_{1}\circ\dots\circ F_{n}(a,b)
and

\widetilde{F_{1}}\circ\dots\circ\widetilde{F_{n}}(a,b)
has equal coordinates.
This explains why
\mathscr{B}_{\mathrm{pc}}(O,r)
is symmetric with respect to
y=x
for all radii
r\geq 0
.
Likewise, the more general balls whose centers are sets of lattice points that are
symmetric with respect to the first diagonal
maintain their symmetry for all
r\geq 0
(see Figure
5

for two such examples).

However, the projections onto the first coordinate of
the cross-sections made in

are not identical

and
c>0

for

In the proofs that follow in the next sections, we present the arguments for one of the cases

Lemma 3.2
.

Let
r\geq 0
be an integer.
If
|c|<r
and
c\not\equiv r\pmod{2}
, then
\mathcal{F}(r,c)=\emptyset

Proof.

This is vacuous for
r=0

r=1
since the points
in the ball of radius
1
centered at
O
in the parabolic-taxicab distance lie on the lines
y=x\pm 1
. Suppose the statement holds for
1\leq r\leq R
. If there is a point
(a,b)\in\mathcal{F}(R+1,c)
such that
|c|<R+1
and
c\not\equiv R+1\pmod{2}
, then since the four points at taxicab distance
1
from
(a,b)
lie on the lines
y=x+(c\pm 1)
and
c\pm 1\not\equiv R\pmod{2}
, these points cannot be members of
\partial\mathscr{B}_{\mathrm{pc}}(O,R)
by induction.

Thus,
L^{\prime\prime}(a,b^{*})=(a,b)
for some
(a,b^{*})\in\partial\mathscr{B}_{\mathrm{pc}}(O,R)
, or
L^{\prime}(a^{*},b)=(a,b)
for some
(a^{*},b)\in\partial\mathscr{B}_{\mathrm{pc}}(O,R)
.

In the former case, we have
b^{*}-a=a-b+1=-(c-1)\not\equiv c\pmod{2}
. However, this is precluded by induction if
|-(c-1)|<R
, since
c\equiv R\pmod{2}
. If
|-(c-1)|\geq R
, since
\mathcal{F}(R,c^{\prime})=\emptyset
for
|c^{\prime}|>R
, we have
|-(c-1)|=R
, contradicting
c\not\equiv R+1\pmod{2}
.

Similarly, in the latter case, we have
b-a^{*}=a-b-1=-(c+1)\not\equiv c\pmod{2}
. This is impossible if
|-(c+1)|<R
by induction, since
c\equiv R\pmod{2}
. Otherwise, we obtain
|-(c+1)|=R
,
which contradicts
c\not\equiv R+1\pmod{2}
.

This concludes the proof of Lemma
3.2
.

Lemma 3.3
.

For any integer
r\geq 0
, we have:

\displaystyle\mathcal{F}(r,r)\setminus\mathcal{Q}_{\mathrm{I}}

\displaystyle=\big(\mathscr{L}(r)\cap\mathcal{Q}_{\mathrm{II}}\big)\setminus\{(0,r)\},

and

\displaystyle\mathcal{F}(r,-r)\setminus\mathcal{Q}_{\mathrm{I}}

\displaystyle=\big(\mathscr{L}(-r)\cap\mathcal{Q}_{\mathrm{IV}}\big)\setminus\{(0,-r)\}.

Proof.

When
r=0
this is clear since
\mathcal{F}(0,0)\setminus\mathcal{Q}_{\mathrm{I}}=\emptyset

and the only point in
\mathscr{L}(0)
that lies in
\mathcal{Q}_{\mathrm{II}}
(resp.
\mathcal{Q}_{\mathrm{IV}}
) is
O
.
For
|r|=1
, the statements are true since
\partial\mathscr{B}_{\mathrm{pc}}(O,1)=\{(0,\pm 1),(\pm 1,0)\}
.
Suppose the the statements hold for
1\leq|r|\leq R
.

Each point
(a,a+R)
in
\mathcal{F}(R,R)\setminus\mathcal{Q}_{\mathrm{I}}
contributes a unique point
(a-1,a+R)
to
\mathcal{F}(R+1,R+1)\setminus\mathcal{Q}_{\mathrm{I}}
. Moreover,
(a,a+R+1)=((a+1)-1,a+R+1)
and the point
(a+1,a+R+1)\in\mathcal{F}(R,R)\setminus\mathcal{Q}_{\mathrm{I}}
as long as
a<-1
. Since
(0,R)\in\mathcal{Q}_{\mathrm{I}}
, we obtain that
(-1,R-1)
is the only point in
\mathcal{F}(R,R)\setminus\mathcal{Q}_{\mathrm{I}}
that contributes two points to
\mathcal{F}(R+1,R+1)\setminus\mathcal{Q}_{\mathrm{I}}
, namely
(-2,R-1)
and
(-1,R)
.

Thus,

\displaystyle|\mathcal{F}(R+1,R+1)\setminus\mathcal{Q}_{\mathrm{I}}|\geq|\mathcal{F}(R,R)\setminus\mathcal{Q}_{\mathrm{I}}|+1.

Note that

\displaystyle|\mathscr{L}(R+1)\cap\mathcal{Q}_{\mathrm{II}}|-1=|\mathscr{L}(R)\cap\mathcal{Q}_{\mathrm{II}}|=|\mathcal{F}(R,R)\setminus\mathcal{Q}_{\mathrm{I}}|+1,

by induction, and each contributed point above is a member of
\mathcal{Q}_{\mathrm{II}}
, lies on the line
y=x+(R+1)
, and has strictly negative first coordinate (that is, each such point is in the set
(\mathscr{L}(R+1)\cap\mathcal{Q}_{\mathrm{II}})\setminus\{(0,R+1)\}
).

We conclude that

\displaystyle\mathcal{F}(R+1,R+1)\setminus\mathcal{Q}_{\mathrm{I}}\supseteq\mathscr{L}(R+1)\cap\mathcal{Q}_{\mathrm{II}}\setminus\{(0,R+1)\}.

If there was another point
(a,b)
in
\mathcal{F}(R+1,R+1)\setminus\mathcal{Q}_{\mathrm{I}}
, it follows that
a<-(R+1)
and
b<0
, and so by induction
(a+1,b),(a,b-1)\not\in\mathcal{F}(R,R)
.
Since
\mathcal{F}(R,c)=\emptyset
for
|c|>R
, the other two points at taxicab distance
1
from
(a,b)
are not in
\partial\mathscr{B}_{\mathrm{pc}}(O,R)
, so it must be the case that
(a,b)=L^{\prime}(a^{*},b)
for some
a^{*}\in\mathbb{Z}
such that
(a^{*},b)\in\partial\mathscr{B}_{\mathrm{pc}}(O,R)
, or
(a,b)=L^{\prime\prime}(a,b^{*})
for some
b^{*}\in\mathbb{Z}
such that
(a,b^{*})\in\partial\mathscr{B}_{\mathrm{pc}}(O,R)
. But then either
a^{*}=2b-a+1
or
b^{*}=2a-b+1
.

In the former case, we have
b-a^{*}=a-b-1=-(R+1)-1=-R-2
, which is impossible since
\mathcal{F}(R,-R-2)=\emptyset
.

In the latter case,
b^{*}-a=a-b+1=-(R+1)+1=-R
: this is also impossible since induction then yields that
(a,b^{*})\in\mathcal{Q}_{\mathrm{IV}}
.

By symmetry or adapting the above argument from this proof,
we also obtain

\mathcal{F}(R+1,-(R+1))\setminus\mathcal{Q}_{\mathrm{I}}=\big(\mathscr{L}(-(R+1))\cap\mathcal{Q}_{\mathrm{IV}}\big)\setminus\{(0,-(R+1))\},

thus concluding the proof of Lemma
3.3
.

Lemma 3.4

For every integer

\displaystyle\max\mathcal{S}(r,r)

\displaystyle=\scalebox{0.81}{$\dbinom{r}{2}$},

.

and by definition of

\displaystyle L^{\prime\prime}\Big(\scalebox{0.69}{$\dbinom{R+1}{2}$},\scalebox{0.69}{$\dbinom{R}{2}$}\Big)=\Big(\scalebox{0.69}{$\dbinom{R+1}{2}$},\scalebox{0.69}{$\dbinom{R+2}{2}$}\Big).

Remark 5.1
.

We list some basic properties of
\operatorname{z}
.

(1)

By definition (

),
\operatorname{z}(x)\neq 0

\operatorname{z}(x)>0

\operatorname{z}(x)<0

(2)

For every integer

\displaystyle\operatorname{z}^{(k)}(x)=\begin{cases}(-1)^{k}(x+k),&\text{ if $x>0$; }\\[8.0pt]

.

(3)

, and

\big([0,r-1-k]\cap\mathbb{Z}\big)+(c+1)k-\scalebox{0.81}{$\dbinom{k}{2}$},&\text{if }c=0.\end{cases}

If

If

and
(c-1)k-\scalebox{0.74}{$\dbinom{k}{2}$}=k-r+(c-1)(k-1)-\scalebox{0.74}{$\dbinom{k-1}{2}$}

6.
Proof of Theorem
2

Recalling the notation
O=(0,0)
for the origin, the boundaries of the smallest balls are:

\partial\mathscr{B}_{\mathrm{pc}}(O,0)=\{O\}
, and

\partial\mathscr{B}_{\mathrm{pc}}(O,1)=\{(\pm 1,0),(0,\pm 1)\}
,
as seen in Figure
6
.
Then formula (
5
) is verified for
r=0
and
r=1
, because

5/2-1+5/2=4
.
We may therefore assume
r\geq 2
.

For every
c\in\mathbb{Z}
, writing
|c|=r-2k
, the following statements hold:

(1)

|\mathcal{F}(r,c)|=2r-2k
, if
c\equiv r\pmod{2}
and
|c|\leq r-2
,
by formula (
3
);

(2)

|\mathcal{F}(r,c)|=0
, if
c\not\equiv r\pmod{2}
and
|c|<r-2
, by
Lemma

3.2
;

(3)

|\mathcal{F}(r,\pm r))|=\scalebox{0.74}{$\dbinom{r}{2}$}+1+r=\scalebox{0.74}{$\dbinom{r+1}{2}$}+1
, by formula (
4
).

Consequently, we may determine the number of points on the boundary

\partial\mathscr{B}_{\mathrm{pc}}(O,r)
.
If
r>0
is even, we have:

\displaystyle\#\partial\mathscr{B}_{\mathrm{pc}}(O,r)

\displaystyle=2\left(\scalebox{0.81}{$\dbinom{r+1}{2}$}+1\right)+r+2\sum_{k=1}^{r/2-1}2r-2k

\displaystyle=r^{2}+2r+2+4r\left(\mbox{\small$\displaystyle\frac{r}{2}$}-1\right)-4\scalebox{0.81}{$\dbinom{r/2}{2}$}

\displaystyle=\mbox{\small$\displaystyle\frac{5r^{2}}{2}$}-r+2.

Likewise, if
r
is odd, we have:

\displaystyle\#\partial\mathscr{B}_{\mathrm{pc}}(O,r)

\displaystyle=2\left(\scalebox{0.81}{$\dbinom{r+1}{2}$}+1\right)+2\sum_{k=1}^{(r+1)/2-1}2r-2k

\displaystyle=r^{2}+r+2+4r\left(\mbox{\small$\displaystyle\frac{r+1}{2}$}-1\right)-4\scalebox{0.81}{$\dbinom{(r+1)/2}{2}$}

\displaystyle=\mbox{\small$\displaystyle\frac{5r^{2}}{2}$}-r+\mbox{\small$\displaystyle\frac{5}{2}$}.

Taking the sum over the interval
[0,R]\cap\mathbb{Z}
of the expressions (
5
) obtained for
\#\partial\mathscr{B}_{\mathrm{pc}}(0,r)
, where
0\leq r\leq R
, we obtain the area of the
the ball
\mathscr{B}_{\mathrm{pc}}(O,R)
:

\displaystyle\sum_{r=0}^{R}\#\partial\mathscr{B}_{\mathrm{pc}}(O,r)

\displaystyle=\#\partial\mathscr{B}_{\mathrm{pc}}(O,0)+\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}r=1\\
r\text{ odd}\end{subarray}}^{R}\#\partial\mathscr{B}_{\mathrm{pc}}(O,r)+\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}r=2\\
r\text{ even}\end{subarray}}^{R}\#\partial\mathscr{B}_{\mathrm{pc}}(O,r)

\displaystyle=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}r=1\\
r\text{ odd}\end{subarray}}^{R}\bigg(\mbox{\small$\displaystyle\frac{5r^{2}}{2}$}-r+\mbox{\small$\displaystyle\frac{5}{2}$}\bigg)+\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}r=2\\
r\text{ even}\end{subarray}}^{R}\#\partial\mathscr{B}_{\mathrm{pc}}(O,r)\bigg(\mbox{\small$\displaystyle\frac{5r^{2}}{2}$}-r+2\bigg).

It then follows that:

\displaystyle\#\mathscr{B}_{\mathrm{pc}}(O,R)

\displaystyle=1+\sum_{r=1}^{R}\bigg(\mbox{\small$\displaystyle\frac{5r^{2}}{2}$}-r+2\bigg)+\mbox{\small$\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}$}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}r=1\\
r\text{ odd}\end{subarray}}^{R}1

\displaystyle=1+\mbox{\small$\displaystyle\frac{5R(R+1)(R+2)}{12}$}-\mbox{\small$\displaystyle\frac{R(R+1)}{2}$}+2R+\mbox{\small$\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}$}\left\lceil\mbox{\small$\displaystyle\frac{R}{2}$}\right\rceil

\displaystyle=\mbox{\small$\displaystyle\frac{1}{12}$}\big(10R^{3}+9R^{2}+23R\big)+\mbox{\small$\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}$}\left\lceil\mbox{\small$\displaystyle\frac{R}{2}$}\right\rceil+1.

This concludes the proof of
Theorem

2
.
