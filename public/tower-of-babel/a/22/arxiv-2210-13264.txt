Localization theorem for homological vector fields
Vera Serganova, Alexander Sherman

Abstract
. We present a general theorem which computes the cohomology of a homological vector field on global sections of vector bundles over smooth affine supervarieties. The hypotheses and results have the clear flavor of a localization theorem.



1.
Introduction

Let
X
be an affine supervariety, and let
Q
be an odd vector field on
X
; that is, an odd derivation of its algebra of functions,
\Bbbk[X]
. We call
Q

homological
if
Q^{2}
acts semisimply on
\Bbbk[X]
. As the name suggests, taking the cohomology of a homological vector field, i.e.
\operatorname{ker}(Q:\Bbbk[X]^{Q^{2}})/\operatorname{Im}(Q:\Bbbk[X]^{Q^{2}})
, is a ‘nice’ operation. Here
\Bbbk[X]^{Q^{2}}
denotes the
Q^{2}
-invariants of
\Bbbk[X]
. We write this cohomology, which is a super vector space, as
DS_{Q}\Bbbk[X]
, because in the case when
Q
comes from the action of a Lie superalgebra
\mathfrak{g}
, it is exactly the value of the Duflo-Serganova functor, from the theory of Lie superalgebras. The goal of the present paper is to understand conditions under which we may localize the computation of
DS_{Q}
to a smooth, closed subvariety
Y
of
X
.

The Duflo-Serganova functor has played a prominent role in the representation theory of Lie superalgebras since being introduced in
[
DS
]
(see, for instance,
[
HW
]
,
[
Ser1
]
,
[
GH
]
,
[
EAS
]
,
[
EAHS
]
). For an up-to-date survey dealing with the Duflo-Serganova functor in the representation theory of Lie superalgebras, we refer to
[
GHSS
]
.

On the other hand, localization theorems have proven to be powerful tools in computations of integrals and cohomology, providing vast generalizations of previously understood results. As an important example for us, in
[
SZ97
]
a localization theorem was proven for computing Berezin integrals on a supermanifold when the volume form admits an odd symmetry satisfying sufficiently nice properties. This was recently used in
[
SS
]
and
[
SV
]
to prove ‘splitting ’properties of distinguished subgroups of certain (almost) simple supergroups, which analogizes the definition of Sylow subgroups in the finite characteristic setting. In this paper, our hypotheses are not too far from the ones of
[
SZ97
]
, except that we work in the algebraic category.

More precisely, suppose that
X
is a smooth affine supervariety with odd vector field
Q
, such that
Q^{2}
acts semisimply on
\Bbbk[X]
. Then if
Q
vanishes on a smooth subvariety
Y
, such that it acts in a certain nondegenerate way on the normal bundle of
Y
in
X
, we show that
DS_{Q}\Bbbk[X]=\Bbbk[Y]
. One can view this theorem as a generalization of a result which is already known: that if
Q
is non-vanishing on all of
X
, then
DS_{Q}\Bbbk[X]=0
.

The precise theorem (
4.1
) is slightly more general, both in that it begins with a vector bundle on
X
, and it weakens the assumption that
Q
needs to vanish on all of
Y
. In any case, the result provides a powerful tool for computations, many of which are of great interest. We attempt to survey some of the applications in Sections 5 and 6.

As will be seen in the proof, the main technical input is from the theory of commutative algebra; namely, the vector fields we consider have the property that locally they define a Koszul complex for a sequence of regular elements in a regular commutative ring. Thus by using what is already known about the Koszul complex in such a setting, we are able to prove the vanishing results for the cohomology of our operator.

Remark 1.1
.

It is clear that one can take the cohomology

\operatorname{ker}(Q:\Bbbk[X]^{Q^{2}})/\operatorname{Im}(Q:\Bbbk[X]^{Q^{2}}),

without the assumption that
Q^{2}
acts semisimply; however this assumption is crucial for our results, as it implies that we are working with the action of a Lie superalgebra which has a ‘nice’ (Frobenius) category of representations, in which case
DS_{Q}
has the interpretation of a semisimplification functor. Thus we have enough projective-injective modules in our category, which is used throughout. See Section 3 for more details.

1.1.
Relation to physics

The Duflo-Serganova functor has appeared under different guises in theoretical physics. Odd operators
Q
in a supersymmetric field theory that satisfy
Q^{2}=0
are examples of BRST operators, and allow one to employ the so-called BRST formalism, which includes taking cohomology in
Q
. Such a situation arises in several places in the literature. In
[
W
]
, it was used as a key part in topological and holomorphic twists of supersymmetric field theories. Twisting a supersymmetric field theory gives rise to simpler field theories that can be topological (giving a TQFT), holomorphic, or something in between, depending on properties of the chosen
Q
. A mathematically rigorous approach to the twisting of supersymmetric field theories is explained in
[
Cos
]
.

Note that our setting is more flexible in that we do not impose the requirement that
Q^{2}=0
; however we show in
4.4
that one can always reduce to the case when
Q^{2}=0
.

In
[
CCMV
]
, the authors also consider the cohomology of homological vector fields (referred to as cohomological reductions in their paper) on the smooth (or
L^{2}
) sections of certain vector bundles on the target spaces of sigma models, which are homogeneous superspaces. In contrast, in the algebraic setting we find unexpected extra components of these cohomological reductions; see for instance the table in
6.6
for the case of symmetric spaces. In fact, when our space is a group
G
and we consider the action of an adjoint vector field, we find extra odd infinitesimal symmetries in the cohomological reduction, which will themselves act on the Duflo-Serganova functor (see
5.4
).

We finally highlight the connection of our work to
[
GK
]
, where the cohomology of a vector field on
Q
-bundles is applied to the study of gauge PDEs. There, they define a notion of equivalent reduction of supermanifolds with a BRST operator
Q
as ones which differ from one another by a ‘
Q
-contractible’ fiber bundle, i.e. a
Q
-bundle with trivial
Q
-cohomology along the fibers. In this case they obtain that the
Q
-cohomology of supermanifolds with equivalent reductions is the same.

1.2.
Outlook for non-affine varieties

Our localization theorem can be extended to the non-affine case, although one must work in the derived category to obtain the correct framework. This will be left to a future work, where we will in particular study applications to vector bundles on flag varieties.

1.3.
Summary of sections

In Section 2 we recall the basic supergeometric language we will use, and some preliminary results. In Section 3 we discuss
\mathfrak{q}
-supervarieties and introduce the fundamental tools we need for our main theorem. Section 4 contains the main theorem and its proof. Section 5 discusses the first applications, to the computation of the supergroup
\widetilde{G_{u}}
. Here we also prove an important criterion for identifying a supervariety as the odd tangent bundle of an even variety. In Section 6 we discuss computations on certain homogeneous superspaces, in particular supersymmetric spaces. Section 7, an appendix, computes
\widetilde{G_{u}}
for a certain root subgroup of
D(2,1;\alpha)
.

2.
Supergeometric preliminaries

We work throughout over an algebraically closed field
\Bbbk
of characteristic zero. For a super vector space
V
we write
V=V_{\overline{0}}\oplus V_{\overline{1}}
for its parity decomposition. If
v\in V
is homogeneous, we write
\overline{v}\in\{\overline{0},\overline{1}\}=\mathbb{Z}/2\mathbb{Z}
for its parity. If
R
is a supercommutative ring, then we denote by
R^{m|n}
a free
R
-module of rank
m|n
. If
M
is a free
R
-module of finite rank
(m|n)
, then we may define
\operatorname{Ber}(M)
, the Berezinian of
M
, as in Sec. 3.3.7 of
[
M
]
. It is again a free
R
-module of rank
(1|0)
if
n
is even and rank
(0|1)
if
n
is odd.

2.1.
Notation for supervarieties

We will use the symbols
X,Y,\dots
for supervarieties with even subschemes
X_{0},Y_{0},\dots.
For a supervariety
X
we write
\mathcal{O}_{X}
for the structure sheaf and
\Bbbk[X]:=\Gamma(X,\mathcal{O}_{X})
for the superalgebra of global functions on
X
. We will be considering supervarieties in the sense of Def. 2.1 of
[
She1
]
, however all spaces of interest will be smooth and affine. A smooth affine supervariety is always given by the exterior algebra of a finite rank vector bundle on a smooth affine variety
X_{0}
(see
[
VMP
]
). For several equivalent conditions to being smooth, which we will use at various points without elaboration, see the appendix of
[
She1
]
.

Note that affine supervarieties and morphisms between them are entirely determined by their algebras of global functions and maps between them, just as with affine varieties. Further, we note that the space of global functions of an affine supervariety is finitely generated as a
\Bbbk
-superalgebra. See
[
CCF
]
for more on the basics of algebraic supergeometry.

We will employ the convention that for functions on a supervariety the symbols
t,s
will denote even functions,
\xi,\eta
odd functions, and
f,g
functions of ambiguous parity.

If
X
is a supervariety, there is a canonical closed embedding
i_{X}:X_{0}\to X
which is a homeomorphism of underlying topological spaces. The closed points of
X
are the
\Bbbk
-points, which we write as
X(\Bbbk)
, and they are canonically identified with the closed points of
X_{0}
via
i_{X}
. If
x
is a closed point of
X
and
\mathcal{F}
is coherent a sheaf on
X
, we write
\mathcal{F}_{x}
for the stalk of
\mathcal{F}
at
x
, and
\mathcal{F}|_{x}
for the fiber of
\mathcal{F}
at
x
. We will write
\mathfrak{m}_{x}
for the maximal ideal of
\Bbbk[X]
corresponding to
x
. Then the cotangent space at
x
is given by
T_{x}^{*}X:=\mathfrak{m}_{x}/\mathfrak{m}_{x}^{2}
, and the tangent space by
T_{x}X=\left(T_{x}^{*}X\right)^{*}
.

A vector bundle on a supervariety
X
is by definition a coherent sheaf
\mathcal{V}
which is locally isomorphic to
\mathcal{O}_{X}^{m|n}
for some
m,n\in\mathbb{N}
. We define
\operatorname{Ber}(\mathcal{V})
, the Berezinian of
\mathcal{V}
, to be the rank one vector bundle obtained by glueing together
\operatorname{Ber}(\mathcal{V}|_{U})
on open sets
U
for which
\mathcal{V}|_{U}
is trivialized.

Given a function
f\in\Bbbk[X]
, we will use the notation
D(f)
of Chapt. 2.2 of
[
Har
]
for the open subvariety on which
f
is non-vanishing.

Finally, if
Y
is a closed subvariety of
X
, we write
\mathcal{I}_{Y}
for the ideal sheaf of
Y
in
X
, and
\mathcal{N}_{Y}^{\vee}=\mathcal{I}_{Y}/\mathcal{I}_{Y}^{2}
for the conormal bundle of
Y
in
X
.

2.2.
Coordinates

Let
X
be a smooth supervariety. We say functions
t_{1},\dots,t_{m},\xi_{1},\dots,\xi_{n}
define a coordinate system on an open set
U
in
X
if they are regular on
U
and
dt_{1},\dots,dt_{m},d\xi_{1},\dots,d\xi_{n}
trivialize the cotangent bundle on
U
. In this case, the tangent bundle will be trivialized on
U
by the sections
\partial_{t_{1}},\dots\partial_{t_{m}},\partial_{\xi_{1}},\dots,\partial_{\xi_{n}}
which are derivations acting in the obvious way.

By general facts about sections of vector bundles, if we have functions
t_{1},\dots,t_{m},\xi_{1},\dots,\xi_{n}
which define linearly independent differentials at a point, then they must define linearly independent differentials in a neighborhood of that point; in particular they define a coordinate system on a neighborhood of said point.

We will use the following lemma later on:

Lemma 2.1
.

Suppose that
X=\operatorname{Spec}A
is a smooth affine supervariety and
\xi_{1},\dots,\xi_{n}\in A_{\overline{1}}
are global odd coordinates on
X
. Then the derivations
\partial_{\xi_{1}},\dots,\partial_{\xi_{n}}
are well-defined, and if we set
A_{0}=\operatorname{ker}\partial_{\xi_{1}}\cap\cdots\cap\partial_{\xi_{n}}
, the natural map

A_{0}[\xi_{1},\dots,\xi_{n}]\to A

is an isomorphism.

Proof.

Because
X
is smooth, the cotangent bundle
\Omega_{X}
is locally free. The global odd coordinates
\xi_{1},\dots,\xi_{n}
define a map
\mathcal{O}_{X}^{0|n}\to\Omega_{X}
which is injective on fibers, and thus splits because
X
is affine. This splitting provides for us our derivations
\partial_{\xi_{i}}
for all
i
. Now let
A_{0}=\operatorname{ker}\partial_{\xi_{1}}\cap\cdots\cap\partial_{\xi_{n}}
, and consider the natural map

A_{0}[\xi_{1},\dots,\xi_{n}]\to A.

To see this map is injective, suppose that
\sum\limits_{I}f_{I}\xi_{I}=0
where
f_{I}\in A_{0}
. Then if we apply
\partial_{I}:=\prod_{i\in I}\partial_{\xi_{i}}
to
f
we learn that
f_{I}=0
for all
I
.

To show surjectivity, first order the subsets
I\subseteq\{1,\dots,n\}
using a lexicographical ordering, meaning that
I=\{i_{1},\dots,i_{r}\}<J=\{j_{1},\dots,j_{s}\}
whenever
|I|<|J|
, and if
|I|=|J|
then
I<J
if for some
k
we have
i_{k}<j_{k}
while
i_{\ell}=j_{\ell}
for
\ell<k
.

Now let
f\in A
and let
I
be the largest subset under this ordering such that
\partial_{I}(f)\neq 0
. Then we must have that
\partial_{\xi_{i}}(\partial_{I}(f))=0
for all
i
, i.e.
\partial_{I}(f)=g_{I}\in A_{0}
. Consider

f^{\prime}:=f-\partial_{I}(\xi_{I})g_{I}\xi_{I}

Then
\partial_{I}(f^{\prime})=0
by construction, and thus we may conclude by induction that
f^{\prime}\in A_{0}[\xi_{1},\dots,\xi_{n}]
, completing the proof.
∎

2.3.
Subvarieties

By abuse of language, we will refer to subsupervarieties as subvarieties. If
Y\subseteq X
is a closed subvariety of
X
, then we write
\mathcal{I}_{Y}
for the ideal sheaf of
Y
, and
\mathcal{N}_{Y}^{\vee}:=\mathcal{I}_{Y}/\mathcal{I}_{Y}^{2}
for the conormal sheaf of
Y
. Then
\mathcal{N}_{Y}^{\vee}
is a coherent sheaf on
Y
, and if
Y
is smooth then
\mathcal{N}_{Y}^{\vee}
is a vector bundle of rank given by the codimension of
Y
. In this case, every point on
Y
has an open neighborhood in
X
where we may choose local functions
t_{1},\dots,t_{m},\xi_{1},\dots,\xi_{n}\in\mathcal{I}_{Y}
such that they will trivialize
\mathcal{N}_{Y}^{\vee}
, and they define linearly independent differentials.

3.
\mathfrak{q}
-supervarieties

We explain several lemmas and definitions we will use repeatedly for the proof of the general theorem.

3.1.
\mathfrak{q}
-modules

Let
\mathfrak{q}
be the
(1|1)
-dimensional Lie superalgebra with basis
Q,Q^{2}:=\frac{1}{2}[Q,Q]
, where
Q
is odd. We will work in
\operatorname{Rep}_{Q^{2}}\mathfrak{q}(1)
, i.e. the category of
\mathfrak{q}
-modules (not necessarily finite-dimensional) on which
Q^{2}
acts semisimply. Thus when we refer to a
\mathfrak{q}
-module we will always assume that
Q^{2}
acts semisimply.

Definition 3.1

(Duflo-Serganova functor)
.

For a
\mathfrak{q}
-module
V
(recall that as explained above, this mean
Q^{2}
acts semisimply), define
DS_{Q}V
to be the super vector space given by the cohomology of
Q
when restricted to
V^{Q^{2}}
, the invariants (equivalently kernel) of
Q^{2}
on
V
. We will also write this occasionally using the shorthand
V_{Q}:=DS_{Q}V
.

The Duflo-Serganova functor was originally introduced in
[
DS
]
, and defines a tensor functor from
\operatorname{Rep}_{Q^{2}}\mathfrak{q}
to the category of super vector spaces. It is in fact a semisimplification functor of
\operatorname{Rep}_{Q^{2}}\mathfrak{q}(1)
, although we will not use this. For a more recent and thorough survey on this functor, we refer to
[
GHSS
]
.

We recall the following results which will be used throughout.

Lemma 3.2
.

Let
V
be a
\mathfrak{q}
-module.

(1)

V
is projective if and only if
DS_{Q}V=0
;

(2)

if
V
admits a finite-length projective resolution, then
V
is projective;

(3)

if the restriction
Q:V_{\overline{1}}\to V_{\overline{0}}
is an isomorphism, then
V
is projective.

3.2.
\mathfrak{q}
-supervarieties

We recall that on an affine supervariety
X=\operatorname{Spec}\Bbbk[X]
, a vector field is nothing but a derivation (in the super sense) of
\Bbbk[X]
.

Definition 3.3
.

An affine
\mathfrak{q}
-supervariety is a pair
(X,Q)
, where
X
is an affine supervariety and
Q
is an odd vector field on
X
such that
Q^{2}
acts semisimply on
\Bbbk[X]:=\Gamma(X,\mathcal{O}_{X})
. In other words,
\Bbbk[X]
is a
\mathfrak{q}
-module where
Q
acts by derivations. In the future we will simply write that
X
is a
\mathfrak{q}
-supervariety, supressing the
Q
.

Let
X
be a
\mathfrak{q}
-supervariety. Then since
\Bbbk[X]
is finitely generated, the
\mathfrak{q}
-action action necessarily integrates to the action of an algebraic supergroup
G_{Q}
on
X
, where
(\operatorname{Lie}G_{Q})_{\overline{1}}
is spanned by
Q
, and
(G_{Q})_{0}
is a central torus (of some finite dimension). Because of the ambiguity in this choice of torus depending on the representation, it is better to avoid fixing a lift to
G_{Q}
and instead only doing so when it is needed in an argument.

Definition 3.4
.

Let
X
be a
\mathfrak{q}
-supervariety. We say that an open subvariety
U\subseteq X
is
\mathfrak{q}
-stable if
Q^{2}
acts semisimply on
\Bbbk[U]
. In this case
U
is itself naturally a
\mathfrak{q}
-supervariety.

Definition 3.5
.

Given a vector field
Q
on
X
and a point
x\in X(\Bbbk)
, we may evaluate
Q
at
x
to obtain a tangent vector
Q_{x}\in T_{x}X
. We say
Q
is non-vanishing at
x
if
Q_{x}\neq 0
, and otherwise say it is vanishing at
x
. We say
Q
is non-vanishing on
X
if it is non-vanishing at every point
x\in X(\Bbbk)
. We write
Z:=Z(Q)
for the closed subvariety of
X_{0}
given by the vanishing set of
Q
.

Remark 3.6
.

Observe that if
\mathcal{I}_{Z}
denotes the ideal sheaf defined by
Z:=Z(Q)\subseteq X
, we have
Q(\mathcal{O}_{X})\subseteq\mathcal{I}_{Z}
.

3.2.1.
Equivariant sheaves

Let
\mathcal{F}
be a quasi-coherent sheaf on a
\mathfrak{q}
-supervariety
X
. We say that
\mathcal{F}
is
\mathfrak{q}
-equivariant if it admits an action of
Q
such that
Q^{2}
acts semisimply on global sections, and

Q(fv)=Q(f)v+(-1)^{\overline{f}}fQ(v),

where
v
is a section of
\mathcal{F}
and
f
is a function.

Lemma 3.7
.

Let
X
be an affine
\mathfrak{q}
-supervariety; then the following are equivalent:

(1)

Z(Q)=\emptyset
;

(2)

there exists
\xi\in\Bbbk[X]
with
Q(\xi)=1
;

(3)

DS_{Q}\Bbbk[X]=0
;

(4)

if
\mathcal{F}
is any quasi-coherent
\mathfrak{q}
-equivariant sheaf on
X
, then
DS_{Q}\Gamma(X,\mathcal{F})=0
.

Proof.

Clearly
(4)\Rightarrow(3)
,
(3)\Rightarrow(2)
, and
(2)\Rightarrow(1)
. Further
(2)\Rightarrow(4)
because if
s\in\Gamma(X,\mathcal{F})
with
Q(s)=0
, then
Q(\xi s)=s
.

Thus it suffices to show
(1)\Rightarrow(2)
. Since
Q
is everywhere non-vanishing and
X
is affine, for each closed point
x\in X(\Bbbk)
there exists an odd function
\xi_{x}\in\Bbbk[X]
such that
(Q(\xi_{x}))(x)\neq 0
. Thus the ideal generated by
\{Q(\xi_{x})\}_{x\in X(\Bbbk)}
is equal to
\Bbbk[X]
, so we may choose finitely many functions in this set, say
Q(\xi_{1}),\dots,Q(\xi_{k})
, and even functions
g_{1},\dots,g_{k}
such that

g_{1}Q(\xi_{1})+\dots+g_{k}Q(\xi_{k})=1.

Set
\eta=g_{1}\xi_{1}+\dots+g_{k}\xi_{k}
. We have

Q\eta=[g_{1}Q(\xi_{1})+\dots+g_{k}Q(\xi_{k})]+[Q(g_{1})\xi_{1}+\dots+Q(g_{k})\xi_{k}]=1+r

where
r:=Q(g_{1})\xi_{1}+\dots+Q(g_{k})\xi_{k}
. Since
Q(g_{i})
is odd,
r\in(\Bbbk[X]_{\overline{1}})^{2}
and thus is nilpotent. We may then write
\eta=\sum\limits_{\lambda}\eta_{\lambda}
and
r=\sum\limits_{\lambda}r_{\lambda}
according to the decomposition of
\Bbbk[X]
into eigenspaces of
Q^{2}
. Since
Q^{2}
preserves
(\Bbbk[X]_{\overline{1}})^{2}
, we have
r_{\lambda}\in(\Bbbk[X]_{\overline{1}})^{2}
for all
\lambda
. Now we see that

Q(\eta)=\sum\limits_{\lambda}Q(\eta_{\lambda})=1+\sum\limits_{\lambda}r_{\lambda}.

Since
Q^{2}(1)=0
and
[Q^{2},Q]=0
, we find that
a:=Q(\eta_{0})=1+r_{0}
is a unit in
\Bbbk[X]
. Further,
Q(a)=Q^{2}(\eta_{0})=0
, so if we set
\xi:=\eta_{0}/a
, we find that

Q(\xi)=Q(\eta_{0}/a)=a/a=1.

∎

The following lemma is one of the main technical inputs in the proof of the main theorem in the next section.

Lemma 3.8
.

Let
\mathcal{W}
be a
\mathfrak{q}
-equivariant vector bundle on a smooth affine
\mathfrak{q}
-supervariety
X
, and suppose that
Q
vanishes at
x\in X(\Bbbk)
. Then there exists an affine open
\mathfrak{q}
-stable neighborhood
U
of
x
such that we have a
Q^{2}
-equivariant isomorphism of sheaves

\mathcal{W}|_{U}\cong\mathcal{O}_{U}\otimes_{\Bbbk}\mathcal{W}|_{x}.

If we suppose further that
\mathcal{W}\to\mathcal{W}|_{x}
admits a
\mathfrak{q}
-splitting, then the isomorphism can be made to be
\mathfrak{q}
-equivariant. In particular in this case,
\Gamma(U,\mathcal{W})
is a projective
\mathfrak{q}
-module if
\mathcal{W}|_{x}
is.

Proof.

Since
Q^{2}
acts semisimply we may split
\Gamma(X,\mathcal{W})\to\mathcal{W}|_{x}
as a
Q^{2}
-module in any case; choose such a splitting, and if a
\mathfrak{q}
-equivariant splitting exists then choose the splitting to be
\mathfrak{q}
-equivariant. Then we have a homogeneous basis
v_{1},\dots,v_{m},w_{1},\dots,w_{n}
of our assumed splitting, i.e. coming from the image of
\mathcal{W}|_{x}
under the splitting map, such that each
v_{i},w_{j}
is a global section and a
Q^{2}
-eigenvector.

Let
U
be the maximal open subset on which
v_{1},\dots,v_{m},w_{1},\dots,w_{n}
trivialize
\mathcal{W}
; then
U
is affine, as it is the complement of the divisor determined by a rational,
Q^{2}
-eigensection of
\operatorname{Ber}(\mathcal{W})
(see Section
2.1
for notation). Since the rational section is a
Q^{2}
-eigenvector,
\Bbbk[U]
will admit a semisimple action of
Q^{2}
. Finally, since
\mathcal{W}
is trivialized on
U
by these sections, the natural map
\mathcal{O}_{U}\otimes_{\Bbbk}\mathcal{W}|_{x}\to\mathcal{W}|_{U}
is an isomorphism.
∎

Lemma 3.9
.

Suppose that
\mathcal{F}
is a
\mathfrak{q}
-equivariant quasicoherent sheaf such that every point
x\in X(\Bbbk)
has a
\mathfrak{q}
-stable, affine open neighborhood
U
such that for every
\mathfrak{q}
-stable affine open
V\subseteq U
, we have that
\Gamma(V,\mathcal{F})
is projective over
\mathfrak{q}
. Then
\Gamma(X,\mathcal{F})
is a projective
\mathfrak{q}
-module.

Proof.

Using our assumptions we may find a
\mathfrak{q}
-stable affine Cech cover
\{U_{i}\}
of
X
such that
\Gamma(U_{i_{1}}\cap\cdots\cap U_{i_{k}},\mathcal{F})
is projective for all
i
. Since
X
is affine, the Cech complex

0\to\Gamma(X,\mathcal{F})\to\prod\limits_{i}\Gamma(U_{i},\mathcal{F})\to\prod\limits_{i,j}\Gamma(U_{i}\cap U_{j},\mathcal{F})\to\cdots

will be exact and finite, so we can conclude by (2) of Lemma
3.2
.
∎

Corollary 3.10
.

Suppose that
\mathcal{W}
is a
\mathfrak{q}
-equivariant vector bundle on a smooth affine
\mathfrak{q}
-supervariety
X
such that at every point
x\in Z(Q)
,
\mathcal{W}|_{x}
is a projective
\mathfrak{q}
-module. Then
\Gamma(X,\mathcal{W})
is a projective
\mathfrak{q}
module.

Proof.

We apply
Lemma

3.9
. If
x\in X(\Bbbk)\setminus Z(Q)
, then we may find a
\mathfrak{q}
-stable, affine open neighborhood
U
of
x
such that
Q
is nonvanishing on
U
. Thus by
Lemma

3.7
,
DS_{Q}\Gamma(V,\mathcal{W})=0
for any
\mathfrak{q}
-stable affine open
V\subseteq U
.

If on the other hand
x\in Z(Q)
, then by
Lemma

3.8
there exists a
\mathfrak{q}
-stable, affine open neighborhood
U
of
x
such that
\mathcal{W}|_{U}\cong\mathcal{O}_{U}\otimes\mathcal{W}|_{x}
. Therefore, for any
\mathfrak{q}
-stable affine open
V\subseteq U
we will have
\mathcal{W}|_{V}\cong\mathcal{O}_{V}\otimes\mathcal{W}|_{x}
, so since
\mathcal{W}|_{x}
is projective,
\Gamma(V,\mathcal{W})
is also projective, and we are done.
∎

In the following example we show that the converse of
3.10
fails.

Example 3.11
.

Let
X=\mathbb{A}^{1|1}
and consider it with a trivial action of
\mathfrak{q}
; in particular
Z(Q)=X(\Bbbk)
. Present
\Bbbk[X]=\Bbbk[t,\xi]
, and give
\mathcal{O}_{X}
the structure of a
\mathfrak{q}
-equivariant sheaf via
Q(f)=\xi f
. Then we have
DS_{Q}\Gamma(X,\mathcal{O}_{X})=0
, even though the
fibers of this vector bundle are not projective as
\mathfrak{q}
-modules.

4.
Main theorem

Theorem 4.1
.

Let
\mathcal{V}
be a
\mathfrak{q}
-equivariant vector bundle on a smooth affine
\mathfrak{q}
-supervariety
X
. Write
Z=Z(Q)\subseteq X_{0}
for the vanishing subvariety of
Q
, and suppose that
Y
is a smooth, closed subvariety of
X
such that:

(1)

Z(\Bbbk)\subseteq Y(\Bbbk)
;

(2)

Q(\mathcal{I}_{Y})\subseteq\mathcal{I}_{Y}
;

(3)

Q:(\mathcal{N}_{Y}^{\vee}|_{z})_{\overline{1}}\to(\mathcal{N}_{Y}^{\vee}|_{z})_{\overline{0}}
is an isomorphism for all
z\in Z(\Bbbk)
.

(4)

For all points
z\in Z(\Bbbk)
, there exists a
\mathfrak{q}
-stable affine open
U_{z}
of
z
such that the map
\Gamma(U_{z},\mathcal{V})\to\mathcal{V}|_{z}
splits over
\mathfrak{q}
.

Then
Y
is naturally a
\mathfrak{q}
-supervariety,
\mathcal{V}|_{Y}
is a
\mathfrak{q}
-equivariant vector bundle on
Y
, and we have:

DS_{Q}\Gamma(X,\mathcal{V})=DS_{Q}\Gamma(Y,\mathcal{V}|_{Y}).

Further this identification is induced by the natural map
\Gamma(X,\mathcal{V})\to\Gamma(Y,\mathcal{V}|_{Y})
.

4.1.
Corollaries of Theorem
4.1

Note that if
\mathcal{V}=\mathcal{O}_{X}
is the trivial vector bundle with the canonical
\mathfrak{q}
-equivariant structure, then condition (4) always holds. Thus we obtain:

Corollary 4.2
.

Under hypotheses (1)-(3) of Theorem
4.1
, we have that the restriction map
\Bbbk[X]\to\Bbbk[Y]
induces an identification:

DS_{Q}\Bbbk[X]=DS_{Q}\Bbbk[Y].

Corollary 4.3
.

Suppose that in the hypotheses of Theorem
4.1
we have
Q(\mathcal{O}_{X})\subseteq\mathcal{I}_{Y}
. Then the restriction map
\Bbbk[X]\to\Bbbk[Y]
induces an identification

DS_{Q}\Bbbk[X]=\Bbbk[Y].

Further, suppose that
\mathcal{V}
is a
\mathfrak{q}
-equivariant vector bundle on
X
satisfying (4) of
4.1
. Then
DS_{Q}\Gamma(X,\mathcal{V})
will be the sections of a vector bundle on
Y
with fibers isomorphic to
DS_{Q}(\mathcal{V}|_{y})
.

Proof.

The first statement follows immediately from
4.1
. For the second statement, we may apply
Lemma

3.8
to show that
Q
defines a constant rank vector bundle endomorphism of
\mathcal{V}|_{Y}
, whose cohomology will have fibers as claimed.
∎

By the following corollary,
4.1
allows us to always reduce to the case when
Q^{2}=0
.

Corollary 4.4
.

If
Y=\operatorname{Spec}\Bbbk[X]/(\operatorname{Im}Q^{2})
where
(\operatorname{Im}Q^{2})
is the ideal generated by
\operatorname{Im}Q^{2}
, then
Y
is a smooth subvariety of
X
and we have

DS_{Q}\Bbbk[X]=DS_{Q}\Bbbk[Y].

Proof.

We need to check that
Y
is smooth and that
Q:(\mathcal{N}_{Y}^{\vee}|_{z})_{\overline{1}}\to(\mathcal{N}_{Y}^{\vee}|_{z})_{\overline{0}}
is an isomorphism for all
z\in Z(\Bbbk)
. Let
I=(\operatorname{Im}Q^{2})
, and let
y\in Y(\Bbbk)
. Choose a coordinate system
t_{1},\dots,t_{m},\xi_{1},\dots,\xi_{n}
around
y
such that these coordinates are eigenvectors. Then we claim that
I_{y}
, the localization of
I
at
y
, is generated by those coordinates with nonzero eigenvalues. The proof of this statement is identical to the one given in Lem. 2.1 of
[
I
]
, via Prop. 3.2 of
[
Sch
]
. Thus we obtain that
Y
is smooth, and further we have that
Q^{2}
is an automorphism of
\mathcal{N}_{Y}^{\vee}|_{y}
for all
y\in Y(\Bbbk)
, which is sufficient for condition (3) of
4.1
.
∎

4.2.
Proof of Theorem
4.1

The proof of Theorem
4.1
will occupy the remainder of this section. In fact, Theorem
4.1
follows as corollary of the following result, which is what we will actually show.

Proposition 4.5
.

Retain the hypotheses of Theorem
4.1
. Let
\mathcal{V}_{Y}
denote the sheaf of sections of
\mathcal{V}
which vanish upon restriction to
Y
. Then
\Gamma(X,\mathcal{V}_{Y})
is a projective
\mathfrak{q}
-module; in particular
DS_{Q}\Gamma(X,\mathcal{V}_{Y})=0
.

Indeed, Proposition
4.5
is enough because then if we apply
DS_{Q}
to

0\to\Gamma(X,\mathcal{V}_{Y})\to\Gamma(X,\mathcal{V})\to\Gamma(Y,\mathcal{V}|_{Y})\to 0,

we obtain that
DS_{Q}\Gamma(X,\mathcal{V})\to DS_{Q}\Gamma(Y,\mathcal{V}|_{Y})
is an isomorphism, see Lemma 2.7 of
[
GHSS
]
.

Proof of Proposition
4.5
.

We first deal with the case of
\mathcal{V}=\mathcal{O}_{X}
, and use Lemma
3.9
:

Points where
Q
doesn’t vanish:
If
x\in X(\Bbbk)\setminus Z(\Bbbk)
, then by affinity and the semisimplicity of the action of
Q^{2}
, there exists a
Q^{2}
-eigenfunction
f\in\Gamma(X,\mathcal{I}_{Z})
such that
f(x)\neq 0
. Thus
D(f)
will be a
\mathfrak{q}
-stable affine open subvariety containing
x
on which
Q
is nonvanishing. Thus, by Lemma
3.7
,
\Gamma(V,\mathcal{V}_{Y})
is projective for all
\mathfrak{q}
-stable affine open subsets
V\subseteq D(f)
.

Finding convenient local coordinates where
Q
does vanish:
It remains to deal with the points in
Z(\Bbbk)
. For this step we begin by assuming that
\mathcal{V}=\mathcal{O}_{X}
. Choose
z\in Z(\Bbbk)
. We want to find a ‘nice’ splitting of
\mathfrak{m}_{z}\to\mathfrak{m}_{z}/\mathfrak{m}_{z}^{2}
; to expect a full
\mathfrak{q}
-module splitting would be too much, but we can obtain enough as follows. Recall that
\mathcal{N}_{Y}^{\vee}|_{z}
is a subspace of
\mathfrak{m}_{z}/\mathfrak{m}_{z}^{2}
.

We have a natural,
\mathfrak{q}
-equivariant surjective map
\Gamma(X,\mathcal{I}_{Y})\to\mathcal{N}^{\vee}_{Y}|_{z}
; by our assumptions on
\mathcal{N}^{\vee}_{Y}|_{z}
it is a projective
\mathfrak{q}
-module, and so we may split this map
\mathfrak{q}
-equivariantly; in particular from this splitting we may choose sections
t_{1},\dots,t_{n},\xi_{1},\dots,\xi_{n}\in\Gamma(X,\mathcal{I}_{Y})
satisfying:

Q(\xi_{i})=t_{i},\ \ \ \ Q(t_{i})=c_{i}\xi_{i},

where
c_{i}\in\Bbbk
. Further, by the discussion at the end of
Section

2
, these functions define linearly independent differentials in a neighborhood of
z
. Write
V
for the vector space spanned by
t_{1},\dots,t_{n},\xi_{1},\dots,\xi_{n}
. Then the natural map
V\to T_{z}^{*}X
is injective and
\mathfrak{q}
-equivariant. Choose a
Q^{2}
-equivariant splitting
T^{*}_{z}X=V\oplus W
, and a
Q^{2}
-equivariant splitting of
W
off
\mathfrak{m}_{z}\to T_{z}^{*}X
, thus giving us functions

t_{1},\dots,t_{n},s_{1},\dots,s_{r},\xi_{1},\dots,\xi_{n},\eta_{1},\dots,\eta_{\ell}\in\mathfrak{m}_{z}

each of which is a
Q^{2}
-eigenvector (here the
s_{i}
’s are even and the
\eta_{i}
’s are odd). These functions define linearly independent differentials in a neighborhood of
z
; we may now apply the proof of
Lemma

3.8
using this splitting, to the case where
\mathcal{W}
is the cotangent bundle, to find a
\mathfrak{q}
-stable, affine open neighborhood
U^{\prime}
of
z
on which these functions continue to define linearly independent differentials.

Cutting down
U^{\prime}
to
U
so that
Y\cap U=(t_{1}=\cdots=t_{n}=\xi_{1}=\cdots\xi_{n}=0)
:
Consider the ideal
J:=(t_{1},\dots,t_{n},\xi_{1},\dots,\xi_{n})\subseteq\Bbbk[U^{\prime}]
; then we need further that
J=I_{Y\cap U^{\prime}}
, i.e. that the ideal
J
exactly cuts out
Y\cap U^{\prime}
. A priori we only have
J\subseteq I_{Y\cap U^{\prime}}
. However since
t_{1},\dots,t_{n},\xi_{1},\dots,\xi_{n}
are part of a coordinate system,
J
will cut out a smooth subvariety of the same dimension as
Y
, and thus the zero set of
J
can be written as
Y\sqcup Y^{\prime}
, i.e.
Y
will be a connected component of its zero set.

Consider the ideal
I_{Y^{\prime}}
of
Y^{\prime}
in
\Bbbk[U^{\prime}]
. Because
J
is
Q^{2}
-stable,
I_{Y^{\prime}}
is also
Q^{2}
-stable; by affinity there must exist a
Q^{2}
-eigenfunction
f\in I_{Y^{\prime}}
such that
f(z)\neq 0
. Now we set
U:=D(f)\subseteq U^{\prime}
; then
U
is a
Q^{2}
-stable affine open neighborhood of
z
, and
I_{Y\cap U}=(t_{1},\dots,t_{n},\xi_{1},\dots,\xi_{n})
.

Form of
Q
on
U
:
Now on
U
we have a trivialization of the tangent bundle defined by the sections
\partial_{t_{1}},\dots,\partial_{t_{n}},\partial_{s_{1}},\dots,\partial_{s_{r}},\partial_{\xi_{1}},\dots,\partial_{\xi_{n}},\partial_{\eta_{1}},\dots,\partial_{\eta_{\ell}}
. Under this trivialization,
Q
takes the local form:

(4.1)

Q=\sum\limits_{i}t_{i}\partial_{\xi_{i}}+\sum\limits_{i}c_{i}\xi_{i}\partial_{t_{i}}+\sum\limits_{i}f_{i}\partial_{s_{i}}+\sum\limits_{i}g_{i}\partial_{\eta_{i}}.

where
f_{1},\dots,f_{r},g_{1},\dots,g_{\ell}
are some functions. Further, by our choice of coordinates we have

Q^{2}=\sum_{i=1}^{n}c_{i}(t_{i}\partial_{t_{i}}+\xi_{i}\partial_{\xi_{i}})+\sum\limits_{i}d_{i}s_{i}\partial_{s_{i}}+\sum\limits_{i}e_{i}\eta_{i}\partial_{\eta_{i}}.

where
d_{i},e_{i}\in\Bbbk
. Define the following vector field on
U
:

h:=\xi_{1}\partial_{\xi_{1}}+\dots+\xi_{n}\partial_{\xi_{n}}.

Then
h
commutes with
Q^{2}
and has integral eigenvalues bounded between 0 and
n
. We may decompose
Q
as

Q=Q_{-1}+Q_{0}+Q_{1}+\dots+Q_{n}

according to the eigenvalues of
h
. By
Eq.

4.1
we have

Q_{-1}=t_{1}\partial_{\xi_{1}}+\dots+t_{k}\partial_{\xi_{k}}.

Realizing
Q_{-1}
as defining a Koszul complex:
Write
A=\Bbbk[U]
; then since
U
is affine and smooth, it is split. By Lemma
2.1
, we may split
A
as

A=A_{0}\otimes{\bigwedge}^{\bullet}\langle\xi_{1},\dots,\xi_{n},\eta_{1},\dots,\eta_{\ell}\rangle.

where
A_{0}=\operatorname{ker}\partial_{\xi_{1}}\cap\cdots\cap\operatorname{ker}\partial_{\xi_{n}}\cap\operatorname{ker}\partial_{\eta_{1}}\cap\cdots\cap\operatorname{ker}\partial_{\eta_{\ell}}
. In particular
t_{1},\dots,t_{n}\in A_{0}
. Then we have

I_{Y\cap U}=\left((t_{1},\dots,t_{n})_{0}\oplus A_{0}\otimes{\bigwedge}^{+}\langle\xi_{1},\dots,\xi_{k}\rangle\right)\otimes{\bigwedge}^{\bullet}\langle\eta_{1},\dots,\eta_{\ell}\rangle.

Here
(t_{1},\dots,t_{n})_{0}
denotes the ideal generated by
t_{1},\dots,t_{n}
in
A_{0}
, which is exactly the ideal defining
Y_{0}\cap U_{0}
in
U_{0}
. Now one sees that
Q_{-1}
is defining the Koszul complex on

A_{0}\otimes{\bigwedge}^{\bullet}\langle\xi_{1},\dots,\xi_{n}\rangle

and the cohomology is
A_{0}/(t_{1},\dots,t_{n})_{0}
because
t_{1},\dots,t_{n}
define a regular sequence in
A_{0}
. Thus the cohomology of
Q_{-1}
on

(t_{1},\dots,t_{n})_{0}\oplus A_{0}\otimes{\wedge}^{+}\langle\xi_{1},\dots,\xi_{k}\rangle

is trivial, implying that its cohomology on
I_{Y\cap U}
is also trivial, i.e.
DS_{Q_{-1}}I_{Y\cap U}=0
, since
Q_{-1}(\eta_{i})=0
.

Obtaining that
DS_{Q}I_{Y\cap U}=0
:
To show that
DS_{Q}I_{Y\cap U}=0
, we appeal to the following lemma.

Lemma 4.6
.

Suppose that
h,Q_{-1},Q_{0},\dots,Q_{n}
are operators on a super vector space
V
such that

(1)

h
is even and semisimple with bounded, integral eigenvalues;

(2)

each
Q_{i}
is an odd operator;

(3)

[h,Q_{i}]=iQ_{i}
.

If
DS_{Q_{-1}}V=0
, then
DS_{Q}V=0
, where
Q=Q_{-1}+Q_{0}+\dots+Q_{n}
.

Proof.

Let
v\in V
be such that
Qv=0
, and write
v=v_{i}+v_{i+1}+\dots+v_{j}
for the
h
-eigenspace decomposition of
v
, where we assume that
i
is such that
v_{i}\neq 0
. Then clearly
Q_{-1}v_{i}=0
, so by assumption there exists
w=w_{i+1}+\dots
such that
v-Qw
only has nonzero components in eigenspaces of
h
with eigenvalue strictly larger than
i
. Because
h
has bounded eigenvalues on
V
, we may thus continue inductively to construct
v^{\prime}\in V
such that
Qv^{\prime}=v
, completing the proof.
∎

Completing the proof for
\mathcal{V}=\mathcal{O}_{X}
:
Now if
V\subseteq U
is an affine
\mathfrak{q}
-stable open subvariety of
U
, then we will still have our coordinates above and so the same arguments imply that
DS_{Q}I_{Y\cap V}=0
, which completes the proof in the case when
\mathcal{V}=\mathcal{O}_{X}
.

The case of
\mathcal{V}
general:
Now we need to deal with the case of general
\mathcal{V}
. However we use hypothesis (4) and apply Lemma
3.8
to the case of
X=U_{z}\cap U
and
\mathcal{W}=\mathcal{V}
to find a
\mathfrak{q}
-stable, affine open neighborhood
U^{\prime}
of
z
on which
\mathcal{V}|_{U^{\prime}}\cong\mathcal{O}_{X}\otimes\mathcal{V}|_{z}
. Now on
U^{\prime}
we have
\mathfrak{q}
-equivariant isomorphisms

(\mathcal{V}_{Y})|_{U^{\prime}}=(\mathcal{I}_{Y}\otimes_{\mathcal{O}_{X}}\mathcal{V})|_{U^{\prime}}\cong(\mathcal{I}_{Y}|_{U^{\prime}})\otimes_{\Bbbk}\mathcal{V}|_{z}.

In particular we have a
\mathfrak{q}
-module isomorphism

\Gamma(U^{\prime},\mathcal{V}_{Y})=\Gamma(U^{\prime},\mathcal{I}_{Y})\otimes_{\Bbbk}\mathcal{V}|_{z}.

Since
DS_{Q}\Gamma(V,\mathcal{I}_{Y})=0
for any affine open subvariety
V\subseteq U^{\prime}
, we have finished the proof of
4.5
, and thus also of
4.1
.
∎

5.
The supergroup
\widetilde{G_{u}}

In this section,
G
will denote an affine algebraic supergroup over
\Bbbk
. Then
\Bbbk[G]
is a Hopf superalgebra, and we write
\Delta
for its coproduct map. Recall that
G_{0}\subseteq G
will be an affine algebraic group.

By the Lie superalgebra
\mathfrak{g}:=\operatorname{Lie}G
, we mean the Lie algebra of left-invariant vector fields on
G
. We write
u
for an element of
\mathfrak{g}
, and we have a natural isomorphism
\mathfrak{g}\to T_{e}G
,
u\mapsto u_{e}
, given by evaluation of the vector field at the identity.

The supergroup
G
acts on itself by conjugation, inducing an action of
\mathfrak{g}
on
G
by vector fields. For
u\in\mathfrak{g}
, the vector field induced by this action is:

u_{ad}=u_{R}+u_{L}=(1\otimes u_{e}-u_{e}\otimes 1)\circ\Delta.

where
u_{R}=(1\otimes u_{e})\circ\Delta
and
u_{L}=-(u_{e}\otimes 1)\circ\Delta
are the infinitesimal right and left translations in the
u_{e}
direction. We refer to
u_{ad}
as the adjoint vector field of
u
.

This adjoint action of
\mathfrak{g}
on
\Bbbk[G]
respects the Hopf superalgebra structure, i.e. all morphisms coming from the Hopf superalgebra structure are morphisms of
\mathfrak{g}
-modules. Let
u\in\mathfrak{g}_{\overline{1}}
be such that
c=u^{2}
has a semisimple adjoint vector field. Then since
DS_{u}
is a tensor functor,
\Bbbk[G]_{u}:=DS_{u_{ad}}\Bbbk[G]
will be a supercommutative Hopf superalgebra.

Definition 5.1
.

We write
\widetilde{G_{u}}
for the algebraic supergroup with
\Bbbk[\widetilde{G_{u}}]=\Bbbk[G]_{u}
, and write
\widetilde{\mathfrak{g}_{u}}
for its Lie superalgebra.

If
V
is a
G
-module, then we have a morphism
V\to V\otimes\Bbbk[G]
, so when we take
DS_{u}
we obtain a morphism
V_{u}\to V_{u}\otimes\Bbbk[G]_{u}=V_{u}\otimes\Bbbk[\widetilde{G_{u}}]
, which gives
V_{u}:=DS_{u}V
the natural structure of a
\widetilde{G_{u}}
-module; in this way
\widetilde{G_{u}}
acts on the functor
DS_{u}
.

The supergroup
\widetilde{G_{u}}
was introduced in
[
She2
]
, and was computed in several cases when
G=GL(m|n),Q(n)
, or when
G
is ’split’, meaning that
\mathfrak{g}_{\overline{1}}
is an ideal of
\mathfrak{g}
(equivalently
G=G_{0}\ltimes\Pi V
, where
V
is a finite-dimensional
G_{0}
-module). However the computations heavily relied on being able to work in coordinates and perform explicit computations. We will see that with the localization theorem we can compute the supergroup
\widetilde{G_{u}}
for a wide range of
u
, and without appealing to coordinates.

In what follows, we write
C(u)
for the centralizer subgroup of
u
in
G
. We begin with the following:

Lemma 5.2
.

The vanishing set of the adjoint vector field
u_{ad}
is
C(u)_{0}\subseteq G_{0}
.

Proof.

Let
g\in G_{0}(\Bbbk)
; then by left/right translation invariance, we have
(u_{R})(g)=L_{g^{-1}}^{*}(u_{e})
, and
(u_{L})(g)=R_{g^{-1}}^{*}(-u_{e})
, where
L_{g^{-1}}
, resp.
R_{g^{-1}}
denote the left, resp. right translation by
g^{-1}
. Therefore
u_{ad}(g)=0
exactly if

L_{g^{-1}}^{*}(u_{e})+R_{g^{-1}}^{*}(-u_{e})=0\iff\operatorname{Ad}(g)(u_{e})=u_{e}.

∎

Proposition 5.3
.

Let
u\in\mathfrak{g}_{\overline{1}}
be such that
c=u^{2}
has that
c_{ad}
is semisimple. Let
C(c)\subseteq G
be the centralizer of
c
in
G
. Then the map
\Bbbk[G]\to\Bbbk[C(c)]
induces a natural isomorphism of Hopf superalgebras

DS_{u}\Bbbk[G]\cong DS_{u}\Bbbk[C(c)],

i.e. a natural isomorphism of supergroups
\widetilde{C(c)_{u}}\cong\widetilde{G_{u}}
.

Proof.

This follows from
4.4
, using that
C(c)=\operatorname{Spec}\Bbbk[G]/(\operatorname{Im}c)
.
∎

5.1.
Computation of
\widetilde{G_{u}}
for Kac-Moody supergroups,
Q(n)
, and
P(n)

We use Proposition
5.3
to compute
\widetilde{G_{u}}
for several important supergroups
G
. In what follows, by a Kac-Moody supergroup we will mean one of the supergroups
GL(m|n)
,
SOSp(m|2n)
,
D(2,1;\alpha)
,
AG(2,1)
, or
AB(3,1)
, where for
D(2,1;\alpha)
,
AG(2,1)
, and
AB(3,1)
we take the simply connected form of these supergroups. We write
Q(n)
for the queer supergroup, and
P(n)
for the periplectic supergroup, both of whom have
G_{0}\cong GL(n)
. We refer to
[
CW
]
for the Lie superalgebras of these supergroups listed here; however we note that in
[
CW
]
the Lie superalgebra of
AG(2,1)
is written as
G(3)
, while the Lie superalgebra of
AB(3,1)
is written as
F(3|1)
.

For the Kac-Moody supergroups and for
P(n)
, choose a maximal torus, and let
\mathfrak{s}\mathfrak{l}(1|1)^{k}\subseteq\mathfrak{g}
be a subalgebra corresponding to a choice of odd roots
\alpha_{1},\dots,\alpha_{k}
such that
-\alpha_{1},\dots,-\alpha_{k}
are also roots, and
\pm\alpha_{i}\pm\alpha_{j}
is not a root whenever
i\neq j
. Now choose
u\in\mathfrak{s}\mathfrak{l}(1|1)^{k}
generic in the sense that
c=u^{2}=(c_{1},\dots,c_{k})
is generic, meaning that
c_{i}\neq 0
for all
i
and so that we have, apart from the case of
D(2,1;\alpha)
,

C(c)=G^{\prime}\times GL(1|1)^{k},

where
G^{\prime}
is the group in the first factor of the below list. Such an element
u
will be said to have rank
k
.

For
D(2,1;\alpha)
, choose an element
u
of nonzero square in
\mathfrak{s}\mathfrak{l}(1|1)
, and let
c=u^{2}
. Then
C(c)
will be a supergroup with Lie superalgebra
\mathfrak{s}\mathfrak{l}(1|1)+\mathfrak{t}
, where
\mathfrak{t}
is a maximal torus; it is computed explicitly in the appendix.

For
G=Q(n)
, we choose a maximal torus; then its centralizer in
G
is given by
Q(1)^{n}
. Choose a factor subgroup
Q(1)^{k}
, and let
u\in\mathfrak{q}(1)_{\overline{1}}^{k}
be generic, in the sense that if
u=(u_{1},\dots,u_{k})
then
u_{i}\neq 0
for all
i
, and we have:

C(c)=Q(n-k)\times Q(1)^{k}.

Again, such an element
u
will be said to have rank
k
.

Before giving the following theorem we recall that
\mathbb{G}_{m}
denotes the one-dimensional complex algebraic torus, and
\mathbb{G}_{a}^{0|n}
denotes the purely odd abelian supergroup of dimension
(0|n)
.

Theorem 5.4
.

For
u\in\mathfrak{g}_{\overline{1}}
generic of rank
k
as described above, we have the following table describing
\widetilde{G_{u}}
:

\begin{array}[]{|c|c|}\hline\cr G&\widetilde{G_{u}}\\
\hline\cr GL(m|n)&GL(m-k|n-k)\times\mathbb{G}_{a}^{0|k}\\
\hline\cr SOSp(m|2n)&SOSp(m-2k|2n-2k)\times\mathbb{G}_{a}^{0|k}\\
\hline\cr D(2,1;\alpha),\alpha\in\mathbb{Q}&\mathbb{G}_{m}\times\mathbb{G}_{a}^{0|1}\\
\hline\cr D(2,1;\alpha),\alpha\notin\mathbb{Q}&\mathbb{G}_{a}^{0|1}\\
\hline\cr G(2,1)&SL(2)\times\mathbb{G}_{a}^{0|1}\\
\hline\cr AB(3,1)&SL(3)\times\mathbb{G}_{a}^{0|1}\\
\hline\cr Q(n)&Q(n-k)\times\mathbb{G}_{a}^{0|k}\\
\hline\cr P(n)&P(n-2k)\times\mathbb{G}_{a}^{0|k}\\
\hline\cr\end{array}

Proof.

For
D(2,1;\alpha)
, the explicit computation is done in the appendix. For the rest of the cases when
G\neq Q(n)
, we have by
5.3
that

DS_{u}\Bbbk[G]\cong DS_{u}\Bbbk[C(c)]=DS_{u}\Bbbk[G^{\prime}\times GL(1|1)^{k}]=\Bbbk[G^{\prime}]\otimes DS_{u}\Bbbk[GL(1|1)^{k}].

Now the computation follows from the fact that for any nonzero
v\in\mathfrak{g}\mathfrak{l}(1|1)_{\overline{1}}^{hom}
, we have
\widetilde{GL(1|1)_{v}}=\mathbb{G}_{a}^{0|1}
, and this was proven in Thm. 4.3,
[
She2
]
. We will also provide another proof of this statement in
5.10
.

When
G=Q(n)
we use the same ideas, only now we use that
\widetilde{Q(1)_{v}}=\mathbb{G}_{a}^{0|1}
for nonzero
v\in\mathfrak{q}(1)_{\overline{1}}
, which was proven in Thm. 4.3,
[
She2
]
and will also be shown in
5.10
.
∎

5.1.1.
Realization of the Lie superalgebra

Let
G
and
u
be as in the setup of Theorem
5.4
, except we exclude now the case
G=Q(n)
. We give an explicit realization of
\operatorname{Lie}\widetilde{G_{u}}
as coming from
\mathfrak{g}
, according to the natural construction introduced in Sec. 3 of
[
She2
]
.

In particular let us assume that
u\in\mathfrak{s}\mathfrak{l}(1|1)^{k}
takes the form

u=e_{\alpha_{1}}+\dots+e_{\alpha_{k}}+c_{1}e_{-\alpha_{1}}+\dots+c_{k}e_{-\alpha_{k}},

where
c_{1},\dots,c_{k}\in\Bbbk^{\times}
are generic and
e_{\pm\alpha_{i}}\in\mathfrak{g}_{\pm\alpha_{i}}
. Let
\mathfrak{h}^{\prime}=\sum\limits_{i}[\mathfrak{g}_{\alpha_{i}},\mathfrak{g}_{-\alpha_{i}}]\subseteq\mathfrak{g}_{\overline{0}}
; then
\mathfrak{h}^{\prime}\subseteq[u,\mathfrak{g}]
and is a toral subalgebra. By Lemma 3.1 of
[
She2
]
, if
V
is any representation on which
\mathfrak{h}^{\prime}
acts semisimply, we have a canonical isomorphism
DS_{u}V\cong DS_{u}V^{\mathfrak{h}^{\prime}}
. Therefore, if we define the Lie superalgebra

\mathfrak{g}(u,\mathfrak{h}^{\prime}):=DS_{u}\left(\mathfrak{c}(\mathfrak{h}^{\prime})/\mathfrak{h}^{\prime}\right),

where
\mathfrak{c}(\mathfrak{h}^{\prime})=\mathfrak{g}^{\mathfrak{h}^{\prime}}
is the centralizer of
\mathfrak{h}^{\prime}
in
\mathfrak{g}
, we will obtain a natural action of
\mathfrak{g}(u,\mathfrak{h}^{\prime})
on
DS_{u}V^{\mathfrak{h}^{\prime}}
. By
[
She2
]
, we have

\mathfrak{g}(u,\mathfrak{h}^{\prime})\cong\mathfrak{g}^{\prime}\times\Bbbk\langle e_{-\alpha_{1}},\dots,e_{-\alpha_{k}}\rangle,

where
\mathfrak{g}^{\prime}=\operatorname{Lie}G^{\prime}
, and
G^{\prime}
is the corresponding first factor in our table from Theorem
5.4
. Now
\mathfrak{g}(u,\mathfrak{h}^{\prime})
will have a natural map to
\operatorname{Lie}\widetilde{G_{u}}
, as follows.

We have a natural action of
G\times G
on
G
by left and right translation, and thus a map
\mathfrak{g}\times\mathfrak{g}\to\operatorname{Vect}(G)
. Recall that
u_{ad}
is the image of
(u,u)\in\mathfrak{g}\times\mathfrak{g}
under this map. We see then that
\mathfrak{h}^{\prime}\times\mathfrak{h}^{\prime}\subseteq[(u,u),\mathfrak{g}\times\mathfrak{g}]
, and thus we obtain an action of

DS_{(u,u)}\left(\mathfrak{c}(\mathfrak{h}^{\prime}\times\mathfrak{h}^{\prime})/(\mathfrak{h}^{\prime}\times\mathfrak{h}^{\prime})\right)=\mathfrak{g}(u,\mathfrak{h}^{\prime})\times\mathfrak{g}(u,\mathfrak{h}^{\prime})

on
\Bbbk[G]
. Further, given
(v,0)\in\mathfrak{g}(u,\mathfrak{h}^{\prime})\times\mathfrak{g}(u,\mathfrak{h}^{\prime})
, the action on
\Bbbk[G]_{u}
is given exactly by
(v\otimes 1)\circ\Delta
, where we are using that the coproduct on
\Bbbk[\widetilde{G_{u}}]=\Bbbk[G]_{u}
is induced by
\Delta
. From this formula it is clear that
\mathfrak{g}(u,\mathfrak{h}^{\prime})
will define right-invariant vector fields on
\widetilde{G_{u}}
, which gives our desired map
\mathfrak{g}(u,\mathfrak{h}^{\prime})\to\operatorname{Lie}\widetilde{G_{u}}
.

Theorem 5.5
.

For
G\neq D(2,1;\alpha)
, the map
\mathfrak{g}(u,\mathfrak{h}^{\prime})\to\operatorname{Lie}\widetilde{G_{u}}
is an isomorphism.

Proof.

We trace through the constructions of each space. First of all, by Proposition
5.3
, we have an isomorphism of Hopf superalgebras

DS_{u}\Bbbk[C(c)]\cong\Bbbk[\widetilde{G_{u}}].

Here
c
is generic in the sense that it satisfies
C(c)=C(\mathfrak{h}^{\prime})
. Clearly
\mathfrak{c}(\mathfrak{h}^{\prime})=\mathfrak{c}(c)
is the Lie superalgebra of
C(c)
, and now
C(c)=G^{\prime}\times GL(1|1)^{k}
, where
u\in\mathfrak{g}\mathfrak{l}(1|1)^{k}
is as written above. Thus it suffices to prove the statement in the case when
\mathfrak{g}=\mathfrak{g}\mathfrak{l}(1|1)
and
u=e_{\alpha}+ce_{-\alpha}
for
c\in\Bbbk
; this is done in 4.3.1 of
[
She2
]
.
∎

5.1.2.
Split supergroups

We present below a special case of the general result on split supergroups given in Sec. 4.6 of
[
She2
]
. By a split supergroup
G
we mean a supergroup for which
[\mathfrak{g}_{\overline{1}},\mathfrak{g}_{\overline{1}}]=0
.

Proposition 5.6
.

Suppose that
G
is a split supergroup, and
u\in\mathfrak{g}_{\overline{1}}
has a reductive stabilizer
C(u)_{0}
in
G_{0}
. Then
\widetilde{G_{u}}
is the supergroup with
(\widetilde{G_{u}})_{0}=C(u)_{0}
and Lie superalgebra
\mathfrak{c}(u)/[u,\mathfrak{g}_{\overline{0}}]
.

Proof.

Since
C(u)_{0}
is reductive and
[u,\mathfrak{g}_{\overline{0}}]
is a
C(u)_{0}
-module, we may find a complimentary
C(u)_{0}
-module
V
so that
\mathfrak{g}_{\overline{1}}=[u,\mathfrak{g}_{\overline{0}}]\oplus V
. In particular, we have the subgroup
K
of
G
with
K_{0}=C(u)_{0}
and Lie superalgebra
\mathfrak{k}=\mathfrak{c}(u)_{\overline{0}}\oplus V
. We apply now Theorem
4.1
with
Y=K
and
Z=Y_{0}
; notice that to check condition (3), we need only look at the identity
e\in K(\Bbbk)
since we may translate by
C(u)_{0}
. Now our map on the fiber of the conormal bundle at
e
is exactly dual to
[u,-]:\mathfrak{g}_{\overline{0}}/\mathfrak{c}(u)_{\overline{0}}\to[u,\mathfrak{g}_{\overline{0}}]
, which is clearly an isomorphism. Thus we have

DS_{u}\Bbbk[G]=DS_{u}\Bbbk[K]=\Bbbk[K].

∎

5.2.
Identifying the odd tangent bundle

We are able to compute several more supergroups
\widetilde{G_{u}}
, but in order to do so we need another tool. For an affine variety
X_{0}
we define the supervariety
\Pi\mathcal{T}_{X_{0}}
, the odd tangent bundle of
X_{0}
, to have functions given by
\Omega^{\bullet}_{X_{0}}
, the algebra of de Rham differentials on
X_{0}
, where
\Omega_{X_{0}}^{1}
consists of odd elements. The supervariety
\Pi\mathcal{T}_{X_{0}}
admits a canonical odd, everywhere vanishing vector field
d
given by the de Rham differential. Observe further that if
U_{0}
is an affine open subvariety of
X_{0}
, then the open subvariety it determines of
\Pi\mathcal{T}_{X_{0}}
is exactly
\Pi\mathcal{T}_{U_{0}}
.

Lemma 5.7
.

Let
X
be a smooth affine supervariety, and let
u
be an odd, everywhere vanishing vector field on
X
such that for all
x\in X(\Bbbk)
we have:

(1)

u(T_{x}^{*}X)_{\overline{1}}=0
;

(2)

the map
u:(T^{*}_{x}X)_{\overline{0}}\to(T_{x}^{*}X)_{\overline{1}}
is an isomorphism.

Then there exists an isomorphism
X\cong\Pi\mathcal{T}_{X_{0}}
such that
u
corresponds to the de Rham differential.

Proof.

We define an isomorphism
X\to\Pi\mathcal{T}_{X_{0}}
locally and prove that it glues.

First of all, because
X
is smooth and affine, it is split by
[
VMP
]
, so we can split
\Bbbk[X]\to\Bbbk[X_{0}]
, allowing us to identify (non-canonically)
\Bbbk[X_{0}]
as a subalgebra of
\Bbbk[X]
. Thus we do so, i.e. we choose once and for all such a subalgebra
\Bbbk[X_{0}]\subseteq\Bbbk[X]
.

Suppose that
U_{0}
is an open subset of
X_{0}
on which
X_{0}
has coordinates
x_{1},\dots,x_{n}
. Write
U
for the corresponding open subset of
X
. Let
\xi_{i}:=u(x_{i})\in\Bbbk[U]
; then by our assumption,
\xi_{1},\dots,\xi_{n}
define a full set of odd coordinates on
U
.

We now define an isomorphism
\varphi_{U}:U\to\Pi\mathcal{T}_{U_{0}}
on functions by mapping
\Bbbk[U_{0}]={\bigwedge}^{0}\Omega_{X}\subseteq\Bbbk[\Pi\mathcal{T}_{U_{0}}]
identically to our copy of
\Bbbk[U_{0}]
determined by our global splitting, and sending
dx_{i}
to
\xi_{i}
. It is clear that under this local isomorphism
u
corresponds to the de Rham differential
d
; in particular, on
U
we have
u=\sum\limits\xi_{i}\partial_{x_{i}}
.

Now we need to check these maps glue. However this follows from showing that the above isomorphism is independent of the coordinates we chose. Thus suppose on
U_{0}
we have another set of coordinates
y_{1},\dots,y_{n}
. Then if we set
\eta_{j}:=u(y_{j})
, we have

\eta_{j}=u(y_{j})=\sum\limits_{i}\xi_{i}\partial_{x_{i}}(y_{j})=\sum\limits_{i}\varphi_{U}^{*}(dx_{i}\partial_{x_{i}}(y_{j}))=\varphi_{U}^{*}(dy_{j}).

It follows that
\varphi_{U}
is indeed independent of the choice of coordinates; it is only dependent on our choice of splitting
\Bbbk[X_{0}]\subseteq\Bbbk[X]
, but this was global and fixed from the start.
∎

Remark 5.8
.

Note that
Lemma

5.7
was proven in
[
Vai
]
in greater generality, although they work in the smooth setting and use different tools.

With Lemma
5.7
in hand we now prove several fun results. For context, we recall that it was originally Hopf who observed in
[
Hop
]
that the de Rham cohomology,
H_{dR}^{\bullet}(G)
, of a Lie group
G
admits the structure of a graded commutative, cocommutative Hopf algebra, and therefore has spectrum given by an odd abelian supergroup.

Proposition 5.9
.

Let
G=Q(n)
, and let

u=\begin{bmatrix}0&I_{n}\\
I_{n}&0\end{bmatrix}

Then
\widetilde{G_{u}}\cong\mathbb{G}_{a}^{0|n}
.

Proof.

In this case the adjoint vector field of
u
vanishes everywhere, and we have
[u,\mathfrak{g}_{\overline{0}}]=0
and
[u,-]:\mathfrak{g}_{\overline{1}}\to\mathfrak{g}_{\overline{0}}
is an isomorphism. Thus Lemma
5.7
applies, and we obtain that

\Bbbk[Q(n)]_{u}=H_{dR}^{\bullet}(GL(n))\cong\mathbb{G}_{a}^{0|n}.

∎

Proposition 5.10
.

(1)

Let
G=GL(n|n)
, and let

u=\begin{bmatrix}0&I_{n}\\
\lambda I_{n}&0\end{bmatrix}

in block form, where
\lambda\in\Bbbk
. Then

\widetilde{GL(n|n)_{u}}\cong\mathbb{G}_{a}^{0|n}.

(2)

Let
G=P(n)
, and let

u=\begin{bmatrix}0&A\\
0&0\end{bmatrix}

in block form, where
A
is any invertible symmetric matrix. Then

\widetilde{P(n)_{u}}\cong\mu_{2}\times\mathbb{G}_{a}^{0|\lfloor n/2\rfloor}.

(3)

Let
G=P(2n)
, and let

u=\begin{bmatrix}0&0\\
B&0\end{bmatrix}

in block form, where
B
is any invertible skew-symmetric matrix. Then

\widetilde{P(2n)_{u}}\cong\mathbb{G}_{a}^{0|n}.

Proof.

The proofs of (1), (2), and (3) are identical. Namely in each case we have a decomposition of our Lie superalgebra
\mathfrak{g}=\mathfrak{g}_{-1}\oplus\mathfrak{g}_{0}\oplus\mathfrak{g}_{1}
where we can assume for (2) and (3) that
u\in\mathfrak{g}_{1}
, and for (1) we write
u=u_{+}+\lambda u_{-}
, where
u_{\pm}\in\mathfrak{g}_{\pm 1}
.

Let
K\subseteq G_{0}
be the stabilizer of
u
in
G_{0}
; then we have in each case that
[u,\mathfrak{g}_{-1}]=\mathfrak{k}:=\operatorname{Lie}K
. Further in all cases
u
defines an isomorphism:

[u,-]:(\mathfrak{g}_{0}\oplus\mathfrak{g}_{-1})/(\mathfrak{k}\oplus\mathfrak{g}_{-1})\to\mathfrak{g}_{1}.

Let
Y\subseteq G
be the subgroup with
Y_{0}=K
and with Lie superalgebra
\mathfrak{k}\oplus\mathfrak{g}_{-1}
. Then the hypotheses of Theorem
4.1
are satisfied with
Z=Y_{0}
, and so we learn that

DS_{u}\Bbbk[G]=DS_{u}\Bbbk[Y].

However on
Y
we have that
u
is everywhere vanishing and satisfies all the hypotheses of Lemma
5.7
, so that we obtain

DS_{u}\Bbbk[Y]\cong H_{dR}^{\bullet}(K).

Thus to finish the proof we see that for (1) we have
K=GL(n)
, for
(2)
we have
K=O(n)
, and (3) we have
K=Sp(2n)
.
∎

6.
Homogeneous superspaces

Recall that a supergroup
G
is called quasireductive if
G_{0}
is reductive. We will always assume in this section that
G
is quasireductive, and write
\operatorname{Rep}G
for the category of
G
-modules. We refer to
[
Ser2
]
for more on the theory of such supergroups. Write
\mathfrak{g}
for its Lie superalgebra, which will be quasireductive, and set

\mathfrak{g}_{\overline{1}}^{hom}:=\{u\in\mathfrak{g}_{\overline{1}}:u^{2}\text{ is semisimple}\}.

An element
u\in\mathfrak{g}_{\overline{1}}^{hom}
has that
u^{2}
acts semisimply on any
G
-module, and thus the functor
DS_{u}
is defined on
\operatorname{Rep}G
.

We will consider homogeneous spaces
G/K
for
K
a quasireductive subgroup of
G
; in this case
G/K
is a smooth affine supervariety. See
[
MaT
]
for more on the construction of this quotient and proof of affinity of
G/K
.

The Lie superalgebra
\mathfrak{g}
acts by vector fields on
G/K
via infinitesimal left translation; for an element
v\in\mathfrak{g}
we will, by abuse of notation, write
v
for the vector field it defines on
G/K
. We continue to write
Z(v)
for the vanishing set of
v
on
G/K
.

Lemma 6.1
.

For
v\in\mathfrak{g}
, and
g\in G(\Bbbk)
,
v
vanishes at the point
gK
on
G/K
if and only if
\operatorname{Ad}(g^{-1})(v)\in\mathfrak{k}
.

Proof.

Indeed, the stabilizer of
gK
is
gKg^{-1}
.
∎

For a homogeneous element
v\in\mathfrak{g}
, we write
N_{G}(v)
for the normalizer of
v
in
G
, and
C_{G}(v)
for the centralizer of
v
in
G
. Notice that for a homogeneous space
G/K
, if
v\in\mathfrak{k}
then
N_{G_{0}}(v)\cdot v\subseteq\mathfrak{k}
, and thus we have

N_{G_{0}}(v)K\subseteq Z(v).

Proposition 6.2
.

Let
G
be quasireductive and
K\subseteq G
a closed quasireductive subgroup. Let
u\in\mathfrak{k}\cap\mathfrak{g}_{\overline{1}}^{hom}
with
c=u^{2}
, and suppose that

Z(u)\subseteq N_{G_{0}}(c)K.

Then

DS_{u}\Bbbk[G/K]=DS_{u}\Bbbk[N_{G}(c)/(N_{G}(c)\cap K)].

Proof.

Let
Y=N_{G}(c)/(N_{G}(c)\cap K)
, which is a smooth, closed subvariety of
G/K
. Then because
\operatorname{ad}(c)
acts with non-zero eigenvalues on
\mathfrak{g}/\operatorname{Lie}N_{G}(c)
, it will act by an automorphism on
\mathcal{N}^{\vee}_{Y}|_{y}
for any
y\in Y(\Bbbk)
, since all such
y
have representatives in
N_{G}(c)
. By our assumption, we have that
Z(u)\subseteq Y_{0}
, so
u
will act by an automorphism on
\mathcal{N}^{\vee}_{Y}|_{z}
for any
z\in Z(u)
, and thus the hypotheses of Theorem
4.1
hold.
∎

Remark 6.3
.

We remind for future use that if
G_{0}
is a reductive algebraic group (in particular it is connected) and
c
is a semisimple element of its Lie algebra, then
C(c)
, the centralizer of
c
in
G_{0}
, is connected.

Before discussing symmetric spaces, we give an example in which condition (4) of Theorem
4.1
holds for any finite-dimensional equivariant vector bundle.

Example 6.4
.

Consider
G=GL(n|n)
,
K=GL(1|1)\times\cdots\times GL(1|1)
. Let
u\in\mathfrak{k}_{\overline{1}}
be generic, i.e. so that if
c:=u^{2}
then
N_{G}(c)=K
. Then we claim that
Z(u)=S_{n}\cdot K
, where
S_{n}\subseteq GL(n|n)_{0}\cong GL(n)\times GL(n)
consists of the diagonally embedded permutation matrices. Indeed, it is clear that this subgroup
S_{n}
stabilizes
K
, so that
S_{n}\cdot K\subseteq Z(u)
. On the other hand if
g\in Z(u)
, then necessarily
\operatorname{Ad}(g)(c)\in\mathfrak{k}_{\overline{0}}
, which implies, by regularity properties of
c
, that
g
lies in the Weyl group
S_{n}\times S_{n}
up to
K_{0}
. It is straightforward to check that only the diagonally embedded copy of
S_{n}
in
S_{n}\times S_{n}
will keep
u
inside
\mathfrak{k}_{\overline{1}}
, so this proves our claim.

Now, we see that the hypotheses of
6.2
do not apply, since
N_{G_{0}}(c)=K_{0}
in this case; however we can still compute
DS_{u}\Bbbk[G/K]
as follows.

Let
Y=Z(u)=S_{n}\cdot K
; i.e.
Y
is a purely even variety consisting of the
n!
points
\sigma K
for
\sigma\in S_{n}
(diagonally embedded in
GL(n)\times GL(n)\cong G_{0}
). Thus for
\sigma\in S_{n}
,
\mathcal{N}^{\vee}_{Y}|_{\sigma K}=T_{\sigma K}^{*}(G/K)
; as a
\mathfrak{k}
-module, this cotangent space is isomorphic to
(\mathfrak{g}/\mathfrak{k})^{\sigma}
, i.e. the
\sigma
twist of the
\mathfrak{k}
-module
\mathfrak{g}/\mathfrak{k}
. Thus it is clear that
c
acts by an automorphism on these fibers, and therefore
u
does as well. Therefore the hypotheses of
4.1
apply, giving:

DS_{u}\Bbbk[G/K]=\prod\limits_{\sigma\in S_{n}}\Bbbk.

Now let
V
be a
K
-module, and write
\mathcal{V}:=G\times_{K}V
, i.e. the
G
-equivariant vector bundle on
G/K
determined by
V
. We may write
\mathfrak{g}=\mathfrak{k}\oplus\mathfrak{m}
for a
\mathfrak{k}
-stable subspace
\mathfrak{m}
, and consider the open subscheme
W\subseteq\mathfrak{m}
defined, via the functor of points, as

W(R):=\{A\in\mathfrak{m}(R):I_{n|n}+A\text{ is invertible in }\operatorname{End}(R^{n|n})\},

where
R
is a supercommutative algebra. We see that
W
is an open,
\operatorname{Ad}(K)
-stable subset of
\mathfrak{m}
containing
0
. We have the map

\phi:W\to GL(n|n)/K,\ \ \ \ A\mapsto(I_{n|n}+A)K,

where the formula is defined on
R
-points, for a supercommutative algebra
R
. One may check that
\phi
is
K
-equivariant, injective on closed points, and has that
d_{0}\phi
is an isomorphism. Thus we may choose a
K
-stable open subvariety
U\subseteq W\subseteq\mathfrak{m}
, containing
0
, such that
\phi
restricts to an open embedding
U\hookrightarrow GL(n|n)/K
. Further, by Sumihiro’s Theorem and the fact that
K_{0}
is torus, we may assume that
U
is affine.

Observe now that if we write
\pi:G\to G/K
for the canonical projection, then we obtain a
K
-equivariant isomorphism
\pi^{-1}(\phi(U))\cong K\times U
, where
K
acts on the right on
\pi^{-1}(U)
and
K
, and by the adjoint action on
U
.

It follows that we have
\Gamma(U,\mathcal{V})\cong(\Bbbk[K]\otimes\Bbbk[U]\otimes V)^{K}
, which in particular contains the
K
-submodule
(\Bbbk[K]\otimes\Bbbk\langle 1\rangle\otimes V)^{K}\cong V
. This
K
-submodule of
\Gamma(U,\mathcal{V})
defines a local,
K
-equivariant trivialization of
\mathcal{V}
. Since
G/K
is a homogeneous space, we may translate the above trivialization to obtain similar local trivializations around each point
\sigma K
for
\sigma\in S_{n}
. It follows that the hypotheses of
4.1
hold, and so we obtain that

DS_{u}\Gamma(G/K,\mathcal{V})\cong\bigoplus\limits_{\sigma\in S_{n}}DS_{u}V^{\sigma^{-1}}.

Before moving to more general computations on supersymmetric spaces, we give a specific example which is of interest on its own for the elegance of its answer.

Example 6.5

(Supersphere)
.

Let
\mathbb{S}^{m-1|2n}=OSp(m|2n)/OSp(m-1|2n)=G/K
denote the supersphere. Explicitly,
G/K
is the closed subvariety of
\Bbbk^{m|2n}
cut out by the equation
q=1
, where
q
is a nondegenerate, supersymmetric quadratic form preserved by
OSp(m|2n)
on
\Bbbk^{m|2n}
. In particular if
m=1
,
(G/K)_{0}=\mathbb{S}^{0}
consists of two points.

Note that if
m>1
then we have
\mathbb{S}^{m-1|2n}=SOSp(m|2n)/SOSp(m-1|2n)
. Let us assume that
m\geq 3
and
n\geq 1
.

Choose a subgroup
S\cong SL(1|1)\subseteq K
corresponding to an odd isotropic root of
K
(with respect to some maximal torus), and let
u\in\operatorname{Lie}S\cong\mathfrak{s}\mathfrak{l}(1|1)_{\overline{1}}
be such that
c=u^{2}\neq 0
. Then there exists an odd isotropic root (with respect to some maximal torus) of
G
with corresponding subgroup
S^{\prime}\cong SL(1|1)
such that
u
is conjugate to an element in
\operatorname{Lie}S^{\prime}
in
G
(note:
S
arises from an isotropic root of
K
, not
G
, whereas
S^{\prime}
arises from an isotropic root of
G
, not
K
). We show in
Section

6.1
that

Z(u)\subseteq N_{G}(c)_{0}K,

so we obtain from
6.2
that

DS_{u}\Bbbk[\mathbb{S}^{m-1|2n}]=DS_{u}\Bbbk[N_{G}(c)/(N_{G}(c)\cap K)].

Now our copy of
SL(1|1)
is normalized by
N_{G}(c)
, and so since it is contained in
K
it will act trivially on
N_{G}(c)/N_{G}(c)\cap K
, meaning that in particular
u
does too, so that

DS_{u}\Bbbk[\mathbb{S}^{m-1|2n}]=\Bbbk[N_{G}(c)/(N_{G}(c)\cap K)].

For
g\in N_{G}(c)(\Bbbk)
, we have
\operatorname{Ad}(g)c=\pm c
; if
m>3
then
K(\Bbbk)
contains an element
g
(coming from the Weyl group of
K
) such that
\operatorname{Ad}(g)c=-c
, and we have in this case that

N_{G}(c)/(N_{G}(c)\cap K)=C_{G}(c)/(C_{G}(c)\cap K).

Here
C(c)=SOSp(m-2|2n-2)\times GL(1|1)
and
C(c)\cap K=SOSp(m-3|2n-2)\times GL(1|1)
, so
C_{G}(c)/(C_{G}(c)\cap K)=\mathbb{S}^{m-3|2n-2}
. If
m=3
, then the even part of
N_{G}(c)/(N_{G}(c)\cap K)
has two points, with one point being
C_{G}(c)/(C_{G}(c)\cap K)
. From this we obtain that
N_{G}(c)/(N_{G}(c)\cap K)=OSp(1|2n-2)/Sp(2n)=\mathbb{S}^{m-3|2n-2}
. Thus in general we have the beautiful formula:

DS_{u}\Bbbk[\mathbb{S}^{m-1|2n}]=\Bbbk[\mathbb{S}^{m-3|2n-2}].

6.1.
Supersymmetric spaces

We apply Theorem
4.1
to several spaces of the form
G/K
where
K
is a symmetric subgroup of
G
. By definition, a symmetric subgroup
K
is one such that there exists an involution
\theta
of
G
with
(G^{\theta})^{\circ}\subseteq K\subseteq G^{\theta}
, where
G^{\theta}
is the fixed points of
\theta
, and
(G^{\theta})^{\circ}
is the connected component of the identity.

Note that the diagonally embedded subgroup
G\subseteq G\times G
is a symmetric subgroup, and we have
G\cong(G\times G)/G
, so in fact our study of
\widetilde{G_{u}}
in Section 5 is a special case of these examples.

Let
G
be one of
GL(m|n)
,
SOSp(m|2n)
,
P(n)
, or the simply connected supergroup of an exceptional basic simple Lie superalgebra. Then we will consider symmetric subgroups
K
of
G
with an element
u\in\mathfrak{k}_{\overline{1}}^{hom}
which is generic of minimal rank, and satisfies the hypothesis of Proposition
6.2
, namely that

Z(u)\subseteq N_{G}(c)_{0}K.\ \ \ \ \ (*)

This condition will hold exactly in the cases for which
\mathfrak{k}
admits at most one factor subalgebra
\mathfrak{k}^{\prime}
which has that
(\mathfrak{k}^{\prime}_{\overline{1}})^{hom}\neq 0
. One can easily go through the classification of supersymmetric pairs, found in
[
Ser3
]
, to check which ones these are.

Let us explain how we compute in each case. First, choose a maximal torus
\mathfrak{t}^{\prime}\subseteq\mathfrak{k}_{\overline{0}}
and extend it to a maximal torus
\mathfrak{t}
of
\mathfrak{g}_{\overline{0}}
. For the cases when
\mathfrak{k}\neq\mathfrak{q}(n)
, choose a root subalgebra
\mathfrak{s}\mathfrak{l}(1|1)\subseteq\mathfrak{k}
, and let
u\in\mathfrak{s}\mathfrak{l}(1|1)_{\overline{1}}
such that
c=u^{2}\neq 0
. First suppose that we have

\operatorname{Lie}C_{G}(c)\cong\mathfrak{g}\mathfrak{l}(1|1)\times\mathfrak{g}_{u}.

The symmetric pairs which will satisfy this along with (*) are:

(\mathfrak{g}\mathfrak{l}(m|n),\mathfrak{g}\mathfrak{l}(m|n-r)\times\mathfrak{g}\mathfrak{l}(r)),\ \ (\mathfrak{o}\mathfrak{s}\mathfrak{p}(m|2n),\mathfrak{o}\mathfrak{s}\mathfrak{p}(m|2n-2r)\times\mathfrak{s}\mathfrak{p}(2r)),

(\mathfrak{o}\mathfrak{s}\mathfrak{p}(m|2n),\mathfrak{o}\mathfrak{s}\mathfrak{p}(m-1|2n-2r)\times\mathfrak{o}\mathfrak{s}\mathfrak{p}(1|2r)),\ \ (\mathfrak{o}\mathfrak{s}\mathfrak{p}(m|2n),\mathfrak{o}\mathfrak{s}\mathfrak{p}(m-r|2n)\times\mathfrak{o}(r)),

(\mathfrak{o}\mathfrak{s}\mathfrak{p}(2m|2n),\mathfrak{g}\mathfrak{l}(m|n)),\ \ (\mathfrak{p}(n),\mathfrak{g}\mathfrak{l}(r|n-r)),

(\mathfrak{d}(1|2;\alpha),\mathfrak{o}\mathfrak{s}\mathfrak{p}(2|2)\times\mathfrak{s}\mathfrak{o}(2)),\ \ (\mathfrak{a}\mathfrak{b}(1|3),\mathfrak{g}\mathfrak{o}\mathfrak{s}\mathfrak{p}(2|4)),\ \ (\mathfrak{a}\mathfrak{b}(1,3),\mathfrak{s}\mathfrak{l}(1|4)),

(\mathfrak{a}\mathfrak{b}(1|3),\mathfrak{d}(1|2,2)\times\mathfrak{s}\mathfrak{l}(2)),\ \ (\mathfrak{g}(1|2),\mathfrak{d}(1|2;3)),\ \ (\mathfrak{g}(1|2),\mathfrak{o}\mathfrak{s}\mathfrak{p}(3|2)\times\mathfrak{s}\mathfrak{l}(2)).

In all of the above cases, an element
c
with this centralizer (up to isomorphism) is unique in
\mathfrak{t}^{\prime}
up to: adding a central element in
\mathfrak{g}
, acting by the Weyl group of
\mathfrak{t}^{\prime}
in
K
, and scaling. Further, either we have
N_{G}(c)=C_{G}(c)
or
N_{G}(c)/C_{G}(c)\cong\mu_{2}
, and the action of
\mu_{2}
on
c
is
c\mapsto-c
.

We now show that (*) holds for the pairs listed above. Suppose that in one of the listed cases we have
u
vanishes at
gK
; then in particular
c
vanishes here, and we have
\operatorname{Ad}(g^{-1})(c)\in\mathfrak{k}
. Choose
k\in K_{0}
such that
c^{\prime}=\operatorname{Ad}(gk)^{-1}(c)\in\mathfrak{t}^{\prime}
. Clearly we have
\operatorname{Lie}C(c^{\prime})\cong\mathfrak{g}\mathfrak{l}(1|1)\times\mathfrak{g}_{u}
. Now to obtain
c
from
c^{\prime}
, the only case where we could add a central element in
\mathfrak{g}
is
\mathfrak{g}=\mathfrak{g}\mathfrak{l}(m|n)
, and by rank considerations we see that this couldn’t happen. Thus there exists
w\in K_{0}
, corresponding to a lift of a Weyl group element, such that
w^{-1}c^{\prime}
is a scalar multiple of
c
. Therefore
gkw\in N_{G}(c)_{0}
, showing that the condition (*) indeed holds. Therefore we obtain by
6.2
for the above cases that

DS_{u}\Bbbk[G/K]=DS_{u}\Bbbk[N_{G}(c)/(N_{G}(c)\cap K)].

Further, in all of the above cases we have that our copy of
SL(1|1)
lies in
K
and is normalized by
N_{G}(c)
, so that
u
will in fact act trivially on
N_{G}(c)/N_{G}(c)\cap K
; it follows that in the above cases we have:

DS_{u}\Bbbk[G/K]=\Bbbk[N_{G}(c)/(N_{G}(c)\cap K)],

and from this we obtain our explicit results given in the table below.

We now consider three other cases:

(\mathfrak{g}\mathfrak{l}(m|2n),\mathfrak{o}\mathfrak{s}\mathfrak{p}(m|2n)),\ \ \ (\mathfrak{g}\mathfrak{l}(n|n),\mathfrak{p}(n)),\ \ \ (\mathfrak{g}\mathfrak{l}(n|n),\mathfrak{q}(n)).

In the first two cases, we again choose a root subalgebra
\mathfrak{s}\mathfrak{l}(1|1)\subseteq\mathfrak{k}
, and let
u\in\mathfrak{s}\mathfrak{l}(1|1)_{\overline{1}}
with
c=u^{2}\neq 0
. Then we have that

C_{G}(c)=GL(1|1)\times GL(1|1)\times G^{\prime}.

where
G^{\prime}=GL(m-2|2n-2)
for the first pair, and
G^{\prime}=GL(n-2|n-2)
for the second pair. For
(\mathfrak{g}\mathfrak{l}(n|n),\mathfrak{q}(n))
we choose a copy of
\mathfrak{q}(1)\subseteq\mathfrak{q}(n)
which is a factor subalgebra of a Cartan subalgebra, and let
u\in\mathfrak{q}(1)_{\overline{1}}
such that
c=u^{2}\neq 0
. Then we have that

C_{G}(u)=GL(1|1)\times GL(n-1|n-1).

For the above three cases, any element
c\in\mathfrak{t}^{\prime}
with such a centralizer is unique up to action of the Weyl group of
K
on
\mathfrak{t}^{\prime}
, scaling, and multiples of the center. Using the same argument as above, we find that the vanishing set of
u
is contained in
N_{G_{0}}(c)K
, and thus

DS_{u}\Bbbk[G/K]=DS_{u}\Bbbk[N_{G}(c)/(N_{G}(c)\cap K)].

Now in all the three above cases we in fact have
N_{G}(c)/(N_{G}(c)\cap K)=C_{G}(c)/(C_{G}(c)\cap K)
; we thus obtain the following:

\displaystyle DS_{u}\Bbbk[GL(m|2n)/OSp(m|2n)]

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\Bbbk[GL(m-2|2n-2)/OSp(m-2|2n-2)]

\displaystyle\otimes

\displaystyle DS_{u}\Bbbk[GL(1|1)^{2}/GL(1|1)],

\displaystyle DS_{u}\Bbbk[GL(n|n)/P(n)]

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\Bbbk[GL(n-2|n-2)/P(n-2)]

\displaystyle\otimes

\displaystyle DS_{u}\Bbbk[GL(1|1)^{2}/GL(1|1)],

and

DS_{u}\Bbbk[GL(n|n)/Q(n)]=\Bbbk[GL(n-1|n-1)/Q(n-1)]\otimes DS_{u}\Bbbk[GL(1|1)/Q(1)].

Now one may compute explicitly that
DS_{u}\Bbbk[GL(1|1)^{2}/GL(1|1)]\cong\mathbb{A}^{0|1}
, where
\mathbb{A}^{0|1}=\operatorname{Spec}\Bbbk[\xi]
for some odd variable
\xi
, and
DS_{u}\Bbbk[GL(1|1)/Q(1)]\cong\Bbbk[x]/(x^{2}=1)=\Bbbk[\mathbb{S}^{0}]
.

Remark 6.7

6.2.
Quotients by Levi subgroups of
GL(n|n)
,
P(n)
, and
Q(n)

Theorem 6.9
.

Let
u=\begin{bmatrix}0&I_{n}\\
\lambda I_{n}&0\end{bmatrix}\in\mathfrak{g}\mathfrak{l}(n|n)
where
\lambda\in\Bbbk
, and consider the subgroup

K=GL(r_{1}|r_{1})\times\cdots\times GL(r_{k}|r_{k}),

where
\sum\limits_{i}r_{i}=n
. Then we have an isomorphism

\displaystyle DS_{u}[GL(n|n)/GL(r_{1}|r_{1})\times\cdots\times GL(r_{k}|r_{k})]

\displaystyle\cong

\displaystyle H_{dR}^{\bullet}(GL(n)/GL(r_{1})\times\cdots\times GL(r_{k})),

where
H_{dR}^{\bullet}(-)
denotes the de Rham cohomology.

Proof.

Let
H\subseteq GL(n|n)
be the subgroup with
\mathfrak{h}=\mathfrak{g}_{-1}\oplus\mathfrak{g}_{0}
and
H_{0}\cong GL(n)
embedded in
GL(n|n)
as the matrices of the form

\begin{bmatrix}A&0\\
0&A\end{bmatrix}

for
A\in GL(n)
. Let
Y=H/(K\cap H)
; then we claim that the vanishing set of
u
is exactly
Y_{0}
. Indeed, suppose that
u
vanishes at
g^{-1}K
, i.e.
\operatorname{Ad}(g)(u)\in\mathfrak{k}
, and write
g=(g_{1},g_{2})\in GL(n)\times GL(n)
. Then we can write
g=(k,I_{n})(g_{2},g_{2})
where
k=g_{1}g_{2}^{-1}
, and now
(g_{2},g_{2})\in H_{0}
. Observe that

\operatorname{Ad}(k,I_{n})(u)=\begin{bmatrix}0&k\\
\lambda k^{-1}&0\end{bmatrix}.

Thus we must have
k\in GL(r_{1})\times\cdots\times GL(r_{k})
so that
(k,I_{n})\in K
. Therefore
g^{-1}K=h^{-1}K
, where
h=(g_{2},g_{2})
. Now from the
4.1
, we obtain that

DS_{u}\Bbbk[GL(n|n)/K]=DS_{u}\Bbbk[Y].

We finish by observing that
u
defines an everywhere vanishing vector field on
Y
, and satisfies the hypotheses of
5.7
. Since
Y_{0}=GL(n)/GL(r_{1})\times\cdots\times GL(r_{k})
, this completes the proof.
∎

Theorem 6.10
.

Let
G=P(n)
,
K=P(r_{1})\times\cdots\times P(r_{k})
, where
\sum\limits_{i}r_{i}=n
. Let

u=\begin{bmatrix}0&I_{n}\\
0&0\end{bmatrix}\in\mathfrak{p}(n)_{\overline{1}};

if
n
is even, then further set

v=\begin{bmatrix}0&0\\
A&0\end{bmatrix}\in\mathfrak{p}(n)_{\overline{1}},

where
A
is an invertible skew-symmetric matrix. Then

DS_{u}\Bbbk[G/K]\cong H^{\bullet}_{dR}(O(n)/O(r_{1})\times\cdots\times O(r_{k})),

and when
n,r_{1},\dots,r_{k}
are all even,

DS_{v}\Bbbk[G/K]\cong H^{\bullet}_{dR}(Sp(n)/Sp(r_{1})\times\cdots\times Sp(r_{k})).

Proof.

In the first case, consider the subgroup
H\subseteq G
with
H_{0}=O(n)\subseteq GL(n)
, and
\mathfrak{h}_{\overline{1}}=\mathfrak{g}_{-1}
. Set
Y=H/H\cap K\subseteq G/K
. In the second case, consider the subgroup
H^{\prime}\subseteq G
with
H^{\prime}_{0}=Sp(\Bbbk^{n},A)\subseteq GL(n)
, the matrices preserving
A
, and
\mathfrak{h}_{\overline{1}}=\mathfrak{g}_{1}
. Then set
Y=H/H\cap K\subseteq G/K
. Now using the equivalence of all symmetric, resp. alternating nondegenerate bilinear forms on
\Bbbk^{n}
, we can prove that the vanishing set of
u
, resp.
v
in
G/K
is given by
Y_{0}
. From here, we use that
[u,-]:\mathfrak{h}_{\overline{1}}\to\mathfrak{h}_{\overline{0}}
, resp.
[v,-]:\mathfrak{h}^{\prime}_{\overline{1}}\to\mathfrak{h}^{\prime}_{\overline{0}}
are isomorphisms, and
Lemma

5.7
.
∎

Theorem 6.11
.

Consider the element in
\mathfrak{q}(n)
given by:

u=\begin{bmatrix}0&I_{n}\\
I_{n}&0\end{bmatrix}.

Let
r_{1},\dots,r_{k}
be positive integers with
\sum\limits r_{i}=n
. Then we have an isomorphism of algebras:

DS_{u}\Bbbk[Q(n)/Q(r_{1})\times\dots\times Q(r_{k})]\cong H_{dR}^{\bullet}(GL(n)/GL(r_{1})\times\dots\times GL(r_{k})).

5.7
; clearly

7.
Appendix: A root subgroup of
D(2,1;\alpha)

Consider the subgroup
H
of
D(2,1;\alpha)
with Lie superalgebra given by
\mathfrak{g}_{\beta}\oplus\mathfrak{g}_{-\beta}\oplus\mathfrak{t}
, where
\mathfrak{t}
is a maximal torus and
\beta
is an isotropic odd root. Its Hopf superalgebra structure is given as follows: it has functions
\Bbbk[x_{1}^{\pm 1},x_{2}^{\pm 1},x_{3}^{\pm 1},\xi,\eta]
, where the coproduct
\Delta
is given by:

\Delta(x_{i})=x_{i}\otimes x_{i}+c_{i}x_{i}\eta\otimes x_{i}\xi,\ \ \ \Delta(\xi)=\xi\otimes 1+x_{1}x_{2}x_{3}\otimes\xi,\ \ \ \Delta(\eta)=\eta\otimes x_{1}x_{2}x_{3}+1\otimes\eta.

where
c_{1}+c_{2}+c_{3}=0
, and explicitly we have
c_{1}=\alpha
,
c_{2}=1
,
c_{3}=-1-\alpha
.

Let
x\in\mathfrak{g}_{\beta}
correspond to the derivation
\partial_{\xi}
at the identity, and
y\in\mathfrak{g}_{-\beta}
correspond to the derivation
\partial_{\eta}
at the identity. Then we obtain, by direct computation, that the adjoint vector field of
u=x+y
is given by:

(1-x_{1}x_{2}x_{3})(\partial_{\eta}-\partial_{\xi})-(\xi+\eta)\sum\limits_{i}c_{i}x_{i}\partial_{x_{i}}.

We can compute its cohomology on
\Bbbk[H]
as follows: set
\zeta=\xi+\eta
and
\gamma=(\eta-\xi)/2
. Then we may rewrite the above as

D_{1}+D_{2}=(1-x_{1}x_{2}x_{3})\partial_{\gamma}-\zeta\sum\limits_{i}c_{i}x_{i}\partial_{x_{i}}.

where

D_{1}:=(1-x_{1}x_{2}x_{3})\partial_{\gamma},\ \ \ \ D_{2}:=-\zeta\sum\limits_{i}c_{i}x_{i}\partial_{x_{i}}.

It is a straightforward computation that
[D_{1},D_{2}]=0,
so we can view this as computing the cohomology of the total complex of the following bicomplex:

\textstyle{A_{0}\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces}
\scriptstyle{D_{2}}
\textstyle{A_{0}\langle\zeta\rangle}
\textstyle{A_{0}\langle\gamma\rangle\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces}
\scriptstyle{D_{1}}
\scriptstyle{D_{2}}
\textstyle{A_{0}\langle\zeta\gamma\rangle\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces}
\scriptstyle{D_{1}}

where
A_{0}=\Bbbk[x_{1}^{\pm 1},x_{2}^{\pm 1},x_{3}^{\pm 1}]
. Thus we may compute the cohomology via the spectral sequence by the filtration along the columns. Cohomology with respect to
D_{1}
will give a Koszul complex, and taking its cohomology we obtain:

\Bbbk[x_{1}^{\pm 1},x_{2}^{\pm 1},x_{3}^{\pm 1},\zeta]/(1-x_{1}x_{2}x_{3}).

The spectral sequence collapses on the second page, and since it is concentrated along a single row, it will in fact exactly compute the cohomology for us.

Thus it remains to compute the cohomology of
D_{2}
; from the relation, we can forget about
x_{3}
, and so we obtain
\Bbbk[x_{1}^{\pm 1},x_{2}^{\pm 1},\zeta]
with operator
D_{2}=\zeta(c_{1}x_{1}\partial_{x_{2}}+c_{2}x_{2}\partial_{x_{2}})
. Recall that
c_{1}=\alpha
and
c_{2}=1
. Thus if
\alpha\notin\mathbb{Q}
, we obtain that the cohomology is
\Bbbk[\zeta]
, i.e. we have
\widetilde{H_{u}}\cong\mathbb{G}^{0|1}
.

On the other hand, suppose that
\alpha=m/n\in\mathbb{Q}
in reduced form. Then the cohomology is given by
\Bbbk[z^{\pm 1},\zeta]
, where
z=x_{1}^{n}x_{2}^{-m}
. One can check that the Hopf algebra structure gives
\widetilde{H_{u}}\cong\mathbb{G}_{m}\times\mathbb{G}^{0|1}
.
