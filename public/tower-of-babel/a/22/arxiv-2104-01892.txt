Universal rigidity on the line, point order
Bryan Chen, Robert Connelly, Anthony Nixon, Louis Theran

Abstract
We show that universal rigidity of a generic bar-joint framework ( G , 𝐩 ) (G,{\bf p}) in R 1 \mdmathbb{R}^{1} depends on more than the ordering of the vertices. In particular, we construct examples of 1 1 -dimensional generic frameworks with the same graph and ordering of the vertices, such that one is universally rigid and one is not. This answers, in the negative, a question of Jordán and Nguyen. Underlying our examples are insights about how universal rigidity behaves under projections. Using these ideas, we also give a simple proof that universal rigidity is invariant under affine transformations.



1.  Introduction

Throughout
G=(V,E)
will be a finite, simple graph with
n
vertices and
m
edges;
we usually assume that
V=\{1,2,\ldots,n\}
for convenience.
We will consider the rigidity and flexibility properties of realisations of graphs in Euclidean spaces.

Frameworks

A
framework
is a pair
(G,{\bf p})
consisting of a
graph
G
with
n
vertices,
m
edges and a
configuration

{\bf p}=(p(1),\ldots,p(n))
of
n
points in
\mdmathbb{R}^{d}
.
A
d
-dimensional framework is in
general position
if no
d+1
points are affinely dependent. More strongly the framework is
generic
if the set of coordinates of
{\bf p}
form an algebraically independent set over
\mdmathbb{Q}
.

Two frameworks
(G,{\bf p})
and
(G,{\bf q})
are said to be
equivalent
if they have the same edge lengths:

\|p_{i}-p_{j}\|=\|q_{i}-q_{j}\|\qquad\text{(for all edges $ij\in E$).}

More strongly, frameworks

(G,{\bf p})
and
(G,{\bf q})
are
congruent
if all pairwise distances are the same:

\|p_{i}-p_{j}\|=\|q_{i}-q_{j}\|\qquad\text{(for all vertices $i,j\in V$).}

This is equivalent to requiring that
{\bf p}
and
{\bf q}
be related by a Euclidean isometry.

In what follows, we will be interested in frameworks that are
not affinely spanning. We will say that

(G,{\bf p})
is a
framework in dimension
d
(or in
\mdmathbb{R}^{d}
) if the affine span of
{\bf p}
is
d
-dimensional.
By picking an appropriate basis, of any
\mdmathbb{R}^{D}
with
D\geq d
, we can identify the affine span of
{\bf p}
with
\mdmathbb{R}^{d}
.

Flavors of rigidity

A framework
(G,{\bf p})
in
\mdmathbb{R}^{d}
is:
rigid
if every equivalent framework in a neighbourhood of
{\bf p}
in
\mdmathbb{R}^{d}
is congruent to
(G,{\bf p})
;
globally rigid
if every equivalent framework
(G,{\bf q})
in
\mdmathbb{R}^{d}
is congruent to
(G,{\bf p})
; and
universally rigid
if every equivalent framework
(G,{\bf q})
in
\mdmathbb{R}^{D}
, for any
D\geq d
, is congruent to
(G,{\bf p})
.

Given a framework, it is a computationally challenging problem to determine if it is rigid, globally rigid or universally rigid.
The situation improves for generic frameworks.
In the case of rigidity one may linearise the problem and consider infinitesimal rigidity
and the rigidity matrix (see
[
11
]
for detailed background on these concepts).
Asimow and Roth
[
1
]
used this to show that rigidity is a
generic property
.
That is, either every generic framework for a given graph is rigid or
every generic framework is flexible. It immediately follows that, generically, rigidity is a
property of the underlying graph.

Equilibrium stresses

To understand global rigidity, Connelly
[
2
]
developed the stress matrix which we now describe.
By ordering the
m
edges of
G
, we can identify
\mdmathbb{R}^{E}
with
\mdmathbb{R}^{m}
.
An
equilibrium stress
is a vector
\omega\in\mdmathbb{R}^{m}
such that:

\sum_{j\neq i}\omega_{ij}(p_{i}-p_{j})=0

(1)

for all
1\leq i\leq n
, where
\omega_{ij}
is taken to be equal to zero if
\{i,j\}\notin E
. Equilibrium stresses are
vectors in the cokernel of the rigidity matrix (see, e.g.,
[
10
]
).
Given an equilibrium stress
\omega
for a framework
(G,{\bf p})
we define the
stress matrix

\Omega(\omega)
to be the
n\times n
symmetric
matrix with off-diagonal entries
-\omega_{ij}
and diagonal entries
\sum_{j}\omega_{ij}
.

As a linear map,
the stress matrix evaluates the l.h.s. of (
1
) for
1
-dimensional configurations.
The rank of an equilibrium stress is the rank of its stress matrix.
The all ones vector is in the kernel of the stress matrix, since certainly (
1
) is satisfied when
all the points are the same. If
{\bf q}
is a
d
-dimensional configuration,
we say that
{\bf q}
satisfies
\omega
if each of its
d
coordinate projections are in the kernel of
\Omega(\omega)
.
Hence, the rank of any equilibrium stress for a
d
-dimensional framework is at most
n-d-1
(but it might be less).

Connelly showed
[
3
]
that every generic framework
(G,{\bf p})
in
\mdmathbb{R}^{d}
with a stress matrix of rank
n-d-1
is globally rigid. Subsequently, Gortler, Healy and Thurston
[
8
]
showed that the stress matrix being full rank is also a necessary condition for generic global rigidity for all graphs on at least
d+2
vertices. (A graph on at most
d+1
generic vertices has no non-zero equilibrium stress and is globally rigid if and only if it is complete.) It follows that global rigidity is also a generic property
and hence generic global rigidity depends only on the graph.

Universal rigidity and genericity

The situation for universal rigidity is different. Even in
\mdmathbb{R}^{1}
it
is very easy to notice graphs which are universally rigid at some generic
configurations and not universally rigid at other generic configurations.
Perhaps the easiest example is the cycle
C_{n}
,
n\geq 4
.
Here one may make a universally rigid framework of
C_{4}
, on vertices
p_{1},p_{2},p_{3},p_{4}
,
in
\mdmathbb{R}^{1}
by choosing
{\bf p}
such that
\|p_{1}-p_{2}\|=\|p_{2}-p_{3}\|+\|p_{3}-p_{4}\|+\|p_{4}-p_{1}\|
.
If instead the positions of
p_{1},\dots,p_{4}
alternate along the line then it is easy to see that
the framework will be flexible in
\mdmathbb{R}^{2}
.
A
stretched cycle
is a framework in
\mdmathbb{R}^{1}
, where the underlying graph is a
cycle
p_{1},p_{2},\dots,p_{n}
, and
p_{1}<p_{2}<\dots p_{n}
.
The smaller edges are “stretched" along the longer edge
\{p_{1},p_{n}\}
.
Figure
1
shows a stretched
4
-cycle.

Figure 1:
The figure on the left is a
stretched cycle
, while the one the right is not stretched. Both are cycles in the line. Part of the edges are lifted up or down in order to better see the graph.

Results and organization

Jordán and Nguyen
[
9
, Question 2.5]
posed the following question:

Does the universal rigidity of a general position framework
(G,{\bf p})
in
\mdmathbb{R}^{1}
depend
only on the ordering of vertices on the line (and not on the coordinates)?

The purpose of this short article is to answer their question in the negative. In fact we show the stronger fact that the universal rigidity and the super stability of a
generic

framework on the line does not depend solely on the ordering of the vertices.
We do this by explicitly constructing generic frameworks of the triangular prism graph on the line
that are universally rigid and not universally rigid despite having the same vertex ordering.
Our examples can be extended to an infinite family of graphs for which the ordering does not suffice.

On the other hand Jordán and Nguyen
[
9
, Theorem 2.2]
showed that the ordering is sufficient for
any bipartite graph. Along the way, we show that universal rigidity is
preserved under orthogonal projections. We can use this result to give a simple
proof that universal rigidity is affinely invariant.

2.  Universal rigidity

Let
(G,{\bf p})
be a framework in
\mdmathbb{R}^{d}
.
We say that the edge directions of
(G,{\bf p})
are
on a conic at infinity
if there is a non-zero
symmetric
d\times d
matrix
{\bf q}
such that, for all edges
i,j\in E
,
p_{i}-p_{j}\in C(Q)
, where
C(Q)=\{x\in\mdmathbb{R}^{d}:x^{t}Qx=0\}
.
Moreover
(G,{\bf p})
is
super stable
in
\mdmathbb{R}^{d}
if there exists an equilibrium stress
\omega
for
(G,{\bf p})

such that
\Omega(\omega)
is PSD of rank
n-d-1
, where
n
is the number of vertices of
G
, and the
edge directions of
(G,{\bf p})
do not lie on a conic at infinity.
At this point, it’s helpful to remind the reader than super-stability implies that

{\bf p}
has
d
-dimensional affine span.

Connelly
[
2
]
proved the following.

2.1
.

Let
(G,{\bf p})
be a super-stable framework in
\mdmathbb{R}^{d}
. Then
(G,{\bf p})
is universally rigid in
\mdmathbb{R}^{d}
.

Notice that super-stability does not need genericity or even general position. The only
non-degeneracy requirement is avoiding a conic at infinity.

To set up some notation, we
define the
configuration matrix

{\bf p}
to be the
d
-by-
n
matrix whose columns consist of
the coordinates of a configuration of
n
points in
\mdmathbb{R}^{d}
. We also define the

augmented configuration matrix
,
\hat{P}
, to be the
(d+1)
-by-
n
matrix obtained from
{\bf p}
by
adding a row of
1
’s at the bottom. Given the configuration
{\bf p}
, and a stress
\omega
,
if the rows of
\hat{P}
span the co-kernel of the associated stress matrix
\Omega
, we say that the
configuration
{\bf p}
is
universal
for the stress
\omega
.

A vector
\omega\in\mdmathbb{R}^{m}
is an equilibrium stress for
(G,{\bf p})
if and only if
\hat{P}\Omega=0
.
Hence,
if a universal configuration for
\omega
has
d
-dimensional affine span, then the cokernel of

\Omega

is
(d+1)
-dimensional, and the rank of
\Omega
is
n-(d+1)
. Conversely, if the rank of

\Omega
is
n-(d+1)
, then any universal configuration
{\bf p}
for
\omega
has a

d
-dimensional affine span.

We recall a standard lemma.

2.2
.

Suppose that the framework
(G,{\bf q})
with
n
vertices in
\mdmathbb{R}^{d}
is in
equilibrium with respect to the stress
\omega
with corresponding stress matrix
\Omega
,
and
{\bf p}
is another configuration for
G
whose affine span is
\mdmathbb{R}^{D}
in
equilibrium with respect to
\omega
such that the rank of
\Omega
is
n-D-1
.
Then
{\bf q}
is an affine image of
{\bf p}
.

We will also use the following theorem of Connelly. See Figure
4
for an example.

2.3
[
2
, Theorem 5]
.

Let
(G,{\bf p})
be a framework in
\mdmathbb{R}^{2}
, so that
{\bf p}
is in strictly convex position
and every edge of the convex hull of
{\bf p}
is an edge of
G
; we call these edges
boundary edges and the rest of the edges interior.
If
\omega
is an equlibrium stress of
(G,{\bf p})
that is positive
on the boundary edges and negative on the interior edges, then
the associated stress matrix is PSD of rank
n-3
.

3.  Projections of universally rigid frameworks

We make a brief digression into projections of frameworks that
are universally and globally rigid.

3.1
.

If
(G,\hat{\bf p})
is a framework that is
globally rigid in
\mdmathbb{R}^{D}
, then the orthogonal projection
of
(G,\hat{\bf p})
onto
(G,{\bf p})
in
\mdmathbb{R}^{d}
, for any
d\leq D
, is globally rigid in
\mdmathbb{R}^{d}
.

Proof.
Let
h_{i}
be the orthogonal projection of
\hat{p}_{i}
into the orthogonal complement of

\mdmathbb{R}^{d}
in
\mdmathbb{R}^{D}
so that
\hat{p}_{i}=p_{i}+h_{i}
, and
h_{i}
and
p_{i}
are orthogonal for all
i
. Then computing distances we have

\|\hat{p}_{i}-\hat{p}_{j}\|=\sqrt{\|p_{i}-p_{j}\|^{2}+\|h_{i}-h_{j}\|^{2}}.

If
{\bf q}
is another configuration corresponding to
{\bf p}
in
\mdmathbb{R}^{d}
, then define
\hat{q}_{i}=q_{i}+h_{i}
. Computing distances again we get

\|\hat{q}_{i}-\hat{q}_{j}\|=\sqrt{\|q_{i}-q_{j}\|^{2}+\|h_{i}-h_{j}\|^{2}}.

Then we see that

\|\hat{p}_{i}-\hat{p}_{j}\|=|\hat{q}_{i}-\hat{q}_{j}\|
if and only if
\|p_{i}-p_{j}\|=\|q_{i}-q_{j}\|
. So
(G,{\bf p})
is equivalent to
(G,{\bf q})
if and only if
(G,\hat{\bf p})
is equivalent to
(G,\hat{q})
. So if
(G,\hat{\bf p})
is globally rigid in
\mdmathbb{R}^{D}
, so is
(G,{\bf p})
in
\mdmathbb{R}^{d}
.

3.2
.

If
(G,\hat{\bf p})
is a framework that is
universally rigid in
\mdmathbb{R}^{D}
, then the orthogonal projection
of
(G,\hat{\bf p})
onto
(G,{\bf p})
in
\mdmathbb{R}^{d}
,
D\geq d
, is universally rigid in
\mdmathbb{R}^{d}
.

Proof.
Universal rigidity of
(G,{\bf p})
in
\mdmathbb{R}^{d}
is just global rigidity applied to
(G,{\bf p})
in
\mdmathbb{R}^{D}
for
D
large. So apply Theorem
3.1
to
\mdmathbb{R}^{D}\times\mdmathbb{R}^{k}\rightarrow\mdmathbb{R}^{d}\times\mdmathbb{R}^{k}
, for
k
large extending the projection
\mdmathbb{R}^{D}\rightarrow\mdmathbb{R}^{d}
.

Several remarks are in order.

1.
If the projection
(G,{\bf p})
is universally rigid in
\mdmathbb{R}^{d}
it is not necessarily true that
(G,\hat{\bf p})
is globally rigid in
\mdmathbb{R}^{D}
.
For example a quadrilateral in the plane can project to a stretched cycle in the line, which is super stable and universally
rigid, while the quadrilateral is not even rigid in the plane.

2.
Figure
2
shows the orthogonal projection of a super stable framework in
\mdmathbb{R}^{3}

into the plane that only has a one-dimensional stress coming from the stress in
\mdmathbb{R}^{3}
, since each vertex is of degree
3
,
all the
3
edges at each vertex have only one linear relation among them as vectors, and the edges are connected.
So the stress in any one edge determines the stress in all the other edges. Thus, in the plane, the rank of its
stress matrix will be
8-3-1=4
, which is not maximal. By Corollary
3.2
it is universally rigid, but
not super stable. See
[
5
]
for a general discussion of universally rigid but not super stable frameworks.

3.
Corollaries of Theorem
3.1
can be extended to other flavors of rigidity, such as local or infinitesimal
rigidity.

4.

We also give a short proof that universal rigidity is preserved under affine
transformations, even singular affine transformations. Plainly translation preserves universal rigidity. Now suppose that
(G,{\bf p})
is a universally rigid
framework in
\mdmathbb{R}^{d}
and
A
is a linear transformation on
\mdmathbb{R}^{d}
.
Using the singular value decomposition we can write
A
as
UDV
where
U
and
V
are orthogonal transformations and
D
is a diagonal
matrix with non-negative entries on the diagonal.

For the moment, assume that
D
has all entries in
[0,1]
. By embedding
(G,{\bf p})
in
\mdmathbb{R}^{D}
, for sufficiently large
D
,
we can model the action of
D
by an orthogonal projection onto an appropriately chosen
d
-dimensional
subspace
X
of
\mdmathbb{R}^{d}
. If
D
has entries that are greater than one, we first scale
{\bf p}
and then
use the same argument.

Finally, we model the map
A
by a product of maps
\tilde{U}P\tilde{V}
where
\tilde{U}
is orthogonal and
acts as
U
on
\mdmathbb{R}^{d}
,
{\bf p}
is the scaling and projection described above, and
\tilde{V}
is orthogonal and
acts as
V
on
X
. By Theorem
3.1
each of these preserves universal rigidity.

5.
A related fact is that, by
[
6
, Corollary 4.8]
, super stability is invariant with respect to
invertible projective
transformations in
\mdmathbb{R}^{d}
that do not send any vertices to infinity.
By contrast, we know that invertible projective
transformations do
not
always preserve universal rigidity.
See
[
6
, Figure 8 and Figure 9]
, which is closely related to Figure
3
below.

6.
Corollary
3.2
and the previous remarks give us a test for universal rigidity.
Suppose that
(G,{\bf p})
is a
d
-dimensional framework that has a PSD equilibrium stress
\omega
of rank
n-D-1
,
where
D\geq d
. If a
D
-dimensional framework

(G,\hat{\bf p})
satisfies
\omega
and its edges are not on a conic at infinity, then
(G,{\bf p})
is universally rigid,
since it is an affine image of a super stable framework. A necessary and sufficient test is given in

[
5
]
, but this one is simpler to apply.

4.  The triangular prism

Here we show that universal rigidity on the line, even when the configuration coordinates are generic do not only depend on the ordering of the vertices. This answers Question 2.5 of
[
9
]
negatively. The fundamental example is the
triangular prism
, the cartesian product of
K_{3}
and
K_{2}
(depicted in Figure
3
), which we denote by
T
.

We will create a PSD equilibrium stress that shows the universal rigidity of the bottom framework of Figure
3
, and yet the top framework of Figure
3
is flexible in the plane. (See Figure 9 in
[
5
]
, where it is shown that any flex of the top framework must have a
2
-dimensional affine span.)

3

Universally rigid example: first proof

We begin with the framework
(G,\hat{\bf p})
in
\mdmathbb{R}^{2}
shown in Figure
4
, which is super-stable by
Theorem
2.3
and hence has a PSD equilibrium stress
\hat{\omega}
of rank
n-3
. We project it orthogonally onto the line to a framework
(G,{\bf p})
. On the line any one of the flat triangles in
(G,{\bf p})
has a PSD equilibrium stress
\omega
. Plainly
\omega+\hat{\omega}
is a PSD equilibrium stress and it is satisfied by
(G,{\bf p})
.

Let
{\bf q}
be any configuration that is universal for

\omega+\hat{\omega}
. By Lemma
2.2
,
{\bf q}
is an affine image of
\hat{\bf p}
. The affine map sending
\hat{\bf p}
to
{\bf q}
is determined by its action on the triangle, and we conclude that
{\bf q}
has
d
-dimensional affine span. Since the edges of
(G,{\bf p})
are not on a conic, we conclude that the framework
(G,{\bf p})
is super-stable. We do not yet know that it is generic. However, since the projection is infinitesimally rigid, a small perturbation will also be super-stable and generic (see
[
6
]
), with the vertices in the same order.

Universally rigid example: second proof

Our second analysis of the universally rigid example uses affine invariance of universal rigidity.
Starting from the super-stable framework in Figure
4
, we have a
6
-dimensional
space of nearby convex frameworks that are also super-stable. We freely specify the two left
vertices of the quadrilateral and the bottom right one; this fixes the position of the
top right one. Then we freely place the bottom vertex, which leaves
1
degree of freedom for the top
one. Removing isometies gives us
6
dimensions.

Hence, an affine projection onto a generic line, such as a small wiggle of the
x
-axis is
generic as a configuration of points in
\mdmathbb{R}^{1}
. As discussed in the previous section, this
projection is universally rigid. Since it is generic, results of
[
7
]

tell us it is, in fact, super-stable.

5.  Final questions

One may also ask: does a universally rigid framework in the line only come from some overlapping stretched cycles, even if some other stresses are needed?

A related question: If a framework in the line is universally rigid, is it necessarily super stable? We have no counterexamples.
