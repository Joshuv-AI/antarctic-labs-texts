Zeros of L L -Functions in Low-Lying Intervals and de Branges spaces
Antonio Pedro Ramos

Abstract
. We consider a variant of a problem first introduced by Hughes and Rudnick (2003) and generalized by Bernard (2015) concerning conditional bounds for small first zeros in a family of L L -functions. Here we seek to estimate the size of the smallest intervals centered at a low-lying height for which we can guarantee the existence of a zero in a family of L L -functions. This leads us to consider an extremal problem in analysis which we address by applying the framework of de Branges spaces, introduced in this context by Carneiro, Chirre, and Milinovich (2022).

1.2.
Hilbert spaces of entire functions

The upper-bound (
1.2
) can be cast as the solution of an extremal problem in Paley–Wiener space, a classical example of a Hilbert space of entire functions. This is generalized in the case of the other symmetry groups by the de Branges spaces.

We recall that an entire function
F:\mathbb{C}\to\mathbb{C}
is said to be of exponential type if

\tau(F):=\limsup_{|z|\to\infty}\,|z|^{-1}\log|F(z)|<\infty,

and, in that case, we say
\tau(F)
is the exponential type of
F
. The spaces
\mathcal{H}_{G,\pi\Delta}
of entire functions of exponential type at most
\pi\Delta
with norm

\|F\|^{2}_{\mathcal{H}_{G,\pi\Delta}}:=\int_{\mathbb{R}}|F(x)|^{2}\,W_{G}(x)\,\text{\rm d}x<\infty

constitute a Hilbert space. We point out that
\mathcal{H}_{\text{U},\pi\Delta}
equipped with the norm
\|\cdot\|_{\mathcal{H}_{\text{U},\pi\Delta}}
is the classical Paley–Wiener space. As demonstrated in
[
9
, Section 3]
, an application of a Fourier uncertainty principle shows that, as sets, the
\mathcal{H}_{G,\pi\Delta}
are all equal. This is the set
\mathcal{H}_{\pi\Delta}
of functions of exponential type at most
\pi\Delta
such that their restriction to
\mathbb{R}
is square-integrable. The content of the Paley–Wiener theorem is that
F\in\mathcal{H}_{\pi\Delta}
if and only if
F\in C(\mathbb{R})\cap L^{2}(\mathbb{R})
and
\mathrm{supp}\,\widehat{F}\subseteq[-\Delta/2,\Delta/2]
.

If one defines

(1.6)

\mathbb{A}({G,\pi\Delta,0}):=\inf_{0\neq f\in\mathcal{H}_{\pi\Delta}}\frac{\int_{\mathbb{R}}|xf(x)|^{2}\,W_{G}(x)\,\text{\rm d}x}{\int_{\mathbb{R}}|f(x)|^{2}\,W_{G}(x)\,\text{\rm d}x},

then the authors of
[
9
]
showed how to obtain Theorem A below. The presence of the parameter
0
will become clear soon.

Theorem A

(cf.
[
9
, Theorem 9]
)
.

Let
\{L(s,f),\,f\in\mathcal{F}\}
be a family of L-functions with an associated symmetry group
G\in\{{\rm U,Sp,O,SO(even),SO(odd)}\}
, and assume that GRH holds for
L
-functions in this family. In addition, suppose (
1.4
) holds for even Schwartz functions
\phi
with
{\rm supp}\,\widehat{\phi}\subseteq(-\Delta,\Delta)
for a fixed
\Delta>0
. Then for the families with
G\in\{\rm U,Sp,SO(even)\}

\limsup_{\mathcal{Q}\rightarrow\infty}\,\min_{\begin{subarray}{c}{\gamma_{f}}\\
{f\in\mathcal{F}(Q)}\end{subarray}}\left|\frac{\gamma_{f}\log c_{f}}{2\pi}\right|\leq\sqrt{\mathbb{A}({G,\pi\Delta,0})}.

Moreover, for the families with
G\in\{\rm O,SO(odd)\}
, we may exclude the zeros at the central point
Z(f)
. Thus, denoting
G^{\sharp}={\rm U}
if
G={\rm O}
and
G^{\sharp}={\rm Sp}
if
G={\rm SO(odd)}

\limsup_{\mathcal{Q}\rightarrow\infty}\,\min_{\begin{subarray}{c}{\gamma_{f}\notin Z(f)}\\
{f\in\mathcal{F}(Q)}\end{subarray}}\left|\frac{\gamma_{f}\log c_{f}}{2\pi}\right|\leq\sqrt{\mathbb{A}({G^{\sharp},\pi\Delta,0})}.

In all of these spaces the evaluation functionals are continuous. In other words, for all
w\in\mathbb{C}
the linear map
F\mapsto F(w)
is continuous. By applying the Riesz representation theorem, we know that for each
w\in\mathbb{C}
there is an associated entire function
K_{G,\pi\Delta}(w,\cdot)\in\mathcal{H}_{G,\pi\Delta}
that corresponds to the evaluation functional at
w
, meaning

F(w)=\langle F,K_{G,\pi\Delta(w,\cdot)}\rangle_{\mathcal{H}_{G,\pi\Delta}}=\int_{\mathbb{R}}F(x)\overline{K_{G,\pi\Delta}(w,x)}\,\text{\rm d}x

for all
F\in\mathcal{H}_{G,\pi\Delta}
. Such a
K_{G,\pi\Delta}(w,\cdot)
is called a reproducing kernel. We thus say these spaces have the
reproducing kernel property
. Carneiro, Chirre, and Milinovich studied the spaces
\mathcal{H}_{G,\pi\Delta}
and found explicit expressions for their reproducing kernels when
\Delta>0
for the groups
G\in\{\rm{U,O}\}
and when
0<\Delta\leq 2
for
G\in\{\rm{Sp,SO(even),SO(odd)}\}
(see
[
9
, Theorems 3–7]
). They also established that the solution to the extremal problem posed by (
1.6

Theorem B

(cf.
[
9
, Theorem 9 and Theorem 14]
)
.

Let
G\in\{\rm U,Sp,O,SO(even),SO(odd)\}
, and let
K=K_{G,\pi\Delta}
be the reproducing kernel of the Hilbert space
(\mathcal{H}_{\pi\Delta}
,
\langle\cdot,\cdot\rangle_{G,\pi\Delta})

Let
\xi_{1}

\mathbb{A}({G,\pi\Delta,0})=\inf_{0\neq f\in\mathcal{H}_{\pi\Delta}}\frac{\int_{\mathbb{R}}|xf(x)|^{2}\,W_{G}(x)\,\text{\rm d}x}{\int_{\mathbb{R}}|f(x)|^{2}\,W_{G}(x)\,\text{\rm d}x}=\xi_{1}^{2}.

Together, Theorem A and Theorem B provide an upper bound for the minimal height of the first zero in a family of
L
-functions once knows the relevant reproducing kernels explicitly. Here we address the question of existence of zeros in small intervals of the critical line at different heights. In analogy with Theorem A, we define the following sharp constant for a given real number

(1.7)

\mathbb{A}(G,\pi\Delta,\alpha):=\inf_{0\neq f\in\mathcal{H}_{\pi\Delta}}\frac{\int_{\mathbb{R}}|(x-\alpha)f(x)|^{2}\,W_{G}(x)\,\text{\rm d}x}{\int_{\mathbb{R}}|f(x)|^{2}\,W_{G}(x)\,\text{\rm d}x}.

Theorem 1
.

Fix

\{L(s,f),\,f\in\mathcal{F}\}

1.4

\phi

\limsup_{\mathcal{Q}\rightarrow\infty}\,\min_{\begin{subarray}{c}{\gamma_{f}}\\
{f\in\mathcal{F}(Q)}\end{subarray}}\left|\frac{\gamma_{f}\log c_{f}}{2\pi}-\alpha\right|\leq\sqrt{\mathbb{A}(G,\pi\Delta,\alpha)}.

Remark:
In orthogonal and odd orthogonal families, where, due to the sign of the functional equation, we may expect at least part of the family to trivially vanish at the central point, we can further bound

\limsup_{\mathcal{Q}\rightarrow\infty}\,\min_{\begin{subarray}{c}{\gamma_{f}}\\

1.3.
An extremal problem in de Branges spaces

We connect the problem of determining the sharp constants
\mathbb{A}(G,\pi\Delta,\alpha)
to the theory of de Branges spaces. Theorem
2
is a consequence of the more general Theorem
4
, which is about sharp inequalities related to the operators of multiplication by
(z-\alpha)^{k}
in a de Branges space, with
k\in\mathbb{N}
and
\alpha\in\mathbb{R}
. This theorem addresses an extremal problem which is a generalization of the one studied by Carneiro et al in
[
10
, Section 8]
, who take up the case
\alpha=0
. In fact, this discussion parallels that of
[
10
]
in many respects. However, as mentioned previously, the broken symmetry introduces new challenges to consider.

We first recall some basic notions from this theory. See de Branges’s monograph
[
6
]
for a more detailed exposition. We use the notation
f^{*}(z)=\overline{f(\bar{z})}
throughout. A function that is analytic in the upper half-plane
\mathbb{C}^{+}
is said to be of
bounded type
if it can be expressed as the quotient of two bounded analytic functions in
\mathbb{C}^{+}
. For such a function
f
, one has
[
6
, Theorems 9 and 10]

v(f):=\limsup_{y\rightarrow+\infty}\,{y}^{-1}\,{\log|f(iy)|}<\infty,

and we call
v(f)
the mean type of
f
.

Let
E:\mathbb{C}\rightarrow\mathbb{C}
be a
Hermite-Biehler
function, that is, an entire function satisfying the inequality

|E^{*}(z)|<|E(z)|

for all
z\in\mathbb{C}^{+}
. The de Branges space
\mathcal{H}(E)
associated to
E
is the space of entire functions
f:\mathbb{C}\rightarrow\mathbb{C}
such that

\|f\|_{\mathcal{H}(E)}^{2}:=\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\left|\frac{f(x)}{E(x)}\right|^{2}\text{\rm d}x<\infty,

and such that
f/E
and
f^{*}/E
are of bounded type with non-positive mean type. This space forms a reproducing kernel Hilbert space with the inner product

\langle f,g\rangle_{\mathcal{H}(E)}:=\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\frac{f(x)\overline{g(x)}}{|E(x)|^{2}}\,\text{\rm d}x.

To every such
E
there exist unique real entire functions
2
2

2

We say an entire function
F:\mathbb{C}\to\mathbb{C}
is real entire if its restriction to
\mathbb{R}
is real-valued. Equivalently,
F(z)=F^{*}(z)
.

A:\mathbb{C}\rightarrow\mathbb{C}
and
B:\mathbb{C}\rightarrow\mathbb{C}
such that
E(z)=A(z)-iB(z)
. These companion functions must be

A(z)=\frac{1}{2}\left(E(z)+E^{*}(z)\right)\text{ and }B(z)=\frac{i}{2}\left(E(z)-E^{*}(z)\right),

and we can write the reproducing kernels for these spaces in terms of these functions. For every
w\in\mathbb{C}
, the corresponding reproducing kernel is given by (see
[
6
, Theorem 19]
)

(1.8)

K(w,z)=\frac{B(z)A(\overline{w})-A(z)B(\overline{w})}{\pi(z-\overline{w})},

and, when
w=\bar{z}
,

K(\bar{z},z)=\frac{1}{\pi}\left(B^{\prime}(z)A(z)-A^{\prime}(z)B(z)\right).

A classical example is the Paley-Wiener space
\mathcal{H}_{\rm U,\pi\Delta}
, which is the space
\mathcal{H}(E)
when
E(z)=e^{-i\pi\Delta z}
. In this case the auxiliary functions are
A(z)=\cos\pi\Delta z
and
B(z)=\sin\pi\Delta z
.

We will be considering the following extremal problem, a generalization of the one defined in (
1.7
).

Extremal Problem
: Fix
k\in\mathbb{N}
and let
\mathcal{X}_{k}:=\{f\in\mathcal{H}(E):z^{k}f\in\mathcal{H}(E)\}
. Find for
\alpha\in\mathbb{R}

(1.9)

(\mathbb{EP})(E,k,\alpha):=\inf_{0\neq f\in\mathcal{X}_{k}}\frac{\|(z-\alpha)^{k}f\|_{\mathcal{H}(E)}^{2}}{\|f\|_{\mathcal{H}(E)}^{2}}.

The fact that we can determine the solutions of equations of the form
(z-\alpha)^{k}=\eta
for
\eta>0
allows us to adapt the argument of
[
10
]
to pin down a precise a value of
(\mathbb{EP})(E,k,\alpha)
when the function
E
satisfies the following hypotheses:

(C1)

E
has no real zeros;

(C2)

the function
z\mapsto E(iz)
is real entire;

(C3)

A\notin\mathcal{H}(E)
.

Condition (C2) is equivalent to the fact that the auxiliary functions
A
and
B
are even and odd, respectively, while conditions (C1) and (C3) allow for the use of the following interpolation formula in de Branges spaces:

(1.10)

f(z)=\sum_{A(\xi)=0}\frac{f(\xi)}{K(\xi,\xi)}K(\xi,z)

for all
f\in\mathcal{H}(E)
, with the sum above converging both uniformly in compact sets and in the norm of
\mathcal{H}(E)

Proof of Theorem 1: Existence of Zeros in Low-Lying Intervals

Let us recall briefly the idea of Hughes and Rudnick to find the smallest interval of the central point for which there exists a zero in

(ii)

\displaystyle 0

4.
The Extremal Problem in de Branges Spaces

We need a few preliminary results concerning the Extremal Problem defined in (
1.9
) before embarking on the proof of Theorem
4
.

Proposition 5
.

Let
E
be a Hermite-Biehler function and suppose
\mathcal{X}_{k}=\{f\in\mathcal{H}(E):z^{k}f\in\mathcal{H}(E)\}\neq\{0\}
. The following statements hold:

(i)

(\mathbb{EP})(E,k,\alpha)>0
.

(ii)

There exist extremizers for
(\mathbb{EP})(E,k,\alpha)
.

Proof.

Essentially as found in
[
10
]
, we include it here for the sake of completion.

First observe that
f\in\mathcal{X}_{k}
if and only if
(z-\alpha)^{k}f\in\mathcal{H}(E)
. This follows because multiplication by
(z-\alpha)^{k}
or
z^{k}
does not change the mean type of a function and the integrability condition is in essence the same. Indeed, outside a compact set the function
|x-\alpha|^{2k}
is of the order of
|x|^{2k}
, and vice versa.

Note now that the function
x\mapsto K(x,x)|E(x)|^{-2}
is continuous, and, in particular, locally integrable. We can thus find
0<\eta<1
for which

\int_{-\eta+\alpha}^{\eta+\alpha}\frac{K(x,x)}{|E(x)|^{2}}\,\text{\rm d}x\leq\frac{1}{2}.

Thus, from the reproducing kernel property and Cauchy-Schwarz

\displaystyle\int_{-\eta+\alpha}^{\eta+\alpha}\left|\frac{f(x)}{E(x)}\right|^{2}\,\text{\rm d}x=\int_{-\eta+\alpha}^{\eta+\alpha}\left|\frac{\langle f,K(x,\cdot)\rangle_{\mathcal{H}(E)}}{E(x)}\right|^{2}\,\text{\rm d}x\leq\|f\|_{\mathcal{H}(E)}^{2}\int_{-\eta+\alpha}^{\eta+\alpha}\frac{K(x,x)}{|E(x)|^{2}}\,\text{\rm d}x\leq\frac{\|f\|_{\mathcal{H}(E)}^{2}}{2}.

So that

\displaystyle\int_{\mathbb{R}}\left|\frac{(x-\alpha)^{k}f(x)}{E(x)}\right|^{2}\,\text{\rm d}x\geq\int_{|x-\alpha|\geq\eta}\left|\frac{(x-\alpha)^{k}f(x)}{E(x)}\right|^{2}\,\text{\rm d}x\geq\eta^{2k}\int_{|x-\alpha|\geq\eta}\left|\frac{f(x)}{E(x)}\right|^{2}\,\text{\rm d}x\geq\eta^{2k}\frac{\|f\|_{\mathcal{H}(E)}^{2}}{2},

which implies
(\mathbb{EP})(E,k,\alpha)\geq\frac{\eta^{2k}}{2}>0
.

To prove (ii), take a minimizing sequence
\{f_{n}\}\subseteq\mathcal{H}(E)
normalized by
\|(z-\alpha)^{k}f_{n}\|_{\mathcal{H}(E)}^{2}=1
, implying

\lim_{n\to\infty}\|f_{n}\|^{2}_{\mathcal{H}(E)}=(\mathbb{EP})(E,k,\alpha)^{-1}.

This is a bounded sequence in
\mathcal{H}(E)
, so by the Banach–Alaoglu theorem it converges weakly to a
g\in\mathcal{H}(E)
. Since evaluation functionals are continuous,
f_{n}(z)\to g(z)
for all
z\in\mathbb{C}
. So that applying Fatou’s lemma

\int_{\mathbb{R}}\bigg|\frac{(x-\alpha)^{k}g(x)}{E(x)}\bigg|^{2}\,\text{\rm d}x\leq\lim_{n\to\infty}\int_{\mathbb{R}}\bigg|\frac{(x-\alpha)^{k}f_{n}(x)}{E(x)}\bigg|^{2}\,\text{\rm d}x=1,

verifying
g\in\mathcal{X}_{k}.

If
0<r<(E,k,\alpha)^{-1}
, then for
n
big enough and for
R>0
,

\displaystyle r

\displaystyle\leq\int_{\mathbb{R}}\bigg|\frac{f_{n}(x)}{E(x)}\bigg|^{2}\,\text{\rm d}x

\displaystyle=\int_{-R+\alpha}^{R+\alpha}\bigg|\frac{f_{n}(x)}{E(x)}\bigg|^{2}\,\text{\rm d}x+\int_{|x-\alpha|\geq R}\bigg|\frac{f_{n}(x)}{E(x)}\bigg|^{2}\,\text{\rm d}x

\displaystyle\leq\int_{-R+\alpha}^{R+\alpha}\bigg|\frac{f_{n}(x)}{E(x)}\bigg|^{2}\,\text{\rm d}x+\frac{1}{R^{2k}}\int_{|x-\alpha|\geq R}\bigg|\frac{(x-\alpha)^{k}f_{n}(x)}{E(x)}\bigg|^{2}\,\text{\rm d}x

\displaystyle\leq\int_{-R+\alpha}^{R+\alpha}\bigg|\frac{f_{n}(x)}{E(x)}\bigg|^{2}\,\text{\rm d}x+\frac{1}{R^{2k}},

by the normalization
\|(z-\alpha)^{k}f_{n}\|^{2}_{\mathcal{H}(E)}=1
. Sending
n\to\infty
and applying the dominated convergence theorem,

r\leq\int_{-R+\alpha}^{R+\alpha}\bigg|\frac{g(x)}{E(x)}\bigg|^{2}\,\text{\rm d}x+\frac{1}{R^{2k}}.

Now we can send
R\to\infty
and
r\to(E,k,\alpha)^{-1}
to conclude
g
is the desired extremizer.
∎

Remark.
The proof above is valid without assuming any of three conditions (C1)–(C3) on the Hermite-Biehler function

are cancelled out by those of

is in any case continuous.

We now establish an equivalence between the extremal problem in
\mathcal{H}(E)

Lemma 6

Let
E

but we may also classify extremizers in the other cases contemplated in this theorem. In view of the proof above, we can obtain a real extremizing sequence
\{a_{n}^{*}\}\in X
to the right-hand side of (
4.1
) by taking
(a_{i_{1}}^{*},\ldots,a_{i_{k}}^{*})\in\ker\mathcal{W}(\lambda_{0})\setminus\{\boldsymbol{0}\}
and building the rest of the sequence by (
4.7
). Applying formula (
1.10
), extremizers must then be in the span over
\mathbb{C}
of the entire functions

f(z)=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}{n=-\infty}\\
{n\neq 0}\end{subarray}}^{\infty}a_{n}^{*}\frac{A(z)}{z-\xi_{n}}

that come from sequences
\{a_{n}^{*}\}\in X
constructed in this way, as the initial reduction in the proof of Lemma
6
guarantees.

4.1.
Proof of Theorem
2

A few steps are missing in order to conclude Theorem
2
, which instead follows directly from a slight adaptation of the argument. In fact, as Carneiro, Chirre, and Milinovich establish in
[
9
, Section 5.2]
, the spaces
(\mathcal{H}_{G,\pi\Delta},\langle\cdot,\cdot\rangle_{\mathcal{H}_{G,\pi\Delta}})
are isometrically isomorphic to certain de Branges spaces. They give a procedure that allows one to obtain a (not necessarily unique) Hermite-Biehler function
E
from the expression of the reproducing kernels of
\mathcal{H}_{G,\pi\Delta}
. It consists of defining

L(w,z):=2\pi i(\overline{w}-z)K_{G,\pi\Delta}(w,z),

and then considering the entire function

E(z):=L(i,i)^{-\frac{1}{2}}L(i,z),

which is Hermite-Biehler (see
[
6
, Problems 50-53]
and
[
8
, Appendix A]
for details). By
[
6
, Theorem 23]
,
E
is such that
\mathcal{H}_{G,\pi\Delta}=\mathcal{H}(E)
as sets and

\displaystyle\|F\|_{\mathcal{H}_{G,\pi\Delta}}=\left(\int_{\mathbb{R}}|F(x)|^{2}\,W_{G}(x)\,\text{\rm d}x\right)^{\frac{1}{2}}=\left(\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\left|\frac{F(x)}{E(x)}\right|^{2}\text{\rm d}x\right)^{\frac{1}{2}}=\|F\|_{\mathcal{H}(E)}

for each
F
therein.
Such an
E
can be seen to satisfy conditions (C1) and (C2), so that Theorem
2
is thus a consequence of the following.

Corollary 7
.

Let
E
be a Hermite-Biehler function satisfying (C1) and (C2) and
\mathcal{X}_{1}\neq\{0\}
. Setting
\lambda_{0}=\big((\mathbb{EP})(E,1,\alpha)\big)^{1/2}
, we have that
\lambda_{0}
is the first positive zero of the equation

K(\alpha+\lambda,\alpha-\lambda)=0.

Proof.

If
A\notin\mathcal{H}(E)
then we can apply Theorem
4
when
k=1
, which states
\lambda_{0}
is the first zero of

\displaystyle A(\alpha+\lambda)A(\alpha-\lambda)\left(C(\alpha+\lambda)-C(\alpha-\lambda)\right)=B(\alpha+\lambda)A(\alpha-\lambda)-A(\alpha+\lambda)B(\alpha-\lambda),

which by (
1.8
) is

2\pi\lambda\,K(\alpha+\lambda,\alpha-\lambda).

If
B\notin\mathcal{H}(E)
, we may use the interpolation formula and Parseval’s identity corresponding to the zeros of
B
. That is, as in (
1.10
) and (
1.11
), we have by
[
6
, Theorem 22]
that

f(z)=\sum_{B(\eta)=0}\frac{f(\eta)}{K(\eta,\eta)}K(\eta,z)\ \ \text{ and }\ \ \|f\|_{\mathcal{H}(E)}^{2}=\sum_{B(\eta)=0}\frac{|f(\eta)|^{2}}{K(\eta,\eta)},

and an analogous version of Lemma
6
holds (see also the remark immediately following that lemma). By (C2), the zeros of
B
are also symmetric in the sense that
B(\eta)=0
if and only if
B(-\eta)=0
. Its non-negative zeros can be enumerated as
0=\eta_{0}<\eta_{1}<\ldots
, and so defining
d_{m}=B^{\prime}(\eta_{m})/A(\eta_{m})
, we likewise have the formula

\frac{A(z)}{B(z)}=\frac{1}{d_{0}z}+\sum_{m=1}^{\infty}\frac{2z}{d_{m}(z^{2}-\eta_{m}^{2})}

converging uniformly in compact sets away from the zeros of
B
. The same procedure in the proof of Theorem
4
can now be repeated, in which case
\lambda_{0}
may be seen to be the first positive zero of

B(\alpha+\lambda)B(\alpha-\lambda)\left(\frac{A(\alpha+\lambda)}{B(\alpha+\lambda)}-\frac{A(\alpha-\lambda)}{B(\alpha-\lambda)}\right)=-2\pi\lambda\,K(\alpha+\lambda,\alpha-\lambda).

But for any given
E
, at most one of
A
and
B
can be in the space
\mathcal{H}(E)
, since by integrability considerations
E(z)=A(z)-iB(z)
cannot itself occur in
\mathcal{H}(E)
, and the result holds.
∎

Remark.
The condition
\mathcal{X}_{1}\neq\{0\}
is, of course, satisfied in the cases that interest us. For instance

f(z)=\frac{\cos^{2}\pi\Delta z/2}{1-4\Delta^{2}z^{2}}

is such that
zf(z)\in\mathcal{H}_{\pi\Delta}
. In general, we may conclude
\mathcal{X}_{k}
is non-trivial if one of the associated functions
A(z)
or
B(z)
has at least
k+1
zeros. If, say,
\xi_{1},\ldots,\xi_{k+1}
are such zeros, then one may obtain
g(z)=\frac{A(z)}{(z-\xi_{1})\ldots(z-\xi_{k+1})}\in\mathcal{H}(E)
(or the analogous for
B
), from an appropriate linear combination of the reproducing kernels
K(\xi_{i},z)
. Since
|g(x)|\sim|A(x)||x|^{-k-1}
for
|x|\gg 1
, this function satisfies
z^{k}g(z)\in\mathcal{H}(E)
.

5.
Concluding Remarks

We conclude with an interesting qualitative property of the extremal problem in de Branges spaces.

Proposition 8
.

Let
E
be Hermite-Biehler and
k\in\mathbb{N}
. If
\mathcal{X}_{k}\neq\{0\}
, then the function
\alpha\in\mathbb{R}\mapsto(\mathbb{EP})(E,k,\alpha)
is continuous.

Proof.

By Proposition
5
, for all
\alpha
there exists an
f_{\alpha}\in\mathcal{H}(E)
that attains the corresponding infimum in (
1.9
). That is, if we denote by

\Phi_{\alpha}(f):=\frac{\|(z-\alpha)^{k}f\|_{\mathcal{H}(E)}^{2}}{\|f\|_{\mathcal{H}(E)}^{2}}

for
0\neq f\in\mathcal{X}_{k}
, we have
\Phi_{\alpha}(f_{\alpha})=(\mathbb{EP})(E,k,\alpha)
.

Now fix
\alpha
and let
\{\alpha_{n}\}\subseteq\mathbb{R}
be a sequence converging to
\alpha
. We can invoke Proposition
5
again to obtain a sequence of extremizers
\{f_{\alpha_{n}}\}\subseteq\mathcal{H}(E)
each satisfying
\Phi_{\alpha_{n}}(f_{\alpha_{n}})=(\mathbb{EP})(E,k,\alpha_{n})
. Without loss of generality, assume
\|f_{\alpha}\|_{\mathcal{H}(E)}=1
and
\|f_{\alpha_{n}}\|_{\mathcal{H}(E)}=1
.

On the one hand we have

\displaystyle(\mathbb{EP})(E,k,\alpha)

\displaystyle=\Phi_{\alpha}(f_{\alpha})=\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\left|\frac{(x-\alpha)^{k}f_{\alpha}(x)}{E(x)}\right|^{2}\text{\rm d}x

\displaystyle=\lim_{n\rightarrow\infty}\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\left|\frac{(x-\alpha_{n})^{k}f_{\alpha}(x)}{E(x)}\right|^{2}\text{\rm d}x=\lim_{n\rightarrow\infty}\Phi_{\alpha_{n}}(f_{\alpha})

(5.1)

\displaystyle\geq\limsup_{n\rightarrow\infty}\,(\mathbb{EP})(E,k,\alpha_{n})

by dominated convergence, so that
\alpha\mapsto(\mathbb{EP})(E,k,\alpha)
is upper semicontinuous.

On the other hand, we can also see that

\displaystyle(\mathbb{EP})(E,k,\alpha)

\displaystyle\leq\Phi_{\alpha}(f_{\alpha_{n}})=\Phi_{\alpha_{n}}(f_{\alpha_{n}})+\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\left\{(x-\alpha)^{2k}-(x-\alpha_{n})^{2k}\right\}\left|\frac{f_{\alpha_{n}}(x)}{E(x)}\right|^{2}\text{\rm d}x

\displaystyle=(\mathbb{EP})(E,k,\alpha_{n})+O\big(|\alpha-\alpha_{n}|\big),

because

\displaystyle\bigg|\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\big\{(x-\alpha)^{2k}-

\displaystyle(x-\alpha_{n})^{2k}\big\}\left|\frac{f_{\alpha_{n}}(x)}{E(x)}\right|^{2}\text{\rm d}x\bigg|

\displaystyle\leq\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\left|(x-\alpha)^{2k}-(x-\alpha_{n})^{2k}\right|\left|\frac{f_{\alpha_{n}}(x)}{E(x)}\right|^{2}\text{\rm d}x

\displaystyle\ll|\alpha-\alpha_{n}|\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}(\max\{|\alpha|,|\alpha_{n}|\}^{2k-1}+|x|^{2k-1})\left|\frac{f_{\alpha_{n}}(x)}{E(x)}\right|^{2}\text{\rm d}x

\displaystyle\ll|\alpha-\alpha_{n}|(\|f_{\alpha_{n}}\|_{\mathcal{H}(E)}^{2}+\|z^{k}f_{\alpha_{n}}\|_{\mathcal{H}(E)}^{2})

\displaystyle=|\alpha-\alpha_{n}|(1+(\mathbb{EP})(E,k,\alpha_{n}))

and
(\mathbb{EP})(E,k,\alpha_{n})
is bounded in
n
by (
5.1
), so that

(\mathbb{EP})(E,k,\alpha)\leq\liminf_{n\rightarrow\infty}\,(\mathbb{EP})(E,k,\alpha_{n}),

i.e., it is lower semicontinuous. Hence, it is also continuous.
∎
