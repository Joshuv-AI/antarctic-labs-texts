1.
Introduction

Gentzen-style sequent calculus is a proof system for sequents
\Gamma\mathbin{\Rightarrow}\Delta
,
where
\Gamma
and
\Delta
are finite sequences of formulae. Since
the arrow symbol
\mathbin{\Rightarrow}
behaves as meta-implication, implicational
axioms can be well transformed to inference rules. For example, the

\land
-introduction axiom (schema)
\varphi\to\psi\to\varphi\land\psi

can be reformulated as the following rule:

\vbox{$\Gamma\mathbin{\Rightarrow}\Delta,\varphi\land\psi\Gamma\mathbin{\Rightarrow}\Delta,\varphi&\Gamma\mathbin{\Rightarrow}\Delta,\psi$}.

On the other hand, sequent calculus does not well manipulate axioms
whose outermost logical symbols are not implications such as the linearity
axiom
\mathbf{LIN}\colon\left(\varphi\to\psi\right)\lor\left(\psi\to\varphi\right)
, the weak law of excluded middle
\mathbf{WLEM}\colon\neg\varphi\vee\neg\neg\varphi
, and the constant domain axiom
\mathbf{CD}\colon\forall x\left(\varphi\lor\psi\left(x\right)\right)\to\varphi\lor\forall x\psi\left(x\right)
. Note that these axioms care about disjunctions (and universal quantifiers). See e.g.
Kashima 2007
for some fundamental
problems concerning
\mathbf{CD}
.

Hypersequent calculus was first introduced by
Avron 1987
. A
hypersequent

is a finite sequence
\left(\Gamma_{i}\mathbin{\Rightarrow}\varphi_{i}\right)_{i=1}^{n}

of sequents, and is usually denoted as follows:

\Gamma_{1}\mathbin{\Rightarrow}\Delta_{1}\mid\Gamma_{2}\mathbin{\Rightarrow}\Delta_{2}\mid\cdots\mid\Gamma_{n}\mathbin{\Rightarrow}\Delta_{n}.

The sequents
\Gamma_{i}\mathbin{\Rightarrow}\Delta_{i}
are called
components

of the hypersequent. Throughout this paper, we denote hypersequents
by meta-symbols
G,H,\ldots
, sequents by
S,T,\ldots
, and formulae
by
\varphi,\psi,\ldots
; the concatenation of (possibly empty) hypersequents

G
and
H
by
G\mid H
.
