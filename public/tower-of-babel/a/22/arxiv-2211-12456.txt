Exponential separations using guarded extension variables
Emre Yolcu, Marijn J. H. Heule

Abstract
We study the complexity of proof systems augmenting resolution with inference rules that allow, given a formula Γ \Gamma in conjunctive normal form, deriving clauses that are not necessarily logically implied by Γ \Gamma but whose addition to Γ \Gamma preserves satisfiability. When the derived clauses are allowed to introduce variables not occurring in Γ \Gamma , the systems we consider become equivalent to extended resolution. We are concerned with the versions of these systems without new variables. They are called 𝖡𝖢 − \mathsf{BC}^{-} , 𝖱𝖠𝖳 − \mathsf{RAT}^{-} , 𝖲𝖡𝖢 − \mathsf{SBC}^{-} , and 𝖦𝖤𝖱 − \mathsf{GER}^{-} , denoting respectively blocked clauses, resolution asymmetric tautologies, set-blocked clauses, and generalized extended resolution. Each of these systems formalizes some restricted version of the ability to make assumptions that hold “without loss of generality,” which is commonly used informally to simplify or shorten proofs. Except for 𝖲𝖡𝖢 − \mathsf{SBC}^{-} , these systems are known to be exponentially weaker than extended resolution. They are, however, all equivalent to it under a relaxed notion of simulation that allows the translation of the formula along with the proof when moving between proof systems. By taking advantage of this fact, we construct formulas that separate 𝖱𝖠𝖳 − \mathsf{RAT}^{-} from 𝖦𝖤𝖱 − \mathsf{GER}^{-} and vice versa. With the same strategy, we also separate 𝖲𝖡𝖢 − \mathsf{SBC}^{-} from 𝖱𝖠𝖳 − \mathsf{RAT}^{-} . Additionally, we give polynomial-size 𝖲𝖡𝖢 − \mathsf{SBC}^{-} proofs of the pigeonhole principle, which separates 𝖲𝖡𝖢 − \mathsf{SBC}^{-} from 𝖦𝖤𝖱 − \mathsf{GER}^{-} by a previously known lower bound. These results also separate the three systems from 𝖡𝖢 − \mathsf{BC}^{-} since they all simulate it. We thus give an almost complete picture of their relative strengths.

Properties of commonly studied proof systems

Most of the commonly studied rule-based propositional proof systems,

Possibly the most prominent example of such a system in proof complexity

With

\overline{x}\lor p\qquad\overline{x}\lor q\qquad x\lor\overline{p}\lor\overline{q}

Extended Frege (and similarly extended resolution) has none of the above properties,
although the reasons are not particularly interesting:

•

Monotonicity fails to hold
since we cannot necessarily derive

from
\Gamma^{\prime}\supseteq\Gamma

\Gamma^{\prime}
.

•

Strong soundness fails to hold

\alpha(x)\neq\alpha(\varphi)
.
Nevertheless, extended Frege is sound
because
\Gamma

\Gamma\cup\{x\leftrightarrow\varphi\}
are equisatisfiable
(i.e.,

is satisfiable if and only if
\Gamma\cup\{x\leftrightarrow\varphi\}
is satisfiable),
seen as follows:
if an assignment
\alpha
satisfies

but falsifies

,
flipping
\alpha(x)
gives a different assignment

satisfying both
\Gamma

x\leftrightarrow\varphi

•

Strong closure under restrictions fails to hold
since otherwise we could choose a partial assignment
that only assigns
x
to
\mathit{True}

and conclude that every formula
\varphi
can be derived from
\Gamma
,
which contradicts the soundness of extended Frege.

In all of the above cases,
the counterexamples rely crucially on the fact that
x
is a new variable.
This ability to abbreviate complex formulas by variables
significantly increases the difficulty of proving lower bounds for extended Frege
and makes it one of the strongest propositional proof systems.
(Extended resolution is equivalent to it over refutations of sets of clauses.)
From this point on, we use “formula” and “set of clauses” interchangeably.

Proof systems that violate the above properties for more sophisticated reasons
(i.e., not simply due to the introduction of new variables)
also exist.
In this paper we compare the proof complexity of four such systems
that augment resolution with inference rules of varying expressiveness.
Given a set
\Gamma
of clauses,
these rules allow deriving clauses
that are not necessarily logically implied by
\Gamma

but whose addition to
\Gamma
preserves satisfiability.
We call such clauses redundant.
Deciding the redundancy of a clause
with respect to a set of clauses is
\mathsf{coDP}
-complete
2
2

2

The class
\mathsf{DP}=\{L_{1}\cap L_{2}\mid L_{1}\in\mathsf{NP},L_{2}\in\mathsf{coNP}\}
,
which is a superset of both
\mathsf{NP}
and
\mathsf{coNP}
,
was defined by Papadimitriou and Yannakakis
[
28
]
.

[
2
]
,
so we consider only the inference rules
that rely on polynomial-time verifiable syntactic conditions
corresponding to restricted versions of redundancy.
These rules may be viewed as capturing the commonly used technique
of making assumptions that hold “without loss of generality”
when writing informal mathematical proofs.
Such assumptions are not logically implied by the hypotheses at hand,
but their use is justified by the fact that they can be eliminated
at the possible cost of an increase in the size of the proof.
The formal rules we study rely on syntactic criteria to justify such assumptions,
with weaker criteria allowing the introduction of stronger assumptions.
In this way, these rules allow us to directly express
various kinds of informal reasoning that are otherwise difficult to formalize.

From the perspective of the broader study of proof complexity,
these systems are somewhat unique
in that Frege does not simulate even the weakest variant
unless Frege and extended Frege are equivalent
[
6
, Corollary 2.5]
.
It would be interesting to determine whether some variant of these systems,
despite having the same limited syntax as resolution and no new variables,
simulates a subsystem of Frege stronger than resolution.

1.2
Related work

1.2.1
Proof complexity

The inference rules we study originate from the notion of blocked clauses,
developed initially by Kullmann
[
22
,
23
]

to give improved deterministic algorithms for 3-SAT.
We call a clause
C
blocked with respect to a set
\Gamma
of clauses
if there exists a literal
p\in C

such that all possible resolvents of
C
on
p
against clauses from
\Gamma

are tautological (i.e., contain a literal and its negation).
Kullmann
[
24
]
showed that blocked clauses are redundant
and thus considered an inference rule that, given a set
\Gamma
of clauses,
allows us to extend
\Gamma
with a clause that is blocked with respect to
\Gamma
.
This rule, along with resolution,
gives the proof system called
blocked clauses

(\mathsf{BC})
.
As illustrated below,
\mathsf{BC}
is not monotonic, not strongly sound,
and not strongly closed under restrictions.

Example 1.1
.

The clause
C=\overline{x}\lor\overline{y}
is blocked
with respect to the set
\Gamma=\{x\lor y,\ x\lor\overline{y}\}
.

•

Monotonicity fails to hold since we cannot derive
C

from
\Gamma^{\prime}=\Gamma\cup\{y\}
in
\mathsf{BC}
:
the set
\Gamma^{\prime}
is satisfiable but
\Gamma^{\prime}\cup\{C\}
is unsatisfiable.

•

Strong soundness fails to hold since
\Gamma
does not imply
C

under assignments that set both
x
and
y
to
\mathit{True}
.

•

Strong closure under restrictions fails to hold
since for an assignment
\alpha
that sets
y
to
\mathit{True}

we cannot derive
C|_{\alpha}=\overline{x}
from
\Gamma|_{\alpha}=\{x\}
in
\mathsf{BC}
:
the set
\Gamma|_{\alpha}
is satisfiable but
\Gamma|_{\alpha}\cup\left\{C|_{\alpha}\right\}
is unsatisfiable.

It is apparent from the definition of a blocked clause
that deleting clauses from
\Gamma
enlarges the set of clauses
that are blocked with respect to
\Gamma
.
With this observation at hand, Kullmann defined a strengthening of
\mathsf{BC}

called
generalized extended resolution
(
\mathsf{GER}
)
that allows the temporary deletion of clauses from
\Gamma
.
Arbitrary deletion of clauses does not necessarily preserve satisfiability;
however, since no subset of a satisfiable
\Gamma
is unsatisfiable,
it is also possible to further strengthen
\mathsf{GER}

by allowing the arbitrary deletion of a clause as a proof step.
The resulting system is called
deletion blocked clauses
(
\mathsf{DBC}
).

Conversely to the above point, the failure of monotonicity becomes particularly important
when deletion is not allowed since it implies that the validity
of blocked clause additions performed in sequence are order dependent.
In particular, not every set of clauses that are all blocked with respect to
\Gamma

can be derived from
\Gamma
by a sequence of blocked clause additions.
For this reason, proving upper bounds for generalizations of
\mathsf{BC}

involves carefully ensuring the validity of sequences of inferences.

Without any additional restrictions,
the above systems all simulate extended resolution
since the clauses in
Eq.

4

can be added in sequence as blocked clauses
if we are allowed to introduce new variables:
starting with a set
\Gamma
of clauses not containing the variable
x
,
we can derive

•

\overline{x}\lor p
followed by
\overline{x}\lor q

since no occurrence of the literal
x
precedes either step
(so both clauses are vacuously blocked), and then

•

x\lor\overline{p}\lor\overline{q}

since its resolvents on
x
against
\overline{x}\lor p
and
\overline{x}\lor q

(i.e., the only preceding occurrences of
\overline{x}
) are tautological.

The study of these systems becomes interesting when we disallow new variables.
A proof of
\Gamma
is
without new variables

if it contains only the variables that already occur in
\Gamma
.
Throughout this paper, we denote a proof system variant
that disallows new variables with the superscript “
-
”
(e.g.,
\mathsf{BC}^{-}
is
\mathsf{BC}
without new variables).
We denote a variant that allows arbitrary deletion with the prefix
\mathsf{D}
.
All of those variants constitute examples of proof systems
that share the peculiarities of extended resolution from

Section

1.1

without being allowed new variables.

Kullmann
[
24
]
proved that extended resolution simulates
\mathsf{GER}
.
He also proved that
\mathsf{GER}^{-}
is exponentially stronger than resolution
and exponentially weaker than extended resolution.
In later work, Järvisalo, Heule, and Biere
[
17
]
defined
a different generalization of
\mathsf{BC}

by essentially replacing “tautological” in the definition of a blocked clause
with “implied by
\Gamma
through unit propagation.”
(Unit propagation is an automatizable but incomplete variant of resolution.)
The result is still a polynomial-time verifiable redundancy criterion
since the only important property of tautologies in the argument
for the redundancy of a blocked clause
C
with respect to
\Gamma

is that tautologies are implied by
\Gamma
.
This generalization is called
resolution asymmetric tautologies
(
\mathsf{RAT}
).
Yet another generalization of
\mathsf{BC}
along a different axis
is called
set-blocked clauses
(
\mathsf{SBC}
),
defined by Kiesl, Seidl, Tompits, and Biere
[
21
]
.
We call a clause
C
set-blocked with respect to a set
\Gamma
of clauses
if there exists some nonempty
L\subseteq C
such that for all
D\in\Gamma

with
D\cap\overline{L}\neq\varnothing
and
D\cap L=\varnothing

the set
\bigl(C\setminus L\bigr)\cup\bigl(D\setminus\overline{L}\bigr)
is tautological.
A blocked clause is the special case where
L
is a singleton,
so set-blockedness expands the scope of the literals in
C
that we consider.
Deciding the set-blockedness of a clause
with respect to a set of clauses is
\mathsf{NP}
-complete
[
21
]
.
To ensure that an
\mathsf{SBC}
proof is polynomial-time verifiable,
every step in the proof that adds a clause
C
as set-blocked
is expected to indicate the subset
L\subseteq C
for which
C
is set-blocked.
With that said, to reduce clutter, we leave this requirement out of our definitions
and indicate those subsets only informally throughout this paper.

Subsequent works
[
15
,
19
]
defined further generalizations,
showed simulations between some variants, and
gave polynomial-size proofs (without new variables)
of the pigeonhole principle in a variant
called
set propagation redundancy
(
\mathsf{SPR}^{-}
)
that combines
\mathsf{SBC}^{-}
and
\mathsf{RAT}^{-}
.
Recently, Buss and Thapen
[
6
]
initiated a systematic study
of the proof complexity of the many generalizations of
\mathsf{BC}^{-}
.
Among other results, they showed that
the bit pigeonhole principle, parity principle, clique-coloring principle,
and Tseitin tautologies have polynomial-size
\mathsf{SPR}^{-}
proofs.
They also showed that
\mathsf{SPR}^{-}
can undo (with polynomial-size derivations)
the effects of or-ification, xor-ification, and lifting with index gadgets.
In view of these results,
\mathsf{SPR}^{-}
appears to be surprisingly strong.
3
3

3

As remarked by Buss and Thapen
[
6
, Section 4]
,
the apparent strength of
\mathsf{SPR}^{-}
stems from the ability to exploit symmetries,
which are abundant in the combinatorial principles used for
proving lower bounds against the commonly studied proof systems.
Other interesting examples of systems that easily prove such combinatorial principles
are the variants of Krishnamurthy’s
symmetric resolution

[
20
,
32
,
1
,
30
]
,
obtained by augmenting resolution with rules
that explicitly support reasoning about symmetries.

Buss and Thapen also proved an exponential size lower bound for
\mathsf{RAT}^{-}
,
separating
\mathsf{DRAT}^{-}
and
\mathsf{SPR}^{-}
from it.
Superpolynomial lower bounds for
\mathsf{SPR}^{-}
or even
\mathsf{SBC}^{-}
are currently open.

1.2.2
SAT solving

As the use of SAT solvers in propositional theorem proving increased,
it became standard to expect a solver to produce a proof alongside an unsatisfiability claim.
Modern SAT solvers are based on conflict-driven clause learning (CDCL)
[
26
]

and essentially search for resolution proofs.
As a result, the initial proof systems developed to help verify
the outputs of CDCL SAT solvers were based on resolution
[
12
,
33
]
.
However, most of the current SAT solvers go beyond CDCL
and employ an array of
inprocessing techniques

[
17
]

that transform the formula during the search.
These techniques are often not strongly sound,
and resolution falls short for expressing them.
Järvisalo, Heule, and Biere
[
17
]
observed
that
\mathsf{DRAT}
simulated all of the common techniques used at the time,
and, following the implementation of a practical verifier
[
34
]
,

\mathsf{DRAT}
became the de facto standard proof system used in SAT solvers.
Extended resolution could also be used for verification; however,
it is only known to simulate
\mathsf{DRAT}
with polynomial overhead
[
19
, Section 4.5]
,
whereas
\mathsf{DRAT}
simulates extended resolution with no overhead.
There are also a few examples of
\mathsf{DRAT}
enabling significant gains
over the smallest known extended resolution proofs (see, e.g.,
[
19
, Table 1]
),
which is important for practical purposes.

Another practical motivation for studying these systems is
their potential usefulness in proof search
due to the surprising strength of the variants without new variables.
Recent works have introduced a SAT solving paradigm
called
satisfaction-driven clause learning
(SDCL)
[
16
,
14
]

that can fully automatically discover small proofs of the pigeonhole principle.
Its usefulness remains limited, though,
since it was observed to improve upon CDCL only on specific classes of formulas.
Exploiting the power of these systems might be a promising avenue
for the research that aims to improve the performance of practical SAT solvers.
To this end, it is important to understand the relative strengths of these systems.

We prove some results concerning
the relative strengths of
\mathsf{BC}^{-}
,
\mathsf{RAT}^{-}
,
\mathsf{SBC}^{-}
, and
\mathsf{GER}^{-}
,

\mathsf{BC}^{-}

Fig.

surrounding these systems after our results.

Our first main result is a two-way separation between
\mathsf{RAT}^{-}
and
\mathsf{GER}^{-}
.

Theorem 1.2
.

There exists an infinite sequence
(\Gamma_{n})_{n=1}^{\infty}
of formulas
such that
\Gamma_{n}
admits
\mathsf{RAT}^{-}
proofs of size
n^{O(1)}

but requires
\mathsf{GER}^{-}
proofs of size
2^{\Omega(n)}
.
Conversely, there exists an infinite sequence
(\Delta_{n})_{n=1}^{\infty}
of formulas
such that
\Delta_{n}
admits
\mathsf{GER}^{-}
proofs of size
n^{O(1)}

but requires
\mathsf{RAT}^{-}
proofs of size
2^{\Omega(n)}
.

Since both
\mathsf{RAT}^{-}
and
\mathsf{GER}^{-}
are generalizations of
\mathsf{BC}^{-}
,
the above result also separates both systems from
\mathsf{BC}^{-}
.
It was already understood that
\mathsf{GER}^{-}
is
between
\mathsf{BC}^{-}
and
\mathsf{DBC}^{-}
in strength,
and
\mathsf{GER}^{-}
is in fact “strictly” between
\mathsf{BC}^{-}
and
\mathsf{DBC}^{-}

by
Theorem

1.2
.

For both directions of the separation, we follow a strategy
that exploits the equivalence of
\mathsf{BC}^{-}
(without new variables)
to extended resolution under
effective simulations

[
13
,
27
]
,
which allow the translation of the formula (in a satisfiability-preserving way)
along with the proof when moving between proof systems.
In particular, Buss and Thapen
[
6
]

observed that it is possible to incorporate new variables
into a formula
\Gamma
in such a way that
\mathsf{BC}^{-}
,
while still technically only using the variables occurring in the formula,
simulates an extended resolution proof of
\Gamma
.

Lemma 1.3

(
[
6
, Lemma 2.2]
)
.

Suppose that a formula
\Gamma
has an extended resolution proof of size
m

and that
\Gamma
and the set
X=\{y\lor x_{1}\lor\dots\lor x_{m},\ y\}
of clauses
have no variables in common.
Then
\Gamma\cup X
has a
\mathsf{BC}^{-}
proof of size
O(m)
.

To separate
\mathsf{RAT}^{-}
and
\mathsf{GER}^{-}
, we incorporate new variables into formulas
in ways that are useful to only one of the two systems.
We achieve this by “guarding” the new variables by clauses
instead of providing them as in
Lemma

1.3
.
Recall that, for a clause to be redundant with respect to a formula
according to some syntactic criterion in this paper,
every clause in the formula has to satisfy a certain condition.
We take advantage of this fact to include the new variables
within a strategically chosen set
\mathcal{X}
of guard clauses
alongside an unsatisfiable formula
\Gamma
.
With a suitable choice of
\mathcal{X}
, we are able to impose
enough limitations upon the redundant clauses derivable in a system
to ensure that the new variables can essentially be ignored in a proof of
\Gamma\cup\mathcal{X}
.

For each direction of the separation,
when proving the upper bound for one of the two systems,
we show that it can efficiently work through the guard clauses
and use the new variables to simulate the extended resolution proof.
When proving the lower bound for the other system, we show essentially
that it is closed under restrictions
4
4

4

We use closure under restrictions here in the quantitative sense
described in
Section

1
.

for the specific formulas
and partial assignments that we construct.
(Neither system is closed under restrictions in general.)
In other words, the guard clauses make it impossible
for the system to efficiently “access” the new variables,
thus preventing it from achieving any speedup.
This allows us to use the existing separations of extended resolution
from
\mathsf{RAT}^{-}
and
\mathsf{GER}^{-}
to separate the two systems
without needing to prove lower bounds entirely from scratch.
The main difficulty is in coming up with the appropriate ways of
incorporating new variables into formulas.

As our next main result, we separate
\mathsf{SBC}^{-}
from
\mathsf{RAT}^{-}

(and hence
\mathsf{BC}^{-}
) with the same strategy.
In fact, we reuse the formulas separating
\mathsf{GER}^{-}
from
\mathsf{RAT}^{-}

and show that
\mathsf{SBC}^{-}
can also efficiently work through the guard clauses in them,
although in a different manner than
\mathsf{GER}^{-}
.

Theorem 1.4
.

There exists an infinite sequence
(\Gamma_{n})_{n=1}^{\infty}
of formulas
such that
\Gamma_{n}
admits
\mathsf{SBC}^{-}
proofs of size
n^{O(1)}

but requires
\mathsf{RAT}^{-}
proofs of size
2^{\Omega(n)}
.

Finally, we give polynomial-size
\mathsf{SBC}^{-}
proofs of the pigeonhole principle,
which exponentially separates
\mathsf{SBC}^{-}
from
\mathsf{GER}^{-}

by a lower bound due to Kullmann
[
24
, Lemma 9.4]
.

Theorem 1.5
.

There exists an infinite sequence
(\Gamma_{n})_{n=1}^{\infty}
of formulas
such that
\Gamma_{n}
admits
\mathsf{SBC}^{-}
proofs of size
n^{O(1)}

but requires
\mathsf{GER}^{-}
proofs of size
2^{\Omega(n)}
.

Along the way to our main results, we prove a partial simulation of
\mathsf{RAT}^{-}
by
\mathsf{BC}^{-}
.
It is partial in the sense that the size of the produced
\mathsf{BC}^{-}
proof
is not always a polynomial in the size of the
\mathsf{RAT}^{-}
proof
(which is impossible due to
Theorem

1.2
).
It also has the property that, although the produced proof may sometimes be small,
the simulation cannot necessarily be carried out
in time polynomial in the size of the produced proof.
This is because the simulation involves generating satisfying assignments
to certain formulas obtained in the process.
To our knowledge, all of the “natural” simulations
between the commonly studied proof systems are efficient,
so the partial simulation of
\mathsf{RAT}^{-}
by
\mathsf{BC}^{-}
is an odd example.
Another notable aspect of the simulation
is that it directly informed the construction of the formulas
that we use for separating
\mathsf{RAT}^{-}
from
\mathsf{GER}^{-}
.
We discuss this further at the end of
Section

4
.
Due to the technical nature of the simulation, we do not state it here in detail.

1.4
Open questions

We leave open the following.

Question 1.6
.

Is
\mathsf{RAT}^{-}
exponentially separated from
\mathsf{SBC}^{-}
?

Question 1.7
.

Is
\mathsf{GER}^{-}
exponentially separated from
\mathsf{SBC}^{-}
?

Answering these questions will complete the picture
of the relative strengths of the weakest generalizations of
\mathsf{BC}^{-}

along each axis in
Fig.

1
.
However, we do not even have any superpolynomial lower bounds for
\mathsf{SBC}^{-}
.
If a separation of extended resolution from
\mathsf{SBC}^{-}
is shown,
it might be possible to relatively easily separate

\mathsf{RAT}^{-}
and
\mathsf{GER}^{-}
from
\mathsf{SBC}^{-}

by tailoring guarded extension variables that
\mathsf{SBC}^{-}
cannot access
(in the manner of the current paper).

As an aside, the formulas that we use for the separations in this paper
are arguably “artificial” in that they do not encode any combinatorial principles.
Separations with “natural” formulas give more intuitive insight
into the relative capabilities of the proof systems being considered,
so it is desirable to reprove
Theorems

1.2
and
1.4

using formulas that encode some combinatorial principles.
On the other hand, those artificial formulas enable
relatively simple and modular proofs of the separations.
An interesting open question is whether our strategy
of separating two proof systems
P
and
Q
,
both of which effectively simulate a strong system
R

through syntactic manipulations of formulas
that separate
R
from
P
and

5
5

5

A slightly similar strategy gives a separation of linear resolution
from tree-like and regular resolution
(and some other variants of resolution)

3

Another desirable goal is to establish tighter connections
between the more commonly studied proof systems
and the generalizations of
\mathsf{BC}^{-}
.
As mentioned earlier, Frege does not simulate
\mathsf{BC}^{-}

unless it also simulates extended Frege.
Additionally, it is already known
that bounded-depth Frege does not simulate
\mathsf{BC}^{-}

We naturally wonder about the converse direction.

Question 1.8
.

Is there a subsystem of Frege stronger than resolution that

2.1
Propositional logic

For notation we mostly follow Buss and Thapen
[
6
]
.

We use
0
and
1
to denote
\mathit{False}
and
\mathit{True}
, respectively.
A

is a propositional variable or its negation.
A set of literals is
tautological

if it contains a pair of complementary literals

and
\overline{x}
.
A

is the disjunction of a nontautological set of literals.
We denote by
\mathbf{V}
,
\mathbf{L}
, and
\mathbf{C}

respectively the sets of all variables, all literals, and all clauses.
A
conjunctive normal form formula
(CNF) is a conjunction of clauses.
We identify clauses with sets of literals and CNFs with sets of clauses.
In the rest of this section we use
C
,
D
to denote clauses
and
\Gamma
,
\Delta
to denote CNFs.

When we know
C\cup D
to be nontautological, we write it as
C\lor D
.
We write
C\mathbin{\dot{\lor}}D
to indicate a
disjoint disjunction
,
where
C
and
D
have no variables in common.
We sometimes write
\Gamma\cup\{C\}
as
\Gamma\land C
.

We denote by
\var(\Gamma)
the set of all the variables occurring in
\Gamma
.
We say
C

subsumes

D
, denoted
C\sqsupseteq D
, if
C\subseteq D
.
For CNFs, we say
\Gamma
subsumes
\Delta
, denoted
\Gamma\sqsupseteq\Delta
,
if for all
D\in\Delta
there exists some
C\in\Gamma
such that
C\sqsupseteq D
.
We take the disjunction of a clause and a CNF as

C\lor\Delta\coloneqq\{C\lor D\mid D\in\Delta\text{ and }C\cup D\text{ is nontautological}\}.

We say
\Gamma
and
\Delta
are
equisatisfiable
, denoted
\Gamma\equiv_{\mathrm{sat}}\Delta
,
if they are either both satisfiable or both unsatisfiable.
With respect to
\Gamma
, a clause
C
is
redundant

if
\Gamma\setminus\{C\}\equiv_{\mathrm{sat}}\Gamma\equiv_{\mathrm{sat}}\Gamma\cup\{C\}
.

A
partial assignment

\alpha
is a partial function
\alpha\colon\mathbf{V}\rightharpoonup\{0,1\}
,
which also acts on literals by letting
\alpha(\overline{x})\coloneqq\overline{\alpha(x)}
.
We identify
\alpha
with the set
\{p\in\mathbf{L}\mid\alpha(p)=1\}
,
consisting of all the literals it satisfies.
For a set
L
of literals, we let
\overline{L}\coloneqq\{\overline{x}\mid x\in L\}
.
In particular, we use
\overline{C}
to denote the smallest partial assignment
that falsifies all the literals in
C
.
We say
\alpha

satisfies

C
, denoted
\alpha\models C
,
if there exists some
p\in C
such that
\alpha(p)=1
.
We say
\alpha
satisfies
\Gamma
if for all
C\in\Gamma
we have
\alpha\models C
.
For
C

\alpha
does not satisfy,
the

of

under

C|_{\alpha}\coloneqq C\setminus\{p\in C\mid\alpha(p)=0\}.

Extending the above to CNFs, the restriction of

\Gamma|_{\alpha}\coloneqq\{C|_{\alpha}\mid C\in\Gamma\text{ and }\alpha\not\models C\}.

2.2
Proof complexity

We recall the definition of a proof system
(in the sense of Cook and Reckhow
[
11
]
)
and the basic notions of proof complexity,
which can also be found in the recent textbook by Krajíček
[
18
, Chapter 1]
.

In the rest of this section we think of
\Gamma
as a formula and
\Pi
as a proof,
each encoded by a string over some finite alphabet.

Definition 2.1
.

A
proof system
is a polynomial-time computable binary relation
P

such that the following hold.

•

Soundness:
For all
\Gamma
and
\Pi
,
if
P(\Gamma,\Pi)
holds then
\Gamma
is unsatisfiable.

•

Completeness:
For all unsatisfiable
\Gamma
,
there exists some
\Pi
such that
P(\Gamma,\Pi)
holds.

We call any
\Pi
satisfying
P(\Gamma,\Pi)
a
P
-proof
of
\Gamma
.

Proof complexity is concerned with the sizes (or lengths) of proofs.
6
6

6

The abstract definition of proof (or formula) size
is based on the length of the string that encodes the object;
however, when studying concrete proof systems
it is standard to use coarser notions of size.
For a resolution proof, the common definition of size is the number of steps.
It is tacitly understood that a minimum-size resolution proof
can be encoded by a string of length polynomial in the size of the proof.

For a proof system
P
and a formula
\Gamma
,
we define

\size_{P}(\Gamma)\coloneqq\min\{\lvert\Pi\rvert\mid\Pi\text{ is a $P$-proof of }\Gamma\}

if
\Gamma
is unsatisfiable and
\size_{P}(\Gamma)\coloneqq\infty
otherwise.

Definition 2.2
.

A proof system
P

simulates

Q

if for all unsatisfiable
\Gamma
we have

\size_{P}(\Gamma)=\size_{Q}(\Gamma)^{O(1)}.

Additionally,
P

polynomially simulates

Q

if there exists a polynomial-time algorithm
for converting a
Q
-proof of
\Gamma
into a
P
-proof of
\Gamma
.

Definition 2.3
.

Proof systems
P
and
Q
are
equivalent

if they simulate each other.
Additionally,
P
and
Q
are
polynomially equivalent

if they polynomially simulate each other.

We say
P
is
exponentially separated
from
Q

if there exists some sequence
\langle\Gamma\rangle
of formulas
such that
\size_{P}(\Gamma_{n})=n^{O(1)}
while
\size_{Q}(\Gamma_{n})=2^{\Omega(n)}
.
We call such
\langle\Gamma\rangle

easy
for
P
and
hard
for
Q
.

2.3
Resolution

Definition 2.4
.

The
resolution rule
is

A\mathbin{\dot{\lor}}x

B\mathbin{\dot{\lor}}\overline{x}

A\lor B

,

where
A
,
B
are clauses and
x
is a variable.
We call
A\lor B
the
resolvent
of
A\lor x
and
B\lor\overline{x}
on
x
.

Definition 2.5
.

The
weakening rule
is

A

A\lor B

,

where
A
and
B
are clauses.
We call
A\lor B
a
weakening
of
A
.

We define a resolution proof in a slightly different form than usual:
as a sequence of CNFs instead of a sequence of clauses.

Definition 2.6
.

A
resolution proof
of a CNF
\Gamma

is a sequence
\Pi=(\Gamma_{1},\dots,\Gamma_{N}
) of CNFs such that
\Gamma_{1}=\Gamma
,
\bot\in\Gamma_{N}
,
and, for all
i\in[N-1]
, we have
\Gamma_{i+1}=\Gamma_{i}\cup\{C\}
,
where

•

C
is a resolvent of two clauses
D,E\in\Gamma_{i}
or

•

C
is a weakening of some clause
D\in\Gamma_{i}
.

The size of
\Pi
is
N
.

We write
\mathsf{Res}
to denote the resolution proof system.
A well known fact is that resolution proofs are preserved under restrictions:
if
(\Gamma_{1},\Gamma_{2},\dots,\Gamma_{N})
is a resolution proof of
\Gamma
,
then, for every partial assignment
\alpha
,
the sequence
(\Gamma_{1}|_{\alpha},\Gamma_{2}|_{\alpha},\dots,\Gamma_{N}|_{\alpha})
contains a resolution proof of
\Gamma|_{\alpha}
.
This implies in particular the following.

Lemma 2.7
.

For every CNF
\Gamma
and every partial assignment
\alpha
, we have

\size_{\mathsf{Res}}(\Gamma|_{\alpha})\leq\size_{\mathsf{Res}}(\Gamma).

We next define a weakened version of resolution
that comes up often in the study of decision algorithms for satisfiability.

Definition 2.8
.

A
unit propagation proof
is a resolution proof
where each use of the resolution rule is of the form

A\mathbin{\dot{\lor}}x

\overline{x}

A

.

Unit propagation is not complete.
With
\Gamma
,
\Delta
CNFs and
L=\{p_{1},\dots,p_{k}\}
a set of literals,
we define
\Gamma\land\overline{L}\coloneqq\Gamma\land\overline{p_{1}}\land\dots\land\overline{p_{k}}

and write

•

\Gamma\vdash_{1}\bot
to denote that there exists a unit propagation proof of
\Gamma
,

•

\Gamma\vdash_{1}L
to denote
\left(\Gamma\land\overline{L}\right)\vdash_{1}\bot
,

•

\Gamma\vdash_{1}\Delta
to denote that for all
D\in\Delta
we have
\Gamma\vdash_{1}D
.

Note that
\Gamma\vdash_{1}\Delta
implies
\Gamma\models\Delta
.
Moreover, whether
\Gamma\vdash_{1}\Delta
holds can be decided in polynomial time.
This makes it useful as a component in defining inference rules.

As in the case of resolution, unit propagation proofs are preserved under restrictions.

Lemma 2.9
.

For every CNF
\Gamma
, every set
L
of literals, and every partial assignment
\alpha

such that
\alpha\not\models L
, if
\Gamma\vdash_{1}L
, then
\Gamma|_{\alpha}\vdash_{1}L|_{\alpha}
.

Proof.

Suppose that
\left(\Gamma\land\overline{L}\right)\vdash_{1}\bot
.
Since unit propagation proofs are preserved under restrictions,
we have
\left.\kern-1.2pt\left(\Gamma\land\overline{L}\right)\right|_{\alpha}\vdash_{1}\bot
.
Unpacking the formula on the left-hand side gives

\Gamma|_{\alpha}\land\left(\overline{L}\setminus\alpha\right)\vdash_{1}\bot.

(5)

(We write
\overline{L}\setminus\alpha
instead of
\overline{L}|_{\alpha}

since
\alpha
may satisfy
\overline{L}
.)
Noting that
\alpha\not\models L
, we have
\overline{L}\setminus\alpha=\overline{L|_{\alpha}}
.
Along with
Eq.

5
,
this implies that
\Gamma|_{\alpha}\vdash_{1}L|_{\alpha}
.
∎

From this point on, we discuss some strengthenings of the resolution proof system.

Definition 2.10
.

Let
\Gamma
be a CNF and
p
,
q
be arbitrary literals.
Consider a
new
variable
x

(i.e., not occurring in any one of
\Gamma
,
p
,
q
).
We call

\{\overline{x}\lor p,\ \overline{x}\lor q,\ x\lor\overline{p}\lor\overline{q}\}

a set of
extension clauses
for
\Gamma
.
In this context, we refer to
x
as the
extension variable
.

Definition 2.11
.

A CNF
\Lambda
is an
extension
for a CNF
\Gamma

if there exists a sequence
(\lambda_{1},\dots,\lambda_{t})
such that
\Lambda=\bigcup_{i=1}^{t}\lambda_{i}
,
and, for all
i\in[t]
,
we have that
\lambda_{i}
is a set of extension clauses for
\Gamma\cup\bigcup_{j=1}^{i-1}\lambda_{j}
.

Definition 2.12
.

An
extended resolution proof
of a CNF
\Gamma

is a pair
(\Lambda,\Pi)
,
where
\Lambda
is an extension for
\Gamma

and
\Pi
is a resolution proof of
\Gamma\cup\Lambda
.
The size of
(\Lambda,\Pi)
is defined to be
\lvert\Lambda\rvert+\lvert\Pi\rvert
.

We write
\mathsf{ER}
to denote the extended resolution proof system.

3
Inference rules

We recall the redundancy criteria that lead to the inference rules
we use to augment resolution proofs.
The definitions are adapted from previous works
[
24
,
17
,
21
,
15
,
6
]
.

Definition 3.1
.

A clause
C=p\mathbin{\dot{\lor}}C^{\prime}

is a
blocked clause
(BC) for
p
with respect to a CNF
\Gamma

if, for every clause
D
of the form
\overline{p}\mathbin{\dot{\lor}}D^{\prime}
in
\Gamma
,
the set
C^{\prime}\cup D^{\prime}
is tautological.

A strict generalization of the notion of a blocked clause
is a resolution asymmetric tautology, defined as follows.

Definition 3.2
.

A clause
C=p\mathbin{\dot{\lor}}C^{\prime}

is a
resolution asymmetric tautology
(RAT) for
p
with respect to a CNF
\Gamma

if, for every clause
D
of the form
\overline{p}\mathbin{\dot{\lor}}D^{\prime}
in
\Gamma
,
we have
\Gamma\vdash_{1}C^{\prime}\cup D^{\prime}
.

Another strict generalization of a blocked clause is a set-blocked clause.
7
7

7

We define a set-blocked clause in a slightly different,
although equivalent, way compared with the original
[
21
, Definition 4.1]
.

Definition 3.3
.

A clause
C
is a
set-blocked clause
(SBC) for a nonempty
L\subseteq C
with respect to a CNF
\Gamma

if, for every clause
D\in\Gamma
with
D\cap\overline{L}\neq\varnothing
and
D\cap L=\varnothing
,
the set
\bigl(C\setminus L\bigr)\cup\bigl(D\setminus\overline{L}\bigr)
is tautological.

We say
C
is a BC with respect to
\Gamma

if there exists a literal
p\in C

for which
C
is a BC with respect to
\Gamma
, and similarly for RAT and SBC.
Note that the above definitions do not prohibit
BCs, RATs, or SBCs with respect to
\Gamma
from containing variables not occurring in
\Gamma
.

It was shown by Kullmann
[
24
]
, Järvisalo, Heule, and Biere
[
17
]
,
and Kiesl, Seidl, Tompits, and Biere
[
21
]

that BCs, RATs, and SBCs are redundant,
which makes it possible to use them to define proof systems.

Theorem 3.4
.

If a clause
C
is a BC, RAT, or SBC with respect to a CNF
\Gamma
,
then
\Gamma\setminus\{C\}\equiv_{\mathrm{sat}}\Gamma\equiv_{\mathrm{sat}}\Gamma\cup\{C\}
.

Definition 3.5
.

A
blocked clauses proof
of a CNF
\Gamma

is a sequence
\Pi=(\Gamma_{1},\dots,\Gamma_{N})
of CNFs such that
\Gamma_{1}=\Gamma
,
\bot\in\Gamma_{N}
,
and, for all
i\in[N-1]
, we have
\Gamma_{i+1}=\Gamma_{i}\cup\{C\}
,
where either

•

C
is a resolvent of two clauses
D,E\in\Gamma_{i}
,

•

C
is a weakening of some clause
D\in\Gamma_{i}
, or

•

C
is a blocked clause with respect to
\Gamma_{i}
.

The size of
\Pi
is
N
.

We write
\mathsf{BC}
to denote the blocked clauses proof system.
8
8

8

In some earlier works,
\mathsf{BC}
and its generalizations are defined
as augmentations of the
reverse unit propagation
(
\mathsf{RUP}
) proof system
[
33
]
instead of resolution.

\mathsf{RUP}
has a single inference rule,
which allows adding a clause
C
to a CNF
\Gamma
if
\Gamma\vdash_{1}C
.
Resolution simulates
\mathsf{RUP}
with an overhead
linear in the number of variables (by
Lemma

3.16
),
and the main results in this paper are not affected by the differences in the definitions.

Replacing “blocked clause” by “resolution asymmetric tautology”
in the above definition
gives the resolution asymmetric tautologies proof system,
which we denote by
\mathsf{RAT}
.
Replacing it by “set-blocked clause” gives the set-blocked clauses proof system,
which we denote by
\mathsf{SBC}
.

\mathsf{RAT}
and
\mathsf{SBC}
are two generalizations of
\mathsf{BC}
, and we now define another,
designed to overcome the dependence of the validity of
\mathsf{BC}
inferences
on the order of clause additions
[see
24
, Section 1.3]
.

For a CNF
\Gamma
and a set
V
of variables, we let

\B_{V}(\Gamma)\coloneqq\{C\in\mathbf{C}\mid C\text{ is a BC for a literal of some }x\in V\text{ with respect to }\Gamma\}.

We also let

•

\B(\Gamma)\coloneqq\B_{\mathbf{V}}(\Gamma)
,

•

\inB(\Gamma)\coloneqq\B(\Gamma)\cap\Gamma
,

•

\B^{-}_{V}(\Gamma)\coloneqq\{C\in\B_{V}(\Gamma)\mid\var(C)\subseteq\var(\Gamma)\}
,

•

\B^{-}(\Gamma)\coloneqq\B^{-}_{\var(\Gamma)}(\Gamma)
.

Before proceeding, we observe the below result,
which follows immediately from the definition of a blocked clause.

Lemma 3.6
.

For all CNFs
\Gamma
and
\Delta
such that
\Gamma\subseteq\Delta
, we have
\B(\Gamma)\supseteq\B(\Delta)
.

Thus, we may assume without loss of generality that
all of the blocked clause additions in a
\mathsf{BC}
proof
are performed before any resolution steps.
(A similar assumption does not necessarily hold for
\mathsf{RAT}
proofs.)

Definition 3.7
.

A sequence
(C_{1},\dots,C_{m})
of some clauses from a CNF
\Gamma

is a
maximal blocked sequence
for
\Gamma
if

•

for all
i\in[m]
the clause
C_{i}

is blocked with respect to
\Gamma\setminus\bigcup_{j=1}^{i-1}\{C_{j}\}
and

•

\inB\left(\Gamma\setminus\bigcup_{i=1}^{m}\{C_{i}\}\right)
is empty.

For a CNF
\Gamma
, a maximal blocked sequence is unique
up to the ordering of its clauses
[
24
, Lemma 6.1]
,
which makes the following notion well defined.

Definition 3.8
.

Let
(C_{1},\dots,C_{m})
be a maximal blocked sequence for a CNF
\Gamma
.
The
kernel
of
\Gamma
is

\ker(\Gamma)\coloneqq\Gamma\setminus\bigcup_{i=1}^{m}\{C_{i}\}.

Definition 3.9
.

A CNF
\Lambda
is a
blocked extension
for a CNF
\Gamma
if
\ker(\Gamma\cup\Lambda)=\ker(\Gamma)
.

Definition 3.10
.

A
generalized extended resolution proof
of a CNF
\Gamma

is a pair
(\Lambda,\Pi)
,
where
\Lambda
is a blocked extension for
\Gamma

and
\Pi
is a resolution proof of
\Gamma\cup\Lambda
.
The size of
(\Lambda,\Pi)
is defined to be
\lvert\Lambda\rvert+\lvert\Pi\rvert
.

We write
\mathsf{GER}
to denote the generalized extended resolution proof system.
The relationship between
\mathsf{GER}
and
\mathsf{BC}
is made clear
by the following characterization of blocked extensions.

Lemma 3.11

(
[
24
, Lemma 6.5]
)
.

A CNF
\Lambda
is a blocked extension for a CNF
\Gamma

if and only if there exists a CNF
\Gamma^{\prime}\subseteq\Gamma

and an ordering
(C_{1},\dots,C_{m})

of all the clauses in
\Lambda\cup(\Gamma\setminus\Gamma^{\prime})

such that for all
i\in[m]

the clause
C_{i}
is blocked with respect to
\Gamma^{\prime}\cup\bigcup_{j=1}^{i-1}\{C_{j}\}
.

This result gives a view of
\mathsf{GER}
as a version of
\mathsf{BC}

that allows the
temporary
deletion of clauses from the initial formula
(i.e., clauses can be deleted as long as they are added back later).

In this paper, we study the variants of
\mathsf{BC}
,
\mathsf{RAT}
,
\mathsf{SBC}
, and
\mathsf{GER}

that disallow the use of new variables.
We say that a proof of a CNF
\Gamma
is
without new variables

if all the variables occurring in the proof are in
\var(\Gamma)
.
In the case of
\mathsf{GER}
, this constraint applies to the blocked extension.
We use
\mathsf{BC}^{-}
,
\mathsf{RAT}^{-}
,
\mathsf{SBC}^{-}
, and
\mathsf{GER}^{-}
to denote the variants without new variables.

3.1
Useful facts

We conclude this section with a few standalone results
that we will refer back to later.

Lemma 3.12
.

For every CNF
\Gamma
such that
\ker(\Gamma)=\Gamma
, we have

\size_{\mathsf{GER}^{-}}(\Gamma)=\size_{\mathsf{BC}^{-}}(\Gamma).

Proof.

Since
\mathsf{GER}^{-}
is a generalization of
\mathsf{BC}^{-}
,
the inequality
\size_{\mathsf{GER}^{-}}(\Gamma)\leq\size_{\mathsf{BC}^{-}}(\Gamma)
immediately holds for all
\Gamma
.
For the other direction, suppose that
\ker(\Gamma)=\Gamma

and that
\Lambda
is a blocked extension for
\Gamma
.
We claim that the clauses in
\Lambda
can be derived in sequence from
\Gamma
in
\mathsf{BC}^{-}
.
By the definition of a blocked extension, we have
\ker(\Gamma\cup\Lambda)=\ker(\Gamma)=\Gamma
.
Then, by the definition of a kernel, we can order the clauses in
\Lambda

into a maximal blocked sequence
(C_{1},\dots,C_{m})
for
\Gamma
.
Finally, we can derive these clauses from
\Gamma
in the reverse order
(C_{m},\dots,C_{1})

by a sequence of blocked clause additions.
∎

Lemma 3.13
.

For every CNF
\Gamma
, we have

\size_{\mathsf{BC}^{-}}(\Gamma)\geq\size_{\mathsf{Res}}(\Gamma\cup\B^{-}(\Gamma)).

Proof.

Let
\Pi
be a
\mathsf{BC}^{-}
proof of
\Gamma
, and let
\beta
be the set of blocked clauses added in
\Pi
.
Due to
Lemma

3.6
, we can rearrange
\Pi
as a resolution proof of
\Gamma\cup\beta
.
Then, since adding clauses to a CNF cannot increase the size of its shortest resolution proof,
and since
\beta\subseteq\B^{-}(\Gamma)
, the result follows.
∎

Definition 3.14
.

The
projection
of a CNF
\Gamma
onto a literal
p
is the CNF

\proj_{p}(\Gamma)\coloneqq\{C\setminus\{p\}\mid C\in\Gamma\text{ and }p\in C\}.

This definition plays a role
in both our (partial) simulation of
\mathsf{RAT}^{-}
by
\mathsf{BC}^{-}

and our
\mathsf{GER}^{-}
lower bounds.
In particular, we use the following fact,
which was already observed by Kullmann
[see
24
, Section 4]
.

Lemma 3.15
.

A clause
C=p\mathbin{\dot{\lor}}C^{\prime}
is a BC for
p
with respect to a CNF
\Gamma

if and only if the partial assignment
\overline{C^{\prime}}
satisfies
\proj_{\overline{p}}(\Gamma)
.

Proof.

By the definition of a blocked clause,
a clause
C=p\mathbin{\dot{\lor}}C^{\prime}
is a BC for
p
with respect to
\Gamma

if and only if for all
D\in\proj_{\overline{p}}(\Gamma)
the set
C^{\prime}\cup D
is tautological.
Since neither of
C^{\prime}
and
D
is tautological,
C^{\prime}\cup D
is tautological
if and only if
\overline{C^{\prime}}\cap D\neq\varnothing
, which is equivalent to
\overline{C^{\prime}}\models D
.
∎

The next result is essentially due to Chang
[
9
, Theorem 1]
.
Although its original form is slightly weaker,
the exact statement below can be obtained
by a modification of Chang’s proof.
We provide its proof in
Appendix

A

for the sake of completeness.

Lemma 3.16
.

For every CNF
\Gamma
and every clause
C
such that
\Gamma\vdash_{1}C
,
there exists a resolution derivation
(\Gamma_{1},\dots,\Gamma_{N})
with
N\leq\lvert\var(\Gamma)\rvert+1

such that
\Gamma_{1}=\Gamma
,
C\in\Gamma_{N}
, and
\Gamma\cup\{C\}\sqsupseteq\Gamma_{N}
.

The following gives a simple condition under which we regain monotonicity.

Lemma 3.17

(
[
6
, Lemma 1.20]
)
.

Let
\Gamma
and
\Delta
be CNFs such that
\Gamma\subseteq\Delta
and
\Gamma\sqsupseteq\Delta
.
If a clause is a BC, RAT, or SBC with respect to
\Gamma
,
then it is a BC, RAT, or SBC with respect to
\Delta
.

4
Partial simulation of
\mathsf{RAT}^{-}
by
\mathsf{BC}^{-}

We will show how to convert a RAT addition
into a sequence of BC additions and resolution steps.
Assume that all the BCs and RATs in this section are without new variables.

Definition 4.1
.

The
nonblocking CNF
of a clause
C
for a literal
p\in C

with respect to a CNF
\Gamma
is

\NB^{\Gamma}_{p}(C)\coloneqq\left\{D\setminus C\mathrel{}\middle|\mathrel{}D\in\proj_{\overline{p}}(\Gamma)\text{ and }(C\setminus\{p\})\cup D\text{ is nontautological}\right\}.

As a consequence of the above definition,
we have
\var\left(\NB^{\Gamma}_{p}(C)\right)\cap\var(C)=\varnothing
.

We say an assignment
\alpha

minimally satisfies
a CNF
\Gamma
, denoted
\alpha\models_{\mathrm{min}}\Gamma
,
if
\alpha
satisfies
\Gamma
while no proper subset
\alpha^{\prime}\subsetneq\alpha
satisfies
\Gamma
.
We let

\mu(\Gamma)\coloneqq\left\{E\in\mathbf{C}\mathrel{}\middle|\mathrel{}\overline{E}\models_{\mathrm{min}}\Gamma\right\}.

Since two different minimally satisfying assignments cannot contain one another,
no clause
E\in\mu(\Gamma)
is contained in a different clause
E^{\prime}\in\mu(\Gamma)
.

Example 4.2
.

Let
\Gamma=\{x,\ y\lor\overline{z}\}
.
This CNF has two minimally satisfying assignments:
\{x,\,y\}
and
\{x,\,\overline{z}\}
.
We thus have
\mu(\Gamma)=\{\overline{x}\lor\overline{y},\ \overline{x}\lor z\}
.

Noting that
\Gamma\cup\mu(\Gamma)
is unsatisfiable for every CNF
\Gamma
, we let

s(\Gamma)\coloneqq\lvert\mu(\Gamma)\rvert+\size_{\mathsf{Res}}(\Gamma\cup\mu(\Gamma)).

When
\Gamma
is unsatisfiable, we simply have
s(\Gamma)=\size_{\mathsf{Res}}(\Gamma)
.

Theorem 4.3
.

Let
C=p\mathbin{\dot{\lor}}C^{\prime}
be a RAT for
p
with respect to a CNF
\Gamma
.
There exists a
\mathsf{BC}^{-}
derivation
(\Gamma_{1},\dots,\Gamma_{N})

such that
\Gamma_{1}=\Gamma
,
C\in\Gamma_{N}
, and
\Gamma\cup\{C\}\sqsupseteq\Gamma_{N}
,
where, letting
\Sigma=\NB^{\Gamma}_{p}(C)
and letting
n=\lvert\var(\Gamma)\rvert
,
we have

N\leq\lvert\Sigma\rvert(n+1)+s(\Sigma).

Proof.

Since
C
is a RAT for
p
, for all
D\in\NB^{\Gamma}_{p}(C)
we have
\Gamma\vdash_{1}C^{\prime}\mathbin{\dot{\lor}}D
,
which implies in particular that
\Gamma\vdash_{1}C\mathbin{\dot{\lor}}D
.
Then, using
Lemma

3.16
, for all
D\in\NB^{\Gamma}_{p}(C)

we derive
C\mathbin{\dot{\lor}}D
from
\Gamma
in resolution using at most
n+1
steps.
More formally, we derive
\Gamma^{\prime}\cup\left(C\mathbin{\dot{\lor}}\NB^{\Gamma}_{p}(C)\right)
from
\Gamma
,
where
\Gamma^{\prime}
is the set of intermediate clauses,
guaranteed by
Lemma

3.16
to satisfy
\{C\}\sqsupseteq\Gamma^{\prime}
.

We proceed differently depending on the satisfiability of
\NB^{\Gamma}_{p}(C)
.

Case 1

(
\NB^{\Gamma}_{p}(C)
is unsatisfiable.
)
There exists a resolution proof

\Pi=(\Delta_{1},\dots,\Delta_{m}),

where
\Delta_{1}=\NB^{\Gamma}_{p}(C)
and
\bot\in\Delta_{m}
.
Suppose
\Pi
is a minimum-size proof, so it does not use weakening.
Consider the sequence

\Pi^{\prime}=(C\lor\Delta_{1},\dots,C\lor\Delta_{m}).

This sequence is a valid resolution derivation of
C
from
C\mathbin{\dot{\lor}}\NB^{\Gamma}_{p}(C)
,
seen as follows:

•

By the definition of
\NB^{\Gamma}_{p}(C)
, it has no variables in common with
C
.
Since we assumed that
\Pi
does not use weakening,
no subsequent CNF in
\Pi
has any variables in common with
C
either.

Let
i\in[m-1]
.
The sequence
\Pi
is a resolution proof, so we have
\Delta_{i+1}=\Delta_{i}\cup\{E\}
,
where
E
is a resolvent of some
F,G\in\Delta_{i}
.
Since
\Delta_{i}
has no variables in common with
C
,
it is not possible to resolve
F
and
G
on a variable of
C
.
Then the clause
C\mathbin{\dot{\lor}}E
is a resolvent of
C\mathbin{\dot{\lor}}F
and
C\mathbin{\dot{\lor}}G
,
which are in
C\lor\Delta_{i}
.
This proves by induction that
\Pi^{\prime}
is a valid resolution derivation.

•

Finally, since
\bot\in\Delta_{m}
, we have
C\in(C\lor\Delta_{m})
.

Thus, resolution can derive
C
from
\Gamma

in at most
\left\lvert\NB^{\Gamma}_{p}(C)\right\rvert(n+1)+\size_{\mathsf{Res}}\left(\NB^{\Gamma}_{p}(C)\right)
steps.

Case 2

(
\NB^{\Gamma}_{p}(C)
is satisfiable.
)
Let
\Psi=\Gamma\cup\Gamma^{\prime}\cup\left(C\mathbin{\dot{\lor}}\NB^{\Gamma}_{p}(C)\right)
(i.e., the current CNF).
Since
C\sqsupseteq(\Psi\setminus\Gamma)
, the literal
\overline{p}
does not occur in
\Psi\setminus\Gamma
.
Then we have
\proj_{\overline{p}}(\Psi)=\proj_{\overline{p}}(\Gamma)
,
and, consequently,
\NB^{\Psi}_{p}(C)=\NB^{\Gamma}_{p}(C)
.

Let
E
be a clause such that
\var(E)\cap\var(C)=\varnothing
.
By
Lemma

3.15
,
the clause
p\mathbin{\dot{\lor}}C^{\prime}\mathbin{\dot{\lor}}E
is a BC for
p
with respect to
\Psi

if and only if the partial assignment
\overline{C^{\prime}\mathbin{\dot{\lor}}E}
satisfies
\proj_{\overline{p}}(\Psi)
.
By the definition of a nonblocking CNF,

\overline{C^{\prime}}
already satisfies
\proj_{\overline{p}}(\Psi)\setminus\NB^{\Psi}_{p}(C)
,
so
p\mathbin{\dot{\lor}}C^{\prime}\mathbin{\dot{\lor}}E
is a BC for
p
with respect to
\Psi

if and only if
\overline{E}
satisfies
\NB^{\Psi}_{p}(C)
.

Let us write
\mu
for
\mu\left(\NB^{\Psi}_{p}(C)\right)
.
All clauses in
C\mathbin{\dot{\lor}}\mu
are blocked for
p
with respect to
\Psi
,
and the addition of each such clause of the form
C\mathbin{\dot{\lor}}E

rules out, for
C\mathbin{\dot{\lor}}\NB^{\Psi}_{p}(C)
,
every partial assignment
\alpha
containing
\overline{C\cup E}
.
Then we have

\left(C\mathbin{\dot{\lor}}\NB^{\Psi}_{p}(C)\right)\cup(C\mathbin{\dot{\lor}}\mu)\models C,

where every partial assignment containing
\overline{C}
falsifies the left-hand side
(i.e.,
\NB^{\Psi}_{p}(C)\cup\mu
is unsatisfiable).
Also, no clause
E\in\mu
contains
\overline{p}

and no clause
E\in\mu
is a subset of a different clause
E^{\prime}\in\mu
.
This implies in particular that, for every subset
\mu^{\prime}\subseteq\mu

and for every clause
E\in\mu\setminus\mu^{\prime}
,
if
\overline{E}\models\NB^{\Psi}_{p}(C)
,
then
\overline{E}\models\NB^{\Psi}_{p}(C)\cup\mu^{\prime}
.
We thus derive
C\mathbin{\dot{\lor}}\mu
from
\Psi
by a sequence of blocked clause additions.

As in the previous case, attaching
C
to a resolution proof of
\NB^{\Psi}_{p}(C)\cup\mu

gives a resolution derivation of
C
,
so we derive
C
from
\left(C\mathbin{\dot{\lor}}\NB^{\Psi}_{p}(C)\right)\cup(C\mathbin{\dot{\lor}}\mu)
in resolution.

In the end,
\mathsf{BC}^{-}
can derive
C
from
\Gamma
in at most

\left\lvert\NB^{\Gamma}_{p}(C)\right\rvert(n+1)+\lvert\mu\rvert+\size_{\mathsf{Res}}\left(\NB^{\Gamma}_{p}(C)\cup\mu\right)

steps. ∎

Given a
\mathsf{RAT}^{-}
proof, we can apply the above theorem
to recursively replace the earliest RAT addition in the proof by a
\mathsf{BC}^{-}
derivation.
The intermediate clauses in the derivation replacing
the addition of a RAT
C
are all subsumed by
C
,
which ensures by
Lemma

3.17

that the validity of later RAT additions are preserved.
We can thus translate an entire
\mathsf{RAT}^{-}
proof to a
\mathsf{BC}^{-}
proof.

In the above simulation, when
\NB^{\Gamma}_{p}(C)
is unsatisfiable,
we do not use any blocked clause additions.
By
Lemma

3.15
,
no blocked clause for
p
exists,
and since the RAT addition by itself only gives useful information
about the clauses containing
\overline{p}
,
a simulation where we add a clause that is blocked for a different literal
needs to be more sophisticated.
In particular, such a simulation is unlikely to be
local

in the sense of the output consisting of a sequence of derivations
that each simulate a single step in the input.
(Most simulations in proof complexity are local.)
Assuming that the above simulation is the best possible,
if every RAT addition in a
\mathsf{RAT}^{-}
proof
\Pi
has an unsatisfiable nonblocking CNF,
then
\mathsf{BC}^{-}
essentially falls back to
refuting the nonblocking CNFs for locally simulating
\Pi
.
This observation hints at the transformation
in
Eq.

6
for separating
\mathsf{RAT}^{-}
from
\mathsf{GER}^{-}
.

5
Incomparability of
\mathsf{RAT}^{-}
and
\mathsf{GER}^{-}

We now show that
\mathsf{RAT}^{-}
is exponentially separated from
\mathsf{GER}^{-}
and vice versa,
which also exponentially separates both systems from
\mathsf{BC}^{-}
.
For both directions, we follow a strategy
similar at a high level to the one that Kullmann
[
24
, Lemma 8.4]
used
to prove an exponential separation of
\mathsf{GER}^{-}
from resolution.

Let
P
and
Q
be proof systems (without new variables) that simulate
\mathsf{BC}^{-}
.
To separate
P
from
Q
,
we take a sequence
\langle\Gamma\rangle
of CNFs separating
\mathsf{ER}
from
Q

and we incorporate extension variables into the formulas
in a way that allows
P
to simulate the
\mathsf{ER}
proof
while preventing
Q
from achieving any speedup.
This strategy is made possible by the fact
that
\mathsf{BC}^{-}
effectively simulates
\mathsf{ER}
.
See also the discussion by Buss and Thapen
[
6
, Section 2.2]
.

From this point on, given a CNF
\Gamma
,
we use
(\Lambda^{*},\Pi^{*})
to denote a minimum-size
\mathsf{ER}
proof of
\Gamma
,
where
\Lambda^{*}
is the union
of a sequence of
t(\Gamma)\coloneqq\lvert\Lambda^{*}\rvert/3
sets of extension clauses
such that the
i
th set
\lambda_{i}
is of the form

\{\overline{x_{i}}\lor p_{i},\ \overline{x_{i}}\lor q_{i},\ x_{i}\lor\overline{p_{i}}\lor\overline{q_{i}}\}.

Thus, we implicitly reserve
\left\{x_{1},\dots,x_{t(\Gamma)}\right\}

as the set of extension variables used in
\Lambda^{*}
.
We assume without loss of generality
that the variables of
p_{i}
and
q_{i}
are
in
\var(\Gamma)\cup\{x_{1},\dots,x_{i-1}\}
for all
i\in[t(\Gamma)]
.

5.1
Exponential separation of
\mathsf{RAT}^{-}
from
\mathsf{GER}^{-}

Let
\Gamma
be a CNF and
(\Lambda^{*},\Pi^{*})
be a minimum-size
\mathsf{ER}
proof of
\Gamma
as described above.
Consider the transformation

\mathcal{G}(\Gamma)\coloneqq\Gamma\cup\bigcup_{i=1}^{t(\Gamma)}\bigl[(x_{i}\lor\Gamma)\cup(\overline{x_{i}}\lor\Gamma)\bigr],

(6)

where
x_{1},\dots,x_{t(\Gamma)}
are the extension variables used in
\Lambda^{*}
.
When
\Gamma
is unsatisfiable,
each extension variable above is “locked” behind the projection
\Gamma
,
which
\mathsf{RAT}^{-}
can overcome but
\mathsf{BC}^{-}
cannot.

Lemma 5.1
.

For every CNF
\Gamma
, we have

\size_{\mathsf{RAT}^{-}}(\mathcal{G}(\Gamma))\leq\size_{\mathsf{ER}}(\Gamma).

Proof.

We will show that the minimum-size
\mathsf{ER}
proof
(\Lambda^{*},\Pi^{*})
of
\Gamma

directly gives a
\mathsf{RAT}^{-}
proof of
\mathcal{G}(\Gamma)
of the same size.

We write
t
for
t(\Gamma)
.
Let
(\lambda_{1},\dots,\lambda_{t})

be the sequence of
t
sets of extension clauses that make up
\Lambda^{*}
.
Consider an arbitrary
i\in[t]
, and suppose that we have derived

\bigcup_{j=1}^{i-1}\lambda_{j}
from
\mathcal{G}(\Gamma)
by a sequence of RAT additions,
so the current CNF is
\Delta=\mathcal{G}(\Gamma)\cup\bigcup_{j=1}^{i-1}\lambda_{j}
.
We will introduce the clauses in

\lambda_{i}=\{\overline{x_{i}}\lor p_{i},\ \overline{x_{i}}\lor q_{i},\ x_{i}\lor\overline{p_{i}}\lor\overline{q_{i}}\}

by a sequence of RAT additions.
Note that, since
\lambda_{i}
is a set of extension clauses for
\Gamma\cup\bigcup_{j=1}^{i-1}\lambda_{j}
,
so far the variable
x_{i}
occurs only in
\mathcal{G}(\Gamma)\setminus\Gamma
.

1.

The clause
\overline{x_{i}}\lor p_{i}
is a RAT for
\overline{x_{i}}
with respect to
\Delta

because all earlier occurrences of
x_{i}

are clauses of the form
x_{i}\lor D
, where
D\in\Gamma
.
We thus require
\Delta\vdash_{1}\{p_{i}\}\cup D
for all
D\in\Gamma
.
This is indeed the case since we actually have
D\in\Delta

by the construction of
\mathcal{G}(\Gamma)
,
which implies
\Delta\vdash_{1}\{p_{i}\}\cup D
.

2.

The clause
\overline{x_{i}}\lor q_{i}
is similarly a RAT for
\overline{x_{i}}

with respect to
\Delta\cup\{\overline{x_{i}}\lor p_{i}\}
.

3.

The clause
x_{i}\lor\overline{p_{i}}\lor\overline{q_{i}}

is similarly a RAT for
x_{i}
with respect to
\Delta
.
Moreover, it is a BC for
x_{i}
with respect to

\{\overline{x_{i}}\lor p_{i},\ \overline{x_{i}}\lor q_{i}\}

since
\{p_{i},\,\overline{p_{i}},\,\overline{q_{i}}\}

and
\{q_{i},\,\overline{p_{i}},\,\overline{q_{i}}\}
are both tautological.
As a result,
x_{i}\lor\overline{p_{i}}\lor\overline{q_{i}}
is a RAT with respect to

\Delta\cup\{\overline{x_{i}}\lor p_{i},\ \overline{x_{i}}\lor q_{i}\}
.

It follows by induction that we can derive
\Lambda^{*}
from
\mathcal{G}(\Gamma)
in
\mathsf{RAT}^{-}
.
Since
\Pi^{*}
is a resolution proof of
\Gamma\cup\Lambda^{*}
,
and since
\mathcal{G}(\Gamma)
contains
\Gamma
,
we also have a resolution proof of
\mathcal{G}(\Gamma)\cup\Lambda^{*}
.
Thus, we have a
\mathsf{RAT}^{-}
proof of
\mathcal{G}(\Gamma)

of size
\lvert\Lambda^{*}\rvert+\lvert\Pi^{*}\rvert=\size_{\mathsf{ER}}(\Gamma)
.
∎

As a consequence of the above,
if a sequence
\langle\Gamma\rangle
of CNFs is easy for
\mathsf{ER}
,
then, independent of whether
\langle\Gamma\rangle
is easy or hard for
\mathsf{RAT}^{-}
,
the sequence
\mathcal{G}(\langle\Gamma\rangle)\coloneqq(\mathcal{G}(\Gamma_{1}),\mathcal{G}(\Gamma_{2}),\dots)
is easy for
\mathsf{RAT}^{-}
.
In contrast, the following result implies
that the extension variables added by
\mathcal{G}
are of no use to
\mathsf{BC}^{-}
.

Lemma 5.2
.

For every CNF
\Gamma
, we have

\size_{\mathsf{BC}^{-}}(\mathcal{G}(\Gamma))\geq\size_{\mathsf{Res}}(\Gamma\cup\B^{-}(\Gamma)).

Proof.

When
\Gamma
is satisfiable, the inequality holds trivially,
so suppose that
\Gamma
is unsatisfiable.

Applying
Lemma

3.13
to
\mathcal{G}(\Gamma)
, we have

\size_{\mathsf{BC}^{-}}(\mathcal{G}(\Gamma))\geq\size_{\mathsf{Res}}\left(\mathcal{G}(\Gamma)\cup\B^{-}(\mathcal{G}(\Gamma))\right).

(7)

We claim that no clause in
\B^{-}(\mathcal{G}(\Gamma))

is blocked for a literal of any of the variables
in
X=\left\{x_{1},\dots,x_{t(\Gamma)}\right\}
.
To see this, consider a clause
C
of the form
x\mathbin{\dot{\lor}}C^{\prime}
, where
x\in X
.
If
C
is blocked for
x
with respect to
\mathcal{G}(\Gamma)
,
then
\overline{C^{\prime}}
is a satisfying assignment to
\proj_{\overline{x}}(\mathcal{G}(\Gamma))=\Gamma

by
Lemma

3.15
.
Since
\Gamma
is unsatisfiable, no such assignment exists.
Therefore,
C
cannot be blocked for
x
, which leaves us with

\B^{-}(\mathcal{G}(\Gamma))=\B^{-}_{\var(\Gamma)}(\mathcal{G}(\Gamma)).

(8)

Furthermore, since
\Gamma\subseteq\mathcal{G}(\Gamma)
,
every clause in
\B^{-}_{\var(\Gamma)}(\mathcal{G}(\Gamma))
has to be blocked
in particular with respect to
\Gamma
.
This requires
\B^{-}_{\var(\Gamma)}(\mathcal{G}(\Gamma))
to consist of clauses of the form
C\mathbin{\dot{\lor}}D
,
where
C\in\B^{-}(\Gamma)
and
\var(D)\subseteq X
(with
D
possibly empty).
In light of this, consider a partial assignment
\alpha
such that

\alpha(z)=\begin{cases}1&\text{if }z\in X\\
\text{undefined}&\text{otherwise}.\end{cases}

It is straightforward to see that
\mathcal{G}(\Gamma)|_{\alpha}=\Gamma
.
Additionally, for every clause
C\mathbin{\dot{\lor}}D
(of the above form) in
\B^{-}_{\var(\Gamma)}(\mathcal{G}(\Gamma))
,
the restriction
(C\mathbin{\dot{\lor}}D)|_{\alpha}
is either
1
or
C
.
We thus have

\left.\kern-1.2pt\left(\mathcal{G}(\Gamma)\cup\B^{-}_{\var(\Gamma)}(\mathcal{G}(\Gamma))\right)\right|_{\alpha}=\Gamma\cup\B^{-}(\Gamma).

(9)

Putting
Eqs.

7
,
8
, and
9
together,
we finally obtain

\displaystyle\size_{\mathsf{BC}^{-}}(\mathcal{G}(\Gamma))

\displaystyle\geq\size_{\mathsf{Res}}\left(\mathcal{G}(\Gamma)\cup\B^{-}(\mathcal{G}(\Gamma))\right)

\displaystyle=\size_{\mathsf{Res}}\left(\mathcal{G}(\Gamma)\cup\B^{-}_{\var(\Gamma)}(\mathcal{G}(\Gamma))\right)

\displaystyle\geq\size_{\mathsf{Res}}\left(\left.\kern-1.2pt\left(\mathcal{G}(\Gamma)\cup\B^{-}_{\var(\Gamma)}(\mathcal{G}(\Gamma))\right)\right|_{\alpha}\right)

(
Lemma

2.7
)

\displaystyle=\size_{\mathsf{Res}}\left(\Gamma\cup\B^{-}(\Gamma)\right),

which is the desired inequality.
∎

For certain CNFs, the above result carries over to
\mathsf{GER}^{-}
.

Lemma 5.3
.

For every CNF
\Gamma
such that
\ker(\Gamma)=\Gamma
, we have

\size_{\mathsf{GER}^{-}}(\mathcal{G}(\Gamma))\geq\size_{\mathsf{Res}}(\Gamma\cup\B^{-}(\Gamma)).

Proof.

Suppose
\ker(\Gamma)=\Gamma
.
We will show that
\inB(\mathcal{G}(\Gamma))=\varnothing
.
A clause
C
in
\inB(\mathcal{G}(\Gamma))
belongs to either
\Gamma
or
\mathcal{G}(\Gamma)\setminus\Gamma
,
and we handle the two cases separately.

Case 1

(
C\in\Gamma
.
)
If
C
is blocked with respect to
\mathcal{G}(\Gamma)
,
then, since
\Gamma\subseteq\mathcal{G}(\Gamma)
, it is blocked with respect to
\Gamma
as well,
which contradicts
\ker(\Gamma)=\Gamma
.

Case 2

(
C\in\mathcal{G}(\Gamma)\setminus\Gamma
.
)
Since
C\in\mathcal{G}(\Gamma)\setminus\Gamma
, it is of the form
p\mathbin{\dot{\lor}}C^{\prime}
,
where
C^{\prime}\in\Gamma
and
p
is a literal of some variable
in
\left\{x_{1},\dots,x_{t(\Gamma)}\right\}
.
The clause
C
is clearly not blocked for
p
with respect to
\mathcal{G}(\Gamma)

since there exists a clause of the form
\overline{p}\mathbin{\dot{\lor}}C^{\prime}
in
\mathcal{G}(\Gamma)

but
C^{\prime}\cup C^{\prime}
is not tautological.
The clause
C
is not blocked for any literal in
C^{\prime}
with respect to
\mathcal{G}(\Gamma)
either
since this implies that
C^{\prime}
is blocked with respect to
\Gamma
,
which contradicts
\ker(\Gamma)=\Gamma
.

We thus have
\ker(\mathcal{G}(\Gamma))=\mathcal{G}(\Gamma)
.
Now, using
Lemmas

3.12
and
5.2
, we have

\size_{\mathsf{GER}^{-}}(\mathcal{G}(\Gamma))=\size_{\mathsf{BC}^{-}}(\mathcal{G}(\Gamma))\geq\size_{\mathsf{Res}}(\Gamma\cup\B^{-}(\Gamma)).\qed

To prove the separation, we invoke the above results
with
\Gamma
as the
pigeonhole principle
,
which states that every “pigeon”
i\in[n+1]
is mapped to some “hole”
k\in[n]

and that no two distinct pigeons
i,j\in[n+1]
are mapped to the same hole.
It is defined for
n\in\mathbb{N}^{+}
as

\mathrm{PHP}_{n}\coloneqq\bigcup_{i\in[n+1]}\left\{p_{i,1}\lor\dots\lor p_{i,n}\right\}\cup\bigcup_{\begin{subarray}{c}i,j\in[n+1],\;i\neq j\\
k\in[n]\end{subarray}}\{\overline{p_{i,k}}\lor\overline{p_{j,k}}\},

where we call the first set of clauses the
pigeon axioms

and the second set the
hole axioms
.

Theorem 5.4
.

\mathsf{RAT}^{-}
is exponentially separated from
\mathsf{GER}^{-}
.

Proof.

Cook
[
10
]
constructed polynomial-size
\mathsf{ER}
proofs of
\mathrm{PHP}_{n}
,
which implies by
Lemma

5.1
that

\size_{\mathsf{RAT}^{-}}(\mathcal{G}(\mathrm{PHP}_{n}))=n^{O(1)}.

Kullmann
[
24
, Theorem 2]
proved
that
\size_{\mathsf{Res}}(\mathrm{PHP}_{n}\cup\B^{-}(\mathrm{PHP}_{n}))=2^{\Omega(n)}
.
Noting that
\ker(\mathrm{PHP}_{n})=\mathrm{PHP}_{n}
for all
n\in\mathbb{N}^{+}
,
we apply
Lemma

5.3
to obtain

\size_{\mathsf{GER}^{-}}(\mathcal{G}(\mathrm{PHP}_{n}))=2^{\Omega(n)}.

Thus,
\mathcal{G}(\langle\mathrm{PHP}\rangle)
exponentially separates
\mathsf{RAT}^{-}
from
\mathsf{GER}^{-}
.
∎

5.2
Exponential separation of
\mathsf{GER}^{-}
from
\mathsf{RAT}^{-}

We proceed in a similar way to the previous section.
Let
\Gamma
be a CNF and
(\Lambda^{*},\Pi^{*})
be a minimum-size
\mathsf{ER}
proof of
\Gamma
.
Take a set

\left\{y_{1},\dots,y_{t(\Gamma)}\right\}\subseteq\mathbf{V}\setminus\var(\Gamma\cup\Lambda^{*})

of
t(\Gamma)
distinct variables.
Consider the transformation

\mathcal{H}(\Gamma)\coloneqq\Gamma\cup\bigcup_{i=1}^{t(\Gamma)}\{\overline{x_{i}}\lor y_{i},\ x_{i}\lor\overline{y_{i}}\},

(10)

where
x_{1},\dots,x_{t(\Gamma)}
are the extension variables used in
\Lambda^{*}
.
As before, only one of the two systems
can make any use of the extension variables incorporated into the formula.
This time, the temporary deletion available to
\mathsf{GER}^{-}
makes the difference.

Lemma 5.5
.

For every CNF
\Gamma
, we have

\size_{\mathsf{GER}^{-}}(\mathcal{H}(\Gamma))\leq\size_{\mathsf{ER}}(\Gamma).

Proof.

Let
(\Lambda^{*},\Pi^{*})
be the minimum-size
\mathsf{ER}
proof of
\Gamma
.
We will show that the clauses in
\Lambda^{*}\cup(\mathcal{H}(\Gamma)\setminus\Gamma)

can be derived from
\Gamma
in some sequence by blocked clause additions,
which implies by
Lemma

3.11

that
\Lambda^{*}
is a blocked extension for
\mathcal{H}(\Gamma)
.

Recall that extension clauses can be derived in sequence
by blocked clause additions.
Then, since
\Lambda^{*}
is an extension for
\Gamma
, we derive
\Lambda^{*}
by such a sequence.
Next, from
\Gamma\cup\Lambda^{*}
, we derive the clauses in
\mathcal{H}(\Gamma)\setminus\Gamma
.
Let us write
t
for
t(\Gamma)
.
Consider the sequence

(\overline{x_{1}}\lor y_{1},\ \dots,\ \overline{x_{t}}\lor y_{t},\ x_{1}\lor\overline{y_{1}},\ \dots,\ x_{t}\lor\overline{y_{t}}).

For each
i\in[t]
, the
i
th clause
\overline{x_{i}}\lor y_{i}

in the first half of the sequence
is blocked for
y_{i}
since
\overline{y_{i}}
does not occur in any of the earlier clauses.
Similarly, the
i
th clause
x_{i}\lor\overline{y_{i}}

in the second half of the sequence
is blocked for
\overline{y_{i}}
since the only earlier occurrence of
y_{i}

is the clause
\overline{x_{i}}\lor y_{i}
and
\{x_{i},\,\overline{x_{i}}\}
is tautological.
As a result,
\Lambda^{*}
is a blocked extension for
\mathcal{H}(\Gamma)
.

Since
\Pi^{*}
is a resolution proof of
\Gamma\cup\Lambda^{*}
,
and since
\mathcal{H}(\Gamma)
contains
\Gamma
,
we also have a resolution proof of
\mathcal{H}(\Gamma)\cup\Lambda^{*}
.
Thus, we have a
\mathsf{GER}^{-}
proof of
\mathcal{H}(\Gamma)

of size
\lvert\Lambda^{*}\rvert+\lvert\Pi^{*}\rvert=\size_{\mathsf{ER}}(\Gamma)
.
∎

Lemma 5.6
.

Let
\Gamma
be a CNF, and let
n=\lvert\var(\Gamma)\rvert
. We have

\size_{\mathsf{RAT}^{-}}(\mathcal{H}(\Gamma))\geq\frac{\size_{\mathsf{RAT}^{-}}(\Gamma)}{n+1}.

Proof.

We write
t
for
t(\Gamma)
.
Let
V=\var(\mathcal{H}(\Gamma)\setminus\Gamma)
(i.e., the set of variables added by
\mathcal{H}
),
and let
\alpha
be a partial assignment such that

\alpha(z)=\begin{cases}1&\text{if }z\in V\\
\text{undefined}&\text{otherwise}.\end{cases}

We claim that, for all
N\in\mathbb{N}^{+}
, given a
\mathsf{RAT}^{-}
derivation

\Pi=(\Delta_{1},\dots,\Delta_{N})

with
\Delta_{1}=\mathcal{H}(\Gamma)
,
there exists a
\mathsf{RAT}^{-}
derivation

\Pi^{\prime}=(\Psi_{1},\dots,\Psi_{N^{\prime}})

with
N^{\prime}\leq N\cdot(\lvert\var(\Gamma)\rvert+1)

such that

•

\Delta_{1}|_{\alpha}=\Gamma=\Psi_{1}
,

•

\Delta_{N}|_{\alpha}\subseteq\Psi_{N^{\prime}}
, and

•

\Delta_{N}|_{\alpha}\sqsupseteq\Psi_{N^{\prime}}
.

The second condition above implies in particular that if
\bot\in\Delta_{N}
, then
\bot\in\Psi_{N^{\prime}}
.
This in turn implies the desired statement,
since it means that if
\mathcal{H}(\Gamma)
has a
\mathsf{RAT}^{-}
proof of size
N
,
then
\Gamma
has a
\mathsf{RAT}^{-}
proof of size at most
N\cdot(\lvert\var(\Gamma)\rvert+1)
.

We proceed by induction.
For a derivation
\Pi=(\Delta_{1})
of size
1
with
\Delta_{1}=\mathcal{H}(\Gamma)
,
the derivation
\Pi^{\prime}=(\Delta_{1}|_{\alpha})
satisfies the conditions above.
Let
\Pi=(\Delta_{1},\dots,\Delta_{m})
be a
\mathsf{RAT}^{-}
derivation with
\Delta_{1}=\mathcal{H}(\Gamma)
.
Suppose that
\Pi^{\prime}=(\Psi_{1},\dots,\Psi_{m^{\prime}})
is a
\mathsf{RAT}^{-}
derivation
with
m^{\prime}\leq m\cdot(\lvert\var(\Gamma)\rvert+1)
satisfying the above conditions.
Let
C
be a clause that is derived from
\Delta_{m}

either by resolution, weakening, or RAT addition.
We will show that there exists a
\mathsf{RAT}^{-}
derivation from
\Psi_{m^{\prime}}|_{\alpha}

of a CNF that contains and is subsumed by
(\Delta_{m}\cup\{C\})|_{\alpha}
.
For simplicity, we will establish this for
\Delta_{m}|_{\alpha}
instead of
\Psi_{m^{\prime}}|_{\alpha}
,
with the understanding that the containment and the subsumption conditions above
imply by
Lemma

3.17

\Psi_{m^{\prime}}|_{\alpha}
.
There exists a trivial derivation when

since it implies that
(\Delta_{m}\cup\{C\})|_{\alpha}=\Delta_{m}|_{\alpha}
,
so suppose that
\alpha\not\models C
.
As a consequence,
\alpha
does not satisfy any subset of

.

From this point on, we write
\Delta
instead of
\Delta_{m}
to reduce clutter.

Case 1

(
C
is a resolvent of
D,E\in\Delta
on
v
.
)
Without loss of generality, suppose that

and
\overline{v}\in E
.

Case 1.1

(

.
)
We have

(

.

Since

\alpha
does not set

D|_{\alpha}\in\Delta|_{\alpha}

.

C|_{\alpha}

(

.

Since

D|_{\alpha}\in\Delta|_{\alpha}

Case 3

(
C

with respect to
\Delta
.
)
Since we assumed

\overline{p}\in V

.

Case 3.1

(
\overline{p}\in V
.
)
Either

.
Suppose

.

Since
C
is a RAT for
\overline{x_{i}}

\Delta
,

2.9
,

\Delta|_{\alpha}\vdash_{1}\left.\kern-1.2pt\bigl((C\setminus\{\overline{x_{i}}\})\cup\{\overline{y_{i}}\}\bigr)\right|_{\alpha},

3.16
,
there exists a resolution derivation of

.

Case 3.2

(

.
)
The clause

is of the form

.
Since

for every clause

Every clause

Let

For a propositional variable

.

\mathsf{GER}^{-}

.

Lemma 6.1

Proof.

We write

Let
(\lambda_{1},\dots,\lambda_{t})

be the sequence of
t

\Lambda^{*}

For each
i\in[t]

\lambda_{i}^{\prime}\coloneqq\{\overline{x_{i}}\lor\overline{y_{i}}\lor p_{i},\ \overline{x_{i}}\lor\overline{y_{i}}\lor q_{i},\ x_{i}\lor y_{i}\lor\overline{p_{i}}\lor\overline{q_{i}}\}

Consider an arbitrary

\Delta=\mathcal{H}(\Gamma)\cup\bigcup_{j=1}^{i-1}\lambda_{j}^{\prime}

1.

The clause

\overline{x_{i}}\lor y_{i}

\Delta

2.

The clause
E_{i}^{2}\coloneqq\overline{x_{i}}\lor\overline{y_{i}}\lor q_{i}
is
similarly an SBC for
L=\{\overline{x_{i}},\,\overline{y_{i}}\}
with respect to
\Delta
.
Furthermore, we have
E_{i}^{1}\cap\overline{L}=\varnothing
,
so
E_{i}^{2}

\Delta\cup\left\{E_{i}^{1}\right\}

3.

\bigl(E_{i}^{3}\setminus M\bigr)\cup\bigl(E_{i}^{1}\setminus\overline{M}\bigr)=\{\overline{p_{i}},\,\overline{q_{i}},\,p_{i}\}

As a result,

It follows by induction that we can derive

In the end, we have an
\mathsf{SBC}^{-}

of size at most

More specifically, we observe that the

[

, Theorem 4.3]

(No knowledge of

Lemma 7.1
.

\size_{\mathsf{SBC}^{-}}(\mathrm{PHP}_{n})=n^{O(1)}
.

Proof.

We essentially formalize in
\mathsf{SBC}^{-}
a short inductive proof of
\mathrm{PHP}_{n}
, which assumes
without loss of generality that the smallest pigeon is mapped to the smallest hole,
derives from this assumption a renamed instance of
\mathrm{PHP}_{n-1}
,
and inductively repeats these steps until deriving a trivial contradiction.

Recall that
\mathrm{PHP}_{n}
consists of the clauses

P_{i}\coloneqq p_{i,1}\lor\dots\lor p_{i,n}
and

H_{i,j,k}\coloneqq\overline{p_{i,k}}\lor\overline{p_{j,k}}

for
i,j\in[n+1]
and
k\in[n]
with
i\neq j
.

For
i\in[n-1]
,
j\in[n+1]
, and
k\in[n]
such that
j,k>i
, let

C_{i,j,k}\coloneqq\overline{p_{i,k}}\lor\overline{p_{j,i}}\lor\left(\bigvee_{\begin{subarray}{c}\ell\in[n+1]\\
\ell\neq i\end{subarray}}p_{\ell,k}\right)\lor\left(\bigvee_{\begin{subarray}{c}\ell\in[n+1]\\
\ell\neq j\end{subarray}}p_{\ell,i}\right).

Also, for
i\in[n-1]
, let

\Lambda_{i}\coloneqq\{C_{i,j,k}\mid j\in[n+1],\ k\in[n],\text{ and }j,k>i\}.

We will first show that, for all
i\in[n-1]
,
from
\Psi_{i-1}\coloneqq\mathrm{PHP}_{n}\cup\bigcup_{\ell=1}^{i-1}\Lambda_{\ell}

we can derive the clauses in
\Lambda_{i}
in any order
by a sequence of set-blocked clause additions,
which implies that we can obtain
\Psi_{n-1}
from
\mathrm{PHP}_{n}
in
\mathsf{SBC}^{-}
.
Afterwards, we will give a polynomial-size resolution derivation
of the empty clause from
\Psi_{n-1}
, concluding the proof.

For every clause
C_{i,j,k}\in\Lambda_{i}
and every subset
\Lambda_{i}^{\prime}\subseteq\Lambda_{i}\setminus\{C_{i,j,k}\}
,
we claim that
C_{i,j,k}
is an SBC for

L=\{\overline{p_{i,k}},\,\overline{p_{j,i}},\,p_{i,i},\,p_{j,k}\}\subseteq C_{i,j,k}

with respect to
\Psi_{i-1}\cup\Lambda_{i}^{\prime}
.
This requires us to show that for all
D\in\Psi_{i-1}\cup\Lambda_{i}^{\prime}

with
D\cap\overline{L}\neq\varnothing
and
D\cap L=\varnothing

the set
\bigl(C_{i,j,k}\setminus L\bigr)\cup\bigl(D\setminus\overline{L}\bigr)
is tautological.
There are three cases.

Case 1

(
D\in\mathrm{PHP}_{n}
.
)
If
D
is a clause in
\mathrm{PHP}_{n}
such that

D\cap\overline{L}\neq\varnothing
and
D\cap L=\varnothing
,
then either
D=H_{i,i^{\prime},i}
for some
i^{\prime}\in[n+1]
such that
i^{\prime}\neq j

or
D=H_{j,j^{\prime},k}
for some
j^{\prime}\in[n+1]
such that
j^{\prime}\neq i
.
If
D=H_{i,i^{\prime},i}
, then we have
p_{i^{\prime},i}\in C_{i,j,k}\setminus L

and
\overline{p_{i^{\prime},i}}\in D\setminus\overline{L}
,
so the union of the two sets is tautological.
The argument for the case of
D=H_{j,j^{\prime},k}
is similar.

Case 2

(
D\in\bigcup_{\ell=1}^{i-1}\Lambda_{\ell}
.
)
Let
D=C_{i^{\prime},j^{\prime},k^{\prime}}
be an arbitrary clause in
\bigcup_{\ell=1}^{i-1}\Lambda_{\ell}
,
where
i^{\prime}<i
and
j^{\prime},k^{\prime}>i^{\prime}
.
A simple inspection shows that we have

D\cap\overline{L}\neq\varnothing
if and only if
k^{\prime}=i
or
k^{\prime}=k
.
If
k^{\prime}=i
, then
p_{i,i}\in D
.
Noting that
i^{\prime}\neq j
, if
k^{\prime}=k
, then
p_{j,k}\in D
.
Either way,
D\cap L=\varnothing
fails to hold,
so there exists no
D\in\bigcup_{\ell=1}^{i-1}\Lambda_{\ell}

such that
D\cap\overline{L}\neq\varnothing
and
D\cap L=\varnothing
.

Case 3

(
D\in\Lambda_{i}^{\prime}
.
)
Let
D=C_{i,j^{\prime},k^{\prime}}
be an arbitrary clause in
\Lambda_{i}^{\prime}
, where
j^{\prime},k^{\prime}>i
.
It is straightforward to see that we have
p_{i,i}\in D
unless
j^{\prime}=i
.
Then, since
j^{\prime}>i
, there exists no
D\in\Lambda_{i}^{\prime}
such that
D\cap L=\varnothing
.

Thus, we obtain
\Psi_{n-1}
from
\mathrm{PHP}_{n}
in
\mathsf{SBC}^{-}
,
and now we construct a resolution proof of
\Psi_{n-1}
.
First, for each
(i,j,k)
such that
C_{i,j,k}
is defined,
we resolve
C_{i,j,k}
against the axioms for the holes
k
and
i

to derive
\overline{p_{i,k}}\lor\overline{p_{j,i}}
.
Then, for each
i\in[n]
and
j\in[n+1]
such that
j>i
,
we derive the clause
\overline{p_{j,i}}
by induction on
i
as follows:
Fix
i
and
j
such that
j>i
. Let

\Delta_{i,j}\coloneqq\{\overline{p_{i,k}}\mid k\in[n]\text{ and }k<i\}\cup\{\overline{p_{i,i}}\lor\overline{p_{j,i}}\}\cup\{\overline{p_{i,k}}\lor\overline{p_{j,i}}\mid k\in[n]\text{ and }k>i\},

where we have the clauses in the first set from the induction hypothesis,
the second set from
\mathrm{PHP}_{n}
, and the third set from resolving
C_{i,j,k}

against the hole axioms in the previous step.
Resolving each clause in
\Delta_{i,j}
against
the pigeon axiom
P_{i}
thus gives
\overline{p_{j,i}}
.
Finally, we resolve for each
i\in[n]
the clause
\overline{p_{n+1,i}}

against
P_{n+1}
to derive the empty clause.
∎
