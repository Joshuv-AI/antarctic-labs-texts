q q -Analogs of strongly regular graphs Corresponding author Faculty of Mathematics, University of Rijeka Department of Mathematical Sciences, University of Memphis
Michael Braun ( michael.braun@h-da.de )

Abstract
We introduce the notion of q q -analogs of strongly regular graphs and give several examples of such structures. We prove a necessary condition on the parameters, show the connection to designs over finite fields, and present a classification.



Keywords:

q
-analog, strongly regular graph, subspace design.

AMS classification numbers:
05E30, 05B05.

1
Introduction

In graph theory, Bose
[
1
]
introduced the notion of a strongly regular graph with parameters
(v,k,\lambda,\mu)
which is a regular graph on
v
vertices with degree
k
such that two adjacent vertices have
\lambda
common neighbors and two non-adjacent vertices have
\mu
common neighbors. Classical examples of strongly regular graphs are the pentagon graph with parameters
(5,2,0,1)
and the Petersen graph with parameters
(10,3,0,1)
. Strongly regular graphs are closely related to a second important combinatorial structure, a block design. Goethals and Seidel
[
9
]
pointed out that strongly regular graphs can be derived from block designs. For an extensive overview on strongly regular graphs we refer to
[
4
]
.

Introduced by Tits
[
13
]
, combinatorics on sets can be considered as the limit case
q
tending to
1
of combinatorics of vector spaces over the finite field with
q
elements. Here, intersection of subsets corresponds to intersection of subspaces, but the union of subsets corresponds to the span of subspaces, as to preserve the similarities between the lattice of subsets of a set with
v
elements and the lattice of subspaces of a
v
-dimensional vector space over
{\mathbb{F}}_{q}
. Naturally, cardinalities of subsets correspond to dimensions of subspaces.

A combinatorial object in the subspace lattice of
{\mathbb{F}}^{v}_{q}
that behaves similarly (i.e. with respect to intersection and span/union) as an object in the subset lattice on a set of
v
elements is called a
q
-analog of the latter. The interest in the concept of
q
-analogs of combinatorial objects has been renewed in the past decade due to the connection between
q
-analogs of block designs (also called designs over finite fields, see
[
2
,
6
,
14
]
), in particular Steiner systems, and error correction in random linear network coding (see
[
3
,
11
]
).

The goal of this article is to apply the concept of
q
-analogs to strongly regular graphs. We define
q
-analogs of strongly regular graphs (see Definition
3.1
) and give some examples. We derive some properties and show connections to classical strongly regular graphs and
q
-analogs of block designs, thereby demonstrating the similarities with classical strongly regular graphs. In Section
4
, we also present a complete classification of strongly regular
q
-ary graphs.

The definitions of
q
-analogs of designs or the
q
-analogs of group divisible designs (recently introduced in
[
7
]
), as well as the definition of the
q
-analog of a strongly regular graph that we will introduce, have a condition on the intersection of the involved subspaces. But while the former specify the number of blocks through subspaces (of a given dimension), the latter will specify the size of the intersection. A connection between both is given in Theorem
3.15
.

2
Preliminaries

In this section, we recall some basic definitions and results for strongly regular graphs. For
0\leq s\leq v
, we denote by
\binom{V}{s}
the set of all
s
-element subsets of a set
V
with
v
elements. Its cardinality is the binomial coefficient
\binom{v}{s}
.

Definition 2.1
.

A simple (undirected) graph
\Gamma
is a pair
(V,E)
where
V
is a set and
E\subseteq\binom{V}{2}
. The elements of
V
and
E
are called
vertices
and
edges
, respectively. If there is an edge
\{X,Y\}
, then we say that
X
and
Y
are
adjacent
.

Remark 2.2
.

Note that we can identify the set of vertices of a graph with the set
\binom{V}{1}
and express everything in terms of 1- and 2-subsets. Via this point of view we will define the
q
-analog, substituting subsets by subspaces and cardinalities by dimensions.

Definition 2.3
.

For a graph
\Gamma
a vertex
y
is called a neighbor of a vertex
x
if
x
and
y
are adjacent. The
(closed) neighborhood

N_{\Gamma}(x)
of a vertex
x
is the set of all its neighbors and
x
itself.

The open neighborhood
N_{\Gamma}(x)
of a vertex
x
in a graph
\Gamma
is the set of its neighbors (so without
x
). Note that most authors use neighborhood (without specification) for the open neighborhood, but to stress the similarity with the
q
-analogs we will only consider closed neighborhoods here, and thus an unspecified neighborhood will always be closed in this article.

Remark 2.4
.

Note that for an isolated vertex (a vertex without neighbors) of a graph
\Gamma=(V,E)
we have
N_{\Gamma}(x)=x
. For all non-isolated vertices we have
N_{\Gamma}(x)=\cup_{x\in e\in E}\;e
.

Remark 2.5
.

Given the set
V
of vertices of a graph, we can identify a graph
\Gamma
with the map
\phi:V\to\mathcal{P}(V):x\mapsto N_{\Gamma}(x)
. This map has as properties that
x\in\phi(x)
for any
x\in V
and that
x\in\phi(y)\iff y\in\phi(x)
.

Definition 2.6
.

A graph is called
k
-regular
if each vertex has exactly
k
neighbors, equivalently if each neighborhood has size
k+1
. The set of all
k
-regular graphs on
v
vertices will be denoted by
\RG(v,k)
.

Remark 2.7
.

In the light of Remark
2.5
and the comment after Definition
2.1
, we can think of a
k
-regular graph as a map
\binom{V}{1}\to\binom{V}{k+1}
for a set
V
.

Definition 2.8
.

A
k
-regular graph on
v
vertices such that any two adjacent vertices have
\lambda
common neighbors and such that any two non-adjacent vertices have
\mu
common neighbors is called strongly regular with parameters
(v,k,\lambda,\mu)
. The set of all strongly regular graphs with parameters
(v,k,\lambda,\mu)
is denoted by
\SRG(v,k,\lambda,\mu)
.

In terms of neighborhoods, the previous definition can be given as follows: a graph
\Gamma\in\RG(v,k)
is in
\SRG(v,k,\lambda,\mu)
if and only if for all vertices
x,y
with
x\neq y
we have that

|(N_{\Gamma}(x)\cap N_{\Gamma}(y))\setminus\{x,y\}|=\begin{cases}\lambda&\text{if $x$ and $y$ are adjacent,}\\
\mu&\text{otherwise.}\end{cases}

The standard result connecting the four parameters of a strongly regular graph is the following.

Theorem 2.9

(
[
1
, Equation (2.5)]
)
.

If there is a strongly regular graph with parameters
(v,k,\lambda,\mu)
, then

k(k-1-\lambda)=(v-k-1)\mu\>.

We conclude this preliminary section by introducing block designs and their connection to strongly regular graphs.

Definition 2.10
.

A (block) design
(\mathcal{P},\mathcal{L})
with parameters
t\text{-}(v,k,\lambda)
consists of a set of
points

\mathcal{P}
and a set of
blocks

{\mathcal{B}}\subseteq\binom{\mathcal{P}}{k}
such that

\forall\,T\in\binom{\mathcal{P}}{t}:|\{K\in{\mathcal{B}}\mid T\subseteq K\}|=\lambda.

The following result connects a specific class of strongly regular graphs with symmetric 2-designs (
[
10
, p. 258]
) via neighborhoods. It is a so-called
neighborhood design
(another can be made considering the open neighborhood).

Theorem 2.11
.

If
\Gamma=(V,E)\in\SRG(v,k,\mu-2,\mu)
, then the set
{\mathcal{B}}_{\Gamma}:=\left\{N_{\Gamma}(x)\mid x\in V\right\}
of all neighborhoods determines a symmetric
2\text{-}(v,k+1,\mu)
design with point set
V
.

3
Strongly regular
q
-ary graphs

In this section, we will introduce and discuss (strongly regular)
q
-ary graphs. When defining the
q
-analog of a graph, we consider a vector space
V
over a finite field
{\mathbb{F}}_{q}
, replacing the set in the graph definition. For a (classical) graph, the edges correspond to 2-subsets, so it is natural to define the edges of a
q
-ary graph as 2-dimensional subspaces of
V
. We mentioned in Remark
2.2
that the vertices of a graph can be considered both as the elements of a set and as its 1-subsets. Looking at the former interpretation one could consider the vectors of
V
as the vertices of the
q
-analog of a graph. However, in that case there is a unique vertex, namely the zero vector, which is automatically contained in every edge, a degenerate situation. So, it is natural to define a
q
-ary graph as follows. We use the notation
\genfrac{[}{]}{0.0pt}{1}{V}{s}
for the set of
s
-dimensional subspaces of the vector space
V
.

Definition 3.1
.

A
q
-ary graph
{\mathcal{E}}
is a pair
(V,E)
where
V=\genfrac{[}{]}{0.0pt}{1}{W}{1}
and
E\subseteq\genfrac{[}{]}{0.0pt}{1}{W}{2}
, with
W
a vector space over
{\mathbb{F}}_{q}
. The elements of
V
and
E
are called
vertices
and
edges
, respectively. If
\langle X,Y\rangle
is an edge, then we say that the vertices
X
and
Y
are
adjacent
.

Remark 3.2
.

A
q
-ary graph is thus a hypergraph whose vertices are the vector lines of a vector space
V
over
{\mathbb{F}}_{q}
, and whose edges correspond to vector planes of
V
. One can also consider this in the context of a projective geometry. Then the vertices of a
q
-ary graph are the points of the projective space
\PG(W)
and the edges are lines of this projective space; the edge set is thus a subset of the line set.

We now define the neighborhood similarly to how we define the neighborhood for classical graphs (Definition
2.3
).

Definition 3.3
.

For a vertex
X
of a
q
-ary graph
{\mathcal{E}}
we define its neighborhood
N_{{\mathcal{E}}}(X)
as the set of all vertices adjacent to it, and itself.

Remark 3.4
.

We note the similarity with Remark
2.4
. For an isolated vertex
X
(a vertex without neighbors) of a graph
{\mathcal{E}}=(V,E)
we have
N_{{\mathcal{E}}}(X)=X
. For a non-isolated vertex
X
we have
N_{{\mathcal{E}}}(X)=\cup_{X\in e\in E}\;e
.

We now introduce regular
q
-ary graphs. Note the similarity with Definition
2.6
.

Definition 3.5
.

A
q
-ary graph
{\mathcal{E}}
is
k
-regular if for any vertex
X
the neighborhood
N_{{\mathcal{E}}}(X)
is a
(k+1)
-dimensional subspace. The set of all
k
-regular
q
-ary graphs with underlying vector space
{\mathbb{F}}_{q}^{v}
will be denoted by
\RG(v,k;q)
.

If all the neighborhoods are
(k+1)
-dimensional, we call a
q
-ary graph
k
-regular instead of
(k+1)
-regular, to preserve to analogy with
k
-regular graphs, where the neighborhood of each vertex has size
k+1
. Alternatively, we could say that for a
k
-regular graph
\Gamma
we have
|N_{\Gamma}(x)\setminus\{x\}|=k
for any vertex
x
, while for a
k
-regular
q
-ary graph
{\mathcal{E}}
we have
\dim(N_{{\mathcal{E}}}(X)/X)=k
for any vertex
X
.

Remark 3.6
.

A
k
-regular
q
-ary graph
{\mathcal{E}}
on a vector space
V
can be identified with the map
\genfrac{[}{]}{0.0pt}{1}{V}{1}\to\genfrac{[}{]}{0.0pt}{1}{V}{k+1}:X\mapsto N_{{\mathcal{E}}}(X)
. This is a
q
-analog for the map described in Remark
2.5
.

We now introduce strongly regular
q
-ary graphs, as a
q
-analog to the classical strongly regular graphs (see Definition
2.8
).

Definition 3.7
.

A regular
q
-ary graph
{\mathcal{E}}\in\RG(v,k;q)
is a strongly regular
q
-ary graph with parameters
(v,k,\lambda,\mu;q)
if and only if for all vertices
X,Y
, with
X\neq Y
, we have that

|(N_{{\mathcal{E}}}(X)\cap N_{{\mathcal{E}}}(Y))\setminus\{X,Y\}|=\begin{cases}\lambda&\text{if $X$ and $Y$ are adjacent,}\\
\mu&\text{otherwise.}\end{cases}

The set of all strongly regular
q
-ary graphs with these parameters
(v,k,\lambda,\mu)
is denoted by
\SRG(v,k,\lambda,\mu;q)
.

Remark 3.8
.

A
k
-regular
q
-ary graph
{\mathcal{E}}
in
{\mathbb{F}}_{q}^{v}
can be considered as a set of lines in
\PG(v-1,q)
, such that for any point
P
the union of the lines from
{\mathcal{E}}
that contain
P
is a
k
-subspace of
\PG(v-1,q)
, see also Remark
3.2
. A
k
-regular
q
-ary graph
{\mathcal{E}}
in
{\mathbb{F}}_{q}^{v}
is strongly regular with parameters
(v,k,\lambda,\mu)
if for every two distinct points
Y
and
Z
the number of points
X
such that both lines
XY
and
XZ
correspond to edges of
{\mathcal{E}}
is equal to
\lambda
if the line
YZ
corresponds to an edge of
{\mathcal{E}}
, and
\mu
otherwise.

We now prove an analog for Theorem
2.9
, stressing the similarity between strongly regular graphs and strongly regular
q
-ary graphs. We use the notation

[a]_{q}=\sum_{i=0}^{a-1}q^{i}=\frac{q^{a}-1}{q-1}=\left|\genfrac{[}{]}{0.0pt}{0}{\F^{a}_{q}}{1}\right|.

The number of vertices of a graph in
\RG(v,k;q)
thus equals
[v]_{q}
.

Theorem 3.9
.

If there is a strongly regular
q
-ary graph with parameters
(v,k,\lambda,\mu;q)
, then

\left([k+1]_{q}-1\right)\left([k+1]_{q}-2-\lambda\right)=\left([v]_{q}-[k+1]_{q}\right)\mu.

Proof.

Let
{\mathcal{E}}=(V,E)\in\SRG(v,k,\lambda,\mu;q)
be a
q
-ary graph and consider a vertex
P\in V
. We count the tuples
(X,Y)\in V\times V
with
P\neq X\neq Y\neq P
,
\langle X,P\rangle,\langle X,Y\rangle\in E
and
\langle P,Y\rangle\notin E
, in two ways.

On the one hand, there are
\left([k+1]_{q}-1\right)
choices for
X
. Given
X
, there are
\lambda
vertices that are adjacent to both
P
and
X
, and so
X
has
\left([k+1]_{q}-1\right)-1-\lambda
neighbors that are different from
P
and not adjacent with
P
.

On the other hand, there are
\left([v]_{q}-[k+1]_{q}\right)
vertices that are not in the neighborhood of
P
, so this is the number of choices for
Y
. Given
Y
, there are
\mu
vertices that are a neighbor of both
P
and
Y
by the definition of a strongly regular graph. The statement follows.
∎

Remark 3.10
.

By considering the limit case
q\to 1
for the equation

([k+1]_{q}-1)([k+1]_{q}-2-\lambda)=([v]_{q}-[k+1]_{q})\mu

we obtain

(k+1-1)(k+1-2-\lambda)=(v-(k+1))\mu

which simplifies to the equation in Theorem
2.9
.

We now present some examples of strongly regular
q
-ary graphs.

Example 3.11
.

For a vector space
V={\mathbb{F}}_{q}^{v}
the set
E=\genfrac{[}{]}{0.0pt}{1}{V}{2}
contains all
2
-dimensional subspaces of
V
. This edge set determines a trivial regular
q
-ary graph
{\mathcal{E}}
which we call the
complete
q
-ary graph
. For any vertex
X\in\genfrac{[}{]}{0.0pt}{1}{V}{1}
we find
N_{{\mathcal{E}}}\left(X\right)=V
. Hence, we have
{\mathcal{E}}\in\RG(v,v-1;q)
.

Each distinct pair of
1
-dimensional subspaces of
V
span an element of
E
. Moreover, they are both adjacent to the
[v]_{q}-2
other vertices (all other 1-dimensional subspaces). Therefore,
{\mathcal{E}}\in\SRG(v,v-1,[v]_{q}-2,\mu;q)
for any
\mu
. Note that
\mu
is not defined for this
q
-ary graphs since there are no two non-adjacent 1-dimensional subspaces in
{\mathcal{E}}
.

This
q
-ary graph is the
q
-analog of the complete graph on
v
vertices, which has parameters
(v,v-1,v-2,\mu)
,
\mu
arbitrary. Any graph can be considered as a subset of the complete graph, and similarly any
q
-ary graph can be considered as a subset of the complete
q
-ary graph.

Example 3.12
.

Consider a vector space
V={\mathbb{F}}_{q}^{v}
and a set
\mathcal{S}\subseteq\genfrac{[}{]}{0.0pt}{1}{V}{t}
such that each 1-dimensional subspace is contained in precisely one element of
\mathcal{S}
,
t\geq 2
. In a projective setting, this is called a
(t-1)
-spread of
\PG(v-1,q)
, and it is known to exist if and only if
t\mid v
(see
[
12
]
). Note that
|\mathcal{S}|=\frac{q^{v}-1}{q^{t}-1}
. Consider now the set
E
of all vector planes which are contained in an element of
\mathcal{S}
; it defines a
q
-ary graph
{\mathcal{E}}
. Then,
{\mathcal{E}}\in\SRG(v,t-1,[t]_{q}-2,0;q)
. It is the disjoint union of
\frac{q^{v}-1}{q^{t}-1}
complete
q
-ary graphs on a
t
-dimensional vector space. This is a
q
-analog of a graph
\Gamma
on
v
vertices which is the disjoint union of
\frac{v}{t}
complete graphs on
t
vertices; we know
\Gamma\in\SRG(v,t-1,t-2,0)
. If
t=v
, then we find the complete (
q
-ary) graph.

In particular, for
t=2
and
v
even, any graph in
\RG(v,1)
is a disjoint union of
\frac{v}{2}
edges, and thus
\RG(v,1)=\SRG(v,1,0,0)
. Similarly, the edge set of any
q
-ary graph
{\mathcal{E}}\in\RG(v,1;q)
is actually a set of 2-dimensional subspaces such that each 1-dimensional subspace is contained in precisely one element of it, and as such
\RG(v,1;q)=\SRG(v,1,q-1,0;q)
.

Example 3.13
.

Let
\varphi
be a symplectic polarity on the vector space
{\mathbb{F}}_{q}^{v}
,
v
even, and let
{\mathcal{E}}
be the
q
-ary graph with as edge set the totally isotropic vector planes of
\varphi
. The totally isotropic vector planes through a vector line form a hyperplane, so
{\mathcal{E}}\in\SRG(v,v-2,\mu-2,\mu;q)
with
\mu=\genfrac{[}{]}{0.0pt}{1}{v-2}{1}_{q}
.

We now want to prove an analog for Theorem
2.11
. To that end we recall the definition of a
q
-analog of a design. These so-called
q
-designs have been the subject of a lot of investigations in recent years (see Section
1
).

Definition 3.14
.

A
t\text{-}(v,k,\lambda;q)
design
(\mathcal{P},\mathcal{B})
consists of a set of
p
oints
\mathcal{P}=\genfrac{[}{]}{0.0pt}{1}{\F_q^v}{1}
and a set of
blocks

{\mathcal{B}}\subseteq\genfrac{[}{]}{0.0pt}{1}{\F_q^v}{k}

such that

\forall\,T\in\genfrac{[}{]}{0.0pt}{0}{\F_q^v}{t}:|\{K\in{\mathcal{B}}\mid T\subseteq K\}|=\lambda.

Theorem 3.15
.

If
{\mathcal{E}}\in\SRG(v,k,\mu-2,\mu;q)
, with vertex set
V
, then the set

{\mathcal{B}}_{{\mathcal{E}}}:=\left\{N_{{\mathcal{E}}}(X)\mid X\in V\right\}

of all neighborhoods determines a
2\text{-}(v,k+1,\mu;q)
design with point set
V
.

Proof.

It follows immediately from the definitions that the blocks are
(k+1)
-subspaces. Now, consider a 2-dimensional subspace
\sigma
of the underlying vector space of
{\mathcal{E}}
. We need to check that there are
\mu
elements of
{\mathcal{B}}_{{\mathcal{E}}}
that contain
\sigma
. Consider two distinct 1-dimensional subspaces
X
and
Y
of
\sigma
(vertices in the
q
-graph setting, points in the
q
-design setting). Any subspace
N_{{\mathcal{E}}}(Z)
that contains
X
and
Y
contains
\sigma
, and vice versa. So,

\displaystyle|\{B\in{\mathcal{B}}_{{\mathcal{E}}}\mid\sigma\subset B\}|

\displaystyle=|\{B\in{\mathcal{B}}_{{\mathcal{E}}}\mid X,Y\subset B\}|

\displaystyle=|\{Z\in V\mid X,Y\in N_{Z}({\mathcal{E}})\}|

\displaystyle=|\{Z\in V\mid Z\in N_{X}({\mathcal{E}})\wedge N_{Y}({\mathcal{E}})\}|

\displaystyle=|N_{{\mathcal{E}}}(X)\cap N_{{\mathcal{E}}}(Y)|\;.

Since
{\mathcal{E}}\in\SRG(v,k,\mu-2,\mu;q)
, we know that
|N_{{\mathcal{E}}}(X)\cap N_{{\mathcal{E}}}(Y)|
equals
(\mu-2)+2=\mu
if
X
and
Y
are adjacent, and equals
\mu
if
X
and
Y
are not adjacent.
∎

It is well known that a
2\text{-}(v,k,\lambda;q)

q
-ary design produces a
2\text{-}([v]_{q},[k]_{q},\lambda)
design. Similarly, the following theorem holds.

Theorem 3.16
.

If there exists a strongly regular
q
-ary graph with parameters
(v,k,\lambda,\mu;q)
, then there exists a strongly regular graph with parameters
([v]_{q},[k+1]_{q}-1,\lambda,\mu)
.

Proof.

Let
{\mathcal{E}}\in\SRG(v,k,\lambda,\mu;q)
. Define a simple graph
\Gamma
whose vertices are the vertices of the
q
-graph, i.e. the 1-spaces of
{\mathbb{F}}_{q}^{v}
, where two vertices of
\Gamma
are adjacent if the corresponding vertices in
{\mathcal{E}}
are adjacent. It follows immediately from the definition of a strongly regular
q
-graph that
\Gamma
is strongly regular with parameters
([v]_{q},[k+1]_{q}-1,\lambda,\mu)
.
∎

4
Classification of the strongly regular
q
-ary graphs

We conclude this paper by classifying the strongly regular
q
-ary graphs. We first prove that there are three families of strongly regular
q
-ary graphs.

Lemma 4.1
.

If
{\mathcal{E}}\in\SRG(v,k,\lambda,\mu;q)
, then

•

{\mathcal{E}}
is the disjoint union of
\frac{q^{v}-1}{q^{k+1}-1}
complete graphs on the elements of a
(k+1)
-spread, or

•

v-2=k
, and
\lambda+2=\mu=\frac{q^{k}-1}{q-1}
, or

•

(v,k,\lambda,\mu)=(5,2,q-1,1)
.

Proof.

The neighborhood of a vertex of
{\mathcal{E}}
is a vector subspace of dimension
k+1
. Consequently, the common neighborhood of two (non-)adjacent vertices is also a subspace. Hence, there are integers
l,m
such that
\lambda=\frac{q^{l}-1}{q-1}-2
and
\mu=\frac{q^{m}-1}{q-1}
. From Theorem
3.9
, we then derive that

\displaystyle\left(\frac{q^{k+1}-1}{q-1}-1\right)\left(\frac{q^{k+1}-1}{q-1}-\frac{q^{l}-1}{q-1}\right)

\displaystyle=\frac{q^{m}-1}{q-1}\left(\frac{q^{v}-1}{q-1}-\frac{q^{k+1}-1}{q-1}\right)

\displaystyle\Leftrightarrow

\displaystyle q^{l+1}\left(q^{k}-1\right)\left(q^{k-l+1}-1\right)

\displaystyle=q^{k+1}\left(q^{m}-1\right)\left(q^{v-k-1}-1\right)\;.

(1)

Consequently, if both sides are different from zero, we have
k=l
. Note that
q^{k}-1>0
, so we have one of the three following cases.

•

If
q^{k-l+1}-1=q^{m}-1=0
, then
\mu=0
and
\lambda+2=\frac{q^{l}-1}{q-1}=\frac{q^{k+1}-1}{q-1}
, which means that any two adjacent vertices have exactly the same neighborhood, and two non-adjacent vertices have no common neighbors. Hence,
{\mathbb{F}}_{q}^{v}
is partitioned in
\frac{q^{v}-1}{q^{k+1}-1}
subspaces of dimension
k+1
, such that the edges of
{\mathcal{E}}
are precisely the vector planes in these subspaces. I.e.,
{\mathcal{E}}
is the disjoint union of
\frac{q^{v}-1}{q^{k+1}-1}
complete graphs on the elements of a
(k+1)
-spread of
{\mathbb{F}}_{q}^{v}
.

•

If
q^{k-l+1}-1=q^{v-k-1}-1=0
, then
v=k+1=l
, so
{\mathcal{E}}
is a complete graph. This is a particular instance of the first possibility in the statement of the theorem.

•

If
k=l
, we have that
q^{k-l+1}-1=q-1>0
, and consequently
m\geq 1
. Equation (
4
) reduces to

\displaystyle\left(q^{k}-1\right)\left(q-1\right)

\displaystyle=\left(q^{m}-1\right)\left(q^{v-k-1}-1\right)

\displaystyle\Leftrightarrow

\displaystyle q^{k}-q^{k-1}-1

\displaystyle=q^{v-k-2+m}-q^{m-1}-q^{v-k-2}\;.

(2)

We again distinguish between two cases.

–

If
m>1
, then reducing (
•
‣
4
) modulo
q
, we see that
v-k-2=0
. From (
•
‣
4
) it then follows that
q^{k-1}(q-1)=q^{m-1}(q-1)
, so
m=k
. We find the second possibility in the statement of the theorem.

–

If
m=1
, then
q^{k-1}(q-1)=q^{v-k-2}(q-1)
, so
v=2k+1
. Clearly, then
\mu=1
. Let
P_{0},P_{1},P_{2}
be vertices such that
P_{1}
is adjacent with both
P_{0}
and
P_{2}
, and
P_{0}
and
P_{2}
are non-adjacent; note that this is always possible. Let
\alpha_{i}=N_{{\mathcal{E}}}(P_{i})\in\genfrac{[}{]}{0.0pt}{1}{\F^{v}_{q}}{k+1}
,

Lemma 4.2
.

If
{\mathcal{E}}=(V,E)\in SRG(k+2,k,\mu-2,\mu;q)
with
\mu=\frac{q^{k}-1}{q-1}
, then
{\mathcal{E}}
is a
q
-ary graph described in Example
3.13
.

Proof.

For each
X\in V
, its neighborhood
N_{{\mathcal{E}}}(X)
is a hyperplane in
{\mathbb{F}}^{k+2}_{q}
. So, for
P\in V
and
e\in E
either
e
is contained in
N_{{\mathcal{E}}}(P)
or it shares a vector line with
N_{{\mathcal{E}}}(P)
. Hence, the point-line geometry
(V,E)
fulfills the one-or-all axiom. Since no point is collinear to all other points, the geometry
(V,E)
is a (non-degenerate) polar space by the Buekenhout-Shult axioms (see
[
5
]
). The corresponding polarity maps a vector line on the hyperplane that is its neighborhood. Since all vector lines are totally isotropic this polarity is necessarily symplectic. So,
{\mathcal{E}}
indeed corresponds to Example
3.13
.
∎

Lemma 4.3
.

There are no strongly regular
q
-ary graphs with parameters
(5,2,q-1,1;q)
.

Proof.

Assume that
{\mathcal{E}}\in\SRG(5,2,q-1,1;q)
. Denote its set of vertices, i.e.
\genfrac{[}{]}{0.0pt}{1}{\F^{5}_{q}}{1}
, by
\mathcal{P}
, and its set of edges by
\mathcal{L}
. Let
G
be the simple, bipartite graph with vertex set
\mathcal{P}\cup\mathcal{L}
where
X\in\mathcal{P}
is adjacent with
Y\in\mathcal{L}
if
X\subset Y
(considered as vector subspaces). Since a vector plane contains
q+1
vector lines, and since there are
q+1
vector planes through a vector line in a 3-space, the graph
G
is
(q+1)
-regular. It is immediate that
|\mathcal{P}|=\frac{q^{5}-1}{q-1}
, and by double counting the adjacent pairs
(X,Y)\in\mathcal{P}\times\mathcal{L}
, we get that
|\mathcal{L}|=\frac{q^{5}-1}{q-1}
.

From
\lambda=q-1
it follows that no two elements of
\mathcal{P}
have the same neighborhood in
{\mathcal{E}}
. Since neighborhoods corresponds to 3-spaces in
{\mathbb{F}}^{5}_{q}
this implies that there are no cycles of length six in
G
; note that there are no cycles of length 4 in
G
since two vector lines determine a unique vector plane. If there would be a cycle of length 8 in
G
, then there are
X_{1},X_{2},X_{3},X_{4}\in\mathcal{P}
such that
X_{i-1},X_{i+1}\subset N_{{\mathcal{E}}}(X_{i})
and
X_{i+2}\not\subset N_{{\mathcal{E}}}(X_{i})
, where the indices are considered modulo 4. However, then
X_{2}
and
X_{4}
are both in
N_{{\mathcal{E}}}(X_{1})\cap N_{{\mathcal{E}}}(X_{3})
, contradicting
\mu=1
.

Consider
X,X^{\prime}\in\mathcal{P}
with
X\neq X^{\prime}
. If
X^{\prime}
is contained in
N_{{\mathcal{E}}}(X)
then
d_{G}(X,X^{\prime})=2
. If
X^{\prime}\not\subset N_{{\mathcal{E}}}(X)
then
d_{G}(X,X^{\prime})=4
since
\mu=1
for
{\mathcal{E}}
. For any
X\in\mathcal{P}
and
Y\in\mathcal{L}
we get that

•

d(X,Y)=1
iff
X\subset Y
,

•

d(X,Y)=3
iff
X\not\subset Y
and
Y
has a non-trivial intersection with
N_{{\mathcal{E}}}(X)
, and

•

d(X,Y)=5
iff
Y
and
N_{{\mathcal{E}}}(X)
intersect trivially.

It follows that for a vertex
Y\in G
that belongs to
\mathcal{L}
there are
q+1
vertices in
G
at distance 1 and
q(q+1)
vertices at distance 2. There are
q^{2}(q+1)
vertices at distance 3 since there are no cycles of length 6, and
q^{3}(q+1)
vertices at distance 4 since there are no cycles of length 8. We see that
1+q(q+1)+q^{3}(q+1)=|\mathcal{L}|
, so two vertices in
\mathcal{L}
are at distance at most
4
in
G
.

We conclude that the diameter of
G
is 5, and since there are no cycles of length 8, its girth must be 10. Consequently,
G
is the incidence graph of a generalized pentagon with order
(q,q)
. However, it follows from the classical Feit-Higman result (see
[
8
]
) that such generalized polygons do not exist, contradicting the assumption.
∎

Theorem 4.4
.

If
{\mathcal{E}}\in\SRG(v,k,\lambda,\mu;q)

\frac{q^{v}-1}{q^{k+1}-1}

(k+1)
-spread, or
{\mathcal{E}}

q

3.13

Proof.

This follows immediately from Lemmas
4.1
,
4.2

4.3
