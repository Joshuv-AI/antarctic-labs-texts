Sparse Linear Regression and Lattice Problems
Aparna Gupte, Neekon Vafa, Vinod Vaikuntanathan

Abstract
Sparse linear regression (SLR) is a well-studied problem in statistics where one is given a design matrix 𝐗 ∈ ℝ m × n \mathbf{X}\in\mathbb{R}^{m\times n} and a response vector 𝐲 = 𝐗 ​ 𝜽 ∗ + 𝐰 \mathbf{y}=\mathbf{X}\bm{\theta}^{*}+\mathbf{w} for a k k -sparse vector 𝜽 ∗ \bm{\theta}^{*} (that is, ‖ 𝜽 ∗ ‖ 0 ≤ k \|\bm{\theta}^{*}\|_{0}\leq k ) and small, arbitrary noise 𝐰 \mathbf{w} , and the goal is to find a k k -sparse 𝜽 ^ ∈ ℝ n \widehat{\bm{\theta}}\in\mathbb{R}^{n} that minimizes the mean squared prediction error 1 m ​ ‖ 𝐗 ​ 𝜽 ^ − 𝐗 ​ 𝜽 ∗ ‖ 2 2 \frac{1}{m}\|\mathbf{X}\widehat{\bm{\theta}}-\mathbf{X}\bm{\theta}^{*}\|^{2}_{2} . While ℓ 1 \ell_{1} -relaxation methods such as basis pursuit, Lasso, and the Dantzig selector solve SLR when the design matrix is well-conditioned, no general algorithm is known, nor is there any formal evidence of hardness in an average-case setting with respect to all efficient algorithms. We give evidence of average-case hardness of SLR w.r.t. all efficient algorithms assuming the worst-case hardness of lattice problems. Specifically, we give an instance-by-instance reduction from a variant of the bounded distance decoding (BDD) problem on lattices to SLR, where the condition number of the lattice basis that defines the BDD instance is directly related to the restricted eigenvalue condition of the design matrix, which characterizes some of the classical statistical-computational gaps for sparse linear regression. Also, by appealing to worst-case to average-case reductions from the world of lattices, this shows hardness for a distribution of SLR instances; while the design matrices are ill-conditioned, the resulting SLR instances are in the identifiable regime. Furthermore, for well-conditioned (essentially) isotropic Gaussian design matrices, where Lasso is known to behave well in the identifiable regime, we show hardness of outputting any good solution in the unidentifiable regime where there are many solutions, assuming the worst-case hardness of standard and well-studied lattice problems.

1.1
Our Results

In this paper, we show hardness results for sparse linear regression that address all of these questions. In a nutshell, and hiding some details, we show an instance-by-instance reduction from (a variant of) the
bounded distance decoding problem
on a lattice with a given basis
\mathbf{B}
to sparse linear regression w.r.t. a design matrix
\mathbf{X}
that is essentially drawn from the Gaussian distribution
\mathcal{N}(\mathbf{0},\bm{\Sigma})
, where
\bm{\Sigma}=\bm{\Sigma}(\mathbf{B})
is determined by
\mathbf{B}
.
In particular, this allows us to start from conjectured hard instances of lattice problems and construct (nearly) Gaussian design matrices (with a covariance matrix related to the lattice basis) for which
k
-SLR is hard, partially addressing Question
1
. Our main theorem (Theorem
1
; see also Remark
2
) addresses Questions
2
, handling a large range of sparsity parameters
k
, and
3
, by showing a fine-grained reduction.

As a secondary result, even for well-conditioned (essentially)
isotropic
Gaussian design matrices, where Lasso is known to behave well in the identifiable regime, we show hardness of outputting
any
good solution in the less standard
unidentifiable
regime where there are many solutions, assuming the worst-case hardness of standard and well-studied lattice problems.

Binary Bounded Distance Decoding.

Our source of hardness is lattice problems. Given a matrix
\mathbf{B}\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times d}
, the lattice generated by
\mathbf{B}
is

\mathcal{L}(\mathbf{B})=\{\mathbf{B}\mathbf{z}:\mathbf{z}\in\mathbb{Z}^{d}\}.

A lattice has many possible bases: indeed,
\mathbf{B}\mathbf{U}
is a basis of
\mathcal{L}(\mathbf{B})
whenever
\mathbf{U}
is a unimodular matrix, i.e., an integer matrix with determinant
\pm 1
. The minimum distance
\lambda_{1}(\mathbf{B})
of the lattice
\mathcal{L}(\mathbf{B})
is the (Euclidean) length of the shortest non-zero vector in
\mathcal{L}(\mathbf{B})
. Note that
\lambda_{1}(\mathbf{B})
does not depend on
\mathbf{B}
, only on
\mathcal{L}(\mathbf{B})
.
3
3

3

We actually use a slightly different definition of
\lambda_{1}(\mathbf{B})
which is unimportant for this exposition; we refer the reader to Section
2
for more details.

A canonical lattice problem is the bounded distance decoding (BDD): given a basis
\mathbf{B}
of a lattice, a target vector
\mathbf{t}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
, and a parameter
\alpha<1/2
, find a lattice vector
\mathbf{v}\in\mathcal{L}(\mathbf{B})
such that
\mathsf{dist}(\mathbf{t},\mathbf{v})\leq\alpha\cdot\lambda_{1}(\mathbf{B})
given the promise that such a vector exists (equivalently, that
\mathbf{t}
is sufficiently close to the lattice). By the bound on
\alpha
, there is a unique such vector. The BDD problem has been very well studied, especially in the last decade, and is widely believed to be hard, for instance, forming the basis of a new generation of post-quantum cryptographic algorithms recently standardized by
[
NIS
]
. The best algorithm known for BDD runs in time
2^{d+o(d)}

[
ADSD15
]
.
4
4

4

Aggarwal et al.
[
ADSD15
]
give an algorithm for solving the exact Closest Vector Problem (CVP), which immediately implies an algorithm for BDD.

Our hardness assumption is a variant called
binary BDD
(see, e.g.
[
KF15
]
) which asks for a vector
\mathbf{v}\in\mathbf{B}\cdot\{\pm 1\}^{d}

that is close to the target
\mathbf{t}
(as above), again under the promise that such a vector exists. Equivalently, given a vector
\mathbf{t}=\mathbf{B}\mathbf{z}+\mathbf{e}
where
\mathbf{z}\in\{\pm 1\}^{d}
and
\lVert\bm{\mathbf{e}}\rVert\leq\alpha\cdot\lambda_{1}(\mathbf{B})
, the problem is to find
\mathbf{z}
. Both BDD and binary BDD are believed to be hard for arbitrary lattice bases
\mathbf{B}
. Indeed, a canonical way to solve (binary as well as regular) BDD is to employ Babai’s rounding algorithm
[
Bab86
]
which works as follows: compute and output

\mathsf{round}(\mathbf{B}^{-1}\mathbf{t})=\mathsf{round}(\mathbf{z}+\mathbf{B}^{-1}\mathbf{e})=\mathbf{z}+\mathsf{round}(\mathbf{B}^{-1}\mathbf{e})~,

namely, round each coordinate of
\mathbf{B}^{-1}\mathbf{t}
to the nearest integer. This works as long as each coordinate of
\mathbf{B}^{-1}\mathbf{e}
is at most
1/2
, which happens as long as
\mathbf{e}
is small
and

\mathbf{B}
has a good condition number. In particular, Babai succeeds if
\alpha=O(1/\kappa(\mathbf{B}))
, where
\kappa(\mathbf{B})=\sigma_{\max}(\mathbf{B})/\sigma_{\min}(\mathbf{B})
is the condition number of
\mathbf{B}
.
In other words, the condition number of
\mathbf{B}
determines the performance of the algorithm.

To be sure, there are algorithms for
binary
BDD that perform slightly better than BDD: in particular,
[
KF15
]
show a
2^{O(d/\log\log(1/\alpha))}
-time algorithm for binary BDD for a large range of
\alpha
(see
Theorem
7
).
In particular, for
\alpha=1/\poly(d)
, this becomes a
2^{O(d/\log\log d)}
-time algorithm for binary BDD, whereas the best run-time for BDD algorithms in this regime is
2^{\Theta(d)}
.
More than that,
[
KF15
]
study binary BDD (and generalizations) in detail and give evidence of hardness: they show reductions from variants of
\mathsf{GapSVP}
and
\mathsf{UniqueSVP}
to (a slight generalization) of binary BDD, as well as a direct reduction from low-density subset sum to binary BDD
[
KF15
, Theorem 14]
. In fact,
[
KF15
]
use this reduction and their
2^{O(d/\log\log(1/\alpha))}
-time algorithm for binary BDD to give a state-of-the-art algorithm for low-density subset sum. Improving on this
2^{O(d/\log\log(1/\alpha))}
run-time bound for binary BDD would consequently give better algorithms for low-density subset sum.
5
5

5

We remark that the binary LWE (learning with errors) problem, where the LWE secret is binary, is
not
a special case of binary BDD even though LWE is a special case of BDD. Indeed, when writing a binary LWE instance as a BDD instance in the canonical way, only a part of the coefficient vector of the closest lattice point is binary. Thus, even though binary LWE is equivalent in hardness to LWE,

Our first result shows that for every lattice basis

1.2
Perspectives and Open Problems

We view our results as an initial foray into understanding the landscape of
average-case hardness
of the sparse linear regression problem. One of our results shows a distribution of average-case hard
k
-SLR instances (under worst-case hardness of lattice problems). However, the design matrices that arise in this distribution are very ill-conditioned; even though the
k
-SLR instances they define are identifiable, the design matrices are singular and have RE constant
0
. An immediate open question arising from this result is whether one can come up with a more robust average-case hard distribution for
k
-SLR.

More broadly, the connection to lattice problems that we exploit in our reduction inspires a fascinating array of open questions. To begin with, as we briefly discussed above, the recent improvements to
k
-SLR
[
KKMR21
,
KKMR23
]
proceed via pre-conditioning the design matrix. Analogously, the famed LLL algorithm of
[
LLL82
]
from the world of lattices is nothing but a pre-conditioning algorithm for lattice bases.
This leads us to ask: is there a constructive way to use (ideas from) lattice basis reduction to get improved
k
-SLR algorithms? We mention here the results of
[
GKZ21
,
ZSWB22
]
for positive indications of the effectiveness of lattice basis reduction in solving statistical problems. In the lattice world, it is known that if one allows for arbitrary (potentially unbounded-time) pre-processing of a given lattice basis, BDD can be solved with a polynomial approximation factor
[
LLM06
]
. Is a similar statement true for
k
-SLR? We believe an exploration of these questions and others is a fruitful avenue for future research.

Concurrent work.

The concurrent work of
[
BDT24
]
shows hardness of achieving non-trivial prediction error in sparse linear regression with Gaussian designs in the improper setting (where the estimator need not be sparse) by reducing from a slight variant of a standard sparse PCA problem. In their setting, they deduce a sample complexity lower bound of (roughly)
k^{2}
for efficient algorithms, where (roughly)
k
is possible information-theoretically, by assuming a (roughly)
k^{2}
sample complexity lower bound on efficient algorithms for the sparse PCA problem. (Their reduction produces instances in which the noise is standard Gaussian. Our main theorem is written in terms of worst-case noise, but we show a similar statement for independent Gaussian noise in Appendix
C
.)

\mathsf{SLR}
in the non-identifiable regime even for
well-conditioned
design matrices), we give the proof in Section
5
.

2
Preliminaries

For
n\in\mathbb{N}
, we use the notation
[n]:=\{1,2,\cdots,n\}
. We use the standard definitions of the
\ell_{1},\ell_{2}
, and
\ell_{\infty}
norms in
\mathbb{R}^{n}
, as well as the
\ell_{0}
“norm” which is defined as the
sparsity
, or number of non-zero entries of a vector
\bm{\mathbf{v}}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
. We use
\bm{\mathbf{bold}}
notation to denote vectors and matrices. For a matrix
\bm{\mathbf{A}}\in\mathbb{R}^{m\times n}
and
i\in[m]
, we write
\mathsf{col}_{i}(\bm{\mathbf{A}})\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
to denote the
i
th column of
\bm{\mathbf{A}}
. We let
I_{n\times n}
denote the standard identity matrix in
\mathbb{R}^{n}
. For
\bm{\mathbf{v}}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
, we use the notation
\mathsf{round}(\bm{\mathbf{v}})\in\mathbb{Z}^{n}
to denote the point-wise rounding function applied to
\bm{\mathbf{v}}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
(with arbitrary behavior at half integers). We use the standard definitions of the maximum and minimum
singular values
of
\bm{\mathbf{A}}
for
m\geq n
:

\sigma_{\min}(\bm{\mathbf{A}}):=\min_{\bm{\mathbf{v}}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}}\frac{\lVert\bm{\mathbf{A}}\bm{\mathbf{v}}\rVert_{2}}{\lVert\bm{\mathbf{v}}\rVert_{2}},\ \ \ \ \ \ \sigma_{\max}(\bm{\mathbf{A}}):=\max_{\bm{\mathbf{v}}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}}\frac{\lVert\bm{\mathbf{A}}\bm{\mathbf{v}}\rVert_{2}}{\lVert\bm{\mathbf{v}}\rVert_{2}}.

We use the notation
\kappa(\bm{\mathbf{A}})
to denote the
condition number
of
\bm{\mathbf{A}}
, defined as

\kappa(\bm{\mathbf{A}}):=\frac{\sigma_{\max}(\bm{\mathbf{A}})}{\sigma_{\min}(\bm{\mathbf{A}})}.

We use the notation
\mathcal{N}(\mu,\sigma^{2})
to denote the univariate normal distribution with mean
\mu
and standard deviation
\sigma
. Similarly, we use the notation
\mathcal{N}(\bm{\mathbf{\mu}},\bm{\mathbf{\Sigma}})
to denote the multivariate normal distribution with mean
\bm{\mathbf{\mu}}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
and positive semi-definite covariance matrix
\bm{\mathbf{\Sigma}}\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times n}
.
We emphasize that we do not require
\bm{\mathbf{\Sigma}}
to be positive definite.

2.1
Bounded Distance Decoding

We now define lattice quantities that will be useful for us.

Definition 1
.

For a full-rank matrix
\bm{\mathbf{B}}\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times d}
, the lattice
\mathcal{L}(\bm{\mathbf{B}})
generated by basis
\bm{\mathbf{B}}
consists of the set all integer linear combinations of the columns of
\bm{\mathbf{B}}
. As is standard, we define

\lambda_{1}(\bm{\mathbf{B}}):=\min_{\bm{\mathbf{z}}\in\mathbb{Z}^{d}\setminus\{\mathbf{0}\}}\lVert\bm{\mathbf{B}}\bm{\mathbf{z}}\rVert_{2},

which corresponds to the shortest distance between any two elements of
\mathcal{L}(\bm{\mathbf{B}})
. In this paper, since we consider a (
\pm 1
)
binary
version of the bounded distance decoding problem, we define the quantity

\lambda_{1,\mathsf{bin}}(\bm{\mathbf{B}}):=\min_{\bm{\mathbf{z}}_{1}\neq\bm{\mathbf{z}}_{2}\in\{1,-1\}^{d}}\lVert\bm{\mathbf{B}}\bm{\mathbf{z}}_{1}-\bm{\mathbf{B}}\bm{\mathbf{z}}_{2}\rVert_{2}.

Note that while
\lambda_{1}(\bm{\mathbf{B}})
is a basis-independent quantity for the lattice
\mathcal{L}(\bm{\mathbf{B}})
,
\lambda_{1,\mathsf{bin}}(\bm{\mathbf{B}})
depends on the basis
\bm{\mathbf{B}}
.

Lemma 1
.

For any matrix
\bm{\mathbf{B}}\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times d}
, we have
\sigma_{\min}(\bm{\mathbf{B}})\leq\lambda_{1}(\bm{\mathbf{B}})\leq\lambda_{1,\mathsf{bin}}(\bm{\mathbf{B}})\leq 2\sigma_{\max}(\bm{\mathbf{B}})
.

Proof.

It is immediate that
\lambda_{1}(\bm{\mathbf{B}})\leq\lambda_{1,\mathsf{bin}}(\bm{\mathbf{B}})
. Let
\bm{\mathbf{e}}\in\mathbb{Z}^{d}
be any standard basis vector. We have

\lambda_{1,\mathsf{bin}}(\bm{\mathbf{B}})=\min_{\bm{\mathbf{z}}_{1}\neq\bm{\mathbf{z}}_{2}\in\{1,-1\}^{d}}\lVert\bm{\mathbf{B}}(\bm{\mathbf{z}}_{1}-\bm{\mathbf{z}}_{2})\rVert_{2}\leq 2\lVert\bm{\mathbf{B}}\bm{\mathbf{e}}\rVert_{2}\leq 2\sigma_{\max}(\bm{\mathbf{B}})\lVert\bm{\mathbf{e}}\rVert_{2}=2\sigma_{\max}(\bm{\mathbf{B}}).

We also have

\lambda_{1}(\bm{\mathbf{B}})=\min_{\bm{\mathbf{z}}\in\mathbb{Z}^{d}\setminus\{\mathbf{0}\}}\lVert\bm{\mathbf{B}}\bm{\mathbf{z}}\rVert_{2}\geq\min_{\bm{\mathbf{z}}\in\mathbb{Z}^{d}\setminus\{\mathbf{0}\}}\sigma_{\min}(\bm{\mathbf{B}})\lVert\bm{\mathbf{z}}\rVert_{2}\geq\sigma_{\min}(\bm{\mathbf{B}}),

since all
\bm{\mathbf{z}}\in\mathbb{Z}^{d}\setminus\{\mathbf{0}\}
satisfy
\lVert\bm{\mathbf{z}}\rVert_{2}\geq 1
.
∎

Definition 2

(
\mathsf{BinaryBDD}_{d,\alpha}
)
.

Let
\mathsf{BinaryBDD}_{d,\alpha}
be the following worst-case search problem in dimension
d\in\mathbb{N}
with parameter
\alpha<1/2
. Given full rank
\bm{\mathbf{B}}\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times d}
and
\bm{\mathbf{t}}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
, output
\bm{\mathbf{z}}\in\{-1,1\}^{d}
such that
\lVert\bm{\mathbf{B}}\bm{\mathbf{z}}-\bm{\mathbf{t}}\rVert_{2}\leq\alpha\cdot\lambda_{1,\mathsf{bin}}(\bm{\mathbf{B}})
(if one exists). Equivalently, given
(\bm{\mathbf{B}},\bm{\mathbf{B}}\bm{\mathbf{z}}+\bm{\mathbf{e}})
for
\lVert\bm{\mathbf{e}}\rVert_{2}\leq\alpha\cdot\lambda_{1,\mathsf{bin}}(\bm{\mathbf{B}})
, output
\bm{\mathbf{z}}\in\{-1,1\}^{d}
.

Note that by definition of
\lambda_{1,\mathsf{bin}}(\bm{\mathbf{B}})
, the constraint
\alpha<1/2
guarantees the uniqueness of
\bm{\mathbf{z}}\in\{-1,1\}^{d}
. As an aside, the standard
\mathsf{BDD}
problem instead uses
\lambda_{1}(\mathbf{B})
and allows outputting any
\bm{\mathbf{z}}\in\mathbb{Z}^{d}
instead of
\bm{\mathbf{z}}\in\{-1,1\}^{d}
. Additionally, note that we could easily consider full-rank
\mathbf{B}\in\mathbb{R}^{d_{1}\times d}
for
d\leq d_{1}\leq O(d)
and our main results would all go through, and similarly for larger
d_{1}
with a slight change in the parameter dependence. Furthermore, even though we write
\bm{\mathbf{B}}\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times d}
, we will assume the entries of
\bm{\mathbf{B}}
have
\poly(d)
bits of precision. (This is just for simplicity and convenience; one can modify the corresponding algorithmic statements so that they run in polynomial time in the description length of the basis
\mathbf{B}
.)

We note that there is a reduction from general BDD to
\mathsf{BinaryBDD}
, at the cost of making the instance degenerate (in the sense of having a non-trivial kernel). See Appendix
A.3
.

We now recall standard spectral bounds for random Gaussian matrices.

Lemma 2

(As in
[
RV10
]
)
.

Let
\bm{\mathbf{R}}\in\mathbb{R}^{m\times d}
be such that
R_{i,j}\sim_{\mathrm{i.i.d.}}\mathcal{N}(0,1)
. For all
t>0
, we have

\Pr\left[\sqrt{m}-\sqrt{d}-t\leq\sigma_{\min}(\bm{\mathbf{R}})\leq\sigma_{\max}(\bm{\mathbf{R}})\leq\sqrt{m}+\sqrt{d}+t\right]\geq 1-2e^{-t^{2}/2}.

In particular, for
m\geq 16d
, by setting
t=\sqrt{m}/4
, we have

\Pr\left[\frac{\sqrt{m}}{2}\leq\sigma_{\min}(\bm{\mathbf{R}})\leq\sigma_{\max}(\bm{\mathbf{R}})\leq\frac{3\sqrt{m}}{2}\right]\geq 1-2e^{-m/32}.

Throughout the paper, let
\chi^{2}(m)
denote the chi-squared distribution with
m
degrees of freedom.

Lemma 3

(As in
[
LM00
]
, Corollary of Lemma 1)
.

Let
X\sim\chi^{2}(m)
, i.e.,
X
is distributed according to the
\chi^{2}
distribution with
m
degrees of freedom. For any
t\geq 0
, we have

\Pr[X\geq m+2\sqrt{tm}+2t]\leq e^{-t}.

In particular, setting
t=m/4
, we get

\Pr[X\geq 5m/2]\leq e^{-m/4}.

2.2
Sparse Linear Regression

We now define the problem of
k
-sparse linear regression (
k
-
\mathsf{SLR}
for short), which, for computational complexity simplicity, is phrased in terms of mean squared error (as opposed to mean squared prediction error).

Definition 3

(
k
-
\mathsf{SLR}
)
.

Given a design matrix
\bm{\mathbf{X}}\in\mathbb{R}^{m\times n}
, a target vector
\bm{\mathbf{y}}\in\mathbb{R}^{m}
, and
\delta>0
, output a
k
-sparse
\bm{\mathbf{\widehat{\theta}}}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
such that

\frac{\|\bm{\mathbf{X}}\bm{\mathbf{\widehat{\theta}}}-\bm{\mathbf{y}}\|^{2}_{2}}{m}\leq\delta^{2},

assuming one exists.

We follow the convention of
[
NRWY12
]
of working with design matrices
\bm{\mathbf{X}}
that are
column-normalized
:

Definition 4

(As in
[
NRWY12
]
)
.

We say a matrix
\widetilde{\bm{\mathbf{X}}}\in\mathbb{R}^{m\times n}
is
column-normalized
if for all
i\in[n]
,

\left\lVert\mathsf{col}_{i}(\widetilde{\bm{\mathbf{X}}})\right\rVert_{2}\leq\sqrt{m}.

Now, we show that a column-normalized
\widetilde{\bm{\mathbf{X}}}
also satisfies a spectral bound with respect to sparse vectors.

Lemma 4
.

Suppose
\widetilde{\bm{\mathbf{X}}}\in\mathbb{R}^{m\times n}
is column-normalized. Then,

\displaystyle\max_{\begin{subarray}{c}\lVert\bm{\mathbf{v}}\rVert_{2}=1,\\
\lVert\bm{\mathbf{v}}\rVert_{0}\leq k\end{subarray}}\lVert\widetilde{\bm{\mathbf{X}}}\bm{\mathbf{v}}\rVert_{2}\leq\sqrt{km}.

Proof.

Let
\bm{\mathbf{v}}
be a vector such that
\lVert\bm{\mathbf{v}}\rVert_{2}=1
and
\lVert\bm{\mathbf{v}}\rVert_{0}\leq k
. By the triangle inequality and column normalization, we have

\displaystyle\lVert\widetilde{\bm{\mathbf{X}}}\bm{\mathbf{v}}\rVert_{2}=\left\lVert\sum_{i\in[n]}v_{i}\cdot\mathsf{col}_{i}(\widetilde{\bm{\mathbf{X}}})\right\rVert_{2}\leq\sum_{i\in[n]}|v_{i}|\cdot\left\lVert\mathsf{col}_{i}(\widetilde{\bm{\mathbf{X}}})\right\rVert_{2}\leq\sqrt{m}\cdot\lVert\bm{\mathbf{v}}\rVert_{1}\leq\sqrt{km},

where the last inequality holds because
\lVert\bm{\mathbf{v}}\rVert_{1}\leq\sqrt{k}\lVert\bm{\mathbf{v}}\rVert_{2}=\sqrt{k}
.
∎

To analyze the performance Lasso algorithm on the instances of
k
-
\mathsf{SLR}
we obtain from our reduction, we slightly modify the analysis of
[
NRWY12
]
to allow for a more flexible definition of the
restricted eigenvalue
(RE) constant of a matrix. First, we define the following cone.

Definition 5
.

Let
S\subseteq[n]
and
\epsilon>0
. We define
\mathbb{C}_{\epsilon}(S)
, the
\epsilon
-cone for
S\subseteq[n]
, as follows:

\mathbb{C}_{\epsilon}(S):=\{\Delta\in\mathbb{R}^{n}:\|\Delta_{\bar{S}}\|_{1}\leq(1+\epsilon)\|\Delta_{S}\|_{1}\}.

The notation
\bar{S}
denotes the complement of
S
, namely
[n]\setminus S
, and the notation
\Delta_{I}\in\mathbb{R}^{|I|}
denotes the restriction of
\Delta
to coordinates in
I\subseteq[n]
.

A standard definition (e.g., as in
[
NRWY12
]
) sets
\epsilon=2
, but we will use the flexibility of setting
\epsilon
close to
0
. Now, we define the restricted eigenvalue condition.

Definition 6
.

Suppose
\widetilde{\bm{\mathbf{X}}}\in\mathbb{R}^{m\times n}
is column-normalized. Then, we say
\widetilde{\bm{\mathbf{X}}}
satisfies the
(\epsilon,\zeta)
-
restricted eigenvalue (RE) condition
for
S\subseteq[n]
if

\frac{\lVert\bm{\mathbf{\widetilde{X}}}\bm{\mathbf{\theta}}\rVert_{2}^{2}}{m\cdot\lVert\bm{\mathbf{\theta}}\rVert_{2}^{2}}\geq\zeta

for all
\bm{\mathbf{\theta}}\in\mathbb{C}_{\epsilon}(S)
.

Note that this restricted eigenvalue condition corresponds to a restricted form of a strong convexity condition over the loss function

\mathcal{L}(\bm{\mathbf{\theta}}):=\frac{1}{2m}\lVert\bm{\mathbf{y}}-\widetilde{\bm{\mathbf{X}}}\bm{\mathbf{\theta}}\rVert_{2}^{2}.

Also note that the
\ell_{1}
-regularization-based Lasso estimator does not output a
k
-sparse solution. To obtain a
k
-sparse solution, we truncate
\bm{\mathbf{\widehat{\theta}}}_{\lambda}
to the
k
entries of largest absolute value, to obtain the
thresholded Lasso
estimate
\bm{\mathbf{\widehat{\theta}}}_{\mathsf{TL}}
. Lemma 9 of
[
ZWJ14
]
shows that
\lVert\bm{\mathbf{\widehat{\theta}}}_{\mathsf{TL}}-\bm{\mathbf{\theta}}^{*}\rVert_{2}\leq 5\lVert\bm{\mathbf{\widehat{\theta}}}_{\lambda}-\bm{\mathbf{\theta}}^{*}\rVert_{2}
.

Now, we state a modified version of Corollary 1 of
[
NRWY12
]
, which tells us an error bound on the thresholded Lasso estimator, given our modified definition of the restricted eigenvalue condition.

Theorem 2

(Modified Version of Corollary 1 of
[
NRWY12
]
)
.

Let
\epsilon\in(0,2]
, let
\widetilde{\bm{\mathbf{X}}}\in\mathbb{R}^{m\times n}
be column-normalized, and let
\widetilde{\bm{\mathbf{y}}}=\widetilde{\bm{\mathbf{X}}}\bm{\mathbf{\theta}}^{*}+\widetilde{\bm{\mathbf{w}}}
for some
k
-sparse vector
\bm{\mathbf{\theta}}^{*}
supported on
S
for
S\subseteq[n],|S|=k
. Suppose
\widetilde{\bm{\mathbf{X}}}
satisfies the
(\epsilon,\zeta)
-restricted eigenvalue condition for
S
. As long as

\lambda\geq\frac{2+\epsilon}{\epsilon}\cdot\left\lVert\frac{1}{m}\widetilde{\bm{\mathbf{X}}}^{\top}\widetilde{\bm{\mathbf{w}}}\right\rVert_{\infty},

and
\lambda>0
, then any Lasso solution
\bm{\mathbf{\widehat{\theta}}}_{\lambda}
with regularization parameter
\lambda
satisfies the bound

\displaystyle\lVert\bm{\mathbf{\widehat{\theta}}}_{\lambda}-\bm{\mathbf{\theta}}^{*}\rVert^{2}_{2}

\displaystyle\leq O\left(\frac{\lambda^{2}k}{\zeta^{2}}\right).

While the bounds in the theorem statement above do not depend on column normalization of
\widetilde{\bm{\mathbf{X}}}
(or corresponding normalization of
\widetilde{\bm{\mathbf{w}}}
), our definition of the restricted eigenvalue condition needs the matrix to be column-normalized (in particular, so that the RE constant is scale invariant with respect to
\bm{\mathbf{B}}
). The
\epsilon
dependence in
\lambda
comes from modifying
[
NRWY12
, Lemma 1]
, which needs the bound on
\lambda
as above to guarantee that the optimal error
\widehat{\Delta}:=\bm{\mathbf{\widehat{\theta}}}_{\lambda}-\bm{\mathbf{\theta}}^{*}
satisfies
\widehat{\Delta}\in\mathbb{C}_{\epsilon}(S)
.

Theorem 3
.

Let
\epsilon\in(0,2]
, let
\widetilde{\bm{\mathbf{X}}}\in\mathbb{R}^{m\times n}
be column-normalized, and let
\widetilde{\bm{\mathbf{y}}}=\widetilde{\bm{\mathbf{X}}}\bm{\mathbf{\theta}}^{*}+\widetilde{\bm{\mathbf{w}}}
for some
k
-sparse vector
\bm{\mathbf{\theta}}^{*}
supported on
S
for
S\subseteq[n],|S|=k
. Suppose
\widetilde{\bm{\mathbf{X}}}
satisfies the
(\epsilon,\zeta)
-restricted eigenvalue condition for
S
. For an optimally chosen positive regularization parameter,
the thresholded Lasso solution
\bm{\mathbf{\widehat{\theta}}}_{\mathsf{TL}}
with satisfies the prediction error bound

\displaystyle\frac{1}{m}\|\widetilde{\bm{\mathbf{X}}}\widehat{\bm{\mathbf{\theta}}}_{\mathsf{TL}}-\widetilde{\bm{\mathbf{X}}}\bm{\mathbf{\theta}}^{*}\|_{2}^{2}\leq O\left(\frac{\lVert\widetilde{\bm{\mathbf{w}}}\rVert_{2}^{2}\cdot k^{2}}{\zeta^{2}\cdot\epsilon^{2}\cdot m}\right).

Proof.

From Theorem
2
, we know that for

\lambda\geq\frac{2+\epsilon}{\epsilon}\cdot\left\lVert\frac{1}{m}\widetilde{\bm{\mathbf{X}}}^{\top}\widetilde{\bm{\mathbf{w}}}\right\rVert_{\infty},

we have

\displaystyle\lVert\bm{\mathbf{\widehat{\theta}}}_{\lambda}-\bm{\mathbf{\theta}}^{*}\rVert_{2}

\displaystyle\leq O\left(\frac{\lambda\sqrt{k}}{\zeta}\right).

By
[
ZWJ14
, Lemma 9]
, we therefore have

\displaystyle\lVert\bm{\mathbf{\widehat{\theta}}}_{\mathsf{TL}}-\bm{\mathbf{\theta}}^{*}\rVert_{2}

\displaystyle\leq O\left(\frac{\lambda\sqrt{k}}{\zeta}\right).

Plugging in the optimal choice of
\lambda
yields

\displaystyle\lVert\bm{\mathbf{\widehat{\theta}}}_{\mathsf{TL}}-\bm{\mathbf{\theta}}^{*}\rVert_{2}

\displaystyle\leq O\left(\frac{\sqrt{k}}{\epsilon\cdot\zeta}\cdot\left\lVert\frac{1}{m}\widetilde{\bm{\mathbf{X}}}^{\top}\widetilde{\bm{\mathbf{w}}}\right\rVert_{\infty}\right).

Since
\widetilde{\mathbf{X}}
is column-normalized, we know

\displaystyle\left\lVert\frac{1}{m}\widetilde{\bm{\mathbf{X}}}^{\top}\widetilde{\bm{\mathbf{w}}}\right\rVert_{\infty}=\frac{1}{m}\max_{i\in[n]}\left|\left\langle\mathsf{col}_{i}(\widetilde{\mathbf{X}}),\widetilde{\bm{\mathbf{w}}}\right\rangle\right|\leq\frac{1}{m}\left\lVert\mathsf{col}_{i}(\widetilde{\mathbf{X}})\right\rVert_{2}\left\lVert\widetilde{\bm{\mathbf{w}}}\right\rVert_{2}\leq\frac{\left\lVert\widetilde{\bm{\mathbf{w}}}\right\rVert_{2}}{\sqrt{m}},

where the first inequality is due to Cauchy-Schwarz, and the second inequality is due to column normalization. Combining the two above inequalities, we have

\displaystyle\lVert\bm{\mathbf{\widehat{\theta}}}_{\mathsf{TL}}-\bm{\mathbf{\theta}}^{*}\rVert_{2}

\displaystyle\leq O\left(\frac{\sqrt{k}\cdot\left\lVert\widetilde{\bm{\mathbf{w}}}\right\rVert_{2}}{\epsilon\cdot\zeta\cdot\sqrt{m}}\right).

Since
\bm{\mathbf{\widehat{\theta}}}_{\mathsf{TL}}-\bm{\mathbf{\theta}}^{*}
is
2k
-sparse, we can apply Lemma
4
to get

\displaystyle\lVert\widetilde{\mathbf{X}}\bm{\mathbf{\widehat{\theta}}}_{\mathsf{TL}}-\widetilde{\mathbf{X}}\bm{\mathbf{\theta}}^{*}\rVert_{2}

\displaystyle\leq O\left(\frac{k\cdot\left\lVert\widetilde{\bm{\mathbf{w}}}\right\rVert_{2}}{\epsilon\cdot\zeta}\right).

Squaring and dividing by
m
gives the desired result.
∎

Remark 1
.

We remark that Theorems
2
and
3
imply that in the noiseless setting of
k
-
\mathsf{SLR}
, that is, when
\bm{\mathbf{y}}=\bm{\mathbf{X}}\bm{\mathbf{\theta}}^{*}
, Lasso not only achieves prediction error
0
but also recovers the ground truth
\bm{\mathbf{\theta}}^{*}
as long as the restricted eigenvalue constant is strictly positive. In particular, this means that any reduction showing the
\mathbf{NP}
-hardness of noiseless sparse linear regression must produce instances with design matrices with restricted eigenvalue
0
(unless
\mathbf{P}=\mathbf{NP}
).

However, there is no reason information theoretically that the prediction error should have dependence on the RE constant
\zeta
. We now state a bound on the prediction error of the optimal
\ell_{0}
predictor, which need not be computationally efficient.

Proposition 1
.

Let
\bm{\mathbf{X}}\in\mathbb{R}^{m\times n}
, and let
\bm{\mathbf{y}}=\bm{\mathbf{X}}\bm{\mathbf{\theta}}^{*}+\bm{\mathbf{w}}
for some
k
-sparse
\bm{\mathbf{\theta}}^{*}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
. The
\ell_{0}
-predictor

\bm{\mathbf{\widehat{\theta}}}\in\argmin_{\lVert\bm{\mathbf{\theta}}\rVert_{0}\leq k}\lVert\bm{\mathbf{X}}\bm{\mathbf{\theta}}-\bm{\mathbf{y}}\rVert_{2}^{2}

satisfies the prediction error bound

\frac{\lVert\bm{\mathbf{X}}\bm{\mathbf{\widehat{\theta}}}-\bm{\mathbf{X}}\bm{\mathbf{\theta}}^{*}\rVert^{2}_{2}}{m}\leq\frac{4\lVert\bm{\mathbf{w}}\rVert_{2}^{2}}{m}.

Proof.

By definition of
\bm{\mathbf{\widehat{\theta}}}
and since
\bm{\mathbf{\theta}}^{*}
is
k
-sparse, we know

\lVert\bm{\mathbf{X}}\bm{\mathbf{\widehat{\theta}}}-\bm{\mathbf{y}}\rVert_{2}\leq\lVert\bm{\mathbf{X}}\bm{\mathbf{\theta}}^{*}-\bm{\mathbf{y}}\rVert_{2}=\lVert\bm{\mathbf{w}}\rVert_{2}.

By the triangle inequality,

\lVert\bm{\mathbf{X}}\bm{\mathbf{\widehat{\theta}}}-\bm{\mathbf{X}}\bm{\mathbf{\theta}}^{*}\rVert_{2}\leq\lVert\bm{\mathbf{X}}\bm{\mathbf{\widehat{\theta}}}-\bm{\mathbf{y}}\rVert_{2}+\lVert\bm{\mathbf{y}}-\bm{\mathbf{X}}\bm{\mathbf{\theta}}^{*}\rVert_{2}\leq 2\lVert\bm{\mathbf{w}}\rVert_{2}.

The bound immediately follows by squaring and dividing by
m
.
∎

Comparing Theorem
3
and Proposition
1
highlights a critical computational-statistical gap: the prediction error for the Lasso algorithm depends quantitatively on the restricted eigenvalue condition of the design matrix, whereas the information-theoretic minimizer has prediction error that is completely independent of the restricted eigenvalue condition.

3
Reduction from Bounded Distance Decoding

Our main result in this section is a reduction from
\mathsf{BinaryBDD}_{d,\alpha}
to
k
-
\mathsf{SLR}
:

Theorem 4
.

Let
d,m,n,k
be integers such that
k
divides
d
,
m\geq 17d
and
n=k\cdot 2^{d/k}
. Then, there is a
\poly(n,m)
-time reduction from
\mathsf{BinaryBDD}_{d,\alpha}
in dimension
d
with parameter
\alpha\leq 1/10
to
k
-
\mathsf{SLR}
in dimension
n
with
m
samples. The reduction is randomized and succeeds with probability
1-e^{-\Omega(m)}
. Moreover, the reduction maps a
\mathsf{BinaryBDD}_{d,\alpha}
instance
(\bm{\mathbf{B}},\cdot)
to
k
-
\mathsf{SLR}
instances with design matrices
\mathbf{X}\in\mathbb{R}^{m\times n}
and parameter
\delta=\Theta(\lambda_{1,\mathsf{bin}}(\mathbf{B}))
such that
\bm{\mathbf{X}}
that can be decomposed as

\bm{\mathbf{X}}=\begin{pmatrix}\bm{\mathbf{X}}_{1}\\
\bm{\mathbf{X}}_{2}\end{pmatrix},

where the distribution of each row of
\bm{\mathbf{X}}_{1}\in\mathbb{R}^{(m-k)\times n}
is distributed as
\mathrm{i.i.d.}

\mathcal{N}(\mathbf{0},\bm{\mathbf{G}}_{\mathsf{sparse}}^{\top}\bm{\mathbf{B}}^{\top}\bm{\mathbf{B}}\bm{\mathbf{G}}_{\mathsf{sparse}})
for a fixed, instance-independent matrix
\bm{\mathbf{G}}_{\mathsf{sparse}}\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times n}
, and
\bm{\mathbf{X}}_{2}\in\mathbb{R}^{k\times n}
is proportional to a fixed, instance-independent matrix that depends only on
n
and
k
.

Remark 2
.

This reduction also gives us a
fine-grained
hardness result. Suppose there is an algorithm for
k
-
\mathsf{SLR}
in
n
dimensions with
m
samples that runs in time
\poly(m,k)\cdot(n/k)^{k^{1-\epsilon}}
. Then, the above reduction gives an algorithm for
\mathsf{BinaryBDD}_{d,\alpha}
in
d
dimensions that runs in time
\poly(d)\cdot 2^{d/k^{\epsilon}}
.

[
GV21
]
also prove a fine-grained hardness result for
k
-
\mathsf{SLR}
from a lattice problem, specifically the closest vector problem. However, the key difference is that their result is in the unidentifiable regime, whereas ours is in the identifiable regime, i.e. the
k
-SLR instances produced by the reduction from binary BDD have a unique solution. Further, their fine-grained hardness results are for worst-case covariates, whereas our hardness results produces covariates drawn from a Gaussian (with a covariance matrix determined by the binary-BDD basis).

3.1
Interpretations

We now interpret the distribution of the design matrices
\bm{\mathbf{X}}
in the reduction in Theorem
4
. As stated, the sub-matrix
\bm{\mathbf{X}}_{2}
is phrased as a worst-case design matrix, while
\bm{\mathbf{X}}_{1}
has i.i.d.
\mathcal{N}(\mathbf{0},\bm{\mathbf{G}}_{\mathsf{sparse}}^{\top}\bm{\mathbf{B}}^{\top}\bm{\mathbf{B}}\bm{\mathbf{G}}_{\mathsf{sparse}})
rows, where the covariance matrix is tightly related to the
\mathsf{BinaryBDD}_{d,\alpha}
instance
\bm{\mathbf{B}}
. (The definition of the fixed, instance-independent gadget matrix
\bm{\mathbf{G}}_{\mathsf{sparse}}
is given in Section
3.2
.)

There are two natural ways to remove the “worst-case” nature of
\bm{\mathbf{X}}_{2}
in our instances:

1.

Our reduction still holds if the
\bm{\mathbf{X}}_{2}
part of our design matrix is removed, and instead, one enforced an exact linear constraint on
\bm{\mathbf{\theta}}
(specifically,
\bm{\mathbf{G}}_{\mathsf{partite}}\bm{\mathbf{\theta}}=\mathbf{1}
, where
\bm{\mathbf{G}}_{\mathsf{partite}}
is defined in Section
3.2
). This corresponds to constraining the
k

Proof of Theorem

Before proving Theorem

Let

Lemma 5
.

The matrix
\bm{\mathbf{G}}_{\mathsf{sparse}}
invertibly maps
\mathcal{S}_{n,k}
to

.

Proof.

Observe that

\bm{\mathbf{z}}

be defined so that for the

is equal to the

th part of

. It then follows that

, as desired.

We now define a different gadget matrix,

, that will help enforce the regression vector to be partite and binary. The matrix

is once again block diagonal with

Lemma 6

Proof.

For the “only if” direction, observe that

-sparse and

For the “if” direction, suppose

enforces the sum of the entrices of

such that
\|\bm{\mathbf{\theta}}_{\bar{S}}\|_{1}\leq(1+\epsilon)\|\bm{\mathbf{\theta}}_{S}\|_{1}
. Let
\eta^{(i)}\in\mathbb{R}
be the
i
th nonzero entry of
\bm{\mathbf{\theta}}_{S}
, and let
\bm{\mathbf{\bar{\eta}}}^{(i)}\in\mathbb{R}^{n/k}
be the
i
th part of
\bm{\mathbf{\theta}}_{\bar{S}}
. Note that without loss of generality, we can assume that all the entries of
\bm{\mathbf{\theta}}_{S}
are non-negative, that is, for all
i
,
\eta^{(i)}\geq 0
. If not, suppose
\eta^{(i)}<0
for some
i\in[k]
. Because of the block diagonal structure of
\bm{\mathbf{G}}_{\mathsf{sparse}}
, we can simply negate the
i
th part of
\bm{\mathbf{\theta}}
so that none of the norms change.

By applying Lemma
9
to the
\ell_{1}
norm of the parts of
\bm{\mathbf{\theta}}_{S}
and
\bm{\mathbf{\theta}}_{\bar{S}}
, we know that there is some part
i\in[k]
such that
\eta^{(i)}\geq\epsilon/k
and
\|\bm{\mathbf{\bar{\eta}}}^{(i)}\|_{1}\leq(1+10\epsilon)\eta^{(i)}
. We will consider this part
i
, and we will write
\eta,\bm{\mathbf{\bar{\eta}}}
instead of
\eta^{(i)},\bm{\mathbf{\bar{\eta}}}^{(i)}
for simplicity, i.e.,

\displaystyle\sum_{j}|\bar{\eta}_{j}|=\|\bm{\mathbf{\bar{\eta}}}\|_{1}\leq(1+10\epsilon)\eta.

(8)

We will provide a lower bound on
\|\bm{\mathbf{G}}_{\mathsf{sparse}}\bm{\mathbf{\theta}}\|_{1}
considering only the
i
th part. Since
\|\bm{\mathbf{G}}_{\mathsf{partite}}\bm{\mathbf{\theta}}\|_{2}\leq\sqrt{\zeta^{\prime}m}
,

\displaystyle\eta+\sum_{j}\bar{\eta}_{j}\leq\left|\eta+\sum_{j}\bar{\eta}_{j}\right|

\displaystyle\leq\frac{1}{\gamma}\|\bm{\mathbf{G}}_{\mathsf{partite}}\bm{\mathbf{\theta}}\|_{2}\leq\frac{\sqrt{\zeta^{\prime}m}}{\gamma}.

(9)

Adding (
8
) and (
9
), we get an upper bound on the sum of the positive entries in
\bm{\mathbf{\bar{\eta}}}
:

\displaystyle\sum_{j}\bar{\eta}_{j}\cdot\mathbbm{1}[\bar{\eta}_{j}>0]\leq\frac{\sqrt{\zeta^{\prime}m}}{2\gamma}+5\epsilon\eta.

(10)

Let
\bm{\mathbf{H}}\in\{-1,1\}^{d/k\times 2^{d/k}}
be a block in
\bm{\mathbf{G}}_{\mathsf{sparse}}
, i.e., whose columns are all of the vectors in
\{-1,1\}^{d/k}
. Let
\bm{\mathbf{h}}^{(j)}
denote the
j
th column of
\bm{\mathbf{H}}
. Without loss of generality, we can choose the location of the
\eta
entry within the block so that it corresponds to the column
\mathbf{1}\in\mathbb{R}^{d/k}
. To see this, if not, we can flip the sign of the rows of
\bm{\mathbf{H}}
that correspond to
-1
entries in the column corresponding to
\eta
. This has the effect of permuting the columns of
\bm{\mathbf{H}}
and has no effect on
\lVert\bm{\mathbf{G}}_{\mathsf{sparse}}\bm{\mathbf{\theta}}\rVert_{1}
or
\lVert\bm{\mathbf{G}}_{\mathsf{partite}}\bm{\mathbf{\theta}}\rVert_{2}
as the entries just flip sign. We will use the convention that first column of
\bm{\mathbf{H}}
is
\mathbf{1}
. By considering only part
i
, we have the inequality

\lVert\bm{\mathbf{G}}_{\mathsf{sparse}}\bm{\mathbf{\theta}}\rVert_{1}\geq\left\lVert\eta\mathbf{1}+\sum_{j=2}^{2^{d/k}}\bar{\eta}_{j}\bm{\mathbf{h}}^{(j)}\right\rVert_{1}\geq\sum_{\ell=1}^{d/k}\left(\eta+\sum_{j=2}^{2^{d/k}}\bar{\eta}_{j}h^{(j)}_{\ell}\right)=\frac{\eta d}{k}+\sum_{j=2}^{2^{d/k}}\bar{\eta}_{j}\sum_{\ell=1}^{d/k}h^{(j)}_{\ell}.

(11)

Since
\bm{\mathbf{h}}^{(j)}\neq\mathbf{1}
for all
j\geq 2
, we know for all
j\geq 2
,

\sum_{\ell=1}^{d/k}h^{(j)}_{\ell}\leq\frac{d}{k}-2,

as at least one entry in
\bm{\mathbf{h}}^{(j)}
is
-1
. This bound is helpful only when
\bar{\eta}_{j}<0
, as this will let us lower bound
\lVert\bm{\mathbf{G}}_{\mathsf{sparse}}\bm{\mathbf{\theta}}\rVert_{1}
. Thankfully, (
10
) gives us a bound on the sum of the positive
\bar{\eta}_{j}
contributions, in which case we use the trivial bound

\sum_{\ell=1}^{d/k}h^{(j)}_{\ell}\geq-\frac{d}{k}.

Let
J_{+},J_{-}\subseteq[2^{d/k}]\setminus\{1\}
denote the set of columns
j
where
\bar{\eta}_{j}\geq 0
and
\bar{\eta}_{j}<0
, respectively. Combining the above bounds with (
11
), we have

\displaystyle\lVert\bm{\mathbf{G}}_{\mathsf{sparse}}\bm{\mathbf{\theta}}\rVert_{1}\geq\frac{\eta d}{k}+\sum_{j=2}^{2^{d/k}}\bar{\eta}_{j}\sum_{\ell=1}^{d/k}

\displaystyle=\frac{\eta d}{k}+\sum_{j\in J_{+}}\bar{\eta}_{j}\sum_{\ell=1}^{d/k}h^{(j)}_{\ell}+\sum_{j\in J_{-}}\bar{\eta}_{j}\sum_{\ell=1}^{d/k}h^{(j)}_{\ell}

(12)

\displaystyle\geq\frac{\eta d}{k}-\frac{d}{k}\sum_{j\in J_{+}}\bar{\eta}_{j}-\left(\frac{d}{k}-2\right)\left|\sum_{j\in J_{-}}\bar{\eta}_{j}\right|

(13)

\displaystyle\geq\frac{\eta d}{k}-\frac{d}{k}\left(\frac{\sqrt{\zeta^{\prime}m}}{2\gamma}+5\epsilon\eta\right)-\left(\frac{d}{k}-2\right)\sum_{j=2}^{2^{d/k}}\left|\bar{\eta}_{j}\right|

(14)

\displaystyle\geq\frac{\eta d}{k}-\frac{d}{k}\left(\frac{\sqrt{\zeta^{\prime}m}}{2\gamma}+5\epsilon\eta\right)-\left(\frac{d}{k}-2\right)(1+10\epsilon)\eta,

(15)

where the penultimate inequality comes from (
10
), and the last inequality comes from (
8
). We now bound the remaining terms. Setting

\epsilon:=\frac{k}{50d},

and assuming

\gamma\geq\frac{5d\sqrt{\zeta^{\prime}m}}{\eta k},

(16)

we have the guarantee that

\frac{\sqrt{\zeta^{\prime}m}}{2\gamma}+5\epsilon\eta\leq\frac{\eta k}{10d}+\frac{\eta k}{10d}=\frac{\eta k}{5d}.

Furthermore, our setting of
\epsilon
guarantees

\left(\frac{d}{k}-2\right)(1+10\epsilon)=\frac{d}{k}-\frac{9}{5}-\frac{2k}{5d}<\frac{d}{k}-1.

Plugging these into (
15
), we have

\displaystyle\lVert\bm{\mathbf{G}}_{\mathsf{sparse}}\bm{\mathbf{\theta}}\rVert_{1}

\displaystyle\geq\frac{\eta d}{k}-\frac{d}{k}\left(\frac{\sqrt{\zeta^{\prime}m}}{2\gamma}+5\epsilon\eta\right)-\left(\frac{d}{k}-2\right)(1+10\epsilon)\eta

\displaystyle\geq\frac{\eta d}{k}-\frac{d}{k}\cdot\frac{\eta k}{5d}-\left(\frac{d}{k}-1\right)\eta

\displaystyle=\frac{4}{5}\eta\geq\frac{4\epsilon}{5k}=\frac{2}{125d},

where the last inequality comes from our application of Lemma
9
, which implies
\eta\geq\epsilon/k
. Recall that we get a contradiction if

\|\bm{\mathbf{G}}_{\mathsf{sparse}}\bm{\mathbf{\theta}}\|_{1}\geq\tau_{1}:=\frac{\sqrt{\zeta^{\prime}dm}}{\sigma_{\min}(\bm{\mathbf{R}}\bm{\mathbf{B}})}.

Therefore, setting
\zeta^{\prime}
so that

\frac{\sqrt{\zeta^{\prime}dm}}{\sigma_{\min}(\bm{\mathbf{R}}\bm{\mathbf{B}})}<\frac{2}{125d}

arrives us at a contradiction. Since
\sigma_{\min}(\bm{\mathbf{R}}\bm{\mathbf{B}})\geq\sigma_{\min}(\bm{\mathbf{R}})\sigma_{\min}(\bm{\mathbf{B}})\geq\sqrt{m_{1}}/2\cdot\sigma_{\min}(\bm{\mathbf{B}})
with probability at least
1-e^{-\Omega(m_{1})}
by Lemma
2
, it suffices to set

\zeta^{\prime}:=\frac{1}{2}\cdot\frac{m_{1}\sigma_{\min}(\bm{\mathbf{B}})^{2}}{125^{2}d^{3}m}=\Theta\left(\frac{\sigma_{\min}(\bm{\mathbf{B}})^{2}}{d^{3}}\right).

Now, we justify our assumption on
\gamma
in (
16
). Recall that our reduction sets

\gamma=\Theta\left(\frac{\sigma_{\max}(\bm{\mathbf{B}})\sqrt{dm}}{\sqrt{k}}\right).

By our setting of
\zeta^{\prime}
and since
\eta\geq\epsilon/k=1/(50d)
, we have

\gamma\gg\frac{\sigma_{\min}(\bm{\mathbf{B}})\sqrt{dm}}{k}\geq\Theta\left(\frac{\sigma_{\min}(\bm{\mathbf{B}})\sqrt{m}}{k\eta\sqrt{d}}\right)=\Theta\left(\frac{d\sqrt{\zeta^{\prime}m}}{\eta k}\right),

showing that (
16
) is indeed satisfied.

Finally, scaling by
Z^{2}
, we have

\zeta=\frac{\zeta^{\prime}}{Z^{2}}=\Theta\left(\frac{k\cdot\sigma_{\min}(\bm{\mathbf{B}})^{2}}{d^{4}\cdot\sigma_{\max}(\bm{\mathbf{B}})^{2}}\right),

as desired.
∎

Lemma 9
.

Suppose
x_{i},y_{i}\in\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}
for
i\in[k]
, such that
\sum_{i=1}^{k}x_{i}+\sum_{i=1}^{k}y_{i}=1
. Further suppose that for some
\epsilon\in(0,1/20)
,
\sum_{i=1}^{k}y_{i}\leq(1+\epsilon)\sum_{i=1}^{k}x_{i}
. Then, there is some
i^{*}\in[k]
such that
x_{i^{*}}\geq\epsilon/k
and
y_{i^{*}}\leq(1+10\epsilon)x_{i^{*}}
.

Proof.

Since
\sum_{i=1}^{k}x_{i}+\sum_{i=1}^{k}y_{i}=1
and
\sum_{i=1}^{k}y_{i}\leq(1+\epsilon)\sum_{i=1}^{k}x_{i}
, this implies that
\sum_{i=1}^{k}x_{i}\geq\frac{1}{2+\epsilon}\geq\frac{1}{2}-\epsilon
, and
\sum_{i=1}^{k}y_{i}\leq\frac{1}{2}+\epsilon
.

Suppose for contradiction that for all
i\in[k]
, either
x_{i}<\epsilon/k
or
y_{i}>(1+10\epsilon)x_{i}
. Let
A
be the set of all
i\in[k]
such that
x_{i}<\epsilon/k
, and let
B=[k]\setminus A
. By assumption, for all
i\in B
, we have that
y_{i}>(1+10\epsilon)x_{i}
. Then, since
\sum_{i\in A}x_{i}\leq\epsilon
,

\displaystyle\sum_{i\in B}y_{i}

\displaystyle>(1+10\epsilon)\sum_{i\in B}x_{i}=(1+10\epsilon)\left(\sum_{i\in[k]}x_{i}-\sum_{i\in A}x_{i}\right)

\displaystyle\geq(1+10\epsilon)\left(\frac{1}{2}-2\epsilon\right)=\frac{1}{2}+5\epsilon-2\epsilon-20\epsilon^{2}\geq\frac{1}{2}+2\epsilon.

This is a contradiction, since we also know that
\sum_{i\in B}y_{i}\leq\sum_{i\in[k]}y_{i}\leq\frac{1}{2}+\epsilon
.
∎

4.3
Prediction Error Achieved by Lasso on Our Instances

We now apply Theorem
3
to our instance as generated in Theorem
4
, to obtain a bound on the prediction error achieved by Lasso on our instances. Note that this is a mean squared prediction error bound as opposed to a mean squared error bound. To translate Lasso into an algorithm for
\mathsf{BinaryBDD}_{d,\alpha}
via the reduction in Theorem
4
, we can bound the mean-squared error by the triangle inequality (see Corollary
1
).

Lemma 10
.

Consider the instances
(\mathbf{X},\bm{\mathbf{y}},\delta=\Theta(\lambda_{1,\mathsf{bin}}(\mathbf{B}))
of
k
-
\mathsf{SLR}
instances obtained in the reduction from a
\mathsf{BinaryBDD}_{d,\alpha}
instance
(\mathbf{B},\cdot)
as in Theorem
4
. On these instances, the thresholded Lasso estimator
\bm{\mathbf{\theta}}_{\mathsf{TL}}
can achieve prediction error bounded as

\displaystyle\frac{1}{m}\lVert\bm{\mathbf{X}}\bm{\mathbf{\widehat{\theta}}}_{\mathsf{TL}}-\bm{\mathbf{X}}\bm{\mathbf{\theta}}^{*}\rVert^{2}_{2}\leq O\left(\frac{\alpha^{2}\cdot\lambda_{1,\mathsf{bin}}(\bm{\mathbf{B}})^{2}\cdot\kappa(\bm{\mathbf{B}})^{4}\cdot d^{10}}{k^{2}}\right).

Proof.

With the goal of applying Theorem
3
, we first bound
\left\lVert\widetilde{\bm{\mathbf{w}}}\right\rVert_{2}
. On our instances, we have

\widetilde{\bm{\mathbf{w}}}=\widetilde{\bm{\mathbf{y}}}-\widetilde{\bm{\mathbf{X}}}\bm{\mathbf{\theta}}^{*}=\frac{1}{Z}\begin{pmatrix}\bm{\mathbf{R}}\bm{\mathbf{e}}\\
\mathbf{0}\end{pmatrix}.

Therefore,

\left\lVert\widetilde{\bm{\mathbf{w}}}\right\rVert_{2}=\frac{1}{Z}\lVert\bm{\mathbf{R}}\bm{\mathbf{e}}\rVert_{2}\leq\frac{1}{Z}\sigma_{\max}(\bm{\mathbf{R}})\lVert\bm{\mathbf{e}}\rVert_{2}\leq O\left(\frac{\alpha\cdot\lambda_{1,\mathsf{bin}}(\bm{\mathbf{B}})\sqrt{m}}{Z}\right)

with probability at least
1-e^{-\Omega(m)}
over
\bm{\mathbf{R}}
by Lemma
2
. Since
Z=\Theta(\sigma_{\max}(\bm{\mathbf{B}})\cdot\sqrt{d/k})
, we have

\left\lVert\widetilde{\bm{\mathbf{w}}}\right\rVert_{2}\leq O\left(\frac{\alpha\cdot\lambda_{1,\mathsf{bin}}(\bm{\mathbf{B}})\sqrt{mk}}{\sigma_{\max}(\mathbf{B})\sqrt{d}}\right).

With this bound on
\left\lVert\widetilde{\bm{\mathbf{w}}}\right\rVert_{2}
, we can plug in Lemma
8
, which says that for
\epsilon=\Theta(k/d)
,
\widetilde{\bm{\mathbf{X}}}
satisfies the
(\zeta,\epsilon)
-restricted eigenvalue condition for all
k
-sparse,
k
-partite
S\subseteq[n]
for

\zeta=\Theta\left(\frac{k}{d^{4}\cdot\kappa(\bm{\mathbf{B}})^{2}}\right).

Plugging in
\left\lVert\widetilde{\bm{\mathbf{w}}}\right\rVert_{2}
,
\epsilon
, and
\zeta
into Theorem
3
, we get

\frac{1}{m}\|\widetilde{\bm{\mathbf{X}}}\widehat{\bm{\mathbf{\theta}}}_{\mathsf{TL}}-\widetilde{\bm{\mathbf{X}}}\bm{\mathbf{\theta}}^{*}\|_{2}^{2}\leq O\left(\frac{\lVert\widetilde{\bm{\mathbf{w}}}\rVert_{2}^{2}\cdot k^{2}}{\zeta^{2}\cdot\epsilon^{2}\cdot m}\right)\leq O\left(\frac{\alpha^{2}\cdot\lambda_{1,\mathsf{bin}}(\mathbf{B})^{2}\cdot\kappa(\mathbf{B})^{4}\cdot d^{9}}{k\cdot\sigma_{\max}(\mathbf{B})^{2}}\right).

Scaling up by
Z^{2}=\Theta(\sigma_{\max}(\mathbf{B})^{2}\cdot d/k)
to convert back to the unnormalized version, we have

\frac{1}{m}\|\bm{\mathbf{X}}\widehat{\bm{\mathbf{\theta}}}_{\mathsf{TL}}-\bm{\mathbf{X}}\bm{\mathbf{\theta}}^{*}\|_{2}^{2}\leq O\left(\frac{\alpha^{2}\cdot\lambda_{1,\mathsf{bin}}(\mathbf{B})^{2}\cdot\kappa(\mathbf{B})^{4}\cdot d^{10}}{k^{2}}\right),

as desired.

∎

Corollary 1

(Lasso-based Algorithm for
\mathsf{BinaryBDD}_{d,\alpha}
)
.

Let
d,k,m
be integers such that
k
divides
d
and
m\geq\Omega(d)

be a real number such that

-time reduction from

such that

5
Reduction from CLWE

We now define the continuous learning with errors (
\mathsf{CLWE}
) assumption as defined by
[
BRST21
]
, which holds assuming the quantum
[
BRST21
]
or classical
[
GVV22
]
hardness of worst-case lattice problems for various parameter regimes. We define
\mathbb{S}^{m-1}
to be the unit sphere in
\mathbb{R}^{m}
, i.e., the set of all unit vectors in
\mathbb{R}^{m}
according to the
\ell_{2}
norm. We write
\bm{\mathbf{v}}\sim\mathbb{S}^{m-1}
to denote sampling
\bm{\mathbf{v}}
from the uniform distribution over the sphere. Furthermore, for a vector
\bm{\mathbf{v}}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
, by
\bm{\mathbf{v}}\pmod{1}
, we mean the representative
\widetilde{\bm{\mathbf{v}}}\in[-1/2,1/2)^{n}
such that
\bm{\mathbf{v}}-\widetilde{\bm{\mathbf{v}}}\in\mathbb{Z}^{n}
. Moreover, by this choice of representative, note that we have
|a\pmod{1}|\leq|a|
for all
a\in\mathbb{R}
.

Definition 7

(CLWE Assumption
[
BRST21
]
)
.

For a secret vector
\bm{\mathbf{s}}\sim\gamma_{\mathsf{CLWE}}\cdot\mathbb{S}^{m-1}
and error vector
\bm{\mathbf{e}}\sim\mathcal{N}(0,\beta^{2}I_{n\times n})
, we have the computational indistinguishability

\displaystyle\left(\bm{\mathbf{A}}\sim\mathcal{N}(0,1)^{m\times n},\bm{\mathbf{b}}^{\top}:=\bm{\mathbf{s}}^{\top}\bm{\mathbf{A}}+\bm{\mathbf{e}}^{\top}\pmod{1}\right)

\displaystyle\approx

\displaystyle\left(\bm{\mathbf{A}}\sim\mathcal{N}(0,1)^{m\times n},\bm{\mathbf{b}}^{\top}\sim[-1/2,1/2)^{m}\right),

up to constant advantage for algorithms running in time
T
.

This assumption is parameterized by
m,n,\gamma_{\mathsf{CLWE}},\beta
and
T
. For example, for the parameter setting
\gamma_{\mathsf{CLWE}}=2\sqrt{m}
and
\beta=1/\poly(m)
, there are no algorithms known that run in time
T=2^{o(m)}
with
n=2^{o(m)}
samples.

Theorem 6
.

Suppose
k=m^{\epsilon}
for some
\epsilon\in(0,1)
. There is a
\poly(n,m)
-time reduction from
\mathsf{CLWE}
in dimension
m
with
n
samples and parameters
\gamma_{\mathsf{CLWE}}=2\sqrt{m},\beta=o(1/\sqrt{k})
to
k
-
\mathsf{SLR}
, for
n=k\cdot 2^{\Theta(m/k\cdot\log m)}
. If the
k
-
\mathsf{SLR}
solver runs in time
T
, the distinguisher for
\mathsf{CLWE}
runs in time
T+\poly(n,m)
. Moreover, the reduction generates design matrices
\bm{\mathbf{X}}
that can be decomposed as

\bm{\mathbf{X}}=\begin{pmatrix}\bm{\mathbf{X}}_{1}\\
\bm{\mathbf{X}}_{2}\end{pmatrix},

where the distribution of each row of
\bm{\mathbf{X}}_{1}\in\mathbb{R}^{m\times n}
is
\mathrm{i.i.d.}

\mathcal{N}(\mathbf{0},I_{n\times n})
, and
\bm{\mathbf{X}}_{2}\in\mathbb{R}^{k\times n}
is proportional to a fixed, instance-independent matrix that depends only on
n
and
k
.

The interpretations of Section
3.1
also apply here as to how one could remove the worst-case sub-matrix
\bm{\mathbf{X}}_{2}
.

Proof.

We first start with the
\mathsf{CLWE}
assumption (see Definition
7
), which says that for secret vector
\bm{\mathbf{s}}\sim\gamma_{\mathsf{CLWE}}\cdot\mathbb{S}^{m-1}
and error vector
\bm{\mathbf{e}}\sim\mathcal{N}(0,\beta^{2}I_{n\times n})
, we have the indistinguishability

\displaystyle\left(\bm{\mathbf{A}}\sim\mathcal{N}(0,1)^{m\times n},\bm{\mathbf{b}}^{\top}:=\bm{\mathbf{s}}^{\top}\bm{\mathbf{A}}+\bm{\mathbf{e}}^{\top}\pmod{1}\right)

\displaystyle\approx

\displaystyle\left(\bm{\mathbf{A}}\sim\mathcal{N}(0,1)^{m\times n},\bm{\mathbf{b}}^{\top}\sim[-1/2,1/2)^{m}\right).

We construct our design matrix
\bm{\mathbf{X}}\in\mathbb{R}^{(m+k)\times n}
and target
\bm{\mathbf{y}}\in\mathbb{R}^{m+k}
as

\displaystyle\bm{\mathbf{X}}:=\begin{pmatrix}\bm{\mathbf{A}}\\
\alpha\bm{\mathbf{G}}_{\mathsf{partite}}\end{pmatrix},\quad\bm{\mathbf{y}}=\begin{pmatrix}\mathbf{0}\\
\alpha\mathbf{1}\end{pmatrix},

with parameter

\delta:=\frac{1}{100\gamma_{\mathsf{CLWE}}\sqrt{m+k}}=\Theta\left(\frac{1}{\gamma_{\mathsf{CLWE}}\sqrt{m+k}}\right).

For a (
k
-sparse)
k
-
\mathsf{SLR}
solution
\bm{\mathbf{\widehat{\theta}}}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
to the instance
(\bm{\mathbf{X}},\bm{\mathbf{y}},\delta)
, we compute
\mathsf{round}(\bm{\mathbf{\widehat{\theta}}})\in\mathbb{Z}^{n}
and check if

\left|\bm{\mathbf{b}}^{\top}\mathsf{round}(\bm{\mathbf{\widehat{\theta}}})\pmod{1}\right|<\frac{1}{4}.

If so, we output
1
(indicating
\mathsf{CLWE}
), and otherwise, we output
0
(indicating null).

Soundness.

To see that we have a distinguisher for
\mathsf{CLWE}
, suppose
\bm{\mathbf{\widehat{\theta}}}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
is a
k
-
\mathsf{SLR}
solution, i.e.,

\frac{\lVert\bm{\mathbf{X}}\bm{\mathbf{\widehat{\theta}}}-\bm{\mathbf{y}}\rVert_{2}^{2}}{m+k}\leq\delta^{2}.

(17)

In particular, we know
\lVert\alpha\bm{\mathbf{G}}_{\mathsf{partite}}\bm{\mathbf{\widehat{\theta}}}-\alpha\mathbf{1}\rVert_{2}^{2}\leq(m+k)\delta^{2}=\frac{1}{100^{2}\gamma_{\mathsf{CLWE}}^{2}}
, or equivalently,

\lVert\bm{\mathbf{G}}_{\mathsf{partite}}\bm{\mathbf{\widehat{\theta}}}-\mathbf{1}\rVert_{2}\leq\frac{1}{100\alpha\gamma_{\mathsf{CLWE}}}.

Throughout, assume we set
\alpha
so that
100\alpha\gamma_{\mathsf{CLWE}}>2
. Each part of
\bm{\mathbf{\widehat{\theta}}}
must have exactly one non-zero entry, as
\bm{\mathbf{\widehat{\theta}}}
is
k
-sparse and cannot have any part with all zero entries. Moreover, we know this non-zero entry must be in
[1-1/(100\alpha\gamma_{\mathsf{CLWE}}),1+1/(100\alpha\gamma_{\mathsf{CLWE}})]
, so this entry must round to
1
. Therefore,
\mathsf{round}(\bm{\mathbf{\widehat{\theta}}})\in\mathcal{S}_{n,k}
, and
\mathsf{round}(\bm{\mathbf{\widehat{\theta}}})
and
\bm{\mathbf{\widehat{\theta}}}
have the same support. By considering the support of
\bm{\mathbf{\widehat{\theta}}}
, this implies

\lVert\mathsf{round}(\bm{\mathbf{\widehat{\theta}}})-\bm{\mathbf{\widehat{\theta}}}\rVert_{2}=\lVert\bm{\mathbf{G}}_{\mathsf{partite}}\bm{\mathbf{\widehat{\theta}}}-\mathbf{1}\rVert_{2}\leq\frac{1}{100\alpha\gamma_{\mathsf{CLWE}}}.

Moreover, we will set
\alpha=\max(\sqrt{n},3/(100\gamma_{\mathsf{CLWE}}))
so that

\lVert\mathsf{round}(\bm{\mathbf{\widehat{\theta}}})-\bm{\mathbf{\widehat{\theta}}}\rVert_{2}<\frac{1}{100\gamma_{\mathsf{CLWE}}\sqrt{n}}.

By (
17
), we also know

\lVert\bm{\mathbf{A}}\bm{\mathbf{\widehat{\theta}}}\rVert_{2}\leq\delta\sqrt{m+k}\leq\frac{1}{100\gamma_{\mathsf{CLWE}}}.

First, suppose we are the non-null case, i.e.,
\bm{\mathbf{b}}^{\top}=\bm{\mathbf{s}}^{\top}\bm{\mathbf{A}}+\bm{\mathbf{e}}^{\top}
. Since
\mathsf{round}(\bm{\mathbf{\widehat{\theta}}})\in\mathbb{Z}^{n}
, we then have

\displaystyle\left|\bm{\mathbf{b}}^{\top}\mathsf{round}(\bm{\mathbf{\widehat{\theta}}})\pmod{1}\right|

\displaystyle=\left|\bm{\mathbf{s}}^{\top}\bm{\mathbf{A}}\mathsf{round}(\bm{\mathbf{\widehat{\theta}}})+\bm{\mathbf{e}}^{\top}\mathsf{round}(\bm{\mathbf{\widehat{\theta}}})\pmod{1}\right|

\displaystyle\leq\left|\bm{\mathbf{s}}^{\top}\bm{\mathbf{A}}(\mathsf{round}(\bm{\mathbf{\widehat{\theta}}})-\bm{\mathbf{\widehat{\theta}}})\right|+\left|\bm{\mathbf{s}}^{\top}\bm{\mathbf{A}}\bm{\mathbf{\widehat{\theta}}}\right|+\left|\bm{\mathbf{e}}^{\top}\mathsf{round}(\bm{\mathbf{\widehat{\theta}}})\right|

\displaystyle\leq\lVert\bm{\mathbf{s}}^{\top}\bm{\mathbf{A}}\rVert_{2}\cdot\lVert\mathsf{round}(\bm{\mathbf{\widehat{\theta}}})-\bm{\mathbf{\widehat{\theta}}}\rVert_{2}+\frac{1}{100}+\left|\bm{\mathbf{e}}^{\top}\mathsf{round}(\bm{\mathbf{\widehat{\theta}}})\right|.

Since
\bm{\mathbf{X}}
and
\bm{\mathbf{y}}
are independent of
\bm{\mathbf{e}}
, and since
\mathsf{round}(\bm{\mathbf{\widehat{\theta}}})\in\mathcal{S}_{n,k}
, we know
\bm{\mathbf{e}}^{\top}\mathsf{round}(\bm{\mathbf{\widehat{\theta}}})\sim\mathcal{N}(0,k\beta^{2})
. By standard tails bounds on the Gaussian, we have
|\bm{\mathbf{e}}^{\top}\mathsf{round}(\bm{\mathbf{\widehat{\theta}}})|\leq O(\beta\sqrt{k})
with probability at least
99/100
. Similarly, since
\bm{\mathbf{s}}
and
\bm{\mathbf{A}}
are independent, we know
\bm{\mathbf{s}}^{\top}\bm{\mathbf{A}}\sim\mathcal{N}(0,\gamma_{\mathsf{CLWE}}I_{n\times n})
, so by Lemma
3
, we know
\lVert\bm{\mathbf{s}}^{\top}\bm{\mathbf{A}}\rVert_{2}\leq 10\gamma_{\mathsf{CLWE}}\sqrt{n}
with probability at least
1-e^{-\Omega(n)}
. Plugging these into our previous bound, for
\beta=o(1/\sqrt{k})
,

\displaystyle\left|\bm{\mathbf{b}}^{\top}\mathsf{round}(\bm{\mathbf{\widehat{\theta}}})\pmod{1}\right|

\displaystyle\leq\lVert\bm{\mathbf{s}}^{\top}\bm{\mathbf{A}}\rVert_{2}\cdot\lVert\mathsf{round}(\bm{\mathbf{\widehat{\theta}}})-\bm{\mathbf{\widehat{\theta}}}\rVert_{2}+\frac{1}{100}+\left|\bm{\mathbf{e}}^{\top}\mathsf{round}(\bm{\mathbf{\widehat{\theta}}})\right|

\displaystyle\leq\frac{1}{10}+\frac{1}{100}+o(1)<\frac{1}{4},

with probability at least
\frac{49}{50}
.

On the other hand, if we are in the null case, then
\bm{\mathbf{b}}\sim[-1/2,1/2)^{m}
independently of
\bm{\mathbf{\widehat{\theta}}}
, and consequently
\bm{\mathbf{b}}^{\top}\mathsf{round}(\bm{\mathbf{\widehat{\theta}}})\pmod{1}\sim[-1/2,1/2)
, in which case

\Pr\left[\left|\bm{\mathbf{b}}^{\top}\mathsf{round}(\bm{\mathbf{\widehat{\theta}}})\pmod{1}\right|\geq 1/4\right]=1/2
.

Therefore, we have a distinguisher for
\mathsf{CLWE}
with
\Omega(1)
advantage.

Completeness.

As a warm-up, we compute the expected number of solutions
\bm{\mathbf{\theta}}\in\mathcal{S}_{n,k}
. In particular, since
\bm{\mathbf{\theta}}\in\mathcal{S}_{n,k}
, we have
\bm{\mathbf{G}}_{\mathsf{partite}}\bm{\mathbf{\theta}}=\mathbf{1}
by Lemma
6
. It therefore suffices to show

\frac{\lVert\bm{\mathbf{A}}\bm{\mathbf{\theta}}\rVert_{2}^{2}}{m+k}\leq\delta^{2}

for
\bm{\mathbf{\theta}}
to be a solution. For simplicity, let
\delta^{\prime}=\delta\sqrt{m+k}=\frac{1}{100\gamma_{\mathsf{CLWE}}}=\frac{1}{200\sqrt{m}}
. Fix some
\bm{\mathbf{\theta}}\in\mathcal{S}_{n,k}
. The random variable
\bm{\mathbf{A}}\bm{\mathbf{\theta}}\in\mathbb{R}^{m}
(over the randomness of
\bm{\mathbf{A}}
) is distributed according to
\mathcal{N}(\mathbf{0},kI_{m\times m})
. Therefore,
\lVert\bm{\mathbf{A}}\bm{\mathbf{\theta}}\rVert_{2}^{2}/k\sim\chi^{2}(m)
. As a result,

p:=\Pr_{\bm{\mathbf{A}}}\left[\lVert\bm{\mathbf{A}}\bm{\mathbf{\theta}}\rVert_{2}^{2}\leq(\delta^{\prime})^{2}\right]=\Pr_{Z\sim\chi^{2}(m)}\left[Z\leq\frac{(\delta^{\prime})^{2}}{k}\right].

Using the CDF of the
\chi^{2}(m)
distribution and the lower incomplete gamma function
\gamma(\cdot,\cdot)
, we have

\displaystyle p=\Pr_{Z\sim\chi^{2}(m)}\left[Z\leq\frac{(\delta^{\prime})^{2}}{k}\right]

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\Gamma(m/2)}\cdot\gamma\left(\frac{m}{2},\frac{(\delta^{\prime})^{2}}{2k}\right)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{(m/2-1)!}\left(\frac{(\delta^{\prime})^{2}}{2k}\right)^{m/2}e^{-(\delta^{\prime})^{2}/2k}\cdot

\displaystyle\quad\quad\quad\sum_{j=0}^{\infty}\frac{\left(\frac{(\delta^{\prime})^{2}}{2k}\right)^{j}}{(m/2)(m/2+1)\cdots(m/2+j)}

\displaystyle=(1\pm o(1))\cdot\frac{(\delta^{\prime})^{m}}{(m/2-1)!(2k)^{m/2}}\cdot\frac{2}{m},

\displaystyle=\left(\frac{\delta^{\prime}}{\sqrt{mk}}\right)^{m(1\pm o(1))}

because in our setting,
(\delta^{\prime})^{2}/2k=o(1)
, so
e^{-(\delta^{\prime})^{2}/2k}=1-o(1)
and the infinite sum is dominated by its first term, and because
\delta^{\prime}=o(1)
and
\sqrt{mk}=\omega(1)
. Therefore, by linearity of expectation, the expected number of solutions
X
in
\mathcal{S}_{n,k}
satisfies

\E[X]=\left(\frac{n}{k}\right)^{k}\cdot p.

Setting
n
so that

p=\left(\frac{n}{k}\right)^{-k/10}

gives

\E[X]=\left(\frac{n}{k}\right)^{9k/10},\;\;n=k\cdot 2^{(10\pm o(1))m/k\cdot\log(\sqrt{mk}/\delta^{\prime})}=k\cdot 2^{\Theta(m/k\cdot\log(\sqrt{mk}\cdot\gamma_{\mathsf{CLWE}}))}.

We turn this expected value bound into a
1-o(1)
bound on the existence of a sparse solution in Appendix
B
.

A.1
Babai’s Rounding Off Algorithm

We analyze the “rounding off” algorithm given by
[
Bab86
]
and bound its performance in terms of the condition number
\kappa(\mathbf{B})
of the lattice basis
\mathbf{B}
.
10
10

10

For the other algorithm of
[
Bab86
]
, the nearest (hyper)plane algorithm, success occurs when
\alpha
is at most a quantity defined in terms of the norms of the Gram-Schmidt orthogonalization of the basis
\bm{\mathbf{B}}
, which cannot be cleanly bounded in terms of
\kappa(\bm{\mathbf{B}})
.
Given a full-rank lattice basis
\bm{\mathbf{B}}\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times d}
and target
\bm{\mathbf{t}}:=\bm{\mathbf{B}}\bm{\mathbf{z}}+\bm{\mathbf{e}}
for
\bm{\mathbf{z}}\in\mathbb{Z}^{d}
and
\lVert\bm{\mathbf{e}}\rVert_{2}\leq\alpha\lambda_{1}(\bm{\mathbf{B}})
, the rounding off algorithm simply outputs
\mathsf{round}(\bm{\mathbf{B}}^{-1}\bm{\mathbf{t}})\in\mathbb{Z}^{d}
.
11
11

11

Often, Babai’s rounding algorithm is performed on an LLL-reduced (
[
LLL82
]
) basis, and the resulting analysis of Babai’s algorithm depends on the properties of the LLL reduction. Here, we instead analyze how Babai’s algorithm would perform on a general basis in terms of the conditioning of the given basis. Note that lattice basis reduction does not preserve
\mathsf{BinaryBDD}_{d,\alpha}
solutions as
\lambda_{1,\mathsf{bin}}(\mathbf{B})
is basis-dependent.
To argue correctness, it suffices to show

\lVert\bm{\mathbf{B}}^{-1}\bm{\mathbf{t}}-\bm{\mathbf{z}}\rVert_{\infty}<\frac{1}{2},

as this would guarantee
\mathsf{round}(\bm{\mathbf{B}}^{-1}\bm{\mathbf{t}})=\bm{\mathbf{z}}
.

Lemma 11
.

For
\alpha<\sigma_{\min}(\bm{\mathbf{B}})/(2\lambda_{1}(\bm{\mathbf{B}}))
, Babai’s rounding off algorithm solves
\mathsf{BDD}
. The same is true for
\mathsf{BinaryBDD}_{d,\alpha}
for
\alpha<\sigma_{\min}(\bm{\mathbf{B}})/(2\lambda_{1,\mathsf{bin}}(\bm{\mathbf{B}}))
.

Proof.

For the case of
\mathsf{BDD}
, we have

\left\lVert\bm{\mathbf{B}}^{-1}\bm{\mathbf{t}}-\bm{\mathbf{z}}\right\rVert_{\infty}=\left\lVert\bm{\mathbf{B}}^{-1}\bm{\mathbf{e}}\right\rVert_{\infty}\leq\left\lVert\bm{\mathbf{B}}^{-1}\bm{\mathbf{e}}\right\rVert_{2}\leq\sigma_{\max}(\bm{\mathbf{B}}^{-1})\lVert\bm{\mathbf{e}}\rVert_{2}=\frac{\lVert\bm{\mathbf{e}}\rVert_{2}}{\sigma_{\min}(\bm{\mathbf{B}})}\leq\frac{\alpha\lambda_{1}(\bm{\mathbf{B}})}{\sigma_{\min}(\bm{\mathbf{B}})}<\frac{1}{2},

as desired. The proof follows very similarly for
\mathsf{BinaryBDD}_{d,\alpha}
.
∎

Corollary 2
.

For
\alpha<1/(4\kappa(\mathbf{B}))
, Babai’s rounding off algorithm solves
\mathsf{BDD}
and
\mathsf{BinaryBDD}
.

Proof.

Since
\lambda_{1}(\bm{\mathbf{B}})\leq\lambda_{1,\mathsf{bin}}(\bm{\mathbf{B}})\leq 2\sigma_{\max}(\bm{\mathbf{B}})
by Lemma
1
, we have

\alpha<\frac{1}{4\kappa(\mathbf{B})}=\frac{\sigma_{\min}(\mathbf{B})}{4\sigma_{\max}(\mathbf{B})}\leq\min\left(\frac{\sigma_{\min}(\bm{\mathbf{B}})}{2\lambda_{1}(\bm{\mathbf{B}})},\frac{\sigma_{\min}(\bm{\mathbf{B}})}{2\lambda_{1,\mathsf{bin}}(\bm{\mathbf{B}})}\right).

Therefore, we can invoke Lemma
11
.
∎

As a result, this algorithm is a better algorithm for

A.3
Reduction from BDD to Binary BDD

We briefly mention the existence of a reduction from (standard) BDD to
\mathsf{BinaryBDD}
, at the cost of making the basis
\mathbf{B}
for the
\mathsf{BinaryBDD}
instance have
\sigma_{\min}(\mathbf{B})=0
and therefore undefined
\kappa(\mathbf{B})
.
First, we note that there is a direct reduction from
\{0,1\}
-
\mathsf{BinaryBDD}
(i.e., where solutions are in
\{0,1\}^{d}
) to
\mathsf{BinaryBDD}
that preserves the basis
\mathbf{B}
by mapping the target
\bm{\mathbf{t}}
to
2\bm{\mathbf{t}}-\bm{\mathbf{B}}\mathbf{1}
.

Now, to reduce from standard BDD to
\{0,1\}
-
\mathsf{BinaryBDD}
, we first reduce the target
\bm{\mathbf{t}}
to lie in the fundamental parallelepiped of
\bm{\mathbf{B}}
, and then we apply LLL
[
LLL82
]
to get a
q:=2^{O(d)}
upper bound on the size of the resulting coefficients of the reduced solution
\bm{\mathbf{z}}\in\{0,1,\cdots,q-1\}^{d}
. For simplicity, we can round
q
up to the nearest power of
2
. Now, we use the gadget matrix

\bm{\mathbf{G}}:=I_{d}\otimes(1,2,\cdots,q/2)\in\mathbb{Z}^{d\times d\log_{2}(q)}

used ubiquitously throughout lattice based cryptography (e.g.,
[
MP12
]
) to map
\bm{\mathbf{z}}\in\{0,\cdots,q-1\}^{d}
to solutions
\bm{\mathbf{z}}^{\prime}\in\{0,1\}^{d\log_{2}(q)}
. Explicitly, for all
\bm{\mathbf{z}}\in\{0,\cdots,q-1\}^{d}
, there exists a unique
\bm{\mathbf{z}}^{\prime}\in\{0,1\}^{d\log_{2}(q)}
such that

\bm{\mathbf{z}}=\bm{\mathbf{G}}\bm{\mathbf{z}}^{\prime}.

To see this, note that for all
i\in\{0,\cdots,d-1\}
, the entries of
(z^{\prime}_{i\cdot\log_{2}(q)+1},\cdots,z^{\prime}_{i\cdot\log_{2}(q)+\log_{2}(q)})
form the binary expansion of
z_{i}\in\{0,\cdots,q-1\}
. Therefore, we can let
\bm{\mathbf{B}}^{\prime}=\bm{\mathbf{B}}\bm{\mathbf{G}}
and write

\bm{\mathbf{t}}=\bm{\mathbf{B}}\bm{\mathbf{z}}+\bm{\mathbf{e}}=\bm{\mathbf{B}}\bm{\mathbf{G}}\bm{\mathbf{z}}^{\prime}+\bm{\mathbf{e}}=\bm{\mathbf{B}}^{\prime}\bm{\mathbf{z}}^{\prime}+\bm{\mathbf{e}},

making
(\bm{\mathbf{B}}^{\prime},\bm{\mathbf{t}})
a
\{0,1\}
-
\mathsf{BinaryBDD}
instance, preserving the error
\bm{\mathbf{e}}
. Furthermore, the new dimension
d^{\prime}
satisfies
d^{\prime}=d\log_{2}(q)=O(d^{2})
.

6

Recall that
X
is the number of solutions in
\mathcal{S}_{n,k}
. For
\bm{\mathbf{v}}\in\mathcal{S}_{n,k}
, let
X_{\bm{\mathbf{v}}}=\mathbbm{1}[\lVert\bm{\mathbf{A}}\bm{\mathbf{v}}\rVert_{2}\leq\delta^{\prime}]

1
.

To do this, we can expand variance into covariance, and group by
I(\cdot,\cdot)
between

\displaystyle\Var(X)=\Var\left(\sum_{\bm{\mathbf{v}}\in\mathcal{S}_{n,k}}X_{\bm{\mathbf{v}}}\right)=\sum_{\bm{\mathbf{u}},\bm{\mathbf{v}}\in\mathcal{S}_{n,k}}\Cov(X_{\bm{\mathbf{u}}},X_{\bm{\mathbf{v}}})=\sum_{t=0}^{k}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\bm{\mathbf{u}},\bm{\mathbf{v}}\in\mathcal{S}_{n,k}\\

Then,

\displaystyle\E[X_{\bm{\mathbf{u}}}X_{\bm{\mathbf{v}}}]=\Pr_{\mathbf{A}}[\lVert\mathbf{A}\bm{\mathbf{u}}\rVert_{2}\leq\delta^{\prime}\land\lVert\mathbf{A}\bm{\mathbf{v}}\rVert_{2}\leq\delta^{\prime}]

\displaystyle=\Pr_{\bm{\mathbf{w}},\bm{\mathbf{x}},\bm{\mathbf{y}}}[\lVert\bm{\mathbf{w}}+\bm{\mathbf{x}}\rVert_{2}\leq\delta^{\prime}\land\lVert\bm{\mathbf{w}}+\bm{\mathbf{y}}\rVert_{2}\leq\delta^{\prime}]

\displaystyle=\Pr_{\bm{\mathbf{w}},\bm{\mathbf{x}},\bm{\mathbf{y}}}[\bm{\mathbf{x}}\in B_{\delta^{\prime}}(-\bm{\mathbf{w}})\land\bm{\mathbf{y}}\in B_{\delta^{\prime}}(-\bm{\mathbf{w}})]

\displaystyle=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{m}}\left(\Pr_{\bm{\mathbf{x}}}[\bm{\mathbf{x}}\in B_{\delta^{\prime}}(-\bm{\mathbf{w}})]\right)^{2}\mu_{t}(\bm{\mathbf{w}})d\bm{\mathbf{w}}

\displaystyle=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{m}}\left(\int_{B_{\delta^{\prime}}(-\bm{\mathbf{w}})}\mu_{k-t}(\bm{\mathbf{x}})d\bm{\mathbf{x}}\right)^{2}\mu_{t}(\bm{\mathbf{w}})d\bm{\mathbf{w}}.

We split this integral up into two terms
Y_{0}
and
Y_{1}
, depending on the norm of
\bm{\mathbf{w}}
: either
\lVert\bm{\mathbf{w}}\rVert_{2}\leq k/\delta^{\prime}
or not. Explicitly, we have

\displaystyle\Cov(X_{\bm{\mathbf{u}}},X_{\bm{\mathbf{v}}})

\displaystyle\leq\int_{\mathbb{R}^{m}}\left(\int_{B_{\delta^{\prime}}(-\bm{\mathbf{w}})}\mu_{k-t}(\bm{\mathbf{x}})d\bm{\mathbf{x}}\right)^{2}\mu_{t}(\bm{\mathbf{w}})d\bm{\mathbf{w}}

(19)

\displaystyle=\underbrace{\int_{B_{k/\delta^{\prime}}(\mathbf{0})}\left(\int_{B_{\delta^{\prime}}(-\bm{\mathbf{w}})}\mu_{k-t}(\bm{\mathbf{x}})d\bm{\mathbf{x}}\right)^{2}\mu_{t}(\bm{\mathbf{w}})d\bm{\mathbf{w}}}_{Y_{0}}

(20)

\displaystyle+\underbrace{\int_{\mathbb{R}^{m}\setminus B_{k/\delta^{\prime}}(\mathbf{0})}\left(\int_{B_{\delta^{\prime}}(-\bm{\mathbf{w}})}\mu_{k-t}(\bm{\mathbf{x}})d\bm{\mathbf{x}}\right)^{2}\mu_{t}(\bm{\mathbf{w}})d\bm{\mathbf{w}}}_{Y_{1}}.

(21)

(Subcase 1) Bounding
Y_{0}
: For
\bm{\mathbf{w}}\in B_{k/\delta^{\prime}}(\mathbf{0})
, we have
\lVert\bm{\mathbf{w}}\rVert_{2}\leq k/\delta^{\prime}
. The point is that in this case, the density
\mu_{k-t}(\bm{\mathbf{x}})
is almost uniform for
\bm{\mathbf{x}}\in B_{\delta^{\prime}}(-\bm{\mathbf{w}})
. To see this, consider two arbitrary points
-\bm{\mathbf{w}}-\bm{\mathbf{a}},-\bm{\mathbf{w}}-\bm{\mathbf{b}}\in B_{\delta^{\prime}}(-\bm{\mathbf{w}})
, where
\lVert\bm{\mathbf{a}}\rVert,\lVert\bm{\mathbf{b}}\rVert\leq\delta^{\prime}
. The ratio of the densities is given by

\displaystyle\frac{\mu_{k-t}(-\bm{\mathbf{w}}-\bm{\mathbf{a}})}{\mu_{k-t}(-\bm{\mathbf{w}}-\bm{\mathbf{b}})}

\displaystyle=\frac{\exp\left(-\lVert\bm{\mathbf{w}}+\bm{\mathbf{a}}\rVert_{2}^{2}/(2(k-t))\right)}{\exp\left(-\lVert\bm{\mathbf{w}}+\bm{\mathbf{b}}\rVert_{2}^{2}/(2(k-t))\right)}

\displaystyle=\exp\left(\frac{-\langle\bm{\mathbf{w}}+\bm{\mathbf{a}},\bm{\mathbf{w}}+\bm{\mathbf{a}}\rangle+\langle\bm{\mathbf{w}}+\bm{\mathbf{b}},\bm{\mathbf{w}}+\bm{\mathbf{b}}\rangle}{2(k-t)}\right)

\displaystyle=\exp\left(\frac{-\lVert\bm{\mathbf{a}}\rVert_{2}^{2}+\lVert\bm{\mathbf{b}}\rVert_{2}^{2}+2\langle\bm{\mathbf{w}},\bm{\mathbf{b}}-\bm{\mathbf{a}}\rangle}{2(k-t)}\right).

By Cauchy-Schwartz and the triangle inequality, we have

|2\langle\bm{\mathbf{w}},\bm{\mathbf{b}}-\bm{\mathbf{a}}\rangle|\leq 2\lVert\bm{\mathbf{w}}\rVert\lVert\bm{\mathbf{b}}-\bm{\mathbf{a}}\rVert\leq 2\lVert\bm{\mathbf{w}}\rVert(\lVert\bm{\mathbf{b}}\rVert+\lVert\bm{\mathbf{a}}\rVert)\leq 4\frac{k}{\delta^{\prime}}\delta^{\prime}=4k.

For the other two terms in the numerator, we have
\lVert\bm{\mathbf{a}}\rVert^{2},\lVert\bm{\mathbf{b}}\rVert^{2}\leq(\delta^{\prime})^{2}=o(1)
. Therefore, the absolute value of the numerator in the exponent is at most
O(k)
. Since
t\leq k/4
, we know
k-t\geq 3k/4
, making the exponent at most
O(k)/\Omega(k)=O(1)
. Therefore, there is a constant
C=O(1)
such that

\frac{1}{C}\leq\frac{\mu_{k-t}(-\bm{\mathbf{w}}-\bm{\mathbf{a}})}{\mu_{k-t}(-\bm{\mathbf{w}}-\bm{\mathbf{b}})}\leq C.

Therefore, we get

\displaystyle Y_{0}

\displaystyle=\int_{B_{k/\delta^{\prime}}(\mathbf{0})}\left(\int_{B_{\delta^{\prime}}(-\bm{\mathbf{w}})}\mu_{k-t}(\bm{\mathbf{x}})d\bm{\mathbf{x}}\right)^{2}\mu_{t}(\bm{\mathbf{w}})d\bm{\mathbf{w}}

\displaystyle\leq C^{2}\cdot\int_{B_{k/\delta^{\prime}}(\mathbf{0})}\left(\int_{B_{\delta^{\prime}}(-\bm{\mathbf{w}})}\mu_{k-t}(-\bm{\mathbf{w}})d\bm{\mathbf{x}}\right)^{2}\mu_{t}(\bm{\mathbf{w}})d\bm{\mathbf{w}}

\displaystyle=C^{2}\int_{B_{k/\delta^{\prime}}(\mathbf{0})}\Vol(B_{\delta^{\prime}}(-\bm{\mathbf{w}}))^{2}\cdot\mu_{k-t}(-\bm{\mathbf{w}})^{2}\mu_{t}(\bm{\mathbf{w}})d\bm{\mathbf{w}}

\displaystyle=C^{2}\Vol(B_{\delta^{\prime}}(\mathbf{0}))^{2}\int_{B_{k/\delta^{\prime}}(\mathbf{0})}\mu_{k-t}(-\bm{\mathbf{w}})^{2}\mu_{t}(\bm{\mathbf{w}})d\bm{\mathbf{w}}

\displaystyle\leq C^{2}\Vol(B_{\delta^{\prime}}(\mathbf{0}))^{2}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{m}}\mu_{k-t}(-\bm{\mathbf{w}})^{2}\mu_{t}(\bm{\mathbf{w}})d\bm{\mathbf{w}}.

Continuing the inequality, we have

\displaystyle Y_{0}

\displaystyle\leq C^{2}\Vol(B_{\delta^{\prime}}(\mathbf{0}))^{2}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{m}}\mu_{k-t}(-\bm{\mathbf{w}})^{2}\mu_{t}(\bm{\mathbf{w}})d\bm{\mathbf{w}}

(22)

\displaystyle=C^{2}\Vol(B_{\delta^{\prime}}(\mathbf{0}))^{2}\frac{1}{\left(\sqrt{2\pi(k-t)}\right)^{2m}\left(\sqrt{2\pi t}\right)^{m}}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{m}}\exp\left(-\frac{2\lVert\bm{\mathbf{w}}\rVert^{2}}{2(k-t)}-\frac{\lVert\bm{\mathbf{w}}\rVert^{2}}{2t}\right)d\bm{\mathbf{w}}

(23)

\displaystyle=C^{2}\Vol(B_{\delta^{\prime}}(\mathbf{0}))^{2}\frac{1}{\left(\sqrt{2\pi(k-t)}\right)^{2m}\left(\sqrt{2\pi t}\right)^{m}}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{m}}\exp\left(-\frac{k+t}{2t(k-t)}\lVert\bm{\mathbf{w}}\rVert^{2}\right)d\bm{\mathbf{w}}

(24)

\displaystyle=C^{2}\Vol(B_{\delta^{\prime}}(\mathbf{0}))^{2}\frac{\left(\sqrt{2\pi\cdot\frac{t(k-t)}{k+t}}\right)^{m}}{\left(\sqrt{2\pi(k-t)}\right)^{2m}\left(\sqrt{2\pi t}\right)^{m}}

(25)

\displaystyle=C^{2}\Vol(B_{\delta^{\prime}}(\mathbf{0}))^{2}\left((2\pi)^{2}(k-t)(k+t)\right)^{-m/2}

(26)

\displaystyle\leq C^{2}\Vol(B_{\delta^{\prime}}(\mathbf{0}))^{2}\left((2\pi)^{2}k^{2}\right)^{-m/2}\left(1+\frac{2t^{2}}{k^{2}}\right)^{m/2}

(27)

\displaystyle\leq C^{2}\Vol(B_{\delta^{\prime}}(\mathbf{0}))^{2}\left((2\pi)^{2}k^{2}\right)^{-m/2}\exp\left(t^{2}m/k^{2}\right),

(28)

where the penultimate inequality uses the fact that
t\leq k/4
. We can similarly lower bound
p
as follows:

\displaystyle p=\E[X_{\bm{\mathbf{v}}}]

\displaystyle=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{m}}\int_{B_{\delta^{\prime}}(-\bm{\mathbf{w}})}\mu_{k-t}(\bm{\mathbf{x}})d\bm{\mathbf{x}}\mu_{t}(\bm{\mathbf{w}})d\bm{\mathbf{w}}

(29)

\displaystyle\geq\int_{B_{k/\delta^{\prime}}(\mathbf{0})}\int_{B_{\delta^{\prime}}(-\bm{\mathbf{w}})}\mu_{k-t}(\bm{\mathbf{x}})d\bm{\mathbf{x}}\mu_{t}(\bm{\mathbf{w}})d\bm{\mathbf{w}}

(30)

\displaystyle\geq\frac{1}{C}\int_{B_{k/\delta^{\prime}}(\mathbf{0})}\int_{B_{\delta^{\prime}}(-\bm{\mathbf{w}})}\mu_{k-t}(-\bm{\mathbf{w}})d\bm{\mathbf{x}}\mu_{t}(\bm{\mathbf{w}})d\bm{\mathbf{w}}

(31)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{C}\Vol(B_{\delta^{\prime}}(\mathbf{0}))\int_{B_{k/\delta^{\prime}}(\mathbf{0})}\mu_{k-t}(-\bm{\mathbf{w}})\mu_{t}(\bm{\mathbf{w}})d\bm{\mathbf{w}}

(32)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{C}\Vol(B_{\delta^{\prime}}(\mathbf{0}))\left(\int_{\mathbb{R}^{m}}\mu_{k-t}(-\bm{\mathbf{w}})\mu_{t}(\bm{\mathbf{w}})d\bm{\mathbf{w}}-\int_{\mathbb{R}^{m}\setminus B_{k/\delta^{\prime}}(\mathbf{0})}\mu_{k-t}(-\bm{\mathbf{w}})\mu_{t}(\bm{\mathbf{w}})d\bm{\mathbf{w}}\right).

(33)

For
\bm{\mathbf{w}}\in\mathbb{R}^{m}\setminus B_{k/\delta^{\prime}}(\mathbf{0})
, we have
\lVert\bm{\mathbf{w}}\rVert\geq k/\delta^{\prime}
. This implies (as a weak bound)

\displaystyle\mu_{k-t}(-\bm{\mathbf{w}})

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\left(\sqrt{2\pi(k-t)}\right)^{m}}\exp\left(-\lVert\bm{\mathbf{w}}\rVert_{2}^{2}/(2(k-t))\right)

\displaystyle\leq\exp(-k/(2(\delta^{\prime})^{2})=\exp(-m^{1+\Omega(1)})=o(p).

Since
\Vol(B_{\delta^{\prime}}(\mathbf{0}))<1
for
\delta^{\prime}=o(1)
, it follows that

\displaystyle\frac{1}{C}\Vol(B_{\delta^{\prime}}(\mathbf{0}))\int_{\mathbb{R}^{m}\setminus B_{k/\delta^{\prime}}(\mathbf{0})}\mu_{k-t}(-\bm{\mathbf{w}})\mu_{t}(\bm{\mathbf{w}})d\bm{\mathbf{w}}\leq o(p)\int_{\mathbb{R}^{m}\setminus B_{k/\delta^{\prime}}(\mathbf{0})}\mu_{t}(\bm{\mathbf{w}})d\bm{\mathbf{w}}\leq o(p).

Therefore, by adding to (
33
) and dividing by
1+o(1)
,

\displaystyle p

\displaystyle\geq\frac{1}{2C}\Vol(B_{\delta^{\prime}}(\mathbf{0}))\int_{\mathbb{R}^{m}}\mu_{k-t}(-\bm{\mathbf{w}})\mu_{t}(\bm{\mathbf{w}})d\bm{\mathbf{w}}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2C}\Vol(B_{\delta^{\prime}}(\mathbf{0}))\frac{1}{\left(\sqrt{2\pi(k-t)}\right)^{m}\left(\sqrt{2\pi t}\right)^{m}}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{m}}\exp\left(\frac{-\lVert\bm{\mathbf{w}}\rVert^{2}}{2(k-t)}-\frac{\lVert\bm{\mathbf{w}}\rVert^{2}}{2t}\right)d\bm{\mathbf{w}}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2C}\Vol(B_{\delta^{\prime}}(\mathbf{0}))\frac{1}{\left(\sqrt{2\pi(k-t)}\right)^{m}\left(\sqrt{2\pi t}\right)^{m}}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{m}}\exp\left(-\frac{k}{2t(k-t)}\lVert\bm{\mathbf{w}}\rVert^{2}\right)d\bm{\mathbf{w}}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2C}\Vol(B_{\delta^{\prime}}(\mathbf{0}))(2\pi k)^{-m/2}.

Using this lower bound on
p
, we can use (
28
) to see that

Y_{0}\leq C^{2}\Vol(B_{\delta^{\prime}}(\mathbf{0}))^{2}\left((2\pi)^{2}k^{2}\right)^{-m/2}\exp\left(t^{2}m/k^{2}\right)\leq C_{1}p^{2}\exp\left(t^{2}m/k^{2}\right),

for some constant
C_{1}=O(1)
.

(Subcase 2) Bounding
Y_{1}
: For
\bm{\mathbf{w}}\in\mathbb{R}^{m}\setminus B_{k/\delta^{\prime}}(\mathbf{0})
, we have
\lVert\bm{\mathbf{w}}\rVert_{2}\geq k/\delta^{\prime}
. For any
\bm{\mathbf{x}}\in B_{\delta^{\prime}}(-\bm{\mathbf{w}})
, it follows by the triangle inequality that

\lVert\bm{\mathbf{x}}\rVert\geq\lVert-\bm{\mathbf{w}}\rVert-\delta^{\prime}\geq k/\delta^{\prime}-\delta^{\prime}\geq k/(2\delta^{\prime}),

since
\delta^{\prime}=o(1)
. Therefore, since
k-t\leq k
, for any
\bm{\mathbf{x}}\in B_{\delta^{\prime}}(-\bm{\mathbf{w}})
, we have

\displaystyle\mu_{k-t}(\bm{\mathbf{x}})

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\left(\sqrt{2\pi(k-t)}\right)^{m}}\exp\left(-\lVert\bm{\mathbf{x}}\rVert_{2}^{2}/(2(k-t))\right)

\displaystyle\leq\frac{1}{\left(\sqrt{2\pi(k-t)}\right)^{m}}\exp\left(-k/(8(\delta^{\prime})^{2})\right)

\displaystyle\leq\exp\left(-m^{1+\Omega(1)}\right).

As a weak bound, since
\Vol(B_{\delta^{\prime}}(-\bm{\mathbf{w}}))\leq 1
(as
\delta^{\prime}=o(1)
), this implies for all
\bm{\mathbf{x}}\in B_{\delta^{\prime}}(-\bm{\mathbf{w}})
,

\left(\int_{B_{\delta^{\prime}}(-\bm{\mathbf{w}})}\mu_{k-t}(\bm{\mathbf{x}})d\bm{\mathbf{x}}\right)^{2}\leq\exp\left(-m^{1+\Omega(1)}\right),

and therefore,

\displaystyle Y_{1}

\displaystyle=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{m}\setminus B_{k/\delta^{\prime}}(\mathbf{0})}\left(\int_{B_{\delta^{\prime}}(-\bm{\mathbf{w}})}\mu_{k-t}(\bm{\mathbf{x}})d\bm{\mathbf{x}}\right)^{2}\mu_{t}(\bm{\mathbf{w}})d\bm{\mathbf{w}}

\displaystyle\leq\exp\left(-m^{1+\Omega(1)}\right)\int_{\mathbb{R}^{m}\setminus B_{k/\delta^{\prime}}(\mathbf{0})}\mu_{t}(\bm{\mathbf{w}})d\bm{\mathbf{w}}\leq\exp\left(-m^{1+\Omega(1)}\right).

Since
p^{2}=\exp\left(-\Theta(m\log m)\right)=\omega\left(\exp\left(-m^{1+\Omega(1)}\right)\right)
, it follows that

Y_{1}=o(p^{2}).

Combining the bounds on
Y_{0}
and
Y_{1}
via Eq. (
21
), there is a constant
C_{2}=O(1)
for which

\Cov(X_{\bm{\mathbf{u}}},X_{\bm{\mathbf{v}}})\leq Y_{0}+Y_{1}\leq C_{1}p^{2}\exp\left(t^{2}m/k^{2}\right)+o(p^{2})\leq C_{2}p^{2}\exp\left(t^{2}m/k^{2}\right).

Using the definition of
Z_{1}
, (
18
), and
t\leq k/4
, we have

\displaystyle Z_{1}=\sum_{t=1}^{k/4}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\bm{\mathbf{u}},\bm{\mathbf{v}}\in\mathcal{S}_{n,k}\\
I(\bm{\mathbf{u}},\bm{\mathbf{v}})=t\end{subarray}}\Cov(X_{\bm{\mathbf{u}}},X_{\bm{\mathbf{v}}})

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{t=1}^{k/4}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\bm{\mathbf{u}},\bm{\mathbf{v}}\in\mathcal{S}_{n,k}\\
I(\bm{\mathbf{u}},\bm{\mathbf{v}})=t\end{subarray}}C_{2}p^{2}\exp\left(t^{2}m/k^{2}\right)

\displaystyle\leq C_{2}p^{2}\sum_{t=1}^{k/4}\binom{k}{t}\left(\frac{n}{k}\right)^{2k-t}\exp\left(t^{2}m/k^{2}\right)

\displaystyle\leq C_{2}p^{2}\left(\frac{n}{k}\right)^{2k}\sum_{t=1}^{k/4}\binom{k}{t}\left(\frac{n}{k}\right)^{-t}\exp\left(tm/(4k)\right)

\displaystyle\leq C_{2}p^{2}\left(\frac{n}{k}\right)^{2k}\sum_{t=1}^{k/4}\left(\frac{k^{2}\exp(m/(4k))}{n}\right)^{t}

\displaystyle=C_{2}\left(\frac{n}{k}\right)^{1.8k}\sum_{t=1}^{k/4}\left(\frac{k^{2}\exp(m/(4k))}{n}\right)^{t}.

Since this geometric sum starts at index
t=1
, to show
Z_{1}=o((n/k)^{1.8k})
, it suffices to show that the base,
\frac{k^{2}\exp(m/(4k))}{n}
, is
o(1)
. To see this, recall that

n=k\cdot\exp\left(\Theta(m/k\cdot\log m)\right),

and
k=m^{\epsilon}
for
\epsilon\in(0,1)
, making

\displaystyle\frac{k^{2}\exp(m/(4k))}{n}

\displaystyle=\frac{k\exp(m/(4k))}{\exp\left(\Theta(m/k\cdot\log m)\right)}

\displaystyle\leq m\exp\left(-\Theta(m/k\cdot\log m)\right)

\displaystyle\leq m\exp\left(-m^{\Omega(1)}\log m\right)

\displaystyle=o(1),

as desired.

Bounding
Z_{2}
: For this case when
t>k/4
, we can use the (very) weak bound that

\Cov(X_{\bm{\mathbf{u}}},X_{\bm{\mathbf{v}}})=\E[X_{\bm{\mathbf{u}}}X_{\bm{\mathbf{v}}}]-\E[X_{\bm{\mathbf{u}}}]\E[X_{\bm{\mathbf{v}}}]\leq\E[X_{\bm{\mathbf{u}}}X_{\bm{\mathbf{v}}}]\leq 1.

Using (
18
), we get

\displaystyle Z_{2}=\sum_{t>k/4}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\bm{\mathbf{u}},\bm{\mathbf{v}}\in\mathcal{S}_{n,k}\\
I(\bm{\mathbf{u}},\bm{\mathbf{v}})=t\end{subarray}}\Cov(X_{\bm{\mathbf{u}}},X_{\bm{\mathbf{v}}})

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{t>k/4}\binom{k}{t}\left(\frac{n}{k}\right)^{2k-t}

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{t>k/4}\binom{k}{t}\left(\frac{n}{k}\right)^{1.75k}

\displaystyle\leq\left(\frac{n}{k}\right)^{1.75k}\cdot 2^{k}

\displaystyle=o\left(\left(\frac{n}{k}\right)^{1.8k}\right),

where the last line uses the fact that
n/k=\omega(1)
.

Appendix C
Reduction to
k
-
\mathsf{SLR}
with Independent Noise

The noise
\bm{\mathbf{w}}=\bm{\mathbf{y}}-\bm{\mathbf{X}}\bm{\mathbf{\theta}}^{*}
in our instance as described in Theorem
4
depends on
\bm{\mathbf{R}}\bm{\mathbf{e}}
, whose entries are marginally Gaussian with variance
\lVert\bm{\mathbf{e}}\rVert_{2}^{2}
, but not independent from the samples in
\bm{\mathbf{X}}
. By adding sufficient flooding noise
\bm{\mathbf{\xi}}
to the vector
\bm{\mathbf{y}}
, and by starting with
\mathsf{BinaryBDD}_{d,\alpha}
instances for which
\alpha=1/\poly(m)
, we can obtain a distribution over the noise that is at most
1/2
-away in total variation distance from being independent Gaussian.

Lemma 12
.

Let
d,m,n,k,m_{1}
be integers such that
k
divides
d
,
m\geq\Omega(d)
and
n=k\cdot 2^{d/k}
and
m_{1}=m-k
. Let
\alpha=o(1/(m\sqrt{\log m}))
be a real number. Then, there is a
\poly(n,m)
-time reduction from
\mathsf{BinaryBDD}_{d,\alpha}
to
k
-
\mathsf{SLR}
in dimension
n
with
m
samples that succeeds with probability at
least
9/10
. If the
\mathsf{BinaryBDD}_{d,\alpha}
instance has lattice basis
\mathbf{B}
, the resulting
k
-
\mathsf{SLR}
instance
(\mathbf{X},\mathbf{y})
has parameter
\delta=\Theta(\lambda_{1,\mathsf{bin}}(\mathbf{B}))
and design matrix
\bm{\mathbf{X}}
as structured in Theorem
4
. Further, there is a
k
-sparse vector
\bm{\mathbf{\theta}}^{*}
such that

\displaystyle\mathbf{X}\bm{\mathbf{\theta}}^{*}+\bm{\mathbf{w}}=\bm{\mathbf{y}}

such that noise
\bm{\mathbf{w}}
is drawn from a distribution that is at most
1/2
-away from
\mathcal{N}(0,\sigma^{2})^{\otimes m}
in total variation distance, where

\displaystyle\sigma=\Theta\left(\alpha\cdot\lambda_{1,\mathsf{bin}}(\mathbf{B})\cdot m\sqrt{\log m}\right).

Sketch.

Let
(\bm{\mathbf{B}},\bm{\mathbf{t}}:=\bm{\mathbf{B}}\bm{\mathbf{z}}+\bm{\mathbf{e}})
be our
\mathsf{BinaryBDD}_{d,\alpha}
instance, where
\bm{\mathbf{B}}\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times d}

\bm{\mathbf{z}}\in\{-1,1\}^{d}

\|\bm{\mathbf{e}}\|_{2}\leq\alpha\cdot\lambda_{1,\mathsf{bin}}(\bm{\mathbf{B}})
. Let
\bm{\mathbf{R}}\in\mathbb{R}^{m_{1}\times d}
be a random matrix where each entry is drawn i.i.d. from
\mathcal{N}(0,1)
. We define the design matrix
\bm{\mathbf{X}}
and target vector
\bm{\mathbf{y}}
for
k
-
\mathsf{SLR}
similar to the definition in Theorem
4
. The main difference is that to achieve Gaussian noise that is independent of the samples
\bm{\mathbf{X}}
, we add noise vector
\bm{\mathbf{\xi}}=\begin{pmatrix}\bm{\mathbf{\xi}}_{1}\\
\bm{\mathbf{\xi}}_{2}\end{pmatrix}
to the vector
\bm{\mathbf{y}}
, to get the instance
(\bm{\mathbf{X}},\bm{\mathbf{y}})
where

\displaystyle\bm{\mathbf{X}}=\begin{pmatrix}\bm{\mathbf{X}}_{1}\\
\bm{\mathbf{X}}_{2}\end{pmatrix}=\begin{pmatrix}\bm{\mathbf{R}}\bm{\mathbf{B}}\bm{\mathbf{G}}_{\mathsf{sparse}}\\
\gamma\bm{\mathbf{G}}_{\mathsf{partite}}\end{pmatrix},\quad\text{ and }\quad\bm{\mathbf{y}}=\begin{pmatrix}\bm{\mathbf{y}}_{1}\\
\bm{\mathbf{y}}_{2}\end{pmatrix}=\begin{pmatrix}\bm{\mathbf{R}}\bm{\mathbf{t}}+\bm{\mathbf{\xi}}_{1}\\
\gamma\bm{\mathbf{1}}+\bm{\mathbf{\xi}}_{2}\end{pmatrix},

where each entry in
\bm{\mathbf{\xi}}_{1},\bm{\mathbf{\xi}}_{2}
is sampled i.i.d from
\mathcal{N}(0,\sigma^{2})
.
As before in Theorem
4
, we get an approximation
\widehat{\lambda}_{1}
for
\lambda_{1,\mathsf{bin}}(\bm{\mathbf{B}})
. (Iterating over different guesses will take at most polynomial time in the size of the problem description.) In the remainder of the proof, we will assume we know a value
\widehat{\lambda}_{1}
such that
\widehat{\lambda}_{1}\in[\lambda_{1,\mathsf{bin}}(\bm{\mathbf{B}}),2\lambda_{1,\mathsf{bin}}(\bm{\mathbf{B}}))
.

We set the parameters
\delta,\sigma,\gamma
such that

\displaystyle\delta^{2}

\displaystyle=\frac{3m_{1}\cdot\widehat{\lambda}_{1}^{2}}{100m}=\Theta(\lambda_{1,\mathsf{bin}}(\bm{\mathbf{B}})^{2}),

\displaystyle\sigma

\displaystyle=\Theta(\alpha\cdot\widehat{\lambda}_{1}\cdot m_{1}\sqrt{\log(m_{1})}),\text{ and}

\displaystyle\gamma

\displaystyle=\Omega\left(\frac{\sigma_{\max}(\bm{\mathbf{B}})}{\widehat{\lambda}_{1}}\cdot\sqrt{\frac{d}{k}}\cdot(\delta\sqrt{m}+\sigma\sqrt{k})\right).

Suppose there is an algorithm that returns a
k
-sparse
\widehat{\bm{\mathbf{\theta}}}
when run on instance
(\bm{\mathbf{X}},\bm{\mathbf{y}})
with parameter
\delta
such that

\displaystyle\frac{\|\bm{\mathbf{X}}\widehat{\bm{\mathbf{\theta}}}-\bm{\mathbf{y}}\|_{2}^{2}}{m}\leq\delta^{2}.

(34)

Then, we round
\widehat{\bm{\mathbf{\theta}}}
to the nearest integer entrywise to get
\mathsf{round}(\widehat{\bm{\mathbf{\theta}}})\in\mathbb{Z}^{n}
, and we output
\bm{\mathbf{G}}_{\mathsf{sparse}}\mathsf{round}(\widehat{\bm{\mathbf{\theta}}})\in\mathbb{Z}^{d}
as the
\mathsf{BinaryBDD}_{d,\alpha}
solution.

First, we show that this reduction is complete. For completeness to hold, it suffices to choose
\delta,\sigma
such that the following holds.

\displaystyle\frac{1}{m}\lVert\bm{\mathbf{X}}\bm{\mathbf{\theta}}^{*}-\bm{\mathbf{y}}\rVert_{2}^{2}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{m}\left(\lVert\bm{\mathbf{R}}\bm{\mathbf{e}}+\bm{\mathbf{\xi}}_{1}\rVert_{2}^{2}+\lVert\bm{\mathbf{\xi}}_{2}\rVert_{2}^{2}\right)\leq O\left(\frac{\lVert\bm{\mathbf{R}}\bm{\mathbf{e}}\rVert_{2}^{2}+\lVert\bm{\mathbf{\xi}}_{1}\rVert_{2}^{2}+\lVert\bm{\mathbf{\xi}}_{2}\rVert_{2}^{2}}{m}\right)\leq O\left(\frac{m_{1}\lVert\bm{\mathbf{e}}\rVert_{2}^{2}+m\sigma^{2}}{m}\right)

\displaystyle\leq O(\lVert\bm{\mathbf{e}}\rVert_{2}^{2}+\sigma^{2})\leq\delta^{2}.

Now, we proceed to prove that the reduction is sound. For the sake of contradiction, suppose that
\bm{\mathbf{G}}_{\mathsf{sparse}}\mathsf{round}(\widehat{\bm{\mathbf{\theta}}})=\bm{\mathbf{z}}^{\prime}
for some
\bm{\mathbf{z}}^{\prime}\neq\bm{\mathbf{z}}
such that Equation (
34
) holds. Since Equation (
34
) holes, in particular, we know that
\|\bm{\mathbf{X}}_{2}-\bm{\mathbf{y}}_{2}\|_{2}^{2}=\lVert\gamma\bm{\mathbf{G}}_{\mathsf{partite}}\widehat{\bm{\mathbf{\theta}}}-\gamma\bm{\mathbf{1}}-\bm{\mathbf{\xi}}_{2}\rVert_{2}^{2}\leq m\delta^{2}
. Since
\|\bm{\mathbf{\xi}}_{2}\|_{2}\leq O(\sigma\sqrt{k})
with high probability, we set
\gamma
such that

\displaystyle\lVert\bm{\mathbf{G}}_{\mathsf{partite}}\widehat{\bm{\mathbf{\theta}}}-\bm{\mathbf{1}}\rVert_{2}\leq\frac{\delta\sqrt{m}+\|\bm{\mathbf{\xi}}_{2}\|_{2}}{\gamma}<1/2.

Since
\widehat{\bm{\mathbf{\theta}}}
is
k
-sparse, this means that it cannot have any part with all zero entries. So each part of
\widehat{\bm{\mathbf{\theta}}}
must have exactly one non-zero entry. By the choice of
\gamma
, each of these non-zero entries must be in the interval
(1/2,3/2)
, and must round to
1
. Therefore
\mathsf{round}(\widehat{\bm{\mathbf{\theta}}})\in\mathcal{S}_{n,k}
and
\mathsf{round}(\widehat{\bm{\mathbf{\theta}}})
and
\widehat{\bm{\mathbf{\theta}}}
have the same support. This implies that
\bm{\mathbf{z}}^{\prime}=\bm{\mathbf{G}}_{\mathsf{sparse}}\mathsf{round}(\widehat{\bm{\mathbf{\theta}}})\in\{-1,+1\}^{d}
and
\bm{\mathbf{G}}_{\mathsf{partite}}\mathsf{round}(\widehat{\bm{\mathbf{\theta}}})=\mathbf{1}
by Lemma
6
. Since we can restrict to the support of
\widehat{\bm{\mathbf{\theta}}}
, we have

\displaystyle\lVert\widehat{\bm{\mathbf{\theta}}}\rVert-\mathsf{round}(\widehat{\bm{\mathbf{\theta}}})_{2}=\lVert\bm{\mathbf{G}}_{\mathsf{partite}}\widehat{\bm{\mathbf{\theta}}}-\bm{\mathbf{G}}_{\mathsf{partite}}\mathsf{round}(\bm{\mathbf{G}})\rVert=\lVert\mathbf{G}_{\mathsf{partite}}\widehat{\bm{\mathbf{\theta}}}-\mathbf{1}\rVert_{2}\leq\frac{\delta\sqrt{m}+\lVert\bm{\mathbf{\xi}}_{2}\rVert_{2}}{\gamma}.

Since
\widehat{\bm{\mathbf{\theta}}}-\mathsf{round}(\widehat{\bm{\mathbf{\theta}}})
is also
k
-sparse and partite, it follows that

\displaystyle\lVert\mathbf{G}_{\mathsf{sparse}}(\widehat{\bm{\mathbf{\theta}}}-\mathsf{round}(\widehat{\bm{\mathbf{\theta}}}))\rVert_{2}\leq\sqrt{\frac{d}{k}}\cdot\frac{\delta\sqrt{m}+\lVert\bm{\mathbf{\xi}}_{2}\rVert_{2}}{\gamma},

as each column of any block of
\bm{\mathbf{G}}_{\mathsf{sparse}}
has
\ell_{2}
norm exactly
\sqrt{d/k}
. Multiplying on the left by
\mathbf{B}
, we have

\displaystyle\lVert\bm{\mathbf{B}}\bm{\mathbf{G}}_{\mathsf{sparse}}(\widehat{\bm{\mathbf{\theta}}}-\mathsf{round}(\widehat{\bm{\mathbf{\theta}}}))\rVert_{2}

\displaystyle=\lVert\bm{\mathbf{B}}\bm{\mathbf{G}}_{\mathsf{sparse}}-\bm{\mathbf{B}}\bm{\mathbf{z}}^{\prime}\rVert_{2}

\displaystyle\leq\sigma_{\max}(\bm{\mathbf{B}})\cdot\sqrt{\frac{d}{k}}\cdot\frac{\delta\sqrt{m}+\lVert\bm{\mathbf{\xi}}_{2}\rVert_{2}}{\gamma}.

On the other hand, by definition of
\lambda_{1,\mathsf{bin}}(\bm{\mathbf{B}})
, since
\bm{\mathbf{z}}\neq\bm{\mathbf{z}}^{\prime}\in\{-1,1\}^{d}
, we know that
\lVert\bm{\mathbf{B}}\bm{\mathbf{z}}-\bm{\mathbf{B}}\bm{\mathbf{z}}^{\prime}\rVert_{2}\geq\lambda_{1,\mathsf{bin}}(\bm{\mathbf{B}})
. Therefore, by triangle inequality, and by our choice of
\gamma
, we have

\displaystyle\lVert\bm{\mathbf{B}}\bm{\mathbf{G}}_{\mathsf{sparse}}\widehat{\bm{\mathbf{\theta}}}-\bm{\mathbf{B}}\bm{\mathbf{z}}\rVert_{2}

\displaystyle\geq\lVert\bm{\mathbf{B}}\bm{\mathbf{z}}-\bm{\mathbf{B}}\bm{\mathbf{z}}^{\prime}\rVert_{2}-\lVert\bm{\mathbf{B}}\bm{\mathbf{G}}_{\mathsf{sparse}}\widehat{\bm{\mathbf{\theta}}}-\bm{\mathbf{B}}\bm{\mathbf{z}}^{\prime}\rVert_{2}\geq\frac{49}{50}\lambda_{1,\mathsf{bin}}(\bm{\mathbf{B}}).

By incorporating the error
\bm{\mathbf{e}}
and multipling on the left by
\bm{\mathbf{R}}
, we have

\displaystyle\lVert\bm{\mathbf{X}}\widehat{\bm{\mathbf{\theta}}}-\bm{\mathbf{y}}\rVert_{2}

\displaystyle\geq\lVert\bm{\mathbf{R}}\bm{\mathbf{B}}\bm{\mathbf{G}}_{\mathsf{sparse}}\widehat{\bm{\mathbf{\theta}}}-\bm{\mathbf{R}}(\bm{\mathbf{B}}\bm{\mathbf{z}}+\bm{\mathbf{e}})-\bm{\mathbf{\xi}}_{1}\rVert_{2}

\displaystyle\geq\sigma_{\min}(\bm{\mathbf{R}})\cdot\left(\lVert\bm{\mathbf{B}}\bm{\mathbf{G}}_{\mathsf{sparse}}\widehat{\bm{\mathbf{\theta}}}-\bm{\mathbf{B}}\bm{\mathbf{z}}\rVert_{2}-\lVert\bm{\mathbf{e}}\rVert_{2}-\lVert\bm{\mathbf{\xi}}_{1}\rVert_{2}\right)

\displaystyle\geq\frac{\sqrt{m_{1}}}{2}\cdot\left(\frac{49}{50}\lambda_{1,\mathsf{bin}}(\bm{\mathbf{B}})-\alpha\lambda_{1,\mathsf{bin}}(\bm{\mathbf{B}})-c_{1}\sigma\sqrt{m_{1}}\right).

The last inequality follows because
\lVert\bm{\mathbf{\xi}}_{1}\rVert_{2}\leq c_{1}\sigma\sqrt{m_{1}}
for some constant
c_{1}>0
with probability at least
1-e^{-\Omega(m_{1})}
. Also, by Lemma
2
, we know that
\sigma_{\min}(\bm{\mathbf{R}})\geq\sqrt{m_{1}}/2
with probability at least
1-e^{-\Omega(m_{1})}
.
By Equation (
34
), we get a contradiction as long as

\displaystyle\delta\sqrt{m}<\sqrt{m_{1}}\cdot\left(\frac{49}{50}\lambda_{1,\mathsf{bin}}(\bm{\mathbf{B}})-\alpha\cdot\lambda_{1,\mathsf{bin}}(\bm{\mathbf{B}})-c_{1}\sigma\sqrt{m_{1}}\right).

On the other hand, we know

\displaystyle\delta\sqrt{m}=\frac{\widehat{\lambda}_{1}\sqrt{3m_{1}}}{10\sqrt{m}}\cdot\sqrt{m}\leq\frac{1}{5}\cdot\lambda_{1,\mathsf{bin}}(\bm{\mathbf{B}})\cdot\sqrt{3m_{1}}.

Combining these inequalities we get a contradiction if
\alpha<c_{2}
and
\sigma<c_{3}\cdot\lambda_{1,\mathsf{bin}}(\bm{\mathbf{B}})/\sqrt{m_{1}}
, for some constants
c_{2},c_{3}>0
. Since
\alpha=o(1/(m\sqrt{\log m}))
, and by the choice of
\sigma
, these conditions are met.

Finally, we want the distributions of
\bm{\mathbf{R}}\bm{\mathbf{e}}+\bm{\mathbf{\xi}}_{1}
and that of
\bm{\mathbf{\xi}}_{1}
to have total variation distance at most
1/2
. With probability at least
99/100
,
\lVert\bm{\mathbf{R}}\bm{\mathbf{e}}\rVert_{\infty}\leq O\left(\lVert\bm{\mathbf{e}}\rVert_{2}\cdot\sqrt{\log(m_{1})}\right)
by standard Gaussian tail bounds and a union bound. By Lemma
13
, and applying a union bound, as long as
\sigma\geq\Omega\left(\lVert\bm{\mathbf{e}}\rVert_{2}\cdot m_{1}\sqrt{\log(m_{1})}\right)
, the distributions of
\bm{\mathbf{R}}\bm{\mathbf{e}}+\bm{\mathbf{\xi}}_{1}
and of
\bm{\mathbf{\xi}}_{1}
are at most
1/2
-apart in total variation distance. It suffices that
\sigma\geq\Omega(\alpha\cdot\lambda_{1,\mathsf{bin}}(\bm{\mathbf{B}})\cdot m_{1}\sqrt{\log(m_{1})})
.

Since
m_{1}=\Theta(m)
, choosing
\sigma=\Theta\left(\alpha\cdot\lambda_{1,\mathsf{bin}}(\bm{\mathbf{B}})\cdot m\sqrt{\log(m)}\right)
,
\delta=\Theta(\lambda_{1,\mathsf{bin}})
and
\alpha=o(1/(m\sqrt{\log m}))
satisfies all of these constraints. This completes the proof.
∎

Lemma 13
.

Let
|x|\leq b
be a real number and let
Y\sim\mathcal{N}(0,\sigma^{2})
be a random variable. If
\sigma\geq\frac{b}{2\epsilon}
, then the distributions of
x+Y
and
Y
have total variation distance at most
\epsilon
.

Proof.

The random variable
x+Y
is distributed as
\mathcal{N}(x,\sigma^{2})
, so the KL divergence between the distributions of
x+Y
and
Y
is
\mathsf{KL}(x+Y\|Y)=\frac{x^{2}}{2\sigma^{2}}\leq\frac{b^{2}}{2\sigma^{2}}
. By Pinsker’s inequality, the total variation distance between the two distributions is
\mathsf{TV}(x+Y,Y)\leq\sqrt{\frac{1}{2}\mathsf{KL}(x+Y\|Y)}\leq\frac{b}{2\sigma}\leq\epsilon
.
∎
