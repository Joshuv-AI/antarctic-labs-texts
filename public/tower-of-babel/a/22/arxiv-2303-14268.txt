Bergman Kernels of Two Dimensional Monomial Polyhedra
Rasha Almughrabi

Abstract
. We compute explicitly the Bergman kernels of all two dimensional monomial polyhedra, a class of domains including the Hartogs triangle and some of its generalizations. The kernel is computed from the representation of such domains as quotient of the product of a disc and punctured disc.



1991 Mathematics Subject Classification
32A36

1.
Introduction

The Bergman kernel is a fundamental object of interest in modern complex analysis. In general, it is not possible to compute the Bergman kernel of a domain in explicit closed form. The major exceptions are the ball and other bounded symmetric domains
[
7
]
.

Apart from this, it is also possible in principle to use transformation laws of the Bergman kernel to compute the Bergman kernels of domains which arise as quotients of domains for which the Bergman kernel is known. However, such computations lead to complicated algebraic and computational issues.

In this paper, we will explicitly compute the Bergman kernel of domains belonging to a class called
monomial polyhedra
which arise as quotients of product domains where the factors are discs or punctured discs. Although this problem is interesting in
\mathbb{C}^{n}
, here we give a full computation for the case
n=2
. A monomial polyhedron can be thought as a generalization of the famous
Hartogs Triangle

\mathcal{H}(1,-1)=\{z\in\mathbb{C}^{2}:\left|z_{1}\right|<\left|z_{2}\right|<1\}
. They are of current interest in complex analysis, see
[
1
]
and
[
6
]
.

We begin by describing the class of domains that we study. By a
domain
we mean an open connected set in
\mathbb{C}^{n}
, for
n\geq 1
.

Definition 1
.

Let
\mathcal{U}\subset\mathbb{C}^{n}
be a bounded domain. We say that
\mathcal{U}
is a
monomial polyhedron
if there is an integer matrix
B\in M_{n}(\mathbb{Z})
such that

(1)

\mathcal{U}=\biggl\{z\in\mathbb{C}^{n}:\left|z_{1}\right|^{b_{1}^{j}}\cdots\left|z_{n}\right|^{b_{n}^{j}}<1,1\leq j\leq n\biggr\},

where
b_{j}^{i}
stands for the entry of
B
in the
i
-th row and
j
-th column.

If
\Omega\subset\mathbb{C}^{n}
is a monomial polyhedron then it is a
Reinhardt domain
, that is, if
z\in\Omega
and
\theta_{j}\in\mathbb{R}
for
j=1,\cdots,n
, then we have

\left(e^{i\theta_{1}}z_{1},\cdots,e^{i\theta_{n}}z_{n}\right)\in\Omega.

If
\Omega
is a Reinhardt domain in
\mathbb{C}^{n}
, its
shadow
is the subset
\left|\Omega\right|
of
\mathbb{R}^{n}
given by

\left|\Omega\right|=\{\left(\left|z_{1}\right|,\cdots,\left|z_{n}\right|\right):z\in\Omega\}.

In fact, a monomial polyhedron is a so-called a
pseudoconvex domain
, the type of domain most important in complex analysis of several variables. A monomial polyhedron typically has a singularity at origin, see
[
10
]
.

Many special examples of computations of Bergman kernel of monomial polyhera are found in the literature.
Bremermann in
[
2
]
calculated the Bergman kernel for the classical Hartogs triangle. Edholm in
[
5
]
(see also
[
6
]
) explicitly computed the Bergman kernel for the thin and fat Hartogs triangle. In
[
3
]
, Chakrabarti, Konkel, Mainkar, and Miller give an explicit form for the Bergman kernels of elementary Reinhardt domains. In
[
4
]
, Chen gives a formula for the Bergman kernel for the
n
-dimensional generalized Hartogs triangle. Chen applies the transformation formula to obtain the Bergman kernel of the
n
-dimensional generalized Hartogs triangle. Later in
[
11
]
, Zhang explicitly calculated the Bergman kernel of the
n
-dimensional generalized Hartogs triangles.

1.2.
Main Results

Let
\displaystyle B=\left[\begin{array}[]{cc}b_{1}^{1}&b_{2}^{1}\\
b_{1}^{2}&b_{2}^{2}\end{array}\right]\in M_{2}(\mathbb{Z})
be a
2\times 2
integer matrix such that the domain

(2)

\mathcal{U}=\left\{(z_{1},z_{2})\in\mathbb{C}^{2}:|z_{1}|^{b_{1}^{1}}|z_{2}|^{b_{2}^{1}}<1,\>|z_{1}|^{b_{1}^{2}}|z_{2}|^{b_{2}^{2}}<1\right\}

is bounded, and therefore a monomial polyhedron in
\mathbb{C}^{2}
. Define the matrix
A\in M_{2}(\mathbb{Z})
by

(3)

A=\left[\begin{array}[]{cc}a_{1}^{1}&a_{2}^{1}\\
a_{1}^{2}&a_{2}^{2}\end{array}\right]=\left[\begin{array}[]{cc}\dfrac{b_{2}^{2}}{\gcd(b_{2}^{2},b_{1}^{2})}&\dfrac{-b_{2}^{1}}{\gcd(b_{2}^{1},b_{1}^{1})}\\
\dfrac{-b_{1}^{2}}{\gcd(b_{2}^{2},b_{1}^{2})}&\dfrac{b_{1}^{1}}{\gcd(b_{2}^{1},b_{1}^{1})}\end{array}\right].

It is not difficult to see that (
2
) represents a bounded domain only if
\det(B)\neq 0
. Without loss of generality, assume that
\det(B)>0
, if not, we can interchange the two rows of
B
and this will not change the domain
\mathcal{U}
.
Our first main result is

Theorem 1
.

The Bergman kernel of domain
\mathcal{U}
defined in (
2
) is of the form

(4)

K_{\mathcal{U}}(z,w)=\dfrac{1}{\pi^{2}\det(A)}\cdot\dfrac{g(t_{1},t_{2})}{\left(t_{2}^{a_{2}^{1}}-t_{1}^{a_{2}^{2}}\right)^{2}\left(t_{1}^{a_{1}^{2}}-t_{2}^{a_{1}^{1}}\right)^{2}},

where
z,w\in\mathcal{U}
,
t_{j}=z_{j}\overline{w_{j}}
,
j=1,2
, and
g(t_{1},t_{2})\in\mathbb{Z}[t_{1},t_{2}]
is a polynomial with integer coefficients, which will be described in Theorem
2
below.

In order to describe the polynomial
g
in Theorem
1
, we introduce the following function. For integers
k\geq 1
and
r
, let

(5)

D_{k}(r)=\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}1+r,&0\leq r\leq k-1,\\
2k-(1+r),&k\leq r\leq 2k-2,\\
0,&r<0\quad\text{or}\quad r>2k-2.\end{array}\right.

The next lemma, whose proof is clear, explains why we define
D_{k}
in this way.

Lemma 1
.

For a positive integer
k
, we have

(6)

\left(\frac{1-x^{k}}{1-x}\right)^{2}={\sum_{r=0}^{2k-2}}D_{k}(r)x^{r},

where
D_{k}
is as in (
5
).

Beside
D_{k}
, we need the following notations.

(1)

The permanent of the matrix
P=\left[\begin{array}[]{cc}p_{1}^{1}&p_{2}^{1}\\
p_{1}^{2}&p_{2}^{2}\end{array}\right]
is denoted by
\mathrm{perm}(P)
.

Recall that
\mathrm{perm}(P)=p_{1}^{1}p_{2}^{2}+p_{2}^{1}p_{1}^{2}.

(2)

We set for a matrix
P
and
\gamma=(\gamma_{1},\gamma_{2})\in\mathbb{N}^{2}
,

(7)

N(P,\gamma)=p_{1}^{1}\gamma_{1}+p_{1}^{2}\gamma_{2}-2p_{1}^{1}p_{1}^{2}+p_{1}^{1}+p_{1}^{2}-1-\mathrm{perm}(P)+\left|\det(P)\right|.

(In this paper,
\mathbb{N}
is the set of positive integers).

(3)

Let
R
denote the matrix

R=\left[\begin{array}[]{cc}0&1\\
1&0\end{array}\right].

Theorem 2
.

In formula (
4
) we have, with above notations

g(t_{1},t_{2})={\sum\limits_{\gamma\in\mathbb{N}^{2}}}D_{\det(A)}(N(A,\gamma))D_{\det(A)}(N(AR,\gamma))t_{1}^{\gamma_{1}}t_{2}^{\gamma_{2}},

where the sum is finite, in fact, the coefficients of
g
can be nonzero only if

(8)

\displaystyle\begin{aligned} 0\leq\gamma_{1}\leq 2a_{1}^{2}+2a_{2}^{2}-2,\\
0\leq\gamma_{2}\leq 2a_{1}^{1}+2a_{2}^{1}-2.\end{aligned}

In fact, one can be more precise about the set of indices
\gamma\in\mathbb{N}^{2}
such that the coefficient of
t_{1}^{\gamma_{1}}t_{2}^{\gamma_{2}}
in
g(t_{1},t_{2})
can be nonzero. Such
\gamma
must satisfy the following inequalities

(9)

\displaystyle\begin{aligned} 0\leq a_{1}^{1}\gamma_{1}+a_{1}^{2}\gamma_{2}-2a_{1}^{1}a_{1}^{2}+a_{1}^{1}+a_{1}^{2}-2a_{1}^{2}a_{2}^{1}-1\leq 2\det{(A)}-2,\\
0\leq a_{2}^{1}\gamma_{1}+a_{2}^{2}\gamma_{2}-2a_{1}^{2}a_{2}^{1}+a_{2}^{1}+a_{2}^{2}-2a_{2}^{1}a_{2}^{2}-1\leq 2\det{(A)}-2.\end{aligned}

Graphically, the region that provides a solution of both inequalities in (
9
) is enclosed by a parallelogram.

2.
Preliminary Results about Matrices

In order to prove the main results in Section
1.2
, we need the following basic lemma and a proposition that decomposes integer matrices. The proof of the following observation is trivial.

Lemma 2
.

Let
\Lambda\in\mathbb{M}_{2}(\mathbb{Z})
be such that
\det(\Lambda)=1
. If for
p,q,r,s\in\mathbb{Z}
, we have

\left[\begin{array}[]{c}p\\
q\end{array}\right]=\Lambda\left[\begin{array}[]{c}r\\
s\end{array}\right],\quad\text{then}\;\gcd(p,q)=\gcd(r,s).

The next proposition is closely related to
Hermite decomposition
of integer matrices. For more details, see
[
9
]
.

Proposition 1
.

Let
A=\left[\begin{array}[]{cc}a&b\\
c&d\end{array}\right]\in\mathbb{M}_{2}(\mathbb{Z})
,
\det(A)>0
,
\gcd(a,c)=\gcd(b,d)=1
. Then there are unique numbers
\ell_{1},\ell_{2}\in\mathbb{Z}
such that

(10)

\left[\begin{array}[]{cc}\ell_{1}&\ell_{2}\\
-c&a\end{array}\right]A=\left[\begin{array}[]{cc}1&h\\
0&\det(A)\end{array}\right],\quad\text{where}\quad h=\ell_{1}b+\ell_{2}d,

and

(1)

0\leq h<\det(A)
and
\gcd(h,\det(A))=1
,

(2)

if
L=\left[\begin{array}[]{cc}\ell_{1}&\ell_{2}\\
-c&a\end{array}\right]
, then
\det(L)=1
.

Proof.

Since
\gcd(a,c)=1
, there exist integers
\alpha,\gamma
such that

(11)

\alpha a+\gamma c=1.

Define
L_{1}=\left[\begin{array}[]{cc}\alpha&\gamma\\
-c&a\end{array}\right]
, then
L_{1}A=\left[\begin{array}[]{cc}\alpha&\gamma\\
-c&a\end{array}\right]\left[\begin{array}[]{cc}a&b\\
c&d\end{array}\right]=\left[\begin{array}[]{cc}1&h_{1}\\
0&\det(A)\end{array}\right],
where
h_{1}=\alpha b+\gamma d
.

Since
\det(A)>0
, by the Division Algorithm there exist unique integers
q
and
h
such that

h_{1}=q\det(A)+h,\quad 0\leq h<\det(A).

Let
\ell_{1}=\alpha+qc
and
\ell_{2}=\gamma-aq
, and as in the statement of the proposition and recall
L=\left[\begin{array}[]{cc}\ell_{1}&\ell_{2}\\
-c&a\end{array}\right]
. Observe that
\det(L)=\ell_{1}a+\ell_{2}c=(\alpha+qc)a+(\gamma-aq)c=\alpha a+\gamma c=1
by (
11
) which proves condition (2).

Observe that

\displaystyle\left[\begin{array}[]{cc}1&h\\
0&\det(A)\end{array}\right]

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\left[\begin{array}[]{cc}1&h_{1}-q\det(A)\\
0&\det(A)\end{array}\right]=\left[\begin{array}[]{cc}1&-q\\
0&1\end{array}\right]\left[\begin{array}[]{cc}1&h_{1}\\
0&\det(A)\end{array}\right]=\left[\begin{array}[]{cc}1&-q\\
0&1\end{array}\right]L_{1}A

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\left[\begin{array}[]{cc}1&-q\\
0&1\end{array}\right]\left[\begin{array}[]{cc}\alpha&\gamma\\
-c&a\end{array}\right]A=\left[\begin{array}[]{cc}\alpha+qc&\gamma-aq\\
-c&a\end{array}\right]A=\left[\begin{array}[]{cc}\ell_{1}&\ell_{2}\\
-c&a\end{array}\right]A=LA,

and (
10
) is satisfied. So
h=\ell_{1}b+\ell_{2}d
. Since we already know that
0\leq h<\det(A)
, the first part of condition (1) is proven.

We claim that
\gcd(h,\det(A))=1
. We can write the two equations
h=\ell_{1}b+\ell_{2}d
and
\det(A)=ad-cb
in the following system

\left[\begin{array}[]{c}h\\
\det(A)\end{array}\right]=L\left[\begin{array}[]{c}b\\
d\end{array}\right].

Since
\det(L)=1
and
\gcd(b,d)=1
, then by Lemma
2
above we have
\gcd(h,\det(A))=\gcd(b,d)=1
which proves the second part of condition (1).

To complete the proof, we must show that
\ell_{1}
and
\ell_{2}
are the only numbers with properties (1) and (2). To do this, let

\mathbb{S}=\{(x,y)\in\mathbb{Z}^{2}:ax+cy=1,\>\>\>0\leq bx+dy<\det(A)\}.

This set is nonempty because
(\ell_{1},\ell_{2})\in\mathbb{S}
. This implies that the general solution of the linear Diophantine equation
ax+cy=1
is

(23)

\displaystyle x=\ell_{1}+nc,\quad y=\ell_{2}-na,

where
n\in\mathbb{Z}
. If
(x,y)\in\mathbb{S}
, then
0\leq bx+dy<\det(A)
, i.e.

\displaystyle 0\leq b(\ell_{1}+nc)+d(\ell_{2}-na)<\det(A).

Simplifying the above and using
h=\ell_{1}b+\ell_{2}d
, we see that

\displaystyle 0\leq h-n\det(A)<\det(A),\quad\text{i.e.}\quad n\det(A)\leq h<(n+1)\det(A).

But we know that

(24)

0\leq h<\det(A).

Notice that the sets
\{\lambda\in\mathbb{N}\mid n\det(A)\leq\lambda<(n+1)\det(A)\}
are disjoint for different integers
n
, so
h
belongs to exactly one of them, which therefore corresponds to
n=0
by (
24
). So,
n=0
and by (
23
) we have
x=\ell_{1}
and
y=\ell_{2}
. So
\ell_{1},\ell_{2}
are unique.
∎

3.
Bergman Kernel of Generalized Hartogs Triangle

In this section, we compute the Bergman kernel of one particular
2
-dimensional monomial polyhedron, namely, the generalized Hartogs triangle
\mathcal{H}(k_{1},-k_{2})
defined by

\mathcal{H}(k_{1},-k_{2})=\{(z_{1},z_{2})\in\mathbb{C}^{2}:|z_{1}|^{k_{1}}<|z_{2}|^{k_{2}}<1\},

Edholm in
[
5
]
(see also
[
6
]
) found the Bergman kernel of
\mathcal{H}(1,-k_{2})
and
\mathcal{H}(k_{1},-1)
. In
[
3
]
, Chakrabarti, Konkel, Mainkar, and Miller give an explicit form for the Bergman kernels of generalized Hartogs triangle in
\mathbb{C}^{n}
, which are special cases of elementary Reinhardt domain.

The classical Hartogs triangle
\mathcal{H}(1,-1)
is an important example in several complex variables. It is a well-known nonsmooth pseduoconvex domain.

Proposition 2
.

Let
k_{1},k_{2}
be positive integers such that
\gcd(k_{1},k_{2})=1
and
0\leq k_{2}<k_{1}
. Then the Bergman kernel of
\mathcal{H}(k_{1},-k_{2})
is given by

\displaystyle K_{\mathcal{H}(k_{1},-k_{2})}(z,w)=\dfrac{{\sum\limits_{\beta\in\mathbb{N}^{2}}}D_{k_{1}}(\beta_{1})D_{k_{1}}(k_{1}\beta_{2}+\beta_{1}k_{2}+k_{1}+k_{2}-1-2k_{1}k_{2})t_{1}^{\beta_{1}}t_{2}^{\beta_{2}}}{\pi^{2}k_{1}\left(t_{2}^{k_{2}}-t_{1}^{k_{1}}\right)^{2}\left(1-t_{2}\right)^{2}},

where the sum is finite,
t_{i}=z_{i}\overline{w_{i}}
for
i=1,2
, and a term the sum in the numerator can be nonzero only if
0\leq\beta_{1}\leq 2k_{1}-2
,
0\leq\beta_{2}\leq 2k_{2}
.

Proof.

Let
\mathbb{D}=\{z\in\mathbb{C}:|z|<1\}
and
\mathbb{D}^{*}=\{z\in\mathbb{C}:0<|z|<1\}
. The associated matrix
B
of the generalized Hartogs triangle
\mathcal{H}(k_{1},-k_{2})
and matrix
C=\mathrm{adj}(B)
are given by

B=\left[\begin{array}[]{cc}k_{1}&-k_{2}\\
0&1\end{array}\right],\quad C=\left[\begin{array}[]{cc}1&k_{2}\\
0&k_{1}\end{array}\right].

Define the map
\phi:\mathbb{D}\times\mathbb{D}^{*}\rightarrow\mathcal{H}(k_{1},-k_{2})
by

(25)

\displaystyle\phi(p_{1},p_{2})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle(p_{1}p_{2}^{k_{2}},p_{2}^{k_{1}}).

It is easy to see that
\phi
is a proper holomorphic map of quotient type, see
[
8
]
.
To apply Bell’s transformation theorem, with
\Omega_{1}=\mathbb{D}\times\mathbb{D}^{*}
and
\Omega_{2}=\mathcal{H}(k_{1},-k_{2})
, we need to calculate the Jacobian determinant of the map
\phi
defined in (
25
) which is given by

\displaystyle\det\phi{{}^{\prime}}(p_{1},p_{2})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\det\left(\begin{array}[]{cc}p_{2}^{k_{2}}&k_{2}p_{1}p_{2}^{k_{2}-1}\\
0&k_{1}p_{2}^{k_{1}-1}\end{array}\right)=k_{1}p_{2}^{k_{1}+k_{2}-1}.

Now, we need to calculate the local branches of
\phi^{-1}
.By Theorem 3.12 in
[
1
]
, there are
\det{A}=k_{1}
locally branches denoted by
\Phi_{j}
,
j=0,\cdots,k_{1}-1
. To find
\Phi_{j}(q)
, we need to solve the equation
\phi(p_{1},p_{2})=(q_{1},q_{2})
. We see that
p_{1}^{(j)}=q_{1}q_{2}^{-k_{2}/k_{1}}\zeta^{-jk_{2}}
and
p_{2}^{(j)}=q_{2}^{1/k_{1}}\zeta^{j}
for
j=0,\cdots,k_{1}-1
where

(29)

\zeta=\exp\left(\frac{2\pi i}{k_{1}}\right).

It follows that we can take

(30)

\displaystyle\Phi_{j}(q_{1},q_{2})=(q_{1}q_{2}^{-k_{2}/k_{1}}\zeta^{-jk_{2}},q_{2}^{1/k_{1}}\zeta^{j}),\quad j=0,\cdots,k_{1}-1.

The next step is to calculate the Jacobian determinant of
\Phi_{j}
, which can be computed as follows

(33)

\displaystyle\det\Phi{{}^{\prime}}(q_{1},q_{2})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\det\left(\begin{array}[]{cc}q_{2}^{-k_{2}/k_{1}}\zeta^{-jk_{2}}&\frac{-k_{2}}{k_{1}}q_{1}q_{2}^{-k_{2}/k_{1}-1}\zeta^{-jk_{2}}\\
0&\frac{1}{k_{1}}q_{2}^{1/k_{1}-1}\zeta^{j}\end{array}\right)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{k_{1}}q_{2}^{\frac{1-k_{1}-k_{2}}{k_{1}}}\zeta^{j(1-k_{2})}.

Observe that the Bergman kernel of the unit disc and the Bergman kernel of punctured disc are the same and therefore

(34)

K_{\mathbb{D}\times\mathbb{D}^{*}}(p_{1},p_{2},q_{1},q_{2})=\frac{1}{\pi^{2}(1-p_{1}\overline{q_{1}})^{2}(1-p_{2}\overline{q_{2}})^{2}}.

Apply Bell’s Transformation law with the map
\phi
defined in (
25
), we get

(35)

\det{\phi^{\prime}(p)}K_{\mathcal{H}(k_{1},-k_{2})}(\phi(p),q)={\sum_{j=0}^{k_{1}-1}}K_{\mathbb{D}\times\mathbb{D}^{*}}(p,\Phi_{j}(q))\cdot\overline{\det{\Phi^{\prime}(q)}}.

Plugging (
3
)-(
34
) in (
35
), we get

(36)

k_{1}p_{2}^{k_{1}+k_{2}-1}K_{\mathcal{H}(k_{1},-k_{2})}(\phi(p),q)={\sum_{j=0}^{k_{1}-1}}\frac{\frac{1}{k_{1}}\left(\overline{q_{2}}\right)^{\frac{1-k_{1}-k_{2}}{k_{1}}}\zeta^{-j(1-k_{2})}}{\pi^{2}(1-p_{1}\overline{q_{1}}\>\left(\overline{q_{2}}\right)^{-k_{2}/k_{1}}\zeta^{jk_{2}})^{2}(1-p_{2}\left(\overline{q_{2}}\right)^{1/k_{1}}\zeta^{-j})^{2}}.

Solving for
K_{\mathcal{H}(k_{1},-k_{2})}(\phi(p),q)
