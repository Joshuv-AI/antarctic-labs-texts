Complexity of 2D Snake Cube Puzzles
MIT Hardness Group, Nithid Anchaleenukoon, Alex Dang, Erik D. Demaine, Kaylee Ji, Pitchayut Saengrungkongka

Abstract
Given a chain of H ​ W HW cubes where each cube is marked “turn 90 ∘ 90^{\circ} ” or “go straight”, when can it fold into a 1 × H × W 1\times H\times W rectangular box? We prove several variants of this (still) open problem NP-hard: (1) allowing some cubes to be wildcard (can turn or go straight); (2) allowing a larger box with empty spaces (simplifying a proof from CCCG 2022); (3) growing the box (and the number of cubes) to 2 × H × W 2\times H\times W (improving a prior 3D result from height 8 8 to 2 2 ); (4) with hexagonal prisms rather than cubes, each specified as going straight, turning 60 ∘ 60^{\circ} , or turning 120 ∘ 120^{\circ} ; and (5) allowing the cubes to be encoded implicitly to compress exponentially large repetitions.

2.1
Problem Statement

First, we define the problems we analyze in more precise terms.

The input to all snake-cube problems is a “box” and a “program”;
refer to Figure

.
A
box
is a

rectangular cuboid that the cubes of the snake-cube puzzle must fit into. This box can be visualized as a cubic grid where each cube occupies one space of the grid.
A
program

\mathcal{P}=(p_{1},p_{2},\ldots,p_{k})
is a length-

, where each instruction

(“straight”) or

(“turn”).
The corresponding

is the sequence
c_{1},c_{2},\dots,c_{k}
of pairwise distinct and consecutively adjacent cubes that must
follow the program
in the sense that each instruction
p_{i}
(where

) constrains the angle between the three cubes
c_{i-1},c_{i},c_{i+1}
to be
180^{\circ}
for
p_{i}=\texttt{S}
(i.e., going straight), and

for
p_{i}=\texttt{T}
(i.e., turning with
absolute
turn angle
90^{\circ}
).
(Note that the first and last instructions have no effect; we include them so that cubes and instructions have a bijection.)
A length-
k

segment
refers to a subchain of
k
consecutive cubes that are constrained to form a straight line (e.g., the subchain following the instructions
T
S
S
S
T
is a length-
5
segment).

We also consider

We use the notation

to denote the instruction sequence
s

k

\texttt{T}(\texttt{SST})^{2}
represents the sequence

. For almost all our proofs, this notation is just convenient shorthand. The exception is our proof that

2.3
Numerical 3D Matching

Our other (strong) NP-hardness proofs are reductions from
Numerical 3D Matching
, defined as follows:

Definition 2.6
.

For any given target sum
t
and sequences
(a_{i})_{i=1}^{n}
,
(b_{i})_{i=1}^{n}
, and
(c_{i})_{i=1}^{n}
, each consisting of
n
positive integers,
Numerical 3D Matching (Numerical 3DM)
is the problem of deciding
whether there exist permutations
\sigma
and
\pi
of the set
\{1,2,\dots,n\}
that satisfies
a_{i}+b_{\sigma(i)}+c_{\pi(i)}=t
for all
i
. We refer to such a solution
(\sigma,\pi)
as a
matching
.

Theorem 2.7
.

Numerical 3DM
is
strongly NP-hard
, i.e., NP-hard when the numbers
a_{i},b_{i},c_{i},t
are encoded in unary (and thus are polynomial in value).

Proof.

This is listed as Problem SP16 in Garey and Johnson’s compilation
[
GJ90
, Section A3.2]
.
∎

In fact, we will consider the following slightly modified version of 3D Matching.

Proposition 2.8
.

Numerical 3DM
is strongly NP-hard even when we assume that

a_{i}\in(0.5t,0.6t)
,
b_{i}\in(0.25t,0.3t)
, and

c_{i}\in(0.125t,0.15t)
for all
1\leq i\leq n
.

Proof.

Suppose that we have an instance of Numerical 3DM
a_{1},\dots,a_{n}
,

b_{1},\dots,b_{n}
,
c_{1},\dots,c_{n}
with target sum
t
.
We can transform the instance by defining a variable
X
and setting

a_{i}^{\prime}=a_{i}+4X,\qquad b_{i}^{\prime}=b_{i}+2X,\qquad c_{i}^{\prime}=c_{i}+X,\qquad t^{\prime}=t+7X.

Intuitively, when
X
is sufficiently large,
we have
t^{\prime}\approx 7X
, so
a_{i}^{\prime}\approx\frac{4}{7}t^{\prime}
,

b_{i}^{\prime}\approx\frac{2}{7}t^{\prime}
, and
c_{i}^{\prime}\approx\frac{1}{7}t^{\prime}
,
which makes all inequalities hold.
To be more precise, we claim that, for any
X
larger than a bound
depending polynomially on
a_{i}
,
b_{i}
,
c_{i}
, and
n
,
we have

a_{i}^{\prime}\in(0.5t^{\prime},0.6t^{\prime}),\qquad b_{i}^{\prime}\in(0.25t^{\prime},0.3t^{\prime}),\quad\text{ and }\quad c_{i}^{\prime}\in(0.125t^{\prime},0.15t^{\prime}).

To see why, note that in order for the first inequality
to hold, we must have

a_{i}+4X>0.5(t+7X)\iff X>t-2a_{i}\quad\text{ and }\quad a_{i}+4X<0.6(t+7X)\iff X>5a_{i}-3t.

Solving the other two inequalities, we find that we need

X>\max\{t-4b_{i},10b_{i}-3t,t-8c_{i},20c_{i}-3t\}.

Thus, we only need
X
to be greater than the maximum of six linear expressions in
a_{i}
,
b_{i}
,
c_{i}
, and
t
.
Taking the maximum across all
i
, we get that the working
X
has polynomial size as claimed.
∎

Overview of Reductions from Numerical 3DM

The reductions in Sections
5
,
6
, and
7

all share a similar infrastructure, which we informally outline here. In this overview, we assume
D=1
. We explain how to adapt this framework to
D=2
in Section

.

We reduce from
Numerical 3DM
. Let
(a_{i})_{i=1}^{n}
,
(b_{i})_{i=1}^{n}
, and
(c_{i})_{i=1}^{n}
be the input numbers to match with target sum
t
. By Proposition
2.8
, we can assume that
a_{i}\in(0.5t,0.6t)
,
b_{i}\in(0.25t,0.3t)
, and
c_{i}\in(0.125t,0.15t)
.
We also pick the following parameters:

\displaystyle g

\displaystyle=\Theta(n)

\displaystyle\text{(the gap size)},

\displaystyle h

\displaystyle=\Theta(n^{2})

\displaystyle\text{(the height of each block)},

\displaystyle m

\displaystyle=\Theta(n^{3})

\displaystyle\text{(the unit width of each block)}.

The structure of the reduction is as follows. The dimensions of the box are
D\times H\times W=1\times(nh+(n+1)g)\times(mt+4g)
. The numbers
(a_{i})_{i=1}^{n}
,
(b_{i})_{i=1}^{n}
, and
(c_{i})_{i=1}^{n}
are represented by

(\langle A_{i}\rangle)_{i=1}^{n}
,
(\langle B_{i}\rangle)_{i=1}^{n}
, and
(\langle C_{i}\rangle)_{i=1}^{n}
, instructions corresponding to the subchains
(A_{i})_{i=1}^{n}
,
(B_{i})_{i=1}^{n}
, and
(C_{i})_{i=1}^{n}
that can fold into cuboids of dimensions
1\times h\times ma_{i}
,
1\times h\times mb_{i}
, and
1\times h\times mc_{i}
, respectively. We call these subchains
blocks
. Block gadgets typically consist of
h

) interspersed by
h-1

2
segments, as shown in Figure
2(a)
, but details vary in different variants. Between every two consecutive block gadgets, we add a

, a sequence of instructions that allows connecting the two blocks no matter where they are in the box.

If a matching exists (i.e., there exist two permutations

for all

together in the same row. Refer to Figure

, we can place the blocks with a gap of

In the other direction, we also need to show that the existence of a chain satisfying the instructions forces the existence of a corresponding matching. One might expect
\langle A_{i}\rangle
,
\langle B_{i}\rangle
, and
\langle C_{i}\rangle

•

First, part of the chain corresponding to
\langle B_{i}\rangle
and
\langle C_{i}\rangle
can slide between the blocks

s, causing various unexpected configurations.

•

Even if the blocks do fold into perfect rectangles, one must rule out the case when it is packed with unequal spacing between blocks.

To get around these issues, we will prove Lemma
4.1
that will force the existence of matching even if blocks do not fold ideally.

Common Results

In this section, we will collect arguments that are shared across different reductions from
Numerical 3DM
.

4.1
Segment Packing Lemma

This subsection concerns the part “Chain
\Rightarrow
Matching” (i.e., proving the existence of Numerical 3DM matching).
As said earlier in Section
3
, we need to force the existence of a matching even when blocks do not fold into ideal rectangles or are unevenly spaced. To do so, we will prove the “Segment Packing Lemma” (Lemma
4.1
), which will be used in Sections
5
and
7
.

We first explain the motivation of this lemma. Recall a block gadget typically consists of
h
long segments interspersed with
h-1
length-2 segments. Therefore, for instance, any block
A_{i}
must have
h
segment of length
ma_{i}
. The idea is to view a block as a collection
h
horizontal segments. Therefore, in any chain satisfying the program in the outline, we must have
3nh
segments,
h
of each length
ma_{1},\dots,ma_{n}
,
mb_{1},\dots,mb_{n}
,
mc_{1},\dots,mc_{n}
that were packed in a grid. This motivates the “Segment Packing Lemma”.

The setup of this lemma is as follows.
Let
a_{1},\dots,a_{n}
,
b_{1},\dots,b_{n}
,
c_{1},\dots,c_{n}
be positive integers summing to
nt
. Assume the condition in Proposition
2.8
: for all
i
, we have

a_{i}\in(0.5t,0.6t),\qquad b_{i}\in(0.25t,0.3t),\quad\text{ and }\quad c_{i}\in(0.125t,0.15t).

Let
m
and
h
be positive integers such that
m>H
, and consider a
H\times W
rectangle.
Inside this rectangle, there are
3nh
segments, each of which is horizontal. The segments are classified into
3n
types:

•

n
types of
A-segments
: types
A_{1}
,
A_{2}
, …,
A_{n}
;

•

n
types of
B-segments
: types
B_{1}
,
B_{2}
, …,
B_{n}
; and

•

n
types of
C-segments
: types
C_{1}
,
C_{2}
, …,
C_{n}
.

Lemma 4.1

(Segment Packing Lemma)
.

Suppose that along with our setup, we also have that

W<m(t+1)\quad\text{and}\quad H<nh+\frac{h}{40}

and all of the following are true:

•

There are exactly
h
segments of each type.

•

For all
1\leq i\leq n
, all segments of type
A_{i}
,
B_{i}
, and
C_{i}
have lengths
ma_{i}
,
mb_{i}
, and
mc_{i}
, respectively.

•

No two segments of the same type are more than
h
rows vertically apart.

Then, there exists a matching of the instance
(a_{i})_{i=1}^{n}
,
(b_{i})_{i=1}^{n}
, and
(c_{i})_{i=1}^{n}
of Numerical 3DM.

The rest of the subsection is devoted to proving the lemma. Assume the lemma setup throughout.
First, we make the following observations.

Proposition 4.2
.

We have the following.

(i)

Any row must have at most one A-segment.

(ii)

If a row contains one A-segment, it must contain at most one B-segment.

(iii)

If a row contains one A-segment and one B-segment, then it must contain at most one C-segment.

Proof.

Note that the length of an A-segment is
ma_{i}\geq 0.5m(t+1)>0.5W
, the length of a B-segment is
mb_{i}\geq 0.25m(t+1)>0.25W
, and the length of a C-segment is
mc_{i}\geq 0.125m(t+1)>0.125W
. With these, we now prove each part as follows.

(i)

More than one A-segment will span a width exceeding
W
, a contradiction.

(ii)

If a row contains one A-segment and more than one
B
-segment, the length will be at least
ma_{i}+2mb_{i}>0.5W+2\cdot 0.25W=W
, a contradiction.

(iii)

If a row contains one A-segment, one
B
-segment, and more than one
C
-segment, the length will be at least
ma_{i}+mb_{i}+2mc_{i}>0.5W+0.25W+2\cdot 0.125W=W
, a contradiction.
∎

From Proposition
4.2
, each row in the grid must be of one of the following four categories.

•

A
good row
, which contains exactly one A-segment, one B-segment, and one C-segment.

•

An
A-bad row
, which contains no A-segment.

•

A
B-bad row
, which contains one A-segment but contains no B-segment.

•

A
C-bad row
, which contains one A-segment, one B-segment, but no C-segment.

Let
n_{\text{good}}
,
n_{A}
,
n_{B}
, and
n_{C}
denote the number of good rows,
A
-bad rows,
B
-bad rows, and
C
-bad rows, respectively.
The critical claim is the following.

Claim 4.3
.

n_{A}+n_{B}+n_{C}<h
.

Proof.

First, we note that there are
H<nh+h/40
rows. Of these,
nh
must contain an
A
-path, so

n_{A}<\frac{h}{40}.

Next, there are
nh
B-segments. The good rows and C-bad rows contain at most one B-segment. Moreover, since the length of each B-segment is strictly greater than
0.25W
, each A-bad row contains at most
3
B-segments. Thus,

\displaystyle nh\leq 3n_{A}+n_{C}+n_{\text{good}}\implies n_{B}

\displaystyle\leq 2n_{A}+(n_{A}+n_{B}+n_{C}+n_{\text{good}})-nh

\displaystyle=2n_{A}+\frac{h}{40}<\frac{3h}{40}.

Finally, there are
nh
C-segments. The good rows contain at most one C-segment. Moreover, the length of each C-segment is strictly greater than
0.125W
, so each A-bad row contains at most
7
C-segments and each B-bad row contains at most
3
C-segments. Thus,

\displaystyle nh\leq 7n_{A}+3n_{B}+n_{\text{good}}\implies n_{C}

\displaystyle\leq 6n_{A}+2n_{B}+(n_{A}+n_{B}+n_{C}+n_{\text{good}})-nh

\displaystyle=6n_{A}+2n_{B}+\frac{h}{40}<\frac{13h}{40}.

Summing these three bounds gives the claim.
∎

Finally, we are ready to prove that the solution to an instance of Numerical 3DM exists.

Proof of Lemma
4.1
.

Color each row by its residue modulo
h
. Thus, each color has either
n
or
n+1
rows. By Claim
4.3
, there are less than
h
bad rows, so there is a color
c
such that all rows of that color are good. Moreover, each block gadget spans the height at most
h
, so each block covers at most one row of color
c
. Thus, there are exactly
n
rows of color
c
. For each row, if that row contains a path from blocks
\langle A_{i}\rangle
,
\langle B_{j}\rangle
,
\langle C_{k}\rangle
, then by counting the lengths of the path, we have

ma_{i}+mb_{j}+mc_{k}\leq W<m(t+1)\implies a_{i}+b_{j}+c_{k}\leq t.

Summing this inequality for each row gives
nt\leq nt
, so all inequalities must be equalities, giving that
a_{i}+b_{j}+c_{k}=t
, forming the desired matching.
∎

7
Snake Cube Puzzles with Hexagonal Prisms

In this section, we consider a version of a 2D Snake cube with a chain of hexagonal prisms. From now on, we represent each prism by points. Two prisms are adjacent if and only if the points corresponding to them are adjacent in the triangular lattice. Thus, the problem becomes a triangular grid variant of the flattening fixed-angle chains problem in
[
DILU22
]
.

An infinite
triangular grid
is a two-dimensional lattice generated by vectors
v_{1}=\left(\begin{smallmatrix}1\\
0\end{smallmatrix}\right)
and
v_{2}=\left(\begin{smallmatrix}\cos 60^{\circ}\\
\sin 60^{\circ}\end{smallmatrix}\right)

adjacent
if they are distance
1
apart. A
\boldsymbol{60^{\circ}}
parallelogram box
of dimension
H\times W
is the set of
HW
points obtained by translating the set
\{iv_{1}+jv_{2}:i\in\{1,\dots,W\},j\in\{1,\dots,H\}\}
by some lattice vector.

For this section, a
program
is a string that consists of only characters
S
,
\texttt{T}_{60}
, and
\texttt{T}_{120}
, where
S
denotes straights,
\texttt{T}_{60}
denotes
60^{\circ}
turns (forming
120^{\circ}
angle), and
\texttt{T}_{120}
denotes
120^{\circ}
turns (forming
60^{\circ}
angle). We say that a chain
C=(p_{1},p_{2},\dots,p_{\lvert s\rvert})
(length
\lvert s\rvert
) satisfies
s
if and only if for every
i\in\{2,3,\dots,\lvert s\rvert-1\}
, the angle between
p_{i-1}
,
p_{i}
,
p_{i+1}
is
180^{\circ}
if
s_{i}=\texttt{S}
,
60^{\circ}
if
s_{i}=\texttt{T}_{120}
, and
120^{\circ}
if
s_{i}=\texttt{T}_{60}
.
C
is
closed
if and only if
p_{1}=p_{|s|}
.

We will consider two versions of this problem.

•

Triangular Closed Chain
: given an program
\mathcal{P}
, decide there is a closed chain that satisfies the program
\mathcal{P}
.

•

Bounded Triangular Path Packing
: given a
60^{\circ}
parallelogram box
B
, an program
\mathcal{P}
, and two adjacent vertices
u
and
v
on a boundary of
B
, decide whether there is a chain from
u
to
v
that satisfies program
s
.

In particular, the
Triangular Closed Chain
version is the triangular analog of the flattening problem in
[
DILU22
]
. We will prove the following.

Theorem 7.1
.

Both
Triangular Closed Chain
and
Bounded Triangular Path Packing
are NP-complete.

Clearly, these two problems are in NP with certificates being the sequence of points in the chain. The rest of this section is devoted to proving that these two problems are NP-hard. In particular, Section

7.2
, and
7.3
will show that

is NP-hard by using a similar reduction from Numerical 3DM following the same outline in Section

to

by introducing a new gadget called a

.

With the same proof, we can show that the problem of “given a

parallelogram box

and a program

, decide whether there is a chain of points in

” is NP-Complete. We omit the proof for this version.

8.2
Reduction

If there exists a solution to
2-Partition
, then construct all the gadgets and flip the layer gadgets so that arms for all numbers in
A_{1}

A_{2}
point to the right. The horizontal displacements of the swivel points must sum to

If there exists a solution for

0
