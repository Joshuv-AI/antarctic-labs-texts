Abstract
We consider finite state channels (FSCs) with feedback and state information known causally at the encoder. This setting is quite general and includes: a memoryless channel with i.i.d. state (the Shannon strategy), Markovian states that include look-ahead (LA) access to the state and energy harvesting. We characterize the feedback capacity of the general setting as the directed information between auxiliary random variables with memory to the channel outputs. We also propose two methods for computing the feedback capacity: (i) formulating an infinite-horizon average-reward dynamic program; and (ii) a single-letter lower bound based on auxiliary directed graphs called Q Q -graphs. We demonstrate our computation methods on several examples. In the first example, we introduce a channel with LA and derive a closed-form, analytic lower bound on its feedback capacity. Furthermore, we show that the mentioned methods achieve the feedback capacity of known unifilar FSCs such as the trapdoor channel, the Ising channel and the input-constrained erasure channel. Finally, we analyze the feedback capacity of a channel whose state is stochastically dependent on the input.

The memory in FSCs is encapsulated in a channel state with a finite set of values.
In this paper, we generalize Shannon’s work to the case of FSCs, i.e., we study FSCs with feedback and causal SI known at the encoder, as depicted in Fig.

This setting we study covers many interesting scenarios. One scenario is a channel with i.i.d. state when the SI is available at the encoder in advance with some finite look-ahead (LA)

. The question whether feedback increases the capacity of this scenario is an open problem. Our setting covers this scenario with feedback since an i.i.d. state with a finite LA can be viewed as a Markovian state process causally known at the encoder.
Additionally, our setting covers scenarios in which the state is input-dependent, that is, the state evolution depends on the channel inputs. A well-known problem in which the state is input-dependent is the energy-harvesting (EH) model

The EH model can be viewed as a FSC where the

1
is Noisy Output is the STate (NOST) channels, i.e., channels where the state is stochastically dependent on the channel output, with feedback and causal SI known at the encoder. The capacity of this scenario was derived in

We derive a multi-letter capacity formula for the general setting
expressed as the directed information between a sequence of auxiliary random variables (RVs) and the sequence of the channel outputs. As in the case of i.i.d. states, we show that the channel input is a function of the auxiliary RV and the state. However, in our setting, the auxiliary RVs should have memory.
Although our capacity expression is multi-letter, we use it to propose sequences of lower and upper bounds on the feedback capacity, whose elements are finite-letter expressions. Furthermore, we develop two methods for computing the feedback capacity. The first method is a formulation of the capacity expression as an infinite-horizon dynamic programming (DP) optimization problem. In the second method, we derive a single-letter lower bound based on
Q
-graphs
[
30

.

The DP framework was introduced in
[
31
]
as a tool for computing the feedback capacity of FSCs. This paved the way for more works that formulated the feedback capacity of certain FSCs as a DP: Markov channels
[

]

[

and the most general class of unifilar FSCs
[
34

The
Q
-graph technique, introduced in
[
30
]
, is another tool for computing lower and upper bounds on the feedback capacity. It maps the receiver’s output sequences to a sequential quantization in a finite set of graphs represented by a directed graph, called a

[

, single-letter lower and upper bounds on the feedback capacity were derived for unifilar FSCs for any

[
35

Q

Generally, both the DP and the
Q
-graph tools can be used to compute achievable rates, but for special cases they capture the precise feedback capacity. We show the tightness of the
Q
-graph bound for known unifilar FSCs such as the trapdoor channel
[
34

[
36

and the binary erasure channel (BEC) with inputs constraint
[
37
]

The Setting

We consider FSCs as shown in Fig.

(3)

\displaystyle f_{i}:\mathcal{M}\times{\mathcal{S}}_{0}^{i-1}\times{\mathcal{Y}}^{i-1}\to{\mathcal{X}},

(4)

(5)

II-C

Average-Reward Dynamic Programming

A DP is defined by a septuple
(\mathcal{Z},\mathcal{U},\mathcal{W},F,P_{Z},P_{w},g)
.
We consider a discrete-time dynamic system evolving according to

z_{i}=F(z_{i-1},a_{i},w_{i}),\ i=1,2,\dots

Each state,
z_{i}
, takes values in a Borel space
\mathcal{Z}
.
Each action,
a_{i}
, takes values in a compact subset
\mathcal{A}
of a Borel space.
Each disturbance,
w_{i}
, takes values in a measurable space
\mathcal{W}
, and is drawn from a distribution
P_{w}(\cdot|z_{i-1},a_{i})
that depends on the state
z_{i-1}
, and action
a_{i}

The initial state,
z_{0}
, is drawn from a distribution
P_{Z}
. All functions considered in this section are assumed to be measurable. The history,
h_{i}=(z_{0},w_{1}^{i-1})
, summarizes information available to the controller at time

th action
. At time

, the controller selects the action,

, by a function

that maps histories to actions, i.e.,

. Given a policy, denoted by

, and the history,

, one can compute the actions vector,

, and the past state vector of the system,

.

Given a bounded reward function

,
the objective is to maximize the infinite-horizon average reward. For a policy

, it is defined by

\rho_{\pi}=\liminf_{N\to\infty}\frac{1}{N}\mathbb{E}_{\pi}\left\{\sum_{i=1}^{N}g(Z_{i-1},\mu_{i}(H_{i}))\right\},

(6)

where the subscript
\pi
indicates that actions

are generated
by the policy
\pi
. The optimal average reward is defined by

\rho^{*}=\sup_{\pi}\rho_{\pi}.

(7)

I
satisfies the DP model. That is,

1.

The DP state is a time-invariant function of the previous DP state, action and disturbance.

2.

The disturbance is conditionally dependent on the DP state and action.

3.

The reward is a time-invariant function of the state and action.

Consequently, the optimal average reward is

\displaystyle\rho^{*}=\sup_{\pi}\liminf_{N\to\infty}\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}I_{\pi}(U_{i},U_{i-1};Y_{i}|Y^{i-1}),

where the subscript

\pi

4
is a direct consequence of Lemma
2
and the following Lemma

, whose proofs are given in Appendices
A
and

, respectively.

Lemma 3
.

The feedback capacity of a connected FSC with SI known causally at the encoder can be expressed by

C_{\text{fb-csi}}=\sup\liminf_{N\to\infty}\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}I(U_{i},U_{i-1};Y_{i}|Y^{i-1})\text{,}

(20)

where the supremum is taken with respect to
{\{P(u_{i}|u_{i-1},y^{i-1})\}}_{i\geq 1}
, and the joint distribution is given by (

We note that if the feedback capacity can be achieved with

the supremum in (

IV-B

Q-Graph Technique and Proof of Theorem
5

An altrenative method to compute lower bounds on the feedback capacity, besides the DP, is the
Q
-graph technique in Theorem
5
.
Here, we explain this theorem, and prove it in the next part of this section. The main idea of the proof is to embed an auxiliary graph into the capacity expression in (
10
). Throughout this section, we assume a fixed cardinality
|{\mathcal{U}}|
and a fixed function
f:\mathcal{U}\times\mathcal{S}\to\mathcal{X}
.

The FSC
P_{Y,S^{+}|X,S}
is embedded into a given
Q
-graph by constructing a new directed, connected graph termed an
(S,U,Q)
-graph
to include the information on the
Q
-graph and on the evolution of the state and the auxiliary RV pair,
(S,U)
. The
(S,U,Q)
-graph is constructed as follows:

1.

Each node in the
Q
-graph is split
to
|{\mathcal{S}}|\times|{\mathcal{U}}|
new nodes represented by
(s,u,q)\in{\mathcal{S}}\times{\mathcal{U}}\times{\mathcal{Q}}
.

2.

An edge
(s,u,q)\rightarrow(s^{+},u^{+},q^{+})
, with a label
(x,y)
, exists if and only if there exists a pair
(x,y)
such that
x=f(u^{+},s)
,
P_{Y,S^{+}|X,S}(y,s^{+}|x,s)>0
and
q^{+}=g(q,y)
.

We denote by
\mathcal{P}_{\pi}
the set of input distributions
P(u^{+}|u,q)
that induce a unique stationary distribution on
(S,U,Q)
, i.e., their corresponding
(S,U,Q)
-graph is irreducible and aperiodic. An input distribution
P(u^{+}|u,q)
is said to be
aperiodic
if its
(S,U,Q)
-graph is aperiodic. An aperiodic input distribution is
BCJR-invariant
if it induces the Markov chain

(S^{+},U^{+})-Q^{+}-(Q,Y).

(21)

For the proof of Theorem
5
, we use similar ideas as in the proof of
[
30
, Theorem 3]
based on our Lemma
3
. We show that a BCJR-invariant input distribution induces for all
i
the Markov chain
Y_{i}-Q_{i-1}-Y^{i-1}
, which leads to the fact that
I(U^{+},U;Y|Q)
with the chosen input distribution is a lower bound on the feedback capacity.

For an integer
i
we define
q_{i}\triangleq\Phi_{i}(y^{i})
and prove by induction that
P(u_{i},s_{i}|y^{i},q_{i})=\pi(u_{i},s_{i}|q_{i})

for the choice of a BCJR-invariant input distribution. At time
i-1
, assume that
P(u_{i-1},s_{i-1}|y^{i-1},q_{i-1})=\pi(u_{i-1},s_{i-1}|q_{i-1})
. Then, at time
i
we have

\displaystyle P(u_{i},s_{i}|y^{i},q_{i})

\displaystyle\stackrel{{\scriptstyle(a)}}{{=}}P(u_{i},s_{i}|q_{i-1},y_{i})

\displaystyle\stackrel{{\scriptstyle(b)}}{{=}}P(u_{i},s_{i}|q_{i}).

(22)

Eq. (a) follows from the fact that the BCJR recursive equation (
16
), for any
u,s\in{\mathcal{U}}\times{\mathcal{S}}
,
can be computed from
\{P(u_{i-1},s_{i-1}|y^{i-1})\},\{P(u_{i}|u_{i-1},y^{i-1})\}
and
y_{i}
, while for the first distribution we use the induction hypothesis, and for the second distribution we use the assumption that the inputs are of the form
\{P(u_{i}|u_{i-1},q_{i-1}(y^{i-1}))\}
. Eq. (b) follows from
q_{i}=g(q_{i-1},y_{i})
and from the BCJR-invariant property.
As a result, the Markov chain
Y_{i}-Q_{i-1}-Y^{i-1}
holds for all
i
:

\displaystyle P(y_{i}|y^{i-1},q_{i-1})

\displaystyle=\sum_{s_{i-1},u_{i-1},u_{i}}P(s_{i-1},u_{i-1},u_{i},y_{i}|y^{i-1},q_{i-1})

\displaystyle=\sum_{s_{i-1},u_{i-1},u_{i}}P(u_{i-1},s_{i-1}|y^{i-1},q_{i-1})P(u_{i}|u_{i-1},s_{i-1},y^{i-1},q_{i-1})P(y_{i}|f(u_{i},s_{i-1}),s_{i-1})

\displaystyle\stackrel{{\scriptstyle(a)}}{{=}}\sum_{s_{i-1},u_{i-1},u_{i}}P(u_{i-1},s_{i-1}|y^{i-1},q_{i-1})P(u_{i}|u_{i-1},q_{i-1})P(y_{i}|f(u_{i},s_{i-1}),s_{i-1})

\displaystyle\stackrel{{\scriptstyle(b)}}{{=}}\sum_{s_{i-1},u_{i-1},u_{i}}\pi(u_{i-1},s_{i-1}|q_{i-1})P(u_{i}|u_{i-1},s_{i-1},q_{i-1})P(y_{i}|f(u_{i},s_{i-1}),s_{i-1})

\displaystyle=P(y_{i}|q_{i-1}),

(23)

where

(a)

follows from the assumption on the form of the input distribution;

(b)

follows from the inductive argument shown above.

We now turn to prove Theorem
5
.

Proof of Theorem
5
.

The proof of the theorem is completed by the following chain of inequalities:

\displaystyle C_{\text{fb-csi}}

\displaystyle\stackrel{{\scriptstyle(a)}}{{=}}\sup_{\begin{subarray}{c}\{P(u_{i}|u_{i-1},y^{i-1})\}_{i\geq 1},\\
x_{i}=f(u_{i},s_{i-1})\end{subarray}}\liminf_{N\to\infty}\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}I(U_{i},U_{i-1};Y_{i}|Y^{i-1})

\displaystyle\stackrel{{\scriptstyle(b)}}{{\geq}}\sup_{\begin{subarray}{c}\{P(u_{i}|u_{i-1},y^{i-1})\}_{i\geq 1},\\

\displaystyle\stackrel{{\scriptstyle(c)}}{{\geq}}\liminf_{N\to\infty}\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}I(U_{i},U_{i-1};Y_{i}|Q_{i-1})-I(Y_{i};Y^{i-1}|Q_{i-1})

\displaystyle\stackrel{{\scriptstyle(d)}}{{=}}\liminf_{N\to\infty}\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}I(U_{i},U_{i-1};Y_{i}|Q_{i-1})

\displaystyle\stackrel{{\scriptstyle(e)}}{{=}}I(U^{+},U;Y|Q),

(24)

where

(a)

follows from Lemma
3
.

(b)

follows from adding and subtracting
H(Y_{i}|Q_{i-1})
, and from

H(Y_{i}|U_{i},U_{i-1},Y^{i-1})\leq H(Y_{i}|U_{i},U_{i-1},Q_{i-1})
.

(c)

follows by considering BCJR input distribution
P(u_{i}|u_{i-1},y^{i-1})=P(u^{+}|u,q)\in\mathcal{P}_{\pi}
for all
i
.

(d)

follows from the Markov chain
Y_{i}-Q_{i-1}-Y^{i-1}
.

(e)

follows from the convergence of Markov chains due to the aperiodic input distribution, and the continuity of the mutual information with respect to the joint distribution.

Unifilar FSCs

A FSC is called a unifilar FSC if for any time

s_{0}

Here, we pick three examples of strongly connected unifilar FSCs whose feedback capacity is known from the literature. For each example, we show analytically that its feedback capacity can be achieved by any of our two computation tools with
|{\mathcal{U}}|=2

VII
Conclusions

The feedback capacity of connected FSCs with SI available causally at the encoder was derived. It is expressed as two equivalent multi-letter expressions which consist of a sequence of auxiliary RVs with memory. The first expression consists of DI and have a finite cardinality bound for the set of the auxiliary RVs, but the expression is complicated. The second expression does not have a finite cardinality bound, yet the expression is more simple since the auxiliary RVs constitute a first-order Markov process given the past outputs. Although both of the capacity expressions are multi-letter, we utilized them to provide computable lower and upper bounds on the feedback capacity.
First, sequences of lower and upper bounds were given, i.e., for any integer
N
, finite-letter, computable bounds are obtained. Furthermore, by fixing a finite cardinality
|{\mathcal{U}}|
in the second multi-letter capacity expression, which renders it an achievable rate, two methods were given to compute lower bounds. The first method was a DP formulation of the achievable rate expression, and the second was a single-letter
Q
-graph lower bound. These methods were demonstrated on several examples and were shown to be useful in deriving achievable rates both analytically and numerically, and they were shown to be tight in some of the examples.

\{P(u_{i}|u_{i-1},y^{i-1})P(x_{i}|u_{i},s_{i-1})\}_{i=1}^{k}
. We prove it by induction.
For
n=1
we have:

\displaystyle P(u_{1},y_{1},s_{1})=\sum_{s_{0},x_{1}}P(y_{1},s_{1}|x_{1},s_{0})P(x_{1}|u_{1},s_{0})P(u_{1})P(s_{0}),

because from the definition
U_{i}\triangleq(M,Y^{i-1})
it follows that
U_{1}=M
is independent of
S_{0}
, and
(Y_{1},S_{1})-(X_{1},S_{0})-U_{1}
form a Markov chain due to the FSC definition. Assume
P(u_{k-1},y_{k-1},s_{k-1})
is determined by
\{P(u_{i}|u_{i-1},y^{i-1}),P(x_{i}|u_{i},s_{i-1})\}_{i=1}^{k-1}
, we have

\displaystyle P(u_{k},y_{k},s_{k})=\sum_{s_{k-1}}P(u_{k},y_{k},s_{k}|u_{k-1},y_{k-1},s_{k-1})P(u_{k-1},y_{k-1},s_{k-1}),

and it is sufficient to show that
P(u_{k},y_{k},s_{k}|u_{k-1},y_{k-1},s_{k-1})
depends on
\{P(u_{i}|u_{i-1},y_{i-1}),P(x_{i}|u_{i},s_{i-1})\}_{i=1}^{k}
:

\displaystyle P(u_{k},y_{k},s_{k}|u_{k-1},y_{k-1},s_{k-1})

\displaystyle=\sum_{x_{k}}P(u_{k},y_{k},s_{k},x_{k}|u_{k-1},y_{k-1},s_{k-1})

\displaystyle\stackrel{{\scriptstyle(a)}}{{=}}\sum_{x_{k}}P(u_{k}|u_{k-1},y_{k-1})P(x_{k}|u_{k},s_{k-1})P(y_{k},s_{k}|x_{k},s_{k-1},u_{k})

\displaystyle\stackrel{{\scriptstyle(b)}}{{=}}\sum_{x_{k}}P(u_{k}|u_{k-1},y_{k-1})P(x_{k}|u_{k},s_{k-1})P(y_{k},s_{k}|x_{k},s_{k-1})

where

(a)

follows from
U_{k}=(U_{k-1},Y_{k-1})
due to the definition of
U_{k}
.

(b)

follows from the Markov chain
(Y_{k},S_{k})-(X_{k},S_{k-1})-U_{k}
that holds due to the definition of
U_{k}
and the fact that the channel is a FSC.

∎

Appendix I
Proof of
|{\mathcal{U}}|=|{\mathcal{X}}|^{|{\mathcal{S}}|}

Proof.

Let
T=\{x_{u}(s):{\mathcal{S}}\to{\mathcal{X}}|u\in\{0,1,\dots,|{\mathcal{X}}|^{|{\mathcal{S}}|}-1\}\}
be the set of all
|{\mathcal{X}}|^{|{\mathcal{S}}|}
different functions.
Assume, to the contrary, that
(
8
) is achieved with distribution
P_{1}(\tilde{u}^{N},y^{N})
where at least one variable
U_{i},i\in[1:N]
has cardinality greater than
|\mathcal{T}|
(i.e.,
|{\mathcal{U}}_{i}|>|{\mathcal{X}}|^{|{\mathcal{S}}|}
for some
i
), and that it is greater than the objective induced by any distribution
P(u^{N},y^{N})
with
|{\mathcal{U}}_{i}|=|\mathcal{T}|=|{\mathcal{X}}|^{|{\mathcal{S}}|},\forall i\in[1:N]
. Our goal is to construct such distribution
P_{2}(u^{N},y^{N})
that achieves (
8
) as well. The DI induced by
P_{1}(\tilde{u}^{N},y^{N})
can be written as

\displaystyle I_{P_{1}}(U^{N}\to Y^{N}|s_{0})

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}I_{P_{1}}(U^{i};Y_{i}|Y^{i-1},s_{0})

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}H_{P_{1}}(Y_{i}|Y^{i-1})-H_{P_{1}}(Y_{i}|Y^{i-1},U^{i}),

(66)

where, using the definition of conditional entropy, the entropies in (
66
) can explicitly be written as

\displaystyle H_{P_{1}}(Y_{i}|Y^{i-1})=-\sum_{y^{i}}\sum_{\tilde{u}^{i}\in{{\mathcal{U}}}^{i}}P_{1}(\tilde{u}^{i},y^{i})\log P_{1}(y_{i}|y^{i-1}),

(67)

\displaystyle H_{P_{1}}(Y_{i}|Y^{i-1},U^{i})=-\sum_{y^{i}}\sum_{\tilde{u}^{i}\in{{\mathcal{U}}}^{i}}P_{1}(\tilde{u}^{i},y^{i})\log P_{1}(y_{i}|\tilde{u}^{i},y^{i-1}).

(68)

We are going to show that a legitimate distribution
P_{2}(u^{N},y^{N})
satisfies
H_{P_{2}}(Y_{i}|Y^{i-1})=H_{P_{1}}(Y_{i}|Y^{i-1})
and
H_{P_{2}}(Y_{i}|Y^{i-1},U^{i})=H_{P_{1}}(Y_{i}|Y^{i-1},U^{i})
. We focus on (
68
) first, in which

\displaystyle P_{1}(y_{i}|\tilde{u}^{i},y^{i-1})

\displaystyle\stackrel{{\scriptstyle(a)}}{{=}}\sum_{s_{i-1}}P_{1}(s_{i-1}|\tilde{u}^{i},y^{i-1})P_{1}(y_{i}|\tilde{u}^{i},s_{i-1},y^{i-1})

\displaystyle\stackrel{{\scriptstyle(b)}}{{=}}\sum_{s_{i-1}}P_{1}(s_{i-1}|\tilde{u}^{i},y^{i-1})P(y_{i}|f(\tilde{u}_{i},s_{i-1}),s_{i-1})

\displaystyle\stackrel{{\scriptstyle(c)}}{{=}}\sum_{s_{i-1}}P_{1}(s_{i-1}|\tilde{u}^{i-1},y^{i-1})P(y_{i}|f(\tilde{u}_{i},s_{i-1}),s_{i-1})

\displaystyle\stackrel{{\scriptstyle(d)}}{{=}}\sum_{s_{i-1}}\frac{\sum_{s^{i-2}}\prod_{j=1}^{i-1}P(y_{j},s_{j}|f(\tilde{u}_{j},s_{j-1}),s_{j-1})P(y_{i}|f(\tilde{u}_{i},s_{i-1}),s_{i-1})}{\sum_{s^{i-2}}\big(\prod_{j=1}^{i-2}P(y_{j},s_{j}|f(\tilde{u}_{j},s_{j-1}),s_{j-1})\big)P(y_{i-1}|f(\tilde{u}_{i-1},s_{i-2}),s_{i-2})},

(69)

where

(a)

follows follows from the law of total probability;

(b)

follows from the channel model;

(c)

follows from the Markov chain
U_{i}-(U^{i-1},Y^{i-1})-S_{i-1}
;

(d)

follows since
P_{1}(s_{i-1}|\tilde{u}^{i-1},y^{i-1})=\frac{P_{1}(s_{i-1},\tilde{u}^{i-1},y^{i-1})}{P_{1}(\tilde{u}^{i-1},y^{i-1})}
where

\displaystyle P_{1}(s_{i-1},\tilde{u}^{i-1},y^{i-1})

\displaystyle=(\prod_{j=1}^{i-1}P_{1}(\tilde{u}_{j}|\tilde{u}^{j-1},y^{j-1}))\sum_{s^{i-2}}\prod_{j=1}^{i-1}P(y_{j},s_{j}|f(\tilde{u}_{j},s_{j-1}),s_{j-1}),

\displaystyle P_{1}(\tilde{u}^{i-1},y^{i-1})

\displaystyle=(\prod_{j=1}^{i-1}P_{1}(\tilde{u}_{j}|\tilde{u}^{j-1},y^{j-1}))\sum_{s^{i-2}}\prod_{j=1}^{i-2}P(y_{j},s_{j}|f(\tilde{u}_{j},s_{j-1}),s_{j-1})

\displaystyle\quad\times P(y_{i-1}|f(\tilde{u}_{i-1},s_{i-2}),s_{i-2}).

That is,
P_{1}(y_{i}|\tilde{u}^{i},y^{i-1})
does not depend on the actual symbols of
\tilde{u}^{i}
; rather, it depends on the strategies
(f(\tilde{u}_{1},s_{0}),f(\tilde{u}_{2},s_{1}),\dots,f(\tilde{u}_{i},s_{i-1}))
for all
s_{0}^{i-1}\in{\mathcal{S}}^{i}
. Hence, it has a single, specific
u^{i}
of all different
|{\mathcal{X}}|^{{|{\mathcal{S}}|}^{i}}
vectors such that
P_{1}(y_{i}|\tilde{u}^{i},y^{i-1})=P_{2}(y_{i}|u^{i},y^{i-1})
, where the latter is defined similarly to (
69
) as

\displaystyle P_{2}(y_{i}|u^{i},y^{i-1})

\displaystyle\stackrel{{\scriptstyle(a)}}{{=}}\sum_{s_{i-1}}\frac{\sum_{s^{i-2}}\prod_{j=1}^{i-1}P(y_{j},s_{j}|f(u_{j},s_{j-1}),s_{j-1})P(y_{i}|f(u_{i},s_{i-1}),s_{i-1})}{\sum_{s^{i-2}}\big(\prod_{j=1}^{i-2}P(y_{j},s_{j}|f(u_{j},s_{j-1}),s_{j-1})\big)P(y_{i-1}|f(u_{i-1},s_{i-2}),s_{i-2})}.

(70)

Here, we denote the
j
-th vector of strategies by
u^{i}_{(j)}
(in this context, it is unimportant how we index the vectors, but it is important to note that
u^{i}_{(j)}
and
u^{i}_{(k)}
are two different vector for
j\neq k
). Further, we denote some
\tilde{u}^{i}
with the same strategies as of a specific
u^{i}_{(j)}
by
\tilde{u}_{(j)}^{i}
.
Consequently, by constructing
P_{2}(u^{i}_{(k)},y^{i})\triangleq\sum_{\tilde{u}^{i}_{(k)}}P_{1}(\tilde{u}^{i}_{(k)},y^{i})

for a fixed
y^{i}
and for all
k=1,2,\dots,|{\mathcal{X}}|^{{|{\mathcal{S}}|}^{i}}
, we obtain that any term in (
68
) satisfies

\displaystyle\sum_{\tilde{u}^{i}}P_{1}(\tilde{u}^{i},y^{i})\log P_{1}(y_{i}|\tilde{u}^{i},y^{i-1})

\displaystyle=\sum_{k=1}^{|{\mathcal{X}}|^{{|{\mathcal{S}}|}^{i}}}\sum_{\tilde{u}_{(k)}^{i}}P_{1}(\tilde{u}_{(k)}^{i},y^{i})\log P_{1}(y_{i}|\tilde{u}_{(k)}^{i},y^{i-1})

\displaystyle=\sum_{k=1}^{|{\mathcal{X}}|^{{|{\mathcal{S}}|}^{i}}}\sum_{\tilde{u}_{(k)}^{i}}P_{1}(\tilde{u}_{(k)}^{i},y^{i})\log P_{2}(y_{i}|u_{(k)}^{i},y^{i-1})

\displaystyle=\sum_{k=1}^{|{\mathcal{X}}|^{{|{\mathcal{S}}|}^{i}}}\log P_{2}(y_{i}|u_{(k)}^{i},y^{i-1})\sum_{\tilde{u}_{(k)}^{i}}P_{1}(\tilde{u}_{(k)}^{i},y^{i})

\displaystyle=\sum_{k=1}^{|{\mathcal{X}}|^{{|{\mathcal{S}}|}^{i}}}P_{2}(u_{(k)}^{i},y^{i})\log P_{2}(y_{i}|u_{(k)}^{i},y^{i-1}).

(71)

Further, the construction of
P_{2}(u^{i}_{(k)},y^{i})
for all
k
and all
y^{i}
fully attains a constructed joint distribution
P_{2}(u^{i},y^{i})
. We repeat this construction for all
i\in[1,\dots,N]
, and then we uniquely get the conditional distribution
P_{2}(u_{i}|u^{i-1},y^{i-1})
by the relation

\displaystyle P_{2}(u^{i},y^{i})=P_{2}(u^{i-1},y^{i-1})P_{2}(u_{i}|u^{i-1},y^{i-1})P_{2}(y_{i}|u^{i},y^{i-1}),

(72)

where
P_{2}(u^{i},y^{i}),P(u^{i-1},y^{i-1})
are known from the previously described construction, and
P_{2}(y_{i}|u^{i},y^{i-1})
is given by (
70
). As a result, we have
a legitimate distribution
P_{2}(u^{N},y^{N})
with
|{\mathcal{U}}|=|{\mathcal{X}}|^{|{\mathcal{S}}|}
that induces the same value of
H(Y_{i}|Y^{i-1},U^{i})
as induced by
P_{1}(\tilde{u}^{N},y^{N})
in (
68
), for all
i\in[1,\dots,N]
.

It remains to prove that
P_{2}(u^{N},y^{N})
induces

H_{P_{2}}(Y_{i}|Y^{i-1})=H_{P_{1}}(Y_{i}|Y^{i-1})
.
Now,

\displaystyle P_{1}(y_{i}|y^{i-1})

\displaystyle=\frac{P_{1}(y^{i})}{P_{1}(y^{i-1})}

\displaystyle=\frac{\sum_{\tilde{u}^{i}\in{\mathcal{U}}^{i}}P_{1}(\tilde{u}^{i},y^{i})}{\sum_{\tilde{u}^{i-1}\in{\mathcal{U}}^{i-1}}P_{1}(\tilde{u}^{i-1},y^{i})}

\displaystyle=\frac{\sum_{k=1}^{|{\mathcal{X}}|^{{|{\mathcal{S}}|}^{i}}}\sum_{\tilde{u}^{i}_{(k)}}P_{1}(\tilde{u}^{i}_{(k)},y^{i})}{\sum_{k=1}^{|{\mathcal{X}}|^{{|{\mathcal{S}}|}^{i-1}}}\sum_{\tilde{u}^{i-1}_{(k)}}P_{1}(\tilde{u}^{i-1}_{(k)},y^{i})}

\displaystyle=\frac{\sum_{k=1}^{|{\mathcal{X}}|^{{|{\mathcal{S}}|}^{i}}}P_{2}(u^{i}_{(k)},y^{i})}{\sum_{k=1}^{|{\mathcal{X}}|^{{|{\mathcal{S}}|}^{i-1}}}P_{2}(u^{i-1}_{(k)},y^{i})}

\displaystyle=\frac{P_{2}(y^{i})}{P_{2}(y^{i-1})}

\displaystyle=P_{2}(y_{i}|y^{i-1}).

(73)

Hence, for a fixed
y^{i}
, any term in (
67
) satisfies

\displaystyle\sum_{\tilde{u}^{i}\in{\mathcal{U}}^{i}}P_{1}(\tilde{u}^{i},y^{i})\log P_{1}(y_{i}|y^{i-1})

\displaystyle=\sum_{k=1}^{|{\mathcal{X}}|^{{|{\mathcal{S}}|}^{i}}}\sum_{\tilde{u}^{i}_{(k)}}P_{1}(\tilde{u}^{i}_{(k)},y^{i})\log P_{1}(y_{i}|y^{i-1})

\displaystyle=\sum_{k=1}^{|{\mathcal{X}}|^{{|{\mathcal{S}}|}^{i}}}P_{2}(u^{i}_{(k)},y^{i})\log P_{1}(y_{i}|y^{i-1})

\displaystyle=\sum_{k=1}^{|{\mathcal{X}}|^{{|{\mathcal{S}}|}^{i}}}P_{2}(u^{i}_{(k)},y^{i})\log P_{2}(y_{i}|y^{i-1}),

(74)

and it holds for all
i\in[1:N]
. Finally,
P_{2}(u^{N},y^{N})
achieves (
8
) as well, and the supposition is false.
∎
