Ranked diffusion, delta Bose gas and Burgers equation
Pierre Le Doussal

Abstract
We study the diffusion of N N particles in one dimension interacting via a drift proportional to their rank. In the attractive case (self-gravitating gas) a mapping to the Lieb Liniger quantum model allows to obtain stationary time correlations, return probabilities and the decay rate to the stationary state. The rank field obeys a Burgers equation, which we analyze. It allows to obtain the stationary density at large N N in an external potential V ⁡ ( x ) V(x) (in the repulsive case). In the attractive case the decay rate to the steady state is found to depend on the initial condition if its spatial decay is slow enough. Coulomb gas methods allow to study the final equilibrium at large N N .

II.3
Correlation functions

Consider an observable, for instance the density (here normalized to
N
so we use the notation
\tilde{\rho}

of the main text)

{\tilde{\rho}}(x;\vec{x})=\sum_{i}\delta(x-x_{i})

(71)

Its expectation value in the stationary state of the RD system, i.e.

P_{N}(\vec{x},0)=\Psi_{0}(\vec{x})^{2}/||\Psi_{0}||^{2}
(which is normalized to unity),
is given by

\langle{\tilde{\rho}}(x;\vec{x}(0))\rangle=\int d^{N}\vec{x}{\tilde{\rho}}(x;\vec{x})\frac{\Psi_{0}(\vec{x})^{2}}{||\Psi_{0}||^{2}}=\frac{\langle\Psi_{0}|\hat{\rho}(x,0)|\Psi_{0}\rangle}{||\Psi_{0}||^{2}}

(72)

where
\hat{\rho}(x,0)
is the density operator corresponding to the observable (
71
).
Hence it is equal to the quantum expectation of the density operator in the
ground state of the LL model. Note that in that state the center of mass has uniform probability
over the system hence the result is simply
N/L
. We will study below the more
interesting case where the center of mass position is fixed.

Consider now a time dependent correlation in the RD system. It can be expressed as a quantum matrix element in imaginary time. Indeed one has

\displaystyle\langle{\tilde{\rho}}(x,t){\tilde{\rho}}(y,0)\rangle=\langle{\tilde{\rho}}(x;\vec{x}(t)){\tilde{\rho}}(y;\vec{x}(0))\rangle=\int d^{N}\vec{x}\int d^{N}\vec{y}{\tilde{\rho}}(x;\vec{x})G_{FP}(\vec{x},\vec{y},t){\tilde{\rho}}(y;\vec{y})P_{N}(\vec{y},0)

(73)

\displaystyle=e^{E_{0}t}\int d^{N}\vec{x}\int d^{N}\vec{y}\Psi_{0}(\vec{x}){\tilde{\rho}}(x;\vec{x})G_{s}(\vec{x},\vec{y},t){\tilde{\rho}}(y;\vec{y})\langle\vec{y}|\Psi(0)\rangle

(74)

\displaystyle=\langle\Psi_{0}|\hat{\rho}(x,t)\hat{\rho}(y,0)|\Psi(0)\rangle

(75)

where
\hat{\rho}(x,t)
is the density operator in the Heisenberg representation, i.e.

\hat{\rho}(x,t)=e^{\hat{H}_{s}t}\hat{\rho}(x,0)e^{-\hat{H}_{s}t}
in imaginary time
(with
t=i\tau
and
\tau
is quantum real time).

For instance consider the time correlation of the density in the stationary state of the RD system,
which corresponds to the
initial (unnormalized) quantum state
\langle\vec{y}|\Psi(0)\rangle=\Psi_{0}(\vec{y})/||\Psi_{0}||^{2}
.
This gives

\displaystyle C(x-y,t):=\langle{\tilde{\rho}}(x,t){\tilde{\rho}}(y,0)\rangle=\frac{\langle\Psi_{0}|\hat{\rho}(x,t)\hat{\rho}(y,0)|\Psi_{0}\rangle}{||\Psi_{0}||^{2}}

(76)

This relation extends to any number of space-times points, although we will focus here on two points.
It extends in fact to any operator, for instance it also applies to correlations of the fields operator

\Phi^{\dagger}(x)
and
\Phi(x)
which insert an additional particle, or respectively remove it.
The correlations of this operator were computed in
CalabreseCauxBosonsPRL
;
CalabreseCauxBosonsLong

but we will not study it here.

The stationary dynamics are thus related in both systems. To make it explicit one defines
the form factors

\Sigma^{\rho}_{\lambda}

of the density operator between the ground state and an arbitrary eigenstate

|\Psi_{\lambda}\rangle
of the LL Hamiltonian

\langle\Psi_{0}|\hat{\rho}(x)|\Psi_{\lambda}\rangle=\Sigma^{\rho}_{\lambda}e^{iK_{\lambda}x}

(77)

where
K_{\lambda}
is the total momentum of the eigenstate
|\Psi_{\lambda}\rangle
.
The two point stationary density correlation function
C(x,t)
in the
RD system can then be expressed using these form factors as a sum over intermediate eigenstates
\Psi_{\lambda}

\displaystyle C(x,t)=\frac{\langle\Psi_{0}|\hat{\rho}(x,t)\hat{\rho}(0,0)|\Psi_{0}\rangle}{||\Psi_{0}||^{2}}=\sum_{\lambda}\frac{|\Sigma^{\rho}_{\lambda}|^{2}}{||\Psi_{0}||^{2}||\Psi_{\lambda}||^{2}}e^{-(E_{\lambda}-E_{0})t+iK_{\lambda}x}

(78)

\displaystyle=\sum_{n_{s}=1}^{N}\frac{\bar{c}^{2N-1-n_{s}}}{n_{s}!N!^{2}N^{2}L}\prod_{j=1}^{n_{s}}\sum_{m_{j}\geq 1}\int\frac{dk_{j}}{2\pi m_{j}}\delta_{N,\sum_{j=1}^{n_{s}}m_{j}}\Phi({\bf k},{\bf m})|\Sigma^{\rho}_{{\bf k},{\bf m}}|^{2}e^{ix\sum_{j=1}^{n_{s}}m_{j}k_{j}-t(E({\bf k},{\bf m})-E_{0})}

(79)

The explicit expressions for these form factors were obtained by Calabrese and Caux

CalabreseCauxBosonsPRL
;
CalabreseCauxBosonsLong

and we can thus apply their results.
One has, in our conventions,

|\Sigma^{\rho}_{\lambda}|=\frac{N!}{\bar{c}^{N}}\frac{(\sum_{j}m_{j}k_{j})^{2}}{\bar{c}}N!(N-1)!\prod_{j=1}^{n_{s}}H_{m_{j}}(k_{j}/\bar{c})\quad,\quad H_{m}(x)=|\frac{\Gamma(\frac{N-m}{2}+ix)}{\Gamma(\frac{N+m}{2}+ix)}|^{2}

(80)

Let us give the explicit result for
N=2
and perform some checks. We write

\langle{\tilde{\rho}}(x,t){\tilde{\rho}}(y,0)\rangle=\int\frac{dK}{2\pi}e^{iK(x-y)}C(K,t)

(81)

Performing the sum over states in (
79
)
we obtain the correlation for
N=2
in Fourier space

\displaystyle C(K,t)=\frac{4}{L}\frac{\bar{c}^{4}}{(\frac{1}{4}K^{2}+\bar{c}^{2})^{2}}e^{-\frac{1}{2}tK^{2}}+\frac{64\bar{c}^{3}e^{-\frac{1}{2}\bar{c}^{2}t}}{L}K^{4}e^{-\frac{1}{2}tK^{2}}\int\frac{dq}{2\pi}\frac{e^{-\frac{1}{2}tq^{2}}}{((q+K)^{2}+\bar{c}^{2})^{2}((q-K)^{2}+\bar{c}^{2})^{2}}\frac{q^{2}}{q^{2}+\bar{c}^{2}}

(82)

where the first term arises from the intermediate excited state
\lambda

being a
2
-string (with total momentum
K=2k_{1}
) and the second term
from the two
1
-strings (with total momentum
K=k_{1}+k_{2}
).

There are two important sum rules which allow to check the result.
The first one is obtained by integrating (
81
) over
x

which gives

C(K=0,t)=\int dx\langle{\tilde{\rho}}(x,t){\tilde{\rho}}(0,0)\rangle=N\langle{\tilde{\rho}}(0,0)\rangle=2\frac{2}{||\Psi_{0}||^{2}}\int dy_{2}\Psi_{0}(y,y_{2})^{2}=\frac{4}{L}

(83)

and is indeed satisfied by (
82
). The second is the so-called f-sum rule

CalabreseCauxBosonsPRL
;
CalabreseCauxBosonsLong
. It is obtained by noticing
that
[[\hat{H},{\tilde{\rho}}_{K}],{\tilde{\rho}}_{-K}]=-\sum_{j}[[\partial_{x_{j}},e^{iKx_{j}}],e^{-iKx_{j}}]=-2NK^{2}
where
{\tilde{\rho}}_{K}=\sum_{j}e^{iKx_{j}}
. Taking its expectation in the ground state leads to

-L\partial_{t}C(K,t)|_{t=0}=\sum_{\lambda}(E_{\lambda}-E_{0})\frac{|\langle\Psi_{\lambda}|\hat{\rho}_{K}|\Psi_{0}\rangle|^{2}}{||\Psi_{\lambda}||^{2}||\Psi_{0}||^{2}}=NK^{2}

(84)

From (
82

-L\partial_{t}C(K,t)|_{t=0}=2K^{2}\frac{\bar{c}^{4}}{(\frac{1}{4}K^{2}+\bar{c}^{2})^{2}}+2K^{4}\frac{K^{2}+8\bar{c}^{2}}{16\left(K^{2}+4\bar{c}^{2}\right)^{2}}=2K^{2}

(85)

N
-string contribution to the density correlation

For general

there are many terms in the sum (

We can see already on (

For the

This leads to the following contribution of the ground state manifold to the density correlation function

which saturates the sum rule

and which we expect to dominate in the large time limit

Large

which saturates the sum rule

In Ref.

more excited

,

That may be an indication of how to recover the full Burgers dynamics from the LL Hamiltonian,

One would also like to study the correlation functions in the RD system with

II.4
Limitations of the mapping

To investigate the decay rate to the stationary state of the RD system, in this subsection we
solve directly the case
N=2
in the repulsive case
\bar{c}>0
, setting
T=1
. The Langevin
equation reads

\displaystyle\dot{x}_{1}=\bar{c}\,{\rm sgn}(x_{2}-x_{1})+\sqrt{2}\xi_{1}(t)\quad,\quad\dot{x}_{2}=\bar{c}\,{\rm sgn}(x_{1}-x_{2})+\sqrt{2}\xi_{2}(t)

(102)

Let us denote
x(t)=\frac{1}{2}(x_{1}(t)+x_{2}(t))
and
y(t)=x_{2}(t)-x_{1}(t)
. While the center of mass
performs an independent unit Brownian motion,
\dot{x}(t)=\xi(t)
, the relative coordinate evolves as

\dot{y}=-2\bar{c}\,{\rm sgn}(y)+2\eta(t)

(103)

where
\eta(t)
is an independent unit white noise.
Its probability density
P(y,t)
then evolves according to

\partial_{t}P=2\partial_{y}^{2}P+2\bar{c}\partial_{y}({\rm sgn}(y)P)

(104)

Introducing the Laplace transform
\tilde{P}(y,s)=\int_{0}^{+\infty}dtP(y,t)
one obtains

s\tilde{P}-P(y,0)=2\partial_{y}^{2}\tilde{P}+2\bar{c}\partial_{y}({\rm sgn}(y)\tilde{P})

(105)

where
P(y,0)
is the initial condition.

Let us choose a simple (even) initial condition,
P(y,0)=\frac{a}{2}e^{-a|y|}
where
a>0
.
It is then easy to solve separately for
y>0
and
y<0
. There are two integration constants on both sides.
One on each side is set to zero by requiring that
\tilde{P}(y,s)\to 0
at
y\to\pm\infty
for
s>0
.
Continuity of
P
at
y=0
gives another condition and finally, from integrating (
104
) on a small
interval around
y-0
one obtains the matching condition

P^{\prime}(0^{+},t)-P^{\prime}(0^{-},t)+2\bar{c}P(0,t)=0

(106)

and the same relation holds for the Laplace transforms.

For simplicity of notations let us choose units so that
\bar{c}=1
. This leads to the unique solution

\tilde{P}(y,s)=\frac{1}{s+2a(1-a)}\left(\frac{a}{2}e^{-a|y|}+\frac{a(1-a)}{\sqrt{1+2s}-1}e^{-\frac{1}{2}(1+\sqrt{1+2s})|y|}\right)

(107)

The first term is a special solution of (
105
), in real time

P_{s}(y,t)=\frac{a}{2}e^{-a|y|}e^{-2a(1-a)t}

(108)

which however does not obey the matching condition (
106
), except for
a=1
in which case
it is the stationary solution

P_{\rm stat}(y)=\frac{1}{2}e^{-|y|}

(109)

Returning to the case of general
a
let us invert the Laplace transform at
y=0
. One finds

P(0,t)=\frac{1}{4}\left((2a-1)e^{2(a-1)at}\left(\text{erf}\left(\frac{(1-2a)\sqrt{t}}{\sqrt{2}}\right)+1\right)+\text{erf}\left(\frac{\sqrt{t}}{\sqrt{2}}\right)+1\right)

(110)

There is a transition in the large time behavior at
a=1/2
. For
a\neq 1/2
one finds as
t\to+\infty

P(0,t)=\frac{1}{2}+e^{-\frac{t}{2}}\left(\frac{a(a-1)\sqrt{2}}{(1-2a)^{2}\sqrt{\pi}t^{3/2}}+O(t^{-5/2})\right)-\frac{1}{2}(1-2a)e^{-2a(1-a)t}\,\theta(a<\frac{1}{2})

(111)

where the asymptotic value is
P_{\rm stat}(0)=1/2
. For

this decay is consistent with the result from the LL model
with decay rate
\gamma_{2}=\frac{1}{2}
. For
a<1/2
there is an additional contribution with a slower decay rate
2a(1-a)\leq\frac{1}{2}
. At the transition point
a=1/2

P(0,t)=\frac{1}{4}\left(\text{erf}\left(\frac{\sqrt{t}}{\sqrt{2}}\right)+1\right)=\frac{1}{2}+e^{-\frac{t}{2}}\left(-\frac{1}{2\sqrt{2\pi t}}+\frac{1}{2\sqrt{2\pi}t^{3/2}}+O(t^{-5/2})\right)

(112)

For completeness we gives also
P(y,t)
from Laplace inversion. One finds for any

\displaystyle P(y,t)=\frac{1}{2}ae^{-2(1-a)at-a|y|}

(113)

\displaystyle\frac{1}{4}e^{-|y|}\left(-ae^{(a-1)(2at-|y|)}\text{erfc}\left(\frac{-4at+2t+|y|}{2\sqrt{2}\sqrt{t}}\right)+(a-1)e^{a(2(a-1)t+|y|)}\text{erfc}\left(\frac{4at-2t+|y|}{2\sqrt{2}\sqrt{t}}\right)+\text{erfc}\left(\frac{|y|-2t}{2\sqrt{2}\sqrt{t}}\right)\right)

At the transition point
a=1/2
it simplifies into

\displaystyle P(y,t)=\frac{1}{4}e^{-|y|}\left(e^{\frac{|y|-t}{2}}\text{erf}\left(\frac{|y|}{2\sqrt{2}\sqrt{t}}\right)+\text{erfc}\left(\frac{|y|-2t}{2\sqrt{2}\sqrt{t}}\right)\right)

Interestingly we see that (

is nicely analytic in
a
across

. Hence there is a way to
extract the result in the regime
a<1/2
from the LL, presumably by analytic continuation of the overlap integrals
(i.e. by moving the contours of integration on the string momenta

). We leave that study to the future.

General initial condition
. Note that the general even initial condition can be solved by introducing
\hat{P}(\mu,s)=\int_{0}^{+\infty}dye^{-\mu y}\tilde{P}(y,s)
.
Denoting
P_{0}(\mu)=\int_{0}^{+\infty}dye^{-\mu y}P(y,0)
, the Laplace transform of the initial
condition, one obtains

s\hat{P}(\mu,s)-P_{0}(\mu)=2\mu(1+\mu)\hat{P}(\mu,s)-2\tilde{P}^{\prime}(0,s)-2(1+\mu)P(0,s)

(115)

and we want to impose
P^{\prime}(0,s)+P(0,s)=0
. The solution is

\hat{P}(\mu,s)=\frac{P_{0}(\mu)-2\mu\tilde{P}(0,s)}{s-2\mu(1+\mu)}

(116)

To determine the unknown function
\tilde{P}(0,s)
one notes that there should not be a pole at
s=2\mu(1+\mu)
. Indeed since
\mu>0
that would lead to a Laplace inverse diverging exponentially with time. Hence one has

\tilde{P}(0,s=2\mu(1+\mu))=\frac{P_{0}(\mu)}{2\mu}

(117)

Equivalently, setting
\mu=\frac{1}{2}(\sqrt{1+2s}-1)
(the positive root) one must have

\tilde{P}(0,s)=\frac{P_{0}(\mu=\frac{1}{2}(\sqrt{1+2s}-1))}{\sqrt{1+2s}-1}

(118)

In the case of the above initial condition
P_{0}(\mu)=\frac{a}{2}\frac{a}{a+\mu}
, Laplace inverting
in
\mu
one recovers the above results. In that case the large time decay rate can be obtained
as
-2\mu(1+\mu)
with
\mu=-a
the pole of
P_{0}(\mu)
. Presumably this is a general feature,
with
\mu
being the smallest singularity of the Laplace transform of the initial condition.

Let us compare with the prediction from the LL model for
N=2
. We set

\bar{c}=1
. We recall that
\Psi_{0}(\vec{x})=2e^{-\frac{1}{2}|x_{2}-x_{1}|}

The contribution of the two

Thus we obtain

The integral over

Large
N

As in the text we first consider the limit of large
N

\bar{c}
,
and scale

and rescale time as
t=\tau/N
.
In this limit the dynamics is described by the equation (
15

\partial_{x}r\geq 0

(i) intervals
[x^{-}(\tau),x^{+}(\tau)]

has a plateau
(i.e.
\rho(x,\tau)=0

x(r,\tau)
(used below) has a jump at

.
Note that the value of the plateau

is time independent (as long as the plateau exists).

(ii) shocks at
x=x_{s}(\tau)
where
r
exhibits a upward jump
r(x_{s}^{\pm},\tau)=r^{\pm}(\tau)

where a macroscopic fraction
r^{+}(\tau)-r^{-}(\tau)>0
of particles accumulate and the density
has a delta peak. There the
reciprocal function
x(r,\tau)
(used below) has a plateau on
[r^{-}(\tau),r^{+}(\tau)]

While empty regions can exist for both signs of

, shocks occurs in the dynamics when
\bar{c}<0

III.1
Repulsive interactions
c=-\bar{c}>0

Let us write in that case with
c>0

\displaystyle\partial_{\tau}r(x,\tau)=\left(\tilde{V}^{\prime}(x)-2c~r(x,\tau)\right)\partial_{x}r(x,\tau)

To interpret the term in factor let us note that from the definition (
12

\dot{x}_{i}=\frac{d}{d\tau}x_{i}(\tau)=2c\,r(x_{i}(\tau),\tau)-\tilde{V}^{\prime}(x_{i}(\tau))+\frac{\sqrt{2T}}{\sqrt{N}}\tilde{\xi}_{i}(\tau)

Setting

,
which is acceptable only if
\tilde{V}^{\prime\prime}(x)\geq 0

Convex potential
. The simplest case is when
\tilde{V}(x)
is convex. There are two subcases.
Suppose first that
\tilde{V}(x)
is sufficiently confining, i.e.
\tilde{V}^{\prime}(+\infty)>c

and
\tilde{V}^{\prime}(-\infty)<-c
. This is shown in Fig.
1

the stationary solution is unique, and the density is supported on a finite interval

[x_{e}^{-},x_{e}^{+}]
where the two edges
x_{e}^{\pm}
are the roots of
\tilde{V}^{\prime}(x_{e}^{\pm})=\pm c
.
On this interval the rank field and the density are given by

r_{\rm stat}(x)=\frac{\tilde{V}^{\prime}(x)}{2c}\quad,\quad\rho_{\rm stat}(x)=\frac{\tilde{V}^{\prime\prime}(x)}{2c}\quad,\quad x_{e}^{-}<x<x_{e}^{+}

(129)

Outside of this interval one has
r_{\rm stat}(x)=\frac{1}{2}{\rm sgn}(x)
and the density vanishes.
The density thus exhibits generically a jump at these edges.

What is the dynamics toward stationarity? From (
15
) we see that
r(x,\tau)
increases with
\tau
in regions such that
r(x,\tau)<\tilde{V}^{\prime}(x)/2c
(i.e.
\dot{x}<0
in (

and decreases if

(i.e.
\dot{x}>0
and the particles move to the right). This is illustrated in Fig.
1
and in that case of the convex potential it leads to the
convergence to the unique stationary solution, Eq (
129
).

There is a second subcase however, when either
\tilde{V}^{\prime}(+\infty)<c
or
\tilde{V}^{\prime}(-\infty)>-c
or both.
It is illustrated in Fig.
2
. The stationary measure is again unique and
given by Eq. (
129
) for all
x
, i.e. the edges
x_{e}^{\pm}
are pushed to infinity. In that case, a finite fraction of the particles are expelled to
\pm\infty
: a fraction
\max(0,\frac{1}{2}-\frac{\tilde{V}^{\prime}(+\infty)}{2c})
to
+\infty

and a fraction
\max(0,\frac{\tilde{V}^{\prime}(+\infty)}{2c}+\frac{1}{2})
to
-\infty
.

Multiple wells
. For double (or multiple) well types potentials the situation is more involved, since there
are regions with
\tilde{V}^{\prime\prime}(x)<0
where (
129
) cannot hold. There are thus families of stationary states
which depend on the initial condition
r_{0}(x)=r(x,0)
. They consist in a sequence of intervals where (
129
)
holds, separated by empty regions. An example is shown in Fig.
3
for a given initial condition.
In that example at large time the support of the density consists of two intervals
[x_{e}^{j,-},x_{e}^{j,+}]
,
j=1,2
.
The outer edges are still given by the roots
\tilde{V}^{\prime}(x_{e}^{1,-})=-c
and
\tilde{V}^{\prime}(x_{e}^{2,+})=-c
.
The inner edges
x_{e}^{1,+}
and
x_{e}^{2,-}
can be found using the following property.
Any point
x=a
such that
r_{0}(a)=\tilde{V}^{\prime}(a)/2c
has
\partial_{\tau}r(a,\tau)=0
hence
r(a,\tau)=r_{0}(a)

at all times. This is true assuming that
\partial_{x}r(x,\tau)|_{x=a}<+\infty
, which holds for the repulsive case
(it fails for the attractive case, see below). The point
x=a
is a fixed point
\dot{x}=0
of the flow
(
128
). There are thus two cases

(i)
\tilde{V}^{\prime\prime}(a)-2cr^{\prime}_{0}(a)>0
in which case
\frac{d\dot{x}}{dx}|_{x=a}<0
and
x=a
is an attractive fixed point for the flow
(such as
x_{1}
,
x_{3}
in Fig.
3
) which thus belongs to the support of the stationary state.

(ii)
\tilde{V}^{\prime\prime}(a)-2cr^{\prime}_{0}(a)<0
in which case
\frac{d\dot{x}}{dx}|_{x=a}>0
and
x=a
is a repulsive fixed point for the flow. If
\tilde{V}^{\prime\prime}(a)>0
, as for
x_{2}
in Fig.
1
), the point
a
belongs to the support of the stationary state.
However if
\tilde{V}^{\prime\prime}(a)<0
, such as
x_{2}
in Fig.
3
), it cannot belong to the support. Then in the large time limit,
r(x,\tau)
develops a plateau around the point
x=a
,
of value
r_{\infty}(x)=r_{0}(a)=V^{\prime}(a)/(2c)
. This determines uniquely the asymptotic state from the initial condition, within the family of possible stationary states, see Fig.
3
. To put it simply, all the particles to the left of
x=a
end up in the first well, and all those to the right of
a
end up in the second well.

Note that the sign of
V^{\prime\prime}(a)-2c\partial_{x}r(x,\tau)|_{x=a}
does not change with time since

\partial_{\tau}(V^{\prime\prime}(a)-2c\partial_{x}r(x,\tau)|_{x=a})=-2c\partial_{x}\partial_{\tau}r(x,\tau)|_{x=a}=-2c(V^{\prime\prime}(a)-2c\partial_{x}r(x,\tau)|_{x=a})\partial_{x}r(x,\tau)|_{x=a}

(130)

Using (
127

(V^{\prime}(a)-2cr(a,\tau))\partial_{x}^{2}r(x,\tau)|_{x=a}=0

(if we assume
\partial_{x}^{2}r(x,\tau)|_{x=a}<+\infty
which holds in the repulsive case).

General solution for the dynamics
. It is easy to obtain the general solution of (
15
) by considering
\tau(x,r)
and
using
\frac{\partial_{x}r}{\partial_{\tau}r}=-\partial_{x}\tau
. We obtain

(\tilde{V}^{\prime}(x)-2cr)\partial_{x}\tau=-1
. Let us denote
x_{0}(r)
the inverse
function of the initial condition
r_{0}(x)=r(x,0)
, i.e.
r_{0}(x_{0}(r))=r
.
The general solution is then for
r\in[-\frac{1}{2},\frac{1}{2}]

\tau=\int_{x_{0}(r)}^{x}\frac{dy}{2cr-\tilde{V}^{\prime}(y)}

(131)

Dynamics without potential
. Consider first the system in the absence of external potential,
\tilde{V}(x)=0
. Eq. (
131
)
recovers the perturbative solution of the inviscid Burgers equation

2cr\tau=x-x_{0}(r)\quad,\quad r=r_{0}(x-2cr\tau)

(132)

equivalently
r(x,\tau)=r_{0}(w(x,\tau))
where

\displaystyle w+2c~r_{0}(w)\tau=x\quad\Leftrightarrow\quad w=w(x,\tau)

(133)

This solution is valid as long as the map
w\to w+2c~r_{0}(w)\tau
is
invertible, i.e. it is valid for all times
\tau
such that the initial density satisfies

\displaystyle 1+2c\tau\rho_{0}(x)>0\quad\forall x

(134)

In the repulsive case,
c>0
, it is thus valid for all times. Since
\tilde{V}(x)=0
the
particles are expelled to infinity, and for a localized initial condition (
132
)
leads to
r(x,\tau)\simeq\frac{x}{2c\tau}
for
|x|<2c\tau

at large
\tau
, a non-stationary solution which has the form of
a symmetric front moving at speed
\pm 2c
.
An instructive example is to consider a square initial condition
\rho_{0}(x)=\rho(x,0)=\frac{1}{2\ell}\theta(\ell-|x|)

with
\ell>0
.
The exact solution is then

\displaystyle r(x,\tau)=\begin{cases}\frac{x}{2(\ell+c\tau)}\quad,\quad|x|<\ell+c\tau\\
\pm 1/2\quad,\quad~~\,|x|>\ell+c\tau\end{cases}

(135)

hence the density remains a square at all times, with a width growing linearly with time

\displaystyle\rho(x,\tau)=\frac{1}{2(\ell+c\tau)}\theta(\ell+c\tau-|x|)

(136)

i.e. the gas expands ballistically.

In general one can check that the perturbative solution satisfies both that (i) the point
w=a
such that

r_{0}(a)=0
is preserved, since at time
\tau
it corresponds from (
133
) to
x=a
, hence

r(a,\tau)=r_{0}(a)=0
, and (ii) the center of mass does not move since its position at any time is

\int_{-1/2}^{1/2}drx=\int_{-1/2}^{1/2}dr_{0}(w+2c\tau r_{0})=\int_{-1/2}^{1/2}dr_{0}w
using that
[r_{0}^{2}]_{-1/2}^{1/2}=0
.
Thus the gas expands both such that the number of particle to the left and to the right of
x=a
remains the same and so that the center of mass of the gas is preserved.

Harmonic well
. For the harmonic well,
\tilde{V}^{\prime}(y)=\mu_{0}y
the solution
r=r(x,\tau)
from
(
131
)
takes the implicit form

e^{-\mu_{0}\tau}=\frac{2cr-\mu_{0}x}{2cr-\mu_{0}x_{0}(r)}

(137)

Consider for instance the case where all particles start at
x=y
, i.e.

x_{0}(r)=y
. One finds that the density is uniform

r(x,\tau)=\frac{\mu_{0}}{2c}\frac{x-ye^{-\mu_{0}\tau}}{1-e^{-\mu_{0}\tau}}~,~\rho(x,\tau)=\frac{\mu_{0}}{2c(1-e^{-\mu_{0}\tau})}

(138)

in the time dependent interval
x\in[x_{e}^{-}(\tau),x_{e}^{+}(\tau)]
with
x_{e}^{\pm}(\tau)=\pm\frac{c}{\mu_{0}}(1-e^{-\mu_{0}\tau})+ye^{-\mu_{0}\tau}
, and zero outside. It converges exponentially fast to the stationary state

\displaystyle\rho_{\rm stat}(x)=\begin{cases}\frac{\mu_{0}}{2c}\quad,\quad|x|<\frac{c}{\mu_{0}}\\
0\quad,\quad|x|>\frac{c}{\mu_{0}}\end{cases}

(139)

i.e. a constant density in a finite interval, as was found in the Jellium studies in the regime
large
N
at fixed
c=-\bar{c}
.

Inverse harmonic well
. Note that the solution (
138
) also holds for
\mu_{0}<0
(concave potential).
In that case the gas expands exponentially fast, the two edges being given by
x_{e}^{\pm}(\tau)=(y\pm\frac{c}{|\mu_{0}|})e^{|\mu_{0}|\tau}\mp\frac{c}{\mu_{0}}
. The density inside the interval vanishes exponentially fast

\rho(x,\tau)=\frac{|\mu_{0}|}{2c(e^{|\mu_{0}|\tau}-1)}
an example of the formation of a plateau
in a region with
\tilde{V}^{\prime\prime}(x)<0
, as discussed above.

III.2
Attractive interactions
\bar{c}=-c>0

In the case of attractive interactions some of the above formula are still valid, but only up to some
finite time at which there is formation of delta peaks in the density, which, in the scaling considered here,
correspond to packets of particles containing a finite fraction of the
N
particles. In the context of the
Burgers equation for
r(x,t)
it corresponds to the formation of shocks. In the absence of external potential
it corresponds to the string solutions of the LL model.

Consider the case
\tilde{V}(x)=0
. The simplest example is
the exact solution (
140
)

\displaystyle\rho(x,\tau)=\frac{1}{2(\ell-\bar{c}\tau)}\theta(\ell-\bar{c}\tau-|x|)

(140)

The gas now contracts ballistically. The density becomes a delta function at
x=0
,
i.e.
r(x,\tau)
develops a jump (i.e. a shock) of unit amplitude at
x=0
,
in a finite time
\tau=\ell/\bar{c}
.

In fact the solution (
140
) is very special, as the final position of the shock is
its ”naive” position. This is given by arguments similar to those of the previous subsection,
i.e. it is the attractive point of the dynamics (
128
), equal to
the root
x=a
of
-\dot{x}\equiv\tilde{V}^{\prime}(x)+2\bar{c}r_{0}(x)=0
, in that case
a=0
(since
\tilde{V}(x)=0
).

More generally, when
\rho(x,0)
is not uniform, the perturbative solution of the Burgers equation
(
132
), (
133
) breaks down before, and from (
134
), a shock appears at time

\displaystyle\tau=\tau_{s}=\frac{1}{2\bar{c}\max_{x}\rho(x,0)}

(141)

and at position
x=x_{s}={\rm argmax}\rho(x,0)
, the location of the maximum of the density.
The dynamics beyond that time is more complex (especially in presence of a potential)
and one cannot use the above ”naive” argument
to predict the position of the shock at large time. This argument to predict the final state works
only in the repulsive case, when the perturbative solution holds for all times.

To describe fully the dynamics in the case of attractive interactions, including shocks,
one must give a more precise meaning to the inviscid Burgers equation (
127
)
allowing for shocks. This is a standard problem, and the solution can be deduced from
the inclusion of the (very small) neglected diffusion term. Anticipating on the study of this term in
the next section (note that the noise term remains
subdominant as compared to the diffusion term),
the proper solution in the case
\tilde{V}(x)=0
to which we restrict here, is

\displaystyle r(x,\tau)=\frac{w(x,\tau)-x}{2\tau\bar{c}}=r(w(x,\tau),0)

(142)

\displaystyle w(x,\tau):={\rm argmin}_{w\in\mathbb{R}}[E_{x,\tau}(w)]\quad,\quad E_{x,\tau}(w)=\frac{(w-x)^{2}}{4\tau}-\bar{c}\int_{0}^{w}dx^{\prime}r_{0}(x^{\prime})

(143)

This formula is valid for any sign of
c=-\bar{c}
. It recovers the perturbative solution of Burgers equation
when there is a single minimum in (
143
), which holds for all
times in the repulsive case (since
E_{x,\tau}(w)
is convex in that case).
In the attractive case, the potential energy
-\bar{c}\int_{0}^{w}dx^{\prime}r_{0}(x^{\prime})
is a concave function
with linear behavior at infinity,
\simeq\frac{\bar{c}}{2}|w|
. Beyond the time
\tau=\tau_{s}
,
E_{x,\tau}(w)
has a (time dependent) number
n+1

of local minima
w_{i}=w_{i}(x,\tau)
,
w_{1}<\dots<w_{n}
, solutions of

\frac{w_{i}-x}{2\bar{c}\tau}=r_{0}(w_{i})

(144)

and
n
maxima (each between two minima).
For any given
\tau
and
x
the global minimum belongs to this set,

w(x,\tau)={\rm argmin}_{w\in\{w_{i}\}}E_{x,\tau}(w)
. As
x
is increased (at fixed
\tau
)
the global minimum
w(x,\tau)
takes increasing values in a subset of
p\leq n
elements

w_{i_{1}}<\dots<w_{i_{p}}
of
\{w_{i}\}
.
This corresponds to
p
shocks, i.e.
p
packets of particules being present at time
\tau
.
The positions of the shocks

x_{s}^{j}=x_{s}^{j}(\tau)
,

x_{s}^{1}(\tau)<\dots x_{s}^{p}(\tau)
, are determined by the energy degeneracy condition

E_{x_{s}^{j},\tau}(w_{i_{j}})=E_{x_{s}^{j},\tau}(w_{i_{j+1}})

(145)

One has
p\leq n
because some of the (higher up, small scale) local minima
w_{i}
never become the global minimum as
x
is varied. Accordingly, as
\tau
increases, the positions of two neighboring shocks may coincide, in which case they merge (i.e. the two delta packets of particles merge), and the intermediate local minima drops from the list.

A simple graphical construction allows to determine these positions according
to the ”equal area law”, see Fig.
4
, which is a simple consequence of
the degeneracy condition (
145
).

). Light black: plot of the initial condition

. Dashed line: plot of

, which

At intermediate time there can be a single or several shocks, i.e. delta packets of particles,
on top a a smooth density background. In the large time limit they merge and it always remain only a
single shock, i.e. a single delta packet containing all the particles, associated to the jump from the leftmost minimum
w_{1}
to the rightmost minimum
w_{n}
. To investigate the late time dynamics let us rewrite the potential in a form convenient
for the large
|w|
asymptotics

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{w}dx^{\prime}r_{0}(x^{\prime})=\frac{w}{2}-\kappa_{+}-g_{+}(w)=-\frac{w}{2}+\kappa_{-}+g_{-}(w)\quad,\quad r_{0}(w)=\frac{1}{2}-g^{\prime}_{+}(w)=-\frac{1}{2}+g^{\prime}_{-}(w)

(146)

where the function
g_{+}(w)
decays to zero as
w\to+\infty
,
and
g_{-}(w)
decays to zero as
w\to-\infty
. From the local minima condition (
144
)
we obtain

w_{1}=x-\bar{c}\tau+2\bar{c}\tau g^{\prime}_{-}(w_{1})\quad,\quad w_{n}=x+\bar{c}\tau-2\bar{c}\tau g^{\prime}_{+}(w_{n})

(147)

since
w_{1}\to-\infty
and
w_{n}\to+\infty
as
\tau\to+\infty
. The shock position
x_{s}
is determined
by
E_{x_{s},\tau}(w_{1})=E_{x_{s},\tau}(w_{n})
which yields using (
144
)

\bar{c}^{2}\tau(r_{0}(w_{n})^{2}-r_{0}(w_{1})^{2})-\bar{c}\int_{w_{1}}^{w_{n}}dx^{\prime}r_{0}(x^{\prime})=0

(148)

which, using (
146
) and its derivative, leads to

\bar{c}\tau(g^{\prime}_{-}(w_{1})-g^{\prime}_{+}(w_{n}))(1-g^{\prime}_{-}(w_{1})-g^{\prime}_{+}(w_{n}))=\frac{w_{1}+w_{n}}{2}-\kappa_{+}-\kappa_{-}-g_{+}(w_{n})-g_{+}(w_{1})

(149)

We now insert (
147
) and after some simplifications we obtain the shock position as

x_{s}=\kappa_{+}+\kappa_{-}+g_{+}(w_{n})+g_{+}(w_{1})+\bar{c}\tau(g^{\prime}_{+}(w_{n})^{2}-g^{\prime}_{-}(w_{1})^{2})

(150)

Until now this is exact as long as there is only one shock. It can also be written as

x_{s}=-\int_{w_{1}}^{w_{n}}dx^{\prime}(r_{0}(x^{\prime})-\frac{1}{2}{\rm sgn}(x^{\prime}))+\bar{c}\tau((r_{0}(w_{n})-\frac{1}{2})^{2}-(r_{0}(w_{1})+\frac{1}{2})^{2})

(151)

which is again exact and only assumes a single shock with
w_{1}<0
and
w_{n}>0
.
Inserting the large
\tau
asymptotics we find
x_{s}(\tau)=x_{s}(+\infty)+\delta x_{s}(\tau)

where the final position is simply given by the initial center of mass position

x_{s}(\infty)=\kappa_{+}+\kappa_{-}=-\int_{-\infty}^{+\infty}(r_{0}(x^{\prime})-\frac{1}{2}{\rm sgn}(x^{\prime}))=\int_{-\infty}^{+\infty}dxx\,\rho(x,0)

(152)

and the convergence towards that position can be estimated as

\delta x(\tau)\simeq g_{+}(\bar{c}\tau)+g_{-}(-\bar{c}\tau)+\bar{c}\tau(g^{\prime}_{+}(\bar{c}\tau)^{2}-g^{\prime}_{-}(-\bar{c}\tau)^{2})

(153)

Similarly the fraction of particles in the shock converges to unity as

r_{0}(w_{n})-r_{0}(w_{1})=1-g^{\prime}_{+}(w_{n})-g^{\prime}_{-}(w_{1})\simeq 1-g^{\prime}_{+}(\bar{c}\tau)-g^{\prime}_{-}(-\bar{c}\tau)

(154)

We have assumed that
r_{0}(x)-\frac{1}{2}{\rm sgn}(x)
converges to zero faster than
1/|x|
at large
|x|
,
equivalently that the center of mass position can be defined. Note that the fact that the center of mass position
is independent of time when
V(x)=0
and upon neglecting the noise
is easily seen by multiplying (
11
) by
x
, integrating over
x
and using
integrations by part.

IV
Large
N
with
c=\gamma/N

We consider now the large
N
limit with
\gamma=Nc
fixed, and we also note
\bar{\gamma}=N\bar{c}=-\gamma
.
This scaling allows to study in more details the attractive case and the structure of the shocks.
Here one does not rescale time, nor the potential
V(x)
.
One sees that in (
13
) one can still neglect the noise term, but now one must keep the diffusion term, and one obtains (
\gamma
has arbitrary sign here) the evolution equation of
r(x,t)
as

\partial_{t}r=T\partial_{x}^{2}r+2\bar{\gamma}~r\partial_{x}r+V^{\prime}(x)\partial_{x}r

(155)

where
\gamma
has arbitrary sign.

IV.1
No external potential

Consider first
V(x)=0
. We will look for a solution for
r(x,t)
in the form

r(x,t)=\frac{T}{\bar{\gamma}}\partial_{x}\log Z(x,t)

(156)

where
Z(x,t)
is the solution of the heat equation

\partial_{t}Z(x,t)=T\partial^{2}_{x}Z(x,t)

(157)

From (
156
), we must choose the initial condition to be
Z(x,t=0)=e^{\frac{\bar{\gamma}}{T}\int_{a}^{x}dx^{\prime}r_{0}(x^{\prime})}
.
Here
a
is arbitrary and we will choose
a=0
for convenience. Given this initial condition,
if
Z(x,t)
obeys (
157
) then
H(x,t)=\log Z(x,t)
obeys
\partial_{t}H=T\partial_{x}^{2}H+T(\partial_{x}H)^{2}
and, taking a derivative,
r=\frac{T}{\bar{\gamma}}\partial_{x}H
satisfies
the Burgers equation (
162
) (with
V(x)=0
), with the proper initial condition.
Since the solution for
Z(x,t)
is

Z(x,t)=\int\frac{dw}{\sqrt{4\pi Tt}}e^{-\frac{(w-x)^{2}}{4Tt}+\frac{\bar{\gamma}}{T}\int_{0}^{w}dx^{\prime}r_{0}(x^{\prime})}

(158)

it implies, using (
156
), that the solution for
r(x,t)
is

\displaystyle r(x,t)=\frac{\int\frac{dw}{\sqrt{4\pi Tt}}\frac{w-x}{2\bar{\gamma}t}e^{-\frac{(w-x)^{2}}{4Tt}+\frac{\bar{\gamma}}{T}\int_{0}^{w}dx^{\prime}r(x^{\prime},0)}}{\int\frac{dw}{\sqrt{4\pi Tt}}e^{-\frac{(w-x)^{2}}{4Tt}+\frac{\bar{\gamma}}{T}\int_{0}^{w}dx^{\prime}r(x^{\prime},0)}}

(159)

Note that this solution is valid irrespective of the sign of
\bar{\gamma}=-\gamma
.

One can recover the results of Section
III
in the fixed
\bar{c}
large
N
limit.
To this aim one sets
t=\tau/N
and
\bar{\gamma}=N\bar{c}
and take
N\to+\infty

in (
159
). The argument of the exponential is then uniformly
of
O(N)
and from the saddle point method the integral is dominated by the maximum of the integrand.
One then recovers the solution of the inviscid Burgers equation
given above in (
143
).

Attractive interactions
\bar{\gamma}>0

Thus, in the large
N
limit with fixed
\bar{\gamma}=N\bar{c}
, if we take
\bar{\gamma}/T
of
O(1)
but large,
there is a smooth crossover to the results of the previous section. At finite
\bar{\gamma}>0
the
shocks have a finite width of order
O(T/\bar{\gamma})
(see below), which vanishes in the limit.
So here, by keeping the diffusion term, we are looking at the system on the scale of the shock width.
For a localized initial density there is usually a single shock whose
amplitude is growing. For a multimodal type distribution there may be several shocks
forming which then merge. Each shock contains a finite fraction of the number of
particles. At large time there remains a single shock, which we now study.

One easily checks that for
\bar{\gamma}>0
the following initial condition is stationary,
r_{\rm stat}(x)=r(x,t)=r_{0}(x)
for all
t

\displaystyle\int^{w}dx^{\prime}r_{\rm stat}(x^{\prime})=\int^{w}dx^{\prime}r_{0}(x^{\prime})=\frac{T}{\bar{\gamma}}\log[\cosh[\frac{1}{2T}\bar{\gamma}(w-x_{s})]]

(160)

\displaystyle r_{\rm stat}(x)=\frac{1}{2}\tanh(\frac{\bar{\gamma}}{2T}(x-x_{s}))\quad,\quad\rho_{\rm stat}(x)=\frac{\bar{\gamma}}{4T}\frac{1}{\cosh^{2}(\frac{\bar{\gamma}}{2T}(x-x_{s}))}

(161)

either by inserting into (
159
) or by checking that it satisfies

\displaystyle T\partial_{x}^{2}r_{\rm stat}(x)+2\bar{\gamma}~r_{\rm stat}(x)\partial_{x}r_{\rm stat}(x)=0

(162)

It describes a single shock, i.e. a packet containing the
N
particles.

The stationary distribution is this not unique since
x_{s}
is arbitrary. The low lying diffusion
modes correspond to the diffusion of the center of mass. Indeed it is exact for any
N
that
the center of mass mode
\bar{x}(t)=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i}x_{i}(t)
undergoes diffusion,
i.e. with
{\rm Var}\bar{x}(t)=\frac{2T}{N}t
when started from
\bar{x}(t)=0
at
t=0
.
This motion is subdominant in the limit studied here.

Remark
. This stationary solution has the same form as the well known soliton of the focusing non-linear Schrödinger equation (NLSE). The NLSE also describes a semi-classical limit of the delta Bose gas.

Convergence to the stationary state: droplet initial condition
. Let us study the dynamics
with initially all particles at
x=0
, hence
r_{0}(x)=\frac{1}{2}{\rm sgn}(x)
. Inserting into (
156
), (
158
)
or (
159
) we obtain

r(x,t)=\frac{1}{\bar{\gamma}}\partial_{x}\log\left(e^{\frac{\bar{\gamma}}{2}x}(1+{\rm erf}(\frac{x+\bar{\gamma}t}{2\sqrt{t}}))+e^{-\frac{\bar{\gamma}}{2}x}(1-{\rm erf}(\frac{x-\bar{\gamma}t}{2\sqrt{t}}))\right)

(163)

In the large time limit, it converges to
r_{\rm stat}(x)
given by (
160
) as

r(x,t)\simeq r_{\rm stat}(x)-\frac{e^{-\frac{\bar{\gamma}^{2}}{4}t}}{\bar{\gamma}^{2}\sqrt{\pi t}}\partial_{x}\left(\frac{e^{-\frac{x^{2}}{4t}}}{\cosh(\frac{\bar{\gamma}x}{2})}\right)

(164)

This decay rate is compatible with the one predicted by the Lieb-Liniger model,

\simeq\frac{N^{2}\bar{c}^{2}}{4}=\frac{\bar{\gamma}^{2}}{4}
at large
N
, as expected
since the initial condition is very localized.

Convergence to the stationary state: slow decaying solutions
.
Consider now the following initial condition with two packets of particles centered at positions
x_{1}
and
x_{2}

(in the remainder of this subsection we set
T=1
for notational simplicity)

\displaystyle\int^{w}dx^{\prime}r_{0}(x^{\prime})=\frac{1}{\bar{\gamma}}\log[\cosh[\frac{p_{1}\bar{\gamma}}{2}(w-x_{1})]+\frac{1}{\bar{\gamma}}\log[\cosh[\frac{p_{2}\bar{\gamma}}{2}(w-x_{2})]]\quad,\quad p_{1}+p_{2}=1

(165)

\displaystyle r_{0}(x)=\frac{p_{1}}{2}\tanh(\frac{p_{1}\bar{\gamma}}{2}(x-x_{1}))+\frac{p_{2}}{2}\tanh(\frac{p_{2}\bar{\gamma}}{2}(x-x_{2}))

(166)

which has the proper boundary conditions
r_{0}(x)=\pm\frac{1}{2}
at
x\to\pm\infty
.
If the packets are well separated one can consider that they contain respectively the fractions
p_{1}
and
p_{2}

of particles with
p_{1}+p_{2}=1
. Inserting (
165
) into
(
158
) and (
159
) one finds

r(x,t)=\frac{1}{\bar{\gamma}}\partial_{x}\log\left(\cosh(\frac{\bar{\gamma}}{2}(x-x_{s}))+e^{-p_{1}p_{2}\bar{\gamma}^{2}t}\cosh(\frac{\bar{\gamma}}{2}((p_{1}-p_{2})x-(p_{1}x_{1}-p_{2}x_{2})))\right)

(167)

which converges at large time to the stationary solution (
160
), i.e to a single shock
containing all the particles at position

x_{s}=p_{1}x_{1}+p_{2}x_{2}

(168)

At finite time this solution describes two coalescing shocks.
Note that the decay rate
1/t_{2}
is

1/t_{2}=p_{1}(1-p_{1})\bar{c}^{2}N^{2}\leq\frac{1}{4}\bar{c}^{2}N^{2}

(169)

i.e. the decay is slower than the one predicted by the gap in the LL bosonic spectrum between
the ground state and the one particle excitation. As explained in the text, this is because the
spatial decay of the initial density, here
\rho_{0}(x)\sim e^{-\min(p_{1},p_{2})N\bar{c}|x|}
at large
x
is
slower than the one of
\Psi_{0}(\vec{x}=(x,0,\dots,0))\sim e^{-\frac{N-1}{2}\bar{c}|x|}
.

When the packets are well separated (as compared to their widths), one can describe their finite time dynamics
by considering piecewise superpositions of elementary solutions interpolating
between
a_{j}-\frac{p_{j}}{2}
and
a_{j}+\frac{p_{j}}{2}
of the form

\displaystyle r(x,t)=a_{j}+\frac{p_{j}}{2}\tanh(\frac{p_{j}}{2}\bar{\gamma}(x-x_{j}+2a_{j}\gamma t))

(170)

with
a_{j}=-\frac{1}{2}+p_{1}+\dots+p_{j-1}
. The center of each packet then moves balistically as

x_{s}=x_{j}-2a_{j}\gamma t
. This solution is a good approximation until the packets get closer together.

Dynamics for repulsive interactions
\gamma>0

To obtain some insight into the Burgers dynamics for the
expanding gas (repulsive case) we can either study
the solution (
163
) setting
\bar{\gamma}=-\gamma
with

\gamma>0
, or study again the case of the square initial condition

\rho(x,0)=\frac{1}{2\ell}\theta(\ell-|x|)
with
a>0
, which was solved in (
135
)
and (
140
) in the fixed
\bar{c}
large
N
limit (inviscid limit). Inserting
into (
159
) we obtain

\displaystyle r(x,t)=-\frac{1}{\gamma}\partial_{x}\log\left(f(x,t)+f(-x,t)\right)

(171)

\displaystyle f(x,t)=\frac{e^{-\frac{\gamma x^{2}}{4({\ell}+\gamma t)}}}{\sqrt{1+\frac{\gamma t}{{\ell}}}}\text{erf}\left(\frac{{\ell}+\gamma t+x}{2\sqrt{t}\sqrt{1+\frac{\gamma t}{{\ell}}}}\right)+e^{\frac{1}{4}\gamma({\ell}+\gamma t+2x)}\text{erfc}\left(\frac{{\ell}+\gamma t+x}{2\sqrt{t}}\right)

(172)

These solutions look like (
140
) but with a smoothed boundary.

IV.2
Harmonic potential, repulsive interactions

Consider the harmonic well
V(x)=\mu\frac{x^{2}}{2}
with
\mu>0
.
In the simplest case of no interactions

\gamma=0
the stationary solution is simply

r_{\rm stat}(x)=\frac{1}{2}{\rm Erf}(\frac{x\sqrt{\mu}}{\sqrt{2T}})\quad,\quad\rho_{\rm stat}(x)=\frac{\sqrt{\mu}}{\sqrt{2\pi T}}e^{-\frac{\mu x^{2}}{2T}}

(173)

Consider now repulsive interactions
\gamma>0
. Defining

r(x,t)=\frac{\mu}{2\gamma}x+\tilde{r}(x,t)
(so that
\tilde{r}(x,t)=0
in the stationary state in the absence of the second
derivative) one can rewrite (
162
) as

\displaystyle\partial_{t}\tilde{r}=T\partial_{x}^{2}\tilde{r}-2\gamma\tilde{r}\partial_{x}\tilde{r}-\mu\tilde{r}

(174)

which is the Burgers equation with friction
\mu
for
\tilde{r}(x,t)
.

Let us search for the stationary solution
\partial_{t}\tilde{r}=0
of (
174
), which we call
\tilde{r}_{\rm stat}
. It is easy to see that

\tilde{r}_{\rm stat}
takes the scaling form
\tilde{r}_{\rm stat}(x)=-\frac{\sqrt{\mu T}}{2\gamma}R(x\sqrt{\frac{\mu}{T}})
,
hence
r_{\rm stat}(x)
is of the form

r_{\rm stat}(x)=\frac{\sqrt{\mu T}}{2\gamma}\hat{r}_{g}(x\sqrt{\frac{\mu}{T}})\quad,\quad\hat{r}_{g}(y)=y-R_{g}(y)\quad,\quad\rho_{\rm stat}(x)=\frac{\mu}{2\gamma}\hat{\rho}_{g}(x\sqrt{\frac{\mu}{T}})\quad,\quad\hat{\rho}_{g}(y)=1-R_{g}^{\prime}(y)

(175)

where we have introduced the dimensionless parameter

g=\frac{\gamma}{\sqrt{\mu T}}

(176)

The scaling functions form a one parameter family depending on the parameter
g
.
The function
R(y)=R_{g}(y)
is a dimensionless function solution of

\displaystyle 0=R^{\prime\prime}(y)+R(y)R^{\prime}(y)-R(y)

(177)

which amounts to set
\mu=1
,
\gamma=1/2
and
T=1
.
Since
r(\pm\infty)=\pm\frac{1}{2}
, hence
R_{g}(y)
must satisfy for
y\to\pm\infty

R(y)\simeq y\mp g

(178)

and we expect
R_{g}(y)
to be an odd function. The condition (
178
) can be
equivalently written as

\int_{-\infty}^{+\infty}dy(1-R^{\prime}(y))=2g

(179)

which will be useful below.

The equation (
177
) is an autonomous equation,
y
does not appear. The usual method is to write

R^{\prime}(y)=w(R(y))
for some function
w(R)
of
R
. Then
R^{\prime\prime}(y)=w(R(y))w^{\prime}(R(y))

and the equation (
177
) becomes an equation for
w(R)
where now
R
is the variable

ww^{\prime}=R(1-w)\quad\Rightarrow\quad\frac{wdw}{1-w}=RdR

(180)

This can be integrated as

w+\log(1-w)=-\frac{1}{2}R^{2}-b

(181)

where
b
is a constant as yet undetermined. Clearly
y\to\pm\infty
corresponds to
R\to\pm\infty

and to
w\to 1^{-}
.

There are several equivalent ways to write the solution. The first one is to solve (
181
)
for
w(R)
as

w=1+W(-e^{-b-1-\frac{R^{2}}{2}})

(182)

where here
W(z)=\sum_{n\geq 1}\frac{(-n)^{n-1}}{n!}z^{n}
is the first branch of the Lambert
function solution of
z=We^{W}
, with
z\in[e^{-1},+\infty[
and
W(e^{-1})=-1
.
The condition (
179
) implies that

\int_{-\infty}^{+\infty}(1-w)dx=\int_{-\infty}^{+\infty}\frac{1-w(R)}{w(R)}dR=2g

(183)

which allows to determine the constant
b=b_{g}>0
as a function of
g
as the solution of

\int_{0}^{+\infty}du\,\left(\frac{1}{1+W(-e^{-b-1-\frac{u^{2}}{2}})}-1\right)=g

(184)

Finally since
\frac{dy}{dR}=\frac{1}{w(R)}
one obtains that
R(y)=R_{g}(y)
is determined by inverting from

\int_{0}^{R}\frac{du}{1+W(-e^{-b-1-\frac{u^{2}}{2}})}=y

(185)

with
b=b_{g}
.
The scaling function of the density is then determined parametrically by eliminating
R

between (
185
) and

\hat{\rho}(y)=-W(-e^{-b-1-\frac{R^{2}}{2}})

(186)

Thus the density depends on the parameter
b=b_{g}
which depends on
g

via (
184
).

In the limit
g\ll 1
one has
b\to+\infty
and one can expand
W(z)
at small
z
.
From (
184
) one obtains

z=e^{-b-1}=\sqrt{\frac{2}{\pi}}g-\frac{2\sqrt{2}g^{2}}{\pi}+\frac{\left(8\sqrt{2}-3\sqrt{6}\right)g^{3}}{\pi^{3/2}}+O\left(g^{5}\right)

(187)

Next one obtains

\displaystyle R_{g}(y)=y-g\,\text{erf}\left(\frac{y}{\sqrt{2}}\right)+\frac{g^{2}\left(\left(\sqrt{2}e^{-\frac{y^{2}}{2}}+2\right)\text{erf}\left(\frac{y}{\sqrt{2}}\right)-2\text{erf}(y)\right)}{\sqrt{\pi}}+O\left(g^{3}\right)

(188)

and finally

\displaystyle\hat{\rho}(y)=\sqrt{\frac{2}{\pi}}ge^{-\frac{y^{2}}{2}}+\frac{g^{2}e^{-y^{2}}\left(\sqrt{2\pi}e^{\frac{y^{2}}{2}}y\text{erf}\left(\frac{y}{\sqrt{2}}\right)-2\sqrt{2}e^{\frac{y^{2}}{2}}+2\right)}{\pi}+O\left(g^{3}\right)

(189)

The leading term recovers the result (
173
) for
\gamma=0

In the other limit
g\gg 1
one finds that in the
variable
u=y/g
the density goes to a square function

\hat{\rho}(y)\simeq\theta(1-\frac{|y|}{g})\quad,\quad\rho(x)\simeq\frac{\mu}{2\gamma}\theta(1-\frac{|x|\mu}{\gamma})

(190)

Since in that limit
b\to 0
, one can check that indeed

\hat{\rho}(0)\to 1
since

W(-e^{-1-b})=-1+\sqrt{2b}-\frac{2}{3}b+\dots

(191)

Alternative method
. To solve the equation one can write instead, starting from (
181
)

R(y)=F(w)\quad,\quad F(w)=\pm\sqrt{-2b-2(w+\log(1-w))}

(192)

From the definition
w=R^{\prime}(x)
one has

dx=\frac{dR}{w}=\frac{F^{\prime}(w)dw}{w}

(193)

This leads to the solution in parametric form

\displaystyle R=F(w)=\pm\sqrt{-2b-2(w+\log(1-w))}=\pm\sqrt{2}\sqrt{-1-b+e^{z}-z}

(194)

\displaystyle y=\int^{w}\frac{F^{\prime}(u)du}{u}=\pm\int^{w}\frac{du}{\sqrt{2}(1-u)\sqrt{-b-u-\log(1-u)}}=\pm\int_{z}\frac{dz}{\sqrt{2}\sqrt{-b-1+e^{z}-z}}

(195)

defining
1-w=e^{z}
.

Remark
. Similar looking equations appear in a problem of self-gravitating gas
Kumar2017
, but with somewhat
different interactions, which are linear and attractive at small scale and quadratic and repulsive
at large scales (and there is no external quadratic well).

V
Coulomb gas

Here we discuss the Coulomb gas formulation, which also allows to determine the stationary state
in the large
N
limit.

The (exact) Dean-Kawasaki equation (
11
) for the RD system in terms of the density field
\tilde{\rho}(x,t)=\sum_{i}\delta(x-x_{i}(t)))
normalized to
N
,
and restoring the temperature
T
, reads

\partial_{t}\tilde{\rho}(x,t)=T\partial_{x}^{2}\tilde{\rho}(x,t)+\partial_{x}[\sqrt{2T\tilde{\rho}(x,t)}\eta(x,t)]+\partial_{x}[V^{\prime}(x)\tilde{\rho}(x,t)+\bar{c}\tilde{\rho}(x,t)\int dy\tilde{\rho}(y,t){\rm sgn}(x-y))]

(196)

where
\eta(x,t)
is a unit space time white noise. Following
Dean
;
DeanPrivate
it can be rewritten as an explicit equilibrium dynamics

\partial_{t}\tilde{\rho}(x,t)=-\int dyR(x,y;\tilde{\rho}(.,t))\frac{\delta H[\tilde{\rho}(.,t)]}{\delta\tilde{\rho}(y,t)}+\xi(x,t)\quad,\quad\langle\xi(x,t)\xi(y,t)\rangle=2TR(x,y;\tilde{\rho}(.,t))\delta(t-t^{\prime})

(197)

where

R(x,y;\tilde{\rho})=\partial_{x}\partial_{y}(\sqrt{\tilde{\rho}(x)\tilde{\rho}(y)}\delta(x-y))=\partial_{x}(\tilde{\rho}(x,t)\partial_{y}\delta(x-y))

(198)

and the energy functional is

H[\tilde{\rho}]=\int dxV(x)\tilde{\rho}(x)+\frac{\bar{c}}{2}\int dxdx^{\prime}|x-x^{\prime}|\tilde{\rho}(x)\tilde{\rho}(x^{\prime})+T\int dx\tilde{\rho}(x)\log\tilde{\rho}(x)

(199)

The last term is the so-called entropy term. Indeed one has

\frac{\delta H[\tilde{\rho}]}{\delta\tilde{\rho}(y)}=V(y)+\bar{c}\int dx^{\prime}|y-x^{\prime}|\tilde{\rho}(x^{\prime})+T(1+\log\tilde{\rho}(y))

(200)

Using that for any function
f(y)
, upon integration by part,

-\int dyR(x,y;\tilde{\rho})f(y)=-\int dy\partial_{x}(\tilde{\rho}(x)\partial_{y}\delta(x-y))f(y)=\partial_{x}(\tilde{\rho}(x)f^{\prime}(x))

(201)

one sees that (
197
) is equivalent to (
196
).

Thus the stochastic dynamics of the system satisfies detailed balance,
and generically converges to the equilibrium measure

{\cal P}_{\rm stat}[\tilde{\rho}]\propto\exp\left(-\frac{H[\tilde{\rho}]}{T}\right)

(202)

whenever the latter is well defined. Note that it does not say how fast this convergence
holds. It thus describes the limit where time is taken to infinity first.

Let us now consider the two large
N
limits studied here.

V.1
Large
N
at fixed
c=-\bar{c}
,
V(x)=N\tilde{V}(x)

Let define, as in the text,
\rho(x,t)=\frac{1}{N}\tilde{\rho}(x,t)=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i}\delta(x-x_{i}(t)))

the density normalized to unity. In this regime we consider large
N
at fixed
c=-\bar{c}
, and
scale the potential as
V(x)=N\tilde{V}(x)
with fixed
\tilde{V}(x)
. The energy becomes

H[\tilde{\rho}]=N^{2}{\cal E}[\rho]+O(N)\quad,\quad{\cal E}[\rho]=-\frac{c}{2}\int dxdx^{\prime}|x-x^{\prime}|\rho(x)\rho(x^{\prime})+\int dx\tilde{V}(x)\rho(x)

(203)

where we can neglect the entropy term in that regime since it is
O(N)
. The equilibrium measure

P_{\rm stat}\propto e^{-N^{2}{\cal E}[\rho]/T}
, is dominated by the energy minimum.
To minimize under
the constraint
\int dx\rho(x)=1
one adds the Lagrange multiplier term
\nu(\int dx\rho(x)-1)
to
{\cal E}[\rho]
.
The saddle point equation gives

\frac{\delta{\cal E}}{\delta\rho(x)}=-c\int dx^{\prime}|x-x^{\prime}|\rho(x^{\prime})+\tilde{V}(x)+\nu=0

(204)

for any
x
in the support of
\rho(x)
. Taking a derivative we find that either
\rho(x)=0
or

\displaystyle\partial_{x}\frac{\delta{\cal E}}{\delta\rho(x)}=-c\int dx^{\prime}{\rm sgn}(x-x^{\prime})\rho(x^{\prime})+\tilde{V}^{\prime}(x)=-2c\,r(x)+\tilde{V}^{\prime}(x)=0

(205)

where we used that
\int dx^{\prime}{\rm sgn}(x-x^{\prime})\rho(x^{\prime})=2r(x)
. Hence we find
that for any point
x
inside the support of the density one has
r(x)=\tilde{V}^{\prime}(x)/(2c)
and

\rho(x)=\tilde{V}^{\prime\prime}(x)/(2c)
(and, since
-1/2\leq r(x)\leq 1/2
, any point such that
\tilde{V}^{\prime}(x)>c

cannot belong to the support). This coincides with the result (
129
) which was obtained
by considering the large time limit for
c>0
(the limit
N\to\infty
being taken first).
There we gave a complete discussion
of the determination of the support for various types of potentials
\tilde{V}(x)
.
We would like to know how it compares with the Coulomb gas formulation.
Let us note that the minimum energy verifies,
using (
217
)

{\cal E}_{\rm min}=\int dx\tilde{V}(x)\rho(x)-\frac{1}{2}\nu

(206)

Let us check whether (
217
) is verified. Suppose that the support is a union of (ordered)
intervals
[a_{i},b_{i}]
. Each interval contributes to the first term in the RHS of (
217
) as

\displaystyle-c\int_{a_{i}}^{b_{i}}dx^{\prime}|x-x^{\prime}|\rho(x^{\prime})=-\frac{1}{2}\int_{a_{i}}^{b_{i}}dx^{\prime}|x-x^{\prime}|\tilde{V}^{\prime\prime}(x^{\prime})=\frac{1}{2}\int_{a_{i}}^{b_{i}}dx^{\prime}{\rm sgn}(x^{\prime}-x)\tilde{V}^{\prime}(x^{\prime})-\frac{1}{2}[|x-x^{\prime}|\tilde{V}^{\prime}(x^{\prime})]_{a_{i}}^{b_{i}}

(207)

\displaystyle=-\int_{a_{i}}^{b_{i}}dx^{\prime}\delta(x-x^{\prime})\tilde{V}(x^{\prime})+\frac{1}{2}[{\rm sgn}(x^{\prime}-x)\tilde{V}(x^{\prime})]_{a_{i}}^{b_{i}}-\frac{1}{2}[|x-x^{\prime}|\tilde{V}^{\prime}(x^{\prime})]_{a_{i}}^{b_{i}}

(208)

If
x
belongs to one of the interval, then (
217
) holds provided the following sum is a constant in
x

\sum_{i}\frac{1}{2}[{\rm sgn}(x^{\prime}-x)\tilde{V}(x^{\prime})]_{a_{i}}^{b_{i}}-\frac{1}{2}\sum_{i}[|x-x^{\prime}|\tilde{V}^{\prime}(x^{\prime})]_{a_{i}}^{b_{i}}=-\nu

(209)

Let us examine several cases

Single interval
. Consider first the case where the support is a single interval
[a_{1},b_{1}]
,
which is the case for convex potentials, as in Fig.
1

(with
a_{1}=x_{e}^{-}
and
b_{1}=x_{e}^{+}
). In that case we know that
\tilde{V}^{\prime}(a_{1})=-c

and
\tilde{V}^{\prime}(b_{1})=c
. The first term gives
\frac{1}{2}(\tilde{V}(b_{i})+\tilde{V}(a_{i}))
and the second

-\frac{c}{2}(b_{i}-a_{i})
, which are constants.
Hence (
217
) is verified with
\nu=-\frac{1}{2}(\tilde{V}(b_{i})+\tilde{V}(a_{i}))+\frac{c}{2}(b_{i}-a_{i})
and the energy at the minimum is then

{\cal E}_{\rm min}=\frac{1}{2c}\int_{a_{1}}^{b_{1}}dx\tilde{V}(x)\tilde{V}^{\prime\prime}(x)+\frac{1}{4}(\tilde{V}(b_{i})+\tilde{V}(a_{i}))-\frac{c}{4}(b_{i}-a_{i})

(210)

In the case of the quadratic potential one finds
{\cal E}_{\rm min}=-\frac{1}{6}c^{2}/\mu_{0}
.

Two intervals
. Consider now the case where the support consists in two intervals, as is the case for the double well potential,
as represented in Fig.
3

with
a_{i}=x_{e}^{i,-}
and
b_{i}=x_{e}^{i,+}
. We have (see the figure)

\tilde{V}^{\prime}(a_{1})=-c\quad,\quad\tilde{V}^{\prime}(b_{2})=+c\quad,\quad\tilde{V}^{\prime}(a_{2})=\tilde{V}^{\prime}(b_{1})

(211)

which implies the normalisation
\int dx\rho(x)=\frac{1}{2c}(\int_{a_{1}}^{b_{1}}\tilde{V}^{\prime\prime}(x)+\int_{a_{2}}^{b_{2}}\tilde{V}^{\prime\prime}(x))=1
. We obtain the two terms in (
209
)

-\nu_{\pm}:=\frac{1}{2}(\pm\tilde{V}(b_{1})+\tilde{V}(a_{1})+\tilde{V}(b_{2})\mp\tilde{V}(a_{2}))-\frac{1}{2}(\pm b_{1}\tilde{V}^{\prime}(b_{1})+a_{1}\tilde{V}^{\prime}(a_{1})+b_{2}\tilde{V}^{\prime}(b_{2})\mp a_{2}\tilde{V}^{\prime}(a_{2}))=-\nu

(212)

where the plus sign is for
x
in the first interval and the
-
sign in the second. Note that the
term linear in
x
cancels using (
211
). In the dynamics we found that
the height of the plateau in the rank field,
r(b_{1})=\frac{\tilde{V}^{\prime}(b_{1})}{2c}=r(a_{2})=\frac{\tilde{V}^{\prime}(b_{2})}{2c}
,
was determined by the initial condition. Here we see that for both signs in (
212
) to give the same (i.e. compatible) result, we need in addition to (
211
)

\tilde{V}(b_{1})-b_{1}\tilde{V}^{\prime}(b_{1})=\tilde{V}(a_{2})-a_{2}\tilde{V}^{\prime}(a_{2})

(213)

These two conditions can also be written as

\int_{a_{2}}^{b_{1}}dx\,\tilde{V}^{\prime\prime}(x)=0\quad,\quad\int_{a_{2}}^{b_{1}}dx\,x\tilde{V}^{\prime\prime}(x)=0

(214)

which determine the position of the plateau in the Coulomb gas. For instance for
a symmetric double well
\tilde{V}(x)=\frac{x^{4}}{12}-\frac{x^{2}}{2}
we obtain that the
plateau is at level
r(b_{1})=r(a_{2})=0
, i.e.
a_{2}=-b_{1}=\sqrt{3}
(setting
c=1
) with

V^{\prime}(a_{2})=V^{\prime}(b_{1})=0
. We have checked that this indeed realizes the minimum of the energy

{\cal E}(\rho)
as compared to allowing different levels for the plateau as obtained in the dynamics.

To conclude this subsection, the Coulomb gas gives the equilibrium state of the system, i.e.
the infinite time limit is taken first, to obtain the Gibbs measure (
202
), and only in a second
stage the large
N
limit is performed on this equilibrium state. In the dynamics of Section

III
we have performed large
N
first and neglected the noise. In cases such as double well potentials, convergence to the true equilibrium requires some noise to cross barriers.
Hence at large
N
there are two time scales in the dynamics.
First a rapid convergence to a metastable state as in Fig.
3
, where the
number of particles in each well is determined by the initial condition. This stage is
captured by the dynamics of Section

III
. Next, on much
larger time scales, particles undergo barrier crossing, so that the ”chemical potentials”

\nu_{\pm}
in each well become identical, leading to the equilibrium plateau (
214
).
It would be interesting to study that slow dynamics.

Remark
. It is interesting to note that one can rewrite the energy (

{\cal E}[\rho]=c\int dx(r(x)-\frac{1}{2}{\rm sgn}(x))^{2}+\int dx\,(c\,{\rm sgn}(x)-\tilde{V}^{\prime}(x))(r(x)-\frac{1}{2}{\rm sgn}(x))

Attractive case

V.2
Large
N
at fixed
\gamma=Nc

In that limit one has (up to a constant)

H[\tilde{\rho}]=N{\cal E}_{2}[\rho]\quad,\quad{\cal E}_{2}[\rho]=-\frac{\gamma}{2}\int dxdx^{\prime}|x-x^{\prime}|\rho(x)\rho(x^{\prime})+\int dxV(x)\rho(x)+T\int dx\tilde{\rho}(x)\log\tilde{\rho}(x)

(216)

The problem still maps to a Coulomb gas, with a different scaling for
the equilibrium measure
P_{\rm stat}\propto e^{-N{\cal E}_{2}[\rho]/T}
. It is still
dominated by the minimum energy configuration, but this time the entropy term becomes important.

The saddle point equation now gives (absorbing constants in
\nu
)

\frac{\delta{\cal E}}{\delta\rho(x)}=-\gamma\int dx^{\prime}|x-x^{\prime}|\rho(x^{\prime})+V(x)+T\log\rho(x)+\nu=0

(217)

for any
x
in the support of
\rho(x)
. Taking a derivative we find that either
\rho(x)=0
or

\displaystyle\partial_{x}\frac{\delta{\cal E}}{\delta\rho(x)}=-2\gamma\,r(x)+\tilde{V}^{\prime}(x)+T\frac{\rho^{\prime}(x)}{\rho(x)}=0

(218)

where we used again that
\int dx^{\prime}{\rm sgn}(x-x^{\prime})\rho(x^{\prime})=2r(x)
. This equation can be rewritten as

0=T\partial_{x}^{2}r-2\gamma r\partial_{x}r+V^{\prime}(x)\partial_{x}r

(219)

which is exactly the stationary equation associated to the Burgers equation
in an external potential (
24

\gamma>0

V(x)=\frac{\mu}{2}x^{2}

(ii) for attractive interactions
