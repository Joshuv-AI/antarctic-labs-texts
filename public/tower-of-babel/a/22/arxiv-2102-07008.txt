Abstract




abstract

This paper introduces an estimator that significantly weakens the relevance condition of conventional instrumental variable (IV) methods, allowing endogenous covariates to be weakly correlated, uncorrelated, or even mean-independent, though not independent of instruments. As a result, the estimator can exploit the maximum number of relevant instruments in any given empirical setting. Identification is feasible without excludability, and the disturbance term does not need to possess finite moments. Identification is achieved under a weak conditional median independence condition on pairwise differences in disturbances, along with mild regularity conditions. Furthermore, the estimator is shown to be consistent and asymptotically normal. The relevance condition required for identification is shown to be testable.

Keywords:
distance covariance, dependence, weak instrument, endogeneity,
U
-statistics

Introduction

Empirical work in economics often relies on instrumental variable (IV) methods. However, when instruments are weakly correlated with endogenous covariates, conventional IV methods such as two-stage least squares (TSLS), the control function (CF) method, and the generalised method of moments (GMM) become unreliable, leading to biased estimates and hypothesis tests with significant size distortions. Furthermore, conventional IV methods are infeasible when excluded instruments are unavailable or uncorrelated with the endogenous variables. These conventional methods are also highly sensitive to outliers or non-existent moments of the disturbance term
U
. While much of the econometric literature on the weak instrument problem is focused on detection and weak-instrument-robust inference, theoretical progress on estimation is scant
(
Andrews et al. 2019a
)
. This paper introduces a new single-step estimator that minimises a scalar-valued measure of distributional dependence between a parameterised disturbance
U(\theta)
and a set of instruments
Z
using the distance covariance measure (dCov) proposed by
Székely et al. 2007a
. The proposed Minimum Dependence estimator (MDep) substantially relaxes the instrument relevance requirement, allows for instruments
Z
that are not independent of covariates
X
, and remains robust even when the disturbance term
U
lacks finite moments.

The MDep has remarkable features that render it
fundamentally
different from existing IV methods. (1) The non-independence identifying variation means the MDep can exploit the maximum number of instruments available in any given empirical setting. (2) In the absence of excluded instruments, identification in the MDep framework continues to hold as long as covariates
X
are not independent of instruments
Z
. (3) Although the MDep does not estimate a quantile model, it shares the “robustness” property of quantile estimators—see, e.g.,
Powell 1991a
;
Oberhofer & Haupt 2016a
—in that its asymptotic properties do not rely on the existence of moments of
U
. By replacing
Z
with a bounded one-to-one mapping such that
Z
and the mapping generate the same Euclidean Borel field, one obviates moment existence conditions on
Z
as well.

An example of such a mapping is
z\mapsto\mathrm{atan}(z)
.
This third feature is important as economic theory can go as far as justifying the exogeneity of instruments but typically
cannot
go far enough to justify the existence of moments of
U
. This paper appears to be the first to introduce an IV estimator that leverages arbitrary distributional identifying variation—of unknown and unspecified form—between
X
and
Z
, within a broad class of models, and under an exogeneity condition that is weaker than full independence.

As the form of identifying variation needs to be neither known nor specified, the MDep framework effectively eliminates the sensitivity of estimates to first-stage model specification.

Related Literature

The MDep minimises a scalar-valued criterion of distributional independence between a parameterised error
U(\theta)
and a set of instruments
Z
. This approach builds on the tradition of minimum distance from independence (MDI) estimators initiated by
Manski 1983a
and further developed by
Brown & Wegkamp 2002a
;
Komunjer & Santos 2010a
;
Gao & Galvao 2014a
;
D’Haultfœuille & Février 2015a
;
Torgovitsky 2017a
;
Poirier 2017a
. Of the foregoing, only
Torgovitsky 2017a
explicitly considers the problem of endogeneity as does this paper.
Torgovitsky 2017a
specifies and models a first-stage infinite-dimensional nuisance parameter (the conditional distribution
X\mid Z
), rendering second-stage estimates sensitive to misspecification
(
Dieterle & Snell 2016a
)
. Additionally,
Komunjer & Santos 2010a
;
D’Haultfœuille & Février 2015a
;
Torgovitsky 2017a
require that covariates be continuously distributed—a substantive restriction, e.g., in binary treatment settings. This paper imposes no support restrictions on
[X,Z]
, thereby accommodating a broader class of covariates and instruments, allowing for potentially non-monotonic first-stage relationships, and avoiding the need for continuity assumptions in the first stage. The proposed MDep avoids first-stage restrictions, such as monotonicity or estimation of first-stage functions, and remains consistent and asymptotically normal under a weaker-than-full independence exogeneity condition. Moreover, the current paper appears to be the first to provide a tractable IV relevance condition in the class of MDI estimators.

The MDep estimator is related to ICM estimators, e.g.,
Domínguez & Lobato 2004c
;
Escanciano 2006c
;
Antoine & Lavergne 2014a
;
Escanciano 2018a
;
Wang 2018a
;
Antoine & Sun 2022a
;
Tsyawo 2023a
;
Song et al. 2024c
. This class of estimators minimises the mean dependence of
U(\theta)
on
Z
. A related category of estimators converts mean independence restrictions into several or a continuum of unconditional moment conditions indexed by a nuisance parameter on an index set, and estimation is typically conducted via IV methods such as Two-Stage Least Squares (TSLS) or the Generalised Method of Moments (GMM)—see, e.g.,
Carrasco & Florens 2000a
;
Donald et al. 2003a
;
Hsu & Kuan 2011a
;
Carrasco & Tchuente 2015a
. In spite of the advantages of both the ICM and Continuum Moment (CM) classes of estimators, two key differences set the MDep apart. First, endogenous covariates in the MDep framework can be mean-independent but distributionally dependent on instruments, e.g., at some quantile(s) that need not be known or determined. Thus, ICM/CM-relevant instruments are MDep-relevant by construction, while the converse is not true. Second, unlike ICM/CM estimators, which require the existence of at least the first two moments of the disturbance for consistency and asymptotic inference, the MDep obviates the existence of any moment of the disturbance. Mean independence assumptions apply to the ICM, CM, and conventional IV classes and are often imposed as replacements for distributional exogeneity conditions. While mean independence is implied by distributional exogeneity, this holds under the
implicit
assumption that the mean exists.

Some existing works consider IV estimation without excludability by exploiting and modelling non-linear forms of dependence between endogenous and exogenous covariates, e.g.,
Cragg 1997a
;
Dagenais & Dagenais 1997a
;
Lewbel 1997a
;
Erickson & Whited 2002a
;
Rigobon 2003a
;
Klein & Vella 2010a
;
Gao & Wang 2023a
. Unlike the foregoing, the MDep does not require the practitioner to construct moments or model first-stage relationships. It suffices that there be dependence between covariates and instruments that ought not to be known, modelled, or estimated. To enhance the practicality of this important feature, this paper demonstrates the testability of the MDep relevance condition.

The econometric literature on weak instruments largely focuses on detection and weak-instrument-robust inference (e.g.,
Staiger & Stock 1997a
;
Andrews et al. 2006a
;
Kleibergen & Paap 2006a
;
Andrews & Mikusheva 2016a
;
Sanderson & Windmeijer 2016a
;
Andrews & Armstrong 2017a
)—see
Andrews et al. 2019a
for a review. Normal distributions of conventional IV estimates can be poor and hypothesis tests based on them can be unreliable when instruments are weak
(
Nelson & Startz 1990b
;
Nelson & Startz 1990c
;
Bound et al. 1995a
)
. The MDep gives a new perspective to handling weak IVs in empirical practice; IV- or ICM/CM-irrelevant instruments can be MDep-strong, and this condition is testable.

By extracting non-linear identifying variation in instruments in order to boost instrument strength, a number of works employ flexible methods such as the non-parametric IV, e.g.,
Donald & Newey 2001a
;
Newey & Powell 2003a
;
Donald et al. 2003a
;
Kitamura et al. 2004a
;
Das 2005a
, machine learning techniques, e.g.,
Chen et al. 2020a
, and regularisation or moment selection schemes, e.g.,
Ng & Bai 2009a
;
Darolles et al. 2011a
;
Belloni et al. 2012a
;
Hansen & Kozbur 2014a
;
Carrasco & Tchuente 2015a
. While it is conceivable to take transformations of instruments to extract more identifying variation, this approach may be limited, for example, when available instruments are non-monotone in endogenous covariates.

Y=X+U

The dCov measure is primarily used in tests of independence that are consistent against all forms of dependence, including linear, non-linear, monotone, and non-monotone alternatives. This feature of the dCov measure accounts for the weak relevance condition in the MDep framework. Several applications of the dCov measure have emerged since the seminal work

3
The MDep Estimator

This section presents (1) motivating illustrative examples highlighting the MDep’s key features, (2) the dCov measure, (3) an interesting class of applicable models, and (4) the dCov-based objective function of the MDep estimator.

3.1
Motivating examples

The MDep estimator has unique strengths relative to existing IV estimators. To explore these, consider the linear model

Y=X_{1}\theta_{1}+X_{2}\theta_{2}+U

in the following examples where
X_{1}
is endogenous. In the MDep framework,
Z
is relevant as long as it is
not
independent of any non-trivial linear combination of
X
.

Example 3.1

(Non-monotone first stage)
.

Suppose
X_{1}
and
Z_{1}
are such that

\displaystyle X_{1}

\displaystyle=Z^{*}+U,\text{ and}

\displaystyle Z_{1}

\displaystyle=\mathbbm{1}\{|Z^{*}|<-\Phi^{-1}(0.25)\},

where
[Z^{*},U]\sim\mathcal{N}(0,\mathrm{I}_{2})
and
\Phi^{-1}(\cdot)
is quantile function of the standard normal distribution. Clearly,
X_{1}
and
Z_{1}
are related through
Z^{*}
. However, there is no possible transformation of
Z_{1}
, without extra information, that induces mean dependence or the usual correlation between
X_{1}
with
Z_{1}
.

Example 3.2

(Identification without excludability I - non-monotone first-stage)
.

Consider a slight modification of Example 3.1, where

X_{2}=Z=0.2Z^{*}+Z^{*2}.

Z
has a direct effect on
Y
and is also perfectly correlated with
X_{2}
. Therefore,
\theta
cannot be identified within IV frameworks such as the TSLS. In the MDep framework, however,
Z
is relevant, without being excluded, as no non-trivial linear combination of
X_{1}
and
X_{2}
is independent of
Z
.

Example 3.3

(Identification without excludability II - skedastic function)
.

Here, consider the setting where

\displaystyle X_{1}=U\sqrt{1+Z^{2}},\ X_{2}=Z,\ \text{and}\ \mathbb{E}[U]=0.

Based on the usual correlation,
Z
is not relevant to
X_{1}
. Moreover,
X_{1}
is mean-independent of
Z
. However, relevance in the MDep framework holds as any non-trivial linear combination of
X_{1}
and
X_{2}
is dependent on
Z
.

Example 3.4

(Non-existent first moment of
U
)
.

The disturbance,
U
, follows the Cauchy distribution
U\sim\mathcal{C}\big(0,\ 0.1+|Z_{1}|\big)
with conditional scale heterogeneity. Existing IV methods such as Conventional IV, non-parametric IV, ICM, and CM estimators are inconsistent when the first moment of
U
does not exist. The MDep, in contrast, is consistent.

The MDep explores identifying variation in all the examples given above while conventional IV, non-parametric IV, ICM, and CM methods fail. The above scenarios serve to highlight the remarkable features of the MDep relative to existing conventional methods.

3.2
The dCov measure

It is instructive to briefly review the distance covariance (dCov) measure introduced by
Székely et al. 2007a
, which underpins the MDep objective function.

Definition 3.1
.

The square of the distance covariance between random variables
\Upsilon
and
Z
with finite first moments is defined by
Székely et al. 2007a
as

(3.1)

\begin{split}\mathcal{V}^{2}(\Upsilon,Z)&=\int|\varphi_{\Upsilon,Z}(t,s)-\varphi_{\Upsilon}(t)\varphi_{Z}(s)|^{2}w(t,s)dtds\\
&=\int\big|\mathbb{E}\big[\exp(\iota(t^{\prime}\Upsilon+s^{\prime}Z))\big]-\mathbb{E}\big[\exp(\iota t^{\prime}\Upsilon)\big]\mathbb{E}\big[\exp(\iota s^{\prime}Z)\big]\big|^{2}w(t,s)dtds\end{split}

where
\varphi_{\xi}(.)
denotes the characteristic function of
\xi
,
\iota=\sqrt{-1}
, and the integrating measure
w(t,s)
is an arbitrary positive function for which the integral exists. The modulus is defined as
|\zeta|^{2}=\zeta\bar{\zeta}
, where
\bar{\zeta}
is the complex conjugate of
\zeta
.

Using the integrating measure
w(t,s)=(c_{p_{\Upsilon}}c_{p_{Z}}||t||^{1+p_{\Upsilon}}||s||^{1+p_{Z}})^{-1}
where
c_{p}=\frac{\pi^{(1+p)/2}}{\Gamma((1+p)/2)},\ p\geq 1
, and
\Gamma(\cdot)
is the complete gamma function,
Székely et al. 2007a
obtains a distance covariance measure, which is shown in
Proposition

F.1
to have the representation

\mathcal{V}^{2}(\Upsilon,Z)=\mathbb{E}[\mathcal{Z}||\Upsilon-\Upsilon^{\dagger}||]

where
\mathcal{Z}:=h(Z,Z^{\dagger})
and
h(z_{a},z_{b}):=||z_{a}-z_{b}||-\mathbb{E}\big[||z_{a}-Z||+||Z-z_{b}||\big]+\mathbb{E}\big[||Z-Z^{\dagger}||\big]
. From (
3.1
), one observes that
|\varphi_{U,Z}(t,s)-\varphi_{U}(t)\varphi_{Z}(s)|_{w}^{2}\geq 0
.

This paper follows
Székely & Rizzo 2014c
in using the following algebraically equivalent form of the unbiased estimator of the distance covariance measure

(3.2)

\mathcal{V}_{n}^{2}(\Upsilon,Z):=\frac{1}{n(n-3)}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\sum_{j\neq i}^{n}\mathcal{Z}_{ij,n}||\widetilde{\Upsilon}_{ij}||

where
\mathcal{Z}_{ij,n}:=h_{n}(Z_{i},Z_{j})
such that

(3.3)

h_{n}(Z_{i},Z_{j})=\begin{cases}||\widetilde{Z}_{ij}||-\frac{1}{n-2}\sum_{l=1}^{n}(||\widetilde{Z}_{il}||+||\widetilde{Z}_{lj}||)+\frac{1}{(n-1)(n-2)}\sum_{k=1}^{n}\sum_{l\neq k}^{n}||\widetilde{Z}_{kl}||,&i\neq j\\
0,&i=j\end{cases}.

Székely et al. 2007a
’s
(
Székely et al. 2007a
)
integrating measure
w(t,s)=(c_{p_{\Upsilon}}c_{p_{Z}}||t||^{1+p_{\Upsilon}}||s||^{1+p_{Z}})^{-1}
, besides yielding a reliable measure of dependence, results in a computationally tractable measure, which does not require numerical integration, obviates the choice of smoothing parameters (e.g., bandwidth or number of approximating terms in non-parametric approaches), and admits multiple instruments
Z
. The simplified formulation (
3.2
) offers two key advantages for the proposed estimator: the permutation symmetry of
\mathcal{Z}_{ij,n}=\mathcal{Z}_{ji,n}
facilitates the use of U-statistic theory in establishing asymptotic normality and reduces the computational burden in evaluating (
3.2
).

For ease of reference, the properties of the dCov measure in
Székely et al. 2007a
;
Székely & Rizzo 2009c
are stated below.

Properties of dCov
.

The following properties hold for the distance covariance measure under the condition
\mathbb{E}\big[||\Upsilon||^{2}+||Z||^{2}\big]<\infty
:

(a)

\mathcal{V}^{2}(\Upsilon,Z)\geq 0
;

(b)

\mathcal{V}^{2}(\Upsilon,Z)=0\text{ if and only if }\Upsilon
is independent of

;

(c)

\mathcal{V}^{2}(\Upsilon,Z)=\mathbb{E}\big[\mathcal{Z}||\Upsilon-\Upsilon^{\dagger}||\big]
; and

(d)

\mathbb{E}[\mathcal{V}_{n}^{2}(\Upsilon,Z)]=\mathcal{V}^{2}(\Upsilon,Z)
for
n>3
and
i.i.d.
samples
\big\{[\Upsilon_{i},Z_{i}]\,:\,i\in[n]\big\}
.

The properties are proved in the following: Property
(a)
in
Székely & Rizzo 2009c
, Property
Item

(b)
in
Székely et al. 2007a
, Property

(c)

3.3
Model specification

For a tractable characterisation and statistical testing of the MDep relevance identification condition, consider regression models in which the outcome
Y_{i}
is generated as

\displaystyle Y_{i}=G\big(\theta_{o,c}+g(X_{i}\theta_{o})+U_{i}\big),

where

G(\cdot)
is a known invertible function and
g(\cdot)
is a known differentiable function with unknown parameter vector
\theta_{o}\in\mathbb{R}^{p_{\theta}}
.
\theta_{o,c}
is the location parameter of
U(\theta_{o})
, where
U(\theta):=G^{-1}(Y)-g(X\theta)
denotes the parameterised disturbance function.
X_{i}
contains a constant term. The dependence of
U_{i}(\theta)
on
[U_{i},X_{i}]
is suppressed for notational convenience.

The class of models under consideration includes interesting examples such as the linear model
U_{i}(\theta)=Y_{i}-X_{i}\theta
(where the location parameter coincides with the intercept), non-linear parametric models, e.g.,
U_{i}(\theta)=Y_{i}-\exp(X_{i}\theta)
, fractional response models, e.g.,
U_{i}(\theta)=\log(Y_{i}/(1-Y_{i}))-X_{i}\theta
, and special cases of Box-Cox models e.g.,
U_{i}(\theta)=\log(Y_{i})-X_{i}\theta
. See
Remark

4.3
for a more general class of applicable models.

3.4
Estimation

Let
\big\{W_{i}=[Y_{i},X_{i},Z_{i}]:i\in[n]\big\}
be a random sample of
[Y,X,Z]
defined on a probability space
(\mathcal{W},\mathscr{W},\mathbb{P})
. The MDep estimator is the minimiser of
\mathcal{V}_{n}^{2}\big(U(\theta),Z\big)
, namely

(3.4)

\widehat{\theta}_{n}=\argmin_{\theta\in\Theta}\frac{1}{n(n-3)}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\sum_{j\neq i}^{n}\mathcal{Z}_{ij,n}|\widetilde{U}_{ij}(\theta)|,

where
\mathcal{Z}_{ij,n}:=h_{n}(Z_{i},Z_{j})
as defined in (
3.3
) and
\widetilde{U}_{ij}(\theta)=U_{i}(\theta)-U_{j}(\theta)
. The permutation symmetry of
\mathcal{Z}_{ij,n}
facilitates the use of U-statistic theory in establishing asymptotic normality while reducing the computational burden in evaluating
\mathcal{V}_{n}^{2}\big(U(\theta),Z\big)
. It may be of interest to estimate a location parameter for
U
, e.g., the median
\displaystyle\widehat{\theta}_{n,c}=\argmin_{t}\sum_{i=1}^{n}|U_{i}(\widehat{\theta}_{n})-t|
.
4
4

4

The asymptotic properties of
\widehat{\theta}_{n,c}
are omitted since they can be derived straightforwardly from those of
\widehat{\theta}_{n}
.

Following
Huber 1967a
, the minimand in (
3.4
) is normalised as

(3.5)

Q_{n}(\theta):=\frac{1}{n(n-3)}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\sum_{j\neq i}^{n}\mathcal{Z}_{ij,n}\big(|\widetilde{U}_{ij}(\theta)|-|\widetilde{U}_{ij}|\big).

Normalising the minimand avoids unnecessary moment conditions on
U
—e.g.,
(
Powell 1991a
;
Oberhofer & Haupt 2016a
)
. This holds even though the dCov measure itself requires the existence of
\mathbb{E}[|U|]
—cf.
Székely et al. 2007a
.

It is instructive to highlight the relationship between the MDep (alongside the MDI class) and (conditional) moment estimators, which include conventional IV methods such as ordinary least squares (OLS), IV, TSLS, method of moments (MM), GMM, ICM, and CM estimators, e.g.,
Domínguez & Lobato 2004c
;
Antoine & Lavergne 2014a
;
Escanciano 2018a
;
Tsyawo 2023a
. A moment estimator typically minimises the (weighted) linear dependence between
U(\theta)
and
Z
. ICM/CM estimators minimise the mean dependence of
U(\theta)
on
Z
. The MDep minimises the (distributional) dependence between
U(\theta)
and
Z
. As distributional independence is the strongest form of independence between variables, the MDep thus minimises a more general form of dependence between
U(\theta)
and
Z
.

One notices from (

;

;

, non-smooth objective functions such as quantile regression (QR), e.g.,
Koenker & Bassett 1978a
;

;
Oberhofer & Haupt 2016a
, and instrumental variable QR methods, e.g.,
Chernozhukov & Hansen 2006a
;
Chernozhukov & Hansen 2008a
, and the CF approach to QR of

. Let the Jacobian and its symmetrised version be defined by

X^{g}(\theta):=\frac{\partial U(\theta)}{\partial\theta^{\prime}}\text{ and }\widetilde{X^{g}}(\theta):=X^{g}(\theta)-X^{g\dagger}(\theta),

4.1
Regularity conditions

Two sets of regularity conditions imposed in the paper guarantee the consistency of the MDep estimator
\widehat{\theta}_{n}
. The first set comprises sampling, smoothing, and dominance conditions, ensuring that the difference between the normalised minimand and its expectation converges to zero uniformly in
\theta\in\Theta
. The second comprises exogeneity and relevance identification conditions.

Assumption 1

(Regularity)
.

(a)

\big\{W_{i}:i\in[n]\big\}
are independently and identically (
i.i.d.
) distributed random vectors, and the outcome is generated as
Y_{i}=G\big(\theta_{o,c}+g(X_{i}\theta_{o})+U_{i}\big)
where
G(\cdot)
and
g(\cdot)
are known functions and
G(\cdot)
is invertible.

(b)

U(\theta)
is measurable in
[U,X]
for all
\theta
and is twice differentiable in
\theta
for all
[U,X]
on the support of

Conditional functionals and parameters of interest

Whenever elements of

When

. For ease of exposition, consider the simple linear model

where
U\mathchoice{\mathrel{\hbox to0.0pt{$\displaystyle\perp$\hss}\mkern 2.0mu{\displaystyle\perp}}}{\mathrel{\hbox to0.0pt{$\textstyle\perp$\hss}\mkern 2.0mu{\textstyle\perp}}}{\mathrel{\hbox to0.0pt{$\scriptstyle\perp$\hss}\mkern 2.0mu{\scriptstyle\perp}}}{\mathrel{\hbox to0.0pt{$\scriptscriptstyle\perp$\hss}\mkern 2.0mu{\scriptscriptstyle\perp}}}X
,

is binary,

, and

. Under
2
Item

(a)
,

is identified. This means the difference in mean outcomes increases by
\theta_{o}
for a unit increase in

. If the first moment of

does not exist, only

by a unit increases the median difference in outcomes by

is not constant for non-linear

6
Conclusion

This paper introduces the MDep estimator—an MDI estimator—which weakens the relevance condition of conventional IV, ICM, CM, and non-parametric IV methods to distributional dependence between non-trivial linear combinations of
X
and
Z
. Thus under the MDep framework, one can exploit the maximum number of relevant instruments possible in any given empirical setting subject to a weaker-than-full-independence exogeneity condition.

The MDep provides a fundamentally different and practically useful approach to dealing with (1) the unavailability of excluded instruments, (2) the weak-IV problem, and (3) the non-existence or contamination of the disturbance term by outliers or random noise with possibly non-existent moments. Consistent estimation and reliable inference are feasible without excludability provided endogenous covariates are non-linearly dependent (in the distributional sense) on exogenous covariates. The MDep handles the weak IV problem by admitting instruments of which the endogenous covariates may be uncorrelated or even mean-independent but not independent. Additionally, the disturbance term
U
need not possess any finite moments, and bounded one-to-one mappings of
Z
obviate moment bounds on
Z
.

Identification, consistency, and asymptotic normality hold in the MDep framework under mild regularity conditions. Moreover, the MDep covariance matrix estimator is shown to be consistent. To ensure the practical usefulness of the MDep, this paper shows the testability of the weak relevance condition. Illustrative examples backed by simulations showcase the remarkable properties of the MDep estimator vis-à-vis existing conventional IV and ICM methods. This paper is a first step in developing a distance covariance-based estimator for econometric models under possible endogeneity without first-stage model specifications or restrictions. Future work could extend the current framework in empirically relevant directions, such as accounting for clustered and temporal dependence in the observations and developing alternative inference procedures, including the bootstrap.

Appendix F
Useful Proposition

The following result justifies the alternative representation of the squared dCov measure used in the paper. Recall
h(z_{a},z_{b}):=||z_{a}-z_{b}||-\mathbb{E}\big[||z_{a}-Z||+||Z-z_{b}||\big]+\mathbb{E}\big[||Z-Z^{\dagger}||\big]
.

Proposition F.1
.

Suppose
\mathbb{E}\big[\|\Upsilon\|^{2}+\|Z\|^{2}\big]<\infty
, then
\mathcal{V}^{2}(\Upsilon,Z)=\mathbb{E}\big[\|\Upsilon-\Upsilon^{\dagger}\|\cdot h(Z,Z^{\dagger})\big]
.

Proof.

Let
[\Upsilon^{\dagger},Z^{\dagger}]
and
[\Upsilon^{\dagger\dagger},Z^{\dagger\dagger}]
be
i.i.d.
copies of
[\Upsilon,Z]
. It follows from
Székely & Rizzo 2009c
(see also
Székely & Rizzo 2014c
) under the given dominance condition
\mathbb{E}\big[\|\Upsilon\|^{2}+\|Z\|^{2}\big]<\infty
that

(F.1)

\begin{split}\mathcal{V}^{2}(\Upsilon,Z)&=\mathbb{E}\big[\|\Upsilon-\Upsilon^{\dagger}\|\cdot\|Z-Z^{\dagger}\|\big]-\mathbb{E}\big[\|\Upsilon-\Upsilon^{\dagger}\|\cdot\|Z-Z^{\dagger\dagger}\|\big]\\
&-\mathbb{E}\big[\|\Upsilon-\Upsilon^{\dagger\dagger}\|\cdot\|Z-Z^{\dagger}\|\big]+\mathbb{E}\big[\|\Upsilon-\Upsilon^{\dagger}\|\big]\cdot\mathbb{E}\big[\|Z-Z^{\dagger}\|\big].\end{split}

By the Law of Iterated Expectations (LIE), independence (IND) and identical (ID) distribution of the copies,

(F.2)

\begin{split}\mathbb{E}\big[\|\Upsilon-\Upsilon^{\dagger}\|\cdot\|Z-Z^{\dagger\dagger}\|\big]&\overset{\mathrm{LIE}}{=}\mathbb{E}\Big[\mathbb{E}\big[\big(\|\Upsilon-\Upsilon^{\dagger}\|\big)\mid Z,Z^{\dagger\dagger}\big]\cdot\|Z-Z^{\dagger\dagger}\|\Big]\\
&\overset{\mathrm{IND}}{=}\mathbb{E}\Big[\mathbb{E}\big[\big(\|\Upsilon-\Upsilon^{\dagger}\|\big)\mid Z\big]\cdot\|Z-Z^{\dagger\dagger}\|\Big]\\
&\overset{\mathrm{LIE}}{=}\mathbb{E}\Big[\mathbb{E}\big[\big(\|\Upsilon-\Upsilon^{\dagger}\|\big)\mid Z\big]\cdot\mathbb{E}\big[\big(\|Z-Z^{\dagger\dagger}\|\big)\mid Z\big]\Big]\\
&\overset{\mathrm{LIE}}{=}\mathbb{E}\Big[\|\Upsilon-\Upsilon^{\dagger}\|\cdot\mathbb{E}\big[\big(\|Z-Z^{\dagger\dagger}\|\big)\mid Z\big]\Big]\\
&\overset{\mathrm{ID}}{=}\mathbb{E}\Big[\|\Upsilon-\Upsilon^{\dagger}\|\cdot\mathbb{E}\big[\big(\|Z-Z^{\dagger}\|\big)\mid Z\big]\Big].\end{split}

In a similar vein,

(F.3)

\begin{split}\mathbb{E}\big[\|\Upsilon-\Upsilon^{\dagger\dagger}\|\cdot\|Z-Z^{\dagger}\|\big]&\overset{\mathrm{ID}}{=}\mathbb{E}\big[\|\Upsilon^{\dagger}-\Upsilon\|\cdot\|Z^{\dagger}-Z^{\dagger\dagger}\|\big]\\
&\overset{\mathrm{\eqref{eqn:dCov_exp_1}}}{=}\mathbb{E}\Big[\|\Upsilon^{\dagger}-\Upsilon\|\cdot\mathbb{E}\big[\big(\|Z^{\dagger}-Z^{\dagger\dagger}\|\big)\mid Z^{\dagger}\big]\Big]\\
&\overset{\mathrm{ID}}{=}\mathbb{E}\Big[\|\Upsilon^{\dagger}-\Upsilon\|\cdot\mathbb{E}\big[\big(\|Z^{\dagger}-Z\|\big)\mid Z^{\dagger}\big]\Big].\end{split}

Combining (
F.1
), (
F.2
), and (
F.3
),

\displaystyle\mathcal{V}^{2}(\Upsilon,Z)

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}\big[\|\Upsilon-\Upsilon^{\dagger}\|\cdot\|Z-Z^{\dagger}\|\big]-\mathbb{E}\Big[\|\Upsilon-\Upsilon^{\dagger}\|\cdot\mathbb{E}\big[\big(\|Z-Z^{\dagger}\|\big)\mid Z\big]\Big]

\displaystyle-\mathbb{E}\Big[\|\Upsilon^{\dagger}-\Upsilon\|\cdot\mathbb{E}\big[\big(\|Z^{\dagger}-Z\|\big)\mid Z^{\dagger}\big]\Big]+\mathbb{E}\big[\|\Upsilon-\Upsilon^{\dagger}\|\big]\cdot\mathbb{E}\big[\|Z-Z^{\dagger}\|\big]

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}\Big[\|\Upsilon-\Upsilon^{\dagger}\|\cdot\Big\{\|Z-Z^{\dagger}\|-\mathbb{E}\big[\big(\|Z-Z^{\dagger}\|\big)\mid Z\big]

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad-\mathbb{E}\big[\big(\|Z^{\dagger}-Z\|\big)\mid Z^{\dagger}\big]+\mathbb{E}\big[\|Z-Z^{\dagger}\|\big]\Big\}\Big]

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}\big[\|\Upsilon-\Upsilon^{\dagger}\|\cdot h(Z,Z^{\dagger})\big],

and the assertion, as claimed, is proved.
∎
