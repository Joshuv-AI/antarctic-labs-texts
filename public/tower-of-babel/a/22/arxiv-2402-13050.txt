On the singular planar Plateau problem
Marco Caroccia, Riccardo Scala

Abstract
. Given any Γ = γ ⁡ ( 𝕊 1 ) ⊂ ℝ 2 \Gamma=\gamma(\mathbb{S}^{1})\subset\mathbb{R}^{2} , image of a Lipschitz curve γ : 𝕊 1 → ℝ 2 \gamma:\mathbb{S}^{1}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^{2} , not necessarily injective, we provide an explicit formula for computing the value of 𝒜 ( γ ) := inf { ∫ B 1 ​ ( 0 ) | det ( ∇ u ) | d x | u = γ on 𝕊 1 } , \mathcal{A}(\gamma):=\inf\left\{\left.\int_{B_{1}(0)}|\det(\nabla u)|\mathrm{d}x\ \right|\ u=\gamma\text{ on }\mathbb{S}^{1}\right\}, where the infimum is computed among all Lipschitz maps u : B 1 ​ ( 0 ) → ℝ 2 u:B_{1}(0)\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^{2} having boundary datum γ \gamma . This coincides with the area of a minimal disk spanning Γ \Gamma , i.e., a solution of the Plateau problem of disk type. The novelty of the results relies in the fact that we do not assume the curve γ \gamma to be injective and our formula allows for countably many self-intersections.



1.
Introduction

In this paper, we consider the Plateau problem for singular curves, i.e., for curves
\gamma
whose image is not necessarily a Jordan curve, but might have self-intersections. Specifically, let us denote by
D:=\{x\in\mathbb{R}^{2}\ |\ |x|\leq 1\}
the unit disk centered at
0
in
\mathbb{R}^{2}
and by
\mathbb{S}^{1}:=\partial D
. We focus on planar singular curves, and we consider the following Plateau problem: given a Lipschitz curve
\gamma\in C^{0,1}(\mathbb{S}^{1};\mathbb{R}^{2})
, compute the value of the infimum

\mathcal{A}(\gamma)=\inf\left\{A(\psi)\ \left|\ \psi\in C^{0,1}(D;\mathbb{R}^{2})\text{ such that }\psi=\gamma\text{ on }\mathbb{S}^{1}\right.\right\}

(1.1)

with

A(\psi):=\int_{D}|\det(\nabla\psi)|\mathrm{d}x.

We do not focus on existence and regularity of solutions
\psi
of the singular Plateau problem (
1.1
) but our scope is to understand how the value of
\mathcal{A}(\gamma)
is related to the geometry of the curve
\gamma
, and in some cases how to compute it. Questions regarding the existence and regularity of solutions for this problem have been first investigated in
[
4
]
in 1991. More recently, following the analysis in
[
1
]
(see also
[
2
]
for a metric space setting), it is shown that a solution of the singular Plateau problem can be chosen in suitable Sobolev spaces.

When
\Gamma:=\gamma(\mathbb{S}^{1})
represents a Jordan curve then (
1.1
) corresponds to the classical Plateau problem, which consists into finding a map
\psi\in H^{1}(\text{{\rm int}}(D);\mathbb{R}^{3})\cap C^{0}(D;\mathbb{R}^{3})
(here
\mathrm{int}(D)
stands for the
interior
of
D
) minimizing the area functional

\displaystyle A(\psi)=\int_{D}|\partial_{x_{1}}\psi\wedge\partial_{x_{2}}\psi|dx.

(1.2)

among all maps whose restrictions on
\mathbb{S}^{1}
coincide, up to reparametrization, with
\gamma
.
We refer to
[
3
]
for a general treatment of the parametric approach to the aforementioned Plateau problem. Here we just recall some well-known facts that are useful for our discussion. For instance, it is well-known that if
\psi
is a minimizer of (
1.2
), for
\Gamma
being a Jordan curve, then it also turns out to be harmonic and conformal in the interior of
D
(cf.
[
3
]
).
In the special case where the Jordan curve is also planar, as in
\gamma:\mathbb{S}^{1}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^{2}
, the solution to the Plateau problem is provided by the Riemann map
\psi
, i.e., a bi-holomorphic bijection between
\text{{\rm int}}(D)
and
S
, the unique open bounded connected component of
\mathbb{R}^{2}\setminus\Gamma
(see Proposition
3.4
below). Since
\psi(D)=S
, it follows that

\mathcal{A}(\gamma)=|S|
. In very few other cases, it is possible to find the exact value of
A(\psi)
, when
\psi
is a solution to the Plateau problem. However, unless
\psi
is not explicit, or unless
\Gamma
has some special geometry,
A(\psi)
is not known. We are here able to provide two very general results (Theorem
1.1
,
1.2
) for computing the value of
\mathcal{A}(\gamma)
, enhancing the role of the geometry of
\gamma
. Our results apply to general Lipscthitz curve (with finite length) that might possibly have up to countably many self-intersections.

We start our analysis by focusing on curves
\gamma
with the following property:

(P)

Letting
\Gamma:=\gamma(\mathbb{S}^{1})
, the set
\mathbb{R}^{2}\setminus\Gamma
has finitely many connected components.

Under hypothesis (P), if
n
denotes the number of bounded connected components of
\mathbb{R}^{2}\setminus\Gamma
, then we fix
n
points
P_{i}
,
i=1,\dots,n
, each belonging to exactly one of these connected components denoted by
U_{i}
(see Figure
1
). We now consider
\gamma
as a loop in
\mathbb{R}^{2}\setminus\{P_{1},\dots,P_{n}\}
, with starting point
P:=\gamma((1,0))
. So there is a representative of
\gamma
in the homotopy group based at
P
,
F(n):=\pi_{1}(\mathbb{R}^{2}\setminus\{P_{1},\dots,P_{n}\})
. We denote this representative by
\gamma
itself. Here and in what follows, for the sake of clarity, we will always omit to specify the dependence of
\pi_{1}
from
P:=\gamma((0,1))
. Our first main result can then be summarized in the following theorem:

Theorem 1.1
.

Let
\gamma:\mathbb{S}^{1}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^{2}
be a Lipschitz curve satisfying Property (P), and let
n\geq 0
be the number of bounded connected components of
\mathbb{R}^{2}\setminus\Gamma
. Then there is a function
f=f^{\gamma}:F(n)\rightarrow\mathbb{N}^{n}
such that the following holds:

\displaystyle\mathcal{A}(\gamma)=\sum_{i=1}^{n}f_{i}(\gamma)\mathcal{L}^{2}(U_{i}),

(1.3)

where
U_{i}
is the
i
-th bounded connected components of
\mathbb{R}^{2}\setminus\Gamma
, and
\mathcal{L}^{2}(U_{i})
denotes its Lebesgue measure.

The function
f^{\gamma}
is explicit. The precise value of
\mathcal{A}(\gamma)
is detailed in Theorem
2.33
. We now provide a brief explanation on computing
f^{\gamma}
aside from the technical details discussed in Section
3
. Since
F(n)
is the free group on
n
elements, here
\gamma
is equivalent to a finite sequence of symbols in
\{\sigma_{1},\dots,\sigma_{n}\}\cup\{\sigma_{1}^{-1},\dots,\sigma_{n}^{-1}\}
, the generators of
F(n)
.
For each word
\eta
in
F(n)
, we associate it with specific
n
-tuples of natural numbers
(k_{1},\ldots,k_{n})\in\mathbb{N}^{n}
. This
n
-tuple is built by counting how many times each element
\sigma_{i}
or
\sigma_{i}^{-1}
must be erased from
\eta
to obtain the null word. For example the word
\sigma_{1}\sigma_{2}
is associated with the
n
-tuple
(1,1,0,\ldots,0)
. This is because the null word can be obtained by erasing one
\sigma_{1}
and one
\sigma_{2}
. The word
\sigma_{1}\sigma_{2}\sigma_{1}^{-1}
instead becomes the null word by erasing either a single
\sigma_{2}
, or by erasing one each of
\sigma_{1}
,
\sigma_{1}^{-1}
, and
\sigma_{2}
. Thus both the
n
-tuple
(2,1,0,\ldots,0)
and
(0,1,0,\ldots,0)
are associated to the word
\sigma_{1}\sigma_{2}\sigma_{1}^{-1}
. We gather all
n
-tuples related to any word representing
\gamma
into a set, denoted as
\mathrm{Ad}(\gamma)\subset\mathbb{N}^{n}
. Let us point out to the reader that the set
\mathrm{Ad}(\gamma)
, formally built in Subsection
2.4
, is created by considering
injections
rather than cancellations. An injection is the insertion of the inverse
\sigma_{i}^{-1}
in the word, to annihilate a
\sigma_{i}
. Actually, due to technical reasons, a slightly more general operation is needed to construct
\mathrm{Ad}(\gamma)
accurately. Once this set is built we show that we can compute the value
\mathcal{A}(\gamma)
as

\mathcal{A}(\gamma):=\min\left\{\left.\sum_{i=1}^{n}k_{i}|U_{i}|\ \right|\ (k_{1},\ldots,k_{n})\in\mathrm{Ad}(\gamma)\right\}

(1.4)

and this is the content of Section
3
, where Theorem
2.33
is proved. Thus
f^{\gamma}
is just the
n
-tuple achieving the minimum in (
1.4
). For instance, considering the curve in Figure
1
we have
\gamma=\sigma_{1}\sigma_{2}^{-1}\sigma_{3}
and thus the minimal way to obtain the null word is by erasing each single generator
\sigma_{1},\sigma_{2},\sigma_{3}
. Thus
(1,1,1)\in\mathrm{Ad}(\gamma)
and

\mathcal{A}(\gamma)=|U_{1}|+|U_{2}|+|U_{3}|.

In Figure
2
instead we have that
\gamma\equiv\sigma_{1}\sigma_{2}\sigma_{3}\sigma_{2}^{-1}
. Thus, by erasing
\sigma_{3}
from the word, we obtain
\gamma^{\prime}=\sigma_{1}\sigma_{2}\sigma_{2}^{-1}\equiv\sigma_{1}
, and then by erasing
\sigma_{1}
, we get the null word. Thus
(1,0,1)\in\mathrm{Ad}(\gamma)
and it is immediate to see that it is the optimum:

\mathcal{A}(\gamma)=|U_{1}|+|U_{3}|.

, such that

\pi_{1}
, we have

in

.

\mathbb{R}^{2}\setminus\Gamma
has

\gamma\equiv\sigma_{1}\sigma_{2}\sigma_{3}\sigma_{2}^{-1}
in
\pi_{1}(\mathbb{R}^{2}\setminus\{P_{1},P_{2},P_{3}\})
and thus
(1,0,1)\in\mathrm{Ad}(\gamma)
.

Note that annihilation is not mandatory for every pair
\sigma_{i},\sigma_{i}^{-1}
: consider a curve supported on
\Gamma
and splitting
\mathbb{R}^{2}\setminus\Gamma
into
2
connected components with
|U_{1}|>|U_{2}|
. Let
\gamma
be its parametrization, where
\gamma\equiv\sigma_{1}\sigma_{2}\sigma_{1}^{-1}\sigma_{2}^{-1}\in\pi_{1}(\mathbb{R}^{2}\setminus{P_{1},P_{2}})
(cf with Figure
3
). Then
(2,0)\in\mathrm{Ad}(\gamma)
as we can erase
\sigma_{1}
,
\sigma_{1}^{-1}
to obtain the null word:

\sigma_{1}\sigma_{2}\cancel{\sigma_{1}^{-1}}\sigma_{2}^{-1}=\sigma_{1}\sigma_{2}\sigma_{2}^{-1}\equiv\sigma_{1},\ \ \cancel{\sigma_{1}}=1.

However the minimum is achieved by
(0,2)
(which similarly belongs to
\mathrm{Ad}(\gamma)
) since
|U_{1}|>|U_{2}|
:

\mathcal{A}(\gamma)=2|U_{2}|.

A non-injective curve supported on a
\Gamma\subset\mathbb{R}^{2}\setminus\{P_{1},P_{2}\}
with a representing word
\gamma\equiv\sigma_{1}\sigma_{2}\sigma_{1}^{-1}\sigma_{2}^{-1}
. Here both
(0,2),(2,0)\in\mathrm{Ad}(\gamma)
but the optimum is given by
(0,2)
since
|U_{1}|>|U_{2}|
.

It is worth noting that
\mathcal{A}(\gamma)
depends not only on the homotopy class of
\gamma
in
F(n)
, but also on the areas
\mathcal{L}^{2}(U_{i})
; Specifically, two curves,
\gamma
and
\eta
which are homotopically equivalent, may have different functions
f^{\gamma}
and
f^{\eta}
.

The proof of Theorem
1.1
is obtained by separately proving the lower bound (in Subsection
3.1
) and the upper bound (in Subsection
3.2
). The lower bound is proven by observing that the set
\mathrm{Ad}(\gamma)
contains information about some common features shared by any
u:D\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^{2}
with
u\mathop{\hbox{\vrule height=7.0pt,width=0.5pt,depth=0.0pt\vrule height=0.5pt,width=6.0pt,depth=0.0pt}}\nolimits\mathbb{S}^{1}=\gamma
. In particular, we observe that if
z_{i}\in U_{i}
are regular points for such a
u
, then
\#(u^{-1}(z_{i}))\in\mathbb{N}
, and the
n
-tuple
(\#(u^{-1}(z_{1})),\ldots,\#(u^{-1}(z_{n})))\in\mathrm{Ad}(\gamma)
. Geometrically, for a fixed family of
\{z_{i}\in U_{i}\}_{i=1}^{n}
, this connection is intuitively justified by observing that the null word must be (homotopically) equivalent to
u\mathop{\hbox{\vrule height=7.0pt,width=0.5pt,depth=0.0pt\vrule height=0.5pt,width=6.0pt,depth=0.0pt}}\nolimits\partial\tilde{D}
where
\tilde{D}\subset D
is a small disk with
u^{-1}(z_{i})\cap\tilde{D}=\varnothing
for all
i=1,\ldots,N
. If we retract the disk
\mathbb{S}^{1}=\partial D
onto
\partial\tilde{D}
we must thus overcome all the points in
u^{-1}(z_{i})
, and each time we trepass a point in in
u^{-1}(z_{i})
, this results in a cancellation of
\sigma_{i}
or
\sigma_{i}^{-1}
in the word describing
\gamma
. This is the content of the crucial Lemma
3.1
establishing this connection. The area formula now allows to link the area of the
u
with its multiplicities in
U_{i}
and Lemma
3.1
links the multiplicities of
u
with
\gamma
.

The upper bound is essentially constructive and is proven by first observing that the area functional is subadditive with respect to concatenation of curves. Then we notice that a list
(k_{1},\ldots,k_{n})\in\mathrm{Ad}(\gamma)
allows us to express
\gamma
as a convenient concatenation of simple Jordan curves, for which we can compute the area with classical results.

After completing our analysis o curves possessing property (P) we observe that Theorem
1.1
extends to the case of general curves with possibly countably many self-intersections. In this case, the bounded connected components
U_{i}
of
\mathbb{R}^{2}\setminus\Gamma
are countably many, and so we can fix a sequence of points
P_{i}\in U_{i}
,
i\geq 1
. For all
n\geq 1
we consider
\gamma^{n}\in F(n)
, the representative of
\gamma
in
\pi_{1}(\mathbb{R}^{2}\setminus\{P_{1},\dots,P_{n}\})
; The value of
\mathcal{A}(\gamma)
is then obtained as a limit process.

Theorem 1.2
.

Let
\gamma:\mathbb{S}^{1}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^{2}
be any Lipschitz curve such that
\mathbb{R}^{2}\setminus\Gamma
consists of countable many bounded connected components
U_{i}
,
i\geq 1
. Then, for all
n\geq 1
there is a function
f^{n}:F(n)\rightarrow\mathbb{N}^{n}
such that

\displaystyle\mathcal{A}(\gamma)=\lim_{n\rightarrow\infty}\sum_{i=1}^{n}f^{n}_{i}(\gamma)\mathcal{L}^{2}(U_{i}).

(1.5)

In particular the limit in the right-hand side does not depend on the choice of the indexes for
P_{i}
, i.e., if
P_{\tau(i)}
is a reordering of the sequence
(P_{i})_{i\geq 1}
, then there are functions
g^{n}:F(n)\rightarrow\mathbb{N}^{n}
such that

\lim_{n\rightarrow\infty}\sum_{i=1}^{n}f^{n}_{i}(\gamma)\mathcal{L}^{2}(U_{i})=\lim_{n\rightarrow\infty}\sum_{i=1}^{n}g^{n}_{i}(\gamma)\mathcal{L}^{2}(U_{\tau(i)}).

Again, as
f^{n}
is explicit, also
\mathcal{A}(\gamma)
is explicitly determined (see Theorem
4.1
for details). The proof of Theorem
1.2
relies in approximating the curve
\gamma
with a smooth curve
\gamma_{\varepsilon}
satisfying (P) and for which we can invoke Theorem
1.1
.

, where we prove the main Theorem

. Finally in Section

2.
Preliminaries and main statement

We will denote by
D=\{x\in\mathbb{R}^{2}:|x|\leq 1\}=\overline{B}_{1}(0)\subset\mathbb{R}^{2}
the closed ball with radius
1
and center the origin. Thus

\mathbb{S}^{1}:=\{x\in\mathbb{R}^{2}:|x|=1\}=\partial D.

2.1.
Area-spanning equivalent curves

We here collect some result required to prove Theorem
2.33
and
4.1
.

Definition 2.1
.

Given the function
\psi:[-1,1]\rightarrow[0,1]
,
\psi(t)=1-|t|
, let
\Delta\subset\mathbb{R}^{2}
be defined as

\Delta:=\{(x_{1},x_{2})\in\mathbb{R}^{2}:-\psi(x_{1})<x_{2}<\psi(x_{1})\}.

We distinguish

\Delta^{\pm}:=\Delta\cap\{x_{2}\gtrless 0\}
.

Notice that
\Delta\subset D
, and that there is a bi-Lipschitz homeomorphism
1
1

1

This can be explicitely built, for instance setting
\Phi(0)=0
and
\Phi(x_{1},x_{2}):=\frac{|x_{1}|+|x_{2}|}{\sqrt{x_{1}^{2}+x_{2}^{2}}}(x_{1},x_{2})
.

\displaystyle\Phi:\overline{\Delta}\rightarrow D.

(2.1)

In particular, composing
\Phi
with any function
\phi:D\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^{2}
, we get a function
\phi\circ\Phi:\overline{\Delta}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^{2}
satisfying

\displaystyle A(\phi\circ\Phi;\Delta):=

\displaystyle\int_{\Delta}|\det(\nabla(\phi\circ\Phi))|dx=\int_{\Delta}|\det(\nabla\phi(\Phi(x)))||\det(\nabla\Phi(x))|dx

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\int_{D}|\det(\nabla\phi(y))|dy=A(\phi).

Hence we deduce
that

\displaystyle\mathcal{A}(\gamma)=\inf\{A(\psi;\Delta)\ |\ \psi\in C^{0,1}(\overline{\Delta};\mathbb{R}^{2})\text{ such that }\psi\circ\Phi^{-1}=\gamma\text{ on }\mathbb{S}^{1}\},

(2.2)

where

A(\psi;\Delta):=\int_{\Delta}|\det(\nabla\psi)|dx.

Similarly, using a bi-Lipschitz map
\Psi
between
D
and
\Delta^{+}
, it is easy to see that

\displaystyle\mathcal{A}(\gamma)=\inf\{A(\psi;\Delta^{+})\ |\ \psi\in C^{0,1}(\overline{\Delta}^{+};\mathbb{R}^{2})\text{ such that }\psi\circ\Psi^{-1}=\gamma\text{ on }\mathbb{S}^{1}\},

(2.3)

where

A(\psi;\Delta^{+}):=\int_{\Delta^{+}}|\det(\nabla\psi)|dx.

Let
\theta_{1},\theta_{2}\in\mathbb{S}^{1}
be two distinct points. We denote by
\widehat{\theta_{1}\theta_{2}}
the unique counterclockwisely oriented arc of
\mathbb{S}^{1}
with endpoints
\theta_{1}
and
\theta_{2}
, starting from
\theta_{1}
and ending at
\theta_{2}
. In particular,
\widehat{\theta_{1}\theta_{2}}\cup\widehat{\theta_{2}\theta_{1}}=\mathbb{S}^{1}
. We also denote by
-\widehat{\theta_{1}\theta_{2}}
the clockwisely oriented arc starting from
\theta_{1}
and ending at
\theta_{2}
; hence
-\widehat{\theta_{1}\theta_{2}}
coincides with
\widehat{\theta_{2}\theta_{1}}
, but has opposite orientation.

Definition 2.2
.

An oriented arc
c\subset\mathbb{S}^{1}
is an arc of the form
\widehat{\theta_{1}\theta_{2}}
or
-\widehat{\theta_{1}\theta_{2}}
.
Given two oriented arcs
c
and
d
we call
\gamma:c\rightarrow d
an
arcs homeomorphism
if
\gamma
is one to one, bi-Lipschitz, and preserves orientation (namely, it maps the starting point of
c
in the starting point of
d
).

Let
\sigma:\mathbb{S}^{1}\rightarrow\mathbb{S}^{1}
; By identifying the domain
\mathbb{S}^{1}
with the interval
[0,2\pi)
we can consider a lifting
\alpha
of
\sigma
, namely
\alpha:[0,2\pi)\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
,
\sigma(t)=e^{i\alpha(t)}
.

Definition 2.3
.

We say that
\sigma:\mathbb{S}^{1}\rightarrow\mathbb{S}^{1}
is a
weakly monotonic re-parametrization
of
\mathbb{S}^{1}
if the lifting
\alpha
is non-decreasing.

Given two curves
\gamma_{1},\gamma_{2}\in C^{0,1}(\mathbb{S}^{1};\mathbb{R}^{2})
,
we denote by
d_{F}(\gamma_{1},\gamma_{2})
the Frechet distance between them, defined as

\displaystyle d_{F}(\gamma_{1},\gamma_{2}):=\inf_{\sigma}\big(\max\{|\gamma_{1}(t)-\gamma_{2}\circ\sigma(t)|,\;t\in\mathbb{S}^{1}\}\big),

(2.4)

where the infimum is computed among all weakly monotonic re-parametrizations
\sigma:\mathbb{S}^{1}\rightarrow\mathbb{S}^{1}
. Of course, if there is a
\sigma
such that
\gamma_{1}=\gamma_{2}\circ\sigma
then
d_{F}(\gamma_{1},\gamma_{2})=0
. The converse is not always true. The following fact is well-known; we sketch the proof for the reader convenience.

Proposition 2.4
.

Let
\gamma_{n}:\mathbb{S}^{1}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^{2}
,
n\geq 1
, be a sequence of Lipschitz curves satisfying

\ell(\gamma_{n}):=\int_{\mathbb{S}^{1}}|\dot{\gamma}_{n}|dt\leq C,\qquad\forall n\geq 1,

(2.5)

for some positive constant
C
. Assume there is a Lipschitz curve
\gamma:\mathbb{S}^{1}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^{2}
such that

d_{F}(\gamma_{n},\gamma)\rightarrow 0\qquad\text{ as }n\rightarrow\infty.

Then
\displaystyle\lim_{n\rightarrow\infty}\mathcal{A}(\gamma_{n})=\mathcal{A}(\gamma)
.

Proof.

Let us fix
n\in\mathbb{N}
. By definition of Frechet distance, there is a weakly monotonic reparametrization
\sigma:\mathbb{S}^{1}\rightarrow\mathbb{S}^{1}
such that
\|\gamma_{n}\circ\sigma-\gamma\|_{L^{\infty}(\mathbb{S}^{1};\mathbb{R}^{2})}\leq d_{F}(\gamma_{n},\gamma)+\frac{1}{n}
. Denote also by
\alpha:[0,2\pi)\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
a continuous non-decreasing lifting of
\sigma
, namely
\sigma(t)=e^{i\alpha(t)}
.

Let
\varphi\in C^{0,1}(D;\mathbb{R}^{2})
be such that
\varphi\mathop{\hbox{\vrule height=7.0pt,width=0.5pt,depth=0.0pt\vrule height=0.5pt,width=6.0pt,depth=0.0pt}}\nolimits\mathbb{S}^{1}=\gamma
, and for
\delta\in(0,\frac{1}{2})
fixed let
\varphi_{\delta}(x):=\varphi\left(\frac{x}{1-2\delta}\right)
be its rescaling on
B_{1-2\delta}(0)
. We define
\psi\in C^{0,1}(D;\mathbb{R}^{2})
as

\displaystyle\psi(x)=\begin{cases}\varphi_{\delta}(x)&\text{if }|x|\leq 1-2\delta,\\
\frac{1-\delta-|x|}{\delta}\gamma(\frac{x}{|x|})+\frac{|x|+2\delta-1}{\delta}(\gamma_{n}\circ\sigma)(\frac{x}{|x|})&\text{if }1-2\delta<|x|\leq 1-\delta\\
\gamma_{n}\left(\exp\left(i\frac{1-|x|}{\delta}\alpha(t)+i\frac{|x|+\delta-1}{\delta}t\right)\right)&\text{if }1-\delta<|x|\leq 1,\end{cases}

(2.6)

where
t\in[0,2\pi)
is introduced in such a way that
\frac{x}{|x|}=e^{it}
.
Passing in polar coordinates, straightforward computations shows that there is a constant
C>0
independent of
n
so that

|\partial_{\rho}\psi|\leq C\delta^{-1}\|\gamma_{n}\circ\sigma-\gamma\|_{L^{\infty}},\qquad\ \ |\partial_{\theta}\psi|\leq C(|\dot{\gamma}|+|\dot{\gamma}_{n}|)

and so, by (
2.5
)

\int_{B_{1-\delta}(0)\setminus B_{1-2\delta}(0)}|\det(\nabla\psi)|dx\leq C\|\gamma_{n}\circ\sigma-\gamma\|_{L^{\infty}}\leq C\left(d_{F}(\gamma_{n},\gamma)+\frac{1}{n}\right).

Furthermore, since
\psi(D\setminus B_{1-\delta})\subseteq\gamma_{n}(\mathbb{S}^{1})
it is straightforward to check that

\int_{D\setminus B_{1-\delta}(0)}|\det(\nabla\psi)|dx=0.

Therefore, since
\psi\mathop{\hbox{\vrule height=7.0pt,width=0.5pt,depth=0.0pt\vrule height=0.5pt,width=6.0pt,depth=0.0pt}}\nolimits\mathbb{S}^{1}=\gamma_{n}
, we have

\mathcal{A}(\gamma_{n})\leq C\left(d_{F}(\gamma_{n},\gamma)+\frac{1}{n}\right)+\int_{B_{1-\delta}(0)}|\det\nabla\varphi_{\delta}|dx=C\left(d_{F}(\gamma_{n},\gamma)+\frac{1}{n}\right)+A(\varphi;D)

where we exploited

\int_{B_{1-\delta}(0)}|\det\nabla\varphi_{\delta}|dx=\int_{B_{1}(0)}|\det\nabla\varphi|dx=A(\varphi;D).

The arbitrariness of
\varphi
implies that
\mathcal{A}(\gamma_{n})\leq\mathcal{A}(\gamma)+o_{n}(1)
, where
o_{n}(1)\rightarrow 0
as
n\rightarrow+\infty
. By switching the role of
\gamma
and
\gamma_{n}
we infer also
\mathcal{A}(\gamma)\leq\mathcal{A}(\gamma_{n})+o^{\prime}_{n}(1)
, where
o^{\prime}_{n}(1)\rightarrow 0
as
n\rightarrow+\infty
. Hence, passing to the limit as
n\rightarrow+\infty
we get the thesis.
∎

From Proposition
2.4
one readily infers that
\mathcal{A}(\gamma)
is not sensible to negligible perturbation in Frechet distance. In particular, we get the following Corollaries.

Corollary 2.5
.

Let
\gamma:\mathbb{S}^{1}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^{2}
be a Lipschitz curve. Then, for all Lipschitz curves
\widehat{\gamma}:\mathbb{S}^{1}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^{2}
with
d_{F}(\widehat{\gamma},\gamma)=0
it holds
\mathcal{A}(\gamma)=\mathcal{A}(\widehat{\gamma})
.

Proof.

It is sufficient to choose
\gamma_{n}=\widehat{\gamma}
in Proposition
2.4
, for all
n\geq 0
.
∎

Corollary 2.6
.

Let
\gamma:\mathbb{S}^{1}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^{2}
be a Lipschitz curve, and let
\sigma:\mathbb{S}^{1}\rightarrow\mathbb{S}^{1}
be a bi-Lipschitz homeomorphism. Then
\mathcal{A}(\gamma)=\mathcal{A}(\gamma\circ\sigma)
.

Definition 2.7

(Concatenation of curves)
.

Let
\gamma_{1},\gamma_{2}\in C^{0,1}(\mathbb{S}^{1};\mathbb{R}^{2})
be two curves and assume that there is
\theta\in\mathbb{S}^{1}
such that
\gamma_{1}(\theta)=\gamma_{2}(\theta)
. We define the concatenation
\gamma_{1}\star\gamma_{2}
as follows: If
\theta=e^{i\alpha}
, we set

\gamma_{1}\star\gamma_{2}(t):=\begin{cases}\gamma_{1}(e^{i2(t-\alpha)})&\text{if }t\in[\alpha,\alpha+\pi)\\
\gamma_{2}(e^{i2(t-\pi-\alpha)})&\text{if }t\in[\alpha+\pi,\alpha+2\pi).\end{cases}

Similarly, if
\gamma_{1},\gamma_{2}\in C^{0,1}(\mathbb{S}^{1};\mathbb{R}^{2})
are curves such that there are
\theta_{1}=e^{i\alpha_{1}},\;\theta_{2}=e^{i\alpha_{2}}\in\mathbb{S}^{1}
such that
\gamma_{1}(\theta_{1})=\gamma_{2}(\theta_{2})
, we can define the concatenation
\gamma
of them simply as

\gamma:=\gamma_{1}\star\widehat{\gamma}_{2},

where
\widehat{\gamma}_{2}(t)=\gamma_{2}(e^{i(t-\alpha_{1}+\alpha_{2})})
, so that
\widehat{\gamma}_{2}(\theta_{1})=\gamma_{1}(\theta_{1})
. In this case, we still denote the concatenation by
\gamma_{1}\star\gamma_{2}
, when there is no risk of confusion.

Lemma 2.8
.

Let
\gamma_{1},\gamma_{2}\in C^{0,1}(\mathbb{S}^{1};\mathbb{R}^{2})
curves such that
\gamma_{1}(\theta_{1})=\gamma_{2}(\theta_{2})
, for some
\theta_{1},\theta_{2}\in\mathbb{S}^{1}
. Then
\gamma:=\gamma_{1}\star\gamma_{2}
is a closed Lipschitz curve satisfying

\mathcal{A}(\gamma)\leq\mathcal{A}(\gamma_{1})+\mathcal{A}(\gamma_{2}).

Proof.

Let
T
denote the triangle in
\mathbb{R}^{2}
with vertices the three points
(0,1)
,
(-2,-1)
,
(2,-1)
.
The map
\Phi_{T,\Delta}:\overline{T}\rightarrow\overline{\Delta}
defined as

\displaystyle\Phi_{T,\Delta}(x,y)=\begin{cases}(x_{1},x_{2})&\text{if }x_{2}\geq 0,\\
\left(x_{1}\frac{1+x_{2}}{1-x_{2}},x_{2}\right)&\text{if }x_{2}<0,\end{cases}

(2.7)

is Lipschitz continuous and one-to-one between the interior of
T
and
\Delta
. Further, the whole segment with vertices
(-2,-1)
and
(2,-1)
is mapped to
(-1,0)
. If
\Phi_{\Delta^{+},T}:\Delta^{+}\rightarrow T
is the homothety
(x,y)\mapsto(2x,2y-1)
then
\Phi_{\Delta^{+},\Delta}:=\Phi_{T,\Delta}\circ\Phi_{\Delta^{+},T}
is a Lipschitz map sending
\Delta^{+}
onto
\Delta
and the segment
[-1,1]\times\{0\}
to
(0,-1)
. Similarly we define
\Phi_{\Delta^{-},\Delta}:\Delta^{-}\rightarrow\Delta
.

We now assume (up composing
\gamma_{i}
with
\Phi
in (
2.1
)) that
\gamma_{i}:\partial\Delta\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^{2}
,
i=1,2
, and moreover, without loss of generality, we suppose that
\theta_{1}=(0,-1)
and
\theta_{2}=(0,1)
. If now
\varphi_{1}
and
\varphi_{2}
are Lipschitz maps such that
\varphi_{i}\mathop{\hbox{\vrule height=7.0pt,width=0.5pt,depth=0.0pt\vrule height=0.5pt,width=6.0pt,depth=0.0pt}}\nolimits\partial\Delta=\gamma_{i}
, we define

\displaystyle\varphi(x):=\begin{cases}\varphi_{1}\circ\Phi_{\Delta^{+},\Delta}(x)&\text{if }x\in\overline{\Delta^{+}},\\
\varphi_{2}\circ\Phi_{\Delta^{-},\Delta}(x)&\text{if }x\in\overline{\Delta^{-}},\end{cases}

(2.8)

and it turns out that
\varphi
is Lipschitz continuous on
\overline{\Delta}
, and its boundary datum is
\gamma=\gamma_{1}\star\gamma_{2}
.

Furthermore, we readily infer

\mathcal{A}(\gamma)\leq A(\varphi,\Delta)=A(\varphi_{1},\Delta)+A(\varphi_{2},\Delta),

so the thesis follows by arbitrariness of
\varphi_{i}
,
i=1,2
and (
2.2
).
∎

Definition 2.9

(Null curves)
.

Let
\gamma:\mathbb{S}^{1}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^{2}
be a Lipschitz curve. We say that
\gamma
is
1
-null
if there are two distinct points
\theta_{1},\theta_{2}\in\mathbb{S}^{1}
and an arcs homeomorphism
\sigma:\widehat{\theta_{1}\theta_{2}}\rightarrow-\widehat{\theta_{1}\theta_{2}}
such that

\gamma(t)=\gamma(\sigma(t))\qquad\forall t\in\widehat{\theta_{1}\theta_{2}}.

We say that a Lipschitz curve
\gamma:\mathbb{S}^{1}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^{2}
is
finitely null
if it is the concatenation of finitely many
1
-null curves.
Finally we say that a Lipschitz curve
\gamma:\mathbb{S}^{1}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^{2}
is
null
if it is the uniform limit of finitely null curves.

Lemma 2.10
.

Let
\gamma:\mathbb{S}^{1}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^{2}
be a Lipschitz null curve. Then
\mathcal{A}(\gamma)=0
.

Proof.

Step 1:
Assume in this step that
\gamma
is
1
-null. Hence we can consider Lipschitz homeomorphisms
\sigma^{\pm}:[-1,1]\rightarrow\pm\widehat{\theta_{1}\theta_{2}}
such that

\sigma^{\pm}(-1)=\theta_{1},\qquad\sigma^{\pm}(1)=\theta_{2},

and such that

\gamma\circ\sigma^{+}(t)=\gamma\circ\sigma^{-}(t)\qquad\text{for all }t\in[-1,1].

Indeed, once
\sigma^{+}
has been defined, if
\sigma
is the paramentrization as in Definition
2.9
, it is sufficient to take
\sigma^{-}:=\sigma\circ\sigma^{+}
.
We define
\phi:\Delta\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^{2}
as

\phi(x_{1},x_{2})=\gamma\circ\sigma^{+}(x_{1}),

which satisfies
\phi\mathop{\hbox{\vrule height=7.0pt,width=0.5pt,depth=0.0pt\vrule height=0.5pt,width=6.0pt,depth=0.0pt}}\nolimits\partial\Delta=\gamma\circ\widetilde{\sigma}
, where

\widetilde{\sigma}(x_{1},x_{2})=\begin{cases}\sigma^{+}(x_{1})&\text{ for }(x_{1},x_{2})\in D_{1}^{+}\\
\sigma^{-}(x_{1})&\text{ for }(x_{1},x_{2})\in D_{1}^{-}\\
\theta_{1}&\text{ for }(x_{1},x_{2})=(-1,0)\\
\theta_{2}&\text{ for }(x_{1},x_{2})=(1,0)\end{cases}

is a bi-Lipschitz homeomorphism between
\partial\Delta
and
\mathbb{S}^{1}
.
It follows that

\displaystyle D_{x_{1}}\phi(x_{1},x_{2})=(\gamma\circ\sigma^{+})^{\prime}(x_{1}),\qquad D_{x_{2}}\phi(x_{1},x_{2})=0,

(2.9)

so that
A(\phi;\Delta)=0
. As a consequence, if
\Phi
is the map in (
2.1
), it follows that
since
\phi\circ\Phi^{-1}=\gamma\circ\widetilde{\sigma}\circ\Phi^{-1}
on
\mathbb{S}^{1}
, we have
d_{F}(\phi\circ\Phi^{-1}\mathop{\hbox{\vrule height=7.0pt,width=0.5pt,depth=0.0pt\vrule height=0.5pt,width=6.0pt,depth=0.0pt}}\nolimits\mathbb{S}^{1},\gamma)=0
, and we infer
\mathcal{A}(\gamma)=0
from (
2.2
) and Corollary
2.5
.

Step 2:
Let
\gamma_{1}:\mathbb{S}^{1}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^{2}
be a Lipschitz curve such that
\mathcal{A}(\gamma_{1})=0
, and let
\gamma_{2}
be
1
-null and such that there exists
\theta\in\mathbb{S}^{1}
with
\gamma_{1}(\theta)=\gamma_{2}(\theta)
. Then
\gamma:=\gamma_{1}\star\gamma_{2}
satisfies
\mathcal{A}(\gamma)=0
by Lemma
2.8
.
This shows, by induction, that any finitely null curve
\gamma
enjoys
\mathcal{A}(\gamma)=0
.

Finally, let
\gamma
be a null curve and let
\gamma_{n}
be finitely null curves tending uniformly to
\gamma
. Since uniform convergence implies
d_{F}(\gamma_{n},\gamma)\rightarrow 0
, the thesis follows from Proposition
2.4
.
∎

Proposition 2.11
.

Let
\gamma\in C^{0,1}(\mathbb{S}^{1};\mathbb{R}^{2})
be a curve and let
\widehat{\gamma}\in C^{0,1}(\mathbb{S}^{1};\mathbb{R}^{2})
be a null curve such that
\gamma(\theta)=\widehat{\gamma}(\widehat{\theta})
. Then

\mathcal{A}(\gamma\star\widehat{\gamma})=\mathcal{A}(\gamma).

Proof.

Step 1:
Assume that
\widehat{\gamma}
is
1
-null as in Definition
2.9
, and that moreover
\theta_{1}=\widehat{\theta}
. Let
\widetilde{\sigma}:[-1,0]\rightarrow\widehat{\theta_{1}\theta_{2}}
be a bi-Lipschitz homeomorphism, and let us extend it on
[-1,1]
in order to have
\widetilde{\sigma}(t)=\widetilde{\sigma}(-t)
for all
t\in[0,1]
. Then define the Lipschitz map
\varphi^{-}:\Delta^{-}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^{2}
as

\varphi^{-}(x_{1},x_{2}):=\widehat{\gamma}\circ\widetilde{\sigma}(|x_{1}|+|x_{2}|)
; notice that
\varphi^{-}(x)=\widehat{\gamma}(\widehat{\theta})=\gamma(\theta)
for all
x\in\partial\Delta^{-}
with
x_{2}<0
. Thence

\nabla\varphi^{-}(x)=\left(\begin{array}[]{c }\frac{x_{1}}{|x_{1}|}(\widehat{\gamma}\circ\widetilde{\sigma})^{\prime}(|x_{1}|+|x_{2}|)\\
\frac{x_{2}}{|x_{2}|}(\widehat{\gamma}\circ\widetilde{\sigma})^{\prime}(|x_{1}|+|x_{2}|)\end{array}\right)

(2.10)

and thus, being the two rows of the matrix
\nabla\varphi^{-}(x)
linearly dependent,
|\det(\nabla\varphi^{-})|=0
.

Let
\varphi^{+}:\overline{\Delta}^{+}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^{2}
be any Lipschitz map satisfying
\varphi^{+}(x_{1},0)=\widehat{\gamma}\circ\widetilde{\sigma}(|x_{1}|)=\varphi^{-}(x_{1},0)
and
\varphi^{+}(x_{1},x_{2})=\gamma(s(x_{1}))
, where
s:[-1,1]\rightarrow\mathbb{S}^{1}
is any Lipschitz homomorphism satisfying
s(-1)=s(1)=\theta
. In this way the map

\varphi(x):=\begin{cases}\varphi^{+}(x)&\text{if }x\in\overline{\Delta}\cap\{x_{2}\geq 0\}\\
\varphi^{-}(x)&\text{if }x\in\overline{\Delta}\cap\{x_{2}<0\},\end{cases}

is Lipschitz from
\overline{\Delta}
to
\mathbb{R}^{2}
and
\varphi\mathop{\hbox{\vrule height=7.0pt,width=0.5pt,depth=0.0pt\vrule height=0.5pt,width=6.0pt,depth=0.0pt}}\nolimits\partial\Delta
is a reparametrization of
\gamma
.

Now,
\varphi^{+}
has, as boundary datum
\varphi^{+}\mathop{\hbox{\vrule height=7.0pt,width=0.5pt,depth=0.0pt\vrule height=0.5pt,width=6.0pt,depth=0.0pt}}\nolimits\partial\Delta^{+}
, a Lipschitz reparamentrization of
\gamma\star\widehat{\gamma}
. Thus

\displaystyle\mathcal{A}(\gamma)\leq A(\varphi;\Delta)=\int_{\Delta^{+}}|\det(\nabla\varphi^{+})|dx=A(\varphi^{+};\Delta^{+}).

(2.11)

So, by arbitrariness of
\varphi^{+}
and from (
2.3
) we infer
\mathcal{A}(\gamma)\leq\mathcal{A}(\gamma\star\widehat{\gamma})
.
The opposite inequality instead follows from Lemmas
2.8
and
2.10
. The thesis is achieved in the case that
\widehat{\gamma}
is
1
-null and such that
\theta_{1}=\widehat{\theta}
.

Step 2:
Let
\widehat{\gamma}
be arbitrary. Then from Lemma
2.8
we get

\mathcal{A}(\gamma\star\widehat{\gamma}\star\widehat{\gamma}^{-1})\leq\mathcal{A}(\gamma\star\widehat{\gamma})+\mathcal{A}(\widehat{\gamma}^{-1})=\mathcal{A}(\gamma\star\widehat{\gamma}),

where the equality follows from Lemma
2.10
. Now,
\widehat{\gamma}\star\widehat{\gamma}^{-1}
is
1
-null and satisfies the hypothesis of Step 1, so from the previous inequality we get
\mathcal{A}(\gamma)\leq\mathcal{A}(\gamma\star\widehat{\gamma})
. We then conclude the opposite inquality as in Step 1.
∎

2.2.
Classes of curves

Let
\gamma:\partial D\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^{2}
be a Lipschitz parametrization of a curve whose image we denote by

\Gamma:=\gamma(\mathbb{S}^{1}).

We do not require that
\gamma
is injective, so that
\Gamma
might have self intersections. In this section we will make the assumption that
\mathbb{R}^{2}\setminus\Gamma
is made by finitely many connected components
\{U_{i}\}_{i=1}^{n}
, namely Property (P) holds.

We denote by
U_{0}
the unique unbounded connected component of
\mathbb{R}^{2}\setminus\Gamma
. Moreover, every
U_{i}
with
i\geq 1
is bounded and simply-connected. Since
\Gamma
is closed and
U_{i}
is a connected component of
\mathbb{R}^{2}\setminus\Gamma
, we infer that
\partial U_{i}\subseteq\Gamma
, for all
i
. In particular
\mathcal{H}^{1}(\partial U_{i})<\infty
.

For all
i=1,\ldots,n
we select
P_{i}\in U_{i}
so that
\{P_{1},\dots,P_{n}\}
is a set of
n
distinct points in
\mathbb{R}^{2}
. Further, for all
i=1,\ldots,n
, we choose closed balls
\overline{B}_{r}(P_{i})\subset U_{i}
in such a way that they are mutually disjoint. Let
P
be a point in
\mathbb{R}^{2}\setminus(\cup_{i}\overline{B}_{r}(P_{i}))
.

Definition 2.12
.

We say that a curve
\alpha:\mathbb{S}^{1}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^{2}\setminus\{P_{1},\dots,P_{n}\}
is based at
P
if
\alpha((0,1))=P
.

Two Lipschitz curves
\alpha,\beta:\mathbb{S}^{1}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^{2}\setminus\{P_{1},\dots,P_{n}\}
based at
P
are said to be homotopically equivalent if there is a Lipschitz homotopy
\Phi_{\alpha,\beta}:[0,1]\times\mathbb{S}^{1}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^{2}\setminus\{P_{1},\dots,P_{n}\}
such that
\Phi_{\alpha,\beta}(0,\cdot)=\alpha
,
\Phi_{\alpha,\beta}(1,\cdot)=\beta
, and
\Phi_{\alpha,\beta}(\cdot,(0,1))\equiv P
. In this case we write

\displaystyle\alpha\equiv\beta.

(2.12)

We introduce the concept of winding number of a curve, which will be useful in the sequel.

Definition 2.13
.

Let
U
be a bounded simply connected open set with Lipschitz boundary and let
\gamma:\partial U\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^{2}
be a Lipschitz curve, and
\Gamma:=\gamma(\partial U)
. For all
z\in\mathbb{R}^{2}\setminus\Gamma
we introduce the winding number
\text{\rm link}(\gamma,z)\in\mathbb{Z}
of
\gamma
around
z=(z_{1},z_{2})
, defined by

\displaystyle\text{\rm link}(\gamma,z):=\frac{1}{2\pi}\int_{\partial U}\left(\frac{\gamma_{1}(s)-z_{1}}{|\gamma(s)-z|^{2}}\dot{\gamma}_{2}(s)-\frac{\gamma_{2}(s)-z_{2}}{|\gamma(s)-z|^{2}}\dot{\gamma}_{1}(s)\right)ds.

(2.13)

Often, we will consider the winding number of curves defined on
\mathbb{S}^{1}
, i.e. with
U=D
in the previous definition.

Definition 2.14
.

For all
i=1,\dots,n
we select a Lipschitz curve
\widetilde{\sigma}_{i}:\mathbb{S}^{1}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^{2}
starting from
P
and reaching a point in
\partial B_{r}(P_{i})
; then we define
\sigma_{i}:=\widetilde{\sigma}_{i}\star\widehat{\sigma}_{i}\star\widetilde{\sigma}_{i}^{-1}
, where
\widehat{\sigma}_{i}
is the costant speed parametrization of
\partial B_{r}(P_{i})
in counterclockwise order. The curve
\sigma_{i}:\mathbb{S}^{1}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^{2}
will be a Lipschitz closed curve based at
P
whose winding number
\textrm{link}(\sigma_{i},P_{j})
around the point
P_{j}
is

\mathrm{link}(\sigma_{i},P_{j})=\delta_{ij},\qquad\qquad\forall j\in\{1,\dots,n\}.

Moreover, we assume that
\sigma_{i}((0,1))=P
for all
i=1,\dots,n
(we refer to Figures
1
and
2
for a depiction of our notation and the complete scenario).

The homotopy group of
\mathbb{R}^{2}\setminus\{P_{1},\dots,P_{n}\}
based at
P
is the free group
F(n)
on
n
elements. A basis for
\pi_{1}(\mathbb{R}^{2}\setminus\{P_{1},\dots,P_{n}\})
is given by the family
\{\sigma_{1},\dots,\sigma_{n}\}
(we recall that we always mean that the
\pi_{1}
is meant to be based at
P
even if is such dependence is not explicit). An element
\eta
of
F(n)
is a string
\eta=(\eta_{1},\dots,\eta_{m})
where every
\eta_{i}
represents an element of the basis (or one of its inverse) and identifies the curve
\eta=\eta_{1}\star\ldots\star\eta_{m}
.
To shortcut the notation, we will denote by

\displaystyle\Sigma(n):=\{\sigma_{1},\dots,\sigma_{n},\sigma_{1}^{-1},\dots,\sigma_{n}^{-1}\}.

(2.14)

We do not specify the dependence of
\Sigma
on
\gamma
since it will always be clear from the context. Moreover, if
\eta\in\pi_{1}(\mathbb{R}^{2}\setminus\{P_{1},\dots,P_{n}\})
we will denote
(\eta_{1},\dots,\eta_{m})
also by
\eta_{1}\dots\eta_{m}
,
\eta_{i}\in\Sigma(n)
.

Given a curve
\gamma\in C^{0,1}(\mathbb{S}^{1};\mathbb{R}^{2}\setminus\{P_{1},\dots,P_{n}\})
based at
P
, it belongs to a unique class in
\pi_{1}(\mathbb{R}^{2}\setminus\{P_{1},\dots,P_{n}\})
.

Definition 2.15
.

Let
\gamma,\widehat{\gamma}\in\pi_{1}(\mathbb{R}^{2}\setminus\{P_{1},\dots,P_{n}\})
be curves based at
P
. By definition, they belong to the same class in
\pi_{1}(\mathbb{R}^{2}\setminus\{P_{1},\dots,P_{n}\})
if and only if
\gamma\equiv\widehat{\gamma}
, where
\equiv
is the relation (
2.12
) of Definition
2.13
.

Notice that if
\gamma\equiv\widehat{\gamma}
and
\gamma_{1}\dots\gamma_{m}
and
\widehat{\gamma}_{1}\dots\widehat{\gamma}_{\widehat{m}}
are representatives of
\gamma
and
\widehat{\gamma}
in
F(n)
, then obviously
\gamma_{1}\dots\gamma_{m}=\widehat{\gamma}_{1}\dots\widehat{\gamma}_{\widehat{m}}
in
F(n)
.

Given two Lipschitz curves
\alpha,\beta:\mathbb{S}^{1}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^{2}\setminus\{P_{1},\dots,P_{n}\}
based at
P
, we can consider their concatenation
\alpha\star\beta
, according to Definition
2.7
where
\theta=P
.
In such a case, denoting by
\alpha
and
\beta
themselves their representatives in
F(n)
, then the
\alpha\beta
is a representative of
\alpha\star\beta
.

Definition 2.16

(Generic representative)
.

Let
\alpha:\mathbb{S}^{1}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^{2}\setminus\{P_{1},\dots,P_{n}\}
be a Lipschitz curve based at
P
; if
\gamma_{1}\dots\gamma_{k}
is a representative of
\alpha
, we call the representation
\gamma_{1}\dots\gamma_{k}
of
\alpha
a
word
representing
\alpha
. The identity in
F(n)
is called the
null word
. We also call a
generic representative
of
\alpha
any word of the form
\gamma_{1+a}\dots\gamma_{k+a}
for all
a\in\mathbb{Z}
(where
i+a
is considered
\textrm{mod}(k)
), obtained from a representative
\gamma_{1}\dots\gamma_{k}
of
\alpha
. The family of generic representative of
\alpha
is noted as

\text{{\rm\textlbrackdbl}}{\alpha}\text{{\rm\textrbrackdbl}}:=\{\gamma=\gamma_{1}\dots\gamma_{k}:\gamma\text{ is a generic representative of }\alpha\}.

Remark 2.17
.

Notice that if
\gamma\in\text{{\rm\textlbrackdbl}}{\alpha}\text{{\rm\textrbrackdbl}}
, we also have
\overline{\gamma}\gamma\overline{\gamma}^{-1}\in\text{{\rm\textlbrackdbl}}{\alpha}\text{{\rm\textrbrackdbl}}
for any
\overline{\gamma}\in\pi_{1}(\mathbb{R}^{2}\setminus\{P_{1},\dots,P_{n}\})
; this follows easily by definition of generic representative. For this reason the notion of generic representative of
\gamma
coincides with the conjugate class of
\gamma
in
\pi_{1}(\mathbb{R}^{2}\setminus\{P_{1},\dots,P_{n}\})
.

Assume now
\alpha:\mathbb{S}^{1}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^{2}\setminus\{P_{1},\dots,P_{n}\}
is not based at
P
. Let
\widehat{\beta}:[0,1]\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^{2}\setminus\{P_{1},\dots,P_{n}\}
be a Lipschitz curve so that
\widehat{\beta}(1)=\alpha((1,0))
, and
\widehat{\beta}(0)=P
. Up to reparamentrization on
\mathbb{S}^{1}
, the curve
\widehat{\alpha}:=\widehat{\beta}^{-1}\star\widehat{\beta}
is a
1
-null curve, and

\widehat{\beta}\star\alpha\star\widehat{\beta}^{-1}

is based at
P
. We claim that the class
\text{{\rm\textlbrackdbl}}{\widehat{\beta}\star\alpha\star\widehat{\beta}^{-1}}\text{{\rm\textrbrackdbl}}
does not depend on the choice of
\widehat{\beta}
. Indeed, if
\widetilde{\beta}
is another Lipschitz curve such that
\widetilde{\beta}(1)=\alpha((1,0))
,
\widetilde{\beta}(0)=P
then, setting
\eta:=\widetilde{\beta}\star\widehat{\beta}^{-1}
, we have

\widetilde{\beta}\star\alpha\star\widetilde{\beta}^{-1}\equiv\widetilde{\beta}\star\widehat{\alpha}\star\alpha\star\widehat{\alpha}^{-1}\star\widetilde{\beta}^{-1}\equiv\eta\star\widehat{\beta}\star\alpha\star\widehat{\beta}^{-1}\star\eta^{-1}\equiv\widehat{\beta}\star\alpha\star\widehat{\beta}^{-1}

where we used Remark
2.17
to infer the last equivalence.
Thanks to this fact the following definition is well-posed.

Definition 2.18
.

Let
\alpha:\mathbb{S}^{1}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^{2}\setminus\{P_{1},\dots,P_{n}\}
be a Lipschitz curve; we define the
generic class of
\alpha
(or
conjugate class
), denoted by
\text{{\rm\textlbrackdbl}}{\alpha}\text{{\rm\textrbrackdbl}}
, as the class of generic representatives of
\beta\star\alpha\star\beta^{-1}
, where
\beta:[0,1]\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^{2}\setminus\{P_{1},\dots,P_{n}\}
is any
1
-null Lipschitz curve such that
\beta(1)=\alpha((1,0))
and
\beta(0)=P
.

Remark 2.19
.

Notice that, from the discussion preceeding Definition
2.18

In order to introduce and prove our first main result we define the following operation that we call

2.33
to any Lipschitz curve
\gamma:\mathbb{S}^{1}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^{2}
.

For such a general Lipschitz curve, setting as usual
\Gamma:=\gamma(\mathbb{S}^{1})
, we know that
\mathbb{R}^{2}\setminus\Gamma
consists of at most countable many open connected components. Let us call
(U_{i})_{i\geq 0}
these connected components, which are bounded (and simply connected) unless
i=0
, corresponding to
U_{0}
, the unique unbounded connected component. For all
i\geq 1
we pick a point
P_{i}\in U_{i}
.

For all
N>0
we consider the homotopy group
\pi_{1}(\mathbb{R}^{2}\setminus\{P_{1},\dots,P_{N}\})=F(N)
; the curve
\gamma
has an indecomposable representation in
F(N)
, for all
N>0
, that we denote by
\gamma_{(N)}
.
We denote by

\displaystyle\mathcal{A}^{N}(\gamma):=\min\left\{\left.\sum_{i=1}^{N}k_{i}|U_{i}|\ \ \right|\ \ (k_{1},\ldots,k_{N})\in\mathrm{Ad}\left(\gamma_{(N)}\right)\right\}.

(4.1)

The following theorem then holds:

Theorem 4.1
.

Let
\gamma:\mathbb{S}^{1}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^{2}
be a Lipschitz curve, let
\tau:\mathbb{N}\rightarrow\mathbb{N}
be a bijection, and let
\gamma_{(N)}^{\tau}\in F(N)
represent
\gamma
in
\pi_{1}(\mathbb{R}^{2}\setminus\{P_{\tau(1)},\dots,P_{\tau(N)}\})
. Then

\lim_{N\rightarrow\infty}\mathcal{A}^{N}(\gamma)=\lim_{N\rightarrow\infty}\left(\min\left\{\left.\sum_{i=1}^{N}k_{\tau(i)}|U_{\tau(i)}|\ \ \right|\ \ \begin{array}[]{c}(k_{1},\ldots,k_{N})\in\mathrm{Ad}\left(\gamma_{(N)}^{\tau}\right)\end{array}\right\}\right),

(4.2)

and moreover

\displaystyle\mathcal{A}(\gamma)=\lim_{N\rightarrow\infty}\mathcal{A}^{N}(\gamma).

(4.3)

Before proving this, we anticipate the following approximation Lemma:

Lemma 4.2
.

Let
\gamma:\mathbb{S}^{1}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^{2}
be a Lipschitz curve; then for all
\varepsilon>0
there exists a smooth curve
\gamma_{\varepsilon}:\mathbb{S}^{1}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^{2}
such that, noting
\Gamma_{\varepsilon}:=\gamma_{\varepsilon}(\mathbb{S}^{1})
, one has

(a)

\mathbb{R}^{2}\setminus\Gamma_{\varepsilon}
consists of finitely many connected components;

(b)

\|\gamma_{\varepsilon}-\gamma\|_{L^{\infty}(\mathbb{S}^{1};\mathbb{R}^{2})}\leq\varepsilon
;

(c)

\int_{\mathbb{S}^{1}}|\dot{\gamma}_{\varepsilon}(t)|dt\leq\int_{\mathbb{S}^{1}}|\dot{\gamma}(t)|dt+\varepsilon
.

Proof.

By mollification, for all
\varepsilon>0
we can find a smooth curve
\eta:\mathbb{S}^{1}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^{2}
so that

\|\eta-\gamma\|_{L^{\infty}(\mathbb{S}^{1};\mathbb{R}^{2})}\leq\varepsilon/2\qquad\text{and }\qquad\int_{\mathbb{S}^{1}}|\dot{\eta}(t)|dt\leq\int_{\mathbb{S}^{1}}|\dot{\gamma}(t)|dt+\varepsilon/2.

For all
t,s\in[0,2\pi)
, with
t\neq s
, we define (we identify
\mathbb{S}^{1}
with
[0,2\pi)
)

\delta_{j}(t,s)=\frac{\eta_{j}(t)-\eta_{j}(s)}{s-t},\qquad j=1,2.

Since
\eta
is smooth, it turns out that
\delta_{j}
extends to the set
\{t=s\}
and
\nabla\delta_{j}\in L^{\infty}([0,2\pi)\times[0,2\pi),\mathbb{R}^{2})
; in particular

\int_{[0,2\pi)^{2}}|\det\nabla\delta(t,s)|dtds
is finite and by area formula
\text{{\rm mul}}(\delta,[0,2\pi)^{2},\cdot)\in L^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{2})
. This implies that
\text{{\rm mul}}(\delta,[0,2\pi)^{2},\lambda)
is finite for a.e.
\lambda\in\mathbb{R}^{2}
. We can then select
\lambda\in\mathbb{R}^{2}\setminus\{0\}
arbitrarily close to
0
in such a way that
\delta^{-1}(\lambda)
is finite; specifically, we can choose
|\lambda|<\varepsilon/16\pi
so that, defining

\zeta(t):=\eta(t)+\lambda t,
for
t\in[0,2\pi),

it turns out

\|\zeta-\eta\|_{L^{\infty}(\mathbb{S}^{1};\mathbb{R}^{2})}\leq\varepsilon/4\qquad\text{and }\qquad\int_{\mathbb{S}^{1}}|\dot{\zeta}(t)|dt\leq\int_{\mathbb{S}^{1}}|\dot{\eta}(t)|dt+\varepsilon/4.

Let us now show that the number of self-intersections of
\zeta
is finite. Indeed, assume that
\zeta(t)=\zeta(s)
, then by definition it follows
\eta(t)-\eta(s)=\lambda(s-t)
. Hence
\delta(t,s)=\lambda
and
(t,s)\in\delta^{-1}(\lambda)
. Therefore the number of couples
(t,s)
for which
\zeta(t)=\zeta(s)
is finite.

Eventually, we design a path
\widetilde{\zeta}
from
\zeta(2\pi)
to
\zeta(0)
in such a way that its length is smaller than
\varepsilon/4
, it intersects the image of
\zeta
at most finitely many times, and
\gamma_{\varepsilon}:=\zeta\star\widetilde{\zeta}
is smooth and satisfies (b) and (c).

It remains to show that
\mathbb{R}^{2}\setminus\Gamma_{\varepsilon}
decomposes in finitely many connected components. We do that by induction on the number

N_{\varepsilon}:=\#\{(t,s)\in[0,2\pi)^{2}:t<s,\;\gamma_{\varepsilon}(t)=\gamma_{\varepsilon}(s)\}.

If
N_{\varepsilon}=0
then
\gamma_{\varepsilon}
parametrizes a Jordan curve and
\mathbb{R}^{2}\setminus\Gamma_{\varepsilon}
consists of two connected components.
Assume the thesis is true for
N_{\varepsilon}\leq N
, for some
N\in\mathbb{N}
, and consider the case
N_{\varepsilon}=N+1
. Take a couple
(t,s)
such that
s-t
is minimal among all such couples, and so the curve
\gamma_{\varepsilon}\mathop{\hbox{\vrule height=7.0pt,width=0.5pt,depth=0.0pt\vrule height=0.5pt,width=6.0pt,depth=0.0pt}}\nolimits[t,s)
is injective. The curve
\gamma_{\varepsilon}\mathop{\hbox{\vrule height=7.0pt,width=0.5pt,depth=0.0pt\vrule height=0.5pt,width=6.0pt,depth=0.0pt}}\nolimits[0,t)\star\gamma_{\varepsilon}\mathop{\hbox{\vrule height=7.0pt,width=0.5pt,depth=0.0pt\vrule height=0.5pt,width=6.0pt,depth=0.0pt}}\nolimits[s,2\pi)
is a closed (Lipschitz) loop whose corresponding number
N_{\varepsilon}
is smaller than
N+1
, so its image
\widehat{\Gamma}_{\varepsilon}
divides
\mathbb{R}^{2}
in finitely many connected components. Now, by hypothesis on
\gamma_{\varepsilon}
, the set

\{r\in[t,s):\gamma_{\varepsilon}(r)\in\widehat{\Gamma}_{\varepsilon}\}=\{r_{1},r_{2},\dots,r_{M_{\varepsilon}}\}

is finite. So, assuming
r_{i}<r_{i+1}
,
\forall i=1,\dots,M_{\varepsilon}-1
in the preceding expression,
every curve
\gamma_{\varepsilon}\mathop{\hbox{\vrule height=7.0pt,width=0.5pt,depth=0.0pt\vrule height=0.5pt,width=6.0pt,depth=0.0pt}}\nolimits[r_{i},r_{i+1})
is contained in a given connected component of
\mathbb{R}^{2}\setminus\widehat{\Gamma}_{\varepsilon}
, and divides it into two components. Since this is true for every
i=1,\dots,M_{\varepsilon}-1
, the number of connected components of
\mathbb{R}^{2}\setminus\Gamma_{\varepsilon}
remains bounded, and item (a) is achieved.
∎

We also need the following

Proposition 4.3
.

Let
\eta:\mathbb{S}^{1}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^{2}
a Lipschitz curve satisfying Property (P); let
V_{1},\dots,V_{N}
be the bounded connected components of
\mathbb{R}^{2}\setminus\eta(\mathbb{S}^{1})
, and let
\mathcal{R}
be a finite family of points in
\mathbb{R}^{2}\setminus\eta(\mathbb{S}^{1})
such that for all
j=1,\dots,N

\{X_{ij}\in\mathcal{R}\}=\mathcal{R}\cap V_{j}\neq\varnothing.

Let
\eta^{\mathcal{R}}
be a word representing
\eta
in
\pi_{1}(\mathbb{R}^{2}\setminus\mathcal{R})
, and assume that
(\dots,\widehat{k}_{ij},\dots)\in\mathbb{N}^{\#(\mathcal{R})}

is a string in
\text{{\rm Ad}}(\eta^{\mathcal{R}})
. Then
(k^{\prime}_{1},\dots,k^{\prime}_{N})\in\text{{\rm Ad}}(\eta)
, where

k^{\prime}_{j}:=\min\{\widehat{k}_{ij}:X_{ij}\in\mathcal{R}\cap V_{j}\}.

Proof.

For all
j=1,\dots,N
, let
R_{j}:=R_{i_{j}j}\in\mathcal{R}\cap V_{j}
be any point (for some index
i_{j}
), and denote by
\overline{\mathcal{R}}:=\{R_{1},\dots,R_{N}\}\subset\mathcal{R}
. Since there is trivially an isomorphism between
\pi_{1}(\mathbb{R}^{2}\setminus\overline{\mathcal{R}})
and
\pi_{1}(\mathbb{R}^{2}\setminus\{P_{1},\dots,P_{N}\})
we can identify the two groups (and in particular the generators) to be both
F(N)
. Let
\pi^{\mathcal{R}}:\pi_{1}(\mathbb{R}^{2}\setminus{\mathcal{R}})\rightarrow\pi_{1}(\mathbb{R}^{2}\setminus\overline{\mathcal{R}})
be the application that associates to a word
w
of
F(\#(\mathcal{R}))
the word
\pi^{\mathcal{R}}(w)\in F(N)
obtained from
w
by erasing all the symbols which belongs to the generators of
F(\#(\mathcal{R}))
but not on that of
F(N)
. The application is a morphism since clearly
\pi^{\mathcal{R}}(\omega\tau)=\pi^{\mathcal{R}}(\omega)\pi^{\mathcal{R}}(\tau)
.

Thus, assuming
(\dots,\widehat{k}_{ij},\dots)\in\text{{\rm Ad}}(\eta^{\mathcal{R}})
, we get a sequence of injections
\beta_{r}\sigma_{r}\beta_{r}^{-1}
such that

\eta\beta_{1}\alpha_{1}\beta_{1}^{-1}\beta_{2}\alpha_{2}\beta_{2}\dots\beta_{H}\alpha_{H}\beta_{H}=1,\ \ \ H=\sum_{i,j}k_{ij},\ \ \alpha_{i}\in\Sigma(\#(\mathcal{R})).

But since
\pi^{\mathcal{R}}(1)=1
, we also have

\overline{\eta}\overline{\beta}_{1}\overline{\alpha}_{1}\overline{\beta}_{1}^{-1}\overline{\beta}_{2}\overline{\alpha}_{2}\overline{\beta}_{2}^{-1}\dots\overline{\beta}_{H}\overline{\alpha}_{H}\overline{\beta}_{H}^{-1}=1,

where, to shortcut the notation we have denoted
\overline{\zeta}:=\pi^{\mathcal{R}}(\zeta)
. On the other hand the injections
\overline{\beta}_{r}\overline{\alpha}_{r}\overline{\beta}_{r}^{-1}
are null if
\alpha_{r}
is a generator of
F(\#(\mathcal{R}))
but not of
F(N)
, and coincides with
\alpha_{r}
otherwise. This means that

(\dots,k_{j},\dots)\in\text{{\rm Ad}}(\eta),

where
k_{j}:=k_{i_{j}j}
. In particular the thesis follows if we choose
i_{j}
so that
k_{i_{j}j}=\min\{k_{ij}:X_{ij}\in\mathcal{R}\cap V_{j}\}
.
∎

The last ingredient needed to prove Theorem
4.1
is the following technical Lemma.

Lemma 4.4
.

Let
\nu
,
\zeta
and
\Psi
be as in Corollary
2.37
. Assume also that the
\theta
is chosen in a way that the map
\Psi(\cdot,\theta):[0,1]\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^{2}
is bi-Lipstchitz and injective. Then if
P_{i}\in E_{\Psi}
for all
i=1,\ldots,N
it holds that

(\mathrm{mul}(\Psi,A,P_{1})+k_{1},\dots,\mathrm{mul}(\Psi,A,P_{n})+k_{n})\in\textrm{Ad}(\zeta),

whenever
(k_{1},\dots,k_{n})\in\textrm{Ad}(\nu)
.

Proof.

Let us denote by
A:=[0,1]\times\mathbb{S}^{1}
the domain of
\Psi
, and let
\eta:=\nu^{-1}\star\Psi(\cdot,\theta)\star\zeta\star\Psi(\cdot,\theta)^{-1}
be the curve given by Corollary
2.37
. Now since the map
\Psi(\cdot,\theta)
is bi-Lipscthitz and injective we can find a diffeomorfism
\Phi:D\rightarrow([0,1]\times\mathbb{S}^{1})\setminus([0,1]\times\{\theta\})
(see Figure
7
) such that

\Phi(\mathbb{S}^{1})=\Phi(\partial D)=(\{0\}\times\mathbb{S}^{1})\cup(\{1\}\times\mathbb{S}^{1})\cup([0,1]\times\{\theta\}).

Figure 7.
A depiction of the diffeomorfism used to prove Lemma
4.4
: we can build
\Phi
so that
\Psi\circ\Phi\mathop{\hbox{\vrule height=7.0pt,width=0.5pt,depth=0.0pt\vrule height=0.5pt,width=6.0pt,depth=0.0pt}}\nolimits\mathbb{S}^{1}=\eta
in order to apply Lemma
3.1
to
\Psi\circ\Phi
. here we are representing
[0,1]\times\mathbb{S}^{1}
as a subset in
\mathbb{R}^{3}
.

Observe now that we can build
\Phi
so that
\Psi\circ\Phi:\mathbb{S}^{1}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^{2}
satisfies also
\Psi\circ\Phi(\mathbb{S}^{1})=\eta
. Now since
\Phi
is a diffeomorphism we have
\mathrm{mul}(\Psi\circ\Phi,D,z)=\mathrm{mul}(\Psi,A,z)
and, by Lemma
3.1
we can infer that
(\mathrm{mul}(\Psi\circ\Phi,D,P_{1}),\ldots,\mathrm{mul}(\Psi\circ\Phi,D,P_{n}))\in\mathrm{Ad}(\eta)
. Then, by (
2.37
)

(\mathrm{mul}(\Psi,A,P_{1})+k_{1},\dots,\mathrm{mul}(\Psi,A,P_{n})+k_{n})\in\textrm{Ad}(\zeta),

whenever
(k_{1},\dots,k_{n})\in\textrm{Ad}(\nu)
.
∎

We are now ready to prove Theorem
4.1
.

Proof of Theorem
4.1
.

Step 1:

On the one hand Corollary
3.3
implies that, for all
N\geq 1
it holds

\mathcal{A}(\gamma)\geq\mathcal{A}^{N}(\gamma).

We now show that for all
N\geq 1
we have
\mathcal{A}^{N+1}(\gamma)\geq\mathcal{A}^{N}(\gamma)
, so that existence of the limit in (
4.3
) is granted, and the previous inequality entails

\mathcal{A}(\gamma)\geq\lim_{N\rightarrow\infty}\mathcal{A}^{N}(\gamma).

Given a word
\eta
in
F(N+1)
, we denote by
\Pi_{N}(\eta)
the word in
F(N)
obtained by erasing all the symbols
\sigma_{N+1}
and
\sigma_{N+1}^{-1}
from
\eta
.

Now it can be easily seen that if the null word is a
(k_{1},\dots,k_{N},k_{N+1})
-injection of
\eta
, then the null word is a
(k_{1},\dots,k_{N},0)
-injection of
\Pi_{N}(\eta)
.
As a consequence for every string
(k_{1},\dots,k_{N},k_{N+1})\in\mathrm{Ad}(\eta)
, it holds
(k_{1},\dots,k_{N})\in\mathrm{Ad}(\Pi_{N}(\eta))
, and thus since
\sum_{i=1}^{N}k_{i}|U_{i}|\leq\sum_{i=1}^{N+1}k_{i}|U_{i}|
, this implies
\mathcal{A}^{N+1}(\gamma)\geq\mathcal{A}^{N}(\gamma)
.
It remains to prove that

\displaystyle\mathcal{A}(\gamma)\leq\lim_{N\rightarrow\infty}\mathcal{A}^{N}(\gamma).

(4.4)

The next steps are devoted to this aim.

Step 2:

We fix
\varepsilon>0
and use Lemma
4.2
to find a smooth curve
\gamma_{\varepsilon}
satisfying (a), (b), and (c). Thanks to (b) and (c), we build a Lipschitz homotopy
\Psi_{\varepsilon}:[0,1]\times\mathbb{S}^{1}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^{2}
between
\gamma
and
\gamma_{\varepsilon}
(e.g., by affine interpolation), in such a way that

\displaystyle\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2}}\text{{\rm mul}}(\Psi_{\varepsilon},[0,1]\times\mathbb{S}^{1},z)dz=\int_{0}^{1}\int_{\mathbb{S}^{1}}|\det\nabla\Psi_{\varepsilon}|dtds\leq C\varepsilon.

(4.5)

If
U_{i}
,
i\geq 1
, denote the bounded connected components of
\mathbb{R}^{2}\setminus\Gamma
(
U_{0}
will denote the unbounded one), we consider the function
m_{\varepsilon}(z):=\text{{\rm mul}}(\Psi_{\varepsilon},[0,1]\times\mathbb{S}^{1},z)
and select points
Q_{i}\in(U_{i}\cap E_{\Psi_{\varepsilon}})\setminus\Gamma_{\varepsilon}
, for all
i\geq 1
, such that

m_{\varepsilon}(Q_{i})=\min_{z\in(U_{i}\cap E_{\Psi_{\varepsilon}})\setminus\Gamma_{\varepsilon}}\{m_{\varepsilon}(z)\}.

It will be useful to choose also a point
Q_{0}\in U_{0}
with null multiplicity
m_{\varepsilon}(Q_{0})=0
. Moreover, we denote by
(V_{j})_{j=0}^{M_{\varepsilon}}
the connected components of
\mathbb{R}^{2}\setminus\Gamma_{\varepsilon}
(where
V_{0}
denotes the unbounded one) and we denote, for all
i\geq 0
and
j=0,\dots,M_{\varepsilon}
,

W_{ij}:=U_{i}\cap V_{j}.

Some of these sets might be empty.
For all
i\geq 0
and
j=0,\dots,M_{\varepsilon}
it is necessary to choose points
R_{ij}\in W_{ij}
(for those
W_{ij}\neq\varnothing
) as follows:

\displaystyle R_{ij}:=\begin{cases}Q_{i}&\text{ if }Q_{i}\in V_{j},\\
\text{{\rm argmin}}\{m_{\varepsilon}(z):z\in W_{ij}\cap E_{\Psi_{\varepsilon}}\}&\text{ otherwise},\end{cases}

(4.6)

where the second choice is arbitrary, whenever possible (if
W_{ij}\neq\varnothing)
. Finally, for all
i\geq 0
and
j=0,\dots,M_{\varepsilon}
, we denote

m_{ij}:=m_{\varepsilon}(R_{ij}),\qquad\qquad\text{if }W_{ij}\neq\varnothing,

and
m_{ij}=0
otherwise. Notice that
m_{\varepsilon}(R_{00})=0
and so
m_{00}=0
.

Now, we know that

\displaystyle\mathcal{A}(\gamma_{\varepsilon})=\sum_{j=1}^{M_{\varepsilon}}k_{j}|V_{j}|,

(4.7)

for some
(k_{1},\dots,k_{M_{\varepsilon}})\in\text{{\rm Ad}}(\gamma_{\varepsilon})
. We set

K:=\max\{1,k_{1},\dots,k_{M_{\varepsilon}}\}\qquad\qquad H:=\min\{|V_{1}|,\dots,|V_{M_{\varepsilon}}|\};

then we fix
N\in\mathbb{N}
big enough so that

\displaystyle\sum_{i>N}|U_{i}|\leq\varepsilon\min\left\{\frac{1}{K},\frac{H}{2}\right\}.

(4.8)

Finally, for all
j=0,\dots,M_{\varepsilon}
, we also set
\widetilde{W}_{(N+1)j}:=\cup_{h>N}{W_{hj}}
.
From (
4.5
) we deduce that

\displaystyle\sum_{j=0}^{M_{\varepsilon}}\sum_{i\geq 1}m_{ij}|W_{ij}|=\sum_{j=0}^{M_{\varepsilon}}\sum_{i\geq 1}m_{\varepsilon}(R_{ij})|W_{ij}|\leq\sum_{j=0}^{M_{\varepsilon}}\sum_{i\geq 1}\int_{W_{ij}}\text{{\rm mul}}(\Psi_{\varepsilon},[0,1]\times\mathbb{S}^{1},z)dz\leq C\varepsilon.

(4.9)

Let us compute
\mathcal{A}^{N}(\gamma)
; let
\gamma^{(N)}
be a word representing
\gamma
in
\pi_{1}(\mathbb{R}^{2}\setminus\{Q_{1},\dots,Q_{N}\})=F(N)
. Then

\displaystyle\mathcal{A}^{N}(\gamma)=\sum_{i=1}^{N}\ell_{i}|U_{i}|=\sum_{i=1}^{N}\sum_{j=0}^{M_{\varepsilon}}\ell_{i}|W_{ij}|,\qquad\qquad\ell_{i}\in\mathbb{N},

(4.10)

for a suitable
(\ell_{1},\dots,\ell_{N})\in\text{{\rm Ad}}(\gamma_{(N)})
. From (
4.9
) we have

\displaystyle\mathcal{A}^{N}(\gamma)\geq-C\varepsilon+\sum_{j=0}^{M_{\varepsilon}}\sum_{i=1}^{N}(\ell_{i}+m_{ij})|W_{ij}|=-C\varepsilon+\sum_{j=0}^{M_{\varepsilon}}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\widehat{k}_{ij}|W_{ij}|.

(4.11)

where we have noted
\widehat{k}_{ij}:=\ell_{i}+m_{ij}
; now we claim that

\displaystyle\mathcal{A}(\gamma_{\varepsilon})\leq C\varepsilon+\sum_{j=0}^{M_{\varepsilon}}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\widehat{k}_{ij}|W_{ij}|.

(4.12)

This will be shown in the next step.
From (
4.12
) and (
4.11
) we will deduce

\displaystyle\mathcal{A}(\gamma_{\varepsilon})\leq 2C\varepsilon+\mathcal{A}^{N}(\gamma),

for all
N
big enough.
But, from (b) and (c) in Lemma
4.2

we also have
\mathcal{A}(\gamma)\leq C\varepsilon+\mathcal{A}(\gamma_{\varepsilon})
, thus we infer

\displaystyle\mathcal{A}(\gamma)\leq 3C\varepsilon+\mathcal{A}^{N}(\gamma)\leq 3C\varepsilon+\lim_{N\rightarrow\infty}\mathcal{A}^{N}(\gamma),

which implies (
4.4
) by arbitraryness of
\varepsilon>0
. The thesis follows.

Step 3:

We are left with proving (
4.12
). Using (
4.7
), this is equivalent to show

\displaystyle\sum_{j=1}^{M_{\varepsilon}}k_{j}|V_{j}|=\sum_{j=1}^{M_{\varepsilon}}\sum_{i=0}^{\infty}k_{j}|W_{ij}|\leq C\varepsilon+\sum_{j=0}^{M_{\varepsilon}}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\widehat{k}_{ij}|W_{ij}|.

(4.13)

Setting
\widehat{k}_{0j}:=m_{0j}=m_{\varepsilon}(R_{0j})
, for all
j=1,\dots,M_{\varepsilon}
, still from (
4.5
) (and arguing as in (
4.9
)), we know that
\sum_{j=1}^{M_{\varepsilon}}\widehat{k}_{0j}|W_{0j}|\leq C\varepsilon.

Hence, using this, in order to show (
4.13
) we will prove that

\displaystyle\sum_{j=1}^{M_{\varepsilon}}k_{j}|V_{j}|\leq C\varepsilon+\sum_{j=0}^{M_{\varepsilon}}\sum_{i=0}^{N}\widehat{k}_{ij}|W_{ij}|

(4.14)

Recalling that
\widetilde{W}_{(N+1)j}=\cup_{h>N}{W_{hj}}
, we set

\widehat{k}_{(N+1)j}:=\min_{i>N}\{m_{ij}+k_{j}\}=m_{h_{j}j}+k_{j}=m_{\varepsilon}(R_{h_{j}j})+k_{j}

(for some index
h_{j}>N
) and we infer from (
4.9
)

\sum_{j=0}^{M_{\varepsilon}}\widehat{k}_{(N+1)j}|\widetilde{W}_{(N+1)j}|\leq C\varepsilon.

Whence, denoting
\widetilde{W}_{(N+1)j}
by
W_{(N+1)j}
(to shortcut notation), (
4.14
) will follow if we prove that, for some constant
C>0
, it holds

\displaystyle\sum_{j=1}^{M_{\varepsilon}}k_{j}|V_{j}|\leq C\varepsilon+\sum_{j=0}^{M_{\varepsilon}}\sum_{i=0}^{N+1}\widehat{k}_{ij}|W_{ij}|.

(4.15)

Denoting
R_{(N+1)j}:=R_{h_{j}j}
, we now consider the family
\mathcal{R}:=\{R_{ij}:i=0,\dots,N+1,\;j=1,\dots,M_{\varepsilon}\}
. We now see that the claim (
4.15
) follows from Proposition
4.3
provided that

\displaystyle(\dots\widehat{k}_{ij}\dots)\in\textrm{Ad}(\gamma_{\varepsilon}^{\mathcal{R}}),

(4.16)

because in this case, setting
k^{\prime}_{j}:=\min_{i\leq N+1}\{\widehat{k}_{ij}\}
, we have

\displaystyle\sum_{j=1}^{M_{\varepsilon}}k_{j}|V_{j}|\leq\sum_{j=1}^{M_{\varepsilon}}k^{\prime}_{j}|V_{j}|=\sum_{j=1}^{M_{\varepsilon}}\sum_{i=0}^{N+1}k_{j}^{\prime}|W_{ij}|\leq\sum_{j=1}^{M_{\varepsilon}}\sum_{i=0}^{N+1}\widehat{k}_{ij}|W_{ij}|,

which implies (
4.15
). Now, using the definition of
\widehat{k}_{ij}
, the fact that
R_{ij}\in V_{j}
for all
i=0,\dots,N+1
, and so defining
P_{j}
as one of them, (
4.16
) readily follows from Lemma
4.4
.
∎
