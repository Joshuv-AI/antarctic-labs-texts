Simplicial resolutions of powers of square-free monomial ideals
Susan M. Cooper, Sabine El Khoury, Sara Faridi, Sarah Mayes-Tang, Susan Morey, Liana M. Şega, Sandra Spiroff

Abstract
. The Taylor resolution is almost never minimal for powers of monomial ideals, even in the square-free case. In this paper we introduce a smaller resolution for each power of any square-free monomial ideal, which depends only on the number of generators of the ideal. More precisely, for every pair of fixed integers r r and q q , we construct a simplicial complex that supports a free resolution of the r t ​ h r^{th} power of any square-free monomial ideal with q q generators. The resulting resolution is significantly smaller than the Taylor resolution, and is minimal for special cases. Considering the relations on the generators of a fixed ideal allows us to further shrink these resolutions. We also introduce a class of ideals called “extremal ideals,” and show that the Betti numbers of powers of all square-free monomial ideals are bounded by Betti numbers of powers of extremal ideals. Our results lead to upper bounds on Betti numbers of powers of any square-free monomial ideal that greatly improve the binomial bounds offered by the Taylor resolution.

1.
Introduction

Important insight about the underlying structure of an ideal in a
polynomial ring is gained from a careful analysis of its minimal free
resolution. As such, significant effort has gone into the development
of methods to compute resolutions. The approach of leveraging
connections between commutative algebra and other fields, such as
combinatorics and topology, has proven to be quite fruitful. Diana
Taylor’s thesis
[
19
]
initiated the exploration of these
connections, followed by simplicial resolutions (Bayer, Peeva, and
Sturmfels
[
1
]
), polytopal complexes (Nagel and Reiner

[
15
]
), and cellular complexes (Bayer and Sturmfels
[
2
]
), to
name just a few. See
[
14
,
16
]
for an overview of these developments.

The Taylor resolution is powerful: given
any
ideal
I

minimally generated by
q
monomials, Taylor constructed a simplicial
complex
\mathrm{Taylor}(I)
by labeling the vertices of a
(q-1)
-simplex with
the monomial generators of
I
. She showed that this complex
supports
a free resolution of
I
, in the sense that its simplicial
chain complex can be transformed, via a process called
homogenization
, to a free resolution of
I
, called the
Taylor
resolution
of
I
.
Unfortunately, even though every monomial ideal has a Taylor
resolution, the Taylor resolution is often far from minimal. In
particular, for powers of ideals it is almost never minimal due to certain syzygies that are automatically created when taking powers.

The central theme of this paper is to find an analogue for the Taylor
complex for powers of square-free monomial ideals.
We seek a construction that takes the automatically generated non-minimal syzygies
into account and removes them from the Taylor resolution to produce a
much smaller free resolution of
I^{r}
that works for any monomial ideal
I
. Our
ultimate goal is to find a uniform combinatorial structure that
depends only on the number of generators and the power of the ideal.
More precisely, the question at the heart of this paper is the
following:

Question
.

Given positive integers
r
and
q
, is it possible to
find a simplicial complex (considerably) smaller than the simplex

\mathrm{Taylor}(I^{r})
that supports a free resolution of
I^{r}
, where
I

is any ideal generated by
q
monomials in a polynomial ring?

When
r=1
,
\mathrm{Taylor}(I)
is in fact the optimal answer to the question
above, as there are ideals
I
for which
\mathrm{Taylor}(I)
supports a
minimal resolution. But when
r=2
, the resolution supported on

\mathrm{Taylor}(I^{2})
is never minimal for any non-principal square-free
monomial ideal
I
(
[
4
]
). As expected, the resolution supported on
\mathrm{Taylor}(I^{r})

becomes further from minimal as
r
grows.

Although Taylor’s complex for the
r^{th}
power of a monomial ideal
with
q
generators can be quite large - a simplex of dimension

{q+r-1}\choose{r}
- we can improve the situation considerably by
studying the general relations among the generators of
I^{r}
that must
always exist for all monomial ideals regardless of the generating set
for
I
. Similar to the idea used in Lyubeznik’s
resolutions
[
12
]
, in the case of square-free monomial ideals, this
investigation involves detecting and trimming redundant faces of the
Taylor complex
\mathrm{Taylor}(I^{r})
, bringing us closer to a minimal
resolution.

To illustrate our underlying process, let
r=2,q=3
and consider
any
ideal
I=(m_{1},m_{2},m_{3})
in the polynomial ring
\mathsf{k}[x_{1},\ldots,x_{n}]
where
m_{1},m_{2}
and
m_{3}
are minimal, square-free
monomial generators and
\mathsf{k}
is a field. Now
I^{2}=({m_{1}}^{2},{m_{2}}^{2},{m_{3}}^{2},m_{1}m_{2},m_{1}m_{3},m_{2}m_{3})
and
\mathrm{Taylor}(I^{2})
is a

5
-dimensional simplex with
6
vertices, where each vertex is
labeled by a generator of
I^{2}
and each face is labeled with the
least common multiple of its vertices. A non-minimal syzygy occurs
when a face and a subface have the same label (see
Theorem

3.1
). When
considering
I^{2}
, no matter what the monomial generators of
I
are,
when

are distinct we always have the following:

\mathrm{lcm}({m_{i}}^{2},{m_{j}}^{2})=\mathrm{lcm}({{m_{i}}^{2},m_{i}m_{j},{m_{j}}^{2}})\quad\mbox{ and }\quad\mathrm{lcm}({m_{i}}^{2},m_{j}m_{k})=\mathrm{lcm}({m_{i}}^{2},m_{j}m_{k},m_{i}m_{k}).

In this paper we show that a similar argument can be made more
generally. If
I
is minimally generated by square-free monomials

m_{1},\ldots,m_{q}
, then we can identify faces of the simplex

\mathrm{Taylor}(I^{r})
which lead to non-minimal syzygies for
I^{r}
.
Eliminating these faces results in a much smaller subcomplex of

\mathrm{Taylor}(I^{r})

Our main result,

, shows that
\mathbb{L}^{r}_{q}
supports a

I^{r}
. This echoes the power of Taylor’s complex in
that we have a topological structure depending solely on
r
and
q

that supports a resolution of
I^{r}
for
any
square-free
monomial ideal
I
with
q
minimal generators.
By further deleting redundancies specific to the generators of
I

\mathbb{L}^{r}(I)
of

\mathbb{L}^{r}(I)\subseteq\mathbb{L}^{r}_{q}\subseteq\mathrm{Taylor}(I^{r}).

Theorem

5.9
also shows
that
\mathbb{L}^{r}(I)
supports a free resolution of
I^{r}
. Our approach
to proving this involves showing that
\mathbb{L}^{r}(I)
and
\mathbb{L}^{r}_{q}
are both

quasi-trees
(see
Definition

2.2
), meaning (roughly) that are
built from a special ordering on their facets. In

Theorem

3.6
, we show that for a quasi-tree to support a
free resolution one only needs to check that certain induced subcomplexes are
connected. This extends the main result of
[
9
]
.

Our complex
\mathbb{L}^{r}(I)
gives natural and useful bounds on homological
information of
I^{r}
. Indeed, the Betti numbers of
I^{r}
are bounded
in terms of the number and size of faces of
\mathbb{L}^{r}(I)
, yielding bounds that are significantly
smaller than those given by the Taylor resolution.
In the later sections of this paper we examine just how much smaller these bounds on the Betti numbers are and when the resolutions obtained are minimal.
We define a class of square-free monomial ideals, which we call
extremal ideals
, whose Betti numbers of powers bound the Betti numbers of powers of any square-free monomial ideal with the same number of generators. As a result, we reduce the question of minimality of
\mathbb{L}^{r}_{q}
to the study of when
\mathbb{L}^{r}_{q}
supports a minimal free resolution of the
r^{th}
power of an extremal ideal.

To put this work in context of the broader literature, studying powers of ideals and bounding their invariants has received much attention in recent years. Powers play an important role in Rees algebras and associated graded rings among other uses, making understanding their behavior desirable but difficult. In another direction, there has been considerable interest in describing minimal topological resolutions for all monomial ideals using a variety of methods, such as using chain maps from multiple simplicial complexes (see
[
7
,
20
]
). In this paper we combine the two interests and seek, for powers of monomial ideals, resolutions that are supported on a single topological structure which is practical to determine based on the generators of the original ideal
I
.

3.
Resolutions supported on quasi-trees

Taylor’s resolution is usually far from
minimal. However, for a given monomial ideal
I
, a
criterion of Bayer, Peeva and Sturmfels (see
Theorem

3.1
)
allows one to check if a subcomplex of
\mathrm{Taylor}(I)
supports a
free resolution of
I
. In this section, using the above
criterion and by observing that quasi-trees are collapsible,
we show that a quasi-tree
\Delta
supports a free resolution
of a given monomial ideal if and only if certain subcomplexes
of
\Delta
are connected.

For a subcomplex
\Delta
of
\mathrm{Taylor}(I)
and a
monomial
M
in
S
, let
\Delta_{M}
be the subcomplex of
\Delta

induced on the vertices of
\Delta
whose labels divide
M
.

The following is the criterion of Bayer, Peeva and Sturmfels

[
1
, Lemma 2.2]
; see also
[
6
, Theorem 2.2]
for the
statement on minimality.

Theorem 3.1

(
Criterion for a simplicial complex to support a free resolution).

Let
\Delta
be a simplicial complex whose vertices are labeled
with a monomial generating set of a monomial ideal

I
in a polynomial ring
S
over a field.
Then
\Delta
supports a free resolution of
I
over
S
if and
only if for every monomial
M
, the induced subcomplex

\Delta_{M}
of
\Delta
on the vertices whose labels divide
M
is
empty or acyclic.

Furthermore, a free resolution supported on
\Delta
is
minimal if and only if
M_{\sigma}\neq M_{\sigma^{\prime}}

for every proper subface
\sigma^{\prime}
of a face
\sigma

of
\Delta
.

Remark 3.2
.

The results of
Theorem

3.1
are usually stated with the assumption that one uses the
minimal
monomial generating set of

for the labels. However, the proof of
[
1

does not make use of this assumption, hence we formulated the result above to reflect this observation.

In particular,
Theorem

3.1
implies that the

\Delta
supporting a resolution of a monomial ideal

is an
upper bound for the vector of Betti numbers of

for each

\beta_{i}(I)\leq f_{i}\quad\mbox{ where }\quad\mathbf{f}(\Delta)=(f_{0},\ldots,f_{d}).

In

Using

3.1
it is straightforward to see that to

\Delta
supports a free resolution of

to check that

is empty or acyclic only for monomials

If the complex
\Delta

is connected, instead of acyclic, see

that

An example of collapsing

In general, by starting with a leaf and the face that contains it, any
quasi-tree can be collapsed to a point.

Proposition 3.5
.

Every quasi-tree is collapsible.

Proof.

Proceed by induction on
q
, the number of facets of
\Delta
. When
q=1
,

\Delta
is a simplex, and known to be collapsible (e.g.
[
9
, Proposition 2.7]
). Suppose the statement holds for all quasi-trees with
fewer than
q
facets.

Let
\Delta
be a quasi-tree with facet ordering
F_{1},\ldots,F_{q}
so
that, for
i=1,\ldots,q
, each
F_{i}
is a leaf of
\langle F_{1},\ldots,F_{i}\rangle
. Since
F_{q}
is a leaf of
\Delta
, it intersects

\Delta^{\prime}=\langle F_{1},\ldots,F_{q-1}\rangle
in a face
F^{\prime}
of
\Delta^{\prime}
. By
[
9
, Proposition 2.7]
,
F_{q}
collapses down to the face

F^{\prime}
by removing faces outside
\Delta^{\prime}
. As a result, this series of
collapses brings
\Delta
to the quasi-tree
\Delta^{\prime}
, which, by the induction
hypothesis, is collapsible.
∎

We now use
Proposition

3.5
and
Proposition

3.3
to
show that the Bayer-Peeva-Sturmfels criterion in
Theorem

3.1
can be extended to the class
of quasi-trees.

Theorem 3.6

(
Criterion for a quasi-tree to support a free resolution).

Let
\Delta
be a quasi-tree whose vertices are labeled
with a monomial generating set of a monomial ideal
I
in a
polynomial ring
S
over a field. Then
\Delta
supports a free
resolution of
I
over
S
if and only if for every monomial
M
, the
induced subcomplex
\Delta_{M}
of
\Delta
on the vertices whose labels divide

M
is empty or connected.

Proof.

By
Theorem

3.1
,
\Delta
supports a
resolution of
I
if and only if
\Delta_{M}
is empty or acyclic for
every monomial
M\in S
. If
M\in S
is a monomial, then by

Proposition

3.3
above,
\Delta_{M}
is a quasi-forest. Moreover,
by
Proposition

3.5
, every connected component of
\Delta_{M}

is contractible, i.e., homotopy equivalent to a point, and hence
acyclic. The only possible homology that
\Delta_{M}
could have would be that which comes
from it being disconnected. As a result,
\Delta
supports a
resolution of
I
if and only if
\Delta_{M}
is empty or connected for
every monomial
M\in S
.
∎

4.
The quasi-tree
\mathbb{L}^{r}_{q}

Recall that our goal is to find a simplicial complex smaller than
Taylor’s complex that supports a free resolution of
I^{r}
when
I

has
q
square-free monomial generators. In this section we construct
a subcomplex
\mathbb{L}^{r}_{q}
of the Taylor simplex, which depends only on the
integers
r
and
q
, and contains a subcomplex supporting a free
resolution of
I^{r}
.

The base case for our construction is the case
r=1
. In this case

\mathbb{L}^{1}_{q}
is the well-known Taylor complex
[
19
]
: a simplex with
q
vertices
that supports a free resolution of any monomial ideal with

q
generators. The case
r=2
was investigated in the earlier work
of the authors
[
4
]
. For example, it was shown in
[
4
]

that
\mathbb{L}^{2}_{3}
is the quasi-tree in
Example

2.4
.

We now collect and formalize the notation needed for the extension to
the case
r\geq 3
.

Notation 4.1
.

Let
r
and
q
be two positive integers.

•

Let
\mathbf{e}_{1},\ldots,\mathbf{e}_{q}
denote the standard basis vectors for

\mathbb{R}^{q}
; i.e., for each
i\in[q]
,
\mathbf{e}_{i}
is the
q
-tuple with 1 in the
i^{th}

coordinate and 0 elsewhere.

•

Define
\mathcal{N}^{r}_{q}
to be the set of points in
\mathbb{Z}_{\geq 0}^{q}
whose
coordinates add up to
r
:

\displaystyle\mathcal{N}^{r}_{q}=

\displaystyle\{(a_{1},\ldots,a_{q})\in\mathbb{Z}_{\geq 0}^{q}\colon a_{1}+\cdots+a_{q}=r\}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\{a_{1}\mathbf{e}_{1}+\cdots+a_{q}\mathbf{e}_{q}\colon a_{i}\in\mathbb{Z}_{\geq 0}{\text{ and }}a_{1}+\cdots+a_{q}=r\}.

•

Set
s=\left\lceil{\frac{r}{2}}\right\rceil
; that is, when
r
is odd,

r=2s-1
, and when
r
is even,
r=2s
.

Definition 4.2

(
The simplicial complex
\mathbb{L}^{r}_{q}
- see also
Proposition

4.3
).

Let
r,q\geq 1
be two integers. Following
4.1
we define

\mathbb{L}^{r}_{q}
to be the simplicial complex with vertex set
\mathcal{N}^{r}_{q}
whose faces
are all subsets of the (not necessarily distinct) sets
F^{r}_{1},\dots,F^{r}_{q},G^{r}_{1},\ldots,G^{r}_{q}
defined as

\displaystyle F^{r}_{i}

\displaystyle=\{(a_{1},\ldots,a_{q})\in\mathcal{N}^{r}_{q}\colon a_{i}\leq\max\{r-1,s\}\mbox{ and }a_{j}\leq s\mbox{ for }j\neq i\}

\displaystyle G^{r}_{i}

\displaystyle=\{(a_{1},\ldots,a_{q})\in\mathcal{N}^{r}_{q}\colon a_{i}\geq r-1\}=\{(r-1)\mathbf{e}_{i}+\mathbf{e}_{j}\colon j\in[q]\}

for each
i\in[q]
. We refer to the set
\{F^{r}_{1},\dots,F^{r}_{q}\}
as
the
first layer
of
\mathbb{L}^{r}_{q}
and the set
\{G^{r}_{1},\dots,G^{r}_{q}\}

as the
second layer
of
\mathbb{L}^{r}_{q}
. We define the
base
of

\mathbb{L}^{r}_{q}
to be the face

B^{r}=\left\{(a_{1},\ldots,a_{q})\in\mathcal{N}^{r}_{q}\colon a_{i}\leq s\mbox{ for all }i\in[q]\right\},

so that

F_{i}^{r}=B^{r}\cup\{(a_{1},\ldots,a_{q})\in\mathcal{N}^{r}_{q}\colon s+1\leq a_{i}\leq r-1\}.

In general,
F^{r}_{1},\ldots F^{r}_{q},G^{r}_{1},\ldots,G^{r}_{q}
are the
facets of
\mathbb{L}^{r}_{q}
(
Proposition

4.3
); however, for small values of
r

and
q
, these sets need not be distinct. We summarize these facts
in
Proposition

4.3
to give a more precise description of the facets of

\mathbb{L}^{r}_{q}
.

Proposition 4.3

(
Equivalent definition of
\mathbb{L}^{r}_{q}
).

The simplicial complex
\mathbb{L}^{r}_{q}
in
Definition

4.2
can be described in
terms of its distinct facets:

\mathbb{L}^{r}_{q}=\begin{cases}\langle F^{r}_{1},F^{r}_{2},\dots,F^{r}_{q},G^{r}_{1},\dots,G^{r}_{q}\rangle&\text{if $r>3$ and $q\geq 2$}\\
\langle B^{r},G^{r}_{1},\dots,G^{r}_{q}\rangle&\text{if $r=3$ and $q\geq 2$ or $r=2$ and $q>2$ }\\
\langle G^{2}_{1},G^{2}_{2}\rangle&\text{if $r=2$ and $q=2$}\\
\langle\mathcal{N}^{r}_{q}\rangle&\text{if $r=1$ or $q=1$.}\\
\end{cases}

Proof.

When
r=1
, then
s=1
and
\mathcal{N}^{1}_{q}=\{\mathbf{e}_{1},\ldots,\mathbf{e}_{q}\}
.
It follows that

B^{1}=F^{1}_{i}=G^{1}_{i}=\mathcal{N}^{1}_{q}\quad\mbox{ for all }\quad i\in[q].

In this case
\mathbb{L}^{1}_{q}
is a simplex with
q
vertices (the
Taylor simplex).

If
q=1
and
r>1
, then

B^{r}=F^{r}_{1}=\emptyset\quad\mbox{ and }\quad G^{r}_{1}=\mathcal{N}^{r}_{1}=\{(r)\}.

If
q=r=2
, then

G^{2}_{1}=\{(2,0),(1,1)\}\quad\mbox{ and }\quad G^{2}_{2}=\{(0,2),(1,1)\}

and since

s=1=r-1
,

F^{2}_{1}=F^{2}_{2}=B^{2}=\{(1,1)\}\subseteq G^{2}_{1}\cap G^{2}_{2}.

When
q\geq 2
and
r=3
, or
q>2
and
r=2
, then the
G_{i}^{r}

are distinct as each
G_{i}^{r}
is the unique facet containing the vertex

r\mathbf{e}_{i}
. Furthermore, since
s+1=r
,

F^{r}_{1}=\dots=F^{r}_{q}=B^{r}

contains all the vertices
(r-1)\mathbf{e}_{i}+\mathbf{e}_{j}

where
i\neq j
, and so
B^{r}
is not embedded in any of the
G_{i}^{r}
.

Finally, when
q\geq 2
and
r>3
, then
s<r-1
. Therefore, the
G_{i}^{r}
are distinct, since each

G_{i}^{r}
is the unique facet containing the vertex

r\mathbf{e}_{i}
.

For
i,j\in[q]
,
i\neq j
,
F^{r}_{j}
is not
contained in
F^{r}_{i}
since

(s+1)\mathbf{e}_{j}+(r-s-1)\mathbf{e}_{i}\in F^{r}_{j}\setminus F^{r}_{i}.

Moreover,
r\mathbf{e}_{j}\notin F^{r}_{i}
, showing that

G^{r}_{j}\nsubseteq F^{r}_{i}.

Lastly, no
F^{r}_{i}
can be contained in
G^{r}_{j}
since
B^{r}\subseteq F^{r}_{i}
,
but
B^{r}\cap G^{r}_{j}=\emptyset
when
s<r-1
.
∎

Proposition 4.6

(
\mathbb{L}^{r}_{q}
is a quasi-tree).

The simplicial complex
\mathbb{L}^{r}_{q}
is a quasi-tree.

Proof.

Following
Proposition

4.3
for a description of the
facets of
\mathbb{L}^{r}_{q}
, we consider each case separately.

When
r=1
or
q=1
,
\mathbb{L}^{1}_{1}
is a simplex,
and hence by definition a quasi-tree.

When
r=q=2
,
\mathbb{L}^{2}_{2}
has only two
facets, and it is trivially a quasi-tree.

When
r=2
and
q>2
, then order the
facets of
\mathbb{L}^{2}_{q}
by

B^{2},G^{2}_{1},\ldots,G^{2}_{q}.

In this case,
if
i\neq j
with
i,j\in[q]
, then

G^{2}_{i}\cap B^{2}\subseteq B^{2}\quad\mbox{ and }\quad G^{2}_{i}\cap G^{2}_{j}=\{\mathbf{e}_{i}+\mathbf{e}_{j}\}\subseteq B^{2}.

Thus each
G^{2}_{i}
is a leaf of
\langle B^{2},G^{2}_{1},\ldots,G^{2}_{i}\rangle
with joint
B^{2}
.

When
r=3
and
q\geq 2
, then as in the previous case,

B^{3},G^{3}_{1},\ldots,G^{3}_{q}

is
a leaf order. Since
G^{3}_{i}\cap G^{3}_{j}=\emptyset
when
i\neq j
, for each
i\in[q]
the facet
G^{3}_{i}
is a leaf of

\langle B^{3},G^{3}_{1},\ldots,G^{3}_{i}\rangle

with joint
B^{3}
.

When
r>3
and
q\geq 2
, we claim

(4.6.1)

F^{r}_{1},F^{r}_{2},\dots,F^{r}_{q},G^{r}_{1},\dots,G^{r}_{q}

is a leaf order for
\mathbb{L}^{r}_{q}
, making it a quasi-tree.
To see this note that if
i\neq j
,
i,j\in[q]
, then

\displaystyle F^{r}_{i}\cap G^{r}_{j}

\displaystyle=\emptyset,\quad F^{r}_{i}\cap F^{r}_{j}=B^{r},\quad G^{r}_{i}\cap G^{r}_{j}=\emptyset,

\displaystyle F^{r}_{i}\cap G^{r}_{i}

. Hence (

) is a leaf order

5.
The quasi-tree
\mathbb{L}^{r}(I)
supporting a free resolution of
I^{r}

Given an ideal
I
with
q
square-free monomial generators, we now
define an induced subcomplex of
\mathbb{L}^{r}_{q}
, denoted
\mathbb{L}^{r}(I)
, which is
obtained by deleting vertices representing redundant generators of

I^{r}
. We show in

5.9
that both
\mathbb{L}^{r}_{q}
and
\mathbb{L}^{r}(I)
support
a free resolution of
I^{r}
.

Definition 5.1

(
The simplicial complex
\mathbb{L}^{r}(I)
).

Let
I
be an ideal minimally generated by monomials

Proposition 5.4
.

Let
I
be an ideal minimally generated by
square-free monomials
m_{1},\ldots,m_{q}
in the polynomial ring

S
. If

then
\mathbb{L}^{r}(I)=\mathbb{L}^{r}_{q}
for all

.

Proof.

If
q=1
, then
I^{r}=({m_{1}}^{r})
for all
r
, and both

\mathbb{L}^{r}(I)
and
\mathbb{L}^{r}_{q}
consist of a single point, so the equality holds.

Assume
1<q\leq 3
. By
Definition

5.1
it suffices to show that

\mathbb{L}^{r}(I)
and
\mathbb{L}^{r}_{q}
have the same vertex set, or in other
words

\mathbf{m}^{\mathbf{a}}\mid\mathbf{m}^{\mathbf{b}}\implies\mathbf{a}=\mathbf{b}\quad\mbox{ for all }\quad\mathbf{a},\mathbf{b}\in\mathcal{N}^{r}_{q}.

Suppose
q=2
and
\mathbf{m}^{\mathbf{a}}\mid\mathbf{m}^{\mathbf{b}}
with

\mathbf{a}=(a_{1},a_{2}),\quad\mathbf{b}=(b_{1},b_{2}),\quad\mbox{ and }\quad a_{1}+a_{2}=b_{1}+b_{2}=r.

If

\mathbf{a}\neq\mathbf{b}
, then we may assume
a_{1}>b_{1}
and
a_{2}<b_{2}
. Since the
monomials
m_{i}
are square-free, we have

{m_{1}}^{a_{1}}{m_{2}}^{a_{2}}\mid{m_{1}}^{b_{1}}{m_{2}}^{b_{2}}\implies{m_{1}}^{a_{1}-b_{1}}\mid{m_{2}}^{b_{2}-a_{2}}\implies m_{1}\mid m_{2}\,,

which contradicts the
minimality of the generators of
I
.

Suppose
q=3
and
\mathbf{m}^{\mathbf{a}}\mid\mathbf{m}^{\mathbf{b}}
with

\mathbf{a}=(a_{1},a_{2},a_{3}),\quad\mathbf{b}=(b_{1},b_{2},b_{3})\quad\mbox{ and }\quad a_{1}+a_{2}+a_{3}=b_{1}+b_{2}+b_{3}=r.

Assume
\mathbf{a}\neq\mathbf{b}
. If
a_{i}=b_{i}
for some
i
then we can reduce to
the case
q=2
, so we may assume
a_{i}\neq b_{i}
for all
i
. Without
loss of generality, assume
a_{1}>b_{1}
.

We have three cases:

(1)

Suppose
a_{2}>b_{2}
. In this case, we must also have

a_{3}<b_{3}
. Then

{m_{1}}^{a_{1}}{m_{2}}^{a_{2}}{m_{3}}^{a_{3}}\mid{m_{1}}^{b_{1}}{m_{2}}^{b_{2}}{m_{3}}^{b_{3}}\implies{m_{1}}^{a_{1}-b_{1}}{m_{2}}^{a_{2}-b_{2}}\mid{m_{3}}^{b_{3}-a_{3}}\implies{m_{1}}^{a_{1}-b_{1}}\mid{m_{3}}^{b_{3}-a_{3}}.

Since

m_{1}
and
m_{3}
are square-free, this implies that
m_{1}\mid m_{3}

which gives a
contradiction.

(2)

Suppose
a_{2}<b_{2}
and

a_{3}<b_{3}
. We have

{m_{1}}^{a_{1}}{m_{2}}^{a_{2}}{m_{3}}^{a_{3}}\mid{m_{1}}^{b_{1}}{m_{2}}^{b_{2}}{m_{3}}^{b_{3}}\implies{m_{1}}^{a_{1}-b_{1}}\mid{m_{2}}^{b_{2}-a_{2}}{m_{3}}^{b_{3}-a_{3}}.

Let
x
be a variable such that
x\mid m_{1}
. It follows that
x\mid m_{2}
or
x\mid m_{3}
. Assume

x\nmid m_{3}
. We then have
x^{a_{1}-b_{1}}\mid{m_{2}}^{b_{2}-a_{2}}
. Since

m_{2}
is square-free, it follows that
b_{2}-a_{2}\geq a_{1}-b_{1}
. This is a contradiction, since

a_{1}-b_{1}=b_{2}-a_{2}+b_{3}-a_{3}>b_{2}-a_{2}.

A similar contradiction is obtained when
x\nmid m_{2}
. We conclude
that for every
x\mid m_{1}
, we must have
x\mid m_{2}
and
x\mid m_{3}
. This implies
m_{1}\mid m_{2}
and
m_{1}\mid m_{3}
,
which is a contradiction.

(3)

Suppose
a_{2}<b_{2}
and
a_{3}>b_{3}
. After relabeling, this case reduces
to Case (1).

Proposition 5.6

Let

,
I

, and all

0\leq u\leq r-a_{i}\quad\mbox{ and }\quad 0\leq v\leq r-b_{j}.

Also note that

\deg_{x}({m_{i}}^{\alpha}{m_{j}}^{a_{i}-\alpha}\mathbf{m}^{\mathbf{a}^{\prime}})=\alpha\cdot\deg_{x}(m_{i})+(a_{i}-\alpha)\cdot\deg_{x}(m_{j})+u.

•

If
x\mid m_{i}
, then

\displaystyle\deg_{x}({m_{i}}^{\alpha}{m_{j}}^{a_{i}-\alpha}\mathbf{m}^{\mathbf{a}^{\prime}})=

\displaystyle\,\alpha+(a_{i}-\alpha)\deg_{x}(m_{j})+u

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\,\alpha+(a_{i}-\alpha)+u=a_{i}+u

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\,\deg_{x}(\mathbf{m}^{\mathbf{a}})

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\,\max\{\deg_{x}(\mathbf{m}^{\mathbf{a}}),\deg_{x}(\mathbf{m}^{\mathbf{b}})\}.

•

If
x\nmid m_{i}
, but
x\mid m_{j}
, then

\displaystyle\deg_{x}({m_{i}}^{\alpha}{m_{j}}^{a_{i}-\alpha}\mathbf{m}^{\mathbf{a}^{\prime}})=

\displaystyle\,(a_{i}-\alpha)+u

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\,(a_{i}-\alpha)+(r-a_{i})\quad\mbox{by \eqref{e:uv-ab}}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\,r-\alpha<b_{j}\leq b_{j}+v

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\,\deg_{x}(\mathbf{m}^{\mathbf{b}})

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\,\max\{\deg_{x}(\mathbf{m}^{\mathbf{a}}),\deg_{x}(\mathbf{m}^{\mathbf{b}})\}.

•

If
x\nmid m_{i}
and
x\nmid m_{j}
, then

\deg_{x}({m_{i}}^{\alpha}{m_{j}}^{a_{i}-\alpha}\mathbf{m}^{\mathbf{a}^{\prime}})=\deg_{x}(\mathbf{m}^{\mathbf{a}^{\prime}})=\deg_{x}(\mathbf{m}^{\mathbf{a}})\leq\max\{\deg_{x}(\mathbf{m}^{\mathbf{a}}),\deg_{x}(\mathbf{m}^{\mathbf{b}})\}.

This finishes the proof of
(ii)
.

Finally,
(iii)
follows directly from the fact that

\displaystyle\deg({m_{i}}^{\alpha}{m_{j}}^{a_{i}-\alpha}\mathbf{m}^{\mathbf{a}^{\prime}})

\displaystyle=\alpha\cdot\deg m_{i}+(a_{i}-\alpha)\cdot\deg m_{j}+\deg(\mathbf{m}^{\mathbf{a}^{\prime}})

\displaystyle=\alpha\cdot\deg m_{i}+(a_{i}-\alpha)\cdot\deg m_{j}+\deg(\mathbf{m}^{\mathbf{a}})-a_{i}\cdot\deg m_{i}

\displaystyle=\deg(\mathbf{m}^{\mathbf{a}})-(a_{i}-\alpha)(\deg(m_{i})-\deg(m_{j})).

∎

Remark 5.10
.

One may feel that part

of the statement above is weaker than

, since

. However, the remarkable aspect of

is that, before labeling,

does not depend on the ideal

. Thus the same topological structure, depending only on

and

, supports a free resolution of the

provides an alternative notion to the Taylor complex for powers of

6.
Bounds on Betti numbers

One of the key applications of
Theorem

5.9
is that we are able to improve the bounds on Betti numbers for powers of ideals from that given by the standard Taylor resolution of
I^{r}
since

\mathbb{L}^{r}(I)\subseteq\mathbb{L}^{r}_{q}\subseteq\mathrm{Taylor}(I^{r}).

This section contains computations that illustrate the extent to which our results improve the Taylor bounds.

When
r=2
, we were able to provide a concrete formula for the number
of faces of
\mathbb{L}^{r}(I)
in
[
4
]
, and as a result we provided
bounds for Betti numbers of
I^{2}
, which are shown in
Proposition

7.11
to be sharp.
When
r>2
, however, such
formulas are not as easy to write and the numbers are very large even
for small examples. In this case we shift our attention to
\mathbb{L}^{r}_{q}
. While the
bound on the Betti numbers stemming from
\mathbb{L}^{r}(I)
is dependent on the
relations among the generators of
I
, one can use the structure of

\mathbb{L}^{r}_{q}
to get a general, albeit weaker, bound on
\beta_{t}(I^{r})
.

Theorem 6.1

(
Bounds on Betti numbers of
I^{r}
).

If
I
is a square-free monomial ideal with
q
minimal generators,
then the Betti numbers of
I^{r}
for
r\geq 2
are bounded above by

\beta_{t}(I^{r})\leq q\left({{q-1}\choose{t}}+{{f}\choose{t+1}}-{{b}\choose{t+1}}\right)+{{b}\choose{t+1}}

where
t\geq 0
,
b
is the coefficient of
x^{r}
in the expansion of

(1+x+x^{2}+\cdots+x^{\left\lceil{\frac{r}{2}}\right\rceil})^{q},

and

f=\frac{{{q+r-1}\choose{r}}-b-q}{q}+b.

In particular,

\mathrm{pd}(I^{r})\leq\max\{q-1,f-1\}.

Proof.

Let
s=\left\lceil{\frac{r}{2}}\right\rceil
.
Note that
\beta_{t}(I^{r})
is bounded above by the number of faces of
dimension
t
of
\mathbb{L}^{r}_{q}
. To count the faces of dimension
t
, we use the
sets
B^{r},F_{i}^{r},
and
G_{i}^{r}
from
Definition

4.2
.

Observe that the coefficient
b
of
x^{r}
in the expansion of

(1+x+x^{2}+\cdots+x^{s})^{q}

is exactly the number of

q
-tuples
(a_{1},\ldots,a_{q})
with
a_{i}\leq s
and
a_{1}+\cdots+a_{q}=r
,
in other words
b=|B^{r}|
.

Note that the vertices of
\mathbb{L}^{r}_{q}
are formed by selecting
r
of the original
q
generators, so using the standard formula for counting with repetition, we have

|V(\mathbb{L}^{r}_{q})|={{q+r-1}\choose{r}}.

Now
|F_{i}^{r}|=|F_{j}^{r}|
for all
i,j
, so let
f=|F_{i}^{r}|
. Since

|G_{i}^{r}\setminus F_{i}^{r}|=1
, there are
|V(\mathbb{L}^{r}_{q})|-q
vertices in

\cup_{i=1}^{q}F_{i}^{r}
. Also
F_{i}^{r}\cap F_{j}^{r}=B^{r}
for all
i,j
such that
i\not=j
,
so we have

(6.1.1)

f=|F_{i}^{r}|={\frac{1}{q}}\big((|V(\mathbb{L}^{r}_{q})|-q)-|B^{r}|\big)+|B^{r}|=\frac{{{q+r-1}\choose{r}}-q-b}{q}+b.

To count the number of faces of dimension
t
, that is, faces with

t+1
vertices, in
\mathbb{L}^{r}_{q}
, we separate the faces into three distinct
types.

(1)

Faces containing
{m_{i}}^{r}
for some
i
: By the definition of

\mathbb{L}^{r}_{q}
, faces of this type must be contained in
G_{i}^{r}
. Since the
vertex corresponding to
{m_{i}}^{r}
has been fixed,
t
additional
vertices of
G_{i}^{r}
are needed. There are
q-1
such vertices since
all vertices of
G_{i}^{r}
have the form
(r-1)\mathbf{e}_{i}+\mathbf{e}_{j}
where
1\leq j\leq q
. Since there are
q
choices for
i
, there are

q{{q-1}\choose{t}}

such faces.

(2)

Faces contained in
B^{r}
: Setting
b=|B^{r}|
as above, there are

{{b}\choose{t+1}}

such faces.

(3)

Faces contained in
F_{i}^{r}
but not in
B^{r}
:
Recalling that
f=|F_{i}^{r}|
and
B^{r}\subseteq F_{i}^{r}
, there are

q\left({{f}\choose{t+1}}-{{b}\choose{t+1}}\right)

such faces.

Combining the three cases, we see that there are

q\left({{q-1}\choose{t}}+{{f}\choose{t+1}}-{{b}\choose{t+1}}\right)+{{b}\choose{t+1}}

faces of
\mathbb{L}^{r}_{q}
of
dimension
t
. Thus for
I^{r}
,

\beta_{t}(I^{r})\leq q\left({{q-1}\choose{t}}+{{f}\choose{t+1}}-{{b}\choose{t+1}}\right)+{{b}\choose{t+1}}.

In particular, if
t>q-1
and
t+1>f
, we must have
\beta_{t}(I^{r})=0
. Thus

(6.1.2)

\mathrm{pd}(I^{r})\leq\max\{q-1,f-1\}.

∎

, then
b\geq 2
and

bq-b>q
.

To see that

holds, we will show

6.1.1
) amounts to

\frac{\binom{q+r-1}{r}-q-b}{q}+b-q=\frac{\binom{q+r-1}{r}-q-b+bq-q^{2}}{q}>0\,.

It is sufficient to show the numerator is positive, so we observe the following, where the first inequality results from
bq-b>q
and the second follows since
r\geq 3
:

\displaystyle\binom{r+q-1}{q-1}-q-b+bq-q^{2}

\displaystyle>\binom{r+q-1}{q-1}-q^{2}

\displaystyle\geq\binom{q+2}{q-1}-q^{2}

\displaystyle=\frac{(q+2)(q+1)q}{6}-q^{2}=\frac{q(q-1)(q-2)}{6}>0\,.

This ends our argument.

Extremal Ideals: When does
\mathbb{L}^{r}_{q}
support a minimal resolution?

Based upon the above work, a natural question that arises is the
following: for given
r
and
q
, can one find ideals
I
for which

I^{r}
has a
minimal
free resolution supported on
\mathbb{L}^{r}_{q}
itself?
When
r=1
,
\mathbb{L}^{r}_{q}
is the Taylor complex, which one can easily see
always supports a minimal free resolution of the ideal generated by

q
variables
(x_{1},x_{2},\ldots,x_{q})
. In the case where
r>1
,

Proposition

7.11
describes exactly when the bounds for Betti numbers in

Theorem

6.1
and
Corollary

6.2
are sharp, in the sense that
there exist ideals for which equality is attained. We call the ideals
which realize these bounds
q
-
extremal ideals
and denote them by

\mathcal{E}_{q}
. In fact we can show a much stronger statement in

Theorem

7.9
: the powers
{\mathcal{E}_{q}}^{r}
have maximal Betti numbers
among the ideals
I^{r}
where
I
is generated by
q
square-free
monomials.

Definition 7.1

(
Extremal ideals).

Let
q
be a positive integer. For every set
A
with
\emptyset\neq A\subsetneq[q]
, introduce a variable
x_{A}
, and consider the
polynomial ring

S_{\mathcal{E}}=\mathsf{k}\big[x_{A}\colon\emptyset\neq A\subseteq[q]\big]

over a field
\mathsf{k}
. For each
i\in[q]
define a square-free
monomial
\epsilon_{i}
in
S_{\mathcal{E}}
as

\epsilon_{i}=\prod_{\begin{subarray}{c}A\subseteq[q]\\
i\in A\end{subarray}}x_{A}.

The square-free monomial ideal
\mathcal{E}_{q}=(\epsilon_{1},\ldots,\epsilon_{q})
is called a
\boldsymbol{q}
-extremal ideal
.

When it is unlikely to lead to confusion, we will simplify the
notation by writing
x_{1}
for
x_{\{1\}}
,
x_{12}
for
x_{\{1,2\}}
,
etc., and refer to a
q
-extremal ideal simply as an extremal ideal. The ring
S_{\mathcal{E}}
has
2^{q}-1
variables, corresponding to the
power set of
[q]
(excluding
\emptyset
), and each
\epsilon_{i}
is the
product of
2^{q-1}
variables; that is, those corresponding to the
subsets of
[q]
that contain
i
. The following example illustrates how this works for
\mathcal{E}_{4}
.

Example 7.2
.

When
q=4
, the ideal
\mathcal{E}_{4}
is generated by the monomials

\begin{array}[]{ll}\epsilon_{1}&=x_{1}x_{12}x_{13}x_{14}x_{123}x_{124}x_{134}x_{1234};\\
\epsilon_{2}&=x_{2}x_{12}x_{23}x_{24}x_{123}x_{124}x_{234}x_{1234};\\
\epsilon_{3}&=x_{3}x_{13}x_{23}x_{34}x_{123}x_{134}x_{234}x_{1234};\\
\epsilon_{4}&=x_{4}x_{14}x_{24}x_{34}x_{124}x_{134}x_{234}x_{1234}\\
\end{array}

in the polynomial ring
\mathsf{k}[x_{1},x_{2},x_{3},x_{4},x_{12},x_{13},x_{14},x_{23},x_{24},x_{34},x_{123},x_{124},x_{134},x_{234},x_{1234}]
.

Using the terminology of
[
18
]
, it naturally follows that
\mathcal{E}_{q}

is precisely the
nearly Scarf ideal
of a
(q-1)
-simplex: we see
this by matching the variable
x_{[q]\smallsetminus B}
with the face

B
of the simplex.

Following
4.1
, let
r
and
q
be positive integers and
I

an ideal generated by square-free monomials

m_{1},\ldots,m_{q}
. If
\mathbf{a}=(a_{1},\ldots,a_{q})\in\mathbb{N}^{q}
, set

\mathbf{m}^{\mathbf{a}}={m_{1}}^{a_{1}}\cdots{m_{q}}^{a_{q}}\quad\mbox{ and }\quad{\boldsymbol{\epsilon}}^{\mathbf{a}}={\epsilon_{1}}^{a_{1}}\cdots{\epsilon_{q}}^{a_{q}}.

Observe

(7.2.1)

{\boldsymbol{\epsilon}}^{\mathbf{a}}=\prod_{j\in[q]}\prod_{\begin{subarray}{c}A\subseteq[q]\\
j\in A\end{subarray}}{x_{A}}^{a_{j}}=\prod_{\emptyset\neq A\subseteq[q]}(x_{A})^{\sum_{j\in A}a_{j}}\,.

The
r^{th}
powers
I^{r}
and
{\mathcal{E}_{q}}^{r}
are generated by monomials of the
form
\mathbf{m}^{\mathbf{a}}
and
{\boldsymbol{\epsilon}}^{\mathbf{a}}
, respectively, with
\mathbf{a}\in\mathcal{N}^{r}_{q}
.
Proposition

7.3
demonstrates that for
{\mathcal{E}_{q}}^{r}
, the elements

{\boldsymbol{\epsilon}}^{\mathbf{a}}
with
\mathbf{a}\in\mathcal{N}^{r}_{q}
form a minimal generating set. In fact, all
faces of
\mathbb{L}^{r}_{q}
are necessary in
\mathbb{L}^{r}(\mathcal{E}_{q})
; i.e., none may be
removed when constructing
\mathbb{L}^{r}(\mathcal{E}_{q})
.

Proposition 7.3
.

Let
r
and
q
be positive integers, and
\mathbf{a}=(a_{1},\ldots,a_{q}),\mathbf{b}=(b_{1},\ldots,b_{q})\in\mathbb{N}^{q}
. Then

(i)

{\boldsymbol{\epsilon}}^{\mathbf{b}}\mid{\boldsymbol{\epsilon}}^{\mathbf{a}}

\iff

b_{i}\leq a_{i}
for every
i\in[q]
.

(ii)

If
\mathbf{a},\mathbf{b}\in\mathcal{N}^{r}_{q}
, then
{\boldsymbol{\epsilon}}^{\mathbf{b}}\mid{\boldsymbol{\epsilon}}^{\mathbf{a}}

\iff

\mathbf{b}=\mathbf{a}
.

In particular, if
\mathbf{a},\mathbf{b}\in\mathcal{N}^{r}_{q}
, then then
\mathbb{L}^{r}_{q}=\mathbb{L}^{r}(\mathcal{E}_{q})
.

Proof.

To prove
(i)
, use (
7.2.1
) to justify the first equivalence below.

{\boldsymbol{\epsilon}}^{\mathbf{b}}\mid{\boldsymbol{\epsilon}}^{\mathbf{a}}\iff\sum_{j\in A}b_{j}\leq\sum_{j\in A}a_{j}\quad\mbox{ for each }\quad\emptyset\neq A\subseteq[q]\iff b_{j}\leq a_{j}\quad\mbox{ for each }\quad j\in[q].

Now
(ii)
follows from
(i)
and the added assumption

b_{1}+\cdots+b_{q}=a_{1}+\cdots+a_{q}=r.

The final claim follows directly from
Definition

5.1
.
∎

In general, given the discussions above (see
also
[
13
]
), for every
r
and
q
we have

(7.3.1)

\mathbb{L}^{r}(\mathcal{E}_{q})=\mathbb{L}^{r}_{q}\subseteq\mathrm{Taylor}({\mathcal{E}_{q}}^{r}),

where we assume that every vertex
\mathbf{a}
of
\mathbb{L}^{r}_{q}

is labeled with a generator
{\boldsymbol{\epsilon}}^{\mathbf{a}}
of
{\mathcal{E}_{q}}^{r}
, and each face
\sigma
is
labeled with the lcm of the labels of its vertices, denoted
M_{\sigma}
.
The following observation will be useful when working with the monomial label
M_{\sigma}
of
\sigma\in\mathrm{Taylor}({\mathcal{E}_{q}}^{r})
.

Remark 7.4
.

Let
\sigma=\{{\boldsymbol{\epsilon}}^{\mathbf{a}_{1}},\dots,{\boldsymbol{\epsilon}}^{\mathbf{a}_{t}}\}\in\mathrm{Taylor}({\mathcal{E}_{q}}^{r})
and set
{\mathbf{a}_{i}}=(a_{i1},a_{i2},\dots,a_{iq})
for
i\in[t]
. Using
(
7.2.1
), we then have

(7.4.1)

M_{\sigma}=\mathrm{lcm}\Big(\prod_{\emptyset\neq A\subseteq[q]}(x_{A})^{\sum_{j\in A}a_{ij}}:i\in[t]\Big)=\prod_{\emptyset\neq A\subseteq[q]}(x_{A})^{\underset{1\leq i\leq t}{\max}\sum_{j\in A}a_{ij}}\,.

Furthermore, if
\mathbf{c}=(c_{1},\dots,c_{q})\in\mathcal{N}^{r}_{q}
, (
7.2.1
) and (
7.4.1
) give

(7.4.2)

{\boldsymbol{\epsilon}}^{\mathbf{c}}\mid M_{\sigma}\iff\sum_{j\in A}c_{j}\leq\underset{1\leq i\leq t}{\max}\sum_{j\in A}a_{ij}\qquad\text{for all}\quad\emptyset\neq A\subseteq[q].

We show in
Theorem

7.9
that powers of extremal ideals
attain maximal Betti numbers among powers of all square-free monomial
ideals with the same number of generators. To prove this, we first
define a ring homomorphism
S_{\mathcal{E}}\to S
and discuss its properties.

Definition 7.5

(
The ring homomorphism
\psi_{I}
).

Let
I
be an ideal of the
polynomial ring
S=\mathsf{k}[x_{1},\ldots,x_{n}]
minimally generated by
square-free monomials
m_{1},\ldots,m_{q}
. For each
k\in[n]
, set

A_{k}=\{j\in[q]\colon x_{k}\mid m_{j}\}\,.

We have thus

j\in A_{k}\iff x_{k}\mid m_{j}\,.

Define
\psi_{I}
to be the
ring homomorphism

\psi_{I}\colon S_{\mathcal{E}}\to S\quad\mbox{ where }\quad\psi_{I}(x_{A})=\begin{cases}\displaystyle\prod_{\begin{subarray}{c}k\in[n]\\
A=A_{k}\end{subarray}}x_{k}&\mbox{if }A=A_{k}\mbox{ for some
}k\in[n],\\
1&\mbox{otherwise}.\end{cases}

Before proceeding directly with our work on extremal ideals, we illustrate how the map
\psi_{I}
works in a sample case where there are four generators and seven variables.

Example 7.6
.

Let
I
be the ideal of
\mathsf{k}[x_{1},\ldots,x_{7}]
generated by the
square-free monomials

m_{1}=x_{1}x_{2}x_{5}x_{7},\quad m_{2}=x_{2}x_{3}x_{7},\quad m_{3}=x_{3}x_{4}x_{6},\quad m_{4}=x_{1}x_{4}.

Since
n=7
and
q=4
, it follows that

A_{1}=\{1,4\},\quad A_{2}=\{1,2\},\quad A_{3}=\{2,3\},\quad A_{4}=\{3,4\},\quad A_{5}=\{1\},\quad A_{6}=\{3\},\quad A_{7}=\{1,2\}.

The function

\psi_{I}:\mathsf{k}\big[x_{A}\colon\emptyset\neq A\subseteq[q]\big]\to\mathsf{k}[x_{1},\ldots,x_{7}]

maps

\begin{array}[]{llll}\psi_{I}(x_{\{1,4\}})=x_{1},&\psi_{I}(x_{\{1,2\}})=x_{2}x_{7},&\psi_{I}(x_{\{2,3\}})=x_{3},&\psi_{I}(x_{\{3,4\}})=x_{4},\\
&&&\\
\psi_{I}(x_{\{1\}})=x_{5},&\psi_{I}(x_{\{3\}})=x_{6},&\mbox{and}&\psi_{I}(x_{A})=1\,\,\mbox{otherwise}.\end{array}

Observe that

\psi_{I}(\epsilon_{1})=\psi_{I}\Big(\prod_{\begin{subarray}{c}A\subseteq[q]\\
1\in A\end{subarray}}x_{A}\Big)=\prod_{\begin{subarray}{c}A\subseteq[q]\\
1\in A\end{subarray}}\psi_{I}(x_{A})=\psi_{I}(x_{\{1,4\}})\psi_{I}(x_{\{1,2\}})\psi_{I}(x_{\{1\}})=x_{1}(x_{2}x_{7})x_{5}=m_{1}\,.

Generalizing the properties of
\psi_{I}
from the example, we arrive at the following lemma.

Lemma 7.7
.

Let
I
be an ideal of the
polynomial ring
S=\mathsf{k}[x_{1},\ldots,x_{n}]
, minimally generated by
square-free monomials
m_{1},\ldots,m_{q}
. Then

(i)

\psi_{I}({\boldsymbol{\epsilon}}^{\mathbf{a}})=\mathbf{m}^{\mathbf{a}}
for each
\mathbf{a}\in\mathcal{N}^{r}_{q}
;

(ii)

\psi_{I}({\mathcal{E}_{q}}^{r})S=I^{r}
for every
r>0
;

(iii)

\psi_{I}
preserves least
common multiples, that is:

\psi_{I}(\mathrm{lcm}({\boldsymbol{\epsilon}}^{\mathbf{a}_{1}},\dots,{\boldsymbol{\epsilon}}^{\mathbf{a}_{t}}))=\mathrm{lcm}(\mathbf{m}^{\mathbf{a}_{1}},\dots,\mathbf{m}^{\mathbf{a}_{t}})\quad\mbox{ for all }\quad\mathbf{a}_{1},\ldots,\mathbf{a}_{t}\in\mathcal{N}^{r}_{q},\quad t\geq 1.

Proof.

By

Definitions

7.1
and
7.5
, for every
j\in[q]

\psi_{I}(\epsilon_{j})=\psi_{I}\Big(\prod_{\begin{subarray}{c}A\subseteq[q]\\
j\in A\end{subarray}}x_{A}\Big)=\prod_{\begin{subarray}{c}A\subseteq[q]\\
j\in A\end{subarray}}\psi_{I}(x_{A})=\prod_{\begin{subarray}{c}A\subseteq[q]\\
j\in A\end{subarray}}\prod_{\begin{subarray}{c}k\in[n]\\
A=A_{k}\end{subarray}}x_{k}=\prod_{\begin{subarray}{c}k\in[n]\\
j\in A_{k}\end{subarray}}x_{k}=\prod_{\begin{subarray}{c}k\in[n]\\
x_{k}\mid m_{j}\end{subarray}}x_{k}=m_{j}.

It follows that
\psi_{I}({\boldsymbol{\epsilon}}^{\mathbf{a}})=\mathbf{m}^{\mathbf{a}}
for all
\mathbf{a}\in\mathcal{N}^{r}_{q}
, which
establishes
(i)
and also
(ii)
. It remains to show
(iii)
.

Set
{\mathbf{a}_{i}}=(a_{i1},a_{i2},\dots,a_{iq})
, for
i\in[t]
. Using (
7.4.1
) in the first equality below, we have:

\displaystyle\psi_{I}(\mathrm{lcm}({\boldsymbol{\epsilon}}^{\mathbf{a}_{1}},\dots,{\boldsymbol{\epsilon}}^{\mathbf{a}_{t}}))

\displaystyle=\psi_{I}\Big(\prod_{\emptyset\neq A\subseteq[q]}(x_{A})^{\underset{1\leq i\leq t}{\max}\sum_{j\in A}a_{ij}}\Big)

\displaystyle=\prod_{\emptyset\neq A\subseteq[q]}\Big(\prod_{\begin{subarray}{c}k\in[n]\\
A=A_{k}\end{subarray}}x_{k}\Big)^{\underset{1\leq i\leq t}{\max}\sum_{j\in A}a_{ij}}

\displaystyle=\prod_{k\in[n],A_{k}\neq\emptyset}(x_{k})^{\underset{1\leq i\leq t}{\max}\sum_{j\in A_{k}}a_{ij}}

\displaystyle=\mathrm{lcm}\Big(\prod_{k\in[n],A_{k}\neq\emptyset}(x_{k})^{\sum_{j\in A_{k}}a_{1j}},\dots,\prod_{k\in[n],A_{k}\neq\emptyset}(x_{k})^{\sum_{j\in A_{k}}a_{tj}}\Big)

\displaystyle=\mathrm{lcm}\Big(\prod_{j\in[q]}\prod_{\begin{subarray}{c}k\in[n]\\
j\in A_{k}\end{subarray}}(x_{k})^{a_{1j}},\dots,\prod_{j\in[q]}\prod_{\begin{subarray}{c}k\in[n]\\
j\in A_{k}\end{subarray}}(x_{k})^{a_{tj}}\Big)

\displaystyle=\mathrm{lcm}\Big(\prod_{j\in[q]}m_{j}^{a_{1j}},\dots,\prod_{j\in[q]}m_{j}^{a_{tj}}\Big)

\displaystyle=\mathrm{lcm}(\mathbf{m}^{\mathbf{a}_{1}},\dots,\mathbf{m}^{\mathbf{a}_{t}}).

∎

Theorem

7.9
below demonstrates why the ideals from

Definition

7.5
are called
extremal
: they have the
greatest Betti numbers among all ideals minimally generated by
q

square-free monomials. The following lemma provides the technical
preliminaries necessary for the proof of
Theorem

7.9
.

Lemma 7.8
.

Let
I
be an ideal minimally generated by
q
square-free monomials in a polynomial ring
\mathsf{k}[x_{1},\ldots,x_{n}]
. With notation as in
Definition

7.5
,
if
S
is viewed as an
S_{\mathcal{E}}
-module via the ring homomorphism
\psi_{I}\colon S_{\mathcal{E}}\to S
, then

S_{\mathcal{E}}/{\mathcal{E}_{q}}^{r}\otimes_{S_{\mathcal{E}}}S\cong S/I^{r}\quad\text{and}\quad\mathrm{Tor}_{i}^{S_{\mathcal{E}}}(S_{\mathcal{E}}/{\mathcal{E}_{q}}^{r},S)=0\quad\text{for all $i>0$}.

Proof.

First, note that

\frac{S_{\mathcal{E}}}{{\mathcal{E}_{q}}^{r}}\otimes_{S_{\mathcal{E}}}S\cong\frac{S}{({\mathcal{E}_{q}}^{r})S}=\frac{S}{\psi_{I}({\mathcal{E}_{q}}^{r})S}=\frac{S}{I^{r}}\,.

To compute
\mathrm{Tor}_{i}^{S_{\mathcal{E}}}(S_{\mathcal{E}}/{\mathcal{E}_{q}}^{r},S)
, use the Taylor
complex
\mathrm{Taylor}({\mathcal{E}_{q}}^{r})
, which supports a free resolution
\mathbb{F}

of
S_{\mathcal{E}}/{\mathcal{E}_{q}}^{r}
; see (
2.4.1
) for a description of the
differentials of
\mathbb{F}
. Since the homomorphism
\psi_{I}

changes the labels
{\boldsymbol{\epsilon}}^{\mathbf{a}}
to the labels
\mathbf{m}^{\mathbf{a}}
and preserves least
common multiples, the chain complex
\mathbb{F}\otimes_{S_{\mathcal{E}}}S
is
isomorphic to a homogenization of the chain complex associated to the
simplex with vertices corresponding to
\mathbf{a}\in\mathcal{N}^{r}_{q}
and labeled with
the monomials
\mathbf{m}^{\mathbf{a}}
. This is a version of the Taylor resolution of

S/I^{r}
defined starting with a possibly non-minimal generating set
of
I^{r}
. Such a non-minimal version is a free resolution of
S/I^{r}

as well, hence
\mathrm{Tor}_{i}^{S_{\mathcal{E}}}(S_{\mathcal{E}}/{\mathcal{E}_{q}}^{r},S)=0
when
i>0
. (See

Remark

2.6
.)
∎

Theorem 7.9

(
Powers of extremal ideals have maximal Betti numbers).

Given positive integers
r
and
q
,

\beta_{i}^{S}(I^{r})\leq\beta_{i}^{S_{\mathcal{E}}}({\mathcal{E}_{q}}^{r})

for any
i\geq 0
and any
ideal
I
minimally generated by
q
square-free monomials in a polynomial ring

S
.

Proof.

Let
\mathbb{F}
be a minimal free resolution of
S_{\mathcal{E}}/{\mathcal{E}_{q}}^{r}
over
S_{\mathcal{E}}
. Then
Lemma

7.8
shows that
\mathbb{F}\otimes_{S_{\mathcal{E}}}S
is a free resolution of
S/I^{r}
over
S
. Consequently,

\beta_{i}^{S_{\mathcal{E}}}({\mathcal{E}_{q}}^{r})=\text{rank}_{S_{\mathcal{E}}}(\mathbb{F}_{i+1})=\text{rank}_{S}(\mathbb{F}_{i+1}\otimes_{S_{\mathcal{E}}}S)\geq\beta_{i}^{S}(I^{r}).

∎

In view of
Theorem

7.9
, the homogenized chain complex of any (cell) complex that supports a minimal free resolution of
{\mathcal{E}_{q}}^{r}
can be thought of as an upper bound for the minimal free resolution of the
r^{th}
power of any ideal minimally generated by
q
square-free monomials.
Proposition

7.10
establishes when our simplicial complex
\mathbb{L}^{r}_{q}
supports a minimal free resolution of
{\mathcal{E}_{q}}^{r}
.

Proposition 7.10

(
When
\mathbb{L}^{r}_{q}
supports a minimal free resolution of
{\mathcal{E}_{q}}^{r}
).

Let
r
and
q
be positive integers. The following statements are equivalent:

(1)

The simplicial complex
\mathbb{L}^{r}_{q}

supports a minimal free resolution of the ideal
{\mathcal{E}_{q}}^{r}
.

(2)

\mathrm{pd}_{S_{\mathcal{E}}}({\mathcal{E}_{q}}^{r})=\dim\mathbb{L}^{r}_{q}
.

(3)

One of the following conditions holds:

•

q=1
and
r\geq 1
;

•

q=2
and
1\leq r\leq 4
;

•

q\geq 3
and
1\leq r\leq 2
.

Proof.

(1)
\implies
(2): This implication is clear.

(2)
\implies
(3): Assume that
\mathrm{pd}_{S_{\mathcal{E}}}({\mathcal{E}_{q}}^{r})=\dim\mathbb{L}^{r}_{q}
and the values of
r
and
q
do not satisfy the conditions in (3). In particular, we must have
q\geq 2
and
r\geq 5
, or
q\geq 3
and
r=3,4
. By
Remark

6.4
and
Equation

6.1.1
, for all
i,j\in[q]
,

(7.10.1)

|F_{i}^{r}|=f>q=|G_{j}^{r}|

and so
F_{1}^{r},\ldots,F_{q}^{r}
are the facets of
\mathbb{L}^{r}_{q}
of highest dimension, with the caveat that
F_{1}^{r}=\cdots=F_{q}^{r}
when
r=3=q
, and thus
\dim\mathbb{L}^{r}_{q}=f-1
. Since we assumed
\mathrm{pd}_{S_{\mathcal{E}}}({\mathcal{E}_{q}}^{r})=\dim\mathbb{L}^{r}_{q}
, we have
\mathrm{pd}_{S_{\mathcal{E}}}({\mathcal{E}_{q}}^{r})=f-1
as well. Let
\mathbb{F}
denote the free resolution supported on
\mathbb{L}^{r}_{q}
, and let
\partial
denote its differential, which is described in (
2.4.1
). Since
\mathrm{pd}_{S_{\mathcal{E}}}({\mathcal{E}_{q}}^{r})=f-1
and a minimal free resolution of
{\mathcal{E}_{q}}^{r}
is isomorphic to a direct summand of
\mathbb{F}
, there must exist a basis element
e\in\mathbb{F}_{f-1}\smallsetminus(S_{\mathcal{E}})_{\geqslant 1}\mathbb{F}_{f-1}
such that

(7.10.2)
