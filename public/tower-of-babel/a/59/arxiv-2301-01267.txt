Optimal convergence rates in stochastic homogenization in a balanced random environment
Xiaoqin Guo, Hung V. Tran

Abstract
We consider random walks in a uniformly elliptic, balanced, i.i.d. random environment in ℤ d \mathbb{Z}^{d} for d ≥ 2 d\geq 2 . We first derive a quantitative law of large numbers for the invariant measure, which is nearly optimal. A mixing property of the field of the invariant measure is then achieved. We next obtain rates of convergence for the homogenization of the Dirichlet problem for non-divergence form difference operators, which are generically optimal for d ≥ 3 d\geq 3 and nearly optimal when d = 2 d=2 . Furthermore, we establish the existence, stationarity and uniqueness properties of the corrector problem for all dimensions d ≥ 2 d\geq 2 . Afterwards, we quantify the ergodicity of the environmental process for both the continuous-time and discrete-time random walks, and as a consequence, we get explicit convergence rates for the quenched central limit theorem of the balanced random walk.

Introduction

In this paper, we consider random walks in a uniformly elliptic, balanced, i.i.d. random environment in
\mathbb{Z}^{d}
for
d\geq 2
.
Our main goals are two-fold.
Firstly, we derive a quantitative large-scale average of the invariant measure, which is nearly optimal, in Theorem
6
.
A mixing property of the field of the invariant measure is achieved.
Secondly, we obtain rates of convergence for the homogenization of the Dirichlet problem in Theorem
8
.
When
d\geq 3
, the convergence rate is
O(R^{-1})
, which is generically optimal.
Afterwards, we quantify the ergodicity of the environmental process for both the continuous- and discrete-time random walks in Theorem
10
, and as a consequence, we get explicit convergence rates for the quenched central limit theorem (QCLT) of the balanced random walk.

1.1
Settings

Let
\mathbb{S}_{d\times d}
denote the set of
d\times d
positive-definite diagonal matrices. A map

\omega:\mathbb{Z}^{d}\to\mathbb{S}_{d\times d}

is called an
environment
. We denote the set of all environments by
\Omega
. Let
\mathbb{P}
be a probability measure on
\Omega
so that

\left\{\omega(x)=\mathrm{diag}[\omega_{1}(x),\ldots,\omega_{d}(x)],x\in\mathbb{Z}^{d}\right\}

are i.i.d. under
\mathbb{P}
. Expectation with respect to
\mathbb{P}
is denoted by
\mathbb{E}
or
E_{\mathbb{P}}
.

Definition 1
.

Let
\{e_{1},\ldots,e_{d}\}
be the canonical basis for
\mathbb{R}^{d}
. Let
U=\{e\in\mathbb{Z}^{d}:|e|_{2}=1\}
be the set of unit vectors.
Define the difference operators
\nabla=(\nabla_{e})_{e\in U}
, and

\nabla^{2}=(\nabla^{2}_{i})_{i=1}^{d}
by

\nabla_{e}u(x)=u(x+e)-u(x),\quad\nabla_{i}^{2}u(x)=u(x+e_{i})+u(x-e_{i})-2u(x).

(1)

Note that
\nabla,\nabla^{2}
are linear operators.
We also write, for
e,\ell\in U
,

\nabla_{e,\ell}^{2}=-\nabla_{e}\nabla_{\ell}.

For
r>0
,
y\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
we let

\mathbb{B}_{r}(y)=\left\{x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}:|x-y|<r\right\},\quad B_{r}(y)=\mathbb{B}_{r}(y)\cap\mathbb{Z}^{d}

denote the continuous and discrete balls with center
y
and radius
r
, respectively.
When
y=0
, we also write

\mathbb{B}_{r}=\mathbb{B}_{r}(0)
and
B_{r}=B_{r}(0)
.
For any
B\subset\mathbb{Z}^{d}
, its
discrete boundary
is defined as

\partial B:=\left\{z\in\mathbb{Z}^{d}\setminus B:\textnormal{dist}(z,x)=1\text{ for some }x\in B\right\}.

Let
\bar{B}=B\cup\partial B
. By abuse of notations, whenever confusion does not occur, we also use
\partial A
and
\bar{A}
to denote the usual continuous boundary and closure of
A\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}
, respectively.

For
x\in\mathbb{Z}^{d}
, a
spatial shift

\theta_{x}:\Omega\to\Omega
is defined by

(\theta_{x}\omega)(\cdot)=\omega(x+\cdot).

In a random environment
\omega\in\Omega
, we consider the discrete elliptic Dirichlet problem

\left\{\begin{array}[]{lr}\tfrac{1}{2}{\rm tr}\big(\omega(x)\nabla^{2}u(x)\big)=\frac{1}{R^{2}}f\left(\tfrac{x}{R}\right)\zeta(\theta_{x}\omega)&x\in B_{R},\\[5.0pt]
u(x)=g\left(\tfrac{x}{|x|}\right)&x\in\partial B_{R},\end{array}\right.

(2)

where
f\in\mathbb{R}^{\mathbb{B}_{1}},g\in\mathbb{R}^{\partial\mathbb{B}_{1}}
are functions with good regularity properties and
\zeta\in\mathbb{R}^{\Omega}
satisfies suitable integrability conditions (A special case is
\zeta\equiv 1
). Stochastic homogenization studies (for
\mathbb{P}
-almost all
\omega
) the convergence of
u
to the solution
\bar{u}
of a deterministic
effective equation

\left\{\begin{array}[]{lr}\tfrac{1}{2}{\rm tr}(\bar{a}D^{2}\bar{u})=f\bar{\psi}&\text{ in }\mathbb{B}_{1},\\
\bar{u}=g&\text{ on }\partial\mathbb{B}_{1},\end{array}\right.

(3)

as
R\to\infty
. Here
D^{2}\bar{u}
denotes the Hessian matrix of
\bar{u}
and
\bar{a}=\bar{a}(\mathbb{P})\in\mathbb{S}_{d\times d}
and
\bar{\psi}=\bar{\psi}(\mathbb{P},\zeta)\in\mathbb{R}
are
deterministic
and do not depend on the realization of the random environment (see the statement of Proposition
C
for formulas for
\bar{a}
and
\bar{\psi}
).

The
non-divergence form
difference equation (
2
) is used to describe random walks in a random environment (RWRE) in
\mathbb{Z}^{d}
. To be specific,
we set

\omega(x,x\pm e_{i}):=\frac{\omega_{i}(x)}{2{\rm tr}\omega(x)}\quad\text{ for }i=1,\ldots d,

(4)

and
\omega(x,y)=0
if
|x-y|\neq 1
. Namely, we normalize
\omega
to get a transition probability. We remark that the configuration of
\{\omega(x,y):x,y\in\mathbb{Z}^{d}\}
is also called a
balanced environment
in the literature
[
42
,
34
,
10
]
.

Definition 2
.

For each fixed
\omega\in\Omega
, the random walk
(X_{n})_{n\geq 0}
in the environment
\omega
with
X_{0}=x
is a Markov chain in
\mathbb{Z}^{d}
with transition probability
P_{\omega}^{x}
specified by

P_{\omega}^{x}\left(X_{n+1}=z|X_{n}=y\right)=\omega(y,z).

(5)

The expectation with respect to
P_{\omega}^{x}
is written as
E_{\omega}^{x}
. When the starting point of the random walk is
0
, we sometimes omit the superscript and simply write
P_{\omega}^{0},E_{\omega}^{0}
as
P_{\omega}
and
E_{\omega}
, respectively. Notice that for random walks
(X_{n})
in an environment
\omega
,

\bar{\omega}^{i}=\theta_{X_{i}}\omega\in\Omega,\quad i\geq 0,

(6)

is also a Markov chain, called the
environment viewed from the particle
process. By abuse of notation, we enlarge our probability space so that
P_{\omega}
still denotes the joint law of the random walks and
(\bar{\omega}^{i})_{i\geq 0}
.

We also consider the
continuous-time
RWRE
(Y_{t})
on
\mathbb{Z}^{d}
. Set, for
\omega\in\Omega
,

a(x)=a_{\omega}(x):=\frac{\omega(x)}{{\rm tr}\omega(x)}={\rm diag}[2\omega(x,x+e_{1}),\ldots,2\omega(x,x+e_{d})],\quad x\in\mathbb{Z}^{d},

(7)

and write the
i
-th diagonal entry of
a(x)
as
a_{i}(x)=\frac{\omega_{i}(x)}{{\rm tr}\omega(x)}
.

Definition 3
.

Let
(Y_{t})_{t\geq 0}
be the Markov process on
\mathbb{Z}^{d}
with generator

L_{\omega}u(x)=\sum_{y}\omega(x,y)[u(y)-u(x)]=\tfrac{1}{2}{\rm tr}(a(x)\nabla^{2}u).

(8)

By abuse of notation, we also denote by
P_{\omega}^{x}
the quenched law of
(Y_{t})
. If there is no ambiguity from the context, we also write, for
x,y\in\mathbb{Z}^{d}
,
n\in\mathbb{Z},t\in\mathbb{R}
, the transition kernels of the discrete and continuous time walks as

p_{n}^{\omega}(x,y)=P_{\omega}^{x}(X_{n}=y),\quad\text{ and }\quad p_{t}^{\omega}(x,y)=P_{\omega}^{x}(Y_{t}=y),

respectively.

In the paper, we use

\kappa

if

, and

We are moreover interested in characterizing the invariant measure
\mathbb{Q}

1.4
Main results

The field
\{\rho_{\omega}(x):x\in\mathbb{Z}^{d}\}
of the invariant measure, which governs the long term behavior of the diffusion and which determines the effective PDE, plays a central role in the theory of homogenization of non-divergence form equations.

We first obtain a rate of convergence of the average
\rho_{\omega}(B_{R})/|B_{R}|
of the invariant measure to
1
as
R\to\infty
.

Theorem 6
.

Assume
(A1), (A2)
.
For any
d\geq 2,p\in(0,0.5)
,
t>0
and
R\geq 2
,

P\left(\Bigr\lvert\tfrac{\rho_{\omega}(B_{R})}{|B_{R}|}-1\Bigl\rvert\geq tR^{-d/2}\log R\right)\leq C_{p}\exp(-ct^{p}).

Note that the rate
R^{-d/2}\log R
is very close to the size
R^{-d/2}
of the diffusive scaling. In other words, to some extent the field
(\rho_{\omega}(x))_{x\in\mathbb{Z}^{d}}
behaves quite similarly to i.i.d. random variables. Hence, we expect the rate
R^{-d/2}\log R
obtained here to be either optimal or nearly optimal.
For non-divergence form PDEs, the volume-doubling property for the measure
\rho_{\omega}(\cdot)
was proved by Bauman
[
7
]
.
An algebraic convergence rate
R^{-\gamma}
for some
\gamma\in(0,1)
was proved recently by Armstrong, Fehrman, Lin
[
2
, Theorem 1.4]
.

In the course of obtaining our homogenization results in this paper, sensitivity estimates together with an Efron-Stein type inequality are used to control fluctuations of a random field around its mean. This method was used in the stochastic homogenization of divergence-form operators, e.g.,
[
45
,
26
]
. To facilitate this strategy, obtaining sensitivity estimates (with respect to the change of the environment) is crucial, and
C^{1,1}
estimates for the random equation is necessary, cf. e.g.,
[
26
,
3
]
.
To obtain
C^{1,1}
regularity for the heterogeneous equation, we follow the idea of Armstrong, Lin
[
3
]
who generalized the compactness argument of Avellaneda, Lin
[
5
]
to the random non-divergence form setting.

The key observation in the proof of Theorem
6
is explained as follows.
Although the invariant measure
\rho_{\omega}(x)
does not have an explicit expression, it can be interpreted as the long term frequency of visits to location
x
. Hence, modifying the local value of the environment is related to the Green function of the RWRE. Guided by this intuition, we will obtain a formula for the sensitivity estimate of the invariant measure in terms of the Green function.

As indicated in Theorem
6
, the field
\{\rho_{\omega}(x):x\in\mathbb{Z}^{d}\}
is expected to have weak enough correlation so that the behavior of its mean fluctuation over
B_{R}
resembles (up to a logarithmic factor) that of i.i.d. random variables. The following proposition reveals the localization and correlation properties of the invariant measure.

Proposition 7
.

Assume
(A1), (A2)
.

(i)

There exists a random variable
\mathscr{Y}>0
with
\mathbb{E}[\mathscr{Y}^{p}]<C_{p}
,
\forall p<2/5
, such that for any
x\in\mathbb{Z}^{d},r\geq 1
, letting
\rho_{r}(x)=\rho_{r,\omega}(x):=\mathbb{E}[\rho_{\omega}(x)|\omega(y):y\in B_{r}(x)]
,

\lvert\rho(x)-\rho_{r}(x)\rvert\leq\left\{\begin{array}[]{lr}\mathscr{Y}r^{-1}\log r,&d=2\\
\mathscr{Y}r^{-d/2},&d\geq 3.\end{array}\right.

(ii)

For any
x,y\in\mathbb{Z}^{d}
with
x\neq y
,

\left|{\rm Cov}_{\mathbb{P}}(\rho(x),\rho(y))\right|\lesssim\left\{\begin{array}[]{lr}|x-y|^{-2}[\log(1+|x-y|)]^{3}&d=2\\
|x-y|^{-d}\log(1+|x-y|)&d\geq 3.\end{array}\right.

This is perhaps the first characterization of the correlation structure of the invariant measure (with algebraic mixing rates) for the non-divergence form operator
{\rm tr}(\omega\nabla^{2}u)
in a random environment.

Next, we derive rates of convergence for the stochastic homogenization of the Dirichlet problem (
2
) for non-divergence form difference operators.

Theorem 8
.

Assume
(A1), (A2)
.
Suppose
f,g
are both in
C^{4}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
, and
\zeta
is a measurable function of
\omega(0)

To prove Theorem

As evident from the two-scale expansion (Lemma

[

2
Large scale
C^{0,1}
and
C^{1,1}
estimates

In this section, we apply the ideas of Avellaneda, Lin
[
5
,
6
]
in the periodic setting to the discrete random setting.
The key idea is quite intuitive and natural: large-scale solutions of
L_{\omega}u(x)=\psi(\theta_{x}\omega)+f(x)
are well-approximated by those of the homogenized equation with an algebraic rate thanks to Proposition
C
.
As the latter are harmonic, they possess rather nice estimates (see Proposition
19
below on the scaling property.)
Therefore, by iterations, scalings and the triangle inequalities, the better regularity of the homogenized equation is inherited by the heterogeneous equation.
It is crucial to note two points here.
Firstly, in each iteration step, the scaling is done by using the nice estimates in Proposition
19
of the homogenized limit, and the triangle inequality and Proposition
C
are used to pass this estimate to the solution
u
of the heterogeneous equation.
Secondly, in the random setting, one can only go down to radii greater than the homogenization radius in the iterations, which therefore gives us only large scale estimates.
The generalization of this idea to the random non-divergence form PDE setting was first done by Armstrong, Lin
[
3
]
who made the observation that an algebraic rate is sufficient for such an iteration.

The main result in this section, Theorem
16
, can be considered as a discrete version of Armstrong, Lin
[
3
, Theorem 3.1, Corollary 3.4]
in terms of the large scale
C^{0,1}
and
C^{1,1}
regularity. We remark that for
\omega
-harmonic functions in a genuinely
d
-dimensional balanced environment, a
C^{0,1-\varepsilon}
regularity was achieved by Berger, Cohen, Deuschel, Guo
[
9
, Corollary 1.4]
using coupling arguments.

As can be seen in the following Subsection
2.1
, this sort of compactness argument, although is applied to the random setting here, is deterministic in its core.

2.1
Some regularity properties of deterministic functions

This subsection contains the key tools for the compactness arguments used in our paper. It is completely deterministic and can be read independently of other parts of the paper. The lemmas presented here concern large scale
C^{k,1}
,
k\geq 0
, properties of deterministic functions.

For any function
f
on a set
A
and
\alpha\in(0,1]
, define

\osc_{A}f:=\sup_{x,y\in A}|f(x)-f(y)|,\qquad[f]_{\alpha;A}=\sup_{x,y\in A,x\neq y}\frac{|f(x)-f(y)|}{|x-y|^{\alpha}},

and, if
A
is a finite set, for
p\in(0,\infty)
, we define

\lVert f\rVert_{p;A}:=\left(\frac{1}{\#A}\sum_{x\in A}|f|^{p}\right)^{1/p},\quad\lVert f\rVert_{\infty;A}=\max_{x\in A}|f(x)|.

For any
j\geq 0
, let
\mathrm{H}_{j}
denote the set of
j
-th order polynomials, with
\mathrm{H}_{0}=\mathbb{R}
. In fact, in our paper we will only use the cases
j=0,1,2
.

Define, for function
f:\mathbb{R}^{d}\to\mathbb{R}
and a bounded set
A\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}
,
j\geq 1
,

{\mathcal{D}}_{A}^{j}(f)=\inf_{p\in\mathrm{H}_{j-1}}\sup_{A}|f-p|=\frac{1}{2}\inf_{p\in\mathrm{H}_{j-1}}\osc_{A}(f-p).

(22)

{\mathcal{D}}_{A}^{j}
satisfies the triangle inequality. Namely,
{\mathcal{D}}_{A}^{j}(f\pm g)\leq{\mathcal{D}}_{A}^{j}(f)+{\mathcal{D}}_{A}^{j}(g)
.
When
A=B_{R}
is the discrete ball,
R>0
, we simply write

{\mathcal{D}}_{R}^{j}:={\mathcal{D}}_{B_{R}}^{j}.

Note that for
j\geq 1
, the above term normalized

{\mathbb{D}}_{R}^{j}(f):=\frac{{\mathcal{D}}_{R}^{j}(f)}{R^{j}}

(23)

is a large scale analogue of the
j
-th order derivative.

For any
r>0
,
\theta\in(0,\tfrac{1}{3})
, define a sequence of exponentially increasing radii
(r_{k})_{k\geq 0}
by

r_{k}=r_{k}(r,\theta):=\theta^{-k}r,\quad k\geq 0.

The following elementary lemma confirms the intuition that “the integral of the
(j+1)
-th derivative is the
j
-th derivative”.

Lemma 13
.

For any function
f:\mathbb{Z}^{d}\to\mathbb{R}
and
r>0,\theta\in(0,\tfrac{1}{3}),n\in\mathbb{N},j\geq 1
,

{\mathbb{D}}_{r_{0}}^{j}(f)\leq{\mathbb{D}}_{r_{n}}^{j}(f)+3\theta^{-j}\sum_{k=0}^{n}r_{k}{\mathbb{D}}_{r_{k}}^{j+1}(f).

Proof.

For any
j
-th order homogeneous polynomials
p,q\in\mathrm{H}_{j}
,
R>r>0
, by the triangle inequality,

\displaystyle{\mathcal{D}}_{r}^{j}(p)

\displaystyle\leq{\mathcal{D}}_{r}^{j}(q)+{\mathcal{D}}_{r}^{j}(p-q)

\displaystyle\leq(\tfrac{r}{R})^{j}{\mathcal{D}}_{R}^{j}(q)+{\mathcal{D}}_{r}^{j}(f-p)+{\mathcal{D}}_{r}^{j}(f-q)

where in the second inequality we used the fact that
{\mathcal{D}}_{r}^{j}(q)=(\tfrac{r}{R})^{j}{\mathcal{D}}_{R}^{j}(q)
for all
j
-th order homogeneous polynomial
q
. Hence, by the inequality above,

\displaystyle{\mathcal{D}}_{r}^{j}(f)

\displaystyle\leq{\mathcal{D}}_{r}^{j}(f-p)+{\mathcal{D}}_{r}^{j}(p)

\displaystyle\leq 2{\mathcal{D}}_{r}^{j}(f-p)+{\mathcal{D}}_{r}^{j}(f-q)+(\tfrac{r}{R})^{j}{\mathcal{D}}_{R}^{j}(q)

\displaystyle\leq 2{\mathcal{D}}_{r}^{j}(f-p)+{\mathcal{D}}_{r}^{j}(f-q)+(\tfrac{r}{R})^{j}[{\mathcal{D}}_{R}^{j}(f)+{\mathcal{D}}_{R}^{j}(f-q)]

\displaystyle\leq 2[{\mathcal{D}}_{r}^{j}(f-p)+{\mathcal{D}}_{R}^{j}(f-q)]+(\tfrac{r}{R})^{j}{\mathcal{D}}_{R}^{j}(f).

Taking infimum over all
j
-th order homogeneous polynomials
p,q\in\mathrm{H}_{j}
, we get

{\mathcal{D}}_{r}^{j}(f)\leq 2[{\mathcal{D}}_{r}^{j+1}(f)+{\mathcal{D}}_{R}^{j+1}(f)]+(\tfrac{r}{R})^{j}{\mathcal{D}}_{R}^{j}(f).

Replacing
r,R
by
r_{k},r_{k+1}
, and using notation (
23
), the above inequality yields

{\mathbb{D}}_{r_{k}}^{j}(f)-{\mathbb{D}}_{r_{k+1}}^{j}(f)\leq 2[r_{k}{\mathbb{D}}_{r_{k}}^{j+1}(f)+\theta^{-j}r_{k+1}{\mathbb{D}}_{r_{k+1}}^{j+1}(f)].

Summing both sides over
k=0,\ldots,n-1
, the lemma is proved.
∎

The following lemma will be crucially employed later in our derivation of large scale regularity estimates in Subsection
2.2
.

Lemma 14
.

Let
j\geq 1
,
m\in\mathbb{N}
,
r,\alpha>0
. Let
A_{r}\geq 0
be a constant depending on
r
. If for
f:\mathbb{Z}^{d}\to\mathbb{R}
,
k=0,\ldots,m-1
, and all
\theta\in(0,\tfrac{1}{3})
,

{\mathcal{D}}_{r_{k}}^{j+1}(f)\lesssim_{j}\theta^{j+1}{\mathcal{D}}_{r_{k+1}}^{j+1}(f)+r_{k+1}^{-\alpha}{\mathcal{D}}_{r_{k+1}}^{j}(f)+r_{k+1}^{j}A_{r_{k+1}},

(24)

then there exist
\theta=\theta(j),N=N(j,\alpha)
such that, for
N\leq r\leq R\leq r_{m}
,

{\mathcal{D}}_{r}^{j}(f)\leq 13\theta^{-2j}\left(\tfrac{r}{R}\right)^{j}{\mathcal{D}}_{R}^{j}(f)+\sum_{k\geq 1:r_{k}\leq R}A_{r_{k}}.

Proof.

For the simplicity of notations, we suppress the dependency on
f
. Let
n=n(R,\theta)\leq m
be such that
r_{n}\leq R<r_{n+1}
. Display (
24
) is equivalent to

r_{k}{\mathbb{D}}_{r_{k}}^{j+1}\lesssim_{j}\theta r_{k+1}{\mathbb{D}}_{r_{k+1}}^{j+1}+\theta^{-j}r_{k+1}^{-\alpha}{\mathbb{D}}_{r_{k+1}}^{j}+\theta^{-j}A_{r_{k+1}}.

Summing this inequality over

\sum_{k=0}^{n-1}r_{k}{\mathbb{D}}_{r_{k}}^{j+1}\lesssim_{j}\theta\sum_{k=1}^{n}r_{k}{\mathbb{D}}_{r_{k}}^{j+1}+\theta^{-j}\sum_{k=1}^{n}r_{k}^{-\alpha}{\mathbb{D}}_{r_{k}}^{j}+\theta^{-j}\sum_{k=1}^{n}A_{r_{k}}.

(25)

Moreover, by Lemma

,
{\mathbb{D}}_{r_{k}}^{j}\leq{\mathbb{D}}_{r_{n}}^{j}+3\theta^{-j}\sum_{\ell=k}^{n}r_{\ell}{\mathbb{D}}_{r_{\ell}}^{j+1}
. Hence

\displaystyle\sum_{k=1}^{n}r_{k}^{-\alpha}{\mathbb{D}}_{r_{k}}^{j}

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{k=1}^{n}r_{k}^{-\alpha}\left({\mathbb{D}}_{r_{n}}^{j}+3\theta^{-j}\sum_{\ell=k}^{n}r_{\ell}{\mathbb{D}}_{r_{\ell}}^{j+1}\right)

\displaystyle\leq C_{\alpha}r^{-\alpha}{\mathbb{D}}_{r_{n}}^{j}+C_{\alpha}\theta^{-j}r^{-\alpha}\sum_{\ell=1}^{n}r_{\ell}{\mathbb{D}}_{r_{\ell}}^{j+1},

(26)

where
C_{\alpha}=1-3^{-\alpha}
. Choosing
\theta=\theta(j)\in(0,\tfrac{1}{3})
sufficiently small,
when
r\geq N
for some

, we get from (

) that

\sum_{k=0}^{n-1}r_{k}{\mathbb{D}}_{r_{k}}^{j+1}\leq\frac{1}{2}\sum_{k=1}^{n}r_{k}{\mathbb{D}}_{r_{k}}^{j+1}+{\mathbb{D}}_{r_{n}}^{j}+C_{j}\theta^{-j}\sum_{k=1}^{n}A_{r_{k}}

3
Mixing properties of the invariant measure

The goal of this section is to investigate the mixing properties of the field
\{\rho_{\omega}(x):x\in\mathbb{Z}^{d}\}
of the invariant measure.
We will obtain a rate of convergence (Theorem
6
) of the average of the invariant measure over balls
B_{R}
. We will also quantify the correlation of the field (Proposition
7
).

The Efron-Stein inequality (
38
) of Boucheron, Bousquet, and Massart
[
14
]
will be used in our derivation of quantitative estimates.

Let
\omega^{\prime}(x),x\in\mathbb{Z}^{d}
, be i.i.d. copies of
\omega(x),x\in\mathbb{Z}^{d}
. For any
y\in\mathbb{Z}^{d}
, let
\omega^{\prime}_{y}\in\Omega
be the environment such that

\omega^{\prime}_{y}(x)=\left\{\begin{array}[]{lr}&\omega(x)\quad\text{ if }x\neq y,\\
&\omega^{\prime}(y)\quad\text{ if }x=y.\end{array}\right.

That is,
\omega^{\prime}_{y}
is a modification of
\omega
only at location
y
.
For any measurable function
Z
of the environment
\omega
, we write, for
y\in\mathbb{Z}^{d}
,

Z_{y}^{\prime}=Z(\omega_{y}^{\prime}),\quad\partial^{\prime}_{y}Z(\omega)=Z^{\prime}_{y}-Z,

(36)

and set

V(Z)=\sum_{y\in\mathbb{Z}^{d}}(\partial^{\prime}_{y}Z)^{2}.

(37)

With abuse of notations, we enlarge the probability space and still use
\mathbb{P}
to denote the distribution of both
\omega,\omega^{\prime}
.

The
L_{p}
version of Efron-Stein inequality in
[
14
, Theorem 3]
states that

\mathbb{E}[|Z-\mathbb{E}Z|^{q}]\leq Cq^{q/2}\mathbb{E}[V^{q/2}]\quad\text{ for }q\geq 2.

(38)

The following variation of (
38
) will be useful in our paper.

Lemma 21
.

Let
p>0
.
Let
Z
be a measurable function of the environment. Assume that there exist
f\in(0,\infty)^{\mathbb{Z}^{d}}
with
F=[\sum_{y\in\mathbb{Z}^{d}}f(y)^{2}]^{1/2}<\infty
and a random variable
\mathscr{X}>0
with
\mathbb{E}[\exp(c\mathscr{X}^{p})]<\infty
such that

\mathbb{E}[\lvert\partial^{\prime}_{y}Z/f(y)\rvert^{n})]\lesssim\mathbb{E}[\mathscr{X}^{n}]
for all
y\in\mathbb{Z}^{d}
,
n\geq 1
. Then there exists
C=C(p)>0
such that

\mathbb{E}\left[\exp\big(C|F^{-1}(Z-\mathbb{E}Z)|^{2p/(2+p)}\big)\right]\lesssim E[\exp(c\mathscr{X}^{p})].

The proof uses the fact that for
\alpha\in[0,1)
, there exists
c=c(\alpha)>0
such that

\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\frac{c^{n}}{n!}x^{n}n^{\alpha n}\leq\exp(x^{1/(1-\alpha)})\quad\text{ for all }x>0.

(39)

Indeed, when
x>0
, putting
c=e^{-\alpha}/2
and using inequality
\tfrac{n^{n}}{n!}\leq e^{n}
,

\displaystyle\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\frac{c^{n}}{n!}x^{n}n^{\alpha n}\leq\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}2^{-n}\frac{x^{n}}{(n!)^{1-\alpha}}=\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}2^{-n}\big(\frac{x^{n/(1-\alpha)}}{n!}\big)^{1-\alpha}\leq\exp(x^{1/(1-\alpha)}),

where we used
\tfrac{y^{n}}{n!}\leq e^{y}
for
y\geq 0
in the last inequality.

Proof.

Set
\mathscr{X}_{y}:=\lvert\partial^{\prime}_{y}Z/f(y)\rvert
.
By Jensen’s inequality, for any
q\geq 2
,

V^{q/2}=F^{q}\big(\sum_{y}\mathscr{X}_{y}^{2}\frac{f(y)^{2}}{F^{2}}\big)^{q/2}\leq F^{q}\sum_{y}\mathscr{X}_{y}^{q}\frac{f(y)^{2}}{F^{2}}.

Taking expectations on both sides, and using (
38
), we get, for
q\geq 2
,

\mathbb{E}\left[|(Z-\mathbb{E}Z)/F|^{q}\right]\lesssim q^{q/2}\mathbb{E}[\mathscr{X}^{q}].

The lemma then follows by using the fact (
39
).
∎

3.1
A sensitivity estimate of the invariant measure

The main contribution of this subsection is a formula for the “vertical derivative” of the invariant measure
\rho
.

Definition 22
.

For
t>0
, we let
V(t,\omega)=\sum_{x\in\mathbb{Z}^{d}}p_{t}^{\omega}(x,0)
. Let
\mathbb{Q}_{t}
be the probability measure on
\Omega

\mathbb{Q}_{t}(\mathrm{d}\omega)=V(t,\omega)\mathbb{P}(\mathrm{d}\omega).

We remark that by Theorem
B
,

V(t,\omega)\lesssim\mathscr{H}^{d-1}\quad\text{ for $\mathbb{P}$-a.e. }\omega

(43)

and so
\mathbb{Q}_{t}
is well-defined. Note that for any bounded measurable function
\zeta
on
\Omega
, we have
E_{\mathbb{Q}_{t}}[\zeta]=\mathbb{E}[P_{t}\zeta]
. In other words,
\mathbb{Q}_{t}
is the distribution of the environment viewed from the particle at time
t
. It is natural to expect that
\mathbb{Q}_{t}\to\mathbb{Q}
as

.

For any function
u
of the environment, we denote by
u_{\omega}
the corresponding function on
\mathbb{Z}^{d}
defined by
u_{\omega}(x):=u(\theta_{x}\omega)
.

Lemma 23
.

As
t\to\infty
,
\mathbb{Q}_{t}
converges weakly to
\mathbb{Q}
.

Proof.

Since
\{\mathbb{Q}_{t}\}
is a sequence of probability measures on the compact space
\Omega
, it has a weak convergent subsequence
\{\mathbb{Q}_{t_{k}}\}
which has a weak limit
\mathbb{Q}_{\infty}
.

To prove that
\mathbb{Q}_{\infty}
is an invariant measure for the Markov chain
(\theta_{Y_{t}}\omega)
, it suffices to show that for any bounded measurable function
f
on
\Omega
,

E_{\mathbb{Q}_{\infty}}[L_{\omega}f_{\omega}(0)]=0.

Indeed, by the translation invariance of the measure
\mathbb{P}
, for any
e
with
|e|=1
,

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[\omega(0,e)V(t,\omega)f(\theta_{e}\omega)]

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}[\omega(-e,0)V(t,\theta_{-e}\omega)f(\omega)]

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}[\rho_{\omega}(0)\omega^{*}(0,-e)\tilde{V}(t,\theta_{-e}\omega)f(\omega)],

(44)

where
\omega^{*}(x,y):=\rho_{\omega}(y)\omega(y,x)/\rho_{\omega}(x)
denotes the

of
\omega
, cf. e.g.,
[
19
]
, and
\tilde{V}(t,\omega):=V(t,\omega)/\rho(\omega)
. Noting that

\sum_{y}\omega^{*}(x,y)=1
, we have

\displaystyle E_{\mathbb{Q}_{t}}[L_{\omega}f_{\omega}(0)]

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}[V(t,\omega)\sum_{e}\omega(0,e)[f(\theta_{e}\omega)-f(\omega)]]

\displaystyle\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\eqref{eq:221219-1}}}{{=}}\mathbb{E}[\rho_{\omega}(0)\sum_{e}\omega^{*}(0,e)[\tilde{V}(t,\theta_{e}\omega)-\tilde{V}(t,\omega)]f(\omega)]

\displaystyle=E_{\mathbb{Q}}[f(\omega)L_{\omega^{*}}\tilde{V}_{\omega}(t,0)],

(45)

where
\tilde{V}_{\omega}(t,x):=\tilde{V}(t,\theta_{x}\omega)
, and
L_{\omega^{*}}
only acts on the spatial (
\mathbb{Z}^{d}
) coordinate of the function
\tilde{V}_{\omega}:\mathbb{R}\times\mathbb{Z}^{d}\to\mathbb{R}
of space and time.
Observe that
\tilde{V}_{\omega}
solves the parabolic equation

(\partial_{t}-L_{\omega^{*}})\tilde{V}_{\omega}=0\quad\text{ in }(0,\infty)\times\mathbb{Z}^{d}.

By the Hölder estimate
[
19
, Corollary 7]
and the Harnack inequality
[
19
, Theorem 6]
for the operator
(\partial_{t}-L_{\omega^{*}})
, there exists
\gamma=\gamma(d,\kappa)>0
such that, for

,

\displaystyle\max_{e:|e|=1}|\tilde{V}_{\omega}(t,e)-\tilde{V}_{\omega}(t,0)|

\displaystyle\lesssim t^{-\gamma}\sup_{(s,x)\in(0.5t,t)\times B_{\sqrt{t}}}\tilde{V}_{\omega}(s,x)

\displaystyle\lesssim t^{-\gamma}\tilde{V}_{\omega}(2t,0)

(46)

\displaystyle\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\eqref{eq:V-bound}}}{{\lesssim}}t^{-\gamma}\rho^{-1}\mathscr{H}^{d-1}.

Thus, by (

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{\infty}\lim_{t\to\infty}\mathbb{E}\left[V_{\omega}(t-s,y)\sum_{i=1}^{d}(\partial^{\prime}_{y}\omega)(y,y+e_{i})\nabla_{i}^{2}p_{s}^{\omega_{y}^{\prime}}(y,0)\mathbbm{1}_{t>s}f\right]\mathrm{d}s

4.1
Estimates of the approximate corrector

We consider the function

(60)

where

, and
\psi
is measurable function of
\omega(0)

Notice that

is stationary, i.e.,
\phi^{\scriptscriptstyle\rm AP}_{\omega}(x)=\phi^{\scriptscriptstyle\rm AP}_{\theta_{x}\omega}(0)

\phi^{\scriptscriptstyle\rm AP}

Without loss of generality, assume

Clearly, by the definition of

in (

By (

[\phi^{\scriptscriptstyle\rm AP}]_{\gamma;B_{R/2}}\lesssim R^{-\gamma}[\max_{B_{R}}|\phi^{\scriptscriptstyle\rm AP}|+R^{2}\lVert R^{-2}\phi^{\scriptscriptstyle\rm AP}+\psi\rVert_{d;B_{R}}]\lesssim R^{2-\gamma}\lVert\psi\rVert_{\infty}.

2\leq D\leq R

\displaystyle|\nabla\phi^{\scriptscriptstyle\rm AP}(0)|

\displaystyle\lesssim\mathscr{H}(D\lVert\psi\rVert_{\infty}+\tfrac{1}{D}\lVert\phi^{\scriptscriptstyle\rm AP}\rVert_{1;B_{D}}),

(63)

\displaystyle\lvert\nabla^{2}\phi^{\scriptscriptstyle\rm AP}(0)\rvert

(64)

The goal of this subsection is to establish the optimal rate of convergence of the approximate corrector. Recall the function

Our result in two dimensions (

4.2
Quantification of the ergodicity of the environmental process: Proof of Theorem
10

In this section we will derive the optimal rates of convergence (as
t\to\infty
) of the ergodic average
\tfrac{1}{t}E_{\omega}[\int_{0}^{t}\psi(\bar{\omega}^{s})\mathrm{d}s]
, where
\bar{\omega}^{s}
denotes the process of the environment viewed from the particle:

\bar{\omega}^{s}:=\theta_{Y_{s}}\omega.

With Lemma
26
, it may be tempting to compare the approximate corrector
\phi^{\scriptscriptstyle\rm AP}
in (
60
) to the corrector within a finite ball
B_{R}
, i.e., the solution
u
to the Dirichlet problem
L_{\omega}u=\psi_{\omega}
in
B_{R}
with
u=0
on
\partial B_{R}
. However, such comparison involves controlling the boundary error
\max_{\partial B_{R}}\phi^{\scriptscriptstyle\rm AP}
which would result in an extra
\log R
factor. In what follows,
we will follow the argument of Kipnis and Varadhan
[
39
]
to approximate
E_{\omega}[\int_{0}^{T}\psi(\theta_{Y_{s}}\omega)\mathrm{d}s]
with a martingale using the approximate corrector.

Proof of Theorem
10
.

Without loss of generality, assume
\lVert\psi\rVert_{\infty}=1
and
\bar{\psi}=0
.

First, we will construct a martingale (for both continuous and discrete time cases) using the approximate corrector.

For any fixed
T>1
, let
\phi:\Omega\to\mathbb{R}
denote the function

\phi(\omega)=\phi_{\psi,T}(\omega):=\phi^{\scriptscriptstyle\rm AP}(0;\psi,\sqrt{T},\omega),

where
\phi^{\scriptscriptstyle\rm AP}
is as in (
60
). Then, for a.s.
\omega\in\Omega
, the process
(M_{t})_{t\geq 0}
defined by

\displaystyle M_{t}:

\displaystyle=\phi(\theta_{Y_{t}}\omega)-\phi(\theta_{Y_{0}}\omega)-\int_{0}^{t}L_{\omega}\phi(\theta_{Y_{s}}\omega)\mathrm{d}s

\displaystyle\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\eqref{eq:eq_of_phihat}}}{{=}}\phi(\bar{\omega}^{t})-\phi(\bar{\omega}^{0})-\int_{0}^{t}[\tfrac{1}{T}\phi(\bar{\omega}^{s})+\psi(\bar{\omega}^{s})]\mathrm{d}s

(73)

is a
P_{\omega}
-martingale with respect to the filtration
\mathscr{F}_{t}=\sigma(Y_{s}:s\leq t)
.
Similarly, for discrete-time RWRE, we have that

N_{n}:=\phi(\bar{\omega}^{n})-\phi(\bar{\omega}^{0})-\sum_{i=0}^{n-1}[\tfrac{1}{T}\phi(\bar{\omega}^{i})+\psi(\bar{\omega}^{i})]

is a
P_{\omega}
-martingale with respect to the filtration
\mathscr{F}_{n}=\sigma(X_{i}:i\leq n)
.

Next, we will derive an exponential moment bounds for
\int_{0}^{t}P_{s}\psi\mathrm{d}s
and
\sum_{i=0}^{n}P_{i}\psi
,
where the operator
P_{s}
is as in (
16

Since
E_{\omega}[M_{s}]=E_{\omega}[M_{0}]=0
, taking expectations in (
4.2
), we get

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{t}P_{s}\psi\mathrm{d}s=P_{t}\phi-\phi-\tfrac{1}{T}\int_{0}^{t}P_{s}\phi\mathrm{d}s.

(74)

Since the process
(\bar{\omega}^{s})
is a stationary sequence under the measure
\mathbb{Q}\times P_{\omega}
, we have, by Jensen’s inequality, for any
t\geq 0
,

,

\displaystyle\lVert P_{t}\phi\rVert_{L^{q}(\mathbb{Q})}^{q}=E_{\mathbb{Q}}[|E_{\omega}\phi(\bar{\omega}^{t})|^{q}]\leq E_{\mathbb{Q}\times P_{\omega}}[|\phi(\bar{\omega}^{t})|^{q}]=E_{\mathbb{Q}}[|\phi|^{q}].

Hence, taking the
L^{q}(\mathbb{Q})
-norms on both sides of (

\lVert\int_{0}^{T}P_{s}\psi\mathrm{d}s\rVert_{L^{q}(\mathbb{Q})}\leq 3\lVert\phi\rVert_{L^{q}(\mathbb{Q})},\quad\forall q\geq 1

which implies

\displaystyle E_{\mathbb{Q}}\left[\exp\bigg(c\Bigr\lvert\int_{0}^{T}P_{s}\psi\mathrm{d}s\big/\mu(\sqrt{T})\Bigl\rvert^{p}\big)\right]

\displaystyle\leq E_{\mathbb{Q}}\left[\exp\bigg(c\Bigr\lvert 3\phi/\mu(\sqrt{T})\Bigl\rvert^{p}\bigg)\right]

\displaystyle\leq\lVert\rho\rVert_{L^{2}(\mathbb{P})}E_{\mathbb{P}}\left[\exp\bigg(0.5c\Bigr\lvert 3\phi/\mu(\sqrt{T})\Bigl\rvert^{p}\bigg)\right]^{1/2}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\eqref{item:old-rho-integ}}}{{\leq}}C,

where we used Hölder’s inequality in the second inequality.

Note that
\nu(T)=T^{-1}\mu(\sqrt{T})
as defined in (
21
).
The theorem follows from the above moment bound and Chebyshev’s inequality.
∎

4.3
A Berry-Esseen estimate for the QCLT: Proof of Corollary
12

To prove Corollary
12
we will apply the Berry-Esseen estimates for martingales by Heyde and Brown
[
36
]
. Here we will use the version in
[
35
, Theorem 2]
which is also applicable to the continuous-time setting.

Proof of Corollary
12
.

For any unit vector
\ell\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
, let
\psi_{0}(\omega)=\ell^{T}\tfrac{\omega(0)}{{\rm tr}\omega(0)}\ell
,
\psi=\psi_{0}-E_{\mathbb{Q}}[\psi_{0}]
. Following the notations in
[
35
]
, we set

\displaystyle N_{n,2}:

\displaystyle=E_{\omega}\left[\Bigr\lvert\sum_{k=0}^{n-1}E_{\omega}\big[\tfrac{1}{\sqrt{n}}\big((X_{k+1}-X_{k})\cdot\ell\big)^{2}|\mathscr{F}_{k}\big]-\ell^{T}\bar{a}\ell\Bigl\rvert^{2}\right]

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{n^{2}}E_{\omega}\left[\big(\sum_{k=0}^{n-1}\psi(\bar{\omega}^{k})\big)^{2}\right],

L_{n,2}:=\sum_{k=0}^{n-1}E_{\omega}[\lvert\tfrac{1}{\sqrt{n}}(X_{k+1}-X_{k})\cdot\ell\rvert^{4}]=\frac{1}{n^{2}}E_{\omega}\left[\sum_{k=0}^{n-1}\psi_{0}(\bar{\omega}^{k})\right].

The term
N_{n,2}
can be further written as

\displaystyle n^{2}N_{n,2}=2\sum_{i=0}^{n-1}E_{\omega}\left[\psi(\bar{\omega}^{i})\sum_{j=0}^{n-i-1}\psi(\bar{\omega}^{i+j})\right]=2\sum_{i=0}^{n-1}E_{\omega}\left[\psi(\bar{\omega}^{i})E_{\omega}^{X_{i}}\left[\sum_{j=0}^{n-i-1}\psi(\bar{\omega}^{j})\right]\right].

Hence, for any
q\geq 1
, using the fact that
(\bar{\omega}^{i})
is a stationary sequence under
\mathbb{Q}\times P_{\omega}
, we get (note
\lVert\psi_{0}\rVert_{\infty}\lesssim 1
)

\displaystyle\lVert N_{n,2}\rVert_{L^{q}(\mathbb{Q})}\lesssim\frac{1}{n^{2}}\sum_{i=0}^{n-1}\lVert\sum_{j=0}^{n-i-1}P_{j}\psi\rVert_{L^{q}(\mathbb{Q}\times P_{\omega})}

which, by Jensen’s inequality and the fact
\tfrac{1}{n^{2}}\sum_{k=1}^{n}\mu(\sqrt{k})\asymp\nu(n)
, implies that for any
0<p<\tfrac{2d}{3d+2}
,

\displaystyle E_{\mathbb{Q}}\left[\exp\big(c\lvert N_{n,2}/\nu(n)\rvert^{p}\big)\right]

\displaystyle\lesssim\frac{1}{n^{2}\nu(n)}\sum_{k=1}^{n}\mu(\sqrt{k})E_{\mathbb{Q}}\left[\exp\big(c\Bigr\lvert\sum_{j=0}^{k-1}P_{j}\psi\big/\mu(\sqrt{k})\Bigl\rvert^{p}\big)\right]\stackrel{{\scriptstyle Theorem~\ref{thm:quant-ergo}}}{{\leq}}C.

Thus, using the moment bound of
\rho^{-1}
in Theorem
B
, by Hölder’s inequality,

E_{\mathbb{P}}\left[\exp\big(0.5c\lvert N_{n,2}/\nu(n)\rvert^{p}\big)\right]\leq\lVert\rho^{-1/2}\rVert_{L^{2}(\mathbb{P})}E_{\mathbb{Q}}\left[\exp\big(c\lvert N_{n,2}/\nu(n)\rvert^{p}\big)\right]^{1/2}\leq C.

By Theorem
10
we already know that
E_{\mathbb{P}}[\exp\big(c|nL_{n,2}|^{p}\big)]\leq C
. Therefore, we conclude that there exists a random variable
\mathscr{Y}^{5}
with
E_{\mathbb{P}}[\exp({\mathscr{Y}}^{5p})]<\infty
such that

L_{n,2}+N_{n,2}\leq C\nu(n)\mathscr{Y}^{5}.

The corollary follows by applying
[
35
, Theorem 2]
.
∎

5.1
The two-scale expansion

Using the classical method two-scale expansion, we will compare the solutions
u
,
\bar{u}

Lemma 29
.

Recall
a
and
\bar{a}
as in (
7
), (
9
).
Let
\omega\in\Omega
,
R>0
. Let
u
and
\bar{u}
be the solutions of (

), respectively.
Let
v^{k},\xi:\mathbb{Z}^{d}\to\mathbb{R}
be functions that satisfy

\displaystyle L_{\omega}v^{k}(x)

\displaystyle=\tfrac{1}{2}(a_{k}(x)-\bar{a}_{k}),\quad\text{ for }x\in B_{R},k=1,\ldots,d

(77)

\displaystyle L_{\omega}\xi(x)

\displaystyle=\tfrac{\zeta(\theta_{x}\omega)}{{\rm tr}\omega(x)}-E_{\mathbb{Q}}[\tfrac{\zeta(\theta_{x}\omega)}{{\rm tr}\omega(x)}]\quad\text{ for }x\in B_{R}.

That is,

are local correctors associated to
\tfrac{1}{2}a_{k}(x)=\omega(x,x+e_{k})
and
\tfrac{\zeta(\theta_{x}\omega)}{{\rm tr}\omega(x)}

Then

\displaystyle\max_{x\in B_{R}}|u(x)-\bar{u}(\tfrac{x}{R})|

\displaystyle\lesssim\tfrac{1}{R}\lVert\bar{u}\rVert_{C^{4}(\bar{\mathbb{B}}_{1})}[\sum_{k=1}^{d}(\lVert\nabla v^{k}\rVert_{d;B_{R}}+\tfrac{1}{R}\osc_{\bar{B}_{R}}v^{k})+\lVert\nabla\xi\rVert_{d;B_{R}}+\tfrac{1}{R}\osc_{\bar{B}_{R}}\xi+\tfrac{1}{R}].

The proof is similar to that in the periodic setting (see
[
33
,
48
,
30
]
for example).
The only differences are that we need the “environment corrector”
\xi
, and we use the local corrector
v^{k}
here instead of the global corrector (whose existence is guaranteed in the periodic setting).

Proof.

We can replace the function
g
in (
2
) by
\bar{u}
, because doing this only introduces an error of size
CR^{-1}\lVert\bar{u}\rVert_{C^{4}(\bar{\mathbb{B}}_{1})}
to
|u(x)-\bar{u}(\tfrac{x}{R})|
.

Without loss of generality, assume
v^{k}(0)=0
for all
k=1,\ldots,d
. Let

\psi_{\omega}(x):=\tfrac{\zeta(\theta_{x}\omega)}{{\rm tr}\omega(x)},\quad x\in\mathbb{Z}^{d}.

Consider the function

w(x)=u(x)-\bar{u}(\tfrac{x}{R})+\tfrac{1}{R^{2}}v^{k}(x)\partial_{kk}\bar{u}(\tfrac{x}{R})-\tfrac{1}{R^{2}}f(\tfrac{x}{R})\xi(x),\quad x\in\bar{B}_{R},

(78)

where we use the convention of summation over repeated indices.
Note that

\displaystyle L_{\omega}u(x)

\displaystyle=\tfrac{1}{R^{2}}f(\tfrac{x}{R})(\psi(x)-\bar{\psi})+\tfrac{1}{R^{2}}f(\tfrac{x}{R})\bar{\psi}

\displaystyle=\tfrac{1}{R^{2}}\big[f(\tfrac{x}{R})L_{\omega}\xi(x)+\tfrac{1}{2}\bar{a}_{k}\partial_{kk}\bar{u}(\tfrac{x}{R})\big]

and
\lvert L_{\omega}[\bar{u}(\tfrac{x}{R})]-\tfrac{1}{2R^{2}}{\rm tr}[a(x)D^{2}\bar{u}(\tfrac{x}{R})]\rvert\lesssim\tfrac{1}{R^{4}}\lVert\bar{u}\rVert_{C^{4}}
.
Then, applying formula

L_{\omega}(uv)=uL_{\omega}v+vL_{\omega}u+\sum_{y:y\sim x}\omega(x,y)[u(y)-u(x)][v(y)-v(x)]

to the last two terms of (
78

x\in B_{R}
,

\displaystyle\lvert L_{\omega}w(x)\rvert

\displaystyle=\tfrac{1}{R^{2}}\Bigr\lvert f(\tfrac{x}{R})L_{\omega}\xi(x)+\tfrac{1}{2}\bar{a}_{k}\partial_{kk}\bar{u}(\tfrac{x}{R})-R^{2}L_{\omega}[\bar{u}(\tfrac{x}{R})]+\tfrac{1}{2}(a_{k}-\bar{a}_{k})\partial_{kk}\bar{u}(\tfrac{x}{R})

\displaystyle+v^{k}L_{\omega}[\partial_{kk}\bar{u}(\tfrac{x}{R})]+\sum_{y\sim x}\omega(x,y)[\partial_{kk}\bar{u}(\tfrac{y}{R})-\partial_{kk}\bar{u}(\tfrac{x}{R})][v^{k}(y)-v^{k}(x)]

\displaystyle-f(\tfrac{x}{R})L_{\omega}\xi(x)-L_{\omega}[f(\tfrac{x}{R})]\xi(x)-\sum_{y\sim x}\omega(x,y)[f(\tfrac{y}{R})-f(\tfrac{x}{R})][\xi(y)-\xi(x)]\Bigl\rvert

\displaystyle\lesssim\tfrac{1}{R^{2}}\Bigr\lvert v^{k}L_{\omega}[\partial_{kk}\bar{u}(\tfrac{x}{R})]+\sum_{y\sim x}\omega(x,y)[\partial_{kk}\bar{u}(\tfrac{y}{R})-\partial_{kk}\bar{u}(\tfrac{x}{R})][v^{k}(y)-v^{k}(x)]

\displaystyle-L_{\omega}[f(\tfrac{x}{R})]\xi(x)-\sum_{y\sim x}\omega(x,y)[f(\tfrac{y}{R})-f(\tfrac{x}{R})][\xi(y)-\xi(x)]\Bigl\rvert+\tfrac{1}{R^{4}}\lVert\bar{u}\rVert_{C^{4}}

\displaystyle\lesssim\tfrac{1}{R^{3}}\lVert\bar{u}\rVert_{C^{4}(\bar{\mathbb{B}}_{1})}\sum_{k=1}^{d}\big(\tfrac{1}{R}|v^{k}(x)|+\tfrac{1}{R}|\xi(x)|+|\nabla v^{k}(x)|+|\nabla\xi(x)|+\tfrac{1}{R}\big).

Thus, by the above inequality, (
78
), and the ABP maximum principle,

\displaystyle\max_{B_{R}}|w|

\displaystyle\lesssim R^{2}\lVert L_{\omega}w\rVert_{d;B_{R}}+\tfrac{1}{R^{2}}\max_{x\in\partial B_{R}}|v^{k}(x)\partial_{kk}\bar{u}(\tfrac{x}{R})-f(\tfrac{x}{R})\xi(x)|

\displaystyle\lesssim\tfrac{1}{R^{2}}\lVert\bar{u}\rVert_{C^{4}(\bar{\mathbb{B}}_{1})}\sum_{k=1}^{d}\big(\lVert|v^{k}|+|\xi|\rVert_{d;B_{R}}+R\lVert|\nabla v^{k}|+|\nabla\xi|\rVert_{d;B_{R}}

\displaystyle+1+\max_{\partial B_{R}}(|v^{k}|+|\xi|)\big).

The lemma follows from the above inequality and (
78
).
∎

Remark 30
.

By Lemma
29
, to control the homogenization error, it suffices to control the size of local correctors and their discrete gradients.

When
d\geq 5
, by
[
31
, Corollary 7]
, the stationary corrector
\phi
exists.
Hence it is not surprising that the generically optimal rate
R^{-1}
of homogenization can be achieved for
d\geq 5
.

Unfortunately, for
d<5
, there is no stationary corrector at our disposition.
Although (as demonstrated in the first arXiv version
[
32
]
of this paper) one may use the approximate correctors
\phi^{\scriptscriptstyle\rm AP}
together with the two-scale expansion argument to quantify the homogenization, the
C^{0,1}
estimate in Theorem
16
only yields a bound for
\nabla\phi^{\scriptscriptstyle\rm AP}
with size
\sqrt{\mu(R)}
, cf. Corollary
27
, which is not good enough for us to obtain optimal rates.

In this regard,
it is not the size of the corrector, but its
C^{0,1}
regularity that is posing the biggest challenge in the course of obtaining the optimal homogenization rates.
To resolve this issue, our strategy is to construct a local corrector and use sensitivity estimates as in (
67
) to obtain optimal bounds for its gradients. Firstly, note that such an argument does not work for
\phi^{\scriptscriptstyle\rm AP}
. Indeed, bounding
\partial^{\prime}_{y}\nabla\phi^{\scriptscriptstyle\rm AP}
(cf. (
67
)) involves estimating
\nabla G^{\scriptscriptstyle\rm AP}_{\omega}(\cdot,y)
. However,
G^{\scriptscriptstyle\rm AP}_{\omega}(\cdot,y)
, which solves

L_{\omega}G^{\scriptscriptstyle\rm AP}(\cdot,y)=\tfrac{1}{R^{2}}G^{\scriptscriptstyle\rm AP}(\cdot,y)-\mathbbm{1}_{y},

is nowhere
\omega
-harmonic, and so our
C^{0,1}
theory (Theorem
16
) is not applicable to obtain a desired bound for
\nabla G^{\scriptscriptstyle\rm AP}_{\omega}(\cdot,y)
.
Secondly, it might be tempting to construct a local corrector by solving a Dirichlet problem in a finite region. For instance, one may solve (
75
) by imposing a zero boundary condition on
\partial B_{R}
. However, an obvious defect for such a construction is that

C^{0,1}
and
C^{1,1}
bounds (using Theorem
16
) near the boundary blows up, which makes an optimal estimate impossible. Intuitively, this is due to the fact that the RWRE which starts near the boundary cannot survive long enough (before hitting the boundary) to “feel” the homogenization.

To overcome this challenge, we will construct a local corrector such that (1) the corresponding Green function is
\omega
-harmonic in
B_{2R}
; (2) the corresponding RWRE survives a time of scale
\approx R^{2}
.

5.2
The construction of a local corrector

Recall the continuous-time random walk

Let

Write

Definition 31

Proposition 32
.

For any
R>1,\omega\in\Omega

Our proof of the existence is constructive.

Let us explain the probabilistic intuition behind the above definitions. Set

\tilde{\eta}(x)=\frac{\eta(x)}{R^{2}+\eta(x)}\in[0,\tfrac{1}{R+1}],\quad x\in\mathbb{Z}^{d}.

Imagine at every site

be the first time that the discrete-time random walk

.

.

Define a sequence of exit times from balls centered at

\tau_{0}(x):=0,\quad\tau_{k}(x)=\inf\{t:X_{n}\notin B_{kR}(x)\},\quad k\geq 1.

The following are some properties of the stopping time

Lemma 33
.

Let
R>1
. For any
\eta:\mathbb{Z}^{d}\to[0,1]
with
\eta|_{\mathbb{Z}^{d}\setminus B_{3R}}=1
, let
\phi^{\scriptscriptstyle\rm loc}=\phi^{\scriptscriptstyle\rm loc}(x;\eta,\psi)

(i)

Recall
\tau_{n}(x)
in (

P_{\omega}^{x}(T>\tau_{k}(x))\lesssim e^{-ck}.

(86)

(ii)

E^{x}_{\omega}[T]\lesssim R^{2}

Proof.

(i)

The intuition is as follows. From all but two spheres
\partial B_{kR}(x)
, the RW has probability less than
1-c
of not being killed by the exponential clock before reaching the next level
\partial B_{(k+1)R}(x)
. Hence, by iteration, it would be exponentially hard to reach
\partial B_{kR}(x)

Indeed, let
n_{0}\in\mathbb{N}
be a constant to be determined later.
Since
(X_{n})
is a martingale on any
\omega\in\Omega
, by Doob’s inequality, for
k>0
,

\displaystyle P_{\omega}(\tau_{n_{0}}\leq kR^{2})

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{e:|e|=1}P_{\omega}(\sup_{n\leq kR^{2}}X_{n}\cdot e\geq n_{0}R/\sqrt{d})

\displaystyle\leq\frac{\sqrt{d}}{n_{0}R}\sum_{e:|e|=1}E_{\omega}[(X_{kR^{2}}\cdot e)_{+}]\lesssim\frac{\sqrt{kR^{2}}}{n_{0}R}\lesssim\frac{\sqrt{k}}{n_{0}}.

Hence, when
n_{0}>8
is chosen appropriately and

,

\displaystyle P_{\omega}^{x}(\tau_{n_{0}}(x)<T)

\displaystyle\leq P_{\omega}^{x}(\tau_{n_{0}}(x)<n_{0}R^{2})+P_{\omega}^{x}(T\geq n_{0}R^{2})

\displaystyle\leq\tfrac{C\sqrt{n_{0}}}{n_{0}}+(1-\tfrac{1}{1+R^{2}})^{n_{0}R^{2}}<e^{-1},

where we used the fact that
T
behaves like a geometric random variable with parameter
\tfrac{1}{1+R^{2}}
when we consider the RW outside of
B_{3R}
. For
k\geq 1
, whenever
\big(B_{(k+1)n_{0}R}(x)\setminus B_{(k-1)n_{0}R}(x)\big)\cap B_{3R}=\emptyset
, we have
B_{n_{0}R}(z)\cap B_{3R}=\emptyset
for all

and thus

\displaystyle P_{\omega}^{x}(\tau_{(k+1)n_{0}}(x)<T|\tau_{kn_{0}}(x)<T)\leq\max_{z\in\partial B_{n_{0}kR}}P_{\omega}^{z}(\tau_{n_{0}}(z)<T)<e^{-1}.

Since for any

(ii)

Proof of the uniqueness in Theorem

\nabla_{-e_{i}}{\boldsymbol{\phi}}(x)=\nabla_{e_{i}}{\boldsymbol{\phi}}(x-e_{i})
for
i=1,\ldots,d
.

By formula (
91
) we have, for
x,y\in\mathbb{Z}^{d}
,

\displaystyle\partial^{\prime}_{y}\nabla^{+}\phi_{R}(x)=-[\partial^{\prime}_{y}\psi(y)-\tfrac{1}{2}{\rm tr}(\partial^{\prime}_{y}a\nabla^{2}\phi^{\scriptscriptstyle\rm loc}_{\omega^{\prime}_{y}})(y)][\nabla^{+}G^{\scriptscriptstyle\rm loc}_{\omega}(x,y)-\nabla^{+}G^{\scriptscriptstyle\rm loc}_{\omega}(0,y)],

where
\nabla^{+}
only acts on the first coordinate of
G^{\scriptscriptstyle\rm loc}
, i.e.,
\nabla^{+}G^{\scriptscriptstyle\rm loc}(x,y)=\nabla^{+}G^{\scriptscriptstyle\rm loc}(\cdot,y)|_{\cdot=x}
.
Taking a subsequential of limit
R\to\infty
we obtain, almost surely,

\partial^{\prime}_{y}\nabla^{+}{\boldsymbol{\phi}}(x)=[\partial^{\prime}_{y}\psi(y)-\tfrac{1}{2}{\rm tr}(\partial^{\prime}_{y}a\nabla^{2}{\boldsymbol{\phi}}_{\omega^{\prime}_{y}})(y)][\nabla^{+}A(x,y)-\nabla^{+}A(0,y)]

where
A(x,y)
is as in (
14
).
Further, by (
106
), Theorem
D
, and Theorem
16
, we get

\displaystyle\lvert\partial^{\prime}_{y}\nabla^{+}{\boldsymbol{\phi}}(x)\rvert\lesssim\mathscr{H}_{x+y}^{{}^{\prime}2}[\frac{\mathscr{H}_{x,y}^{2}}{|x-y|+1}\log(|x-y|\vee 2)+\frac{\mathscr{H}_{0,y}^{2}}{|y|+1}\log(|y|\vee 2)],

where
\mathscr{H}_{x,y}=\mathscr{H}_{x}+\mathscr{H}_{y}
. Moreover, for any affine function
\ell
, with
A_{\ell}(\cdot,y)=A(\cdot,y)+\ell
, we have

\nabla^{+}A(x,y)-\nabla^{+}A(0,y)=\nabla^{+}A_{\ell}(x,y)-\nabla^{+}A_{\ell}(0,y)\leq\osc_{B_{|x|+1}}A_{\ell}(\cdot,y).

Hence,
for
3|x|<|y|
, by Theorem
16
we get

\lvert\partial^{\prime}_{y}\nabla^{+}{\boldsymbol{\phi}}(x)\rvert\lesssim\mathscr{H}_{x+y}^{{}^{\prime}2}{\mathcal{D}}_{B_{|x|+1}}^{2}(A(\cdot,y))\lesssim\mathscr{H}_{x+y}^{{}^{\prime}2}\frac{(\mathscr{H}\vee|x|)^{2}}{|y|^{2}}\mathscr{H}_{y}\log|y|.

Hence, for
x\neq 0
, with

g(y):=\left\{\begin{array}[]{lr}\frac{1}{|x-y|+1}\log(|x-y|\vee 2)+\frac{1}{|y|+1}\log(|y|\vee 2)&\text{ when }|y|\leq 3|x|\\
\frac{|x|^{2}}{|y|^{2}}\log|y|&\text{ when }|y|>3|x|,\end{array}\right.

we obtain, for
x\in\mathbb{Z}^{d}
and
n\geq 1
,

\mathbb{E}[|\partial^{\prime}_{y}\nabla^{+}{\boldsymbol{\phi}}(x)/g(y)|^{n}]\lesssim\mathbb{E}[\mathscr{H}^{5n}].

Computations show that

\sum_{y}g(y)^{2}\lesssim(\log(|x|\vee 2))^{3}.

Hence, applying Lemma
21
to
Z=\nabla^{+}{\boldsymbol{\phi}}(x)
we have

\mathbb{E}\big[\exp\big(C|\frac{Z-\mathbb{E}Z}{(\log(|x|\vee 2))^{3/2}}|^{p}\big)\big]\leq C_{p}\quad\text{ for }p\in(0,\tfrac{2d}{10+d}).

∎

A.2
Verification of (
4.1
)

In this subsection we will verify the inequality

\int_{0}^{\infty}(1+t)^{-d/2}\exp\left[-\tfrac{t}{R^{2}}-c\mathfrak{h}(|y|,t)\right]\mathrm{d}t\lesssim e^{-c|y|/R}v(|y|+1),\quad\forall y\in\mathbb{Z}^{d}.

We break the integral on the left side of the above inequality as

\int_{0}^{\infty}=\int_{0}^{|y|/2}+\int_{|y|/2}^{|y|^{2}}+\int_{|y|^{2}}^{\infty}=:\text{I}+\text{II}+\text{III}.

It suffices to consider the case
|y|\geq 1
.
First, with
c_{2}>0
sufficiently small,

\displaystyle\text{I}=\int_{0}^{|y|/2}(1+t)^{-d/2}\exp\left(-\tfrac{t}{R^{2}}-c|y|\log\tfrac{|y|}{t}\right)\mathrm{d}t\leq|y|e^{-c|y|}\lesssim e^{-c_{2}|y|/R}v(|y|).

Moreover, noting that
-\tfrac{t}{2R^{2}}-c\tfrac{|y|^{2}}{t}\lesssim-\tfrac{|y|}{R}
,

II

\displaystyle=\int_{|y|/2}^{|y|^{2}}(1+t)^{-d/2}\exp\left(-\tfrac{t}{R^{2}}-c\tfrac{|y|^{2}}{t}\right)\mathrm{d}t

\displaystyle\lesssim e^{-c|y|/R}\int_{0}^{|y|^{2}}t^{-d/2}e^{-c|y|^{2}/t}\mathrm{d}t

\displaystyle\lesssim e^{-c|y|/R}|y|^{2-d}\int_{1}^{\infty}s^{d/2-2}e^{-cs}\mathrm{d}s\lesssim e^{-c|y|/R}v(|y|).

Similarly, for
d=2
,

III

\displaystyle\lesssim e^{-c|y|/R}\int_{|y|^{2}}^{\infty}(1+t)^{-d/2}\exp\left(-\tfrac{t}{2R^{2}}\right)\mathrm{d}t

\displaystyle\lesssim e^{-c|y|/R}\int_{|y|^{2}/R^{2}}^{\infty}s^{-1}e^{-s/2}\mathrm{d}s

\displaystyle\lesssim e^{-c|y|/R}\left[1+\int_{0}^{\infty}s^{-1}\mathbbm{1}_{\{|y|^{2}/R^{2}\leq s\leq 1\}}\mathrm{d}s\right]\lesssim e^{-c|y|/R}v(y|).

For
d\geq 3
, we have

\text{III}\lesssim e^{-c|y|/R}\int_{|y|^{2}}^{\infty}(1+t)^{-d/2}\mathrm{d}t\lesssim e^{-c|y|/R}v(|y|).

Therefore, the above bounds of I, II, III imply inequality(
