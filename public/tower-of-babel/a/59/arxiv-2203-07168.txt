Edge Waves and Transmissions for Temporal Laminates and Imperfect Chiral Interfaces
A.B. Movchan, N.V. Movchan, I.S. Jones,, G.W. Milton, H.-M. Nguyen

Abstract
The analysis of wave patterns in a structure which possesses periodicity in the spatial and temporal dimensions is presented. The topic of imperfect chiral interfaces is also considered. Although causality is fundamental for physical processes, natural wave phenomena can be observed when a wave is split at a temporal interface. A wave split at a spatial interface is a more common occurrence; however when the coefficients of the governing equations are time-dependent, the temporal interface becomes important. Here, the associated edge waves are studied, and regimes are analysed where the growth of the solution in time is found. Imperfect interfaces, across which the displacements are discontinuous, are also considered in the vector case of chiral elastic systems. Analytical study and asymptotic approximations are supplied with illustrative numerical examples.

2
Formal settings - temporal interfaces

Let two-dimensional vector fields
{\bf j}
and
{\bf e}
be divergence-free and curl-free, respectively in
{\bf R}^{2}
. Assume that

{\bf j}({\bf x})=\mbox{\boldmath${\sigma}$}({\bf x}){\bf e}({\bf x}),

(1)

where

\nabla\cdot{\bf j}({\bf x})=0,\penalty\ {\bf e}=-\nabla V,

(2)

with the diagonal matrix
\mbox{\boldmath${\sigma}$}({\bf x})=\mbox{diag}(\alpha({\bf x}),-\beta({\bf x})).

Here,
\alpha
and
\beta
are positive functions.

Thus, the potential function
V
satisfies the governing equation

\frac{\partial}{\partial x_{1}}\Bigg(\alpha({\bf x})\frac{\partial}{\partial x_{1}}V({\bf x})\Bigg)-\frac{\partial}{\partial x_{2}}\Bigg(\beta({\bf x})\frac{\partial}{\partial x_{2}}V({\bf x})\Bigg)=0.

(3)

The initial conditions are set at the boundary of the upper half-plane:

V(x_{1},0)=\Phi(x_{1}),\penalty\ \frac{\partial V}{\partial x_{2}}(x_{1},0)=\Psi(x_{1}),\penalty\ \penalty\ \mbox{as}\penalty\ x_{1}\in(-\infty,+\infty),

(4)

where
\Phi
and
\Psi
are given bounded functions.

In particular, choose the independent variables
x_{1}
and
x_{2}
to be a length coordinate
X
and a time variable
T
, respectively, and refer to the above problem (
3
)–(
4
) as the Cauchy problem for the wave equation in a temporally and spatially inhomogeneous elastic string. In this case,
V
stands for the elastic displacement, and the relations (
4
) represent the initial conditions for the displacement and velocity at
T=0.

2.1
Temporally inhomogeneous medium

Figure 1
:
Temporal laminate

Consider the special case where
\alpha
and
\beta
are
X-
independent, but may change with
T
, i.e.
\alpha=\alpha(T),\penalty\ \beta=\beta(T)
.
In the context of the physical interpretation, related to vibration of an elastic string, which extends along the
X-
axis, the coefficients
\alpha
and
\beta
represent the elastic stiffness and the mass density, respectively.
Firstly, consider the case when these are continuous and bounded functions of
T
.
The wave equation (
3
) then becomes

\alpha(T)\frac{\partial^{2}}{\partial X^{2}}V(X,T)-\frac{\partial}{\partial T}\Bigg(\beta(T)\frac{\partial}{\partial T}V(X,T)\Bigg)=0.

(5)

It is assumed that the materials are non-dispersive, since otherwise the governing equation (
5
) would take a different form (see, for example,
[
22
]
).
Note that in (
5
) by interchanging
T
and
X
we obtain the governing equation in a spatial laminate. This allows one to map results for spatial laminates to temporal laminates. The difference is that one has to respect causality: an instantaneous point source can only generate waves in the future, not the past. The response to such point sources, or periodic arrays of them, will be the main focus of our investigations in subsequent sections.

If one has a band gap in the spatial laminate then, in the temporal laminate, one may have, for a fixed wavevector and for a frequency in the bandgap, complex conjugate solutions,
one of which corresponds to waves growing exponentially in time.

The formal application of the Fourier transform

\hat{V}(T,k)=\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}V(X,T)e^{iXk}dX

(6)

leads to

\alpha(T)k^{2}\hat{V}(k,T)+\frac{\partial}{\partial T}\Bigg(\beta(T)\frac{d}{dT}\hat{V}(k,T)\Bigg)=0.

(7)

In particular, if
\beta=1
, and the normalised stiffness coefficient
\alpha
is a periodic function of time, for example
\alpha(T)=1-2q\cos(2T),\penalty\ 0<q<1/2
, then the equation (
7
) becomes

\frac{d^{2}}{dT^{2}}\hat{V}(k,T)+k^{2}(1-2q\cos(2T))\hat{V}(k,T)=0,

which is the well-known Mathieu differential equation. The solution can be represented in terms of the Mathieu functions, and Floquet theory can be used accordingly (see, for example,
[
24
]
).

Consider an alternative configuration, where both the stiffness coefficient
\alpha
and the mass density coefficient
\beta
are piece-wise constant functions of
T
, which implies a “temporal stratification” (see Fig.
1
). In this case, the interface conditions should be set at every temporal interface, as discussed in Section
2.2
.

In the general case, equation (
7
) can be reduced to

\frac{\partial}{\partial T}\begin{pmatrix}\hat{W}\cr\hat{V}\end{pmatrix}+\begin{pmatrix}0&-\tilde{\alpha}(k,T)\cr({\beta}(T))^{-1}&0\end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix}\hat{W}\cr\hat{V}\end{pmatrix}=0,

(8)

where

\tilde{\alpha}(\xi,T)=k^{2}\alpha(T)\penalty\ \mbox{and}\penalty\ \hat{W}(k,T)=-\beta(T)\frac{\partial}{\partial T}\hat{V}(k,T).

The relations (
4
) yield the initial conditions for
\hat{W}
and
\hat{V}
:

\hat{V}\Bigg|_{T=0}=\hat{\Phi}(k),\penalty\ \hat{W}\Bigg|_{T=0}=\beta(0)\hat{\Psi}(k),

(9)

where
\hat{\Phi}(k),\hat{\Psi}(k)
are the Fourier transforms of the right-hand sides in (
4
).

Using the matrix notations

{\bf Y}(T)=\begin{pmatrix}\hat{W}\cr\hat{V}\end{pmatrix},\penalty\ {\mbox{\boldmath${\cal M}$}}(T)=\begin{pmatrix}0&-\tilde{\alpha}(k,T)\cr({\beta}(T))^{-1}&0\end{pmatrix},

(10)

and regarding
k
as a fixed parameter, we have the following initial boundary value problem

\frac{d}{dT}{\bf Y}(T)=-{\mbox{\boldmath${\cal M}$}}(T){\bf Y}(T),

(11)

{\bf Y}(0)={\bf Y}_{0}=\begin{pmatrix}\hat{W}\cr\hat{V}\end{pmatrix}\Bigg|_{T=0}.

(12)

Hence, the vector function
{\bf Y}(T)
evaluated at
T=T_{*}
can be written in the form

{\bf Y}(T_{*})=\exp(-\int_0^{T_*} \BCM(\tau) d \tau){\bf Y}_{0}.

(13)

In the case where

{\mbox{\boldmath${\cal M}$}}(\tau)
is piecewise constant in
\tau
the exponential term can be written as a product of matrices. This corresponds to the transfer matrix approach in spatial laminates.

This implies that the wave propagating with the speed of

where

should be replaced by
\alpha_{1}\beta_{1}
for odd

and by
\alpha_{2}\beta_{2}
for even
\Omega_{n}
, respectively; the values

and
T^{(n)}_{+}
stand for the lower and upper limits of

corresponding to the boundaries of the strip
\Omega_{n}
. The matrix in the right-hand side of (

This gives an iterative scheme enabling the Fourier transform of the solution to be found in the temporally stratified half-plane, with wave reflections at the temporal interfaces.

5
Chiral interfaces

The scalar problem of vibration of an elastic string considered above will here be extended to a vector case. Both transverse and longitudinal displacement are included in the formulation, while the coupling process is governed by a chiral term, present in the equation or interface transmission conditions.

Firstly, the model of a chiral continuum will be summarised since this continuum will be used as a chiral temporal interface. Secondly, energy considerations will be discussed followed by the analysis of a transient configuration of a spatial chiral interface.

5.1
The chiral medium

A model for a chiral medium has been introduced in stages. Firstly, gyroscopic resonators connected to elastic beams were introduced in
[
20
]
where a gyroscopic resonator was replaced by suitable displacement boundary conditions replacing the resonator and describing the gyroscopic action. The linearised framework of the gyroscopic motion was used and a small angle of nutation was assumed. The governing equations for a one-dimensional discrete chain of such resonators with hinged bases and connected by massless springs was discussed in
[
21
]
and the resonators were again replaced by appropriate time dependent displacement boundary conditions. The system of coupled governing equations in the longitudinal and transverse displacements was homogenised in
[
23
]
leading to a model with governing equations, written for a chiral medium, in the vector form

-\frac{\partial^{2}}{\partial t^{2}}{{\bf U}}+{\bf D}\frac{\partial^{2}}{\partial x^{2}}{\bf U}+\alpha{\bf R}\frac{\partial}{\partial t}{{\bf U}}={\bf 0},

(30)

where
{\bf U}=(u(x,t),v(x,t))^{T}
is a real-valued vector function, representing the longitudinal,
u(x,t)
, and transverse,
v(x,t)
, displacements,

{\bf D}=\mbox{diag}\{c_{1}^{2},c_{2}^{2}\},\penalty\ \penalty\ {\bf R}=\begin{pmatrix}0&1\cr-1&0\end{pmatrix},

(31)

where
c_{1}
and
c_{2}
are the wave speeds for the longitudinal and transverse waves respectively, and
\alpha
is the (re-defined) gyricity parameter. The third term in (
30
) is the coupling term, and it represents the gyroscopic force, which is orthogonal to the velocity vector.

Although, in the linearised setting, the gyroscopic force appears to be non-conservative, it is orthogonal to the velocity vector, and for a finite chiral elastic rod the classical energy conservation holds, as demonstrated in the illustrative example below.

5.2
Energy consideration

Here, an elementary demonstration is given, based on two examples which include vibration of a chiral finite rod and time-harmonic vibration of a single chiral inertial resonator.

5.2.1
The finite elastic chiral rod

Let
{\bf U}=(u(x,t),v(x,t))^{T}
be a real-valued vector function, representing the displacements, which satisfy the equations of motion (
30

x\in(0,1)

{\bf U}(0,t)={\bf U}(1,t)=0\penalty\ \penalty\ \mbox{for any admissible }t.

(32)

Multiplying equation (

(33)

Note that

By integrating (

When

(41)

5.3

5.3.2
A model scattering problem

Assume that a step-like front of the longitudinal wave is propagating in the positive direction of the
x-

{\bf U}_{\mbox{\tiny inc}}=\begin{pmatrix}1-H(x-x_{0}-c_{1}(t-t_{0}))\cr 0\end{pmatrix},

(58)

and at time

this front meets a chiral interface, positioned at the point
x=x_{0},
and characterised by gyricity
\beta
, as discussed in Section

.

The total field includes two terms

{\bf U}(x,t)={\bf U}_{\mbox{\tiny inc}}+{\bf U}_{\mbox{\tiny sc}},

(59)

where the incident field has only the first non-zero component
u_{\mbox{\tiny inc}}
, which satisfies the homogeneous wave equation

\frac{\partial^{2}}{\partial t^{2}}{u_{\mbox{\tiny inc}}}-c_{1}^{2}\frac{\partial^{2}}{\partial x^{2}}u_{\mbox{\tiny inc}}={0},

(60)

whereas the scattered field has only the second non-zero component
v_{\mbox{\tiny sc}}
, corresponding to the transverse vibration, and it is triggered by an instantaneous point force, whose magnitude is proportional to the velocity of the longitudinal vibration but the orientation of the force is orthogonal to the longitudinal velocity. Taking into account (
57
), we observe that the scattered field is the transverse transient wave, which satisfies the following initial value problem

c_{2}^{2}\frac{\partial^{2}}{\partial x^{2}}v_{\mbox{\tiny sc}}-\frac{\partial^{2}}{\partial t^{2}}{v_{\mbox{\tiny sc}}}-\beta\frac{\partial u_{\mbox{\tiny inc}}}{\partial t}(x_{0},t)\delta(x-x_{0})={0},

(61)

subject to the initial conditions

v_{\mbox{\tiny sc}}(x,0)=0,\penalty\ \frac{\partial}{\partial t}v_{\mbox{\tiny sc}}(x,0)=0,

(62)

with
\alpha
being the chirality parameter characterising the chiral point scatterer.
Taking into account (
57
) and (
58
), the equation (

) can be re-written in the form

c_{2}^{2}\frac{\partial^{2}}{\partial x^{2}}v_{\mbox{\tiny sc}}-\frac{\partial^{2}}{\partial t^{2}}{v_{\mbox{\tiny sc}}}-\beta\delta(t-t_{0})\delta(x-x_{0})={0}.

(63)

Taking the Fourier transform
\hat{v}_{\mbox{\tiny sc}}(k,t)=\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}v_{\mbox{\tiny sc}}(x,t)\exp(i k x)dx
, we deduce

k^{2}c_{2}^{2}\hat{v}_{\mbox{\tiny sc}}(k,t)+\frac{\partial^{2}}{\partial t^{2}}{\hat{v}_{\mbox{\tiny sc}}}(k,t)+\beta\delta(t-t_{0})e^{ikx_{0}}={0},

(64)

\hat{v}_{\mbox{\tiny sc}}(k,0)=0,\penalty\ \frac{\partial}{\partial t}\hat{v}_{\mbox{\tiny sc}}(k,0)=0,

(65)

and hence

\hat{v}_{\mbox{\tiny sc}}(k,t)=-\beta\frac{\sin(k c_2 (t-t_0))}{kc_{2}}H(t-t_{0})e^{ikx_{0}}.

(66)

Using the identity

\int_{0}^{\infty}\frac{\sin(k q)}{k}dk=\frac{\pi}{2}\mbox{sign}(q),

(67)

together with the inverse Fourier transform, we obtain

v_{\mbox{\tiny sc}}(x,t)=\frac{1}{2\pi}\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\hat{v}_{\mbox{\tiny sc}}(k,t)e^{-ikx}dk

(68)

=-\frac{\beta}{2\pi c_{2}}H(t-t_{0})\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\frac{\sin(k c_2 (t-t_0))}{k}e^{ik(x_{0}-x)}dk

=-\frac{\beta}{4c_{2}}H(t-t_{0})\Big(\mbox{sign}(c_{2}(t-t_{0})+x-x_{0})+\mbox{sign}(c_{2}(t-t_{0})+x_{0}-x)\Big)

=-\frac{\beta}{2c_{2}}H(c_{2}(t-t_{0})-|x-x_{0}|)

=-\frac{\beta}{2c_{2}}\Big(H(x-x_{0}+c_{2}(t-t_{0}))-H(x-x_{0}-c_{2}(t-t_{0}))\Big)

The resulting scattered field represents the transverse wave, as follows

{\bf U}_{\mbox{\tiny sc}}(x,t)=\begin{pmatrix}0\cr\frac{\beta}{2c_{2}}\Big(H(x-x_{0}-c_{2}(t-t_{0}))-H(x-x_{0}+c_{2}(t-t_{0}))\Big)\end{pmatrix}.

(69)

We note that the above scattered field is independent of
c_{1}
. This is not a general result, but a special feature of the model problem considered here, where the incident field is defined via a step function and the homogeneity properties
H(\lambda x)=H(x),\delta(\lambda x)=\lambda^{-1}\delta(x),\lambda>0,
have been used.

In the general case of an incident wave, the scattered field may depend on both
c_{1}
and
c_{2}
.

Such a chiral interface can be viewed as an active interface, as it initiates a force, orthogonal to the orientation of the velocity of the incident field, and the magnitude of this force depends on the gyricity parameter
\beta

For
x<x_{0},
the resulting reflected field

compared to the speed

{\bf U}_{\mbox{\tiny refl}}(x,t)={\bf U}_{\mbox{\tiny sc}}(x,t)=\begin{pmatrix}0\cr-\frac{\beta}{2c_{2}}H(x-x_{0}+c_{2}(t-t_{0}))\end{pmatrix},\penalty\ x<x_{0}.

(70)

On the other hand, for

{\bf U}_{\mbox{\tiny transm}}(x,t)={\bf U}_{\mbox{\tiny inc}}(x,t)+{\bf U}_{\mbox{\tiny sc}}(x,t)

=\begin{pmatrix}1-H(x-x_{0}-c_{1}(t-t_{0}))\cr\frac{\beta}{2c_{2}}(H(x-x_{0}-c_{2}(t-t_{0}))-1)\end{pmatrix},\penalty\ x>x_{0}.

(71)

We note that the coupling between the longitudinal and transverse vibrations is an essential feature of the chiral scatterer. In the above example, for large values of
\beta

the chiral transient scatterer can be interpreted as a “switch”, which initiates a transverse wave, with amplitude controlled by gyricity, that may be significantly larger than that of the incident longitudinal wave.

6.3
An imperfect temporal interface

Assume that outside the time-interval
t\in(T^{*},T^{*}+d)
the gyricity parameter
\alpha
is zero, and hence the equation (
72
) has the solution
{\bf U}(x,t)
, which describes two uncoupled waves propagating with constant speeds
c_{1}
(longitudinal) and
c_{2}
(transverse), respectively, which are classical D’Alembert’s travelling waves. We note that
{\mbox{\boldmath${\cal M}$}}(0)={\bf I}
.

However, it is assumed that within the interval
(T^{*},T^{*}+d)
the gyricity parameter
\alpha
takes a large value, and within that time-interval, on a cross-section with fixed
x
, the solution is described in Section
6.2
. The continuity of the displacement and of the momentum (see (
74
)) are set at
t=T_{*}
and
t=T^{*}+d
.

For small
d
and large
\alpha
, we assume that
\alpha d=(2n-1)\pi
, where
n
is positive integer.
Then it follows from (
85
)–(
87
) that across the thin temporal chiral interface the field
{\bf U}
is discontinuous and the following interface conditions hold:

\Big[\frac{\partial}{\partial t}{\bf U}(x,t)\Big]_{t=T_{*}}^{t=T^{*}+d}=0,\penalty\ \Big[{\bf U}(x,t)\Big]_{t=T_{*}}^{t=T_{*}+d}=2\alpha^{-1}{\bf R}\frac{\partial}{\partial t}{\bf U}(x,T_{*}).

(90)

The above interface conditions represent the imperfect temporal interface, across which the displacement vector has the discontinuity. For large
\alpha
and a finite magnitude of the velocity vector, the right-hand side in the second interface condition (
90
) is small. On the other hand, when the incident wave approaches the interface with the instantaneous velocity, which is large, then the small coefficient
\alpha^{-1}
may counterbalance it, and hence a finite magnitude in the displacement jump may be observed across the imperfect temporal interface.

8
Concluding remarks

As shown in Sections
2.1
and
2.2
, periodic time-variation in the mass density may lead to interesting features of solutions of wave problems.
More importantly, the model of a temporally stratified medium can be considered as a result of the discretisation of the wave equations with time-dependent mass density and stiffness coefficients, as in (
5
).

The advantage of the discretised formulation in the temporally stratified medium is that in every temporal layer, where the coefficients of the governing equations are time-independent, the problem can be solved and a transmission matrix, as in Section
2.2
, can be identified. Furthermore, the process can be carried out in iterations to obtain the solution of the Cauchy problem at any given time.
Although, the diagrams of characteristics, as in Figs.
2
,
6
,
8
,
10
, do not provide information about the wave magnitude, they readily show possible wave patterns corresponding to selected initial conditions as well as the parameters of temporal stratification.

Sections
2
,
3
,
4
describe a scalar problem of vibrations of an elastic string with time-dependent elastic and inertial properties, with the emphasis on the resonance blow-up as
T\to+\infty.
The second part of the paper is focused on a vector problem, where the longitudinal and transverse displacements of the vibrating string are dynamically coupled.

Sections
5
and
6
introduce coupled governing equations through chirality, together with the notion of imperfect chiral interfaces. Three examples of Cauchy problems in temporally stratified chiral elastic systems are discussed in detail in Section
7
, and they show the wave patterns, as well the coupling process between the longitudinal and transverse vibrations within the string. A particularly interesting feature, displayed in Figs.
6
,
8
,
10
, is the presence of the
transition layer
adjacent to the
edge wave
in all three cases. Such transition layers also appear in other field pattern geometries: see, for example, Fig. 16 in
[
8
]
.
