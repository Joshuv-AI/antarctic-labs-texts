Urns & Tubes
Bart Jacobs

Abstract
Urn models play an important role to express various basic ideas in probability theory. Here we extend this urn model with tubes. An urn contains coloured balls, which can be drawn with probabilities proportional to the numbers of balls of each colour. For each colour a tube is assumed. These tubes have different sizes (lengths). The idea is that after drawing a ball from the urn it is dropped in the tube of the corresponding colour. We consider two associated probability distributions. The first-full distribution on colours gives for each colour the probability that the corresponding tube is full first, before any of the other tubes. The negative distribution on natural numbers captures for a number k k the probability that all tubes are full for the first time after k k draws. This paper uses multisets to systematically describe these first-full and negative distributions in the urns & tubes setting, in fully multivariate form, for all three standard drawing modes (multinomial, hypergeometric, and Pólya).

Commonly three modes of drawing balls from an urn are distinguished,

“0”

for the multinomial mode, in which the drawn ball is

“-1”

“+1”

to the urn, together with an additional copy, of the same colour

The distinction between multinomial and hypergeometric modes
is most familiar and is often expressed in terms of:
with
or

without
replacement. The Pólya mode is less well known. The
additional ball that is added to the urn after drawing has a
strengthening effect that can capture situations with a cluster
dynamics, like in the spread of contagious diseases
HayhoeAG17

or the flow of tourists
LauKW20
.

In a physical explanation of the first-full distributions we need for
the multinomial and Pólya modes an auxiliary box of balls on the
side (with sufficiently many balls). In multinomial (resp. Pólya)
mode, the ball drawn from the urn is dropped in the right tube, but
one (resp. two) ball(s) of the same colour are taken from the box and
added to the urn, before the next draw. In the Pólya case the urn
grows in size with each draw. In the multinomial case the urn remains
the same and is best described as a probability distribution (over the
set of colours). In the hypergeometric mode the urn decreases in size;
we thus have to assume that initially the urn contains sufficiently
many balls of each colour: more than the length of each tube.

The urn & tubes set-up as introduced here generalises the famous
‘problem of points’, studied in the 17th century by Pierre Fermat and
Blaise Pascal, that played an important role in the development of
modern probability theory — in particular for the notion of
expectation. See
Edwards82
for a historical and
Ma16
for
a popular account. The problem of points involves a game between two
players that is terminated prematurely and where the stakes so far
have to be divided between the two. The solution there is to look at
the remaining number of steps for winning (and associated
probabilities) for each of the players. These remaining steps
translate directly into lengths of two tubes, one for each player,
with multinomial draws, see Subsection
5.1
below for
further details.

The current urn & tubes set-up ‘inverts’ the problem of points, and
also generalises it in two ways: (1) urns & tubes are analysed is in
fully multivariate form, and (2) the analysis covers the three drawing
modes described above.

Although the same urn & tubes setting is used both for first-full and
for negative distributions, these distributions are really
different. First of all, negative distributions have the natural
numbers
{\mathbb{N}}
as sample space, with infinite support — in
multinomial and Pólya mode. To
k\in{\mathbb{N}}
the probability is
assigned that
all
tubes are full, for the first time, after
k

draws — where some tubes may overflow, while others are not full
yet. Such negative distributions are studied in the literature, see

e.g.

Panaretos81
;
SchusterS87
;
SibuyaYS64
and the
textbooks
JohnsonKK05
;
JohnsonKB97
, but typically with one tube
only. Still, these negative distributions are not mainstream, and are
even called ‘forgotten’, see

. Here we describe
negative distributions, in the general urn & tubes setting, in fully
multivariate form, for all three drawing modes (“0”, “-1”,
“+1”).

In this paper we make extensive use of multisets, like in other recent
publications
Jacobs19b
;
Jacobs21b
;
Jacobs21a
;
JacobsS20
. A
multiset is like a subset, except that elements may occur multiple
times. For instance, an urn is a multiset, over the set of colours. A
draw of multiple balls from such an urn is a multiset. Also, the tubes
of different colours are represented as a multiset. Multisets form the
proper formalism for multivariate probabilities, see
Section
3
below. Sending an arbitrary set
X
to the set
of multisets over
X
has the structure of a monad. Similarly, taking
distributions over a set forms a monad. These monad structures play an
important role in the various ways that the multinomial and
hypergeometric (and Pólya) operations, as Kleisli maps, can be
composed, see
Jacobs21b
and
Jacobs21d
. In this paper the
underlying categorical structure is kept in the background. This is a
deliberate choice, in order not to limit the potential audience. For
instance, in the beginning of Section
6
the
composition steps for Markov models with output are spelled-out
concretely; their abstract categorical form as Kleisli composition is
elaborated in the appendix.

This paper is organised as follows. It starts with a concrete
description of first-full distributions, for all three modes (“0”,
“-1”, “+1”), in Section
2
. Subsequently,
Section
3
introduces relevant notation and terminology
for multisets and distributions, and
Section
4
formulates multivariate versions
of the multinomial, hypergeometric and Pólya distributions, as
distributions on multisets of a fixed size. For snappy formulation of
the hypergeometric and Póly distributions we use binomial
coefficients with multisets instead of numbers, both for ordinary
binomial coefficients and for the multichoose version. We introduce
suitable generalisations of Vandermonde’s formula, for multisets, also
in multichoose form.

The next two sections are devoted to first-full distributions. They
are defined in Section
5
in a pointwise manner, as
sums over multisets. These probabilities are illustrated in several
bar plots. Next, Section
6
introduces three
probabilistic automata, in the form of Markov models with output,
which are used to show that we actually get three distributions, with
probabilities adding up to one. The heart of the argument is that
composition (iteration) of the steps of these automata preserves
distributions.

Section
7
introduces and illustrates negative
distributions in the urns & tubes setting. We pay special attention
to the bivariate case, with one tube only, which is the form in which
they occur in the literature. We illustrate how the probabilities add
up to one, and thus yield actual distributions, vie the same
compositional argument, by sketching the relevant Markov models with
output.

These descriptions of the first-full distributions set the scene for
two additional topics. It is known that the hypergeometric and Pólya
distributions can be obtained via conditioning from binomial and from
negative binomial distributions. Section
8
recalls
these results in the current setting, in uniform descriptions.
Finally, Section
9
concentrates on the bivariate
first-full and negative distributions, with two tubes. It translates
the fact that probabilities in these distributions add up to one into
number-theoretic corollaries. These results seem to be new. It is left
as a challenge to prove them directly.

Examples of first-full distributions

This section illustrates how first-full distributions come about, in
the three drawing modes. We keep things simple at this stage and use
only two colours, written
R
for red and
B
for blue. We assume
length
2

3
for the blue tube. We
briefly describe the draw probabilities in the three modes, in this

•

In the multinomial mode “0”, we assume that there are three
balls in the urn, one red and two blue. The probability of drawing
red is thus
\frac{1}{3}
and for blue it is
\frac{2}{3}
. These
probabilities remain the same, since drawn balls are returned to the
urn. This “0” case is elaborated in
Example
1
below.

•

In the hypergeometric “-1” mode we assume that the urn
initially has three red and six blue balls. The initial probability
of drawing red from this urn is thus
\frac{3}{9}=\frac{1}{3}
. It
leaves an urn with two red and six blue balls. So the probability of
drawing a second red ball from the resulting urn is
\frac{2}{8}=\frac{1}{4}
. Example
2
gives the
first-full details in this mode.

•

In our illustration of the Pólya “+1” mode we assume
initially just one red and one blue ball in the urn. The probability
of drawing red is thus initially
\frac{1}{2}
. Upon drawing red,
not only the drawn red ball, but also an additional red ball, is
added to the urn, so that it subsequently contains three balls, two

below.

3
Preliminaries, on multisets and distributions

We briefly describe the notation and terminology for multisets
and distributions, in two separate subsections.

3.1
Multisets

As mentioned in the introduction, a multiset (or bag) is a finite
‘subset’ in which elements may occur multiple times. We use a ‘ket’
notation
|{\kern 1.00006pt}-{\kern 1.00006pt}\rangle
borrowed from quantum theory, as convenient way of
writing such multisets. For instance, the initial urn with tree red
and six blue balls in Example
2
forms a
multiset
3|{\kern 1.00006pt}R{\kern 1.00006pt}\rangle+6|{\kern 1.00006pt}B{\kern 1.00006pt}\rangle
. And the three tubes
in (
1
) form a multiset
3|{\kern 1.00006pt}R{\kern 1.00006pt}\rangle+6|{\kern 1.00006pt}B{\kern 1.00006pt}\rangle+5|{\kern 1.00006pt}G{\kern 1.00006pt}\rangle
. In general, a multiset over a set
X
is a finite formal
combination
\sum_{i}n_{i}|{\kern 1.00006pt}x_{i}{\kern 1.00006pt}\rangle
with
n_{i}\in{\mathbb{N}}
and
x_{i}\in X
. Alternatively, a multiset is a function
\varphi\colon X\rightarrow{\mathbb{N}}
with finite support
\mathrm{supp}(\varphi)=\{x\in X\;|\;\varphi(x)>0\}
. The number
\varphi(x)\in{\mathbb{N}}
tells
how many times the element
x
occurs in the multiset
\varphi
. We
freely switch between the formal sum and the function notation.

We shall write
\mathcal{M}(X)
for the set of multisets over
X
. Notice
that each multiset is finite, in our description, but the underlying
set
X
itself need not be finite. Via pointwise addition, multisets
form a commutative monoid, and in fact,
\mathcal{M}(X)
is the free
commutative monoid on
X
, via the unit map
\eta\colon X\rightarrow\mathcal{M}(X)
, given by
\eta(x)=1|{\kern 1.00006pt}x{\kern 1.00006pt}\rangle
. We shall write

\mathbf{0}\in\mathcal{M}(X)
for the empty multiset, with
\mathbf{0}(x)=0
for all

x\in X
.

We associate several numbers with a multiset.

Definition 1

For a multiset
\varphi\in\mathcal{M}(X)
, write:

1.

\|\varphi\|\coloneqq\sum_{x}\varphi(x)
for the size of

\varphi
, taking multiplicities into account;

2.

\varphi{\kern-2.5pt}\raisebox{-2.5pt}{\includegraphics[width]{exclamation}}\coloneqq\prod_{x}\varphi(x)!
for the
multiset factorial;

3.

(\varphi)\coloneqq\displaystyle\frac{\|\varphi\|!}{\varphi{\kern-2.5pt}\raisebox{-2.5pt}{\includegraphics[width]{exclamation}}}
for the
multinomial coefficient.

We are often interested in multisets of a particular size
K\in{\mathbb{N}}
,
so we define a subset:

\begin{array}[]{rcl}\mathcal{M}[K](X)&\coloneqq&\{\varphi\in\mathcal{M}(X)\;|\;\|\varphi\|=K\}.\end{array}

This
\mathcal{M}[K]
is a functor, but not a monad.

Sequences can be turned into multisets, via ‘accumulator’ functions

\mathsl{acc}\colon X^{K}\rightarrow\mathcal{M}[K](X)
, given by
\mathsl{acc}(x_{1},\ldots,x_{K})\coloneqq 1|{\kern 1.00006pt}x_{1}{\kern 1.00006pt}\rangle+\cdots+1|{\kern 1.00006pt}x_{K}{\kern 1.00006pt}\rangle
. Thus, for
instance,
\mathsl{acc}(a,a,b,a)=3|{\kern 1.00006pt}a{\kern 1.00006pt}\rangle+1|{\kern 1.00006pt}b{\kern 1.00006pt}\rangle
. The multinomial
coefficient
(\varphi)
is used in this paper in the following
two ways.

Fact 1

1.

For a multiset
\varphi
there are

(\varphi)
lists that accumulate to
\varphi
, that is,

\big|\,\mathsl{acc}^{-1}(\varphi)\,\big|=(\varphi)
.

2.

For real numbers
a_{1},\ldots,a_{n}
, the multinomial theorem says:

\begin{array}[b]{rcl}\big(a_{1}+\cdots+a_{n}\big)^{K}&=&\displaystyle\sum_{\varphi\in\mathcal{M}[K](\{1,\ldots n\})}(\varphi)\cdot\textstyle{\displaystyle\prod}_{i}\,a_{i}^{\varphi(i)}.\end{array}

[formula]

Multisets can be ordered pointwise, giving rise to some subtle distinctions.

Definition 2

Let
\varphi,\psi\in\mathcal{M}(X)
be given. We write:

1.

\varphi\leq\psi
if
\varphi(x)\leq\psi(x)
for all
x\in X
;
in that case we define the multiset difference
\psi-\varphi
via
pointwise subtraction, as:
(\psi-\varphi)(x)=\psi(x)-\varphi(x)
;

2.

\varphi\leq_{K}\psi
if
\|\varphi\|=K
and
\varphi\leq\psi
;

3.

\varphi<\psi
if
\varphi\leq\psi
but
\varphi\neq\psi
;

4.

\varphi\prec\psi
if
\varphi(x)<\psi(x)
for all
x\in X
.

The relation
\prec
will be called
fully below
. It
is different from
<
,
e.g.
in:

\begin{array}[]{rclcrcl}2|{\kern 1.00006pt}a{\kern 1.00006pt}\rangle+3|{\kern 1.00006pt}b{\kern 1.00006pt}\rangle&<&3|{\kern 1.00006pt}a{\kern 1.00006pt}\rangle+3|{\kern 1.00006pt}b{\kern 1.00006pt}\rangle&\mbox{\qquad and \qquad}&2|{\kern 1.00006pt}a{\kern 1.00006pt}\rangle+2|{\kern 1.00006pt}b{\kern 1.00006pt}\rangle&\prec&3|{\kern 1.00006pt}a{\kern 1.00006pt}\rangle+3|{\kern 1.00006pt}b{\kern 1.00006pt}\rangle.\end{array}

For multisets
\varphi,\psi\in\mathcal{M}(X)
with
\varphi\leq\psi
,
we define the
multiset binomial
as:

\begin{array}[]{rcl}\displaystyle\binom{\psi}{\varphi}\hskip 2.0pt\coloneqq\hskip 2.0pt\displaystyle\frac{\psi{\kern-2.5pt}\raisebox{-2.5pt}{\includegraphics[width]{exclamation}}}{\varphi{\kern-2.5pt}\raisebox{-2.5pt}{\includegraphics[width]{exclamation}}\cdot(\psi-\varphi){\kern-2.5pt}\raisebox{-2.5pt}{\includegraphics[width]{exclamation}}}&=&\displaystyle\frac{\prod_{x}\psi(x)!}{\big(\prod_{x}\varphi(x)!\big)\cdot\big(\prod_{x}(\psi(x)-\varphi(x)\big)!)}\\[10.00002pt]
&=&\displaystyle\prod_{x\in X}\,\frac{\psi(x)!}{\varphi(x)!\cdot(\psi(x)-\varphi(x))!}\\[10.00002pt]
&=&\displaystyle\prod_{x\in X}\,\binom{\psi(x)}{\varphi(x)}.\end{array}

(2)

Intuitively, this is the number of ways
\varphi
can sit
inside
\psi
.

The next result guarantees that hypergeometric draws form a
distribution. It is well known, but not in this form given below, with
binomials for multisets.

Proposition 1

For a multiset
\psi\in\mathcal{M}(X)
of size
L=\|\psi\|
and for
a number
K\leq L
,

\begin{array}[]{rcl}\displaystyle\sum_{\varphi\leq_{K}\psi}\,\binom{\psi}{\varphi}&=&\displaystyle\binom{L}{K}.\end{array}

The binary case, when the set
X
has two elements, is known as
Vandermonde’s formula, see (
4
) below. The
above generalisation can be obtained from it by induction on the
number of elements in the support of
\psi
. For completeness, we
include the proof. It uses Pascal’s rule, which says:

\begin{array}[]{rcl}\displaystyle\binom{n}{m}+\binom{n}{m+1}&=&\displaystyle\binom{n+1}{m}.\end{array}

(3)

We use induction on the number of elements in the support

\mathrm{supp}(\psi)
of the multiset
\psi
. We go through some initial
values explicitly. If the number of elements is
0
, then
\psi=\mathbf{0}
and so
L=0=K
and
\varphi\leq_{K}\psi
means
\varphi=\mathbf{0}
, so that the result holds. Similarly, if

3.2
Probability distributions

In this paper we concentrate on finite, discrete probability
distributions. Such a distribution, over a set
X
, is a finite formal
convex combination
\sum_{i}r_{i}|{\kern 1.00006pt}x_{i}{\kern 1.00006pt}\rangle
with
r_{i}\in[0,1]

satisfying
\sum_{i}r_{i}=1
and with
x_{i}\in X
. Alternatively,
it may be described as a function
\omega\colon X\rightarrow[0,1]

with finite support
\mathrm{supp}(\omega)\coloneqq\{x\in X\;|\;\omega(x)>0\}
and with
\sum_{x}\omega(x)=1
. We shall write
\mathcal{D}(X)
for
the set of distributions on a set
X
. This
\mathcal{D}
forms a monad,
just like
\mathcal{M}
.

Distributions on a product set
X\times Y
are often called

joint
distributions. One way to obtains such distributions is
to put
\omega\in\mathcal{D}(X)
and
\rho\in\mathcal{D}(Y)
in parallel as

\omega\otimes\rho\in\mathcal{D}(X\times Y)
, where:

\begin{array}[]{rcl}\omega\otimes\rho&=&\displaystyle\sum_{x\in X,y\in Y}\,\omega(x)\cdot\rho(y)\,\big|{\kern 1.00006pt}x,y{\kern 1.00006pt}\big\rangle.\end{array}

We then write
\omega^{K}=\omega\otimes\cdots\otimes\omega\in\mathcal{D}(X^{K})
, for numbers
K\geq 1
.

Each non-empty multiset can be turned into a distribution, via
normalisation. We shall call this operation
frequentist
learning
, written as
\mathsl{Flrn}
, since it involves learning by
counting. Explicitly:

\begin{array}[]{rclcrcl}\mathsl{Flrn}\left({\displaystyle\sum}_{i}\,n_{i}|{\kern 1.00006pt}x_{i}{\kern 1.00006pt}\rangle\right)&\coloneqq&{\displaystyle\sum}_{i}\,\displaystyle\frac{n_{i}}{n}|{\kern 1.00006pt}x_{i}{\kern 1.00006pt}\rangle&\mbox{\quad where \quad}&n&=&\sum_{i}n_{i}.\end{array}

Alternatively,
\mathsl{Flrn}(\varphi)(x)=\displaystyle\frac{\varphi(x)}{\|\varphi\|}
, or simply,
\mathsl{Flrn}(\varphi)=\displaystyle\frac{1}{\|\varphi\|}\cdot\varphi
.

Multisets and distributions as defined above have finite support. We
shall also need (discrete) distributions with infinite support. Therefore
we define, for an arbitrary set
X
,

\begin{array}[]{rcl}\mathcal{D}_{\infty}(X)&\coloneqq&\{\omega\colon X\rightarrow[0,1]\;|\;\sum_{x}\omega(x)=1\}.\end{array}

It can be shown that the support of
\omega\in\mathcal{D}_{\infty}(X)
is
necessarily countable or finite. In practice one typically encounters

X={\mathbb{N}}
. For instance, the Poisson distribution can be described as
an element of
\mathcal{D}_{\infty}({\mathbb{N}})
. Later on we shall describe negative
distributions that also live in
\mathcal{D}_{\infty}({\mathbb{N}})
.

4
Multinomial, hypergeometric, and Pólya
distributions

This section introduces the multinomial, hypergeometric and Pólya
distributions, in multivariate form. This is most conveniently done
via (binomial / multichoose) coefficients for multisets, which is
non-standard. The formulations that are used below can be derived in a
compositional manner via iterated drawing of single elements, using a
suitable form of Kleisli composition, see
Jacobs21a
;
Jacobs19d

for details.

4.1
Multinomial distributions

Since the urn remains unchanged for multinomial draws, it is most
appropriate to describe it as a distribution
\omega\in\mathcal{D}(X)
,
for a set of colours
X
. The multinomial distribution

\mathsl{mn}[K](\omega)
is a distribution on draws of size
K
, and
thus an element of the set
\mathcal{D}\big(\mathcal{M}[K](X)\big)

\begin{array}[]{rcl}\mathsl{mn}[K](\omega)&\coloneqq&\displaystyle\sum_{\varphi\in\mathcal{M}[K](X)}\,(\varphi)\cdot\prod_{x\in X}\omega(x)^{\varphi(x)}\,\big|{\kern 1.00006pt}\varphi{\kern 1.00006pt}\big\rangle.\end{array}

(7)

one by the Multinomial Theorem, see
Fact
1
(

Proposition
1

\begin{array}[]{rcl}\mathsl{hg}[K](\upsilon)&\coloneqq&\displaystyle\sum_{\varphi\leq_{K}\upsilon}\,\frac{\binom{\upsilon}{\varphi}}{\binom{L}{K}}\,\big|{\kern 1.00006pt}\varphi{\kern 1.00006pt}\big\rangle.\end{array}

4.3
Pólya distributions

The Pólya distribution can be described in a similar way, using the
multichoose binomial coefficients. It yields a distribution on
multisets of size
K
, for a non-empty urn
\upsilon
, via:

\begin{array}[]{rcl}\mathsl{pl}[K](\upsilon)&\coloneqq&\displaystyle\sum_{\varphi\in\mathcal{M}[K](\mathrm{supp}(\upsilon))}\,\frac{\big(\!\binom{\upsilon}{\varphi}\!\big)}{\big(\!\binom{L}{K}\!\big)}\,\big|{\kern 1.00006pt}\varphi{\kern 1.00006pt}\big\rangle.\end{array}

(10)

This is well-defined by Proposition
2
. A
subtle point is that the draws
\varphi
must be restricted to
elements that occur in the urn
\upsilon
. That’s achieved by summing over

\varphi\in\mathcal{M}[K](\mathrm{supp}(\upsilon))
, so that
\mathrm{supp}(\varphi)\subseteq\mathrm{supp}(\upsilon)
.

The Pólya distribution is known
Mahmoud08
, sometimes as
Dirichlet-multinomial. Its formulation in terms of multichoose
multinomial coefficients of multisets (
10
), in analogy
with the multinomial coefficients of multisets in the hypergeometric
distribution (
9
), seems new. Formulation that come
close are
(
SibuyaYS64
, Eqn. (A.1))

or
(
JohnsonKB97
, Eqn. (40.7))
. The details that this captures the
Pólya urn — where a drawn ball is returned together with an
additional copy of the same colour — are elaborated
in
Jacobs19d
.

6
First-full yields distributions

Our goal in this section is to prove that the probabilities in the
three pointwise first-full formulations in
Definition
3
all add up to one, and thus form
proper probability distributions. The trick is to use the multiset of
tubes as a position in a probabilistic automaton that changes with
every draw-and-drop action. The automaton precisely records the
relevant probabilities and terminates after a finite number of
iterations, with a first-full distribution on colours as result. This
works because in each composition step distributions are
preserved. Hence, if we start with a distribution, we will also end up
with a distribution, namely a first-full one.

We use Markov models with output (MOOs) as probabilistic
automata. They can be described as functions (coalgebras) of the form:

\textstyle{Y\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces}
\scriptstyle{c}
\textstyle{\mathcal{D}\big(Y+X\big)}

(12)

where
Y
is a set of positions and
X
is a set of outputs.
The
+
is a coproduct (disjoint union). We will not use separate
‘coprojection’ functions for these coproducts; the types of the
elements will make it clear whether they live in the left or right
component of a coproduct
Y+X
. What’s important is that a function

c
as above can be composed with itself, giving the required
iterations (or transitions) of the automaton: with a successor
position in
Y
the automaton can continue, and with an output in
X
,
the automaton halts.

A compositional argument underlying the next
iterations (
13
) of an automaton (
12
) is
provided in the appendix. At this stage we use such iterations
c^{n}\colon Y\rightarrow\mathcal{D}(Y+X)
, for
n\in{\mathbb{N}}
, via the concrete
formulations given below, where
y\in Y
is a start position.

\begin{array}[]{rcl}c^{0}(y)&\coloneqq&1\big|{\kern 1.00006pt}y{\kern 1.00006pt}\big\rangle\\[3.00003pt]
c^{n+1}(y)&\coloneqq&\displaystyle\sum_{z\in Y+X}\textstyle\left(\sum_{y^{\prime}\in Y}c(y)(y^{\prime})\cdot c^{n}(y^{\prime})(z)\right)\big|{\kern 1.00006pt}z{\kern 1.00006pt}\big\rangle\;+\;\displaystyle\sum_{x\in X}c(y)(x)\big|{\kern 1.00006pt}x{\kern 1.00006pt}\big\rangle.\end{array}

(13)

The first sum defines the transitions and the second sum the
outputs. Notice that these are defined as proper distributions. Hence
via iterated composition only distributions arise. This fact will be
crucial.

In the next three subsections we define three appropriate Markov models
with output (
12
) with transitions that incorporate the
first-full steps.

Lemma 6

Consider the above Markov model with output

for a

.

1.

For a multiset

\hskip-5.0pt\begin{array}[]{rcl}\mathsl{MN}(\omega)^{n+1}(\tau)(x)&=&\displaystyle\sum\,\Big\{\,\mathsl{mn}[K](\omega)(\chi)\cdot\omega(x)\,\Big|\,K\leq n\mbox{ and }\chi\leq\tau-\mathbf{1}\\
&&\hskip 50.00008pt\mbox{ with }\|\chi\|=K\mbox{ and }(\tau\!-\!\chi)(x)=1\,\Big\}.\end{array}

\begin{array}[]{rcl}\mathsl{MN}(\omega)^{n+1}(\tau)(\varphi)&=&\displaystyle\sum_{x\in X}\omega(x)\cdot\mathsl{MN}(\omega)^{n}(\tau\!-\!1|{\kern 1.00006pt}x{\kern 1.00006pt}\rangle))(\varphi)\\[11.99998pt]

Theorem 7

Let set
X
have
N
elements and let tubes
\tau\in\mathcal{M}_{\geq 1}(X)
have size (combined length)
L=\|\tau\|\geq N
. For

\omega\in\mathcal{D}(X)
one has:

\begin{array}[]{rclcrcl}\mathrm{supp}\Big(\mathsl{MN}(\omega)^{L-N+1}(\tau)\Big)&\subseteq&X,&\mbox{\hskip 20.44434ptand then\hskip 20.44434pt}&\mathsl{MN}(\omega)^{L-N+1}(\tau)&=&\mathsl{mnff}(\omega,\tau).\end{array}

In particular, this shows that multinomial first-full

\mathsl{mnff}(\omega,\tau)
is a probability distribution, with probabilities
adding up to one.

Since
\tau\geq\mathbf{1}
, we have
L=\|\tau\|\geq\|\mathbf{1}\|=N
. With
each transition of the Markov model
\mathsl{MN}(\omega)
, say going from
multiset
\varphi
to
\varphi^{\prime}
, one has
\|\varphi^{\prime}\|=\|\varphi\|-1
. Hence after
L-N
steps, starting from
\tau
, at most
a multiset of singletons remains. It transitions to single elements in
one step. Hence after at most
L-N+1
steps,
\mathsl{MN}(\omega)(\tau)

stabilises as distribution over elements
x\in X
, in the

X
-component of
\mathcal{M}_{\geq 1}(X)+X
. By
Lemma
6
(
2
) we then get:

\begin{array}[]{rcl}\mathsl{MN}(\omega)^{L-N+1}(\tau)(x)&=&\displaystyle\sum\,\Big\{\,\mathsl{mn}[K](\omega)(\chi)\cdot\omega(x)\,\Big|\,K\leq L-N\mbox{ and }\chi\in\mathcal{M}[K](X)\\
&&\hskip 60.00009pt\mbox{ with }\chi\leq\tau-\mathbf{1}\mbox{ and }(\tau-\chi)(x)=1\,\Big\}\\[5.0pt]
&=&\displaystyle\sum\,\Big\{\,\mathsl{mn}(\omega)(\chi)\cdot\omega(x)\,\Big|\;\chi\prec\tau\mbox{ with }\chi(x)=\tau(x)-1\,\Big\}\\[5.0pt]
&=&\mathsl{mnff}(\omega,\tau)(x),\qquad\mbox{see Definition\penalty\ \ref{FirstFullDstDef}\penalty\ \eqref{FirstFullDstDefMulnom}.}\end{array}

We use that
\chi\leq\tau-\mathbf{1}
iff
\chi\prec\tau
,
where, recall,
\prec
is the fully-below order.
\square

6.2
Hypergeometric first-full distributions

Recall that for a hypergeometric first-full distribution we use an

\upsilon

removed, and then dropped in the right tube. Thus, the probability of
drawing a particularly coloured ball changes throughout the filling of

with output, we have to carry the urn along. This leads to the
following set-up.

Let’s use the
ad-hoc

\begin{array}[]{rcl}\mathcal{M}_{\geq\geq 1}(X)&\coloneqq&\{(\upsilon,\tau)\in\mathcal{M}(X)\times\mathcal{M}(X)\;|\;\upsilon\geq\tau\geq\mathbf{1}\}.\end{array}

MMO.

\textstyle{\mathcal{D}\Big(\mathcal{M}_{\geq\geq 1}(X)+X\Big)}
\textstyle{(\upsilon,\tau)\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces}

6.3

The Pólya first-full mode is very similar to the hypergeometric

\upsilon
with
as only requirement
\upsilon\geq\mathbf{1}
so that at least one ball of each
colour is present. We thus use a Pólya MMO of the following form.

Remark 1

In the end we have two (equivalent) ways to compute first-full
distributions, namely via their pointwise formulations (in
Definition
3
) and via their three MMO’s, as
described above. The latter can easily be turned into recursive
definitions. Experiments with both implementations show that the
recursive approach is slower than the one based on the
definitions. This is not surprising since the recursive approach
computes probabilities for sequences, with much duplication, instead
of for multisets (as accumulations of those sequences). In contrast,
reasoning with sequences is easier than with multisets. In the end
that is the whole reason why we use the MMO-approach — for proving
that combined first-full probabilities form a distribution.

7
Negative distributions

The urns & tubes set-up that we have used to introduce first-full
distributions can also be used to describe ‘negative’ distributions.
The latter are known from the literature, in bivariate form, with one
tube only. Here we use our multiset-based approach to describe them
systematically, in multivariate form, for all three modes
(multinomial, hypergeometric and Pólya). We concentrate on the
definitions and on illustrations. The fact that these definitions lead
to actual probability distributions is addressed, by giving the
corresponding Markov models with output, but without all the
mathematical details. After all, these distributions are not new.

We start with an example. Consider a group of people consisting of
five males (
M
) and four females (
F
). From this group we like to
form a committee with two male and two female members. Iteratively we
choose members from the group, at random, until the committee is
formed. How many choices are needed? More precisely, what is the
probability — that the committee is first formed — for each number
of choices. We might be done after four choices, if they immediately
involve two males and two females. But we may also first pick three
men, and then two females, which involves five choices. What is the
highest possible number of choices? It is seven, when all five men are
chosen, before the two females. Hence this situation involves a
distribution on the set
\{4,5,6,7\}
. It is:

\textstyle\frac{10}{21}\big|{\kern 1.00006pt}4{\kern 1.00006pt}\big\rangle\,+\,\frac{20}{63}\big|{\kern 1.00006pt}5{\kern 1.00006pt}\big\rangle\,+\,\frac{10}{63}\big|{\kern 1.00006pt}6{\kern 1.00006pt}\big\rangle\,+\,\frac{1}{21}\big|{\kern 1.00006pt}7{\kern 1.00006pt}\big\rangle\quad\mbox{in}\quad\vbox{\hbox{\includegraphics[width]{committee-selection}}}

How does it come about? We can see the group of people as an
urn
\upsilon=5|{\kern 1.00006pt}M{\kern 1.00006pt}\rangle+4|{\kern 1.00006pt}F{\kern 1.00006pt}\rangle
from which we ‘draw’ candidate
committee members, in hypergeometric mode: after drawing a member from
the urn/group, this person is either put in the committee, or is
skipped, when there are already two committee members with this
person’s gender in the committee.

•

The probability of being done in four steps is given by the
hypergeometric distribution at multiset
2|{\kern 1.00006pt}M{\kern 1.00006pt}\rangle+2|{\kern 1.00006pt}F{\kern 1.00006pt}\rangle
. Indeed,

\begin{array}[]{rcl}\mathsl{hg}[4](\upsilon)\Big(2|{\kern 1.00006pt}M{\kern 1.00006pt}\rangle+2|{\kern 1.00006pt}F{\kern 1.00006pt}\rangle\Big)&=&\frac{10}{21}.\end{array}

•

We may need five steps in two cases: (1) when we first choose three
men and one woman, in any order, and finally a woman, or (2) when we
first choose three women and one man, in any order, and finally a
man. This leads to the probability:

\begin{array}[]{rcccl}\mathsl{hg}[4](\upsilon)\Big(3|{\kern 1.00006pt}M{\kern 1.00006pt}\rangle+1|{\kern 1.00006pt}F{\kern 1.00006pt}\rangle\Big)\cdot\frac{3}{5}+\mathsl{hg}[4](\upsilon)\Big(1|{\kern 1.00006pt}M{\kern 1.00006pt}\rangle+3|{\kern 1.00006pt}F{\kern 1.00006pt}\rangle\Big)\cdot\frac{4}{5}&=&\frac{20}{63}\cdot\frac{3}{5}+\frac{10}{63}\cdot\frac{4}{5}&=&\frac{20}{63}.\end{array}

•

We need six steps when we first choose four men and one woman, and
then one woman, or when we first choose one man and four women. In
the latter case we are done at the next selection, because we can
only choose a male. The associated probability is thus obtained as:

\begin{array}[]{rcccl}\mathsl{hg}[5](\upsilon)\Big(4|{\kern 1.00006pt}M{\kern 1.00006pt}\rangle+1|{\kern 1.00006pt}F{\kern 1.00006pt}\rangle\Big)\cdot\frac{3}{4}+\mathsl{hg}[5](\upsilon)\Big(1|{\kern 1.00006pt}M{\kern 1.00006pt}\rangle+4|{\kern 1.00006pt}F{\kern 1.00006pt}\rangle\Big)&=&\frac{10}{63}\cdot\frac{3}{4}+\frac{5}{126}&=&\frac{10}{63}.\end{array}

•

Finally, there is only one possibility that requires the maximum
number of seven steps, namely when we first choose five men and one woman.
The associated probability is simply:

\begin{array}[]{rcl}\mathsl{hg}[6](\upsilon)\Big(5|{\kern 1.00006pt}M{\kern 1.00006pt}\rangle+1|{\kern 1.00006pt}F{\kern 1.00006pt}\rangle\Big)&=&\frac{1}{21}.\end{array}

In this example we may consider the committee that needs to be filled
with two males and two females as a pair of tubes, both of length two.
Thus, the urns & tubes model can be used here as well, but with a
different question, namely what is the probability of filling
all

tubes in a certain number of steps.

Thus, abstractly, our starting point is the same as in the previous
section, see Picture
1
: we have an urn filled with
coloured balls, together with coloured tubes. The question that we now
look at is as follows.

Suppose we draw
k
balls from the urn, to fill the tubes, for

k\in{\mathbb{N}}
. What is the probability that all tubes are full for the
first time after drawing these
k
balls? This means that there is one
tube that becomes full with the
k
-th ball, while sufficiently many
balls — typically more than needed — have already been drawn to
fill all other tubes.

The filling of all tubes can be seen as a desired condition,
or as a risk. The probability distribution that we are after gives for
each
k\in{\mathbb{N}}
the probability of reaching this threshold condition
for the first time.

•

Historically the distributions that arise in this manner are
called
negative
. They are not very well known, and are even
called ‘forgotten’ in
MillerF07
. These negative distributions
may occur in different forms, depending on the mode of drawing
(“-1”, “0”, or “+1”). Accordingly, we shall speak of

negative hypergeometric
,
negative multinomial
, and

negative Pólya
distributions. Below we cover all three
modes.

•

The negative hypergeometric distribution has finite support,
since at some stage the urn is empty. In multinomial mode the urn
does not change, and in Pólya mode the urn grows in size. Hence in
these last two cases the support of the negative distributions are
infinite subsets of the natural numbers. Recall that we write

\mathcal{D}_{\infty}({\mathbb{N}})
for set of discrete distributions on
{\mathbb{N}}
with
(possibly) infinite support.

•

In the literature (see

e.g.

JohnsonKK05
;
MillerF07
;
Panaretos81
;
SchusterS87
;
SibuyaYS64
)
negative distributions are studied only for the (simple) case with a
single tube and usually with only two colours. Here we deal with the
general, multivariate and multi-tube, scenario, where there are
multiple colours and as many tubes as colours. Like before, we shall
write
\tau
for the tubes and
L\coloneqq\|\tau\|
for the sum of
the lengths of all tubes. We assume that
L>0
so that there is at
least one non-empty tube that can be filled. We shall write

\mathcal{M}_{*}(X)\hookrightarrow\mathcal{M}(X)
for the subset of

non-empty
multisets; we thus require

\tau\in\mathcal{M}_{*}(X)
. The negative distributions on
{\mathbb{N}}
that we
are after will ‘start at
L

k<L
, since one
needs to draw at least
L
balls to fill all tubes.

•

As in the first-fill case, in Section
5
,
there is a challenge to show that negative probabilities add up to
one, and thus form a proper distribution. Again we use Markov models
with output, like in the previous section, but without elaborating
all details. Previously, we only had finitely many possible
transitions. Here, in the negative setting, there may be infinitely
many transitions, leading to infinite supports.

Definition 4

The common one-tube situation

In the introduction to this section we mentioned that the negative
distributions that are commonly considered in the literature involve
one tube only. We describe what happens then, as special case of the
above formulations in Definition

, and recover
familiar formulations. It turns out, in all three drawing modes, that
the relevant probabilities can also be described via the
‘non-negative’ bivariate distribution.

Theorem 12

Let
X
be a set of colours, with a special fixed element
y\in X
,
and with single-tube multiset
\tau=m|{\kern 1.00006pt}y{\kern 1.00006pt}\rangle
for

1.

\omega\in\mathcal{D}(X)
satisfy

\begin{array}[]{rcl}\mathsl{nmn}\big(\omega,\,m|{\kern 1.00006pt}y{\kern 1.00006pt}\rangle\big)(m\!+\!k)&=&\displaystyle\frac{m}{m\!+\!k}\cdot\mathsl{mn}[m\!+\!k]\Big(\omega(y)|{\kern 1.00006pt}0{\kern 1.00006pt}\rangle+(1\!-\!\omega(y))|{\kern 1.00006pt}1{\kern 1.00006pt}\rangle\Big)\Big(m|{\kern 1.00006pt}0{\kern 1.00006pt}\rangle+k|{\kern 1.00006pt}1{\kern 1.00006pt}\rangle\Big)\\[5.0pt]

The latter expression involves the negative binomial
distribution, of the form:

\begin{array}[]{rcl}\mathsl{nbn}[m](s)&\coloneqq&\displaystyle\sum_{i\geq 0}\left({\kern-3.0pt}\binom{m}{i}{\kern-3.0pt}\right)\cdot s^{m}\cdot(1-s)^{i}\,\big|{\kern 1.00006pt}m+i{\kern 1.00006pt}\big\rangle\,\in\,\mathcal{D}_{\infty}({\mathbb{N}}).\end{array}

(16)

2.

Similarly, for an urn

\upsilon\in\mathcal{M}[L](X)

,

\begin{array}[]{rcl}\mathsl{nhg}\big(\upsilon,\,m|{\kern 1.00006pt}y{\kern 1.00006pt}\rangle\big)(m\!+\!k)&=&\displaystyle\frac{m}{m\!+\!k}\cdot\mathsl{hg}[m\!+\!k]\big(\upsilon(y)|{\kern 1.00006pt}0{\kern 1.00006pt}\rangle+(L\!-\!\upsilon(y))|{\kern 1.00006pt}1{\kern 1.00006pt}\rangle\big)\big(m|{\kern 1.00006pt}0{\kern 1.00006pt}\rangle+k|{\kern 1.00006pt}1{\kern 1.00006pt}\rangle\big)\\[5.0pt]

single sum over multisets:

\begin{array}[]{rcl}\mathsl{nmn}\big(\omega,m|{\kern 1.00006pt}y{\kern 1.00006pt}\rangle\big)(m\!+\!k)&=&\displaystyle\sum_{\varphi\in\mathcal{M}[m+k-1](X),\,\varphi(y)=m-1}\mathsl{mn}[m\!+\!k\!-\!1](\omega)(\varphi)\cdot\omega(y)\\[13.00005pt]
&=&\displaystyle\sum_{\varphi\in\mathcal{M}[k](X-y)}\,\mathsl{mn}[m\!+\!k\!-\!1](\omega)\big((m\!-\!1)|{\kern 1.00006pt}y{\kern 1.00006pt}\rangle+\varphi\big)\cdot\omega(y)\\[10.00002pt]
&=&\displaystyle\sum_{\varphi\in\mathcal{M}[k-m](X-y)}\,\frac{(m\!+\!k\!-\!1)!}{(m\!-\!1)!\cdot\varphi{\kern-2.5pt}\raisebox{-2.5pt}{\includegraphics[width]{exclamation}}}\cdot\omega(y)^{m}\cdot\prod_{x\neq y}\omega(x)^{\varphi(x)}\\[15.00002pt]
&=&\displaystyle\binom{m\!+\!k\!-\!1}{m\!-\!1}\cdot\omega(y)^{m}\cdot\sum_{\varphi\in\mathcal{M}[k](X-y)}\,\frac{k!}{\varphi{\kern-2.5pt}\raisebox{-2.5pt}{\includegraphics[width]{exclamation}}}\cdot\prod_{x\neq y}\omega(x)^{\varphi(x)}\\[10.00002pt]
&=&\displaystyle\left({\kern-3.0pt}\binom{m}{k}{\kern-3.0pt}\right)\cdot\omega(y)^{m}\cdot\textstyle\big(\sum_{x\neq y}\omega(x)\big)^{k}\qquad\mbox{by Fact\penalty\ \ref{CoefficientFact}\penalty\ \eqref{CoefficientFactMulnomThm}}\\[10.00002pt]
&=&\displaystyle\left({\kern-3.0pt}\binom{m}{k}{\kern-3.0pt}\right)\cdot\omega(y)^{m}\cdot(1-\omega(y))^{k}\\[8.00003pt]
&=&\mathsl{nbn}[m]\big(\omega(y)\big)(m\!+\!k).\end{array}

At the same time we can write:

\hskip-10.00002pt\begin{array}[]{rcl}\displaystyle\left({\kern-3.0pt}\binom{m}{k}{\kern-3.0pt}\right)\cdot\omega(y)^{m}\cdot(1-\omega(y))^{k}&=&\displaystyle\frac{m}{m\!+\!k}\cdot\binom{m\!+\!k}{k}\cdot\omega(y)^{m}\cdot(1-\omega(y))^{k}\\[10.00002pt]
&=&\displaystyle\frac{m}{m\!+\!k}\cdot\mathsl{mn}[m\!+\!k]\Big(\omega(y)|{\kern 1.00006pt}0{\kern 1.00006pt}\rangle+(1\!-\!\omega(y))|{\kern 1.00006pt}1{\kern 1.00006pt}\rangle\Big)\Big(m|{\kern 1.00006pt}0{\kern 1.00006pt}\rangle+k|{\kern 1.00006pt}1{\kern 1.00006pt}\rangle\Big).\end{array}

2.

We write
\upsilon^{\prime}=\upsilon-\upsilon(y)|{\kern 1.00006pt}y{\kern 1.00006pt}\rangle
for the urn from
which all balls of colour
y
have been removed.

\begin{array}[]{rcl}\lx@intercol\lx@add@cssclass{ltx_eqn_lefteqn}$\displaystyle\mathsl{nhg}\big(\upsilon,m|{\kern 1.00006pt}y{\kern 1.00006pt}\rangle\big)(m\!+\!k)$\mbox{}\hfil\lx@intercol\\[10.00002pt]
&=&\displaystyle\sum_{\varphi\leq_{m+k-1}\upsilon,\,\varphi(y)=m-1}\mathsl{hg}[m\!+\!k\!-\!1](\upsilon)(\varphi)\cdot\mathsl{Flrn}\big(\upsilon-\varphi\big)(y)\\[13.00005pt]
&=&\displaystyle\sum_{\varphi\leq_{k}\upsilon^{\prime}}\,\mathsl{hg}[m\!+\!k\!-\!1](\upsilon)\big((m\!-\!1)|{\kern 1.00006pt}y{\kern 1.00006pt}\rangle+\varphi\big)\cdot\mathsl{Flrn}\Big(\upsilon-(m\!-\!1)|{\kern 1.00006pt}y{\kern 1.00006pt}\rangle-\varphi\Big)(y)\\[10.00002pt]
&=&\displaystyle\sum_{\varphi\leq_{k}\upsilon^{\prime}}\,\frac{\binom{\upsilon(y)}{m-1}\cdot\prod_{x\neq y}\binom{\upsilon(x)}{\varphi(x)}}{\binom{L}{m+k-1}}\cdot\frac{\upsilon(y)-(m-1)}{L-(m+k-1)}\qquad\mbox{where }L=\|\upsilon\|\\[13.00005pt]
&=&\displaystyle\frac{m\cdot\binom{\upsilon(y)}{m}\cdot\sum_{\varphi\leq_{k}\upsilon^{\prime}}\binom{\upsilon^{\prime}}{\varphi}}{(m\!+\!k)\cdot\binom{L}{m+k}}\qquad\mbox{since }\;\textstyle\binom{n}{m}\cdot(n\!-\!m)=(m\!+\!1)\cdot\binom{n}{m+1}\\[13.00005pt]
&=&\displaystyle\frac{m}{m\!+\!k}\cdot\frac{\binom{\upsilon(y)}{m}\cdot\binom{L-\upsilon(y)}{k}}{\binom{L}{m+k}}\qquad\mbox{by Proposition\penalty\ \ref{HypgeomCountProp}}\\[11.99998pt]
&=&\displaystyle\frac{m}{m\!+\!k}\cdot\mathsl{hg}[m\!+\!k]\big(\upsilon(y)|{\kern 1.00006pt}0{\kern 1.00006pt}\rangle+(L\!-\!\upsilon(y))|{\kern 1.00006pt}1{\kern 1.00006pt}\rangle\big)\big(m|{\kern 1.00006pt}0{\kern 1.00006pt}\rangle+k|{\kern 1.00006pt}1{\kern 1.00006pt}\rangle\big).\end{array}

3.

In the Pólya case we proceed in a similar manner, for

\upsilon\in\mathcal{M}[L](X)
.

\displaystyle\mathsl{npl}\big(\upsilon,m|{\kern 1.00006pt}y{\kern 1.00006pt}\rangle\big)(m\!+\!k)

\displaystyle=\displaystyle\sum_{\varphi\in\mathcal{M}[m+k-1](X),\,\varphi(y)=m-1}\mathsl{pl}[m\!+\!k\!-\!1](\upsilon)(\varphi)\cdot\mathsl{Flrn}(\upsilon+\varphi)(y)

\displaystyle=\displaystyle\sum_{\varphi\in\mathcal{M}[k](X-y)}\,\mathsl{pl}[m\!+\!k\!-\!1](\upsilon)\big((m\!-\!1)|{\kern 1.00006pt}y{\kern 1.00006pt}\rangle+\varphi\big)

\displaystyle\hskip 100.00015pt\cdot\,\mathsl{Flrn}\Big(\upsilon+(m\!-\!1)|{\kern 1.00006pt}y{\kern 1.00006pt}\rangle+\varphi\Big)(y)

\displaystyle=\displaystyle\sum_{\varphi\in\mathcal{M}[k](X-y)}\,\frac{\big(\!\binom{\upsilon(y)}{m-1}\!\big)\cdot\prod_{x\neq y}\big(\!\binom{\upsilon(x)}{\varphi(x)}\!\big)}{\big(\!\binom{L}{m+k-1}\!\big)}\cdot\frac{\upsilon(y)+(m-1)}{L+(m+k-1)}

\displaystyle=\displaystyle\frac{m\cdot\big(\!\binom{\upsilon(y)}{m}\!\big)\cdot\sum_{\varphi\in\mathcal{M}[k](X-y)}\big(\!\binom{\upsilon^{\prime}}{\varphi}\!\big)}{(m\!+\!k)\cdot\big(\!\binom{L}{m+k}\!\big)}\qquad\mbox{where }\upsilon^{\prime}=\upsilon-\upsilon(y)|{\kern 1.00006pt}y{\kern 1.00006pt}\rangle

\displaystyle=\displaystyle\frac{m}{m\!+\!k}\cdot\frac{\big(\!\binom{\upsilon(y)}{m}\!\big)\cdot\big(\!\binom{L-\upsilon(y)}{k}\!\big)}{\big(\!\binom{L}{m+k}\!\big)}\qquad\mbox{by Proposition\penalty\ \ref{PolyaCountProp}}

\displaystyle=\displaystyle\frac{m}{m\!+\!k}\cdot\mathsl{pl}[m\!+\!k]\big(\upsilon(y)|{\kern 1.00006pt}0{\kern 1.00006pt}\rangle+(L\!-\!\upsilon(y))|{\kern 1.00006pt}1{\kern 1.00006pt}\rangle\big)\big(m|{\kern 1.00006pt}0{\kern 1.00006pt}\rangle+k|{\kern 1.00006pt}1{\kern 1.00006pt}\rangle\big).

\square

7.2
Negatives yield distributions

We will illustrate that the probabilities in the ‘negative’
formulations in Definition
4
yield actual
distributions. We shall proceed as in Section
6

and introduce appropriate Markov models with output (MMO). The
positions in these MMOs are tuples involving a ‘stage’ number

i\in{\mathbb{N}}
. Each step involves one of the following three options.

1.

For colour
x
with already full tube, so
\tau(x)=0
, one can
draw another ball of colour
x
and move to a next position in the
MMO. We then have an overflow situation for colour
x
so this next
position has the same tubes
\tau
and an incremented stage, which
change from
i
to
i+1
.

2.

In case colour
x
is the last one whose tube needs to be
filled, we have
\tau(x)>0
and
\|\tau\|=1
, or equivalently

\tau(x)=1
and
\tau(y)=0
for all
y\neq x
. Then we can draw
this last ball and move to the output
i+1
, from which no further
transitions are possible.

3.

When colour
x
’s tube is not full yet, and there are other
non-full tubes as well — so when
\tau(x)>0
and
\|\tau\|>1

— we can draw a ball of colour
x
and drop it in the tube of
colour
x
. The next position then involves new tubes
\tau-1|{\kern 1.00006pt}x{\kern 1.00006pt}\rangle
, where the number of missing balls of colour
x
is
reduced by
1
, with incremented stage
i+1
.

Negative multinomial distributions

The MMO for negative multinomials has pairs
(\tau,i)
as positions,
with tubes
\tau\in\mathcal{M}_{*}(X)
and stage
i\in{\mathbb{N}}
. The above three
options are captured as follows.

\textstyle{\mathcal{M}_{*}(X)\times{\mathbb{N}}\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces}
\scriptstyle{\mathsl{NMN}(\omega)}
\textstyle{\mathcal{D}\Big(\mathcal{M}_{*}(X)\times{\mathbb{N}}\;+\;{\mathbb{N}}\Big)}
\textstyle{(\tau,i)\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces}
\textstyle{{\begin{array}[t]{l}\displaystyle\sum_{x,\,\tau(x)=0}\!\omega(x)\big|{\kern 1.00006pt}\tau,i\!+\!1{\kern 1.00006pt}\big\rangle\,+\,\sum_{x,\,\tau(x)>0,\,\|\tau\|=1}\!\omega(x)\big|{\kern 1.00006pt}i\!+\!1{\kern 1.00006pt}\big\rangle\\[10.00002pt]
\qquad+\displaystyle\sum_{x,\,\tau(x)>0,\,\|\tau\|>1}\!\omega(x)\big|{\kern 1.00006pt}\tau\!-\!|{\kern 1.00006pt}x{\kern 1.00006pt}\rangle,i\!+\!1{\kern 1.00006pt}\big\rangle.\end{array}}}

(17)

Starting from initial position
(\tau,0)
one eventually
ends up with an output in
{\mathbb{N}}
. We reason informally from the
contrapositive: an infinite sequences
(\rho,k)\rightarrow(\rho,k+1)\rightarrow(\rho,k+2)\rightarrow\cdots
exists only if the colours

x\in\mathrm{supp}(\rho)
are never drawn when the size of the draws
goes to infinity. This is impossible.

Negative hypergeometric distributions

In the hypergeometric (and Pólya) mode the urn changes with every
draw, so we have to incorporate not only the tubes
\tau
but also the
urn
\upsilon
in the positions of our MMO. For convenience, we
introduce the following special notation.

\begin{array}[]{rcl}\mathcal{M}_{\geq*}(X)&\coloneqq&\{(\upsilon,\tau)\in\mathcal{M}_{*}(X)\times\mathcal{M}_{*}(X)\;|\;\upsilon\geq\tau\}.\end{array}

The inequality
\upsilon\geq\tau
expresses that the urn
contains sufficiently many balls of each colour to fill the
tubes. This inequality acts as an invariant for the following negative
hypergeometric MMO.

\textstyle{\mathcal{M}_{\geq*}(X)\times{\mathbb{N}}\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces}
\scriptstyle{\mathsl{NHG}}
\textstyle{\mathcal{D}\Big(\mathcal{M}_{\geq*}(X)\times{\mathbb{N}}+{\mathbb{N}}\Big)}
\textstyle{(\upsilon,\tau,i)\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces}
\textstyle{{\begin{array}[t]{l}\displaystyle\sum_{x,\,\tau(x)=0,\,\upsilon(x)>0}\!\mathsl{Flrn}(\upsilon)(x)\big|{\kern 1.00006pt}\upsilon\!-\!1|{\kern 1.00006pt}x{\kern 1.00006pt}\rangle,\tau,i\!+\!1{\kern 1.00006pt}\big\rangle\\[11.99998pt]
\qquad+\displaystyle\sum_{x,\,\tau(x)>0,\,\|\tau\|=1}\!\mathsl{Flrn}(\upsilon)(x)\big|{\kern 1.00006pt}i\!+\!1{\kern 1.00006pt}\big\rangle\\[13.00005pt]
\qquad+\displaystyle\sum_{x,\,\tau(x)>0,\,\|\tau\|>1}\!\mathsl{Flrn}(\upsilon)(x)\big|{\kern 1.00006pt}\upsilon\!-\!1|{\kern 1.00006pt}x{\kern 1.00006pt}\rangle,\tau\!-\!|{\kern 1.00006pt}x{\kern 1.00006pt}\rangle,i\!+\!1{\kern 1.00006pt}\big\rangle.\end{array}}}

(18)

It is obvious that there are no infinite transitions
starting from
(\upsilon,\tau,0)
since in each non-output step
the urn
\upsilon
decreases in size.

Negative Pólya

We now require that initially, the urn
\upsilon
contains for all
colours of the tubes
\tau
at least one ball. This can expressed as
inclusion of supports. Hence we define:

\begin{array}[]{rcl}\mathcal{M}_{\supseteq*}(X)&\coloneqq&\{(\upsilon,\tau)\in\mathcal{M}_{*}(X)\times\mathcal{M}_{*}(X)\;|\;\mathrm{supp}(\upsilon)\supseteq\mathrm{supp}(\tau)\}.\end{array}

This is used in the following negative Pólya MMO. It
looks very much like the hypergeometric one in (
18
),
with removal of balls from the urn replaced by addition.

\textstyle{\mathcal{M}_{\supseteq*}(X)\times{\mathbb{N}}\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces}
\scriptstyle{\mathsl{NPL}}
\textstyle{\mathcal{D}\Big(\mathcal{M}_{\supseteq*}(X)\times{\mathbb{N}}+{\mathbb{N}}\Big)}
\textstyle{(\upsilon,\tau,i)\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces}
\textstyle{{\begin{array}[t]{l}\displaystyle\sum_{x,\,\tau(x)=0}\!\mathsl{Flrn}(\upsilon)(x)\big|{\kern 1.00006pt}\upsilon\!+\!1|{\kern 1.00006pt}x{\kern 1.00006pt}\rangle,\tau,i\!+\!1{\kern 1.00006pt}\big\rangle\\[11.99998pt]
\qquad+\displaystyle\sum_{x,\,\tau(x)>0,\,\|\tau\|=1}\!\mathsl{Flrn}(\upsilon)(x)\big|{\kern 1.00006pt}i\!+\!1{\kern 1.00006pt}\big\rangle\\[13.00005pt]
\qquad+\displaystyle\sum_{x,\,\tau(x)>0,\,\|\tau\|>1}\!\mathsl{Flrn}(\upsilon)(x)\big|{\kern 1.00006pt}\upsilon\!+\!1|{\kern 1.00006pt}x{\kern 1.00006pt}\rangle,\tau\!-\!|{\kern 1.00006pt}x{\kern 1.00006pt}\rangle,i\!+\!1{\kern 1.00006pt}\big\rangle.\end{array}}}

(19)

Also in this case there are no infinite transitions from an
initial position
(\upsilon,\tau,0)
, because the probability that
certain colours do not occur in Pólya draws becomes zero as the size
of draws goes to infinity.

8
Hypergeometric and Pólya distributions via (negative)
binomials

In Section
4
we have have introduced the
(ordinary, non-negative) hypergeometric and Pólya distributions

\mathsl{hg}[K](\upsilon)
and
\mathsl{pl}[K](\upsilon)
, for an urn

\upsilon
. It is known that these distributions can also be obtained
via conditioning, namely of parallel binomials in the hypergeometric
case, and of parallel negative binomials in the Pólya case (see

e.g.

SibuyaYS64
, for the bivariate, and
also
Janardan74
for the multivariate case). These conditionings
build on Propositions
1
,
2
and
fit very well in the current account, and are therefore included here,
in fully multivariate form. In order to do so we need to recall the
basics of probabilistic conditioning, see

e.g.

Jacobs19b
;
Jacobs19c
;
Jacobs19d
;
Jacobs21b
;
Jacobs21a

for more information.

Let
\omega\in\mathcal{D}(X)
be distribution and
p\colon X\rightarrow[0,1]
be a (fuzzy) predicate. We write
\omega\models p\coloneqq\sum_{x}\omega(x)\cdot p(x)
for the validity (expected value) of
p

in
\omega
. If this validity is non-zero, we can define the updated
distribution
\omega|_{p}\in\mathcal{D}(X)
as the normalised product:

\begin{array}[]{rcl}\omega|_{p}(x)&\coloneqq&\displaystyle\frac{\omega(x)\cdot p(x)}{\omega\models p}.\end{array}

See
e.g.

Jacobs19b
;
Jacobs19c
;
Jacobs21a
for
more information.

The two propositions below describe the two conditioning results for
hypergeometric and Pólya distributions. They both use the following
sum predicate
\mathsl{sum}_{K}\colon{\mathbb{N}}^{\ell}\rightarrow[0,1]
,
for
K\in{\mathbb{N}}
.

\begin{array}[]{rcl}\mathsl{sum}_{K}(n_{1},\ldots,n_{\ell})&\coloneqq&\begin{cases}1&\mbox{if }n_{1}+\cdots+n_{\ell}=K\\
0&\mbox{otherwise.}\end{cases}\end{array}

(20)

Proposition 13

Conditioning parallel binomials with this sum
predicate (
20
) yields the hypergeometric distribution:
for
K\leq\sum_{i}k_{i}
,

\begin{array}[]{rcl}\big(\mathsl{bn}[k_{1}](r)\otimes\cdots\otimes\mathsl{bn}[k_{\ell}](r)\big)\big|_{\mathsl{sum}_{K}}&=&\mathsl{hg}[K]\big(\sum_{i}k_{i}|{\kern 1.00006pt}i{\kern 1.00006pt}\rangle\big).\end{array}

This works for any number
r\in[0,1]
.

We first compute the validity:

\displaystyle\mathsl{bn}[k_{1}](r)\otimes\cdots\otimes\mathsl{bn}[k_{\ell}](r)\models\mathsl{sum}_{K}

(21)

\displaystyle=\displaystyle\sum_{n_{i}\leq k_{i}}\,\big(\mathsl{bn}[k_{1}](r)\otimes\cdots\otimes\mathsl{bn}[k_{\ell}](r)\big)(n_{1},\ldots,n_{\ell})\cdot\mathsl{sum}_{K}(n_{1},\ldots,n_{\ell})

\displaystyle=\displaystyle\sum_{n_{i}\leq k_{i},\,{\scriptscriptstyle\sum_{i}}n_{i}\,=\,K}\,\mathsl{bn}[k_{1}](r)(n_{1})\cdot\ldots\cdot\mathsl{bn}[k_{\ell}](r)(n_{\ell})

\displaystyle=\displaystyle\sum_{n_{i}\leq k_{i},\,{\scriptscriptstyle\sum_{i}}n_{i}\,=\,K}\,\binom{k_{1}}{n_{1}}\cdot r^{n_{1}}\cdot(1\!-\!r)^{k_{1}-n_{1}}\cdot\ldots\cdot\binom{k_{\ell}}{n_{\ell}}\cdot r^{n_{\ell}}\cdot(1\!-\!r)^{k_{\ell}-n_{\ell}}

\displaystyle=\displaystyle\sum_{n_{i}\leq k_{i},\,{\scriptscriptstyle\sum_{i}}n_{i}\,=\,K}\,\binom{\sum_{i}k_{i}|{\kern 1.00006pt}i{\kern 1.00006pt}\rangle}{\sum_{i}n_{i}|{\kern 1.00006pt}i{\kern 1.00006pt}\rangle}\cdot r^{K}\cdot(1\!-\!r)^{(\sum_{i}k_{i})-K}

\displaystyle=\displaystyle\binom{\sum_{i}k_{i}}{K}\cdot r^{K}\cdot(1\!-\!r)^{(\sum_{i}k_{i})-K}\qquad\mbox{by Proposition\penalty\ \ref{HypgeomCountProp}}

\displaystyle=\mathsl{bn}[{\scriptstyle\sum_{i}}k_{i}](r)(K).

Now we can move on to the conditioning itself. We see that
the probability
r\in[0,1]
drops out of the calculation.

\begin{array}[]{rcl}\lx@intercol\lx@add@cssclass{ltx_eqn_lefteqn}$\displaystyle\big(\mathsl{bn}[k_{1}](r)\otimes\cdots\otimes\mathsl{bn}[k_{\ell}](r)\big)\big|_{\mathsl{sum}_{K}}$\mbox{}\hfil\lx@intercol\\[3.99994pt]
&=&\displaystyle\sum_{n_{i}\leq k_{i}}\,\frac{\big(\mathsl{bn}[k_{1}](r)\otimes\cdots\otimes\mathsl{bn}[k_{\ell}](r)\big)(\vec{n})\cdot\mathsl{sum}_{K}(\vec{n})}{\mathsl{bn}[k_{1}](r)\otimes\cdots\otimes\mathsl{bn}[k_{\ell}](r)\models\mathsl{sum}_{K}}\,\big|{\kern 1.00006pt}n_{1},\ldots,n_{\ell}{\kern 1.00006pt}\big\rangle\\[11.99998pt]
&=&\displaystyle\sum_{n_{i}\leq k_{i},\,{\scriptscriptstyle\sum_{i}}n_{i}\,=\,K}\frac{\binom{k_{1}}{n_{1}}\!\cdot\!r^{n_{1}}\!\cdot\!(1\!-\!r)^{k_{1}-n_{1}}\!\cdot\ldots\cdot\!\binom{k_{\ell}}{n_{\ell}}\!\cdot\!r^{n_{\ell}}\!\cdot\!(1\!-\!r)^{k_{\ell}-n_{\ell}}}{\binom{\sum_{i}k_{i}}{K}\cdot r^{K}\cdot(1\!-\!r)^{(\sum_{i}k_{i})-K}}\big|{\kern 1.00006pt}n_{1},\ldots,n_{\ell}{\kern 1.00006pt}\big\rangle\\[11.99998pt]
&=&\displaystyle\sum_{n_{i}\leq k_{i},\,{\scriptscriptstyle\sum_{i}}n_{i}\,=\,K}\frac{\prod_{i}\binom{k_{i}}{n_{i}}\cdot r^{K}\cdot(1\!-\!r)^{(\sum_{i}k_{i})-K}}{\binom{\sum_{i}k_{i}}{K}\cdot r^{K}\cdot(1\!-\!r)^{(\sum_{i}k_{i})-K}}\big|{\kern 1.00006pt}n_{1},\ldots,n_{\ell}{\kern 1.00006pt}\big\rangle\\[11.99998pt]
&=&\displaystyle\sum_{n_{i}\leq k_{i},\,{\scriptscriptstyle\sum_{i}}n_{i}\,=\,K}\frac{\prod_{i}\binom{k_{i}}{n_{i}}}{\binom{\sum_{i}k_{i}}{K}}\big|{\kern 1.00006pt}n_{1},\ldots,n_{\ell}{\kern 1.00006pt}\big\rangle\\[18.00005pt]
&\smash{\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\eqref{HypgeomEqn}}}{{=}}}&\mathsl{hg}[K]\Big(\sum_{i}k_{i}|{\kern 1.00006pt}i{\kern 1.00006pt}\rangle\Big).\end{array}

In the last line we implicitly identify the sequence
n_{1},\ldots,n_{\ell}
with the multiset
n_{1}|{\kern 1.00006pt}1{\kern 1.00006pt}\rangle+\cdots+n_{\ell}|{\kern 1.00006pt}\ell{\kern 1.00006pt}\rangle
.
\square

There is a similar result for Pólya distributions, using negative
binomials. It requires some care since it involves a shift of
arguments, since negative distributions (on
{\mathbb{N}}
) start only after a
certain number of steps.

Proposition 14

The multivariate Pólya distribution can be described as conditioning
of negative binomials: for
K\geq\sum_{i}k_{i}
,

\begin{array}[]{rcl}\lx@intercol\lx@add@cssclass{ltx_eqn_lefteqn}$\displaystyle\big(\mathsl{nbn}[k_{1}](r)\otimes\cdots\otimes\mathsl{nbn}[k_{\ell}](r)\big)\big|_{\mathsl{sum}_{K}}\big(k_{1}\!+\!n_{1},\ldots,k_{\ell}\!+\!n_{\ell}\big)$\mbox{}\hfil\lx@intercol\\[3.99994pt]
&=&\begin{cases}\mathsl{pl}[K\!-\!\sum_{i}k_{i}]\big(\sum_{i}k_{i}|{\kern 1.00006pt}i{\kern 1.00006pt}\rangle\big)\big(\sum_{i}n_{i}|{\kern 1.00006pt}i{\kern 1.00006pt}\rangle\big)&\mbox{if }\sum_{i}k_{i}\!+\!n_{i}=K\\
0&\mbox{otherwise.}\end{cases}\end{array}

The number
r\in[0,1]
is arbitrary, and the predicate

\mathsl{sum}_{K}
is from (
20
).

We start with the validity:

\displaystyle\big(\mathsl{nbn}[k_{1}](r)\otimes\cdots\otimes\mathsl{nbn}[k_{\ell}](r)\big)\models\mathsl{sum}_{K}

(22)

\displaystyle=\displaystyle\sum_{n_{1},\ldots,n_{\ell}}\,\big(\mathsl{nbn}[k_{1}](r)\otimes\cdots\otimes\mathsl{nbn}[k_{\ell}](r)\big)(n_{1},\ldots,n_{\ell})\cdot\mathsl{sum}_{K}(n_{1},\ldots,n_{\ell})

\displaystyle=\displaystyle\sum_{n_{i},\,{\scriptscriptstyle\sum_{i}}k_{i}+n_{i}\,=\,K}\,\mathsl{bn}[k_{1}](r)(k_{1}\!+\!n_{1})\cdot\ldots\cdot\mathsl{bn}[k_{\ell}](r)(k_{\ell}\!+\!n_{\ell})

\displaystyle\smash{\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\eqref{NegBinomEqn}}}{{=}}}\displaystyle\sum_{n_{i},\,{\scriptscriptstyle\sum_{i}}k_{i}+n_{i}\,=\,K}\,\textstyle{\displaystyle\prod}_{i}\,\displaystyle\left({\kern-3.0pt}\binom{k_{i}}{n_{i}}{\kern-3.0pt}\right)\cdot r^{k_{i}}\cdot(1\!-\!r)^{n_{i}}

\displaystyle=\displaystyle\sum_{n_{i},\,{\scriptscriptstyle\sum_{i}}n_{i}\,=\,K-{\scriptscriptstyle\sum_{i}}k_{i}}\,\textstyle{\displaystyle\prod}_{i}\,\displaystyle\left({\kern-3.0pt}\binom{\sum_{i}k_{i}|{\kern 1.00006pt}i{\kern 1.00006pt}\rangle}{\sum_{i}n_{i}|{\kern 1.00006pt}i{\kern 1.00006pt}\rangle}{\kern-3.0pt}\right)\cdot r^{\sum_{i}k_{i}}\cdot(1\!-\!r)^{\sum_{i}n_{i}}

\displaystyle=\displaystyle\left({\kern-3.0pt}\binom{\sum_{i}k_{i}}{K-\sum_{i}k_{i}}{\kern-3.0pt}\right)\cdot r^{\sum_{i}k_{i}}\cdot(1\!-\!r)^{K-\sum_{i}k_{i}}\qquad\mbox{by Proposition\penalty\ \ref{PolyaCountProp}}

\displaystyle=\mathsl{nbn}[{\scriptstyle\sum_{i}}k_{i}](r)(K).

Conditioning yields, when
\sum_{i}k_{i}\!+\!n_{i}=K
,

\hskip-30.00005pt\begin{array}[b]{rcl}\lx@intercol\lx@add@cssclass{ltx_eqn_lefteqn}$\displaystyle\big(\mathsl{nbn}[k_{1}](r)\otimes\cdots\otimes\mathsl{nbn}[k_{\ell}](r)\big)\big|_{\mathsl{sum}_{K}}\big(k_{1}\!+\!n_{1},\ldots,k_{\ell}\!+\!n_{\ell}\big)$\mbox{}\hfil\lx@intercol\\[3.99994pt]
&=&\displaystyle\frac{\big(\mathsl{bn}[k_{1}](r)\otimes\cdots\otimes\mathsl{bn}[k_{\ell}](r)\big)((k_{1}\!+\!n_{1},\ldots,k_{\ell}\!+\!n_{\ell})}{\mathsl{nbn}[k_{1}](r)\otimes\cdots\otimes\mathsl{nbn}[k_{\ell}](r)\models\mathsl{sum}_{K}}\\[10.00002pt]
&=&\displaystyle\frac{\prod_{i}\,\big(\!\binom{k_{i}}{n_{i}}\!\big)\cdot r^{k_{i}}\cdot(1\!-\!r)^{n_{i}}}{\big(\!\binom{\sum_{i}k_{i}}{K-\sum_{i}k_{i}}\!\big)\cdot r^{\sum_{i}k_{i}}\cdot(1\!-\!r)^{K-\sum_{i}k_{i}}}\\[22.0pt]

&\smash{\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\eqref{PolyaEqn}}}{{=}}}&\mathsl{pl}[K\!-\!\sum_{i}k_{i}]\big(\sum_{i}k_{i}|{\kern 1.00006pt}i{\kern 1.00006pt}\rangle\big)\big(\sum_{i}n_{i}|{\kern 1.00006pt}i{\kern 1.00006pt}\rangle\big).\end{array}

10
Conclusions

This paper extends the familiar urn model to an urn & tubes model. It
raises several research questions, with possible applications in risk
modeling. The extension is first used to introduce first-full
distributions, which have a historical basis in the ‘problem of
points’ of Pascal and Fermat. Next, the urn & tubes models is used
for negative distributions. The contribution of this paper lies in
systematisation, via a clear model, formalised via multisets (for
urns, draws, tubes), covering the three main drawing modes
(multinomial, hypergeometric, Pólya).

This paper concentrates on the conceptual basis, formalisation, and
illustration of first-full and negative distributions. There is more
to say, for instance about associated statistical properties like mean
and (co)variance. They exist in the literature for the single tube
case. Extension to general tubes is a challenge that is left open
here.

The urn & tubes model may be generalised, for instance to multiple
urns, where there is a choice from which urn one wishes to draw a
ball. When the contents of the urns are known, one can consider
different strategies for such choices, in different drawing modes, via
Markov decision processes (see

e.g.

BaierK08
;
Puterman94
). When the contents are
unknown, the setting may be used for reinforcement
learning
KaelblingLM96
;
SuttonB18
: jointly learning these
contents and developing a strategy.
