The Banach algebras A ​ C ​ ( σ ) AC(\sigma) and B ​ V ​ ( σ ) BV(\sigma)
Ian Doust, Michael Leinert, Alan Stoneham

Abstract
The spaces B ​ V ​ ( σ ) BV(\sigma) and A ​ C ​ ( σ ) AC(\sigma) were introduced as part of a program to find a general theory which covers both well-bounded operators and trigonometrically well-bounded operators acting on a Banach space. Since their initial appearance it has become clear that the definitions could be simplified somewhat. In this paper we give a self-contained exposition of the main properties of these spaces using this simplified approach.

Introduction

The classical definition of the variation of a function of one real variable depends strongly on the order of the real line. Different applications have required extensions of this definition to functions of two or more variables, yielding the challenge of how to replace the order property in a suitable way. Already by the 1930s, Clarkson and Adams
CA
had found quite a collection of mutually inequivalent concepts.

In the 1960s, Smart and Ringrose
Sm
;
Rin1
;
Rin2
introduced the theory of well-bounded Banach space operators as an analogue of the theory of self-adjoint operators on a Hilbert space, but now allowing operators whose spectral expansions might only converge conditionally. A Banach space operator
T
is said to be well-bounded if it admits an
AC[a,b]
functional calculus for some compact interval
[a,b]\subseteq\mathbb{R}
. It was shown by Cheng and Doust
CD
, for example, that every compact well-bounded operator
T
has a representation of the form
T=\sum_{j=1}^{\infty}\lambda_{j}P_{j}
as a norm convergent (but possibly conditionally convergent) combination of projections onto the eigenspaces of
T
. At least on reflexive Banach spaces, general well-bounded operators admit an integral representation reminiscent of that given by the spectral theorem for self-adjoint operators.
The spectral theorem for well-bounded operators on reflexive Banach spaces allows one to extend the
AC[a,b]
functional calculus to a
BV[a,b]
functional calculus, in a similar manner to the way in which the
C(\sigma(T))
functional calculus for a self-adjoint operator can be extended to the algebra of bounded Borel measurable functions on
\sigma(T)
.

A natural problem then was to provide analogues of normal and unitary operators in this context. After some initial work by Ringrose
Rin2
, steps in this direction were taken by Berkson and Gillespie
BG1
;
BG2
and Wilson
Wil
. Following Hardy and Krause, Berkson and Gillespie defined a Banach algebra
AC(J\times K)
of ‘absolutely continuous’ functions defined on a rectangle
J\times K\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{2}
and used this to define the class of
AC
operators. A little later, they defined a corresponding analogue of unitary operators, the class of trigonometrically well-bounded operators based on an algebra
AC(\mathbb{T})
of functions on the unit circle, and used this in work on operator-valued harmonic analysis (see, for example,
BG3
;
BGM
). It became apparent however that the more general theory of
AC
operators had some undesirable properties in terms of spectral theory. For example there exist
AC
operators
T
such that
(1+i)T
is not an
AC
operator. It is also more natural (even in the case where
\sigma(T)\subseteq\mathbb{R}
) to look for a functional calculus model based on functions whose domain is
\sigma(T)
rather than some potentially much larger set.

To address some of these shortcomings, a new measure of variation of a function defined on a compact subset
\sigma
of the plane was developed by Ashton
Ash
in 2000. This was used to define the Banach algebras
BV(\sigma)
and
AC(\sigma)
, the spaces of functions of bounded variation and of absolutely continuous functions respectively, and these algebras were used to define the class of
AC(\sigma)
operators
AD3
. Importantly, this was consistent with the successful theories for well-bounded and trigonometrically well-bounded operators in the cases that
\sigma\subseteq\mathbb{R}
and
\sigma\subseteq\mathbb{T}
.

Since the original development of the theory in
Ash
;
AD1
, a number of simplifications of the definitions have been found, and several important new observations concerning these definitions have been made. A number of gaps in some of the earlier arguments were also identified. There is now much more known about the structure of these spaces (see
AS2
;
AS3
;
DA
;
DL
) and new applications have arisen (see, for example,
NM
).
Unfortunately this provides a challenge for readers, as foundational results are somewhat scattered through the literature and were often shown using different (but equivalent) definitions of variation. The aim of this paper is to give a self-contained development of the spaces
BV(\sigma)
and
AC(\sigma)
, indicating where there are still some gaps in our knowledge about these spaces.

In Section
2
we gather together the basic definitions and properties of the Ashton definition of variation in the plane, and the Banach algebra
BV(\sigma)
. This includes some new results on estimating the variation of a function.

Section
3
examines some of the important subalgebras of
BV(\sigma)
, including the polynomials in two (real) variables, the Lipschitz functions and the functions which admit a
C^{2}
extension to a neighbourhood of
\sigma
. The absolutely continuous functions are defined as the closure of the set of polynomials in two variables. The final section includes some important properties of absolute continuity in this context. In particular it is shown that absolute continuity can be considered to be a ‘local property’ in the sense that a function is absolutely continuous over the whole set
\sigma
if and only if it is absolutely continuous on a compact neighbourhood of each point of
\sigma
. (This work is continued in
DLS2
where it is shown, for example, that all functions with a
C^{1}
extension to a neighbourhood of
\sigma
lie in
AC(\sigma)
.)

2
Basic definitions and properties

For a complex-valued function
f
defined on a compact interval
[a,b]
, the concept of its variation is well understood. Given a partition
\mathcal{P}=\{a=x_{0}<x_{1}<...<x_{n}=b\}
of
[a,b]
, the variation of
f
over
\mathcal{P}
is defined to be

\myvar(f,\mathcal{P})=\sum_{j=1}^{n}\left\lvert f(x_{j})-f(x_{j-1})\right\rvert.

The variation of
f
is then defined as
\myvar(f)=\displaystyle\sup_{\mathcal{P}}\myvar(f,\mathcal{P})
. One proceeds to define the vector space
BV[a,b]
of functions of bounded variation over
[a,b]
as

BV[a,b]=\{f:[a,b]\to\mathbb{C}\,:\,\myvar(f)<\infty\}.

The space
BV[0,1]
is a large and rich Banach algebra when endowed with the norm
\|f\|_{BV}=\|f\|_{\infty}+\myvar(f)
. (Note that throughout,
\left\lVert f\right\rVert_{\infty}
denotes the supremum rather than the essential supremum of
|f|
.)

Suppose now that
\sigma
is a nonempty compact subset of the plane and that
f:\sigma\to\mathbb{C}
. The original definition of the variation of
f
on
\sigma
in
Ash
and
AD1
involved calculating the variation of
f
along suitable curves in the plane. Simpler, but equivalent, definitions were subsequently developed which are in many ways more reminiscent of the one-dimensional definition, and we follow that simplified development here. The equivalence of the different definitions is shown in the appendix to
AS3
. The central concept here is that of the variation factor of a list of points.

For the motivating applications in spectral theory, the set
\sigma
is the spectrum of a bounded operator, and hence is a subset of
\mathbb{C}
. The definitions however apply equally well to subsets of
\mathbb{R}^{2}
and so we shall freely swap between the two models of the plane.

The
curve variation of
f
on a list
S=\bigl[{\boldsymbol{x}}_{0},{\boldsymbol{x}}_{1},\dots,{\boldsymbol{x}}_{n}\bigr]
of elements of
\sigma
will be defined to be

\cvar(f,S)=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\left\lvert f({\boldsymbol{x}}_{i})-f({\boldsymbol{x}}_{i-1})\right\rvert.

Unless
f
is constant,
\cvar(f,S)
can be made arbitrarily large, so to arrive at a useful measure of variation, one needs to
introduce a quantity called the variation factor of the list
S
. The properties of this quantity are central to the later theory, so the next subsection is devoted to developing these.

Proposition 2.4

Let

It is worth recording a few simple properties of the variation factor which will be needed later. The first of which shows that if we reverse the order of the points in

Lemma 2.5

Let
S=[{\boldsymbol{x}}_{0},{\boldsymbol{x}}_{1},\dots,{\boldsymbol{x}}_{n}]

(S,\ell)=\textup{vf}(S^{\prime},\ell)

\vf(S)=\vf(S^{\prime})

The next result states that if one starts with a list of points
S
and then removes a point from
S

Proposition 2.6
.

Let
\sigma\subseteq\mathbb{C}
be nonempty and compact, let
S
be an ordered list of elements of
\sigma
, and let
S^{+}
be a list formed by adding an additional
element into the list at some point. Then for any line in the plane
\vf(S,\ell)\leq\vf(S^{+},\ell)

\vf(S)\leq\vf(S^{+})

Proof:
Let
S=\bigl[{\boldsymbol{x}}_{0},\dots,{\boldsymbol{x}}_{n}\bigr]
and let
\ell
be any line in the plane. Suppose that
{\boldsymbol{w}}\in\sigma

S
to form
S^{+}
.
Suppose first that
S^{+}=\bigl[{\boldsymbol{w}},{\boldsymbol{x}}_{0},\dots,{\boldsymbol{x}}_{n}\bigr]
. If
{\boldsymbol{x}}_{0}\not\in\ell
, or if
{\boldsymbol{x}}_{0}\in\ell
and
{\boldsymbol{w}}\not\in\ell
, then each of the original line segments in
S
retains its original status as either a crossing or non-crossing segment of
S^{+}
on
\ell
. If
{\boldsymbol{x}}_{0},{\boldsymbol{w}}\in\ell
, then
\overline{\vphantom{\vbox to5.16663pt{}}{\boldsymbol{w}}\,{\boldsymbol{x}}_{0}}
replaces
\overline{\vphantom{\vbox to5.16663pt{}}{\boldsymbol{x}}_{0}\,{\boldsymbol{x}}_{1}}
as a crossing segment. In either case, the number of crossing segments is not decreased.

The case that
S^{+}=\bigl[{\boldsymbol{x}}_{0},\dots,{\boldsymbol{x}}_{n},{\boldsymbol{w}}\bigr]
follows from Lemma
2.5

{\boldsymbol{w}}
is added between the

and
(j+1)

We consider the four possibilities determined by combinations of
{\boldsymbol{x}}_{j}
and
{\boldsymbol{x}}_{j+1}
lying on
\ell
or not. Suppose that
{\boldsymbol{x}}_{j},{\boldsymbol{x}}_{j+1}\notin\ell
. Then if
\overline{\vphantom{\vbox to5.16663pt{}}{\boldsymbol{x}}_{j}\,{\boldsymbol{x}}_{j+1}}
is a crossing segment (necessarily of type (1)), then either
\overline{\vphantom{\vbox to5.16663pt{}}{\boldsymbol{x}}_{j}\,{\boldsymbol{w}}}
or
\overline{\vphantom{\vbox to5.16663pt{}}{\boldsymbol{w}}\,{\boldsymbol{x}}_{j+1}}
will be a replacement crossing segment. If
\overline{\vphantom{\vbox to5.16663pt{}}{\boldsymbol{x}}_{j}\,{\boldsymbol{x}}_{j+1}}
is not a crossing segment, then the addition of
{\boldsymbol{w}}
will maintain or increase the variation factor.

If
{\boldsymbol{x}}_{j},{\boldsymbol{x}}_{j+1}\in\ell

{\boldsymbol{w}}
will maintain or increase the variation factor when
{\boldsymbol{w}}\in\ell
or
{\boldsymbol{w}}\notin\ell

If
{\boldsymbol{x}}_{j}\notin\ell

{\boldsymbol{x}}_{j+1}\in\ell

\overline{\vphantom{\vbox to5.16663pt{}}{\boldsymbol{x}}_{j}\,{\boldsymbol{x}}_{j+1}}

2.2
Variation in the plane

Suppose that
\sigma
is a nonempty compact subset of the plane, that
f:\sigma\to\mathbb{C}
, and that
S=\bigl[{\boldsymbol{x}}_{0},\dots,{\boldsymbol{x}}_{n}\bigr]
is a list of elements of
\sigma
.
We define the
curve variation of
f
on the list
S
to be

\cvar(f,S)=\begin{cases}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\left\lvert f({\boldsymbol{x}}_{i})-f({\boldsymbol{x}}_{i-1})\right\rvert&\text{if $n\geq 1$,}\\
0&\text{if $n=0$.}\end{cases}

The
two-dimensional variation
of a function
f:\sigma\rightarrow\mathbb{C}
is defined to be

\myvar(f,\sigma)=\sup_{S}\frac{\cvar(f,S)}{\vf(S)},

where the supremum is taken over all finite ordered lists of elements of
\sigma
.
Letting
\|f\|_{\infty}=\sup\{|f(z)|\,:\,z\in\sigma\}
, the
variation norm
is

\left\lVert f\right\rVert_{{BV}(\sigma)}=\left\lVert f\right\rVert_{\infty}+\myvar(f,\sigma)

and this is used to define the set of functions of bounded variation on
\sigma
,

{BV}(\sigma)=\{f:\sigma\to\mathbb{C}\thinspace:\thinspace\left\lVert f\right\rVert_{{BV}(\sigma)}<\infty\}.

Our first task is to show that
\left\lVert\cdot\right\rVert_{{BV}(\sigma)}
is indeed a norm, and that
{BV}(\sigma)
is always a Banach algebra. Perhaps, just as importantly, we shall shortly show that
{BV}(\sigma)
always contains all sufficiently nice functions, and in the case that
\sigma=[a,b]\subseteq\mathbb{R}
, the above definition just gives the usual space met in real analysis.

We begin with a simple lemma which just requires straightforward uses of the triangle inequality.

Lemma 2.7
.

Let
\sigma\subseteq\mathbb{C}
be a nonempty compact set and suppose that
f,g:\sigma\rightarrow\mathbb{C}
. Then

1.

\myvar(f+g,\sigma)\leq\myvar(f,\sigma)+\myvar(g,\sigma)
,

2.

\myvar(fg,\sigma)\leq\left\lVert f\right\rVert_{\infty}\myvar(g,\sigma)+\left\lVert g\right\rVert_{\infty}\myvar(f,\sigma)
,

3.

\myvar(\alpha f,\sigma)=\left\lvert\alpha\right\rvert\myvar(f,\sigma)
,

4.

|\myvar(f,\sigma)-\myvar(g,\sigma)|\leq\myvar(f-g,\sigma)
,

5.

\myvar(|f|,\sigma)\leq\myvar(f,\sigma)
.

Proof:
The first, third and fifth statements are straightforward.
For the second statement, we have

\displaystyle\myvar(fg,\sigma)

\displaystyle=\sup_{S}\frac{\cvar(fg,S)}{\vf(S)}

\displaystyle=\sup_{S}\frac{1}{\vf(S)}\sum_{i=1}^{n}|f({\boldsymbol{x}}_{i})g({\boldsymbol{x}}_{i})-f({\boldsymbol{x}}_{i-1})g({\boldsymbol{x}}_{i-1})|

\displaystyle\leq\sup_{S}\frac{1}{\vf(S)}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\big(|f({\boldsymbol{x}}_{i})||g({\boldsymbol{x}}_{i})-g({\boldsymbol{x}}_{i-1})|+|f({\boldsymbol{x}}_{i})-f({\boldsymbol{x}}_{i-1})||g({\boldsymbol{x}}_{i-1})|\big)

\displaystyle\leq\sup_{S}\frac{\|f\|_{\infty}\cvar(g,S)}{\vf(S)}+\sup_{S}\frac{\|g\|_{\infty}\cvar(f,S)}{\vf(S)}

\displaystyle=\|f\|_{\infty}\myvar(g,\sigma)+\|g\|_{\infty}\myvar(f,\sigma).

The fourth statement follows by writing
f=(f-g)+g
and using (1) and (3).

A simple consequence of the above lemma is that

BV(\sigma)
is indeed a normed algebra.

Lemma 2.8
.

Let
\sigma\subseteq\mathbb{C}
be nonempty compact. Let
f,g:\sigma\rightarrow\mathbb{C}
. Then

1.

\left\lVert f+g\right\rVert_{{BV}(\sigma)}\leq\left\lVert f\right\rVert_{{BV}(\sigma)}+\left\lVert g\right\rVert_{{BV}(\sigma)}
,

2.

\left\lVert fg\right\rVert_{{BV}(\sigma)}\leq\left\lVert f\right\rVert_{{BV}(\sigma)}\left\lVert g\right\rVert_{{BV}(\sigma)}
,

3.

\left\lVert\alpha f\right\rVert_{{BV}(\sigma)}=\left\lvert\alpha\right\rvert\left\lVert f\right\rVert_{{BV}(\sigma)}
.

It is easy to check that
f\in BV(\sigma)
if and only if both
\mathop{\mathrm{Re}}f
and
\mathop{\mathrm{Im}}f
lie in
BV(\sigma)
. We shall denote the real normed algebra of real-valued functions of bounded variation by
BV_{\mathbb{R}}(\sigma)
.

Before continuing it is worth confirming that unless
\sigma
is a singleton set,
BV(\sigma)
always contains more than just the constant functions. Given
U\subseteq\sigma
, let
\chi_{U}
denote the characteristic function of

\sigma

\mathbb{C}

Lemma 2.9
.

Suppose that
\sigma\subseteq\mathbb{C}

\chi_{\{{\boldsymbol{z}}\}}\in BV(\sigma)

{\boldsymbol{z}}\in\sigma

Proof:
The result is clear if
\sigma

\sigma

Fix
{\boldsymbol{z}}\in\sigma

f=\chi_{\{{\boldsymbol{z}}\}}

S=[{\boldsymbol{x}}_{0},\dots,{\boldsymbol{x}}_{n}]
be a list of points in
\sigma

\cvar(f,S)\neq 0
. Since, by Proposition
2.6

\sigma
cannot increase the variation factor, we may assume that
|f({\boldsymbol{x}}_{j})-f({\boldsymbol{x}}_{j+1})|=1
for all
j
, and so
\cvar(f,S)=n
. Now choose a line
\ell
which passes through
{\boldsymbol{z}}
but none of the other elements of
S
. Note that if a segment
\overline{\vphantom{\vbox to5.16663pt{}}{\boldsymbol{x}}_{j-1}\,{\boldsymbol{x}}_{j}}
is not a crossing segment of
S
on
\ell
, then
\overline{\vphantom{\vbox to5.16663pt{}}{\boldsymbol{x}}_{j}\,{\boldsymbol{x}}_{j+1}}
must be and hence
\vf(S)\geq\vf(S,\ell)\geq n/2
. It follows that
\cvar(f,S)/\vf(S)\leq 2
and hence
\myvar(f,\sigma)\leq 2
.

Easy consequences of this lemma are recorded in the following proposition.

Proposition 2.10
.

Suppose that
\sigma
is an infinite compact subset of
\mathbb{C}
. Then

1.

BV(\sigma)
is infinite dimensional,

2.

there exists a discontinuous function in
BV(\sigma)
.

Of course, if
\sigma
is a finite set, then every function
f:\sigma\to\mathbb{C}
is of bounded variation.

2.3
BV(\sigma)
is a Banach algebra

Theorem 2.11
.

Let
\sigma\subseteq\mathbb{C}
be nonempty and compact. Then
BV(\sigma)
is a Banach algebra.

Proof:
Lemma 2.7 shows that
BV(\sigma)
is a normed algebra, so it remains to show that
BV(\sigma)
is complete.

Suppose then that
(f_{k})_{k=1}^{\infty}
is a Cauchy sequence in
BV(\sigma)
. By the definition of the variation norm, this sequence certainly converges uniformly to a function
f
.

Fix
\varepsilon>0
. Choose
N
such that for all
j,k\geq N
,
\left\lVert f_{j}-f_{k}\right\rVert_{{BV}(\sigma)}<\frac{\varepsilon}{2}
. Suppose now that
S=[{\boldsymbol{x}}_{0},\dots,{\boldsymbol{x}}_{n}]
is a list of points in
\sigma
. As
f_{k}\to f
uniformly we can choose
N_{S}\geq N
such that
\max\limits_{0\leq i\leq n}|f({\boldsymbol{x}}_{i})-f_{N_{S}}({\boldsymbol{x}}_{i})|<\frac{\varepsilon}{4n}
. Then whenever
k\geq N

\displaystyle\cvar(f-f_{k},S)

\displaystyle=\cvar(f-f_{N_{S}}+f_{N_{S}}-f_{k},S)

\displaystyle\leq\cvar(f-f_{N_{S}},S)+\cvar(f_{N_{S}}-f_{k},S)

\displaystyle=\sum_{i=1}^{n}|(f({\boldsymbol{x}}_{i})-f_{N_{S}}({\boldsymbol{x}}_{i}))-(f({\boldsymbol{x}}_{i-1})-f_{N_{S}}({\boldsymbol{x}}_{i-1}))|

\displaystyle\hskip 28.45274pt+\cvar(f_{N_{S}}-f_{k},S)

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{i=1}^{n}|f({\boldsymbol{x}}_{i})-f_{N_{S}}({\boldsymbol{x}}_{i})|+\sum_{i=1}^{n}|f({\boldsymbol{x}}_{i-1})-f_{N_{S}}({\boldsymbol{x}}_{i-1})|

\displaystyle\hskip 28.45274pt+\cvar(f_{N_{S}}-f_{k},S)

\displaystyle<\frac{\varepsilon}{2}+\cvar(f_{N_{S}}-f_{k},S).

Recalling that
\vf(S)\geq 1
,

\frac{\cvar(f-f_{k},S)}{\vf(S)}<\frac{\varepsilon}{2\vf(S)}+\frac{\cvar(f_{N_{S}}-f_{k},S)}{\vf(S)}<\frac{\varepsilon}{2}+\left\lVert f_{N_{S}}-f_{k}\right\rVert_{BV(\sigma)}<\varepsilon

and so
\myvar(f-f_{k},\sigma)\leq\varepsilon
.
Hence
f\in BV(\sigma)
and
f_{k}\to f
in
BV(\sigma)
.

One of the ‘design criteria’ for the definition of variation in the plane is that it should be invariant under translations and rotations. Since all of the quantities involved in the definition of variation are invariant under affine transformations of
\sigma
it is easy to see that this is the case.

Theorem 2.12
.

Suppose that
\alpha,\beta\in\mathbb{C}
, where
\alpha\neq 0
, and let
\phi(z)=\alpha z+\beta
.
Suppose that
\sigma
is a nonempty compact subset of
\mathbb{C}
, and let
{\hat{\sigma}}=\phi(\sigma)
.
For
f:\sigma\to\mathbb{C}
let
{\hat{f}}:{\hat{\sigma}}\to\mathbb{C}
be defined by
{\hat{f}}=f\circ\phi^{-1}
.
Then the map
f\to{\hat{f}}
is an isometric Banach algebra isomorphism from
BV(\sigma)
to
BV({\hat{\sigma}})
.

Affine transformations of
\mathbb{R}^{2}
are of course slightly more general, and one could just as easily rewrite the theorem in that setting.

Another more or less immediate consequence of the definition is that restrictions of functions of bounded variation are also of bounded variation.

Theorem 2.13
.

Suppose that
\sigma_{1},\sigma
are nonempty compact subsets of the plane with
\sigma_{1}\subseteq\sigma
. If
f\in BV(\sigma)
then
f|\sigma_{1}\in BV(\sigma_{1})
, with
\left\lVert f|\sigma_{1}\right\rVert_{BV(\sigma_{1})}\leq\left\lVert f\right\rVert_{BV(\sigma)}
.

Proof:
Suppose that
\sigma,\sigma_{1}
are nonempty compact subsets of
\mathbb{C}
,
\sigma_{1}\subseteq\sigma
and that
f\in BV(\sigma)
. Since the set of finite lists of points in
\sigma_{1}
is contained in the set of finite lists of points in
\sigma
, we obtain that
\myvar(f|\sigma_{1},\sigma_{1})\leq\myvar(f,\sigma)
, and the result follows since
\|f|\sigma_{1}\|_{\infty}\leq\|f\|_{\infty}
.

2.4
The case
\sigma\subseteq\mathbb{R}

The space of functions of bounded variation on an interval
[a,b]
is one of the classical spaces of real analysis. Indeed there is no great complication in considering more general subsets of the real line and this was already considered in Saks’ classic 1930’s text
S
. In order to justify our use of the term ‘bounded variation’ for functions defined on more general subsets of the plane, we should at this point check that in the case that
\sigma\subseteq\mathbb{R}
, the definition above does just reduce to the classical one.

Suppose then that
\sigma
is a nonempty compact subset of
\mathbb{R}
and that
f:\sigma\to\mathbb{C}
. Suppose that
\mathcal{P}=\{{\boldsymbol{x}}_{0}<{\boldsymbol{x}}_{1}<\dots<{\boldsymbol{x}}_{n}\}\subseteq\sigma
is a partition. Any such partition has a corresponding list
S=[{\boldsymbol{x}}_{0},{\boldsymbol{x}}_{1},\dots,{\boldsymbol{x}}_{n}]
which has variation factor
1
, so it is immediate from the classical definition of variation that
\myvar(f)\leq\myvar(f,\sigma)
.

Proposition 2.14
.

Suppose that
\sigma
and
f
are as above. Then
\myvar(f)=\myvar(f,\sigma)
.

Proof:
From the remark above, we only need to show that

\myvar(f)\geq\myvar(f,\sigma)
.
Suppose that
S=\bigl[{\boldsymbol{x}}_{0},\dots,{\boldsymbol{x}}_{n}\bigr]
is a list of points in
\sigma
. The list
S
generates a partition
\mathcal{P}=\{t_{0},\dots,t_{m}\}
by ordering the distinct elements of
S
. Now form a list
{\hat{S}}
by starting with the list
S
and, for each
j
, inserting between
{\boldsymbol{x}}_{j}
and
{\boldsymbol{x}}_{j+1}
all of the points of
\mathcal{P}
that are passed on the line segment
s_{j}=\overline{\vphantom{\vbox to5.16663pt{}}{\boldsymbol{x}}_{j}\,{\boldsymbol{x}}_{j+1}}
. (By way of an example, if
S=[2,4,1,1,3,2]
then
\mathcal{P}=\{1,2,3,4\}
and
{\hat{S}}=[2,3,4,3,2,1,1,2,3,2]
.)

By the triangle inequality,

\cvar(f,S)=\sum_{j=1}^{n}|f({\boldsymbol{x}}_{j})-f({\boldsymbol{x}}_{j-1})|\leq\cvar(f,{\hat{S}})=\sum_{k=1}^{m}c_{k}|f(t_{k})-f(t_{k-1})|

where
c_{k}
counts the number of times the term
|f(t_{k})-f(t_{k-1})|
appears in the sum for
\cvar(f,{\hat{S}})
. (In the example
(c_{1},c_{2},c_{3})=(2,4,2)
.) Recall that
\gamma_{S}
denotes the piecewise linear curve joining the points of
S
in order. Note that the number of times that
\gamma_{S}
crosses any point in the interior of the line segment
\overline{\vphantom{\vbox to5.16663pt{}}t_{k-1}\,t_{k}}
is
c_{k}
, and so
\vf(S)\geq\max\limits_{k}c_{k}
. It follows that

\cvar(f,S)\leq\vf(S)\sum_{k=1}^{m}|f(t_{k})-f(t_{k-1})|\leq\vf(S)\cdot\myvar f

and hence that

\myvar(f,\sigma)\leq\myvar(f)

which completes the proof.

Corollary 2.15
.

If
\sigma\subseteq\mathbb{R}
is nonempty and compact, then
BV(\sigma)
is the classical space of functions of bounded variation on
\sigma
.

By the affine invariance of these spaces (Theorem
2.12
) essentially the same is true whenever
\sigma
is contained in any line in the plane.

2.5
Functions constant on lines

Calculating
\myvar(f,\sigma)
for a function
f
is generally quite challenging. One class of functions which is easier to deal with are the functions which only vary in one direction. In this section we shall make that idea precise and then use this fact to show that all polynomials in two real variables are of bounded variation.

Suppose that
\sigma
is a nonempty compact subset of
\mathbb{C}
. Let
\sigma_{\mathbb{R}}
denote the projection of
\sigma
on to the real axis and suppose that
u:\sigma_{\mathbb{R}}\to\mathbb{C}
. The function
u
can be extended to all of
\sigma
by setting

{\hat{u}}(x+iy)=u(x),\qquad x+iy\in\sigma,

so that
\hat{u}
is constant on all vertical lines.

Theorem 2.16
.

Suppose that
\sigma
and
u
are as above. If
u\in BV(\sigma_{\mathbb{R}})
then
{\hat{u}}\in BV(\sigma)
and
\left\lVert{\hat{u}}\right\rVert_{{BV}(\sigma)}\leq\left\lVert u\right\rVert_{BV(\sigma_{\mathbb{R}})}
.

Proof:
Suppose that
S=\bigl[{\boldsymbol{x}}_{0},\dots,{\boldsymbol{x}}_{n}\bigr]
is a list of points in
\sigma
and let
S_{\mathbb{R}}=\bigl[x_{0},\dots,x_{n}\bigr]
be the list of the real parts of these points (in the same order). By Proposition
2.4
,
\vf(S_{\mathbb{R}})\leq\vf(S)
and so

\frac{\cvar({\hat{u}},S)}{\vf(S)}=\frac{\cvar(u,S_{\mathbb{R}})}{\vf(S)}\leq\frac{\cvar(u,S_{\mathbb{R}})}{\vf(S_{\mathbb{R}})}\leq\myvar(u,\sigma_{\mathbb{R}}).

Taking the supremum over all lists
S
and adding
\left\lVert\hat{u}\right\rVert_{\infty}=\left\lVert u\right\rVert_{\infty}
completes the proof.

Again, by the affine invariance of the variation, one can easily extend this result by replacing the real line with any other line
L
in the plane.

It is worth noting that in the above theorem, one does not get equality in general.

Example 2.17
.

Let
\sigma=\{(-1,1),(0,0),(1,1)\}
, so
\sigma_{\mathbb{R}}=\{-1,0,1\}
, and
u(x)=|x|
. Then we have that
\myvar(u,\sigma_{\mathbb{R}})=2
but
\myvar({\hat{u}},\sigma)=1
.

To see this, note that taking
S=[(-1,1),(0,0)]
shows that
\myvar({\hat{u}},\sigma)\geq 1
. Also, we could consider
{\hat{u}}
to be the extension function
{\hat{u}}(x+iy)=v(y)
where
v\in BV(\{0,1\})
,
v(y)=y
, and so by the theorem,
\myvar({\hat{u}},\sigma)\leq\myvar(v,\{0,1\})=1
.

2.6
The case
\sigma=\mathbb{T}

In
BG2
, Berkson and Gillespie defined a notion of variation for functions defined on the complex unit circle
\mathbb{T}
. It was a rather natural definition in the sense that it used the standard parametrisation of the circle to represent a function defined on
\mathbb{T}
as a function defined on
[0,2\pi]
. To be explicit, for a function
f:\mathbb{T}\to\mathbb{C}
, its variation in the sense of Berkson and Gillespie is defined to be
\myvar_{\mathbb{T}}(f)=~\myvar_{[0,2\pi]}f\big(e^{i(\cdot)}\big)
. The set of all functions on the circle satisfying
\myvar_{\mathbb{T}}(f)<\infty
forms a Banach algebra when endowed with the norm
\|f\|=\|f\|_{\infty}+\myvar_{\mathbb{T}}(f)
. We shall denote this algebra by
BV_{BG}(\mathbb{T})
. This definition was used then to define the class of trigonometrically well-bounded operators. As part of the aim of introducing our current definition was to generalize the theories of well-bounded and trigonometrically well-bounded operators, it was important that in fact
BV(\mathbb{T})=BV_{BG}(\mathbb{T})
. As was shown in
AD2
, the two algebras are isomorphic, but not isometrically. Instead, we obtain an equivalence of norms. The proof in
AD2
was given using the original definition of variation, so we give here a proof in line with the current development.

Lemma 2.18
.

For all
f\in BV(\mathbb{T})
,
\myvar_{\mathbb{T}}(f)\leq 2\myvar(f,\mathbb{T})
.

Proof:
Suppose that

f\in BV(\mathbb{T})
and
\{0=\theta_{0}<\theta_{1}<\dots<\theta_{n}=2\pi\}

is a partition of
[0,2\pi]
with
n\geq 2
. For
N\in\mathbb{Z}^{+}
, define
S_{N}
to be the list of points obtained by starting at
e^{i\theta_{0}}
and then repeating
N
times the sublist
S=[e^{i\theta_{1}},\dots,e^{i\theta_{n}}]
. Let
\ell
be the line which passes through the circle at
e^{i\theta_{0}}
and the midpoint on the circle between
e^{i\theta_{1}}
and
e^{i\theta_{2}}
. Then
\vf(S_{N})\geq\vf(S_{N},\ell)=2N+1
.
Hence

\displaystyle\myvar(f,\mathbb{T})

\displaystyle=\sup_{S}\frac{\cvar(f,S)}{\vf(S)}

\displaystyle\geq\sup_{N}\frac{\cvar(f,S_{N})}{\vf(S_{N})}

\displaystyle=\sup_{N}\frac{N}{2N+1}\sum_{j=1}^{n}|f(e^{i\theta_{j}})-f(e^{i\theta_{j-1}})|,

from which it follows that
\myvar_{\mathbb{T}}(f)\leq 2\myvar(f,\mathbb{T})
.

It follows that
BV(\mathbb{T})\subseteq BV_{BG}(\mathbb{T})
. Establishing that the reverse inclusion holds requires some work. Before that, it is worth noting that the bound given in Lemma
2.18
is sharp, since if
f
is taken to be the characteristic function of
\{1\}
, then
\myvar_{\mathbb{T}}(f)=2
, and
\myvar(f,\mathbb{T})
can be calculated to be 1 as follows. By taking a simple list, such as
S=[1,i]
, one sees that
\myvar(f,\mathbb{T})\geq 1
. For the reverse inequality, take an arbitrary finite list
S=[e^{i\theta_{0}},\dots,e^{i\theta_{n}}]
of points in
\mathbb{T}
. Moreover, we can assume that
e^{i\theta_{j}}=1
for some
j
. Then

\displaystyle\cvar(f,S)=\big|\{j\,:\,|f(e^{i\theta_{j}})-f(e^{i\theta_{j-1}})|=1\}\big|,

that is the number of times the piecewise linear curve
\gamma_{S}
leaves or returns to 1. Consider any vertical line
\ell
such that the only point of
S
lying to the right of
\ell
is 1 and all other points of
S
lie to the left of
\ell
. Then one finds that
\cvar(f,S)=\vf(S,\ell)\leq\vf(S)
. This establishes the reverse inequality
\myvar(f,\mathbb{T})\leq 1
, and consequently
\myvar_{\mathbb{T}}(f)=2\myvar(f,\mathbb{T})
.

We now move on to the task of showing
BV_{BG}(\mathbb{T})\subseteq BV(\mathbb{T})
. We shall say that any continuous, orientation preserving bijection
\tau:\mathbb{T}\to\mathbb{T}
is a
reparametrisation
of
\mathbb{T}
. For any
f:\mathbb{T}\to\mathbb{C}
, let
f_{\tau}=f\circ\tau
.

Lemma 2.19
.

Suppose that
f:\mathbb{T}\to\mathbb{C}
and that
\tau
is a reparametrisation of
\mathbb{T}
. For a list of points
S=[{\boldsymbol{z}}_{0},\dots,{\boldsymbol{z}}_{n}]
, let
S_{\tau}=\tau(S)=[\tau({\boldsymbol{z}}_{0}),\dots,\tau({\boldsymbol{z}}_{n})]
. Then

1.

\myvar_{\mathbb{T}}(f)=\myvar_{\mathbb{T}}(f_{\tau})
;

2.

\vf(S)=\vf(S_{\tau})
;

3.

\myvar(f,S)=\myvar(f_{\tau},S_{\tau})
.

Proof:
It should be clear that (1) holds. For (2), suppose that
\ell
is a line in
\mathbb{C}
such that
\vf(S,\ell)=\vf(S)
. The cases where
\ell
is disjoint from
\mathbb{T}
or where
\ell
only touches
\mathbb{T}
at a single point are easily dealt with, so we will assume that
\ell
intersects
\mathbb{T}
at two distinct points, say
{\boldsymbol{x}}
and
{\boldsymbol{y}}
. Define
\ell_{\tau}
to be the line passing through
\tau({\boldsymbol{x}})
and
\tau({\boldsymbol{y}})
. Since
\mathbb{T}
is strictly convex, it is then simple to check that if
\overline{\vphantom{\vbox to5.16663pt{}}{\boldsymbol{z}}_{j}\,{\boldsymbol{z}}_{j+1}}
is a crossing segment of
S
on
\ell
, then
\overline{\vphantom{\vbox to5.16663pt{}}\tau({\boldsymbol{z}}_{j})\,\tau({\boldsymbol{z}}_{j+1})}
is a crossing segment of
S_{\tau}
on
\ell_{\tau}
of the same type. Thus
\vf(S)\leq\vf(S_{\tau})
. A similar argument establishes the reverse inequality, and so
\vf(S_{\tau})=\vf(S)
. Since
\cvar(f,S)=\cvar(f_{\tau},S_{\tau})
, this also proves (3).

Theorem 2.20
.

For all
f\in BV_{BG}(\mathbb{T})
,
\myvar(f,\mathbb{T})\leq\myvar_{\mathbb{T}}(f)
.

Proof:
Fix a list of points
S=[{\boldsymbol{z}}_{0},\dots,{\boldsymbol{z}}_{n}]
in
\mathbb{T}
and choose a paramterisation
\tau
such that
\text{Im}({\boldsymbol{z}}_{j})>0
for each
j
. By Lemma
2.19
, we have that
\myvar(f,S)=\myvar(f_{\tau},S_{\tau})
.
The map
h(x)=x+i\sqrt{1-x^{2}}
,
x\in[-1,1]
has range
\mathbb{T}^{+}
, the upper half of
\mathbb{T}
. Define
\hat{f_{\tau}}:[-1,1]\to\mathbb{C}
by
\hat{f_{\tau}}(x)=f_{\tau}(h(x))
. By Proposition
2.14
and Theorem
2.16
, we have that

\displaystyle\frac{\cvar(f,S)}{\vf(S)}=\frac{\cvar(f_{\tau},S_{\tau})}{\vf(S_{\tau})}\leq\frac{\cvar\bigl(\hat{f_{\tau}},(S_{\tau})_{\mathbb{R}}\bigr)}{\vf\big((S_{\tau})_{\mathbb{R}}\big)}\leq\myvar_{[-1,1]}\bigl(\hat{f_{\tau}}\bigr).

Noting that

\displaystyle\myvar_{[-1,1]}\bigl(\hat{f_{\tau}}\bigr)=\myvar_{[0,\pi]}(f_{\tau}(e^{i(\cdot)}))\leq{\textstyle\myvar_{\mathbb{T}}(f_{\tau})}={\textstyle\myvar_{\mathbb{T}}(f)},

on taking the supremum over all lists
S
we conclude that
\myvar(f,\mathbb{T})\leq\myvar_{\mathbb{T}}(f)
.

Combining Lemma
2.18
with Theorem
2.20
yields the following corollary.

Corollary 2.21
.

BV(\mathbb{T})
is isomorphic to
BV_{BG}(\mathbb{T})
as Banach algebras.

Theorem 2.22
.

If
\sigma
is a strictly convex closed curve, then
BV(\sigma)
is isometrically isomorphic to
BV(\mathbb{T})
as Banach algebras.

Proof:
By affine invariance, we may assume that the origin lies within the interior of
\sigma
. As
\sigma
is a convex closed curve, it can be parametrized by the angle from the positive real axis:
\gamma_{\sigma}:[0,2\pi]\to\mathbb{C}
where
\gamma_{\sigma}(\theta)=r(\theta)e^{i\theta}
for some continuous
r:[0,2\pi]\to[0,\infty)
. We can then define a mapping
\Phi:BV(\mathbb{T})\to BV(\sigma)
by
\Phi(f)(\gamma_{\sigma}(\theta))=f(e^{i\theta})
. This mapping is an isomorphism of algebras, and is an isometry. To see this, we note that for any finite list of points
S=[e^{i\theta_{0}},\dots,e^{i\theta_{n}}]
of
\mathbb{T}
, write
\hat{S}=[r(\theta_{0})e^{i\theta_{0}},\dots,r(\theta_{n})e^{i\theta_{0}}]
. Then it should be clear that
\cvar(f,S)=\cvar(\Phi(f),\hat{S})
. Now let
\ell
be a line such that
\vf(S)=\vf(S,\ell)
. Without loss,
\ell
intersects
\mathbb{T}
at two distinct points, say
e^{i\alpha}
and
e^{i\beta}
. Let
\ell_{\sigma}
be the line joining
r(\alpha)e^{i\alpha}
and
r(\beta)e^{i\beta}
. Then it can be checked that due to the strict convexity of
\mathbb{T}
and
\sigma
that every crossing segment of
\ell
on
S
corresponds to a crossing segment the same type of
\ell_{\sigma}
on
\hat{S}
. Hence
\vf(S)\leq\vf(\hat{S},\ell_{\sigma})\leq\vf(\hat{S})
. Similarly,
\vf(\hat{S})\leq\vf(S)
. Hence
\myvar(f,S)=\myvar(\Phi(f),\sigma)
and
\|f\|_{BV(\mathbb{T})}=\|\phi(f)\|_{BV(\sigma)}
.

2.7
Finite sets

Even in the case that the set
\sigma
is finite, the definition of the variation of a function involves a supremum over an infinite family of lists of elements of the set. Of course when
\sigma
is a finite subset of
\mathbb{R}
this supremum is achieved by taking the list
S
to contain all the points of
\sigma
in order, and hence is easily calculated.
It would be desirable to have a ‘finitely computable’ formula which applied for more general finite sets. In general, no such formula is currently known, but some information is available in special cases.

If we say that
\sigma=\{{\boldsymbol{z}}_{i}\}_{i=1}^{m}
consists of the vertices of a convex polygon
C_{\sigma}
we shall assume that the points
{\boldsymbol{z}}_{1},\dots,{\boldsymbol{z}}_{m}
are listed so that they are consecutive vertices if the boundary of
C_{\sigma}
is followed in an anticlockwise manner.

Theorem 2.23
.

Suppose that
\sigma=\{{\boldsymbol{z}}_{i}\}_{i=1}^{m}
and
{\hat{\sigma}}=\{{\boldsymbol{w}}_{i}\}_{i=1}^{m}
each consist of the vertices of convex polygons
C_{\sigma}
and
C_{\hat{\sigma}}
. Define
h:\sigma\to{\hat{\sigma}}
by
h({\boldsymbol{z}}_{i})={\boldsymbol{w}}_{i}
,
i=1,\dots,m
, and define
\Phi:BV(\sigma)\to BV({\hat{\sigma}})
by
\Phi(f)=f\circ h^{-1}
. Then
\Phi
is an isometric Banach algebra isomorphism.

Proof:
The proof is almost identical to that of Theorem
2.22
. Again it is clear that
\Phi
is an algebra isomorphism. Given
f\in BV(\sigma)
, a list
S=[{\boldsymbol{x}}_{0},\dots,{\boldsymbol{x}}_{n}]
of points in
\sigma
, and a corresponding set of points
{\hat{S}}=[h({\boldsymbol{x}}_{0}),\dots,h({\boldsymbol{x}}_{n})]
, then as in that proof, one can show that
\vf(S)=\vf({\hat{S}})
and
\cvar(f,S)=\cvar(\Phi(f),{\hat{S}})
. This is enough to show that
\myvar(f,\sigma)=\myvar(\Phi(f),{\hat{\sigma}})
and hence that
\left\lVert f\right\rVert_{BV(\sigma)}=\left\lVert\Phi(f)\right\rVert_{BV(\hat{\sigma})}
.

A consequence of this theorem is that for finite sets which comprise the vertices of a convex set, one may, for the purposes of calculating the variation, assume that the points lie on the unit circle.

Theorem 2.24
.

Suppose that
\sigma=\{{\boldsymbol{z}}_{i}\}_{i=1}^{m}
consists of the vertices of a convex polygon, and that
f:\sigma\to\mathbb{C}
. Then

\frac{1}{2}\Bigl(|f({\boldsymbol{z}}_{m})-f({\boldsymbol{z}}_{1})|+\sum_{i=1}^{m-1}|f({\boldsymbol{z}}_{i})-f({\boldsymbol{z}}_{i+1})|\Bigr)\leq\myvar(f,\sigma)\leq\sum_{i=1}^{m-1}|f({\boldsymbol{z}}_{i})-f({\boldsymbol{z}}_{i+1})|.

(2.1)

Proof:
The left hand inequality follows exactly as in the proof of Lemma
2.18
. For the right hand inequality, we can use Theorem
2.23
to move the points into the upper half of the unit circle so that
{\boldsymbol{z}}_{j}=e^{i\theta_{j}}
with
0\leq\theta_{1}<\theta_{2}<\dots<\theta_{m}\leq\pi
. Then
\sigma_{\mathbb{R}}=\{\cos\theta_{j}\}_{j=1}^{m}
and, if one sets
g(\theta_{j})=f({\boldsymbol{z}}_{j})
,
j=1,\dots,m
, then by Theorem
2.16
,
\myvar(f,\sigma)\leq\myvar(g,\sigma_{\mathbb{R}})
. But
\myvar(g,\sigma_{\mathbb{R}})
is just the expression on the right-hand side of (
2.1
).

In the case that
\sigma=\{{\boldsymbol{z}}_{j}\}_{j=1}^{3}
comprises three non-collinear points we can say a little more.

Theorem 2.25
.

Suppose
\sigma=\{{\boldsymbol{z}}_{1},{\boldsymbol{z}}_{2},{\boldsymbol{z}}_{3}\}
is a set of three non-collinear points in
\mathbb{C}
and that
f:\sigma\to\mathbb{C}
. Then

\displaystyle\myvar(f,\sigma)=\frac{1}{2}\sum_{i<j}|f({\boldsymbol{z}}_{i})-f({\boldsymbol{z}}_{j})|.

Proof:
Theorem
2.24
for
m=3
says that the expression given is a lower bound for
\myvar(f,\sigma)
, so it just remains to show that it is also an upper bound.

Let
S=[{\boldsymbol{x}}_{0},\dots,{\boldsymbol{x}}_{n}]
be a list of points in
\sigma
. (We may safely assume that the list does not contain the same point in consecutive positions in the list, and that
n\geq 1
.) Each term in
\cvar(f,S)
is of the form
f_{ij}:=|f({\boldsymbol{z}}_{i})-f({\boldsymbol{z}}_{j})|
with
i<j
. Let
c_{ij}
denote the number of times that
f_{ij}
occurs in the sum for
\cvar(f,S)
. Let
f_{\rm max},f_{\rm med},f_{\rm min}
be the
f_{ij}
’s arranged in non-increasing order, and let
c_{\rm max}
,
c_{\rm med}
and
c_{\rm min}
be the
c_{ij}
’s arranged in non-increasing order.

Using the rearrangement inequality, and noting that
\vf(S)\geq c_{\rm max}+c_{\rm med}\geq 1
, we have that

\displaystyle\frac{\cvar(f,S)}{\vf(S)}\leq\frac{c_{\rm max}f_{\rm max}+c_{\rm med}f_{\rm med}+c_{\rm min}f_{\rm min}}{c_{\rm max}+c_{\rm med}}.

The triangle inequality implies that
f_{\rm med}+f_{\rm min}\geq f_{\rm max}
and hence that

0\leq(c_{\rm max}-c_{\rm med})(f_{\rm med}+f_{\rm min}-f_{\rm max})+2f_{\rm min}(c_{\rm med}-c_{\rm min}).

This can be rearranged to

2(c_{\rm max}f_{\rm max}+c_{\rm med}f_{\rm med}+c_{\rm min}f_{\rm min})\leq(c_{\rm max}+c_{\rm med})(f_{\rm max}+f_{\rm med}+f_{\rm min})

or equivalently,

\frac{c_{\rm max}f_{\rm max}+c_{\rm med}f_{\rm med}+c_{\rm min}f_{\rm min}}{c_{\rm max}+c_{\rm med}}\leq\frac{f_{\rm max}+f_{\rm med}+f_{\rm min}}{2}.

Thus taking the supremum over all such
S
will yield

\displaystyle\myvar(f,\sigma)\leq\frac{1}{2}\left(f_{\rm max}+f_{\rm med}+f_{\rm min}\right)=\frac{1}{2}\sum_{i<j}|f({\boldsymbol{z}}_{i})-f({\boldsymbol{z}}_{j})|.

The requirement that the points in
\sigma
are non-collinear in Theorem
2.25
is essential.

It is currently an open question as to whether
\myvar(f,\sigma)
is always equal to the lower bound in (
2.1
) if
\sigma
has more than 3 points. We note that this is the case if
f
is real valued with
f({\boldsymbol{z}}_{1})\leq f({\boldsymbol{z}}_{2})\leq\dots\leq f({\boldsymbol{z}}_{m})
, since then the upper and lower bounds coincide.

2.8
Lattice properties of
BV_{\mathbb{R}}(\sigma)

Here we establish some additional properties of
BV(\sigma)
, particularly that the space
BV_{\mathbb{R}}(\sigma)
of real-valued functions is a lattice under pointwise operations. We first note that
BV_{\mathbb{R}}(\sigma)
is closed under taking absolute values since, as stated in Lemma
2.7
,
\myvar(|f|,\sigma)\leq\myvar(f,\sigma)
.

Lemma 2.26
.

If
f\in BV(\sigma)
, then
\left\lVert\,|f|\,\right\rVert_{BV(\sigma)}\leq\left\lVert f\right\rVert_{BV(\sigma)}
and so
|f|\in BV(\sigma)
.

Given
f,g:\sigma\to\mathbb{R}
, let
f\vee g
and
f\wedge g
denote the pointwise maximum and minimum of
f
and
g
respectively.

Proposition 2.27
.

If
f,g\in BV_{\mathbb{R}}(\sigma)
, then
f\vee g
and
f\wedge g
are in
BV(\sigma)
with

\displaystyle\left\lVert f\vee g\right\rVert_{{BV}(\sigma)},\left\lVert f\wedge g\right\rVert_{{BV}(\sigma)}\leq\left\lVert f\right\rVert_{{BV}(\sigma)}+\left\lVert g\right\rVert_{{BV}(\sigma)}.

Proof:
Suppose that
f,g\in BV(\sigma)
are real valued. Noting that

\displaystyle f\vee g=\frac{1}{2}\big(f+g+|f-g|\big),

and that
|f-g|\in BV(\sigma)
by Lemma
2.26
, we find that
f\vee g\in BV(\sigma)
. Similarly, using
f\wedge g=\frac{1}{2}(f+g-|f-g|)
, we see that
f\wedge g\in BV(\sigma)
. The claimed bound follows by applying the triangle inequality to the identities for
f\vee g
and
f\wedge g
.

Remark 2.28
.

If one is only interested in estimating the variation, then by Lemma
2.7
one has that

\displaystyle\myvar(f\vee g,\sigma),\myvar(f\wedge g,\sigma)\leq\myvar(f,\sigma)+\myvar(g,\sigma).

Remark 2.29
.

Though a lattice,
BV_{\mathbb{R}}(\sigma)
is not a Banach lattice under pointwise operations. Recall that for a lattice to be a Banach lattice, the norm must respect the lattice ordering. That is,
\left\lVert|x|\right\rVert\leq\left\lVert|y|\right\rVert
whenever
|x|\leq|y|
(where
|x|=x\vee(-x)
). In
BV[0,1]
, consider the constant
f=3
and the function
g=2-\chi_{\{0.5\}}
. Then
f>g\geq 1
but
\left\lVert f\right\rVert=3<\left\lVert g\right\rVert=4
.

3
Subalgebras

Before progressing further we shall demonstrate that
BV(\sigma)
always contains a suitably large collections of useful functions. In particular,
BV(\sigma)
always contains the polynomials in two real variables.

3.1
Absolutely continuous functions

In this section we shall consider
\sigma
to be a subset of
\mathbb{R}^{2}
.
Define
p_{x},p_{y}:\sigma\to\mathbb{C}
by
p_{x}(x,y)=x
and
p_{y}(x,y)=y
. By Theorem
2.16
,
p_{x},p_{y}\in BV(\sigma)
.
Let
\mathcal{P}_{2}
denote the algebra of complex-valued polynomials in two variables,
p(x,y)=\sum_{m,n=0}^{N}c_{mn}\,x^{m}y^{n}
. Since
BV(\sigma)
is an algebra, the following is immediate.

Lemma 3.1
.

\mathcal{P}_{2}
is a subalgebra of
BV(\sigma)
.

The space
\mathcal{P}_{2}
should more correctly be denoted as
\mathcal{P}_{2}(\sigma)
, and the reader is cautioned that the same function may have distinct representations as a polynomial if the set
\sigma
is small.
Here and throughout the paper we shall usually use the shorter form of the notation if there is no risk of confusion.

Definition 3.2
.

The space of
absolutely continuous functions
of
\sigma
, denoted
{AC}(\sigma)
, is defined to be the closure of
\mathcal{P}_{2}
in
({BV}(\sigma),\|\cdot\|_{BV(\sigma)})
.

Since
BV
convergence implies uniform convergence, it is
clear that all absolutely continuous functions are in fact
continuous. From Proposition
2.10
, we know that when
\sigma
is infinite and compact,
AC(\sigma)
is a proper Banach subalgebra of
BV(\sigma)
.

In the case that
\sigma=[a,b]\subseteq\mathbb{R}
, this definition agrees with the more classical one, and in that case one can readily find continuous functions of bounded variation (such as the Cantor function) which are not absolutely continuous. Moreover, it follows from Corollary
2.21
that
AC(\mathbb{T})
is isomorphic to
AC_{BG}(\mathbb{T})
, the Banach algebra of absolutely continuous functions as defined by Berkson and Gillespie in
BG2
.

The following simple but useful lemma is worth recording.

Lemma 3.3
.

Suppose that
\sigma
is a nonempty compact subset of
\mathbb{R}^{2}
. Then
f\in AC(\sigma)
if and only if
\text{Re}(f),\text{Im}(f)\in AC(\sigma)
.

Proof:
This simply follows from the fact that
AC(\sigma)
is a vector space that is closed under complex conjugation.

Theorem 3.4
.

Suppose that
\sigma_{1},\sigma
are nonempty compact subsets of the plane with
\sigma_{1}\subseteq\sigma
. If
f\in AC(\sigma)
then
f|\sigma_{1}\in AC(\sigma_{1})
, with
\left\lVert f|\sigma_{1}\right\rVert_{BV(\sigma_{1})}\leq\left\lVert f\right\rVert_{BV(\sigma)}
.

Proof:
If
f\in AC(\sigma)
, then there is a sequence of polynomials
\{p_{n}\}_{n=1}^{\infty}
such that
p_{n}\to f
as
n\to\infty
in
BV(\sigma)
. By Theorem
2.13
,
\{p_{n}|\sigma_{1}\}_{n=1}^{\infty}
defines a sequence of polynomials in
AC(\sigma_{1})
such that
p_{n}|\sigma_{1}\to f|\sigma_{1}
in
BV(\sigma_{1})
and

\displaystyle\|f|\sigma_{1}\|_{BV(\sigma_{1})}=\lim_{n\to\infty}\|p_{n}|\sigma_{1}\|_{BV(\sigma_{1})}\leq\lim_{n\to\infty}\|p_{n}\|_{BV(\sigma)}=\|f\|_{BV(\sigma)}.

Let
\phi
be an invertible affine map of the plane and let
{\hat{\sigma}}=\phi(\sigma)
. It is an easy exercise to check that
p\in\mathcal{P}_{2}(\sigma)
if and only if
{\hat{p}}=p\circ\phi^{-1}\in\mathcal{P}_{2}({\hat{\sigma}})
. (We note that this is true whether one is considering real or complex affine maps.) This immediately implies the following analogues of Theorems
2.12
and
2.16
.

Theorem 3.5
.

Let
\sigma
be a nonempty compact subset of
\mathbb{R}^{2}
and let
\phi
be an invertible affine map of the plane. Then
AC(\phi(\sigma))
is isometrically isomorphic to
AC(\sigma)
.

Theorem 3.6
.

Suppose that
\sigma
is a nonempty compact subset of the plane with projection
\sigma_{\mathbb{R}}
onto the real axis. If
u\in AC(\sigma_{\mathbb{R}})
and
{\hat{u}}
is the extension of
u
to
\sigma
given by
{\hat{u}}(x,y)=u(x)
, then
{\hat{u}}\in AC(\sigma)
.

Proof:
Clearly if
p:\sigma_{\mathbb{R}}\to\mathbb{C}
is a polynomial of one real variable, then its extension
{\hat{p}}
lies in
\mathcal{P}_{2}
. By Theorem
2.16
, if
p_{n}\to u
in
BV(\sigma_{\mathbb{R}})
then
{\hat{p}}_{n}\to{\hat{u}}
in
BV(\sigma)
.

Again, one might replace the real axis here with any other line in the plane.

Some natural questions that arise are:

1.

If
\sigma_{1}\subset\sigma
and
f\in AC(\sigma_{1})
, does there exist
\hat{f}\in AC(\sigma)
such that
\hat{f}|\sigma_{1}=f
?

2.

If
\sigma
can be decomposed as
\sigma=\sigma_{1}\cup\sigma_{2}
and
f:\sigma\to\mathbb{C}
such that
f|\sigma_{1}\in AC(\sigma_{1})
and
f|\sigma_{2}\in AC(\sigma_{2})
, then
f\in AC(\sigma)
?

The first question is still open in general, but we answer the second in the negative.

Example 3.7
.

Let
\sigma_{1}=\{0\}\cup\{\frac{1}{2k-1}\}_{k=1}^{\infty}
,
\sigma_{2}=\{0\}\cup\{\frac{1}{2k}\}_{k=1}^{\infty}
, and
\sigma=\sigma_{1}\cup\sigma_{2}
. Define
f:\sigma\to\mathbb{C}
by

\displaystyle f(x)=\begin{cases}0&\text{ if $x=0$},\\
(-1)^{k}x&\text{ if $x=\frac{1}{k}$}.\end{cases}

First we observe that since
f|\sigma_{1}(x)=-x
and
f|\sigma_{2}(x)=x
, we have that
f|\sigma_{1}
and
f|\sigma_{2}
are polynomials and hence absolutely continuous. On the other hand,
f
is not of bounded variation since

\displaystyle\myvar(f,\sigma)

\displaystyle=\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}\left|f(\tfrac{1}{k})-f(\tfrac{1}{k+1})\right|

\displaystyle=\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}\left|\frac{(-1)^{k}}{k}-\frac{(-1)^{k+1}}{k+1}\right|

\displaystyle=\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}\frac{1}{k}+\frac{1}{k+1},

which is divergent.

However, if one imposes some conditions on
\sigma_{1}
and
\sigma_{2}
, then the second question can be answered positively. We shall say that two nonempty compact sets
\sigma_{1}
and
\sigma_{2}

join convexly
if for all
{\boldsymbol{x}}\in\sigma_{1}\setminus\sigma_{2}
and
{\boldsymbol{y}}\in\sigma_{2}\setminus\sigma_{1}
there exists
{\boldsymbol{w}}
on the line joining
{\boldsymbol{x}}
and
{\boldsymbol{y}}
with
{\boldsymbol{w}}\in\sigma_{1}\cap\sigma_{2}
. Clearly if
\sigma_{1}\cup\sigma_{2}
is convex then the two sets join convexly. The following result was stated in
AD3
for convex sets, although the proof only uses the property of joining convexly. Below we present the slightly more general version along with a proof using the updated definitions of variation in the plane.

Theorem 3.8
.

Suppose that
\sigma_{1},\sigma_{2}\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{2}
are
nonempty compact sets which are disjoint except at their boundaries and that
\sigma_{1}
and
\sigma_{2}
join convexly. Let
\sigma=\sigma_{1}\cup\sigma_{2}
. If
f:\sigma\to\mathbb{C}
,
then

\max\{\myvar(f,\sigma_{1}),\myvar(f,\sigma_{2})\}\leq\myvar(f,\sigma)\leq\myvar(f,\sigma_{1})+\myvar(f,\sigma_{2})

and hence

\max\{\left\lVert f\right\rVert_{{BV}(\sigma_{1})},\left\lVert f\right\rVert_{{BV}(\sigma_{2})}\}\leq\left\lVert f\right\rVert_{{BV}(\sigma)}\leq\left\lVert f\right\rVert_{{BV}(\sigma_{1})}+\left\lVert f\right\rVert_{{BV}(\sigma_{2})}.

Thus, if
f|\sigma_{1}\in{BV}(\sigma_{1})
and
f|\sigma_{2}\in{BV}(\sigma_{2})
, then
f\in{BV}(\sigma)
.

Proof:
The left hand inequalities are a simple consequence of Theorem
2.13
.

Suppose then that
S=[{\boldsymbol{z}}_{0},{\boldsymbol{z}}_{1},\dots,{\boldsymbol{z}}_{n}]
is a finite list of points in
\sigma
.
Since
\sigma_{1}
and
\sigma_{2}
join convexly, for any
j
for which
{\boldsymbol{z}}_{j-1}
and
{\boldsymbol{z}}_{j}
do not lie in the same
\sigma_{i}
, we can find a point
{\boldsymbol{w}}_{j}
in
\sigma_{1}\cap\sigma_{2}
which lies on the line segment
\overline{\vphantom{\vbox to5.16663pt{}}{\boldsymbol{z}}_{j-1}\,{\boldsymbol{z}}_{j}}
. Inserting each such
{\boldsymbol{w}}_{j}
into the list
S
between
{\boldsymbol{z}}_{j-1}
and
{\boldsymbol{z}}_{j}
produces an extended list
S^{+}=[{\boldsymbol{z}}_{0}^{+},{\boldsymbol{z}}_{1}^{+},\dots,{\boldsymbol{z}}_{m}^{+}]
with the property that for each
j
either
\{{\boldsymbol{z}}_{j-1}^{+},{\boldsymbol{z}}_{j}^{+}\}\subseteq\sigma_{1}
, or
\{{\boldsymbol{z}}_{j-1}^{+},{\boldsymbol{z}}_{j}^{+}\}\subseteq\sigma_{2}
(or both).
Note that adding these points does not change the variation factor, and can only increase the curve variation of
f
. That is
\vf(S)=\vf(S^{+})
and
\cvar(f,S)\leq\cvar(f,S^{+})
.

For
i=1,2
, let
S_{i}^{+}=[{\boldsymbol{z}}_{0}^{(i)},{\boldsymbol{z}}_{1}^{(i)},\dots,{\boldsymbol{z}}_{n_{i}}^{(i)}]
be the list obtained by removing from
S^{+}
all points which do not lie in
\sigma_{i}
.
Every pair of consecutive points
{\boldsymbol{z}}_{j-1}^{+}
and
{\boldsymbol{z}}_{j}^{+}
in
S^{+}
is in at least one of
S_{1}^{+}
and
S_{2}^{+}
. Thus

\displaystyle\sum_{j=1}^{m}|f({\boldsymbol{z}}_{j}^{+})-f({\boldsymbol{z}}_{j-1}^{+})|

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{i=1}^{2}\sum_{j=1}^{n_{i}}\left|f\big({\boldsymbol{z}}_{j}^{(i)}\big)-f\big({\boldsymbol{z}}_{j-1}^{(i)}\big)\right|,

where an empty sum is interpreted to have a value of zero. Hence

\displaystyle\myvar(f,S)

\displaystyle=\frac{\cvar(f,S)}{\vf(S)}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{\cvar(f,S^{+})}{\vf(S^{+})}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\vf(S^{+})}\sum_{j=1}^{m}|f({\boldsymbol{z}}_{j}^{+})-f({\boldsymbol{z}}_{j-1}^{+})|

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{i=1}^{2}\frac{1}{\vf(S_{i}^{+})}\sum_{j=1}^{n_{i}}\left|f\big({\boldsymbol{z}}_{j}^{(i)}\big)-f\big({\boldsymbol{z}}_{j-1}^{(i)}\big)\right|

\displaystyle=\frac{\cvar(f|\sigma_{1},S_{1}^{+})}{\vf(S_{1}^{+})}+\frac{\cvar(f|\sigma_{2},S_{2}^{+})}{\vf(S_{2}^{+})}

\displaystyle\leq\myvar(f|\sigma_{1},\sigma_{1})+\myvar(f|\sigma_{2},\sigma_{2}).

The result follows from the fact that
\|f\|_{\infty}
is equal to
\|f|\sigma_{1}\|_{\infty}
or
\|f|\sigma_{2}\|_{\infty}
.

When
\sigma\subset\mathbb{R}
, it follows from the classical definition of absolute continuity that
f\in AC(\sigma)
implies
|f|\in AC(\sigma)
, and so the real-valued absolutely continuous functions
AC_{\mathbb{R}}(\sigma)
form a lattice. Moreover, we have seen that
{BV}_{\mathbb{R}}(\sigma)
is a lattice under pointwise operations.

Open Problem.
For general compact
\sigma\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{2}
, is
AC_{\mathbb{R}}(\sigma)
a lattice under pointwise operations?

The obstacle in proving that
AC_{\mathbb{R}}(\sigma)
is a lattice is that it is unclear whether
f\in AC(\sigma)
implies
|f|\in AC(\sigma)
. For a positive answer, it would be sufficient to have the following result, which is certainly true in the case of
\sigma\subset\mathbb{R}
.

Conjecture 3.9
.

Suppose that
\sigma
is a nonempty compact subset of
\mathbb{C}
,
f\in AC(\sigma)
and
\varepsilon>0
. Then there is a compact neighbourhood
U_{\varepsilon}
of
Z(f)
such that
\myvar(f,U_{\varepsilon})<\varepsilon
.

3.2
Lipschitz functions

In this section we shall consider
\sigma
to be a subset of
\mathbb{C}
. By the standard identifications,
the identity function
\zeta:\sigma\to\mathbb{C}
,
\zeta(x+iy)=x+iy=p_{x}(x,y)+ip_{y}(x,y)
lies in
\mathcal{P}_{2}
, and so it is certainly always a function of bounded variation. Recall that the diameter of
\sigma
is defined as
\mathrm{diam}(\sigma)=\max\{|{\boldsymbol{x}}-{\boldsymbol{y}}|\thinspace:\thinspace{\boldsymbol{x}},{\boldsymbol{y}}\in\sigma\}
. Let
|\sigma_{\mathbb{R}}|
and

|\sigma_{i\mathbb{R}}|
denote the diameters of the projections of
\sigma
onto the real and imaginary axes. Then, by the proof of Theorem
2.16
, we have that
\myvar(p_{x},\sigma)\leq|\sigma_{\mathbb{R}}|
and
\myvar(p_{y},\sigma)\leq|\sigma_{i\mathbb{R}}|
, and so

\max\{|\sigma_{\mathbb{R}}|,|\sigma_{i\mathbb{R}}|\}\leq\mathrm{diam}(\sigma)\leq\myvar(\zeta,\sigma)\leq\myvar(p_{x},\sigma)+\myvar(p_{y},\sigma)\leq|\sigma_{\mathbb{R}}|+|\sigma_{i\mathbb{R}}|.

(3.1)

Definition 3.10
.

Supppose that
\sigma
is a nonempty compact subset of
\mathbb{C}
. The
variation constant
of
\sigma
is the number
C_{\sigma}=\myvar(\zeta,\sigma)
.

The upper and lower bounds for
C_{\sigma}
given in (
3.1
) can both be obtained. Certainly if
\sigma
lies in a line, then
C_{\sigma}=\mathrm{diam}(\sigma)=|\sigma_{\mathbb{R}}|
(and of course
|\sigma_{i\mathbb{R}}|=0
). On the other hand, if
\sigma=\{0,1,i,1+i\}
, Theorem
2.24
implies that
C_{\sigma}\geq 2=|\sigma_{\mathbb{R}}|+|\sigma_{i\mathbb{R}}|
.

For a set
\sigma
containing at least 2 elements, the Banach algebra
{\mathrm{Lip}}(\sigma)
of
Lipschitz functions on
\sigma
is the space
of all functions
f
such that

\left\lVert f\right\rVert_{{\mathrm{Lip}}(\sigma)}=\left\lVert f\right\rVert_{\infty}+L_{\sigma}(f)
is finite,
where

L_{\sigma}(f)=\sup\Bigl\{\frac{|f({\boldsymbol{x}})-f({\boldsymbol{x}}^{\prime})|}{|{\boldsymbol{x}}-{\boldsymbol{x}}^{\prime}|}\thinspace:\thinspace{\boldsymbol{x}}\neq{\boldsymbol{x}}^{\prime}\in\sigma\Bigr\}.

(Again one might include the trivial case of a singleton set by defining
L_{\sigma}(f)
to be always zero in that case.)

Theorem 3.11
.

Let
\sigma
be a nonempty compact subset of
\mathbb{C}
. Then for
f\in{\mathrm{Lip}}(\sigma)
,

\displaystyle\left\lVert f\right\rVert_{{BV}(\sigma)}\leq\max\{1,C_{\sigma}\}\left\lVert f\right\rVert_{{\mathrm{Lip}}(\sigma)}

and so
{\mathrm{Lip}}(\sigma)\subseteq BV(\sigma)
.

Proof:
Suppose that
f\in{\mathrm{Lip}}(\sigma)
and that
S=\bigl[{\boldsymbol{x}}_{0},\dots,{\boldsymbol{x}}_{n}\bigr]
is a list of points in
\sigma
. Then

\displaystyle\frac{\cvar(f,S)}{\vf(S)}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\vf(S)}\sum_{i=1}^{n}|f({\boldsymbol{x}}_{i})-f({\boldsymbol{x}}_{i-1})|

\displaystyle\leq\frac{L_{\sigma}(f)}{\vf(S)}\sum_{i=1}^{n}|{\boldsymbol{x}}_{i}-{\boldsymbol{x}}_{i-1}|

\displaystyle\leq L_{\sigma}(f)C_{\sigma}.

It follows that

\left\lVert f\right\rVert_{{BV}(\sigma)}=\left\lVert f\right\rVert_{\infty}+\myvar(f,\sigma)\leq\left\lVert f\right\rVert_{\infty}+L_{\sigma}(f)C_{\sigma}\leq\max\{1,C_{\sigma}\}\left\lVert f\right\rVert_{{\mathrm{Lip}}(\sigma)}.

If
\sigma\subseteq\mathbb{R}
then every Lipschitz function is absolutely continuous and indeed
AC(\sigma)
is the closure of
{\mathrm{Lip}}(\sigma)
in that case. For more general sets this inclusion may fail. Before giving the example we require the following lemma from
AD1
.

Lemma 3.12
.

Let
\sigma=[0,1]^{2}
and for
t\in[0,1]
, let
\sigma_{t}=\{(x,t)\thinspace:\thinspace x\in[0,1]\}
. For all
f\in AC(\sigma)
,

\displaystyle\lim_{t\to 0^{+}}\myvar(f,\sigma_{t})=\myvar(f,\sigma_{0})
.

Proof:
Suppose that
f\in AC(\sigma)
. Fix
\varepsilon>0
. By definition, there exists
p\in\mathcal{P}_{2}
such that
\left\lVert f-p\right\rVert_{BV(\sigma)}<\varepsilon/3
. This implies in particular that (using Lemma
2.7
) that for all
t
,
|\myvar(f,\sigma_{t})-\myvar(p,\sigma_{t})|\leq\myvar(f-p,\sigma_{t})\leq\myvar(f-p,\sigma)<\varepsilon/3
.

Let
M=\sup\{\left|\frac{\partial^{2}p}{\partial x\partial y}(x,y)\right|\thinspace:\thinspace(x,y)\in\sigma\}
. By the Mean Value Theorem, there exist values
c(x,t)\in(0,1)
such that

\frac{\partial p}{\partial x}(x,t)-\frac{\partial p}{\partial x}(x,0)=t\frac{\partial^{2}p}{\partial x\partial y}(x,c(x,t)).

Suppose that
0<t<\varepsilon/(3M)
.
By the Reverse Triangle Inequality then

\displaystyle|\myvar(p,\sigma_{t})-\myvar(p,\sigma_{0})|

\displaystyle=\left|\int_{0}^{1}\left|\frac{\partial p}{\partial x}(x,t)\right|-\left|\frac{\partial p}{\partial x}(x,0)\right|\,dx\right|

\displaystyle\leq\int_{0}^{1}t\left|\frac{\partial^{2}p}{\partial x\partial y}(x,c(x,t))\right|\,dx

\displaystyle<\frac{\varepsilon}{3M}\,M=\frac{\varepsilon}{3}.

Thus

\displaystyle|\myvar(f,\sigma_{t})-\myvar(f,\sigma_{0})|

\displaystyle\leq|\myvar(f,\sigma_{t})-\myvar(p,\sigma_{t})|+|\myvar(p,\sigma_{t})-\myvar(p,\sigma_{0})|

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad+|\myvar(p,\sigma_{0})-\myvar(f,\sigma_{0})|

\displaystyle<\varepsilon.

The following example of a function which is in
{\mathrm{Lip}}(\sigma)
but not in
AC(\sigma)
was first given in
Ash
.

Example 3.13
.

Let
\psi:\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R}
be the
1
-periodic function satisfying
\psi(x)=|x|
for
-\frac{1}{2}\leq x\leq\frac{1}{2}
. Let
\sigma=[0,1]^{2}
and for
t\in[0,1]
let
\sigma_{t}=[0,1]\times\{t\}
.
For
n=0,1,2,\dots
, let
t_{n}=2^{-n}
and define
f
on
\sigma_{t_{n}}
by
f(x,2^{-n})=2^{-n}\psi(2^{n}x)
. For each
x\in[0,1]
, now fix
f(x,t)
to be linear in
t
on each interval of the form
(t_{n+1},t_{n})
. Finally let
f(x,0)=0
for all
x\in[0,1]
.

The graph of
f
on each rectangle of the form
[k/2^{m},(k+1)/2^{m}]\times[t_{n+1},t_{n}]
is just a scaled version of graph of

on
[0,1]\times[\frac{1}{2},1]
. One can use this to see that

is indeed Lipschitz with

. Note however that
\myvar(f,\sigma_{t_{n}})=1
for all

\myvar(\sigma_{0})=0
, and so by Lemma

.

Despite such examples, it is often easier to calculate

than

give a useful tool for proving convergence in

is a sequence in

and

then

in

and so

.

3.3
Differentiable functions

One expects that sufficiently smooth functions should be absolutely continuous. Making that precise requires a degree of care since our functions are defined on compact rather than open subsets of the plane.

Even in the classical case of
\sigma=[0,1]
, there are continuous functions which fail to be of bounded variation. Indeed, it is easy to construct functions
f\in C[0,1]
such that
f
is infinitely differentiable on
(0,1)
but
f
is not of bounded variation. The function

f(x)=\begin{cases}x^{2}\sin(x^{-2})&x\neq 0,\\
0&x=0\end{cases}

is differentiable on any open neighbourhood of
[0,1]
, but it does not lie in
BV[0,1]
.

Of course if
f^{\prime}
exists everywhere and is bounded, then
f
is Lipschitz and hence
f\in BV[0,1]
and indeed, in this classical setting,
f\in AC[0,1]
. We have then that
C^{1}[0,1]\subseteq{\mathrm{Lip}}[0,1]\subseteq AC[0,1]
.

For this section we shall consider the plane as being
\mathbb{R}^{2}
since we do not want to require complex differentiability of our functions.

Definition 3.14
.

Suppose that
\sigma
is a nonempty compact subset of

and that

.
Similarly, we shall denote by

The reader is cautioned that there are several nonequivalent definitions of

It is worth noting that if

then in fact

admits a

and hence in particular to any open subset of the plane.

We show in

that in the general case, every

function is absolutely continuous. This requires a significant amount of extra machinery, so here we will just show the easier result that being
C^{2}
is sufficient. We begin with the case that
\sigma
is a rectangle.

Theorem 3.15
.

Suppose that

is a rectangle in the plane. Then

.

Proof:
Suppose that

Then there exist polynomials (in two variables)

Note that

\displaystyle|F_{x}(x,y)-h^{x}(x,y)|

\displaystyle\leq\int_{0}^{x}|F_{xx}(t,0)-g^{xx}(t,0)|\,dt+\int_{0}^{y}|F_{xy}(x,s)-g^{xy}(x,s)|\,ds

\displaystyle<2\varepsilon.

(3.2)

A similar calculation shows that

. Note also that

\frac{\partial h^{y}}{\partial x}=g^{xy}.

\displaystyle=h^{x}(x,0)+\int_{0}^{y}g^{xy}(x,s)\,ds

\displaystyle|F_{x}(x,y)-p_{x}(x,y)|

4
Absolute continuity is a local property

In this section we shall show that being in
{AC}(\sigma)
is determined by the behaviour of the function in a neighbourhood of each point.

Recall that the classical definition of absolute continuity for
f:[0,1]\to~\mathbb{C}
is that for all
\varepsilon>0
there exists
\delta>0
such that if
\{[x_{i},x_{i}^{\prime}]\}_{i=1}^{n}
is any collection of disjoint subintervals of total length less than
\delta
, then
\sum_{i=1}^{n}|f(x_{i})-f(x_{i}^{\prime})|<\varepsilon
.

Suppose that
f:[0,1]\to\mathbb{C}
, and that for all

there exists an open interval

containing

such that
f|\overline{V}_{x}\in AC(\overline{V}_{x})
. By compactness we can cover

with a finite collection of these intervals

. It is then easy to show that if

satisfies the classical definition on each interval

then it must satisfy it on the whole interval
[0,1]
. Conversely, if

there must be some point

such that

is not absolutely continuous on the closure of any open interval containing

. The aim of this section is to show that this property extends to the new definition of absolute continuity given in this paper.

We shall say that a set

(with respect to

) if there exists an open set

containing

such that

.

Definition 5.1
.

Let

\Omega
.

\mathfrak{U}
is

;

for every

and every

\displaystyle f_{\xi}(z)=\begin{cases}\frac{f(z)}{\xi-z}&\text{ if $z\neq\xi$},\\

{AC}(\sigma)
is normal since
C^{\infty}(\sigma)\subseteq{AC}(\sigma)
is normal, and we have already seen that
{AC}(\sigma)
is inverse closed, so all that remains is to show that
{AC}(\sigma)
is topologically admissible. So suppose that
f\in{AC}(\sigma)
and
\xi\in\mathbb{C}\backslash\supp(f)
. Then there is an open set
U\supseteq\supp(f)
disjoint from some neighbourhood of
\xi
and a
g\in C^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{2})
such that
g|_{\supp(f)}=1
and
g|_{U^{c}}=0
. Then
g_{\xi}
defined by

\displaystyle g_{\xi}(z)=\begin{cases}0&\qquad\text{ if }z\in U^{c},\\
\frac{g(z)}{\xi-z}&\qquad\text{ if }z\in U\end{cases}

is in
C^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{2})
and
fg_{\xi}=f_{\xi}
on
\sigma
. Thus
f_{\xi}\in{AC}(\sigma)
and
{AC}(\sigma)
is admissible.

As
{AC}(\sigma)
is complete and the norm topology on
{AC}(\sigma)
is stronger than the topology of pointwise convergence, any Cauchy sequence that converges pointwise to 0 must converge to 0 in
\|\cdot\|_{BV(\sigma)}
. So it remains to check that
\xi\mapsto f_{\xi}
is continuous on
\mathbb{C}\backslash\supp(f)
for all
f\in{AC}(\sigma)
. Of course this is true when
f=0
, so we assume that
f\neq 0
from now.

We will denote by
\zeta
the identity function on
\sigma
, by
M_{\sigma}
the maximum of
1
and
\|\zeta\|_{BV(\sigma)}
, and by
s
the square function
s(z)=z^{2}
. Let
\varepsilon>0
and
\xi\in\mathbb{C}\backslash\supp(f)
. For
w\in\mathbb{C}
, define
d_{w}=\inf\limits_{z\in\supp(f)}|w-z|
and let
\eta\in\mathbb{C}\backslash\supp(f)
satisfy

\displaystyle|\xi-\eta|<\min\left\{1,\frac{d_{\xi}}{2},\frac{d_{\xi}^{2}\varepsilon}{4\|f\|_{BV(\sigma)}},\frac{d_{\xi}^{4}\varepsilon}{8\left\lVert f\right\rVert_{BV(\sigma)}\big((|\xi|^{2}+3|\xi|+1)M_{\sigma}+\|s\|_{BV(\sigma)}\big)}\right\}.

Noting that
2d_{\eta}\geq d_{\xi}
(as
2|\xi-\eta|<d_{\xi}
), we have that

\displaystyle\|f_{\xi}-f_{\eta}\|_{\infty}\leq\|f\|_{\infty}\sup_{z\in\supp(f)}\left|\frac{\xi-\eta}{(\xi-z)(\eta-z)}\right|\leq\frac{2\|f\|_{\infty}|\xi-\eta|}{d_{\xi}^{2}}.

(5.1)

Moreover, suppose
S=[z_{j}]_{j=0}^{n}
is a finite sequence in
\sigma
and define

\displaystyle J_{1}

\displaystyle=\{j\,:\,z_{j}\in\supp(f),z_{j-1}\notin\supp(f)\}

\displaystyle J_{2}

\displaystyle=\{j\,:\,z_{j-1}\in\supp(f),z_{j}\notin\supp(f)\}

\displaystyle J_{3}

\displaystyle=\{1,2,\dots,n\}\backslash(J_{1}\cup J_{2}).

Then we have that

\displaystyle\frac{\cvar(f_{\xi}-f_{\eta},S)}{\vf(S)}

\displaystyle=\,

\displaystyle\frac{1}{\vf(S)}\sum_{j\in J_{1}}\left|\frac{f(z_{j})(\xi-\eta)}{(\xi-z_{j})(\eta-z_{j})}\right|+\frac{1}{\vf(S)}\sum_{j\in J_{2}}\left|\frac{f(z_{j-1})(\xi-\eta)}{(\xi-z_{j-1})(\eta-z_{j-1})}\right|

\displaystyle\hskip 14.22636pt+\frac{1}{\vf(S)}\sum_{j\in J_{3}}\left|f(z_{j})\left[\frac{\xi-\eta}{(\xi-z_{j})(\eta-z_{j})}\right]-f(z_{j-1})\left[\frac{\xi-\eta}{(\xi-z_{j-1})(\eta-z_{j-1})}\right]\right|

\displaystyle\,=

\displaystyle\frac{|\xi-\eta|}{\vf(S)}\sum_{j\in J_{1}}\left|\frac{f(z_{j})-f(z_{j-1})}{(\xi-z_{j})(\eta-z_{j})}\right|+\frac{|\xi-\eta|}{\vf(S)}\sum_{j\in J_{2}}\left|\frac{f(z_{j})-f(z_{j-1})}{(\xi-z_{j-1})(\eta-z_{j-1})}\right|

\displaystyle\hskip 14.22636pt+\frac{|\xi-\eta|}{\vf(S)}\sum_{j\in J_{3}}\left|\frac{f(z_{j})(\xi-z_{j-1})(\eta-z_{j-1})-f(z_{j-1})(\xi-z_{j})(\eta-z_{j})}{(\xi-z_{j})(\eta-z_{j})(\xi-z_{j-1})(\eta-z_{j-1})}\right|

\displaystyle\,\leq

\displaystyle\frac{2|\xi-\eta|\myvar(f,\sigma)}{d_{\xi}^{2}}+\frac{4|\xi-\eta|}{d_{\xi}^{4}\vf(S)}\sum_{j\in J_{3}}\Big|\xi\eta\big[f(z_{j})-f(z_{j-1})\big]

\displaystyle\hskip 14.22636pt-(\xi+\eta)\big[z_{j-1}f(z_{j})-z_{j}f(z_{j-1})\big]+z_{j-1}^{2}f(z_{j})-z_{j}^{2}f(z_{j-1})\Big|

\displaystyle\leq\,

\displaystyle\frac{4|\xi-\eta|}{d_{\xi}^{4}}\Big(|\xi\eta|\myvar(f,\sigma)+|\xi+\eta|\big(\|\zeta\|_{{BV}(\sigma)}\|f\|_{{BV}(\sigma)}\big)+\|s\|_{{BV}(\sigma)}\|f\|_{{BV}(\sigma)}\Big)

\displaystyle\hskip 14.22636pt+\frac{2|\xi-\eta|\myvar(f,\sigma)}{d_{\xi}^{2}}.

Recalling that
|\xi-\eta|<1
, we then have that

\displaystyle\frac{\cvar(f_{\xi}-f_{\eta},S)}{\vf(S)}<\,\frac{4|\xi-\eta|}{d_{\xi}^{4}}\left\lVert f\right\rVert_{BV(\sigma)}\left(|\xi|^{2}+|\xi|+\left(2|\xi|+1\right)\|\zeta\|_{BV(\sigma)}+\|s\|_{BV(\sigma)}\right)

\displaystyle\hskip 99.58464pt+\frac{2|\xi-\eta|\myvar(f,\sigma)}{d_{\xi}^{2}}

\displaystyle\qquad\leq\frac{4|\xi-\eta|}{d_{\xi}^{4}}\left\lVert f\right\rVert_{BV(\sigma)}\big((|\xi|^{2}+3|\xi|+1)M_{\sigma}+\|s\|_{BV(\sigma)}\big)+\frac{2|\xi-\eta|\myvar(f,\sigma)}{d_{\xi}^{2}}

\displaystyle\qquad<\frac{\varepsilon}{2}+\frac{2|\xi-\eta|\myvar(f,\sigma)}{d_{\xi}^{2}}.

Taking the supremum over all
S
then yields

\displaystyle\myvar(f_{\xi}-f_{\eta},\sigma)\leq\frac{\varepsilon}{2}+\frac{2|\xi-\eta|\myvar(f,\sigma)}{d_{\xi}^{2}}.

Combining this with (
5.1
) we have that

\displaystyle\|f_{\xi}-f_{\eta}\|_{BV(\sigma)}\leq\frac{\varepsilon}{2}+\frac{2|\xi-\eta|\left\lVert f\right\rVert_{{BV}(\sigma)}}{d_{\xi}^{2}}<\varepsilon.

So
\xi\mapsto f_{\xi}
is continuous on
\mathbb{C}\backslash\supp(f)
for all
f\in{AC}(\sigma)
and thus
{AC}(\sigma)
is topologically admissible.
