Infinitesimal behavior of Quadratically Regularized Optimal Transport and its relation with the Porous Medium Equation
Alejandro Garriz-Molina, Alberto González-Sanz, Gilles Mordant

Abstract
The quadratically regularized optimal transport problem has recently been considered in various applications where the coupling needs to be sparse , i.e., the density of the coupling needs to be zero for a large subset of the product of the supports of the marginals. However, unlike the acclaimed entropy-regularized optimal transport, the effect of quadratic regularization on the transport problem is not well understood from a mathematical standpoint. In this work, we take a first step towards its understanding. We prove that the difference between the cost of optimal transport and its regularized version multiplied by the ratio ε − 2 d + 2 \varepsilon^{-\frac{2}{d+2}} converges to a nontrivial limit as the regularization parameter ε \varepsilon tends to 0. The proof confirms a conjecture from Zhang et al. (2023) where it is claimed that a modification of the self-similar solution of the porous medium equation, the Barenblatt–Pattle solution, can be used as an approximate solution of the regularized transport cost for small values of ε \varepsilon .



1
Introduction and main result

Optimal transport has an extremely rich history that started with Monge more than two centuries ago
(
Monge, 1781
)
. The problem is, given two positive measures
\rho_{0}
and
\rho_{1}
with the same finite mass defined on
\Omega_{0}
and
\Omega_{1}
and a cost function from
\Omega_{0}\times\Omega_{1}
to
[0,\infty)
, to find the optimal way of transporting one measure onto the other at the minimal overall cost. That is, the optimal transport cost between two probability measures having densities
\rho_{0}
and
\rho_{1}
with respect to the
d
-dimensional Lebesgue measure
\ell_{d}
is defined as

\mathcal{W}_{2}^{2}(\rho_{0},\rho_{1})=\inf_{\pi\in\Pi(\rho_{0},\rho_{1})}\int\|\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{y}\|^{2}\mathrm{d}\pi(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y}),

(1)

where
\Pi(\rho_{0},\rho_{1})
denotes the set of probability measures
\pi\in\mathcal{P}(\mathbb{R}^{d}\times\mathbb{R}^{d})
such that

\int f(\mathbf{x})\mathrm{d}\pi(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y})=\int f(\mathbf{x})\mathrm{d}\rho_{0}(\mathbf{x})\quad\text{ and }\quad\int f(\mathbf{y})d\pi(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y})=\int f(\mathbf{y})\mathrm{d}\rho_{1}(\mathbf{y}),

for any bounded continuous function
f\in\mathcal{C}_{B}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
. In the case where
\rho_{1}
is absolutely continuous with respect to the Lebesgue measure
\ell_{d}
, the solution
\tilde{\pi}
of (
1
) is unique and of the form
\tilde{\pi}=(\nabla g\times{\rm Id})_{\#}\rho_{1}
, where
g
is a convex function (see
Brenier (1991)
;
Cuesta and
Matran (1989)
). In such a case, it is said that
\nabla g
pushes
\rho_{1}
forward to
\rho_{0}
. We refer to the two monographs by
Villani (2003)
;
Villani (2008)
for a detailed exposition.

We highlight that, in a common abuse of notation, we will refer—when ambiguity is absent—to the densities
\rho_{0}
and
\rho_{1}
and to the measures defined by them often by just speaking about the measures
\rho_{0}
and
\rho_{1}
or
\mathrm{d}\rho_{0}
and
\mathrm{d}\rho_{1}
.

After a series of fundamental theoretical developments, among which those of Kantorovich,
Brenier (1991)
;
McCann (1995)
;
Cuesta and
Matran (1989)
and many more,
the interest for optimal transport exploded in recent years as its usefulness for various application domains such as Machine Learning
(
Courty
et al., 2018
;
De Lara et al., 2023
;
Gordaliza et al., 2019
)
, Statistics
(
Carlier
et al., 2016
;
Hallin et al., 2021
;
Mordant and
Segers, 2022
;
Hundrieser et al., 2023
;
del
Barrio et al., 2024
;
Chewi
et al., 2024
)
, Biology
(
Schiebinger
et al., 2019
;
Tameling et al., 2021
;
González-Delgado et al., 2023
)
, Astronomy
(
Levy
et al., 2021
)
or Economy
(
Galichon, 2016
)
got acknowledged. Note that it became impossible to list all the contributions to the field.

Moving away from the classical framework, a recent line of research has been inspired by new computational developments available for a particular relaxation of the optimal transport problem. The latter relaxation consists in adding a (convex) penalty to the initial problem given in equation (
1
) above. A particular such penalty, spearheaded by
Cuturi (2013)
for its easy computability on GPU, relies on the addition of a Kullback–Leibler regularization term
in the initial objective function, i.e.,

\mathcal{T}_{2,\varepsilon,KL}(\rho_{0},\rho_{1})=\inf_{\pi\in\Pi(\rho_{0},\rho_{1})}\int\|\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{y}\|^{2}d\pi(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y})+\varepsilon\,{\rm KL}\big(\pi|(\rho_{0}\otimes\rho_{1})\big),

where
(\rho_{0}\otimes\rho_{1})
denotes the product measure and
{\rm KL}(\alpha|\beta)=\int\log\left(\mathrm{d}\alpha/\mathrm{d}\beta\right)\mathrm{d}\alpha
if
\alpha
is absolutely continuous w.r.t.
\beta
(i.e.,
\alpha\ll\beta
) and
{\rm KL}(\alpha|\beta)=+\infty
otherwise. Here
{\mathrm{d}\alpha/\mathrm{d}\beta}
stands for the Radon–Nikodym derivative of
\alpha
w.r.t.
\beta
. Conversely, given a positive function
\omega
we denote as
\omega\mathrm{d}\beta
the measure having Radon–Nikodym derivative
\omega
w.r.t.
\beta
.
The addition of this penalty term enables the use of a matrix-scaling algorithm: the Sinkhorn algorithm
(
Sinkhorn, 1964
)
. A plethora of works and applications followed, see
Peyré and
Cuturi (2019)
for a book-long exposition.

Studying the infinitesimal behavior of the difference in the regularized cost as the regularization parameter
\varepsilon
tends to 0,
Pal (2019)
proved the limit

\lim_{\varepsilon\to 0^{+}}\frac{\mathcal{T}_{2,\varepsilon,KL}(\rho_{0},\rho_{1})-\mathcal{W}_{2}^{2}(\rho_{0},\rho_{1})-\frac{d}{2}\,\log(\pi\varepsilon)}{\varepsilon}=\frac{\int\log(\rho_{0})d\rho_{0}-\int\log(\rho_{1})d\rho_{1}}{2}

(2)

using a Gaussian approximation of the minimizers of the regularized problem. This is a natural approximation from a heuristic point of view, given that Gaussian distributions are the fundamental solutions of the heat equation, whose Wasserstein gradient flow is over the Kullback–Leibler entropy
(
Otto, 2001
)
.

Just like in the heat equation, whose solution has maximum support for any positive time, the solutions of the optimal transport problem regularized by logarithmic entropy share the same property. That is, for every
\varepsilon>0
, the resulting coupling is dense, i.e., each point on the product space
\Omega_{0}\times\Omega_{1}
has a positive density. On the contrary, the (unregularized) optimal transport plan for the squared Euclidean distance as a cost is concentrated on the gradient of a convex function. To remedy this,
Blondel
et al. (2018)
introduced the Quadratically Regularized Optimal Transport (QOT)

\mathcal{T}_{2,\varepsilon,(\cdot)^{2}}(\rho_{0},\rho_{1})=\inf_{\pi\in\Pi(\rho_{0},\rho_{1})}\int\|\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{y}\|^{2}\mathrm{d}\pi(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y})+\varepsilon\left\|\frac{\mathrm{d}\pi}{\mathrm{d}(\rho_{0}\otimes\rho_{1})}\right\|_{L^{2}(\rho_{0}\otimes\rho_{1})}^{2},

(3)

where

\left\|\frac{\mathrm{d}\pi}{\mathrm{d}(\rho_{0}\otimes\rho_{1})}\right\|_{L^{2}(\rho_{0}\otimes\rho_{1})}^{2}=\int\left(\frac{\mathrm{d}\pi}{\mathrm{d}(\rho_{0}\otimes\rho_{1})}\right)^{2}\mathrm{d}(\rho_{0}\otimes\rho_{1})

in the case where
{\mathrm{d}\pi}\ll{\mathrm{d}(\rho_{0}\otimes\rho_{1})}
, and
+\infty
otherwise.
Applications of QOT have since then started blossoming. Examples include
Essid and
Solomon (2018)
;
Zhang et al. (2023)
;
Van Assel et al. (2023)
;
Mordant (2023)
. For some theoretical results we refer to
Nutz (2024)
.
Blondel
et al. (2018)
observed that the minimizer of (
3
) is
“sparse”
in the sense that its topological support seems to increase progressively as
\varepsilon
gets larger.
This increase of a sparse support suggests a diffusion behavior like that of the Porous Medium Equation (PME) where epsilon would play the role of the time parameter. For this reason, before setting this parallel on solid ground, we provide a short introduction to the PME in the next section.

1.1
On the Porous Medium Equation and the QOT

The Porous Medium Equation posed in the whole space reads, for an initial datum
u_{0}
,

\begin{cases}\partial_{t}u(t,\mathbf{x})=\Delta u(t,\mathbf{x})^{m},\quad&t>0,\ \mathbf{x}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}\\
u(0,\mathbf{x})=u_{0}(\mathbf{x})&x\in\mathbb{R}^{d},\end{cases}

where
m>1
is the non-linearity exponent. A mathematical study for this equation appeared first, in the case
m=2
, in the study of groundwater infiltration by
Boussinesq (1903)
and since then it has attracted the attention of numerous experts. The general case
m>1
can be derived from the study of an an-isotropic gas in a porous medium (hence the name) subject to Darcy’s Law, see the seminal (and independent of each other) works of
Leibenzon (1930)
and
Muskat (1937)
. Since then, it has enjoyed a continuous attention from the mathematical community due to its applications in many fields, like plasma radiation, biology, population theory or even cosmology. We refer the interested reader to the book of
Vázquez (2007)
, broadly considered as the go-to reference on this matter. Therein, the reader can find a more detailed explanation of the main characteristics of the PME that are relevant for the study of the QOT. Even though in this article we focus on the case
m=2
, we will discuss the general case
m>1
.

The first property that leaps to the eye is the fact that the PME is non-linear, i.e., the sum of two solutions of the equation is not a solution itself. This makes it impossible for the existence of a linear description of its solutions similar to the heat equation, where the solution can be expressed as the convolution of the initial datum
u_{0}
and the fundamental solution, the self-similar Gaussian profile (also referred as the kernel of the equation). The solution of the PME cannot be expressed as the convolution of the initial datum with any kernel but, nevertheless, there exists a fundamental solution
\mathcal{B}(t,x)
of the PME, in the sense that

\begin{cases}\partial_{t}\mathcal{B}(t,\mathbf{x})=\Delta\mathcal{B}(t,\mathbf{x})^{m},\quad&t>0,\ x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}\\
\mathcal{B}(0,\mathbf{x})=\delta_{0}(\mathbf{x})&\mathbf{x}\in\mathbb{R}^{d},\end{cases}

where
\delta_{0}(\mathbf{x})
is Dirac’s Delta function located at
\mathbf{x}=0
and the previous equalities should be understood in the weak sense (or integral sense). This fundamental solution is called Barenblatt or Barenblatt–Kompaneets–Zeldovich profile due to its discoverers
Barenblatt (1952)
and
Zeldovich and
Kompaneets (1950)
. It reads

\mathcal{B}(t,\mathbf{x})=\frac{1}{t^{\alpha}}\left[C-\beta\frac{m-1}{2m}\frac{\|\mathbf{x}\|^{2}}{t^{2\beta}}\right]_{+}^{\frac{1}{m-1}},\quad\text{where}\quad\alpha=\frac{d}{d(m-1)+2},\ \beta=\frac{1}{d(m-1)+2},

(4)

and
C>0
is a free constant that dictates the mass of the profile.
Contrarily to the Kullback–Leibler regularisation case, we cannot work with linear tools, i.e., semigroup theory. Still, the profile of the solution above will play a key role in our work, see formula (
9
) to mention but one example.

The next property that is relevant in our study is the
slow
nature of the diffusion in the PME. If one writes the equation in terms of its diffusivity
D(u)
then we see that

\partial_{t}u={\rm div}\left(D(u)\cdot\nabla u\right),\quad\text{with}\quad D(u)=mu^{m-1}

and therefore the diffusivity disappears whenever
u=0
; in other words, our equation is degenerate parabolic at the points where
u=0
. This provokes the phenomena known as
slow diffusion
, where every solution whose initial datum has compact support will maintain a compact support that grows over time, in sharp contrast with the heat equation, where every solution becomes positive everywhere at any positive time. This characteristic of the PME relates closely to the
sparse
nature of the minimizer in the QOT observed by
Blondel
et al. (2018)
.

Finally, there is one last connection between the PME and the QOT that we would like to mention.
Otto (2001)
explained how that the PME could be understood as a gradient flow with the 2-Wasserstein metric of the functional formed by the second moment of the solution and its free energy, namely

E(u)=\int\frac{1}{2}\|\mathbf{x}\|^{2}u(\mathbf{x})+\frac{1}{m-1}u(\mathbf{x})^{m}\ {\rm d}\ell_{d}(\mathbf{x}),

(5)

see also
Zhang et al. (2023)
for some applications. More or less at the same time
Carrillo and
Toscani (2000)
followed this approach, using
E(u)
as the natural entropy of the system, to describe the large-time behavior of the solutions of the problem. Note that the resemblance between (
3
) and (
5
) in the case
m=2
. Quadratically regularized optimal transport thus mimics the minimisation of the free energy just defined under the coupling constraints. As we shall see later, this last observation is where the geometry of unregularized optimal transport comes into the picture and plays a crucial role.

1.2
Assumptions and main results

The existence of a potential link between QOT and the porous medium equation was considered folklore in the community in the last years even though never rigorously established. The first developments clearly fleshing this out were proposed by
Zhang et al. (2023)
. Their work further suggests that, by using Barenblatt profile approximations instead of Gaussian approximations (for the case of the Kullback–Leibler regularisation studied by
Pal (2019)
), one could characterize the non-trivial limit of

r(\varepsilon)\big(\mathcal{T}_{2,\varepsilon,(\cdot)^{2}}(\rho_{0},\rho_{1})-\mathcal{W}_{2}^{2}(\rho_{0},\rho_{1})\big)

for a proper choice of
r(\varepsilon)
. Via a proof based on quantization,
Eckstein and
Nutz (2023)
determined the value of the ratio
r(\varepsilon)
as
r(\varepsilon)=\varepsilon^{-\frac{2}{d+2}}
. It is worth highlighting, however, that the nonlinear nature of the penalty in (
3
) makes impossible to utilize the linear tools present in
Pal (2019)
or most of the techniques present in the existing literature. The method we propose here is novel and relies mostly on the understanding of Barenblatt-like densities, inspired by (
4
), and on the clever usage of mass transportation theory.

In this work, we prove the complete (implicit) conjecture of
Zhang et al. (2023)
with this ratio under the following assumptions.

Assumption 1
.

We assume the following:

1.

\Omega_{1}
and
\Omega_{0}
be open bounded sets such that

and
\sigma>0
such that for every

and angle

The functions

\rho_{1}\in\mathcal{C}^{\beta}(\bar{\Omega_{1}})

1=\int_{\Omega_{i}}\rho_{i}(\mathbf{x})\mathrm{d}\ell_{d}(\mathbf{x})=\int d\rho_{i}(\mathbf{x})=\rho_{i}(\Omega),\quad i=1,2.

3.

Such densities are upper lower bounded in their domain, i.e.,

\lambda\leq\rho_{0}(\mathbf{x})\leq\Lambda\ {\rm and}\ \lambda\leq\rho_{1}(\mathbf{y})\leq\Lambda\quad\text{for all}\ \mathbf{x}\in\Omega_{0}\ {\rm and}\ \mathbf{y}\in\Omega_{1}

(6)

Note that Assumption

The following is our main result.

Theorem 1.1
.

Let Assumption

\lim_{\varepsilon\to 0^{+}}\frac{\mathcal{T}_{2,\varepsilon,(\cdot)^{2}}(\rho_{0},\rho_{1})-\mathcal{W}_{2}^{2}(\rho_{0},\rho_{1})}{\varepsilon^{\frac{2}{d+2}}}={\frac{d^{\frac{d+4}{d+2}}(d+2)^{\frac{2}{d+2}}}{\left(\mathcal{H}^{d-1}(\mathcal{S}^{d-1})\right)^{\frac{2}{d+2}}}}\int_{\Omega_{0}}\big(\rho_{0}(\mathbf{x})\rho_{1}[\nabla g^{*}(\mathbf{x})]\big)^{-\frac{1}{(d+2)}}\mathrm{d}\rho_{0}(\mathbf{x}),

As a consequence of this result, we can write explicitly the relationship between solutions of the PME and the optimizers of the QOT. Define the energy

E_{\varepsilon}(\pi):=\int\frac{1}{2}\|\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{y}\|^{2}\pi(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y})+{\varepsilon}\cdot\pi^{2}(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y})\mathrm{d}(\rho_{0}\otimes\rho_{1})(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y})

and let
g
be the unique function such that
\nabla g
pushes
\rho_{1}
forward to
\rho_{0}
. Define then, for any
\mathbf{x}^{\prime}\in\Omega_{0}

\varrho(\mathbf{x}^{\prime}):=\rho_{0}\left(\left[\nabla^{2}g^{*}(\mathbf{x}^{\prime})\right]^{-\frac{1}{2}}\mathbf{x}^{\prime}\right)\cdot\rho_{1}\left(\nabla g^{*}\left(\left[\nabla^{2}g^{*}(\mathbf{x}^{\prime})\right]^{-\frac{1}{2}}\mathbf{x}^{\prime}\right)\right).

Choose now any
\mathbf{x}^{\prime}\in\Omega_{0}
and let
u(t,\mathbf{x};\mathbf{x}^{\prime})
be the solution of

\begin{cases}\partial_{t}u(t,\mathbf{x};\mathbf{x}^{\prime})=\frac{1}{2(d+2)}\Delta_{\mathbf{x}}u(t,\mathbf{x};\mathbf{x}^{\prime})^{2},\quad&t>0,\ \mathbf{x}\in\Omega_{0}\\[10.0pt]
u(0,\mathbf{x};\mathbf{x}^{\prime})=\varrho(\mathbf{x}^{\prime})^{-\frac{1}{d+2}}\cdot\delta_{\mathbf{x}^{\prime}}(\mathbf{x})&\mathbf{x}\in\mathbb{R}^{d},\end{cases}

where
\Delta_{\mathbf{x}}
represents the Laplacian only in the
\mathbf{x}

\delta_{\mathbf{x}^{\prime}}(\mathbf{x})
denotes the Dirac’s Delta function on the point
\mathbf{x}=\mathbf{x}^{\prime}
and the previous solution must be understood in the weak sense. The constant
1/2(d+2)
in front of the Laplacian operator can be considered just as a scaling factor in the
\mathbf{x}
variable. The formula for
u
reads

u(t,\mathbf{x};\mathbf{x}^{\prime})=\frac{1}{t}\left(\frac{C_{d}\ t^{\frac{2}{d+2}}}{\varrho(\mathbf{x}^{\prime})^{\frac{1}{d+2}}}-\frac{1}{2}\|\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{x}^{\prime}\|^{2}\right)_{+}

where
C_{d}
is just a normalizing constant depending only on the dimension of the space that will be stated later.

Next, to each
\mathbf{x}^{\prime}\in\Omega_{0}
and each solution
u
of the previous PME it corresponds a transformed function

\displaystyle v(t,\mathbf{x};\mathbf{x}^{\prime})

\displaystyle:=u\left(t,\left[\nabla^{2}g^{*}(\mathbf{x}^{\prime})\right]^{\frac{1}{2}}\mathbf{x};\left[\nabla^{2}g^{*}(\mathbf{x}^{\prime})\right]^{\frac{1}{2}}\mathbf{x}^{\prime}\right)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{t}\left(\frac{C_{d}\ t^{\frac{2}{d+2}}}{(\rho_{0}(\mathbf{x}^{\prime})\rho_{1}(\nabla g^{*}(\mathbf{x}^{\prime})))^{\frac{1}{d+2}}}-\frac{1}{2}\|\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{x}^{\prime}\|^{2}_{\nabla^{2}g^{*}(\mathbf{x}^{\prime})}\right)_{+},

where

\|\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{x}^{\prime}\|^{2}_{\nabla^{2}g(\mathbf{x})}=\langle\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{x}^{\prime},\nabla^{2}g(\mathbf{x})(\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{x}^{\prime})\rangle,

We are ready now to state the corollary.

Corollary 1.2
.

Under the assumptions of Theorem
1.1
let
\pi_{(\varepsilon)}
be the minimizer of (
3
) for
\varepsilon>0
and. Then it holds

\lim_{\varepsilon\to 0^{+}}\varepsilon^{-\frac{2}{2+d}}|E_{\varepsilon}(\pi_{(\varepsilon)}(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y}))-E_{\varepsilon}(v(\varepsilon,\mathbf{x},\nabla g(\mathbf{y})))|=0

We sketch a proof of the corollary for the interested reader. The details are omitted, as they can be readily deduced by repeating the calculations provided in the proof of Theorem
1.1
. Later on we will define a family of approximations
\pi_{\varepsilon}
to the true minimizer
\pi_{(\varepsilon)}
. The key of these approximations is that, as
\varepsilon\to 0
,

\pi_{\varepsilon}(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y})\sim v(\varepsilon,\mathbf{x};\nabla g(\mathbf{y})).

(7)

The idea consists then in writing, adding and subtracting terms,

\displaystyle\varepsilon^{-\frac{2}{2+d}}\left[E_{\varepsilon}(\pi_{(\varepsilon)}(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y}))-E_{\varepsilon}(v(\varepsilon,\mathbf{x},\nabla g(\mathbf{y})))\right]

\displaystyle=\underbrace{\varepsilon^{-\frac{2}{2+d}}\left[E_{\varepsilon}(\pi_{(\varepsilon)}(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y}))-\mathcal{W}^{2}_{2}(\rho_{0},\rho_{1})\right]}_{\mathcal{I}}

\displaystyle+\underbrace{\varepsilon^{-\frac{2}{2+d}}\left[\mathcal{W}^{2}_{2}(\rho_{0},\rho_{1})-E_{\varepsilon}(\pi_{\varepsilon}(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y}))\right]}_{\mathcal{II}}

\displaystyle+\underbrace{\varepsilon^{-\frac{2}{2+d}}\left[E_{\varepsilon}(\pi_{\varepsilon}(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y}))-E_{\varepsilon}(v(\varepsilon,\mathbf{x},\nabla g(\mathbf{y})))\right]}_{\mathcal{III}}.

Then thanks to Theorem

and the fact that
\pi_{\varepsilon}

Apart from obtaining the limit conjectured by
Zhang et al. (2023)
, the main interest of this article consists on providing a constructive approach to the study of such limits for more general penalties. Our technique should work with very little changes for penalties of the form
\left\|\cdot\right\|_{L^{m}(\rho_{0}\otimes\rho_{1})}^{m}
for
m>1
, which corresponds to the full range of parameters of the Porous Medium Equation. We leave this generalization for further work and we highlight that we do not know if this technique could work for non-convex penalties of the form
\left\|\cdot\right\|_{L^{m}(\rho_{0}\otimes\rho_{1})}^{m}
for
0<m<1
. This approach differs greatly from the one taken by
Pal (2019)
, which relies deeply on the linearity of the heat equation. In addition, our analysis further elucidates why the correct rate of convergence in Theorem
1.1
is
\varepsilon^{-\frac{2}{d+2}}
.

To prove Theorem
1.1
, we need to provide both an upper bound and a lower bound for
\mathcal{T}_{2,\varepsilon,(\cdot)^{2}}(\rho_{0},\rho_{1})
. To provide the lower bound, we will use the dual formulation (see
Nutz (2024)

\mathcal{T}_{2,\varepsilon,(\cdot)^{2}}(\rho_{0},\rho_{1})={2\cdot}\sup_{a,b\in L^{2}(\rho_{0})\times L^{2}(\rho_{1})}\int\bigg\{a(\mathbf{x})+b(\mathbf{y})\\
-\frac{1}{{2\varepsilon}}\left(a(\mathbf{x})+b(\mathbf{y})-\frac{1}{{2}}\|\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{y}\|^{2}\right)_{+}^{2}\bigg\}\mathrm{d}\rho_{0}(\mathbf{x})\mathrm{d}\rho_{1}(\mathbf{y})

(8)

of (
3
), whose solutions are the so-called regularized potentials. The solutions of the primal and dual problems are related in the following way;
(a_{\varepsilon},b_{\varepsilon})
is a maximizer of (
8

\frac{1}{\varepsilon}\left(a_{\varepsilon}(\mathbf{x})+b_{\varepsilon}(\mathbf{y})-\frac{1}{{2}}\|\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{y}\|^{2}\right)_{+}\mathrm{d}\rho_{0}(\mathbf{x})\mathrm{d}\rho_{1}(\mathbf{y})

(9)

is a minimizer of (
3
). The reader should note the resemblance between (
9

4

m=2
, with

and

. This relation suggests as a candidate a pair of functions

such that

\displaystyle\int\left(\tilde{f}_{\varepsilon}(\mathbf{x})+\frac{1}{2}\|\mathbf{y}\|^{2}-g(\mathbf{y})-\frac{1}{{2}}\|\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{y}\|^{2}\right)_{+}d\rho_{1}(\mathbf{y})=\varepsilon\quad\quad\text{for all }\mathbf{x}\in\Omega_{0}.

After some calculations,

2.2

\tilde{f}_{\varepsilon}(\mathbf{x})\asymp\frac{1}{2}\|\mathbf{x}\|^{2}-g^{*}(\mathbf{x})+C_{\varepsilon}(\mathbf{x}),

where

C_{\varepsilon}(\mathbf{x}):=\frac{\varepsilon^{\frac{2}{d+2}}}{C_{d}^{\frac{2}{d+2}}\big(\rho_{0}(\mathbf{x})\rho_{1}[\nabla g^{*}(\mathbf{x})]\big)^{\frac{1}{d+2}}}\quad{\rm for}\quad C_{d}:=2^{\frac{d+2}{2}}\mathcal{H}^{d-1}(\mathcal{S}^{d-1})\frac{1}{d(d+2)}.

Hence, we will find the lower bound by finding the limit as
\varepsilon\to 0
of

{2\cdot}{\varepsilon^{-\frac{2}{d+2}}}\left({\Gamma_{\varepsilon}\left(f_{\varepsilon},\frac{1}{2}\|\cdot\|^{2}-g\right)-{\frac{1}{2}}\mathcal{W}_{2}^{2}(\rho_{0},\rho_{1})}\right)

(10)

where

{f}_{\varepsilon}(\mathbf{x})=\frac{1}{2}\|\mathbf{x}\|^{2}-g^{*}(\mathbf{x})+{C_{\varepsilon}(\mathbf{x})}
and

\Gamma_{\varepsilon}(a,b)=\int\bigg\{a(\mathbf{x})+b(\mathbf{y})-\frac{1}{2\varepsilon}\left(a(\mathbf{x})+b(\mathbf{y})-\frac{1}{2}\|\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{y}\|^{2}\right)_{+}^{2}\bigg\}\mathrm{d}\rho_{0}(\mathbf{x})\mathrm{d}\rho_{1}(\mathbf{y}).

For the upper bound, we will find a coupling
\pi_{\varepsilon}\in\Pi(\rho_{0},\rho_{1})
such that the functional

\mathbb{H}_{\varepsilon}(\pi)=\int\|\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{y}\|^{2}d\pi(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y})+{\varepsilon}\left\|\frac{d\pi}{d(\rho_{0}\otimes\rho_{1})}\right\|_{L^{2}(\rho_{0}\otimes\rho_{1})}^{2},

at
\pi_{\varepsilon}
achieves the same limit. The natural candidate is the measure
\tilde{\pi}
with
\frac{\mathrm{d}\tilde{\pi}}{\mathrm{d}(\rho_{0}\otimes\rho_{1})}(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y})=\frac{1}{\varepsilon}(C_{\varepsilon}(\mathbf{x})-D(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y}))_{+}
,
where

D(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y})=g^{*}(\mathbf{x})+g(\mathbf{y})-\langle\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y}\rangle

(11)

is the well-known Bregman divergence.
However,
\tilde{\pi}
is neither a coupling nor a probability measure in the product space.
Therefore, it must be adjusted using the following procedure: Firstly, transport
\rho_{0}
to
\rho_{1}
to work exclusively with the measure

\frac{1}{\varepsilon}(C_{\varepsilon}(\mathbf{x})-D(\mathbf{x},\nabla g^{*}(\mathbf{x}^{\prime})))_{+}\,\mathrm{d}\rho_{0}(\mathbf{x})\,\mathrm{d}\rho_{0}(\mathbf{x}^{\prime}).

Since
D(\mathbf{x},\nabla g(\mathbf{x}^{\prime}))
is more difficult to control than its quadratic approximation (see Lemma
2.2
)

\frac{1}{2}\|\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{x}^{\prime}\|^{2}_{\nabla^{2}g(\mathbf{x})}=\frac{1}{2}\langle\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{x}^{\prime},\nabla^{2}g(\mathbf{x})(\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{x}^{\prime})\rangle,

we construct the symmetric version of its density

m_{\varepsilon}(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{x}^{\prime})=\frac{1}{2\varepsilon}\left(C_{\varepsilon}(\mathbf{x})+C_{\varepsilon}(\mathbf{x}^{\prime})-\frac{1}{2}\|\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{x}^{\prime}\|^{2}_{\nabla^{2}g(\mathbf{x})}-\frac{1}{2}\|\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{x}^{\prime}\|^{2}_{\nabla^{2}g(\mathbf{x}^{\prime})}\right)_{+}.

Again, we highlight the resemblance between this last definition and (
4
) in the case
m=2
, with
C_{\varepsilon}(\mathbf{x}),C_{\varepsilon}(\mathbf{y})\sim C\varepsilon^{\frac{2}{d+2}}
, also
\|\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{x}^{\prime}\|_{\nabla^{2}g(\mathbf{x})},\|\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{x}^{\prime}\|_{\nabla^{2}g(\mathbf{x}^{\prime})}\sim\|\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{x}^{\prime}\|
(see Lemma
2.2
later on) and finally
t=\varepsilon
.

Subsequently, we normalize to create the measure with density

\xi_{\varepsilon}(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{x}^{\prime})=\frac{m_{\varepsilon}(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{x}^{\prime})}{\int m_{\varepsilon}(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{x}^{\prime})\,\mathrm{d}(\rho_{0}\otimes\rho_{0})(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{x}^{\prime})}

with respect to
\rho_{0}\otimes\rho_{0}
.
Within the product space, the symmetry
\xi_{\varepsilon}
is important because it implies that both its marginals are the same and with density
\rho_{\varepsilon}(\mathbf{x}^{\prime})=\int{\xi}_{\varepsilon}(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{x}^{\prime})\mathrm{d}\rho_{0}(\mathbf{x})
with respect to
\rho_{0}
. Then, transport
\rho_{\varepsilon}\mathrm{d}\rho_{0}
to
\mathrm{d}\rho_{0}
and define the probability measure

\mathrm{d}\mu_{\varepsilon}(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y})=\frac{\xi_{\varepsilon}(\nabla\phi_{\varepsilon}^{*}(\mathbf{x}),\nabla\phi_{\varepsilon}^{*}(\mathbf{x}^{\prime}))}{\rho_{\varepsilon}(\nabla\phi_{\varepsilon}^{*}(\mathbf{x}))\rho_{\varepsilon}(\nabla\phi_{\varepsilon}^{*}(\mathbf{x}^{\prime}))}\mathrm{d}\rho_{0}(\mathbf{x})\,\mathrm{d}\rho_{0}(\mathbf{x}^{\prime}),

where
\nabla\phi_{\varepsilon}
is the optimal transport map between
\mathrm{d}\rho_{0}
and
\rho_{\varepsilon}\mathrm{d}\rho_{0}
. For the rates of this coupling to be appropriate, it is necessary that the Lipschitz constant of
\nabla\phi_{\varepsilon}
tends to 1 uniformly (see (
30
)). Using Caffarelli’s regularity theory (see
Caffarelli (1992)
;
Caffarelli (1990)
), one can obtain the norm
\|\phi_{\varepsilon}\|_{\mathcal{C}^{2,\alpha}(\Omega_{0}^{\delta})}
, where

\Omega_{0}^{\delta}=\{\mathbf{x}\in\Omega_{0}:\mathrm{dist}(\mathbf{x},\partial\Omega_{0})\geq\delta\}

and
\delta>0
, uniformly bounded in
\varepsilon
. In one dimension or in the flat torus, this holds for
\delta=0
, and the proof concludes at this step. In higher dimensions and with Euclidean cost, it is not entirely clear that this bound has been established in the literature—regularity up to the boundary is known for strongly convex and smooth domains
(
Urbas, 1997
;
Caffarelli, 1996
)
, but the dependence on the constants is not clear to us. The same issue appears in
Manole et al. (2021)
in a different context. Instead of attempting to prove this result, we will circumvent the issue by dividing the space
\Omega_{0}
into two parts: points that are less than
\delta
away from the boundary and those that are farther. For the latter, we can apply interior regularity, where the dependence on the constants is explicit. For points near the boundary, we will use a different coupling—the so-called
“frame coupling”
—that does not achieve the appropriate limit but does not cause the functional to diverge. After taking the limit as
\varepsilon
tends to 0, we will then take the limit as
\delta
tends to 0 and check that it matches the lower bound.

As Caffarelli’s results are only valid for convex domains, the previous discussion is applicable only to convex domains.
The final step of the proof is to remove that assumption. It involves creating a tessellation of the domain
\Omega_{0}
into disjoint squares
\{I_{i}\}_{i=1}^{m}
and constructing a frame coupling in
\Omega_{0}\setminus\{I_{i}\}_{i=1}^{m}
, applying the previous coupling to each of the squares. This piecewise-defined coupling is referred to as the
“stained glass"
coupling (see Figure
1
).

The rest of the paper is organized as follows. In Section
2
, we introduce the notation that will be utilized throughout the paper. In Section
2.1
, we present the necessary results from optimal transport theory that are essential for proving the main theorem. In Section
3
, we will provide the lower bound, and in Section
4
, we will establish the upper bound. As mentioned earlier, the upper bound will be proven first for convex supports (Section
4.1
) and then for arbitrary supports (Section
4.4
).

Notation and auxiliary results

In order to present the foundational concepts more seamlessly, we begin by defining a finite positive constant
C(a_{1},\dots,a_{d})
that depends solely on a set of parameters
(a_{1},\dots,a_{d})
. For the sake of brevity, we denote a constant that depends only on
\rho_{0}
,
\rho_{1}
,
\Omega_{0}
,
\Omega_{1}
and
g
by
C
, specifically
C=C(\rho_{0},\rho_{1},g)
. A function
v
of
\varepsilon
is said to be a little-o of another function
u
(denoted as
v=o(u)
) if
\frac{v(\varepsilon)}{u(\varepsilon)}\to 0
as
\varepsilon\to 0
. Occasionally, the function
v(\varepsilon,\mathbf{x})
may depend on
\mathbf{x}
within
\Omega_{0}
. In such cases, we use the notation
v=o(u)
if
\sup_{\mathbf{x}\in\Omega_{0}}\frac{v(\varepsilon,\mathbf{x})}{u(\varepsilon)}\to 0
as
\varepsilon
approaches zero. In the same manner, we will use the standard big-O notation.
The notation
x\asymp_{\kappa}y
is used to express an equivalence of order of convergence, considering multiplicative constants that depend solely on a parameter
\kappa>0
, particularly in contexts approaching the limit as
\varepsilon\to 0
. As before
\kappa=(\rho_{0},\rho_{1},\Omega_{0},\Omega_{1},g)
we just write
x\asymp y
.

Moreover, within a compact set
\Omega
, the space
\mathcal{C}^{p,\alpha}(\Omega)
is the space of Hölder continuous functions of order
p
with Hölder continuity parameter
\alpha
in the open interval
(0,1)
. The norm for this function space is defined as

\|f\|_{\mathcal{C}^{p,\alpha}(\Omega)}=\sum_{k=0}^{p}\sum_{|\alpha|=k}\sup_{x\in\Omega}\left|\frac{d^{k}}{dx_{a_{1}}\cdots dx_{a_{k}}}f(x)\right|+\sum_{|\alpha|=p}\sup_{\begin{subarray}{c}x,y\in\Omega\\
x\neq y\end{subarray}}\frac{|f(x)-f(y)|}{\|x-y\|^{\alpha}}.

Additionally, the notation
\ell_{d}
is employed to denote the Lebesgue measure, and
g^{*}
refers to the convex conjugate of
g
, calculated as
g^{*}(x)=\sup_{y\in\mathbb{R}^{d}}(\langle x,y\rangle-g(y))
. The Hausdorff measure of dimension
d-1
is represented by
\mathcal{H}^{d-1}
.

Furthermore, for a positive definite matrix
A
, its square root, denoted
A^{1/2}
, is the unique positive definite matrix
V
such that
V^{2}=A
. The product measure
\rho_{0}\otimes\rho_{1}
and the space
L^{2}(\mu)
, which includes functions
f
with a finite
L^{2}(\mu)
-norm given by

\|f\|_{L^{2}(\mu)}=\left(\int f^{2}(x)\mathrm{d}\mu(x)\right)^{1/2},

are also crucial. In instances where Borel measures
\mu
and
\nu
satisfy
\mu\ll\nu
, the notation
\frac{d\mu}{d\nu}
represents the Radon-Nikodym derivative of
\mu
with respect to
\nu
. For the particular cases of
\rho_{i}
, for
i=1,2
, to avoid complicating the notation, we will use the following abuses of notation:
\frac{d\rho_{i}}{d\ell_{d}}=\rho_{i}
and

\int f(\mathbf{x})d\rho_{i}(\mathbf{x})=\int_{\Omega_{i}}f(\mathbf{x})\rho_{i}(\mathbf{x})\mathrm{d}\ell_{d}(\mathbf{x}),\quad i=1,2.

For a Borel set
A
,
\chi_{A}
denotes the indicator function of
A
. Sometimes, when it can cause confusion, we write
\chi_{[A]}
. We say that a function
T
pushes a measure
\mu
forward to another measure
\nu
if
\nu(A)=\mu(T^{-1}(A))
for all Borel set
A
. In such a case, we write
T_{\#}\mu=\nu
. A set
A
is compactly contained in
B
(
A\subset\subset B
) if there exists a compact set
K
and an open set
\mathcal{U}
such that
A\subset K\subset\mathcal{U}\subset B
. The Euclidean Ball with center
\mathbf{x}
and radius
\delta>0
is denoted as
\mathbb{B}(\mathbf{x},\delta)
.

2.1
Optimal transport background

The optimal transport problem (
1
) admits the dual formulation

\mathcal{W}_{2}^{2}(\rho_{0},\rho_{1})={2\cdot}\sup_{(a,b)\in\Phi}\int a(\mathbf{x})\mathrm{d}\rho_{0}(\mathbf{x})+\int b(\mathbf{y})\mathrm{d}\rho_{1}(\mathbf{y}),

(12)

where

\Phi=\{(a,b)\in(\mathcal{C}(\mathbb{R}^{d}))^{2}:\ a(\mathbf{x})+b(\mathbf{y})\leq\|\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{y}\|^{2}/2,\ \forall\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}\}.

Under Assumption
1
, the solution of the primal problem (
1
) is given by
({\rm Id}\times\nabla g)_{\#}\rho_{1}
and the solution of (
12
) by the pair

\left(\frac{1}{2}\|\cdot\|^{2}-g^{*},\frac{1}{2}\|\cdot\|^{2}-g\right).

(13)

In general, the function
g
solves the Monge-Ampère equation

\det(\nabla^{2}g)=\frac{\rho_{1}}{\rho_{0}(\nabla g)}\quad{\rm in}\ \Omega_{1},\quad\nabla g(\Omega_{1})=\Omega_{0}

in the so-called Brenier sense (see
Figalli (2017)
). In our case, by assumption,
g
solves it in the strong sense. The Monge-Ampère equation is a degenerate elliptical nonlinear differential equation. The regularity of convex solutions has been studied by Caffarelli in
Caffarelli (1992)
;
Caffarelli (1990)
. Here, we highlight the result that we will use in this work.

Theorem 2.1

(Caffarelli)
.

Let
\Omega
be open and convex
q_{1},q_{2}\in\mathcal{C}^{0,\alpha}(\Omega)
be positive functions such that

\int_{\Omega}q_{1}\,d\ell_{d}=\int_{\Omega}q_{2}\,d\ell_{d}=1.

Then there exists a
unique (up to additive constant) convex solution
u
of the boundary value problem

\det(\nabla^{2}u)=\frac{q_{1}}{q_{2}(\nabla u)}\quad{\rm in}\ \Omega,\quad\nabla u(\Omega)=\Omega

in
\mathcal{C}^{2,\alpha}_{Loc}(\Omega)
.
Moreover, for each
K\subset\subset\Omega

\|u\|_{\mathcal{C}^{2,\alpha}(K)}\leq C\left(K,\inf_{\mathbf{x}\in\Omega}q_{1},\inf_{\mathbf{x}\in\Omega}q_{2},\|q_{1}\|_{\mathcal{C}^{0,\alpha}(K)},\|q_{2}\|_{\mathcal{C}^{0,\alpha}(K)}\right)

The assumption of regularity on
g
is key in the infinitesimal developments we are undertaking. Lemma
2.2
is fundamental as it relates
D(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y})
with its linearized inner product

\langle\mathbf{x},\mathbf{z}\rangle_{\nabla^{2}g^{*}(\mathbf{x})}=\langle\mathbf{x},\nabla^{2}g^{*}(\mathbf{x})\mathbf{z}\rangle.

We omit its proof as it is a mere second-order Taylor expansion of the Bregman divergence

D(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y})=g^{*}(\mathbf{x})+g(\mathbf{y})-\langle\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y}\rangle

of
g
.

Lemma 2.2
.

Let Assumption
1
hold. Then there exists
\sigma_{M}(g)\geq\sigma_{m}(g)>0
such that

\displaystyle\frac{\sigma_{m}(g)}{2}\|\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{x}^{\prime}\|^{2}

\displaystyle\leq D(\mathbf{x},\nabla g^{*}(\mathbf{x}^{\prime}))\leq\frac{\sigma_{M}(g)}{2}\|\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{x}^{\prime}\|^{2}.

(14)

Moreover, there exists
C>0
such that

\left|D(\mathbf{x},\nabla g^{*}(\mathbf{x}^{\prime}))-\frac{1}{2}\|\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{x}^{\prime}\|^{2}_{\nabla^{2}g^{*}(\mathbf{x})}\right|\leq C\|\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{x}^{\prime}\|^{2+\alpha},

(15)

where

\frac{1}{2}\|\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{x}^{\prime}\|^{2}_{\nabla^{2}g^{*}(\mathbf{x})}=\frac{1}{2}\langle\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{x}^{\prime},\nabla^{2}g^{*}(\mathbf{x})(\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{x}^{\prime})\rangle

and
\alpha
is as in Assumption
1
.

As a direct consequence of Lemma
2.2
we obtain the following.

Lemma 2.3
.

Let Assumption
1
hold. Then the following estimates hold:

1.

There exists a constant
C
and
\varepsilon_{0}>0
such that

\left\{(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{x}^{\prime})\in\Omega_{0}\times\Omega_{0}:\ C_{\varepsilon}(\mathbf{x})\geq D(\mathbf{x},\nabla g^{*}(\mathbf{x}^{\prime}))\right\}\subset\left\{(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{x}^{\prime})\in\mathbb{R}^{2d}:\ \|\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{x}^{\prime}\|\leq C\varepsilon^{\frac{1}{d+2}}\right\}

holds for every

\varepsilon\leq\varepsilon_{0}
.

2.

For
\varepsilon\leq\varepsilon_{0}
,

\sup_{\mathbf{x},\mathbf{x}^{\prime}\in\Omega_{0}}\bigg|\left(C_{\varepsilon}(\mathbf{x})-D(\mathbf{x},\nabla g^{*}(\mathbf{x}^{\prime}))\right)_{+}-\left(C_{\varepsilon}(\mathbf{x})-\frac{1}{2}\|\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{x}^{\prime}\|_{\nabla^{2}g^{*}(\mathbf{x})}^{2}\right)_{+}\bigg|=o(\varepsilon^{\frac{2}{d+2}}).

2.2
Properties of the integrals

The following results are well-known and easy to prove passing to spherical coordinates. We state them for further reference.

Proposition 2.4
.

For any
a\geq 0
, it holds that

\int\left(a-\frac{\|\mathbf{u}\|^{2}}{2}\right)_{+}\mathrm{d}\ell_{d}(\mathbf{u})=|a|^{\frac{d+2}{2}}C_{d}^{(1)},

where

C_{d}^{(1)}:=\frac{2^{\frac{d+2}{2}}}{d(d+2)}\mathcal{H}^{d-1}(\mathcal{S}^{d-1}),

and also

\int\left(a-\frac{\|\mathbf{u}\|^{2}}{2}\right)_{+}^{2}\mathrm{d}\ell_{d}(\mathbf{u})=|a|^{\frac{d+4}{2}}C_{d}^{(2)},

where

C_{d}^{(2)}:=\frac{2^{\frac{d+6}{2}}}{d(d+2)(d+4)}\mathcal{H}^{d-1}(\mathcal{S}^{d-1}).

3
Lower bound

The aim of this section is to show

\liminf_{\varepsilon\to 0^{+}}\frac{\mathcal{T}_{2,\varepsilon,(\cdot)^{2}}(\rho_{0},\rho_{1})-\mathcal{W}_{2}^{2}(\rho_{0},\rho_{1})}{\varepsilon^{\frac{2}{d+2}}}\geq{\frac{d^{\frac{d+4}{d+2}}(d+2)^{\frac{2}{d+2}}}{\left(\mathcal{H}^{d-1}(\mathcal{S}^{d-1})\right)^{\frac{2}{d+2}}}}\int\big(\rho_{0}(\mathbf{x})\rho_{1}[\nabla g^{*}(\mathbf{x})]\big)^{-\frac{1}{(d+2)}}\mathrm{d}\rho_{0}(\mathbf{x}).

A sufficient condition for this to occur is given by the following result —see equation (
10
) in the introduction, whose proof will be provided below.
One of the key aspects of this proof is that we divide the set
\Omega_{0}
into two, the points near its boundary or far away, namely

\Omega_{0}^{\delta}:=\{\mathbf{x}\in\Omega_{0}:\ {\rm dist}(\mathbf{x},\partial\Omega_{0})>\delta\}\quad\text{ and }\quad\Omega_{0}^{\delta,c}:=\{\mathbf{x}\in\Omega_{0}:\ {\rm dist}(\mathbf{x},\partial\Omega_{0})\leq\delta\}.

(16)

These sets will also play a fundamental role in the proofs of Section
4
, hence their importance. As the reader will see, being at a distance
\delta
from the boundary will provide many useful uniform estimates that are in the core of our analysis. Then, making
\delta\to 0
we will find that our results hold true.

Lemma 3.1
.

Let Assumption
1
hold. Set
C_{d}:=2^{\frac{d+2}{2}}\mathcal{H}^{d-1}(\mathcal{S}^{d-1})\frac{1}{d(d+2)}
. Then

\lim_{\varepsilon\to 0^{+}}\frac{\Gamma_{\varepsilon}(f_{\varepsilon},\frac{1}{2}\|\cdot\|^{2}-g)-\frac{1}{2}\mathcal{W}_{2}^{2}(\rho_{0},\rho_{1})}{\varepsilon^{\frac{2}{d+2}}}=\frac{d}{C_{d}^{\frac{2}{d+2}}}\int\big(\rho_{0}(\mathbf{x})\rho_{1}[\nabla g^{*}(\mathbf{x})]\big)^{-\frac{1}{(d+2)}}\mathrm{d}\rho_{0}(\mathbf{x}),

where

{f}_{\varepsilon}(\mathbf{x})=\frac{1}{2}\|\mathbf{x}\|^{2}-g^{*}(\mathbf{x})+C_{\varepsilon}(\mathbf{x})
for
C_{\varepsilon}(\mathbf{x})={\varepsilon^{\frac{2}{d+2}}}{C_{d}^{\frac{-2}{d+2}}\big(\rho_{0}(\mathbf{x})\rho_{1}[\nabla g^{*}(\mathbf{x})]\big)^{\frac{-1}{d+2}}}
.

Proof.

As

\Gamma_{\varepsilon}(f_{\varepsilon},\frac{1}{2}\|\cdot\|^{2}-g)=\iint\bigg\{\frac{1}{2}\|\mathbf{x}\|^{2}-g^{*}(\mathbf{x})+C_{\varepsilon}(\mathbf{x})+\frac{1}{2}\|\mathbf{y}\|^{2}-g^{*}(\mathbf{y})\\
-\frac{1}{2\varepsilon}\left(C_{\varepsilon}(\mathbf{x})-g(\mathbf{y})-g^{*}(\mathbf{x})+\frac{1}{2}\|\mathbf{x}\|^{2}+\frac{1}{2}\|\mathbf{y}\|^{2}-\frac{1}{2}\|\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{y}\|^{2}\right)_{+}^{2}\bigg\}\mathrm{d}\rho_{0}(\mathbf{x})\mathrm{d}\rho_{1}(\mathbf{y})

and, by (
13
),

\mathcal{W}_{2}^{2}(\rho_{0},\rho_{1})=\int\left(\frac{1}{2}\|\cdot\|^{2}-g^{*}\right)\mathrm{d}\rho_{0}+\int\left(\frac{1}{2}\|\cdot\|^{2}-g\right)\mathrm{d}\rho_{1},

the value of

L=\lim_{\varepsilon\to 0^{+}}\frac{\Gamma_{\varepsilon}(f_{\varepsilon},\frac{1}{2}\|\cdot\|^{2}-g)-\mathcal{W}_{2}^{2}(\rho_{0},\rho_{1})}{\varepsilon^{\frac{2}{d+2}}}

is given by the limit (multiplied by
\varepsilon^{-\frac{2}{d+2}}
) of

\displaystyle L_{\varepsilon}

\displaystyle=\int C_{\varepsilon}(\mathbf{x})\mathrm{d}\rho_{0}(\mathbf{x})-\frac{1}{2}\underbrace{\frac{1}{\varepsilon}\iint\left(C_{\varepsilon}(\mathbf{x})-D(\mathbf{x},\nabla g^{*}(\mathbf{x}^{\prime}))\right)_{+}^{2}\mathrm{d}\rho_{0}(\mathbf{x})\mathrm{d}\rho_{0}(\mathbf{x}^{\prime})}_{L^{\prime}_{\varepsilon}}.

Since

\varepsilon^{-\frac{2}{d+2}}\int C_{\varepsilon}(\mathbf{x})\mathrm{d}\rho_{0}(\mathbf{x})=\int\frac{1}{C_{d}^{\frac{2}{d+2}}\big(\rho_{0}(\mathbf{x})\rho_{1}[\nabla g^{*}(\mathbf{x})]\big)^{\frac{1}{d+2}}}d\rho_{0}(\mathbf{x}),

we only need to deal with the limit of
\varepsilon^{-\frac{2}{d+2}}L^{\prime}_{\varepsilon}
.

Set now
\delta>0
and define the sets
\Omega_{0}^{\delta}
and
\Omega_{0}^{\delta,c}
as in (
16
). Then, due to Lemma
2.3
, there exists
\varepsilon_{\delta}
such that for every
\varepsilon\leq\varepsilon_{\delta}
, the set
\Omega_{0}^{\delta,c}
contains the set

\mathbb{V}_{\varepsilon}=\{\mathbf{x}\in\Omega_{0}:\exists\,\mathbf{x}^{\prime}\in\partial\Omega_{0}:\ C_{\varepsilon}(\mathbf{x})\geq D(\mathbf{x},\nabla g^{*}(\mathbf{x}^{\prime}))\}.

Therefore, we split

\varepsilon^{-\frac{2}{d+2}}L^{\prime}_{\varepsilon}
into two terms;

\mathbb{T}_{1}=\varepsilon^{-\frac{d+4}{d+2}}\int_{\Omega_{0}^{\delta}}\int\left(C_{\varepsilon}(\mathbf{x})-D(\mathbf{x},\nabla g^{*}(\mathbf{x}^{\prime}))\right)_{+}^{2}\mathrm{d}\rho_{0}(\mathbf{x}^{\prime})\mathrm{d}\rho_{0}(\mathbf{x})

(17)

and

\mathbb{T}_{2}=\varepsilon^{-\frac{d+4}{d+2}}\int_{\Omega_{0}^{\delta,c}}\int\left(C_{\varepsilon}(\mathbf{x})-D(\mathbf{x},\nabla g^{*}(\mathbf{x}^{\prime}))\right)_{+}^{2}\mathrm{d}\rho_{0}(\mathbf{x}^{\prime})\mathrm{d}\rho_{0}(\mathbf{x}),

(18)

and estimate each one separately.

Estimate for (
17
).
Since
\rho_{0}
is uniformly continuous over
\Omega_{0}
and the support of the function
\left(C_{\varepsilon}(\mathbf{x})-D(\mathbf{x},\nabla g^{*}(\mathbf{x}^{\prime}))\right)_{+}
concentrates uniformly on the diagonal
\mathbf{x}=\mathbf{x}^{\prime}
(see Lemma
2.3
), it holds that

\displaystyle\mathbb{T}_{1}=\varepsilon^{-\frac{d+4}{d+2}}\int_{\Omega_{0}^{\delta}}\int_{\Omega_{0}}\left(C_{\varepsilon}(\mathbf{x})-D(\mathbf{x},\nabla g^{*}(\mathbf{x}^{\prime}))\right)_{+}^{2}\mathrm{d}\ell_{d}(\mathbf{x}^{\prime})(\rho_{0}(\mathbf{x})+o\left(1\right))\mathrm{d}\rho_{0}(\mathbf{x}^{\prime}).

Considering that
\Omega_{0}^{\delta}
and
\mathbb{V}_{\varepsilon}
are disjoint, it follows that the inner integral can be taken over
\mathbb{R}^{d}
, i.e.,

\displaystyle\mathbb{T}_{1}=\varepsilon^{-\frac{d+4}{d+2}}\int_{\Omega_{0}^{\delta}}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\left(C_{\varepsilon}(\mathbf{x})-D(\mathbf{x},\nabla g^{*}(\mathbf{x}^{\prime}))\right)_{+}^{2}\mathrm{d}\ell_{d}(\mathbf{x}^{\prime})(\rho_{0}(\mathbf{x})+o\left(1\right))\mathrm{d}\rho_{0}(\mathbf{x}^{\prime}).

Lemma
2.3
implies

\displaystyle\mathbb{T}_{1}\asymp\varepsilon^{-\frac{d+4}{d+2}}\int_{\Omega_{0}^{\delta}}\int_{\Omega_{0}}\left(C_{\varepsilon}(\mathbf{x})-\frac{1}{2}\langle\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{x}^{\prime},\nabla^{2}g^{*}(\mathbf{x})(\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{x}^{\prime})\rangle)\right)_{+}^{2}\mathrm{d}\ell_{d}(\mathbf{x}^{\prime}){\rho}_{0}(\mathbf{x})\mathrm{d}\rho_{0}(\mathbf{x}).

We focus on the inner integral

I(\mathbf{x})={\rho}_{0}^{2}(\mathbf{x})\int_{\Omega_{0}}\left(C_{\varepsilon}(\mathbf{x})-\frac{1}{2}\langle\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{x}^{\prime},\nabla^{2}g^{*}(\mathbf{x})(\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{x}^{\prime})\rangle)\right)_{+}^{2}\mathrm{d}\ell_{d}(\mathbf{x}^{\prime}).

First we change variables
\mathbf{u}=[\nabla^{2}g^{*}(\mathbf{x})]^{\frac{1}{2}}(\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{x}^{\prime}),
to get

I(\mathbf{x})=\frac{{\rho}_{0}^{2}(\mathbf{x})}{\det(\nabla^{2}g^{*}(\mathbf{x}))^{\frac{1}{2}}}\int\left(C_{\varepsilon}(\mathbf{x})-\frac{1}{2}\|\mathbf{u}\|^{2}\right)_{+}^{2}\mathrm{d}\ell_{d}(\mathbf{u}).

Using Proposition
2.4
, we find

\displaystyle I(\mathbf{x})

\displaystyle=\frac{\big(C_{\varepsilon}(\mathbf{x})\big)^{\frac{d+4}{2}}C_{d}^{(2)}{\rho}_{0}^{2}(\mathbf{x})}{\det(\nabla^{2}g^{*}(\mathbf{x}))^{\frac{1}{2}}}

\displaystyle=\frac{\varepsilon^{\frac{d+4}{d+2}}C_{d}^{(2)}{\rho}_{0}^{2}(\mathbf{x})}{C_{d}^{\frac{d+4}{d+2}}\big(\rho_{0}(\mathbf{x})\rho_{1}[\nabla g^{*}(\mathbf{x})]\big)^{\frac{d+4}{2(d+2)}}\det(\nabla^{2}g^{*}(\mathbf{x}))^{\frac{1}{2}}}

, we get

and the claim follows.

4.4
General case

The aim of this section is to prove Theorem
1.1
in the general case, namely, to eliminate the assumption of convex support. To this end, we will construct the aforementioned
“stained glass”
structure. Let

T_{\delta}=\{I_{1}\}_{i\in\mathbb{N}}
be a regular tessellation of squares
I_{i}
of same length
\beta>0
of
\mathbb{R}^{d}
. Set

\mathcal{G}(\beta)=\{i\in\mathbb{N}:\ I_{i}\subset\Omega_{0}\}

and note that it is finite due to the compactness of
\Omega_{0}
. It is clear that we can write
\Omega_{0}
as the union of

\Omega^{(\beta)}_{0}=\bigcup_{i\in\mathcal{G}(\beta)}I_{i}
and
\Omega^{(\beta,c)}_{0}=\Omega\setminus\bigcup_{i\in\mathcal{G}(\beta)}I_{i}
. We define the measure

\pi_{\beta,\varepsilon}=\sum_{i\in\mathcal{G}(\beta)}\pi_{i}^{\varepsilon}\chi_{\big(I_{i}\times\nabla g^{*}(I_{i})\big)}+\gamma_{\beta,\varepsilon}\chi_{\big(\Omega_{0}^{(\beta,c)}\times\Omega_{0}^{(\beta,c)}\big)},

where

\pi_{i}^{\varepsilon}\in\Pi(\chi_{I_{i}}\mathrm{d}\rho_{0},\chi_{[\nabla g^{*}(I_{i})]}\mathrm{d}\rho_{1})
is the true minimizer of

\frac{1}{2}\int\|\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{y}\|^{2}d\pi(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y})+\frac{\varepsilon}{2}\left\|\frac{d\pi}{d(\rho_{0}\otimes\rho_{1})}\right\|_{L^{2}(\chi_{I_{i}}\mathrm{d}\rho_{0},\chi_{[\nabla g^{*}(I_{i})]}\mathrm{d}\rho_{1})}^{2},

among all
\pi\in\Pi(\chi_{I_{i}}\mathrm{d}\rho_{0},\chi_{[\nabla g^{*}(I_{i})]}\mathrm{d}\rho_{1})
, and
\gamma_{\beta,\varepsilon}\in\Pi(\chi_{\Omega_{0}^{(\beta,c)}}\mathrm{d}\rho_{0},\chi_{\nabla^{2}g^{*}(\Omega_{0}^{(\beta,c)})}\mathrm{d}\rho_{1})
to be determined. By construction,

\pi_{\beta,\varepsilon}\in\Pi(\mathrm{d}\rho_{0},\mathrm{d}\rho_{1}).
Moreover, since
\nabla g
is the gradient of a convex function and pushes
\chi_{[\nabla g^{*}(I_{i})]}\mathrm{d}\rho_{1}
forward to
\chi_{[I_{i}]}\mathrm{d}\rho_{0}
, it holds that

\mathcal{W}_{2}^{2}(\rho_{0},\rho_{1})=\mathcal{W}_{2}^{2}\left(\chi_{\left[\Omega_{0}^{(\beta,c)}\right]}\mathrm{d}\rho_{0},\chi_{\left[\nabla g^{*}\left(\Omega_{0}^{(\beta,c)}\right)\right]}\mathrm{d}\rho_{1}\right)+\sum_{i\in\mathcal{G}(\beta)}\mathcal{W}_{2}^{2}\left(\chi_{[I_{i}]}\mathrm{d}\rho_{0},\chi_{[\nabla g^{*}(I_{i})]}\mathrm{d}\rho_{1}\right).

It also holds that

\mathbb{H}_{\varepsilon}(\pi_{\beta,\varepsilon})=\sum_{i\in\mathcal{G}(\beta)}\mathcal{T}_{2,\varepsilon,(\cdot)^{2}}\left(\chi_{[I_{i}]}\mathrm{d}\rho_{0},\chi_{[\nabla g^{*}(I_{i})]}\mathrm{d}\rho_{1}\right)+\\
\int\|\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{y}\|^{2}\mathrm{d}\gamma_{\beta,\varepsilon}(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y})+\varepsilon\cdot\left\|\frac{\mathrm{d}\gamma_{\beta,\varepsilon}}{\mathrm{d}(\rho_{0}\otimes\rho_{1})}\right\|_{L^{2}(\rho_{0}\otimes\rho_{1})}^{2}.

We define
\gamma_{\beta,\varepsilon}
following the same steps as
\pi_{\varepsilon}^{(2)}
in the previous section. Note that
\Omega_{0}^{(\beta,c)}
satisfies the assumptions of Lemma
4.1
. Denote

W_{\varepsilon}=\int\|\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{y}\|^{2}\,\mathrm{d}\gamma_{\beta,\varepsilon}(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y})+\varepsilon\cdot\left\|\frac{\mathrm{d}\gamma_{\beta,\varepsilon}}{\mathrm{d}(\rho_{0}\otimes\rho_{1})}\right\|_{L^{2}(\rho_{0}\otimes\rho_{1})}^{2},

and repeat the calculations yielding (
47
) and (
50
). As a consequence,

\limsup_{\varepsilon\to 0}\frac{W_{\varepsilon}-\mathcal{W}_{2}^{2}\left(\chi_{[\Omega_{0}^{(\beta,c)}]}\mathrm{d}\rho_{0},\chi_{[\nabla g^{*}(\Omega_{0}^{(\beta,c)})]}\mathrm{d}\rho_{1}\right)}{\varepsilon^{\frac{2}{d+2}}}\leq C\ell_{d}\left(\Omega_{0}^{(\beta,c)}\right).

Applying Lemma
4.10
for each
i\in\mathcal{G}(\beta)
yields

\displaystyle\limsup_{\varepsilon\to 0^{+}}\frac{\mathbb{H}_{\varepsilon}(\pi_{\beta,\varepsilon})-\mathcal{W}_{2}^{2}(\rho_{0},\rho_{1})}{\varepsilon^{\frac{2}{d+2}}}

\displaystyle\qquad\leq\frac{d^{\frac{d+4}{d+2}}(d+2)^{\frac{2}{d+2}}}{\left(\mathcal{H}^{d-1}(\mathcal{S}^{d-1})\right)^{\frac{2}{d+2}}}\sum_{i\in\mathcal{G}(\beta)}\int_{I_{i}}\left(\rho_{0}(\mathbf{x})\rho_{1}[\nabla g^{*}(\mathbf{x})])\right)^{-\frac{1}{d+2}}\mathrm{d}\rho_{0}(\mathbf{x})+C\ell_{d}\left(\Omega_{0}^{(\beta,c)}\right)

\displaystyle\qquad\leq\frac{d^{\frac{d+4}{d+2}}(d+2)^{\frac{2}{d+2}}}{\left(\mathcal{H}^{d-1}(\mathcal{S}^{d-1})\right)^{\frac{2}{d+2}}}\int_{\Omega\setminus\Omega_{0}^{(\beta,c)}}\left(\rho_{0}(\mathbf{x})\rho_{1}[\nabla g^{*}(\mathbf{x})])\right)^{-\frac{1}{d+2}}\mathrm{d}\rho_{0}(\mathbf{x})+C\ell_{d}\left(\Omega_{0}^{(\beta,c)}\right).

Theorem
1.1
follows by letting
