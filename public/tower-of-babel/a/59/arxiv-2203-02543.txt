Ridges, Neural Networks, and the Radon Transform
Michael Unser michael.unser@epfl.ch

Abstract
A ridge is a function that is characterized by a one-dimensional profile (activation) and a multidimensional direction vector. Ridges appear in the theory of neural networks as functional descriptors of the effect of a neuron, with the direction vector being encoded in the linear weights. In this paper, we investigate properties of the Radon transform in relation to ridges and to the characterization of neural networks. We introduce a broad category of hyper-spherical Banach subspaces (including the relevant subspace of measures) over which the back-projection operator is invertible. We also give conditions under which the back-projection operator is extendable to the full parent space with its null space being identifiable as a Banach complement. Starting from first principles, we then characterize the sampling functionals that are in the range of the filtered Radon transform. Next, we extend the definition of ridges for any distributional profile and determine their (filtered) Radon transform in full generality. Finally, we apply our formalism to clarify and simplify some of the results and proofs on the optimality of ReLU networks that have appeared in the literature.

Introduction

A ridge is a multidimensional function
{\boldsymbol{x}}\mapsto r({\boldsymbol{w}}^{\mathsf{T}}{\boldsymbol{x}})
from
\mathbb{R}^{d}\to\mathbb{R}
that is characterized by a 1D profile
r:\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R}
and a weight vector
{\boldsymbol{w}}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}\backslash\{{\boldsymbol{0}}\}

(
Pinkus 2015
)
. Ridges are ubiquitous in mathematics and engineering. Most significantly, the elementary unit (neuron) in a neural network is a function of the form
f_{k}({\boldsymbol{x}})=\sigma({\boldsymbol{w}}_{k}^{\mathsf{T}}{\boldsymbol{x}}-t_{k})
, which is a ridge with a shifted profile
r=\sigma(\cdot-t_{k})
, where
\sigma:\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R}
is the activation function and where
t_{k}\in\mathbb{R}
(bias) and
{\boldsymbol{w}}_{k}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
(linear weights) are the trainable parameters of the
k
th neuron
(
Bishop 2006
)
. Variants of the universal-approximation theorem ensure that any continuous function can be approximated as closely as desired by a weighted sum of ridges with a fixed activation under mild conditions on
\sigma

(
Cybenko 1989
;
Hornik et al. 1989
;
Barron 1993
)
.

Ridges are also intimately tied to the Radon transform
(
Logan and Shepp 1975
;
Madych 1990
)
under the condition that the weight vector
{\boldsymbol{w}}
has a unit norm, so that
{\boldsymbol{w}}\in\mathbb{S}^{d-1}
where
\mathbb{S}^{d-1}
is the unit sphere in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
whose generic elements will be denoted by
{\boldsymbol{\xi}}
. This connection is exploited in the ridgelet transform, which provides a wavelet-like representation of functions where the basis elements are ridges
(
Murata 1996
;
Rubin 1998
;
Candès 1999
;
Candès and Donoho 1999
;
Kostadinova et al. 2014
)
.
The expansion of a function in terms of ridgelets is a precusor to sparse signal approximation. There, the idea is to represent a function by a linear combination of a small number of atoms taken within a dictionary
(
Elad 2010
;
Foucart and Rauhut 2013
)
. This paradigm, which is the basis for compressed sensing
(
Donoho 2006
;
Candès and Romberg 2007
)
, has been adapted to shallow neural networks by considering a dictionary that consists of a continuum of neurons. Mathematically, this can be implemented through the integral representation
(infinite-width neural network)

\displaystyle f({\boldsymbol{x}})

\displaystyle=\int_{\mathbb{R}\times\mathbb{S}^{d-1}}\sigma({\boldsymbol{\xi}}^{\mathsf{T}}{\boldsymbol{x}}-t){\rm d}\mu(t,{\boldsymbol{\xi}}),

(1)

where
\mu
is a measure
on
\mathbb{R}\times\mathbb{S}^{d-1}
(hyper-spherical domain). This model is fitted to data subject to a penalty on the total-variation norm of
\mu

(
Bach 2017
)
. Remarkably, this infinite-dimensional convex optimization problem results in sparse minimizers of the form
\mu=\sum_{k=1}^{K}a_{k}\delta_{{\boldsymbol{z}}_{k}}
with
{\boldsymbol{z}}_{k}=(t_{k},{\boldsymbol{\xi}}_{k})\in\mathbb{R}\times\mathbb{S}^{d-1}
, which then map into standard two-layer neural networks
(
Bach 2017
)
.
Interestingly, we can relate
(
1
) to the Radon transform by identifying
\mu
as the (generalized) function
g_{\mu}
(with
{\rm d}\mu(t,{\boldsymbol{\xi}})=g_{\mu}(t,{\boldsymbol{\xi}}){\rm d}t{\rm d}{\boldsymbol{\xi}}
) and by rewriting the integral as

\displaystyle f({\boldsymbol{x}})

\displaystyle=\int_{\mathbb{S}^{d-1}}\left(\int_{\mathbb{R}}\sigma({\boldsymbol{\xi}}^{\mathsf{T}}{\boldsymbol{x}}-t)g_{\mu}(t,{\boldsymbol{\xi}}){\rm d}t\right){\rm d}{\boldsymbol{\xi}}={\mathrm{R}}^{\ast}{\mathrm{L}}_{\rm rad}\{g_{\mu}\}({\boldsymbol{x}}),

(2)

where
{\mathrm{R}}^{\ast}
(the adjoint of the Radon transform) is the back-projection operator of computer tomography
(
Natterer 1984
)
. Our “radial” operator
{\mathrm{L}}_{\rm rad}:g_{\mu}\mapsto\sigma\ast_{t}g_{\mu}
on the right-hand side of (
2
) implements the Radon-domain convolution with
\sigma
along the variable
t
.

While the synthesis approach to the learning problem proposed by
Bach 2017
is insightful, there is a strong incentive to make the connection with regularization theory in direct analogy with the classical theory of learning that relies on reproducing-kernel Hilbert spaces
(
Wahba 1990
;
Poggio and Girosi 1990
;
Schölkopf et al. 1997
;
Schölkopf et al. 2001
;
Alvarez et al. 2012
;
Unser 2021
)
. This is feasible provided that the linear relation between
f
and
g_{\mu}
expressed by (
2
) be one-to-one. This requires that the operators
{\mathrm{L}}_{\rm rad}
and
{\mathrm{R}}^{\ast}
in (
2
)
be both invertible.
Ongie et al. 2020
made an important step in that direction by showing that ReLU networks are minimizers of a Radon-domain total-variation norm that involves the Laplacian of
f
. Their optimality result was then generalized by
Parhi and Nowak 2021

who considered a broader class of differential operators inspired by spline theory
(
Unser et al. 2017
)
. The leading idea there is that the operator
{\mathrm{L}}_{\rm rad}
in (
2
) should implement some variant of an
n
th-order integrator, with
\sigma={\rm ReLU}
being the solution for
n=2
. Such an
{\mathrm{L}}_{\rm rad}
can be formally inverted by applying an
n
th-order partial derivative (e.g.,
{\mathrm{L}}^{-1}_{\rm rad}=\partial^{n}_{t}
), which motivates the use of the latter as (filtered) Radon-domain regularization operator.

The proposed spline-based approach to the inversion of (
2
) is elegant and intuitively appealing.
However, the formulation and resolution of the corresponding optimization problem requires special care because the underlying function spaces have a nontrivial kernel (null space) that needs to be factored out. The latter statement applies not only to the regularization operator (e.g., Laplacian and/or Radon-domain radial derivatives) but also to
{\mathrm{R}}^{\ast}
, which is an aspect that has been overlooked.
While there is a rich theory on the invertibility of the Radon transform
(
Helgason 2011
;
Rubin 1998
;
Boman and Lindskog 2009
;
Ramm and Katsevich 2020
)
, there are comparatively fewer—and not as strong—results on the invertibility of
{\mathrm{R}}^{\ast}
, the problem being that this operator has a huge null space
(
Ludwig 1966
)
. The primary spaces on which
{\mathrm{R}}^{\ast}
is known to be injective, and hence invertible, are

•

{\mathcal{S}}(\mathbb{P}^{d})
(the even part of Schwartz’ hyper-spherical—or Radon-domain—test functions)
(
Solmon 1987
)
;

•

L_{\infty,c}(\mathbb{P}^{d})
(the bounded even functions of compact support)
(
Ramm 1996
)
;

•

{\mathcal{S}}^{\prime}_{\rm Liz}(\mathbb{P}^{d})
(the even Lizorkin distributions)
(
Kostadinova et al. 2014
)
.

The space
{\mathcal{S}}^{\prime}_{\rm Liz}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
of Lizorkin distributions, which is the topological dual of
{\mathcal{S}}_{\rm Liz}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
(the subspace of Schwartz functions that are orthogonal to all polynomials), is especially attractive in that context. Indeed, the Radon transform being an homeomorphism from
{\mathcal{S}}^{\prime}_{\rm Liz}(\mathbb{R}^{d})

onto
{\mathcal{S}}^{\prime}_{\rm Liz}(\mathbb{P}^{d})
, the inversion process is straightforward
(
Kostadinova et al. 2014
)
. Lizorkin distributions also interact very nicely with the Laplace operator, which makes then well suited to the investigation of fractional integrals
(
Samko et al. 1993
)
and of wavelets
(
Saneva and Vindas 2010
)
.
The Lizorkin framework, however, has one basic limitation. The underlying objects—Lirzorkin distributions—are abstract entities, with
{\mathcal{S}}^{\prime}_{\rm Liz}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
being isomorphic to the quotient space
{\mathcal{S}}^{\prime}(\mathbb{R}^{d})/{\mathcal{P}}
, where
{\mathcal{S}}^{\prime}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
and
{\mathcal{P}}
are the spaces of tempered distributions and polynomials, respectively. Thus, Lizorkin distributions are generally identifiable only modulo some polynomial.
Fortunately, this is not a problem when dealing with ordinary functions
f\in L_{p}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
since
L_{p}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
is continuously embedded in
{\mathcal{S}}^{\prime}_{\rm Liz}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
for any
p>1

(
Samko 1982
)
. This implies that the Lizorkin distribution
f+{\mathcal{P}}\in{\mathcal{S}}^{\prime}_{\rm Liz}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
has a unique “concrete” representer
f\in L_{p}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
, which amounts to simply setting the polynomial to zero.
The situation, however, is not as clearcut for functions and ridge profiles that exhibit polynomial growth at infinity. To offer insights on the nature of the problem, let us consider three distinct neuronal units
f_{1}(x)=(x-t_{k})_{+}
(ReLU activation),
f_{2}(x)=\tfrac{1}{2}|x-t_{k}|
, and
f_{3}(x)=x+(x-t_{k})_{+}
(ReLU with skip connection), which are all valid representers of the same Lizorkin distribution
f=f_{i}+{\mathcal{P}}\in{\mathcal{S}}^{\prime}_{\rm Liz}(\mathbb{R})
for
i=1,2,3
(since the
f_{i}
’s only differ by a first-order polynomial). Suppose that a theoretical argument can be made concerning the optimality of the announced
f\in{\mathcal{S}}^{\prime}_{\rm Liz}(\mathbb{R})
. The practical difficulty then is to map this result into a concrete architecture. Should the choice be one of the
f_{i}
, if any? The least we can say is that the convenient rule of “setting the polynomial to zero” is not applicable here because it is unclear what the underlying polynomial truly is. This intrinsic ambiguity jeopardizes some of the conclusions regarding the connection between ReLU neural networks, ridge splines, and the Radon transform that have been reported in the literature
(
Sonoda and Murata 2017
;
Parhi and Nowak 2021
)
. We are in the opinion that adjustments are needed.

In this paper, we revisit the topic and extend the existing formulation so that it can handle arbitrary ridge profiles, without (polynomial) ambiguity. Our four primary contributions are as follows.

•

A detailed investigation of the invertibility of the back-projection operator for a broad family of Radon-compatible Banach subspaces
{\mathcal{X}}^{\prime}_{\rm Rad}\subset{\mathcal{X}}^{\prime}
, where
{\mathcal{X}}^{\prime}
is the topological dual of some generic hyper-spherical parent space
{\mathcal{X}}
.

•

A constructive characterization of the extreme points of the space of Radon-compatible hyper-spherical measures.

•

The extension of ridges to distributional profiles
r\in{\mathcal{S}}^{\prime}(\mathbb{R})

and the determination of their Radon transform.

•

The application of the formalism to the investigation of a functional-optimization problem that results in solutions that are parameterized by ReLU neural networks. Our contribution there is to clarify the analysis of
Parhi and Nowak 2021
and to provide a characterization of the full solution set.

The paper is organized as follows: We start with notations and mathematical preliminaries in Section 2. In particular, we recall the main properties of the classical Radon transform and its adjoint, and show how to extend them to tempered distributions by duality.
In Section
3
, we develop a formulation that leads to the identification of a generic family of Radon-compatible Banach spaces over which the back-projection operator is invertible. Our results are summarized in Theorem
7
, which can be viewed as the Banach counterpart of the classical result for tempered distributions
(
Ludwig 1966
)
.
In Section
4
, we use our framework to characterize the sampling functionals (Radon-compatible Diracs) that are in the range of the filtered Radon transform
(Theorem
8
).
In Section
5
, we introduce a general definition of a ridge with an arbitrary distributional profile and derive its
(filtered) Radon transform. Finally, in Section
6
, we apply our formalism to the resolution of a multidimensional supervised-learning problem with a 2nd-order Radon-domain regularization formulated by
Parhi and Nowak 2021
, the outcome being Theorem
12
on the optimality of ReLU networks.

Mathematical Preliminaries

2.1
Notations

We shall consider multidimensional functions
f
on
\mathbb{R}^{d}
that are indexed by the variable
{\boldsymbol{x}}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
. To describe their partial derivatives, we use the multi-index

{\boldsymbol{k}}=(k_{1},\dots,k_{d})\in\mathbb{N}^{d}
with the notational conventions
{\boldsymbol{k}}!\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\vartriangle}}{{=}}\prod_{i=1}^{d}k_{i}!
,
|{\boldsymbol{k}}|\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\vartriangle}}{{=}}k_{1}+\cdots+k_{d}
,

{\boldsymbol{x}}^{{\boldsymbol{k}}}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\vartriangle}}{{=}}\prod_{i=1}^{d}x_{i}^{k_{i}}
for any
{\boldsymbol{x}}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
, and
\partial^{\boldsymbol{k}}f({\boldsymbol{x}})\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\vartriangle}}{{=}}\frac{\partial^{|{\boldsymbol{k}}|}f(x_{1},\dots,x_{d})}{\partial^{k_{1}}_{x_{1}}\cdots\partial^{k_{d}}_{x_{d}}}
. This allows us to write the multidimensional Taylor expansion around
{\boldsymbol{x}}_{0}
of an analytical function
f:\mathbb{R}^{d}\to\mathbb{R}
explicitly as

\displaystyle f({\boldsymbol{x}})=\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\sum_{|{\boldsymbol{k}}|=n}\frac{\partial^{\boldsymbol{k}}f({\boldsymbol{x}}_{0})({\boldsymbol{x}}-{\boldsymbol{x}}_{0})^{\boldsymbol{k}}}{{\boldsymbol{k}}!\lx@nobreakspace},

(3)

where the internal summation is over all multi-indices
{\boldsymbol{k}}=(k_{1},\dots,k_{d})
such that
k_{1}+\cdots+k_{d}=n
.

Schwartz’ space of smooth and rapidly decreasing test functions
\varphi:\mathbb{R}^{d}\to\mathbb{R}
equipped with the usual Fréchet-Schwartz topology is denoted by
{\mathcal{S}}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
. Its continuous dual is the space
{\mathcal{S}}^{\prime}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
of tempered distributions. In this setting, the Lebesgue spaces
L_{p}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
for
p\in[1,\infty)
can be specified as the completion of

{\mathcal{S}}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
equipped with the
L_{p}
-norm
\|\cdot\|_{L_{p}}
; that is,
L_{p}(\mathbb{R}^{d})=\overline{({\mathcal{S}}(\mathbb{R}^{d}),\|\cdot\|_{L_{p}})}
.
For the end point
p=\infty
, we have that
\overline{({\mathcal{S}}(\mathbb{R}^{d}),\|\cdot\|_{L_{\infty}})}=C_{0}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
with
\|\varphi\|_{L_{\infty}}=\sup_{{\boldsymbol{x}}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}}|\varphi({\boldsymbol{x}})|
, where
C_{0}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
is the space of continuous functions that vanish at infinity. Its continuous dual is the space
{\mathcal{M}}(\mathbb{R}^{d})=\{f\in{\mathcal{S}}^{\prime}(\mathbb{R}^{d}):\|f\|_{{\mathcal{M}}}<\infty\}
of bounded
Radon measures with

\|f\|_{{\mathcal{M}}}=\sup_{\varphi\in{\mathcal{S}}(\mathbb{R}^{d}):\|\varphi\|_{L_{\infty}}\leq 1}\langle f,\varphi\rangle.

The latter is a superset of
L_{1}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
, which is isometrically embedded in it, meaning that
\|f\|_{L_{1}}=\|f\|_{{\mathcal{M}}}
for all
f\in L_{1}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
.

The Fourier transform of a function
\varphi\in L_{1}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
is defined as

\displaystyle\widehat{\varphi}({\boldsymbol{\omega}})\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\vartriangle}}{{=}}\mathcal{F}\{\varphi\}({\boldsymbol{\omega}})=\frac{1}{(2\pi)^{d}}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\varphi({\boldsymbol{x}})\mathrm{e}^{-\mathrm{j}\langle{\boldsymbol{\omega}},{\boldsymbol{x}}\rangle}{\rm d}{\boldsymbol{x}}.

(4)

Since the Fourier operator
\mathcal{F}
continuously maps
{\mathcal{S}}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
into itself, the transform can be extended by duality to the whole space
{\mathcal{S}}^{\prime}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
of tempered distribution. Specifically,
\widehat{f}=\mathcal{F}\{f\}\in{\mathcal{S}}^{\prime}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
is the (unique)
generalized Fourier transform
of
f\in{\mathcal{S}}^{\prime}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
if and only if
\langle\widehat{f},\varphi\rangle=\langle f,\widehat{\varphi}\rangle
for all
\varphi\in{\mathcal{S}}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
, where
\widehat{\varphi}=\mathcal{F}\{\varphi\}
is the “classical” Fourier transform of
\varphi
defined by (
4
).

2.2
Polynomial Spaces and Related Projectors

The regularization operators (e.g., the Laplacian) that are of interest to us are isotropic and have a growth-restricted null space formed by the space
\Pi_{n_{0}}
of polynomials of degree
n_{0}
.
Here, we choose to expand these polynomials
in the monomial/Taylor basis

\displaystyle m_{\boldsymbol{k}}({\boldsymbol{x}})\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\vartriangle}}{{=}}\frac{{\boldsymbol{x}}^{{\boldsymbol{k}}}}{{\boldsymbol{k}}!}

(5)

with
|{\boldsymbol{k}}|\leq n_{0}
. Defining the
\ell_{2}
-norm of the Taylor coefficients of the polynomial

p_{0}=\sum_{|{\boldsymbol{k}}|\leq n_{0}}b_{\boldsymbol{k}}m_{{\boldsymbol{k}}}
as

\displaystyle\|p_{0}\|_{{\mathcal{P}}}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\vartriangle}}{{=}}\|(b_{\boldsymbol{k}})_{|{\boldsymbol{k}}|\leq n_{0}}\|_{2},

(6)

we add a topological structure that results in the description

\displaystyle{\mathcal{P}}_{n_{0}}=\{p_{0}\in\Pi_{n_{0}}:\|p_{0}\|_{{\mathcal{P}}}<\infty\}.

(7)

The important point here is that (
7
) specifies a finite-dimensional Banach subspace of
{\mathcal{S}}^{\prime}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
. Its continuous dual
{\mathcal{P}}^{\prime}_{n_{0}}
is finite-dimensional as well, although it is composed of “abstract” elements that are, in fact, equivalence classes in
{\mathcal{S}}^{\prime}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
. Yet, it possible to
identify every dual element
p_{0}^{\ast}\in{\mathcal{P}}^{\prime}_{n_{0}}
concretely as a function by selecting a particular dual basis
\{m_{{\boldsymbol{k}}}^{\ast}\}_{|{\boldsymbol{k}}|\leq n_{0}}
such that
\langle m^{\ast}_{{\boldsymbol{k}}},m_{{\boldsymbol{k}}^{\prime}}\rangle=\delta_{{\boldsymbol{k}}-{\boldsymbol{k}}^{\prime}}
(Kroneker delta). Our specific choice is

\displaystyle m^{\ast}_{\boldsymbol{k}}=(-1)^{|{\boldsymbol{k}}|}\partial^{{\boldsymbol{k}}}\kappa_{\rm iso}\in{\mathcal{S}}(\mathbb{R}^{d})

(8)

with
{\boldsymbol{k}}\in\mathbb{N}^{d}
, where
\kappa_{\rm iso}
is the isotropic function described below.

Lemma 1
.

There exists an entire isotropic function
\kappa_{\rm iso}\in{\mathcal{S}}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
with
0\leq\widehat{\kappa}_{\rm iso}({\boldsymbol{\omega}})\leq 1
and
\widehat{\kappa}_{\rm iso}({\boldsymbol{\omega}})=0
for
\|{\boldsymbol{\omega}}\|\geq 1

such that

\displaystyle\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\frac{{\boldsymbol{x}}^{{\boldsymbol{k}}}}{{\boldsymbol{k}}!}(-1)^{|{\boldsymbol{n}}|}\partial^{{\boldsymbol{n}}}\kappa_{\rm iso}({\boldsymbol{x}}){\rm d}{\boldsymbol{x}}=\;\delta_{{\boldsymbol{k}}-{\boldsymbol{n}}}

(9)

for all
{\boldsymbol{k}},{\boldsymbol{n}}\in\mathbb{N}^{d}
.

Proof.

We take
\kappa_{\rm iso}=\mathcal{F}^{-1}\{\widehat{\kappa}_{\rm rad}(\|\cdot\|)\}
, where the radial profile
\widehat{\kappa}_{\rm rad}:\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R}
is such that
\widehat{\kappa}_{\rm rad}\in{\mathcal{S}}(\mathbb{R})
,

\widehat{\kappa}_{\rm rad}(\omega)=1
for
0\leq|\omega|\leq R_{0}\leq\tfrac{1}{2}
, and
\widehat{\kappa}_{\rm rad}(\omega)=0
for
|\omega|\geq 1
. A particular construction with
R_{0}=\tfrac{1}{2}
is

\widehat{\kappa}_{\rm rad}={\rm rect}\ast\varphi
, where
\varphi\in{\mathcal{S}}(\mathbb{R})
is a symmetric, non-negative test function (to avoid oscillations) with

{\rm support}(\varphi)\subseteq[-\tfrac{1}{2},\tfrac{1}{2}]
and
\int_{\mathbb{R}}\varphi(x){\rm d}x=1
. Next, we observe that

\displaystyle\langle m_{{\boldsymbol{k}}},(-1)^{|{\boldsymbol{n}}|}\partial^{{\boldsymbol{n}}}\kappa_{\rm iso}\rangle=\langle\partial^{{\boldsymbol{n}}}m_{{\boldsymbol{k}}},\kappa_{\rm iso}\rangle=\begin{cases}\langle m_{{\boldsymbol{k}}-{\boldsymbol{n}}},\kappa_{\rm iso}\rangle,&{\boldsymbol{k}}-{\boldsymbol{n}}\geq{\boldsymbol{0}}\\
\langle 0,\kappa_{\rm iso}\rangle=0,&\mbox{otherwise.}\end{cases}

(10)

We evaluate the duality product for the case
{\boldsymbol{m}}=({\boldsymbol{k}}-{\boldsymbol{n}})\geq{\boldsymbol{0}}
in the Fourier domain as

\displaystyle\langle m_{{\boldsymbol{m}}},\kappa_{\rm iso}\rangle

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{(2\pi)^{d}}\langle\mathcal{F}\{m_{{\boldsymbol{m}}}\},\widehat{\kappa}_{\rm iso}\rangle

\displaystyle=\langle\frac{\mathrm{j}^{|{\boldsymbol{m}}|}}{{\boldsymbol{m}}!}\delta^{({\boldsymbol{m}})},\widehat{\kappa}_{\rm iso}\rangle=\frac{-\mathrm{j}^{|{\boldsymbol{m}}|}}{{\boldsymbol{m}}!}\partial^{{\boldsymbol{m}}}\widehat{\kappa}_{\rm iso}({\boldsymbol{0}})=\begin{cases}1,&{\boldsymbol{m}}={\boldsymbol{0}}\\
0,&\mbox{otherwise},\end{cases}

(11)

where we have used the relation
\mathcal{F}\{{\boldsymbol{x}}^{{\boldsymbol{m}}}\}({\boldsymbol{\omega}})=(2\pi)^{d}\;\mathrm{j}^{|{\boldsymbol{m}}|}\delta^{({\boldsymbol{m}})}({\boldsymbol{\omega}})
. Finally, since
\widehat{\kappa}_{\rm iso}
is compactly supported, its inverse Fourier transform
\kappa_{\rm iso}
is an entire function of exponential-type (by the Paley-Wiener theorem). This means that the function
{\boldsymbol{x}}\mapsto\kappa_{\rm iso}({\boldsymbol{x}})
is analytic
with a convergent Taylor series of the form (
3
) for any
{\boldsymbol{x}},{\boldsymbol{x}}_{0}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
.
∎

This allows us to describe the dual space explicitly as

\displaystyle{\mathcal{P}}^{\prime}_{n_{0}}=\{p_{0}^{\ast}=\sum_{|{\boldsymbol{k}}|\leq n_{0}}b^{\ast}_{\boldsymbol{k}}m^{\ast}_{{\boldsymbol{k}}}:\|p_{0}^{\ast}\|_{{\mathcal{P}}^{\prime}}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\vartriangle}}{{=}}\|(b^{\ast}_{\boldsymbol{k}})\|_{2}<\infty\}

(12)

where each elements
p_{0}^{\ast}
has a unique representation in terms of its coefficients
(b^{\ast}_{\boldsymbol{k}})_{|{\boldsymbol{k}}|\leq n_{0}}
.
We can use the dual basis
\{m_{\boldsymbol{k}}^{\ast}\}
to specify the projection operator

\mathrm{Proj}_{{\mathcal{P}}_{n_{0}}}:{\mathcal{S}}^{\prime}(\mathbb{R}^{d})\to{\mathcal{P}}_{n_{0}}
as

\displaystyle\mathrm{Proj}_{{\mathcal{P}}_{n_{0}}}\{f\}

\displaystyle=\sum_{|{\boldsymbol{k}}|\leq n_{0}}\langle f,m_{\boldsymbol{k}}^{\ast}\rangle\;m_{\boldsymbol{k}},

(13)

which is well-defined for any
f\in{\mathcal{S}}^{\prime}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
since
m_{\boldsymbol{k}}^{\ast}\in{\mathcal{S}}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
.

2.3
Radon Transform

The Radon transform integrates of a function of
\mathbb{R}^{d}
over all
hyperplanes of dimension
(d-1)
. These hyperplanes are indexed over

\mathbb{R}\times\mathbb{S}^{d-1}
, where
\mathbb{S}^{d-1}=\{{\boldsymbol{\xi}}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}:\|{\boldsymbol{\xi}}\|_{2}=1\}
is the unit sphere in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
.
Specifically, the coordinates
{\boldsymbol{x}}
of a hyperplane associated with an offset
t\in\mathbb{R}
and a normal vector
{\boldsymbol{\xi}}\in\mathbb{S}^{d-1}
satisfy

\displaystyle{\boldsymbol{\xi}}^{\mathsf{T}}{\boldsymbol{x}}=\xi_{1}x_{1}+\dots+\xi_{d}x_{d}=t.

(14)

The transform is first described for ordinary (test) functions and then extended to tempered distributions by duality.

2.3.1
Classical Integral Formulation

The Radon transform of the function
f\in L_{1}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
is defined as

\displaystyle{\mathrm{R}}\{f\}(t,{\boldsymbol{\xi}})

\displaystyle=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\delta(t-{\boldsymbol{\xi}}^{\mathsf{T}}{\boldsymbol{x}})f({\boldsymbol{x}}){\rm d}{\boldsymbol{x}},\quad(t,{\boldsymbol{\xi}})\in\mathbb{R}\times\mathbb{S}^{d-1}.

(15)

The adjoint of
{\mathrm{R}}
is the back-projection operator
{\mathrm{R}}^{\ast}
.
Its action on
g:\mathbb{R}\times\mathbb{S}^{d-1}\to\mathbb{R}
yields the function

\displaystyle{\mathrm{R}}^{\ast}\{g\}({\boldsymbol{x}})=\int_{\mathbb{S}^{d-1}}g(\underbrace{{\boldsymbol{\xi}}^{\mathsf{T}}{\boldsymbol{x}}}_{t},{\boldsymbol{\xi}}){\rm d}{\boldsymbol{\xi}},\quad{\boldsymbol{x}}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}.

(16)

Given the
d
-dimensional Fourier transform
\hat{f}=\mathcal{F}\{f\}
of
f\in L_{1}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
, one can calculate
{\mathrm{R}}\{f\}(\cdot,{\boldsymbol{\xi}}_{0})
for any fixed
{\boldsymbol{\xi}}_{0}\in\mathbb{S}^{d-1}
through

\displaystyle{\mathrm{R}}\{f\}(t,{\boldsymbol{\xi}}_{0})=\frac{1}{2\pi}\int_{\mathbb{R}}\hat{f}(\omega{\boldsymbol{\xi}}_{0})\mathrm{e}^{\mathrm{j}\omega t}{\rm d}\omega=\mathcal{F}^{-1}_{\omega\to t}\{\hat{f}(\omega{\boldsymbol{\xi}}_{0})\}\{t\}.

(17)

In other words, the restriction of
\hat{f}:\mathbb{R}^{d}\to\mathbb{C}
along the ray
\{{\boldsymbol{\omega}}=\omega{\boldsymbol{\xi}}_{0}:\omega\in\mathbb{R}\}
is equal to the 1D Fourier transform of
{\mathrm{R}}\{f\}(\cdot,{\boldsymbol{\xi}}_{0})
, a property that is referred to as the
Fourier slice theorem
.

To describe the functional properties of the Radon transform, one needs the
(hyper)spherical (or Radon-domain) counterparts of the spaces described in Section
2.1
where the Euclidean indexing with
{\boldsymbol{x}}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
is replaced by
(t,{\boldsymbol{\xi}})\in\mathbb{R}\times\mathbb{S}^{d-1}
.
The spherical counterpart of
{\mathcal{S}}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
is
{\mathcal{S}}(\mathbb{R}\times\mathbb{S}^{d-1})
. Correspondingly, an element
g\in{\mathcal{S}}^{\prime}(\mathbb{R}\times\mathbb{S}^{d-1})
is a continuous linear functional on
{\mathcal{S}}(\mathbb{R}\times\mathbb{S}^{d-1})
whose action on the test function
\phi(t,{\boldsymbol{\xi}})
is represented by the duality product
g:\phi\mapsto\langle g,\phi\rangle_{\rm Rad}
. When
g
can be identified as an ordinary function
g:(t,{\boldsymbol{\xi}})\mapsto\mathbb{R}
, one has that

\displaystyle\langle g,\phi\rangle_{\rm Rad}=\int_{\mathbb{S}^{d-1}}\int_{\mathbb{R}}g(t,{\boldsymbol{\xi}})\phi(t,{\boldsymbol{\xi}}){\rm d}t{\rm d}{\boldsymbol{\xi}},

(18)

where
{\rm d}{\boldsymbol{\xi}}
stands for a surface element on
\mathbb{S}^{d-1}
with
\|{\boldsymbol{\xi}}\|_{2}=1
. For instance, for
d=2
, we parameterize
\mathbb{S}^{1}
by setting
{\boldsymbol{\xi}}=(\cos\theta,\sin\theta)
with
{\rm d}{\boldsymbol{\xi}}={\rm d}\theta
for
\theta\in[0,2\pi]
, which then yields

\displaystyle\langle g,\phi\rangle_{\rm Rad}=\int_{0}^{2\pi}\int_{\mathbb{R}}g(t,\theta)\phi(t,\theta){\rm d}t{\rm d}\theta.

(19)

Such explicit representations are also available in higher dimensions using hyper-spherical polar coordinates.
Of special importance to us is the translated and rotated hyper-spherical Dirac distribution

\delta_{(t_{0},{\boldsymbol{\xi}}_{0})}=\delta(\cdot-t_{0})\delta(\cdot-{\boldsymbol{\xi}}_{0})\in{\mathcal{S}}^{\prime}(\mathbb{R}\times\mathbb{S}^{d-1})
, which is defined as

\langle\delta_{{\boldsymbol{z}}_{0}},\phi\rangle_{\rm Rad}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\vartriangle}}{{=}}\phi({\boldsymbol{z}}_{0})
for all

\phi\in{\mathcal{S}}(\mathbb{R}\times\mathbb{S}^{d-1})
and any offset
{\boldsymbol{z}}_{0}=(t_{0},{\boldsymbol{\xi}}_{0})\in\mathbb{R}\times\mathbb{S}^{d-1}
.
This Dirac impulse, which is separable in the index variables
t
and
{\boldsymbol{\xi}}
, is included in the Banach space
{\mathcal{M}}(\mathbb{R}\times\mathbb{S}^{d-1})
(hyper-spherical Radon measures) with the property that

\|\delta_{{\boldsymbol{z}}_{0}}\|_{{\mathcal{M}}}=1
.

The key property for analysis is that the Radon transform is continuous on
{\mathcal{S}}
and invertible (see
(
Ludwig 1966
;
Helgason 2011
;
Ramm and Katsevich 2020
)
for details).

Theorem 2

(Continuity and Invertibility of the Radon Transform on
{\mathcal{S}}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
)
.

The Radon operator
{\mathrm{R}}
continuously maps
{\mathcal{S}}(\mathbb{R}^{d})\to{\mathcal{S}}(\mathbb{R}\times\mathbb{S}^{d-1})
. Moreover,
{\mathrm{R}}^{\ast}{\mathrm{K}}_{\rm rad}{\mathrm{R}}={\mathrm{K}}{\mathrm{R}}^{\ast}{\mathrm{R}}={\mathrm{R}}^{\ast}{\mathrm{R}}{\mathrm{K}}=\mathrm{Id}\mbox{ on\lx@nobreakspace}{\mathcal{S}}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
,
where
{\mathrm{K}}=({\mathrm{R}}^{\ast}{\mathrm{R}})^{-1}=c_{d}(-\Delta)^{\tfrac{d-1}{2}}
with
c_{d}=\frac{1}{2(2\pi)^{d-1}}
is the so-called “filtering” operator, and is
{\mathrm{K}}_{\rm rad}
its one-dimensional radial counterpart that acts along the Radon-domain variable
t
. These filtering operators are characterized by their frequency response

\widehat{K}({\boldsymbol{\omega}})=c_{d}\|{\boldsymbol{\omega}}\|^{d-1}
and

\widehat{K}_{\rm rad}(\omega)=c_{d}|\omega|^{d-1}
.

The image of
{\mathcal{S}}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
through the Radon transform is the space
{\mathcal{S}}_{\rm Rad}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\vartriangle}}{{=}}{\mathrm{R}}\big({\mathcal{S}}(\mathbb{R}^{d})\big)
: a subset of the space of hyper-spherical test functions
{\mathcal{S}}(\mathbb{R}\times\mathbb{S}^{d-1})
that can be characterized explicitly
(
Gelfand and Shilov 1966
;
Helgason 2011
;
Ludwig 1966
)
. In particular, a function
\phi={\mathrm{R}}\{\varphi\}\in{\mathcal{S}}_{\rm Rad}
must be symmetric and such as
\int_{\mathbb{R}}{\mathrm{R}}\{\varphi\}(t,{\boldsymbol{\xi}}){\rm d}t=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\varphi({\boldsymbol{x}}){\rm d}{\boldsymbol{x}}
for all
{\boldsymbol{\xi}}\in\mathbb{S}^{d-1}
(see Theorem
9
for details).
While Theorem
2

implies that
{\mathrm{R}}
is invertible on
{\mathcal{S}}_{\rm Rad}
, there is also a stronger version of this property for
{\mathcal{S}}_{\rm Rad}
equipped with the Schwartz-Fréchet topology inherited from
{\mathcal{S}}(\mathbb{R}\times\mathbb{S}^{d-1})

(
Helgason 2011
, p. 60)

(
Hertle 1983
)
.

Theorem 3
.

The operator
{\mathrm{R}}:{\mathcal{S}}(\mathbb{R}^{d})\to{\mathcal{S}}_{\rm Rad}
is a continuous bijection, with a continuous inverse given by
{\mathrm{R}}^{-1}=({\mathrm{R}}^{\ast}{\mathrm{K}}_{\rm rad}):{\mathcal{S}}_{\rm Rad}\to{\mathcal{S}}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
.

The bottom line is that the classical Radon transform is a homeomorphism
{\mathcal{S}}(\mathbb{R}^{d})\to{\mathcal{S}}_{\rm Rad}
.

2.3.2
Distributional Extension

To extend the framework to distributions, one proceeds by duality. For instance,
by invoking the property that

{\mathrm{R}}^{\ast}{\mathrm{K}}_{\rm rad}{\mathrm{R}}=\mathrm{Id}

on
{\mathcal{S}}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
and noting that

{\mathrm{K}}_{\rm rad}

is self-adjoint, we make the manipulation

\displaystyle\forall\varphi\in{\mathcal{S}}(\mathbb{R}^{d}),\quad\langle f,\varphi\rangle

\displaystyle=\langle f,{\mathrm{R}}^{\ast}{\mathrm{K}}_{\rm rad}{\mathrm{R}}\{\varphi\}\rangle

\displaystyle=\langle{\mathrm{K}}_{\rm rad}{\mathrm{R}}\{f\},{\mathrm{R}}\{\varphi\}\rangle_{\rm Rad}=\langle{\mathrm{K}}_{\rm rad}{\mathrm{R}}\{f\},\phi\rangle_{\rm Rad},

(20)

with
\phi={\mathrm{R}}\{\varphi\}\in{\mathcal{S}}_{\rm Rad}
and
\varphi={\mathrm{R}}^{\ast}{\mathrm{K}}_{\rm rad}\{\phi\}

f\in L_{1}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
. Otherwise, it is used as definition to extend the scope of the operator for
f\in{\mathcal{S}}^{\prime}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
.

Definition 4
.

The distribution
g\in{\mathcal{S}}^{\prime}(\mathbb{R}\times\mathbb{S}^{d-1})
(respectively,

is a formal Radon transform of

if

\displaystyle\forall\phi\in{\mathrm{K}}_{\rm rad}{\mathrm{R}}\big({\mathcal{S}}(\mathbb{R}^{d})\big):\quad\langle g,\phi\rangle_{\rm Rad}=\langle f,{\mathrm{R}}^{\ast}\{\phi\}\rangle.

(21)

Likewise,
\tilde{g}\in{\mathcal{S}}^{\prime}(\mathbb{R}\times\mathbb{S}^{d-1})
(respectively,
\tilde{g}={\mathrm{K}}_{\rm rad}{\mathrm{R}}\{f\}\in{\mathcal{S}}_{\rm Rad}^{\prime}

if

\displaystyle\forall\phi\in{\mathcal{S}}_{\rm Rad}:\quad\langle\tilde{g},\phi\rangle_{\rm Rad}=\langle f,{\mathrm{R}}^{\ast}{\mathrm{K}}_{\rm rad}\{\phi\}\rangle.

(22)

Finally, the tempered distribution
f={\mathrm{R}}^{\ast}\{g\}\in{\mathcal{S}}^{\prime}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
is the back-projection of
g\in{{\mathcal{S}}^{\prime}(\mathbb{R}\times\mathbb{S}^{d-1})}
if

\displaystyle\forall\varphi\in{\mathcal{S}}(\mathbb{R}^{d}):\quad\langle{\mathrm{R}}^{\ast}\{g\},\varphi\rangle=\langle g,{\mathrm{R}}\{\varphi\}\rangle_{\rm Rad}.

(23)

While (

{\mathrm{R}}^{\ast}:{\mathcal{S}}^{\prime}(\mathbb{R}\times\mathbb{S}^{d-1})\to{\mathcal{S}}^{\prime}(\mathbb{R}^{d})

extended operators
{\mathrm{R}}
and

is trickier because there are always infinitely many distributions

that satisfy (

or (

The root of the problem is that the Radon transform

is not surjective:
Since the test functions
\phi
in (
21
) and (
22
) do not span the whole space
{\mathcal{S}}(\mathbb{R}\times\mathbb{S}^{d-1})
, they are unable to separate out
the components that are in the null space of
{\mathrm{R}}^{\ast}
, which is huge

1

Theorem 5

(Invertibility of the Radon Transform on
{\mathcal{S}}^{\prime}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
)
.

It holds that
{\mathrm{R}}^{\ast}{\mathrm{K}}_{\rm rad}{\mathrm{R}}=\mathrm{Id}
on
{\mathcal{S}}^{\prime}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
.
Moreover, the filtered Radon transform
{\mathrm{K}}_{\rm rad}{\mathrm{R}}:{\mathcal{S}}^{\prime}(\mathbb{R}^{d})\to{\mathcal{S}}^{\prime}_{\rm Rad}
is bicontinuous and one-to-one, with its continuous inverse
({\mathrm{K}}_{\rm rad}{\mathrm{R}})^{-1}
being the back-projection operation
{\mathrm{R}}^{\ast}:{\mathcal{S}}^{\prime}_{\rm Rad}\to{\mathcal{S}}^{\prime}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
.

The distributional extension of the Radon transform inherits most of the properties of the “classical” operator defined in (
15
). Of special relevance to us is the quasi-commutativity of
{\mathrm{R}}
with convolution, also known as the
intertwining property
. Specifically, let
h,f\in{\mathcal{S}}^{\prime}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
be two distributions whose convolution

is well defined in
{\mathcal{S}}^{\prime}(\mathbb{R}^{d})

\displaystyle{\mathrm{R}}\{h\ast f\}={\mathrm{R}}\{h\}\ast_{t}{\mathrm{R}}\{f\}

(27)

where the symbol “
\ast_{t}

(u\ast_{t}g)(t,{\boldsymbol{\xi}})=\langle u(\cdot,{\boldsymbol{\xi}}),g(t-\cdot,{\boldsymbol{\xi}})\rangle

h={\rm L}\{\delta\}
is the (isotropic) impulse response of an LSI operator whose frequency response
\widehat{L}({\boldsymbol{\omega}})=\widehat{L}_{\rm rad}(\|{\boldsymbol{\omega}}\|)
is purely radial, we get that

\displaystyle{\mathrm{R}}\{h\ast f\}={\mathrm{R}}{\rm L}\{f\}={\rm L}_{\rm rad}{\mathrm{R}}\{f\},

(28)

where
{\rm L}_{\rm rad}
is the “
\ast_{t}
” convolution operator whose 1D frequency response is
\widehat{L}_{\rm rad}(\omega)
. Likewise, by duality, for

we have that

\displaystyle{\rm L}{\mathrm{R}}^{\ast}\{g\}={\mathrm{R}}^{\ast}{\rm L}_{\rm rad}\{g\}

(29)

under the implicit assumption that
{\rm L}\{{\mathrm{R}}^{\ast}g\}
and
{\rm L}_{\rm rad}\{g\}
are well-defined distributions.
By taking inspiration from Theorem
2

28

29

to show that
{\mathrm{R}}^{\ast}{\mathrm{K}}_{\rm rad}{\mathrm{R}}\{f\}={\mathrm{R}}^{\ast}{\mathrm{R}}{\mathrm{K}}\{f\}={\mathrm{K}}{\mathrm{R}}^{\ast}{\mathrm{R}}\{f\}=f
for a broad class of distributions. While the first form is valid for all

{\mathrm{K}}\{f\}

be well-defined in

(Radon Transform of Isotropic Distributions)
.

Let

be an isotropic distribution whose radial frequency profile is

\displaystyle{\mathrm{R}}\{\rho_{\rm iso}(\cdot-{\boldsymbol{x}}_{0})\}(t,{\boldsymbol{\xi}})

\displaystyle=\rho_{\rm rad}(t-{\boldsymbol{\xi}}^{\mathsf{T}}{\boldsymbol{x}}_{0})

\displaystyle{\mathrm{K}}_{\rm rad}{\mathrm{R}}\{\rho_{\rm iso}(\cdot-{\boldsymbol{x}}_{0})\}(t,{\boldsymbol{\xi}}\}

Proof.

3
Radon-Compatible Banach Spaces

The distribution formalism associated with Theorem
5

is extremely powerful. It provides a definition of the (filtered) Radon transform of any tempered distribution
f\in{\mathcal{S}}^{\prime}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
, along with a way to invert it via the back-projection operator
{\mathrm{R}}^{\ast}:{\mathcal{S}}^{\prime}_{\rm Rad}\to{\mathcal{S}}^{\prime}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
.
While this is conceptually very satisfying, it is only moderately helpful for our purpose because the members of
{\mathcal{S}}^{\prime}_{\rm Rad}
are “abstract” equivalence classes of distributions. In fact, the investigation of functional-optimization problems with Radon-domain regularization requires some Banach counterpart of Theorems
3
and
5
, where both
{\mathrm{R}}\{f\}
and
{\mathrm{K}}_{\rm Rad}{\mathrm{R}}\{f\}
have concrete representations as hyper-spherical functions or measures. In particular, we are interested in identifying specific Radon-domain Banach spaces—for instance, an appropriate subspace of hyper-spherical measures—over which the back-projection operator
{\mathrm{R}}^{\ast}
is guaranteed to be invertible.

To that end, we pick a “parent” hyper-spherical Banach space
{\mathcal{X}}=({\mathcal{X}},\|\cdot\|_{{\mathcal{X}}})
such that

{\mathcal{S}}(\mathbb{R}\times\mathbb{S}^{d-1})\xhookrightarrow{\mbox{\tiny\rm d.}}{\mathcal{X}}\xhookrightarrow{\mbox{\tiny\rm d.}}{\mathcal{S}}^{\prime}(\mathbb{R}\times\mathbb{S}^{d-1})
.
This dense embedding hypothesis has several implications.

1.

The Banach space
{\mathcal{X}}
is the completion of
{\mathcal{S}}(\mathbb{R}\times\mathbb{S}^{d-1})
in the
\|\cdot\|_{{\mathcal{X}}}
norm; i.e.,

\displaystyle{\mathcal{X}}=\overline{\big({\mathcal{S}}(\mathbb{R}\times\mathbb{S}^{d-1}),\|\cdot\|_{{\mathcal{X}}}\big)}.

(35)

2.

The dual space
{\mathcal{X}}^{\prime}\xhookrightarrow{}{\mathcal{S}}^{\prime}(\mathbb{R}\times\mathbb{S}^{d-1})
is equipped with the norm

\displaystyle\|g\|_{{\mathcal{X}}^{\prime}}=\sup_{\phi\in{\mathcal{X}}:\;\|\phi\|_{{\mathcal{X}}}\leq 1}\langle g,\phi\rangle=\sup_{\phi\in{\mathcal{S}}(\mathbb{R}\times\mathbb{S}^{d-1}):\;\|\phi\|_{{\mathcal{X}}}\leq 1}\langle g,\phi\rangle,

(36)

where the restriction of
\phi\in{\mathcal{S}}(\mathbb{R}\times\mathbb{S}^{d-1})
on the rightmost side of (
36
) is justified by the denseness of

{\mathcal{S}}(\mathbb{R}\times\mathbb{S}^{d-1})
in
{\mathcal{X}}
.

3.

The definition of
\|g\|_{{\mathcal{X}}^{\prime}}
found in the rightmost side of (
36
) is valid for
any distribution
g\in{\mathcal{S}}^{\prime}(\mathbb{R}\times\mathbb{S}^{d-1})
with
\|g\|_{{\mathcal{X}}^{\prime}}=\infty
for
g\notin{\mathcal{X}}^{\prime}
. Accordingly, we can specify the topological dual of
{\mathcal{X}}^{\prime}
as

\displaystyle{\mathcal{X}}^{\prime}=\big\{g\in{\mathcal{S}}^{\prime}(\mathbb{R}\times\mathbb{S}^{d-1}):\|g\|_{{\mathcal{X}}^{\prime}}<\infty\big\}.

(37)

Prototypical examples where those properties are met are
({\mathcal{X}},{\mathcal{X}}^{\prime})=\big(L_{p}(\mathbb{R}\times\mathbb{S}^{d-1}),L_{q}(\mathbb{R}\times\mathbb{S}^{d-1})\big)
with
p\in[1,\infty)
and

(conjugate exponent), as well as

({\mathcal{X}},{\mathcal{X}}^{\prime})=\big(C_{0}(\mathbb{R}\times\mathbb{S}^{d-1}),{\mathcal{M}}(\mathbb{R}\times\mathbb{S}^{d-1})\big)
for

.

Likewise, by considering the dual pair
({\mathcal{S}}_{\rm Rad},{\mathcal{S}}^{\prime}_{\rm Rad})
, we specify our Radon-compatible Banach subspaces

\displaystyle{\mathcal{X}}_{\rm Rad}

\displaystyle=\overline{({\mathcal{S}}_{\rm Rad},\|\cdot\|_{{\mathcal{X}}})}

(38)

\displaystyle{\mathcal{X}}^{\prime}_{\rm Rad}

\displaystyle=\big({\mathcal{X}}_{\rm Rad}\big)^{\prime}=\big\{g\in{\mathcal{S}}^{\prime}_{\rm Rad}:\|g\|_{{\mathcal{X}}^{\prime}_{\rm Rad}}<\infty\big\},

(39)

where the underlying dual norms have a definition that is analogous to (
36
) with

{\mathcal{S}}(\mathbb{R}\times\mathbb{S}^{d-1})
and
{\mathcal{X}}
being instanciated by

{\mathcal{S}}_{\rm Rad}
and
{\mathcal{X}}_{\rm Rad}
.
We now show that
{\mathrm{R}}^{\ast}
(resp.,
{\mathrm{K}}_{\rm rad}{\mathrm{R}}^{\ast}
) is invertible on
{\mathcal{X}}^{\prime}_{\rm Rad}
(resp., on
{\mathcal{X}}_{\rm Rad}
), which is the main theoretical contribution of this work.

Since
{\mathrm{R}}:{\mathcal{S}}(\mathbb{R}^{d})\to{\mathcal{S}}_{\rm Rad}
is a homeomorphism and the
\|\cdot\|_{{\mathcal{X}}}
-norm is continuous in the Fréchet topology of
{\mathcal{S}}_{\rm Rad}

(
resp., of
{\mathcal{S}}(\mathbb{R}\times\mathbb{S}^{d-1})
)
, we can consider the normed space
({\mathcal{S}}(\mathbb{R}^{d}),\|\cdot\|_{{\mathcal{Y}}})
with

\|\varphi\|_{{\mathcal{Y}}}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\vartriangle}}{{=}}\|{\mathrm{R}}\{\varphi\}\|_{{\mathcal{X}}}
and identify the operator

{\mathrm{R}}:({\mathcal{S}}(\mathbb{R}^{d}),\|\cdot\|_{{\mathcal{Y}}})\to({\mathcal{S}}_{\rm Rad},\|\cdot\|_{{\mathcal{X}}})

as an isometry. We then invoke the BLT theorem
(
Reed and Simon 1980
)
to specify the unique

Proof.

First part: On the one hand, the existence of an extended isometry

4
Radon-Domain Sampling Functionals

The canonical sampling functional that acts on continuous functions expressed in hyper-spherical coordinates is the shifted hyper-spherical Dirac distribution
\delta_{{\boldsymbol{z}}_{0}}\in{\mathcal{M}}(\mathbb{R}\times\mathbb{S}^{d-1})
with
{\boldsymbol{z}}_{0}=(t_{0},{\boldsymbol{\xi}}_{0})\in\mathbb{R}\times\mathbb{S}^{d-1}
and
\|\delta_{{\boldsymbol{z}}_{0}}\|_{{\mathcal{M}}}=1
, which also happens to be a “formal” filtered projection of
\delta({\boldsymbol{\xi}}_{0}^{\mathsf{T}}{\boldsymbol{x}}-t_{0})

We now show how we can describe
[\delta_{{\boldsymbol{z}}_{0}}]\in{\mathcal{S}}^{\prime}_{\rm Rad}
by its unique representer
e_{{\boldsymbol{z}}_{0}}:g\mapsto g({\boldsymbol{z}}_{0})
in
{\mathcal{M}}_{\rm Rad}=(C_{0,{\rm Rad}})^{\prime}
.
We start with a theoretical investigation where
e_{{\boldsymbol{z}}_{0}}
is characterized indirectly through its functional properties. We then provide an explicit construction that allows us to identity

e_{{\boldsymbol{z}}_{0}}
as the limit of a normalized Radon-compatible distribution whose unit mass get concentrated at
{\boldsymbol{z}}=\pm{\boldsymbol{z}}_{0}
.

4.1
Abstract Characterization of Radon-Compatible Diracs

Since
{\mathcal{S}}_{\rm Rad}\subseteq{\mathcal{S}}_{\rm even}(\mathbb{R}\times\mathbb{S}^{d-1})
(see Theorem
9
),
we have that
C_{0,\rm Rad}\subseteq C_{0,\rm even}(\mathbb{R}\times\mathbb{S}^{d-1})
,
which implies that all functions
g\in C_{0,\rm Rad}
are continuous and even.
This ensures that the evaluation functional

e_{{\boldsymbol{z}}_{0}}:g\mapsto\langle e_{{\boldsymbol{z}}_{0}},f\rangle=g({\boldsymbol{z}}_{0})
is well-defined for any
{\boldsymbol{z}}_{0}\in\mathbb{R}\times\mathbb{S}^{d-1}
.
We now prove that
e_{{\boldsymbol{z}}_{0}}
is a continuous linear functional on

C_{0,\rm Rad}
—that is,
e_{{\boldsymbol{z}}_{0}}\in(C_{0,\rm Rad})^{\prime}={\mathcal{M}}_{\rm Rad}
(the space of Radon-compatible measures)—and establish its basic properties, which are compabible with those of the Dirac distribution.
By the same token, we get a characterization of the extreme points of the unit ball in
{\mathcal{M}}_{\rm Rad}

Theorem 8

(Properties of

)

The Radon-domain functionals

(43)

Let

5.

4.2

In complement to the abstract characterization of

\displaystyle g_{\epsilon}({\boldsymbol{x}})

\displaystyle=\frac{\epsilon^{d-1}}{(2\pi)^{d/2}}\exp\left(-\frac{1}{2}\big(\frac{x^{2}_{1}}{\epsilon^{2}}+\epsilon^{2}(x_{2}^{2}+\cdots+x_{d}^{2})\big)\right)\in{\mathcal{S}}(\mathbb{R}^{d})

\displaystyle\hat{g}_{\epsilon}({\boldsymbol{{\boldsymbol{\omega}}}})=\exp\left(-\frac{1}{2}\big(\epsilon^{2}\omega_{1}^{2}+\frac{\omega_{2}^{2}+\cdots+\omega_{d}^{2}}{\epsilon^{2}}\big)\right).

\displaystyle\hat{g}_{\epsilon}(\omega{\boldsymbol{\xi}})

\displaystyle=\exp\left(-\frac{\omega^{2}}{2}\sigma_{\epsilon}^{2}({\boldsymbol{\xi}})\right)\mbox{ with }\sigma_{\epsilon}^{2}({\boldsymbol{\xi}})=\epsilon^{2}\xi_{1}^{2}+\frac{\xi_{2}^{2}+\cdots+\xi_{d}^{2}}{\epsilon^{2}}.

\displaystyle d_{\epsilon}(t,{\boldsymbol{\xi}})={\mathrm{R}}\{g_{\epsilon}\}(t,{\boldsymbol{\xi}})

{\boldsymbol{z}}=(0,{\boldsymbol{e}}_{1})

\int_{\mathbb{S}^{d-1}}\int_{\mathbb{R}}d_{\epsilon}(t,{\boldsymbol{\xi}}){\rm d}t{\rm d}{\boldsymbol{\xi}}=\int_{\mathbb{S}^{d-1}}{\rm d}{\boldsymbol{\xi}}=\frac{2\pi^{d/2}}{\Gamma(d/2)}
for any
\epsilon>0
.
This allows us to identify our Radon-compatible sampling functional as

\displaystyle e_{(t_{0},{\boldsymbol{\xi}}_{0})}(t,{\boldsymbol{\xi}})=\lim_{\epsilon\to 0+}d_{\epsilon}(t-{\boldsymbol{\xi}}^{\mathsf{T}}{\boldsymbol{x}}_{0},{\bf{U}}_{0}{\boldsymbol{\xi}}),

(48)

where
{\bf{U}}_{0}\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times d}
is a rotation matrix such that
{\boldsymbol{e}}_{1}={\bf{U}}_{0}{\boldsymbol{\xi}}_{0}

and
{\boldsymbol{x}}_{0}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
is such that
{\boldsymbol{\xi}}^{\mathsf{T}}{\boldsymbol{x}}_{0}=t_{0}
. Examples of such functions for
d=2
,
{\boldsymbol{\xi}}_{0}={\boldsymbol{e}}_{1}=(1,0)
, and
{\boldsymbol{x}}_{0}=(2,2)
are shown in Figure

.

5
Ridges Revisited

A 1D profile along the direction
{\boldsymbol{e}}_{1}=(1,0,\dots,0)
is a generalized function of the form

r_{{\boldsymbol{e}}_{1}}({\boldsymbol{x}})=r(x_{1})\times 1
with
r\in{\mathcal{S}}^{\prime}(\mathbb{R})
. Since the latter is separable, its generalized Fourier transform
is

\displaystyle\mathcal{F}\{r_{{\boldsymbol{e}}_{1}}\}({\boldsymbol{\omega}})=\hat{r}(\omega_{1})\prod_{k=2}^{d}2\pi\delta(\omega_{k})=\hat{r}(\omega_{1})(2\pi)^{d-1}\delta(\omega_{2},\dots,\omega_{d}),

(49)

which is a weighted Dirac mass localized along the
\omega_{1}
axis.
An equivalent formulation of (
49
) that involves test functions is

\displaystyle\forall\varphi\in{\mathcal{S}}(\mathbb{R}^{d}):\langle r_{{\boldsymbol{e}}_{1}},\varphi\rangle=\frac{1}{2\pi}\int_{\mathbb{R}}\hat{r}(\omega)\hat{\varphi}(\omega{\boldsymbol{e}}_{1}){\rm d}\omega

(50)

where
\hat{\varphi}\in{\mathcal{S}}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
is the
d
-dimensional Fourier transform of
\varphi
.
The argument remains valid when we rotate the coordinate system, which allows us to consider more general ridges of the form

r_{{\boldsymbol{\xi}}_{0}}({\boldsymbol{x}})\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\vartriangle}}{{=}}r({\boldsymbol{\xi}}_{0}^{\mathsf{T}}{\boldsymbol{x}})
; that is, 1D profiles along the direction
{\boldsymbol{\xi}}_{0}\in\mathbb{S}{d-1}
.

5.1
Generalized Ridges

By identifying
\hat{\varphi}(\omega{\boldsymbol{e}}_{1})
in (
50
) as the 1D Fourier transform of
{\mathrm{R}}\{\varphi(\cdot,{\boldsymbol{e}}_{1})\}
and by substituting
{\boldsymbol{e}}_{1}
by
{\boldsymbol{\xi}}_{0}
, we obtain the general signal-domain relation:

\displaystyle\forall\varphi\in{\mathcal{S}}(\mathbb{R}^{d}):\quad\langle r_{{\boldsymbol{\xi}}_{0}},\varphi\rangle=\langle r,{\mathrm{R}}\{\varphi\}(\cdot,{\boldsymbol{\xi}}_{0})\rangle,

(51)

which will be referred to as the
ridge identity
. Under the assumption that
r
is a locally integrable function,
we can establish (
51
) by making the change of coordinates
{\boldsymbol{y}}={\bf{U}}{\boldsymbol{x}}
, where

{\bf{U}}\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times d}
is any rotation matrix such that
y_{1}={\boldsymbol{\xi}}_{0}^{\mathsf{T}}{\boldsymbol{x}}
. We then rewrite the integral explicitly as

\displaystyle\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\varphi({\boldsymbol{x}})r_{{\boldsymbol{\xi}}_{0}}({\boldsymbol{x}}){\rm d}{\boldsymbol{x}}

\displaystyle=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\varphi({\boldsymbol{y}})r(y_{1}){\rm d}y_{1}\dots{\rm d}y_{d}

\displaystyle=\int_{\mathbb{R}}\underbrace{\left(\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d-1}}\varphi({\boldsymbol{y}}){\rm d}y_{2}\dots{\rm d}y_{d}\right)}_{{\mathrm{R}}\varphi(y_{1},{\boldsymbol{\xi}}_{0})}r(y_{1}){\rm d}y_{1}.

Otherwise, when
r\in{\mathcal{S}}^{\prime}(\mathbb{R})
has no pointwise interpretation, we simply use (
51
) as definition for the ridge distribution
r_{{\boldsymbol{\xi}}_{0}}\in{\mathcal{S}}^{\prime}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
, which is legitimate since
{\mathrm{R}}\{\varphi\}(\cdot,{\boldsymbol{\xi}}_{0})\in{\mathcal{S}}(\mathbb{R})
.

Note that the special case of (
51
) with
r(t)=\mathrm{e}^{-\mathrm{j}\omega t}
and

r_{\boldsymbol{\xi}}({\boldsymbol{x}})=\mathrm{e}^{-\mathrm{j}\omega{\boldsymbol{\xi}}^{\mathsf{T}}{\boldsymbol{x}}}=\mathrm{e}^{-\mathrm{j}{\boldsymbol{\omega}}^{\mathsf{T}}{\boldsymbol{x}}}
yields the Fourier-slice theorem (
17
).

The ridge identity (
51
) also helps us delineate the
range of the Radon transform. Take
r(t)=t^{k}
and define

\Phi_{k}({\boldsymbol{\xi}})\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\vartriangle}}{{=}}\int_{\mathbb{R}}{\mathrm{R}}\{\varphi\}(t,{\boldsymbol{\xi}})\;t^{k}{\rm d}t=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\varphi({\boldsymbol{x}})({\boldsymbol{\xi}}^{\mathsf{T}}{\boldsymbol{x}})^{k}{\rm d}{\boldsymbol{x}}=\sum_{|{\boldsymbol{k}}|=k}a_{{\boldsymbol{k}}}{\boldsymbol{\xi}}^{\boldsymbol{k}}

with
a_{{\boldsymbol{k}}}=k!\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\frac{{\boldsymbol{x}}^{\boldsymbol{k}}}{{\boldsymbol{k}}!}\varphi({\boldsymbol{x}}){\rm d}{\boldsymbol{x}}
.

Theorem 9

(
Gelfand and Shilov 1966
;
Helgason 2011
;
Ludwig 1966
)
.

A hyper-spherical test function
\phi\in{\mathcal{S}}(\mathbb{R}\times\mathbb{S}^{d-1})
is a valid Radon transform in the sense that
\phi\in{\mathcal{S}}_{\rm Rad}=\{{\mathrm{R}}\{\varphi\}:\varphi\in{\mathcal{S}}(\mathbb{R}^{d})\}
if and only if

1.

Evenness:
\phi(t,{\boldsymbol{\xi}})=\phi(-t,-{\boldsymbol{\xi}})
.

2.

\Phi_{k}({\boldsymbol{\xi}})=\int_{\mathbb{R}}\phi(t,{\boldsymbol{\xi}})t^{k}{\rm d}t
is a homogeneous polynomial in
{\boldsymbol{\xi}}\in\mathbb{S}^{d-1}
for any
k\in\mathbb{N}
.

In particular, for
k=0
, we must have that
\int_{\mathbb{R}}\phi(t,{\boldsymbol{\xi}}){\rm d}t=a_{{\boldsymbol{0}}}={\rm Constant}
for all

{\boldsymbol{\xi}}\in\mathbb{S}^{d-1}
.

The most basic version of a ridge is
\delta({\boldsymbol{\xi}}_{0}^{\mathsf{T}}{\boldsymbol{x}}-t_{0})
with
r=\delta(\cdot-t_{0})
, which is a Dirac ridge along
{\boldsymbol{\xi}}_{0}
with offset
t_{0}
.
Since the Fourier transform of such ridges is entirely localized along the ray
\{{\boldsymbol{\omega}}=\omega{\boldsymbol{\xi}}_{0}:\omega\in\mathbb{R}\}
, we can expect their Radon transform to vanish
away from
\pm{\boldsymbol{\xi}}_{0}
. The latter can be readily identified as follows, where the square bracket notation
[g]
with

g\in{\mathcal{S}}^{\prime}(\mathbb{R}\times\mathbb{S}^{d-1})
reminds us that the members of

{\mathcal{S}}^{\prime}_{\rm Rad}
(resp., of
{\mathrm{K}}_{\rm rad}{\mathrm{R}}\big({\mathcal{S}}(\mathbb{R}^{d})\big)^{\prime}

are equivalence classes of distributions.

Proposition 10

(Radon transform of ridge distributions)
.

Let
(t_{0},{\boldsymbol{\xi}}_{0})={\boldsymbol{z}}_{0}\in\mathbb{R}\times\mathbb{S}^{d-1}
and
r\in{\mathcal{S}}^{\prime}(\mathbb{R})
.
Then,

\displaystyle{\mathrm{K}}_{\rm rad}{\mathrm{R}}\{\delta({\boldsymbol{\xi}}_{0}^{\mathsf{T}}{\boldsymbol{x}}-t_{0})\}(t,{\boldsymbol{\xi}})

\displaystyle=[\delta(\cdot-t_{0})\delta(\cdot-{\boldsymbol{\xi}}_{0})]\quad\in{\mathcal{S}}^{\prime}_{\rm Rad}

\displaystyle{\mathrm{R}}\{\delta({\boldsymbol{\xi}}_{0}^{\mathsf{T}}{\boldsymbol{x}})\}(t,{\boldsymbol{\xi}})

\displaystyle=[q_{d}(t)\delta({\boldsymbol{\xi}}-{\boldsymbol{\xi}}_{0})]\quad\in{\mathrm{K}}_{\rm rad}{\mathrm{R}}\big({\mathcal{S}}(\mathbb{R}^{d})\big)^{\prime}

\displaystyle{\mathrm{K}}_{\rm rad}{\mathrm{R}}\{r({\boldsymbol{\xi}}_{0}^{\mathsf{T}}{\boldsymbol{x}})\}(t,{\boldsymbol{\xi}})

\displaystyle=[r(t)\delta({\boldsymbol{\xi}}-{\boldsymbol{\xi}}_{0})]\quad\in{\mathcal{S}}^{\prime}_{\rm Rad}

\displaystyle{\mathrm{R}}\{r({\boldsymbol{\xi}}_{0}^{\mathsf{T}}{\boldsymbol{x}})\}(t,{\boldsymbol{\xi}})

\displaystyle=[(q_{d}\ast r)(t)\delta({\boldsymbol{\xi}}-{\boldsymbol{\xi}}_{0})]\quad\in{\mathrm{K}}_{\rm rad}{\mathrm{R}}\big({\mathcal{S}}(\mathbb{R}^{d})\big)^{\prime}

where
q_{d}(t)=2(2\pi)^{d-1}\mathcal{F}^{-1}\{1/|\omega|^{d-1}\}(t)
is the 1D impulse response of the Radon-domain inverse filtering operator
{\mathrm{K}}_{\rm rad}^{-1}
.

Proof.

The fact that
\delta_{{\boldsymbol{z}}_{0}}=\delta(t-t_{0})\delta({\boldsymbol{\xi}}-{\boldsymbol{\xi}}_{0})
is a formal filtered projection of
\delta({\boldsymbol{\xi}}_{0}^{\mathsf{T}}{\boldsymbol{x}}-t_{0})
has already been mentioned in the text—it is a direct consequence of Definition (
15
).

To derive the third identity, we observe that, for all
\varphi\in{\mathcal{S}}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
, one has that

\displaystyle\langle r(\cdot)\delta(\cdot-{\boldsymbol{\xi}}_{0}),{\mathrm{R}}\{\varphi\}\rangle_{\rm Rad}

\displaystyle=\int_{\mathbb{R}}r(t){\mathrm{R}}\{\varphi\}(t,{\boldsymbol{\xi}}_{0}){\rm d}t=\langle r,{\mathrm{R}}\{\varphi\}(\cdot,{\boldsymbol{\xi}}_{0})\rangle

\displaystyle=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}r({\boldsymbol{\xi}}_{0}^{\mathsf{T}}{\boldsymbol{x}})\varphi({\boldsymbol{x}}){\rm d}{\boldsymbol{x}}=\langle r({\boldsymbol{\xi}}_{0}^{\mathsf{T}}\cdot),{\mathrm{R}}^{\ast}{\mathrm{K}}_{\rm rad}\{{\mathrm{R}}\varphi\}\rangle,

where we have made use of (
51
).
By setting
\phi={\mathrm{R}}\{\varphi\}\in{\mathcal{S}}_{\rm rad}
, we then refer to (
22
) to deduce that

r(t)\delta({\boldsymbol{\xi}}-{\boldsymbol{\xi}}_{0})
is a formal filtered projection of
r({\boldsymbol{\xi}}_{0}^{\mathsf{T}}{\boldsymbol{x}})
.
This then also yields the second and fourth identities by substituting
r
with

q_{d}(\cdot-t_{0})
and
q_{d}\ast r
, respectively.
The additional element there is
r({\boldsymbol{\xi}}_{0}^{\mathsf{T}}{\boldsymbol{x}})={\mathrm{K}}{\mathrm{K}}^{-1}\{r({\boldsymbol{\xi}}_{0}^{\mathsf{T}}{\boldsymbol{x}})\}({\boldsymbol{x}})={\mathrm{K}}\{\tilde{r}({\boldsymbol{\xi}}_{0}^{\mathsf{T}}{\boldsymbol{x}})\}({\boldsymbol{x}})
with
\tilde{r}(t)=(q_{d}\ast r)(t)
, which is readily verified in the Fourier domain.

∎

An equivalent form of the first identity in Proposition
10
is

\displaystyle\delta({\boldsymbol{\xi}}_{0}^{\mathsf{T}}{\boldsymbol{x}}-t_{0})

\displaystyle={\mathrm{R}}^{\ast}\{\delta_{(t_{0},{\boldsymbol{\xi}}_{0})}\}({\boldsymbol{x}}),

(52)

which results from
{\mathrm{R}}^{\ast}{\mathrm{K}}_{\rm rad}{\mathrm{R}}=\mathrm{Id}
on
{\mathcal{S}}^{\prime}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
. Likewise, when the back-projection in (

\displaystyle{\rm L}{\mathrm{R}}^{\ast}\{\delta_{(t_{0},{\boldsymbol{\xi}}_{0})}\}({\boldsymbol{x}})={\mathrm{R}}^{\ast}\{{\rm L}_{\rm rad}\delta_{(t_{0},{\boldsymbol{\xi}}_{0})}\}({\boldsymbol{x}})=r({\boldsymbol{\xi}}_{0}^{\mathsf{T}}{\boldsymbol{x}}-t_{0})

where

.

5.2
Connection with Prior Works

Most authors who use the Radon transform in connection with neural networks do not distinguish “formal” from “range-compatible” Radon transforms of distributions
(
Candès 1999
;
Sonoda and Murata 2017
;
Ongie et al. 2020
;
Parhi and Nowak 2021
)
. They bypass the difficulty by focusing their analysis on some appropriate subspace of

{\mathcal{S}}^{\prime}_{\rm Rad}
over which the backpropagation operator
{\mathrm{R}}^{\ast}
is known to be invertible, for instance
{\mathcal{M}}_{\rm even}(\mathbb{R}\times\mathbb{S}^{d-1})={\mathcal{M}}(\mathbb{P}^{d})
and/or
{\mathcal{S}}^{\prime}_{\rm Liz,even}
.
To specify their norm for Radon-domain measures,
Ongie et al. 2020
consider the subspace of test functions

{\mathcal{S}}_{\rm even}=\{\phi\in{\mathcal{S}}(\mathbb{R}\times\mathbb{S}^{d}):\phi(t,{\boldsymbol{\xi}})=\phi(-t,-{\boldsymbol{\xi}})\}
for which the validity of the inversion formula
{\mathrm{K}}_{\rm rad}{\mathrm{R}}{\mathrm{R}}^{\ast}=\mathrm{Id}

has been established by
Solmon 1987
. The caveat is that the functions
f\in{\mathrm{R}}^{\ast}({\mathcal{S}}_{\rm even})
that are in the range of
{\mathrm{R}}^{\ast}
can decay as badly as
O(1/\|{\boldsymbol{x}}\|)

(
Ramm and Katsevich 2020
, Corollary 3.1.1, p. 73)
, meaning that their “classical” Radon transform specified by (
15
) can be ill-defined.

Parhi and Nowak 2021
follow a different path and identify
{\mathcal{M}}_{\rm even}(\mathbb{R}\times\mathbb{S}^{d-1})
as a subspace of the space of even Lizorkin distributions
{\mathcal{S}}^{\prime}_{\rm Liz,even}
, which is the topological dual of
{\mathcal{S}}_{\rm Liz,even}=\{\phi\in{\mathcal{S}}_{\rm even}:\int_{\mathbb{R}}\phi(t,{\boldsymbol{\xi}})t^{k}=0,\forall k\in\mathbb{N},{\boldsymbol{\xi}}\in\mathbb{S}^{d-1}\}
.
Implicit in the calculation of Example 1 in
(
Ongie et al. 2020
)
is the property that

\displaystyle{\mathrm{K}}_{\rm rad}{\mathrm{R}}\{\delta({\boldsymbol{\xi}}_{0}^{\mathsf{T}}{\boldsymbol{x}}-t_{0})\}(t,{\boldsymbol{\xi}})=\frac{1}{2}\Big(\delta(t-t_{0})\delta({\boldsymbol{\xi}}-{\boldsymbol{\xi}}_{0})+\delta(t+t_{0})\delta({\boldsymbol{\xi}}+{\boldsymbol{\xi}}_{0})\Big),

(54)

which needs to be related to the “abstract” description of
e_{{\boldsymbol{z}}_{0}}
in Theorem
8
.
As it turns out,
the two forms are equivalent. The abstract version, combined with the properties in Theorem
8
, conveys the same information as (
54
). In complement is the concrete representation of
e_{(t_{0},{\boldsymbol{\xi}}_{0})}(t,{\boldsymbol{\xi}})
given by (
48
), which gives us a sense of how and why the Radon-domain mass concentrates around the points

\pm{\boldsymbol{z}}_{0}
as the Gaussian “blob”
g_{\epsilon}
gets thinner along the primary axis and elongates in the perpendicular directions.

Even though the test functions used in the listed works are different from ours
with
{\mathcal{S}}_{\rm Liz}\subset{\mathcal{S}}_{\rm Rad}\subset{\mathcal{S}}_{\rm even}
, the approaches are reconciled by invoking the property that

\displaystyle C_{0,{\rm even}}=\overline{({\mathcal{S}}_{\rm even},\|\cdot\|_{L_{\infty}})}=\overline{({\mathcal{S}}_{\rm Liz},\|\cdot\|_{L_{\infty}})}=\overline{({\mathcal{S}}_{\rm Rad},\|\cdot\|_{L_{\infty}})}=C_{0,{\rm Rad}}

(55)

where the domain of all underlying spaces is
(\mathbb{R}\times\mathbb{S}^{d-1})

(
Neumayer and Unser 2022
, Lemma 1)
. While Proposition
10
gives the filtered Radon transform of ridges with the greatest possible level of generality, the caveat is that
{\mathrm{K}}_{\rm rad}{\mathrm{R}}\{r_{{\boldsymbol{\xi}}_{0}}\}\in{\mathcal{S}}^{\prime}_{\rm Rad}
is an abstract equivalence class.
As complement, we are providing the “concrete” version of the main result for the case where the profile is a measure.

Corollary 11

(Filtered Radon Transform of Ridge Measures)
.

Let
r_{{\boldsymbol{\xi}}_{0}}=r({\boldsymbol{\xi}}_{0}^{\mathsf{T}}{\boldsymbol{x}})
be the ridge with profile
r\in{\mathcal{S}}^{\prime}(\mathbb{R})
and direction
{\boldsymbol{\xi}}_{0}\in\mathbb{S}^{d-1}
. If
r\in{\mathcal{M}}(\mathbb{R})
, then the equality

\displaystyle{\mathrm{K}}_{\rm rad}{\mathrm{R}}\{r_{{\boldsymbol{\xi}}_{0}}\}(t,{\boldsymbol{\xi}})

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\big(r(t)\delta({\boldsymbol{\xi}}-{\boldsymbol{\xi}}_{0})+r(-t)\delta({\boldsymbol{\xi}}+{\boldsymbol{\xi}}_{0})\big)

(56)

holds in
{\mathcal{M}}_{\rm Rad}={\mathcal{M}}_{\rm even}(\mathbb{R}\times\mathbb{S}^{d-1})
.

Indeed, we know that
r(t)\delta({\boldsymbol{\xi}}-{\boldsymbol{\xi}}_{0})
is a formal filtered Radon transform of
r_{{\boldsymbol{\xi}}_{0}}
and that it is included in
{\mathcal{M}}(\mathbb{R}\times\mathbb{S}^{d-1})
if
r\in{\mathcal{M}}(\mathbb{R})
.
Moreover, Theorem
7
with
{\mathcal{X}}=C_{0}(\mathbb{R}\times\mathbb{S}^{d-1})
, together with (
55
), implies that
{\mathcal{M}}_{\rm Rad}=(C_{0,{\rm even}})^{\prime}={\mathcal{M}}_{\rm even}
, whose Banach complement in
{\mathcal{M}}(\mathbb{R}\times\mathbb{S}^{d-1})
is
{\mathcal{M}}_{\rm odd}
. This ensures the validity of the second part of Theorem
7
with
{\mathrm{P}}^{\ast}_{\rm Rad}={\mathrm{P}}_{\rm even}
, where
{\mathrm{P}}_{\rm even}\{g\}(t,{\boldsymbol{\xi}})=\tfrac{1}{2}\big(g(t,{\boldsymbol{\xi}})+g(-t,-{\boldsymbol{\xi}})\big)
.
Accordingly, we can identify
{\mathrm{P}}_{\rm even}\{r(t)\delta({\boldsymbol{\xi}}-{\boldsymbol{\xi}}_{0})\}
as the unique representer in
{\mathcal{M}}_{\rm Rad}
of
{\mathrm{K}}_{\rm rad}{\mathrm{R}}\{r_{{\boldsymbol{\xi}}_{0}}\}=[r(t)\delta({\boldsymbol{\xi}}-{\boldsymbol{\xi}}_{0})]\in{\mathcal{S}}_{\rm Rad}^{\prime}
.

The argument also suggests that (
56
) is likely to be extendable to broader families of distributions. The condition for its validity is that
r(t)\delta({\boldsymbol{\xi}}-{\boldsymbol{\xi}}_{0})

be included in a space
{\mathcal{X}}^{\prime}=\big({\mathcal{X}}_{\rm Rad}\oplus{\mathcal{X}}^{\rm c}_{\rm Rad}\big)^{\prime}

with
{\mathcal{X}}_{\rm Rad}=\overline{({\mathcal{S}}_{\rm Rad},\|\cdot\|_{{\mathcal{X}}})}=\overline{({\mathcal{S}}_{\rm even},\|\cdot\|_{{\mathcal{X}}})}
, so that the underlying projector can be readily identified as
{\mathrm{P}}^{\ast}_{\rm Rad}={\mathrm{P}}_{\rm even}:{\mathcal{X}}^{\prime}\to{\mathcal{X}}^{\prime}_{\rm Rad}
.

6
Variational Optimality of ReLU Networks

As application of the proposed formalism, we shall now link ReLU neural networks with functional optimization, revisiting the energy-minimization property uncovered in
(
Ongie et al. 2020
)
as well as the general variational-learning problem investigated in
(
Parhi and Nowak 2021
)
.

6.1
Learning with Radon-Domain Regularization

In order to state the relevant optimization problem, we consider the regularization operator

\displaystyle\Delta_{\rm R}={\mathrm{K}}_{\rm rad}{\mathrm{R}}\Delta:{\mathcal{M}}_{{\mathrm{\Delta}}_{\rm R}}(\mathbb{R}^{d})\to{\mathcal{M}}(\mathbb{R}\times\mathbb{S}^{d-1})

(57)

that was first proposed by
Ongie et al. 2020
, where
\Delta
is the
d
-dimensional Laplace operator. The
{\mathcal{M}}
-norm (a.k.a. total variation)
of this “Radonized” Laplacian is then used as regularization. The corresponding native space
{\mathcal{M}}_{{\mathrm{\Delta}}_{\rm R}}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
is the largest Banach space over which this seminorm is well-defined under the constraint that the null space of
\Delta_{\rm R}
be limited to polynomials of degree
1
.

Theorem 12

(Optimality of Shallow ReLU Networks)
.

Let
E:\mathbb{R}\times\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R}
be a strictly convex loss function,

({\boldsymbol{x}}_{m},y_{m})\in\mathbb{R}^{d}\times\mathbb{R}
with
m=1,\dots,M
a given set of distinct data points, and
\lambda>0
some fixed regularization parameter.
Then, for
M>d+1
, the solution set

\displaystyle S=\left\{\arg\min_{f\in{\mathcal{M}}_{\Delta_{\rm R}}(\mathbb{R}^{d})}\left(\sum_{m=1}^{M}E(y_{m},f({\boldsymbol{x}}_{m}))+\lambda\|\Delta_{\rm R}f\|_{{\mathcal{M}}}\right)\right\},

(58)

of the
functional optimization problem
is nonempty and weak* compact. It is the weak* closure of the convex hull of its extreme points, which all take the form

\displaystyle f_{\rm ridge}({\boldsymbol{x}})=b+{\boldsymbol{b}}^{\top}{\boldsymbol{x}}+\sum_{k=1}^{K_{0}}a_{k}{\rm ReLU}({\boldsymbol{\xi}}_{k}^{\mathsf{T}}{\boldsymbol{x}}-\tau_{k})

(59)

with
(b,{\boldsymbol{b}})\in\mathbb{R}\times\mathbb{R}^{d}
, and a number
K_{0}<M
of adaptive ridges with weight, direction, and offset parameters
(a_{k},{\boldsymbol{\xi}}_{k},\tau_{k})\in\mathbb{R}\times\mathbb{S}^{d-1}\times\mathbb{R}
. The corresponding regularization cost, which is common to all solutions, is
\|\Delta_{\rm R}f_{\rm ridge}\|_{{\mathcal{M}}}=\sum_{k=1}^{K_{0}}|a_{k}|
.

The key here is that the search space
{\mathcal{M}}_{\Delta_{\rm R}}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
is isometrically isomorphic to
{\mathcal{M}}_{\rm Rad}\times{\mathcal{P}}_{1}
, where

{\mathcal{M}}_{\rm Rad}=\big(C_{0,\rm Rad}\big)^{\prime}

(see Section
3
with
\|\cdot\|_{{\mathcal{X}}}=\|\cdot\|_{L_{\infty}}

and
{\mathcal{X}}_{\rm Rad}=C_{0,{\rm Rad}}
)
and

{\mathcal{P}}_{1}
is the space of polynomials of degree
1
specified by
(
7
) with
n_{0}=1
. An equivalent representation of the latter space of affine functions on
\mathbb{R}^{d}
is
{\mathcal{P}}_{1}=\{b+{\boldsymbol{b}}^{\mathsf{T}}{\boldsymbol{x}}\}
with
b=b_{\boldsymbol{0}}
and
{\boldsymbol{b}}=(b_{{\boldsymbol{e}}_{i}})_{i=1}^{d}
, which matches the leading term in (
59
).

The crucial element for this construction is the pseudoinverse operator

\displaystyle\Delta_{\rm R}^{\dagger}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\vartriangle}}{{=}}(\mathrm{Id}-\mathrm{Proj}_{{\mathcal{P}}_{1}})\Delta^{-1}{\mathrm{R}}^{\ast}:{\mathcal{M}}_{\rm Rad}\to{\mathcal{M}}_{\Delta_{\rm R}}(\mathbb{R}^{d}),

(60)

where
\mathrm{Proj}_{{\mathcal{P}}_{1}}:{\mathcal{S}}^{\prime}(\mathbb{R}^{d})\to{\mathcal{P}}_{1}
is the projector defined by (
13
)
with
n_{0}=1
.
The native space is then given by

\displaystyle{\mathcal{M}}_{{\mathrm{\Delta}}_{\rm R}}(\mathbb{R}^{d})

\displaystyle=\Delta_{\rm R}^{\dagger}\big({\mathcal{M}}_{\rm Rad}\big)\oplus{\mathcal{P}}_{1}

\displaystyle=\{\Delta_{\rm R}^{\dagger}\{w\}+p_{0}:(w,p_{0})\in{\mathcal{M}}_{\rm Rad}\times{\mathcal{P}}_{1}\}

(61)

equipped with the composite norm induced by
{\mathcal{M}}_{\rm Rad}\times{\mathcal{P}}_{1}
.
Equivalently, given the generic form of
f
in (
61
), it is possible to retrieve the components
(w,p_{0})
with the help of suitable linear maps. Specifically, letting
f=\Delta_{\rm R}^{\dagger}\{w\}+p_{0}
, one verifies that

\displaystyle\Delta_{\rm R}\{f\}

\displaystyle=\Delta_{\rm R}\{\Delta_{\rm R}^{\dagger}w\}+\Delta_{\rm R}\{p_{0}\}

\displaystyle={\mathrm{K}}_{\rm rad}{\mathrm{R}}\Delta(\mathrm{Id}-\mathrm{Proj}_{{\mathcal{P}}_{1}})\Delta^{-1}{\mathrm{R}}^{\ast}\{w\}+0

\displaystyle={\mathrm{K}}_{\rm rad}{\mathrm{R}}\Delta\Delta^{-1}{\mathrm{R}}^{\ast}\{w\}-{\mathrm{K}}_{\rm rad}{\mathrm{R}}\underbrace{\Delta\{\mathrm{Proj}_{{\mathcal{P}}_{1}}\Delta^{-1}{\mathrm{R}}^{\ast}w\}}_{0}

\displaystyle={\mathrm{K}}_{\rm rad}{\mathrm{R}}{\mathrm{R}}^{\ast}\{w\}={\mathrm{P}}^{\ast}_{\rm Rad}\{w\}=w\in{\mathcal{M}}_{\rm Rad}

and

\displaystyle\mathrm{Proj}_{{\mathcal{P}}_{1}}\{f\}

\displaystyle=\mathrm{Proj}_{{\mathcal{P}}_{1}}\{\Delta_{\rm R}^{\dagger}w\}+\mathrm{Proj}_{{\mathcal{P}}_{1}}\{p_{0}\}

\displaystyle=\mathrm{Proj}_{{\mathcal{P}}_{1}}(\mathrm{Id}-\mathrm{Proj}_{{\mathcal{P}}_{1}})\Delta^{-1}{\mathrm{R}}^{\ast}\{w\}+p_{0}=0+p_{0}\in{\mathcal{P}}_{1},

where the annihilation of the
w
-component follows from the
idempotence of the projector.

Proof of Theorem
12
.

Theorem
7
with
{\mathcal{X}}=C_{0}(\mathbb{R}\times\mathbb{S}^{d-1})

ensures that the back-projection operator
{\mathrm{R}}^{\ast}
is invertible on
{\mathcal{X}}^{\prime}_{\rm Rad}={\mathcal{M}}_{\rm Rad}=(C_{0,\rm Rad})^{\prime}
.
This is the fundamental ingredient that makes

{\mathcal{M}}_{\Delta_{\rm R}}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
isometrically isomorphic to
{\mathcal{M}}_{\rm Rad}\times{\mathcal{P}}_{1}
via the reversible mapping
w=\Delta_{\rm R}\{f\}\in{\mathcal{M}}_{\rm Rad}
and
p_{0}=\mathrm{Proj}_{{\mathcal{P}}_{1}}\{f\}\in{\mathcal{P}}_{1}
.
This equivalent representation of
f
enables us to derive the result as a corollary of the third case of the abstract representer theorem for direct sums in

(
Unser and Aziznejad 2022
, Theorem 3)
. This representer theorem gives the generic form of the extreme points of the solution set
S
as
f_{\rm extreme}=p_{0}+\sum_{k=1}^{K_{0}}a_{k}e_{k}
, where
p_{0}\in{\mathcal{P}}_{1}
is a null-space component and where the
e_{k}
are extreme points of the unit ball
B_{{\mathcal{U}}^{\prime}}
of the primary-component space
{\mathcal{U}}^{\prime}=\Delta_{\rm R}^{\dagger}\big({\mathcal{M}}_{\rm Rad}\big)
.
Based on the form of the extreme points of
{\mathcal{M}}_{\rm Rad}
given by Theorem

and the property that
{\rm Ext}B_{{\mathcal{U}}^{\prime}}=\Delta_{\rm R}^{\dagger}\big({\rm Ext}B_{{\mathcal{M}}_{\rm Rad}}\big)
(since
\Delta_{\rm R}^{\dagger}
is an isometry), we deduce that any
e_{k}\in{\rm Ext}B_{{\mathcal{U}}^{\prime}}
can be written as

\displaystyle e_{k}

\displaystyle=\pm\Delta_{\rm R}^{\dagger}\{e_{(t_{k},{\boldsymbol{\xi}}_{k})}\}=(\mathrm{Id}-\mathrm{Proj}_{{\mathcal{P}}_{1}})\Delta^{-1}{\mathrm{R}}^{\ast}\{e_{(t_{k},{\boldsymbol{\xi}}_{k})}\}

\displaystyle=\pm(\mathrm{Id}-\mathrm{Proj}_{{\mathcal{P}}_{1}})\{\tfrac{1}{2}|{\boldsymbol{\xi}}_{k}^{\mathsf{T}}\cdot-t_{k}|\}

\displaystyle=\pm\tfrac{1}{2}|{\boldsymbol{\xi}}_{k}^{\mathsf{T}}\cdot-t_{k}|\pm p_{k}\quad\mbox{ with }\quad p_{k}\in{\mathcal{P}}_{1},

where we have used (
53
) with
{\rm L}=\Delta^{-1}
and
\widehat{L}_{\rm rad}(\omega)=1/\omega^{2}
to evaluate the back-projection.
Since
\tfrac{1}{2}|t|=(t)_{+}-\tfrac{1}{2}t
, we can also write
e_{k}
as

\displaystyle e_{k}

\displaystyle=\pm({\boldsymbol{\xi}}_{k}^{\top}\cdot-t_{k})_{+}\pm\tilde{p}_{k},

where

. We then obtain (

) by forming
the linear combination of extreme points and by grouping all polynomial components
in a single term
b+{\boldsymbol{b}}^{\mathsf{T}}{\boldsymbol{x}}
. Since
\Delta_{\rm R}\{f_{\rm ridge}\}=\sum_{k=1}^{K_{0}}a_{k}e_{(t_{k},{\boldsymbol{\xi}}_{k})}
, we also deduce that

6.2
Discussion

Our formulation of Theorem
12

owes a lot to the pioneering works of

Ongie et al. 2020
and
Parhi and Nowak 2021
(PN). Our is merely a refinement of the results
published by these authors together with a clarification of the underlying mathematics.
The interesting outcome is that the solution of the functional-optimization problem in (
58
) can be implemented by a 2-layer ReLU network.

In their work which pulls together ideas from
Unser et al. 2017
and
Ongie et al. 2020
, PN restrict the domain of the test functions to the so-called Lizorkin functions

\displaystyle{\mathcal{S}}_{\rm Liz}(\mathbb{R}^{d})=\{\varphi\in{\mathcal{S}}(\mathbb{R}^{d}):\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}{\boldsymbol{x}}^{{\boldsymbol{m}}}\varphi({\boldsymbol{x}}){\rm d}{\boldsymbol{x}}=0,\forall{\boldsymbol{m}}\in\mathbb{N}^{d}\},

(62)

which are orthogonal to the polynomials. This choice is motivated by the property that
the Radon transform is a homeomorphism

{\mathrm{R}}:{\mathcal{S}}_{\rm Liz}(\mathbb{R}^{d})\to{\mathcal{S}}_{\rm Liz,even}
, where

{\mathcal{S}}_{\rm Liz,even}
denotes the even part of the Radon-domain Lizorkin space
{\mathcal{S}}_{\rm Liz}(\mathbb{R}\times\mathbb{S}^{d-1})
with
{\mathrm{R}}^{\ast}{\mathrm{R}}{\mathrm{K}}_{\rm rad}=\mathrm{Id}
on
{\mathcal{S}}_{\rm Liz,even}
, as well as on

{\mathcal{S}}^{\prime}_{\rm Liz,even}
, by duality
(
Helgason 2011
;
Kostadinova et al. 2014
)
.

While the adoption of this formalism leads to a well-defined functional-optimization problem, PN’s derivation/interpretation of Lemmas 17, 18, and 21 is flawed because they implicitly assume that there is a systematic, one-to-one association between
a “concrete” spline ridge
\rho_{m}({\boldsymbol{\xi}}_{0}^{\mathsf{T}}{\boldsymbol{x}}-t_{0})
with
\rho_{m}(t)=\tfrac{1}{2}{\rm sign}(t)\frac{t^{m-1}}{m!}
and some abstract Lizorkin distribution
\rho_{m}({\boldsymbol{\xi}}_{0}^{\mathsf{T}}{\boldsymbol{x}}-t_{0})+{\mathcal{P}}\in{\mathcal{S}}_{\rm Liz}^{\prime}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
, which is unlikely to be the case for the reasons exposed in the introduction. We have recent evidence that such an association can be made, but that it requires a specific polynomial correction that depends on the shift
t_{0}

(
Neumayer and Unser 2022
)
.
That said, it remains that the main results and conclusions reported by
Parhi and Nowak 2021
are qualitatively correct and in agreement with Theorem
12
(up to the mentioned technicalities). Also, the mathematical arguments proposed by these authors can easily be corrected/upgraded by extending their space of test functions to
{\mathcal{S}}_{\rm Rad}
and by using our results in Theorems
7

and
8
.
The same holds true for PN’s higher-order generalizations (ridge splines). In fact, PN’s definition of the native space of the
m
th-order ridge splines is equivalent to that of
{\mathcal{M}}_{{\mathrm{\Delta}}_{\rm R}}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
for
m=2
.

Now, the one aspect where Theorem
12
improves upon

Ongie et al. 2020
and
Parhi and Nowak 2021
is that it contains the characterization of the full solution set. The theorem tells us that
all extreme points
of the optimization problem in (
58
) have the same parametric form (
59
), which is a much stronger statement than the existence of one such neural-network-like solution. Ideally, one would like to identify the sparsest solution within the solution set, in other words, the one with the fewest neurons. While there is a direct algorithm that will find the sparsest solution for
d=1

(
Debarre et al. 2022
)
, it is not known yet if this can be generalized to a larger number of dimensions.

We also like to point out that the functional-learning problem in (
58
) is invariant to similarity transformations of the data points
{\boldsymbol{x}}_{m}
. This is to say that any such transformation of the data characterized by a scale
s
can be accounted for via a proper rescaling of the regularization parameter
\lambda\to s\lambda
.

Proposition 13
.

The regularization functional in (
58
) is translation-, scale- and rotation-invariant in the sense that

\displaystyle\|\Delta_{\rm R}\{f(s{\bf{U}}\cdot-{\bf{b}})\}\|_{{\mathcal{M}}}=s\|\Delta_{\rm R}\{f\}\|_{{\mathcal{M}}}

(63)

for any
f\in{\mathcal{M}}_{\Delta_{\rm R}}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
, and any scaling factor
s\in\mathbb{R}^{+}
, offset
{\bf{b}}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
, and rotation matrix

{\bf{U}}\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times d}
with
{\bf{U}}^{\mathsf{T}}{\bf{U}}={\bf{I}}
.

There has been concern about the well-posedness of the generative model used in
(
Parhi and Nowak 2021
)
and summarized by
f=\Delta_{\rm R}^{\dagger}\{w\}
with
w\in{\mathcal{M}}_{\rm Rad}={\mathcal{M}}_{\rm even}
in the present formulation.
To make the link with
(
Bartolucci et al. 2021
)
, it is instructive to describe our inverse operator
\Delta_{\rm R}^{\dagger}=(\mathrm{Id}-\mathrm{Proj}_{{\mathcal{P}}_{1}})\Delta^{-1}{\mathrm{R}}^{\ast}:{\mathcal{M}}_{\rm Rad}\to{\mathcal{M}}_{\Delta_{\rm R}}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
explicitly in terms of the generic integral equation

\displaystyle\Delta_{\rm R}^{\dagger}\{w\}({\boldsymbol{x}})=\int_{\mathbb{R}}\int_{\mathbb{S}^{d-1}}h({\boldsymbol{x}};t,{\boldsymbol{\xi}})w(t,{\boldsymbol{\xi}}){\rm d}{\boldsymbol{\xi}}{\rm d}t,

(64)

with a “kernel”
h:\mathbb{R}^{d}\times(\mathbb{R}\times\mathbb{S}^{d-1})\to\mathbb{R}
that is given by

\displaystyle h({\boldsymbol{x}};t,{\boldsymbol{\xi}})

\displaystyle=\tfrac{1}{2}|{\boldsymbol{\xi}}^{\mathsf{T}}{\boldsymbol{x}}-t|-\sum_{|{\boldsymbol{k}}|\leq 1}\frac{{\boldsymbol{x}}^{\boldsymbol{k}}}{{\boldsymbol{k}}!}q_{\boldsymbol{k}}(t,{\boldsymbol{\xi}}),

(65)

where
q_{\boldsymbol{k}}(t,{\boldsymbol{\xi}})=\langle\tfrac{1}{2}|{\boldsymbol{\xi}}^{\mathsf{T}}\cdot-t|,m_{\boldsymbol{k}}^{\ast}\rangle
and where the dual basis
(m^{\ast}_{\boldsymbol{k}})_{|{\boldsymbol{k}}|\leq 1}
is specified by (
8
).
This form is compatible with
(
Parhi and Nowak 2021
, Lemma 21)
and has been interpreted as an infinite-width neural network. By evaluating the underlying duality products explicitly and by regrouping the first-order correction terms, we obtain the simplified formula

\displaystyle h({\boldsymbol{x}};t,{\boldsymbol{\xi}})

\displaystyle=\tfrac{1}{2}|t-{\boldsymbol{\xi}}^{\mathsf{T}}{\boldsymbol{x}}|-(\kappa_{\rm rad}\ast\tfrac{1}{2}|\cdot|)(t)+({\boldsymbol{\xi}}^{\mathsf{T}}{\boldsymbol{x}})(\kappa_{\rm rad}\ast\tfrac{1}{2}{\rm sign})(t),

(66)

where the radial convolution with
\kappa_{\rm rad}
has a mollifying effect on the two correction terms.
If we fix
t,{\boldsymbol{\xi}}
in (
66
), then
{\boldsymbol{x}}\mapsto h({\boldsymbol{x}};t,{\boldsymbol{\xi}})
grows like
O(|{\boldsymbol{\xi}}^{\mathsf{T}}{\boldsymbol{x}}|)
with the correction contributing a first-order polynomial. The effect of the correction is more remarkable along the radial variable
t
, in that it neutralizes the growth of the leading term
\tfrac{1}{2}|t-{\boldsymbol{\xi}}^{\mathsf{T}}{\boldsymbol{x}}|=O(|t|)
: This is because the profile
t\mapsto h({\boldsymbol{x}};t,{\boldsymbol{\xi}})
is bounded with a maximum proportional to
|{\boldsymbol{\xi}}^{\mathsf{T}}{\boldsymbol{x}}|
around the origin, continuous (due to the convolution of
{\rm sign}
with
\kappa_{\rm rad}
), and vanishing towards
0
as
t\to\pm\infty
.
This can be translated into the following properties, which are the mathematical bases for the present construction.

1.

For any
{\boldsymbol{x}}_{0}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
,
h({\boldsymbol{x}}_{0};\cdot,\cdot)=\Delta_{\rm R}^{\dagger\ast}\{\delta(\cdot-{\boldsymbol{x}}_{0})\}\in C_{0}(\mathbb{R}\times\mathbb{S}^{d-1})
, which is equivalent
to the weak* continuity of the sampling functionals
\delta(\cdot-{\boldsymbol{x}}_{0}):f\mapsto f({\boldsymbol{x}}_{0})
; that is,

\delta(\cdot-{\boldsymbol{x}}_{0})\in C_{0,\Delta_{\rm R}}(\mathbb{R}^{d})=\Delta_{\rm R}^{\ast}\big(C_{0,\rm Rad}\big)\oplus{\mathcal{P}}_{1}^{\prime}
with
{\mathcal{P}}^{\prime}_{1}={\rm span}\{m^{\ast}_{\boldsymbol{k}}\}_{|{\boldsymbol{k}}|\leq 1}
, where
C_{0,\Delta_{\rm R}}
is the predual of
{\mathcal{M}}_{\Delta_{\rm R}}
.

2.

Stability: the integral operator (
64
) satisfies the bound

\displaystyle\big|\Delta_{\rm R}^{\dagger}\{w\}({\boldsymbol{x}})\big|\leq\|h({\boldsymbol{x}};\cdot,\cdot)\|_{L_{\infty}}\;\|w\|_{{\mathcal{M}}}\leq C(1+\|{\boldsymbol{x}}\|)\;\|w\|_{{\mathcal{M}}(\mathbb{R}\times\mathbb{S}^{d-1})},

(67)

which ensures that
\Delta_{\rm R}^{\dagger}\{w\}({\boldsymbol{x}})
is well-defined (and continuous) for any
{\boldsymbol{x}}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
and

w\in{\mathcal{M}}_{\rm even}(\mathbb{R}\times\mathbb{S}^{d-1})
.

Note that one can also take
w\in{\mathcal{M}}(\mathbb{R}\times\mathbb{S}^{d-1})
in (
64
) since the null space of
\Delta_{\rm R}^{\dagger}:{\mathcal{M}}(\mathbb{R}\times\mathbb{S}^{d-1})\to{\mathcal{M}}_{\Delta_{\rm R}}(\mathbb{R}^{d})

(
and of
{\mathrm{R}}^{\ast}:{\mathcal{M}}(\mathbb{R}\times\mathbb{S}^{d-1})\to{\mathcal{S}}^{\prime}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
)
is precisely the complementary space
{\mathcal{M}}_{\rm Rad^{\perp}}={\mathcal{M}}_{\rm odd}(\mathbb{R}\times\mathbb{S}^{d-1})
(see Theorem
7
, Item 6).

An alternative to (
66
) is to consider a “tempered” kernel of the form

h_{\beta}({\boldsymbol{x}};t,{\boldsymbol{\xi}})=\frac{1}{2}|{\boldsymbol{\xi}}^{\mathsf{T}}{\boldsymbol{x}}-t|\,\beta(t)
, where
\beta(t)>0
is a weighting function (e.g.,
\beta(t)=\frac{1}{1+|t|^{2+\epsilon}})
that compensates the linear growth of the first factor
3
3

3

The first factor can also be replaced by
({\boldsymbol{\xi}}^{\mathsf{T}}{\boldsymbol{x}}-t)_{+}
modulo some adjustments in
\beta
, as shown by the authors.
(
Bartolucci et al. 2021
)
. In effect, this mechanism, whose stability is intrinsically guaranteed, reduces the size of the native space. Interestingly, the corresponding optimization problem admits the same form of solution—a neural network with one hidden ReLU layer
(
Bartolucci et al. 2021
)
—with the caveat that the underlying regularization is no longer translation-invariant. In this modified scenario, the optimal cost is
\|\Delta_{\rm R}f_{\rm ridge}\|_{{\mathcal{M}}_{1/\beta}}=\sum_{n=1}^{K_{0}}|a_{k}|\frac{1}{|\beta(\tau_{k})|}
, which tends to favor smaller biases
\tau_{k}
. This also means that one then looses the invariance to similarity transformations of the data (Proposition
13
).

Appendix A
Direct-Sum Topologies

There are two standard ways to define direct sums: explicitly, via the use of projectors; or abstractly, via the use of quotient spaces. The two methods are equivalent whenever one can explicitly identify the underlying quotient space as a (concrete) complemented subspace of the original space.

A.1
Projectors

Let
{\mathcal{X}}
be a topological vector space.
A continuous linear operator
{\mathrm{P}}:{\mathcal{X}}\to{\mathcal{X}}
with the property that
{\mathrm{P}}={\mathrm{P}}\circ{\mathrm{P}}={\mathrm{P}}^{2}
(idempotence) on
{\mathcal{X}}
is called a projection operator
(
Dunford and Schwartz 1988
, p 480)
. In particular, when
{\mathcal{X}}
is a Banach space or a Fréchet space, the range
{\mathcal{U}}={\mathrm{P}}({\mathcal{X}})
of
{\mathrm{P}}
is necessarily a closed subspace of
{\mathcal{X}}
.
In that case,
{\mathrm{P}}=\mathrm{Proj}_{{\mathcal{U}}}
is a projector from
{\mathcal{X}}
onto
{\mathcal{U}}
, and
{\mathcal{X}}={\mathcal{U}}\oplus{\mathcal{V}}
where
{\mathcal{V}}={\rm ker}{\mathrm{P}}
is the null space of
{\mathrm{P}}
or, equivalently, the range of the complementary projector
\mathrm{Proj}_{\mathcal{V}}=(\mathrm{Id}-{\mathrm{P}})
.

More generally, when
{\mathcal{X}}
is a topological vector space, the space
{\mathrm{P}}({\mathcal{X}})
equipped with the topology induced by
{\mathcal{X}}
is a topological space as well, with the same properties as the original space
{\mathcal{X}}
(e.g., completeness). Likewise, if
({\mathcal{X}},{\mathcal{X}}^{\prime})
is a dual pair of topological spaces, then so is
\Big({\mathrm{P}}({\mathcal{X}}),{\mathrm{P}}^{\ast}({\mathcal{X}}^{\prime})\big)
,
where
{\mathrm{P}}^{\ast}:{\mathcal{X}}^{\prime}\to{\mathcal{X}}^{\prime}
is the dual projection operator.

A.2
Direct Sums

1) Direct-sum decomposition of a vector space
{\mathcal{X}}
: Let
{\mathcal{U}}
and
{\mathcal{V}}
be two (complementary) closed subspaces of
{\mathcal{X}}
.
The notation
{\mathcal{X}}={\mathcal{U}}\oplus{\mathcal{V}}
indicates that every element
x\in{\mathcal{X}}
has a unique decomposition as
x=u+v

with
(u,v)\in{\mathcal{U}}\times{\mathcal{V}}
.
The underlying projection operators are

\displaystyle x=u+v

\displaystyle\mapsto\mathrm{Proj}_{{\mathcal{U}}}\{x\}=u

\displaystyle x

\displaystyle\mapsto\mathrm{Proj}_{{\mathcal{V}}}\{x\}=(\mathrm{Id}-\mathrm{Proj}_{{\mathcal{U}}})\{x\}=v.

To summarize, given a (closed) subspace
{\mathcal{U}}
of a normed vector space
{\mathcal{X}}
, the search of a complement
{\mathcal{V}}
for
{\mathcal{U}}
in
{\mathcal{X}}
is equivalent to the search of a (continuous) projection operator
{\mathrm{P}}
on
{\mathcal{X}}
(with
{\mathrm{P}}^{2}={\mathrm{P}}
) whose range is
{\mathcal{U}}
. Then,
{\mathcal{V}}=\mathrm{Proj}_{{\mathcal{V}}}({\mathcal{X}})
with
\mathrm{Proj}_{{\mathcal{V}}}=(\mathrm{Id}-{\mathrm{P}})
.

2) Annihilator: Let
{\mathcal{U}}
be a closed subset of
{\mathcal{X}}
. One then defines
{\mathcal{U}}^{\perp}
as the annihilator of
{\mathcal{U}}
in
{\mathcal{X}}^{\prime}
, which is the subset

{\mathcal{U}}^{\perp}=\{f\in{\mathcal{X}}^{\prime}:\langle f,u\rangle=0\mbox{ for all\lx@nobreakspace}u\in{\mathcal{U}}\}\subseteq{\mathcal{X}}^{\prime}.

3) Dual space:
The dual of the direct sum
{\mathcal{X}}={\mathcal{U}}\oplus{\mathcal{V}}
is
{\mathcal{X}}^{\prime}={\mathcal{U}}^{\prime}\oplus{\mathcal{V}}^{\prime}
, where
{\mathcal{U}}^{\prime}={\mathrm{P}}^{\ast}({\mathcal{X}}^{\prime})={\mathcal{V}}^{\perp}
and

{\mathcal{U}}^{\prime}={\mathcal{U}}^{\perp}
.

4) Quotient space:
Under the assumption that
{\mathcal{V}}
is a closed subset of
{\mathcal{X}}
, one defines the quotient space
{\mathcal{X}}/{\mathcal{V}}
whose
elements are equivalence classes (or cosets) denoted by

[x]=x+{\mathcal{V}}
.
The corresponding quotient map
q:{\mathcal{X}}\to{\mathcal{X}}/{\mathcal{V}}

is linear and its kernel (null space) is
{\mathcal{V}}
.
When
{\mathcal{X}}
is a Banach space, the quotient norm is

\|[x]\|_{{\mathcal{X}}/{\mathcal{V}}}=\inf_{v\in{\mathcal{V}}}\|x+v\|_{{\mathcal{X}}},

which measures the distance from
x
to
{\mathcal{V}}
. The quotient space
{\mathcal{X}}/{\mathcal{V}}
equipped with the quotient norm is a Banach space as well. Moreover, there is a natural isomorphism between
({\mathcal{X}}/{\mathcal{V}})^{\prime}
(the dual of the quotient of
{\mathcal{X}}
by
{\mathcal{V}}
) and
{\mathcal{V}}^{\perp}
(the annihilator of
{\mathcal{V}}
in
{\mathcal{X}}^{\prime}
), so that
({\mathcal{X}}/{\mathcal{V}})^{\prime}\xhookrightarrow{}{\mathcal{X}}^{\prime}
.

Also of relevance is the property that the bounded operators on
{\mathcal{X}}
that annihilate the elements of
{\mathcal{V}}
“factor through”
{\mathcal{X}}/{\mathcal{V}}
.
Let
{\mathrm{T}}:{\mathcal{X}}\to{\mathcal{Y}}
with
{\mathcal{V}}\subseteq{\rm ker}{\mathrm{T}}
. Then, there exists a unique linear operator
{\mathrm{T}}_{q}:{\mathcal{X}}/{\mathcal{V}}\to{\mathcal{Y}}
such that

{\mathrm{T}}_{q}q(x)={\mathrm{T}}(x)
and
\|{\mathrm{T}}_{q}\|=\|{\mathrm{T}}\|
.

The kernel of any bounded operator
{\mathrm{T}}:{\mathcal{X}}\to{\mathcal{Y}}
is a closed subspace of
{\mathcal{X}}

(
Markin 2020
, Proposition 4.2, p. 172)
. Hence,
the quotient space
{\mathcal{X}}/{\rm ker}({\mathrm{T}})
is a vector space that is isomorphic to
{\mathrm{T}}({\mathcal{X}})
.

Appendix B
Extreme Points

Definition 14

(Extreme Points)
.

Let
C
be a convex set of a Banach space
{\mathcal{X}}
. The extreme points of
C
are the points

x\in C
such that, if there exist
x_{1},x_{2}\in C
and
\theta\in(0,1)
such that
x=\theta x_{1}+(1-\theta)x_{2}
, then it necessarily holds that
x_{1}=x=x_{2}
. The set of these extreme points is denoted by
{\rm Ext}(C)
.

We now present a classical result that gives the explicit form of the extreme points of the dual
{\mathcal{X}}^{\prime}

of any closed subspace
{\mathcal{X}}\subseteq C({\mathcal{Z}})
, where
C({\mathcal{Z}})
is the space of continuous functions
z\mapsto f(z)
on some compact Hausdorff space
{\mathcal{Z}}
equipped with the norm
\|f\|=\sup_{z\in{\mathcal{Z}}}|f(z)|
.

Lemma 15

(
(
Dunford and Schwartz 1988
, p. 441)
)
.

Let
{\mathcal{X}}
be a closed linear manifold of the Banach space
C({\mathcal{Z}})
of all real continuous functions on the compact Hausdorff space
{\mathcal{Z}}
. For each
z\in{\mathcal{Z}}
, let the evaluation functional
e_{z}\in{\mathcal{X}}^{\prime}
be defined by

\displaystyle\langle e_{z},f\rangle=f(z),\quad f\in{\mathcal{X}}.

(68)

Then, every extreme point of the closed unit ball in
{\mathcal{X}}^{\prime}
,

\displaystyle B_{{\mathcal{X}}^{\prime}}=\{x^{\ast}\in{\mathcal{X}}^{\prime}:\|x^{\ast}\|_{{\mathcal{X}}^{\prime}}=\sup_{f\in{\mathcal{X}}:|f(z)|\leq 1}\langle x^{\ast},f\rangle\leq 1\},

(69)

is of the form
\pm e_{z}
with
z\in{\mathcal{Z}}
. If
{\mathcal{X}}=C({\mathcal{Z}})
, then the converse is true as well; that is,

{\rm Ext}B_{{\mathcal{M}}({\mathcal{Z}})}=\{\pm e_{z}:z\in{\mathcal{Z}}\}
with
{\mathcal{M}}({\mathcal{Z}})=\big(C({\mathcal{Z}})\big)^{\prime}
.

Lemma
15
generalizes to
C_{0}({\mathcal{Z}})
, where
{\mathcal{Z}}
is a locally compact Hausdorff space, which covers the case that is of interest to us:

{\mathcal{Z}}=\mathbb{R}\times\mathbb{S}^{d-1}
.

Proof.

Let
E
be the set of all points in
{\mathcal{X}}^{\prime}
of the form
\pm e_{z}
with
z\in{\mathcal{Z}}
.
The space
{\mathcal{X}}^{\prime}
is equipped with its weak
\ast
(or
{\mathcal{X}}
) topology for the Krein-Milman theorem to apply.
As
\|e_{z}\|_{{\mathcal{X}}^{\prime}}\leq 1
,
E\subseteq B_{{\mathcal{X}}^{\prime}}
. Since
B_{{\mathcal{X}}^{\prime}}
is convex, weak*-compact and, hence, weak*-closed, the inclusion also holds for the closed convex hull, with
{\rm cch}E\subseteq B_{{\mathcal{X}}^{\prime}}
.
Next, we invoke a variant of the Hahn-Banach theorem
(
Rudin 1991
, Theorem 3.5, p. 59)
.
For any
x^{\ast}\notin{\rm cch}E
, there exists a linear functional
f\in{\mathcal{X}}
that separates
x^{\ast}\in{\mathcal{X}}^{\prime}
from the closed convex set
{\rm cch}E
.
This means that there are a constant
c>0
and some
\epsilon>0
such that

\pm f(z)\leq c-\epsilon<c\leq\langle x^{\ast},f\rangle

for all
z\in{\mathcal{Z}}
.
Hence,
\|f\|\leq(c-\epsilon)
which, when combined with
\|x^{\ast}\|_{{\mathcal{X}}^{\prime}}\|f\|\geq c
, gives that
\|x^{\ast}\|_{{\mathcal{X}}^{\prime}}>1
. Thus,
{\rm cch}E\supseteq B_{{\mathcal{X}}^{\prime}}
, from which we conclude that

{\rm cch}E=B_{{\mathcal{X}}^{\prime}}
. Finally, since
E
is compact, the extreme points of
{\rm cch}E
necessarily lie in
E
(see
(
Rudin 1991
, Milman’s theorem, p. 76)
).

For the converse implication, we
invoke the Riesz-representation theorem, which allows us to represent any unit-norm functional on

C_{0}({\mathcal{Z}})
by a real-valued measure
\mu\in{\mathcal{M}}({\mathcal{Z}})
of total variation
1
. If the support of
\mu

consists of one point, it is a signed multiple of a Dirac mass. Otherwise,
{\rm supp}\mu
contains two distinct points
z_{1}\neq z_{2}
. Let
U,V\subset{\mathcal{Z}}

be disjoint neighborhoods of
z_{1}
and
z_{2}
.
By the definition of the support,
|\mu|(U)>0
and
|\mu|(V)>0
. Define

t=|\mu|(U)
, which is such that
0<t<1
. Now let
\lambda=t^{-1}\mu|_{U}

and
\nu=(1-t)^{-1}\mu|_{U^{c}}
.
Then, both
\lambda
and
\nu
are unit-norm functionals and

\mu=t\lambda+(1-t)\nu
, which proves that
\mu
is not extreme.
∎
