Optimizing Information-theoretical Generalization Bounds via Anisotropic Noise in SGLD
Bohan Wang, Huishuai Zhang, Jieyu Zhang, Qi Meng, Wei Chen, Tie-Yan Liu

Abstract
Recently, the information-theoretical framework has been proven to be able to obtain non-vacuous generalization bounds for large models trained by Stochastic Gradient Langevin Dynamics (SGLD) with isotropic noise. In this paper, we optimize the information-theoretical generalization bound by manipulating the noise structure in SGLD. We prove that with constraint to guarantee low empirical risk, the optimal noise covariance is the square root of the expected gradient covariance if both the prior and the posterior are jointly optimized. This validates that the optimal noise is quite close to the empirical gradient covariance. Technically, we develop a new information-theoretical bound that enables such an optimization analysis. We then apply matrix analysis to derive the form of optimal noise covariance. Presented constraint and results are validated by the empirical observations.

Introduction

Generalization ability is one of the core questions in learning theory
[
4
]
, but remains unclear for deep learning models
[
17
,
38
]
. Existing generalization bounds based on the capacity control become vacuous for practical deep learning models due to the over-parameterization property
[
1
,
26
,
39
]
.

From the information theoretical perspective, recent works
[
30
,
37
]
bound the generalization error by the mutual information between the dataset and the learned parameters.
This result reveals that good generalization occurs when the learned parameters do not depend on a specific dataset too much, which is intuitively reasonable and closely related with the idea of algorithm stability
[
6
,
15
,
19
,
24
,
20
]
and differential privacy
[
8
,
10
]
. Meanwhile, based on information-theoretic metrics, one can analyze general classes of updates and models, e.g., stochastic iterative algorithms for non-convex objectives, hence applicable to deep learning. It has been shown that the information theoretical bounds are non-vacuous and closely related with the real generalization error even in deep learning
[
14
,
26
,
9
,
39
]
.

Notably, the information theoretical bound is realized in
[
28
]
via decomposing the mutual information across iterations. This framework can perform a step-wise analysis of Stochastic Gradient Langevin Dynamics (SGLD)
[
33
,
29
]
, by evaluating the mutual information conditional on the previous learned parameters at each step. We note that the noise added in SGLD is critical for both the mutual information evaluation and the empirical risk minimization. Most existing work focus on SGLD with constant and isotropic noise covariance
[
22
,
25
,
14
]
due to the technical difficulty. However, it is observed that the test accuracy of SGLD with isotropic noise has a considerable gap compared to that of the widely-used Stochastic Gradient Descent (SGD)
[
40
]
. This empirical gap motivates us to consider the following question:

Can we find the optimal noise added in SGLD in terms of generalization?

An affirmative answer will lead to an algorithm imitating SGD better, which helps us to better understand the generalization behavior of SGD.

Specifically, we propose to optimize the structure of the noise in SGLD such that the generalization bound is minimized while a low empirical risk is guaranteed. To this end, we first show that the trace of the noise covariance in SGLD is a valid constraint that governs the empirical risk behavior both theoretically and empirically. Then we devise a new information theoretical generalization bound that are parallel to those bounds in
[
25
]
, but facilitate the derivation of the optimal noise.
With these technical preparations, we prove that when jointly optimizing both the prior and the posterior, the optimal noise covariance is the square root of the expected gradient covariance, i.e., their eigenvectors are the same and the corresponding eigenvalues of the former are the square root of the latter, and the optimal prior recovers the prior in
[
25
]
. This indicates the optimal noise covariance of SGLD would be quite close to the empirical gradient covariance, i.e., the noise covariance of SGD, because of the concentration of measure.

Our result lends support to the belief that the noise introduced by Stochastic Gradient Descent (SGD) is superior to the isotropic noise, which has been widely observed
[
18
,
36
,
40
,
38
]
. As an illustrative example, we plot the generalization errors of SGD, SGLD with the isotropic noise and SGLD with the optimal noise in Figure
1
, where their training curves behave almost the same (do not show here). We can see the optimal noise captures the behavior of SGD much better than the isotropic noise.

Specifically, our contribution can be summarized as follows:

1. We formulate a problem of finding the optimal noise covariance by optimizing an information-theoretical bound;

2. We develop a new information-theoretical bound to facilitate the analysis of the above optimization problem;

3. We obtain the optimal structure of the noise covariances, and demonstrate the similarity to empirical/expected gradient covariance;

\operatorname{Gerr}\overset{\triangle}{=}\mathbb{E}_{\bm{S},Q^{\bm{S}}}\left[\mathcal{R}_{\mathcal{D}}(\bm{W})-\mathcal{R}_{\bm{S}}(\bm{W})\right].

SGD and SGLD.
The update rule of Stochastic Gradient Descent (SGD) at step

\bm{W}_{t}\leftarrow\bm{W}_{t-1}-\eta_{t}\nabla\mathcal{R}_{\bm{S}_{\bm{V}_{t}}}(\bm{W}_{t-1}),

(2)

(3)

where

and
\bm{W}_{t-1}

3
Formulate the Problem: Proper Constraints and Optimization Target

In this part, we formulate the optimization problem, i.e., finding the optimal noise covariance of SGLD in terms of the information-theoretical generalization bound, by selecting the proper optimization constraint and optimization target. Specifically, in Section
3.1
, we argue that the trace of the noise covariance is a proper constraint to ensure the same optimization error; in Section
3.2
, we argue that existing generalization bounds are not proper candidates as the optimization target, and propose a new information-theoretical bound parallel to existing ones but easier to analyze.

3.1
Constraint on the Covariance to Control the Empirical Risk

We first derive the constraint of noise covariance
\bm{\Sigma}_{t}
in Eq. (
3
) from the perspective of training performance, under which we optimize the generalization bounds in the rest of this paper.

Without any constraint on the noise covariance, optimizing generalization error is trivial but meaningless: a direct combination of Theorem 1 of
[
37
]
and Theorem 1 of
[
28
]
shows that for isotropic SGLD with
\bm{\Sigma}_{t}=\sigma_{t}\mathbb{I}
, the generalization error after
T
step satisfies
\operatorname{Gerr}\leq\mathcal{O}((\sum_{t=1}^{T}\log(1+1/\sigma_{t}))^{1/2})
.
Therefore, as
\sigma_{t}\rightarrow\infty
for
t\in[T]
, we have
\operatorname{Gerr}\rightarrow 0
, but then the update of SGLD is dominated by the noise, leading to arbitrary bad empirical risk. Hence, we need constraints on the covariance in order to control the empirical risk when minimizing the generalization error. Specifically, the expected decrease of the empirical risk for one iteration can be bounded as follows.

Lemma 2
.

Let empirical risk
\mathcal{R}_{\bm{S}}(\bm{W})
be
\beta
-smooth w.r.t.
\bm{W}
. Let
\bm{W}_{[T]}
be given by state-dependent SGLD (Eq. (
3
)). Then,

\mathbb{E}_{t+1|t}\mathcal{R}_{\bm{S}}(\bm{W}_{t+1})-\mathcal{R}_{\bm{S}}(\bm{W}_{t})\leq-\left(1-\frac{\beta\eta_{t+1}}{2}\right)\eta_{t+1}\left\|\nabla\mathcal{R}_{\bm{S}}\left(\bm{W}_{t}\right)\right\|^{2}+\frac{\beta}{2}\operatorname{tr}\left(\frac{\eta_{t+1}^{2}(N-b_{t})}{(N-1)b_{t}}\bm{\Sigma}^{sd}_{\bm{S},\bm{W}_{t}}+\bm{\Sigma}_{t+1}(\bm{S},\bm{W}_{t})\right).

The proof can be obtained by a standard analysis in optimization, and we defer it to Appendix
B
. By Lemma
2
, the noise covariance
\bm{\Sigma}_{t}
affects the upper bound of the empirical risk by its
trace
. Therefore, it is reasonable to keep
\operatorname{tr}(\bm{\Sigma}_{t}(\bm{S},\bm{W}_{t-1}))
unchanged while seeking the optimal
\bm{\Sigma}_{t}(\bm{S},\bm{W}_{t-1})
to minimize the generalization error. The constraint is given formally as follows:

Constraint 1
.

The trace of
\bm{\Sigma}_{t}(\bm{S},\bm{W}_{t-1})
is fixed when optimizing the generalization error. That is, there exist positive constants
c_{t}(\bm{S},\bm{W}_{t-1})
depending on
\bm{S}
and
\bm{W}_{t-1}
, such that,

\operatorname{tr}(\bm{\Sigma}_{t}(\bm{S},\bm{W}_{t-1}))=c_{t}(\bm{S},\bm{W}_{t-1}).

We do not put any constraint on the value of
c_{t}
in our latter analyses. Therefore, it is also possible to manipulate
c_{t}
in order to jointly optimize the empirical risk and the generalization bound, which however, is beyond the scope of this paper and we defer it to future works. Similar constraint is also proposed by
[
40
]
from the standpoint of kinetic energy when analyzing the effect of noise structure on the escaping efficiency from saddle points.

We next verify this constraint empirically. We run SGLD with different covariances on a four-layer neural network for the Fashion-MNIST classification problem. Concretely, the noise covariances are chosen respectively as
\bm{\Sigma}^{(1)}_{t}=\bm{\Sigma}^{sd}_{\bm{S},\bm{W}_{t-1}}
, i.e., “EC-SGLD” ( Empirical Covariance SGLD) curve in Fig.
2
, and
\bm{\Sigma}^{(2)}_{t}=

\frac{1}{d}\operatorname{tr}(\bm{\Sigma}^{sd}_{\bm{S},\bm{W}_{t-1}})\mathbb{I}
, i.e., the “Iso-SGLD (C)” curve in Fig.
2
. It is easy to verify that
\operatorname{tr}(\bm{\Sigma}^{(1)}_{t})=\operatorname{tr}(\bm{\Sigma}^{(2)}_{t})
, which is exactly the Constraint
1
. We can see from Fig.
2
that the convergence curves corresponding to
\bm{\Sigma}^{(1)}_{t}
and
\bm{\Sigma}^{(2)}_{t}
almost coincide with each other, validating the Constraint
1
.

Figure 2
:
Training errors of SGLD with different noise covariances. The experiment is run on the Fashion-MNIST dataset with a four-layer neural network (see Appendix
F
).

In comparison, another quantity
\operatorname{tr}(\mathcal{H}_{\bm{S},\bm{W}_{t-1}}\bm{\Sigma}_{t})
, the trace of the empirical risk’s Hessian times the empirical covariance, has also been proposed to govern the convergence behavior
[
34
, Theorem 4.1]
. We plot the convergence curve of SGLD with noise covariance
\bm{\Sigma}^{(3)}_{t}=\frac{\operatorname{tr}(\mathcal{H}_{\bm{S},\bm{W}_{t-1}}\bm{\Sigma}^{sd}_{\bm{S},\bm{W}_{t-1}})}{\operatorname{tr}(\mathcal{H}_{\bm{S},\bm{W}_{t-1}})}\mathbb{I}
("Iso-SGLD (H)" in Fig.
2
). While
\operatorname{tr}(\mathcal{H}_{\bm{S},\bm{W}_{t-1}}\bm{\Sigma}^{(1)}_{t})=\operatorname{tr}(\mathcal{H}_{\bm{S},\bm{W}_{t-1}}\bm{\Sigma}^{(3)}_{t})
, there is a significant gap between curves of “EC-SGLD ” and “Iso-SGLD (H)” in Fig.
2
. This implies that
\operatorname{tr}(\mathcal{H}_{\bm{S},\bm{W}_{t-1}}\bm{\Sigma}_{t})
is not a good constraint for the empirical risk of SGLD, validating the Constraint
1
from another side.

In the rest of this paper, we will optimize the information-theoretical bound under Constraint
1
to ensure low empirical risk.

New Information-theoretical Bounds as the Optimization Target

3.2.1
Difficulties When Applying Traditional Information-theoretical Bounds

By Lemma
1
, the generalization error bound in Proposition
1
can be rewritten as

\mathbb{E}_{\bm{S},\bm{V}_{[T]},\bm{J}}\sqrt{\frac{(a_{2}-a_{1})^{2}}{2}\sum_{s=1}^{T}\mathbb{E}_{Q_{s-1}^{\bm{S},\bm{V}_{[s-1]}}}\operatorname{KL}\left(Q_{s|(s-1)}^{\bm{S},\bm{V}_{s}}\left\|P_{s|(s-1)}^{\bm{J},\bm{S}_{\bm{J}},\bm{V}_{s}}\right.\right)}.

(4)

For
Problem

1
, there are two difficulties. For a fixed

, in order to obtain the optimal

, one would first calculate the outside expectation of Eq. (

\bm{S}_{\bm{J}^{c}}

where

\bm{\Sigma}_{i}

\bm{\Sigma}_{i}

3.2.2
Information-theoretical Generalization Bound for Identifying the Noise Effect

We then establish a new information-theoretical generalization bound as the optimization target that are parallel to the results in
[
25
]
but more suitable for analyzing the effect of noise structure. The basic idea is to reverse the order of prior and posterior in the KL divergence to let
\bm{\Sigma}_{t}
only affect one term in the generalization bound for each
t
when finding the optimal point. The formal theorem is stated as follows:

Theorem 1
.

Let sample set
\bm{S}
, mini-batch
\bm{V}_{[T]}
, random subset
\bm{J}
, the posterior distribution
Q
output by Algorithm
\mathcal{A}
with update rule
\mathcal{M}
and the prior distribution
P
output by Algorithm
\mathcal{B}
with update rule
\tilde{\mathcal{M}}
be defined as Proposition
1
. Let
[a_{1},a_{2}]
be the range of loss. Then, the generalization error of
\mathcal{A}
can be bounded as

\displaystyle\operatorname{Gerr}\leq\mathbb{E}_{\bm{S},\bm{V}_{[T]},\bm{J}}\sqrt{\frac{(a_{2}-a_{1})^{2}}{2}\operatorname{KL}\left(P^{\bm{J},\bm{S}_{\bm{J}},\bm{V}_{[T]}}\left\|Q^{\bm{S},\bm{V}_{[T]}}\right.\right)}.

(5)

Compared to Proposition
1
, Theorem
1
can be viewed as a parallel version with the positions of the prior distribution
P
and the posterior distribution
Q
reversed. This reverse benefits the optimization of the generalization error bound with respect to the noise covariance under Constraint
1
in two ways. First, by Lemma
1
, the KL term in Eq. (
5
) can be further decomposed into

\operatorname{KL}\left(P^{\bm{S}_{\bm{J}},\bm{V}_{[T]}}\left\|Q^{\bm{S},\bm{V}_{[T]}}\right.\right)=\sum_{s=1}^{T}\mathbb{E}_{P_{1:s-1}^{\bm{J},\bm{S}_{\bm{J}},\bm{V}_{[s-1]}}}\operatorname{KL}\left(P_{s|(s-1)}^{\bm{J},\bm{S}_{\bm{J}},\bm{V}_{s}}||Q_{s|(s-1)}^{\bm{S},\bm{V}_{s}}\right),

in which only each KL term depends on each
\bm{\Sigma}_{s}
, respectively. Secondly, for each summand in the above decomposition, only
Q_{s|(s-1)}^{\bm{S},\bm{V}_{s}}
depends on
\bm{S}_{\bm{J}^{c}}
, making it easy to compute the optimal prior in a "greedy" sense (see details in Section
4
).

Hence in the rest of this paper, we study the optimal structure of
\bm{\Sigma}_{t}
in terms of the generalization bound in Theorem
1
under the Constraint
1
. Specifically, our ultimate goal can be stated as follows:

Problem 1
.

What is the optimal structure of the noise covariance
\bm{\Sigma}_{[T]}
for SGLD in terms of the generalization bound in Theorem
1
under the Constraint
1
?

In the rest of this paper, we focus on solving Problem
1
and its variants.

4
Obtain the Optimal Noise Covariance with the Greedy Prior

The generalization bound in Theorem
1
depends on both the prior distribution
P
and the posterior distribution
Q
(consequently on
\bm{\Sigma}_{[T]}
). Therefore it requires searching
P
and
\bm{\Sigma}_{[T]}
jointly to optimize the generalization error.

In this section, we solve
Problem 1
with greedily selected priors.
Due to that the square root in the bound Eq. (
5
) make the dependency on
\bm{\Sigma}_{[T]}
even more complex, we optimize a slightly different version of the bound by taking the expectation with respect to
\bm{S}_{\bm{J}^{c}}
into the square root, i.e.,

{\operatorname{Gen}}_{T}\overset{\triangle}{=}\mathbb{E}_{\bm{S}_{\bm{J}},\bm{V}_{[T]},\bm{J}}\sqrt{\frac{(a_{2}-a_{1})^{2}}{2}\mathbb{E}_{\bm{S}_{\bm{J}^{c}}}\operatorname{KL}\left(P^{\bm{J},\bm{S}_{\bm{J}},\bm{V}_{[T]}}\left\|Q^{\bm{S},\bm{V}_{[T]}}\right.\right)}.

(6)

By Jensen’s Inequality, Eq. (
6
) is still a generalization bound, but allows the expectation with respect to
\bm{S}_{\bm{J}^{c}}
to interact with the KL divergence directly.
One can easily observe that
\operatorname{Gen}_{T}
is a mapping from
P
and
Q
(and thus from
P
and
\bm{\Sigma}_{[T]}
) to a positive real.
Therefore, we can reformulate Problem
1
mathematically as the following optimization problem
(P1)
:

\textbf{(P1)}.\min_{P,\bm{\Sigma}_{[T]}}\operatorname{Gen}_{T}(P,\bm{\Sigma}_{[T]}),\text{ subject to: Constraint \ref{constraint: trace}}.

For simplicity, we restrict that the considered noise covariance
\bm{\Sigma}_{[T]}
only depends on the parameter
\bm{W}
, and is independent of the sample
\bm{S}
. By Lemma
1
, the KL term in the optimization target
\operatorname{Gen}_{T}
can be decomposed into

\operatorname{KL}\left(P^{\bm{J},\bm{S}_{\bm{J}},\bm{V}_{[T]}}\left\|Q^{\bm{S},\bm{V}_{[T]}}\right.\right)=\sum_{s=1}^{T}\mathbb{E}_{P_{s-1}^{\bm{J},\bm{S}_{\bm{J}},\bm{V}_{[s-1]}}}\operatorname{KL}\left(P_{s|(s-1)}^{\bm{J},\bm{S}_{\bm{J}},\bm{V}_{s}}\left\|Q_{s|(s-1)}^{\bm{S},\bm{V}_{s}}\right.\right).

(7)

Therefore, when optimizing
\operatorname{Gen}_{T}
with respect to prior
P
, both
\operatorname{KL}(P_{s|(s-1)}^{\bm{J},\bm{S}_{\bm{J}},\bm{V}_{s}}||Q_{s|(s-1)}^{\bm{S},\bm{V}_{s}})
and
P_{i-1}^{\bm{J},\bm{S}_{\bm{J}},\bm{V}_{[i-1]}}

(i>s)
have dependence on
P_{s|(s-1)}^{\bm{J},\bm{S}_{\bm{J}},\bm{V}_{s}}
. Similar to the discussion in Section
3.2.1
, the dependence of
P_{i-1}^{\bm{J},\bm{S}_{\bm{J}},\bm{V}_{[i-1]}}
with
i>s
on
P_{s|(s-1)}^{\bm{J},\bm{S}_{\bm{J}},\bm{V}_{s}}
can be very complex. Therefore, we approximate the optimal
P_{s|(s-1)}^{\bm{J},\bm{S}_{\bm{J}},\bm{V}_{s}}
by the greedy prior which is defined as follows:

Definition 1

(Greedy Prior)
.

We say
P^{*}
is the optimal prior in the greedy sense, or the greedy prior for brevity, if for any
1\leq s\leq T
and any
\bm{S}_{\bm{J}}
and
\bm{V}_{[T]}
,
{P^{*}}^{\bm{J},\bm{S}_{\bm{J}},\bm{V}_{s}}_{s|(s-1)}={P^{s}}^{\bm{J},\bm{S}_{\bm{J}},\bm{V}_{s}}_{s|(s-1)}
, where
P^{s}
is defined as follows:

P^{s}\overset{\triangle}{=}\arg\min_{P}\left(\min_{\bm{\Sigma}_{[s]}}\operatorname{Gen}_{s}(P,\bm{\Sigma}_{[s]})\right),\text{ subject to: Constraint \ref{constraint: trace}}.

Intuitively, the conditional probability of
P^{*}
of the step
s
is the optimal one if we only consider the generalization bound for steps up to
s
, and a special case is that the step
T
conditional probability of
P^{*}
agrees with the step
T
conditional probability of
P^{T}
, which is the desired optimal prior. This is why we call
P^{*}
"greedy" and use it to approximate the optimal prior.

With the greedy prior, we characterize the optimal noise covariance by the following theorem.

Theorem 2
.

Let the iteration of SGLD with state-dependent noise
Q^{\bm{S},\bm{V}_{[T]}}
be given as Eq.(
3
). Under Constraint
1
, the greedy optimal prior of step
t
is given by

\bm{W}_{t}=\bm{W}_{t-1}-\eta_{t}\left(\frac{|\bm{V}_{t}\cap\bm{J}|}{|\bm{V}_{t}|}\nabla\mathcal{R}_{\bm{S}_{\bm{V}_{t}\cap\bm{J}}}\left(\bm{W}_{t-1}\right)+\frac{|\bm{V}_{t}\cap\bm{J}^{c}|}{|\bm{V}_{t}|}\nabla\mathcal{R}_{\mathcal{D}}\left(\bm{W}_{t-1}\right)\right)+\mathcal{N}\left(\bm{0},\bm{\Sigma}^{*}_{t}(\bm{W}_{t-1})\right),

while the optimal covariance of noise
\bm{\Sigma}^{*}_{[T]}
for
\operatorname{Gen}_{T}
with the greedy prior is given by
\bm{\Sigma}^{*}_{t}(\bm{W})=\lambda_{t}(\bm{W})\left(\bm{\Sigma}^{pop}_{\bm{W}}\right)^{\frac{1}{2}}

(\forall t\in[T])
, where
\lambda_{t}(\bm{W})=c_{t}(\bm{W})/\operatorname{tr}(\left(\bm{\Sigma}^{pop}_{\bm{W}}\right)^{\frac{1}{2}})
.

As the sample size is large enough, we have
\bm{\Sigma}^{sd}_{\bm{S},\bm{W}}\rightarrow\bm{\Sigma}^{pop}_{\bm{W}}
almost surely, which demonstrates the similarity between the solution of
Problem 1
and the noise covariance of SGD. Also, by the Law of Large Numbers,
\nabla\mathcal{R}_{\bm{S}_{\bm{J}}}\approx\nabla\mathcal{R}_{\mathcal{D}}
, and the mean of the greedy optimal prior recovers the mean of the prior used in
[
25
]
(one can also refer to Eq. (
10
) in this paper for the form).

We briefly state the proof skeleton of Theorem
2
, with the proof details deferred to Appendix
D
.
To obtain the final optimal noise covariance in Theorem
2
, we need to first derive the greedy prior, i.e., the optimal conditional distribution
P_{s|(s-1)}
of the prior of step
s
in terms of the generalization bound
\operatorname{Gen}_{s}
, which has the form

\mathbb{E}_{\bm{S}_{\bm{J}},\bm{V}_{[s]},\bm{J}}\sqrt{\frac{(a_{2}-a_{1})^{2}}{2}\mathbb{E}_{\bm{S}_{\bm{J}^{c}}}\sum_{t=1}^{s}\mathbb{E}_{P_{t-1}^{\bm{J},\bm{S}_{\bm{J}},\bm{V}_{[t-1]}}}\operatorname{KL}\left(P_{t|(t-1)}^{\bm{J},\bm{S}_{\bm{J}},\bm{V}_{t}}\left\|Q_{t|(t-1)}^{\bm{S},\bm{V}_{t}}\right.\right)}.

(8)

Typically, solving the optimal
P_{s|(s-1)}
requires optimizing
\operatorname{Gen}_{s}
with respect to all
P_{t|(t-1)}

t\in[s]
and
\bm{\Sigma}_{[s]}
, which is still very complex. However, we can tackle this problem in a rather elegant way. We first investigate the optimal noise covariance in terms of a single KL divergence term in
\operatorname{Gen}_{s}
.

Lemma 3
.

Under Constraint
1
, the optimal noise covariance of the following problem

\min_{\bm{\Sigma}_{s}}\left(\min_{{P}_{s|(s-1)}^{\bm{J},\bm{S}_{\bm{J}},\bm{V}_{s}}}\mathbb{E}_{\bm{S}_{\bm{J}^{c}}\sim\mathcal{D}}\operatorname{KL}\left({P}_{s|(s-1)}^{\bm{J},\bm{S}_{\bm{J}},\bm{V}_{s}}\left\|Q_{s|(s-1)}^{\bm{S},\bm{V}_{s}}\right.\right)\right).

(9)

is attained at
\bm{\Sigma}^{*}_{t}(\bm{W})
, where
\bm{\Sigma}^{*}_{t}(\bm{W})
is defined as Theorem
2
.

By Lemma
3
, the optimal solution of Eq. (
9
) doesn’t rely on
\bm{J},\bm{S}_{\bm{J}}
, or
\bm{V}_{[s]}
. On the other hand, by Eq. (
8
),

\mathbb{E}_{\bm{S}_{\bm{J}^{c}}\sim\mathcal{D}}\operatorname{KL}\left({P}_{s|(s-1)}^{\bm{J},\bm{S}_{\bm{J}},\bm{V}_{s}}\left\|Q_{s|(s-1)}^{\bm{S},\bm{V}_{s}}\right.\right)

(\forall\bm{J},\bm{S}_{\bm{J}},\bm{V}_{[s]})
are the only terms depending on
{P}_{s|(s-1)}^{\bm{J},\bm{S}_{\bm{J}},\bm{V}_{s}}
and
\bm{\Sigma}_{s}
. Therefore, the optimal
\bm{\Sigma}_{s}
is also
\lambda_{t}(\bm{W})\left(\bm{\Sigma}^{pop}_{\bm{W}}\right)^{\frac{1}{2}}
, which is formally stated as the following lemma.

Lemma 4
.

The optimal
\bm{\Sigma}_{s}
and
P_{s|(s-1)}
in terms of
\operatorname{Gen}_{s}
are the same as
\bm{\Sigma}^{*}_{s}
and
P^{*}_{s|(s-1)}
given by Theorem
2
, respectively.

With the greedy prior derived, we apply it back to the generalization bound
\operatorname{Gen}_{T}
. As
\operatorname{Gen}_{T}
depends on
\bm{\Sigma}_{s}
also through
\mathbb{E}_{\bm{S}_{\bm{J}^{c}}\sim\mathcal{D}}\operatorname{KL}\left({P}_{s|(s-1)}^{\bm{J},\bm{S}_{\bm{J}},\bm{V}_{s}}\left\|Q_{s|(s-1)}^{\bm{S},\bm{V}_{s}}\right.\right)
, by applying Lemma
3
again, we derive Theorem
2
.

5
Extension: Optimal Noise Covariance with Fixed Priors

In existing works
[
25
,
12
]
, the prior distribution is set to be the SGLD with isotropic noise, which (with the notations in Theorem
1
) is given by

\displaystyle\tilde{\mathcal{M}}_{t}(\bm{W}_{t-1},\bm{S}_{\bm{J}},\bm{V}_{t},\bm{J})

\displaystyle~~~~~~~~~=\bm{W}_{t-1}-\eta_{t}\left(\frac{|\bm{V}_{t}\cap\bm{J}|}{|\bm{V}_{t}|}\nabla\mathcal{R}_{\bm{S}_{\bm{V}_{t}\cap\bm{J}}}\left(\bm{W}_{t-1}\right)+\frac{|\bm{V}_{t}\cap\bm{J}^{c}|}{|\bm{V}_{t}|}\nabla\mathcal{R}_{\bm{S}_{\bm{J}}}\left(\bm{W}_{t-1}\right)\right)+\mathcal{N}\left(\bm{0},\sigma_{t}\mathbb{I}_{d}\right),

(10)

where
\sigma_{t}>0
is the noise scale of prior noise covariance. In our latter analysis, we generalize the prior by allowing
\sigma_{t}
depend on
\bm{W}_{t-1}
. The formal description of the iteration of the prior is deferred to Appendix
E.1
for completeness.

Therefore, it is also interesting to see what the optimal noise covariance looks like if the prior is fixed as the one commonly adopted in the existing analyses, e.g., Eq. (
10
). We still set the optimization constraint the same as Section
4
, but change the optimization target a little to

\widetilde{\operatorname{Gen}}_{T}\overset{\triangle}{=}\mathbb{E}_{\bm{S}}\sqrt{\frac{(a_{2}-a_{1})^{2}}{2}\mathbb{E}_{\bm{V}_{[T]},\bm{J}}\operatorname{KL}\left(P^{\bm{J},\bm{S}_{\bm{J}},\bm{V}_{[T]}}\left\|Q^{\bm{S},\bm{V}_{[T]}}\right.\right)}.

By Jensen’s Inequality and Theorem
1
,
\widetilde{\operatorname{Gen}}_{T}
is still a generalization bound, but allows us to treat the expectation of the KL divergence with respect to
\bm{V}_{[T]}
and
\bm{J}
for given
\bm{S}
as a whole in optimization. Similar trick is also adopted in
[
25
]
to obtain the final generalization error of SGLD. The problem can be mathematically formulated as the following optimization problem
(P2)
:

\textbf{(P2)}\;\;\;\;\;\min_{\bm{\Sigma}_{[T]}}\widetilde{\operatorname{Gen}}_{T}(P,\bm{\Sigma}_{[T]}),\text{ subject to: Constraint \ref{constraint: trace}},

where
P
is given by the update rule Eq. (
10
). As
\bm{S}_{\bm{J}}
is obtained by removing only one sample from the dataset
\bm{S}
and the size of
\bm{S}
is usually large in practice, it is reasonable to make the assumption that for any fixed
\bm{V}_{[T]}
and
\bm{S}
, the prior distribution is the same regardless of
\bm{J}
.

Assumption 1
.

For any fixed dataset
\bm{S}
and mini-batches
\bm{V}_{[T]}
, the distribution
P^{\bm{J},\bm{S}_{\bm{J}},\bm{V}_{[T]}}
is invariant of
\bm{J}
.

We also restrict that
c_{t}(\bm{S},\bm{W})\leq d\sigma_{t}
in order to guarantee the noise scale of the prior comparable to that of the posterior.
The optimal noise covariance with prior fixed can then be characterized by the following theorem.

Theorem 3
.

Let prior and posterior be defined as Eq.(
10
) and Eq.(
3
), respectively. Let Assumption
1
hold. Then, the solution of
(P2)
is given by

\bm{\Sigma}^{*}_{t}(\bm{S},\bm{W})=\bm{O}_{\bm{S},\bm{W}}^{sd}\operatorname{Diag}(\tilde{\omega}^{\bm{S},\bm{W}}_{1},\cdots,\tilde{\omega}^{\bm{S},\bm{W}}_{d})\left(\bm{O}_{\bm{S},\bm{W}}^{sd}\right)^{\top},

where
\tilde{\omega}^{\bm{S},\bm{W}}_{i}\geq 0

(i\in[d])
(the exact form is omitted here) and

\left(\bm{O}_{\bm{S},\bm{W}}^{sd}\right)
is the orthogonal matrix that diagonalizes
\bm{\Sigma}^{sd}_{\bm{S},\bm{W}}
as

\bm{\Sigma}^{sd}_{\bm{S},\bm{W}}=\bm{O}_{\bm{S},\bm{W}}^{sd}\operatorname{Diag}(\omega^{\bm{S},\bm{W}}_{1},\cdots,\omega^{\bm{S},\bm{W}}_{d})\left(\bm{O}_{\bm{S},\bm{W}}^{sd}\right)^{\top}.

Moreover, for any
i\neq j
,
\tilde{\omega}^{\bm{S},\bm{W}}_{i}\geq\tilde{\omega}^{\bm{S},\bm{W}}_{j}
if and only if
\omega^{\bm{S},\bm{W}}_{i}\geq\omega^{\bm{S},\bm{W}}_{j}
.

The proof together with the the exact formula of
\tilde{\omega}_{i}^{\bm{S},\bm{W}}
is deferred to Appendix
5
.
From Theorem
3
, the optimal point
(\bm{\Sigma}^{*}_{t})_{t=1}^{T}
is similar to the empirical gradient covariance matrix
\bm{\Sigma}^{sd}
in two ways: 1)
\{\bm{\Sigma}^{*}_{t}\}_{t=1}^{T}
share the same eigenvectors with
\bm{\Sigma}^{sd}
; 2) the corresponding eigenvalues of
\bm{\Sigma}^{*}_{t}
has the same order as
\bm{\Sigma}^{sd}
.
Though the value of
\tilde{\omega}_{i}^{S,W}
is not comparable to
\omega_{i}^{S,W}
because
\Sigma_{t}^{*}
is affected by the prior noise and the posterior noise covariance, which are freely chosen, it can be shown that the condition number of
\Sigma_{t}^{*}
is smaller than
\Sigma^{sd}_{S,W}
(please refer to Appendix
E.4
for details).

Theorem
3
also reveals an interesting correlation between the noise covariance matrices of the prior and posterior. While the optimal noise covariance is affected by the prior noise covariance, it also depends on the distance between the means of the prior and the posterior (see Lemma
13
in Appendix
E.2
), which brings the information of empirical gradient covariance into the optimal noise structure. That being said, the optimal posterior noise covariance is biased to the empirical gradient covariance from prior covariance. We note that such analysis can be easily extended to arbitrary priors.

7
Conclusion and Future Direction

In this paper, we study the optimal noise covariance of SGLD in terms of its generalization ability. Specifically, we first formulate the optimization problem both by deriving constraints from the optimization performance and proposing the optimization target by constructing a new information theoretical bound. We then solve the problem with both greedy optimal prior and fixed prior. Interestingly, we observe that the optimal noise covariance aligns with the empirical gradient covariance, which indicates the superiority of the noise covariance of SGD in terms of generalization.

A.1
Preparations in Probability Theory

The first lemma is a standard result characterizing the KL divergence between two Gaussian distributions.

Lemma 5

(KL divergence between Gaussian distributions)
.

Let
\mathrm{P}_{1}
and
\mathrm{P}_{2}
are multivariate Gaussian distributions on
\mathbb{R}^{d}
with mean and covariance respectively
\mu_{1}
,
\bm{\Sigma}_{1}
and
\mu_{2}
,
\bm{\Sigma}_{2}
. Then the KL divergence between
\mathrm{P}_{1}
and
\mathrm{P}_{2}
are given as follows:

\mathrm{KL}(\mathrm{P}_{1}||\mathrm{P}_{2})=\frac{1}{2}\left(\operatorname{tr}\left(\bm{\Sigma}_{2}^{-1}\bm{\Sigma}_{1}\right)+\left(\mu_{2}-\mu_{1}\right)^{\top}\bm{\Sigma}_{2}^{-1}\left(\mu_{2}-\mu_{1}\right)-d+\ln\left(\frac{\operatorname{det}\bm{\Sigma}_{2}}{\operatorname{det}\bm{\Sigma}_{1}}\right)\right).

We then provide a lemma which gives the expected difference between two uniform sampling variables.

Lemma 6

(Two step sampling)
.

Suppose
\bm{z}
is a discrete random variable with
\mathbb{P}(\bm{z}=\bm{z}_{i})=\frac{1}{N}
,
\forall i=1,2,\cdots,N
, where the support set is
\mathcal{Z}=\{\bm{z}_{1},\cdots,\bm{z}_{N}\}\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}
. Suppose further
\bm{U}
is a random index set with size
b
and sampled uniformly without replacement from
[N]
. Suppose
\bm{V}
is another random index set independent of
\bm{U}
with size
N-1
and sampled uniformly without replacement from
[N]
. Denote subset of
\mathcal{Z}
with index in
\bm{U}\cap\bm{V}^{c}
and
\bm{V}
respectively as
\mathcal{Z}_{\bm{U}\cap\bm{V}^{c}}=\{\bm{z}_{i},i\in\bm{U}\cap\bm{V}^{c}\}
,
\mathcal{Z}_{\bm{V}}=\{\bm{z}_{i},i\in\bm{V}\}
, and the average of
\mathcal{Z}_{\bm{U}\cap\bm{V}^{c}}
and
\mathcal{Z}_{\bm{V}}
respectively as
\bar{\mathcal{Z}}_{\bm{U}\cap\bm{V}^{c}}
and
\bar{\mathcal{Z}}_{\bm{V}}
. Then the following equation holds:

\mathbb{E}_{\bm{U},\bm{V}}\left(\frac{(b-|\bm{U}\cap\bm{V}|)^{2}}{b^{2}}(\bar{\mathcal{Z}}_{\bm{V}}-\bar{\mathcal{Z}}_{\bm{U}\cap\bm{V}^{c}})(\bar{\mathcal{Z}}_{\bm{V}}-\bar{\mathcal{Z}}_{\bm{U}\cap\bm{V}^{c}})^{\top}\right)=\frac{1}{Nb}\left(\frac{N}{N-1}\right)^{2}\mathrm{Cov}(\bm{z}).

Proof.

We rewrite
\mathbb{E}_{\bm{U},\bm{V}}\left(\frac{(b-|\bm{U}\cap\bm{V}|)^{2}}{b^{2}}(\bar{\mathcal{Z}}_{\bm{V}}-\bar{\mathcal{Z}}_{\bm{U}\cap\bm{V}^{c}})(\bar{\mathcal{Z}}_{\bm{V}}-\bar{\mathcal{Z}}_{\bm{U}\cap\bm{V}^{c}})^{\top}\right)
by taking conditional expectation with respect to
|\bm{U}\cap\bm{V}|
as follows:

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{\bm{U},\bm{V}}\left(\frac{(b-|\bm{U}\cap\bm{V}|)^{2}}{b^{2}}(\bar{\mathcal{Z}}_{\bm{V}}-\bar{\mathcal{Z}}_{\bm{U}\cap\bm{V}^{c}})(\bar{\mathcal{Z}}_{\bm{V}}-\bar{\mathcal{Z}}_{\bm{U}\cap\bm{V}^{c}})^{\top}\right)

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}_{|\bm{U}\cap\bm{V}^{c}|}\mathbb{E}^{|\bm{U}\cap\bm{V}^{c}|}_{\bm{U},\bm{V}}\left(\frac{|\bm{U}\cap\bm{V}^{c}|^{2}}{b^{2}}(\bar{\mathcal{Z}}_{\bm{V}}-\bar{\mathcal{Z}}_{\bm{U}\cap\bm{V}^{c}})(\bar{\mathcal{Z}}_{\bm{V}}-\bar{\mathcal{Z}}_{\bm{U}\cap\bm{V}^{c}})^{\top}\right)

\displaystyle=\mathbb{P}(|\bm{U}\cap\bm{V}^{c}|=1)\mathbb{E}_{\bm{V}}\left(\frac{1}{b^{2}}(\bar{\mathcal{Z}}_{\bm{V}}-\bar{\mathcal{Z}}_{\bm{V}^{c}})(\bar{\mathcal{Z}}_{\bm{V}}-\bar{\mathcal{Z}}_{\bm{V}^{c}})^{\top}\right)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{Nb}\left(\frac{N}{N-1}\right)^{2}\mathrm{Cov}(\bm{z}).

The proof is completed.

Lemma 7
.

\bm{X}

\bm{Y}

(\bm{Y},\bm{Z})
can be decomposed into

\displaystyle\operatorname{KL}\left((\bm{X},\bm{Z})\|(\bm{Y},\bm{Z})\right)=\mathbb{E}_{\bm{Z}}\operatorname{KL}\left((\bm{X}|\bm{Z})\|(\bm{Y}|\bm{Z})\right).

Proof.

By the definition of KL divergence,

\displaystyle\operatorname{KL}\left((\bm{X},\bm{Z})\|(\bm{Y},\bm{Z})\right)=

\displaystyle\int\mathbb{P}(\bm{X},\bm{Z})\log\frac{\mathbb{P}(\bm{X},\bm{Z})}{\mathbb{P}(\bm{Y},\bm{Z})}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\int\mathbb{P}(\bm{Z})\mathbb{P}^{\bm{Z}}(\bm{X})\log\frac{\mathbb{P}^{\bm{Z}}(\bm{X})\mathbb{P}(\bm{Z})}{\mathbb{P}^{\bm{Z}}(\bm{Y})\mathbb{P}(\bm{Z})}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\int\mathbb{P}(\bm{Z})\int\mathbb{P}^{\bm{Z}}(\bm{X})\log\frac{\mathbb{P}^{\bm{Z}}(\bm{X})}{\mathbb{P}^{\bm{Z}}(\bm{Y})}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{\bm{Z}}\operatorname{KL}\left((\bm{X}|\bm{Z})\|(\bm{Y}|\bm{Z})\right).

The proof is completed.
∎

Lemma 8
.

Let
\bm{A}
and
\bm{B}
be two
d\times d
real symmetric matrices. Then,
\bm{A}
and
\bm{B}
commute (i.e.,
\bm{A}\bm{B}=\bm{B}\bm{A}
), if and only if there exists an orthogonal matrix
\bm{O}
which can diagonalize
\bm{A}
and
\bm{B}
simultaneously, i.e., both
\bm{O}^{\top}\bm{A}\bm{O}
and
\bm{O}^{\top}\bm{B}\bm{O}
are diagonal.

The next lemma is a key technique to obtain the optimal noise covariance of Theorem
2
and Theorem
3
.

Lemma 9
.

Let
\bm{B}\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times d}
be a (fixed) positive definite matrix with eigenvalues
(\beta_{1},\cdots,\beta_{d})
, where
\beta_{i}\geq 0
. Let
\bm{G}\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times d}
be a positive definite matrix variable with fixed trace
\operatorname{tr}{\bm{G}}=c
, where
c
is a positive constant and
c\leq\operatorname{tr}(\bm{B})
. Then the minimum of
\operatorname{tr}(\bm{G}^{-1}\bm{B})+\ln\operatorname{det}(\bm{G})
is achieved at
\bm{G}=\bm{O}^{\top}\operatorname{Diag}(\alpha_{1},\cdots,\alpha_{d})\bm{O}
, where

\alpha_{i}^{*}=\frac{\sqrt{1-4\lambda^{*}\beta_{i}}-1}{-2\lambda^{*}},

\bm{O}
is any orthogonal matrix which diagonalize
\bm{B}
as

\bm{B}=\bm{O}^{\top}\operatorname{Diag}(\beta_{1},\cdots,\beta_{d})\bm{O},

and
\lambda^{*}\leq 0
is the unique solution of

\sum_{i=1}^{d}\frac{2\beta_{i}}{1+\sqrt{1-4\lambda^{*}\beta_{i}}}=c.

(11)

.

is a monotonously increasing function with respect to

, which guarantees the uniqueness of the solution of

\bm{G}

\bm{G}
and
\bm{B}

can then be obtained by applying Lemma

\bm{B}=\sigma_{t}\mathbb{I}+\frac{\eta_{t}^{2}}{Nb_{t}}\left(\frac{N}{N-1}\right)^{2}\bm{\Sigma}^{sd}_{\bm{S},\bm{W}}

\bm{G}

.

Lemma 10
.

Let
\bm{G}\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times d}
be a positive definite matrix variable with fixed eigenvalues
(\alpha_{i})_{i=1}^{d}
. Specifically, let
\alpha_{1}\geq\alpha_{2}\geq\cdots\geq\alpha_{d}>0
be all the eigenvalues of
\bm{G}
, and
\bm{G}
can be any element from the following set

\{\bm{Q}^{\top}\operatorname{Diag}(\alpha_{1},\cdots,\alpha_{d})\bm{Q}:\bm{Q}\text{ is orthogonal}\}.

Let
\bm{B}
be a fixed positive semi-definite matrix, with eigenvalues
(\beta_{i})_{i=1}^{d}
satisfies
\beta_{1}\geq\beta_{2}\cdots\geq\beta_{d}\geq 0
.
Then, the optimal (minimal) value of
g(\bm{G})=\operatorname{tr}(\bm{G}^{-1}\bm{B})
is achieved when

\bm{G}^{*}=\bm{O}^{\top}\operatorname{Diag}(\alpha_{1},\cdots,\alpha_{d})\bm{O},

where
\bm{O}
is any orthogonal matrix which diagonalizes
\bm{B}
as

\bm{B}=\bm{O}^{\top}\operatorname{Diag}(\beta_{1},\cdots,\beta_{d})\bm{O}

and the optimal value of
g(\bm{G})
is
\sum_{i=1}^{d}\frac{\beta_{i}}{\alpha_{i}}
.

Proof.

Let
\bm{G}^{*}
be a optimal point of
\operatorname{tr}(\bm{G}^{-1}\bm{B})
. We will then obtain the condition of
\bm{G}^{*}
by adding a disturbance. Specifically, let
\bm{A}
be an anti-symmetric matrix. Then,

(\mathbb{I}-\varepsilon\bm{A})(\mathbb{I}-\varepsilon\bm{A})^{T}=\mathbb{I}+\varepsilon^{2}\bm{A}\bm{A}^{\top}.

As
\varepsilon
is small enough,
\mathbb{I}+\varepsilon^{2}\bm{A}\bm{A}^{\top}
is inevitable, and positive definite. Therefore,
(\mathbb{I}-\varepsilon\bm{A})(\mathbb{I}+\varepsilon^{2}\bm{A}\bm{A}^{\top})^{-\frac{1}{2}}
is orthogonal. As
\varepsilon\rightarrow 0
,

\lim_{\varepsilon\rightarrow 0}(\mathbb{I}-\varepsilon\bm{A})(\mathbb{I}+\varepsilon^{2}\bm{A}\bm{A}^{\top})^{-\frac{1}{2}}=\mathbb{I},

and

(\mathbb{I}-\varepsilon\bm{A})(\mathbb{I}+\varepsilon^{2}\bm{A}\bm{A}^{\top})^{-\frac{1}{2}}-\mathbb{I}=-\varepsilon\bm{A}+\bm{o}(\varepsilon).

Since
\bm{G}^{*}
is an optimal point of
\operatorname{tr}(\bm{G}^{-1}\bm{B})
, we have

\displaystyle\operatorname{tr}((\bm{G}^{*})^{-1}\bm{B})\leq

\displaystyle\operatorname{tr}\left((\mathbb{I}-\varepsilon\bm{A})(\mathbb{I}+\varepsilon^{2}\bm{A}\bm{A}^{\top})^{-\frac{1}{2}}(\bm{G}^{*})^{-1}\left((\mathbb{I}-\varepsilon\bm{A})(\mathbb{I}+\varepsilon^{2}\bm{A}\bm{A}^{\top})^{-\frac{1}{2}}\right)^{\top}\bm{B}\right)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\operatorname{tr}\left((\mathbb{I}-\varepsilon\bm{A})(\mathbb{I}+\varepsilon^{2}\bm{A}\bm{A}^{\top})^{-\frac{1}{2}}(\bm{G}^{*})^{-1}(\mathbb{I}+\varepsilon^{2}\bm{A}\bm{A}^{\top})^{-\frac{1}{2}}(\mathbb{I}+\varepsilon\bm{A})\bm{B}\right),

which further leads to

\displaystyle-\varepsilon\operatorname{tr}\left(\bm{A}(\bm{G}^{*})^{-1}\bm{B}\right)+\varepsilon\operatorname{tr}\left((\bm{G}^{*})^{-1}\bm{A}\bm{B}\right)+\bm{o}(\varepsilon)\geq 0.

By letting
\varepsilon\rightarrow 0
, we further have

\displaystyle-\operatorname{tr}\left(\bm{A}(\bm{G}^{*})^{-1}\bm{B}\right)+\operatorname{tr}\left((\bm{G}^{*})^{-1}\bm{A}\bm{B}\right)=0,

which further leads to

\displaystyle 0=

\displaystyle-\operatorname{tr}\left(\bm{A}(\bm{G}^{*})^{-1}\bm{B}\right)+\operatorname{tr}\left((\bm{G}^{*})^{-1}\bm{A}\bm{B}\right)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\operatorname{tr}\left(\bm{A}^{\top}(\bm{G}^{*})^{-1}\bm{B}\right)+\operatorname{tr}\left(\bm{B}(\bm{G}^{*})^{-1}\bm{A}\right)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle 2\operatorname{tr}\left(\bm{B}(\bm{G}^{*})^{-1}\bm{A}\right).

(12)

Since Eq.(
12
) holds for any anti-symmetry matrix
\bm{A}
, let
\bm{A}=\bm{E}_{i,j}-\bm{E}_{j,i}
, where
i,j\in[d]
and
i\neq j
. By Eq.(
12
), we have

\displaystyle\left(\bm{B}(\bm{G}^{*})^{-1}\right)_{i,j}=\left(\bm{B}(\bm{G}^{*})^{-1}\right)_{j,i},

which further leads to

\displaystyle\bm{B}(\bm{G}^{*})^{-1}=\left(\bm{B}(\bm{G}^{*})^{-1}\right)^{\top}=(\bm{G}^{*})^{-\top}\bm{B}^{\top}=(\bm{G}^{*})^{-1}\bm{B}.

By simple rearranging, we have

\displaystyle\bm{G}^{*}\bm{B}=\bm{B}\bm{G}^{*}.

Therefore, by Lemma
8
, we have that there exists an orthogonal matrix
\bm{O}_{0}
, such that both
\bm{O}_{0}\bm{G}^{*}\bm{O}_{0}^{\top}
and
\bm{O}_{0}\bm{B}\bm{O}_{0}^{\top}
are diagonal. By multiplying a permutation matrix, we further have there exists an orthogonal matrix
\tilde{\bm{O}}
such that
\tilde{\bm{O}}\bm{G}^{*}\tilde{\bm{O}}^{\top}
is diagonal, and

\tilde{\bm{O}}\bm{B}\tilde{\bm{O}}^{\top}=\operatorname{Diag}(\beta_{1},\cdots,\beta_{d}).

(13)

Since
\tilde{\bm{O}}\bm{G}^{*}\tilde{\bm{O}}^{\top}
is diagonal, there exists a permutation mapping
\mathcal{T}:[d]\rightarrow[d]
, such that

\tilde{\bm{O}}\bm{G}^{*}\tilde{\bm{O}}^{\top}=\operatorname{Diag}\left(\alpha_{\mathcal{T}(1)},\cdots,\alpha_{\mathcal{T}(d)}\right).

(14)

Denote the order of
\beta_{i}

(i=1,2,\cdots,d)
as

\displaystyle\beta_{1}=\cdots=\beta_{s_{1}}>\beta_{s_{1}+1}=\cdots=\beta_{s_{1}+s_{2}}>\cdots>\beta_{\sum_{i=1}^{k-1}s_{i}+1}=\cdots=\beta_{\sum_{i=1}^{k}s_{i}}>0,

(15)

where
\sum_{i=1}^{k}s_{i}=d
, and we denote
s_{0}=0
. Since
\bm{G}^{*}
is the optimal point of
\operatorname{tr}((\bm{G}^{*})^{-1}\bm{B})
, for any
1\leq i<j\leq d
and
\beta_{i}>\beta_{j}
, we have
\alpha_{\mathcal{T}(i)}>\alpha_{\mathcal{T}(j)}
: otherwise, let

\bm{G}^{\prime}=\tilde{\bm{O}}^{\top}\operatorname{Diag}\left(\alpha_{\mathcal{T}(1)},\cdots,\alpha_{\mathcal{T}(i-1)},\alpha_{\mathcal{T}(j)},\alpha_{\mathcal{T}(i+1)},\cdots,\alpha_{\mathcal{T}(j-1)},\alpha_{\mathcal{T}(i)},\alpha_{\mathcal{T}(j+1)},\cdots,\alpha_{\mathcal{T}(d)}\right)\tilde{\bm{O}},

we have

\operatorname{tr}((\bm{G}^{*})^{-1}\bm{B})>\operatorname{tr}((\bm{G}^{\prime})^{-1}\bm{B}),

which contradicts that
\bm{G}^{*}
is optimal.

Therefore,
\mathcal{T}(\sum_{i=1}^{j}s_{i}+1),\cdots,\mathcal{T}(\sum_{i=1}^{j+1}s_{i})
is then a permutation of
\sum_{i=1}^{j}s_{i}+1,\cdots,\sum_{i=1}^{j+1}s_{i}
, and there exists permutation matrix
\bm{Q}
such that

\bm{Q}=\operatorname{Diag}\left(\bm{Q}_{1},\cdots,\bm{Q}_{k}\right),

(16)

where
\bm{Q}_{i}
is a
s_{i}\times s_{i}
permutation sub-matrix, such that,

\bm{Q}\operatorname{Diag}\left(\alpha_{\mathcal{T}(1)},\cdots,\alpha_{\mathcal{T}(d)}\right)\bm{Q}^{\top}=\operatorname{Diag}\left(\alpha_{1},\cdots,\alpha_{d}\right).

(17)

Furthermore, by Eq.(
16
) and Eq.(
15
), we have

\bm{Q}\operatorname{Diag}\left(\beta_{1},\cdots,\beta_{d}\right)\bm{Q}^{\top}=\operatorname{Diag}\left(\beta_{1},\cdots,\beta_{d}\right).

(18)

Therefore, by Eqs.(
13
), (
14
), (
17
), and (
18
), we have

\displaystyle\bm{Q}\tilde{\bm{O}}\bm{B}\left(\bm{Q}\tilde{\bm{O}}\right)^{\top}=\operatorname{Diag}\left(\beta_{1},\cdots,\beta_{d}\right),

\displaystyle\bm{Q}\tilde{\bm{O}}\bm{G}^{*}\left(\bm{Q}\tilde{\bm{O}}\right)^{\top}=\operatorname{Diag}\left(\alpha_{1},\cdots,\alpha_{d}\right).

Furthermore,

\displaystyle\operatorname{tr}\left(\bm{G}^{-1}\bm{B}\right)

\displaystyle=\operatorname{tr}\left(\left(\bm{Q}\tilde{\bm{O}}\right)^{\top}\operatorname{Diag}\left(\alpha^{-1}_{1},\cdots,\alpha^{-1}_{d}\right)\left(\bm{Q}\tilde{\bm{O}}\right)\left(\bm{Q}\tilde{\bm{O}}\right)^{\top}\operatorname{Diag}\left(\beta_{1},\cdots,\beta_{d}\right)\left(\bm{Q}\tilde{\bm{O}}\right)\right)

\displaystyle=\sum_{i=1}^{d}\frac{\beta_{i}}{\alpha_{i}}.

Therefore, the optimal value of
\operatorname{tr}(\bm{G}^{-1}\bm{B})
is
\sum_{i=1}^{d}\frac{\beta_{i}}{\alpha_{i}}
, and the corresponding optimal point
\bm{G}^{*}
belongs to the following set

\mathcal{G}=\{\bm{O}^{\top}\operatorname{Diag}(\alpha_{1},\cdots,\alpha_{d})\bm{O}:\bm{B}=\bm{O}^{\top}\operatorname{Diag}(\beta_{1},\cdots,\beta_{d})\bm{O}\}.

On the other hand, it is easy to verify that for any element
\bm{G}\in\mathcal{G}
,

\operatorname{tr}(\bm{G}^{-1}\bm{B})=\sum_{i=1}^{d}\frac{\beta_{i}}{\alpha_{i}}.

The proof is completed.
∎

Lemma
10
indicates that with eigenvalues fixed, the eigenvectors of
\bm{G}
should agree with those of
\bm{B}
by the order of eigenvalues. We then provide the following lemma to determine the optimal eigenvalues.

Lemma 11
.

Let
\beta_{1},\beta_{2},\cdots,\beta_{d}
be a series of fixed positive reals. Let
\alpha_{1},\alpha_{2},\cdots,\alpha_{d}\in\mathbb{R}^{+}
be a series of real variables with constraint
\sum_{i=1}^{d}\alpha_{i}=c
, where
c
is a positive real constant which satisfies
c\leq\sum_{i=1}^{d}\beta_{i}
. Then the minimum of function

f(\alpha_{1},\cdots,\alpha_{d})=\sum_{i=1}^{d}\frac{\beta_{i}}{\alpha_{i}}+\sum_{i=1}^{d}\ln\alpha_{i}

is achieved at

\alpha_{i}^{*}=\frac{\sqrt{1-4\lambda^{*}\beta_{i}}-1}{-2\lambda^{*}},

where
\lambda^{*}\leq 0
is the unique solution of

\sum_{i=1}^{d}\frac{2\beta_{i}}{1+\sqrt{1-4\lambda^{*}\beta_{i}}}=c.

Proof.

We find the minimum of
f
under the constraint that
\alpha_{1}+\cdots+\alpha_{d}=c
by the method of Lagrange Multiplier. Specifically, as for any
i\in[d]
,
\alpha_{i}\rightarrow 0^{+}
or
\alpha_{i}\rightarrow c^{-}
will lead to
f(\alpha_{1},\cdots,\alpha_{d})\rightarrow\infty
, we have that for any global optimal (minimal) point
(\alpha_{1}^{*},\cdots,\alpha_{d}^{*})
of
f
under the constraint
\alpha_{1}+\cdots+\alpha_{d}=c
, we have that there exist a real
\lambda^{*}
, such that
((\alpha_{1}^{*},\cdots,\alpha_{d}^{*}),\lambda^{*})
is a saddle point of
\mathcal{L}((\alpha_{1},\cdots,\alpha_{d}),\lambda)
, which is defined as

\mathcal{L}((\alpha_{1},\cdots,\alpha_{d}),\lambda)=f(\alpha_{1},\cdots,\alpha_{d})+\lambda(c-\alpha_{1}-\cdots-\alpha_{d}).

By taking partial derivative of
\mathcal{L}
with respect to
\alpha_{i}
, we have

-\lambda^{*}=-\frac{1}{\alpha_{i}}+\frac{\beta_{i}}{\alpha_{i}^{2}}=\frac{\beta_{i}-\alpha_{i}}{\alpha_{i}^{2}},

(19)

which further leads to

\sum_{i=1}^{d}\beta_{i}-c=\sum_{i=1}^{d}\left(\beta_{i}-\alpha_{i}\right)=-\lambda^{*}\left(\sum_{i=1}^{d}\alpha_{i}^{2}\right).

Since
\sum_{i=1}^{d}\beta_{i}\geq c
, we have
\lambda^{*}\leq 0
. Therefore, for any
i\in[d]
, the quadratic equation
\beta_{i}x^{2}-x+\lambda^{*}=0
has only one positive solution
\frac{1+\sqrt{1-4\lambda^{*}\beta_{i}}}{2\beta_{i}}
, and

\alpha_{i}^{*}=\frac{2\beta_{i}}{1+\sqrt{1-4\lambda^{*}\beta_{i}}}=\frac{\sqrt{1-4\lambda^{*}\beta_{i}}-1}{-2\lambda^{*}}.

On the other hand, by taking derivative of
\mathcal{L}
with respect to
\lambda^{*}
, we have

\sum_{i=1}^{d}\alpha_{i}^{*}=\sum_{i=1}^{d}\frac{2\beta_{i}}{1+\sqrt{1-4\lambda^{*}\beta_{i}}}=c.

(20)

Since
\sum_{i=1}^{d}\alpha_{i}^{*}=\sum_{i=1}^{d}\frac{2\beta_{i}}{1+\sqrt{1-4\lambda^{*}\beta_{i}}}
is a monotonously increasing function of
\lambda^{*}
, there is only one solution of
\lambda^{*}
of Eq.(
20
).

The proof is completed.
∎

The proof is completed.

Proof of Theorem

.

For any two random measures

, by the Donsker-Varadhan variational formula
[
3
]
, for any function
g

\displaystyle\operatorname{KL}(P^{\bm{J},\bm{S}_{\bm{J}},\bm{V}_{[T]}}||Q^{\bm{S},\bm{V}_{[T]}})\geq P^{\bm{J},\bm{S}_{\bm{J}},\bm{V}_{[T]}}(g)-Q^{\bm{S},\bm{V}_{[T]}}(g)-\log Q^{\bm{S},\bm{V}_{[T]}}\left(\exp(g-Q^{\bm{S},\bm{V}_{[T]}}(g))\right).

Letting

, we further have

\displaystyle\operatorname{KL}(P^{\bm{J},\bm{S}_{\bm{J}},\bm{V}_{[T]}}||Q^{\bm{S},\bm{V}_{[T]}})

\displaystyle\geq\lambda\left(\mathcal{R}_{\mathcal{D}}(Q^{\bm{S},\bm{V}_{[T]}})-\hat{\mathcal{R}}_{\bm{S}_{\bm{J}^{c}}}(Q^{\bm{S},\bm{V}_{[T]}})-\left(\mathcal{R}_{\mathcal{D}}(P^{\bm{J},\bm{S}_{\bm{J}},\bm{V}_{[T]}})-\hat{\mathcal{R}}_{\bm{S}_{\bm{J}^{c}}}(P^{\bm{J},\bm{S}_{\bm{J}},\bm{V}_{[T]}})\right)\right)

\displaystyle\;\;-\log Q^{\bm{S},\bm{V}_{[T]}}\left(\exp\left(\lambda\left(\hat{\mathcal{R}}_{\bm{S}_{\bm{J}^{c}}}-\mathcal{R}_{\mathcal{D}}-\left(\hat{\mathcal{R}}_{\bm{S}_{\bm{J}^{c}}}(Q^{\bm{S},\bm{V}_{[T]}})-\mathcal{R}_{\mathcal{D}}(Q^{\bm{S},\bm{V}_{[T]}})\right)\right)\right)\right).

On the other hand, since
\ell\in[a_{1},a_{2}]
,
\lambda\left(\hat{\mathcal{R}}_{\bm{S}_{\bm{J}^{c}}}(\bm{W})-\mathcal{R}_{\mathcal{D}}(\bm{W})\right)
is
\frac{\lambda(a_{2}-a_{1})}{2}
subgaussian.
Therefore,

\displaystyle\left(\mathcal{R}_{\mathcal{D}}(Q^{\bm{S},\bm{V}_{[T]}})-\hat{\mathcal{R}}_{\bm{S}_{\bm{J}^{c}}}(Q^{\bm{S},\bm{V}_{[T]}})\right)-\left(\mathcal{R}_{\mathcal{D}}(P^{\bm{J},\bm{S}_{\bm{J}},\bm{V}_{[T]}})-\hat{\mathcal{R}}_{\bm{S}_{\bm{J}^{c}}}(P^{\bm{J},\bm{S}_{\bm{J}},\bm{V}_{[T]}})\right)

\displaystyle\leq\inf_{\lambda>0}\frac{\operatorname{KL}(P^{\bm{J},\bm{S}_{\bm{J}},\bm{V}_{[T]}}||Q^{\bm{S},\bm{V}_{[T]}})+\frac{1}{8}\lambda^{2}(a_{2}-a_{1})^{2}}{\lambda}.

Since
P^{\bm{J},\bm{S}_{\bm{J}},\bm{V}_{[T]}}
is independent of
\bm{S}_{\bm{J}^{c}}
then we have
\mathbb{E}^{\bm{S}_{\bm{J}},\bm{J},\bm{V}_{[T]}}\left[\mathcal{R}_{\mathcal{D}}(P^{\bm{J},\bm{S}_{\bm{J}},\bm{V}_{[T]}})-\hat{\mathcal{R}}_{\bm{S}_{\bm{J}^{c}}}(P^{\bm{J},\bm{S}_{\bm{J}},\bm{V}_{[T]}})\right]=0
.
Hence, by averaging over
\bm{S}_{\bm{J}^{c}}
(equivalently, taking the conditional expectation conditional on
(\bm{S}_{\bm{J}},\bm{J},\bm{V}_{[T]})
) we have, with probability one

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}^{\bm{S}_{\bm{J}},\bm{J},\bm{V}_{[T]}}\left[\mathcal{R}_{\mathcal{D}}(Q^{\bm{S},\bm{V}_{[T]}})-\hat{\mathcal{R}}_{\bm{S}_{\bm{J}^{c}}}(Q^{\bm{S},\bm{V}_{[T]}})\right]

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}^{\bm{S}_{\bm{J}},\bm{J},\bm{V}_{[T]}}\left[\mathcal{R}_{\mathcal{D}}(Q^{\bm{S},\bm{V}_{[T]}})-\hat{\mathcal{R}}_{\bm{S}_{\bm{J}^{c}}}(Q^{\bm{S},\bm{V}_{[T]}})-\left(\mathcal{R}_{\mathcal{D}}(P^{\bm{J},\bm{S}_{\bm{J}},\bm{V}_{[T]}})-\hat{\mathcal{R}}_{\bm{S}_{\bm{J}^{c}}}(P^{\bm{J},\bm{S}_{\bm{J}},\bm{V}_{[T]}})\right)\right]

\displaystyle\leq\mathbb{E}^{\bm{S}_{\bm{J}},\bm{J},\bm{V}_{[T]}}\left(\inf_{\lambda>0}\frac{\operatorname{KL}(P^{\bm{J},\bm{S}_{\bm{J}},\bm{V}_{[T]}}||Q^{\bm{S},\bm{V}_{[T]}})+\frac{1}{8}\lambda^{2}(a_{2}-a_{1})^{2}}{\lambda}\right)

Finally, by taking the full expectation, since
J\perp\!\!\!\perp Q^{\bm{S},\bm{V}_{[T]}}
we get:

\mathbb{E}_{\bm{S},\bm{V}_{[T]}}\left[\mathcal{R}_{\mathcal{D}}(Q^{\bm{S},\bm{V}_{[T]}})-\hat{\mathcal{R}}_{\bm{S}}(Q^{\bm{S},\bm{V}_{[T]}})\right]\leq\mathbb{E}_{\bm{S},\bm{V}_{[T]},\bm{J}}\left[\inf_{\lambda>0}\frac{\operatorname{KL}(P^{\bm{J},\bm{S}_{\bm{J}},\bm{V}_{[T]}}||Q^{\bm{S},\bm{V}_{[T]}})+\frac{1}{8}\lambda^{2}(a_{2}-a_{1})^{2}}{\lambda}\right]

where the final
\operatorname{KL}(P^{\bm{J},\bm{S}_{\bm{J}},\bm{V}_{[T]}}||Q^{\bm{S},\bm{V}_{[T]}})
on the right hand side is between two random measures, and hence is a random variable depending on
(\bm{S},\bm{J},\bm{V}_{[T]})
; and the expectation on the right hand side integrates over
(\bm{S},\bm{J},\bm{V}_{[T]})
.

Since

\frac{\operatorname{KL}(P^{\bm{J},\bm{S}_{\bm{J}},\bm{V}_{[T]}}||Q^{\bm{S},\bm{V}_{[T]}})+\frac{1}{8}\lambda^{2}(a_{2}-a_{1})^{2}}{\lambda}\geq\sqrt{\frac{1}{2}(a_{2}-a_{1})^{2}\operatorname{KL}(P^{\bm{J},\bm{S}_{\bm{J}},\bm{V}_{[T]}}||Q^{\bm{S},\bm{V}_{[T]}})},

the proof is completed.

E.2
Calculation of the Generalization Bound

To obtain the optimal noise covariance of
(P2)
, we first derive the explicit form of the generalization bound
\widetilde{\operatorname{Gen}}_{T}
with the prior given by Eq. (
10
) as the following lemma:

Lemma 13

(Calculate
\widetilde{\operatorname{Gen}}_{T}
)
.

Let Assumption
1
hold. Let the prior
P
is given by the update rule Eq. (
10
). Then, the generalization bound
\widetilde{\operatorname{Gen}}_{T}
can be represented as

\widetilde{\operatorname{Gen}}_{T}=\mathbb{E}_{\bm{S}_{\bm{J}}}\sqrt{\frac{(a_{2}-a_{1})^{2}}{2}\sum_{t=1}^{T}\mathbb{E}_{\bm{S}_{\bm{J}^{c}},\bm{V}_{[t-1]}}\mathbb{E}_{P_{s-1}^{\bm{J},\bm{S}_{\bm{J}},\bm{V}_{[s-1]}}}A_{t}(\bm{S},\bm{W}_{t-1})},

where
A_{t}(\bm{S},\bm{W})
is given as

\displaystyle A_{t}(\bm{S},\bm{W})\overset{\triangle}{=}

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}\left(\sigma_{t}(\bm{W})\operatorname{tr}\left(\bm{\Sigma}_{t}(\bm{S},\bm{W})^{-1}\right)+\ln\left(\operatorname{det}\bm{\Sigma}_{t}(\bm{S},\bm{W})\right)-d\right)

\displaystyle+\frac{\eta_{t}^{2}}{2Nb_{t}}\left(\frac{N}{N-1}\right)^{2}\operatorname{tr}\left(\bm{\Sigma}_{t}(\bm{S})^{-1}\bm{\Sigma}^{sd}_{\bm{S},\bm{W}_{t-1}}\right)-\frac{1}{2}d\ln\sigma_{t}(\bm{W}).

Proof.

By the definition of
\widetilde{\operatorname{Gen}}_{T}
, we have

\widetilde{\operatorname{Gen}}_{T}=\mathbb{E}_{\bm{S}}\sqrt{\frac{(a_{2}-a_{1})^{2}}{2}\mathbb{E}_{\bm{V}_{[T]},\bm{J}}\operatorname{KL}\left(P^{\bm{J},\bm{S}_{\bm{J}},\bm{V}_{[T]}}\left\|Q^{\bm{S},\bm{V}_{[T]}}\right.\right)},

which by the decomposition of KL divergence (Lemma
1
) further leads to

\displaystyle\widetilde{\operatorname{Gen}}_{T}=

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{\bm{S}}\sqrt{\frac{(a_{2}-a_{1})^{2}}{2}\mathbb{E}_{\bm{V}_{[T]},\bm{J}}\sum_{s=1}^{T}\mathbb{E}_{P_{s-1}^{\bm{J},\bm{S}_{\bm{J}},\bm{V}_{[T]}}}\operatorname{KL}\left(P_{s|(s-1)}^{\bm{J},\bm{S}_{\bm{J}},\bm{V}_{[T]}}\left\|Q_{s|(s-1)}^{\bm{S},\bm{V}_{[T]}}\right.\right)}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{\bm{S}}\sqrt{\frac{(a_{2}-a_{1})^{2}}{2}\sum_{s=1}^{T}\mathbb{E}_{\bm{V}_{[s]},\bm{J}}\mathbb{E}_{P_{s-1}^{\bm{J},\bm{S}_{\bm{J}},\bm{V}_{[s-1]}}}\operatorname{KL}\left(P_{s|(s-1)}^{\bm{J},\bm{S}_{\bm{J}},\bm{V}_{s}}\left\|Q_{s|(s-1)}^{\bm{S},\bm{V}_{s}}\right.\right)}

\displaystyle\overset{(*)}{=}

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{\bm{S}}\sqrt{\frac{(a_{2}-a_{1})^{2}}{2}\sum_{s=1}^{T}\mathbb{E}_{\bm{V}_{[s-1]}}\mathbb{E}_{P_{s-1}^{\bm{J},\bm{S}_{\bm{J}},\bm{V}_{[s-1]}}}\mathbb{E}_{\bm{V}_{s},\bm{J}}\operatorname{KL}\left(P_{s|(s-1)}^{\bm{J},\bm{S}_{\bm{J}},\bm{V}_{s}}\left\|Q_{s|(s-1)}^{\bm{S},\bm{V}_{s}}\right.\right)}

\displaystyle\overset{(**)}{=}

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{\bm{S}}\sqrt{\frac{(a_{2}-a_{1})^{2}}{2}\sum_{s=1}^{T}\mathbb{E}_{\bm{V}_{[s-1]},\bm{J}}\mathbb{E}_{P_{s-1}^{\bm{J},\bm{S}_{\bm{J}},\bm{V}_{[s-1]}}}\mathbb{E}_{\bm{V}_{s},\bm{J}}\operatorname{KL}\left(P_{s|(s-1)}^{\bm{J},\bm{S}_{\bm{J}},\bm{V}_{s}}\left\|Q_{s|(s-1)}^{\bm{S},\bm{V}_{s}}\right.\right)},

where in Eq.
(*)
we exchange the order between
\mathbb{E}_{P_{s-1}^{\bm{J},\bm{S}_{\bm{J}},\bm{V}_{[s-1]}}}
and
\mathbb{E}_{\bm{V}_{s},\bm{J}}
due to Assumption
1
, and Eq.
(**)
is due to that
\mathbb{E}_{P_{s-1}^{\bm{J},\bm{S}_{\bm{J}},\bm{V}_{[s-1]}}}\mathbb{E}_{\bm{V}_{s},\bm{J}}\operatorname{KL}\left(P_{s|(s-1)}^{\bm{J},\bm{S}_{\bm{J}},\bm{V}_{s}}\left\|Q_{s|(s-1)}^{\bm{S},\bm{V}_{s}}\right.\right)
is independent of
\bm{J}
by Assumption
1
.

Therefore, we only need to prove
\mathbb{E}_{\bm{V}_{t},\bm{J}}\operatorname{KL}\left(P_{t|(t-1)}^{\bm{J},\bm{S}_{\bm{J}},\bm{V}_{t}}\left\|Q_{t|(t-1)}^{\bm{S},\bm{V}_{t}}\right.\right)=A(t)
, which can be obtained by

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{\bm{V}_{t},\bm{J}}\operatorname{KL}\left(P_{t|(t-1)}^{\bm{J},\bm{S}_{\bm{J}},\bm{V}_{t}}\left\|Q_{t|(t-1)}^{\bm{S},\bm{V}_{t}}\right.\right)

\displaystyle\overset{(\bullet)}{=}\frac{1}{2}\mathbb{E}_{\bm{V}_{t},\bm{J}}\bigg(\left(\mu^{\bm{S},\bm{V}_{t},\bm{J},\bm{W}_{t-1}}\right)^{\top}\bm{\Sigma}_{t}(\bm{S},\bm{W}_{t-1})^{-1}\mu^{\bm{S},\bm{V}_{t},\bm{J},\bm{W}_{t-1}}+\ln\frac{\det\bm{\Sigma}_{t}(\bm{S},\bm{W}_{t-1})}{\sigma_{t}(\bm{W}_{t-1})^{d}}

\displaystyle\;\;+\operatorname{tr}\left(\sigma_{t}(\bm{W}_{t-1})\bm{\Sigma}_{t}(\bm{S},\bm{W}_{t-1})^{-1}\right)\bigg)-\frac{d}{2}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\mathbb{E}_{\bm{V}_{t},\bm{J}}\bigg(\operatorname{tr}\left(\bm{\Sigma}_{t}(\bm{S},\bm{W}_{t-1})^{-1}\mu^{\bm{S},\bm{V}_{t},\bm{J},\bm{W}_{t-1}}\left(\mu^{\bm{S},\bm{V}_{t},\bm{J},\bm{W}_{t-1}}\right)^{\top}\right)+\ln\frac{\det\bm{\Sigma}_{t}(\bm{S},\bm{W}_{t-1})}{\sigma_{t}(\bm{W}_{t-1})^{d}}

\displaystyle\;\;+\operatorname{tr}\left(\sigma_{t}(\bm{W}_{t-1})\bm{\Sigma}_{t}(\bm{S},\bm{W}_{t-1})^{-1}\right)\bigg)-\frac{d}{2}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\operatorname{tr}\left(\bm{\Sigma}_{t}(\bm{S},\bm{W}_{t-1})^{-1}\mathbb{E}_{\bm{J},\bm{V}_{t}}\mu^{\bm{S},\bm{V}_{t},\bm{J},\bm{W}_{t-1}}\left(\mu^{\bm{S},\bm{V}_{t},\bm{J},\bm{W}_{t-1}}\right)^{\top}\right)+\frac{1}{2}\ln\frac{\det\bm{\Sigma}_{t}(\bm{S},\bm{W}_{t-1})}{\sigma_{t}(\bm{W}_{t-1})^{d}}

\displaystyle\;\;+\frac{1}{2}\operatorname{tr}\left(\sigma_{t}(\bm{W}_{t-1})\bm{\Sigma}_{t}(\bm{S},\bm{W}_{t-1})^{-1}\right)-\frac{d}{2}

\displaystyle\overset{(\circ)}{=}\frac{1}{2}\left(\sigma_{t}(\bm{W}_{t-1})\operatorname{tr}\left(\bm{\Sigma}_{t}(\bm{S},\bm{W}_{t-1})^{-1}\right)+\ln\left(\operatorname{det}\bm{\Sigma}_{t}(\bm{S},\bm{W}_{t-1})\right)-d\right)-\frac{1}{2}d\ln\sigma_{t}(\bm{W}_{t-1})

\displaystyle\;\;+\frac{\eta_{t}^{2}}{2Nb_{t}}\left(\frac{N}{N-1}\right)^{2}\operatorname{tr}\left(\bm{\Sigma}_{t}(\bm{S},\bm{W}_{t-1})^{-1}\bm{\Sigma}^{sd}_{\bm{S},\bm{W}_{t-1}}\right),

where Eq.
(\bullet)

The proof is completed.

Theorem 4
.

Let prior and posterior be defined as Eq.(

\displaystyle\bm{\Sigma}^{*}_{s}(\bm{S},\bm{W})

E.4
Smaller Condition Number

In this section, we demonstrate why the optimal noise of Theorem
3
has smaller condition number than
\bm{\Sigma}^{sd}
as the following corollary.

Corollary 1
.

The optimal noise covariance
\bm{\Sigma}^{*}
given by Theorem
3
has smaller condition number than
\bm{\Sigma}^{sd}
.

Proof.

We prove this claim following two steps.

Firstly, the noise covariance of the prior is isotropic, has condition number
1
, and push the condition number of
\sigma_{t}\mathbb{I}+\frac{\eta_{t}^{2}}{Nb_{t}}\left(\frac{N}{N-1}\right)^{2}\Sigma^{sd}_{\bm{S},\bm{W}}
smaller than
\Sigma^{sd}_{\bm{S},\bm{W}}
.

Secondly, the optimal solution
G
of Lemma
9
always has a smaller condition number than
B
, which implies that
\Sigma_{t}^{*}(\bm{S},\bm{W})
has smaller condition number than
B=\sigma_{t}\mathbb{I}+\frac{\eta_{t}^{2}}{Nb_{t}}\left(\frac{N}{N-1}\right)^{2}\Sigma^{sd}_{\bm{S},\bm{W}}
. Hence the condition number of
\Sigma_{t}^{*}(\bm{S},\bm{W})
is smaller than
\Sigma^{sd}_{\bm{S},\bm{W}}
.
∎
