New Bounds for the Extreme and the Star Discrepancy of Double-Infinite Matrices
Jasmin Fiedler and Michael Gnewuch and Christian Weiß

Abstract
According to Aistleitner and Weimar, there exist two-dimensional (double) infinite matrices whose star-discrepancy D N ∗ s D_{N}^{*s} of the first N N rows and s s columns, interpreted as N N points in [ 0 , 1 ] s [0,1]^{s} , satisfies an inequality of the form D_N^*s ≤ α A+B ln(log 2 (N)) s s N with α = ζ − 1 ​ ( 2 ) ≈ 1.73 , A = 1165 \alpha=\zeta^{-1}(2)\approx 1.73,A=1165 and B = 178 B=178 . These matrices are obtained by using i.i.d sequences, and the parameters s s and N N refer to the dimension and the sample size respectively. In this paper, we improve their result in two directions: First, we change the character of the equation so that the constant A A gets replaced by a value A s A_{s} dependent on the dimension s s such that for s > 1 s>1 we have A s < A A_{s}<A . Second, we generalize the result to the case of the (extreme) discrepancy. The paper is complemented by a section where we show numerical results for the dependence of the parameter A s A_{s} on s s .

Introduction

The task of high-dimensional integration occurs in different practical applications, with one of them being computational finance (see e.g.
Gla 03
,
DHB 20
,
Pas 94
,
WN 19
). Since it is often impossible to give analytical solutions of the integrals, it is necessary to use numerical integration instead. Then the
goodness
of the approximation is in general mainly governed by the star-discrepancy.

Definition 1

For two points
x,y\in[0,1]^{s}
, we write
x\leq y
if the inequality holds component-wise and we set
[0,x):=\left\{y\in[0,1)^{s}\middle|0\leq y<x\right\}
and
[x,y):=\left\{z\in[0,1)^{s}\middle|x\leq z<y\right\}
. Let
(x_{1},\ldots,x_{n})
be a sequence of points in
[0,1)^{s}
. Then we define their (extreme) discrepancy as

D_{N}^{s}(x_{1},\ldots,x_{N})=\sup_{a,b\in[0,1]^{s}}\left|\frac{1}{N}\sum_{k=1}^{N}\mathds{1}_{[a,b)}\left(x_{k}\right)-\mathbb{\lambda}([a,b))\right|,

(1)

where
\mathds{1}_{[a,b)}
is the characteristic function of the interval
[a,b)
and
\mathbb{\lambda}
is the
s
-dimensional Lebesgue measure. Similarly, we define the star-discrepancy as

D_{N}^{*s}(x_{1},\ldots,x_{N})=\sup_{y\in[0,1]^{s}}\left|\frac{1}{N}\sum_{k=1}^{N}\mathds{1}_{[0,y)}\left(x_{k}\right)-\mathbb{\lambda}([0,y))\right|.

(2)

The extreme discrepancy is also known under different names such as unanchored discrepancy, see e.g.
(
DGW 14
, Introduction)
.

The precise dependence of the approximation error on the star-discrepancy is given by the iconic Koksma-Hlawka inequality, see e.g.
(
Nie 92
, Theorem 2.11 and 2.12)
.

Theorem 1.1
(Koksma-Hlawka inequality)

Let
f
be a function on
[0,1]^{s}
with bounded variation
V(f)
in the sense of Hardy and Krause . Then for any sequence
(x_{1},\ldots,x_{n})
of points in
[0,1)^{s}
the inequality

\left|\frac{1}{n}\sum_{k=1}^{n}f(x_{k})-\int_{[0,1]^{s}}f(x)\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}x\right|\leq V(f)\cdot D_{n}^{*s}(x_{1},\ldots,x_{n})

(3)

holds and the inequality is sharp.

Classically, low-discrepancy point sets are used for numerical integration. These are sets which satisfy the conjectured (known as the
great open problem
of discrepancy theory) optimal bound

D_{N}^{*s}(x_{1},\ldots,x_{N})\leq c_{s}\frac{\log(N)^{s-1}}{N},

where
c_{s}
is a constant only depending on the dimension
s
. In fact, the conjecture is trivially true for dimension
1
and has been proven to be correct in dimension
2
by
Sch 72
. However, these low-discrepancy sets (or more precisely the upper bound of their star-discrepancy) suffer from the so-called
curse of dimensionality
, i.e. the star-discrepancy exponentially depends on the dimension. If the sample size is small in comparison to the dimension, they are therefore hardly of practical use. In the paper
HNWW 01
, an alternative approach was therefore suggested. Therein, it was theoretically shown that the smallest achievable star-discrepancy with explicitly given dependence on the number of points as well as on the dimension satisfies

D_{N}^{*s}(x_{1},\ldots,x_{N})\leq C\sqrt{\frac{s}{N}}

without giving an explicit value for
C
. The first explicit value for
C\approx 9.65
was derived in
Ais 11
, while currently, the best known possible value is
C\approx 2.4968
due to
GPW 21
. In
Dic 07
;
DGKP 08
corresponding bounds were derived for double infinite matrices. The best currently known bound is due to
AW 13
.

Remark 1

In this paper,
\zeta:(1,\infty)\to{\mathbb{R}}
denotes the Riemann Zeta function on the reals, which is strictly decreasing, so the inverse
\zeta^{-1}
exists.

Theorem 1.2
(Aistleitner, Weimar,
AW 13
, Theorem 1)

Let
\alpha>\zeta^{-1}(2)\approx 1.73
be arbitrarily fixed. Then with probability strictly larger than
1-(\zeta(\alpha)-1)^{2}\geq 0
the double-infinite matrix
\left(X_{n,s}\right)_{n,s\in{\mathbb{N}}}
with all
X_{n,s}\in[0,1)
independently uniformly distributed satisfies for all

and every

we have

D_{N}^{*S}({\mathcal{P}}_{N,S})\leq\sqrt{\alpha}\sqrt{A+B\frac{\ln(\log_{2}(N))}{S}}\sqrt{\frac{S}{N}},

(4)

where
A=1165
,
B=178
and

{\mathcal{P}}_{N,S}=\left(X_{n,s}\right)_{n,s=1}^{N,S}=\begin{pmatrix}X_{1,1}&X_{1,2}&\cdots&X_{1,S}\\
X_{2,1}&X_{2,2}&\cdots&X_{2,S}\\
\vdots&\vdots&\ddots&\vdots\\
X_{N,1}&X_{N,2}&\cdots&X_{N,S}\end{pmatrix}.

The proof in
AW 13
relied on using the so-called maximal Bernstein inequality for independent random variables which will also be a main tool for our paper.

Theorem 1.3
(Einmahl, Mason,
EM 96
, Lemma 2.2)

Let
X_{1},\ldots,X_{n}
be independent random variables with
\mathbb{E}\left(X_{k}\right)=0
,
\left|X_{k}\right|\leq M<\infty
a.s. and
\variance(X_{k})=\sigma_{k}^{2}<\infty
for all

. Then for every

\mathbb{P}\left(\max_{1\leq k\leq n}\left|\sum_{i=1}^{k}X_{i}\right|>t\right)\leq 2\exp\left(-\frac{t^{2}}{2\sum_{k=1}^{n}\sigma_{k}^{2}+\frac{2}{3}Mt}\right).

(5)

By combining the maximal Bernstein inequality with recent results proven in
GPW 21
, we are able to improve the numerical constants in the formulation of Theorem
1.2
.

Theorem 1.4

Let
\left(X_{n,s}\right)_{n,s\in{\mathbb{N}}}
be a sequence of independent uniformly distributed random variables in
[0,1)
. Furthermore, take
\alpha>1
and
\beta>1
with

(\zeta(\alpha)-1)(\zeta(\beta)-1)<1
. Then we have

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\left(D_{N}^{*s}({\mathcal{P}}_{N,s})\leq\sqrt{\alpha A_{s}+\beta B\frac{\ln(\log_{2}(N))}{s}}\sqrt{\frac{s}{N}}\mathrm{~for~all~}s,N\in{\mathbb{N}}\right)

\displaystyle\ \hskip 142.26378pt>1-(\zeta(\alpha)-1)(\zeta(\beta)-1)

where
B=178
and
A_{2}\leq 942
,
A_{s}
is dependent on
s
and decreases as
s
increases.

Note that for dimension
s=1
, the result is not of a big practical relevance, because in this case low-discrepancy sequences (see e.g.
Nie 92
for a definition) yield better results than random sequences
and would therefore be preferred anyhow. In contrast to Theorem
1.2
, the constant
A_{s}
is chosen dependent on the dimension
s
such that for
s>1
we have
A_{s}<A
. In all practically relevant cases, our result is therefore an improvement in comparison to
Ais 13
. Detailed numerical results on the constant
A_{s}
will be discussed in Section
3
.

In addition and even more importantly, the methods applied here also cover the case of the (extreme) discrepancy. It is known that

D_{N}^{s}(x_{1},\ldots,x_{N})\leq 2^{s}D_{N}^{*s}(x_{1},\ldots,x_{N}),

see for example
(
Mat 99
, 1.4 Observation)
, but this approximation is not useful in determining bounds for the discrepancy that scale well with the dimension. Therefore, we are going to use a different approach in the proof of Theorem
1.5
.

Theorem 1.5

Let
\left(X_{n,s}\right)_{n,s\in{\mathbb{N}}}
be a sequence of independent, uniformly distributed random variables in
[0,1)
. Furthermore, take
\alpha>1
and
\beta>1
with

(\zeta(\alpha)-1)(\zeta(\beta)-1)<1
. Then we have

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\left(D_{N}^{s}({\mathcal{P}}_{N,s})\leq\sqrt{\alpha A_{s}+\beta B\frac{\ln(\log_{2}(N))}{s}}\sqrt{\frac{s}{N}}\mathrm{~for~all~}s,N\in{\mathbb{N}}\right)

\displaystyle\ \hskip 142.26378pt>1-(\zeta(\alpha)-1)(\zeta(\beta)-1)

where
B=178
and
A_{s}\leq 2548
is dependent on
s
and decreases as
s
increases.

The paper is organized as follows: In Section
2
we will apply Theorem

in order to derive bounds for the discrepancy and the star-discrepancy of double infinite matrices. Afterwards, in Section

, we will calculate the numerical values for the implied constants of the discrepancy bounds.

2
Proofs of results

In this section, we will apply the maximal Bernstein inequality from Theorem
1.3
to derive bounds for the discrepancy of double-infinite matrices of elements in
[0,1)
with entries stemming from uniformly distributed, independent random variables. In order to achieve this aim, we amend the structure of
(
AW 13
, Theorem 1)
to our situation. First, we introduce some notation which allows for a coherent presentation of the topic. For
k\geq-1
, let

b_{k}=\begin{cases}3&\text{if }k=-1,0\\
2^{-k+3}(1-2^{-k})&\text{if }k>0\end{cases}.

For a sequence
\left(X_{n,s}\right)_{n,s\in{\mathbb{N}}}
of random variables we define

X^{(N)}_{s}=(X_{N,1},\ldots,X_{N,s})

and

{\mathcal{P}}_{N,s}=\begin{pmatrix}X^{(1)}_{s}\\
X^{(2)}_{s}\\
\vdots\\
X^{(N)}_{s}\end{pmatrix}=\begin{pmatrix}X_{1,1}&X_{1,2}&\cdots&X_{1,s}\\
X_{2,1}&X_{2,2}&\cdots&X_{2,s}\\
\vdots&\vdots&\ddots&\vdots\\
X_{N,1}&X_{N,2}&\cdots&X_{N,s}\end{pmatrix}.

Another technical tool which is needed for the proof are
\delta
-covers and
\delta
-bracketing covers that we introduce next.

Definition 2

Let
\delta>0
and
{\mathcal{A}}\subseteq\mathcal{P}([0,1]^{s})
. A
\delta
-cover for
{\mathcal{A}}
is a set
\Gamma\subseteq{\mathcal{A}}
such that for every
A\in{\mathcal{A}}
there exist
U,V\in\Gamma\cup\{\emptyset\}
satisfying
U\subseteq A\subseteq V
and

\mathbb{\lambda}(V\setminus U)\leq\delta.

A
\delta
-bracketing cover for
{\mathcal{A}}
is a set
\Delta\subseteq{\mathcal{A}}\times{\mathcal{A}}
such that for every
(A,B)\in\Delta
with
A\subseteq B
the inequality

\mathbb{\lambda}(B\setminus A)\leq\delta

holds and for every
A\in{\mathcal{A}}
there exist
(U,V)\in\Delta
with
U\subseteq A\subseteq V
. Furthermore, we define
N(\delta,{\mathcal{A}})
to be the minimal size of a
\delta
-cover for
{\mathcal{A}}
and
N_{[~]}(\delta,{\mathcal{A}})
as the minimal size of a
\delta
-bracketing cover for
{\mathcal{A}}
.

These two concepts are closely related. In fact, it is easy to show that for any
\delta>0
we have

N(\delta,{\mathcal{A}})\leq 2N_{[~]}(\delta,{\mathcal{A}})\leq N(\delta,{\mathcal{A}})(1+N(\delta,{\mathcal{A}})).

In this paper we will mostly use two families of sets, namely

\displaystyle{\mathcal{C}}^{s}=\left\{[0,x)\middle|x\in[0,1]^{s}\right\}

\displaystyle{\mathcal{R}}^{s}=\left\{[x,y)\middle|x,y\in[0,1]^{s}\right\}

The following recent inequality from
GPW 21
, which improved an earlier result from
Gne 08
, give us an estimate on the minimal size of
\delta
-bracketing covers and therefore by extension of
\delta
-covers.

Theorem 2.1

(
GPW 21
, Theorem 2.5.)
Let
C_{s}:=\max\left\{1,1.1^{s-101}\right\}
. Then for
s\in{\mathbb{N}}
the minimal size of a
\delta
-bracketing cover for
{\mathcal{C}}^{s}
has the upper bound

N_{[~]}(\delta,{\mathcal{C}}^{s})\leq C_{s}\frac{s^{s}}{s!}(\delta^{-1}+1)^{s}.

(6)

Additionally, there are also bounds for the sets in
{\mathcal{R}}^{s}
.

Theorem 2.2

(
Gne 08
, Lemma 1.17 and 1.18)

N(\delta,{\mathcal{R}}^{s})\leq N\left(\frac{\delta}{2},{\mathcal{C}}^{s}\right)^{2}

(7)

N_{[~]}(\delta,{\mathcal{R}}^{s})\leq N_{[~]}\left(\frac{\delta}{2},{\mathcal{C}}^{s}\right)^{2}

(8)

Finally, we define

{\mathcal{D}}^{s}:=\left\{A\setminus B\middle|A,B\in{\mathcal{R}}^{s}\right\}.

This puts us into the position to prove Theorem
1.5
. We decided to include the complete proof for the (extreme) discrepancy in our paper because this case is to our knowledge not treated in the existing literature. For the case of the star-discrepancy we will keep our presentation very short to avoid unnecessary repetitions.

Now, if

For

we have
\mathbb{\lambda}(I)\leq 2^{-k}
since

. Basic calculus tells us that the functions

and

take their maxima on the interval

at

or

respectively. Furthermore,
4\lambda(1-\lambda)+2^{-k+1}\lambda
also takes its maximum at
2^{-k}

2\cdot 2^{-k+1}(1-2^{-k})+2^{-k+1}2^{-k}\leq 3\cdot 2^{-k+1}(1-2^{-k})

Lastly, we come to

\max_{\lambda\in[0,2^{-L}]}\{4\lambda(1-\lambda)+2^{-L+2}\}\leq 2^{-L+3}(1-2^{-L}).

Combining all cases, we see that

\mathbb{P}\left(E_{k}(I)\right)\leq\exp\left({-\frac{y_{k,s}\cdot s}{b_{k}}}\right)

(14)

always holds.

4
Future Research

We are currently working on generalizing the approach used in this paper from i.i.d. sequences towards other types of random variables, for example negatively associated and
\gamma
-negatively dependent random variables.
The primary challenge when trying to do so, lies in finding appropriate maximal inequalities for these types of random variables, for example versions of the maximal Bernstein inequality.
There has been extensive research on this topic, see e.g.
Bou 21
;
KM 11
;
Mór 79
;
Sze 13
. We believe that methods from the existing literature can be adapted to the mentioned classes of examples. The next challenge in this context is then to prove the existence of infinite sequences or matrices with such characteristics. To the best of our knowledge, there are no known examples of this apart from some relatively simple ones, so this is another question which is currently wide open.
