Stein’s Method for Distributions Modelling Competing and Complementary Risk Problems
Anum Fatima, Gesine Reinert

Abstract
Competing and Complementary risk (CCR) problems are often modelled using a class of distributions of the maximum, or minimum, of a random number of i.i.d. random variables; we call this class the CCR class of distributions. While the CCR distributions generally do not have an easy-to-calculate density or probability mass function, two special cases, namely the Poisson-exponential and the exponential geometric distributions, can easily be calculated. Hence, it is of interest to approximate CCR distributions with these simpler distributions. In this paper, we develop Stein’s method for the CCR class of distributions to provide a general comparison approach to bound the distance between two CCR distributions and contrast this approach to bounds obtained using a Lindeberg argument. We detail the comparison for Poisson-exponential, and exponential-geometric distributions.



Keywords: Stein’s Method, competing and complementary risk, Poisson-exponential distribution, Poisson-geometric distribution, exponential-geometric distribution, distributional distance

AMS 2020 Subject Classification: 60F05, 60E05

Introduction

Competing and complementary risk (CCR) problems typically focus on the failure of a system composed of multiple components, or a system with several, sometimes even a countably infinite number of risk factors that can cause its failure. Here we think of components and risk factors as having a random lifetime, denoted by a sequence of i.i.d. positive  random variables
X_{1},X_{2},\ldots
; at the end of its lifetime the component fails, or the risk occurs.

CCR systems are either sequential or parallel; in sequential systems, the whole system fails at the occurrence of the first among an unknown number
N
of risk factors; risks compete with each other to cause failure. In this case, the observed failure time is the minimum of the lifetimes of the risk factors. In parallel systems, the system fails after all of an unknown number
N
of risk factors occur, and the observed failure time is the maximum of lifetimes of these risk factors. In both setups, the lifetimes of the components or risks are modelled random, as is the number of risks
N
, which is assumed to be independent of the lifetimes. These two CCR settings are frequently studied together as
\max(X_{1},\ldots,X_{N})=-\min(-X_{1},\ldots,-X_{N})
; see
[
6
]
.

CCR settings arise in many fields such as industrial reliability, demography, biomedical studies, public health, and actuarial sciences. For example, in a study of vertical transmission of HIV from an affected mother to the newborn child, several factors increase the risk of transmission, such as high maternal virus load and low birth weight. Exactly which factors determine the timing of transmission is an ongoing area of research. Even with a list of known risk factors, there are possibly many unknown risk factors involved. Hence the problem is modelled as a CCR problem in
[
35
]
,
[
21
]
,
[
8
]
and
[
20
]
. The number of successive failures of the air conditioning system of each member of a fleet of 13 Boeing 720 jet aeroplanes has been modelled as a CCR problem in
[
17
]
, with potential risk factors including defective components in the air conditioning system or errors committed during the production process. In
[
3
]
the period between successive coal-mining disasters is modelled as a CCR problem, with construction faults or human errors committed by inexperienced miners as examples of risks. The daily ozone concentrations in New York during May–September 1973 is treated as a CCR problem in
[
16
]
.

We call the distributions used to model the lifetimes in CCR problems the
CCR family of distributions
. Examples from this family which are studied in the literature are the exponential-Poisson distribution
[
17
]
, the Poisson-exponential lifetime distribution
[
8
]
, the Weibull–Poisson distribution
[
22
]
, the extended Weibull–Poisson distribution, and the extended generalised extreme value Poisson distribution
[
29
]
.
[
34
]
gives a detailed review of CCR distributions and proposes additional ones.

The probability mass function of a CCR distribution can be quite unwieldy. Hence, it is of interest to approximate a CCR distribution by a simpler CCR distribution such as the Poisson-exponential distribution or the exponential geometric distribution. For the latter distributions, the number of risks, or components, follows a zero-truncated Poisson or geometric distribution, respectively. To assess such approximations we develop Stein’s method for CCR distributions.

The seminal work of Charles Stein
[
32
]
derives bounds on the approximation error for normal approximations. In
[
10
]
, Stein’s method is adapted to Poisson approximation, see also
[
4
]
and
[
5
]
. Generalisations to many other distributions and dimensions are available, see for example
[
9
]
,
[
25
]
, and
[
24
]
. The Stein operators in this paper are based on the density approach; see
[
18
]
. A main difficulty in distributional comparison problems is the comparison of a discrete and a continuous distribution; related works on Stein’s method include
[
15
]
, which bounds the Wasserstein distance between a beta distribution and the distribution of the number of white balls in a Pólya-Eggenberger urn, and
[
14
]
, where standardisations related to the ones we propose are used. Maxima of a fixed number of random variables have been treated with Stein’s method in
[
13

; Stein’s method applied to a random sum of random variables can be found in
[
28
]

In this paper we propose a comparison of the distributions of a maximum (or minimum) of discrete and a maximum (or minimum) of continuous random variables when the number of these random variables is itself an independent random variable. In future work, the Stein characterisations derived in our paper could be employed to construct Stein-based goodness of fit statistics as in
[
7
]
.

The remainder of this paper is organised as follows. Section
2

6

A

Stein’s method for CCR distributions

We use the notation
\mathbb{R}^{+}_{{>0}}=(0,\infty)
and
\mathbb{N}=\{1,2,3,\ldots\}.
The backward difference operator
\Delta^{-}
operates on a function
g:\mathbb{R}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
by
\Delta^{-}g(x)=g(x)-g(x-1)
. We note that
\Delta^{-}(gq)(y)=q(y)\Delta^{-}g(y)+g(y-1)\Delta^{-}q(y)
. For a function
h\in{\rm Lip}_{b}(1)
its derivative is denoted by
h^{\prime}
; it exists almost everywhere (by Rademacher’s theorem).

For two probability distributions

q
and
p
on
\mathbb{R}^{+}_{{>0}}
, we seek bounds on distances of the form

d_{\mathcal{H}}(p,q):=\underset{h\in\mathcal{H}}{\sup}\,|\mathbb{E}h(X)-\mathbb{E}h(Z)|

(2.1)

where
\mathcal{H}
is a set of test functions,
Z\sim q
and
X\sim p
. The sets of functions
\mathcal{H}
in (
2.1
) are, for the total variation distance (
d_{TV}
),
\mathcal{H}=\{\mathbb{I}[.\in A]:A\in\mathcal{B}(\mathbb{R})\}
; and for the bounded Wasserstein distance (
d_{BW}
),

\mathcal{H}={\rm Lip}_{b}(1):=\{h:\mathbb{R}^{+}_{{>0}}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}:|h(x)-h(y)|\leq|x-y|\;\mbox{ for all }\;x,y\in\mathbb{R};\|h\|\leq 1\}.

Here
\mathcal{B}(\mathbb{R})
denotes the Borel sets of
\mathbb{R}
, and
\|\cdot\|
is the supremum norm in
\mathbb{R}
. We note here the alternative formulation for total variation distance based on Borel-measurable functions
h:\mathbb{R}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
;

\displaystyle d_{TV}(p,q)=\frac{1}{2}\underset{\|h\|\leq 1}{\sup}|\mathbb{E}h(X)-\mathbb{E}h(Z)|.

(2.2)

2.1
Stein’s method for distributional comparisons

To obtain explicit bounds on the distance between a probability distribution
\mathcal{L}(X)
of interest and a usually well-understood approximating distribution
\mathcal{L}_{0}(Z)
, often called the
target
distribution, Stein’s method connects the test function
h\in\mathcal{H}
as in (
2.1
) to the distribution of interest through a
Stein Equation

h(x)-\mathbb{E}h(Z)=\mathcal{T}g(x).

(2.3)

In (
2.3
),
\mathcal{T}
is a
Stein operator
for the distribution
\mathcal{L}_{0}(Z)
, with an associated
Stein class

\mathcal{F}(\mathcal{T})
of functions such that
\mathbb{E}[\mathcal{T}g(Z)]=0\mbox{ for all }g\in\mathcal{F}(\mathcal{T})
if and only if
Z\sim\mathcal{L}_{0}(Z);
thus; a Stein operator characterises the distribution. The distance (
2.1
) can then be bounded by
d_{\mathcal{H}}(\mathcal{L}(X),\mathcal{L}_{0}(Z))\leq\underset{g\in\mathcal{F}(\mathcal{H})}{\sup}|\mathbb{E}\mathcal{T}g(X)|
where
\mathcal{F}(\mathcal{H})=\{g_{h}|h\in\mathcal{H}\}
is the set of solutions of the Stein equation (
2.3
) for the set of test functions
h\in\mathcal{H}
.

The Stein operator
\mathcal{T}
for a probability distribution is not unique, see for example
[
18
]
. In this paper, we employ the so-called
density method
which uses the score function of a probability distribution to construct a Stein operator, called a
score Stein operator
. Following
[
33
,
19
]
, a score Stein operator for a continuous distribution with probability density function (pdf)
p
and support
[a,b]\in\mathbb{R}
acts on functions
g
, such that the derivative exists, as

{\mathcal{T}}_{p}g(x)=\frac{(gp)^{\prime}(x)}{p(x)};

(2.4)

here,
0/0=0
. For differentiable
g
and
p
, (
2.4
) simplifies to
{\mathcal{T}}_{p}g(x)=g^{\prime}(x)+g(x)\rho(x)
where
\rho=p^{\prime}/p
is the score function of
p
. The Stein class
{\mathcal{F}}(\mathcal{T}_{p})
is the collection of functions
g:\mathbb{R}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
such that
g(x)p(x)
is differentiable with integrable derivative and
\lim_{x\to{a,b}}g(x)p(x)=0
. It is straightforward to see that for
p
, a pdf on
{[a,b]\subset\mathbb{R}}
,

\displaystyle g(x)=g_{h}(x)=\frac{1}{p(x)}\int_{0}^{x}[h(t)-\mathbb{E}h(X)]p(t)\,\mathrm{d}t

(2.5)

solves (
2.3
) for
h
, and that, if
h
is bounded, then
g\in\mathcal{F}(\mathcal{T}_{p})
.

To use the Stein equation for a distributional comparison, let
X
and
Y
be two random variables with pdf
p_{X}
and
p_{Y}
, defined on the same probability space and with nested supports
{\rm{supp}}(p_{Y})\subset{\rm{supp}}(p_{X})=[a,b]\subset\mathbb{R}
, score functions
\rho_{X}
and
\rho_{Y}
and the corresponding score Stein operators
\mathcal{T}_{X}
and
\mathcal{T}_{Y}
. Then

\mathbb{E}h(Y)-\mathbb{E}h(X)=\mathbb{E}g_{X,h}(Y)(\rho_{Y}(Y)-\rho_{X}(Y))

(2.6)

where
g_{X,h}(x)
is the solution of the Stein equation for
h
and
\mathcal{T}_{X}
. Equation (
2.6
) is a special case of Equation 23 given in Section 2.5 of
[
18
]
.

For a discrete distribution with probability mass function (pmf)
q
having support
\mathcal{I}=[a,b]\subset{\mathbb{N}}
, the discrete backwards score function is
\frac{\Delta^{-}q(y)}{q(y)}
, and, as in Remark 3.2 and Example 3.13 in
[
18
]
, a discrete backward score Stein operator is

{\mathcal{T}}_{q}g(y)=\Delta^{-}g(y)+\frac{\Delta^{-}q(y)}{q(y)}g(y-1),\quad y\in\mathcal{I}.

(2.7)

In abuse of notation, we often refer to a Stein operator for the distribution of
X
as the Stein operator for
X
, and similarly to the score function of the distribution of
X
as the score function of
X
. Further, if
X\sim p
we also write
\mathcal{T}_{X}
for
\mathcal{T}_{p}
.

2.2
CCR distributions

Let
N\in\mathbb{N}
be a random variable with finite second moment and let
{\bf{Y}}=(Y_{1},Y_{2},\ldots)
be a sequence of positive i.i.d. random variables, with cdf
F_{Y}
, independent of
N
. Then

W_{\alpha}=W_{\alpha}(N,{\bf{Y}})=\begin{cases}\min\{Y_{1},Y_{2},\ldots,Y_{N}\},&\mbox{ if }\alpha=-1;\\
\max\{Y_{1},Y_{2},\ldots,Y_{N}\},&\mbox{ if }\alpha=1,\end{cases}

(2.8)

is called a CCR random variable with type indicator
\alpha\in\{-1,1\}.

Setting

U_{Y}^{\alpha}(\cdot)=\begin{cases}1-F_{Y}(\cdot),&\mbox{ if }\alpha=-1;\\
F_{Y}(\cdot),&\mbox{ if }\alpha=1,\end{cases}

(2.9)

and denoting
G_{N}(x)=\mathbb{E}\,x^{N}
the probability generating function of
N
,

W_{\alpha}>0
has cumulative distribution function (cdf)

\displaystyle F_{W_{\alpha}}(w)

\displaystyle=\mathbb{P}(W_{\alpha}\leq w)

(2.10)

\displaystyle=\begin{cases}1-\sum_{n}\mathbb{P}(N=n)U_{Y}^{\alpha}(w)^{n}=1-(G_{N}\circ U_{Y}^{\alpha})(w),&\mbox{ if }\alpha=-1;\\
\sum_{n}\mathbb{P}(N=n)U_{Y}^{\alpha}(w)^{n}=(G_{N}\circ U_{Y}^{\alpha})(w),&\mbox{ if }\alpha=1.\end{cases}

(2.11)

If the
Y_{i}
’s have a continuous distribution with pdf
f_{Y}
then
W_{\alpha}
has pdf

{f}_{W_{\alpha}}(w)=\sum_{n}\mathbb{P}(N=n)n({U_{Y}^{\alpha}}(w))^{n-1}{f}_{Y}(w)=f_{Y}(w)(G_{N}^{\prime}\circ U_{Y}^{\alpha})(w),

(2.12)

see also
[
34
]
: here we used that
(U_{Y}^{\alpha})^{\prime}(w)=\alpha f_{Y}(w)
.

If the
Y_{i}
’s are discrete with pmf
p_{Y}
on
\mathbb{N},
the resulting random variable
W_{\alpha}
has pmf
p_{W_{\alpha}}
which can be expressed in terms of
G_{N}(\cdot)
as

p_{W_{\alpha}}(x)=\alpha\left(G_{N}(U_{Y}^{\alpha}(x))-G_{N}(U_{Y}^{\alpha}(x-1))\right)=\alpha\Delta^{-}(G_{N}\circ U_{Y}^{\alpha})(x).

(2.13)

Example 2.1
.

1.

If
N
is a zero-truncated Poisson random variable with parameter
\theta
, then (
2.12
) simplifies to

f_{W_{\alpha}}(w)=f_{Y}(w)\frac{\theta}{(1-e^{-\theta})}e^{-\theta(1-U_{Y}^{\alpha}(w))},

This is the pdf of the extended Poisson family of distributions given in Equation 3 of

]

]

2.

If
N
is a Geometric(

) random variable, with pmf
\mathbb{P}(N=n)=(1-p)^{(n-1)}p
for
n\in\mathbb{N}
, then (
2.12
) yields

which gives the distributions in equations 5.2 and 5.3 of

2.3
Stein’s method for the CCR class of distributions

To obtain a Stein operator for the CCR random variable
W_{\alpha}=W_{\alpha}(N,{\bf Y})
in (
2.8
), we use the density method. First, we assume that the
Y_{i}
’s are continuous with differentiable pdf
f_{Y}
. From (
2.12
) the score function for the distribution of
W_{{\alpha}}
is

\rho_{W_{{\alpha}}}({w})={\alpha}{f}_{Y}({w})\frac{(G_{N}^{\prime\prime}\circ U_{Y}^{\alpha})(w)}{(G_{N}^{\prime}\circ U_{Y}^{\alpha})({w})}+\rho_{Y}(w),

(2.14)

with
\rho_{Y}={f_{Y}^{\prime}}/{f_{Y}}
the score function of
Y
. Hence
\mathcal{T}_{W_{\alpha}}
given by

\mathcal{T}_{W_{\alpha}}g(w)=g^{\prime}(w)+\rho_{W_{\alpha}}(w)g(w),

(2.15)

for
g
differentiable, is a Stein operator acting on the functions
g\in{\mathcal{F}}(\mathcal{T}_{W_{\alpha}})
. For a test function
h\in\mathcal{H}
the corresponding Stein equation is

g^{\prime}({w})+\rho_{{W_{{\alpha}}}}({w})g({w})=h({w})-\mathbb{E}h({W_{\alpha}}).

(2.16)

Thus, for any random variable
X
, the distance
d_{\mathcal{H}}
from (
2.1
) between the distribution of
X
and
W_{\alpha}
can be bounded by bounding the expectation of the left-hand side of (
2.16
). For
Y_{i}
’s taking values in
\mathbb{N}
with pmf
p_{Y}
, the backward score function is

\rho_{W_{{\alpha}}}(w)=\frac{\Delta^{-}p_{W_{\alpha}}(w)}{p_{W_{\alpha}}(w)}=\frac{\Delta^{-}(\Delta^{-}(G_{N}\circ U_{Y}^{\alpha}))(w)}{\Delta^{-}(G_{N}\circ U_{Y}^{\alpha})(w)},

(2.17)

with corresponding discrete backward score Stein operator
\mathcal{T}_{W_{\alpha}}
operating as

\displaystyle\mathcal{T}_{W_{\alpha}}g(w)=\Delta^{-}g(w)+\rho_{W_{\alpha}}(w)g(w-1).

(2.18)

A general comparison approach

\displaystyle\left|\mathbb{E}h(W_{\alpha}(N,{\bf{E}}))-\mathbb{E}h({W_{\alpha}(M,{\bf{X}})})\right|\leq||h^{\prime}||\sum_{i=1}^{\infty}\mathbb{E}|E_{i}-X_{i}|\mathbb{P}(M\geq i)

\displaystyle\quad\quad\quad+{\sum_{m,n=1}^{\infty}\mathbb{P}(M=m,N=n)\left|\mathbb{E}h(W_{\alpha}(n,{\bf{E}}))-\mathbb{E}h(W_{\alpha}(m,{\bf{E}}))\right|.}

If
M
and

are identically distributed random variables, then

|\mathbb{E}h(W_{\alpha}(N,{\bf{E}}))-\mathbb{E}h(W_{\alpha}(N,{\bf{X}}))|\leq\|h^{\prime}\|\sum_{i=1}^{\infty}\mathbb{E}|E_{i}-X_{i}|\mathbb{P}(N\geq i).

(3.9)

Proof.

We employ a Lindeberg argument, as follows. Defining
X_{1},X_{2},\ldots
and
E_{1},E_{2},\ldots
on the same probability space,
we have

\displaystyle\left|\mathbb{E}h(W_{\alpha}(N,{\bf{E}}))-\mathbb{E}h({W_{\alpha}(M,{\bf{X}})})\right|\leq

\displaystyle\left|\mathbb{E}h(W_{\alpha}(N,{\bf{E}}))-\mathbb{E}h({W_{\alpha}(M,{\bf{E}})})\right|

(3.10)

\displaystyle+\left|\mathbb{E}h(W_{\alpha}(M,{\bf{E}}))-\mathbb{E}h({W_{\alpha}(M,{\bf{X}})})\right|.

(3.11)

To bound (
3.10
), we simply note that

\displaystyle|\mathbb{E}h(W_{\alpha}(N,{\bf{E}}))-\mathbb{E}h(W_{\alpha}(M,{\bf{E}}))|

(3.12)

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle||h^{\prime}||\sum_{m,n=1}^{\infty}\mathbb{P}(M=m,N=n)\mathbb{E}\left|W_{\alpha}(n,{\bf{E}})-W_{\alpha}(m,{\bf{E}})\right|

Now to bound (
3.11
), if
\alpha=1
, then

\displaystyle{\left|\mathbb{E}h\left(\max\{E_{1},\ldots,E_{M}\}\right)-\mathbb{E}h\left(\max\{X_{1},\ldots,X_{M}\}\right)\right|}

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\sum_{m=1}^{\infty}\mathbb{P}(M=m)\sum_{i=1}^{m}\mathbb{E}\left|h\left(\max\{E_{1},\ldots,E_{i},X_{i+1},\ldots,X_{m}\}\right)\right.

\displaystyle\left.-h\left(\max\{E_{1},\ldots,E_{i-1},X_{i},\ldots,X_{m}\}\right)\right|

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\sum_{m=1}^{\infty}\mathbb{P}(M=m)\sum_{i=1}^{m}\mathbb{E}\left|E_{i}-X_{i}\right|\|h^{\prime}\|=\sum_{i=1}^{\infty}\mathbb{E}\left|E_{i}-X_{i}\right|\mathbb{P}(M\geq i)\|h^{\prime}\|.

(3.13)

Similarly, the minimum case (
\alpha=-1
) follows as
\min(X_{i})\leq\max(X_{i})\leq\sum X_{i}
.
Adding (
3.12
) and (

) and taking the supremum over all

gives the first assertion. The second assertion follows from coupling
M
and
N
so that

a.s., in which case (

) vanishes.
∎

Propositions

,

and

, while the last result can be translated into a bound in

4
Application to the Poisson-exponential distribution

Cancho et al.
[
8
]
introduced the Poisson-exponential distribution as a distribution of the maximum of
N
independently and identically distributed exponential random variables from an infinite sequence
{\bf{E}}=(E_{1},E_{2},\ldots,)
so that
E_{i}\sim{\rm Exp}(\lambda)
with parameter
\lambda
(having mean
1/{\lambda}
), and
N
following a zero-truncated Poisson distribution with parameter
\theta
, independently of
{\bf{E}}
. This maximum has the PE distribution with parameters
\theta,\lambda>0
, denoted by
PE(\theta,\lambda)
, which has the differentiable pdf

p({w}|\theta,\lambda)=\frac{\theta\lambda e^{-\lambda{w}-\theta e^{-\lambda{w}}}}{1-e^{-\theta}};\quad\quad{w}>0.

(4.1)

To obtain a Stein operator for
PE(\theta,\lambda)
we use (
2.14
) with

G_{N}({u})=\frac{e^{-\theta}}{1-e^{-\theta}}\left(e^{\theta{u}}-1\right)
and

\frac{G_{N}^{\prime\prime}({u})}{G_{N}^{\prime}({u})}=\theta,
yielding the score function

\displaystyle\rho({w})=\lambda(\theta e^{-\lambda{w}}-1);

(4.2)

(
2.15
) gives

{\mathcal{T}}f({w})=f^{\prime}({w})+\lambda(\theta e^{-\lambda{w}}-1)f({w}).

(4.3)

For a bounded test function
h\in\mathcal{H}
, the score
PE(\theta,\lambda)
-Stein equation is

f^{\prime}(w)+\lambda(\theta e^{-\lambda{w}}-1)f({w})=h({w})-\mathbb{E}h({W}),

(4.4)

with the solution
f_{h}
, given by (
2.5
), satisfying the following bounds.

Lemma 4.1
.

Let
h:\mathbb{R}^{+}_{{>0}}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
be bounded and
f
denote the solution (
2.5
) of the Stein equation (
4.4
) for
h
. Let
\tilde{h}(w)=h(w)-\mathbb{E}h(W)
for

. Then for all

\displaystyle\left|e^{-\lambda w}f(w)\right|

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\frac{\|\tilde{h}\|}{\theta\lambda}\left(1-e^{-\theta+\theta e^{-\lambda w}}\right)

(4.5)

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\frac{\|\tilde{h}\|}{\theta\lambda};

(4.6)

\displaystyle|\lambda(\theta e^{-\lambda w}-1)f(w)|

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\|\tilde{h}\|;

(4.7)

\displaystyle|f(w)|

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\frac{2\|\tilde{h}\|}{\lambda};

(4.8)

\displaystyle|f^{\prime}(w)|

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle 2\|\tilde{h}\|.

(4.9)

If in addition

, then at all points

at which

exists,

|f^{\prime\prime}(w)|\leq\|h^{\prime}\|+2\lambda\theta\|\tilde{h}\|+3\lambda\|\tilde{h}\|.

(4.10)

Proof.

We write

for the pdf of
PE(\theta,\lambda)
. To prove (

), we bound

\displaystyle\left|e^{-\lambda w}f(w)\right|\leq\|\tilde{h}\|\frac{e^{-\lambda w}}{p(w)}\int_{0}^{w}p(t)\mathrm{d}t=\|\tilde{h}\|\frac{1-e^{-\theta(1-e^{-\lambda w})}}{\theta\lambda},

and (
4.5
) follows. From
1-e^{-y}<1\ \forall\ y>0
, we get (
4.6
).

Proof of (
4.7
)
.

Case 1:
\theta e^{-\lambda w}-1>0
.
In this case

0<w<\frac{\ln\theta}{\lambda}
and we have

\displaystyle|\lambda(\theta e^{-\lambda w}-1)f(w)|\leq\|\tilde{h}\|\frac{\lambda(\theta e^{-\lambda w}-1)}{p(w)}\int_{0}^{w}p(t)\mathrm{d}t.

As
p^{\prime}(t)=\lambda(\theta e^{-\lambda t}-1)p(t)\geq\lambda(\theta e^{-\lambda w}-1)p(t)
for

, it follows that

\displaystyle 0

\displaystyle<\frac{\lambda(\theta e^{-\lambda w}-1)}{p(w)}\int_{0}^{w}p(t)\mathrm{d}t\leq\frac{1}{p(w)}\int_{0}^{w}p^{\prime}(t)\mathrm{d}t=(1-e^{-\theta(1-e^{-\lambda w})+\lambda w})\leq 1.

Hence we obtain the bound (
4.7
) for
0<w<\frac{\ln\theta}{\lambda}
.

Case 2:
\theta e^{-\lambda w}-1\leq 0
.

In this case
w\geq\frac{\ln\theta}{\lambda}
and

0<\lambda(1-\theta e^{-\lambda w})p(t)<\lambda(1-\theta e^{-\lambda t})p(t)=p^{\prime}(t).

Using (
2.5
) gives

\displaystyle|\lambda(\theta e^{-\lambda w}-1)f(w)|\leq\|\tilde{h}\|\frac{\lambda(1-\theta e^{-\lambda w})}{p(w)}\int_{w}^{\infty}p(t)\mathrm{d}t\leq\|\tilde{h}\|\frac{1}{p(w)}\int_{w}^{\infty}p^{\prime}(t)\mathrm{d}t\leq\|\tilde{h}\|.

Hence the bound (
4.7
) follows for all

.

Proof of (
4.8
), (
4.9
) and (
4.10
)
.
As
\lambda(\theta e^{-\lambda w}-1)f(w)=\lambda\theta e^{-\lambda w}f(w)-\lambda f(w),
the triangle inequality along with (

) and (

) gives (
4.8
). To show (

), using the triangle inequality, from (

) we obtain

|f^{\prime}({w})|\leq|h({w})-\mathbb{E}h({W})|+|\lambda(\theta e^{-\lambda{w}}-1)f({w})|

and using (

) yields the bound (
4.9
).

Now for
h
differentiable at

, taking the first order derivative in (

) gives

Using (

) and (

\displaystyle|f^{\prime\prime}(w)|

\displaystyle\leq\|h^{\prime}\|+\lambda|\theta e^{-\lambda w}-1|2\|\tilde{h}\|+\theta\lambda^{2}\frac{\|\tilde{h}\|}{\theta\lambda}\leq\|h^{\prime}\|+2\lambda\theta e^{-\lambda w}\|\tilde{h}\|+2\lambda\|\tilde{h}\|+\lambda\|\tilde{h}\|.

∎

Approximating the distribution of the maximum of a random number of i.i.d. random variables by a PE distribution

. For a zero-truncated Poisson random variable

4.2
Approximating the generalised Poisson-exponential distribution

The PE distribution has an increasing or constant failure rate. To model decreasing failure rate as well, Fatima & Roohi
[
12
]
introduced the family of generalised Poisson-exponential (GPE) distributions. The differentiable pdf of a GPE distribution with parameters
\beta,\theta,\lambda>0
, denoted by
GPE(\theta,\lambda,\beta)
, is

p(x|\theta,\lambda,\beta)=\frac{\beta\theta\lambda e^{-\lambda x-\theta\beta e^{-\lambda x}}}{(1-e^{-\theta})^{\beta}}(1-e^{-\theta+\theta e^{-\lambda x}})^{\beta-1};\quad\quad x>0.

(4.12)

For
\beta=1
the density of the GPE distribution simplifies to that of the PE distribution given in (
4.1
). For
{0<\beta<1}
the pdf of the GPE distribution is monotonically decreasing, while for
\beta\geq 1
it is unimodal positively skewed with skewness depending on the shape parameters
\beta
and
\theta
. The shape of the hazard function also depends on these two shape parameters. For example, for
\theta=1
and
\lambda=2
, the failure rate is decreasing for
0<\beta<1
and is increasing for
\beta\geq 1
, as in Figure 2 of
[
12
]
.

For a data set from
[
1
]
which consists of 50 observations on the time to first failure of devices,
[
12
]
showed that the GPE distribution provides a better fit than the PE and some other candidate distributions. However, a GPE distribution is not as easy to manipulate and interpret as a PE distribution. Hence it is a natural question to quantify the sacrifice when approximating a GPE distribution with a PE distribution. Here we note that GPE is not from the CCR family and hence Proposition
3.3
cannot be applied. Instead we use Stein’s method to bound the approximation error.

For such an approximation to be intuitive, the failure rate of the approximating distribution should be qualitatively similar. Hence we restrict attention to the case
\beta\geq 1
, for which both the GPE and the PE distributions have an increasing failure rate. As an aside, we note that for
\beta\geq 1
, the limit of the pdf at
0
is
0
, while for
0<\beta<1
it is undefined; the latter condition leads to a Stein class for the GPE Stein score operator which differs from the Stein class for the PE Stein score operator.

We note here that if
X\sim GPE(\theta,\lambda,\beta)
, then, for
\beta\geq 1
,

\mathbb{E}X\leq\frac{\beta\theta}{\lambda(1-e^{-\theta})^{\beta}}.

(4.13)

The proof of (
4.13
) is deferred to the appendix.

The GPE random variable is not of the CCR form (
2.8
) but its score function, given in (
4.14
) below, can be derived from its pdf (
4.12
) with parameters
\beta,\theta,\lambda>0
as

\rho(x)=\frac{p^{\prime}(x)}{p(x)}=\lambda\theta e^{-\lambda x}\Bigg[\beta+\frac{(\beta-1)e^{-\theta+\theta e^{-\lambda x}}}{1-e^{-\theta+\theta e^{-\lambda x}}}\Bigg]-\lambda,\quad\quad x>0.

(4.14)

In the following Theorem we bound the distance between a GPE with
\beta\geq 1
and a PE distribution, using their corresponding score Stein operators in (
2.6
).

Theorem 4.6
.

Let
W\sim PE(\theta_{1},\lambda_{1})
,

and

Proof.

Let
p_{W}
and

denote the pdfs of

and

, and
\mathcal{T}_{X}

is in the Stein class for
\mathcal{T}_{X}
. Applying (

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}h(X)-\mathbb{E}h(W)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\bigg[\lambda_{2}\theta_{2}e^{-\lambda_{2}X}\Bigg(\beta+\frac{(\beta-1)e^{-\theta_{2}+\theta_{2}e^{-\lambda_{2}X}}}{1-e^{-\theta_{2}+\theta_{2}e^{-\lambda_{2}X}}}\Bigg)-\lambda_{2}-\lambda_{1}(\theta_{1}e^{-\lambda_{1}X}-1)\bigg]f_{W}(X)

{|\theta_{2}\lambda_{2}e^{-\lambda_{2}x}-\theta_{1}\lambda_{1}e^{-\lambda_{1}x}|}\\

since

\displaystyle|\mathbb{E}h(X)-\mathbb{E}h(W)|

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\lambda_{2}\theta_{2}|\beta-1|\mathbb{E}\left|\frac{e^{-\theta_{2}+\theta_{2}e^{-\lambda_{2}X}}}{1-e^{-\theta_{2}+\theta_{2}e^{-\lambda_{2}X}}}e^{-\lambda_{1}X}f_{W}(X)\right|+(\lambda_{2}-\lambda_{1})\mathbb{E}|f_{W}(X)|

\displaystyle\left|\frac{e^{-\theta_{2}+\theta_{2}e^{-\lambda_{2}x}}}{1-e^{-\theta_{2}+\theta_{2}e^{-\lambda_{2}x}}}e^{-\lambda_{1}x}f_{W}(x)\right|

The

is the distribution of the minimum of

(5.3)

(5.4)

2.5
) of this EG Stein equation.

Lemma 5.1
.

For any bounded test function

, we let

for

. Then its solution

of the EG Stein equation (
5.4
) satisfies

\displaystyle|g(w)|

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\frac{\|\tilde{h}\|}{\lambda},

(5.5)

\displaystyle|g^{\prime}(w)|

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\frac{2\|\tilde{h}\|}{p}.

(5.6)

Proof.

For the solution (
2.5
) of the EG Stein equation (
5.4
) we have

\displaystyle|g(w)|\leq\|\tilde{h}\|\left|\frac{-1}{f(w)}\int_{w}^{\infty}f(t)\mathrm{d}t\right|=\|\tilde{h}\|\left|\frac{1-F(w)}{f(w)}\right|=\|\tilde{h}\|\left|\frac{(1-qe^{-\lambda w})}{\lambda}\right|\leq\frac{\|\tilde{h}\|}{\lambda}.

The last inequality follows since with
0\leq e^{-\lambda w}\leq 1
and
0\leq q\leq 1

1-q\leq 1-qe^{-\lambda w}\leq 1
, and

. For a bound on

\displaystyle|g^{\prime}(w)|\leq\|\tilde{h}\|+\left|\lambda\left(\frac{1+qe^{-\lambda w}}{1-qe^{-\lambda w}}\right)\right|\frac{\|\tilde{h}\|}{\lambda}\leq\|\tilde{h}\|\left(1+\frac{1+q}{1-q}\right).

Simplifying the last inequality gives (

5.2
Approximating the extended
exponential-geometric distribution

Motivated by population heterogeneity, Adamidis et al.
[
2
]
developed the extended exponential-geometric distribution (EEG) by assuming that individual units in a population have increasing failure rates that depend on a random scale parameter
A
. Their lifetimes
X/A
are modelled by a modified extreme value distribution; if
A=\alpha
then the pdf is
f(x|\alpha;\beta)=\alpha\beta e^{\beta x+\alpha(1-e^{\beta x})}
, where
x,\alpha,\beta\in\mathbb{R}^{+}_{>0}
; it is assumed that
A
has
{\rm Exp}(\gamma)
distribution. Then the unconditional lifetime distribution
X
has pdf

f(x;\beta,\gamma)=\frac{\beta\gamma e^{-\beta x}}{(1-(1-\gamma)e^{-\beta x})^{2}},\quad x>0;

(5.7)

with
\beta,\gamma\in\mathbb{R}^{+}_{>0}
; we use the notation
X\sim EEG(\beta,\gamma)
. Its
score function is

\rho(x)=\frac{f^{\prime}(x)}{f(x)}=-\beta\left(\frac{1+(1-\gamma)e^{-\beta x}}{1-(1-\gamma)e^{-\beta x}}\right).

(5.8)

This distribution is not in the CCR family. However, the exponential-geometric distribution
EG(\beta,\gamma)
is a special case when
\gamma\in(0,1)
. To assess their total variation distance, we use the general methodology developed in Section
2
.

Theorem 5.4
.

For
X\sim EEG(\beta,\gamma)
and
W\sim EG(\lambda,p)
with
\beta,\gamma,\lambda\in\mathbb{R}^{+}_{>0}
,
p\in(0,1)
and a bounded test function
h
, we have

\displaystyle|\mathbb{E}h(X)-\mathbb{E}h(W)|\leq\begin{cases}\frac{\|\tilde{h}\|}{\lambda}\left[|\lambda-\beta|\left(\frac{2-p}{p}+\frac{2(1-\gamma)}{e\gamma^{2}}\right)+\frac{2\beta}{\min(\gamma,p)^{2}}|p-\gamma|\right]&,0<\gamma<1;\\
\frac{\|\tilde{h}\|}{\lambda p}\left[\left|\lambda-\beta\right|\left(1+(\gamma-1)(1-p)\right)+(\lambda+\beta)\left(\gamma-p\right)\right]&,\gamma\geq 1.\end{cases}

(5.9)

Proof.

Using the score functions (
5.2
) and (
5.8
) in (
2.6
) yields

\displaystyle|\mathbb{E}h(X)-\mathbb{E}h(W)|=|\mathbb{E}(\rho_{X}(X)-\rho_{Y}(X))g(X)|

(5.10)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\left|\mathbb{E}\left(-\beta\left(\frac{1+(1-\gamma)e^{-\beta X}}{1-(1-\gamma)e^{-\beta X}}\right)+\lambda\left(\frac{1+(1-p)e^{-\lambda X}}{1-(1-p)e^{-\lambda X}}\right)\right)g(X)\right|

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\frac{\|\tilde{h}\|}{\lambda}\mathbb{E}\left|-\beta\left(\frac{1+(1-\gamma)e^{-\beta X}}{1-(1-\gamma)e^{-\beta X}}\right)+\lambda\left(\frac{1+(1-p)e^{-\lambda X}}{1-(1-p)e^{-\lambda X}}\right)\right|.

To bound the expectation in the above equation, we let

R(x)=-\beta\left(\frac{1+(1-\gamma)e^{-\beta x}}{1-(1-\gamma)e^{-\beta x}}\right)+\lambda\left(\frac{1+(1-p)e^{-\lambda x}}{1-(1-p)e^{-\lambda x}}\right)

Case 1:

0<\gamma<1
. In this case we decompose
|R(x)|
as

\displaystyle|R(x)|\leq\left|(\lambda-\beta)\frac{1+(1-p)e^{-\lambda x}}{1-(1-p)e^{-\lambda x}}\right|

(5.11)

\displaystyle+

\displaystyle\left|\beta\left(\frac{1+(1-p)e^{-\lambda x}}{1-(1-p)e^{-\lambda x}}-\frac{1+(1-\gamma)e^{-\lambda x}}{1-(1-\gamma)e^{-\lambda x}}\right)\right|

(5.12)

\displaystyle+

\displaystyle\left|\beta\left(\frac{1+(1-\gamma)e^{-\lambda x}}{1-(1-\gamma)e^{-\lambda x}}-\frac{1+(1-\gamma)e^{-\beta x}}{1-(1-\gamma)e^{-\beta x}}\right)\right|,

(5.13)

and bound these terms separately.
For (
5.11
) we use that

\frac{1+\alpha q}{1-\alpha q}\leq\frac{1+q}{1-q}
when
\alpha\in[0,1]
and
q\in(0,1).
Thus

\displaystyle\left|(\lambda-\beta)\frac{1+(1-p)e^{-\lambda x}}{1-(1-p)e^{-\lambda x}}\right|

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle|\lambda-\beta|\frac{2-p}{p}.

For (
5.12
), Taylor expansion around
1-\gamma
of the function

f(q)=\frac{1+aq}{1-aq}
with
a=e^{-\lambda x}\in(0,1)
, and
q=1-p
, gives

f^{\prime}(q)=\frac{a}{1-aq}+\frac{a(1+aq)}{(1-aq)^{2}}=\frac{2a}{(1-aq)^{2}}>0
. Moreover,
for
0<a<1
we have

\frac{2a}{(1-aq)^{2}}\leq\frac{2}{(1-q)^{2}}
and thus, for
\theta\in(0,1),
we have

0<f^{\prime}(\theta(1-p)+(1-\theta)(1-\gamma))\leq\frac{2}{(1-\max(1-\gamma,1-p))^{2}}=\frac{2}{(\min(\gamma,p))^{2}}
.
Hence,

\displaystyle\left|\beta\left(\frac{1+(1-p)e^{-\lambda x}}{1-(1-p)e^{-\lambda x}}-\frac{1+(1-\gamma)e^{-\lambda x}}{1-(1-\gamma)e^{-\lambda x}}\right)\right|

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\beta\frac{2|p-\gamma|}{(\min(\gamma,p))^{2}}.

For (
5.13
), first order Taylor expansion of the function

f(\beta)=\frac{1+(1-\gamma)e^{-\beta x}}{1-(1-\gamma)e^{-\beta x}}
gives

\displaystyle\left|\beta\left(\frac{1+(1-\gamma)e^{-\lambda x}}{1-(1-\gamma)e^{-\lambda x}}-\frac{1+(1-\gamma)e^{-\beta x}}{1-(1-\gamma)e^{-\beta x}}\right)\right|

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\beta|\lambda-\beta||f^{\prime}(\theta\lambda+(1-\theta)\beta)|

for some
\theta\in(0,1).

Now the function
f^{\prime}(\beta)=\frac{{2x(1-\gamma)}e^{-\beta x}}{(1-(1-\gamma)e^{-\beta x})^{2}}
is positive; moreover
xe^{-\beta x}\leq(e\beta)^{-1}
for
x\geq 0
, so that

f^{\prime}(\beta)\leq\frac{1}{\beta e}\frac{2(1-\gamma)}{(1-(1-\gamma)e^{-\beta x})^{2}}\leq\frac{2(1-\gamma)}{e\beta\gamma^{2}}.

Hence we can bound

\displaystyle\left|\beta\left(\frac{1+(1-\gamma)e^{-\lambda x}}{1-(1-\gamma)e^{-\lambda x}}-\frac{1+(1-\gamma)e^{-\beta x}}{1-(1-\gamma)e^{-\beta x}}\right)\right|

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\frac{2(1-\gamma)}{e\gamma^{2}}|\lambda-\beta|.

This gives as overall bound

\displaystyle|R(x)|

\displaystyle\leq|\lambda-\beta|\left(\frac{2-p}{p}+\frac{2(1-\gamma)}{e\gamma^{2}}\right)+\frac{2\beta}{\min(\gamma,p)^{2}}|p-\gamma|.

Substituting this bound in (
5.10
) gives the final bound for the case when
0<\gamma<1
.

Case 2:
For
\gamma\geq 1
, we have

\displaystyle|R(x)|\leq\left|\frac{(\lambda-\beta)(1-(1-\gamma)(1-p)e^{-(\lambda+\beta)x})}{\left(1-(1-\gamma)e^{-\beta x}\right)\left(1-(1-p)e^{-\lambda x}\right)}\right|

\displaystyle+\left|\frac{(\lambda+\beta)\left((1-p)e^{-\lambda x}-(1-\gamma)e^{-\beta x}\right)}{\left(1-(1-\gamma)e^{-\beta x}\right)\left(1-(1-p)e^{-\lambda x}\right)}\right|

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\frac{\left|\lambda-\beta\right|\left(1+(\gamma-1)(1-p)\right)}{p}+\frac{(\lambda+\beta)\left(\gamma-p\right)}{p}.

Substituting this result in (
5.10
) gives the bound for
\gamma\geq 1
in (
5.9
).
∎

6
The maximum waiting time of sequence patterns in Bernoulli trials

This application is motivated by the results in Section 4 of
[
26
]
which gives bounds on the distribution of the number of trials preceding the first appearance of a pattern in dependent Bernoulli trials. Here we are interested in the distribution of the maximum of such random variables.

Consider
M
independent parallel systems
(X_{1}^{(i)},X_{2}^{(i)},...)
,
i=1,2,\ldots,M
of possibly dependent Bernoulli
(a)
trials, which are jointly independent of
M\in\mathbb{N}
. For each sequence
i
, let
I_{j}^{(i)}
be the indicator function that a fixed non-overlapping binary sequence pattern of length
k
occurs starting at
X_{j}^{(i)}
; the pattern may be specific to sequence
i
. Let
V_{i}=\min\{j:I_{j}^{(i)}=1\}
denote the first occurrence of the pattern of interest in the
i^{th}
system; assume that
\mathbb{P}(V_{i}=1)=p
for all
i\in\mathbb{N}
. We denote the maximum waiting time for the occurrence of a corresponding sequence pattern in all
M
parallel systems by
W=W_{1}(M,{\bf{V}})
where
{\bf{V}}=(V_{1},V_{2}\ldots)
.

Example 6.1
.

If the Bernoulli trials are independent, and the pattern of interest is a run of 1’s of length
k
, starting with a
0
and followed by
k
1’s, then
\mathbb{P}(V_{i}=1)=p=(1-a)a^{k}
, as given in Corollary 2 of
[
26
]
.
Intuitively, the waiting time for the occurrence of this pattern in an individual sequence is approximately geometric with parameter
p
.
For an approximation by a Poisson-exponential distribution
we are particularly interested in instances where we can write the probability
\mathbb{P}(V_{i}=1)=p
as
p=\lambda/n
; here this leads to scaling the run length
k
as
k\sim a\log n
.

Corollary 6.2
.

In the above setting, let
U_{n}=W/n
, with
p=\frac{\lambda}{n}
, and
W_{1}(M^{\prime},{\bf{E}})\sim PE(\theta,\lambda)
, with
M^{\prime}
, a zero-truncated Poisson random variable and
E_{i}\sim{\rm Exp}(\lambda)
, we have

\displaystyle d_{BW}(\mathcal{L}(U_{n}),PE(\theta,\lambda))

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\frac{2\lambda(k-1)}{n}\mathbb{E}M+\frac{1}{\ln(p^{-1})}\mathbb{E}\sum_{k=\min(M,M^{\prime})}^{\max(M,M^{\prime})}\frac{1}{k}+\frac{e^{\frac{\lambda}{n}}}{n}\left(1+\frac{e^{\frac{\theta\lambda}{ne}}}{n}B_{1}(\theta,\lambda)\right)

\displaystyle+

\displaystyle\frac{1}{n}\left(\left(1-\frac{\lambda}{n}\right)^{-6}e^{\theta e^{\frac{\lambda}{n}}+2\frac{\theta\lambda}{n}+\frac{\theta\lambda}{ne}+\frac{\lambda}{n}}B_{2}(\theta,\lambda)+\frac{1}{2n}e^{\frac{\lambda}{n}+\frac{\theta\lambda}{ne}}B_{3}(\theta,\lambda)\right),

where
B_{1}(\theta,\lambda)
,
B_{2}(\theta,\lambda)
, and
B_{3}(\theta,\lambda)
are as given in Theorem
4.11
.

Example 6.3
.

In the above example of independent Bernoulli trials and the pattern of interest being a run of 1’s of length

, the bound in Corollary

is of order

We couple

, where

for

, by using the same random variable

(6.1)

\displaystyle d_{TV}(\mathcal{L}(U_{n}),\mathcal{L}(Z_{n}))

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{m=1}^{\infty}\sum_{i=1}^{m}{d_{TV}\left(\mathcal{L}\left(\frac{V{{}_{i}}}{n}\right),\mathcal{L}\left(\frac{T{{}_{i}}}{n}\right)\right)}\,\mathbb{P}(M=m)\leq\frac{\lambda(k-1)}{n}\mathbb{E}M,

using Corollary 1 in
[
26
]
in the last step. With (
2.2

(6.2)

Now

\displaystyle d_{BW}\left(\mathcal{L}(Z_{n}),PE(\theta,\lambda)\right)

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle d_{BW}\left(\mathcal{L}(Z_{n}),\mathcal{L}\left(\frac{W_{1}(M^{\prime},{\bf{T}})}{n}\right)\right)

(6.3)

\displaystyle+d_{BW}\left(\mathcal{L}\left(\frac{W_{1}(M^{\prime},{\bf{T}})}{n}\right),PE(\theta,\lambda)\right).

(6.4)

The term (

). To bound (

) gives

\displaystyle d_{BW}\left(\mathcal{L}(Z_{n}),\mathcal{L}\left(\frac{W_{1}(M^{\prime},{\bf{T}})}{n}\right)\right)

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\sum_{m,m^{\prime}=1}^{\infty}\mathbb{P}(M=m,M^{\prime}=m^{\prime})

∎

Remark 6.4
.

For

\displaystyle d_{BW}(\mathcal{L}(U_{n}),PE(\theta,\lambda))\leq\frac{2\theta\lambda(k-1)}{n(1-e^{-\theta})}+\frac{e^{\frac{\lambda}{n}}}{n}\left(1+\frac{e^{\frac{\theta\lambda}{ne}}}{n}B_{1}(\theta,\lambda)\right)
