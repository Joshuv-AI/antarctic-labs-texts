Introduction

All (di)graphs considered in this paper are finite and simple, that is, no multiple arcs, edges or loops, and all groups are finite. For a (di)graph
\Gamma
, denote by
V(\Gamma)
,
E(\Gamma)
,
\hbox{\rm Arc}(\Gamma)
and
\hbox{\rm Aut}(\Gamma)
the vertex set, edge set, arc set, and full automorphism group of
\Gamma
, respectively.

Let
G
be a finite group and let
S
be a subset of
G
with
1\not\in S
. The
Cayley digraph
\hbox{\rm Cay}(G,S)
of
G
with respect to
S
is defined to be the digraph with vertex set
G
and arc set
\{(g,sg)\ |\ g\in G,s\in S\}
. A Cayley digraph
\hbox{\rm Cay}(G,S)
is called a
Cayley graph
of
G
with respect to
S
if
S
is an inverse-closed set, that is,
S=S^{-1}:=\{x^{-1}\mid x\in S\}
, and in this case,
\hbox{\rm Cay}(G,S)
is viewed as an undirected graph by identifying two arcs
(u,v)
and
(v,u)
as an edge
\{u,v\}
. Thus, Cayley graph is a special case of Cayley digraph.

For a given
g\in G
, the right multiplication
R(g)
is a permutation on
G
such that
x^{R(g)}=xg
for all
x\in G
. Then the permutation group
R(G)=\{R(g)\ |\ g\in G\}
on
G
is called the
right regular representation
of
G
, and it is a regular subgroup of
\hbox{\rm Aut}(\hbox{\rm Cay}(G,S))
, so that
\hbox{\rm Cay}(G,S)
is vertex-transitive. In fact, Sabidussi
[
47
]
proved that a digraph
\Gamma
is a Cayley digraph of a group
G
if and only if
\hbox{\rm Aut}(\Gamma)
contains a regular subgroup isomorphic to
G
. Furthermore, a Cayley digraph
\hbox{\rm Cay}(G,S)
has out-valency
|S|
, and is connected if and only if
\langle S\rangle=G
, namely,
S
generates
G
.

Let
G
be a group. Two Cayley (di)graphs
\hbox{\rm Cay}(G,S)
and
\hbox{\rm Cay}(G,T)
of
G
are called Cayley isomorphic if
S^{\sigma}=T
for some
\sigma\in\hbox{\rm Aut}(G)
. It is easy to see that Cayley isomorphic Cayley (di)graphs are isomorphic as (di)graphs. However, the converse is not true, and there are isomorphic Cayley (di)graphs which are not Cayley isomorphic.
A subset
S
of
G
with
1\not\in S
is called a
CI-subset
if for
T\subseteq G
,
\hbox{\rm Cay}(G,S)\cong\hbox{\rm Cay}(G,T)
implies that they are Cayley isomorphic, that is,
T=S^{\sigma}
for some
\sigma\in\hbox{\rm Aut}(G)
, and in this case,
\hbox{\rm Cay}(G,S)
is said to be a
CI-digraph
or a
CI-graph
when it is a digraph or graph respectively, where CI represents Cayley isomorphic. A group
G
is said to be a
DCI-group
or a
CI-group
if all Cayley digraphs or graphs of
G
are CI-digraphs or CI-graphs, respectively.

A Cayley (di)graph
\hbox{\rm Cay}(G,S)
is called
normal
if the right regular representation
R(G)
of
G
is a normal subgroup of
\hbox{\rm Aut}(\hbox{\rm Cay}(G,S))
. We call a group
G
a
NDCI-group
or a
NCI-group
if all normal Cayley digraphs or graphs of
G
are CI-digraphs or CI-graphs, respectively. Obviously, a DCI-group is a NDCI-group and a CI-group is a NCI-group. In this paper, we determine cyclic NDCI-groups and cyclic NCI-groups.

Theorem 1.1

A cyclic group of order

-group if and only if

. A cyclic group of order

\rm{NCI}
-group if and only if either
n=8
or
8\nmid n
.

Let

Proposition 2.1

Let

N_{A}(R(G))=R(G)\rtimes\hbox{\rm Aut}(G,S)

.

-digraph if and only if

The following proposition describes a family of non-normal Cayley digraphs, and its main idea comes from the so-called generalized wreath circulants
[
8
]
and generalized wreath products of Cayley schemes
[

Proposition 2.6
.

Let
G
be a finite group, and let
1\not\in S\subset G
,
1<H\leq K<G
and
H\unlhd G
. Assume that
S\backslash K
is a union of some cosets of
H
in
G
and that there exist
x\not\in K
and
y\in H
such that
y^{x}\not=y^{-1}
. Then the Cayley digraph
\hbox{\rm Cay}(G,S)
is non-normal.

Proof.
Write
\Gamma=\hbox{\rm Cay}(G,S)
and
A=\hbox{\rm Aut}(\Gamma)
. Note that
R(G)\leq A
.

Take
u,v\in G
with
Ku\not=Kv
. Note that for
g\in G
, the out-neighbourhood of
g
in
\Gamma
is
\{sg\ |\ s\in S\}
. Since
S\backslash K
is a union of some cosets of
H
in
G
, for all cosets
Hk_{1}u
of
H
in
Ku
and all cosets
Hk_{2}v
in
Kv
, where
k_{1},k_{2}\in K
, the normality of
H
in
G
implies that the induced bipartite sub-digraph
[Hk_{1}u,Hk_{2}v]
is isomorphic to either the empty graph with
2|H|
isolated vertices, or the complete bipartite digraph
\vec{K}_{|H|,|H|}
of order
2|H|
.
By the arbitrariness of
u
and
v
, if an automorphism of the induced sub-digraph
[Ku]
of
Ku
in
\Gamma
fixes every coset of
H
in
Ku
setwise, then it can be extended to an automorphism of
\Gamma
fixing
G\backslash Ku
pointwise.

For every
h\in H
, define
\bar{h}
to be the permutation on
V(\Gamma)
such that
\bar{h}
fixes
G\backslash Kx
pointwise and
t^{\bar{h}}=t^{R(h)}=th
for all
t\in Kx
, where
x\not\in K
by hypothesis. Then
\bar{H}:=\{\bar{h}\ |\ h\in H\}
is a permutation group on
V(\Gamma)
. Clearly, the restriction of
\bar{H}
on
Kx
is the same as
R(H)
, and since
R(H)\leq A
and
H\unlhd G
, this restriction is a group of automorphisms of the induced sub-digraph
[Kx]
of
Kx
in
\Gamma
fixing every coset of
H
setwise, and then the above paragraph implies that
\bar{H}\leq A
. Since
Kx\not=K
, we have
\bar{H}\leq A_{1}
, where
A_{1}
is the stabilizer of
1
in
A
.

Suppose to the contrary that
\Gamma
is normal. By Proposition
2.1
,
A_{1}=\hbox{\rm Aut}(G,S)
and hence
\bar{H}\leq\hbox{\rm Aut}(G)
. Since
Kx^{2}\not=Kx
,
\bar{H}
fixes
Kx^{2}
pointwise. Since
y\in H
by hypothesis, we have
\bar{y}\in\bar{H}
, and hence
(x^{2})^{\bar{y}}=x^{2}
and

as

. On the other hand, since
\bar{H}\leq\hbox{\rm Aut}(G)
we obtain
(x^{2})^{\bar{y}}=x^{\bar{y}}x^{\bar{y}}=xyxy
.
It follows that

, that is,

, contrary to the hypothesis

. Thus,
\Gamma

\Box

Lemma 3.1
.

For a positive integer

Let
\Gamma=\hbox{\rm Cay}(C_{n},S)
and

. Write

can be uniquely factorized as

the

, denoted by

, that is,
x_{i}=a_{i}^{s_{i}}\in\langle a_{i}\rangle=C_{p_{i}^{r_{i}}}
.

Since
p_{t}
is odd,
\hbox{\rm Aut}(C_{p_{t}^{r_{t}}})\cong C_{(p_{t}-1)p_{t}^{r_{t}-1}}
, and since
\hbox{\rm Aut}(C_{n},S)
contains an element of order
p_{t}
in
\hbox{\rm Aut}(C_{p_{t}^{r_{t}}})
, we have

. Note that the map
a_{t}\mapsto a_{t}^{p_{r}^{r_{t}-1}+1}
induces an automorphism of order
p_{t}
of
C_{p_{t}^{r_{t}}}
.
By Eq (
2.1
), an element
\delta
of order
p_{t}
in
\hbox{\rm Aut}(C_{p_{t}^{r_{t}}})
can be defined by

(a_{1}^{j_{1}}a_{2}^{j_{2}}\dots a_{t-1}^{j_{t-1}}a_{t}^{j_{t}}a_{t+1}^{j_{t+1}}\dots a_{m}^{j_{m}})^{\delta}=a_{1}^{j_{1}}a_{2}^{j_{2}}\dots a_{t-1}^{j_{t-1}}(a_{t}^{j_{t}})^{p_{t}^{r_{t}-1}+1}a_{t+1}^{j_{t+1}}\dots a_{m}^{j_{m}}

for every

, or alternatively by

The uniqueness of subgroup of order
p_{t}
in
\hbox{\rm Aut}(C_{p_{t}^{r_{t}}})
implies that
\langle\delta\rangle\leq\hbox{\rm Aut}(C_{n},S)
. Let

K=\langle a_{1},a_{2},\cdots,a_{t-1},a_{t}^{p_{t}},a_{t+1},\cdots,a_{m}\rangle,\mbox{ and }H=\langle a_{t}^{p_{t}^{r_{t}-1}}\rangle.

Then
|C_{n}:K|=p_{t}
and

. Since
r_{t}\geq 2
,

has a subgroup of order
p_{t}
, and since

has a unique subgroup of order

, we have

. By taking

. To finish the proof, by Proposition

.

o(b)=m
, and
S=\{(ab)^{\pm 1},(a^{2}b^{2})^{\pm 1},(a^{2^{s-1}+1}b)^{\pm 1}\}
. Then
\hbox{\rm Cay}(C_{2^{s}m},S)
is a normal Cayley graph but not a CI-graph.

Proof.
Let
\Gamma=\hbox{\rm Cay}(C_{2^{s}m},S)
and
A=\hbox{\rm Aut}(\Gamma)
. Since
S=S^{-1}
,
\Gamma
is a Cayley graph. For
u,v\in V(\Gamma)
, denote by
d(u,v)
the distance between
u
and
v
in
\Gamma
. Write
\Gamma_{k}(u)=\{w\ |\ d(u,w)=k\}
, the
k
-step neighborhood of the vertex
u
in
\Gamma
.

Let
A_{1}
be the stabilizer
1
in
A
, and let
A^{*}_{1}=\{\alpha\in A_{1}\ |\ u^{\alpha}=u\mbox{ for every }u\in\Gamma_{1}(1)=S\}
, namely, the subgroup of
A_{1}
fixing
S
pointwise. Define
\alpha
to be the automorphism of
C_{2^{s}m}
induced by
a\mapsto a^{-1}
and
b\mapsto b^{-1}
, and
\beta
to be the automorphism of
C_{2^{s}m}
induced by
a\mapsto a^{2^{s-1}+1}
and
b\mapsto b
. It is easy to check that
C_{2}\times C_{2}\cong\langle\alpha,\beta\rangle\leq\hbox{\rm Aut}(G,S)\leq A_{1}
.

Clearly,
\Gamma_{2}(1)=\{(a^{4}b^{4})^{\pm 1},(a^{3}b^{3})^{\pm 1},(a^{2^{s-1}+3}b^{3})^{\pm 1},(a^{2^{s-1}+2}b^{2})^{\pm 1},a^{2^{s-1}}\}
.
Since
s\geq 3
,
(2,m)=1
and
{2^{s}}m\neq 8
, we derive that
|\Gamma_{2}(1)|=9
, and the induced subgroup
[\Gamma_{0}(1)\cup\Gamma_{1}(1)\cup\Gamma_{2}(1)]
can be depicted as Figure
1
.

Figure 1.
The induced subgroup
[\Gamma_{0}(1)\cup\Gamma_{1}(1)\cup\Gamma_{2}(1)]
in
\Gamma
.

Note that
A_{1}^{*}
fixes
1
and
S
pointwise. For every two distinct vertices
u,v\in\Gamma_{2}(1)
, from Figure
1
we have that
\Gamma_{1}(u)\cap S\not=\Gamma_{1}(v)\cap S
, that is, every two distinct vertices in
\Gamma_{2}(1)
cannot have the same neighbours in
\Gamma_{1}(1)
. Since
A_{1}^{*}
fixes
\Gamma_{0}(1)\cup\Gamma_{1}(1)
pointwise and
\Gamma_{2}(1)
setwise,
A_{1}^{*}
fixes
u
and
v
and hence
A_{1}^{*}
fixes
\Gamma_{2}(1)
pointwise. By the transitivity of
A
on
V(\Gamma)
,
A_{w}^{*}
fixes
\Gamma_{2}(w)
pointwise, for every
w\in V(\Gamma)
. Since
\langle S\rangle=C_{2^{s}m}
,
\Gamma
is connected, and an easy inductive argument on
k
gives rise to that
A_{1}^{*}
fixes
\Gamma_{k}(1)
pointwise for every positive integer
k
. Thus,
A_{1}^{*}
fixes each vertex of
\Gamma
, that is,
A_{1}^{*}=1
, and in particular,
A_{1}
acts faithfully on
S
.

Again by Figure
1
, the induced subgraph
[S]
is a cycle of length
6
and hence
\hbox{\rm Aut}([S])\cong D_{12}
, the dihedral group of order
12
. Since
A_{1}
is faithful on
S
, we may assume that
A_{1}\leq\hbox{\rm Aut}([S])
, and since
C_{2}\times C_{2}\cong\langle\alpha,\beta\rangle\leq\hbox{\rm Aut}(G,S)\leq A_{1}
, we have either
A_{1}=\hbox{\rm Aut}([S])\cong D_{12}
, or
A_{1}=\hbox{\rm Aut}(G,S)
.

Suppose that
A_{1}=\hbox{\rm Aut}([S])\cong D_{12}
. Then
A_{1}
has a unique Sylow
3
-subgroup of order
3
, and since
[S]
is a cycle of length
6
, the unique Sylow
3
-subgroup can be generated by an element of order
3
, say
\gamma
, such that
(ab)^{\gamma}=a^{2^{s-1}+1}b
,
(a^{2^{s-1}+1}b)^{\gamma}=a^{-2}b^{-2}
and
(a^{-2}b^{-2})^{\gamma}=ab
. From Figure
1
,
a^{2^{s-1}}
is the unique vertex in
\Gamma_{2}(1)
that has four neighbours in
\Gamma_{1}(1)
, which yields that
A_{1}
fixes
a^{2^{s-1}}
, namely,
(a^{2^{s-1}})^{\gamma}=a^{2^{s-1}}
. Since
\{ab,a^{2^{s-1}}\}\in E(\Gamma)
, we have
\{ab,a^{2^{s-1}}\}^{\gamma^{-1}}\in E(\Gamma)
, that is,
\{a^{-2}b^{-2},a^{2^{s-1}}\}\in E(\Gamma)
, which is not true by Figure
1
.

It follows that
A_{1}=\hbox{\rm Aut}(G,S)
. By Proposition
2.1
,
\Gamma
is normal. To finish the proof, we are left to show that
\Gamma
is not a
\rm{CI}
-graph.

Let
H=\langle R(ab)\beta\rangle
. Since
\beta^{-1}R(ab)\beta=R((ab)^{\beta})=R(a^{2^{s-1}+1}b)
and
\beta^{2}=1
, we have

(R(ab)\beta)^{2}=R(ab)R(ab)^{\beta}=R(ab)R(a^{2^{s-1}+1}b)=R(a^{2^{s-1}+2}b^{2}),

and since
o(a^{2^{s-1}+2}b^{2})=2^{s-1}m
, we obtain that
o(R(ab)\beta)
is even and
o(R(ab)\beta)=(2,o(R(ab)\beta))o((R(ab)\beta)^{2})=2^{s}m
, that is,
H\cong C_{2^{s}m}
.
Note that
\langle R(a^{2^{s-1}+2}b^{2})\rangle
is semiregular on
V(\Gamma)
with two orbits of length
2^{s-1}m
, that is,
\langle a^{2^{s-1}+2}b^{2}\rangle=\langle a^{2}b\rangle
and
a\langle a^{2}b\rangle
. Since
1^{R(ab)\beta}=(ab)^{\beta}=a^{2^{s-1}+1}b\in a\langle a^{2}b\rangle
and
\langle R(a^{2^{s-1}+2}b^{2})\rangle
is a normal subgroup of index
2
in
H
,
R(ab)\beta
interchanges the two orbits
\langle a^{2}b\rangle
and
a\langle a^{2}b\rangle
, implying that
H
is transitive on
V(\Gamma)
. Since
|H|=|V(\Gamma)|
,
H
is regular on
V(\Gamma)
, and since
R(C_{2^{s}m})\not=H
,
\Gamma
is not a CI-graph by Proposition
2.2
.
\Box

Let
G
be a finite group and let
p
be a prime. Denote by
G_{p}
a Sylow
p
-subgroup of
G
. The right multiplication
R(G)
and the automorphism group
\hbox{\rm Aut}(G)
of
G
are permutation groups on
G
. It is easy to see that
R(g)^{\alpha}=R(g^{\alpha})
for every
g\in G
and
\alpha\in\hbox{\rm Aut}(G)
, implying
R(G)
is normalized by
\hbox{\rm Aut}(G)
. Furthermore,
R(G)\cap\hbox{\rm Aut}(G)=1
, and hence we have
R(G)\hbox{\rm Aut}(G)=R(G)\rtimes\hbox{\rm Aut}(G)
. This group is called the
holomorph
of the group
G
, denoted by
\hbox{\rm Hol}(G)
, that is,
\hbox{\rm Hol}(G)=\langle R(G),\hbox{\rm Aut}(G)\rangle=R(G)\rtimes\hbox{\rm Aut}(G)
(see
[
46
, Lemma 7.16]
).

Now we are ready to prove Theorem
1.1
.

Proof of Theorem
1.1
:
First we prove the first half of Theorem
1.1
, that is,
C_{n}
is a NDCI-group if and only if
8\nmid n
.

Let
8\mid n
. By Proposition
2.3
and Lemma
3.2
, there exists a normal non-CI Cayley digraph on
C_{n}
, that is,
C_{n}
is not a NDCI-group. The necessity follows. To prove the sufficiency, assume that
8\nmid n
and we only need to prove that
C_{n}
is a NDCI-group.

For convenience, write
n=p_{1}^{r_{1}}p_{2}^{r_{2}}\cdots p_{m}^{r_{m}}2^{s}
as the prime factorization of
n
, where
s\leq 2
and
p_{1},p_{2},\cdots,p_{m}
are distinct odd prime factors of
n
such that
p_{1}>p_{2}>\cdots>p_{m}
. By Proposition
2.4
,
C_{n}
is a DCI-group for
n=1,2,4
, and so a NDCI-group. Thus, we may assume that
m\geq 1
. In particular,
r_{i}\geq 1
for each
1\leq i\leq m
.

Let
\Gamma=\hbox{\rm Cay}(C_{n},S)
be a normal Cayley digraph and let
A=\hbox{\rm Aut}(\Gamma)
. To prove that
C_{n}
is a NDCI-group, it suffices to show that
\Gamma
is a CI-digraph.

Let
C_{n}=C_{p_{1}^{r_{1}}}\times C_{p_{2}^{r_{2}}}\times\cdots C_{p_{m}^{r_{m}}}\times C_{2^{s}}
. By Proposition
2.5
,

\hbox{\rm Aut}(C_{n})=\hbox{\rm Aut}(C_{p_{1}^{r_{1}}})\times\hbox{\rm Aut}(C_{p_{2}^{r_{2}}})\times\cdots\times\hbox{\rm Aut}(C_{p_{m}^{r_{m}}})\times\hbox{\rm Aut}(C_{2^{s}}).

Recall that
\hbox{\rm Hol}(C_{n})=R(C_{n})\hbox{\rm Aut}(C_{n})
, and
R(x)^{\alpha}=R(x^{\alpha})
for all
\alpha\in\hbox{\rm Aut}(C_{n})
and
x\in C_{n}
. It is easy to see that for all
\alpha_{i}\in\hbox{\rm Aut}(C_{p_{i}^{r_{i}}})
,

(z_{1}\cdots z_{i-1}z_{i}z_{i+1}\cdots z_{n})^{\alpha_{i}}=z_{1}\cdots z_{i-1}z_{i}^{\alpha_{i}}z_{i+1}\cdots z_{m},\mbox{ where }z_{j}\in C_{p_{j}^{r^{j}}}.

It follows that for all
i\not=j
,

[R(C_{p_{j}^{r_{j}}}),\hbox{\rm Aut}(C_{p_{i}^{r_{i}}})]=[R(C_{2^{s}}),\hbox{\rm Aut}(C_{p_{i}^{r_{i}}})]=1,

that is,
\hbox{\rm Aut}(C_{p_{i}^{r_{i}}})
commutes with
R(C_{p_{j}^{r_{j}}})
and
R(C_{2^{s}})
elementwise. Since
\Gamma=\hbox{\rm Cay}(C_{n},S)
is normal, Proposition
2.1
implies that
A=R(C_{n})\hbox{\rm Aut}(C_{n},S)\leq\hbox{\rm Hol}(C_{n})
and
A_{1}=\hbox{\rm Aut}(C_{n},S)
.

Let
G
be a regular subgroup of
A
with
G\cong C_{n}
. To prove that
\Gamma
is a CI-digraph, by Proposition
2.2
it suffices to show that
G=R(C_{n})
.

Note that
G
has a unique Sylow
p_{i}
-subgroup
G_{p_{i}}
with
|G_{p_{i}}|=p_{i}^{r_{i}}
, and
G\leq A\leq\hbox{\rm Hol}(C_{n})=R(C_{n})\rtimes(\hbox{\rm Aut}(C_{p_{1}^{r_{1}}})\times\hbox{\rm Aut}(C_{p_{2}^{r_{2}}})\times\cdots\times\hbox{\rm Aut}(C_{p_{m}^{r_{m}}})\times\hbox{\rm Aut}(C_{2^{s}}))
.

Claim 1:
Assume that
g=R(a)\alpha_{1}\alpha_{2}\cdots\alpha_{m}\alpha_{m+1}\in G
, where
a\in C_{n}
,
\alpha_{m+1}\in\hbox{\rm Aut}(C_{2^{s}})
and
\alpha_{i}\in\hbox{\rm Aut}(C_{p_{i}^{r_{i}}})
for each
1\leq i\leq m
, and assume that
R(C_{p_{k}^{r_{k}}})\leq G
for some
1\leq k\leq m
. Then
\alpha_{k}=1
.

Since
R(C_{p_{k}^{r_{k}}})\leq G
and
G\cong C_{n}
, we have
[g,R(C_{p_{k}^{r_{k}}})]=1
. Since
[\hbox{\rm Aut}(C_{p_{i}^{r_{i}}}),R(C_{p_{k}^{r_{k}}})]=1
for every
i\not=k
and
[\hbox{\rm Aut}(C_{2^{s}}),R(C_{p_{k}^{r_{k}}})]=1
, we have
[\alpha_{i},R(C_{p_{k}^{r_{k}}})]=1
for every
i\not=k
, and since
[R(a),R(C_{p_{k}^{r_{k}}})]=1
and
g=R(a)\alpha_{1}\alpha_{2}\cdots\alpha_{m}\alpha_{m+1}
, we have
[\alpha_{k},R(C_{p_{k}^{r_{k}}})]=1
. This implies that
\alpha_{k}=1
as
\alpha_{k}\in\hbox{\rm Aut}(C_{p_{k}^{r_{k}}})
.

Claim 2:

R(C_{p_{k}^{r_{k}}})\leq G
for each
1\leq k\leq m
.

Recall that
\hbox{\rm Hol}(C_{n})_{p_{i}}
is a Sylow
p_{i}
-subgroup of
\hbox{\rm Hol}(C_{n})
and
R(C_{n})\unlhd\hbox{\rm Hol}(C_{n})=R(C_{n})\hbox{\rm Aut}(C_{n})
. Then
\hbox{\rm Hol}(C_{n})_{p_{i}}\leq R(C_{n})(\hbox{\rm Aut}(C_{n}))_{p_{i}}
for every
1\leq i\leq m
, and since
\hbox{\rm Aut}(C_{n})
is abelian,
(\hbox{\rm Aut}(C_{n}))_{p_{i}}
is the unique Sylow
p_{i}
-subgroup of
\hbox{\rm Aut}(C_{n})
. Since
\hbox{\rm Aut}(C_{p_{i}^{r_{i}}})\cong C_{(p_{i}-1)p_{i}^{r_{i}-1}}
,
\hbox{\rm Aut}(C_{p_{i}^{r_{i}}})
has a unique subgroup of order
p_{i}-1
, denoted by
\hbox{\rm Aut}(C_{p_{i}^{r_{i}}})_{p_{i}-1}
, and a unique Sylow
p_{i}
-subgroup, that is,
\hbox{\rm Aut}(C_{p_{i}^{r_{i}}})_{p_{i}}
. It follows that
\hbox{\rm Aut}(C_{p_{i}^{r_{i}}})=\hbox{\rm Aut}(C_{p_{i}^{r_{i}}})_{p_{i}-1}\times\hbox{\rm Aut}(C_{p_{i}^{r_{i}}})_{p_{i}}
. Since
p_{1}>p_{2}>\cdots>p_{m}
, we have

\hbox{\rm Aut}(C_{n})_{p_{i}}\leq\hbox{\rm Aut}(C_{p_{1}^{r_{1}}})_{p_{1}-1}\times\hbox{\rm Aut}(C_{p_{2}^{r_{2}}})_{p_{2}-1}\times\cdots\times\hbox{\rm Aut}(C_{p_{i-1}^{r_{i-1}}})_{p_{i-1}-1}\times\hbox{\rm Aut}(C_{p_{i}^{r_{i}}})_{p_{i}},

and in particular,
\hbox{\rm Aut}(C_{n})_{p_{1}}=\hbox{\rm Aut}(C_{p_{1}^{r_{1}}})_{p_{1}}
.

We process the proof by induction on
k
. For simplicity, we may assume that
0\leq k\leq m
and for
k=0
, let
R(C_{p_{k}^{r_{k}}})=1
. Then the claim is true for
k=0
.

Let
k\geq 1
. By inductive hypothesis, we assume that
R(C_{p_{i}^{r_{i}}})\leq G
for every
0\leq i<k
. We suppose that
R(C_{p_{k}^{r_{k}}})\nleq G
and will obtain a contradiction. Since
|G_{p_{k}}|=p_{k}^{r_{k}}
, there exists
h\in G_{p_{k}}
but
h\not\in R(C_{p_{k}^{r_{k}}})
. Since
h\in\hbox{\rm Hol}(C_{n})_{p_{k}}\leq R(C_{n})(\hbox{\rm Aut}(C_{n}))_{p_{k}}
and
\hbox{\rm Aut}(C_{n})_{p_{k}}\leq\hbox{\rm Aut}(C_{p_{1}^{r_{1}}})_{p_{1}-1}\times\hbox{\rm Aut}(C_{p_{2}^{r_{2}}})_{p_{2}-1}\times\cdots\times\hbox{\rm Aut}(C_{p_{k-1}^{r_{k-1}}})_{p_{k-1}-1}\times\hbox{\rm Aut}(C_{p_{k}^{r_{k}}})_{p_{k}}
, we have
h=R(h_{1})\beta
for some
h_{1}\in C_{n}
and
\beta\in(\hbox{\rm Aut}(C_{n}))_{p_{k}}
, where
\beta=\beta_{1}\beta_{2}\cdots\beta_{k}
for some
\beta_{j}\in\hbox{\rm Aut}(C_{p_{j}^{r_{j}}})_{p_{j}-1}
with
1\leq j<k
and
\beta_{k}\in\hbox{\rm Aut}(C_{p_{k}^{r_{k}}})_{p_{k}}
. Note that
h
is a
p_{k}
-element and
R(C_{p_{k}^{r_{k}}})
is the unique Sylow
p_{k}
-subgroup of
R(C_{n})
. Then
h\not\in R(C_{p_{k}^{r_{k}}})
implies that
h\not\in R(C_{n})
, forcing
\beta\not=1
. Since
R(C_{p_{i}^{r_{i}}})\leq G
for every
1\leq i<k
, by Claim 1 we have
\beta_{i}=1
. It follows
\beta=\beta_{k}\not=1
, and
\beta_{k}=R(h_{1})^{-1}h\in R(C_{n})G\leq A
. Furthermore,
\beta_{k}\in A_{1}=\hbox{\rm Aut}(C_{n},S)
, and hence
\hbox{\rm Aut}(C_{n},S)
contains an element of order
p_{k}
in
\hbox{\rm Aut}(C_{p_{k}^{r_{k}}})
. By Lemma
3.1
,
\Gamma
is non-normal, a contradiction. Thus,
R(C_{p_{k}^{r_{k}}})\leq G
. By induction,
R(C_{p_{k}^{r_{k}}})\leq G
for each
0\leq k\leq m
, as claimed.

To prove
G=R(C_{n})=R(C_{p_{1}^{r_{1}}})\times R(C_{p_{2}^{r_{2}}})\times\cdots\times R(C_{p_{m}^{r_{m}}})\times R(C_{2^{s}})
with
s\leq 2
, by Claim 2 we are only left to show
R(C_{2^{s}})\leq G
, and it suffices to show that
G_{2}\leq R(C_{2^{s}})
, because
|G_{2}|=2^{s}
.

Let
x\in G_{2}
. Since

G\leq A\leq\hbox{\rm Hol}(C_{n})=R(C_{n})\rtimes(\hbox{\rm Aut}(C_{p_{1}^{r_{1}}})\times\hbox{\rm Aut}(C_{p_{2}^{r_{2}}})\times\cdots\times\hbox{\rm Aut}(C_{p_{m}^{r_{m}}})\times\hbox{\rm Aut}(C_{2^{s}})),

we have
x=R(x_{1})\gamma_{1}\gamma_{2}\cdots\gamma_{m}\alpha
, where
x_{1}\in C_{n}
,
\alpha\in\hbox{\rm Aut}(C_{2^{s}})
and
\gamma_{i}\in\hbox{\rm Aut}(C_{p_{i}^{r_{i}}})_{p_{i}-1}
for
1\leq i\leq m
. By Claims 2 and 1,
\gamma_{i}=1
for each
1\leq i\leq m
, and hence
x=R(x_{1})\alpha\in R(C_{n})\hbox{\rm Aut}(C_{2^{s}})
. Since
[R(C_{p_{i}^{r_{i}}}),\hbox{\rm Aut}(C_{2^{s}})]=1
,
R(C_{n})\hbox{\rm Aut}(C_{2^{s}})
has a unique Sylow
2
-subgroup, that is,
R(C_{2^{s}})\hbox{\rm Aut}(C_{2^{s}})
. It follows that
x\in R(C_{2^{s}})\hbox{\rm Aut}(C_{2^{s}})
, and hence
G_{2}\leq R(C_{2^{s}})\hbox{\rm Aut}(C_{2^{s}})
.

If
\hbox{\rm Aut}(C_{2^{s}})=1
, then
G_{2}\leq R(C_{2^{s}})
, as required. We may assume that
\hbox{\rm Aut}(C_{2^{s}})\not=1
, and hence
s=2
as
s\leq 2
. It follows that
\hbox{\rm Aut}(C_{2^{s}})=\hbox{\rm Aut}(C_{4})\cong C_{2}
. In particular,
R(C_{2^{s}})\hbox{\rm Aut}(C_{2^{s}})
is a dihedral group of order
8
and hence has a unique cyclic subgroup of order
4
, that is,
R(C_{2^{s}})
. Since
G\cong C_{n}
,
G_{2}
is a cyclic group of order
4
, and since
G_{2}\leq R(C_{2^{s}})\hbox{\rm Aut}(C_{2^{s}})
, we obtain that
G_{2}=R(C_{2^{s}})
, as required. This completes the proof of the first half of the theorem.

Now we are ready to prove the second half of the theorem, that is,
C_{n}
is a
\rm{NCI}
-group if and only if either
n=8
or
8\nmid n
. If
n=8
then by Proposition
2.4
,
C_{n}
is a CI-group and hence a NCI-group; if
8\nmid n
then by above proof of the first half of Theorem
1.1
,
C_{n}
is a NDCI-group and hence a NCI-group. This proves the sufficiency, and the necessity follows from Lemma
3.2
.
\Box

4.
Ideas from S-ring theory

One of the approaches to study CI-property is based on the method of S-rings. This approach was suggested in
[
31
]
and realized in
[
27
,
32
,
33
,
42
]
. A Cayley digraph
\Gamma=\hbox{\rm Cay}(G,S)
of a group
G
is CI if and only if the smallest S-ring
\mathcal{A}
over
G
such that
\underline{S}=\sum_{s\in S}s\in\mathcal{A}
is CI (see
[
31
, Definition 3]
for the definition of a CI-S-ring). The automorphism group of any S-ring over
G
contains the group
R(G)
and an S-ring is called
normal
if
R(G)
is normal in its automorphism group. Due to
[
10
, Theorem 2.6.4]
, we have
\hbox{\rm Aut}(\Gamma)=\hbox{\rm Aut}(\mathcal{A})
. So
\Gamma
is normal if and only if
\mathcal{A}
is normal. Normal S-rings over cyclic groups were studied in
[
20
]
. In fact, using
[
20
, Corollary 6.5, Theorem 6.6, Lemma 7.1]
, it is possible to derive that if
8\nmid n
then every normal S-ring and hence every normal Cayley digraph over a cyclic group of order
n
is CI.
