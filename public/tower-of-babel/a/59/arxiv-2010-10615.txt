Some Algebraic Aspects of the Inhomogeneous Six-Vertex Model This paper is a contribution to the Special Issue on Mathematics of Integrable Systems: Classical and Quantum in honor of Leon Takhtajan. The full collection is available at https://www.emis.de/journals/SIGMA/Takhtajan.html
Vladimir V. BAZHANOV a, Gleb A. KOTOUSOV b, Sergii M. KOVAL a and Sergei L. LUKYANOV cd

Abstract
The inhomogeneous six-vertex model is a 2 D D multiparametric integrable statistical system. In the scaling limit it is expected to cover different classes of critical behaviour which, for the most part, have remained unexplored. For general values of the parameters and twisted boundary conditions the model possesses U ⁡ ( 1 ) {\rm U}(1) invariance. In this paper we discuss the restrictions imposed on the parameters for which additional global symmetries arise that are consistent with the integrable structure. These include the lattice counterparts of 𝒞 {\mathcal{C}} , 𝒫 {\mathcal{P}} and 𝒯 {\mathcal{T}} as well as translational invariance. The special properties of the lattice system that possesses an additional 𝒵 r {\mathcal{Z}}_{r} invariance are considered. We also describe the Hermitian structures, which are consistent with the integrable one. The analysis lays the groundwork for studying the scaling limit of the inhomogeneous six-vertex model.

Introduction

The six-vertex model has played a
central rôle in the theory of exactly solvable
models in statistical mechanics.
Its origins go back to the Pauling ice model
[
34
]

and it was later employed in the description of
a two-dimensional ferroelectric
(for a review see
[
32
,
36

The free energy per site in the
thermodynamic limit for a special “square ice” case
was calculated by Lieb
[

.
The result was then extended to the more general ferroelectric model
by Sutherland, Yang and Yang

[
38

41

39
]

All these works dealt with the homogeneous statistical system defined on the square lattice.
They used the technique pioneered by Bethe

[

Later on, Baxter in the work
[

by means of the
Bethe ansatz technique.
This was an important step
that led to the concept of commuting transfer matrices.
Subsequently, in his
seminal paper on the exact solution of the
eight-vertex model
[
4
]
, Baxter developed
the method of commuting transfer matrices, laying
the foundation of the modern theory of integrable quantum systems.
The approach was further extended
by Faddeev, Sklyanin and Takhtajan
within the quantum inverse scattering method (QISM)

[

,

The local Boltzmann weights in the six-vertex model
are expressed through the
elements of the

The inhomogeneous model considered in

4

is defined on the square lattice, where each
horizontal and vertical line is associated with its own
variable. The local Boltzmann weights at each vertex
have the same value of the anisotropy parameter, while the spectral

passing through the vertex.
In this setting the row-to-row transfer matrix

{\mathbb{T}}

for the lattice with

\zeta

\{\eta_{1},\eta_{2},\dots,\eta_{N}\}

The Yang–Baxter equation implies that for the (quasi-)periodic
boundary conditions the transfer
matrices with different values of
\zeta
, but with the same sets
\{\eta_{J}\}

form a commuting family

\displaystyle\big[{\mathbb{T}}(\zeta),{\mathbb{T}}(\zeta^{\prime})\big]=0.

The inhomogeneous six-vertex model turns out to be a multiparametric, integrable, statistical system
that depends on the variables associated with the vertical lines,
\eta_{J}
,
those labeling the horizontal lines and the anisotropy parameter.
The latter will be referred to as

.

The most studied case of the model is
when the set of local Boltzmann weights
is the same at each vertex.
Then

\eta_{1}=\eta_{2}=\cdots=\eta_{N}

and the transfer matrix commutes with the spin
\frac{1}{2}
Heisenberg

XXZ
Hamiltonian. The anisotropy parameter
of the spin chain, which is usually denoted as

, is related to

as

\Delta=\frac{1}{2}\big(q+q^{-1}\big)
.
It determines the most interesting
physical properties of the model. In particular when
-1<\Delta<1

is unimodular (
|q|=1

the system turns out to be critical and its scaling behaviour is governed by
a conformal field theory (CFT) of
a massless Gaussian field
[
33
,
28
,
1
]
.
Perhaps the simplest inhomogeneous six-vertex model
corresponds to a staggering of the
variables associated with the vertical lines of the lattice
such that

\eta_{1}=\eta_{3}=\cdots
,
\eta_{2}=\eta_{4}=\cdots
,
while
\eta_{1}\neq\eta_{2}
and a similar staggering for the variables
assigned to the horizontal lines. In the case when

\eta_{1}/\eta_{2}>0

where

the lattice model exhibits universal behaviour that is described by the massive Thirring/sine–Gordon model

[

.

The identification of the critical surfaces in the space of couplings of
the inhomogeneous six-vertex model
as well as a description of the corresponding
universality classes is, without doubt, an interesting problem.
However, apart from the few cases mentioned above not much work
has been done in this direction. An evident starting point to
approach the problem
would be to explore the restrictions on the parameters
which give rise to extra symmetries in the model.
The latter would include
translational invariance as well as global involutions such as
{\cal C}
,
{\cal P}
and
{\cal T}

which are common attributes of a local QFT.
There may also be other global symmetries, e.g., the
{\cal Z}_{2}

of the staggered model with
\eta_{J}/\eta_{1}=(-1)^{J-1}
. Of course, the
identification of the universality classes requires more than just an analysis of the symmetries.
The integrability of the model makes possible a detailed quantitative study of the scaling limit.
The goal of this work is to describe the
integrable structure underlying the general inhomogeneous six-vertex model
as well as its interplay with the global symmetries.

In Section

We also present the construction of the transfer matrix as well as its eigenstates (Bethe states)

The commuting family for the inhomogeneous six-vertex model,

\mathbb{T}(\zeta)

other important members – the so-called

Their construction

is presented in Section

Section

{\cal C}
,

{\cal T}
.
We describe the conditions under which these conjugations

For a generic set of the inhomogeneities
the transfer matrix and

w.r.t. the standard matrix Hermitian conjugation.
In Section
5

a family of
Hermitian structures is introduced,
which are also consistent with the integrable

The norm of the Bethe states associated with these Hermitian structures
can be computed via the formula
originally conjectured by Gaudin, McCoy and Wu
[
22

for the homogeneous case
and then extended and proven, using the formalism of the QISM, for

[
30
]

Further restrictions on the parameters of the model,
which lead to the presence of additional global symmetries,

6

7

the invariance w.r.t. lattice translations.
The latter appears when the number of columns of the lattice is
divisible by some integer

and the inhomogeneities
\eta_{J}

satisfy the
r

\eta_{J+r}=\eta_{J}
.
In this case the commuting family of the model
contains

are given by a sum of
local operators.
By the latter we mean that they are built from the local spin operators acting
on
r+1

Section
7
discusses the lattice system with the

\{\eta_{J}\}
being further restricted such that

\eta_{J}/\eta_{1}=\mbox{e}^{2\pi{\rm i}(J-1)/r}

{\cal Z}_{r}
cyclic group symmetry.
The last section contains a collection of explicit
formulae for the model with
r=1
(homogeneous) and
r=2
(
{\cal Z}_{2}
invariant).
They lay the groundwork for a detailed analysis of the scaling limit for these two cases,
which is performed in
[
9
]
.

The eigenvalues

The latter also implies that the matrices

It turns out that

which, together with the transfer matrix, contains

[

First note that, introducing the functions

\displaystyle A_{+}(\zeta)=\prod_{m=1}^{M}\big(1-\zeta/\zeta_{m}\big),\qquad f(\zeta)=\prod_{J=1}^{N}\big(1+\zeta/\eta_{J}\big),

(3.1)

\displaystyle T(\zeta)A_{+}(\zeta)=\omega^{+1}q^{+S^{z}}f\big(q^{-1}\zeta\big)A_{+}\big(q^{+2}\zeta\big)+\omega^{-1}q^{-S^{z}}f\big(q^{+1}\zeta\big)A_{+}\big(q^{-2}\zeta\big).

(3.2)

Since

is a polynomial, the r.h.s. of

at the zeroes of

\displaystyle\omega^{+1}q^{+S^{z}}f\big(q^{-1}\zeta_{m}\big)A_{+}\big(q^{+2}\zeta_{m}\big)+\omega^{-1}q^{-S^{z}}f\big(q^{+1}\zeta_{m}\big)A_{+}\big(q^{-2}\zeta_{m}\big)=0,\quad\ m=1,2,\dots,M.

These are precisely the Bethe ansatz equations (

The functional relation (

{\mathbb{A}}_{+}(\zeta)
, with eigenvalues

(

),
that commutes with the transfer matrix

{\mathbb{T}}(\zeta)
and satisfies the operator version of (

3

In this paper we use
blackboard bold symbols such as
{\mathbb{T}}
,
{\mathbb{A}}_{\pm}
,
{\mathbb{Q}}_{\pm}
,

{\mathbb{S}}^{z}
, …to denote the mutually commuting operators
acting in the space
{\mathscr{V}}_{N}
(

). For the operators acting in the same space which do not belong to the commuting family, say the entries

{\hat{{\mathsf{A}}}}
,
{\hat{\mathsf{B}}}
,
{\hat{\mathsf{C}}}
,
{\hat{\mathsf{D}}}

of the monodromy matrix (
2.16
), the “hat” notation is employed.

Actually there are two such
operators, which we denote as
{\mathbb{A}}_{\pm}(\zeta)
, that satisfy the operator relations of this
type

\displaystyle{\mathbb{T}}(\zeta){\mathbb{A}}_{\pm}(\zeta)=q^{\pm 2{\mathbb{P}}}f\big(q^{-1}\zeta\big){\mathbb{A}}_{\pm}\big(q^{+2}\zeta\big)+q^{\mp 2{\mathbb{P}}}f\big(q^{+1}\zeta\big){\mathbb{A}}_{\pm}\big(q^{-2}\zeta\big),

(3.3)

where for convenience we have used the notation

\displaystyle q^{2{\mathbb{P}}}=\omega q^{\mathbb{S}^{z}}.

(3.4)

Note that equation (
3.3
) can be rewritten in a way
so that it has the same form for the
“
+
” and “
-
” cases. Introducing the operators

\mathbb{Q}_{\pm}(\zeta)
,
which
differ from
{\mathbb{A}}_{\pm}(\zeta)
by simple factors involving
fractional powers of
\zeta
,

\displaystyle{\mathbb{Q}}_{\pm}(\zeta)=\zeta^{\pm{\mathbb{P}}}{\mathbb{A}}_{\pm}(\zeta),

one has

\displaystyle{\mathbb{T}}(\zeta){\mathbb{Q}}_{\pm}(\zeta)=f\big(q^{-1}\zeta\big){\mathbb{Q}}_{\pm}\big(q^{+2}\zeta\big)+f\big(q^{+1}\zeta\big){\mathbb{Q}}_{\pm}\big(q^{-2}\zeta\big).

The latter is the famous Baxter “
TQ
-relation”
[
4
]
.
We prefer to work with the operators
{\mathbb{A}}_{\pm}(\zeta)

as their matrix elements and eigenvalues are polynomials in
\zeta
.

Similar to
\mathbb{T}(\zeta)
, which can be expressed as
a trace of a
2\times 2
matrix (
2.18
),
the operators
{\mathbb{A}}_{\pm}(\zeta)
are also
constructed as traces of certain monodromy matrices.
However, this time the trace is taken over
infinite dimensional representations of the so-called
q
-oscillator algebra.
The latter arises as an evaluation
representation of the Borel subalgebra of the quantum affine
algebra
U_{q}\big(\widehat{\mathfrak{sl}}_{2}\big)
. The required
R
-matrices
(see (
3.6
) below) are obtained by
a suitable specialization of the universal

R
-matrix
[
29
]
.
Originally these calculations were performed
in the context of the quantum KdV theory

[
10
,
11
]
, but the
same procedure can be readily applied to the inhomogeneous
six-vertex model.

The
q
-oscillator algebra is generated by the elements
{\cal H}
and

{\cal E}_{\pm}
satisfying the commutation relations

\displaystyle[{\cal H},{\cal E}_{\pm}]=\pm 2{\cal E}_{\pm},\qquad q{\cal E}_{+}{\cal E}_{-}-q^{-1}{\cal E}_{-}{\cal E}_{+}=\frac{1}{q-q^{-1}}.

(3.5)

The basic building blocks for the construction of
\mathbb{A}_{\pm}

are the
2\times 2
matrices

\displaystyle{\mathbfcal{R}}^{(\pm)}_{m}(\zeta)=q^{\pm\frac{{\cal H}\otimes\sigma^{z}_{m}}{2}}+\big(q-q^{-1}\big)\big({\cal E}_{\mp}\otimes\sigma^{+}_{m}-\zeta{\cal E}_{\pm}\otimes\sigma^{-}_{m}\big)

\displaystyle\hphantom{{\mathbfcal{ R}}^{(\pm)}_m(\zeta)=}{}+\zeta\big(q-q^{-1}\big)q^{-\frac{{\cal H}}{2}}\big({\cal E}_{+}{\cal E}_{-}-{\cal E}_{-}{\cal E}_{+}\big)\otimes\sigma^{\mp}_{m}\sigma^{\pm}_{m},

(3.6)

which act in the
m
-th component of the tensor product (
2.3
) and whose entries
involve the formal generators (
3.5
).
Let
\rho_{\pm}
be representations of the
q
-oscillator algebra such that
the traces

\displaystyle{\rm Tr}_{\rho_{\pm}}\big[q^{\pm 2{P}{\cal H}}\big]\neq 0\qquad{\rm with}\qquad\big|q^{2P}\big|>1

exist and are non-vanishing.
Then, using the notation (
3.4
) and
assuming that the twist parameter
\omega
is such that

\displaystyle|\omega|>\max\big(\big|q^{N/2}\big|,\big|q^{-N/2}\big|\big)

(3.7)

one can introduce the operators

\displaystyle{\mathbb{Z}}_{\pm}={\rm Tr}_{\rho_{\pm}}\big[q^{\pm 2{\mathbb{P}}{\cal H}}\big]

(3.8)

that act in the quantum space
{\mathscr{V}}_{N}
(
2.3
).
With these preparations, define

\displaystyle{\mathbb{A}}_{\pm}(\zeta)={\mathbb{Z}}^{-1}_{\pm}{\rm Tr}_{\rho_{\pm}}\big[\omega^{\pm{\cal H}}{\mathbfcal{R}}^{(\pm)}_{N}(\zeta/\eta_{N}){\mathbfcal{R}}^{(\pm)}_{N-1}(\zeta/\eta_{N-1})\cdots{\mathbfcal{R}}^{(\pm)}_{1}(\zeta/\eta_{1})\big].

(3.9)

It is easy to see that such operators commute with
{\mathbb{S}}^{z}

and, therefore, with
\mathbb{Z}_{\pm}
.
As was first pointed out in
[
10
]
, the (normalized)
trace in (
3.9
) is completely determined
by the commutation relations (
3.5
) and the cyclic property of the
trace, so that the specific choice of the representations

\rho_{\pm}
is not
significant as long as the traces (
3.8
) exist and are non-vanishing.
This way it is possible to define

{\mathbb{A}}_{\pm}(\zeta)\in{\rm End}(\mathscr{V}_{N})

for all complex
\omega
(except some discrete set
of isolated points, see below) through analytic continuation,
despite that
the definition (
3.9
) applies literally to the case
(
3.7
) only.

Following the line of
[
11
]
,
one can show that the operators
\mathbb{A}_{\pm}(\zeta)
commute with
the transfer matrix and among themselves for different values of the
spectral parameter:

\displaystyle\big[\mathbb{A}_{\pm}(\zeta_{1}),{\mathbb{T}}(\zeta_{2})\big]=\big[\mathbb{A}_{\pm}(\zeta_{1}),\mathbb{A}_{\pm}(\zeta_{2})\big]=\big[\mathbb{A}_{\pm}(\zeta_{1}),\mathbb{A}_{\mp}(\zeta_{2})\big]=0.

Furthermore they
satisfy a number of important operator valued relations,
which are derived algebraically from the decomposition properties
of products of representations of the
q
-oscillator algebra.
This includes the quantum Wronskian relation

\displaystyle\big(q^{+2\mathbb{P}}-q^{-2\mathbb{P}}\big)f(\zeta)=q^{+2\mathbb{P}}\mathbb{A}_{+}\big(q^{+1}\zeta\big)\mathbb{A}_{-}\big(q^{-1}\zeta\big)-q^{-2\mathbb{P}}\mathbb{A}_{-}\big(q^{+1}\zeta\big)\mathbb{A}_{+}\big(q^{-1}\zeta\big)

(3.10)

as well as

\displaystyle\big(q^{+2\mathbb{P}}-q^{-2\mathbb{P}}\big){\mathbb{T}}(\zeta)=q^{+4\mathbb{P}}\mathbb{A}_{+}\big(q^{+2}\zeta\big)\mathbb{A}_{-}\big(q^{-2}\zeta\big)-q^{-4\mathbb{P}}\mathbb{A}_{-}\big(q^{+2}\zeta\big)\mathbb{A}_{+}\big(q^{-2}\zeta\big).

As a simple corollary of
the above two formulae, the operators
\mathbb{A}_{\pm}

satisfy (
3.3
).

From the definition (
3.9
) it follows that the
eigenvalues of
\mathbb{A}_{\pm}(\zeta)
are polynomials

\displaystyle A_{\pm}(\zeta)=\prod_{m=1}^{M_{\pm}}\big(1-\zeta/\zeta_{m}^{(\pm)}\big)

(3.11)

of degree

\displaystyle M_{\pm}=\frac{1}{2}{N}\mp S^{z}.

Their roots
\zeta_{m}^{(\pm)}
are determined by the Bethe ansatz
equations

\displaystyle\frac{f\big(\zeta_{m}^{(\pm)}q^{+1}\big)}{f\big(\zeta_{m}^{(\pm)}q^{-1}\big)}=-\omega^{\pm 2}q^{\pm 2S^{z}}\frac{{A}_{\pm}\big(\zeta_{m}^{(\pm)}q^{+2}\big)}{{A}_{\pm}\big(\zeta_{m}^{(\pm)}q^{-2}\big)},\qquad m=1,2,\dots,M_{\partial}).

Formula (
3.11
) implies that

\displaystyle{\mathbb{A}}_{\pm}(\zeta)\to\begin{cases}1&{\rm as}\quad\zeta\to 0,\\
\zeta^{M_{\pm}}{\mathbb{A}}^{(\infty)}_{\pm}&{\rm as}\quad\zeta\to+\infty,\end{cases}

(3.12)

where the operators
{\mathbb{A}}^{(\infty)}_{\pm}
act in

{\mathscr{V}}_{N}
(
2.3
) and their eigenvalues on the Bethe state (
2.19
) read as

\displaystyle{A}^{(\infty)}_{\pm}=\prod_{j=1}^{M_{\pm}}\big({-}\zeta^{(\pm)}\big)^{-1}.

Combining (
3.12
) with (
3.10
) one obtains

\displaystyle{\mathbb{A}}^{(\infty)}_{+}{\mathbb{A}}^{(\infty)}_{-}=\frac{1-\omega^{2}q^{2\mathbb{S}^{z}}}{q^{2\mathbb{S}^{z}}-\omega^{2}}.

It should be pointed out that, defined through equation (
3.9
),
the operators
\mathbb{A}_{\pm}(\zeta)
obey the normalization condition

\mathbb{A}_{\pm}(0)=\hat{\bf 1}
. However, in
the subspace with fixed
S^{z}
and when

\omega=\pm q^{-S^{z}+m}

with
m=\pm 1,\pm 2,\dots

the Bethe roots
\{\zeta_{m}\}
for
some of the eigenvalues of
\mathbb{A}_{\pm}

may become zero.
In this case, as it follows from (
3.11
),
the normalization imposed by (
3.9
) is not
suitable and that formula requires modification.
As our considerations are not sensitive to this subtlety,
we will continue to use (
3.9
)
and assume that (
3.11
) always holds.

Finally note that for
S^{z}=0
both

\mathbb{A}_{\pm}(\zeta)
tend to the same operator

\mathbb{A}(\zeta)
as
\omega\to 1
.
A careful taking of this limit in equation (
3.9
) leads
to a generalization of Baxter’s formula
for the matrix elements of the
Q
-operator of the zero-field
six-vertex model in the sector with
S^{z}=0
:

\displaystyle\big(\mathbb{A}(\zeta)\big)_{a_{N},a_{N-1},\dots,a_{1}}^{b_{N},b_{N-1},\dots,b_{1}}=q^{\frac{1}{4}\sum_{1\leq J<K\leq N}(a_{K}b_{J}-a_{J}b_{K})}\prod_{J=1}^{N}\big({-}\zeta/\eta_{J}\big)^{\frac{1}{4}(1-a_{J})(1+b_{J})}.

(3.13)

In the homogeneous case, when all the
\eta_{J}=1
, the last formula reduces
to equation (101) of
[
5
]
.
Note that the
operator (
3.13
) is still normalized as

\mathbb{A}(0)=1
, which may not be immediately obvious.

4
{\cal C}
,
{\cal P}
and
{\cal T}
conjugations

So far we have briefly reviewed the formal
algebraic aspects of the diagonalization problem for the commuting family
of operators
in the general inhomogeneous six-vertex model. All the parameters

\{\eta_{J}\}
,
q
and
\omega
were assumed to be generic complex
numbers. Though the constraint (
2.13
) was imposed,
it does not reduce the generality, as it can always be achieved via a redefinition of the spectral parameter
\zeta
.
In this section we introduce three global conjugations, which are similar to
the charge conjugation
{\cal C}
, parity inversion
{\cal P}
,
and time reversal transformation
{\cal T}
from quantum field theory.
We describe the restrictions on the parameters such that the
global involutions are consistent with the integrable structure, in the
sense that they preserve the family of commuting operators.

Let us first consider parity inversion.
This transformation may be defined via the following adjoint action on the
local spin operators

\displaystyle\hat{\cal P}\sigma^{A}_{J}\hat{\cal P}=(\eta_{{N}+1-J})^{+\frac{1}{2}\sigma_{{N}+1-J}^{z}}\sigma^{A}_{{N}+1-J}(\eta_{{N}+1-J})^{-\frac{1}{2}\sigma_{{N}+1-J}^{z}},\qquad A=x,y,z,\quad J=1,\dots,{N},

supplemented by the condition

\displaystyle\hat{\cal P}\,|\,0\,\rangle=|\,0\,\rangle,

(4.1)

where
|\,0\,\rangle
stands for the pseudovacuum (
2.6
).
4
4

4

The definition of the generator
\hat{\cal P}
contains an ambiguity in
the choice of the overall sign
\hat{\cal P}\mapsto c_{N}\hat{\cal P}

with
c_{N}^{2}=1
. In this work it is fixed by the condition (
4.1
).
In
[
9
]
the sign factor is chosen differently. The same also applies to
the generator of the charge conjugation
\hat{\cal C}
(
4.5
).

Its matrix elements read as

\displaystyle(\hat{\cal P})^{b_{N}b_{N-1}\cdots b_{1}}_{a_{N}a_{N-1}\cdots a_{1}}=\delta_{a_{N}}^{b_{1}}\delta_{a_{N-1}}^{b_{2}}\cdots\delta_{a_{1}}^{b_{N}}\prod_{J=1}^{N}\eta_{J}^{a_{J}/2}.

(4.2)

In order for
\hat{\cal P}^{2}=\hat{\bf 1}
, one must require that

\displaystyle\eta_{J}=(\eta_{N+1-J})^{-1},\qquad J=1,2,\dots,N.

(4.3)

Then the action of the parity conjugation on the transfer matrix and
\mathbb{A}_{\pm}(\zeta)
is given by

\displaystyle{\hat{\cal P}}{\mathbb{T}}(\zeta\,|\,{\omega}){\hat{\cal P}}=\zeta^{N}{\mathbb{T}}\big(\zeta^{-1}\,|\,{\omega^{-1}}\big),

\displaystyle{\hat{\cal P}}{\mathbb{A}}_{\pm}(\zeta\,|\,\omega){\hat{\cal P}}=\zeta^{(\frac{N}{2}\mp\mathbb{S}^{z})}{\mathbb{A}}_{\pm}\big(\zeta^{-1}\,|\,\omega^{-1}\big)\big[{\mathbb{A}}^{({\infty})}_{\pm}\big(\omega^{-1}\big)\big]^{-1},

(4.4)

where we explicitly indicate the dependence
on the twist parameter
\omega
.
These relations may be deduced directly from the definition of
\mathbb{T}(\zeta)
and
\mathbb{A}_{\pm}(\zeta)
.
For instance, the first equality follows from (
2.18
) and (
2.14
)
as well as the simple property

\displaystyle\big(\sigma^{x}\otimes\zeta^{\frac{1}{2}\sigma^{z}_{m}}\big)\big(\bm{R}_{m}(q\zeta)\big)^{t}\big(\sigma^{x}\otimes\zeta^{\frac{1}{2}\sigma^{z}_{m}}\big)^{-1}=\zeta\bm{R}_{m}\big(q\zeta^{-1}\big),

where
\big(\bm{R}_{m}(q\zeta)\big)^{t}

stands for the transpose of the
2\times 2
operator valued matrix (
2.15
). Since

\mathbb{T}(\zeta\,|\,\omega)
and
\mathbb{A}_{\pm}(\zeta\,|\,\omega)
do not commute amongst themselves for different values of the twist parameter
\omega
,
formula (
4.4
) shows that unless
\omega^{2}=1
, the parity
inversion does not preserve the commuting family of operators.

The situation is similar for the charge conjugation.
Its generator may be introduced as

\displaystyle\hat{\cal C}=\prod_{J=1}^{N}(\eta_{J})^{\frac{1}{2}\sigma^{z}_{J}}\sigma^{x}_{J},

(4.5)

whose adjoint action on the local spin operators is given by

\displaystyle{\hat{\cal C}}\sigma_{J}^{\pm}{\hat{\cal C}}=\eta_{J}^{\mp 1}\sigma_{J}^{\mp},\qquad{\hat{\cal C}}\sigma_{J}^{z}{\hat{\cal C}}=-\sigma_{J}^{z},\qquad J=1,\dots,{N}.

For arbitrary complex values of the inhomogeneities
one has

\displaystyle{\hat{\cal C}}{\mathbb{T}}(\zeta\,|\,\omega){\hat{\cal C}}={\mathbb{T}}\big(\zeta\,|\,\omega^{-1}\big),\qquad{\hat{\cal C}}{\mathbb{A}}_{\pm}(\zeta\,|\,\omega){\hat{\cal C}}={\mathbb{A}}_{\mp}\big(\zeta\,|\,\omega^{-1}\big).

(4.6)

The proof of the first equality is based on the relation

\displaystyle\big(\sigma^{x}\otimes\zeta^{-\frac{1}{2}\sigma^{z}_{m}}\sigma^{y}_{m}\big)\bm{R}_{m}(\zeta)\big(\sigma^{x}\otimes\zeta^{-\frac{1}{2}\sigma^{z}_{m}}\sigma^{y}_{m}\big)^{-1}=\bm{R}_{m}(\zeta).

Though
the
{\cal C}
and
{\cal P}
transformations do not respect the integrable structure themselves
for
\omega^{2}\neq 1
,
by combining them together
one gets

\displaystyle{\hat{\cal C}}{\hat{\cal P}}{\mathbb{T}}(\zeta){\hat{\cal C}}{\hat{\cal P}}=\zeta^{N}{\mathbb{T}}\big(\zeta^{-1}\big),

\displaystyle{\hat{\cal C}}{\hat{\cal P}}{\mathbb{A}}_{\pm}(\zeta){\hat{\cal C}}{\hat{\cal P}}=\zeta^{\frac{N}{2}\mp\mathbb{S}^{z}}{\mathbb{A}}_{\mp}\big(\zeta^{-1}\big)\big[\mathbb{A}^{(\infty)}_{\pm}\big]^{-1}

(4.7)

provided that the conditions (
4.3
) are imposed.
Here all the original and transformed operators
correspond to the same value of
\omega
so that they commute with each other and
have the same set of eigenvectors. This has an important consequence.
Since the
{\cal C}{\cal P}
conjugation intertwines
the sectors with
S^{z}
and
-S^{z}

it is always possible to focus on the case with
S^{z}\geq 0
.
Further, the second line of equation (
4.7
) implies
that for the construction of the Bethe states
(
2.19
) in the sector with arbitrary
S^{z}

it is sufficient to consider the operator
\mathbb{A}_{+}(\zeta)
.
For these reasons we focus on the zeroes of the eigenvalues of

\mathbb{A}_{+}
and use the notation
\zeta_{m}\equiv\zeta_{m}^{(+)}
and

M\equiv M_{+}\leq\frac{N}{2}
.

Up to this point there was no essential need to impose any reality conditions
on the parameters of the model. However, for the
time reversal transformation, the reality conditions become crucial.
Having in mind applications to the
{\cal Z}_{2}
invariant model studied in

[
26
,
23
,
25
,
20
,
16
,
21
,
8
,
9
]
,
we will assume that
q
and
\omega
are unimodular
and parameterize them as

\displaystyle q=\mbox{e}^{{\rm i}\gamma},\qquad 0<\gamma<\pi,

and

\displaystyle\omega=\mbox{e}^{{\rm i}\pi{\tt k}},\qquad-\frac{1}{2}<{\tt k}\leq\frac{1}{2}.

The
R
-matrix then obeys the property

\displaystyle(\sigma^{x}\otimes\sigma^{x})\big({\bm{R}}(q\zeta)\big)^{*}(\sigma^{x}\otimes\sigma^{x})=q^{-1}\zeta^{*}{\bm{R}}(q/\zeta^{*}).

As for the inhomogeneities,
three cases will be considered:

(i)

All
\eta_{J}
are unimodular complex numbers satisfying (
4.3
):

\displaystyle\eta_{J}^{*}=\eta_{J}^{-1}=\eta_{N+1-J}.

(4.8)

(ii)

All the inhomogeneities are real:

\displaystyle\eta_{J}^{*}=\eta_{J}=\big(\eta_{N+1-J}\big)^{-1}.

(4.9)

(iii)

In the third case we take the number of columns in the lattice to be even
and divide the inhomogeneities into two groups:
\{\eta_{J}\}_{J=1}^{N/2}
and

\{\eta_{N/2+J}\}_{J=1}^{N/2}
.
The first group
\{\eta_{J}\}_{J=1}^{N/2}
are taken to have the same absolute value
\Lambda>0
,
while for the other group
|\eta_{N/2+1}|=\cdots=|\eta_{N}|=\Lambda^{-1}
.
The arguments of
\eta_{J}
and
\eta_{N/2+J}
are chosen to be the same

\displaystyle\eta_{J}=\Lambda^{-1}\omega_{J},\qquad\eta_{N/2+J}=\Lambda\omega_{J},\qquad J=1,2,\dots,N/2,

(4.10)

where

\{\omega_{J}\}_{J=1}^{N/2}
are unimodular complex numbers.
Notice that the constraint (
4.3
), which is always assumed,
implies

\displaystyle\omega_{J}=(\omega_{J}^{*})^{-1}=(\omega_{N/2+1-J})^{-1}.

(4.11)

The time reversal transformation
is realized as an anti-unitary operator acting on
an arbitrary state
{\bm{\psi}}\in{\mathscr{V}}_{N}
as

\displaystyle\hat{\cal T}{\bm{\psi}}=\hat{\mathsf{U}}{\bm{\psi}}^{*},

(4.12)

where the asterisk
(*)
stands for complex conjugation.
The matrix
\hat{\mathsf{U}}
satisfies the condition

\displaystyle\hat{{\mathsf{U}}}^{*}=\hat{{\mathsf{U}}}^{-1}

(4.13)

which ensures that
\hat{\cal T}^{2}=\hat{\bf 1}
.
For the first case (
4.8
) we set the matrix

\hat{\mathsf{U}}
to be

\displaystyle\eta_{J}^{*}=\eta_{J}^{-1}=\eta_{N+1-J}\colon\quad\hat{\mathsf{U}}=\prod_{J=1}^{N}\sigma^{x}_{J}.

(4.14)

Then it follows that

\displaystyle{\hat{\cal T}}{\mathbb{T}}(\zeta){\hat{\cal T}}=(\zeta^{*})^{N}{\mathbb{T}}\big((\zeta^{*})^{-1}\big),

\displaystyle{\hat{\cal T}}{\mathbb{A}}_{\pm}(\zeta){\hat{\cal T}}=(\zeta^{*})^{\frac{N}{2}\mp\mathbb{S}^{z}}{\mathbb{A}}_{\mp}\big((\zeta^{*})^{-1}\big)\big[{\mathbb{A}}^{(\infty)}_{\mp}\big]^{-1},

(4.15)

i.e., defined in this way the time reversal transformation
preserves the integrable structure of the model similar to
{\cal C}{\cal P}
.
Combining all three transformations (
4.6
), (
4.4
) and
(
4.15
) together, yields

\displaystyle{\hat{\cal C}}{\hat{\cal P}}{\hat{\cal T}}{\mathbb{A}}_{\pm}(\zeta){\hat{\cal C}}{\hat{\cal P}}{\hat{\cal T}}={\mathbb{A}}_{\pm}(\zeta^{*}),\qquad{\hat{\cal C}}{\hat{\cal P}}{\hat{\cal T}}{\mathbb{T}}(\zeta){\hat{\cal C}}{\hat{\cal P}}{\hat{\cal T}}={\mathbb{T}}(\zeta^{*}).

(4.16)

For the Bethe states, the above equation
together with (
3.1
) implies that the sets
\{\zeta_{m}\}
corresponding to

\bm{\Psi}
and
{\cal\hat{C}\hat{P}\hat{T}}\bm{\Psi}
are complex conjugate to each other. Notice that the phase assignment in (
2.10
) and consequently in (
2.17
)
has been chosen in such a way that

\displaystyle{\cal\hat{C}\hat{P}\hat{T}}\bm{\Psi}(\{\zeta_{j}\})=\bm{\Psi}(\{\zeta_{j}^{*}\}).

(4.17)

In the second case
(ii)

it is also possible to introduce the time-reversal symmetry in such
a way that relations (
4.15
) are preserved.
To proceed we will need to establish how the transfer matrix and,
more generally, the monodromy matrix
\bm{M}(\zeta\,|\,\eta_{N},\eta_{N-1},\dots,\eta_{1})
(
2.14
),
behaves under an interchange of the inhomogeneities.
The eigenvalues of the transfer matrix (
2.11
) depend on
\{\eta_{J}\}

both explicitly
and implicitly via the Bethe roots which satisfy equation (
2.9
).
In both cases the inhomogeneities enter only through symmetric combinations,
so that the eigenvalues are symmetric functions of them.
Therefore two transfer matrices obtained from each other by a permutation of the same set of
inhomogeneities must be connected by a similarity transformation in the quantum space
\mathscr{V}_{N}
.
In fact, the same holds true for all the entries of the monodromy matrix as well.
The group of permutations of the ordered set
(\eta_{J})
is generated by
elementary permutations of neighbouring pairs:

(\eta_{N},\dots,\eta_{n+1},\eta_{n},\dots,\eta_{1})\mapsto(\eta_{N},\dots,\eta_{n},\eta_{n+1},\dots,\eta_{1}).

The latter are realized through the relation

\displaystyle{\bm{M}}(\zeta\,|\,\eta_{N},\dots,\eta_{n+1},\eta_{n},\dots,\eta_{1})\check{\bm{R}}_{n+1,n}(\eta_{n+1}/\eta_{n})

\displaystyle\qquad=\check{\bm{R}}_{n+1,n}(\eta_{n+1}/\eta_{n}){\bm{M}}(\zeta\,|\,\eta_{N},\dots,\eta_{n},\eta_{n+1},\dots,\eta_{1}),

(4.18)

which is a simple consequence of the Yang–Baxter equation.
Here
\check{\bm{R}}_{n+1,n}

is expressed in terms of the
R
-matrix (
2.1
) as

\displaystyle\check{\bm{R}}_{n+1,n}(\zeta)=\frac{1}{q-q^{-1}\zeta}{\bm{R}}_{n+1,n}(-\zeta){\bm{P}}_{n+1,n}

(4.19)

with
{\bm{P}}_{n+1,n}
standing for the operator that permutes the
(n+1)
-th and
n
-th
factor in the tensor product (
2.3
).
For the operation that completely reverses the order
of the
\{\eta_{J}\}
it is easy to see that

\displaystyle{\bm{M}}(\zeta\,|\,\eta_{N},\eta_{N-1},\dots,\eta_{1})\mapsto{\bm{M}}(\zeta\,|\,\eta_{1},\eta_{2},\dots,\eta_{N})=\hat{\mathsf{S}}_{2}^{-1}{\bm{M}}(\zeta\,|\,\eta_{N},\eta_{N-1},\dots,\eta_{1})\hat{\mathsf{S}}_{2},

where the matrix

\hat{\mathsf{S}}_{2}\in{\rm End}({\mathscr{V}}_{N})
is given by the ordered product

\displaystyle\hat{\mathsf{S}}_{2}=\overset{\displaystyle\curvearrowleft}{\prod_{m=2}^{{N}}}\Bigg[\,\overset{\displaystyle\curvearrowleft}{\prod_{n=N-m+1}^{{N}-1}}\check{\bm{R}}_{n+1,n}\big(\eta_{m}/\eta_{n+m-N}\big)\Bigg].

(4.20)

The time-reversal transformation corresponding to (
4.9
)
is defined through (
4.12
) with

\displaystyle\eta_{J}^{*}=\eta_{J}=(\eta_{N+1-J})^{-1}\colon\quad\hat{\mathsf{U}}=\hat{\mathsf{S}}_{2}\prod_{J=1}^{N}\sigma^{x}_{J}.

One can check that
the condition (
4.13
) is satisfied, and
that equations (
4.15
), (
4.16
) remain intact.

Finally let’s turn to the last case
(iii)
, where the inhomogeneities are constrained by the conditions (
4.10
) and (
4.11
).
For this purpose we need to consider the similarity transformation in the quantum space
that interchanges the two
groups of inhomogeneities for the monodromy matrix, while preserving their ordering
inside of each group

\displaystyle{\bm{M}}(\zeta\,|\,\eta_{N/2},\dots,\eta_{1},\eta_{N},\dots,\eta_{N/2+1})=\hat{\mathsf{S}}_{3}^{-1}{\bm{M}}(\zeta\,|\,\eta_{N},\dots,\eta_{N/2+1},\eta_{N},\dots,\eta_{1})\hat{\mathsf{S}}_{3}.

One can show that

\displaystyle\hat{\mathsf{S}}_{3}=\overset{\displaystyle\curvearrowleft}{\prod_{m=N/2+1}^{N}}\Bigg[\overset{\displaystyle\curvearrowleft}{\prod_{n=0}^{N/2-1}}\check{\bm{R}}_{m-n,m-n-1}\big(\eta_{N-n}/\eta_{m-N/2}\big)\Bigg].

(4.21)

Introduce the matrix

\displaystyle\eta_{J}=\Lambda^{-1}\omega_{J},\qquad\eta_{J+N/2}=\Lambda\omega_{J},\qquad|\omega_{J}|=1\colon\quad\hat{\mathsf{U}}=\hat{\mathsf{S}}_{3}\prod_{J=1}^{N}\sigma^{x}_{J}.

It turns out that it also satisfies (

) and,
for the time-reversal conjugation (

) with

defined as above,

) and (

This way

–
(iii)
of the reality conditions imposed on the inhomogeneities,

) such that

) and (

\displaystyle\hat{\mathsf{U}}=\hat{\mathsf{S}}_{\cal T}\prod_{J=1}^{N}\sigma^{x}_{J},

where

Namely when the inhomogeneities are constrained by (

For the cases (

5
Hermitian structure

There are many ways to introduce the
Hermitian structure, i.e.,
the sesquilinear form for the states
and
the Hermitian conjugation of operators
in the
2^{N}
dimensional linear space

\mathscr{V}_{N}
(
2.3
).
Here we discuss
the Hermitian structures that are
consistent with the
integrable structure of the model.

Consider each factor in the product in the r.h.s. of
equation (
2.14
).
Under the standard matrix (“dagger”) conjugation
in the quantum space
\mathscr{V}_{N}
, they transform as

\displaystyle\big[q^{-\frac{1}{2}}\bm{R}_{m}\big(q\zeta/\eta_{m}\big)\big]^{\dagger}=\sigma^{x}\big(\zeta^{*}/\eta_{m}^{*}\big)^{-\frac{1}{2}\sigma^{z}}\big[q^{-\frac{1}{2}}\bm{R}_{m}\big(q\zeta^{*}/\eta_{m}^{*}\big)\big]\big(\zeta^{*}/\eta_{m}^{*}\big)^{+\frac{1}{2}\sigma^{z}}\sigma^{x}.

In turn, taking the
{\dagger}
-conjugation of the monodromy matrix results in

\displaystyle\big[\bm{M}(\zeta\,|\,\eta_{N},\eta_{N-1},\dots,\eta_{1})\big]^{\dagger}=\sigma^{x}\big(\zeta^{*}\big)^{-\frac{1}{2}\sigma^{z}}\hat{\mathsf{V}}\bm{M}(\zeta^{*}\,|\,\eta_{N}^{*},\eta_{N-1}^{*},\dots,\eta_{1}^{*})\hat{\mathsf{V}}^{-1}\big(\zeta^{*}\big)^{+\frac{1}{2}\sigma^{z}}\sigma^{x},

(5.1)

where

\displaystyle\hat{\mathsf{V}}=\prod_{J=1}^{N}(\eta_{J}^{*})^{-\frac{1}{2}\sigma_{J}^{z}}.

Now suppose that the (non-ordered) set of inhomogeneities coincides with the complex conjugated set:

\displaystyle\big\{\eta_{J}^{*}\big\}_{J=1}^{N}=\big\{\eta_{J}\big\}_{J=1}^{N}.

(5.2)

In particular, this property holds for all the three cases (
4.8
),
(
4.9
) and (
4.10
) considered above.
Then there exists a similarity transformation such that

\displaystyle\bm{M}(\zeta\,|\,\eta_{N},\eta_{N-1},\dots,\eta_{1})=\hat{\mathsf{S}}^{-1}\bm{M}(\zeta\,|\,\eta_{N}^{*},\eta_{N-1}^{*},\dots,\eta_{1}^{*})\hat{\mathsf{S}},\qquad\hat{\mathsf{S}}\in{\rm End}(\mathscr{V}_{N}).

(5.3)

Combining the latter with equation (
5.1
), yields

\displaystyle[\bm{M}(\zeta)]^{\dagger}=\sigma^{x}(\zeta^{*})^{-\frac{1}{2}\sigma^{z}}\big[\hat{\mathsf{X}}\bm{M}(\zeta^{*})\hat{\mathsf{X}}^{-1}\big]\big(\zeta^{*}\big)^{+\frac{1}{2}\sigma^{z}}\sigma^{x}

(5.4)

with the Hermitian matrix
\hat{\mathsf{X}}
given by

\displaystyle\hat{\mathsf{X}}=\hat{\mathsf{X}}^{\dagger}=\hat{\mathsf{V}}\hat{\mathsf{S}}^{-1},\qquad\hat{\mathsf{X}}\in{\rm End}(\mathscr{V}_{N}).

(5.5)

Formula (
5.4
) implies that, in general, the transfer matrix is
not Hermitian w.r.t. the
{\dagger}
-conjugation. However, for an arbitrary

\hat{\mathsf{O}}\in{\rm End}({\mathscr{V}}_{N})
, one can
introduce the non-standard conjugation as

\displaystyle\hat{\mathsf{O}}^{\ddagger}=\hat{\mathsf{X}}^{-1}\hat{\mathsf{O}}^{\dagger}\hat{\mathsf{X}}.

(5.6)

Since the matrix
\hat{{\mathsf{X}}}
is Hermitian (
5.5
), it is guaranteed
that
\big(\hat{\mathsf{O}}^{\ddagger}\big)^{\ddagger}=\hat{\mathsf{O}}
so that (
5.6
)
indeed defines a conjugation.
Then equation (
5.4
) can be rewritten as the
{\ddagger}
-conjugation
condition for the operator valued entries of the monodromy matrix (
2.16
):

\displaystyle\big[{\hat{\mathsf{A}}}(\zeta)\big]^{\ddagger}={\hat{\mathsf{D}}}(\zeta^{*}),\qquad\big[{\hat{\mathsf{D}}}(\zeta)\big]^{\ddagger}={\hat{\mathsf{A}}}(\zeta^{*}),

\displaystyle\big[{\hat{\mathsf{B}}}(\zeta)\big]^{\ddagger}=\zeta^{*}{\hat{\mathsf{C}}}(\zeta^{*}),\qquad\big[{\hat{\mathsf{C}}}(\zeta)\big]^{\ddagger}=(\zeta^{*})^{-1}{\hat{\mathsf{B}}}(\zeta^{*}).

(5.7)

In turn, these relations imply

\displaystyle\big[\mathbb{T}(\zeta)\big]^{\ddagger}=\mathbb{T}(\zeta^{*}).

(5.8a)

In a similar way, one can show that

\displaystyle\big[\mathbb{A}_{\pm}(\zeta)\big]^{\ddagger}=\mathbb{A}_{\pm}(\zeta^{*}).

(5.8b)

As a matter of fact, there is a more general version of (
5.6
)
for which the condition similar to (
5.8
) holds.
Indeed consider the conjugation of the form

\displaystyle\hat{\mathsf{O}}^{\star}=\mathbb{Y}^{-1}\hat{\mathsf{O}}^{\ddagger}\mathbb{Y}=\mathbb{Y}^{-1}\hat{{\mathsf{X}}}^{-1}\hat{\mathsf{O}}^{\dagger}\hat{{\mathsf{X}}}\mathbb{Y}.

(5.9)

In order to fulfill the requirement
\big(\hat{\mathsf{O}}^{\star}\big)^{\star}=\hat{\mathsf{O}}
,
the operator
\mathbb{Y}
should be such that

\displaystyle\mathbb{Y}^{\ddagger}=\mathbb{Y}.

(5.10)

If, in addition,

\displaystyle\big[\mathbb{Y},\mathbb{A}_{\pm}(\zeta)\big]=0,

(5.11)

then it follows from (
5.8
) that

\displaystyle\big[\mathbb{T}(\zeta)\big]^{\star}=\mathbb{T}(\zeta^{*}),\qquad\big[\mathbb{A}_{\pm}(\zeta)\big]^{\star}=\mathbb{A}_{\pm}(\zeta^{*}).

(5.12)

The
{\ddagger}
-conjugation is a special case of the
\star
-conjugation with
\mathbb{Y}=\hat{\bf 1}
.

For a given
{\mathbb{Y}}\in{\rm End}({\mathscr{V}}_{N})
satisfying (
5.10
)
and (
5.11
) there is a unique
sesquilinear form
({\bm{\psi}}_{2},{\bm{\psi}}_{1})_{\star}

({\bm{\psi}}_{1,2}\in{\mathscr{V}}_{N})

that is compatible with the
\star

\displaystyle\big(\bm{\psi}_{2},\hat{\mathsf{O}}\bm{\psi}_{1}\big)_{\star}=\big(\hat{\mathsf{O}}^{\star}\bm{\psi}_{2},\bm{\psi}_{1}\big)_{\star}

and the overall normalization condition for the pseudovacuum
\bm{\Psi}_{0}\equiv|\,0\,\rangle
(
2.6

\displaystyle\big(\bm{\Psi}_{0},\bm{\Psi}_{0}\big)_{\star}=1.

The second equality in (
5.12
), together with the fact that the spectrum of the operators

\mathbb{A}_{\pm}(\zeta)
is expected to be non-degenerate for generic values of
{\tt k}
, implies that the Bethe states (

\displaystyle\big({\bm{\Psi}}^{\prime},{\bm{\Psi}}\big)_{\star}=0\qquad{\rm unless}\qquad{\bm{\Psi}}^{\prime}={\hat{\cal C}}{\hat{\cal P}}{\hat{\cal T}}{\bm{\Psi}}.

Let us define the “norm” for an arbitrary state

as

\displaystyle{\mathfrak{N}}_{Y}[{\bm{\psi}}]:=\big({\hat{\cal C}}{\hat{\cal P}}{\hat{\cal T}}{\bm{\psi}},{\bm{\psi}}\big)_{\star}.

For the Bethe state
corresponding to the set of Bethe roots

) and (

where

stands for the eigenvalue of the operator
{\mathbb{Y}}
on
the Bethe state
{\bm{\Psi}}
.

One can construct a variety of matrices

obeying equations (

) and (

In this case the eigenvalues of

For the three cases from the previous section one has:

6
Lattice translation symmetry

Translational invariance is a fundamental symmetry of a local
quantum field theory. Having in mind the study of the universality classes
for the inhomogeneous six-vertex model, it is natural to focus
on the lattice, where the number of columns
N
is divisible by
some integer
r
,

\displaystyle N=rL,

(6.1)

while the inhomogeneities satisfy the
r
-site periodicity conditions

\displaystyle\eta_{J+r}=\eta_{J}\qquad(J=1,2,\dots,N)

(6.2)

(here by definition we take
\eta_{J+N}\equiv\eta_{J}
).
With these restrictions, the commuting family contains
a set of operators that play a central rôle for the
description of the critical behaviour.
The aim of this section is to introduce these operators and discuss
their basic properties.

6.1
\bm{r}
-site translation operator

The one-site translation operator may be defined
without any restrictions imposed on the parameters of the model.
Taking into account the quasi-periodic boundary conditions with twist parameter

\omega=\mbox{e}^{{\rm i}\pi{\tt k}}
, its matrix elements are given by

\displaystyle\big(\hat{{\cal K}}\big)_{a_{N}a_{N-1}\cdots a_{1}}^{b_{N}b_{N-1}\cdots b_{1}}=\mbox{e}^{{\rm i}\pi{\tt k}a_{1}}\delta_{a_{1}}^{b_{N}}\delta_{a_{N}}^{b_{N-1}}\cdots\delta^{b_{1}}_{a_{2}}.

(6.3)

The action of the one-site translation on the local
spin operators reads as

\displaystyle\hat{\cal K}\sigma_{J}^{A}\hat{\cal K}^{-1}=\sigma^{A}_{J+1}\qquad(A=x,y,z;\,J=1,\dots,N-1),

\displaystyle\hat{\cal K}\sigma_{N}^{A}\hat{\cal K}^{-1}=\mbox{e}^{+{\rm i}\pi{\tt k}\sigma_{1}^{z}}\sigma^{A}_{1}\mbox{e}^{-{\rm i}\pi{\tt k}\sigma_{1}^{z}}.

In turn, the adjoint action of
{\cal K}
on the transfer matrix (
2.18
) results in
a cyclic permutation of the inhomogeneities:

\displaystyle\hat{\cal K}{\mathbb{T}}(\zeta\,|\,\eta_{N},\eta_{N-1},\dots,\eta_{1})\hat{\cal K}^{-1}={\mathbb{T}}(\zeta\,|\,\eta_{N-1},\eta_{N-2},\dots,\eta_{N}).

(6.4)

This relation shows that with the conditions (
6.1
) and (
6.2
) imposed,
the
r
-site translation operator belongs to the commuting family,

\displaystyle{\mathbb{K}}=\hat{\cal K}^{r}\colon\quad{\mathbb{K}}^{L}=\mbox{e}^{2\pi{\rm i}{\tt k}\mathbb{S}^{z}},\qquad[\mathbb{K},\mathbb{T}(\zeta)]=0.

In fact
\mathbb{K}
not only commutes, but it can be expressed in terms of the
transfer matrix. Assuming the normalization (
2.12
) for
\mathbb{T}(\zeta)

and taking into account (
2.13
), it’s straightforward to show

\displaystyle{\mathbb{K}}=(q-q^{-1})^{-N}\bigg[\prod_{1<m<\ell<r}\bigg(q^{2}+q^{-2}-\frac{\eta_{\ell}}{\eta_{m}}-\frac{\eta_{m}}{\eta_{\ell}}\bigg)^{-L}\bigg]\prod_{\ell=1}^{r}q^{\frac{N}{2}}\mathbb{T}(-q^{-1}\eta_{\ell}).

(6.5)

It follows that the eigenvalue of the
r
-site translation operator on the Bethe state (
2.19
)
is given by

\displaystyle\mathbb{K}\bm{\Psi}=K\bm{\Psi}\colon\quad K=\mbox{e}^{{\rm i}\pi r{\tt k}}q^{rS^{z}-\frac{rN}{2}}\prod_{\ell=1}^{r}\frac{A_{+}(-q^{+1}\eta_{\ell})}{A_{+}(-q^{-1}\eta_{\ell})},

where
A_{+}(\zeta)
is the same as in (
3.1
).

6.2
Quasi-shift operators

In
[
25
]
, which was devoted to the study of the

{\cal Z}_{2}
invariant model, the authors introduced the so-called quasi-shift operator.
It turns out to be a key player for the description of the scaling limit.
Similar operators may be defined for the general model provided the
restrictions (
6.1
) and (
6.2
) are imposed.
They are likewise expected to be important
for analyzing the critical behaviour.

The construction of the quasi-shift operators is based on the following observation.
When the restrictions (
6.2
) on the
\eta_{J}
are imposed, the cyclic permutation of
the inhomogeneities within each group

(\eta_{J+r},\eta_{J+r-1},\dots,\eta_{J+1})\mapsto(\eta_{J+1},\eta_{J+r},\dots,\eta_{J+2})

with
J=\ell({\rm mod}\,r)
is equivalent to an overall cyclic permutation of the ordered set

(\eta_{N},\eta_{N-1},\dots,\eta_{1})
. In view of equation (
4.18
), this implies that
there exists
r
inequivalent operators
\hat{\cal D}^{(\ell)}
, such that

\displaystyle\big(\hat{\cal D}^{(\ell)}\big)^{-1}{\mathbb{T}}(\zeta\,|\,\eta_{N},\eta_{N-1},\dots,\eta_{1})\hat{\cal D}^{(\ell)}={\mathbb{T}}(\zeta\,|\,\eta_{N-1},\eta_{N-2},\dots,\eta_{N}),\qquad\ell=1,2,\dots,r.

(6.6)

Explicitly, they are given by

\displaystyle\hat{\cal D}^{(\ell)}=\prod_{j=0}^{L-1}\bigg(\hat{{\mathcal{K}}}^{\ell+rj}\bigg[\overset{\displaystyle\curvearrowleft}{\prod_{m=1}^{r-1}}\check{\bm{R}}_{m+1,m}\big(\eta_{\ell}/\eta_{\ell+m}\big)\bigg]\hat{{\mathcal{K}}}^{-\ell-rj}\bigg),

(6.7)

where the outer product is unordered since the factors inside it
commute with each other. It is worth mentioning that the operator

\displaystyle\hat{\cal D}^{(r)}=\overset{\displaystyle\curvearrowleft}{\prod_{m=1}^{N-1}}\check{\bm{R}}_{m+1,m}\big(\eta_{r}/\eta_{m}\big)

plays a special rôle.
Though the adjoint action of
\hat{\cal D}^{(\ell)}
on the transfer matrix
results in a permutation of the inhomogeneities as in (
6.6
), a similar
formula does not hold for the entries
\hat{\mathsf{A}}
,

\hat{\mathsf{B}}
,
\hat{\mathsf{C}}
and
\hat{\mathsf{D}}
of the monodromy matrix.
However, it turns out that

\displaystyle\big(\hat{\cal D}^{(r)}\big)^{-1}{\bm{M}}(\zeta\,|\,\eta_{N},\eta_{N-1},\dots,\eta_{1})\hat{\cal D}^{(r)}={\bm{M}}(\zeta\,|\,\eta_{N-1},\eta_{N-2},\dots,\eta_{N}).

(6.8)

Combining equations (
6.4
) and (
6.6
) it is easy to see that the operators

\displaystyle\mathbb{K}^{(\ell)}=\hat{\cal D}^{(\ell)}\hat{\cal K},\qquad\ell=1,2,\dots,r,

(6.9)

belong to the commuting family,

\displaystyle\big[\mathbb{K}^{(\ell)},\mathbb{T}(\zeta)\big]=0.

Moreover, their product coincides with the
r
-site translation:

\displaystyle\prod_{\ell=1}^{r}\mathbb{K}^{(\ell)}=\mathbb{K}.

We will refer to
\mathbb{K}^{(\ell)}
as the quasi-shift operators.

6.3
Hamiltonians

In the case of the homogeneous six-vertex model the transfer matrix
commutes with the spin
\frac{1}{2}
Heisenberg
XXZ
Hamiltonian.
A similar important property holds true for the
model, where the parameters
\eta_{J}

satisfy the periodicity conditions (
6.2
) with any
r\geq 1
.
Namely the corresponding commuting family
contains spin chain Hamiltonians
that are given by a sum of
terms, each of which is built out of local spin operators from
r+1
consecutive sites of the lattice.
There are
r
such Hamiltonians and, in terms of the row-to-row transfer matrix, they are expressed as

\displaystyle\mathbb{H}^{(\ell)}=2{\rm i}\zeta\partial_{\zeta}\log\big(\mathbb{T}\big({-}q^{-1}\zeta\big)\big)\big|_{\zeta=\eta_{\ell}}-2{\rm i}\sum_{J=1}^{N}(1-q^{2}\eta_{J}/\eta_{\ell})^{-1},\qquad\ell=1,2,\dots,r.

A calculation shows that

\displaystyle\mathbb{H}^{(\ell)}=\sum_{m=0}^{L-1}\mathbb{K}^{m}\hat{\mathsf{H}}^{(\ell)}\mathbb{K}^{-m},

where
\hat{\mathsf{H}}^{(\ell)}\in{\rm End}\big(\mathscr{V}_{N}\big)
are built
from the
\sigma^{A}_{J}
with
J=\ell,\ell+1,\dots,\ell+r
.
Assuming that
L\geq 2
so that the sites
\ell
and
\ell+r
are not identified with one another,
the operators
\hat{\mathsf{H}}^{(\ell)}
are given by

\displaystyle\hat{\mathsf{H}}^{(\ell)}=\sum_{m=1}^{r}\hat{\mathsf{S}}_{m}^{({\ell})}\hat{\mathsf{J}}_{\ell+m,\ell+m-1}(\eta_{\ell}/\eta_{\ell+m-1})\big(\hat{\mathsf{S}}^{({\ell})}_{m}\big)^{-1}.

Here we use the notation

\displaystyle\hat{\!\mathsf{J}}_{n+1,n}(\zeta)=2{\rm i}\zeta\partial_{\zeta}\log(\check{\bm{R}}_{n+1,n}(\zeta))

and

\displaystyle\hat{\mathsf{S}}^{(\ell)}_{m}=\overset{\displaystyle\curvearrowleft}{\prod_{n=\ell+m}^{\ell+r-1}}\check{\bm{R}}_{n+1,n}({\eta_{\ell}}/{\eta_{n}}),\qquad\hat{\mathsf{S}}^{(\ell)}_{r}=\hat{\bf 1},

with
\check{\bm{R}}_{n+1,n}
as in (
4.19
).

6.4
Interplay with
{\cal CP}
,
{\cal T}
and Hermitian conjugation

In order to incorporate the
{\cal C}
,
{\cal P}
and

{\cal Z}_{\bm{r}}

The operators

It turns out that if the inhomogeneities are specified to be

\displaystyle\hat{\cal D}^{(\ell)}=\hat{{\cal K}}^{\ell}\hat{\cal D}\hat{\cal K}^{-\ell}

When the condition (

and

\displaystyle\hat{\cal D}^{-1}{\mathbb{T}}(\zeta)\hat{\cal D}={\mathbb{T}}\big(\mbox{e}^{+2\pi{\rm i}/r}\zeta\big),\qquad\hat{\cal D}^{-1}{\mathbb{A}}_{\pm}(\zeta)\hat{\cal D}={\mathbb{A}}_{\pm}\big(\mbox{e}^{+2\pi{\rm i}/r}\zeta\big),

\displaystyle\hat{\cal K}^{-1}{\mathbb{T}}(\zeta)\hat{\cal K}={\mathbb{T}}\big(\mbox{e}^{-2\pi{\rm i}/r}\zeta\big),\qquad\hat{\cal K}^{-1}{\mathbb{A}}_{\pm}(\zeta)\hat{{\cal K}}={\mathbb{A}}_{\pm}\big(\mbox{e}^{-2\pi{\rm i}/r}\zeta\big).

\displaystyle\hat{\cal D}^{-1}\mathbb{H}^{(\ell)}\hat{\cal D}=\mathbb{H}^{(\ell+1)},\qquad\hat{\cal D}^{-1}\mathbb{K}^{(\ell)}\hat{\cal D}=\mathbb{K}^{(\ell+1)},\qquad\ell=1,2,\dots,r-1,

\displaystyle\hat{\cal D}^{-1}\mathbb{H}^{(r)}\hat{\cal D}=\mathbb{H}^{(1)},\qquad\hat{\cal D}^{-1}\mathbb{K}^{(r)}\hat{\cal D}=\mathbb{K}^{(1)}.

r=2
, i.e.,

\displaystyle\hat{\cal D}=\prod_{m=1}^{N/2}\check{\bm{R}}_{2m,2m-1}(-1)\colon\quad\hat{\cal D}^{2}=1.

(8.5)

One can show that the adjoint action of
\hat{\cal D}
on the local spin operators is given by

\displaystyle\hat{\cal D}\sigma^{\pm}_{m}\hat{\cal D}=\frac{1}{\cos(\gamma)}\big(\sigma^{\pm}_{m+1}-{\rm i}\sin(\gamma)\sigma^{z}_{m+1}\sigma^{\pm}_{m}\big),

\displaystyle\hat{\cal D}\sigma^{z}_{m}\hat{\cal D}=\frac{1}{\cos^{2}(\gamma)}\big(\sigma^{z}_{m+1}-\sin^{2}(\gamma)\sigma^{z}_{m}+2{\rm i}\sin(\gamma)\big(\sigma^{+}_{m+1}\sigma^{-}_{m}+\sigma^{-}_{m+1}\sigma^{+}_{m}\big)\big)

for odd values of

\displaystyle\hat{\cal D}\sigma^{\pm}_{m}\hat{\cal D}=\frac{1}{\cos(\gamma)}\big(\sigma^{\pm}_{m-1}+{\rm i}\sin(\gamma)\sigma^{\pm}_{m}\sigma^{z}_{m-1}\big),

\displaystyle\hat{\cal D}\sigma^{z}_{m}\hat{\cal D}=\frac{1}{\cos^{2}(\gamma)}\big(\sigma^{z}_{m-1}-\sin^{2}(\gamma)\sigma^{z}_{m}-2{\rm i}\sin(\gamma)\big(\sigma^{+}_{m}\sigma^{-}_{m-1}+\sigma^{-}_{m}\sigma^{+}_{m-1}\big)\big)

for even

\displaystyle{\cal\hat{D}}\,|\,0\,\rangle=|\,0\,\rangle.

{\cal\hat{D}}
with the operators from the commuting family

7.3
) with
\mbox{e}^{2\pi{\rm i}/r}=-1

).
As for the Hamiltonians
{\mathbb{H}}^{(\pm)}

\displaystyle\hat{\cal D}{\mathbb{H}}^{(\pm)}\hat{\cal D}={\mathbb{H}}^{(\mp)},\qquad\hat{\cal D}{\mathbb{K}}^{(\pm)}\hat{\cal D}={\mathbb{K}}^{(\mp)},\qquad{\rm whereas}\qquad\hat{\cal D}{\mathbb{B}}\hat{\cal D}=\mathbb{B}^{-1}.

Hermitian structures.

Consider the Hermitian matrix
\hat{{\mathsf{X}}}
entering into the conjugation condition (
5.6
).
It is given by equation (

\eta_{J}={\rm i}(-1)^{J-1}

\displaystyle\hat{\mathsf{X}}=\bigg(\prod_{J=1}^{N}(\eta_{J})^{\frac{1}{2}\sigma^{z}_{J}}\bigg)\hat{\cal D}=\Sigma^{z}\hat{\cal D}\mbox{e}^{\frac{{\rm i}\pi}{2}({\mathbb{S}}^{z}-N/2)}.

Here
\Sigma^{z}

\displaystyle\Sigma^{z}=\sigma_{N}^{z}\otimes\sigma_{N-2}^{z}\otimes\cdots\otimes\sigma_{2}^{z}.

Note that the Hermiticity of

\displaystyle\Sigma^{z}{\cal\hat{D}}^{{\dagger}}\Sigma^{z}=\mbox{e}^{{{\rm i}\pi}({\mathbb{S}}^{z}-N/2)}{\cal\hat{D}},

Among others, this implies that the operators

\displaystyle{\mathbb{Y}}=\mbox{e}^{{{\rm i}\pi}({\mathbb{S}}^{z}-N/2)}{\mathbb{A}}_{+}^{(\infty)}.

\displaystyle\Sigma^{z}\big({\mathbb{A}}_{+}^{(\infty)}\big)^{\dagger}\Sigma^{z}={\cal\hat{D}}\big({\mathbb{A}}_{+}^{(\infty)}\big)^{\dagger}{\cal\hat{D}}=\mbox{e}^{{{\rm i}\pi}({\mathbb{S}}^{z}-N/2)}{\mathbb{A}}_{+}^{(\infty)}.

\displaystyle\big({\cal\hat{C}\hat{P}\hat{T}}{\bm{\Psi}},{\bm{\Psi}}\big)_{\star}={\mathfrak{K}}[{\bm{\Psi}}]\qquad\big({\mathbb{Y}}=\mbox{e}^{{{\rm i}\pi}({\mathbb{S}}^{z}-N/2)}{\mathbb{A}}_{+}^{(\infty)}\big)

\displaystyle{\mathbb{H}}^{\star}={\mathbb{H}},\qquad{\mathbb{K}}^{\star}={\mathbb{K}}^{-1},\qquad{\mathbb{B}}^{\star}={\mathbb{B}}.

9
Conclusion

The subject matter of this paper is the integrable inhomogeneous six-vertex model on the square lattice.
The discussion is focused on various algebraic properties of the model, which
are important for studying the scaling limit.

We summarized the results concerning the diagonalization problem for the transfer matrix.
Explicit formulae for the Baxter
Q
-operators were presented as
special transfer matrices associated with the infinite dimensional
representations of the
q
-oscillator algebra.

The
{\cal C}
,
{\cal P}

and
{\cal T}
conjugations were introduced, and we described the constraints
on the parameters of the model such that these are consistent
with the integrable structure.
The special features of the model possessing translational
invariance were discussed. Among these, it was pointed out that the commuting family contains
a set of quasi-shift operators
\mathbb{K}^{(\ell)}
as well as Hamiltonians

\mathbb{H}^{({\ell})}
. The latter are distinguished in that they are
given by a sum of “local” operators.
We also formulated the conditions for which the translationally invariant model
possesses an extra
{\cal Z}_{r}
global symmetry and described its basic properties.

Both
\mathbb{H}^{({\ell})}
and
\mathbb{K}^{(\ell)}

are expected to play a key rôle for the study of the critical behaviour.
The Hamiltonians are sparse matrices
(most of their elements are vanishing) and
there exist efficient algorithms for
finding the eigenvectors and eigenvalues belonging to the low energy part of the spectrum.
For a given eigenvector, it is straightforward to compute
the eigenvalue of the
Q
-operators and, in turn,
obtain the corresponding solution of the Bethe ansatz equations.
In our subsequent paper
[
9
]
this approach is used for analyzing the scaling limit
of the homogeneous and
{\cal Z}_{2}
invariant six-vertex models.
The explicit formulae collected in the last section form the starting point of that work.

For the case when both the anisotropy and twist parameters are unimodular,

|q|=|\omega|=1
, as well as when the set of inhomogeneities
\{\eta_{J}\}
coincides with the complex
conjugated set
\{\eta_{J}^{*}\}
,
a family of Hermitian structures were introduced, which are consistent
with the integrable one. Again, this analysis forms the preliminary set up
for the study of the scaling limit of the Hermitian structures performed in
[
9
]
.

Here we have only considered the square
lattice models. One could also define
solvable inhomogeneous six-vertex models on
arbitrary planar lattices formed by intersecting straight
lines
[
6
]
.
Some of these are related to
the quantizations of circle
patterns
[
12
]

arising in the context of a discrete counterpart of the
Riemann mapping theorem
[
15
]
.
It would be interesting to explore the
scaling limit of these constructions and their connection to CFT.
Finally note that more general lattice systems with an alternating set of
inhomogeneities,
which still belong to the integrability class of the six-vertex model,
were used in the construction of
discrete integrable versions of the sine-Gordon model
[
14
,
18
,
7
]
.
