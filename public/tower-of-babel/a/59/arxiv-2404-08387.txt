The asymptotic distribution of the scaled remainder for pseudo golden ratio expansions of a continuous random variable
I. Herbst, J. Møller, A.M. Svane

Abstract
Let X = ∑ k = 1 ∞ X k ​ β − k X=\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}X_{k}\beta^{-k} be the (greedy) base- β \beta expansion of a continuous random variable X X on the unit interval where β \beta is the positive solution to β n = 1 + β + ⋯ + β n − 1 \beta^{n}=1+\beta+\cdots+\beta^{n-1} for an integer n ≥ 2 n\geq 2 (i.e., β \beta is a generalization of the golden mean corresponding to n = 2 n=2 ). We study the asymptotic distribution and convergence rate of the scaled remainder ∑ k = 1 ∞ X m + k ​ β − k \sum_{k=1}^{\infty}X_{m+k}\beta^{-k} when m m tends to infinity.



1
Introduction and main results

In this paper we consider an absolutely continuous random variable
X
with probability density function (PDF)
f
concentrated on
[0,1)
. For
\beta>1
we let
X=\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}X_{k}\beta^{-k}
be its base
\beta
-expansion with digits
X_{k}
defined recursively by
X_{1}=\lfloor\beta X\rfloor
and
X_{k}=\lfloor\beta^{k}(X-\sum_{i=1}^{k-1}X_{i}\beta^{-i})\rfloor
for
k\geq 2
where
\lfloor x\rfloor
denotes the integer part of
x
. We are interested in the distribution of the scaled remainder
\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}X_{k+m}\beta^{-k}
when the first
m
digits are removed. In the case where
\beta
is an integer, the limiting distribution of the scaled remainder and the speed at which it converges to this limit has been discussed in
[
2
]
. In this paper, we consider the situation where
\beta
is
the positive solution to

\beta^{n}=1+\beta+\cdots+\beta^{n-1}

(1.1)

for a given integer
n\geq 2
. The golden mean
corresponds to the case
n=2
in (
1.1
). The study of
\beta
-expansions when
\beta
is the golden mean is a classical topic in metric number theory
[
7
]
. For
n\geq 2
the positive solution
\beta=\beta_{n}
to (
1.1
) is called a pseudo golden mean and is an example of a Parry number
[
5
]
.

Let us introduce some notation before stating the main result of this paper.
Given
x\in[0,1)
and
\beta>1
let

t(x)=\beta x-\lfloor\beta x\rfloor.

Define
t^{0}(x)=x
and for
m\in\mathbb{N}
, where
\mathbb{N}
denotes the natural numbers
1,2,...
, let
t^{m}
be the composition of
t
with itself
m
times. Define the digits in base
\beta
as
x_{k}\coloneqq\lfloor\beta t^{k-1}(x)\rfloor
. It is well-known that we have the
\beta
-expansion

x=\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}x_{k}\beta^{-k}

(1.2)

and since
\beta
-expansions are not unique this is sometimes referred to as the greedy
\beta
-expansion, see e.g.
[
1
]
. Note that

0\leq t(x)<1
and
0\leq\beta^{-m}t^{m}(x)=x-\sum_{k=1}^{m}x_{k}\beta^{-k}<\beta^{-m}
.

As shown in Appendix B, there is a unique positive solution to (
1.1
) and this number is strictly between 1 and 2. Henceforth, let
\beta
denote this number. It follows that
x_{k}\in\{0,1\}
for
k=1,2,...
.
Not all sequences
(x_{1},x_{2},...)\in\{0,1\}^{\mathbb{N}}
give rise to a
\beta
-expansion of a number in
[0,1)
, for example
\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}\beta^{-k}=(\beta-1)^{-1}>1
. Moreover, due to the relation (
1.1
), expansions of the form (
1.2
) are not necessarily unique.
It follows from Theorem 3, page 406 of
[
5
]
that the sequences that arise as
x_{k}\coloneqq\lfloor\beta t^{k-1}(x)\rfloor
for some
x\in[0,1)
are exactly those having no more than
n-1

x_{k}
’s in succession equal to
1
and not ending with the sequence
(\ldots,0,1,\ldots,1,,0,1,\ldots,1,\ldots)
where each sequence of 1’s has
n-1
elements.
We refer to these conditions as the standard restrictions on the
x_{k}
’s.
A self-contained proof is given in Appendix A.

The main object of our interest is the scaled remainder

t^{m}(X)=\beta^{m}\sum_{k=m+1}^{\infty}X_{k}\beta^{-k}=\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}X_{k+m}\beta^{-k}

(1.3)

for
m\in\mathbb{N}
. Denote by
\mathcal{B}
the Borel subsets of
[0,1)
. Then
t
induces a transformation
T
on PDF’s concentrated on
[0,1)
with
Tf
characterized by
P(t(X)\in B)=\int_{B}Tf(x)\,\mathrm{d}x
for all
B\in\mathcal{B}
where
X
is a random variable having
f
as PDF. Then
f_{m}\coloneqq T^{m}f
for
m\in\mathbb{N}
is the PDF of
t^{m}(X)
, i.e. with the definition
P_{m}(B)\coloneqq P(t^{m}(X)\in B)
we have
P_{m}(B)=\int_{B}f_{m}(x)\,\mathrm{d}x
. In
[
6
]
, Rényi showed the existence of a unique PDF
f_{\beta}
such that
Tf_{\beta}=f_{\beta}
. A formula for
f_{\beta}
(up to a constant factor) for arbitrary
\beta>1
is given in
[
5
]
.
One may define
f_{\beta}
by

f_{\beta}(x)=\left(\sum_{j=1}^{n}F_{j}(x)\right)D(\beta)^{-1},\quad F_{j}=(\beta^{j}-\beta^{j-1}-\beta^{j-2}-\cdots-\beta)\chi_{[\beta^{-1}+\cdots+\beta^{-(j-1)},\beta^{-1}+\cdots+\beta^{-j})}

(1.4)

for
0\leq x<1
where
\chi_{I}
is the indicator function for the interval
I
and

D(\beta)
is a constant defined so that
\int_{[0,1)}f_{\beta}(x)\,\mathrm{d}x=1
(an expression for
D(\beta)
can be found in (
4.18
) below). In (
5.21
) below we give an alternative formula for
f_{\beta}
which is equivalent the one in
[
5
]
(as one may realize by noting that in that paper
t^{j}(1)=\beta^{-1}+\cdots+\beta^{n-j}
for
j\in\mathbb{N}
).

Define
P_{\beta}(B)\coloneqq\int_{B}f_{\beta}(x)\,\mathrm{d}x
for
B\in\mathcal{B}
.
Our main theorem identifies
P_{\beta}
as the asymptotic distribution of
t^{m}(X)
when
m\to\infty
. To state the theorem, we recall that the total variation distance between two probability distributions
P_{1}
and
P_{2}
is defined as

d_{TV}(P_{1},P_{2})=\sup_{B\in\mathcal{B}}|P_{1}(B)-P_{2}(B)|

Moreover, we use the notation
\|h-f_{\beta}\|_{\infty}=\sup_{x\in|0,1)}|h(x)-f_{\beta}(x)|
.
Finally, we let
\lambda_{2}
be the root of the polynomial
p(x)=x^{n}-(1+x+\cdots+x^{n-1})
with the second largest absolute value among all
n
roots. From Appendix B we know
|\lambda_{2}|<1
.

Theorem 1.1
.

Suppose
X
is absolutely continuous with PDF
f
concentrated on
[0,1)
.
We have

\lim_{m\to\infty}d_{TV}(P_{m},P_{\beta})=0.

(1.5)

In addition, if
f
is a Lipschitz function with
|f(x)-f(y)|\leq L_{f}|x-y|
for all
x,y\in[0,1)
,
then for any integer
m>2n

\|f_{m}-f_{\beta}\|_{\infty}=O(|\beta^{-1}\lambda_{2}|^{m_{2}})+O(\beta^{-m_{1}})L_{f}

(1.6)

whenever
m_{1},m_{2}\in\mathbb{N}
with
m_{2}>2n-1
,
m_{1}\geq n-1
and
m_{1}+m_{2}=m
.

Remark 1.2
.

Here,
m_{1}
and
m_{2}
should be chosen so that (
1.6
) is optimized once additional information is known about
|\lambda_{2}|
. This and the values of
\beta
and
\|\lambda_{2}|
as well as the convergence rates of
\|f_{m}-f_{\beta}\|_{\infty}
in specific cases of
f
are investigated in Section
2
.
Instead of assuming
f
is Lipschitz, we could assume that
f
is only Hölder continuous with an appropriate change in the exponential rate of convergence in (
1.6
) depending on the Hölder exponent.

We remark that

1>|\lambda_{2}|\geq\beta^{-1/(n-1)}

(1.7)

and so
|\lambda_{2}|
approaches 1 as
n
grows.
For
n=2
or
3
we have the equality
|\lambda_{2}|=\beta^{-1/(n-1)}
(see Appendix B). Thus under the Lipschitz continuity assumption in Theorem
1.1
, for
n=2

\|f_{m}-f_{\beta}\|_{\infty}=O(\beta^{-2m/3}),

and for
n=3

\|f_{m}-f_{\beta}\|_{\infty}=O(\beta^{-3m/5}),

while for
n>3
we have no upper bound on
|\lambda_{2}|
other than
|\lambda_{2}|<1
which yields

\|f_{m}-f_{\beta}\|_{\infty}=O(\beta^{-m/2}).

(1.8)

The paper is organized as follows. Section
2
discusses various numerical results in relation to Theorem
1.1
.
The remainder of the paper
concerns the proof of Theorem
1.1
: We first determine the PDF
f_{m}
in
Section
3
. Some technical considerations necessary for the proof are given in Section

. (We mention that a very different proof of the limit (

) will be given in an upcoming paper of Cornean, Herbst, and Marcelli by analyzing the operator

3
Formula for the PDF of
t^{m}(X)

As we saw above

t^{m}(X)=\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}X_{m+k}\beta^{-k}.

To determine the distribution of
t^{m}(X)
, we divide
[0,1)
into intervals.
For
m\in\mathbb{N}
define

\Omega_{m}=\{(j_{1},j_{2},\cdots,j_{m})\,|\,j_{k}\in\{0,1\}\mbox{ and no more than $n-1$ $j_{k}$'s in succession equal 1}\}.

If
J=(j_{1},\ldots,j_{m})\in\Omega_{m}
we define

L_{J,m}=\sum_{k=1}^{m}j_{k}\beta^{-k}.

Let
r(J)
be the number of 1’s in succession at the end of
J
.
Then
0\leq r(J)\leq\min(m,n-1).
Suppose
x=\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}\beta^{-k}x_{k}
with
x_{k}=j_{k}
for
k=1,...,m
and the
x_{k}
’s following the standard restrictions. Clearly
L_{J,m}\leq x
. We show in Appendix A that

\displaystyle x<L_{J,m}+\beta^{-(m+1)}+\beta^{-(m+2)}+\cdots+\beta^{-(m+n-r(J))}.

Setting

K(J)=\beta^{-1}+\cdots+\beta^{-(n-r(J))},

this leads us to define the interval

I_{J}=I_{J,m}=[L_{J,m},L_{J,m}+\beta^{-m}K(J)).

(3.9)

Proposition 3.1
.

The following properties are satisfied.

1)

If
J,J^{\prime}\in\Omega_{m}
then
I_{J}\cap I_{J^{\prime}}=\emptyset
unless
J=J^{\prime}
.

2)

\cup_{J\in\Omega_{m}}I_{J}=[0,1)
.

3)

Any
x\in[0,1)
has a unique expansion
x=\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}x_{k}\beta^{-k}
with the
x_{k}
’s obeying the standard restrictions.

4)

For
J\in\Omega_{m}
,
I_{J}
consists of those
x=\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}x_{k}\beta^{-k}
with the
x_{k}
’s obeying the standard restrictions and
J=(x_{1},\ldots,x_{m})
.

Proof.

See Appendix A. ∎

Recalling (
1.3
)
we have
P(t^{m}(X)\leq x)=\sum_{J\in\Omega_{m}}P(t^{m}(X)\leq x,X\in I_{J,m})
, where
t^{m}(X)\leq x
is the same as
\sum_{k=m+1}^{\infty}X_{k}\beta^{-k}\leq\beta^{-m}x
, so

P(t^{m}(X)\leq x,X\in I_{J,m})=P(L_{J,m}\leq X\leq L_{J,m}+\beta^{-m}x,X\in I_{J,m}).

(3.10)

From (
3.9
) and (
3.10
) we have for every
x\in[0,1)
that

\displaystyle P(t^{m}(X)\leq x)=\sum_{J\in\Omega_{m}}[

\displaystyle\chi_{[0,K(J))}(x)P(L_{J,m}\leq X\leq L_{J,m}+\beta^{-m}x)

\displaystyle+\chi_{[K(J),1]}(x)P(L_{J,m}\leq X\leq L_{J,m}+\beta^{-m}K(J))].

It follows that
t^{m}(X)
has a PDF given by

T^{m}f(x)=f_{m}(x)=\beta^{-m}\sum_{J\in\Omega_{m}}\chi_{[0,K(J))}(x)f(L_{J,m}+\beta^{-m}x).

(3.11)

4
On the cardinality of a certain set

To proceed with the proof of Theorem
1.1
, we need to know how many intervals
J\in\Omega_{m}
assume a given value of
r(J)
. For
m\in\mathbb{N}
and
r\in\{0,...,\min(m,n-1)\}
let

\Omega_{m,r}=\{J\in\Omega_{m}\,|\,r(J)=r\}.

Denote the cardinality of
\Omega_{m,r}
by
N_{r}(m)
. This is determined in Lemma
4.2
below using the following considerations.

We have

N_{0}(m+1)=\sum_{r=0}^{\min(m,n-1)}N_{r}(m)

and for
1\leq r\leq\min(m+1,n-1)

N_{r}(m+1)=N_{r-1}(m).

For
1\leq m\leq n-1
we have
N_{0}(m)=2^{m-1}
since
j_{m}=0
and
j_{k}
can be arbitrary for
k<m
, and for
2\leq m\leq n-1
,
N_{1}(m)=2^{m-2}
since
j_{m}=1
,
j_{m-1}=0
and the remaining
j_{k}
are arbitrary. Similarly if
r+1\leq m\leq n-1
,
N_{r}(m)=2^{m-(r+1)}
. Finally
N_{m}(m)=1
.

Define a matrix
M
such that the equation

\begin{bmatrix}N_{0}(m+1)\\
\vdots\\
N_{n-1}(m+1)\\
\end{bmatrix}=\begin{bmatrix}1&1&1&\cdots 1&1\\
1&0&0&\cdots 0&0\\
0&1&0&\cdots 0&0\\
\vdots\\
0&0&0&\cdots 1&0\\
\end{bmatrix}\times\begin{bmatrix}N_{0}(m)\\
\vdots\\
N_{n-1}(m)\\
\end{bmatrix}=M\times\begin{bmatrix}N_{0}(m)\\
\vdots\\
N_{n-1}(m)\\
\end{bmatrix}.

(4.12)

holds whenever
m\geq n-1
. Thus

\begin{bmatrix}N_{0}(m+1)\\
\vdots\\
N_{n-1}(m+1)\\
\end{bmatrix}=M^{m+1-(n-1)}\times\begin{bmatrix}N_{0}(n-1)\\
\vdots\\
N_{n-1}(n-1)\\
\end{bmatrix}=M^{m+1-(n-1)}\times\begin{bmatrix}2^{n-2}\\
\vdots\\
2^{n-2-r}\\
\vdots\\
1\\
\end{bmatrix}.

(4.13)

It is not hard to show using expansion by minors of the first column of
xI-M
that

\det(xI-M)=(x-1)x^{n-1}-(x^{n-2}+x^{n-3}+\cdots+x+1)=x^{n}-(1+x+\cdots+x^{n-1}).

(4.14)

Thus
M
has a unique positive eigenvalue
\beta
. Using (
1.1
), an unnormalized eigenvector corresponding to
\beta
is seen to be

u=\begin{bmatrix}\beta^{n-1}\\
\beta^{n-2}\\
\vdots\\
1\\
\end{bmatrix}

(4.15)

and an unnormalized eigenvector with eigenvalue
\beta
for the transposed matrix
M^{*}
is

v=\begin{bmatrix}1\\
\beta-1\\
\beta^{2}-\beta-1\\
\vdots\\
\beta^{n-1}-(\beta^{n-2}+\cdots+1)\end{bmatrix}.

(4.16)

The components of (
4.16
) all have the same sign. In fact, for
j=1,...,n
,

\beta^{j-1}-(\beta^{j-2}+\beta^{j-3}+\cdots+1)=\beta^{-1}+\beta^{-2}+\cdots+\beta^{-(n-(j-1))}.

(4.17)

To see this, subtract the right hand side from the left, factor out
\beta^{j-1}
and use (
1.1
).

Let
\langle v,u\rangle
be the usual inner product and define

D(\beta)=\beta^{-(n-1)}\langle v,u\rangle

(4.18)

where
D(\beta)
is the same constant as in (
1.4
), which can be verified by direct computation. Moreover, we calculate

\langle v,u\rangle=n\beta^{n-1}-\sum_{m=0}^{n-2}(m+1)\beta^{m}=\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}x}(x^{n}-x^{n-1}-x^{n-2}-\cdots-1)\bigg|_{x=\beta}=\prod_{j=2}^{n}(\beta-\lambda_{j})

where the
\lambda_{j}
’s are the roots of
x^{n}-(x^{n-1}+\cdots+1)
. We order the
\lambda_{j}
’s so that
\beta=\lambda_{1}>|\lambda_{2}|\geq\cdots\geq|\lambda_{n}|
. By (
4.14
) these are the eigenvalues of
M
.

Let

w=\begin{bmatrix}2^{n-2}\\
\vdots\\
2^{n-2-r}\\
\vdots\\
2^{n-n}\\
1\\
\end{bmatrix}.

(4.19)

Lemma 4.1
.

We have

\langle v,w\rangle=\beta^{n-1}.

(4.20)

Proof.

The lemma is true for
\beta
replaced by any
x\in{\mathbb{R}}
not necessarily satisfying (
1.1
). Thus, for the purpose of this proof, we replace
\beta
by
x
in (
4.16
). First note that the lemma is true for
n=2
. Assuming it is true for
n
, we have for
n+1

\displaystyle\langle v,w\rangle=

\displaystyle\,2[2^{n-2}+(x-1)2^{n-3}+\cdots+(x^{n-2}-(x^{n-3}+\cdots+1))]+x^{n-1}-

\displaystyle\,(x^{n-2}+\cdots+1)+x^{n}-(x^{n-1}+\cdots+1)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\,2[x^{n-1}-(x^{n-1}-(x^{n-2}+\cdots+1))]+x^{n-1}-

\displaystyle\,(x^{n-2}+\cdots+1)+x^{n}-(x^{n-1}+\cdots+1)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\,x^{n}.

∎

We can now determine
N_{r}(m)
from (
4.13
).

Lemma 4.2
.

We have for
m\in\{n,n+1,...\}
and
r\in\{0,...,\min(m,n-1)\}
that

N_{r}(m)=\beta^{m+n-1-r}\langle v,w\rangle^{-1}+O(|\lambda_{2}|^{m}).

Proof.

We use the fact that the eigenvalues of
M
are simple, see
[
4
]
. Thus
M
has a basis of eigenvectors,
Mu_{j}=\lambda_{j}u_{j},1\leq j\leq n,u_{1}=u
, and similarly
M^{*}
has a basis of eigenvectors,
M^{*}v_{j}=\bar{\lambda_{j}}v_{j},1\leq j\leq n,v_{1}=v
.
We have
\langle v_{j},u_{k}\rangle=0
if
j\neq k
, since
(\lambda_{k}-\lambda_{j})\langle v_{j},u_{k}\rangle=\langle v_{j},Mu_{k}\rangle-\langle M^{*}v_{j},u_{k}\rangle=0
. Thus
if
w=\sum_{j=1}^{n}\alpha_{j}u_{j}
we have
\langle v_{j},w\rangle=\alpha_{j}\langle v_{j},u_{j}\rangle
or

w=\sum_{j=1}^{n}\langle v_{j},w\rangle\,\langle v_{j},u_{j}\rangle^{-1}u_{j}.

It follows that

N_{r}(m)=\sum_{j=1}^{n}(M^{m-(n-1)}\langle v_{j},w\rangle\,\langle v_{j},u_{j}\rangle^{-1}u_{j})_{r}=\beta^{m-(n-1)}\langle v,w\rangle\,\langle v,u\rangle^{-1}(u)_{r}+O(|\lambda_{2}|^{m}).

Using
\langle v,w\rangle=\beta^{n-1}
and
(u)_{r}=\beta^{n-1-r}
, we find the result.
∎

Proof of main theorem

We need the following alternative expression for
f_{\beta}
.

Lemma 5.1
.

With
f_{\beta}
as in (
1.4
), we have for
0\leq x<1

f_{\beta}(x)=\sum_{s=0}^{n-1}\chi_{[0,\beta^{-1}+\cdots+\beta^{-(n-s)})}(x)\beta^{-s}D(\beta)^{-1}.

(5.21)

Appendix A: The beta expansion

In this appendix we prove Proposition
3.1
. We use the notation from the main text. We let
S=(1,\ldots,1,0,1,\ldots,1,0,\ldots)
denote the sequence having alternatingly
n-1
1’s in succession and a 0.

Lemma 5.3
.

For any sequence of
x_{k}
’s satisfying the standard restrictions,
\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}x_{k}\beta^{-k}
is a number in
[0,1)
.
If for
x\in[0,1)
we let
x_{k}=\lfloor\beta t^{k-1}(x)\rfloor
, then
x=\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}x_{k}\beta^{-k}
is the unique
\beta
-expansion of
x
satisfying the standard restrictions.

Proof.

To show the first statement, note that the largest value of a
\beta
-expansion without
n
1’s in succession is
\sum_{i=1}^{n-1}\sum_{l=0}^{\infty}\beta^{-i-nl}=\sum_{l=0}^{\infty}\beta^{-nl}(1-\beta^{-n})=1
corresponding to the sequence
(x_{1},x_{2},\ldots)=S
. The
\beta
-expansion of any other sequence of
x_{k}
’s without
n
1’s in succession yields a strictly smaller number (indeed, for any such sequence one obtains a strictly larger
\beta
-expansion by swapping
(0,1)
to
(1,0)
repeatedly moving all 1’s as far to the left as possible and (if there are finitely many 1’s) adding extra 1’s until
S
is reached).

Setting
x_{k}=\lfloor\beta t^{k-1}(x)\rfloor
we have
|x-\sum_{k=1}^{m}x_{k}\beta^{-k}|=\beta^{-m}t^{m}(x)<\beta^{-m}
which goes to zero for
m\to\infty
showing that
x=\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}x_{k}\beta^{-k}
. This expansion must satisfy the standard restrictions. Indeed, if
(x_{k+1},\ldots,x_{k+n})=(1,\ldots,1)
for some
k\geq 0
, then
t^{k}(x)\geq\sum_{i=1}^{n}x_{k+i}\beta^{-i}=1
, which is a contradiction. Similarly, if
(x_{k+1},\ldots)=S
, then
t^{k}(x)=\sum_{i=1}^{n-1}\sum_{l=0}^{\infty}\beta^{-i-nl}=\sum_{l=0}^{\infty}\beta^{-nl}(1-\beta^{-n})=1
which is again a contradiction.

To show uniqueness, suppose
\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}x_{k}\beta^{-k}=\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}y_{k}\beta^{-k}
and let
m
be minimal with
x_{m}\neq y_{m}
, say
x_{m}=0
and
y_{m}=1
. Then
\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}x_{m+k}\beta^{-k}=1+\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}y_{k+m}\beta^{-k}
. By the above considerations, the only way this can hold is if
(x_{m+1},x_{m+2},\ldots)=S
and
(y_{m+1},y_{m+2},\ldots)=(0,0,\ldots)
showing that only the
y_{k}
’s satisfy the standard restrictions.
∎

Lemma 5.4
.

If
x,y\in[0,1)
have
\beta
-expansions
x=\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}x_{k}\beta^{-k}
and

Let

Since

, this is equivalent to
x_{m}=\lfloor\beta t^{m-1}(x)\rfloor=0

y_{m}=\lfloor\beta t^{m-1}(y)\rfloor=1

∎

If

Appendix B: Some properties of the roots of (
4.14
)

Lemma 5.6
.

Fix
n\geq 2
and consider the polynomial
p(x)=x^{n}-(1+\cdots+x^{(n-1)})
.

1)

There is a unique positive
root
\beta
of
p
. This satisfies
\beta\in(1,2)
.

2)

If
n
is even there is one negative root. All other roots are non-real. If
n
is odd all roots other than
\beta
are non-real.

3)

If we label the roots
\lambda_{j}
with
|\lambda_{1}|>|\lambda_{2}|\geq\cdots\geq|\lambda_{n}|
, with
\lambda_{1}=\beta
, then
|\lambda_{j}|<1
for
j\geq 2
and
|\lambda_{2}|\geq\beta^{-1/(n-1)}
for all
n
. If
n
is even then
\lambda_{n}<0
and in fact
|\lambda_{n}|<|\lambda_{n-1}|
. We also have
|\lambda_{j}|\geq 3^{-1/n}
for
j=1,...,n
. The inequality
|\lambda_{2}|\geq\beta^{-1/(n-1)}
is actually an equality for
n=2
and
3
.

4)

All roots are simple.

5)

\beta
is increasing in
n
. In fact
2-\beta=\beta^{-n}
so that
\beta
approaches
2
exponentially fast as
n
increases.

Proof.

Much of this is well known, see
[
3
,
4
]
.
We note first that
p(x)
is the determinant of the matrix
xI-M
, cf. (
4.12
).

M
has non-negative entries and
M^{n}
has all positive entries and therefore it is well known
[
3
]
that there is a unique positive root which is strictly larger than the absolute value of all other roots. All roots are simple and
|\lambda_{j}|<1
for
j>1
(see
[
4
]
).
The equation
x^{n}=1+x+\cdots+x^{n-1}
can be rewritten
1=x^{-1}+x^{-2}+\cdots+x^{-n}
. From this equation it is easily seen that the positive solution,
\beta>1
, increases with
n
. We have
1+x+\cdots+x^{n-1}=(1-x^{n})/(1-x)
so that we can rewrite the first equation as

x^{n}=(x^{n}-1)/(x-1)

or

x^{n+1}+1=2x^{n}

which gives

2-x=x^{-n}.

This shows
\beta<2
and since
\beta
increases with
n
,
2-\beta
must decrease exponentially in
n
.
If we factor
p(x)=\prod_{j=1}^{n}(x-\lambda_{j})
we see that
\prod_{j=1}^{n}\lambda_{j}=(-1)^{n+1}
which implies
\beta|\lambda_{2}|^{n-1}\geq 1
or
|\lambda_{2}|\geq\beta^{-1/(n-1)}
. From the equation
x^{n+1}+1=2x^{n}
we find
2|x|^{n}\geq 1-|x|^{n+1}
or
|x|^{n}\geq(2+|x|)^{-1}
. Applying this to
x=\lambda_{j},j\geq 2
, using
|\lambda_{j}|<1
, we find
|\lambda_{j}|>3^{-1/n}
. In particular all roots other than
\beta
approach the unit circle as
n
increases.

Suppose
x
is a negative root. If
n
is odd we have
-2|x|^{n}=1+|x|^{n+1}
which is impossible, so there are no negative roots if
n
is odd. If
n
is even we have
2|x|^{n}=1-|x|^{n+1}
. If
\lambda
is another root then
2|\lambda|^{n}=|1+\lambda^{n+1}|\geq 1-|\lambda|^{n+1}
. Subtracting we find
2(|\lambda|^{n}-|x|^{n})\geq|x|^{n+1}-|\lambda|^{n+1}
and therefore
|\lambda|\geq|x|
. If
|\lambda|=|x|
then
2|x|^{n}=1-|x|^{n+1}
implies
2|\lambda|^{n}=1-|\lambda|^{n+1}=|1+\lambda^{n+1}|
. This implies
\lambda^{n+1}<0
. Since
2\lambda^{n}=1+\lambda^{n+1}
we must have
\lambda<0
. Finally this means
\lambda=x
. Thus for
n
even there is at most one negative root and all other roots are strictly larger in absolute value. Moreover, we know there is one positive root and as before
\prod_{j=1}^{n}\lambda_{j}=(-1)^{n+1}
. Since all non-real roots come in complex conjugate pairs this shows that if
n
is even there is precisely one negative root.

If
n=2
or
3
we have
\beta\lambda_{2}=-1
and
\beta|\lambda_{2}|^{2}=1
respectively, so in both cases we have
|\lambda_{2}|=\beta^{-1/(n-1)}.

∎
