Socially Fair Reinforcement Learning
Debmalya Mandal, Jiarui Gan

Abstract
We consider the problem of episodic reinforcement learning where there are multiple stakeholders with different reward functions. Our goal is to output a policy that is socially fair with respect to different reward functions. Prior works have proposed different objectives that a fair policy must optimize including minimum welfare, and generalized Gini welfare. We first take an axiomatic view of the problem, and propose four axioms that any such fair objective must satisfy. We show that the Nash social welfare is the unique objective that uniquely satisfies all four objectives, whereas prior objectives fail to satisfy all four axioms. We then consider the learning version of the problem where the underlying model i.e. Markov decision process is unknown. We consider the problem of minimizing regret with respect to the fair policies maximizing three different fair objectives – minimum welfare, generalized Gini welfare, and Nash social welfare. Based on optimistic planning, we propose a generic learning algorithm and derive its regret bound with respect to the three different policies. For the objective of Nash social welfare, we also derive a lower bound in regret that grows exponentially with n n , is the number of agents. Finally, we show that for the objective of minimum welfare, one can improve regret by a factor of O ⁡ ( H ) O(H) for a weaker notion of regret.

Introduction

In recent years, reinforcement learning has been immensely successful in various domains including game playing
[
Sil+17
]
, and robotics
[
And+20
]
. These breakthroughs have opened up new avenues for applying reinforcement learning in real-world decision making systems like healthcare
[
Got+19
]
, and finance
[
Li+21
]
. However, such human-facing systems often include multiple stakeholders with different preferences, and the classical goal of maximization of rewards is no longer sufficient. A major design challenge is the selection of right objective that provides certain fairness guarantees across different agents.

Our first example comes from the use of reinforcement learning for vaccine distribution
[
Mei+21
]
. In this context, the vulnerable population is in immediate need, and their utilities should be prioritized. Similarly, multi-armed bandit algorithms have been deployed for improving access to maternal and child healthcare
[
Mat+22
]
. In this domain, researchers have found that maximizing the minimum reward provides better fairness guarantees.

These examples suggest that in the context of multi-objective sequential decision-making, we should be careful about selecting the right measure. But what should be the right measure? Besides maximizing minimum welfare discussed above, researchers have proposed selecting a policy at the pareto frontier
[
DCR18
]
, or a policy maximizing generalized Gini social welfare
[
SWZ20
]
. In this paper, we focus on the problem of selecting a measure that ensures fairness in multi-objective sequential decision-making. For the static setting (i.e. single decision), this is a well-studied problem in economics
[
Mou04
]
, and also in computational social choice
[
Bra+16
]
. Motivated by the success of axiomatic framework in these fields, we aim to lay out a reasonable set of axioms for selecting a fairness measure in dynamic multi-objective sequential decision making systems.

Even though a set of axioms might uniquely characterize a fairness measure, the optimal policy according to this fairness measure is unknown. This is because the underlying MDP is unknown, and we must interact with the environment to learn such an optimal policy. Moreover, given the use of different fairness measures in different contexts, can we come up with a generic reinforcement learning algorithm that learns different fair optimal policies? This is the second question we study in this paper, and in particular, we consider the problem of minimizing regret with respect to different fair optimal policies. Note that, this question is challenging since most fairness measures are non-linear in value functions and dynamic programming
[
Ber12
]
based learning algorithms cannot be applied.

1.1
Contributions

At a high level, we propose a set of axioms for selecting fairness measure in multi-objective sequential decision-making systems. Three natural fairness measures are then analyzed against the proposed axioms.
When the underlying MDP is unknown, we propose a generic learning algorithm for minimizing regret with respect to different fairness measures.
In more detail, we make the following contributions.

•

We consider the episodic reinforcement learning with multiple agents, and propose a set of four axioms that any fairness measure should satisfy. We show that these axioms uniquely characterize Nash social welfare (
\mathrm{NW}
), whereas prior measures like minimum welfare (
\mathrm{MW}
) or generalized Gini welfare (
\mathrm{GGW}
) violates some of them.

•

When the underlying model is unknown, we propose a generic learning algorithm for minimizing regret with respect to the optimal fair according to a fairness measure. For a setting with
n
agents,
S
states,
A
actions, and horizon length
H
, we instantiate this algorithm for different measures, and obtain regret upper bounds of
\widetilde{O}(nH^{n+1}S\sqrt{AT})
for the objective of Nash social welfare, and
\widetilde{O}(H^{2}S\sqrt{AT})
for both the minimum welfare, and generalized Gini welfare.

•

For the objective of Nash social welfare, we derive a lower bound in regret that scales exponentially with
n
, whereas for the other two measures lower bound immediately follows from lower bound in regret for single-agent setting. Finally, for the objective of minimum welfare, we show that one can improve regret by a factor of
O(H)
for a weaker notion of regret.

Preliminaries

We consider the episodic reinforcement learning problem with multiple reward functions. Before describing the general setting, we begin with the single-agent episodie reinforcement learning. We are given an MDP
\mathcal{M}=(\mathcal{S},\mathcal{A},P,r,\rho)
with
S
states and
A
actions i.e.
\left|\mathcal{S}\right|=S
and
\left|\mathcal{A}\right|=A
.The initial state
s_{1}
is drawn from a distribution
\rho
. We will write
s_{h}
(resp.
a_{h}
) to denote the state visited (resp. action taken) at time-step
h
. For
h=1,\ldots,H-1
the next state
s_{h+1}\sim P(\cdot|s_{h},a_{h})
. The goal is to maximize the expected sum of rewards over the
H
steps i.e.
\mathbb{E}\left[\sum_{h=1}^{H}r(s_{h},a_{h})\mid s_{1}\sim\rho\right]
.

In this paper, we consider a setting with
n
different reward functions
\mathbf{r}=\{r_{i}\}_{i\in[n]}
corresponding to
n
different agents. We will assume
r_{i}(s,a)\in[0,1]
for all
i
and state,action pair
s,a
. Given a policy
\pi
, we can define the value function corresponding to the
i
-th reward function as follows.

V^{\pi}(\rho;r_{i})=\mathbb{E}_{\pi}\left[\sum_{h=1}^{H}r_{i}(s_{h},a_{h})\mid s_{1}\sim\rho\right]

(1)

Often the starting state distribution
\rho
will be clear from the context and we will simply write
V^{\pi}(r_{i})
instead of
V^{\pi}(\rho;r_{i})
.
Note that we are considering an episodic reinforcement learning setup, so the policy
\pi
need not be a stationary policy. We will write
\pi=(\pi_{1},\ldots,\pi_{H})
where
\pi_{h}
is the (non-stationary) policy used at time-step
h
. We will write
\Pi
to denote the set of such non-stationary policies.

Any policy
\pi
affects different reward functions differently (e.g. through value functions), and we want to build a measure to evaluate how fair the policy
\pi
is with respect to
n
different reward functions.
In particular, a fairness measure
W
maps a policy
\pi
and a set of
n
reward functions to a real number i.e.
W:\Pi\times\mathbb{R}^{S\times A\times n}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
.
Therefore,
W\left(\pi;\{r_{i}\}_{i\in[n]}\right)
provides a way to evaluate the fairness of a policy
\pi
and we want to maximize the measure to find the optimal fair policy. The most basic measure is the utilitarian measure which is the sum of values across the
n
agents. However, this measure violates basic axioms and we will consider the following three measures of fairness.

Minimum Welfare.

We maximize the minimum value function across the
n
agents.

\textstyle\pi^{\star}_{\mathrm{MW}}\in\mathrm{argmax}_{\pi}\min_{i\in[n]}V^{\pi}\left(r_{i}\right).

Generalized Gini Social Welfare (GGW).

This notion of fairness generalizes max-min fairness and has been considered previously in the literature on multi-objective Markov decision processes
[
SWZ20
,
Zim+21
]
. We are given a vector of weights
w\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
so that
w_{i}\geq 0
for each
i
,
\sum_{i}w_{i}=1
, and
w_{1}\geq w_{2}\geq\ldots\geq w_{n}
.
Given a policy
\pi
, let
i_{1},\ldots,i_{n}
be an ordering of the agents so that
V^{\pi}(r_{i_{1}})\leq V^{\pi}(r_{i_{2}})\leq\ldots\leq V^{\pi}(r_{i_{n}})
. We then maximize the following objective:

\textstyle\pi^{\star}_{\mathrm{GGW}}\in\mathrm{argmax}_{\pi}\sum_{j=1}^{n}w_{i}V^{\pi}(r_{i_{j}}).

When
w_{1}=1
, the above objective coincides with minimum welfare.

Nash Social Welfare.

We maximize the product of the value functions of the
n
agents, which is known as the Nash social welfare.

\textstyle\pi^{\star}_{\mathrm{NW}}\in\mathrm{argmax}_{\pi}\prod_{i=1}^{n}V^{\pi}(r_{i}).

When the full MDP is known, a policy that maximizes each of the measures can be computed by linear programming. The details can be found in the appendix.

Prior work on (multi-objective) fair reinforcement learning has mostly focused on measures minimum welfare and generalized Gini welfare. In this work, we want to emphasize the Nash social welfare as an alternative measure of fairness because of its attractive axiomatic properties.

2.1
Axioms

We view the problem of solving a fair reinforcement learning problem as maximization of a fairness measure
W
. Therefore, we propose four axioms that any such fairness measure
W
should satisfy. These axioms are inspired by classical axioms in economics, particularly social choice theory
[
Sen18
]
. For our setting, we build on the axiomatic framework developed by
[
KN79
]
.
To define the axioms we will need some additional notations.

•

Given a policy
\pi
we will write
q^{\pi}
to denote its state-action occupancy measure, i.e.,

q^{\pi}_{h}(s,a)=\Pr_{\pi}\left(s_{h}=s,a_{h}=a\right).

(2)

•

Given an occupancy measure
q
we will write
\pi^{q}
to denote the corresponding policy, i.e.,

\pi^{q}_{h}(s,a)=\begin{cases}\frac{q_{h}(s,a)}{\sum_{b}q_{h}(s,b)}&\text{if }\sum_{b}q_{h}(s,b)>0\\
\frac{1}{A}&\text{otherwise}\end{cases}

It is known that the occupancy measure of the policy
\pi^{q}
is
q
.

•

Given two occupancy measures
q_{1}
and
q_{2}
, we will write
\pi^{\alpha q_{1}+(1-\alpha)q_{2}}
to denote the policy corresponding to the convex combination of the occupancy measures
q_{1}
and
q_{2}
. In particular, first we define the occupancy measure
q=\alpha q_{1}+(1-\alpha)q_{2}
and then take the policy
\pi^{q}
.

Axiom 1

(
Pareto Optimality).

If
V^{\pi}(r_{i})\geq V^{\widetilde{\pi}}(r_{i})
for all
i\in[n]
and
V^{\pi}(r_{j})>V^{\widetilde{\pi}}(r_{j})
for some
j
, then it must be that

W\left(\pi;\mathbf{r}\right)>W\left(\widetilde{\pi};\mathbf{r}\right).

Namely, if policy
\pi
pareto-improves policy
\widetilde{\pi}
, then
W
must assign higher value to a policy
\pi
.

Axiom 2

(
Independence of Irrelevant Alternatives with Neutrality).

Suppose that for all
i\in[n]
,

\frac{V^{\pi_{1}}(r_{i})}{V^{\pi_{2}}(r_{i})}=\frac{V^{\widetilde{\pi}_{1}}(\widetilde{r}_{i})}{V^{\widetilde{\pi}_{2}}(\widetilde{r}_{i})}.

Then
W\left(\pi_{1};\mathbf{r}\right)\geq W\left(\pi_{2};\mathbf{r}\right)
if and only if
W\left(\widetilde{\pi}_{1};\widetilde{\mathbf{r}}\right)\geq W\left(\widetilde{\pi}_{2};\widetilde{\mathbf{r}}\right)
.

Namely, if
\pi_{1}
to
\pi_{2}
is the same as
\widetilde{\pi}_{1}
to
\widetilde{\pi}_{2}
to each agent in terms of value function, then
W(\pi_{1})
to
W(\pi_{2})
is the same as
W(\widetilde{\pi}_{1})
to
W(\widetilde{\pi}_{2})
.

Axiom 3

(
Anonymity).

For any permutation
\sigma:[n]\rightarrow[n]
of the agents and policy
\pi
, it must be that

W\left(\pi;\mathbf{r}\right)=W\left(\pi;\{r_{\sigma(i)}\}_{i\in[n]}\right).

Namely,
W
is independent of the indices of the agents.

Axiom 4

(
Continuity).

Suppose
W(\pi_{1};\mathbf{r})\geq W(\pi_{2};\mathbf{r})\geq W(\pi_{3};\mathbf{r})
and let
q_{j}=q^{\pi_{j}}
for
j\in\left\{1,2,3\right\}
. Then there exists
\alpha\in[0,1]
such that

W(\pi_{2};\mathbf{r})=W\left(\pi^{\alpha q_{1}+(1-\alpha)q_{3}};\mathbf{r}\right).

Namely, there is an intermediate policy between
\pi_{1}
and
\pi_{3}
that attains the same value as
\pi_{2}
under
W
. Note that, we don’t take direct convex combination of policies, rather we take convex combination in the occupancy measure. This is because the value function in an MDP is a non-linear function of policy, and there might not be a policy of the form
\alpha\pi_{1}+(1-\alpha)\pi_{3}
achieving the same value as
\pi_{2}
.

3
Axiomatic Analysis

We investigate which of the above axioms are satisfied by different fairness measures.
First, we show that even though the axioms appear basic, not all of them are satisfied by the fairness measures considered previously in the literature. In fact, we can show that minimum welfare violates
PO and IIAN, whereas the measure generalized Gini welfare (
\mathrm{GGW}
) violates IIAN under any choice of weight vector
w
.
On the other hand, we show that
\mathrm{NW}
satisfies all four axioms. The main difference with
\mathrm{GGW}
, and
\mathrm{MW}
is the satisfaction of axiom IIA. The proofs of these results are rather tedious, and they are provided in the appendix. Next state our first main result and show that up to a transformation by a monotonically increasing function,
\mathrm{NW}
is the unique fairness measure that satisfies all four axioms.

Theorem 5
.

4

for any policy
\pi

\mathbf{r}

\phi:\mathbb{R}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
is some monotonically increasing function.

The proof of Theorem

Regret.

We define regret with respect to a generic fair objective
\mathbb{F}\in\left\{\mathrm{NW},\mathrm{GGW},\mathrm{MW}\right\}
. For a policy
\pi
we will write
\mathbb{F}(\pi)
to denote its value according to the objective
\mathbb{F}
e.g.
\mathrm{NW}(\pi)=\prod_{i=1}^{n}V^{\pi}(\rho;r_{i})
. Let
\pi^{\star}_{\mathbb{F}}
be the policy that maximizes the objective
\mathbb{F}
i.e.

\pi^{\star}_{\mathbb{F}}\in\mathrm{argmax}_{\pi}\mathbb{F}(\pi).

Then we measure the regret of a learning algorithm
(\pi_{1},\ldots,\pi_{T})
with respect to this optimal fair policy.

\textrm{Reg}_{\mathbb{F}}(T)=\sum_{t=1}^{T}\left(\mathbb{F}(\pi^{\star}_{\mathbb{F}})-\mathbb{F}(\pi_{t})\right)

(4)

Algorithm.

Algorithm
1
presents our learning algorithm for a generic objective
\mathbb{F}
. The algorithm is based on the principle of optimistic planning, introduced by
[
AJO08
]
. The learner interacts with the environment over
T

H
. At the start of episode

, the learner first computes the the optimistic model
\widetilde{P}_{t}
and the optimistic policy
\widetilde{\pi}_{t}
(
eq.

3
). Then the learner plays policy

for
H

.

The most important step of algorithm

3

we can solve optimistic planning in time

. This implies that as long as the number of agents
n
is constant, we can also solve the optimistic planning of
\mathrm{GGW}
efficiently.

The main idea is to consider an extended MDP where the state space is the same but the action at any state

is all tuples of the form
(a,\widetilde{P}_{s,a})
where
\widetilde{P}_{s,a}
is a probability distribution over next states so that
\left\lVert\widetilde{P}_{s,a}-\widehat{P}(s,a,\cdot)\right\rVert_{1}\leq\epsilon_{t}(s,a)
. We then show how to compute the optimal policy according to the different objectives in the extended MDP. In particular, we set up an optimization problem with variables

which denotes the probability a policy takes action
(a,\widetilde{P}_{s,a})
at state
s
at time-step
h
. The number of such variables is infinite. However, for the objectives
\mathrm{MW}
and
\mathrm{NW}
, the number of constraints is bounded by

\mathrm{GGW}
, the primal problem has

variables, and solving this dual problem requires time
n^{O(n)}\cdot\textrm{poly}(S,A,H)
. We believe that it might be possible to get a PTAS for the optimistic planning with objective
\mathrm{GGW}
but we leave this question for the future. The details of solving the optimistic planning steps are provided in the appendix. We now turn to proving the regret guarantees for algorithm (

Lower Bound on Regret

If all the

\textrm{Reg}_{\mathrm{MW}}(T)

We now consider the regret in learning for the Nash welfare objective.
Note that the upper bound in regret scales exponentially with

Theorem 10
.

Suppose
T\geq\Omega(\log H)
and
H\geq\Omega(\log S)
. Then
for any policy
\pi
, there exists a MDP with
S
states, and
A
actions so that
\mathbb{E}[\textrm{Reg}_{\mathrm{NW}}(\pi;T)]\geq Cn\left(\frac{H}{2}\right)^{n}\sqrt{SAT}
for some universal constant
C>0
.

Here we briefly highlight the main ideas of the proof.
We use the same instance used in the lower bound construction of average reward MDP
[
LS20
,
AJO08
]
, where there is a collection of
O(SA)
MDPs. Each MDP has
S-2
states arranged in the form of an
A
-ary tree, and two other states – good state
s_{g}
(reward
1
) and bad state
s_{b}
(reward
0
). Any action from the leaves of this tree transition uniformly at random to the remaining two states are good (
s_{g}
) and bad (
s_{b}
). However, on model
M_{\ell,a}
we define

\displaystyle\textstyle P(s_{g}|\ell,a)={1}/{2}+\Delta,\ P(s_{b}|\ell,a)={1}/{2}-\Delta.

Now observe that on Model
M_{\ell,a}
the optimal policy can navigate to leaf
\ell
and take action
a
. Moreover, the transition probabilities are designed in such a way that once either the good or the bad state is reached, it takes at least
\Theta(H)
steps to leave that state. This means the optimal policy achieves Nash welfare of at least
(1/2+\Delta)^{n}H^{n}
per episode. On the other hand, if the learning policy does not take action
a
at leaf node
\ell
, it achieves a Nash welfare of at most
(1/2)^{n}H^{n}
that episode. Therefore, the regret in these episodes is at least
\left((1/2+\Delta)^{n}-(1/2)^{n}\right)H^{n}\geq n(H/2)^{n}
. Then the rest of the proof shows that one can choose
\Delta
small enough so that there exists a model under which the number of such misses by the learning algorithm is at least
O(T)
.

4.2
Improved Bound for Minimum Welfare

We now consider a weaker notion of regret for the fairness measure
\mathrm{MW}
, minimum social welfare.

\textrm{Reg}_{\mathrm{MW}}^{W}(T)=\sum_{t=1}^{T}\mathrm{MW}(\pi^{\star}_{\mathrm{MW}};\mathbf{r})-\min_{i}\sum_{t=1}^{T}V^{\pi_{t}}(r_{i})

(5)

The difference with the regret definition (
4

T
episodes is upper bounded by the minimum of the total welfare across
T
episodes. This implies that
\textrm{Reg}^{W}_{\mathrm{MW}}(T)\leq\textrm{Reg}_{\mathrm{MW}}(T)
, and we show that it is possible to improve the bound for
\textrm{Reg}^{W}_{\mathrm{MW}}(T)
.

Before deriving a regret bound with respect to the new definition, we first discuss when the new regret
eq.

5
is more appropriate. Consider a setting where all the
n
agents interact with the centralized learner for
T
episodes and cares about her total reward over the
T
episodes. In such a setting, the total utility received by agent

\sum_{t=1}^{T}V^{\pi_{t}}(r_{i})

\min_{i}\sum_{t=1}^{T}V^{\pi_{t}}(r_{i})

\textstyle\max_{\pi\in\Pi}\min_{i}\sum_{t=1}^{T}V^{\pi}(r_{i})=T\max_{\pi\in\Pi}\min_{i}V^{\pi}(r_{i}).

The difference between these two quantities is precisely the regret in

\displaystyle\begin{split}&\max_{v\in\mathbb{R},\ q\in\mathcal{Q}(\rho,P)}\ v\\

Discussion and Future Work

Multi-Agent Setting:

Our setting can be extended to multi-agent setting where each agent plays her own action. In particular, suppose the state space is
\mathcal{S}
but the action space is
\mathcal{A}^{n}=\left\{\bm{a}=(a_{1},\ldots,a_{n}):a_{i}\in\mathcal{A}\ \forall i\right\}
. Then we can show that the fair policies according to the objective (
\mathrm{MW},\mathrm{GGW}
, and
\mathrm{NW}
) can be computed in time polynomial in
S,H
, and
A^{n}
. Indeed our current approach is to set up a convex optimization problem where the variables are
q_{h}(s,a)
, the probability of visiting state
s
at time-step
h
and taking action
a
. For the multi-agent setting the set of variables become
q_{h}(s,\bm{a})
for each
h,s,\bm{a}\in\mathcal{A}^{n}
, and solving the corresponding optimization problem takes time exponential in
n
. Our learning algorithm (
1
) also generalizes for the multi-agent setting if one maintains an estimate of
P(s,\bm{a},s^{\prime})
for each
s,s^{\prime}
and action profile
\bm{a}
. However, the regret now scales exponentially with
n
(e.g.
\textrm{Reg}_{\mathrm{NW}}=O\left(nH^{n+1}SA^{n/2}\sqrt{T}\right)
) without any assumption on the reward functions of the agents.

Conclusion

In this paper, we proposed a set of axioms for selecting fairness measure in multi-agent sequential decision making systems. We analyzed three different fairness measures (
\mathrm{NW},\mathrm{MW}
, and
\mathrm{GGW}
) against the axioms.
When the underlying MDP is unknown, we proposed a generic learning algorithm for minimizing regret with respect to different fair optimal policies.
There are many interesting directions for future work. First,
it would be interesting to extend our framework to consider RL with general function approximation. Our axioms should generalize easily, but the computation of fair optimal policies might be challenging. Second,
it would be great to bridge the gap between the lower and upper bounds in regret (table
1
). For single-agent RL, value iteration based methods achieve minimax regret bounds. However, fairness measures are non-linear, and
we might need to devise different learning algorithms. Finally, it would also be interesting to consider a setting where agents have partial information about the underlying MDP
[
DCR18
]
.

We first recall the linear programming formulation of reinforcement learning. Consider an MDP

\max_{\pi}V^{\pi}(\rho;r_{i})

\displaystyle\min_{\{V_{h}\}_{h\in[H]}}

\displaystyle V_{h}(s)\geq r(s,a)+\sum_{s^{\prime}}P(s,a,s^{\prime})V_{h+1}(s^{\prime})\ \forall h<H

We will use the dual formulation of the above linear program.

\displaystyle\begin{split}\max_{q}&\sum_{h=1}^{H}\sum_{s,a}q_{h}(s,a)r(s,a)\\
\textrm{s.t.}&\sum_{a}q_{1}(s,a)=\rho(s)\ \forall s\\
&\sum_{a}q_{h+1}(s,a)=\sum_{s^{\prime},a}q_{h}(s^{\prime},a)P(s^{\prime},a,s)\\
&\qquad\qquad\ \forall s,\ \forall h\in[H-1]\\
&q\geq 0\end{split}

(7)

should be interpreted as the probability that the policy visits state

at time-step

and takes action
a
. We will refer to the variables

Once we solve the LP

\displaystyle\pi_{h}(a|s)=\left\{\begin{array}[]{cc}\frac{q_{h}(s,a)}{\sum_{b}q_{h}(s,b)}&\textrm{ if }\sum_{b}q_{h}(s,b)>0\\
\frac{1}{A}&\textrm{o.w.}\end{array}\right.

Generalized Gini Social Welfare

(GGW): This notion of fairness generalizes max-min fairness and has been considered previously in the literature on multi-objective Markov decision processes
[
SWZ20
,
Zim+21
]
. We are given a vector of weights
w\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
so that
w_{i}\geq 0
for each
i

\sum_{i}w_{i}=1
, and
w_{1}\geq w_{2}\geq\ldots\geq w_{n}
.
Given a policy
\pi
, let
i_{1},\ldots,i_{n}
be an ordering of the
n
agents so that
V^{\pi}(r_{i_{1}})\leq V^{\pi}(r_{i_{2}})\leq\ldots\leq V^{\pi}(r_{i_{n}})
. Then we maximize the following objective

\pi^{\star}_{\mathrm{GGW}}\in\mathrm{argmax}_{\pi}\sum_{j=1}^{n}w_{j}V^{\pi}(r_{i_{j}})

The optimal GGW policy can also be computed through a linear program.

\displaystyle\begin{split}\max_{q,t}\ &t\\
\textrm{s.t.}&\sum_{i}w_{i}\sum_{h=1}^{H}\sum_{s,a}q_{h}(s,a)r_{\sigma(i)}(s,a)\geq t\ \forall\sigma\in\mathcal{S}_{n}\\
&q\in\mathcal{Q}(\rho,P)\end{split}

(13)

Even though the linear program above has exponentially many constraints, one can easily solve the LP using ellipsoid method. In order to apply the ellipsoid method we just need to show that there exists an efficient separation oracle. Given a candidate solution
q,t
the bellman flow constraints i.e. the last three constraints can be checked efficiently. Then for each agent
i
we compute
V_{i}(q)=\sum_{h=1}^{H}\sum_{s,a}q_{h}(s,a)r_{i}(s,a)
. Let
i_{1},i_{2},\ldots,i_{n}
be an ordering of the agents so that
V_{i_{1}}(q)\geq V_{i_{2}}(q)\geq\ldots\geq V_{i_{n}}(q)
. Then we check if
\sum_{j}w_{j}V_{i_{j}}(q)\geq t
or not. If this constraint is violated then we have a violating permutation
\sigma
where
\sigma(j)=i_{j}
. On the other hand, if this constraint is satisfied then all the other constraints are satisfied since for any other permutation
\sigma
we have
\sum_{j}w_{j}V_{\sigma(j)}(q)\geq\sum_{j}w_{j}V_{i_{j}}(q)
. This is because the weights are arranged in a non-increasing order.

\nabla\mathrm{NW}(q^{\star})^{\top}(q-q^{\star})\leq 0\quad\forall q\in\mathcal{Q}(\rho,P)

(14)

The derivative of the Nash welfare objective with respect to
q_{h}(s,a)
is the following.

\displaystyle\frac{\partial\mathrm{NW}(q)}{\partial q_{h}(s,a)}

\displaystyle=\sum_{i}\prod_{j\neq i}\left(\sum_{h=1}^{H}\sum_{s,a}q_{h}(s,a)r_{i}(s,a)\right)r_{i}(s,a)

\displaystyle=\mathrm{NW}(q)\sum_{i}\frac{r_{i}(s,a)}{V_{i}(q)}

Then equation (
14
) gives us the following result.

\displaystyle\mathrm{NW}(q^{\star})\sum_{i}\sum_{h,s,a}\frac{r_{i}(s,a)}{V_{i}(q^{\star})}\left(q_{h}(s,a)-q_{h}^{\star}(s,a)\right)\leq 0

\displaystyle\Rightarrow

\displaystyle\mathrm{NW}(q^{\star})\sum_{i}\frac{V_{i}(q)-V_{i}(q^{\star})}{V_{i}(q^{\star})}\leq 0

\displaystyle\Rightarrow

\displaystyle\sum_{i}\frac{V_{i}(q)}{V_{i}(q^{\star})}\leq n

This immediately implies that
\max_{i}\frac{V_{i}(q)}{V_{i}(q^{\star})}\leq n
.

Case 2.

\mathrm{NW}(q^{\star})=0
:
This implies that for every policy
\pi
,
V^{\pi}(r_{i})=0
for some
i
.
Hence, the minimum value of every policy
\pi
is zero and the MM social welfare is zero, contradicting the assumption of the theorem.

Upper Bound

We now construct an instance for the upper bound on the value functions under
\pi^{\star}_{\mathrm{NW}}
. Given any
\delta>0
we construct the following instance.
Consider the following example with a single-state MDP and two actions
a
and
b
available.
The rewards are

\displaystyle r_{1}(s,a)

\displaystyle=\delta^{n},\quad r_{i}(s,a)=1\text{ for all }i\neq 1,

\displaystyle\text{and}\quad r_{i}(s,b)

\displaystyle=\delta\text{ for all }i.

Clearly,
\pi^{b}
, which selects action
b
with probability
1
maximizes the minimum value, whereby
V^{\pi^{b}}(r_{i})=\delta
for all
i\in[n]
.
To find out
\pi_{\mathrm{NW}}^{\star}
, consider an arbitrary policy which plays action
a
with probability
x
, and we optimize
x
with respect to the Nash welfare.
Consider
\mathrm{NW}
as a function of
x

\frac{\partial\log\mathrm{NW}(x)}{\partial x}=(n-1)\log(x+(1-x)\delta)+\log\left(x\delta^{n}+(1-x)\delta\right),

of which the zero is given by

\frac{(1-\delta)(n-1)}{x+(1-x)\delta}=\frac{1-\delta^{n-1}}{x\delta^{n-1}+(1-x)}.

Rearranging the terms we get

Hence,

\frac{V^{\pi_{\mathrm{NW}}^{\star}}(r_{1})}{V^{\pi_{\mathrm{MW}}^{\star}}(r_{1})}=\frac{\frac{n-1}{n}\delta^{n}+\frac{1}{n}\delta+o(1)}{\delta}=\frac{1}{n}+o(1).

(20)

Since

But the vector of value functions under policy

Appendix E
Learning Algorithm

The learning algorithm first relabels the states so that each state appears only once in an episode of length
H
. This can be done by increasing the state space by a factor of
H
. In particular, if state
s
appears at time step
h
, we will call that state
s_{h}
.

E.1
Optimistic Planning

We aim to solve the following optimization problem.

\displaystyle\widetilde{P}_{t}\in\mathrm{argmax}_{P\in C_{t}(\widehat{P})}\max_{\pi}\mathbb{F}(\pi;P)

(22)

where
C_{t}(\widehat{P})
is the set of plausible transition functions at time
t
i.e.

C_{t}(\widehat{P})=\left\{P:\forall s\left\lVert P(s,a,\cdot)-\widehat{P}(s,a,\cdot)\right\rVert_{1}\leq\varepsilon_{t}(s,a)\right\}

for
\varepsilon_{t}(s,a)=\sqrt{\frac{4S\log(SAt/\delta)}{\max\left\{1,N_{t}(s,a)\right\}}}
.

We will consider an
extended MDP

\widetilde{M}
with state space
S
. For each state
s
the set of available actions is given as

\mathcal{A}_{s}=\left\{(a,P_{s,a}):\left\lVert P_{s,a}-\widehat{P}(s,a,\cdot)\right\rVert_{1}\leq\varepsilon_{t}(s,a)\right\}

Moreover, taking an action
(a,\widetilde{P}_{s,a})
at state
s
leads to transitioning to the next state
s^{\prime}
with probability
\widetilde{P}(s^{\prime}\mid s,a)
. The reward for agent
i
is the same as in the original MDP i.e.
r_{i}(s;a,\widetilde{P}_{s,a})=r_{i}(s,a)
. First, note that for the three objectives
\mathbb{F}\in\left\{\mathrm{NW},\mathrm{MW},\mathrm{GGW}\right\}
the value of the objective in (
22
) is the same as the value of the optimal fair policy in the extended MDP i.e.

\max_{P\in C_{t}(\widehat{P})}\max_{\pi}\mathbb{F}(\pi;P)=\max_{\pi}\mathbb{F}(\pi;\widetilde{M})

In order to see why this is the case, given any model
\widetilde{P}\in C_{t}(\widehat{P})
and a policy
{\pi}
we can consider a policy
\widetilde{\pi}
for the extended MDP such that

\widetilde{\pi}(a,P_{s,a}\mid s)=\left\{\begin{array}[]{cc}\pi(a\mid s)&\textrm{ if }P_{s,a}=\widetilde{P}(s,a,\cdot)\\
0&\textrm{o.w.}\end{array}\right.

Notice that from the relabeling of the state space, each state appears at most once in an episode of length
H
and therefore a state
s
is always associated with a unique action
a
and hence the choice of
P_{s,a}
in the above definition is well-defined. Now from the definition of extended MDP we have
V_{i}(\widetilde{\pi};\widetilde{M})=V_{i}(\pi;\widetilde{P})
for each agent
i
. Then for any function
\mathbb{F}\in\left\{\mathrm{NW},\mathrm{MW},\mathrm{GGW}\right\}
the objective in the extended MDP under policy
\widetilde{\pi}
is the same as the objective in model
\widetilde{P}
and policy
\pi
.

On the other hand, suppose we are given a policy
\pi^{\prime}
that is optimal for the extended MDP. Without loss of generality, we can assume that
\pi^{\prime}
is deterministic and for any state
s
and action
a
we can choose the model
P_{s,a}
so that
\pi^{\prime}(a,P_{s,a}\mid s)=1
. Note that there is no ambiguity in the choice of
P_{s,a}
since each state appears only once in an episode of length
H
. We now show how to compute the optimal policy in the extended MDP.

E.1.1
Minimum Welfare

Let
q_{h}(s;a,\widetilde{P}_{s,a})
be the probability that the policy visits state
s
at time-step
h
and takes action
(a,\widetilde{P}_{s,a})
. The optimal policy can be computed through the following linear program.

\displaystyle\begin{split}\max_{q,t}\ &t\\
\textrm{s.t.}\ &\sum_{h}\sum_{s,a}\sum_{\widetilde{P}_{s,a}}q_{h}(s;a,\widetilde{P}_{s,a})r_{i}(s,a)\geq t\ \forall i\\
&\sum_{a}\sum_{\widetilde{P}_{s,a}}q_{1}(s;a,\widetilde{P}_{s,a})=\rho(s)\ \forall s\\
&\sum_{a,\widetilde{P}_{s,a}}q_{h+1}(s;a,\widetilde{P}_{s,a})=\sum_{s^{\prime},a,\widetilde{P}_{s^{\prime},a}}q_{h}(s^{\prime};a,\widetilde{P}_{s^{\prime},a})\widetilde{P}_{s^{\prime},a}(s)\\
&\qquad\ \forall s,h\geq 1\\
&q_{h}(s;a,\widetilde{P}_{s,a})\geq 0\end{split}

(23)

Note that this linear program has infinitely many variables but only
O(n+HS)
constraints. Therefore, we consider its dual formulation.

\displaystyle\min_{u,V}\

\displaystyle\sum_{s}\rho(s)V_{1}(s)

s.t.

\displaystyle V_{h}(s)\geq\sum_{i}u_{i}r_{i}(s,a)+\sum_{s^{\prime}}\widetilde{P}_{s,a}(s^{\prime})V_{h+1}(s^{\prime})

\displaystyle\qquad\ \forall s,a,\widetilde{P}_{s,a},h<H

(24)

\displaystyle V_{H}(s)\geq\sum_{i}u_{i}r_{i}(s,a)\ \forall s,a

\displaystyle\sum_{i}u_{i}\geq 1

\displaystyle u_{i}\geq 0\ \forall i

This optimization problem has
n+HS
variables but infinite number of constraints. We now show that it is possible to efficiently solve for a separation oracle so that one can apply the ellipsoid method to solve this optimization problem. Given variables
u,V
, the last three constraints can be checked easily. For checking the last constraint
eq.

24
consider the following optimization problem for each
s,a
and
h
.

\displaystyle\max_{\widetilde{P}_{s,a}}

\displaystyle\ \sum_{i}u_{i}r_{i}(s,a)+\sum_{s^{\prime}}\widetilde{P}_{s,a}(s^{\prime})V_{h+1}(s^{\prime})

s.t

\displaystyle\ \left\lVert\widetilde{P}_{s,a}-\widehat{P}(s,a,\cdot)\right\rVert_{1}\leq\varepsilon_{t}(s,a)

This optimization problem is a concave optimization problem over a convex set. If the objective value is larger than
V_{h}(s)
then the corresponding solution (i.e.
\widetilde{P}_{s,a}
) provides with a violating constraint. If the objective is less than
V_{h}(s)
for all
s,h,a
then the variables
u,V
are feasible.

E.1.2
Generalized Gini Welfare

Let
q_{h}(s;a,\widetilde{P}_{s,a})
be the probability that the policy visits state
s
at time-step
h
and takes action
(a,\widetilde{P}_{s,a})
. The optimal policy can be computed through the following linear program.

\displaystyle\begin{split}\max_{q,t}\ &t\\
\textrm{s.t.}&\sum_{i}w_{i}\sum_{h=1}^{H}\sum_{s,a}\sum_{\widetilde{P}_{s,a}}q_{h}(s;a,\widetilde{P}_{s,a})r_{\sigma(i)}(s,a)\geq t\ \forall\sigma\in\mathcal{S}_{n}\\
&\sum_{a}\sum_{\widetilde{P}_{s,a}}q_{1}(s;a,\widetilde{P}_{s,a})=\rho(s)\ \forall s\\
&\sum_{a,\widetilde{P}_{s,a}}q_{h+1}(s;a,\widetilde{P}_{s,a})=\sum_{s^{\prime},a,\widetilde{P}_{s^{\prime},a}}q_{h}(s^{\prime};a,\widetilde{P}_{s^{\prime},a})\widetilde{P}_{s^{\prime},a}(s)\\
&\qquad\ \forall s,h\geq 1\\
&q_{h}(s;a,\widetilde{P}_{s,a})\geq 0\end{split}

(25)

We now construct the dual problem of the optimization problem shown above.

\displaystyle\min_{u,V}\

\displaystyle\sum_{s}\rho(s)V_{1}(s)

s.t.

\displaystyle V_{h}(s)\geq\sum_{i}w_{i}\sum_{\sigma}u_{\sigma}r_{\sigma(i)}(s,a)+\sum_{s^{\prime}}\widetilde{P}_{s,a}(s^{\prime})V_{h+1}(s^{\prime})

\displaystyle\qquad\ \forall s,a,\widetilde{P}_{s,a},h<H

\displaystyle V_{H}(s)\geq\sum_{i}w_{i}\sum_{\sigma}u_{\sigma}r_{\sigma(i)}(s,a)\ \forall s,a

\displaystyle\sum_{\sigma}u_{\sigma}\geq 1

\displaystyle u_{\sigma}\geq 0\ \forall\sigma

This linear program has
n!+HS
variables. We now show that a separation oracle of this problem can be constructed in time
\textrm{poly}(S,A,H)\cdot n!\cdot n!
time. The last three constraints can be checked in time
SA\cdot n!
time. In order to check the first constraint consider the following optimization problem for each
s,a
and
h
.

\displaystyle\max_{\widetilde{P}_{s,a}}

\displaystyle\sum_{i}w_{i}\sum_{\sigma}u_{\sigma}r_{\sigma(i)}(s,a)+\sum_{s^{\prime}}\widetilde{P}_{s,a}(s^{\prime})V_{h+1}(s^{\prime})

s.t.

\displaystyle\left\lVert\widetilde{P}_{s,a}-\widehat{P}(s,a,\cdot)\right\rVert_{1}\leq\varepsilon_{t}(s,a)

Note that this optimization is a concave optimization problem but evaluating the objective for any choice of the parameter
\widetilde{P}_{s,a}
takes time
SA\cdot n\cdot n!
time. This implies that the optimization problem above can be solved in time
\textrm{poly}(S,A)\cdot n\cdot n!
. Since we need to solve the above optimization problem for each
s,a
, and
h
the total running time for the separation oracle is
\textrm{poly}(S,A,H)\cdot n\cdot n!
.

For a linear programming with
d
variables, ellipsoid method runs in
\widetilde{O}(d^{2})
iterations. Substituting
d=n!+HS
and running time of the sepration oracle, we get that the total running time to solve the optimistic optimization problem for the objective
\mathrm{GGW}
is
\textrm{poly}(S,A,H)\cdot n\cdot(n!)^{3}=n^{O(n)}\cdot\textrm{poly}(S,A,H)
.

E.1.3
Nash Welfare

The primal optimization problem is given as follows.

\displaystyle\begin{split}\max_{q}\ &\sum_{i=1}^{n}\log\left(\sum_{h=1}^{H}\sum_{s,a}\sum_{\widetilde{P}_{s,a}}q_{h}(s;a,\widetilde{P}_{s,a})r_{i}(s,a)\right)\\
\textrm{s.t.}\ &\sum_{a}\sum_{\widetilde{P}_{s,a}}q_{1}(s;a,\widetilde{P}_{s,a})=\rho(s)\ \forall s\\
&\sum_{a,\widetilde{P}_{s,a}}q_{h+1}(s;a,\widetilde{P}_{s,a})=\sum_{s^{\prime},a,\widetilde{P}_{s^{\prime},a}}q_{h}(s^{\prime};a,\widetilde{P}_{s^{\prime},a})\widetilde{P}_{s^{\prime},a}(s)\\
&\qquad\ \forall s,h\geq 1\\
&q_{h}(s;a,\widetilde{P}_{s,a})\geq 0\end{split}

(26)

As before, this optimization problem has infinite number of variables but
HS
constraints. Therefore, we look at the corresponding dual problem.

\displaystyle\min_{\beta,V}\

\displaystyle-\sum_{i=1}^{n}\log(\beta_{i})+\sum_{s}\rho(s)V_{1}(s)

s.t.

\displaystyle V_{h}(s)\geq\sum_{i}{r_{i}(s,a)}{\beta_{i}}+\sum_{s^{\prime}}\widetilde{P}_{s,a}(s^{\prime})V_{h+1}(s^{\prime})

\displaystyle\qquad\ \forall s,a,\widetilde{P}_{s,a},h<H

\displaystyle V_{H}(s)\geq\sum_{i}{r_{i}(s,a)}{\beta_{i}}\ \forall s,a

\displaystyle\sum_{i}\beta_{i}\geq 1

\displaystyle\beta_{i}\geq 0\ \forall i

Now we can follow the same steps as the objective
\mathrm{MW}
and construct an efficient separation oracle for the dual problem shown above. Finally observe that the dual problem has
O(n+HS)
variables and can be solved efficiently through ellipsoid method.

We will write

\displaystyle\mathrm{NW}(\widetilde{\pi}_{t};\widetilde{P}_{t})

\displaystyle\textrm{Reg}_{\mathrm{NW}}(T)

\displaystyle\textrm{Reg}_{i}

Lemma 5
.

Let
\epsilon(s^{\prime},a)=\left\lVert P(s^{\prime},a,\cdot)-\widetilde{P}(s^{\prime},a,\cdot)\right\rVert_{1}
for all tuples
(s^{\prime},a)
. Then for any policy
\widetilde{\pi}
we have

\left|V^{\widetilde{\pi}}(r;\widetilde{P})-V^{\widetilde{\pi}}(r;P)\right|\leq H\sum_{h=1}^{H-1}\sum_{s^{\prime},b}q_{h}(s^{\prime},b)\epsilon(s^{\prime},b)

where
q_{h}(s,a)
is the probability that policy
\widetilde{\pi}
visits state
s
at time-step
h
and takes action
a
.

Proof.

We will use
\widetilde{q}
to write the state-action occupancy measure under policy
\widetilde{\pi}
and probability transition function
\widetilde{P}
i.e.
\widetilde{q}_{h}(s,a)=\Pr_{\widetilde{\pi},\widetilde{P}}(s_{h}=s,a_{h}=a)
. Similarly, we will use
q
to denote the state-action occupancy measure under policy
\widetilde{\pi}
and probability transition function
P
. We will also write
\epsilon(s^{\prime},b,s)=\widetilde{P}(s^{\prime},b,s)-P(s^{\prime},b,s)

Since rewards are bounded between
0
and
1
we have the following inequality.

\displaystyle\left|V^{\widetilde{\pi}}(r;\widetilde{P})-V^{\widetilde{\pi}}(r;P)\right|

(27)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\left|\sum_{h=1}^{H}\sum_{s,a}\widetilde{q}_{h}(s,a)r(s,a)-\sum_{h=1}^{H}\sum_{s,a}q_{h}(s,a)r(s,a)\right|

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{h=1}^{H}\underbrace{\sum_{s,a}\left|\widetilde{q}_{h}(s,a)-q_{h}(s,a)\right|}_{:=\Delta_{h}}

We now establish a recurrence relation for the term

. First note that,
\Delta_{1}=0
since

\displaystyle\Delta_{1}

\displaystyle=\sum_{s,a}\left|\widetilde{q}_{1}(s,a)-q_{1}(s,a)\right|

\displaystyle=\sum_{s,a}\left|\rho(s)\widetilde{\pi}(s,a)-\rho(s)\widetilde{\pi}(s,a)\right|=0

Here we use the equality constraint

.

\displaystyle=\sum_{s,a}\left|\sum_{b}\widetilde{q}_{h}(s,b)\widetilde{\pi}(a|s)-\sum_{b}q_{h}(s,b)\widetilde{\pi}(a|s)\right|

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{s,a}\widetilde{\pi}(a|s)\left|\sum_{b}\widetilde{q}_{h}(s,b)-\sum_{b}q_{h}(s,b)\right|

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{s}\left|\sum_{s^{\prime},b}\left(\widetilde{q}_{h-1}(s^{\prime},b)-q_{h-1}(s^{\prime},b)\right)P(s^{\prime},b,s)\right|

\displaystyle+\sum_{s}\left|\sum_{s^{\prime},b}q_{h-1}(s^{\prime},b)\left(P(s^{\prime},b,s)-\widetilde{P}(s^{\prime},b,s)\right)\right|

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{s}\sum_{s^{\prime},b}\left|\widetilde{q}_{h-1}(s^{\prime},b)-q_{h-1}(s^{\prime},b)\right|P(s^{\prime},b,s)

\displaystyle+\sum_{s}\sum_{s^{\prime},b}q_{h-1}(s^{\prime},b)\epsilon(s^{\prime},b,s)

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{s^{\prime},b}\left|\widetilde{q}_{h-1}(s^{\prime},b)-q_{h-1}(s^{\prime},b)\right|

\displaystyle+\sum_{s^{\prime},b}q_{h-1}(s^{\prime},b)\sum_{s}\epsilon(s^{\prime},b,s)

\displaystyle\leq\Delta_{h-1}+\sum_{s^{\prime},b}q_{h-1}(s^{\prime},b)\sum_{s}\epsilon(s^{\prime},b,s)

\Delta_{1}=0
gives us the following bound on

\Delta_{h}\leq\sum_{h^{\prime}=1}^{h-1}\sum_{s^{\prime},b}q_{h^{\prime}}(s^{\prime},b)\sum_{s}\epsilon(s^{\prime},b,s)

\displaystyle\left|V^{\widetilde{\pi}}(r;\widetilde{P})-V^{\widetilde{\pi}}(r;P)\right|

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{h=1}^{H}\sum_{h^{\prime}=1}^{h-1}\sum_{s^{\prime},b}q_{h^{\prime}}(s^{\prime},b)\sum_{s}\epsilon(s^{\prime},b,s)

\displaystyle\leq H\sum_{h=1}^{H-1}\sum_{s^{\prime},b}q_{h}(s^{\prime},b)\left\lVert\epsilon(s^{\prime},b,\cdot)\right\rVert_{1}

E.4
Unknown Reward Functions

When the reward function is unknown we update the optimistic planning step as follows

\displaystyle(\widetilde{P}_{t},\widetilde{r}_{t})\leftarrow\mathrm{argmax}_{P\in C_{t}(\widehat{P}),r\in D_{t}(\hat{r})}\max_{\pi}\mathbb{F}(\pi;P,r)

\displaystyle\widetilde{\pi}_{t}\leftarrow\mathrm{argmax}_{\pi}\mathbb{F}(\pi;\widetilde{P}_{t},\widetilde{r}_{t})

Here
D_{t}(\hat{r})
is a confidence set around the empirical reward function
\hat{r}
and it can be constructed by using Chernoff-Hoeffding inequality and the union bound. Here we consider the case
\mathbb{F}=\mathrm{NW}
. The proof for
\mathrm{MW}
, and
\mathrm{GGW}
are similar. As in the proof of theorem
7
we can upper bound regret as

\displaystyle\textrm{Reg}_{\mathrm{NW}}(T)\leq H^{n-1}\sum_{t=1}^{T}\sum_{i\in[n]}\left|V^{\widetilde{\pi}_{t}}\left(\widetilde{r}_{t,i};\widetilde{P}_{t}\right)-V^{\widetilde{\pi}_{t}}\left(r_{i};P^{\star}\right)\right|

\displaystyle\leq H^{n-1}\sum_{i\in[n]}\underbrace{\sum_{t=1}^{T}\left|V^{\widetilde{\pi}_{t}}\left(\widetilde{r}_{t,i};\widetilde{P}_{t}\right)-V^{\widetilde{\pi}_{t}}(r_{i};\widetilde{P}_{t})\right|}_{:=\textrm{Reg}_{i}^{r}}

\displaystyle+H^{n-1}\sum_{i\in[n]}\underbrace{\sum_{t=1}^{T}\left|V^{\widetilde{\pi}_{t}}\left(r_{i};\widetilde{P}_{t}\right)-V^{\widetilde{\pi}_{t}}(r_{i};P^{\star})\right|}_{:=\textrm{Reg}^{P}_{i}}

\textrm{Reg}_{i}
introduced in theorem

and is bounded by

. The first term
\textrm{Reg}^{r}_{i}

is defined as
\overline{q}_{h}(s,a)=\frac{1}{T}\sum_{t=1}^{T}q^{t}_{h}(s,a)
.
Let
i^{\star}\in\mathrm{argmin}_{i\in[n]}\sum_{t=1}^{T}V^{\pi_{t}}(r_{i})
. Since
\mathbb{E}[V^{\pi_{t}}(r_{i})]=\sum_{h}\sum_{s,a}q_{h}^{t}(s,a)r_{i}(s,a)
, an application of Chernoff’s implies the following inequality holds with probability at least
1-\delta
.

\displaystyle\left|\sum_{t=1}^{T}\sum_{h}\sum_{s,a}q^{t}_{h}(s,a)r_{i^{\star}}(s,a)-\sum_{t=1}^{T}V^{\pi^{t}}(r_{i^{\star}})\right|

\displaystyle\leq O\left(H\sqrt{T\log(n/\delta)}\right)

Let
e_{i^{\star}}
be the unit vector with exactly
1
at index
i^{\star}
, and
0
otherwise. Then from
eq.

32
we get the following bound.

\displaystyle T\cdot\mathcal{L}(\overline{q},Be_{i^{*}})\geq\min_{\lambda\in\mathcal{C}}T\cdot\mathcal{L}(\overline{q},\lambda)

\displaystyle\geq\sum_{t=1}^{T}\mathcal{L}(q^{t},\lambda^{t})-O\left(BH\sqrt{nT\log A}\right)

\displaystyle\geq\max_{q}T\cdot\mathcal{L}(q,\overline{\lambda})

\displaystyle-\widetilde{O}\left(BHS\sqrt{AT}\right)-O\left(BH\sqrt{nT\log A}\right)\ \textrm{[By~\eqref{eq:temp-regret-1}]}

\displaystyle=T\cdot v^{\star}\left(1-\sum_{i}\overline{\lambda}_{i}\right)+T\cdot\max_{q}\sum_{i}\overline{\lambda}_{i}\sum_{h}\sum_{s,a}q_{h}(s,a)

\displaystyle-\widetilde{O}\left(BHS\sqrt{AT}\right)-O\left(BH\sqrt{nT\log A}\right)

\displaystyle\geq T\cdot v^{\star}\left(1-\sum_{i}\overline{\lambda}_{i}\right)+T\cdot v^{\star}\sum_{i}\overline{\lambda}_{i}

\displaystyle-\widetilde{O}\left(BHS\sqrt{AT}\right)-O\left(BH\sqrt{nT\log A}\right)

\displaystyle=T\cdot v^{\star}-\widetilde{O}\left(BHS\sqrt{AT}\right)-O\left(BH\sqrt{nT\log A}\right)

We can upper bound
T\cdot\mathcal{L}(\overline{q},Be_{i^{\star}})
as

\displaystyle T\cdot\left(v^{\star}+B\left(\sum_{h=1}^{H}\sum_{s,a}\overline{q}_{h}(s,a)r_{i^{\star}}(s,a)-v^{\star}\right)\right)

\displaystyle=Tv^{\star}(1-B)+B\sum_{t=1}^{T}\sum_{h=1}^{H}\sum_{s,a}q_{h}^{t}(s,a)r_{i^{\star}}(s,a)

(33)

This equality gives us the following bound.

\displaystyle-Tv^{\star}B+B\min_{i}\sum_{t=1}^{T}V^{\pi_{t}}(r_{i})\geq

\displaystyle-\widetilde{O}\left(BHS\sqrt{AT}\right)-O\left(BH\sqrt{nT\log A}\right)

After rearranging, and dividing throughout by
B
we get the following bound on regret.

Tv^{\star}-\min_{i}\sum_{t=1}^{T}V^{\pi_{t}}(r_{i})\leq\widetilde{O}\left(HS\sqrt{AT}\right)+O\left(H\sqrt{nT\log A}\right)

Now consider the case when
v^{\star}\neq v^{\star}_{\mathrm{MW}}
. Pick any
v^{\star}\geq v^{\star}_{\mathrm{MW}}
and run algorithm
2
. By a similar argument as above, we can establish the following bound.

\displaystyle T\cdot\mathcal{L}(\overline{q},Be_{i^{\star}})\geq T\cdot v^{\star}\left(1-\sum_{i}\overline{\lambda}_{i}\right)+T\cdot v^{\star}_{\mathrm{MW}}\sum_{i}\overline{\lambda}_{i}

\displaystyle-\widetilde{O}\left(BHS\sqrt{AT}\right)-O\left(BH\sqrt{nT\log A}\right)

Using the upper bound established in (
33
) we get the following bound.

\displaystyle Tv^{\star}(1-B)+B\min_{i}\sum_{t=1}^{T}V^{\pi_{t}}(r_{i})\geq T\cdot v^{\star}\left(1-\sum_{i}\overline{\lambda}_{i}\right)

\displaystyle+T\cdot v^{\star}_{\mathrm{MW}}\sum_{i}\overline{\lambda}_{i}-\widetilde{O}\left(BHS\sqrt{AT}\right)-O\left(BH\sqrt{nT\log A}\right)

After rearranging and dividing throughout by
B
we get the following inequality.

\displaystyle Tv^{\star}-\min_{i}\sum_{t=1}^{T}V^{\pi_{t}}(r_{i})\leq\frac{T}{B}\sum_{i}\overline{\lambda}_{i}\left(v^{\star}-v^{\star}_{\mathrm{MW}}\right)

\displaystyle+\widetilde{O}\left(HS\sqrt{AT}\right)+{O}\left(H\sqrt{nT\log A}\right)

This gives us the following bound on regret.

\displaystyle Tv^{\star}_{\mathrm{MW}}-\min_{i}\sum_{t=1}^{T}V^{\pi_{t}}(r_{i})

\displaystyle\leq Tv^{\star}-\min_{i}\sum_{t=1}^{T}V^{\pi_{t}}(r_{i})+T(v^{\star}_{\mathrm{MW}}-v^{\star})

\displaystyle\leq T(v^{\star}_{\mathrm{MW}}-v^{\star})\left(1-\frac{\sum_{i}\overline{\lambda}_{i}}{B}\right)

\displaystyle+\widetilde{O}\left(HS\sqrt{AT}\right)+{O}\left(H\sqrt{nT\log A}\right)

\displaystyle\leq\widetilde{O}\left(HS\sqrt{AT}\right)+{O}\left(H\sqrt{nT\log A}\right)

The last inequality follows because
v^{\star}_{\mathrm{MW}}\leq v^{\star}
and
\sum_{i}\overline{\lambda}_{i}\leq B
.
∎
