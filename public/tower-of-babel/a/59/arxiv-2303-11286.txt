Almost flat highest weights and application to Wilson loops on compact surfaces
Thibaut Lemoine

Abstract
We derive new formulas for the expectation and variance of Wilson loops for any contractible simple loop on a compact orientable surface of genus 1 1 and higher, in the model of two-dimensional Yang–Mills theory with structure group U ⁡ ( N ) {\mathrm{U}}(N) . They are written in terms of a Gaussian measure on the dual of U ⁡ ( N ) {\mathrm{U}}(N) introduced recently by the author and M. Maïda [ LM25a ] . From these formulas, we prove a quantitative result on the convergence of the expectation and variance as N N tends to infinity, refining a result of [ DL23 ] . We finally derive the large g g limit of the Wilson loop expectation and variance, by analogy with the study of integrals on moduli spaces of compact hyperbolic surfaces. Surprisingly, the variance does not vanish in this regime, but there are no nontrivial fluctuations of any order.



Keywords—
Almost flat highest weights, Asymptotic representation theory, Two-dimensional Yang–Mills theory, Wilson loops

1.1
Yang–Mills measure and Wilson loops

The two-dimensional quantum Yang–Mills theory is a toy model for the quantum gauge theory in four dimensions used notably in the Standard model of particle physics. It was started by Migdal

]

\Sigma

is endowed with a

\Sigma

are homeomorphic to disks. The

The

They can be somehow related to the so-called stable representations of the unitary group . In this case, if we denote
\lambda_{N}(\alpha,\beta,n)
such highest weight, one can prove that

1.4.3
Weil–Petersson model of random hyperbolic surfaces

In the Weil–Petersson model, the representation space of a compact hyperbolic surface

\pi_{1}(\Sigma)
. The geodesic lengths
L_{1},\ldots,L_{n}
of the boundary components define a set of parameters for the corresponding Teichmüller space, and we write
\mathcal{T}_{g,n}(L_{1},\ldots,L_{n})
to underline the dependence on the geodesic lengths. The representation space admits a symplectic volume form
\mathrm{Vol}_{g,n}
that defines the
Weil–Petersson measure
on the Teichmüller space. If one quotients the Teichmüller space by the mapping class group
\mathrm{MCG}_{g,n}
of the surface, one obtains the
moduli space

\mathcal{M}_{g,n}(L_{1},\ldots,L_{n})=\mathcal{T}_{g,n}(L_{1},\ldots,L_{n})/\mathrm{MCG}_{g,n}.

The Weil–Petersson measure has an infinite mass on the Teichmüller space, but it descends on the moduli space to a finite measure. The Weil–Petersson volume on
\mathcal{M}_{g,n}
is the analog of the Yang–Mills partition function, and analogs of Wilson loops for simple loops are the so-called
geometric functions
, which are functions
f_{\gamma}:\mathcal{M}_{g,n}\to\mathbb{R}
defined by

f_{\gamma}(X)=\sum_{[\alpha]\in\mathcal{O}(\gamma)}f(\ell_{\alpha}(X)),

where
\mathcal{O}_{\gamma}
is the set of homotopy classes in the orbit of
\gamma
on
X\in\mathcal{M}_{g,n}
by the action of the mapping class group,
\ell_{\alpha}(X)
is the length of the geodesic representative of
[\alpha]
in
Y\in\mathcal{T}_{g,n}
, and
f:\mathbb{R}_{+}\to\mathbb{R}
is a given function. Their expectation with respect to the Weil–Petersson measure is by definition

{\mathbb{E}}_{WP}[f_{\gamma}(X)]=\frac{1}{\mathrm{vol}_{WP}(\mathcal{M}_{g})}\int_{\mathcal{M}_{g}}f_{\gamma}(X)d\mathrm{vol}_{WP}(X).

Mirzakhani described in
[
Mir07
]
how to compute such expectations for geometric functions, and investigated in
[
Mir13
]
the asymptotic behaviour of these integrals in the large
g
limit. For instance, if
c
is a separating simple loop on a compact surface
\Sigma
of genus
g
without boundary that splits
\Sigma
into
\Sigma_{1,1}
and
\Sigma_{g-1,1}
with one boundary component and respective genera 1 and
g-1
. Let

F_{c}^{L}(X)=|\{\gamma\in\mathcal{O}_{c},\ell_{\gamma}(X)\leqslant L\}|

be the counting function of closed geodesics in the
\mathrm{MCG}_{g,0}
-orbit of a given closed curve
c
with length at most
L
. Then Mirzakhani proved in
[
Mir13
]

{\mathbb{E}}_{WP}[F_{c}^{L}(X)]\sim\frac{e^{L/2}L^{3}}{g},

as
g\to\infty
. Further results have been obtained since then
[
MZ15
,
MP19
,
AM22
,
LW24
]
, leading to asymptotic expansions of expectations of geometric functions in powers of
\frac{1}{g}
. The main motivation in these papers is to describe the spectral gap of random hyperbolic surfaces with respect to the Weil–Petersson measure. In comparison, Theorem
1.4
shows that after the leading term, there is no polynomial term in
\frac{1}{g}
of any order, which seems to be a surprising difference. Note that, however, no contractible simple loop has any use in the Weil–Petersson model: one rather deals with nonseparating simple loops or non-contractible separating simple loops, in order to find a geodesic representative of its homotopy class.

2
Preliminary tools

In this section, we study irreducible representations of
{\mathrm{U}}(N)
as couplings of integer partitions. The irreducible representations of
{\mathrm{U}}(N)
are labelled by nonincreasing
N
-tuples of integers
\lambda=(\lambda_{1},\ldots,\lambda_{N})\in{\mathbb{Z}}^{N}
with
\lambda_{1}\geqslant\cdots\geqslant\lambda_{N}
(we will use the notation
\lambda=(\lambda_{1}\geqslant\cdots\geqslant\lambda_{N})
for nonincreasing
N
-tuples) called
highest weights
, and we denote respectively by
d_{\lambda}
and
c_{2}(\lambda)
their
dimension
and
Casimir number
, given by

(17)

d_{\lambda}=\prod_{1\leqslant i<j\leqslant N}\frac{\lambda_{i}-\lambda_{j}+j-i}{j-i}

and

(18)

c_{2}(\lambda)=\frac{1}{N}\left(\sum_{i=1}^{N}\lambda_{i}^{2}+\sum_{1\leqslant i<j\leqslant N}(\lambda_{i}-\lambda_{j})\right).

The set of irreducible representations is denoted by
\widehat{{\mathrm{U}}}(N)
and is in bijection with the set of highest weights. The
character
of a representation of highest weight
\lambda
is given by the
Schur function

s_{\lambda}
, which is a symmetric polynomial when
\lambda_{N}\geqslant 0
and a symmetric Laurent polynomial otherwise. We will not need its explicit formula for our computations, but refer to
[
Mac15
]
and
[
Sta99
]
for details about it. The character decomposition (
2
) of the heat kernel on
{\mathrm{U}}(N)
becomes then

(19)

p_{T}(U)=\sum_{\lambda\in\widehat{{\mathrm{U}}}(N)}e^{-c_{2}(\lambda)\frac{T}{2}}d_{\lambda}s_{\lambda}(U),~\forall T>0,~\forall U\in{\mathrm{U}}(N).

In order to compute Wilson loop expectations and variances in a convenient way, we will need two ingredients: a specific measure on
\widehat{{\mathrm{U}}}(N)
that was introduced in
[
DL23
]
and studied in detail in
[
LM25a
]
, and branching rules on highest weights.

2.1
Gaussian measure and observables of highest weights

We will consider the following measure on
\widehat{{\mathrm{U}}}(N)
:

\mathbb{P}_{\widehat{{\mathrm{U}}}(N),T}(\{\lambda\})=\frac{1}{Z_{N}(1,T)}e^{-\frac{T}{2}c_{2}(\lambda)},\quad\forall\lambda\in\widehat{{\mathrm{U}}}(N).

This measure can be considered as an analog of the discrete Gaussian measure on
{\mathbb{Z}}
, which is
\mathbb{P}_{\widehat{{\mathrm{U}}}(1),T}
, as already observed in
[
DL23
,
LM25a
]
. In this particular case, we also find that
Z_{1}(1,T)=\theta(e^{-\frac{T}{2}})
, where
\theta
is the following Jacobi theta function:

\theta(q)=\sum_{n\in{\mathbb{Z}}}q^{n^{2}}.

From two integer partitions
\alpha=(\alpha_{1}\geqslant\cdots\geqslant\alpha_{r}>0)
and
\beta=(\beta_{1}\geqslant\cdots\geqslant\beta_{s}>0)
of respective lengths
\ell(\alpha)=r
and
\ell(\beta)=s
, and an integer
n\in{\mathbb{Z}}
, we can form, for all
N\geqslant r+s+1
, the
composite
highest weight

(20)

\lambda_{N}(\alpha,\beta,n)=(\alpha_{1}+n,\ldots,\alpha_{r}+n,\underbrace{n,\ldots,n}_{N-r-s},n-\beta_{s},\ldots,n-\beta_{1})\in\widehat{\mathrm{U}}(N).

This definition is extended in the obvious way to the cases where one or both of the partitions
\alpha
and
\beta
are the empty partition.

We proved in
[
Lem21
]
that this construction can be reversed: given a highest weight
\lambda\in\widehat{{\mathrm{U}}}(N)
, we can define unambiguously
\alpha,\beta
and
n
such that
\lambda=\lambda_{N}(\alpha,\beta,n)
. It provides a bijection
\lambda_{N}:\Lambda_{N}^{0}\to\widehat{{\mathrm{U}}}(N),
where
\Lambda_{N}^{0}
is a subset of
\mathfrak{Y}\times\mathfrak{Y}\times{\mathbb{Z}}
given by
(\alpha,\beta,n)
such that
\ell(\alpha)\leqslant\lfloor(N+1)/2\rfloor
and
\ell(\beta)\leqslant N-\lfloor(N+1)/2\rfloor
. The main weights of interest will be
\lambda_{N}(\alpha,\beta,n)
such that
\alpha
and
\beta
are small perturbations of the constant weight
(n,\ldots,n)
; such weights were already studied by Gross and Taylor in
[
GT93
]
for finite
\alpha
and
\beta
. Our approach actually enables
\alpha
and
\beta
to grow with
N
while remaining small enough, so that
\lambda_{N}(\alpha,\beta,n)
remains close to
(n,\ldots,n)
,
i.e.
, “almost flat”.

More recently, we also proved, together with Mylène Maïda, that the coupling of partitions can also pass to measures. For any
q\in(0,1)
, the
q
-uniform measure
\mathscr{U}(q)
on the set
\mathfrak{Y}
of integer partitions is given by

\mathbb{P}(\{\alpha\})=\phi(q)q^{|\alpha|},

where
\phi(q)=\prod_{m=1}^{\infty}(1-q^{m})
is Euler’s function. Below are the main results from
[
LM25a
]
that we will need in the current paper. The first one expresses expectations with respect to
\mathbb{P}_{\widehat{{\mathrm{U}}}(N),T}
in terms of random partitions.

Proposition 2.1

(
[
LM25a
]
)
.

For any
N\geqslant 1
,
q\in(0,1)
, and any measurable
f:\widehat{{\mathrm{U}}}(N)\to\mathbb{R}
,

(21)

{\mathbb{E}}_{\widehat{{\mathrm{U}}}(N),T}[f(\lambda)]=\frac{\theta(q_{T})}{Z_{N}(1,T)\phi(q_{T})^{2}}{\mathbb{E}}\left[f(\lambda_{N}(\alpha,\beta,n))q_{T}^{\frac{2}{N}F(\alpha,\beta,n)}\mathbf{1}_{\Lambda_{N}^{0}}(\alpha,\beta,n)\right],

where
\alpha,\beta,n
are independent random variables such that
\alpha,\beta\sim\mathscr{U}(q_{T})
and
n\sim\mathbb{P}_{\widehat{{\mathrm{U}}}(1),T}
.

The next two results are general estimates on partitions.

Proposition 2.2

(
[
LM25a
]
)
.

Let
q\in(0,1)
be fixed, and
\alpha\sim\mathscr{U}(q)

M>0,

(22)

\mathbb{P}(\ell(\alpha)>M)\leqslant C_{q}e^{M\log q}

\mathbb{P}(|\alpha|>M)\leqslant C_{q}e^{\frac{M}{2}\log q}.

(
[
LM25a

For any

c_{2}(\lambda_{N}(\alpha,\beta,n))\geqslant\frac{1}{2}(|\alpha|+|\beta|)+\left(n+\frac{|\alpha|-|\beta|}{N}\right)^{2}=:C_{N}(\alpha,\beta,n),

We will also need a convergence estimate for
\mathbb{P}_{\widehat{{\mathrm{U}}}(1),T}
.

Proposition 2.4
.

(26)

{\mathbb{E}}_{\widehat{{\mathrm{U}}}(1),T}\left[e^{-\frac{tn}{N}}-\theta(q_{T})\right]=O(N^{-2}).

Proof.

Using a Taylor expansion of the exponential with integral remainder, we have

\displaystyle\left(\sum_{n\in{\mathbb{Z}}}e^{-\frac{T}{2}n^{2}-\frac{t}{N}n}\right)-\theta(q_{T})=

\displaystyle\sum_{n\in{\mathbb{Z}}}e^{-\frac{T}{2}n^{2}}\left(e^{-\frac{t}{N}n}-1\right)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{n\in{\mathbb{Z}}}e^{-\frac{T}{2}n^{2}}\left(-\frac{t}{N}n+\frac{t^{2}n^{2}}{N^{2}}+I_{N}(n)\right),

where
I_{N}(n)
is a quantity that is
O(N^{-3})
uniformly in
n
. Hence,

\left(\sum_{n\in{\mathbb{Z}}}e^{-\frac{T}{2}n^{2}-\frac{t}{N}n}\right)-\theta(q_{T})=-\frac{t}{N}\sum_{n\in{\mathbb{Z}}}nq_{t}^{n^{2}}+\frac{t^{2}}{N^{2}}\sum_{n\in{\mathbb{Z}}}n^{2}q_{t}^{n^{2}}+O(N^{-3}).

The first sum vanishes by symmetry, and the second one is bounded. Equation (
26
) follows by dividing both terms by
\theta(q_{T})
.
∎

2.2
Branching rules

Let us describe how the branching rules
\mu\nearrow\lambda,\mu\searrow\lambda
and
\mu\sim\lambda
described in the introduction are translated into the decomposition
\lambda_{N}(\alpha,\beta,n)
.

Proposition 2.5
.

Let
\alpha,\beta,\alpha^{\prime},\beta^{\prime}
be integer partitions, and
n,n^{\prime},N
three integers such that
N\geqslant\max(\ell(\alpha)+\ell(\beta)+1,\ell(\alpha^{\prime})+\ell(\beta^{\prime})+1)
. Then the following assertions are equivalent:

(i)

\lambda_{N}(\alpha^{\prime},\beta^{\prime},n^{\prime})\searrow\lambda_{N}(\alpha,\beta,n)
,

(ii)

(
\alpha^{\prime}\searrow\alpha
,
\beta^{\prime}=\beta
and
n^{\prime}=n
) or (
\beta^{\prime}\nearrow\beta
,
\alpha^{\prime}=\alpha
and
n^{\prime}=n
).

Proof.

In order to see the equivalence, recall the construction of
\lambda_{N}(\alpha,\beta,n)
given in (
20
):

\lambda_{N}(\alpha,\beta,n)=(\alpha_{1}+n,\ldots,\alpha_{r}+n,\underbrace{n,\ldots,n}_{N-r-s},n-\beta_{s},\ldots,n-\beta_{1})=(\lambda_{1},\ldots,\lambda_{N}).

The only way of having
\lambda_{N}(\alpha^{\prime},\beta^{\prime},n^{\prime})\searrow\lambda_{N}(\alpha,\beta,n)
is to increment a coefficient
\lambda_{i}
such that
\lambda_{i}>\lambda_{i+1}
. It clearly excludes the coefficients
\lambda_{r+1},\ldots,\lambda_{N-s}
. Two only ways remain: either we increment one of the coefficients
\lambda_{1},\ldots,\lambda_{r}
, or we increment one of the coefficients
\lambda_{r+s+1},\ldots,\lambda_{N}
. The first case corresponds to
\alpha^{\prime}\searrow\alpha
and the second one to
\beta^{\prime}\nearrow\beta
(while leaving the other parameters unchanged), according to the description of the coefficients in terms of
\alpha,\beta
and
n
. The equivalence follows immediately.
∎

Using these informations, we can estimate the difference of Casimir numbers from two branching highest weights. Recall that the
content
of the box
(i,j)
of a diagram corresponding to the partition
\alpha=(\alpha_{1}\geqslant\cdots\geqslant\alpha_{r}>0)
is the quantity
c(i,j)=j-i
, and that the
total content

K(\alpha)
of the diagram is the sum of contents of all its boxes. We denote also by
|\alpha|
the sum of the parts of the partition:
|\alpha|=\alpha_{1}+\cdots+\alpha_{r}
.

Proposition 2.6

(
[
Lem21
]
)
.

Let
\alpha
and
\beta
be two partitions of respective lengths
r
and
s
. Let
n
be an integer. Then, provided
N\geqslant r+s+1
, we have

(27)

c_{2}(\lambda_{N}(\alpha,\beta,n))=|\alpha|+|\beta|+n^{2}+\frac{2}{N}\big(K(\alpha)+K(\beta)+n(|\alpha|-|\beta|)\big).

Here is how Casimir numbers behave with respect to branching rules.

Proposition 2.7
.

Let
\alpha,\beta
be two integer partitions and
N\geqslant\ell(\alpha)+\ell(\beta)+1

and any

\square^{\prime}

(28)

c_{2}(\lambda_{N}(\alpha^{\prime},\beta,n))-c_{2}(\lambda_{N}(\alpha,\beta,n))=1+\frac{2}{N}(c(\square^{\prime})+n),

\square^{\prime}

\beta

(29)

c_{2}(\lambda_{N}(\alpha,\beta^{\prime},n))-c_{2}(\lambda_{N}(\alpha,\beta,n))=-1+\frac{2}{N}(-c(\square^{\prime})+n),

For any
\alpha^{\prime}\searrow\alpha
and
\beta^{\prime}\searrow\beta

\square_{1}

Now, let us turn to dimensions and their ratios. We first need to introduce a particular regime to deal with dimensions. Let us fix a real
\gamma\in(0,\frac{1}{3})
, that we can consider as a control parameter
5
5

5

Intuitively, we want it to be as small as possible, while remaining positive.
. Set

\Lambda_{N}^{\gamma}=\{(\alpha,\beta,n)\in\mathfrak{Y}\times\mathfrak{Y}\times{\mathbb{Z}}:|\alpha|\leqslant N^{\gamma},|\beta|\leqslant N^{\gamma}\}.

A crucial point is the following: for
N
large enough, any partition of an integer not greater than
N^{\gamma}
has less than
\frac{N}{2}
positive parts. Thus,
\Lambda_{N}^{\gamma}\subset\Lambda_{N}^{0}
. The set
\{\lambda_{N}(\alpha,\beta,n):(\alpha,\beta,n)\in\Lambda_{N}^{\gamma}\}
is a subset of
\widehat{{\mathrm{U}}}(N)
for

large enough, and correspond to the heuristic notion of ‘almost flat’ highest weights. Sometimes, it will be sufficient to consider highest weights that are truly flat, and we denote by

\Lambda_{N}=\{\lambda_{N}(\varnothing,\varnothing,n),n\in{\mathbb{Z}}\}\simeq{\mathbb{Z}}

the corresponding set. The dimensions of the corresponding irreducible representations can be estimated in terms of representations of the symmetric group. Recall that the irreducible representations of
S_{n}
are in one-to-one correspondence with integer partitions
\alpha\vdash n
. We denote by
d^{\alpha}
(resp.
\chi^{\alpha}
) the dimension (resp. character) of the irreducible representation of

corresponding to
\alpha
.

Proposition 2.8
.

Let
\alpha
and
\beta
be two integer partitions,
N\geqslant\ell(\alpha)+\ell(\beta)+1
an integer and
\gamma\in(0,\tfrac{1}{3})

|\alpha|\leqslant N^{\gamma}
and
|\beta|\leqslant N^{\gamma}
. The partitions
\alpha
and
\beta
induce two highest weights of

,
\tilde{\alpha}=\lambda_{N}(\alpha,\varnothing,0)
and
\tilde{\beta}=\lambda_{N}(\beta,\varnothing,0)
. We have the following facts.

(i)

For
N
large enough,

(31)

\frac{d^{\alpha}N^{|\alpha|}}{|\alpha|!}(1-2N^{2\gamma-1})\leqslant d_{\tilde{\alpha}}\leqslant\frac{d^{\alpha}N^{|\alpha|}}{|\alpha|!}(1+2N^{2\gamma-1}),

and the same result holds for
\beta
.

(ii)

For any
n\in{\mathbb{Z}}
, the dimension of
\lambda_{N}(\alpha,\beta,n)
satisfies the following estimation, assuming that

is large enough:

(32)

\frac{d^{\alpha}d^{\beta}N^{|\alpha|+|\beta|}}{|\alpha|!|\beta|!}(1-24N^{3\gamma-1})\leqslant d_{\lambda_{N}(\alpha,\beta,n)}\leqslant\frac{d^{\alpha}d^{\beta}N^{|\alpha|+|\beta|}}{|\alpha|!|\beta|!}(1+24N^{3\gamma-1})

[

were finite and not depending on

Proof of Proposition
2.8
.

(i)
let us first recall that (cf.
[
GT93
,
VO04
]
)

(33)

d_{\tilde{\alpha}}=\frac{d^{\alpha}N^{|\alpha|}}{|\alpha|!}\prod_{\begin{subarray}{c}1\leqslant i\leqslant r\\
1\leqslant j\leqslant\alpha_{i}\end{subarray}}\left(1+\frac{j-i}{N}\right).

But for any
1\leqslant i\leqslant r
and
1\leqslant j\leqslant\alpha_{i}
, we have
1-r\leqslant j-i\leqslant\alpha_{i}-1
, and under the assumption
|\alpha|\leqslant N^{\gamma}
it implies that
|j-i|\leqslant N^{\gamma}
. Thus,

(1-N^{\gamma-1})^{|\alpha|}\leqslant\prod_{\begin{subarray}{c}1\leqslant i\leqslant r\\
1\leqslant j\leqslant\alpha_{i}\end{subarray}}\left(1+\frac{j-i}{N}\right)\leqslant(1+N^{\gamma-1})^{|\alpha|}.

From the convexity inequality of the exponential function, we have

(1+N^{\gamma-1})^{|\alpha|}\leqslant e^{|\alpha|N^{\gamma-1}}.

We can use the following reverse inequalities, that hold for any
x\in(0,\tfrac{1}{2})
:

e^{x}\leqslant 1+2x,\ \log(1-x)\geqslant-2x.

It implies that, for
N
such that
N^{\gamma-1}<\tfrac{1}{2}
(which is true for
N
large enough),

1-2N^{2\gamma-1}\leqslant e^{-2|\alpha|N^{\gamma-1}}\leqslant(1-N^{\gamma-1})^{|\alpha|}\leqslant\prod_{\begin{subarray}{c}1\leqslant i\leqslant r\\
1\leqslant j\leqslant\alpha_{i}\end{subarray}}\left(1+\frac{j-i}{N}\right)\leqslant e^{|\alpha|N^{\gamma-1}}\leqslant 1+2N^{2\gamma-1}.

This estimation, applied to (
33
), gives the expected result.

(ii)
Let us first remark that, from the Weyl dimension formula, we have for any
n\in{\mathbb{Z}}

(34)

d_{\lambda_{N}(\alpha,\beta,n)}=d_{\tilde{\alpha}}d_{\tilde{\beta}}Q(\alpha,\beta),

Q(\alpha,\beta)=\prod_{\begin{subarray}{c}1\leqslant i\leqslant r\\
1\leqslant j\leqslant s\end{subarray}}\frac{(N+1-i-j)(\alpha_{i}+\beta_{j}+N+1-i-j)}{(\alpha_{i}+N+1-i-j)(\beta_{j}+N+1-i-j)}.

As

and

implies that we also have

. For any

-2N^{\gamma}\leqslant 3-2N^{\gamma}\leqslant\alpha_{i}+\beta_{j}-i-j+1\leqslant 2N^{\gamma}-1\leqslant 2N^{\gamma}.

It implies that

\left|\frac{1+\alpha_{i}+\beta_{j}-i-j}{N}\right|\leqslant 2N^{\gamma-1},

and we have the same bound for
\left|\frac{1+\alpha_{i}-i-j}{N}\right|
,
\left|\frac{1+\beta_{j}-i-j}{N}\right|
and

Q(\alpha,\beta)=\prod_{\begin{subarray}{c}1\leqslant i\leqslant r\\
1\leqslant j\leqslant s\end{subarray}}\frac{(1+A_{N}(i,j))(1+B_{N}(i,j))}{(1+C_{N}(i,j))(1+D_{N}(i,j))},

with
|A_{N}(i,j)|,|B_{N}(i,j)|,|C_{N}(i,j)|,|D_{N}(i,j)|\leqslant 2N^{\gamma-1}
.

For any

\frac{1}{1+C_{N}(i,j)}=1-\frac{C_{N}(i,j)}{1+C_{N}(i,j)}=1+C^{\prime}_{N}(i,j),

. It implies that

Q(\alpha,\beta)=\prod_{\begin{subarray}{c}1\leqslant i\leqslant r\\

with

. Hence, using the same inequalities as in

e^{-8N^{3\gamma-1}}\leqslant(1-4N^{\gamma-1})^{N^{2\gamma}}\leqslant Q(\alpha,\beta)\leqslant(1+4N^{\gamma-1})^{N^{2\gamma}}\leqslant e^{4N^{3\gamma-1}},

1-8N^{3\gamma-1}\leqslant Q(\alpha,\beta)\leqslant 1+8N^{3\gamma-1}.

which can be simplified considering that for

(1+x)(1+y)(1+z)\leqslant e^{x+y+z}\leqslant 1+2(x+y+z).

d_{\lambda_{N}(\alpha,\beta,n)}\leqslant\frac{d^{\alpha}d^{\beta}N^{|\alpha|+|\beta|}}{|\alpha|!|\beta|!}(1+8N^{2\gamma-1}+16N^{3\gamma-1})\leqslant\frac{d^{\alpha}d^{\beta}N^{|\alpha|+|\beta|}}{|\alpha|!|\beta|!}(1+24N^{3\gamma-1}),

and

d_{\lambda_{N}(\alpha,\beta,n)}\geqslant\frac{d^{\alpha}d^{\beta}N^{|\alpha|+|\beta|}}{|\alpha|!|\beta|!}(1-24N^{3\gamma-1}),

Lemma 2.9
.

Let

n\in{\mathbb{Z}}
,

(35)

\frac{d_{\lambda_{N}(\alpha^{\prime},\beta,n)}}{d_{\lambda_{N}(\alpha,\beta,n)}}=\frac{Nd^{\alpha^{\prime}}}{|\alpha^{\prime}|d^{\alpha}}(1+O(N^{3\gamma-1})),

(36)

\frac{d_{\lambda_{N}(\alpha,\beta^{\prime},n)}}{d_{\lambda_{N}(\alpha,\beta,n)}}=\frac{|\beta|}{N}\frac{d^{\beta^{\prime}}}{d^{\beta}}(1+O(N^{3\gamma-1})),

where the
O
is uniform on
N,\alpha,\beta,n
.

Proof.

Using Proposition
2.8
, we have

\frac{d_{\lambda_{N}(\alpha^{\prime},\beta,n)}}{d_{\lambda_{N}(\alpha,\beta,n)}}=\frac{d^{\alpha^{\prime}}N^{|\alpha^{\prime}|}|\alpha|!}{d^{\alpha}N^{|\alpha|}|\alpha^{\prime}|!}(1+O(N^{3\gamma-1}))=\frac{Nd^{\alpha^{\prime}}}{d^{\alpha}|\alpha^{\prime}|}(1+O(N^{3\gamma-1})),

where the
O
is indeed independent from
N
and also from
\alpha,\beta
.
The second equality follows from a similar computation.
∎

Finally, let us mention a branching formula for dimensions of irreducible representations of the symmetric group. It will be mainly useful for the large
N
asymptotics.

Proposition 2.10
.

Let
\lambda\vdash n
for any positive integer
n
. We have

(37)

\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\mu\vdash(n+1)\\
\mu\searrow\lambda\end{subarray}}\frac{d^{\mu}}{(n+1)d^{\lambda}}=1,

and

(38)

\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\mu\vdash(n-1)\\
\mu\nearrow\lambda\end{subarray}}\frac{d^{\mu}}{d^{\lambda}}=1.

Proof.

Let us recall the so-called branching rules on
S_{n}
, cf.
[
Sag01
]
for example:

\chi^{\lambda}\uparrow^{S_{n+1}}=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\mu\vdash(n+1)\\
\mu\searrow\lambda\end{subarray}}\chi^{\mu},

and

\chi^{\lambda}\downarrow_{S_{n-1}}=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\mu\vdash(n-1)\\
\mu\nearrow\lambda\end{subarray}}\chi^{\mu}.

As the character of a restricted representation is equal to the restriction of the character, the second branching rule directly implies (
38
). For the character of an induced representation we have the following result
[
FH91
, Eq.(3.18)]
: if
G
is a finite group and
H
a subgroup of
G
, then for any character
\chi
of a representation of
H
we have

\chi\uparrow^{G}(g)=\frac{1}{|H|}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}x\in G\\
xgx^{-1}\in H\end{subarray}}\chi(xgx^{-1}),\ \forall g\in G.

If we apply this formula to
G=S_{n+1}
,
H=S_{n}
,
\chi=\chi^{\lambda}
and
g=1
we get
(
37
) as expected.
∎

In this section we prove Theorem

Lemma 3.1
.

Let

its normalized Haar measure. If

\int_{G^{2}}\chi_{\rho}(x[y,z])dydz=\frac{\chi_{\rho}(x)}{d_{\rho}^{2}},\ \forall x\in G.

In order to prove Lemma

Proposition 3.2
.

Let
G
be a compact group, and
dg
its normalized Haar measure. For any irreducible representation
(\rho,V)
of
G
, we have

\displaystyle\int_{G}\chi_{\rho}(xgyg^{-1})dg=\frac{1}{d_{\rho}}\chi_{\rho}(x)\chi_{\rho}(y),\ \forall(x,y)\in G^{2}.

Proposition 3.3
.

Let
G
be a compact group and
(\rho,V)
,
(\pi,W)
two irreducible representations of

\displaystyle\chi_{\rho}*\chi_{\pi}=\left\{\begin{array}[]{cc}\frac{\chi_{\rho}}{d_{\rho}}&\text{if }\rho\sim\pi,\\
0&\text{otherwise.}\end{array}\right.

Proof of Lemma
3.1
.

First, according to Proposition
3.2
, we have for any

\int_{G}\chi_{\rho}(xyzy^{-1}z^{-1})dz=\frac{1}{d_{\rho}}\chi_{\rho}(xy)\chi_{\rho}(y^{-1}).

If we integrate out

\int_{G^{2}}\chi_{\rho}(x[y,z])dydz=\frac{(\chi_{\rho}*\chi_{\rho})(x)}{d_{\rho}},

which yields (

∎

4

In this section, we prove Theorems
1.2
and
1.3
. We need to control the convergence of partition functions first. Let us recall the following.

Theorem 4.1

(
[
Lem21
]
)
.

Let
\Sigma_{g,T}
be an orientable surface of genus
g\geqslant 1
and area
T\geqslant 0
. Then:

(i)

If
g\geqslant 2
and
T>0
:

(41)

\lim_{N\to\infty}Z_{N}(g,T)=\theta(q_{T}).

(ii)

If
g=1
and
T>0
:

(42)

\lim_{N\to\infty}Z_{N}(1,T)=\frac{\theta(q_{T})}{\phi(q_{T})^{2}}.

We shall need a stronger statement in order to quantify the fluctuations.

Lemma 4.2
.

Let
T>0
be a fixed real number. We have uniformly, for
N
large enough,

(43)

Z_{N}(1,T)-\frac{\theta(q_{T})}{\phi(q_{T})^{2}}=O(N^{-2}).

For any

, we have uniformly

(44)

Z_{N}(g,T)-\theta(q_{T})=O(N^{2-2g}).

Proof.

The estimate for
g=1
is a direct consequence of
[
LM25a
, Theorem 1.2]
. Let us prove the case for
g\geqslant 2
. From the expression of the partition function we see that only flat highest weights contribute to the limit:

\sum_{\lambda\in\Lambda_{N}}q_{T}^{c_{2}(\lambda)}d_{\lambda}^{2-2g}=\sum_{n\in{\mathbb{Z}}}q_{T}^{n^{2}}=\theta(q_{T}).

We get then

Z_{N}(g,T)-\theta(q_{T})=\sum_{\lambda\in\widehat{{\mathrm{U}}}(N)\setminus\Lambda_{N}}q_{T}^{c_{2}(\lambda)}d_{\lambda}^{2-2g}.

Recall that
d_{\lambda}\geqslant N
for any
\lambda\not\in\Lambda_{N}
, therefore

0\leqslant Z_{N}(g,T)-\theta(q_{T})\leqslant N^{2-2g}\sum_{\lambda\in\widehat{{\mathrm{U}}}(N)\setminus\Lambda_{N}}q_{T}^{c_{2}(\lambda)}\leqslant N^{2-2g}\theta(q_{T}).

The result follows directly.
∎

The previous lemma yields an immediate consequence, that will be useful for the fluctuations of Wilson loops.

Corollary 4.3
.

For any
T>0
and any
g\geqslant 2
, we have uniformly, for
N
large enough,

(45)

\frac{Z_{N}(g,T)}{Z_{N}(1,T)}=\phi(q_{T})^{2}+O(N^{-2}).

The proofs of Theorems
1.2
and

will be split into two parts: we will first deal with

using flat highest weights, then we will prove them for

is quite special.

Proof of Theorem

.

Let us start from Equation (

\Lambda_{N}

is

. For any
\lambda=(n,\ldots,n)\in\Lambda_{N}

\mu\in\widehat{{\mathrm{U}}}(N)

Furthermore, it is straightforward that

has dimension

and Casimir number

.
It yields

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[\mathrm{tr}(H_{\ell})]=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{Z_{N}(g,T)}\sum_{n\in{\mathbb{Z}}}e^{-\frac{T}{2}n^{2}-\frac{t}{2}\left(1+\frac{2n}{N}\right)}

\displaystyle+\frac{1}{NZ_{N}(g,T)}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\lambda\in\widehat{{\mathrm{U}}}(N)\setminus\Lambda_{N}\end{subarray}}\frac{q^{c_{2}(\lambda)}}{d_{\lambda}^{2g-2}}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\mu\in\widehat{{\mathrm{U}}}(N)\\

We rewrite it as
A_{N}+B_{N}
, where

(46)

A_{N}=\frac{1}{Z_{N}(g,T)}e^{-\frac{t}{2}}\sum_{n\in{\mathbb{Z}}}e^{-\frac{T}{2}n^{2}-\frac{t}{N}n}=\frac{1}{Z_{N}(g,T)}e^{-\frac{t}{2}+\frac{t^{2}}{2TN^{2}}}\sum_{n\in{\mathbb{Z}}}e^{-\frac{T}{2}\left(n+\frac{t}{TN}\right)^{2}},

B_{N}=\frac{1}{NZ_{N}(g,T)}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\lambda\in\widehat{{\mathrm{U}}}(N)\setminus\Lambda_{N}\end{subarray}}\frac{q^{c_{2}(\lambda)}}{d_{\lambda}^{2g-2}}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\mu\in\widehat{{\mathrm{U}}}(N)\\

A_{N}-q_{t}=\frac{1+O(N^{-2})}{\theta(q_{T})}\sum_{n\in{\mathbb{Z}}}q_{T}^{n^{2}}q_{t}^{1+\frac{2n}{N}}-q_{t}.

Putting

2.9
, we get

E_{1}=\frac{\theta(q_{T})}{Z_{N}(1,T)\phi(q_{T})^{2}}{\mathbb{E}}[\mathbf{1}_{\Lambda_{N}^{\gamma}}(\alpha,\beta,n)\Psi_{N}(\alpha,\beta,n)](1+O(N^{3\gamma-1})).

It remains to prove that the
E_{2}
is negligible. First, from Proposition
2.7
we get that

c_{2}(\mu)-c_{2}(\lambda)\geqslant-1-\frac{1}{N}(|\alpha_{\lambda}|+|\beta_{\lambda}|+2n_{\lambda}),

which only depends on
\lambda
, therefore

E_{2}\leqslant{\mathbb{E}}_{\widehat{{\mathrm{U}}}(N),T}\left[\mathbf{1}_{\Omega_{N}^{2}}(\lambda)q_{t}^{-1-\frac{1}{N}(|\alpha_{\lambda}|+|\beta_{\lambda}|+2n)}\frac{1}{N}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\mu\in\widehat{{\mathrm{U}}}(N):\mu\searrow\lambda\end{subarray}}\frac{d_{\mu}}{d_{\lambda}}\right].

For any
\lambda
, we deduce from Lemma
3.4
that

\sum_{\mu\searrow\lambda}\frac{d_{\mu}}{d_{\lambda}}=N,

so that

E_{2}\leqslant{\mathbb{E}}_{\widehat{{\mathrm{U}}}(N),T}\left[\mathbf{1}_{\Omega_{N}^{2}}(\lambda)q_{t}^{-1-\frac{1}{N}(|\alpha_{\lambda}|+|\beta_{\lambda}|+2n)}\right].

Using Proposition
2.1
, we get

E_{2}\leqslant\frac{\theta(q_{T})}{Z_{N}(1,T)\phi(q_{T})^{2}}{\mathbb{E}}\left[\mathbf{1}_{\Lambda_{N}^{0}\setminus\Lambda_{N}^{\gamma}}(\alpha,\beta,n)q_{T}^{\frac{2}{N}F(\alpha,\beta,n)}q_{t}^{-1-\frac{1}{N}(|\alpha|+|\beta|+2n)}\right].

The expectation in the RHS can be bounded by the Cauchy–Schwarz inequality

\displaystyle{\mathbb{E}}\big[\mathbf{1}_{(\Lambda_{N}^{\gamma})^{c}}(\alpha,\beta,n)

\displaystyle\mathbf{1}_{\Lambda_{N}^{0}}(\alpha,\beta,n)q_{T}^{\frac{2}{N}F(\alpha,\beta,n)}q_{t}^{-1-\frac{1}{N}(|\alpha|+|\beta|+2n)}\big]

\displaystyle\leqslant

\displaystyle\sqrt{\mathbb{P}((\alpha,\beta,n)\not\in\Lambda_{N}^{\gamma}){\mathbb{E}}\big[\mathbf{1}_{\Lambda_{N}^{0}}(\alpha,\beta,n)q_{2T}^{\frac{2}{N}F(\alpha,\beta,n)}q_{2t}^{-1-\frac{1}{N}(|\alpha|+|\beta|+2n)}\big]}.

We can apply Lemma
4.4
for
k=4
to get

E_{2}\leqslant O(N^{-2})\frac{\theta(q_{T})}{Z_{N}(1,T)\phi(q_{T})^{2}}\sqrt{{\mathbb{E}}\big[\mathbf{1}_{\Lambda_{N}^{0}}(\alpha,\beta,n)q_{2T}^{\frac{2}{N}F(\alpha,\beta,n)}q_{2t}^{-1-\frac{1}{N}(|\alpha|+|\beta|+2n)}\big]}.

However, the quantity

\frac{\theta(q_{T})}{Z_{N}(1,T)\phi(q_{T})^{2}}\sqrt{{\mathbb{E}}\big[\mathbf{1}_{\Lambda_{N}^{0}}(\alpha,\beta,n)q_{2T}^{\frac{2}{N}F(\alpha,\beta,n)}q_{2t}^{-1-\frac{1}{N}(|\alpha|+|\beta|+2n)}\big]}

converges to a finite value as
N\to\infty
, therefore it is bounded uniformly in
N
. We obtain that
E_{2}=O(N^{-2})
.
∎

We can now turn to the proof of Theorem
1.2
for
g=1
.

Proof of Theorem
1.2
for
g=1
.

Let
\gamma\in(0,\frac{1}{3})
be a fixed real number. From Lemma
4.5
and Corollary
4.3
,

|{\mathbb{E}}[\mathrm{tr}(H_{\ell})]-q_{t}|=\left|{\mathbb{E}}[\mathbf{1}_{\Lambda_{N}^{\gamma}}(\alpha,\beta,n)\Psi_{N}(\alpha,\beta,n)]-q_{t}\right|(1+O(N^{-2})(1+O(N^{3\gamma-1})).

The proof therefore boils down to prove

(50)

\left|{\mathbb{E}}[\mathbf{1}_{\Lambda_{N}^{\gamma}}(\alpha,\beta,n)\Psi_{N}(\alpha,\beta,n)]-q_{t}\right|=O(N^{\gamma-1}).

In fact,

\displaystyle|{\mathbb{E}}[\mathbf{1}_{\Lambda_{N}^{\gamma}}(\alpha,\beta,n)\Psi_{N}(\alpha,\beta,n)]-q_{t}|=

\displaystyle|{\mathbb{E}}[\mathbf{1}_{\Lambda_{N}^{\gamma}}(\alpha,\beta,n)\left(\Psi_{N}(\alpha,\beta,n)-q_{t}\right)]-q_{t}{\mathbb{E}}[\mathbf{1}_{\Lambda_{N}^{0}\setminus\Lambda_{N}^{\gamma}}(\alpha,\beta,n)]|

\displaystyle\leqslant

\displaystyle|{\mathbb{E}}[\mathbf{1}_{\Lambda_{N}^{\gamma}}(\alpha,\beta,n)\left(\Psi_{N}(\alpha,\beta,n)-q_{t}\right)]|+q_{t}\mathbb{P}((\alpha,\beta,n)\in\Lambda_{N}^{0}\setminus\Lambda_{N}^{\gamma}).

First, from Lemma
4.4
we have

q_{t}\mathbb{P}((\alpha,\beta,n)\in\Lambda_{N}^{0}\setminus\Lambda_{N}^{\gamma})=O(N^{-2}).

It remains to bound the other term. For any
(\alpha,\beta,n)\in\Lambda_{N}^{\gamma}
, we have

\Psi_{N}(\alpha,\beta,n)=\sum_{\alpha^{\prime}\searrow\alpha}\frac{d^{\alpha^{\prime}}}{|\alpha^{\prime}|d^{\alpha}}q_{t}^{1+\frac{2}{N}(c(\square^{\prime})+n)}+\frac{|\beta|}{N^{2}}\sum_{\beta^{\prime}\nearrow\beta}\frac{d^{\beta^{\prime}}}{d^{\beta}}q_{t}^{-1+\frac{2}{N}(n-c(\square^{\prime}))}.

For any
\alpha^{\prime}\searrow\alpha
, if we denote by
\square^{\prime}
the box added to
\alpha
to obtain
\alpha^{\prime}
, we find that

|c(\square^{\prime})|\leqslant\max\{\alpha_{1}+1,\ell(\alpha)+1\}\leqslant N^{\gamma}.

In particular,

\left|\frac{c(\square^{\prime})}{N}\right|\leqslant N^{\gamma-1}.

Hence,

\sum_{\alpha^{\prime}\searrow\alpha}\frac{d^{\alpha^{\prime}}}{|\alpha^{\prime}|d^{\alpha}}q_{t}^{1+\frac{2}{N}(c(\square^{\prime})+n)}\leqslant\sum_{\alpha^{\prime}\searrow\alpha}\frac{d^{\alpha^{\prime}}}{|\alpha^{\prime}|d^{\alpha}}q_{t}^{1+\frac{2n}{N}-2N^{\gamma-1}}=q_{t}^{1+\frac{2n}{N}-2N^{\gamma-1}}.

Analogously,

\sum_{\alpha^{\prime}\searrow\alpha}\frac{d^{\alpha^{\prime}}}{|\alpha^{\prime}|d^{\alpha}}q_{t}^{1+\frac{2}{N}(c(\square^{\prime})+n)}\geqslant q_{t}^{1+\frac{2n}{N}+2N^{\gamma-1}}.

From these inequalities, we find that

\sum_{\alpha^{\prime}\searrow\alpha}\frac{d^{\alpha^{\prime}}}{|\alpha^{\prime}|d^{\alpha}}q_{t}^{1+\frac{2}{N}(c(\square^{\prime})+n)}=q_{t}^{1+\frac{2n}{N}}(1+O(N^{\gamma-1})),

where the
O
is uniform in
\alpha,\beta,n
. We also get, with the same arguments,

\frac{|\beta|}{N^{2}}\sum_{\beta^{\prime}\nearrow\beta}\frac{d^{\beta^{\prime}}}{d^{\beta}}q_{t}^{-1+\frac{2n}{N}-\frac{2c(\square^{\prime})}{N}}=O(N^{\gamma-2})q_{t}^{-1+\frac{2n}{N}}(1+O(N^{\gamma-1}))=q_{t}^{-1+\frac{2n}{N}}O(N^{\gamma-2}).

Combining these estimates leads to

(51)

\Psi_{N}(\alpha,\beta,n)=q_{t}^{1+\frac{2n}{N}}(1+O(N^{\gamma-1}))+q_{t}^{-1+\frac{2n}{N}}O(N^{\gamma-2}).

From there,

{\mathbb{E}}[\mathbf{1}_{\Lambda_{N}^{\gamma}}(\alpha,\beta,n)(\Psi_{N}(\alpha,\beta,n)-q_{t})]=q_{t}{\mathbb{E}}\left[\mathbf{1}_{\Lambda_{N}^{\gamma}}(\alpha,\beta,n)\left(q_{t}^{\frac{2n}{N}}(1+O(N^{\gamma-1}))+q_{t}^{-2+\frac{2n}{N}}O(N^{\gamma-2})-1\right)\right].

\displaystyle{\mathbb{E}}[\mathbf{1}_{\Lambda_{N}^{\gamma}}(\alpha,\beta,n)(\Psi_{N}(\alpha,\beta,n)-q_{t})]=

\displaystyle q_{t}{\mathbb{E}}\left[\mathbf{1}_{\Lambda_{N}^{\gamma}}(\alpha,\beta,n)\left(q_{t}^{\frac{2n}{N}}-1\right)\right]+q_{t}{\mathbb{E}}[\mathbf{1}_{\Lambda_{N}^{\gamma}}(\alpha,\beta,n)q_{t}^{\frac{2n}{N}}]O(N^{\gamma-1})

\displaystyle+{\mathbb{E}}[\mathbf{1}_{\Lambda_{N}^{\gamma}}(\alpha,\beta,n)q_{t}^{-1+\frac{2n}{N}}]O(N^{\gamma-2}).

Using Proposition
2.4
, we find that

{\mathbb{E}}[\mathbf{1}_{\Lambda_{N}^{\gamma}}(\alpha,\beta,n)(\Psi_{N}(\alpha,\beta,n)-q_{t})]=O(N^{\gamma-1}),

which yields (
50
).
∎

Proof of Theorem

for
g=1
.

Given
(\alpha,\beta,n)\in\Lambda_{N}^{\gamma}
, set

\Lambda_{N}^{(1)}(\alpha,\beta,n)=\{(\alpha^{\prime},\beta^{\prime},n),\alpha^{\prime}\searrow\alpha,\beta^{\prime}\searrow\beta\},

\Lambda_{N}^{(2)}(\alpha,\beta,n)=\{(\alpha^{\prime},\beta^{\prime},n),\alpha^{\prime}\nearrow\alpha,\beta^{\prime}\nearrow\beta\},

\Lambda_{N}^{(3)}(\alpha,\beta,n)=\{(\alpha,\beta^{\prime},n),\beta^{\prime}\sim\beta,\beta^{\prime}\neq\beta\},

Using the same arguments as in the proof of Lemma
4.5
, we can obtain

{\mathbb{E}}[|\mathrm{tr}(H_{\ell})|^{2}]=(E_{1}+E_{2}+E_{3}+E_{4}+E_{5}),

with

E_{i}=\frac{\theta(q_{T})}{N^{2}Z_{N}(1,T)\phi(q_{T})^{2}}{\mathbb{E}}\left[\mathbf{1}_{\Lambda_{N}^{\gamma}}(\alpha,\beta,n)\sum_{(\alpha^{\prime},\beta^{\prime},n)\in\Lambda_{N}^{(i)}}\frac{d_{\lambda_{N}(\alpha^{\prime},\beta^{\prime},n)}}{d_{\lambda_{N}(\alpha,\beta,n)}}q_{t}^{c_{2}(\lambda_{N}(\alpha^{\prime},\beta^{\prime},n))-c_{2}(\lambda_{N}(\alpha,\beta,n))}\right].

From Lemma

E_{i}=\frac{1+O(N^{-2})}{N^{2}}{\mathbb{E}}\left[\mathbf{1}_{\Lambda_{N}^{\gamma}}(\alpha,\beta,n)\sum_{(\alpha^{\prime},\beta^{\prime},n)\in\Lambda_{N}^{(i)}}\frac{d_{\lambda_{N}(\alpha^{\prime},\beta^{\prime},n)}}{d_{\lambda_{N}(\alpha,\beta,n)}}q_{t}^{c_{2}(\lambda_{N}(\alpha^{\prime},\beta^{\prime},n))-c_{2}(\lambda_{N}(\alpha,\beta,n))}\right].

We shall first estimate
E_{1}
, then prove that
E_{i}=O(N^{-2})
for
i\geqslant 2
.

E_{1}=\frac{1+O(N^{-2})}{N^{2}}{\mathbb{E}}\left[\mathbf{1}_{\Lambda_{N}^{\gamma}}(\alpha,\beta,n)\sum_{\alpha^{\prime}\searrow\alpha}\sum_{\beta^{\prime}\searrow\beta}\frac{d_{\lambda_{N}(\alpha^{\prime},\beta^{\prime},n)}}{d_{\lambda_{N}(\alpha,\beta,n)}}q_{t}^{c_{2}(\lambda_{N}(\alpha^{\prime},\beta^{\prime},n))-c_{2}(\lambda_{N}(\alpha,\beta,n))}\right].

Using Proposition
2.7
and Lemma
2.9
, we find on the one hand

E_{1}\leqslant\frac{e^{-t-\frac{t}{2}2N^{\gamma-1}}}{N^{2}}{\mathbb{E}}\left[\mathbf{1}_{\Lambda_{N}^{\gamma}}(\alpha,\beta,n)\sum_{\alpha^{\prime}\searrow\alpha}\sum_{\beta^{\prime}\searrow\beta}\frac{N^{2}d^{\beta^{\prime}}d^{\alpha^{\prime}}}{|\beta|d^{\beta}|\alpha|d^{\alpha}}\right](1+O(N^{3\gamma-1})).

It follows that
E_{1}=e^{-t}(1+O(N^{3\gamma-1}))
as
N\to\infty
. Now, it remains to show that
E_{i}=O(N^{-2})
for
i\geqslant 2
.

E_{2}=\frac{1+O(N^{-2})}{N^{2}}{\mathbb{E}}\left[\mathbf{1}_{\Lambda_{N}^{\gamma}}(\alpha,\beta,n)\sum_{\alpha^{\prime}\nearrow\alpha}\sum_{\beta^{\prime}\nearrow\beta}\frac{d_{\lambda_{N}(\alpha^{\prime},\beta^{\prime},n)}}{d_{\lambda_{N}(\alpha,\beta,n)}}q_{t}^{c_{2}(\lambda_{N}(\alpha^{\prime},\beta^{\prime},n))-c_{2}(\lambda_{N}(\alpha,\beta,n))}\right].

2.7

2.9

2.10

\displaystyle E_{2}\leqslant

5
Large
g
asymptotics

In this section, we will prove Theorem
1.4
. The main idea will be the same as for Theorem
1.2
and
1.3
for
g\geqslant 2
, which is that only flat highest weights contribute to the limit. As in Section
4
, we first need an estimate of the partition function.

Theorem 5.1
.

For any
T>0
and any fixed integers
N\geqslant 2
and
k\geqslant 1
, as
g\to\infty
,

(53)

Z_{N}(g,T)=\theta(q_{T})+O(g^{-k}).

Proof.

Recall that

Z_{N}(g,T)=\sum_{\lambda\in\widehat{{\mathrm{U}}}(N)}q_{t}^{c_{2}(\lambda)}d_{\lambda}^{2-2g}=\sum_{n\in{\mathbb{Z}}}q_{T}^{n^{2}}+\sum_{\lambda\in\widehat{{\mathrm{U}}}(N)\setminus\Lambda_{N}}q_{T}^{c_{2}(\lambda)}d_{\lambda}^{2-2g}.

However, as
d_{\lambda}\geqslant N
for all
\lambda\notin\Lambda_{N}
,

0\leqslant\sum_{\lambda\in\widehat{{\mathrm{U}}}(N)\setminus\Lambda_{N}}q_{T}^{c_{2}(\lambda)}d_{\lambda}^{2-2g}\leqslant N^{2-2g}\sum_{\lambda\in\widehat{{\mathrm{U}}}(N)}q_{T}^{c_{2}(\lambda)}=N^{2-2g}Z_{N}(1,T).

As a consequence,

Z_{N}(g,T)=\theta(q_{T})+O(N^{2-2g}),

as
g\to\infty
. For any fixed
N
and
k
, as
g\to\infty,

N^{2-2g}
is dominated by
g^{-k}
, therefore the result follows.
∎

Proof of Theorem
1.4
.

Let us start with the formula (
4
). We can split the expectation on
\Lambda_{N}
and
\widehat{{\mathrm{U}}}(N)\setminus\Lambda_{N}
as in previous proofs.

{\mathbb{E}}[\mathrm{tr}(H_{\ell})]=\frac{1}{Z_{N}(g,T)}(A+B_{g}),

where

A=\sum_{n\in{\mathbb{Z}}}q_{T}^{n^{2}}\sum_{\mu\in\widehat{{\mathrm{U}}}(N):\mu\searrow(n,\ldots,n)}\frac{d_{\mu}}{N}q_{t}^{c_{2}(\mu)-n^{2}},

which does not depend on
g
, and

B_{g}=\sum_{\lambda\in\widehat{{\mathrm{U}}}(N)\setminus\Lambda_{N}}q_{T}^{c_{2}(\lambda)}\sum_{\mu\searrow\lambda}\frac{d_{\mu}}{Nd_{\lambda}^{2g-1}}.

Using again the bound
d_{\lambda}\geqslant N
for
\lambda\not\in\Lambda_{N}
, we have

B_{g}\leqslant N^{2-2g}\sum_{\lambda\in\widehat{{\mathrm{U}}}(N)\setminus\Lambda_{N}}q_{T}^{c_{2}(\lambda)}\sum_{\mu\searrow\lambda}\frac{d_{\mu}}{Nd_{\lambda}}.

From there, it is quite obvious that for any
k
,
B_{g}=O(g^{-k})
as
g\to\infty
. Now, let us compute
A
more precisely: given
n\in{\mathbb{Z}}
, there is only one
\mu\searrow(n,\ldots,n)
, which is
(n+1,n,\ldots,n)
. It can be written as
\lambda_{N}(\alpha,\beta,n)
with
\alpha=(1)
and
\beta=\varnothing
. It has dimension
N
and

c_{2}(\mu)-c_{2}(n,\ldots,n)=1+\frac{2n}{N}.

Hence,

A=\sum_{n\in{\mathbb{Z}}}q_{T}^{n^{2}}q_{t}^{1+\frac{2n}{N}}.

We then obtain

{\mathbb{E}}[\mathrm{tr}(H_{\ell})]=\frac{1}{\theta(q_{T})}\sum_{n\in{\mathbb{Z}}}q_{T}^{n^{2}}q_{t}^{\frac{2n}{N}+1}+O(g^{-k}),

which implies (
13
) if we use again Theorem
1.2
.

The proof of (
14
) is quite the same, but derived from (
5
): we obtain

{\mathbb{E}}[|\mathrm{tr}(H_{\ell})|^{2}]=\frac{1}{Z_{N}(g,T)}(A^{\prime}+B^{\prime}_{g}),

where

A^{\prime}=\sum_{n\in{\mathbb{Z}}}q_{T}^{n^{2}}\sum_{\mu\sim(n,\ldots,n)}\frac{d_{\mu}}{N^{2}}q_{t}^{c_{2}(\mu)-n^{2}},

and

B^{\prime}_{g}=O(g^{-k}),\quad\forall k\geqslant 1.

In order to compute
A^{\prime}
, let us notice that if
N\geqslant 2
, two highest weights of
{\mathrm{U}}(N)
satisfy
\mu\sim(n,\ldots,n)
: there is
(n,\ldots,n)
itself, as well as
(n+1,n,\ldots,n,n-1)
. The latter corresponds to
\lambda_{N}(\alpha,\beta,n)
with
\alpha=\beta=(1)
. We have

c_{2}(n+1,n,\ldots,n,n-1)-c_{2}(n,\ldots,n)=2.

Furthermore, one finds that
d_{\lambda_{N}((1),(1),n)}=N^{2}
. The expression of
A^{\prime}
can be simplified as follows:

A^{\prime}=\sum_{n\in{\mathbb{Z}}}q_{T}^{n^{2}}\left(\frac{1}{N^{2}}+q_{t}^{2}\right)=\theta(q_{T})\left(\frac{1}{N^{2}}+e^{-t}\right).

The result follows.
∎
