Algorithmic Analysis of Termination Problems for Nondeterministic Quantum Programs CCS: Theory of computation Verification by model checking CCS: Software and its engineering Formal software verification CCS: Security and privacy Logic and verification
Jianling Fu, Hui Jiang, Ming Xu, Yuxin Deng, Zhi-Bin Li

Abstract
. Verifying quantum programs has attracted a lot of interest in recent years. In this paper, we consider the following two categories of termination problems of quantum programs with nondeterminism, namely: (1) Is an input of a program terminating with probability one under all schedulers? If not, how can a scheduler be synthesized to evidence the nontermination? (2) Are all inputs terminating with probability one under their respective schedulers? If yes, a further question asks whether there is a scheduler that forces all inputs to be terminating with probability one together with how to synthesize it; otherwise, how can an input be provided to refute the universal termination? For the effective verification of the first category, we over-approximate the reachable set of quantum program states by the reachable subspace, whose algebraic structure is a linear space. On the other hand, we study the set of divergent states from which the program terminates with probability zero under some scheduler. The divergent set also has an explicit algebraic structure. Exploiting these explicit algebraic structures, we address the decision problem by a necessary and sufficient condition, i. e. the disjointness of the reachable subspace and the divergent set. Furthermore, the scheduler synthesis is completed in exponential time, whose bottleneck lies in computing the divergent set, reported for the first time. For the second category, we reduce the decision problem to the existence of invariant subspace, from which the program terminates with probability zero under all schedulers. The invariant subspace is characterized by linear equations and thus can be efficiently computed. The states on that invariant subspace are evidence of the nontermination. Furthermore, the scheduler synthesis is completed by seeking a pattern of finite schedulers that forces all inputs to be terminating with positive probability. The repetition of that pattern yields the desired universal scheduler that forces all inputs to be terminating with probability one. All the problems in the second category are shown, also for the first time, to be solved in polynomial time. Finally, we demonstrate the aforementioned methods via a running example — the quantum Bernoulli factory protocol.

Introduction

In the field of quantum computing,
physical devices have been rapidly developed in the last decades,
particularly in very recent years.
From the original experimental Deutsch’s problem on a working 2-qubit quantum computer in 1998
(
CGK98
)
,
to in 2020 Chinese quantum computer JiuZhang’s implementing a type of Boson sampling on 76 photons
(
ZWD+20
)
,
and in 2021 IBM’s releasing its latest 127-bit “Eagle” processor
(
IBM127
)
,
quantum computers have showed the advantage of computing
and attracted people to explore particular problems at a new level of complexity,
which cannot be achieved by their classical counterparts.

Equally important are quantum algorithms,
which are realized by quantum programming languages.
They build a bridge between the hardware devices and quantum algorithms to harness the power of quantum computers.
The first practical quantum programming language QCL appeared in
(
Omer98
)
.
The quantum guarded command language (qGCL)
was presented to program a “universal” quantum computer
(
SZ99
)
,
and a nondeterministic structure was embedded into qGCL in the follow-up work
(
Zul04
)
.
Selinger
(
Sel04
)
and Altenkirch and Grattage
(
AG05
)
respectively proposed
functional programming languages QFC and QML with high-level features.
Nowadays, several quantum programming languages,
e. g., Q#
(
qsharp
)
and Cirq
(
cirq
)
,
have been proposed for real-world applications,
enabling researchers to develop quantum software more conveniently and efficiently.

However, quantum programs have been found difficult to be tested or analyzed.
It is necessary to develop formal verification methods for quantum programs.
For classical program verification,
Morgan et al. extended the standard weakest precondition calculus
(
Dij76
)

into both probabilistic and nondeterministic settings
(
MMS96
)
.
Inspired by probabilistic predicate transformer,
D’Hondt and Panangaden
(
DHP06
)
directly proposed a quantum analogy of the weakest preconditions
for a particular quantum programming language (QPL),
which was further employed by
(
FDJY07
)
and
(
YDF10
)

in reasoning about the correctness of deterministic quantum programs.
The quantum weakest precondition was designed for reasoning about
the expected runtime of quantum program
(
LZBY22
)

and the expexted cost of various quantum resources
(
AMP+22
)
.
Meanwhile, Yu and Palsberg
(
YuP21
)
presented a framework of quantum abstract interpretation
(
CoC77
)

to check assertions for the properties of large-scale quantum programs.
More theoretically,
one can decompose “total correctness”
into “partial correctness” plus “termination” as advocated by C. A. R. Hoare
(
Hoa69
)
.
Hence termination analysis plays a central role in program verification,
which especially deserves studying when colliding with features in quantum setting.

In this paper,
we study the termination and the universal termination problems on nondeterministic quantum programs.
The former asks
whether
an input state
of a program terminating with probability one under all schedulers;
the latter asks
whether
all input states
are terminating with probability one under their respective schedulers.
We will give a series of positive results toward solving them.

Expressiveness on Program Models

First of all,
we consider two models of quantum Markov decision processes with states in finite-dimensional Hilbert spaces
that interpret the operational semantics of nondeterministic quantum programs.
One has finitely many program locations,
so that it is easier to model practical scenarios;
the other has only one.
We show that they are of the same expressiveness,
and thus adopt the latter for ease of verification.

Precise Over-approximation of the Reachable Set

In general, the set of reachable states of a program
does not exhibit any explicit algebraic structure,
which brings nontrivial hardness in verification.
To overcome it,
we give two definitions of reachable space
that over-approximate the set of reachable states.
The I-reachable space has the type of a subspace of the Hilbert space,
as proposed in
(
LYY14
)
,
which is spanned by those vector representations of reachable states;
the II-reachable space is spanned by those Hermitian matrix representations of reachable states.
Both are computable in polynomial time,
specified in terms of the size of program model as usual,
i. e. the dimension of Hilbert space.
But the latter is more precise,
which is validated by the running example of quantum Bernoulli factory protocol.

Algorithmic Complexity

Moreover, we study the set of divergent states
from which the program terminates with probability zero under some scheduler.
By exploiting the algebraic structure of the divergent set,
an effective approach is developed to compute it in exponential time.
Combining the reachable spaces and the divergent set,
the termination is decided by a necessary and sufficient condition,
i. e., the reachable subspace and the divergent set are disjoint.
The complexity of the decision procedure is in exponential time,
which is reported for the first time.

Scheduler Synthesis

If the termination is decided to be false,
we know there are some schedulers that force the program not to terminate with probability one.
Scheduler synthesis is particularly important to resolve the nondeterminism in system design.
To achieve this,
we confine the nontermination scheduler into finitely many
\omega
-regular ones as candidates.
Conditioning on each candidate,
we derive a system of linear equations by Brouwer’s fixedpoint theorem,
whose nonzero solutions help us to recognize the candidate as the nontermination scheduler.

We finally attack the universal termination problem.
It is reduced to the existence of invariant subspace,
from which the program terminates with probability zero under any scheduler.
The invariant subspace is also characterized by linear equations
and can be computed in polynomial time,
thus deciding the universal termination.
The states on that invariant subspace are evidence of the nontermination.
If the universal termination is decided to be true,
the scheduler synthesis is completed in polynomial time
by seeking a pattern of finite schedulers
that forces all input states to be terminating with positive probability.
The repetition of that pattern yields the desired universal scheduler
that forces all input states to be terminating with probability one.

Related Work

Verification on Probabilistic Programs

In contrast to deterministic programs,
probabilistic programs have several syntactic constructors —
probabilistic choice, nondeterministic choice and observation
(
KGJ+15
)
.
The termination problem yields many variants to be studied,
e. g.,

•

almost-sure termination
—
Does a program terminate with probability one?

•

positive almost-sure termination
—
Is the expected running time of a program finite?

Although the almost-sure termination of probabilistic programs was proved to be undecidable in general
(
KaK15
)
,
there are many approaches to attack it.
Fioriti and Hermanns
(

)
proposed
a framework to prove almost-sure termination
by

(
BAG14
)

Chakarov and Sankaranarayanan applied constraint-based techniques
to generate linear ranking super-martingales
(

)
.
Chatterjee
et al.

(

)
constructed polynomial ranking super-martingales extending linear ones.
A polynomial-time procedure was given in
(
ACN18
)

to synthesize lexicographic ranking super-martingales
for linear probabilistic programs.
Fu and Chatterjee
(
FuC19
)
applied ranking super-martingales to
study the positive almost-sure termination of nondeterministic probabilistic programs.
McIver and Morgan
(
McM06
)
generalized the
weakest preconditions
of Dijkstra
(an approach to prove correctness)
to the
weakest pre-expectations

for analyzing properties of

(
HSM97
)

and for establishing almost-sure termination
(
MMKK18
)
.
Kaminski
et al.

(
KKM+16
)
presented a calculus of weakest pre-expectation style
for obtaining bounds on the expected running time of probabilistic programs.
Verification tools
like
Amber

(
MBKK21
)

have been released to automatically prove almost-sure and positive almost-sure termination.
However, in the setting of quantum computing,
a program state is no longer simply a probabilistic distribution over program variables;
it is instead a density operator (positive semi-definite matrix with unit trace) on Hilbert space,
which would be considered in the following.
Moreover, the state space admitting the finite-dimensional Hilbert space
could bring extra sugar in our analysis,
breaking the undecidability for probabilistic programs.

Verification on Quantum Programs

Ying and Feng
(
YiF10
)
first studied the verification of quantum loop programs
by giving some necessary and sufficient conditions to ensure termination and almost-sure termination.
Later on, the classical Floyd–Hoare logic was extended in the quantum setting
to be quantum Floyd–Hoare logic

Ying11

and the Sharir–Pnueli–Hart method was also extended

The quantum Markov chain

XHF21

YiY18

could interpret the operational semantics of nondeterministic quantum programs.
Yu and Ying
(
YuY12
)
considered concurrent quantum programs,
and reduced the termination problem to the reachability problem of quantum Markov chains.

The work closest to ours is
(
LYY14
)
where Li
et al.
dealt with nondeterministic quantum programs.
The nondeterminism in that program is used to model quantum processes,
and the program execution relies on a scheduler specified by the users.
Given a nondeterministic quantum programs,
the set of reachable states from an input state has no explicit algebraic structure in general,
which yields nontrivial hardness in verification.
The authors of
(
LYY14
)
proposed a polynomial-time method

i) how to analyze the complexity of computing the divergent set

Recently, Li and Ying

LiY18

proposed the notions of additive and multiplicative invariants

They further synthesized linear ranking super-martingales

It was revealed in
(
LZBY22

almost-sure termination of a deterministic quantum program with an input state

There are also many works for verifying various kinds of quantum protocols

Contribution and Innovation

The contributions of the current paper are summarized as follows:

(1)

We propose a precise over-approximation of the reachable set,
which can be computed in polynomial time.

(2)

The complexity of computing the set of divergent states is given for the first time,
thus settling an open problem.

(3)

We decide the termination problem in exponential time
and synthesize a nontermination scheduler provided that it exists.

(4)

We decide the universal termination problem in polynomial time
together with the synthesis of a universal scheduler for termination.

To achive them, our technical innovations lie in:
i) using a tree construction for demonstrating the derivation of divergent states,
whose explicit structure could analyze the complexity of deciding termination;
ii) applying Knaster–Tarski fixedpoint theorem to ensure the efficiency of computing reachable spaces
while applying Brouwer’s fixedpoint theorem to ensure the effectivity of scheduler synthesis.

Preliminaries

Let
\mathbb{H}
be a finite Hilbert space
that is a complete vector space over complex numbers
\mathbb{C}
equipped with an inner product operation,
and
d
the dimension of
\mathbb{H}
throughout this paper.
We recall the standard Dirac notations from quantum computing.
Interested readers can refer to
(
NC00
)
for more details.

•

\left|{\psi}\right\rangle
stands for a unit column vector in
\mathbb{H}
labelled with
\psi
;

•

\left\langle{\psi}\right|:=\left|{\psi}\right\rangle^{\dagger}

(transpose and complex conjugate entrywise) of
\left|{\psi}\right\rangle
;

•

\left\langle{\psi_{1}}\middle|{\psi_{2}}\right\rangle:=\left\langle{\psi_{1}}\right|\left|{\psi_{2}}\right\rangle

\left|{\psi_{1}}\right\rangle

\left|{\psi_{2}}\right\rangle
;

•

\left|{\psi_{1}}\middle\rangle\middle\langle{\psi_{2}}\right|:=\left|{\psi_{1}}\right\rangle\otimes\left\langle{\psi_{2}}\right|
is the outer product
where
\otimes
denotes tensor product;

•

\left|{\psi,\psi^{\prime}}\right\rangle
is a shorthand of
the tensor product
\left|{\psi}\right\rangle\left|{\psi^{\prime}}\right\rangle=\left|{\psi}\right\rangle\otimes\left|{\psi^{\prime}}\right\rangle
.

Let
\{\left|{i}\right\rangle:i=1,2,\dots,d\}
be an orthonormal basis of
\mathbb{H}
.
Then any element
\left|{\psi}\right\rangle
, interpreted as a
state
, of
\mathbb{H}

can be entirely determined as
\left|{\psi}\right\rangle=\sum_{i=1}^{d}c_{i}\left|{i}\right\rangle
,
where
c_{i}\in\mathbb{C}
(
i=1,2,\dots,d
) satisfy the normalization condition
\sum_{i=1}^{d}|c_{i}|^{2}=1
.
If
\left|{\psi}\right\rangle
is linearly expressed by two or more elements
\left|{i}\right\rangle
with nonzero coefficients,
it is said to be a
super-position
of those elements
\left|{i}\right\rangle
.
For two spaces
\mathbb{S}
and
\mathbb{S}^{\prime}
,
the join
\mathbb{S}\vee\mathbb{S}^{\prime}
is the space spanned by the elements of
\mathbb{S}
and
\mathbb{S}^{\prime}
,

\mathrm{span}(\mathbb{S}\cup\mathbb{S}^{\prime})
.
For two quantum systems
\mathbb{H}
and
\mathbb{H}^{\prime}
,
the state space of their composite system is given by
the tensor product
\mathbb{H}\otimes\mathbb{H}^{\prime}

that is the Hilbert space spanned by the tensor products of elements in
\mathbb{H}
and
\mathbb{H}^{\prime}
,
i. e.
\mathrm{span}(\{\left|{\psi,\psi^{\prime}}\right\rangle:\left|{\psi}\right\rangle\in\mathbb{H}\wedge\left|{\psi^{\prime}}\right\rangle\in\mathbb{H}^{\prime}\})
,
equipped with the inner product

\langle\psi_{1},\psi_{1}^{\prime}\,|\,\psi_{2},\psi_{2}^{\prime}\rangle=\left\langle{\psi_{1}}\middle|{\psi_{2}}\right\rangle\langle\psi_{1}^{\prime}\,|\,\psi_{2}^{\prime}\rangle

for any
\left|{\psi_{1}}\right\rangle,\left|{\psi_{2}}\right\rangle\in\mathbb{H}
and
|\psi_{1}^{\prime}\rangle,|\psi_{2}^{\prime}\rangle\in\mathbb{H}^{\prime}
.

Linear Operators

Let
\gamma
be a linear operator on
\mathbb{H}
.
It is
Hermitian
if
\gamma=\gamma^{\dagger}
.
A Hermitian operator
\gamma
is entirely determined
by its
d
diagonal elements
\left\langle{i}\right|\gamma\left|{i}\right\rangle\in\mathbb{R}
(
i=1,2,\dots,d
)
and
d(d-1)/2
off-diagonal elements
\left\langle{i}\right|\gamma\left|{j}\right\rangle\in\mathbb{C}
with
1\leq i<j\leq d

for a total of
d^{2}
real numbers.
Let
\mathcal{H}(\mathbb{H})
be the set of Hermitian operators on
\mathbb{H}
.
For brevity, such a parameter
\mathbb{H}
in
\mathcal{H}(\mathbb{H})
can be omitted if it is clear from the context.
For a Hermitian operator
\gamma
, we have the spectral decomposition

\gamma=\sum_{i=1}^{d}\lambda_{i}\left|{\lambda_{i}}\middle\rangle\middle\langle{\lambda_{i}}\right|

where
\lambda_{i}\in\mathbb{R}
(
i=1,2,\dots,d
) are the eigenvalues of
\gamma

and
\left|{\lambda_{i}}\right\rangle
(taking the meaningful labels
\lambda_{i}
) are the corresponding eigenvectors.
The
support
of
\gamma
is the subspace of
\mathbb{H}
spanned by
all eigenvectors associated with nonzero eigenvalues,
i. e.
\mathrm{supp}(\gamma):=\mathrm{span}(\{\left|{\lambda_{i}}\right\rangle:i=1,2,\dots,d\wedge\lambda_{i}\neq 0\})
.
Although the spectral decomposition of
\gamma
may be not unique,
the support of
\gamma
must be unique,
since it is the orthocomplement of
the null space
\mathrm{span}(\{\left|{\psi}\right\rangle\in\mathbb{H}:\gamma\left|{\psi}\right\rangle=0\})
of
\gamma
.
So the notion of support is well defined.
A Hermitian operator
\gamma
is
positive

if
\left\langle{\psi}\right|\gamma\left|{\psi}\right\rangle\geq 0
holds for any
\left|{\psi}\right\rangle\in\mathbb{H}
.
A
projector

\mathbf{P}
is a positive operator of
the form
\sum_{i=1}^{m}\left|{\psi_{i}}\middle\rangle\middle\langle{\psi_{i}}\right|
with
m\leq d
,
where
\left|{\psi_{i}}\right\rangle
(
i=1,2,\dots,m
) are orthonormal.
It implies that all eigenvalues of
\mathbf{P}
are in
\{0,1\}
.

Quantum States

The
trace
of a linear operator
\gamma
is defined as

\mathrm{tr}(\gamma):=\sum_{i=1}^{d}\left\langle{\psi_{i}}\right|\gamma\left|{\psi_{i}}\right\rangle

for any orthonormal basis
\{\left|{\psi_{i}}\right\rangle:i=1,2,\dots,d\}
.
It is unique as it equals the sum of all eigenvalues of
\gamma
.
A
density
operator
\rho
is a positive operator with unit trace;
a partial density operator
\rho
is a positive operator with trace not greater than
1
.
Let
\mathcal{D}
be the set of density operators,
and
\mathcal{D}^{\leq 1}
the set of partial density operators.
For a density operator
\rho
,
we have the spectral decomposition

\rho=\sum_{i=1}^{m}\lambda_{i}\left|{\lambda_{i}}\middle\rangle\middle\langle{\lambda_{i}}\right|

where
\lambda_{i}
(
i=1,2,\dots,m
) are positive eigenvalues.
We call such eigenvectors
\left|{\lambda_{i}}\right\rangle

eigenstates
of
\rho
explained below.
The density operators are usually used to describe quantum states.
Under that decomposition,
it means that the quantum system is in state
\left|{\lambda_{i}}\right\rangle
with probability
\lambda_{i}
.
When
m=1
, we know that the system is surely in state
\left|{\lambda_{1}}\right\rangle
(with probability one),
which is the so-called
pure
state;
otherwise the state is
mixed
.
Both the vector notation
\left|{\lambda_{i}}\right\rangle
and the outer product notation
\left|{\lambda_{i}}\middle\rangle\middle\langle{\lambda_{i}}\right|

could be employed to denote pure states.
An alternative way to describe quantum states is
the
probabilistic ensemble

\{(\left|{\psi_{k}}\right\rangle,p_{k}):k=1,2,\ldots\}
with
\sum_{k}p_{k}=1
.
It means that the system is the mixture of being in state
\left|{\psi_{k}}\right\rangle
with probability weight
p_{k}
.
Here
\left|{\psi_{k}}\right\rangle
(
k=1,2,\ldots
) are not necessarily orthogonal.

Quantum Operations

Super-operators
\mathcal{E}
on
\mathbb{H}
are linear operators on the (ground) linear operators on
\mathbb{H}
.
Particularly, (completely-positive) super-operators are employed to describe quantum operations.
It is usually described by the Kraus representation
\mathcal{E}=\{\mathbf{E}_{i}:i=1,2,\dots,m\}
,
entailing that for a given density operator
\rho
,
we have
\mathcal{E}(\rho)=\sum_{i=1}^{m}\mathbf{E}_{i}\rho\mathbf{E}_{i}^{\dagger}
.
Here the number
m
of
Kraus operators

\mathbf{E}_{i}
could be bounded by
d^{2}

without loss of generality,
since the (ground) linear operators
\rho
are Hermitian,
i. e. a linear space of dimension
d^{2}
,
and thereby there are at most
d^{2}
linearly independent linear operators on that space.
We will use the bracket notation
\{\mathbf{E}_{i}:i=1,2,\dots,m\}

to denote (the Kraus representation of) a super-operator
\mathcal{E}
.
For two super-operators
\mathcal{E}=\{\mathbf{E}_{i}:i=1,2,\dots,m\}
and
\mathcal{E}^{\prime}=\{\mathbf{E}_{j}^{\prime}:j=1,2,\dots,m^{\prime}\}
,
their sum
\mathcal{E}+\mathcal{E}^{\prime}
is given by
\{\mathbf{E}_{i}:i=1,2,\dots,m\}\cup\{\mathbf{E}_{j}^{\prime}:j=1,2,\dots,m^{\prime}\}
;
their composition
\mathcal{E}\circ\mathcal{E}^{\prime}

with associative law
\mathcal{E}\circ\mathcal{E}^{\prime}(\rho)=\mathcal{E}(\mathcal{E}^{\prime}(\rho))

is
\{\mathbf{E}_{i}\mathbf{E}_{j}^{\prime}:i=1,2,\dots,m\wedge j=1,2,\dots,m^{\prime}\}
.
Let
\mathcal{I}
be the identity super-operator, and
\mathbf{I}
the identity operator.
A super-operator
\mathcal{E}
is
trace-preserving
, denoted
\mathcal{E}\eqsim\mathcal{I}
,
if
\sum_{i=1}^{m}\mathbf{E}_{i}^{\dagger}\mathbf{E}_{i}=\mathbf{I}
,
due to

\mathrm{tr}(\mathcal{E}(\rho))=\mathrm{tr}\left(\sum_{i=1}^{m}\mathbf{E}_{i}\rho\mathbf{E}_{i}^{\dagger}\right)=\mathrm{tr}\left(\sum_{i=1}^{m}\mathbf{E}_{i}^{\dagger}\mathbf{E}_{i}\rho\right)=\mathrm{tr}(\mathbf{I}\rho)=\mathrm{tr}(\rho);

it is
trace-nonincreasing
, denoted
\mathcal{E}\lesssim\mathcal{I}
,
if
\mathbf{I}-\sum_{i=1}^{m}\mathbf{E}_{i}^{\dagger}\mathbf{E}_{i}
is positive,
due to

\displaystyle\mathrm{tr}(\left|{\psi}\middle\rangle\middle\langle{\psi}\right|)-\mathrm{tr}(\mathcal{E}(\left|{\psi}\middle\rangle\middle\langle{\psi}\right|))

\displaystyle=\mathrm{tr}(\mathbf{I}\left|{\psi}\middle\rangle\middle\langle{\psi}\right|)-\mathrm{tr}\left(\sum_{i=1}^{m}\mathbf{E}_{i}^{\dagger}\mathbf{E}_{i}\left|{\psi}\middle\rangle\middle\langle{\psi}\right|\right)

\displaystyle=\mathrm{tr}\left(\left(\mathbf{I}-\sum_{i=1}^{m}\mathbf{E}_{i}^{\dagger}\mathbf{E}_{i}\right)\left|{\psi}\middle\rangle\middle\langle{\psi}\right|\right)=\left\langle{\psi}\right|\left(\mathbf{I}-\sum_{i=1}^{m}\mathbf{E}_{i}^{\dagger}\mathbf{E}_{i}\right)\left|{\psi}\right\rangle\geq 0.

Let
\mathcal{S}
be the set of super-operators,

\mathcal{S}^{\eqsim\mathcal{I}}
the set of trace-preserving ones,
and
\mathcal{S}^{\lesssim\mathcal{I}}
the set of trace-nonincreasing ones.

Quantum Measurement

A finite set of projectors
\mathbf{P}_{i}
with index
i
ranging over
\mathit{IDX}
forms a
projective measurement

if
\sum_{i\in\mathit{IDX}}\mathbf{P}_{i}=\mathbf{I}
.
The measurement aims to extract classical information from quantum states,
but it may destroy the quantum state.
Specifically, given a quantum state
\rho
,
after the above projective measurement,
we will get an outcome

i\in\mathit{IDX}
with probability
p_{i}=\mathrm{tr}(\mathbf{P}_{i}\rho)
;
when the outcome is
i
, the final state would be
collapsed
to
\mathbf{P}_{i}\rho\mathbf{P}_{i}/p_{i}
.
The measurement process is not reversible.
For a projector
\mathbf{P}_{i}
and a quantum state
\rho
,

\mathrm{tr}(\mathbf{P}_{i}\rho)=0
implies that the outcome
i
does not occur,
which holds if and only if
\mathrm{supp}(\mathbf{P}_{i})
is orthogonal to
\mathrm{supp}(\rho)
.
For a super-operator
\mathcal{E}=\{\mathbf{E}_{i}:i=1,2,\ldots,m\}
and a pure state
\left|{\psi}\middle\rangle\middle\langle{\psi}\right|
,
we have
\mathrm{supp}(\mathcal{E}(\left|{\psi}\middle\rangle\middle\langle{\psi}\right|))=\mathrm{span}(\{\mathbf{E}_{i}\left|{\psi}\right\rangle:i=1,2,\ldots,m\})
.
Finally we would mention a useful inclusion:

(1)

\mathrm{supp}(\mathcal{E}(\left|{\psi}\middle\rangle\middle\langle{\psi}\right|))\subseteq\bigvee_{k=1}^{K}\mathrm{supp}(\mathcal{E}(\left|{\psi_{k}}\middle\rangle\middle\langle{\psi_{k}}\right|))

holds for any
\left|{\psi}\right\rangle\in\mathrm{span}(\{\left|{\psi_{k}}\right\rangle:k=1,2,\ldots,K\})
.
It follows from the fact:
Assume the RHS of (
1
) is a proper subspace of
\mathbb{H}
;
otherwise the inclusion follows trivially.
Let
|\psi^{\perp}\rangle
be an element of
\mathbb{H}

orthogonal to all
\mathrm{supp}(\mathcal{E}(\left|{\psi_{k}}\middle\rangle\middle\langle{\psi_{k}}\right|))
with
k=1,2,\ldots,K
.
It is also orthogonal to
\mathbf{E}_{i}\left|{\psi_{k}}\right\rangle

for each
i=1,2,\ldots,m
and
k=1,2,\ldots,K
,
i. e.
\langle\psi^{\perp}|\mathbf{E}_{i}\left|{\psi_{k}}\right\rangle=0
.
It implies that
\langle\psi^{\perp}|\mathbf{E}_{i}\left|{\psi}\right\rangle=0
holds for each
i=1,2,\ldots,m
,
and thus
|\psi^{\perp}\rangle
is orthogonal to
\mathrm{supp}(\mathcal{E}(\left|{\psi}\middle\rangle\middle\langle{\psi}\right|))
.

3.
Program Model and Termination Problems

In this section,
we introduce a nondeterministic extension of a quantum
while
-language,
and interpret its operational semantics by two models of quantum Markov decision processes (quantum MDPs).
One model is more complicated but easier to model practical scenarios
while the other is simpler and easier to be verified.
They are shown to have the same expressiveness.
For ease of verification,
we will adopt the latter to represent nondeterministic quantum programs later on.
Finally, we introduce the termination problems of nondeterministic quantum programs considered in the paper.

3.1.
Program Model

Definition 3.1 (
(
Yin16
, Chapter 6)
).

A nondeterministic quantum program is generated by the following syntax:

\displaystyle S\ \triangleq\ q:=\left|{0}\right\rangle\,|

\displaystyle\bar{q}:=U[\bar{q}]\,|\,S_{1};S_{2}\,|\,\square_{j=1}^{m}\ S_{j}

\displaystyle\,|

\displaystyle\mathbf{if}\ \mathbf{M}[\bar{q}]=\mathbf{true}\ \mathbf{then}\ S_{1}

\displaystyle\,|

\displaystyle\mathbf{while}\ \mathbf{M}[\bar{q}]=\mathbf{true}\ \mathbf{do}\ S_{1}\kern 5.0pt.

We briefly explain the syntax as follows:

•

The initialization “
q:=\left|{0}\right\rangle
” sets quantum variable
q
to the basis state
\left|{0}\right\rangle
on
\mathbb{H}_{q}
,
where the Hilbert space
\mathbb{H}_{q}
of
q
is supposed to
have basis states
\left|{0}\right\rangle,\left|{1}\right\rangle
for
q
being Boolean
and
\left|{0}\right\rangle,\left|{1}\right\rangle,\ldots,\left|{k-1}\right\rangle
for
q
being integer.
For any appointed pure state
\left|{\psi}\right\rangle\in\mathbb{H}
,
there is a unitary operation
\mathbf{U}

such that the state
\left|{\psi}\right\rangle=\mathbf{U}\left|{0}\right\rangle
can be prepared.

•

The assignment “
\bar{q}:=U[\bar{q}]
” performs the unitary transformation
U
on the register
\bar{q}
.
For operations in classical programs,
we can implement the quantum analogue by constructing a corresponding unitary operator.
For example,
we can take the unitary operation
\mathbf{U}_{+1}=\sum_{i=0}^{2^{32}-1}|i+1\mod 2^{32}\rangle\langle i|

as the quantum counterpart to implement the classical increment assignment
x:=x+1

on a
32
-bit integer
x
.

•

The statement “
S_{1};S_{2}
” represents the sequential composition.

•

The nondeterministic choice “
\square_{j=1}^{m}\ S_{j}
” means that
a subprogram
S_{j}
(
j=1,2,\ldots,m
) is nondeterministically chosen to execute.
The nondeterminism will be resolved by
some action
\alpha_{j}
from the set
\mathbf{ACT}=\{\alpha_{1},\alpha_{2},\ldots,\alpha_{m}\}
,
indicating the subprogram
S_{j}
to be executed.

•

The statement

\mathbf{if}\ \mathbf{M}[\bar{q}]=\mathbf{true}\ \mathbf{then}\ S_{1}

is a quantum analogue of the classical condition statement.
As the guard condition,
a quantum measurement
\mathbf{M}=\{\mathbf{M}_{\mathbf{true}},\mathbf{M}_{\mathbf{false}}\}

is performed on the register
\bar{q}
.
If the outcome of the measurement is
\mathbf{true}
whose probability is
p_{\mathbf{true}}=\mathrm{tr}(\mathbf{M}_{\mathbf{true}}[\bar{q}])
,
the state in register
\bar{q}
is collapsed into
\bar{q}_{\mathbf{true}}:=\mathbf{M}_{\mathbf{true}}[\bar{q}]/p_{\mathbf{true}}
,
and the subprogram
S_{1}
will be applied to
\bar{q}_{\mathbf{true}}
,
resulting in
S_{1}[\bar{q}_{\mathbf{true}}]
;
otherwise the state in register
\bar{q}
is collapsed into
\bar{q}_{\mathbf{false}}:=\mathbf{M}_{\mathbf{false}}[\bar{q}]/p_{\mathbf{false}}

where
p_{\mathbf{false}}=\mathrm{tr}(\mathbf{M}_{\mathbf{false}}[\bar{q}])

and the subprogram
S_{1}
will not be applied.
It is easy to see
p_{\mathbf{true}}+p_{\mathbf{false}}=1
.
Note that if
p_{\mathbf{true}}=0
(resp.
p_{\mathbf{false}}=0
),
meaning the outcome
\mathbf{true}
(resp.
\mathbf{false}
) cannot be observed,
we do not need to further consider this branch.
In the viewpoint of static analysis,
the resulting state would be the mixture of
S_{1}[\bar{q}_{\mathbf{true}}]

and
\bar{q}_{\mathbf{false}}
with probability
p_{\mathbf{true}}
and
p_{\mathbf{false}}
, respectively.

•

The loop statement

\mathbf{while}\ \mathbf{M}[\bar{q}]=\mathbf{true}\ \mathbf{do}\ S_{1}

admits a projective measurement
\mathbf{M}=\{\mathbf{M}_{\mathbf{true}},\mathbf{M}_{\mathbf{false}}\}
as the guard condition,
so that exactly one of the two outcome
\mathbf{true}
and
\mathbf{false}
would occur
after performing that measurement
\mathbf{M}
.
If the outcome
\mathbf{false}
is observed,
the program ends in the collapsed state
\bar{q}_{\mathbf{false}}
;
if the outcome
\mathbf{true}
occurs,
the subprogram
S_{1}
will be applied to
\bar{q}_{\mathbf{true}}
and repeat the above process.
All in all, the resulting state would be a mixture of countably many states in static analysis.

A nondeterministic quantum program is a
finite
statement
generated by the syntax in Definition
3.1
.
For a program
\mathcal{P}
,
we could assign each statement with a program location
as specified in the left column of Table
3.1
,
in which the nondeterministic choice, the condition and the loop statements are of appropriate wrapping.
All of such locations are collected into the finite set
\mathbf{LOC}=\{\ell_{i}:i=1,2,\ldots,n\}
,
in which
\ell_{n}
is the end location, indicating no statement to be executed.
Let
\mathbf{VAR}
be the set of program variables of
\mathcal{P}
,
and
\mathbf{ACT}
the set of actions that resolve the nondeterministic choices.
For a variable
q\in\mathbf{VAR}
, the state space is
\mathbb{H}_{q}
.
The state space
\mathbb{H}_{\mathbf{VAR}}
of all program variables is simply
the tensor product of individual state spaces
\mathbb{H}_{q}
,
i. e.
\mathbb{H}_{\mathbf{VAR}}=\bigotimes_{q\in\mathbf{VAR}}\mathbb{H}_{q}
.
