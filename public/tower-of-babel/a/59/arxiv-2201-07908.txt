Markov decision processes with observation costs: framework and computation with a penalty scheme
Christoph Reisinger, Jonathan Tam

Abstract
We consider Markov decision processes where the state of the chain is only given at chosen observation times and of a cost. Optimal strategies involve the optimisation of observation times as well as the subsequent action values. We consider the finite horizon and discounted infinite horizon problems, as well as an extension with parameter uncertainty. By including the time elapsed from observations as part of the augmented Markov system, the value function satisfies a system of quasi-variational inequalities (QVIs). Such a class of QVIs can be seen as an extension to the interconnected obstacle problem. We prove a comparison principle for this class of QVIs, which implies uniqueness of solutions to our proposed problem. Penalty methods are then utilised to obtain arbitrarily accurate solutions. Finally, we perform numerical experiments on three applications which illustrate our framework.

Introduction

In this article, we examine the observation cost model (OCM) for Markov Decision Processes (MDPs). A cost must be paid in order to observe the state of the underlying MDP, and only then can adjustments be made to the action which influences the dynamics of the MDP. We propose a penalty scheme for efficient numerical computation for the resulting system of equations.

MDPs are mathematical tools that model the optimisation of a random process, in order to maximise the expected profit over time. Applications are common in maintenance, portfolio optimisation, sensor detection, reinforcement learning and more. Most setups implicitly assume a fixed source of information upon which the user relies to select an optimal action. However, such a steady stream of information might not be available in situations where resources are constrained, either by the expensive cost of measurements, or by the impracticality of frequent sampling. This calls for an extra layer of optimisation, where the user has to decide on the optimal observation times of the information source, as well as the optimal sequence of actions to maximise the expected returns.

The inclusion of time elapsed as a variable for the augmented Markovian system leads to a system of discrete quasi-variational inequalities (QVI). We assume that the Markov chain takes values in a finite state space
\mathcal{X}
and that its dynamics are known and are given by the transition matrices
\{P_{a}\}_{a\in A}

A
is a finite action set. We also assume that the actions can only be adjusted at the observation times. The one-step reward function is given by
r(x,a)=r_{a,x}

\displaystyle\min\bigg\{

\displaystyle v^{n}_{a,x}-\gamma v^{n+1}_{a,x}-\Big(P^{n}_{a}\ r_{a}\Big)_{x}\ ,

\displaystyle v^{n}_{a,x}-\bigg(P^{n}_{a}\ \overline{\gamma v^{1}+r}\bigg)_{x}+c_{\mathrm{obs}}\bigg\}=0,

(1.1)

where
v
is the value function, indexed by:

, the state of the chain at the previous observation;

, the time elapsed since the previous observation; and

, the action applied at the previous observation. The vector
\left(\overline{\gamma v^{1}+r}\right)_{x}=\max_{a\in A}(\gamma v^{1}_{a,x}+r_{a,x})
represents the ‘inner loop’ optimisation over the space of actions after an observation is made.

]

constrained resources — e.g., long NHS (National Health Service in the UK) waiting times
especially after the pandemic — which clearly lead to suboptimal times of scans (observations)
and treatment (actions). Suboptimal actions can also arise due to users’ general inattentiveness and inertia to reacting quickly to information

The main contributions of our paper are as follows:

–

We formulate the observation cost model (OCM) for Markov decision processes where the time elapsed after an observation is considered as part of the augmented Markov system. We present the optimality equations obtained from dynamic programming for the finite horizon problem, discounted infinite horizon problem, and the respective problems with parameter uncertainty. In all cases the optimality equations are in the form of a QVI, which are structurally different to the Bellman-type equations from existing approaches in the literature.

–

We establish a comparison principle for the class of QVIs (
1.2
), of which the solution to the OCM belongs to. The class of QVIs are a generalisation of monotone systems with interconnected obstacles as seen in
[
28
]
. We propose a penalty scheme for this class of QVIs (
1.2
), and demonstrate the monotone convergence of the penalised solutions towards the solutions of said QVI, thereby establishing constructively the existence of solutions.

–

We demonstrate the numerical performance of our model by applying it to the time-discretised version of the HIV-treatment model
[
34
]
. Our framework is compatible with the original results, and also shows qualitatively different optimal behaviour when dealing with large observation gaps.

1.1
Notation for MDPs and POMDPs

As the goal of the next section is to layout the OCM precisely by formulating the model in terms of a POMDP, we shall quote here some of the standard notation for MDPs, POMDPs and a brief overview of its construction. These are largely taken from
[
15
, Ch 4]
and we refer the reader to the references within for further detail.

We will generally be considering Markov decision processes on finite state spaces. Whilst most arguments extend naturally to more general state spaces, we shall focus on the finite setting here to retain a simplified presentation. Let
\mathcal{P}(\mathcal{X})
denote the space of probability measures over a set
\mathcal{X}
. If
\mathcal{X}
is finite, we will also identify
\mathcal{P}(\mathcal{X})
with the simplex
\Delta_{\mathcal{X}}
.

Definition 1.3
.

A Markov control model is a tuple
\langle\mathcal{X},A,p,r\rangle
, where

–

\mathcal{X}
is the finite
underlying state space
;

–

A
is the finite
action space
;

–

p:\mathcal{X}\times A\to\Delta_{\mathcal{X}}
is the
transition kernel
;

–

r:\mathcal{X}\times A\to\mathbb{R}
is the
one-step reward function
.

At each time
t
, a state
x_{t}\in\mathcal{X}
is observed. The controller chooses an action
a_{t}\in A
and receives a reward
r(x_{t},a_{t})
. The system then moves to a new state
x_{t+1}\in\mathcal{X}
with probability
p(x_{t+1}|x_{t},a_{t})
and the process repeats at time
t+1
. Actions are chosen according to a
policy

\pi=(\pi_{t})_{t}
, which is a sequence of kernels
\pi_{t}:H_{t}\to A
, where
H_{0}\coloneqq\mathcal{X}
and
H_{t}\coloneqq(\mathcal{X}\times A)^{t}\times\mathcal{X}
for
t\geq 1
, known as the
history set
at time
t
. The set of all policies is denoted by
\Pi
. Given an initial state
x_{0}\in\mathcal{X}
and policy
\pi\in\Pi
, by the Ionescu-Tulcea theorem (see
[
15
, Appendix C]
), there exists a unique probability measure
\mathbb{P}^{\pi}_{x_{0}}
on the canonical sample space
\Omega\coloneqq H_{\infty}\coloneqq(\mathcal{X}\times A)^{\infty}
, such that given
\omega=(x_{0},a_{0},x_{1},a_{1},\ldots)\in\Omega
,

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}^{\pi}_{x_{0}}(\omega)=\delta(x_{0})\ \pi_{0}(a_{0}\mid x_{0})\ p(x_{1}\mid x_{0},a_{0})\ \pi(a_{1}\mid x_{0},a_{0},x_{1})\ldots

The objective is to maximise an objective function over the set of policies
\Pi
, for example, in the finite horizon case,

\displaystyle J(\pi,x_{0})=\mathbb{E}^{\pi}_{x_{0}}\left[\sum^{N}_{n=0}r(x_{n},a_{n})\right],\quad\pi\in\Pi,\ x_{0}\in\mathcal{X},

where
N\in\mathbb{N}
is the time horizon, and
\mathbb{E}^{\pi}_{x_{0}}
is the expectation under the measure
\mathbb{P}^{\pi}_{x_{0}}
. The value function is given by

\displaystyle v(x)=\sup_{\pi\in\Pi}J(\pi,x),\quad x\in\mathcal{X}.

It is well known that an optimal policy
\pi^{*}
for an MDP is deterministic and Markovian, i.e. there exists deterministic functions
\{\phi_{t}\}_{t\geq 0}
such that for
h_{t}=(x_{0},a_{0},\ldots,x_{t})\in H_{t}
,
\pi^{*}_{t}(h_{t})=\phi_{t}(x_{t})
, and
v(x)=J(\pi^{*},x)
.

In many cases, rather than having full information of the MDP, one instead has access to noisy observations correlated to the underlying MDP. This gives rise to the notion of partially observable Markov decision processes (POMDPs), which can also be described by a given tuple as follows.

Definition 1.4
.

A partially observable control model is a tuple
\langle\mathcal{X},\mathcal{O},A,p,p_{0},q,q_{0},r\rangle
, where

–

\mathcal{X}
is the finite
state space
;

–

\mathcal{O}
is the finite
observation space
;

–

A
is the finite
action space
;

–

p:\mathcal{X}\times A\to\Delta_{\mathcal{X}}
is the
transition kernel
;

–

p_{0}\in\Delta_{\mathcal{X}}
is the
initial distribution
;

–

q:A\times\mathcal{X}\to\Delta_{\mathcal{O}}
is the
observation kernel
;

–

q_{0}:\mathcal{X}\to\Delta_{\mathcal{O}}
is the
initial observation kernel
;

–

r:\mathcal{X}\times A\to[0,\infty)
is the
one-step reward function
.

In this setting, given an underlying state
x_{t}\in\mathcal{X}
, an observation
\bar{x}_{t}\in\mathcal{O}
is generated according to the observation kernel
q(\bar{x}_{t}|a_{t-1},x_{t})
. The controller chooses an action
a_{t}\in A
based on their observations, rather than the values of the underlying states. For this, define the
observable history sets

\displaystyle\mathcal{H}_{0}\coloneqq\mathcal{O},\ \mathcal{H}_{t}\coloneqq(\mathcal{O}\times A)^{t}\times\mathcal{O},\quad t\geq 1.

A policy for a POMDP is now a sequence of kernels
\pi_{t}:\mathcal{H}_{t}\to A
. Denote the set of policies for the POMDP as
\Pi_{\mathrm{po}}
. By the Ionescu-Tulcea theorem again, given an initial distribution
p_{0}\in\Delta_{\mathcal{X}}
and policy
\pi\in\Pi_{\mathrm{po}}
, there exists a unique probability measure
\mathbb{P}^{\pi}_{p_{0}}
on the canonical space
\Omega=(\mathcal{X}\times\mathcal{O}\times A)^{\infty}
such that for
\omega=(x_{0},\bar{x}_{0},a_{0},x_{1},\bar{x}_{1},a_{1},\ldots)\in\Omega
,

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}^{\pi}_{p_{0}}(\omega)=p_{0}(x_{0})\ q_{0}(\bar{x}_{0}\mid x_{0})\ \pi_{0}(a_{0}\mid\bar{x}_{0})\ p(x_{1}\mid x_{0},a_{0})\ q(\bar{x}_{1}\mid x_{1},a_{0})\ \pi(x_{1}\mid\bar{x}_{0},a_{0},\bar{x}_{1})\ldots

The maximisation is now performed over
\pi\in\Pi_{\mathrm{po}}
, with the objective and value function

\displaystyle J(\pi,p_{0})=\mathbb{E}^{\pi}_{p_{0}}\left[\sum^{N}_{n=0}r(x_{n},a_{n})\right],\ v(p)=\sup_{\pi\in\Pi_{\mathrm{po}}}J(\pi,p),\quad\pi\in\Pi_{\mathrm{po}},\ p,p_{0}\in\Delta_{\mathcal{X}}.

Without knowledge of the underlying states, the POMDP is a non-Markovian problem. The standard approach to solve a POMDP is to consider an equivalent (fully observable) problem, known as the
belief MDP
, on the space
Z\coloneqq\Delta_{\mathcal{X}}
. The Markovian strucutre is recovered when lifted to the belief MDP, so that classical dynamic programming techniques can be applied. The transition kernel for the
belief state

z=(z_{t})_{t}
can be constructed as follows: given the POMDP, construct a kernel
R:Z\times A\to\mathcal{X}\times\mathcal{O}
such that

\displaystyle R(x,\bar{x}\mid z,a)=q(\bar{x}\mid a,{x}^{\prime})p({x}^{\prime}\mid x,a)z(x),\quad(x,\bar{x})\in\mathcal{X}\times\mathcal{O},\ (z,a)\in Z\times A.

It can be shown that there exists a kernel
{H}^{\prime}:Z\times A\to\mathcal{X}
, such that
R
can be disintegrated into

\displaystyle R(x,\bar{x}\mid z,a)={H}^{\prime}(x\mid z,a,\bar{x}){R}^{\prime}(\bar{x}\mid z,a),\quad(x,\bar{x})\in\mathcal{X}\times\mathcal{O},\ (z,a)\in Z\times A.

where
{R}^{\prime}
is the marginal of
R
on
\mathcal{O}
. Then, letting
H_{z,a,\bar{x}}={H}^{\prime}(\cdot\mid z,a,\bar{x})\in Z
, define the kernel
{q}^{\prime}:Z\times A\to Z
by

\displaystyle{q}^{\prime}({z}^{\prime}\mid z,a)=\sum_{\bar{x}\in\mathcal{O}}\delta_{H_{z,a,\bar{x}}}({z}^{\prime}){R}^{\prime}(\bar{x}\mid z,a),\quad z,{z}^{\prime}\in Z,\ a\in A.

Then one takes
{q}^{\prime}
as the transition kernel for the belief state, and construct the initial kernel
{q}^{\prime}_{0}
analogously. The belief MDP is then
\langle Z,A,{q}^{\prime},{q}^{\prime}_{0},r_{z}\rangle
, where
r_{z}:Z\times A\to\mathbb{R}
as
r_{z}(z,a)=\sum_{x\in\mathcal{X}}r(x,a)z(x)
. The belief state can be interpreted as the conditional distribution of the underlying state
x_{t}
, given the observed history
(\bar{x}_{0},a_{0},\ldots,\bar{x}_{t})
. Let
\Pi^{z}
be the set of policies for the belief MDP, which are now a sequence of kernels on
A
, given the history sets
\mathcal{H}^{z}_{t}=(Z\times A)^{t}\times Z
. The objective and value function for the belief MDP are given by

\displaystyle J_{z}(\pi^{z},z_{0})\coloneqq\mathbb{E}^{\pi^{z}}_{z_{0}}\left[\sum^{N}_{n=0}r_{z}(z_{n},a_{n})\right],\ v_{z}(z)=\sup_{\pi^{z}\in\Pi^{z}}J_{z}(\pi^{z},z),\quad\pi^{z}\in\Pi^{z},\ z,z_{0}\in Z,

where
\mathbb{E}^{\pi^{z}}_{z_{0}}
is the expectation over the canonical space
(Z\times A)^{\infty}
under the policy
\pi^{z}
and initial condition
z_{0}\in Z
. It can then be shown that policies in
\Pi_{\mathrm{po}}
are equivalent to policies in
\Pi^{z}
, in the sense that any
h_{t}\in\mathcal{H}_{t}
can be mapped to a corresponding
h^{z}_{t}\in\mathcal{H}^{z}_{t}
, so that given
\pi^{z}\in\Pi^{z}
, one can construct a corresponding
\pi\in\Pi_{\mathrm{po}}
via

\displaystyle\pi(\cdot\mid h_{t})\coloneqq\pi^{z}(\cdot\mid h^{z}_{t}),

and the conditional probabilities assigned on the action set
A
are the same. Moreover,
\pi^{*}\in\Pi_{\mathrm{po}}
is optimal for
J
if and only if
\pi^{*,z}\in\Pi^{z}
is optimal for
J_{z}
, and it holds that

\displaystyle v_{z}(z)=J_{z}(\pi^{*,z},z)=J(\pi^{*},z)=v(z),\quad z\in Z=\Delta_{\mathcal{X}}.

Thus, when considering a POMDP, it is sufficient to consider its equivalent MDP in the belief state, of which the optimal policy is Markovian with respect to
z=(z_{t})_{t}
.

2
Problem formulation

In the Observation Cost Model, the process evolves sequentially as follows. At each time
t
, the controller decides if they would like to pay
an observation cost

c_{\mathrm{obs}}>0
to observe the state
x_{t}\in\mathcal{X}
. If they decide to do so, then the controller applies the action
a_{t}\in A
according to some suitable policy
\pi
, and receives a reward
r(x_{t},a_{t})
. If they decide not to observe, then no cost is paid, but the controller cannot change the action value, so that
a_{t}=a_{t-1}
. We assume that the reward
r(x_{t},a_{t})
is collected and ‘locked in’ at time
t
, but is not observable to the user if
x_{t}
is not observable. In both instances, the system moves to a new state
x_{t+1}\in\mathcal{X}
according to the transition kernel
p(x_{t+1}\mid x_{t},a_{t})
.

We now formally write down the objective function, and shall make precise the terms appearing within in the rest of this section. In view of the description above, the controller wishes to maximise (for example, in the finite horizon case)

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}^{\pi}\left[\sum^{N}_{n=0}\left(r(x_{n},a_{n})-i_{n}\cdot c_{\mathrm{obs}}\right)\right],

(2.1)

where
\pi
a suitably admissible control policy to be made precise later. The sequence
i=(i_{n})_{n}
takes values in
\mathcal{I}\coloneqq\{0,1\}
and will be referred to as the
inspection values
. A value of
i_{n}=1
represents an observation made at time
n
, so that observation cost
c_{\mathrm{obs}}
is deducted from the total reward in (
2.1
). Conversely no observations are made if
i_{n}=0
.

We now proceed to establish the OCM as a non-standard form of a POMDP, in order to fully make sense of (
2.1
). A policy should output an action value
a_{n}\in A
, as well as an inspection value
i_{n}\in\mathcal{I}
. The observation space is represented by
\mathcal{X}\cup\{\varnothing\}
: either the underlying chain with values in
\mathcal{X}
is observed, or the dummy variable
\varnothing
nothing is observed, which represents the case of no observations. A final but subtle point is the difference in the sequential structure of the OCM compared to a POMDP. In the case of a POMDP, first a state
x_{n}
is generated, follow by the observation
\bar{x}_{n}
, and then the action
a_{n}
. Thus a realisation on the canonical space of a POMDP looks like:

\displaystyle(x_{0},\bar{x}_{0},a_{0},x_{1},\bar{x}_{1},a_{1},\ldots).

In the OCM, as depicted in
Figure

2.1
, the observation occurs
after
the inspection value, after which the action value follows. Thus a realisation of the system of an OCM will instead look like

\displaystyle(x_{0},i_{0},\bar{x}_{0},a_{0},x_{1},i_{1},\bar{x}_{1},a_{1},\ldots).

(2.2)

In order to obtain the sequential structure of ‘state - observation - action’ for the OCM, we augment the sequence (
2.2
) with fictitious state and observation values, and treat both
a_{n}
and
i_{n}
as an ‘action’. By suitably augmenting the transition and observation kernels, the OCM takes the form of a POMDP, over the timescale of
\frac{1}{2}\mathbb{N}
. This augmented sequence then takes the form of

\displaystyle(x_{0},\bar{x}_{0},\pi_{0},x_{1/2},\bar{x}_{1/2},\pi_{1/2},x_{1},\bar{x}_{1},\pi_{1},\ldots)

\displaystyle\coloneqq

\displaystyle\ (x_{0},\bar{x}_{0},i_{0},x_{0},\bar{x}_{0},a_{0},x_{1},\bar{x}_{1},i_{1},\ldots).

This leads us to the following definition.

Definition 2.1
.

Given an MDP
\langle\mathcal{X},A,p,r\rangle
, the associated observation cost model (OCM) is defined as the POMDP
\langle\mathcal{X},\mathcal{X}_{\varnothing},\mathcal{A},p,p_{0},q,q_{0},r_{\mathrm{obs}}\rangle
(see
1.4
), on the time scale
\frac{1}{2}\mathbb{N}=\{0,1/2,1,\ldots\}
, where

–

\mathcal{X}_{\varnothing}=\mathcal{X}\ \cup\{\varnothing\}
is the
observation space
, with
\varnothing
a dummy variable representing no observations.

–

\mathcal{A}
is the
disjoint
union of
A
and
\mathcal{I}
, with time dependent admissible sets given by
\mathcal{A}(n)=\mathcal{I}
and
\mathcal{A}(n+\frac{1}{2})=A
;

–

p:\mathcal{X}\times\mathcal{A}\to\Delta_{\mathcal{X}}
is the transition kernel, with its domain extended to
\mathcal{X}\times\mathcal{A}
by defining

\displaystyle p(\cdot\mid x,i)=\delta_{x}(\cdot),\quad i\in\mathcal{I}.

–

q:\mathcal{A}\times\mathcal{X}\to\Delta_{\mathcal{X}_{\varnothing}}
is the
observation kernel
,
given by

\displaystyle q(\cdot\mid a,x)

\displaystyle=\delta_{\varnothing}(\cdot),\quad a\in A,

\displaystyle q(\cdot\mid i,x)

\displaystyle=i\cdot\delta_{x}(\cdot)+(1-i)\delta_{\varnothing}(\cdot),\quad i\in\mathcal{I}.

–

r_{\mathrm{obs}}:\mathcal{X}\times\mathcal{A}\to\mathbb{R}
is the
one-step reward function
given by

\displaystyle r_{\mathrm{obs}}(x,a)

\displaystyle=r(x,a),\quad a\in A,

\displaystyle r_{\mathrm{obs}}(x,i)

\displaystyle=-i\cdot c_{\mathrm{obs}},\quad i\in\mathcal{I}.

The kernels
p
and
q
above are defined such that transitions of the underlying chain only occurs at the integer steps, and new observations at the half steps. Let us write the observable history sets here as

\displaystyle\mathcal{H}_{0}=\mathcal{X}_{\varnothing},\ \mathcal{H}_{t}=(\mathcal{X}_{\varnothing}\times\mathcal{A})^{2t}\times\mathcal{X}_{\varnothing},\quad t\in\frac{1}{2}\mathbb{N}.

To be precise, we should only consider
admissible
history sets, that is state-action pairs that satisfy the constraints
\mathcal{A}(n)=\mathcal{I}
and
\mathcal{A}(n+\frac{1}{2})=A
, but we shall take this assumption as implicit for ease of notation. A policy
\pi
is hence a sequence of kernels
\pi_{t}:\mathcal{H}_{t}\to\mathcal{A}
.

As before, a policy
\pi
as defined above and an initial distribution
p_{0}\in\Delta_{\mathcal{X}}
induces a unique measure
\mathbb{P}^{\pi}_{p_{0}}
on the canonical sample space
\Omega=(\mathcal{X}\times\mathcal{X}_{\varnothing}\times\mathcal{A})^{\infty}
. With slight abuse of notation, we also write
\pi
for the values applied by the policy. Then, the action values
a=(a_{n})_{n}
and inspection values
i=(i_{n})_{n}
appearing in (
2.1
) can be recovered by defining

\displaystyle i_{n}=\pi_{n},\quad a_{n}=\pi_{n+\frac{1}{2}}.

As we are assuming in the OCM that actions remain constant between new observations. We will have to consider a smaller class of admissible policies.

Definition 2.2
.

An
admissible policy

\pi=(\pi_{t})_{t}
for the OCM is a sequence of kernels
\pi_{t}:\mathcal{H}_{t}\to\Delta_{\mathcal{A}}
which satisfies the following: for
n\in\mathbb{N}
, if
h_{n+\frac{1}{2}}=(\bar{x}_{0},\pi_{0},\ldots,\bar{x}_{n-\frac{1}{2}},\pi_{n-\frac{1}{2}},\bar{x}_{n},\pi_{n},\bar{x}_{n+\frac{1}{2}})
with
\pi_{n}=i_{n}=0
, then

\displaystyle\pi_{n+\frac{1}{2}}\left(\ \cdot\ \middle|\ h_{n+\frac{1}{2}}\right)=\delta_{\pi_{n-\frac{1}{2}}}=\delta_{a_{n-1}}.

The set of admissible policies for the observation cost model is denoted
\Pi_{\mathrm{obs}}
.

With the above setup, we can give a full meaning to the expression (
2.1
) by writing

\displaystyle J(\pi,p_{0})\coloneqq\mathbb{E}^{\pi}_{p_{0}}\left[\sum^{2N}_{n=0}r_{\mathrm{obs}}(x_{n/2},\pi_{n/2})\right]=\mathbb{E}^{\pi}_{p_{0}}\left[\sum^{N}_{n=0}\left(r(x_{n},a_{n})-i_{n}\cdot c_{\mathrm{obs}}\right)\right],\quad\pi\in\Pi_{\mathrm{obs}},\ p_{0}\in\Delta_{\mathcal{X}}.

Remark 2.3
.

Regarding the initial distribution
p_{0}
and observation kernel
q_{0}
:
for the purposes of this paper, we will assume that an observation (made at some previous time) is always available. This allows a consistent characterisation of the belief state by a finite tuple, which leads to a system of finite-dimensional QVIs in
Sections

2.1
and
2.2
. Thus, we will only consider initial kernels
p_{0}
that is in the form of some
n
-step transition probabilities of the kernel
p
, and the initial observation kernel
q_{0}
will be taken as the Dirac measure
q_{0}(\cdot\mid x)=\delta_{x}(\cdot)
.

Remark 2.4
.

The formulation with the half time-steps and the inclusion of fictitious state/ observation variables above are strictly a theoretical construct, such that the OCM can be reframed as a POMDP, and thus allowing us to directly appeal to standard results to formulate dynamic programming. It will be shown later that the half steps become redundant once dynamic programming is established, and the value function will only need to be considered over the integer steps.

2.1
Finite horizon problem

For the finite horizon problem, let
N\in\mathbb{N}
be the time horizon. For a policy
\pi\in\Pi_{\mathrm{obs}}
, the objective function is

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}^{\pi}_{p_{0}}\left[\sum^{2N}_{n=0}r_{\mathrm{obs}}(x_{n/2},\pi_{n/2})\right]=\mathbb{E}^{\pi}_{p_{0}}\left[\sum^{N}_{n=0}\left(r(x_{n},a_{n})-i_{n}\cdot c_{\mathrm{obs}}\right)\right].

With the OCM problem characterised as a POMDP, we consider the belief MDP, with the belief state given by
\mathbb{P}^{\pi}_{p_{0}}(x_{t}\mid h_{t})
, where
h_{t}\in\mathcal{H}_{t}
. By the Markov property, the belief state is fully characterised by the controller’s most recently observed information, in that

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}^{\pi}_{p_{0}}(x_{t}\mid h_{t})=p^{(\lfloor t\rfloor-k)}(x_{t}\mid x_{k},a_{k}),

where
p^{(\lfloor t\rfloor-k)}
is the
(\lfloor t\rfloor-k)
-step transition kernel of the underlying process
X
, and
k\in\mathbb{N}
is the last occurrence such that
i_{k}=1
. Note that
k=t
implies an immediate observation, and the belief state reduces trivially to a Dirac measure at
x_{t}
. The belief state therefore has a finite dimensional parametrisation, and we can consider instead the
augmented state

y=(y_{t})_{t\in\frac{1}{2}\mathbb{N}}
:

\displaystyle y_{t}\coloneqq\begin{cases}(k,x_{k},a_{k}),&\ \mbox{if $\mathbb{N}\ni k=\argmax_{n\leq t}\{i_{n}=1\}\neq t$ },\\
(t,x_{t},\varnothing),&\ \mbox{otherwise},\end{cases}

where
\varnothing
also acts as a dummy variable here. The three components of
y_{t}
represent the most recent observation time
k
, with the correspondingly observed state
x_{k}\in\mathcal{X}
, and applied action
a_{k}\in A
.

Given this equivalence in representation, we can consider the OCM as an MDP with the tuple
\langle\mathcal{Y},\mathcal{A},p_{y},r_{y}\rangle
on the timescale
\frac{1}{2}\mathbb{N}
, where

–

\mathcal{Y}\coloneqq\mathbb{N}\times\mathcal{X}\times A_{\varnothing}
is the
augmented state space
;

–

\mathcal{A}
is the
disjoint
union of
A
and
\mathcal{I}
, with admissible sets
\mathcal{A}(n)=\mathcal{I}
and
\mathcal{A}(n+\frac{1}{2})=A
;

–

p_{y}=(p_{y,t})_{t\in\frac{1}{2}\mathbb{N}}
is a (time-inhomogeneous) transition kernel on
\mathcal{Y}
given
\mathcal{Y}\times\mathcal{A}
: for
n\in\mathbb{N}
,
y=(k,x,a),\ \hat{y}\in\mathcal{Y}
,
i\in\mathcal{I}
, and
a^{\prime}\in A
,

\displaystyle p_{y,n}(\hat{y}\mid y,i)

\displaystyle=i\cdot p^{(n-k)}(\hat{x}\mid x,a)\mathbbm{1}_{\{\hat{y}=(n,\hat{x},\varnothing)\}}+(1-i)\mathbbm{1}_{\{\hat{y}=y\}},

(2.3)

\displaystyle p_{y,n+\frac{1}{2}}(\hat{y}\mid y,a^{\prime})

\displaystyle=\mathbbm{1}_{\{\hat{y}=(k,x,a^{\prime}),\ {a}^{\prime}=a\}},

(2.4)

and for
y=(n,x,\varnothing)
,

\displaystyle p_{y,n+\frac{1}{2}}(\hat{y}\mid y,a^{\prime})=\mathbbm{1}_{\{\hat{y}=(n,x,a^{\prime})\}}.

(2.5)

–

r_{y}=(r_{y,t})_{t\in\frac{1}{2}\mathbb{N}}
is a time-dependent one-step reward function on
\mathcal{Y}\times\mathcal{A}
: for
y=(k,x,a)
,
i\in\mathcal{I}
,
a^{\prime}\in A
,

\displaystyle r_{y,n}(y,i)

\displaystyle=-i\cdot c_{\mathrm{obs}},

\displaystyle r_{y,n+\frac{1}{2}}(y,a^{\prime})

\displaystyle=\sum_{x^{\prime}\in\mathcal{X}}r(x^{\prime},a^{\prime})p^{(n-k)}(x^{\prime}\mid x,a),

(2.6)

and for
y=(n,x,\varnothing)
,

\displaystyle r_{y,n+\frac{1}{2}}(y,a^{\prime})

\displaystyle=r(x,a^{\prime}).

(2.7)

In this augmented problem, define its observable history sets as
\mathcal{H}^{y}_{t}=(\mathcal{Y}\times\mathcal{A})^{2t}\times\mathcal{Y}
,
t\in\frac{1}{2}\mathbb{N}
. Policies
\pi^{y}
are then a sequence of kernels
\pi^{y}_{t}:\mathcal{Y}\to\Delta_{\mathcal{A}}
. For the set of admissible policies of this augmented MDP, we will have to consider the corresponding ‘image’ of
\Pi_{\mathrm{obs}}
. This in turn, is equivalent to imposing a state constraint on the admissible action sets:

\displaystyle\mathcal{A}(n+1/2,(k,x,a))=\{a\},\quad n\in\mathbb{N},\ a\in A.

With the above constraints noted, we shall not distinguish between a policy
\pi\in\Pi_{\mathrm{obs}}
and its corresponding policy
\pi^{y}
in the augmented MDP, and write
\pi
for both.

For the finite horizon problem, the objective function for the augmented state MDP is

\displaystyle J(t,y,\pi)=\mathbb{E}^{\pi}_{y}\left[\sum^{2N}_{n=t}r_{y,\frac{n}{2}}\left(y_{\frac{n}{2}},\pi_{\frac{n}{2}}\right)\right],\quad 0\leq t\leq 2N,\ y\in\mathcal{Y},\ \pi\in\Pi_{\mathrm{obs}},

with value function

\displaystyle v(t,y)=\sup_{\pi\in\Pi_{\mathrm{obs}}}J(t,y,\pi).

(2.8)

The proposition below shows the dynamic programming equation in the form of a quasi-variational inequality, for which the value function for the OCM satisfies.

Proposition 2.5
.

For
n\in\mathbb{N}
,
y=(k,x,a)\in\mathcal{Y}
, define
v^{n,k}_{a,x}=v(n,y)
as in (
2.8
). Then the value function satisfies the following quasi-variational inequality (QVI): for all
{0\leq k<n\leq N-1}
,
x\in\mathcal{X}
, and
a\in A
,

\displaystyle\min\bigg\{

\displaystyle v^{n,k}_{a,x}-v^{n+1,k}_{a,x}-\Big(P^{n-k}_{a}\ r_{a}\Big)_{x}\ ,

\displaystyle v^{n,k}_{a,x}-\bigg(P^{n-k}_{a}\ \overline{v^{n+1,n}+r}\bigg)_{x}+c_{\mathrm{obs}}\bigg\}=0,

(2.9)

with the terminal condition

\displaystyle v^{N,k}_{a,x}

\displaystyle=\big(P^{N-k}r_{a}\big)_{x},

where
P^{n}_{a}
is the
n
-step transition matrix with constant action
a
, and

\displaystyle(r_{a})_{x}=r(x,a),\quad\bar{r}_{x}=\max_{a\in A}r(x,a),

\displaystyle\left(\overline{v^{n+1,n}+r}\right)_{x}=\max_{a\in A}(v^{n+1,n}_{a,x}+r_{a,x}).

Proof.

A standard application of dynamic programming gives us

\displaystyle v(t,y)=\sup_{\pi\in\Pi_{\mathrm{obs}}}\left\{r_{y,t}(y,\pi_{t})+\mathbb{E}^{\pi}\left[v\left(t+\frac{1}{2},y_{t+\frac{1}{2}}\right)\right]\right\}.

Expanding explicitly, for
n\in\mathbb{N}
and
y=(k,x,a)\in\mathcal{Y}
,

\displaystyle v(n,(k,x,a))=\max\left\{-c_{\mathrm{obs}}+\max_{a\in A}\ \mathbb{E}^{a}\left[v\left(n+\frac{1}{2},(n,x_{n},\varnothing)\right)\right],\ v\left(n+\frac{1}{2},(k,x,a)\right)\right\},

where
\mathbb{E}^{a}
is the expectation taken with respect to a constant
a\in A
. Furthermore

\displaystyle v\left(n+\frac{1}{2},(k,x,a)\right)

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}^{a}\left[r(x_{n},a)\right]+v(n+1,(k,x,a)),

\displaystyle v\left(n+\frac{1}{2},(n,x,\varnothing)\right)

\displaystyle=\max_{a\in A}\left\{r(x,a)+v(n+1,(n,x,a))\right\}

Thus, by combining the inspection stage and the action stage together, and rearranging the terms accordingly, we obtain the QVI in the desired form.
∎

The optimal policy
\pi^{*}
is then Markovian with respect to the augmented state
y
, i.e. the optimal policy depends on the most recent observation. Given a solution to the QVI (
2.5
), one can retrieve the optimal policy at time
n
, by first finding the region where the minimum is achieved, which determines if an inspection is optimal. If an inspection is optimal, one observes the latest state, say
x
, and the optimal action is given by
\argmax_{a\in A}(v^{n+1,n}_{a,x}+r_{a,x})
.

Remark 2.6

(
On the relation between observation costs and switching costs
)
.

Due to the nature of the piecewise constant controls, the OCM resembles an optimal switching problem, where switching costs
\{g_{ij}\}_{i,j\in A}
are present, such that
g_{ij}
is paid each time action
i
is changed to action
j
. There are two main differences between the two problems. Firstly, for the OCM, the optimal policy on both inspections and actions have to take into account the lack of updated observations. Secondly, in switching problems,
g_{ii}
is assumed to be 0 for all
i\in A
, i.e., no cost is paid if no switching occurs. This contrasts to the OCM, where
c_{\mathrm{obs}}>0
is paid at each observation, regardless of the action applied afterwards. Indeed, one may decide that no change in action is required upon observation, but nonetheless the observation cost has to be paid upfront in order to arrive at such a conclusion. We can incorporate switching costs
\{g_{ij}\}_{i,j\in A}
into the OCM by writing the augmented reward in the form (see (
2.6
,
2.7
))

\displaystyle r_{y,n+\frac{1}{2}}(y,{a}^{\prime})

\displaystyle=\sum_{x^{\prime}\in\mathcal{X}}r(x^{\prime},a^{\prime})p^{(n-k)}(x^{\prime}\mid x,a)-g_{a,{a}^{\prime}},\

\displaystyle y=(k,x,a),

\displaystyle r_{y,n+\frac{1}{2}}(y,a^{\prime})

\displaystyle=r(x,a^{\prime})-g_{a,{a}^{\prime}},\

\displaystyle y=(n,x,\varnothing).

2.2
Infinite horizon problem

For the discounted infinite horizon problem of the OCM, we will have to consider the appropriate stationary formulations. This can easily be obtained by further considering
(n,y_{n})
as an augmented state. Now recall that the transition kernel of
y
in
Equations

2.3
,
2.4
and
2.5

depends on
n
and
k
strictly through the difference
n-k
. Hence, after relabelling, it is sufficient to consider
y=(n,x,a)
as the augmented state, where here
n
now represents the time elapsed from the previous observation, rather than the standard linear passage of time. The objective function of this equivalent MDP is

\displaystyle J(y,\pi)=\mathbb{E}^{\pi}\left[\sum^{\infty}_{n=0}\gamma^{\lfloor\frac{n}{2}\rfloor}r_{y}\left(y_{\frac{n}{2}},\pi_{\frac{n}{2}}\right)\right],\quad y\in\mathcal{Y},\ \pi\in\Pi_{\mathrm{obs}},

where
\gamma\in(0,1)
is the discount factor, the value function is

\displaystyle v(y)=\sup_{\pi\in\Pi_{\mathrm{obs}}}J(y,\pi).

(2.10)

This gives us the following QVI for the value function, which we shall state here without proof.

Proposition 2.7
.

For
y=(n,x,a)\in\mathcal{Y}
, define
v^{n}_{a,x}=v(y)
. Then the value function (
2.10
) satisfies the following quasi-variational inequality (QVI): for all
n\geq 1
,
x\in\mathcal{X}
, and
a\in A
,

\displaystyle\min\bigg\{

\displaystyle v^{n}_{a,x}-\gamma v^{n+1}_{a,x}-\Big(P^{n}_{a}\ r_{a}\Big)_{x}\ ,

\displaystyle v^{n}_{a,x}-\bigg(P^{n}_{a}\ \overline{\gamma v^{1}+r}\bigg)_{x}+c_{\mathrm{obs}}\bigg\}=0,

(2.11)

where
P^{n}_{a}
is the
n
-step transition matrix with constant action
a
, and

\displaystyle(r_{a})_{x}=r(x,a),\quad\bar{r}_{x}=\max_{a\in A}r(x,a)

\displaystyle\left(\overline{\gamma v^{1}+r}\right)_{x}=\max_{a\in A}(\gamma v^{1}_{a,x}+r_{a,x}).

Note that the QVI (
2.7
) is defined on the infinite domain
\mathbb{N}_{\geq 1}\times\mathcal{X}\times A
. In practice, we will have to truncate this domain for the time variable. A natural boundary condition is to enforce an inspection of the underlying chain after some large time
N
has elapsed. This is equivalent to further restricting the admissible policies in
\Pi_{\mathrm{obs}}
to those such that
i_{N}=1
.

2.3
Approximating the infinite horizon QVI by truncation

Note that the QVI (
2.7
) is defined on the infinite domain
\mathbb{N}_{\geq 1}\times\mathcal{X}\times A
. In practice, we will have to truncate this domain for the time variable. A natural boundary condition is to enforce an inspection of the underlying chain after some large time
N
has elapsed.

We can justify the truncation in the following way. For the original untruncated problem, we can write the reward functional and value function as

\displaystyle J(\pi,p)

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}^{\pi}_{p}\left[\sum^{\infty}_{n=0}\gamma^{n}\left(r(x_{n},a_{n})-i_{n}\cdot c_{\mathrm{obs}}\right)\right],\quad\pi\in\Pi_{\mathrm{obs}},\ p\in\Delta_{\mathcal{X}},

\displaystyle v(p)

\displaystyle=\sup_{\pi\in\Pi_{\mathrm{obs}}}J(\pi,p).

For the truncated problem, enforcing a maximum time for inspection is equivalent to further restricting the set of admissible policies within
\Pi_{\mathrm{obs}}
. To this end, define
\Pi^{N}_{\mathrm{obs}}\subset\Pi_{\mathrm{obs}}
such that any
\pi^{N}=(a^{N},i^{N})\in\Pi^{N}_{\mathrm{obs}}
satisfies

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}(\min\{n>k:i^{N}_{n}=1\}\leq k+N\mid i^{N}_{k}=1)=1\quad\mbox{for all $k\geq 0$.}

Then we can define the truncated value function

\displaystyle v^{N}(p)\coloneqq\sup_{\pi\in\Pi^{N}_{\mathrm{obs}}}J(\pi,p).

Proposition 2.8
.

The truncated value functions
v^{N}
are monotonically increasing in
N
, and furthermore
v^{N}\uparrow v
pointwise.

Proof.

The first part is clear as
\Pi^{M}_{\mathrm{obs}}\subset\Pi^{N}_{\mathrm{obs}}\subset\Pi_{\mathrm{obs}}
for any
M<N
, so that
v^{N}
is monotonically increasing and bounded above by
v
. Therefore
v^{N}
converges to a limit
v^{*}
and

\displaystyle v^{*}(p)=\lim_{N\to\infty}v^{N}(p)\leq v(p).

To show the reverse inequality, let
\{\pi_{n}\}\subset\Pi_{\mathrm{obs}}
be a maximising sequence for
v
. As both
\mathcal{X}
and
A
are finite, for each
n>0
, we can choose
N_{n}\in\mathbb{N}
such that

\displaystyle\left\lvert\sum^{\infty}_{n=N_{n}}\gamma^{n}(r(x,a)-c_{\mathrm{obs}})\right\rvert\leq 2^{-n-1}\ \mbox{ for all $x\in\mathcal{X}$ and $a\in A$.}

Then, choose
\pi^{N}_{n}\in\Pi^{N}_{\mathrm{obs}}
such that
\pi^{N}_{n}(k)=\pi_{n}(k)
for all
k\leq N_{n}
, so that

\displaystyle J(\pi_{n},p)

\displaystyle\leq J(\pi^{N}_{n},p)+2^{-n}

\displaystyle\leq v^{N_{n}}(y)+2^{-n}.

But since
\{\pi_{n}\}
is a maximising sequence for
v
, and
N_{n}\to\infty
by construction, letting
n\to\infty
on both sides leads to

\displaystyle v(y)\leq\lim_{n\to\infty}v^{N_{n}}(y)=v^{*}(y),

as required.
∎

2.4
Parametrised time-inhomogeneous actions

Here, we show how the assumption of constant controls between observations can be relaxed to allow for
parametrised time-dependent actions.
Optimising over open-loop actions in general leads to an unbounded number of parametrising variables for the belief state
y
. For tractability, we consider instead a set of parametrised time-inhomogeneous open-loop actions. This can be incorporated into the previous framework as follows. Let
\Theta
be a finite parameter set. Let
\{f_{\theta}\}_{\theta\in\Theta}
be a parametrised set of open-loop actions, with
f_{\theta}:\mathbb{N}\to A
. Then we write the OCM POMDP as
\langle\mathcal{Y},\mathcal{A},p_{y},r_{y}\rangle
on the timescale
\frac{1}{2}\mathbb{N}
, but now with
\mathcal{A}
being the union of
\Theta
and
\mathcal{I}
, with admissible sets
\mathcal{A}(n)=\mathcal{I}
and
{\mathcal{A}(n+\frac{1}{2})=\Theta}
. Then the augmented transition kernel
p_{y}=(p_{y,t})_{t\in\frac{1}{2}\mathbb{N}}
can be written, for
n\in\mathbb{N}
,
y=(k,x,\theta)
,
\hat{y}\in\mathcal{Y}
,
i\in\mathcal{I}=\{0,1\}
, and
\theta^{\prime}\in\Theta
, as

\displaystyle p_{y,n}(\hat{y}\mid y,i)

\displaystyle=i\cdot p^{(n-k)}(\hat{x}\mid x,f_{\theta})\mathbbm{1}_{\{\hat{y}=(n,\hat{x},\varnothing)\}}+(1-i)\mathbbm{1}_{\{\hat{y}=y\}},

\displaystyle p_{y,n+\frac{1}{2}}(\hat{y}\mid y,\theta^{\prime})

\displaystyle=\mathbbm{1}_{\{\hat{y}=(k,x,\theta^{\prime}),\ {a}^{\prime}=a\}},

and for
y=(n,x,\varnothing)
,

\displaystyle p_{y,n+\frac{1}{2}}(\hat{y}\mid y,\theta^{\prime})=\mathbbm{1}_{\{\hat{y}=(n,x,\theta^{\prime})\}},

where the term
p^{(n-k)}(\hat{x}\mid x,f_{\theta})
represents the
(n-k)
-step transition kernel starting from
x\in\mathcal{X}
, applying the successive actions
f_{\theta}(1),\ldots,f_{\theta}(n-k)
. A similar replacement can be done with the augmented reward
r_{y}
. Then the QVI can be rewritten, for the infinite horizon case for example, as

\displaystyle\min\bigg\{

\displaystyle v^{n}_{\theta,x}-\gamma v^{n+1}_{\theta,x}-\Big(Q^{n}_{\theta}\ r_{f_{\theta}(n)}\Big)_{x}\ ,

\displaystyle v^{n}_{\theta,x}-\bigg(Q^{n}_{\theta}\ \overline{\gamma v^{1}+r}\bigg)_{x}+c_{\mathrm{obs}}\bigg\}=0,

with

Q^{n}_{\theta}=\prod^{n}_{k=1}P_{f_{\theta}(k)}
.

\displaystyle\min\bigg\{

\displaystyle v^{n}_{0,0}-\gamma v^{n+1}_{0,0}-\Big(P^{n}_{0}\ r_{0}\Big)_{x}\ ,

\displaystyle v^{n}_{0,0}-\left(P^{n}_{0}\overline{\gamma v^{1}+r}\right)_{x}+c_{\mathrm{obs}}\bigg\}=0.

(2.12)

Due to the symmetry of the problem, we have
v^{n}_{0,0}=v^{n}_{1,1}
. Moreover, it is clear that given the knowledge of
x_{n}
, the optimal action is to set
{a_{n}=x_{n}}
. Hence we can write (
2.5
) as

\displaystyle\min\{v^{n}_{0,0}-\gamma v^{n+1}_{0,0}-p^{n},\ v^{n}_{0,0}-\gamma v^{1}_{0,0}-1+c_{\mathrm{obs}}\}=0

or simply by writing
v(n)=v^{n}_{0,0}
:

v(n)=\max\{p^{n}+\gamma v(n+1),\ 1-c_{\mathrm{obs}}+\gamma v(1)\}.

Let
T
be the first optimal inspection time (where by convention
T=\infty
if it is optimal to never inspect). The value function is given recursively by

v(n)=\begin{cases}1-c_{\mathrm{obs}}+\gamma v(1),&\quad\mbox{if }n\geq T;\\
p^{n}+\gamma v(n+1),&\quad\mbox{otherwise}.\end{cases}

(2.13)

Solving the above for
v(1)
, we obtain the explicit solution

v(1)=\max{\left\{\sup_{m\geq 2}{\left(\frac{p\sum^{m-2}_{k=0}(\gamma p)^{k}+\gamma^{m-1}(1-c_{\mathrm{obs}})}{1-\gamma^{m}}\right)},\frac{1-c_{\mathrm{obs}}}{1-\gamma}\right\}},

(2.14)

from which
v(n)
for
n\geq 1
can be calculated from (
2.13
). The first term in (
2.14
) is a geometric series, where
p\sum^{m-2}_{k=0}(\gamma p)^{k}+\gamma^{m-1}(1-c_{\mathrm{obs}})
is the expected returns across the optimal inspection interval, and
\gamma^{m}
is the discount factor over the whole interval. We can interpret (
2.14
) as searching for the optimal inspection interval to maximise the sum of the rewards, minus the observation cost.

3
Comparison principle and penalisation

In this section, we consider the well-posedness of solutions to the QVI for the OCM. We establish uniqueness by proving a comparison principle, and existence by constructing arbitrarily close approximations via truncation and penalisation. To this end, we shall recast the QVIs in
Section

2
in a more abstract form.

Problem 3.1
.

Find a function
u:\mathbb{N}\times\mathcal{X}\to\mathbb{R}^{A}
, with
u_{a}(\cdot,\cdot)
as its components, satisfying

\displaystyle\min\left\{F_{a}(n,x,u(n,x),u_{a}),\ u_{a}(n,x)-\mathcal{M}u(n,x)\right\}=0,\quad n\in\mathbb{N},\ x\in\mathcal{X},\ a\in A,

(3.1)

where
F_{a}:\mathbb{N}\times\mathcal{X}\times\mathbb{R}^{A}\times\mathbb{R}^{\mathbb{N}\times\mathcal{X}}\to\mathbb{R}
satisfies
3.2
, and
{\mathcal{M}u:\mathbb{N}\times\mathcal{X}\to\mathbb{R}}
is defined for all

(n,x)\in\mathbb{N}\times\mathcal{X}
by

\displaystyle\mathcal{M}u(n,x)=\left(Q_{n}\overline{u^{1}-c}\right)_{x},\quad\left(\overline{u^{1}-c}\right)_{x}=\max_{a\in A}\left(u^{1}_{a,x}-c_{a,x}\right),

(3.2)

for a given vector
c\in\mathbb{R}^{\mathcal{X}\times A}
, and
\{Q_{n}\}\subset\mathbb{R}^{\mathcal{X}\times\mathcal{X}}
is a sequence of substochastic matrices, i.e., with non negative row sums bounded by 1.

Assumption 3.2
.

The functions
\{F_{a}\}_{a\in A}
in Problem
3.1
satisfy:

1.

There exists
\beta>0
such that for any
n\in\mathbb{N}
,
x\in\mathcal{X}
, bounded functions
u,v:\mathbb{N}\times\mathcal{X}\to\mathbb{R}^{A}
such that
u\leq v
, and
r,s\in\mathbb{R}^{A}
such that
\theta\coloneqq r_{a}-s_{a}=\max_{j\in A}(r_{j}-s_{j})\geq 0
, then

\displaystyle F_{a}(n,x,r,u_{a}+\theta)-F_{a}(n,x,s,v_{a})\geq\beta\theta.

2.

For any
n\in N
,
a\in A
,
r\in\mathbb{R}^{A}
and continuous bounded function
\phi:\mathbb{N}\times\mathcal{X}\to\mathbb{R}
, the functions
x\mapsto F_{a}(n,x,r,\phi)
are continuous and bounded. Moreover, the functions
r\mapsto F_{a}(n,x,r,\phi)
are uniformly continuous for bounded
r
, uniformly with respect to
(n,x)\in\mathbb{N}\times\mathcal{X}
.

3.2
are monotonicity and regularity assumptions typically used in viscosity solution theory
[
8
]
, which applies to standard approximation schemes, such as finite differences. The monotonicity condition arises naturally, for example, from the discretisation of QVIs in continuous time involving switching controls. The operator
\mathcal{M}
, which shall be referred to as the
inspection operator
, is a non-local term in the QVI that couples the solution
u
across the different action values. In general, the vector
c
in (
3.2
) can also depend on
n
, and the proofs in this section extends naturally to that case.

We now prove a comparison principle for the QVI (
3.1
), extending a similar result in
[
28
]
for finite QVIs and with the identity matrix in place of
Q_{n}
. This establishes the uniqueness of solutions to
3.1
.

Proposition 3.3
.

Given
3.2
, suppose
u:\mathbb{N}\times\mathcal{X}\to\mathbb{R}^{A}
(resp.
v
) is bounded and satisfies for
c>0
,

\displaystyle\min\left\{F_{a}(n,x,u(n,x),u_{a}),\ u_{a}(n,x)-\mathcal{M}u(n,x)\right\}\leq 0\quad\mbox{(resp. $\geq 0$)}.

Then we have
u\leq v
.

Proof.

We write
u^{n}_{a,x}
for
u_{a}(n,x)
, and let
M=\sup_{n,x,a}(u^{n}_{a,x}-v^{n}_{a,x})
. Let
(n_{k},x_{k},a_{k})
be a maximising sequence, with
M_{k}=u^{n_{k}}_{a_{k},x_{k}}-v^{n_{k}}_{a_{k},x_{k}}
. Suppose that along the whole sequence,
u_{a_{k}}-\mathcal{M}u\leq 0
. As
v
is a supersolution, we also have that
v_{a_{k}}-\mathcal{M}v\geq 0
. Therefore,

\displaystyle u^{n_{k}}_{a_{k},x_{k}}-v^{n_{k}}_{a_{k},x_{k}}

\displaystyle\leq\left(Q_{n_{k}}\overline{u^{1}-c}\right)_{x_{k}}-\left(Q_{n_{k}}\overline{v^{1}-c}\right)_{x_{k}}

\displaystyle\leq\left(Q_{n_{k}}\overline{u^{1}-v^{1}}\right)_{x_{k}}

\displaystyle\leq\left(\overline{u^{1}-v^{1}}\right)_{x^{*}}\quad\quad

\displaystyle\mbox{(for some $x^{*}\in\mathcal{X}$)}.

As the RHS is independent of
k
, taking the supremum on the LHS leads to

\displaystyle M\leq\left(\overline{u^{1}-v^{1}}\right)_{x^{*}}\leq\max_{a\in A}\ (u^{1}_{a,x^{*}}-v^{1}_{a,x^{*}}).

As
A
is finite, the supremum for
M
is achieved at some
(1,b_{1},y_{1})
,
b_{1}\in A
,
y_{1}\in\mathcal{X}
. Applying the same argument as above, we have

\displaystyle u^{1}_{b_{1},y_{1}}-v^{1}_{b_{1},y_{1}}\leq-c\leq u^{1}_{b_{2},y_{2}}-v^{1}_{b_{2},y_{2}},

for some
(b_{2},y_{2})\neq(b_{1},y_{1})
, since by assumption
c\geq 0
. Repeating the same argument leads to successive different indices
(b_{i},y_{i})
such that

\displaystyle u^{1}_{b_{1},y_{1}}-u^{1}_{b_{n},y_{n}}\leq\sum^{n-1}_{j=1}\left(u^{1}_{b_{j},y_{j}}-u^{1}_{b_{j+1},y_{j+1}}\right)\leq-(n-1)c<0.

(3.3)

But since both
\mathcal{X}
and
A
are finite, by the pigeonhole principle, we must eventually have some
n
such that
(b_{1},y_{1})=(b_{n},y_{n})
, which contradicts
(
3.3
)
. Therefore, we must have some
(n_{k},x_{k},a_{k})
such that

\displaystyle F_{a_{k}}(n_{k},x_{k},u(n_{k},x_{k}),u_{a_{k}})-F_{a_{k}}(n_{k},x_{k},v(n_{k},x_{k}),v_{a_{k}})\leq 0.

(3.4)

Now by construction,
u_{a_{k}}\leq v_{a_{k}}+M
. Hence, by taking
r=u(n_{k},x_{k})
,
s=v(n_{k},x_{k})+M_{k}
and
\theta\coloneqq\max_{a\in A}(r_{a}-s_{a})=0
in the monotonicity condition in
3.2
, we have

\displaystyle F_{a_{k}}(n_{k},x_{k},v(n_{k},x_{k})+M_{k},v_{a_{k}}+M)\leq F_{a_{k}}(n_{k},x_{k},u(n_{k},x_{k}),u_{a_{k}}).

Next, as
r\mapsto F_{a}(n,x,r,\phi)
is uniformly continuous, it admits a modulus of continuity, which we denote by
\omega(|r-s|)
, so that we have

\displaystyle F_{a_{k}}(n_{k},x_{k},v(n_{k},x_{k})+M,v_{a_{k}}+M)-\omega(M-M_{k})\leq F_{a_{k}}(n_{k},x_{k},v(n_{k},x_{k})+M_{k},v_{a_{k}}+M).

Combining the above with (
3.4
), we arrive at

\displaystyle\beta M-\omega(M-M_{k})\leq F_{a_{k}}(n_{k},x_{k},u(n_{k},x_{k}),u_{a_{k}})-F_{a_{k}}(n_{k},x_{k},v(n_{k},x_{k}),v_{a_{k}})\leq 0.

Then, as
k\to\infty
, we arrive at a contradiction, since the modulus of continuity
\omega\to 0
and
\beta M>0
by assumption.
∎

Corollary 3.4
.

The solutions to the QVI for the OCM infinite horizon problem (
2.7
) are unique.

Proof.

Clearly (
2.7
) can be cast in the form of (
3.1
), given by

\displaystyle F_{a}(n,x,u,\phi)

\displaystyle=u-\gamma\phi(n+1,x)-\left(P^{n}_{a}r_{a}\right)_{x},

\displaystyle Q_{n}

\displaystyle=\gamma P^{n}_{a},

\displaystyle c_{a,x}

\displaystyle=c_{\mathrm{obs}}-\frac{1}{\gamma}r_{a,x}.

Moreover,
F_{a}
satisfies
3.2
with
\beta=1-\gamma
. Since a solution to (
3.1
) is both a subsolution and supersolution, it follows from
3.3
that any solution must be unique.
∎

As alluded to in
Section

2
, in order to compute the solutions to the QVI (
3.1
), we have to resort to computing a finite QVI, truncated at some large time. Here we shall also show the comparison principle for the truncated QVI. Let
N
be the truncated time,
\lvert\mathcal{X}\rvert=L
,

. Then a function

\mathbb{R}^{N\times L\times d}

in
3.1

\displaystyle F_{a}(n,x,u(n,x),u_{a})\eqqcolon\tilde{F}_{a}(u)^{n}_{x},

i.e., we consider

as functions from
\mathbb{R}^{N\times L\times d}
to
\mathbb{R}^{N\times L}

Lemma 3.5
.

\tilde{F}_{a}:\mathbb{R}^{N\times L\times d}\to\mathbb{R}^{N\times L}
is a continuous function that satisfies the following property: there exists a constant
\beta>0
such that for any
u,v\in\mathbb{R}^{N\times L\times d}
with
{u^{\bar{n}}_{\bar{a},\bar{l}}-v^{\bar{n}}_{\bar{a},\bar{l}}=\max_{n,a,l}(u^{n}_{a,l}-v^{n}_{a,l})\geq 0}
, we have

\tilde{F}_{\bar{a}}(u)^{\bar{n}}_{\bar{l}}-\tilde{F}_{\bar{a}}(v)^{\bar{n}}_{\bar{l}}\geq\beta(u^{\bar{n}}_{\bar{a},\bar{l}}-v^{\bar{n}}_{\bar{a},\bar{l}}).

(3.5)

Proof.

For the first part, let
u,v\in\mathbb{R}^{N\times L\times d}
with
\theta\coloneqq{u^{\bar{n}}_{\bar{a},\bar{l}}-v^{\bar{n}}_{\bar{a},\bar{l}}=\max_{n,a,l}(u^{n}_{a,l}-v^{n}_{a,l})\geq 0}
. Then by definition of
\theta

\tilde{u}\coloneqq u-\theta\leq v
. Applying
3.2
with
r=u(\bar{n},\bar{l})
,
s=v(\bar{n},\bar{l})
and functions
\tilde{u},v
lead to

\displaystyle F_{\bar{a}}(\bar{n},\bar{l},u(\bar{n},\bar{l}),\tilde{u}_{\bar{a}}+\theta)-F_{\bar{a}}(\bar{n},\bar{l},v(\bar{n},\bar{l}),v_{\bar{a}})\geq\beta\theta.

(3.6)

Noting that
\tilde{u}_{\bar{a}}+\theta=u_{\bar{a}}
, it then follows from (
3.6
) that
\tilde{F}_{a}(u)^{n}_{x}=F_{a}(n,x,u(n,x),u_{a})
satisfies (
3.5

∎

Thus, the finite truncated QVI can be summarised by the problem below.

Problem 3.6
.

Find
u=(u_{1},\ldots,u_{d})\in\mathbb{R}^{N\times L\times d}
such that

\displaystyle\min\left\{\tilde{F}_{a}(u),u_{a}-\mathcal{M}u\right\}=0,\quad a\in\{1,\ldots,d\}\eqqcolon A,

(3.7)

with time boundary condition

\displaystyle u_{a}(N,\cdot)=\mathcal{M}u(N,\cdot),

where
\tilde{F}_{a}:\mathbb{R}^{N\times L\times d}\to\mathbb{R}^{N\times L}
satisfies (
3.5
), and
\mathcal{M}:\mathbb{R}^{N\times L\times d}\to\mathbb{R}^{N\times L}
is defined by

\displaystyle(\mathcal{M}u)^{n}_{l}=\left(Q_{n}\overline{u^{1}-c}\right)_{l},\quad\left(\overline{u^{1}-c}\right)_{l}=\max_{a\in A}\left(u^{1}_{a,l}-c_{a,l}\right),

for a given vector

and
\{Q_{n}\}\subset\mathbb{R}^{L\times L}
is a sequence of substochastic matrices.

Remark 3.7
.

For the case with parameter uncertainty, if the measures
\rho_{n}(d\theta)
can be parametrised by a finite number of parameters
w
, this can also be considered as part of the spatial domain. In this case
L=\lvert\mathcal{X}\rvert\cdot\lvert w\rvert
.

When the domain is finite as above, and in the case where the

’s are the identity matrix, then (
3.7

[
28
]
for a more detailed analysis for such classes of QVIs. Naturally, the truncated QVI also satisfies a comparison principle:

Proposition 3.8
.

Suppose
u=(u_{a})_{a\in A}
(resp.
v=(v_{a})_{a\in A}
) satisfies for
c>0
:

\min\left\{\tilde{F}_{a}(u),\ u_{a}-\mathcal{M}u\right\}\leq 0\quad(\mathrm{resp.}\geq 0),\quad a\in A;

then
u\leq v
.

Proof.

Let
M\coloneqq u^{\bar{n}}_{\bar{a},\bar{l}}-v^{\bar{n}}_{\bar{a},\bar{l}}=\max_{n,a,l}(u^{n}_{a,l}-v^{n}_{a,l})
. The proof follows directly from the arguments in
3.3
. Assuming that the subsolution
u
satisfies
u_{\bar{a}}\leq\mathcal{M}u
leads to

\displaystyle u^{\bar{n}}_{\bar{a},\bar{l}}-v^{\bar{n}}_{\bar{a},\bar{l}}\leq-c\leq u^{1}_{a^{*},l^{*}}-v^{1}_{a^{*},l^{*}}\quad\mbox{for some $a^{*}$ and $l^{*}$,}

so that the maximum is also achieved at
(1,a^{*},l^{*})
. Then the same contradiction argument leads to
\tilde{F}_{\bar{a}}(u)^{\bar{n}}_{\bar{l}}\leq 0
, but then since
v
is a supersolution and
M>0

\beta(u^{\bar{n}}_{\bar{a},\bar{l}}-v^{\bar{n}}_{\bar{a},\bar{l}})\leq\tilde{F}_{\bar{a}}(u)^{\bar{n}}_{\bar{l}}-\tilde{F}_{\bar{a}}(v)^{\bar{n}}_{\bar{l}}\leq 0

which is again a contradiction. Hence we must have

∎

We now present a penalty approximation to the QVI (
3.7
). The motivations behind this are threefold. Firstly, the penalty method holds a crucial advantage over a more traditional policy iteration approach, as using the latter can lead to numerical instabilities or indeed ill-defined iterates, due to the lack of a guarantee of the invertibility of the matrix arising from the intervention operator
\mathcal{M}
. A simple example of such an instance is given in
[
29
, Section 6]
, with a more in depth explanation given in
[
3
]
.
Secondly, for QVIs that arise from the discretisation of diffusions on continuous state spaces, the number of policy iterations is typically large for fine meshes, as exposed in
[
27
]
, while semismooth Newton iterations for the penalised system are robust under mesh refinement. Lastly, penalisation provides naturally a constructive proof of existence to the solutions of the QVI (
3.7
). Consider the following penalised problem.

Problem 3.9
.

Let
\rho\geq 0
be the penalty parameter. Find
u^{\rho}=(u^{\rho}_{a})_{a\in A}\in\mathbb{R}^{N\times L\times d}
such that

G^{\rho}_{a}(v)\coloneqq\tilde{F}_{a}(u^{\rho})-\rho\ \pi\left(\mathcal{M}u^{\rho}-u^{\rho}_{a}\right)=0,\ a\in A,

(3.8)

where the penalisation function
\pi:\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R}
is continuous, non-decreasing with
\pi|_{(-\infty,0]}=0
and
\pi|_{(0,\infty)}>0
, and is applied elementwise.

Thus in the penalised problem, a penalty
\rho
is applied whenever the condition
{u^{\rho}_{a}-\mathcal{M}u^{\rho}\geq 0}
is violated. As
\rho\uparrow\infty
, the penalised solution should then converge to the solution of the discrete QVI (
3.7
). We first show below that for each fixed
\rho
, (
3.8
) satisfies a comparison principle. This implies uniqueness for
3.9
. The argument follows similarly to the approach in
[
28
]
and
3.8
.

Proposition 3.10
.

For any penalty parameter
\rho\geq 0
, if
u^{\rho}=(u^{\rho}_{a})_{a\in A}
(resp.,
v^{\rho}=(v^{\rho}_{a})_{a\in A}
) satisfies

\tilde{F}_{a}(u^{\rho})-\rho\ \pi\left(\mathcal{M}u^{\rho}-u^{\rho}_{a}\right)\leq 0\quad(\mathrm{resp.,}\geq 0),

then
u^{\rho}\leq v^{\rho}
.

Proof.

Let
M\coloneqq u^{\rho,\bar{n}}_{\bar{a},\bar{l}}-v^{\rho,\bar{n}}_{\bar{a},\bar{l}}=\max_{n,a,l}(u^{\rho,n}_{a,l}-v^{\rho,n}_{a,l})
. Suppose for a contradiction that
M>0
. From the previous proposition, we have that

\displaystyle\left(Q_{\bar{n}}\overline{u^{\rho,1}-c}\right)_{\bar{l}}-\left(Q_{\bar{n}}\overline{v^{\rho,1}-c}\right)_{\bar{l}}\leq u^{\rho,\bar{n}}_{\bar{a},\bar{l}}-v^{\rho,\bar{n}}_{\bar{a},\bar{l}}.

As
\pi
is non-decreasing,

\pi\left(\left(Q_{\bar{n}}\overline{u^{\rho,1}-c}\right)_{\bar{l}}-u^{\rho,\bar{n}}_{\bar{a},\bar{l}}\right)\leq\pi\left(\left(Q_{\bar{n}}\overline{v^{\rho,1}-c}\right)_{\bar{l}}-v^{\rho,\bar{n}}_{\bar{a},\bar{l}}\right).

(3.9)

As
u^{\rho}
and
v^{\rho}
are respectively sub and super solutions of (
3.8
), we have

\displaystyle\tilde{F}_{\bar{a}}(u^{\rho})^{\bar{n}}_{\bar{l}}-\rho\ \pi\left(\left(\mathcal{M}u^{\rho}\right)^{\bar{n}}_{\bar{l}}-u^{\rho,\bar{n}}_{\bar{a},\bar{l}}\right)-\left(\tilde{F}_{\bar{a}}(v^{\rho})^{\bar{n}}_{\bar{l}}-\rho\ \pi\left(\left(\mathcal{M}v^{\rho}\right)^{\bar{n}}_{\bar{l}}-v^{\rho,\bar{n}}_{\bar{a},\bar{l}}\right)\right)

\displaystyle\leq\

\displaystyle\tilde{F}_{\bar{a}}(u^{\rho})^{\bar{n}}_{\bar{l}}-\tilde{F}_{\bar{a}}(v^{\rho})^{\bar{n}}_{\bar{l}}-\rho\left(\pi\left(\left(Q_{\bar{n}}\overline{u^{\rho,1}-c}\right)_{\bar{l}}-u^{\rho,\bar{n}}_{\bar{a},\bar{l}}\right)-\pi\left(\left(Q_{\bar{n}}\overline{v^{\rho,1}-c}\right)_{\bar{l}}-v^{\rho,\bar{n}}_{\bar{a},\bar{l}}\right)\right)

\displaystyle\leq\

\displaystyle 0

Hence by (
3.9
),
\tilde{F}_{\bar{a}}(u^{\rho})^{\bar{n}}_{\bar{l}}-\tilde{F}_{\bar{a}}(v^{\rho})^{\bar{n}}_{\bar{l}}\leq 0
. The monotonicity assumption of
F
then leads to a contradiction, so that
M\leq 0
as required.
∎

Next, we show that the penalised solutions are uniformly bounded above, which is crucial for the convergence towards the unpenalised problem.

Lemma 3.11
.

For
\rho\geq 0
and
c\geq 0
, let
u^{\rho}
be the solution to (

\lVert u^{\rho}\rVert\leq\lVert F(0)\rVert/\beta
.

Proof.

This is a direct adaptation of
[

, where the same estimate is shown for discrete switching problems, relying on the monotonicity condition and the non-negativity of the penalty function.
∎

. As
\mathcal{M}
is continuous, we have
u_{a}-\mathcal{M}u\geq 0
. By
3.12
and the pointwise convergence of
u^{N}
, we can choose a sufficiently large
{N}^{\prime}
such that

\displaystyle(u_{a}-u^{{N}^{\prime}}_{a})|_{\Omega_{n,x}}

\displaystyle\leq\varepsilon,

\displaystyle{\varepsilon}^{\prime}=u_{a}(n,x)-u^{{N}^{\prime}}_{a}(n,x)

\displaystyle\leq\varepsilon.

Moreover, we can find
\tilde{N}\geq{N}^{\prime}
such that for all
b\neq a\in A
,

\displaystyle u_{b}(n,x)-u^{{N}^{\prime}}_{b}(n,x)\leq{\varepsilon}^{\prime},

so that we can define
\tilde{u}
by
\tilde{u}_{a}=u^{{N}^{\prime}}_{a}(n,x)
and
\tilde{u}_{b}=u^{\tilde{N}}_{b}(n,x)
for
b\neq a
. Therefore,

\displaystyle u_{a}(n,x)-\tilde{u}_{a}=\max_{b\in A}(u_{b}(n,x)-\tilde{u}_{b})={\varepsilon}^{\prime}.

Then, we apply the monotonicity condition in
3.2
, with
r=u(n,x)+(\varepsilon-{\varepsilon}^{\prime})
,
s=\tilde{u}
, noting also that the functions
u_{a}-\varepsilon\leq u^{\tilde{N}}_{a}
on
\Omega_{n,x}
. This gives

\displaystyle F_{a}(n,x,u(n,x)+\varepsilon-{\varepsilon}^{\prime},u_{a})-F_{a}(n,x,\tilde{u},u^{\tilde{N}}_{a})\geq\beta\varepsilon.

By the uniform continuity of
r\mapsto F_{a}(n,x,r,\phi)
, we have

\displaystyle F_{a}(n,x,u(n,x)+\varepsilon-{\varepsilon}^{\prime},u_{a})

\displaystyle\geq F_{a}(n,x,u^{\tilde{N}}(n,x),u^{\tilde{N}}_{a})-\omega\left(\lvert u^{\tilde{N}}_{a}(n,x)-u^{{N}^{\prime}}_{a}(n,x)\rvert\right)+\beta\varepsilon

\displaystyle\geq-\omega\left(\lvert u^{\tilde{N}}_{a}(n,x)-u^{{N}^{\prime}}_{a}(n,x)\rvert\right)+\beta\varepsilon,

where the inequality follows from the fact that
u^{\tilde{N}}
satisfies the QVI (
3.7
), and
\omega
is the modulus of continuity. Finally, by the convergence of
u^{N}
to
u
, and the uniform continuity of
{r\mapsto F_{a}(n,x,r,\phi)}
again, we have
F_{a}(n,x,u(n,x),u_{a})\geq 0
.

We now have
F_{a}(n,x,u(n,x),u_{a})\geq 0
and
u_{a}-\mathcal{M}u\geq 0
. Suppose
u_{a}-\mathcal{M}u>0
. We show that
F_{a}(n,x,u(n,x),u_{a})=0
to conclude. By the uniform continuity of
{r\mapsto F_{a}(n,x,r,\phi)}
, for any
\varepsilon>0
there exists
{N}^{\prime}\geq 0
such that for all
N\geq{N}^{\prime}
,

\displaystyle 0\leq F_{a}(n,x,u(n,x),u_{a})

\displaystyle<F_{a}(n,x,u^{N}(n,x),u_{a})+\varepsilon

\displaystyle\leq F_{a}(n,x,u^{N}(n,x),u^{N}_{a})+\varepsilon,

where the second inequality follows from the monotonicity condition. By the continuity of
\mathcal{M}
,
u^{N}_{a}-\mathcal{M}u^{N}>0
for sufficiently large
N
, and therefore
F_{a}(n,x,u^{N}(n,x),u^{N}_{a})=0
. But as
\varepsilon
is arbitrary, we have
F_{a}(n,x,u(n,x),u_{a})=0
as required.
∎

Thus, we have a straightforward computation scheme to solve for the OCM. We first set up the discrete QVIs arising from the problem and choose a suitable time for truncation, then the equations are approximated by the penalised problem. The solution of the penalised problem can then be in turn approximated iteratively with semismooth Newton methods
[
36
]
. Formally speaking, starting with an initialisation
v^{(0)}
to the penalised problem

G^{\rho}(v)=\tilde{F}(v)-\rho\ \pi\left(\mathcal{M}v-v\right)=0,

we obtain the next iterate by solving for

v^{(k+1)}=v^{(k)}-\mathcal{L}^{\rho}(v^{(k)})^{-1}G^{\rho}(v^{(k)}),

where
\mathcal{L}^{\rho}
denotes the generalised derivative of the function
G^{\rho}
. This shall be the numerical scheme that we adopt for our numerical experiments in the next section.

4
Numerical experiments

In this section, we apply our observation cost framework to three numerical experiments. Sections
4.1
and
4.2
analyse two infinite horizon problems. For these examples, we examine the numerical performance of the penalty method and Newton iterations, as well as the effects of the observation cost on the qualitative behavior of the solutions. For the penalised equations, we will employ the penalty function
\pi(x)=x^{+}
as in
[
28
]
. Section
5.1
considers the parameter uncertainty formulation over a finite horizon. The solutions are obtained through backwards recursion from the terminal conditions. We examine the impact that the extra parameter uncertainty has on the optimal trajectories.

4.1
Random walk with drift

Consider an integer-valued random walk whose drift depends on the action space
A=\{+1,-1\}
. The probability of each step is parametrised by
\theta
. Specifically, for any
x\in\mathcal{X}=\mathbb{N}
,

\displaystyle p(x+1\mid x,+1)=\theta,\quad

\displaystyle p(x-1\mid x,+1)=1-\theta;

\displaystyle p(x+1\mid x,-1)=1-\theta,\quad

\displaystyle p(x-1\mid x,-1)=\theta.

(4.1)

We also adopt the following reward function:

r(x,a)=\frac{1}{|x|+1}.

The mass of this reward function
r
is concentrated around the origin, so naturally, the optimal action is one that reverts the process back towards the origin.

For this example, we consider the infinite horizon problem. Recall that the discrete QVI (
2.7
) reads: for all
n\geq 0
,
x\in\mathcal{X}
, and
a\in A
,

\displaystyle\min\bigg\{

\displaystyle v^{n}_{a,x}-\gamma v^{n+1}_{a,x}-\Big(P^{n}_{a}\ r_{a}\Big)_{x}\ ,\ v^{n}_{a,x}-\bigg(P^{n}_{a}\ \overline{\gamma v^{1}+r}\bigg)_{x}+c_{\mathrm{obs}}\bigg\}=0,

(4.2)

Note that there exists a path from
x
to
y
over
m
units of time if and only if
m\geq|y-x|
and
m\equiv y\ (\mathrm{mod}\ 2)
. If
\mathcal{S}^{x}_{m}
denotes the set of states that can be reached from
x
after
m
units of time, then for a constant action, the
n
-step transition probabilties are given by

\displaystyle p^{(n)}(x^{\prime}\mid x,+1)

\displaystyle=\begin{cases}\hfill\binom{n}{k}\theta^{k}(1-\theta)^{n-k}&,\ x^{\prime}\in\mathcal{S}^{x}_{n};\\
\hfill 0&,\ x^{\prime}\notin\mathcal{S}^{x}_{n},\end{cases}

\displaystyle p^{(n)}(x^{\prime}\mid x,-1)

\displaystyle=\begin{cases}\hfill\binom{n}{k}\theta^{n-k}(1-\theta)^{k}&,\ x^{\prime}\in\mathcal{S}^{x}_{n};\\
\hfill 0&,\ x^{\prime}\notin\mathcal{S}^{x}_{n},\end{cases}

where
k=(n+x^{\prime}-x)/2
. For this problem, we shall also incorporate a switching cost
g
whenever the drift changes (see
2.6
). Hence, in full, the QVI reads:

\displaystyle\min

\displaystyle\left\{v^{n}_{+1,x}-\gamma v^{n+1}_{+1,x}-\sum_{x^{\prime}\in\mathcal{S}^{x}_{n}}\frac{1}{|x^{\prime}|+1}\binom{n}{k}\theta^{k}(1-\theta)^{n-k},\right.

\displaystyle\quad\left.v^{n}_{+1,x}-\gamma\sum_{x^{\prime}\in\mathcal{S}^{x}_{n}}\binom{n}{k}\theta^{k}(1-\theta)^{n-k}\left(\frac{1}{|x^{\prime}|+1}+\max\{v^{1}_{+1,x^{\prime}},v^{1}_{-1,x^{\prime}}-g\}\right)+c_{\mathrm{obs}}\right\}=0,

\displaystyle\min

\displaystyle\left\{v^{n}_{-1,x}-\gamma v^{n+1}_{-1,x}-\sum_{x^{\prime}\in\mathcal{S}^{x}_{n}}\frac{1}{|x^{\prime}|+1}\binom{n}{k}\theta^{n-k}(1-\theta)^{k},\right.

\displaystyle\quad\left.v^{n}_{-1,x}-\gamma\sum_{x^{\prime}\in\mathcal{S}^{x}_{n}}\binom{n}{k}\theta^{n-k}(1-\theta)^{k}\left(\frac{1}{|x^{\prime}|+1}+\max\{v^{1}_{+1,x^{\prime}}-g,v^{1}_{-1,x^{\prime}}\}\right)+c_{\mathrm{obs}}\right\}=0.

(4.3)

To close the system to ensure a unique solution, we enforce the following time and spatial boundary conditions. We impose a reflecting boundary at
x=\pm L
, where
L
is suitably large. In particular,

\displaystyle p(L\mid L,+1)=\theta,\quad

\displaystyle p(L-1\mid L,+1)=1-\theta,

\displaystyle p(L\mid L,-1)=1-\theta,\quad

\displaystyle p(L-1\mid L,-1)=\theta,

\displaystyle p(-L\mid-L,+1)=1-\theta,\quad

\displaystyle p(-L+1\mid-L,+1)=\theta,

\displaystyle p(-L\mid-L,-1)=\theta,\quad

\displaystyle p(-L+1\mid-L,-1)=1-\theta,

(4.4)

so that the QVI (
4.2
) for the states
x=\pm L
will use the transition probabilities (
4.1
) instead.

For the time boundary, we enforce an observation at some large time
N>0
. The terminal condition then reads (for
-L<x<L
):

\displaystyle\begin{cases}v^{N}_{+1,x}-\gamma\sum_{x^{\prime}\in\mathcal{S}^{x}_{N}}\binom{N}{k^{\prime}}\theta^{k}(1-\theta)^{N-k^{\prime}}\left(\frac{1}{|x^{\prime}|+1}+\max\{v^{1}_{+1,x^{\prime}},v^{1}_{-1,x^{\prime}}-g\}\right)+c_{\mathrm{obs}}=0,\\
v^{N}_{-1,x}-\gamma\sum_{x^{\prime}\in\mathcal{S}^{x}_{N}}\binom{N}{k^{\prime}}\theta^{N-k^{\prime}}(1-\theta)^{k^{\prime}}\left(\frac{1}{|x^{\prime}|+1}+\max\{v^{1}_{+1,x^{\prime}}-g,v^{1}_{-1,x^{\prime}}\}\right)+c_{\mathrm{obs}}=0.\end{cases}

(4.5)

where
k^{\prime}=(N+x^{\prime}-x)/2
. The analogous equations hold for the spatial boundary
x=\pm L
, but with the transition probabilities (
4.1
). These terminal conditions can be interpreted as the largest possible interval between two observations.

We now proceed to solve the penalised problem for the system (
4.1
), with boundary conditions (
4.1
) and (
4.5
), through the use of semismooth Newton methods. To initialise the iteration, we solve for the uncoupled system

\begin{cases}v^{n}_{+1,x}-\gamma v^{n+1}_{+1,x}-\sum_{x^{\prime}\in\mathcal{S}^{x}_{n}}\frac{1}{|x^{\prime}|+1}\binom{n}{k}\theta^{k}(1-\theta)^{n-k}=0,\\
v^{n}_{-1,x}-\gamma v^{n+1}_{-1,x}-\sum_{x^{\prime}\in\mathcal{S}^{x}_{n}}\frac{1}{|x^{\prime}|+1}\binom{n}{k}\theta^{n-k}(1-\theta)^{k}=0,\\
0\leq n<N,\ -L<x<L,\end{cases}

(4.6)

with the spatial boundary transition probabilities (
4.1
) and uncoupled time boundary condition

\begin{cases}v^{N}_{+1,x}-\gamma\sum_{x^{\prime}\in\mathcal{S}^{x}_{N}}\binom{N}{k^{\prime}}\theta^{{k}^{\prime}}(1-\theta)^{N-k^{\prime}}\left(\frac{1}{|x^{\prime}|+1}+v^{1}_{+1,x^{\prime}}\right)+c_{\mathrm{obs}}=0,\\
v^{N}_{-1,x}-\gamma\sum_{x^{\prime}\in\mathcal{S}^{x}_{N}}\binom{N}{k^{\prime}}\theta^{N-k^{\prime}}(1-\theta)^{k^{\prime}}\left(\frac{1}{|x^{\prime}|+1}+v^{1}_{-1,x^{\prime}}\right)+c_{\mathrm{obs}}=0,\\
-L<x<L.\end{cases}

The system (
4.6
) corresponds to the penalised equation with penalty parameter
\rho=0
. The uncoupled time boundary condition is equivalent to enforcing an observation but with no switching (i.e., assuming that
\overline{v}=v_{a}
in each equation for
v_{a}
). The iteration terminates once a relative tolerance threshold of
10^{-8}
is reached.

4.2
Extension of an HIV-treatment model

In this subsection, we implement our formulation of the OCM to an HIV-treatment scheduling problem in
[
34
]
. There, the authors modelled the problem with a continuous-time MDP with observation costs, but does not include the time elapsed variable in dynamic programming. This can be interpreted as an implicit assumption that the observer is given the state of the underlying process at initialisation. We shall implement a discretised version of their model under our formulation with the time elapsed variable. As alluded to in the introduction, this allows in addition initial conditions that are outdated or sub-optimal relative to the objective. We demonstrate the qualitative difference in the optimal policies when varying the initial conditions, whilst replicating the results in the original paper when the initial conditions coincide. We also examine the numerical performance of the penalty method when applied to the system of QVIs for this larger system, compared to that in Section
4.1
.

We now proceed to describe the original problem in
[
34
]
. A continuous-time MDP is used to model virus levels of HIV-positive patients over time. With two types of treatment available, the action space is
A=\{0,1,2\}
(where 0 represents no treatment given). Four virus strains are considered: WT denotes the wild type (susceptible to both treatments), R1 and R2 denotes strains that are each resistant to Treatment 1 and Treatment 2 respectively, and HR denotes the strain that is highly resistant to both. The level of each strain is represented by the states ‘none’ (0), ‘low’ (
l
), ‘medium’ (
m
), and ‘high’ (
h
). Therefore, the state space for the Markov chain is
{\mathcal{X}=\{0,l,m,h\}^{4}\ \cup\{\ast\}}
, where the asterisk represents patient death. Note in particular that
*
is an absorbing state. The goal in the original model is to then minimise a cost functional
J:\mathcal{X}\times A\to\mathbb{R}
of the form:

J(x,\alpha)=\mathbb{E}\left[\sum^{\infty}_{j=0}\left(\int^{\tau_{j+1}}_{\tau_{j}}e^{-\gamma s}c(X_{s},\iota(X_{\tau_{j}}))\ ds+e^{-\gamma\tau_{j+1}}c_{\mathrm{obs}}\right)\right],

where
\{\tau_{j}\}^{\infty}_{j=0}
are the observation times, and the cost function
c:\mathcal{X}\times A\to\mathbb{R}
is a linear combination of the productivity loss resulting from each patient’s condition and their received treatment.

To adapt the model above for our formulation, we first discretise the MDP, taking each step to represent one day. We then take the model parameters from the original article
[
34
, Section 3]
, which provides the transition rate matrices
\{Q_{a}\}_{i\in A}
and the cost function
c(x,a)
. The transition matrices
\{P_{a}\}_{a\in A}
are then given by
P_{a}=e^{Q_{a}}
(as the time unit in
[
34
]
is one day). For illustration purposes, a sparse plot of the transition matrix
P_{0}
is shown in
Figure

4.3
. As we are considering maximisation problems in this article, we take
r=-c
for the reward function. We can now formulate our problem in terms of the following QVI:

\displaystyle\min\bigg\{

\displaystyle v^{n}_{a,x}-\gamma v^{n+1}_{a,x}+\Big(e^{nQ_{a}}c_{a}\Big)_{x}\ ,\ v^{n}_{a,x}-\Big(e^{nQ_{a}}\overline{\gamma v^{1}+c}\Big)_{x}+c_{\mathrm{obs}}\bigg\}=0.

Figure 4.3:
Sparsity pattern of the transition matrix
P_{0}
(the pattern is the same across all control states). The state space is encoded as
\{1,\ldots,256\}
, by considering the state vectors [WT, R1, R2, HR] as a base-
4

[h,0,l,l]

*
is represented by

We now follow the same procedure in
Section

4.1
to obtain a numerical solution. Note that for this problem, the spatial domain is finite and we also have a natural spatial boundary arising from the absorbing death state
\ast
, that is, for all
n\geq 0
and
a\in A
,

v^{n}_{a,\ast}=\sum^{\infty}_{k=0}l\gamma^{k}=\frac{l}{1-\gamma},

where
l
is a constant representing the average GDP loss due to patient death, the value of which is taken from the parameters in
[
34
,
33
]
. A time boundary is once again enforced at some large time
N>0
, which can be interpreted as a mandatory observation at time
N
. Explicitly, this reads

v^{N}_{a,x}-\Big(e^{NQ_{a}}\overline{\gamma v^{1}+c}\Big)_{x}+c_{\mathrm{obs}}=0,\quad x\in\mathcal{X}\setminus\{\ast\},\ a\in A.

We now solve the associated penalised problem with semismooth Newton methods. As in
Section

4.1
, we choose the initial guess to be the solution to the penalised problem with
\rho=0
, with uncoupled time boundary conditions. The iterations terminate once a relative tolerance threshold of
10^{-8}
is reached. The numerical experiments are performed on MATLAB R2019b.

We now analyse the behaviour of the value function when plotted as a function against time. The top-left graph of
Figure

4.6
depicts an instance where the patient is under a stable condition. Here the observation region is
[15,N]
. There are limited benefits of frequently paying a high observation cost when it is unlikely that the patient’s condition will deteriorate over a short period of time. On the other hand, the top-right graph has an observation region of

with high probability, and the negative reward associated with this absorbing state outweighs any potential benefits of paying the observation cost
c_{\mathrm{obs}}
for information. In the original model in
[

]
, one determines the optimal policy based on an immediate observation in hand. Putting this in the context of our formulation, this amounts to fixing an initial condition in the form of

, and looking forward ahead in time to find the first observation time. This overlooks situations such as that occurring in the top-right graph of

4.6
: by initialising at the time origin, one immediately ‘loops back’ for an immediate observation, and therefore does not see the effect of the passage of time on the optimal observation policy.

, underneath their respective graphs of the value function. If we consider the plots as a discretisation of a continuous value function, we see that there is much bigger variation within the observation region. Critically, there is non-smoothness across the boundary in the bottom-left graph. This suggests that the solution in continuous-time is
C^{2}
in time within each decision region, but only
C^{1}
across the boundary. This is in line with theoretical results on the regularity of viscosity solutions in optimal stopping and switching problems
[

, Chapter 5]
, which is a potential direction for future analysis.

5
Observation cost with parameter uncertainty

For this final section, we consider an extrension of the OCM with parameter uncertainty in the dynamics of the Markov chain. We shall adopt the approach of Bayesian adaptive control. Suppose that the transition kernel
p
now depends on an unknown parameter
\theta\in\Theta
, where
\Theta
denotes a finite parameter space. We write
p_{\theta}(\cdot\mid x,a)
for a fixed value of
\theta
. To consider a Markov system for the problem, we take
\mathcal{X}\times\Theta
as our underlying space, with transition kernel

\displaystyle\mathfrak{p}((x^{\prime},\theta^{\prime})\mid(x,\theta),a)\coloneqq\mathbbm{1}_{\{\theta=\theta^{\prime}\}}p_{\theta}(x^{\prime}\mid x,a),\quad(x,\theta),(x^{\prime},\theta^{\prime})\in\mathcal{X}\times\Theta,\ a\in A.

For each value of
\theta\in\Theta
, we associate a probability measure
p^{\theta}_{0}\in\Delta_{\mathcal{X}}
, so that the initial distribution is given by

\displaystyle\mathfrak{p}_{0}(x,\theta)=\sum_{\theta\in\Theta}p^{\theta}_{0}(x)\rho_{0}(\theta)

for some
\rho_{0}\in\Delta_{\Theta}
. Given the half-step construction as laid out in the beginning of this section, the OCM with parameter uncertainty can be written as a POMDP
\langle\mathcal{X}\times\Theta,\mathcal{X}_{\varnothing},\mathcal{A},\mathfrak{p},\mathfrak{p}_{0},\mathfrak{q},\mathfrak{q}_{0},r_{\mathrm{obs}}\rangle
over the timescale
\frac{1}{2}\mathbb{N}
, where the domain of the transition kernel
\mathfrak{p}
is extended to
(\mathcal{X}\times\Theta)\times\mathcal{A}
by defining

\displaystyle\mathfrak{p}(\cdot\mid x,\theta,i)=\delta_{(x,\theta)}(\cdot),\quad i\in\mathcal{I},

and the observation kernel
\mathfrak{q}
is now defined on
\mathcal{A}\times(\mathcal{X}\times\Theta)
, with

\displaystyle\mathfrak{q}(\cdot\mid a,x,\theta)

\displaystyle=\delta_{\varnothing}(\cdot),\quad a\in A,

\displaystyle\mathfrak{q}(\cdot\mid i,x,\theta)

\displaystyle=i\cdot\delta_{x}(\cdot)+(1-i)\delta_{\varnothing}(\cdot),\quad i\in\mathcal{I}.

The initial observation kernel
\mathfrak{q}_{0}
will be taken as
\mathfrak{q}_{0}(\cdot\mid x,\theta)=\delta_{x}(\cdot)
(see
2.3
). The set of admissible policies, denoted by
\Pi^{\Theta}_{\mathrm{obs}}
in this case, can be established analogously as in
2.2
. Denote the canonical measure here by
\mathbb{P}^{\pi}_{\mathfrak{p}_{0}}
, under which
\theta
can be considered as a constant process, i.e.
\theta_{n+1}\equiv\theta_{n}
with
\theta_{0}\sim\rho_{0}\in\Delta_{\Theta}
, and
\rho_{0}
can be interpreted as a prior estimate for
\theta
.

When considering the belief MDP for this problem, the observable sequence at time
t
remains as previously,

\displaystyle h_{t}=(\bar{x}_{0},\pi_{0},\ldots,\bar{x}_{t-1/2},\pi_{t-1/2},\bar{x}_{t})\in\mathcal{H}_{t}.

Then, the belief state
\mathbb{P}^{\pi}_{\mathfrak{p}_{0}}(x_{t},\theta_{t}\mid h_{t})
can be decomposed as

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}^{\pi}_{\mathfrak{p}_{0}}(x_{t},\theta_{t}\mid h_{t})

\displaystyle=\sum_{\theta\in\Theta}\mathbb{P}^{\pi}_{\mathfrak{p}_{0}}(x_{t}\mid\theta_{t},h_{t})\ \mathbb{P}^{\pi}_{p_{0}}(\theta_{t}\mid h_{t}).

(5.1)

For a fixed value of
\theta
,
{\mathbb{P}^{\pi}_{\mathfrak{p}_{0}}(x_{t}\mid\theta_{t},h_{t})}
has a finite dimensional characterisation by the Markov property. As in the previous section, we denote this characterisation by
y=(y_{t})_{t}
, which is a tuple given by

\displaystyle y_{t}\coloneqq\begin{cases}(k,x_{k},a_{k}),&\ \mbox{if $k=\argmax_{n\leq t}\{i_{n}=1\}\neq t$ },\\
(t,x_{t},\varnothing),&\ \mbox{otherwise}.\end{cases}

The second term on the right hand side of (
5.1
),
\mathbb{P}^{\pi}_{\mathfrak{p}_{0}}(\theta_{t}\mid h_{t})
can be interpreted as the posterior distribution of
\theta
at time
t
. Denote this term by
\rho_{t}(\theta_{t})
. Note that
\rho_{t}
can be computed online via the classical Bayes’ Theorem,

\displaystyle\rho_{t}(\theta)

\displaystyle=\frac{\mathbb{P}^{\pi}_{\mathfrak{p}_{0}}(\bar{x}_{t}\mid\theta,h_{t-\frac{1}{2}},\pi_{t-\frac{1}{2}})}{\sum_{{\theta}^{\prime}\in\Theta}\mathbb{P}^{\pi}_{\mathfrak{p}_{0}}(\bar{x}_{t}\mid{\theta}^{\prime},h_{t-\frac{1}{2}},\pi_{t-\frac{1}{2}})\ \rho_{t-\frac{1}{2}}({\theta}^{\prime})}\ \rho_{t-\frac{1}{2}}(\theta).

(5.2)

In the OCM, observations only occur at the half steps, therefore we have in fact
\rho_{n}\equiv\rho_{n-1/2}
for
n\in\mathbb{N}
. Thus, the update (
5.2
) can be reduced to

\displaystyle\rho_{n}(\theta)

\displaystyle=\frac{\mathbb{P}^{\pi}_{\mathfrak{p}_{0}}(\bar{x}_{n-\frac{1}{2}}\mid\theta,y_{n-1},i_{n-1})}{\sum_{{\theta}^{\prime}\in\Theta}\mathbb{P}^{\pi}_{\mathfrak{p}_{0}}(\bar{x}_{n-\frac{1}{2}}\mid{\theta}^{\prime},y_{n-1},i_{n-1})\ \rho_{n-1}({\theta}^{\prime})}\ \rho_{n-1}(\theta)

\displaystyle\eqqcolon U(\rho_{n-1},y_{n-1},i_{n-1}),\quad n\in\mathbb{N}.

(5.3)

The belief state at time
t
can now be represented by
y_{t}\in\mathcal{Y}
and
\rho_{t}\in\Delta_{\Theta}
, with its transitions given by the kernel
{\mathfrak{p}}^{\prime}=({\mathfrak{p}}^{\prime}_{t})_{t\in\frac{1}{2}\mathbb{N}}
on
\mathcal{Y}\times\Delta_{\Theta}
, given
(\mathcal{Y}\times\Delta_{\Theta})\times\mathcal{A}
:

\displaystyle{\mathfrak{p}}^{\prime}_{n}({y}^{\prime},{\rho}^{\prime}\mid y,\rho,i)

\displaystyle=i\cdot\sum_{\theta\in\Theta}p^{(n-k)}_{\theta}(\hat{x}\mid x,a)\rho(\theta)\ \mathbbm{1}_{\{{y}^{\prime}=(n,\hat{x},\varnothing),\ {\rho}^{\prime}=U(\rho,y,i)\}}

\displaystyle\qquad+(1-i)\mathbbm{1}_{\{\hat{y}=y,\ {\rho}^{\prime}=\rho\}},\

\displaystyle y=(k,x,a),

\displaystyle{\mathfrak{p}}^{\prime}_{n+\frac{1}{2}}({y}^{\prime},{\rho}^{\prime}\mid y,\rho,a^{\prime})

\displaystyle=\mathbbm{1}_{\{{y}^{\prime}=(k,x,a^{\prime}),\ {a}^{\prime}=a,\ {\rho}^{\prime}=\rho\}},\

\displaystyle y=(k,x,a),

\displaystyle{\mathfrak{p}}^{\prime}_{n+\frac{1}{2}}({y}^{\prime},{\rho}^{\prime}\mid y,\rho,a^{\prime})

\displaystyle=\mathbbm{1}_{\{\hat{y}=(n,x,a^{\prime}),\ {\rho}^{\prime}=\rho\}},\

\displaystyle y=(n,x,\varnothing).

As before, we shall not distinguish between policies for the POMDP and policies for the belief state MDP. For the finite horizon problem, let
y=(k,x,a)\in\mathcal{Y}
,
\rho\in\Delta_{\Theta}
, and consider

J(t,y,\rho,\pi)=\mathbb{E}^{\pi}_{y,\rho}\left[\sum^{2N}_{n=t}r^{\rho}_{n/2}\left(y_{\frac{n}{2}},\pi_{\frac{n}{2}}\right)\right],\quad 0\leq t\leq 2N,\ \pi\in\Pi_{\mathrm{obs}},

(5.4)

where for
y=(k,x,a)
,
i\in\mathcal{I}
,
a^{\prime}\in A
,
n\in\mathbb{N}
,

\displaystyle r^{\rho}_{n}(y,i)

\displaystyle=-i\cdot c_{\mathrm{obs}},

\displaystyle r^{\rho}_{n+\frac{1}{2}}(y,a^{\prime})

\displaystyle=\sum_{\theta\in\Theta}\sum_{x^{\prime}\in\mathcal{X}}r(x^{\prime},a^{\prime})p^{(n-k)}_{\theta}(x^{\prime}\mid x,a)\rho(\theta),

and for
y=(n,x,\varnothing)
,

\displaystyle r^{\rho}_{n+\frac{1}{2}}(y,a^{\prime})

\displaystyle=r(x,a^{\prime}).

The value function is

v(t,y,\rho)=\sup_{\pi\in\Pi_{\mathrm{obs}}}J(t,y,\rho,\pi).

(5.5)

As in the previous case without parameter uncertainty, the dynamic programming equation can be reduced to only the integer time steps. We state the optimality equation for the observation cost model under parameter uncertainty below.

Proposition 5.1
.

For
y=(k,x,a)\in\mathcal{Y}
and
\rho\in\Delta_{\Theta}
, the value function (
5.5
) satisfies the following equation:

\displaystyle v(n,(k,x,a),\rho)=

\displaystyle\max\Bigg\{v(n+1,(k,x,a),\rho)+\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\theta\in\Theta\\
x^{\prime}\in\mathcal{X}\end{subarray}}p^{(n-k)}_{\theta}(x^{\prime}\mid x,a)r(x^{\prime},a)\rho(\theta),

\displaystyle\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\theta\in\Theta\\
x^{\prime}\in\mathcal{X}\end{subarray}}p^{(n-k)}_{\theta}(x^{\prime}\mid x,a)\rho(\theta)\ \Big[\max_{a^{\prime}\in A}\Big(v(n+1,(n,x^{\prime},a^{\prime}),\rho^{\prime})+r(x^{\prime},a^{\prime})\Big)\Big]-c_{\mathrm{obs}}\Bigg\},

(5.6)

where
\rho^{\prime}=U(\rho,y,1)
as in (
5.3
).

For the infinite horizon case, a similar stationary argument leads us to the objective function and value function:

\displaystyle J(y,\rho,\pi)

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}^{\pi}\left[\sum^{\infty}_{n=0}\gamma^{\lfloor\frac{n}{2}\rfloor}r^{\rho}\left(y_{\frac{n}{2}},\pi_{\frac{n}{2}}\right)\right],\ y\in\mathcal{Y},\ \rho\in\Delta_{\Theta},\ \pi\in\Pi_{\mathrm{obs}},

\displaystyle v(y,\rho)

\displaystyle=\sup_{\pi\in\Pi_{\mathrm{obs}}}J(y,\rho,\pi).

(5.7)

Proposition 5.2
.

For
y=(n,x,a)\in\mathcal{Y}
and
\rho\in\Delta_{\Theta}
, the value function (
5.7
) satisfies the following equation:

\displaystyle v((n,x,a),\rho)=

\displaystyle\max\Bigg\{\gamma v((n+1,x,a),\rho)+\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\theta\in\Theta\\
x^{\prime}\in\mathcal{X}\end{subarray}}p^{(n)}_{\theta}(x^{\prime}\mid x,a)r(x^{\prime},a)\rho(\theta),

\displaystyle\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\theta\in\Theta\\
x^{\prime}\in\mathcal{X}\end{subarray}}p^{(n)}_{\theta}(x^{\prime}\mid x,a)\rho(\theta)\ \Big[\max_{a^{\prime}\in A}\Big(\gamma v((1,x^{\prime},a^{\prime}),\rho^{\prime})+r(x^{\prime},a^{\prime})\Big)\Big]-c_{\mathrm{obs}}\Bigg\},

(5.8)

where
\rho^{\prime}=U(\rho,y,1)
as in (
5.3
).

It is worth noting that both (
5.1
) and (
5.2
) are MDPs over the augmented space
{\mathcal{Y}\times\Delta_{\Theta}}
. The inclusion of the simplex
\Delta_{\Theta}
makes the MDP non-discrete. For computation, one would have to approximate the solution, either via computing a discrete MDP on a finite grid on
{\mathcal{Y}\times\Delta_{\Theta}}
, or via functional approximation methods such as the use of neural networks on larger scale problems. We refer the reader to the textbook
[
21
]
and survey paper
[
31
]
for a comprehensive review of choosing appropriate approximating grids. Then, given a finite grid
\mathbb{G}=\{s_{1},\ldots,s_{G}\}
on the simplex
\Delta_{\Theta}
, one can define the approximating transition kernels by

\displaystyle\mathfrak{p}_{\mathbb{G}}({y}^{\prime},s_{i}\mid y,s_{j},\pi)=\frac{{\mathfrak{p}}^{\prime}({y}^{\prime},s_{i}\mid y,s_{j},a)}{\sum_{i=1}^{G}{\mathfrak{p}}^{\prime}({y}^{\prime},s_{i}\mid y,s_{j},a)},\quad y,{y}^{\prime}\in\mathcal{Y},\ s_{i},s_{j}\in\mathbb{G},\ \pi\in\mathcal{A},

so that one solves the approximating finite MDP on
\mathcal{Y}\times\mathbb{G}
instead. For the QVIs resulting from the observation cost problems, we can solve the MDPs by penalisation, as detailed in
Section

3
.

5.1
Random walk with drift with parameter uncertainty

In this subsection, we consider a random walk with drift, as set up in
Section

4.1
, but with the additional assumption that the true value of the drift parameter
\theta
is unknown to the user. To avoid complications with boundary conditions and infinite domains, we shall only consider the finite horizon problem. Recall that for a fixed value of
\theta
and constant action, the
n
-step transition probabilities are given by

\displaystyle p^{(n)}_{\theta}(x^{\prime}\mid x,+1)=\binom{n}{k}\theta^{k}(1-\theta)^{n-k},\ p^{(n)}_{\theta}(x^{\prime}\mid x,-1)=\binom{n}{k}\theta^{n-k}(1-\theta)^{k},\quad{x}^{\prime}\in\mathcal{S}^{x}_{n},

where
\mathcal{S}^{x}_{n}
is the set of states that can be reached from
x
after
n
units of time, and
k=\frac{1}{2}(n+x^{\prime}-x)
. We choose the prior from a family of beta distributions to obtain conjugacy in the parameter distributions. The posterior can be updated as follows. Suppose the prior
\rho_{0}\sim\mathrm{Beta}(\alpha,\beta)
and the next observation occurs at time
n
at a state
{x}^{\prime}\in\mathcal{X}
. Then a standard calculation shows that

\displaystyle\int_{\Theta}p^{(n)}_{\theta}(x^{\prime}\mid x,+1)\ \rho_{0}(d\theta)=g(k\mid n,\alpha,\beta),\ \int_{\Theta}p^{(n)}_{\theta}(x^{\prime}\mid x,-1)\ \rho_{0}(d\theta)=g(n-k\mid n,\alpha,\beta),

where

g(k\mid n,\alpha,\beta)=\binom{n}{k}\frac{B(k+\alpha,n-k+\beta)}{B(\alpha,\beta)},\ k=\frac{1}{2}(n+{x}^{\prime}-x),

and
g
is the probability mass function of the Beta-binomial distribution,
B(\alpha,\beta)
is the Beta function. The posterior distribution is then given by

\displaystyle\rho_{n}\sim\begin{cases}\mathrm{Beta}(\alpha+k,\beta+n-k),&a_{0}=+1,\\
\mathrm{Beta}(\alpha+n-k,\beta+k),&a_{0}=-1.\\
\end{cases}

The distributions
\{\rho_{n}\}
can then be described by a finite number of values over a finite time horizon. Hence we write
v(n,(k,x,a),(\alpha,\beta))
for
v(n,y,\rho)
. Let us first consider the same reward function
r(x,a)=r(x)=\frac{1}{\lvert x\rvert+1}
as in the previous section. We can write the QVI (
5.1
) as

\displaystyle v(n,(k,x,a),(\alpha,\beta))

\displaystyle=\

\displaystyle\max\Bigg\{v(n+1,(k,x,a),(\alpha,\beta))+\sum_{x^{\prime}\in\mathcal{X}}{g}^{\prime}({x}^{\prime},x\mid n-k,\alpha,\beta)\ r({x}^{\prime}),

\displaystyle\ \sum_{x^{\prime}\in\mathcal{X}}{g}^{\prime}({x}^{\prime},x\mid n-k,\alpha,\beta)\Big[\max_{a^{\prime}\in A}\Big(v(n+1,(n,x^{\prime},a^{\prime}),({\alpha}^{\prime},{\beta}^{\prime}))+r(x^{\prime})\Big)\Big]-c_{\mathrm{obs}}\Bigg\},

where we define
{g}^{\prime}({x}^{\prime},x\mid n,\alpha,\beta)\coloneqq g((n-{x}^{\prime}+x)/2\mid k,\alpha,\beta)
. The terminal conditions are

v(N,(N-k,x,a),(\alpha,\beta))=\sum_{x^{\prime}\in\mathcal{S}^{x}_{k}}{g}^{\prime}({x}^{\prime},x\mid k,\alpha,\beta)\ r(x^{\prime}),\quad k<N.

For our experiment, we set the true value of
\theta=0.3
and a time horizon of
N=50
.
Figure

5.1
illustrates a sample realisation of an optimal trajectory, given a prior of
\mathrm{Beta}(5,2)
, as well as the evolution of the estimate over
\theta
over time.

Figure 5.1:
Left: sample realisation of the controlled random walk along the optimal trajectory. Right: prior and posterior distribution of
\theta
; the grey lines indicate ‘intermediate posteriors’ obtained from earlier observations.

We consider three different choices of
(\alpha,\beta)
for the prior
\rho_{0}
as well as varying the observation cost. For each parameter combination we compute the optimal policy and compare their respective performances across
5000
sampled trajectories. A typical criteria of measuring the performance of the policy is to examine its regret, defined as

\displaystyle\mathrm{reg}(N,\pi)=J^{\theta^{*}}_{0}(\pi^{*})-J^{N}(\pi),

where
J^{\theta^{*}}_{0}(\pi^{*})=\mathbb{E}^{\pi^{*}}[\sum r(x,a)]
is the reward functional with no observation cost, with known parameter
{\theta^{*}}
and optimal policy
{\pi^{*}}
, and
J^{N}(\pi)
is the reward functional (
5.4
) under a policy
{\pi\in\Pi_{\mathrm{obs}}}
. In this case we consider
\pi
to be the optimal policy under observation costs and parameter uncertainty. The regret is therefore the cumulative sum of the suboptimal gap from the optimal policy. For the observation cost problem, the control between observations are constant and therefore suboptimal in general, as such we do not expect the regret to achieve asymptotically sublinear growth. Instead, we consider the following alternative criteria:

\mathrm{reg}_{c_{\mathrm{obs}}}(N,\pi)=J^{\theta^{*}}_{c_{\mathrm{obs}}}(\pi^{*})-J^{N}(\pi),

where here
J^{\theta^{*}}_{c_{\mathrm{obs}}}
is the reward functional with known parameter
\theta^{*}
and observation cost
c_{\mathrm{obs}}
, so that
\mathrm{reg}_{c_{\mathrm{obs}}}
measures the contribution of the regret that arises from parameter uncertainty. On the left of
Figure

5.2
, we show the overall regret for varying the observation cost for a fixed prior, and on the right,
\mathrm{reg}_{c_{\mathrm{obs}}}
is plotted with a fixed observation cost of
c_{\mathrm{obs}}=0.1
across different initial priors
\rho_{0}
. As expected, the regret is generally higher when the prior estimate
\rho_{0}
is less accurate, or when a larger
c_{\mathrm{obs}}
value is used. Moreover the regret grows in a rather linear fashion. However, when examining the graph involving
\mathrm{reg}_{c_{\mathrm{obs}}}
on the right side, we empirically observe sublinear growth. This can be interpreted as a gradual learning of the unknown parameters, despite the fact that observations only arrive in intervals. The results suggests that
\mathrm{reg}_{c_{\mathrm{obs}}}
can be used as an alternative notion to capture the learning rate in problems involving observation costs, which we see as a possible direction for future analysis.
