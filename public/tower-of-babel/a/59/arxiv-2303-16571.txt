Correlations of tensor field components in isotropic systems with an application to stress correlations in elastic bodies
J.P. Wittmer, A.N. Semenov, J. Baschnagel

Abstract
Correlation functions of components of second-order tensor fields in isotropic systems can be reduced to an isotropic forth-order tensor field characterized by a few invariant correlation functions (ICFs). It is emphasized that components of this field depend in general on the coordinates of the field vector variable and thus on the orientation of the coordinate system. These angular dependencies are distinct from those of ordinary anisotropic systems. As a simple example of the procedure to obtain the ICFs we discuss correlations of time-averaged stresses in isotropic glasses where only one ICF in reciprocal space becomes a finite constant e e for large sampling times and small wavevectors. It is shown that e e is set by the typical size of the frozen-in stress components normal to the wavevectors, i.e. it is caused by the symmetry breaking of the stress for each independent configuration. Using the presented general mathematical formalism for isotropic tensor fields this finding explains in turn the observed long-range stress correlations in real space. Under additional but rather general assumptions e e is shown to be given by a thermodynamic quantity, the equilibrium Young modulus E E . We thus relate for certain isotropic amorphous bodies the existence of finite Young or shear moduli to the symmetry breaking of a stress component in reciprocal space.

Isotropic tensors and tensor fields

Isotropic tensors.

Isotropic systems are described by “isotropic tensors” and “isotropic tensor fields”.
Components of an isotropic tensor remain unchanged by
any
orthogonal coordinate transformation

;
Tadmor et al. 2012

T_{\alpha_{1}\ldots\alpha_{o}}^{\ast}=T_{\alpha_{1}\ldots\alpha_{o}}.

(10)

As noted at the end of Sec.

for a reflection of one axis
if the number of indices equal to the inverted axis is

Consistency with Eq. (
10

T_{12}=T_{1112}=T_{1222}=T_{1234}=T_{1344}=0.

Isotropic tensor fields.

The corresponding isotropy condition for tensor fields is given by

Another way to state this is to say that the left hand fields are computed at

the “actively transformed” vector
\mbox{$\bf q$}^{\ast}=(q_{1}^{\ast},\ldots,q_{d}^{\ast})

It is for this reason that Eq. (
11

does not hold in general for tensor fields,
i.e. finite components with an odd number of equal indices, e.g.,

II.7
Response to point sources

Isotropic tensor fields may also be constructed by taking
the (inner or outer) product of a tensor field and a (constant) tensor.
We focus here on the second-order tensor field

R_{\alpha\beta}(\mbox{$\bf q$})=\frac{1}{V}\ C_{\alpha\beta\gamma\delta}(\mbox{$\bf q$})\ s_{\gamma\delta}

(35)

obtained from a forth-order tensor field
C_{\alpha\beta\gamma\delta}(\mbox{$\bf q$})
and a second-order
tensor
s_{\alpha\beta}
and where (as always) summation over repeated indices is implied.
For later convenience we have introduced the volume
V
of the system.
The results presented below are readily generalized for different types of products
of tensor fields and tensors and for dimensions other than
d=2
.

If both
C_{\alpha\beta\gamma\delta}(\mbox{$\bf q$})
and
s_{\alpha\beta}
are isotropic,
the product theorem Eq. (
14
) discussed in Sec.
II.2

implies that
R_{\alpha\beta}(\mbox{$\bf q$})
must also be an isotropic tensor field.
Under the additional assumptions stated in Sec.
II.3

R_{\alpha\beta}(\mbox{$\bf q$})

is then given by Eq. (
24
) in terms of two invariants
k_{1}(q)
and
k_{2}(q)
.
These invariants can in turn be expressed in terms of the invariants of

C_{\alpha\beta\gamma\delta}(\mbox{$\bf q$})
and
s_{\alpha\beta}
.
It is important to emphasize that albeit being closely related
R_{\alpha\beta}(\mbox{$\bf q$})
and

C_{\alpha\beta\gamma\delta}(\mbox{$\bf q$})
have in general different angular dependences.
This may be readily seen by focusing on the specific cases
R_{12}(\mbox{$\bf q$})
and
C_{1212}(\mbox{$\bf q$})
.
It follows from Eq. (
24
) that

R_{12}(\mbox{$\bf q$})\propto{\hat{q}}_{1}{\hat{q}}_{2}\propto\sin(2\theta)
with

{\hat{q}}_{1}=\cos(\theta)
and
{\hat{q}}_{2}=\sin(\theta)

and from Eq. (
34
) that

C_{1212}(\mbox{$\bf q$})=i_{2}(q)+i_{4}(q){\hat{q}}_{1}^{2}{\hat{q}}_{2}^{2}
is given
by an angular-independent scalar plus a term proportional to
\cos(4\theta)
.
In other words,
R_{12}(\mbox{$\bf q$})
is a quadrupolar field
whereas
C_{1212}(\mbox{$\bf q$})
is octupolar
foo c
.
Both fields thus reveal distinct angular patterns.

For reasons which will become obvious below we shall call

R_{\alpha\beta}(\mbox{$\bf q$})
the “response”,

C_{\alpha\beta\gamma\delta}(\mbox{$\bf q$})
the “correlation function” or “propagator”
and
s_{\gamma\delta}
the “source” or “perturbation”.
Up to now we have not used that the field vector
\bf q
refers to the
wavevector characterizing fields in reciprocal space.
Using Eq. (
91
) it is seen that the tensor
s_{\alpha\beta}/V
in reciprocal space
corresponds to a “point source”

S_{\alpha\beta}(\mbox{$\bf r$})=s_{\alpha\beta}\delta(\mbox{$\bf r$})

in real space where we have used Dirac’s delta function. The response

R_{\alpha\beta}(\mbox{$\bf r$})={\cal F}^{-1}[R_{\alpha\beta}(\mbox{$\bf q$})]

in real space is then given by

R_{\alpha\beta}(\mbox{$\bf r$})=C_{\alpha\beta\gamma\delta}(\mbox{$\bf r$})s_{\gamma\delta}

(36)

using the correlation function

C_{\alpha\beta\gamma\delta}(\mbox{$\bf r$})={\cal F}^{-1}[C_{\alpha\beta\gamma\delta}(\mbox{$\bf q$})]

in real space.
For a more general source term
S_{\alpha\beta}(\mbox{$\bf r$})
we have of course a convolution relation

R_{\alpha\beta}(\mbox{$\bf r$})=\frac{1}{V}\int\mathrm{d}\mbox{$\bf r$}^{\prime}C_{\alpha\beta\gamma\delta}(\mbox{$\bf r$}-\mbox{$\bf r$}^{\prime})\ S_{\gamma\delta}(\mbox{$\bf r$}^{\prime})

(37)

which reduces to Eq. (
36
) for a point source.
Importantly, all statements made above for the reciprocal space remain valid in real space,
i.e. that
R_{\alpha\beta}(\mbox{$\bf r$})
is an isotropic tensor field if

C_{\alpha\beta\gamma\delta}(\mbox{$\bf r$})
and
S_{\alpha\beta}(\mbox{$\bf r$})
are isotropic
and, more specifically, that
R_{12}(\mbox{$\bf r$})
is a quadrupolar field while

C_{1212}(\mbox{$\bf r$})
is octupolar.

Importantly, in many physical situations the source is in fact
not
isotropic
and thus in turn the response field
not
consistent with Eq. (
24
).
We remind that, e.g., the mechanical response of amorphous solids under loading proceeds from
local and irreversible rearrangements, resetting disorder locally thus generating a highly
non-trivial mechanical noise (“shear transformation zones”)

Argon and Kuo 1979
;
Picard et al. 2004
;
Rodney et al. 2011
;
Nicolas et al. 2018
;
Falk and Langer 1998
.
According to a popular model of localized plastic failure two orthogonal twin force
dipoles of
opposite
signs may be imposed at the origin
Picard et al. 2004
.
This suggests to relax the isotropy condition for
s_{\alpha\beta}
.
Since the source tensor is still symmetric it may be diagonalized by an appropriate rotation
of the coordinate system where
s_{12}=s_{21}=0
and
s_{11}
and
s_{22}
become the two
(in general not identical) eigenvalues. Hence,

R_{\alpha\beta}(\mbox{$\bf q$})=s_{11}C_{\alpha\beta 11}(\mbox{$\bf q$})+s_{22}C_{\alpha\beta 22}(\mbox{$\bf q$})

with the isotropic correlation tensor field still being given by Eq. (
34
).
Specifically, this implies

R_{12}(\mbox{$\bf q$})={\hat{q}}_{1}{\hat{q}}_{2}\left[i_{3}(q)(s_{11}+s_{22})+i_{4}(q)\underline{(s_{11}{\hat{q}}_{1}^{2}+s_{22}{\hat{q}}_{2}^{2})}\right].

For
s_{11}=s_{22}
the underlined term becomes a constant
and we recover the isotropic and quadrupolar response field discussed above.
Interestingly, for eigenvalues of opposite sign,
s_{11}=-s_{22}
, we obtain

R_{12}(\mbox{$\bf q$})=s_{11}i_{4}(q)\ {\hat{q}}_{1}{\hat{q}}_{2}({\hat{q}}_{1}^{2}-{\hat{q}}_{2}^{2})\propto\sin(4\theta).

(38)

The (non-isotropic) response field
R_{12}(\mbox{$\bf q$})
thus has in this case the same multipole pattern
as the (isotropic) correlation field
C_{1212}(\mbox{$\bf q$})
albeit shifted by an angle
\pi/8

foo c
.
It is readily seen by inverse FT that the same behavior applies in real space.

In summary,
two different types of angular dependence of a response field must be distinguished.
If the angular dependence is consistent with Eq. (
24
),
this behavior should not be called “anisotropy” since the angle dependence is basically
a trivial consequence of the fact that tensor field components are measured.
If on the other hand
R_{\alpha\beta}(\mbox{$\bf q$})
is not consistent with Eq. (
24
)
this suggests that either the correlation tensor field
C_{\alpha\beta\gamma\delta}(\mbox{$\bf q$})

and/or the source tensor
s_{\alpha\beta}
are not isotropic. In many physical situations
this is in fact expected for the source term while the correlation fields may be assumed
to be isotropic. The physical behavior of response and correlation fields,
albeit closely related, then differ and should thus not be lumped together.

V.1
Introduction

The phenomenological parameter
e
was defined in Sec.
IV

by the limit Eq. (
40

Note that

has the same dimension energy/volume as the stress or an elastic modulus.
We have verified (cf. Fig.

indeed becomes
\Delta\tau

for sufficiently large sampling times

for our pLJ particle glasses.
Having fitted the value

and using that the ICFs

\bar{c}_{\perp}(q)
and
\bar{c}_{\mathrm{T}}(q)
vanish,
all numerical results for sufficiently large

We have observed, however, that

is similar to the (equilibrium static) Young modulus

Albeit not strictly necessary for the main thrust of this work,
a demonstration that both constants should be similar or even equal
— under to be specified assumptions and approximations — must be an important finding
allowing to estimate
apriori
the angular dependence of the correlation functions.
This can indeed be done following Refs.
Klochko et al. 2018
;
Klochko et al. 2022
and
in agreement with a different and complementary approach developed in Refs.
Maier et al. 2017
;
Maier et al. 2018
;
Vogel et al. 2019
.
Naturally, this requires additional physical input.
We remind first in Sec.
V.2
how the correlation functions

\bar{c}(\Delta\tau)
of time-averaged fields, the focus of the present work, are related to the
correlation functions
c(\tau)
of instantaneous fields under the assumption that the relevant
stochastic processes are
stationary

foo b
.
A short recap of linear viscoelasticity is given in Sec.
V.3
.
Using the general theoretical predictions for the ICFs of instantaneous stress fields

Klochko et al. 2018
;
Klochko et al. 2022
, reminded in Sec.

,
it is shown (cf. Sec.

for

V.2
Instantaneous and time-averaged fields

General connection for stationary processes.

We discuss in this work spatial correlation functions
\bar{c}
(both in real and

instantaneous configurations.
The various correlation functions thus depend in principle on the sampling time

As shown elsewhere

Allen and Tildesley 2017

This has the advantage that the integral Eq. (

irrelevant high frequencies, i.e.

is a natural smoothing function
of the instantaneous field correlation function

on which previous work has focused on

Plateau values and asymptotic behavior.

Obviously, Eq. (

Hence, if

Generalized Maxwell model.

It follows directly from Eq. (
49

\displaystyle\bar{c}(\Delta\tau)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle c_{p}D(\Delta\tau/\tau_{p})\mbox{ with }

\displaystyle D(x)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle 2[\exp(-x)-1+x]/x^{2}

Rubinstein and Colby 2003

For systems with overdamped dynamics, such as for our MC simulations,

of exponentially decaying Maxwell modes

;
Ferry 1980

For such a “Generalized Maxwell model” Eq. (

to the superposition

)

Note that

corresponds to the modes with virtually infinite relaxation times.
We remind that given a sufficiently high number of modes

(or a distribution of modes)

standard numerical techniques

;
Provencher 1982
;
Klochko et al. 2022
.
In any case, for
\Delta\tau\gg\tau_{\star}

\tau_{\star}=\tau_{1}

(54)

\Delta\tau

4

Stress relaxation tensor for isotropic systems.

The static elastic modulus tensor

can be generalized in the time domain

;
Rubinstein and Colby 2003

Evans and Morriss 1990

This is important in general for viscoelastic fluids but also for the dynamical response
of (crystalline or amorphous) solid bodies.
The forth-order stress relaxation tensor
E_{\alpha\beta\gamma\delta}(\tau)
characterizes the time-dependence
of the stress increment
\delta\sigma_{\alpha\beta}(\tau)
as a function of an imposed strain
\epsilon_{\alpha\beta}(\tau)

Generalizing Eq. (

\displaystyle E_{\alpha\beta\gamma\delta}(\tau)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle M(\tau)\ \delta_{\alpha\beta}\delta_{\gamma\delta}

\displaystyle G(\tau)\ \left(\delta_{\alpha\gamma}\delta_{\beta\delta}+\delta_{\alpha\delta}\delta_{\beta\gamma}\right)

two
invariant response functions.

Note that

being the known Lamé moduli.

for two different MC variants.
As expected for equilibrium liquids the shear modulus vanishes,
\mu=0
, for the variant
with swap MC hopping moves (squares) used for the equilibration of our systems.
The total ensemble of independent configurations

thus corresponds to a liquid system
while each configuration

We note for later convenience that, hence,

L(\tau)-\frac{M(\tau)^{2}}{L(\tau)}\to\lambda+2\mu-\frac{\lambda}{\lambda+2\mu}=4\mu\frac{\lambda+\mu}{\lambda+2\mu},

(63)

being due to Eq. (

VI
Conclusion

Summary.

After presenting in Sec.
II
a survey of general useful mathematical relations
for isotropic tensor fields we have focused in Sec.
IV
on the numerical description of spatial
correlations of stress tensor fields in isotropic pLJ particle systems
as described in Sec.
III
and Appendix
C
.
The phenomenological parameter
e

was related to the Young modulus
E
in Sec.
V
.
Several general results deserve to be emphasized.

Angular dependences.

As demonstrated by our computational example,
correlation functions of tensor field components of perfectly
isotropic systems must generally depend on the components of the field
vector and not only on its magnitude, cf. Eq. (
43
).
They thus depend both on the direction of this vector in the physical system (angle
\theta
)
and on the orientation of the coordinate system (angle
\alpha
) as shown in Fig.
1
.
Importantly, the angular dependence of all correlation functions
boils down to a dependence on the difference
\theta-\alpha
for all
\theta
and
\alpha

as shown in panel (b) of Fig.
1
and Fig.
5
.
Obviously,
this simple scaling (without characteristic angles) cannot hold for true anisotropic
systems which have material functions depending explicitly on the direction of the field vector.
Unfortunately, many recent studies do not clarify, e.g., simply by rotating the coordinate system
or equivalently the orientations of the experimental devices measuring the local tensor field components,
which of these two mathematically and physically very different types of “anisotropy” is involved.

Invariant correlation functions.

Just as the invariance of a tensor under orthogonal transformations is only revealed
by the full set of tensor components, one component of the correlation tensor field
may appear to be inconsistent with the assumed isotropy which becomes only
manifested for the total set of correlation functions.
Fortunately, this set is completely characterized by a few (
4
in
d=2
and
5
in
d>2
) ICFs,
cf. Eq. (
26
).
Only if a measured subset of correlation functions cannot be consistently expressed by ICFs
a legitimate claim of “anisotropy” can be made.
As we have seen, these ICFs are best described both theoretically
and numerically (cf. Fig.
3
) in reciprocal space using NRC.
The investigated system was deliberately simple since
only
one
ICF, namely the normal stress ICF
\bar{c}_{\mathrm{N}}(q)
,
remains finite for large
\Delta\tau
and, moreover, constant for sufficiently small
q
,
Eq. (
40
).
This plateau in reciprocal space explains directly (cf. Appendix
B
)
the long-ranged
1/r^{2}
-decay of the stress correlations in real space
demonstrated in Fig.
6

foo f
.
Only one phenomenological parameter
e
is needed for the fitting of our data
in the large-
\Delta\tau
limit, cf. Eq. (
40
).

Time-averaged stress fields.

We have studied in this work correlation functions
\bar{c}_{\alpha\beta\gamma\delta}(\mbox{$\bf q$},\Delta\tau)
of time-averaged stress fields

\bar{\sigma}_{\alpha\beta}(\mbox{$\bf q$},\Delta\tau)
and not the correlation functions
c_{\alpha\beta\gamma\delta}(\mbox{$\bf q$},t)
of instantaneous fields
\sigma_{\alpha\beta}(\mbox{$\bf q$},t)
.
As reminded in Sec.
V.2
,
for stationary processes
\bar{c}_{\alpha\beta\gamma\delta}(\mbox{$\bf q$},\Delta\tau)
and
c_{\alpha\beta\gamma\delta}(\mbox{$\bf q$},t)

are closely related, cf. Eq. (
49
), and both correlation functions have the same
asymptote for large (sampling) times, cf. Eq. (
50
),
i.e. the same
static
properties are probed.
From the numerical side
\bar{c}_{\alpha\beta\gamma\delta}(\mbox{$\bf q$},\Delta\tau)
basically has the advantage that irrelevant high
frequencies are filtered off (as it is usually the case for Einstein relations compared to
Green-Kubo relations
Allen and Tildesley 2017
).
Compared with the common method to determine the system’s inherent quenched stresses by
quenching to the local energy minima
Lemaître 2015
;
Lemaître 2018
this has the advantage that
we measure the relevant stresses at the given finite temperature and not at a
different

thermodynamic state. We believe that this method is therefore not only simpler (no additional quench
required) but thermodynamically and conceptionally better defined. The technical downside is that
time-series of many frames need to be stored.

Symmetry breaking.

The main physical reason for focusing on
t
-averaged stress fields
\bar{\sigma}_{\alpha\beta}(\mbox{$\bf q$},\Delta\tau)
is, however,
that the systematic time-averaging naturally allows to focus on the
quenched stresses
\sigma^{\mathrm{q}}_{\alpha\beta}(\mbox{$\bf q$})
for each independent configuration
c

by projecting out the trivial instantaneous thermal stress fluctuations
using that
\bar{\sigma}_{\alpha\beta}(\mbox{$\bf q$},\Delta\tau)\to\sigma^{\mathrm{q}}_{\alpha\beta}(\mbox{$\bf q$})
for large
\Delta\tau
.
Importantly, the parameter
e
is set by Eq. (
42
) in terms of the typical size
of the quenched normal stresses
\bar{\sigma}^{\circ\mathrm{q}}_{22}(\mbox{$\bf q$})
in reciprocal space using NRC.
Due to Eq. (
41
) these stresses may be finite without violating static mechanical
equilibrium as shown by the finite attractor in Fig.
4
.

Connection to thermodynamic response properties.

Interestingly,
e
was found to be numerically similar to the macroscopic
Young modulus
E
at thermal equilibrium in
d=2
.
As discussed in Sec.
V
, this finding was theoretically anticipated
Klochko et al. 2022

since the predictions for equilibrium viscoelastic fluids,
must hold approximatively for our glasses due to the swap MC moves used for the system preparation.
(The same identification is obtained by an independent and complementary approach to long-wavelength
stress-correlations in glass-forming liquids based on the Zwanzig-Mori projection
operator formalism
Maier et al. 2017
;
Maier et al. 2018
;
Vogel et al. 2019
.)
Altogether this implies

E=e\simeq\beta V\left<\bar{\sigma}^{\circ\mathrm{q}}_{22}(\mbox{$\bf q$})\bar{\sigma}^{\circ\mathrm{q}}_{22}(-\mbox{$\bf q$})\right>

(86)

for sufficiently small wavevectors.
The existence of a finite equilibrium Young modulus
E
(and, hence, of a finite shear modulus
\mu
)
and the broken (continuous) symmetry of the stress field in reciprocal space,
characterized by the typical size of the quenched stresses
\bar{\sigma}^{\circ\mathrm{q}}_{22}(\mbox{$\bf q$})
,
are thus intimately connected.

Outlook.

The presented work suggests several natural extensions:

•

The given tensor fields relations for isotropic systems naturally generalize to higher spatial dimensions,
to tensor fields of different order and to crosscorrelation functions of different tensorial fields
(say, between stress and strain fields) for which the major suffix symmetry Eq. (
18
) may not hold
(which merely introduces one additional ICF).

•

We have focused in the present work on Euclidean spaces and Carthesian coordinates.
It is possible to generalize our relations for systems embedded in non-Euclidean spaces,
say for glasses on spheres
Vest et al. 2015
;
Turci et al. 2017
,
and more general curvilinear coordinate systems
McConnell 2021
;
Lambourne 2010
.
Assuming the system to be
locally
isotropic the goal is always to construct from the
available correlation functions the local (tangent) isotropic invariants and to check
whether this can be done consistently with all available data sets.

•

The proposed methodology could, e.g., also be used for irreversibly crosslinked polymer
networks
Rubinstein and Colby 2003
or
active
non-equilibrium systems

Grosberg and Joanny 2015
;
Elismaili et al. 2020
which are both isotropic and highly viscous or even jammed.

e
is in such cases just a fitting parameter characterizing the typical size
of the quenched stresses.

•

The presented work is also of relevance for the correlations of strain tensor fields

Nicolas et al. 2014
;
Liu et al. 2021
;
Chacko et al. 2021

This contribution is naturally not accessible in an MC simulation.
Since we want anyway ultimately to time-average all instantaneous stresses,
Eq. (
129
) may be replaced without loss of information
by its ensemble average
\langle\hat{\sigma}^{\mathrm{id}}_{\alpha\beta}\rangle=-P_{\mathrm{id}}\delta_{\alpha\beta}

with
P_{\mathrm{id}}=Tn/V

Allen and Tildesley 2017
.
The excess contribution is given by the sum
\hat{\sigma}^{\mathrm{ex}}_{\alpha\beta}=\sum_{l}w^{l}_{\alpha\beta}/V

over all interacting pairs
l
of particles
a<b
.
Let us denote by
\mbox{$\bf r$}^{l}=\mbox{$\bf r$}^{b}-\mbox{$\bf r$}^{a}
the vector from the position
\mbox{$\bf r$}^{a}
of
a

to the position
\mbox{$\bf r$}^{b}
of
b
, by
r^{l}=|\mbox{$\bf r$}^{l}|
its length and by
\hat{r}^{l}_{\alpha}

a component of its unit vector. The contribution
w^{l}_{\alpha\beta}

of the interaction
l
to the excess stress is then
Allen and Tildesley 2017

w^{l}_{\alpha\beta}\equiv r_{l}u^{\prime}(r_{l})\hat{r}^{l}_{\alpha}\hat{r}^{l}_{\beta}

(130)

with
u^{\prime}(r)
being the first derivative of the pair potential.

Stress tensor fields in reciprocal space.

The corresponding stress tensor field

\hat{\sigma}_{\alpha\beta}(\mbox{$\bf q$})=\hat{\sigma}^{\mathrm{id}}_{\alpha\beta}(\mbox{$\bf q$})+\hat{\sigma}^{\mathrm{ex}}_{\alpha\beta}(\mbox{$\bf q$})
in reciprocal space
may be directly obtained from the local momentum equation

Landau and Lifshitz 1959
;
Hansen and McDonald 2006

\frac{\partial}{\partial t}\hat{P}_{\alpha}(\mbox{$\bf r$})=\frac{\partial}{\partial r_{\beta}}\hat{\sigma}_{\alpha\beta}(\mbox{$\bf r$})

(131)

as shown long ago
Lutsko 1988
;
Hansen and McDonald 2006
.
Note that the momentum density
\hat{P}_{\alpha}(\mbox{$\bf r$})
is defined by
Hansen and McDonald 2006

\hat{P}_{\alpha}(\mbox{$\bf r$})=\sum_{a=1}^{n}p^{a}_{\alpha}\delta(\mbox{$\bf r$}-\mbox{$\bf r$}^{a})

(132)

in terms of the individual particle momenta and positions.
Rewritten in Fourier space Eq. (
131
) becomes

\displaystyle iq_{\beta}\hat{\sigma}_{\alpha\beta}(\mbox{$\bf q$})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{\partial}{\partial t}\hat{P}_{\alpha}(\mbox{$\bf q$})

(133)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-iq_{\beta}\frac{1}{V}\sum_{a=1}^{n}\frac{p^{a}_{\alpha}p^{a}_{\beta}}{m}\exp(-i\mbox{$\bf q$}\cdot\mbox{$\bf r$}^{a})

\displaystyle+

\displaystyle\frac{1}{V}\sum_{a=1}^{n}\dot{p}^{a}_{\alpha}\exp(-i\mbox{$\bf q$}\cdot\mbox{$\bf r$}^{a}).

(134)

The first term in the previous relation gives the ideal stress contribution
Lutsko 1988
;
Hansen and McDonald 2006

\hat{\sigma}^{\mathrm{id}}_{\alpha\beta}(\mbox{$\bf q$})=-\frac{1}{V}\sum_{a=1}^{n}\frac{p^{a}_{\alpha}p^{a}_{\beta}}{m}\exp(-i\mbox{$\bf q$}\cdot\mbox{$\bf r$}^{a}).

(135)

As before for the macroscopic ideal stress we may integrate out the momenta
\{\mbox{$\bf p$}\}

and replace Eq. (
135
) by

\hat{\sigma}^{\mathrm{id}}_{\alpha\beta}(\mbox{$\bf q$})=-\frac{T}{V}\delta_{\alpha\beta}\sum_{a=1}^{n}\exp(-i\mbox{$\bf q$}\cdot\mbox{$\bf r$}^{a})

(136)

which is used for our MC simulations.
Using Newton’s second law for
\dot{p}^{a}_{\alpha}
and a total potential energy given
by pair interactions such as Eq. (
125
) the excess stress in reciprocal space is
obtained from the second term in Eq. (
134
) as shown
in Refs.
Lutsko 1988
;
Hansen and McDonald 2006
. This gives

\hat{\sigma}^{\mathrm{ex}}_{\alpha\beta}(\mbox{$\bf q$})=\frac{1}{V}\sum_{l}w^{l}_{\alpha\beta}\frac{e^{-i\mbox{$\bf q$}\cdot\mbox{$\bf r$}^{b}}-e^{-i\mbox{$\bf q$}\cdot\mbox{$\bf r$}^{a}}}{-i\mbox{$\bf q$}\cdot(\mbox{$\bf r$}^{b}-\mbox{$\bf r$}^{a})}.

(137)

Let us define
\mbox{$\bf m$}^{l}=(\mbox{$\bf r$}^{a}+\mbox{$\bf r$}^{b})/2
and
\mbox{$\bf h$}^{l}=(\mbox{$\bf r$}^{b}-\mbox{$\bf r$}^{a})/2
.
As noted by Lemaître
Lemaître 2014
the previous relation may be rewritten
more conveniently as

\hat{\sigma}^{\mathrm{ex}}_{\alpha\beta}(\mbox{$\bf q$})=\frac{1}{V}\sum_{l}w^{l}_{\alpha\beta}\frac{\sin(\mbox{$\bf q$}\cdot\mbox{$\bf h$}^{l})}{\mbox{$\bf q$}\cdot\mbox{$\bf h$}^{l}}e^{-i\mbox{$\bf q$}\cdot\mbox{$\bf m$}^{l}}.

(138)

We remark first that
\hat{\sigma}^{\mathrm{id}}_{\alpha\beta}(\mbox{$\bf q$})
and
\hat{\sigma}^{\mathrm{ex}}_{\alpha\beta}(\mbox{$\bf q$})
reduce properly for
\mbox{$\bf q$}\to{\bf 0}

to the macroscopic stress contributions noted above.
Note also that the term
\sin(x)/x\to 1
for
x=\mbox{$\bf q$}\cdot\mbox{$\bf h$}^{l}\ll 1
,
i.e. for wavelengths
2\pi/q
larger than the typical interaction range of order unity
in the present model. In this limit Eq. (
138
) further simplifies to

\hat{\sigma}^{\mathrm{ex}}_{\alpha\beta}(\mbox{$\bf q$})\approx\frac{1}{V}\sum_{l}w^{l}_{\alpha\beta}e^{-i\mbox{$\bf q$}\cdot\mbox{$\bf m$}^{l}},

(139)

i.e. only the mean position
\mbox{$\bf m$}^{l}
of two particles matters,
not their relative orientation. Since this study anyway focuses on the universal large-wavelength limit,
the approximation Eq. (
139
) should be sufficient.

Computation in reciprocal space.

Using Eq. (
136
) and Eq. (
138
) or
Eq. (
139
) one may directly compute
\hat{\sigma}_{\alpha\beta}(\mbox{$\bf q$})

for a given configuration using a discrete grid of linear length
n_{L}

as shown in Fig.
2
. Since we anyway need the stress fields in reciprocal
space to obtain the correlation functions, Eq. (
97
),
this is the most direct method and a useful exercise to test less direct definitions and approximations.
Unfortunately, this is numerically not the most efficient procedure since it evolves

n_{L}^{d}\times n\simeq V^{2}
operations. It is computationally much faster to first
obtain the stress fields in real space with a number of operations of order
n\propto V

and then to FFT transform with a number of operations of order
V\log(V)
.

Stress tensor fields in real space.

We thus need to state the corresponding relations in real space.
Inverse Fourier transformation yields for Eq. (
135
)

\hat{\sigma}^{\mathrm{id}}_{\alpha\beta}(\mbox{$\bf r$})=-\sum_{a=1}^{n}\frac{p^{a}_{\alpha}p^{a}_{\beta}}{m}\ \delta(\mbox{$\bf r$}-\mbox{$\bf r$}^{a})

(140)

and for the preaveraged ideal stress, Eq. (
136
),

\hat{\sigma}^{\mathrm{id}}_{\alpha\beta}(\mbox{$\bf r$})=-T\delta_{\alpha\beta}\sum_{a=1}^{n}\delta(\mbox{$\bf r$}-\mbox{$\bf r$}^{a}).

(141)

One confirms using Eq. (
92
) that Eq. (
137
) becomes

\displaystyle\hat{\sigma}^{\mathrm{ex}}_{\alpha\beta}(\mbox{$\bf r$})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{l}w_{\alpha\beta}^{l}I_{l}(\mbox{$\bf r$})\mbox{ with }

(142)

\displaystyle I_{l}(\mbox{$\bf r$})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{1}\mathrm{d}s\ \delta(\mbox{$\bf r$}-(\mbox{$\bf r$}^{a}+\mbox{$\bf r$}^{l}s))

being a line integral between the two particle positions
\mbox{$\bf r$}^{a}
and
\mbox{$\bf r$}^{b}
of the interaction
l
.
This relation has an old history going at least back to the work by Kirkwood and Buff

Kirkwood and Buff 1949
for the layer-resolved slabs of microcells
Allen and Tildesley 2017
.
Various rediscoveries, reformulations and generalizations (e.g., for multibody potentials
and constraint dynamics) of the inverse Fourier transform
Eq. (
142
) are discussed elsewhere

Tadmor and Miller 2011
;
Kirkwood and Buff 1949
;
Irving and Kirkwood 1950
;
Noll 1955
;
Varnik et al. 2000
;
Goldhirsch and Goldenberg 2002
;
Vanegas et al. 2014
;
Lemaître 2014
;
Behbahani et al. 2021
.
If we use instead the approximation Eq. (
139
) the line integral
is replaced by

I_{l}(\mbox{$\bf r$})\approx\delta(\mbox{$\bf r$}-\mbox{$\bf m$}^{l}).

(143)

We emphasize that this coarse-grained expression is completely sufficient for most applications.
(The
\delta
-functions in Eq. (
142
) or Eq. (
139
)
are sometimes “blurred” using more general weighting distributions
Lemaître 2014
.)
Please note that for all stated expressions the volume averages are identical to the
macroscopic relation, i.e.

\displaystyle\hat{\sigma}^{\mathrm{id}}_{\alpha\beta}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{V}\int\mathrm{d}\mbox{$\bf r$}\ \hat{\sigma}^{\mathrm{id}}_{\alpha\beta}(\mbox{$\bf r$})=\hat{\sigma}^{\mathrm{id}}_{\alpha\beta}(\mbox{$\bf q$}={\bf 0})

(144)

\displaystyle\hat{\sigma}^{\mathrm{ex}}_{\alpha\beta}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{V}\int\mathrm{d}\mbox{$\bf r$}\ \hat{\sigma}^{\mathrm{ex}}_{\alpha\beta}(\mbox{$\bf r$})=\hat{\sigma}^{\mathrm{ex}}_{\alpha\beta}(\mbox{$\bf q$}={\bf 0})

Discrete grid implementations.

The numerical calculation of the above continuous space relations on a discrete grid of
linear length
n_{L}
(cf. Fig.
2
) is obvious for the ideal contributions,
Eq. (
136
) and Eq. (
141
), and also
for the excess contribution Eq. (
138

coarse-graining approximations Eq. (
139

Eq. (

Slightly less trivial is the implementation of the line integral Eq. (
142
).
Different variants exist for distributing the information of an interaction
l

on the grid as discussed in the literature

Allen and Tildesley 2017
;
Tadmor and Miller 2011
.
In our view this issue is not crucial since the corresponding small wavelengths
have no universal physical meaning being due to an artificial computer model and
are, moreover, readily renormalized away as shown by Eq. (
139
).
An important technical point is only that any reasonable method must strictly obey
Eq. (
145
), i.e. that the contributions to all grid
points from the line integral must be properly weighted. We use a simple
numerical rendering of Eq. (
142
) on the grid:
for each of
k
equidistant points on the continuous line between
\mbox{$\bf r$}^{a}
and
\mbox{$\bf r$}^{b}

the closest grid point is incremented by
w_{\alpha\beta}^{l}/k
.
We do not care if sometimes a grid point gets several or even all contributions
(which happens if
a_{\mathrm{grid}}
is large) or none (which happens especially if the grid is too fine).
All data presented in other parts of this work have been obtained using Eq. (
140
)
and Eq. (
142
) with
k=20
and a grid spacing
a_{\mathrm{grid}}\approx 0.2
.
