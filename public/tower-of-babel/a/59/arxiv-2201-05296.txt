Dirac equation with Morse potetnial under the influence of position-dependent mass and local Fermi velocity
Bijan Bagchi, Rahul Ghosh

Abstract
We solve the one-dimensional Dirac equation by taking into account the possibility of position-dependence in the mass function. We also take the Fermi velocity to act as a local variable and examine the combined effects of the two on the solvability of the Dirac equation with respect to the Morse potential. Our results for the wave functions and the energy levels corresponding to such an extended scheme are furnished in closed forms.

Introduction

Dirac equation is a relativistic equation that is relevant to the dynamics of spin one-half particles
[
1
]
. Solving its non-relativistic version with different variants of governing potential has been actively pursued in the literature for long. In recent times, hyperbolic graphene surface under perpendicular magnetic fields
[
2
]
has received attention not only from the perspective of the confinement of nonuniform magnetic fields of massless fermions
[
3
]
but also from the point of view of the production of graphene crystals in the context of two-dimensional, single carbon
atom sheets (see, for instance,
[
4
]
). This has enhanced our understanding of the electronic properties of the charge carriers
[
5
,
6
]
. On the other hand, from a theoretical side, the techniques of supersymmetric quantum mechanics SUSYQM have been exploited by setting up first-order intertwining operators
[
7
]
. studying the influence of scalar and pseudoscalar potentials
[
8
,
9
]
and looking at the general solution against spin invariant eigenstates
[
10
]
.

In another major development of quantum mechanics, significant analysis has been carried out over the past few years on the one-dimensional position-dependent mass (PDM) problems
[
11
]
including adaptation of elaborate spatial structures
[
12
]
. In a PDM scenario, an extended Schrödinger equation operates that depends on a generalized class of potentials possessing a set of ambiguity parameters
[
13
,
14
,
15
]
. In particular, shape invariance relation in the framework of SUSYQM has been examined and a deformed version of it within PDM was set up
[
16
,
17
]
. Further, invariants were found embracing spectrum generating algebras
[
18
]
and consistency of indefinite effective mass was shown to exist
[
19
]
.

In the light of PDM the kinetic energy operator
\hat{T}
acquires the form

\displaystyle\hat{T}=-\frac{1}{4}(m^{\eta}(x)pm^{\beta}(x)pm^{\gamma}(x)+m^{\gamma}(x)pm^{\beta}(x)pm^{\eta}(x))

(1.1)

where because of the hermiticity requirement the ambiguity parameters
\eta,\beta
and
\gamma
have to satisfy the constraint

\eta+\beta+\gamma=-1

(1.2)

Other forms of
\hat{T}
inevitably show mutual equivalence
[
20
]
.

Corresponding to (1.1) the time-independent Schrödinger equation can be cast as

\displaystyle H\phi(x)=\Big[-\frac{1}{2}\frac{d}{dx}\frac{1}{m(x)}\frac{d}{dx}+V_{eff}(x)\Big]\phi(x)=\epsilon\phi(x)\qquad\text{(assuming $\hbar=1$)}

(1.3)

where the effective potential
V_{eff}(x)
depends on
m(x)
and its spatial derivatives denoted by primes

\displaystyle V_{eff}(x)=\mathcal{V}(x)+\frac{1}{4}(\beta+1)\frac{m^{\prime\prime}(x)}{m(x)^{2}}-\frac{1}{2}\left(\eta(\eta+\beta+1)+\beta+1\right)\frac{m^{\prime 2}(x)}{m^{3}(x)}

(1.4)

Among various types of
\hat{T}
we focus on the Ben Daniel-Duke model involving as the choice of the ambiguity parameters,
\alpha=\gamma=0,\beta=-1

[
21
]
. An advantage of it is that in such a scheme the effective potential
V_{eff}(x)
is the same as the system potential
\mathcal{V}(x)
.

Apart from the PDM, one can consider treating the Fermi velocity as a local variable (PDFV). Such a possibility was first proposed in
[
22
]
to ascertain how localization effects affect graphene-like Dirac materials. Indeed, nonuniform strain in graphene suggests position-dependence could be present in the Fermi velocity. This was also revealed from scanning tunneling spectroscopy experiments
[
23
,
24
,
25
]
. The idea of PDFV got further impetus when gap-formation was noticed in graphene
[
26
]
. Subsequently, several interesting theoretical works have appeared where the role of PDFV was looked into
[
27
,
28
,
29
]
including the study on the electronic transport in two-dimensional strained Dirac materials
[
30
]
.

The intention of the present work is to give a comprehensive solution of the Dirac equation in the presence of the Morse potential by incorporating position-dependence of the mass as well taking into consideration the local behaviour of the Fermi velocity. As is well known Morse potential occupies an important place in quantum mechanics in that is of much use in spectroscopic applications
[
31
]
. Moreover, it could be connected with the Coulomb potential under a coordinate transformation
[
32
,
33
,
34
]
.

2
Aspects of SUSYQM

The basic formalism
[
35
,
36
,
37
,
38
,
39
]
of SUSYQM involves the supercharges
Q,Q^{\dagger}
that depict fermionic-like properties. The governing Hamiltonian can be expressed in the manner

\mathcal{H}=\{Q,Q^{\dagger}\}

(2.1)

The basic properties of
Q,Q^{\dagger}
are

\displaystyle(Q)^{2}=0=(Q^{\dagger})^{2}

(2.2)

\displaystyle[Q,\mathcal{H}]=0=[Q^{\dagger},\mathcal{H}]

(2.3)

To represent them, we introduce the corresponding operators
O
and
O^{\dagger}
and write

Q=\mathcal{O}\otimes\sigma_{-},\quad Q^{\dagger}=\quad\mathcal{O}^{\dagger}\otimes\sigma_{+}

(2.4)

where the quantities
\sigma_{\pm}
denote
\sigma_{\pm}=\frac{1}{2}(\sigma_{1}\pm i\sigma_{2})
,
\sigma_{1}
and
\sigma_{2}
are the usual Pauli matrices. Taking a first-order differential realization of
\mathcal{O},\mathcal{O}^{\dagger}

\mathcal{O}=\frac{\hbar}{\sqrt{2m}}\partial+\mathcal{W}(x),\quad\mathcal{O}^{\dagger}=-\frac{\hbar}{\sqrt{2m}}\partial+\mathcal{W}(x)

(2.5)

where
m
is the mass, and
\mathcal{W}(x)
is the superpotential of the system, we can project
Q
and
Q^{\dagger}
in the matrix forms

Q=\left(\begin{array}[]{cc}0&0\\
\frac{\hbar}{\sqrt{2m}}\partial+\mathcal{W}(x)&0\end{array}\right),\quad Q^{\dagger}=\left(\begin{array}[]{cc}0&-\frac{\hbar}{\sqrt{2m}}\partial+\mathcal{W}(x)\\
0&0\end{array}\right)

(2.6)

where
\partial\equiv\frac{d}{dx}
. The Hamiltonian
\mathcal{H}
is thus rendered diagonal

\mathcal{H}=\left(\begin{array}[]{cc}\mathcal{H}_{+}&0\\
0&\mathcal{H}_{-}\end{array}\right)

(2.7)

with the components
H_{\pm}
given by the factorization
[
40
]

\displaystyle\mathcal{H}_{+}=\mathcal{O}^{\dagger}\mathcal{O}=-\frac{\hbar^{2}}{2m}\partial^{2}+U^{(+)}(x)-\Lambda

(2.8)

\displaystyle\mathcal{H}_{-}=\mathcal{O}\mathcal{O}^{\dagger}=-\frac{\hbar^{2}}{2m}\partial^{2}+U^{(-)}(x)-\Lambda

(2.9)

These are in typical Schr̈odinger form defined at some cut-off energy value
\Lambda
.

The SUSY partner potentials
U^{(+)}
and
U^{(-)}
can be projected as

{}U_{\pm}(x)=\mathcal{W}^{2}(x)\mp\frac{\hbar}{\sqrt{2m}}\mathcal{W}^{\prime}(x)+\Lambda

(2.10)

where the prime represents a derivative with respect to
x
. For unbroken
SUSY
the ground state wavefunction
\psi_{0}^{+}(x)
is non-degenerate which we can associate with the component
\mathcal{H}_{+}
. This has the implication

\displaystyle\mathcal{O}\psi_{0}^{+}(x)=0

(2.11)

\displaystyle\implies\psi_{0}^{+}(x)\propto\exp\left(-\frac{\sqrt{2m}}{\hbar}\int^{x}\mathcal{W}(t)dt\right)

(2.12)

reflecting that the normalizability of
\psi_{0}^{+}(x)
restricts the superpotential to obey
\int^{x}W(t)dt>0
as
x\rightarrow\infty
.

The double-degeneracy of the spectrum is guided by the following
intertwining relationships of
\mathcal{H}

\mathcal{O}\mathcal{H}_{+}=\mathcal{H}_{-}\mathcal{O},\quad\mathcal{H}_{+}\mathcal{O}^{\dagger}=\mathcal{O}^{\dagger}\mathcal{H}_{-}

(2.13)

and furnishes the isospectral connections between
\mathcal{H}^{(+)}
and
\mathcal{H}^{(-)}
. These are of course consistent with the projections
(2.8)
and
(2.9)
.

3
PDM and PDFV

In the standard constant mass
m_{0}
and constant Fermi velocity
v_{F}
case the one-dimensional Dirac Hamiltonian is given by
[
8
]
(see also
[
41
]
)

H_{D}=v_{f}\sigma_{x}p_{x}+\sigma_{y}W(x)+\sigma_{z}m_{0}v_{f}^{2}+\mathds{1}V(x)

(3.1)

where
V(x)
is the electrostatic potential,
W(x)
is the pseudoscalar potential and
\mathds{1}
is the block-diagonal unit matrix. In
[
9
]
,
W(x)
was interpreted to behave like the superpotential of the system. The Pauli matrices are known to be

\displaystyle\sigma_{x}=\left(\begin{array}[]{cc}0&1\\
1&0\end{array}\right),\quad\sigma_{y}=\left(\begin{array}[]{cc}0&-i\\
i&0\end{array}\right),\quad\sigma_{z}=\left(\begin{array}[]{cc}1&0\\
0&-1\end{array}\right)

The material properties of the Dirac particle are carried by the combined effects of the Fermi velocity and the mass function that produce a heterostructure
[
42
]
. The possibility of the Fermi velocity varying from material to material is quite comprehensible in condensed matter physics
[
43
]
. This motivates us to look into a situation when both the mass function and Fermi velocity become local function of position. In setting up of our scheme we ignore the effects of the electrostatic potential
V
as is suggested by the analysis of the intertwining relations
[
8
,
44
]
.

We therefore consider PDM as well as PDFV to rewrite (
3.1
) as follows

\displaystyle H_{D}=\sqrt{v_{f}(x)}\sigma_{x}p_{x}\sqrt{v_{f}(x)}+\sigma_{y}W(x)+\sigma_{z}m(x)v_{f}^{2}(x)

(3.8)

In the two-dimensional matrix form
H_{D}
becomes

\displaystyle H_{D}=\left(\begin{array}[]{cc}mv_{f}^{2}&-i\hbar\sqrt{v_{f}}\partial\sqrt{v_{f}}-iW\\
-i\hbar\sqrt{v_{f}}\partial\sqrt{v_{f}}+iW&-mv_{f}^{2}\end{array}\right)

where note that
m=m(x)
and
v_{f}=v_{f}(x)
. Further,
V
and
W
are arbitrary function of
x
.

Operating on a spinor with components
(\psi_{+}\quad\psi_{-})^{T}
emerges the coupled pair of equations

\displaystyle(-i\hbar\sqrt{v_{f}}\partial\sqrt{v_{f}}-iW)\psi_{-}=D_{-}\psi_{+}

(3.11)

\displaystyle(-i\hbar\sqrt{v_{f}}\partial\sqrt{v_{f}}+iW)\psi_{+}=D_{+}\psi_{-}

(3.12)

where
E
is the energy eigenvalue and we have chosen to work with natural units
\hbar=1
. In the above the quantities
D_{\pm}
stand for
D_{\pm}=E\pm mv_{f}^{2}
.
Specifically the equation for the upper component reads

\displaystyle-\frac{v_{f}^{2}}{D_{+}}\frac{d^{2}\psi_{+}}{dx^{2}}-\frac{d}{dx}\Big(\frac{v_{f}^{2}}{D_{+}}\Big)\frac{d\psi_{+}}{dx}+\Big[\frac{1}{D_{+}}\Big(W^{2}-\frac{1}{4}{v^{\prime}_{f}}^{2}-\frac{1}{2}v_{f}v^{\prime\prime}_{f}\Big)+v_{f}\frac{d}{dx}\Big(\frac{W}{D_{+}}\Big)

\displaystyle-\frac{1}{2}v_{f}v^{\prime}_{f}\frac{d}{dx}\Big(\frac{1}{D_{+}}\Big)\Big]\psi_{+}=D_{-}\psi_{+}

(3.13)

while for the lower component we have

\displaystyle-\frac{v_{f}^{2}}{D_{-}}\frac{d^{2}\psi_{-}}{dx^{2}}-\frac{d}{dx}\Big(\frac{v_{f}^{2}}{D_{-}}\Big)\frac{d\psi_{-}}{dx}+\Big[\frac{1}{D_{-}}\Big(W^{2}-\frac{1}{4}{v^{\prime}_{f}}^{2}-\frac{1}{2}v_{f}v^{\prime\prime}_{f}\Big)-v_{f}\frac{d}{dx}\Big(\frac{W}{D_{-}}\Big)

\displaystyle-\frac{1}{2}v_{f}v^{\prime}_{f}\frac{d}{dx}\Big(\frac{1}{D_{-}}\Big)\Big]\psi_{-}=D_{+}\psi_{-}

(3.14)

To proceed further we employ the constancy condition
[
27
]

m(x)v_{f}^{2}(x)=\mbox{constant}=m_{0}v_{0}^{2}

(3.15)

where
m_{0}
and
v_{0}
are both constants. This helps greatly in the reduction of (3.7) and (3.8). In particular both
D_{\pm}
are rendered constant. As a result the coupled equations (3.7) and (3.8) are disentangled leaving us with two separate equations, one for
\psi_{+}
and another for
\psi_{-}

\displaystyle-v_{f}^{2}\frac{d^{2}\psi_{+}}{dx^{2}}-\frac{d}{dx}(v_{f}^{2})\frac{d\psi_{+}}{dx}+\Big[\Big(W^{2}-\frac{1}{4}{v^{\prime}_{f}}^{2}-\frac{1}{2}v_{f}v^{\prime\prime}_{f}\Big)+v_{f}W^{\prime})\Big]\psi_{+}=(E^{2}-m_{0}^{2}v_{0}^{4})\psi_{+}

(3.16)

\displaystyle-v_{f}^{2}\frac{d^{2}\psi_{-}}{dx^{2}}-\frac{d}{dx}(v_{f}^{2})\frac{d\psi_{-}}{dx}+\Big[\Big(W^{2}-\frac{1}{4}{v^{\prime}_{f}}^{2}-\frac{1}{2}v_{f}v^{\prime\prime}_{f}\Big)-v_{f}W^{\prime})\Big]\psi_{-}=(E^{2}-m_{0}^{2}v_{0}^{4})\psi_{-}

(3.17)

Making a change of variable through defining

\displaystyle\psi_{\pm}(x)=\frac{1}{\sqrt{v_{f}(x)}}\Phi_{\pm}(y(x))

(3.18)

where the new quantity
y(x)
stands for the integral

y(x)=\int^{x}\frac{dz}{v_{f}(z)}+\mbox{constant}

(3.19)

results in the set of differential equations

\displaystyle-\frac{d^{2}\Phi_{\pm}(y)}{dy^{2}}+\Big[W^{2}(x(y))\pm v_{f}(x(y))W^{\prime}(x(y))\Big]\Phi_{\pm}(y)=(E^{2}-m_{0}^{2}v_{0}^{4})\Phi_{\pm}(y)

(3.20)

We thus have the SUSY form for the partner potentials as defined below albeit in terms of the variable
y

\displaystyle V_{\pm}(x(y))=W^{2}(x(y))\mp v_{f}(x(y))W^{\prime}(x(y))

(3.21)

These match with
U_{\pm}
in
(2.10)
for
\Lambda=0
.

An interesting off-soot is that if we compare the basic PDM guided Schödinger equation (1.3) with either (3.10) or (3.11) the mass function
m(x)
coincides with the inverse square of the Fermi velocity
[
29
]

\displaystyle m(x)=\frac{1}{2v_{f}^{2}(x)}

(3.22)

This result has a significance in the context of deformed shape-invariance within SUSYQM. In fact, if we look at the equation for the positive-definite deforming function
f
introduced in
[
16
]
namely

H\phi(x)=\left[-\left(\sqrt{f(x)}\frac{d}{dx}\sqrt{f(x)}\right)^{2}+V_{eff}(x)\right]\phi(x)=E\phi(x)

(3.23)

(which follows as a consequence of (1.3) by implementing a deformed shape-invariance relation), then such a function
f
is easily recognized to play the role of PDFV.

With this background we now turn to the Morse potential.

4
Morse potential

We assume for
W(x)
the following choice

\displaystyle W(x)=\begin{cases}\omega_{0}-\omega_{1}x\quad\text{$x>0$}\\
0\quad\text{$x\leq 0$}\end{cases}

(4.1)

where
\omega_{0}
and
\omega_{1}
are positive constants, and take for the PDFV the representation

\displaystyle v_{f}(x)=\begin{cases}\alpha x\quad\text{$x>0$}\\
0\quad\text{$x\leq 0$}\end{cases}

(4.2)

where
\alpha
is a positive constant. From (3.10) the mass function
(\ref{mass})
turns out to be

\displaystyle m(x)=\frac{1}{2\alpha^{2}x^{2}}\quad\text{$x>0$}

(4.3)

As a result, from
(\ref{extendedV+-})
, the extended SUSY partner potentials read

\displaystyle V_{\pm}(x)=\begin{cases}\omega_{0}^{2}+\omega^{2}_{1}x^{2}-2\omega_{1}(\omega_{0}\pm\frac{\alpha}{2})x\quad\text{ for $x>0$}\\
0\quad\text{ for $x\leq 0$}\end{cases}

(4.4)

which correspond to shifted harmonic oscillator potentials.

If we now make a change of variable to
t=\frac{1}{\alpha}\ln x\in(-\infty,\infty)
, the partner potentials get transformed to the forms

\displaystyle V_{\pm}(t)=\omega_{0}^{2}+\omega^{2}_{1}e^{2\alpha t}-2\omega_{1}(\omega_{0}\pm\frac{\alpha}{2})e^{\alpha t},\quad t\in(-\infty,\infty)

(4.5)

Clearly, the two cases in (4.5) correspond to the potentials of the one-dimensional Morse potential.

The accompanying Schrödinger equation in the
t
-variable for
\Phi_{+}(t)
reads

\displaystyle\frac{d^{2}\Phi_{+}(t)}{dt^{2}}+\left[k^{2}-\omega_{0}^{2}+\omega^{2}_{1}e^{2\alpha t}-2\omega_{1}(\omega_{0}-\frac{\alpha}{2})e^{\alpha t}\right]\Phi_{+}(t)=0

(4.6)

where
k^{2}=E^{2}-\frac{1}{4}
. Its solution in terms of associated Laguerre polynomials
L_{n}^{\kappa}(\xi)
is
[
48
,
37
]

\displaystyle\Phi_{+}(\xi)\propto\xi^{\frac{\kappa}{2}}e^{-\frac{\xi}{2}}L_{n}^{\kappa}\left(\xi\right)

(4.7)

where
\kappa=\frac{2\omega_{0}}{\alpha}-2n\in\Re
and
\xi=\frac{2\omega_{1}}{\alpha}e^{\alpha t}

\in(0,\infty)
, along with the eigenvalues

E_{n}^{2}=\frac{1}{4}+\omega_{0}^{2}-(\omega_{0}-\alpha n)^{2}

(4.8)

The allowed values for
n
are
n=0,1,...,n_{max}(n_{max}<\frac{\omega_{0}}{\alpha})
.

Reverting to
x
-coordinate,
(4.7)
moves over to the representation

\displaystyle\psi_{+}(x)=Ne^{-\frac{\omega_{1}x}{\alpha}}x^{\frac{\kappa-1}{2}}L_{n}^{\kappa}\left(\frac{2\omega_{1}x}{\alpha}\right)

(4.9)

where
N
is the normalization constant. As a check, note that if we take the Fermi velocity to be constant scaled to unity,
\psi_{+}(x)
will be same as furnished in
[
49
]
. For completeness we also provide the solution for
\psi_{-}(x)

\displaystyle\psi_{-}(x)=\frac{Nie^{-\frac{\omega_{1}x}{\alpha}}x^{\frac{\kappa-2}{2}}}{2\sqrt{\alpha}\mathcal{D}}

\displaystyle\Bigg[4\omega_{1}xL_{n-1}^{\kappa+1}\left(\frac{2\omega_{1}x}{\alpha}\right)

\displaystyle+(\alpha+2n\alpha+4\omega_{1}x)L^{\kappa}_{n}\left(\frac{2\omega_{1}x}{\alpha}\right)\Bigg]

(4.10)

where the quantity
\mathcal{D}
is given by
\mathcal{D}=\sqrt{\frac{1}{4}+\omega_{0}^{2}-(\omega_{0}-\alpha n)^{2}}+\frac{1}{4}
.
(4.9) and (4.10) give the complete closed-form solutions of the Morse partner wavefunctions in the presence of PDM and PDFV along with energy levels (4.8).

5
Summary

To summarize, we have shown in this paper that the Dirac equation in the presence of the PDM and PDFV admits of an exact solution for the Morse potential. Employing the factorization method and using von Roos prescription of the modified kinetic energy term for the PDM, and further observing that the underlying pseudoscalar potential acts as the superpotential of the system in the setup of SUSYQM, we solved a pair of coupled equations containing the components of the Dirac spinor. In this regard we utilized the result that the mass function behaves like the inverse square of the Fermi velocity. Through disentanglement we derived the complete solution of the Morse-like partner potentials.
