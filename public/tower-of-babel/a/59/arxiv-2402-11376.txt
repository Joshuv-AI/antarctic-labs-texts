Cartan geometry, supergravity and group manifold approach
J. François, L. Ravera

Abstract
We make a case for the unique relevance of Cartan geometry for gauge theories of gravity and supergravity. We introduce our discussion by recapitulating historical threads, providing motivations. In a first part we review the geometry of classical gauge theory, as a background for understanding gauge theories of gravity in terms of Cartan geometry. The second part introduces the basics of the group manifold approach to supergravity, hinting at the deep rooted connections to Cartan supergeometry. The contribution is intended, not as an extensive review, but as a conceptual overview, and hopefully a bridge between communities in physics and mathematics.

Introduction

It is by now well known that the differential geometry of connections on fiber bundles is the mathematical underpinning of classical gauge field theory, which is itself the framework nesting the best current theories of fundamental physics accounting for the structure and interactions of the elementary constituents of the universe.

One may draw an analogy from physics, with the development of quantum mechanics (QM): Its first incarnation as “matrix mechanics” devised in 1924-25 by Heisenberg, Born and Jordan used mathematics unfamiliar to physicists (matrices). The second version devised by Schrödinger in 1925-26 using standard differential equation was welcomed as more familiar and intuitive. Dirac is credited for first convincingly argue that both approaches were equivalent, but it took Von Neumann to give the broad encompassing (and now textbook) formulation in terms of operators on (possibly infinite dimensional) Hilbert spaces.
Later developments in terms of
C^{*}
-algebras are considered more advanced formulation of QM, and the basis of refined mathematical formulation of quantum field theory (QFT); one may wonder what would be the analogue in the development of (higher) supergeometry.

In the same way that Cartan geometry is the foundation of classical gauge theories of gravity, one expects that Cartan supergeometry is the right mathematical framework for supergravity theories.

2.1.2
Dynamics: Lagrangian and action functionals

The dynamics of a classical field theory is given by field equations, derived from a Lagrangian functional
L
via the variational principle applied to the corresponding action
S=\int L
.

The field space of a gauge field theory is
\Phi=\mathcal{A}\times\Gamma(E)
, with
\mathcal{A}
the space of (loc. rep. of) connections, so that a point is a collection of elementary fields
\upphi=\{A,\phi\}
or
\upphi=\{\omega,\varphi\}
. The Lagrangian is then

\displaystyle L:\Phi\ \ (\text{or }J^{1}(\Phi))

\displaystyle\rightarrow\Omega^{m}(M)\ \ (\text{or }\Omega^{m}(P)),

(1)

\displaystyle\upphi

\displaystyle\mapsto L(\upphi),

where
J^{1}(\Phi)
is the first jet bundle of
\phi

m=\text{dim}M

For most physical purpose, it is built as a polynomial in the elementary field variables
\phi

– and their Hodge duals, the Hodge operator
\,*:\Omega^{p}(M)\rightarrow\Omega^{p-m}(M)
being a natural tool to form top forms on
M

The action is thus

\displaystyle S:\Phi\ \ (\text{or }J^{1}(\Phi))

\displaystyle\rightarrow\mathbb{R},

(2)

\displaystyle\upphi

\displaystyle\mapsto S(\upphi)=\int_{D}L(\upphi),

with
D\subset M
a domain of spacetime. Physically realised field configurations are those that make the action stationary
\delta S=0
.
The variation of the Lagrangian gives

\displaystyle\delta L=\boldsymbol{E}+d\boldsymbol{\theta}\vcentcolon=E(\delta\upphi;\upphi)+d\theta(\delta\upphi;\upphi),

(3)

where
\boldsymbol{E}=E(\delta\upphi;\upphi)
is the field equation term. The boundary term
\boldsymbol{\theta}=\theta(\delta\upphi;\upphi)
– sometimes called the presymplectic potential of the theory – has vanishing contribution if
\partial D=\emptyset
, or given adequate boundary conditions (b.c.) on

5
5

5

Neumann b.c. are
\upphi_{|\partial D}=0
, Dirichlet b.c. are
\delta\upphi_{|\partial D}=0
.
Either conditions are required

\delta S=0\ \ \Rightarrow\ \ \boldsymbol{E}=0
, for all
\delta\upphi
.

The choice of Lagrangian is usually constrained by

:

is required to be invariant (up to boundary terms) under some continuous transformation groups. This ensures that the field equations derived from it are

The two principles at the heart of relativistic gauge field theory are the General Covariant Principle (GPC), or General Relativity Principle, and the Gauge Principle (GP).

The GCP requires that physical laws (equations) are invariant or covariant under arbitrary changes of coordinates. It can be satisfied easily by working with tensors and differential forms.

The GP requires that

has trivial gluings on

and 2)

—

on

: the Killing form, or the trace,

;

—

on

We remark that the non-degeneracy condition is essential if

It specifies the dynamics of the Yang-Mills potential

We remark that Lagrangians that do not involve the Hodge operator (or the soldering form), thus do not rely on a metric structure on

(8)

We observe that it is not gauge-invariant; yet, as we will see, the field equation remains gauge-covariant: from

2.1.3
Noether charges and gauge symmetries

Noether theorems express that variational symmetries of the Lagrangian and action functionals give physical quantities, known as charges, conserved on-shell, i.e. when the field equations hold. Since
L
is gauge-invariant, we expect charges to abound in gauge-field theory.

Denote
\chi\in
Lie
\mathcal{H}
any element of the Lie algebra of the gauge group and
\delta_{\chi}
the corresponding infinitesimal gauge transformation operator.
8
8

8

If we are on the bundle
P
, it is
L_{\chi^{v}}
, the Lie derivative along the vertical vector field
\chi^{v}\in\Gamma(VP)
generated by
\chi
.

As a special case of the variational principle one gets

\displaystyle\delta_{\chi}L=E(\delta_{\chi}\upphi;\upphi)+d\theta(\delta_{\chi}\upphi;\upphi).

(9)

According to the GP,
\delta_{\chi}L=0
, so the quantity
J(\chi;\upphi)\vcentcolon=\theta(\delta_{\chi}\upphi;\upphi)
is
d
-closed on-shell, which we denote

dJ(\chi;\upphi)\ \hat{=}\ 0
.
It is the Noether
(m-1)
-form current.
It is thus on-shell a boundary term:
J(\chi;\upphi)\ \hat{=}\ dq(\chi;\upphi)
.
The corresponding Noether charge is its integral over a codimension 1 submanifold
\Sigma\subset D
,

\displaystyle Q_{\Sigma}(\chi;\upphi)\vcentcolon=\int_{\Sigma}J(\chi;\upphi)=\int_{\Sigma}\theta(\delta_{\chi}\upphi;\upphi)\ \hat{=}\ \int_{\partial\Sigma}q(\chi;\upphi).

(10)

By the last equality, the Noether charge is a boundary term. For this reason it is often called a “quasi-local ” charge, as it is a quantity associated to a region, not a single point, of spacetime.

Let us consider the notable examples of YM theory. Variation of its Lagrangian gives

\displaystyle\delta L_{\text{YM}}

\displaystyle=E(\delta A;A)+d\theta(\delta A;A)

(11)

\displaystyle=\Tr(\delta A\ D\!*\!F)+d\Tr(\delta A*\!F).

From this one reads the (vacuum) YM equation
\boldsymbol{E}=0\ \Rightarrow\ D*\!F=0
. The Abelian case is the Maxwell equation
d*\!F=0
.
Given the infinitesimal gauge transformation of the YM potential,
\delta_{\chi}A=D\chi=d\chi+[A,\chi]
, the Noether charge is

\displaystyle Q^{\text{YM}}_{\Sigma}(\chi;A)=\int_{\Sigma}\theta(\delta_{\chi}A;A)=\int_{\Sigma}\Tr(D\chi*\!F)\ \,\hat{=}\int_{\partial\Sigma}\Tr(\chi*\!F).

(12)

For constant
\chi
, i.e. for elements of the Lie algebra of the structure group of the underlying bundle
P
, this gives a non-Abelian Gauss law
[
Abbott-Deser1982
;
Francois-et-al2021
]
. Indeed, in the Abelian case,
Q^{\text{YM}}_{\Sigma}(\chi;A)=\chi\int_{\partial\Sigma}(*F)
, the integral is the Gauss law: the electric flux through a bounded region which gives the enclosed electric charge.

2.2
Classical gauge theory of gravity and Cartan geometry

In this section we review elementary notions of standard Cartan geometry and show how they apply naturally to the gauge theoretic description of gravitational physics. For in depth treatments, one should consult the reference texts
[
Cap-Slovak09
;
Sharpe
;
Kobayashi1972
]
. See also
[
Kobayashi1957
]
which is of historical interest.

2.2.1
Cartan geometry in a nutshell

A Cartan geometry
(P,\varpi)
is a

H
-principal bundle
P
endowed with a Cartan connection
\varpi\in\Omega^{1}(P,\mathfrak{g})
, with
\mathfrak{g}\supset\mathfrak{h}
, s.t.

i

R^{*}_{h}\varpi_{|ph}=\text{Ad}_{h^{-1}}\varpi_{|p}
,

ii

\varpi_{|p}(X^{v}_{p})=X\in

\mathfrak{h}
, where
X^{v}_{p}\in V_{p}\mathcal{P}
,

iii

\varpi_{|p}:T_{p}P\rightarrow

\mathfrak{g}\
is a linear isomorphism
\forall p\in\mathcal{P}
.

Properties i-ii are the same as for an Ehresmann connection. The last property iii is key and has several consequences special to Cartan geometry. The essential idea, from a physical standpoint, is that it implies
P
encodes the geometry of
M
. Since the Einsteinian insight is that gravity is the geometry of spacetime, Cartan geometry
(P,\varpi)
is perfectly adapted to describe the kinematics of gauge theories of gravitation, the Cartan connection being a generalised gravitational gauge potential. Let us see how.

Gauge symmetries
.
As gauge approaches to gravity were often modeled on the habits of YM theory, whenever one saw a
\mathfrak{g}
-valued 1-form, understood as a gauge potential, it was assumed that the associated theory should have a gauge symmetry
\mathcal{G}
– or gauge algebra
\mathfrak{g}
– corresponding to the Lie algebra
\mathfrak{g}
.
This led often to technical complications and conceptual issues to be dealt with. For example, in the case
\mathfrak{g}=\mathfrak{h}\ltimes\mathbb{R}^{4}
typical of affine gravity (
\mathfrak{h}=\mathfrak{gl}(4)
) or Poincaré gravity (
\mathfrak{h}=\mathfrak{so}(1,3)
), the thorny issue of “internal gauge translations” and their redundancy with diffeomorphisms
\Diff(M)
needed resolution. Mostly the strategy has been to try to identify “gauge translations” with
\mathfrak{diff}(M)
(via the tetrad field/soldering form)
[
Blagojevi-et-al2013
]
.

No such problem arises once one knows Cartan geometry. The underlying bundle being a
H
-bundle
P
, the gauge group is
\mathcal{H}
, i.e. the gauge transformations result from the action of its vertical automorphisms group
\Aut_{v}(P)
. All the relevant symmetries of gravity, including
\Diff(M)
, are then naturally organised in the short exact sequence (SES) of groups associated to the Cartan bundle,

\displaystyle\mathcal{H}\simeq\Aut_{v}(P)\rightarrow\Aut(P)\rightarrow\Diff(M).

(13)

The corresponding SES of Lie algebras is

\displaystyle Lie\mathcal{H}\simeq\mathfrak{aut}_{v}(P)\rightarrow\mathfrak{aut}(P)\rightarrow\mathfrak{diff}(M),

(14)

where
Lie\mathcal{H}
is the ‘gauge algebra’ and
\mathfrak{aut}(P)
is isomorphic to
\Gamma_{\text{inv}}(TP)
, the right-invariant vector fields of
P
. The sequence (
14
) describes the Atiyah Lie algebroid of
P
, and encodes the infinitesimal symmetries or gravity
[
Attard-et-al2019
]
.

Soldering
.
Given its defining properties, the Cartan connection
\varpi
induces a soldering on
M
, i.e. the bundle isomorphism
TM\simeq P\times\mathfrak{g}/\mathfrak{h}
.
9
9

9

Unless
\mathfrak{h}
is an ideal of
\mathfrak{g}
,
\mathfrak{g}/\mathfrak{h}
is a vector space, not a subalgebra. In any case,
H
acts on it via the
Ad
representation, which may reduce to simple left multiplication in some cases. As for
\mathfrak{g}
, it acts via the
ad
representation.

So
\Gamma(TM)\simeq\Omega^{0}_{\text{eq}}(P,\mathfrak{g}/\mathfrak{h})
.
Conceptually, it shows how
P
encodes the geometry of
M
: its tangent bundle being naturally an associated bundle of
P
.

Further consequences unfold, that are relevant for a field theoretic standpoint.
Consider the projection
\tau:\mathfrak{g}\rightarrow\mathfrak{g}/\mathfrak{h}
. One defines the soldering form
\theta\vcentcolon=\tau\circ\varpi\,\in\Omega^{1}_{\text{tens}}(P,\mathfrak{g}/\mathfrak{h})
. It is tensorial indeed; equivariant by property i and horizontal by ii.
Locally, on an open set
U\subset M
, given a trivialising section
\sigma:U\rightarrow P_{|U}
, the local representative of the Cartan connection is
\bar{A}\vcentcolon=\sigma^{*}\varpi\,\in\Omega^{1}(U,\mathfrak{g})
, while the local soldering form is

e\vcentcolon=\sigma^{*}\theta\,\in\Omega^{1}(U,\mathfrak{g}/\mathfrak{h})
.
Given a coordinate system
\{x^{\mu}\}
on
U
and
\{a\}
abstract index for
\mathfrak{g}/\mathfrak{h}
, we may write
e=e^{a}={e^{a}}_{\mu}\,dx^{\,\mu}
. The components
{e^{a}}_{\mu}
of the local soldering are called
tetrad field
, or
vielbein
, in physics.

If there is a non-degenerate bilinear form
\eta:\mathfrak{g}/\mathfrak{h}\times\mathfrak{g}/\mathfrak{h}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
, then the Cartan connection induces a metric via

\displaystyle g\vcentcolon=\eta\circ e:\Gamma(TM)\times\Gamma(TM)

\displaystyle\rightarrow C^{\infty}(M),

(15)

\displaystyle(X,Y)

\displaystyle\mapsto g(X,Y)=\eta\left(e(X),e(Y)\right).

In coordinates this reads

g_{\mu\nu}=\eta_{ab}{e^{a}}_{\mu}{e^{b}}_{\nu}
.
Under another local section
\sigma^{\prime}=R_{g}\sigma
, with
g:U\rightarrow H
a transition function of
P
,
e^{\prime}=\sigma^{\prime*}\theta=R^{*}_{g}e=\text{Ad}_{g^{-1}}e
.
If
\eta
is
H
-invariant,
\eta\circ\text{Ad}(H)=\eta
, then
g^{\prime}=\eta\circ e^{\prime}=\eta\circ e=g
: the induced metric is well-defined on
M
. It thus allows to define a Hodge operator
*:\Omega^{p}(M)\rightarrow\Omega^{p-m}(M)
, an essential ingredient to built Lagrangians of
(non-topological) gauge field theories (even non-gravitational), as we have seen.

One may observe that, in the case
(\mathfrak{g}/\mathfrak{h},\eta)=(\mathbb{R}^{4},\eta)=\sf M
the Minkowski space, the existence of the local soldering
e\in\Omega^{1}(U,\mathbb{R}^{4})
, and the expression (
15
), are mathematical implementation of one formulation of the
equivalence principle
: Spacetime is locally Minkowskian, and it is always possible to find a coordinate system in which the metric appears Minkowskian, i.e. spacetime appears locally flat.

In standard metric-formulation of GR, the metric is often understood as the gravitational potential (as the Newtonian potential in the weak field limit). We also observe that the
\mathfrak{h}
-part
\omega
of
\varpi
is Ehresmann by properties i-ii, i.e. an exact analogue of Yang-Mills potential in YM gauge field theory. So, the Cartan connection
\varpi
(or rather its local representative
\bar{A}
) is a generalised
gravitational gauge potential
.

Cartan curvature
.
The curvature 2-form of a Cartan connection is
defined
via the Cartan structure formula:

\bar{\Omega}=d\varpi+\nicefrac{{1}}{{2}}[\varpi,\varpi]\,\in\Omega^{2}(P,\mathfrak{g})
. It thus satisfies the Bianchi identity
d\bar{\Omega}+[\varpi,\bar{\Omega}]\equiv 0
, a strictly algebraic identity.
The torsion of a Cartan connection is

\Theta=\tau\circ\bar{\Omega}\,\in\Omega^{2}(P,\mathfrak{g}/\mathfrak{h})
. This is typical of what Cartan called the “espaces généralisés”. This is a notion of course absent for Ehresmann connections, thus in YM theory.
Locally, on
U\subset M
, we have the local representatives
\bar{F}=d\bar{A}+\nicefrac{{1}}{{2}}[\bar{A},\bar{A}]\,\in\Omega^{2}(U,\mathfrak{g})
and

T\in\Omega^{2}(U,\mathfrak{g}/\mathfrak{h})
.

In direct anology to gauge field theory, where the YM field strength is the curvature of an Ehresmann connection (Table
1
), the Cartan curvature (its local representative) is the
gravitational field strength
.

Associated (tractor) bundles and covariant (tractor) derivative
.
As usual, given representations
(\rho,V)
of
H
, one have associated bundles

E=P\times_{\rho}V
and
\Gamma(E)\simeq\Omega^{0}_{\text{eq}}(P,V)
.
If
V
is also a
\mathfrak{g}
-module, with
\rho_{*}:\mathfrak{g}\rightarrow\mathfrak{gl}(V)
,
on account of i-ii,
\varpi
induces a covariant derivative

\bar{D}=d\ +\bar{\rho}_{*}(\varpi):\Omega^{\bullet}_{\text{tens}}(P,\rho)\rightarrow\Omega^{\bullet+1}_{\text{tens}}(P,\rho)
. The Bianchi identity can thus be written
\bar{D}\bar{\Omega}\equiv 0
.
It is easily seen that
\bar{D}\circ\bar{D}=\rho(\bar{\Omega})
. In view of Table
1
, one can see that
\bar{D}
will represent the
minimal coupling to gravity
.

For
(\bar{\rho},V)
a representation of
G
(the group with Lie algebra
\mathfrak{g}
), which is a representation of
H
by restriction, one defines tractor bundles:
\mathcal{T}\vcentcolon=P\times_{\bar{\rho}}V
.
The covariant derivative acting on
\Gamma(\mathcal{T})\simeq\Omega_{\text{tens}}(P,\bar{\rho})
is then called a
tractor derivative
, or
tractor connection
. An important example is, for
(\text{Ad},\mathfrak{g})
, the adjoint tractor bundle
\mathcal{A}M\vcentcolon=P\times_{\text{Ad}}\mathfrak{g}
. The Cartan curvature can be seen as a 2-form on
M
valued in
\mathcal{A}M
.

When
H
or
G
are
S\!O
groups, and
\tilde{\rho}:G\rightarrow\tilde{G}
are their spin lifts (representations), one may define spin bundles
\mathcal{S}\vcentcolon=P\times_{\tilde{\rho}}\tilde{V}
and spinor fields
\Gamma(\mathcal{S})\simeq\Omega_{\text{tens}}(P,\tilde{\rho})
.
For example, when
H=S\!O(1,3)
, so
\tilde{H}=S\!L(2,\mathbb{C})
,
\Gamma(\mathcal{S})
are Dirac spinors describing matter fields (fermions), as we have recalled earlier, and
\bar{D}
their coupling to gravity. For
G=S\!O(2,4)
, so
\tilde{G}=S\!U(2,2)
,
\Gamma(\mathcal{S})
are twistor fields (à la Penrose) and
\bar{D}
is the twistor connection.

Normal Cartan connection
.
The notion of normal Cartan connection, or normal Cartan geometry, generalises the idea of Levi-Civita connection in (pseudo-)Riemannian geometry. The idea, at least for physically interesting geometries (e.g. reductive or parabolic, see below), is to find a Cartan connection expressed uniquely in terms of the soldering part.
One thus imposes (algebraic and/or differential) constraints on
\varpi
so as to express its
\mathfrak{h}
-part
\omega
in terms of its
\mathfrak{g}/\mathfrak{h}
-part
\theta
.
Those constraints should be gauge-invariant, so they must be imposed on
\bar{\Omega}
.
The unique normal Cartan connection is thus
\varpi_{\text{{\tiny N}}}=\varpi_{\text{{\tiny N}}}(\theta)
.

For example, in Cartan-Riemann or Cartan-de Sitter (that we will see shortly) it is enough to impose the vanishing of the
\mathfrak{g}/\mathfrak{h}
-part of
\Omega
, i.e. vanishing torsion
\Theta=0
. In these cases, the
\mathfrak{h}
-part
\omega=\omega(\theta)
of
\varpi_{\text{{\tiny N}}}
is the usual Levi-Civita connection.
In the class of parabolic geometries, defined in the next section, there is a nice characterisation of normal Cartan connection in terms of the Kostant-Spencer cohomology of the parabolic algebra
\mathfrak{g}
– see
[
Cap-Slovak09
;
Ochiai
;
Cap-et-al1997
]
. As an example, we will give the normality conditions in the conformal Cartan geometry in the next section.

Physically, in vacuum General Relativity or for non-spinorial matter sources (scalar fields, like fluids, or dust as in cosmology), the gravitational d.o.f. are those of the metric, or the soldering form. The normality conditions thus eliminate the extra d.o.f. in the
\mathfrak{h}
-part of the Cartan connection, so that
\varpi_{\text{{\tiny N}}}
contains precisely the gravitational d.o.f.: the normal Cartan connection is exactly the gravitational potential.
In a more fundamental description, matter sources are described by spinor fields
\psi\in\Gamma(\mathcal{S})\simeq\Omega^{0}_{\text{tens}}(P,\tilde{\rho})
, and their coupling to gravity is given by
\bar{D}\psi=d\psi+\tilde{\rho}_{*}(\varpi)
(which contains the Dirac operator). The spinor field sources both the Einstein tensor and the torsion tensor: the Cartan connection, as the gravitational potential, thus cannot be normal.

Special cases and application to physics

Klein geometry
.
Consider a Lie group
G
and a closed subgroup
H
