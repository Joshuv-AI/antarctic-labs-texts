. 1.
Introduction

Endowed with the binary operation of setwise multiplication

(X,Y)\mapsto\{xy\colon x\in X,\kern 1.00006pty\in Y\}
,
the finite subsets of a (multiplicatively written) monoid
H
containing the neutral element
1_{H}
of
H
form a (multiplicatively written) monoid, hereby denoted by
\mathcal{P}_{\mathrm{fin},1}(H)
and called the
reduced
(
finitary
)
power monoid
of
H
. In particular, the neutral element of
\mathcal{P}_{\mathrm{fin},1}(H)
is the singleton
\{1_{H}\}
. (We refer to Howie’s monograph
[
12
]
for generalities on monoids.)

Reduced power monoids are the most basic items in a complex hierarchy of “highly non-cancellative” monoids, generically referred to as
power monoids
. These structures have been first systematically studied by Tamura and Shafer in the late 1960s
[
17
]
, and more recently investigated in a series of papers by Fan and Tringali
[
8
]
, Antoniou and Tringali
[
3
]
, Bienvenu and Geroldinger
[
4
]
, and Tringali and Yan
[
20
]
.

There are many reasons why power monoids are interesting. First, they provide a natural algebraic framework for a number of important problems in additive combinatorics and related fields, like Sárközy’s conjecture
[
16
, Conjecture 1.6]
on the “additive irreducibility” of the set of quadratic residues modulo
p
for all but finitely many primes
p\in\mathbb{N}
.
Second, the arithmetic of power monoids — with emphasis on questions concerned with the possibility or impossibility of factoring certain sets as a product of other sets that, in a sense, cannot be “broken down into smaller pieces” — is a rich topic in itself and has been pivotal to an ongoing radical transformation
[
19
,
5
,
6
,
18
,
7
]
of the “abstract theory of factorization”, a subfield of algebra so far largely confined to commutative domains and cancellative commutative monoids
[
9
,
11
,
2
]
. Third, power monoids (and, more generally, power semigroups) have long been known to play a central role in the theory of automata and formal languages
[
1
,
15
]
.

In the present paper, we address the problem of determining the automorphism group of
\mathcal{P}_{\mathrm{fin},1}(H)
. (Unless specified otherwise, a morphism will always mean a
monoid homomorphism
, cf. Remark
2.1
(1)
.)
Apart from being a natural question (the automorphisms of an object in a certain category are a measure of the “symmetries” of the object itself and their investigation is a fundamental problem in many areas of mathematics), a better understanding of the algebraic properties of power monoids will hopefully lead to a better understanding of other aspects of their theory, such as the following question, which arose from a conjecture of Bienvenu and Geroldinger
[
4
, Conjecture 4.7]
and was settled by the authors in
[
20
]
:

Question 1
.

Given a class
\mathcal{C}
of monoids, prove or disprove that
\mathcal{P}_{\mathrm{fin},1}(H)
is isomorphic to
\mathcal{P}_{\mathrm{fin},1}(K)
, for some
H,K\in\mathcal{C}
, if and only if
H
is isomorphic to
K
.

To set the stage, let
\mathrm{Aut}(M)
be the automorphism group of a monoid
M
. It is not difficult to show (see
[
20
, Remark 1.1]
) that, for each
f\in\mathrm{Aut}(H)
, the function

\mathcal{P}_{\mathrm{fin},1}(H)\to\mathcal{P}_{\mathrm{fin},1}(H)\colon X\mapsto f[X]:=\{f(x)\colon x\in X\}

is an automorphism of
\mathcal{P}_{\mathrm{fin},1}(H)
, henceforth referred to as the
augmentation
of
f
. Accordingly, we call an automorphism of
\mathcal{P}_{\mathrm{fin},1}(H)

inner
if it is the augmentation of an automorphism of
H
.

So, we have a well-defined map
\Phi\colon\mathrm{Aut}(H)\to\mathrm{Aut}(\mathcal{P}_{\mathrm{fin},1}(H))
sending an automorphism of
H
to its augmentation.
In addition, it is easily checked that

\Phi(g\circ f)(X)=g\circ f[X]=g[f[X]]=g[\Phi(f)(X)]=\Phi(g)(\Phi(f)(X))=\Phi(g)\circ\Phi(f)(X),

for all
f,g\in\mathrm{Aut}(H)
and
X\in\mathcal{P}_{\mathrm{fin},1}(H)
.
If, on the other hand,
f(x)\neq g(x)
for some
x\in H
and we take
X:=\{1_{H},x\}
, then
\Phi(f)(X)=\{1_{H},f(x)\}\neq\{1_{H},g(x)\}=\Phi(g)(X)
and hence
\Phi(f)\neq\Phi(g)
.

In other words,
\Phi
is an injective (group) homomorphism from
\mathrm{Aut}(H)
to
\mathrm{Aut}(\mathcal{P}_{\mathrm{fin},1}(H))
. It is therefore natural to ask if
\Phi
is also surjective and hence an isomorphism, which is tantamount to saying that every automorphism of
\mathcal{P}_{\mathrm{fin},1}(H)
is inner. Unsurprisingly, this is not generally the case and determining
\mathrm{Aut}(\mathcal{P}_{\mathrm{fin},1}(H))
is usually much harder than determining
\mathrm{Aut}(H)
, as we are going to demonstrate in the basic case where
H
is the additive monoid
(\mathbb{N},+)
of non-negative integers.

More precisely, let us denote by
\mathcal{P}_{\mathrm{fin},0}(\mathbb{N})
the reduced power monoid of
(\mathbb{N},+)
. In contrast to what we have done so far with
\mathcal{P}_{\mathrm{fin},1}(H)
, we will write
\mathcal{P}_{\mathrm{fin},0}(\mathbb{N})
additively, so that the operation on
\mathcal{P}_{\mathrm{fin},0}(\mathbb{N})
maps a pair
(X,Y)
of finite subsets of
\mathbb{N}
containing
0
to their sumset

X+Y:=\{x+y\colon x\in X,\kern 1.00006pty\in Y\}.

We establish in Theorem
2.5
that an endomorphism of
\mathcal{P}_{{\rm fin},0}(\mathbb{N})
is an automorphism if and only if it is surjective, if and only if it is injective and sends
\{0,1\}
to
\{0,1\}
. Then, in Theorem
3.2
, we prove that the only non-trivial automorphism of
\mathcal{P}_{{\rm fin},0}(\mathbb{N})
is the involution
X\mapsto\allowbreak\max X-\allowbreak X
. Since the automorphism group of
(\mathbb{N},+)
is trivial, this shows in particular that the automorphisms of
\mathcal{P}_{\mathrm{fin},0}(\mathbb{N})
are not all inner. The proof of the latter result proceeds through an unconventional induction on
what we call the
boxing dimension
of a finite set of integers, i.e., the smallest number of intervals whose union is the set itself.

We have striven to make our arguments as elementary and self-contained as possible, up to a classical theorem of Nathanson
[
13
, Theorem 1.1]
, sometimes called the
Fundamental Theorem of Additive Number Theory

[
14
, p. 3]
, that enters the proof of Lemma
2.2
. We propose directions for further research in Sect.
4
.

Notation and terminology.

We denote by
\mathbb{N}
the (set of) non-negative integers, by
\mathbb{N}^{+}
the positive integers, and by
|\cdot|
the cardinality of a set. If
a,b\in\mathbb{Z}\cup\{\pm\infty\}
, we let
\llbracket a,b\rrbracket:=\{x\in\allowbreak\mathbb{Z}\colon\allowbreak a\leq x\leq b\}
be the (
discrete
)
interval
from
a
to
b
. Given
h\in\mathbb{N}
and
k\in\mathbb{Z}
, we use
hX
for the
h
-fold sum and
k\times X:=\allowbreak\{kx\colon x\in X\}
for the
k
-dilate

of a set
X\subseteq\mathbb{Z}
, resp. Moreover, we put
-X:=-1\times X
and
X^{+}:=X\cap\mathbb{N}^{+}
.
Further notation and terminology, if not explained, are standard or should be clear from the context.

2.
Preliminaries

In this section, we work out a variety of preliminaries that will be used in Sect.
3
to prove Theorem
3.2
(that is, the main result of the paper).
We start with a short list of remarks.

Remarks 2.1
.

(1)

Let
H
and
K
be (multiplicatively written) monoids. A
semigroup
homomorphism from
H
to
K
is, by definition, a function
f
from
H
to
K
that satisfies the functional equation:

f(xy)=f(x)f(y),\qquad\text{for all }x,y\in H.

(1)

A solution to Eq. (
1
) will send the neutral element
1_{H}
of
H
to an idempotent of
K
. So, if the only idempotent of
K
is the neutral element
1_{K}
, then any semigroup homomorphism from
H
to
K
is also a
monoid
homomorphism (namely, it maps
1_{H}
to
1_{K}
). This happens, in particular, when
K
is the reduced power monoid of
(\mathbb{N},+)
. In fact, if
2X=X
for some
X\in\mathcal{P}_{\mathrm{fin},0}(\mathbb{N})
, then
2\max X=\max X
and hence
\max X=0
, which forces
X
to be the singleton
\{0\}
(i.e., the neutral element of
\mathcal{P}_{\mathrm{fin},0}(\mathbb{N})
).

(2)

The only automorphism of
(\mathbb{N},+)
is the identity function on
\mathbb{N}
, because
(\mathbb{N},+)
is a cyclic monoid generated by
1
and isomorphisms map generators to generators. Yet, the automorphism group of
\mathcal{P}_{\mathrm{fin},0}(\mathbb{N})
is non-trivial, for it contains the
reversion map

\textrm{rev}\colon\mathcal{P}_{\mathrm{fin},0}(\mathbb{N})\to\mathcal{P}_{\mathrm{fin},0}(\mathbb{N})\colon X\mapsto\max X-X.

Indeed,
\mathrm{rev}(\cdot)
is an involution (or self-inverse function); and it is clear that, for all
X,Y\in\mathcal{P}_{\mathrm{fin},0}(\mathbb{N})
,

\mathrm{rev}(X+Y)=\max(X+Y)-(X+\allowbreak Y)=\allowbreak(\max X-\allowbreak X)+\allowbreak(\max Y-\allowbreak Y)=\mathrm{rev}(X)+\allowbreak\mathrm{rev}(Y).

Since

\mathrm{rev}(\{0,1,3\})=\{0,\allowbreak 2,\allowbreak 3\}
, we thus get from item
(1)
that
\mathrm{rev}(\cdot)
is a non-trivial automorphism of
\mathcal{P}_{\mathrm{fin},0}(\mathbb{N})
. We will see in Theorem
3.2
that
\mathcal{P}_{\mathrm{fin},0}(\mathbb{N})
has no other non-trivial automorphism.

(3)

Let
H
be a (multiplicatively written) monoid. If
x
and
y
are fixed points of an endomorphism
f
of
H
, then so is, of course, the product
xy
. Thus, the set of fixed points of
f
is a submonoid of
H
.

We continue with a series of lemmas that will be crucial in the subsequent analysis. The first of these lemmas is essentially a generalization of
[
20
, Lemma 2.2]
.

Lemma 2.2
.

If
A\in\mathcal{P}_{\mathrm{fin},0}(\mathbb{N})
and
\{0,\max A\}\subseteq B\subseteq A
, then
(k+1)A=kA+B
for all large
k\in\mathbb{N}
.

Proof.

Set
a:=\max A
and
q:=\gcd A
. Since
kA+\{0,a\}\subseteq kA+B\subseteq(k+1)A
for every
k\in\mathbb{N}
, it suffices to show that
(k+1)A\subseteq kA+\{0,a\}
when
k
is large enough. We may also suppose
A\neq\{0\}
, or else
kA=\allowbreak\{0\}
for all
k\in\mathbb{N}
and the conclusion is obvious. Accordingly,
q
is a positive integer and there is no loss of generality in assuming that
q=1
, because taking
\hat{A}:=\{x/q\colon x\in A\}\subseteq\mathbb{N}
and
\hat{a}:=\max\hat{A}
yields (i)
\gcd\hat{A}=\allowbreak 1
and (ii)
(k+1)A\subseteq kA+\{0,a\}
, for some
k\in\mathbb{N}
, if and only if
(k+1)\hat{A}\subseteq k\hat{A}+\{0,\hat{a}\}
.

Consequently, we gather from the Fundamental Theorem of Additive Number Theory
[
13
, Theorem 1.1]
that there exist
c,d,k_{0}\in\mathbb{N}
and sets
C\subseteq\llbracket 0,c-2\rrbracket
and
D\subseteq\llbracket 0,d-2\rrbracket
such that

kA=C\cup\llbracket c,ka-d\kern 1.00006pt\rrbracket\cup(ka-D),\qquad\text{for each integer }k\geq k_{0}.

(2)

Now, fix an integer
h\geq\max\{k_{0},1+(c+d)/a\}
, and let
x\in(h+1)A
.
Since
h\geq k_{0}
, Eq. (
2
) holds for both
k=h
and
k=h+1
. So, either
x\in C\cup\llbracket c,ha-d-1\rrbracket
, and it is then clear that
x\in hA
; or
x-\allowbreak a\in\allowbreak\llbracket(h-1)a-d,ha-d\kern 1.00006pt\rrbracket\cup(ha-D)\subseteq hA
and then
x\in hA+a
, where we have especially used that
h\geq\allowbreak 1+\allowbreak(c+d)/a
and hence
(h-1)a-d\geq c
. In both cases, the conclusion is that
x\in hA+\{0,a\}
, which finishes the proof because
x
is an arbitrary element in
(h+1)A
.
∎

Lemma 2.3
.

Any automorphism of
\mathcal{P}_{\mathrm{fin},0}(\mathbb{N})
sends
2
-element sets to
2
-element sets.

Proof.

Let
\phi
be an automorphism of
\mathcal{P}_{\mathrm{fin},0}(\mathbb{N})
and fix
a\in\mathbb{N}^{+}
. We need to show that
B:=\phi(\{0,\allowbreak a\})=\allowbreak\{0,b\}
for a certain
b\in\mathbb{N}^{+}
.
For, set
n:=|B|-1
. Clearly,
n
is a positive integer, because
0\in B
and
B\neq\phi(\{0\})=\{0\}
(by the injectivity of
\phi
). Accordingly, let
b_{1},\ldots,b_{n}
be an enumeration of the positive elements of
B
with
b_{n}=\max B
. By Lemma
2.2
, we have that
(k+1)B=kB+\{0,b_{n}\}
for some
k\in\mathbb{N}
.

Put
A:=\phi^{-1}(\{0,b_{n}\})
, where
\phi^{-1}
is the functional inverse of
\phi
. Since
\phi^{-1}
is an automorphism of
\mathcal{P}_{\mathrm{fin},0}(\mathbb{N})
with
\phi^{-1}(B)=\{0,a\}
, we get from the above that

(k+1)\{0,a\}=(k+1)\phi^{-1}(B)=k\phi^{-1}(B)+\phi^{-1}(\{0,b_{n}\})=k\{0,a\}+A.

It follows that
\{0\}\subsetneq A\subseteq(k+1)\{0,a\}
and
\max A=(k+1)a-ka=a
. Considering that
a
is the least non-zero element of
(k+1)\{0,a\}
, this yields
A=\{0,\allowbreak a\}
and hence
B=\allowbreak\phi(\{0,a\})=\phi(A)=\{0,b_{n}\}
.
∎

Lemma 2.4
.

Let
f
be an endomorphism of
\mathcal{P}_{\mathrm{fin},0}(\mathbb{N})
, and let
X\in\mathcal{P}_{\mathrm{fin},0}(\mathbb{N})
. The following hold:

(i)

f(X)+kf(\{0,1\})=(k+\max X)f(\{0,1\})
for every integer
k\geq\max X
.

(ii)

\max f(X)=\max X\cdot\max f(\{0,1\})
.

Proof.

(i)
Let
k\in\mathbb{N}
. We have
\{0,\max X\}\subseteq X\subseteq\llbracket 0,\max X\rrbracket
and
h\kern 1.00006pt\{0,1\}=\llbracket 0,h\rrbracket
for all
h\in\mathbb{N}
. It is therefore seen that, for
k\geq\max X
,

\llbracket 0,k+\max X\rrbracket=\llbracket 0,k\rrbracket\cup(\max X+\llbracket 0,k\rrbracket)\subseteq X+k\kern 1.00006pt\{0,1\}\subseteq\llbracket 0,k+\max X\rrbracket,

which ultimately shows that

X+k\kern 1.00006pt\{0,1\}=\llbracket 0,k+\max X\rrbracket=(k+\max X)\{0,1\}.

Since
f(A+B)=f(A)+f(B)
for all
A,B\in\mathcal{P}_{\mathrm{fin},0}(\mathbb{N})
, this suffices to conclude.

(ii)

Using that
\max(A+B)=\max A+\max B
for all
A,B\in\mathcal{P}_{\mathrm{fin},0}(\mathbb{N})
, we get from item
(i)
that

\max f(X)+k\cdot\max f(\{0,1\})=(k+\max X)\cdot\max f(\{0,1\})

for every integer
k\geq\max X
. Clearly, this is enough to finish the proof.
∎

Our first theorem gives a characterization of the automorphisms of
\mathcal{P}_{\mathrm{fin},0}(\mathbb{N})
that is of independent interest and complementary to the main result (Theorem
3.2
).

Theorem 2.5
.

The following are equivalent for an endomorphism
f
of
\mathcal{P}_{\mathrm{fin},0}(\mathbb{N})
:

(a)

f
is injective and
f(\{0,1\})=\{0,1\}
.

(b)

f
is surjective.

(c)

f
is an automorphism.

Proof.

Obviously, “
(a)
and
(b)
”
\Rightarrow

(c)

\Rightarrow

(b)
. So, it suffices to show that
(a)

\Rightarrow

(b)

\Rightarrow

(a)
. For, denote by
\mathscr{S}_{A}
the family of all subsets
B
of a set
A\in\allowbreak\mathcal{P}_{\mathrm{fin},0}(\mathbb{N})
such that
\{0,\allowbreak\max A\}\subseteq\allowbreak B
. We will use without further comment that, since
\mathscr{S}_{A}
is a finite family, a function
\mathscr{S}_{A}\to\mathscr{S}_{A}
is injective if and only if it is surjective.

(a)

\Rightarrow

(b)
:
Let
Y\in\mathcal{P}_{\mathrm{fin},0}(\mathbb{N})
, and put
k:=\max Y
and
\mathscr{S}_{k}:=\mathscr{S}_{\llbracket 0,k\rrbracket}
. We need to check that
Y=f(X)
for some
X\in\allowbreak\mathcal{P}_{\mathrm{fin},0}(\mathbb{N})
. Considering that
f(\{0,\allowbreak 1\})=\allowbreak\{0,1\}
(by hypothesis), we have from Lemma
2.4
(ii)
that, for every
X\in\mathscr{S}_{k}
,
\max f(X)=\max X=k
and hence
f(X)\in\mathscr{S}_{k}
. Thus, the restriction of
f
(which is supposed to be injective) to the family
\mathscr{S}_{k}
results in a well-defined injection
f_{k}
from
\mathscr{S}_{k}
into itself. It follows that
f_{k}
is bijective and, since
Y\in\mathscr{S}_{k}
, there exists
X\in\mathscr{S}_{k}
such that
Y=\allowbreak f_{k}(X)=\allowbreak f(X)
.

(b)

\Rightarrow

(a)
: Set
h:=\max f(\{0,1\})\in\mathbb{N}
. By Lemma
2.4
(ii)
,
\max f(A)=h\max A
for each
A\in\allowbreak\mathcal{P}_{\mathrm{fin},0}(\mathbb{N})
. So, the surjectivity of
f
implies
h=\allowbreak 1
and
f(\{0,1\})=\{0,1\}
; in fact, if
h=0
then
f
is identically zero, and if
h\geq 2
then
\max f(A)\neq\allowbreak 1
for all
A\in\allowbreak\mathcal{P}_{\mathrm{fin},0}(\mathbb{N})
. It remains to show that
f
is also injective.

Assume
f(X)=f(Y)
for some
X,Y\in\mathcal{P}_{\mathrm{fin},0}(\mathbb{N})
. We need to verify that
X=Y
. Set
k:=\max f(X)
and
\mathscr{S}_{k}:=\mathscr{S}_{\llbracket 0,k\rrbracket}
. It follows from the above that
\max f(A)=\max A=k
for all
A\in\mathscr{S}_{k}
. Consequently, the sets
X
and
Y
are both in
\mathscr{S}_{k}
, and the restriction of
f
to
\mathscr{S}_{k}
yields a well-defined surjection
f_{k}
from
\mathscr{S}_{k}
onto itself. So, similarly as in the proof of the implication
(a)

\Rightarrow

(b)
,
f_{k}
is a bijection and, since
f_{k}(X)=\allowbreak f(X)=f(Y)=f_{k}(Y)
, we conclude that
X=Y
.
∎

Corollary 2.6
.

For an automorphism
f
of
\mathcal{P}_{\mathrm{fin},0}(\mathbb{N})
, the following hold:

(i)

\max X=\max f(X)
for every
X\in\mathcal{P}_{\mathrm{fin},0}(\mathbb{N})
.

(ii)

\{0,k\}
and
\llbracket 0,k\rrbracket
are fixed points of
f
for all
k\in\mathbb{N}
.

(iii)

Either
f(\{0,2,3\})=\{0,1,3\}
or
f(\{0,2,3\})=\{0,2,3\}
.

Proof.

(i)
This is a straightforward consequence of Lemma
2.4
(ii)
and Theorem
2.5
.

(ii)

Let
k\in\mathbb{N}
. By Theorem
2.5
, we have
f(\{0,1\})=\{0,1\}
. Since
\llbracket 0,k\rrbracket=k\kern 1.00006pt\{0,1\}
, it is thus clear that
f(\llbracket 0,k\rrbracket)=kf(\{0,1\})=\llbracket 0,k\rrbracket
; and since
\{0,k\}+\llbracket 0,k\rrbracket=\llbracket 0,2k\rrbracket=2\kern 1.00006pt\llbracket 0,k\rrbracket
, we find that

f(\{0,k\})+\llbracket 0,k\rrbracket=f(\{0,k\}+\llbracket 0,k\rrbracket)=2f(\llbracket 0,k\rrbracket)=\llbracket 0,2k\rrbracket.

(3)

In particular, we get from Eq. (
3
) that
\max f(\{0,k\})=2k-k=k
. So, recalling from Lemma
2.3
that an automorphism of
\mathcal{P}_{\mathrm{fin},0}(\mathbb{N})
sends
2
-elements sets to
2
-elements sets, we obtain that
f(\{0,\allowbreak k\})=\allowbreak\{0,k\}
.
In other words, we have proved that
\{0,k\}
and
\llbracket 0,k\rrbracket
are both fixed points of
f
.

(iii)
Set
Y:=f(\{0,2,3\})
. We gather from item
(ii)
that
f(\{0,3\})=\{0,3\}
and
f(\llbracket 0,3\rrbracket)=\llbracket 0,3\rrbracket
, and from item
(i)
that
\max Y=3
. By the injectivity of
f
, it follows that
\{0,3\}\subsetneq Y\subsetneq\llbracket 0,3\rrbracket
. Therefore, the only possibility is that either
Y=\{0,1,3\}
or
Y=\{0,2,3\}
.
∎

One consequence of Corollary
2.6
(i)
is that any automorphism
f
of
\mathcal{P}_{\mathrm{fin},0}(\mathbb{N})
gives rise to a well-defined function

, henceforth referred to as the

of

f^{\ast}(X):=\max X-\allowbreak f(X)
for all
X\in\mathcal{P}_{\mathrm{fin},0}(\mathbb{N})

The next lemma shows that something more is true.

Lemma 2.7
.

The reversal of an automorphism of
\mathcal{P}_{\mathrm{fin},0}(\mathbb{N})
is itself an automorphism of
\mathcal{P}_{\mathrm{fin},0}(\mathbb{N})
.

Proof.

Let
f
be an automorphism of
\mathcal{P}_{\mathrm{fin},0}(\mathbb{N})
, and let
X,Y\in\mathcal{P}_{\mathrm{fin},0}(\mathbb{N})
. On account of Remark
2.1
(1)
, the reversal
f^{\ast}
of
f
is an endomorphism of
\mathcal{P}_{\mathrm{fin},0}(\mathbb{N})
, as it is readily seen that

f^{\ast}(X+Y)=\max(X+Y)-f(X+Y)=(\max X-f(X))+(\max Y-f(Y))=f^{\ast}(X)+f^{\ast}(Y).

In addition, it is clear from the definition itself of
f^{\ast}
that
\max f^{\ast}(X)=\max X
and hence

f^{\ast}(f^{\ast}(X))=\max f^{\ast}(X)-f^{\ast}(X)=\max f(X)-(\max X-f(X))=f(X),

(4)

which yields
f^{\ast}\circ f^{\ast}=f
and shows that
f^{\ast}
is a bijection (since
f
is so). Hence,
f^{\ast}\in\mathrm{Aut}(\mathcal{P}_{\mathrm{fin},0}(\mathbb{N}))
.
∎

Given
X\subseteq\mathbb{Z}
, we denote by
\Delta(X)
the
gap set
of
X
, i.e., the set of all integers
d\geq 1
such that
\{x,\allowbreak x+\allowbreak d\}=\allowbreak X\cap\llbracket x,\allowbreak x+\allowbreak d\rrbracket
for some
x\in\mathbb{Z}
. Accordingly, we define
\overline{\mathrm{gap}}(X):=\allowbreak\sup\Delta(X)
, where
\sup\varnothing:=0
.

Remark 2.8
.

It is a simple exercise (and we leave it to the reader) to show that

\{0,\max X+h\}\subseteq X+\llbracket 0,h\rrbracket\subseteq\llbracket 0,\max X+h\rrbracket,\qquad\text{for all }X\in\mathcal{P}_{\mathrm{fin},0}(\mathbb{N})\text{ and }h\in\mathbb{N}.

(5)

Moreover, the right-most inclusion in Eq. (
5
) is strict if and only if
h\leq\overline{\mathrm{gap}}(X)-2
.

Note that, in the sequel, we shall often use the observations in Remark
2.8
without further comment.

Lemma 2.9
.

If
f
is an automorphism of
\mathcal{P}_{\mathrm{fin},0}(\mathbb{N})
, then
\overline{\mathrm{gap}}(X)=\overline{\mathrm{gap}}(f(X))
for all
X\in\mathcal{P}_{\mathrm{fin},0}(\mathbb{N})
.

Proof.

Let
X\in\mathcal{P}_{\mathrm{fin},0}(\mathbb{N})
and put
d:=\overline{\mathrm{gap}}(f(X))
. Since the functional inverse of
f
is itself an automorphism of
\mathcal{P}_{\mathrm{fin},0}(\mathbb{N})
, it suffices to prove that
\overline{\mathrm{gap}}(X)\leq d
. For, set
X^{\prime}:=X+\llbracket 0,d-1\rrbracket
and suppose to the contrary that
d<\overline{\mathrm{gap}}(X)
. Since
f(\{0\})=\allowbreak\{0\}
, the gap set of
X
is then a non-empty finite subset of
\mathbb{N}^{+}
and hence
d
is a
positive
integer. By Remark
2.8
and Corollary
2.6
(i)
, it follows that

f(X)+\llbracket 0,d-1\rrbracket=\llbracket 0,d-1+\max f(X)\rrbracket=\llbracket 0,d-1+\max X\rrbracket,

which, along with Corollary
2.6
(ii)
, implies

f(X^{\prime})=f(X)+f(\llbracket 0,d-1\rrbracket)=f(X)+\llbracket 0,d-1\rrbracket=f(\llbracket 0,d-1+\max X\rrbracket).

So, from the injectivity of
f
, we conclude that
X^{\prime}=\llbracket 0,d-1+\max X\rrbracket
. This is, however, in contradiction with Remark
2.8
, as we are supposing
d<\overline{\mathrm{gap}}(X)
.
∎

By Corollary
2.6
(iii)
, any automorphism of
\mathcal{P}_{\mathrm{fin},0}(\mathbb{N})
belongs to one of two classes; namely, it either fixes the set
\{0,2,3\}
, or it maps
\{0,2,3\}
to
\{0,1,3\}
. Our next goal is to demonstrate (in Lemma
2.11
) that an automorphism in the first class has also to fix certain sets of the form
\{0\}\cup\llbracket b,c\rrbracket
with
b,\allowbreak c\in\mathbb{N}
.

Proposition 2.10
.

For all
a,n\in\mathbb{N}
with
n\geq a+1
, it holds that

\sum_{i=0}^{n-1}\{0,a+i,a+i+1\}=\{0\}\cup\bigl\llbracket a,na+\tfrac{1}{2}n(n+1)\bigr\rrbracket.

(6)

Proof.

Let
A
be the set on the left-hand side of Eq. (
6
). For each
j\in\llbracket 1,n\rrbracket
, we have

\begin{split}A_{j}&:=\bigcup_{k=0}^{n-j}\kern 1.00006pt\sum_{i=k}^{k+j-1}\{a+i,a+i+1\}=\bigcup_{k=0}^{n-j}\sum_{i=0}^{j-1}(a+k+i+\llbracket 0,1\rrbracket)=\bigcup_{k=0}^{n-j}\left(aj+kj+\tfrac{1}{2}(j-1)j+\llbracket 0,j\rrbracket\right)\\
&\phantom{:}=aj+\tfrac{1}{2}(j-1)j+\bigcup_{k=0}^{n-j}(kj+\llbracket 0,j\rrbracket)=aj+\tfrac{1}{2}(j-1)j+\llbracket 0,(n-j+1)j\rrbracket;\end{split}

in particular,
A_{j}
is an interval contained in
A
, with

m_{j}:=\min A_{j}=aj+\frac{1}{2}(j-1)j\qquad\text{and}\qquad M_{j}:=\max A_{j}=m_{j}+(n-j+1)j.

(7)

Since
\min A^{+}=a
and
\max A=\sum_{i=0}^{n-1}(a+i+1)=na+\frac{1}{2}n(n+1)
, it follows that

B:=\{0\}\cup A_{1}\cup\cdots\cup A_{n}\subseteq A\subseteq\{0\}\cup\left\llbracket a,na+\tfrac{1}{2}n(n+1)\right\rrbracket=:A^{\prime}.

(8)

Now, using that
n\geq a+1
(by hypothesis), we need to show that
A=A^{\prime}
. If
n=1
(and hence
a=0
), then it is readily checked that
A=A^{\prime}=\{0,1\}
(and we are done). So, we may suppose
n\geq 2
.

The function
\phi\colon\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R}:x\mapsto(n-x)x
is increasing for
x\leq\frac{1}{2}n
and decreasing for
x\geq\frac{1}{2}n
, so that the minimum of
\phi
over the closed interval
[1,n-1]
is given by
\phi(1)=\phi(n-1)=n-1\geq\allowbreak a
.
Therefore, it is straightforward from Eq. (
7
) that, for all
j\in\llbracket 1,n-1\rrbracket
,

m_{j+1}=a(j+1)+\tfrac{1}{2}j(j+1)=m_{j}+a+j\leq m_{j}+(n-j)j+j=M_{j}.

Recalling that
A_{1},\ldots,A_{n}
are intervals and considering that
m_{1}=a
and
M_{n}=an+\frac{1}{2}n(n+1)
, we thus find that

B=\{0\}\cup\llbracket m_{1},M_{1}\rrbracket\cup\cdots\cup\llbracket m_{n},M_{n}\rrbracket=\{0\}\cup\llbracket m_{1},M_{n}\rrbracket=A^{\prime}
, which, by Eq. (
8
), yields
A=A^{\prime}
(as wished) and completes the proof.
∎

Lemma 2.11
.

Assume
\{0,2,3\}
is a fixed point of an automorphism
f
of
\mathcal{P}_{\mathrm{fin},0}(\mathbb{N})
. The following hold:

(i)

If
1\in X\in\mathcal{P}_{\mathrm{fin},0}(\mathbb{N})
, then
1\in f(X)
.

(ii)

f(\{0,a,a+1\})=\{0,a,a+1\}
for every
a\in\mathbb{N}
.

(iii)

\{0\}\cup\left\llbracket a,na+\frac{1}{2}n(n+1)\right\rrbracket
is a fixed point of
f
for all
a,n\in\mathbb{N}
with
n\geq a+1
.

Proof.

(i)
Let
X\in\mathcal{P}_{\mathrm{fin},0}(\mathbb{N})
and
k\in\mathbb{N}
. It is routine to show (by induction on
k
) that
k\{0,\allowbreak 2,3\}=\{0\}\cup\allowbreak\llbracket 2,\allowbreak 3k\rrbracket
(we leave the details to the reader). It is thus clear that, if
1\in X
and
k\geq(2+\max X)/3
, then

\llbracket 0,3k+\max X\rrbracket=(\{0,1\}\cup\{\max X\})+(\{0\}\cup\llbracket 2,3k\rrbracket)\subseteq X+k\{0,2,3\}\subseteq\llbracket 0,3k+\max X\rrbracket;

that is,
X+k\{0,2,3\}
is the interval
\llbracket 0,3k+\max X\rrbracket
and hence, by Corollary
2.6
(ii)
, a fixed point of
f
. So, by the hypothesis that
f
is a homomorphism with
f(\{0,2,3\})=\{0,2,3\}
, we conclude that, if
1\in X
and
k
is a sufficiently large integer, then

1\in\llbracket 0,3k+\max X\rrbracket=f(X+k\{0,2,3\})=f(X)+k\{0,2,3\}=f(X)+(\{0\}\cup\llbracket 2,3k\rrbracket).

(9)

But this is only possible if
1\in f(X)
, since the least
positive
element of the set on the right-most side of Eq. (
9
) is the minimum between
2
and the least positive element of
f(X)
(note that
\{0\}\subsetneq f(X)
).

(ii)
Let
a\in\mathbb{N}
, and set
X:=\{0,a,a+1\}
and
Y:=f(X)
. We need to prove
Y=X
. If
a=0
or
a=1
, then
X
is an interval and we are done by Corollary
2.6
(ii)
. So, assume
a\geq 2
.

We gather from Corollary
2.6
(i)
that
\max Y=\max X=a+1
; and from Lemma
2.9
that
\delta:=\overline{\mathrm{gap}}(Y)=\overline{\mathrm{gap}}(X)=a\geq 2
.
It follows that
Y
does not contain any integer
y
in the interval
\llbracket 2,a-1\rrbracket
, or else we would find that

\delta\leq\allowbreak\min(y,\allowbreak a+\allowbreak 1-\allowbreak y)\leq\allowbreak a-1<\delta
(a contradiction). Considering that the functional inverse of
f
is an automorphism of
\mathcal{P}_{\mathrm{fin},0}(\mathbb{N})
sending
Y
to
X
, we infer on the other hand from item
(i)
that
1\notin Y
. So putting everything together, we conclude from Corollary
2.6
(ii)
that
\{0,a+1\}\subsetneq Y\subseteq\allowbreak\{0,\allowbreak a,\allowbreak a+1\}=\allowbreak X
, which shows that
Y=X
and completes the proof.

(iii)
Given
a,n\in\mathbb{N}
with
n\geq a+1
, we have from Proposition
2.10
that
\{0\}\cup\bigl\llbracket a,na+\frac{1}{2}n(n+1)\bigr\rrbracket
can be written as the sum of the sets
\{0,a+i,\allowbreak a+\allowbreak i+1\}
as
i
ranges over the interval
\llbracket 0,n-1\rrbracket
.
By item
(ii)
and Remark
2.1
(3)
(specialized to
\mathcal{P}_{\mathrm{fin},0}(\mathbb{N})
), this is enough to prove the claim.
∎

3.
Main result

Given a set
S\subseteq\mathbb{Z}
, we denote by
\mathrm{b.dim}(S)
the smallest integer
k\geq 0
for which there exist
k
(discrete) intervals whose union is
S
, with the understanding that if no such
k
exists then
\mathrm{b.dim}(S):=\infty
. We call
\mathrm{b.dim}(S)
the
boxing dimension
of
S
. A couple of remarks are in order.

Remark 3.1
.

(1)

The boxing dimension of a set
S\subseteq\mathbb{Z}
is zero if and only if
S
is empty, and it is one if and only if
S
is an interval. More generally, let us say that two sets
X,Y\subseteq\mathbb{Z}
are
well separated
if
|x-y|\geq 2
for all
x\in X
and
y\in Y
(so, well-separated sets are disjoint). It is then easily checked that the boxing dimension of
S
is equal to a certain integer
k\geq 0
if and only if
S
can be written as a union of
k
well-separated non-empty intervals (we leave the details to the reader).

For instance, the sets
A:=\{0,5\}\cup\mathbb{N}_{\geq 7}
and
B:=\{-2,2,3\}
are well separated, with
\mathrm{b.dim}(A)=3
and
\mathrm{b.dim}(B)=2
. To the contrary, the (set of) even integers and the odd integers are not well separated, and the boxing dimension of each of them is infinite.

(2)

It is obvious that
\mathrm{b.dim}(X\cup Y)\leq\mathrm{b.dim}(X)+\allowbreak\mathrm{b.dim}(Y)
for all
X,Y\subseteq\mathbb{Z}
, a property we refer to as
subadditivity
(in analogy with other notions of “dimension” in union-closed families of sets).

The proof of the next theorem (that is, the main theorem of the paper) is essentially an induction on the boxing dimension of a set
X\in\mathcal{P}_{\mathrm{fin},0}(\mathbb{N})
.

Theorem 3.2
.

The only automorphisms of the reduced power monoid
\mathcal{P}_{\mathrm{fin},0}(\mathbb{N})
of
(\mathbb{N},+)
are the identity
X\mapsto X
and the reversion map
X\mapsto\max X-X
.

Proof.

Let
\Gamma
be the set of automorphisms of
\mathcal{P}_{\mathrm{fin},0}(\mathbb{N})
that fix
\{0,2,3\}
, and define
\Gamma^{\prime}:=\{f^{\ast}\colon f\in\Gamma\}
, where
f^{\ast}
denotes the reversal of
f
. We infer from Corollary
2.6
(iii)
and Lemma
2.7
that
\mathrm{Aut}(\mathcal{P}_{\mathrm{fin},0}(\mathbb{N}))=\Gamma\cup\allowbreak\Gamma^{\prime}
.
It is therefore enough to show that the only automorphism in
\Gamma
is the identity
X\mapsto X
, whose reversal is indeed the reversion map
X\mapsto\max X-X
.

For, let
f\in\Gamma
and
X\in\mathcal{P}_{\mathrm{fin},0}(\mathbb{N})
, and put
Y:=f(X)
,
r:=\mathrm{b.dim}(X)-1
,
s:=\mathrm{b.dim}(Y)-1
, and
t:=\allowbreak\min(r,\allowbreak s)
. We get from Corollary
2.6
(i)
that
\mu:=\max(X)=\max(Y)
. Moreover,
r
and
s
are non-negative integers, since the empty set is the only set whose boxing dimension is zero. Hence, by Remark
3.1
, there exist increasing sequences
x_{0},x_{1},\ldots,x_{2r+1}
and
y_{0},y_{1},\ldots,y_{2s+1}
of integers such that

(I)

x_{2i-1}+2\leq x_{2i}
for each
i\in\llbracket 1,r\rrbracket
and
y_{2j-1}+2\leq y_{2j}
for each
j\in\llbracket 1,s\rrbracket
;

(II)

X=\llbracket x_{0},x_{1}\rrbracket\cup\cdots\cup\llbracket x_{2r},x_{2r+1}\rrbracket
and
Y=\llbracket y_{0},y_{1}\rrbracket\cup\cdots\cup\llbracket y_{2s},y_{2s+1}\rrbracket
.

In particular,
x_{0}=y_{0}=0
and
x_{2r+1}=y_{2s+1}=\mu
. We will prove by induction on
r
that
X=Y
.

If
r=0
, then
X
is an interval and we have from Corollary
2.6
(ii)
that
X=f(X)=Y
(as wished).
So, let
r\geq\allowbreak 1
, assume for the sake of induction that
f(S)=S
for all
S\in\mathcal{P}_{\mathrm{fin},0}(\mathbb{N})
with
\mathrm{b.dim}(S)\leq r
, and suppose by way of contradiction that
X\neq Y
.
Accordingly, there is a smallest index
v\in\allowbreak\llbracket 1,2t+1\rrbracket
such that
x_{v}\neq y_{v}
; otherwise, since
x_{0}\leq x_{1}\leq\cdots\leq x_{2r+1}
and
y_{0}\leq y_{1}\leq\cdots\leq y_{2s+1}
, we would get from conditions
(I)
and
(II)
that

X=Y
, which is absurd. We distinguish two cases, depending on whether
v
is even or odd. But first, some observations are in order.

To start with, there is no loss of generality in assuming
x_{v}<y_{v}
; otherwise, we could replace
f
with its functional inverse
f^{-1}
, noting that
f^{-1}
is an automorphism of
\mathcal{P}_{\mathrm{fin},0}(\mathbb{N})
with the same fixed points as
f
. Next, it is clear that
r\leq s
, or else
Y
is fixed by
f
(by the inductive hypothesis) and hence
f(Y)=\allowbreak Y=\allowbreak f(X)
, which is still absurd since
f
is injective (and we have
X\neq Y
). It follows that
1\leq\allowbreak v\leq\allowbreak 2r
, because
v=2r+1
would imply
\mu=x_{2r+1}<y_{2r+1}\leq y_{2s+1}=\mu
. Lastly, we may suppose that, if
X^{\prime}
is another set in
\mathcal{P}_{\mathrm{fin},0}(\mathbb{N})
with
\mathrm{b.dim}(X^{\prime})=r+1
and
X^{\prime}\neq f(X^{\prime})
, then
\mathrm{b.dim}(Y)\leq\allowbreak\mathrm{b.dim}(f(X^{\prime}))
; otherwise, we could trade
X
for
X^{\prime}
and iterate the argument until the condition is satisfied (since
\mathrm{b.dim}(Y)<\infty
, this will happen after finitely many iterations). In other words, we may assume that
X
is
\mathrm{b.dim}
-cominimal
in the (non-empty) family
\mathscr{S}_{r}
of all
S\in\mathcal{P}_{\mathrm{fin},0}(\mathbb{N})
with
\mathrm{b.dim}(S)=r+\allowbreak 1
and
S\neq f(S)
, by which we mean that
\mathrm{b.dim}(Y)\leq\mathrm{b.dim}(f(S))
for every
S\in\mathscr{S}_{r}
.

Case 1:

v=2u
for some
u\in\llbracket 1,r\rrbracket
. Put
d:=x_{2u}-x_{2u-1}-1
and
I:=\llbracket 0,r\rrbracket\smallsetminus\{u-1,u\}
, and set

X_{1}:=\llbracket x_{2(u-1)},x_{2u+1}+d\rrbracket\qquad\text{and}\qquad X_{2}:=\bigcup_{i\in I}\llbracket x_{2i},x_{2i+1}+d\rrbracket;

in particular, if
I=\varnothing
, then
X_{2}=\varnothing
. It is a basic fact that

\llbracket a,b\rrbracket+\llbracket a^{\prime},b^{\prime}\rrbracket=\llbracket a+a^{\prime},b+b^{\prime}\rrbracket,\qquad\text{for all }a,b,a^{\prime},b^{\prime}\in\mathbb{Z}\text{ with }a\leq b\text{ and }a^{\prime}\leq b^{\prime}.

So, using that
X=\llbracket x_{0},x_{1}\rrbracket\cup\cdots\cup\llbracket x_{2r},x_{2r+1}\rrbracket
and
x_{2u-1}+d=x_{2u}-1
, we have

X+\llbracket 0,d\rrbracket=\bigcup_{i=0}^{r}(\llbracket x_{2i},x_{2i+1}\rrbracket+\llbracket 0,d\rrbracket)=\bigcup_{i=0}^{r}\llbracket x_{2i},x_{2i+1}+d\rrbracket=X_{1}\cup X_{2}.

(10)

In a similar way,
Y=\llbracket y_{0},y_{1}\rrbracket\cup\cdots\cup\llbracket y_{2s},y_{2s+1}\rrbracket
leads to

Y+\llbracket 0,d\rrbracket=\bigcup_{j=0}^{s}\llbracket y_{2j},y_{2j+1}+d\rrbracket.

(11)

It follows from Eq. (
10
) and Remark
3.1
(2)
that

\mathrm{b.dim}(X+\llbracket 0,d\rrbracket)\leq\mathrm{b.dim}(X_{1})+\mathrm{b.dim}(X_{2})\leq 1+|I|=r<\mathrm{b.dim}(X).

Consequently, we conclude from the inductive hypothesis and Corollary
2.6
(ii)
that

X+\llbracket 0,d\rrbracket=f(X+\llbracket 0,d\rrbracket)=f(X)+f(\llbracket 0,d\rrbracket)=Y+\llbracket 0,d\rrbracket.

This is however impossible, as we get from Eqs. (
10
) and (
11
) that
x_{2u}
is an element of
X+\llbracket 0,d\rrbracket
but not of
Y+\allowbreak\llbracket 0,d\rrbracket
(note that
y_{2u-1}+d=x_{2u-1}+(x_{2u}-x_{2u-1}-1)<x_{2u}<y_{2u}
).

Case 2:

v=2u+1
for some
u\in\llbracket 0,r-1\rrbracket
.
Let
b
be an integer
\geq b_{0}:=y_{2u+1}+\max(x_{2r},y_{2s})
.
Since
x_{0},\allowbreak\ldots,x_{2r+1}
and
y_{0},\allowbreak\ldots,\allowbreak y_{2s+1}
are increasing sequences of non-negative integers with
0=y_{0}=x_{0}<\allowbreak x_{2r}<\allowbreak x_{2r+1}=y_{2s+1}=\mu
, we have

\begin{split}X^{\prime}&:=\bigcup_{i=0}^{r}\llbracket x_{2i}+y_{2u+1}+1,x_{2i+1}+b\rrbracket\subseteq\llbracket y_{2u+1}+1,\mu+b\rrbracket=\llbracket y_{2u+1}+1,b\rrbracket\cup\llbracket b+1,\mu+b\rrbracket\\
&\phantom{:}\subseteq\llbracket x_{0}+y_{2u+1}+1,x_{1}+b\rrbracket\cup\llbracket x_{2r}+y_{2u+1}+1,x_{2r+1}+b\rrbracket\subseteq X^{\prime}\end{split}

(12)

and, in an analogous fashion,

\begin{split}Y^{\prime}&:=\bigcup_{j=0}^{s}\llbracket y_{2j}+y_{2u+1}+1,y_{2j+1}+b\rrbracket\subseteq\llbracket y_{2u+1}+1,\mu+b\rrbracket=\llbracket y_{2u+1}+1,b\rrbracket\cup\llbracket b+1,\mu+b\rrbracket\\
&\phantom{:}\subseteq\llbracket y_{0}+y_{2u+1}+1,y_{1}+b\rrbracket\cup\llbracket y_{2s}+y_{2u+1}+1,y_{2s+1}+b\rrbracket\subseteq Y^{\prime}.\end{split}

(13)

From the above and condition
(II)
(cf. the derivation of Eqs. (
10
) and (
11
) in
Case 1
), it follows that

X+\llbracket y_{2u+1}+1,b\rrbracket=X^{\prime}=\llbracket y_{2u+1}+1,\mu+b\rrbracket=Y^{\prime}=Y+\llbracket y_{2u+1}+1,b\rrbracket.

(14)

Let us define

a:=\min(x_{2r},y_{2u+1}+1)\qquad\text{and}\qquad Z:=\{0\}\cup\llbracket y_{2u+1}+1,b\rrbracket.

Taking
X_{\ast}:=\llbracket x_{0},x_{1}\rrbracket\cup\cdots\cup\llbracket x_{2(r-1)},x_{2(r-1)+1}\rrbracket
and considering that

X=X_{\ast}\cup\llbracket x_{2r},x_{2r+1}\rrbracket\qquad\text{and}\qquad\llbracket x_{2r},x_{2r+1}\rrbracket\cup\llbracket y_{2u+1}+1,\mu+b\rrbracket=\llbracket a,\mu+b\rrbracket,

(15)

it is readily seen that

X+Z=X\cup(X+\llbracket y_{2u+1}+1,b\rrbracket)\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\eqref{equ:double-equality}}}{{=}}X\cup\llbracket y_{2u+1}+1,\mu+b\rrbracket\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\eqref{equ:decomposing-X}}}{{=}}X_{\ast}\cup\llbracket a,\mu+b\rrbracket.

(16)

Likewise, taking
Y^{\ast}:=\llbracket y_{2u},y_{2u+1}\rrbracket\cup\cdots\cup\llbracket y_{2s},y_{2s+1}\rrbracket
and
considering that

Y_{\ast}:=Y\smallsetminus Y^{\ast}=\bigcup_{j=0}^{u-1}\llbracket y_{2j},y_{2j+1}\rrbracket\qquad\text{and}\qquad Y^{\ast}\cup\llbracket y_{2u+1}+1,\mu+b\rrbracket=\llbracket y_{2u},\mu+b\rrbracket,

(17)

we find that

Y+Z=Y\cup(Y+\llbracket y_{2u+1}+1,b\rrbracket)\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\eqref{equ:double-equality}}}{{=}}Y\cup\llbracket y_{2u+1}+1,\mu+b\rrbracket\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\eqref{equ:decomposing-Y}}}{{=}}Y_{\ast}\cup\llbracket y_{2u},\mu+b\rrbracket.

(18)

In view of Remark
3.1
(2)
, we thus get from Eq. (
16
) that

\mathrm{b.dim}(X+Z)\leq\mathrm{b.dim}(X_{\ast})+\mathrm{b.dim}(\llbracket a,\mu+b\rrbracket)=r+1=\mathrm{b.dim}(X),

(19)

and from Eq. (
18
) that

\mathrm{b.dim}(Y+Z)\leq\mathrm{b.dim}(Y_{\ast})+\mathrm{b.dim}(\llbracket y_{2u},\mu+b\rrbracket)=u+1\leq(r-1)+1\leq s<\mathrm{b.dim}(Y).

(20)

In light of Lemma
2.11
(iii)
, we can on the other hand choose the integer
b
(subjected to the constraint
b\geq b_{0}
) in such a way that
f(Z)=Z
and, hence,

f(X+Z)=f(X)+f(Z)=Y+Z.

(21)

Accordingly, we gather from Eqs. (
19
) and (
20
) that either
\mathrm{b.dim}(X+Z)\leq r
, or
\mathrm{b.dim}(X+Z)=r+1
and
\mathrm{b.dim}(f(X+Z))<\mathrm{b.dim}(Y)
. In any case, we conclude by the inductive hypothesis and the
\mathrm{b.dim}

X
that
X+Z

f

This is, however, impossible and finishes the proof. In fact, Eq. (

4.
Prospects for future research

We have found (Theorem
3.2
) that the automorphism group of the reduced power monoid
\mathcal{P}_{\mathrm{fin},0}(\mathbb{N})
of the additive monoid
(\mathbb{N},+)
of non-negative integers has order
2
, the only non-trivial automorphism of
\mathcal{P}_{\mathrm{fin},0}(\mathbb{N})
being the reversion map
X\mapsto\max X-X
. In fact, there is some evidence that the result can be pushed a little further.
More precisely, recall that a
numerical monoid
is a submonoid
S
of
(\mathbb{N},+)
such that
\mathbb{N}\smallsetminus S
is a finite set. We then have the following:

Conjecture
.

The automorphism group of the reduced power monoid of a numerical monoid
properly
contained in
\mathbb{N}
is trivial (that is, the only automorphism is the identity).

Moreover, regardless of whether the conjecture is true or not, we are naturally led to consider an “inverse problem” that is probably woven much deeper in the theory:

Question 2
.

For which groups
G
does there exist a monoid
H
such that the automorphism group of the reduced power monoid
\mathcal{P}_{\mathrm{fin},1}(H)
of
H
is isomorphic to
G
?

We know from Sect.
1
that the automorphism group of the monoid
H
embeds into the automorphism group of
\mathcal{P}_{\mathrm{fin},1}(H)
. Yet, it is not quite clear how this can help to answer the question, as we gather from Theorem
3.2
that the latter group can be larger than the former.
