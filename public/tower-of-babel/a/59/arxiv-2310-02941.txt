Hoeffding’s Inequality for Markov Chains under Generalized Concentrability Condition
Hao Chen chen.6945@osu.edu, Yin Sun yzs0078@auburn.edu, Ness Shroff shroff.11@osu.edu

Abstract
This paper studies Hoeffding’s inequality for Markov chains under the generalized concentrability condition defined via integral probability metric (IPM). The generalized concentrability condition establishes a framework that interpolates and extends the existing hypotheses of Markov chain Hoeffding-type inequalities. The flexibility of our framework allows Hoeffding’s inequality to be applied beyond the ergodic Markov chains in the traditional sense. We demonstrate the utility by applying our framework to several non-asymptotic analyses arising from the field of machine learning, including (i) a generalization bound for empirical risk minimization with Markovian samples, (ii) a finite sample guarantee for Ployak-Ruppert averaging of SGD, and (iii) a new regret bound for rested Markovian bandits with general state space.



Hao Chen, Abhishek Gupta, Yin Sun, and Ness Shroff

Introduction

In this paper, we revisit Hoeffding’s inequality for general state space Markov Chains through the lens of integral probability metric (IPM). The general state space considered here is
Polish space
, i.e., a separable completely metrizable space. Concentration inequalities provide upper bounds on the tail probability of large deviation, that is, the deviation of the sum of random variables from its mean. One of the most important and celebrated concentration inequalities was first proposed by
Hoeffding 1994
. Hoeffding’s inequality states that for
n
independent bounded random variables
Y_{i}\in[a_{i},b_{i}]
,
i=1,\cdots,n
and some
\epsilon>0
, the following holds

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\bigg(\bigg|\sum_{i=1}^{n}Y_{i}-\sum_{i=1}^{n}\mathbb{E}(Y_{i})\bigg|\geq n\epsilon\bigg)\leq 2\exp\bigg(\frac{-2(n\epsilon)^{2}}{\sum_{i=1}^{n}(b_{i}-a_{i})^{2}}\bigg).

(1)

Inequalities of this type are also called concentration of measure. Hoeffding’s inequality gives a sub-Gaussian bound on the tail probability, i.e., the right-hand side of Equation
1
decays as
\exp[-\Theta(n\epsilon^{2})]
. There are numerous other concentration inequalities for
independent
random variables that provide tail bounds with varying decay rates depending on the probability distributions of the random variables. Some related results are Bernstein’s inequality and Bennett’s inequality with decay rates
\exp[-\Theta(n\epsilon)]
and
\exp[-\Theta(n\epsilon\log\epsilon+n\epsilon)]
, respectively. Interested readers can refer to
Boucheron et al. 2013
for a complete overview of this topic. These results are widely used in statistics, econometrics, causal inference, machine learning, reinforcement learning, empirical process theory, and other fields as a tool for non-asymptotic analysis.

The independence assumption in the original Hoeffding’s inequality limits its usage to applications where dependence among random variables naturally arises. Many research efforts have been dedicated to the extension of this result to random variables with various dependence structures, such as martingales
(
Azuma 1967
, see Azuma-Hoeffding inequality)
. In particular, Hoeffding-type inequalities for Markov chains have attracted much attention during the past few decades, due to their prevalence in statistics, machine learning, and stochastic dynamic systems. For example, Markovian dependence occurs broadly in Markov chain Monte Carlo (MCMC), time series prediction, and reinforcement learning.

Hoeffding-type inequalities for finite state space Markov chains are well-studied in the past
(
Davisson et al. 1981
;
Watanabe and Hayashi 2017
)
. See
Moulos and Anantharam 2019
;
Moulos 2020
for a recent survey on this topic. For
general
state space Markov chains, Hoeffding’s inequality was originally studied
(
Glynn and Ormoneit 2002
)
under Doeblin’s condition
(
Douc et al. 2018
)
which implies uniform ergodicity of Markov chains. More relaxed conditions were not considered until very recently.

Unlike finite or countably infinite state space Markov chains, uniform ergodicity for general state space Markov chains is not met easily. As implied by Doeblin’s condition, the Markov chain is required to jump, within a few steps, to anywhere in the state space with nontrivial probability given an arbitrary initial condition. Physical processes, such as the motion of vehicles and the articulation of robotic arms, often naturally follow continuity, which means the system state is not likely to evolve far from its starting point within a short period of time. Therefore, uniform ergodicity assumption can lead to two major inefficiencies in the analysis: (i) excluding many “well-behaving” systems, such as a stable linear time-invariant system; and (ii) yielding loose bounds for the tail probability, which are made precise in Section
4
.

For example, a common assumption in reinforcement learning is that the system is uniformly ergodic under any control policy. However, many reinforcement learning algorithms are tested in OpenAI Gym
(
Brockman et al. 2016
)
environments that are deterministic robotic systems. They do not satisfy the uniform ergodicity under a deterministic control policy, as the singular nature of Dirac measures violates the minorization condition. However, under a stabilizing control policy, these systems satisfy weaker versions of ergodicity, such as ergodicity in the sense of the Wasserstein metric. Similarly, in multi-armed bandit algorithms, the usual assumption is that the reward distributions are i.i.d. However, bandits that are driven by physical systems feature reward samples that are Markovian in nature
(
Tekin and Liu 2012
;
Anantharam et al. 1987
)
with hard-to-determine convergence properties. The existing results on this topic assume finite state space and Markov chains are uniformly ergodic, which is a strong assumption to impose on physical systems and leads to limited utility in practice. Online risk minimization with temporally correlated samples is another example. A generalization error bound was proposed for
\beta
-mixing processes
(
Agarwal and Duchi 2012
)
. In the context of Markovian processes, this corresponds to uniform ergodicity which, again, is very rigid to use in practice.

The aforementioned bottlenecks prohibit the application of Hoeffding-type inequality in the analysis of learning algorithms in dynamic systems which are commonly found in reinforcement learning. Various research efforts have been dedicated to the relaxation of uniform ergodicity used in
Glynn and Ormoneit 2002
. In
Miasojedow 2014
and
Fan et al. 2021
, the Markov transition kernel is viewed as a linear operator on
L^{2}(\pi)
where
\pi
is the invariant distribution. By assuming a positive spectral gap of the transition kernel, the authors are able to show Hoeffding’s inequalities for geometrically ergodic Markov chains. A recent study
(
Sandrić and Šebek 2023
)
shows a version of Hoeffding’s inequality for Markov chains by using the Wasserstein metric in place of the total variation (TV) distance. By considering a weaker metric for the Markov chain convergence condition, Hoeffding’s inequality now can be extended to non-irreducible Markov chains which are not covered by the classical ergodicity conditions (Example
38
).

Despite the various research efforts made to relax the uniform ergodicity, they still leave something to be desired—
does there exist a flexible notion of ergodicity that generalizes the existing ones and adapts to different use cases in practice?
We answer this question positively by proposing a novel notion of Markov chains convergence via integral probability metric (IPM)
(
Müller 1997
)
and studying Hoeffding’s inequality for Markov chains converging in general integral probability metric. IPM is a statistical distance function that has been successfully applied to generative adversarial networks (GAN), distributionally robust optimization (DRO), and other machine learning problems (see Section
2.3
). It can be intuitively thought of as the maximal discrepancy between to probability distribution seen by a class of functions
\mathbf{F}
, or generators:

\displaystyle\textnormal{{IPM}}_{\mathbf{F}}(\mu,\nu)=\sup_{f\in\mathbf{F}}|\mathbb{E}_{\mu}f-\mathbb{E}_{\nu}f|,

where
f
is referred to as the witness function. IPM provides a framework that recovers many probability metrics by choosing the appropriate function class (or generator)
\mathbf{F}
(Table
3
). The strength of the probability metric can be easily controlled by the size of
\mathbf{F}
. This allows us to extend the convergence of Markov chains in terms of total variation metric to a wide array of distance functions. Our main results, formally stated in Theorem
9
and
13
show that, depending on the function applied to the Markov chain, Hoeffding-type inequality holds when the Markov chain converges in a weaker sense (than TV metric). In most cases, it is unnecessary to enforce the ergodicity on the Markov chain if the functions of interest lie in a smaller set than bounded measurable functions. Thus, our results consider richer classes of Markov chains and the corresponding function classes for computing the concentration of measure results, which have not been considered before. Additionally, we refine the existing results by providing a sharper tail bound than those assuming the ergodicity. Our assumption on the Markov is adaptive to the functions applied to the Markov chain, which makes our theory a flexible framework that generalizes many existing studies.

The remainder of the paper is organized as follows. The rest of Section
1
contains the summary of the main contributions of this paper and an introduction to the notations. Section
2
is dedicated to the review of the integral probability metric and its application. Section
3
presents the main results of this paper and the proofs are deferred to the Appendix. Section
4
reviews the existing Hoeffding-type inequalities in the literature and establishes their connections with our framework. Applications of Hoeffding’s inequality to machine learning are presented in Section
5
.

1.1
Contributions

The contributions of this paper are summarized as follows.

1.

Extending the Notion for Markov Chain Convergence.

We propose, via IPM, the
generalized concentrability condition
and
IPM Dobrushin coefficient
for general state space Markov chain. We argue that these two new concepts along with IPM itself should be treated as a framework to study the convergence of Markov chains. We demonstrate their unifying power by recovering and extending the traditional conditions on Markov chains assumed for concentration inequalities, such as the uniform ergodicity
(
Douc et al. 2018
;
Meyn and Tweedie 2012
)
, V-uniformly ergodicity
(
Douc et al. 2011
)
, Wasserstein ergodicity
(
Sandrić and Šebek 2023
;
Rudolf and Schweizer 2018
)
, and weak convergence
(
Diaconis and Freedman 1999
;
Breiman 1960
)
.

The weak convergence of Markov chains to an invariant distribution is studied in
Diaconis and Freedman 1999
and
Breiman 1960
under the iterated random operator theory. The convergence of Markov chains in the Wasserstein metric is studied in
Hairer et al. 2011
and
Butkovsky 2014
. Our motivation for further expanding the notion of Markov chain convergence is to increase the flexibility of the theory to accommodate the ever-changing real-world challenges. Characterizing the Markov chain convergence with IPM is suitable for this task as the strength of the distance function can be controlled via the choice of generators.

2.

Hoeffding’s Inequality under Weaker Assumptions on the Markov Chain.

We relax the assumptions on the Markov chains required by Hoeffding-type inequalities. In the main results, we emphasize the link between the functions applied on the Markov chains and the generator of the IPM. Armed with this insight, one can identify the unnecessarily strong conditions used in some existing works. For example, the empirical mean of a bounded Lipschitz function concentrates around its mean (under invariant distribution) with Hoeffding-type tail bound as soon as the Markov chain converges in the bounded Lipschitz metric under a certain speed. However, according to the existing state-of-the-art result
(see
Sandrić and Šebek 2023
, Theorem 1)
, the convergence needs to happen under the Wasserstein-1 metric which is known to be slightly stronger.

3.

Unifying and Strengthening Different Versions of Concentration Inequalities.

Our work unifies, and in some cases strengthens, multiple previous results on Hoeffding’s inequality for general state space Markov chains. Many existing results on Hoeffding-type inequalities for Markov chains emerge as special cases under our framework, including
Glynn and Ormoneit 2002
;
Douc et al. 2011
;
Fan et al. 2021
;
Sandrić and Šebek 2023
to name a few. For example, we strengthen the Hoeffding inequality for uniformly ergodic Markov chain derived in
Glynn and Ormoneit 2002
by a tighter upper bound on the martingale difference term and exploit the Hoeffding lemma for martingales with bounded martingale differences. This also allowed us to derive the constants in the tail bound. In Section
4
, we establish the link with various Hoeffding-type inequalities in the literature.

Our tail bounds also feature explicit constants, whereas some results in the literature only provide the existence of constants
(see
Douc et al. 2018
, Chapter 23)
rendering them less convenient to use in practice.

4.

Applications to Learning Problems.

We provide three examples of machine learning and reinforcement learning in Section
5
, where our concentration result is used to weaken the hypotheses under which the results have been proved previously. The first example covers the well-studied generalization error bound for empirical risk minimization with non-i.i.d. samples
(
Agarwal and Duchi 2012
)
. We derive the generalization bound of empirical risk minimization with Markovian samples, where the sample-generating Markov chain converges in IPM. This result extends the result in
Agarwal and Duchi 2012
. The second example tackles the finite sample analysis of averaging of iterates of the stochastic gradient descent (SGD), extending the results in
Gupta et al. 2020
. The last example establishes the logarithmic regret bound for general state space Markovian bandit under a UCB-type algorithm, which extends the existing finite state results reported in
Anantharam et al. 1987
and
Tekin and Liu 2012
.

Remark 8

With

3.2
Relation to Dobrushin Coefficient

Dobrushin contraction coefficient was first proposed by R. Dobrushin
(
Dobrushin 1956
)
for discrete state space Markov chains. Later studies by
Del Moral et al. 2003
extended the contraction coefficient to general state space Markov chains. The traditional Dobrushin coefficient describes the contraction rate of the Markov kernel on the space of probability measures with respect to the TV metric. The formal definition can be found in Chapter 18 of
Douc et al. 2018

Rudolf and Schweizer 2018
studied the properties of the coefficient where
\textnormal{{IPM}}_{\mathbf{F}}
is the Wasserstein metric and called it the
generalized Dobrushin coefficient
of the Markov kernel.

In this section, we establish the connection between the Dobrushin coefficient and the concentrability constant. It is beneficial to the understanding of the generalized concentrability condition as it provides a way to estimate the concentrability constant under scenarios. As demonstrated later in section
5.2
, the concentrability constant of a Markov chain generated by a random operator can be estimated from the contraction coefficient of the operator.

We start by defining the

as the analog of the usual Dobrushin coefficient.

Definition 14

(IPM Dobrushin Coefficient)

The IPM Dobrushin contraction coefficient of

\displaystyle\Delta_{\mathbf{F}}(P)=\sup\{\textnormal{{IPM}}_{\mathbf{F}}(\xi P,\xi^{\prime}P):\xi,\xi^{\prime}\in\mathcal{P}(\mathcal{X}),0<\textnormal{{IPM}}_{\mathbf{F}}(\xi,\xi^{\prime})\leq 1\}.

(8)

Remark 15

One can recover the usual definition by simply setting

4
Comparison to Existing Results

In this section, we demonstrate the generalization power of our result by recovering some notable Hoeffding’s inequality for Markov chains in the recent literature. We focus our attention on the general state space setting, thus excluding results obtained under finite state space settings. According to the assumption on the Markov chains, we separate the existing Hoeffding-type inequalities into three categories: (i) uniformly ergodic chains, (ii) geometric ergodic chains, and (iii) non-irreducible chains. We start from the strongest assumption (uniform ergodicity) and move toward more relaxed assumptions. We demonstrate that our theory is a natural extension and generalizes the previous results.

4.1
Ergodic Chains

Traditionally, the ergodicity of the Markov chain is defined with respect to the
total variation metric
(TV). The TV metric can be written as IPM with
\mathbb{M}_{1}(\mathbb{X})
as its generator. For
\mu,\nu\in\mathcal{P}(\mathcal{X})
,

\displaystyle\textnormal{{TV}}(\mu,\nu)\coloneqq\sup_{E\in\mathcal{X}}|\mu(E)-\nu(E)|=\frac{1}{2}\sup_{f\in\mathbb{M}_{1}(\mathbb{X})}|\mu(f)-\nu(f)|.

(12)

The definition of the TV metric indicates that it ranges between 0 and 1, whereas IPM is between 0 and 2 by Definition
1
. We say an irreducible, aperiodic, and positive Markov kernel
P:\mathbb{X}\to\mathcal{X}
is said to be geometrically ergodic if it converges to its invariant distribution
\pi
in the following sense. For
n\in\mathbb{N}
and
x\in\mathbb{X}
, there exist
\beta\in[0,1)
and measurable function
\alpha:\mathbb{X}\to[0,\infty)
with
\pi(\alpha)<\infty
such that

\displaystyle\|P^{n}(x,\cdot)-\pi\|_{\textnormal{{TV}}}\leq\alpha(x)\beta^{n}.

(13)

The Markov kernel
P
is
uniformly geometrically ergodic
if
\sup_{x\in\mathbb{X}}\alpha(x)
is finite. Since uniform ergodicity implies geometric ergodicity
(
Douc et al. 2018
, Proposition 15.2.3)
, we refer to uniform geometric ergodicity as uniform ergodicity in the sequel.

We start our discussion with uniformly ergodic Markov chains. We provide two versions of Hoeffding inequality with this type of Markov chains in Proposition
22
and
24
. Proposition
22
assumes
Doeblin’s condition
(Assumption
1
), and Proposition
24
poses requirements on the
TV Dobrushin coefficient
. For aperiodic Markov chains, Doeblin’s condition (Assumption
1
) is equivalent to the uniform ergodicity of the Markov kernel
P

(
Meyn and Tweedie 2012
, Theorem 16.0.2)
. Thus, the latter is more general as it allows subgeometrically ergodic Markov chains. As an extension to the usual ergodic condition, Hoeffding’s inequality under
V
-
uniformly ergodicity
(Definition
26
) is studied in
Douc et al. 2011
which we present in Proposition
27
. Instead of the TV metric, the
V
-total variation metric
(Definition
25
) is used to measure the convergence. Additionally,
Miasojedow 2014
and
Fan et al. 2021
view a Markov kernel as a bounded linear operator on
L^{2}(\pi)
and study the Hoeffding-type inequalities under the
L^{2}(\pi)
-geometric ergodicity
. We summarize the result from
Fan et al. 2021
in Proposition
30
. Additionally, certain periodic Markov chains can also satisfy Doeblin’s condition. In fact, the extension of Hoeffding’s inequality to periodic Markov chains was recently studied in
Liu and Liu 2021
which we omit here as it is similar in form to
Glynn and Ormoneit 2002
.

We demonstrate in this section that the aforementioned results fit into our framework which allows easy interpolation and extension.

4.1.1
MC converging in TV Metric

In this section, we review the main result of
Glynn and Ormoneit 2002
which states a Hoeffding-type inequality for uniformly ergodic Markov chains. We start by introducing Doeblin’s condition in Assumption
1
which is the main assumption on Markov chains used by

Assumption 1

(Doeblin’s Condition)

A Markov kernel

\epsilon>n^{-1}(4\|f\|_{\infty}/\lambda)
, we have

\displaystyle\exp\bigg\{-\frac{2\{n\epsilon-[2\|f\|_{\infty}/\lambda+\texttt{span}(f)]\}^{2}}{n[2\|f\|_{\infty}/\lambda+\texttt{span}(f)]^{2}}\bigg\}\leq\exp\bigg\{-\frac{\{n\epsilon-4\|f\|_{\infty}/\lambda\}^{2}}{2n[2\|f\|_{\infty}/\lambda]^{2}}\bigg\},

(17)

and the right-hand side readily recovers the tail bound in Equation
15
. Equation
17
also shows our tail bound is tighter than
Glynn and Ormoneit 2002
, especially when
\lambda
is close to 0, i.e., when the convergence rate is slow. This discrepancy is a result of different proof techniques used to obtain the tail bounds. We refer readers to Appendix
A.1
for details.

The following result found in
Douc et al. 2018
has an alternative hypothesis that resembles ours in Theorem
21
. Indeed, it can be readily recovered by setting
\mathbf{F}=\mathbb{M}_{1}(\mathbb{X})
in Theorem
21
.

Proposition 24

(
Douc et al. 2018
)
.

Suppose the Markov chain
\{X_{n}\}_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
with transition kernel
P
admits an invariant probability measure
\pi
is uniformly ergodic. Set

\displaystyle\tilde{\Gamma}_{\textnormal{{TV}}}=\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\Delta(P^{n})<\infty.

(18)

Given
f\in\mathbb{M}_{1}(\mathbb{X})
and
\texttt{span}(f)<\infty
. Let
S_{n}=\sum_{i=0}^{n-1}f(X_{i})
. Then, for all
\mu\in\mathcal{P}(\mathcal{X})
and
\epsilon\geq n^{-1}\textnormal{{TV}}(\mu,\pi)(1+\Gamma_{\textnormal{{TV}}})\texttt{span}(f)
,

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}_{\mu}(|S_{n}-n\pi(f)|>n\epsilon)\leq 2\exp\bigg\{-\frac{2n[\epsilon-n^{-1}\textnormal{{TV}}(\mu,\pi)(1+\Gamma_{\textnormal{{TV}}})\texttt{span}(f)]^{2}}{\texttt{span}(f)^{2}(1+\Gamma_{\textnormal{{TV}}})^{2}}\bigg\}

(19)

Our proof is largely inspired by the proof of
Douc et al. 2018
, thus the constants in the exponent of our tail bound are identical to theirs after setting
\mathbf{F}=\mathbb{M}_{1}(\mathbb{X})
. By Remark
23
, the tail bound in Equation
19
is tighter than that in Equation
15
.

To conclude this section, we note that uniform ergodicity is known to be rather strict for dynamic systems. We give two simple cases where Doeblin’s condition (Assumption
1
) can easily fail. Consider a deterministic stable linear system (or an AR(1) process) on
\mathbb{X}
,
P^{m}(x,\cdot)
and
P^{m}(x^{\prime},\cdot)
are Dirac measures with disjoint supports for any
x\neq x^{\prime}\in\mathbb{X}
and
m\in\mathbb{Z}_{+}
, thus the minorization measure
\phi
does not exist in this case. Suppose
\mathbb{X}
is unbounded and the linear system is now perturbed by an additive noise supported on the entire
\mathbb{X}
(such as a Gaussian random variable). One can readily verify that for any
x\neq x^{\prime}\in\mathbb{X}
and
m\in\mathbb{Z}_{+}
the minorization measure for
P^{m}(x,\cdot)
and
P^{m}(x^{\prime},\cdot)
converges to the trivial measure as
\mathsf{d}(x,x^{\prime})\to\infty
. The exclusion of simple systems as such leaves more to be desired, which motivates us to search for a more flexible notion of convergence.

In fact, it is rather tedious to verify the uniform ergodicity for general linear and nonlinear systems. See
Meyn and Tweedie 2012
for a complete overview of the characterization of uniformly ergodic state-space models. It is also available as a summary in Section III-C in
Chen et al. 2022
.

4.1.2
MC converging in
V
-Norm

Hoeffding’s inequality for
V
-uniform ergodic Markov chains is presented in
Douc et al. 2011
and
Douc et al. 2018
. It generalizes the TV metric to
V
-total variation metric (Definition
25
), which is an IPM with
\mathbb{M}_{V}(\mathbb{X})
as its generator. The resulting Hoeffding-type inequality can be applied to
V
-geometrically ergodic Markov chains.

Definition 25

(
V
-TV Metric)
.

For probability measures
\mu,\nu\in\mathcal{P}_{V}(\mathcal{X})
and function
V:\mathbb{X}\to[1,+\infty)
,
V
-total variation metric is defined as

\displaystyle\textnormal{{IPM}}_{V}(\mu,\nu)=\sup_{f\in\mathbb{M}_{V}}\bigg|\int fd\mu-\int fd\nu\bigg|,

where
|\cdot|
denotes the absolute value.

From the definition of the V-TV metric, we can see that for
\mu,\nu\in\mathcal{P}_{V}(\mathcal{X})
,
\textnormal{{TV}}(\mu,\nu)\leq\textnormal{{IPM}}_{V}(\mu,\nu)
. Thus, convergence in
\textnormal{{IPM}}_{V}
constitutes a stronger assumption of the Markov chain than the TV metric.

Definition 26

(
V
-uniform ergodicity)
.

A Markov kernel
P
is
V
-uniform ergodic if it possesses an invariant distribution
\pi
and there exists constant
R<\infty
and
\beta\in[0,1)
such that

\displaystyle\textnormal{{IPM}}_{V}(P^{n}(x,\cdot),\pi)\leq RV(x)\beta^{n},

(20)

for
\forall x\in\mathbb{X},\ \forall n\in\mathbb{N}
.

Proposition 27

(
Douc et al. 2011
)
.

Suppose the Markov chain
\{X_{n}\}_{n\geq 0}
is
V
-uniform ergodic with unique invariant probability distribution
\pi
and initial distribution
\eta
. Let
f:\mathbb{X}\to\mathbb{R}
be a measurable function with
\|f\|_{\infty}<\infty
and
S_{n}=\sum_{i=0}^{n-1}f(X_{i})
. Then, for any
\epsilon>0
and initial distribution
\eta
, there exists a constant
K>0
such that

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}_{\eta}(|S_{n}-n\pi(f)|>n\epsilon)\leq K\mathbb{E}_{\eta}[V]\exp\left[-\frac{1}{K}\left(\frac{n\epsilon^{2}}{\|f\|^{2}_{\infty}}\right)\wedge\frac{n\epsilon}{\|f\|_{\infty}}\right],

(21)

where
V
is defined in Definition
25
.

Remark 28
.

This tail bound is a hybrid between a Bernstein-type inequality and a Hoeffding-type inequality. In the regime where
n\to\infty
, the exponential term would be
\exp\left[-\frac{1}{K}\left(\frac{n\epsilon^{2}}{\|f\|^{2}_{\infty}}\right)\right]
which behaves more like a Hoeffding-type inequality. Although in this case, the generator of
\textnormal{{IPM}}_{V}
is
\mathbb{M}_{V}(\mathbb{X})
, the functions applied to the Markov chain still belong to
\mathbb{M}_{1}(\mathbb{X})
which is a subset of
\mathbb{M}_{V}(\mathbb{X})
for general choices of
V
other than
V\equiv 1
.
\textnormal{{IPM}}_{V}
chosen here is unnecessarily strong for the task. In fact, if one only considers the bounded functions of Markov chains, it suffices to use an IPM as strong as the TV metric.

4.1.3
MC converging in
L^{2}(\pi)

In this section, we review a Hoeffding-type inequality obtained with spectral methods for Markov chains. Markov transition kernels can be thought of as a bounded linear operator on a functional space, such as
L^{2}(\pi)=\{f\in\mathbb{M}(\mathbb{X}):\|f\|_{L^{2}(\pi)}<\infty\}
. One can study the convergence behavior of Markov chains by looking at the spectrum of the corresponding operator. For a comprehensive overview of spectral methods for Markov chains, we advise interested readers to look at
Douc et al. 2018
. We only introduce the necessary facts to facilitate our discussion in the sequel.

We start by introducing the definition of
L^{2}(\pi)
-absolute spectral gap. Denote
\{f\in L^{2}(\pi):\pi(f)=0\}
as
L^{2}_{0}(\pi)
.

Definition 29

(
Douc et al. 2018
)
.

Let
P
be a Markov transition kernel with invariant probability
\pi
. The transition kernel
P
has an
L^{2}(\pi)
-absolute spectral gap
if

\displaystyle\textnormal{{Abs.Gap}}_{L^{2}(\pi)}(P)\coloneqq 1-\sup\{|\lambda|:\lambda\in\textnormal{{Spec}}(P|L^{2}_{0}(\pi))\}>0,

(22)

where
\sup\{|\lambda|:\lambda\in\textnormal{{Spec}}(P|L^{2}_{0}(\pi))\}=\lim_{m\to\infty}\sup\{\|P^{m}g\|_{L^{2}(\pi)}:g\in L^{2}_{0}(\pi),\|g\|_{L^{2}(\pi)}\leq 1\}^{1/m}
.

Under the assumption that
P
has a positive absolute spectral gap,
Fan et al. 2021
obtains a Hoeffding-type for bounded functions. We repeat their main result in Proposition
30
for completeness.

Proposition 30

(
Fan et al. 2021
)
.

Let
\{X_{n}\}_{n\geq 0}
be a Markov chain with invariant measure
\pi
and absolute spectral gap
1-\lambda>0
. For any
r\in\mathbb{R}
, uniformly for all bounded functions
f_{i}:\mathbb{X}\to[a_{i},b_{i}]
, we have

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{\pi}\bigg[e^{r[\sum_{i=1}^{n}f_{i}(X_{i})-\sum_{i=1}^{n}\pi(f_{i})]}\bigg]\leq\exp\bigg(\frac{1+\lambda}{1-\lambda}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\frac{(b_{i}-a_{i})^{2}r^{2}}{8}\bigg).

(23)

It follows that for any
\epsilon>0
,

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}_{\pi}\bigg(\sum_{i=1}^{n}f_{i}(X_{i})-\sum_{i=1}^{n}\pi(f_{i})>n\epsilon\bigg)\leq\exp\bigg(-\frac{1-\lambda}{1+\lambda}\cdot\frac{2(n\epsilon)^{2}}{\sum_{i=1}^{n}(b_{i}-a_{i})^{2}}\bigg)

(24)

Remark 31
.

Notice that the initial distribution is required to be
\pi
. The authors discussed it could be extended to all probability measures with
{L^{2}(\pi)}
Radon-Nikodym derivative against
\pi
. In contrast, our framework and results mentioned earlier do not have this restriction on the initial distribution.

To draw the connection with our framework, we need to further understand the implications of the absolute spectral gap. By Proposition 22.2.4 in
Douc et al. 2018
, having an
L^{2}(\pi)
-absolute spectral gap implies being
L^{2}(\pi)
geometrically ergodic which is defined formally as follows. Denote the stationary transition kernel as
\Lambda_{\pi}(x,\cdot)\coloneqq\pi(\cdot)
and the unit ball in
L^{2}(\pi)
as
\mathbf{F}_{L^{2}}\coloneqq\{f\in L^{2}(\pi):\|f\|_{L^{2}(\pi)}\leq 1\}
. With an abuse of notation, we write the
L^{2}(\pi)
-norm for a probability measure as
\|\mu\|_{L^{2}(\pi)}\coloneqq\|d\mu/d\pi\|_{L^{2}(\pi)}
whenever
\mu
in dominated by
\pi
and
\|\mu\|_{L^{2}(\pi)}=\infty
otherwise. Denote the space of probability measures with finite
{L^{2}(\pi)}
-norm as
\mathcal{P}_{L^{2}(\pi)}(\mathcal{X})\coloneqq\{\nu\in\mathcal{P}(\mathcal{X}):\|\nu\|_{L^{2}(\pi)}<\infty\}
.

Definition 32

(
Douc et al. 2018
)
.

P
is said to be
L^{2}(\pi)
-
geometrically ergodic
if there exist
\beta\in[0,1)
, a constant
\tilde{\alpha}(\mu)<\infty
, and for all
\mu\in\mathcal{P}_{L^{2}(\pi)}(\mathcal{X})
satisfying

\displaystyle\|\mu P^{n}-\pi\|_{L^{2}(\pi)}\leq\tilde{\alpha}(\mu)\beta^{n},

(25)

for
n\in\mathbb{N}
.

Let us consider the
L^{2}(\pi)
-IPM denoted as
\textnormal{{IPM}}_{L^{2}(\pi)}
with generator
\mathbf{F}_{L^{2}}
. A geometric convergence in
\textnormal{{IPM}}_{L^{2}(\pi)}
can be analogously defined as follows.

Definition 33
.

P
is said to be
geometrically convergent in
\textnormal{{IPM}}_{L^{2}(\pi)}
if there exist
\beta\in[0,1)
, a constant
\alpha(\mu)<\infty
, and for all
\mu\in\mathcal{P}_{L^{2}(\pi)}(\mathcal{X})
satisfying

\displaystyle\textnormal{{IPM}}_{L^{2}(\pi)}(\mu P^{n},\pi)=\sup_{f\in\mathbf{F}_{L^{2}(\pi)}}|\mu P^{n}f-\pi(f)|\leq\alpha(\mu)\beta^{n},

(26)

for
n\in\mathbb{N}
.

The following lemma establishes the equivalence between a geometric convergence in
\textnormal{{IPM}}_{L^{2}(\pi)}
and
L^{2}(\pi)

Lemma 34
.

Suppose
P
has invariant probability
\pi

1.

P
is
L^{2}(\pi)
-geometrically ergodic;

2.

P
is geometrically convergent in
\textnormal{{IPM}}_{L^{2}(\pi)}
.

Proof:

Let
\mu\in\mathcal{P}_{L^{2}(\pi)}(\mathcal{X})
be the initial distribution. Let
g_{\mu}=d\mu/d\pi
be the Radon–Nikodym derivative and
g_{\mu}\in L^{2}(\pi)
. We write the inner product between
\mu
and a real-valued function
f
in
L^{2}(\pi)
as
\langle\mu,f\rangle=\int fd\mu=\int fg_{\mu}d\pi=\langle g_{\mu},f\rangle_{{L^{2}(\pi)}}
.

It is easy to show that
\|\mu P\|_{L^{2}(\pi)}\leq\|\mu\|_{L^{2}(\pi)}

(
Douc et al. 2018
, Lemma 22.1.3)
. Thus, it follows
\mu P^{n}
is dominated by
\pi
and
\|\mu P^{n}\|_{L^{2}(\pi)}\leq\|\mu\|_{L^{2}(\pi)}
for

. We can write
\textnormal{{IPM}}_{L^{2}(\pi)}
using the inner product form,

\displaystyle\textnormal{{IPM}}_{L^{2}(\pi)}(\mu P^{n},\pi)

\displaystyle=\sup_{f\in\mathbf{F}_{L^{2}(\pi)}}|\langle\mu P^{n},f\rangle-\langle\pi,f\rangle|=\sup_{f\in\mathbf{F}_{L^{2}(\pi)}}|\langle\mu P^{n}-\pi,f\rangle|

\displaystyle=\|\mu P^{n}-\pi\|_{L^{2}(\pi)},

(27)

where the last equality is due to Cauchy–Schwarz inequality and the equality can be obtained with an appropriate choice of

\mathbf{F}_{L^{2}(\pi)}

It is obvious that the geometric convergence in
\textnormal{{IPM}}_{L^{2}(\pi)}

\displaystyle\Gamma_{L^{2}(\pi)}(\mu)\coloneqq\sum_{i=1}^{\infty}\textnormal{{IPM}}_{L^{2}(\pi)}(\mu P^{i},\pi)<\infty,

(28)

for
\mu\in\mathcal{P}_{L^{2}(\pi)}(\mathcal{X})
. Equation

depends on the initial distribution
\mu
, thus being less demanding than Equation

used in Theorem

which requires a uniform property across the state space
\mathbb{X}
. Naturally, one would be skeptical if geometric convergence in
\textnormal{{IPM}}_{L^{2}(\pi)}
leads to the same conclusion as in Theorem

where the initial distribution is arbitrary. In the rest of this section, however, we demonstrate a similar consequence (with restricted initial distribution) to that of Proposition
30
using Equation

. From previous discussions, we can deduce

\textnormal{{Abs.Gap}}_{L^{2}(\pi)}(P)>0
, which implies our framework generalizes that of
Fan et al. 2021
.

\displaystyle\Delta_{L^{2}(\pi)}(P)\coloneqq\sup\bigg\{\textnormal{{IPM}}_{L^{2}(\pi)}(\xi P,\xi^{\prime}P):\xi\neq\xi^{\prime}\in\mathcal{P}_{L^{2}(\pi)}(\mathcal{X}),\textnormal{{IPM}}_{L^{2}(\pi)}(\xi,\xi^{\prime})\leq 1\bigg\}.

We draw the connection between

and the operator norm of

Lemma 35
.

Let
\vvvert P\vvvert_{L^{2}_{0}(\pi)}\coloneqq\sup_{f\in L^{2}_{0}(\pi)}\frac{\|Pf\|_{L^{2}(\pi)}}{\|f\|_{L^{2}(\pi)}}
denote the operator norm on

.

Proof:

Using Equation

\displaystyle\Delta_{L^{2}(\pi)}(P)=\sup\bigg\{\|(\xi-\xi^{\prime})P\|_{L^{2}(\pi)}:\xi\neq\xi^{\prime}\in\mathcal{P}_{L^{2}(\pi)}(\mathcal{X}),\|\xi-\xi^{\prime}\|_{L^{2}(\pi)}\leq 1\bigg\}.

Since
\xi
and
\xi^{\prime}
are arbitrary positive measures in
\mathcal{P}_{L^{2}(\pi)}(\mathcal{X})
,
\xi-\xi^{\prime}
can be any signed measure in
\mathcal{P}_{L^{2}(\pi)}(\mathcal{X})
and

. Let

be the adjoint operator of

\langle Pf,g\rangle_{L^{2}(\pi)}=\langle f,P^{*}g\rangle_{L^{2}(\pi)}

\displaystyle\sup\bigg\{\|(\xi-\xi^{\prime})P\|_{L^{2}(\pi)}:\xi\neq\xi^{\prime}\in\mathcal{P}_{L^{2}(\pi)}(\mathcal{X}),\|\xi-\xi^{\prime}\|_{L^{2}(\pi)}\leq 1\bigg\}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sup\bigg\{\bigg\|P^{*}\frac{d\xi-d\xi^{\prime}}{d\pi}\bigg\|_{L^{2}(\pi)}:\xi\neq\xi^{\prime}\in\mathcal{P}_{L^{2}(\pi)}(\mathcal{X}),\bigg\|\frac{d(\xi-\xi^{\prime})}{d\pi}\bigg\|_{L^{2}(\pi)}\leq 1\bigg\}

where the last equality is a result of combining Theorem 22.1.8 and Lemma 22.2.1 from

Combining

4.2
Non-Ergodic MC

In this section, we discuss the Markov chains that are not covered by the aforementioned ergodicity conditions, namely, non-irreducible chains and periodic chains.

4.2.1
MC converging in Wasserstein metric

Hoeffding’s inequality for Markov chains converging in the Wasserstein metric is studied in
Sandrić and Šebek 2023
which we review in this section. Their hypothesis includes certain non-irreducible chains.
From the definition of the small set and Doeblin’s condition, we can see that they enforce the probability measure
P(x,\cdot)
to be non-singular for
x
in at least some subset of the state space
\mathbb{X}
. Since small sets and Doeblin’s condition play a central role in the ergodicity of Markov chains, one can make the educated guess that the ergodicity may fail when
P(x,\cdot)
is singular. In fact, the real problem here is caused by the TV metric. For example, if we consider two Dirac measures
\delta_{0}
and
\delta_{1/n}
for
n\in\mathbb{Z}_{+}
, it is easy to see that
\delta_{1/n}
does not converge to
\delta_{0}
in TV metric as
\textnormal{{TV}}(\delta_{1/n},\delta_{0})=1
for all
n\in\mathbb{Z}_{+}
. Nevertheless,
\delta_{1/n}\to\delta_{0}
in some weaker sense which is to be specified later. This “incompatibility" with singular measures raises problems when one tries to apply Markov chain theory to deterministic systems or systems with discrete noise. The solution to the issue is to consider weaker metrics that induce coarser topologies in the space of probability measures. One of the notable alternatives researchers have considered is the
Wasserstein metric
. For Polish space
(\mathbb{X},\mathcal{X},\mathsf{d})
, define the Wasserstein-(
p,\mathsf{d}
) metric on
\mathcal{P}(\mathcal{X})
as

\displaystyle\textnormal{{W}}_{p,\mathsf{d}}(\mu,\nu)\coloneqq\bigg[\inf_{\Pi\in\mathcal{C}(\mu,\nu)}\int_{\mathbb{X}\times\mathbb{X}}\mathsf{d}(x,y)^{p}\Pi(dx,dy)\bigg]^{1/p},

(31)

where
\mathcal{C}(\mu,\nu)
denotes the family of couplings of
\mu
and
\nu
. For Wasserstein-1 metric in particular (
p=1
), it holds, by Kantorovich-Rubinstein duality theorem
Dudley 2018
, that

\displaystyle\textnormal{{W}}_{1,\mathsf{d}}(\mu,\nu)=\sup_{\{f:\textnormal{{Lip}}(f)\leq 1\}}\bigg|\int_{\mathbb{X}}fd\mu-\int_{\mathbb{X}}fd\nu\bigg|,

(32)

whenever the integrals are well-defined. Note that the presence of metric
\mathsf{d}
is contained in the Lipschitz condition of the witness function
f
, i.e.,
|f(x)-f(y)|\leq\textnormal{{Lip}}(f)\mathsf{d}(x,y)
, for
x,y\in\mathbb{X}
. Sufficient conditions for geometric and subgeometric convergence for Markov chains in Wasserstein-1 metric have been established in
Hairer et al. 2011
and
Butkovsky 2014
, respectively. We borrow the following example from
Butkovsky 2014
to illustrate the necessity of using the Wasserstein metric.

Example 38

(
Butkovsky 2014
)
.

Let
\boldsymbol{X}=\{X_{n}\}_{n\geq 0}
be an autoregressive process satisfying the following equation:

\displaystyle X_{n+1}=\frac{1}{10}X_{n}+\epsilon_{n+1},

(33)

where
\epsilon_{1},\epsilon_{2},\cdots
are i.i.d. random variables uniformly distributed on the set
\{0,\frac{1}{10},\frac{2}{10},\cdots,\frac{9}{10}\}
and independent of
X_{0}
.
X_{0}
is uniformly distributed on
[0,1)
. Then,
\boldsymbol{X}
is a Markov chain with state space
(\mathbb{X},\mathcal{X})=([0,1),\mathcal{B}([0,1)))
with a unique invariant measure
\pi
which is the uniform distribution on
[0,1)
. Let
d(x,y)=|x-y|
be the metric on
\mathbb{X}
. One can show
(
Butkovsky 2014
)
the following facts:

1.

\boldsymbol{X}
converge to
\pi
in Wasserstien-(1,
\mathsf{d}
) metric and
\textnormal{{W}}_{1,\mathsf{d}}(P^{n}(x,\cdot),\pi(\cdot))\leq 10^{-n}
.

2.

\textnormal{{TV}}(P^{n}(x,\cdot),\pi(\cdot))=1
for
\forall n\in\mathbb{N}
.

3.

\boldsymbol{X}
is not
\psi
-irreducible. Consider
\mathbb{Q}\cap[0,1)
and
\mathbb{Q}^{c}\cap[0,1)
which are disjoint and absorbing.

Since
\boldsymbol{X}
is not ergodic in the usual sense, Proposition
22
,
24
,
27
, and
30
cannot be used.

Along the same line, one can deduce that systems with stable dynamics and additive discrete noise are excluded by the usual ergodicity condition as well. To bridge this gap,
Sandrić and Šebek 2023
has made a step in this direction by giving a version of Hoeffding’s inequality for non-irreducible Markov chains. We copy their theorem below for completeness.

Proposition 39

(
Sandrić and Šebek 2023
)
.

Let
f:\mathbb{X}\to\mathbb{R}
be bounded and Lipschitz continuous, and let
S_{n}\coloneqq\sum_{i=0}^{n-1}f(X_{i})
for
n\in\mathbb{Z}_{+}
. Assume that
\{X_{n}\}_{n\geq 0}
admits an invariant probability measure
\pi(dx)
such that

\displaystyle\Gamma_{\textnormal{{W}}}\coloneqq\sup_{x\in\mathbb{X}}\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\textnormal{{W}}(P^{n}(x,\cdot),\pi(\cdot))<\infty

(34)

Then for any
\epsilon>2n^{-1}\textnormal{{Lip}}(f)\Gamma_{\textnormal{{W}}}
,

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}_{x}[|S_{n}-n\pi(f)|>n\epsilon]\leq 2\exp\bigg\{\frac{-(n\epsilon-2\textnormal{{Lip}}(f)\Gamma_{\textnormal{{W}}})^{2}}{8n(\textnormal{{Lip}}(f)\Gamma_{\textnormal{{W}}}+\|f\|_{\infty})^{2}}\bigg\}

(35)

Remark 40
.

The term
\textnormal{{Lip}}(f)\Gamma_{\textnormal{{W}}}+\|f\|_{\infty}
in the denominator is not squared in the original statement of
Sandrić and Šebek 2023
. This term should be squared when
Devroye et al. 2013
is applied but it is missing in their original proof.

With our framework, we present the following corollary which achieves the same concentration result as in Proposition
39
but under a slightly more general condition. We notice that Proposition
39
considers bounded Lipschitz functions applied to the Markov chain, which indicates that the convergence in the Wasserstein-1 metric is stronger than needed. As pointed out in Remark
10
and
28
, the generator of the IPM should be large enough to cover the function of interests and anything more is unnecessary. In this case, the appropriate IPM generator is bounded Lipschitz functions which is smaller than that of Wasserstein-1 metric, resulting in a weaker convergence requirement. Let
\|\cdot\|_{\textnormal{{BL}}}
denote the bounded Lipschitz norm and is given by

\displaystyle\|f\|_{\textnormal{{BL}}}=\max\bigg\{\|f\|_{\infty},\textnormal{{Lip}}(f)\bigg\}.

Consider the generalized concentrability condition defined with the generator
\mathbf{F}_{\textnormal{{BL}}}=\{f\in\mathbb{M}(\mathbb{X}):\|f\|_{\textnormal{{BL}}}\leq 1\}
. Let the IPM generated by
\mathbf{F}_{\textnormal{{BL}}}
be denoted as
BL
. We define
\Gamma_{\textnormal{{BL}}}
using
BL
in a similar fashion as Equation
5
. The following corollary is given under the condition
\Gamma_{\textnormal{{BL}}}<\infty
.

Corollary 41
.

Let
\boldsymbol{X}=\{X_{n}\}_{n\in\mathbb{N}}\subset\mathbb{X}
be a Markov chain with transition kernel
P:\mathbb{X}\times\mathcal{X}
. Assume
P
admits a unique invariant probability measure
\pi
such that

\displaystyle\Gamma_{\textnormal{{BL}}}\coloneqq\sup_{x\in\mathbb{X}}\sum_{i=0}^{\infty}\textnormal{{BL}}(P^{i}(x,\cdot),\pi)<\infty.

(36)

Suppose
\boldsymbol{X}
initializes with
\mu\in\mathcal{P}(\mathcal{X})
. Consider a bounded Lipschitz continuous function
f:\mathbb{X}\to\mathbb{R}
and
S_{n}=\sum_{i=0}^{n-1}f(X_{i})
for
n\in\mathbb{Z}_{+}
. Then, for
\epsilon>(2n)^{-1}\textnormal{{BL}}(\mu,\pi)[2\|f\|_{\textnormal{{BL}}}\Gamma_{\textnormal{{BL}}}+\texttt{span}(f)]
, we have

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}_{\mu}\bigg[\bigg|S_{n}-n\pi(f)\bigg|>n\epsilon\bigg]\leq 2\exp\bigg(-\frac{2[n\epsilon-\textnormal{{BL}}(\mu,\pi)[2\|f\|_{\textnormal{{BL}}}\Gamma_{\textnormal{{BL}}}+\texttt{span}(f)]/2]^{2}}{n[2\|f\|_{\textnormal{{BL}}}\Gamma_{\textnormal{{BL}}}+\texttt{span}(f)]^{2}}\bigg).

(37)

Proof:

This is a restatement of the main result using
\mathbf{F}_{\textnormal{{BL}}}
.

Remark 42
.

For any bounded Lipschitz function
f
, let
M
be the minimal stretch of
\mathbf{F}_{\textnormal{{BL}}}
that includes
f
. Then,
M=\|f\|_{\textnormal{{BL}}}
and
f\in\|f\|_{\textnormal{{BL}}}\cdot\Gamma_{\textnormal{{BL}}}
.

We apply Corollary
41
to obtain the sample complexity of Ployak-Ruppert averaging of SGD in Section
5.2
. The function of interest there is the identity function on a compact space. Thus, it can be treated as a bounded Lipschitz function.

4.2.2
Periodic Chains

A version of Hoefdding’s inequality for periodic Markov chains is given in
Liu and Liu 2021
. The work is motivated by the observation that Markov chains satisfying Doeblin’s condition in Assumption
1
are not necessarily aperiodic, which indicates the uniform ergodicity condition used in
Glynn and Ormoneit 2002
can be relaxed. Since the key step in
Glynn and Ormoneit 2002
is to upper-bound the solution of the Poisson equation which is obtained via Doeblin’s condition. The authors in
Liu and Liu 2021
refine this argument by directly bounding the solution of the Poisson equation in terms of the drift condition and the ergodicity coefficient. We skip their results as it is similar in form as
Glynn and Ormoneit 2002
.

Applications

In this section, we provide three application examples in machine learning to demonstrate the utility of our theory.

5.1
Generalization Error Bound for Non-IID Samples

The assumption of i.i.d. samples is prevalent in machine learning literature. In practice, the samples may not be independent. To relax the i.i.d. assumption, samples drawn from mixing processes have been considered from the perspective of empirical process theory
(
Yu 1994
)
, time series prediction
(
Alquier and Wintenberger 2012
)
, online learning
(
Agarwal and Duchi 2012
)
, empirical risk minimization (ERM)
(
Zou et al. 2009
)
, etc. Stationary processes are considered in the aforementioned papers except for
Agarwal and Duchi 2012
which considers asymptotically mixing processes, i.e. converging to stationary distribution in a suitable sense.

In this section, we study the generalization error for online learning algorithms with samples drawn from a Markov chain converging in general IPM. Consider the following stochastic optimization problem

\displaystyle\minimize_{\theta\in\Theta}F(\theta)\coloneqq\mathbb{E}[f(u;\theta)],

(38)

where
f:\mathcal{U}\times\Theta\to\mathbb{R}
is the loss function depending on the random sample
u\in\mathcal{U}
and parameter
\theta\in\Theta
, and the expectation is taken with respect to the distribution of
u
. Before moving forward, we state the following assumptions.

Assumption 2
.

1.

f
is uniformly bounded on
\mathcal{U}\times\Theta
.

2.

f
is strongly convex in
\theta
.

In practice, the objective in Equation
38
is approximated with the empirical loss estimated from data. Suppose we have
N
samples
\{u_{i}\}_{i=1}^{N}\subset\mathcal{U}
. The empirical loss in Equation
38
is written as

\displaystyle\minimize_{\theta\in\Theta}\hat{F}(\theta)\coloneqq\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}f(u_{i};\theta).

(39)

Under appropriate assumption on
f
, both the original problem in Equation
38
and the approximated problem in Equation
39
have unique solutions, which are denoted as
\theta^{*}
and
\hat{\theta}
respectively. In general, the optimal solution
\theta^{*}
and approximated solution
\hat{\theta}
differ, and one would like to bound the performance degradation caused by the approximated solution. That is, finding an upper bound for
|F(\hat{\theta})-F(\theta^{*})|
which is referred to as the generalization error bound. When the samples are i.i.d., the problem can be solved readily using Hoeffding’s inequality for independent random variables
(
Hoeffding 1994
)
. Similarly, if we assume the samples
\{u_{i}\}_{i=1}^{N}
follow a Markov chain converging in some sense to be specified, then the generalization error bound can be obtained by applying our version of Hoeffding’s inequality for Markov chains.

Theorem 43
.

Suppose Assumption
2
holds and
f\in M\cdot\mathbf{F}
for
M\in(0,\infty)
and
\mathbf{F}\subset\mathbb{M}_{1}
. Let
\{u_{i}\}_{i=1}^{\infty}
be a Markov chain converging in
\textnormal{{IPM}}_{\mathbf{F}}
with constant
\Gamma_{\mathbf{F}}
. For
\delta\in(0,1)
, the following holds with probability at least
1-\delta

\displaystyle F(\hat{\theta})\leq F(\theta^{*})+\frac{4M\Gamma_{\mathbf{F}}+2\texttt{span}(f)}{N}\bigg(1+\sqrt{\frac{N}{2}\log(\frac{1}{\delta})}\bigg).

(40)

Proof:

Since
\mathbf{F}\subset\mathbb{M}_{1}
, we can apply Theorem
21
to
F(\hat{\theta})-\hat{F}(\hat{\theta})
,

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\{F(\hat{\theta})-\hat{F}(\hat{\theta})<\epsilon\}=

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\bigg\{\mathbb{E}_{\pi}f(u;\hat{\theta})-\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}f(u_{i};\hat{\theta})<\epsilon\bigg\}

(41)

\displaystyle\geq

\displaystyle 1-\exp\bigg\{-\frac{2\{N\epsilon-[2M\Gamma_{\mathbf{F}}+\texttt{span}(f)]\}^{2}}{N[2M\Gamma_{\mathbf{F}}+\texttt{span}(f)]^{2}}\bigg\}.

(42)

With probability at least
1-\delta
, we have

\displaystyle F(\hat{\theta})

\displaystyle\leq\hat{F}(\hat{\theta})+\frac{2M\Gamma_{\mathbf{F}}+\texttt{span}(f)}{N}\bigg(1+\sqrt{\frac{N}{2}\log(\frac{1}{\delta})}\bigg)

\displaystyle\leq\hat{F}(\theta^{*})+\frac{2M\Gamma_{\mathbf{F}}+\texttt{span}(f)}{N}\bigg(1+\sqrt{\frac{N}{2}\log(\frac{1}{\delta})}\bigg),

(43)

where the last inequality is due to
\theta^{*}
being the minimizer of
\hat{F}
. Applying Theorem
21
again to
\hat{F}(\theta^{*})-F(\theta^{*})
yields

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\{\hat{F}(\theta^{*})-F(\theta^{*})<\epsilon\}=

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\bigg\{\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}f(u_{i};\theta^{*})-\mathbb{E}_{\pi}f(u;\theta^{*})<\epsilon\bigg\}

(44)

\displaystyle\geq

\displaystyle 1-\exp\bigg\{-\frac{2\{N\epsilon-[2M\Gamma_{\mathbf{F}}+\texttt{span}(f)]\}^{2}}{N[2M\Gamma_{\mathbf{F}}+\texttt{span}(f)]^{2}}\bigg\}.

(45)

With probability at least
1-\delta
, we have

\displaystyle\hat{F}(\theta^{*})\leq F(\theta^{*})+\frac{2M\Gamma_{\mathbf{F}}+\texttt{span}(f)}{N}\bigg(1+\sqrt{\frac{N}{2}\log(\frac{1}{\delta})}\bigg).

(46)

Combine Equation
43
and Equation
46
, we have, with probability at least
1-\delta
,

\displaystyle F(\hat{\theta})\leq F(\theta^{*})+\frac{4M\Gamma_{\mathbf{F}}+2\texttt{span}(f)}{N}\bigg(1+\sqrt{\frac{N}{2}\log(\frac{1}{\delta})}\bigg).

(47)

5.2
Ployak-Ruppert averaging for SGD

Stochastic recursive algorithms (RSA), including SGD, empirical value iteration (EVI), etc., are widely used in optimization, stochastic approximation, and machine learning. In
Gupta et al. 2020
, the authors model the general form of RSAs with constant step size as a Markov chain and study its convergence properties. In particular, the time average of the RSA iterates is shown to have the variance reduction property given the Markov chain induced by the RSA converges weakly to its invariant distribution
(
Gupta et al. 2020
)
. This result aligns well with the common acceleration technique used in SGD and stochastic approximation (SA), namely Ployak-Ruppert averaging
(
Polyak and Juditsky 1992
;
Ruppert 1988
;
Nemirovski et al. 2009
, see)
. In the following example, we complement the existing study by demonstrating a
finite sample
analysis of the Ployak-Ruppert averaging of the SGD algorithm. For
finite time
convergence guarantees are derived for SGD-based algorithms with constant step size, please refer to
Dieuleveut et al. 2017
;
Bach and Moulines 2013
;
Bach 2014
.

We first introduce the necessary theoretic background on stochastic contractive operators, then dive into a case study of the empirical risk minimization (ERM) via SGD. Let us start with the contractive property for deterministic operators on complete separable metric spaces. Let the Polish space
\mathbb{X}
be equipped with metric
\mathsf{d}:\mathbb{X}\times\mathbb{X}\to[0,\infty)
. Let
T:\mathbb{X}\to\mathbb{X}
be a contraction operator with coefficient
\alpha\in(0,1)
and the unique fixed point
x^{*}\in\mathbb{X}
, i.e.,

\displaystyle\mathsf{d}(T(x_{1}),T(x_{2}))\leq\alpha\mathsf{d}(x_{1},x_{2}),

(48)

for all
x_{1},x_{2}\in\mathbb{X}
, and
T(x^{*})=x^{*}
. Given any starting point
x_{0}\in\mathbb{X}
, recursively applying the operator
T
generates a sequence
(x_{k})_{k\in\mathbb{N}}
where
x_{k+1}=T(x_{k})
for
k\in\mathbb{N}
. By Banach contraction mapping theorem
Granas and Dugundji 2003
,
\mathsf{d}(x_{k},x^{*})\leq\alpha^{k}\mathsf{d}(x_{0},x^{*})
, thus
x_{k}\to x^{*}
geometrically fast as
k\to\infty
.

For stochastic operators, we need a few more notations. Let
(\Omega,\mathcal{F},\mathbb{P})
be a probability space where
\Omega
is the set of uncertainties,
\mathcal{F}
is the Borel
\sigma
-algebra over
\Omega
, and
\mathbb{P}
is the base probability measure. Let
\hat{T}^{n}_{k}:\mathbb{X}\times\Omega\to\mathbb{X}
be a random operator used at
k
-th iteration and indexed by
n\in\mathbb{N}
. This random operator is used to model the approximation of the true operator
T
carried out by RSAs. In the case of SGD,
n
is the number of samples (batch size) used to estimate the gradient. In EVI,
n
is the number of samples used to estimate the expected cost-to-go. For simplicity of the presentation, we suppress the dependence of
\hat{T}^{n}_{k}
on
\omega\in\Omega
and we write
\hat{T}^{n}_{k}(x)\coloneqq\hat{T}^{n}_{k}(x,\omega)
. For each
x\in\mathbb{X}
,
\hat{T}^{n}_{k}(x)
is a random variable, and we make the following independence assumption for random operators at a different time step.

Assumption 3

(
Gupta et al. 2020
)
.

For each
x\in\mathbb{X}
,
\hat{T}^{n}_{k}(x)
and
\hat{T}^{n}_{k^{\prime}}(x)
are independent and identically distributed (i.i.d.) for
k\neq k^{\prime}
.

Assumption 4

(
Diaconis and Freedman 1999
)
.

The following conditions are satisfied:

1.

\mathbb{X}
is a Polish space.

2.

There exists
a,b>0
such that the operator
\hat{T}^{n}_{k}
satisfy

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\bigg\{\mathsf{d}\bigg(\hat{T}^{n}_{k}(z^{*}),z^{*}\bigg)>\epsilon\bigg\}\leq\frac{a}{\epsilon^{b}},

(49)

for all
\epsilon>0
and
k\in\mathbb{N}
.

3.

Let
\hat{\alpha}^{n}_{k}
denote the Lipschitz coefficient of
\hat{T}_{k}^{n}
. Then, for all
k\in\mathbb{N}
,

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[\hat{\alpha}^{n}_{k}]<\infty,\quad\mathbb{E}[\log(\hat{\alpha}^{n}_{k})]<0.

(50)

Lemma 44

(
Diaconis and Freedman 1999
)
.

Suppose that Assumption
3
and
4
hold, then there exists a unique invariant probability measure
\pi_{n}
such that the distribution of
\hat{z}^{n}_{k}
converges to
\pi_{n}
in weak* topology.

Now, we look at a concrete machine learning example. Consider an empirical risk minimization problem with the loss function
l:\mathcal{U}\times\Theta\to\mathbb{R}
, where
\mathcal{U}\subset\mathbb{R}^{d_{s}}
is the sample space ,
\Theta\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{d_{p}}
is the parameter space. Suppose
\Theta
is convex, equipped with the Euclidean metric
\|\cdot\|_{2}
, and has a finite diameter. Let
\mathcal{D}=\{u_{i}\}_{i=1}^{N}\subset\mathcal{U}
be the training data set with i.i.d. samples. Let
l(u_{i},\theta)
be the loss associated with sample
u_{i}
when the parameter is
\theta
. We formulate the problem as follows:

\displaystyle\minimize_{\theta\in\Theta}F_{\mathcal{D}}(\theta)\coloneqq\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}l(u_{i},\theta).

(51)

We make the following assumptions before moving forward.

Assumption 5
.

Suppose the following conditions hold:

1.

l(u,\cdot)
is twice-differentiable almost everywhere, for all
u\in\mathcal{U}
.

2.

\nabla_{\theta}l(u,\cdot)
is Lipschitz continuous with constant
L>0
for all
u\in\mathcal{U}
, i.e.,

\displaystyle\|\nabla_{\theta}l(u,\theta)-\nabla_{\theta}l(u,\theta^{\prime})\|_{2}\leq L\|\theta-\theta^{\prime}\|_{2},\quad\text{for }\forall\theta,\theta^{\prime}\in\Theta.

3.

l(u,\cdot)
is strongly convex with modulus
\eta\in(0,L)
for all
u\in\mathcal{U}
, i.e.,

\displaystyle\langle\nabla_{\theta}l(u,\theta)-\nabla_{\theta}l(u,\theta^{\prime}),\theta-\theta^{\prime}\rangle\geq\eta\|\theta-\theta^{\prime}\|_{2}^{2},\quad\text{for }\forall\theta,\theta^{\prime}\in\Theta.

4.

\nabla_{\theta}l(u,\theta)
is uniformly bounded function of
u\in\mathcal{U}
for all
\theta\in\Theta
.

5.

\Theta\subset\mathbb{R}^{d_{p}}
is compact.

As an immediate result, the problem in Equation
51
has a unique minimizer
\theta^{*}\in\Theta
and can be readily solved by the projected gradient descent algorithm. The gradient of
F_{\mathcal{D}}
is given by

\displaystyle\nabla_{\theta}F_{\mathcal{D}}(\theta)=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\nabla_{\theta}l(u_{i},\theta).

The usual projected gradient descent algorithm approximates
\theta^{*}
by performing the following iterative operation
T:\Theta\to\Theta
. Given an initial point
\theta_{0}\in\Theta
,

\displaystyle\theta_{k+1}=T(\theta_{k})\coloneqq\textnormal{{Proj}}_{\Theta}\{\theta_{k}-\beta\nabla_{\theta}F_{\mathcal{D}}(\theta_{k})\}=\textnormal{{Proj}}_{\Theta}\bigg\{\theta_{k}-\frac{\beta}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\nabla_{\theta}l(u_{i},\theta_{k})\bigg\},

(52)

where
\beta\in(0,1/L]
is the learning rate and
\textnormal{{Proj}}_{\Theta}\{\cdot\}
is the projection operator on to
\Theta
. The following Lemma gives the fact that
T
is a contraction with a unique fixed point
\theta^{*}
.

Lemma 45
.

Suppose Assumption
5
is satisfied. Then,
F_{\mathcal{D}}
has unique minimizer
\theta^{*}\in\mathbb{R}^{d_{p}}
. Given
\theta_{0}\in\Theta
and
\beta\in(0,2/L)
,
T
is a contraction with coefficient
\alpha_{T}=\max\{|1-\beta\eta|,|1-\beta L|\}
and unique fixed point
\theta^{*}
, and the sequence
(\theta_{k})_{k\in\mathbb{N}}
generated by Equation
52
converges geometrically,

\displaystyle\|\theta_{k}-\theta^{*}\|_{2}\leq\alpha_{T}^{k}\|\theta_{0}-\theta^{*}\|_{2}.

(53)

Proof:

F_{\mathcal{D}}
has a unique minimizer comes from the fact
F_{\mathcal{D}}
is continuous and strongly convex. To see
T
is a contraction, we first consider
h(\theta)=F_{\mathcal{D}}(\theta)-\frac{\eta}{2}\|\theta\|_{2}^{2}
which is convex and with
(L-\eta)
-Lipschitz gradient. Due to the co-coercivity of
\nabla_{\theta}h
, we have, for
\theta,\theta^{\prime}\in\Theta
,

\displaystyle\langle\nabla_{\theta}F_{\mathcal{D}}(\theta)-\nabla_{\theta}F_{\mathcal{D}}(\theta^{\prime}),\theta-\theta^{\prime}\rangle\geq\frac{L\eta}{L+\eta}\|\theta-\theta^{\prime}\|^{2}_{2}+\frac{1}{\eta+L}\|\nabla_{\theta}F_{\mathcal{D}}(\theta)-\nabla_{\theta}F_{\mathcal{D}}(\theta^{\prime})\|_{2}^{2}.

(54)

Now, we bound the different between
T(\theta)
and
T(\theta^{\prime})
using Equation
54
and the nonexpansiveness of the projection operator,

\displaystyle\|T(\theta)-T(\theta^{\prime})\|_{2}^{2}\leq\|(\theta-\theta^{\prime})-\beta(\nabla_{\theta}F_{\mathcal{D}}(\theta)-\nabla_{\theta}F_{\mathcal{D}}(\theta))\|^{2}_{2}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\|\theta-\theta^{\prime}\|_{2}^{2}+\beta^{2}\|\nabla_{\theta}F_{\mathcal{D}}(\theta)-\nabla_{\theta}F_{\mathcal{D}}(\theta)\|^{2}_{2}-2\beta\langle\nabla_{\theta}F_{\mathcal{D}}(\theta)-\nabla_{\theta}F_{\mathcal{D}}(\theta^{\prime}),\theta-\theta^{\prime}\rangle

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\bigg[1-2\beta\bigg(\frac{L\eta}{L+\eta}\bigg)\bigg]\|\theta-\theta^{\prime}\|_{2}^{2}+\bigg(\beta^{2}-\frac{2\beta}{L+\eta}\bigg)\|\nabla_{\theta}F_{\mathcal{D}}(\theta)-\nabla_{\theta}F_{\mathcal{D}}(\theta)\|^{2}_{2}.

Suppose
\beta\in(0,2/L)
, we consider the following two cases:

Case 1

\beta\leq\frac{2}{L+\eta}
: in this case,
\beta^{2}-\frac{2\beta}{L+\eta}\leq 0
, we replace
\|\nabla_{\theta}F_{\mathcal{D}}(\theta)-\nabla_{\theta}F_{\mathcal{D}}(\theta)\|_{2}
with its lower bound
\eta\|\theta-\theta^{\prime}\|_{2}
which is a consequence of the
\eta
-strong convexity. Then, we arrive at

\displaystyle\|T(\theta)-T(\theta^{\prime})\|_{2}\leq(1-\beta\eta)\|\theta-\theta^{\prime}\|_{2},

which indicates the contraction rate of
T
is
1-\beta\eta
. One can check
1-\beta\eta\geq|1-\beta L|
when
\beta\in(0,\frac{2}{L+\eta})
.

Case 2

\beta\geq\frac{2}{L+\eta}
: in this case,
\beta^{2}-\frac{2\beta}{L+\eta}\geq 0
, we replace
\|\nabla_{\theta}F_{\mathcal{D}}(\theta)-\nabla_{\theta}F_{\mathcal{D}}(\theta)\|_{2}
with its upper bound
L\|\theta-\theta^{\prime}\|_{2}
which is a consequence of having a
L
-Lipschitz gradient. Then, we arrive at

\displaystyle\|T(\theta)-T(\theta^{\prime})\|_{2}\leq(\beta L-1)\|\theta-\theta^{\prime}\|_{2},

which indicates the contraction rate of
T
is
(\beta L-1)
. One can check
1-\beta\eta\geq|1-\beta L|
when
\beta\in(\frac{2}{L+\eta},\frac{2}{L})
.

Combining the above two cases, we have that
T
is a contraction with rate
\alpha_{T}=\max\{|1-\beta\eta|,|1-\beta L|\}
and
\beta\in(0,2/L)
ensures
\alpha_{T}<1
. By Banach contraction mapping theorem
(
Granas and Dugundji 2003
, Theorem 1.1)
, we arrive at the desired result after verifying
T(\theta^{*})=\theta^{*}
.

In practice, the exact gradient descent in Equation
52
is replaced by the SGD to reduce the computational complexity, especially when
N
is large. At the time
k
, the algorithm chooses a subset of
n
samples
\mathcal{N}_{k}\subset\{1,\cdots,N\}
randomly and independently from previous steps. The gradient at time
k
is approximated with samples in
\mathcal{N}_{k}
. Let
\hat{T}_{k}^{n}
be the SGD operator, and we write the update equation as

\displaystyle\hat{\theta}^{n}_{k+1}=\hat{T}_{k}^{n}(\hat{\theta}^{n}_{k})\coloneqq\hat{\theta}_{k}^{n}-\frac{\beta}{n}\sum_{j\in\mathcal{N}_{k}}\nabla_{\theta}l(u_{j},\hat{\theta}^{n}_{k}),

and
\boldsymbol{\hat{\theta}}^{n}\coloneqq\{\hat{\theta}^{n}_{k}\}_{k\in\mathbb{N}}
is in fact a Markov chain. Under some mild conditions, we show, in Lemma
47
, that
\boldsymbol{\hat{\theta}}^{n}
admits a unique invariant distribution
\pi_{n}
and converges to
\pi_{n}
weakly and in Wasserstein metric as
k\to\infty
. Lemma
44
enables the weak convergence of
\boldsymbol{\hat{\theta}}
, however, it remains to check that the random operators
\{\hat{T}^{n}_{k}\}_{k\in\mathbb{N}}
satisfy Assumption
4
.

Lemma 46
.

Under Assumption
5
,
\{\hat{T}^{n}_{k}\}_{k\in\mathbb{N}}
satisfies Assumption
4

Proof:

Assumption
4
(1) is satisfied by Assumption
5
(5). Assumption
4
(2) can be shown by applying Hoeffding’s inequality for bounded i.i.d. random variables. Without loss of generality, we can pick the metric
\mathsf{d}
in Equation
49
to be
\|\cdot\|_{1}
as the
\Theta
has finite dimensions. Given
\epsilon>0
and
k\in\mathbb{N}
,

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\bigg\{\bigg\|\hat{T}^{n}_{k}(\theta^{*})-\theta^{*}\bigg\|_{1}>\beta\epsilon\bigg\}

\displaystyle=\mathbb{P}\bigg\{\bigg\|\frac{1}{n}\sum_{j\in\mathcal{N}_{k}}\nabla_{\theta}l(u_{j},\theta^{*})\bigg\|_{1}>\epsilon\bigg\}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle(a)}}{{\leq}}2d_{p}\exp\bigg\{\frac{-n\epsilon^{2}}{\sigma}\bigg\},

where
(a)
is due to
Hoeffding 1994
and
\sigma\in(0,\infty)
is a constant related to the uniform norm of
\nabla_{\theta}l(\cdot,\theta)
.

Assumption
4
(3) can be established by Assumption
5
(1)(2)(3) which, together, imply the fact that eigenvalues of
\nabla^{2}_{\theta\theta}l(u,\theta)
fall in the interval of
[\eta,L]
. By picking the appropriate learning rate
\beta
,
\hat{T}^{n}_{k}
is a contraction for every realization with contraction rate
\hat{a}_{k}^{n}=\max\{|1-\beta L|,|1-\beta\eta|\}<1
.

Combining the results in Lemma
46
and
44
, we can conclude
\boldsymbol{\hat{\theta}}^{n}
converges to
\pi_{n}
weakly. Conveniently, for metric spaces with bounded diameter, which is the case for
\Theta
, Wasserstein-(1,
\|\cdot\|_{2}
) metrizes the weak topology
(
Gibbs and Su 2002
)
, i.e. weak convergence is equivalent to convergence in Wasserstein-(1,
\|\cdot\|_{2}

5.3
Rested Bandit with UCB

In this section, we consider the finite sample analysis of the UCB-type policy for the multi-arm bandit (MAB) with Markovian reward. Suppose
\mathbb{X}
is a Polish space. We assume the state of the
i
-th arm
\{X_{i,n}\}_{n\in\mathbb{N}}\subset\mathbb{X}
is an Markov chain with transition kernel

, for
i\in\{1,\cdots,K\}
. The states of arms evolve independently. The reward of the

. This is known as the rested or restless bandit problem in the literature. The distinction between rested and restless bandits is that the Markov chain associated with an arm in the former evolves only when the arm is pulled, whereas the Markov chains in the latter evolve regardless of which arm is pulled at each time step. Since the purpose of this example is to demonstrate the usage of Hoeffding’s inequality in the analysis of the regret bound, we opt for the classical UCB-type policy for the sake of simplicity. For finite state space and uniform ergodic Markov chains, the UCB-type policy for both rested and restless bandits is studied in
Tekin and Liu 2012
. We restate their UCB-M algorithm for rested bandit in Algorithm
1
for completeness.

With our theory, the regret analysis can be readily extended to general state space Markov chains with weaker convergence requirements. The logarithmic regret bound is presented in Theorem
55
. Before presenting the regret analysis, we make the following further assumptions for the state process and reward function of each arm.

Remark 51

For Markov chains converging in TV metric, necessary and sufficient conditions exist for both geometric and subgeometric rate

6
Conclusion

In this paper, we study the Hoeffding-type inequality for Markov chains under the generalized concentrability condition and the IPM Dobrushin coefficient. The generalized concentrability condition provides a flexible notion of convergence for Markov chains, which allows easy application of the Hoeffding-type inequality in practice. The IPM Dobrushin coefficient provides an approach to estimate the concentrability constant whenever the problem structure allows it. Under our framework, several existing Hoeffding-type inequalities for Markov chains in the literature emerge as special cases. We provide three application examples from machine learning and reinforcement learning to demonstrate the utility of our theory.

We hope our work can shed some light on the concentration of measure and convergence of Markov chains from the IPM perspective. Nevertheless, there are many problems left unanswered which we list a few here for future studies:

•

What are the necessary and sufficient conditions for the Markov chains to satisfy the generalized concentrability condition?

•

Can we establish the connection between generalized concentrability condition and usual conditions studied in Markov chain theory, such as the drift condition, Harris recurrence, and small set?

•

How to extend the Hoeffding inequality to the unbounded functions with sub-Gaussian distribution assumption?

•

Are there more ways to estimate the concentrability constant in Equation
5
other than the IPM Dobrushin coefficient?
