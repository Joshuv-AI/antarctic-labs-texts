Pascal’s Triangle Fractal Symmetries
Nayan E. Myerson-Jain, Shang Liu, Wenjie Ji, Cenke Xu, Sagar Vijay

Abstract
We introduce a model of interacting bosons exhibiting an infinite collection of fractal symmetries – termed “Pascal’s triangle symmetries” – which provides a natural U ⁡ ( 1 ) {\rm U}(1) generalization of a spin-(1/2) system with Sierpinski triangle fractal symmetries introduced in Ref. Newman and Moore 1999 . The Pascal’s triangle symmetry gives rise to exact degeneracies, as well as a manifold of low-energy states which are absent in the Sierpinski triangle model. Breaking the U ⁡ ( 1 ) {\rm U}(1) symmetry of this model to Z p Z_{p} , with prime integer p p , yields a lattice model with a unique fractal symmetry which is generated by an operator supported on a fractal subsystem with Hausdorff dimension d H = ln ⁡ ( p ⁡ ( p + 1 ) / 2 ) / ln ⁡ p d_{H}=\ln(p(p+1)/2)/\ln p . The Hausdorff dimension of the fractal can be probed through correlation functions at finite temperature. The phase diagram of these models at zero temperature in the presence of quantum fluctuations, as well as the potential physical construction of the U ⁡ ( 1 ) {\rm U}(1) model are discussed.



Introduction:
In recent years, generalizations of the notion
of symmetry have significantly broadened our view on states of
matter. Many previously developed phases, such as
Z_{2}

topological order

;
Wen 1991
;
Moessner and Sondhi 2001
;
Kitaev 2003

(3+1)d

excitations
Wen 2003
;
Moessner and Sondhi 2003
;
Hermele et al. 2004
, were thought to be
beyond the notion of spontaneous symmetry breaking (SSB) of
ordinary global symmetries. But it has been realized in recent
years that these phases still have a unified description as the
SSB of generalized higher-form
symmetries
Nussinov and Ortiz 2009
;
Aharony et al. 2013
;
Gukov and Kapustin 2013
;
Kapustin and Thorngren 2013a
;
Kapustin and Thorngren 2013b
;
Kapustin and Seiberg 2014
;
Gaiotto et al. 2015
;
Hsin et al. 2019
;
Córdova et al. 2019
.
The notion of subsystem symmetry has further enriched our
understanding along this line. Long-range-entangled quantum phases
with fractionalized excitations that exhibit restricted mobility
– termed fracton orders – exhibit emergent subsystem symmetries

Vijay et al. 2016
;
Vijay et al. 2015
;
Haah 2011
;
Nandkishore and Hermele 2019
;
Pretko et al. 2020
. Subsystem symmetries can be
categorized into type-I and II

subsystem symmetry has generators and conserved charges defined on
regular submanifolds of the system, such as lines and planes,
while type-II subsystem symmetries have conserved charges defined
on a fractal-shaped subsystem

;
Haah 2011
, often with
non-integer spatial dimensions.

The simplest model with a fractal subsystem symmetry is the
Sierpinski-triangle model, which was first introduced for the
purpose of studying glassy dynamics in the absence of
disorder
Newman and Moore 1999
:

H_{\mathrm{ST}}=\sum_{\bigtriangledown}-K\sigma^{z}_{1}\sigma^{z}_{2}\sigma^{z}_{3}.

(1)

Here
\sigma^{z}_{i}
is an Ising spin
defined on each site of a triangular lattice, and the sum is only
over the downward-facing triangular plaquettes of the lattice.
This simple model has the following desirable features. (1) The
model has an exotic “fractal symmetry”, which becomes most
explicit when the system is defined on a
L\times L
lattice with

L=2^{k}-1
: the Hamiltonian is invariant under flipping spins
on a Sierpinski-triangle fractal subsystem; (2) at finite
temperature, the three point correlation function
\langle\sigma^{z}_{0,0}\sigma^{z}_{r,0}\sigma^{z}_{0,r}\rangle
is nonzero
only when
r=2^{k}
, and scales as
\sim\exp(-r^{d_{H}})
with

d_{H}=\ln 3/\ln 2
, which is the Hausdorff dimension of the
Sierpinski-triangle
Yoshida 2013
; (3) with a transverse field

\sum_{i}-h\sigma^{x}_{i}
, the system becomes a quantum
Sierpinski-triangle model, and at
h=K
there is a quantum phase
transition at zero temperature
Vasiloiu et al. 2020
;
Zhou et al. 2021
, which
separates the “fractal-ordered” phase that spontaneously breaks
the fractal symmetry (
K>h
), and a disordered phase with (
h>K
).

In this work we introduce generalizations of both the classical
and quantum Sierpinski-triangle models. These models are obtained
from a
{\rm U}(1)

an “approximate” Pascal’s triangle symmetry gives rise to

The
{\rm U}(1)
parent model:
The Hamiltonian of the
{\rm U}(1)

\displaystyle H_{{\rm U}(1)}=\sum_{\bigtriangledown}-t\cos\left(\theta_{1}+\theta_{2}+\theta_{3}\right).

(2)

It is straightforward to see that Eq.

\displaystyle{\rm U}(1)_{1}:\theta_{j\in A}\rightarrow\theta_{j\in A}+\alpha,\ \ \theta_{j\in B}\rightarrow\theta_{j\in B}-\alpha,

(3)

(4)

\displaystyle{\rm U}(1)_{2}:\theta_{j\in A}\rightarrow\theta_{j\in A}+\beta,\ \ \theta_{j\in C}\rightarrow\theta_{j\in C}-\beta.

(5)

A,B,C

\theta=0

Besides the two ordinary

The exact series of fractal transformations of Eq.

\displaystyle\theta_{i}\longrightarrow\theta_{i}+\frac{2\pi}{p}(-1)^{i_{x}+i_{y}}{i_{x}+i_{y}\choose i_{y}}.

(6)

(i_{x},i_{y})

L\times L

Any

{\rm U}(1)

6

excitations created by a

At finite temperature, the
{\rm U}(1)

\displaystyle H_{{\rm U}(1)}^{d}=\sum_{\bigtriangledown}-t\cos(\phi_{\bigtriangledown}).

The three-body interactions of the Sierpinski-triangle model, as

The Quantum Phase diagram:
So far we have only discussed the
classical version of the models. To turn quantum fluctuations on
in Eq.
2
, one can modify the model as

\displaystyle H_{Q-{\rm U}(1)}=\sum_{\bigtriangledown}-t\cos\left(\theta_{1}+\theta_{2}+\theta_{3}\right)+\sum_{j}\frac{U}{2}n_{j}^{2},

(20)

where
n_{j}
is the boson number operator defined on each site of
the triangular lattice, which are conjugate to the boson phase

[n_{i},\theta_{j}]=\mathrm{i}\delta_{ij}
.

As shown previously, a class of ground states of the classical

{\rm U}(1)
model Eq.
2
have the conventional

\sqrt{3}\times\sqrt{3}
order, which spontaneously breaks the
Pascal’s triangle symmetry, and the two
{\rm U}(1)
symmetries in
Eq.
5
. We now investigate whether this classical order
is stable against quantum fluctuation, i.e. whether it is stable
against the
U
term in Eq.
20
. We argue that all
symmetries of the Hamiltonian (
20
) are restored by
quantum fluctuations.

In an ordered phase that spontaneously breaks the
{\rm U}(1)
global
symmetry, one can ignore the fact that the phase angle
\theta
is
a compact boson (i.e.
\theta\sim\theta+2\pi
), and hence
expand the cosine function of Eq.
20
to the lowest
nontrivial order. This procedure leads to an approximate Gaussian
Hamiltonian:

\displaystyle H^{g}_{Q-{\rm U}(1)}=\sum_{i,j}t\theta_{i}\theta_{j}+\sum_{j}3t\theta_{j}^{2}+\frac{U}{2}n_{j}^{2}.

(21)

The band structure of
\theta
based on this
Gaussian Hamiltonian has minima at
\pm\mathbf{K}=\pm(4\pi/3,0)
, which is consistent with the
\sqrt{3}\times\sqrt{3}
order
of the classical Hamiltonian. The spectrum of the Gaussian
Hamiltonian is gapless.

The Gaussian expansion of the Hamiltonian ignored the compactness
of
\theta
. In a quantum model constructed with
\theta
,

\theta
being a compact boson is equivalent to the constraint
that its quantum conjugate variable
n
take discrete values. To
check the stability of a semiclassical state of
\theta
under
quantum fluctuation, one needs to investigate whether the
compactness of
\theta
, or equivalently the discrete nature of

n
would destabilize the semiclassical state described by the
Gaussian Hamiltonian Eq.
21
. For example, the
(2+1)d

quantum dimer model on the square lattice can be mapped to a
compact
{\rm U}(1)
gauge theory
Rokhsar and Kivelson 1988
;
FRADKIN and KIVELSON 1990
, a Gaussian
expansion would lead to gapless photons. But the compactness of
the gauge field is always relevant in a semiclassical photon state
unless the system is at a fine-tuned multicritical point (the
so-called RK point
Rokhsar and Kivelson 1988
), hence the Gaussian state is
generally unstable against quantum fluctuations. This effect is
also referred to as confinement of a lattice gauge theory. The
standard method of the analysis relies on the dual formalism of
Eq.
20
and Eq.
21
. The dual model is defined on
the dual triangular lattice (Fig.
2
), by
introducing the following variables:

\displaystyle\sum_{j\in\bigtriangledown}\theta_{j}=\phi_{\bar{j}},\ \ \ \ n_{j}=\sum_{\bar{j}\in\triangle\ \mathrm{around}\ j}\Psi_{\bar{j}}.

(22)

where
\bar{j}
denotes the sites
of the dual triangular lattice.
\phi_{\bar{j}}
and

\Psi_{\bar{j}}
are canonically conjugate variables.

\Psi_{\bar{j}}
takes half-integer values, and
\phi_{\bar{j}}

is compact. The dual Hamiltonian reads

\displaystyle H_{Q-{\rm U}(1)}^{d}=\sum_{\bar{j}}-t\cos(\phi_{\bar{j}})+\sum_{\bar{\triangle}}\frac{U}{2}(\Psi_{\bar{1}}+\Psi_{\bar{2}}+\Psi_{\bar{3}})^{2}.

(23)

Instead of directly dealing with the discrete variable
\Psi

may view
\Psi_{\bar{j}}

\phi_{\bar{j}}

\Psi

\displaystyle H_{Q-{\rm U}(1)}^{d}

\displaystyle\sim

\displaystyle\sum_{\bar{\triangle}}\frac{U}{2}(\Psi_{\bar{1}}+\Psi_{\bar{2}}+\Psi_{\bar{3}})^{2}-\sum_{\bar{j}}t\cos(\phi_{\bar{j}})

(24)

\displaystyle-

\displaystyle\alpha\cos(2\pi\Psi_{\bar{j}}).

(26)

-terms, and expand
-t\cos(\phi_{j})

Appendix D
A related model with stable
\sqrt{3}\times\sqrt{3}
order

We consider a model directly related to Eq.
20
, but
instead is stable against the inclusion of quantum fluctuations.
In the
{\rm U}(1)
parent model, the Pascal’s triangle fractal
symmetries only exist because a single orientation of triangular
plaquettes is being summed over. If we include the sum over
three-boson interactions on upward facing plaquettes as well, the
model becomes

\displaystyle H=\sum_{\bigtriangledown,\triangle}-t\cos\left(\theta_{1}+\theta_{2}+\theta_{3}\right)+\sum_{j}\frac{U}{2}n_{j}^{2}.

(95)

While this model does not have the
Pascal’s triangle fractal symmetries of Eq.
20
, it still
retains the global
{\rm U}(1)_{1}\times{\rm U}(1)_{2}
symmetry in
Eq.
5
and hence the ground state of this model at small

U
will still have the
\sqrt{3}\times\sqrt{3}
order. In this
case, unlike Eq.
20
, the boson ordered phase will
actually be stable against quantum fluctuations. This can be seen
by analyzing the dual of Eq.
95
. Now, the dual variables
are defined on the sites
\bar{i}
of the dual honeycomb lattice
(Fig.
5
):

\displaystyle\theta_{1}+\theta_{2}+\theta_{3}=\phi_{\bar{i}},\ \ \ \mathrm{site}\ 1,2,3\ \mathrm{around}\ \bar{i};

(96)

(97)

\displaystyle n_{j}=\sum_{\bar{i}}\Psi_{\bar{i}},\ \ \ \bar{i}=1,\cdots 6\ \mathrm{around}\ j.

(98)

The dual variable is subject to a local gauge constraint
around each site
j
:

\displaystyle\sum_{\bar{i}\ \mathrm{around}\ j}(-1)^{\bar{i}}\phi_{\bar{i}}=0\ \mathrm{mod}\ 2\pi.

(99)

Again,
\phi_{\bar{i}}
are compact variables, while

\Psi_{\bar{i}}
takes discrete values. The dual Hamiltonian of
Eq.
95
reads
Xu and Vishwanath 2006

\displaystyle H_{d}=\sum_{\bar{j}}-t\cos(\phi_{\bar{j}})+\sum_{j}\frac{U}{2}\left(\sum_{\bar{i}\ \mathrm{around}\ j}\Psi_{\bar{i}}\right)^{2}.

(100)

The key difference from the model considered
in the main text is that, this dual model Eq.
100
has a
gauge symmetry, i.e. the dual Hamiltonian is invariant under the
following local gauge transformation:

\displaystyle\Psi_{\bar{i}}\rightarrow\Psi_{\bar{i}}+(-1)^{\bar{i}}\epsilon_{j},\ \ \ \bar{i}\ \mathrm{around}\ j,

(101)

where

\epsilon_{j}
is an arbitrary integer-valued function on the
original triangular lattice
j
.

Now we can again replace
\Psi_{\bar{i}}
by continuous variables,
and the constraint that
\Psi
takes discrete values will be
enforced through “vertex operators” such as
\cos(2\pi\Psi)
. If
we ignore these vertex operators, the Lagrangian of the Gaussian
theory of Eq.
100
reads:

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}_{d}=\sum_{\bar{i}}\frac{1}{2t}(\partial_{\tau}\Psi_{\bar{i}})+\sum_{j}\frac{U}{2}\left(\sum_{\bar{i}\ \mathrm{around}\ j}\Psi_{\bar{i}}\right)^{2}.

(102)

This Gaussian
theory has a continuous gauge symmetry, which corresponds to
allowing
\epsilon_{j}
to take arbitrary continuous values in
Eq.
101
. Then, the Gaussian fixed point of
Eq.
102
is stable against weak vertex operators, since
the vertex operators break the continuous gauge symmetry of the
Gaussian theory Eq.
102
.
