Grounded persistent path homology: a stable, topological descriptor for weighted digraphs
Thomas Chaplin, Heather A. Harrington, Ulrike Tillmann

Abstract
Weighted digraphs are used to model a variety of natural systems and can exhibit interesting structure across a range of scales. In order to understand and compare these systems, we require stable, interpretable, multiscale descriptors. To this end, we propose grounded persistent path homology ( GrPPH ) – a new, functorial, topological descriptor that describes the structure of an edge-weighted digraph via a persistence barcode. We show there is a choice of circuit basis for the graph which yields geometrically interpretable representatives for the features in the barcode. Moreover, we show the barcode is stable, in bottleneck distance, to both numerical and structural perturbations. GrPPH arises from a flexible framework, parametrised by a choice of digraph chain complex and a choice of filtration; for completeness, we also investigate replacing the path homology complex, used in GrPPH , by the directed flag complex.

2.4
Persistent homology

Topological data analysis (TDA) is a field of applied mathematics which employs the powerful, discriminative tools of algebraic topology to study complex datasets .
The cornerstone of the field is persistent homology (PH) which yields a stable, discrete, topological invariant, called a barcode (see
[
5
,
9
,
33
]
for an overview).
The barcode summarises topological features in the data (e.g. connected components and loops) and measures the range of scales across which they persist.

Definition 2.31
.

(a)

A

is a functor
\bm{\mathrm{R}}\to\bm{\mathrm{Ch}}
.

(b)

A

\bm{\mathrm{R}}\to\bm{\mathrm{Vec}}
.

{\color[rgb]{0.5,0,1}\bm{\mathrm{PersVec}}}:=\textbf{[}\bm{\mathrm{R}},\,\bm{\mathrm{Vec}}\textbf{]}
.

.

{\color[rgb]{0.5,0,1}\bm{\mathrm{Persvec}}}:=\textbf{[}\bm{\mathrm{R}},\,\bm{\mathrm{vec}}\textbf{]}
.

3
Motivation and definition of GrPPH

Firstly, in Section
3.1
, we describe a standard pipeline for extracting a topological summary from a weighted digraph, and illustrate a number of issues that naturally arise.
Motivated by this in Section
3.2
, we alter the standard pipeline in order to define a ‘grounded pipeline’.
We prove that this new pipeline is functorial in an appropriate sense, which we will later exploit for stability results.
The pipeline is parameterised by two choices; in Section
3.3
we fix these choices in order to define our proposed descriptor.

3.1
Standard pipeline

A typical TDA pipeline for weighted digraphs consists of three ingredients:

1.

a map
F:\mathrm{Obj}(\bm{\mathrm{WDgr}})\to\mathrm{Obj}(\textbf{[}\bm{\mathrm{R}},\,\bm{\mathrm{Dgr}}\textbf{]})

a chain complex functor

which maps each digraph to a chain complex and induces chain map for every digraph map; and finally

a choice of homology functor

in some degree

These components can then be combined into the following pipeline.

\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\textbf{[}\bm{\mathrm{R}},\,C\textbf{]}}

4.1
Decreasing Betti curves

In the first example we saw (Example
3.18
) we saw that all features were born at
t=0
.
Indeed, this is always the case and
\prescript{g}{}{H}_{1}(G,0)
is in fact the real cycle space of
\mathcal{U}(G)
.

Lemma 4.1
.

Given a digraph
G=(V,E,w)
, two distinct nodes
a,b\in V
and a trail
p:a\leadsto b
, then for all
t\geq\pathlen(p)

\mathfrak{R}({p})=\sum_{\tau\in E(p)}\tau=ab\pmod{\prescript{g}{}{B}_{1}(G,t)}.

(4.1)

Proof.

Fix arbitrary
t\geq\pathlen(p)
and denote the vertices of the path as
a=v_{0},\dots,v_{m}=b
.
Whenever
i<j
we can truncate
p
to obtain a path
v_{i}\leadsto v_{j}
of length at most
t
and so
(v_{i},v_{j})\in E(G^{t})
.
Hence, whenever
i<j<k
, there is a directed triangle
v_{i}v_{j}v_{k}\in C_{2}(G^{t})
and hence

v_{i}v_{k}=v_{i}v_{j}+v_{j}v_{k}\pmod{\prescript{g}{}{B}_{1}(G,t)}
.
Therefore, inductively we can write

ab=v_{0}v_{m}=v_{0}v_{1}+v_{1}v_{m}=v_{0}v_{1}+v_{1}v_{2}+v_{2}v_{m}=\dots=\sum_{i=1}^{m}v_{i-1}v_{i}\pmod{\prescript{g}{}{B}_{1}(G,t)}

(4.2)

as required.
∎

Proposition 4.2
.

Fix a weighted digraph
(G,w)
and
t\geq 0
.
For any cycle
v\in\prescript{g}{}{Z}_{1}(G,t)
, there is an initial cycle
v^{\prime}\in\prescript{g}{}{Z}_{1}(G,0)
, supported on the edge in
G
, such that
v
is homologous to
{\prescript{g}{}{\iota(0,t)}}_{\#}v^{\prime}
.

Proof.

Given any edge
\tau=(a,b)\in E(G^{t})
, there is a path
p:a\leadsto b
in
G
of length at most
t
.
Denoting the edges of
p
by
(\tau_{1},\dots,\tau_{m})
,
Lemma
4.1
tells us,
\tau=\sum_{i=1}^{m}\tau_{i}\pmod{\prescript{g}{}{B}_{1}(G,t)}
.
Now, since
\tau_{i}\in E(G)
, we see
{\prescript{g}{}{\iota(0,t)}}_{\#}\tau_{i}=\tau_{i}
for each edge in
p
.
∎

Corollary 4.3
.

Given
G\in\bm{\mathrm{WDgr}}
,

(a)

any interval in
\mathcal{B}{\prescript{g}{}{\mathcal{H}}_{1}(G)}
has birth time
0
;

(b)

\prescript{g}{}{H}_{1}(G,0)
has a basis of simple undirected circuits; and

(c)

\#\Dgm(\prescript{g}{}{\mathcal{H}}_{1}(G))
coincides with the circuit rank of the underlying undirected graph.

Proof.

The first point follows immediately from Lemma
4.2
.
To see the final two points, consider the chain complex,
\prescript{g}{}{C}_{\bullet}(G,0)
at the start of the filtration.
Since
G^{0}
has no edges, the chain complex is simply

{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\cdots 0}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces 0}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces C_{1}(G)}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces C_{0}(G)\cdots}

Since
G
is an orientation of
\mathcal{U}(G)
, the first homology of this chain complex is precisely the real cycle space of
\mathcal{U}(G)
.
Fix an arbitrary spanning forest
T
of
\mathcal{U}(G)

e_{1},\dots,e_{k}

Note

\mathcal{U}(G)

Let

4.2
Circuit lifetimes

While all features in the barcode (and hence all cycles) are born at time
t=0
, their death times generally differ.
We can assign a death-time to any cycle
v\in\prescript{g}{}{Z}_{1}(G,0)
, as the first time
v
becomes null-homologous.

Definition 4.4
.

Given
v\in\prescript{g}{}{Z}_{1}(G,0)
, the
death-time
of
v
is

{\color[rgb]{0.5,0,1}\death(v)}:=\inf\left\{t\geq 0\mathrel{}\middle|\mathrel{}{\prescript{g}{}{\iota(0,t)}}_{\ast}[v]=0\right\}

(4.3)

where we let
\death(v):=\infty
if there is no such
t
.
The
lifetime
of
v
is the interval
{\color[rgb]{0.5,0,1}\lifetime(v)}:=[0,\death(v))
.

Remark 4.5
.

Let
p
be an undirected circuit in
G
and
p^{\prime}
the same circuit, traversed in the opposite direction so that
\mathfrak{R}({p^{\prime}})=-\mathfrak{R}({p})
.
Since
{\prescript{g}{}{\iota(0,t)}}_{\ast}
is linear,
\death(\mathfrak{R}({p}))=\death(\mathfrak{R}({p^{\prime}}))
.
Also, since
\mathfrak{R}({p})
does not depend on the starting vertex of
p
, neither does
\death(\mathfrak{R}({p}))
.

This pipeline gives us a method for associating a lifetime to an undirected circuit in
G
which is ‘geometric’ in the sense that it does not depend on the starting vertex or direction.
The length of this lifetime gives us a ‘size’ to the circuit from the perspective of the filtration.
Since we use the shortest-path filtration, we interpret this size as the time it takes for the flow to ‘fill in’ the circuit.

Lemma 4.6
.

Given
G=(V,E,w)\in\bm{\mathrm{WDgr}}
and two directed paths
p_{1},p_{2}:a\leadsto b
between distinct vertices
a,b\in V
, let
p_{c}
denote the undirected circuit which traverses
p_{1}
forwards and then
p_{2}
in reverse.
For
i=1,2
, define

h_{i}:=\min\left\{t\geq 0\mathrel{}\middle|\mathrel{}\exists v_{i}\in V\text{ along }p_{i}\text{ such that }d(a,v_{i})\leq t\text{ and }d(v_{i},b)\leq t\right\}.

(4.4)

Then
\death(p_{c})\leq\max(h_{1},h_{2})
.

Proof.

Denote
T:=\max(h_{1},h_{2})
.
First assume that there are at least 2 edges in each
p_{i}
.
Then, by the definition of
h_{i}
, there exists
v_{i}\in V\setminus\left\{a,b\right\}
along each
p_{i}
such that
d(a,v_{i})\leq T
and
d(v_{i},b)\leq T
.
Hence there is a long square
av_{1}b-av_{2}b\in\prescript{g}{}{C}_{2}(G,T)
.
By Lemma
4.1
,

0=\prescript{g}{}{\partial}_{2}(av_{1}b-av_{2}b)=\mathfrak{R}({p_{c}})\pmod{\prescript{g}{}{B}_{1}(G,T)}.

(4.5)

Finally, if
p_{1}
contains one edge then
h_{1}=\pathlen(p_{1})
so
d(a,b)\leq h_{1}\leq T
.
The definition of
h_{2}
ensures there is
v_{2}\in V
along
p_{2}
such that
d(a,v_{2}),d(v_{2},b)\leq T
.
Hence there is a directed triangle
av_{2}b\in\prescript{g}{}{C}_{2}(G,T)
.
By Lemma
4.1
,

0=\prescript{g}{}{\partial}_{2}(av_{2}b)=\mathfrak{R}({p_{c}})\pmod{\prescript{g}{}{B}_{1}(G,T)}

(4.6)

which concludes the proof.
∎

4.3
Representatives

After computing persistent homology, it is common to compute cycles with represent the features of the barcode.
In general, representatives are not unique and may be quite complicated.
In practice, one can often compute representatives with integer (and even unit) coefficients
[
26
]
.
These representatives are frequently used for interpreting features (e.g.
[
2
,
35
]
).

Definition 4.8
.

A
persistence basis
for
\prescript{g}{}{\mathcal{H}}_{1}(G)
is a choice of initial cycles

B=\left\{b_{i}\in\prescript{g}{}{Z}_{1}(G,0)\right\}

such that for each
t\geq 0
, the set

\left\{{\prescript{g}{}{\iota(0,t)}}_{\ast}[b_{i}]\right\}\setminus\left\{0\right\}

yields a basis for
\prescript{g}{}{H}_{1}(G,t)
.
We call elements of a persistence basis
representatives
.

Lemma 4.9
.

Given any
G\in\bm{\mathrm{WDgr}}
, a persistence basis for
\prescript{g}{}{\mathcal{H}}_{1}(G)
always exists.

Proof.

This follows from the structure theorem (Theorem
2.32
) and Corollary
4.3
.
∎

Obtaining a persistence basis
B\subseteq\prescript{g}{}{Z}_{1}(G,0)
is desirable because the constituent cycles
represent
the features of the barcode, in the following sense.
If the barcode is
\mathcal{B}{\prescript{g}{}{\mathcal{H}}_{1}(G)}=\left\{\!\!\left\{I_{1},\dots,I_{m}\right\}\!\!\right\}

then there is an ordering on the cycles
B=\left\{b_{1},\dots,b_{m}\right\}
such that
\lifetime(b_{i})=I_{i}
and

\bigoplus_{i=1}^{m}P(I_{i})\cong\prescript{g}{}{\mathcal{H}}_{1}(G).

(4.8)

Moreover, the isomorphism
\phi:\oplus_{i=1}^{m}P(I_{i})\to\prescript{g}{}{\mathcal{H}}_{1}(G)
is given by mapping

1\in I_{i}(t)\mapsto{\prescript{g}{}{\iota(0,t)}}_{\ast}[b_{i}]\text{ whenever }t\in I_{i}.

(4.9)

This mapping gives an isomorphism because
\{{\prescript{g}{}{\iota(0,t)}}_{\ast}[b_{i}]\}\setminus\{0\}
is always a basis for
\prescript{g}{}{H}_{1}(G,t)
.
In this sense, the representatives in
B
generate
\prescript{g}{}{\mathcal{H}}_{1}(G)
.

Representatives live in

In order to more easily compute

, it is desirable to understand how decompositions of the input weighted digraphs give rise to decompositions of the descriptor.
The simplest such decomposition is a disjoint union; as one might expect, the descriptor decomposes as a direct sum.

Theorem 4.18
.

Suppose
G\in\bm{\mathrm{WDgr}}
decomposes as a disjoint union,
G=G_{1}\sqcup G_{2}
, then

\prescript{g}{}{\mathcal{H}}_{1}(G)\cong\prescript{g}{}{\mathcal{H}}_{1}(G_{1})\oplus\prescript{g}{}{\mathcal{H}}_{1}(G_{2}).

(4.21)

Proof.

Note that for each
t\geq 0
,

G^{t}=G_{1}^{t}\sqcup G_{2}^{t}\quad\text{ and }\quad G\cup G^{t}=(G_{1}\cup G_{1}^{t})\sqcup(G_{2}\cup G_{2}^{t}).

(4.22)

For each degree
k\geq 0
, if
H=H_{1}\sqcup H_{2}
then
C_{k}(H)=C_{k}(H_{1})\oplus C_{k}(H_{2})
.
Therefore, for each
k\geq 0
,
\prescript{g}{}{C}_{k}(G,t)
splits as direct sum
\prescript{g}{}{C}_{k}(G_{1},t)\oplus\prescript{g}{}{C}_{k}(G_{2},t)
.
The boundary operator respects this split, mapping
\prescript{g}{}{C}_{k}(G_{i},t)\to\prescript{g}{}{C}_{k-1}(G_{i},t)
and the maps
{\prescript{g}{}{\iota(s,t)}}_{\#}
also respect this split, mapping
\prescript{g}{}{C}(G_{i},s)\to\prescript{g}{}{C}(G_{i},t)
.
Taking homology in degree
1
maintains this direct sum decomposition.
∎

Definition 4.19

(a)

Given a weighted digraph

with

.

(b)

Given a wedge vertex,
\hat{v}
as above the corresponding

of

(G_{1},G_{2})
where
G_{1}
and
G_{2}
are the induced subgraphs on

We write
{\color[rgb]{0.5,0,1}G=G_{1}\vee_{\hat{v}}G_{2}}
.

(c)

.

Remark 4.20
.

Given a wedge decomposition
G=G_{1}\vee_{\hat{v}}G_{2}
note that
G=G_{1}\cup G_{2}
.

In the case of a wedge decomposition
G=G_{1}\vee_{\hat{v}}G_{2}
, since each simple circuit is contained either entirely in
G_{1}
or entirely in
G_{2}
, one expects that
GrPPH
also decomposes.
The proof is more complicated because, in general,
G^{t}\neq G_{1}^{t}\vee_{\hat{v}}G_{2}^{t}
, since there may be paths between separated vertices, through
\hat{v}
.
However, using a chain homotopy, we can show that these edges do not affect the homology.

Theorem 4.21
.

For a weighted digraph
G=(V,E,w)
and a wedge decomposition
G=G_{1}\vee_{\hat{v}}G_{2}
,

\prescript{g}{}{\mathcal{H}}_{1}(G)\cong\prescript{g}{}{\mathcal{H}}_{1}(G_{1})\oplus\prescript{g}{}{\mathcal{H}}_{1}(G_{2}).

(4.24)

Proof.

There are natural inclusion digraph maps
j_{i}:G_{i}\to G
which are also contractions.
Less obviously, there are contraction digraph maps
f_{i}:G\to G_{i}
, where

f_{i}(v):=\begin{cases}v&\text{if }v\in V_{i},\\
\hat{v}&\text{otherwise}.\end{cases}

(4.25)

Since these are all morphisms in
\bm{\mathrm{Cont}}\bm{\mathrm{WDgr}}
, we obtain induced morphisms
{\prescript{g}{}{j_{i}}}_{\#}
and
{\prescript{g}{}{f_{i}}}_{\#}
.
We combine these morphisms to get two morphisms as follows

\displaystyle J:\prescript{g}{}{C}(G_{1})\oplus\prescript{g}{}{C}(G_{2})\to\prescript{g}{}{C}(G),\quad

\displaystyle J(\gamma_{1},\gamma_{2}):={\prescript{g}{}{j_{1}}}_{\#}\gamma_{1}+{\prescript{g}{}{j_{2}}}_{\#}\gamma_{2};

(4.26)

\displaystyle F:\prescript{g}{}{C}(G)\to\prescript{g}{}{C}(G_{1})\oplus\prescript{g}{}{C}(G_{2}),\quad

\displaystyle F(\gamma):=({\prescript{g}{}{f_{1}}}_{\#}\gamma,{\prescript{g}{}{f_{2}}}_{\#}\gamma).

(4.27)

Composing with homology in degree
1
, denote

{J}_{\ast}:=\textbf{[}\bm{\mathrm{R}},\,H_{1}\textbf{]}\circ J

.

Claim 4.21.1
.

In degree

,

is the identity map on
\prescript{g}{}{C}(G_{1})\oplus\prescript{g}{}{C}(G_{2})

Proof of Claim.

First note that,

is the identity digraph map

.

is the constant digraph map

which maps all of
G_{i}
to the vertex
\hat{v}
.
Hence, in matrix form, we can write

{\prescript{g}{}{c_{1}}}_{\#}&{\prescript{g}{}{\mathrm{id}_{2}}}_{\#}\end{pmatrix}.

{\prescript{g}{}{c_{i}}}_{\#}

.

In degree
1

{J}_{\ast}\circ{F}_{\ast}=\mathrm{id}

\prescript{g}{}{\mathcal{H}}_{1}(G)

Proof of Claim.

First, we compute

in degree

.
Recall that

However, if

(J\circ F)(e)=a\hat{v}+\hat{v}b

If

Hence,

\prescript{g}{}{\mathcal{H}}_{1}(G)
.
∎

Since
{J}_{\ast}

{F}_{\ast}
are mutually inverse, they induce isomorphisms of persistent vector spaces.
∎

In order to prove stability, we will have to build interleaving chain maps.

5.2
Weight perturbation

The classical stability theorem of persistent homology (first shown in
[
13
]
) is that for two continuous tame function
f,g:X\to\mathbb{R}
of a trianguable topological space
X
, denoting the persistence barcode of their sub-level set filtration by
\mathcal{B}{}_{f}
and
\mathcal{B}{}_{g}
respectively,

d_{B}(\mathcal{B}{}_{f},\mathcal{B}{}_{g})\leq|\!|f-g|\!|_{\infty}.

(5.5)

In our setting, the closet analogy to changing the function is changing the weighting, as well as the corresponding effect that has on the shortest-path quasimetric.
We find that
GrPPH
is stable to perturbations of the edge weights.
Moreover, the stability is local since it depends only of the weights of the perturbed edges.

Definition 5.5
.

Given a weighted digraph
G=(V,E,w)
and a new weight function
w^{\prime}:E(G)\to\mathbb{R}_{>0}
, we define
{\color[rgb]{0.5,0,1}\mathbb{O}^{p}_{w^{\prime}}{G}}:=(V,E,w^{\prime})
.

Theorem 5.6
.

Given a weighted digraph
G=(V,E,w)\in\bm{\mathrm{WDgr}}
and a new weighting function
w^{\prime}:E(G)\to\mathbb{R}_{>0}
,
let
d
and
d^{\prime}
denote the shortest-path quasimetric on
G
and
\mathbb{O}^{p}_{w^{\prime}}{G}
respectively.
Then

d_{B}\big(\mathcal{B}{\prescript{g}{}{\mathcal{H}}_{1}(G)},\mathcal{B}{\prescript{g}{}{\mathcal{H}}_{1}(\mathbb{O}^{p}_{w^{\prime}}{G})}\big)\leq\max_{i,j\in V}\left|d(i,j)-d^{\prime}(i,j)\right|\leq\sum_{e\in E}\left|w(e)-w^{\prime}(e)\right|.

(5.6)

Proof.

For brevity, denote
G^{\prime}:=\mathbb{O}^{p}_{w^{\prime}}{G}
and
\delta:=\max_{i,j\in V}\left|d(i,j)-d^{\prime}(i,j)\right|
.
First note that for any
(i,j)
and any path
p\in\mathcal{P}({i}\to{j})

\left|\sum_{e\in p}w(e)-\sum_{e\in p}w^{\prime}(e)\right|\leq\sum_{e\in p}\left|w(e)-w^{\prime}(e)\right|\leq\sum_{e\in E}\left|w(e)-w^{\prime}(e)\right|=:W_{1}.

(5.7)

So the cost of
p
differs by at most
W_{1}
.
Minimising over
\mathcal{P}({i}\to{j})
, we see
\left|d(i,j)-d^{\prime}(i,j)\right|\leq W_{1}
.

Since
V(G)=V(G^{\prime})
, there are identity vertex maps
i_{1}:V(G)\to V(G^{\prime})
and
i_{2}:V(G^{\prime})\to V(G)
.
Now
i_{1}
defines a digraph map
G\to G^{\prime}
since
G=G^{\prime}
as digraphs.
Moreover, given
(i,j)\in E(G^{t})
, then
d(i,j)\leq t
so
d^{\prime}(i,j)\leq t+\delta
and hence
(i,j)\in E((G^{\prime})^{t+\delta})
.
This shows
i_{1}
defines a digraph map
G^{t}\to(G^{\prime})^{t+\delta}
for all
t\geq 0
.
Therefore
i_{1}
(and likewise
i_{2}
) is a
\delta
-shifting vertex map.
Hence, we obtain morphisms

{\mathcal{S}(i_{1},\delta)}_{\#}:\prescript{g}{}{C}(G)\to\prescript{g}{}{C}(G^{\prime})[\delta]\quad\text{ and }\quad{\mathcal{S}(i_{2},\delta)}_{\#}:\prescript{g}{}{C}(G^{\prime})\to\prescript{g}{}{C}(G)[\delta].

(5.8)

Moreover, since composing
i_{1}
and
i_{2}
in either ordered yields the identity vertex map, these morphisms constitute a
\delta
-interleaving.
The first inequality then follows by the isometry theorem.
∎

Remark 5.7
.

Continuing the analogy to the classical stability theorem, note that the sharper bound obtained by Theorem
5.6
is
|\!|d-d^{\prime}|\!|_{\infty}
while the weaker bound is
|\!|w-w^{\prime}|\!|_{1}
.

5.3
Edge subdivision

Weighted digraphs arising in applications are subject not only to numerical noise (i.e. weight perturbation) but also
structural noise
.
For the remainder of this section, we investigate the effects of various structural perturbations.

First, we consider edge subdivision, in which one or mare parent edge is split into multiple daughter edges with the weight distributed amongst them.
Since we are interpreting edge weights as corresponding to a length, it is natural to require that the sum of the weights of the daughter edges equals the weight of the parent edge.
In order to formalise how the weight of an edge is subdivided amongst its daughters, we use maps into the standard

5.4
Edge collapse

Another potential structural perturbation is that of edge collapses, in which the two end points of an edge are identified and the edge deleted.
In applications, this may happen particularly to low-weight edges, which cannot be discerned by the imaging method and hence collapsed to a vertex instead.
Since we interpret edge-weights as corresponding to distance, we add half the weight of the collapsed edge to each of its neighbours so that the length of paths through the collapsed edge are not changed.
