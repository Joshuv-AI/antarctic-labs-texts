Volume vs. Complexity of Hyperbolic Groups
Nir Lazarovich

Abstract
We prove that for a one-ended hyperbolic graph X X , the size of the quotient X / G X/G by a group G G acting freely and cocompactly bounds from below the number of simplices in an Eilenberg-MacLane space for G G . We apply this theorem to show that one-ended hyperbolic cubulated groups (or more generally, one-ended hyperbolic groups with globally stable cylinders à la Rips-Sela) cannot contain isomorphic finite-index subgroups of different indices.



1
Introduction

Complexity vs volume.

Let
G
act freely and cocompactly on
\mathbb{H}^{n}
,
n\geq 2
. For
n=2
, the Gauss-Bonnet formula shows that the Euler characteristic of the group
G
is proportional to the volume of the quotient
\mathbb{H}^{2}/G
.
In higher dimensions, Gromov and Thurston
[
18
,
28
]
showed that the simplicial volume of
G
is proportional to the hyperbolic volume of the quotient
\mathbb{H}^{n}/G
.

In the above results, a topological feature of the group
G
is shown to be related to the volume of the quotient
\mathbb{H}^{n}/G
.
This phenomenon was extensively studied for groups acting on non-positively curved manifolds
[
7
,
10
,
2
,
13
,
12
,
14
,
1
,
3
,
15
]
.
In this work, we study the relation between topological complexity and volume in the discrete setting of group actions on hyperbolic simplicial complexes.
For this purpose let us first define what we mean by “complexity” and “volume”.

Let
G
be a group. An Eilenberg-MacLane space
K=K(G,1)
for
G
is a CW-complex such that
\pi_{1}(K)=G
and
\pi_{n}(K)=0
for all
n\geq 2
.
We define the
topological complexity

\Cx(G)
of
G
to be the minimal number of cells in an Eilenberg-MacLane
\Delta
-complex for
G
.
The topological complexity of a group can be thought of as the group analogue of the Kneser complexity
k(M)
of a manifold
M

[
20
]
which is the minimal number of simplices in a triangulation of
M
.

For a locally finite connected
\Delta
-complex
X
, we define the
volume

\Vol_{X}(G)
of a free action
G\curvearrowright X
to be the number of
G
-orbits of cells of
X
.
Note that if
X
is contractible and
G
acts on
X
freely, then
X/G
is an Eilenberg-MacLane space for
G
and it follows in this case that
\Cx(G)\leq\Vol_{X}(G)
.
Thus, we will be interested in bounding the complexity
\Cx(G)
of
G
from below by
\Vol_{X}(G)
.

Lower bounds do not hold in general.
For example, if we consider actions of
\mathbb{Z}^{n}\curvearrowright\mathbb{R}^{n}
by translations, then while the complexity of
\mathbb{Z}^{n}
is fixed, the volume of the action can be arbitrarily large.
However, for one-ended hyperbolic graphs Theorems
A
and
B
below provide lower bounds for the complexity in terms of the volume.

Theorem A
.

Let
X
be a one-ended hyperbolic graph. Then there exists
\alpha=\alpha(X)>0
such that if
G\curvearrowright X
freely and cocompactly then

\alpha\cdot\sqrt{\Vol_{X}(G)}\leq\Cx(G).

The next theorem provides a linear lower bound for one-ended hyperbolic spaces
X
which admit
\Aut(X)
-globally stable cylinders à la Rips-Sela
[
24
]
(see §
2
for definitions). These include locally finite
C^{\prime}(1/8)
-small cancellation polygonal complexes by Rips-Sela
[
24
]
and hyperbolic CAT(0) cube complexes by L.-Sageev
[
21
]
. It is unknown whether all hyperbolic groups admit globally stable cylinders.

Theorem B
.

Let
X
be a one-ended hyperbolic graph which admits
\Aut(X)
-globally stable cylinders. Then there exists
\alpha=\alpha(X)>0
such that if a group
G
acts freely and cocompactly on
X
then

\alpha\cdot\Vol_{X}(G)\leq\Cx(G).

The following is an immediate application of the inequalities above to the case of finite-index subgroups.

Theorem C
.

Let
G
be a torsion-free one-ended hyperbolic group. Then, there exist
\alpha,\beta>0
such that if
H\leq G
is a finite index subgroup then

\alpha\cdot\sqrt{[G:H]}\leq\Cx(H)\leq\beta\cdot[G:H].

If moreover
G
admits globally stable cylinders then

\alpha\cdot[G:H]\leq\Cx(H)\leq\beta\cdot[G:H].

Note that
\Cx(G)<\infty
if and only if
G
is of type
F
. For hyperbolic groups this happens if and only if
G
is torsion free.
More refined versions of the above theorems for hyperbolic groups with torsion appear in §
5
.

The proofs of Theorems
A
,
B
, and
C
appear in §
5
. We sketch the main idea here.

Sketch of proof of Theorem
B
.

Let
K
be a the
K(G,1)
with the minimal number of cells.
We may assume that
X
is the 1-skeleton of a contractible finite dimensional complex (namely, its Rips complex).
The free action of
G\curvearrowright X
endows the 2-skeleton of
K
with a singular foliation by works of Delzant
[
8
]
and Rips-Sela
[
24
]
.
The details of this foliation are described in §
2
and §
3
.
In Lemma
3.1
, we provide an upper bound, that depends linearly on the number of 2-cells in
K
, for the number of leaves in this foliation.
On the other hand, the action
G\curvearrowright X
induces a quasi-isometry between
G
and
X
.
Using Bestvina-Mess
[
5
]
, we show in Proposition
4.1
that any continuous quasi-isometry between Rips complexes of hyperbolic groups must be
uniformly
quasi-surjective.
Proposition
4.1
is used to get a lower bound, that depends linearly on
\Vol_{X}(H)
, on the number of leaves of the foliation.
Combining the two inequalities we get the desired result.

Isomorphic finite-index subgroups.

We now turn to an application of Theorem
C
to the following question.

Question 1.1
.

Let
G
be a non-elementary hyperbolic group. Can
G
contain two isomorphic finite index subgroups of different indices?

Note that this is possible for
G\simeq\mathbb{Z}
, so the assumption that
G
is non-elementary is necessary for a negative answer.
By Sela
[
25
]
torsion-free one-ended hyperbolic groups are co-Hopfian, so the answer is negative if one of the subgroups is equal to
G
.
The answer is negative if the group has non-zero Euler characteristic, e.g free groups (of rank
\geq 2
) and (hyperbolic) surface groups.
By Mostow Rigidity
[
22
]
, it is also negative for hyperbolic manifold groups since the volume is an isomorphism invariant and depends linearly on the index.
By Sykiotis
[
27
]
, the answer is negative if
G
has a JSJ decomposition with a maximal hanging Fuchsian group.
Surprisingly, Stark-Woodhouse
[
26
]
found examples of one-ended hyperbolic groups with isomorphic finite-index and infinite-index subgroups.

We first answer Question
1.1
in the case
G
has more than two ends.

Theorem D
.

Let
G
be a finitely presented group, then either
G
has at most 2 ends or
G
does not contain two isomorphic subgroups of different indices.

If
G
is virtually torsion free, Theorem
D
is a corollary of
[
27
,
29
]
. Its proof is independent of the rest of the paper and occupies §
6
.

Finally we answer the question under the additional assumption that
G
admits globally stable cylinders (e.g cubulated).

Theorem E
.

Let
G
be a non-elementary hyperbolic group that admits globally stable cylinders, then
G
does not contain two isomorphic finite index subgroups of different indices.

The proof of Theorem
E
occupies §
7
, and uses a bootstrapping argument, Lemma
7.1
, that shows that if
G
contains two isomorphic finite index subgroups of different indices then one can assume that the ratio of their indices is arbitrarily large.

Related results and open questions.

We end this introduction with a discussion of related results and open questions. Theorem
B
assumes that
G
admits globally stable cylinders to get a linear lower bound.

Question 1.2

(Rips-Sela)
.

Do all hyperbolic groups admit globally stable cylinders?

Question 1.3
.

Does the linear lower bound in Theorem
B
hold for all non-elementary hyperbolic groups?

Let
X
be a locally finite
\Delta
-complex. If
G
acts freely and cocompactly on
X
then we can think of
G
as a uniform lattice in
\Aut(X)
. Upon fixing a Haar measure on
\Aut(X)
, we can define the co-volume
\covol(G)=\Vol(\Aut(X)/G)
. It is easy to see that
\covol(G)\asymp_{X}\Vol_{X}(G)
for every group
G
acting freely and cocompactly on
X
.
Thus, one can study the relation between measures of complexity of lattices and their co-volumes.
In the setting of non-positive curvature we ask:

Question 1.4
.

Let
X
be a geodesically complete CAT(0) space without Euclidean factors, does
\covol(G)\prec\Cx(G)
hold for torsion-free uniform lattices
G
in
\Isom(X)
?

There are other natural notions of complexity for groups. The most basic one is
\rank(G)
– the minimal number of generators. As the following example shows Theorem
C
and hence Theorems
A
and
B
are false when replacing
\Cx(G)
by
\rank(G)
.

Example 1.5
.

Let
G
be the fundamental group of a closed hyperbolic fibered 3-manifold group. That is,
G=S\rtimes\left<\phi\right>
where
S
is a (closed) surface group, and
\phi\curvearrowright S
is a pseudo-Anosov mapping class. Then the subgroups
G_{n}=S\rtimes\left<\phi^{n}\right>\leq G
have
[G:G_{n}]=n
but
\rank(G_{n})\leq\rank(S)+1
.

The same example shows that the minimal number of relations in a presentation will also not be bounded from below. Perhaps the next natural notion of complexity is Delzant’s T-invariant
[
9
]
defined to be the minimal number
T(G)
of relations of length 3 in a presentation for
G
in which all relations have length
\leq 3
.
Cooper
[
7
]
showed that for a hyperbolic 3-manifold
M
one has
\Vol(M)\leq\pi\cdot T(\pi_{1}(M))
.

Question 1.6
.

Can
\Cx(G)
be replaced by
T(G)
in Theorems
A
,
B
and
C
? (cf. Theorems
5.1
and
5.2
.)

As the following example shows, one should not expect the volume to bound
\Cx(G)
or
T(G)
from above in the non-discrete setting.

Example 1.7

(Thurston
[
28
]
)
.

Let
M
be a cusped hyperbolic 3-manifold. By Dehn filling
M
, one can get non-isomoprhic hyperbolic 3-manifolds
M_{i}
with
\Vol(M_{i})<\Vol(M)
. Since
M_{i}
are non-isomorphic, by Mostow Rigidity
[
22
]
they have non-isomorphic fundamental groups
G_{i}=\pi_{1}(M_{i})
, which thus have arbitrarily large
\Cx(G_{i})
and
T(G_{i})
.

With this exception in mind, Gelander
[
13
, Conjecture 1.3]
conjectures that if
G
acts cocompactly and freely on a non-compact symmetric space
X
(and
G
is arithmetic if
\dim(X)=3
), then a minimal Eilenberg-Maclane simplicial complex for
G
with bounded vertex degree (that depends only on
X
) has at most
\beta\cdot\Vol(X/G)
vertices. The conjecture holds for non-uniform arithmetic lattices
[
13
]
, and a slightly weaker bound was recently obtained in
[
15
]
.
Other measures of complexity that are bounded from above by the co-volume include: Betti numbers of non-positively curved manifolds
[
2
]
;
\rank(G)
for lattices in various symmetric spaces
[
3
,
14
,
12
]
;
\log(|Tor(H_{k}(G;\mathbb{Z})|)
for manifolds with normalized bounded negative curvature
[
1
]
; a relative T-invariant for hyperbolic 3-manifolds
[
10
]
.

Regarding Question
1.1
and Theorems
D
and
E
we ask the following.

Question 1.8
.

Which groups have isomorphic finite index subgroups of different indices?

In particular, such groups include the class of
finitely non-co-Hopfian groups
defined in
[
6
]
, i.e groups that are isomorphic to proper finite-index of themselves. Some results and conjectures highlight “nilpotent features” in finitely non-co-Hopfian groups (see
[
30
,
23
]
), can similar phenomena occur for groups with isomorphic finite index subgroups of different indices?

Cylinders, slices and global stability

In this section we recall (and slightly adapt) the definition and properties of cylinders, slices and global stability from Rips-Sela
[
24
]
.

Let
X
be a graph. Let
d
be the minimal path metric on the vertices of
X
.
Assume
X
is
\delta
-hyperbolic.

Cylinders and slices.

A
\theta
-
cylinder
from
x
to
y
is the triple
(C,x,y)
where
x,y\in X
and
C
is a subset that satisfies
\gamma\subseteq C\subseteq N_{\theta}(\gamma)
for every geodesic
\gamma
connecting
x,y
.
By abuse of notation we will denote cylinders simply by
C
and regard
x,y
as implicit.
For a point
u\in C
we set

L_{C}(u)=\{w\in C\;|\;d(w,x)\leq d(u,x)\text{ and }d(u,w)\geq 5\theta\}

and similarly

R_{C}(u)=\{w\in C\;|\;d(w,y)\leq d(u,y)\text{ and }d(u,w)\geq 5\theta\}.

The
difference

\diff_{C}(u,v)
between
u,v\in C
is defined by

\diff_{C}(u,v)=|L_{C}(u)-L_{C}(v)|-|L_{C}(v)-L_{C}(u)|\\
+|R_{C}(v)-R_{C}(u)|-|R_{C}(u)-R_{C}(v)|.

The following lemma summarizes the main properties of the difference function.
[
8
]
]

Lemma 2.1

(Lemma 3.4 in
[
24
]
)
.

If
C
is a
\theta
-cylinder from
x
to
y
then for all
u,v,w\in C
:

(1)

if
\bar{C}=(C,y,x)
denotes the reverse
\theta
-cylinder from
y
to
x
then
\diff_{\bar{C}}(u,v)=-\diff_{C}(u,v)
.

(2)

\diff_{C}(u,v)+\diff_{C}(v,w)=\diff_{C}(u,w)
and
\diff_{C}(u,v)=-\diff_{C}(v,u)
.∎

It follows that the relation
u\sim v\in C
if
\diff_{C}(u,v)=0
is an equivalence relation. The equivalence classes of this relation are called
slices
and are denoted by
[u]_{C}
. The relation
[x]_{C}\prec[y]_{C}
if
\diff(x,y)<0
is well-defined and gives a well-ordering of the slices of

.

Lemma 2.2

(Proposition 3.6 in
[
24
]
and Lemme I.1 in
[
8
]
)
.

If
C
is a
\theta
-cylinder from
x

then for all

(1)

The slices have a bounded diameter, namely

\diam([u]_{C})\leq 10\theta.

(2)

for every two slices
[u]_{C}\prec[v]_{C}
we have

\frac{1}{10\theta}\#\{[w]_{C}\;|\;[u]_{C}\preceq[w]_{C}\prec[v]_{C}\}\leq d(u,v).\qed

The next lemma shows that when two cylinders coincide on a large ball their slices coincide, and the order

Lemma 2.3

(Proposition 3.6 in
[

]

[
8
]
)
.

If

(1)

(stability of the difference)

.

(3)

(stability of the ordering) If in addition

[u]_{C}\prec[v]_{C}
in
C
and
[u]_{C}=[u]_{C^{\prime}}
and
[v]_{C}=[v]_{C^{\prime}}
then
[u]_{C^{\prime}}\prec[v]_{C^{\prime}}

C^{\prime}

-hyperbolic graph

•

(

Lemma 6.1

Let

Two isomorphisms

are

The

of
G
is the group

whose elements are equivalence classes of isomorphisms between finite-index subgroups of
G
, and group multiplication of

is the restriction of the composition
\psi\circ\phi
to its natural domain
\phi^{-1}(B_{\phi}\cap A_{\psi})
