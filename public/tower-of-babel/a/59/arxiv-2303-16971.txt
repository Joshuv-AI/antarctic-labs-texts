Sparse joint shift in multinomial classification


Abstract
. Sparse joint shift (SJS) was recently proposed as a tractable model for general dataset shift which may cause changes to the marginal distributions of features and labels as well as the posterior probabilities and the class-conditional feature distributions. Fitting SJS for a target dataset without label observations may produce valid predictions of labels and estimates of class prior probabilities. We present new results on the transmission of SJS from sets of features to larger sets of features, a conditional correction formula for the class posterior probabilities under the target distribution, identifiability of SJS, and the relationship between SJS and covariate shift. In addition, we point out inconsistencies in the algorithms which were proposed for estimating the characteristics of SJS, as they could hamper the search for optimal solutions, and suggest potential improvements.

1.
Introduction

The notion of
Sparse Joint Shift (SJS)
was introduced by Chen et al.
[
4
]
as
a tractable model of dataset shift “which considers the joint shift of both labels and a few features”.
In this paper, we re-analyse the notion in some depth, looking closer at its connection to
prior probability shift and the link between SJS and covariate shift. For facilitating this intent,
we take recourse to some observations on general dataset shift
by Tasche
[
22
]
.

We revisit the notion of SJS in a probabilistic measure theory setting that allows for the
easy deployment of powerful mathematical
tools like the tower property of conditional expectation or Dynkin’s theorem which are less readily available
within the density-focussed approach used by Chen et al.
[
4
]
. This paper can be
read as a commentary on Chen et al.
[
4
]
but also as a self-contained presentation of SJS.

The main contributions with this paper to the subject are as follows:

•

We extend the definition of SJS such that it also covers (for instance) distributional
shift of
\|\mathbf{X}\|=\sqrt{\sum_{i=1}^{d}X_{i}^{2}}
if
\mathbf{X}=(X_{1},\dots,X_{d})

is an
\mathbb{R}^{d}
-valued feature vector (see Remark
3.6
below), without having to enlarge
the set of available features.

•

We show that SJS for a set of features
X_{i},i\in I,
implies SJS for all sets of features
that include this set of features (Corollary
4.3
below).

•

We derive for SJS a conditional version of the posterior correction formula of Saerens et
al.
[
15
]

and Elkan
[
5
]
, see Proposition
4.5
below.

•

In Theorem
4.7
, we present a result on the identifiability of SJS that can be interpreted
as a generalisation
of the confusion matrix approach to class prior estimation under prior probability shift
(Saerens et al.
[
15
]
).

•

We explore in depth the relationship between SJS and covariate shift (Theorem
4.16
below).

•

We comment on the approaches to the estimation of importance weights proposed by
Chen et al.
[
4
]
, identify certain issues that might
affect their performance (Section
5
below), and suggest improvements to avoid these issues.

In Section
2
, an overview of related work on the subject is given. Section
3

sets the scene for the paper by specifying the model considered and introducing the notion of
sparse joint shift (SJS). Properties of SJS are discussed in some detail in Section
4
.
In Section
5
, two approaches by Chen et al.
[
4
]

to the estimation of densities under the assumption of SJS are presented and commented on.

See Appendix
A
for a brief glossary of concepts and notation
from probabilistic measure theory (like indicator function
\mathbf{1}_{S}
,

\sigma
-algebra, or conditional expectation) which are frequently used in the following.
Short proofs of results are integrated in the text, longer proofs are
presented in Appendix
B
. Appendix
C
provides the formulae relevant
for applications of SJS in the special case of all features being discrete.

2.
Related work

In a wide sense, this paper is related to all work on dataset shift, transfer learning, domain adaptation,
and class prior estimation in multinomial classification settings, including focussed literature on the binary case.

More specifically, there is closely related work on dataset shift models that are based on invariance assumptions:

•

Covariate shift, including density ratio estimation
(Shimodaira
[
17
]
, Sugiyama et al.
[
19
]
, and the references therein).
For this type of shift,
the posterior class probabilities are assumed to be invariant between source and target distribution.

•

Prior probability shift, also called label shift or target shift
(Storkey
[
18
]
, Azizzadenesheli
[
1
]
, and the references therein).
In this case, the class-conditional
feature distributions are assumed to be invariant between source and target distribution.

•

Factorizable joint shift (He et al.
[
7
]
). According to the original definition of He et al.,
at first glance, no invariance assumption is involved. Tasche
[
22
]
showed that with
this type of shift, the ratios of the class-conditional feature densities remain invariant up to constant
factors between source and target distribution.

•

Covariate shift with posterior drift (Scott
[
16
]
). This type of shift is characterised by the
weak invariance assumption that the posterior probabilities of the source distribution are statistically
sufficient for the full feature vector also under the target distribution (Tasche
[
20
]
).

Sparse joint shift (SJS) is also characterised by an invariance assumption: According to
Section 3 of Chen et al.
[
4
]
,
“Roughly speaking, SJS allows both labels and a few features to shift,
but assumes the remaining features’ conditional distribution to stay the same.”

An assumption of absolute continuity of the target distribution with respect to the source distribution
appears to be fundamental for the study of dataset shift with invariance assumptions.
Consequently, there is a clear demarcation between
the approach chosen in this paper and a large thread of literature that begins with the seminal paper
by Ben-David et al.
[
2
]
and investigates ways involving
so-called representations (transformations of the features in order to make their
source and target domains similar) to get around the absolute continuity assumption.
See Johansson et al.
[
10
]
for a discussion of limitations to the representation-based approach
to domain adaptation.

A third general approach to estimating dataset shift is based on mixture models and solving transport problems,
under the assumption that the shift is enacted by moving as little as possible (probability)
mass (Hofer
[
8
]
). Such an approach may also involve the construction of representations
(Kirchmeyer et al.
[
11
]
).

3.
Setting

In this paper, we use the probabilistic setting of Tasche
[
22
]
. See also Section 2.2 of
Tasche
[
21
]
for a detailed reconciliation of mainstream machine learning
and probabilistic measure theory settings.

Assumption 3.1
.

(\Omega,\mathcal{M})
is a measurable space. The
source distribution

P
and the
target distribution

Q
are probability measures on
(\Omega,\mathcal{M})
.
For some positive integer
\ell\geq 2
, events
A_{1},\ldots,A_{\ell}\in\mathcal{M}
and
a sub-
\sigma
-algebra
\mathcal{H}\subset\mathcal{M}
are given. The events
A_{i}
,
i=1,\ldots,\ell
,
and
\mathcal{H}
have the following properties:

(i)

\bigcup_{i=1}^{\ell}A_{i}=\Omega
.

(ii)

A_{i}\cap A_{j}=\emptyset
,
i,j=1,\ldots,\ell
,
i\neq j
.

(iii)

0<P[A_{i}]
,
i=1,\ldots,\ell
.

(iv)

0<Q[A_{i}]
,
i=1,\ldots,\ell
.

(v)

A_{i}\notin\mathcal{H}
,
i=1,\ldots,\ell
.

\mathcal{H}
represents the features in a classification problem,
typically denoted
x
or
\mathbf{x}
in the machine learning literature. We suppose that
the set of features has been selected and fixed once for all such that in the following
\mathcal{H}

is also fixed unless stated otherwise. Think of
\mathcal{H}
as the data referred to by
a ‘long list’ of explanatory variables.

The events
A_{i}
can be understood as markers or labels of classes (typically denoted
y
in machine learning)
or layers with different feature
distributions. Statistical classification means predicting the classes
A_{i}
based on possibly
incomplete information as represented by
\mathcal{H}
.

Two measures
P
and
Q
are mentioned in Assumption
3.1
in order to reflect
dataset shift and the resulting tasks of transfer learning and domain adaptation.
These tasks have in common that they both require inference on
the full target (test) distribution
Q
from partially known (by observations)

Q
on
\mathcal{H}
(the marginal distribution of the features) and completely known source (training) distribution

P
.

Assumption
3.1
, complemented by Assumption
3.7
below, can be reconciled with the setting
of Chen et al.
[
4
]
in the following way:

•

P
is the source distribution (‘domain’)
\mathbb{P}_{s}
and
Q
is the target distribution

\mathbb{P}_{t}
of Chen et al.
[
4
]
.

•

Chen et al. assume that
\mathbb{P}_{s}
and
\mathbb{P}_{t}
have got densities
or mass functions
p_{s}=p_{s}(\mathbf{x},y)
and

p_{t}=p_{t}(\mathbf{x},y)
with respect to an unspecified measure. In this paper, we make consequent use
of the source distribution
P
as the common reference measure such that only a density

\bar{h}
of the target distribution

Q
with respect to the source distribution
P
is needed for expressing the relationship of
Q
and
P
.

•

The density
\bar{h}
is also studied by Chen et al.
[
4
]
.
They call it
weight function

and denote is by
w(\mathbf{x},y)
. In the literature,
\bar{h}
is also called
importance weight

(see, e.g., Zhang et al.
[
23
]
and the references therein).

•

The aim of Chen et al.
[
4
]
in their paper
is to estimate the weight function
w(\mathbf{x},y)

in order to obtain full knowledge of the target distribution.

•

In this paper, we rather focus on determining the target posterior class probabilities

Q[A_{i}\,|\,\mathcal{H}]
. When the target feature distribution is known, its combination with the
posterior probabilities (equivalently to having recourse to the weight function) also
provides full knowledge of the joint distribution of features and
class labels in the target domain. Then, in particular, the class prior probabilities
Q[A_{i}]
can
be computed according to the law of total probability as

Q[A_{i}]\ =\ E_{Q}\bigl[Q[A_{i}\,|\,\mathcal{H}]\bigr],\qquad i=1,\ldots,\ell,

thus providing a solution to the class prior estimation problem (also called
‘quantification’) in the target domain.

The following two definitions introduce crucial notation for the subsequent discussion of
dataset shifts.

Definition 3.2
.

We denote by
\mathcal{A}=\sigma(\{A_{1},\ldots,A_{\ell}\})
the minimal sub-
\sigma
-algebra of

\mathcal{M}
containing all
A_{i}
,
i=1,\ldots,\ell
.

Note that the
\sigma
-algebra
\mathcal{A}
can be represented as

\mathcal{A}\ =\ \bigl\{\bigcup_{i=1}^{\ell}(A_{i}\cap M_{i}):M_{1},\ldots,M_{\ell}\in\{\emptyset,\Omega\}\bigr\},

(1a)

while the minimal
\sigma
-algebra
\sigma(\mathcal{H}\cup\mathcal{A})
that contains both
\mathcal{H}
and

\mathcal{A}
can be written as

\sigma(\mathcal{H}\cup\mathcal{A})\ =\ \bigl\{\bigcup_{i=1}^{\ell}(A_{i}\cap H_{i}):H_{1},\ldots,H_{\ell}\in\mathcal{H}\bigr\}.

(1b)

Definition 3.3
.

Under Assumption
3.1
, define the following class-conditional distributions,
by letting for
M\in\mathcal{M}
and

i=1,\ldots,\ell

P_{i}[M]=P[M\,|\,A_{i}]=\frac{P[A_{i}\cap M]}{P[A_{i}]}\quad\text{and}\quad Q_{i}[M]=Q[M\,|\,A_{i}]=\frac{Q[A_{i}\cap M]}{Q[A_{i}]}.

(2)

In the literature, when restricted to the feature information set
\mathcal{H}
, the
P_{i}
and
Q_{i}

sometimes are called
class-conditional feature distributions
.

The situation where
P[M]\neq Q[M]
holds for at least one
M\in\sigma(\mathcal{H}\cup\mathcal{A})

is called
dataset shift

(Definition 1 of Moreno-Torres et al.
[
14
]
)
and is also known as ‘distribution shift’ or ‘population drift’.
Dataset shift is called “joint distribution shift” by
Chen et al.
[
4
]
in the case where the joint distribution of features and
labels is shifted in contrast
to shifts of one of the marginal distributions of the labels or features only.

The following special type of dataset shift, ‘sparse joint shift’, was
introduced by Chen et al.
[
4
]
, in a slightly less general way.

Definition 3.4

(Sparse Joint Shift)
.

Under Assumption
3.1
, assume that
\mathcal{F}
is a sub-
\sigma
-algebra of

\mathcal{H}
. Then the target distribution

Q
is related to the source distribution
P
through
(\mathcal{F},\mathcal{H})
-
sparse joint shift
(SJS)
if for all
H\in\mathcal{H}
and
i\in\{1,\ldots,\ell\}
it holds that

Q_{i}[H\,|\,\mathcal{F}]\ =\ P_{i}[H\,|\,\mathcal{F}].

(3)

(
3
) is understood in the sense that for each
H\in\mathcal{H}
and each
i=1,\ldots,\ell

there is an
\mathcal{F}
-measurable random variable
Z
such that for all
F\in\mathcal{F}
it holds that

P_{i}[F\cap H]=E_{P_{i}}[\mathbf{1}_{F}\,Z]\qquad\text{and}\qquad Q_{i}[F\cap H]=E_{Q_{i}}[\mathbf{1}_{F}\,Z].

Remark 3.5
.

Observe that if
Q
is related to the source distribution
P
through
(\mathcal{F},\mathcal{H})
-SJS then

Q
also is related to the source distribution
P
through
(\mathcal{F},\mathcal{H}^{\prime})
-SJS for
any sub-
\sigma
-algebra
\mathcal{H}^{\prime}
of
\mathcal{H}
with
\mathcal{H}^{\prime}\supset\mathcal{F}
.
In the following, we use the short hand
\mathcal{F}
-SJS for
(\mathcal{F},\mathcal{H})
-SJS
whenever there is no risk of ambiguity. ∎

Remark 3.6
.

Definition
3.4
can be reconciled with the definition of sparse joint shift of Definition 1 of
Chen et al.
[
4
]
as follows:

•

Consider a feature vector
\mathbf{X}=(X_{1},\dots,X_{d})
mapping

•

\mathbf{X}

Chen et al. describe

However, Definition

,

denotes the usual Euclidean norm

Note that (
3

(4a)

since thanks to the generalized Bayes formula (Theorem 10.8 of Klebaner

]
) we have

\mu_{i}[H\,|\,\mathcal{F}]=\frac{\mu[H\cap A_{i}\,|\,\mathcal{F}]}{\mu[A_{i}\,|\,\mathcal{F}]}

\sum_{i=1}^{\ell}\mathbf{1}_{A_{i}}\,\mu_{i}[H\,|\,\mathcal{F}]\ =\ \mu\bigl[H\,|\,\sigma(\mathcal{F}\cup\mathcal{A})\bigr],

(4b)

where
\mu
is
P
or
Q
.

Consider the following two special cases in Definition
3.4
:

•

\mathcal{F}=\mathcal{H}
. Then (
3
) is equivalent to
\mathbf{1}_{H}=\mathbf{1}_{H}
such
that under Assumption

each target distribution

is related to the source
distribution

through
\mathcal{H}
-SJS. Hence,

is not particularly meaningful in this case but it does no harm to include it

•

\mathcal{F}=\{\emptyset,\Omega\}
. Then (

) is equivalent to

Q_{i}[H]\ =\ P_{i}[H],\quad\text{for}\ i=1,\ldots,\ell\ \text{and}\ H\in\mathcal{H}.

Hence

-SJS is nothing else but prior probability shift in the sense of
Definition 3 of Moreno-Torres et al.
[
14
]
.

If
Q
and
P
from Assumption
3.1
are mutually singular, i.e. there is
an event
\Omega^{\prime}\in\mathcal{M}
such that
P[\Omega^{\prime}]=1
and
Q[\Omega^{\prime}]=0
, then
(
3
) holds true for any
\mathcal{F}\subset\mathcal{H}
because conditional
probabilities are uniquely defined only up to events of probability

. Thus it makes
sense to exclude this case by an additional assumption of absolute continuity (as implicitly also did

[
4
]
) which basically means that impossible events under the source distribution
are also impossible under the target distribution.
With this natural assumption,
important properties of SJS can be proved. See Appendix

for

Assumption 3.7
.

Assumption
3.1
holds, and
Q
is absolutely continuous with respect to
P
on

\sigma(\mathcal{H}\cup\mathcal{A})
, i.e.,

Q|\sigma(\mathcal{H}\cup\mathcal{A})\ \ll\ P|\sigma(\mathcal{H}\cup\mathcal{A}),

where
\mu|\sigma(\mathcal{H}\cup\mathcal{A})
stands for the measure
\mu\in\{P,Q\}
with domain restricted to

\sigma(\mathcal{H}\cup\mathcal{A})
.

Why requesting
Q\ll P
instead of
P\ll\mu
and
Q\ll\mu
as is common in the machine learning
literature, for instance with
\mu=
Lebesgue measure? Doing so has a couple of advantages:

•

The notation is simplified.

•

Q\ll P
is implied by
P\ll\mu
and
Q\ll\mu

if
\mu\left[\frac{dP}{d\mu}=0,\frac{dQ}{d\mu}>0\right]=0
.
This is a natural assumption, see the discussion in Tasche
[
22
]
.

•

Special mathematical tools are available, e.g. the Radon-Nikodym theorem or the conditional
expectation operator for determining densities on sub-
\sigma
-algebras.

In practical applications, under Assumption
3.7
, the density
\bar{h}=\frac{dQ}{dP}

must be estimated as density
ratio
\frac{dQ}{d\mu}/\frac{dP}{d\mu}
. For literature on this, see
Sugiyama et al.
[
19
]
and
references therein.

.

Under Assumption

, suppose that

is a sub-

-algebra of

. Then the following three statements are equivalent:

(i)

is related to

through
\mathcal{F}
-SJS in the sense of Definition

.

(ii)

For each

\mathcal{H}
-measurable non-negative random variable

it holds that

(5a)

(iii)

There is a sub-
\sigma
-algebra
\mathcal{G}
of

\mathcal{H}
such that
\mathcal{H}=\sigma(\mathcal{F}\cup\mathcal{G})
and

(5b)

4.
Analyses of sparse joint shift

In the following, we assume that the target distribution
Q
is absolutely continuous with respect
to
P
. By allowing us to take recourse to results from Tasche
[
22
]
, this assumption
facilitates thorough analyses of SJS.

4.1.
Characterising SJS

By Lemma 1 of Tasche
[
22
]
, under Assumption
3.7
the target
conditional feature distributions

Q_{i}|\mathcal{H}
,
i=1,\ldots,\ell
, are absolutely continuous with respect to the source conditional feature
distributions
P_{i}|\mathcal{H}
,
i=1,\ldots,\ell
. For the respective densities, we write

h_{i}\ =\ \frac{dQ_{i}|\mathcal{H}}{dP_{i}|\mathcal{H}},\qquad i=1,\ldots,\ell.

(6a)

By Theorem 1 of Tasche
[
22
]
, under Assumption
3.7
the density

\bar{h}
of
Q
with
respect to
P
on the sub-
\sigma
-algebra
\sigma(\mathcal{H}\cup\mathcal{A})
of

can be represented as

\bar{h}\ =\ \sum_{i=1}^{\ell}h_{i}\,\frac{Q[A_{i}]}{P[A_{i}]}\,\mathbf{1}_{A_{i}}.

(6b)

(Equivalent conditions for SJS)
.

Under Assumption
3.7
, let
\mathcal{F}
be a sub-
\sigma
-algebra of

\mathcal{H}
. Then the following three
statements are equivalent:

(i)

The target distribution

is related to the source distribution

through
\mathcal{F}
-SJS.

(ii)

For each

, there exists an
\mathcal{F}
-measurable density

of

with respect to the source conditional feature

on
\mathcal{H}
.

(iii)

There exists a

-measurable density
\bar{f}
of

with respect to

on
\sigma(\mathcal{H}\cup\mathcal{A})
.

Moreover, if

is related to the source distribution

through
\mathcal{F}
-SJS then

mentioned in (iii) can be represented as

\bar{f}\ =\ \sum_{i=1}^{\ell}f_{i}\,\frac{Q[A_{i}]}{P[A_{i}]}\,\mathbf{1}_{A_{i}},

with

,

Theorem

is a rather straightforward consequence of the following observation.

Lemma 4.2
.

Let
Q
and
P
be probability measures on the measurable space
(\Omega,\mathcal{M})
. Suppose that

Q
is absolutely continuous with respect to
P
on
\mathcal{M}
and that

\mathcal{G}
is a sub-
\sigma
-algebra of
\mathcal{M}
. Then the two following statements are
equivalent:

(i)

For all
M\in\mathcal{M}
, it holds that
P[M\,|\,\mathcal{G}]=Q[M\,|\,\mathcal{G}]
.

(ii)

There exists a
\mathcal{G}
-measurable density
g
of
Q
with respect to
P
on
\mathcal{M}
.

If
\varphi
is any
\mathcal{M}
-measurable density of
Q
with respect to
P
on
\mathcal{M}
and
(i) or (ii) are true, then
g=E_{P}[\varphi\,|\,\mathcal{G}]
is a density of
Q
with respect to
P
on
\mathcal{M}
.

Proof of Lemma
4.2
.

Assume that
P[M\,|\,\mathcal{G}]=Q[M\,|\,\mathcal{G}]
is true for all
M\in\mathcal{M}
. Choose any
density
\varphi
of
Q
with respect to
P
on
\mathcal{M}
and define
g=E_{P}[\varphi\,|\,\mathcal{G}]
.
For any
M\in\mathcal{M}
then we obtain

Q[M]=E_{Q}\bigl[Q[M\,|\,\mathcal{G}]\bigr]=E_{P}\bigl[\varphi\,Q[M\,|\,\mathcal{G}]\bigr]=\\
E_{P}\bigl[\varphi\,P[M\,|\,\mathcal{G}]\bigr]=E_{P}\bigl[g\,P[M\,|\,\mathcal{G}]\bigr]=E_{P}[g\,\mathbf{1}_{M}].

This proves (i)
\Rightarrow
(ii) and the statement on how to choose

.

Assume now that (ii) is true. Fix
M\in\mathcal{M}
. For any
G\in\mathcal{G}
then we obtain

E_{Q}\bigl[\mathbf{1}_{G}\,P[M\,|\,\mathcal{G}]\bigr]=E_{P}\bigl[g\,\mathbf{1}_{G}\,P[M\,|\,\mathcal{G}]\bigr]=E_{P}[g\,\mathbf{1}_{G\cap M}]=Q[G\cap M].

By the definition of conditional probability, this implies (ii)
\Rightarrow
(i).
∎

4.2.

Lemma 4.6
.

Let
(\Omega,\mathcal{M},\mu)
a probability space,
\mathcal{G}
a sub-
\sigma
-algebra of
\mathcal{M}
, and

A_{1},\ldots,A_{\ell}\in\mathcal{M}
a decomposition of

\begin{pmatrix}E_{\mu}[X_{1}\,|\,\mathcal{G}]\\

\mu[A_{\ell}\,|\,\mathcal{G}]\end{pmatrix}.

for a proof of Lemma

(Identifiability)
.

The sufficient condition for identifiability presented in Theorem 1 Chen et al.

involves picking a set of

for

How strong is Theorem

whether we can find

-measurable non-negative random variables

Denote by

\begin{split}&C_{i}\in\mathcal{H}\ \text{for all}\ i=1,\ldots,\ell,\\
&C_{1},\ldots,C_{\ell}\ \text{is a disjoint decomposition of}\ \Omega,\ \text{and}\\
&A_{i}\ \text{is predicted when}\ C_{i}\ \text{is observed.}\end{split}

Intuitively,

A similar choice is

.

In the setting of Theorem

The definition of sufficiency is formally given below as we are having a closer look at the relationship between

4.3.
SJS vs. covariate shift

In this section, we show that the relationship between SJS and covariate shift is intertwined with the
question of whether or not the target
marginal distribution of the features
Q|\mathcal{H}
has got an
\mathcal{F}
-measurable
density with respect to the source unconditional feature distribution
P|\mathcal{H}
, or in other words
if
P
and
Q
are related through
\mathcal{F}
-conditional distribution invariance.

Definition 4.13

(Covariate shift)
.

Under Assumption
3.1
, let
\mathcal{F}
be a sub-
\sigma
-algebra of
\mathcal{H}
. Then
the source distribution
P
and the target distribution
Q
are related through
\mathcal{F}
-
covariate shift

if it holds that

Q[A_{i}\,|\,\mathcal{F}]\ =\ P[A_{i}\,|\,\mathcal{F}],\qquad\text{for all}\ i=1,\ldots,\ell.

(17)

The most relevant case in Definition
4.13
is incurred with
\mathcal{F}=\mathcal{H}
. However,
as we will see below, in the context of SJS also the case of
\mathcal{F}
-covariate shift with a proper

\sigma
-algebra
\mathcal{F}
of
\mathcal{H}
is of interest.

Chen et al.
[
4
]
showed in Theorem 2 by example that in general
SJS does neither imply prior probability shift nor
covariate shift. In the following example – which is a slight modification of the example of
Chen et al. – we show that SJS and covariate shift can be present at the same time in
proper distribution shift, i.e. in shift where source and target distribution truly differ.
Moreover, in Example
4.15
below we point out that covariate shift does not
imply SJS. These three observations together motivate
the results presented in this section, regarding the triangle-relationship of covariate shift, SJS and
conditional distribution invariance as introduced in Definition
4.11
.

5.
How to estimate sparse joint shift?

Chen et al.
[
4
]
proposed two estimation approaches for the weight function that
corresponds to the density
\bar{h}=\frac{dQ}{dP}
in the setting of this paper.
In the following, we begin with discussing their estimation strategy in general in Section
5.1
.
Then we continue with descriptions of their approach SEES-c in Section
5.2

and of their approach SEES-d in Section
5.3
below,
and show how these approaches can be derived in the setting of this article.

5.1.
The general approach

The general estimation approach proposed by Chen et al.
[
4
]
can be described this way:
Approximate the observed feature density
\widehat{h}

(on the target dataset) by the hypothetical density
h_{\mathcal{F}}
it would have under
\mathcal{F}
-SJS.
In the following, we present this approach in mathematical terms.

In the context of Assumption
3.7
, let
\mathcal{F}
be a sub-
\sigma
-algebra of
\mathcal{H}

and keep the source distribution
P
fixed. Define

S(\mathcal{F})\ =\ \left\{h_{Q}=\frac{dQ|\mathcal{H}}{dP|\mathcal{H}}:Q\ll P,\ Q\ \text{related to}\ P\ \text{through}\ \mathcal{F}\text{-SJS}\right\}.

(20a)

In the following, we make use of the fact that
as a consequence of (
7
) densities
h_{Q}\in S(\mathcal{F})
can be represented as

h_{Q}=\sum_{i=1}^{\ell}f_{i}\,\frac{Q[A_{i}]}{P[A_{i}]}\,P[A_{i}\,|\,\mathcal{H}],

(20b)

where
f_{i}
,
i=1,\ldots,\ell
, is an
\mathcal{F}
-measurable density of the target class-conditional
feature distribution
Q_{i}
with respect to the source class-conditional feature distribution
P_{i}

(see Definition
3.3
) on
\mathcal{H}
.

Assume that
\widehat{h}
is a density of the observed target feature distribution
\widehat{Q}|\mathcal{H}

with respect to the (also known) source feature distribution
P|\mathcal{H}
.
The task is to approximate
\widehat{h}
as best as possible by an element
h_{Q}
of
S(\mathcal{F})
. Hence
the following optimisation problem is to be solved:

h_{Q}^{\ast}\ =\ \arg\min\limits_{h_{Q}\in S(\mathcal{F})}D(\widehat{h},h_{Q}),

(21)

for some distance measure
D
for densities. Together with (
20b
), this is the representation
of Eq. (4.1) of Chen et al.
[
4
]
translated into the notions and notation of this paper.
An optimal density
h_{Q}^{\ast}
is specified by the ingredients
f_{i}
,
Q[A_{i}]
,
P[A_{i}]

and
P[A_{i}\,|\,\mathcal{H}]
,
i=1,\ldots,\ell
, of (
20b
). The
P[A_{i}]
and
P[A_{i}\,|\,\mathcal{H}]

are supposed to be known from the source dataset. In any case, once the
f_{i}
and the
Q[A_{i}]

have been determined, the full target distribution density
\bar{h}_{Q}
on
\sigma(\mathcal{H}\cup\mathcal{A})

is given by the right-hand side of (
7
).

5.2.
Minimising the Kullback-Leibler divergence

Chen et al.
[
4
]
made two proposals for the choice of
D
in (
21
). The first
proposal, called SEES-c, is to make use of the Kullback-Leibler (KL) divergence.

Definition 5.1
.

Assume that
\pi
and
\kappa
are probability measures on a measurable space
(\Omega,\mathcal{M})
,
with densities
p
and

q
respectively with regard to another measure
\mu
on
(\Omega,\mathcal{M})
. Then the
KL divergence
of

p
and
q
is defined as

D_{KL}(p\|q)\ =\ \int p\,\log\left(\frac{p}{q}\right)d\mu\ =\ E_{\pi}\left[\log\left(\frac{p}{q}\right)\right],

with
0\,\log(0)\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mathrm{def}}}{{=}}0
and
D_{KL}(p\|q)=\infty

if
\kappa(M)=0<\pi(M)
for any
M\in\mathcal{M}
(i.e. if
\pi
is not
absolutely continuous with respect to
\kappa
).

Note that in general
D_{KL}(p\|q)\neq D_{KL}(q\|p)
. By Jensen’s inequality,

D_{KL}(p\|q)
is non-negative and equals
0
if and only if
\pi
-almost surely it holds that
p=q
.

Writing down
D_{KL}
with
\mu=P
,
\pi=\widehat{Q}|\mathcal{H}
, as well as

\kappa=Q|\mathcal{H}
and making use of (
20b
) gives

\displaystyle D_{KL}(\widehat{h}\|h_{Q})

\displaystyle\ =\ E_{\widehat{Q}}\left[\log\left(\frac{\widehat{h}}{\sum_{i=1}^{\ell}f_{i}\,\frac{Q[A_{i}]}{P[A_{i}]}\,P[A_{i}\,|\,\mathcal{H}]}\right)\right]

\displaystyle\ =\ E_{\widehat{Q}}[\log(\widehat{h})]-E_{\widehat{Q}}\left[\log\left(\sum\nolimits_{i=1}^{\ell}f_{i}\,\tfrac{Q[A_{i}]}{P[A_{i}]}\,P[A_{i}\,|\,\mathcal{H}]\right)\right].

(22a)

On the right-hand side of (
22a
), when optimisation over
h_{Q}
is intended,

E_{\widehat{Q}}[\log(\widehat{h})]
is a constant and
can be ignored. As a consequence, estimation of
\widehat{h}
in (
21
) can be avoided
for
D=D_{KL}
. In the second term on the right-hand side of (
22a
),
the
P[A_{i}]
and
P[A_{i}\,|\,\mathcal{H}]
are observable and can be
treated as known quantities such that the
f_{i}
and
Q[A_{i}]
are left as variables whose values are to be optimally
chosen.

If
\widehat{\varphi}_{i}
denotes an estimator for
f_{i}\,Q[A_{i}]
, the
\widehat{\varphi}_{i}
must be

\mathcal{F}
-measurable and satisfy the constraints

E_{P_{i}}[\widehat{\varphi}_{i}]\ =\ Q[A_{i}],\qquad i=1,\ldots,\ell.

(22b)

This implies

1\ =\ \sum_{i=1}^{\ell}E_{P_{i}}[\widehat{\varphi}_{i}]\ =\ E_{P}\left[\sum_{i=1}^{\ell}\widehat{\varphi}_{i}\,\frac{P[A_{i}\,|\,\mathcal{H}]}{P[A_{i}]}\right].

(22c)

Combining (
22a
) and (
22c
) for the specification of (
21
) with

D=D_{KL}
gives the optimisation problem

\begin{split}\max\limits_{\widehat{\varphi}_{1},\ldots,\widehat{\varphi}_{\ell}\geq 0,\,\mathcal{F}\text{-measurable}}E_{\widehat{Q}}\left[\log\left(\sum_{i=1}^{\ell}\widehat{\varphi}_{i}\,\tfrac{P[A_{i}\,|\,\mathcal{H}]}{P[A_{i}]}\right)\right],&\\
\text{subject to:}\quad E_{P}\left[\sum_{i=1}^{\ell}\widehat{\varphi}_{i}\,\frac{P[A_{i}\,|\,\mathcal{H}]}{P[A_{i}]}\right]\ =\ 1.&\end{split}

(23)

Observe that in (
23
) the expectation
E_{P}
can be approximated as a sample mean on
the source dataset while
E_{\widehat{Q}}
can be approximated as a sample mean on the target dataset.
Once optimal
\varphi_{1}^{\ast},\ldots,\varphi_{\ell}^{\ast}
have been found, estimates of the target
class prior probabilities
Q[A_{i}]
can be determined by means of (
22b
), with the expectations

E_{P_{i}}
replaced by sample means on the strata of the source dataset defined by the observed class labels.

(
23
) corresponds to the optimisation objective for SEES-c of Chen et al.
[
4
]

in the case where
m
and
I
in Definition 1 of Chen et al.
[
4
]
are fixed (case of fixed
shift index set).
In the paragraph ‘SEES-c’, Chen et al.
[
4
]
suggest keeping

\mathcal{F}
(i.e. the set of input variables
to the functions
\widehat{\varphi}_{i}
) variable in (
23
) in order to be able to
find an ‘optimal’
\mathcal{F}
.
For achieving this, Chen et al. add a term on the right-hand side of (
23
) that
rewards solutions with low numbers of input features.

However, it is not clear that better fit by
\mathcal{F}
than by
\mathcal{F}^{\prime}
implies that
\mathcal{F}
-SJS is
a more appropriate model than
\mathcal{F}^{\prime}
-SJS. For instance, assuming straightforward covariate shift would
give an exact fit. Similarly, with ‘factorizable joint shift’, exact fit could even be achieved for arbitrary target
label distributions (in the binary case, see Proposition 2 of Tasche
[
22
]
).
Moreover, Chen et al.
[
4
]
do not provide the details of how they identify the set of features
to explain the SJS once an optimal solution to their version of (
23
) has been found.
Remark
4.4
above suggests that picking the features with the
d-1
largest contributions

\hat{\beta}_{i}
as defined in Section 4.2 of Chen et al.
[
4
]
would
be a theoretically sound approach.

5.3.
Second estimation strategy

The second proposal of Chen et al.
[
4
]
for implementing (
21
) is
intended to apply to the case where
all the features are discrete (or have been discretised). The proposal is not straightforward at first glance because

which in the context of their SEES-d approach appears to be a hard classifier as in (

In abstract terms, the rationale behind the SEES-d approach of Chen et al.
[
4
]

can be described as follows:

In practice, one issue with the optimisation problem (
21

that
\widehat{h}
and the

As a workaround for this problem, Chen et al.
[

]
suggest replacing the density

as defined by (

defined

where

\sigma
-algebra of

\mathcal{H}

\sigma
-algebra generated by a lower-dimensional set of features.

(
21

h_{Q}^{\ast}\ =\ \arg\min\limits_{h_{Q}\in S(\mathcal{F},\mathcal{H}^{\prime})}D(\widehat{h}^{\prime},h_{Q}),

(24b)

where
\widehat{h}^{\prime}
is a density of
\widehat{Q}|\mathcal{H}^{\prime}
with respect to

which can be represented as

\widehat{h}^{\prime}\ =\ E_{P}[\widehat{h}\,|\,\mathcal{H}^{\prime}].

(25)

Assume that the probability measure
\widehat{Q}
whose
\mathcal{H}
-density
\widehat{h}

appears in (

are related through
\mathcal{F}
-SJS. Then it follows that

and

has the representation

while in the second case, thanks to (

6.
Conclusions

Modelling dataset shift with sparse joint shift (SJS) as proposed by Chen et al.
[
4
]

is a promising approach to inferring the full joint distribution of features and labels of
a target dataset for which only the features have been observed. The approach is based on learning from
a source dataset with complete information on features and labels. It represents a proper alternative to
the popular inference approaches based on assumptions of prior probability and covariate shift respectively.

In this paper, we have revisited and complemented the theory for SJS in some aspects, in particular
with regard to the transmission of SJS from sets of features to larger sets of features, a conditional
correction formula for the class posterior probabilities, identifiability of SJS, and
the relationship between SJS and covariate shift.

In addition, with regard to the estimation of the shift characteristics in practice, we have
complemented the rationale for the algorithms proposed by Chen et al.
[
4
]
and pointed out
inconsistencies which could prevent them from identifying optimal solutions, while at the same
time suggesting improvements to avoid these issues.

Appendix A
Some concepts and notation from probability theory

The general approach to the subject in this paper is based on application of
probabilistic measure theory, as described for instance in the textbooks of
Billingsley
[
3
]
and Klenke
[
13
]
.

For quick reference, here is a brief glossary of concepts and notation frequently used
in the paper:

Measurable space
(\Omega,\mathcal{M})
.
According to Billingsley
[
3
]
(p. 16)
“In probability theory
\Omega
consists of all the possible results or outcomes

\omega
of an experiment or observation.”
\mathcal{M}
is a
\sigma
-
algebra

(also called
\sigma
-field), i.e. a family of
subsets of
\Omega
with the following three properties:

(i)

\emptyset\in\mathcal{M}
,

(ii)

M\in\mathcal{M}\Rightarrow\Omega\setminus M\in\mathcal{M}
.

(iii)

M_{n}\in\mathcal{M}
for
n\in\mathbb{N}

\Rightarrow

\bigcup_{n\in\mathbb{N}}M_{n}\in\mathcal{M}
.

The intersections of
\sigma
-algebras again are
\sigma
-algebras. Is
\mathcal{M}_{0}
is any family of
subsets of
\Omega
, the (non-empty) intersection of all
\sigma
-algebras on
\Omega
that contain

\mathcal{M}_{0}
is called
\sigma
-
algebra generated by

\mathcal{M}_{0}
and denoted by
\sigma(\mathcal{M}_{0})
.
The
\sigma
-algebra
\sigma(\mathcal{M}_{0})
is also the smallest
\sigma
-algebra containing
\mathcal{M}_{0}
.

Probability space
(\Omega,\mathcal{M},P)
.

(\Omega,\mathcal{M})
is a measurable space,

P
is a
probability measure
on
(\Omega,\mathcal{M})
, i.e. a measure
(see Chapter 2 of Billingsley
[
3
]
) with
P[\Omega]=1
.
For
M\in\mathcal{M}
,
P[M]
is the

probability of the event

M
. A property of outcomes
\omega\in\Omega
is said
to hold
P
-

if
the event of all the
\omega
with the property has probability
1
under
P
.

Mappings and random variables.
For a fixed set
S
, the function
\mathbf{1}_{S}
with

\mathbf{1}_{S}(s)=1
for
s\in S
and

\mathbf{1}_{S}(s)=0
for
s\notin S
is called
indicator function
.

A mapping
X:(\Omega,\mathcal{M})\to(\Omega^{\prime},\mathcal{M}^{\prime})
is
\mathcal{M}^{\prime}
-
\mathcal{M}
-

if

.

A real-valued measurable mapping

defined on a probability space

or

Information set.

\sigma
-algebras, on the one hand, are needed for consistent definitions of
integrals and probabilities. On the other hand, they are of interest because they can be used to reflect
available information. If

For these reasons,

Absolute continuity and densities.
Let
(\Omega,\mathcal{M})
be a measurable space and
\mu
and

be measures on
(\Omega,\mathcal{M})
. The measure
\mu

By the Radon-Nikodym theorem (Theorem 32.2 of Billingsley
[

]

-finite then

-measurable function

.

Let

and

We use short hand

needed for the description of the model:

•

Posterior probabilities conditional on
\mathcal{H}
, for
i=1,\ldots,\ell
:

P[A_{i}\,|\,\mathcal{H}]\ =\ \sum_{(n,r)\in I_{\mathcal{H}}}\mathbf{1}_{H_{n,r}}\,P[A_{i}\,|\,H_{n,r}].

•

Density
h_{i}
,
i=1,\ldots,\ell
, of
Q_{i}
with respect to
P_{i}
on
\mathcal{H}
,
without making use of the SJS assumption:

h_{i}\ =\ \sum_{(n,r)\in I_{\mathcal{H}}}\mathbf{1}_{H_{n,r}}\,\frac{Q[H_{n,r}\,|\,A_{i}]}{P[H_{n,r}\,|\,A_{i}]}\ =\ \sum_{(n,r)\in I_{\mathcal{H}}}\mathbf{1}_{H_{n,r}}\,\frac{Q_{i}[H_{n,r}]}{P_{i}[H_{n,r}]}.

•

Density
f_{i}
,
i=1,\ldots,\ell
, of
Q_{i}
with respect to
P_{i}
on
\mathcal{H}
under
the SJS assumption:

f_{i}\ =\ \sum_{n\in\mathbb{N}}\mathbf{1}_{F_{N}}\,\frac{Q_{i}[F_{n}]}{P_{i}[F_{n}]}\ =\ \sum_{n\in\mathbb{N}}\mathbf{1}_{F_{N}}\,E_{P_{i}}[h_{i}\,|\,F_{n}].

•

Density
h
of
Q
with respect to
P
on
\mathcal{H}
,
without making use of the SJS assumption:

h\ =\ \sum_{(n,r)\in I_{\mathcal{H}}}\mathbf{1}_{H_{n,r}}\,\frac{Q[H_{n,r}]}{P[H_{n,r}]}.

•

h_{Q}
from (
20b
) in the discrete setting (i.e. the density
h_{Q}
of
Q
with respect to
P
on
\mathcal{H}
,
explicitly making use of the SJS assumption):

\displaystyle h_{Q}

\displaystyle=\sum_{i=1}^{\ell}\left(\sum_{n\in\mathbb{N}}\mathbf{1}_{F_{N}}\,\frac{Q_{i}[F_{n}]}{P_{i}[F_{n}]}\right)\frac{Q[A_{i}]}{P[A_{i}]}\Big(\sum_{(n,r)\in I_{\mathcal{H}}}\mathbf{1}_{H_{n,r}}\,P[A_{i}\,|\,H_{n,r}]\Big)

\displaystyle=\sum_{i=1}^{\ell}\sum_{n\in\mathbb{N}}\frac{Q[F_{n}\cap A_{i}]}{P[F_{n}\cap A_{i}]}\left(\sum_{r=1}^{r_{n}}\mathbf{1}_{H_{n,r}}\,P[A_{i}\,|\,H_{n,r}]\right).

•

Difference of
h
and
h_{Q}
:

h-h_{Q}\ =\ \sum_{(n,r)\in I_{\mathcal{H}}}\mathbf{1}_{H_{n,r}}\left(\frac{Q[H_{n,r}]}{P[H_{n,r}]}-\sum_{i=1}^{\ell}\frac{Q[F_{n}\cap A_{i}]}{P[F_{n}\cap A_{i}]}\,P[A_{i}\,|\,H_{n,r}]\right).

(35)

Consequence of (
35
): On the event
F_{n}
, it holds that

(h-h_{Q})\,\mathbf{1}_{F_{n}}\ =\ \sum_{r=1}^{r_{n}}\mathbf{1}_{H_{n,r}}\left(\frac{Q[H_{n,r}]}{P[H_{n,r}]}-\sum_{i=1}^{\ell}Q[F_{n}\cap A_{i}]\,\frac{P[A_{i}\,|\,H_{n,r}]}{P[F_{n}\cap A_{i}]}\right).

Hence, for each
n\in\mathbb{N}
, the unobservable probabilities
Q[F_{n}\cap A_{1}],\ldots,Q[F_{n}\cap A_{\ell}]

must satisfy the following system of
r_{n}
linear equations for
\ell
unknowns:

\sum_{i=1}^{\ell}Q[F_{n}\cap A_{i}]\,\frac{P[A_{i}\,|\,H_{n,r}]}{P[F_{n}\cap A_{i}]}\ =\ \frac{Q[H_{n,r}]}{P[H_{n,r}]},\qquad r=1,\ldots,r_{n}.

(36)

Note that once the
Q[F_{n}\cap A_{i}]
have been determined for all
n\in\mathbb{N}
,
the target class priors are obtained by summing them up:

Q[A_{i}]\ =\ \sum_{n\in\mathbb{N}}Q[F_{n}\cap A_{i}],\qquad i=1,\ldots,\ell.

(37a)

Moreover, evoking Proposition
4.5
gives for all
(n,r)\in I_{\mathcal{H}}
and

i=1,\ldots,\ell
the
conditional posterior correction formula
in the discrete case:

Q[A_{i}\,|\,H_{n,r}]\ =\ \frac{\frac{Q[A_{i}\cap F_{n}]}{P[A_{i}\cap F_{n}]}\,P[A_{i}\,|\,H_{n,r}]}{\sum_{j=1}^{\ell}\frac{Q[A_{j}\cap F_{n}]}{P[A_{j}\cap F_{n}]}\,P[A_{j}\,|\,H_{n,r}]}.

(37b)

As alluded to in Section
5.3
above,
in order to have any chance of uniqueness of the solution to (
36
) (for fixed
n\in\mathbb{N}
),
the number of equations ought to be at least equal to or greater than the number of unknowns. Hence
r_{n}\geq\ell

is a natural requirement. Depending on the nature of the features, this requirement might not be satisfied, in
particular if the original feature information set
\mathcal{H}
is replaced by some subset
\mathcal{H}^{\prime}\supset\mathcal{F}
for efficiency reasons like the one that problem (
24b
) has to be solved instead of
(
21
).

Chen et al.
[
4
]
deal with this issue by introducing a (hard) classifier
\mathbf{C}=(C_{1},\ldots,C_{\ell})
as in (
12
).
They then consider an augmented sub-
\sigma
-algebra
\mathcal{H}_{\mathcal{C}}=\sigma(\mathcal{H}^{\prime}\cup\mathcal{C})

of
\mathcal{H}
. Substituting this in (
36
) produces the equation system

\sum_{i=1}^{\ell}Q[F_{n}\cap A_{i}]\,\frac{P[A_{i}\,|\,H_{n,r}\cap C_{j}]}{P[F_{n}\cap A_{i}]}\ =\ \frac{Q[H_{n,r}\cap C_{j}]}{P[H_{n,r}\cap C_{j}]},\qquad r=1,\ldots,r_{n},j=1,\ldots,\ell.

(38)

The special choice
\mathcal{H}_{\mathcal{C}}=\sigma(\mathcal{F}\cup\mathcal{C})
in (
38
)
gives a conditional version of the confusion matrix approach to class prior estimation
(Saerens et al.
[
15
]
) in the discrete setting:

\sum_{i=1}^{\ell}Q[F_{n}\cap A_{i}]\,\frac{P[A_{i}\,|\,F_{n}\cap C_{j}]}{P[F_{n}\cap A_{i}]}\ =\ \frac{Q[F_{n}\cap C_{j}]}{P[F_{n}\cap C_{j}]},\qquad j=1,\ldots,\ell.

(39)

By combining (
37a
) and (
39
), a two-step approach to class prior estimation
under (proper) sparse joint shift can be implemented.
