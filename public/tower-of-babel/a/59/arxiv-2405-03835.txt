2.
Introduction

The classical work of Wilson
[
20
]
was the first contribution to the theory of Wilson’s functional equation

f(x+y)+f(x-y)=2f(x)g(y),\quad x,y\in\mathbb{R},

which is a generalization of the cosine functional equation

g(x+y)+g(x-y)=2g(x)g(y),\quad x,y\in\mathbb{R}.

(2.1)

Aczel’s monograph
[
1
, Section 3.2.1 and Section 3.2.2]
discusses the real valued, continuous solutions of Wilson’s equation and contains references to earlier works. These functional equations have been extended to abelian groups, and they have been solved on that setting. An extension of Wilson’s functional equation above is treated by Stetkær in
[
12
, Corollary 5]
who derived the solution formulas for the functional equation

f(x+y)-f(x+\sigma(y))=2g(x)h(y),\quad x,y\in G,

on an abelian group
G
, where
\sigma:G\rightarrow G
is an involution. The d’Alembert’s functional equation

f(xy)+f(x\sigma(y))=2f(x)f(y),\quad x,y\in S,

where
\sigma:S\rightarrow S
is an involutive anti-automorphism (i.e,
\sigma(xy)=\sigma(y)\sigma(x)
and
\sigma(\sigma(x))=x
for all
x,y\in S
) has been solved on groups by Davison
[
6
]
and others. See for example
[
14
, Chapter 9]
. It was also studied on semigroups by Stetkær
[
18
]
.

In
[
15
]
, Stetkær solved the variant of d’Alembert’s functional equation

f(xy)+f(\sigma(y)x)=2f(x)f(y),\quad x,y\in S,

on a semigroup
S
, where
\sigma
is an involutive automorphism. The solutions are abelian and are of the form
f=\dfrac{\chi+\chi^{*}}{2}
, where
\chi:S\rightarrow\mathbb{C}
is a multiplicative function. Elqorachi and Redouani
[
8
]
obtained the solutions of the variant of Wilson’s functional equation

f(xy)+\tau(y)f(\sigma(y)x)=2f(x)g(y),\quad x,y\in S,

(2.2)

on groups, where
\sigma:S\rightarrow S
is an involutive automorphism, and
\tau:S\rightarrow\mathbb{C}
is a multiplicative function such that
\tau(x\sigma(x))=1
for all
x\in S
. It was also solved on groups with
\tau=1
by Fadli et al.
[
9
]
. The results were extended to semigroups generated by their squares by Ajebbar and Elqorachi
[
2
]
. Recently, Aserrar, Chahbi and Elqorachi
[
3
]
obtained the solutions on semigroups. Theorem
(3)
reproduces their main points in terms of multiplicative functions, and solutions
\phi:S\rightarrow\mathbb{C}
of Eq. (
1.1
) with
\tau=1
.

Theorem 2.1
.

The solutions
f,g:S\rightarrow\mathbb{C}
of Eq. (
2.2
) with
g\neq 0
and
\tau=1
, namely

f(xy)+f(\sigma(y)x)=2f(x)g(y),\quad x,y\in S,

are the following pairs

(1)

f=0
and
g\neq 0
arbitrary.

(2)

f=\alpha\chi+\beta\chi^{*}
and
g=\dfrac{\chi+\chi^{*}}{2}
, where
\alpha,\beta\in\mathbb{C}
are constants such that
(\alpha,\beta)\neq(0,0)
and
\chi:S\rightarrow\mathbb{C}
is a non-zero multiplicative function.

(3)

f=\gamma_{1}\chi+\phi
and
g=\chi
, where
\gamma_{1}\in\mathbb{C}
is a constant,
\chi:S\rightarrow\mathbb{C}
is a non-zero multiplicative function,
\phi
is a non-zero solution of the sine addition law (
1.1
) such that
\chi^{*}=\chi
and
\phi^{*}=-\phi
.

Kannappan
[
10
]
generalized the cosine functional equation (
2.1
) to the functional equation

f(x+y+z_{0})+f(x-y+z_{0})=2f(x)f(y),\quad x,y\in\mathbb{R},

where
z_{0}\neq 0
is a real constant. He showed that any solution
f:\mathbb{R}\rightarrow\mathbb{C}
of this equation has the form
f(x)=g(x-z_{0})
, where
g:\mathbb{R}\rightarrow\mathbb{C}
is a periodic solution of (
2.1
) with period
2z_{0}
.

In 2017, Stetkær
[
17
]
generalized the result of Kannappan by solving the functional equation

f(xyz_{0})+f(xy^{*}z_{0})=2f(x)f(y),\quad x,y\in S,

on a semigroup
S
such that
x\mapsto x^{*}
is an involutive anti-automorphism and
z_{0}\in Z(S)
fixed. An other similar looking functional equation is Van Vleck’s functional equation

f(x-y+z_{0})-f(x+y+z_{0})=2f(x)f(y),\quad x,y\in\mathbb{R},

which was studied by Van Vleck
[
19
]
with
z_{0}>0
fixed. Stetkær
[
16
]
extended Van Vleck’s result to general semigroups by solving the functional equation

f(xy^{*}z_{0})-f(xyz_{0})=2f(x)f(y),\quad x,y\in S,

where
z_{0}\in Z(S)
is fixed,
x\mapsto x^{*}
is an involutive anti-automorphism. He proved that the solutions are all abelian even in the setting of semigroups. Elqorachi
[
7
]
described the solutions of the functional equation

\displaystyle\int_{S}f(xyt)d\mu(t)-\displaystyle\int_{S}f(x\sigma(y)t)d\mu(t)=2f(x)f(y),\ x,y\in S,

where
S
is a semigroup,
\sigma
is an involutive automorphism and
\mu
is a measure that is
a linear combination of Dirac measures
(\delta_{z_{i}})_{i\in I}
with
z_{i}\in Z(S)
for all
i\in I
. Other results about these functional equations can be found in
[
5
]
and
[
11
]
.

The first purpose of the present paper is to solve the Kannappan-Wilson functional equation

\displaystyle\int_{S}f(xyt)d\mu(t)+\displaystyle\int_{S}f(\sigma(y)xt)d\mu(t)=2f(x)g(y),\ x,y\in S,

(2.3)

where
S
is a semigroup,
\mu
is a measure that is
a linear combination of Dirac measures
(\delta_{z_{i}})_{i\in I}
with
z_{i}\in Z(S)
for all
i\in I
and
\sigma
is an involutive automorphism. Equation (
2.3
) is a generalization of (
2.2
), so here we show that the two equations are related and consequently we find explicit formulas for the solutions expressed in terms of multiplicative functions, and solutions of the special sine addition law (
1.1
). As a consequence, we solve the Kannappan-Wilson functional equation

f(xyz_{0})+f(\sigma(y)xz_{0})=2f(x)g(y),\quad x,y\in S,

(2.4)

on semigroups, where
\sigma
is an involutive automorphism and
z_{0}\in Z(S)
fixed. We also give the solutions of Jensen’s functional equation

f(xyz_{0})+f(\sigma(y)xz_{0})=2f(x),\quad x,y\in S,

(2.5)

and by taking
\sigma=id
, we derive the solutions of the functional equation

f(xyz_{0})+f(yxz_{0})=2f(x)g(y),\quad x,y\in S,

(2.6)

which is a generalization of the symmetrized multiplicative Cauchy equation

f(xy)+f(yx)=2f(x)f(y),\quad x,y\in S,

which was solved by Stetkær in
[
13
]
.

The second aim of the paper is to solve the Van Velck-Wilson functional equation

\displaystyle\int_{S}f(xyt)d\mu(t)-\displaystyle\int_{S}f(\sigma(y)xt)d\mu(t)=2f(x)g(y),\ x,y\in S,

(2.7)

and the Van Vleck functional equation

f(xyz_{0})-f(\sigma(y)xz_{0})=2f(x)g(y),\quad x,y\in S,

(2.8)

where
z_{0}\in Z(S)
. We also give some results that are not in the literature about the functional equation

f(xy)-f(\sigma(y)x)=2f(x)g(y),\quad x,y\in S,

(2.9)

on a semigroup
S
, where
\sigma:S\rightarrow S
is an involutive automorphism. We show that the functional equations (
2.7
) and (
2.8
) are related to (
2.2
), and we find explicit formulas for the solutions in terms of multiplicative functions.

Until now, the functional equation (
2.2
) is the only one among all Wilson’s type functional equations which is solved (with
g\neq 0
) on any semigroup, and it is not difficult to see that the treatment of Eq. (
2.3
) on monoids or groups compared with its treatment on semigroups is much easier thanks to the existence of a neutral element which allows the equation to be related to Eq. (
2.2
) easily. In the following remark, we give a proof of this fact.

Remark 2.2
.

Let
f,g:S\rightarrow\mathbb{C}
be a solution of Eq. (
2.3
). Suppose
S
is a monoid and
e
its neutral element. If we put
y=e
in Eq. (
2.3
) and taking into account that
\sigma(e)=e
, we obtain

\displaystyle\int_{S}f(xt)d\mu(t)+\displaystyle\int_{S}f(xt)d\mu(t)=2f(x)g(e),

for all
x\in S
. That is
\displaystyle\int_{S}f(xt)d\mu(t)=f(x)g(e)
. Thus, Eq. (
2.3
) becomes

g(e)f(xy)+g(e)f(\sigma(y)x)=2f(x)g(y),\quad x,y\in S.

Therefore, if
g(e)=0
then
f=0
or
g=0
. Now, if
g(e)\neq 0
, the last equation above can be written as

f(xy)+f(\sigma(y)x)=2f(x)\dfrac{g(y)}{g(e)},\quad x,y\in S,

which means that the pair
\left(f,\dfrac{g}{g(e)}\right)
is a solution of Eq. (
2.2
).

(4)

f=\lambda(\gamma_{1}\chi+\phi)
and
g=\displaystyle\int_{S}\chi(s)d\mu(s)\chi
, where
\chi:S\rightarrow\mathbb{C}
is a non-zero multiplicative function,
\phi

) and

are constants such that
\chi^{*}=\chi
,
\phi^{*}=-\phi
,
\displaystyle\int_{S}\chi(s)d\mu(s)\neq 0

\displaystyle\int_{S}\phi(s)d\mu(s)=0
.

Note that, of the exceptional case (1),

and

Proof.

Let
f,g:S\rightarrow\mathbb{C}
be a solution of (

). If
f=0

2.3
) that
g\neq 0

f\neq 0
and
g\neq 0
. We split the discussion into the cases:
\displaystyle\int_{S}g(s)d\mu(s)=0
and
\displaystyle\int_{S}g(s)d\mu(s)\neq 0
.

Case 1:

\displaystyle\int_{S}g(s)d\mu(s)=0
. According to Lemma
3.1
(4), there exists a constant
\lambda_{1}\in\mathbb{C}\backslash\{0\}

\displaystyle\int_{S}f(xk)d\mu(k)=\lambda_{1}f(x)
for all

. So, Eq. (

f(xy)+f(\sigma(y)x)=2f(x)\dfrac{g(y)}{\lambda_{1}},\quad x,y\in S.

Then, according to Theorem
(3)
and taking into account that
f\neq 0
, we have two possibilities

(i)
f=\alpha\chi+\beta\chi^{*}
and
\dfrac{g}{\lambda_{1}}=\dfrac{\chi+\chi^{*}}{2}
, where
\chi:S\rightarrow\mathbb{C}
is a non-zero multiplicative function and
(\alpha,\beta)\neq(0,0)
are constants. Since
\displaystyle\int_{S}g(s)d\mu(s)=0

\displaystyle\int_{S}\chi(s)d\mu(s)=-\displaystyle\int_{S}\chi^{*}(s)d\mu(s)

\displaystyle\begin{split}\left(\alpha\displaystyle\int_{S}\chi(s)d\mu(s)-\alpha\lambda_{1}\right)\chi(x)(\chi(y)+\chi^{*}(y))\\
=\chi^{*}(x)\left(\beta\displaystyle\int_{S}\chi(s)d\mu(s)+\beta\lambda_{1}\right)(\chi(y)+\chi^{*}(y)).\end{split}

If
\chi=\chi^{*}
, then
g=\lambda_{1}\chi
. So
\displaystyle\int_{S}\chi(s)d\mu(s)=0
and the identity above reduces to
\alpha=-\beta
. This implies

, contradiction. So
\chi\neq\chi^{*}
. Then by the help of
[
14
, Theorem 3.18]
, we deduce that

\left\{\begin{array}[]{l}\alpha\displaystyle\int_{S}\chi(s)d\mu(s)-\alpha\lambda_{1}=0\\
\beta\displaystyle\int_{S}\chi(s)d\mu(s)+\beta\lambda_{1}=0\end{array}\right.

(3.12)

Since
f\neq 0
, then at least one of
\alpha
and
\beta
is non-zero.

(a) Assume
\alpha\neq 0
and
\beta=0
. Then Eq.(
3.12
) implies
\lambda_{1}=\displaystyle\int_{S}\chi(s)d\mu(s)
. This occurs in case (2) with
\lambda_{2}:=\alpha
and
m:=\chi
.

(b) Suppose
\alpha=0
and
\beta\neq 0
. It follows from Eq. (
3.12
) that
\lambda_{1}=-\displaystyle\int_{S}\chi(s)d\mu(s)
. So, we get family (2) with
\lambda_{2}:=\beta
and
m:=\chi^{*}
.

(c) If
\alpha\neq 0
and
\beta\neq 0
. Then Eq. (
3.12
) implies
\lambda_{1}=\displaystyle\int_{S}\chi(s)d\mu(s)=-\displaystyle\int_{S}\chi(s)d\mu(s)
. Thus
\lambda_{1}=0
. This case does not occur.

(ii)
f=\gamma_{1}\chi+\phi
and
\dfrac{g}{\lambda_{1}}=\chi
, where
\chi:S\rightarrow\mathbb{C}
is a non-zero multiplicative function,
\phi
is a non-zero solution of the sine addition law (
1.1
) and
\gamma_{1}\in\mathbb{C}
is a constant such that
\chi^{*}=\chi
and
\phi^{*}=-\phi
. In addition

\displaystyle\int_{S}\chi(s)d\mu(s)=0
. Thus, inserting the forms of
f
and

in Eq. (

), we get after some simplifications

\phi=\left(\displaystyle\int_{S}\phi(s)d\mu(s)-\lambda_{1}\gamma_{1}\right)\chi.

This implies
\phi=0
, since
\chi^{*}=\chi
and
\phi^{*}=-\phi
, which is not possible because
\phi\neq 0
. This case does not occur.

Case 2:

\displaystyle\int_{S}g(s)d\mu(s)\neq 0
. By Lemma
3.1
(3), we have

F(xy)+F(\sigma(y)x)=2F(x)G(y),\quad x,y\in S,

(3.13)

where

and
h(x)=\displaystyle\int_{S}f(xt)d\mu(t)
for all
x\in S
. In addition, according to Lemma
3.1
(2) and (3),
F\neq 0
and
G\neq 0
. Therefore, applying Theorem
(3)
to Eq. (
3.13

(i)
F=\alpha\chi+\beta\chi^{*}
and
G=\dfrac{\chi+\chi^{*}}{2}
, where

is a non-zero multiplicative function and
(\alpha,\beta)\neq(0,0)

(f,g)

), we can see that for all

\dfrac{f(x)g(y)}{\displaystyle\int_{S}g(s)d\mu(s)}=F(x)G(y).

(3.14)

Thus, replacing
(x,y)
by
(x,s)

.

Now, if

, then by applying

\alpha\neq 0
and
\beta=0

. This is case (2) with

(b)
\alpha=0
and

. This implies
\gamma=\displaystyle\int_{S}\chi^{*}(s)d\mu(s)

(c)
\alpha\neq 0
and

(ii)
F=\gamma_{1}\chi+\phi
and

, where

Since

\displaystyle\int_{S}\chi(s)d\mu(s)\neq 0

and

\left(\gamma-\displaystyle\int_{S}\chi(s)d\mu(s)\right)\phi=\left(\gamma_{1}\left(\displaystyle\int_{S}\chi(s)d\mu(s)-\gamma\right)+\displaystyle\int_{S}\phi(s)d\mu(s)\right)\chi.

Conversely, if

4.

4.1.
Solutions of Eq. (
2.9
)

The following lemma will be used later

Lemma 4.1
.

Let
F,G:S\rightarrow\mathbb{C}
be a solution of the functional equation

F(xy)=F(x)G(y)-F(y)G(x)\quad x,y\in S.

(4.1)

Then,
F
and
G
are linearly dependent.

Proof.

Follows from
[
4
, Theorem 5.2]
by taking
\sigma=id
.
∎

The next theorem gives the general solution of (
2.9
).

Theorem 4.2
.

The solutions
f,g:S\rightarrow\mathbb{C}
of Eq. (
2.9
) with
g\neq 0
, namely

f(xy)-f(\sigma(y)x)=2f(x)g(y),\quad x,y\in S,

are the following

(1)

f=0
and
g\neq 0
arbitrary.

(2)

f=\lambda\chi
and
g=\dfrac{\chi-\chi^{*}}{2}
, where
\chi:S\rightarrow\mathbb{C}
is a multiplicative function and
\lambda\in\mathbb{C}\backslash\{0\}
is a constant such that
\chi\neq\chi^{*}
.

Note that in case (2),
f
and
g
are abelian.

Proof.

Let
f,g:S\rightarrow\mathbb{C}
be a solution of Eq. (
2.9
). If
f=0
, we can see easily from (
2.9
) that
g\neq 0
is arbitrary. This is case (1). From now on, we assume that
f\neq 0
and
g\neq 0
. Let
x,y\in S
be arbitrary. By applying Eq. (
2.9
) to the pair
(\sigma(y),x)
, we get that

f(\sigma(y)x)-f^{*}(xy)=2f^{*}(y)g(x).

By adding this to (
2.9
) we obtain

f^{\circ}(xy)=f(x)g(y)+f^{*}(y)g(x).

(4.2)

On the other hand, since
f=f^{e}+f^{\circ}
and taking (
4.2
) into account, we get from (
2.9
) that

\displaystyle f^{e}(xy)-f^{e}(\sigma(y)x)

\displaystyle=2f(x)g(y)-f^{\circ}(xy)+f^{\circ}(\sigma(y)x)

\displaystyle=f(x)g(y)-f^{*}(y)g(x)+f^{\circ}(\sigma(y)x)

\displaystyle=f(x)g(y)+f^{*}(x)g^{*}(y).

So, since
f^{*}=f^{e}-f^{\circ}
we deduce that

\displaystyle\begin{split}f^{e}(xy)-f^{e}(\sigma(y)x)=\left[f^{e}(x)+f^{\circ}(x)\right]\left[g^{e}(y)+g^{\circ}(y)\right]+\\
\left[f^{e}(x)-f^{\circ}(x)\right]\left[g^{e}(y)-g^{\circ}(y)\right].\end{split}

That is

f^{e}(xy)-f^{e}(\sigma(y)x)=2f^{e}(x)g^{e}(y)+2f^{\circ}(x)g^{\circ}(y).

(4.3)

Now, replacing
(x,y)
by
(\sigma(y),x)
in (
4.3
) and using that
f^{e}\circ\sigma=f^{e}
and
f^{\circ}\circ\sigma=-f^{\circ}
, we obtain

f^{e}(\sigma(y)x)-f^{e}(xy)=2f^{e}(y)g^{e}(x)-2f^{\circ}(y)g^{\circ}(x).

(4.4)

Thus, by comparing Eq. (
4.3
) with Eq. (
4.4
), we see that

f^{e}(x)g^{e}(y)+f^{\circ}(x)g^{\circ}(y)=f^{\circ}(y)g^{\circ}(x)-f^{e}(y)g^{e}(x).

This implies

f^{e}(x)g^{e}(y)+f^{e}(y)g^{e}(x)=f^{\circ}(y)g^{\circ}(x)-f^{\circ}(x)g^{\circ}(y).

(4.5)

In Eq. (
4.5
), the left hand side is an even function of
x
while the right is an odd function of
x
, so

f^{e}(x)g^{e}(y)+f^{e}(y)g^{e}(x)=0,

(4.6)

and

f^{\circ}(y)g^{\circ}(x)-f^{\circ}(x)g^{\circ}(y)=0,

(4.7)

for all
x,y\in S
. According to
[
14
, Exercise 1.1 (b)]
we deduce from (
4.6
) that we have two possibilities:
f^{e}=0
or
g^{e}=0
.

First case:
f^{e}=0
. That is
f=f^{\circ}
, so
f^{*}=-f
and from Eq. (
4.3
) we can see that
g^{\circ}=0
, i.e.,
g=g^{e}
. In addition Eq. (
4.2
) becomes

f(xy)=f(x)g(y)-f(y)g(x).

According to Lemma
4.1
,
f
and
g
are linearly dependent. Then
f=0
or
g=0
, since
f
is odd and
g
is even. This case does not occur since
f\neq 0
and
g\neq 0
.

Second case:
g^{e}=0
. That is
g=g^{\circ}
. Since
g\neq 0
, we get from Eq. (
4.7
) that
f^{\circ}=\lambda g
for some constant
\lambda\in\mathbb{C}
. Thus, equation (
4.2
) becomes

\lambda g(xy)=f(x)g(y)+f^{*}(y)g(x).

(4.8)

Now, replacing
(x,y)
by
(x,\sigma(y))
in (
4.8
) we obtain, since
g
is odd

\lambda g(x\sigma(y))=f(y)g(x)-f(x)g(y).

(4.9)

If
\lambda=0
then
f^{\circ}=0
, i.e.,
f=f^{e}
. In addition, we get from (
4.9
) that
f
and
g
are linearly dependent. Therefore
f=0
or
g=0
, since
f
is even and
g
is odd. This is a contradiction, since
f\neq 0
and
g\neq 0
. So
\lambda\neq 0
. Thus, equation (
4.9
) can be written as

g(x\sigma(y))=\dfrac{1}{\lambda}f(y)g(x)-\dfrac{1}{\lambda}f(x)g(y).

According to
[
4
, Theorem 5.2]
and taking into account that
g
is a non-zero odd function, we have two cases to consider

Case 1:
g=\alpha(\chi-\chi^{*})
and
\dfrac{1}{\lambda}f=\dfrac{\chi+\chi^{*}}{2}+\alpha_{1}\dfrac{\chi-\chi^{*}}{2}
, where
\chi:S\rightarrow\mathbb{C}
is a multiplicative function such that
\chi\neq\chi^{*}
,
\alpha_{1}\in\mathbb{C}
and
\alpha\in\mathbb{C}\backslash\{0\}
. This implies that
f=\dfrac{\lambda+\lambda\alpha_{1}}{2}\chi+\dfrac{\lambda-\lambda\alpha_{1}}{2}\chi^{*}
. Thus, inserting these forms into (
2.9
), we obtain after some simplifications that

\dfrac{\lambda+\lambda\alpha_{1}}{2}\chi(x)+\dfrac{\lambda\alpha_{1}-\lambda}{2}\chi^{*}(x)=\lambda\alpha(1+\alpha_{1})\chi(x)+\lambda\alpha(1-\alpha_{1})\chi^{*}(x),

for all
x\in S
. Therefore, since
\chi\neq\chi^{*}
, by the help of
[
14
, Theorem 3.18]
we obtain, since
\lambda\neq 0

1+\alpha_{1}=2\alpha(1+\alpha_{1})\ \ \text{and}\ \ \ \alpha_{1}-1=2\alpha(1-\alpha_{1}).

This implies
\alpha_{1}=2\alpha
. Then
\alpha_{1}^{2}=1
. That is
\alpha_{1}=1
or
\alpha_{1}=-1
. So,
\alpha=\dfrac{1}{2}
or
\alpha=\dfrac{-1}{2}
. Thus

\left\{\begin{array}[]{l}f=\lambda\chi\\
g=\dfrac{\chi-\chi^{*}}{2}\end{array}\right.\quad\text{or}\quad\left\{\begin{array}[]{l}f=\lambda\chi^{*}\\
g=\dfrac{\chi^{*}-\chi}{2}\end{array}.\right.

The pair
(f,g)
on the left falls into case (2). So does the pair on the right, except that there
\chi
is replaced by
\chi^{*}
.

Case 2:
g=\phi
and
\dfrac{1}{\lambda}f=\chi+\lambda_{2}\phi
, where
\chi:S\rightarrow\mathbb{C}
is a non-zero multiplicative
function,
\phi
is a non-zero solution of the sine addition law (
1.1
) such that
\chi^{*}=\chi
,
\phi^{*}=-\phi
and
\lambda_{2}\in\mathbb{C}
is a constant. So
f=\lambda\chi+\lambda c_{2}\phi
. Therefore, inserting these forms into (
2.9
), we get after some simplifications that

c_{2}\chi(x)\phi(y)=(\chi(x)+\lambda_{2}\phi(x))\phi(y),

for all
x,y\in S
. This implies, since
\phi\neq 0
that
(\lambda_{2}-1)\chi(x)=c_{2}\phi(x)
for all
x\in S
. Thus
\lambda_{2}=1
and
\lambda_{2}=0
since
\chi
is even and
\phi
is add, which is impossible. This case does not occur.

Conversely, we check by elementary computations that the pairs described in Theorem
4.2
are solutions of Eq. (
2.9
). This completes the proof of Theorem
4.2
.
∎

Remark 4.3
.

The only solution
f:S\rightarrow\mathbb{C}
of the functional equation

f(xy)-f(\sigma(y)x)=2f(x)f(y),\quad x,y\in S,

is
f\equiv 0
.

4.2.
Solutions of Eq. (
2.7
) and Eq. (
2.8
)

In the next lemma we give some key properties of solutions of (
2.7
), namely

\displaystyle\int_{S}f(xyt)d\mu(t)-\displaystyle\int_{S}f(\sigma(y)xt)d\mu(t)=2f(x)g(y),\ x,y\in S.

Lemma 4.4
.

Let
f,g:S\rightarrow\mathbb{C}
be a solution of Eq. (
2.7
) such that
f\neq 0
and
g\neq 0
. Let
h
and
F
be the functions defined in Lemma
3.1
. The following statements hold

(1)

Suppose
\displaystyle\int_{S}g(s)d\mu(s)\neq 0
.Then
F\neq 0
, and

F(xy)+F(\sigma(y)x)=2F(x)G_{1}(y),\quad x,y\in S,

(4.10)

where
G_{1}(x):=\dfrac{\displaystyle\int_{S}g(xt)d\mu(t)-\displaystyle\int_{S}g(x\sigma(t))d\mu(t)}{2\displaystyle\int_{S}g(s)d\mu(s)}
and
G_{1}\neq 0
.

(2)

If
\displaystyle\int_{S}g(s)d\mu(s)=0
, then
h(x)=\lambda_{1}f(x)
for all
x\in S
, where
\lambda_{1}\in\mathbb{C}\backslash\left\{0\right\}
is a constant.

Proof.

(1) follows by using similar computations to those of the proof of Lemma
3.1
(3).

(2) By using similar computations to those of the proof of Lemma
3.1
(4), we show that

\displaystyle\int_{S}\int_{S}f(xyst)d\mu(t)d\mu(s)-\displaystyle\int_{S}\int_{S}f(\sigma(y)sxt)d\mu(t)d\mu(s)=2f(x)\displaystyle\int_{S}g(ys)d\mu(s),

(4.11)

and

\displaystyle\int_{S}\int_{S}f(xyst)d\mu(t)d\mu(s)-\displaystyle\int_{S}\int_{S}f(\sigma(y)xst)d\mu(t)d\mu(s)=2g(y)\displaystyle\int_{S}f(xs)d\mu(s).

(4.12)

Thus, comparing Eq. (
4.11
) with Eq. (
4.12
), we see that for all
x,y\in S

f(x)\displaystyle\int_{S}g(ys)d\mu(s)=g(y)\displaystyle\int_{S}f(xs)d\mu(s).

Now, the result follows easily since
g\neq 0
. This completes the proof.
∎

Now, we are ready to solve the functional equation (
2.7
).

Theorem 4.5
.

The solutions
f,g:S\rightarrow\mathbb{C}
of Eq. (
2.7
) with
g\neq 0
can be listed as follows

(1)

f=0
and
g\neq 0
arbitrary.

(2)

f=\alpha_{1}\chi+\alpha_{2}\chi^{*}
and
g=\displaystyle\int_{S}\chi(s)d\mu(s)\dfrac{\chi-\chi^{*}}{2}
, where
\chi:S\rightarrow\mathbb{C}
is a multiplicative function and
\alpha_{1},\alpha_{2}\in\mathbb{C}
are constants such that
(\alpha_{1},\alpha_{2})\neq(0,0)
,
\chi\neq\chi^{*}
and
\displaystyle\int_{S}\chi(s)d\mu(s)=-\displaystyle\int_{S}\chi^{*}(s)d\mu(s)\neq 0
.

Note that in case (2),
f
and
g
are abelian.

Proof.

Let
f,g:S\rightarrow\mathbb{C}
be a solution of (
2.7
). If
f=0
, then we can see easily from (
2.7
) that
g\neq 0
is arbirary. This occurs in (1). Now, we assume that
f\neq 0
and
g\neq 0
. We discuss two cases :

\displaystyle\int_{S}g(s)d\mu(s)=0\ \ \text{and}\ \ \displaystyle\int_{S}g(s)d\mu(s)\neq 0.

Case 1:

\displaystyle\int_{S}g(s)d\mu(s)=0
. According to Lemma
4.4
(2), there exists a constant
\lambda_{1}\in\mathbb{C}\backslash\{0\}
such that
\displaystyle\int_{S}f(xk)d\mu(k)=\lambda_{1}f(x)
for all
x\in S
. Thus, Eq. (
2.7
) can be written as

f(xy)-f(\sigma(y)x)=2f(x)\dfrac{g(y)}{\lambda_{1}},\quad x,y\in S.

Then, according to Theorem
4.2
and taking into account that
f\neq 0
, we have one possibility

f=\lambda\chi\ \ \text{and}\ \ \dfrac{g}{\lambda_{1}}=\dfrac{\chi-\chi^{*}}{2},

where
\chi:S\rightarrow\mathbb{C}
is a multiplicative function and
\lambda\in\mathbb{C}\backslash\{0\}
is a constant such that
\chi\neq\chi^{*}
. Inserting the forms of
f
and
g
into Eq. (
2.7
), we obtain after some simplifications that
\lambda_{1}=\displaystyle\int_{S}\chi(s)d\mu(s)
. This is part (2) with
\alpha:=\lambda
.

Case 2:

\displaystyle\int_{S}g(s)d\mu(s)\neq 0
. By Lemma
4.4
(1), we have that

F(xy)+F(\sigma(y)x)=2F(x)G_{1}(y),\quad x,y\in S,

where

F(x):=\dfrac{h(x)}{\displaystyle\int_{S}g(s)d\mu(s)}\quad\text{and}\quad G_{1}(x):=\dfrac{\displaystyle\int_{S}g(xt)d\mu(t)-\displaystyle\int_{S}g(x\sigma(t))d\mu(t)}{2\displaystyle\int_{S}g(s)d\mu(s)},

and
h(x):=\displaystyle\int_{S}f(xt)d\mu(t)
for all
x\in S
. Thus, according to Theorem
(3)
and taking into account that
F\neq 0
and
G_{1}\neq 0
, we get

F=\alpha\chi+\beta\chi^{*}\quad\text{and}\quad G_{1}=\dfrac{\chi+\chi^{*}}{2},

where
\alpha,\beta\in\mathbb{C}
are constants such that
(\alpha,\beta)\neq(0,0)
and
\chi:S\rightarrow\mathbb{C}
is a non-zero multiplicative function. Therefore, proceeding similarly to the proof of Theorem
3.2
case (2)(i), we find that

f=\alpha_{1}\chi+\alpha_{2}\chi^{*}\ \ \text{and}\ \ g=\displaystyle\int_{S}\chi(s)d\mu(s)\dfrac{\chi-\chi^{*}}{2},

where
\alpha_{1},\alpha_{2}\in\mathbb{C}
are constants such that
(\alpha_{1},\alpha_{2})\neq(0,0)
,
\chi\neq\chi^{*}
and
\displaystyle\int_{S}\chi(s)d\mu(s)=-\displaystyle\int_{S}\chi^{*}(s)d\mu(s)\neq 0
. This occurs in case (2).

The second possibility according to Theorem
(3)
is that
F=\gamma(c\chi+\phi)
and
G_{1}=\chi
, where
\gamma\in\mathbb{C}\backslash\{0\}
is a constant. This leads to the contradiction
g=0
.

The converses are straightforward verifications. This completes the proof.
∎

The final result of the paper is the following corollary about the solutions of the functional equation (
2.8
), namely

f(xyz_{0})-f(\sigma(y)xz_{0})=2f(x)g(y),\quad x,y\in S.

Corollary 4.6

The solutions
f,g:S\rightarrow\mathbb{C}
of Eq. (

) with

can be listed as follows

(1)

f=0
and
g\neq 0

(2)

Note that in case (2),

Proof.

The result follows from Theorem
4.5

∎
