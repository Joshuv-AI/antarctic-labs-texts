Abstract
In this paper we introduce a new sampling and reconstruction approach for multi-dimensional analog signals. Building on top of the Unlimited Sensing Framework (USF), we present a new folded sampling operator called the multi-dimensional modulo-hysteresis that is also backwards compatible with the existing one-dimensional modulo operator. Unlike previous approaches, the proposed model is specifically tailored to multi-dimensional signals. In particular, the model uses certain redundancy in dimensions 2 2 and above, which is exploited for input recovery with robustness. We prove that the new operator is well-defined and its outputs have a bounded dynamic range. For the noiseless case, we derive a theoretically guaranteed input reconstruction approach. When the input is corrupted by Gaussian noise, we exploit redundancy in higher dimensions to provide a bound on the error probability and show this drops to 0 0 for high enough sampling rates leading to new theoretical guarantees for the noisy case. Our numerical examples corroborate the theoretical results and show that the proposed approach can handle a significantly larger amount of noise compared to USF.

Introduction

Shannon’s sampling theory is the workhorse of almost all modern-world digital systems. Its practical implementation is carried out via electronic hardware, namely, the analog-to-digital converter (ADC). However, there is a gap between theory and practice which leads to a few fundamental deviations from the ideal sampling model, including, among others, quantization (see the extensive survey by Gray & Neuhoff
[
1
]
) non-pointwise sampling
[
2
]
and ADC saturation. The latter deviation arises from the fact that the ADC is a physical device and hence, one can only record a fixed range of amplitudes (typically, a prescribed voltage range). This input amplitude range defines the
dynamic range
(or DR) of the ADC, say
\lambda>0
. Any signal exceeding (in absolute value)
\lambda
would result in permanent loss of information due to saturation or clipping. Mathematically, this is synonymous to
hard thresholding

[
3
,
4
]
, but the difference is that it occurs in hardware and is highly undesirable. Clipped sample values lead to high frequency components, which in turn leads to aliasing. Typical solutions to the saturation problem rely on:

(a)

hardware approaches such us
companding

[
5
]
or adaptively matching the dynamic range to the input signal range via
automatic gain control
. There are also techniques that re-think ADC design (see, for example,
[
6
]
).

(b)

algorithmic approaches that aim to solve the inverse problem of
de-clipping

[
7
,
8
]
or
inpainting

[
9
]
.

Clipping or saturation is also highly relevant in the context of digital imaging, so much so that almost all modern smartphones are equipped with the
High Dynamic Range
or “HDR” mode, based on multiple captures that are combined numerically
[
10
]
.

The progress in the last many decades has led to deepened understanding of the nuances involved with the quantization and limited DR aspects. Clearly, ADCs need to be matched to the DR of the input signal to avoid saturation or clipping. Beyond this calibration step—typically addressed by the engineers—there is an additional challenge: higher dynamic range requires a higher number of bits to achieve a given resolution; this in turn leads to a higher power consumption in the ADC, thus highlighting the integral role of DR in digital acquisition.

1.1
Unlimited Sensing Framework (USF)

Recently, the Unlimited Sensing Framework (USF)
[
11
,
15
,
16
,
17
,
18
,
12
,
13
]
has been proposed in the literature that serves as an alternative digital acquisition protocol for avoiding the DR limitation in conventional ADCs. The USF is based on a joint design of hardware and mathematical algorithms.

•

In hardware, the modulo non-linearity ensures that HDR inputs are folded back in to the ADC’s DR; this is because the modulo threshold is chosen such that the modulo ADC’s range is bounded by
\lambda
. Consequently, the modulo ADC results in
folded samples
.

•

To recover the HDR input from folded, modulo samples, mathematically guaranteed recovery algorithms are deployed.

Similar to the Shannon–Nyquist sampling criterion where a higher input bandwidth can be traded off for higher sampling rates, it was shown that HDR signals can also be tackled by sampling more densely. This is made precise by the following theorem.

Theorem 1

(Unlimited Sampling Theorem
[
11
]
)
.

Let
f(t)
be a continuous-time function with maximum frequency
\Omega
(rads/s). Then, a sufficient condition for recovery of
f(t)
from its modulo samples (up to an additive constant) taken every
T
seconds apart is
T\leqslant 1/\left(2\Omega\mathrm{e}\right)
where
\mathrm{e}
is Euler’s number.

Thus, the USF addresses a major bottleneck in physical sensors by allowing the recovery of inputs beyond the sensor dynamic range. A first validation of the USF with experiments based on a modulo ADC were presented in
[
13
]
. In particular, it was shown that signals as large as
\approx 25\lambda
can be recovered in a laboratory setup. The full sampling and reconstruction pipeline for the USF is shown in Fig.
1
.

The initial works based on USF tackled signals supported on the real line spanned in a bandlimited
[
11
,
12
]
or spline spaces
[
19
]
. There are also methods to recover signals with compressive priors
[
20
,
21
]
and using wavelet filters
[
22
]
. Further extensions of the USF include compactly supported inputs
[
13
]
, sparse signals
[
23
]
and also new acquisition models
[
14
]
.

A new acquisition model called modulo-hysteresis was introduced in
[
24
]
and further discussed in
[
14
]
, which considers hardware non-idealities and enables new recovery guarantees. The modulo-hysteresis was also implemented in a hardware prototype
[
14
]
.
This line of work also paved the path to novel and exciting low-power acquisition neuromorphic applications
[
24
,
25
]
.

The methods discussed so far assume that the input is one-dimensional . However, in many applications, such as photography
[
19
]
, X-ray imaging or Computed Tomography
[
26
]
, the input signal is multi-dimensional.

Motivation for a multi-dimensional model

There have been attempts to address multi-dimensional inputs with modulo architectures by rasterizing and processing the signal line-by-line in imaging
[
27
,
19
,
26
]
or for lattice sampling
[
28
]
. However, the existing modulo sampling approaches for multi-dimensional data are based on a one-dimensional modulo operator that is applied sequentially on the slices of a multi-dimensional input. This considers each slice a distinct signal, and does not exploit that they are all part of a multi-dimensional input. In other words, this modulo operation represents a separable transformation that does not exploit the multi-dimensional nature of the input. Furthermore, it is known that non-separable transformations represent much more powerful tools in analysing multi-dimensional data
[
29
,
30
]
.

We consider only the problem of input recovery for noisy inputs, distinct from that of input denoising, which was addressed before for modulo sampling
[
31
,
32
]
. Methods such as USF recover the noise corrupted input samples while keeping the noise sequence intact. However, USF (Theorem
1
) is fundamentally restricted to work with noise amplitudes smaller than the modulo threshold. When this requirement is not satisfied, the input recovery is heavily distorted. This limitation is carried over to the existing attempts to apply USF to multi-dimensional data.

Contributions

Here we present a modulo model that exploits the multi-dimensional structure of the data in the encoding process. Specifically, via multi-dimensional sampling in
D
dimensions, we are able to dedicate a
D-1
-dimensional subspace to deal with noise reduction, leaving dimension
d=1
for estimating the modulo folds. Specifically, our contributions are below:

\textrm{C}_{1})

We introduce a generalized
D
-dimensional modulo operator for sampling on a lattice.

\textrm{C}_{2})

We prove that the operator is well-defined and the folding discontinuities are located along directions given by the lattice vectors.

\textrm{C}_{3})

We provide recovery guarantees under noiseless assumption.

\textrm{C}_{4})

Under Gaussian noise assumption, we provide an upper bound on the recovery error probability that drops to
0
for high enough sampling rates.

\textrm{C}_{5})

Using a numerical study we show that the proposed model offers significantly better noise robustness than USF.

Modulo acquisition and recovery

2.1
Recovery from one-dimensional modulo data

The centered modulo with threshold
\lambda

\mathscr{M}_{\lambda}:\mathbb{R}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
satisfying
[
12

\mathscr{M}_{\lambda}\left(x\right)=2\lambda\left({\left[\kern-3.00003pt\left[{\frac{x}{{2\lambda}}+\frac{1}{2}}\right]\kern-3.00003pt\right]-\frac{1}{2}}\right).

(4)

When applied to a one-dimensional function
g
the modulo non-linearity generates values
\mathscr{M}_{\lambda}\left(g\left(t\right)\right)\in\left[-\lambda,\lambda\right]

In analogy to Shannon-Nyquist sampling theory, the first recovery result in the Unlimited Sensing Framework (USF) utilized bandlimited inputs, namely,
g\in\mathsf{PW}_{\Omega}

unlimited sampling theorem

[
11

12
]

[
12
]

\left(T\Omega e\right)^{N}g_{\infty}+2^{N}\eta_{\infty}<\lambda.

(5)

The recovery approach used in
[
11
,
12
]
aims to reconstruct the residual function
\varepsilon_{g}\left(t\right)

\varepsilon_{g}\left(t\right)\triangleq g\left(t\right)-\mathscr{M}_{\lambda}\left(g\left(t\right)\right)\in 2\lambda\cdot\mathbb{Z}

A generalized model of the modulo operator, called

34
,
14
]

3
,
4
, and
5

•

3
Multi-dimensional modulo sampling

3.1
Multi-dimensional lattice sampling preliminaries

Let
f:\mathbb{R}^{D}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
be a
D
-dimensional scalar function. The data is then sampled on a lattice
\boldsymbol{\Lambda}=\{\mathbf{V}\mathbf{T}\mathbf{k}|\mathbf{k}\in\mathbb{Z}^{D}\}
. We denote the resulting samples by
\gamma\left[\mathbf{k}\right]=f\left(\mathbf{VTk}\right)
.
We assume that
f
has a Fourier transform satisfying

\mathrm{supp}\left(\mathcal{F}f\right)\subseteq\mathbb{D}.

Upon sampling, the spectrum of
f
is copied periodically, to produce the multi-dimensional discrete-time Fourier transform
F_{\boldsymbol{\Lambda}}\left(\boldsymbol{\omega}\right)=\sum_{\mathbf{k}\in\mathbb{Z}^{D}}f\left(\mathbf{VTk}\right)e^{-\jmath\left\langle\boldsymbol{\omega},\mathbf{k}\right\rangle}

\mathrm{supp}\left(F_{\boldsymbol{\Lambda}}\right)=\mathbf{T}\mathbf{V}^{\top}\cdot\underset{\mathbf{n}\in\widehat{\boldsymbol{\Lambda}}}{\bigcup}\left(\mathbb{D}+2\pi\mathbf{n}\right).

(9)

It was shown that

if
[
36
,
37
]

\mathbf{T}\mathbf{V}^{\top}\left(\mathbb{D}+2\pi\mathbf{n}_{1}\right)\cap\mathbf{T}\mathbf{V}^{\top}\left(\mathbb{D}+2\pi\mathbf{n}_{2}\right)=\emptyset,\quad\forall\mathbf{n_{1}},\mathbf{n_{2}}\in\widehat{\boldsymbol{\Lambda}},\mathbf{n_{1}}\neq\mathbf{n_{2}}.

(10)

To ensure that recovery is possible, we assume that the spectrum of
f
has a compact support satisfying
\mathbb{D}\subseteq\widehat{\mathbf{V}}\prod_{d=1}^{D}\left(-\Omega_{d},\Omega_{d}\right)
. Formally, our assumption is
f\in\mathsf{PW}_{\boldsymbol{\Omega}}\left(\mathbb{R}^{D}\right)
.
Then
f
can be reconstructed from samples
\gamma\left[\mathbf{k}\right]
if (
10
) is satisfied, which is sufficiently guaranteed if we replace
\mathbb{D}
by
\widehat{\mathbf{V}}\cdot\prod_{d=1}^{D}\left(-\Omega_{d},\Omega_{d}\right)
, for which the terms on the left-hand-side of (

\mathbf{T}\mathbf{V}^{\top}\left(\mathbb{D}+2\pi\mathbf{n}\right)=\mathbf{T}\mathbf{V}^{\top}\left[\mathbf{V}^{-\top}\prod_{d=1}^{D}\left(-\Omega_{d},\Omega_{d}\right)+2\pi\mathbf{n}\right],\quad\mathbf{n}\in\widehat{\boldsymbol{\Lambda}}.

(11)

We use that
\mathbf{n}=\mathbf{V}^{-\top}\mathbf{T}^{-1}\mathbf{k},\quad\mathbf{k}\in\mathbb{Z}^{D}
, yielding

\displaystyle\mathbf{T}\mathbf{V}^{\top}\left(\mathbb{D}+2\pi\mathbf{n}\right)=\mathbf{T}

\displaystyle\prod_{d=1}^{D}\left(-\Omega_{d},\Omega_{d}\right)+2\pi\mathbf{k},\quad\mathbf{k}\in\mathbb{Z}^{D}

(12)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\prod_{d=1}^{D}\left(-T_{d}\Omega_{d},T_{d}\Omega_{d}\right)+2\pi\mathbf{k}.

(13)

Therefore, (
10

T_{d}<\frac{\pi}{\Omega_{d}},\quad\forall d\in\left\{1,\dots,D\right\}.

(14)

Just as in the one-dimensional case, considering the problem of sensor saturation motivates using the concept of modulo folding also in the multi-dimensional case. Next we go through some of the attempts to apply modulo for multi-dimensional data.

3.2
Previous approaches for recovery from multi-dimensional data

As in the one-dimensional case, the problem with computing directly
\gamma\left[\mathbf{k}\right]
is that the sample values may be very large which would saturate an analog-to-digital (ADC) acquisition device, which has a restricted dynamic range
[
12
]
.
The modulo operator was applied previously for multi-dimensional inputs by processing a 1D slice at a time
[
19
,
28
]
. However, these methods are not truly multi-dimensional because they don’t exploit the multi-dimensional structure of the data. Furthermore, when dealing with noise in 1D, the modulo samples

\varepsilon_{\gamma}
and
\eta

\varepsilon_{\gamma}
requires a high-pass filter (such as
\Delta^{N}
in the case of USF), while denoising is typically done with low-pass filters
[
34
]
.
Furthermore, a denoising approach on modulo data was tested for multi-dimensional signals
[
31
]
. However, we center our analysis on purely modulo inversion techniques, where the noise sequence remains unaltered.

We define the following functions, representing slices of function
f(\mathbf{V}\mathbf{x})
. Let
f_{\mathbf{V}}\left(\mathbf{x}\right)\triangleq f(\mathbf{V}\mathbf{x})
. Let
f_{\bar{\mathbf{x}}}:\mathbb{R}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
denote the slice along dimension
x_{1}
defined as
f_{\bar{\mathbf{x}}}\left(x\right)\triangleq f\left(x\mathbf{v}_{1}+\sum_{d=2}^{D}x_{d}\mathbf{v}_{d}\right),\forall\bar{\mathbf{x}}\in\mathbb{R}^{D-1},\bar{\mathbf{x}}=\left[x_{2},\dots,x_{D}\right]
. In the next proposition we also use a generic slices defined as the one-dimensional function
g_{d}\left(x_{d}\right)\triangleq f\left(\sum_{n=1}^{D}x_{n}\mathbf{v}_{n}\right)
by fixing dimensions
\left\{x_{1},\dots,x_{d-1},x_{d+1},\dots,x_{D}\right\},d\neq 1
. The following proposition was proven in
[
28
]
.

Proposition 1

(Bandlimited slices)
.

The function
f_{\bar{\mathbf{x}}}
satisfies
f_{\bar{\mathbf{x}}}\in\mathsf{PW}_{\Omega_{1}}\left(\mathbb{R}\right)
. Furthermore,
g_{d}\in\mathsf{PW}_{\Omega_{d}}\left(\mathbb{R}\right)
,
\forall d\in\left\{1,\dots,D\right\},d\neq 1
.

Proof.

The proof is in Section
7.1
.
∎

The proposition above proves that any
slice
of the multi-dimensional function
f
along lattice dimension
d
has the spectrum compactly supported within
\left(-\Omega_{d},\Omega_{d}\right)
. Furthermore, this implies that a Bernšteĭn bound can be applied for each variable such that

\displaystyle\begin{split}\left\lvert\frac{\partial}{\partial x_{d}}f_{\mathbf{V}}\left(\mathbf{x}\right)\right\rvert&\leqslant\Omega_{d}\max_{x_{d}}\left\lvert f_{\mathbf{V}}\left(\mathbf{x}\right)\right\rvert\leqslant\Omega_{d}\|f_{\mathbf{V}}\|_{\infty}\\
&=\Omega_{d}\|f\|_{\infty},\quad\forall d\in\left\{1,\dots,D\right\},\forall\bar{\mathbf{x}}\in\mathbb{R}^{D-1}.\end{split}

(15)

The works in
[
19
]
and
[
28
]
apply one-dimensional ideal modulo to
f_{\bar{\mathbf{x}}}
:

\mathscr{M}_{\lambda}f_{\bar{\mathbf{x}}}\left(x\right)=f_{\bar{\mathbf{x}}}\left(x\right)-2\lambda_{h}\sum_{r\in\mathbb{Z}}s_{\bar{\mathbf{x}},r}\mathbbmtt{1}_{\left[\tau_{\bar{\mathbf{x}},r},\infty\right)}\left(x\right),x\in\mathbb{R}.

Therefore we can define the "folded" multi-dimensional function
z\left(\mathbf{x}\right)
as

z\left(\mathbf{Vx}\right)=\mathscr{M}_{\lambda}\left(f_{\bar{\mathbf{x}}}\left(x_{1}\right)\right),

(16)

where
\mathbf{x}=\left[x_{1},\dots,x_{D}\right],\bar{\mathbf{x}}=\left[x_{2},\dots,x_{D}\right],x_{d}\in\mathbb{R},\forall d\in\left\{1,\dots,D\right\}
. Subsequently, the output samples are
z\left(\mathbf{VTk}\right)=\mathscr{M}_{\lambda}f_{\bar{\mathbf{T}}\bar{\mathbf{k}}}\left(k_{1}T_{1}\right)
, where
\bar{\mathbf{T}}=\mathrm{diag}\left\{T_{2},\dots,T_{D}\right\}
.

Then, recovering
f_{\bar{\mathbf{T}}\bar{\mathbf{k}}}\left(k_{1}T_{1}\right)
for all
\bar{\mathbf{T}}\bar{\mathbf{k}}
from
z\left(\mathbf{VTk}\right)
represents a line-by-line approach used in
[
19
,
26
,
28
]
, which is guaranteed to work if (
5
) holds true. However, as explained previously, this approach does not exploit the multi-dimensional structure of the input data. This is further motivated by the accepted knowledge in image processing that non-separability in multiple dimensions has a lot more to offer than separability
[
29
,
30
]
.
This motivates introducing a multi-dimensional modulo-hysteresis model in the next section. We will show that the new model allows a significantly large amount of noise, which is not possible with USF that processes the data line-by-line.

3.3
Towards multi-dimensional modulo-hysteresis acquisition

Inspired from the 1D modulo-hysteresis operator that showed improvements for noise robustness
[
34
]
, we define in the following a new operator called multi-dimensional modulo-hysteresis that addresses the issues discussed in the previous subsection. The idea is to split the domain
\mathbb{R}^{D-1}
in disjoint sets confined in polytopes
\mathcal{P}_{\bar{\mathbf{b}}},\bar{\mathbf{b}}\in\mathbb{Z}^{D-1}
defined as

\mathcal{P}_{\bar{\mathbf{b}}}=\left\{\bar{\mathbf{V}}B\left(\bar{\mathbf{b}}+\bar{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}\right)|\forall\bar{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}\in\left[0,1\right)^{D-1}\right\},

(17)

where
\bar{\mathbf{b}}=\left[b_{2},\dots,b_{D}\right]
and
B\in\mathbb{R}_{+}^{*}
is the polytope edge length along directions parallel with versors
\mathbf{v}_{2},\dots,\mathbf{v}_{D}
. The set
\mathcal{P}_{\bar{\mathbf{b}}}
is created via the last
D-1
vectors of the lattice basis
\mathbf{v}_{2},\dots,\mathbf{v}_{D}
where the basis coordinates lie in a set of rectangular polytopes
\mathcal{R}_{\bar{\mathbf{b}}}
such that
\mathcal{P}_{\bar{\mathbf{b}}}=\left\{\bar{\mathbf{V}}\bar{\mathbf{x}}|\bar{\mathbf{x}}\in\mathcal{R}_{\bar{\mathbf{b}}}\right\}
,
where

\mathcal{R}_{\bar{\mathbf{b}}}=\left\{B\left(\bar{\mathbf{b}}+\bar{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}\right)|\bar{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}\in\left[0,1\right)^{D-1}\right\}=\prod_{d=2}^{D}\left[b_{d}B,\left(b_{d}+1\right)B\right).

(18)

Note that
\mathbb{R}^{D-1}=\bigcup_{\bar{\mathbf{b}}\in\mathbb{Z}^{D-1}}\mathcal{P}_{\bar{\mathbf{b}}}
. Sets
\mathcal{P}_{\bar{\mathbf{b}}}
thus can be used to split the domain of function
f
in disjoint
bands
of width
B
given by
\mathcal{B}_{\bar{\mathbf{b}}}=\left(\mathbb{R}\mathbf{v}_{1}\right)\times\mathcal{P}_{\bar{\mathbf{b}}}
identified using the indices in
\bar{\mathbf{b}}
, such that
\mathbb{R}^{D}=\bigcup_{\bar{\mathbf{b}}\in\mathbb{Z}^{D-1}}\mathcal{B}_{\bar{\mathbf{b}}}
.
By exploiting the smoothness of
f
we can derive that, for a fixed
x_{1}\in\mathbb{R}
,
f
has bounded variation within each band, and thus the folding can occur simultaneously on all coordinates
x_{2},\dots,x_{D}
, which gives the folded signal a particular structure to be exploited in recovery. The definition of the new operator is given as follows.

Definition 2

(Multi-dimensional modulo-hysteresis)
.

The operator
\mathscr{M}_{\boldsymbol{\mathsf{H}}}^{D}
with threshold
\lambda
and hysteresis
h\in\left[0,2\lambda/3\right)
, where
\boldsymbol{\mathsf{H}}=\left[\lambda\ h\right]
, generates a function
z
for input
f\in\mathsf{PW}_{\boldsymbol{\Omega}}\left(\mathbb{R}^{D}\right)
such that, for
\left[\mathbf{x}\right]_{1}=x_{1}\geqslant 0

z\left(\mathbf{Vx}\right)=f\left(\mathbf{Vx}\right)-\varepsilon_{f}\left(\mathbf{Vx}\right),

(19)

where
\varepsilon_{f}
denotes the modulo-hysteresis residual defined as

\varepsilon_{f}\left(\mathbf{Vx}\right)=h\left[M_{\bar{\mathbf{b}}}+\sum_{r=0}^{R_{\bar{\mathbf{b}}}^{+}}s_{\bar{\mathbf{b}},r}\mathbbmtt{1}_{\left[\tau_{\bar{\mathbf{b}},r},\infty\right)}\left(x_{1}\right)\right],\forall\bar{\mathbf{x}}=\left[x_{2},\dots,x_{D}\right]\in\mathcal{R}_{\bar{\mathbf{b}}},

(20)

where
\mathcal{R}_{\bar{\mathbf{b}}}
satisfies (
18
),
\mathbf{x}=\left[x_{1},\dots,x_{D}\right]
,
\bar{\mathbf{x}}=\left[x_{2},\dots,x_{D}\right]
, and
M_{\bar{\mathbf{b}}}\in\mathbb{Z}
satisfies

M_{\bar{\mathbf{b}}}=\left\lfloor\frac{\inf_{\bar{\mathbf{x}}\in\mathcal{R}_{\bar{\mathbf{b}}}}f_{\bar{\mathbf{x}}}\left(0\right)+\lambda}{h}\right\rfloor-1,

(21)

and
\tau_{\bar{\mathbf{b}},r}\in\left\{0,\dots,R_{\bar{\mathbf{b}}}^{+}\right\},s_{\bar{\mathbf{b}},r}\in\left\{-1,0,1\right\}
are the folding times and signs in band
\mathcal{B}_{\bar{\mathbf{b}}}
, respectively, defined as

\displaystyle\tau_{\bar{\mathbf{b}},r+1}=\inf\left\{x_{1}>\tau_{\bar{\mathbf{b}},r}|\sup_{\bar{\mathbf{x}}\in\mathcal{R}_{\bar{\mathbf{b}}}}\left\lvert f_{\bar{\mathbf{x}}}\left(x_{1}\right)-\varepsilon_{\bar{\mathbf{b}},r}\left(x_{1}\right)\right\rvert=\lambda\right\},

(22)

\displaystyle s_{\bar{\mathbf{b}},r+1}=\mathrm{sign}\left[f_{B\bar{\mathbf{b}}}\left(\tau_{\bar{\mathbf{b}},r+1}\right)-\varepsilon_{\bar{\mathbf{b}},r}\left(\tau_{\bar{\mathbf{b}},r+1}\right)\right],

(23)

\displaystyle\varepsilon_{\bar{\mathbf{b}},r+1}\left(x_{1}\right)=\varepsilon_{\bar{\mathbf{b}},r}\left(x_{1}\right)+hs_{\bar{\mathbf{b}},r+1}\mathbbmtt{1}_{\left[\tau_{\bar{\mathbf{b}},r+1},\infty\right)}\left(x_{1}\right),

(24)

where
r\in\left\{0,\dots,R_{\bar{\mathbf{b}}}^{+}-1\right\}
,
\varepsilon_{\bar{\mathbf{b}},r}
is a recursive sequence of functions for computing the residual
\varepsilon_{f}
such that
\varepsilon_{\bar{\mathbf{b}},0}\left(x_{1}\right)=hM_{\bar{\mathbf{b}}}
,
\varepsilon_{\bar{\mathbf{b}},R_{\bar{\mathbf{b}}}^{+}}\left(x_{1}\right)=\varepsilon_{f}\left(\mathbf{Vx}\right),\bar{\mathbf{x}}\in\mathcal{R}_{\bar{\mathbf{b}}}
and
\tau_{\bar{\mathbf{b}},0}=s_{\bar{\mathbf{b}},0}=0
. Furthermore
R_{\bar{\mathbf{b}}}^{+}\in\mathbb{Z}_{+}
satisfies

\left\{x_{1}>\tau_{\bar{\mathbf{b}},R_{\bar{\mathbf{b}}}^{+}}|\sup_{\bar{\mathbf{x}}\in\mathcal{R}_{\bar{\mathbf{b}}}}\left\lvert f_{\bar{\mathbf{x}}}\left(x_{1}\right)-\varepsilon_{\bar{\mathbf{b}},R_{\bar{\mathbf{b}}}^{+}}\left(x_{1}\right)\right\rvert=\lambda\right\}=\emptyset.

(25)

Furthermore, for
x_{1}<0
we have

\mathscr{M}_{\boldsymbol{\mathsf{H}}}^{D}f\left(\mathbf{Vx}\right)=\mathscr{M}_{\boldsymbol{\mathsf{H}}}^{D}\left[f^{-}\right]\left(-x_{1}\mathbf{v}_{1}+x_{2}\mathbf{v}_{2}+\dots+x_{D}\mathbf{v}_{D}\right),

where
f^{-}\left(\mathbf{x}\right)=f_{\bar{\mathbf{x}}}\left(-x_{1}\right)
. Let
\tau_{\bar{\mathbf{b}},r}^{-},s_{\bar{\mathbf{b}},r}^{-},r\geqslant 1
be the folding times and signs computed via (
22
), (
23
) for
\mathscr{M}_{\boldsymbol{\mathsf{H}}}^{D}\left[f^{-}\right]
. Then we define
\tau_{r}\triangleq-\tau^{-}_{-r},s_{r}\triangleq s^{-}_{-r},r\in\mathbb{Z},r<0
.

We note that the operator in Definition
2
is backwards compatible with the one-dimensional operator in Definition
1
. Specifically, if one chooses
\mathcal{R}_{\bar{\mathbf{b}}}
to be a single point
\mathcal{R}_{\bar{\mathbf{b}}}=B\bar{\mathbf{b}}\in\mathbb{R}^{D-1}
instead of a hypercube, then
\tau_{\bar{\mathbf{b}},r}
and
s_{\bar{\mathbf{b}},r}
in (
22
) are the same as
\tau_{r}
in Definition
1
. Furthermore, it was shown that, for
h=0
, the one-dimensional modulo-hysteresis operator in Definition
1
is identical to an ideal modulo operator (
4
)
[
14
]
.

Figure 2:
A random two-dimensional bandlimited input

was generated (a). The ideal modulo output
\mathscr{M}_{\lambda}f
is in (b) and the generalized modulo output

a priori
. In the case of the modulo-hysteresis, folding occurs along straight lines with directions dictated by the lattice
\boldsymbol{\Lambda}
which is known

. This property will be exploited in recovery.

In the following we give a number of properties of the multi-dimensional modulo-hysteresis operator for a bandlimited input.

(Neighboring bands)

The set

M_{\bar{\mathbf{b}}^{*}}-M_{\bar{\mathbf{b}}}\in\left\{-1,0,1\right\}
where
\bar{\mathbf{b}}^{*}\in\mathrm{Neighbors}\hskip-1.99997pt\left(\bar{\mathbf{b}}\right)

Proposition 5

(Variation of
M_{\bar{\mathbf{b}}}
)
.

For
\forall\bar{\mathbf{b}}\in\mathbb{Z}^{D-1}
, let
\bar{\mathbf{b}}^{*}\in\mathrm{Neighbors}\hskip-1.99997pt\left(\bar{\mathbf{b}}\right)
. Let
M_{\bar{\mathbf{b}}^{*}}
and

be the modulo-hysteresis constants for an input

\|f\|_{\infty}B\sqrt{D}\cdot\sqrt{\sum_{d=1}^{D}\Omega_{d}^{2}}<\min\left\{\frac{h}{2},2\lambda-3h\right\}.

.

\mathcal{R}_{\bar{\mathbf{b}}}

(30)

\mathcal{D}f<h/2
due to Corollary

\sup_{\bar{\mathbf{x}}\in\mathcal{R}_{\bar{\mathbf{b}}}}f_{\bar{\mathbf{x}}}\left(0\right)-\inf_{\bar{\mathbf{x}}\in\mathcal{R}_{\bar{\mathbf{b}}}}f_{\bar{\mathbf{x}}}\left(0\right)<h/2.

Using (
30
) and (

\inf_{\bar{\mathbf{x}}\in\mathcal{R}_{\bar{\mathbf{b}}^{*}}}f_{\bar{\mathbf{x}}}\left(0\right)-\inf_{\bar{\mathbf{x}}\in\mathcal{R}_{\bar{\mathbf{b}}}}f_{\bar{\mathbf{x}}}\left(0\right)<h/2.

and

3.5
Problem formulation

The measurements
y\left[\mathbf{k}\right]
are assumed to be samples on a multi-dimensional lattice
\boldsymbol{\Lambda}=\left\{\mathbf{VTk}|\mathbf{k}\in\mathbb{Z}^{D}\right\}
, such that

y\left[\mathbf{k}\right]=\mathscr{M}_{\boldsymbol{\mathsf{H}}}^{D}f(\mathbf{VTk})+\eta\left[\mathbf{k}\right]=\gamma\left[\mathbf{k}\right]-\varepsilon_{\gamma}\left[\mathbf{k}\right]+\eta\left[\mathbf{k}\right],

(34)

where
\gamma\left[\mathbf{k}\right]=f\left(\mathbf{VTk}\right)
,
\varepsilon_{\gamma}\left[\mathbf{k}\right]=\varepsilon_{f}\left(\mathbf{VTk}\right)
and
\eta\left[\mathbf{k}\right]\sim\mathcal{N}\left(0,\sigma^{2}\right)
.
The known variables are the input bandwidth
\boldsymbol{\Omega}
, number of dimensions
D
, the lattice
\boldsymbol{\Lambda}
, modulo-hysteresis parameters
B,\lambda,h
and output samples
y\left[\mathbf{k}\right]
. The proposed reconstruction problem is to compute the input lattice samples
\widetilde{\gamma}\left[\mathbf{k}\right]
defined as

\widetilde{\gamma}\left[\mathbf{k}\right]=\gamma\left[\mathbf{k}\right]+\eta\left[\mathbf{k}\right]+Mh,

(35)

where
M\in\mathbb{Z}
is an unknown integer. The input
\gamma\left[\mathbf{k}\right]
can only be reconstructed up to an integer multiple of
h
given that
\mathscr{M}_{\boldsymbol{\mathsf{H}}}^{D}f\left(\mathbf{x}\right)=\mathscr{M}_{\boldsymbol{\mathsf{H}}}^{D}\left[f+Mh\right]\left(\mathbf{x}\right),

\forall M\in\mathbb{Z}
(
19
,
20
).

4
Detecting modulo-hysteresis discontinuities

We define
N_{d}^{B}=\frac{B}{T_{d}}
. For simplicity, we assume that
N_{d}^{B}\in\mathbb{Z},N_{d}^{B}\geqslant 1,\forall d\in\left\{2,\dots D\right\}
, i.e., for a polytope
\mathcal{P}_{\bar{\mathbf{b}}}
we have an integer number of sampling periods
T_{d}
along each of its edges of length
B
and direction
\mathbf{v}_{d}
. The recovery is achieved in several steps

1.

Compute
M_{\bar{\mathbf{b}}}
, the folding times
\tau_{\bar{\mathbf{b}},r}
and signs
s_{\bar{\mathbf{b}},r}
in each band
\mathcal{B}_{\bar{\mathbf{b}}}
.

2.

Compute residual
\varepsilon_{f}\left(\mathbf{Vx}\right)
(
20
).

3.

Compute the samples
\widetilde{\gamma}\left[\mathbf{k}\right]
.

For step 1, we define a filter
\psi
and compute
\left\langle y,\psi\right\rangle
. For detecting the folding times and signs, we choose
\psi=\psi_{\bar{\mathbf{b}},m}
, which is centered in sample
m
along dimension
x_{1}
in band
\bar{\mathbf{b}}
as

\psi_{\bar{\mathbf{b}},m}\left[\mathbf{k}\right]=\left\{\begin{array}[]{cc}\Delta^{N}\left[k_{1}-m\right]\cdot\frac{1}{N^{B}},&\bar{\mathbf{T}}\bar{\mathbf{k}}\in\mathcal{R}_{\bar{\mathbf{b}}},\\
0,&\bar{\mathbf{T}}\bar{\mathbf{k}}\notin\mathcal{R}_{\bar{\mathbf{b}}},\end{array}\right.

(36)

where
\bar{\mathbf{k}}\in\mathbb{Z}^{D-1}
and
N^{B}=\prod_{d=2}^{D}N_{d}^{B}
is the number of samples in each band
\bar{\mathbf{b}}
for
k_{1}
fixed. For fixed
\bar{\mathbf{k}}\in\mathbb{Z}^{D-1}
,
\psi_{\bar{\mathbf{b}},m}\left[\mathbf{k}\right]
is a finite difference filter along dimension
k_{1}
.

For detecting the
M_{\bar{\mathbf{b}}}
, we need a filter detecting the change in
M_{\bar{\mathbf{b}}}
for two bands
\bar{\mathbf{b}}
,
\bar{\mathbf{b}}^{*}
, such that
\bar{\mathbf{b}}^{*}\in\mathrm{Neighbors}\hskip-1.99997pt\left(\bar{\mathbf{b}}\right)
and
\left[\bar{\mathbf{b}}^{*}\right]_{d^{*}}=\left[\bar{\mathbf{b}}\right]_{d^{*}}+1
. We define
\psi=\psi_{\bar{\mathbf{b}},\bar{\mathbf{b}}^{*}}\left[\mathbf{k}\right]
as

\psi_{\bar{\mathbf{b}},\bar{\mathbf{b}}^{*}}\left[\mathbf{k}\right]=\left\{\begin{array}[]{cc}\Delta^{N}\left[k_{d^{*}}-k_{\bar{\mathbf{b}}}\right]\cdot\frac{1}{N^{B*}},&\bar{\mathbf{T}}\bar{\mathbf{k}}\in\mathcal{R}_{\bar{\mathbf{b}}}\cup\mathcal{R}_{\bar{\mathbf{b}}^{*}},k_{1}=0,\\
0,&\quad\mathrm{otherwise},\end{array}\right.

(37)

where
k_{\bar{\mathbf{b}}}=N_{d^{*}}^{B}\cdot\left[\bar{\mathbf{b}}\right]_{d^{*}}-1
and
N^{B*}=\prod_{d=2,d\neq d^{*}}^{D}N_{d}^{B}
is the number of samples in each band
\bar{\mathbf{b}}
for
k_{1}
,
k_{d^{*}}
fixed. Therefore, similar to
\psi_{\bar{\mathbf{b}},m}
, filter
\psi_{\bar{\mathbf{b}},\bar{\mathbf{b}}^{*}}
is a finite difference filter along dimension
x_{d^{*}}
which is perpendicular to the hyperplane separating bands
\bar{\mathbf{b}}
and
\bar{\mathbf{b}}^{*}
. Furthermore,
\psi_{\bar{\mathbf{b}},\bar{\mathbf{b}}^{*}}
is constant within each band
\bar{\mathbf{b}}
and
\bar{\mathbf{b}}^{*}
. This means that the finite difference filter is repeated
N^{B*}
times across dimensions
d\in\left\{2,\dots,D\right\}\setminus d^{*}
, which has a noise averaging effect.

4.1
Detecting folding times and signs

To detect
\tau_{\bar{\mathbf{b}},r}
and
s_{\bar{\mathbf{b}},r}
, we use filter
\psi_{\bar{\mathbf{b}},m}
to compute sequence
y_{\bar{\mathbf{b}},m}

y_{\bar{\mathbf{b}},m}=\left\langle y,\psi_{\bar{\mathbf{b}},m}\right\rangle=\underbrace{\left\langle\gamma,\psi_{\bar{\mathbf{b}},m}\right\rangle}_{\textsf{Input}}-\underbrace{\left\langle\varepsilon_{\gamma},\psi_{\bar{\mathbf{b}},m}\right\rangle}_{\textsf{Residual}}+\underbrace{\left\langle\eta,\psi_{\bar{\mathbf{b}},m}\right\rangle}_{\textsf{Noise}}.

(38)

In (
38
) the filtered samples
y_{\bar{\mathbf{b}},m}
are composed of three terms: the
input term

\left\langle\gamma,\psi_{\bar{\mathbf{b}},m}\right\rangle
, the
residual term

\left\langle\varepsilon_{\gamma},\psi_{\bar{\mathbf{b}},m}\right\rangle
and the
noise term

\left\langle\eta,\psi_{\bar{\mathbf{b}},m}\right\rangle
.
Given that all three are unknown, the general recovery strategy is to separate them via thresholding; as will be shown later, thresholding samples
y_{\bar{\mathbf{b}},m}
allows to compute the folding times and signs. While this will be derived rigorously later in propositions
6
and
7
, here we give a brief intuitive explanation of the recovery method, by explaining the effect that filter
\psi_{\bar{\mathbf{b}},m}
has on all
3
terms in the right-hand-side of (
38
). A similar analysis applies to filter
\psi_{\bar{\mathbf{b}},\bar{\mathbf{b}}^{*}}
which will be described in Section
4.2
.

As noted before,
\psi_{\bar{\mathbf{b}},m}
is a finite difference filter along dimension
x_{1}
. It was shown for the one-dimensional case that this causes
\gamma\left[\mathbf{k}\right]
to vanish for large
N
. Furthermore, it generates peaks at the folding times in residual
\varepsilon_{\gamma}
and also amplifies the noise
\eta

[
12
,
14
]
. This latter effect is undesirable for recovery. To decrease the effect of the noise the finite difference filter was convolved with a spline in the one-dimensional case, but this also makes the detection of
\varepsilon_{\gamma}
more difficult
[
34
]
. Here we can address noise filtering without affecting the folding time detection by exploiting the multi-dimensional structure of
\mathscr{M}_{\boldsymbol{\mathsf{H}}}^{D}
.

For fixed
k_{1}\in\mathbb{Z}
, the filter
\psi_{\bar{\mathbf{b}},m}\left[\mathbf{k}\right]
is constant along dimensions
k_{2},\dots,k_{D}
as long as
\bar{\mathbf{V}}\bar{\mathbf{T}}\bar{\mathbf{k}}\in\mathcal{R}_{\bar{\mathbf{b}}}
and thus the inner product has an averaging effect. However, we know that the modulo residual corresponding to
\mathscr{M}_{\boldsymbol{\mathsf{H}}}^{D}f\left(\mathbf{Vx}\right),\bar{\mathbf{x}}\in\mathcal{R}_{\bar{\mathbf{b}}}
is also constant within
\mathcal{R}_{\bar{\mathbf{b}}}
by definition, and therefore the averaging effect does not affect the residual edges, which are along dimension
k_{1}
. Moreover, given that
f
is smooth and changes slowly within a band
\mathcal{B}_{\bar{\mathbf{b}}}
, the filter averaging along dimensions
\bar{\mathbf{k}}
has very little effect on
\gamma
. Therefore, along dimensions
\bar{\mathbf{k}}
, the filter acts mainly on the noise sequence
\eta\left[\bar{\mathbf{k}}\right]
by narrowing its p.d.f. around the origin such that its effect gradually vanishes.

As in the one-dimensional case, the modulo output
z
is smooth in-between the folds, and has discontinuities at the folding times. The filter
\psi_{\bar{\mathbf{b}},m}
responds with pulses of non-zero support to the input discontinuities. To account for this, we define by
\mathbb{S}_{N}
the support of the filtered residual such that (see
[
14
]
for details)

\mathbb{S}_{N}=\mathrm{supp}\left[\left\langle\varepsilon_{\gamma},\psi_{\bar{\mathbf{b}},m}\right\rangle\right]=\underset{r\in\mathbb{Z}^{*}}{\bigcup}\left\{\left\lfloor\frac{\tau_{\bar{\mathbf{b}},r}}{T_{1}}\right\rfloor-N,\dots,\left\lfloor\frac{\tau_{\bar{\mathbf{b}},r}}{T_{1}}\right\rfloor\right\}.

The following theorem shows how
\mathbb{S}_{N}
can be recovered by thresholding sequence
\left\langle y,\psi_{\bar{\mathbf{b}},m}\right\rangle
, which is an important step in computing folding times
\tau_{\bar{\mathbf{b}},r}
.

Proposition 6

(Detection of folding times)
.

Let
f\in\mathsf{PW}_{\boldsymbol{\Omega}}\left(\mathbb{R}^{D}\right)
and let
z\left(\mathbf{x}\right)=\mathscr{M}_{\boldsymbol{\mathsf{H}}}^{D}f\left(\mathbf{x}\right)
be the output of a multi-dimensional modulo-hysteresis model with parameters
\lambda,h,B
. Furthermore, let
y\left[\mathbf{k}\right]=z\left(\mathbf{VTk}\right)+\eta\left[\mathbf{k}\right]
be the samples of the modulo output computed on lattice
\boldsymbol{\Lambda}
corrupted by a noise sequence
\eta\left[\mathbf{k}\right]\sim\mathcal{N}\left(0,\sigma^{2}\right)
. Furthermore, assume that
T_{d}<B,\forall d\in\left\{2,\dots,D\right\}
and that

\displaystyle\begin{split}\|f\|_{\infty}B\sqrt{D}\cdot\sqrt{\sum_{d=1}^{D}\Omega_{d}^{2}}&<\min\left\{\frac{h}{2},2\lambda-3h\right\},\\
\left(T_{1}\Omega_{1}e\right)^{N}\|f\|_{\infty}&<h/2.\end{split}

(39)

If
\left\lvert\left\langle y,\psi_{\bar{\mathbf{b}},m}\right\rangle\right\rvert\geqslant h/2
then
m\in\mathbb{S}_{N}
with probability
p>1-p_{\mathsf{err}}
where

p_{\mathsf{err}}\leqslant e^{-C^{2}},\quad\mathrm{where}\ C=\frac{h/2-\left(T_{1}\Omega_{1}e\right)^{N}\|f\|_{\infty}}{\sigma\sqrt{2^{N+1}}}\prod_{d=2}^{D}\sqrt{\frac{B}{T_{d}}}.

(40)

Proof.

The proof is in Section
7.2
.
∎

Due to Proposition
6
, for
\forall\sigma,\lambda,h,\boldsymbol{\Omega}\in\mathbb{R}^{D}_{+}
satisfying (
39
) and a fixed
m\in\mathbb{Z}
, one can choose
T_{1},\dots,T_{D}>0
such that the truth value of
m\in\mathbb{S}_{N}
is evaluated correctly with an arbitrarily large probability. We note that
p_{\mathsf{err}}
measures the probability when a recovery error is possible, but not guaranteed, therefore the error probability is smaller in a real scenario.
A small error in Proposition
6
means a large
C
, which can be achieved by decreasing the sampling periods
T_{1},\dots,T_{d}
or increasing
B,h
or number of dimensions
D
.

The residual
\varepsilon_{\gamma}
, used for reconstructing
\gamma
, requires detecting constants
M_{\bar{\mathbf{b}}}
in addition to the folding times
{\tau}_{\bar{\mathbf{b}},r}
and signs
{s}_{\bar{\mathbf{b}},r}
, as will be explained in the next subsection.

4.2
Detecting constants
M_{\bar{\mathbf{b}}}

Given that we can only recover
\gamma
up to an integer multiple of
h
(
35
), we define
\widetilde{M}_{\bar{\mathbf{b}}}\triangleq M_{\bar{\mathbf{b}}}-M_{\bar{\mathbf{0}}}
, where
\bar{\mathbf{0}}
is the null vector of
\mathbb{Z}^{D-1}
, and recover
\widetilde{M}_{\bar{\mathbf{b}}}
.
Just as the folding times, different values of
M_{\bar{\mathbf{b}}}
for adjacent bands cause discontinuities. However, unlike the detection of the folding times, here we have additional information. Specifically, we know that the discontinuities may only be located at the neighboring sides of polytopes
\mathcal{P}_{\bar{\mathbf{b}}}
. We use the filter
\psi_{\bar{\mathbf{b}},\bar{\mathbf{b}}^{*}}
(
37
) to detect the discontinuities in a similar fashion to detecting the folding times via
\psi_{\bar{\mathbf{b}},m}
. This time, however, the finite differences
\Delta^{N}
computed via
\psi_{\bar{\mathbf{b}},\bar{\mathbf{b}}^{*}}
evaluate variations across dimensions
x_{2},\dots,x_{D}
.

Proposition 7

(Detection of constants
M_{\bar{\mathbf{b}}}
)
.

Let
y\left[\mathbf{k}\right]=z\left(\mathbf{VTk}\right)+\eta\left[\mathbf{k}\right]
, where
\eta\left[\mathbf{k}\right]\sim\mathcal{N}\left(0,\sigma^{2}\right)
and
z\left(\mathbf{x}\right)
is the output of a modulo-hysteresis operator

z\left(\mathbf{x}\right)=\mathscr{M}_{\boldsymbol{\mathsf{H}}}^{D}f\left(\mathbf{x}\right)
with parameters
\lambda,h
and
f\in\mathsf{PW}_{\boldsymbol{\Omega}}\left(\mathbb{R}^{D}\right)
satisfying

\|f\|_{\infty}B\sqrt{D}\cdot\sqrt{\sum_{d=1}^{D}\Omega_{d}^{2}}<\min\left\{h/2,2\lambda-3h\right\},

(41)

Furthermore, let
\bar{\mathbf{b}}\in\mathbb{Z}^{D-1}
,
\bar{\mathbf{b}}^{*}\in\mathrm{Neighbors}\hskip-1.99997pt\left(\bar{\mathbf{b}}\right)
and
d^{*}\in\left\{2,\dots,D\right\}
such that
\left[\bar{\mathbf{b}}^{*}\right]_{d^{*}}=\left[\bar{\mathbf{b}}\right]_{d^{*}}+1
. Assume that

\displaystyle\left(T_{d^{*}}\Omega_{d^{*}}e\right)^{N}\|f\|_{\infty}<h/2,

(42)

\displaystyle\left(N+1\right)T_{d^{*}}<B.

(43)

Then the following is true with probability
p>1-p_{\mathsf{err}}

\begin{cases}M_{\bar{\mathbf{b}}^{*}}=M_{\bar{\mathbf{b}}}+\mathrm{sign}\left(\left\langle y,\psi_{\bar{\mathbf{b}},\bar{\mathbf{b}}^{*}}\right\rangle\right)&\mathrm{if}\left\lvert\left\langle y,\psi_{\bar{\mathbf{b}},\bar{\mathbf{b}}^{*}}\right\rangle\right\rvert\geqslant h/2,\\
M_{\bar{\mathbf{b}}^{*}}=M_{\bar{\mathbf{b}}}&\mathrm{otherwise}.\end{cases}

(44)

where

p_{\mathsf{err}}\leqslant e^{-\kappa^{2}},\quad\mathrm{where}\ \kappa=\frac{h/2-\left(T_{d^{*}}\Omega_{d^{*}}e\right)^{N}\|f\|_{\infty}}{\sigma\sqrt{2^{N+1}}}\prod_{d=2,d\neq d^{*}}^{D}\sqrt{\frac{B}{T_{d}}}.

(45)

.

5.1
Recovery with the proposed operator

We begin with the noiseless input recovery scenario
\sigma=0
where, via Proposition
6
, the set
\mathbb{S}_{N}
is perfectly identified with probability
1
. Furthermore, a constant
\widetilde{M}_{\bar{\mathbf{b}}}=M_{\bar{\mathbf{b}}}-M_{\bar{\mathbf{0}}}
can be perfectly recovered from
\widetilde{M}_{\bar{\mathbf{b}}^{*}}
where
\bar{\mathbf{b}}^{*}\in\mathrm{Neighbors}\hskip-1.99997pt\left(\bar{\mathbf{b}}\right)
according to Proposition
7
. The following theorem proves the input recovery conditions in the case
\sigma=0
.

Theorem 2

(Noiseless input reconstruction)
.

Let
f\in\mathsf{PW}_{\boldsymbol{\Omega}}\left(\mathbb{R}^{D}\right)
and let
z\left(\mathbf{x}\right)=\mathscr{M}_{\boldsymbol{\mathsf{H}}}^{D}f\left(\mathbf{x}\right)
be the output of a multi-dimensional modulo-hysteresis model with parameters
\lambda,h,B
. Furthermore, let
y\left[\mathbf{k}\right]=z\left(\mathbf{VTk}\right)
be the samples of the modulo output computed on lattice
\boldsymbol{\Lambda}
. Furthermore, for
N\in\mathbb{Z},N\geqslant 1
, assume that

\displaystyle\|f\|_{\infty}B\sqrt{D}\cdot\|\boldsymbol{\Omega}\|_{2}<\min\left\{h/2,2\lambda-3h\right\},

(46)

\displaystyle\left(T_{d}\Omega_{d}e\right)^{N}\|f\|_{\infty}<h/2,\quad\forall d\in\left\{1,\dots,D\right\},

(47)

\displaystyle\left(N+1\right)T_{1}<\frac{h}{\Omega_{1}\|f\|_{\infty}},

(48)

\displaystyle\left(N+1\right)T_{d}<B,\quad\forall d\in\left\{2,\dots,D\right\}.

(49)

Then samples
\widetilde{\gamma}\left[\mathbf{k}\right]=\gamma\left[\mathbf{k}\right]-hM_{\bar{\mathbf{0}}}
can be perfectly reconstructed from
y\left[\mathbf{k}\right]
.

Proof.

The proof is in Section
7.2
.
∎

The interpretation of the sufficient conditions in Theorem
2
is as follows. The modulo-hysteresis is well-defined due to a bounded intra-band variation guaranteed by (
46
). Condition (
47
) bounds the
N
-th order difference of the input along all of the dimensions, ensuring that the filter has enough shrinking effect on the input. Finally, (
48
) and (
49
) guarantee enough samples in between the folds (
48
) and within each band (
49
) so that the supports of the filters detecting consecutive discontinuities don’t overlap.

In the general case where
\sigma>0
the following result holds true.

Theorem 3

(Noisy input reconstruction)
.

Let
f\in\mathsf{PW}_{\boldsymbol{\Omega}}\left(\mathbb{R}^{D}\right)
and
z\left(\mathbf{x}\right)=\mathscr{M}_{\boldsymbol{\mathsf{H}}}^{D}f\left(\mathbf{x}\right)
. Assume that
y\left[\mathbf{k}\right]=z\left(\mathbf{VTk}\right)+\eta\left[\mathbf{k}\right]
are known for
k_{d}\in\left\{1,\dots,K^{d}_{\mathsf{max}}\right\}
where
\eta\left[\mathbf{k}\right]\sim\mathcal{N}\left(0,\sigma^{2}\right)
, such that
B_{\mathsf{max}}^{d}\triangleq\frac{K^{d}_{\mathsf{max}}}{N_{d}^{B}}\in\mathbb{Z}
for
d\in\left\{2,\dots,D\right\}
. Then, if (
46
-
49
) are true, then the input samples
\widetilde{\gamma}\left[\mathbf{k}\right]
can be recovered from
y\left[\mathbf{k}\right]
with a probability
p>p_{\mathsf{acc}}
such that

p_{\mathsf{acc}}\geqslant\left(1-e^{-C^{2}}\right)^{K_{\mathsf{max}}^{1}\cdot\prod_{d=2}^{D}B_{\mathsf{max}}^{d}}\hskip-1.99997pt\cdot\left(1-e^{-\kappa_{\mathsf{min}}^{2}}\right)^{\prod_{d=2}^{D}B_{\mathsf{max}}^{d}}\hskip-1.99997pt,

where

\displaystyle C

\displaystyle=\frac{h/2-\left(T_{1}\Omega_{1}e\right)^{N}\|f\|_{\infty}}{\sigma\sqrt{2^{N+1}}}\prod_{d=2}^{D}\sqrt{\frac{B}{T_{d}}},

\displaystyle\kappa_{\mathsf{min}}

\displaystyle=\frac{h/2-\left[\left(\max_{d}T_{d}\Omega_{d}\right)\cdot e\right]^{N}\|f\|_{\infty}}{\sigma\sqrt{2^{N+1}}}\sqrt{\frac{B^{D-2}}{T_{\mathsf{max}}^{D-2}}},

where
T_{\mathsf{max}}=\max_{d\in\left\{2,\dots,D\right\}}T_{d}
.

Proof.

Theorem
2
assumes that Proposition
6
and
7
hold with
p_{\mathsf{err}}=0
for all filters
\psi_{\bar{\mathbf{b}},m}
and
\psi_{\bar{\mathbf{b}},\bar{\mathbf{b}}^{*}}
. To calculate the overall error probability when this assumption is not true, we count the filters above, when used in reconstruction, as follows. There are a total of
\prod_{d=2}^{D}B_{\mathsf{max}}^{d}
bands, and
K_{\mathsf{max}}^{1}
samples along dimension
x_{1}
. Then the probability that Proposition
6
holds for all filters
\psi_{\bar{\mathbf{b}},m}
is
\left(1-e^{-C^{2}}\right)^{K_{\mathsf{max}}^{1}\cdot\prod_{d=2}^{D}B_{\mathsf{max}}^{d}}\hskip-1.99997pt
.

Next, in the case of Proposition
7
, we bound the error probability as follows

\displaystyle p_{\mathsf{err}}\leqslant e^{-\kappa^{2}}\leqslant e^{-\kappa_{\mathsf{min}}^{2}},\quad\kappa_{\mathsf{min}}=\tfrac{h/2-\left[\left(\max_{d\in\left\{2,\dots,D\right\}}T_{d}\Omega_{d}\right)e\right]^{N}\|f\|_{\infty}}{\sigma\sqrt{2^{N+1}}}\cdot\sqrt{\tfrac{B^{D-2}}{T_{\mathsf{max}}^{D-2}}}.

(50)

We note that we do not use all filters
\psi_{\bar{\mathbf{b}},\bar{\mathbf{b}}^{*}}
. Given that we use a set of samples that is contiguous along all dimensions, any band
\bar{\mathbf{b}}
containing samples has at least one neighboring band
\bar{\mathbf{b}}^{*}\in\mathrm{Neighbors}\hskip-1.99997pt\left(\bar{\mathbf{b}}\right)
that contains samples. Then, each constant
\widetilde{M}_{\bar{\mathbf{b}}}=M_{\bar{\mathbf{b}}}-M_{\bar{\mathbf{0}}}
can be computed using a single evaluation of
\psi_{\bar{\mathbf{b}},\bar{\mathbf{b}}^{*}}
, which, in total, is evaluated
\prod_{d=2}^{D}B_{\mathsf{max}}^{d}-1
times, and the theorem follows.
∎

5.2
Comparison to ideal modulo recovery with Gaussian noise measurements

The USF was not analysed in the presence of Gaussian noise, but rather on bounded noise
[
12
,
28
]
. However, USF can still be applied for recovery in the context of Gaussian noise, and the reconstruction would still be accurate in the instances when the noise sample with maximum amplitude satisfies the USF conditions. The modulo operator with Gaussian noise was considered before, but the main objective was denoising, rather than input reconstruction
[
32
,
31
]
. In order to assess the advantage of the new
\mathscr{M}_{\boldsymbol{\mathsf{H}}}^{D}
operator, we provide some insight on reconstruction via USF for multi-dimensional inputs. Specifically, we note that, for an input
f\in\mathsf{PW}_{\boldsymbol{\Omega}}\left(\mathbb{R}^{D}\right)
and a lattice
\boldsymbol{\Lambda}
, the ideal modulo output is decomposed as (see also Section
2.1
)

y\left[\mathbf{k}\right]=\gamma\left[\mathbf{k}\right]-\varepsilon_{\gamma}\left[\mathbf{k}\right]+\eta\left[\mathbf{k}\right].

The recovery method from
[
28
]
involves a
line-by-line
approach, meaning that the recovery is performed along dimension
k_{1}
,
\forall\bar{\mathbf{k}}=\left[k_{2},\dots,k_{D}\right]
. By defining
y_{\bar{\mathbf{k}}}\left[k_{1}\right]=y\left[\mathbf{k}\right]
,
\gamma_{\bar{\mathbf{k}}}\left[k_{1}\right]=\gamma\left[\mathbf{k}\right]
,
\gamma_{\bar{\mathbf{k}}}\left[k_{1}\right]=\gamma\left[\mathbf{k}\right]
,
\varepsilon_{\gamma,\bar{\mathbf{k}}}\left[{k_{1}}\right]=\varepsilon_{\gamma}\left[\mathbf{k}\right]
, and
\eta_{\bar{\mathbf{k}}}\left[{k_{1}}\right]=\eta\left[\mathbf{k}\right]
, the recovery is performed by computing

\displaystyle\begin{split}\left\langle y_{\bar{\mathbf{k}}},\Delta^{N}\left[\cdot-m\right]\right\rangle&=\left\langle\gamma_{\bar{\mathbf{k}}},\Delta^{N}\left[\cdot-m\right]\right\rangle-\left\langle\varepsilon_{\gamma,\bar{\mathbf{k}}},\Delta^{N}\left[\cdot-m\right]\right\rangle\\
&+\left\langle\eta_{\bar{\mathbf{k}}},\Delta^{N}\left[\cdot-m\right]\right\rangle.\end{split}

(51)

We remark that the processing in (
51
) is equivalent to applying filter
\psi_{\bar{\mathbf{b}},m}
(
36
) in the case of the multidimensional modulo-hysteresis operator when there is only one sample per band in all dimensions, i.e.,
N_{2}^{B}=N_{3}^{B}=\dots=N_{d}^{B}=N^{B}=1
.
Even though the noise here is not bounded, we can derive the condition when the USF would work for a specific noise instance, which is
[
12
]

\left\lvert\left\langle\gamma_{\bar{\mathbf{k}}},\Delta^{N}\left[\cdot-m\right]\right\rangle+\left\langle\eta_{\bar{\mathbf{k}}},\Delta^{N}\left[\cdot-m\right]\right\rangle\right\rvert\leqslant\left(T_{1}\Omega_{1}e\right)^{N}\|f\|_{\infty}+\left\lvert\left\langle\eta_{\bar{\mathbf{k}}},\Delta^{N}\left[\cdot-m\right]\right\rangle\right\rvert<\lambda.

for all
m\in\mathbb{Z}
. Thus, recovery is only guaranteed if the noise instance is bounded by

\left\lvert\left\langle\eta_{\bar{\mathbf{k}}},\Delta^{N}\left[\cdot-m\right]\right\rangle\right\rvert<\lambda-\left(T_{1}\Omega_{1}e\right)^{N}\|f\|_{\infty}.

(52)

In a similar fashion to the derivation of (
98
) it can be shown that
\left\langle\eta_{\bar{\mathbf{k}}},\Delta^{N}\left[\cdot-m\right]\right\rangle\sim\mathcal{N}\left(0,\sigma^{2}\cdot 2^{N}\right)
. We remark that the standard deviation of
\left\langle\eta_{\bar{\mathbf{k}}},\Delta^{N}\left[\cdot-m\right]\right\rangle
is always at least
\sigma\sqrt{2}
. Therefore, depending on the values of
T_{1},\Omega_{1}
and
\lambda
, the probability that (
52
) holds may be very low. Conversely, in the recovery with the proposed operator
\mathscr{M}_{\boldsymbol{\mathsf{H}}}^{D}
,
\left\langle\eta_{\bar{\mathbf{k}}},\psi_{\bar{\mathbf{b}},m}\right\rangle
satisfies
\left\langle\eta_{\bar{\mathbf{k}}},\psi_{\bar{\mathbf{b}},m}\right\rangle\sim\mathcal{N}\left(0,\sigma^{2}\cdot\frac{2^{N}}{N^{B}}\right)
(
98
), where the standard deviation can be made arbitrarily small by increasing the number of samples
N^{B}
within each band
\bar{\mathbf{b}}
. In Section
6
this fact will be exploited to achieve significantly higher recovery performance for the proposed operator
\mathscr{M}_{\boldsymbol{\mathsf{H}}}^{D}
compared to ideal modulo
\mathscr{M}_{\lambda}
.

We use two properties of the p.d.f. of Gaussian distributions. First, given

. Second, for a random variable
\eta_{1}\sim\mathcal{N}\left(0,\sigma^{2}\right)
, the multiplication with a constant

Then the noise term

(96)

(98)

,
14
,
34
]

Therefore, the noise term

(99)

where

and

as follows, which finalizes the proof

,

p_{\mathsf{err}}\leqslant e^{-\left(\frac{\eta_{\mathsf{max}}}{\sigma_{0}\sqrt{2}}\right)^{2}}.

(100)

8
Conclusion

The Unlimited Sampling Framework (USF) provides sampling rate guarantees that allow tackling high dynamic range signals in the one-dimensional case. For multi-dimensional signals, USF is typically applied sequentially, thus not exploiting the multi-dimensional structure of the input.
In this paper, we

•

introduced the first multi-dimensional modulo operator and associated input reconstruction method from lattice samples,

•

derived sampling rate conditions under which the reconstruction is perfect in the noiseless scenario,

•

provided probability error bounds under Gaussian noise assumption,

•

showed numerically that, while USF does not allow noise amplitudes larger than the modulo threshold, the proposed approach allows arbitrarily high noise for sufficiently small sampling times.

This work can be extended in a number of ways

It can be coupled with modulo denoising approaches such as

to yield enhanced reconstruction algorithms.

2.

While it is assumed that the input is bandlimited, this work can be extended for inputs generated with B-splines or sparse inputs.

3.

The model in our work can be extended to a wider range of models such as modulo neuromorphic architectures, that could exploit multi-dimensional inputs in a similar way to the biological systems

]

[

]
.

4.

Alternative sampling mechanisms that would benefit from a multi-dimensional modulo operation include one-bit sampling

or average sampling
[

]
.

5.

The current line of work can lead to a the implementation of a new multi-dimensional hardware prototype.
