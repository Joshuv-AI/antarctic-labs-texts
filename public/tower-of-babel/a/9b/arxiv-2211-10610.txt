On the slow roll expansion of one-field cosmological models
Calin Iuliu Lazaroiu

Abstract
We study the infrared scale expansion of single field cosmological models using the Hamilton-Jacobi formalism, showing that its specialization at unit scale parameter recovers the slow roll expansion. In particular, we show that the latter coincides with a Laurent expansion of the Hamilton-Jacobi function in powers of the Planck mass, whose terms are controlled by certain recursively-defined polynomials. This allows us to give an explicit recursion procedure for constructing all higher order terms of the slow roll expansion. We also discuss the corresponding effective potential and the action of the universal similarity group.



Introduction

An important problem in scalar field cosmology is to develop useful
approximation methods for various regimes. Ideally, such methods should
have a sound conceptual basis which is grounded in the dynamical
features of the model. They should also be
effective
in the
sense that they lead to systematic higher order expansion schemes
which are computable in an efficient manner.

In this paper, we consider the scale expansion for single field scalar
cosmology, following the proposal made in
ren
;
grad
for the much
more general case of multifield models. We show that the scale
expansion recovers the slow roll expansion of single field models
while presenting it in a new conceptual light, which allows one to
give an explicit recursion procedure for computing its higher order
terms.

An important special feature of one-field models (as compared to their
multifield counterparts) is the existence of a straightforward
description of cosmological flow orbits through the so-called
Hamilton-Jacobi formalism
of Salopek and Bond
SB
. This
encodes the geometry of regular flow orbits through regular
Hamilton-Jacobi functions
, which are solutions of a certain first
order non-autonomous and non-linear ODE. In this approach, the
cosmological curves of the model can be recovered (up to a constant
translation of cosmological time) by integrating the
speed
equation
of a given flow orbit. The formalism was used for various
purposes in references
LPB
;
Alvarez
;
Handley
(see

(
LL
, Sec. 18.6)
for a brief introduction) and we give a
mathematically precise treatment in the present work.

The scale transformations and universal similarities of
ren

have a natural action on Hamilton-Jacobi functions, which leads to the
consideration of
scale expansions
of the latter. These
expansions can be constructed by looking for solutions of an
appropriate scale deformation of the Hamilton-Jacobi equation which
admit an appropriate expansion in the scale parameter. In this paper,
we focus on the
infrared expansion
, which is relevant in the
slow motion regime. We show that the latter is essentially equivalent
with the
small Planck mass expansion
, which produces an
infrared Hamilton-Jacobi function

\mathfrak{H}_{\mathrm{IR}}
, distinguished by the
property that it admits a Laurent expansion in powers of the Planck
mass. This follows from the structure of the cosmological and
Hamilton-Jacobi equations and matches the Wilsonian expectation that
infrared dynamics is controlled by the Planck mass, which sets the
natural energy scale of the problem.

The coefficients of the Laurent expansion of
\mathfrak{H}_{\mathrm{IR}}
are governed by
a recursion relation, which encodes the condition that
\mathfrak{H}_{\mathrm{IR}}

formally satisfies the Hamilton-Jacobi equation. Studying this
recursion, we show that all terms can be expressed in terms of certain
universal multivariate polynomials
Q_{n}
with rational coefficients
which are themselves determined by an explicit recursion. These
Hamilton-Jacobi polynomials
encode all relevant information
required for describing the expansion of
\mathfrak{H}_{\mathrm{IR}}
, whose coefficients
are recovered by evaluating certain universal rational functions
v_{n}

constructed from
Q_{n}
on the scalar potential of the model and its
successive derivatives. These results reduce the problem of
constructing
\mathfrak{H}_{\mathrm{IR}}
to the recursive computation of Hamilton-Jacobi
polynomials, which can be achieved effectively on a computer. As an
application, we list the polynomials
Q_{n}
and rational functions

v_{n}
up to tenth order inclusively.

We also study the homogeneity properties of
Q_{n}
and
v_{n}
with
respect to two relevant gradings on the algebra
\mathcal{A}=\mathbb{R}[\mathbb{N}]
of
polynomials in a countable number of variables, showing that these
properties allow one to rewrite the expansion of
\mathfrak{H}_{\mathrm{IR}}
as an
infinite sum of monomials in the
potential slow roll parameters

of the model as defined in
LPB
. This shows that the small
Planck mass expansion is equivalent with the slow roll expansion of

LPB
, which therefore also describes the infrared expansion of
one field models in the sense of
ren
up to an appropriate
rescaling of the Planck mass by the scale parameter. Performing the
corresponding substitutions to fifth order in the square of the Planck
mass, we show explicitly that our expansion recovers the fifth order
slow roll expansion as computed in
LPB
. Unlike the approach of
loc. cit., which is rather involved and somewhat inefficient due to
the lack of a master recursion formula, our procedure uses the
recursive construction of Hamilton-Jacobi polynomials and hence allows
for the efficient computation of higher order terms in the slow roll
expansion. This opens the way for precision studies of one-field
cosmological models, thus removing one of the technical limitations in
the subject.

The paper is organized as follows. In Section
1
, we
give a mathematical review of one-field cosmological models,
introducing the notation and terminology used in latter sections. In
Section
2
, we discuss the one-field realization of the
universal similarities and classical RG transformations which were
introduced in a much more general setting in reference

ren
. Section
3
gives a careful treatment of the
Hamilton-Jacobi formalism of
SB
and describes the action of
universal similarities and RG transformations on Hamilton-Jacobi
functions. Section
4
introduces the deformed
Hamilton-Jacobi equation and studies the formal infrared scale
expansion of Hamilton Jacobi functions. We express the terms of this
expansion through Hamilton-Jacobi polyomials and study their
bihomogeneity properties, showing that the infrared expansion at unit
scale parameter is equivalent with the slow roll expansion of

LPB
, which turns out to be equivalent with a Laurent expansion
in powers of the Planck mass. Section
5
presents
our conclusions and some directions for further research. Appendix

A
recalls some basic facts about jets of univariate
real-valued functions while Appendix
B
lists the
Hamilton-Jacobi polynomials
Q_{n}
and associated rational functions

v_{n}
up to order
10
.

1
Single field cosmological models

By a
single field cosmological model
we mean a classical
cosmological model with one real scalar field (called
inflaton
)
and target space
\mathbb{R}
, which is derived from the following action on a
spacetime with topology
\mathbb{R}^{4}
:

\mathcal{S}[g,\varphi]=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{4}}\mathrm{vol}(g^{\prime})\mathcal{L}[g,\varphi]~~.

(1)

Here
\mathrm{vol}(g)
is the volume form of the Lorentzian spacetime metric
g
defined on

\mathbb{R}^{4}
(which we take to be of “mostly plus” signature) and:

\mathcal{L}[g,\varphi]=\frac{M^{2}}{2}\mathrm{R}(g)-\frac{1}{2}g^{\mu\nu}\partial_{\mu}\varphi\partial_{\nu}\varphi-\Phi\circ\varphi~~,

(2)

where the positive constant
M
is the reduced Planck mass,
\mathrm{R}(g)
is
the Ricci scalar of
g
, the
coordinatized scalar field

\varphi:\mathbb{R}^{4}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
is a smooth map and the smooth function

\Phi:\mathbb{R}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}_{>0}
is the
coordinatized scalar
potential
. For simplicity, we assume throughout this paper that

\Phi
is strictly positive everywhere. The action (
1
) is
well-defined when
g
and
\varphi
obey appropriate conditions at
infinity.

The Lagrangian density of the scalar field can be written in the
standard form of a sigma model with potential:

\mathcal{L}_{s}[{\hat{\varphi}}]=-\frac{1}{2}\mathrm{Tr}_{g}{\hat{\varphi}}^{\ast}(\mathcal{G})-{\hat{\Phi}}\circ{\hat{\varphi}}~~,

(3)

where
\mathcal{G}
is a Riemannian metric on an abstract one-dimensional manifold

\mathcal{M}
which is diffeomorphic with the real line and plays the role of
target space of the
uncoordinatized scalar field
, which is a smooth function
{\hat{\varphi}}:\mathbb{R}^{4}\rightarrow\mathcal{M}
. The
uncoordinatized scalar potential

{\hat{\Phi}}:\mathcal{M}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}_{>0}
is a smooth function.
The coordinatized formulation given above is recovered as follows. Let

x:\mathcal{M}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\sim}}{{\rightarrow}}\mathbb{R}
be a global coordinate
(uniquely determined by
\mathcal{G}
and by a choice of orientation for

\mathcal{M}
) for which
\mathcal{G}
takes the standard Euclidean form:

\mathrm{d}s_{\mathcal{G}}^{2}=\mathrm{d}x^{2}~~.

(4)

Then the coordinatized scalar field
\varphi
and coordinatized scalar
potential
\Phi
used in (
2
) are recovered as:

\varphi=x\circ{\hat{\varphi}}~~,~~\Phi\stackrel{{\scriptstyle{\rm def.}}}{{=}}{\hat{\Phi}}\circ x^{-1}~~.

(5)

In particular, the formulation in terms of
\varphi
and
\Phi
has a
hidden dependence on the choice of the Riemannian metric
\mathcal{G}
on the
target space
\mathcal{M}
(which, together with the orientation of
\mathcal{M}
,
determines the choice of the Euclidean coordinate
x
). This fact will
play a role in the discussion of universal similarities given in
Subsection
2
below, namely those
similarities differ from the general treatment given in
ren

when expressed in the coordinatized formulation.

1.2
The cosmological equation

The classical single field cosmological model parameterized
by
(M_{0},\Phi)
is obtained by assuming that

Friedmann-Lemaitre-Robinson-Walker (FLRW) metric with flat spatial

\mathrm{d}s^{2}_{g}=-\mathrm{d}t^{2}+a(t)^{2}\sum_{i=1}^{3}\mathrm{d}x_{i}^{2}

(10)

(where

is a smooth positive function) and that
\varphi

t\stackrel{{\scriptstyle{\rm def.}}}{{=}}x^{0}\in\mathbb{R}

H(t)\stackrel{{\scriptstyle{\rm def.}}}{{=}}\frac{\dot{a}(t)}{a(t)}~~,

.

When

1.3
Cosmological curves and cosmological orbits

An interval
I\subset\mathbb{R}
is called non-degenerate if it is
nonempty and not reduced to a point. Let
\mathrm{Int}
be the set of all
non-degenerate intervals in
\mathbb{R}
and:

\mathfrak{C}(\mathbb{R})\stackrel{{\scriptstyle{\rm def.}}}{{=}}\sqcup_{I\in\mathrm{Int}}\mathcal{C}^{\infty}(I,\mathbb{R})

be the set of all smooth curves in
\mathbb{R}
.

Definition 1.4
.

The solutions
\varphi:I\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
of the cosmological equation
(
12
) (where
I
is a non-degenerate interval) are called

cosmological curves
, while their images
\varphi(I)\subset\mathbb{R}
(which are intervals on the real axis) are called
cosmological orbits
. The cosmological curve
\varphi
is
called
pointed
if
0\in I
.

We say that a cosmological orbit is
trivial
if it
is reduced to a point. This happens iff the orbit is the image of a
constant cosmological curve. Given a cosmological curve

\varphi
, relations (
11
) and (
13
) determine the scale
parameter
a
of the FLRW metric (
10
) up to a positive
multiplicative constant:

a(t)=Ce^{\frac{1}{3}\int_{t_{0}}^{t}\mathrm{d}t\mathcal{H}_{\varphi}(t)}~~\forall t\in I~~,

(14)

where
C>0
and
t_{0}\in I
are arbitrary.

We denote by
\mathrm{Sol}(M_{0},\Phi)\subset\mathfrak{C}(\mathbb{R})
the set of cosmological
curves of the model
(M_{0},\Phi)
and by
\mathrm{Sol}_{m}(M_{0},\Phi)
the subset
of pointed maximal cosmological curves. Given
T\in\mathbb{R}
, the
T
-translate
of a smooth curve
\varphi\in\mathfrak{C}(\mathbb{R})
is the curve

\varphi^{T}:I^{T}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
defined through:

I^{T}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle{\rm def.}}}{{=}}I-T=\{t-T~|~t\in I\}~~,~~\varphi^{T}(t)\stackrel{{\scriptstyle{\rm def.}}}{{=}}\varphi(t+T)~~\forall t\in I^{T}.

This gives an action of the abelian group
(\mathbb{R},+)
on the set

\mathfrak{C}(\mathbb{R})
. Since the cosmological equation (
12
) is
autonomous, the
T
-translate of any cosmological curve is again a
cosmological curve for all
T\in\mathbb{R}
and hence the set

\mathrm{Sol}(M_{0},\Phi)
is invariant under this action. Notice that any
cosmological curve admits translates which are pointed.

A cosmological curve
\varphi:I\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
need not be an immersion.
Accordingly, we define the
singular and regular parameter sets

of
\varphi
through:

\displaystyle I_{\mathrm{sing}}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle{\rm def.}}}{{=}}\{t\in I~|~\dot{\varphi}(t)=0\}\subset I

(15)

\displaystyle I_{\mathrm{reg}}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle{\rm def.}}}{{=}}I\setminus I_{\mathrm{sing}}=\{t\in I~|~\dot{\varphi}(t)\neq 0\}\subset I~~.

(16)

When
\varphi
is non-constant, it is easy to see that
I_{\mathrm{sing}}

is an at most countable set and that the restriction of
\varphi
to
each connected component of
I_{\mathrm{reg}}
is a homeomorphism onto its image
and hence an embedded curve in
\mathbb{R}
.

The sets of
critical and noncritical times
of a cosmological
curve
\varphi:I\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
are defined through:

\displaystyle I_{\mathrm{crit}}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle{\rm def.}}}{{=}}\{t\in I~|~\varphi(t)\in\mathrm{Crit}\Phi\}

\displaystyle I_{\mathrm{noncrit}}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle{\rm def.}}}{{=}}I\setminus I_{\mathrm{crit}}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle{\rm def.}}}{{=}}\{t\in I~|~\varphi(t)\not\in\mathrm{Crit}\Phi\}~~.

(17)

The cosmological curve
\varphi
is called
noncritical
if

I_{\mathrm{crit}}=\emptyset
, which means its orbit is contained in the
non-critical set of
\Phi
. It is easy to see that a cosmological
curve
\varphi
is constant iff its image coincides with a critical
point of
\Phi
, which in turn happens iff there exists some

such that
\varphi(t)\in\mathrm{Crit}\Phi
. Hence for any

cosmological curve we have:

I_{\mathrm{sing}}\cap I_{\mathrm{crit}}=\emptyset~~.

1.4
The cosmological dynamical system, cosmological flow curves and cosmological flow orbits

The cosmological equation (
12
) is equivalent
with the following first order system of ODEs:

\displaystyle\dot{x}=v

\displaystyle\dot{v}=-\mathcal{H}(x,v)v-\Phi^{\prime}(x)~~,

(18)

which describes the integral curves of the vector field
S\in\mathcal{X}(\mathcal{T})
defined through:

S(x,v)\stackrel{{\scriptstyle{\rm def.}}}{{=}}v\partial_{x}-\left[\mathcal{H}(x,v)v+\Phi^{\prime}(x)\right]\partial_{v}~~\forall(x,v)\in\mathcal{T}=\mathbb{R}^{2}~~,

which is the single field model incarnation of the cosmological
semispray of
ren
. The flow of this vector field on the phase
space
\mathcal{T}=\mathbb{R}^{2}
is called the
cosmological flow
of the model

(M_{0},\Phi)
. The planar dynamical system
(\mathbb{R}^{2},S)
is called the

cosmological dynamical system
(see
Palis
;
Katok
for an
introduction to geometric dynamical systems theory).

Definition 1.5
.

An integral curve
\gamma:I\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^{2}
of the vector field
S

is called a
cosmological flow curve
while its image
\gamma(I)

(considered as a submanifold of
\mathcal{T}=\mathbb{R}^{2}
) is called a
cosmological flow orbit
.

Notice that
S
vanishes precisely at the

trivial lifts

{\tilde{x}}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle{\rm def.}}}{{=}}(x,0)\in Z
of the critical
points
x\in\mathrm{Crit}\Phi
of the scalar potential. Accordingly, a
cosmological flow curve is constant iff its orbit coincides with a
point of the trivial lift:

\widetilde{\mathrm{Crit}\Phi}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle{\rm def.}}}{{=}}\{(x,0)|x\in\mathrm{Crit}\Phi\}\subset Z

of the critical set of
\Phi
; in this case, we say that the
cosmological flow orbit is
trivial
. It is easy to see that any
non-constant cosmological flow curve
\gamma
is an immersion. Since
the cosmological energy is strictly decreasing along such a curve (see
Proposition
1.14
below), it follows that
\gamma(I)
has
no self-intersections and hence is an embedded submanifold of

\mathbb{R}^{2}
. The canonical phase space lift
c(\varphi)
of any
cosmological curve
\varphi:I\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
is a cosmological flow
curve
c(\varphi):I\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^{2}
. Conversely, any cosmological
flow curve
\gamma:I\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^{2}
determines a cosmological curve

\varphi\stackrel{{\scriptstyle{\rm def.}}}{{=}}\pi\circ\gamma
by projection to
\mathbb{R}
:

\varphi(t)=\pi(\gamma(t))~~\forall t\in I

and this cosmological curve satisfies
c(\varphi)=\gamma
. In
particular, we have
\varphi(I)=\pi(\mathcal{O})
, where
\mathcal{O}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle{\rm def.}}}{{=}}\gamma(I)

is the cosmological flow orbit determined by
\gamma
.

Also notice that a cosmological flow orbit
\mathcal{O}
determines cosmological
curves
\varphi:I\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
such that
\mathcal{O}=c(\varphi)(I)
through
the first order ODE:

F(\varphi(t),\dot{\varphi}(t))=0~~,

where
F(x,v)=0
is the implicit equation of
\mathcal{O}
. Since this ODE is
autonomous, it determines
\varphi
only up to translation of
t
by an
arbitrary constant.

1.5
Regular cosmological flow curves and regular cosmological flow orbits

Definition 1.6
.

A smooth curve
\varphi:I\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
is called
regular

an immersion, i.e. if its derivative does not vanish on
I
.

Let
\varphi:I\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
be a regular smooth curve
whose image (which is an interval in

) we denote by
J\stackrel{{\scriptstyle{\rm def.}}}{{=}}\varphi(I)
. Then
\dot{\varphi}
has constant sign on
I
, which we
denote by
\zeta_{\varphi}
and call the

of

\varphi
. Since the map
\varphi:I\rightarrow J
is a diffeomorphism,

\varphi
. Set:

\mathrm{t}_{\varphi}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle{\rm def.}}}{{=}}\varphi^{-1}:J\rightarrow I

(so that

) and let

be the speed of
\varphi
.

Definition 1.7
.

The
speed function
of the regular curve
\varphi:I\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
is the map:

\mathrm{v}_{\varphi}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle{\rm def.}}}{{=}}v_{\varphi}\circ\mathrm{t}_{\varphi}=v_{\varphi}\circ\varphi^{-1}:J\stackrel{{\scriptstyle{\rm def.}}}{{=}}\varphi(I)\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^{\times}~~.

Notice that:

\mathrm{v}_{\varphi}=\frac{1}{\mathrm{t}^{\prime}_{\varphi}}~~\mathrm{and}~~\mathrm{sign}(\mathrm{v}_{\varphi})=\mathrm{sign}(\mathrm{t}^{\prime}_{\varphi})=\zeta_{\varphi}~~,

where the prime denotes derivation with respect to

. The regular
curve
\varphi
can be recovered up to a time translation from its
speed function
\mathrm{v}:=\mathrm{v}_{\varphi}
by solving the
speed equation
:

\dot{\varphi}(t)=\mathrm{v}(\varphi(t))~~,

(19)

which determines
\varphi
up to a shift of

by an arbitrary
constant. The speed equation amounts to:

\mathrm{t}^{\prime}_{\varphi}(x)=\frac{1}{\mathrm{v}(x)}~~(x\in J)

and has general solution:

\mathrm{t}_{\varphi}(x)=C+\int_{x_{0}}^{x}\frac{\mathrm{d}y}{\mathrm{v}(y)}~~\forall x\in J~~,

where
C\in\mathbb{R}
and
x_{0}\in J
are arbitrary.

Definition 1.8
.

A one-dimensional connected smooth submanifold

of
\mathcal{T}=\mathbb{R}^{2}
is called

if
\mathcal{O}\cap Z=\emptyset
. A smooth phase space curve
\gamma:I\rightarrow\mathcal{T}

is called

is a regular submanifold of

, i.e. if
\pi_{2}(\gamma(t))\neq 0
for all

.

The discussion above implies that a connected submanifold

\mathcal{O}\subset\mathcal{T}
is the image of the phase space lift

of

iff it is the graph of a

\mathrm{v}_{\mathcal{O}}:\pi(\mathcal{O})\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^{\times}
, which we call

.

Let

Proposition 1.10

Let
\varphi:I\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
be a regular curve and set
J\stackrel{{\scriptstyle{\rm def.}}}{{=}}\varphi(I)
. Then the following statements are equivalent:

(a)

\varphi
is a cosmological curve, i.e. its phase space manifold

\mathcal{O}
is a cosmological flow orbit.

(b)

The speed function
\mathrm{v}
of
\varphi
(which coincides with that
of
\mathcal{O}
) satisfies the regular flow orbit equation:

\mathrm{v}^{\prime}(x)=-\mathcal{H}(x,\mathrm{v}(x))-\frac{\Phi^{\prime}(x)}{\mathrm{v}(x)}~~.

(21)

Proof.

Follows by noticing that (
21
) is the ratio of the
second equation in (
1.4
) to the first equation.
∎

By Proposition
1.9
, a cosmological flow curve

\gamma:I\rightarrow\mathcal{T}=\mathbb{R}^{2}
and its cosmological flow orbit

\mathcal{O}=\gamma(I)\subset\mathbb{R}^{2}
are regular iff the cosmological curve

\varphi=\pi\circ\gamma:I\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
is regular. In this case, we
define the signature of
\gamma
and
\mathcal{O}
to equal the signature

\zeta
of
\varphi
. By Proposition
1.9
, we have

\mathcal{O}=\gamma(I)\subset\mathcal{T}^{\zeta}
. The map which takes a regular
one-dimensional submanifold
\mathcal{O}
of
\mathbb{R}^{2}
to its speed function

\mathrm{v}:\pi(\mathcal{O})\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^{\times}
restricts to a bijection between regular
cosmological flow orbits and solutions of the regular flow orbit
equation, which presents the former as the graphs of the latter.

Remark 1.11
.

The function
\mathfrak{H}:\pi(\mathcal{O})\rightarrow\mathbb{R}_{>0}
,
\mathfrak{H}(x)\stackrel{{\scriptstyle{\rm def.}}}{{=}}\mathcal{H}(x,\mathrm{v}(x))

which appears in the right hand side of (
21
) is called
the
Hamilton-Jacobi function
of the cosmological flow orbit

\mathcal{O}
(see Subsection
3.2
). We will see in Section

3
that the regular flow orbit equation (
21
)
is equivalent with the
Hamilton-Jacobi equation
by the change of
function
\mathrm{v}(x)\rightarrow\mathfrak{H}(x)
, which leads to
the
Hamilton-Jacobi parameterization
of regular cosmological
flow orbits. This corresponds to using
Hamilton-Jacobi
coordinates

(x,h)
on
\mathcal{T}^{\pm}
instead of the Cartesian
coordinates
(x,v)
used above.

1.6
Basic cosmological observables and the dissipation equation

Definition 1.12
.

A
basic local cosmological observable
is a real-valued function

F:\mathcal{T}=\mathbb{R}^{2}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
. The
dynamical reduction
of
F
along
a cosmological curve
\varphi:I\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
is the function

F_{\varphi}:I\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
defined through:

F_{\varphi}(t)\stackrel{{\scriptstyle{\rm def.}}}{{=}}F(\varphi(t),\dot{\varphi}(t))~~\forall t\in I~~.

Notice that the rescaled Hubble function
\mathcal{H}
is a basic
local cosmological observable. Its dynamical reduction
\mathcal{H}_{\varphi}

along a cosmological curve
\varphi
is also called the
rescaled
Hubble parameter
of that curve.

Definition 1.13
.

The
cosmological energy function
is the basic local cosmological
observable
E:\mathbb{R}^{2}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}_{>0}
defined through:

E(x,v)\stackrel{{\scriptstyle{\rm def.}}}{{=}}\frac{1}{2}v^{2}+\Phi(x)~~\forall(x,v)\in\mathbb{R}^{2}~~.

The
cosmological kinetic energy function

E_{\mathrm{kin}}:\mathbb{R}^{2}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}
and the
cosmological potential energy function

E_{\mathrm{pot}}:\mathbb{R}^{2}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}_{>0}
are the basic local cosmological
observables defined through:

E_{\mathrm{kin}}(x,v)\stackrel{{\scriptstyle{\rm def.}}}{{=}}\frac{1}{2}v^{2}~~,~~E_{\mathrm{pot}}(x,v)\stackrel{{\scriptstyle{\rm def.}}}{{=}}\Phi(x)~~\forall(x,v)\in\mathbb{R}^{2}~~.

(22)

With these definitions, we have:

E=E_{\mathrm{kin}}+E_{\mathrm{pot}}~~\mathrm{and}~~\mathcal{H}=\frac{1}{M_{0}}\sqrt{2E}~~.

(23)

Proposition 1.14
.

The dynamical reduction of
E
along any cosmological curve

\varphi:I\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
satisfies the cosmological dissipation
equation:

\frac{\mathrm{d}E_{\varphi}(t)}{\mathrm{d}t}=-\frac{\sqrt{2E_{\varphi}(t)}}{M_{0}}\dot{\varphi}(t)^{2}\Longleftrightarrow\frac{\mathrm{d}\mathcal{H}_{\varphi}}{\mathrm{d}t}=-\frac{\dot{\varphi}^{2}}{M_{0}^{2}}~~.

(24)

In particular,
E_{\varphi}
and
\mathcal{H}_{\varphi}
are strictly decreasing functions of
t

if
\varphi
is a non-constant cosmological curve.

Proof.

Follows immediately by using the cosmological equation
(
12
).
∎

Corollary 1.15
.

The Hubble inequality:

\mathcal{H}_{\varphi}(t_{1})-\mathcal{H}_{\varphi}(t_{2})\geq\frac{(\varphi(t_{1})-\varphi(t_{2}))^{2}}{M_{0}^{2}(t_{2}-t_{1})}

(25)

holds for any cosmological curve
\varphi:I\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
and any

t_{1},t_{2}\in I
such that
t_{1}\leq t_{2}
.

Proof.

Integrating (
24
) from
t_{1}
to
t_{2}
gives:

\mathcal{H}_{\varphi}(t_{1})-\mathcal{H}_{\varphi}(t_{2})=\frac{1}{M_{0}^{2}}\int_{t_{1}}^{t_{2}}\mathrm{d}t\dot{\varphi}(t)^{2}

When the endpoints
\varphi(t_{1})
and
\varphi(t_{2})
are fixed, the
integral in the right hand side is minimized for the curve
\varphi_{0}
which satisfies
the equation
\ddot{\varphi}_{0}=0
, which has solution

\varphi_{0}(t)=\varphi(t_{1})+\frac{\varphi(t_{2})-\varphi(t_{1})}{t_{2}-t_{1}}(t-t_{1})
. The minimum value is:

\int_{t_{1}}^{t_{2}}\mathrm{d}t\dot{\varphi}_{0}(t)^{2}=\frac{(\varphi(t_{2})-\varphi(t_{1}))^{2}}{t_{2}-t_{1}}~~,

which gives (
25
).
∎

Proposition 1.16
.

Suppose that
\varphi:I\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
is a regular curve. Then
the dissipation equation (
24
) for
\varphi
is equivalent
with the cosmological equation (
12
) and hence also with the
regular flow orbit equation (
21
).

Proof.

Let
J\stackrel{{\scriptstyle{\rm def.}}}{{=}}\varphi(I)
. Since
\varphi
is a regular curve, the map

\varphi:I\rightarrow J
is a diffeomorphism and hence the
cosmological equation (
12
) for
\varphi
is equivalent
with the regular flow orbit equation (
21
) by
Proposition
1.10
. In turn, the regular flow orbit
equation can be written as:

\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}x}[\mathcal{H}(x,\mathrm{v}(x))^{2}]=-\frac{2\mathcal{H}(x,\mathrm{v}(x))\mathrm{v}(x)}{M_{0}^{2}}\Longleftrightarrow\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}x}\mathcal{H}(x,\mathrm{v}(x))=-\frac{\mathrm{v}(x)}{M_{0}^{2}}~~,

where we used the fact that
\mathcal{H}>0
. This is equivalent with the
dissipation equation (
24
) when combined with the speed
equation (
19
) of
\varphi
.
∎

2
Universal similarities and the dynamical renormalization group

Cosmological models with an arbitrary number of scalar
fields admit a two-parameter group of similarities which relate the
cosmological curves of models with different parameters (see
reference
ren
). This group acts naturally on the rescaled Planck mass,
scalar field metric and scalar potential of multifield models, as well
as on their phase space and basic observables. As we shall see below,
the realization of these actions in single field cosmological models
is somewhat special since our formulation uses the
coordinatized

scalar field and scalar potential – unlike the abstract formulation
(
3
), whose multifield version was used in loc. cit.

Let
\mathrm{Pot}(\mathbb{R})
be the set of smooth and everywhere positive functions
defined on
\mathbb{R}
. Then the space of parameters of single-field models
with smooth and positive scalar potential is:

\mathrm{Par}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle{\rm def.}}}{{=}}\mathbb{R}_{>0}\times\mathrm{Pot}(\mathbb{R})~~,

where the first factor parameterizes the rescaled Planck mass

M_{0}
. Since most physically interesting basic observables depend on
the parameters
M_{0}
and
\Phi
, in this section we will view them as
functions
F:\mathrm{Par}\times\mathbb{R}^{2}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
rather than as functions
from
\mathcal{T}=\mathbb{R}^{2}
to
\mathbb{R}
.

Definition 2.1
.

Let
\epsilon>0
. The
\epsilon
-scale transform
of a smooth map

f:I\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
(where
I
is a non-degenerate interval) is the
smooth map
f_{\epsilon}:I_{\epsilon}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
defined through:

I_{\epsilon}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle{\rm def.}}}{{=}}\epsilon I=\{\epsilon t|t\in I\}

and:

f_{\epsilon}(t)\stackrel{{\scriptstyle{\rm def.}}}{{=}}f(t/\epsilon)~~\forall t\in I_{\epsilon}~~.

Notice that
f
and
f_{\epsilon}
have the same image. Scale
transforms define an action of the multiplicative group
\mathbb{R}_{>0}
on
the set
\mathfrak{C}(\mathbb{R})
of smooth curves in
\mathbb{R}
. The parameter
\epsilon
is
called the
scale parameter
.

Remark 2.2
.

Let
\psi
be the scale transform of
\varphi
at scale parameter

\epsilon=M_{0}
:

\psi\stackrel{{\scriptstyle{\rm def.}}}{{=}}\varphi_{M_{0}}~~{\mathrm{i}.e.}~~\psi(t)=\varphi(t/M_{0})\Longleftrightarrow\varphi(t)=\psi(M_{0}t)~~.

Then:

\frac{\mathrm{d}^{k}\varphi}{\mathrm{d}t^{k}}=M_{0}^{k}\frac{\mathrm{d}^{k}\psi}{\mathrm{d}t^{k}}~~\forall k\geq 0

and the cosmological equation (
12
) is equivalent with:

M_{0}^{2}\ddot{\psi}(t)+\sqrt{M_{0}^{2}\dot{\psi}(t)^{2}+2\Phi(\psi(t))}\dot{\psi}(t)+\Phi^{\prime}(\psi(t))=0~~.

(26)

This form of the cosmological equation has the property that
M_{0}

“counts the number of derivatives of
\psi
”. When
M_{0}
is small,
one can look for solutions
\psi
of (
26
) which are expanded
as a power series in
M_{0}
:

\psi(t)=\sum_{n\geq 0}\psi_{n}(t)M_{0}^{n}\Longleftrightarrow\varphi(t)=\sum_{n\geq 0}\psi_{n}(M_{0}t)M_{0}^{n}=\sum_{n\geq 0}\varphi_{n}(t)M_{0}^{n}~~,

(27)

where
\varphi_{n}(t)\stackrel{{\scriptstyle{\rm def.}}}{{=}}\psi_{n}(M_{0}t)
for all
n\geq 0
.
Substituting this expansion into (
26
) gives a differential
recursion relation for
\psi_{n}
, together with the zeroth order
condition:

\sqrt{2\Phi(\psi_{0}(t))}\dot{\psi}_{0}(t)+\Phi^{\prime}(\psi_{0}(t))=0\Longleftrightarrow M_{0}\dot{\psi}_{0}(t)=-V^{\prime}_{0}(\psi_{0}(t))\Longleftrightarrow\dot{\varphi}_{0}(t)=-V^{\prime}_{)}(\varphi_{0}(t))~~,

where
V_{0}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle{\rm def.}}}{{=}}M_{0}\sqrt{2\Phi}
is the “leading classical effective
potential” of reference
ren
. This shows that
\varphi_{0}
is a
gradient flow curve of
V_{0}
. In latter sections, we will construct the
rescaled Planck mass expansion (
27
) indirectly, namely by
expanding the Hamilton-Jacobi function of the cosmological flow orbit
of
\varphi
as a Laurent series in
M_{0}
. Notice that (
27
)
can be viewed as a particular instance of the infrared scale expansion
proposed in
ren
, namely the scale expansion considered at scale
parameter
\epsilon=M_{0}
. This is natural from the Wilsonian perspective
adopted in loc. cit., since
M_{0}
sets the natural energy scale of cosmological
dynamics.

Following
ren
, we define:

Definition 2.3
.

The
cosmological similarity group
is the Abelian multiplicative group

\mathrm{T}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle{\rm def.}}}{{=}}\mathbb{R}_{>0}\times\mathbb{R}_{>0}
(where elements multiply componentwise).

Remark 2.4
.

Notice that
\mathrm{T}
is isomorphic with the additive group
(\mathbb{R}^{2},+)

through the componentwise exponential map:

\exp:\mathbb{R}^{2}\rightarrow\mathrm{T}~~,~~\exp(s_{1},s_{2})\stackrel{{\scriptstyle{\rm def.}}}{{=}}(e^{s_{1}},e^{s_{2}})~~\forall s_{1},s_{2}\in\mathbb{R}~~.

However, the multiplicative formulation is more convenient for our purpose.

Definition 2.5
.

The
parameter action

\rho_{\mathrm{par}}
is the action of
\mathrm{T}
on the
set of parameters
\mathrm{Par}=\mathbb{R}_{>0}\times\mathrm{Pot}(\mathbb{R})
given by:

\rho_{\mathrm{par}}(\lambda,\epsilon)(M_{0},\Phi)\stackrel{{\scriptstyle{\rm def.}}}{{=}}(\lambda^{1/2}M_{0},\frac{\lambda}{\epsilon^{2}}\Phi_{\lambda^{1/2}})~~\forall(\lambda,\epsilon)\in\mathrm{T}~~\forall(M_{0},\Phi)\in\mathrm{Par}~~.

The
curve action

\rho_{0}
is the action of
\mathrm{T}
on the
set
\mathfrak{C}(\mathbb{R})
given by:

\rho_{0}(\lambda,\epsilon)(\varphi)=\lambda^{1/2}\varphi_{\epsilon}~~\forall(\lambda,\epsilon)\in\mathrm{T}~~\forall\varphi\in\mathfrak{C}(\mathbb{R})~~.

The
similarity action
is the action
\rho\stackrel{{\scriptstyle{\rm def.}}}{{=}}\rho_{\mathrm{par}}\times\rho_{0}
of
\mathrm{T}
on the set
\mathrm{Par}\times\mathfrak{C}(\mathbb{R})
:

\rho(\lambda,\epsilon)(M_{0},\Phi,\varphi)\stackrel{{\scriptstyle{\rm def.}}}{{=}}(\lambda^{1/2}M_{0},\frac{\lambda}{\epsilon^{2}}\Phi_{\lambda^{1/2}},\lambda^{1/2}\varphi_{\epsilon})~~\forall(\lambda,\epsilon)\in\mathrm{T}~~\forall(M_{0},\Phi,\varphi)\in\mathrm{Par}\times\mathfrak{C}(\mathbb{R})~~.

The transformations
\rho(\lambda,1)
are called
parameter
homotheties
, while the transformations
\rho(1,\epsilon)
are called

scale similarities
. As in
ren
, scale similarities fix the
abstract scalar field
{\hat{\varphi}}:\mathbb{R}^{4}\rightarrow\mathcal{M}
of
(
3
) while acting as follows on the system
(M_{0},\mathcal{G},{\hat{\Phi}})
, where
\mathcal{G}
is the target space metric (
4
) of the
abstract single field model and
{\hat{\Phi}}:\mathcal{M}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}_{>0}

is the abstract scalar potential:

(M_{0},\mathcal{G},{\hat{\Phi}})\rightarrow(\lambda^{1/2}M_{0},\lambda\mathcal{G},\lambda{\hat{\Phi}})~~.

Then the formulation given above follows by noticing that under such a
transformation the Euclidean coordinate
x
of (
4
)
changes as:

x\rightarrow\lambda^{1/2}x

and hence the coordinatized scalar field
\varphi=x\circ{\hat{\varphi}}
and potential
\Phi={\hat{\Phi}}\circ x^{-1}
of
(
5
) change as:

\varphi\rightarrow\lambda^{1/2}\varphi~~,~~\Phi\rightarrow\lambda\Phi_{\lambda^{1/2}}~~.

In particular, a parameter homothety induces a rescaling of the
coordinatized
scalar field
\varphi
, because the latter is defined
using the Euclidean coordinate
x
on
\mathcal{M}
, which itself depends on the
target space metric
\mathcal{G}
. Notice that the action
\rho_{\mathrm{par}}
of
\mathrm{T}
on
the parameter set
\mathrm{Par}
is free.

Let
\mathrm{Sol}(M_{0},\Phi)\subset\mathfrak{C}(\mathbb{R})
be the set of cosmological curves of
the model parameterized by
(M_{0},\Phi)\in\mathrm{Par}
.

Proposition 2.6
.

We have:

\rho_{0}(\lambda,\epsilon)(\mathrm{Sol}(M_{0},\Phi))=\mathrm{Sol}(\rho_{\mathrm{par}}(M_{0},\Phi))~~\forall(\lambda,\epsilon)\in\mathrm{T}~~\forall(M_{0},\Phi)\in\mathrm{Par}~~.

In particular, the set:

\mathrm{Sol}=\sqcup_{(M_{0},\Phi)\in\mathrm{Par}}\mathrm{Sol}(M_{0},\Phi)=\{(M_{0},\Phi,\varphi)\in\mathrm{Par}\times\mathfrak{C}(\mathbb{R})|\varphi\in\mathrm{Sol}(M_{0},\Phi)\}

is invariant under the similarity action
\rho
of
\mathrm{T}
.

Proof.

Let
\varphi
be a solution of the cosmological equation of the model

(M_{0},\Phi)
. Then it is easy to see that

\lambda^{1/2}\varphi_{\epsilon}
is a solution of the cosmological
equation of the model
(\lambda^{1/2}M_{0},\frac{\lambda}{\epsilon^{2}}\Phi_{\lambda^{1/2}})
. In other words, the cosmological equation is
invariant under the universal similarity action.
∎

Remark 2.7
.

The parameter homothety
\rho(\lambda,1)
with parameter

\lambda=M_{0}^{-2}
can be used to eliminate the rescaled Planck mass

M_{0}
. For this, let:

\varphi_{0}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle{\rm def.}}}{{=}}\rho_{0}(M_{0}^{-2},1)(\varphi)=\frac{\varphi}{M_{0}}

(28)

and define the
reduced
scalar potential

\Phi_{0}:\mathbb{R}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}_{>0}
and the
reduced
Hubble function

\mathcal{H}_{0}:\mathcal{T}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}_{>0}
by:

\Phi_{0}(x)\stackrel{{\scriptstyle{\rm def.}}}{{=}}\frac{1}{M_{0}^{2}}\Phi_{M_{0}^{-1}}(x)=\frac{\Phi(M_{0}x)}{M_{0}^{2}}~~,~~\mathcal{H}_{0}(x,v)\stackrel{{\scriptstyle{\rm def.}}}{{=}}\sqrt{v^{2}+2\Phi_{0}(x)}~~.

(29)

Then:

\rho_{\mathrm{par}}(M_{0}^{-2},1)(M_{0},\Phi)=(1,\Phi_{0})

and we have
\Phi(\varphi(t))=M_{0}^{2}\Phi_{0}(\varphi_{0}(t))
and

\mathcal{H}(\varphi(t),\dot{\varphi}(t))=\mathcal{H}_{0}(\varphi_{0}(t),\dot{\varphi}_{0}(t))
. Since

\Phi^{\prime}_{0}(x)=\frac{1}{M_{0}}\Phi^{\prime}(M_{0}x)
, we also have

\Phi^{\prime}(\varphi(t))=M_{0}\Phi_{0}^{\prime}(\varphi_{0}(t))
and the cosmological
equation (
12
) of the model
(M_{0},\Phi)
is equivalent
with:

\ddot{\varphi}_{0}(t)+\mathcal{H}_{0}(\varphi_{0}(t),\dot{\varphi}_{0}(t))\dot{\varphi}_{0}(t)+\Phi^{\prime}_{0}(\varphi_{0}(t))=0~~,

which is the cosmological equation of the model
(1,\Phi_{0})
. In
particular, the positive homothety classes of the cosmological curves
of the model
(M_{0},\Phi)
depend only the reduced scalar potential

\Phi_{0}
. Accordingly, one can set
M_{0}=1
without loss of
generality. Every orbit of the action
\rho_{\mathrm{par}}
intersects the
subset:

\mathrm{Par}_{1}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle{\rm def.}}}{{=}}\{(M_{0},\Phi)\in\mathrm{Par}|M_{0}=1\}=\{1\}\times\mathrm{Pot}(\mathbb{R})\simeq\mathrm{Pot}(\mathbb{R})~~.

The latter is stabilized by the scale subgroup:

\mathrm{T}_{1}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle{\rm def.}}}{{=}}\{(\lambda,\epsilon)\in\mathrm{T}~|~\lambda=1\}=\{1\}\times\mathbb{R}_{>0}\simeq\mathbb{R}_{>0}

of
\mathrm{T}
, whose action on
\mathrm{Par}_{1}
identifies with the action of
\mathbb{R}_{>0}
given by:

\Phi_{0}\rightarrow\frac{1}{\epsilon^{2}}\Phi_{0}~~\forall\epsilon>0~~\forall\Phi_{0}\in\mathrm{Pot}(\mathbb{R})~~.

Hence the quotient
\mathrm{Par}/\mathrm{T}
is in bijection with the quotient of

\mathrm{Pot}(\mathbb{R})
through this action, which coincides with the set:

\mathrm{Proj}_{+}(\mathrm{Pot}(\mathbb{R}))\stackrel{{\scriptstyle{\rm def.}}}{{=}}\mathrm{Pot}(\mathbb{R})/\mathbb{R}_{>0}

of positive homothety classes of smooth positive functions defined on

\mathbb{R}
. In particular, the overall scale of the scalar potential can
also be eliminated by performing a scale transform of
\varphi
.

The
\rho_{0}(\lambda,\epsilon)
-transform of a curve
\varphi\in\mathfrak{C}(\mathbb{R})

induces the transformation
\dot{\varphi}(t)\rightarrow\frac{\lambda^{1/2}}{\epsilon}\dot{\varphi}(t/\epsilon)
of the tangent
vector field to that curve. Since the pointed maximal cosmological
curve
\varphi_{(x,v)}:I(x,v)\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
which satisfies

\varphi_{x,v}(0)=x
and
\dot{\varphi}_{x,v}(0)=v
is uniquely
determined by the vector
(x,v)\in\mathbb{R}^{2}
, we have:

\rho_{0}(\lambda,\epsilon)(\varphi_{(x,v)})=\varphi_{\lambda^{1/2}x,\lambda^{1/2}\epsilon^{-1}v}~~\forall(x,v)\in\mathbb{R}^{2}~~\forall(\lambda,\epsilon)\in\mathrm{T}~~.

In particular, the set
\mathrm{Sol}_{m}
of pointed maximal cosmological curves
of the model
(M_{0},\Phi)
identifies with
\mathcal{T}=\mathbb{R}^{2}
and the

\rho_{0}(\lambda,\epsilon)
-transform of such curves identifies with
the transformation:

(x,v)\rightarrow(\lambda^{1/2}x,\frac{\lambda^{1/2}}{\epsilon}v)~~

of
\mathbb{R}^{2}
. Accordingly, we define:

Definition 2.8
.

The
phase space scaling action
is the action
\rho_{s}
of
\mathrm{T}
on
the phase space
\mathcal{T}=\mathbb{R}^{2}
defined through:

{\hat{\rho}}_{0}(\lambda,\epsilon)(x,v)\stackrel{{\scriptstyle{\rm def.}}}{{=}}(\lambda^{1/2}x,\frac{\lambda^{1/2}}{\epsilon}v)~~\forall(\lambda,\epsilon)\in\mathbb{R}^{2}~~\forall(x,v)\in\mathbb{R}^{2}~~.

(30)

The remarks above are encoded by the following:

Proposition 2.9
.

Let
\varphi:I\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
be a cosmological curve,

\gamma_{\varphi}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle{\rm def.}}}{{=}}c(\varphi):I\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^{2}
the corresponding
cosmological flow curve and
\mathcal{O}_{\varphi}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle{\rm def.}}}{{=}}\gamma_{\varphi}(I)
its
cosmological flow orbit. Then the following relations hold for all

(\lambda,\epsilon)\in\mathrm{T}
:

•

\gamma_{\rho_{0}(\lambda,\epsilon)(\varphi)}={\hat{\rho}}_{0}(\lambda,\epsilon)\circ(\gamma_{\varphi})_{\epsilon}
.

•

\mathcal{O}_{\rho_{0}(\lambda,\epsilon)(\varphi)}={\hat{\rho}}_{0}(\lambda,\epsilon)(\mathcal{O}_{\varphi})
.

The restriction of
\rho(\lambda,\epsilon)
to the
subset
\mathrm{Par}\times\mathrm{Sol}_{m}\simeq\mathrm{Par}\times\mathbb{R}^{2}

identifies with the map:

{\hat{\rho}}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle{\rm def.}}}{{=}}\rho_{\mathrm{par}}\times{\hat{\rho}}_{0}:\mathrm{Par}\times\mathbb{R}^{2}\rightarrow\mathrm{Par}\times\mathbb{R}^{2}~~,~~(M_{0},\Phi,x,v)\rightarrow(\lambda^{1/2}M_{0},\frac{\lambda}{\epsilon^{2}}\Phi_{\lambda^{1/2}},\lambda^{1/2}x,\frac{\lambda^{1/2}}{\epsilon}v)~~,

(31)

which we call the
phase space similarity action
. Following
ren
, we define:

Definition 2.10
.

The
cosmological renormalization group

\mathrm{T}_{\mathrm{ren}}
is defined through:

\mathrm{T}_{\mathrm{ren}}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle{\rm def.}}}{{=}}\{(\lambda,\epsilon)\in\mathrm{T}~|~\lambda=\epsilon^{2}\}\simeq\mathbb{R}_{>0}~~.

Definition 2.11
.

The
similarity action of
\mathrm{T}_{\mathrm{ren}}
is the restriction of
\rho

to
\mathrm{T}_{\mathrm{ren}}
, which is given by:

\rho_{\mathrm{ren}}(\epsilon)(M_{0},\Phi,\varphi)=(\epsilon M_{0},\Phi_{\epsilon},\epsilon\varphi_{\epsilon})~~\forall\epsilon>0~~\forall(M_{0},\Phi,\varphi)\in\mathrm{Par}\times\mathfrak{C}(\mathbb{R})~~.

The
parameter action
of
\mathrm{T}_{\mathrm{ren}}
is the restriction of

\rho_{\mathrm{par}}
to
\mathrm{T}_{\mathrm{ren}}
, which is given by:

\rho_{\mathrm{ren}}^{\mathrm{par}}(\epsilon)(M_{0},\Phi)=(\epsilon M_{0},\Phi_{\epsilon})~~\forall\epsilon>0~~\forall(M_{0},\Phi)\in\mathrm{Par}~~.

The
curve action
of
\mathrm{T}_{\mathrm{ren}}
is the restriction of

\rho_{0}
to
\mathrm{T}_{\mathrm{ren}}
, which is given by:

\rho_{\mathrm{ren}}^{0}(\epsilon)(\varphi)=\epsilon\varphi_{\epsilon}~~\forall\epsilon>0~~\forall\varphi\in\mathfrak{C}(\mathbb{R})~~.

Proposition
2.6
implies:

\rho_{\mathrm{ren}}^{0}(\epsilon)(\mathrm{Sol}(M_{0},\Phi))=\mathrm{Sol}(\rho_{\mathrm{ren}}^{\mathrm{par}}(M_{0},\Phi))~~\forall\epsilon>0~~\forall(M_{0},\Phi)\in\mathrm{Par}~~.

The restriction of
\rho_{\mathrm{ren}}
to
\mathrm{Par}\times\mathrm{Sol}_{m}
identifies with
the action of
\mathrm{T}_{\mathrm{ren}}
on
\mathrm{Par}\times\mathbb{R}^{2}
given by the corresponding
restriction of
{\hat{\rho}}
:

{\hat{\rho}}_{\mathrm{ren}}(\epsilon)(M_{0},\Phi,x,v)=(\epsilon M_{0},\Phi_{\epsilon},\epsilon x,v)~~\forall\epsilon>0~~\forall(M_{0},\Phi,x,v)\in\mathrm{Par}\times\mathcal{T}~~.

Definition 2.12
.

The
phase space renormalization group action
is the
action of
\mathrm{T}_{\mathrm{ren}}
on
\mathcal{T}=\mathbb{R}^{2}
obtained by restricting (
30
):

{\hat{\rho}}_{\mathrm{ren}}^{0}(\epsilon)(x,v)=(\epsilon x,v)~~\forall\epsilon>0~~\forall(x,v)\in\mathcal{T}~~.

Remark 2.13
.

Notice that the rescaled Planck mass can also be eliminated by
performing a renormalization group transformation with parameter

\epsilon=\frac{1}{M_{0}}
, since:

\rho_{\mathrm{ren}}(1/M_{0})(M_{0},\Phi,\varphi)=(1,\Phi_{1/M_{0}},\frac{1}{M_{0}}\varphi_{1/M_{0}})~~.

3
The Hamilton-Jacobi formalism

In this section, we discuss the Hamilton-Jacobi equation of
regular cosmological flow orbits, which is equivalent with the regular
flow orbit equation (
21
) of Subsection

1.5
. We also discuss the action of the universal
similarity group
\mathrm{T}
on Hamilton-Jacobi functions.

3.1
The Hamilton-Jacobi transformation

By definition, the
Hamilton-Jacobi half-plane
is the
open upper half-plane
\mathbb{R}\times\mathbb{R}_{>0}
endowed with Cartesian
coordinates
(x,h)
, where
x\in\mathbb{R}
and
h\in\mathbb{R}_{>0}
. The restriction
of the rescaled Hubble function
\mathcal{H}
to the zero section
Z
of
\mathcal{T}=T\mathbb{R}

gives the positive smooth function:

\mathfrak{H}_{0}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle{\rm def.}}}{{=}}\frac{\sqrt{2\Phi}}{M_{0}}:\mathbb{R}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}_{>0}~~.

(32)

Definition 3.1
.

The
Hamilton-Jacobi region
of the model
(M_{0},\Phi)
is the
closed overgraph of the function
\mathfrak{H}_{0}
inside the
Hamilton-Jacobi half-plane:

\mathcal{U}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle{\rm def.}}}{{=}}\{(x,h)\in\mathbb{R}\times\mathbb{R}_{>0}|h\geq\frac{\sqrt{2\Phi(x)}}{M_{0}}\}\subset\mathbb{R}\times\mathbb{R}_{>0}~~.

The
Hamilton-Jacobi locus
of the model is the frontier
of the Hamilton-Jacobi region:

\mathcal{B}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle{\rm def.}}}{{=}}\partial\mathcal{U}=\mathrm{graph}(\mathfrak{H}_{0})=\Big\{\Big(x,\frac{\sqrt{2\Phi(x)}}{M_{0}}\Big)\big|x\in\mathbb{R}\Big\}\subset\mathbb{R}\times\mathbb{R}_{>0}~~.

Definition 3.2
.

The
Hamilton-Jacobi transformation
of the model
(M_{0},\Phi)
is the
surjective map
\mathfrak{h}:\mathcal{T}\rightarrow\mathcal{U}
defined through:

\mathfrak{h}(x,v)=(x,\mathcal{H}(x,v))~~\forall(x,v)\in\mathcal{T}=\mathbb{R}^{2}~~,

where
\mathcal{H}
is the rescaled Hubble function of the model.

The Hamilton-Jacobi transformation is a double cover of the
Hamilton-Jacobi region
\mathcal{U}
which is ramified above the
Hamilton-Jacobi locus and whose ramification set is the image
Z
of
the zero section of
T\mathbb{R}
:

\mathfrak{h}^{-1}(\mathcal{B})=Z=\mathbb{R}\times\{0\}\subset\mathcal{T}=\mathbb{R}^{2}~~.

The
positive sheet
of this cover is the open upper half-plane

\mathcal{T}^{+}
while its
negative sheet
is the open lower half-plane

\mathcal{T}^{-}
. The closures of the two sheets meet along
Z
. Since

\mathcal{H}(x,v)=\frac{1}{M_{0}}\sqrt{v^{2}+2\Phi(x)}
, the
\mathfrak{h}
-preimage of a
point
(x,h)\in\mathrm{Int}\mathcal{U}
is given by:

\mathfrak{h}^{-1}(\{(x,h)\})=\{(x,-\sqrt{M_{0}^{2}h^{2}-2\Phi(x)}),(x,+\sqrt{M_{0}^{2}h^{2}-2\Phi(x)})\}

and hence consists of two points of the phase space
\mathcal{T}=\mathbb{R}^{2}
which
are symmetric with respect to the
x
axis
Z
. The preimage reduces
to a point which lies on the
x
axis when
(x,h)\in\mathcal{B}
. The Galois
group of the cover is
\mathbb{Z}_{2}
, whose action by
(x,v)\rightarrow(x,-v)
on
\mathcal{T}
permutes the two sheets. The fixed point set of this
action coincides with the ramification set
Z
. The restriction of

\mathfrak{h}
to any of the sheets
\mathcal{T}^{\pm}
is a diffeomorphism between
that sheet and
\mathrm{Int}\mathcal{U}
and hence gives coordinates
(x,h)\in\mathrm{Int}\mathcal{U}
defined on that sheet.

Definition 3.3
.

The coordinates
(x,h)\in\mathrm{Int}\mathcal{U}
defined by
\mathfrak{h}
on each of the
sheets
\mathcal{T}^{+}
and
\mathcal{T}^{-}
are called
phase space Hamilton-Jacobi
coordinates
.

Given a non-trivial cosmological flow orbit
\mathcal{O}\subset\mathbb{R}^{2}
, its
image through
\mathfrak{h}
is a one-dimensional submanifold of the
Hamilton-Jacobi half-plane which is contained in the Hamilton-Jacobi
region
\mathcal{U}
. We denote this image by:

\mathcal{V}_{\mathcal{O}}=\mathfrak{h}(\mathcal{O})\subset\mathcal{U}

and call it the
Hamilton-Jacobi manifold
of
\mathcal{O}
. The
Hamilton-Jacobi manifold is called
regular
if
\mathcal{V}_{\mathcal{O}}\cap\mathcal{B}=\emptyset
, which happens iff the orbit
\mathcal{O}
is regular
(i.e. contained in one of the open sheets
\mathcal{T}^{+}
or
\mathcal{T}^{-}
).
The Hamilton-Jacobi manifold of an irregular flow orbit
\mathcal{O}

meets the Hamilton-Jacobi locus along the discrete set:

\mathcal{V}_{\mathcal{O}}\cap\mathcal{B}=\mathfrak{h}(\mathcal{O}\cap Z)~~.

The connected components of
\mathcal{O}\setminus Z
are the
regular
components
of
\mathcal{O}
. The connected components of

\mathcal{V}_{\mathcal{O}}\setminus\mathcal{B}
are regular Hamilton-Jacobi manifolds. As we
shall see below, regular Hamilton-Jacobi manifolds coincide with
the graphs of regular
Hamilton-Jacobi functions
.

Definition 3.4
.

Let
\mathcal{O}\subset\mathcal{T}\setminus Z
be a connected and regular
one-dimensional submanifold of the phase space which is the graph of a
smooth function
\mathrm{v}_{\mathcal{O}}
. The
H-function
of
\mathcal{O}
is the smooth
function
\mathfrak{H}_{\mathcal{O}}:\pi(\mathcal{O})\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
defined though:

\mathfrak{H}_{\mathcal{O}}=\mathfrak{h}\circ\mathrm{v}_{\mathcal{O}}~~\mathrm{i.e.}~~\mathfrak{H}_{\mathcal{O}}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle{\rm def.}}}{{=}}\mathcal{H}(x,\mathrm{v}_{\mathcal{O}}(x))~~\forall x\in\pi(\mathcal{O})~~.

Definition 3.5
.

The
H
-function of a regular curve
\varphi:I\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
is the

H
-function:

\mathfrak{H}_{\varphi}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle{\rm def.}}}{{=}}\mathfrak{H}_{\mathcal{O}_{\varphi}}

of the phase space manifold
\mathcal{O}_{\varphi}
of
\varphi
.

Let
\varphi:I\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
be a regular curve of signature

\zeta_{\varphi}
and image
\varphi(I)=J
. Since the map

\varphi:I\rightarrow J
is a diffeomorphism, we can use
x\in J
as a
parameter for
\varphi
. As in Subsection
1.5
, set:

\mathrm{t}_{\varphi}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle{\rm def.}}}{{=}}\varphi^{-1}:J\rightarrow I

and let
v_{\varphi}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle{\rm def.}}}{{=}}\dot{\varphi}:I\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
and

\mathrm{v}_{\varphi}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle{\rm def.}}}{{=}}v_{\varphi}\circ\mathrm{t}_{\varphi}:J\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^{\times}
.
Then the
H
-function of
\varphi
is given by:

\mathfrak{H}_{\varphi}(x)=\mathcal{H}(x,\mathrm{v}_{\varphi}(x))=\frac{1}{M_{0}}\left[\mathrm{v}_{\varphi}(x)^{2}+2\Phi(x)\right]^{1/2}=\frac{1}{M_{0}}\left[\frac{1}{\mathrm{t}^{\prime}_{\varphi}(x)^{2}}+2\Phi(x)\right]^{1/2}~~\forall x\in J~~,

(33)

i.e.:

\mathfrak{H}_{\varphi}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle{\rm def.}}}{{=}}\mathcal{H}_{\varphi}\circ\mathrm{t}_{\varphi}:J\rightarrow\mathbb{R}~~,

where
\mathcal{H}_{\varphi}:I\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
is the rescaled Hubble parameter
of
\varphi
:

\mathcal{H}_{\varphi}(t)=\frac{1}{M_{0}}\sqrt{\dot{\varphi}(t)^{2}+2\Phi(\varphi(t))}~~\forall t\in I~~.

We have
\mathrm{sign}(\mathrm{v}_{\varphi})=\mathrm{sign}(\mathrm{t}^{\prime}_{\varphi})=\zeta_{\varphi}
.

Remark 3.6
.

The functions
\Phi
and
\mathcal{H}
provide local coordinates on each
connected component of the open subset:

\mathcal{N}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle{\rm def.}}}{{=}}(\mathrm{Noncrit}\Phi)\times\mathbb{R}^{\times}\subset\mathbb{R}^{2}\setminus Z

of the phase space. Writing
\mathrm{Noncrit}\Phi=\mathbb{R}\setminus\mathrm{Crit}\Phi
as an
at most countable disjoint union of intervals:

\mathrm{Noncrit}\Phi=\sqcup_{j}K_{j}~~,

these connected components are
K_{j}\times\mathbb{R}_{\pm}
, where
\mathbb{R}_{+}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle{\rm def.}}}{{=}}\mathbb{R}_{>0}
and
\mathbb{R}_{-}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle{\rm def.}}}{{=}}\mathbb{R}_{<0}
. If
U_{j,\zeta}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle{\rm def.}}}{{=}}K_{j}\cup\mathbb{R}_{\zeta}
with
\zeta\in\{-1,1\}
is such a connected component, then
the map
(\Phi,\mathcal{H}):U_{j,\zeta}\rightarrow S_{j}
is bijective,
where:

S_{j}=\{(\phi,h)\in F_{j}\times\mathbb{R}_{>0}|h\geq\frac{\sqrt{2\phi}}{M_{0}}\}

is the intersection of the region
h\geq\frac{\sqrt{2\phi}}{M_{0}}
of
the
(\phi,h)
plane with a vertical strip through the interval

F_{j}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle{\rm def.}}}{{=}}\Phi(K_{j})\subset\mathbb{R}
located along the
\phi
-axis. This
map has inverse given by:

x=(\Phi|_{K_{j}})^{-1}(\phi)~~,~~v=\zeta\sqrt{M_{0}^{2}h^{2}-2\phi}~~.

In particular, one can express the restriction of any basic
cosmological observable to a connected component of
\mathcal{N}
as a
function of
\Phi
and
\mathcal{H}
. The derivative of
\Phi
can be
expressed as a function of
\Phi
, which we denote by
W_{j}
:

W_{j}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle{\rm def.}}}{{=}}\Phi^{\prime}\circ(\Phi|_{K_{j}})^{-1}:K_{j}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^{\times}

and we have
\frac{\mathrm{d}\phi}{\mathrm{d}x}=W_{j}(\phi)
for
\phi\in F_{j}
.

3.2
The Hamilton-Jacobi equation and Hamilton-Jacobi functions

Definition 3.7
.

The
Hamilton-Jacobi equation
of the model
(M_{0},\Phi)
is the
following first order nonlinear and non-autonomous ODE for the
real-valued function
\mathfrak{H}
:

\mathfrak{H}(x)=\sqrt{M_{0}^{2}\mathfrak{H}^{\prime}(x)^{2}+\frac{2\Phi(x)}{M_{0}^{2}}}~~.

(34)

A
Hamilton-Jacobi function
of the model is a function

\mathfrak{H}:J\rightarrow\mathbb{R}_{>0}
of class
C^{1}
(where
J
is a
non-degenerate interval) which satisfies the Hamilton-Jacobi equation.

Notice that any Hamilton-Jacobi function
\mathfrak{H}
satisfies the
inequality
\mathfrak{H}(x)\geq\frac{\sqrt{2\Phi(x)}}{2M_{0}}
and hence its
graph is contained in the Hamilton-Jacobi region of the model:

\mathrm{graph}(\mathfrak{H})\subset\mathcal{U}~~.

Moreover, we have
\mathfrak{H}^{\prime}(x)=0
iff
(x,\mathfrak{H}(x))\in\partial\mathcal{U}=\mathcal{B}
. Define:

J_{\mathrm{sing}}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle{\rm def.}}}{{=}}\{x\in J~|~\mathfrak{H}^{\prime}(x)=0\}=\Big\{x\in J~|~\mathfrak{H}(x)=\frac{\sqrt{2\Phi(x)}}{2M_{0}}\Big\}

and
J_{\mathrm{reg}}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle{\rm def.}}}{{=}}J\setminus J_{\mathrm{sing}}
. Then the usual bootstrap
argument shows that
\mathfrak{H}
is piecewise-smooth, namely its restriction
to the open set
J_{\mathrm{reg}}
is smooth. Notice that
\mathfrak{H}^{\prime}
may
have different signs on the various connected components of
J_{\mathrm{reg}}
.

Remark 3.8
.

Let
{\hat{\mathfrak{H}}}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle{\rm def.}}}{{=}}M_{0}\mathfrak{H}
. Then the Hamilton-Jacobi equation
(
34
) is equivalent with the following equation for
{\hat{\mathfrak{H}}}
:

{\hat{\mathfrak{H}}}(x)=\sqrt{M_{0}^{2}{\hat{\mathfrak{H}}}^{\prime}(x)^{2}+2\Phi(x)}~~,

(35)

which has the advantage that the parameter
M_{0}
appears only in the
term involving the first derivative of
{\hat{\mathfrak{H}}}
. One can seek
solutions of (
35
) which expand as a power series in
M_{0}^{2}
:

{\hat{\mathfrak{H}}}(x)=\sum_{n\geq 0}{\hat{\mathfrak{H}}}_{n}(x)M_{0}^{2n}~~,

which amounts to seeking solutions of (
34
) which expand as a
Laurent series of the form:

\mathfrak{H}(x)=\frac{1}{M_{0}}\sum_{n\geq 0}\mathfrak{H}_{n}(x)M_{0}^{2n}~~.

We will see below (see Remark
3.13
) that this is
equivalent with the rescaled Planck mass expansion of cosmological
curves mentioned in Remark
2.2
.

Definition 3.9
.

A Hamilton-Jacobi function
\mathfrak{H}:J\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
is called
regular
if it satisfies the condition
\mathfrak{H}>2\Phi
(i.e.
\mathfrak{H}^{\prime}\neq 0
)
on its interval of definition. In this case, the sign
\xi_{\mathfrak{H}}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle{\rm def.}}}{{=}}\mathrm{sign}(\mathfrak{H}^{\prime}(x))\in\{-1,1\}
is constant on
J
and is called the
signature
of
\mathfrak{H}
.

A Hamilton-Jacobi function
\mathfrak{H}:J\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
is regular
iff
\mathrm{graph}(\mathfrak{H})\cap\mathcal{B}=\emptyset
. A regular Hamilton-Jacobi function
of signature
\xi
satisfies the
\xi
-signed Hamilton-Jacobi equation
:

\mathfrak{H}^{\prime}(x)=\frac{\xi}{M_{0}}\sqrt{\mathfrak{H}(x)^{2}-\frac{2\Phi(x)}{M_{0}^{2}}}~~\forall x\in J~~.

(36)

The following proposition gives a bijection between regular
Hamilton-Jacobi functions and regular cosmological flow orbits. The
bijection is obtained by writing the regular flow orbit equation
(
21
) in terms of Hamilton-Jacobi coordinates on that
sheet of the Hamilton-Jacobi map which contains a given regular flow
orbit. This presents the
\mathfrak{h}
-image of that flow orbit as the graph of
the corresponding regular Hamilton-Jacobi function.

Proposition 3.10
.

The regular flow orbit equation (
21
) for regular flow
orbits of signature
\zeta
is equivalent with the signed
Hamilton-Jacobi equation (
36
) with
\xi=-\zeta
. More
precisely, a connected and regular one-dimensional submanifold

\mathcal{O}\subset\mathcal{T}^{\zeta}
signature
\zeta
which is the graph of a
smooth function is a regular cosmological flow orbit iff its

H
-function
\mathfrak{H}_{\mathcal{O}}:J\stackrel{{\scriptstyle{\rm def.}}}{{=}}\pi(\mathcal{O})\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
is a regular
Hamilton-Jacobi function of signature
\xi=-\zeta
. In this case,
we have:

\mathfrak{h}(\mathcal{O})=\mathrm{graph}(\mathfrak{H}_{\mathcal{O}})=\{(x,\mathfrak{H}_{\mathcal{O}}(x))~|~x\in J\}~~.

Conversely, every regular Hamilton-Jacobi function
\mathfrak{H}:J\rightarrow\mathcal{U}
of signature
\xi
is the
H
-function of a regular cosmological
flow orbit
\mathcal{O}_{\mathfrak{H}}
of signature
\zeta=-\xi
which is defined by
the speed equation of
\mathfrak{H}
:

v=-M_{0}^{2}\mathfrak{H}^{\prime}(x)\Longleftrightarrow v=-\mathrm{sign}(\mathfrak{H}^{\prime}(x))\sqrt{M_{0}^{2}\mathfrak{H}(x)^{2}-2\Phi(x)}~~\forall x\in J~~.

(37)

In particular, the regular Hamilton-Jacobi manifolds of the model

(M_{0},\Phi)
coincide with the graphs of its regular Hamilton-Jacobi
functions.

We stress that the flow orbit
\mathcal{O}
and its associated
Hamilton-Jacobi function
\mathfrak{H}
have opposite signatures. The
proposition shows that the speed function
\mathrm{v}
of a regular flow orbit
can be recovered from the Hamilton-Jacobi function
\mathfrak{H}
of that orbit
through the formula:

\mathrm{v}(x)=-M_{0}^{2}\mathfrak{H}^{\prime}(x)=-\mathrm{sign}(\mathfrak{H}^{\prime}(x))\sqrt{M_{0}^{2}\mathfrak{H}(x)^{2}-2\Phi(x)}

while
\mathfrak{H}
can be recovered from
\mathrm{v}
as:

\mathfrak{H}(x)=\mathcal{H}(x,\mathrm{v}(x))~~.

Proof.

Since
\mathcal{O}
is regular, we have
\mathrm{v}_{\mathcal{O}}(x)\neq 0
for all
x\in J
. The
regular flow orbit equation (
21
) is equivalent with:

\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}x}[\mathrm{v}(x)^{2}+2\Phi(x)]=-2\mathrm{v}(x)\mathcal{H}(x,\mathrm{v}(x))\Longleftrightarrow\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}x}\mathcal{H}(x,\mathrm{v}(x))=-\frac{\mathrm{v}(x)}{M_{0}^{2}}~~,

(38)

where we used the relation
\mathrm{v}(x)^{2}+2\Phi(x)=M_{0}^{2}\mathcal{H}(x,\mathrm{v}(x))^{2}
and
the fact that
\mathcal{H}>0
. Since:

\mathrm{v}(x)=\zeta\sqrt{M_{0}^{2}\mathcal{H}(x,\mathrm{v}(x))^{2}-2\Phi(x)}~~,

the second equation in (
38
) amounts to:

\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}x}\mathcal{H}(x,\mathrm{v}(x))=-\frac{\zeta}{M_{0}^{2}}\sqrt{M_{0}^{2}\mathcal{H}(x,\mathrm{v}(x))^{2}-2\Phi(x)}~~,

which is equivalent with (
36
) with
\xi=-\zeta
and

\mathfrak{H}(x)=\mathcal{H}(x,\mathrm{v}(x))
for all
x\in J
. Since
\mathrm{v}\neq 0
, we have

\mathfrak{H}>\frac{\sqrt{2\Phi}}{M_{0}}
on
J
and hence
\mathfrak{H}
is a regular
Hamilton-Jacobi function. Fixing a regular flow orbit
\mathcal{O}
amounts to
choosing a solution
\mathrm{v}
of the regular flow orbit equation, which by
the above amounts to choosing a regular Hamilton-Jacobi function

\mathfrak{H}(x)\stackrel{{\scriptstyle{\rm def.}}}{{=}}\mathcal{H}(x,\mathrm{v}(x))
. The remaining statements of the
proposition are obvious.
∎

Remark 3.11
.

The signed Hamilton-Jacobi equation for a regular orbit of signature

\zeta
which is contained in the open subset
U_{j,\zeta}=K_{j}\times\mathbb{R}_{\zeta}
of the phase space can be written as follows:

\frac{\mathrm{d}h}{\mathrm{d}\phi}=-\frac{\zeta}{M_{0}}\frac{\sqrt{h^{2}-\frac{2\phi}{M_{0}^{2}}}}{W_{j}(\phi)}~~(\phi\in F_{j})~~,

where
W_{j}
was defined in Remark
3.6
.

3.3
Reconstruction of regular cosmological curves

Let
\varphi:I\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
be a regular smooth
curve of signature
\zeta_{\varphi}
and image
\varphi(I)=J
. Since
the map
\varphi:I\rightarrow J
is a diffeomorphism, we can use
x\in J
as a parameter for
\varphi
. As above, set:

\mathrm{t}_{\varphi}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle{\rm def.}}}{{=}}\varphi^{-1}:J\rightarrow I

and let
\mathrm{v}_{\varphi}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle{\rm def.}}}{{=}}\dot{\varphi}\circ\mathrm{t}_{\varphi}:J\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^{\times}
be the speed function of
\varphi
.

Proposition 3.12
.

Let
\varphi:I\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
be a regular curve of signature

\zeta_{\varphi}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle{\rm def.}}}{{=}}\mathrm{sign}(\dot{\varphi})
and image
J\stackrel{{\scriptstyle{\rm def.}}}{{=}}\varphi(I)
. Then
\varphi
is a cosmological curve of the model

(M_{0},\Phi)
iff one (and hence both) of the following equivalent
conditions is satisfied:

(a)

\mathfrak{H}_{\varphi}
is a regular Hamilton-Jacobi function of signature

\mathrm{sign}(\mathfrak{H}_{\varphi})=-\zeta_{\varphi}
, i.e. it satisfies the signed
Hamilton-Jacobi equation:

\mathfrak{H}_{\varphi}^{\prime}(x)=-\frac{\zeta_{\varphi}}{M_{0}^{2}}\sqrt{M_{0}^{2}\mathfrak{H}_{\varphi}(x)^{2}-2\Phi(x)}~~\forall x\in J~~.

(39)

(b)

\mathrm{v}_{\varphi}
satisfies the speed equation of
\mathfrak{H}_{\varphi}
:

\mathrm{v}_{\varphi}(x)=-M_{0}^{2}\mathfrak{H}^{\prime}_{\varphi}(x)~~.

(40)

In this case,
\mathfrak{H}_{\varphi}
coincides with the Hamilton-Jacobi
function
\mathfrak{H}_{\mathcal{O}}
of the cosmological flow orbit
\mathcal{O}
determined by

\varphi
.

Remark 3.13
.

Notice that the speed equation of
\mathfrak{H}_{\varphi}
can be written as:

\mathrm{t}^{\prime}_{\varphi}(x)=-\frac{1}{M_{0}^{2}\mathfrak{H}^{\prime}_{\varphi}(x)}~~\forall x\in J~~,

with general solution:

\mathrm{t}_{\varphi}(x)=C-\frac{1}{M_{0}^{2}}\int_{x_{0}}^{x}\frac{\mathrm{d}y}{\mathfrak{H}_{\varphi}^{\prime}(y)}=C+\zeta_{\varphi}\int_{x_{0}}^{x}\frac{\mathrm{d}y}{\sqrt{M_{0}^{2}\mathfrak{H}_{\varphi}(y)^{2}-2\Phi(y)}}~~,

(41)

where
C\in\mathbb{R}
and
x_{0}\in J
are arbitrary. This relation can be
inverted to find the equation
x=\varphi(t)
of
\varphi
. In
particular
\mathrm{t}_{\varphi}
and hence
\varphi
depend non-locally on

\mathfrak{H}_{\varphi}
. Also notice that the scale transform
\psi\stackrel{{\scriptstyle{\rm def.}}}{{=}}\varphi_{M_{0}}

of Remark
2.2
has speed function:

\mathrm{v}_{\psi}=\frac{1}{M_{0}}\mathrm{v}_{\varphi}=-M_{0}\mathfrak{H}^{\prime}_{\varphi}(x)=-{\hat{\mathfrak{H}}}^{\prime}_{\varphi}(x)~~

(42)

and hence is obtained by inverting the function:

\mathrm{t}_{\psi}(x)=M_{0}C^{\prime}-\int_{x_{0}}^{x}\frac{\mathrm{d}y}{{\hat{\mathfrak{H}}}_{\varphi}^{\prime}(y)}=M_{0}\left(C^{\prime}+\zeta_{\varphi}\int_{x_{0}}^{x}\frac{\mathrm{d}y}{\sqrt{{\hat{\mathfrak{H}}_{\varphi}(y)}^{2}-2\Phi(y)}}\right)~~.

where
C=M_{0}C^{\prime}
. Relation (
42
) shows that the expansion
of
\psi
in powers of
M_{0}
mentioned in Remark
2.2

corresponds to the power series expansion of
{\hat{\mathfrak{H}}}_{\varphi}
in

M_{0}
and hence to the Laurent expansion of
\mathfrak{H}_{\varphi}
in
M_{0}
.

Proof.

Since
\varphi
is a regular curve, the cosmological equation for

\varphi
is equivalent with the dissipation equation (
24
)
by Proposition
1.16
. We have:

\dot{\mathcal{H}}_{\varphi}=(\mathfrak{H}_{\varphi}^{\prime}\circ\varphi)\dot{\varphi}~~.

(43)

Hence the dissipation equation (
24
) is equivalent
with:

\dot{\varphi}^{2}=-M_{0}^{2}\dot{\varphi}\mathfrak{H}_{\varphi}^{\prime}\circ\varphi\Longleftrightarrow\dot{\varphi}=-M_{0}^{2}\mathfrak{H}_{\varphi}^{\prime}\circ\varphi~~,

(44)

which is the speed equation (
40
). On the other hand, relation
(
33
) is equivalent with:

\mathrm{v}_{\varphi}=\zeta_{\varphi}\sqrt{M_{0}^{2}\mathfrak{H}_{\varphi}^{2}-2\Phi}~~,

where we used the fact that

\mathrm{sign}(\mathrm{v}_{\varphi})=\mathrm{sign}(\dot{\varphi})=\zeta_{\varphi}
. This shows
that the speed equation (
40
) of
\mathfrak{H}_{\varphi}
is equivalent
with the signed Hamilton-Jacobi equation (
39
). Since

\dot{\mathcal{H}}_{\varphi}<0
, relation (
43
) gives

\mathrm{sign}(\mathfrak{H}_{\varphi}^{\prime})=-\mathrm{sign}(\dot{\varphi})=-\zeta_{\varphi}
(this also
follows from the speed equation (
40
)). It is clear from
(
33
) that
\mathfrak{H}_{\varphi}
coincides with the Hamilton-Jacobi
function of the cosmological flow orbit
\mathcal{O}
determined by
\varphi
.
∎

3.4
The Hamilton-Jacobi potential of a regular cosmological flow orbit

Definition 3.14
.

The
Hamilton-Jacobi potential
of a regular cosmological flow
orbit
\mathcal{O}
of the model
(M_{0},\Phi)
is the function
V_{\mathcal{O}}:J\stackrel{{\scriptstyle{\rm def.}}}{{=}}\pi(\mathcal{O})\rightarrow\mathcal{U}
defined through:

V_{\mathcal{O}}(x)\stackrel{{\scriptstyle{\rm def.}}}{{=}}M_{0}^{2}\mathfrak{H}_{\mathcal{O}}(x)~~\forall x\in J~~,

where
\mathfrak{H}_{\mathcal{O}}
is the Hamilton-Jacobi function of
\mathcal{O}
.

Let
\varphi:I\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
be a regular cosmological
curve with cosmological flow orbit
\mathcal{O}
. Then the
cosmological orbit of
\varphi
is the interval
J=\pi(\mathcal{O})\subset\mathbb{R}
. Since
x=\varphi(t)
and
v_{\varphi}(t)=\dot{\varphi}(t)
, the
speed equation (
40
) of
\mathfrak{H}_{\varphi}
is equivalent with the
gradient flow equation:

\dot{\varphi}(t)=-V^{\prime}_{\mathcal{O}}(\varphi(t))~~

(45)

of
V_{\mathcal{O}}
. This equation determines
\varphi
up to an arbitrary
constant translation of
t
when the cosmological flow orbit
\mathcal{O}
is
fixed. Hence the problem of computing the regular cosmological curves
of a given model can be approached in two steps:

1.

Solve the Hamilton-Jacobi equation to find the regular
Hamilton-Jacobi functions, which determine the regular cosmological
flow orbits
\mathcal{O}
of the model.

5
Conclusions and further directions

Following the ideas of
ren
;
grad
, we constructed the infrared
scale expansion of single field cosmological models using the
Hamilton-Jacobi formalism of
SB
, finding that
it corresponds to seeking a solution of the Hamilton-Jacobi equation
of such models which admits a Laurent expansion in powers of the
Planck mass. We showed that this recovers the celebrated slow roll
expansion of Liddle, Parsons and Barrow
LPB
, which it
identifies with the small Planck mass expansion of such models. We
also described the single field realization of the universal
similarity group action of
ren
;
grad
both at the level of
cosmological curves and in the Hamilton-Jacobi formalism.

Unlike the approach of
LPB
, the method of the present paper
leads to an explicit recursive construction of the coefficients of the
slow roll expansion. We showed that the latter are obtained by
evaluating certain model-independent rational functions
v_{n}
on the
scalar potential of the model and its successive derivatives. In turn,
these rational functions are obtained by explicit universal formulas
from the
Hamilton-Jacobi polynomials

Q_{n}
. The latter are
model-independent multivariate polynomials with rational coefficients
defined by a recursion relation which allows for their efficient
computation. As an application, we listed the coefficients of the
Hamilton-Jacobi slow roll/small Planck mass expansion up to order 10
inclusively (see Appendix
B
). The identification of the
small Planck mass and slow roll expansions follows from the fact that
the polynomials
Q_{n}
are bihomogeneous with respect to a certain
bigrading of the algebra of polynomials in a countable number of
variables.

The present paper opens up a few avenues for further research. First,
one could perform a systematic study of Hamilton-Jacobi polynomials
and their deeper properties, on which we only touched upon in the
present paper. This would make it possible to extract asymptotic
bounds for the small Planck mass/slow roll expansion which would
enable one to analyze its summability and resummation. In this regard,
we notice that the deformed Hamilton-Jacobi equation considered in the
present paper can be approached with the well-established methods of
singular perturbation theory for ODEs with a small
parameter. Second, one can apply similar methods to the UV expansion
of
ren
, which in the single field case should be related to the
psi series expansion discussed in
MA
;
HLT
. Finally, one can
study the IR and UV expansions proposed in
ren
for multifield
cosmological models, which should provide a generalization of the slow
roll and psi series expansions to the multifield case. We hope to
report on these and related questions in future work.
