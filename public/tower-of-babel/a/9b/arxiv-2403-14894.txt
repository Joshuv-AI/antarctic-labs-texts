On the trajectories of a particle in a translation invariant involutive field Thanks: Key words and phrases: lattice points, partition with parabolas, modular prime covering, discrete trajectory, parabolic-taxicab distance, translation-invariant-involutive operator
Cristian Cobeli, Alexandru Zaharescu

Abstract
. We introduce a double-folded operator that, upon iterative application, generates a dynamical system with two types of trajectories: a cyclic one and, another that grows endlessly on parabolas. These trajectories produce two distinct partitions of the set of lattice points in the plane. Our object is to analyze these trajectories and to point out a few special arithmetic properties of the integers they represent. We also introduce and study the parabolic-taxicab distance, which measures the fast traveling on the steps of the stairs defined by points on the parabolic trajectories whose coordinates are based on triangular numbers.

2.
Preparatory Lemmas

Lemma 2.1
.

The operators defined by (
1
)
have the following properties:

(1)

F^{\prime}
and
F^{\prime\prime}
are involutions
if and only if either
\alpha=-1
or
they are equal to the identity.

(2)

F^{\prime}
and
F^{\prime\prime}
are invariant under translations
if and only if
\alpha+\beta=1
.

(3)

F^{\prime}
and
F^{\prime\prime}
are at the same time both involutions and invariant to translations
if and only if either
\alpha=-1
and
\beta=2
or
F^{\prime}
and
F^{\prime\prime}
are
equal to the identity.

Proof.

Due to the symmetry of the definitions,
it is enough to prove the lemma for just one of the operators
F^{\prime}
or
F^{\prime\prime}
.

Checking for the involution property,
we note that

F^{\prime}\big(F^{\prime}(x,y)\big)=F^{\prime}(\alpha x+\beta y,y)=(\alpha(\alpha x+\beta y)+\beta y,y)=(\alpha^{2}x+\beta(\alpha+1)y,y).

Then, the condition for
F^{\prime}
to be an
involution is that

\alpha^{2}x+\beta(\alpha+1)y=x

for all integers
x
and
y
,
which occurs only if both equalities

\alpha^{2}=1
and
\beta(\alpha+1)=0
hold.
Therefore
F^{\prime}
is an involution if either
\alpha=-1
or
\alpha=1
and
\beta=0
, that is,

\alpha=-1
or
F^{\prime}=Id
.

The condition for
F^{\prime}
to be invariant under translations is

F^{\prime}(x+h,y+h)=F^{\prime}(x,y)+h
for all integers
x,y,h
.
Since the equality holds on the second coordinate,
this is equivalent with

\alpha(x+h)+\beta(y+h)+1=\alpha x+\beta y+1+h,\ \ \text{ for all $x,y,h\in\mathbb{Z}$},

which holds if and only if
\beta=1-\alpha
.
This concludes the proof of the lemma.
∎

Lemma 2.2
.

Let
a,b
be integers and, for any integer

, let

denote the composition of
n

3.3.
Cycles representing only primes

Some other interesting sets of integers represented by cycles are:

\displaystyle\mathcal{R}_{K}^{{|\cdot|}}(13,31)

in which all six represented natural numbers are primes.

Since the elements in any
\mathcal{R}_{K}^{{|\cdot|}}
must verify condition (
11

these sets can be expressed as the
Goldbach-twin-prime
hex-tuples

\mathcal{R}_{K}^{{|\cdot|}}(p,q)=\big\{p,q,p-q+1,p-2,q-2,p-q-1\big\}.

By the works of Montgomery and Vaughn
[

[

[

an exceptional set, the ‘Goldbach-prime’ requirements on

and
z-2=p-q-1
are separately satisfied
infinitely often.
However, imposing the additional twin-prime condition makes it difficult
to show the existence of infinitely many hex-prime triples (
16
).

An investigation in small ranges shows that there are actually plenty of
sets
\mathcal{R}_{K}^{{|\cdot|}}(x,y)
that are composed of prime numbers only.
Thus, let
\mathcal{P}
denote the set of prime numbers and, for any

,

,

\mathcal{G}(T)=\big\{(7,13);(13,19);(19,31);(31,43);(43,61);(61,73);(73,103);(103,109);\dots\big\}.

where we chose to show only the first pair from the lexicographically sorted
\mathcal{G}(T)
so that the first components of the pairs are different.
If
T=10^{5}
, then
\mathcal{G}(T)
has
\#\mathcal{G}(10^{5})=4120
elements.

Naturally, there are many pairs of primes
(p,q)\neq(p^{\prime},q^{\prime})

for which
\mathcal{R}_{K}^{{|\cdot|}}(p,q)=\mathcal{R}_{K}^{{|\cdot|}}(p^{\prime},q^{\prime})
, but still,

\mathcal{G}(10^{5})
contains
2064
pairs
(p,q)

that generate distinct sets of primes
\mathcal{R}_{K}^{{|\cdot|}}(p,q)
.

We remark that for
T\leq 10^{5}
the first components
of the pairs in
\mathcal{G}(T)
are the largest of the primes in the
twin prime pairs that are
\geq 7
,
and this is likely to hold for larger

.

3.4.
An
\ell^{2}
average length of the steps of paths in
K
-generated cycles

The size of steps in the path of a
K
-cycle generated by
(x,y)
are, according to (
10
), the terms of the following total length:

\operatorname{length}_{K}(x,y):=|2x-y-1|+|x+y-2|+|x-2y+1|+|2x-y-1|+|x+y-2|+|x-2y+1|.

(17)

In order to simplify the discussion on the signs of the six terms of

\operatorname{length}_{K}(x,y)
, we introduce
S(x,y)
defined to be the sum of their squares.
This proves to be

S(x,y)=12(x^{2}+y^{2}-xy-x-y+1).

Then, for integers
T\geq 1
, we define the square-steps average by

A_{step}(T):=\frac{1}{\sqrt{6}(2T+1)}\left(\sum_{x=-T}^{T}\sum_{y=-T}^{T}S(x,y)\right)^{1/2}.

(18)

The double sum above can be calculated exactly. Indeed, we have:

\begin{split}\sum_{x=-T}^{T}\sum_{y=-T}^{T}S(x,y)&=\sum_{x=-T}^{T}\Big(8T^{3}+12(2T+1)x^{2}+12T^{2}-12(2T+1)x+28T+12\Big)\\
&=4\big(8T^{4}+16T^{3}+22T^{2}+14T+3\big)\\
&=4(2T+1)^{2}\big(2T^{2}+2T+3\big).\end{split}

On inserting this in (
18
), we obtain

\begin{split}A_{step}(T)&=\mbox{\small$\displaystyle\frac{2}{\sqrt{6}}$}\left(2T^{2}+2T+3\right)^{1/2}=\mbox{\small$\displaystyle\frac{2}{\sqrt{3}}$}T\big(1+O(1/T)\big)^{1/2}=\mbox{\small$\displaystyle\frac{2}{\sqrt{3}}$}T+O(1).\end{split}

3.5.
The average length of paths in
K
-generated cycles

Let
T\geq 1
, and define

A_{path}(T):=\frac{1}{(2T+1)^{2}}\sum_{x=-T}^{T}\sum_{y=-T}^{T}\operatorname{length}(x,y).

Note that both

A_{step}(T)
and
A_{path}(T)
are slightly biased
being weighted sums, since each path is counted in the sums as many times
as the number of its nodes that lie in
[-T,T]^{2}
.
Thus, the multiplicities of the appearance of these paths in the sums are
1,2,3,4,6
.

Due to the symmetries, the steps in a cycle may have only two different lengths, so that

\operatorname{length}_{K}(x,y)=4|2x-y-1|+2|x+y-2|.

Further, it follows that

\begin{split}A_{path}(T)&=\frac{1}{(2T+1)^{2}}\int_{-T}^{T}\int_{-T}^{T}\big(4|2x-y-1|+2|x+y-2|\big)\,\differential x\differential y+O(1)\\
&=\frac{1}{4T^{2}}\int_{-T}^{T}\int_{-T}^{T}\big(4|2x-y|+2|x+y|\big)\,\differential x\differential y+O(1),\end{split}

(19)

since on each unit square
u(x,y)\subset[-T,T]^{2}
, the size of

\operatorname{length}(x,y)
differs by at most a fixed independent, absolute constant from the double integral of

\operatorname{length}(x,y)
on
u(x,y)
.
Then, taking into account the signs of the terms, (
19
) becomes

\begin{split}A_{path}(T)=&\frac{1}{2T^{2}}\int_{-T/2}^{T/2}\left(\int_{-T}^{2x}4(2x-y)\,\differential y\right)\differential x+\frac{1}{2T^{2}}\int_{T/2}^{T}\left(\int_{-T}^{T}4(2x-y)\,\differential y\right)\differential x\\
&+\frac{1}{2T^{2}}\int_{-T}^{T}\left(\int_{-x}^{T}2(x+y)\,\differential y\right)\differential x+O(1).\end{split}

Evaluating the integrals, we find

\begin{split}A_{path}(T)=&\frac{17}{3}T+O(1).\end{split}

One should remark the difference between the average
length of the path given as the six times the
\ell^{2}
average of the steps
by
6A_{steps}=\mbox{\small$\displaystyle\frac{12}{\sqrt{3}}$}T+O(1)
and the shorter average estimate

A_{path}(T)=\frac{17}{3}T+O(1)
,
fact influenced by the varying lengths of the steps in the same path.

Proof of Theorem

4.1.
One equivalence class

Let the pair
(a,b)\in\mathbb{Z}^{2}
be fixed and suppose there exists
m\in\mathbb{Z}

such that
m\leq\min(x,y)
for any
\mathscr{P}(a,b)
.
Suppose
m
is the largest of these lower bounds.
Then,
m
is a component of a pair in
\mathscr{P}(a,b)
,
which by the symmetry we may assume that it is the first,
that is, there exists
u\in\mathbb{Z}
such that
(m,u)\in\mathscr{P}(a,b)
.

Then
L^{\prime\prime}(m,u)=(m,-u+2m+1)\in\mathscr{P}_{L}(a,b)
, which implies

m\leq 2m-u+1
. Therefore
u\leq m+1
.
There are only two possibilities: either
u=m
or
u=m+1
.

If
u=m
, then
(m,m)\in\mathscr{P}_{L}(a,b)
, which implies that

\mathscr{P}(a,b)=\mathscr{P}(0,0)+m
.

If
u=m+1
, then we find that the pair
(m,m+1)
is equivalent
with the pair
(m,m)
, because
L^{\prime\prime}(m,m+1)=(m,-(m+1)+2m+1)=(m,m)
.
Therefore, in this case,
(m,m)
also belongs to
\mathscr{P}(a,b)
.

In conclusion, any set of pairs
\mathscr{P}(a,b)
is equivalent with

\mathscr{P}(0,0)
because they are the same modulo a translation by
m

\mathscr{P}(a,b)=\mathscr{P}(0,0)+m,

where
m=\min\big\{\mathscr{R}_{L}(a,b)\big\}
.
One can follow this relation over the parabolas represented in Figure
8
.

The parabolas of equations

from

4.2.
The existence of
\min\big\{\mathscr{R}_{L}(a,b)\big\}

Let

.
First, we show that there is a point in
\mathscr{P}_{L}(a,b)

Then, we will observe that
starting from that point and iteratively applying the operators
in
\{L^{\prime},L^{\prime\prime}\}
alternatively,
we will see that none of the points obtained in this way will
have coordinates smaller than those of its predecessor.

Let
(x,y)\in\mathscr{P}_{L}(a,b)
and let
s=\absolutevalue{x-y}
be the distance
between the coordinates. By the definition, we gave

L^{\prime}(x,y)=(-x+2y+1,y)\ \ \text{ and }\ \ L^{\prime\prime}(x,y)=(x,2x-y+1).

(20)

Then, the distances between the coordinates of
the points obtained are

\absolutevalue{-x+2y+1-y}=\absolutevalue{(x-y)-1}\ \ \text{ and }\ \ \absolutevalue{2x-y+1-x}=\absolutevalue{(x-y)+1}.

(21)

This shows that, unless
x=y
, the distances between
the coordinates of points
L^{\prime}(x,y)
and
L^{\prime\prime}(x,y)

are equal to
s-1
and
s+1
respectively.
By repeatedly applying the operators
L^{\prime}
and
L^{\prime\prime}

on those points with coordinates that gradually approach each

s

\mathscr{P}_{L}(a,b)
that has both coordinates equal.

We should also note one more thing in regard to (
20
) and (
21
).
By checking separately the cases
x>y
and
x<y
,
we further find that when
the components of the points move away from each other,
their size does not decrease.

As a consequence, we have shown that there exists
m\in\mathbb{Z}

such that
(m,m)\in\mathscr{P}_{L}(a,b)

.

6.
The parabolic-taxicab distance

Besides the operators
K
and
L
defined by (
2
) and (
3
),
we consider a third operator that enables
movement horizontally and vertically towards the closest neighbors:

\displaystyle M^{\prime}(x,y)

\displaystyle=(x+1,y),

\displaystyle M^{\prime\prime}(x,y)

\displaystyle=(x-1,y),

(26)

\displaystyle M^{\prime\prime\prime}(x,y)

\displaystyle=(x,y+1),

\displaystyle M^{iv}(x,y)

\displaystyle=(x,y-1).

(27)

Denote
\mathcal{M}:=\{M^{\prime},M^{\prime\prime},M^{\prime\prime\prime},M^{iv}\}
.
Then, the operators in
\mathcal{M}
can be used to define

\operatorname{d_{M}}(P,Q)
,
the
taxicab distance
between two points
P
and
Q
,
as the minimum length of the path that connects points
P
and
Q

by moving on the square grid that connects the lattice points with integer coordinates, that is,

\operatorname{d_{M}}(P,Q):=\min\big\{k\geq 0:\varphi^{[k]}(P)=Q,\,\varphi^{[k]}=\varphi_{1}\circ\dots\circ\varphi_{k},\,\varphi_{j}\in\mathcal{M},\,1\leq j\leq k\big\}.

(28)

Similarly, we define the
parabolic-taxicab
distance distance
\operatorname{d_{pc}}(P,Q)
, which counts as units the successive application of operators from
\mathcal{L}\cup\mathcal{M}

to generate a path of minimum length that connects
P
and
Q
:

\operatorname{d_{pc}}(P,Q):=\min\big\{k\geq 0:\varphi^{[k]}(P)=Q,\,\varphi^{[k]}=\varphi_{1}\circ\dots\circ\varphi_{k},\,\varphi_{j}\in\mathcal{L}\cup\mathcal{M},\,1\leq j\leq k\big\}.

(29)

For example, the shortest path described in Figure
5
is

\begin{split}(5,2)&\xrightarrow{\text{ $L^{\prime\prime}$ }}(5,9)\xrightarrow{\text{ $L^{\prime}$ }}(14,9)\xrightarrow{\text{ $M^{iv}$ }}(14,8)\xrightarrow{\text{ $M^{{}^{\prime}}$ }}(15,8)\xrightarrow{\text{ $L^{\prime\prime}$ }}(15,23),\end{split}

so that
\operatorname{d_{pc}}\big((5,2),(15,23)\big)=5
.

In the next proposition we compare the parabolic-taxicab distance
\operatorname{d_{pc}}

with the taxicab distance
\operatorname{d_{M}}
and the Euclidean distance
\operatorname{d_{E}}
.

Proposition 6.1
.

The function defined by (
29
) has the
following properties.

(1)

\operatorname{d_{pc}}
is a distance;

(2)

\operatorname{d_{E}}(P,Q)\leq\operatorname{d_{M}}(P,Q)
;

(3)

\operatorname{d_{pc}}(P,Q)\leq\operatorname{d_{M}}(P,Q)
,

for any lattice points
P,Q\in\mathbb{Z}^{2}
.

Proof.

(1) The non-negativity and the identity conditions are both part of the definition
since, by convention,
\varphi^{[0]}=Id
.

The symmetry follows since the inverses
of
M^{\prime},M^{\prime\prime},M^{\prime\prime\prime},M^{iv}
are in
\mathcal{M}

as well (since
(M^{\prime})^{-1}=M^{\prime\prime}
and

(M^{\prime\prime\prime})^{-1}=M^{iv}
)
and both
L^{\prime}
and
L^{\prime\prime}
are involutions,
according to Lemma
2.1
.

The triangle inequality axiom is satisfied
because any successive operators from
\mathcal{M}\cup\mathcal{L}

applied to
P
generate the nodes of a path
that starts at
P
and, in this specific case, ends at
Q
,
and, as defined,

\operatorname{d_{pc}}(P,Q)
is given by the smallest number of steps required
to go from
P
to
Q
, while in
\operatorname{d_{pc}}(P,R)+\operatorname{d_{pc}}(R,Q)

two minimum number of steps are added, which count also for paths from
P
to
Q
,
but these are particular ones, as they additionally
pass through
R
, for any lattice points
P,Q,R
.

(2) This is the triangle inequality applied in a right triangle.

(3) The inequality follows because, while both sides calculate the minimum length
of paths from
P
to
Q
, the set of paths considered in calculating
the minimum on the left side is larger, including the one used
to calculate the minimum on the right side.
∎

Let us examine a few other examples that compare
the distances between two points
P
and
Q
, measured using
\operatorname{d_{E}},\operatorname{d_{M}}
, and
\operatorname{d_{pc}}
.
Because all operators in
\mathcal{L}\cup\mathcal{M}
are invariant under translations, we can choose
P=O=(0,0)
.

If
Q=(0,1)
, all distances are equal:

\operatorname{d_{E}}(O,Q)=\operatorname{d_{M}}(O,Q)=\operatorname{d_{pc}}(O,Q)=1
.

If
Q=(1,1)
, we have:

\operatorname{d_{E}}(O,Q)=\sqrt{2}<\operatorname{d_{M}}(O,Q)=\operatorname{d_{pc}}(O,Q)=2
,
because
O
and
Q
are neither adjacent on the lattice network
nor belong to the same parabola of those that partition
\mathbb{Z}^{2}
as shown in Figure
8
.

If
Q
is farther away from the origin, than the parabolic-taxicab distance
\operatorname{d_{pc}}(O,Q)
is smaller than
\operatorname{d_{E}}(O,Q)
,
because there are ladder steps on the parabolas that provides shorter paths.
For example, if
Q=(3,6)
,
then
\operatorname{d_{E}}(P,Q)=\sqrt{6^{2}+3^{2}}=3\sqrt{5}\approx 6.7

and
\operatorname{d_{M}}(O,Q)=3+6=9
.
But
\operatorname{d_{pc}}(O,Q)=3
, because

\begin{split}(0,0)&\xrightarrow{\text{ $L^{\prime\prime}$ }}(0,1)\xrightarrow{\text{ $L^{\prime}$ }}(3,1)\xrightarrow{\text{ $L^{\prime\prime}$ }}(3,6),\end{split}

so that
\big(L^{\prime\prime}\circ L^{\prime}\circ L^{\prime\prime}\big)(0,0)=(3,6)
,
and one can check as well, case by case, that

(3,6)
cannot be obtained from
(0,0)

by applying fewer than

\mathcal{L}\cup\mathcal{M}
.

A way to farther away points

In order to get a better understanding of the subject, let us evaluate the distance
