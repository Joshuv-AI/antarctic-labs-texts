On transitive sets of derangements in primitive groups
Peter Müller

Abstract
We construct a primitive permutation action of the Steinberg triality group D 4 3 ​ ( 2 ) {}^{3}D_{4}(2) of degree 4064256 4064256 and show that there are distinct points α , β \alpha,\beta such that there is no derangement g ∈ D 4 3 ​ ( 2 ) g\in{{}^{3}D_{4}}(2) with α g = β \alpha^{g}=\beta . This answers a question by John G. Thompson (Problem 8.75 in the Kourovka notebook [ 10 ] ) in the negative.

Remarks

(a)

Let
H
be a subgroup of a finite group
G
. Then the
inclusion
Hx\subseteq\cup_{g\in G}H^{g}
holds for
x\in G
if and
only if
Hy\subseteq\cup_{g\in G}H^{g}
for every
y
in the double
coset
HxH
. For
G
and
H
as in the theorem, there are precisely

78366
double cosets
HyH
different from
H
, and it turns out
that exactly one of these double cosets accounts for the
counterexample!

(b)

Let
G
,
H
,
a,b,c
and
x
be as in the theorem and
its proof. Set
s=ab
, so
H=\langle s\rangle\rtimes\langle c\rangle
. The normalizer
of
\langle c\rangle
in
G
has order
128
. It contains exactly
4

elements

such that

has order
4
,
c^{z}=c^{-1}
, and
s

. These four elements are in the double
coset

with

as in the theorem.

(c)

Initially, I had tried to explore a vast generalization of
the question. The primitivity of a group can not be read off from
the permutation character, see the negative answer

[
7
]
by
Guralnick and Saxl to a question of Wielandt. However, by a result
of Higman (see
[
8
]
or
[
5
, Theorem
3.2A]
), primitivity is encoded in the orbital graphs: The
group
G
is primitive if and only if each non-diagonal orbital
graph is connected.

Now there was some experimental evidence that the following
generalization of the question could hold: Let
G
act transitively
on the finite set
\Omega
. Pick distinct
\alpha,\beta
in

\Omega
. If the orbital graph which contains the edge

(\alpha,\beta)
is connected, then there is a derangement
g\in G

with
\alpha^{g}=\beta
. The smallest counterexample I’m aware of is

G=\PSL_{3}(4)
acting on
4032
points (so the point stabilizer has
order
5
).

(d)

I have some evidence (and partial results) that the
following could hold: Let
G
act transitively on the finite set

\Omega
, and pick distinct
\alpha,\beta
in
\Omega
. Then there
is an element
g\in G
with
\alpha^{g}=\beta
whose number of fixed
points is different from
1
. Of course, Frobenius theorem (see the
Introduction) would be an almost immediate consequence.
