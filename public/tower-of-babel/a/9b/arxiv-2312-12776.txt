Computational homogenization of phase-field fracture.
Felix Schmidt

Abstract




Abstract

Abstract.

In this contribution we investigate the application of phase-field fracture models on non-linear multiscale computational homogenization schemes. In particular, we introduce different phase-fields on a two-scale problem and develop a thermodynamically consistent model. This allows on the one hand for the prediction of local micro-fracture patterns, which effectively acts as an anisotropic damage model on the macroscale. On the other and, the macro-fracture phase-field model allows to predict complex fracture pattern with regard to local microstructures. Both phase-fields are introduced in a common framework, such that a joint consistent linearization for the Newton-Raphson iteration can be developed. Finally, the limits of both models as well as the applicability are shown in different numerical examples.

: Hallo, Welt.

Introduction

The prediction of the overall mechanical response of materials with dedicated microstructures including various failure mechanisms require direct numerical simulations. However, fully resolved microstructures may lead to economically or technically unsustainable computational costs. Therefore, it is convenient to apply computational homogenization techniques, assuming that the material may appear perfectly homogeneous on a higher, macroscale and highly heterogeneous at a lower, microscale. To be more specific, we assume that the scale separation is valid, i.e. that the wavelengths of physical fields at the higher scale are very much larger than the dimensions of heterogeneities at the lower scale, see
[
33
]

A straightforward minimisation of the fracture energy is not feasible due to the arising discontinuities. This contribution is devoted to the application of phase-field methods on multiscale computational homogenization schemes. Obviously, a phase-field fracture approach can be applied to both scales on a two-scale homogenization method, leading to two different dissipation functions. We aim at a thermodynamical consistent formulation to avoid unphysical energy transfer either in the wrong direction or between the different dissipation potentials. This issue has already been investigated in the context of ductile fracture, using a multiplicative triple split of the deformation gradient to satisfy the Clausius-Planck inequality equation. Analoguesly, we introduce here a triple split into local micro-fracture and global macro-fracture. For the former part, the model is restricted by the effective scale separation, leading to limitations in the applicability due to the absence of non-local and gradient contributions on the macroscale. Finally, we develop in the discrete setting a consistent linearization for the non-linear two-field two-scale problem.

2
Governing equations

We start with a short summary of the governing equations for the macro phase-field fracture formulation at hand. Consider a Lipschitz bounded continuum body in its reference configuration
\mathcal{B}_{0}\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}
with
n\in\{2,3\}
undergoing a motion characterized by the deformation mapping

\boldsymbol{\varphi}(\boldsymbol{X}):\mathcal{B}_{0}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^{d}\quad\mbox{such that}\quad\boldsymbol{x}=\boldsymbol{\varphi}(\boldsymbol{X}),

(1)

which maps material points
\boldsymbol{X}\in\mathcal{B}_{0}
of the reference configuration
\mathcal{B}_{0}
onto points
\boldsymbol{x}\in\mathcal{B}_{t}
of the current configuration
\mathcal{B}_{t}=\boldsymbol{\varphi}(\mathcal{B}_{0})
. The material deformation gradient is defined by
\boldsymbol{F}:=\nabla_{\hskip-2.56073pt\mbox{\scriptsize$\mathrm{\boldsymbol{X}}$}}\hskip 0.85358pt(\boldsymbol{\varphi}(\boldsymbol{X}))
with
J\!:=\!\mbox{det}[\boldsymbol{F}]>0
. Next, a phase-field is introduced as

{\mathfrak{s}}(\boldsymbol{X}):\mathcal{B}_{0}\times\mathcal{T}\rightarrow[0,1]\subset\mathbb{R},\quad\mbox{with}\quad\dot{\mathfrak{s}}\geq 0,

(2)

where the value
{\mathfrak{s}}=0
refers to the undamaged and
{\mathfrak{s}}=1
to the fully broken state of the material. Here,
(\dot{\bullet})
refers to the derivative in time, such that
\dot{\mathfrak{s}}\geq 0
prevents healing effects. For the static system considered here, we re-interpret the temporal derivative by preventing healing from load step to load step.

2.1
Kinematics and regularised crack phase-field topology

In analogy to the multiplicative decomposition of non-linear plasticity (cf.
[
6
]
), we define the elastic, fracture insensitive part of the deformation gradient, written in terms of principal stretches
\lambda_{a}
, as follows

\boldsymbol{F}^{\mathrm{e}}=\sum\limits_{a=1}^{d}(\lambda_{a}^{+})^{g({\mathfrak{s}})}(\lambda_{a}^{-})\boldsymbol{\mathfrak{n}}_{a}\otimes\boldsymbol{\mathfrak{N}}_{a},

(3)

postulating that fracture requires a local state of tension. Here,
\boldsymbol{\mathfrak{n}}_{a}
and
\boldsymbol{\mathfrak{N}}_{a}
denote the principal directions of the left and right stretch tensors and
g({\mathfrak{s}})
a suitable degeneration function; in the most simplest setting
g({\mathfrak{s}})=1-{\mathfrak{s}}
. Moreover,
\lambda_{a}
are principal stretches, decomposed into tensile and compressive components using
\lambda_{a}^{\pm}=[(\lambda_{a}-1)\pm|\lambda_{a}-1|]/2+1
. Cracks can be considered as propagating internal boundaries
\Gamma^{cr}(t)\subset\mathbb{R}^{n-1}
and we assume that crack initiates or continues upon attainment of a critical local fracture energy density
g_{c}
. Thus, the total energy within the sharp crack interface reads

E^{cr}=\int\limits_{\Gamma^{cr}}g_{c}\operatorname{d}\!\Gamma.

(4)

Since the numerical evaluation of this sharp crack interface is not suitable within a standard finite element framework, a regularised crack interface using a specific regularisation profile
\gamma({\mathfrak{s}})
is introduced, such that the critical fracture energy is approximated by

\int_{\Gamma^{cr}}g_{c}\operatorname{d}\!\Gamma\approx\int_{\mathcal{B}_{0}}g_{c}\gamma_{l}({\mathfrak{s}})\operatorname{d}\!V.

(5)

The chosen regularisation profile for a second order phase-field is assumed to take the form
{\mathfrak{s}}(x)=e^{\frac{-|x|}{2\,l}}
(see Miehe et al.
[
27
,
29
]
for details), which is the solution of the partial differential equation

\displaystyle{\mathfrak{s}}-l^{2}{\Delta{\mathfrak{s}}}

\displaystyle=0\;\text{in}\;\mathcal{B}_{0},

(6)

\displaystyle\nabla{\mathfrak{s}}\cdot\boldsymbol{n}

\displaystyle=0\;\text{on}\;\partial\mathcal{B}_{0}.

(7)

The corresponding variational formulation was first introduced by Francfort and Marigo
[
11
]
. Therefore, we introduce the space of admissible solution an test functions for the phase field

\mathcal{V}^{{\mathfrak{s}}}=\{{{\mathfrak{s}}}\in H^{1}(\mathcal{B}_{0})|\dot{{\mathfrak{s}}}\geq 0\;\text{on}\;\mathcal{B}_{0}\},\quad\mathcal{V}^{\delta{\mathfrak{s}}}=\{{\delta{\mathfrak{s}}}\in H^{1}(\mathcal{B}_{0})|{\delta{\mathfrak{s}}}=0\;\text{on}\;\Gamma^{cr}\},

(8)

where
H^{1}
denotes the Sobolev functional space of square integrable functions and derivatives. Applying integration by parts, the weak form of (
6
) can be written as

\int\limits_{\mathcal{B}_{0}}\frac{1}{l}{\delta{\mathfrak{s}}}\,{\mathfrak{s}}+l\nabla({\delta{\mathfrak{s}}})\cdot\nabla({\mathfrak{s}})\,\operatorname{d}\!V=0.

(9)

This, in turn, can be related to the regularized crack surface topology,

\Gamma_{l}^{cr}({\mathfrak{s}}):=\int\limits_{\mathcal{B}_{0}}\gamma_{l}({\mathfrak{s}})\,\operatorname{d}\!V=\int\limits_{\mathcal{B}_{0}}\frac{1}{2l}{\mathfrak{s}}^{2}+\frac{l}{2}\nabla({\mathfrak{s}})\cdot\nabla({\mathfrak{s}})\,\operatorname{d}\!V,

(10)

such that the diffusive crack zone (
10
) converges to the sharp crack surface
\Gamma^{cr}(t)
for
l\rightarrow 0
.

Remark 1
.

Often, a split in isochoric and volumetric parts using
\boldsymbol{F}^{\mathrm{e}}=J^{\frac{1}{n}}\bar{\boldsymbol{F}}^{\mathrm{e}}
and
\lambda_{a}=J^{\frac{1}{n}}\bar{\lambda}_{a}
, is applied, such that the isochoric elastic deformation gradient reads

\bar{\boldsymbol{F}}^{\mathrm{e}}=\sum\limits_{a}\left(\bar{\lambda}_{a}\right)^{g({\mathfrak{s}})}\,\boldsymbol{n}_{a}\otimes\boldsymbol{N}_{a}\,,

(11)

Moreover, assuming that fracture requires a local state of expansion, the fracture insensitive, volumetric part of the Jacobian is defined as

{J}^{\mathrm{e}}=\begin{cases}\left(J\right)^{g({\mathfrak{s}})}&\text{if}\quad J>1\\
J&\text{else}\end{cases}\,.

(12)

Depending on the specific situation,this approach has proven to be more robust.

2.2
Balance equation

Introducing the space of virtual or admissible test functions for the deformation

\mathcal{V}^{\delta\varphi}=\{\delta\boldsymbol{\varphi}\in{H}^{1}(\mathcal{B}_{0})\,|\,\delta\boldsymbol{\varphi}=\boldsymbol{0}\,\text{on}\,\Gamma^{\varphi}\},

(13)

where
\Gamma^{\varphi}
refers to the Dirichlet boundary, the internal virtual work and the virtual work of the inertia terms along with the internal virtual work of the crack driving force reads

\delta\Pi^{\mathrm{int},\varphi}=\int\limits_{\mathcal{B}_{0}}\boldsymbol{P}:\nabla_{\hskip-2.56073pt\mbox{\scriptsize$\mathrm{\boldsymbol{X}}$}}\hskip 0.85358pt(\delta\boldsymbol{\varphi})\,\operatorname{d}\!V,\quad\delta\Pi^{\mathrm{int},{\mathfrak{s}}}=-\int\limits_{\mathcal{B}_{0}}\delta{\mathfrak{s}}\,\mathcal{H}\,\operatorname{d}\!V.

(14)

For localization, we apply integration by parts on (
14
)
1

\int\limits_{\mathcal{B}_{0}}\boldsymbol{P}:\nabla_{\hskip-2.56073pt\mbox{\scriptsize$\mathrm{\boldsymbol{X}}$}}\hskip 0.85358pt(\delta\boldsymbol{\varphi})\,\operatorname{d}\!V=\int\limits_{\partial\mathcal{B}_{0}}\delta\boldsymbol{\varphi}\cdot\boldsymbol{P}\boldsymbol{N}\,\operatorname{d}\!A-\int\limits_{\mathcal{B}_{0}}\delta\boldsymbol{\varphi}\cdot(\nabla_{\hskip-2.56073pt\mbox{\scriptsize$\mathrm{\boldsymbol{X}}$}}\hskip 0.85358pt\cdot\boldsymbol{P})\,\operatorname{d}\!V,

(15)

where
\boldsymbol{N}
is the outward normal in the reference configuration.

Introducing
\boldsymbol{B}
as local body load and
\boldsymbol{T}
as local Neumann surfaces load on
\Gamma^{\sigma}
, noting that
\Gamma^{\varphi}\cap\Gamma^{\sigma}=\emptyset
and
\Gamma^{\varphi}\cup\Gamma^{\sigma}=\Gamma
with
\Gamma
an open set on
\partial\mathcal{B}_{0}
, we can equilibrate internal and external virtual work and obtain

\int\limits_{\mathcal{B}_{0}}\boldsymbol{P}:\nabla_{\hskip-2.56073pt\mbox{\scriptsize$\mathrm{\boldsymbol{X}}$}}\hskip 0.85358pt(\delta\boldsymbol{\varphi})\,\operatorname{d}\!V=\int\limits_{\mathcal{B}_{0}}\delta\boldsymbol{\varphi}\cdot\boldsymbol{B}\,\operatorname{d}\!V+\int\limits_{\Gamma^{\sigma}}\delta\boldsymbol{\varphi}\cdot\boldsymbol{T}\,\operatorname{d}\!A.

(16)

In contrast, the virtual work of the phase-field driving force
\mathcal{H}
is equilibrated with the regularised virtual work of the critical fracture energy

\int\limits_{\mathcal{B}_{0}}\delta{\mathfrak{s}}\left(\mathcal{H}+\frac{g_{c}}{l}{\mathfrak{s}}\right)+g_{c}l\nabla(\delta{\mathfrak{s}})\cdot\nabla({\mathfrak{s}})\,\operatorname{d}\!V=0,

(17)

such that we control the dissipated energy into the regularised crack interface. To demonstrate this, we set
\delta\boldsymbol{\varphi}=\dot{\boldsymbol{\varphi}}
and
\delta{\mathfrak{s}}=\dot{{\mathfrak{s}}}
and restate (
16
) as

\int\limits_{\mathcal{B}_{0}}\boldsymbol{P}:\,\dot{\boldsymbol{F}}\,\operatorname{d}\!V=\underbrace{\int\limits_{\mathcal{B}_{0}}\dot{\boldsymbol{\varphi}}\cdot\boldsymbol{B}\,\operatorname{d}\!V+\int\limits_{\Gamma^{\sigma}}\dot{\boldsymbol{\varphi}}\cdot\boldsymbol{T}\,\operatorname{d}\!A}_{P^{ext}},

(18)

where
P^{ext}
the external power. Moreover, we obtain from (
17
)

\underbrace{\int\limits_{\mathcal{B}_{0}}\dot{{\mathfrak{s}}}\frac{g_{c}}{l}{\mathfrak{s}}+g_{c}l\nabla(\dot{{\mathfrak{s}}})\cdot\nabla({\mathfrak{s}})\,\operatorname{d}\!V}_{\int\limits_{\mathcal{B}_{0}}g_{c}\dot{\gamma}_{l}\,\operatorname{d}\!V=D^{\operatorname{ma}}}=-\int\limits_{\mathcal{B}_{0}}\mathcal{H}\dot{{\mathfrak{s}}}\,\operatorname{d}\!V

(19)

such that

\underbrace{\int\limits_{\mathcal{B}_{0}}\boldsymbol{P}:\,\dot{\boldsymbol{F}}+\mathcal{H}\dot{{\mathfrak{s}}}\,\operatorname{d}\!V}_{P^{\operatorname{int}}}+D^{\operatorname{ma}}=P^{\operatorname{ext}}.

(20)

which is the first law of thermodynamics excluding the kinetic energy. Without proof, we state that
\dot{{\mathfrak{s}}}\geq 0
ensures that the Clausius Duhem inequality equation is valid, see
[
19
]
for details.

3
Multiscale phase-field boundary value problem

Based on the previous considerations, we introduce often a phenomenological, i.e. constitutive strain energy function, such that the temporal derivative recovers the internal power
P^{\operatorname{int}}
introduced in (
20
). However, the construction of phenomenological strain energy functions becomes nowadays more and more complex as production tools like additive manufacturing allow for (geometrically) inhomogeneous materials. To capture these geometrical effects in a computationally efficient way, a dimensional expansion is introduced. In particular, we introduce a representative volume element (RVE)
\Omega_{0}=\Omega_{0}(\boldsymbol{X})\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{n}
to each point
\boldsymbol{X}\in\mathcal{B}_{0}\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}
, on which we introduce a deformation map
\tilde{\boldsymbol{\varphi}}:\mathcal{B}_{0}\times\Omega_{0}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^{n}
and the quantities derived from it analogue to the macro continuum. Note, that all quantities on
\Omega_{0}
will be marked with a superimposed tilde. Hence,
\tilde{\boldsymbol{\varphi}}
is defined on a
n+n
dimensional space.

3.1
Micro-continuum

The deformation map
\tilde{\boldsymbol{\varphi}}
is defined as a first order Taylor approximation

\tilde{\boldsymbol{\varphi}}(\boldsymbol{X},\tilde{\boldsymbol{X}})=\boldsymbol{F}(\boldsymbol{X})\tilde{\boldsymbol{X}}+\tilde{\boldsymbol{w}}(\boldsymbol{X},\tilde{\boldsymbol{X}}),\quad\boldsymbol{X}\in\mathcal{B}_{0},\,\tilde{\boldsymbol{X}}\in\Omega_{0},

(21)

such that the micro deformation consists of a homogeneous part
\boldsymbol{F}(\boldsymbol{X})\tilde{\boldsymbol{X}}
and a non-homogeneous field
\tilde{\boldsymbol{w}}\colon\mathcal{B}_{0}\times\Omega_{0}\to\mathbb{R}^{n}
referred to as microscopic fluctuations. Accordingly, the deformation gradient reads

\tilde{\boldsymbol{F}}=\nabla_{\hskip-2.56073pt\mbox{\scriptsize$\mathrm{\tilde{\boldsymbol{X}}}$}}\hskip 0.85358pt(\tilde{\boldsymbol{\varphi}})=\boldsymbol{F}(\boldsymbol{X})+\boldsymbol{F}^{\prime},\quad\boldsymbol{F}^{\prime}:=\nabla_{\hskip-2.56073pt\mbox{\scriptsize$\mathrm{\tilde{\boldsymbol{X}}}$}}\hskip 0.85358pt(\tilde{\boldsymbol{w}}).

(22)

As usual in multiscale techniques, we relate the macro- and the microscopic deformation gradient and elastic potential via volume averages

\boldsymbol{F}=\frac{1}{|\Omega_{0}|}\int\limits_{\Omega_{0}}\tilde{\boldsymbol{F}}\,\operatorname{d}\!\tilde{V}\quad\text{and}\quad\Psi=\frac{1}{|\Omega_{0}|}\int\limits_{\Omega_{0}}\tilde{\Psi}\,\operatorname{d}\!\tilde{V},

(23)

where
|\Omega_{0}|
denotes the volume of the RVE. The former requirement is enforced using suitable boundary condition

\int\limits_{\partial\Omega_{0}}\tilde{\boldsymbol{w}}\otimes\tilde{\boldsymbol{N}}\,\operatorname{d}\!\tilde{A}=\boldsymbol{0},

(24)

which directly enforces (
23
)
1
. Therefore, we assume that one of the following conditions is valid

\text{(}i)\;\tilde{\boldsymbol{w}}=\boldsymbol{0}\quad\text{in }\Omega_{0},\qquad\text{(}ii)\;\tilde{\boldsymbol{w}}=\boldsymbol{0}\quad\text{on }\partial\Omega_{0},\qquad\text{(}iii)\;\tilde{\boldsymbol{w}}^{+}=\boldsymbol{w}^{-}\quad\text{on }\partial\Omega_{0},

(25)

where the first one reconstruct a Voight type homogenization, the second demand homogeneous deformations on the boundary and the last addresses periodic boundary conditions. Requiring the macro-homogeneity or Hill-Mandel condition

\frac{1}{|\Omega_{0}|}\int\limits_{\Omega_{0}}\tilde{\boldsymbol{P}}:\delta\tilde{\boldsymbol{F}}\,\operatorname{d}\!\tilde{V}=\boldsymbol{P}:\delta\boldsymbol{F},

(26)

we can show after a few calculations assuming that the static equilibrium of the micro-continuum is governed by
\nabla_{\hskip-2.56073pt\mbox{\scriptsize$\mathrm{\tilde{\boldsymbol{X}}}$}}\hskip 0.85358pt\cdot\tilde{\boldsymbol{P}}=\boldsymbol{0}
in
\Omega_{0}
, that this leads to the volume average relationship between macro- and micro-stresses

\boldsymbol{P}=\frac{1}{|\Omega_{0}|}\int\limits_{\Omega_{0}}\tilde{\boldsymbol{P}}\,\operatorname{d}\!\tilde{V},

(27)

which is equivalent of enforcing (
23
)
2
.

3.2
Micro-fracture

Next, we introduce a phase-field for micro-fracture. Assuming the absent of macro phase-field
{\mathfrak{s}}:\mathcal{B}_{0}\rightarrow[0,\,1]
, we introduce a micro phase-field
\tilde{d}:\mathcal{B}_{0}\times\Omega_{0}\rightarrow[0,\,1]
, using the space of solution and trial functions

\displaystyle\mathcal{V}^{\tilde{d}}

\displaystyle=\{\tilde{d}\in L^{2}(\mathcal{B}_{0};H^{1}(\Omega_{0}))\,|\,\dot{\tilde{d}}\geq 0\},\quad\mathcal{V}^{\delta\tilde{d}}=\{\delta\tilde{d}\in L^{2}(\mathcal{B}_{0};H^{1}(\Omega_{0}))\},

(28)

\displaystyle\mathcal{V}^{\tilde{\boldsymbol{w}}}

\displaystyle=\{\tilde{\boldsymbol{w}}\in L^{2}(\mathcal{B}_{0};H^{1}(\Omega_{0}))\},\quad\mathcal{V}^{\delta\tilde{\boldsymbol{w}}}=\{\delta\tilde{\boldsymbol{w}}\in L^{2}(\mathcal{B}_{0};H^{1}(\Omega_{0}))\,|\,\delta\tilde{\boldsymbol{w}}=\boldsymbol{0}\;\text{on}\;\partial\Omega_{0}\}.

(29)

This leads to the microscale balance equations

\displaystyle G^{\tilde{\varphi}}:=\int\limits_{\Omega_{0}}\frac{\partial\tilde{\Psi}(\tilde{\boldsymbol{F}},\tilde{d})}{\partial\tilde{\boldsymbol{F}}}:\delta\tilde{\boldsymbol{F}}\,\operatorname{d}\!\tilde{V}

\displaystyle=0,

(30)

\displaystyle G^{\tilde{d}}:=\int\limits_{\Omega_{0}}\delta\tilde{d}\left(\frac{\partial\tilde{\Psi}(\tilde{\boldsymbol{F}},\tilde{d})}{\partial\tilde{d}}+\frac{\tilde{g}_{c}}{\tilde{l}}\,\tilde{d}\right)+\tilde{g}_{c}\tilde{l}\,\nabla(\delta\tilde{d})\cdot\nabla(\tilde{d}),\operatorname{d}\!\tilde{V}

\displaystyle=0,

where
G^{\tilde{d}}
is the total virtual work of the phase-field of the respective
\Omega_{0}
. This allows for pure micro-fracture and we can calculate the homogenized, macroscopic first Piola-Kirchhoff stress tensor
\boldsymbol{P}
via

\frac{1}{|\Omega_{0}|}\int\limits_{\Omega_{0}}\frac{\partial\tilde{\Psi}(\tilde{\boldsymbol{F}},\tilde{d})}{\partial\tilde{\boldsymbol{F}}}\,\operatorname{d}\!\tilde{V}=:\boldsymbol{P},

(31)

analogues to (
33
). As usual for dissipative and thus, path dependent formulations, we have to store
\tilde{d}
for every single
\Omega_{0}
.

Remark 2
.

Here, we do not apply any kind of Taylor approximation on the macro phase-field, as micro-fracture can be considered as a local dissipation. More importantly, this kind of microscale fracture can be considered as continuous damage model on the macroscale as presented in, among other, Lemaitre
[
25
,
26
]
. Therein, a scalar damage variable is introduced using the thermodynamic potential
Y=\frac{\partial\Psi}{\partial{\mathfrak{s}}}
analoguesly to the phase-field driving force
\mathcal{H}
. The existence of a scalar damage variable is discussed noting that
{\mathfrak{s}}
„represents the surface density of intersection of micro-cracks and micro-cavities with any plane in the body“, which are calculated here explicitly as micro-cracks within the RVE.

As shown in several publications in the 1990th, the introduction of scalar damage models without consideration of the neighborhood, e.g. using an additional gradient term, is posed to instabilities. This is reflected here by the fact, that a full fracture of the RVE using periodic boundary conditions can be considered as infinite large cracks on the microscale, contradicting the assumption of an appropriate scale separation. As shown in the numerical examples section below, we obtain checkerboard patterns of the phase-field if evaluated on the macroscale within areas of large gradients of the deformation gradient. This has already been described by Peerlings et al.
[
32
]
, stating that the „numerical analysis […] becomes sensitive to the discretization“, demonstrated here by the checkerboard modes. Thus, we observe that the curvature of the deformation restricts the validity of the chosen approach.

3.3
Macro-fracture

Assuming, that a localization results become physically unrealistic and sensitive to the discretization, nonlocal phase-field approaches on the macroscale become of interest. Basically, we assume the existence of the macro phase-field
{\mathfrak{s}}:\mathcal{B}_{0}\rightarrow[0,\,1]
and the absence of the micro phase-field
\tilde{d}:\mathcal{B}_{0}\times\Omega_{0}\rightarrow[0,\,1]
, i.e. we solve

G^{\tilde{\varphi}}:=\int\limits_{\Omega_{0}}\frac{\partial\tilde{\Psi}(\tilde{\boldsymbol{F}},{\mathfrak{s}})}{\partial\tilde{\boldsymbol{F}}}:\delta\tilde{\boldsymbol{F}}\,\operatorname{d}\!\tilde{V}=0,

(32)

where
G^{\tilde{\varphi}}
is the total virtual work of the mechanical field on the microscale using a constant macroscale
{\mathfrak{s}}
within each RVE. Then, we evaluate the macro stresses again using the volume average

\frac{1}{|\Omega_{0}|}\int\limits_{\Omega_{0}}\frac{\partial\tilde{\Psi}(\tilde{\boldsymbol{F}},{\mathfrak{s}})}{\partial\tilde{\boldsymbol{F}}}\,\operatorname{d}\!\tilde{V}=:\boldsymbol{P},

(33)

For the macro phase-field driving force, we also evaluate the volume average

\frac{1}{|\Omega_{0}|}\int\limits_{\Omega_{0}}\tilde{\mathcal{H}}\,\operatorname{d}\!\tilde{V}=\mathcal{H},\quad\text{where}\quad\tilde{\mathcal{H}}:=\frac{\partial\tilde{\Psi}(\tilde{\boldsymbol{F}},{\mathfrak{s}})}{\partial{\mathfrak{s}}}.

(34)

3.4
Dissipation

Both approaches for pure micro- and pure macro-fracture can be combined in a common framework. Therefore, we introduce a new phase-field variable on the microscale as
\tilde{{\mathfrak{s}}}={\mathfrak{s}}+\tilde{d}
, noting that only one of both, either
{\mathfrak{s}}
or
\tilde{d}
can be active and the other is set to zero. Using the degeneration function
g(\tilde{{\mathfrak{s}}})=1-({\mathfrak{s}}+\tilde{d})
yields

\boldsymbol{F}^{\mathrm{e}}=\sum\limits_{a=1}^{d}(\lambda_{a}^{+})(\lambda_{a}^{+})^{-{\mathfrak{s}}}(\lambda_{a}^{+})^{-\tilde{d}}(\lambda_{a}^{-})\boldsymbol{\mathfrak{n}}_{a}\otimes\boldsymbol{\mathfrak{N}}_{a},

(35)

which demonstrates the general character of the triple split of the tensile stretches. To obtain the dissipated energy within the RVE, we set
\delta\tilde{\boldsymbol{\varphi}}=\dot{\tilde{\boldsymbol{\varphi}}}
and
\delta\tilde{{\mathfrak{s}}}=\dot{\tilde{{\mathfrak{s}}}}
. Thus, we obtain

\dot{\tilde{\boldsymbol{\varphi}}}=\dot{\boldsymbol{\varphi}}+\dot{\boldsymbol{F}}\tilde{\boldsymbol{X}}+\dot{\tilde{\boldsymbol{w}}},\quad\dot{\tilde{\boldsymbol{F}}}=\dot{\boldsymbol{F}}+\nabla_{\hskip-2.56073pt\mbox{\scriptsize$\mathrm{\tilde{\boldsymbol{X}}}$}}\hskip 0.85358pt\dot{\tilde{\boldsymbol{w}}}\quad\text{and}\quad\dot{\tilde{{\mathfrak{s}}}}=\dot{{\mathfrak{s}}}+\dot{\tilde{d}}.

(36)

Focusing first on the micro-fracture contributions using
\dot{\tilde{{\mathfrak{s}}}}=\dot{\tilde{d}}
gives

\displaystyle-\frac{1}{|\Omega_{0}|}\int\limits_{\Omega_{0}}\dot{\tilde{d}}\underbrace{\frac{\partial\tilde{\Psi}(\tilde{\boldsymbol{F}},\tilde{{\mathfrak{s}}}}{\partial\tilde{{\mathfrak{s}}}})}_{=\tilde{\mathcal{H}}}\,\operatorname{d}\!\tilde{V}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{|\Omega_{0}|}\int\limits_{\Omega_{0}}\dot{\tilde{d}}\,\frac{\tilde{g}_{c}}{\tilde{l}}\,\tilde{{\mathfrak{s}}}+\tilde{g}_{c}\tilde{l}\,\nabla(\dot{\tilde{d}})\cdot\nabla(\tilde{{\mathfrak{s}}}),\operatorname{d}\!\tilde{V}

(37)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{|\Omega_{0}|}\int\limits_{\Omega_{0}}\tilde{g}_{c}\dot{\tilde{\gamma}}_{l}\,\operatorname{d}\!\tilde{V}=:\tilde{D}^{\operatorname{mi}}.

where
\tilde{D}^{\operatorname{mi}}
denotes the volumetric average dissipation density of the micro-system. Next, we introduce the volumetric average of the strain energy as
\Psi=\frac{1}{|\Omega_{0}|}\int_{\Omega_{0}}\tilde{\Psi}\operatorname{d}\!\tilde{V}
, where
\tilde{\Psi}:=\tilde{\Psi}(\boldsymbol{F},{\mathfrak{s}},\nabla_{\hskip-2.56073pt\mbox{\scriptsize$\mathrm{\tilde{\boldsymbol{X}}}$}}\hskip 0.85358pt(\tilde{\boldsymbol{w}}),\tilde{d})
and take the temporal derivative with respect to all four arguments as follows

\dot{\Psi}=\frac{1}{|\Omega_{0}|}\int\limits_{\Omega_{0}}\dot{\tilde{\Psi}}\operatorname{d}\!\tilde{V}=\boldsymbol{P}:\dot{\boldsymbol{F}}+\mathcal{H}\dot{{\mathfrak{s}}}+\frac{1}{|\Omega_{0}|}\int\limits_{\Omega_{0}}\tilde{\boldsymbol{P}}:\nabla_{\hskip-2.56073pt\mbox{\scriptsize$\mathrm{\tilde{\boldsymbol{X}}}$}}\hskip 0.85358pt\dot{\tilde{\boldsymbol{w}}}+\tilde{\mathcal{H}}\dot{\tilde{d}}\,\operatorname{d}\!\tilde{V},

(38)

Insertion into the balance equation on the macroscale in (
18
) along with (
19
) allows us to write for the energy balance

\frac{\operatorname{d}\!}{\operatorname{d}\!t}T+\underbrace{\int\limits_{\mathcal{B}_{0}}\dot{\Psi}+\tilde{D}^{\operatorname{mi}}\,\operatorname{d}\!V}_{=P^{\operatorname{int}}+D^{\operatorname{mi}}}+D^{\operatorname{ma}}=P^{\operatorname{ext}}

(39)

Thus, we obtain

\frac{\operatorname{d}\!}{\operatorname{d}\!t}T+P^{\operatorname{int}}+D^{\operatorname{mi}}+D^{\operatorname{ma}}=P^{\operatorname{ext}},

(40)

D^{\operatorname{mi}}=0
, whereas pure micro-fracture sets the macro dissipation
D^{\operatorname{ma}}=0
. Allowing for only one phase-field variable at the same position, i.e. either
{\mathfrak{s}}
or
\tilde{d}
prevents the transfer of energy between both dissipation potentials
D^{\operatorname{mi}}
and
D^{\operatorname{ma}}
, which would result in an unphysical behavior. Note, that the additive construction of
\tilde{{\mathfrak{s}}}={\mathfrak{s}}+\tilde{d}
yields the assumed additive construction of the micro and macro dissipation. The switch between both models depends on the scale separation and the gradient of the deformation gradient. We will demonstrate this in the Section on numerical examples.

6
Conclusions

Phase-field fracture approaches are now used for more than a decade with tremendous success, mainly due to the consistent variational structure and its easy applicability in complex situations. It is most natural to adapt this approach on mutliscale analysis. Two different phase-fields are applied, the first on the micro- and the second on the macroscale. For a thermodynamically consistent formulation, only one of both phase-fields can be active at the same time, although a joint framework has been presented.

The first micro phase-field allows for local fracture in the microstructure, using a phenomenological, non-linear strain energy function with a split of the tensile stretches. This leads to an anisotropic degeneration of the macroscale stress tensor, calculated as the volumetric average of the microscale stresses. Hence, from a macroscale point of view this approach acts like an anisotropic damage formulation. As shown, a full fracture of the RVE using periodic boundary conditions, i.e. an infinite crack through the microscale leads to local instabilities, as the required scale separation does not hold any more.

On the other hand, the macroscale phase-field approach suffers not from any instabilities, as usual for non-local gradient formulations. Therefore, we calculate the phase-field driving force analogues to the stress tensor using a volumetric average on the RVE. Within a joint framework, we can show that this formulation can be considered as a multiplicative triple split of the tensile stretches, resulting in an additive decomposition of the different dissipation terms, the micro- and the macro-dissipation.
