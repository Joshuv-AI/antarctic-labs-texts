Fully lifted random duality theory
Mihailo Stojnic

Abstract




Abstract

We study a generic class of
random optimization problems
(rops) and their typical behavior. The foundational aspects of the random duality theory (RDT), associated with rops, were discussed in
[
59
]
, where it was shown that one can often infer rops’ behavior even without actually solving them. Moreover,
[
59
]
uncovered that various quantities relevant to rops (including, for example, their typical objective values) can be determined (in a large dimensional context) even completely analytically. The key observation was that the
strong deterministic duality
implies the, so-called,
strong random duality
and therefore the full exactness of the analytical RDT characterizations. Here, we attack precisely those scenarios where the strong deterministic duality is not necessarily present and connect them to the recent progress made in studying bilinearly indexed (bli) random processes in
[
62
,
61
]
. In particular, utilizing a fully lifted (fl) interpolating comparison mechanism introduced in
[
62
]
, we establish corresponding
fully lifted
RDT (fl RDT). We then rely on a stationarized fl interpolation realization introduced in
[
61
]
to obtain complete
statitionarized
fl RDT (sfl RDT).
A few well known problems are then discussed as illustrations of a wide range of practical applications implied by the generality of the considered rops.

Index Terms: Random optimization/feasibility problems; Fully lifted random duality theory
.

1
Introduction

In this paper we study a generic class of random linearly constrained optimization problems. For a given function
f({\bf x}):{\mathbb{R}}^{n}\rightarrow{\mathbb{R}}
and a given set
{\mathcal{X}}\subseteq{\mathbb{R}}^{n}
, we consider

\displaystyle\min_{{\bf x}\in{\mathcal{X}}}

\displaystyle f({\bf x})

subject to

\displaystyle A{\bf x}=0

(1)

\displaystyle B{\bf x}\leq 0,

where
A\in{\mathbb{R}}^{m_{1}\times n}
is an
m_{1}\times n
matrix and
B\in{\mathbb{R}}^{m_{2}\times n}
is an
m_{2}\times n
matrix. To facilitate the exposition, unless stated otherwise, we consider only the scenarios where the objective can be computed, i.e., where it exists and is bounded.

The program in (
1
) is a classical optimization problem that has been studied throughout rather vast literature over the last century (see, e.g.,
[
11
,
43
,
37
,
33
,
34
,
7
,
9
,
8
]
and references therein). Various aspects of these types of problems are often of interest.
For example, within the standard optimization theory treatments (e.g.,
[
11
,
43
,
37
,
33
,
34
,
7
,
9
,
8
]
), when facing problem (
1
), most often, the primary goal is simply finding the minimal value of
f({\bf x})
(and, if possible the
{\bf x}
’s that achieve it). The secondary goal is usually to do so in a computationally as efficient way as possible. If, say,
f({\bf x})
and
{\mathcal{X}}
are such that they allow for the solving process to be done in polynomial time, one usually, from an algorithmic point of view, considers the above problem as solvable.

Our interest in this paper is slightly different from this classical approach and it relates to a somewhat different aspect of these problems. In particular, we don’t necessarily look to find solutions of (
1
) or even to find its objective’s optimal value. Instead,
we look at a special, random, class of problems from (
1
), and consider whether or not their analytical characterization is possible without actually solving them. For small
n
(say
n=2
or
n=3
), any given instance of (
1
) is typically solvable even fully analytically. We, however, consider large
n

linear
regime, i.e. the regime where
\alpha_{1}=\lim_{n\rightarrow\infty}\frac{m_{1}}{n}
and
\alpha_{2}=\lim_{n\rightarrow\infty}\frac{m_{2}}{n}
and both
\alpha_{1}
and
\alpha_{2}
remain constants as
n
grows. Solving (
1
) analytically in such a regime is in general very hard. Things, however, change if one moves to random mediums and views
A
and
B
as random matrices. These scenarios are precisely the ones initially considered in
[
59
,
49
,
48
,
51
,
52
]
where the foundational principles of the so-called (
non-lifted
) random duality theory (RDT) were introduced. Relying on these principles,
[
59
,
49
,
48
,
51
,
52
]
proved the existence of the so-called strong (
non-lifted
) random duality for a subclass of problems given in (
1
) where the strong deterministic duality holds. This then turned out to be completely sufficient to enable a full analytical characterization of the behavior of (
1
) even without actually solving it.

It soon became clear though that, despite their vast generality, the results of
[
59
,
49
,
48
,
51
,
52
]
might not ultimately be strong enough to handle more generic instances of (
1
). A long line of work then followed attempting to improve on
[
59
,
49
,
48
,
51
,
52
]
. In particular,

[
56
,
58
,
60
,
54
,
57
]
moved things to another level and introduced a
partially lifted
upgrade, as a way of attacking the scenarios where the non-lifted RDT is not at its full power. We here proceed along the same lines and move things even further by establishing
fully lifted
(fl) RDT. To do so,
we first recognize a connection between the random linearly constrained optimization programs considered here and the recent progress made in studying bilinearly indexed (bli) random processes in
[
62
,
61
]
. Recognition of such a connection allows us to ultimately introduce the fl RDT and its a complete
stationarized
variant, sfl RDT.

In sections that follow, we slowly introduce all the needed ingredients to eventually establish the sfl RDT. Before proceeding with such a presentation, we mention, right here at the beginning, that our exposition does not rely on using any of the algorithmic or analytical results known for any specific (deterministic) instance of (
1
). This basically means that the reader is not really required to have any background in the optimization theory beyond a few classical concept that will be rather obvious from the presentation itself. Moreover, as it will become clear throughout the presentation, any such concepts will be fairly general and in no way tailored particularly for the classes of problems that we study here.

2
Random linearly constrained programs

As emphasized above, our focus is on random linearly constrained optimization program (
1
). To be more specific, we assume that all elements of matrices
A\in{\mathbb{R}}^{m_{1}\times n}
and
B\in{\mathbb{R}}^{m_{2}\times n}
are i.i.d. standard normals and for a given set
{\mathcal{X}}\subseteq{\mathbb{R}}^{n}
and function

consider

\displaystyle\xi(f,{\mathcal{X}})=\min_{{\bf x}}

\displaystyle f({\bf x})

subject to

\displaystyle A{\bf x}=0

(2)

\displaystyle B{\bf x}\leq 0

\displaystyle{\bf x}\in{\mathcal{X}}.

To facilitate writing, we assume throughout the paper that the objective of the above program exists and is either deterministically bounded (i.e., bounded for any particular realization of
A
and
B
) or that it is bounded with overwhelming probability over the randomness of
A
and
B
(under overwhelming probability, we always assume a probability that goes to
1
as

2
)

\displaystyle\xi(f,{\mathcal{X}})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\min_{{\bf x}\in{\mathcal{X}}}\max_{\lambda\geq 0,\nu}\left(f({\bf x})+\nu^{T}A{\bf x}+\lambda^{T}B{\bf x}\right)=\min_{{\bf x}\in{\mathcal{X}}}\max_{\lambda\geq 0,\nu}\left(f({\bf x})+\begin{bmatrix}\nu^{T}\lambda^{T}\end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix}A\\
B\end{bmatrix}{\bf x}\right)

(3)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\min_{{\bf x}\in{\mathcal{X}}}\max_{{\bf y}\in{\mathcal{Y}}}\left(f({\bf x})+{\bf y}^{T}G{\bf x}\right),

where
\nu\in{\mathbb{R}}^{m_{1}},
and
\lambda\in{\mathbb{R}}^{m}_{2},
are column vectors,
G\in{\mathbb{R}}^{m\times n},
is a matrix comprised of i.i.d. standard normals,
m=m_{1}+m_{2},
and
{\mathcal{Y}}\subseteq{\mathbb{R}}^{m}
is comprised of
{\bf y}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{m}
such that
{\bf y}_{i}\geq 0,m_{1}+1\leq i\leq m_{1}+m_{2}
. After recognizing that
{\bf y}^{T}G{\bf x}
is a bli process, we are in position to utilize recent results
[
62

61
]
(related to such processes) to characterize
\xi(f,{\mathcal{X}})

3.1

Conclusion

A class of famous linearly constrained optimization problems is considered and their behavior in a random medium is studied. These types of problems were initially considered in
[

The whole machinery is presented in a generic way and is consequently a very powerful generic mathematical engine that can be applied in a plethora of different scenarios. To give a hint at the range of potential applications, we first showed that in addition to handling the random
optimization
problems (rops), it can also be equally successfully used in handling random
