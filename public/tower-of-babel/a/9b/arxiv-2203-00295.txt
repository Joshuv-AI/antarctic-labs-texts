A Domain-Theoretic Framework for Robustness Analysis of Neural Networks
Can Zhou, Razin A. Shaikh, Yiran Li, Amin Farjudian

Abstract
A domain-theoretic framework is presented for validated robustness analysis of neural networks. First, global robustness of a general class of networks is analyzed. Then, using the fact that Edalat’s domain-theoretic L L -derivative coincides with Clarke’s generalized gradient, the framework is extended for attack-agnostic local robustness analysis. The proposed framework is ideal for designing algorithms which are correct by construction. This claim is exemplified by developing a validated algorithm for estimation of Lipschitz constant of feedforward regressors. The completeness of the algorithm is proved over differentiable networks, and also over general position ReLU \ReLU networks. Computability results are obtained within the framework of effectively given domains. Using the proposed domain model, differentiable and non-differentiable networks can be analyzed uniformly. The validated algorithm is implemented using arbitrary-precision interval arithmetic, and the results of some experiments are presented. The software implementation is truly validated, as it handles floating-point errors as well.



Keywords:
domain theory, neural network, robustness,
Lipschitz constant, Clarke-gradient

06B35, 68Q55, 49J52, 68T37

Introduction

A system is said to be robust with respect to perturbations of a set
of parameters, if small changes to those parameters do not result in
significant changes in the behavior of the system. Robustness is a
core requirement of safety-critical systems, including neural networks
deployed in high-stakes
applications
[
18
,
44
]
. As
a result, several methods have been suggested for
measuring

robustness, e. g., through adversarial
attacks
[
40
,
9
]

or via attack-agnostic
methods
[
45
,
85
,
52
,
49
,
50
]
. A
common and effective attack-agnostic approach involves estimation of
the local Lipschitz constant of the network. This makes sense as
larger Lipschitz constants signify more sensitivity to input values.
As such, a useful estimate of the Lipschitz constant should be a

tight upper bound
. A lower bound of the Lipschitz constant
results in false negatives—which are more serious than false
positives—and a very loose upper bound generates too many false
positives, which renders the estimate ineffective. Sound and accurate
computation of the Lipschitz constant is also essential in Lipschitz
regularization of neural
networks
[
4
,
66
]
.

Of particular interest is the estimation of Lipschitz constant of
non-differentiable networks, such as
\ReLU
networks. Computing the
Lipschitz constant of
\ReLU
networks is not just an NP-hard
problem
[
82
]
, it is
strongly
inapproximable
[
49
, Theorem 4]
. As
a result, methods of Lipschitz analysis fall into two
disjoint

categories:

(1)

Scalable
, but not validated, e. g.,

[
85
,
12
,
56
]
;

(2)

Validated
, but not scalable, e. g.,

[
45
,
49
,
50
]
,
and the method of the current article.

The validated methods—though not scalable—are essential in
providing the ground truth for verification of the scalable
methods. For instance, consider the scalable Cross Lipschitz Extreme Value for nEtwork Robustness method
of
[
85
]
.
[
49
]

demonstrate that, on certain parts of the input domain, Cross Lipschitz Extreme Value for nEtwork Robustness
outputs inaccurate results, even on small networks. The method of

[
49
,
50
]
,
in turn, although based on a validated theory, is implemented using
floating-point arithmetic, which is prone to inaccuracies, as will be
demonstrated in Section
5
. It is well-known that
floating-point errors lead to vulnerability in safety-critical neural
networks
[
48
,
89
]
.

Based on the theory of continuous domains, we develop a framework for
robustness analysis of neural networks, within which we design an
algorithm for estimation of local Lipschitz constant of feedforward
regressors. We prove the soundness of the algorithm. Moreover, we
prove completeness of the algorithm over differentiable networks, and
also over general position
\ReLU
networks.

To avoid the pitfalls of floating-point errors, we implement our
algorithm using arbitrary-precision interval arithmetic (with outward
rounding) which guarantees soundness of the implementation as well. As
such, our method is
fully validated
, that is, not only does it
follow a validated theory, but also it has a validated
implementation.

In contrast,

Global robustness analysis:

2.1
Metric Structure

We must first specify the metric used in measuring perturbations of
the input, and the norm used on the relevant gradients. For every

n\in\mathbb{N}
and
p\in[1,\infty]
, let
\ell_{p}^{n}
denote the normed
space
(\mathbb{R}^{n},{\left\lVert\thinspace{.}\thinspace\right\rVert}^{(n)}_{p})
, in which the norm
{\left\lVert\thinspace{.}\thinspace\right\rVert}^{(n)}_{p}

is defined for all
x=(x_{1},\ldots,x_{n})\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
by

{\left\lVert\thinspace{x}\thinspace\right\rVert}^{(n)}_{p}\coloneqq\left(\sum_{i=1}^{n}\lvert\thinspace{x_{i}}\thinspace\rvert^{p}\right)^{1/p}
, if
p\in[1,\infty)
, and

{\left\lVert\thinspace{x}\thinspace\right\rVert}^{(n)}_{p}\coloneqq\max\{{\lvert\thinspace{x_{i}}\thinspace\rvert}\mid{1\leq i\leq n}\}
, if
p=\infty
. We let

d_{p}^{(n)}:\mathbb{R}^{n}\times\mathbb{R}^{n}\to[0,\infty)
denote the metric
induced by
{\left\lVert\thinspace{.}\thinspace\right\rVert}^{(n)}_{p}
, i. e.,

d_{p}^{(n)}(x,y)={\left\lVert\thinspace{x-y}\thinspace\right\rVert}^{(n)}_{p}
. When
n
is clear from the
context, we drop the superscript and write
{\left\lVert\thinspace{.}\thinspace\right\rVert}_{p}
and
d_{p}
. For
every
p\in[1,\infty]
, define the conjugate
p^{\prime}\in[1,\infty]
as
follows:

p^{\prime}\coloneqq\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}\infty,&\text{if }p=1,\\
p/(p-1),&\text{if }p\in(1,\infty),\\
1,&\text{if }p=\infty.\\
\end{array}\right.

(1)

In our discussion, whenever the perturbation of the input is measured
using
{\left\lVert\thinspace{.}\thinspace\right\rVert}^{(n)}_{p}
-norm, then the norm of the gradient must be
taken to be
{\left\lVert\thinspace{.}\thinspace\right\rVert}^{(n)}_{p^{\prime}}
. Furthermore, as the spaces

\ell^{n}_{p}
are finite-dimensional, the topology on these spaces does
not depend on
p
, a fact which is not true in infinite-dimensional

\ell_{p}
spaces. Thus, in the current article, whenever we mention a
closed, open, or compact subset of
\mathbb{R}^{n}
, the topology is assumed to
be that of
\ell^{n}_{\infty}
.

Remark 2.1
.

The readers familiar with functional analysis may notice that the
Banach space
\ell^{n}_{p^{\prime}}
is the continuous dual of the Banach
space
\ell^{n}_{p}
. This explains the correspondence between the norm
on the gradient and the perturbation norm. To keep the discussion
simple, we do not present any technical background from functional
analysis. The interested reader may refer to classical texts on the
subject, such
as
[
70
,
3
]

Remark 2.2
.

We will only consider perturbations with respect to the

{\left\lVert\thinspace{.}\thinspace\right\rVert}^{(n)}_{p}
-norm, which is the default norm considered in
most of the related literature. We point out, however, that other
metrics have also been suggested for quantifying perturbations, most
notably, the Wasserstein
metric
[
88
]
. In
fact, the choice of an adequate metric remains an open
problem
[
72
]
.

2.2
Compact Subsets

Assume that
f:\mathbb{R}^{n}\to\mathbb{R}
is (the semantic model of) a regressor
neural network with
n
input neurons. In robustness analysis, we must
consider the action of
f
over suitable
subsets
of the input
space. Specifically, we work with closed subsets of both the input and
output spaces. Let us justify this choice formally.

Let
(\mathbb{S},d)
be a metric space with
distance
d:\mathbb{S}\times\mathbb{S}\to\mathbb{R}
. For any

X\subseteq\mathbb{S}
and
\delta>0
, we define the open neighborhood:

B(X,\delta)\coloneqq\{y\in\mathbb{S}\mid\exists x\in X:d(x,y)<\delta\}.

(2)

The set
B(X,\delta)
is indeed open, because it is the union of open
balls
B(\left\{{s}\right\},\delta)
with
s\in X
. Intuitively,
B(X,\delta)

is the set of points in
X
with imprecision less than
\delta
. For
all
X\subseteq\mathbb{S}
and
\delta>0
, we have

X\subseteq\overline{X}\subseteq B(X,\delta)=B(\overline{X},\delta)
, in which

\overline{X}
denotes the closure of
X
. Thus,
in the presence of
imprecision,
X
and
\overline{X}
are indistinguishable
. Furthermore,
it is straightforward to verify that

\overline{X}=\bigcap_{\delta>0}B(X,\delta)=\bigcap_{\delta>0}\overline{B(X,\delta)}
. Intuitively,

\overline{B(X,\delta)}
is the set of points in
X
with imprecision less
than or equal to
\delta
. Thus,
the closure
\overline{X}
is the set
of points that are in
X
with arbitrarily small imprecision
.

In practice, the input space of a network is a bounded region such as

\mathbb{S}=[-M,M]^{n}
. As
[-M,M]^{n}
is compact, and closed subsets of
compact spaces are compact, in this paper, we will be working with
compact subsets. A more detailed discussion of the choice of closed
subsets, in the broader context of robustness analysis, may be found
in
[
62
]
.

2.3
Domain Theory

Domain theory has its roots in topological
algebra
[
39
,
51
]
, and it
has enriched computer science with powerful methods from order theory,
algebra, and topology. Domains gained prominence when they were
introduced as a mathematical model for lambda calculus
by
[
71
]
. We have already mentioned that our
domain-theoretic framework provides a unified foundation for validated
methods of robustness and security analysis of neural networks. Domain
theory, however, provides more than just a unifying framework:

(1)

Domain-theoretic models are ideal for designing algorithms which
are
correct by construction
, as will be demonstrated in
Section
4
.

(2)

Domains provide a denotational semantics for computable analysis
according to
Type-II Theory of Effectivity
[
84
, Theorem 9.5.2]
. Our
computability results (Theorems
4.16
,

4.19
, and
4.21
) are
obtained within the framework of effectively given
domains
[
75
]
. As a
consequence, our constructs can be implemented directly on digital
computers.

We present a brief reminder of the concepts and notations that will be
needed later. The interested reader may refer
to
[
39
,
1
,
38
,
41
]

for more on domains in general, and refer
to
[
33
,
27
]
for the
interval domain, in particular. A succinct and informative survey of
the theory may be found
in
[
51
]
.

For any subset
X
of a partially ordered set (poset)
(D,\sqsubseteq)
, we write

\bigsqcup X
and
\bigsqcap X
to denote the least upper bound, and
the greatest lower bound, of
X
, respectively, whenever they
exist. In our discussion,
x\sqsubseteq y
may be interpreted as
‘
y
contains more information than
x
’. A subset

X\subseteq D
is said to be
directed
if
X\neq\emptyset
and
every two elements of
X
have an upper bound in
X
, i. e.,

\forall x,y\in X:\exists z\in X:(x\sqsubseteq z)\wedge(y\sqsubseteq z)
. An
\omega
-chain—i. e., a set of the form

x_{0}\sqsubseteq x_{1}\sqsubseteq\cdots\sqsubseteq x_{n}\sqsubseteq\cdots
—is an important special case of a directed set. A partially ordered set

(D,\sqsubseteq)
is said to be a
directed-complete partial order
if every directed
subset
X\subseteq D
has a least upper bound in
D
. The partially ordered set

(D,\sqsubseteq)
is said to be
pointed
if it has a bottom
element, which we usually denote by
\bot_{D}
, or if
D
is clear from
the context, simply by
\bot
. Directed-completeness is a basic
requirement for almost all the partially ordered sets in our discussion.

Example 2.3
.

Assume that
\Sigma\coloneqq\left\{{0,1}\right\}
and let
\Sigma^{*}
denote the
set of finite strings over
\Sigma
, ordered under the prefix
relation. Then,
\Sigma^{*}
is a partially ordered set, but it is not
directed-complete. For instance, the set

\{{0^{n}}\mid{n\in\mathbb{N}}\}
is an
\omega
-chain, but it does
not have the least upper bound, or any upper bound. If we let

\Sigma^{\omega}
denote the set of countably infinite sequences over

\Sigma
, then the partially ordered set

\Sigma^{\infty}\coloneqq\Sigma^{*}\cup\Sigma^{\omega}
, ordered under
prefix relation, is a directed-complete partial order, and may be regarded as a completion
of
\Sigma^{*}
. The partially ordered set
\Sigma^{\infty}
is indeed a pointed directed-complete partial order
with the empty string as the bottom element.

We let
\mathbb{K}\mathbb{R}^{n}_{\bot}
denote the partially ordered set with the carrier set

\{{C\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{n}}\mid{C\text{ is non-empty and compact}}\}\cup\left\{{\mathbb{R}^{n}}\right\}
, ordered by reverse inclusion, i. e.,

\forall X,Y\in\mathbb{K}\mathbb{R}^{n}_{\bot}:X\sqsubseteq Y\iff Y\subseteq X
. By further requiring the subsets to be convex, we obtain the
sub-poset
\mathbb{C}\mathbb{R}^{n}_{\bot}
. Finally, we let
{\mathbb{I}}\mathbb{R}^{n}_{\bot}

denote the partially ordered set of hyperboxes of
\mathbb{R}^{n}
—i. e., subsets of the
form
\prod_{i=1}^{n}[a_{i},b_{i}]
—ordered under reverse inclusion, with

\mathbb{R}^{n}
added as the bottom element. The three partially ordered sets are pointed directed-complete partial orders
and
\bigsqcup X=\bigcap X
, for any directed subset
X
.

Remark 2.4
.

The reverse inclusion order
X\sqsubseteq Y\iff Y\subseteq X

conforms to the interpretation of
X\sqsubseteq Y
as ‘
Y
contains
more information than
X
’, in that, compared with the larger set

X
, the subset
Y
is a more accurate approximation of the result.

A central concept in domain theory is that of the
way-below

relation. Assume that
(D,\sqsubseteq)
is a directed-complete partial order and let

x,y\in D
. The element
x
is said to be
way-below

y
—written as
x\ll y
—iff for every directed subset
X
of
D
,
if
y\sqsubseteq\bigsqcup X
, then there exists an element
d\in X
such
that
x\sqsubseteq d
. Informally,
x
is way-below
y
iff it is
impossible to get past
y
(by suprema of directed sets) without first
getting past
x
. We may also interpret
x\ll y
as ‘
x
is a
finitary approximation of
y
’, or even as, ‘
x
is a lot simpler than

y
’
[
1
, Section 2.2.1]
.

It is easy to prove that

\forall x,y\in D:x\ll y\implies x\sqsubseteq y
. The converse,
however, is not always true. For instance, over
\mathbb{K}\mathbb{R}^{n}_{\bot}

(hence, also over
{\mathbb{I}}\mathbb{R}^{n}_{\bot}
and
\mathbb{C}\mathbb{R}^{n}_{\bot}
) we
have the following characterization:

\forall K_{1},K_{2}\in\mathbb{K}\mathbb{R}^{n}_{\bot}:K_{1}\ll K_{2}\iff K_{2}\subseteq{K_{1}}^{\circ},

(3)

in which
{K_{1}}^{\circ}
denotes the interior of
K_{1}
.

Example 2.5
.

Consider the interval domain
{\mathbb{I}}\mathbb{R}^{1}_{\bot}
and let

x\coloneqq[0,2]
and
y\coloneqq[0,1]
. Although
x\sqsubseteq y
,
we have
{x}^{\circ}=(0,2)
. Hence,
by (
3
), we have
x\not\ll y
. We can
also prove that
x\not\ll y
without referring
to (
3
). Take the
\omega
-chain

y_{n}\coloneqq[-2^{-n},1+2^{-n}]
for
n\in\mathbb{N}
. We have

y=\bigsqcup_{n\in\mathbb{N}}y_{n}
, but

\forall n\in\mathbb{N}:x\nsqsubseteq y_{n}
.

Example 2.6
.

In the poset
\Sigma^{\infty}
of
Example
2.3
, we have:
x\ll y\iff(x\sqsubseteq y)\wedge(x\in\Sigma^{*})
.

For every element
x
of a directed-complete partial order
(D,\sqsubseteq)
, let

\twoheaddownarrow{x}\coloneqq\{{a\in D}\mid{a\ll x}\}
. In
domain-theoretic terms, the elements of
\twoheaddownarrow{x}
are the true
approximants of
x
. In fact, the way-below relation is also known as
the order of
approximation
[
1
, Section 2.2.1]
. A subset

B
of a directed-complete partial order
(D,\sqsubseteq)
is said to be a
basis
for

D
, if for every element
x\in D
, the set

B_{x}\coloneqq\twoheaddownarrow{x}\cap B
is a directed subset with supremum

x
, i. e.,
x=\bigsqcup B_{x}
. A directed-complete partial order
(D,\sqsubseteq)
is said to
be continuous if it has a basis, and
\omega
-continuous if it has a
countable basis. We call
(D,\sqsubseteq)
a
domain
if it is a
continuous pointed directed-complete partial order. The pointed directed-complete partial orders
{\mathbb{I}}\mathbb{R}^{n}_{\bot}
,

\mathbb{K}\mathbb{R}^{n}_{\bot}
, and
\mathbb{C}\mathbb{R}^{n}_{\bot}
, are all

\omega
-continuous domains, because:

•

B_{{\mathbb{I}}\mathbb{R}^{n}_{\bot}}\coloneqq\left\{{\mathbb{R}^{n}}\right\}\cup\{{C\in{\mathbb{I}}\mathbb{R}^{n}_{\bot}}\mid{C\text{ is a hyperbox with
rational coordinates}}\}
is a basis for
{\mathbb{I}}\mathbb{R}^{n}_{\bot}
;

•

B_{\mathbb{K}\mathbb{R}^{n}_{\bot}}\coloneqq\left\{{\mathbb{R}^{n}}\right\}\cup\{{C\in\mathbb{K}\mathbb{R}^{n}_{\bot}}\mid{C\text{ is a finite union of hyperboxes with
rational coordinates}}\}
is a basis for
\mathbb{K}\mathbb{R}^{n}_{\bot}
;

•

B_{\mathbb{C}\mathbb{R}^{n}_{\bot}}\coloneqq\left\{{\mathbb{R}^{n}}\right\}\cup\{{C\in\mathbb{C}\mathbb{R}^{n}_{\bot}}\mid{C\text{ is a convex polytope with rational
coordinates}}\}
is a basis for
\mathbb{C}\mathbb{R}^{n}_{\bot}
.

The poset
\Sigma^{\infty}
of Example
2.3
is
also an
\omega
-continuous domain, with
\Sigma^{*}
as a countable
basis. There is a significant difference between
\Sigma^{\infty}
and
the domains
{\mathbb{I}}\mathbb{R}^{n}_{\bot}
,
\mathbb{K}\mathbb{R}^{n}_{\bot}
, and

\mathbb{C}\mathbb{R}^{n}_{\bot}
, in that, for the elements of the basis
\Sigma^{*}

of
\Sigma^{\infty}
, we have:
\forall x\in\Sigma^{*}:x\ll x
. By (
3
), this is clearly not true of
the bases
B_{{\mathbb{I}}\mathbb{R}^{n}_{\bot}}
,
B_{\mathbb{K}\mathbb{R}^{n}_{\bot}}
and

B_{\mathbb{C}\mathbb{R}^{n}_{\bot}}
, or indeed, any other bases for

{\mathbb{I}}\mathbb{R}^{n}_{\bot}
,
\mathbb{K}\mathbb{R}^{n}_{\bot}
, and

\mathbb{C}\mathbb{R}^{n}_{\bot}
. As such, the domain
\Sigma^{\infty}
is an example

A domain-theoretic framework for abstract robustness analysis was

Assume that
\mathbb{S}
is a metric space,
\mathbb{K}\mathbb{S}
is the
pointed directed-complete partial order of non-empty compact subsets of
\mathbb{S}

\uparrow\negthickspace{X}\coloneqq\{{C\in\mathbb{K}\mathbb{S}}\mid{C\subseteq X}\}

. Intuitively, we regard a collection

U\subseteq\mathbb{K}\mathbb{S}
of compact subsets of
\mathbb{S}
as

is closed under sufficiently small
perturbations. Formally, we say that

\forall C\in U:\exists\delta>0:\uparrow\negthickspace{B(C,\delta)}\subseteq U

In a typical application, the input space of a neural network is a
bounded region of some Euclidean space. Therefore, we consider
feedforward neural networks with input space
[-M,M]^{n}
, for some

M>0
. For the output, we will mainly focus on the regressors of
type
N:[-M,M]^{n}\to\mathbb{R}
. The reason is that, within the framework
that we have adopted, robustness analysis of classifiers reduces to
Lipschitz estimation of regressors. Assume that

\hat{N}:[-M,M]^{n}\to\left\{{c_{1},\ldots,c_{k}}\right\}
is a classifier
network. A common architecture for such a classifier comprises a
regressor

followed
by an
\arg\max
operation. Thus, for a given input

x_{0}\in[-M,M]^{n}
, the index of the class predicted by
\hat{N}
is

i_{0}=\arg\max_{1\leq i\leq k}N_{i}(x_{0})
. In certified local
robustness analysis, the aim is to provide a radius
\beta>0
such
that, for any point
x
in the neighborhood of radius
\beta
around

x_{0}
, we have
\hat{N}(x)=\hat{N}(x_{0})
, i. e., a neighborhood
within which no adversarial examples can be found. A lower bound for
such a radius can be obtained by estimating the Lipschitz constants
of the functions

\{{N_{i_{0}}-N_{i}}\mid{1\leq i\leq k,i\neq i_{0}}\}
. As
such, robustness analysis of classifiers reduces to Lipschitz
estimation of regressors with one output. For more details, the
reader may refer
to
[
85

.

When

of (
7
) satisfies

\forall X\in\mathbb{K}[-M,M]^{n}:A_{N}(X)=N(X)
.

Before focusing on regressors, however, we briefly discuss some
issues related to robustness analysis of classifiers.

3.1
Classifiers

Let
C=\left\{{c_{1},\ldots,c_{k}}\right\}
be a set of classes, with
k\geq 1
,
and assume that the function
N:[-M,M]^{n}\to C
is (the semantic model
of) a classifier. As
C
is discrete, it is natural to metrize
C

using the discrete metric:

\forall x,y\in C:\quad d(x,y)\coloneqq\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}0,&\text{if }x=y,\\
1,&\text{if }x\neq y.\\
\end{array}\right.

The main problem with a classifier of type
N:[-M,M]^{n}\to C
is the
mapping of a connected space to a discrete one, where the topology on

[-M,M]^{n}
is the Euclidean topology, and the topology on
C
is the
discrete topology:

Proposition 3.4
.

A classifier
N:[-M,M]^{n}\to C
is continuous iff
N
is constant.

Proof.

The
\Leftarrow
direction is obvious. For the
\Rightarrow

direction, as
C
is a discrete space, then each singleton

\left\{{c_{i}}\right\}
is an open set. If
N
is continuous, then each inverse
image
N^{-1}(c_{i})
must also be open. If
N
takes more than one
value, then
[-M,M]^{n}=\bigcup_{1\leq i\leq k}N^{-1}(c_{i})
must be
the union of at least two disjoint non-empty open sets, which is
impossible as
[-M,M]^{n}
is a connected space.
∎

Proposition
3.4
implies that a
classifier may not be computable—in the framework of
Type-II Theory of Effectivity
[
84
]
—unless if it is
constant. This is because, by
[
53
, Theorem 2.13]
, if

N
is computable, then it must be continuous.

By moving to compact subsets, however, it is possible to obtain tight
robust approximations of classifiers, even in the presence of
discontinuities. As
C
is a finite set, it is compact under the
discrete topology, and
\mathbb{K}C
is the set of non-empty subsets of

C
, ordered under reverse inclusion.

Theorem 3.5
.

Assume that
A_{N}:\mathbb{K}[-M,M]^{n}\to\mathbb{K}C
is the reachability
map for a classifier
N:[-M,M]^{n}\to C
, defined
in (
7
). Then,
A_{N}
has a tightest
robust approximation
A_{N}^{\circledcirc}:\mathbb{K}[-M,M]^{n}\to\mathbb{K}C

which is also Scott-continuous.

Proof.

Since both
[-M,M]^{n}
and
C
are compact, the result follows from
Corollary
3.3
.
∎

Example 3.6
.

Assume that the classifier
N:[-1,1]\to\left\{{c_{1},c_{2}}\right\}
satisfies:

\forall x\in[-1,1]:\quad N(x)=\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}c_{1},&\text{if }x\in[-1,0],\\
c_{2},&\text{if }x\in(0,1].\\
\end{array}\right.

Then, according to (
7
), we have:

\forall x\in[-1,1]:\quad A_{N}(\left\{{x}\right\})=\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}\left\{{c_{1}}\right\},&\text{if }x\in[-1,0],\\
\left\{{c_{2}}\right\},&\text{if }x\in(0,1],\\
\end{array}\right.

while according to (
8
), we must have:

\forall x\in[-1,1]:\quad A_{N}^{\circledcirc}(\left\{{x}\right\})=\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}\left\{{c_{1}}\right\},&\text{if }x\in[-1,0),\\
\left\{{c_{1},c_{2}}\right\},&\text{if }x=0,\\
\left\{{c_{2}}\right\},&\text{if }x\in(0,1].\\
\end{array}\right.

As can be seen from Example
3.6
, the tightest
robust approximation of a non-constant classifier may be

multi-valued
over certain parts of the input domain.

Theorem 3.7
.

Assume that
N:[-M,M]^{n}\to C
is a non-constant classifier, and let

\hat{N}:\mathbb{K}[-M,M]^{n}\to\mathbb{K}C
be any robust approximation
of the reachability map
A_{N}
. Then,
\hat{N}(\left\{{x}\right\})
must be
multivalued for some
x\in[-M,M]^{n}
.

Proof.

To obtain a contradiction, assume that
\hat{N}(x)
is single-valued
for all
x\in[-M,M]^{n}
. Therefore, the restriction of
\hat{N}

over
[-M,M]^{n}
—which is the set of maximal elements of

\mathbb{K}[-M,M]^{n}
—must be equal to
N
. By
Corollary
2.11
, the classifier

N:[-M,M]^{n}\to C
must be continuous, which, combined with
Proposition
3.4
, implies that
N
is
a constant classifier, which is a contradiction.
∎

In Theorem
3.7
, assume that

C=\left\{{c_{1},\ldots,c_{k}}\right\}
. In case
\hat{N}
is the
tightest

robust approximation
A_{N}^{\circledcirc}
of
A_{N}
, then
\hat{N}
is required
to be multivalued only over the boundaries of

N^{-1}(c_{1}),\ldots,N^{-1}(c_{k})
. In typical applications, these
boundaries have Lebesgue measure zero, and the restriction of

A_{N}^{\circledcirc}
over
[-M,M]^{n}
is single-valued and equal to
N
,

almost everywhere
, e. g., as in
Example
3.6
. Computability of
A_{N}^{\circledcirc}
,
however, is an open problem in general, and even in cases where it is
known to be computable, little is known about complexity of its
computation. Thus, in practice, one aims for robust approximations

\hat{N}
that are ‘sufficiently’ tight, and over
[-M,M]^{n}
, may be
multivalued on sets that are not Lebesgue measure zero.

3.2
Regressors

Assume that a given feedforward regressor
N:[-M,M]^{n}\to\mathbb{R}
has
H

hidden layers. We let
n_{0}\coloneqq n
, and for each
1\leq i\leq H
,
let
n_{i}
denote the number of neurons in the
i
-th layer. Then, for
each
1\leq i\leq H
, we represent:

•

the weights as a matrix
W_{i}
, with
\dim(W_{i})=n_{i}\times n_{i-1}
;

•

the biases as a vector
b_{i}
, with
\dim(b_{i})=n_{i}\times 1
;

•

and the activation function (seen as a vector field) by

\sigma_{i}:\mathbb{R}^{n_{i}}\to\mathbb{R}^{n_{i}}
.

From the last hidden layer to the single output neuron, the weights
are represented as the vector
W_{H+1}
, with

\dim(W_{H+1})=1\times n_{H}
, and there are no biases or activation
functions. For any input
x\in[-M,M]^{n}
, the output of the neural
network may be calculated layer-by-layer as follows:

\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}Z_{0}(x)=x,\\
\hat{Z}_{i}(x)=W_{i}Z_{i-1}(x)+b_{i},&(1\leq i\leq H),\\
Z_{i}(x)=\sigma_{i}\left(\hat{Z}_{i}(x)\right),&(1\leq i\leq H),\\
N(x)=W_{H+1}Z_{H}(x).\end{array}\right.

(9)

We let
{\cal F}
denote the class of feedforward networks with a
single output neuron, and Lipschitz continuous activation functions,
whose output may be described using (
9
). Each

N\in{\cal F}
is continuous. Hence, it maps the compact set

[-M,M]^{n}
to a compact set, which we assume is included in

[-M^{\prime},M^{\prime}]
, for
M^{\prime}
large enough.

Proposition 3.8
.

Assume that
N:[-M,M]^{n}\to[-M^{\prime},M^{\prime}]
is a regressor in
{\cal F}
,
and
A_{N}
is its reachability map. Then, the map
A_{N}
has a
tightest robust approximation which is a robust extension of
N
,
i. e., an extension according to
Definition
2.19

(i)
.

Proof.

Since both
[-M,M]^{n}
and
[-M^{\prime},M^{\prime}]
are compact, by
Corollary
3.3
, the reachability
map
A_{N}
has a tightest robust approximation
A_{N}^{\circledcirc}:\mathbb{K}[-M,M]^{n}\to\mathbb{K}[-M^{\prime},M^{\prime}]
. To prove that

A_{N}^{\circledcirc}
is indeed an extension of
N
, we must show that over
the maximal elements of
\mathbb{K}[-M,M]^{n}
, the map
A_{N}^{\circledcirc}

takes singleton values. This is a straightforward consequence of the
fact that
N
is continuous.
∎

This is in contrast with the case of classifiers, where the network
itself did not have to be even continuous, and we had to be content
with tightest robust approximations.

Of particular interest is the interval enclosure of a given regressor,
which may not be the tightest robust extension of the regressor, but
it is a robust extension nonetheless, which is commonly used in
interval analysis of neural networks:

Theorem 3.9
.

Let
N:[-M,M]^{n}\to[-M^{\prime},M^{\prime}]
be a regressor in
{\cal F}
, and assume that:

•

(\cdot)^{\Box}:\mathbb{K}[-M,M]^{n}\to{\mathbb{I}}[-M,M]^{n}

is the hyperbox closure map of (
4
).

•

\tilde{N}:{\mathbb{I}}[-M,M]^{n}\to{\mathbb{I}}[-M^{\prime},M^{\prime}]

is any Scott-continuous extension of
N
, e. g., the canonical
interval extension of
Definition
2.19

(iii)
.

•

\iota:{\mathbb{I}}[-M^{\prime},M^{\prime}]\to\mathbb{K}[-M^{\prime},M^{\prime}]
is the
embedding of the subdomain
{\mathbb{I}}[-M^{\prime},M^{\prime}]
into

\mathbb{K}[-M^{\prime},M^{\prime}]
, as in Corollary
2.18
.

Then, the map
\hat{N}:\mathbb{K}[-M,M]^{n}\to\mathbb{K}[-M^{\prime},M^{\prime}]

defined by

\forall X\in\mathbb{K}[-M,M]^{n}:\hat{N}(X)\coloneqq\iota(\tilde{N}(X^{\Box}))
is a robust extension of
N
.

Proof.

We know that
(\cdot)^{\Box}
,
\tilde{N}
, and
\iota
are
Scott-continuous. Therefore, the map
\hat{N}
—being a composition
of the three—is Scott-continuous. Since both
[-M,M]^{n}
and

[-M^{\prime},M^{\prime}]
are compact, by Theorem
3.2
, the map

\hat{N}
is robust. Hence,
\hat{N}
is a robust approximation of

N
. To prove that
\hat{N}
is a robust extension of
N
, we must
show that over maximal input values it returns singletons. But this
is again a straightforward consequence of continuity of
N
.
∎

4
Validated Local Robustness Analysis

The results of Section
3
concern
globally robust approximation of neural networks. In the machine
learning literature, robustness analysis is mainly carried out
locally, that is, over specific locations in the input domain. A
classifier
N:\mathbb{R}^{n}\to\left\{{c_{1},\ldots,c_{k}}\right\}
may be regarded as
robust at
v\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
if, for some neighborhood
B(\left\{{v}\right\},r_{0})
of
radius
r_{0}>0
, we have:

\forall x\in B(\left\{{v}\right\},r_{0}):N(x)=N(v)
. In the current article,
by attack-agnostic robustness measurement we mean obtaining a tight
lower bound for the largest such
r_{0}
. This, in turn, can be reduced
to obtaining a tight upper bound for the local Lipschitz constant in a
neighborhood of the
point
v

[
45
,
85
]
,
which is the focus of the rest of the current article.

From a theoretical angle, the more challenging networks for Lipschitz
analysis are those with non-differentiable activation functions, such
as
\ReLU
. For such functions, a generalized notion of gradient must
be used, e. g.,
Clarke-gradient
[
14
]
. Indeed,
Clarke-gradient has been used recently for robustness analysis of
Lipschitz neural
networks
[
49
,
50
]
. On
the other hand, the
domain-theoretic
L
-derivative

(Definition
4.4

below]def:L_derivative)
introduced by
[
25
]
also
coincides with Clarke-gradient, a fact which was proven for
finite-dimensional Banach spaces
by
[
25
]
, and later generalized
to infinite-dimensional Banach spaces
by
[
46
]
. Using this, we develop a
domain-theoretic framework for Lipschitz analysis of feedforward
networks, which provides a theoretical foundation for methods such
as
[
49
,
50
]
.

Working with the
L
-derivative, however, is computationally
costly. It requires computation over
\mathbb{C}\mathbb{R}^{n}_{\bot}
and involves
a rather complicated chain rule
(Proposition
4.3
). Therefore, we consider a
hyperbox approximation of the
L
-derivative, which we call the

\hat{L}
-derivative (Definition
4.7
). This will
allow us to work with the relatively simpler domain

{\mathbb{I}}\mathbb{R}^{n}_{\bot}
and also a much simpler chain rule
(Lemma
4.9
). Finally, we will move on to
computation of the Lipschitz constant of a given regressor, according
to the following overall strategy.

Assume that
N\in{\cal F}
is a feedforward regressor. By
using (
9
), we obtain a unique closed-form
expression for
N
in terms of the weights, biases, and the activation
functions. Let

\tilde{L}(N):{\mathbb{I}}[-M,M]^{n}\to{\mathbb{I}}\mathbb{R}^{n}_{\bot}

denote the interval approximation of the
\hat{L}
-derivative of
N

obtained by applying the chain rule of
Lemma
4.9
over this closed-form expression. The
map
\tilde{L}(N)
is Scott-continuous. Since we consider

{\left\lVert\thinspace{.}\thinspace\right\rVert}_{\infty}
as the perturbation norm, we must compute the

{\left\lVert\thinspace{.}\thinspace\right\rVert}_{1}
-norm of the gradients (see
Remark
2.1
). First, we extend the
absolute value function to real intervals by defining

\lvert\thinspace{I}\thinspace\rvert\coloneqq\{{\lvert\thinspace{x}\thinspace\rvert}\mid{x\in I}\}
for any interval

I
. Then, we extend the
{\left\lVert\thinspace{.}\thinspace\right\rVert}_{1}
norm to hyperboxes by defining

{\left\lVert\thinspace{\prod_{i=1}^{n}I_{i}}\thinspace\right\rVert}_{1}\coloneqq\sum_{i=1}^{n}\lvert\thinspace{I_{i}}\thinspace\rvert
. For
instance, we have

{\left\lVert\thinspace{[-1,1]\times[-1,2]}\thinspace\right\rVert}_{1}=[0,1]+[0,2]=[0,3]
. The function

{\left\lVert\thinspace{.}\thinspace\right\rVert}_{1}:{\mathbb{I}}\mathbb{R}^{n}_{\bot}\to{\mathbb{I}}\mathbb{R}^{1}_{\bot}
is also
Scott-continuous. Therefore, the composition

{\left\lVert\thinspace{\tilde{L}(N)}\thinspace\right\rVert}_{1}:{\mathbb{I}}[-M,M]^{n}\to{\mathbb{I}}\mathbb{R}^{1}_{\bot}
is a Scott-continuous function. All that
remains is to find the maximum value and the maximum set of

{\left\lVert\thinspace{\tilde{L}(N)}\thinspace\right\rVert}_{1}
to approximate the Lipschitz constant and the
set of points where the maximum Lipschitz values are attained, within
any given neighborhood. This is one of the main contributions of the
current article and will be presented in detail in
Section
4.4
.

If, instead of
{\mathbb{I}}\mathbb{R}^{n}_{\bot}
, we use the finer domain

\mathbb{C}\mathbb{R}^{n}_{\bot}
, then the suitable chain rule will be that of
Proposition
4.3
, which provides a more
accurate result, at the cost of calculating the closed convex
hull. This has also been observed
in
[
50
]
. By using the

\hat{L}
-derivative and the chain rule of
Lemma
4.9
, we avoid computation of the convex
hull, and trade some accuracy for efficiency. As it turns out,
however,
we will not lose any accuracy over general position

\ReLU
networks (Theorem
4.19
), or over
differentiable networks
(Theorem
4.21
)
.

Remark 4.1
.

In this article, we focus on the
{\left\lVert\thinspace{.}\thinspace\right\rVert}_{\infty}
-norm for
perturbations because we will be working with hyperboxes, and in the
implementation, we will use interval arithmetic. This does not mean,
however, that we cannot analyze robustness with respect to

{\left\lVert\thinspace{.}\thinspace\right\rVert}_{p}
for other values of
p
. For example, assume that we
are given a closed neighborhood

V(x_{0},r)\coloneqq\{{x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}}\mid{{\left\lVert\thinspace{x-x_{0}}\thinspace\right\rVert}_{p}\leq r}\}
, for some
p\in[1,\infty)
. We can first cover the
neighborhood
V(x_{0},r)
with (a large number of sufficiently small)
hyperboxes
B=\{{b_{i}}\mid{i\in I}\}
. We obtain an
overapproximation of the
{\left\lVert\thinspace{.}\thinspace\right\rVert}_{p^{\prime}}
-norm of the Clarke-gradient
over each box
b_{i}
, and then obtain an approximation of the
Lipschitz constant over the neighborhood
V(x_{0},r)
.

4.1
Lipschitz Continuity and Lipschitz Constant

Assume that
(X,\delta_{X})
and
(Y,\delta_{Y})
are two metric spaces,

f:X\to Y
, and
A\subseteq X
. A real number
L\geq 0
is said
to be a
Lipschitz bound
for
f
over
A
if:

\forall a,b\in A:\delta_{Y}(f(a),f(b))\leq L\delta_{X}(a,b).

(10)

A function for which such a bound exists is said to be Lipschitz
continuous over
A
, and the smallest
L

satisfying (
10
)—which must exist—is called
the
Lipschitz constant
of
f
over
A
.

If
f
is Lipschitz continuous over
A
, then it is also continuous
over
A
, although the converse is not true. For instance, the
function
h:\ell^{1}_{\infty}\to\ell^{1}_{\infty}
, defined by

h(x)\coloneqq\sqrt{\lvert\thinspace{x}\thinspace\rvert}
, is continuous everywhere, but not
Lipschitz continuous over any neighborhood of
0
. Whenever
X
is a
compact subset of
\ell^{n}_{\infty}
and
f:X\to\mathbb{R}
is continuously
differentiable, then
f
is Lipschitz over
X
, but again, the
converse is not true. For example, the function
h:[-1,1]\to\mathbb{R}
,
defined by
h(x)\coloneqq\lvert\thinspace{x}\thinspace\rvert
, is Lipschitz continuous, but not
differentiable at
0
. To summarize, we have the following inclusions
for functions over compact subsets of
\mathbb{R}^{n}
:

\text{Continuously Differentiable}\subset\text{Lipschitz
Continuous}\subset\text{Continuous}.

Even though the inclusions are strict, there is a close relationship
between Lipschitz continuity and differentiability. Rademacher’s
theorem states that if
f
is Lipschitz continuous over an open subset

A
of
\mathbb{R}^{n}
, then it is (Fréchet) differentiable almost
everywhere (with respect to the Lebesgue measure) over

A

[
13
, Corollary 4.19]
. For
example, the function
\ReLU
—defined by

\forall x\in\mathbb{R}:\ReLU(x)\coloneqq\max(0,x)
—is (classically)
differentiable everywhere except at zero:

(\ReLU)^{\prime}(x)=\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}1,&\text{if }x>0,\\
0,&\text{if }x<0,\\
\mathrm{undefined},&\text{if }x=0.\end{array}\right.

4.2
Clarke-Gradient

Although
\ReLU
is not differentiable at
0
, one-sided limits of the
derivative exist, i. e.:

\lim_{x\to 0^{-}}(\ReLU)^{\prime}(x)=0\quad\text{and}\quad\lim_{x\to 0^{+}}(\ReLU)^{\prime}(x)=1.

(11)

As shown in Figure
1
(Left), in geometric terms, for every

\lambda\in[0,1]
, the line
y_{\lambda}(x)\coloneqq\lambda x

satisfies:

y_{\lambda}(0)=\ReLU(0)\quad\wedge\quad\forall x\in\mathbb{R}:y_{\lambda}(x)\leq\ReLU(x).

(12)

The interval
[0,1]
is the largest set of
\lambda
values which
satisfy (
12
), and may be considered as a gradient

set
for
\ReLU
at zero. The concept of generalized (Clarke)
gradient formalizes this idea. Clarke-gradient may be defined for
functions over any Banach space
X

[
14
, page
27]
. When
X
is
finite-dimensional, however, the Clarke-gradient of
f:X\to\mathbb{R}
has
a simpler characterization. Assume that
X
is an open subset of

\mathbb{R}^{n}
, and
f:X\to\mathbb{R}
is Lipschitz continuous over
X
. By
Rademacher’s theorem, we know that the set

{\cal N}_{f}\coloneqq\{{z\in X}\mid{f\text{ is not
differentiable at }z\,}\}
has Lebesgue measure zero. The
Clarke-gradient
\partial f(x)
of
f
at any
x\in X
satisfies:

\partial f(x)=\convHull\left\{{\lim_{i\to\infty}f^{\prime}(x_{i})}\;\vrule\;{\lim_{i\to\infty}x_{i}=x\wedge\forall i\in\mathbb{N}:x_{i}\notin{\cal N}_{f}}\right\}.

(13)

Equation (
13
) should be interpreted as
follows: take any sequence
(x_{i})_{i\in\mathbb{N}}
, which completely avoids
the set
{\cal N}_{f}
of points where
f
is not differentiable,
converges to
x
, and for which
\lim_{i\to\infty}f^{\prime}(x_{i})

exists. The Clarke-gradient is the convex hull of these
limits
[
14
, page 63]
. It
should be clear from (
11
)
and (
13
)
that
\partial\ReLU(0)=[0,1]
.

Proposition 4.2

(
[
14
, Proposition 2.1.2]
)
.

Assume that
p\in[1,\infty]
. Let
X
be an open subset of

\ell^{n}_{p}
and
f:X\to\mathbb{R}
be Lipschitz continuous over
X
with a
Lipschitz bound
L
. Then, for every
x\in X
:

(i)

\partial f(x)
is a
non-empty, convex, and compact subset of
\mathbb{R}^{n}
.

(ii)

\forall\xi\in\partial f(x):{\left\lVert\thinspace{\xi}\thinspace\right\rVert}_{p^{\prime}}\leq L
, in which
p^{\prime}
is the
conjugate of
p
as defined in (
1
).

The following chain rule for the Clarke-gradient is crucial for
proving the soundness of methods such
as
[

,
10
,
49
,
50
,

]
:

Proposition 4.3

(
Chain Rule
,

[
14
, Theorem 2.3.9]
)
.

Assume that
X\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{n}
is open,

h=(h_{1},\ldots,h_{m}):X\to\mathbb{R}^{m}
is Lipschitz continuous in a
neighborhood of
x\in X
, and

is Lipschitz

. Then,

f\coloneqq g\circ h
is Lipschitz in a neighborhood of
x
, and we
have:

\partial f(x)\subseteq\overline{\convHull}\left\{{\sum_{i=1}^{m}\alpha_{i}\zeta_{i}}\;\vrule\;{\zeta_{i}\in\partial h_{i}(x)\wedge\alpha\in\partial g(h(x))}\right\},

(14)

in which,
\overline{\convHull}
denotes the closed convex hull.

Unlike the chain rule for the classical derivative, the chain rule
of (

) provides only a subset relation,
which, in general, is not an equality. A simple example of lack of
equality is shown in Figure

b=[0,1]

and
O=(-1,1)
. We have
\ReLU\in\delta(O,b)
.

Definition 4.4

(
L
-derivative
[
21
, Section 2]
)
.

Assume that
O\subseteq U\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{n}
, both
O
and
U
are
open, and
b\in\mathbb{C}\mathbb{R}^{n}_{\bot}
:

(i)

The single-step tie
\delta(O,b)
is the set of all functions

f:U\to\mathbb{R}
that satisfy

\forall x,y\in O:b(x-y)\sqsubseteq f(x)-f(y)
. The set

b(x-y)
is obtained by taking the inner product of every element
of
b
with the vector
x-y
, and
\sqsubseteq
is the reverse
inclusion order on
\mathbb{C}\mathbb{R}^{1}_{\bot}
.

(ii)

The
L
-derivative of any function
f:U\to\mathbb{R}
is defined
as:

L(f)\coloneqq\bigsqcup\left\{{b\chi_{O}}\;\vrule\;{O\subseteq U,b\in\mathbb{C}\mathbb{R}^{n}_{\bot},f\in\delta(O,b)}\right\}.

As such, the map
L
has type
L:(U\to\mathbb{R})\to(U\to\mathbb{C}\mathbb{R}^{n}_{\bot})
.

Example 4.5
.

Assume that
U=(-2,2)
. Consider the function

\ReLU:(-2,2)\to\mathbb{R}
and the step function

b\chi_{O}:(-2,2)\to{\mathbb{I}}\mathbb{R}_{\bot}
with
b=[0,1]
and

O=(-1,1)
, as depicted in
Figure
1
. We have

\ReLU\in\delta(O,b)
. In fact, we have
\ReLU\in\delta(O,b)

for any open set
O
containing zero, e. g., the interval

(-2^{n},2^{n})
, for any
n\in\mathbb{N}
. Thus, one may verify that:

\forall x\in(-2,2):\quad L(\ReLU)(x)=\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}0,&\text{if }x<0,\\
{[0,1]},&\text{if }x=0,\\
1,&\text{if }x>0.\end{array}\right.

For any
f:U\to\mathbb{R}
, the
L
-derivative

L(f):U\to\mathbb{C}\mathbb{R}^{n}_{\bot}
is Euclidean-Scott-continuous. When

f
is classically differentiable at
x\in U
, the
L
-derivative and
the classical derivative
coincide
[
25
]
. Many of the
fundamental properties of the classical derivative can be generalized
to the domain-theoretic one, e. g., additivity and the chain
rule
[
23
]
. Of particular
importance to the current paper is the following result:

Theorem 4.6

(
[
25
,
46
]
)
.

Over Lipschitz continuous functions
f:U\to\mathbb{R}
, the
domain-theoretic
L
-derivative coincides with the Clarke-gradient,
i. e.,
\forall x\in U:L(f)(x)=\partial f(x)
.

In this paper, instead of working with the general convex sets in

\mathbb{C}\mathbb{R}^{n}_{\bot}
, we work with the simpler hyperboxes in

{\mathbb{I}}\mathbb{R}^{n}_{\bot}
:

Definition 4.7

(
\hat{L}
-derivative)
.

Let
U\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{n}
be an open set. In the definition of

L
-derivative (Definition
4.4
), if

\mathbb{C}\mathbb{R}^{n}_{\bot}
is replaced with
{\mathbb{I}}\mathbb{R}^{n}_{\bot}
, then
we obtain the concept of
\hat{L}
-derivative for functions of type

U\to\mathbb{R}
. For vector valued functions

f=(f_{1},\ldots,f_{m}):U\to\mathbb{R}^{m}
, we define:

\hat{L}(f)\coloneqq\left(\hat{L}(f_{1}),\ldots,\hat{L}(f_{m})\right)^{\intercal},

in which,
(\cdot)^{\intercal}
denotes the transpose of a matrix. In
other words, for each
1\leq i\leq m
and
x\in U
, let

\hat{L}(f_{i})(x)\equiv(\alpha_{i,1},\ldots,\alpha_{i,n})\in{\mathbb{I}}\mathbb{R}^{n}_{\bot}
. Then, for
each
x\in U
,
\hat{L}(f)(x)
is the
m\times n
interval matrix

[\alpha_{i,j}]_{1\leq i\leq m,1\leq j\leq n}
.

The
\hat{L}
-derivative is, in general, coarser than the

L
-derivative, as the following example demonstrates:

Example 4.8
.

Let
D_{n}
denote the closed unit disc in
\mathbb{R}^{n}
, and define

f:\mathbb{R}^{n}\to\mathbb{R}
by
f(x)={\left\lVert\thinspace{x}\thinspace\right\rVert}_{2}
, in which
{\left\lVert\thinspace{\cdot}\thinspace\right\rVert}_{2}

is the Euclidean norm. Then,
L(f)(0)=D_{n}
, while

\hat{L}(f)(0)=[-1,1]^{n}
.

As such, we lose some precision by using hyperboxes. On the other
hand, on hyperboxes, the chain rule has a simpler form:

Lemma 4.9

(
Chain Rule on Hyperboxes
,
[
23
, Lemma 3.3]
)
.

For any two functions
g:\mathbb{R}^{k}\to\mathbb{R}^{m}
and
f:\mathbb{R}^{m}\to\mathbb{R}^{n}
,
we have:

\hat{L}(f)(g(x))\times\hat{L}(g)(x)\sqsubseteq\hat{L}(f\circ g)(x).

4.4
Maximization Algorithm

For any closed compact interval
I
, we write

I=[{\underline{I}},{\overline{I}}]
. Assume that

f:\mathbb{R}^{n}\to{\mathbb{I}}\mathbb{R}^{1}_{\bot}
and
X\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{n}
is a
compact set. We define the maximum value and maximum set of
f
over

X
as follows:

\left\{\begin{array}[]{l}\maxV(f,X)\coloneqq[{\underline{z}},{\overline{z}}],\quad\text{where ${\underline{z}}\coloneqq\sup_{x\in X}{\underline{f(x)}}$ and
${\overline{z}}\coloneqq\sup_{x\in X}{\overline{f(x)}}$ },\\
\maxS(f,X)\coloneqq\left\{{x\in X}\;\vrule\;{f(x)\cap\maxV(f,X)\neq\emptyset}\right\}.\end{array}\right.

(16)

For any
\hat{f}:{\mathbb{I}}\mathbb{R}^{n}_{\bot}\to{\mathbb{I}}\mathbb{R}^{1}_{\bot}
,
the maximum value and maximum set are defined by considering the
restriction of
\hat{f}
over the maximal elements
\mathbb{R}^{n}
.

Proposition 4.10
.

Assume that
\hat{f}:{\mathbb{I}}\mathbb{R}^{n}_{\bot}\to{\mathbb{I}}\mathbb{R}^{1}_{\bot}
is
Scott-continuous, and
X
is a compact subset of
\mathbb{R}^{n}
. Then

\maxS(\hat{f},X)
is a compact subset of
\mathbb{R}^{n}
.

Proof.

Let
f:\mathbb{R}^{n}\to{\mathbb{I}}\mathbb{R}^{1}_{\bot}
be the restriction of

\hat{f}
over the maximal elements
\mathbb{R}^{n}
. As
X
is compact, it
suffices to prove that
\maxS(f,X)
is a closed subset of
X
. Let

x\in X\setminus\maxS(f,X)
be an arbitrary point, and write

f(x)=[{\underline{f(x)}},{\overline{f(x)}}]
. Since
x\not\in\maxS(f,X)
,
then
{\overline{f(x)}}<{\underline{z}}
, where

z\equiv[{\underline{z}},{\overline{z}}]=\maxV(f,X)
. As
\hat{f}
is
Scott-continuous, then, by
Corollary
2.11
, the map
f
must be
Euclidean-Scott-continuous. In turn, by
Proposition
2.14
, the function
{\overline{f}}

must be upper semicontinuous. As
{\overline{f(x)}}<{\underline{z}}
, for some
neighborhood
O_{x}
of
x
, we must have:

\forall y\in O_{x}:{\overline{f(y)}}<{\underline{z}}
. This entails that

O_{x}\cap\maxS(f,X)=\emptyset
.
∎

Thus, in the sequel, we consider the functionals:

\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}\maxV:&({\mathbb{I}}\mathbb{R}^{n}_{\bot}\Rightarrow{\mathbb{I}}\mathbb{R}^{1}_{\bot})\to\mathbb{K}\mathbb{R}^{n}_{\bot}\to{\mathbb{I}}\mathbb{R}^{1}_{\bot},\\
\maxS:&({\mathbb{I}}\mathbb{R}^{n}_{\bot}\Rightarrow{\mathbb{I}}\mathbb{R}^{1}_{\bot})\to\mathbb{K}\mathbb{R}^{n}_{\bot}\to\mathbb{K}\mathbb{R}^{n}_{\bot},\end{array}\right.

which, given any Scott-continuous

f\in{\mathbb{I}}\mathbb{R}^{n}_{\bot}\Rightarrow{\mathbb{I}}\mathbb{R}^{1}_{\bot}
and
compact set
X\in\mathbb{K}\mathbb{R}^{n}_{\bot}
, return the maximum value and
the maximum set of
f
over
X
, respectively. As an example, assume
that
f\in{\mathbb{I}}\mathbb{R}^{1}_{\bot}\Rightarrow{\mathbb{I}}\mathbb{R}^{1}_{\bot}

is the canonical interval extension of

\partial\ReLU:\mathbb{R}\to{\mathbb{I}}\mathbb{R}^{1}_{\bot}
. Then:

\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}\maxV(f,[-1,1])=[1,1],&\maxS(f,[-1,1])=[0,1],\\
\maxV(f,[-1,0])=[0,1],&\maxS(f,[-1,0])=[-1,0],\\
\end{array}\right.

(17)

which shows that the functionals
\maxV
and
\maxS
are not even
monotone in their second argument. For fixed second arguments,
however, both are Scott-continuous in their first argument:

Theorem 4.11

(
Scott-continuity
)
.

For any
X\in\mathbb{K}\mathbb{R}^{n}_{\bot}
, the functionals

\maxV(\cdot,X):({\mathbb{I}}\mathbb{R}^{n}_{\bot}\Rightarrow{\mathbb{I}}\mathbb{R}^{1}_{\bot})\to{\mathbb{I}}\mathbb{R}^{1}_{\bot}
and

\maxS(\cdot,X):({\mathbb{I}}\mathbb{R}^{n}_{\bot}\Rightarrow{\mathbb{I}}\mathbb{R}^{1}_{\bot})\to\mathbb{K}\mathbb{R}^{n}_{\bot}
are Scott-continuous.

Proof.

As the domains

{\mathbb{I}}\mathbb{R}^{n}_{\bot}\Rightarrow{\mathbb{I}}\mathbb{R}^{1}_{\bot}
,

{\mathbb{I}}\mathbb{R}^{1}_{\bot}
, and
\mathbb{K}\mathbb{R}^{n}_{\bot}
are

\omega
-continuous, by Proposition
2.9
, it
suffices to show that both functionals are monotone and preserve the
suprema of
\omega
-chains.

To prove monotonicity, assume that

f,g\in{\mathbb{I}}\mathbb{R}^{n}_{\bot}\Rightarrow{\mathbb{I}}\mathbb{R}^{1}_{\bot}
, and

f\sqsubseteq g
. Thus,

\forall x\in X:({\underline{f(x)}}\leq{\underline{g(x)}})\wedge({\overline{g(x)}}\leq{\overline{f(x)}})
. This implies that

\sup_{x\in X}{\underline{f(x)}}\leq\sup_{x\in X}{\underline{g(x)}}
and

\sup_{x\in X}{\overline{g(x)}}\leq\sup_{x\in X}{\overline{f(x)}}
. Therefore:

\maxV(f,X)\sqsubseteq\maxV(g,X).

(18)

Take an arbitrary
x\in\maxS(g,X)
. By definition, we must have

g(x)\cap\maxV(g,X)\neq\emptyset
, which, combined with the
assumption
f\sqsubseteq g
, implies

f(x)\cap\maxV(g,X)\neq\emptyset
. This, combined
with (
18
), entails that

f(x)\cap\maxV(f,X)\neq\emptyset
. Hence,
x\in\maxS(f,X)
,
and we have
\maxS(f,X)\sqsubseteq\maxS(g,X)
. The proof of
monotonicity is complete.

Next, let
(f_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
be an
\omega
-chain in

{\mathbb{I}}\mathbb{R}^{n}_{\bot}\Rightarrow{\mathbb{I}}\mathbb{R}^{1}_{\bot}
, with

f=\bigsqcup_{n\in\mathbb{N}}f_{n}
. As both
\maxV
and
\maxS
are
monotone, we have

\bigsqcup_{n\in\mathbb{N}}\maxV(f_{n},X)\sqsubseteq\maxV(f,X)
and

\bigsqcup_{n\in\mathbb{N}}\maxS(f_{n},X)\sqsubseteq\maxS(f,X)
. Thus, it
only remains to prove the
\sqsupseteq
direction in both cases. In
what follows, we let

z\equiv[{\underline{z}},{\overline{z}}]\coloneqq\maxV(f,X)
, and for each

n\in\mathbb{N}
,
z_{n}\equiv[{\underline{z_{n}}},{\overline{z_{n}}}]\coloneqq\maxV(f_{n},X)
.

\maxV
:

First, consider an arbitrary
t<{\underline{z}}
and let

\epsilon\coloneqq({\underline{z}}-t)/4
. By definition,

\exists x_{0}\in X:{\underline{f(x_{0})}}>{\underline{z}}-\epsilon
. Since

f=\bigsqcup_{n\in\mathbb{N}}f_{n}
, we must have

\exists N_{0}\in\mathbb{N}:\forall n\geq N_{0}:{\underline{f_{n}(x_{0})}}>{\underline{f(x_{0})}}-\epsilon>{\underline{z}}-2\epsilon>t
.
Therefore:

\forall t\in\mathbb{R}:\quad t<{\underline{z}}\implies\exists n\in\mathbb{N}:t<{\underline{z_{n}}}.

(19)

Next, we consider an arbitrary
t>{\overline{z}}
and let

\epsilon\coloneqq(t-{\overline{z}})/4
. Since

f=\bigsqcup_{n\in\mathbb{N}}f_{n}
, we must have:

\forall x\in X:\exists N_{x}\in\mathbb{N}:\forall n\geq N_{x}:\quad{\overline{f_{n}(x)}}<{\overline{f(x)}}+\epsilon.

For each fixed
n\in\mathbb{N}
, the map
{\overline{f_{n}}}
is upper
semicontinuous. Hence, for any
x\in X
, there exists a
neighborhood
O_{x}
of
x
for which we have

\forall y\in O_{x}:\quad{\overline{f_{N_{x}}(y)}}<{\overline{f_{N_{x}}(x)}}+\epsilon<{\overline{f(x)}}+2\epsilon\leq{\overline{z}}+2\epsilon.

As the sequence
(f_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
is assumed to be an

\omega
-chain, the upper bounds
({\overline{f_{n}}})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
are
non-increasing, and we obtain:

\forall x\in X:\forall y\in O_{x}:\forall n\geq N_{x}:\quad{\overline{f_{n}(y)}}\leq{\overline{z}}+2\epsilon.

As
X
is compact and
X\subseteq\bigcup_{x\in X}O_{x}
, then for
finitely many neighborhoods
\left\{{O_{x_{1}},\ldots,O_{x_{m}}}\right\}
we must
have
X\subseteq\bigcup_{i=1}^{m}O_{x_{i}}
. Thus, if we let

N_{0}\coloneqq\max\left\{{N_{x_{1}},\ldots,N_{x_{m}}}\right\}
, we must have:

\forall n\geq N_{0}:\forall y\in X:\quad{\overline{f_{n}(y)}}\leq{\overline{z}}+2\epsilon,

which entails that:

\forall t\in\mathbb{R}:\quad{\overline{z}}<t\implies\exists N_{0}\in\mathbb{N}:\forall n\geq N_{0}:{\overline{z_{n}}}\leq{\overline{z}}+2\epsilon<t.

(20)

From (
19
) and (
20
), we deduce
that
\bigsqcup_{n\in\mathbb{N}}\maxV(f_{n},X)\sqsupseteq\maxV(f,X)
.

\maxS
:

Assume that
x\in X\setminus\maxS(f,X)
, which
implies that
{\overline{f(x)}}<{\underline{z}}
. Take

\epsilon=\left({\underline{z}}-{\overline{f(x)}}\right)/4
. Since

{\underline{z}}=\sup_{y\in X}{\underline{f(y)}}
, there exists
x_{1}\in X
such
that
{\underline{f(x_{1})}}>{\underline{z}}-\epsilon
. As

f=\bigsqcup_{n\in\mathbb{N}}f_{n}
, there exists
N_{1}\in\mathbb{N}
, such
that:

\forall n\geq N_{1}:{\underline{f_{n}(x_{1})}}>{\underline{f(x_{1})}}-\epsilon>{\underline{z}}-2\epsilon.

(21)

Furthermore, there exists
N_{2}\in\mathbb{N}
, such that:

\forall n\geq N_{2}:{\overline{f_{n}(x)}}<{\overline{f(x)}}+\epsilon={\underline{z}}-3\epsilon.

(22)

Taking
N_{3}=\max\left\{{N_{1},N_{2}}\right\}
, from (
21
)
and (
22
), we obtain

\forall n\geq N_{3}:{\overline{f_{n}(x)}}<{\underline{f_{n}(x_{1})}}\leq{\underline{z_{n}}}
,
which implies that
x\not\in\maxS(f_{n},X)
. Therefore,

\bigsqcup_{n\in\mathbb{N}}\maxS(f_{n},X)\sqsupseteq\maxS(f,X)
.

The proof is complete.
∎

Algorithm
1
presents an implementation of
\maxV

and
\maxS
, which we use in our experiments. As pointed out before,
the set
B_{\mathbb{K}\mathbb{R}^{n}_{\bot}}
, consisting of finite unions of
rational hyperboxes, forms a countable basis for the

\omega
-continuous domain
\mathbb{K}\mathbb{R}^{n}_{\bot}
. We have used this fact
in Algorithm
1
, which outputs enclosures of the
maximum set using elements of
B_{\mathbb{K}\mathbb{R}^{n}_{\bot}}
.

Apart from the function
f
and the neighborhood
X
,
Algorithm
1
requires three other input
parameters:
m_{\iota}
,
m_{b}
, and
\delta
. These are crucial for making
sure that the algorithm stops below a reasonable threshold. In
line
9
, we discard all the boxes in
B_{i}

which cannot possibly contain any maximum points. How many boxes are
discarded depends on a number of factors, most importantly, whether
the function
f
has a flat section around the maximum set or not. The
condition
2^{n}\lvert\thinspace{\hat{B}_{i}}\thinspace\rvert\geq m_{b}
in
line
13
is necessary, because in cases where
flat sections exist, the number of elements of
\hat{B}_{i}
grows
exponentially with each iteration. In the
if
statement of
line
10
, we make sure that bisecting
\hat{B}_{i}

will not create too many boxes before executing the bisection command
in the first place.

Algorithm 1

Enclosing
\maxV(f,X)
and
\maxS(f,X)
.

0:

X\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{n}
: A finite union of hyperboxes;

f\in{\mathbb{I}}X\Rightarrow{\mathbb{I}}\mathbb{R}^{1}_{\bot}
: A Scott-continuous interval function;

m_{\iota}\in\mathbb{N}
: maximum iterations;

m_{b}\in\mathbb{N}
: maximum number of boxes;

\delta\geq 0
: a dyadic number indicating target width.

Enclosures of
\maxV(f,X)
and
\maxS(f,X)

i\leftarrow 0
// number of iterations

2:

B_{1}\leftarrow X
// initialize the set of hyperboxes

3:

repeat

4:

i\leftarrow i+1

5:

L_{i}\coloneqq\{{f(b)}\mid{b\in B_{i}}\}

6:

{\overline{z_{i}}}\leftarrow\max\{{{\overline{I}}}\mid{I\in L_{i}}\}
// the maximum upper end point

7:

{\underline{z_{i}}}\leftarrow\max\{{{\underline{I}}}\mid{I\in L_{i}}\}

// the maximum lower end point

I_{i}\coloneqq[{{\underline{z_{i}}},{\overline{z_{i}}}}]
// maximum value

\hat{B}_{i}\leftarrow\{{b\in B_{i}}\mid{{\underline{z_{i}}}\in f(b)}\}
// retain only those boxes
that could contain maximum points

10:

(2^{n}\lvert\thinspace{\hat{B}_{i}}\thinspace\rvert<m_{b})

then

11:

B_{i+1}\leftarrow\mathtt{bisect}(\hat{B}_{i})

// bisect all boxes in
\hat{B}_{i}
, along all dimensions

end

if

13:

until

(i\geq m_{\iota})
or
(2^{n}\lvert\thinspace{\hat{B}_{i}}\thinspace\rvert\geq m_{b})

Algorithm
1
closely follows the definition of

\maxV
and
\maxS
given in (
16
). Hence, the proof of
soundness is straightforward:

Lemma 4.12

(
Soundness
)
.

For any given
f
,
X
,
m_{\iota}
,
m_{b}
, and
\delta
,
Algorithm
1
halts in finite time and returns
enclosures of
\maxV(f,X)
and
\maxS(f,X)
.

Proof.

We prove the lemma by induction over
i
, that is, the number of
iterations of the main
repeat-until
loop. When
i=1
, we
have
B_{i}=X
, which trivially contains
\maxS(f,X)
.

For the inductive step, assume that at iteration
i
, before the start
of the loop, the finite set of hyperboxes
B_{i}
satisfies

\maxS(f,X)\subseteq\bigcup B_{i}
. Assume that

\tilde{f}\in X\Rightarrow{\mathbb{I}}\mathbb{R}^{1}_{\bot}
is the restriction of
the interval function
f
over the maximal elements
X
, which entails
that
f
is an interval approximation of
\tilde{f}
. As a result,

I_{i}
is an overapproximation of
\maxV(\tilde{f},X)
, which entails
that the set
\hat{B}_{i}
—obtained in
line
9
—is an overapproximation of

\maxS(\tilde{f},X)
.

The fact that the algorithm always halts in finite time follows from
the stopping condition
i\geq m_{\iota}
of line
13
.
∎

The algorithm combines the definition of
\maxV
and
\maxS
given
in (
16
) with a bisection operation that increases the
accuracy at each iteration. We prove that the result can be obtained
to any degree of accuracy. To that end, we first define the following
sequence:

\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}X_{0}\coloneqq X,&\\
X_{i+1}\coloneqq\mathtt{bisect}(X_{i}),&(\forall i\in\mathbb{N}).\end{array}\right.

(23)

Note that by bisecting a box
b\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{n}
we mean bisecting
along all the
n
coordinates, which generates
2^{n}
boxes. It is
straightforward to verify that

\forall i\in\mathbb{N}:\hat{B}_{i}\subseteq B_{i}\subseteq X_{i}
.

Theorem 4.13

(
Completeness
)
.

Assume that
\Psi(\hat{f},X,m_{\iota},m_{b},\delta)
is the function
implemented by Algorithm
1
. For any given
\hat{f}

and
X
:

\lim_{m_{\iota}\to\infty,m_{b}\to\infty,\delta\to 0}\Psi(\hat{f},X,m_{\iota},m_{b},\delta)=\left(\maxV(\hat{f},X),\maxS(\hat{f},X)\right).

Proof.

We write the restriction of

\hat{f}\in{\mathbb{I}}X\Rightarrow{\mathbb{I}}\mathbb{R}^{1}_{\bot}
over the
maximal elements as

f\equiv[{\underline{f}},{\overline{f}}]:X\to{\mathbb{I}}\mathbb{R}^{1}_{\bot}
. As

\hat{f}
is Scott-continuous, by
Corollary
2.11
and
Proposition
2.14
,
{\underline{f}}
and
{\overline{f}}

are lower and upper semicontinuous, respectively.

\maxV
:

Let
z\equiv[{\underline{z}},{\overline{z}}]\coloneqq\maxV(\hat{f},X)
. As

{\overline{f}}
is upper semicontinuous, then it must attain its maximum
over the compact set
X
at some point
x_{0}\in X
. Thus,

\forall x\in X:{\overline{f}}(x)\leq{\overline{f}}(x_{0})
. Suppose that

\epsilon>0
is given. By upper semicontinuity of
{\overline{f}}
, for
each
x\in X
, there exists a neighborhood
O_{x}
such that

\forall y\in O_{x}:{\overline{f}}(y)\leq{\overline{f}}(x)+\epsilon\leq{\overline{f}}(x_{0})+\epsilon
. By referring to the sequence

(X_{i})_{i\in\mathbb{N}}
defined in (
23
), and using Scott
continuity of
\hat{f}
, we deduce that, for each
x\in X
, there
exists a sufficiently large index
N_{x}\in\mathbb{N}
that satisfies the
following property:

•

There exist
k_{x}\in\mathbb{N}
and finitely many boxes

\{{B_{N_{x},j}}\mid{1\leq j\leq k_{x}}\}
such that:

x\in{\left(\bigcup_{1\leq j\leq k_{x}}B_{N_{x},j}\right)}^{\circ}\text{ and }\left(\bigcup_{1\leq j\leq k_{x}}B_{N_{x},j}\right)\subseteq O_{x},

(24)

in which
{(\cdot)}^{\circ}
denotes the interior operator, and:

\forall j\in\left\{{1,\ldots,k_{x}}\right\}:\quad{\overline{\hat{f}(B_{N_{x},j})}}\leq{\overline{f}}(x_{0})+\epsilon.

(25)

The reason for considering
k_{x}
boxes

\{{B_{N_{x},j}}\mid{1\leq j\leq k_{x}}\}
—rather than just one
box—is that, if at some iteration
i_{0}\in\mathbb{N}
, the point
x
lies
on the boundary of a box in
X_{i_{0}}
, then it will remain on the
boundary of boxes in
X_{i}
for all
i\geq i_{0}
. In other words, it
will not be in the interior of any boxes in subsequent bisections. Of
course, as each point
x\in X
may be on the boundaries of at most

2^{n}
adjacent boxes in
X_{N_{x}}
, then we can assume that

k_{x}\leq 2^{n}
.

For each
x\in X
, we define the neighborhood

U_{x}\coloneqq{(\bigcup_{1\leq j\leq k_{x}}B_{N_{x},j})}^{\circ}
. As

X
is compact, then it may be covered by finitely many of such
neighborhoods (say)
\{{U_{x_{i}}}\mid{1\leq i\leq m}\}
. By
taking
N=\max_{1\leq i\leq m}N_{x_{i}}
, and
considering (
25
), we deduce that, at the latest, at
iteration
N
, we must have
{\overline{z_{i}}}\leq{\overline{z}}+\epsilon
.

One the other hand, as
{\underline{z}}\coloneqq\sup_{x\in X}{\underline{f(x)}}
, for
some
x^{\prime}_{0}\in X
we must have
{\underline{f}}(x^{\prime}_{0})>{\underline{z}}-\epsilon/2
. Furthermore, as
{\underline{f}}
is lower semicontinuous, then
for some neighborhood
X^{\prime}_{0}
of
x^{\prime}_{0}
, we must have

\forall y\in X^{\prime}_{0}:{\underline{f}}(y)>{\underline{f}}(x^{\prime}_{0})-\epsilon/2>{\underline{z}}-\epsilon
. Similar to the previous argument, we can show that, after
a sufficient number of bisections, we must have

{\underline{z_{i}}}\geq{\underline{z}}-\epsilon
.

\maxS
:

Assume that
x\in X\setminus\maxS(\hat{f},X)
. By
Proposition
4.10
, the set
\maxS(\hat{f},X)
is
compact. Hence, the set
X\setminus\maxS(\hat{f},X)
is relatively
open in
X
and there exists a neighborhood
O_{x}
of
x
which
satisfies
\forall y\in O_{x}:{\overline{f}}(y)<{\underline{z}}
. Once again, we
can show that, after a sufficiently large number of bisections (say)

N_{x}
, for a finite number of boxes, the
relation (
24
) holds. This entails that all these
boxes must be discarded in line
9
of the
algorithm after at most
N_{x}
iterations.

∎

4.5
Computability

For computable analysis of the operators discussed in this work, we
need an effective structure on the underlying domains. To that end, we
use the concept of effectively given
domains
[
75
]
. Specifically, we follow the approach
taken
in
[
24
, Section 3]
.

Assume that
(D,\sqsubseteq)
is an
\omega
-continuous domain, with
a countable basis
B
that is enumerated as follows:

B=\left\{{b_{0}=\bot,b_{1},\ldots,b_{n},\ldots}\right\}.

(26)

We say that the domain
D
is
effectively given
with
respect to the enumeration (
26
), if the set

\{{(i,j)\in\mathbb{N}\times\mathbb{N}}\mid{b_{i}\ll b_{j}}\}
is
recursively enumerable, in which
\ll
is the way-below relation
on
D
.

In the following proposition, computability is to be understood
according to Type-II Theory of Effectivity
[
84
]
.

Proposition 4.14

(Computable elements and functions)
.

Let
D
and
E
be two effectively given domains, with enumerated
bases
B_{1}=\left\{{d_{0},d_{1},\ldots,d_{n},\ldots}\right\}
and

B_{2}=\left\{{e_{0},e_{1},\ldots,e_{n},\ldots}\right\}
, respectively. An
element
x\in D
is computable iff the set

\{{i\in\mathbb{N}}\mid{d_{i}\ll x}\}
is recursively enumerable. A
map
f:D\to E
is computable iff

\{{(i,j)\in\mathbb{N}\times\mathbb{N}}\mid{e_{i}\ll f(d_{j})}\}
is
recursively enumerable.

Proof.

See
[
24
, Proposition 3 and Theorem 9]
.
∎

Arithmetic operators have computable domain-theoretic extensions, so
do the common activation functions (e. g.,
\ReLU
,
\tanh
, or
sigmoidal) and their
Clarke-gradients
[
20
,
33
,
34
,
21
]
. Our
aim here is to study the computability of
\maxV
and

\maxS
. From (
17
), we know that

\maxV
and
\maxS
are not even monotone in their second argument,
while, by Theorem
4.11
, if we fix

X\in\mathbb{K}\mathbb{R}^{n}_{\bot}
, then the functionals

\maxV(\cdot,X):({\mathbb{I}}\mathbb{R}^{n}_{\bot}\Rightarrow{\mathbb{I}}\mathbb{R}^{1}_{\bot})\to{\mathbb{I}}\mathbb{R}^{1}_{\bot}
and

\maxS(\cdot,X):({\mathbb{I}}\mathbb{R}^{n}_{\bot}\Rightarrow{\mathbb{I}}\mathbb{R}^{1}_{\bot})\to\mathbb{K}\mathbb{R}^{n}_{\bot}
are Scott-continuous. By
referring to Proposition
4.14
, to study
computability of these two operators, we need to enumerate specific
countable bases for the domains

{\mathbb{I}}\mathbb{R}^{n}_{\bot}\Rightarrow{\mathbb{I}}\mathbb{R}^{1}_{\bot}
,

{\mathbb{I}}\mathbb{R}^{1}_{\bot}
, and
\mathbb{K}\mathbb{R}^{n}_{\bot}
. We already know that

B_{{\mathbb{I}}\mathbb{R}^{1}_{\bot}}
and
B_{\mathbb{K}\mathbb{R}^{n}_{\bot}}
form countable
bases for
{\mathbb{I}}\mathbb{R}^{1}_{\bot}
and
\mathbb{K}\mathbb{R}^{n}_{\bot}
,
respectively, and it is straightforward to effectively enumerate them.

For the function space

{\mathbb{I}}\mathbb{R}^{n}_{\bot}\Rightarrow{\mathbb{I}}\mathbb{R}^{1}_{\bot}
, we use
the common step function construction. Recall the single-step
functions as defined in (
15
). Assume that
D

and
E
are two domains. For any
d\in D
and
e\in E
, we must
have:

e\chi_{\twoheaduparrow{d}}(x)\coloneqq\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}e,&\text{if }d\ll x,\\
\bot,&\text{if }d\not\ll x.\end{array}\right.

Note that, by Proposition
2.10
, the set

\twoheaduparrow{d}
is Scott-open. By a step function we mean the supremum
of a finite set of single-step functions. If the domains
D
and
E

are bounded-complete, with countable bases

B_{D}=\left\{{d_{0},\ldots,d_{n},\ldots}\right\}
and

B_{E}=\left\{{e_{0},\ldots,e_{n},\ldots}\right\}
, then the countable collection:

B_{D\Rightarrow E}\coloneqq\left\{{\bigsqcup_{i\in I}e_{i}\chi_{\twoheaduparrow{d_{i}}}}\;\vrule\;{I\text{ is finite},\{{e_{i}\chi_{\twoheaduparrow{d_{i}}}}\mid{i\in I}\}\text{ is bounded}}\right\}

(27)

forms a basis for the function space

D\Rightarrow E

[
1
, Section 4]
.

Thus, we consider the bases
B_{{\mathbb{I}}\mathbb{R}^{n}_{\bot}}
and

B_{{\mathbb{I}}\mathbb{R}^{1}_{\bot}}
, and construct the countable basis

B_{{\mathbb{I}}\mathbb{R}^{n}_{\bot}\Rightarrow{\mathbb{I}}\mathbb{R}^{1}_{\bot}}
for the
function space

{\mathbb{I}}\mathbb{R}^{n}_{\bot}\Rightarrow{\mathbb{I}}\mathbb{R}^{1}_{\bot}
, according
to (
27
). The key point is that, each step function in

B_{{\mathbb{I}}\mathbb{R}^{n}_{\bot}\Rightarrow{\mathbb{I}}\mathbb{R}^{1}_{\bot}}
maps any
given hyperbox with rational coordinates to a rational interval. We
formulate this as:

Proposition 4.15
.

Each step function in

B_{{\mathbb{I}}\mathbb{R}^{n}_{\bot}\Rightarrow{\mathbb{I}}\mathbb{R}^{1}_{\bot}}
maps
any given element of
B_{{\mathbb{I}}\mathbb{R}^{n}_{\bot}}
to an element of
B_{{\mathbb{I}}\mathbb{R}^{1}_{\bot}}
.

As such, we call the elements of

B_{{\mathbb{I}}\mathbb{R}^{n}_{\bot}\Rightarrow{\mathbb{I}}\mathbb{R}^{1}_{\bot}}
rational
step functions.

Theorem 4.16

(
Computability
)
.

Assume that
X\in B_{\mathbb{K}\mathbb{R}^{n}_{\bot}}
is fixed, that is,
X
is
a finite union of hyperboxes with rational coordinates. Then,
the functionals

\maxV(\cdot,X):({\mathbb{I}}\mathbb{R}^{n}_{\bot}\Rightarrow{\mathbb{I}}\mathbb{R}^{1}_{\bot})\to{\mathbb{I}}\mathbb{R}^{1}_{\bot}
and

\maxS(\cdot,X):({\mathbb{I}}\mathbb{R}^{n}_{\bot}\Rightarrow{\mathbb{I}}\mathbb{R}^{1}_{\bot})\to\mathbb{K}\mathbb{R}^{n}_{\bot}
are computable.

Proof.

We prove computability according to
Proposition
4.14
. Let

f\in B_{{\mathbb{I}}\mathbb{R}^{n}_{\bot}\Rightarrow{\mathbb{I}}\mathbb{R}^{1}_{\bot}}

be a rational step function.

\maxV
:

Assume that

e\equiv[{\underline{e}},{\overline{e}}]\in B_{{\mathbb{I}}\mathbb{R}^{1}_{\bot}}
, that
is,
e
is a rational interval. To decide

e\ll\maxV(f,X)=[{\underline{z}},{\overline{z}}]
, we run
Algorithm
1
. As
\twoheaduparrow{e}
is
Scott-open, then by completeness (Theorem
4.13
),
after sufficiently large number of iterations (say)
N_{f}
, we must
have
\forall i\geq N_{f}:e\ll[{{\underline{z_{i}}},{\overline{z_{i}}}}]
. By (
3
), the relation

e\ll[{{\underline{z_{i}}},{\overline{z_{i}}}}]
is equivalent to

{\underline{e}}<{\underline{z_{i}}}\leq{\overline{z_{i}}}<{\overline{e}}
. By
Proposition
4.15
, both
{\underline{z_{i}}}
and

{\overline{z_{i}}}
are rational numbers. Hence, deciding

e\ll[{{\underline{z_{i}}},{\overline{z_{i}}}}]
reduces to comparing rational
numbers, which is decidable. Thus, overall, the problem

e\ll\maxV(f,X)
is semi-decidable.

\maxS
:

The proof for
\maxS
is similar to that for

\maxV
. Assume that
C\in B_{\mathbb{K}\mathbb{R}^{n}_{\bot}}
. To decide

C\ll\maxS(f,X)=K
, we run
Algorithm
1
. As
\twoheaduparrow{C}
is
Scott-open, then by completeness (Theorem
4.13
),
after sufficiently large number of iterations (say)
N_{f}
, we must
have
\forall i\geq N_{f}:C\ll\hat{B}_{i}
. By (
3
), the relation

C\ll\hat{B}_{i}
is equivalent to

\hat{B}_{i}\subseteq{C}^{\circ}
. As we have assumed that

C\in B_{\mathbb{K}\mathbb{R}^{n}_{\bot}}
and
X\in B_{\mathbb{K}\mathbb{R}^{n}_{\bot}}
,
then all the hyperboxes in
C
and
\hat{B}_{i}
have rational
coordinates. Therefore, deciding
C\ll\hat{B}_{i}
reduces to
comparing rational numbers, which is decidable. Thus, overall, the
problem
C\ll\maxS(f,X)
is semi-decidable.

∎

4.6
Interval Arithmetic

We will use arbitrary-precision interval arithmetic for approximation
of the
\hat{L}
-derivative. Interval
arithmetic
[
63
]
is performed over sets of numbers,
in contrast with the classical arithmetic which is performed over
individual numbers. This allows incorporating all the possible errors
(e. g., round-off and truncations errors) into the result. As an
example, note that:

(x\in[{\underline{I}},{\overline{I}}])\wedge(y\in[{\underline{J}},{\overline{J}}])\implies x+y\in[{\underline{I}}+{\underline{J}},{\overline{I}}+{\overline{J}}].

(28)

In practice, inaccuracies enter into interval computations for a
variety of reasons. The following are the most relevant to the current article:

4.6.1
Finite Representations

In arbitrary-precision interval arithmetic, every real number
x
is
represented as the limit of a sequence
(I_{i})_{i\in\mathbb{N}}
of intervals
with dyadic end-points, i. e.,
[x,x]=\bigcap_{i\in\mathbb{N}}I_{i}
. Assume
that we want to compute
\exp([1,1])=[\mathrm{e},\mathrm{e}]
, in which
\mathrm{e}
is
the Euler number. As
\mathrm{e}
is an irrational number, it does not have a
finite binary expansion. For instance, in
MPFI
[
68
]
—the arbitrary-precision
interval library that we use—when the precision is set at
10
, the
result of
\exp([1,1])
is given as
[2.718281828,2.718281829]
.

Furthermore, as the precision of the end-points of each interval must
be set first, inaccuracy is incurred even on simpler computations,
such as (
28
). For instance, if the bit size is set
at
n
, then the result of
[0,0]+[-2^{-2n},2^{-2n}]
must be rounded
outwards to
[-2^{-n},2^{-n}]
.

This source of inaccuracy is inevitable and, to a large extent,
harmless. The reason is that higher accuracy may be obtained by simply
increasing the bit size of the representation of the end-points.

4.6.2
Dependency

By default, interval computations do not take into account any
dependencies between parameters. For instance, assume that

x\in[0,1]
. By applying interval subtraction we obtain

(x-x)\in[-1,1]
, which is a significant overestimation as
x-x=0
,
regardless of which interval
x
belongs to.

Figure 2:
Dependency problem: The network computes the constant
function zero. Hence, we must have
N^{\prime}(x)=0
for all
x
. But the
Clarke-gradient at
x=0
is overestimated as
[-1,1]
.

The dependency problem causes inaccuracies in estimations of the
gradient as well. As an example, consider the neural network of
Figure
2
. This network computes the
constant function
N(x)=0
, for which we must have

\forall x\in\mathbb{R}:N^{\prime}(x)=0
. We estimate the Clarke-gradient of
N

using the chain rule, which, at
x=0
, leads to the following
overestimation:

\partial N(0)\subseteq\partial\ReLU(0)-\partial\ReLU(0)=[0,1]-[0,1]=[-1,1].

Our framework, however, is not affected by the dependency problem for
general position
\ReLU
networks (Theorem
4.19
) or
differentiable networks (Theorem
4.21
).

4.6.3
Wrapping Effect

Whenever a set which is not a hyperbox is overapproximated by a
bounding hyperbox, some accuracy is lost. This source of
inaccuracy in interval arithmetic is referred to as the wrapping
effect
[
65
]
. As stated in
Proposition
4.2
, the Clarke-gradient
is, in general, a convex set. Hence, by overapproximating the
Clarke-gradient with a hyperbox, some accuracy will be lost, as
exemplified in Example
4.8
.

Similar to the dependency problem, our framework is not affected by
the wrapping effect for general position
\ReLU
networks
(Theorem
4.19
) and differentiable networks
(Theorem
4.21
).

4.7
General Position
\boldsymbol{\ReLU}
Networks

We have pointed out the trade-off between accuracy and efficiency in
using the hyperbox domain
{\mathbb{I}}\mathbb{R}^{n}_{\bot}
, and further sources
of inaccuracy in interval arithmetic. In this section, we show that,
for a broad class of
\ReLU
networks, no accuracy is lost by using
hyperboxes. Assume that
N:\mathbb{R}^{n}\to\mathbb{R}
is a feedforward
\ReLU

network with
k\geq 0
hidden neurons (and an arbitrary number of
hidden layers). For convenience, we adopt the set-theoretic definition
of natural numbers
[
47
]
for which we have

k=\left\{{0,\ldots,k-1}\right\}
. For each
i\in k
, let

\hat{z}_{i}:\mathbb{R}^{n}\to\mathbb{R}
be the pre-activation function of the

i
-th hidden neuron and define:

H_{i}\coloneqq\{{x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}}\mid{\hat{z}_{i}(x)=0}\}.

(29)

The following
definition, which we take
from
[
49
]
, is an adaptation of
the concept of hyperplane arrangements in general
position
[
77
]
:

Definition 4.17
.

A
\ReLU
network
N:\mathbb{R}^{n}\to\mathbb{R}
with
k\geq 0
hidden neurons
is said to be in
general position
if, for every subset

S\subseteq k
, the intersection
\bigcap_{i\in S}H_{i}
is a
finite union of
(n-\lvert\thinspace{S}\thinspace\rvert)
-dimensional polytopes.

A
\ReLU
network
N:\mathbb{R}^{n}\to\mathbb{R}
with
k\geq 0
hidden neurons and
only one hidden layer is in general position if for every

S\subseteq k
, the intersection
\bigcap_{i\in S}H_{i}
is an

(n-\lvert\thinspace{S}\thinspace\rvert)
-dimensional subspace of
\mathbb{R}^{n}
. For instance, when

n=2
, the network is in general position if no two hyperplanes
H_{i}

and
H_{j}
(with
i\neq j
) are parallel and no three such hyperplanes
meet at a point.

Recall that

\tilde{L}(N):{\mathbb{I}}\mathbb{R}^{n}_{\bot}\to{\mathbb{I}}\mathbb{R}^{n}_{\bot}

denotes the interval approximation of the
\hat{L}
-derivative of
N

obtained by applying the chain rule of
Lemma
4.9
. In general, we have:

\forall x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}:\quad\partial N(x)\subseteq\hat{L}(N)(x)\subseteq\tilde{L}(N)(\left\{{x}\right\}).

(30)

Over general position networks, however, we obtain equality:

Lemma 4.18
.

Let
N:\mathbb{R}^{n}\to\mathbb{R}
be a general position
\ReLU

network. Then,
\forall x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}:\partial N(x)=\hat{L}(N)(x)=\tilde{L}(N)(\left\{{x}\right\})
.

Proof.

The fact that

\forall x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}:\partial N(x)=\tilde{L}(N)(\left\{{x}\right\})

follows
from
[
49
, Theorem 2]
. The
full claim now follows from (
30
).
∎

As a result, we have an effective procedure which, for any given

\ReLU
network
N:[-M,M]^{n}\to\mathbb{R}
in general position, and any

X\in B_{\mathbb{K}[-M,M]^{n}}
, returns the Lipschitz constant of
N

over
X
, and the set of points in
X
where the maximum Lipschitz
values are attained, to within any degree of accuracy:

Theorem 4.19
.

Let
N:[-M,M]^{n}\to\mathbb{R}
be a general position
\ReLU
network and

X\in B_{\mathbb{K}[-M,M]^{n}}
. Then:

\left\{\begin{array}[]{lcl}\maxV(\partial N,X)&=&\maxV(\tilde{L}(N),X),\\
\maxS(\partial N,X)&=&\maxS(\tilde{L}(N),X),\end{array}\right.

and both the maximum value and the maximum set can be obtained
effectively to any degree of accuracy.

Proof.

The result follows from
Lemma
4.18
,
Theorem
4.13
, and Theorem
4.16
.
∎

The network of Figure
2
is not in general
position, and we have shown that
Lemma
4.18
fails on that
network. With respect to the Lebesgue measure over the parameter
space, however, almost every
\ReLU
network is in general
position
[
49
, Theorem 4]
. In
simple terms,
we have a sound and effective procedure for
Lipschitz analysis of networks, which provides the results to within
any given degree of accuracy on almost every
\ReLU
network
.

4.8
Differentiable Networks

One of the main strengths of our domain-theoretic framework is that it
can handle differentiable and non-differentiable networks alike
seamlessly. For differentiable networks as well, we obtain results
similar to those for
\ReLU
networks in general position.

We know that when
N
is classically differentiable at

x\in[-M,M]^{n}
, the
L
-derivative and the classical derivative
coincide
[
25
]
. Furthermore, for
classically differentiable functions, the chain rules of
Proposition
4.3
and
Lemma
4.9
reduce to equalities which incur no
overapproximation, because both sides are single-valued. As a result,
we obtain:

Lemma 4.20
.

Let
N:[-M,M]^{n}\to\mathbb{R}
be a differentiable feedforward network in

{\cal F}
. Then:

\forall x\in[-M,M]^{n}:\quad\partial N(x)=\hat{L}(N)(x)=\tilde{L}(N)(\left\{{x}\right\}).

In other words,
\hat{L}(N)
is an interval extension of

\partial N
.

Theorem 4.21
.

Let
N:[-M,M]^{n}\to\mathbb{R}
be a differentiable feedforward network in

{\cal F}
. Then:

\left\{\begin{array}[]{lcl}\maxV(\partial N,X)&=&\maxV(\tilde{L}(N),X),\\
\maxS(\partial N,X)&=&\maxS(\tilde{L}(N),X),\end{array}\right.

and both the maximum value and the maximum set can be obtained
effectively to any degree of accuracy.

For a network with input dimension

5.2
Scalability: depth and width

As we have mentioned previously, validated methods of Lipschitz
estimation (such as lipDT) cannot be scalable. Nonetheless, such
methods are valuable for purposes such as:

(i)

verficiation of other Lipschitz estimation methods, as
demonstrated in Table
1
;

(ii)

robustness analysis of low-dimensional networks, especially
those that appear in safety-critical applications, e. g., in power
electronics
[
19
]
,
analog-to-digital converters
[
81
]
, and
solution of partial differential equations
[
69
]
;

(iii)

investigation of how
Lipschitz constant is affected by factors such as
under-parametrization, over-parametrization, and through successive
epochs of a training process.

Item
(iii)
is particularly relevant for
networks with low input dimensions, but with depths and widths that
can be very large relative to their input dimensions. The reasons is
that, although lipDT does not scale well with input dimension of the
network, it has a much better scalability with respect to the depth
and width of the network.

To investigate how lipDT scales with the number of layers (i. e.,
depth) of the network, we generated some multilayer networks randomly
with 4 input neurons, 2 output neurons, and various number of hidden
layers (from 1 to 10), each containing 10 neurons. We took the input
domain with center
[1,1,1,1]
and radius
0.001
, and compared the
timing of lipDT with the other methods, except for
Clever
, which
takes significantly longer than all the other methods. As can be seen
in Figure
5
, lipDT scales quite well—almost
linearly—with the number of layers of the network.

width of the network. We ran an experiment in which we considered

where
n

1
to
10
. The weights and biases were
assigned randomly, and to make the comparison fair, we performed the

only contributing factor is the width. We recorded the time, and the
result is shown in Figure

.

5.3
Case study: benign overfitting

The traditional consensus in machine learning postulated that
over-parametrized models would have poor generalization
properties. For instance, in a regression task over a dataset of size

m
, if the capacity
K
of the model were significantly larger than

m
, then the model would interpolate the dataset, and would oscillate
erratically over points outside of the dataset. In a fairly recent
development, it has been observed that over-parametrized models can
indeed exhibit superior generalization compared with
under-parametrized ones, even over noisy
data
[
64
]
. An
extensive account of this phenomenon—termed
‘benign
overfitting’

[
5
]
—can
be found in
[
6
]
.

In this section, we investigate benign overfitting from the angle of
Lipschitz analysis. To that end, we consider a univariate regression
task similar to that depicted
in
[
6

. The dataset
consists of eleven points over the interval
[-5,5]
. We train a set
of
\ReLU
networks with one input neuron, one output neuron, and a
hidden layer with width ranging from
3
to
3000
(in steps of

3
). Even though lipDT scales well with respect to the depth of the
network, for this experiment, we consider only networks with one
hidden layer, and take the width of the single hidden layer as the
capacity of the network.

Each network is trained for
100\,000
epochs. Then, the Lipschitz
constant is calculated over the interval
[-5,5]
using lipDT. The
result is shown in Figure

5.3.1
Validated versus non-validated Lipschitz analysis

We ran our experiments using the validated method lipDT, and the
non-validated statistical method Cross Lipschitz Extreme Value for nEtwork Robustness. The Lipschitz estimates
returned by Cross Lipschitz Extreme Value for nEtwork Robustness are almost always a slight under-estimate of
those returned by lipDT. To be more precise, the average relative
error of Cross Lipschitz Extreme Value for nEtwork Robustness is around
-1\%
, calculated according
to (
31
). In most cases, qualitatively, the results of
Cross Lipschitz Extreme Value for nEtwork Robustness and lipDT are similar. Some outliers, however, were
observed. For instance,
Figure
10
(Left) shows the graph of
the result returned by Cross Lipschitz Extreme Value for nEtwork Robustness when measuring the Lipschitz
constant of the
\ReLU

3000
neurons, up to epoch

, Cross Lipschitz Extreme Value for nEtwork Robustness estimated the Lipschitz constant
to be around

, while for all the other epochs, the Lipschitz
constant was estimated below

Without recourse to a validated method, it is not possible to
determine whether the Lipschitz constant truly has a jump at epoch

methods are randomized, which makes it possible to obtain genuine

with lipDT reveals that, at epoch

the wrong result. The interval returned by lipDT at epoch

[2.445572934050687,2.445572934050933].

6
Concluding remarks

Continuous domains are powerful models of computation and provide a
semantic model for the concept of approximation. We have demonstrated
this claim by presenting a framework for robustness analysis of neural
networks. The main focus in the current article has been laying the
theoretical foundation and obtaining fundamental results on soundness,
completeness, and computability. We also demonstrated how
straightforward it is to translate our algorithms into fully validated
implementations using interval arithmetic. In particular, we devised
an algorithm for validated computation of Lipschitz constants of
feedforward networks, which is sound and complete. Based on the
foundation laid in this paper, our next step will be extending the
framework to recurrent neural networks.

The maximization algorithm of Algorithm
1
has an
imperative style, which is in line with the common practice in machine
learning. Continuous domains, however, are best known as models of
lambda calculus and it is common to adopt a functional style in
language design for real number
computation
[
33
,
34
,
17
]
. Thus,
another direction for future work is the design of a functional
algorithm for maximization which suits our non-algebraic domain
framework.
