Approximate CVP \mathrm{CVP} in time 2 0.802 ​ n 2^{0.802\,n} - now in any norm!
Thomas Rothvoss, Moritz Venzin

Abstract
We show that a constant factor approximation of the shortest and closest lattice vector problem in any norm can be computed in time 2 0.802 ​ n 2^{0.802\,n} . This contrasts the corresponding 2 n 2^{n} time, (gap)-SETH based lower bounds for these problems that even apply for small constant approximation. For both problems, SVP \mathrm{SVP} and CVP \mathrm{CVP} , we reduce to the case of the Euclidean norm. A key technical ingredient in that reduction is a twist of Milman’s construction of an M M -ellipsoid which approximates any symmetric convex body K K with an ellipsoid ℰ \mathcal{E} so that 2 ε ​ n 2^{\varepsilon n} translates of a constant scaling of ℰ \mathcal{E} can cover K K and vice versa.

Introduction

Figure 1:
The shortest and the closest vector problem.

For some basis
B\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times n}
, the
d
dimensional lattice
\mathscr{L}
of rank
n
is a discrete subgroup of
\mathbb{R}^{d}
given by

\mathscr{L}(B)=\{Bx\;:\;x\in\mathbb{Z}^{n}\}.

In this work, we will consider the
shortest vector problem
(
\mathrm{SVP}
) and the
closest vector problem
(
\mathrm{CVP}
), the two most important computational problems on lattices. Given some lattice, the shortest vector problem is to compute a shortest
non-zero
lattice vector. When in addition some target
t\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
is given, the closest vector problem is to compute a lattice vector closest to
t
.

Here, ”short” and ”close” are defined in terms of a given norm
\|\cdot\|_{K}
, induced by some symmetric convex body
K\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{d}
with
\bm{0}
in its interior. Specifically,
\|x\|_{K}=\min\{s\in\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}\mid x\in s\cdot K\}
. When we care to specify what norm we are working with, we denote these problems by
\mathrm{SVP}_{K}
and
\mathrm{CVP}_{K}
respectively and by
\mathrm{SVP}_{p}
and
\mathrm{CVP}_{p}
respectively for the important case of
\ell_{p}
norms. It is important to note that the dimension, rank and span of the lattice have a considerable effect on the norm
\|\cdot\|_{K}
that is
induced
on
\mathscr{L}
. For the shortest vector problem, the norm induced on
\mathscr{L}
corresponds to
K

intersected
with the span of
\mathscr{L}
, see Figure
1
for an illustration. For different
n
and different rotations of the lattice, these resulting convex bodies vary considerably. However, when the convex body
K
is centered in the span of the lattice, these sections of
K
still satisfy the required properties to define a norm. In particular, up to changing the norm, any (
\alpha
-approximation of the) shortest vector problem in dimension
d\geq n
can be directly reduced to (
\alpha
-approximate)
\mathrm{SVP}
with
d=n
. The situation is slightly more delicate for
\mathrm{CVP}
. Whenever
t\notin\text{span}(\mathscr{L})
, the function measuring the distance to
t
, i.e.
\|t-\,\cdot\,\|_{K}
, can be asymmetrical on
\text{span}(\mathscr{L})
, meaning it does not define a norm on
\text{span}(\mathscr{L})
. This can be seen by lifting

and the cross-polytope with it in Figure
1
. However, up to a loss in the approximation guarantee, we can always take the target to lie in
\text{span}(\mathscr{L})
and consider the norm induced by
K
intersected with
\text{span}(\mathscr{L})
. More precisely, we can always reduce
(2\cdot\alpha+1)
-approximate
\mathrm{CVP}
to
\alpha
-approximate
\mathrm{CVP}
in any norm with
d=n
. This loss in the approximation factor is not surprising, seeing that exact
\mathrm{CVP}
under general norms is extremely versatile. In fact,
Integer Programming
with
n

\mathrm{CVP}_{\infty}
on a
m
-dimensional lattice of rank
n
.

Both

Theorem

For any lattice

For the case of the shortest vector problem, we can significantly generalize this result.

Theorem
.

For any lattice
\mathscr{L}\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{d}
of rank
n
, any norm
\|\cdot\|_{K}
on
\mathbb{R}^{d}
and for each
\varepsilon>0
, there exists a constant
\gamma_{\varepsilon}
such that there is a

time, randomized reduction from

-approximate
\mathrm{SVP}_{K}
to an oracle for
\alpha
-approximate
\mathrm{CVP}_{2}

\mathrm{CVP}

Our main idea is to cover
K
by a special class of ellipsoids to obtain the approximate closest vector by using an approximate closest vector algorithm with respect to
\ell_{2}
. This covering idea draws from
[

]
and is also similar to the approach of Dadush et al. in

]

, one can compute some ellipsoid

of

, so that

can be covered by

translates of
\mathcal{E}

can be covered by
2^{\varepsilon n}
translates of

. Here,

is a constant that only depends on

\mathrm{SVP}_{K}
to

calls to an oracle for (approximate)
\mathrm{CVP}_{2}
. Specifically, using an oracle for
\alpha
-
\mathrm{CVP}_{2}
, we will obtain a
\alpha\cdot(2\cdot c_{\varepsilon})
approximation to the shortest vector problem with respect to
\|\cdot\|_{K}

\mathrm{CVP}_{Q}

K

This covering idea can be used to solve constant factor approximate
\mathrm{CVP}_{K}

\mathrm{SVP}_{2}
that was first exploited in
[

]

In this section, we describe our main geometric observation and how it leads to an algorithm for the approximate shortest and closest vector problem respectively. In a first part, we state the main geometric theorem and informally present how it leads to a reduction from approximate
\mathrm{SVP}_{K}
to an oracle for approximate
\mathrm{CVP}_{2}
. In the second part, we make this formal using lattice sparsification and some further geometric considerations. Finally, in a third part, we show how to replace the oracle for approximate
\mathrm{CVP}_{2}
by an oracle for
\mathrm{CVP}
under any norm.

2.1
Covering
K
with few ellipsoids and vice versa

The (approximate) shortest vector problem in the norm
\|\cdot\|_{K}
can be rephrased as follows.

Does
K
contain a lattice point different from
\bm{0}
?

This follows by guessing the length
\ell
of the shortest non-zero lattice vector, scaling the lattice by
1/\ell
and then confirm the right guess by finding this lattice vector in
K
. By guessing we mean to try out all possibilities for
\ell
, this can be limited to a polynomial in the relevant parameters.

Imagine now that one can cover
K
using a collection of (Euclidean) balls
\mathcal{B}_{i}
of any radii such that

K\subseteq\bigcup_{i=1}^{N}(x_{i}+\mathcal{B}_{i})\subseteq c\cdot K.

To then find a
c
-approximation to the shortest vector, one could use a solver for
\mathrm{CVP}_{2}
using targets
x_{1}\cdots,x_{N}
. However, this naïve approach is already doomed for
K=B_{\infty}^{n}
. To achieve a constant factor approximation,
N=n^{O(n)}
translates of (any scaling of) the Euclidean ball
B_{2}^{n}:=\{x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}\mid\|x\|_{2}\leq 1\}
are required and can only be brought down to
N=2^{O(n)}
if one is willing to settle for
c=O(\sqrt{n})

[
Joh48
]
.

However, if we impose a second condition on these balls (and even relax the above condition), this idea will work. Specifically, if we can cover
K=-K\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{n}
by
N
translates of
c_{1}\cdot B_{2}^{n}

and

B_{2}^{n}
by
N
translates of
c_{2}\cdot K
,

K\subseteq\bigcup_{i=1}^{N}(x_{i}+c_{1}\cdot B_{2}^{n})\quad\text{and}\quad B_{2}^{n}\subseteq\bigcup_{i=1}^{N}(y_{i}+c_{2}\cdot K),

we can solve
\alpha\cdot(c_{1}\cdot c_{2})
-approximate
\mathrm{SVP}_{K}
using (essentially)
N
calls
to a solver for
\alpha
-
\mathrm{CVP}_{2}
. It turns out that such a covering is always possible for any symmetric and convex
K
, and, in particular, the number of translates
N
can be made smaller than
2^{\varepsilon n}
for any
\varepsilon>0
. To be precise, the covering is by ellipsoids (affine transformations of balls), but we can always apply a linear transformation to restrict to Euclidean unit norm-balls, i.e. we may take
c_{1}=1
.
The precise properties of the covering are now stated in the following theorem, where we denote by
N(T,L)
the
translative covering number
of
T
by
L
, i.e. the least number of translates of
L
required to cover
T
.

Theorem 2.1
.

For any symmetric and convex body
K\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{n}
and for any
\varepsilon>0
, there exists an (invertible) linear transformation
T_{\varepsilon}:\mathbb{R}^{n}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^{n}
and a constant
c_{\varepsilon}\in\mathbb{R}_{>0}
such that

1.

N(T_{\varepsilon}(K),B_{2}^{n})\leq 2^{\varepsilon n}
(even
\vol(T_{\varepsilon}(K)+B_{2}^{n})\leq 2^{\varepsilon n}\cdot\vol(B_{2}^{n})
) and

2.

N(B_{2}^{n},c_{\varepsilon}\cdot T_{\varepsilon}(K))\leq 2^{\varepsilon n}
(even
\vol(B_{2}^{n}+c_{\varepsilon}\cdot T_{\varepsilon}(K))\leq 2^{\varepsilon n}\cdot\vol(c_{\varepsilon}\cdot T_{\varepsilon}(K)))
.

The linear transformation
T_{\varepsilon}
can be computed in (randomized)
n^{O(\log(n))}
time (times some polynomial in the encoding length of
K
).

The volume estimate
\text{Vol}(T_{\varepsilon}(K)+B_{2}^{n})\leq 2^{\varepsilon n}\cdot\text{Vol}(B_{2}^{n})
in
(\ref{covering:1})
is stronger than
N(T_{\varepsilon}(K),B_{2}^{n})\leq 2^{\varepsilon n}
, similar in (
2
). It makes the covering of
T_{\varepsilon}(K)
by translates of
B_{2}^{n}
constructive.
Indeed, any point inside
T_{\varepsilon}(K)
will be covered with probability at least
2^{-\varepsilon n}
if we sample a random point within
T_{\varepsilon}(K)+B_{2}^{n}
and place a copy of
B_{2}^{n}
around it. Repeating this for
O(n\cdot\log(n)\cdot 2^{\varepsilon n})
iterations yields, with high probability, a full covering of
T_{\varepsilon}(K)
by translates of
B_{2}^{n}
. See
[
Nas14
]
for details.

We defer the proof of Theorem
2.1
to Section
4
and first discuss how we intend to use it to solve the shortest vector problem in arbitrary norms. To do so, we first fix some notations and do some simplifications. We will denote by
\mathbf{s}
a shortest, non-zero lattice vector of the given lattice
\mathscr{L}\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{n}
with respect to
\|\cdot\|_{K}
, the norm under consideration. We will assume that
1-1/n\leq\|\mathbf{s}\|_{K}\leq 1
, i.e.
\mathbf{s}\in K\setminus(1-1/n)\cdot K
. We fix
\varepsilon>0
, and denote by
T_{\varepsilon}
be the linear transformation that is guaranteed by Theorem
2.1
. Up to replacing
\mathscr{L}
by
T_{\varepsilon}^{-1}(\mathscr{L})
and
K
by
T_{\varepsilon}^{-1}(K)
, we can also assume that
T_{\varepsilon}=\text{Id}
(
\|\cdot\|_{K}=\|T_{\varepsilon}^{-1}(\cdot)\|_{T_{\varepsilon}^{-1}(K)}
).

We can now describe how we will use the covering guaranteed by Theorem
2.1
. Since
K
is covered by translates of
B_{2}^{n}
, there is some translate that holds
\mathbf{s}
. We denote it by
t+B_{2}^{n}
.
Now, suppose there is a procedure that either returns
\mathbf{s}
or generates at least
2^{\varepsilon n}+1

distinct
lattice vectors lying in
t+\alpha\cdot B_{2}^{n}
. For the latter case, while these vectors may all have very large norm with respect to
\|\cdot\|_{K}
or may even equal the zero vector, by taking pairwise differences, we are still able to find a
\alpha\cdot(2\cdot c_{\varepsilon})
-approximation to the shortest vector. Indeed, by property (
2
) of Theorem
2.1
,
t+\alpha\cdot B_{2}^{n}
can be covered by fewer than
2^{\varepsilon n}
translates of
(\alpha\cdot c_{\varepsilon})\cdot K
. Thus, one translate of
(\alpha\cdot c_{\varepsilon})\cdot K
must hold
two distinct
lattice vectors. Their pairwise difference is then a
\alpha\cdot((1-1/n)^{-1}\cdot 2\cdot c_{\varepsilon})
-approximation to the shortest vector
\mathbf{s}
. This is depicted in Figure
2
.

Figure 2:
The covering of
K
by translates of the Euclidean norm ball. One translate of
(\alpha\cdot c_{\varepsilon})\cdot K
covering
t+\alpha\cdot B_{2}^{n}
holds two lattice vectors
u
and
v
. Their difference
u-v
is a
(\alpha\cdot\gamma_{\varepsilon})
-approximation to the shortest vector problem.

To finish the argument, it remains to argue that such a procedure can be simulated with an oracle for
\alpha
-approximate
\mathrm{CVP}_{2}
at hand. This will be achieved through the use of
lattice sparsification
. This technique will allow us to delete lattice points in an almost uniform manner. Specifically, when the oracle has already returned lattice vectors
v_{1},\cdots,v_{N}
, sparsifying the lattice ensures that with sufficiently high probability,
\mathbf{s}
is retained and
v_{1},\cdots,v_{N}
are deleted. This then
forces
the
\alpha
-approximate
\mathrm{CVP}_{2}
oracle to return a lattice vector
v_{N+1}
distinct from
v_{1},\cdots,v_{N}
with
\|t-v_{N+1}\|_{K}\leq\alpha\cdot\|t-\mathbf{s}\|_{K}
.

2.2
Approximate
\mathrm{SVP}_{K}
using an oracle for approximate
\mathrm{CVP}_{2}

In this subsection we are going to formalize the exponential time reduction from approximate
\mathrm{SVP}
to an oracle for (approximate)
\mathrm{CVP}_{2}
as outlined in the previous section. We are going to make use of the following theorem from
[
Ste16
]
, slightly rephrased for our purpose.

Theorem 2.2
.

For any prime
p
, any lattice
\mathscr{L}\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{d}
of rank
n
and lattice vectors
w,v_{1},v_{2},\cdots,v_{N}\in\mathscr{L}
with
v_{i}\notin w+p\cdot\mathscr{L}\setminus\{\bm{0}\}
, one can, in polynomial time, sample a shifted sub-lattice
u+\mathscr{L}^{\prime}
with
\mathscr{L}^{\prime}\subseteq\mathscr{L}
and
u\in\mathscr{L}
such that:

\Pr\big[w\in u+\mathscr{L}^{\prime}\text{ and }v_{1},\cdots,v_{N}\notin u+\mathscr{L}^{\prime}\big]\geq\frac{1}{p}-\frac{N}{p^{2}}-\frac{N}{p^{n}}.

The condition
v_{i}\notin w+p\cdot\mathscr{L}\setminus\{\bm{0}\}
is slightly inconvenient. We will deal with these type of vectors by showing that, for large enough
p
, they will be too large and will not be considered by our
\alpha
-approximate
\mathrm{CVP}_{2}
oracle. This is done by the following lemma.

Lemma 2.3
.

Let
\mathscr{L}
be a lattice, let
K
be a symmetric and convex body containing no lattice vector other than
\bm{0}
in its interior and suppose that
N(K,B_{2}^{n}),N(B_{2}^{n},\beta\cdot K)\leq 2^{\varepsilon n}
. Then, the following three properties hold:

1.

\forall v\in p\cdot\mathscr{L}\setminus\{\bm{0}\}:

\|v\|_{2}\geq(2^{-\varepsilon n}/\beta)\cdot p
.

2.

K\subseteq 2^{\varepsilon n}\cdot B_{2}^{n}

3.

(2^{-\varepsilon n}/\beta)\cdot B_{2}^{n}\subseteq K

Proof.

We first show the last two properties by using the translative covering numbers. Since
N(K,B_{2}^{n})\\
\leq 2^{\varepsilon n}
, we must have
K\subseteq 2^{\varepsilon n}\cdot B_{2}^{n}
. To see this, we note that otherwise, the largest segment contained in
K
cannot be covered using
2^{\varepsilon n}
translates of
B_{2}^{n}
. Conversely, since
N(B_{2}^{n},\beta\cdot K)\leq 2^{\varepsilon n}
and the convexity of
K=-K
, the smallest inradius of
K
cannot be smaller than
2^{-\varepsilon n}/\beta
. It follows that
(2^{-\varepsilon n}/\beta)\cdot B_{2}^{n}\subseteq K
.

We can now show the first property. Let
v\in p\cdot\mathscr{L}\setminus\{\bm{0}\}
. By assumption
\frac{v}{p}\notin\text{int}(K)
and so
\frac{v}{p}\notin(2^{-\varepsilon n}/\beta)\cdot\textrm{int}(B_{2}^{n})
, where
\textrm{int}(K)
denotes the interior of the body
K
. This means that
\|v\|_{2}\geq(2^{-\varepsilon n}/\beta)\cdot p
.
∎

We now state our randomized reduction. We rotate the lattice and
K
so that
\text{span}(\mathscr{L})=\mathbb{R}^{n}\times\{0\}^{d-n}
. Up to replacing
K
by
K\cap\text{span}(\mathscr{L})
and deleting the last

zeros, we may assume that

. For details, we refer to the second part of the proof of Lemma
3.1
. We fix some
\varepsilon>0
and compute the invertible linear transformation
T_{\varepsilon}
guaranteed by Theorem
2.1
. Up to applying the inverse of
T_{\varepsilon}
and scaling the lattice, we may assume that
T_{\varepsilon}=\text{Id}
and
1-1/n\leq\|\mathbf{s}\|_{K}\leq 1
, where
\mathbf{s}
is a shortest lattice vector with respect to the norm
\|\cdot\|_{K}
. One iteration of the reduction will consist of the following steps and will succeed with probability
2^{-O(\varepsilon)n}
.

(1)

Fix a prime number
p
between
2^{3\varepsilon n}
and
2\cdot 2^{3\varepsilon n}
.

(2)

Sample a random point

.

(3)

Using the number
p
, sparsify the lattice as in Theorem
2.2
. Denote the resulting lattice by

.

(4)

Run the oracle for
\alpha
-approximate
\mathrm{CVP}_{2}
for
\mathscr{L}^{\prime}
with target

, add
u
to the vector returned and store it.

(5)

Repeat steps (3) and (4)
n^{2}\cdot 2^{5\varepsilon n}
times. Among the resulting lattice vectors and their pairwise differences, output the shortest (non-zero) with respect to
\|\cdot\|_{K}
.

Theorem 2.4
.

Let
\mathscr{L}\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{d}
be any lattice of rank
n
. For any
\varepsilon>0
, there is a constant
\gamma_{\varepsilon}
, such that there is a randomized,
2^{\varepsilon n}
time reduction from
(\alpha\cdot\gamma_{\varepsilon})
-approximate
\mathrm{SVP}_{K}
for
\mathscr{L}
to an oracle for
\alpha
-approximate
\mathrm{CVP}_{2}
for
n
-dimensional lattices.

Proof.

Let us already condition on the event that the sampled point
t
verifies
\mathbf{s}\in t+B_{2}^{n}
. This happens with probability at least
2^{-\varepsilon n}
by property (
1
) of Theorem
2.1
. This probability can be boosted to
1-2^{-n}
by repeating steps (2) to (5)
O(n\cdot 2^{\varepsilon n})
times and outputting the shortest non-zero vector with respect to
\|\cdot\|_{K}
.

We are now going to show that, with high probability,
\mathbf{s}
is going to be retained in the shifted sub-lattice and the lattice vector that is returned in that iteration is different from the ones returned from a previous iteration (where also
\mathbf{s}
was retained).
To this end, denote by
L:=\{v_{1},\cdots,v_{j}\}
the (possibly empty) list of lattice vectors that were obtained in an iteration where
\mathbf{s}
belonged in the corresponding (shifted) sub-lattice. This implies that
\|t-v_{i}\|_{2}\leq\alpha\cdot\|t-\mathbf{s}\|_{2}

\|v_{i}\|_{2}\leq(2\cdot\alpha)\cdot 2^{\varepsilon n}
for all

. On the other hand, by slightly rescaling

in Lemma

, the triangle inequality and our choice of

, any vector in

is larger than
2^{2\varepsilon n}/c_{\varepsilon}
in the Euclidean norm. It follows that

v_{1},\cdots,v_{j}\notin\mathbf{s}+p\cdot\mathscr{L}

(We are assuming that
\alpha=2^{o(n)}
, this is without loss of generality. For
\alpha=2^{n\log\log(n)/\log(n)}
, Babai’s algorithm runs in polynomial time.)

Thus, provided
j\leq 2^{\varepsilon n}
and by Theorem
2.2
, in any iteration of the algorithm and with probability at least
2^{-3\varepsilon n}/2
, we add a lattice vector to the list
L
that is either distinct from all other vectors in

or that equals
\mathbf{s}
. In other words, the number of distinct lattice vectors in our list follows a binomial distribution with parameter
2^{-3\varepsilon n}/2
.
Since we repeat this
n^{2}\cdot 2^{5\varepsilon n}
times, by Chernov’s inequality and with probability at least
1-2^{-n}
, after the final iteration the list

contains at least
2^{\varepsilon n}+1
distinct lattice vectors lying within
t+\alpha\cdot B_{2}^{n}
or contains
\mathbf{s}
. In the latter case we are done. In the former case, since
N(\alpha\cdot B_{2}^{n},(\alpha\cdot c_{\varepsilon})\cdot K)\leq 2^{\varepsilon n}
, by checking all
2^{10\varepsilon n}
pairwise differences, we will find a non-zero lattice vector with
\|\cdot\|_{K}
-norm at most
\alpha\cdot(2\cdot c_{\varepsilon})
. Since
\|\mathbf{s}\|_{K}\geq 1-1/n
and setting
\gamma_{\varepsilon}:=(1-1/n)^{-1}\cdot 2\cdot c_{\varepsilon}
, this vector is a
(\alpha\cdot\gamma_{\varepsilon})
-approximation to
\mathbf{s}
.
∎

Remark 2.5

As described, this reduction requires

The main geometric idea as outlined in the previous subsection leading to Theorem

The same covering idea as depicted in Figure

(with

Let

3
Approximate
\mathrm{CVP}
in time

In this section we are going to describe a

d
-dimensional lattice
\mathscr{L}
of rank
n
, target

.

To achieve this, we will adapt the geometric ideas as outlined in the previous subsection to the setting of the closest vector problem. To do so, we are first going to describe how to restrict to the full-dimensional case.

Lemma 3.1
.

Consider an instance of the closest vector problem,
\mathrm{CVP}_{K}(\mathscr{L},t)
,
\mathscr{L}\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{d}
of rank
n
. In polynomial time, we can find a lattice
\tilde{\mathscr{L}}\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{n}
of dimension
n
, target
\tilde{t}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
and norm
\|\cdot\|_{\tilde{K}}
so that an
\alpha
-approximation to
\mathrm{CVP}_{\tilde{K}}
on
\tilde{\mathscr{L}}
with target
\tilde{t}
can be efficiently transformed in a
(2\alpha+1)
-approximation to
\mathrm{CVP}_{K}(\mathscr{L},t)
. Whenever
t\in\text{span}(\mathscr{L})
, the latter is a
\alpha
-approximation to
\mathrm{CVP}_{K}(\mathscr{L},t)
.

Proof.

Let us define
t^{\prime}:=\text{argmin}\{\|t-x\|_{K},\,x\in\text{span}(\mathscr{L})\}
. Such a point may not be unique, for instance for
K=B_{1}^{n}
or
K=B_{\infty}^{n}
, but it suffices to consider any point
t^{\prime}
realizing this minimum or an approximation thereof. Given a (weak) separation oracle for
K
, this can be computed in polynomial time. We now show that an
\alpha
-approximation to
\mathrm{CVP}_{K}(\mathscr{L},t^{\prime})
yields a
(2\cdot\alpha+1)
approximation to
\mathrm{CVP}_{K}(\mathscr{L},t)
. Indeed, since
\|t-t^{\prime}\|_{K}
is smaller than
\dist_{K}(t,\mathscr{L})
, the distance of
t
to the closest lattice vector, we have that

\dist_{K}(t^{\prime},\mathscr{L})\leq 2\cdot\dist_{K}(t,\mathscr{L}).

Denote by
c_{\alpha}\in\mathscr{L}
an
\alpha
-approximation to the closest lattice vector to
t^{\prime}
. By the triangle inequality,

\|t-c_{\alpha}\|_{K}\leq\|t-t^{\prime}\|_{K}+\|t^{\prime}-c_{\alpha}\|_{K}\leq\dist(t,\mathscr{L})+\alpha\cdot\dist(t^{\prime},\mathscr{L})\leq(2\cdot\alpha+1)\cdot\dist(t,\mathscr{L}).

This means that an
\alpha
-approximation to the closest vector to
t^{\prime}
is a
(2\cdot\alpha+1)
approximation to the closest vector to
t
. Now that
t^{\prime}\in\text{span}(\mathscr{L})
, we can restrict to the case
d=n
.

Let
\mathcal{O}_{n}
be a linear transformation that first applies a rotation sending
\text{span}(\mathscr{L})
to
\mathbb{R}^{n}\times\{0\}^{d-n}
and then restricts onto its first
n
coordinates. The transformation
\mathcal{O}_{n}:\text{span}(\mathscr{L})\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^{n}
is invertible. The
n
-dimensional instance of the closest vector problem is then obtained by setting
\tilde{\mathscr{L}}\leftarrow\mathcal{O}_{n}(\mathscr{L})
,
\tilde{t}\leftarrow\mathcal{O}_{n}(t^{\prime})
and
\|\cdot\|_{\tilde{K}}
where
\tilde{K}\leftarrow\mathcal{O}_{n}(K)
. Whenever

is an
\alpha
-approximation to
\mathrm{CVP}_{\tilde{K}}(\tilde{\mathscr{L}},\tilde{t})
,

\mathcal{O}_{n}^{-1}(c_{\alpha})
is a
(2\cdot\alpha+1)
-approximation to
\mathrm{CVP}_{K}(\mathscr{L},t)
(or an
\alpha
-approximation to
\mathrm{CVP}_{K}(\mathscr{L},t)
, if
t\in\text{span}(\mathscr{L})
).
∎

Theorem 3.2
.

Given
\varepsilon>0
,
R>0
,
N\in\mathbb{N}
and a lattice

of rank

, where the distribution
\mathcal{D}
satisfies the following two properties:

1.

Every sample

has

and
\|v\|_{2}\leq a_{\varepsilon}\cdot R
, where
a_{\varepsilon}
is a constant only depending on
\varepsilon
.

2.

For any
\mathbf{s}\in\mathscr{L}
with

\mathcal{D}^{\mathbf{s}}_{0}
and
\mathcal{D}^{\mathbf{s}}_{1}

(b)

With probability

.

This procedure takes time
2^{(0.802+\varepsilon)n}+N\cdot 2^{(0.401+\varepsilon)n}
and requires

space and succeeds with probability at least

.

With this randomized procedure, we obtain our main result.

Theorem 3.3

For any
\varepsilon>0

Proof.

Using the reduction given by Lemma

We now sample a uniformly random point

4
Constructing the ellipsoid

We now discuss Theorem
2.1
. Specifically, for a given symmetric convex body
K\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{n}
and any fixed
\varepsilon>0
, we are going to outline the construction of a linear transformation
T_{\varepsilon}
such that

1.

\text{Vol}(T_{\varepsilon}(K)+B_{2}^{n})\leq 2^{\varepsilon n}\cdot\text{Vol}(B_{2}^{n})
and

2.

\vol(B_{2}^{n}+c_{\varepsilon}\cdot T_{\varepsilon}(K))\leq 2^{\varepsilon n}\cdot\vol(c_{\varepsilon}\cdot T_{\varepsilon}(K))
.

The number
c_{\varepsilon}
only depends on
\varepsilon
and equals
C^{2}\cdot\varepsilon^{-4}
. Here,
C
is an universal constant.

This construction is based on a procedure called
isomorphic symmetrization
. It is taken almost verbatim from the proof of existence of
M
-ellipsoids due to Milman
[
Mil88
]
, see also the wonderful textbook of
[
AGM15
]
. In particular, this technique has been made algorithmic by Dadush and Vempala
[
DV13
]
to obtain (deterministic) algorithms for volume computation. Our contribution is thus largely the (simple) observation that this procedure can be stopped earlier to yield the desired properties. For this reason, we prefer to keep this part rather informal and only sketch the relevant techniques
2
2

2

For the sake of completeness we should point out that the mere existence of such a linear transformation has been known. Pisier
[
Pis89
]
proved the existence of a
regular

M
-ellipsoid. More precisely, he proved that for any constant
0\leq p<2
and any symmetric convex body
K\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{n}
, there exists an ellipsoid
\mathcal{E}\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{n}
so that

N(K,t\mathcal{E}),N(\mathcal{E},tK),N(K^{\circ},t\mathcal{E}^{\circ}),N(\mathcal{E}^{\circ},tK^{\circ})\leq\exp\big(A_{p}n/t^{p}\big)
for all
t\geq 1
. The implication is that this ellipsoid
\mathcal{E}
approximates
K
for the
whole
range of
t
while our
modification of the isomorphic symmetrization gives an ellipsoid
\mathcal{E}
that works for a
particular
value of
t
. However, it is less clear how to make Pisier’s complex-analytic argument constructive.
.

We first introduce the
polar
(or
dual
)
K^{\circ}
of
K
. Given
K\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{n}
, we define

\displaystyle K^{\circ}:=\big\{x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}\mid x^{T}y\leq 1,\,\forall y\in K\big\}.

Whenever
K=-K\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{n}
is full dimensional and with
\bm{0}
in its interior, so is
K^{\circ}
. Note that applying a linear invertible transformation
A
to
K
transforms
K^{\circ}
by
A^{-1}
, i.e.
(A\cdot K)^{\circ}=A^{-1}\cdot K^{\circ}
. We can now define the
M-values

M(K)
and
M(K^{\circ})
of
K
and
K^{\circ}
respectively.

M(K)=\mathbb{E}_{x\sim\mathbb{S}^{n-1}}[\|x\|_{K}]\,\,\text{ and }\,\,M(K^{\circ})=\mathbb{E}_{x\sim\mathbb{S}^{n-1}}[\|x\|_{K^{\circ}}].

Here
\mathbb{S}^{n-1}:=\{x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}\mid\|x\|_{2}=1\}
is the sphere.
These quantities can be estimated to arbitrary precision using samples from
K
and
K^{\circ}
respectively.

We can now state the celebrated

\displaystyle\max\det(T)

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{x\in\gamma_{n}}\big[\|T(x)\|_{K}^{2}\big]^{1/2}\leq 1

\displaystyle T\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times n}\text{ positive-definite}.

5.2
Approximate
\mathrm{CVP}_{K}
through a sieving algorithm for
\mathrm{SVP}_{Q}

In Section
3
, we have seen how approximate
\mathrm{CVP}_{K}
can be solved using some technical property inherent in any sieving algorithm based on
[
AKS01
]
for approximate
\mathrm{SVP}_{2}
. More specifically, we have assumed that we are given access to a certain procedure that samples independent lattice vectors according to some special distribution, see Theorem
3.2
. The procedure and its time and space requirements depend on (a slightly stronger variant of) the kissing number with respect to the Euclidean norm that equals
2^{0.401n}
. While this procedure could be made to work in any norm, only the Euclidean norm was considered because among all
known
bounds on kissing numbers, the one for the Euclidean norm is lowest. Here, we show that this is the only reason. More specifically, we show that if for
some
norm
\|\cdot\|_{Q}
the corresponding kissing number (i.e. the variant relevant for sieving) is better than the one for the Euclidean norm, then this would directly improve the running time for
\mathrm{CVP}
in
any
norm.

Before we state the corresponding generalisation of the procedure Theorem
3.2
to norms other than the Euclidean norm, we discuss how this procedure relates to a variant of the kissing number. Given some convex body
L=-L\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{n}
, the kissing number for
L
is the maximum number of non-overlapping translates of
\tfrac{1}{2}\cdot L
with centers on
\mathrm{bd}\left(L\right)
. Equivalently, it is the maximum number of points
v_{1},\cdots,v_{N}\in\mathrm{bd}\left(L\right)
so that
\|v_{i}-v_{j}\|_{L}\geq 1
for all
i,j\in[N]
,
i\neq j
. It is now easy to see how this relates to sieving algorithms as first described by
[
AKS01
]
. In sieving, we sample exponentially many lattice vectors and take pairwise differences to obtain shorter and shorter lattice vectors with respect to
\|\cdot\|_{L}
. Clearly, the kissing number with respect to
L
is a lower bound on the number of lattice vectors that we need to sample. Indeed, we need to sample at least this many lattice vectors just to guarantee that the difference of two of them becomes strictly smaller in order to find short vectors. The space requirement of this procedure would equal the kissing number and the time requirement the square of this number, since we need to check all pairs. For some special norms, better data-structures or trade-offs time/space are known,
[
AM18
,
Muk19
]
.
However, it is currently not known and an interesting open question, see
[
Ste20
]
, whether a bound on the kissing number is sufficient to obtain a sieving algorithm. Indeed, the procedure might only produce short vectors that equal
\bm{0}
and a technical argument is needed to ensure that this does not happen. For this, a slight variation (strengthening) of the kissing number is needed. Specifically, for
L=-L\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{n}
and
\gamma>0
, we define
\tilde{k}(L,\gamma)
as the maximum number of points
v_{1},\cdots,v_{N}
on
L\setminus(1-\gamma/n)\cdot L
so that
\|v_{i}-v_{j}\|_{L}\geq 1-\gamma
for all
i\neq j\in[N]
. For the Euclidean norm, i.e.
L=B_{2}^{n}
, this condition is equivalent to require that the angle between the
v_{i}
and
v_{j}
are larger than
60^{\circ}-\tilde{\gamma}
, where
\tilde{\gamma}
is a function of
\gamma
. For approximation factors of order
O(1+1/\gamma)
, the running time of
\mathrm{SVP}_{L}
,
\mathrm{CVP}_{L}
and that of procedure in Theorem
3.2
are then of order
\tilde{k}(L,\gamma)^{2}
. Whenever special data structures or trade-offs are available for finding close-by vectors with respect to
\|\cdot\|_{Q}
, this would directly improve the running time. This also motivates finding some norm
\|\cdot\|_{Q}
where such data-structures or trade-off are available, even if its (variant of the) kissing number is worse than that of the Euclidean norm, see
[
MS19
]
for a recent attempt.

We can now fix some origin-symmetric convex body

.

(b)

With probability
1-\rho_{\mathbf{s}}
, sample
u\sim\mathcal{D}^{\mathbf{s}}_{1}
.

This procedure takes time
\tilde{k}(Q^{+1},\gamma)^{2}\cdot 2^{\varepsilon n}+N\cdot\tilde{k}(Q^{+1},\gamma)\cdot 2^{\varepsilon n}
and
N+\tilde{k}(Q^{+1},\gamma)\cdot 2^{\varepsilon n}
space and succeeds with probability at least
1/2
.

We now how to solve approximate
\mathrm{CVP}_{K}
on
n
-dimensional lattices assuming we have access to the above distribution for some convex body
Q\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{n}
.

Theorem 5.4
.

Let
Q=-Q\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{n}
be such that
\tilde{k}(Q,\gamma)\leq 2^{\beta n}
for some
\gamma>0
. Then, for any norm
\|\cdot\|_{K}
and in time
2^{(2\beta+\varepsilon)n}
and space
2^{(\beta+\varepsilon)n}
, we obtain a
O_{\varepsilon,\gamma}(1)
-approximation to
\mathrm{CVP}_{K}
on any lattice of rank
n
.

Proof.

We first invoke Lemma
3.1
to restrict to a lattice of dimension
n
. Analogously to subsection
5.1
, we may assume that Lemma
5.1
holds with
T_{\varepsilon}^{Q}=T_{\varepsilon}^{K}=\text{Id}
. Indeed, it is enough to solve
\mathrm{CVP}_{T^{-1}_{\varepsilon}(K)}
and for any invertible transformation
T
, we have that
\tilde{k}(Q,\gamma)=\tilde{k}(T(Q),\gamma)
. Finally, we scale the lattice so that distance of the closest lattice lattice vector
\mathbf{c}\in\mathscr{L}
to
t
is close to
1
, i.e.
1-1/n\leq\|t-\mathbf{c}\|_{K}\leq 1
.

We now sample a random point
\tilde{t}
within
t+K+c_{\varepsilon}\cdot Q
. By Lemma
5.1
and with probability at least
2^{-2\varepsilon n}
,
\mathbf{c}\in\tilde{t}+c_{\varepsilon}\cdot Q
, we condition on this event. For this vector
\tilde{t}
we define the lattice
\mathscr{L}^{\prime}\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{n+1}
of rank
n+1
with the following basis:

\tilde{B}=\begin{pmatrix}B&\tilde{t}\\
0&1/n\\
\end{pmatrix}\in\mathbb{Q}^{(n+1)\times(n+1)}.

We now consider the norm
\|\cdot\|_{Q^{+1}}
induced by
Q^{+1}\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{n+1}
. We define
\mathbf{s}:=(\tilde{t}-\mathbf{c},1/n)\in\mathbb{R}^{n+1}
, observe that
1-1/n\leq\|\mathbf{s}\|_{Q^{+1}}\leq 1
. By Lemma
5.1
, each layer of the form
(c_{\varepsilon}\cdot a_{\varepsilon,\gamma})\cdot Q^{+1}\cap\{x_{n+1}=1/n\}
can be covered using
2^{2\varepsilon n}
translates of
(c_{\varepsilon}^{2}\cdot a_{\varepsilon,\gamma})\cdot K\times\{0\}
. In particular, all lattice vectors of length at most
a_{\varepsilon,\gamma}\cdot c_{\varepsilon}
(with respect to
\|\cdot\|_{Q^{+1}}
) can be covered by
(2\cdot a_{\varepsilon,\gamma}\cdot n+1)\cdot 2^{2\varepsilon n}
translates of
(c_{\varepsilon}^{2}\cdot a_{\varepsilon,\gamma})\cdot K\times\{0\}
.

We now use the procedure from Theorem
5.3
with
R:=c_{\varepsilon}
and sample
N:=2
lattice vectors of length at most
a_{\varepsilon,\gamma}\cdot R
. With probability at least
\tfrac{1}{2}\cdot 2^{-2\varepsilon n}
this succeeds and both lattice vectors are generated according to (
2a
). We condition on this event and denote by
v_{1},v_{2}
the resulting lattice vectors. Since
v_{1}
and
v_{2}
are i.i.d. and with probability at least
(2\cdot a_{\varepsilon,\gamma}\cdot n+1)^{-2}\cdot 2^{-4\varepsilon n}
, there is a translate of
(c_{\varepsilon}^{2}\cdot a_{\varepsilon,\gamma})\cdot K\times\{0\}
that holds both
v_{1}
and
v_{2}
. It follows that

v_{1}-v_{2}\in(2\cdot c_{\varepsilon}^{2}\cdot a_{\varepsilon,\gamma})\cdot K\times\{0\}.

We can now use property (
2a
). With probability at least
1/4
, instead of returning
v_{1}
and
v_{2}
, the procedure returns
v_{1}+\mathbf{s}
and
v_{2}
. We condition on this event. Their difference is then

(v_{1}+\mathbf{s})-v_{2}\in(2\cdot c_{\varepsilon}^{2}\cdot a_{\varepsilon,\gamma})\cdot K\times\{0\}+\mathbf{s}

We can rewrite this as

(v_{1}+\mathbf{s})-v_{2}=\begin{pmatrix}u\\
0\end{pmatrix}+\mathbf{s}=\begin{pmatrix}u-\mathbf{c}\\
0\end{pmatrix}+\begin{pmatrix}\tilde{t}\\
1/n\end{pmatrix},

for some
u\in\mathscr{L}
. Crucially

\|u\|_{Q}=\left\lVert\begin{pmatrix}u\\
0\end{pmatrix}\right\rVert_{Q^{+1}}\leq(2\cdot c_{\varepsilon}^{2}\cdot a_{\varepsilon,\gamma}).

The lattice vector
\mathbf{c}-u
is then the desired approximation. Indeed, by the triangle inequality,

\|t-(\mathbf{c}-u)\|_{K}\leq\|t-\mathbf{c}\|_{K}+\|u\|_{K}\leq 1+(2\cdot c_{\varepsilon}^{2}\cdot a_{\varepsilon}):=\beta_{\varepsilon}

We set
\gamma_{\varepsilon}:=(1-1/n)^{-1}\cdot(2\beta_{\varepsilon}+1)
as we have used Lemma
3.1
and scaled the lattice so that
\|t-\mathbf{c}\|_{K}\geq 1-1/n
. The lattice vector
\mathbf{c}-u
is then a
\gamma_{\varepsilon}
-approximation to the closest lattice vector to
t
.

Repeating this procedure
2^{\Omega(\varepsilon)n}
times starting from where we sampled
\tilde{t}\in t+K+c_{\varepsilon}\cdot Q
boosts the probability of success to
1-2^{-n}
. Using Lemma
5.2
, the time and space requirements follow from those of the procedure from Theorem
5.3
.
∎
