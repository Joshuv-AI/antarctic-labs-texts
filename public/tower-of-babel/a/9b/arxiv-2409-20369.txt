Numerical solutions of ordinary differential equations using Spline-Integral Operator
Gustavo H. O. Salgado, João P. R. Romanelli

Abstract
In this work, we introduce a novel numerical method for solving initial value problems associated with a given differential. Our approach utilizes a spline approximation of the theoretical solution alongside the integral formulation of the analytical solution. Furthermore, we offer a rigorous proof of the method’s order and provide a comprehensive stability analysis. Additionally, we showcase the effectiveness method through some examples, comparing with Taylor’s methods of same order.



1
Introduction

An Initial Value Problem (IVP) given by

\left\{\begin{array}[]{ccl}y^{\prime}(t)&=&f(t,y(t)),\\
y(t_{0})&=&y_{0},\end{array}\right.

(1)

consists of find a function
y(t)
that solves the Ordinary Differential Equation (ODE) in IVP (
1
) such that
y(t_{0})=y_{0}
.
It is well known that if
f(\cdot,y)
is Lipschitz continuous in some rectangle around the initial condition
(t_{0},y_{0})
, there is a unique solution
y
of Eq. (
1
)
[
1
]
.

In most situations, finding the explicit solution of a given IVP can be awkward or even impossible.
In order to address this difficult, numerical methods are used and play an important role in most applications involving differential equations.
Since numerical methods for solving an ODE generally result in approximations of the theoretical solution, the study and analysis of errors play another important role in these methods.

An important class of numerical methods widely used to approximate solutions of IVP (
1
) is the Runge–Kutta (RK) methods
[
2
,
3
]
.
They are obtained using Taylor’s expansion of a theoretical solution
y(t)
of (
1
) written in terms of the vector field
f(t,y)
and comparing with an incremental function that yields a nonlinear algebraic system with infinitely many solutions, where each solution provides a set of parameters for the incremental function.
A general methodology based on the use of tables and tree diagrams was developed by Butcher (1963)
[
4
]
, which simplifies the algebraic operations involved in Taylor’s expansions to obtain the RK parameters.

Another important class of numerical methods to solve ODEs is the linear multi-step methods
[
5
]
.
These methods are divided into explicit and implicit categories.
In an implicit multi-step, the numerical solution is obtained by solving a nonlinear equation that depends on
f(t,y)
.
This solution can be found by employing a Newton method to solve the nonlinear equation.
To avoid the evaluation of
f(t,y)
at each step where the Newton method is applied, a prediction is obtained from an explicit multi-step method followed by a correction using a fixed-point scheme, leading to Predictor-Corrector Methods.
However, the application of multi-step methods relies on knowing a number of solutions of the IVP, which are generally obtained from the initial condition
y(t_{0})=y_{0}
using other methods, such as Taylor or RK methods, which are one-step methods
[
6
]
.
Butcher (2000)
[
7
]
provides an overview of some major developments of Runge-Kutta and multi-step procedures over the last century.

The spline approach has been used to obtain numerical solutions for IVPs. Typically, the basic idea is consider a spline as an approximation of the solution.
The spline depends on a parameter associated with the term of the larger degree, which is computed. Loscalzo (1969)
[
8
]
used such an approach to obtain numerical approximations of first-order ODEs. In particular, it is demonstrated how to obtain the trapezoidal rule from spline of degree two and the Milne-Simpson method from a spline of degree three.
In
[
9
]
, approximations for second order value problems and boundary problems for periodic solutions are obtained.
The spline approach is also used to approximate solution of nonlinear matrix ordinary differential problems
[
10
,
11
]
.
A spline collocation method is proposed in
[
12
]
, where the collocation points coincide with the knots in the mesh. The method, called B-Spline (BS) Method, compute the spline values at the knots and is associated a class of linear multi-step methods.
The BS Method can also be applied for a non-uniform meshes
[
13
]
, and classes of quadrature formulas associated to the BS Method is studied in
[
14
]
.
In
[
15
]
, the Spline Quasi-Interpolation method, based on B-Splines, is presented and is applied to numerical solution of Gauss-Lobatto and Gauss-Legendre methods using Runge-Kutta methods.

In this work, we propose a one-step method derived from a function obtained through spline approximation of the solution of the IVP combined with its integral formulation. We define a spline that approximates the solution, but depends on an unknown parameter, which will be computed by a fixed-point iteration and taken as the approximated solution of the IVP.
We also demonstrate that there is an interval containing the initial condition where the approximate solution given by the proposed method is the unique solution in that interval.
Moreover, the method has order
(m+1)
when the spline approximation of the solution is expressed using derivatives up to order
(m-1)
. Additionally, we present numerical experiments where the approximation obtained does not depend on any numerical integral method.

This work is organized as follow: in Section
2
, we define the Spline Intregal Operator and prove its convergence; in Section
3
, we discuss the order of the SIO method; in Section
4
, we analyze the stability; in Section
5
, we present some numerical experiments; and finally, in Section
6
, we provide the conclusion and final remarks.

2
The Spline-Integral Operator

Let
y(t)
the theoretical solution of IVP (
1
).
If
y(t)
has continuous derivatives up to
m\!-\!1
at
t=t_{0}
, there is a unique spline of degree
m
given by

S_{m}(t_{0},t,w)=\sum_{k=0}^{m-1}\dfrac{1}{k!}\dfrac{d^{k}y(t_{0})}{dt^{k}}\,(t-t_{0})^{k}+s_{m}(t-t_{0})^{m},

(2)

where
s_{m}
satisfies
S_{m}(t_{0},t_{0}+h,\,w)=w
for a given pair
h
and
w
.
Is straightforward that

s_{m}=\dfrac{1}{h^{m}}\left(w-\sum_{k=0}^{m-1}\dfrac{1}{k!}\dfrac{d^{k}y(t_{0})}{dt^{k}}\,h^{k}\right).

(3)

Remark 2.1

By the application of the chain rule, if
f(t,y)
has continuous partial derivatives up to order
m\!-\!1
at
(t_{0},y_{0})
, then the spline
S_{m}(t_{0},t,w)
can be obtained from IVP (
1
).

Remark 2.2

The Taylor polynomial of degree
m-1
of a function
y(t)
at
t=t_{0}
is given by

T_{m-1}(t_{0},t)=\sum_{k=0}^{m-1}\dfrac{1}{k!}\dfrac{d^{k}y(t_{0})}{dt^{k}}\,(t-t_{0})^{k}=y(t_{0})+\sum_{k=1}^{m-1}\dfrac{d^{k-1}f(t_{0},y(t_{0}))}{dt^{k-1}}(t-t_{0})^{k}.

(4)

Hence,
s_{m}=(w-T_{m-1}(t_{0},t_{0}+h))/h^{m}
and

S_{m}(t_{0},t,w)=T_{m-1}(t_{0},t)+s_{m}(t-t_{0})^{m}.

We will define an integral operator inspired on the integral form of the solution of IVP (
1
),

y(t)=y(t_{0})+\int_{t_{0}}^{t}\,f(\tau,y(\tau))\,d\tau,

(5)

where
y(\tau)
will be replaced by the spline defined in Eqs. (
2
) and (
3
).
However, we need to establish conditions for
h
and
w
such that
f(t,\,S_{m}(t_{0},t,w))
is well-defined.
In the following proposition, we will demonstrate conditions under which
f(t,S_{m}(t_{0},t,w))
is defined.

Proposition 2.1

Let the rectangle

\mathcal{R}=\{(t,y);|t-t_{0}|\leq a,|y-y_{0}|\leq b\},

(6)

where
f(t,y)
is continuous.
If the theoretical solution
y(t)
of IVP (
1
) has
m-1
continuous derivatives at
t=t_{0}
, then there exist
h
and
w
such that
(t,S_{m}(t_{0},t,w))\subset\mathcal{R}
for all
|t-t_{0}|\leq|h|
.

Proof:
Since Taylor polynomial
T_{m-1}(t_{0},t)
is continuous at
t=t_{0}
, there is a
\delta>0
such that

|T_{m-1}(t_{0},t)-y_{0}|<\dfrac{b}{3}

for each
|t-t_{0}|<\delta
.

For a given
h
and
w
, such that
0<|h|<\delta
and
|w-y_{0}|\leq\dfrac{b}{3},
we have

\displaystyle|S_{m}(t_{0},t,w)-y_{0}|

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle|T_{m-1}(t_{0},t)+s_{m}(t-t_{0})^{m}-y_{0}|

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle|T_{m-1}(t_{0},t)-y_{0}|+|s_{m}(t-t_{0})^{m}|

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle|T_{m-1}(t_{0},t)-y_{0}|+|s_{m}h^{m}|

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle|T_{m-1}(t_{0},t)-y_{0}|+|w-T_{m-1}(t_{0},t_{0}+h)|

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle|T_{m-1}(t_{0},t)-y_{0}|+|w-y_{0}|+|T_{m-1}(t_{0},t_{0}+h)-y_{0}|

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\dfrac{b}{3}+\dfrac{b}{3}+\dfrac{b}{3}=b,

for all
|t-t_{0}|\leq|h|
.

Remark 2.3

In Proposition
2.1
, the existence of
h
is guaranteed by the continuity of spline
S_{m}(t_{0},t,w)
for any
w
. However, in the proof of the aforementioned proposition, we show that
h
can be chosen to account for the Taylor polynomial used in the spline definition. Also, for an appropriated choice of the independent values
w
and
h
, the graph of
S_{m}(t_{0},t,w)
for
|t-t_{0}|\leq|h|
is entirely contained in
\mathcal{R}
.

The spline
S_{m}(t_{0},t,w)
will be used to define the integral operator as follows.

Definition 2.1

For a given IVP (
1
), where
f(t,y)
is continuous in a rectangle
\mathcal{R}
, and for a fixed
h
, the Spline-Integral Operator (SIO)
G_{h}(w)
is defined as

G_{h}(w)=\,y_{0}+\int_{t_{0}}^{t_{0}+h}\,f(t,S_{m}(t_{0},t,w))\,dt,

(7)

where
h
and
w
are such that
(t,S_{m}(t_{0},t,w))\subset\mathcal{R}
for all
|t-t_{0}|\leq|h|
.

In the next theorem, we shall see that if
f(t,y)
is continuous and Lipschitz with respect the variable
y
in
\mathcal{R}
, then there is a suficient small
h
such that
G_{h}(w)
has a unique fixed point.

Theorem 2.2

Let
f(t,y)
continuous in
\mathcal{R}
(as defined in Proposition
2.1
) and Lipschitz with respect variable
y
with constant
K
, and
h
such that

\left|T_{m-1}(t_{0},t)-y_{0}\right|<\dfrac{b}{3},\penalty\ \mbox{for all}\penalty\ |t-t_{0}|\leq|h|.

If
|f|\leq M
in
\mathcal{R}
, then

G_{\alpha}:I\longrightarrow I,\penalty\ \penalty\ I=[y_{0}-b/3,y_{0}+b/3],

has a unique fixed point whenever
|\alpha|<\min\left\{|h|,\,\dfrac{b}{3M},\,\dfrac{m+1}{K}\right\}
.

Proof:
For each fixed
|\alpha|<\min\left\{|h|,\dfrac{b}{3M},\dfrac{m+1}{K}\right\}
we have

\displaystyle|G_{\alpha}(w)-y_{0}|

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\left|\int_{t_{0}}^{t_{0}+\alpha}\,f(t,S_{m}(t_{0},t,w))\,dt\right|\leq M|\alpha|<\dfrac{b}{3},

for each
w\in I
.
In the sequel, we shall show that
G_{\alpha}(w)
is a contraction.
Given
w_{1},w_{2}\in I
, then

\displaystyle|G_{\alpha}(w_{1})-G_{\alpha}(w_{2})|

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\left|\int_{t_{0}}^{t_{0}+\alpha}[f(t,S_{m}(t_{0},t,w_{1}))-f(t,S_{m}(t_{0},t,w_{2}))]dt\right|

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\int_{t_{0}}^{t_{0}+\alpha}|f(t,S_{m}(t_{0},t,w_{1}))-f(t_{0},t,S_{m}(t_{0},t,w_{2}))|dt

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\int_{t_{0}}^{t_{0}+\alpha}K|S_{m}(t_{0},t,w_{1})-S_{m}(t_{0},t,w_{2})|dt

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle K\int_{t_{0}}^{t_{0}+\alpha}\left|\dfrac{(w_{1}-w_{2})(t-t_{0})^{m}}{\alpha^{m}}\right|dt

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\dfrac{K|\alpha|}{m+1}|w_{1}-w_{2}|.

Therefore,
G_{\alpha}(w)
is a contraction since
|\alpha|<\dfrac{m+1}{K}
and, from the Banach fixed point theorem
[
16
]
,
G_{\alpha}(w)
has a unique fixed point.

Remark 2.4

It is well known that if
\partial f/\partial y
is continuous in
\mathcal{R}
, then
f(t,y)
is Lipschitz. In that case,
K
can be replaced by
\displaystyle\max\{\partial f/\partial y\}
on
\mathcal{R}
in Theorem
2.2
.

In the next section, we will show that the fixed point of
G_{h}(w)
, obtained as the limit of sequence
w_{n+1}=G_{h}(w_{n})
, with
w_{0}=y_{0}
, is an approximation of the theoretical solution
y(t_{0}+h)
of order
m+1
when
h
satisfies the hypothesis of Theorem
2.2
.
Additionally, we will extend the method for a set of nodes in an interval containing
t_{0}
as a method to solve IVPs.

3
Order approximation of SIO

The SIO given by Eq. (
7
) can be used as an approximation of the theoretical solution
y(t_{0}+h)
of an IVP (
1
).
In the sequel, we shall show that each term of the sequence
w_{n+1}=G_{h}(w_{n})
, with
n>0
and
|w_{0}-y_{0}|\leq b/3
is at least an approximation of order
m
.

Proposition 3.1

If
f(t,y)
,
w
, and
h
satisfy the hypothesis of Theorem
2.2
and the theoretical solution
y(t)
has
m+1
continuous derivatives, then

|G_{h}(w)-y(t_{0}+h)|
is
\mathcal{O}(h^{m+1})
.

Proof:
Let
g_{{w}}(t)
defined by

g_{{w}}(t)=y_{0}+\int_{t_{0}}^{t}f(\tau,S_{m}(t_{0},\tau,{w}))d\tau.

By definition of
S_{m}(t_{0},t,{w})
,
\dfrac{d^{k}S_{m}(t_{0},t,{w})}{dt^{k}}\Big|_{t=t_{0}}=\dfrac{d^{k}y(t_{0})}{dt^{k}}
for
k<m
, and

\dfrac{d^{m}S_{m}(t_{0},t,w)}{dt^{m}}\Big|_{t=t_{0}}=m!s_{m},

by Eq. (
2
).
Then,

\dfrac{d^{k}g_{{w}}(t)}{dt^{k}}\Big|_{t=t_{0}}=\dfrac{d^{k}y(t_{0})}{dt^{k}},\mbox{ for }k\leq m.

(8)

Also,

\dfrac{d^{m+1}g_{{w}}(t)}{dt^{m+1}}\Big|_{t=t_{0}}=\dfrac{d^{m+1}y(t_{0})}{dt^{m+1}}+\dfrac{\partial f(t_{0},y_{0})}{\partial y}\left(m!s_{m}-\dfrac{d^{m}y(t_{0})}{dt^{m}}\right),

by application of
(m+1)
th derivative on the integral form of
y(t)
, given by Eq. (
5
), and comparing with
\dfrac{d^{m+1}g_{w}(t)}{dt^{t^{m+1}}}
at
t=t_{0}
.
Then, taking the Taylor series of
g_{{w}}(t)
and the Taylor series of theoretical solution
y(t)
at
t=t_{0}
up to order
m+1
, we have

\displaystyle g_{{w}}(t_{0}+h)-y(t_{0}+h)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\dfrac{\partial f(t_{0},y(t_{0}))}{\partial y}\left(m!s_{m}-\dfrac{d^{m}y(t_{0})}{dt^{m}}\right)\dfrac{h^{m+1}}{(m+1)!}+\mathcal{O}(h^{m+2}),

which proves the assertion, since
G_{h}(w)=g_{w}(t_{0}+h)
.

The order of approximation at the fixed point of
G_{h}(w)
can be improved as a consequence of the proof of Proposition
3.1
.

Corolary 3.2

If
\tilde{w}
is the fixed point of
G_{h}(w)
, then
|\tilde{w}-y(t_{0}+h)|
is
\mathcal{O}(h^{m+2})
.

Proof:
Since
\tilde{w}=G_{h}(\tilde{w})
, then
\tilde{w}=g_{\tilde{w}}(t_{0}+h)
. From Eqs. (
3
) and (
8
) it follows that

\displaystyle m!s_{m}-\dfrac{d^{m}y(t_{0})}{dt^{m}}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\dfrac{m!}{h^{m}}\left(\tilde{w}-\sum_{k=0}^{m-1}\dfrac{1}{k!}\dfrac{d^{k}y(t_{0})}{dt^{k}}\,h^{k}-\dfrac{d^{m}y(t_{0})}{dt^{m}}\dfrac{h^{m}}{m!}\right)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\dfrac{m!}{h^{m}}\left(g_{\tilde{w}}(t_{0}+h)-\sum_{k=0}^{m}\dfrac{1}{k!}\dfrac{d^{k}y(t_{0})}{dt^{k}}\,h^{k}\right)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\dfrac{m!}{h^{m}}\dfrac{d^{m+1}g_{\tilde{w}}(\xi)}{dt^{m+1}}\dfrac{h^{m+1}}{(m+1)!},

for some
\xi\in[t_{0},t_{0}+h]
. Hence,

\displaystyle\tilde{w}-y(t_{0}+h)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\dfrac{\partial f(t_{0},y(t_{0}))}{\partial y}\left(\dfrac{d^{m+1}g_{\tilde{w}}(\xi)}{dt^{m+1}}\dfrac{h}{m+1}\right)\dfrac{h^{m+1}}{(m+1)!}+\mathcal{O}(h^{m+2})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\dfrac{\partial f(t_{0},y(t_{0}))}{\partial y}\dfrac{d^{m+1}g_{\tilde{w}}(\xi)}{dt^{m+1}}\dfrac{h^{m+2}}{(m+1)^{2}\,(m!)}+\mathcal{O}(h^{m+2})\penalty\ =\penalty\ \mathcal{O}(h^{m+2}).

Therefore, the approximation obtained from
G_{h}(w)
of
y(t_{0}+h)
is of order
m
, and it becomes an approximation of order
m+1
for
\tilde{w}=G_{h}(\tilde{w})
. Henceforth, if the spline used in the SIO is of degree
m
, we will refer to it as
SIO of order

\boldsymbol{m+1}
, denoted as
SIO
\boldsymbol{(m+1)}
.

The SIO of order
m+1
can be extended to determine numerical solutions for an IVP (
1
) over a discretization nodes
t_{i+1}=t_{i}+h
, with
i=0,\dots,N-1
,
using the implicit recursion schema

\left\{\begin{array}[]{l}\tilde{w}_{0}=y(t_{0}),\\[8.5359pt]
\tilde{w}_{i+1}=\tilde{w}_{i}+\displaystyle\int_{t_{i}}^{t_{i}+h}f(t,S_{m}(t_{i},t,\tilde{w}_{i+1}))\,dt,\penalty\ i=0,\dots,N-1,\end{array}\right.

(9)

where the Taylor polynomial part of the spline
S_{m}(t_{i},t,w)
is evaluated by replacing
y(t_{i})
with
\tilde{w}_{i}
in the right-hand side (RHS) of Eq. (
4
) at each step
i
.
Clearly,
\tilde{w}_{i+1}
is the unique fixed point of the SIO(
m
+1) in the second equation of (
9
), if
t_{i}
,
\tilde{w}_{i}
, and
h
satisfy an analogous condition to that of Theorem
2.2
.

In the next section, we will provide a comprehensive stability analysis for this method, encompassing cases for each
m\geq 1
.
Additionally, we will present a detailed and explicit derivation of the SIO method outlined in Eq. (
9
) for a linear homogeneous differential equation.
This derivation will serve as a foundation for demonstrating the stability analysis.

4
Stability Analysis

The stability of the proposed method is determined by examining numerical solutions of the IVP

y^{\prime}=\lambda y,\penalty\ \penalty\ y(t_{0})=y_{0},

(10)

where
\lambda
is a complex parameter. For a given
m
, the associated spline of Eq. (
10
) at
t_{0}
is given by

S_{m}(t_{0},t,w)=y_{0}\,\sum_{k=0}^{m-1}\dfrac{\lambda^{k}(t-t_{0})^{k}}{k!}+s_{m}(t-t_{0})^{m},

where
s_{m}=\dfrac{1}{h^{m}}\left(w-y_{0}\displaystyle\sum_{k=0}^{m-1}\dfrac{(\lambda h)^{k}}{k!}\right)
.
Then, using the schema in Eq. (
9
) for
i=1
(
\tilde{w}_{0}=y_{0}
), we obtain the Spline-Integral Operator

\displaystyle G_{h}(w)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle y_{0}+\int_{t_{0}}^{t_{0}+h}\lambda\left(y_{0}\,\sum_{k=0}^{m-1}\dfrac{\lambda^{k}(t-t_{0})^{k}}{k!}+s_{m}(t-t_{0})^{m}\right)dt

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle y_{0}+y_{0}\lambda\,\sum_{k=0}^{m-1}\dfrac{\lambda^{k}h^{k+1}}{(k+1)!}+\dfrac{s_{m}\lambda h^{m+1}}{m+1}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle y_{0}\left(1+\sum_{k=0}^{m-1}\dfrac{(\lambda h)^{k+1}}{(k+1)!}\right)+\dfrac{\lambda h}{m+1}\left(w-y_{0}\displaystyle\sum_{k=0}^{m-1}\dfrac{(\lambda h)^{k}}{k!}\right)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\dfrac{\lambda hw}{m+1}+y_{0}\left(1+\sum_{k=0}^{m-1}\dfrac{(m-k)(\lambda h)^{k+1}}{(m+1)(k+1)!}\right).

The fixed point of
G_{h}(w)
at
t_{0}
,
i.e.
, for
i=1
, is given by

\tilde{w}_{1}=\dfrac{y_{0}}{m+1-\lambda h}\left(m+1+\sum_{k=0}^{m-1}\dfrac{(m-k)(\lambda h)^{k+1}}{(k+1)!}\right).

(11)

By induction in
i
, we have

\tilde{w}_{i}=\dfrac{y_{0}}{(m+1-\lambda h)^{i}}\left(m+1+\sum_{k=0}^{m-1}\dfrac{(m-k)(\lambda h)^{k+1}}{(k+1)!}\right)^{i}.

(12)

This shows the following proposition

Proposition 4.1

The SIO(
m
+1) is stable if, and only if,

\dfrac{1}{|m+1-\lambda h|}\left|m+1+\sum_{k=0}^{m-1}\dfrac{(m-k)(\lambda h)^{k+1}}{(k+1)!}\right|<1,\penalty\ \penalty\ \lambda h\in\mathbb{C}.

(13)

In the following discussion, we will utilize Proposition
4.1
to define the stability region of SIO(
m+1
) and compare its stability with that of Taylor’s methods across various values of
m
.

The stability of the SIO(2) is equivalent to that of the implicit trapezoidal method
[
5
]
. In fact, we have

\tilde{w}_{i}=y_{0}\,\left(\dfrac{2+\lambda h}{2-\lambda h}\right)^{i},\penalty\ \mbox{for}\penalty\ m=1\penalty\ \mbox{and}\penalty\ i\geq 0.

(14)

Hence, the absolute stability region for SIO(2) is the half semi-plane
\{\lambda h\in\mathbb{C};\penalty\ \mathrm{Re}(\lambda h)<0\}
.

Numerical Experiments

In this section, we will present the results of numerical experiments obtained used SIO method developed in Sections

The SIO(
m
+1) can be recursively applied to generate a series of numerical approximations,
\tilde{w}_{i}
, which approximate the theoretical solution
y(t_{i})
of an IVP (
1
) across discretization nodes
t_{i}=t_{0}+ih
, with
i=0,\dots,N
. This is achieved through the following procedure

\left\{\begin{array}[]{l}\tilde{w}_{0}\,=\,y_{0},\\[21.33955pt]
\penalty\ \penalty\ \penalty\ {w}_{i+1}^{[0]}\,=\,\tilde{w}_{i},\\[8.5359pt]
\penalty\ \penalty\ \penalty\ {w}_{i+1}^{[\ell]}\,=\,\tilde{w}_{i}+\displaystyle\int_{t_{i}}^{t_{i}+h}\!f\left(t,S_{m}\left(t_{i},t,{w}_{i+1}^{[\ell-1]}\right)\right)dt,\,\ell=1,\dots,M,\\[14.22636pt]
\tilde{w}_{i+1}=w_{i+1}^{[M]},\penalty\ \penalty\ i=0,\dots,N-1,\end{array}\right.

(15)

for a sufficient large
M
or when
\left|w_{i+1}^{[\ell]}-w_{i+1}^{[\ell+1]}\right|
is less than a tolerance in each step
i
.

Remark 5.1

From Proposition
2.1
and Theorem
2.2
, if the function
f(t,y)
has continuous partial derivatives up to order
m\!-\!1
in a step
(t_{i},\tilde{w}_{i})
, then
f(t,y)
is Lipschitz continuous in a rectangle

\mathcal{R}_{i}=\{(t,y);\penalty\ |t-t_{i}|\leq a,\penalty\ |y-\tilde{w}_{i}|\leq b\},

and there is an
h>0
where the approximation
\tilde{w}_{i+1}
can be obtained.

In each example below, the degree of the spline
S_{m}(t_{i},t,w)
will be set as
m=3
, meaning the SIO has an approximation of order
4
.
To demonstrate the efficiency and robustness of the proposed method, we will also present results obtained using Taylor’s methods of orders 2 and 4.
Furthermore, we will discuss the cases where the integral in Eq.(
15
) can be explicitly obtained and instances where it is approximated using numerical methods.

Remark 5.2

In a general scenario, the integral of
f(t,S(t_{i},t,w))
may not yield expressions in terms of elementary functions. Consequently, an explicit expression for the fixed point of
G_{h}(w)
cannot always be obtained.

6
Conclusions

The central aspect of this work lies in the utilization of the spline function
S_{m}(t_{i},t,w)
, describe in Eq. (
2
), where
w
is obtained as an approximation to the analytical solution
y(t_{i+1})
via the iterative recursive method outlined in Eq. (
15
), employing the proposed Spline-Integral Operator (SIO) (
7
).
The SIO(
m+1
) method (
9
) is an implicit method that only requires information about the initial condition to be initialized. In contrast, other implicit methods of higher order, such as Predictor-Corrector methods, need a set of initial conditions for initialization, typically obtained using other numerical methods from the initial condition.

Nevertheless, this method relies on computing of
m\!-\!1
derivatives of the theoretical solution
y(t)
to obtain the
S_{m}(t_{i},t,w)
spline.
These derivatives of the theoretical solution can be obtained from
f(t,y)
by applying the RHS of Eq. (
4
), similar to how Taylor methods handle the numerical solution of IVPs.
Moreover, determining the fixed point of SIO(
m+1
) necessitates computing the integral stated in Eq. (
7
).
However, straightforward methods such as Gauss Quadrature, as utilized here, or even Simpson’s methods, can be employed to compute the integral of
f(t,S_{m}(t_{i},t,w))
over each interval
[t_{i},t_{i}+h]
without compromising the method’s order.

As a significant aspect of this work, we demonstrated in Proposition
3.1
that SIO(
m+1
) method is an approximation of order
m
. Furthermore, in Corollary
3.2
, we established that it becomes an approximation of order
m+1
when the fixed point is reached.
Additionally, through examples, we illustrated that SIO(
m+1
) method achieves better approximations while requiring fewer derivative calculations of the theoretical solution compared to Taylor’s methods of the same order.
