Arithmetical properties at the level of idempotence
Fethi Bouzeffour a , Wissem Jedidi b

Abstract
. In this paper we give an attempt to extend some arithmetic properties such as multiplicativity, convolution products to the setting of operators theory. We provide a significant examples which are of interest in number theory. We also give a representation of the Euler differential operator by means of the Euler totient arithmetic function and idempotent elements of some associative unital algebra.

1.
Introduction

In number theory, an arithmetical, or number-theoretic function is a function
\alpha:\,\mathbb{N}\rightarrow\mathbb{C}
. Their various properties were investigated by several authors and they represent an important research topic up to now. An important property shared by many number-theoretic functions is multiplicativity: an arithmetical function
\alpha
is said to be multiplicative, if for all relatively prime positive integers
n,\,m
, we have

\alpha(nm)=\alpha(n)\alpha(m).

Examples of important arithmetical functions include: the Euler totient function, denoted
\varphi
, and defined as the number of positive integers less than and relatively prime to
n
. The Möbius function given by

\mu(n)=\begin{cases}(-1)^{\omega(n)}&\mbox{ if }n\mbox{ is a square-free integer}\\
0&\mbox{ otherwise },\end{cases}

where
\omega(n)
is the number of prime factors of
n
is also a multiplicative functions. Another important multiplicative function is the Ramanujan sum’s, which is defined by
[
8
]

c_{n}(j):=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\gcd(k,n)=1\\
1\leq k\leq n\end{subarray}}\varepsilon_{n}^{jk},

where
\varepsilon_{n}
denotes a primitive
n
-th root of unity and
\gcd(k,n)
to denote the greatest common divisor of the positive integers
k
and
n.
These sums fit naturally with other number-theoretic functions. For instance, one has

(1.1)

c_{n}(1)=\mu(n),\quad c_{n}(n)=\varphi(n),

For a more elaborate account on the multiplicative functions, we refer the reader to the texts
[
1
,
2
]
and the survey articles
[
6
]
.

In this work we suggest to extend the rang of the number-theoretic function and consider functions
f
such that:

- their domain are the positive integers and whose range is a subset of an unital associative algebra
A
over
\mathbb{C}
;

- they satisfy the property

(1.2)

f(nm)=f(n)f(m),\,\,\text{when}\,\,\gcd(n,m)=1.

Our first objective is to give a variety of significant examples, which are of interest in number theory. Notice that if
\mathcal{A}
is an unital algebra then every number-theoretic function
\alpha:\mathbb{N}\rightarrow\mathbb{C}
can be identified with the function
n\rightarrow\alpha(n)\,e
(
e
is the unit of
\mathcal{A}
).

Recall that an element
P\in\mathcal{A}
such that
P^{2}=P
is called idempotent. A set of idempotent elements
P_{1},\dots,P_{n}
is called orthogonal if,

P_{i}\,P_{j}=\delta_{ij}P_{i}\quad\mbox{and}\quad\sum_{i=1}^{n}P_{i}=e.

In this paper, we consider a set of orthogonal idempotent
P_{j}(n)
, indexed by two integers
n\geq 1
and
j\geq 0
, satisfying:

- for every fixed integer
n,
the sequence
j\rightarrow P_{j}(n)
is periodic with period
n
;

- for every arithmetic progression
j+n,\dots j+rn
, we have

P_{j}(n)=\sum_{k=1}^{r}P_{j+kn}(nr).

Under these conditions and for every fixed
j\geq 0
, the function
n\rightarrow P_{j}(n)
is multiplicative.

Another example considered in this note, consists to replace the root of unity in the Ramanujan sum’s by an element
s
of
\mathcal{A}
satisfying
s^{n}=e
. Then, the following sum

(1.3)

\displaystyle\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\gcd(k,n)=1\\
1\leq k\leq n\end{subarray}}s^{k}

is a multiplicative function.

2.
Arithmetic properties of Idempotents

2.1.
Multiplicative functions

Throughout,
\mathcal{A}
will be an associative unital algebra over
\mathbb{C}
. We recall the following definitions and basic facts

Definition 2.1
.

A function
f:\mathbb{N}\rightarrow\mathcal{A}
is called multiplicative if

f(nm)=f(n)f(m),\,\,\text{when}\,\,\gcd(n,m)=1.

Lemma 2.1
.

If
f:\mathbb{N}\rightarrow\mathcal{A}
is multiplicative, then
f(1)
is idempotent.

Proof.

Taking
n=m=1
, we have

f(1)=f(1.1)=f(1)\textperiodcentered f(1).

So,
f(1)
is idempotent.
∎

The following proposition is a characterization of multiplicative functions.

Proposition 2.2
.

For an arithmetical function
f:\mathbb{N}\rightarrow\mathcal{A}
, the following are equivalent:

1.
f
is multiplicative,

2. for all
n=p_{1}^{\alpha_{1}}\dots p_{k}^{\alpha_{k}}
(the standard form factorization of
n
), we have

f(n)=f(p_{1}^{\alpha_{1}})\dots f(p_{k}^{\alpha_{k}}).

We naturally extend the convolution product such as Drichlet product,
\operatorname{lcm}
product and unitary product (see,
[
1
,
14
,
12
]
) as follows:

(2.1)

\displaystyle(f\,*\,g)(n)=\sum_{kl=n}f(k)g(l)\quad(\text{Dirichlet product}).

(2.2)

\displaystyle(f\,\Box\,g)(n)=\sum_{\operatorname{lcm}(k,l)=n}f(k)g(l)\quad(\text{lcm-product)}.

(2.3)

\displaystyle(f\,\sqcup\,g)(n)=\sum_{kl=n\,\,\gcd(k,l)=1}f(k)g(l)\quad(\text{unitary product)}.

where
f,\,g:\mathbb{N}\rightarrow\mathcal{A}
.

The convolution products defined in (
2.1
), (
2.2
) and (
2.3
) are associative but not commutative in general. If, in addition, if for all integers
n,m
, and every prime numbers
p
and
q,

f(p^{n})
commutes with
g(q^{m})
, then we have

f\,*\,g=g\,*\,f,\quad f\,\Box\,g=g\,\Box\,f,\quad f\,\sqcup\,g=g\,\sqcup\,f.

The following propositions are easy to prove.

Proposition 2.3
.

1) If
f
is an arithmetic function on
\mathcal{A}
, then

f\,*\,I=I\,*\,f=f,\quad f\,\Box\,I=I\,\Box\,f=f,\quad f\,\sqcup\,I=I\,\sqcup\,f=f,

where

I(n)=\left\{\begin{array}[]{l l}e\quad\text{if}\quad n=1\\
0\quad\text{otherwise}.\end{array}\right.

2) If
f
is an arithmetical function such that
f(1)
is invertible element in
\mathcal{A}
, then
f
has a unique inverse with respect to the Dirichlet product.

3) If
f
is a multiplicative function on
\mathcal{A}
and
b\in\mathcal{A}
invertible element , then the function
n\mapsto bf(n)b^{-1}
is multiplicative.

4) If
f
and
g
are multiplicative functions, then
f*g
is also a multiplicative function.

Proposition 2.4
.

1)If
\mathcal{A}
is equipped with a norm
\|.\|
and
f
a multiplicative function on
A
, then
n\rightarrow\|f(n)\|
is a multiplicative function.

2)If
\mathcal{A}
is the algebra of
N\times N
-matrix and
f
is multiplicative on
\mathbb{C}^{N}
, then function
n\mapsto\det(f(n))
is multiplicative.

2.2.
Idempotents

Definition 2.2
.

Let
\mathcal{A}
be an algebra with unity
e
. We say that a collection
\,\{P_{j}(n)\}_{j=0,n=1}^{\infty}\,
is an arithmetic system of idempotent if the following conditions are satisfied:

I) for all
i,\,j=1,\,2,\dots,
we have
P_{i}(n)P_{j}(n)=\delta_{ij}P_{i}
(n),

II) for all
n,\,j=1,\,2,\dots,
we have
P_{j+n}(n)=P_{j}(n),

III) for every arithmetic progression
\{j+n,\dots,j+nr\}
we have

P_{j}(n)=\sum_{k=1}^{r}P_{j+kn}(nr).

The integer
n
in
P_{j}(n)
is called level of the idempotence.

Theorem 2.5
.

Let
\{P_{j}(n)\}_{j=0,n=1}^{\infty}
be an arithmetic system of idempotents of
\mathcal{A}
.
Then for arbitrary positive integers
n
,
m

k
and
l
, we have

(2.4)

P_{k}(n)P_{l}(m)=\left\{\begin{array}[]{l l}P_{j}(\operatorname{lcm}(n,m))\quad\text{if}\quad\gcd(n,m)\mid l-k\\
0\quad\text{otherwise}.\end{array}\right.

where
j
is the unique solution
\mod(\operatorname{lcm}(n,m))
of the system of congruences

(2.5)

\left\{\begin{array}[]{l l}j\equiv k\mod(n)\\
j\equiv l\mod(m).\end{array}\right.

Here
\operatorname{lcm}(n,m)
is the least common multiple of the integer
n,\,m.

Lemma 2.6
.

(The Chinese Remainder Theorem)

The system of congruences

(2.6)

\left\{\begin{array}[]{l l}x\equiv a\mod(n)\\
x\equiv b\mod(m).\end{array}\right.

is solvable if, and only if
\gcd(n,m)|a-b,
any two solutions of the system are incongruent mod
\operatorname{lcm}(n,m)
.

Proof.

From condition
III)
we can write

\displaystyle P_{k}(n)P_{l}(m)

\displaystyle=\sum_{s=0}^{m-1}\sum_{r=0}^{n-1}P_{k+sn}(nm)P_{l+rm}(nm).

If
\gcd(n,m)\nmid l-k
, then
k+sn\neq l+rm
and
P_{k}(n)P_{l}(m)=0
.

If
\gcd(n,m)\mid l-k,
then by Chinese remainder theorem there exist unique integer
j

\mod(\operatorname{lcm}(n,m))
such that

(2.7)

\left\{\begin{array}[]{l l}j\equiv k\mod(n)\\
j\equiv l\mod(m).\end{array}\right.

Then from
II)
we get

\displaystyle P_{k}(n)P_{l}(m)

\displaystyle=\sum_{s=0}^{m-1}\sum_{r=0}^{n-1}P_{j+sn}(nm)P_{j+rm}(nm).

On the other hand, from
I)
we see that for
0\leq r\leq n-1
and
0\leq s\leq m-1
the term
P_{j+rn}(nm)P_{j+sm}(nm)
is different from zero, if and only if
rn=sm
, which is equivalent to
r=r^{\prime}\frac{n}{\gcd(n,m)}
and
s=r^{\prime}\frac{m}{\gcd(n,m)}
, with
0\leq r^{\prime}\leq\gcd(n,m)-1
.

Then

\displaystyle P_{k}(n)P_{l}(m)

\displaystyle=\sum_{r^{\prime}=0}^{\gcd(n,m)-1}P_{j+r^{\prime}\operatorname{lcm}(n,m)}(nm)=P_{j}(\operatorname{lcm}(n,m)).

Therefore

\displaystyle P_{k}(n)P_{l}(m)=\left\{\begin{array}[]{l l}P_{j}(\operatorname{lcm}(n,m))\quad\text{if}\quad\gcd(n,m)\mid l-k\\
0\quad\text{otherwise}\end{array}\right.

where
j
is the unique integer
\mod(\operatorname{lcm}(n,m))
such that

(2.8)

\left\{\begin{array}[]{l l}j\equiv l\mod(n)\\
j\equiv k\mod(m).\end{array}\right.

∎

Corollary 2.7
.

The following hold

1. If
n
and
m
are relatively prime, we have

P_{j}(n)P_{j}(m)=P_{j}(nm).

2. If
n\mid m
, we have

P_{j}(n)P_{k}(m)=\left\{\begin{array}[]{l l}P_{k}(m)\,\,\,\text{if}\,\,\,k\equiv j\mod(n)\\
0\,\,\,\text{otherwise}.\end{array}\right.

Proposition 2.8
.

Let
\alpha,\,\beta:\,\mathbb{N}\rightarrow\mathbb{C}
two arithmetic functions and
j=0,1\dots
, we have

(2.9)

\displaystyle\alpha P_{j}\,\square\,\beta P_{j}=(\alpha\square\beta)P_{j},

(2.10)

\displaystyle\alpha P_{j}\sqcup\beta P_{j}=(\alpha\sqcup\beta)\,P_{j},

(2.11)

\displaystyle\nu_{0}*(\alpha\square\beta)P_{j}=(\nu_{0}*\alpha P_{j})\,(\nu_{0}*\beta P_{j}).

In particular,

(2.12)

\displaystyle P_{j}\,\square\,P_{j}(n)=M_{2}(n)P_{j}(n),\quad P_{j}\sqcup P_{j}(n)=2^{\omega(n)}P_{j}(n)

where

(2.13)

M_{s}(n)=\left\{\begin{array}[]{l l}1\quad\text{if}\,\,\,n=1,\\
\prod_{k=1}^{r}\big((a_{s}+1)^{s}-a_{k}^{s}\big)\quad\,\text{if}\quad n=\prod_{k=1}^{r}p_{k}^{a_{k}},\end{array}\right.

and
\nu_{0}(n)=1.

Proof.

Let
n
be a positive integer, we have

\displaystyle\alpha P_{j}\,\square\,\beta P_{j}(n)

\displaystyle=\sum_{\operatorname{lcm}(k,l)=n}\alpha(k)\beta(l)P_{j}(k)P_{j}(l)

\displaystyle=\sum_{\operatorname{lcm}(k,l)=n}\alpha(k)\beta(l)P_{j}(\operatorname{lcm}(k,l))

\displaystyle=(\alpha\,\square\,\beta)(n)P_{j}(n).

To prove (
2.10
)

\displaystyle\alpha P_{j}\,\sqcup\,\beta P_{j}(n)

\displaystyle=\sum_{kl=n,\gcd(k,l)=1}\alpha(k)\beta(l)P_{j}(k)P_{j}(l)

\displaystyle=\sum_{kl=n,\gcd(k,l)=1}\alpha(k)\beta(l)P_{j}(n)

\displaystyle=(\alpha\sqcup\beta)(n)P_{j}(n).

The equation (
2.11
) follows from the following identity
[
9
]

(2.14)

(\nu_{0}\ast\alpha)(\nu_{0}\ast\alpha)=\nu_{0}\ast(\alpha\Box\beta).

∎

3.
Ramanujan sum’s

The function
\alpha:\,\mathbb{N}\rightarrow\,\mathbb{C}
is called even function (
\mod\,d
) if
\alpha(n)=\alpha(\gcd(n,d))
for
all
n
, that is if the value
\alpha(n)
depends only on the
\gcd(n,d)
. Hence if
\alpha
is even(
\mod\,d
), then it is sufficient to know the values
f(r)
, where
r\mid d
.

The Ramanujan
sum’s

(3.1)

c_{n}(j):=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\gcd(k,n)=1\\
1\leq k\leq n\end{subarray}}\varepsilon_{n}^{jk}.

If
\alpha
is even (
\mod\,d
), then it has a Ramanujan-Fourier expansion of
the form

\alpha(n)=\sum_{r\mid d}(\mathcal{R}_{\alpha})(r)c_{r}(n)\qquad(n\in{\mathbb{N}}),

where the (Ramanujan-)Fourier coefficients
(\mathcal{R}_{\alpha})(r)
are
uniquely determined and given by

(\mathcal{R}_{\alpha})(r)=\sum_{\delta\mid d}\alpha(\tfrac{d}{\delta})c_{\delta}(\tfrac{d}{r}).

Definition 3.1
.

We say that a set
\{P_{j}(n):n\geq 1,j\geq 0\}
is an arithmetic system of orthogonal idempotent, if

1.
\{P_{j}(n)\}_{j,n=1}^{\infty}
is an arithmetic system of idempotent,

2. for every
n

(3.2)

\sum_{j=0}^{n-1}P_{j}(n)=e.

Consider the sum

(3.3)

\displaystyle C_{j}(n)=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\gcd(k,n)=1\\
1\leq k\leq n\end{subarray}}\varepsilon_{n}^{-jk}S^{k}(n),\quad j=0,\,1,\,\dots,

where
S(n)=\sum_{j=1}^{n}\varepsilon_{n}^{j}P_{j}(n).

For every divisor
r
of
n
, we denote by

(3.4)

\displaystyle T_{r,j}(n)=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\gcd(k,n)=\tfrac{n}{r}\\
1\leq k\leq n\end{subarray}}P_{k+j}(n).

Note that
T_{n,j}(n)
is denoted simply by
T_{j}(n)
.

Proposition 3.1
.

The following properties hold

C_{j}(n)=\mu*\nu_{1}P_{j}(n),\quad\&\quad T_{j}(n)=\nu_{0}*\mu P_{j}(n),

where
\nu_{k}(n)=n^{k}.

Proof.

We have

\displaystyle\mu*\nu_{1}P_{j}(n)

\displaystyle=\sum_{d\mid n}\mu(d)\tfrac{n}{d}P_{j}(\tfrac{n}{d})

\displaystyle=\sum_{d\mid n}\mu(d)\sum_{k=1}^{\tfrac{n}{d}}\varepsilon_{n}^{-jkd}S_{n}^{kd}

\displaystyle=\sum_{k=1}^{n}\varepsilon_{n}^{-jk}S_{n}^{k}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}d\mid n\\
d\mid k\end{subarray}}\mu(d)

\displaystyle=\sum_{k=1}^{n}\varepsilon_{n}^{-jk}S_{n}^{k}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}d\mid\gcd(k,n)\end{subarray}}\mu(d).

From Theorem 2.1 in
[
1
]
we have

(3.5)

\sum_{d\mid\gcd(k,n)}\mu(r)=\left\{\begin{array}[]{l l}1\quad\,\text{if}\quad\gcd(k,n)=1,\\
0\quad\,\text{if}\quad\gcd(k,n)\,>1.\end{array}\right.

Hence

(3.6)

C_{j}(n)=\mu*\nu_{1}P_{j}(n).

To prove 2)

\displaystyle T_{j}(n)

\displaystyle=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\gcd(k,n)=1\\
1\leq k\leq n\end{subarray}}P_{j+k}(n)

\displaystyle=\sum_{k=1}^{n}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\delta\mid\gcd(k,n)\end{subarray}}\mu(\delta)P_{j+k}(n)

\displaystyle=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\delta\mid n\end{subarray}}\mu(\delta)\sum_{k=1}^{\tfrac{n}{\delta}}P_{j+k\delta}(n)

\displaystyle=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\delta\mid n\end{subarray}}\mu(\delta)P_{j}(\delta)

∎

Corollary 3.2
.

\displaystyle C_{j}(n)=n\prod_{l=1}^{N}\big(P_{j}(p^{\alpha_{l}})-\frac{1}{p}P_{j}(p^{\alpha_{l}-1}),

\displaystyle T_{j}(n)=\prod_{\begin{subarray}{c}p\mid n\\
p\,\,\text{prime}\end{subarray}}(e-P_{j}(p)).

Proof.

The functions
C_{j}(n)
and
T_{j}
are multiplicative. So it suffice to compute
C_{j}(p^{k})
and
T_{j}(p^{k})
where
p
is prime number.
Form proposition 4.1, we have

\displaystyle C_{j}(p^{k})

\displaystyle=\sum_{l=0}^{k}\mu(p^{l})p^{k-l}P_{j}(p^{k-l})

\displaystyle=p^{k}P_{j}(p^{k})-p^{k-1}P_{j}(p^{k-l})

\displaystyle=p^{k}\big(P_{j}(p^{k})-p^{-1}P_{j}(p^{k-1})\big),

and

T_{j}(p^{k})=\left\{\begin{array}[]{l l}e-P_{j}(p)\quad\,\text{if}\quad k=1,\\
0\quad\,\text{otherwise}.\end{array}\right.

Since
C_{j}
is multiplicative we have

\displaystyle C_{j}(n)

\displaystyle=\prod_{l=1}^{N}C_{j}(p^{\alpha_{l}})=n\prod_{l=1}^{N}\big(P_{j}(p^{\alpha_{l}})-\frac{1}{p}P_{j}(p^{\alpha_{l}-1}).

Similarly,

T_{j}(n)=\prod_{\begin{subarray}{c}p\mid n\\
p\,\,\text{prime}\end{subarray}}(e-P_{j}(p))

∎

Theorem 3.3
.

For fixed integers
n
and
j
,
\{T_{r,j}(n):r\mid n\}
is a set of
\tau(n)
orthgonal idempotent:

(3.7)

\sum_{r\mid n}T_{r,j}(n)=e\quad\text{and}\quad T_{r,j}(n)T_{r^{\prime},j}(n)=\delta_{r,r^{\prime}}T_{r,j}(n)

where
\tau(n)
is the number of divisor of
n.

Lemma 3.4
.

The operators
T_{j}(n)
and
C_{j}(n)
are related by

C_{j}(n)=\sum_{r\mid n}c_{n}(n/r)T_{r,j}(n),\quad T_{n,j}(n)=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{r\mid n}c_{n}(n/r)\,C_{j}(r).

Proof.

(3.8)

\displaystyle\sum_{r\mid n}c_{n}(n/r)T_{r,j}(n)

\displaystyle=\sum_{r\mid n}c_{n}(n/r)\sum_{\gcd(k,n)=\tfrac{n}{r}}P_{k+j}(n)

(3.9)

\displaystyle=\sum_{l=1}^{n}\sum_{r\mid n}c_{n}(n/r)\sum_{\gcd(k,n)=\tfrac{n}{r}}\varepsilon_{n}^{-(k+j)l}S_{n}^{l}

(3.10)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{l=1}^{n}\varepsilon_{n}^{-lj}\sum_{r\mid n}c_{n}(n/r)c_{r}(l)S_{n}^{l}

From formula Exercise 2.23 in
[
3
]
, we have

(3.11)

\sum_{r\mid n}c_{n}(n/r)c_{r}(l)=\left\{\begin{array}[]{l l}n\quad\,\text{if}\quad\gcd(l,n)=1,\\
0\quad\text{if}\quad\gcd(l,n)>1.\end{array}\right.

Hence

(3.12)

\displaystyle\sum_{r\mid n}c_{n}(n/r)T_{r,j}(r)=\sum_{\gcd(l,n)=1}\varepsilon_{n}^{-lj}S_{n}^{l}=C_{j}(n).

Similarly,

(3.13)

\displaystyle\sum_{r\mid n}c_{n}(n/r)C_{j}(r)

\displaystyle=\sum_{r\mid n}c_{n}(n/r)\sum_{\gcd(k,n)=\frac{n}{r}}\varepsilon_{n}^{-jk}S_{n}^{k}

(3.14)

\displaystyle=\sum_{l=1}^{n}\sum_{r\mid n}c_{n}(n/r)c_{r}(l-j)P_{l}(n)

(3.15)

\displaystyle=\sum_{l=1}^{n}\sum_{r\mid n}c_{n}(n/r)c_{r}(l)P_{l+j}(n)

The result follows from formula (see, Exercise 2.23
[
3
]
)

(3.16)

\sum_{r\mid n}c_{n}(\tfrac{n}{r})c_{r}(k)=\left\{\begin{array}[]{l l}n\quad\,\text{if}\quad\gcd(k,n)=1,\\
0\quad\text{if}\quad\gcd(k,n)>1.\end{array}\right.

Hence

(3.17)

\displaystyle\sum_{r\mid n}c_{n}(n/r)C_{j}(r)=n\sum_{\gcd(k,n)=1}P_{k+j}(n)=nT_{n,j}(n).

∎

Theorem 3.5
.

Let
\alpha:\mathbb{N}\rightarrow\mathbb{C}
be even function
(mod\,\,n)
, then for all
j=0,\,1,\,\dots,
, we have

(3.18)

\sum_{r\mid n}\alpha(n/r)C_{j}(r)=\sum_{r\mid n}\mathcal{R}(\alpha)(r)T_{r,j}(n).

Proof.

\displaystyle\sum_{r\mid n}\alpha(r)C_{j}(r)

\displaystyle=\sum_{r\mid n}\sum_{\delta\mid r}c_{r}(r/\delta)T_{\delta,j}(r)\,\alpha(n/r)

\displaystyle=\sum_{r\mid n}\sum_{\delta\mid r}c_{r}(r/\delta)\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\gcd(k,r)=\tfrac{r}{\delta}\\
1\leq k\leq r\end{subarray}}P_{k+j}(r)\,\alpha(n/r)

\displaystyle=\sum_{r\mid n}\alpha(n/r)\sum_{k=1}^{r}c_{r}(k)P_{k+j}(r)

The Ramanujan sum
c_{r}
is periodic function with period equal to
r
, then from condition
III)
of the Definition 2.2, we can write

\displaystyle\sum_{k=1}^{r}c_{r}(k)P_{k+j}(r)

\displaystyle=\sum_{k=1}^{r}\sum_{l=0}^{n/r-1}c_{r}(k+lr)P_{k+lr+j}(r)

\displaystyle=\sum_{k=1}^{n}c_{r}(k)P_{k+j}(n)

\displaystyle=\sum_{\delta\mid n}c_{r}(n/\delta)\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\gcd(k,n)=\tfrac{n}{\delta}\\
1\leq k\leq k\end{subarray}}P_{k+j}(n)

\displaystyle=\sum_{\delta\mid n}c_{r}(n/\delta)T_{\delta,j}(n).

Hence

\displaystyle\sum_{r\mid n}\alpha(r)C_{j}(r)

\displaystyle=\sum_{\delta\mid n}\sum_{r\mid n}c_{r}(n/\delta)\alpha(n/r)T_{\delta,j}(n)

\displaystyle=\sum_{\delta\mid n}\mathcal{R}(\alpha)(\delta)T_{\delta,j}(n).

∎

4.
Example

We denote by
H_{R}
the vector space of all analytic functions on the open ball

B(0,R)
in complex plane
\mathbb{C}
.
H_{R}
endowed with the topology of compact convergence, it is a complete locally convex topological vector spaces.

For
n=2,\,3,\,\dots,
we denote by
S(n)
the diagonal operator acting on monomials
