Classical solutions of a mean field system for pulse-coupled oscillators: long time asymptotics versus blowup
José A. Carrillo, Xu’an Dou, Pierre Roux and Zhennan Zhou

Abstract
We introduce a novel reformulation of the mean-field system for pulse-coupled oscillators. It is based on writing a closed equation for the inverse distribution function associated to the probability density of oscillators with a given phase in a suitable time scale. This new framework allows to show a hidden contraction/expansion of certain distances leading to a full clarification of the long-time behavior, existence of steady states, rates of convergence, and finite time blow-up of classical solutions for a large class of monotone phase response functions. In the process, we get insights about the origin of obstructions to global-in-time existence and uniform in time estimates on the firing rate of the oscillators.



Keywords:
pulse-coupled oscillators, mean field equation, long time behavior, blow-up

Introduction

Systems of pulse-coupled oscillators model synchronization by singular interactions occurring as phase jumps changing in a particular pulsatile manner. They have numerous applications in science and engineering (e.g.
[
49
,
46
,
43
,
26
,
50
,
27
,
44
,
59
]
). Yet their unique singular coupling mechanism renders them challenging to analyze. A partial differential equation (PDE) for pulse-coupled oscillators, which characterizes the nonlinear interaction at a continuous level, has been derived in physics literature 30 years ago by a formal mean-field argument
[
32
,
1

. However, it still lacks a systematic mathematical treatment. In this work, we study the mean-field equation from a novel reformulation, which reveals several hidden structures of the equation, and facilitates the study on its basic properties, including well-posedness, blow-up and long time convergence to the steady state.

1.1
The particle system

Pulse-coupled oscillators, as a particle system, describe a finite number of oscillators coupled in a pulselike manner. Here we present a model following
[
48
,
39
]
. Consider
\mathcal{N}
oscillators (
\mathcal{N}\in\mathbb{N}_{+}
), each described by a phase variable
\phi_{i}(t)
, for time
t\geq 0
and
i=1,2,...,\mathcal{N}
. The phase variable
\phi_{i}
takes value in
[0,\Phi_{F}]
, where
\Phi_{F}>0
is the firing phase. When all
\phi_{i}\in[0,\Phi_{F})
, i.e., strictly less than
\Phi_{F}
, each
\phi_{i}
is subject to its own dynamics, which is here assumed to be the simple ordinary differential equation (ODE)

\frac{d}{dt}\phi_{i}(t)=1,\hskip 18.49988pti=1,2,...,\mathcal{N}.

(1.1)

An oscillator (
\phi_{i}
) reaching
\Phi_{F}
constitutes a firing or spiking event, which has two consequences. For
\phi_{i}
itself, it is immediately reset to
0
, i.e.

\phi_{i}(t^{-})=\Phi_{F},\hskip 9.24994pt\Rightarrow\hskip 9.24994pt\phi_{i}(t)=0.

(1.2)

Also, the firing of
\phi_{i}
induces an immediate phase jump in other oscillators, and the phase jump magnitude depends on the recipient’s phase. More specifically, we have

\phi_{i}(t^{-})=\Phi_{F},\hskip 9.24994pt\Rightarrow\hskip 9.24994pt\phi_{j}(t)=\phi_{j}(t^{-})+\frac{K(\phi_{j}(t^{-}))}{\mathcal{N}},\hskip 9.24994ptj\neq i.

(1.3)

Here the function
K(\phi)
reflects how oscillators at different phases respond
differently
to the same spike. We call
K(\phi)
the phase response function, which is related to the infinitesimal phase response curve (iPRC) in some literature (e.g.
[
35
]
). In the induced jump size in (
1.3
), the factor
\frac{1}{\mathcal{N}}
is a scaling to ensure (at least formally) that there is a meaningful mean-field limit, i.e. the limit when the number of particles
\mathcal{N}
goes to infinity. Throughout this work we assume
K(\phi)>0
, which implies that oscillators at lower phases become closer to
\Phi_{F}
after receiving a spike. Note that
\phi_{j}(t)
in (
1.3
) may exceed
\Phi_{F}
, in which case
\phi_{j}
will also spike at the same time
t
. This can cause more oscillators to spike at the same time in a chain-reaction manner
[
39
,
15
]
, which we will however not discuss in details here.

In (
1.1
)-(
1.3
), the oscillators interact with each other only through the sudden impulse (
1.3
) when firing events occur. This singular interaction mechanism is in accordance with the name pulse-coupled oscillators. We also note that the phase response function
K(\phi)
can already induce intricate dynamics, despite that here the inter-spike dynamics (
1.1
) appears simple.

The particle system in the form of (
1.1
)-(
1.3
) was proposed by Peskin in 1975
[
48
]
, whose original motivation was to model pacemaker cells in the heart. Nowadays, pulse-coupled oscillators have found widespread applications, both in modeling various natural phenomena, including flashing fireflies
[
49
,
46
]
, spiking neurons
[
20
,
19
,
22
,
12
]
and earthquakes
[
43
,
26
]
, as well as in engineering fields, including clustering algorithms
[
50
]
and wireless sensor networks
[
27
,
44
,
59
]
. These diverse applications share the common feature of the pulselike coupling (
1.2
)-(
1.3
). For instance, in the context of neurons, (
1.1
)-(
1.3
) gives a network of the so-called integrate-and-fire neurons
[
20
,
19
,
22
,
12
]
, where
\phi_{i}
represents a rescaled version of a neuron’s voltage (membrane potential). The interaction here is through the spike (
1.3
), triggered when a neuron’s voltage reaches the threshold
\Phi_{F}
(
1.2
), followed by a reset of the voltage to
0
(
1.2
).

While the pulselike interaction (
1.3
) is relevant for various applications, its discontinuous nature also contributes to the challenges for the mathematical analysis of (
1.1
)-(
1.3
). In 1990, Mirollo and Strogatz
[

proved that when the phase response function

is monotonically increasing, the particle system will converge to a perfect synchronization state in finite time, for almost every initial data. A key idea was to introduce a tool called the

, which became a popular framework in the analysis of such systems. The seminal work
[

]
initiated a wide range of studies on (
1.1
)-(
1.3
) in various settings and extensions, including decreasing
K

[
36
]
, delays
[
21
]
, refractory periods
[
29
]
, noise
[

,
23
]
, non-identical oscillator
[
56
]
and graph structures
[
42
,
34
]
. However, there are still many problems open even for the simple model (
1.1
)-(
1.3
) (e.g.
[

]

1.2
The mean-field continuity equation

A powerful approach to study the particle system (
1.1
)-(
1.3
) is to take the mean-field limit, seeking statistical descriptions when the number of particles
\mathcal{N}
goes to infinity. PDEs characterize the limit of the system, for which continuous tools can be applicable to gain insights and to make the problem tractable for analysis. Such an approach has been widely used in many fields, from statistical physics to biology and social science
[
8
,
31
,
45
,
28
]
.

For the pulse-coupled oscillator (
1.1
)-(
1.3
), the state of the mean-field system is represented by a distribution function
\rho(t,\phi)
, where
\rho(t,\cdot)
is the phase distribution for each time
t
, and the spike activity is characterized by a mean firing rate
N(t)
, the number of spikes per unit time. The dynamics of
(\rho,N)
is governed by the following PDE system, which has been formally derived in physics literature
[
32
,
1
,
38
]
,

\displaystyle\partial_{t}\rho+\partial_{\phi}\bigl([1+K(\phi)N(t)]\rho\bigr)=0,

\displaystyle t>0,\ \phi\in(0,\Phi_{F}),

(1.4)

\displaystyle N(t)=[1+K(\Phi_{F})N(t)]\rho(t,\Phi_{F}),

\displaystyle t>0,

(1.5)

\displaystyle[1+K(0)N(t)]\rho(t,0)=[1+K(\Phi_{F})N(t)]\rho(t,\Phi_{F}),

\displaystyle t>0,

(1.6)

\displaystyle\rho(t=0,\phi)=\rho_{\init}(\phi),

\displaystyle\phi\in[0,\Phi_{F}].

(1.7)

Equation (
1.4
) is a continuity equation describing the transport of density
\rho
with the velocity field
\left(1+K(\phi)N(t)\right)
, for
\phi\in(0,\Phi_{F})
, where the
K(\phi)N(t)
term reflects the pulse-coupled interaction (
1.3
). The phase response function
K(\phi)
describes how oscillators at different phases respond to the same stimulus differently. The basic assumptions on
K
along this work can be summarized as

Assumption 1
.

The phase response function
K
satisfies

\displaystyle K\in C^{2}(\mathbb{R})\hskip 18.49988pt\text{and $K^{\prime},K^{\prime\prime}$ are bounded on $\mathbb{R}$,}

(1.8)

\displaystyle K(\phi)>0,\hskip 18.49988pt\phi\in[0,\Phi_{F}].

(1.9)

Note that while physically
K(\phi)
is only defined on
[0,\Phi_{F}]
, it is sometimes mathematically convenient to extend it as a function on
\mathbb{R}
. A simple example is
K(\phi)=k\phi+b
with some constants
k,b
. The phase response function
K
has a crucial influence on the dynamics. In fact, we shall see increasing or decreasing (in
\phi
) phase response functions
K
lead to drastically different qualitative behaviors.

The firing rate
N(t)
is defined via the out-going boundary flux at
\Phi_{F}
in (
1.5
), since a particle spikes when it reaches
\Phi_{F}
(
1.2
)-(
1.3
). As the velocity field itself also depends on
N(t)
, (
1.5
) is a self-consistent equation, from which we can derive an expression of
N(t)
in terms of the boundary value
\rho(t,\Phi_{F})

N(t)=\frac{\rho(t,\Phi_{F})}{1-K(\Phi_{F})\rho(t,\Phi_{F})}.\hskip 9.24994pt

(1.10)

We note that
N(t)
is vital to the dynamics of
(\rho,N)
. To ensure physical values for the firing rate
N(t)\in[0,+\infty)
, by (
1.10
) the following constraint on
\rho(t,\Phi_{F})
is needed

0\leq\rho(t,\Phi_{F})<\frac{1}{K(\Phi_{F})}.

(1.11)

The case
\rho(t,\Phi_{F})\geq\frac{1}{K(\Phi_{F})}
is interpreted as
N(t)=+\infty
, the blow-up of
N(t)
.

Due to the boundary condition (
1.6
), the flux at
\phi=0
matches that at
\phi=\Phi_{F}
, since a particle is reset at
\phi=0
after a spike at
\phi=\Phi_{F}
(
1.2
). This reset process gives the conservation of mass for the system (
1.4
)

\int_{0}^{\Phi_{F}}\rho(t,\phi)d\phi=\int_{0}^{\Phi_{F}}\rho_{\init}(\phi)d\phi=1,

where we assume the initial data
\rho_{\init}(\phi)
is a probability density on
[0,\Phi_{F}]
. Note that as
K(0)
is not necessarily equal to
K(\Phi_{F})
, and thus (
1.6
) does not imply a periodic boundary condition in
\rho
.

Despite being derived formally in the nineties
[
32
,
1
]
, the mathematical literature on (
1.4
)-(
1.7
) is scarce. In physics and engineering literature, most studies focus on local linearized analysis around steady states for various extensions of (
1.4
)-(
1.7
) (e.g.
[

,

,

,
3
,

). A pioneering work is
[

by Mauroy and Sepulchre in 2012. They constructed a Lyapunov function and showed some global qualitative behaviors for (
1.4
)-(
1.7
) in certain regimes. In contrast, there is a much richer literature on

oscillators (e.g.
[
54
,
2
,
17
,
24
,
30
]
), where the interaction is in a smooth and in some cases more symmetric way compared to the

oscillators considered here.

More recently, some closely related models from neuroscience have attracted many mathematical studies, from both PDE
[
5
,
11
,
51
]
and probability
[
15
,
33
,
13
]
perspectives, which also connect to models in finance
[

]
and physics
[

]

[

]
for recent advances). One of our initial motivations towards (

)-(
1.7
) is an asymptotic limit for the voltage-conductance equation, another PDE from neuroscience
[

7

1.3
Our work: reformulation and main results

In this work, we revisit the mean-field equation (
1.4
)-(
1.7
) from a PDE perspective, aiming to derive a suitable framework for a rigorous PDE analysis. We aim to do so with a
reformulation
, which reveals hidden structures, leads to fruitful results, and opens new directions.

The reformulation consists of two steps. First, instead of working with the probability density
\rho(t,\phi)
, we consider the quantile function
Q(t,\eta)
, also called the pseudo-inverse of the cumulative distribution function
F(t,\phi)
associated with
\rho
. These relations can be informally illustrated as (see Section
2.1
for full rigorous details)

F(t,\phi)=\int_{0}^{\phi}\rho(t,\tilde{\phi})d\tilde{\phi},\hskip 18.49988pt\phi\in[0,\Phi_{F}],\hskip 18.49988pt\hskip 18.49988pt\hskip 18.49988ptF(t,Q(t,\eta))=\eta,\hskip 18.49988pt\eta\in[0,1].

(1.12)

The pseudo-inverse reformulation has been applied to many other PDEs (e.g.
[
41
,
10
,
6
,
9
]
), in particular in the presence of the Dirac masses. In the context of pulse-coupled oscillators, the pseudo-inverse
Q
has been used in the pioneering work
[
38
]
to construct a Lyapunov function. Yet a self-contained equation for
Q
itself was not explored before.

Second, instead of working in the original timescale
t
, we introduce a new timescale
\tau
, called the dilated timescale, which relates to the firing rate
N(t)
as follows

d\tau=N(t)dt.

(1.13)

The classical framework to study the particle system (
1.1
)-(
1.3
) focuses on the firing map
[
39
]
, that is, by evolving the system from a spike time to the next spike time, regardless of the elapsed time
t
in between. The use of the dilated timescale
\tau
can be understood as a continuous abstract analogy of this firing map. Note that if
N(t)
is a sum of Dirac deltas at the spiking times of a finite number of particles, then the dilated time scale
\tau
is a piecewise constant non-decreasing function of
t
. The idea of using a firing-rate related timescale was introduced more recently in
[
55
,
52
]
and
[
18
]
, for related models which can be seen as noisy variants of (
1.4
)-(
1.7
). Here we adopt the terminology “dilated timescale” from
[
18
]
. The primary motivation to introduce the new timescale in
[
55
,
52
,
18
]
was to define solutions beyond the blow-up of
N(t)
. Nevertheless, this work shows that the dilated timescale is also useful even in the classical regime with finite
N(t)
.

With these two ingredients, we reformulate (
1.4
)-(
1.7
), the system for
\rho
in
t
timescale, as the following system for
Q
in
\tau
timescale (see Section
2
)

\displaystyle\partial_{\tau}Q+\partial_{\eta}Q=\frac{1}{N(\tau)}+{K}(Q),

\displaystyle\tau>0,\ \eta\in(0,1),

(1.14)

\displaystyle Q(\tau,0)=0,

\displaystyle\tau>0,

(1.15)

\displaystyle\frac{1}{N(\tau)}=\partial_{\eta}Q(\tau,1)-{K}(\Phi_{F}),

\displaystyle\tau>0,

(1.16)

\displaystyle Q(\tau=0,\eta)=Q_{\init}(\eta),

\displaystyle\eta\in[0,1].

(1.17)

Here, a counterpart of (
1.11
) to ensure
N(\tau)<+\infty
is

K(\Phi_{F})<\partial_{\eta}Q(\tau,1)<+\infty.

(1.18)

Many advantages emerge with the reformulation (
1.14
)-(
1.15
)-(
1.16
)-(
1.17
).
In the reformulation, the nonlinear coupling through the firing rate is isolated, as

appears in the right-hand side of (
1.14
). Indeed, we can further obtain an alternative characterization of
1/N(\tau)
, as a Lagrangian multiplier to ensure a hidden constraint

(Proposition
2.2
). Moreover, at a technical level, the characteristics of (
1.14

\partial_{\tau}+\partial_{\eta}
are moving with a constant speed compared to (
1.4
), which facilitates analysis.

(Well-posedness, Section

Under suitable compatibility conditions on the initial data, there exists a unique classical solution to (

(i) (Dichotomy on the steady state, Proposition

(i) Suppose

38
]
. At the particle system level, dynamics with monotone phase response function
K
are studied in
[
36
,
37
]
for
K^{\prime}<0
together with convexity/concavity assumptions on
K
, and in
[
39
]
for
K^{\prime}>0
.
Our results supersedes these previous works for classical solutions of the mean-field equation (
1.4
)-(
1.7
). In particular, the blow-up of every solution, when (
1.22
) is violated, is new at the mean-field level.

Summarizing, the main contribution of this work, is to build a rigorous framework to analyze the mean-field equation for pulse-coupled oscillators, based on the new
reformulation
: the pseudo-inverse
Q
and the dilated timescale
\tau
. Such a framework is
natural
in our opinion as new structures are revealed and fruitful results can be formulated in a convenient way and improved from existing literature. It is worth emphasizing that the key stability Theorem
1.2
, which gives much information on the qualitative behavior, relies deeply on the reformulation both in its statement and proof. There is much more unknown concerning the mean-field equation, including but not restricted to the dynamics of general non-monotone
K
and beyond the blow-up, let alone its generalizations motivated from various fields of science and engineering.

The rest of this paper is arranged as follows: In Section
2
, we derive the reformulation and prove its basic properties. Section
3
is devoted to studying the well-posedness, where we prove Theorem
1.1
. Section
4
focuses on proving the key stability estimate, establishing Theorem

2
Pseudo-inverse formulation in dilated timescale

In this section, we introduce a new formulation for the mean-field system of pulse-coupled oscillators, whose analysis is the main goal of this work.

We start with the mean-field system (
1.4
)-(
1.7
) for the distribution in the phase variable
\rho(t,\phi)
. Recall that Assumption
1
is supposed throughout this paper. Moreover, to ensure physical values for the firing rate, the constraint (
1.11
) is imposed. We define classical solutions to (
1.4
)-(
1.7
) as follows.

Definition 2.1

(Classical solution to (
1.4
)-(
1.7
))
.

Given
0<T\leq+\infty
, initial data
\rho_{\init}(\phi)
which is a
C^{1}
probability density with
\rho_{\init}(\Phi_{F})<1/K(\Phi_{F})
, we say
(\rho,N)
is a classical solution to (
1.4
)-(
1.7
) on
[0,T)
if

1.

\rho(t,\phi)\in C^{1}([0,T)\times[0,\Phi_{F}])
and
N(t)\in C[0,T)
are non-negative.

2.

Equations (
1.4
)-(
1.7
) are satisfied in the classical sense for
t\in[0,T)
.

3.

The constraint (
1.11
)
0\leq\rho(t,\Phi_{F})<{1}/{K(\Phi_{F})}
holds for all
t\in[0,T)
.

The new formulation for (
1.4
)-(
1.7
) consists of two key ingredients: the pseudo-inverse function and the time-dilation, which are introduced next.

2.1
Reformulation in terms of pseudo-inverses

Given a probability distribution
\mu
on
[0,\Phi_{F}]
, we define its (right-continuous) cumulative function by

F(\phi)=\mu\bigl([0,\phi]\bigr),\hskip 9.24994pt\phi\in[0,\Phi_{F}].

(2.1)

In general
F
may not be either continuous or strictly increasing, but we can always define its pseudo-inverse via

Q(\eta)=\inf\{\phi\geq 0:F(\phi)\geq\eta\},\hskip 18.49988pt\eta\in[0,1].

(2.2)

Here we focus on the classical case when
\mu
has a positive density function
\rho
, and then
Q
is indeed the regular inverse of
F
. See Figure
1
for an illustration of the general case.

Figure 1:
Illustration of the relations between the probability distribution
\mu
(or its density
\rho
), the cumulative function
F
and the pseudo-inverse of the cumulative function
Q
. Upper left: Plot of the density
\rho
with a red circle indicating a Dirac mass part of
\mu
(here the size of the Dirac mass is
0.3
). Lower left: Plot of the cumulative function
F
. Right: Plot of the pseudo-inverse

. In all three figures, the red solid line indicates the Dirac mass part, which manifests as a jump in

and a constant part in

. The orange dashed line represents the vacuum part, where the density is zero, resulting in a flat segment in
F
that induces a jump in
Q
. And the blue dash-dotted line indicates the regular part where there is no Dirac mass, and the density is positive.

We carry on such reformulations for (
1.4
)-(
1.7
), assuming that
\rho(t,\phi)
is positive everywhere. We define for each
t
the cumulative function via

F(t,\phi)=\int_{0}^{\phi}\rho(t,\tilde{\phi})d\tilde{\phi},\hskip 18.49988pt\phi\in[0,\Phi_{F}].

(2.3)

Then for each
t
we can define the pseudo-inverse for the cumulative function by

Q(t,\eta)=\inf\{\phi\geq 0:F(t,\phi)\geq\eta\},\hskip 18.49988pt\eta\in[0,1].

(2.4)

Due to the positivity of
\rho
,
F(t,\cdot)
is indeed strictly increasing and
C^{1}
. Therefore
Q(t,\cdot)
is actually its
C^{1}
inverse, satisfying

F(t,Q(t,\eta))=\eta,\hskip 18.49988pt\eta\in[0,1],

(2.5)

\partial_{\phi}F(t,\phi)=\rho(t,\phi),\hskip 9.24994pt\phi\in[0,\Phi_{F}],

(2.6)

and

\partial_{\eta}Q(t,\eta)=\frac{1}{\partial_{\phi}F(t,Q)}=\frac{1}{\rho(t,Q(t,\eta))},\hskip 9.24994pt\eta\in[0,1].

(2.7)

Using the chain rule and the boundary conditions, it is not difficult to obtain

\partial_{t}Q=(1+{K}(Q)N(t))-N(t)\partial_{\eta}Q,\hskip 9.24994ptt>0,\eta\in(0,1).

(2.8)

The definition of
Q
(
2.4
) implies a boundary condition at
\eta=0

Q(t,0)=0,\hskip 9.24994ptt>0.

(2.9)

To close the system, we need to represent
N(t)
in terms of
Q
. Thanks to the assumption that
\rho
is positive everywhere, we have

Q(t,\eta=1)=\Phi_{F},\hskip 9.24994ptt\geq 0.

(2.10)

Therefore using (
2.7
) we get

\rho(t,\Phi_{F})=\rho(t,Q(t,\eta=1))=\frac{1}{\partial_{\eta}Q(t,1)}.

Hence by (
1.10
) we obtain

N(t)=\frac{\rho(t,\Phi_{F})}{1-{K}(\Phi_{F})\rho(t,\Phi_{F})}=\frac{1}{\partial_{\eta}Q(t,1)-{K}(\Phi_{F})}.

(2.11)

To summarize we have derived from (
1.4
)-(
1.7
) a system for the pseudo-inverse function
Q(t,\eta)

\displaystyle\partial_{t}Q+N(t)\partial_{\eta}Q=1+{K}(Q)N(t),\hskip 9.24994pt

\displaystyle t>0,\ \eta\in(0,1),

(2.12)

\displaystyle Q(t,0)=0,\hskip 9.24994pt

\displaystyle t>0,

\displaystyle N(t)=\frac{1}{\partial_{\eta}Q(t,1)-{K}(\Phi_{F})},\hskip 9.24994pt

\displaystyle t>0,

which is complemented by the initial data

Q(t=0,\eta)=Q_{\init}(\eta),\hskip 9.24994pt\eta\in[0,1].

(2.13)

We need an additional constraint on
\partial_{\eta}Q(t,1)
to ensure that
0<N(t)<+\infty
in (
2.11
)

K(\Phi_{F})<\partial_{\eta}Q(t,1)<+\infty.

(2.14)

Here
\partial_{\eta}Q(t,1)>K(\Phi_{F})
is the same as (
1.11
), which ensures
N(t)<+\infty
. We also impose
\partial_{\eta}Q(t,1)<+\infty
, which gives
N(t)>0
and
\rho(t,\Phi_{F})>0
, aligning with our assumption that
\rho
is positive everywhere.

2.2
Time-dilation and some basic properties

Next, we rewrite the system in the dilated timescale
\tau
with

d\tau=N(t)dt.

(2.15)

This change of time is invertible when
0<N<+\infty
. By dividing the equation (
2.8
) by

, we can derive the system in
\tau
(
1.14
)-(
1.18

\displaystyle\partial_{\tau}Q+\partial_{\eta}Q=\frac{1}{N(\tau)}+{K}(Q),

\displaystyle\tau>0,\ \eta\in(0,1),

(2.16)

\displaystyle Q(\tau,0)=0,

\displaystyle\tau>0,

(2.17)

\displaystyle\frac{1}{N(\tau)}=\partial_{\eta}Q(\tau,1)-{K}(\Phi_{F}),

\displaystyle\tau>0,

(2.18)

\displaystyle Q(\tau=0,\eta)=Q_{\init}(\eta),

\displaystyle\eta\in[0,1].

(2.19)

with the constraint to ensure that

given by

K(\Phi_{F})<\partial_{\eta}Q(\tau,1)<+\infty.

(2.20)

We shall show that the new timescale

Equation (
2.16

2.17

\eta=0
. Its nonlinearity arises from two factors. Firstly the phase response function

can be nonlinear. The second source of nonlinearity is the term

depends on the boundary derivative, and the constraint (

, reflects the pulse-coupled interaction between oscillators, and contributes to a major challenge of this system.

Remark 2.1
.

The idea of introducing a firing-rate-dependent timescale has been used by
[
55
,
52
]
and
[
18
]
for different but related models. Their main motivation was to define generalized solutions allowing the blow-up of

. Formally, when

is large, a small time interval in

[

Similar to Definition

)-(

Definition 2.2

(Classical solution to (

)-(

))

Given
0<T\leq+\infty

\frac{d}{d\eta}Q_{\init}(\eta=1)>K(\Phi_{F})

(Q,N)
is a classical solution to (

2.19

2.16

Proposition 2.1
.

Suppose

\partial_{\eta}Q(\tau,0)=\frac{1}{N(\tau)}+K(0).

(2.21)

If additionally (

\partial_{\eta}Q(\tau,1)-\partial_{\eta}Q(\tau,0)=K(\Phi_{F})-K(0).

(2.22)

Proof.

The regularity of
Q
ensures that the equation holds at
\eta=0
, resulting in

\displaystyle\partial_{\tau}Q(\tau,0)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{N(\tau)}+K(Q(\tau,0))-\partial_{\eta}Q(\tau,0)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{N(\tau)}+K(0)-\partial_{\eta}Q(\tau,0),

(2.23)

where the boundary condition (
2.17

2.17

, that combined with (

2.21

Now suppose additionally

(\partial_{\tau}+\partial_{\eta})(\partial_{\eta}Q)=K^{\prime}(Q)\partial_{\eta}{Q}.

Therefore we conclude that

\displaystyle(\partial_{\tau}+\partial_{\eta})Z=K^{\prime}(Q)Z,\hskip 9.24994pt

\displaystyle\tau\in(0,T),\eta\in(0,1),

\displaystyle Z(\tau,0)=\frac{1}{N(\tau)}+K(0)>0,\hskip 9.24994pt

Remark 2.2
.

The

.

Suppose

\displaystyle 0\equiv\partial_{\tau}Q(\tau,1)

3
Well-posedness via a relaxed problem

To state the well-posedness result for classical solutions, we need the following assumption on initial data.

Assumption 2
.

We assume that the initial data
Q_{\init}\in C^{1}[0,1]
is non-decreasing and satisfies
Q_{\init}(0)=0,Q_{\init}(1)=\Phi_{F}

\frac{d}{d\eta}Q_{\init}(1)>K(\Phi_{F}),\hskip 18.49988pt\frac{d}{d\eta}Q_{\init}(1)-\frac{d}{d\eta}Q_{\init}(0)=K(\Phi_{F})-K(0).

(3.1)

Remark 3.1

Assumption

2.1

\displaystyle\frac{d}{d\eta}Q_{\init}(1)=\frac{1}{N_{\init}}+K(\Phi_{F}),\hskip 18.49988pt\frac{d}{d\eta}Q_{\init}(0)=\frac{1}{N_{\init}}+K(0).

For the phase response function

is supposed throughout this paper.

Now we state the main results of this section. First we have the existence and uniqueness of a classical solution to (
2.16
)-(
2.19
), up to a maximal existence time

, which can be viewed as the first blow-up time of

Theorem 3.1
.

Suppose the initial data satisfies Assumption
2
. Then there exists a unique classical solution
(Q,N)
to (
2.16
)-(
2.19
) as in Definition
2.2

[0,\tau^{*})
, where
0<\tau_{*}\leq+\infty
is the maximal existence time.

In addition,
\tau_{*}
can be viewed as the first blow-up time of
N(\tau)
. More precisely, if
\tau^{*}<+\infty
then

\lim_{\tau\rightarrow(\tau^{*})^{-}}N(\tau)=+\infty.

(3.2)

Next, we show that the solution depends continuously on the initial data in
C^{1}
norm, up to the first blow-up time.

Theorem 3.2
.

Let
Q_{\init,\varepsilon}
be a family of initial data indexed by
\varepsilon\geq 0
, satisfying Assumption
2
, and such that

Q_{\init,\varepsilon}(\cdot)\rightarrow Q_{\init,0}(\cdot),\hskip 18.49988pt\text{in $C^{1}[0,1]$,}\hskip 18.49988pt\text{as $\varepsilon\rightarrow 0^{+}.$}

(3.3)

Denote the corresponding classical solution to (
2.16
)-(
2.19
) as
(Q_{\varepsilon},N_{\varepsilon})
with maximal existence times
\tau^{*}_{\varepsilon}
, as in Theorem
3.1
. In particular,
Q_{0},N_{0}
and
\tau^{*}_{0}
correspond to the initial data
Q_{\init,0}
. Then we have

\liminf_{\varepsilon\rightarrow 0^{+}}\tau^{*}_{\varepsilon}\geq\tau^{*}_{0}.

(3.4)

Moreover, for every
0<T<\tau^{*}_{0}
, we have

N_{\varepsilon}(\tau)\rightarrow N_{0}(\tau)\hskip 18.49988pt\text{in $C[0,T]$,}\hskip 18.49988pt\text{as $\varepsilon\rightarrow 0^{+},$}

(3.5)

and

Q_{\varepsilon}(T,\cdot)\rightarrow Q_{0}(T,\cdot)\hskip 18.49988pt\text{in $C^{1}[0,1]$,}\hskip 18.49988pt\text{as $\varepsilon\rightarrow 0^{+}.$}

(3.6)

Finally, we can show
C^{2}
regularity of
Q
, provided that the initial data satisfies the following additional assumption.

Assumption 3
.

We assume
Q_{\init}(\eta)\in C^{2}[0,1]
with

\frac{d^{2}}{d\eta^{2}}Q_{\init}(1)-\frac{d^{2}}{d\eta^{2}}Q_{\init}(0)=K^{\prime}(\Phi_{F})\frac{d}{d\eta}Q_{\init}(1)-K^{\prime}(0)\frac{d}{d\eta}Q_{\init}(0).

(3.7)

Remark 3.2
.

Equation (
3.7
) is a second order condition for the initial data to be compatible with the boundary conditions (c.f. the first order condition (
3.1
) in Assumption
2
). It will be explained later in Section

.

Theorem 3.3
.

In the same setting as Theorem
3.1
, suppose the initial data additionally satisfies Assumption
3
. Then we have

N(\tau)\in C^{1}[0,\tau^{*})

and
Q\in C^{2}([0,\tau^{*})\times[0,1])
.

3.1
The relaxed problem

3.1.1
Definition and basic propetries

A main challenge of (
2.16
) is the term
1/N(\tau)
, which is required to be positive in (
2.20
). To study the well-posedness, we first consider an auxiliary problem where this positive constraint is removed. More precisely, we introduce
\tilde{N}(\tau)\in\mathbb{R}
in place of
1/N(\tau)>0
. The relaxed system is given as follows

\displaystyle\partial_{\tau}Q+\partial_{\eta}Q=\tilde{N}(\tau)+{K}(Q),

\displaystyle\tau>0,\ \eta\in(0,1),

(3.8)

\displaystyle Q(\tau,0)=0,

\displaystyle\tau>0,

(3.9)

\displaystyle{\tilde{N}(\tau)}=\partial_{\eta}Q(\tau,1)-{K}(\Phi_{F}),

\displaystyle\tau>0,

(3.10)

\displaystyle Q(\tau=0,\eta)=Q_{\init}(\eta),

\displaystyle\eta\in[0,1].

(3.11)

Compared to the original system (
2.16
), the only difference is that we use
\tilde{N}(\tau)
, which is allowed to take any value in
\mathbb{R}
, to replace
1/N(\tau)>0
. Hence, under such a relaxation, we no longer need the constraint (
2.20
) on
\partial_{\eta}Q
. While a negative
\tilde{N}
is not physical, it is convenient to study the well-posedness for the relaxed problem (
3.8
)-(
3.9
)-(
3.10
)-(
3.11
) as a first step.

The definition of a classical solution to (
3.8
)-(
3.11
) is given as follows.

Definition 3.1

(Classical solution to the relaxed problem (

)-(
3.11
))
.

Given
0<T\leq+\infty
, and
C^{1}
initial data

with

. We say
(Q,\tilde{N})
is a classical solution to (
3.8
)-(
3.11
) on
[0,T)
if

1.

Q(\tau,\eta)\in C^{1}([0,T)\times[0,1])
and
{\tilde{N}}(\tau)\in C[0,T)
.

2.

Equations (
3.8
)-(
3.11
) are satisfied in the classical sense for
\tau\in[0,T)
.

We have an equivalent characterization of
\tilde{N}
, whose proof is the same as Proposition
2.2
for
1/N(\tau)
.

Proposition 3.1
.

Suppose

and
\tilde{N}(\tau)\in C[0,T)
satisfies (

), with an initial condition (
3.11
) satisfying

. Then the expression (

) for

on

is equivalent to

Q(\tau,1)\equiv\Phi_{F},\hskip 18.49988pt\tau\in[0,T).

(3.12)

For later use, we also list the following properties of the relaxed problem (

)-(
3.11
), whose proof is similar to Corollary

for the original problem (

)-(

).

Proposition 3.2
.

Let
(Q,\tilde{N})
be a classical solution (
3.8
)-(

). Then it satisfies (

) and

\partial_{\eta}Q(\tau,0)=\tilde{N}(\tau)+K(0),\hskip 18.49988pt\partial_{\eta}Q(\tau,1)-\partial_{\eta}Q(\tau,0)=K(\Phi_{F})-K(0).

(3.13)

3.1.3
Connections to the original problem

The classical solution to the relaxed problem (
3.8
)-(
3.11
) (Definition
3.1
) connects to the original one (
2.16
)-(
2.19
) (Definition
2.2
) as in the following lemma.

Lemma 3.1
.

(i) Suppose
(Q,N)
is a classical solution to the original problem (
2.16
)-(
2.19
) on the time interval
[0,\tau)
. Then with
\tilde{N}:=1/N
the pair
(Q,\tilde{N})
is a classical solution to the relaxed problem (
3.8
)-(
3.11
) on the same time interval
[0,\tau)
.

(ii) Suppose
(Q,\tilde{N})
is a classical solution to the relaxed problem (
3.8
)-(
3.11
) on
[0,\tau)
. Moreover assume that
Q_{\init}(\eta)
is increasing in
\eta
, and that there exists
0<\tau^{*}\leq\tau
such that
\tilde{N}>0
on
[0,\tau^{*})
. Then with
N:=1/\tilde{N}
the pair
(Q,N)
gives a classical solution to the original problem (
2.16
)-(
2.19
), on the time interval
[0,\tau^{*})
.

Proof.

For (i), it directly follows from Definition
2.2
and
3.1
. For (ii), it is similar. We just note that the positivity of
\tilde{N}
on
[0,\tau^{*})
allows
N:=1/\tilde{N}
to be well-defined, and that the two assumptions:
Q_{\init}(\eta)
is increasing in
\eta
, and that there exists
0<\tau^{*}\leq\tau
such that
\tilde{N}>0
on
[0,\tau^{*})
, are enough to ensure that
Q(s,\eta)
is increasing in
\eta
at each time
s\in[0,\tau^{*})
, thanks to Proposition
2.1
.
∎

Note that in the second part of Lemma
3.1
,
\tau^{*}
can be smaller than
\tau
. We can recover the original problem from the relaxed one only when
\tilde{N}
stays positive.

Remark 3.3
.

Both
\tilde{N}
and
1/N
can be understood as some Lagrange multipliers to ensure the additional boundary condition
Q(\tau,1)=\Phi_{F}
, as shown in Proposition
2.2
and
3.1
. However, in the original problem we require
1/N
to be positive (
2.20
), while here
\tilde{N}
is allowed to take any values in
\mathbb{R}
, including negative ones.

By allowing negative values for
\tilde{N}
, the relaxed problem can be continued after the blow-up of
N
. However, such a continuation might no longer represent the dynamics of pulse-coupled oscillators. Indeed, in the relaxed problem
Q
can be non-monotone after
\tilde{N}
being negative. This implies that
Q
no longer correspond to a pseudo-inverse for a probability distribution. See Figure
2
for an illustration.

)-(

in log scale. In the original problem, the firing rate

3.2
Well-posedness of the original problem

Theorem
3.4
shows that the solution to the relaxed problem (
3.8
)-(
3.11
) is global, but it may not correspond to the physical dynamics of the pulse-coupled oscillator whenever
\tilde{N}
touches zero or becomes negative. Nevertheless, we can use the well-posedness results for the relaxed problem (Theorem
3.4
-
3.6
) to prove
the well-posedness of the original problem (Theorem
3.1
-
3.3
).

First, we use Theorem
3.4
to derive the maximal existence and blow-up criteria of the original problem (
2.16
)-(
2.19
), and therefore prove Theorem
3.1
.

Proof of Theorem
3.1
.

1. Uniqueness.
Suppose there are two solutions to the original problem. Then by Lemma
3.1
-(i) they are also solutions to the relaxed problem. Therefore, they must be the same, as the uniqueness of the relaxed problem is given in Theorem
3.4
.

2. Construct the maximal classical solution.
Let
(Q,\tilde{N})
be the solution to the relaxed problem with the same initial data, whose global existence is ensured by Theorem
3.4
. Now we aim to find the first time
\tilde{N}(\tau)
touching zero, defined via

\tau^{*}:=\inf\{\tau\geq 0:\,\tilde{N}(\tau)=0\}.

(3.17)

We have
\tau^{*}>0
and
\tilde{N}(\tau)>0
for all
\tau
in
[0,\tau^{*})
, which follows from the continuity and that initially
\tilde{N}(0)>0
by Assumption
2
(c.f. Remark
3.1
). Hence by Lemma
3.1
-(ii) we can construct a classical solution to the original problem on
[0,\tau^{*})
, with the same
Q
and
N=1/\tilde{N}
.

When
\tau^{*}=+\infty
, we have constructed the global classical solution to the original problem.

When otherwise
0<\tau^{*}<\infty
, we have
\tilde{N}(\tau^{*})=0
, hence we derive that the firing rate blows up at
\tau^{*}

\lim_{\tau\rightarrow(\tau^{*})^{-}}N(\tau)=\lim_{\tau\rightarrow(\tau^{*})^{-}}\frac{1}{\tilde{N}(\tau)}=+\infty.

(3.18)

In this case, we have constructed a classical solution on
[0,\tau^{*})
with
\tau^{*}
being the first blow-up time of the firing rate
N(\tau)
.
∎

We summarize the construction in the proof of Theorem
3.1
as the following corollary.

Corollary 3.1
.

Suppose the initial data satisfies Assumption
2
. Then there exists a unique global classical solution
(Q,\tilde{N})
to the relaxed problem (
3.8
)-(
3.11
). Define
\tau^{*}>0
as in (
3.17
). Then, restricting to
[0,\tau^{*})
, with
N:=1/\tilde{N}
,
(Q,N)
gives a classical solution to the original problem (
2.16
)-(
2.19
) with
\tau^{*}
as its maximal existence time.

Together with Theorem
3.5
-
3.6
, we can obtain the continuous dependence on initial data and regularity before the blow-up, and thus prove Theorem
3.2
-
3.3
.

Proof of Theorem
3.2
.

We follow the construction in Corollary
3.1
.

Let
(Q_{\varepsilon},\tilde{N}_{\varepsilon})
be the corresponding solution to the relaxed problem (
3.8
)-(
3.11
). In particular,
\tau_{0}^{*}
is the first time that
\tilde{N}_{0}
touches zero and
\tilde{N}_{0}(\tau)>0
for all
0\leq\tau<\tau_{0}^{*}
. Then for every
0<T<\tau^{*}_{0}
,
\tilde{N}_{0}
has a uniform positive lower bound on
[0,T]
, which allows us to use (
3.15
) in Theorem
3.5
to deduce that
\tilde{N}_{\varepsilon}(\tau)>0
on
[0,T]
for
\varepsilon
small enough. Hence we derive

\liminf_{\varepsilon\rightarrow 0^{+}}\tau^{*}_{\varepsilon}\geq T,

(3.19)

for every
0<T<\tau^{*}_{0}
, which implies (
3.4
).

Then by the construction
N_{\varepsilon}=1/{\tilde{N}}_{\varepsilon}
, (
3.5
) follows from (
3.15
) and the uniform positive lower bound on
[0,T]
. And (
3.6
) follows from (
3.16
).

∎

Proof of Theorem
3.3
.

The proof is direct, as the solution to the original problem is constructed via a restriction in time by Corollary
3.1
, which will inherit the regularity for the relaxed problem in Theorem
3.6
.
∎

Finally, we note that when a classical solution to the original problem blows up, the profile
Q
still has a well-defined limit towards the blow-up time
\tau^{*}
. This is a direct consequence of the construction in Corollary
3.1
.

Corollary 3.2
.

Let
(Q,N)
be a classical solution to (
2.16
)-(
2.19
) on the maximal existence interval
[0,\tau^{*})
as in Theorem
3.1
. If
\tau^{*}<\infty
, then the pre-blow-up profile

Q((\tau^{*})^{-},\cdot):=\lim_{\tau\rightarrow(\tau^{*})^{-}}Q(\tau,\cdot),\hskip 18.49988pt\text{where the limit is in $C^{1}[0,1]$,}

(3.20)

is a well-defined function. Moreover, it satisfies
\partial_{\eta}Q((\tau^{*})^{-},1)=K(\Phi_{F})
.

3.3
Relaxed problem: mild solution

In what follows, we study the well-posedness and regularity for the relaxed problem and provide proofs for Theorem
3.4
-
3.6
. We take a “bottom-up” approach, starting with simpler problems for which the existence is more directly available, then step by step move to the full relaxed problem (
3.8
)-(
3.11
) and recover its regularity.

We start with a further simplified problem, replacing
\tilde{N}(\tau)
in (
3.8
) by an external source
f(\tau)
.

\displaystyle\partial_{\tau}Q+\partial_{\eta}Q=f(\tau)+{K}(Q),\hskip 18.49988pt\hskip 18.49988pt

\displaystyle\tau>0,\ \eta\in(0,1),

(3.21)

\displaystyle Q(\tau,0)=0,\hskip 18.49988pt\hskip 18.49988pt

\displaystyle\tau>0,

\displaystyle Q(\tau=0,\eta)=Q_{\init}(\eta),\hskip 18.49988pt\hskip 18.49988pt

\displaystyle\eta\in[0,1].

Equation (
3.21
) is still nonlinear due to a possibly nonlinear
K(Q)
. But we can already solve it via the characteristics when
f
is given. This motivates us to define the flow map and the mild solution as follows.

For a given (locally bounded) function
f
, we introduce the flow map
\Psi_{\tau_{s}\rightarrow\tau}^{f}(x)
, starting at time
\tau_{s}
with initial position
x
, defined via the solution to

\displaystyle\frac{d}{d\tau}\Psi_{\tau_{s}\rightarrow\tau}^{f}(x)

\displaystyle={K}(\Psi_{\tau_{s}\rightarrow\tau}^{f}(x))+f(\tau),\hskip 9.24994pt\tau>\tau_{s},

(3.22)

\displaystyle\Psi_{\tau_{s}\rightarrow\tau_{s}}^{f}(x)

\displaystyle=x,\hskip 9.24994ptx\geq 0.

The flow map is well-defined thanks to Assumption
1
on
K
. Then we can define the mild solution to (
3.21
).

Definition 3.2

(Mild solution to external source problem (
3.21
))
.

Given a pointwise-defined initial data
Q_{\init}(\eta)
with
Q_{\init}(0)=0
and
f(\tau)
a locally bounded function in time, a mild solution to (
3.21
) is a pointwisely defined function
Q(\tau,\eta)
given by

Q(\tau,\eta)=\begin{cases}\Psi_{\tau-\eta\rightarrow\tau}^{f}(0),\hskip 18.49988pt\tau\geq\eta\geq 0,\\
\Psi_{0\rightarrow\tau}^{f}(Q_{\init}(\eta-\tau)),\hskip 18.49988pt0\leq\tau\leq\eta\leq 1,\end{cases}

(3.23)

where
\Psi^{f}_{\tau_{s}\rightarrow\tau}
is the flow map associated with
f
as defined in (
3.22
).

Now we define the mild solution to the full relaxed problem (
3.8
)-(
3.11
). Compared to Definition
3.2
we need to determine the correct external source
\tilde{N}
.

Definition 3.3

(Mild solution to the relaxed problem (
3.8
))
.

Given initial data
Q_{\init}(\eta)
satisfying
Q_{\init}(0)=0
and
Q_{\init}(1)=\Phi_{F}
, a mild solution to (
3.8
)-(
3.11
) is a pair
(Q,\tilde{N})
where
\tilde{N}
is a locally bounded function and
Q
is the corresponding mild solution to (
3.21
) with
f(\tau)={\tilde{N}(\tau)}
as the external source, such that

Q(\tau,1)\equiv\Phi_{F},\hskip 18.49988pt\forall\tau>0.

(3.24)

Compared to Definition
3.1
of the classical solution, we require less regularity. Here we use (
3.24
) to determine
\tilde{N}
instead of (
3.10
). Note that the condition (
3.24
) does not involve
\partial_{\eta}Q
in contrast to (
3.10
). We know these two conditions are equivalent for the classical solution by Proposition
3.1
. In particular a classical solution in Definition
3.1
is a mild solution here.

As a first step towards Theorem
3.4
, we prove the well-posedness to (
3.8
)-(
3.11
) in terms of the mild solution.

Proposition 3.3

(Existence and Uniqueness for the mild solution to the relaxed problem)
.

Given initial data with the regularity
Q_{\init}(\eta)\in W^{1,\infty}(0,1)
satisfying
Q_{\init}(0)=0
and
Q_{\init}(1)=\Phi_{F}
, there exists a unique mild solution
(Q,\tilde{N})
to the relaxed problem (
3.8
) in the sense of Definition
3.3
, which is global in time.
Moreover, for each
\tau>0
,
\partial_{\eta}Q(\tau,\cdot)
is in
W^{1,\infty}(0,1)
with growth estimates

\displaystyle\|\partial_{\eta}Q(\tau,\cdot)\|_{L^{\infty}(0,1)}

\displaystyle\leq e^{K_{1}\tau}\left(\|\frac{d}{d\eta}Q_{\init}(\eta)\|_{L^{\infty}(0,1)}+C\tau+C\right),\hskip 18.49988pt\tau>0,

(3.25)

\displaystyle\|\tilde{N}\|_{L^{\infty}(0,\tau)}

\displaystyle\leq e^{K_{1}\tau}\left(\|\frac{d}{d\eta}Q_{\init}(\eta)\|_{L^{\infty}(0,1)}+C\tau+C\right),\hskip 18.49988pt\tau>0,

(3.26)

where
K_{1}:=\|K^{\prime}\|_{L^{\infty}(\mathbb{R})}>0
and
C>0
are constants independent of initial data.

The following classical properties of the flow map
\Psi_{\tau_{s}\rightarrow\tau}^{f}(x)
will be useful for the proof of Proposition
3.3
. Recall we take Assumption
1
for
K
throughout this paper.

Lemma 3.2

(Classical properties of the flow map)
.

Let
f
be a locally bounded function. Then the flow map
\Psi_{\tau_{s}\rightarrow\tau}^{f}(x)
defined in (
3.22
) has the following properties.

1.

(Dependence on
x
) It is
C^{1}
w.r.t. the initial position
x
and the derivative is given by

\frac{\partial}{\partial x}(\Psi^{f}_{\tau_{s}\rightarrow\tau}(x))=\exp\left(\int_{\tau_{s}}^{\tau}K^{\prime}(\Psi^{f}_{\tau_{s}\rightarrow t}(x))dt\right).

(3.27)

2.

(Dependence on
\tau_{s}
) It is absolutely continuous w.r.t. the starting time
\tau_{s}
, and the almost everywhere derivative is given by

\frac{\partial}{\partial\tau_{s}}(\Psi^{f}_{\tau_{s}\rightarrow\tau}(x))=-\Bigl(K(x)+f(\tau_{s})\Bigr)\exp\left(\int_{\tau_{s}}^{\tau}K^{\prime}(\Psi^{f}_{\tau_{s}\rightarrow t}(x))dt\right).

(3.28)

3.

(Stability in
f
) For two different external sources
f_{1},f_{2}
, we have the following stability estimate

|\Psi^{f_{1}}_{\tau_{s}\rightarrow\tau}(x)-\Psi^{f_{2}}_{\tau_{s}\rightarrow\tau}(x)|\leq e^{C\Delta\tau}\Delta\tau\|f_{1}-f_{2}\|_{L^{\infty}(\tau_{s},\tau)},\hskip 18.49988pt\Delta\tau:=\tau-\tau_{s}\geq 0,

(3.29)

with a constant
C>0
depending only on
K
.

The proof of Lemma
3.2
is classical.

Proof of Proposition
3.3
.

Step 1.
\tau\in[0,1)
, reduce to a fixed point problem.
Denote the solution to the simplified problem (
3.21
) with external source
f
as
Q^{f}
. Then by Definition
3.2
for
\tau\in[0,1)
,
Q^{f}(\tau,1)=\Psi_{0\rightarrow\tau}^{f}(Q_{\rm init}(1-\tau))
. Therefore on this time interval (
3.24
) is equivalent to

\Psi_{0\rightarrow\tau}^{f}(Q_{\init}(1-\tau))\equiv\Phi_{F},\hskip 9.24994pt\tau\in[0,1).

(3.30)

Due to the regularity assumed for
Q_{\init}
,
K
and
f
, we note the map
\tau\rightarrow\Psi_{0\rightarrow\tau}^{f}(Q_{\rm init}(1-\tau))
is absolutely continuous in
\tau
. Hence as
Q_{\init}(1)=\Phi_{F}
, taking the derivative in
\tau
, we see (
3.30
) is equivalent to

\frac{d}{d\tau}\left(\Psi_{0\rightarrow\tau}^{f}(Q_{\init}(1-\tau))\right)\equiv 0,\hskip 9.24994pt\tau\in[0,1),

(3.31)

where the derivative is understood in the almost everywhere sense.

We calculate

\displaystyle\frac{d}{d\tau}\left(\Psi_{0\rightarrow\tau}^{f}(Q_{\init}(1-\tau))\right)

\displaystyle=\frac{\partial}{\partial\tau}\Psi_{0\rightarrow\tau}^{f}(x)|_{x=Q_{\init}(1-\tau)}-\frac{\partial}{\partial x}\Psi_{0\rightarrow\tau}^{f}(Q_{\init}(1-\tau))\frac{d}{d\eta}Q_{\init}(1-\tau),

(3.32)

where we use the chain rule for the second term, relying on the regularity of
Q_{\init}
.

To treat the first term on the right hand side of (
3.32
), by definition of the flow map we have

\displaystyle\frac{\partial}{\partial\tau}\Psi_{0\rightarrow\tau}^{f}(x)|_{x=Q_{\init}(1-\tau)}

\displaystyle=f(\tau)+K(\Psi_{0\rightarrow\tau}^{f}(Q_{\init}(1-\tau)))

(3.33)

\displaystyle=f(\tau)+K(Q^{f}(\tau,1)).

(3.34)

For the second term we use Lemma
3.2
-1 to derive

\displaystyle\frac{\partial}{\partial x}(\Psi^{f}_{0\rightarrow\tau}(Q_{\init}(1-\tau)))

\displaystyle=\exp\left(\int_{0}^{\tau}K^{\prime}(\Psi^{f}_{0\rightarrow s}(Q_{\init}(1-\tau)))ds\right)

(3.35)

\displaystyle=\exp\left(\int_{0}^{\tau}K^{\prime}(Q^{f}(s,1-\tau+s))ds\right).

(3.36)

Plugging the above expressions (
3.34
)-(
3.36
) into (
3.32
), we deduce that (
3.31
) is equivalent to the following
fixed point problem
, for
\tau\in[0,1)

\displaystyle f(\tau)

\displaystyle=\exp\left(\int_{0}^{\tau}K^{\prime}(Q^{f}(s,1-\tau+s))ds\right)\frac{d}{d\eta}Q_{\init}(1-\tau)-K(Q^{f}(\tau,1))

(3.37)

\displaystyle=:\mathcal{F}(f)(\tau).

Recall here that
Q^{f}
is the solution to the simplified problem (
3.21
) with external source
f
, as defined in (
3.2
). More explicitly we have for
0\leq s\leq\tau\leq 1

\displaystyle Q^{f}(s,1-\tau+s)=\Psi_{0\rightarrow s}^{f}(Q_{\init}(1-\tau)),

(3.38)

\displaystyle Q^{f}(\tau,1)=\Psi_{0\rightarrow\tau}^{f}(Q_{\init}(1-\tau)).

(3.39)

Step 2.
\tau\in[0,\Delta\tau)
, locally solve the fixed point problem.
Next, we show that the fixed point problem (
3.37
) can be locally solved via the Banach fixed point theorem. More precisely, we shall prove the following estimate, for all
0<\Delta\tau\leq 1
,

\|\mathcal{F}(f_{1})-\mathcal{F}(f_{2})\|_{L^{\infty}(0,\Delta\tau)}\leq C\Delta\tau\left(1+\left\|\frac{d}{d\eta}Q_{\init}(\eta)\right\|_{L^{\infty}(0,1)}\right)\|f_{1}-f_{2}\|_{L^{\infty}(0,\Delta\tau)},

(3.40)

where
C>0
is a constant independent of the initial data. The estimate (
3.40
) implies that
\mathcal{F}
is a contraction on
L^{\infty}(0,\Delta\tau)
with
\Delta\tau
given by, e.g.

\Delta\tau=\min\left(1,\frac{1}{2C\left(1+\|\frac{d}{d\eta}Q_{\init}(\eta)\|_{L^{\infty}(0,1)}\right)}\right).

(3.41)

Hence, by the Banach fixed point theorem, on
(0,\Delta\tau)
we can obtain a unique
f=:\tilde{N}
as a fixed point to (
3.37
), therefore a unique mild solution on that time interval.

Now we prove (
3.40
). First we apply Lemma
3.2
-3 to (
3.38
)-(
3.39
) to obtain

\displaystyle|Q^{f_{1}}(s,1-\tau+s)-Q^{f_{2}}(s,1-\tau+s)|

\displaystyle\leq Cs\|f_{1}-f_{2}\|_{L^{\infty}(0,s)}

(3.42)

\displaystyle\leq C\Delta\tau\|f_{1}-f_{2}\|_{L^{\infty}(0,\Delta\tau)},\hskip 18.49988pt0\leq s\leq\tau\leq\Delta\tau\leq 1,

(3.43)

and

\displaystyle|Q^{f_{1}}(\tau,1)-Q^{f_{2}}(\tau,1)|

\displaystyle\leq C\tau\|f_{1}-f_{2}\|_{L^{\infty}(0,\tau)}

(3.44)

\displaystyle\leq C\Delta\tau\|f_{1}-f_{2}\|_{L^{\infty}(0,\Delta\tau)},\hskip 18.49988pt0\leq\tau\leq\Delta\tau\leq 1.

(3.45)

Next we estimate the difference
\mathcal{F}(f_{1})-\mathcal{F}(f_{2})
. For the first term in (
3.37
), we calculate

\left|\exp\left(\int_{0}^{\tau}K^{\prime}(Q^{f_{1}}(s,1-\tau+s))ds\right)\frac{d}{d\eta}Q_{\init}(1-\tau)-\exp\left(\int_{0}^{\tau}K^{\prime}(Q^{f_{2}}(s,1-\tau+s))ds\right)\frac{d}{d\eta}Q_{\init}(1-\tau)\right|\leq\\
\|\frac{d}{d\eta}Q_{\init}(\eta)\|_{L^{\infty}}\left|\exp\left(\int_{0}^{\tau}K^{\prime}(Q^{f_{1}}(s,1-\tau+s))ds\right)-\exp\left(\int_{0}^{\tau}K^{\prime}(Q^{f_{2}}(s,1-\tau+s))ds\right)\right|\\
\leq C\|\frac{d}{d\eta}Q_{\init}(\eta)\|_{L^{\infty}}\left|\int_{0}^{\tau}K^{\prime}(Q^{f_{1}}(s,1-\tau+s))ds-\int_{0}^{\tau}K^{\prime}(Q^{f_{2}}(s,1-\tau+s))ds\right|.

(3.46)

And using (
3.43
), we have

\displaystyle\left|\int_{0}^{\tau}K^{\prime}(Q^{f_{1}}(s,1-\tau+s))ds-\int_{0}^{\tau}\right.

\displaystyle\left.K^{\prime}(Q^{f_{2}}(s,1-\tau+s))ds\right|

(3.47)

\displaystyle\leq C\tau\sup_{s\in[0,\tau]}|K^{\prime}(Q^{f_{1}}(s,1-\tau+s))ds-K^{\prime}(Q^{f_{2}}(s,1-\tau+s))|

(3.48)

\displaystyle\leq C\tau^{2}\|f_{1}-f_{2}\|_{L^{\infty}(0,\tau)}

(3.49)

\displaystyle\leq C\Delta\tau\|f_{1}-f_{2}\|_{L^{\infty}(0,\Delta\tau)},\hskip 18.49988pt0<\tau\leq\Delta\tau\leq 1.

(3.50)

Hence together with (
3.46
), we derive for
\tau\leq\Delta\tau

\displaystyle\left|\exp\left(\int_{0}^{\tau}K^{\prime}(Q^{f_{1}}(s,1-\tau+s))ds\right)\frac{d}{d\eta}Q_{\init}(1-\tau)-\right.

\displaystyle\left.\exp\left(\int_{0}^{\tau}K^{\prime}(Q^{f_{2}}(s,1-\tau+s))ds\right)\frac{d}{d\eta}Q_{\init}(1-\tau)\right|

(3.51)

\displaystyle\leq C\Delta\tau\|\frac{d}{d\eta}Q_{\init}(\eta)\|_{L^{\infty}}\|f_{1}-f_{2}\|_{L^{\infty}(0,\Delta\tau)}.

(3.52)

For the second term in (
3.37
), we use (
3.45
) to derive for
\tau\leq\Delta\tau

|K(Q^{f_{1}}(\tau,1))-K(Q^{f_{2}}(\tau,1))|\leq C\|{K^{\prime}}\|_{\infty}\Delta\tau\|f_{1}-f_{2}\|_{L^{\infty}(0,\Delta\tau)}\leq C\Delta\tau\|f_{1}-f_{2}\|_{L^{\infty}(0,\Delta\tau)}.

(3.53)

Finally, as both terms in (
3.37
) are estimated, we combine (
3.52
) and (
3.53
) to obtain (
3.40
).

For later uses, we note that since (
3.30
) holds when
f
solves the fixed point problem (
3.37
), we can replace
Q^{f}(\tau,1)
by
\Phi_{F}
to derive

f(\tau)=\exp\left(\int_{0}^{\tau}K^{\prime}(Q^{f}(s,1-\tau+s))ds\right)\frac{d}{d\eta}Q_{\init}(1-\tau)-K(\Phi_{F}).

(3.54)

Step 3. A Priori Growth Estimates.
In Step 2 we have shown that we can locally obtain a unique mild solution, on a small time interval depending on
\|\frac{d}{d\eta}Q_{\init}(\eta)\|_{L^{\infty}(0,1)}
, as in (
3.41
). To obtain a global solution, now we control the growth of the
L^{\infty}
norm of the spatial derivative.

We first work for
\tau\in[0,1]
. For the desired solution,
f=\tilde{N}
solves the fixed point problem (
3.37
) and therefore (
3.54
), which allows us to estimate as follows

\displaystyle|\tilde{N}(\tau)|=|f(\tau)|

\displaystyle\leq\left|\exp\left(\int_{0}^{\tau}K^{\prime}(Q^{f}(s,1-\tau+s))ds\right)\frac{d}{d\eta}Q_{\init}(1-\tau)\right|+\left|K(\Phi_{F})\right|

(3.55)

\displaystyle\leq e^{K_{1}\tau}\|\frac{d}{d\eta}Q_{\init}(\eta)\|_{L^{\infty}(0,1)}+C,\hskip 18.49988pt\tau\in[0,1],

(3.56)

where we have used the boundedness of
K^{\prime}
with
K_{1}=\|K^{\prime}\|_{L^{\infty}(\mathbb{R})}
. Then we calculate
\partial_{\eta}Q
, using Definition
3.2
and Lemma
3.2

\partial_{\eta}Q(\tau,\eta)=\begin{cases}(K(0)+f(\tau-\eta))\exp\left(\int_{\tau-\eta}^{\tau}K^{\prime}(Q^{f}(s,s-\tau+\eta))ds\right),\hskip 9.24994pt0\leq\eta<\tau,\\
\frac{d}{d\eta}Q_{\init}(\eta-\tau)\exp\left(\int_{0}^{\tau}K^{\prime}(Q^{f}(s,s-\tau+\eta))ds\right),\hskip 9.24994pt\tau<\eta\leq 1.\end{cases}

(3.57)

Note that at this stage whether
\partial_{\eta}Q(\tau,\eta)
is continuous at
\eta=\tau
does not come into play as we look for the
L^{\infty}
weak derivative. For
0\leq\tau<\eta
, we estimate using (
3.56
)

\displaystyle|\partial_{\eta}Q(\tau,\eta)|

\displaystyle\leq(K(0)+|f(\tau-\eta)|)e^{K_{1}\eta}\leq\left(C+e^{K_{1}(\tau-\eta)}\|\frac{d}{d\eta}Q_{\init}(\eta)\|_{L^{\infty}}\right)e^{K_{1}\eta}\leq e^{K_{1}\tau}\|\frac{d}{d\eta}Q_{\init}(\eta)\|_{L^{\infty}}+C.

(3.58)

For
\tau<\eta<1
, it is direct to obtain

\displaystyle|\partial_{\eta}Q(\tau,\eta)|\leq\left|\frac{d}{d\eta}Q_{\init}(\eta-\tau)\right|e^{K_{1}\tau}\leq e^{K_{1}\tau}\|\frac{d}{d\eta}Q_{\init}(\eta)\|_{L^{\infty}}.

(3.59)

All together we have

\|\partial_{\eta}Q(\tau,\cdot)\|_{L^{\infty}(0,1)}\leq e^{K_{1}\tau}\|\frac{d}{d\eta}Q_{\init}(\eta)\|_{L^{\infty}(0,1)}+C,\hskip 9.24994pt\tau\in[0,1].

(3.60)

For
\tau>1
we similarly have

\|\partial_{\eta}Q(\tau,\cdot)\|_{L^{\infty}(0,1)}\leq e^{K_{1}}\|\partial_{\eta}Q(\tau-1,\cdot)\|_{L^{\infty}(0,1)}+C,

(3.61)

which iteratively yields

\|\partial_{\eta}Q(\tau,\cdot)\|_{L^{\infty}(0,1)}\leq e^{K_{1}[\tau]}\left(\|\partial_{\eta}Q(\tau-[\tau],\cdot)\|_{L^{\infty}(0,1)}+C[\tau]\right),

(3.62)

where we use
[\tau]
to denote the integer part of
\tau
. Combining (
3.62
) and (
3.60
) we obtain the desired estimate (
3.25
).

For
\tilde{N}
, similar to (
3.56
) we have for
\tau>1

|\tilde{N}(\tau)|\leq e^{K_{1}}\|\partial_{\eta}Q(\tau-1,\cdot)\|_{L^{\infty}(0,1)}+C,

(3.63)

combining which with (
3.25
) gives (
3.26
).

Step 4. Towards a global solution.
In Step 2 we have shown the local well-posedness on
[0,\Delta\tau]
, with a timestep related to the
L^{\infty}
norm of the spatial derivative (
3.41
). The bound (
3.25
) ensures that this
L^{\infty}
norm, despite may grow exponentially as time goes to infinity, is uniformly bounded on every finite time interval
[0,T]
, which allows us to extend the solution towards
\tau=T
for every
T<+\infty
. Therefore, we can obtain a global solution.

∎

Proof.

Step 1. Local convergence of
\tilde{N}
.

\displaystyle f(\tau)

for the dependence on

. Similar to the calculations behind (

Relaxed problem: regularity

We proceed to improve the regularity of the mild solution obtained in the previous section, under more requirements on initial data.

3.4.1
From mild to classical: Proof of Theorem
3.4
-
3.5

Proposition 3.4
.

Suppose
Q_{\init}\in C^{1}[0,1]
with
Q_{\init}(0)=0,Q_{\init}(1)=\Phi_{F}
and suppose additionally

\frac{d}{d\eta}Q_{\init}(1)-\frac{d}{d\eta}Q_{\init}(0)=K(\Phi_{F})-K(0).

(3.77)

Then the mild solution obtained in Proposition
3.3
is classical, in the sense of Definition
3.1
, and thus it satisfies the properties in Proposition
3.2
.

Compared to Proposition
3.3
, two more conditions on initial data are imposed:
C^{1}
regularity instead of
W^{1,\infty}
, and the compatibility condition (
3.77
), which is (
3.1
) in Assumption
2
. Proposition
3.4
claims that with these two additional conditions, the mild solution (Definition
3.3
) as constructed in Proposition
3.3
is indeed a classical solution (Definition
3.1
).

Proof of Proposition
3.4
.

We first show the desired regularity for
\tau\in[0,1)
.

Step 1. Continuity of
\tilde{N}
.
We already know
\tilde{N}(\tau)
is locally bounded in Proposition
3.3
. To show the continuity, we shall use the fixed point equation (
3.76
) in Corollary
3.4
. For convenience, we still use the notation of the external source
f
with
f=\tilde{N}
. Note that as long as
f
is locally bounded and
Q_{\init}
is continuous, then
\Psi_{0\rightarrow s}^{f}(Q_{\init}(1-\tau))
is also continuous with respect to
\tau
by Lemma
3.2
. Therefore we can readily check that the right-hand side in (
3.76
) is continuous in
\tau
, when we further know
\frac{d}{d\eta}Q_{\init}
is continuous. This gives the continuity of
f=\tilde{N}(\tau)
on
[0,1)
.

Step 2. Regularity of
Q
.
Now we proceed to show that
Q(\tau,\eta)
is
C^{1}([0,1)\times[0,1])
. By Definition
3.2
for the mild solution, we see
Q^{f}
is continuous when the initial data and the external source
f=\tilde{N}
is continuous.

Moreover, we can compute the spatial derivative as (
3.57
)

\partial_{\eta}Q(\tau,\eta)=\begin{cases}(K(0)+f(\tau-\eta))\exp\left(\int_{\tau-\eta}^{\tau}K^{\prime}(Q^{f}(s,s-\tau+\eta))ds\right),\hskip 9.24994pt0\leq\eta<\tau,\\
\frac{d}{d\eta}Q_{\init}(\eta-\tau)\exp\left(\int_{0}^{\tau}K^{\prime}(Q^{f}(s,s-\tau+\eta))ds\right),\hskip 9.24994pt\tau<\eta\leq 1.\end{cases}

(3.78)

For this piecewise expression, we need to check the continuity at
\tau=\eta
. Here the compatible condition (
3.77
) comes into play. We derive, using (
3.76
) at
\tau=0
and (
3.77
)

\displaystyle K(0)+f(0)

\displaystyle=K(0)+\frac{d}{d\eta}Q_{\init}(1)-K(\Phi_{F})=\frac{d}{d\eta}Q_{\init}(0),

(3.79)

which implies that the expression (
3.78
) is continuous at
\eta=\tau
. Furthermore, together with the continuity of
Q^{f}
we can check that (
3.78
) gives a continuous function on
[0,1)\times[0,1]
.
For the temporal derivative, by definition (
3.23
) we compute

\partial_{\tau}Q=K(Q)+\tilde{N}(\tau)-\partial_{\eta}Q,

which gives both the regularity of
\partial_{\tau}Q
as the right hand side is a sum of three

functions, and that the equation (
3.8
) is satisfied in the classical sense. From (
3.75
) and (
3.78
), we also recover the relation (
3.10
).

Step 3. Beyond
[0,1)
.
Above, we have worked for
\tau\in[0,1)
. To extend the results for all
\tau\geq 0
, it suffices to show that the conditions on initial data holds for
Q(\tau,\cdot)
when
\tau\in[0,1)
.

Indeed, we have already shown that
\partial_{\eta}Q
is
C^{1}
and that the boundary values of
Q
are prescribed in the construction of the mild solution. It only remains to check the compatibility condition (
3.77
), for which we compute as follows, using (
3.78
) and (
3.75
)

\displaystyle\partial_{\eta}Q(\tau,1)=\frac{d}{d\eta}Q_{\init}(1-\tau)\exp\left(\int_{0}^{\tau}K^{\prime}(Q^{f}(s,s+\tau-1))ds\right)=f(\tau)+K(\Phi_{F})=\partial_{\eta}Q(\tau,0)-K(0)+K(\Phi_{F}).

(3.80)

Hence, we can start from e.g.
\tau=1/2
, and extend the arguments in Step 1-2 to obtain the regularity for
\tau\in[1/2,3/2)
. We can conclude the result for all
\tau\geq 0
by iteration.

∎

Proposition 3.5

In the same setting of Proposition

Let us explain how a second order condition (

\displaystyle(\partial_{\tau}+\partial_{\eta})Z=K^{\prime}(Q)Z,\hskip 9.24994pt

\displaystyle\tau>0,\eta\in(0,1),

\displaystyle Z(\tau,0)=\tilde{N}(\tau)+K(0)>0,\hskip 9.24994pt

\displaystyle\tau>0,

\displaystyle Z(0,\eta)=\frac{d}{d\eta}Q_{\init}(\eta),\hskip 9.24994pt

\displaystyle\eta\in[0,1].

In terms of

Z(\tau,1)-Z(\tau,0)=K(\Phi_{F})-K(0).

Taking derivatives w.r.t

\partial_{\tau}Z(\tau,1)-\partial_{\tau}Z(\tau,0)=0.

(3.84)

Now we use the equation (

\displaystyle\partial_{\eta}Z(\tau,1)-\partial_{\eta}Z(\tau,0)

\displaystyle=K^{\prime}(Q(\tau,1))Z(\tau,1)-K^{\prime}(Q(\tau,0))Z(\tau,0)

\displaystyle=K^{\prime}(\Phi_{F})Z(\tau,1)-K^{\prime}(0)Z(\tau,0).

Set

4
Contraction/Expansion estimates

In this section, we prove stability estimates in different norms for classical solutions of the
Q
-formulation (
2.16
)-(
2.19
). Later on, in Sections
5

6

Q
-formulation, we shall derive key estimates for the dynamics of classical solutions, which are in the following form

e^{k_{\min}\tau}\|Q_{1}(0)-Q_{2}(0)\|\leq\|Q_{1}(\tau)-Q_{2}(\tau)\|\leq e^{k_{\max}\tau}\|Q_{1}(0)-Q_{2}(0)\|.

(4.1)

Here
Q_{1},Q_{2}
are two classical solutions to (
2.16
)-(
2.19
), and
k_{\min},k_{\max}
relate to

via

{k}_{\min}:=K^{\prime}(0)+\int_{0}^{\Phi_{F}}(K^{\prime\prime}(\phi))_{-}d\phi,\hskip 18.49988pt{k}_{\max}:=K^{\prime}(0)+\int_{0}^{\Phi_{F}}(K^{\prime\prime}(\phi))_{+}d\phi.

(4.2)

The estimates hold as long as the classical solutions exist, and
\|\cdot\|
is a suitable norm to be specified. A particular class of phase response functions

will simplify some of our main results:

Assumption 4
.

K\in C^{2}[0,\Phi_{F}]
is either convex on
[0,\Phi_{F}]
or concave on
[0,\Phi_{F}]
.

In other words, Assumption
4
is equivalent to

does not change sign on
[0,\Phi_{F}]
.

Remark 4.1
.

If Assumption
4
holds, then

k_{\min}=\min_{\phi_{\in}[0,\Phi_{F}]}K^{\prime}(\phi),\hskip 18.49988ptk_{\max}=\max_{\phi_{\in}[0,\Phi_{F}]}K^{\prime}(\phi)\,.

Note that in general

k_{\min}\leq\min_{\phi\in[0,\Phi_{F}]}K^{\prime}(\phi)\leq\max_{\phi\in[0,\Phi_{F}]}K^{\prime}(\phi)\leq k_{\max}.

(4.3)

In Section
4.1
we shall present and explain the estimate (
4.1
) when

is a BV norm. Section

is devoted to the case when

is a variant of the

norm. These two cases can be unified into a framework in Section

, utilizing the perspective that

is a “Lagrangian multiplier” for the constraint

(see Proposition

4.1

Let
(Q_{1},N_{1})
,

If we directly compare

Theorem 4.1
.

Let
(Q_{1},N_{1})
and
(Q_{2},N_{2})
be two classical solutions to (
2.16
)-(
2.19
). Then we have

e^{k_{\min}\tau}\|\partial_{\eta}Q_{1}(0)-\partial_{\eta}Q_{2}(0)\|_{L^{1}(0,1)}\leq\|\partial_{\eta}Q_{1}(\tau)-\partial_{\eta}Q_{2}(\tau)\|_{L^{1}(0,1)}\leq e^{k_{\max}\tau}\|\partial_{\eta}Q_{1}(0)-\partial_{\eta}Q_{2}(0)\|_{L^{1}(0,1)}.

(4.5)

The estimates hold whenever the two classical solutions exist up to time
\tau
. Here

and

are defined in (

).

Before proving Theorem

For instance, when

, (

) implies that
\|\partial_{\eta}Q_{1}(\tau)-\partial_{\eta}Q_{2}(\tau)\|_{L^{1}(0,1)}
grows exponentially. However, it shall be uniformly bounded since

\displaystyle\|\partial_{\eta}Q_{1}(\tau)-\partial_{\eta}Q_{2}(\tau)\|_{L^{1}(0,1)}

\displaystyle\leq\|\partial_{\eta}Q_{1}(\tau)\|_{L^{1}(0,1)}+\|\partial_{\eta}Q_{2}(\tau)\|_{L^{1}(0,1)}

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{1}\partial_{\eta}Q_{1}(\tau,\eta)d\eta+\int_{0}^{1}\partial_{\eta}Q_{2}(\tau,\eta)d\eta

\displaystyle=Q_{1}(\tau,1)-Q_{1}(\tau,0)+Q_{2}(\tau,1)-Q_{2}(\tau,0)=2\Phi_{F},

where we use
Q_{i}
is increasing in space in the second line, and use boundary condition (

) and Proposition

in the last line. This leads to a contradiction with the lower bound in (

and

On the other hand if

In view of Remark

,

and

[0,\Phi_{F}]

R:=\int_{0}^{1}\left[\left(\int_{0}^{Q_{1}}K^{\prime\prime}(\phi)d\phi\right)f_{1}-\left(\int_{0}^{Q_{2}}K^{\prime\prime}(\phi)d\phi\right)f_{2}\right]{\rm sign}\left({f_{1}-f_{2}}\right)d\eta.

(4.34)

We use Fubini theorem to rewrite
R
. For the first term we have

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{1}\left(\int_{0}^{Q_{1}}K^{\prime\prime}(\phi)d\phi\right)f_{1}{\rm sign}\left({f_{1}-f_{2}}\right)d\eta=\int_{0}^{\Phi_{F}}K^{\prime\prime}(\phi)\left(\int_{Q_{1}(\eta)\geq\phi}f_{1}(\eta){\rm sign}\left({f_{1}-f_{2}}\right)(\eta)d\eta\right)d\phi.

Note that for
\phi\in(0,\Phi_{F})
, the set
\{\eta:Q_{1}(\eta)\geq\phi\}
is a (non-empty) interval since
Q_{1}
is non-decreasing (and satisfies (
4.27
)). Similarly we use the Fubini theorem to treat the second term in (
4.34
), and arrive at

\displaystyle R=\int_{0}^{\Phi_{F}}K^{\prime\prime}(\phi)r(\phi)d\phi,

(4.35)

where

r(\phi)=\int_{Q_{1}(\eta)\geq\phi}f_{1}(\eta){\rm sign}\left({f_{1}-f_{2}}\right)(\eta)d\eta-\int_{Q_{2}(\eta)\geq\phi}f_{2}(\eta){\rm sign}\left({f_{1}-f_{2}}\right)(\eta)d\eta.

(4.36)

Combining (
4.33
) and (
4.35
), we actually have

I_{K}=K^{\prime}(0)I+\int_{0}^{\Phi_{F}}K^{\prime\prime}(\phi)r(\phi)d\phi.

(4.37)

We claim that

0\leq r(\phi)\leq I,\hskip 9.24994pt\forall\phi\in[0,\Phi_{F}].

(4.38)

Indeed, if (
4.38
) holds, we conclude

k_{\min}I=\left(K^{\prime}(0)+\int_{0}^{\Phi_{F}}(K^{\prime\prime}(\phi))_{-}d\phi\right)I\leq I_{K}\leq\left(K^{\prime}(0)+\int_{0}^{\Phi_{F}}(K^{\prime\prime}(\phi))_{+}d\phi\right)I=k_{\max}I

(4.39)

Finally, we prove (
4.38
). For a fixed
\phi\in[0,\Phi_{F}]
, let
\eta_{1},\eta_{2}\in[0,1]
such that

Q_{i}(\eta_{i})=\phi,\hskip 18.49988pti=1,2,

(4.40)

whose existence is ensured by (
4.27
) the continuity of
Q_{i}
(here we do not need them to be unique). Without loss of generality, we assume
\eta_{1}\geq\eta_{2}
. Then we deduce

\displaystyle r(\phi)

\displaystyle=\int_{\eta_{1}}^{1}f_{1}(\eta){\rm sign}\left({f_{1}-f_{2}}\right)(\eta)d\eta-\int_{\eta_{2}}^{1}f_{2}(\eta){\rm sign}\left({f_{1}-f_{2}}\right)(\eta)d\eta

(4.41)

\displaystyle=\int_{\eta_{1}}^{1}|f_{1}-f_{2}|d\eta-\int_{\eta_{2}}^{\eta_{1}}f_{2}(\eta){\rm sign}\left({f_{1}-f_{2}}\right)(\eta)d\eta.

(4.42)

We first estimate

\displaystyle r(\phi)

\displaystyle\geq\int_{\eta_{1}}^{1}(f_{1}-f_{2})d\eta-\int_{\eta_{2}}^{\eta_{1}}f_{2}d\eta,

where we use
f_{2}\geq 0
since
Q_{2}
is non-decreasing. Then we conclude

\displaystyle r(\phi)

\displaystyle\geq\int_{\eta_{1}}^{1}f_{1}d\eta-\int_{\eta_{2}}^{1}f_{2}d\eta=Q_{1}(1)-Q_{1}(\eta_{1})-(Q_{2}(1)-Q_{2}(\eta_{2}))=0,

where we use (
4.27
) and (
4.40
).
Similarly we can prove
r(\phi)\leq I
taking into account (
4.42
) and noting

\displaystyle I-r(\phi)

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{\eta_{1}}|f_{1}-f_{2}|d\eta+\int_{\eta_{2}}^{\eta_{1}}f_{2}(\eta){\rm sign}\left({f_{1}-f_{2}}\right)(\eta)d\eta

\displaystyle\geq\int_{0}^{\eta_{1}}(f_{2}-f_{1})d\eta-\int_{\eta_{2}}^{\eta_{1}}f_{2}d\eta=-\int_{0}^{\eta_{1}}f_{1}d\eta+\int_{0}^{\eta_{2}}f_{2}d\eta=-(Q_{1}(\eta_{1})-Q_{1}(0))+Q_{2}(\eta_{2})-Q_{2}(0)=0,

where we use
f_{2}\geq 0
, (
4.27
) and (
4.40
) again.
∎

4.2
A
L^{2}
estimate

In Theorem
4.1
, the distance involved
a spatial derivative, which assumes strong regularity for solutions. Moreover, this distance may not be useful for establishing the stability of generic empirical measures with different mass configurations. Though in this work, we focus on classical solutions (which exclude empirical measures), it is still desirable to find estimates in a distance with weaker regularity assumptions.

The derivative
\partial_{\eta}
in Theorem
4.1
was used to remove the
1/N
term in (
2.16
), which is a constant in space. Another way to circumvent the challenge due to the
1/N
term, without resorting to derivatives, is to subtract the integral average. Let

P_{0}:=\text{id}-\int_{0}^{1}d\eta.

(4.46)

More precisely, for an integrable function
f
on
[0,1]
, we define another function
P_{0}f
via

(P_{0}f)(\eta):=f(\eta)-\int_{0}^{1}f(\tilde{\eta})d\tilde{\eta},\hskip 9.24994pt\eta\in[0,1].

(4.47)

Then clearly
P_{0}f=P_{0}(f+c)
for any constant
c
. In this way, we introduce the following distance

\|P_{0}Q_{1}(\tau,\cdot)-P_{0}Q_{2}(\tau,\cdot)\|_{L^{2}(0,1)}=\left(\int_{0}^{1}(P_{0}Q_{1}(\tau,\eta)-P_{0}Q_{2}(\tau,\eta))^{2}d\eta\right)^{1/2},

(4.48)

where

P_{0}Q_{i}(\tau,\eta)=Q_{i}(\tau,\eta)-\int_{0}^{1}Q_{i}(\tau,\tilde{\eta})d\tilde{\eta},\hskip 18.49988pti=1,2.

(4.49)

For a pesudo-inverse
Q
,
\int_{0}^{1}Q(\eta)d\eta
corresponds to the first moment of its corresponding probability measure. And
P_{0}Q
is the pesudo-inverse for the translation of the probability measure such that the first moment is zero, see Figure
3
.

Figure 3:
Illustration of
P_{0}Q
for a pseudo-inverse
Q
. The red dashed-dotted line represents
P_{0}Q
, while the blue solid line corresponds to
Q
. Right: Plots of
Q
and
P_{0}Q
. Left: Plots of the corresponding probability density
\rho
for
Q
and
P_{0}Q
. Here
\int_{0}^{1}Qd\eta=\frac{1}{2}\Phi_{F}
. And
P_{0}Q
is the pesudo-inverse for a probability measure supported on
[-\Phi_{F}/2,\Phi_{F}/2]
whose first moment is zero.

An interpretation of the distance (
4.48
), linking it to the Wasserstein distance in optimal transport, is given in the next result.

Proposition 4.1
.

Let
\mu_{1},\mu_{2}
be two probability measures on
\mathbb{R}
(with finite second moments), and let
Q_{1}(\eta),Q_{2}(\eta)
be their corresponding pseudo-inverse functions. Then for
P_{0}=\text{id}-\int_{0}^{1}d\eta

\displaystyle\|P_{0}Q_{1}-P_{0}Q_{2}\|_{L^{2}(0,1)}

\displaystyle=\min_{c\in\mathbb{R}}\|Q_{1}-Q_{2}-c\|_{L^{2}(0,1)}=\min_{c\in\mathbb{R}}W_{2}(\mu_{1},T_{c}^{\#}\mu_{2}).

(4.50)

Here
T_{c}^{\#}\mu_{2}
is the push-forward of
\mu_{2}
under the translation map
T_{c}:=\text{id}+c
.

Proof.

For the first equality in (
4.50
), it follows from the fact that
P_{0}=\text{id}-\int_{0}^{1}d\eta
is the orthogonal projection to the orthogonal complement of constants,
\text{span}\{1\}^{\perp}
, in the Hilbert space
L^{2}(0,1)
with the usual inner product. The minimizer is given by
c=\int_{0}^{1}(Q_{1}(\eta)-Q_{2}(\eta))d\eta
. Using (
4.9
)
and taking into account that by definition
Q_{2}+c
coincides with the pseudo-inverse function of
T_{c}^{\#}\mu_{2}
, we conclude the desired identity.
∎

We now show the following result parallel to Theorem
4.1
.

Theorem 4.2
.

Let
(Q_{1},N_{1})
and
(Q_{2},N_{2})
be two classical solutions to (
2.16
)-(
2.19
). Then we have

\frac{d}{d\tau}\int_{0}^{1}(P_{0}(Q_{1})-P_{0}(Q_{2}))^{2}d\eta=2\int_{0}^{1}P_{0}(Q_{1}-Q_{2})P_{0}(K(Q_{1})-K(Q_{2}))d\eta.

(4.51)

In particular, if
K
is a linear function, i.e.
K(Q)=kQ+b
, we have

\displaystyle\frac{d}{d\tau}\int_{0}^{1}(P_{0}(Q_{1})-P_{0}(Q_{2}))^{2}d\eta=2k\int_{0}^{1}(P_{0}(Q_{1})-P_{0}(Q_{2}))^{2}d\eta

(4.52)

which implies

\|P_{0}Q_{1}(\tau)-P_{0}Q_{2}(\tau)\|_{L^{2}(0,1)}=e^{k\tau}\|P_{0}Q_{1}(0)-P_{0}Q_{2}(0)\|_{L^{2}(0,1)}.

(4.53)

This identity holds whenever the two classical solutions exist up to time
\tau
.

Proof.

First, we show (
4.51
) implies (
4.52
) when
K(Q)=kQ+b
, in which case we have

K(Q_{1})-K(Q_{2})=kQ_{1}+b-(kQ_{2}+b)=k(Q_{1}-Q_{2}).

(4.54)

Then as
P_{0}=\text{id}-\int_{0}^{1}d\eta
is a linear operator, it commutes with the multiplication by a scalar
k\in\mathbb{R}

P_{0}(K(Q_{1})-K(Q_{2}))=P_{0}(k(Q_{1}-Q_{2}))=kP_{0}(Q_{1}-Q_{2}).

(4.55)

Substitute this into (
4.51
) and we obtain (
4.52
), which can be rewritten as an ODE

\frac{d}{d\tau}S(\tau)=2kS(\tau),

(4.56)

with
S(\tau)=\|P_{0}Q_{1}(\tau)-P_{0}Q_{2}(\tau)\|_{L^{2}(0,1)}^{2}
. Solving this ODE we obtain (
4.53
).

It remains to prove (
4.51
). As preparations, we study the conmutation properties of the linear operator
P_{0}=\text{id}-\int_{0}^{1}d\eta
with respect to
\partial_{\tau}
and
\partial_{\eta}
. Since
P_{0}
only acts on
\eta
direction, it commutes with
\partial_{\tau}
as follows

\displaystyle P_{0}(\partial_{\tau}Q)

\displaystyle=\partial_{\tau}Q(\tau,\eta)-\int_{0}^{1}\partial_{\tau}Q(\tau,\tilde{\eta})d\eta=\partial_{\tau}Q(\tau,\eta)-\partial_{\tau}\int_{0}^{1}Q(\tau,\tilde{\eta})d\eta

(4.57)

\displaystyle=\partial_{\tau}\left(Q(\tau,\eta)-\int_{0}^{1}Q(\tau,\tilde{\eta})d\eta\right)=\partial_{\tau}(P_{0}Q).

For
\partial_{\eta}
we can compute the commutator, we first realize that

\displaystyle P_{0}(\partial_{\eta}Q)

\displaystyle=\partial_{\eta}Q(\tau,\eta)-\int_{0}^{1}\partial_{\eta}Q(\tau,\tilde{\eta})d\eta

(4.58)

\displaystyle=\partial_{\eta}Q(\tau,\eta)-(Q(\tau,1)-Q(\tau,0))=\partial_{\eta}Q(\tau,\eta)-\Phi_{F},

where we use the boundary condition (
2.17
) and (
2.25
) in Proposition
2.2
. And on the other hand, we calculate

\displaystyle\partial_{\eta}(P_{0}Q)

\displaystyle=\partial_{\eta}\left(Q(\tau,\eta)-\int_{0}^{1}Q(\tau,\tilde{\eta})d\eta\right)=\partial_{\eta}Q(\tau,\eta).

(4.59)

Combining (
4.58
) and (
4.59
) we obtain the commutator between
P_{0}
and
\partial_{\eta}
as

P_{0}(\partial_{\eta}Q)-\partial_{\eta}(P_{0}Q)=-\Phi_{F}.

(4.60)

Next, we apply the linear operator
P_{0}
to both sides in equation (
2.16
) to deduce

P_{0}(\partial_{\tau}Q_{i})+P_{0}(\partial_{\eta}Q_{i})=P_{0}(K(Q_{i})),

(4.61)

for
i=1,2
. Using (
4.57
) and (
4.60
) in (
4.61
), we get

\partial_{\tau}(P_{0}Q_{i})+\partial_{\eta}(P_{0}Q_{i})+\Phi_{F}=P_{0}(K(Q_{i})).

(4.62)

Hence, subtracting (
4.62
) for
i=2
from that for
i=1
, we derive

\partial_{\tau}(P_{0}(Q_{1}-Q_{2}))+\partial_{\eta}(P_{0}(Q_{1}-Q_{2}))=P_{0}(K(Q_{1})-K(Q_{2})).

(4.63)

Now, we multiply both side of (
4.63
) by
{2}P_{0}(Q_{1}-Q_{2})
to obtain

\partial_{\tau}((P_{0}(Q_{1}-Q_{2}))^{2})+\partial_{\eta}((P_{0}(Q_{1}-Q_{2}))^{2})=2P_{0}(K(Q_{1})-K(Q_{2}))P_{0}(Q_{1}-Q_{2}),

(4.64)

and integrating in
\eta
yields

\displaystyle\frac{d}{d\tau}\int_{0}^{1}(P_{0}(Q_{1}-Q_{2}))^{2}d\eta+(P_{0}(Q_{1}-Q_{2}))^{2}|_{\eta=0}^{\eta=1}=2\int_{0}^{1}P_{0}(Q_{1}-Q_{2})P_{0}(K(Q_{1})-K(Q_{2}))d\eta.

(4.65)

In view of (
4.65
), to prove (
4.48
), it all reduces to show

(P_{0}(Q_{1}-Q_{2}))^{2}|_{\eta=0}^{\eta=1}=0.

(4.66)

Indeed we shall show a slightly stronger result

(P_{0}(Q_{1}-Q_{2}))|_{\eta=0}^{\eta=1}=0.

(4.67)

Thanks to (
2.17
) at
\eta=0
and (
2.25
) at
\eta=1
, we have

\displaystyle P_{0}Q_{i}(\tau,\eta=0)

\displaystyle=Q_{i}(\tau,\eta=0)-\int_{0}^{1}Q_{i}(\tau,\tilde{\eta})d\tilde{\eta}=-\int_{0}^{1}Q_{i}(\tau,\tilde{\eta})d\tilde{\eta},

and

\displaystyle P_{0}Q_{i}(\tau,\eta=1)

\displaystyle=Q_{i}(\tau,\eta=1)-\int_{0}^{1}Q_{i}(\tau,\tilde{\eta})d\tilde{\eta}=\Phi_{F}-\int_{0}^{1}Q_{i}(\tau,\tilde{\eta})d\tilde{\eta},

for
i=1,2
. Hence

\displaystyle P_{0}(Q_{1}-Q_{2})(\tau,\eta=0)

\displaystyle=-\int_{0}^{1}(Q_{1}(\tau,\tilde{\eta})-Q_{2}(\tau,\tilde{\eta}))d\tilde{\eta}=P_{0}(Q_{1}-Q_{2})(\tau,\eta=1).

Therefore (
4.67
) is proved. Combining (
4.67
) and (
4.65
) we obtain (
4.51
).
∎

Let two

4.3
A unified framework

Now we unify Theorem
4.1
and
4.2
into a general framework, which may better illustrate how we use the equation structure of (
2.16
), which we recall here for convenience,

\partial_{\tau}Q+\partial_{\eta}Q=\frac{1}{N(\tau)}+{K}(Q).

(4.71)

A first crucial step in both Theorem
4.1
and
4.2
is to derive an modulated equation, such that the challenging term
1/N(\tau)
does not show up explicitly. The idea is to utilize the fact that in terms of spatial dependence,
1/N(\tau)
is just a constant. To remove it, we can apply an operator
P
, whose kernel contains constants, to equation (
2.16
). For Theorem
4.1
the choice
P=\partial_{\eta}
. For Theorem
4.2
,
P=\text{id}-\int_{0}^{1}d\eta
.

Here we try to discuss a general linear operator
P
acting on the spatial variable
\eta
. It maps a function
f
on
[0,1]
to another function on
[0,1]
, denoted as
Pf
. For a space-time function
f(\tau,\eta)
,
Pf
naturally extends to mean the spatial operation being applied to each time slide
\tau
, or more formally

(Pf)(\tau,\eta):=(Pf(\tau,\cdot))(\eta).

(4.72)

We do not specify the precise space or regularity for
P
here to focus on more algebraic structures. To get rid of
1/N(\tau)
, we require that its kernel
\ker P
contains constants. Then we can apply
P
to equation (
2.16
) to derive

P(\partial_{\tau}Q)+P(\partial_{\eta}Q)=P(K(Q)).

(4.73)

To proceed, we need more assumptions on
P
, roughly meaning it goes well with a solution
Q
. We summarize all assumptions as follows.

Assumption 5
.

For the linear spatial operator
P
, we assume

(i) Its kernel contains all constants, i.e.
\text{span}\{1\}\subseteq\ker P
.

(ii) For any classical solution
Q
, the commutator between
P
and
\partial_{\eta}

[P,\partial_{\eta}]Q:=P(\partial_{\eta}Q)-\partial_{\eta}(PQ)\equiv C(\eta),

(4.74)

gives a fixed spatial function.

(iii) For any classical solution
Q
, the following

(PQ)(\eta=1)-(PQ)(\eta=0)\equiv C,

(4.75)

is a fixed constant.

It is not difficult to check that both
P=\partial_{\eta}
in Theorem
4.1
and
P=\text{id}-\int_{0}^{1}d\eta
in Theorem
4.2
satisfy Assumption
5
. An analogous computation as in Theorem
4.2
implies the following general result.

Theorem 4.3
.

Suppose
P
is a linear operator satisfying Assumption
5
and
f
is a
C^{1}
function. Let
Q_{1},Q_{2}
be two classical solutions to (
2.16
). Then we have

\frac{d}{d\tau}\int_{0}^{1}f(P(Q_{1}-Q_{2}))d\eta=\int_{0}^{1}f^{\prime}(P(Q_{1}-Q_{2}))P(K(Q_{1})-K(Q_{2}))d\eta.

(4.76)

By choosing
f
such that
f(0)=0
and
f(x)>0
for
x\neq 0
, we point out that the quantity

\int_{0}^{1}f(P(Q_{1}-Q_{2}))d\eta

can be understood as a “distance” between
Q_{1}
and
Q_{2}
, see Corollary
4.2
. Controlling the right-hand-side of (
4.76
) is difficult for general
K
, see Theorem
4.1
and Lemma
4.2
. However, in the simple linear
K(Q)=kQ+b
and
f(x)=|x|^{p}
(
p>1
), we can obtain similarly to Theorem
4.2
the following:

Corollary 4.3
.

Suppose
K(Q)=kQ+b
is a linear function. Let
P
be an operator satisfying Assumption
5
. Let
Q_{1},Q_{2}
be two classical solutions to (
2.16
). Then we have

\|PQ_{1}(\tau)-PQ_{2}(\tau)\|_{L^{p}(0,1)}=e^{k\tau}\|PQ_{1}(0)-PQ_{2}(0)\|_{L^{p}(0,1)},

(4.77)

for every
p>1
. The identity holds whenever the two classical solutions exist towards time
\tau
.

In view of this framework, previous Theorem
4.1
corresponds to the case
P=\partial_{\eta}
and
f=|x|
, and previous Theorem
4.2
corresponds to the case
P=\text{id}-\int_{0}^{1}d\eta
and
f=x^{2}
.

5
Global existence and convergence to the steady state: mild interaction,
K^{\prime}<0

In this section, we prove the global existence and long term convergence to the steady state for a solution to (
2.16
)-(
2.19
). The result is in the regime when
K^{\prime}<0
and the size of
K
is not very large. We have already seen in Section
4
that the sign of
K^{\prime}
can make a crucial difference to the dynamics – in particular the distance between two solutions shrinks as soon as
k_{\max}<0
. For instance, if
K^{\prime}<0
and Assumption
4
holds. Here the size of
K
also comes into play for the existence of a global solution and the steady state. The complementary regime when
K^{\prime}>0
or
K
is large will be studied in the next section, where finite-time blow-ups can be shown.

We first state and prove a global existence result in Section
5.1
. As a key tool for the proof, we derive an integral equation for
1/N
. In Section
5.2
, we first prove a dichotomy on the existence of the steady state. Then combining these results with Theorem
4.1
, we show the long time convergence to the steady state.

Remark 5.1
.

The main results in this section agree with
[
38
, Proposition 1-3]
which are presented in different forms. Their statements are for the
\rho
-formulation (
1.4
)-(
1.7
) in
t
timescale. Here we develop proofs for
Q
-formulation (
2.16
)-(
2.19
) in the dilated timescale
\tau
, which reveal several hidden structures of pulse-coupled oscillators. For example, during the global existence proof we work with an auxiliary variable
H
(Proposition
5.1
) and develop an integral equation for
1/N
(Proposition
5.2
). These relations are not obvious in the previous formulation for
\rho
in
t
timescale. Besides, in
[
38
]
Assumption
4
is needed for the convergence result, which is relaxed here to
k_{\max}<0
.

5.1
Global existence

We define the following auxiliary variable, which is useful for the statement of global existence results,

(5.1)

In particular we impose the following assumption on the initial data.

Assumption 6
.

With
H_{\init}
defined in (
5.1

H_{\init}(\eta)>0,\hskip 18.49988pt\forall\eta\in[0,1].

(5.2)

Remark 5.2

(Connection to
\rho

.

By definition of
H_{\init}
, Assumption
6

\partial_{\eta}Q_{\init}(\eta)>K(Q_{\init}(\eta)),\hskip 18.49988pt\forall\eta\in[0,1].

(5.3)

In the
\rho

5.3
) becomes

\rho_{\init}(\phi)<\frac{1}{K(\phi)},\hskip 18.49988pt\forall\phi\in[0,\Phi_{F}],

\displaystyle\frac{d}{ds}h(s)=(\partial_{\tau}+\partial_{\eta})H(s,1-(\tau-s))=K^{\prime}(Q(s,1-(\tau-s)))(h(s)-\tilde{N}(s)),\hskip 9.24994pts\in[\tau-1,\tau],

Proposition 5.3
.

Let

Lemma 5.1
.

Let
(Q,\tilde{N})
be a classical solution to the relaxed problem (
3.8
)-(
3.11
). Suppose additionally
K^{\prime}\leq 0
on
\mathbb{R}
. Then for

\min_{\eta\in[0,1]}H_{\init}(\eta)\leq\tilde{N}(\tau)\leq\max_{\eta\in[0,1]}H_{\init}(\eta).

Proof.

Suppose
\tau_{\max}\in[0,1]
is a maximum point of
\tilde{N}
on
[0,1]
, i.e.
\tilde{N}(\tau_{\max})=\max_{\tau\in[0,1]}\tilde{N}(\tau)
, whose existence is guaranteed by the continuity of
\tilde{N}
. Then by (
5.17
) in Proposition
5.2
, we derive

\displaystyle\tilde{N}(\tau_{\max})

\displaystyle=\left(1-\int_{0}^{\tau_{\max}}c(\tau_{\max},s)ds\right)H_{\init}(1-\tau_{\max})+\int_{0}^{\tau_{\max}}c(\tau_{\max},s)\tilde{N}(s)ds

(5.30)

\displaystyle\leq\left(1-\int_{0}^{\tau_{\max}}c(\tau_{\max},s)ds\right)H_{\init}(1-\tau_{\max})+\tilde{N}(\tau_{\max})\int_{0}^{\tau_{\max}}c(\tau_{\max},s)ds,

where we have used that

when

is the maximum point on

. Then (

\left(1-\int_{0}^{\tau_{\max}}c(\tau_{\max},s)ds\right)\tilde{N}(\tau_{\max})\leq\left(1-\int_{0}^{\tau_{\max}}c(\tau_{\max},s)ds\right)H_{\init}(1-\tau_{\max}),

(5.32)

which implies

\tilde{N}(\tau_{\max})\leq H_{\init}(1-\tau_{\max})\leq\max_{\eta\in[0,1]}H_{\init}(\eta),

(5.33)

thanks to the positivity of

in (
5.19
). This proves the upper bound in (
5.29
), and the proof of the lower bound is similar.
∎

The second lemma allows us to control

on

Lemma 5.2
.

Let
(Q,\tilde{N})
be a classical solution to the relaxed problem (
3.8
)-(
3.11
). Suppose additionally
K^{\prime}\leq 0
on
\mathbb{R}
.
For

Then the following holds for

m_{\tau-1}\leq m_{\tau}\leq M_{\tau}\leq M_{\tau-1}.

(5.35)

Proof.

Note that
m_{\tau}\leq M_{\tau}
by definition. It boils down to prove
m_{\tau-1}\leq m_{\tau}
and
M_{\tau}\leq M_{\tau-1}
. We only give a proof of
m_{\tau-1}\leq m_{\tau}
here as the other is similar.

The proof is similar to that of Lemma

. Suppose
\tau_{\min}\in[0,1]
is a minimum point of
\tilde{N}

[\tau,\tau+1]

\tilde{N}(\tau_{\min})=\min_{s\in[\tau,\tau+1]}\tilde{N}(s)=m_{\tau-1}
, whose existence is ensured by the continuity of
\tilde{N}
. Then by (

, we have

\displaystyle m_{\tau}=\tilde{N}(\tau_{\min})

\displaystyle=\left(1-\int_{\tau_{\min}-1}^{\tau_{\min}}c(\tau_{\min},s)ds\right)\tilde{N}(\tau_{\min}-1)+\int_{\tau_{\min}-1}^{\tau_{\min}}c(\tau_{\min},s)\tilde{N}(s)ds.

Note that
\tau_{\min}\in[\tau,\tau+1]
implies that
\tau_{\min}-1\in[\tau-1,\tau]

\tilde{N}(\tau_{\min}-1)\geq m_{\tau-1}
and

\displaystyle\int_{\tau_{\min}-1}^{\tau_{\min}}c(\tau_{\min},s)\tilde{N}(s)ds

\displaystyle=\int_{\tau_{\min}-1}^{\tau}c(\tau_{\min},s)\tilde{N}(s)ds+\int_{\tau}^{\tau_{\min}}c(\tau_{\min},s)\tilde{N}(s)ds

\displaystyle\geq m_{\tau-1}\int_{\tau_{\min}-1}^{\tau}c(\tau_{\min},s)ds+m_{\tau}\int_{\tau}^{\tau_{\min}}c(\tau_{\min},s)ds,

\displaystyle m_{\tau}\geq\left(1-\int_{\tau_{\min}-1}^{\tau_{\min}}c(\tau_{\min},s)ds\right)m_{\tau-1}+\left(\int_{\tau_{\min}-1}^{\tau}c(\tau_{\min},s)ds\right)m_{\tau-1}+\left(\int_{\tau}^{\tau_{\min}}c(\tau_{\min},s)ds\right)m_{\tau},

which proves

. Note that we have used the positivity of the following two terms thanks to

(5.41)

∎

Remark 5.6

5.2
Convergence to the steady state

We first give a sharp condition about the existence of a steady state. We have the following dichotomy: either there exists a unique steady state to (
2.16
)-(
2.19
) when
K
is not very large (precisely (
5.47
)), or otherwise there is no steady state.

Proposition 5.4

(See also
[
38
, Proposition 1]
)
.

There is a dichotomy on the existence of the steady state: If

\int_{0}^{\Phi_{F}}\frac{1}{K(\phi)}d\phi>1,

(5.47)

then there exists a unique steady state to (
2.16
)-(
2.20
). Otherwise if

\int_{0}^{\Phi_{F}}\frac{1}{K(\phi)}d\phi\leq 1,

(5.48)

then there is no steady state.

Proof.

The steady state
(Q^{*},N^{*})
satisfies

\frac{d}{d\eta}Q^{*}=K(Q^{*})+\frac{1}{N^{*}},\hskip 18.49988ptQ(0)=0,\,Q(1)=\Phi_{F},

(5.49)

with
N^{*}>0
.
We can recast the formulation as to find a
N=N^{*}>0
such that the solution to the initial value problem

\frac{d}{d\eta}q_{N}(\eta)=K(q_{N}(\eta))+\frac{1}{N},\hskip 9.24994ptq_{N}(0)=0,

(5.50)

satisfies that the “first hitting time” to
\Phi_{F}
is
1
, or more precisely

\eta_{N}=1,\hskip 18.49988pt\eta_{N}:=\inf\{\eta>0:q_{N}(\eta)=\Phi_{F}\}.

(5.51)

Note that

\Phi_{F}=q_{N}(\eta_{N})-q_{N}(0)=\int_{0}^{\eta_{N}}(K(q_{N})+\frac{1}{N})d\eta\geq\frac{\eta_{N}}{N},

(5.52)

which gives an upper bound
\eta_{N}\leq N\Phi_{F}
and implies that
\eta_{0^{+}}:=\lim_{N\rightarrow 0^{+}}\eta_{N}=0
.

We can check that
\eta_{N}
is strictly decreasing w.r.t.
N
and depends on
N
continuously, by standard ODE arguments. Then, with
\eta_{0^{+}}=0
, we see a dichotomy that if
\eta_{\infty}:=\lim_{N\rightarrow 0^{+}}\eta_{N}>1
, then there is a unique
N>0
satisfying (
5.51
), corresponding to a unique steady state; otherwise
\eta_{\infty}\leq 1
and there is no solution, which means that there is no steady state.

Therefore it remains to calculate
\eta_{\infty}
. It can be verified that
\eta_{\infty}
is the “first hitting time” to
\Phi_{F}
for the following
q_{\infty}(\eta)

\frac{d}{d\eta}q_{\infty}(\eta)=K(q_{\infty}(\eta)),\hskip 9.24994ptq_{\infty}(0)=0,

(5.53)

which is formally just the case
N=\infty
in (
5.50
). Solving (
5.50
) using the change of variable
u(\eta)=\int_{0}^{q_{\infty}(\eta)}\frac{1}{K(\phi)}d\phi
gives

\eta_{\infty}=u(\eta_{\infty})=\int_{0}^{q_{\infty}(\eta_{\infty})}\frac{1}{K(\phi)}d\phi=\int_{0}^{\Phi_{F}}\frac{1}{K(\phi)}d\phi,

(5.54)

which induces the desired result.
∎

satisfying condition (

Now we have enough preparations to prove the long time convergence to the steady state.

6
Blow-up:
K^{\prime}>0
or strong interaction

In Section
5
, we have shown the global existence and convergence to the steady state, in the regime when
K^{\prime}<0
and the size of
K
is not very large. In contrast, this section presents finite time blow-up results in the regime when
K^{\prime}>0
or the size of
K
is large.

Remark 6.1
.

Let
\tau^{*}
be the maximal existence time in
\tau
timescale. Then the maximal existence time in the original
t
timescale is given by
T^{*}=\int_{0}^{\tau^{*}}\frac{1}{N(\tau)}d\tau
due to (
2.15
). We note that
\tau^{*}<+\infty
directly implies
T^{*}<+\infty
. More specifically, notice that
\sup_{\tau\in[0,\tau^{*})}\frac{1}{N(\tau)}<+\infty
whenever
\tau^{*}
is finite, which follows from (
3.2
) in Theorem
3.1
. Hence, we have

T^{*}=\int_{0}^{\tau^{*}}\frac{1}{N(\tau)}d\tau\leq\tau^{*}\sup_{\tau\in[0,\tau^{*})}\frac{1}{N(\tau)}<+\infty,

(6.1)

whenever
\tau^{*}<+\infty
. Therefore, finite time blow-up in timescale
\tau
implies finite time blow-up in timescale
t
.

6.1
Blow-up 1:
K^{\prime}>0

We recall from Remark
4.1
that
\min_{\phi\in[0,\Phi_{F}]}K^{\prime}(\phi)\geq k_{\min}
, thus assuming
k_{\min}>0
implies that
K^{\prime}>0
. Moreover, they are equivalent if additionally Assumption
4
holds.

Theorem 6.1
.

Suppose
k_{\min}>0
, where
k_{\min}
is defined in (
4.2
). Then every solution to (
2.16
)-(
2.19
) blows up in finite time, except if the solution were the unique steady state, when it exists.

Proof.

Suppose there are
two
global solutions, denoted as
Q_{1}(\tau,\cdot)
and
Q_{2}(\tau,\cdot)
, with
different
initial data, then by Theorem
4.1
the following distance grows exponentially in time

\|\partial_{\eta}Q_{1}(\tau)-\partial_{\eta}Q_{2}(\tau)\|_{L^{1}(0,1)}\geq e^{k_{\min}\tau}\|\partial_{\eta}Q_{1}(0)-\partial_{\eta}Q_{2}(0)\|_{L^{1}(0,1)}\rightarrow+\infty,\hskip 18.49988pt\text{as $\tau\rightarrow+\infty$}.

(6.2)

However, as in (
4.6
), we know the distance shall be uniformly bounded as

\|\partial_{\eta}Q_{1}(\tau)-\partial_{\eta}Q_{2}(\tau)\|_{L^{1}(0,1)}\leq 2\Phi_{F},

(6.3)

which leads to a contradiction.

We have shown that there is
at most
one initial data that leads to a global solution, otherwise we contradict the previous statement. Denote it as
Q_{1}(\tau,\cdot)
. Note that for all
\Delta\tau>0
,
Q_{1}(\tau+\Delta\tau,\cdot)
is also a global solution with initial data
Q_{1}(\Delta\tau,\cdot)
. Hence by previous arguments
Q_{1}(\Delta\tau,\cdot)
must coincide with
Q_{1}(0,\cdot)
, for all
\Delta\tau>0
. This implies that
Q_{1}
is the unique steady state.
∎

Remark 6.2
.

Similar blow-up results have appeared in
[
38
, Proposition 4]
under Assumption
4
, see Remark
4.1
, where the existence of the steady state is needed as an additional requirement. Here in Theorem
6.1
we do not need either an assumption on the steady state or Assumption
4
. We just need
k_{\min}>0
which is stronger than
K^{\prime}>0
so there is still some requirement on the second derivatives. Such an improvement is due to that in
\tau
timescale we can compare two arbitrary solutions (c.f. Theorem
4.1
), not necessarily one solution to the steady state (c.f. Corollary
4.1
).

Using the steady state as a reference, we can also obtain an estimate on the blow-up time.

Corollary 6.1
.

Suppose
k_{\min}>0
, where
k_{\min}
is defined in (
4.2
). Additionally assume (
5.47
) which ensures that there is a unique steady state
Q^{*}
. Then for every non-steady initial data
Q_{\init}(\eta)
, the first blow-up time
\tau^{*}
to the classical solution of (
2.16
)-(
2.19
) is bounded by

\tau^{*}\leq\frac{1}{k_{\min}}\ln\left(\frac{2\Phi_{F}}{\|\partial_{\eta}Q_{\init}(\cdot)-\partial_{\eta}Q^{*}(\cdot)\|_{L^{1}(0,1)}}\right)<+\infty.

(6.4)

Proof.

Denote the solution with initial data
Q_{\init}
as
Q(\tau,\cdot)
. A combination of (
6.2
)-(
6.3
) with
Q_{1}=Q
and
Q_{2}=Q^{*}
leads to

e^{k_{\min}\tau}\|\partial_{\eta}Q_{\init}(\cdot)-\partial_{\eta}Q^{*}(\cdot)\|_{L^{1}(0,1)}\leq 2\Phi_{F},\hskip 18.49988pt\forall\tau<\tau^{*},

(6.5)

which yields the result.
∎

Remark 6.3
.

Using Theorem
4.2
, we can obtain a similar estimate on the blow-up time which involves the modified
L^{2}
distance (
4.48
) in the setting
K(Q)=kQ+b
(
k>0
).

With the steady state criteria in Proposition
5.4
, we can refine Theorem
6.1
as

Corollary 6.2
.

Suppose
k_{\min}>0
, where
k_{\min}
is defined in (
4.2
).

(i) Assume (
5.47
). Then every solution to (
2.16
)-(
2.19
) except the unique steady state blows up in finite time.

(ii) Otherwise (
5.48
) holds. Then every solution blows up in finite time.

In summary, no matter the size of
K
, as long as
k_{\min}>0
, then every non-steady solution blows up. When
K
is small (
5.47
), the unique steady state is unstable. And when
K
is large (
5.48
), there is no steady state and therefore every solution blows up.

Indeed, next we will show in Section
6.2
that if (
5.48
) holds, then every solution blows up no matter the sign of
K^{\prime}
. This in particular implies that the second part of Corollary
6.2
holds, even without the assumption
k_{\min}>0
.

6.2
Blow-up 2: strong interaction

6.2.1
Proof via the characteristics

Theorem 6.2
.

Assume (
5.48
), then every classical solution to (
2.16
)-(
2.19
) blows up in finite time. Furthermore, we have the following upper bound on its maximal existence time
\tau^{*}

\tau^{*}\leq\int_{0}^{\Phi_{F}}\frac{1}{K(\phi)}d\phi\leq 1<\infty.

(6.6)

Proof.

We recall the following auxiliary equation for
q_{\infty}
(
5.53
)

\frac{d}{d\eta}q_{\infty}(\eta)=K(q_{\infty}(\eta)),\hskip 9.24994ptq_{\infty}(0)=0.

(6.7)

In the proof of Proposition
5.4
, we have shown that

q_{\infty}(\eta_{\infty})=\Phi_{F},\hskip 18.49988pt0<\eta_{\infty}=\int_{0}^{\Phi_{F}}\frac{1}{K(\phi)}d\phi\leq 1,

(6.8)

where the last inequality is (
5.48
).

Now we argue by contradiction to show the estimate on the existence time (
6.6
). Suppose the solution exists at least up to
\tau=\eta_{\infty}
. We consider
q(\eta):=Q(\eta,\eta)
along the characteristic of
\partial_{\tau}+\partial_{\eta}
in (
2.16
). Then we have

\displaystyle\frac{d}{d\eta}q(\eta)

\displaystyle=K(Q(\eta,\eta))+\frac{1}{N(\eta)}

(6.9)

\displaystyle=K(q(\eta))+\frac{1}{N(\eta)}>K(q(\eta)),\hskip 18.49988pt\eta\in[0,\eta_{\infty}],

(6.10)

with
q(0)=0=q_{\infty}(0)
. Therefore, we can apply the comparison principle for one-dimensional ODE to compare
q
and
q_{\infty}
and derive at
\eta_{\infty}

\displaystyle Q(\eta_{\infty},\eta_{\infty})=q(\eta_{\infty})>q_{\infty}(\eta_{\infty})=\Phi_{F}.

(6.11)

This is a contradiction since for a classical solution we shall have
Q(\eta_{\infty},\eta_{\infty})\leq Q(\eta_{\infty},1)=\Phi_{F}
.

∎

Recall that by Proposition
5.4
, (
5.48
) is the sharp criteria for non-existence of steady states. As steady states are naturally global solutions, Corollary
6.2
implies a sharp dichotomy.

Corollary 6.3
.

If there is no steady state, then every classical solution to (
2.16
)-(
2.19
) blows up in finite time.

6.2.2
Proofs via moments

Now we approach the question of blow-up via a different method, namely calculating the moments

\int_{0}^{1}m(Q(\tau,\eta))d\eta,

(6.12)

for some function
m(\phi)
. Note that
\int_{0}^{1}m(Q)d\eta
corresponds to
\int_{0}^{\Phi_{F}}m(\phi)\rho(t,\phi)d\phi
in the
\rho
formulation in
t
timescale (
1.4
)-(
1.7
). Our calculations are based on the following lemma. Using similar quantities to discuss blow-up in computational neuroscience models have been used in
[
5
,
51
]
.

Lemma 6.1
.

Let
(Q,N)
be a classical solution to (
2.16
)-(
2.19
). Then for
m(\phi)\in C^{1}[0,\Phi_{F}]
with
m(0)=0
, we have

\frac{d}{d\tau}\int_{0}^{1}m(Q)d\eta=\left(\int_{0}^{1}m^{\prime}(Q)K(Q)d\eta-m(\Phi_{F})\right)+\frac{1}{N(\tau)}\int_{0}^{1}m^{\prime}(Q)d\eta.

(6.13)

Proof.

Multiplying (
2.16
) by
m^{\prime}(Q)
, we derive

\partial_{\tau}m(Q)+\partial_{\eta}m(Q)=m^{\prime}(Q)K(Q)+\frac{1}{N(\tau)}m^{\prime}(Q),

(6.14)

Integrating (
6.14
) in
\eta
, we obtain

\frac{d}{d\tau}\int_{0}^{1}m(Q)d\eta+m(\Phi_{F})-m(0)=\int_{0}^{1}m^{\prime}(Q)K(Q)d\eta+\frac{1}{N(\tau)}\int_{0}^{1}m^{\prime}(Q)d\eta,

(6.15)

which simplifies to (
6.13
) as
m(0)=0
.
∎

Remark 6.4
.

In the
\rho
formulation in
t
timescale (
1.4
)-(
1.7
), the formula (
6.13
) corresponds to

\frac{d}{dt}\int_{0}^{\Phi_{F}}m(\phi)\rho(t,\phi)d\phi=N(t)\left(\int_{0}^{\Phi_{F}}m^{\prime}(\phi)K(\phi)\rho(t,\phi)d\phi-m(\Phi_{F})\right)+\int_{0}^{\Phi_{F}}m^{\prime}(\phi)\rho(t,\phi)d\phi.

(6.16)

By choosing appropriate
m(\phi)
in Lemma
6.1
, we can prove the following estimates.

Theorem 6.3
.

Assume (
5.48
). Let
(Q,N)
be a classical solution to (
2.16
)-(
2.19
), and denote
\tau^{*}
as its maximal existence time. Then we have

\left(\min_{\phi\in[0,\Phi_{F}]}K(\phi)-\Phi_{F}\right)\tau^{*}+\int_{0}^{\tau^{*}}\frac{1}{N(\tau)}d\tau\leq\Phi_{F}-\int_{0}^{1}Q_{\init}(\eta)d\eta,

(6.17)

and

\left(1-\int_{0}^{\Phi_{F}}\frac{1}{K(\phi)}d\phi\right)\tau^{*}+\frac{1}{\max_{\phi\in[0,\Phi_{F}]}K(\phi)}\int_{0}^{\tau^{*}}\frac{1}{N(\tau)}d\tau\leq\int_{0}^{\Phi_{F}}\frac{1}{K(\phi)}d\phi-\int_{0}^{1}\left(\int_{0}^{Q_{\init}(\eta)}\frac{1}{K(\phi)}d\phi\right)d\eta.

(6.18)

Proof.

Under (
5.48
), by Theorem
6.2
we already know
\tau^{*}\leq 1<+\infty
. Here our goal is to provide additional estimates (
6.17
)-(
6.18
). For (
6.17
) we choose
m(\phi)=\phi
in Lemma
6.1
to derive

\displaystyle\frac{d}{d\tau}\int_{0}^{1}Qd\eta

\displaystyle=\left(\int_{0}^{1}K(Q)d\eta-\Phi_{F}\right)+\frac{1}{N(\tau)}

\displaystyle\geq\left(\min_{\phi\in[0,\Phi_{F}]}K(\phi)-\Phi_{F}\right)+\frac{1}{N(\tau)}.

Integrating in time on
(0,\tau)
with
\tau<\tau^{*}
, we have

\displaystyle\left(\min_{\phi\in[0,\Phi_{F}]}K(\phi)-\Phi_{F}\right)\tau+\int_{0}^{\tau}\frac{1}{N(\tilde{\tau})}d\tilde{\tau}

\displaystyle\leq\int_{0}^{1}Q(\tau,\eta)d\eta-\int_{0}^{1}Q_{\init}(\eta)d\eta

\displaystyle\leq\Phi_{F}-\int_{0}^{1}Q_{\init}(\eta)d\eta.

Taking the limit
\tau\rightarrow(\tau^{*})^{-}
gives (
6.17
).

For (
6.18
), we choose more sophisticated
m(\phi)=\int_{0}^{\phi}\frac{1}{K(\tilde{\phi})}d\tilde{\phi}
in Lemma
6.1
, which satisfies
m^{\prime}(\phi)=\frac{1}{K(\phi)}
. Then we derive

\displaystyle\frac{d}{d\tau}\int_{0}^{1}m(Q)d\eta

\displaystyle=\left(1-m(\Phi_{F})\right)+\frac{1}{N(\tau)}\int_{0}^{1}\frac{1}{K(Q)}d\eta

\displaystyle\geq\left(1-m(\Phi_{F})\right)+\frac{1}{\max_{\phi\in[0,\Phi_{F}]}K(\phi)}\frac{1}{N(\tau)}.

Integrating in time on
(0,\tau)
with
\tau<\tau^{*}
, we get

\displaystyle\left(1-m(\Phi_{F})\right)\tau+\frac{1}{\max_{\phi\in[0,\Phi_{F}]}K(\phi)}\int_{0}^{\tau}\frac{1}{N(\tilde{\tau})}d\tilde{\tau}

\displaystyle\leq\int_{0}^{1}m(Q(\tau,\eta))d\eta-\int_{0}^{1}m(Q_{\init}(\eta))d\eta

\displaystyle\leq m(\Phi_{F})-\int_{0}^{1}m(Q_{\init}(\eta))d\eta.

Taking the limit
\tau\rightarrow(\tau^{*})^{-}
and recalling the choice here
m(\phi)=\int_{0}^{\phi}\frac{1}{K(\tilde{\phi})}d\tilde{\phi}
, we obtain (
6.18
).
∎

The term
\int_{0}^{\tau^{*}}\frac{1}{N(\tau)}d\tau
in the theorem above is the maximal existence time in
t
timescale, as denoted by
T^{*}
in Remark
6.1
. We conclude with the following remarks.

Remark 6.5
.

If
\min_{\phi\in[0,\Phi_{F}]}K(\phi)>\Phi_{F}
, then (
6.17
) implies an upper bound on
\tau^{*}
. Note however this pointwise condition is stronger than the integral condition
\int_{0}^{\Phi_{F}}\frac{1}{K(\phi)}d\phi>1
. The latter can be understood as the harmonic average of
K
on
[0,\Phi_{F}]
is greater than
\Phi_{F}
, i.e.

\frac{\Phi_{F}}{\int_{0}^{\Phi_{F}}\frac{1}{K(\phi)}d\phi}>\Phi_{F}.

(6.19)

Remark 6.6
.

The right hand side of (
6.17
) corresponds to
\int_{0}^{\Phi_{F}}(\Phi_{F}-\phi)\rho_{\init}(\phi)d\phi
in
\rho
formulation. Hence (
6.17
) may be interpreted as that a higher concentration of mass near
\Phi_{F}
results in an earlier blow-up. With the high connectivity assumption
\min_{\phi\in[0,\Phi_{F}]}K(\phi)>\Phi_{F}
, we have the following simple bound in original timescale

T^{*}=\int_{0}^{\tau^{*}}\frac{1}{N(\tau)}d\tau\leqslant\int_{0}^{\Phi_{F}}(\Phi_{F}-\phi)\rho_{\init}(\phi)d\phi.
