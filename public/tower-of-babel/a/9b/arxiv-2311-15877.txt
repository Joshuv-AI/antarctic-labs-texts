Why the controversy over displacement currents never ends?
Masao Kitano

Abstract
Displacement current is the last piece of the puzzle of electromagnetic theory. Its existence implies that electromagnetic disturbance can propagate at the speed of light and finally it led to the discovery of Hertzian waves. On the other hand, since magnetic fields can be calculated only with conduction currents using Biot-Savart’s law, a popular belief that displacement current does not produce magnetic fields has started to circulate. But some people think if this is correct, what is the displacement current introduced for. The controversy over the meaning of displacement currents has been going on for more than hundred years. Such confusion is caused by forgetting the fact that in the case of non-stationary currents, neither magnetic fields created by conduction currents nor those created by displacement currents can be defined. It is also forgotten that the effect of displacement current is automatically incorporated in the magnetic field calculated by Biot-Savart’s law. In this paper, mainly with the help of Helmholtz decomposition, we would like to clarify the confusion surrounding displacement currents and provide an opportunity to end the long standing controversy. Keywords: displacement current, Biot-Savart law, Ampere’s law, Maxwell-Ampere’s law, Helmholtz’s decomposition, non-stationary current

2
Magnetic Action of Electric Currents
and Displacement Current

In 1820, Hans Christian Ørsted discovered that
a compass needle swings
in response to an electric current flowing near it.
That same year, the relation between current and magnetic field was formulated in two ways;
Biot-Savart’s law and Ampère’s law,
which correspond to Coulomb’s law
and
Gauss’s law in electrostatics, respectively.

These magnetic field laws were based on the assumption that the current is flowing through a closed circuit.
Almost half a century later,
considering the case of unclosed current,
as in the case of charging capacitor,
Maxwell theoretically derived the necessity of
displacement currents.

His argument goes as follows.
By taking the divergence of both sides of
Ampère’s equation,

\mathop{\mathrm{curl}}{\mathchoice{\mbox{\boldmath$H$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$H$}}{\mbox{\scriptsize\boldmath$H$}}{\mbox{\scriptsize\boldmath$H$}}}={\mathchoice{\mbox{\boldmath$J$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$J$}}{\mbox{\scriptsize\boldmath$J$}}{\mbox{\scriptsize\boldmath$J$}}}
,
we have,

0=\mathop{\mathrm{div}}{\mathchoice{\mbox{\boldmath$J$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$J$}}{\mbox{\scriptsize\boldmath$J$}}{\mbox{\scriptsize\boldmath$J$}}}
,
with the identity
\mathop{\mathrm{div}}\mathop{\mathrm{curl}}=0
.
In other words, Ampère’s equation implicitely
assumes divergence-free currents.
This is also called the ”steady-state current condition,”
because
the charge conservation law,

\partial_{t}\varrho=-\mathop{\mathrm{div}}{\mathchoice{\mbox{\boldmath$J$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$J$}}{\mbox{\scriptsize\boldmath$J$}}{\mbox{\scriptsize\boldmath$J$}}}
,
implies steady charge distributions.
(
\partial_{t}=\partial/\partial t
is used for brevity.)
If this condition is not satisfied, i.e.,
\mathop{\mathrm{div}}{\mathchoice{\mbox{\boldmath$J$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$J$}}{\mbox{\scriptsize\boldmath$J$}}{\mbox{\scriptsize\boldmath$J$}}}\neq 0
,
Ampère’s law must
be modified as follows:

\displaystyle\mathop{\mathrm{curl}}{\mathchoice{\mbox{\boldmath$H$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$H$}}{\mbox{\scriptsize\boldmath$H$}}{\mbox{\scriptsize\boldmath$H$}}}={\mathchoice{\mbox{\boldmath$J$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$J$}}{\mbox{\scriptsize\boldmath$J$}}{\mbox{\scriptsize\boldmath$J$}}}+\partial_{t}{\mathchoice{\mbox{\boldmath$D$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$D$}}{\mbox{\scriptsize\boldmath$D$}}{\mbox{\scriptsize\boldmath$D$}}}.

(1)

The displacement current density term,
\partial_{t}{\mathchoice{\mbox{\boldmath$D$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$D$}}{\mbox{\scriptsize\boldmath$D$}}{\mbox{\scriptsize\boldmath$D$}}}
is added.
Now, taking the divergence of both sides, we have

\displaystyle 0=\mathop{\mathrm{div}}{\mathchoice{\mbox{\boldmath$J$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$J$}}{\mbox{\scriptsize\boldmath$J$}}{\mbox{\scriptsize\boldmath$J$}}}+\partial_{t}\mathop{\mathrm{div}}{\mathchoice{\mbox{\boldmath$D$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$D$}}{\mbox{\scriptsize\boldmath$D$}}{\mbox{\scriptsize\boldmath$D$}}}=\mathop{\mathrm{div}}{\mathchoice{\mbox{\boldmath$J$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$J$}}{\mbox{\scriptsize\boldmath$J$}}{\mbox{\scriptsize\boldmath$J$}}}+\partial_{t}\varrho.

The time derivative of Gauss’s formula,
\mathop{\mathrm{div}}{\mathchoice{\mbox{\boldmath$D$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$D$}}{\mbox{\scriptsize\boldmath$D$}}{\mbox{\scriptsize\boldmath$D$}}}=\varrho
, is used.
This is consistent with the charge conservation law.

Based on this reasoning,
in his treatise

One of the chief pecurialities of this treatise is the

\displaystyle\mathfrak{C}=\mathfrak{K}+\dot{\mathfrak{D}},\quad\text{\footnotesize(Equation of True Currents)}.

Hereafter we write the true (total) current as

\displaystyle{\mathchoice{\mbox{\boldmath$J$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$J$}}{\mbox{\scriptsize\boldmath$J$}}{\mbox{\scriptsize\boldmath$J$}}}_{\mbox{\scriptsize tot}}={\mathchoice{\mbox{\boldmath$J$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$J$}}{\mbox{\scriptsize\boldmath$J$}}{\mbox{\scriptsize\boldmath$J$}}}+\partial_{t}{\mathchoice{\mbox{\boldmath$D$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$D$}}{\mbox{\scriptsize\boldmath$D$}}{\mbox{\scriptsize\boldmath$D$}}}.

Equation (
1

Maxwell’s electromagnetic theory thus created is being organized
by the followers and spread to the academic world

Oddly, however, the doctrine

The main reasons are

•

Even in the presence of displacement currents,

•

A typical displacement current is one that occurs where the linear current is interrupted.
Then charges are accumulated at the endpoint and yield a spherically

The corresponding displacement current is also spherically symmetric

Various arguments against, for, or from a neutral standpoint about this theory,

\displaystyle\int_{C}{\mathchoice{\mbox{\boldmath$H$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$H$}}{\mbox{\scriptsize\boldmath$H$}}{\mbox{\scriptsize\boldmath$H$}}}\cdot{\mathrm{d}}{\mathchoice{\mbox{\boldmath$l$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$l$}}{\mbox{\scriptsize\boldmath$l$}}{\mbox{\scriptsize\boldmath$l$}}}=\int_{S}{\mathchoice{\mbox{\boldmath$J$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$J$}}{\mbox{\scriptsize\boldmath$J$}}{\mbox{\scriptsize\boldmath$J$}}}\cdot{\mathrm{d}}{\mathchoice{\mbox{\boldmath$S$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$S$}}{\mbox{\scriptsize\boldmath$S$}}{\mbox{\scriptsize\boldmath$S$}}},

Inappropriate Problem Setting

In this section, we show that
the question whether the displacement current creates a magnetic field or not
is totally meaningless.

The question can be broken down
in the following way.
When the total current density
{\mathchoice{\mbox{\boldmath$J$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$J$}}{\mbox{\scriptsize\boldmath$J$}}{\mbox{\scriptsize\boldmath$J$}}}_{\mbox{\scriptsize tot}}

And if we can prove
{\mathchoice{\mbox{\boldmath$H$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$H$}}{\mbox{\scriptsize\boldmath$H$}}{\mbox{\scriptsize\boldmath$H$}}}_{\mbox{\scriptsize d}}=0\,(\neq 0)

then the answer is no (yes).
But, in the first place, what are the (local) equations that these magnetic fields obey?
The only possible choice seems to be

\displaystyle\mathop{\mathrm{curl}}{\mathchoice{\mbox{\boldmath$H$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$H$}}{\mbox{\scriptsize\boldmath$H$}}{\mbox{\scriptsize\boldmath$H$}}}_{\mbox{\scriptsize c}}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle?}}{{=}}{\mathchoice{\mbox{\boldmath$J$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$J$}}{\mbox{\scriptsize\boldmath$J$}}{\mbox{\scriptsize\boldmath$J$}}},\quad\mathop{\mathrm{curl}}{\mathchoice{\mbox{\boldmath$H$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$H$}}{\mbox{\scriptsize\boldmath$H$}}{\mbox{\scriptsize\boldmath$H$}}}_{\mbox{\scriptsize d}}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle?}}{{=}}\partial_{t}{\mathchoice{\mbox{\boldmath$D$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$D$}}{\mbox{\scriptsize\boldmath$D$}}{\mbox{\scriptsize\boldmath$D$}}},

(4)

but it
leads us to the contradiction when we take the divergence;

\displaystyle 0\stackrel{{\scriptstyle?}}{{=}}\mathop{\mathrm{div}}{\mathchoice{\mbox{\boldmath$J$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$J$}}{\mbox{\scriptsize\boldmath$J$}}{\mbox{\scriptsize\boldmath$J$}}}\neq 0,\quad 0\stackrel{{\scriptstyle?}}{{=}}\mathop{\mathrm{div}}\partial_{t}{\mathchoice{\mbox{\boldmath$D$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$D$}}{\mbox{\scriptsize\boldmath$D$}}{\mbox{\scriptsize\boldmath$D$}}}\neq 0.

What did we introduce the displacement current for?

We have found that

created the displacement current cannot be defined

it makes no sense to ask whether such
an undefinable quantity is zero or not.

In general, if we want to solve the

\mathop{\mathrm{curl}}{\mathchoice{\mbox{\boldmath$H$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$H$}}{\mbox{\scriptsize\boldmath$H$}}{\mbox{\scriptsize\boldmath$H$}}}={\mathchoice{\mbox{\boldmath$J$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$J$}}{\mbox{\scriptsize\boldmath$J$}}{\mbox{\scriptsize\boldmath$J$}}}+\partial_{t}{\mathchoice{\mbox{\boldmath$D$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$D$}}{\mbox{\scriptsize\boldmath$D$}}{\mbox{\scriptsize\boldmath$D$}}}

\displaystyle\mathop{\mathrm{curl}}{\mathchoice{\mbox{\boldmath$H$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$H$}}{\mbox{\scriptsize\boldmath$H$}}{\mbox{\scriptsize\boldmath$H$}}}_{1}={\mathchoice{\mbox{\boldmath$J$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$J$}}{\mbox{\scriptsize\boldmath$J$}}{\mbox{\scriptsize\boldmath$J$}}}_{1}+\partial_{t}{\mathchoice{\mbox{\boldmath$D$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$D$}}{\mbox{\scriptsize\boldmath$D$}}{\mbox{\scriptsize\boldmath$D$}}}_{1},\quad\mathop{\mathrm{curl}}{\mathchoice{\mbox{\boldmath$H$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$H$}}{\mbox{\scriptsize\boldmath$H$}}{\mbox{\scriptsize\boldmath$H$}}}_{2}

\displaystyle={\mathchoice{\mbox{\boldmath$J$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$J$}}{\mbox{\scriptsize\boldmath$J$}}{\mbox{\scriptsize\boldmath$J$}}}_{2}+\partial_{t}{\mathchoice{\mbox{\boldmath$D$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$D$}}{\mbox{\scriptsize\boldmath$D$}}{\mbox{\scriptsize\boldmath$D$}}}_{2},

and divide not only the current density but also the displacement current density term, so that each of them satisfies

\displaystyle\mathop{\mathrm{div}}({\mathchoice{\mbox{\boldmath$J$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$J$}}{\mbox{\scriptsize\boldmath$J$}}{\mbox{\scriptsize\boldmath$J$}}}_{1}+\partial_{t}{\mathchoice{\mbox{\boldmath$D$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$D$}}{\mbox{\scriptsize\boldmath$D$}}{\mbox{\scriptsize\boldmath$D$}}}_{1})=0,\quad\mathop{\mathrm{div}}({\mathchoice{\mbox{\boldmath$J$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$J$}}{\mbox{\scriptsize\boldmath$J$}}{\mbox{\scriptsize\boldmath$J$}}}_{2}+\partial_{t}{\mathchoice{\mbox{\boldmath$D$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$D$}}{\mbox{\scriptsize\boldmath$D$}}{\mbox{\scriptsize\boldmath$D$}}}_{2})=0.

\displaystyle\Delta{\mathchoice{\mbox{\boldmath$H$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$H$}}{\mbox{\scriptsize\boldmath$H$}}{\mbox{\scriptsize\boldmath$H$}}}({\mathchoice{\mbox{\boldmath$r$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$r$}}{\mbox{\scriptsize\boldmath$r$}}{\mbox{\scriptsize\boldmath$r$}}})=I\Delta{\mathchoice{\mbox{\boldmath$l$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$l$}}{\mbox{\scriptsize\boldmath$l$}}{\mbox{\scriptsize\boldmath$l$}}}\times{\mathchoice{\mbox{\boldmath$G$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$G$}}{\mbox{\scriptsize\boldmath$G$}}{\mbox{\scriptsize\boldmath$G$}}}({\mathchoice{\mbox{\boldmath$r$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$r$}}{\mbox{\scriptsize\boldmath$r$}}{\mbox{\scriptsize\boldmath$r$}}}).

6
Helmholtz Decomposition of Vector Fields

From a mathematical point of view,
we take a closer look at
how the Biot-Savart equation automatically incorporates
the effect of displacement currents.
The current density field
J
(or a three dimensional
vector field in general)
can be divided into
the ”curl-free” component
{\mathchoice{\mbox{\boldmath$J$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$J$}}{\mbox{\scriptsize\boldmath$J$}}{\mbox{\scriptsize\boldmath$J$}}}_{\mbox{\scriptsize L}}
and the ”divergence-free” component
{\mathchoice{\mbox{\boldmath$J$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$J$}}{\mbox{\scriptsize\boldmath$J$}}{\mbox{\scriptsize\boldmath$J$}}}_{\mbox{\scriptsize T}}
, namely,

\displaystyle{\mathchoice{\mbox{\boldmath$J$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$J$}}{\mbox{\scriptsize\boldmath$J$}}{\mbox{\scriptsize\boldmath$J$}}}({\mathchoice{\mbox{\boldmath$r$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$r$}}{\mbox{\scriptsize\boldmath$r$}}{\mbox{\scriptsize\boldmath$r$}}})={\mathchoice{\mbox{\boldmath$J$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$J$}}{\mbox{\scriptsize\boldmath$J$}}{\mbox{\scriptsize\boldmath$J$}}}_{\mbox{\scriptsize L}}({\mathchoice{\mbox{\boldmath$r$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$r$}}{\mbox{\scriptsize\boldmath$r$}}{\mbox{\scriptsize\boldmath$r$}}})+{\mathchoice{\mbox{\boldmath$J$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$J$}}{\mbox{\scriptsize\boldmath$J$}}{\mbox{\scriptsize\boldmath$J$}}}_{\mbox{\scriptsize T}}({\mathchoice{\mbox{\boldmath$r$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$r$}}{\mbox{\scriptsize\boldmath$r$}}{\mbox{\scriptsize\boldmath$r$}}}),

\displaystyle\hskip 10.00002pt\mathop{\mathrm{curl}}{\mathchoice{\mbox{\boldmath$J$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$J$}}{\mbox{\scriptsize\boldmath$J$}}{\mbox{\scriptsize\boldmath$J$}}}_{\mbox{\scriptsize L}}({\mathchoice{\mbox{\boldmath$r$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$r$}}{\mbox{\scriptsize\boldmath$r$}}{\mbox{\scriptsize\boldmath$r$}}})=0,\quad\mathop{\mathrm{div}}{\mathchoice{\mbox{\boldmath$J$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$J$}}{\mbox{\scriptsize\boldmath$J$}}{\mbox{\scriptsize\boldmath$J$}}}_{\mbox{\scriptsize T}}({\mathchoice{\mbox{\boldmath$r$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$r$}}{\mbox{\scriptsize\boldmath$r$}}{\mbox{\scriptsize\boldmath$r$}}})=0.

(12)

This is the so-called Helmholtz decomposition

[
19
,
20
,
22
]
.
The uniqueness of the decomposition requires that the field quantities converge quickly to zero at infinity, which is satisfied in the present case.

The curl-free and divergence-free fields are also called
the “longitudinal” and “transverse” fields,
respectively, which
are denoted by subscripts “L” and “T”.
The latter names are derived from the relations
of their Fourier transform to the wavevector
k
;

\displaystyle{\mathchoice{\mbox{\boldmath$k$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$k$}}{\mbox{\scriptsize\boldmath$k$}}{\mbox{\scriptsize\boldmath$k$}}}\times(\mathcal{F}{\mathchoice{\mbox{\boldmath$J$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$J$}}{\mbox{\scriptsize\boldmath$J$}}{\mbox{\scriptsize\boldmath$J$}}}_{\mbox{\scriptsize L}})({\mathchoice{\mbox{\boldmath$k$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$k$}}{\mbox{\scriptsize\boldmath$k$}}{\mbox{\scriptsize\boldmath$k$}}})=0,\quad{\mathchoice{\mbox{\boldmath$k$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$k$}}{\mbox{\scriptsize\boldmath$k$}}{\mbox{\scriptsize\boldmath$k$}}}\cdot(\mathcal{F}{\mathchoice{\mbox{\boldmath$J$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$J$}}{\mbox{\scriptsize\boldmath$J$}}{\mbox{\scriptsize\boldmath$J$}}}_{\mbox{\scriptsize T}})({\mathchoice{\mbox{\boldmath$k$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$k$}}{\mbox{\scriptsize\boldmath$k$}}{\mbox{\scriptsize\boldmath$k$}}})=0.

The Biot-Savart law
for the three-dimensional current density
{\mathchoice{\mbox{\boldmath$J$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$J$}}{\mbox{\scriptsize\boldmath$J$}}{\mbox{\scriptsize\boldmath$J$}}}({\mathchoice{\mbox{\boldmath$r$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$r$}}{\mbox{\scriptsize\boldmath$r$}}{\mbox{\scriptsize\boldmath$r$}}})
is

\displaystyle{\mathchoice{\mbox{\boldmath$H$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$H$}}{\mbox{\scriptsize\boldmath$H$}}{\mbox{\scriptsize\boldmath$H$}}}({\mathchoice{\mbox{\boldmath$r$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$r$}}{\mbox{\scriptsize\boldmath$r$}}{\mbox{\scriptsize\boldmath$r$}}})=\int_{V}\!{\mathrm{d}}v^{\prime}{\mathchoice{\mbox{\boldmath$J$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$J$}}{\mbox{\scriptsize\boldmath$J$}}{\mbox{\scriptsize\boldmath$J$}}}({\mathchoice{\mbox{\boldmath$r$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$r$}}{\mbox{\scriptsize\boldmath$r$}}{\mbox{\scriptsize\boldmath$r$}}}^{\prime})\times{\mathchoice{\mbox{\boldmath$G$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$G$}}{\mbox{\scriptsize\boldmath$G$}}{\mbox{\scriptsize\boldmath$G$}}}({\mathchoice{\mbox{\boldmath$r$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$r$}}{\mbox{\scriptsize\boldmath$r$}}{\mbox{\scriptsize\boldmath$r$}}}-{\mathchoice{\mbox{\boldmath$r$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$r$}}{\mbox{\scriptsize\boldmath$r$}}{\mbox{\scriptsize\boldmath$r$}}}^{\prime}),

(13)

and its vortex is

\displaystyle\mathop{\mathrm{curl}}{\mathchoice{\mbox{\boldmath$H$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$H$}}{\mbox{\scriptsize\boldmath$H$}}{\mbox{\scriptsize\boldmath$H$}}}({\mathchoice{\mbox{\boldmath$r$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$r$}}{\mbox{\scriptsize\boldmath$r$}}{\mbox{\scriptsize\boldmath$r$}}})={\mathchoice{\mbox{\boldmath$J$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$J$}}{\mbox{\scriptsize\boldmath$J$}}{\mbox{\scriptsize\boldmath$J$}}}({\mathchoice{\mbox{\boldmath$r$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$r$}}{\mbox{\scriptsize\boldmath$r$}}{\mbox{\scriptsize\boldmath$r$}}})-\int_{V}\!{\mathrm{d}}v^{\prime}({\mathchoice{\mbox{\boldmath$\nabla$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\nabla$}}{\mbox{\scriptsize\boldmath$\nabla$}}{\mbox{\scriptsize\boldmath$\nabla$}}}^{\prime}{\cdot}{\mathchoice{\mbox{\boldmath$J$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$J$}}{\mbox{\scriptsize\boldmath$J$}}{\mbox{\scriptsize\boldmath$J$}}}({\mathchoice{\mbox{\boldmath$r$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$r$}}{\mbox{\scriptsize\boldmath$r$}}{\mbox{\scriptsize\boldmath$r$}}}^{\prime})){\mathchoice{\mbox{\boldmath$G$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$G$}}{\mbox{\scriptsize\boldmath$G$}}{\mbox{\scriptsize\boldmath$G$}}}({\mathchoice{\mbox{\boldmath$r$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$r$}}{\mbox{\scriptsize\boldmath$r$}}{\mbox{\scriptsize\boldmath$r$}}}\!-\!{\mathchoice{\mbox{\boldmath$r$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$r$}}{\mbox{\scriptsize\boldmath$r$}}{\mbox{\scriptsize\boldmath$r$}}}^{\prime}).

(14)

The first term is just the given current density.
With the charge conservation of law

{\mathchoice{\mbox{\boldmath$\nabla$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\nabla$}}{\mbox{\scriptsize\boldmath$\nabla$}}{\mbox{\scriptsize\boldmath$\nabla$}}}\cdot{\mathchoice{\mbox{\boldmath$J$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$J$}}{\mbox{\scriptsize\boldmath$J$}}{\mbox{\scriptsize\boldmath$J$}}}({\mathchoice{\mbox{\boldmath$r$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$r$}}{\mbox{\scriptsize\boldmath$r$}}{\mbox{\scriptsize\boldmath$r$}}})+\partial_{t}\varrho({\mathchoice{\mbox{\boldmath$r$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$r$}}{\mbox{\scriptsize\boldmath$r$}}{\mbox{\scriptsize\boldmath$r$}}},t)=0
,
the second term turns out to be the displacement current density:

\displaystyle\int_{V}\!{\mathrm{d}}v^{\prime}\partial_{t}\varrho({\mathchoice{\mbox{\boldmath$r$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$r$}}{\mbox{\scriptsize\boldmath$r$}}{\mbox{\scriptsize\boldmath$r$}}}^{\prime},t){\mathchoice{\mbox{\boldmath$G$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$G$}}{\mbox{\scriptsize\boldmath$G$}}{\mbox{\scriptsize\boldmath$G$}}}({\mathchoice{\mbox{\boldmath$r$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$r$}}{\mbox{\scriptsize\boldmath$r$}}{\mbox{\scriptsize\boldmath$r$}}}-{\mathchoice{\mbox{\boldmath$r$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$r$}}{\mbox{\scriptsize\boldmath$r$}}{\mbox{\scriptsize\boldmath$r$}}}^{\prime})=\partial_{t}{\mathchoice{\mbox{\boldmath$D$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$D$}}{\mbox{\scriptsize\boldmath$D$}}{\mbox{\scriptsize\boldmath$D$}}}({\mathchoice{\mbox{\boldmath$r$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$r$}}{\mbox{\scriptsize\boldmath$r$}}{\mbox{\scriptsize\boldmath$r$}}},t).

We can verify that
\partial_{t}{\mathchoice{\mbox{\boldmath$D$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$D$}}{\mbox{\scriptsize\boldmath$D$}}{\mbox{\scriptsize\boldmath$D$}}}
is
longitudinal.

Equation (
14
) can be rewritten as

\displaystyle\mathop{\mathrm{curl}}{\mathchoice{\mbox{\boldmath$H$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$H$}}{\mbox{\scriptsize\boldmath$H$}}{\mbox{\scriptsize\boldmath$H$}}}({\mathchoice{\mbox{\boldmath$r$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$r$}}{\mbox{\scriptsize\boldmath$r$}}{\mbox{\scriptsize\boldmath$r$}}})={\mathchoice{\mbox{\boldmath$J$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$J$}}{\mbox{\scriptsize\boldmath$J$}}{\mbox{\scriptsize\boldmath$J$}}}({\mathchoice{\mbox{\boldmath$r$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$r$}}{\mbox{\scriptsize\boldmath$r$}}{\mbox{\scriptsize\boldmath$r$}}})-\hat{L}{\mathchoice{\mbox{\boldmath$J$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$J$}}{\mbox{\scriptsize\boldmath$J$}}{\mbox{\scriptsize\boldmath$J$}}}({\mathchoice{\mbox{\boldmath$r$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$r$}}{\mbox{\scriptsize\boldmath$r$}}{\mbox{\scriptsize\boldmath$r$}}})=(\hat{1}-\hat{L}){\mathchoice{\mbox{\boldmath$J$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$J$}}{\mbox{\scriptsize\boldmath$J$}}{\mbox{\scriptsize\boldmath$J$}}}({\mathchoice{\mbox{\boldmath$r$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$r$}}{\mbox{\scriptsize\boldmath$r$}}{\mbox{\scriptsize\boldmath$r$}}}),

where
\hat{1}
is the identity operator, and
the operator

\hat{L}
acts on the vector field
{\mathchoice{\mbox{\boldmath$J$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$J$}}{\mbox{\scriptsize\boldmath$J$}}{\mbox{\scriptsize\boldmath$J$}}}({\mathchoice{\mbox{\boldmath$r$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$r$}}{\mbox{\scriptsize\boldmath$r$}}{\mbox{\scriptsize\boldmath$r$}}})
to
create a new vector field:

\displaystyle(\hat{L}{\mathchoice{\mbox{\boldmath$J$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$J$}}{\mbox{\scriptsize\boldmath$J$}}{\mbox{\scriptsize\boldmath$J$}}})({\mathchoice{\mbox{\boldmath$r$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$r$}}{\mbox{\scriptsize\boldmath$r$}}{\mbox{\scriptsize\boldmath$r$}}})

\displaystyle:=\int_{V}\!{\mathrm{d}}v^{\prime}({\mathchoice{\mbox{\boldmath$\nabla$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\nabla$}}{\mbox{\scriptsize\boldmath$\nabla$}}{\mbox{\scriptsize\boldmath$\nabla$}}}^{\prime}{\cdot}{\mathchoice{\mbox{\boldmath$J$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$J$}}{\mbox{\scriptsize\boldmath$J$}}{\mbox{\scriptsize\boldmath$J$}}}({\mathchoice{\mbox{\boldmath$r$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$r$}}{\mbox{\scriptsize\boldmath$r$}}{\mbox{\scriptsize\boldmath$r$}}}^{\prime})){\mathchoice{\mbox{\boldmath$G$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$G$}}{\mbox{\scriptsize\boldmath$G$}}{\mbox{\scriptsize\boldmath$G$}}}({\mathchoice{\mbox{\boldmath$r$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$r$}}{\mbox{\scriptsize\boldmath$r$}}{\mbox{\scriptsize\boldmath$r$}}}-{\mathchoice{\mbox{\boldmath$r$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$r$}}{\mbox{\scriptsize\boldmath$r$}}{\mbox{\scriptsize\boldmath$r$}}}^{\prime}).

(15)

The operator
\hat{L}
gives the longitudinal field components
of a vector field.

We define another operator

\hat{T}:=\hat{1}-\hat{L}
,
which gives the transverse field component

\hat{T}{\mathchoice{\mbox{\boldmath$J$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$J$}}{\mbox{\scriptsize\boldmath$J$}}{\mbox{\scriptsize\boldmath$J$}}}
.
The operators

\hat{L}
and
\hat{T}
are equipped with
the properties of projection operators, namely,

\displaystyle\hat{T}^{2}=\hat{T},\quad\hat{L}^{2}=\hat{L},\quad\hat{T}\hat{L}=\hat{L}\hat{T}=0.

As shown in Fig.
3
, the Biot-Savart law
gives the magnetic field due to
the transverse component of the
current density
{\mathchoice{\mbox{\boldmath$J$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$J$}}{\mbox{\scriptsize\boldmath$J$}}{\mbox{\scriptsize\boldmath$J$}}}_{\mbox{\scriptsize T}}=\hat{T}{\mathchoice{\mbox{\boldmath$J$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$J$}}{\mbox{\scriptsize\boldmath$J$}}{\mbox{\scriptsize\boldmath$J$}}}={\mathchoice{\mbox{\boldmath$J$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$J$}}{\mbox{\scriptsize\boldmath$J$}}{\mbox{\scriptsize\boldmath$J$}}}-{\mathchoice{\mbox{\boldmath$J$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$J$}}{\mbox{\scriptsize\boldmath$J$}}{\mbox{\scriptsize\boldmath$J$}}}_{\mbox{\scriptsize L}}
,
or due to the total current density

\displaystyle{\mathchoice{\mbox{\boldmath$J$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$J$}}{\mbox{\scriptsize\boldmath$J$}}{\mbox{\scriptsize\boldmath$J$}}}_{\mbox{\scriptsize tot}}:={\mathchoice{\mbox{\boldmath$J$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$J$}}{\mbox{\scriptsize\boldmath$J$}}{\mbox{\scriptsize\boldmath$J$}}}+{\mathchoice{\mbox{\boldmath$J$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$J$}}{\mbox{\scriptsize\boldmath$J$}}{\mbox{\scriptsize\boldmath$J$}}}_{\mbox{\scriptsize disp}}.

From these equations,
we know that the displacement current is
the curl-free (longitudinal) component with sign changed of the given current:

\displaystyle{\mathchoice{\mbox{\boldmath$J$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$J$}}{\mbox{\scriptsize\boldmath$J$}}{\mbox{\scriptsize\boldmath$J$}}}_{\mbox{\scriptsize disp}}=-{\mathchoice{\mbox{\boldmath$J$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$J$}}{\mbox{\scriptsize\boldmath$J$}}{\mbox{\scriptsize\boldmath$J$}}}_{\mbox{\scriptsize L}}\>(=\partial_{t}{\mathchoice{\mbox{\boldmath$D$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$D$}}{\mbox{\scriptsize\boldmath$D$}}{\mbox{\scriptsize\boldmath$D$}}}).

(16)

Adding the displacement current density
{\mathchoice{\mbox{\boldmath$J$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$J$}}{\mbox{\scriptsize\boldmath$J$}}{\mbox{\scriptsize\boldmath$J$}}}_{\mbox{\scriptsize disp}}

to the current density
J
means the cancellation of the longitudinal component
{\mathchoice{\mbox{\boldmath$J$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$J$}}{\mbox{\scriptsize\boldmath$J$}}{\mbox{\scriptsize\boldmath$J$}}}_{\mbox{\scriptsize L}}

to yield
{\mathchoice{\mbox{\boldmath$J$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$J$}}{\mbox{\scriptsize\boldmath$J$}}{\mbox{\scriptsize\boldmath$J$}}}_{\mbox{\scriptsize T}}
or
{\mathchoice{\mbox{\boldmath$J$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$J$}}{\mbox{\scriptsize\boldmath$J$}}{\mbox{\scriptsize\boldmath$J$}}}_{\mbox{\scriptsize tot}}
.
The difference between the subtraction and the sum gives
a very different impression.

In the cource of deriving
the Biot-Savart’s law from Maxwell’s
equations, we confirm why the former includes
the effect of displacement currents.
With the curl of the Maxwell-Ampères’s law,

\mathop{\mathrm{curl}}\mathop{\mathrm{curl}}{\mathchoice{\mbox{\boldmath$H$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$H$}}{\mbox{\scriptsize\boldmath$H$}}{\mbox{\scriptsize\boldmath$H$}}}=\mathop{\mathrm{curl}}{\mathchoice{\mbox{\boldmath$J$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$J$}}{\mbox{\scriptsize\boldmath$J$}}{\mbox{\scriptsize\boldmath$J$}}}_{\mbox{\scriptsize T}}
,

{\mathchoice{\mbox{\boldmath$J$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$J$}}{\mbox{\scriptsize\boldmath$J$}}{\mbox{\scriptsize\boldmath$J$}}}_{\mbox{\scriptsize T}}={\mathchoice{\mbox{\boldmath$J$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$J$}}{\mbox{\scriptsize\boldmath$J$}}{\mbox{\scriptsize\boldmath$J$}}}\,+\,\partial_{t}{\mathchoice{\mbox{\boldmath$D$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$D$}}{\mbox{\scriptsize\boldmath$D$}}{\mbox{\scriptsize\boldmath$D$}}}
,
and
\mathop{\mathrm{div}}(\mu_{0}{\mathchoice{\mbox{\boldmath$H$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$H$}}{\mbox{\scriptsize\boldmath$H$}}{\mbox{\scriptsize\boldmath$H$}}})=0
, we have

\displaystyle\nabla^{2}{\mathchoice{\mbox{\boldmath$H$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$H$}}{\mbox{\scriptsize\boldmath$H$}}{\mbox{\scriptsize\boldmath$H$}}}=-\mathop{\mathrm{curl}}{\mathchoice{\mbox{\boldmath$J$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$J$}}{\mbox{\scriptsize\boldmath$J$}}{\mbox{\scriptsize\boldmath$J$}}}_{\mbox{\scriptsize T}},

where
\mathop{\mathrm{curl}}\mathop{\mathrm{curl}}=\mathop{\mathrm{grad}}\mathop{\mathrm{div}}-\nabla^{2}

is used.
The solution to this (vector) Poisson’s equation

[
22
]

is

\displaystyle{\mathchoice{\mbox{\boldmath$H$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$H$}}{\mbox{\scriptsize\boldmath$H$}}{\mbox{\scriptsize\boldmath$H$}}}({\mathchoice{\mbox{\boldmath$r$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$r$}}{\mbox{\scriptsize\boldmath$r$}}{\mbox{\scriptsize\boldmath$r$}}})=\int{\mathrm{d}}v^{\prime}{\mathchoice{\mbox{\boldmath$J$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$J$}}{\mbox{\scriptsize\boldmath$J$}}{\mbox{\scriptsize\boldmath$J$}}}_{\mbox{\scriptsize T}}({\mathchoice{\mbox{\boldmath$r$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$r$}}{\mbox{\scriptsize\boldmath$r$}}{\mbox{\scriptsize\boldmath$r$}}}^{\prime})\times{\mathchoice{\mbox{\boldmath$G$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$G$}}{\mbox{\scriptsize\boldmath$G$}}{\mbox{\scriptsize\boldmath$G$}}}({\mathchoice{\mbox{\boldmath$r$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$r$}}{\mbox{\scriptsize\boldmath$r$}}{\mbox{\scriptsize\boldmath$r$}}}-{\mathchoice{\mbox{\boldmath$r$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$r$}}{\mbox{\scriptsize\boldmath$r$}}{\mbox{\scriptsize\boldmath$r$}}}^{\prime})=:(\hat{f}_{\mbox{\scriptsize T}}{\mathchoice{\mbox{\boldmath$J$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$J$}}{\mbox{\scriptsize\boldmath$J$}}{\mbox{\scriptsize\boldmath$J$}}}_{\mbox{\scriptsize T}})({\mathchoice{\mbox{\boldmath$r$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$r$}}{\mbox{\scriptsize\boldmath$r$}}{\mbox{\scriptsize\boldmath$r$}}}),

(17)

where the operator
\hat{f}_{\mbox{\scriptsize T}}
is defined
so as to map a transverse current
{\mathchoice{\mbox{\boldmath$J$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$J$}}{\mbox{\scriptsize\boldmath$J$}}{\mbox{\scriptsize\boldmath$J$}}}_{\mbox{\scriptsize T}}

to the corresponding magnetic field
H
.
This map,
which can be written symbolically

{\mathchoice{\mbox{\boldmath$H$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$H$}}{\mbox{\scriptsize\boldmath$H$}}{\mbox{\scriptsize\boldmath$H$}}}=\mathop{\mathrm{curl}}^{-1}{\mathchoice{\mbox{\boldmath$J$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$J$}}{\mbox{\scriptsize\boldmath$J$}}{\mbox{\scriptsize\boldmath$J$}}}_{\mbox{\scriptsize T}}
, is
invertible, i.e., one-to-one.
For a general current
J
, we should have

\displaystyle{\mathchoice{\mbox{\boldmath$H$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$H$}}{\mbox{\scriptsize\boldmath$H$}}{\mbox{\scriptsize\boldmath$H$}}}=\hat{f}_{\mbox{\scriptsize T}}\hat{T}{\mathchoice{\mbox{\boldmath$J$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$J$}}{\mbox{\scriptsize\boldmath$J$}}{\mbox{\scriptsize\boldmath$J$}}}=:\hat{f}_{\mbox{\scriptsize BS}}{\mathchoice{\mbox{\boldmath$J$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$J$}}{\mbox{\scriptsize\boldmath$J$}}{\mbox{\scriptsize\boldmath$J$}}},

which correponds to
the Biot-Savart’s law (
13
).
The two-step operation,

\hat{f}_{\mbox{\scriptsize BS}}=\hat{f}_{\mbox{\scriptsize T}}\hat{L}

is
not invertible
.
The current distribution cannot be
uniquely determined from the magnetic field.
This fact is overlooked so often.

In order to fix the problem
of improper division of Eq. (
4
),
we can apply
\hat{T}
for each current.

\displaystyle\mathop{\mathrm{curl}}{\mathchoice{\mbox{\boldmath$H$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$H$}}{\mbox{\scriptsize\boldmath$H$}}{\mbox{\scriptsize\boldmath$H$}}}=\hat{T}{\mathchoice{\mbox{\boldmath$J$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$J$}}{\mbox{\scriptsize\boldmath$J$}}{\mbox{\scriptsize\boldmath$J$}}}={\mathchoice{\mbox{\boldmath$J$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$J$}}{\mbox{\scriptsize\boldmath$J$}}{\mbox{\scriptsize\boldmath$J$}}}+\partial_{t}{\mathchoice{\mbox{\boldmath$D$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$D$}}{\mbox{\scriptsize\boldmath$D$}}{\mbox{\scriptsize\boldmath$D$}}},\quad\mathop{\mathrm{curl}}{\mathchoice{\mbox{\boldmath$H$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$H$}}{\mbox{\scriptsize\boldmath$H$}}{\mbox{\scriptsize\boldmath$H$}}}_{\mbox{\scriptsize d}}=\hat{T}(\partial_{t}{\mathchoice{\mbox{\boldmath$D$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$D$}}{\mbox{\scriptsize\boldmath$D$}}{\mbox{\scriptsize\boldmath$D$}}})=0.

Then,
in the second equation,
the displacement current
\partial_{t}{\mathchoice{\mbox{\boldmath$D$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$D$}}{\mbox{\scriptsize\boldmath$D$}}{\mbox{\scriptsize\boldmath$D$}}}

certainly disappears and
{\mathchoice{\mbox{\boldmath$H$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$H$}}{\mbox{\scriptsize\boldmath$H$}}{\mbox{\scriptsize\boldmath$H$}}}_{\mbox{\scriptsize d}}
vanishes.
But we should remember that it reapears in the first equation and contribute to
H
.
We cannot erase the displacement current.

7.1
Semi-infinite linear current

If a constant current
I
is flowing along
the half line (
z\leq 0
) along the
z

the charge must be accumulated at the origin
as

Q(t)=It+Q(0)
, where
Q(0)
is the charge at time

.

Electric flux density of Coulomb type and
the associated displacement current density

\partial_{t}{\mathchoice{\mbox{\boldmath$D$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$D$}}{\mbox{\scriptsize\boldmath$D$}}{\mbox{\scriptsize\boldmath$D$}}}(t,{\mathchoice{\mbox{\boldmath$r$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$r$}}{\mbox{\scriptsize\boldmath$r$}}{\mbox{\scriptsize\boldmath$r$}}})=I{\mathchoice{\mbox{\boldmath$G$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$G$}}{\mbox{\scriptsize\boldmath$G$}}{\mbox{\scriptsize\boldmath$G$}}}({\mathchoice{\mbox{\boldmath$r$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$r$}}{\mbox{\scriptsize\boldmath$r$}}{\mbox{\scriptsize\boldmath$r$}}})

are generated.
We can derive the magnetic fields in two ways, i.e.,
with Biot-Savart’s law and
Maxwell-Ampère’s formula.

Using cylindrical coordinates
(\rho,\phi,z)
, the
Biot-Savart law for an open path,

L=\{{\mathchoice{\mbox{\boldmath$r$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$r$}}{\mbox{\scriptsize\boldmath$r$}}{\mbox{\scriptsize\boldmath$r$}}}^{\prime}=z{\mathchoice{\mbox{\boldmath$e$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$e$}}{\mbox{\scriptsize\boldmath$e$}}{\mbox{\scriptsize\boldmath$e$}}}_{z}\mid-\infty<z\leq 0\}
,
we have

\displaystyle H_{\phi}({\mathchoice{\mbox{\boldmath$r$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$r$}}{\mbox{\scriptsize\boldmath$r$}}{\mbox{\scriptsize\boldmath$r$}}})=H_{\phi}(\rho,z)=\int_{L}I{\mathrm{d}}z{\mathchoice{\mbox{\boldmath$e$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$e$}}{\mbox{\scriptsize\boldmath$e$}}{\mbox{\scriptsize\boldmath$e$}}}_{z}\times{\mathchoice{\mbox{\boldmath$G$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$G$}}{\mbox{\scriptsize\boldmath$G$}}{\mbox{\scriptsize\boldmath$G$}}}({\mathchoice{\mbox{\boldmath$r$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$r$}}{\mbox{\scriptsize\boldmath$r$}}{\mbox{\scriptsize\boldmath$r$}}}-{\mathchoice{\mbox{\boldmath$r$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$r$}}{\mbox{\scriptsize\boldmath$r$}}{\mbox{\scriptsize\boldmath$r$}}}^{\prime})

\displaystyle=\frac{I}{4\pi}\int_{-\infty}^{0}\frac{\rho\,{\mathrm{d}}z^{\prime}}{[(z-z^{\prime})^{2}+\rho^{2}]^{3/2}}=\frac{I}{4\pi\rho}\left(1-\frac{z}{\sqrt{z^{2}+\rho^{2}}}\right).

Secondly, to apply Maxwell-Ampère’s formula,
consider a spherical surface with radius
R=\sqrt{\varrho^{2}+z^{2}}
centered
at the origin.
We have a latitude line
C
defined by

\theta=\tan^{-1}(\varrho/z)=\text{const}
.
Let
S_{+}
(
S_{-}
) be the northern (southern) spherical crown
cut by
C
.
Note that
C=\partial S_{+}=-\partial S_{-}
.
Applying the Maxwell-Ampère formula (
1
) for both surfaces, we have

\displaystyle\oint_{C}{\mathchoice{\mbox{\boldmath$H$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$H$}}{\mbox{\scriptsize\boldmath$H$}}{\mbox{\scriptsize\boldmath$H$}}}\cdot{\mathrm{d}}{\mathchoice{\mbox{\boldmath$l$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$l$}}{\mbox{\scriptsize\boldmath$l$}}{\mbox{\scriptsize\boldmath$l$}}}=\int_{S_{+}}\partial_{t}{\mathchoice{\mbox{\boldmath$D$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$D$}}{\mbox{\scriptsize\boldmath$D$}}{\mbox{\scriptsize\boldmath$D$}}}\cdot{\mathrm{d}}{\mathchoice{\mbox{\boldmath$S$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$S$}}{\mbox{\scriptsize\boldmath$S$}}{\mbox{\scriptsize\boldmath$S$}}}=-\int_{S_{-}}({\mathchoice{\mbox{\boldmath$J$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$J$}}{\mbox{\scriptsize\boldmath$J$}}{\mbox{\scriptsize\boldmath$J$}}}+\partial_{t}{\mathchoice{\mbox{\boldmath$D$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$D$}}{\mbox{\scriptsize\boldmath$D$}}{\mbox{\scriptsize\boldmath$D$}}})\cdot{\mathrm{d}}{\mathchoice{\mbox{\boldmath$S$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$S$}}{\mbox{\scriptsize\boldmath$S$}}{\mbox{\scriptsize\boldmath$S$}}}.

The left hand side is

2\pi\rho H_{\phi}(\rho,z)
.
The middle side is evaluated as follows.
Since
the magnitude of the displacement current
on a sphere of radius
R
is

\partial_{t}D=I/(4\pi R^{2})
,
it is perpendicular to the sphere, and
the area of
S_{+}
is

|S_{+}|=2\pi R^{2}(1-\cos\theta)
, we have

\displaystyle\partial_{t}D|S_{+}|=\frac{I}{2}(1-\cos\theta).

Similary for the right hand side
using
|S_{-}|=2\pi R^{2}(1+\cos\theta)

and

\int_{S_{-}}{\mathchoice{\mbox{\boldmath$J$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$J$}}{\mbox{\scriptsize\boldmath$J$}}{\mbox{\scriptsize\boldmath$J$}}}\cdot{\mathrm{d}}{\mathchoice{\mbox{\boldmath$S$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$S$}}{\mbox{\scriptsize\boldmath$S$}}{\mbox{\scriptsize\boldmath$S$}}}=-I
, we have

\displaystyle I-\partial_{t}D|S_{-}|=I-\frac{I}{2}(1+\cos\theta).

Thus,
the same result is obtained by using either of the surfaces:

\displaystyle H_{\phi}(\rho,z)=\frac{I}{4\pi\rho}(1-\cos\theta)=\frac{I}{4\pi\rho}\left(1-\frac{z}{\sqrt{\rho^{2}+z^{2}}}\right).

This result agrees with the previous result by Bio-Savart’s law.
In this method we had to consider the displacement currents
explicitly, otherwise,
the solution is not uniquely determined.

7.2
Spherically symmetric current distribution

To back up the false claim that displacement currents do not create magnetic fields,
the fallacious theory that ”spherically symmetric currents do not create a magnetic field due to their symmetry” is developed.
Combining Biot-Savart’s equation and symmetry for each part of the current,
it is attempted to to show that the magnetic field is zero.

As an example,
let’s take a look at section 9.2
of Purcell’s textbook
[
18
]
.
Noting that a curl-free field like
displacement current density can be written as
a superposition of spherically symmetric Coulomb-type vector fields,
it continues,

… the magnetic fields of any radial, symmetrical current
distribution, calculated via Biot-Savart, is zero.
To understand why, consider a radial line through a given line
through a given location.
At this location, the Biot-Savart’s contributions from a pair of
points symmetrically located with respect to this line are equal and
opposite, as you can verify.
The contributions therefore cancel in pairs, yielding zero field
at the given location.

Here the fact is forgotten that Biot-Savart’s law includes the effect of displacement current, which
in this case is spherically symmetric
but flows in the opposite direction.

In his famous series of text book
[
23
]
,
Feynman correctly explained the situation of spherically
symmetric current
using a model where a small sphere with radioactive
material is squirting out some charged particles.

Physically speaking, as shown in Fig.
3
,
spherically symmetric currents can be thought of
as a large number of semi-linear currents isotropically
combined at a single point.
In this case,
the displacement currents at the endpoints add up to exactly cancel the original currents.
The total current becomes zero in all places,
and therefore the magnetic field is also zero.
The magnetic field vanishes not owing to the cancellation of real currents.
In short,
if the symmetric current (conducting or displacement)
is purely longitudinal,
the total current is zero.

Charging capacitor.
The spacing
d
is exaggerated compared with the
radius
R
.
The displacement current is uniformly distributed.

7.3
Charging capacitor

The Charging capacitor problem is one of the reasons why Maxwell came up with the idea of displacement current.
The debate continues over this model, as to whether the displacement current between the electrodes creates a magnetic field or not

[

,

24
]

Surprisingly, miscalculations are found,
from time to time, in dealing with this problem.
For example,
Roche
[

]

made wrong calculation
in his historical survey paper on the controversy
over the reality of displacement current.
Jackson
[
15
]
pointed out the mistake

For simplicity, let us assume an axisymmetric system around
z
-axis as shown in Fig.
5
.
Let
R
be the radius of the electrodes
and
d
be the spacing.
The system is assumed to be charged
by a constant current

through the conducting

Assuming that

is spatially uniform and

Since the current has no angular component,

H_{\phi}
.
The magnetic field due to the current
I

\displaystyle H^{\mbox{\scriptsize(1)}}_{\phi}(\rho,z)=\frac{I}{2\pi\rho}.

(18)

The current
I

z<-d/2

The linear density of this radial current

With the two-dimensional divergence formula we have

\rho^{-1}({\mathrm{d}}/{\mathrm{d}}\rho)[\rho K_{\rho}(\rho)]=\text{const}
.
Under the boundary conditions

(2\pi\rho K_{\rho})(0)=I
and
K_{\rho}(R)=0
, the
differential equation can be solved;

\displaystyle K_{\rho}(\rho)=\frac{I}{2\pi\rho}\left(1-\frac{\rho^{2}}{R^{2}}\right).

(19)

For the other electrode at
z=d/2
,
the surface current density is
-K_{\rho}(\rho)
.

The straight conductor has a gap

-d/2<z<d/2
of length

.
The

includes its contribution.
Therefore, we have to introduce a current of
-I

flowing in this segment
[

]
.
(Roche
[

]
missed this contribution,

If we connect this current element to the two surface currents,

In order to apply

\rho=\text{const}

between the electrodes (

).
For
\rho\leq R
,
we have

2\pi\rho H^{(2)}_{\phi}=-I+I{\rho^{2}}/{R^{2}}
,
or

\displaystyle H^{(2)}_{\phi}(\rho,z)=\frac{I}{2\pi\rho}\left(-1+\frac{\rho^{2}}{R^{2}}\right),

For
\rho>R
, we note
H^{(2)}_{\phi}=0
.
By superposition, we have
the magnetic field of all position as

\displaystyle H_{\phi}(\rho,z)=H^{(1)}_{\phi}(\rho,z)+H^{(2)}_{\phi}(\rho,z)

\displaystyle\hskip 12.91663pt=\begin{cases}\displaystyle\hskip 5.0pt\frac{I}{2\pi}\frac{\rho}{R^{2}}&(\rho\leq R,\,-d/2<z<d/2)\\[8.61108pt]

(20)

The difference between the magnetic fields inside and outside the electrodes

\displaystyle H_{\phi}(\rho,d/2+0)-H_{\phi}(\rho,d/2-0)=K_{\rho}(\rho).

This current splitting in Fig.

,
which follows the

is a clever way to avoid displacement currents except those between these electrodes.

.

As authors admitted in the very last paragraph

What we have shown, then, is that the Biot-Savart

8
Displacement Currents and Electromagnetic Waves

Let us look at the relationship between displacement currents and electromagnetic waves utilizing the Helmholtz decomposition (
12
).
From two of the Maxwell equations and the constitutive relation of vacuum

\displaystyle\frac{\partial{\mathchoice{\mbox{\boldmath$B$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$B$}}{\mbox{\scriptsize\boldmath$B$}}{\mbox{\scriptsize\boldmath$B$}}}}{\partial t}=-\mathop{\mathrm{curl}}{\mathchoice{\mbox{\boldmath$E$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$E$}}{\mbox{\scriptsize\boldmath$E$}}{\mbox{\scriptsize\boldmath$E$}}},\quad\frac{\partial{\mathchoice{\mbox{\boldmath$D$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$D$}}{\mbox{\scriptsize\boldmath$D$}}{\mbox{\scriptsize\boldmath$D$}}}}{\partial t}=\mathop{\mathrm{curl}}{\mathchoice{\mbox{\boldmath$H$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$H$}}{\mbox{\scriptsize\boldmath$H$}}{\mbox{\scriptsize\boldmath$H$}}}

(21)

\displaystyle{\mathchoice{\mbox{\boldmath$D$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$D$}}{\mbox{\scriptsize\boldmath$D$}}{\mbox{\scriptsize\boldmath$D$}}}=\varepsilon_{0}{\mathchoice{\mbox{\boldmath$E$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$E$}}{\mbox{\scriptsize\boldmath$E$}}{\mbox{\scriptsize\boldmath$E$}}},\quad{\mathchoice{\mbox{\boldmath$H$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$H$}}{\mbox{\scriptsize\boldmath$H$}}{\mbox{\scriptsize\boldmath$H$}}}=\mu_{0}^{-1}{\mathchoice{\mbox{\boldmath$B$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$B$}}{\mbox{\scriptsize\boldmath$B$}}{\mbox{\scriptsize\boldmath$B$}}},

we have the equations for
the plane waves propageted
in the
z
direction
(assuming
\partial_{x}=\partial_{y}=0
and
x
polarization, i.e.,
E_{y}=0
)

\displaystyle\varepsilon_{0}\frac{\partial E_{x}}{\partial z}=-\frac{\partial H_{y}}{\partial t},\quad{\mu_{0}\frac{\partial H_{y}}{\partial z}}=-\frac{\partial E_{x}}{\partial t}.

The d’Alembert solution to the hyperbolic
partial differential equations is

\displaystyle E_{x}(t,z)=f(z-c_{0}t)+g(z+c_{0}t).

The
f
and
g
are arbitrary functions.
These waves are propagated at
the velocity
\pm c_{0}=\pm\sqrt{\mu_{0}/\varepsilon_{0}}
.
If there were no displacement current term, then
c_{0}\rightarrow\infty
and no wave solution existed.

So far, we assumed the case where the steady current condition is violated
(
\mathop{\mathrm{div}}{\mathchoice{\mbox{\boldmath$J$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$J$}}{\mbox{\scriptsize\boldmath$J$}}{\mbox{\scriptsize\boldmath$J$}}}\neq 0
, i.e.

\partial_{t}{\mathchoice{\mbox{\boldmath$D$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$D$}}{\mbox{\scriptsize\boldmath$D$}}{\mbox{\scriptsize\boldmath$D$}}}\neq 0
).
We now relax the condition further and consider the case where
the magnetic field is also time-varying
(
\partial_{t}{\mathchoice{\mbox{\boldmath$B$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$B$}}{\mbox{\scriptsize\boldmath$B$}}{\mbox{\scriptsize\boldmath$B$}}}\neq 0
).

We separate Maxwell’s equations into the longitudinal and transverse components.
Since the magnetic flux density satisfies
\mathop{\mathrm{div}}{\mathchoice{\mbox{\boldmath$B$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$B$}}{\mbox{\scriptsize\boldmath$B$}}{\mbox{\scriptsize\boldmath$B$}}}=0
, we have

{\mathchoice{\mbox{\boldmath$B$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$B$}}{\mbox{\scriptsize\boldmath$B$}}{\mbox{\scriptsize\boldmath$B$}}}\equiv{\mathchoice{\mbox{\boldmath$B$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$B$}}{\mbox{\scriptsize\boldmath$B$}}{\mbox{\scriptsize\boldmath$B$}}}_{\mbox{\scriptsize T}}
, or
{\mathchoice{\mbox{\boldmath$B$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$B$}}{\mbox{\scriptsize\boldmath$B$}}{\mbox{\scriptsize\boldmath$B$}}}_{\mbox{\scriptsize L}}=0
.
The equation of electromagnetic induction is

\displaystyle\mathop{\mathrm{curl}}({\mathchoice{\mbox{\boldmath$E$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$E$}}{\mbox{\scriptsize\boldmath$E$}}{\mbox{\scriptsize\boldmath$E$}}}_{\mbox{\scriptsize T}}+{\mathchoice{\mbox{\boldmath$E$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$E$}}{\mbox{\scriptsize\boldmath$E$}}{\mbox{\scriptsize\boldmath$E$}}}_{\mbox{\scriptsize L}})=\mathop{\mathrm{curl}}{\mathchoice{\mbox{\boldmath$E$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$E$}}{\mbox{\scriptsize\boldmath$E$}}{\mbox{\scriptsize\boldmath$E$}}}_{\mbox{\scriptsize T}}=-\partial_{t}{\mathchoice{\mbox{\boldmath$B$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$B$}}{\mbox{\scriptsize\boldmath$B$}}{\mbox{\scriptsize\boldmath$B$}}}_{\mbox{\scriptsize T}}.

(22)

For the electric flux density,
from
\mathop{\mathrm{div}}({\mathchoice{\mbox{\boldmath$D$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$D$}}{\mbox{\scriptsize\boldmath$D$}}{\mbox{\scriptsize\boldmath$D$}}}_{\mbox{\scriptsize T}}+{\mathchoice{\mbox{\boldmath$D$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$D$}}{\mbox{\scriptsize\boldmath$D$}}{\mbox{\scriptsize\boldmath$D$}}}_{\mbox{\scriptsize L}})=\mathop{\mathrm{div}}{\mathchoice{\mbox{\boldmath$D$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$D$}}{\mbox{\scriptsize\boldmath$D$}}{\mbox{\scriptsize\boldmath$D$}}}_{\mbox{\scriptsize L}}=\varrho
,
the longitudinal component
{\mathchoice{\mbox{\boldmath$D$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$D$}}{\mbox{\scriptsize\boldmath$D$}}{\mbox{\scriptsize\boldmath$D$}}}_{\mbox{\scriptsize L}}

is related to the charge density
\varrho
.
On the other hand, the transverse component
{\mathchoice{\mbox{\boldmath$D$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$D$}}{\mbox{\scriptsize\boldmath$D$}}{\mbox{\scriptsize\boldmath$D$}}}_{\mbox{\scriptsize T}}=\varepsilon_{0}{\mathchoice{\mbox{\boldmath$E$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$E$}}{\mbox{\scriptsize\boldmath$E$}}{\mbox{\scriptsize\boldmath$E$}}}_{\mbox{\scriptsize T}}
is
related to the electromagnetic induction equation (
22
).

Substituting

{\mathchoice{\mbox{\boldmath$H$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$H$}}{\mbox{\scriptsize\boldmath$H$}}{\mbox{\scriptsize\boldmath$H$}}}={\mathchoice{\mbox{\boldmath$H$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$H$}}{\mbox{\scriptsize\boldmath$H$}}{\mbox{\scriptsize\boldmath$H$}}}_{\mbox{\scriptsize T}}=\mu_{0}^{-1}{\mathchoice{\mbox{\boldmath$B$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$B$}}{\mbox{\scriptsize\boldmath$B$}}{\mbox{\scriptsize\boldmath$B$}}}_{\mbox{\scriptsize T}}
,
into Maxwell-Ampère’s equation, we have

\displaystyle\mathop{\mathrm{curl}}{\mathchoice{\mbox{\boldmath$H$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$H$}}{\mbox{\scriptsize\boldmath$H$}}{\mbox{\scriptsize\boldmath$H$}}}_{\mbox{\scriptsize T}}

\displaystyle={\mathchoice{\mbox{\boldmath$J$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$J$}}{\mbox{\scriptsize\boldmath$J$}}{\mbox{\scriptsize\boldmath$J$}}}_{\mbox{\scriptsize T}}+\partial_{t}{\mathchoice{\mbox{\boldmath$D$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$D$}}{\mbox{\scriptsize\boldmath$D$}}{\mbox{\scriptsize\boldmath$D$}}}_{\mbox{\scriptsize T}}+({\mathchoice{\mbox{\boldmath$J$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$J$}}{\mbox{\scriptsize\boldmath$J$}}{\mbox{\scriptsize\boldmath$J$}}}_{\mbox{\scriptsize L}}+\partial_{t}{\mathchoice{\mbox{\boldmath$D$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$D$}}{\mbox{\scriptsize\boldmath$D$}}{\mbox{\scriptsize\boldmath$D$}}}_{\mbox{\scriptsize L}}).

Since the left-hand side is a transverse field (divergence-free), as in the case of a stationary field,
the longitudinal components cancel each other;

{\mathchoice{\mbox{\boldmath$J$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$J$}}{\mbox{\scriptsize\boldmath$J$}}{\mbox{\scriptsize\boldmath$J$}}}_{\mbox{\scriptsize L}}+\partial_{t}{\mathchoice{\mbox{\boldmath$D$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$D$}}{\mbox{\scriptsize\boldmath$D$}}{\mbox{\scriptsize\boldmath$D$}}}_{\mbox{\scriptsize L}}\equiv 0
.
This can be regarded as the role of the longitudinal component of the displacement current.
In summary, we have

\displaystyle\mathop{\mathrm{curl}}{\mathchoice{\mbox{\boldmath$E$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$E$}}{\mbox{\scriptsize\boldmath$E$}}{\mbox{\scriptsize\boldmath$E$}}}_{\mbox{\scriptsize T}}=-\partial_{t}{\mathchoice{\mbox{\boldmath$B$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$B$}}{\mbox{\scriptsize\boldmath$B$}}{\mbox{\scriptsize\boldmath$B$}}}_{\mbox{\scriptsize T}},\quad\mathop{\mathrm{curl}}{\mathchoice{\mbox{\boldmath$H$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$H$}}{\mbox{\scriptsize\boldmath$H$}}{\mbox{\scriptsize\boldmath$H$}}}_{\mbox{\scriptsize T}}={\mathchoice{\mbox{\boldmath$J$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$J$}}{\mbox{\scriptsize\boldmath$J$}}{\mbox{\scriptsize\boldmath$J$}}}_{\mbox{\scriptsize T}}+\partial_{t}{\mathchoice{\mbox{\boldmath$D$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$D$}}{\mbox{\scriptsize\boldmath$D$}}{\mbox{\scriptsize\boldmath$D$}}}_{\mbox{\scriptsize T}},

(23)

9
Summary

The controversy
over the meaning of displacement currents tends to get lost in the choice between creating a magnetic field or not.
Such confusion arises from
the following facts.

•

In the case of non-stationary currents, neither
magnetic field created by conduction current nor
that created by displacement current can be defined.

•

In solving Maxwell-Ampère’s equation by superposition,
the right-hand side
cannot be divided arbitrary ignoring
the inseparability of the displacement current and the current.

•

The effect of displacement current is automatically incorporated in the magnetic field calculated by Biot-Savart’s law.

The existence of displacement current is subtle and elusive, and it was only discovered through Maxwell’s deep insight.
He emphasized the importance of the unity of the current and the displacement current, and defined the sum of them as
total current
{\mathchoice{\mbox{\boldmath$J$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$J$}}{\mbox{\scriptsize\boldmath$J$}}{\mbox{\scriptsize\boldmath$J$}}}_{\mbox{\scriptsize tot}}
.
He wrote down the fundamental equations of the electromagnetic field using the total current
{\mathchoice{\mbox{\boldmath$J$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$J$}}{\mbox{\scriptsize\boldmath$J$}}{\mbox{\scriptsize\boldmath$J$}}}_{\mbox{\scriptsize tot}}
.

As we have seen,
each of the longitudinal and transverse components of
displacement currents plays a different role.
Although the former was the initial impetus for
the introduction of displacement currents,
it plays a shadow role
to counteract the longitudinal component of the current.
On the other hand,
the latter
plays a prominent role
in propagating electromagnetic waves

While attempting to distinguish the roles of current and displacement current,
researchers have fallen for the trap of Bio-Savart’s equation.
In particular, the statement that the displacement current does not produce a magnetic field may
leads to underestimation of displacement currents,
or even to lost sight of its essential role in electromagnetic waves.
