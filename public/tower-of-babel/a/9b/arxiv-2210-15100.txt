Diffusion orthogonal polynomials in 3-dimensional domains bounded by developable surfaces
S. Yu. Orevkov

Abstract
The following problem is studied: describe the triplets ( Ω , g , μ ) (\Omega,g,\mu) , μ = ρ ​ d ​ x \mu=\rho\,dx , where g = ( g i ​ j ​ ( x ) ) g=(g^{ij}(x)) is the (co)metric associated with the symmetric second order differential operator 𝕃 ⁡ ( f ) = 1 ρ ​ ∑ i ​ j ∂ i ( g i ​ j ​ ρ ​ ∂ j f ) \mathbb{L}(f)=\frac{1}{\rho}\sum_{ij}\partial_{i}(g^{ij}\rho\,\partial_{j}f) defined on a domain Ω \Omega of ℝ n \mathbb{R}^{n} and such that there exists an orthonormal basis of L 2 ​ ( μ ) L^{2}(\mu) made of polynomials which are eigenvectors of 𝕃 \mathbb{L} , and the basis is compatible with the filtration of the space of polynomials with respect to some weighted degree. In a joint paper with D. Bakry and M. Zani this problem was solved in dimension 2 for the usual degree. In the author’s subsequent paper this problem was solved in dimension 2 for any weighted degree. In the present paper this problem is solved in dimension 3 for the usual degree under the condition that ∂ Ω \partial\Omega contains a piece of a tangent developable surface. The proof is based on Plücker-like formulas in the form given by Ragni Piene. All the found solutions are generalized for any dimension.

1. Introduction

This paper continues the study of the diffusion orthogonal polynomials started
in [3] (see also [1], [7], [11], [12]).
It is devoted to the following problem posed by Dominique Bakry: describe
all triples
(\Omega,\mathbb{L},\mu)
where
\Omega

is a domain in
\mathbb{R}^{n}
such that
\Omega=\operatorname{Int}\overline{\Omega}
,

\mathbb{L}
is an elliptic second order operator of the form

\mathbb{L}(f)=\sum_{i,j}g^{ij}(x)\partial_{ij}f+\sum_{i}b^{i}(x)\partial_{i}f

[formula]

with
g^{ij}
and
b^{i}
continuous
in
\Omega
, and
\mu=\rho\,dx
a probability measure on
\Omega

with
C^{1}
-smooth density
\rho
,
and such that there exists a polynomial orthogonal basis of

L^{2}(\Omega,\mu)
formed by eigenvectors of
\mathbb{L}
, which is also a basis
(in the algebraic sense) of
\mathbb{R}[x]
,
x=(x_{1},\dots,x_{n})
,
and which is compatible with the filtration of
\mathbb{R}[x]
by the degree
(a variant: by a weighted degree, see [1], [7]). The latter
condition means that the space

P_{m}
of polynomials of degree
\leq m
is
\mathbb{L}
-invariant for any
m
.
We say that such a triple
(\Omega,\mathbb{L},\mu)

is a solution of the
Diffusion Orthogonal Polynomial problem

(DOP problem for short).
If in addition

\int_{\Omega}f_{1}\mathbb{L}f_{2}\,d\mu=\int_{\Omega}f_{2}\mathbb{L}f_{1}\,d\mu

for any pair of compactly supported functions
(for bounded domains this condition follows from the other ones), we say that

(\Omega,\mathbb{L},\mu)
is a solution of the
strong DOP problem
(SDOP problem
for short). In this case

\mathbb{L}(f)=\frac{1}{\rho}\sum_{i,j}\partial_{i}\Big(g^{ij}\rho\,\partial_{j}f\Big),

[formula]

thus

\mathbb{L}
is determined by
g=(g^{ij})
and
\rho
, and we therefore
talk about
(\Omega,g,\rho)
as a solution of the SDOP problem.
If
\rho=(\det g)^{-1/2}
, then
\mathbb{L}
given by (2) is the Laplace-Beltrami
operator for the metric
(g_{ij})=g^{-1}
.

As shown in [3, Thm. 2.21],
(\Omega,g,\rho)
is a solution of the SDOP problem
(and hence of the DOP problem when
\Omega
is bounded) if and only if
there exists a squarefree polynomial
\Gamma
such that:

Condition (A3) is equivalent to the fact that for any
germ
\xi:(\mathbb{R}^{n-1},0)\to(\mathbb{R}^{n},x)

such that
\Gamma\circ\xi=0
, one has

\xi^{*}(\omega_{i})=0,\quad\omega_{i}=\sum_{j}(-1)^{j}g^{ij}\,dx_{1}\wedge\dots\wedge\widehat{dx}_{j}\wedge\dots\wedge dx_{n},\quad i=1,\dots,n.

[formula]

Note that Conditions (A1)–(A3) are purely algebraic and they make sense for
polynomials with coefficient in any field
\mathbb{K}
. If they are satisfied for a field
\mathbb{K}
,
we say that
(g,\Gamma)
is a solution of the

algebraic counterpart of the DOP problem over

\mathbb{K}

(AlgDOP/
\mathbb{K}
problem for short).

In dimension 2, all solutions of the DOP problem are found in [3]
for the usual degree and in [7]
for any weighted degree. In the present paper we attack the classification
of the solutions in dimension 3 for the usual degree.
By (A3),
\partial\Omega
sits on an algebraic hypersurface
of degree at most
2n
, thus on a quartic curve when
n=2
.
The arguments in [3] essentially rely on the Plücker-like formulas
relating the singularities of this curve and those of its projectively dual curve.
It seems that this approach can be also applied at least in dimension 3.
Here we take the first step in this direction.
Namely, we describe all
irreducible surfaces
\Sigma
in
\mathbb{R}^{3}
whose projective dual has dimension
1

(i.e., is a curve, which we denote by
\check{C}
)
and such that a relatively open piece of
\Sigma
appears in
\partial\Omega

for some solution
(\Omega,g,\rho)
of the SDOP problem. Moreover, in the case
when
\check{C}
is not contained in any plane (in this case
\Sigma
is the tangent
developable of another curve
C
called the dual curve of
\check{C}
), we
describe all such solutions
(\Omega,g,\rho)
(Theorems 5.1 and 5.2).
If
\check{C}
is contained in some plane, then
\Sigma
is a cylinder
or a cone over some planar curve
A
. If
\Sigma
is a cylinder, then it is easy to
show that a piece of
A
occurs in the boundary of a two-dimensional solution.
If
\Sigma
is a cone, we prove in Theorem 7.1 that
\deg A=2
, thus
\Sigma
is
a standard quadratic cone. In the conical case there indeed exist some solutions
(see Remark 7.2) but we do not know
if this list is exhaustive.

To prove Theorems 5.1 and 5.2, we follow the strategy similar to that
in [3, §3].
Condition (3) yields equations for the coefficients of the
polynomials
g^{ij}
and those of local parametrizations of the curve
C
.
By solving them we obtain in §3 rather strong restrictions on a priori possible
types of local branches (real and complex) of
C
. Then in §4,
using Plücker-like formulas due to Ragni Piene [8] (introduced in §2),
we find all solutions of the AlgDOP problem
over
\mathbb{C}
, and then (in §5) we
find
\Omega
,
\rho
, and the real form of
g
satisfying
the remaining conditions (A4)–(A6).

In §6, for each bounded domain in Theorem 5.1, we show that the
Laplace-Beltrami solution is the image of Euclidean or spherical Laplace operator
through on an appropriate realization of quotient of
\mathbb{R}^{3}
or
\mathbb{S}^{3}
by
a Coxeter group, and we generalize this construction to any dimension.
In §7 we prove the aforementioned result about conical surfaces.

(b). If

, then

has parametrization

in some homogeneous coordinates.

Proof

We have
\widetilde{C}=\mathbb{P}^{1}
and we may assume that
p=(0:1)
,

,
and the mapping
\nu
is given by

(t:s)\mapsto(f_{0}(t,s):\dots:f_{3}(t,s))
,

Let the notation be as in §2 but we fix an affine chart in
\mathbb{P}^{3}

(x,y,z)

Using the approach from [3, §4], in this section we realize

6.1. Generalities

With a second order differential operator
\mathbb{L}
with no 0-order term
on a manifold
M
, is associated the operator “carré du champ”

{\lx@ams@boldsymbol@{\Gamma}}_{\mathbb{L}}(f_{1},f_{2})=\frac{1}{2}\Big(\mathbb{L}(f_{1}f_{2})-f_{1}\mathbb{L}(f_{2})-f_{2}\mathbb{L}(f_{1})\Big).

(see [2]). Notice that the operator
{\lx@ams@boldsymbol@{\Gamma}}_{{\lx@ams@boldsymbol@{\Delta}}}
(for the Laplace
operator on
\mathbb{R}^{n}
) plays a key rôle in [10] where it is denoted
by
\langle df_{1},df_{2}\rangle
.
If
\mathbb{L}
is given by (1) in some coordinates
(x_{1},\dots,x_{n})
, then

g^{ij}=\Gamma_{\mathbb{L}}(x_{i},x_{j})
and
b^{i}=\mathbb{L}(x_{i})
.
Let
\mathbb{f}:M\to\mathbb{R}^{n}
,
p\mapsto(f_{1}(p),\dots,f_{n}(p))

be a mapping such that
\Gamma_{\mathbb{L}}(f_{i},f_{j})=G^{ij}\circ\mathbb{f}
and

\mathbb{L}(f_{i})=B^{i}\circ\mathbb{f}
for some functions
G^{ij}
and
B^{i}
defined on
\mathbb{f}(M)
.
Then a direct computation shows that the operator

\mathbb{f}_{*}(\mathbb{L})=\sum_{i,j}G^{ij}\partial_{ij}+\sum_{i}B^{i}\partial_{i}

is such that
\mathbb{f}_{*}(\mathbb{L})(\varphi)=\mathbb{L}(\varphi\circ\mathbb{f})
for any smooth

\varphi:\mathbb{f}(M)\to\mathbb{R}
. We say that
\mathbb{L}_{*}(\mathbb{f})
is the

image of
\mathbb{L}
through

\mathbb{f}
.

Let
G
be a discrete group generated by orthogonal reflections acting on
\mathbb{R}^{n}

(see [4] for a general introduction to the subject).
We discuss here only bounded solutions of the DOP problem. Therefore,
when
G
is finite (a
spherical Coxeter group
or just
Coxeter group
),
we assume that the origin is a fixed point and we
restrict the action form
\mathbb{R}^{n}
to the unit sphere
\mathbb{S}^{n-1}
.
If
G
is infinite (an
affine Coxeter group
),
we assume that it contains a full rank subgroup of translations.
So, in both cases the orbit space
M/G
is compact (
M
is
\mathbb{R}^{n}
or
\mathbb{S}^{n-1}
).

If
G
is finite, it is known (see [5], [4, Ch. V, §§5–6]) that
the ring of invariant polynomials is freely generated by some invariant homogeneous
forms
I_{1},\dots,I_{n}
. The choice of the invariants
I_{j}
’s
is not unique (see, e.g., [6], [10] for different concrete choices)
but their degrees
d_{1},\dots,d_{n}
are uniquely determined.
These numbers (called
exponents
in [4])
for each Coxeter group can be found in Tables (Planches) I–X in [4].
One of the basic invariants is (if the action is irreducible)
or can be chosen to be
x_{1}^{2}+\dots+x_{n}^{2}
.
Let it be
I_{1}
. Then [3, Eq. (4.5)] implies that the image of the Laplace
operator
{\lx@ams@boldsymbol@{\Delta}}_{\mathbb{S}^{n-1}}
for
\mathbb{f}:\mathbb{S}^{n-1}\to\mathbb{R}^{n-1}
,

p\mapsto(I_{2}(p),\dots,I_{n}(p))
, is a solution
of the weighted DOP problem (see [1], [7], [12] for the definition)
with weights
(d_{2},\dots,d_{n})
on
\mathbb{f}(\mathbb{S}^{n-1})
.
However, in §§6.2–6.8 we show that for the Coxeter groups of types

A_{n}
,
B_{n}
, and their direct products, as well as for
D_{4}
, one can choose
the basic invariants so that the image of the Laplace operator is a solution
of the DOP problem (with weights
(1,\dots,1)
).

Consider now the case when
G
is an affine Coxeter group acting on
E=\mathbb{R}^{n}
.
It is shown in [4, Ch. VI, §3.4] that the ring of invariant Fourier
polynomials is freely generated by certain elements
f_{1},\dots,f_{n}
which are
explicitly described via the fundamental weights
\omega_{1},\dots,\omega_{n}

corresponding to some Weyl chamber
C
. One can check that the image of
{\lx@ams@boldsymbol@{\Delta}}_{E}
through

p\mapsto(f_{1}(p),\dots,f_{n}(p))
is a solution of the weighted DOP problem
with the weights
\alpha(\omega_{1}),\dots,\alpha(\omega_{n})
where
\alpha

is any linear function positive on
C
. In §§6.9–6.11 we show that
these are also solutions of the DOP problem (with weights
(1,\dots,1)
) for
the affine Coxeter groups of types
\tilde{A}_{n}
and
\tilde{C}_{n}
.
It seems plausible that the quotients by other spherical or affine
Coxeter groups never give a solution of the DOP problem.
In dimension
2
this fact follows from the classification in [3].

For each solution
(\Omega,g,\rho)
obtained as the image of a Laplace operator through
the quotient by a Coxeter group,

\mathbb{L}
is the Laplace-Beltrami operator for the metric
g^{-1}
,
hence
\rho=(\det g)^{-1/2}
.

D_{n}
acting on
E
is generated by the reflections in
the hyperplanes
x_{i}\pm x_{j}=0
. The ring of polynomial invariants is generated
by
s_{1},\dots,s_{n-1}
and
\hat{s}_{n}=\sqrt{s_{n}}=x_{1}\dots x_{n}
.
The values of
{\lx@ams@boldsymbol@{\Gamma}}(s_{a},s_{b})
and
{\lx@ams@boldsymbol@{\Delta}}(s_{a})

are already computed in §6.3, and we have (recall that

(s_{0},s_{1})|_{\mathbb{S}^{n-1}}=(1,1)
)

\begin{split}{\lx@ams@boldsymbol@{\Gamma}}(s_{a},\hat{s}_{n})&={\lx@ams@boldsymbol@{\Gamma}}(s_{a},s_{n}^{1/2})=\tfrac{1}{2}s_{n}^{-1/2}{\lx@ams@boldsymbol@{\Gamma}}(s_{a},s_{n})\overset\text{(18)}\to{=}-2ans_{a}\hat{s}_{n}+2(n-a+1)s_{a-1}\hat{s}_{n},\\
{\lx@ams@boldsymbol@{\Gamma}}(\hat{s}_{n},\hat{s}_{n})&={\lx@ams@boldsymbol@{\Gamma}}(s_{n}^{1/2},s_{n}^{1/2})=\tfrac{1}{4}s_{n}^{-1}{\lx@ams@boldsymbol@{\Gamma}}(s_{n},s_{n})\overset\text{(18)}\to{=}-n^{2}\hat{s}_{n}^{2}+s_{n-1},\\
{\lx@ams@boldsymbol@{\Delta}}(\hat{s}_{n})&={\lx@ams@boldsymbol@{\Delta}}_{E}(\hat{s}_{n})-2n(n-1)\hat{s}_{n}=-2n(n-1)\hat{s}_{n}\qquad\text{(by [3, Eq.~(4.5)]).}\end{split}

Thus, for a given
n
, the image of
{\lx@ams@boldsymbol@{\Delta}}
is a solution of the DOP problem
if and only if, for any
a,b<n
,
{\lx@ams@boldsymbol@{\Gamma}}(s_{a},s_{b})
does not contain any monomial
of the form
s_{k}s_{n}
with
2\leq k\leq n
. This is the case for
n=4
.
The corresponding matrix
g
is

\left(\begin{matrix}-16x^{2}+4x+12y&-24xy+8y+16z^{2}&-16xz+6z\\
-24xy+8y+16z^{2}&-36y^{2}+4xy+12z^{2}&-24yz+4xz\\
-16xz+6z&-24yz+4xz&-16z^{2}+y\end{matrix}\right)

The projection of the cuspidal edge of the surface
\deg g=0

onto the
xz
-plane is the deltoid
(see Figure 5 and [3, §4.12]) up to affine
transformation of
\mathbb{R}^{2}
.

If
n\geq 5
, then
\Phi_{*}({\lx@ams@boldsymbol@{\Delta}})
is not a solution of the DOP problem
because, for example,

{\lx@ams@boldsymbol@{\Gamma}}(s_{4},s_{n-1})=-16(n-1)s_{4}s_{n-1}+4(n-4)s_{3}s_{n-1}+4(n-2)s_{2}\hat{s}_{n}^{2}

has a monomial of degree
3
.
However, for any
n
, it is, evidently, a solution of the
weighted DOP problem with weights
(1,\dots,1,\tfrac{1}{2})
.

6.6. Direct products of Coxeter groups

Let finite Coxeter groups
G_{\alpha}
,

\alpha=1,\dots,m
,
act on vector spaces
E_{\alpha}
,
\dim E_{\alpha}=n_{\alpha}
.
We assume that these representations are irreducible or trivial.
Consider the diagonal action of
G=G_{1}\times\dots\times G_{m}

on
E=\bigoplus_{\alpha}E_{\alpha}
. Let
n=\sum_{\alpha}n_{\alpha}
.
We denote the Laplace operator and the corresponding “carré du champ” on the
unit sphere in
E
(resp. in
E_{\alpha}
) by
{\lx@ams@boldsymbol@{\Delta}}
and
{\lx@ams@boldsymbol@{\Gamma}}

(resp. by
{\lx@ams@boldsymbol@{\Delta}}_{\alpha}
and
{\lx@ams@boldsymbol@{\Gamma}}_{\alpha}
).
Let
I_{\alpha,k}
,
k=1,\dots,n_{\alpha}
, be sets of basic invariant
homogeneous polynomials for the respective group actions,

d_{\alpha,k}=\deg I_{\alpha,k}
.
We assume that
d_{\alpha,1}
is minimal among the
d_{\alpha,k}
’s.
Then
d_{\alpha,1}=2
unless
G_{\alpha}
is trivial.

Let
g_{\alpha}^{ij}\big(x_{\alpha,1},\dots,x_{\alpha,{n_{\alpha}}}\big)

and
b_{\alpha}^{i}\big(x_{\alpha,1},\dots,x_{\alpha,{n_{\alpha}}}\big)
be the polynomials
such that

{\lx@ams@boldsymbol@{\Gamma}}_{\alpha}(I_{\alpha,i},I_{\alpha,j})=g_{\alpha}^{ij}\big(I_{\alpha,1},\dots,I_{\alpha,n_{\alpha}}\big),\qquad{\lx@ams@boldsymbol@{\Delta}}_{\alpha}(I_{\alpha,i})=b_{\alpha}^{i}\big(I_{\alpha,1},\dots,I_{\alpha,n_{\alpha}}\big).

We assume that
\deg g^{ij}_{\alpha}\leq 2
and
b^{i}_{\alpha}\leq 1

for any
i,j,\alpha\geq 1

(notice that this condition is fulfilled for

A_{n}
and
B_{n}
, but not for
D_{4}
; see §§6.2–6.5).

First construction.

Suppose that
d_{1,1}=\dots=d_{m,1}=2
,
i.e. all the
I_{\alpha,1}
are positive definite quadratic forms.
Let
\mathbb{S}^{n-1}
be the sphere in
E
given by the equation

\sum_{\alpha}I_{\alpha,1}=0
and let

\Phi:\mathbb{S}^{n-1}\to\mathbb{R}^{n-1}
be the mapping defined by

p\mapsto\big(\tilde{I}_{1}(p),I_{2}(p),\dots,I_{m}(p)\big)
, where

I_{\alpha}=(I_{\alpha,1},\dots,I_{\alpha,n_{\alpha}})
and

\tilde{I}_{1}=(I_{1,2},\dots,I_{1,n_{1}})
.
It is easy to see that the image of

{\lx@ams@boldsymbol@{\Delta}}
through
\Phi
is a solution of the DOP problem.
Denote the coordinates in the target space
\mathbb{R}^{n-1}
by

(\tilde{\mathbb{x}}_{1},\mathbb{x}_{2},\dots,\mathbb{x}_{m})
where

\mathbb{x}_{\alpha}=(x_{\alpha,1},\dots,x_{\alpha,{n_{\alpha}}})
and

\tilde{\mathbb{x}}_{\alpha}=(x_{\alpha,2},\dots,x_{\alpha,{n_{\alpha}}})
.
Then the corresponding matrix

g
is the block matrix
(g_{\alpha\beta})_{\alpha,\beta=1}^{m}
with the
block dimensions
(n_{1}-1,n_{2},\dots,n_{m})
and the blocks

g_{\alpha\beta}=\big(g_{\alpha\beta}^{ij}(\tilde{\mathbb{x}}_{1},\mathbb{x}_{2},\dots,\mathbb{x}_{m})\big)_{i,j}

defined by

g_{\alpha\beta}^{ij}=\begin{cases}g_{1}^{ij}(1-x_{2,1}-\dots-x_{m,1},\tilde{\mathbb{x}}_{1}),&\alpha=\beta=1,\\
g_{\alpha}^{ij}(\mathbb{x}_{\alpha}),&\alpha=\beta\geq 2,\\
-d_{\alpha,i}\,d_{\beta,j}\,x_{\alpha,i}\,x_{\alpha,j},&\alpha\neq\beta.\end{cases}

Up to affine linear change of coordinates,
\Phi_{*}({\lx@ams@boldsymbol@{\Delta}})
does not depend on
the order of the summands. For example,
if we exchange
E_{1}
and
E_{2}
, then
the resulting solution is obtained from the initial one by
the affine linear change of coordinates

(\tilde{\mathbb{x}}_{1},\mathbb{x}_{2},\dots,\mathbb{x}_{m})\mapsto(\tilde{\mathbb{x}}_{2},\,1-x_{2,1}-\dots-x_{m,1},\tilde{\mathbb{x}}_{1},\,\mathbb{x}_{3},\dots,\mathbb{x}_{m}).

Second construction.

Suppose now that
G_{1}
is trivial,
d_{2,1}=\dots=d_{m,1}=2
, and
\dim E_{m}=1
.
Then
I_{1,1},\dots,I_{1,n_{1}}
are just linear coordinates on
E_{1}
and

d_{1,1}=\dots=d_{1,n_{1}}=1
.
Let
\mathbb{S}^{n-1}
be the sphere in
E
given by the equation

\sum_{i}I_{1,i}^{2}+\sum_{\alpha\geq 2}I_{\alpha,1}=0
and let

\Phi:\mathbb{S}^{n-1}\to\mathbb{R}^{n-1}
be the mapping defined by

p\mapsto\big(I_{1}(p),\dots,I_{m-1}(p)\big)
, where

I_{\alpha}=(I_{\alpha,1},\dots,I_{\alpha,n_{\alpha}})
.
Then
\Phi_{*}({\lx@ams@boldsymbol@{\Delta}})
is a solution of the DOP problem.
Denote the coordinates in the target space
\mathbb{R}^{n-1}
by

(\mathbb{x}_{1},\dots,\mathbb{x}_{m-1})
where

\mathbb{x}_{\alpha}=(x_{\alpha,1},\dots,x_{\alpha,{n_{\alpha}}})
.
Then the corresponding matrix

g
is the block matrix
\big(g(\alpha,\beta)\big)_{\alpha,\beta=1}^{m}
with the
block dimensions
(n_{1},n_{2},\dots,n_{m-1})
and with the blocks

g(\alpha,\beta)=\big(g_{\alpha\beta}^{ij}\big)_{i,j}

defined by

g_{\alpha\beta}^{ij}=\begin{cases}\delta^{ij}-x_{1,i}\,x_{1,j},&\alpha=\beta=1,\\
g_{\alpha}^{ij}(\mathbb{x}_{\alpha}),&2\leq\alpha=\beta\leq m-1,\\
-d_{\alpha,i}\,d_{\beta,j}\,x_{\alpha,i}\,x_{\alpha,j},&\alpha\neq\beta.\end{cases}

6.9. Quotient of
\mathbb{R}^{n}
by the affine Coxeter group
\widetilde{C}_{n}

Let
E=\mathbb{R}^{n}
with coordinates
\theta_{1},\dots,\theta_{n}
.
The ring of invariant Fourier polynomials for the affine Coxeter group

\widetilde{B}_{n-1}
is freely generated by
s_{1},\dots,s_{n}
where

P(u)=(u+t_{1})\dots(u+t_{n})=\sum_{k=0}^{n}s_{k}u^{n-k}
,
t_{i}=\cos\theta_{i}
.
We consider the mapping
\Phi:\mathbb{R}^{n}\to\mathbb{R}^{n}
,

(\theta_{1},\dots,\theta_{n})\mapsto(s_{1},\dots,s_{n})
. Its image
\Omega

is the set of all
n
-tuples
X=(X_{1},\dots,X_{n})
such that all roots of
the polynomial
P_{X}(u)=u^{n}+\sum_{k=1}^{n}X_{k}u^{n-k}
are real and belong to
the interval
[-1,1]
. Therefore
\Omega
is bounded by the union of the hypersurface

\{X\mid\text{discr}_{u}P_{X}(u)=0\}
and two hyperplanes
\{X\mid P_{X}(\pm 1)=0\}
.
When the point
X
moves from
\Omega
crossing the discriminantal hypersurface,
two real roots disappears. When it crosses the hyperplane
X=\pm 1
, one of the roots
gets out from the interval
[-1,1]
.
One easily checks that
\Omega
is the only bounded component of the complement
(cf. Thm. 5.1(i
5
), Figure 4, §5.3; for
n=2
see [3, §4.7]).

By linearity,
{\lx@ams@boldsymbol@{\Gamma}}(s_{k},s_{m})
is the coefficient of
u^{n-k}v^{n-m}
in
{\lx@ams@boldsymbol@{\Gamma}}(P(u),P(v))
.
We have

{\lx@ams@boldsymbol@{\Gamma}}(t_{i},t_{j})={\lx@ams@boldsymbol@{\Gamma}}(\cos\theta_{i},\cos\theta_{j})=\delta_{ij}\sin\theta_{i}\sin\theta_{j}=\delta_{ij}(1-t_{i}^{2}).

Hence

{\lx@ams@boldsymbol@{\Gamma}}\big(P(u),P(v)\big)=\sum_{i,j}\big(\partial_{t_{i}}P(u)\big)\big(\partial_{t_{j}}P(v)\big){\lx@ams@boldsymbol@{\Gamma}}(t_{i},t_{j})=\sum_{i}\frac{P(u)P(v)(1-t_{i}^{2})}{(u+t_{i})(v+t_{i})}

=\frac{P(u)P(v)}{v-u}\sum_{i}\Big(\frac{1+ut_{i}}{u+t_{i}}-\frac{1+vt_{i}}{v+t_{i}}\Big)=\frac{Q(u)P(v)-Q(v)P(u)}{v-u}

where

\begin{split}Q(u)&=\sum_{i}\frac{P(u)(1+ut_{i})}{u+t_{i}}=\sum_{i}P(u)\Big(u+\frac{1-u^{2}}{u+t_{i}}\Big)\\
&=nuP(u)+(1-u^{2})P^{\prime}(u)=\sum_{k}s_{k}\Big(ku^{n-k+1}+(n-k)u^{n-k-1}\Big),\end{split}

thus
{\lx@ams@boldsymbol@{\Gamma}}\big(P(u),P(v)\big)
is equal to

\begin{split}&\sum_{k,m}s_{k}s_{m}\!\left(k\frac{u^{n-k+1}v^{n-m}-v^{n-k+1}u^{n-m}}{v-u}+(n-k)\frac{u^{n-k-1}v^{n-m}-v^{n-k-1}u^{n-m}}{v-u}\right)\\
&\qquad=\sum_{k,m}s_{k}s_{m}\Bigg\{k\left(\sum_{l=1}^{k-m-1}u^{n-k+l}v^{n-m-l}-\sum_{l=0}^{m-k}u^{n-k-l}v^{n-m+l}\right)\\
&\qquad\qquad\quad+(n-k)\left(\sum_{l=0}^{k-m}u^{n-k+l-1}v^{n-m-l-1}-\sum_{l=1}^{m-k-1}u^{n-k-l-1}v^{n-m+l-1}\right)\Bigg\}.\end{split}

Hence, for
a\leq b
, we have

{\lx@ams@boldsymbol@{\Gamma}}(s_{a},s_{b})=(n-b+1)s_{a-1}s_{b-1}-as_{a}s_{b}+\sum_{l\geq 1}(b-a+2l)(s_{a-l}s_{b+l}-s_{a-l-1}s_{b+l-1})

It is easy to see that
{\lx@ams@boldsymbol@{\Delta}}(s_{a})=-as_{a}
.

6.10.
Quotients of
\mathbb{R}^{n}
by the affine Coxeter groups
\widetilde{B}_{n}

and
\widetilde{D}_{n}

Let the notation be as in §6.9.
The ring of invariant Fourier polynomials for the affine Coxeter group

\widetilde{B}_{n}
is freely generated by
s_{1},\dots,s_{n-1}

and

\hat{s}_{n}=\prod_{i=1}^{n}\sqrt{2}\,\cos(\theta_{i}/2)=\prod_{i=1}^{n}\sqrt{1+\cos\theta_{i}}=P(1)^{1/2}.

\Gamma(s_{a},s_{b})
for
a\leq b\leq n-1
are as in §6.9 with the substitution

s_{n}=\hat{s}_{n}^{2}-\sum_{k=0}^{n-1}s_{k}
(recall that
s_{0}=1
).
Using the computations in §6.9 we obtain

2{\lx@ams@boldsymbol@{\Gamma}}(P,\hat{s}_{n})=\frac{{\lx@ams@boldsymbol@{\Gamma}}(P(u),P(1))}{P(1)^{1/2}}=\frac{Q(u)P(1)-Q(1)P(u)}{P(1)^{1/2}(1-u)}=\hat{s}_{n}\big((1+u)P^{\prime}-nP\big),

hence

2{\lx@ams@boldsymbol@{\Gamma}}(s_{a},\hat{s}_{n})=((n-a+1)s_{a-1}-as_{a})\hat{s}_{n}
and

\begin{split}4{\lx@ams@boldsymbol@{\Gamma}}(\hat{s}_{n},\hat{s}_{n})&=\frac{{\lx@ams@boldsymbol@{\Gamma}}(P(1),P(1))}{P(1)}=\frac{1}{P(1)}\sum_{i}\frac{P(1)^{2}(1-t_{i}^{2})}{(1+t_{i})^{2}}=\sum_{i}\left(\frac{2P(1)}{1+t_{i}}-P(1)\right)\\
&=2P^{\prime}(1)-nP(1)=-n\hat{s}_{n}^{2}+\sum_{k=0}^{n-1}(n-k)s_{k}.\end{split}

We have
{\lx@ams@boldsymbol@{\Delta}}(s_{a})=-as_{a}
and
{\lx@ams@boldsymbol@{\Delta}}(\hat{s}_{n})=-\tfrac{1}{4}n\hat{s}_{n}
.

The image of
{\lx@ams@boldsymbol@{\Delta}}
through
\Phi:\mathbb{R}^{n}\to\mathbb{R}^{n}
,

(\theta_{1},\dots,\theta_{n})\mapsto(s_{1},\dots,s_{n-1},\hat{s}_{n})

is not a solution of the DOP problem when
n\geq 3

(and
\widetilde{B}_{2}
is the same as
\widetilde{C}_{2}
).
Indeed,
{\lx@ams@boldsymbol@{\Gamma}}(s_{2},s_{n-1})
has monomial
(n-1)s_{1}\hat{s}_{n}^{2}
of degree
3
.
However,
\Phi_{*}({\lx@ams@boldsymbol@{\Delta}})
is a solution of the weighted DOP problem with
weights
(1,\dots,1,\frac{1}{2})
.

For the affine group
\widetilde{D}_{n}
,
all the computations are almost the same and we omit the details.
The ring of invariant Fourier polynomials
is generated by
s_{1},\dots,s_{n-2}
,
\hat{s}_{n}
,
and
\hat{s}_{n-1}=\prod_{i=1}^{n}\sqrt{2}\,\sin(\theta/2)=\sqrt{(-1)^{n}P(-1)}
.
For
a\leq b\leq n-2
,
\;{\lx@ams@boldsymbol@{\Gamma}}(s_{a},s_{b})
,
{\lx@ams@boldsymbol@{\Gamma}}(s_{a},\hat{s}_{n})
, and
{\lx@ams@boldsymbol@{\Gamma}}(\hat{s}_{n},\hat{s}_{n})

are the same as above but with the substitutions

s_{n}=\tfrac{1}{2}\big(\hat{s}_{n}^{2}+(-1)^{n}\hat{s}_{n-1}^{2}\big)-\sum_{k\geq 1}s_{n-2k},\quad s_{n-1}=\tfrac{1}{2}\big(\hat{s}_{n}^{2}+(-1)^{n}\hat{s}_{n-1}^{2}\big)-\sum_{k\geq 1}s_{n-2k-1}.

The values of
{\lx@ams@boldsymbol@{\Gamma}}(\hat{s}_{n-1},*)

and we arrive to the same conclusion as above:
the quotient by
\widetilde{D}_{n}
,
n\geq 4
,
does not provide a solution of the DOP problem, but it provides a solution
of the weighted DOP problem with weights
(1,\dots,1,\frac{1}{2},\frac{1}{2})
.

P(u)=(u+t_{1})\dots(u+t_{n})=\sum_{k=0}^{n}s_{k}u^{n-k}
,
t_{i}=\exp(\mathbb{i}\,\theta_{i})
,

\mathbb{i}=\sqrt{-1}
.
Notice that
\bar{s}_{k}|_{H}=s_{n-k}|_{H}
, in particular
s_{n}|_{H}=1
and
s_{n/2}|_{H}
is real
when
n
is even.
We consider the mapping
\Phi:H\to\mathbb{R}^{n-1}
,

(\theta_{1},\dots,\theta_{n})\mapsto s=(s_{1},\dots,s_{\lfloor n/2\rfloor})

where we identify
\mathbb{R}^{n-1}
with
\mathbb{C}^{(n-1)/2}
(which we define as

\mathbb{C}^{(n-2)/2}\times\mathbb{R}
when
n
is even).
Then
\Phi(H)
is bounded by the hypersurface

\text{discr}_{u}P(u,Z)=0
,
Z=(Z_{1},\dots,Z_{\lfloor n/2\rfloor})\in\mathbb{C}^{(n-1)/2}
,

P(u,Z)=\begin{cases}\!u^{2k}+Z_{1}u^{2k-1}+\dots+Z_{k}u^{k}+\bar{Z}_{k-1}u^{k-1}+\dots+\bar{Z}_{1}u+1,&\!n=2k,\\
\!u^{2k+1}+Z_{1}u^{2k}+\dots+Z_{k}u^{k}+\bar{Z}_{k}u^{k+1}+\dots+\bar{Z}_{1}u+1,&\!n=2k+1\end{cases}

(cf. Thm. 5.1(vi) and Figure 3; for
n=2
see [3, §4.12]).
Let
{\lx@ams@boldsymbol@{\Delta}}={\lx@ams@boldsymbol@{\Delta}}_{H}
and let
{\lx@ams@boldsymbol@{\Gamma}}
be the associated carré du champ.
Then
{\lx@ams@boldsymbol@{\Gamma}}(s_{k},s_{m})
is the coefficient of
u^{n-k}v^{n-m}

in
{\lx@ams@boldsymbol@{\Gamma}}(P(u),P(v))
.
For any functions
f
,
g
we have

{\lx@ams@boldsymbol@{\Gamma}}(f,g)={\lx@ams@boldsymbol@{\Gamma}}_{E}(f,g)-\tfrac{1}{n}(\partial_{0}f)(\partial_{0}g)
where

\partial_{0}=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\tfrac{\partial}{\partial\theta_{i}}
(see (14)).
Denote
\tfrac{\partial}{\partial t_{i}}
by
\partial_{i}
.
We have

\begin{split}\partial_{0}P(u)&=\sum_{i}\mathbb{i}\,t_{i}\partial_{i}P=\mathbb{i}\sum_{i}\frac{t_{i}P}{u+t_{i}}=\mathbb{i}\sum_{i}\Big(1-\frac{u}{u+t_{i}}\Big)P=\mathbb{i}\big(nP(u)-uP^{\prime}(u)\big),\end{split}

thus

{\lx@ams@boldsymbol@{\Gamma}}\big(P(u),P(v)\big)={\lx@ams@boldsymbol@{\Gamma}}_{E}\big(P(u),P(v)\big)+\tfrac{1}{n}\big(nP(u)-uP^{\prime}(u)\big)\big(nP(v)-vP^{\prime}(v)\big).

We also have

{\lx@ams@boldsymbol@{\Gamma}}_{E}(t_{i},t_{j})=(dt_{i}/d\theta_{i})(dt_{j}/d\theta_{j})\Gamma_{E}(\theta_{i},\theta_{j})=-\delta_{ij}t_{i}^{2}
.
Then

-{\lx@ams@boldsymbol@{\Gamma}}_{E}\big(P(u),P(v)\big)=-\sum_{i,j}\big(\partial_{i}P(u)\big)\big(\partial_{j}P(v)\big){\lx@ams@boldsymbol@{\Gamma}}_{E}(t_{i},t_{j})=\sum_{i}\frac{P(u)P(v)t_{i}^{2}}{(u+t_{i})(v+t_{i})}

=\frac{P(u)P(v)}{v-u}\sum_{i}\Big(v-u+\frac{u^{2}}{u+t_{i}}-\frac{v^{2}}{v+t_{i}}\Big)=nP(u)P(v)+\frac{u^{2}P^{\prime}(u)P(v)-v^{2}P^{\prime}(v)P(u)}{v-u}

=nP(u)P(v)+\sum_{k,m}(n-k)s_{k}s_{m}\frac{u^{n-k+1}v^{n-m}-v^{n-k+1}v^{n-m}}{v-u}

=nP(u)P(v)+\sum_{k,m}(n-k)s_{k}s_{m}\left(\sum_{l=1}^{k-m-1}u^{n-k+l}v^{n-m-l}-\sum_{l=0}^{m-k}u^{n-k-l}v^{n-m+l}\right)

Hence, for
a\leq b
, we have

{\lx@ams@boldsymbol@{\Gamma}}(s_{a},s_{b})=\frac{a(b-n)}{n}s_{a}s_{b}+\sum_{l\geq 1}(b-a+2l)s_{a-l}s_{b+l}.

Setting
s_{k}=x_{k}+\mathbb{i}y_{k}
,
{\lx@ams@boldsymbol@{\Gamma}}(s_{a},s_{b})=A+\mathbb{i}B
,
and
{\lx@ams@boldsymbol@{\Gamma}}(s_{a},\bar{s}_{b})=C+\mathbb{i}D
, we obtain
for
a\leq b\leq n/2
:

2{\lx@ams@boldsymbol@{\Gamma}}(x_{a},x_{b})=A+C,\;2{\lx@ams@boldsymbol@{\Gamma}}(x_{a},y_{b})=B-D,\;2{\lx@ams@boldsymbol@{\Gamma}}(y_{a},x_{b})=B+D,\;2{\lx@ams@boldsymbol@{\Gamma}}(y_{a},y_{b})=C-A,

\begin{split}&A=\frac{a(b-n)}{n}(x_{a}x_{b}-y_{a}y_{b})+\sum_{l\geq 1}(b-a+2l)(x_{a-l}x_{b+l}-y_{a-l}y_{b+l}),\\
&B=\frac{a(b-n)}{n}(x_{a}y_{b}+y_{a}x_{b})+\sum_{l\geq 1}(b-a+2l)(x_{a-l}y_{b+l}+y_{a-l}x_{b+l}),\\
&C=-\frac{ab}{n}(x_{a}x_{b}+y_{a}y_{b})+\sum_{l\geq 1}(n-a-b+2l)(x_{a-l}x_{b-l}+y_{a-l}y_{b-l}),\\
&D=\frac{ab}{n}(x_{a}y_{b}-y_{a}x_{b})-\sum_{l\geq 1}(n-a-b+2l)(x_{a-l}y_{b-l}-y_{a-l}x_{b-l}).\end{split}

For
a\leq n/2
we have
{\lx@ams@boldsymbol@{\Delta}}(x_{a})=\lambda_{a}x_{a}
,

{\lx@ams@boldsymbol@{\Delta}}(y_{a})=\lambda_{a}y_{a}
,
\lambda_{a}=a(a-n)/n
.

Proposition \propC

Let
(g,\Gamma)
be a solution of the AlgDOP problem in
\mathbb{C}^{3}
such that

\Gamma
is an irreducible homogeneous polynomial and

\det g
is not a homogeneous polynomial of degree
6
.
Then
\deg\Gamma\leq 2
.

One can easily derive Theorem 7.1 from Proposition 7.3.
Indeed, if
\det g
(in the setting of Theorem 7.1) were
a homogeneous polynomial of degree
6
, then
affine coordinates
(x,y,z)
could be chosen so that
\deg_{x}\det g=6
and

\Omega
contains a half-cylinder
\{x>0,y^{2}+z^{2}<1\}
, which
contradicts [3, Cor. 2.19].

The rest of this section is devoted to the proof of Proposition 7.3.
Let
\Gamma
be as in Proposition 7.3.
Let
\Sigma
be the surface in
\mathbb{C}^{3}
defined by the equation
\Gamma=0
, and
let
C
be the curve in
\mathbb{P}^{3}=\mathbb{P}(\mathbb{C}^{3})
defined by the same equation.
Any local branch
\gamma
of
C
has a parametrization of the form

t\mapsto(t^{p},t^{q}+o(t^{q}))
,
1\leq p<q
, in some affine coordinates.
We then say that
\gamma
is of type
(p,q)
.

Lemma \lemC

Any local branch of
C
is of type
(1,2)
or
(2,4)
.

Proof

The arguments are as in §3 but simpler.
Let
\pi:\mathbb{C}^{3}\setminus\{(0,0,0)\}\to\mathbb{P}^{2}
be the quotient map
(then
\Sigma=\pi^{-1}(C)
).
Let
\gamma
be a local branch of
C
at
p\in\mathbb{CP}^{2}
parametrized by

t\mapsto\gamma(t)=(\xi_{1}(t):\xi_{2}(t):\xi_{3}(t))
.
Then
\Sigma
near the line
\pi^{-1}(p)
is parametrized by

(t,u)\mapsto(u\xi_{1}(t),u\xi_{2}(t),u\xi_{3}(t))
.
Similarly to §3, we rewrite the equations (3)
in the form
E_{1}=E_{2}=E_{3}=0
where

E_{i}=u\sum_{j=1}^{3}(\dot{\xi}_{j+1}\xi_{j-1}-\dot{\xi}_{j-1}\xi_{j+1})g^{ij}(u\xi_{1},u\xi_{2},u\xi_{3})=\sum_{\alpha=0}^{\infty}t^{\alpha}\sum_{\beta=1}^{3}E_{\alpha,\beta,i}\,u^{\beta}

(
i
and
j
are considered mod
3
)
and the
E_{\alpha,\beta,i}
are linear forms in
g_{klm}^{ij}
whose coefficients are
polynomial functions of the coefficients of the
\xi_{i}
’s.

We have
\deg C\leq 5
because otherwise
\det g
would be homogeneous of degree
6
.
Hence
C
may have only local branches of type
(p,q)
with
q\leq 5
.
For each pair
(p,q)
,
1\leq p<q\leq 5
, except
(1,2)
and
(2,4)

(thus for
8
pairs) we consider a branch
\gamma
of type
(p,q)
of the form

t\mapsto(1:t^{p}:t^{q}+\sum_{k>q}a_{k}t^{k})

and solve the maximal triangular subsystem of the system of
equations
E_{\alpha,\beta,i}=0
for the unknowns

.
This means that we find an equation implying that some unknown is zero,
replace this unknown by zero in all other equations,

For all pairs
(p,q)
except
(1,4)
,
(1,5)
we obtain that

\deg g

6

\det g

\det g=z^{2}\Gamma

This means that
\det g
is homogeneous of degree
6
. ∎

Let
d
and
\mathbb{g}
be the degree and the genus of
C
respectively.
Let
a_{2k}
,
k\geq 2
, be the number of local branches of type
(2,4)

which admit a parametrization
t\mapsto(t^{2},t^{2k+1})

Let

Let

\mathbb{n}=\sum_{\gamma}\delta(\gamma)+\sum_{\gamma_{1},\gamma_{2}}(\gamma_{1}\cdot\gamma_{2})
