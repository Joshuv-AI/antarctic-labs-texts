Relative monodromy of ramified sections on abelian schemes
Paolo Dolce, Francesco Tropeano

Abstract
Let’s fix a complex abelian scheme 𝒜 → S \mathcal{A}\to S of relative dimension g g , without fixed part, and having maximal variation in moduli. We show that the relative monodromy group M σ rel M^{\textrm{rel}}_{\sigma} of a ramified section σ : S → 𝒜 \sigma\colon S\to\mathcal{A} is nontrivial. Moreover, under some hypotheses on the action of the monodromy group Mon ​ ( 𝒜 ) \textrm{Mon}(\mathcal{A}) we show that M σ rel ≅ ℤ 2 ​ g M^{\textrm{rel}}_{\sigma}\cong\mathbb{Z}^{2g} . We discuss several examples and applications. For instance we provide a new proof of Manin’s kernel theorem and of the algebraic independence of the coordinates of abelian logarithms with respect to the coordinates of periods.

Main results of this paper.

We investigate the obstruction that prevents the existence of a globally defined abelian logarithm along loops which induce a trivial monodromy action on the period map. This obstruction is measured by the
relative monodromy group
(of the section)
M^{\textrm{rel}}_{\sigma}\subseteq\mathbb{Z}^{2g}
.
The group
M_{\sigma}^{\textrm{rel}}
gives relevant information on the section, in particular its non-triviality is supposed be related to the property of
\sigma
of being non-torsion, and its rank should give information on the minimal group subscheme containing the image of
\sigma
.

In the case of non-isotrivial elliptic schemes over a curve Corvaja and Zannier proved in
[
13
]
that when
\sigma
is a non-torsion section the group
M^{\textrm{rel}}_{\sigma}
is non-trivial and moreover it is isomorphic to
\mathbb{Z}^{2}
(so it has full rank). In the same paper, they provided an example showing that a priori, if we just limit ourselves to group theoretic arguments, the relative monodromy group could be smaller than expected (even trivial) when compared to the relative group arising from Zariski closures of non-relative monodromy groups. Hence, in this setting there are some groups which cannot arise as monodromy groups of sections. We point out that the second author of the present paper in
[
40
]
gives an explicit proof of the results of Corvaja and Zannier.

Application I: properties of the Betti map.

Our theorem has immediate implications on the arithmetic properties of the sections of abelian schemes, in particular on some properties of the Betti map. On one hand periods and abelian logarithms in general cannot be globally defined, but on the other hand it is also true that they can be always be globally defined after going to the universal cover
\widetilde{S}
of the base
S(\mathbb{C})
, so the Betti map is also globally defined on
\widetilde{S}
. Let us denote by
S_{*}\to S(\mathbb{C})
the minimal topological cover where periods are globally defined and by
S_{\sigma}\to S_{*}
the minimal topological cover where abelian logarithm of
\sigma
is globally defined: then,
S_{\sigma}\to S_{*}
is also the minimal topological cover of
S_{*}
where the Betti map is globally defined and we have the following situation

\widetilde{S}\to S_{\sigma}\to S_{*}\to S(\mathbb{C})\,.

The map
S_{*}\to S(\mathbb{C})
is a Galois cover whose Galois group is
\textrm{Mon}(\mathcal{A},s)
, while the map
S_{\sigma}\to S(\mathbb{C})
is a Galois cover with Galois group
M_{\sigma}
. Our main theorem determines properties of the Galois group
M_{\sigma}^{\textrm{rel}}
of the Galois cover
S_{\sigma}\to S_{*}
, giving precise information about the global definition of the Betti map which is useful to know for several applications.

For instance, it is straightforward to see that if
\sigma
is a torsion section then the Betti map of
\sigma
is constant and thus globally defined on the base. The converse statement is also true but more difficult to prove, it is widely known as Manin’s kernel theorem. As a consequence of
Theorem

1.2
, we obtain a new proof of a stronger version of Manin’s kernel theorem. We call it “strong version” because in its statement we just assume the Betti coordinates to be globally defined instead of rational constant a priori.

Application II: the functional transcendence step in the Pila-Zannier method.

The Betti map is used in the work
[
36
]
of Pila and Zannier together with a result of Pila and Wilkie in transcendental Diophantine geometry (see
[
35
]
) to give a new proof of the Manin-Mumford conjecture. Such a strategy is referred as “the Pila-Zannier method”; in the case of abelian schemes over
\overline{\mathbb{Q}}
it works in the following way: the goal is to have a control on the distribution of torsion values of
\sigma
(i.e. elements of
\sigma^{-1}(\mathcal{A}_{\tor})
) with bounded heights. First one gives lower bounds on the number of such torsion values using Galois conjugates and the height inequality of Dimitrov-Gao-Habegger. On the other hand the Betti map
\beta_{\sigma}
transforms the torsion values in rational points of a definable set in the
o
-minimal structure
\mathbb{R}_{\an,\exp}
. At this point a “Pila-Wilkie type” result gives a control on the rational points on the transcendental part of such definable set. Still we have no information on the rational points in the algebraic part of the definable set, but here the crucial point is to use some functional transcendence theorem of “Ax-Schanuel type” to control the algebraic part of the definable set. Let us take a closer look at the last step involving the functional transcendence arguments: a classical way to tackle this step is by using a result of André (see
[
2
, Theorem 3]
). It says that the coordinates of the abelian logarithm
\log_{\sigma}
are pairwise algebraically independent over the extension of
\mathbb{C}(S)
generated by the coordinates of the period map
\mathfrak{P}
. Such result has often very strong implications, for instance in
[
17
]
, thanks to such result, it is shown that the algebraic part of the definable set constructed with the Pila-Zannier method is empty.

Such type of functional transcendence results are related to the study of the relative monodromy. In fact André’s result can be interpreted in terms of differential Galois theory. More precisely, considering directional derivatives along a tangent vector field
\partial
with respect to the Gauss-Manin connection, the transcendence degree of the extension

\mathbb{C}(S)(\mathfrak{P},\partial\mathfrak{P})(\log_{\sigma},\partial\log_{\sigma})/\mathbb{C}(S)(\mathfrak{P},\partial\mathfrak{P})

is equal to the dimension of the kernel of the homomorphism from the differential Galois group of
\mathbb{C}(S)(\mathfrak{P},\partial\mathfrak{P})(\log_{\sigma},\partial\log_{\sigma})/\mathbb{C}(S)
to the differential Galois group of
\mathbb{C}(S)(\mathfrak{P},\partial\mathfrak{P})/\mathbb{C}(S)
. Recall that by general theory, the differential Galois group of a Picard-Vessiot extension is exactly the Zariski closure of the corresponding monodromy group. In
[
13
]
it is pointed out that André’s theorem gives no information about the relative monodromy group
M_{\sigma}^{\textrm{rel}}
. Thus,
Corollary

1.3
can be interpreted as a strengthening of André’s theorem (under our hypothesis). We shall in fact prove how André’s result is a consequence of
Corollary

1.3
, and this in turns leads to a new proof of the algebraic independence of the coordinates of
\log_{\sigma}
with respect to periods.

Further developments.

After the works of André, Corvaja, Masser and Zannier (see for instance
[
4
]
,
[
11
]
,
[
13
]
,
[
44
]
) the Betti map became a standard tool in Diophantine geometry. Remarkably, Dimitrov, Gao and Habbegger in
[
16
]
used the Betti map and a novel height inequality (then generalized by Yuan and Zhang in
[
43
]
) to prove a uniform version of the Mordell-Lang conjecture. The Pila-Zannier method was in turns employed by Gao and Habbegger in combination with new ideas involving some “degeneracy loci”, to solve the relative Manin-Mumford conjecture (see
[
23
]
). Other very recent applications of the Betti map can be found in the works of Xie and Yuan in
[
42
]
towards the geometric Bombieri-Lang conjecture.

Our hope for future developments is twofold. First of all, the main results of this paper could be applied for the solution of some “special points problems” when in the functional transcendence step of the Pila-Zannier method André’s theorem fails to give a control on rational points of the algebraic part of the constructed definable set. It is not clear yet if such cases exist and if they are interesting. Secondly, it would be nice to remove the hypothesis of maximal variation in moduli and also to obtain the result for all non-torsion sections in
Theorem

1.2
.

Strategy of the proofs.

Our proofs develop the ideas introduced in
[
13
]
, interpreting the obstruction to defining a global logarithm as a Galois cohomology class relative to the monodromy action of
\pi_{1}(S(\mathbb{C}))
on
\mathbb{Z}^{2g}
. The key innovation involves applying the Lefschetz hyperplane theorem for quasi-projective varieties (see
[
24
, Part II, 1.2, Theorem]
) to reduce the problem to generic curves in the base variety. We then utilize properties of the trace operator with respect to the pullback of sections (in the case of finite flat morphisms). Unlike the approach in
[
13
]
, our method does not require the construction of abelian schemes with finite Mordell-Weil groups.

For the proof of
Theorem

1.2
we proceed in the following way: we construct an intermediate abelian scheme
\mathcal{A}^{\prime}\to S^{\prime}
which factorizes the universal diagram induced by the modular map
p:S\to T
. This construction is carried out by means of topological techniques in such a way that
\mathcal{A}^{\prime}\to S^{\prime}
is a finite unramified base change (after normalization) of the universal family. Then we find a suitable curve inside
S(\mathbb{C})
which preserves the complete monodromy action of the fundamental group of
S(\mathbb{C})
on periods and logarithms. The curve is obtained after iterating the aforementioned Lefschetz theorem and turns out to be a Stein space. This last property allows us to construct holomorphic sections of the unramified family which come from a Galois cohomology class describing the obstruction of the global logarithms. The proof then follows: we assume the triviality of
M_{\sigma}^{\textrm{rel}}
, then by using the previous apparatus and the properties of trace and pull-back operators, we deduce that
\sigma
should be unramified. This proves the non-triviality of
M_{\sigma}^{\textrm{rel}}
for ramified sections (over
U_{p}
).

Once that we have addressed the non-triviality of
M^{\textrm{rel}}_{\sigma}
, the corollaries follow immediately from the extra assumptions.

In general, the normalization map

is not unramified, and understanding its ramification is an interesting problem. For instance, the normalization of a nodal curve is unramified but not étale, while the normalization of a cuspidal curve is ramified. On the other hand, if we start with a normal irreducible variety, the relative normalizations within finite field extensions of the function field yield natural examples of unramified morphisms. In fact, there is a strong classification result: étale covers of a normal integral variety, such as

We aim to establish criteria for identifying unramified sections

may itself be ramified.

We remark that the normality condition is essential in order to have the correspondence between the notion of topological covers and finite étale covers. In fact while finite étale morphisms induce topological covers of the corresponding complex spaces, we need the normality condition to conclude that an unramified (finite) topological cover induces a finite étale map. Monodromy groups

3.1
Monodromy of abelian schemes

Let
\mathcal{F}
be a sheaf of abelian groups over a topological space
X
and denote by
\Gamma
the functor of “global sections” which to
\mathcal{F}
associates
\Gamma(X,\mathcal{F})=\mathcal{F}(X)
, with values in the category of abelian groups. Since the category of sheaves of abelian groups has sufficiently many injective objects, then the derived functors
R^{k}\Gamma
do exist. They are generally written

R^{k}\Gamma(\mathcal{F})=:H^{k}(X,\mathcal{F}).

Let
X
be a locally contractible topological space and denote by
\underline{\mathbb{Z}}
the constant sheaf of abelian groups over
X
with constant stalk
\mathbb{Z}
. Then we have a canonical isomorphism

H^{q}_{\textrm{sing}}(X,\mathbb{Z})\cong H^{q}(X,\underline{\mathbb{Z}}),

where we are considering the cohomology of
X
with coefficients in the constant sheaf of stalk
\underline{\mathbb{Z}}
on the right, and the singular cohomology with coefficients in
\mathbb{Z}
on the left. The same result holds with
\mathbb{Z}
replaced by any commutative ring
G
.

Now, let
\phi:\mathcal{A}\to S
be an abelian scheme as defined above. We want to define a sheaf on the base
S
containing information on periods of the abelian schemes, to this end let us consider the direct image functor
\phi_{*}
. Since
\phi_{*}
is left exact and the category of sheaves of abelian groups has enough injectivities, the right derived functors of
\phi_{*}
are well defined from the category of sheaves on
\mathcal{A}
to the category of sheaves on
S
: they are called
higher direct image functors
and will be denoted by
R^{k}\phi_{*}
. For any
k\geq 0
, define the sheaf

\mathcal{P}_{S}^{k}:=R^{k}\phi_{*}\underline{\mathbb{Z}}.

By
[
28
, Proposition 8.1]
, this is the sheaf on
S
associated to the presheaf

U\mapsto H^{k}(\phi^{-1}(U),\underline{\mathbb{Z}}_{|\phi^{-1}(U)}).

Since
\phi:\mathcal{A}\to S
is a smooth surjective morphism of algebraic varieties over
\mathbb{C}
, by
[
28
, Theorem 10.4]
then
\phi
is a submersion. We can apply Ehresmann’s Lemma to conclude that the proper submersion
\phi
is a
C^{\infty}
-fiber bundle.

Since
S(\mathbb{C})
is locally contractible, then for sufficiently small
U\subseteq S(\mathbb{C})
, the open sets
\mathcal{A}_{U}:=\phi^{-1}(U)
have the same homotopy type as the fibre
\mathcal{A}_{s}(\mathbb{C})
with
s\in U
. Using the invariance under homotopy, i.e. the fact that if
U
is a contractible space then
H^{k}(\mathcal{A}_{s}(\mathbb{C})\times U,\underline{\mathbb{Z}})=H^{k}(\mathcal{A}_{s}(\mathbb{C}),\underline{\mathbb{Z}})
for all
k\geq 0
, we deduce that the sheaves
\mathcal{P}_{S}^{k}=R^{k}\phi_{*}\underline{\mathbb{Z}}
are locally constant sheaves (or equivalenty
local systems
) on
S
with stalk

(\mathcal{P}_{S}^{k})_{s}=(R^{k}\phi_{*}\underline{\mathbb{Z}})_{s}=H^{k}(\mathcal{A}_{s}(\mathbb{C}),\underline{\mathbb{Z}}).

Now, the fundamental group
\pi_{1}(S(\mathbb{C}),s)
acts via linear transformations on
(\mathcal{P}_{S}^{k})_{s}
: this gives rise to a monodromy representation. We are going to give a more detailed description of the monodromy representation but the rough idea is the following: pick a loop
\gamma(t)
at

and use a trivialization of the bundle along the loop to move vectors in
(\mathcal{P}_{S}^{k})_{s}
along
(\mathcal{P}^{k}_{S})_{\gamma(t)}
, back to
(\mathcal{P}_{S}^{k})_{s}
. Actually this is a more general construction regarding monodromy representations associated to local systems, but in our case the monodromy action can be induced by homeomorphisms of the fiber. In order to be more precise, consider
\gamma\in\pi_{1}(S(\mathbb{C}),s)
represented by a loop
\gamma:[0,1]\to S(\mathbb{C})
based at
s
. Consider the fibration
\mathcal{A}_{\gamma}
defined as the fiber product

{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\mathcal{A}_{\gamma}}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\mathcal{A}(\mathbb{C})}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\left[0,1\right]}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces S(\mathbb{C}).}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\phi_{\gamma}}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\phi}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\gamma}

By the compactness of

Thus, thanks to the fact the local systems we are considering is obtained using a fibration, the monodromy representation is in fact induced by homeomorphisms of the fibre

is a ring automorphism for the ring structure given by the cup-product.

Recall that by Poincaré duality we have isomorphisms

and we only deal with the monodromy action induced on the first homology group identifying

:

\rho:\pi_{1}(S(\mathbb{C}),s)\to\textrm{GL}_{2g}(\mathbb{Z}).

Furthermore, we have a non-degenerate intersection form

Any functional

is an intersection index of some homology class from

and, if a class

is such that

, then

vanishes as element of

. The abelian variety

has a polarization inducing an alternating form

which takes integer values on the lattice

. Identifying

and fixing a symplectic basis

For any functional

there exists a

-cycle

such that

Since the intersection form is dual to the cup-product and since each

is a ring automorphism for the ring structure given by the cup-product, then

has to preserve the intersection matrix induced by

In other words the image of the representation

\textrm{Sp}_{2g}(\mathbb{Z})=\{M\in\textrm{GL}_{2g}(\mathbb{Z})\mid M^{\top}PM=P\}.

Thus, monodromy representation of periods introduced in

\rho:\pi_{1}(S(\mathbb{C}),s)\to\textrm{Sp}_{2g}(\mathbb{Z}).

s\in S(\mathbb{C})
: in other words, the monodromy group is defined up to an inner automorphism of the group
\textrm{GL}_{2g}(\mathbb{Z})
. Thus, fix once and for all a base point
s\in S(\mathbb{C})
and denote the group
\textrm{Mon}(\mathcal{A},s)
simply by
\textrm{Mon}(\mathcal{A})
, without writing any dependencies on the base point.

Since we can identify each first homology group of the fibers with the corresponding lattice
\Lambda_{s}
, then the sheaf
\mathcal{P}
can be identified with the sheaf
\Lambda_{S}
defined in
Equation

4
; it will be called
period sheaf
and will be denoted simply by
\Lambda
. In more concrete terms, notice that by construction studying the monodromy of the period sheaf corresponds to studying the analytic continuation of period functions (i.e. the coordinates of the period map defined in
Equation

6
) along loops in
S(\mathbb{C})
representing classes in the fundamental group based at
s
.

3.2
The case of universal families

For universal families, a more explicit description of the monodromy action on periods is described in
[
22
, Section 3]
. Let
\mathbb{H}_{g}
denote Siegel’s upper half space, i.e., the symmetric matrices in
\textrm{Mat}_{g\times g}(\mathbb{C})
with positive definite imaginary part. We have holomorphic uniformizing maps

u_{B}:\mathbb{H}_{g}\to\mathbb{A}_{g}(\mathbb{C})\qquad\textrm{and}\qquad u:\mathbb{C}_{g}\times\mathbb{H}_{g}\to\mathfrak{A}_{g}(\mathbb{C}).

Recall that
\textrm{Sp}_{2g}(\mathbb{R})
, the group of real points of the symplectic group, acts on
\mathbb{H}_{g}
. Let
x\in\mathbb{A}_{g}(\mathbb{C})
and fix
\tau\in\mathbb{H}_{g}
such that
x=u_{B}(\tau)
. If
1_{g}
denotes the
g\times g
unit matrix, then the columns of
(\tau,1_{g})
are an
\mathbb{R}
-basis of
\mathbb{C}^{g}
and we have
\mathfrak{A}_{g,x}(\mathbb{C})\cong\mathbb{C}^{g}/(\tau\mathbb{Z}^{g}+\mathbb{Z}^{g})
. The period lattice basis
(\tau,1_{g})
allows us to identify
H_{1}(\mathfrak{A}_{g,x}(\mathbb{C}),\mathbb{Z})
with
\mathbb{Z}^{2g}
. Let us consider a loop
\gamma
in
\mathbb{A}_{g}(\mathbb{C})
based at
x
representing a class of
\pi_{1}(\mathbb{A}_{g}(\mathbb{C}),x)
. Then a lift
\widetilde{\gamma}
of
\gamma
to
\mathbb{H}_{g}
starting at
\tau
ends at
M\tau\in\mathbb{H}_{g}
for some

M=\begin{pmatrix}a&b\\
c&d\end{pmatrix}\in\textrm{Sp}_{2g}(\mathbb{Z}).

Then
M\tau
is the period matrix of the abelian variety
\mathbb{C}^{g}/(M\tau\mathbb{Z}^{g}+\mathbb{Z}^{g})
which is isomorphic to
\mathbb{C}^{g}/(\tau\mathbb{Z}^{g}+\mathbb{Z}^{g})
: more precisely, the isomorphism
\mathbb{C}^{g}/(\tau\mathbb{Z}^{g}+\mathbb{Z}^{g})\to\mathbb{C}^{g}/(M\tau\mathbb{Z}^{g}+\mathbb{Z}^{g})
is induced by the map

\tau u+v\mapsto((c\tau+d)^{\top})^{-1}(\tau u+v)=(M\tau,1_{g})(M^{\top})^{-1}\begin{pmatrix}u\\
v\end{pmatrix},

where
u,v\in\mathbb{R}^{g}
are column vectors; in what follows sometimes we will interpret
u,v
as row vectors and we will consider the transposition of the previous relation. Therefore, the monodromy representation expressed in these coordinates is given by

\displaystyle\rho:\pi_{1}(\mathbb{A}_{g}(\mathbb{C})),x)

\displaystyle\to\textrm{Sp}_{2g}(\mathbb{Z})

(11)

\displaystyle[\gamma]

\displaystyle\mapsto(M^{\top})^{-1}.

Notice that the monodromy action is a right action on period functions, it can be obviously interpreted as a left action by putting
h\cdot(M\tau,1_{g}):=(M\tau,1_{g})\rho(h)
.

The previous considerations about periods of universal families allow us to obtain some results on periods of the abelian scheme
\phi:\mathcal{A}\to S
: we are able to construct period functions of
\phi:\mathcal{A}\to S
by means of periods of the universal family
\pi:\mathfrak{A}_{g}\to\mathbb{A}_{g}
. Let
V\subseteq\mathbb{A}_{g}(\mathbb{C})
be a simply connected open set of an open covering of
\mathbb{A}_{g}
where holomorphic period functions do exist for the universal abelian scheme, see
Equation

5
; denote by
\omega^{\mathfrak{A}_{g}}_{V,1},\ldots,\omega^{\mathfrak{A}_{g}}_{V,2g}
such local (holomorphic) period functions. Fix a base point
s\in S(\mathbb{C})
, not a ramification point for the modular map
p:S\rightarrow\mathbb{A}_{g}
, and let
x=p(s)\in\mathbb{A}_{g}(\mathbb{C})
. In a connected and simply connected neighborhood
U
of
s
in
S(\mathbb{C})
such that
p(U)=V
, we can holomorphically define a basis
\omega_{U,1},\ldots,\omega_{U,2g}
of the period lattices by the equations

\omega_{U,i}=\omega^{\mathfrak{A}_{g}}_{V,i}\circ p.

(12)

Locally on suitable open subsets
U\subset S(\mathbb{C})
, this gives a basis for
\Lambda_{s}
made up of holomorphic functions
\omega_{U,1},\ldots,\omega_{U,2g}:U\rightarrow\mathbb{C}^{g}
.

Remark 3.2
.

Recall that the monodromy group of the universal family
\textrm{Mon}(\mathfrak{A}_{g})
is a finite index subgroup of
\textrm{Sp}_{2g}(\mathbb{Z})
. When the modular map
p:S\to\mathbb{A}_{g}
is finite, the monodromy group
\textrm{Mon}(\mathcal{A})
of
\mathcal{A}\to S
is still a finite index subgroup of
\textrm{Sp}_{2g}(\mathbb{Z})
thanks to
Equation

12
; in particular,
\textrm{Mon}(\mathcal{A})
is Zariski-dense in
\textrm{Sp}_{2g}(\mathbb{Z})
. Moreover, when the image of the modular map
T
is not contained in any proper special subvariety of
\mathbb{A}_{g}
, the monodromy group
\textrm{Mon}(\mathcal{A})
is still Zariski-dense in
\textrm{Sp}_{2g}(\mathbb{Z})
by
[
4
, Remark 10.1.2]
.

Lemma 3.3
.

The natural action of the group
\textrm{Sp}_{2g}(\mathbb{Z})
on
\mathbb{Q}^{2g}
is irreducible.

Proof.

Assume that there exists a non-trivial subvector space
W
of
\mathbb{Q}^{2g}
which is fixed by the action of
\textrm{Sp}_{2g}(\mathbb{Z})
. Since the symplectic group
\textrm{Sp}_{2g}
is a simple algebraic Lie group defined over
\mathbb{Q}
, by a theorem of Borel and Harish-Chandra
[
8
, Theorem 7.8]
the group
\textrm{Sp}_{2g}(\mathbb{Z})
is a lattice in
\textrm{Sp}_{2g}(\mathbb{R})
. By Borel density theorem (see
[
7
]
) we have that
\textrm{Sp}_{2g}(\mathbb{Z})
is Zariski dense in
\textrm{Sp}_{2g}(\mathbb{R})
, and so it is Zariski dense in
\textrm{Sp}_{2g}(\mathbb{Q})
. This implies that
W
is fixed by the action of
\textrm{Sp}_{2g}(\mathbb{Q})
. Since the action of
\textrm{Sp}_{2g}(\mathbb{Q})
on
\mathbb{Q}^{2g}
is irreducible (see
[
27
, Proposition 3.2]
), we get
W=\mathbb{Q}^{2g}
which concludes the proof.

∎

.

If the image of the modular map

on the lattice of periods is irreducible.

Proof.

This is an easy consequence of

and

.

Remark 3.5
.

The previous considerations yield some conclusion on the global definition of periods on the base
S(\mathbb{C})
. We have seen that period functions
\omega_{1},\ldots,\omega_{2g}
can be locally defined on simply connected open sets
U\subseteq S(\mathbb{C})
via
Equation

12
for any abelian scheme
\mathcal{A}\rightarrow S
. These functions may be analytically continued through the whole of
S(\mathbb{C})
, but it’s impossible to globally define them: they turn out to be multi-valued functions, i.e. they have quite nontrivial monodromy when traveling along closed paths.

To be more precise, since the group
\textrm{Mon}(\mathcal{A})
is non-trivial then the functions
\omega_{1},\ldots,\omega_{2g}
cannot be all defined continuously on the whole of
S(\mathbb{C})
. Moreover, when the action of
\textrm{Mon}(\mathcal{A})
on the lattice of periods is irreducible, neither one of
\omega_{1},\ldots,\omega_{2g}
nor any single non-zero element of the period lattice can be defined on the whole of
S(\mathbb{C})
. This last thing is also proved in
[
21
, Lemma 5.6]
for any abelian scheme over a curve without using irriducibility properties of the monodromy action.

3.3
Monodromy groups of sections

Let us consider the exponential map
\exp:\Lie(\mathcal{A})\to\mathcal{A}(\mathbb{C})
defined in
Section

2.2
. Notice that it is a topological cover. Thus, it induces a monodromy action of the fundamental group
\pi_{1}(\mathcal{A}(\mathbb{C}),p)
on the fiber
\exp^{-1}(p)
, where we assume
\phi(p)=s
. Let
\sigma:S\to\mathcal{A}
be a section of the abelian scheme, we obtain a monodromy action of
\pi_{1}(S(\mathbb{C}),s)
on the fiber
\exp^{-1}(\sigma(s))
.

Recalling the definition of logarithm given in
Equation

7
, the set
\exp^{-1}(\sigma(s))
is the set of all possible determinations of the abelian logarithm of
\sigma
at the point
s
. Every two such determinations differ each other by an element of the lattice
\Lambda_{s}
, therefore, after fixing a branch
\log_{\sigma}(s)\in\exp^{-1}(\sigma(s))
of the logarithm at
s
, we get the monodromy action

\rho^{\prime}:\pi_{1}(S(\mathbb{C}),s)\to\Aut(\mathbb{Z}^{2g})\,.

The representation
\rho^{\prime}
induces a map (which is not a group homomorphism):

\displaystyle c:\pi_{1}(S(\mathbb{C}),s)

\displaystyle\to\mathbb{Z}^{2g}

(13)

\displaystyle h

\displaystyle\mapsto\rho^{\prime}(h)(\log_{\sigma}(s))\,.

The function
c
can be described explicitly. In fact, any element
h\in\pi_{1}(S(\mathbb{C}),s)
acts by analytic continuation on
\log_{\sigma}(s)
in the following way:

h\cdot\log_{\sigma}=\log_{\sigma}+u_{1}^{h}\omega_{1}+\cdots+u_{2g}^{h}\omega_{2g},

(14)

where
u_{1}^{h},\ldots,u_{2g}^{h}\in\mathbb{Z}
. Then
c(h)=(u^{h}_{1},\ldots,u^{h}_{2g})^{\top}
. Again, since
S(\mathbb{C})
is path connected we can fix the base point
s\in S(\mathbb{C})
once and for all.

Moreover, if we choose
h_{1},h_{2}\in\pi_{1}(S(\mathbb{C}),s)
we can consider the action of the product
h_{1}h_{2}
. Recalling
Equation

11
, we have

\displaystyle\log_{\sigma}\xrightarrow{h_{2}}\log_{\sigma}+(u_{1}^{h_{2}},\ldots,u_{2g}^{h_{2}})\left(\begin{matrix}\omega_{1}\\
\vdots\\
\omega_{2g}\end{matrix}\right)\xrightarrow{h_{1}}\log_{\sigma}+(u_{1}^{h_{1}},\ldots,u_{2g}^{h_{1}})\left(\begin{matrix}\omega_{1}\\
\vdots\\
\omega_{2g}\end{matrix}\right)+(u_{1}^{h_{2}},\ldots,u_{2g}^{h_{2}})\rho(h_{1})^{\top}\left(\begin{matrix}\omega_{1}\\
\vdots\\
\omega_{2g}\end{matrix}\right).

Therefore, we obtain the cocycle relation

(u_{1}^{h_{1}h_{2}},\ldots,u_{2g}^{h_{1}h_{2}})=(u_{1}^{h_{1}},\ldots,u_{2g}^{h_{1}})+(u_{1}^{h_{2}},\ldots,u_{2g}^{h_{2}})\rho(h_{1})^{\top}.

(15)

Now, let us look at the simultaneous monodromy action of
\pi_{1}(S(\mathbb{C}),s)
on periods and logarithm. Thanks to
Equation

15
, using the functions described in
Equation

10
and
Equation

13
, we can provide a new representation

\theta_{\sigma}:\pi_{1}(S(\mathbb{C}),s)\rightarrow\textrm{SL}_{2g+1}(\mathbb{Z}),

where every matrix
\theta_{\sigma}(h)
is of the form

\theta_{\sigma}(h)=\left(\begin{matrix}\rho(h)&c(h)\\
0&1\end{matrix}\right),

(16)

for
\rho(h)\in\textrm{Sp}_{2g}(\mathbb{Z}),c(h)\in\mathbb{Z}^{2g}
. Note that the matrix
\rho(h)
acts on the periods and does not depend on
\sigma
. Moreover, the vector
c(h)
encodes the action of
h
on determinations of the logarithm
\log_{\sigma}
.

Definition 3.6
.

The monodromy group of the section
\sigma
is
M_{\sigma}:=\theta_{\sigma}(\pi_{1}(S(\mathbb{C}),s))
.

Let’s continue considering an abelian scheme
\mathcal{A}\rightarrow S
and a non-zero section
\sigma:S\rightarrow\mathcal{A}
. Generally neither a basis of periods nor a logarithm
\log_{\sigma}
can be globally defined on the whole of
S(\mathbb{C})
(see
Remark

3.5
and
Remark

3.10
below), but obviously they can be globally defined on the universal cover of
S(\mathbb{C})
. Studying the related monodromy problems corresponds to finding out the minimal topological cover of
S(\mathbb{C})
on which both a basis of the periods and a logarithm of the section can be defined. With this in mind, we first call
S^{*}\rightarrow S(\mathbb{C})
the minimal topological cover of
S(\mathbb{C})
on which a basis for the period lattice can be globally defined and we set
S_{\sigma}\rightarrow S^{*}
to be the minimal cover of
S^{*}
where one can define the logarithm of
\sigma
. The tower of covers is represented in the diagram:

S_{\sigma}\rightarrow S^{*}\rightarrow S(\mathbb{C}).

(17)

In particular, the group
\textnormal{Mon}(\mathcal{A})
corresponds to the Galois group of the covering map
S^{*}\rightarrow S(\mathbb{C})
, while the group
M_{\sigma}
corresponds to the Galois group of the covering map
S_{\sigma}\rightarrow S(\mathbb{C})
. Our interest is in studying the relative monodromy of the logarithm of a section with respect to the monodromy of periods, i.e. studying the covering map
S_{\sigma}\rightarrow S^{*}
. Topologically, this is the same as looking at the variation of logarithm via analytic continuation along loops on
S(\mathbb{C})
which leave periods unchanged. With the notations of
Equation

10
and
Equation

16
this corresponds to studying group defined below:

Definition 3.7
.

M_{\sigma}^{\textnormal{rel}}:=\theta_{\sigma}(\ker{\rho})
is called the
relative monodromy group of
\sigma
.

The group
M_{\sigma}^{\textnormal{rel}}
gives information on the “pure monodromy” of the Betti map, and this is a strong knowledge for obtaining transcendence results which for example allow to count torsion points in issues with “unlikely intersection” flavour. Note that
M_{\sigma}^{\textnormal{rel}}
is clearly a subgroup of
\mathbb{Z}^{2g}
; it is useful for applications knowing when this subgroup is trivial and how large it is.

Example 3.8
.

Let us illustrate what happens in a simple case, i.e. when the section is torsion. Thus, let
\sigma:S\rightarrow\mathcal{A}
be a torsion section. By the properties of Betti map, any logarithm of
\sigma
is a rational constant combination of periods; i.e.

\log_{\sigma}=q_{1}\omega_{1}+\cdots+q_{2g}\omega_{2g},

where
q_{1},\ldots,q_{2g}\in\mathbb{Q}
. Therefore, a loop which leaves unchanged periods via analytic continuation, leaves also unchanged the logarithm of such a section. In other words, the cover
S_{\sigma}\rightarrow S^{*}
is trivial in this case and then
M_{\sigma}^{\textnormal{rel}}\cong\{0\}
.

Remark 3.9
.

Note that the Zariski-closures of the discrete groups
\textnormal{Mon}(\mathcal{A})
and
M_{\sigma}
as far as the kernel of the projection
\textrm{ker}(\overline{M_{\sigma}}\to\overline{\textnormal{Mon}(\mathcal{A})})
are the differential Galois groups of some Picard-Vessiot extensions of
\mathbb{C}(S)
obtained with
\omega_{1},\ldots,\omega_{2g},\log_{\sigma}
and their derivatives (see
[
14
]
for further details about differential Galois theory and Picard-Vessiot extensions). In these terms, the differential Galois group
\textrm{ker}(\overline{M_{\sigma}}\to\overline{\textnormal{Mon}(\mathcal{A})})
was just studied in
[
2
]
,
[
5
]
,
[
6
]
. Anyway, those results give no information on the relative monodromy of the logarithm over
S^{*}
because they involve the Zariski closures of
M_{\sigma}
and
\textnormal{Mon}(\mathcal{A})
: in fact, in
[
13
]
the authors pointed out that it may happen that the group
\textrm{ker}(\overline{M_{\sigma}}\to\overline{\textnormal{Mon}(\mathcal{A})})
contains quite limited information on
M_{\sigma}^{\textrm{rel}}
since the former may be larger than expected in comparison with the latter. Thus, in order to obtain information on
M_{\sigma}^{\textnormal{rel}}
, we have to introduce considerations of different nature with respect to Bertrand’s and André’s theorems.

Similarly to period functions, once we have locally defined the logarithm of a section as in
Equation

7
we can think about its analytic continuation through the whole of
S(\mathbb{C})
. As a first example, let us focus on the zero-section
\sigma_{0}
which associates to each
s\in S(\mathbb{C})
the origin
O_{s}
of the corresponding fiber
\mathcal{A}_{s}
. A logarithm of
\sigma_{0}
is given by the zero function

\log_{\sigma_{0}}:S(\mathbb{C})\rightarrow\mathbb{C}^{g},\qquad\log_{\sigma_{0}}(s)=0\textrm{ for each }s\in S(\mathbb{C}).

Thus, in this case we can find a globally defined logarithm on the whole of
S(\mathbb{C})
: in fact it has no monodromy. For algebraic sections, this is the only case in which such a global logarithm exists. For the sake of completeness, we briefly resume a proof of this fact.

Remark 3.10
.

Let
\mathcal{A}\to S
be an abelian scheme. We use Lang-Néron theorem
[
30
, Thorem 1]
to show that a non-zero algebraic section
\sigma:S\rightarrow\mathcal{A}
which is not contained in the fixed part cannot admit a globally defined logarithm on
S(\mathbb{C})
. In fact, assume by contradiction that
\log_{\sigma}
exists globally on
S(\mathbb{C})
, then
\frac{\sigma}{n}:=\exp\circ\left(\frac{1}{n}\log_{\sigma}\right)
for any
n\in\mathbb{Z}\setminus\{0\}
is a holomorphic section such that
n\cdot\frac{\sigma}{n}=\sigma
. This means that
\sigma
is infinitely divisible in
\Sigma(S)
contradicting Lang-Néron theorem.

Fix a determination of
\log_{\sigma}
on an open set
U
containing the base point
s
and use the notation
G:=\pi_{1}(S(\mathbb{C}),s)
. Thanks to
Equation

15
, the map
c:h\mapsto(u_{1}^{h},\ldots,u_{2g}^{h})

is a cocycle for the action of
G
on
\mathbb{Z}^{2g}
described in
Equation

14
. Moreover, in the next proposition we prove that a section admitting a globally defined logarithm is characterized by the fact that the map
h\mapsto(u_{1}^{h},\ldots,u_{2g}^{h})
is a coboundary for the aforementioned action, i.e. there exists a fixed vector
(u_{1},\ldots,u_{2g})\in\mathbb{Z}^{2g}
such that
(u_{1}^{h},\ldots,u_{2g}^{h})=(u_{1},\ldots,u_{2g})(\rho(h)^{\top}-1_{2g})
for all
h\in G
. Thus, the just defined cocycle determines an element in the cohomology group
H^{1}(G,\mathbb{Z}^{2g})
that describes the obstruction for a(n analytic) section to have a globally defined logarithm.

Proposition 3.11
.

Let
\sigma:S\rightarrow\mathcal{A}
be a holomorphic section and
\log_{\sigma}
a determination of its logarithm over an open set
U\subset S(\mathbb{C})
. The section admits a globally defined logarithm on
S(\mathbb{C})
if and only if the associated cocycle
h\mapsto(u_{1}^{h},\ldots,u_{2g}^{h})
is a coboundary.

Proof.

Suppose that
\sigma
admits a globally defined logarithm
\ell:S(\mathbb{C})\rightarrow\mathbb{C}^{g}
. Then the two determinations
\ell
and
\log_{\sigma}
over
U
differ by a period
\omega:=n_{1}\omega_{1}+\cdots+n_{2g}\omega_{2g}
, i.e.
\log_{\sigma}=\ell+n_{1}\omega_{1}+\cdots+n_{2g}\omega_{2g}
. For
h\in G
, we have

\displaystyle h\cdot\log_{\sigma}

\displaystyle=h\cdot(\ell+n_{1}\omega_{1}+\cdots+n_{2g}\omega_{2g})=\ell+(n_{1},\ldots,n_{2g})\rho(h)^{\top}\left(\begin{matrix}\omega_{1}\\
\vdots\\
\omega_{2g}\end{matrix}\right)=

\displaystyle=\log_{\sigma}+\left[(n_{1},\ldots,n_{2g})\rho(h)^{\top}-(n_{1},\ldots,n_{2g})\right]\left(\begin{matrix}\omega_{1}\\
\vdots\\
\omega_{2g}\end{matrix}\right).

Thus the corresponding cocycle is given by

h\mapsto(n_{1},\ldots,n_{2g})\rho(h)^{\top}-(n_{1},\ldots,n_{2g})

for
h\in G
and a fixed pair
(n_{1},\ldots,n_{2g})\in\mathbb{Z}^{2g}
, hence it is a coboundary.

Viceversa, let us suppose to have a logarithm
\log_{\sigma}
over
U
and that there exists a fixed
2g
-tuple
(n_{1},\ldots,n_{2g})\in\mathbb{Z}^{2g}
such that

h\cdot\log_{\sigma}=\log_{\sigma}+\left[(n_{1},\ldots,n_{2g})\rho(h)^{\top}-(n_{1},\ldots,n_{2g})\right]\left(\begin{matrix}\omega_{1}\\
\vdots\\
\omega_{2g}\end{matrix}\right)

for each
h\in G
. Let us define the function

\ell:=\log_{\sigma}-n_{1}\omega_{1}-\cdots-n_{2g}\omega_{2g},

which is another determination of the logarithm of
\sigma
. Looking at the action of
G
we obtain

\displaystyle h\cdot\ell

\displaystyle=h\cdot(\log_{\sigma}-n_{1}\omega_{1}-\cdots-n_{2g}\omega_{2g})=

\displaystyle=\log_{\sigma}+\left[(n_{1},\ldots,n_{2g})\rho(h)^{\top}-(n_{1},\ldots,n_{2g})\right]\left(\begin{matrix}\omega_{1}\\
\vdots\\
\omega_{2g}\end{matrix}\right)-(n_{1},\ldots,n_{2g})\rho(h)^{\top}\left(\begin{matrix}\omega_{1}\\
\vdots\\
\omega_{2g}\end{matrix}\right)=

\displaystyle=\log_{\sigma}-n_{1}\omega_{1}-\cdots-n_{2g}\omega_{2g}=\ell.

Therefore,
\ell
is a globally defined logarithm of
\sigma
on
S(\mathbb{C})
.

∎

4
Proof of the main results

4.1
Invariance results

In this section we want to provide some preliminary results about the relative monodromy group
M_{\sigma}^{\textnormal{rel}}
in order to prove that our main theorem are insensitive to some natural operations. First of all, let us notice that
Theorem

1.2
is invariant under isogenies: this was proven in
[
41
, Theorem 3.6]
. In what follows we describe other invariance results.

Lemma 4.1
.

Theorem

1.2
is invariant by finite base change: in other words, if
\varphi:\widetilde{S}\rightarrow S
is a finite morphism and
\widetilde{\mathcal{A}}\to\widetilde{S}
is the pullback of
\mathcal{A}\rightarrow S
via
\varphi
, then
Theorem

1.2
for
\widetilde{\mathcal{A}}\rightarrow\widetilde{S}
implies
Theorem

1.2
for
\mathcal{A}\rightarrow S
.

Proof.

Let us denote by
\omega_{1},\ldots,\omega_{2g}
a basis of periods of
\mathcal{A}\rightarrow S
. We can define
\widetilde{\omega_{1}},\ldots,\widetilde{\omega_{2g}}
, a basis of periods of
\widetilde{\mathcal{A}}\rightarrow\widetilde{S}
, by the equations

\widetilde{\omega_{1}}=\omega_{1}\circ\varphi,\;\ldots,\;\widetilde{\omega_{2g}}=\omega_{2g}\circ\varphi.

(18)

Fix two base points
\widetilde{s}\in\widetilde{S}(\mathbb{C})
and
s\in S(\mathbb{C})
such that
\varphi(\widetilde{s})=s
. We have the associated monodromy representations:

\rho:\pi_{1}(S(\mathbb{C}),s)\rightarrow\textrm{Mon}(\mathcal{A}),\qquad\widetilde{\rho}:\pi_{1}(\widetilde{S}(\mathbb{C}),\widetilde{s})\rightarrow\textrm{Mon}(\widetilde{A}).

By
Equation

18
, we have
\widetilde{\rho}(h)=\rho(\varphi_{*}(h))
for each
h\in\pi_{1}(\widetilde{S}(\mathbb{C}),\widetilde{s})
where
\varphi_{*}:\pi_{1}(\widetilde{S}(\mathbb{C}),\widetilde{s})\rightarrow\pi_{1}(S(\mathbb{C}),s)
denotes the induced homomorphism between fundamental groups. In particular, we obtain

\varphi_{*}(\ker{\widetilde{\rho}})=\ker{\rho}\cap\varphi_{*}(\pi_{1}(\widetilde{S}(\mathbb{C}),\widetilde{s})).

(19)

Let
\sigma:S\rightarrow\mathcal{A}
be a non-torsion section of
\mathcal{A}\rightarrow S
. Since
\widetilde{\mathcal{A}}\rightarrow\widetilde{S}
is obtained as pullback of
\mathcal{A}\rightarrow S
, then the abelian varieties
\widetilde{\mathcal{A}}_{\widetilde{s}}
and
\mathcal{A}_{s}
are canonically identified; therefore the pullback
\varphi^{*}\sigma:\widetilde{S}\rightarrow\widetilde{\mathcal{A}}
is a non-torsion section of
\widetilde{\mathcal{A}}\rightarrow\widetilde{S}
. We have the associated monodromy representations

\theta_{\sigma}:\pi_{1}(S(\mathbb{C}),s)\rightarrow\textrm{SL}_{2g+1}(\mathbb{Z}),\qquad\theta_{\varphi^{*}\sigma}:\pi_{1}(\widetilde{S}(\mathbb{C}),\widetilde{s})\rightarrow\textrm{SL}_{2g+1}(\mathbb{Z}).

Using again the fact that the abelian varieties
\widetilde{\mathcal{A}}_{\widetilde{s}}
and
\mathcal{A}_{s}
are canonically identified, we obtain that a determination of the logarithm of
\varphi^{*}\sigma
at
\widetilde{s}
can be defined by the equation

\log_{\varphi^{*}\sigma}(\widetilde{s}):=\log_{\sigma}(s).

(20)

Hence, we have
\theta_{\varphi^{*}\sigma}(h)=\theta_{\sigma}(\varphi_{*}(h))
for each
h\in\pi_{1}(\widetilde{S}(\mathbb{C}),\widetilde{s})
. Now, let us consider the relative monodromy group

M_{\varphi^{*}\sigma}^{\textnormal{rel}}=\theta_{\varphi^{*}\sigma}(\ker{\widetilde{\rho}}).

By
Equation

19
and
Equation

20
, we obtain

M_{\varphi^{*}\sigma}^{\textnormal{rel}}=\theta_{\sigma}(\varphi_{*}(\ker{\widetilde{\rho}}))=\theta_{\sigma}(\ker{\rho}\cap\varphi_{*}(\pi_{1}(\widetilde{S}(\mathbb{C}),\widetilde{s}))).

Since
\ker{\rho}\cap\varphi_{*}(\pi_{1}(\widetilde{S}(\mathbb{C}),\widetilde{s}))\subset\ker{\rho}
, we get
M_{\varphi^{*}\sigma}^{\textnormal{rel}}\subseteq M_{\sigma}^{\textnormal{rel}}
. The conclusion follows.

∎

Let us consider a non-torsion section
\sigma:S\rightarrow\mathcal{A}
. Note that the representation
\theta_{\sigma}
is defined in terms of
Equation

14
, thus it depends on the branch of logarithm we fix. Here, we want to prove that
M_{\sigma}^{\textnormal{rel}}
is independent of the choice of branch of
\log_{\sigma}
and remains unchanged under some operations on the section.

Proposition 4.2
.

Let
\sigma:S\rightarrow\mathcal{A}
be a non-torsion section. Then

(i)

the group
M_{\sigma}^{\textnormal{rel}}
does not depend on the choice of branch of
\log_{\sigma}
;

(ii)

the groups
M_{\sigma}^{\textnormal{rel}}
and
M_{n\sigma}^{\textnormal{rel}}
are isomorphic.

Proof.

(i)
: Choose two branches of
\log_{\sigma}
, say
\ell_{\sigma}^{1}
and
\ell_{\sigma}^{2}
and denote by
M_{\sigma,1}^{\textnormal{rel}}
and
M_{\sigma,2}^{\textnormal{rel}}
the corresponding relative monodromy groups. Since the two branches
\ell_{\sigma}^{1}
and
\ell_{\sigma}^{2}
have to differ by a period, the conclusion follows: in fact, since any loop
\alpha
in
S(\mathbb{C})
whose homotopy class lies in
\ker{\rho}
leaves periods unchanged, then
\ell_{\sigma}^{1}
and
\ell_{\sigma}^{2}
have the same variation by analytic continuation along
\alpha
; thus we get
M_{\sigma,1}^{\textnormal{rel}}=M_{\sigma,2}^{\textnormal{rel}}
.

(ii)
: Fixed a branch
\ell_{\sigma}
of
\log_{\sigma}
, a determination
\ell_{n\sigma}
of the logarithm of
n\sigma
can be defined by the equation
\ell_{n\sigma}=n\ell_{\sigma}
. For any loop
\alpha
representing a homotopy class
h\in\pi_{1}(S(\mathbb{C}),s)
we have the corresponding variations:

h\cdot\ell_{n\sigma}=\ell_{n\sigma}+\omega_{\alpha}^{n\sigma},\qquad h\cdot\ell_{\sigma}=\ell_{\sigma}+\omega_{\alpha}^{\sigma},

where
\omega_{\alpha}^{n\sigma}=n\omega_{\alpha}^{\sigma}
. Thus
M_{\sigma}^{\textnormal{rel}}
and
M_{n\sigma}^{\textnormal{rel}}
are isomorphic.

∎

4.2
Non triviality of the relative monodromy

In this section we prove
Theorem

1.2
, i.e. that
M_{\sigma}^{\textnormal{rel}}
is non-trivial for all sections which ramify outside a fixed proper closed subset of the base. Recall that by
Lemma

4.1
, we can assume that the abelian scheme
\phi:\mathcal{A}\to S
is endowed with a dominant morphism
p^{\nu}:S\to T^{\nu}
coming from the normalization of
T
.

By the hypothesis of the theorem, the modular map
p\colon S\to T
is assumed to be generically finite. Using
[
28
, Lemma 10.5]
, this implies that the locus
U_{p}\subseteq S
where
p
is finite and flat forms a dense Zariski-open subset. The non-triviality of
M_{\sigma}^{\textnormal{rel}}
is preserved under restriction to dense Zariski-open subsets of the base, as the induced homomorphism between fundamental groups is surjective by
[
29
, Proposition 2.10]
. Therefore, after possibly replacing
S
with
U_{p}
, we may assume that the morphism
p=\nu\circ p^{\nu}\colon S\to T
is finite and flat. Observe that
p^{\nu}
is quasi-finite by construction. Moreover, since
\nu
is separated, the morphism
p^{\nu}
is proper and consequently finite and surjective.

Remark 4.3
.

The restriction to
U_{p}
is necessary because our argument relies on the “trace operator” for sections, as defined in
Equation

24
. The key properties of this operator, particularly the one established in
Equation

25
, are valid when
p
is flat and finite.

Given a section
\sigma
of
\mathcal{A}/S
, the proof proceeds by assuming that the group
M_{\sigma}^{\textnormal{rel}}
is trivial and showing that
\sigma
must then be unramified. The key idea involves constructing an “unramified family”, i.e. an unramified base change of the universal family, associated with the abelian scheme, which ensures that the cohomology class arising from the logarithm of
\sigma
“descends”. Applying the Lefschetz hyperplane theorem then imposes strong restrictions on the logarithm of
\sigma
. Combining this with
Proposition

2.1
and
Corollary

2.3
, we conclude that
\sigma
is indeed unramified.

In order to construct the aforementioned unramified family, we consider the map
p^{\nu}:S\to T^{\nu}
and we look at the induced homomorphism on fundamental groups
p^{\nu}_{*}:\pi_{1}(S(\mathbb{C}),s)\to\pi_{1}(T^{\nu}(\mathbb{C}),x)
where
s\in S(\mathbb{C})
and
x\in T^{\nu}(\mathbb{C})
are two base points such that
x=p^{\nu}(s)
. By
[
31
, Corollary 12.19]
the inclusion
p^{\nu}_{*}\pi_{1}(S(\mathbb{C}),s)\subseteq\pi_{1}(T^{\nu}(\mathbb{C}),x)
gives rise to a finite topological cover
q^{\prime}:S^{\prime}(\mathbb{C})\to T^{\nu}(\mathbb{C})
that satisfies

q^{\prime}_{*}\pi_{1}(S^{\prime}(\mathbb{C}),s^{\prime})=p^{\nu}_{*}\pi_{1}(S(\mathbb{C}),s),

(21)

where
s^{\prime}
is a fixed base point with
q^{\prime}(s^{\prime})=x
. By the Riemann existence theorem (see
[
26
, Exposé XII, Théorème 5.1]
) at the level of schemes
q^{\prime}:S^{\prime}\to T^{\nu}
is a finite étale cover. By the lifting theorem for étale fundamental groups and the relation with the topological fundamental group we can lift
p^{\nu}
to a morphism
q:S(\mathbb{C})\rightarrow S^{\prime}(\mathbb{C})
such that
q(s)=s^{\prime}
. Considering the corresponding abelian schemes we obtain the diagram

{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\mathcal{A}(\mathbb{C})}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\mathcal{A}^{\prime}(\mathbb{C})}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\mathfrak{A}_{g,T^{\nu}}(\mathbb{C})}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces S(\mathbb{C})}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces S^{\prime}(\mathbb{C})}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces T^{\nu}(\mathbb{C}).}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces q}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces p^{\nu}}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces q^{\prime}}

(22)

Notice that the abelian scheme
\mathcal{A}^{\prime}\to S^{\prime}
is an unramified family by construction, since
q^{\prime}:S^{\prime}(\mathbb{C})\to T^{\nu}(\mathbb{C})\subseteq\mathbb{A}_{g}(\mathbb{C})
is in particular an unramified morphism.

Remark 4.4
.

We want to point out some algebraic and topological properties of the above construction. By
[
26
, Exposé I, Corollaire 10.2]
the finite étale morphism
q^{\prime}
is obtained as normalization of
T^{\nu}
in the field extension
\mathbb{C}(S^{\prime})
of
\mathbb{C}(T^{\nu})
. By universal property of normalization in a field extension, the morphism
q
is dominant. Since
p
and
q^{\prime}
are both finite morphisms then
q
is quasi-finite and proper, hence a finite surjective morphism as well. Additionally, since
S^{\prime}
is connected and
T^{\nu}
is integral and normal it follows that
S^{\prime}
is normal and irreducible (see
[
39
, Tag 0BQL]
).

From the topological point of view, observe that the induced homomorphism
q_{*}:\pi_{1}(S(\mathbb{C}),s)\to\pi_{1}(S^{\prime}(\mathbb{C}),s^{\prime})
is surjective. This follows from the diagram

{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\pi_{1}(S(\mathbb{C}),s)}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\pi_{1}(S^{\prime}(\mathbb{C}),s^{\prime})}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces p^{\nu}_{*}\pi_{1}(S(\mathbb{C}),s),}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces q_{*}}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces p^{\nu}_{*}}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces q^{\prime}_{*}}

(23)

where
q^{\prime}_{*}:\pi_{1}(S^{\prime}(\mathbb{C}),s^{\prime})\to p^{\nu}_{*}\pi_{1}(S(\mathbb{C}),s)
is an isomorphism.

Let’s fix some notations. We denote by
\exp
and
\exp^{\prime}
the abelian exponential maps of the schemes
\mathcal{A}\to S
and
\mathcal{A}^{\prime}\to S^{\prime}
, respectively. Moreover, recall that
\Sigma^{\an}
is the sheaf of holomorphic sections of the scheme
\mathcal{A}\to S
, while
\Sigma(S)
denotes its algebraic sections. Analogously, we denote by
\Sigma^{\prime,\an}
and
\Sigma^{\prime}(S^{\prime})
the sheaf of holomorphic sections and the algebraic sections of the abelian scheme
\mathcal{A}^{\prime}\to S^{\prime}
, respectively. Now, we want to define a way of mapping sections of
\mathcal{A}\to S
to sections of
\mathcal{A}^{\prime}\to S^{\prime}
, and viceversa.

First of all we define the the pull-back operator: in fact notice that for any
\tau^{\prime}\in\Sigma^{\prime,\an}(S^{\prime})
the composition
\tau^{\prime}\circ q
defines a holomorphic map
S(\mathbb{C})\to\mathcal{A}^{\prime}(\mathbb{C})
; after canonically identifying the fibers of the type
\mathcal{A}_{y}
and
\mathcal{A}^{\prime}_{q(y)}
, we obtain an element
q^{*}(\tau^{\prime})\in\Sigma^{\an}(S)
. In other words, we obtain a morphism of abelian groups

q^{*}:\Sigma^{\prime,\an}(S^{\prime})\to\Sigma^{\an}(S).

In addition if
\tau^{\prime}
is algebraic then
q^{*}(\tau^{\prime})
is still algebraic, i.e.
q^{*}(\Sigma^{\prime}(S^{\prime}))\subseteq\Sigma(S)
.

In the opposite direction, since
q
is a finite flat morphism the notion of degree is well-behaved with respect to the ramification index (see
[
25
, Proposition 12.21]
). Thus, we can define the trace operator

\Tr:\Sigma^{\an}(S)\to\Sigma^{\prime,\an}(S^{\prime})

in the following way: we already noticed that for each
y\in S(\mathbb{C})
the fibers
\mathcal{A}_{y}
and
\mathcal{A}^{\prime}_{q(y)}
are canonically identified, so for
\tau\in\Sigma^{\an}(S)
we define
\Tr(\tau):S^{\prime}(\mathbb{C})\to\mathcal{A}^{\prime}(\mathbb{C})
by setting

\Tr(\tau)(y^{\prime}):=\sum_{y\in q^{-1}(y^{\prime})}m_{y}\tau(y)\in\mathcal{A}^{\prime}_{y^{\prime}},

(24)

where
m_{y}
is the ramification index of
q
at
y
. We have
\Tr(\Sigma(S))\subseteq\Sigma^{\prime}(S^{\prime})
. Moreover, we point out that the following property holds for each holomorphic section
\tau^{\prime}:S^{\prime}(\mathbb{C})\rightarrow\mathcal{A}^{\prime}(\mathbb{C})
:

\Tr\circ\,q^{*}(\tau^{\prime})=\deg{q}\cdot\tau^{\prime}.

(25)

It is clear that the trace operator and the pullback operator can be defined also for the sections of the vector bundles
\left(\mathcal{O}^{\an}_{S}\right)^{\oplus g}
and
\left(\mathcal{O}^{\an}_{S^{\prime}}\right)^{\oplus g}
, i.e. for vectors of holomorphic functions on
S
and
S^{\prime}
. They commute with the exponentials, in the sense that for all holomorphic functions
f:S(\mathbb{C})\rightarrow\mathbb{C}^{g}
and
f^{\prime}:S^{\prime}(\mathbb{C})\rightarrow\mathbb{C}^{g}
we get

\Tr(\exp(f))=\exp^{\prime}(\Tr(f))\qquad\textrm{and}\qquad\exp(q^{*}(f^{\prime}))=q^{*}(\exp^{\prime}(f^{\prime})).

(26)

Now we want to consider some cohomology groups related to the monodromy of logarithms. In our situation the bases of abelian schemes are too large to be able of having control on their cohomology. If we consider an algebraic curve
C\subset S(\mathbb{C})
, we can think of restricting the diagram of
Equation

22
to
C
: more precisely, if we denote
C^{\prime}:=q(C)
and
D:=q^{\prime}(C^{\prime})
we get a new diagram:

{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\mathcal{A}_{|_{C}}(\mathbb{C})}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\mathcal{A}^{\prime}_{|_{C^{\prime}}}(\mathbb{C})}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\mathfrak{A}_{g|_{D}}(\mathbb{C})}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces C}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces C^{\prime}}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces D,}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces q_{C}}

\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces q^{\prime}_{C^{\prime}}}

(27)

where
q_{C},q^{\prime}_{C^{\prime}}
and

denote the restrictions of the corresponding maps. In this situation, if
C^{\prime}
and
D
are still of dimension
1

\sigma:S\to\mathcal{A}
to
C
, which will be denoted by
\sigma_{C}

\rho_{C},\theta_{\sigma_{C}},\textrm{Mon}(\mathcal{A}|_{C})
and

for the obvious objects associated to the scheme

C^{\prime}
. Moreover, if we denote by

and

the natural embeddings and choose base points
s\in C
and

Anyway, this kind of restriction causes the loss of much information from the starting schemes. With the next result we want to overcome the problem of having large bases, by showing that we can restrict the abelian schemes to some suitable curves lying into the bases so that the topological and monodromy properties are preserved.

Lemma 4.5
.

There exists a curve
C\subseteq S(\mathbb{C})
with the following properties:

(i)

the morphism
q_{C}:C\to C^{\prime}
appearing in
Equation

27
is finite, the curves
C
and
C^{\prime}
are affine and the abelian schemes
\mathcal{A}_{|_{C}}\to C
,
\mathcal{A}^{\prime}_{|_{C^{\prime}}}\to C^{\prime}
are without fixed part;

(ii)

\Tr(\sigma_{C})=\Tr(\sigma)|_{C^{\prime}}
for any
\sigma\in\Sigma^{\textnormal{an}}(S)
and
\textnormal{Tr}\circ q_{C}^{*}(\tau)=\deg{q}\cdot\tau
for any
\tau\in\Sigma^{\textnormal{an}}(C^{\prime})
;

(iii)

the homomorphisms
i_{*}
and
i^{\prime}_{*}
appearing in
Equation

28
are surjective;

(iv)

if we denote by
\rho^{\prime}
the monodromy representation of the abelian scheme
\mathcal{A}^{\prime}\to S^{\prime}
, then we have
\rho_{C}=\rho\circ i_{*},\rho_{C^{\prime}}=\rho^{\prime}\circ i^{\prime}_{*}
and
\textrm{Mon}(\mathcal{A})=\textrm{Mon}(\mathcal{A}|_{C}),\textrm{Mon}(\mathcal{A}^{\prime})=\textrm{Mon}(\mathcal{A}^{\prime}|_{C^{\prime}})
;

(v)

\ker{{q_{C}}_{\ast}}\subseteq\ker{\rho_{C}}
;

(vi)

\theta_{\sigma_{C}}=\theta_{\sigma}\circ i_{*}
and
\theta_{\sigma}(\pi_{1}(S(\mathbb{C}),s))=\theta_{\sigma_{C}}(\pi_{1}(C,s))
, for any
\sigma\in\Sigma^{\textnormal{an}}(S)
;

(vii)

if
M_{\sigma}^{\textrm{rel}}=\{0\}
, then
M_{\sigma_{C}}^{\textrm{rel}}=\{0\}
, for any
\sigma\in\Sigma^{\textnormal{an}}(S)
.

Proof.

(i)

In order to construct the curve
C
let us consider the general hyperplane section
Y=H\cap S(\mathbb{C})
and choose a base point
s\in Y
. By Lefschetz theorem on quasi-projective varieties
[
24
, Part II, 1.2, Theorem]
, the natural map
\pi_{1}(Y,s)\to\pi_{1}(S(\mathbb{C}),s)
between fundamental groups is surjective. After iterating the process of taking such generic hyperplane sections we obtain an irreducible curve
C_{1}
with the property that the natural map
\pi_{1}(C_{1},s)\to\pi_{1}(S(\mathbb{C}),s)
is surjective for a base point
s\in C_{1}
. Note that
C_{1}
can be assumed to be affine up to remove a finite number of points and using
[
29
, Proposition 2.10]
. The restriction of
q
to
C_{1}
defines a morphism
q_{C_{1}}
which is finite: in fact, since every closed embedding is a finite morphism and
q:S\to S^{\prime}
is a finite morphism (see

4.4
), then the restriction
q_{C_{1}}
is finite. We define
C^{\prime}:=q_{C_{1}}(C_{1})
and
C:=q^{-1}(C^{\prime})
. Notice that
C^{\prime}
is an affine curve since
q_{C_{1}}
is finite and surjective; moreover,
C
could be no longer irreducible but it is still affine because
q
is an affine morphism (since it is finite). Furthermore, since
q:S\to S^{\prime}
is finite then it is closed. We have a closed embedding
i:C\hookrightarrow S(\mathbb{C})
, and we can conclude that the restriction
q_{C}:C\to C^{\prime}
is a finite morphism. The abelian scheme
\mathcal{A}_{|_{C}}\to C
is without fixed part by genericity of the hyperplane sections; this implies that also
\mathcal{A}^{\prime}_{|_{C^{\prime}}}\to C^{\prime}
is without fixed part.

(ii)

This properties are true by construction, since
C=q^{-1}(C^{\prime})
.

(iii)

By
(i)
we have a surjective homomorphism
\pi_{1}(C_{1},s)\to\pi_{1}(S(\mathbb{C}),s)
. Recall that
C_{1}
is an irreducible component of
C
. Since the inclusion map
C_{1}\hookrightarrow S(\mathbb{C})
factors through
C
, then the homomorphism
\pi_{1}(C_{1},s)\to\pi_{1}(S(\mathbb{C}),s)
factors through
\pi_{1}(C,s)
. Thus,
i_{*}:\pi_{1}(C,s)\to\pi_{1}(S(\mathbb{C}),s)
is surjective. Then, the surjectivity of
i^{\prime}_{*}:\pi_{1}(C^{\prime},s^{\prime})\to\pi_{1}(S^{\prime}(\mathbb{C}),s^{\prime})
follows by
Equation

28
.

(iv)

Just recall that for abelian schemes obtained as base change we define periods as in
Equation

12
, so that we have
\rho_{C}=\rho\circ i_{*}
and
\rho_{C^{\prime}}=\rho^{\prime}\circ i^{\prime}_{*}
. Therefore, the claim follows from
(iii)
.

(v)

Let
h\in\ker{q_{C*}}
. Since we can define periods of
\mathcal{A}|_{C}\to C
as in
Equation

12
and
q_{C*}(h)
has a trivial monodromy action on the periods of
\mathcal{A}^{\prime}|_{C^{\prime}}\to C^{\prime}
, then we get

; hence the claim.

(vi)

Since we have the embedding
i:C\hookrightarrow S(\mathbb{C})
, we clearly have
\theta_{\sigma_{C}}(\pi_{1}(C),s)\subseteq\theta_{\sigma}(\pi_{1}(S(\mathbb{C}),s))
. In the other direction, by
(iv)
it is enough to show that each variation of a logarithm of
\sigma
induced by an element of
\pi_{1}(S(\mathbb{C}),s)
can be obtained as a variation of a logarithm of
\sigma_{C}
induced by the monodromy action of some element of
\pi_{1}(C,s)
; and this is true thanks to the surjectivity of
i_{*}
which was proven in part
(iii)
. This proves the claim.

(vii)

Let us assume
M_{\sigma}^{\textrm{rel}}=\{0\}
and consider
h\in\ker{\rho_{C}}
. By part
(iv)
we have
\rho_{C}=\rho\circ i_{*}
, and this implies that
i_{*}(h)\in\ker{\rho}
. The assumption
M_{\sigma}^{\textrm{rel}}=\{0\}
implies that
\theta_{\sigma}(i_{*}(h))=0
. By part

we have
\theta_{\sigma_{C}}=\theta_{\sigma}\circ i_{*}
which implies
\theta_{\sigma_{C}}(h)=\theta_{\sigma}(i_{*}(h))=0
, i.e.
M_{\sigma_{C}}^{\textrm{rel}}=\{0\}
.

∎

Now, let us consider an algebraic curve

satisfying

. We want to consider the restriction of the exact sequence in

.

.

Let us point out the relationship between the cohomology groups

4.3
Rank of relative monodromy

Here, we prove
Corollary

1.3
.

We keep the same notations as above, in particular recall that
\rho:\pi_{1}(S(\mathbb{C}),s)\to\textrm{Mon}(\mathcal{A})
denotes the monodromy representation associated to the abelian scheme
\phi:\mathcal{A}\to S
, which we are assuming to induce an irreducible action of
\textrm{Mon}(\mathcal{A})
on the lattice of periods. In what follows we identify the
\mathbb{Z}
-module
M_{\sigma}^{\textrm{rel}}
with the corresponding
\mathbb{Z}
-submodule of the lattice of periods.

Proof of
Corollary

1.3

Let us proceed by contradiction by assuming that
M_{\sigma}^{\textrm{rel}}
is a submodule of
\mathbb{Z}^{2g}
of rank
g^{\prime}
with
g^{\prime}<2g
. By the non-triviality of
M_{\sigma}^{\textrm{rel}}
proved in
Theorem

1.2
we have
g^{\prime}>0
. In other words, this means that for every
h\in H:=\ker{\rho}
the logarithm
\log_{\sigma}
is transformed by
h
as

\log_{\sigma}\xmapsto{h}\log_{\sigma}+u_{1}^{h}\mu_{1}+\cdots+u_{g^{\prime}}^{h}\mu_{g^{\prime}},

where
\mu_{1},\ldots,\mu_{g^{\prime}}
are fixed non-zero periods (depending on
\sigma
). Recall that, for
k\in\pi_{1}(S(\mathbb{C}),s)
the logarithm
\log_{\sigma}
will be sent by
k
to a new determination of the form

\log_{\sigma}+v_{1}\omega_{1}+\cdots+v_{2g}\omega_{2g},

where
v_{1},\ldots,v_{2g}\in\mathbb{Z}
and
\omega_{1},\ldots,\omega_{2g}
denote period functions. Since we are assuming that the action of
\textrm{Mon}(\mathcal{A})
is irreducible, there exists
k\in\pi_{1}(S(\mathbb{C}),s)
such that
\rho(k)\cdot M_{\sigma}^{\textrm{rel}}\nsubseteq M_{\sigma}^{\textrm{rel}}
, i.e. the action of
\rho(k)
does not preserve the relative monodromy group. Therefore, we can fix
k\in\pi_{1}(S(\mathbb{C}),s)
and
h\in H
such that
\rho(k^{-1})\cdot(u_{1}^{h}\mu_{1}+\cdots+u_{g^{\prime}}^{h}\mu_{g^{\prime}})\notin M_{\sigma}^{\textrm{rel}}
; define
h^{\prime}:=k^{-1}hk
and observe that
h^{\prime}\in H
since
H\unlhd G
. If we look at the monodromy action of
h^{\prime}
on the abelian logarithm we obtain

\displaystyle\log_{\sigma}

\displaystyle\xmapsto{k}\log_{\sigma}+v_{1}\omega_{1}+\cdots+v_{2g}\omega_{2g}\xmapsto{h}\log_{\sigma}+v_{1}\omega_{1}+\cdots+v_{2g}\omega_{2g}+u_{1}^{h}\mu_{1}+\cdots+u_{g^{\prime}}^{h}\mu_{g^{\prime}}

\displaystyle\xmapsto{k^{-1}}\log_{\sigma}+\rho(k^{-1})\cdot(u_{1}^{h}\mu_{1}+\cdots+u_{g^{\prime}}^{h}\mu_{g^{\prime}}).

Since
h^{\prime}\in H
, then we have
\rho(k^{-1})\cdot(u_{1}^{h}\mu_{1}+\cdots+u_{g^{\prime}}^{h}\mu_{g^{\prime}})\in M_{\sigma}^{\textrm{rel}}
, which contradicts the choice of
k
and
h
. This concludes the proof.
∎

4.4
The case of thin Monodromy of periods

In
[
3
]
, André employs Hodge-theoretic methods to study the problems considered in this paper. Under the following hypotheses, he establishes
M_{\sigma}^{\textrm{rel}}\cong\mathbb{Z}^{2g}
:

(i)

\mathcal{A}\to S
is without fixed part, even after pull-back to a finite étale cover of
S
.

(ii)

\mathbb{Z}\sigma(S)
is Zariski dense in
\mathcal{A}
.

(iii)

The monodromy group
\textrm{Mon}(\mathcal{A})
is not thin.

Essentially, André’s hypothesis
(iii)
serves as an alternative to our assumption on the modular map
p
. The geometric distinction is clear: while we require a certain “sparseness” condition on isomorphic fibers of the family, hypothesis
(iii)
intuitively imposes that the image of the modular map is “sufficiently large.” A more precise comparison of these conditions, or a characterization of when they coincide, would be an interesting direction for further investigation.

Notice that our hypotheses in
Corollary

1.3
don’t exclude the case when
\textrm{Mon}(\mathcal{A})
is thin. Indeed, as a consequence of
Corollary

1.3
and the study of fiber products, we provide below an explicit construction showing a case where
M_{\sigma}^{\textrm{rel}}
is of maximal rank even though
\textrm{Mon}(\mathcal{A})
is thin.

Example 4.9
.

We consider the same construction as in
Example

2.4
. Note that the two elliptic schemes
\mathcal{E}_{1}\to B
and
\mathcal{E}_{2}\to B
are not isogenous, since they have a diﬀerent set of bad reduction. The fundamental group
G
of
B(\mathbb{C})
is free on three generators, and the monodromy representation on the periods of
\phi:\mathcal{A}\to B
gives a homomorphism
\rho:G\to\Gamma_{2}\times\Gamma_{2}
, where

\Gamma_{2}:=\left\langle\begin{pmatrix}1&2\\
0&1\end{pmatrix},\;\begin{pmatrix}1&0\\
2&1\end{pmatrix}\right\rangle\leq\textrm{SL}_{2}(\mathbb{Z}).

We denote the monodromy group of periods as
\textrm{Mon}(\mathcal{A}):=\rho(G)
. Since
\phi:\mathcal{A}\to B
is the product of two non-isogenous elliptic schemes, then the Zariski closure of
\textrm{Mon}(\mathcal{A})
is the whole
\textrm{SL}_{2}\times\textrm{SL}_{2}

[
12
, Theorem 5.3.10]
.

Notice that
\textrm{Mon}(\mathcal{A})
is generated by three elements, and the same holds for its projection on the abelianized group of
\Gamma_{2}\times\Gamma_{2}
, which is
\mathbb{Z}^{4}
. In particular, the projection in the abelianized group must have infinite index. Therefore, the group
\textrm{Mon}(\mathcal{A})
is not of finite index in the integral points of its Zariski-closure: in other words, the group
\textrm{Mon}(\mathcal{A})
is thin.

The abelian scheme
\phi\colon\mathcal{A}\to B
has trivial Mordell-Weil rank. Let us consider the map
\sigma:B\to\mathcal{A}
defined by

\sigma(\lambda):=\left(\left(2,\sqrt{2(2-\lambda)}\right),\left(3,\sqrt{6(1+\lambda)}\right)\right).

This map is a multisection of the abelian scheme
\phi
, but it becomes a section whether we consider the base change induced by the degree
4
field extension
\mathbb{C}(B)\subseteq\mathbb{C}(B)(\psi,\mu)
where
\psi^{2}+\mu^{2}=3
and
\mu^{2}=1+\lambda
. Denote by
\phi^{\prime}:\mathcal{A}^{\prime}\to B^{\prime}
the base change we just described. The monodromy of periods
\textrm{Mon}(\mathcal{A}^{\prime})
is a finite index subgroup of
\textrm{Mon}(\mathcal{A})
: in particular, it is Zariski dense in
\textrm{SL}_{2}\times\textrm{SL}_{2}
and it has infinite index in
\Gamma_{2}\times\Gamma_{2}
; so
\textrm{Mon}(\mathcal{A}^{\prime})
is still thin. The morphism
\sigma
is a section of
\phi^{\prime}
whose image is not contained in any proper group subscheme, thus the relative monodromy group
M_{\sigma}^{\textrm{rel}}
is isomorphic to
\mathbb{Z}^{4}
by
[
41
, Theorem 4.11]
. Notice that
\sigma
is ramified, since the field extension defining
B^{\prime}
induces a ramified morphism onto
B
and therefore this gives rise to a ramified (finite flat) modular map.

5
Some applications

We present some useful applications of our results. As a consequence of the non-triviality of relative monodromy we obtain a strong version of Manin’s kernel theorem under some hypothesis on unramified sections. In the case of elliptic schemes over a curve the same result was obtained in
[
13
]
. We refer to the Betti coordinates defined in
Section

2.2
(see
Equation

7
).

Theorem 5.1
.

Let
\phi:\mathcal{A}\to S
be an abelian scheme without fixed part such that the modular map
p:S\to T\subseteq\mathbb{A}_{g}
is generically finite. Denote by
U_{p}\subseteq S
the Zariski open subset on which
p
is finite and flat. Assume that all the sections unramified over
U_{p}
are torsion. Let
\sigma:S\to\mathcal{A}
be a section. If the Betti coordinates
\beta_{\sigma,i}
with
i=1,\ldots,2g
are globally defined functions on
S(\mathbb{C})
, then
\sigma
is a torsion section. In that case, the Betti coordinates are rational constants.

Proof.

If the Betti coordinates are globally defined on
S(\mathbb{C})
then the relative monodromy group
M_{\sigma}^{\textrm{rel}}
is trivial. The claim then follows by
Theorem

1.2
.
