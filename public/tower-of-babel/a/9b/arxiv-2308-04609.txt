Topological k k -metrics
Willow Barkan-Vered, Huck Bennett, Amir Nayyeri

Abstract
Metric spaces ( X , d ) (X,d) are ubiquitous objects in mathematics and computer science that allow for capturing (pairwise) distance relationships d ⁡ ( x , y ) d(x,y) between points x , y ∈ X x,y\in X . Because of this, it is natural to ask what useful generalizations there are of metric spaces for capturing “ k k -wise distance relationships” d ⁡ ( x 1 , … , x k ) d(x_{1},\ldots,x_{k}) among points x 1 , … , x k ∈ X x_{1},\ldots,x_{k}\in X for k > 2 k>2 . To that end, Gähler (Math. Nachr., 1963) (and perhaps others even earlier) defined k k -metric spaces , which generalize metric spaces, and most notably generalize the triangle inequality d ⁡ ( x 1 , x 2 ) ≤ d ⁡ ( x 1 , y ) + d ⁡ ( y , x 2 ) d(x_{1},x_{2})\leq d(x_{1},y)+d(y,x_{2}) to the “simplex inequality” d ⁡ ( x 1 , … , x k ) ≤ ∑ i = 1 k d ⁡ ( x 1 , … , x i − 1 , y , x i + 1 , … , x k ) d(x_{1},\ldots,x_{k})\leq\sum_{i=1}^{k}d(x_{1},\ldots,x_{i-1},y,x_{i+1},\ldots,x_{k}) . (The definition holds for any fixed k ≥ 2 k\geq 2 , and a 2 2 -metric space is just a (standard) metric space.) In this work, we introduce strong k k -metric spaces , k k -metric spaces that satisfy a topological condition stronger than the simplex inequality, which makes them “behave nicely.” We also introduce coboundary k k -metrics , which generalize ℓ p \ell_{p} metrics (and in fact all finite metric spaces induced by norms) and minimum bounding chain k k -metrics , which generalize shortest path metrics (and capture all strong k k -metrics). Using these definitions, we prove analogs of a number of fundamental results about embedding finite metric spaces including Fréchet embedding (isometric embedding into ℓ ∞ \ell_{\infty} ) and isometric embedding of all tree metrics into ℓ 1 \ell_{1} . We also study relationships between families of (strong) k k -metrics, and show that natural quantities, like simplex volume, are strong k k -metrics.

Introduction

Metric spaces
(X,d)
consist of a set of points
X
and a metric function
d
that specifies the pairwise distances of elements in
X
.
Metric spaces capture and abstract most familiar notions of distance, such as the
\ell_{p}
distance between pairs of points in
\mathbb{R}^{m}
and the length of the shortest path between pairs of vertices in a weighted graph. A major line of research has studied
metric embeddings
, which work to relate different families of metrics and classify them according to their “richness.” Formally, it seeks to construct isometric or low-distortion embeddings between different families of metric spaces.
Well-known examples of such results are Fréchet embedding into
\ell_{\infty}

[
Fre06
]
, Bourgain’s theorem for embedding into
\ell_{1}

[
Bou85
]
, the Johnson-Lindenstrauss lemma for dimension reduction in
\ell_{2}

[
JL84
]
, the embeddability of tree metrics into
\ell_{1}

[
LMS98
]
, and Bartal’s theorem for embedding into a distribution of tree metrics
[
Bar96
]
. See Matoušek
[
Mat13
]
for a survey.
Besides being inherently mathematically interesting, these results have many applications in computer science, including most notably in the design of approximation algorithms for problems involving flows and cuts, as well as for problems in network design. See, e.g.,
[
AD91
,
LLR94
,
Bar96
,
LR99
,
ARV09
]
.

Generalizing to larger
k
.

Metrics capture pairwise distance relationships between points
x,y
in a set
X
, but do not (directly) capture relationships among more points.
Moreover, notions of “distance” that capture relationships between
k>2
points have been studied less extensively, and much less is known about their structural properties and possible applications.
In fact, it is not even a priori clear what the right way to generalize metric spaces to
k>2

Potentially the most notable and natural generalization of metric spaces is
k
-metric spaces
, which were apparently introduced by
[

]
.

These

Let

(3)

It is straightforward to check that plugging

in
Item

4

However, the condition in the simplex inequality is not the only natural generalization of the triangle inequality, and is not obviously strong enough to prove good analogs of many of the core embedding results for finite metric spaces mentioned above.

Strong

In this work, we introduce

-metrics

Item

As motivation for the definition of strong

Let

be a finite (
2

is at most the cost of any unit
(s,t)

A

-flow

f:E\to\mathbb{R}

is
1

The

An

(s,t)

Moreover, many metric embedding results crucially rely on this equivalence. However, unfortunately, the analogous equivalence does not hold for

We next define a notion of

1.1.3
The Power of
\ell_{\infty}

It is well-known that any (finite) metric space is an is isometrically embedabble into
\ell_{\infty}
, via a map that is usually known as the Fréchet embedding.
We next describe a generalization of this result that we show for coboundary metrics. Specifically, we show that any strong pseudo
k
-metric belongs to
{\cal C}_{k,\infty}
, and therefore
{\cal C}_{k,\infty}={\cal S}_{k}
(
Theorem

4.9
).

Fréchet’s isometric embedding of finite metric spaces into
\ell_{\infty}
is simple and elegant. We sketch the idea here. Let
(X,d)
be a finite metric space, and fix
x,x^{\prime}\in X
. The main idea is that there is an embedding of
X
into the line (
\mathbb{R}^{1}
) that (1) does not expand any distance and (2) exactly preserves the distance from
x
to
x^{\prime}
. For example, the embedding that maps each
y\in X
to
d(x,y)
has this property. To obtain an isometric embedding of
(X,d)
into
\ell_{\infty}
, one can concatenate the
\binom{n}{2}
line embeddings corresponding to distinct pairs of elements
x,x^{\prime}\in X
.
3
3

3

In fact, it suffices to use
n
line embeddings in the Frćhet embedding as each line embedding preserve all distances to a single point.
But, we find this embedding (which uses
\binom{n}{2}
line embeddings) more conducive to generalization to
k
-metrics.

Note that each distance is never expanded and is preserved at least once, and so this does in fact give an isometric embedding into
\ell_{\infty}
.

We follow the outline of the proof above to show that any strong
k
-metric
(X,d)
is in
{\cal C}_{k,\infty}^{m}
, where
m=\binom{n}{k}
(
Theorem

4.9
).
First, we construct analogs of the line embeddings used above. Namely, for each
k
-tuple
t=(x_{1},\ldots,x_{k})\in X^{k}
we construct a
k
-metric
\phi_{t}
in
{\cal C}_{k,\infty}^{1}
that preserves the value of
d(t)
and does not increase the value of
d(t^{\prime})
for any other such
k
-tuple
t^{\prime}
(
Lemma

4.8
).
Then, we concatenate the
m=\binom{n}{k}
functions
\phi_{t}
to obtain a
k
-metric
\phi
in
{\cal C}_{k,\infty}^{m}
that preserves all
d
values.

While the latter step is straightforward and similar to the case of standard metrics, the former presents a greater challenge as explicit constructions, like the mapping
y\mapsto d(x,y)
used for standard metrics, are not readily available.

Instead, we show how to obtain such a coboundary
k
-metric as the solution of a certain (feasible) linear program. To that end, let
t=(x_{1},\ldots,x_{k})
. We will attempt to find a
k
-metric in
{\cal C}_{k,\infty}^{1}={\cal C}_{k,\left|{\cdot}\right|}^{1}
that preserves the value of
d(t)
and does not expand the
d
value on other simplices.
4
4

4

We note that all
\ell_{p}
norms are equivalent in one dimension. I.e., for scalars
x\in\mathbb{R}
and
p,q\in[1,\infty]
we have that
\lVert x\rVert_{p}=\lVert x\rVert_{q}=\left|{x}\right|
. Because of this,
{\cal C}_{k,\infty}^{1}={\cal C}_{k,\left|{\cdot}\right|}^{1}
.

Equivalently, we look for a
(k-2)
-chain
\boldsymbol{f}
such that
|\delta_{k-2}\cdot\boldsymbol{f}|\leq\boldsymbol{d}
, i.e., such that
|\delta_{k-2}\cdot\boldsymbol{f}|
assigns one non-negative value to each
(k-1)
-simplex
s
that is not larger than
d(s)
, hence not expanding.
(Here
|\cdot|
and
\leq
are treated element-wise.)
On the other hand, we want
\delta_{k-2}\cdot\boldsymbol{f}
at
t
to be as large as possible and ideally equal to
d(t)
.
So, we try to maximize
\boldsymbol{e}_{t}^{T}\cdot\delta_{k-2}\cdot\boldsymbol{f}
by solving the following linear program with variables
\boldsymbol{f}
corresponding to the
\binom{n}{k-1}
many
(k-2)
-simplices.

\displaystyle\max

\displaystyle\quad\boldsymbol{e}_{t}^{T}\cdot\delta_{k-2}\cdot\boldsymbol{f}

s.t.

\displaystyle\penalty\ \penalty\ -\boldsymbol{d}\leq\delta_{k-2}\cdot\boldsymbol{f}\leq\boldsymbol{d}

\displaystyle\penalty\ \quad\partial_{k-2}\cdot\boldsymbol{f}=0\ \text{.}

The equality is added for technical reasons. Namely, it ensures that the feasible region of the linear program is bounded while maintaining the same optimal value. We refer the reader to the proof of
Theorem

4.9
for details.
Because its feasible region is a (bounded) polytope, this linear program has an optimal solution that is a vertex of this polytope.
We show that this optimal solution corresponds to a “non-expanding”
d
, in which
d(t)
is preserved.

Using the fact that
\delta_{k-2}=\partial_{k-1}^{T}
, we rewrite the objective function of the linear program as
(\partial_{k-1}\cdot\boldsymbol{e}_{t})^{T}\cdot\boldsymbol{f}
, and the first set of constraints as
-d(\tau)\leq(\partial_{\tau}\cdot\boldsymbol{e}_{\tau})^{T}\cdot\boldsymbol{f}\leq d(\tau)
for all
(k-1)
-simplices
\tau
.
We let
\{t_{1},\ldots,t_{r}\}
be the simplices whose inequality constraints are tight at the optimal solution
\boldsymbol{f}^{*}
, and we assume (without loss of generality) that
(\partial_{k-1}\cdot\boldsymbol{e}_{t_{i}})^{T}\cdot\boldsymbol{f}=d(t_{i})
for
i\in[r]
. Since
\boldsymbol{f}^{*}
is an (optimal) solution to the linear program, the coefficient vector
\partial_{k-1}\cdot\boldsymbol{e}_{t}
in the linear program must be in the cone of vectors of the tight constraint. That is, there are non-negative
\beta_{1},\ldots,\beta_{r}
such that,

\partial_{k-1}\cdot\boldsymbol{e}_{t}=\sum_{i=1}^{r}{\beta_{i}\cdot(\partial_{k-1}\cdot\boldsymbol{e}_{t_{i}})}=\partial_{k-1}\cdot\left(\sum_{i=1}^{r}{\beta_{i}\boldsymbol{e}_{t_{i}}}\right)\ \text{.}

(1)

From that, we show

\displaystyle\sum_{i=1}^{r}{\beta_{i}\cdot d(t_{i})}

\displaystyle\geq d(t)\geq(\partial_{k-1}\cdot\boldsymbol{e}_{t})^{T}\boldsymbol{f}^{*}=\sum_{i=1}^{r}{\beta_{i}\cdot d(t_{i})}.

The first inequality uses the strong simplex inequality (the condition holds by
Equation

1
), and the second holds since the coboundary metric induced by
\boldsymbol{f}^{*}
is non-expanding. Therefore, we have that

d(t)=(\partial_{k-1}\cdot\boldsymbol{e}_{t})^{T}\boldsymbol{f}^{*}
, as desired.
Again, we refer the reader to
Lemma

4.8
for details.
We also remark that we crucially used the fact that
(X,d)
was a
strong
(pseudo)
k
-metric space, which we believe is good motivation for our definition of strong
k
-metrics.

1.1.4
Other
\ell_{p}
Metrics Are Not as Powerful

Other
\ell_{p}

\ell_{\infty}
. Indeed, for any

that is not an

(

Apex extension.

In order to prove this, we use a simple but powerful technique called

for constructing a
(k+1)
-metric space from a
k
-metric space in such a way that the new space shares certain properties of the original space.
Somewhat more specifically, given a

-metric

and a function

such that
(X,d)
is in
{\cal C}_{k,p}
if and only if
(X^{\prime},d^{\prime})
is in
{\cal C}_{k+1,p}
. We can then combine this technique with examples of non-
\ell_{p}
-metric spaces (i.e., examples of finite metric spaces not isometrically embeddable into
\ell_{p}
) to construct strong
k
-metric spaces that do not belong to
{\cal C}_{k,p}
for all

.

Let
(X,d)
be a
k

.
Now, let

be a function on

(i)

.

(ii)

We call

the

of

. In addition to the fact that

), we show that

Since

1.1.5
Other Generalizations of Embedding Results

We also generalize several other well-known results from the study of metric embeddings.
First, we give a “meta” result for converting linear norm embedding results to results for coboundary metrics (
Proposition

4.12
). From this, we get two corollaries: (1) a generalization of the Johnson-Lindenstrauss lemma
[
JL84
]
for dimension reduction in
\ell_{2}
and (2) a generalization of embedding
\ell_{2}
into

[
Dvo61

FLM77

Specifically, we show any

k

4.16

Second, we show the generalization of the fact that all tree metrics are
\ell_{1}
-metrics (see, e.g.,
[
Mat13
, Chapter 1, Exercise 4]
).
We start by defining a higher-order analog of tree metrics called
hypertree

-metrics

(

4.17

Hypertree

-metrics where the underlying complex

is a
(k-1)
-hypertree, that is,

does not have any
(k-1)
-cycles and all of its

-cycles are boundaries of
(k-1)
-chains.
5
5

5

A

-cycle is a non-zero
(k-1)
-chain with no boundary. In particular,
1
-cycles are circulations in a graph, i.e., flows that have net value zero on every vertex.

In particular,
1
-trees are “standard” trees from graph theory, i.e., they are acyclic and connected graphs.

1.1.6
Volume

-metrics

We conclude by studying what is likely the most natural generalization of Euclidean distance to
k

(k-1)

More formally, we study the spaces
(X,d)

k

As volume

A
3
-metric in
{\cal C}_{3,\left|{\cdot}\right|}^{1}
that is not a low-dimensional volume metric.

We now summarize the proof of the third result described above.
Let
(X,d)
be the
n

3
-metric that is one on all triples of distinct points in
X
, i.e., the “discrete
3
-metric” on
X
.
As evidence that coboundary metrics are richer than volume metrics, we show that
(X,d)\in{\cal C}_{3,2}^{1}
and yet
(X,d)
requires
\Omega(\log n)
dimensions to be realized as a volume
3
-metric. (In fact, we can show the stronger statement that any embedding of
(X,d)
into the volume metric with
m\in o(\log n)
dimensions requires super-constant distortion, although we focus on the simpler result.)

To see the former, let
K
be a complete
2
-simplicial complex and orient edges so that there is no directed cycle in its
1
-skeleton (its
1
-skeleton is a tournament). Now, consider the
1
-chain
f
that assigns
1
to all these oriented edges. Since the tournament has no directed cycle,
\delta_{1}f
is either
1
or
-1
on all triangles, and therefore
|\delta_{1}\cdot\boldsymbol{f}|
is a
3
-metric that is one on all distinct triples of points in
X
.

Now, let
g:X\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^{m}
be any isometric embedding of
(X,d)
into a
\mathcal{V}_{k}^{m}
space.
Let
x,x^{\prime}\in X
be such that
g(x),g(x^{\prime})
is a furthest pair of points in

(i.e., such that
\lVert g(x)-g(x^{\prime})\rVert_{2}
is maximized), and let
\ell=\lVert g(x)-g(x^{\prime})\rVert_{2}
.
Now, consider any third point
y\in X\setminus\{{x,x^{\prime}}\}
. Since the area of the triangle
(g(x),g(x^{\prime}),g(y))
is one,
g(y)
is on on the surface of the cylinder with axis
(g(x),g(x^{\prime}))
that has radius

. Thus, all the points are on the surface of this cylinder.
We apply the linear transformation that shrinks by a factor of
\ell
in the direction of
g(x)-g(x^{\prime})
and scales by a factor of
\ell
in every orthogonal direction.
If we assume without loss of generality that
g(x)=\boldsymbol{0}
and
g(x^{\prime})=\ell\cdot\boldsymbol{e}_{1}
, then this linear transformation is expressed by the diagonal matrix
\diag(1/\ell,\ell,\ell,\cdots,\ell)
.
After the transformation, all the points are on the surface of a cylinder of constant height and constant radius. So, they are within a ball of constant radius. Further, our transformation has the property that it does not shrink the area of any triangle.

So, we obtain
n
points in a ball of constant radius such that the area of the triangle of each triple of them is
at least
one.
These two conditions imply a constant lower bound on the distance between every pair of points. Since everything is in a ball of constant radius, a simple packing argument implies that the dimension of the space is
m\geq\Omega(\log n)
.

Related Work

As we have already discussed, prior work on
k
-metrics is closely related to this work. See, e.g.,
[
Gä63
,
Gä64
,
DR00
,
DD09
,
MS06
]
. We also again note that according to
[
DR00
]
, around 1990 Gähler collected a bibliography of over a hundred works related to
k
-metrics, and it seems that a substantial amount of additional work has been done since then (e.g.,
[
MS06
]
). We do not attempt to summarize this extensive body of work, but note that, to the best of our knowledge, none of it has defined or studied our topological (i.e., strong)
k
-metrics.

Past that, the large body of work on (finite) metric spaces and (algorithmic applications of) metric embeddings is related to this paper. We refer the interested reader to the books and surveys
[
Mat13
,
IMS17
,
Lin03
]
for summaries of this body of work. Indeed, we have provided higher-order analogs of most of the standard concepts in metric spaces, and it will be interesting to see which results on metric embeddings can be adapted to these higher-order analogs and what algorithmic applications these results have.

Additionally, we note two other notions of “higher-order metrics” besides
k
-metrics.
First, we note the work of Feige
[
Fei98
]
, which defines a notion of “volume” for finite metric spaces in terms of embeddings into Euclidean space and studies volume-preserving embeddings.
More specifically, Feige defines the volume of
k
points in a finite metric space
(X,d)
to be the maximum volume of the convex hull of their images for any non-expanding embedding
f
into Euclidean space (i.e., embedding
f
such that
\lVert f(x)-f(y)\rVert\leq d(x,y)
for all
x,y\in X
). Then, he uses embeddings that (nearly) preserve this notion of volume to design an approximation algorithm for the minimum bandwidth problem. While our work is not directly related to this type of (nearly) volume-preserving embedding, we pose as an open question whether Feige’s notion of volume is a (strong)
k
-metric.

Second, we note the work of Bryant and Tupper
[
BT12
,
BT14
]
on
diversities
. Diversities are spaces
(X,d)
where
d
is a function from (finite) subsets
S\subseteq X
to the non-negative reals that satisfies the “triangle inequality” condition
d(A\cup C)\leq d(A\cup B)+d(B\cup C)
for all finite subsets
A,B,C\subseteq X
with
B
non-empty.
They note that the restriction of
d
to subsets
S
of size
2
induces a “standard” metric, and so one can view diversities as a different generalization of metrics from (strong)
k
-metrics.
Indeed, we note that diversities are substantially different from (strong)
k
-metrics. In particular, the
d
in diversities is defined on sets of elements of different sizes, and the “triangle inequality” in diversities upper bounds a given evaluation of
d
as the sum of two other evaluations of
d
(as with the “standard” triangle inequality) as opposed to
k
other evaluations of
d
in the (weak) simplex inequality.
A primary goal of Bryant and Tupper’s work on diversities is to extend graph algorithms based on metric embeddings to hypergraph algorithms based on diversity embeddings.
Their definition and techniques indeed seem well-suited to hypergraph problems, however, our definition is better suited to problems in computational topology (i.e., problems on simplicial complexes) like the topological sparsest cut problem and the minimum bounding chain problem.

2.1
Algebraic Topology

A
simplicial complex

K
is a set of finite subsets of a set
X=\{{x_{1},\dots,x_{n}}\}
, with the property that if a subset of
X
is in
K
then all its subsets are in
K
as well. I.e.,
K
is a downward-closed set system on
X
. We call an element of
K
a
simplex
and an element of
K
with cardinality
k+1
a
k
-simplex
. We call
0
-,
1
-, and
2
-simplices vertices, edges and triangles, respectively. We use
K_{k}
to denote the set of all
k
simplices of
K
, and
n_{k}\leq\binom{n}{k+1}
to denote the cardinality of
K_{k}
. We call
K
a
k
-simplicial complex if it has at least one
k
-simplex and no
k^{\prime}
-simplex for any
k^{\prime}>k
. The
k
-complete
simplicial complex on set
X
is composed of all subsets of
X
of cardinality at most
k
.
The
complete

(k-1)
-simplicial complex on
X
is the simplicial complex that contains all
(k-1)
-simplices and no
k
-simplex. We use
K_{k}
to denote the set of all
k
-simplices in a simplicial complex
K
. Further, we denote
n_{k}=|K_{k}|
, in particular,
n_{0}
is the number of vertices of a complex.

Fix a (global) ordering of
X=(x_{1},x_{2},\ldots,x_{n})
of the vertex set
X
of the complex
K
.
An
oriented
(k-1)
-simplex
of the complex
K
is given by an (ordered) sequence
(x_{i_{1}},x_{i_{2}},\ldots,x_{i_{k}})
of its vertices.
Two orientations of a simplex are
equivalent
if their sequences have the same permutation parity, i.e., if one can be transformed into the other with an even number of transpositions. It follows that each
(k-1)
-simplex for
k\geq 1
has exactly two orientations. The
standard orientation
of a simplex is the one whose vertices are ordered according to the global ordering of
X
. I.e, the standard orientation of the
k
-simplex
\{{x_{i_{1}},\ldots,x_{i_{k+1}}}\}\subseteq X
with
i_{1}<\cdots<i_{k+1}
is
(x_{i_{1}},\ldots,x_{i_{k+1}})
.
The sign of any oriented simplex
(x_{i_{1}},\ldots,x_{i_{k+1}})
is the sign of the permutation of its indices
i_{1},\ldots,i_{k+1}
. In particular, the sign of any standard oriented simplex ix
1
.

Let
\widehat{K}_{k}
be the set of all oriented simplices of
K
.
A
k
-chain
is a function
\alpha:\widehat{K}_{k}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
that assigns real values to oriented
k
-simplices of
K
such that (i) if
t^{\prime}
and
t^{\prime\prime}
are equivalent orientations of the same simplex
t
then
\alpha(t^{\prime})=\alpha(t^{\prime\prime})
, and (ii) if
t^{\prime}
and
t^{\prime\prime}
are non-equivalent orientations of the same simplex
t
then
\alpha(t)=-\alpha(t^{\prime})
. By (i) and (ii), it suffices to specify values of
\alpha
on the standard orientation of every simplex, or, equivalently, just non-oriented simplices. Hence with the abuse of notation, we write
\alpha:K_{k}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
, as a function on the non-oriented
k
-simplices.
We use
C_{k}(K)
to denote the set of all
k
-chains over the complex
K
.
We can also view
\alpha
as a vector
\boldsymbol{\alpha}:=(\alpha(\sigma))_{\sigma\in K_{k}}
over the set of (standard oriented)
k
-simplices. In particular, we use
\boldsymbol{e}_{t}
to denote the
k
-chain that is one on the simplex
t
and zero everywhere else.
These vectors
\boldsymbol{e}_{t}
form a basis of the
n_{k}
-dimensional vector space spanned by the vectors
\boldsymbol{\alpha}
for
\boldsymbol{\alpha}\in C_{k}(K)
.
We use the notation
\boldsymbol{e}_{t}^{T}\cdot\boldsymbol{\alpha}
and
\boldsymbol{\alpha}[t]
interchangeably to refer to the value that the chain
\boldsymbol{\alpha}
assigns to
t
. Slightly abusing notation, we use this convention even if
t
is not a simplex with standard orientation.

The
k
-boundary
operator, denoted
\partial_{k}:C_{k}(K)\rightarrow C_{k-1}(K)
, is a map from
k
-chains to
(k-1)
-chains. Since
\partial_{k}
is linear, it suffices to define its action on every
k
-simplex
t=(x_{1},x_{2},\ldots x_{k})
.

\partial_{k}(\boldsymbol{e}_{t})=\partial_{k}(\boldsymbol{e}_{x_{1},x_{2},\ldots x_{k}})=\sum_{i=1}^{k}{(-1)^{i+1}\cdot\boldsymbol{e}_{x_{1},x_{2},\ldots x_{i-1},x_{i+1},\ldots x_{k}}}.

The
k
-boundary operator
\partial_{k}
is a linear operator that can be presented by a
n_{k-1}\times n_{k}
matrix; we slightly overload notation and use
\partial_{k}
to refer to this matrix as well. The transpose of this matrix is called the
(k-1)
-coboundary
operator, denoted
\delta_{k-1}=\partial_{k}^{T}
. We will use the coboundary operator to define coboundary
k
-metrics.

Consider
\partial_{k}:C_{k}(K)\rightarrow C_{k-1}(K)
,
\delta_{k}:C_{k}(K)\rightarrow C_{k+1}(K)
, and their transposes
\delta_{k-1}
and
\partial_{k+1}
, respectively.
The vector space
C_{k}(K)
has the following four natural subspaces: (1)
\text{im}(\partial_{k+1})
, the
boundary space
, (2)
\ker(\partial_{k})
, the
cycle space
, (3)
\text{im}(\delta_{k-1})
, the
coboundary space
, and (4)
\ker(\delta_{k})
, the
cocycle
space.
An important property of the boundary and coboundary operators is that
\partial_{k-1}\partial_{k}=0
, and similarly
\delta_{k}\delta_{k-1}=0
. It follows that the boundary space is a subspace of the cycle space, and the coboundary space is a subspace of the cocycle space. Further, since
\partial_{k}=\delta_{k-1}^{T}
,
C_{k}(K)
orthogonally decomposes to the cycle space and the coboundary space. Similarly, since
\delta_{k}=\partial_{k+1}^{T}
,
C_{k}(K)
orthogonally decomposes into the cocycle space and the boundary space. We use
\partial_{k}[K]
and
\delta_{k}[K]
to emphasize that the operators are with respect to the complex
K
, when the complex is not clear from the context.

For a
k
-chain
\alpha
and a
(k-1)
-chain
\beta
in a complex
K
such that
\partial_{k}\boldsymbol{\alpha}=\boldsymbol{\beta}
, we say that
\alpha
is a
bounding chain
of
\beta
. If
\boldsymbol{w}
is a vector of non-negative weights indexed by the
(k-1)
-simplices
t
of
K
, the
cost
of
\alpha
with respect to
\boldsymbol{w}
is
\left|{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}\right|\cdot\boldsymbol{w}=\sum_{t\in K_{k-1}}\left|{\alpha[t]}\right|\cdot w(t)
.
A
minimum bounding chain
of
\beta
is a bounding chain of
\beta
with minimum cost.

We also use special terminology for graphs that are
1
-simplicial complexes. A
1
-chain is equivalent to a
flow
. Let
s
and
t
be vertices of a graph, and
f
a
1
-chain (or flow) such that
\partial_{1}f=c(\boldsymbol{e}_{t}-\boldsymbol{e}_{s})
for a non-negative constant
c
. We call such an
f
an
(s,t)
-flow
with
value

c
. We refer to it as a
unit
(s,t)
-flow
when
c=1
. A
1
-cycle as defined above coincides with the notion of a closed walk in graphs.
We specifically use
simple cycle
to refer to closed walks with no repeated vertices in a graph.

2.2
Metric Geometry

Definition 2.1

(
k
-metric spaces)
.

Let
X
be a finite set, and let
d:X^{k}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
.
We call
(X,d)
a
k
-metric space
, and
d
a
k
-metric function
if for any
x_{1},\dots,x_{k}\in X
:

(1)

d(x_{1},\dots,x_{k})\geq 0
.

(2)

d(x_{1},\dots,x_{k})=0
if and only if the values
x_{1},\dots,x_{k}
are not all distinct.

(3)

d(x_{1},\dots,x_{k})=d(x_{\pi(1)},\dots,x_{\pi(k)})
for any permutation
\pi:[k]\to[k]
.

(4)

d(x_{1},\dots,x_{k})\leq\sum_{i=1}^{k}{d(x_{1},\ldots,x_{i-1},y,x_{i+1},\dots,x_{k})}
for any
y\in X
.

A
k
-metric
(X,d)
is a
pseudo

k
-metric if
Item

2
is replaced with the weaker property
d(x_{1},\dots,x_{k})=0

if

x_{i}=x_{j}
for any
i\neq j
, and that it is a
meta

k
-metric if
Item

2
is replaced with the weaker property
d(x_{1},\dots,x_{k})=0

only if

x_{i}=x_{j}
for any
i\neq j
.

We call
Item

4
the
(weak) simplex inequality
to differentiate it from the strong simplex inequality that takes its place in strong
k
-metrics.
For the same reason, sometime, we refer to
k
-metric spaces as
weak

k
-metric spaces.
The
2
-metric spaces are
standard metric spaces
that have been the main object of study in metric geometry.

Let
(X,d)
and
(X^{\prime},d^{\prime})
be finite (pseudo)
k
-metric spaces, and let
f:X\rightarrow X^{\prime}
.
We say that
f
is an
isometry
if for all
(x_{1},\ldots,x_{k})\in X^{k}
,
d(x_{1},\ldots,x_{k})=d^{\prime}(f(x_{1}),\ldots,f(x_{k}))
.

A
norm
on
\mathbb{R}^{m}
is a function
\lVert\cdot\rVert:\mathbb{R}^{m}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
that satisfies the following properties for all
\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{y}\in\mathbb{R}^{m}
and all constants
c>0
:

(1)

\|\boldsymbol{x}\|\geq 0
, with equality if and only if
\boldsymbol{x}=\boldsymbol{0}
.

(2)

\|c\boldsymbol{x}\|=|c|\cdot\|\boldsymbol{x}\|
, where
|\cdot|
is the absolute value.

(3)

\|\boldsymbol{x}\|+\|\boldsymbol{y}\|\geq\|\boldsymbol{x}+\boldsymbol{y}\|
.

Let
X
be a finite subset of
\mathbb{R}^{m}
and
\lVert\cdot\rVert
any norm on
\mathbb{R}^{m}
.
The norm induces a metric
d_{\lVert\cdot\rVert}
on all points of
\mathbb{R}^{m}
, and in particular all points of
X
, where for any
x,x^{\prime}\in\mathbb{R}^{m}
,
d_{\lVert\cdot\rVert}(x,x^{\prime})=\lVert x-x^{\prime}\rVert
. In this case,
(\mathbb{R}^{m},d_{\lVert\cdot\rVert})
is a metrics space and
(X,d_{\lVert\cdot\rVert})
is a finite metric space.

For any real
p\geq 1
, the
\ell_{p}
norm
of
\boldsymbol{x}\in\mathbb{R}^{m}
is defined as

\lVert\boldsymbol{x}\rVert_{p}:=\Big(\sum_{i=1}^{m}{\left|{x_{i}}\right|^{p}\Big)^{1/p}}\ \text{,}

and the
{\ell_{\infty}}
norm
of
\boldsymbol{x}
is defined as
\lVert\boldsymbol{x}\rVert_{\infty}:=\max_{i}(\left|{x_{i}}\right|)
.
Let
d_{p}=d_{\lVert\cdot\rVert_{p}}
be the distance function implied by the
p
-norm.
An
\ell_{p}^{m}
metric is a finite metric space that admits an isometric embedding into
(\mathbb{R}^{m},\lVert\cdot\rVert_{p})
and an
\ell_{p}
metric
is a finite metric space that admits an isometric map into
(\mathbb{R}^{m},\lVert\cdot\rVert_{p})
for some
m\in\mathbb{Z}^{+}
. Euclidean metrics are
\ell_{p}
metrics with
p=2
.

We start by calling recalling the definition of a strong

-metric space.

Definition 3.1

(Strong
k
-metric spaces)
.

Let
X
be a finite set, and let
d:X^{k}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
.
We call
(X,d)
a
strong
k
-metric space
, and
d
a
strong
k
-metric function
if for any
x_{1},\dots,x_{k}\in X
:

(1)

d(x_{1},\dots,x_{k})\geq 0
.

(2)

d(x_{1},\dots,x_{k})=0
if and only if the values
x_{1},\dots,x_{k}
are not all distinct.

(3)

d(x_{1},\dots,x_{k})=d(x_{\pi(1)},\dots,x_{\pi(k)})
, for any permutation
\pi:[k]\to[k]
.

(4)

Let
K
be the complete

(k-1)
-dimensional simplicial complex on the vertex set
X
. Let
t
be the (oriented)
(k-1)
-simplex in
K
with vertices
x_{1},\ldots,x_{k}
, and let
\alpha\in C_{k-1}(K)
be such that
\partial\cdot\alpha=\partial\cdot\boldsymbol{e}_{t}
(i.e., the boundary of the
(k-1)
-chain
\alpha
is the same as the boundary of
t
).
Then

d(x_{1},\ldots,x_{k})=d(t)\leq|\alpha|\cdot\boldsymbol{d}=\sum_{\tau\in K_{k}}|\alpha[\tau]|\cdot d(\tau)\ \text{,}

where

|\alpha|=(|\alpha[\tau]|)_{\tau\in K_{k}}
and

\boldsymbol{d}=(d(\tau))_{\tau\in K_{k}}
.

3.1
Relating Weak and Strong
k
-metrics

First, we show that strong
k
-metrics are a subset of weak
k
-metrics, as suggested by their name. This inclusion follows from the following lemma.

Lemma 3.2
.

Consider a
k
-simplex with vertex set
\{x_{1},\ldots,x_{k},y\}
. Then

\partial_{k-1}\cdot\boldsymbol{e}_{x_{1},\ldots,x_{k}}=\partial_{k-1}\cdot\left(\sum_{i=1}^{k}{\boldsymbol{e}_{x_{1},\ldots,x_{i-1},y,x_{i+1},\ldots,x_{k}}}\right)\ \text{.}

Proof.

We have

\displaystyle\partial_{k}(\boldsymbol{e}_{x_{1},\ldots,x_{k},y})

\displaystyle=\sum_{i=1}^{k}{(-1)^{i-1}\cdot\boldsymbol{e}_{x_{1},\ldots,x_{i-1},x_{i+1},\ldots,x_{k},y}}+(-1)^{k}\cdot\boldsymbol{e}_{x_{1},\ldots,x_{k}}

\displaystyle=(-1)^{k-1}\cdot\sum_{i=1}^{k}{\boldsymbol{e}_{x_{1},\ldots,x_{i-1},y,x_{i+1},\ldots,x_{k}}}+(-1)^{k}\cdot\boldsymbol{e}_{x_{1},\ldots,x_{k}}

\displaystyle=(-1)^{k}\cdot\left(\boldsymbol{e}_{x_{1},\ldots,x_{k}}-\sum_{i=1}^{k}{\boldsymbol{e}_{x_{1},\ldots,x_{i-1},y,x_{i+1},\ldots,x_{k}}}\right)\ \text{.}

Therefore,
\boldsymbol{e}_{x_{1},\ldots,x_{k}}-\sum_{i=1}^{k}{\boldsymbol{e}_{x_{1},\ldots,x_{i-1},y,x_{i+1},\ldots,x_{k}}}
is in the image of
\partial_{k}
, and hence is in the kernel of
\partial_{k-1}
. The lemma follows by using the fact that
\partial_{k-1}
is a linear operator and rearranging.
∎

Corollary 3.3
.

For every integer
k\geq 2
, any strong pseudo
k
-metric is a weak pseudo
k
-metric, i.e.,

{\cal S}_{k}\subseteq{\cal W}_{k}
.

Proof.

Since all other properties are identical, it suffices to show that for a strong
k
-metric space
(X,d)
, if
d
satisfies the strong simplex inequality then it also satisfies the weak simplex inequality.
To that end, let
x_{1},\ldots,x_{n},y\in X
, and let

\boldsymbol{\alpha}=\sum_{i=1}^{k}{\boldsymbol{e}_{x_{1},\ldots,x_{i-1},y,x_{i+1},\ldots,x_{k}}}\ \text{.}

By
Lemma

3.2
,
\partial_{k-1}\cdot\boldsymbol{\alpha}=\partial_{k-1}\cdot\boldsymbol{e}_{x_{1},\ldots,x_{k}}
.
Thus, by the strong simplex inequality,

d(x_{1},\ldots,x_{k})\leq\sum_{\tau\in K_{k}}|\boldsymbol{\alpha}[\tau]|\cdot d(\tau)=\sum_{i=1}^{k}{d(x_{1},\ldots,x_{i-1},y,x_{i+1},\ldots,x_{k})}\ \text{,}

as needed.
∎

Next, we show a weak
3
-metric that is not a strong
3
-metric. Hence, strong
3
-metric is strictly stronger than weak
3
-metrics, unlike strong and weak
2
-metrics which are equivalent (
Lemma

3.5
).

Lemma 3.4
.

There exists a weak
3
-metric space that is not a strong
3
-metric space, i.e.,
{\cal W}_{3}\not\subset{\cal S}_{3}
.

Proof.

We will consider a
3
-metric space
(X=\{x_{0},x_{1},x_{2},x_{3},x_{4},x_{5}\},d)
, with
d
defined below corresponding to
Figure

5
.

Figure 5:
A subdivision of the triangle
\{x_{0},x_{1},x_{2}\}
into seven sub-triangles. Let
d:X\times X\times X\to\mathbb{R}^{\geq 0}
be such that
d(t)=1
for each triangle
t
in the subdivision, and
d(t)=10
for each triangle
t
(with distinct vertices) not in the subdivision (including
t=\{x_{0},x_{1},x_{2}\}
). Then
(X=\{x_{0},x_{1},x_{2},x_{3},x_{4},x_{5}\},d)
gives an example of a weak

-metric space.

Let

S:=\{{\{{x_{0},x_{1},x_{4}}\},\{{x_{0},x_{3},x_{4}}\},\{{x_{1},x_{2},x_{5}}\},\{{x_{1},x_{4},x_{5}}\},\{{x_{0},x_{2},x_{3}}\},\{{x_{2},x_{3},x_{5}}\},\{{x_{3},x_{4},x_{5}}\}}\}

denote the set of seven triangles in the subdivision of the triangle
\{{x_{0},x_{1},x_{2}}\}
shown in
Figure

, and define

d(x,y,z):=\begin{cases}0&\text{if $x=y$ or $x=z$ or $y=z$ ,}\\
1&\text{if $\{{x,y,z}\}\in S$ ,}\\
10&\text{otherwise .}\end{cases}

Now, let
\alpha
be a
2
-chain that is
1
on all triangles of
S
with counterclockwise orientation,

\alpha=\boldsymbol{e}_{x_{0}x_{1}x_{4}}+\boldsymbol{e}_{x_{0}x_{4}x_{3}}+\boldsymbol{e}_{x_{1}x_{2}x_{5}}+\boldsymbol{e}_{x_{1}x_{5}x_{4}}+\boldsymbol{e}_{x_{0}x_{3}x_{2}}+\boldsymbol{e}_{x_{2}x_{3}x_{5}}+\boldsymbol{e}_{x_{3}x_{4}x_{5}}.

We have that
\partial\cdot\alpha=\boldsymbol{e}_{x_{0}x_{1}}+\boldsymbol{e}_{x_{1}x_{2}}+\boldsymbol{e}_{x_{2}x_{0}}=\partial\cdot\boldsymbol{e}_{x_{0}x_{1}x_{2}}=\partial t
, but

. So,

is
not
a strong

-metric space, as it violates condition (3) of strong metric spaces.

On the other hand, one can check that
d(x,y,z)\leq d(w,x,y)+d(w,x,z)+d(w,y,z)
for any

, and so

The cases when

.
∎

We remark that the stronger statement that

for

also holds (

4.11
). Proving this stronger statement, however, requires additional tools, and thus we defer it to the next section.

We also remark that, more generally, any triangulation of the triangle
(x_{0},x_{1},x_{2})
that does not contain
K_{4}
as a subgraph can be turned into an example to show that the strong simplex inequality is strictly stronger than the weak one. Consider such a triangulation with
n
triangles. We assign weight one to all the triangles of such a triangulation and weight
n+1
to all other triangles of the complete
2
-simplicial complex. Since the triangulation does not contain
K_{4}
as a subgraph, the induced
2
-simplicial complex of any four vertices has at least two weight-
(n+1)
triangles as faces, and thus satisfies the weak simplex inequality. On the other hand, the chain of all
n
weight-
1
triangles has boundary
(x_{0},x_{1},x_{2})
(since those triangles form a subdivision of
(x_{0},x_{1},x_{2})
) and has weight
n
. This is strictly less than the weight of
(x_{0},x_{1},x_{2})
, which is
n+1
.

Finally, we show that in contrast to
k
-metrics for
k>2
, weak and strong
2
-metrics are equivalent. The proof follows from the folklore flow decomposition theorem.

Lemma 3.5
.

Any weak pseudo
2
-metric is a strong pseudo
2
-metric, i.e.,
{\cal W}_{2}\subseteq{\cal S}_{2}
.

Proof.

The definitions of weak and strong 2-metrics are identical, except for the weak simplex inequality (
Definition

2.1
,
Item

4
) and its strong metric equivalent (
Definition

3.1
,
Item

4
). Thus, it remains to show that the former implies the latter for
2
-metrics.

Let
(X,d)
be a
2
-metric space, and let
G
be the weighted complete graph on vertex set
X
where the weight of edge
(x,y)
is
d(x,y)
. (
G
is the graph representation of
(X,d)
.)
Let
s,t\in X
and let
\boldsymbol{\alpha}
be a
1
-chain on
G
such that
\partial_{1}\boldsymbol{\alpha}=\partial_{1}\boldsymbol{e}_{s,t}
, i.e., such that
\boldsymbol{\alpha}
is a unit
(s,t)
-flow.
We show that
|\alpha|\cdot\boldsymbol{d}=\sum_{\tau\in E}|\alpha[\tau]|\cdot d(\tau)\geq d(s,t)
, and therefore the strong simplex inequality holds.

By the flow decomposition theorem (see, e.g.,
[
Ahu93
, Theorem 3.5]
) we can decompose
\boldsymbol{\alpha}
into a set of cyclic flows
{\cal C}
and a set of flows
{\cal P}
where
P\in\mathcal{P}
has edges
E_{p}
and value
0<f_{P}\leq 1
.
So, we get that

\displaystyle|\alpha|\cdot d=\sum_{\tau\in E}|\alpha[\tau]|\cdot d(\tau)

\displaystyle=\sum_{C\in{\cal C}}\sum_{\tau\in E}|C[\tau]|\cdot d(\tau)+\sum_{P\in{\cal P}}\sum_{\tau\in E}P[\tau]\cdot d(\tau)

\displaystyle\geq\sum_{P\in{\cal P}}\sum_{\tau\in E}P[\tau]\cdot d(\tau)

\displaystyle=\sum_{P\in{\cal P}}\sum_{\tau\in E_{P}}f_{P}\cdot d(\tau)

\displaystyle=\Big(\sum_{\tau\in E_{P}}d(\tau)\Big)\cdot\sum_{P\in{\cal P}}f_{P}

\displaystyle\geq d(s,t)\sum_{P\in{\cal P}}f_{P}

\displaystyle=d(s,t)

.

The second inequality holds because the edges
\tau\in E_{P}
form an
(s,t)
-path, which one can show has weight at least
d(s,t)
by an inductive application of the triangle inequality.
The last equality holds because
\sum_{P\in{\cal P}}f_{P}
is equal to the flow value of
\boldsymbol{\alpha}
(from
s
to
t
), which is
1
.
∎

We end this section by summarizing the inclusion and exclusion result in the following corollaries. The first corollary follows from
Corollary

3.3
and
Lemma

3.5
.

Corollary 3.6
.

Any weak pseudo
2
-metric is a strong pseudo
2
-metric and vice versa, i.e.,
{\cal W}_{2}={\cal S}_{2}
.

3.2
Verifying Strong
k
-Metrics in Polynomial Time

Probably the most basic algorithmic question about finite
k
-metric spaces is whether we can verify them efficiently. More specifically, we would like to solve the following problem(s) for fixed
k\geq 2
.
Given a finite set
X
and a function
d:X^{k}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
given by its evaluation table as input, decide whether
(X,d)
is a weak or strong
k
-metric space.

Deciding whether
(X,d)
is a weak
k
-metric space in
O(n^{k+1})
time is straightforward, as each condition can be checked in time only depending on
k
for each of the
\binom{n}{k+1}
sets of values
\{{x_{1},\ldots,x_{k},y}\}\subseteq X
, and
k
is a constant.
Deciding whether
(X,d)
is a strong
k
-metric is more challenging because of the strong simplex inequality. In particular, it is not clear that we can enumerate all chains
\boldsymbol{\alpha}
with the required properties.
So, instead of enumerating all such chains
\alpha
, for every
(k-1)
-simplex
t=(x_{1},\ldots,x_{k})
we compute the minimum cost of a
(k-1)
-chain that has the same boundary as
t
and compare its cost with
d(t)
.
To do this, we use known algorithms for computing minimum bounding chains over the reals
[
DHK10
,
TJ10
]
using linear programming.
6
6

6

Computing minimum bounding chains over the integers or finite fields is known to be
\mathsf{NP}
-hard. See, e.g.,
[
BMN20
]
.

More specifically, we use these algorithms to compute minimum bounding chains for the boundary of every
(k-1)
-simplex
t=(x_{1},\ldots,x_{k})\in K_{k-1}
.
We present a detailed proof for the sake of completeness, but again note that the linear programs in the proof are already described in previous literature
[
DHK10
,
TJ10
]
.

Lemma 3.7
.

Let
(X,d)
,
d:X^{k}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
, be a finite
k
-metric space for constant
k
, with
n=|X|
. We can check whether
d
is a strong
k
-metric in polynomial time (by solving
O(n^{k})
linear programs, each with
n^{O(k)}
variables and constraints).

Proof.

It suffices to check, for every
(k-1)
-simplex
t
, that the optimal value of the following optimization problem is at least
d(t)
(in fact, the optimal value would be equal to
d(t)
, realized by
\boldsymbol{\alpha}=\boldsymbol{e}_{t}
among other possible optimum solutions).

\min_{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}{\sum_{\tau\in X^{k}}{|\boldsymbol{\alpha}[\tau]|\cdot d(\tau)}}\quad\text{s.t.}\quad\partial_{k-1}\cdot\boldsymbol{\alpha}=\partial_{k-1}\cdot\boldsymbol{e}_{t}.

(2)

We reduce this problem to a linear programming.
First, we modify
Equation

2
by introducing
(k-1)
-chains
\alpha^{+}
and
\alpha^{-}
and constraints

\boldsymbol{\alpha}[\tau]=\boldsymbol{\alpha}^{+}[\tau]-\boldsymbol{\alpha}^{-}[\tau]\quad\text{and}\quad\boldsymbol{\alpha}^{+}[\tau],\boldsymbol{\alpha}^{-}[\tau]\geq 0,

for every
(k-1)
-simplex
\tau
. We obtain the following modified optimization problem.

\min_{\boldsymbol{\alpha}^{+},\boldsymbol{\alpha}^{-}}{\sum_{\tau\in X^{k}}{|\boldsymbol{\alpha}^{+}[\tau]-\boldsymbol{\alpha}^{-}[\tau]|\cdot d(\tau)}}\quad\text{s.t.}\quad\partial_{k-1}\cdot(\boldsymbol{\alpha}^{+}-\boldsymbol{\alpha}^{-})=\partial_{k-1}\cdot\boldsymbol{e}_{t},\quad\text{and}\quad\boldsymbol{\alpha}^{+},\boldsymbol{\alpha}^{-}\geq\boldsymbol{0},

(3)

where by
\boldsymbol{\alpha}^{+},\boldsymbol{\alpha}^{-}\geq\boldsymbol{0}
, we mean that they are vectors with non-negative entries.

For any feasible solution
\boldsymbol{\alpha}^{+},\boldsymbol{\alpha}^{-}
of
Equation

3
there is a feasible solution
\boldsymbol{\alpha}=\boldsymbol{\alpha}^{+}-\boldsymbol{\alpha}^{-}
in
Equation

2
with the same optimization value. On the other hand, for every feasible solution
\boldsymbol{\alpha}
of
Equation

2
, consider the solution
\boldsymbol{\alpha}^{+},\boldsymbol{\alpha}^{-}
in
Equation

3
, where for any
\tau\in X^{k}
, if
\boldsymbol{\alpha}[\tau]\geq 0
then
\boldsymbol{\alpha}^{+}[\tau]=\boldsymbol{\alpha}[\tau]
and
\boldsymbol{\alpha}^{-}[\tau]=0
, and otherwise
\boldsymbol{\alpha}^{+}[\tau]=0
and
\boldsymbol{\alpha}^{-}[\tau]=-\boldsymbol{\alpha}[\tau]
. The optimization value of
\boldsymbol{\alpha}^{+},\boldsymbol{\alpha}^{-}
in
Equation

3
is the same as the optimization value of
\boldsymbol{\alpha}
in
Equation

2
. Hence,
Equation

2
and
Equation

3
have the same optimal value.

Next, we show that the following LP has the same optimal value as
Equation

3
.

\min_{\boldsymbol{\alpha}^{+},\boldsymbol{\alpha}^{-}}{\sum_{\tau\in X^{k}}{(\boldsymbol{\alpha}^{+}[\tau]+\boldsymbol{\alpha}^{-}[\tau])\cdot d(\tau)}}\quad\text{s.t.}\quad\partial(\boldsymbol{\alpha}^{+}-\boldsymbol{\alpha}^{-})=\partial t,\quad\text{and}\quad\boldsymbol{\alpha}^{+},\boldsymbol{\alpha}^{-}\geq 0.

(4)

To that end, we observe that in both
Equation

3
and
Equation

4
there are optimal solutions for both with the property that
for any
\tau\in X^{k}
, we have
\boldsymbol{\alpha}^{+}[\tau]=0
or
\boldsymbol{\alpha}^{-}[\tau]=0
.
Let
\boldsymbol{\alpha}^{+},\boldsymbol{\alpha}^{-}
be any feasible solution., and let
\tau
be any
(k-1)
-simplex.
Let
h=\min(\boldsymbol{\alpha}^{-}[\tau],\boldsymbol{\alpha}^{+}[\tau])
.
Replacing
\boldsymbol{\alpha}^{-}[\tau],\boldsymbol{\alpha}^{+}[\tau]
with
\boldsymbol{\alpha}^{-}[\tau]-h,\boldsymbol{\alpha}^{+}[\tau]-h
does not violate the constraints of the optimization problems, and it can only reduce their objective functions as
d(\tau)\geq 0
. So, it is safe to assume that for any
\tau\in X^{k}
,
\boldsymbol{\alpha}[\tau]^{+}=0
or
\boldsymbol{\alpha}[\tau]^{-}=0
. This implies that if
\boldsymbol{\alpha}^{-}[\tau]=0
,

|\boldsymbol{\alpha}^{+}[\tau]-\boldsymbol{\alpha}^{-}[\tau]|=|\boldsymbol{\alpha}^{+}[\tau]|=\boldsymbol{\alpha}^{+}[\tau]=\boldsymbol{\alpha}^{+}[\tau]+\boldsymbol{\alpha}^{-}[\tau]\ \text{,}

and similarly if
\boldsymbol{\alpha}^{+}[\tau]=0
,

|\boldsymbol{\alpha}^{+}[\tau]-\boldsymbol{\alpha}^{-}[\tau]|=|-\boldsymbol{\alpha}^{-}[\tau]|=\boldsymbol{\alpha}^{-}[\tau]=\boldsymbol{\alpha}^{+}[\tau]+\boldsymbol{\alpha}^{-}[\tau]\ \text{.}

In either case, we have

|\boldsymbol{\alpha}^{+}[\tau]-\boldsymbol{\alpha}^{-}[\tau]|=\boldsymbol{\alpha}^{+}[\tau]+\boldsymbol{\alpha}^{-}[\tau]
.

Overall, the optimization problems in
Equation

3
and
Equation

4
have the following properties:

(i)

they have the same set of constraints,

(ii)

they both have optimal solutions with the property above, and

(iii)

for any optimal solution with this property in one of these optimization problems there is a feasible solution in the other one with the same objective functions.

We conclude that these two optimization problems have the same optimal value. Moreover,
Equation

4
is a linear program with
n^{O(k)}
variables and constraints, as needed.
∎

4
Coboundary Metrics

Normed vector spaces have been extensively studied as special cases of metric spaces. Such spaces have nice properties as their structures match our geometric intuition, and accordingly they appear in many applications.
In this section, we extend the notion of normed vector spaces to
k
-metrics (
Section

4.1
). We call these spaces coboundary
k
-metric spaces because of the use of coboundary operator in their definition. Note that our coboundary
k
-metrics are in fact pseudo
k
-metrics, but we refer to them as metrics for simplicity. We denote a coboundary
k
-metric that is realized from vectors in
\mathbb{R}^{m}
with respect to the norm
\lVert\cdot\rVert
by
{\cal C}_{k,\lVert\cdot\rVert}^{m}
, or
{\cal C}_{k,p}^{m}
when the norm is the
\ell_{p}
norm for some
p\in[1,\infty]
; see
Definition

4.1
below for the formal definition of coboundary metrics.
Coboundary
k
-metrics are generalizations of
\ell_{p}
metrics, specifically,
\ell_{p}^{m}={\cal C}_{2,p}^{m}
.
We use
{\cal C}_{k,p}
to denote the family of all finite coboundary
k
-metrics in
m
dimensions with respect to the
\ell_{p}
norm; in particular,
{\cal C}_{2,p}
is the space of all
\ell_{p}
metrics.
The
\ell_{p}
norms have been very well studied, with many insightful results that relates them via low distortion embeddings. We describe generalizations of some significant results of this type in this section.

In
Section

4.2
, we show that for any
p\neq\infty
, the space of
{\cal C}_{k,p}
is strictly contained in the space of strong pseudo
k
-metrics. In
Section

4.3
, we show that
{\cal C}_{k,\infty}
contains all strong pseudo
k
-metrics spaces, hence
{\cal C}_{k,\infty}={\cal S}_{k}
. This is a generalization of the Fréchet embedding for finite metrics, obtained when
k=2
.
In
Section

4.4
, we give a recipe for adapting results about norm embeddings to analogous results about embedding coboundary
k
-metrics. From this, we get generalizations of the Johnson-Lindenstrauss lemma and Dvoretzky’s theorem as corollaries.
Finally, in
Section

4.5
we show a generalization of the fact that any tree metric is an
\ell_{1}
metric.

4.1
Definition and Basic Properties

We begin by defining coboundary
k
-metrics and proving that they are strong pseudo
k
-metrics. In particular, coboundary
2
-metrics are equivalent to normed spaces as we know them from the study of metric spaces.
Intuitively, a coboundary
k
-metric can be constructed on a point set
X
as follows. We consider the complete
(k-1)
-simplicial complex on
X
. Then we assign vectors to the
(k-2)
-simplices of
K
. We apply the coboundary operator to these vectors to obtain vectors on
(k-1)
-simplices. The norm of these vectors are our metric values.
Figure

2
illustrates
2
- and
3
-coboundary metrics.

Now, we formally define coboundary
k
-metrics. In our definition, the vectors assigned to the
(k-2)
-simplices are the rows of the matrix
F
, which is
equivalently defined by its columns that are
(k-2)
-chains of
K
. See the example in
Figure

2
.

Definition 4.1

(Coboundary
k
-metrics)
.

Let
k\geq 2
be an integer, let
X
be a finite set, let
d:X^{k}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
, and let
K
be the complete
(k-1)
-simplicial complex with vertex set
X
.
Also, let
m\in\mathbb{Z}^{+}
and let
\lVert\cdot\rVert
be any norm on
\mathbb{R}^{m}
.
We say that
(X,d)
is a
coboundary
k
-metric
in
m
dimensions with respect to norm
\lVert\cdot\rVert
, if there exist
(k-2)
-chains
f_{i}:K_{k-2}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
for
i\in[m]
in
K
such that for any distinct
x_{1},\dots,x_{k}\in X
,

d(x_{1},x_{2},\ldots,x_{k})=\begin{cases}0,&\text{if there exist $i\neq j$ with $x_{i}=x_{j}$,}\\
\lVert\boldsymbol{e}_{x_{1},x_{2},\ldots,x_{k}}^{T}\cdot\delta_{k-2}\cdot F\rVert,&\text{otherwise},\end{cases}

(5)

where
F=(\boldsymbol{f}_{1},\ldots,\boldsymbol{f}_{m})
is an
n_{k-2}\times m
matrix specified by its columns, which are
(k-2)
-chains in
K
. We say that
F

induces
the coboundary
k
-metric
(X,d)
. For any non-distinct
x_{1},\dots,x_{k}\in X
,
d(x_{1},\dots,x_{k})=0
.

We denote the space of all coboundary
k
-metrics in
m
dimensions with respect to the norm
\lVert\cdot\rVert
by
{\cal C}_{k,\lVert\cdot\rVert}^{m}
. We slightly simplify notation for the
p
-norms, denoting
{\cal C}_{k,p}^{m}={\cal C}_{k,\lVert\cdot\rVert_{p}}^{m}
. Further, we denote
{\cal C}_{k,p}=\bigcup_{m\in\mathbb{Z}^{+}}{\cal C}_{k,p}^{m}
.
In particular, we remark that
{\cal C}_{2,p}^{m}
is the space of
\ell_{p}^{m}
-metrics, and
{\cal C}_{2,p}
is the space of
\ell_{p}
-metrics, denoted
\mathbb{L}_{p}
in some texts
[
Mat13
]
.
We show that coboundary
k
-metrics are strong pseudo
k
-metrics.

Lemma 4.2
.

Let
k\geq 2
and
m\geq 1
be integers, and let
\lVert\cdot\rVert
be any vector norm. If
(X,d)
belongs to
{\cal C}_{k,\lVert\cdot\rVert}^{m}
then
(X,d)
is a strong pseudo
k
-metric space, i.e.,
{\cal C}_{k,p}\subseteq{\cal S}_{k}
.

Proof.

Let
K
be the complete simplicial complex with vertex set
X
.
Since
(X,d)\in{\cal C}_{k,\lVert\cdot\rVert}^{m}
, there exist
(k-2)
-chains
F=(\boldsymbol{f}_{1},\ldots,\boldsymbol{f}_{m})
such that
for any
x_{1},\ldots,x_{k}\in X
,
d(x_{1},\ldots,x_{k})=\lVert\boldsymbol{e}^{T}_{x_{1},\ldots,x_{k}}\cdot\delta\cdot F\rVert
if
x_{1},\ldots,x_{k}
are distinct and
d(x_{1},\ldots,x_{k})=0
otherwise.

Therefore,
d
is non-negative since any norm is non-negative, and
d
is zero if
x_{1},\ldots,x_{k}
contains duplicated elements. Hence the properties in
Items

1
and
2
of a strong pseudo
k
-metric hold.

We next show the symmetry property (
Item

3
). Let
\pi:[k]\to[k]
be a permutation. If
x_{1},\ldots,x_{k}
are not distinct then
d(x_{1},\ldots,x_{k})=d(x_{\pi(1)},\ldots,x_{\pi(k)})=0
by the “repeated elements property” (
Item

2
) of strong pseudo
k
-metrics (which we already showed holds). Otherwise, if
x_{1},\ldots,x_{k}
are distinct,

\boldsymbol{e}_{\pi(x_{1},\ldots,x_{k})}^{T}\cdot\delta_{k-2}\cdot\boldsymbol{f}_{i}=\textrm{sign}(\pi)\cdot\boldsymbol{e}_{x_{1},\ldots,x_{k}}^{T}\cdot\delta_{k-2}\cdot\boldsymbol{f}_{i}

for every
i\in[m]
. Thus,

\displaystyle d(\pi(x_{1},\ldots,x_{k}))=\lVert\boldsymbol{e}_{x_{\pi(1)},\ldots,x_{\pi(k)})}^{T}\cdot\delta\cdot F\rVert=\lVert\boldsymbol{e}_{x_{1},\ldots,x_{k}}^{T}\cdot\delta\cdot F\rVert=d(x_{1},\ldots,x_{k}).

To show strong simplex inequality, fix a
(k-1)
-simplex
t
, and let
\alpha
be any
(k-1)
-chain such that
\partial_{k-1}\boldsymbol{\alpha}=\partial_{k-1}\boldsymbol{e}_{t}
, thus
\boldsymbol{\alpha}^{T}\delta_{k-2}=\boldsymbol{e}_{t}^{T}\delta_{k-2}
. We have,

d(t)=\lVert\boldsymbol{e}_{t}^{T}\cdot\delta_{k-2}\cdot F\rVert=\lVert\alpha^{T}\cdot\delta_{k-2}\cdot F\rVert\leq|\boldsymbol{\alpha}|\cdot(\boldsymbol{e}_{s}^{T}\cdot\delta_{k-2}\cdot F)_{s\in K_{k-1}}=|\boldsymbol{\alpha}|\cdot\boldsymbol{d},

as desired.
The inequality holds since for any finite set of vectors
(v_{1},\ldots,v_{k})
, any set of scalars
(\beta_{1},\ldots,\beta_{k})
, and any norm
\lVert\cdot\rVert
we have
\lVert\sum_{i=1}^{k}{\beta_{i}v_{i}}\rVert\leq\sum_{i=1}^{k}{|\beta_{i}|\cdot\lVert v_{i}\rVert}
, by properties (
2
) and (
3
) of norms.
∎

4.2
Coboundary
k
-metrics with
p\neq\infty

The relation between different normed spaces and the expressiveness of one compared to others have been studied extensively.
See Matousek
[
Mat13
, Section 1.5]
for a concise summary. In this section, we generalize a known result of this sort, namely that the space of finite
\ell_{p\neq\infty}
metric spaces is strictly contained in the space of finite metric spaces: there exist finite metric spaces that are not
\ell_{p}
metrics if
p\neq\infty
. To this end, we define the apex extension of a
k
-metric, which we show is a
(k+1)
-metric. Intuitively, this
(k+1)
-metric behaves very similarly to the given
k
-metric. (Yet, this apex extensions are very specific
(k+1)
-metrics, thus we expect the space of
(k+1)
-metrics to be much richer than the space of
k
-metrics.)

4.2.1
Apex Extension

Let
(X,d)
be a (weak) pseudo
k
-metric, and let
a
be a new element that we call the
apex
, and let
X^{\prime}=X\cup\{a\}
. We define the function
d^{\prime}
on the
(k+1)
-tuples of
X^{\prime}
, as follows.

(i)

For any
x_{1},\dots,x_{k+1}\in X^{\prime}
, if
x_{1},\ldots,x_{k+1}
are not distinct or do not include
a
, it holds that
d^{\prime}(x_{1},\dots,x_{k+1})=0
.

(ii)

Otherwise, if
x_{i}=a
for some
i
,
d^{\prime}(x_{1},\dots,x_{k+1})=d(x_{1},\ldots,x_{i-1},x_{i+1},\ldots,x_{k+1})
.

We refer to
(X^{\prime},d^{\prime})
as the
apex extension
of
(X,d)
with apex
a
.
We show that the apex extension gives a
k
-metric.

Lemma 4.3
.

The apex extension of a (weak) pseudo
k
-metric is a (weak) pseudo
(k+1)
-metric.

Proof.

Let
(X,d)
be a
k
-metric space, and
(X^{\prime}=X\cup\{a\},d^{\prime})
be its apex extension with apex
a
.
By the definition of apex extension
d^{\prime}
is nonnegative, and
d^{\prime}(x_{1},\ldots,x_{k+1})=0
if
x_{1},\ldots,x_{k+1}
are not distinct.
Also, by the definition of apex extension,
d^{\prime}
is permutation invariant since
d
is permutation invariant. Hence,
(X^{\prime},d^{\prime})
has the first three properties of weak pseudo
(k+1)
-metrics.

To show simplex inequality for
(X^{\prime},d^{\prime})
, let
x_{1},\dots,x_{k},x_{k+1},y\in X^{\prime}
.
If
x_{1},\ldots,x_{k+1}
are not distinct or they if they do not contain
a
then
d^{\prime}(x_{1},\ldots,x_{k+1})=0
and the simplex inequality trivially holds.
Further, if
y\in\{x_{1},\ldots,x_{k+1}\}
again the simplex inequality trivially holds.
So, we assume that
x_{1},\ldots,x_{k+1}
are distinct and that
x_{k+1}=a
, and
y\neq a
. So,

\displaystyle d^{\prime}(x_{1},\ldots,x_{k+1})

\displaystyle=d^{\prime}(x_{1},\ldots,x_{k},a)

\displaystyle=d(x_{1},\ldots,x_{k})

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{i=0}^{k}{d(x_{1},\ldots,x_{i},y,x_{i+1},\ldots,x_{k})}

\displaystyle=\sum_{i=0}^{k}{d^{\prime}(x_{1},\ldots,x_{i},y,x_{i+1},\ldots,x_{k},a)}

\displaystyle=\sum_{i=0}^{k}{d^{\prime}(x_{1},\ldots,x_{i},y,x_{i+1},\ldots,x_{k},a)}+d^{\prime}(x_{1},\ldots,x_{k},y)

\displaystyle=\sum_{i=0}^{k+1}{d^{\prime}(x_{1},\ldots,x_{i},y,x_{i+1},\ldots,x_{k},x_{k+1})}.

The second last equality holds as
d^{\prime}(x_{1},\ldots,x_{k},y)=0
because
y\neq a
.
∎

-chain space of
K^{\prime}
that is spanned by
h
-simplices that include
a
and the
(h-1)
-chain space of
K
. We define
L_{h-1}=P_{h}^{T}
. Equivalently, for any distinct
x_{1},\ldots,x_{h}\in X
,
L_{h-1}\cdot\boldsymbol{e}_{x_{1}\ldots,x_{h}}=\boldsymbol{e}_{x_{1}\ldots x_{h}a}
. See
Figure

6
for examples of the application of the projection and lift operators.
We refer to
P_{h}
and
L_{h}
as
h
-projection and
h
-lift operators, respectively.
A crucial property of
P_{h}
and
L_{h}
is stated in the following lemma.

Lemma 4.4
.

Let
K
be a complete complex on vertex set
X
, and
K^{\prime}
the complete complex on vertex set
X\cup\{a\}
.
For any
h\in[|X|-1]
, let
P_{h}
and
L_{h}
as defined above. We have,

\partial_{h-1}[K]\cdot P_{h}=P_{h-1}\cdot\partial_{h}[K^{\prime}],

(6)

and, equivalently,

L_{h-1}\cdot\delta_{h-2}[K]=\delta_{h-1}[K^{\prime}]\cdot L_{h-2}.

(7)

Proof.

It suffices to show
\partial_{h-1}[K]\cdot P_{h}\cdot\boldsymbol{e}_{t^{\prime}}=P_{h-1}\cdot\partial_{h}[K^{\prime}]\cdot\boldsymbol{e}_{t^{\prime}}
for every
h
-simplex
(x_{1},\ldots,x_{h+1})
in
K^{\prime}
. We consider the following two cases.

If
x_{1},\ldots,x_{h+1}
does not include
a
, then

P_{h}\cdot\boldsymbol{e}_{x_{1},\ldots,x_{h+1}}=0,

by the definition of
P_{h}
, thus the left-hand side of
Equation

6
is zero.
Further, none of the
(h-1)
-simplices in
\partial_{h}[K^{\prime}]\cdot\boldsymbol{e}_{x_{1},\ldots,x_{h+1}}
include
a
, so

P_{h-1}\cdot\partial_{h}[K^{\prime}]\cdot\boldsymbol{e}_{x_{1},\ldots,x_{h+1}}=0
, thus the right-hand side of
Equation

6
is also zero, as desired.

Otherwise, we assume without loss of generality that
x_{k+1}=a
. First, we simplify left-hand side of
Equation

6

\partial_{h-1}[K]\cdot P_{h}\cdot\boldsymbol{e}_{x_{1},\ldots,x_{h},a}=\partial_{h-1}[K]\cdot\boldsymbol{e}_{x_{1},\ldots,x_{h}}=\sum_{i=1}^{h}{(-1)^{i+1}\boldsymbol{e}_{x_{1},\ldots x_{i-1}x_{i+1}\ldots x_{h}}},

by the definition of
P_{h}
and the boundary map. Then, we simplify right-hand side of
Equation

6
.

\displaystyle P_{h-1}\cdot\partial_{h}[K^{\prime}]\cdot\boldsymbol{e}_{x_{1},\ldots,x_{h},a}

\displaystyle=P_{h-1}\cdot\left(\sum_{i=1}^{h}{(-1)^{i+1}\boldsymbol{e}_{x_{1},\ldots x_{i-1}x_{i+1}\ldots x_{h}a}}+(-1)^{h+2}\boldsymbol{e}_{x_{1},\ldots x_{i-1}x_{i}x_{i+1}\ldots x_{h}}\right)

\displaystyle=\sum_{i=1}^{h}{(-1)^{i+1}\boldsymbol{e}_{x_{1},\ldots x_{i-1}x_{i+1}\ldots x_{h}}}.

So, the left-hand side and right-hand side of
Equation

6
are equal and so the lemma holds.
∎

4.2.2
Extending
k
-metrics to Similar
(k+1)
-metrics

We now show that the apex extension of a coboundary metric is a coboundary metric with respect to the same norm.
This result together with a later result of this paper will imply that the apex extensions of a strong pseudo
k
-metric is a strong pseudo
(k+1)
-metric.
To that end, we need the following auxiliary lemma.

Lemma 4.5
.

Let
K
be a simplicial complex, and let
k\in\mathbb{Z}^{+}
. Also, let
\Lambda
be a subset of
(k-1)
-simplices of
K
such that no nonzero linear combination of them is a
(k-1)
-cycle.
Then, for any coboundary
k
-chain
\boldsymbol{h}
, there exists a
(k-1)
-chain
\boldsymbol{f}
in
K
, such that

(1)

\boldsymbol{h}=\delta_{k-1}\cdot\boldsymbol{f}
, and

(2)

\boldsymbol{f}
is zero on all simplices in
\Lambda
.

Proof.

Since
\boldsymbol{h}
is a coboundary
k
-chain, there exists a
(k-1)
-chains
\boldsymbol{f}^{\prime}
, such that
\boldsymbol{h}=\delta_{k-1}\cdot\boldsymbol{f}^{\prime}
. Let
\boldsymbol{f}^{\prime\prime}
be the
coboundary

(k-1)
-chain that agrees with
\boldsymbol{f}^{\prime}
on the values assigned to the simplices of
\Lambda
. To see that such an
\boldsymbol{f}^{\prime\prime}
exists, let
K_{\Lambda}
be the simplicial complex whose set of
(k-1)
-simplices is
\Lambda
, and whose set of
(k-2)
-simplices agrees with
K
. Since
\Lambda
has no cycle,
\boldsymbol{f}^{\prime\prime}
is a coboundary chain in
K_{\Lambda}
, thus, there is
(k-2)
-chain
\boldsymbol{\beta}
in
L_{\Lambda}
such that
\delta_{k-2}[K_{\Lambda}]\cdot\boldsymbol{\beta}
is equal to
\boldsymbol{f}^{\prime\prime}
restricted to
K_{\Lambda}
.
But,
\boldsymbol{f}^{\prime\prime}=\delta_{k-2}[K]\cdot\boldsymbol{\beta}
assigns the same set of values to the simplices in
\Lambda
, so
\boldsymbol{e}_{\lambda}^{T}\cdot\boldsymbol{f}^{\prime}=\boldsymbol{e}_{\lambda}^{T}\cdot\boldsymbol{f}^{\prime\prime}
, for any
\lambda\in\Lambda
.

Now let
\boldsymbol{f}=\boldsymbol{f}^{\prime}-\boldsymbol{f}^{\prime\prime}
. Since
\boldsymbol{f}^{\prime}
and
\boldsymbol{f}^{\prime\prime}
agree on the values assigned to simplices of
\Lambda
,
\boldsymbol{f}
assigns zero to these simplices. Further, since
\boldsymbol{f}^{\prime\prime}
is a coboundary
(k-1)
-chain,
\delta_{k-1}\cdot\boldsymbol{f}^{\prime\prime}=0
, therefore,
\delta_{k-1}\cdot\boldsymbol{f}=\delta_{k-1}\cdot\boldsymbol{f}^{\prime}=\boldsymbol{h}
.
∎

Now, we are ready to show that the apex extension of a
k
-metric is a couboundary metric if any only if the
k
-metric itself is a coboundary metric.

Lemma 4.6
.

The apex extension of a
k
-metric is in
{\cal C}_{k+1,\lVert\cdot\rVert}^{m}
if and only if the
k
-metric is in
{\cal C}_{k,\lVert\cdot\rVert}^{m}
.

Proof.

Let
(X,d)
be a
k
-metric, and let
(X^{\prime}=\{X\cup\{a\}\},d^{\prime})
be its apex extension. Also, let
K
and
K^{\prime}
be the complete
(k-1)
- and
k
-simplicial complexes with vertex sets
X
and
X^{\prime}
, respectively.

First, we show
(X,d)\in{\cal C}_{k,\lVert\cdot\rVert}^{m}\Rightarrow(X^{\prime},d^{\prime})\in{\cal C}_{k+1,\lVert\cdot\rVert}^{m}
.
Since
(X,d)\in{\cal C}_{k,\lVert\cdot\rVert}^{m}
, there are
(k-2)
-chains
F=(\boldsymbol{f}_{1},\ldots,\boldsymbol{f}_{m})
in
K
such that for any
t\in K_{k-1}
,
d(t)=\lVert\boldsymbol{e}_{t}^{T}\cdot\delta_{k-2}[K]\cdot F\rVert
. Let
F^{\prime}=L_{k-2}F
, where
L_{k-2}
is the lift operator defined above.
We show, for any
t^{\prime}\in K^{\prime}_{k}
,
d^{\prime}(t^{\prime})=\lVert\boldsymbol{e}_{t^{\prime}}^{T}\cdot\delta_{k-1}[K^{\prime}]\cdot F^{\prime}\rVert
, hence
(X^{\prime},d^{\prime})\in{\cal C}_{k+1,\lVert\cdot\rVert}^{m}
.
If
t^{\prime}
does not include
a
, then
F^{\prime}=L_{k-2}F=0
by the definition of
L_{k-2}
. Therefore,

\lVert\boldsymbol{e}_{t^{\prime}}^{T}\cdot\delta_{k-1}[K^{\prime}]\cdot F^{\prime}\rVert=0=d^{\prime}(t^{\prime})
.
Otherwise, assume without loss of generality that
t^{\prime}=(x_{1},\ldots,x_{k},a)\in K^{\prime}_{k}
, and let
t=(x_{1},\ldots,x_{k})\in K^{\prime}_{k-1}
, so
d^{\prime}(t^{\prime})=d(t)
. Also, we know
d(t)=\lVert\boldsymbol{e}_{t}^{T}\cdot\delta_{k-2}[K]\cdot F\rVert
. Finally,

\boldsymbol{e}_{t^{\prime}}^{T}\cdot\delta_{k-1}[K^{\prime}]\cdot F^{\prime}=\boldsymbol{e}_{t^{\prime}}^{T}\cdot\delta_{k-1}[K^{\prime}]\cdot L_{k-2}\cdot F=\boldsymbol{e}_{t^{\prime}}^{T}\cdot L_{k-1}\cdot\delta_{k-2}[K]\cdot F=\boldsymbol{e}_{t}^{T}\cdot\delta_{k-2}[K]\cdot F,

where the second equality holds by
Lemma

4.4
, and
\boldsymbol{e}_{t^{\prime}}^{T}\cdot L_{k-1}=\boldsymbol{e}_{t}
as
L_{k-1}
maps
\boldsymbol{e}_{t}
to
\boldsymbol{e}_{t^{\prime}}
by its definition. Therefore,
\lVert\boldsymbol{e}_{t^{\prime}}\cdot\delta_{k-1}[K^{\prime}]\cdot F^{\prime}\rVert=\lVert\boldsymbol{e}_{t}^{T}\cdot\delta_{k-2}[K]\cdot F\rVert=d(t)=d^{\prime}(t^{\prime})
, as needed.

Second, we show
(X^{\prime},d^{\prime})\in{\cal C}_{k+1,\lVert\cdot\rVert}^{m}\Rightarrow(X,d)\in{\cal C}_{k,\lVert\cdot\rVert}^{m}
.
Since
(X^{\prime},d^{\prime})\in{\cal C}_{k+1,\lVert\cdot\rVert}^{m}
, there are
(k-1)
-chains
F^{\prime}=(\boldsymbol{f}^{\prime}_{1},\ldots,\boldsymbol{f}^{\prime}_{m})
in
K^{\prime}
such that for any
t^{\prime}\in K^{\prime}_{k}
,
d^{\prime}(t^{\prime})=\lVert\boldsymbol{e}_{t^{\prime}}^{T}\cdot\delta_{k-1}[K^{\prime}]\cdot F^{\prime}\rVert
.
Let
\Lambda=\{\lambda_{1},\ldots,\lambda_{q}\}
be the set of all
k
-simplices in
K^{\prime}
that contain
a
, and consider any nonzero linear combination
\beta=\sum_{i=1}^{q}{c_{i}{\boldsymbol{e}_{\lambda_{i}}}}
. Since the linear combination is nonzero there exists
1\leq j\leq q
such that
c_{j}\neq 0
. Let
\lambda_{j}=(y_{1},\ldots,y_{k},a)
, and note that
\boldsymbol{e}_{y_{1},\ldots,y_{k}}^{T}\cdot\partial_{k}[K^{\prime}]\cdot\beta
is either
c_{j}
or
-c_{j}
, as coefficients for the simplex
(y_{1},\ldots,y_{k})
in
\beta
can only be generated from applying the boundary operator to
\lambda_{j}
and not any other
\lambda_{i}
. Therefore,
\partial_{k}[K^{\prime}]\cdot\beta\neq 0
,
\beta
is not a cycle. Thus, by
Lemma

4.5
, we can assume that
F^{\prime}
is zero on all simplices in
\Lambda
. Let
F^{\prime\prime}=(\boldsymbol{f}^{\prime\prime}_{1},\ldots,\boldsymbol{f}^{\prime\prime}_{m})
be the restriction of
F^{\prime}
to the
(k-1)
-simplices in
K
, that is for each
i\in[m]
, and each
(k-1)
-simplex
t=(x_{1},\ldots,x_{k})\in K_{k-1}
,
\boldsymbol{e}_{t}^{T}\cdot\boldsymbol{f}^{\prime\prime}_{i}=\boldsymbol{e}_{t}^{T}\cdot\boldsymbol{f}^{\prime}_{i}
. So,

\lVert\boldsymbol{e}_{x_{1},\ldots,x_{k}}^{T}\cdot F^{\prime\prime}\rVert=\lVert\boldsymbol{e}_{x_{1},\ldots,x_{k}}^{T}\cdot F^{\prime}\rVert=\lVert\boldsymbol{e}_{x_{1},\ldots,x_{k}a}^{T}\cdot\delta_{k-1}[K^{\prime}]\cdot F^{\prime}\rVert=d^{\prime}(x_{1},\ldots,x_{k},a)=d(x_{1},\ldots,x_{k}),

where the second equality holds because
F^{\prime}
is zero on simplices that contains
a
.
To finish the proof, we show that
\boldsymbol{f}^{\prime\prime}_{i}
are coboundary chains in
K
.
To that end, observe for every
x_{1},\ldots,x_{k+1}\in X
,

\lVert\boldsymbol{e}_{x_{1},\ldots,x_{k+1}}^{T}\cdot\delta_{k-1}[K]\cdot F^{\prime\prime}\rVert=\lVert\boldsymbol{e}_{x_{1},\ldots,x_{k+1}}^{T}\cdot\delta_{k-1}[K^{\prime}]\cdot F^{\prime}\rVert=d^{\prime}(x_{1},\ldots,x_{k+1})=0,

by the definition of
d^{\prime}
. So,
\delta_{k-1}[K]\cdot F^{\prime\prime}=0
, in particular
\delta_{k-1}[K]\cdot\boldsymbol{f}^{\prime\prime}_{i}=0
for any
i\in[m]
. Therefore,
\boldsymbol{f}^{\prime\prime}_{i}
is a coboundary chain.
∎

This lemma is powerful in generalizing non-inclusion results from regular metric spaces.
Specifically, consider
{\cal C}_{2,p}
and
{\cal C}_{2,q}
that are the spaces of
\ell_{p}
and
\ell_{q}
metrics. Given a metric space that belongs to
{\cal C}_{2,p}
but not
{\cal C}_{2,q}
, one can build a metric space that belongs to
{\cal C}_{k,p}
but not
{\cal C}_{k,q}
, for
k>2
using induction and the lemma above. In particular, one can generalize the fact that for
p\neq\infty
, the
\ell_{p}
-metrics does not include all metrics (see Matousek
[
Mat13
, Section 1.5-(iv)]
).

Corollary 4.7
.

For every
p\neq\infty
and
k\geq 2
, there exists a strong
k
-metric space that is not in
{\cal C}_{k,p}
.

4.3
Fréchet Embedding for
k
-metrics

Contrary to other
\ell_{p}
spaces, it is well-known that
\ell_{\infty}
contains all finite metric spaces, a result obtained via Fréchet embedding. In this section, we show that this result generalizes to strong
k
-metrics. More specifically, we show that any strong
n
-point
k
-metric belongs to
{\cal C}_{k,\infty}^{m}
where
m=\binom{n}{k}
.

The embedding works as follows.
Let
(X,d)
be a strong pseudo
k
-metric. For each
k
-tuple
x_{1},\ldots,x_{k}\in X
, we show there exists a
k
-metric in
{\cal C}_{k,\infty}^{1}
that preserves the value of
d
for this specific tuple
x_{1},\ldots,x_{k}
, and does not expand the value of
d
for any other
k
-tuples. This is the main technical part for obtaining our embedding. Our construction is different from the well-known explicit construction of the Fréchet embedding for regular metrics. In particular, we build a linear program and show that its (optimal) solution has the properties that we need.

Lemma 4.8
.

Let
(X,d)
be a strong pseudo
k
-metric, and let
x_{1},\ldots,x_{k}\in X
. There exists a coboundary
k
-metric
(X,d^{\prime})\in{\cal C}_{k,\infty}^{1}
such that

(1)

(Non-expansion.) For any
y_{1},\ldots,y_{k}\in X
,
d^{\prime}(y_{1},\ldots,y_{k})\leq d(y_{1},\ldots,y_{k})
, and

(2)

(One-tuple distance preservation.)
d^{\prime}(x_{1},\ldots,x_{k})=d(x_{1},\ldots,x_{k})
.

Proof.

Let
K
be the complete
(k-1)
-simplicial complex with vertex set
X
. We show how to compute a
(k-2)
-chain
\boldsymbol{f}
such that
\boldsymbol{d}^{\prime}=|\delta_{k-2}\cdot\boldsymbol{f}|
has the required properties of this lemma.

To satisfy
Item

1
of the lemma, we need

|\delta_{k-2}\cdot\boldsymbol{f}|\leq\boldsymbol{d}\Longleftrightarrow-\boldsymbol{d}\leq\delta_{k-2}\cdot\boldsymbol{f}\leq\boldsymbol{d}.

Here, we have two parallel hyperplane for each
(k-1)
-simplex, so we get
\binom{n}{k}
non-parallel constraints.
Note that any two
(k-2)
-chains
\boldsymbol{f}
and
\boldsymbol{f^{\prime}}
with the same cyclic part realize the same metrics as
\delta_{k-2}\cdot(\boldsymbol{f}-\boldsymbol{f^{\prime}})=0
.
So, we can further reduce our search space for
\boldsymbol{f}
that give rise to metrics with
Item

1
, by adding the extra constraint that
\boldsymbol{f}
is a cocycle (it is orthogonal to the cycle space, which is equal to the boundary space in a complete complex), that is
\partial_{k-2}\boldsymbol{f}=0
.
These two sets of constraints together specify a polytope
P
within the space of all
(k-2)
-chains.
Since,
\boldsymbol{d}
is non-negative and the boundary of the zero vector is the zero vector,
P
contains the zero vector, so it is not empty.
To obtain an
\boldsymbol{f}
that satisfies
Item

2
as well, we solve the following linear program for
\boldsymbol{f}
in the space of
(k-2)
-chains in
K
.

\displaystyle\max\penalty\ \left(\boldsymbol{e}_{t}^{T}\cdot\delta_{k-2}\cdot\boldsymbol{f}\right)

\displaystyle\text{s.t.\penalty\ }-\boldsymbol{d}\leq\delta_{k-2}\cdot\boldsymbol{f}\leq\boldsymbol{d}

\displaystyle\penalty\ \penalty\ \penalty\ \penalty\ \penalty\ \penalty\ \penalty\ \partial_{k-2}\cdot\boldsymbol{f}=0,

where
t=(x_{1},\ldots,x_{k})
.
Intuitively, we would like to maximize the coboundary value on
t
while preserving
Item

1
for all simplices. We show that a certain point in
P
that maximizes the coboundary value at
t
satisfies
Item

2
. This is sufficient to prove the lemma as every point in
P
satisfies
Item

1
.

Let
\boldsymbol{f}
be an optimal
vertex
solution of our linear program; such a vertex solution exists since any vector in the kernel of
\partial_{k-2}
is a linear combination of coboundary vectors.
Consider a maximal number of independent conditions in the linear program that are tight at
\boldsymbol{f}
, suppose these conditions hold at
(k-2)
-simplices
\{t_{1},t_{2},\ldots,t_{r}\}
, for which at least one of the corresponding conditions in the linear program is tight. Note that each of these simplices contributes two conditions to the linear program, namely for each
i\in[r]
, we have
\boldsymbol{e}_{t_{i}}^{T}\cdot\delta_{k-2}\cdot\boldsymbol{f}\leq d(t_{i})
and
-\boldsymbol{e}_{t_{i}}^{T}\cdot\delta_{k-2}\cdot\boldsymbol{f}\leq d(t_{i})
. Without loss of generality we can assume that all of our tight conditions are of the first type, if not we change the orientation of the simplices to make it so.

Note
\boldsymbol{e}_{t}^{T}\cdot(\delta_{k-2}\cdot\boldsymbol{f})=(\partial_{k-1}\cdot\boldsymbol{e}_{t})^{T}\boldsymbol{f}
. Therefore,
\partial_{k-1}\cdot\boldsymbol{e}_{t}
is the objective vector of our linear program. Also, since
\partial_{k-2}\partial_{k-1}=\boldsymbol{0}
, the vector
\partial_{k-1}\cdot\boldsymbol{e}_{t}
and the vectors
\partial_{k-1}\cdot\boldsymbol{e}_{t_{i}}
for
i\in[r]
are in the subspace specified by
\partial_{k-2}\boldsymbol{f}=\boldsymbol{0}
.
Since
\boldsymbol{f}
is optimal,
\partial_{k-1}\cdot\boldsymbol{e}_{t}
is in the cone defined by
\partial_{k-1}\cdot\boldsymbol{e}_{t_{i}}
for
i\in[r]
. So,
\partial_{k-2}\cdot\boldsymbol{e}_{t}
is a non-negative linear combination of these vectors, that is

\partial_{k-1}\cdot\boldsymbol{e}_{t}=\sum_{i=1}^{r}{\beta_{i}(\partial_{k-1}\cdot\boldsymbol{e}_{t_{i}})}=\partial_{k-1}\cdot\left(\sum_{i=1}^{r}{\beta_{i}\boldsymbol{e}_{t_{i}}}\right),

(8)

with all non-negative
\beta_{i}
’s. Since
d
is a strong
k
-metrics, we have

d(t)=\boldsymbol{e}_{t}\cdot\boldsymbol{d}\leq\sum_{i=1}^{r}|\beta_{i}|\cdot d(t_{i})=\sum_{i=1}^{r}\beta_{i}\cdot d(t_{i})\ \text{.}

(9)

On the other hand,

(\partial_{k-1}\cdot\boldsymbol{e}_{t})^{T}\boldsymbol{f}=\boldsymbol{e}_{t}^{T}\cdot(\delta_{k-2}\cdot\boldsymbol{f})\leq d(t)

(10)

as
\boldsymbol{f}
is a feasible solution of the linear program, and that for all
i\in[r]
,

(\partial\cdot\boldsymbol{e}_{t_{i}})^{T}\boldsymbol{f}=d(t_{i})

(11)

because these are tight conditions. So,

\displaystyle\sum_{i=1}^{r}{\beta_{i}d(t_{i})}

\displaystyle\geq d(t)

(see
Equation

9
)

\displaystyle\geq(\partial_{k-1}\cdot\boldsymbol{e}_{t})^{T}\boldsymbol{f}

(see
Equation

10
)

\displaystyle=(\sum_{i=1}^{r}{\beta_{i}(\partial_{k-1}\cdot\boldsymbol{e}_{t_{i}})})^{T}\boldsymbol{f}

(see
Equation

8
)

\displaystyle=\sum_{i=1}^{r}{\beta_{i}\left((\partial_{k-1}\cdot\boldsymbol{e}_{t_{i}})^{T}\boldsymbol{f}\right)}

\displaystyle=\sum_{i=1}^{r}{\beta_{i}d(t_{i})}

(see
Equation

11
)

But, since the first and last term are the same, the inequalities in the middle have to be equalities. Therefore,
d(t)=(\partial_{k-1}\cdot\boldsymbol{e}_{t})^{T}\boldsymbol{f}
as desired.
∎

Now, we are ready to prove the main theorem of this section. Specifically, for
k=2
, our theorem implies that any
n
-point metric space is isometrically embeddable into
\ell_{\infty}^{m}
with
m=\binom{n}{2}
, which is similar to the Fréchet embedding, but embeds into
m=O(n^{2})
dimensions rather than
m=O(n)
.

Theorem 4.9
.

For any integer
k\geq 2
, any
n
-point strong pseudo
k
-metric belongs to
{\cal C}_{k,\infty}^{\binom{n}{k}}
. Therefore,
{\cal C}_{k,\infty}=\mathcal{S}_{k}
.

Proof.

Let
(X,d)
be a strong pseudo
k
-metric, and let
K
be a complete simplex with vertex set
X
.
By
Lemma

4.8
, for any
(k-1)
-simplex
\lambda
in
K
, there exists a coboundary
k
-metric
(X,d_{\lambda})\in{\cal C}_{k,\infty}^{1}
such that (1)
d_{\lambda}(t)\leq d(t)
for any
(k-1)
-simplex
t
, and (2)
d_{\lambda}(\lambda)=d(\lambda)
. Since
(X,d_{\lambda})\in{\cal C}_{k,\infty}^{1}
, there exists
(k-2)
-chain
\boldsymbol{f}_{\lambda}
such that
\boldsymbol{d}_{\lambda}=\lVert\delta\cdot\boldsymbol{f}_{\lambda}\rVert_{\infty}
.

Now, let
F=\left(\boldsymbol{f}_{\lambda}\right)_{\lambda\in K_{k-1}}
be a matrix specified by its
\binom{n}{k}
columns, and let for any
(k-1)
-simplex
t
,
d^{\prime}(t)=\lVert\boldsymbol{e}_{t}^{T}\cdot\delta_{k-2}\cdot F\rVert_{\infty}
.
We show that
d=d^{\prime}
. For any
(k-1)
-simplex
t
, we have

\boldsymbol{e}_{t}^{T}\cdot\delta_{k-1}\cdot F=\left(\boldsymbol{e}^{T}_{t}\cdot\delta_{k-1}\cdot\boldsymbol{f}_{\lambda}\right)_{\lambda\in K_{k-1}},

therefore,

d^{\prime}(t)=\lVert\boldsymbol{e}_{t}^{T}\cdot\delta_{k-1}\cdot F\rVert_{\infty}=\max_{\lambda\in K_{k-1}}{\left(\boldsymbol{e}_{t}^{T}\cdot\delta_{k-1}\cdot\boldsymbol{f}_{\lambda}\right)}=\max_{\lambda\in K_{k-2}}{\left(d_{\lambda}(t)\right)}.

But, we know that
d_{\lambda}(t)\leq d(t)
for all
\lambda\in K_{k-1}
, and that
d_{t}(t)=d(t)
. Thus,

\max_{\lambda\in K_{k-2}}{\left(d_{\lambda}(t)\right)}=d(t).

Hence,
d^{\prime}=d
, as desired.
∎

We now get two corollaries of
Theorem

4.9
(and in particular its implication that
{\cal C}_{k,\infty}={\cal S}_{k}
).
First, by combining
Theorem

4.9
with the guarantee about apex extensions of coboundary
k
-metrics in
Lemma

4.6
we get the following.

Corollary 4.10
.

For every integer
k\geq 2
, the apex extension of a
k
-metric is in
{\cal S}_{k+1}
if and only if the
k
-metric is in
{\cal S}_{k}
.

Second, by
Lemma

3.4
there exists a weak
3
-metric that is not a strong
3
-metric.
Combining this with
Lemmas

4.3
and
4.10
and using an induction argument we get the following.

Corollary 4.11
.

For every integer
k\geq 3
, there exists a (weak) pseudo

-metric space that is not a strong pseudo

-metric space, i.e.,

.

We remark that

shows that the restriction of the Fréchet embedding result in

to strong

4.4
Relating Coboundary
k
-metrics using Norm Embeddings

We now give a technique for generalizing linear norm embeddings to embeddings between coboundary
k
-metric spaces.
Somewhat more precisely, the following proposition says that if there exists a (family of) linear embeddings from
(\mathbb{R}^{m},\lVert\cdot\rVert_{p})
to
(\mathbb{R}^{m^{\prime}},\lVert\cdot\rVert_{q})
that are norm-preserving to within a

\mathcal{C}_{k,p}^{m}
into

that preserves

Proposition 4.12
.

Let
k,m,m^{\prime},n\in\mathbb{Z}^{+}
,
p,q\in[1,\infty]
, and
\varepsilon\geq 0
. Suppose that there exists a distribution
\mathcal{D}
of linear maps

such that for every
\boldsymbol{x}\in\mathbb{R}^{m}

\Pr[(1-\varepsilon)\cdot\lVert\boldsymbol{x}\rVert_{p}\leq\lVert R\cdot\boldsymbol{x}\rVert_{q}\leq(1+\varepsilon)\cdot\lVert\boldsymbol{x}\rVert_{p}]\geq 1-\eta

(12)

for
\eta=\eta(\varepsilon,k,m,m^{\prime},n)<1/\binom{n}{k}
.
Then for any
n
-point
C_{k,p}^{m}
space
(X,d)
there exists an
n
-point
\mathcal{C}_{k,q}^{m^{\prime}}
space
(X,d^{\prime})
such that for any

(1-\varepsilon)\cdot d(t)\leq d^{\prime}(t)\leq(1+\varepsilon)\cdot d(t)\ \text{.}

(13)

Proof.

By definition of
\mathcal{C}_{k,p}^{m}
, there exists a matrix

that induces

. I.e.,

is a matrix whose rows are indexed by simplices

and whose columns

are
(k-2)
-chains such that

for
\boldsymbol{y}_{t}:=(\boldsymbol{e}_{t}^{T}\delta_{k-2}F)^{T}
.
Sample

and let

.
We claim that with positive probability

induces a
\mathcal{C}_{k,q}^{m^{\prime}}
metric

satisfying the condition in
Equation

13
for all

We now prove the claim.
By definition,

satisfies

. Furthermore, by

12
we have that for any fixed
(k-1)
-simplex

\Pr_{R\sim\mathcal{D}}[(1-\varepsilon)\cdot\lVert\boldsymbol{y}_{t}\rVert_{p}\leq\lVert R\cdot\boldsymbol{y}_{t}\rVert_{q}\leq(1+\varepsilon)\cdot\lVert\boldsymbol{y}_{t}\rVert_{p}]\geq 1-\eta\ \text{.}

The claim follows by taking a union bound over all

such simplices

with standard orientation and using the fact that

.

∎

We next give two corollaries of

4.5
Hypertree

In this section, we study minimum bounding chain

-metric

Minimum bounding chain

Let

(k-2)

(k-2)

5
Volume Metrics

We study volume metrics as intuitive examples of
k
-metrics. They are also natural generalization of Euclidean metrics.
Therefore, it makes sense to study them with coboundary metrics under
\ell_{2}
norm, the other, probably less immediate, generalization of Euclidean metrics, introduced in this paper. We show evidence that
\ell_{2}
coboundary
k
-metrics are richer than the volume
k
-metrics. Specifically, we show that volume
k
-metric of points in
\mathbb{R}^{m}
belong to coboundary
k
-metrics with the
2
-norm in
O(m^{k})
dimensions (
Section

5.2
). On the other hand, we show a coboundary
n
-point metric in
{\cal C}_{3,2}^{1}
that can not be realized as the volume metric of points in
\Omega(\log n)
dimensions (
Section

5.3
).

5.1
High-Dimensional Volume

In this section, we review definitions and useful facts concerning high-dimensional volume.
The
k
-dimensional
signed volume
of the
k
-simplex with vertices
x_{0},x_{1},\ldots x_{k}\in\mathbb{R}^{k}
is defined as

\svol_{k}(\boldsymbol{x}_{0},\boldsymbol{x}_{1},\ldots,\boldsymbol{x}_{k}):=\frac{1}{k!}\cdot\det(A)\ \text{,}

(14)

where
A:=(\boldsymbol{x}_{1}-\boldsymbol{x}_{0},\ldots,\boldsymbol{x}_{k}-\boldsymbol{x}_{0})
.
In turn, the
volume
of this simplex is

\vol_{k}(\boldsymbol{x}_{0},\boldsymbol{x}_{1},\ldots,\boldsymbol{x}_{k}):=|\svol_{k}(\boldsymbol{x}_{0},\boldsymbol{x}_{1},\ldots,\boldsymbol{x}_{k})|\ \text{.}

(15)

More generally, for
x_{0},\ldots,x_{k}\in\mathbb{R}^{m}
(where
m
is not necessarily
k+1
) the volume of the simplex
\conv(x_{0},x_{1},\ldots,x_{k})
is equal to the square root of the determinant of the Gram matrix of
A:=(\boldsymbol{x}_{1}-\boldsymbol{x}_{0},\ldots,\boldsymbol{x}_{k}-\boldsymbol{x}_{0})
:

\vol_{k}(\boldsymbol{x}_{0},\boldsymbol{x}_{1},\ldots,\boldsymbol{x}_{k})=\frac{1}{k!}\sqrt{\det(A^{T}A)}\ \text{.}

(16)

We note that the (signed) volume of the simplex
(x_{0},x_{1},\ldots,x_{k})
is non-zero if and only if
A
has full column rank.

One can also compute the volume of a
k
-simplex in
\mathbb{R}^{m}
in terms of its projections onto axis-aligned hyperplanes using the
Cauchy-Binet formula
, as we describe below (see, e.g.,
[
Sch16
]
for a proof).
For a matrix
A\in\mathbb{R}^{m\times k}
and sets
I\subseteq[m]
and
J\subseteq[k]
, let
A_{I,J}\in\mathbb{R}^{\left|{I}\right|\times\left|{J}\right|}
denote the submatrix of
A
formed by the rows of
A
indexed by
i\in I
and columns of
A
indexed by
j\in J
.

Theorem 5.1

(Cauchy-Binet formula)
.

Let
A\in\mathbb{R}^{m\times k}
for
k,m\in\mathbb{Z}^{+}
with
k\leq m
. Then

\det(A^{T}A)=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}I\subseteq[m],\\
\left|{I}\right|=k\end{subarray}}\det(A^{T}_{[k],I}\cdot A_{I,[k]})\ \text{.}

Combining
Theorem

5.1
with
Equation

16
, we get as a corollary that the squared volume of a
k
-simplex
\sigma\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{m}
is equal to the sum of the squared volumes of the
\binom{m}{k}
projections
\pi_{I}(\sigma)
for
I\subseteq[m]
,
\left|{I}\right|=k
of
\sigma
onto
k
-dimensional axis-aligned hyperplanes.

Corollary 5.2
.

Let
k,m\in\mathbb{Z}^{+}
with
m\geq k
, and let
\sigma\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{m}
be a
k
-simplex. Then

\vol_{k}(\sigma)^{2}=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}I\subseteq[m],\\
\left|{I}\right|=k\end{subarray}}\vol_{k}(\pi_{I}(\sigma))^{2}=\lVert\vol_{k}(\pi_{I}(\sigma)_{I\subseteq[m],\left|{I}\right|=k})\rVert^{2}\ \text{,}

(17)

where
\pi_{I}
denotes orthogonal projection onto the axis-aligned hyperplane spanned by the vectors
\boldsymbol{e}_{i}
for
i\in I
.

We note in passing that one could also define the “
(k,p)
-volume” of a
k
-simplex
\sigma\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{m}
as

\nu_{k,p}(\sigma):=\Big(\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}I\subseteq[m],\\
\left|{I}\right|=k\end{subarray}}\vol_{k}(\pi_{I}(\sigma))^{p}\Big)^{1/p}\ \text{,}

and that these functions
\nu_{k,p}
are also strong pseudo
(k+1)
-metrics.
For example,
\nu_{2,1}
corresponds to adding up the areas of the projections of a triangle embedded in
\mathbb{R}^{m}
onto axis-aligned planes.
These functions might also be of interest.

5.2
Volume
k
-metrics

Now, we are ready to define volume metrics and study their relationship with coboundary metrics under the Euclidean norm.

Definition 5.3

(Volume
k
-metrics)
.

Let
X
be a finite set of points, and let
d
be a function on
k
-tuples of
X
.
We say that
(X,d)
is an
m
-dimensional
volume metric
if there exists a map
f:X\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^{m}
, such that for any
k
points
x_{1},\ldots,x_{k}\in X
, we have

d(x_{1},\ldots,x_{k})=\vol_{k-1}(f(x_{1}),\ldots,f(x_{k})).

We define the family of all
m
-dimensional volume metrics by
{\cal V}_{k}^{m}
. Further, we define
{\cal V}_{k}=\bigcup_{k\in\mathbb{Z}^{+}}{{\cal V}_{k}^{m}}
.

We can show that if
(X,d)
is an
m
-dimensional volume
k
-metric then it belongs to
{\cal C}_{k,2}^{m^{\prime}}
where
m^{\prime}=\binom{m}{k}
. So, the family of coboundary metrics is equivalent or richer. The following lemma is the main technical step for proving this result.

Lemma 5.4
.

For any integer
k\geq 2
, the
(k-1)
-dimensional volume
k
-metric is a
1
-dimensional coboundary
k
-metric with respect to the
\ell_{2}
norm, i.e.,
{\cal V}_{k}^{k-1}\subseteq{\cal C}_{k,2}^{1}
.

Proof.

We show any
(X,d)\in{\cal V}_{k}^{k-1}
belongs to
{\cal C}_{k,2}^{1}
.
By the definition of volume
k
-metrics, there exists a function
g:X\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^{k-1}
, such that for any
x_{1},\ldots,x_{k}
,
d(x_{1},\ldots,x_{k})=\vol_{k-1}(g(x_{1}),\ldots,g(x_{k}))=\vol_{k-1}(\boldsymbol{y}_{1},\ldots,\boldsymbol{y}_{k})
, where
\boldsymbol{y}_{i}:=g(x_{i})
for
i\in[k]
.

Let
K
be a complete
(k-1)
-dimensional complex with vertex set
X
. For any
(k-2)
-simplex
s=(x_{1},\ldots,x_{k-1})
,

f(s)=f((x_{1},\ldots,x_{k-1})):=\text{svol}_{k-1}(\boldsymbol{0},\boldsymbol{y}_{1},\ldots,\boldsymbol{y}_{k})\ \text{.}

Note, this is a well-defined chain, as swapping two columns changes the sign of the signed volume and the sign of the corresponding oriented simplex.
We show that
|\boldsymbol{e}_{t}^{T}\cdot\delta_{k-2}\cdot\boldsymbol{f}|=d(t)
for every
(k-1)
-simplex
t=(x_{1},\ldots,x_{k})
in
K
.

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{e}_{t}^{T}\cdot\delta_{k-2}\boldsymbol{f}=(\partial_{k-1}\cdot\boldsymbol{e}_{t})^{T}\cdot\boldsymbol{f}

\displaystyle=(\partial_{k-1}\cdot\boldsymbol{e}_{x_{1},\ldots,x_{k}})^{T}\cdot\boldsymbol{f}

\displaystyle=\left(\sum_{i=1}^{{k}}{(-1)^{i+1}\boldsymbol{e}^{T}_{x_{1}\ldots x_{i-1}x_{i+1}\ldots x_{k}}}\right)\cdot\boldsymbol{f}

\displaystyle=\sum_{i=1}^{{k}}{(-1)^{i+1}f((x_{1},\ldots,x_{i-1},x_{i+1},\ldots,x_{k}))}

\displaystyle=\sum_{i=1}^{{k}}{(-1)^{j+1}\cdot\text{svol}_{k-1}\left(\boldsymbol{0},\boldsymbol{y}_{1},\ldots,\boldsymbol{y}_{j-1},\boldsymbol{y}_{j+1},\ldots,\boldsymbol{y}_{k}\right)}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{(k-1)!}\cdot\sum_{i=1}^{{k}}{(-1)^{j+1}\cdot\det\left(\boldsymbol{y}_{1},\ldots,\boldsymbol{y}_{j-1},\boldsymbol{y}_{j+1},\ldots,\boldsymbol{y}_{k})\right)}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{(k-1)!}\cdot\det\begin{pmatrix}1&1&\ldots&1\\
\boldsymbol{y}_{1}&\boldsymbol{y}_{2}&\ldots&\boldsymbol{y}_{k}\end{pmatrix}

The last equality follows from expanding the determinant using the first (i.e., all-
1
s) row of the matrix on the right-hand side.
Furthermore, by subtracting the first column from all other columns, we have

\displaystyle\det\begin{pmatrix}1&\ldots&1&1\\
\boldsymbol{y}_{1}&\ldots&\boldsymbol{y}_{k-1}&\boldsymbol{y}_{k}\end{pmatrix}

\displaystyle=\det\begin{pmatrix}1&0&\ldots&0\\
\boldsymbol{y}_{1}&\boldsymbol{y}_{2}-\boldsymbol{y}_{1}&\ldots&\boldsymbol{y}_{k}-\boldsymbol{y}_{1}\end{pmatrix}

\displaystyle=\det\left(\boldsymbol{y}_{2}-\boldsymbol{y}_{1},\ldots,\boldsymbol{y}_{k}-\boldsymbol{y}_{1}\right)

\displaystyle=(k-1)!\cdot\text{svol}_{k-1}(\boldsymbol{y}_{1},\ldots,\boldsymbol{y}_{k}).

So, we conclude
|\boldsymbol{e}_{t}^{T}\cdot\delta\boldsymbol{f}|=\text{vol}_{k-1}(\boldsymbol{y}_{1},\ldots,\boldsymbol{y}_{k})=d(t)
, as desired.
∎

We now show how to use
Lemma

5.4
, which shows
\mathcal{V}_{k}^{k-1}\subseteq\mathcal{C}_{k,2}^{1}
, to obtain a general embedding from
\mathcal{V}_{k}^{m}
to
{\cal C}_{k,2}^{m^{\prime}}
with
m^{\prime}=\binom{m}{k-1}
.

Theorem 5.5
.

Any

In this section, we show three results either showing the impossibility of realizing a coboundary metric as a volume metric (i.e., the impossibility of isometrically embedding a coboundary metric space into a volume metric space), or giving a lower bound on the dimension and distortion necessary to embed a coboundary metric space into a volume metric space.

5.3.2
A Lower Bound for Embedding Coboundary
k
-metrics

In this subsection, we show that for every triple of distinct points in
X=\{{\boldsymbol{x}_{1},\ldots,\boldsymbol{x}_{n}}\}\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{m}
to span a triangle of area at least
c_{1}
and at most
c_{2}
for constants
0<c_{1}\leq c_{2}
we must have
m\geq\Omega(\log n)
. From this, we conclude that the discrete
3
-metric, which is a coboundary
3
-metric (
Lemma

5.8
), is not embeddable into a volume
3
-metric space with
m=o(\log n)
dimensions using constant distortion.

The idea behind the proof is as follows.
By rotation and shift invariance of distance and area, we assume without loss of generality that
\boldsymbol{0},\ell\boldsymbol{e}_{1}\in X
, where
\ell>0
is the maximum distance between two points in
X
, and
\boldsymbol{e}_{1}
is the unit vector whose first coordinate is
1
(with all other coordinates equal to
0
). Because each triple of points
\boldsymbol{0},\ell\boldsymbol{e}_{1},\boldsymbol{z}
for
\boldsymbol{z}\in X\setminus\{{\boldsymbol{0},\ell\boldsymbol{e}_{1}}\}
spans a triangle of area between
c_{1}
and
c_{2}
, we have that all such
\boldsymbol{z}
lie in a cylinder of radius
2c_{2}/\ell
whose axis is the line segment
(\boldsymbol{0},\ell\boldsymbol{e}_{1})
.
Then, by applying an appropriate linear transformation
D
to
X
(and implicitly the cylinder containing the points in
X
), we get a constant upper bound on the distance between pairs of points in
DX
and retain a constant lower bound on the area of triangles spanned by distinct triples of points in
DX
. From this, we then additionally get a constant lower bound on
\lVert D(\boldsymbol{x}-\boldsymbol{y})\rVert
for distinct
\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{y}\in X
, from which we derive an upper bound on
n
using a packing argument.

We start with an elementary claim relating the lengths of the sides of a triangle to the area of a triangle.

Claim 5.10
.

Let
m\in\mathbb{Z}^{+}
, let
\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{y},\boldsymbol{z}\in\mathbb{R}^{m}
be distinct, let
T=\conv(\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{y},\boldsymbol{z})
, and let

\displaystyle\mu^{+}:=

\displaystyle\max\{{\lVert\boldsymbol{y}-\boldsymbol{x}\rVert,\lVert\boldsymbol{z}-\boldsymbol{x}\rVert,\lVert\boldsymbol{z}-\boldsymbol{y}\rVert}\}\ \text{,}

\displaystyle\mu^{-}:=

\displaystyle\min\{{\lVert\boldsymbol{y}-\boldsymbol{x}\rVert,\lVert\boldsymbol{z}-\boldsymbol{x}\rVert,\lVert\boldsymbol{z}-\boldsymbol{y}\rVert}\}\ \text{.}

Then
\mu^{-}\geq 2\vol_{2}(T)/\mu^{+}
.

Proof.

Assume without loss of generality that
\mu^{+}=\lVert\boldsymbol{y}-\boldsymbol{x}\rVert
and that
\mu^{-}=\lVert\boldsymbol{z}-\boldsymbol{x}\rVert
. Let
M:=(\boldsymbol{y}-\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{z}-\boldsymbol{x})
. Then

\vol_{2}(T)=\frac{1}{2}\sqrt{\det(M^{T}M)}=\frac{1}{2}(\lVert\boldsymbol{y}-\boldsymbol{x}\rVert^{2}\lVert\boldsymbol{z}-\boldsymbol{x}\rVert^{2}-\langle\boldsymbol{y}-\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{z}-\boldsymbol{x}\rangle^{2})^{1/2}\leq\frac{1}{2}\lVert\boldsymbol{y}-\boldsymbol{x}\rVert\lVert\boldsymbol{z}-\boldsymbol{x}\rVert=\frac{1}{2}\mu^{+}\mu^{-}\ \text{.}

The claim follows by multiplying through by
2/\mu^{+}
.
∎

We now prove our main theorem.

Theorem 5.11
.

Let
X\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{m}
with
n:=\left|{X}\right|
satisfying
3\leq n<\infty
, and suppose that there exist constants
c_{1},c_{2}
satisfying
0<c_{1}\leq c_{2}
such that
c_{1}\leq\vol_{2}(\conv(\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{y},\boldsymbol{z}))\leq c_{2}
for all distinct triples of points
\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{y},\boldsymbol{z}\in X
. Then
m\geq\Omega(\log n)
.

Proof.

Let
\ell:=\max\lVert\boldsymbol{x}-\boldsymbol{y}\rVert
, where the maximum is taken over all distinct pairs of points
\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{y}\in X
, and assume without loss of generality that
\boldsymbol{0},\ell\boldsymbol{e}_{1}\in X
.
Let
D:=\diag(1/\ell,\ell,\ldots,\ell)\in\mathbb{R}^{m\times m}
.
Let
\pi_{1}
denote projection onto
\lspan(\boldsymbol{e}_{1})
and let
\pi_{1}^{\perp}
denote projection onto
\lspan(\boldsymbol{e}_{1})^{\perp}=\lspan(\boldsymbol{e}_{2},\ldots,\boldsymbol{e}_{m})
.

We claim that for all
\boldsymbol{x}\in X
,
\lVert\pi_{1}^{\perp}(\boldsymbol{x})\rVert\leq 2c_{2}/\ell
. The claim is clearly true for
\boldsymbol{x}=\boldsymbol{0}
and
\boldsymbol{x}=\ell\boldsymbol{e}_{1}
, and so assume
\boldsymbol{x}\in X\setminus\{{\boldsymbol{0},\ell\boldsymbol{e}_{1}}\}
. Then using the formula for the area of a triangle,
\vol_{2}(\conv(\boldsymbol{0},\ell\boldsymbol{e}_{1},\boldsymbol{x}))=\ell/2\cdot\lVert\pi_{1}^{\perp}(\boldsymbol{x})\rVert\leq c_{2}
, from which the claim follows by rearranging.

Then for any
\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{y}\in X
,

\displaystyle\begin{split}\lVert D(\boldsymbol{x}-\boldsymbol{y})\rVert^{2}&=\lVert\pi_{1}(D(\boldsymbol{x}-\boldsymbol{y}))\rVert^{2}+\lVert\pi_{1}^{\perp}(D(\boldsymbol{x}-\boldsymbol{y}))\rVert^{2}\\
&=1/\ell^{2}\cdot\lVert\pi_{1}(\boldsymbol{x}-\boldsymbol{y})\rVert^{2}+\ell^{2}\cdot\lVert\pi_{1}^{\perp}(\boldsymbol{x}-\boldsymbol{y})\rVert^{2}\\
&\leq 1/\ell^{2}\cdot\ell^{2}+\ell^{2}\cdot\lVert\pi_{1}^{\perp}(\boldsymbol{x}-\boldsymbol{y})\rVert^{2}\\
&\leq 1/\ell^{2}\cdot\ell^{2}+\ell^{2}\cdot(4c_{2}/\ell)^{2}\\
&=16(c_{2})^{2}+1\ \text{.}\end{split}

(18)

The first inequality uses
\lVert\pi_{1}(\boldsymbol{x}-\boldsymbol{y})\rVert\leq\lVert\boldsymbol{x}-\boldsymbol{y}\rVert\leq\ell
, which follows from the definition of
\ell
. The second inequality uses triangle inequality and the claim above, which implies that
\lVert\pi_{1}^{\perp}(\boldsymbol{x})\rVert\leq 2c_{2}/\ell
and
\lVert\pi_{1}^{\perp}(\boldsymbol{y})\rVert\leq 2c_{2}/\ell
.

Now, let
\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{y},\boldsymbol{z}\in X

\vol_{2}(DT)^{2}=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}I\subseteq[m],\\
\left|{I}\right|=2\end{subarray}}\vol_{2}(\pi_{I}(DT))^{2}=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}I\subseteq[m],\\
\left|{I}\right|=2\end{subarray}}\vol_{2}(D_{I,I}\cdot\pi_{I}(T))^{2}\geq\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}I\subseteq[m],\\
\left|{I}\right|=2\end{subarray}}\vol_{2}(\pi_{I}(T))^{2}=\vol_{2}(T)^{2}\geq(c_{1})^{2}\ \text{,}

(19)

for all

with
\left|{I}\right|=2
.

Combining

Therefore, the scaled, shifted Euclidean balls

10

It follows that the number
