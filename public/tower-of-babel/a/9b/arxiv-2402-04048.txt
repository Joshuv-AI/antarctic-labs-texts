A nodal ghost method based on variational formulation and regular square grid for elliptic problems on arbitrary domains in two space dimensions
Clarissa Astuto, Daniele Boffi, Giovanni Russo, Umberto Zerbinati

Abstract
This paper focuses on the numerical solution of elliptic partial differential equations (PDEs) with Dirichlet and mixed boundary conditions, specifically addressing the challenges arising from irregular domains. Both finite element method (FEM) and finite difference method (FDM), face difficulties in dealing with arbitrary domains. The paper introduces a novel nodal symmetric ghost method based on a variational formulation, which combines the advantages of FEM and FDM. The method employs bilinear finite elements on a structured mesh and provides a detailed implementation description. A rigorous a priori convergence rate analysis is also presented. The convergence rates are validated with many numerical experiments, in both one and two space dimensions.



1
Introduction

Elliptic partial differential equations (PDEs) are used to describe a large variety of physical phenomena with applications ranging from modelling the diffusion of a certain chemical species in a solvent to determining the electric field corresponding to a given charge density.
In the interest of keeping our presentation as streamlined as possible, we will only focus our attention on the simplest elliptic PDE, i.e., the Poisson equation with mixed boundary conditions,

-\Delta u=f\;{\rm in}\;\Omega,\;u=g_{D}\;{\rm on}\;\Gamma_{D}\subset\partial\Omega\;{\rm and}\;\frac{\partial u}{\partial n}=g_{N}\;{\rm on}\;\Gamma_{N}\subset\partial\Omega.

(1)

The previous equation is defined on a domain
\Omega\subset R\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{d}
,
where
R
is a rectangular region, and
d=1,2
is the number of space
dimensions.

When solving (
1
) in two space dimensions, a significant difficulty comes from the arbitrary shape of
\Omega
. Indeed, while for simple geometries, such as a square or a circle, it is possible to find an analytical solution in terms of trigonometric and Bessel functions, for more complex geometries this is not possible and one has to resort to numerical methods.

Among several schemes developed to solve (
1
) on arbitrary domains, two methods of great importance are the finite element method (FEM) and the finite difference method (FDM).

The body-fitted FEM is a very general method that relies on a tessellation of the domain
\Omega
, the construction of “good” tessellation is far from being a trivial task,
[
27
,
28
,
34
,
44
]
.
Since the generation of a mesh for
\Omega
is such a delicate task, using descriptions that are closely related to the geometric data acquisition can dramatically reduce the cost of preprocessing an acquired geometry into representations suitable for the FEM.

For example, the generalization of standard finite elements, known as isogeometric analysis, uses a choice of basis function compatible with the description of the geometry adopted by most CAD software,
[
35
,
26
]
.
Another extremely powerful approaches, known as the CutFEM, are finite element discretizations where the boundary of
\Omega
is represented on a background mesh, which can be of low quality, using a level set function. In the CutFEM the background mesh is also used to represent the numerical solution of (
1
),
[
15
,
36
,
37
,
17
]
.
Since CutFEM does not require the use of a mesh fitted to the body over which we are solving the Poisson equation, it belongs to the realm of the “unfitted” finite element methods.

A hybrid approach between fitted and unfitted finite elements methods is the Fictitious Domain method, first presented in
[
31
]
. In this method, a body-fitted mesh is only constructed for boundary
\partial\Omega
and a background mesh of the computational domain is used to represent the numerical solution.
In the Fictitious Domain method, the boundary conditions are imposed via distributed Lagrange multipliers, similar to
[
10
]
and
[
11
]
.
A comparative study between CutFEM and the Lagrange multiplier method previously discussed can be found in
[
9
]
.

The CutFEM is just one of the methods that makes use of level sets to describe the geometry of the domain. Level set functions in general allow us to describe the geometry of the domain in a very flexible way,
[
41
,
47
,
42
]
.

Not only the FEM can be used in combination with level set techniques to describe the geometry of the domain, but there is also a vast literature of unfitted finite difference schemes
[
30
,
18
,
46
,
20
,
21
,
23
]
, just to mention a few.
A common feature of most unfitted FDMs previously mentioned is the addition of ghost points, i.e. points that are not part of the domain
\Omega
but are used to impose the boundary conditions and retrieve the optimal order of convergence. We can find many applications in
[
22
,
5
,
1
,
3
,
4
]
.

While the unfitted FDM, compared to unfitted FEM, might be easier to implement and understand for a novice user, these finite difference schemes have the disadvantage of preserving less structural properties of the continuous problem, such as the symmetry and the positive definiteness of the stiffness matrix.
In the present paper, we aim to introduce ideas from the unfitted FEM and FDM literature and introduce the reader to a nodal ghost finite element method for elliptic problems on irregular domains with mixed boundary conditions. An explicit comparison is shown in
[
2
]
.

The choice of bilinear finite elements on a structured mesh yields a similar stencil to the one typical of FDM, allowing a much more natural and straightforward implementation for a novice user.

To achieve this goal, the paper is structured as follows: we begin deriving the variational formulation of (
1
) in the one-dimensional case and describe our nodal symmetric ghost point method for this simple case. We then proceed to extend our nodal symmetric ghost point method to the two-dimensional case.
In Section
5
, we provide a detailed description of the implementation aspects of the scheme we propose. In particular, we discuss the exact quadrature of the boundary integral, which is one of the main novelties of our work. In Section
6
, we prove a priori convergence rate for the proposed scheme. Furthermore, to the best of our knowledge, we give for the first time a rigorous treatment of the snapping back to grid perturbation. Lastly, we validate the method and the a priori estimates proved via extensive numerical experiments in both one and two space dimensions.

We hope that our treatment of the proposed nodal symmetric ghost point method, which starts from the most basic concepts and builds up to a more complicated scenario, will facilitate the novice reader’s understating of our scheme.

2
The continuous one-dimensional problem

In this section, we will introduce the reader to the variational formulation of the Poisson equation with Dirichlet and mixed boundary conditions in one dimension.
While we know these ideas are well known to the reader familiar with the finite element literature, we decided to present them anyway to make the paper as self-contained as possible for the novice reader.

Let us start our discussion from the Poisson equation with homogeneous Dirichlet conditions at the boundary defined
in a domain
\Omega=[a,b]\subset R=[0,1]
:

-u^{\prime\prime}(x)=f(x),\quad x\in[a,b],\quad u(a)=0,\>u(b)=0.

(2)

Multiplying the above equation by a test function
{v\in V=H^{1}_{0}([a,b])}
, provides the following variational
formulation of the problem:

\int_{a}^{b}\left[u^{\prime}(x)v^{\prime}(x)-f(x)v(x)\right]\,dx=u^{\prime}(b)v(b)-u^{\prime}(a)v(a),\quad\forall v\in H^{1}_{0}([a,b])

(3)

where
H^{1}_{0}([a,b])
is the standard Sobolev space with homogeneous trace at the boundary
[
13
]
.
Thanks to this condition, the right-hand side of the equation vanishes,
leading to the formulation:

\int_{a}^{b}\left[u^{\prime}(x)v^{\prime}(x)-f(x)v(x)\right]\,dx=0,\quad\forall v\in H^{1}_{0}([a,b])

(4)

The variational formulation can be obtained by setting to zero the first variation of the functional

J_{D0}[u]=\int_{a}^{b}\left[\frac{1}{2}(u^{\prime}(x))^{2}\,dx-f(x)u(x)\right]\,dx,

(5)

as can be immediately verified by performing the first variation of the functional by replacing

u
by
u+\varepsilon v
, with
u
and
v
in
H^{1}_{0}([a,b])
, and imposing

\left.\frac{d}{d\varepsilon}J_{D0}[u+\varepsilon v]\right|_{\varepsilon=0}=0\quad\forall v\in H^{1}_{0}([a,b]).

(6)

In particular, we notice that since
J_{D0}
is a convex functional, looking for functions for which the first variation of
J_{D0}
vanishes is equivalent to minimizing the functional.

A different way to approximately impose zero Dirichlet conditions on the boundary is to penalise any
discrepancy from zero. This is obtained by adding a penalisation term to the functional:

J_{D0\lambda}[u]=\int_{a}^{b}\left[\frac{1}{2}(u^{\prime}(x))^{2}\,dx-f(x)u(x)\right]\,dx+\frac{\lambda}{2}[u(a)^{2}+u(b)^{2}].

(7)

where
\lambda
is a positive penalty parameter
[
16
]
that will be discussed in Section
6
, and it is chosen proportional to a power of the space step.

Notice that the energy functional
J_{D0\lambda}
corresponds to (
2
) where the Dirichlet boundary conditions are replaced by Robin boundary conditions.
It is a well-established fact that as the penalisation grows to infinity it will force zero Dirichlet conditions as

\lambda\to\infty

[
24
,
25
]
.
When integrating by parts, we obtain the following variational formulation of the problem:

\displaystyle\int_{a}^{b}\left[u^{\prime}(x)v^{\prime}(x)-f(x)v(x)\right]\,dx

\displaystyle+[\lambda u(a)+u^{\prime}(a)]v(a)

(8)

\displaystyle+[\lambda u(b)-u^{\prime}(b)]v(b)=0,\quad\forall v\in H^{1}([a,b])

where now we do not need to impose that
v
is zero on the boundary.
Notice that the variational formulation (
8
) is the starting point for the penalty finite element proposed in
[
6
,
7
]
and analyzed in
[
38
,
8
]
.

If we want to impose non-homogeneous Dirichlet conditions, say we impose
u(a)=u_{a}
,
u(b)=u_{b}
, we have to consider the functional

J_{D\lambda}[u]=\int_{a}^{b}\left[\frac{1}{2}(u^{\prime}(x))^{2}-f(x)u(x)\right]\,dx+\frac{\lambda}{2}[(u(a)-u_{a})^{2}+(u(b)-u_{b})^{2}]

(9)

which leads to the following variational formulation:

\begin{split}\int_{a}^{b}\left[u^{\prime}(x)v^{\prime}(x)\right.&\left.-f(x)v(x)\right]\,dx+[\lambda(u(a)-u_{a})+u^{\prime}(a)]v(a)\\
&+[\lambda(u(b)-u_{b})-u^{\prime}(b)]v(b)=0,\quad\forall v\in H^{1}([a,b])\end{split}

(10)

The variational formulation (
10
) is non-symmetric, because of the term
\displaystyle\left.(u^{\prime}v)\right|_{a}^{b}
.
We will now address this issue by introducing an alternative formulation, which is equivalent at a continuous level and is obtained as follows.
We add and subtract the quantity
\displaystyle\left.(uv^{\prime})\right|_{a}^{b}
:

\int_{a}^{b}u^{\prime}(x)v^{\prime}(x)\,dx-\left.(u^{\prime}v)\right|_{a}^{b}-\left.(uv^{\prime})\right|_{a}^{b}=\int_{a}^{b}f(x)v(x)\,dx-u_{b}v^{\prime}(b)+u_{a}v^{\prime}(a)

(11)

where we made use of the Dirichlet boundary conditions in
a
and
b
on the right-hand side.

The coercivity of the operator may be improved by adding a penalisation term, therefore for continuous formulation can be written as

\displaystyle\int_{a}^{b}u^{\prime}(x)v^{\prime}(x)\,dx-\left.(u^{\prime}v)\right|_{a}^{b}-\left.(uv^{\prime})\right|_{a}^{b}+

\displaystyle\lambda(u(a)-u_{a})v(a)+\lambda(u(b)-u_{b})v(b)

\displaystyle=\int_{a}^{b}f(x)v(x)\,dx-u_{b}v^{\prime}(b)+u_{a}v^{\prime}(a)

(12)

and bringing to the right-hand side the known term, we have

\displaystyle\int_{a}^{b}u^{\prime}(x)v^{\prime}(x)\,dx-\left.(u^{\prime}v)\right|_{a}^{b}-\left.(uv^{\prime})\right|_{a}^{b}+\lambda u(a)v(a)+\lambda u(b)v(b)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\int_{a}^{b}f(x)v(x)\,dx-u_{b}v^{\prime}(b)+u_{a}v^{\prime}(a)+\lambda u_{a}v(a)+\lambda u_{b}v(b)

(13)

It is trivial to obtain the analogous variational formulation for the Neumann problem, in fact, if we consider

-u^{\prime\prime}(x)=f(x),\quad x\in[a,b]\qquad u^{\prime}(a)=g_{a},\quad u^{\prime}(b)=g_{b},

(14)

we can multiply by a test function
v\in H^{1}[a,b]
, integrate over
[a,b]
and integrate by parts to see that the solution of (
14
)
is

u\in H^{1}[a,b]
such that

\int_{a}^{b}u^{\prime}(x)v^{\prime}(x)\,dx=\int_{a}^{b}f(x)v(x)\,dx+g_{b}v(b)-g_{b}av(a)\quad\forall v\in H^{1}([a,b]).

(15)

The only aspects we need to be careful when considering a variational formulation for the Neumann problem is that a compatibility condition has to be verified by
f\in L^{2}([a,b])
and we need to choose a unique solution in the kernel of the gradient, imposing a null-average condition, i.e.

\int_{a}^{b}f\,dx=g_{b}-g_{a},\qquad\int_{a}^{b}u\,dx=0.

Let us now consider the following mixed problem:

-u^{\prime\prime}(x)=f(x),\quad x\in[a,b]\qquad u(a)=0,\quad u^{\prime}(b)=g_{b}

(16)

We shall look for solutions of
(
16
) in
H^{1}([a,b])
, respecting the boundary conditions.
Multiplying by a test function

H^{1}_{a}([a,b]):=\left\{v\in H^{1}([a,b])\;:\;v(a)=0\right\},

and integrating by parts in
[a,b]
one
obtains:

\int_{a}^{b}u^{\prime}(x)v^{\prime}(x)\,dx-\left.(u^{\prime}v)\right|_{a}^{b}=\int_{a}^{b}f(x)v(x)\,dx.

Now, making use of the boundary condition in
b
and the fact that for any
v\in H^{1}_{a}([a,b])

v(a)=0
, we obtain
the following variational formulation of problem (
16
):

\int_{a}^{b}u^{\prime}(x)v^{\prime}(x)\,dx=\int_{a}^{b}f(x)v(x)\,dx+g_{b}v(b)\quad\forall v\in H^{1}_{a}([a,b]).

(17)

This equation can be obtained by looking for the function
u\in H^{1}_{a}([a,b])
that minimizes the following functional:

J[u]=\frac{1}{2}\int_{a}^{b}[(u^{\prime}(x))^{2}\,dx-f(x)u(x)]\,dx+g_{b}u(b)

(18)

Indeed, by equating to zero the first variation of the functional, i.e. imposing

\left.\frac{d}{d\varepsilon}J[u+\varepsilon v]\right|_{\varepsilon=0}=0

\forall v\in H^{1}_{a}([a,b])
, gives exactly (
17
).
As in the case of the Dirichlet problem, we may impose an approximate Dirichlet condition on the left hand boundary by penalising the functional as follows:

J[u]=\int_{a}^{b}\left[\frac{1}{2}(u^{\prime}(x))^{2}\,dx-f(x)u(x)\right]\,dx+g_{b}u(b)+\frac{\lambda}{2}(u(a)-u_{a})^{2},\quad u\in H^{1}([a,b])

which leads to the following variational formulation of the problem in
H^{1}([a,b])
:

\int_{a}^{b}u^{\prime}(x)v^{\prime}(x)\,dx+[\lambda(u(a)-u_{a})+u^{\prime}(a)]v(a)=\int_{a}^{b}f(x)v(x)\,dx+g_{b}v(b).

(19)

As before, we look for a term that symmetries (
19
). For this reason, we add and subtract the term
u(a)v^{\prime}(a)
, making use of the Dirichlet boundary condition on
a

\displaystyle\int_{a}^{b}u^{\prime}(x)v^{\prime}(x)\,dx+[\lambda(u(a)-u_{a})+u^{\prime}(a)]v(a)+u(a)v^{\prime}(a)

(20)

\displaystyle=\int_{a}^{b}f(x)v(x)\,dx+u_{a}v^{\prime}(a)+g_{b}v(b)

and bringing to the right-hand side the known terms, we have

\displaystyle\int_{a}^{b}u^{\prime}(x)v^{\prime}(x)\,dx+\lambda u(a)v(a)+u^{\prime}(a)v(a)+u(a)v^{\prime}(a)

(21)

\displaystyle=\int_{a}^{b}f(x)v(x)\,dx+\lambda u_{a}v(a)+u_{a}v^{\prime}(a)+g_{b}v(b).

The technique we used to symmetrise the variational problem is known as the Nitsche technique
[
40
]
.

3
The discrete one-dimensional problem

The region
R=[0,1]
is discretized by a uniform mesh of grid size
h=1/N
.
Without loss of generality, we assume that
a\in[0,h]
, and
b\in[1-h,1]
. If
a=0
and
b=1
, standard finite element discretization in the interval
[0,1]
can be used.

We denote by
\theta_{1}=1-a/h
and
\theta_{2}={1-(1-b)/h}
, so that
\theta_{1},\theta_{2}\in[0,1]
.
We denote by
I_{j}=[j-1,j]h
, with
j=1,\ldots,N
, the
j
-th interval of the
subdivision.
We consider the space
V_{h}
of dimension
N+1
as the space of the splines
S_{1}

on the mesh
\{I_{j}\}_{i=1}^{N}
.
As basis for the space
V_{h}
we choose the functions
\varphi_{i}(x)
with
i=0,\ldots,N,

defined as

\varphi_{i}(x)=\max\{1-|x-x_{i}|/h,0\},\quad x\in[0,1],\quad i=0,\ldots,N.

(22)

Fig.
1
provides a schematic representation of the discretization setup.

A generic function
u_{h}\in V_{h}
can be expressed using the basis
\{\varphi_{i}\}_{i=1}^{N}
as follows:

u_{h}(x)=\sum_{j=1}^{N}u_{j}\varphi_{j}(x)

(23)

Discretization setup in 1D: the region
R=[0,1]\supset[a,b]=\Omega
, and the basis function
\varphi_{j},j=0,\ldots,N
, defined in (
22
).

We now make use of the conformity of the discrete space, i.e.,
V_{h}\subset H^{1}_{0}([a,b])
, to obtain a discrete variational problem, by considering (
10
) with both test and trial functions in
V_{h}
:

\begin{split}&\int_{a}^{b}u_{h}^{\prime}(x)\varphi_{i}^{\prime}(x)\,dx-\int_{a}^{b}f(x)\varphi_{i}(x)\,dx+\lambda(u_{h}(a)-u_{a})\varphi_{i}(a)+u_{h}^{\prime}(a)\varphi_{i}(a)\\
&+u_{h}(a)\varphi_{i}^{\prime}(a)+\lambda(u_{h}(b)-u_{b})\varphi_{i}(b)-u_{h}^{\prime}(b)\varphi_{i}(b)-u_{h}(b)\varphi_{i}^{\prime}(b)=0,\quad i=0,\ldots,N\end{split}

By replacing
u_{h}
with its representation (
23
), we obtain the following system of
equations for the unknowns
u_{i}
,
i=1,\ldots,N
:

\displaystyle\sum_{j=0}^{N}u_{j}\underbrace{(\varphi_{j}^{\prime},\varphi_{i}^{\prime})_{L^{2}([a,b])}}_{\rm S}+\sum_{j=0}^{N}u_{j}\underbrace{\left[\varphi_{j}^{\prime}(a)\varphi_{i}(a)+\varphi_{j}(a)\varphi_{i}^{\prime}(a)-\varphi_{j}^{\prime}(b)\varphi_{i}(b)-\varphi_{j}(b)\varphi_{i}^{\prime}(b)\right]}_{\rm S_{T}}

\displaystyle+\lambda\sum_{j=0}^{N}u_{j}\left(\underbrace{\varphi_{j}(a)\varphi_{i}(a)}_{\rm P_{a}}+\underbrace{\varphi_{j}(b)\varphi_{i}(b)}_{\rm P_{b}}\right)

(24)

\displaystyle=\sum_{j=0}^{N}f_{j}\underbrace{(\varphi_{j},\varphi_{i})_{L^{2}([a,b])}}_{\rm M}\,dx+\sum_{j=0}^{N}{u_{a}}\underbrace{\varphi_{j}(a)\varphi_{i}^{\prime}(a)}_{\rm D_{a}}+\sum_{j=0}^{N}{u_{b}}\underbrace{\varphi_{j}(b)\varphi_{i}^{\prime}(b)}_{\rm D_{b}}+

(25)

\displaystyle+\lambda\left(\sum_{j=0}^{N}{u_{a}}\underbrace{\varphi_{j}(a)\varphi_{i}(a)}_{\rm P_{a}}+\sum_{j=0}^{N}{u_{b}}\underbrace{\varphi_{j}(b)\varphi_{i}(b)}_{\rm P_{b}}\right),\quad i=0,\ldots,N

(26)

and in matrix form it becomes

\sum_{j=0}^{N}a^{D}_{ij}u_{j}=f^{D}_{i},\quad i=0,\ldots,N

(27)

where
A^{D}=\{a^{D}_{i,j}\}
and
F^{D}=\{f^{D}_{i}\}
, and

\begin{array}[]{c}\displaystyle A^{D}={\rm S+S_{T}+}\lambda\,\left({\rm P_{a}+P_{b}}\right)\\
\displaystyle F^{D}={\rm M}f+\left({\rm D_{a}}+\lambda{\rm P_{a}}\right)u_{a}+\left({\rm D_{b}}+\lambda{\rm P_{b}}\right)u_{b}\end{array}

(28)

where the matrices
{\rm A^{D},M,D_{a},D_{b},P_{a},P_{b}}\in\mathbb{R}^{(N+1)\times(N+1)}
.

We now turn our attention to the mixed Dirichlet-Neumann problem (
16
).
We consider the penalised variational formulation in (
21
)
and look for a solution in
V_{h}
.
Replacing
u
by
u_{h}
and
v
by
\varphi_{j}
in (
23
), we obtain the system

\displaystyle\int_{a}^{b}u_{h}^{\prime}(x)\varphi_{i}^{\prime}(x)\,dx+u_{h}^{\prime}(a)\varphi_{i}(a)+u_{h}(a)\varphi_{i}^{\prime}(a)+\lambda u_{h}(a)\varphi_{i}(a)

(29)

\displaystyle=\int_{a}^{b}f_{h}(x)\varphi_{i}(x)\,dx+{u_{a}}\varphi_{i}^{\prime}(a)+{g_{b}}_{h}\varphi_{i}(b)+\lambda{u_{a}}\varphi_{i}(a),\quad\forall i=1,\cdots,N

and if we use the expression in (
23
) for the function
u_{h}
, it becomes

\displaystyle\sum_{j=0}^{N}u_{j}\underbrace{(\varphi_{j}^{\prime},\varphi_{i}^{\prime})_{L^{2}([a,b])}}_{\rm S}+\sum_{j=0}^{N}u_{j}\underbrace{\left[\varphi_{j}^{\prime}(a)\varphi_{i}(a)+\varphi_{j}(a)\varphi_{i}^{\prime}(a)\right]}_{\rm S_{T}}+\lambda\sum_{j=0}^{N}u_{j}\underbrace{\varphi_{j}(a)\varphi_{i}(a)}_{\rm P_{a}}

\displaystyle=\sum_{j=0}^{N}f_{j}\underbrace{(\varphi_{j},\varphi_{i})_{L^{2}([a,b])}}_{\rm M}\,dx+\sum_{j=0}^{N}{u_{a}}\underbrace{\varphi_{j}\varphi_{i}^{\prime}(a)}_{\rm D_{a}}+\sum_{j=0}^{N}{g_{b}}_{j}\underbrace{\varphi_{j}(b)\varphi_{i}(b)}_{\rm N_{b}}

which leads to the following system of equations for

where

, and

\begin{array}[]{c}\displaystyle A^{M}={\rm S+S_{T}+}\lambda\,{\rm P_{a}}\\

The non-zero values of the stiffness matrices

and

, and of the source terms

4
The two-dimensional problem

In this section, we introduce the two-dimensional problem and define our numerical scheme based on finite-element discretization. We begin by imposing Dirichlet boundary conditions on the elliptic equation and then extend our discussion to include Neumann and mixed boundary conditions. Detailed derivations of the spatial discretization are provided in
A
.

Let us consider now the Poisson equation with Dirichlet boundary conditions in
\Omega\subseteq R\subset\mathbb{R}^{2}
, where
R
is a rectangular region:

-\Delta u=f\quad{\rm in}\>\Omega,\quad u=g_{D}\>{\rm on}\>\partial\Omega

(33)

Defining the space of test functions

V=\{v\in H^{1}(\Omega)\},

multiplying Eq.(
33
) by
v\in V
, integrating in
\Omega
and then integrating by parts, one obtains:

\int_{\Omega}\nabla u\cdot\nabla v\,d\Omega=\int_{\Omega}fv\,d\Omega+\int_{\partial\Omega}v\frac{\partial u}{\partial n}\,ds.

(34)

The discretization of the problem is obtained by discretizing the rectangular region
R\subset\mathbb{R}^{2}
, containing the domain
\Omega
, by a regular rectangular grid (see Figure
3
).

Two dimensional problem domain

:

, with Dirichlet and Neumann boundary conditions, respectively.

Following the approach shown in
[
47
,
41
,
42
,

]
, the domain
\Omega
is implicitly defined by a level set function
\phi(x,y)
that is negative inside
\Omega
, positive outside and zero on the boundary
\Gamma
:

\displaystyle\Omega=\{(x,y):\phi(x,y)<0\},\qquad\Gamma=\{(x,y):\phi(x,y)=0\}.

The unit normal vector
n
in (
42
) can be computed as
n=\frac{\nabla\phi}{|\nabla\phi|}
.
For example, for a circular domain centered in
(x_{c},y_{c})
, the most convenient level-set function in terms of numerical stability is the signed distance function between
(x,y)
and
\Gamma
, i.e.,
\phi=r-\sqrt{(x-x_{c})^{2}+(y-y_{c})^{2}}
, where
r
is the radius of the circle.

The set of grid points will be denoted by
\mathcal{N}
, with
\#\mathcal{N}=(1+N)^{2}
, the active nodes (i.e., internal
\mathcal{I}
or ghost
\mathcal{G}
) by
\mathcal{I}\cup\mathcal{G}={\mathcal{A}}\subset\mathcal{N}
, while the set of inactive points by
\mathcal{O}\subset\mathcal{N}
, with
\mathcal{O}\cup\mathcal{A}=\mathcal{N}
and
\mathcal{O}\cap\mathcal{A}=\emptyset
and the set of cells by
\mathcal{C}

6
A priori convergence analysis

In this section, we will focus our attention on developing a priori error estimates for the scheme described in the paper.

We will begin the analysis of the method under the following geometrical constraint on the boundary
\partial\Omega
:

\forall K\in\{\mathcal{T}_{h}\}_{h>0}\quad\widehat{C}_{\Omega}h^{\alpha}\leq\lvert\partial\Omega\cap K\rvert\leq\widehat{C}_{\Omega}h^{\gamma},

(53)

with
\gamma<\alpha
. In principle
\gamma
could be arbitrarily small or even negative and
\alpha
could be arbitrarily large.
Yet we are interested in the case when
\partial\Omega\cap K
is comparable to the edges of the cell, which translates to
\gamma=1
.
Furthermore, we are interested in allowing
\partial\Omega\cap K
to have smaller length than the edges of the cell, but not too small, i.e.
\alpha=2
.
While general values of
\alpha
and
\gamma
are considered in the analysis, our work has as main focus the case when
\alpha\sim 2
and
\gamma\sim 1
.

The analysis here presented will follow closely
[
37
]
.
To lighten the notation we introduce the discrete bilinear form
a_{h,\alpha}:H^{\frac{3}{2}+\varepsilon}(\Omega)\times H^{\frac{3}{2}+\varepsilon}(\Omega)\to\mathbb{R}
corresponding to (
A
), i.e.,

a_{h,\alpha}(u,v):=(\nabla u,\nabla v)_{L^{2}(\Omega)}+C_{a}h^{-\alpha}(u,v)_{L^{2}(\Gamma_{D})}-(\partial_{n}u,v)_{L^{2}(\Gamma_{D})}-(u,\partial_{n}v)_{L^{2}(\Gamma_{D})}.

(54)

To study the well-posedness of the discrete variational problem: find
u_{h}\in V_{h}
such that

a_{h,\alpha}(u_{h},v_{h}):=(f,v_{h})_{L^{2}(\Omega)}+(g_{N},v_{h})_{L^{2}(\Gamma_{N})}-(g_{D},\partial_{n}v_{h})_{L^{2}(\Gamma_{D})},\qquad v_{h}\in V_{h}

(55)

we will consider an auxiliary eigenvalue problem, i.e., find
(w_{h},\lambda)\in V_{h}\times\mathbb{R}
such that

b(w_{h},z_{h}):=(\partial_{n}w_{h},\partial z_{h})_{L^{2}(\partial\Omega)}=\lambda a(w_{h},z_{h}),\qquad\forall z_{h}\in V_{h}.

(56)

We characterize the first eigenvalue problem using the Rayleigh quotient we have,

\lambda=\underset{z_{h}\in V_{h}}{\max}\frac{b(z_{h},z_{h})}{a(z_{h},z_{h})}=\underset{z_{h}\in V_{h}}{\max}\lVert\partial_{n}z_{h}\rVert_{L^{2}(\partial\Omega)}^{2}\lVert\nabla z_{h}\rVert^{-2}_{L^{2}(\partial\Omega)}.

(57)

The above characterization ensures that for any
v_{h}\in V_{h}
the following estimate holds

\lVert\partial_{n}v_{h}\rVert^{2}_{L^{2}(\Omega)}\leq\lambda\lVert\nabla v_{h}\rVert_{L^{2}(\Omega)}^{2}.

(58)

We will now bound
\lambda
in terms of the mesh size, this will be useful to prove the coercivity of the bilinear form
a_{h}(\cdot,\cdot)
for a sufficiently large value of
\alpha
.
The following lemma is an adaptation of
[
29
, Lemma 12.8]
.

Lemma 6.1
.

Let
V_{h}
be the space of bilinear functions on each element
K
of
\mathcal{T}_{h}
, then

\lVert\partial_{n}u_{h}\rVert^{2}_{L^{2}(\partial\Omega\cap K)}\leq C_{2}h^{\gamma-{\alpha}-1}\lVert\nabla u_{h}\rVert_{L^{2}(K)}^{2},\qquad\forall v_{h}\in V_{h},

(59)

provided that the following geometric assumption is verified by
\mathcal{T}_{h}
:

\widehat{C}_{\Omega}h^{\alpha}\leq\lvert\partial\Omega\cap K\rvert\leq C_{\Omega}h^{\gamma},\quad{\forall K\in\mathcal{T}_{h}.}

(60)

Proof.

We will obtain this estimate via a scaling argument, for this reason, we consider the map from the physical element
K
to the reference element
\widehat{K}
, i.e.,
F_{K}:K\to\widehat{K}
.
Notice that since we are working with a structured mesh the map
F_{K}:K\to\widehat{K}
is an affine map, i.e.,
F_{K}(\mathbf{x})=\mathbb{A}\mathbf{x}+\mathbf{b}
.
We then consider the pullback of
v_{h}
, i.e.,

\widehat{v}_{h}(\widehat{\mathbf{x}}):=v_{h}\left(F_{K}^{-1}(\widehat{\mathbf{x}})\right):\widehat{K}\to\mathbb{R}.

(61)

We can then compute via a scaling argument the following bound,

\displaystyle\lVert\partial_{n}u_{h}\rVert_{L^{2}(\partial\Omega\cap K)}

\displaystyle=\lVert\nabla u_{h}\cdot n\rVert_{L^{2}(\partial\Omega\cap K)}\leq\lVert\mathbf{n}\rVert_{L^{\infty}(\partial\Omega\cap K)}\lVert\nabla u_{h}\rVert_{L^{2}(\partial\Omega\cap K)}

(62)

\displaystyle\leq\lVert\mathbb{A}\rVert^{-1}\left(\frac{\lvert\partial\Omega\cap K\rvert}{\lvert F_{K}(\partial\Omega\cap K)\rvert}\right)^{\frac{1}{2}}\lVert\nabla\widehat{u}_{h}\rVert_{L^{2}\left(F_{K}(\partial\Omega\cap K)\right)}.

(63)

We now notice that since all norms in finite dimension are equivalent there exists a constant
C_{1}
such that

\lVert\nabla\widehat{u}_{h}\rVert_{L^{2}\left(F_{K}(\partial\Omega\cap K)\right)}\leq C_{1}\lVert\nabla\widehat{u}_{h}\rVert_{L^{2}(\widehat{K})},

(64)

where
C_{1}
may depend on
\alpha
and
\gamma
but it does not depend on
h
because we are working on the reference element.
Combining (
64
) with (
62
) we have

\displaystyle\lVert\partial_{n}u_{h}\rVert_{L^{2}(\partial\Omega\cap K)}

\displaystyle\leq C_{1}\lVert\mathbb{A}\rVert^{-1}\left(\frac{\lvert\partial\Omega\cap K\rvert}{\lvert F_{K}(\partial\Omega\cap K)\rvert}\right)^{\frac{1}{2}}\lVert\nabla\widehat{u}_{h}\rVert_{L^{2}(\widehat{K})}

(65)

\displaystyle\leq C_{1}\lVert\mathbb{A}\rVert^{-1}\lVert\mathbb{A}\rVert\left(\frac{\lvert\partial\Omega\cap K\rvert}{\lvert F_{K}(\partial\Omega\cap K)\rvert}\right)^{\frac{1}{2}}\left(\frac{\lvert\widehat{K}\rvert}{\lvert K\rvert}\right)^{\frac{1}{2}}\lVert\nabla u_{h}\rVert_{L^{2}(K)}

(66)

\displaystyle\leq C_{1}\left(\frac{\lvert\widehat{K}\rvert}{\lvert F_{K}(\partial\Omega\cap K)\rvert}\right)^{\frac{1}{2}}\left(\frac{\lvert\partial\Omega\cap K\rvert}{\lvert K\rvert}\right)^{\frac{1}{2}}\lVert\nabla u_{h}\rVert_{L^{2}(K)}

(67)

We proceed bounding
\left(\frac{\lvert\widehat{K}\rvert}{\lvert F_{K}(\partial\Omega\cap K)\rvert}\right)^{\frac{1}{2}}\left(\frac{\lvert\partial\Omega\cap K\rvert}{\lvert K\rvert}\right)^{\frac{1}{2}}
using (
60
).
We begin observing that
\widehat{K}
has a unit area and that from (
60

\lvert F_{K}(\partial\Omega\cap K)\rvert\geq\widehat{C}_{\Omega}h^{{\alpha}-1}\;\Rightarrow\;\lvert F_{K}(\partial\Omega\cap K)\rvert^{-1}\leq\widehat{C}_{\Omega}^{-1}h^{1-{\alpha}}.

(68)

Furthermore
\lvert K\rvert=h^{2}
and from (
60
) we know that

\frac{\lvert\partial\Omega\cap K\rvert}{\lvert K\rvert}\leq C_{\Omega}h^{\gamma-2}.

(69)

Combining (
68
), (
69

67
) we have,

\displaystyle\lVert\partial_{n}u_{h}\rVert_{L^{2}(\partial\Omega\cap K)}

\displaystyle\leq C_{1}\left(\frac{\lvert\widehat{K}\rvert}{\lvert F_{K}(\partial\Omega\cap K)\rvert}\right)^{\frac{1}{2}}\left(\frac{\lvert\partial\Omega\cap K\rvert}{\lvert K\rvert}\right)^{\frac{1}{2}}\lVert\nabla u_{h}\rVert_{L^{2}(K)}

(71)

\lVert\cdot\rVert_{h,\alpha}^{2}=\lvert\cdot\rvert_{H^{1}(\Omega)}^{2}+h^{-\alpha}C_{a}\lVert\cdot\rVert^{2}_{L^{2}(\Gamma_{D})}+C_{a}^{-1}h^{\alpha}\lVert\partial_{n}\cdot\rVert^{2}_{L^{2}(\Gamma_{D})},

Lemma 6.2
.

The bilinear form
a_{h,\alpha}:H^{\frac{3}{2}+\varepsilon}(\Omega)\times H^{\frac{3}{2}+\varepsilon}(\Omega)\to\mathbb{R}
, defined as in (

, with respect to
\lVert\cdot\rVert_{h,\alpha}
, provided we choose
C_{a}=\frac{1}{2}C_{2}^{-1}
and

Proof.

We begin proving coercivity. We have

\displaystyle a_{h}(v_{h},v_{h})

\displaystyle=(\nabla v_{h},\nabla v_{h})_{L^{2}(\Omega)}+C_{a}h^{-\alpha}(v_{h},v_{h})_{L^{2}(\Gamma_{D})}-2(\partial_{n}v_{h},v_{h})_{L^{2}(\Gamma_{D})}

(73)

\displaystyle=\lvert v_{h}\rvert^{2}_{H^{1}(\Omega)}+C_{a}h^{-\alpha}\lVert v_{h}\rVert^{2}_{L^{2}(\Gamma_{D})}-2(C_{a}^{-\frac{\alpha}{2}}h^{\frac{\alpha}{2}}\partial_{n}v_{h},C_{a}^{\frac{\alpha}{2}}h^{-\frac{\alpha}{2}}v_{h})_{L^{2}(\Gamma_{D})}

(74)

\displaystyle\geq\lvert v_{h}\rvert^{2}_{H^{1}(\Omega)}+(1-\varepsilon)C_{a}^{\alpha}h^{-\alpha}\lVert v_{h}\rVert^{2}_{L^{2}(\Gamma_{D})}-\varepsilon^{-1}C_{a}^{-\alpha}h^{\alpha}\lVert\partial_{n}v_{h}\rVert^{2}_{L^{2}(\Gamma_{D})},

(75)

where the last inequality comes from Young’s inequality with
\varepsilon\in(0,1)
.
We add and subtract
\varepsilon\lvert v_{h}\rvert_{H^{1}(\Omega)}^{2}
, fix
\gamma\geq 1
and use (
59
) to control from below
\varepsilon\lvert v_{h}\rvert_{H^{1}(\Omega)}^{2}
, i.e.,

\displaystyle a_{h,\alpha}(v_{h},v_{h})

\displaystyle\geq\lvert v_{h}\rvert^{2}_{H^{1}(\Omega)}+(1-\varepsilon)C_{a}^{\alpha}h^{-\alpha}\lVert v_{h}\rVert^{2}_{L^{2}(\Gamma_{D})}-\varepsilon^{-1}C^{-\alpha}_{a}h^{\alpha}\lVert\partial_{n}v_{h}\rVert^{2}_{L^{2}(\Gamma_{D})}

(76)

\displaystyle\geq(1-\varepsilon)\lvert v_{h}\rvert^{2}_{H^{1}(\Omega)}+(1-\varepsilon)C_{a}^{\alpha}h^{-\alpha}\lVert v_{h}\rVert^{2}_{L^{2}(\Gamma_{D})}

(77)

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad+(2\varepsilon-\varepsilon^{-1})C_{a}^{-\alpha}h^{\alpha}\lVert\partial_{n}v_{h}\rVert^{2}_{L^{2}(\Gamma_{D})}\geq C_{3}\lVert v_{h}\rVert_{h,\alpha},

(78)

where we choose
C_{a}=\frac{1}{2}C_{2}^{-1}
, and
C_{3}=(1-\varepsilon^{*})
, with
\varepsilon^{*}
being the positive root of
1-\varepsilon={2}\varepsilon-\varepsilon^{-1}
. In particular, notice that
C_{3}\geq\frac{1}{5}
.
We now proceed to prove the continuity of the bilinear form for
u,v\in H^{\frac{3}{2}+\varepsilon}(\Omega)
. We have

\displaystyle a_{h,\alpha}(u,v)

\displaystyle\leq\lvert u\rvert_{H^{1}(\Omega)}\lvert v\rvert_{H^{1}(\Omega)}

\displaystyle+\lVert h^{\frac{\alpha}{2}}C_{a}^{-\frac{\alpha}{2}}\partial_{n}u\rVert_{L^{2}(\Gamma_{D})}\lVert h^{-\frac{\alpha}{2}}C_{a}^{\frac{\alpha}{2}}v\rVert_{L^{2}(\Gamma_{D})}

(79)

\displaystyle+\lVert h^{\frac{\alpha}{2}}C_{a}^{-\frac{\alpha}{2}}\partial_{n}v\rVert_{L^{2}(\Gamma_{D})}\lVert h^{-\frac{\alpha}{2}}C_{a}^{\frac{\alpha}{2}}u\rVert_{L^{2}(\Gamma_{D})}

(80)

\displaystyle+\lVert h^{\frac{\alpha}{2}}C_{a}^{-\frac{\alpha}{2}}u\rVert_{L^{2}(\Gamma_{D})}\lVert h^{\frac{\alpha}{2}}C_{a}^{-\frac{\alpha}{2}}v\rVert_{L^{2}(\Gamma_{D})}

(81)

\displaystyle{+\lVert h^{\frac{\alpha}{2}}C_{a}^{-\frac{\alpha}{2}}\partial_{n}u\rVert_{L^{2}(\Gamma_{D})}\lVert h^{\frac{\alpha}{2}}C_{a}^{-\frac{\alpha}{2}}\partial_{n}v\rVert_{L^{2}(\Gamma_{D})}}

(82)

\displaystyle\leq\lvert u\rvert_{H^{1}(\Omega)}\lvert v\rvert_{H^{1}(\Omega)}

\displaystyle+\left(h^{\frac{\alpha}{2}}C_{a}^{-\frac{\alpha}{2}}\lVert\partial_{n}u\rVert_{L^{2}(\Gamma_{D})}+h^{-\frac{\alpha}{2}}C_{a}^{\frac{\alpha}{2}}\lVert u\rVert_{L^{2}(\Gamma_{D})}\right)

(83)

\displaystyle\cdot\left(h^{\frac{\alpha}{2}}C_{a}^{-\frac{\alpha}{2}}\lVert\partial_{n}v\rVert_{L^{2}(\Gamma_{D})}+h^{-\frac{\alpha}{2}}C_{a}^{\frac{\alpha}{2}}\lVert v\rVert_{L^{2}(\Gamma_{D})}\right)

(84)

\displaystyle a_{h,\alpha}(u,v)

\displaystyle\leq\left[\lvert u\rvert^{2}_{H^{1}(\Omega)}+\left(h^{\frac{\alpha}{2}}C_{a}^{-\frac{\alpha}{2}}\lVert\partial_{n}u\rVert_{L^{2}(\Gamma_{D})}+h^{-\frac{\alpha}{2}}C_{a}^{\frac{\alpha}{2}}\lVert u\rVert_{L^{2}(\Gamma_{D})}\right)^{2}\right]^{\frac{1}{2}}

(85)

\displaystyle\cdot\;\;\left[\lvert v\rvert^{2}_{H^{1}(\Omega)}+\left(h^{\frac{\alpha}{2}}C_{a}^{-\frac{\alpha}{2}}\lVert\partial_{n}v\rVert_{L^{2}(\Gamma_{D})}+h^{-\frac{\alpha}{2}}C_{a}^{\frac{\alpha}{2}}\lVert v\rVert_{L^{2}(\Gamma_{D})}\right)^{2}\right]^{\frac{1}{2}}

(86)

\displaystyle\leq\left(\lvert u\rvert^{2}_{H^{1}(\Omega)}+{2h^{\alpha}C_{a}^{-\alpha}\lVert\partial_{n}u\rVert^{2}_{L^{2}(\Gamma_{D})}+2h^{-\alpha}C_{a}^{\alpha}\lVert u\rVert^{2}_{L^{2}(\Gamma_{D})}}\right)^{\frac{1}{2}}

(87)

\displaystyle\cdot\;\;\left(\lvert v\rvert^{2}_{H^{1}(\Omega)}+{2h^{\alpha}C_{a}^{-\alpha}\lVert\partial_{n}v\rVert^{2}_{L^{2}(\Gamma_{D})}+2h^{-\alpha}C_{a}^{\alpha}\lVert v\rVert^{2}_{L^{2}(\Gamma_{D})}}\right)^{\frac{1}{2}}

(88)

\displaystyle\leq C_{4}\lVert u\rVert_{h,\alpha}\lVert v\rVert_{h,\alpha},

(89)

hence
C_{4}=2
is the continuity constant.
∎

Corollary 6.2.1
.

Using Lax–Milgram lemma we know that for any
(f,g)\in L^{2}(\Omega)\times H^{\frac{1}{2}}(\partial\Omega)
there exists a unique
u_{h}\in V_{h}
solving (
55
).

Proposition 7
.

The discrete solution
u_{h}\in V_{h}
of (
55
) is an approximation of the solution
u\in H^{1+\delta}(\Omega)
, with respect to
\lVert\cdot\rVert_{h,\alpha}
i.e.,

\lVert u-u_{h}\rVert_{h,\alpha}\leq\underset{v_{h}\in V_{h}}{\inf}\lVert u-v_{h}\rVert_{h,\alpha}\leq C_{5}{h^{\min\{\delta,1,\frac{1}{2}+\delta-\frac{\alpha}{2},\frac{\alpha}{2}\}}}\lvert u\rvert_{H^{1+\delta}(\Omega)},

(90)

provided (
60
) holds,
\delta\in(\frac{1}{2},\frac{3}{2}]
and
\gamma\geq 1
.

Proof.

Via triangle inequality, we know that for any
v_{h}\in V_{h}
,

\displaystyle\lVert u-u_{h}\rVert_{h,\alpha}\leq\lVert u-v_{h}\rVert_{h,\alpha}+\lVert v_{h}-u_{h}\rVert_{h,\alpha},

(91)

we can now bound the second term in the inequality, using the coercivity of the bilinear form
a_{h,\alpha}(\cdot,\cdot)
with constant
\frac{1}{5}
:

\displaystyle\lVert v_{h}-u_{h}\rVert_{h,\alpha}^{2}\leq 5a_{h}(v_{h}-u_{h},v_{h}-u_{h})=5a_{h}(u-v_{h},u_{h}-v_{h}),

(92)

where the last identity is a consequence of Galerkin orthogonality, i.e.,

\displaystyle a_{h}(u_{h}-v_{h},u_{h}-v_{h})

\displaystyle=a_{h}(u_{h},u_{h}-v_{h})-a_{h}(v_{h},u_{h}-v_{h})

\displaystyle=(f,u_{h}-v_{h})_{L^{2}(\Omega)}-a_{h}(v_{h},u_{h}-v_{h})

\displaystyle=a_{h}(u,u_{h}-v_{h})-a_{h}(v_{h},u_{h}-v_{h})

\displaystyle=a_{h}(u-v_{h},u_{h}-v_{h}).

(93)

Since
1+\delta>3/2
, from (
92
) and the continuity of
a_{h}
, we obtain

\displaystyle\lVert v_{h}-u_{h}\rVert_{h,\alpha}^{2}\leq 5a_{h}(u-v_{h},u_{h}-v_{h}){\leq}10\lVert v_{h}-u_{h}\rVert_{h,\alpha}\lVert u-v_{h}\rVert_{h,\alpha}.

(94)

Hence we have that
\forall v_{h}\in V_{h}
the following inequality holds,

\displaystyle\lVert u-u_{h}\rVert_{h,\alpha}\leq 11\lVert u-v_{h}\rVert_{h,\alpha}

(95)

and, since the previous inequality holds for all
v_{h}
in
V_{h}
we have that,

\displaystyle\lVert u-u_{h}\rVert_{h,\alpha}\leq 11\underset{v_{h}\in V_{h}}{\inf}\lVert u-v_{h}\rVert_{h,\alpha}.

(96)

We choose as
v_{h}
the nodal
\mathbb{Q}_{1}
interpolant
\mathcal{I}_{h}u
of
u
and proceed to bound every term in
\lVert u-\mathcal{I}_{h}u\rVert_{h,\alpha}
, i.e.

\lVert u-\mathcal{I}_{h}u\rVert_{h,\alpha}^{2}=\lvert u-\mathcal{I}_{h}u\rvert_{H^{1}(\Omega)}^{2}+h^{-\alpha}C_{a}\lVert u-\mathcal{I}_{h}u\rVert_{L^{2}(\Gamma_{D})}^{2}+C_{a}^{-1}h^{\alpha}\lVert\partial_{n}(u-\mathcal{I}_{h}u)\rVert_{L^{2}(\Gamma_{D})}^{2}.

(97)

From standard interpolation estimates we know that

\lvert u-\mathcal{I}_{h}u\rvert_{H^{1}(\Omega)}\leq D_{1}h^{\min\{\delta,1\}}\lvert u\rvert_{H^{1+\delta}(\Omega)}.

(98)

Since the nodal interpolant along the polygonal boundary corresponds to the one-dimensional interpolant we have that

\lVert u-\mathcal{I}_{h}u\rVert_{L^{2}(\Gamma_{D})}\leq D_{2}h^{\frac{1}{2}+\delta}\lvert u\rvert_{H^{\frac{1}{2}+\delta}(\partial\Omega)}.

(99)

We now need to bound the last remaining term, i.e.

\displaystyle\lVert\partial_{n}(u-\mathcal{I}_{h}u)\rVert_{L^{2}(\Gamma_{D})}^{2}

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{F}\lVert\partial_{n}(u-\mathcal{I}_{h}u)\rVert_{L^{2}(F)}^{2}\leq\sum_{F}\lVert\nabla(u-\mathcal{I}_{h}u)\rVert_{L^{2}(F)}^{2}

(100)

\displaystyle\leq D_{3}\sum_{K}\lVert\nabla(u-\mathcal{I}_{h}u)\rVert_{H^{\delta}(K)}^{2}\leq D_{3}\lVert u\rVert_{H^{1+\delta}(\Omega)}^{2},

(101)

where
F
are the faces of
\Omega
, i.e. the polygonal domain described interesting the boundary
\Gamma_{D}
with the points where the level set function evaluates to zero and
K
are the elements assosciated to the faces
F
.
The last bound comes from the element wise stability of the nodal interpolant with respect tot the
H^{1+\delta}
norm.
Combining all of the previous estimates we have that

\lVert u-\mathcal{I}_{h}u\rVert_{h,\alpha}\leq D_{4}h^{\min\{\delta,1,\frac{1}{2}+\delta-\frac{\alpha}{2},\frac{\alpha}{2}\}}\lvert u\rvert_{H^{1+\delta}(\Omega)}.

(102)

∎

We can now proceed to use the Aubin–Nitsche duality argument to give an estimate of the convergence rate of the scheme here proposed with respect to the
L^{2}(\Omega)
norm.

Proposition 8
.

Under the assumption that (
60
) holds the discrete solution
u_{h}\in V_{h}
of (
55
) is an approximation of the solution
u\in H^{1+\delta}(\Omega)
with respect to the
L^{2}(\Omega)
norm i.e.,

\lVert u-u_{h}\rVert_{L^{2}(\Omega)}\leq C_{6}{h^{2\min\{\delta,1,\frac{1}{2}+\delta-\frac{\alpha}{2},\frac{\alpha}{2}\}}\lvert u\rvert_{H^{1+\delta}(\Omega)}},

(103)

provided (
60
) holds,
\delta\in(\frac{1}{2},\frac{3}{2}]
and
\gamma\geq 1
and that the adjoint problem is sufficiently regular.

Proof.

We consider the auxiliary problem, find
w\in H^{1}_{\Gamma_{D}}(\Omega)
such that

a(w,z)=(u-u_{h},z)_{L^{2}(\Omega)},\qquad\forall z\in H^{1}_{\Gamma_{D}}(\Omega),

(104)

and we assume
w\in H^{1+\delta}(\Omega)
.
We now notice that

\displaystyle\lVert u-u_{h}\rVert^{2}_{L^{2}(\Omega)}

\displaystyle=\left(\nabla w,\nabla(u-u_{h})\right)_{L^{2}(\Omega)}-(u-u_{h},\partial_{n}w)_{L^{2}(\partial\Omega)}

(105)

\displaystyle=\left(\nabla w,\nabla(u-u_{h})\right)_{L^{2}(\Omega)}-(u-u_{h},\partial_{n}w)_{L^{2}(\Gamma_{D})}

(106)

\displaystyle-(\partial_{n}u-\partial_{n}u_{h},w)_{L^{2}(\Gamma_{D})}+C_{a}h^{-1}\left(u-u_{h},w\right)_{L^{2}(\Gamma_{D})}

(107)

\displaystyle=a_{h}(u-u_{h},w).

(108)

We now notice that
a_{h}(u-u_{h},w_{h})=(f,w_{h})_{L^{2}(\Omega)}-(f,w_{h})_{L^{2}(\Omega)}=0
therefore using the continuity of
a_{h}
we can rewrite the previous identity as

\displaystyle\lVert u-u_{h}\rVert^{2}_{L^{2}(\Omega)}

\displaystyle=a_{h}(u-u_{h},w-w_{h})\leq\lVert u-u_{h}\rVert_{h,1}\lVert w-w_{h}\rVert_{h,1}.

(109)

Using Proposition
7
we can bound the
L^{2}(\Omega)
error as

\displaystyle\lVert u-u_{h}\rVert^{2}_{L^{2}(\Omega)}

\displaystyle\leq C_{5}^{2}h^{\delta}\lvert u\rvert_{H^{2}(\Omega)}h^{\delta}\lvert w\rvert_{H^{2}(\Omega)}\leq C_{5}h^{2\delta}\lVert u-u_{h}\rVert_{L^{2}(\Omega)}\lvert u\rvert_{H^{2}(\Omega)}.

(110)

Dividing the previous inequality by
\lVert u-u_{h}\rVert_{L^{2}(\Omega)}
we obtain the desired result.
∎

We now focus our attention on the analysis of the snapping back to the grid perturbation discussed in Section
5
.

When adopting a snapping back to grid technique we are solving (
55
) on a domain
\widetilde{\Omega}
, which is a perturbation of the original
\Omega
.
We introduce this perturbation to get rid of cells that do not respect the geometrical assumption (
60
).

We aim to use the theory presented in
[
43
]
, and to develop an a priori error estimate for the snapping back to grid approach viewed as a perturbation method.

Before diving into the results presented in
[
43
]
we need to discuss the eventual regularity issue that might arise from considering the perturbed domain
\widetilde{\Omega}
.
In the one-dimensional case, we know that the snapping presented in Section
5
does not affect the elliptic regularity discussed at the beginning of this section.
Instead, in the two-dimensional case the worst possible scenario comes when, as a consequence of the snapping back to grid, the perturbed domain
\widetilde{\Omega}
presents a re-entrant corner of aperture
\frac{3}{2}\pi
.
An example of such a situation can be seen in Figure
4
(a).

In this case elliptic regularity only guarantees that the
\widetilde{u}\in H^{1}_{\Gamma_{D}}(\widetilde{\Omega})
solving

(\nabla\widetilde{u},\nabla v)_{L^{2}(\widetilde{\widetilde{\Omega}})}=(f,v)_{L^{2}(\widetilde{\Omega})}+\prescript{}{H^{-\frac{1}{2}}(\widetilde{\Gamma}_{N})}{\langle}g_{N},v\rangle_{H^{\frac{1}{2}}(\widetilde{\Gamma}_{N})},\qquad\forall v\in H^{1}_{\Gamma_{D}}(\widetilde{\Omega}),

(111)

lives in the Sobolev space
H^{\frac{5}{3}+\varepsilon}(\widetilde{\Omega})
no matter how regular
f
,
g_{D}
, and
g_{N}
are.
Hence we might have a mismatch between the regularity of
\widetilde{u}
and
u
, and this should be taken into consideration when using (
103
).

Let us state the following simplified result from
[
43
, Theorem 8.4]
to bound the
L^{2}
distance over
R
between
\widetilde{u}
and
u
.

Theorem 8.1
.

Let
\Omega\subset R
and
\widetilde{\Omega}\subset R
be Lipschitz domain with corners of aperture at most
\frac{3}{2}\pi
.
We consider
u\in H^{1}(\Omega)
and
\widetilde{u}\in H^{1}(\widetilde{\Omega})
solutions of the original variational problem and (
111
) for a data
f\in L^{2}(R)
and with homogeneous Dirichlet boundary condition, i.e.,
g_{D}=0
on the entire boundary, i.e.,
\Gamma_{D}=\partial\Omega
and
\widetilde{\Gamma}_{D}=\partial\widetilde{\Omega}
respectively.
Under these hypotheses the following bound for the
L^{2}
distance over
R
between
\widetilde{u}
and
u
, holds:

\lVert u-\widetilde{u}\rVert_{L^{2}(R)}\leq C_{7}\left(\sup_{\mathbf{x}\in\Omega\Delta\widetilde{\Omega}}d\left(\mathbf{x},\partial\Omega\right)\right)\lVert f\rVert_{L^{2}(R)},

(112)

where
\Omega\Delta\widetilde{\Omega}
denotes the set difference between
\Omega
and
\widetilde{\Omega}
and
C_{7}
is a constant depending only the diameter of
R
and the aperture of all the re-entrant corners of
\widetilde{\Omega}
.

Corollary 8.1.1
.

Let
\widetilde{u}_{h}\in V_{h}
be the solution of the perturbed discrete variational problem,

\displaystyle(\nabla\widetilde{u}_{h},\nabla{v}_{h})_{L^{2}(\widetilde{\Omega})}

\displaystyle+C_{a}h^{-\alpha}(\widetilde{u}_{h},{v}_{h})_{L^{2}(\Gamma_{D})}

(113)

\displaystyle-(\partial_{n}\widetilde{u}_{h},{v}_{h})_{L^{2}(\Gamma_{D})}-(\widetilde{u}_{h},\partial_{n}{v}_{h})_{L^{2}(\Gamma_{D})}=(f,{v}_{h})_{L^{2}(\widetilde{\Omega})},\;\;\forall{v}_{h}\in V_{h},

where
V_{h}
is the usual space of
\mathbb{Q}_{1}
functions over each element of a mesh
\mathcal{T}_{h}
for
R\subset\mathbb{R}
. If
\widetilde{\Omega}
is the geometry obtained from
\Omega
after snapping back to cell for every
K
such that

\lvert\partial\Omega\cap K\rvert\leq\widehat{C}_{\Omega}h^{\alpha},

(114)

then for
\gamma\geq 1
there exists
\widetilde{\delta}\in(\frac{1}{2},\frac{5}{2}]
, depending only on the regularity of
\partial\widetilde{\Omega}
, such that

\lVert u-\widetilde{u}_{h}\rVert_{L^{2}(R)}\leq C_{8}h^{\min\{\widetilde{\delta},1,\frac{1}{2}+\delta-\frac{\alpha}{2},\frac{\alpha}{2}\}}\lVert f\rVert_{L^{2}(R)}.

(115)

Proof.

The proof of this result is almost immediate.

It is sufficient to observe that in Algorithm
2
we are snapping back to the grid the cells that do not respect the geometrical assumption (
60
), hence the distance between the newly obtained domain
\widetilde{\Omega}
and the original domain
\Omega
is bounded by the diameter of the cells that have been snapped back, implying that

\sup_{\mathbf{x}\in\Omega\Delta\widetilde{\Omega}}d\left(\mathbf{x},\partial\Omega\right)\leq\sqrt{2}\widehat{C}_{\Omega}h^{\alpha}.

(116)

We then observe that as discussed previously
\widetilde{\Omega}
can only have re-entrant corners of aperture
\frac{3}{2}\pi
and therefore we can apply the previous theorem to obtain,

\lVert u-\widetilde{u}\rVert_{L^{2}(R)}\leq\sqrt{2}\widehat{C}_{\Omega}C_{7}h^{\alpha}\lVert f\rVert_{L^{2}(R)}.

(117)

Since we know that
\widetilde{u}\in H^{1+\widetilde{\delta}}
, with
\widetilde{\delta}\in(\frac{1}{2},\frac{5}{2}]
, and that (
60
) is verified by the pair
(\mathcal{T}_{h},\widetilde{\Omega})
if
\gamma\geq 1
we can apply (
103
) to obtain the following bound,

\lVert\widetilde{u}-\widetilde{u}_{h}\rVert_{L^{2}(\widetilde{\Omega})}\leq C_{6}h^{2\min\{\widetilde{\delta},1,\frac{1}{2}+\widetilde{\delta}-\frac{\alpha}{2},\frac{\alpha}{2}\}}\lVert f\rVert_{L^{2}(\widetilde{\Omega})}.

(118)

Using the fact that
\lVert\cdot\rVert_{L^{2}(\widetilde{\Omega})}\leq\lVert\cdot\rVert_{L^{2}(R)}
and the triangular inequality we get that,

\lVert u-\widetilde{u}_{h}\rVert_{L^{2}(R)}\leq C_{8}h^{2\min\{\widetilde{\delta},1,\frac{1}{2}+\widetilde{\delta}-\frac{\alpha}{2},\frac{\alpha}{2}\}}\lVert f\rVert_{L^{2}(R)},

(119)

where
u
and
\widehat{u}
are extended to zero outside of
\Omega
and
\widehat{\Omega}
and
C_{8}=(C_{6}+\sqrt{2}\widehat{C}_{\Omega}C_{7})
.
∎

9
Numerical tests

In this section, we provide numerical tests showing second-order accuracy in one and two-dimensions. We test different smooth geometries, and for each one we show the accuracy of the solution and of its divergence, which are computed as follows:

\displaystyle{\rm error}

\displaystyle=\frac{\sqrt{\int_{\mathcal{A}}|u_{h}-u_{\rm exa}|^{2}dxdy}}{\sqrt{\int_{\mathcal{A}}|u_{\rm exa}|^{2}dxdy}}

\displaystyle\nabla{\rm error}

\mathcal{A}

In the accuracy analysis, the penalisation parameter plays an important role. We find a compromise between accuracy and a well-conditioned numerical scheme.

Conclusions

In the present work, we have introduced a novel nodal ghost discretisation posed on regular square meshes. In particular, our derivation of the numerical scheme here presented found its root in the variational formulation of the Poisson equation. Such formulation, often known also as weak formulation, is at the heart of the finite element method.

Resorting to a variational formulation allows us to preserve the symmetry and the coercivity of the continuous problem in the discrete setting. The conservation of such properties at a discrete level represents a substantial difference and a great improvement with respect to other nodal ghost numerical schemes such as the Coco-Russo method
[
20
,
21
]
.

There are many differences between the method here proposed and a standard finite element discretisation. One particular difference, between our scheme and a standard body-fitted FEM, is that we described the geometry of the domain
\Omega
via a level-set function. Such an approach is also adopted in CutFEM,
[
33
,
15
,
16
,
17
]
, which differs from our scheme for the following reasons.
First of all, in the CutFEM in order to compute the boundary integrals, they introduce a sub-triangulation of cut elements. Such a process requires additional computational work. We avoid the construction of a sub-triangulation via the quadrature technique presented in Section
5
.
Secondly, we escape the so-called
small cut
issue, which might result in losing the coercivity of the bilinear form, by perturbing mesh geometry.
In particular, the perturbation procedure we adopted is well known to the engineering community by the name of snapping back to grid.

Adopting standard techniques, as in the FEM and the CutFEM, we can prove optimal converge rate for our scheme with respect to the
L^{2}
norm.
To the best of the authors’ knowledge up until now, there has not been a rigorous study of a priori convergence rates when the snapping back to grid technique is adopted.
For this reason, we have presented a novel approach to proving a priori convergence rates for our scheme even when the snapping back to grid technique is used to address the
small cuts
issue.

Lastly, we have presented many numerical experiments that validate the
L^{2}
convergence rate theoretically proven. Furthermore, we would like to point out that the numerical experiments shown in Section
9
suggest that the error in energy norm is super-converging with respect to the analysis here presented.

In future, we plan to further investigate the scheme here proposed. In particular, we would like to study in greater detail the eigenvalue problem (
56
) and the role it plays when determining the optimal value of the penalisation constant. We are also interested in developing fast solvers for our discretization based on a multigrid approach, similar to
[
20
]
.

Appendix A
Discretization of the two-dimensional problem

In this section we derive in detail the computation to obtain a space discretization in two-dimensions. We start considering the Poisson equation with Dirichlet boundary conditions in
\Omega\subseteq R\subset\mathbb{R}^{2}
, where
R
is a rectangular region:

-\Delta u=f\quad{\rm in}\>\Omega,\quad u=g_{D}\>{\rm on}\>\partial\Omega

(123)

Multiplying by a test function
v\in H^{1}(\Omega)
, integrating in
\Omega
and then integrating by parts, one obtains:

\int_{\Omega}\nabla u\cdot\nabla v\,d\Omega=\int_{\Omega}fv\,d\Omega+\int_{\partial\Omega}v\frac{\partial u}{\partial n}\,ds.

(124)

Following the same strategy that we applied in the one-dimensional case, we penalise the distance from the prescribed value of the Dirichlet boundary condition by minimizing the following functional

J_{D\lambda}[u]=\int_{\Omega}\left[\frac{1}{2}(\nabla u)^{2}-fu\right]\,d\Omega+\frac{\lambda}{2}\int_{\partial\Omega}(u-g_{D})^{2}\,ds

(125)

By imposing the first variation of the functional equal to zero we obtain the following penalised variational formulation of the problem:

\int_{\Omega}\nabla u\cdot\nabla v\,d\Omega+\int_{\partial\Omega}\left[\lambda\cdot(u-g_{D})-\frac{\partial u}{\partial n}\right]v\,ds=\int_{\Omega}fv\,d\Omega

(126)

The discretization of the problem is obtained by discretizing the rectangular region
R\subset\mathbb{R}^{2}
, containing the domain
\Omega
, by a regular rectangular grid (see Figure
3
).

The discrete space
V_{h}
defined in (
35
) is given by the piecewise bilinear functions which are continuous in
R
.
As a basis, we choose the following functions:

{\varphi_{i}(x,y)=\max\left\{\left(1-\frac{|x-x_{i}|}{\Delta x}\right),0\right\}\cdot\max\left\{\left(1-\frac{|y-y_{i}|}{\Delta x}\right),0\right\}}

(127)

here we denote by
i=(i_{1},i_{2})
an index that identifies a node on the grid.

The generic element
u_{h}\in V_{h}
will have the following representation:

u_{h}(x,y)=\sum_{i\in{\mathcal{A}}}u_{i}\varphi_{i}(x,y).

(128)

The discretization of the problem (
126
) is obtained by replacing

u
and
v
by
u_{h}
and
v_{h}
, both in
V_{h}
, and evaluating the integral instead of over
\Omega
over its polygonal approximation
\Omega_{h}
. Since we are approximating
\Omega
with
\Omega_{h}
, we are also approximating
\Gamma
by its polygonal approximation
\Gamma_{h}
. This leads to the following discrete variational problem

\int_{\Omega_{h}}\nabla u_{h}\cdot\nabla v_{h}\,d{\Omega_{h}}+\int_{\Gamma_{h}}\left[\lambda\cdot(u_{h}-g_{D})-\frac{\partial u_{h}}{\partial n}\right]v_{h}\,ds=\int_{\Omega_{h}}fv_{h}\,d{\Omega_{h}},\quad\forall v_{h}\in V_{h}

(129)

When applied to the subspace
V_{h}
, and using the standard basis, the above equations become

\displaystyle\sum_{j\in\mathcal{N}}u_{j}(\nabla\varphi_{i},\nabla\varphi_{j})_{L^{2}(\Omega_{h})}-\sum_{j\in\mathcal{N}}u_{j}\left(\frac{\partial\varphi_{i}}{\partial n},\varphi_{j}\right)_{L^{2}(\Gamma_{D,h})}+\lambda\sum_{j\in\mathcal{N}}u_{j}(\varphi_{j},\varphi_{i})_{L^{2}(\Gamma_{D,h})}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{j\in\mathcal{N}}f_{j}(\varphi_{j},\varphi_{i})_{L^{2}(\Omega_{h})}+\lambda\sum_{j\in\mathcal{N}}{g_{D}}_{j}(\varphi_{j},\varphi_{i})_{L^{2}(\Gamma_{D,h})}.

(130)

where the quantities
f_{i}
,
g^{D}_{i}
denote the node values of the source data and of the Dirichlet boundary conditions.

Here we rewrite (
129
), applying the Nitsche technique, to make the equation symmetric

\displaystyle\int_{\Omega_{h}}\nabla u_{h}\cdot\nabla v_{h}\,d{\Omega_{h}}+\int_{\Gamma_{h}}\lambda\cdot(u_{h}-g_{D})v_{h}\,ds-\int_{\Gamma_{h}}\frac{\partial u_{h}}{\partial n}v_{h}\,ds-\int_{\Gamma_{h}}u_{h}\frac{\partial v_{h}}{\partial n}\,ds

\displaystyle=\int_{\Omega_{h}}fv_{h}\,d{\Omega_{h}}-\int_{\Gamma_{h}}g_{D}\frac{\partial v_{h}}{\partial n}\,ds,\quad\forall v_{h}\in V_{h}

(131)

as follows

\displaystyle\sum_{j\in\mathcal{N}}u_{j}\left(\underbrace{(\nabla\varphi_{j},\nabla\varphi_{i})_{L^{2}(\Omega_{h})}}_{\rm S}-\underbrace{\left(\left(\frac{\partial\varphi_{j}}{\partial n},\varphi_{i}\right)_{L^{2}(\Gamma_{D,h})}+\left(\varphi_{j},\frac{\partial\varphi_{i}}{\partial n}\right)_{L^{2}(\Gamma_{D,h})}\right)}_{\rm S_{T}}+\lambda\underbrace{(\varphi_{j},\varphi_{i})_{L^{2}(\Gamma_{D,h})}}_{\rm P_{\Gamma}}\right)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{j\in\mathcal{N}}f_{j}\underbrace{(\varphi_{j},\varphi_{i})_{L^{2}(\Omega_{h})}}_{\rm M}+\sum_{j\in\mathcal{N}}{g_{D}}_{j}\left(-\underbrace{\left(\varphi_{j},\frac{\partial\varphi_{i}}{\partial n}\right)_{L^{2}(\Gamma_{D,h})}}_{\rm D_{\Gamma}}+\underbrace{\lambda(\varphi_{j},\varphi_{i})_{L^{2}(\Gamma_{D,h})}}_{\rm P_{\Gamma}}\right)

(132)

Making use of representation (
128
) and using the functions of the basis as test functions we obtain the following system of equations for
u_{i}
,
i\in{\mathcal{A}}

\displaystyle\sum_{j\in\mathcal{N}}u_{j}(\nabla\varphi_{i},\nabla\varphi_{j})_{L^{2}(\Omega_{h})}=\sum_{j\in\mathcal{N}}f_{j}(\varphi_{j},\varphi_{i})_{L^{2}(\Omega_{h})}+\sum_{j\in\mathcal{N}}{g_{N}}_{j}(\varphi_{j},\varphi_{i})_{L^{2}(\Gamma_{N,h})}

where the quantities

Appendix B
Computational details of the one-dimensional problem

\begin{array}[]{c}\displaystyle(\varphi_{0}^{\prime},\varphi_{0}^{\prime})_{L^{2}([a,b])}=\frac{\theta_{1}}{h},\quad(\varphi_{0}^{\prime},\varphi_{1}^{\prime})_{L^{2}([a,b])}=-\frac{\theta_{1}}{h},\quad(\varphi_{1}^{\prime},\varphi_{1}^{\prime})_{L^{2}([a,b])}=\frac{1+\theta_{1}}{h},\\[11.38109pt]
\displaystyle(\varphi_{N-1}^{\prime},\varphi_{N-1}^{\prime})_{L^{2}([a,b])}=\frac{1+\theta_{2}}{h},\quad(\varphi_{N-1}^{\prime},\varphi_{N}^{\prime})_{L^{2}([a,b])}=-\frac{\theta_{2}}{h},\quad(\varphi_{N}^{\prime},\varphi_{N}^{\prime})_{L^{2}([a,b])}=\frac{\theta_{2}}{h},\\[11.38109pt]

\displaystyle\varphi_{0}(a)=\theta_{1},\quad\varphi_{1}(a)=1-\theta_{1},\quad\varphi_{N}(b)=\theta_{2},\quad\varphi_{N-1}(b)=1-\theta_{2},\\[11.38109pt]
\displaystyle\varphi^{\prime}_{0}(a)=-1/h,\quad\varphi^{\prime}_{1}(a)=1/h,\quad\varphi^{\prime}_{N}(b)=1/h,\quad\varphi^{\prime}_{N-1}(b)=-1/h.\end{array}

The rest of the source terms can be computed as follows. The term related to the mass matrix is

\begin{array}[]{c}\displaystyle(f,\varphi_{i})_{L^{2}([a,b])}\approx\sum_{j=0}^{N}f_{j}(\varphi_{j},\varphi_{i})_{L^{2}([a,b])}=\frac{h}{6}\left(f_{i-1}+4f_{i}+f_{i+1}\right),\quad i=2,\ldots,n-2.\\
\displaystyle(f,\varphi_{0})_{L^{2}([a,b])}\approx\sum_{j=0}^{N}f_{j}(\varphi_{j},\varphi_{0})_{L^{2}([a,b])}=h\left(\frac{\theta_{1}^{3}}{3}f_{0}+\left(\frac{\theta_{1}^{2}}{2}-\frac{\theta_{1}^{3}}{3}\right)f_{1}\right),\\[14.22636pt]
\displaystyle(f,\varphi_{1})_{L^{2}([a,b])}\approx\sum_{j=0}^{N}f_{j}(\varphi_{j},\varphi_{1})_{L^{2}([a,b])}=h\left(\left(\frac{\theta_{1}^{2}}{2}-\frac{\theta_{1}^{3}}{3}\right)f_{0}+\frac{1+\theta_{1}^{3}}{3}f_{1}\right),\\[14.22636pt]
\displaystyle(f,\varphi_{N-1})_{L^{2}([a,b])}\approx\sum_{j=0}^{N}f_{j}(\varphi_{j},\varphi_{N-1})_{L^{2}([a,b])}=h\left(\left(\frac{1+\theta_{2}^{3}}{3}-\theta_{2}^{2}+\theta_{2}\right)f_{N-1}+\left(\frac{\theta_{2}^{2}}{2}-\frac{\theta_{2}^{3}}{3}\right)f_{N}\right)\\[14.22636pt]
\displaystyle(f,\varphi_{N})_{L^{2}([a,b])}\approx\sum_{j=0}^{N}f_{j}(\varphi_{j},\varphi_{N-1})_{L^{2}([a,b])}=h\left(\left(\frac{\theta_{2}^{2}}{2}-\frac{\theta_{2}^{3}}{3}\right)f_{N-1}+\frac{\theta_{2}^{3}}{3}f_{N}\right).\end{array}
