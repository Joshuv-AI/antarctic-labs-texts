Abstract
. Neglecting many motivating details for the Park-Pham theorem (previously known as the Kahn-Kalai conjecture), the result starts with a finite set X X , a non-trivial upper set ℱ ⊆ 2 X \mathcal{F}\subseteq 2^{X} , and a particular parameterized family of random variables X p X_{p} , then proceeds to provide an upper bound on the value p c ​ ( ℱ ) p_{c}(\mathcal{F}) such that ℙ ⁡ ( X p c ​ ( ℱ ) ∈ ℱ ) = 1 / 2 \mathbb{P}(X_{p_{c}(\mathcal{F})}\in\mathcal{F})=1/2 . A natural question to ask is if there is an analog to the Park-Pham theorem for upper sets in finite posets other than 2 X 2^{X} and other parameterized families of random variables taking values in them. 1.
Introduction

The Park-Pham theorem
[
11
]
(previously the Kahn-Kalai conjecture
[
6
]
) bounds the probability that a given subset of a finite set, chosen at random from a finite set of objects in a particular way, has a specified property under the proviso that the property respects supersets. As it should be, this bound involves (either directly or indirectly) how large the set is, how large the smallest subset with the property is, and the way one randomly chooses subsets. The precise statement of this requires some definitions (for a more detailed introduction, see
[
10
]
), which we give in Section
2
.

In this paper, we prove a conditional version of the
\epsilon
-dependent Park-Pham theorem. Aside from the fact that it extends the Park-Pham theorem in a limited way, the reason a conditional form of the
\epsilon
-dependent version is interesting is as follows: Suppose one has a finite poset
P
and an upper set
U
in it. Then, if one takes an injection
f:P\rightarrow 2^{X}
for a finite set
X
, the conditional probability
\mathbb{P}\big(X_{p}\in f(U)|X_{p}\in f(P)\big)
is the same as
\mathbb{P}(Y_{p}\in U)
for the random variable
Y_{p}
with distribution
\frac{\mu_{p}\circ f}{\mu_{p}\big(f(P)\big)}
. That is, a conditional version will allow us to show there is at least one case where the Park-Pham theorem can be extended to a more general context of upper sets in finite posets and parameterized families of random variables taking values in them. We discuss this point more in Remark
3.1
following some background, but first an example to show that this has important implications.

Example 1.1
.

Adopting the notation of
[
8
]
, let
A(\ell,\mathbf{n},\sigma)
be the set of neural network with
\ell
fully connected feed-forward layers of widths
\mathbf{n}=(n_{0},n_{1},\ldots,n_{\ell})
, such that
n_{0}
is the input dimension and
n_{\ell}
is the output dimension, and
\sigma
is the activation function. Explicitly, an element of
A(\ell,\mathbf{n},\sigma)
is a function
f_{W}:\mathbb{R}^{n_{0}}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^{n_{\ell}}
of the form

f_{W}(x)=f_{W_{\ell}}\circ f_{W_{\ell-1}}\circ\ldots\circ f_{W_{1}}(x)

where
W=(W_{1},\ldots W_{\ell})
for
W_{i}\in[w_{min},w_{max}]^{n_{i}\times n_{i-1}}\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{n_{i}\times n_{i-1}}
and the functions
f_{W_{i}}:\mathbb{R}^{n_{i-1}}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^{n_{i}}
are defined as
\sigma_{i}(W_{i}x)
, understanding
\sigma_{i}:\mathbb{R}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
as being applied component-wise.

The lottery ticket hypothesis
[
3
]
, as posed by Frankle and Carbin, conjectured that dense, randomly-initialized, feed-forward networks contain subnetworks (winning tickets) that—when trained in isolation—reach test accuracy comparable to the original network in a similar number of iterations. Later, a stronger form was posed by Ramanujan et al.
[
12
]
and soon thereafter proved by Malach, Yehudai, et al.
[
7
]
, which removed the requirement that a similar amount of training be performed on the masked network. That is, sufficiently large networks are likely to contain subnetworks that perform just as well, with no training at all, as the overall network does after training.

Consider now the case where the weights of a network are initialized from some finite set
R
of representative weights instead of a continuous one–a harmless simplification that occurs in practice, since everything is done in some floating point system. Let
(G,w)
be the weighted graph representation of a network
f_{W}\in A(\ell,n,\sigma)
, where
G
is the underlying graph of the network and
w:E(G)\rightarrow R
is the corresponding weighting function, so that we may identify each choice of weights with the the graph of the corresponding weighting function for
G
; i.e.,
\textrm{Gph}(w)=\left\{\big(e,w(e)\big):e\in E(G)\right\}
. With this, we may form the poset

P(G)=\left\{\textrm{Gph}(w):H\leq G,\enskip w:E(H)\rightarrow R\right\}

as a subposet of
2^{E(G)\times R}
with containment as the ordering. Then,
P(G)
contains all networks and subnetworks of any network with underlying graph
G
. As each subnetwork determines a masking, the strong lottery ticket hypothesis then states that for any fixed collection of target networks, the upper set
U
generated by their weighting functions contains many of the maximal elements of
P(G)
provided
G
is sufficiently large. More precisely, supposing
G_{n}
is a sequence of increasingly wide networks of sufficient constant depth,
U_{n}
is the upper set in
P(G_{n})
generated by the networks approximating some sought function to a desired level of accuracy, and let
X_{p}
be the random variable on
2^{E(G_{n})\times R}
with distribution
\mu_{p}
, then we have

\mathbb{P}\big(X_{1/2}\in U_{n}|X_{1/2}\in\textrm{max}P(G_{n})\big)>1-\epsilon

for any choice of
\epsilon\in(0,1)
whenever
n
is sufficiently large. To put things in the language of thresholds, this says that
\mathbb{P}(X_{1/2}\in U_{n}|X_{1/2}\in\textrm{max}P(G_{n}))\rightarrow 1
as
n\rightarrow\infty
, which means that the threshold for a given sequence of sufficiently networks to contain a lottery ticket (i.e. a subnetwork belonging to a specified collection of target networks) is less than the constant function
\frac{1}{2}
.

But how much less, and how does it change with both
U_{n}
and
G_{n}
? Knowing the answers to these questions would be quite beneficial in practice, since random networks obtained by choosing smaller values of
p
are sparser and less computational expensive to deal with. Hence, one may want to know what the threshold is, or at least get a good estimate for it. Vexingly, the estimate provided by the Kahn-Kalai conjecture cannot be used here directly, since all of this is occurring in posets that are not power sets but, rather, subposets of power sets. In this paper, we fix this and extend Park and Pham’s result to this case as well.

2.
Background

Given a finite set
X
, we say that a
property
of
X
is a subset
\mathcal{F}\subseteq 2^{X}
. A property of
X
is said to be an
upper set
if
B\in\mathcal{F}
whenever
A\subseteq B
for some
A\in\mathcal{F}
. For a customary example, the subset of subgraphs
H
of a given graph
G
such that
H
contains a subgraph isomorphic to some target graph
K
forms an upper set, as the addition of vertices or edges to a graph preserves subgraph inclusion
[
2
]
. A
lower set
is defined similarly, where
B\in\mathcal{F}
whenever
B\subseteq A
for some
A\in\mathcal{F}
. We say a property
\mathcal{F}
is
nontrivial
if
\mathcal{F}\neq 2^{X},\emptyset
. The notion of an upper set and a lower set can be extended to posets
P
other than
2^{X}
as well:
S\subseteq P
is an upper set (resp. lower set) if
x\in S
and
x\leq y
implies
y\in S
(resp.
x\in S
and
y\leq x
implies
y\in S
).

We will regard
thresholds
for
sequences
of upper sets in
2^{X}
particularly. For
p\in[0,1]
define the product measure
\mu_{p}
on
X
by
\mu_{p}(S)=p^{|S|}(1-p)^{|X|-|S|}
and extend this to a probability measure on
2^{X}
by
\mu_{p}(\mathcal{F}):=\sum_{S\in\mathcal{F}}\mu_{p}(S)
.

Definition 2.1

(Threshold)
.

Given a sequence of finite sets
X_{n}
and a sequence of upper sets
\mathcal{F}_{n}\subseteq 2^{X_{n}}
, we say that a function
p^{*}(n)
is a
threshold
for
\mathcal{F}_{n}
if
\mu_{p(n)}\big(\mathcal{F}_{n}\big)\rightarrow 0
when
\frac{p(n)}{p^{*}(n)}\rightarrow 0
and
\mu_{p(n)}\big(\mathcal{F}_{n}\big)\rightarrow 1
when
\frac{p(n)}{p^{*}(n)}\rightarrow\infty
.

Every sequence of nontrivial upper sets
\mathcal{F}_{n}
has a threshold. One choice in particular is the
critical probability of
\mathcal{F}
, denoted
p_{c}(\mathcal{F})
, which is the unique value
p\in[0,1]
such that
\mu_{p}(\mathcal{F})=\frac{1}{2}
. That is,
p^{*}(n):=p_{c}\big(\mathcal{F}_{n}\big)
is a threshold for the sequence
\mathcal{F}_{n}

[
5
]
.

The Kahn-Kalai conjecture
[
6
]
(now, the Park-Pham theorem
[
11
]
) concerns the relationship between the threshold obtained from the critical probability
p_{c}
and the
expectation threshold
of an upper set, a quantity derived from considering certain covers of upper sets. Given an upper set
\mathcal{F}\subseteq 2^{X}
, we say that a subset
\mathcal{G}\subseteq 2^{X}
is a
cover
of
\mathcal{F}
if
\mathcal{F}\subseteq\bigcup_{S\in\mathcal{G}}\langle S\rangle
, where
\langle S\rangle
denotes the upper set generated by
S
, i.e.
\langle S\rangle=\left\{T:S\subseteq T\right\}
. For
p\in[0,1]
, we say that
\mathcal{F}
is
p
-small
if there exists a cover
\mathcal{G}
of
\mathcal{F}
such that
\sum_{S\in\mathcal{G}}p^{|S|}\leq\frac{1}{2}
. The
expectation threshold
of an upper set
\mathcal{F}
, denoted
q(\mathcal{F})
, is then defined to be the largest
p
for which
\mathcal{F}
is
p
-small.

Now, let
\mathcal{F}_{0}
denote the set of minimal elements of
\mathcal{F}
; i.e.,

\mathcal{F}_{0}=\left\{S\in\mathcal{F}:\enskip S^{\prime}\not\subseteq S\textrm{ for all }S^{\prime}\in\mathcal{F}\setminus S\right\}

, let
\ell_{0}(\mathcal{F})=\textrm{max}\left\{|S|:S\in\mathcal{F}_{0}\right\}
and write
\ell(\mathcal{F})=\textrm{max}\left\{\ell_{0}(\mathcal{F}),2\right\}
. The Park-Pham theorem
[
11
]
(previously the Kahn-Kalai conjecture
[
6
]
) bounds the critical probability by a logarithmic factor of the expectation threshold:

Theorem 2.2

(Park-Pham Theorem)
.

There exists a universal constant
K
such that for every finite set
X
and every nontrivial upper set
\mathcal{F}\subseteq 2^{X}
,

q(\mathcal{F})\leq p_{c}(\mathcal{F})\leq Kq(\mathcal{F})\log\ell(\mathcal{F}).

A version of the above, given by Bell
[
1
]
, finds an optimized value of
K
:

Theorem 2.3

(Bell’s Park-Pham theorem with optimal constant
[
1
]
)
.

For every finite set
X
and every nontrivial upper set
\mathcal{F}\subseteq 2^{X}
,

q(\mathcal{F})\leq p_{c}(\mathcal{F})\leq 8q(\mathcal{F})\log\left(2\ell_{0}(\mathcal{F})\right).

It is worth noting that one can optimize the above further in certain cases. For instance, it was shown in
[
13
]
that one can set
K\approx 3.998
in the case where
\ell(F_{n})\rightarrow\infty
. It is interesting to note as well that the analog of Theorem
2.2
for lower sets does not end up working out as one might expect
[
4
,
14
]
.

Remark 2.4
.

If
X_{p}
is the random variable with distribution
\mu_{p}
, then Theorem
2.2
can be recast as
\mathbb{P}(X_{p}\in\mathcal{F})>1/2
whenever
p>Kq(\mathcal{F})\log\ell(\mathcal{F})
. This version makes it clear that there should be a more general variant of the bound, which replaces
\mathbb{P}(X_{p}\in\mathcal{F})>1/2
with
\mathbb{P}(X_{p}\in\mathcal{F})>1-\epsilon
for any
\epsilon\in(0,1)
and modifies the lower bound for
p
with some dependence on
\epsilon
. That this is possible was also provided by Park and Pham alongside their original proof, and was then later optimized by Bell:

Theorem 2.5

(Bell’s optimal
\epsilon
-dependent Park-Pham theorem
[
1
]
)
.

For every finite set
X
, every nontrivial upper set
\mathcal{F}\subseteq 2^{X}
, and any
\epsilon\in(0,1)
,
\mathbb{P}(X_{p}\in\mathcal{F})>1-\epsilon
whenever

p>48q(\mathcal{F})\log\left(\frac{\ell_{0}(\mathcal{F})}{\epsilon}\right)
.

3.
A Conditional Variant

In this paper, we will prove that a conditional version of Theorem
2.5
is possible with some caveats. Throughout,
K
will refer to the constant in Theorem
2.2
,
X
will be a finite set, and
X_{p}
will be the random variable with distribution
\mu_{p}
as in Remark
2.4
.

Remark 3.1
.

As noted in the introduction, a conditional form of the
\epsilon
-dependent version of the Park-Pham theorem allows us to use the conditional probability
\mathbb{P}\big(X_{p}\in f(U)|X_{p}\in f(P)\big)
to model the unconditional probability
\mathbb{P}(Y_{p}\in U)
for the random variable
Y_{p}
with distribution
\frac{\mu_{p}\circ f}{\mu_{p}\big(f(P)\big)}
(see Lemma
3.14
for a proof of this), where
P
here is a finite poset,
U
is an upper set in it, and
f:P\rightarrow 2^{X}
is an injection. That is, the conditional version is a toy that lets us show there is at least one case where the Park-Pham theorem can be extended to a more general context. The main result is the following:

Theorem
3.15

Let
P
be a finite poset, let
U\subseteq P
be an upper set, let
f:P\rightarrow 2^{X}
be an injection, and suppose
Kq(\langle f(U)\rangle)\log\ell_{0}(\langle f(U)\rangle)<1
. Writing
Y_{p}
for the random variable with distribution
\frac{1}{\mu_{p}\big(f(P)\big)}(\mu_{p}\circ f)
, there exists a value
0<a<1
such that for any
\epsilon\in(a,1)
a nonempty interval
I\subseteq(0,1)
can be found so that for all
p\in I
,
\mathbb{P}\big(Y_{p}\in U\big)>1-\epsilon.

Said another way, if the Park-Pham bounds for the upper set
\langle f(U)\rangle
tells us anything at all (i.e.
Kq(\langle f(U)\rangle)\log\ell_{0}(\langle f(U)\rangle)<1
), then we get the
\epsilon
-dependent result for the variables
Y_{p}
relative to the upper set
U
in the poset
P
. The only difference is the slight change in the acceptable values of
\epsilon
(i.e.
(a,1)
instead of
(0,1)
) and the potential existence of both a lower and upper bound on the values
p
such that we can ensure
\mathbb{P}\big(Y_{p}\in U\big)>1-\epsilon
(i.e. the interval
I\subseteq(0,1)
need not be of the form
(b,1)
for some
0<b<1
). This is sensible: If the poset
P
does not have a greatest element, then two nontrivial upper sets can have an empty intersection and
Y_{p}
can become ‘stuck’ in the lower set generated by a maximal element of
P
that does not belong to
U
.

To produce our conditional variant of the
\epsilon
-dependent Park-Pham theorem, we will first require a lemma so simple that it almost need not be noted.

Lemma 3.2
.

Let
(E,\mathcal{E})
be a measurable space and let
X
be a
(E,\mathcal{E})
-valued random variable. Suppose
A,B\in\mathcal{E}
where
A\subseteq B
and
\mathbb{P}(X\in A)\neq 0
. Then,

\mathbb{P}(X\in\langle A\rangle)=\frac{\mathbb{P}\big(X\in A|X\in B\big)\mathbb{P}\big(X\in B\big)}{\mathbb{P}\big(X\in A|X\in\langle A\rangle\big)}.

Proof.

If
\mathbb{P}(X\in A)\neq 0
, then
A\subseteq\langle A\rangle
and
A\subseteq B
gives
\mathbb{P}(X\in\langle A\rangle)\neq 0
and
\mathbb{P}(X\in B)\neq 0
. This lets us write

\mathbb{P}\big(X\in A|X\in\langle A\rangle\big)\mathbb{P}(X\in\langle A\rangle)=\mathbb{P}\big(X\in A\big)=\mathbb{P}\big(X\in A|X\in B\big)\mathbb{P}\big(X\in B\big).

∎

Alongside the previous basic result, we will temporarily need a small tool (which is, to a large extent, just for notational convenience). Given nested subsets
A\subseteq B\subseteq 2^{X}
, we can approximate how ’large’
B
is relative to how ’far up’
A
is in
2^{X}
by taking the ratio of the following two measurements: (1) for each
p
, how likely one is to obtain an element of
A
relative to the upper set it generates (i.e. how many new elements are gained when moving from
A
to
\langle A\rangle
tells us how ’far up’
A
is in
2^{X}
); and, (2), how ‘large’
B
is in
2^{X}
by measuring how likely one is to obtain an element of
B
at the same value of
p
. Here is the precise definition.

Definition 3.3
.

Let
\emptyset\neq A\subseteq B\subseteq 2^{X}
and write
X_{p}
for the random variable with distribution
\mu_{p}
. Let
r_{A,B}(p)
denote the quantity

r_{A,B}(p)=\frac{\mathbb{P}\big(X_{p}\in A|X_{p}\in\langle A\rangle\big)}{\mathbb{P}\big(X_{p}\in B\big)}.

Example 3.4
.

Let
B
correspond to the shaded portion of the Haase diagram for
2^{\left\{a,b,c\right\}}
as depicted below. Then, if
A=\big\{\left\{a\right\},\left\{c\right\}\big\}
, we have:
r_{A,B}(p)=2-\frac{2}{2-p}
, which is less than
1
for all
p\in(0,1)
.
\emptyset
\left\{a\right\}
\left\{b\right\}
\left\{c\right\}
\left\{a,b\right\}
\left\{a,c\right\}
\left\{b,c\right\}
\left\{a,b,c\right\}

Intuitively, if
r_{A,B}(p)
is greater than
1
, then
A
is much higher up than
B
is large, while the reverse says that
B
makes up a large part of
2^{X}
with
A
not too high up. This is the basic idea of how we will use it: Since we have
A\subseteq B
by assumption, Lemma
3.2
tells us that
r_{A,B}(p)\mathbb{P}(X_{p}\in\langle A\rangle)=\mathbb{P}\big(X_{p}\in A|X_{p}\in B\big)
. That is, if
r_{A,B}(p)>1
, then it is easier to randomly ‘hit’
A
in
B
than it is to ‘hit’
\langle A\rangle
in
2^{X}
, while having
r_{A,B}(p)<1
tells us the opposite. This gives us the following bargain-bin inequality:

Lemma 3.5
.

Let
X
be a finite set, suppose
\emptyset\neq A\subseteq B\subseteq 2^{X}
, and write
X_{p}
for the random variable with distribution
\mu_{p}
. Then,
\mathbb{P}\big(X_{p}\in A|X_{p}\in B\big)>\frac{1}{2}r_{A,B}(p)
whenever
p>Kq(\langle A\rangle)\log\ell\left(\langle A\rangle\right)
.

Proof.

By the Park-Pham theorem
[
11
]
(or, for a specific
K
, by Bell’s version in Theorem
2.3
), we have
\mathbb{P}(X_{p}\in\langle A\rangle)>1/2
whenever
p>Kq(\langle A\rangle)\log\ell\big(\langle A\rangle\big)
. Since
A\subseteq B
and by the definition of
\mu_{p}
, it is clear that
\mathbb{P}(X_{p}\in A)\neq 0
. By Lemma
3.2
, we get

\frac{1}{2}<\mathbb{P}(X_{p}\in\langle A\rangle)=\frac{\mathbb{P}\big(X_{p}\in A|X_{p}\in B\big)\mathbb{P}\big(X_{p}\in B\big)}{\mathbb{P}\big(X_{p}\in A|X_{p}\in\langle A\rangle\big)}=\frac{\mathbb{P}\big(X_{p}\in A|X_{p}\in B\big)}{r_{A,B}(p)}.

∎

Remark 3.6
.

It is straightforward to show that the above can be strengthened to the
\epsilon
-dependent version if
r_{A,B}(p)\geq 1
for all
p
. In this case, from Definition
3.3
, we have
\mathbb{P}\big(X_{p}\in A|X_{p}\in B\big)\geq\mathbb{P}(X_{p}\in\langle A\rangle\big)
. So, if
p>48q(\langle A\rangle)\log\left(\frac{\ell_{0}\big(\langle A\rangle\big)}{\epsilon}\right)
, we immediately get
\mathbb{P}\big(X_{p}\in A|X_{p}\in B\big)\geq\mathbb{P}(X_{p}\in\langle A\rangle\big)>1-\epsilon
by Theorem
2.5
.

To contrast how the
\epsilon
-dependent form is obtained Remark
3.6

r_{A,B}(p)<1

.

\displaystyle\epsilon

\displaystyle>1-r_{A,B}(p)\left(1-\ell_{0}(\langle A\rangle)2^{-p/\big(Kq(\langle A\rangle)\big)}\right).

Now, from
S\subseteq P
, we have
A\subseteq B
, and by the definition of
\mu_{p}
it is clear that
\mathbb{P}(X_{p}\in A)\neq 0
. By Lemma
3.2
, we get

\displaystyle(1-\eta)=(1-\epsilon)\frac{1}{r_{A,B}(p)}

\displaystyle<\frac{\mathbb{P}\big(X_{p}\in A|X_{p}\in B\big)}{r_{A,B}(p)}=\mathbb{P}(X_{p}\in\langle A\rangle)

\displaystyle(1-\epsilon)

\displaystyle<\mathbb{P}\big(X_{p}\in A|X_{p}\in B\big).

∎

Remark 3.8
.

The values of
\epsilon
satisfying the inequality in Lemma
3.7
are not very optimal when
r_{A,B}(p)\geq 1
, since (as noted in Remark
3.6
) Lemma
3.5
can be strengthened to
\mathbb{P}(X_{p}\in A\mid X_{p}\in B)>1-\epsilon
for any
\epsilon\in(0,1)
whenever
p
exceeds the bound given in Theorem
2.5
for the upper set
\langle A\rangle
.

Example 3.9
.

Consider
A\subseteq B\subseteq 2^{\left\{a,b,c\right\}}
as in Example
3.4
. Then, Lemma
3.7
tell us that
\mathbb{P}\big(X_{p}\in A|X_{p}\in B\big)>1-\epsilon
whenever

\displaystyle\epsilon

\displaystyle>1-r_{A,B}(p)\left(1-\ell_{0}(\langle A\rangle)2^{-p/\big(Kq(\langle A\rangle)\big)}\right)

\displaystyle\epsilon

\displaystyle>1-\left(2-\frac{2}{2-p}\right)\left(1-2^{-4p/K}\right)

Taking
K=48
as in Theorem
2.5
, we get that the minimum of
1-\left(2-\frac{2}{2-p}\right)\left(1-2^{-p/12}\right)
on the interval
[0,1]
occurs when
p=0.582289
and has a value of approximately
0.98051
. Therefore, it is possible to select a value
p\in(0,1)
such that
\mathbb{P}\big(X_{p}\in A|X_{p}\in B\big)>1-\epsilon
for any
\epsilon
satisfying
0.98051<\epsilon<1
. Indeed, this value can be found by first choosing such an epsilon and solving the inequality
p>48q(\langle A\rangle)\log\left(\frac{r_{A,B}(p)\ell_{0}(\langle A\rangle)}{r_{A,B}(p)-(1-\epsilon)}\right)
. For example, if
\epsilon=0.99
, then taking
p
to satisfy
0.195217<p<0.889027
then ensures that
\mathbb{P}\big(X_{p}\in A|X_{p}\in B\big)>0.01
.

In the previous example, since
A
is not an upper set in
B
, it is sensible that there should be an upper bound for
p
as well as a lower bound. As the next example shows, if we take an upper set in
B
, then this upper bound should vanish.

Example 3.10
.

If, in Example
3.4
and Example
3.9
, we had instead taken
A^{\prime}=\left\{\left\{a\right\},\left\{c\right\},\left\{a,c\right\}\right\}
so that
A^{\prime}
becomes an upper set in
B
, then it is routine to show that we get
r_{A^{\prime},B}(p)=1
. In this case, Lemma
3.7
tells us that
\mathbb{P}\big(X_{p}\in A^{\prime}|X_{p}\in B\big)>1-\epsilon
whenever

\displaystyle\epsilon

\displaystyle>1-r_{A^{\prime},B}(p)\left(1-\ell_{0}(\langle A^{\prime}\rangle)2^{-p/\big(Kq(\langle A^{\prime}\rangle)\big)}\right)

\displaystyle\epsilon

\displaystyle>1-\left(1-2^{-4p/K}\right)=2^{-4p/K}

Taking
K=48
again as in Theorem
2.5
, this says
\epsilon>1-2^{-p/12}
. Since
2^{-p/12}
is minimized on
[0,1]
at
p=1
where it takes on the value
2^{-1/12}\approx 0.943874
, it is possible to select a value
p\in(0,1)
such that
\mathbb{P}\big(X_{p}\in A^{\prime}|X_{p}\in B\big)>1-\epsilon
for any
\epsilon
satisfying
0.943874<\epsilon<1
. As before, we find this value by first choosing such an
\epsilon
and solving the inequality
p>48q(\langle A^{\prime}\rangle)\log\left(\frac{r_{A^{\prime},B}(p)\ell_{0}(\langle A^{\prime}\rangle)}{r_{A^{\prime},B}(p)-(1-\epsilon)}\right)=12\log\left(\frac{1}{\epsilon}\right)
. For example, if
\epsilon=0.99
as before, then taking
p>0.173995
ensures that
\mathbb{P}\big(X_{p}\in A^{\prime}|X_{p}\in B\big)>0.01
.

Remark 3.11
.

Example
3.10
shows that we do not always get an upper bound on
p
like we did in Example
3.9
. This is as it should be. For any subset
A^{\prime}\subseteq B\subseteq 2^{X}
that is an upper set in
B
and contains the maximal elements of
B
, increasing
p
can only make it more likely for
X_{p}
to land in
A^{\prime}
when it is already in
B
. This also highlights what was said in Remark
3.8
: Indeed, the bounds we get from Theorem
3.7
are not very good in cases like this where the
p
-ratio is never smaller than
1
. Here, we can only ensure that
\mathbb{P}\big(X_{p}\in A^{\prime}|X_{p}\in B\big)>1-\epsilon
for, approximately, the range
0.943874<\epsilon<1
. Using Lemma
3.5
instead, it is easy to see that we can actually do this for any
\epsilon\in(0,1)
.

To finish, we note that our use of the quantity
r_{A,B}(p)
has been a bit silly so far: If we need to compute an expression involving
r_{A,B}(p)
to say something about
\mathbb{P}(X_{p}\in A|X_{p}\in B)
, we most likely have not made our job any easier. The next result, however, shows that we do not actually need to perform this computation. In fact, any time the Kahn-Kalai bounds give us any information at all about
p_{c}(\langle A\rangle)
, it is possible to bound the conditional probability below, up to a certain maximum value, by choosing
p
within some subinterval of
(0,1)
.

Lemma 3.12
.

Let
X
be a finite set and suppose
\emptyset\neq A\subseteq B\subseteq 2^{X}
. Whenever
Kq(\langle A\rangle)\log\ell_{0}(\langle A\rangle)<1
, there is some
0<a<1
such that for every
\epsilon\in(a,1)
the inequality

\epsilon>1-r_{A,B}(p)\left(1-\ell_{0}(\langle A\rangle)2^{-p/\big(Kq(\langle A\rangle)\big)}\right)

holds for all
p
in some nonempty subinterval of
(0,1)
.

Proof.

Write
g(p):=1-r_{A,B}(p)\left(1-\ell_{0}(\langle A\rangle)2^{-p/\big(Kq(\langle A\rangle)\big)}\right)
and suppose there is no pair of values
(p,\epsilon)\in(0,1)^{2}
such that the inequality
\epsilon>g(p)
holds. Then, for all
p\in(0,1)
, it must be the case that
g(p)\geq 1
. Suppose as well that
Kq(\langle A\rangle)\log\ell_{0}(\langle A\rangle)<1
and take
1>p>Kq(\langle A\rangle)\log\ell_{0}(\langle A\rangle)
. Then, we have

\displaystyle g(p)=1-r_{A,B}(p)\left(1-\ell_{0}(\langle A\rangle)2^{-p/\big(Kq(\langle A\rangle)\big)}\right)

\displaystyle\geq 1

\displaystyle 1-\ell_{0}(\langle A\rangle)2^{-p/\big(Kq(\langle A\rangle)\big)}

\displaystyle\leq 0

\displaystyle 2^{p/\big(Kq(\langle A\rangle)\big)}

\displaystyle\leq\ell_{0}(\langle A\rangle)

\displaystyle p

\displaystyle\leq Kq(\langle A\rangle)\log\ell_{0}(\langle A\rangle).

But the last line is a contradiction, so
\textrm{min}_{p\in(0,1)}g(p)<1
. Hence, for any
\epsilon\in(\textrm{min}_{p\in(0,1)}g(p),1)
, the continuity of
g
yields that
g^{-1}(-\infty,\epsilon)
is open and has a nonempty intersection with
(0,1)
.
∎

Dispensing now with the crutch of
r_{A,B}(p)
, we reach the following as a corollary.

Corollary 3.13
.

Suppose
\emptyset\neq A\subseteq B\subseteq 2^{X}
. If
Kq(\langle A\rangle)\log\ell_{0}(\langle A\rangle)<1
, then there is some
0<a<1
such that for any
\epsilon\in(a,1)
, a nonempty subinterval interval
I\subseteq(0,1)
can be found such that for all
p\in I
,

\mathbb{P}\big(X_{p}\in A|X_{p}\in B\big)>1-\epsilon.

Using the following small lemma, the previous result gives an extension of the
\epsilon
-dependent Park-Pham theorem:

Lemma 3.14
.

Let
P
be a poset and let
f:P\rightarrow 2^{X}
be an injection, where
X
is some sufficiently large finite set. Taking the sigma algebra generated by
f
and a
P
-valued random variable
Y_{p}
with distribution
\frac{1}{\mu_{p}\big(f(P)\big)}(\mu_{p}\circ f)
, then for
S\in\sigma(f)
, we have

\mathbb{P}(Y_{p}\in S)=\mathbb{P}\big(X_{p}\in f(S)|X_{p}\in f(P)\big).

Proof.

If
f:P\rightarrow 2^{X}
is injective, then
f(A)\cap f(B)=f(A\cap B)
for all
A,B\subseteq P
. So,

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\big(X_{p}\in f(S)|X_{p}\in f(P)\big)

\displaystyle=\frac{\mathbb{P}\big(X_{p}^{-1}(f(S))\cap X_{p}^{-1}(f(P))\big)}{\mathbb{P}\big(X_{p}^{-1}(f(P))\big)}

\displaystyle=\frac{\mathbb{P}\big(X_{p}^{-1}(f(S)\cap f(P))\big)}{\mathbb{P}\big(X_{p}^{-1}(f(P))\big)}

\displaystyle=\frac{\mathbb{P}\big(X_{p}^{-1}(f(S\cap P))\big)}{\mathbb{P}\big(X_{p}^{-1}(f(P))\big)}

\displaystyle=\mathbb{P}(Y_{p}\in S).

∎

The immediate consequence of Lemma
3.14
and Corollary
3.13
is the following:

Theorem 3.15
.

Let
P
be a finite poset, let
U\subseteq P
be an upper set, let
f:P\rightarrow 2^{X}
be an injection, and suppose
Kq(\langle f(U)\rangle)\log\ell_{0}(\langle f(U)\rangle)<1
. Writing
Y_{p}
for the random variable with distribution
\frac{1}{\mu_{p}\big(f(P)\big)}(\mu_{p}\circ f)
, there exists a value
0<a<1
such that for any
\epsilon\in(a,1)
a nonempty interval
I\subseteq(0,1)
can be found so that for all
p\in I
,
\mathbb{P}\big(Y_{p}\in U\big)>1-\epsilon.

4.
Conclusion

In this paper, we have shown that the
\epsilon
-dependent Park-Pham theorem can be generalized to a conditional form for subsets
\emptyset\neq A\subseteq B\subseteq 2^{X}
, albeit with a smaller range of values
\epsilon
and with a potential upper and lower bound (instead of just a lower bound) on the values
p
for which we can ensure
\mathbb{P}(X_{p}\in A|X_{p}\in B)>1-\epsilon
. Since one can always embed a finite poset in the power set of a finite set, this shows that there is an analog to the Park-Pham theorem applicable to random variables with distributions other than
\mu_{p}
and upper sets
U
in posets other than
2^{X}
(indeed, it demonstrates it for at least one case, see Theorem
3.15
and the discussion in Remark
3.1
).

A great deal of further work along this line is possible–for instance, using the non-uniform version of the Park-Pham theorem recently proven in
[
9
]
, Theorem
3.15
could be further generalized to admit a wider range of random variables. We end with a few questions: In what cases does one have the upper bound on the interval
I
in Theorem
3.15
disappear? Is there some easy-to-check condition an upper set
\mathcal{F}\subseteq 2^{X}
must satisfy to ensure that
Kq(\mathcal{F})\log\ell_{0}(\mathcal{F})<1
? What are the most minimally-restrictive requirements a parameterized family of random variables
Y_{p}
must satisfy to admit a Park-Pham-type theorem (i.e. a result analogous to Theorem
3.15
)? What is the appropriate generalization of thresholds to upper sets in other posets and other parameterized families of random variables?
