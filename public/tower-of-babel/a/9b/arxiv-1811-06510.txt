Abstract
. We prove anti-concentration bounds for the inner product of two independent random vectors. For example, we show that if A , B A,B are subsets of the cube { ± 1 } n \{\pm 1\}^{n} with | A | ⋅ | B | ≥ 2 1.01 ​ n |A|\cdot|B|\geq 2^{1.01n} , and X ∈ A X\in A and Y ∈ B Y\in B are sampled independently and uniformly, then the inner product ⟨ X , Y ⟩ \left\langle X,Y\right\rangle takes on any fixed value with probability at most O ⁡ ( 1 n ) O(\tfrac{1}{\sqrt{n}}) . Extending Halász work, we prove stronger bounds when the choices for x x are unstructured. We also describe applications to communication complexity, randomness extraction and additive combinatorics.



1.
Introduction

Anti-concentration bounds establish that the distribution of outcomes of a random process is
not concentrated in any small region. No single outcome is obtained too often.
Anti-concentration plays an important role in
mathematics and computer
science. It is used in the study of roots of random polynomials
[
16
]
,
random matrix theory
[
13
,
23
]
,
communication complexity
[
4
,
26
,
19
]
,
quantum computation
[
1
]
,
and more.

A well-known example is the sum of independent identically distributed random
variables. If
Y\in\{\pm 1\}^{n}
is uniformly distributed, then
the probability that
\sum_{j=1}^{n}Y_{j}
takes any specific value
is at most
\binom{n}{\lceil n/2\rceil}/2^{n}=O(\tfrac{1}{\sqrt{n}})
.

This was studied and generalized
by Littlewood and Offord
[
16
]
,
Erdös
[
7
]
,
and many others. The classical Littlewood-Offord problem is about understanding the anti-concentration of the inner product
\left\langle x,Y\right\rangle=\sum_{j=1}^{n}x_{j}Y_{j}
, for arbitrary
x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
and
Y\in\{\pm 1\}^{n}
chosen uniformly.
Various generalizations were
studied by Frankl and Furedi
[
10
]
,
Halász
[
11
]

and others.

It is interesting to understand the most general conditions under which anti-concentration holds (see
[
22
]

and references within).
Anti-concentration certainly fails when the
entries of
Y
are not independent.
We can, for example, sample
Y
uniformly from the set of strings with exactly
\lceil n/2\rceil
entries that are
1
. Then
\sum_{j}Y_{j}
is always the same, yet
Y
has almost full entropy.

Can we somehow recover anti-concentration?
A natural setting is to consider the inner-product
\left\langle X,Y\right\rangle
of two independent variables.
This indeed recovers anti-concentration,
as the following theorem shows.

Theorem

(Chakrabarti and Regev
[
4
]
)
.

There is a constant
c>0
such that if
A,B\subseteq\{\pm 1\}^{n}
are each of size at least
2^{(1-c)n}
and
X\in A,Y\in B
are sampled uniformly and independently, then

\Pr[|\left\langle X,Y\right\rangle|\leq c\sqrt{n}]\leq 1-c.

The theorem shows that
\left\langle X,Y\right\rangle

does not land in an interval of length
much smaller than
\sqrt{n}
with high probability.
When studying anti-concentration, however,
what we are ultimately interested in is
proving point-wise estimates.
We would like to control the

concentration probability

\max_{k\in{\mathbb{Z}}}\ \Pr[\left\langle X,Y\right\rangle=k];

see
[
23
]

and references within.

Here we prove
a sharp bound on the concentration probability.
The bound holds for an overwhelming majority of
directions
x
.

Theorem 1
.

For every
\beta>0
and

\delta>0
,
there exists
C>0
such that the following holds. If
B\subseteq\{\pm 1\}^{n}
is of size
2^{\beta n}
, then for all but
2^{n(1-\beta+\delta)}
directions
x\in\{\pm 1\}^{n}
,

\max_{k\in{\mathbb{Z}}}\Pr_{Y}[\left\langle x,Y\right\rangle=k]\leq\tfrac{C}{\sqrt{n}}.

The theorem is sharp in the following sense.
As mentioned above, the
O(\tfrac{1}{\sqrt{n}})
bound is tight even when
A
and
B
are
\{\pm 1\}^{n}
.
To see that the bound on the number of bad directions is sharp, observe that if
B\subset\{\pm 1\}^{n}
is the set of
y
’s where the first
(1-\beta)n
coordinates are set to
1
and
\sum_{j>(1-\beta)n}y_{j}=0
, and
A\subset\{\pm 1\}^{n}
is the set of
x
’s where the last
\beta n
coordinates are set to
1
and
\sum_{j\leq(1-\beta)n}x_{j}=0
, then

|B|\approx\tfrac{1}{\sqrt{n}}2^{\beta n}\quad\&\quad|A|\approx\tfrac{1}{\sqrt{n}}2^{(1-\beta)n},

yet

\left\langle x,y\right\rangle=0
for every
x\in A
and
y\in B
. There is no anti-concentration in this case,
although
|A|\cdot|B|
is roughly
2^{n}
.

Our proof builds a flexible framework for proving anti-concentration results in discrete domains. We use this framework to prove a more general theorem, stated below. The theorem extends results from
[
8
,
18
,
20
,
11
]

from the uniform distribution on
\{\pm 1\}^{n}

to the case of non-uniform distributions.

When
Y
is uniformly distributed, the additive structure of the entries in the direction vector
x

controls anti-concentration
[
10
]
. If
x
is unstructured, we get stronger anti-concentration bounds for
\left\langle x,Y\right\rangle
.
This idea is instrumental when analyzing random
matrices
[
13
,
23
]
.

We choose the direction
x

from sets of the following form.
We call a set
A\subset{\mathbb{Z}}^{n}
a two-cube
if
A=A_{1}\times A_{2}\times\cdots A_{n}
,
where each
A_{j}=\{u_{j},v_{j}\}
consists of two distinct integers.
The differences of
A
are the numbers
d_{j}=u_{j}-v_{j}

for
j\in[n]
.

The following theorem describes three cases
that yield different anti-concentration bounds.
It shows that the additive structure of

A
is deeply related to the bounds we obtain.
The less structured
A
is, the stronger the bounds are.

The first bound in the theorem holds for arbitrary two-cubes.
The second bound holds
when all the differences
d_{1},\ldots,d_{n}
are distinct.
The third bound applies in more general settings where
the set of differences is unstructured.
This is captured by the following definition.
A set
S\subset\mathbb{N}
of size
n
is called a Sidon set, or a Golomb ruler, if the number of solutions to the equation

s_{1}+s_{2}=s_{3}+s_{4}
for
s_{1},s_{2},s_{3},s_{4}\in S

is
4\cdot{n\choose 2}+n
.
In other words, every pair of integers has a distinct sum.
Sidon sets were defined by Erdös and Turán
[
9
]

and have been studied by many others since.
We say that

S\subset{\mathbb{Z}}
is a weak Sidon set
if the number of solutions to the equation

\epsilon_{1}s_{1}+\epsilon_{2}s_{2}=\epsilon_{3}s_{3}+\epsilon_{4}s_{4}
for

\epsilon_{1},\ldots,\epsilon_{4}\in\{\pm 1\}
and

s_{1},\ldots,s_{4}\in S

is at most
100n^{2}
. The number
100
can be replaced by any other constant, we use it here just to be concrete.

Theorem 2
.

For every
\beta>0
and
\delta>0
,
there exists
C>0
such that the following holds.
Let
A\subset{\mathbb{Z}}^{n}
is a two-cube with differences

d_{1},\ldots,d_{n}
.
Let
B\subseteq\{\pm 1\}^{n}
be of size
2^{\beta n}
.
Let
Y
be uniformly distributed in
B
.

(1)

For all but
2^{n(1-\beta+\delta)}
directions
x\in A
,

\max_{k\in{\mathbb{Z}}}\Pr_{Y}\left[\left\langle x,Y\right\rangle=k\right]\leq C\sqrt{\ln(n)}n^{-0.5}.

(2)

If
d_{1},\ldots,d_{n}
are distinct, then
for all but
2^{n(1-\beta+\delta)}
directions
x\in A
,

\max_{k\in{\mathbb{Z}}}\Pr_{Y}\left[\left\langle x,Y\right\rangle=k\right]\leq C\sqrt{\ln(n)}n^{-1.5}.

(3)

If
\{d_{1},\ldots,d_{n}\}
is a weak Sidon set of size
n
, then
for all but
2^{n(1-\beta+\delta)}
directions
x\in A
,

\max_{k\in{\mathbb{Z}}}\Pr_{Y}\left[\left\langle x,Y\right\rangle=k\right]\leq C\sqrt{\ln(n)}n^{-2.5}.

The first bound in Theorem
2
nearly implies Theorem
1
. It is weaker by a factor of
\sqrt{\ln(n)}
. However, it holds for all two-cubes, not just the hypercube
\{\pm 1\}^{n}
.
The second bound almost
matches the sharp
O(n^{-1.5})
bound
that holds when
(u_{j},v_{j})=(j,-j)
for each
j
and
Y
is uniform in the hypercube
[
18
,
20
]
.
We believe that the
\sqrt{\ln(n)}
factor
is not needed, but were not able to eliminate it.
The theorem is, in fact, part of a more general phenomenon.
We postpone the full technical description to
Section
4
.

The proof.

Chakrabarti and Regev’s proof
uses the deep connection between the
discrete cube and Gaussian space.
They proved a geometric correlation inequality in
Gaussian space, and then translated it to the cube.
Vidick
[
26
]

later simplified part of their argument,
but stayed in the geometric setting.
Sherstov
[
19
]

found a third proof
that uses Talagrand’s inequality from convex geometry
[
21
]

and ideas of Babai, Frankl and Simon
from communication complexity
[
2
]
.

There are several differences between our argument and the ones in
[
4
,
26
,
19
]
.
The main difference is that
the arguments from
[
4
,
26
,
19
]
are based, in one way or another,
on the geometry of Euclidean space.
The arguments in
[
4
,
26
]

prove a correlation inequality in Gaussian space and translate
it to the discrete world.
It seems that such an argument
can not yield point-wise bounds on the
concentration probability.
A common ingredient in
[
4
,
19
]

is a step showing that every set of large enough measure
contains many almost orthogonal vectors
(this is called ‘identifying the hard core’ in
[
19
]
).
In
[
26
]
this part of the argument
is replaced by a statement about a relevant matrix.
Our argument does not contain such steps.

Let us briefly discuss our proof at a high level.
The proof is based on harmonic analysis (Section
2
).
The argument consists of two parts.
In the first part, we analyze the Fourier behavior
of
\left\langle x,Y\right\rangle

for
x
fixed and
Y
random.
We are able to identify a collection
of good
x
’s for which the Fourier spectrum
of the distribution of
\left\langle x,Y\right\rangle
decays rapidly.
In the second part,
we show that the number of bad
x
’s
is small by giving an explicit encoding of all of them.

Although the proofs of the two theorems
follow a similar strategy,
we were not able to completely merge them.
The proof for the hypercube (Theorem
1
)
is carried in Section
3
.
The proof for general two-cubes (Theorem
2
)
is given in Section
4
.

Remark
.

Theorem
2
can be used as a black box to prove the same bounds when
A_{1},\dotsc,A_{n}
are pairs of real numbers. To see this, think of the relevant real numbers as a finite dimensional vector space
over the rationals.

Applications

Communication Complexity

Chakrabarti and Regev’s main motivation was understanding
the randomized communication
complexity of the gap-hamming problem.
The gap-hamming problem was introduced
by Indyk and Woodruff in the context of streaming algorithms
[
12
]
.
Proving lower bounds on its communication
complexity was a central open problem for almost ten years,
until Chakrabarti and Regev solved it
[
4
]
.
Vidick
[
26
]
and Sherstov
[
19
]

later simplified the proof.

Our results also imply the lower bound for the randomized
communication complexity of the gap-hamming problem
(see e.g.
[
19
]
).
As opposed to
[
4
,
26
,
19
]
, the proof presented here lies entirely in the discrete domain.
The underlying ideas may therefore be
of independent interest.

Pseudorandomness

Randomness is a computational resource
[
25
]
.
There are many sources of randomness,
and some of them are
weak
or imperfect.
Randomness extractors allow to use weak sources
of randomness as if they were perfect.

The study of randomness extractors is
about constructing explicit maps
that transform weak sources of randomness
to almost uniform outputs.
The main goal
is generating a uniform output
in the most general scenario possible.
This often requires ingenious constructions.

The scenario described above fits
nicely in the context of

two-source extractors
.
A two-source extractor maps two independent
random variables
X
and
Y
with significant min-entropy
to a single almost uniform output.

Chor and Goldreich
[
6
]
used
Lindsey’s lemma to show that
inner product modulo two is a two-source extractor.
Namely, the distribution of the bit
\left\langle X,Y\right\rangle\ \mathsf{mod}\ 2

is close to uniform as long as
|A|\cdot|B|\gg 2^{n}
.
Bourgain
[
3
]
,
Raz
[
17
]
and
Chattopadhyay and Zuckerman
[
5
]

constructed two-source extractors with much better
parameters. In our work, we study a related but somewhat
different question.

The high-level suggestion is to
investigate other pseudorandom properties satisfied by
known extractors. We already know that inner product is an excellent
two-source extractor. Our work shows that the
inner product is anti-concentrated over the integers. Theorems
1

and
2
, in fact, imply a stronger statement.
It is the analog of “strong” extraction in extractor lingo.
Not only is
\left\langle X,Y\right\rangle
anti-concentrated,
but an overwhelming majority of fixings
X=x
lead to
\left\langle x,Y\right\rangle
being anti-concentrated.

Additive Combinatorics

Additive combinatorics studies the behavior
of sets under algebraic operations
[
24
]
.
It has many deep results,
and connections to other areas of mathematics,
as well as many applications in computer science.
Our main result can be interpreted
as showing that Hamming spheres are far from being sum-sets. Our results give quantitative bounds on the size of the intersection of any Hamming sphere with a sum-set.

Replace
\{\pm 1\}
by the field
\mathbb{F}_{2}

with two elements.
The sum-set of
A\subseteq\mathbb{F}_{2}^{n}

and
B\subseteq\mathbb{F}_{2}^{n}
is

A+B=\{x+y:x\in A,y\in B\}.

If
X
and
Y
are sampled uniformly at random from
A
and
B
, then
X+Y
is supported on
A+B
.

The cube
\mathbb{F}_{2}^{n}
is endowed with a natural metric—the Hamming distance
\Delta(x,y)
.
The sphere around
0
is the collection
of all vectors with a fixed number of ones in them
(a.k.a. a slice).
The inner product
I=\sum_{j}(-1)^{X_{j}}(-1)^{Y_{j}}

is similar to the inner product studied above
(here
X_{j},Y_{j}\in\{0,1\}
).
The inner product is related to the Hamming distance
by
I(X,Y)=n-2\Delta(X,Y)
. We saw that if
|A|\cdot|B|>2^{1.01n}
, then
I
is anti-concentrated.
We can conclude that the distribution of the Hamming
distance of
X+Y
is anti-concentrated.
The set
A+B
is far from any sphere.

2.
Harmonic Analysis

We are interested in proving
anti-concentration for integer-valued random variables. Harmonic analysis is a natural framework
for studying such random variables
[
11
]
.

Let
Y
be distributed in
\{\pm 1\}^{n}
.
Let
x\in{\mathbb{Z}}^{n}
be a direction.
Let
\theta
be uniformly distributed in
[0,1]
,
independently of
Y
.
The idea is to use

\displaystyle\Pr_{Y}[\left\langle x,Y\right\rangle=k]

\displaystyle=\mathop{\mathbb{E}}\displaylimits_{Y}\left[\mathop{\mathbb{E}}\displaylimits_{\theta}\left[\exp(2\pi i\theta\cdot(\left\langle x,Y\right\rangle-k))\right]\right]

to bound

(
\star
)

\displaystyle\max_{k\in{\mathbb{Z}}}\Pr_{Y}[\left\langle x,Y\right\rangle=k]\leq\mathop{\mathbb{E}}\displaylimits_{\theta}\left[\Big|\mathop{\mathbb{E}}\displaylimits_{Y}\left[\exp(2\pi i\theta\cdot\left\langle x,Y\right\rangle)\right]\Big|\right].

This inequality is useful for two reasons.
First, the left hand side is a maximum over
k
,
while the right hand side is not.
So, there is one less quantifier to worry about.
Secondly, the right hand side lives in the Fourier
world, where it is easier to argue about
the underlying operators.
For example, when the coordinates of
Y

are independent, the expectation over
Y

breaks into a product of
n
simple terms.

3.
The Hypercube

We start by considering directions in the hypercube
\{\pm 1\}^{n}
.
The following theorem controls the Fourier coefficients in most
directions.

Theorem 3
.

For every
\beta>0

and
\delta>0
,
there is
c>0
so that the following holds.
Let
B\subseteq\{\pm 1\}^{n}
be of size
2^{\beta n}
.
For each
\theta\in[0,1]
,
for all but
2^{n(1-\beta+\delta)}
directions

x\in\{\pm 1\}^{n}
,

\displaystyle\left|\mathop{\mathbb{E}}\displaylimits_{Y}\left[\exp(2\pi i\theta\cdot\left\langle x,Y\right\rangle)\right]\right|>2\exp(-cn\sin^{2}(4\pi\theta))

The rest of this section is devoted for proving
this theorem
(the proof appears in Section
3.3
).

Proof of Theorem
1
.

Theorem
3
promises that for each
\theta\in[0,1]
,
the size of

\displaystyle A_{\theta}=\Big\{x\in\{\pm 1\}^{n}:\Big|\mathop{\mathbb{E}}\displaylimits_{Y}\left[\exp(2\pi i\theta\cdot\left\langle x,Y\right\rangle)\right]\Big|>2\exp(-cn\sin^{2}(4\pi\theta))\Big\}

is at most
2^{n(1-\beta+\delta)}
.
For each
x
, define

S_{x}=\{\theta\in[0,1]:x\in A_{\theta}\}.

Since

\mathop{\mathbb{}}{E}_{x}|S_{x}|=\mathop{\mathbb{}}{E}_{\theta}\tfrac{|A_{\theta}|}{2^{n}}\leq 2^{n(-\beta+\delta)},

by Markov’s inequality,
the number of
x\in A
for which
|S_{x}|>2^{-\delta n}
is at most
2^{(1-\beta+2\delta)n}
.
Fix
x
such that
|S_{x}|\leq 2^{-\delta n}
.
Bound

\displaystyle\Pr_{Y}[\left\langle x,Y\right\rangle=k]

\displaystyle\leq\mathop{\mathbb{E}}\displaylimits_{\theta}\left[\Big|\mathop{\mathbb{E}}\displaylimits_{Y}\left[\exp(2\pi i\theta\cdot\left\langle x,Y\right\rangle)\right]\Big|\right]

\displaystyle\leq 2^{-\delta n}+2\int_{0}^{1}\exp(-cn\sin^{2}(4\pi\theta))\,\mathrm{d}\theta.

Each term in the integral occurs eight times;
it circles the origin twice.
Using the inequality
\sin(\zeta)>\tfrac{\zeta}{\pi}
for
0<\zeta<\tfrac{\pi}{2}
, we can bound it by

\displaystyle\leq 2^{-\delta n}+16\int_{0}^{1/8}\exp(-16cn\theta^{2})\,\mathrm{d}\theta

\displaystyle\leq 2^{-\delta n}+\frac{8}{\sqrt{n}}\cdot\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\exp(-16c\zeta^{2})\,\mathrm{d}\zeta.

The integral converges, so this quantity is at most
\tfrac{C}{\sqrt{n}}
.

∎

3.1.
A Single Direction

In this section we analyze
the behavior of
\left\langle x,Y\right\rangle
for a single direction
x\in{\mathbb{Z}}^{n}
.
We also focus on a single Fourier coefficient
\mathop{\mathbb{E}}\displaylimits_{Y}\left[\exp({i\eta\left\langle x,Y\right\rangle})\right]
for a fixed
angle
\eta\in[0,2\pi]
.

We reveal the entropy of
Y
coordinate by coordinate.
To keep track of this entropy, define
the following functions
\gamma_{1},\ldots,\gamma_{n}

from
B=\text{supp}(Y)
to
\mathbb{R}
.
For each
j\in[n]
, let

\gamma_{j}(y)=\gamma_{j}(y_{<j})=\min_{\epsilon\in\{\pm 1\}}\Pr[Y_{j}=\epsilon|Y_{<j}=y_{<j}].

To understand the interaction between
x

and
y
, we use the following
n
measurements.
For
j\in[n-1]
,
define
\phi_{j}(x,y)
to be half of the phase of the complex number

\mathop{\mathbb{E}}\displaylimits_{Y_{>j}|Y_{j}=1,Y_{<j}=y_{<j}}\left[\exp({i\eta\left\langle x_{>j},Y_{>j}\right\rangle})\right]\cdot\overline{\mathop{\mathbb{E}}\displaylimits_{Y_{>j}|Y_{j}=-1,Y_{<j}=y_{<j}}\left[\exp({i\eta\left\langle x_{>j},Y_{>j}\right\rangle})\right]}.

This quantity is not defined when
\gamma_{j}(y)=0
.
In this case, set
\phi_{j}(x,y)
to be zero.
Define
\phi_{n}(x,y)
to be zero.
The number
\phi_{j}(x,y)
is determined by
y_{<j}
and
x_{>j}
.

In the following we think of
x
as fixed,
and of
\gamma_{j}
and
\phi_{j}
as random variables
that are determined by the random variable
Y
.

Lemma 4
.

For each
x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
,
every random variable
Y
over
\{\pm 1\}^{n}
,
and every angle
\eta\in\mathbb{R}
,

\displaystyle\left|\mathop{\mathbb{E}}\displaylimits_{Y}\left[\exp({i\eta\left\langle x,Y\right\rangle})\right]\right|^{2}

\displaystyle\leq\mathop{\mathbb{E}}\displaylimits_{Y}\left[\prod_{i\in[n]}(1-\gamma_{j}\sin^{2}(\phi_{j}+x_{j}\eta))\right].

Proof.

The proof is by induction on
n
.
We prove the base case of the induction and the inductive step
simultaneously.
Express

\displaystyle\left|\mathop{\mathbb{E}}\displaylimits_{Y}\left[\exp(i\eta\left\langle x,Y\right\rangle)\right]\right|^{2}

\displaystyle=\left|\mathop{\mathbb{E}}\displaylimits_{Y_{1}}\left[\exp(i\eta x_{1}Y_{1})\cdot\mathop{\mathbb{E}}\displaylimits_{Y_{>1}|Y_{1}}\left[\exp(i\eta\left\langle x_{>1},Y_{>1}\right\rangle)\right]\right]\right|^{2}

\displaystyle=\left|p_{1}\exp(i\eta x_{1})Z_{1}+p_{-1}\exp(-i\eta x_{1})Z_{-1}\right|^{2},

where for
\epsilon\in\{\pm 1\}
,

p_{\epsilon}=\Pr[Y_{1}=\epsilon]\qquad\&\qquad Z_{\epsilon}=\mathop{\mathbb{E}}\displaylimits_{Y|Y_{1}=\epsilon}\left[\exp(i\eta\left\langle x_{>1},Y_{>1}\right\rangle)\right].

When
n=1
, we have
Z_{1}=Z_{-1}=1
. Rearranging,

\displaystyle\left|p_{1}\exp(i\eta x_{1})Z_{1}+p_{-1}\exp(-i\eta x_{1})Z_{-1}\right|^{2}

\displaystyle=p_{1}^{2}|Z_{1}|^{2}+p_{-1}^{2}|Z_{-1}|^{2}+p_{1}p_{-1}(Z_{1}\overline{Z_{-1}}\exp(i2\eta x_{1})+\overline{Z_{1}}Z_{-1}\exp(-i2\eta x_{1}))

\displaystyle=p_{1}^{2}|Z_{1}|^{2}+p_{-1}^{2}|Z_{-1}|^{2}+2p_{1}p_{-1}|Z_{1}||Z_{-1}|\cos(2\phi_{1}+2x_{1}\eta).

The last equality holds by the definition of
\phi_{1}
.

There are two cases to consider.
When
\cos(2\phi_{1}+2x_{1}\eta)<0
, we continue to bound

\displaystyle<p_{1}^{2}|Z_{1}|^{2}+p_{-1}^{2}|Z_{-1}|^{2}

\displaystyle\leq(p_{1}|Z_{1}|^{2}+p_{-1}|Z_{-1}|^{2})(1-\gamma_{1})

\displaystyle\leq(p_{1}|Z_{1}|^{2}+p_{-1}|Z_{-1}|^{2})(1-\gamma_{1}\sin^{2}(\phi_{1}+x_{1}\eta)).

Recall that
\gamma_{1}
and
\phi_{1}

do not depend on
Y
.
When
\cos(2\phi_{1}+2x_{1}\eta)\geq 0
,
using the inequality
a^{2}+b^{2}\geq 2ab
, we bound

\displaystyle\leq p_{1}^{2}|Z_{1}|^{2}+p_{-1}^{2}|Z_{-1}|^{2}+p_{1}p_{-1}(|Z_{1}|^{2}+|Z_{-1}|^{2})\cos(2\phi_{1}+2x_{1}\eta)

\displaystyle=p_{1}|Z_{1}|^{2}(p_{1}+p_{-1}\cos(2\phi+2x_{1}\eta))+p_{-1}|Z_{-1}|^{2}(p_{-1}+p_{1}\cos(2\phi_{1}+2x_{1}\eta))

\displaystyle\leq(p_{1}|Z_{1}|^{2}+p_{-1}|Z_{-1}|^{2})(1-\gamma_{1}+\gamma_{1}\cos(2\phi_{1}+2x_{1}\eta))

\displaystyle=\mathop{\mathbb{E}}\displaylimits_{Y_{1}}\left[|Z_{Y_{1}}|^{2}\right](1-2\gamma_{1}\sin^{2}(\phi_{1}+x_{1}\eta)).

When
n=1
, we have proved the base case of the induction.
When
n>1
, apply induction
on
|Z_{\epsilon}|^{2}
.
∎

3.2.
A Few Bad Directions

Lemma
4
suggests
proving that the expression

\sum_{j}\gamma_{j}\sin^{2}(\phi_{j}+x_{j}\eta)

is typically large. Namely, we aim to show that there are usually many coordinates
j
for which
both
\gamma_{j}
and
\sin^{2}(\phi_{j}+x_{j}\eta)
are bounded away from zero.
Our approach is to explicitly encode the cases
where this fails to hold.

Recall that
Y
is uniformly distributed in a set
B
of size
|B|=2^{\beta n}
.
Let
1\geq\lambda>1/n
be a parameter.
Set
0<\kappa<\tfrac{1}{2}
and
1\geq\tau>0
to be parameters satisfying the conditions

(1)

\displaystyle H\left(\tfrac{1}{\log(1/\kappa)}\right)=\tau+H\left(\tau\right)=\lambda,

where
H
is the binary entropy function:

H(\xi)=\xi\log(1/\xi)+(1-\xi)\log(1/(1-\xi)).

The encoding is based on the following two sets:

J(y)=J_{B,\kappa}(y)=\{j\in[n]:\gamma_{j}(y)\geq\kappa\}

and

G(x,y)=G_{B,\kappa,\theta}(x,y)=\Big\{j\in J(y):\sin^{2}(\phi_{j}(x,y)+x_{j}\eta)\geq\tfrac{\sin^{2}(2\eta)}{4}\Big\}.

We start by showing that there
are few
y
’s for which
|J(y)|
is small.

Lemma 5
.

The number of
y\in B
with
|J(y)|\leq n(\beta-3\lambda)
is at most
2^{n(\beta-2\lambda)}
.

Proof.

If
3\lambda>\beta
,
the statement is trivially true. So, in the rest of the proof, assume that
3\lambda\leq\beta
.
Each
y\in B
with
|J(y)|\leq n(\beta-3\lambda)
can be uniquely encoded by the following data:

–

An vector
q\in\{\pm 1\}^{t}
with

t=\lfloor n(\beta-3\lambda)\rfloor
.

–

A subset
S\subseteq[n]
of size
|S|\leq\tfrac{n}{\log(1/\kappa)}
.

Let us describe the encoding.
The vector
q
encodes
the values taken by
y
in the coordinates
J(y)
. We do not encode
J(y)
itself, only the values of
y
in the coordinates corresponding to
J(y)
.
The set
S
includes
j\in[n]
if and only if

\gamma_{j}(y)<\kappa
.
Each string
y\in B
has probability at least
2^{-n}
.
This implies that

\kappa^{|S|}\geq 2^{-n}
.

We can reconstruct
y
from
q
and
S

by iteratively computing
y_{1}
, then
y_{2}
, and so on, until we
get to
y_{n}
. Whether or not

is determined even before we know
y
. If
1\in J(y)
then
q
tells us
what
y_{1}
is.
If
1\not\in J(y)
and
1\in S
then

y_{1}
is the least likely value between
\pm 1
.
If
1\not\in J(y)
and
1\not\in S

then
y_{1}
is the more likely value.
Given the value of
y_{1}
,
we can continue in the same way to compute the rest of
y
.

The number of choices for
q
is at most
2^{n(\beta-3\lambda)}
.
The number of choices for
S
is at most

2^{nH(1/\log(1/\kappa))}=2^{\lambda n}
.
∎

Next, we argue that there
are few
x
’s for which there are many

’s with small

.

Lemma 6
.

The number of
x\in A
for which

\Pr_{Y}[|G(x,Y)|\leq\tau n]\geq 2^{-\lambda n}

is at most
2^{n(1-\beta+6\lambda)}
.

Proof.

The lemma is proved by double-counting
the edges in a bipartite graph.
Let
{{\mathcal{X}}}
be the set we are interested in:

{\mathcal{X}}=\big\{x:\Pr_{Y}[|G(x,Y)|\leq\tau n]\geq 2^{-\lambda n}\big\}.

The left side of the bipartite graph is
{\mathcal{X}}

and the right side is
B
.
Connect
x\in{\mathcal{X}}
to
y\in B
by an edge
if and only if
G(x,y)\leq\tau n
.
Let
E
denote the set of edges in this graph.

First, we bound the number of edges from below.
The number of edges that
touch each
x\in{\mathcal{X}}
is at least

2^{-\lambda n}|B|
.
It follows that

|E|\geq 2^{-\lambda n}\cdot|{\mathcal{X}}|\cdot|B|.

Next, we bound the number of edges from above.
By Lemma
5
,
the number of

y\in B
so that
|J(y)|\leq n(\beta-3\lambda)

is at most
2^{-2\lambda n}|B|
.
We shall prove that the number of edges
that touch each
y
with
|J(y)|>n(\beta-3\lambda)
is at most

2^{n(1-\beta+4\lambda)}
.
It follows that

|E|\leq 2^{-2\lambda n}\cdot|{\mathcal{X}}|\cdot|B|+|B|\cdot 2^{n(1-\beta+4\lambda)}.

We can conclude that

\displaystyle 2^{-\lambda n}\cdot|{\mathcal{X}}|\cdot|B|

\displaystyle\leq 2^{-2\lambda n}\cdot|{\mathcal{X}}|\cdot|B|+|B|\cdot 2^{n(1-\beta+4\lambda)}

\displaystyle\Rightarrow|{\mathcal{X}}|

\displaystyle\ \leq 2^{n(1-\beta+6\lambda)},

since
\lambda n>1
.

It remains to fix
y
so that

|J(y)|>n(\beta-3\lambda)
and bound its degree from above.
This too is achieved by an encoding argument.
Encode each
x
that is connected
to
y
by an edge using the following data:

–

A vector
q\in\{\pm 1\}^{t}

with
t=\lfloor n(1-\beta+3\lambda)\rfloor
.

–

The set
G(x,y)
.

–

A vector
r\in\{\pm 1\}^{s}
with

s=\lfloor\tau n\rfloor
.

Let us describe the encoding.
The vector
q
specifies the values of
x
on coordinates not in
J(y)
.
There are at most
n-n(\beta-3\lambda)=n(1-\beta+3\lambda)
such coordinates.
The size of
G(x,y)
is at most
\tau n
.
The vector
r
specifies the values of
x
in the coordinates of
G(x,y)
, written in descending order.

The decoding of
x
from
q,S
and

is done as follows.
Decode the coordinates of
x
in descending order from
n
to
1
.
If
n\not\in J(y)
then we read the value of
x_{n}
from
q
.
If
n\in J(y)
and
n\in G(x,y)
, we decode
x_{n}
by reading its value from

. If
n\in J(y)
and

n\notin G(x,y)
,
then

\sin^{2}(\phi_{n}(x,y)+x_{n}\eta)\leq\tfrac{\sin^{2}(2\eta)}{4}.

The number
\phi_{n}(x,y)
does not depend on
x
.
The following claim implies that there is at most one value of
x_{n}
that satisfies this property.

Claim 7
.

For all
\varphi\in\mathbb{R}
and
u,v\in{\mathbb{Z}}
,

\displaystyle\max\{|\sin(\varphi+\eta u)|,|\sin(\varphi+\eta v)|\}\geq\tfrac{|\sin(\eta(u-v))|}{2}.

Proof.

Consider the map

\varphi\mapsto g(\varphi)=\max\{|\sin(\varphi+\eta u)|,|\sin(\varphi+\eta v)|\}.

The minimum of this map
is attained when

|\sin(\varphi+\eta u)|=|\sin(\varphi+\eta v)|.

This happens only when

\varphi=-\tfrac{\eta(u+v)}{2}\ \mathop{\mathsf{mod}}\ \tfrac{\pi}{2}{\mathbb{Z}}
.
By symmetry,

\displaystyle g(\varphi)

\displaystyle\geq\min\{g(-\eta(u+v)/2),g(-\eta(u+v)/2+\pi/2)\}

\displaystyle\geq|\sin(\eta(u-v)/2)\cdot\cos(\eta(u-v)/2)|

\displaystyle=\frac{|\sin(\eta(u-v))|}{2}.\qed

The claim implies that we can indeed reconstruct
x_{n}
.
Given
x_{n}
, we can similarly reconstruct

x_{n-1}
, since
\phi_{n-1}
depends only on
y
and
x_{n}
. Continuing in this way, we can reconstruct
x_{n-2},\dotsc,x_{1}
. The total number of choices for

is at most

2^{n(1-\beta+3\lambda)+nH(\tau)+\tau n}=2^{n(1-\beta+4\lambda)}
.∎

Putting It Together

Proof of Theorem
3
.

Set
\lambda=\tfrac{\delta}{6}

\displaystyle\left|\mathop{\mathbb{E}}\displaylimits_{Y}\left[\exp(2\pi i\theta\left\langle x,Y\right\rangle)\right]\right|

4.
General Two-Cubes

Now we move to the setting
where the direction
x
is chosen from
an arbitrary two-cube
A\subset{\mathbb{Z}}^{n}

with differences
d_{1},\ldots,d_{n}
.
The way we measure the structure
of
A
follows ideas of Halász
[
11
]
.
For an integer
\ell>0
, define
r_{\ell}(A)
to be
the number of elements
(\epsilon,j)\in\{\pm 1\}^{2\ell}\times[n]^{2\ell}
that satisfy

\epsilon_{1}\cdot d_{j_{1}}+\dotsb+\epsilon_{2\ell}\cdot d_{j_{2\ell}}=0.

The smaller
r_{\ell}(A)
is, the less structured
A
is.

The theorem below shows that
r_{\ell}(A)
allows us
to control the concentration probability. More concretely, for
C>0
and
\ell>0
, define

R_{C,\ell}(A)=\frac{C^{\ell}r_{\ell}(A)}{n^{2\ell+1/2}}+\exp(-\tfrac{n}{C}).

Define

R_{C}(A)=\inf\{R_{C,\ell}(A):\ell\in\mathbb{N}\}.

This is essentially the bound on the concentration
probability that Halász obtained in
[
11
]

when
Y
is uniform in
\{\pm 1\}^{n}
.
Our upper bounds are slightly weaker. Let

\mu_{C}(A)=\inf\Big\{\mu\in[0,1]:\exists\nu\in(0,1]\ \ \mu^{(1+\nu)^{2}}\geq 3\exp(-\tfrac{\nu n}{C})+\tfrac{R_{C}(A)}{50\sqrt{\nu}}\Big\},

where we adopt the convention that the infimum of the empty set is
1
.
Before stating the theorem, let us go over the three
examples from Theorem
2
:

(1)

For arbitrary
A
,
since
r_{1}(A)\leq O(n^{2})
, we get
1
1

1

Here
and below the big

notation
hides a constant that may depend on
C
.

\mu_{C}(A)\leq O(\tfrac{\sqrt{\ln n}}{\sqrt{n}})

with
\nu=\tfrac{1}{\ln(1/R_{C,1}(A))}
.

(2)

When all the differences are distinct,
since
r_{1}(A)\leq O(n)
, we get

\mu_{C}(A)\leq O(n^{-1.5}\sqrt{\ln n})

with
\nu=\tfrac{1}{\ln(1/R_{C,1}(A))}
.

(3)

When
\{\pm d_{1},\ldots,\pm d_{n}\}
is a Sidon set,
since
r_{2}(A)\leq O(n^{2})
,
we get

\mu_{C}(A)\leq O(n^{-2.5}\sqrt{\ln n})

with
\nu=\tfrac{1}{\ln(1/R_{C,2}(A))}
.

More generally, when
R_{C}(A)
is bound
from below by some
polynomial in
\tfrac{1}{n}

then
\mu_{C}(A)
is at most
O(R_{C}(A)\sqrt{\log(4/R_{C}(A))})
.

Theorem 8
.

For every
\beta>0
and
\delta>0
,
there is

so that the following holds.
Let

.
Let

be uniformly distributed in

Let

be a two-cube.
Then, for all but
2^{n(1-\beta+\delta)}

,

\displaystyle\mathop{\mathbb{E}}\displaylimits_{\theta}\left[\left|\mathop{\mathbb{E}}\displaylimits_{Y}\left[\exp(2\pi i\theta\cdot\left\langle x,Y\right\rangle)\right]\right|\right]\leq\mu_{C}(A).

Before moving on, we discuss a fourth
extreme example.
When
A_{j}=\{2^{j},-2^{j}\}
for each

,
we have

.
In this case, setting

gives exponentially small anti-concentration with

.
This result is trivial, but it illustrates that the mechanism
underlying the proof yields strong bound in

By (
\star
) and the explanation above,

2
.
The rest of this section is devoted to the proof

.
The high-level structure of the proof is similar

However, there are several new technical challenges

The main technical challenge that needs to be overcome has to do with the definition of the set

.
This is problematic for the proof in the generality

So, we need to find a different set of

.
Our solution is based on

Claim 9
.

For every
\kappa>0
,
there is a constant

so that the following holds.
For every random variable

\min\big\{\Pr[W=1],\Pr[W=-1]\big\}\geq\kappa,

4.1.
A Single Direction

The following lemma generalizes Lemma
4
.
Recall the definition of
\gamma_{j}
,
\phi_{j}

and
J(y)
from Sections
3.1

and
3.2
.

Lemma 10
.

For every
\kappa>0
,
there is a constant
c_{0}>0
so that the following holds. For every
0<\nu\leq 1
, every angle
\eta\in\mathbb{R}
, every direction
x\in{\mathbb{Z}}^{n}
, and every random variable
Y
over
\{\pm 1\}^{n}
,

\displaystyle\left|\mathop{\mathbb{E}}\displaylimits_{Y}\left[\exp({i\eta\left\langle x,Y\right\rangle})\right]\right|^{1+\nu}

\displaystyle\leq\mathop{\mathbb{E}}\displaylimits_{Y}\left[\prod_{j\in J}(1-c_{0}\nu\sin^{2}(\phi_{j}+x_{j}\eta)\right].

Proof.

The proof is by induction on
n
. If
1\notin J
, the proof holds by induction. The base case of
n=1
is trivial.
So assume that
1\in J
.
Express

\displaystyle\mathop{\mathbb{E}}\displaylimits_{Y}\left[\exp(i\eta\left\langle x,Y\right\rangle)\right]

\displaystyle=p_{1}\exp(i\eta x_{1})Z_{1}+p_{-1}\exp(-i\eta x_{1})Z_{-1},

where for
\epsilon\in\{\pm 1\}
,

p_{\epsilon}=\Pr[Y_{1}=\epsilon]\qquad\&\qquad Z_{\epsilon}=\mathop{\mathbb{E}}\displaylimits_{Y|Y_{1}=\epsilon}\left[\exp(i\eta\left\langle x_{>1},Y_{>1}\right\rangle)\right].

When
n=1
, we have
Z_{1}=Z_{-1}=1
. Using the definition of
\phi_{1}
,

\displaystyle\left|p_{1}\exp(i\eta x_{1})Z_{1}+p_{-1}\exp(-i\eta x_{1})Z_{-1}\right|^{2}

\displaystyle=p_{1}^{2}|Z_{1}|^{2}+p_{-1}^{2}|Z_{-1}|^{2}+p_{1}p_{-1}(Z_{1}\overline{Z_{-1}}\exp(i2\eta x_{1})+\overline{Z_{1}}Z_{-1}\exp(-i2\eta x_{1}))

\displaystyle=p_{1}^{2}|Z_{1}|^{2}+p_{-1}^{2}|Z_{-1}|^{2}+2p_{1}p_{-1}|Z_{1}||Z_{-1}|\cos(2\phi_{1}+2x_{1}\eta)

\displaystyle=p_{1}^{2}|Z_{1}|^{2}+p_{-1}^{2}|Z_{-1}|^{2}+2p_{1}p_{-1}|Z_{1}||Z_{-1}|

\displaystyle\qquad-2p_{1}p_{-1}|Z_{1}||Z_{-1}|(1-\cos(2\phi_{1}+2x_{1}\eta))

\displaystyle=\mathop{\mathbb{E}}\left[|Z_{Y_{1}}|\right]^{2}-4p_{1}p_{-1}|Z_{1}||Z_{-1}|\sin^{2}(\phi_{1}+x_{1}\eta),

Without loss of generality, assume that
|Z_{1}|\geq|Z_{-1}|
. There are two cases to consider.
The first case is that
Z_{1}
and
Z_{-1}
are comparable in magnitude:
|Z_{1}|\leq 2|Z_{-1}|
. In this case, we can continue the bound by

\displaystyle\leq\mathop{\mathbb{E}}\left[|Z_{Y_{1}}|\right]^{2}-2p_{1}p_{-1}|Z_{1}|^{2}\sin^{2}(\phi_{1}+x_{1}\eta)

\displaystyle\leq\mathop{\mathbb{E}}\left[|Z_{Y_{1}}|\right]^{2}(1-2\kappa(1-\kappa)\sin^{2}(\phi_{1}+x_{1}\eta)),

since
1\in J
. This gives

\displaystyle\left|p_{1}\exp(i\eta x_{1})Z_{1}+p_{-1}\exp(-i\eta x_{1})Z_{-1}\right|^{1+\nu}

\displaystyle\leq\mathop{\mathbb{E}}\left[|Z_{Y_{1}}|\right]^{1+\nu}(1-2\kappa(1-\kappa)\sin^{2}(\phi_{1}+x_{1}\eta))^{(1+\nu)/2}

\displaystyle\leq\mathop{\mathbb{E}}\left[|Z_{Y_{1}}|^{1+\nu}\right](1-\kappa(1-\kappa)\sin^{2}(\phi_{1}+x_{1}\eta)),

since the map
\zeta\mapsto\zeta^{1+\nu}
is convex.

The second case is when
|Z_{1}|>2|Z_{-1}|
.
Recall that we have already shown

\displaystyle\left|p_{1}\exp(i\eta x_{1})Z_{1}+p_{-1}\exp(-i\eta x_{1})Z_{-1}\right|^{2}

\displaystyle=\mathop{\mathbb{E}}\left[|Z_{Y_{1}}|\right]^{2}-4p_{1}p_{-1}|Z_{1}||Z_{-1}|\sin^{2}(\phi_{1}+x_{1}\eta)

\displaystyle\leq\mathop{\mathbb{E}}\left[|Z_{Y_{1}}|\right]^{2}.

Claim
9
implies that

\displaystyle\left|p_{1}\exp(i\eta x_{1})Z_{1}+p_{-1}\exp(-i\eta x_{1})Z_{-1}\right|^{1+\nu}

\displaystyle\leq\mathop{\mathbb{E}}\left[|Z_{Y_{1}}|\right]^{1+\nu}

\displaystyle\leq(1-c_{1}\nu)\cdot\mathop{\mathbb{E}}\left[|Z_{Y_{1}}|^{1+\nu}\right]

\displaystyle\leq(1-c_{1}\nu\sin^{2}(\phi_{j}+x_{j}\eta))\cdot\mathop{\mathbb{E}}\left[|Z_{Y_{1}}|^{1+\nu}\right].

Finally, setting
c_{0}=\min\{c_{1},\kappa(1-\kappa)\}
, we get a bound that applies in both cases:

\displaystyle\left|\mathop{\mathbb{E}}\displaylimits_{Y}\left[\exp({i\eta\left\langle x,Y\right\rangle})\right]\right|^{1+\nu}

\displaystyle\leq(1-c_{0}\nu\sin^{2}(\phi_{j}+x_{j}\eta))\cdot\mathop{\mathbb{E}}\left[|Z_{Y_{1}}|^{1+\nu}\right].

This proves the base case of the induction and
also allows to perform the inductive step.

∎

4.2.
An Average Direction

In this section we analyze

X

Recall that
\gamma_{j}
,

,
on

In the following lemma, we fix an arbitrary

Lemma 11
.

For every
\kappa>0
and

there is a constant

so that the following holds. For every

\displaystyle\mathop{\mathbb{E}}\displaylimits_{X}\left[\prod_{j\in J}(1-c_{0}\nu\sin^{2}(\phi_{j}+X_{j}\eta))\right]^{1+\nu}

\displaystyle\leq\mathop{\mathbb{E}}\displaylimits_{X}\left[\exp\Big(-c\nu\sum_{j\in G}\sin^{2}(d_{j}\eta)\Big)\right].

Proof.

The proof is by induction on

Recall that

\phi_{j}
and

In particular, whether or not

So assume that

Start with

\displaystyle\mathop{\mathbb{E}}\displaylimits_{X}\left[\prod_{j\in J}(1-c_{0}\zeta\sin^{2}(\phi_{j}+X_{j}\eta))\right]

\displaystyle=\mathop{\mathbb{E}}\displaylimits_{X_{n}}\left[(1-c_{0}\zeta\sin^{2}(\phi_{n}+X_{n}\eta))Z_{X_{n}}\right],\

where for
a\in A_{n}:=\{u,v\}
,

Z_{a}=\mathop{\mathbb{E}}\displaylimits_{X|X_{n}=a}\left[\prod_{j\in J:j<n}(1-c_{0}\sin^{2}(\phi_{j}+X_{j}\eta))\right].

If
n=1
, then
Z_{u}=Z_{v}=1
.
Assume without loss of generality
that
Z_{u}\geq Z_{v}
.
There are two cases to consider.
The first case is that
Z_{u}>2Z_{v}
.
In this case, Claim
9
implies

\displaystyle\mathop{\mathbb{E}}\displaylimits_{X}\left[\prod_{j\in J}(1-c_{0}\nu\sin^{2}(\phi_{j}+X_{j}\eta))\right]^{1+\nu}

\displaystyle\leq\mathop{\mathbb{E}}\left[Z_{X_{n}}\right]^{1+\nu}

\displaystyle\leq(1-c_{1}\nu)\mathop{\mathbb{E}}\left[Z_{X_{n}}^{1+\nu}\right]

\displaystyle\leq\exp(-c_{1}\nu)\mathop{\mathbb{E}}\left[Z_{X_{n}}^{1+\nu}\right].

The second case is when
Z_{u}\leq 2Z_{v}
.
By Claim
7
,

\max\big\{|\sin(\phi_{n}+u\eta)|,|\sin(\phi_{n}+v\eta)|\big\}\geq\tfrac{\sin(d_{n}\eta)}{2}.

Since
\mu_{n}(x)\geq\kappa
,

\displaystyle\mathop{\mathbb{E}}\displaylimits_{X_{n}}\left[(1-c_{0}\nu\sin^{2}(\phi_{n}+X_{n}\eta))Z_{X_{n}}\right]^{1+\nu}

\displaystyle\leq(\mathop{\mathbb{E}}\displaylimits_{X_{n}}\left[Z_{X_{n}}\right]-\kappa c_{0}\nu\tfrac{\sin^{2}(d_{n}\eta)}{4}\tfrac{Z_{u}}{2})^{1+\nu}

\displaystyle\leq(\mathop{\mathbb{E}}\displaylimits_{X_{n}}\left[Z_{X_{n}}\right](1-\tfrac{\kappa c_{0}\nu}{8}\sin^{2}(d_{n}\eta)))^{1+\nu}

\displaystyle\leq\mathop{\mathbb{E}}\displaylimits_{X_{n}}\left[Z^{1+\nu}_{X_{n}}\right]\exp(-\tfrac{c_{0}\kappa\nu}{8}\sin^{2}(d_{n}\eta)).

In both cases,

\mathop{\mathbb{E}}\displaylimits_{X}\left[\prod_{j\in J}(1-c_{0}\sin^{2}(\phi_{j}+X_{j}\eta))\right]^{1+\nu}\leq\exp(-c\nu\sin^{2}(d_{n}\eta))\mathop{\mathbb{E}}\displaylimits_{X_{n}}\left[Z_{X_{n}}^{1+\nu}\right],

for some constant
c(\kappa,c_{0})>0
.
This proves the base case of the induction and
also allows to perform the inductive step.

∎

4.3.
Putting It Together

Proof of Theorem
8
.

Let
\mu>0
and
0<\nu\leq 1

be so that

\mu^{(1+\nu)^{2}}\geq 3\exp(-\tfrac{\nu n}{C})+\tfrac{R_{C}(A)}{50\sqrt{\nu}};

if no such
\mu,\nu
exist then the theorem is trivially true.
Let

A_{0}=\Big\{x\in A:\mathop{\mathbb{E}}\displaylimits_{\theta}\left[\Big|\mathop{\mathbb{E}}\displaylimits_{Y}\left[\exp(2\pi i\theta\cdot\left\langle x,Y\right\rangle)\right]\Big|\right]\geq\mu\Big\}.

Denote the size of
A_{0}
by
2^{\alpha n}
.
Assume towards a contradiction that

\alpha+\beta\geq 1+\delta
.
Let
X
be uniformly distributed in
A_{0}
, independently of
Y
and
\theta
.
Let
\lambda=\tfrac{\delta}{7}
,
and let
\kappa
be as in (
1
).
By Lemma
10
,

\displaystyle\mathop{\mathbb{E}}\displaylimits_{X,\theta}\left[\Big|\mathop{\mathbb{E}}\displaylimits_{Y}\left[\exp({i2\pi\theta\left\langle x,Y\right\rangle})\right]\Big|\right]^{(1+\nu)^{2}}

\displaystyle\leq\mathop{\mathbb{E}}\displaylimits_{X,\theta}\left[\Big|\mathop{\mathbb{E}}\displaylimits_{Y}\left[\exp({i2\pi\theta\left\langle x,Y\right\rangle})\right]\Big|^{1+\nu}\right]^{1+\nu}

\displaystyle\leq\mathop{\mathbb{E}}\displaylimits_{X,\theta}\left[\mathop{\mathbb{E}}\displaylimits_{Y}\left[\prod_{j\in J}(1-c_{0}\nu\sin^{2}(\phi_{j}+x_{j}2\pi\theta)\right]\right]^{1+\nu}.

By Lemma
11
, we can continue

\displaystyle=\mathop{\mathbb{E}}\displaylimits_{Y,\theta}\left[\mathop{\mathbb{E}}\displaylimits_{X}\left[\prod_{j\in J}(1-c_{0}\nu\sin^{2}(\phi_{j}+x_{j}2\pi\theta)\right]\right]^{1+\nu}

\displaystyle\leq\mathop{\mathbb{E}}\displaylimits_{Y,\theta}\left[\mathop{\mathbb{E}}\displaylimits_{X}\left[\prod_{j\in J}(1-c_{0}\nu\sin^{2}(\phi_{j}+x_{j}2\pi\theta)\right]^{1+\nu}\right]

\displaystyle\leq\mathop{\mathbb{E}}\displaylimits_{X,Y,\theta}\left[\exp(-c\nu D(\theta))\right],

where

D(\theta)=D_{x,y}(\theta)=\sum_{j\in G(x,y)}\sin^{2}(2\pi\theta d_{j}).

By Lemma
5
,
|J(y)|>n(\beta-3\lambda)
for all but
2^{n(\beta-2\lambda)}
choices for
y
. Similarly,
|J^{\prime}(x)|>n(\alpha-3\lambda)
for all but
2^{n(\alpha-2\lambda)}
choices of
x
.
By assumption,

\beta-3\lambda+\alpha-3\lambda\geq\lambda
.
Since
|G(x,y)|\geq|J(y)|+|J^{\prime}(x)|-n
,

\displaystyle\Pr[|G(X,Y)|\leq\lambda n]

\displaystyle\leq\Pr[|J(Y)|\leq n(\beta-3\lambda)]+\Pr[|J^{\prime}(X)|\leq n(\alpha-3\lambda)]

\displaystyle\leq 2^{-2\lambda n}+2^{-2\lambda n}.

Claim
.

Let
x,y
be so that
G(x,y)\geq\lambda n
.
For every
0\leq\rho\leq\tfrac{\lambda n}{4}

and integer
\ell>0
,

\Pr_{\theta}[D(\theta)<\rho]\leq\frac{4r_{\ell}(A)}{(\lambda n)^{2\ell+1/2}}\sqrt{\rho}.

Given the claim,
for every
x,y
so that
G(x,y)\geq\lambda n

and
\ell>0
,

\displaystyle\mathop{\mathbb{E}}\displaylimits_{\theta}\left[\exp(-c\nu D(\theta))\right]

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{1}\Pr_{\theta}[\exp(-c\nu D(\theta))>t]\,\mathrm{d}t

\displaystyle\leq\exp(-\tfrac{c\nu\lambda n}{4})+\int_{\exp(-c\nu\lambda n/4)}^{1}\Pr_{\theta}[D(\theta)<-\tfrac{\ln t}{c\nu}]\,\mathrm{d}t

\displaystyle\leq\exp(-\tfrac{c\nu\lambda n}{4})+\frac{4r_{\ell}(A)}{(\lambda n)^{2\ell+1/2}}\int_{0}^{1}\sqrt{-\tfrac{\ln t}{c\nu}}\,\mathrm{d}t.

The integral

\int_{0}^{1}\sqrt{-\ln t}\,\mathrm{d}t\leq 1
converges to a constant.
For an appropriate
C=C(\beta,\delta)>0

and
\ell>0
,
we get the desired contradiction.

\displaystyle\mu^{(1+\nu)^{2}}

\displaystyle\leq 2\cdot 2^{-2\lambda n}+\exp(-\tfrac{c\nu\lambda n}{4})+\frac{4r_{\ell}(A)}{\sqrt{c\nu}(\lambda n)^{2\ell+1/2}}

\displaystyle<3\exp(-\tfrac{\nu n}{C})+\tfrac{R_{C}(A)}{50\sqrt{\nu}}.

Proof of Claim.

Let
G=G(x,y)
.
Observe that

\displaystyle\mathop{\mathbb{E}}\displaylimits_{\theta}\left[(|G|-2D(\theta))^{2\ell}\right]

\displaystyle=\mathop{\mathbb{E}}\displaylimits_{\theta}\left[\Big(\sum_{j\in G}\cos(4\pi d_{j}\theta)\Big)^{2\ell}\right]

\displaystyle=2^{-2\ell}\mathop{\mathbb{E}}\displaylimits_{\theta}\left[\Big(\sum_{j\in G}\exp(4\pi id_{j}\theta)+\exp(-4\pi id_{j}\theta)\Big)^{2\ell}\right]

\displaystyle\leq 2^{-2\ell}r_{\ell}(A);

the last equality follows from the fact that of the
\leq(2|G|)^{2\ell}
terms in the expansion, the only ones that survive are the ones with phase
0
. There are at most
r_{\ell}(A)
such terms, and each contributes
1
.

By Markov’s inequality, since
|G|\geq\lambda n
,

\displaystyle\Pr_{\theta}[D(\theta)\leq\tfrac{\lambda n}{4}]

\displaystyle\leq\Pr_{\theta}\Big[(|G|-2D(\theta))^{2\ell}\geq(\tfrac{\lambda n}{2})^{2\ell}\Big]\leq\frac{2^{-2\ell}r_{\ell}(A)}{(\lambda n/2)^{2\ell}}=\frac{r_{\ell}(A)}{(\lambda n)^{2\ell}}.

This proves the claim for
\rho=\tfrac{\lambda n}{4}
.

It remains to prove the claim
for
\rho<\tfrac{\lambda n}{4}
.
This part uses Kemperman’s theorem
[
14
]

from group theory (in fact Kneser’s theorem
[
15
]

for abelian groups suffices).
Think of
[0,1)
as the group
\mathbb{R}/{\mathbb{Z}}
.
Let

S_{\rho}=\{\theta\in\mathbb{R}/{\mathbb{Z}}:D(\theta)\leq\rho\}.

We claim that the
m
-fold sum
S_{\rho}+S_{\rho}+\dotsb+S_{\rho}\subseteq\mathbb{R}/{\mathbb{Z}}

is contained in
S_{\rho m^{2}}
. Indeed,

\displaystyle|\sin(\eta_{1}+\eta_{2})|

\displaystyle=|\sin(\eta_{1})\cos(\eta_{2})+\sin(\eta_{2})\cos(\eta_{1})|

\displaystyle\leq|\sin(\eta_{1})|+|\sin(\eta_{2})|,

and so

\displaystyle\sin^{2}(\eta_{1}+\dotsb+\eta_{m})

\displaystyle\leq(|\sin(\eta_{1})|+\dotsb+|\sin(\eta_{m})|)^{2}

\displaystyle\leq m(\sin^{2}(\eta_{1})+\dotsb+\sin^{2}(\eta_{m})).

It follows that

\displaystyle D(\theta_{1}+\theta_{2}+\cdots+\theta_{m})

\displaystyle\leq m(D(\theta_{1})+D(\theta_{2})+\cdots+D(\theta_{m}))

\displaystyle\leq m^{2}\max\{D(\theta_{1}),D(\theta_{2}),\ldots,D(\theta_{m})\}.

Kemperman’s theorem thus implies that

|S_{\rho m^{2}}|\geq|S_{\rho}+\dotsb+S_{\rho}|\geq m|S_{\rho}|,

as long as
S_{\rho m^{2}}
is not
all of
\mathbb{R}/{\mathbb{Z}}
.
Since

\displaystyle\mathop{\mathbb{E}}\displaylimits_{\theta}\left[D(\theta)\right]=\sum_{j\in G}\mathop{\mathbb{E}}\displaylimits_{\theta}\left[\sin^{2}(2\pi\theta d_{j})\right]=\frac{|G|}{2},

we can deduce that
|S_{\lambda n/4}|=\Pr_{\theta}[D(\theta)\leq\tfrac{\lambda n}{4}]

is strictly less than one.
Hence,
S_{\lambda n/4}
is not the full group
\mathbb{R}/{\mathbb{Z}}
.
Setting
m
to be the largest integer so that
m^{2}\rho\leq\tfrac{\lambda n}{4}
, we can conclude

\displaystyle\Pr_{\theta}[D(\theta)\leq\rho]

\displaystyle\leq\tfrac{1}{m}\Pr_{\theta}[D(\theta)\leq\rho m^{2}]\leq\tfrac{1}{m}\Pr_{\theta}[D(\theta)\leq\tfrac{\lambda n}{4}].\qed

∎

Appendix A
Strict Convexity

Proof of Claim
9
.

If
\alpha_{1}=0
, then the claim is trivially true. So,
assume that
\alpha_{1}>0
. Without loss of generality, we may also assume that
\kappa>0
is small enough so that
4^{\kappa}>\exp(\kappa+\kappa^{2})
.

Let
p=\Pr[W=1]\in[\kappa,1-\kappa]
and
\xi=\tfrac{\alpha_{-1}}{\alpha_{1}}\in[0,\tfrac{1}{2}]
.
So,

\displaystyle\frac{\mathop{\mathbb{E}}\left[\alpha_{W}\right]^{1+\nu}}{\mathop{\mathbb{E}}\left[\alpha_{W}^{1+\nu}\right]}

\displaystyle=\frac{(p+(1-p)\xi)^{1+\nu}}{p+(1-p)\xi^{1+\nu}}.

We need to upper bound this ratio by
1-c_{1}\nu
, for some constant
c_{1}
that depends only on
\kappa
.
Let

\Phi(\xi,p,\nu)=(p+(1-p)\xi^{1+\nu})-(p+(1-p)\xi)^{1+\nu}.

We shall argue that there is a constant
c_{1}=c_{1}(\kappa)>0
such that
\Phi(\xi,p,\nu)\geq c_{1}\nu
. This completes the proof, since

\displaystyle\frac{(p+(1-p)\xi)^{1+\nu}}{(p+(1-p)\xi^{1+\nu})}=1-\frac{\Phi(\xi,p,\nu)}{(p+(1-p)\xi^{1+\nu})}<1-c_{1}\nu.

First, we show that for every
\nu
and
\xi
,
the function
\Phi(\xi,p,\nu)
is minimized when
p=\kappa
. Consider

\displaystyle\frac{\partial\Phi}{\partial p}

\displaystyle=1-\xi^{1+\nu}-(1+\nu)(p+(1-p)\xi)^{\nu}(1-\xi)

\displaystyle\geq 1-\xi^{1+\nu}-(1+\nu)(1-\xi)

\displaystyle\geq\xi(1+\nu-\xi^{\nu})>0,

since
\xi^{\nu}<1
.
So, the minimum is achieved when
p=\kappa
.

Second, we claim that for every
\nu
and
p
,
the function
\Phi(\xi,p,\nu)
is minimized when
\xi=\tfrac{1}{2}
. Consider

\displaystyle\frac{\partial\Phi}{\partial\xi}

\displaystyle=(1-p)(1+\nu)\xi^{\nu}-(1+\nu)(p+(1-p)\xi)^{\nu}(1-p)

\displaystyle=(1-p)(1+\nu)(\xi^{\nu}-(p+(1-p)\xi)^{\nu})<0,

since
p+(1-p)\xi>\xi
. So, the minimum is achieved when
\xi=1/2
.

Third, we control the derivative with respect to
\nu

for
\xi=\tfrac{1}{2}
and
p=\kappa
.
Consider

\displaystyle\frac{\partial\Phi}{\partial\nu}(\tfrac{1}{2},\kappa,\nu)

\displaystyle=(1-\kappa)\ln(\tfrac{1}{2})(\tfrac{1}{2})^{1+\nu}-\ln(\tfrac{1+\kappa}{2})(\tfrac{1+\kappa}{2})^{1+\nu}

\displaystyle\geq(\tfrac{1}{2})^{2}((1-\kappa)\ln(\tfrac{1}{2})-\ln(\tfrac{1+\kappa}{2})(1+\kappa)^{1+\nu}),

since
\nu\leq 1
. The expression

(1-\kappa)\ln(\tfrac{1}{2})-\ln(\tfrac{1+\kappa}{2})(1+\kappa)^{1+\nu}

only increases with
\nu
. When
\nu=0
, this expression is

\displaystyle\ln(\tfrac{2^{2\kappa}}{(1+\kappa)^{1+\kappa}})\geq\ln(\tfrac{4^{\kappa}}{\exp(\kappa(1+\kappa))})>0,

since
4^{\kappa}>\exp(\kappa+\kappa^{2})
. This proves that
\frac{\partial\Phi}{\partial\nu}(\tfrac{1}{2},\kappa,\nu)>c_{1}
for some constant
c_{1}(\kappa)>0
.

Finally,

\Phi(\xi,p,\nu)\geq\Phi(\tfrac{1}{2},\kappa,\nu)=\int_{0}^{\nu}\frac{\partial\Phi}{\partial\nu}(\tfrac{1}{2},\kappa,\zeta)\,\mathrm{d}\zeta\geq\int_{0}^{\nu}c_{1}\,\mathrm{d}\zeta=c_{1}\nu.\qed
