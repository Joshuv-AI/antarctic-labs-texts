A Unified Study on Sequentiality in Universal Classification with Empirically Observed Statistics
Ching-Fang Li, I-Hsiang Wang

Abstract
In the binary hypothesis testing problem, it is well known that sequentiality in taking samples eradicates the trade-off between two error exponents, yet implementing the optimal test requires the knowledge of the underlying distributions, say P 0 P_{0} and P 1 P_{1} . In the scenario where the knowledge of distributions is replaced by empirically observed statistics from the respective distributions, the gain of sequentiality is less understood when subject to universality constraints over all possible P 0 , P 1 P_{0},P_{1} . In this work, the gap is mended by a unified study on sequentiality in the universal binary classification problem, where the universality constraints are set on the expected stopping time as well as the type-I error exponent. The type-I error exponent is required to achieve a pre-set distribution-dependent constraint λ ⁡ ( P 0 , P 1 ) \lambda(P_{0},P_{1}) for all P 0 , P 1 P_{0},P_{1} . Under the proposed framework, different sequential setups are investigated so that fair comparisons can be made with the fixed-length counterpart. By viewing these sequential classification problems as special cases of a general sequential composite hypothesis testing problem, the optimal type-II error exponents are characterized. Specifically, in the general sequential composite hypothesis testing problem subject to universality constraints, upper and lower bounds on the type-II error exponent are proved, and a sufficient condition for which the bounds coincide is given. The results for sequential classification problems are then obtained accordingly. With the characterization of the optimal error exponents, the benefit of sequentiality is shown both analytically and numerically by comparing the sequential and the fixed-length cases in representative examples of type-I exponent constraint λ \lambda .



I
Introduction

It is known that sequentiality in taking samples greatly enhances reliability in statistical inference. Take the binary hypothesis testing problem as an example. The decision maker observes a sequence of samples drawn i.i.d. from one of the two known distributions
P_{0}
or
P_{1}
. It aims to infer from which of the two distributions the sequence is generated. Both type-I and type-II error probabilities vanish exponentially fast as the number of samples
n
tends to infinity. The exponential rates are denoted as the error exponents
e_{0}
and
e_{1}
respectively, between which there exists a fundamental trade-off
[
1
]
. When samples are taken sequentially and the decision maker is free to decide when to stop as long as the expected stopping time is less than a given constraint
n
, Wald’s Sequential Probability Ratio Test (SPRT)
[
2
]
is shown to be optimal
[
3
]
and the error exponents can simultaneously achieve the two extremes, namely, the two KL divergences
\mathrm{D}\mkern-1.5mu\left(P_{1}\middle\|P_{0}\right)
and
\mathrm{D}\mkern-1.5mu\left(P_{0}\middle\|P_{1}\right)
. In words, sequentiality in taking samples eradicates the trade-off between error exponents. The optimal design of the stopping and inference strategies, however, critically relies on the knowledge of the underlying distributions
[
2
,
3
,
4
]
.

In this work, we aim to investigate the benefit of sequentiality when the underlying distributions are unknown to the decision maker.
Instead, the decision maker only has access to empirical statistics of training sequences sampled from these distributions, say,
P_{0}
and
P_{1}
in the binary-hypothesis case.
Since the underlying distributions are unknown, it is natural to ask for a
universal
guarantee on certain performances. One such framework focused on the asymptotic performance as the number of samples in all sequences tend to infinity was proposed and studied by Ziv
[
5
]
, where a universality constraint was set on the type-I error exponent to be no less than a given constant
\lambda_{0}>0
regardless of the underlying distributions. Gutman
[
6
]
later proved the asymptotic optimality of Ziv’s universal test in the sense that it attains the optimal type-II error exponent (as a function of
P_{0},P_{1},\lambda_{0}
).
Further extension was made by Levitan and Merhav
[
7
]
where a competitive criterion was taken, replacing the constant constraint
\lambda_{0}
by a
distribution-dependent
one,
\lambda(P_{0},P_{1})
. From these results in the fixed-length settings, it can be seen that there also exists a trade-off between the two error exponents.
A natural question emerges: in this universal classification problem, can sequentiality improve the trade-off, and even eradicate them
without
knowing the underlying distributions?

Full resolution to the question above is provided in this paper, and as we will see, the answer depends on the distribution-dependent constraint
\lambda(P_{0},P_{1})
and the setup of sequentiality.
Since the decision maker observes three sequences (one testing and two training), there are different ways of considering sequentiality. The main focus is on the following setups: (1) the
\mathsf{Fully\text{-}Sequential}
setup where testing and training samples all arrive sequentially, (2) the
\mathsf{Semi\text{-}Sequential\text{-}1}
setup where the training sequences have fixed lengths and testing samples arrive sequentially, and (3) the
\mathsf{Semi\text{-}Sequential\text{-}2}
setup where the testing sequence has a fixed length and training samples arrive sequentially.
To have a fair comparison with the
\mathsf{Fixed\text{-}Length}
setup in previous works, universality constraints are set on the expected stopping time and the type-I error exponent.

Our main contribution is the characterization of the optimal type-II error exponent when the type-I error exponent universality constraint
\lambda(P_{0},P_{1})
satisfies mild continuity conditions.
For the
\mathsf{Fully\text{-}Sequential}
setup, the optimal type-II error exponent is
e_{1,\mathsf{seq}}^{*}(P_{0},P_{1})=\min\big\{\mathrm{D}_{\frac{\alpha}{1+\alpha}}\mkern-1.5mu\left(P_{0}\middle\|P_{1}\right),\kappa(P_{0},P_{1})\big\}
. Here the first term
\mathrm{D}_{\frac{\alpha}{1+\alpha}}\mkern-1.5mu\left(P_{0}\middle\|P_{1}\right)
is the Rényi divergence and
\alpha
is the expected ratio of the number of the
P_{0}
-training samples to that of testing samples. It corresponds to the error event that the test stops at around time
n
with testing and
P_{0}
-training samples looking like they are generated by the same distribution.
Likewise, the second term
\kappa(P_{0},P_{1})
corresponds to the error event that the test stops at around time
n
while the testing samples look like
P_{1}
-training samples instead but the empirical distributions fall in the optimal
\mathsf{Fixed\text{-}Length}
decision region of the null hypothesis.
For the
\mathsf{Semi\text{-}Sequential\text{-}1}
setup, there is an additional term as the performance is further restricted by the fixed-length training sequences. Specifically, the optimal type-II error exponent is
e_{1,\mathsf{semi1}}^{*}(P_{0},P_{1})=\min\big\{e_{1,\mathsf{seq}}^{*}(P_{0},P_{1}),\mu(P_{0},P_{1})\big\}
.
The additional term
\mu(P_{0},P_{1})
corresponds to an error event in the regime where the number of testing samples is much larger than the fixed lengths of training sequences and is hence the optimal type-II error exponent in an induced fixed-length composite hypothesis testing problem
[
7
]
when the distribution of the testing samples is fixed as
P_{1}
and known.
Similarly, for the
\mathsf{Semi\text{-}Sequential\text{-}2}
setup, the additional restriction is from the fixed-length testing sequence, and
e_{1,\mathsf{semi2}}^{*}(P_{0},P_{1})=\min\big\{e_{1,\mathsf{seq}}^{*}(P_{0},P_{1}),\nu(P_{0},P_{1})\big\}
.
Here
\nu(P_{0},P_{1})
instead corresponds to an error event in the regime where the numbers of training samples are both much larger than the fixed length of the testing sequence so that effectively the distributions of the two hypotheses are both known. Hence, it is the optimal type-II error exponent for the binary hypothesis problem when the type-I error exponent is
\lambda(P_{0},P_{1})
.
For the
\mathsf{Fixed\text{-}Length}
setup, the optimal type-II error exponent is given in
[
7
]
and denoted as
e_{1,\mathsf{fix}}^{*}(P_{0},P_{1})
. To illustrate the benefit of sequentiality and the optimal type-II error exponents in different setups, a numerical example is given in Figure
1
, where
\mathsf{Fully\text{-}Sequential}
strictly outperforms
\mathsf{Semi\text{-}Sequential\text{-}1}
/
\mathsf{Semi\text{-}Sequential\text{-}2}
, and
\mathsf{Semi\text{-}Sequential\text{-}1}
/
\mathsf{Semi\text{-}Sequential\text{-}2}
strictly outperforms
\mathsf{Fixed\text{-}Length}
. Meanwhile,
\mathsf{Semi\text{-}Sequential\text{-}1}
is sometimes better than
\mathsf{Semi\text{-}Sequential\text{-}2}
and sometimes worse.

, where
\beta

,

,

, and

.

to

Analytical comparisons are also made besides numerical evaluation.
In particular, for the choice of
\lambda(P_{0},P_{1})
being a constant
\lambda_{0}>0
, we show that
\mathsf{Fixed\text{-}Length}
tests have strictly smaller type-II error exponent than
\mathsf{Fully\text{-}Sequential}
ones. Moreover,
\mathsf{Semi\text{-}Sequential\text{-}1}
and
\mathsf{Fully\text{-}Sequential}
tests have the same optimal type-II error exponents, indicating that there is no additional gain due to sequentiality in taking training samples when testing samples are sequentially observed.
On the other hand, when the choice of
\lambda(P_{0},P_{1})
permits exponentially vanishing error probabilities for all
P_{0},P_{1}
, it is shown that the trade-off between error exponents is eradicated in the
\mathsf{Fully\text{-}Sequential}
case, demonstrating strict error exponent gain over
\mathsf{Fixed\text{-}Length}
tests. Regarding the additional benefit of sequentiality in taking training samples when testing samples are already sequential, we characterize the necessary and sufficient condition of whether or not there exists a strict gap between the optimal exponents of
\mathsf{Semi\text{-}Sequential\text{-}1}
and
\mathsf{Fully\text{-}Sequential}
tests. The condition pertains to
\alpha
and
\beta
, the expected ratios of the number of the
P_{0}/P_{1}
-training samples to that of the testing samples, respectively.

A unified proof of the optimal type-II error exponents in the classification problem is developed by investigating a more general composite hypothesis testing problem with
s

independent sequences, each of which is either fixed-length or sequentially observed. In this problem, there are two disjoint composite hypothesis sets,
\mathscr{P}_{0}
and
\mathscr{P}_{1}
,
collecting the possible underlying distributions of the sequences under two hypotheses.
When all sequences are fixed-length, the problem was investigated in
[
7
]
, and hence the proposed problem can be viewed as a generalization of that in
[
7
]
with sequential components. Similar to the setup in the classification problem, the universality constraints are set on the expected stopping time and the type-I error exponent. For this general composite hypothesis testing problem with sequential components, we establish upper and lower bounds on the type-II error exponents, as well as a sufficient condition
such that the upper and lower bounds coincide.
The optimal type-II error exponents of the classification problem can be obtained by identifying the different sequential setups as special cases of the composite hypothesis testing problem.
We show that the aforementioned sufficient condition holds in these cases, and characterize the the optimal type-II error exponents in the classification problems.

For the sequential version of binary classification problem,
Haghifam
et al.

[
8

considered the

\mathsf{Fixed\text{-}Length}

[

]

[
8

Then it cannot be said which test is better. Even if a test may be instance optimal, there might not exist a universally optimal test. As a result, it would be difficult or even impossible to derive a tight converse bound matching the achievability results. Such difficulties are experienced in both
[
8
]
and
[
9
]
. In comparison, by setting universality constraints, we can focus on tests that are (at least to certain extent) comparable to each other. For such tests, optimality can be clearly defined and there even exist tests that are universally optimal.

Partial results of this work also appear in

II
Problem Formulation

II-A

Binary Classification

Let
\mathcal{X}
be a finite alphabet with
|\mathcal{X}|=d\geq 2
and
consider the set
\mathcal{P}_{\varepsilon}=\left\{P\in\mathcal{P}(\mathcal{X})\,\middle|\,\forall\,x\in\mathcal{X},\ P(x)\geq\varepsilon\right\}
for some
\varepsilon>0
.
Note that
\mathcal{P}_{\varepsilon}
is compact and chosen to ensure that the KL divergences between these distributions are bounded and uniformly continuous. The underlying distributions are described by a pair of distinct distributions
(P_{0},P_{1})\in\mathcal{D}_{\varepsilon}=\left\{(P,Q)\,\middle|\,P,Q\in\mathcal{P}_{\varepsilon},\ P\neq Q\right\}
,
and
(P_{0},P_{1})
is
unknown
to the decision maker.
Under ground truth
\theta\in\{0,1\}
, the decision maker observes i.i.d. testing samples
X_{k}
’s following
P_{\theta}
, along with i.i.d. training samples
T_{0,k}
’s and
T_{1,k}
’s following
P_{0}
and
P_{1}
respectively to learn about the unknown underlying distributions. Note that the testing and training samples are mutually independent.
Let
n\in\mathbb{N}
be an index of the problem, which will later be related to the number of testing samples in expectation.
Let
\alpha,\beta>0
be two fixed problem parameters indicating the asymptotic ratios of the number of training samples to
n
. The objective of the decision maker is to output
\hat{\theta}\in\{0,1\}
as an estimation of the unknown ground truth
\theta
, based on the observed samples.

Next let us specify different setups regarding the sequentiality of taking samples.

•

\mathsf{Fully\text{-}Sequential}
: the testing and training samples are all sequentially observed. At time
k
, there are
k
testing samples and
N_{k}=\left\lceil\alpha k\right\rceil,M_{k}=\left\lceil\beta k\right\rceil
training samples from each distribution.
A test is a pair
\Phi_{n}=(\tau_{n},\delta_{n})
where
\tau_{n}\in\mathbb{N}
is a Markov stopping time with respect to the filtration
\mathcal{F}_{k}=\sigma(X^{k},T_{0}^{N_{k}},T_{1}^{M_{k}})
. Recall that
T_{0}^{N_{k}}
denote the first
N_{k}
training samples
T_{0,i}
’s. We may write
\tau_{n}
as
\tau
when it is clear from the context. The decision rule
\delta_{n}:\mathcal{X}^{\tau}\times\mathcal{X}^{N_{\tau}}\times\mathcal{X}^{M_{\tau}}\to\{0,1\}
is a
\mathcal{F}_{\tau}
-measurable function, and the output is denoted as
\hat{\theta}
.

•

\mathsf{Semi\text{-}Sequential\text{-}1}
: the testing samples
X_{k}
’s arrive sequentially, while the numbers of
P_{0}
/
P_{1}
-training samples are fixed to
N=\left\lceil\alpha n\right\rceil
and
M=\left\lceil\beta n\right\rceil
. Denote the two training sequences as
T_{0}^{N}
and
T_{1}^{M}
.
A test
\Phi_{n}=(\tau_{n},\delta_{n})
is similarly defined as in the
\mathsf{Fully\text{-}Sequential}
setup, with time-dependent variables
N_{k},M_{k}
replaced by the constants
N,M
.

•

\mathsf{Semi\text{-}Sequential\text{-}2}
: the number of the testing samples is fixed to
n
and the training samples are sequentially observed. That is, at time
k
, there are
N_{k}=\left\lceil\alpha k\right\rceil
and
M_{k}=\left\lceil\beta k\right\rceil
training samples from each distribution. A test
\Phi_{n}=(\tau_{n},\delta_{n})
is similarly defined as in the
\mathsf{Fully\text{-}Sequential}
setup, with the number of testing samples
k
replaced by the constant
n
.

•

\mathsf{Fixed\text{-}Length}
: all three sequences have fixed-length. Note that it can be viewed as restricting
\tau_{n}=n
in either one of the above sequential setups.

When the collection of training samples are done way earlier than the collection of testing samples, it is reasonable to consider the
\mathsf{Semi\text{-}Sequential\text{-}1}
setup, as it may be infeasible to collect the training samples once the collection of testing samples starts. On the other hand, if the collection of training samples can be done on-the-fly or after the collection of testing samples, it may be reasonable to consider the
\mathsf{Semi\text{-}Sequential\text{-}2}
and
\mathsf{Fully\text{-}Sequential}
setups.
For example, a real-world application is mentioned in
[
13
]
about determining whether the Higgs boson exists. In this case, the actual data from experiment is expensive to obtain. However, one can generate simulated data under different hypotheses. Since simulation has lower cost and easy to resume, both the
\mathsf{Semi\text{-}Sequential\text{-}2}
and
\mathsf{Fully\text{-}Sequential}
setups apply.

In the following, when
X^{k}
is observed, denote the empirical distribution as
\hat{P}^{k}
, where
\hat{P}^{k}(x)=\frac{1}{k}\sum_{i=1}^{k}\mathbbm{1}{\left\{X_{i}=x\right\}}
for
x\in\mathcal{X}
.
The empirical distributions of
T_{0}^{N}
and
T_{1}^{M}
are denoted as
\hat{P}_{0}^{N}
and
\hat{P}_{1}^{M}
(we omit
N,M
if it is clear from the context).

The performance of tests is measured by the error probability and the number of samples used. Given
(P_{0},P_{1})\in\mathcal{D}_{\varepsilon}
and
\theta\in\{0,1\}
, the error probability is defined as
\pi_{\theta}(\Phi_{n}|P_{0},P_{1})=\mathbb{P}_{\theta}\{\hat{\theta}\neq\theta\}
, where
\mathbb{P}_{\theta}
is the shorthand notation for the joint probability law of the testing sequence and training sequences. The average number of samples used can be described by the expected stopping time
\mathbb{E}_{\theta}[\tau_{n}|P_{0},P_{1}]
, where the expectation is taken under
\mathbb{P}_{\theta}
.
Since the underlying distributions are unknown, it is natural to ask for some universal guarantees on the performance. The universality constraints are twofold.

•

First, to compare with fixed-length tests, we set a
universality constraint on the expected stopping time
to be at most
n
.
Let
\{\Phi_{n}\}
be a sequence of tests where
\Phi_{n}
satisfies
\mathbb{E}_{\theta}[\tau_{n}|P_{0},P_{1}]\leq n
for all underlying distributions
(P_{0},P_{1})\in\mathcal{D}_{\varepsilon}
and ground truth
\theta
. The type-I and type-II error exponents of
\{\Phi_{n}\}
given
(P_{0},P_{1})\in\mathcal{D}_{\varepsilon}
are defined as

e_{\theta}(P_{0},P_{1})=\liminf\limits_{n\to\infty}\frac{-\log{\pi_{\theta}(\Phi_{n}|P_{0},P_{1})}}{n},\ \text{for }\theta=0,1.

(1)

•

Second, we adopt the competitive Neyman-Pearson criterion proposed in
[
7
]
and set a
universality constraint on the type-I error exponent
.
Let
\lambda:\mathcal{D}_{\varepsilon}\to(0,\infty)
be a pre-set distribution-dependent constraint function. We focus on tests satisfying

e_{0}(P_{0},P_{1})\geq\lambda(P_{0},P_{1}),\quad\forall\,(P_{0},P_{1})\in\mathcal{D}_{\varepsilon}.

(2)

The goal is to characterize the maximum
e_{1}(P_{0},P_{1})
that can be achieved for tests satisfying these universality constraints and to find such a test, if possible, that achieves the maximum
uniformly
over all possible underlying distributions.

We close this sub-section with the following assumption on
\lambda
that will be used in proving the achievability in the
\mathsf{Semi\text{-}Sequential\text{-}1}
and
\mathsf{Semi\text{-}Sequential\text{-}2}
setup.

Assumption 1
.

The function
\lambda:\mathcal{D}_{\varepsilon}\to(0,\infty)
can be extended to a continuous function
\bar{\lambda}:\mathcal{P}_{\varepsilon}\times\mathcal{P}_{\varepsilon}\to[0,\infty)
.

Remark 1
.

The competitive Neyman-Pearson criterion can be viewed as a generalization of the generalized Neyman-Pearson criterion which requires the type-I error exponent to be lower bounded by a constant regardless of the underlying distributions. Since
P_{0}
and
P_{1}
can be arbitrarily close to each other, in some cases it might be too stringent to ask for a constant type-I error exponent as that will inevitably make the type-II error tend to
1
. In
[
7
]
, the competitive Neyman-Pearson criterion is proposed to allow more flexibility and make it possible that both error probabilities decay to zero exponentially for all the underlying distributions. Two examples of the threshold functions are
\lambda\equiv\lambda_{0}
and
\lambda(P_{0},P_{1})=\xi\mathrm{D}_{\frac{\beta}{1+\beta}}\mkern-1.5mu\left(P_{1}\middle\|P_{0}\right)
for some
\xi\in(0,1)
, corresponding to the two scenarios mentioned above.
Further discussions can be found in Section
IV-C
. It is worth noting that since the distributions are unknown, one should define
\lambda(P_{0},P_{1})
for all
(P_{0},P_{1})\in\mathcal{D}_{\varepsilon}
, specifying the performance requirement for every possibly encountered case.

II-B

Composite Hypothesis Testing

In
[
7
]
, a more general composite hypothesis testing problem was considered, and the binary classification problem in the
\mathsf{Fixed\text{-}Length}
setup can be viewed as a special case.
To provide a unified proof of our main results, the optimal error exponents of binary classification problem, we propose a more general version of the problem in
[
7
]
so that the setups with sequentially arrived samples such as
\mathsf{Semi\text{-}Sequential\text{-}1}
,
\mathsf{Semi\text{-}Sequential\text{-}2}
, and
\mathsf{Fully\text{-}Sequential}
can be incorporated.

:
The setup of the generalized problem.

Let us first formalize the general setup.
For convenience, in the context of the generalized problem, some notations are reused and overridden to avoid introducing too many new ones. The definitions of the reused notations should be clear in the respective contexts.
Let
s\in\mathbb{N}
be the number of sequences. For each
i=1,2,\dots,s
,
\mathcal{X}_{i}
is a finite alphabet and
\alpha_{i}>0
is a constant. Let
\mathscr{D}:=\mathcal{P}(\mathcal{X}_{1})\times\mathcal{P}(\mathcal{X}_{2})\times\dots\times\mathcal{P}(\mathcal{X}_{s})
and disjoint sets
\mathscr{P}_{0},\mathscr{P}_{1}\subseteq\mathrm{int}(\mathscr{D})
be the collections of possible underlying distributions when the ground truth is
0
and
1
, respectively.
Fix some integer
0\leq\ell\leq s
, indicating the number of fixed-length sequences.

Given an index
n\in\mathbb{N}
, the decision maker observes
\ell
fixed-length sequences
X_{1}^{N_{1}},X_{2}^{N_{2}},\dots,X_{\ell}^{N_{\ell}}
, where
N_{i}=\left\lceil\alpha_{i}n\right\rceil
is the length of the
i
-th sequence
X_{i}^{N_{i}}=(X_{i,1},X_{i,2},\dots,X_{i,N_{i}})\in\mathcal{X}_{i}^{N_{i}}
for
i=1,2,\dots,\ell
. In addition, some samples are sequentially observed. Specifically, for
i=\ell+1,\dots,s
, at time
k
, there are
\left\lceil\alpha_{i}k\right\rceil
samples in the
i
-th sequence.
Given the unknown ground truth
\theta\in\{0,1\}
, each sample
X_{i,k}
of the
i
-th sequence i.i.d. follow
P_{\theta,i}
, which is the
i
-th component of the underlying distribution
\bm{P}_{\theta}=(P_{\theta,1},P_{\theta,2},\dots,P_{\theta,s})\in\mathscr{P}_{\theta}
.
Note that all the sequences are assumed to be mutually independent.
The objective of the decision maker is to output
\hat{\theta}\in\{0,1\}
as an estimation of the ground truth
\theta
, based on the observed samples.
A test
\Phi_{n}=(\tau_{n},\delta_{n})
consists of a Markov stopping time with respect to the filtration
\mathcal{F}_{k}=\sigma(X_{1}^{N_{1}},\dots,X_{\ell}^{N_{\ell}},X_{\ell+1}^{\left\lceil\alpha_{\ell+1}k\right\rceil},\dots,X_{s}^{\left\lceil\alpha_{s}k\right\rceil})
and a decision rule that maps observations to
\hat{\theta}
. Given ground truth
\theta
and underlying distributions
\bm{P}_{\theta}
, the error probability
\pi_{\theta}(\Phi_{n}|\bm{P}_{\theta})=\mathbb{P}_{\theta}\{\hat{\theta}\neq\theta\}
, where
\mathbb{P}_{\theta}
is the shorthand notation for the joint probability law of all the sequences. The expected stopping time is
\mathbb{E}_{\theta}[\tau|\bm{P}_{\theta}]
, where the expectation is taken under
\mathbb{P}_{\theta}
.

Again, we set a constraint on the expected stopping time to be at most
n
, that is,
\mathbb{E}_{\theta}[\tau|\bm{P}_{\theta}]\leq n
for all
\theta=0,1
and
\bm{P}_{\theta}\in\mathcal{P}_{\theta}
.
The error exponent is defined as

e_{\theta}(\bm{P}_{\theta})=\liminf_{n\to\infty}\frac{-\log\pi_{\theta}(\Phi_{n}|\bm{P}_{\theta})}{n}.

(3)

We also consider the competitive Neyman-Pearson criterion, where a constraint is set on the type-I error exponent. Let
\lambda:\mathscr{P}_{0}\to(0,\infty)
, and we focus on tests satisfying
e_{0}(\bm{P}_{0})\geq\lambda(\bm{P}_{0})
for all
\bm{P}_{0}\in\mathscr{P}_{0}
.

It is not hard to see that the different sequential binary classification problems are instances of this general composite hypothesis testing problem. Detailed specifications can be found in
Sections

IV-A
and
IV-B
.

III
Results for Composite Hypothesis Testing

In this section, we present converse (
Proposition

1
) and achievability (
Proposition

2
) bounds on the type-II error exponent in the general composite hypothesis testing problem. Then we provide sufficient conditions for which the two bounds coincide.

Let us first take a look at the special case
\ell=s
, which is essentially the fixed-length setup in
[
7
]
. The slight difference is that there is only one sequence in the original setup
[
7
]
.
It is not difficult to modify the arguments in
[
7
]
to show that the optimal type-II error exponent is

e^{*}_{1,\mathsf{fix}}(\bm{P}_{1})=\inf_{\begin{subarray}{c}\bm{Q}\in\mathscr{D}\\
g_{1}(\bm{Q})<0\end{subarray}}\sum_{i=1}^{s}\alpha_{i}\mathrm{D}\mkern-1.5mu\left(Q_{i}\middle\|P_{1,i}\right),

(4)

where
\bm{Q}
denotes the tuple
(Q_{1},Q_{2},\dots,Q_{s})
, and

g_{1}(\bm{Q})=\inf_{\bm{P}_{0}^{\prime}\in\mathscr{P}_{0}}\Big(\sum_{i=1}^{s}\alpha_{i}\mathrm{D}\mkern-1.5mu\left(Q_{i}\middle\|P_{0,i}^{\prime}\right)-\lambda(\bm{P}_{0}^{\prime})\Big).

(5)

Also, there exist tests satisfying the constraint on the type-I error exponent and achieve
e^{*}_{1,\mathsf{fix}}(\bm{P}_{1})
simultaneously for all
\bm{P}_{1}\in\mathscr{P}_{1}
.
Notice that
\sum_{i=1}^{s}\alpha_{i}\mathrm{D}\mkern-1.5mu\left(Q_{i}\middle\|P_{1,i}\right)
in (
4
) is the divergence of
\bm{Q}
from the real distribution
\bm{P}_{1}
, and
g_{1}(\bm{Q})<0
means
\bm{Q}
is “close” to some distribution
\bm{P}_{0}^{\prime}\in\mathscr{P}_{0}
. Here “closeness” is measured by the KL divergence, and the function
\lambda
is used as the threshold. We say
\bm{Q}
is “
\lambda
-close” to some
\bm{P}_{0}^{\prime}\in\mathscr{P}_{0}
, and such
\bm{Q}
constitutes the decision region for hypothesis
0
.

In the following treatment, we shall focus on the case
\ell<s
.
The results are summarized in the following propositions.

Proposition 1

(Converse)
.

Let
\lambda:\mathscr{P}_{0}\to(0,\infty)
, and
\{\Phi_{n}\}
be a sequence of tests such that

•

for each
\Phi_{n}=(\tau_{n},\delta_{n})
, the expected stopping time

\mathbb{E}_{\theta}[\tau_{n}|\bm{P}_{\theta}]\leq n
for all
\theta\in\{0,1\}
and
\bm{P}_{\theta}\in\mathscr{P}_{\theta}
,

•

e_{0}(\bm{P}_{0})\geq\lambda(\bm{P}_{0})
for all
\bm{P}_{0}\in\mathscr{P}_{0}
.

Then for any
\bm{P}_{1}\in\mathscr{P}_{1}
,

e_{1}(\bm{P}_{1})\leq e_{1}^{*}(\bm{P}_{1}):=\inf_{\bm{Q}\in\mathscr{P}_{0}\cup\Gamma_{0}\cup\Omega(\bm{P}_{1})}\sum_{i=1}^{s}\alpha_{i}\mathrm{D}\mkern-1.5mu\left(Q_{i}\middle\|P_{1,i}\right),

(6)

where
\Gamma_{0}=\left\{\bm{Q}\in\mathscr{P}_{1}\,\middle|\,g_{1}(\bm{Q})<0\right\}
and

\displaystyle\Omega(\bm{P}_{1})

\displaystyle=\left\{\bm{Q}\in\mathcal{S}(\bm{P}_{1})\,\middle|\,g^{\bm{P}_{1}}(\bm{Q})<0\right\},

(7)

\displaystyle g^{\bm{P}_{1}}(\bm{Q})

\displaystyle=\inf_{\bm{P}_{0}^{\prime}\in\mathscr{P}_{0}\cap\mathcal{S}(\bm{P}_{1})}\Big(\sum_{i=1}^{\ell}\alpha_{i}\mathrm{D}\mkern-1.5mu\left(Q_{i}\middle\|P_{0,i}^{\prime}\right)-\lambda(\bm{P}_{0}^{\prime})\Big),

(8)

\displaystyle\mathcal{S}(\bm{P}_{1})

\displaystyle=\left\{\bm{Q}\in\mathscr{D}\,\middle|\,Q_{i}=P_{1,i}\ \forall\,i=\ell+1,\dots,s\right\}.

(9)

The type-II error exponent (
6
) is the KL divergence of some distribution
\bm{Q}\in\mathscr{P}_{0}\cup\Gamma_{0}\cup\Omega(\bm{P}_{1})
from the real underlying distribution
\bm{P}_{1}
. The first set
\mathscr{P}_{0}
collects the distributions under hypothesis 0.
The second set
\Gamma_{0}
consists of distributions in
\mathscr{P}_{1}
that are “
\lambda
-close” to some
\bm{P}_{0}^{\prime}\in\mathscr{P}_{0}
.
The idea behind these two sets is that if the empirical distributions of the observed sequences are close to the real underlying distributions, then in order to satisfy the universality constraint on the expected stopping time, the test should not stop too late as this event happens with high probability. However, since the real underlying distributions are unknown, the test should stop early as long as the empirical distributions are close to any possible distributions in
\mathscr{P}_{0}
or
\mathscr{P}_{1}
.
Moreover, if the test stops at some time around time
n
, it should follow the optimal decision rule in the fixed-length setup
[
7
]
in order to satisfy the universality constraint on the type-I error exponent. That is, output
0
if the empirical distributions are “
\lambda
-close” to some
\bm{P}_{0}^{\prime}\in\mathscr{P}_{0}
. The main technique to make these arguments rigorous is the method of types.

The third set
\Omega(\bm{P}_{1})
represents the restriction caused by the first
\ell
fixed-length sequences, and is obtained by a reduction from a new fixed-length composite hypothesis testing problem where the distributions behind sequentially observed samples are perfectly learned.
Apparently the decision maker cannot do better than when it exactly knows those distributions.
To understand the notation, notice that the set
\mathcal{S}(\bm{P}_{1})
is a slice where the last
s-\ell
coordinates are restricted to be the same as
\bm{P}_{1}
. This corresponds to the situation that the distributions of the last
s-\ell
sequences are known (perfectly learned from the sequentially observed samples). The set
\Omega(\bm{P}_{1})
consists of distributions that are too close to some distribution
\bm{P}_{0}^{\prime}\in\mathscr{P}_{0}
, with everything restricted to the slice
\mathcal{S}(\bm{P}_{1})
.
The complete proof of
Proposition

1
is provided in
Section

V
.

Proposition 2

(Achievability)
.

Suppose the following two conditions hold:

1.

\mathscr{P}_{0}
is bounded away from the boundary of
\mathscr{D}
.

2.

\lambda:\mathscr{P}_{0}\to(0,\infty)
can be extended to a continuous function
\bar{\lambda}:\mathrm{cl}(\mathscr{P}_{0})\to[0,\infty)
.

Then there exists a sequence of tests
\{\Phi_{n}\}
satisfying the universality constraints

•

for each
\Phi_{n}=(\tau_{n},\delta_{n})
, the expected stopping time

\mathbb{E}_{\theta}[\tau_{n}|\bm{P}_{\theta}]\leq n
for all
\theta\in\{0,1\}
and
\bm{P}_{\theta}\in\mathscr{P}_{\theta}
,

•

e_{0}(\bm{P}_{0})\geq\lambda(\bm{P}_{0})
for all
\bm{P}_{0}\in\mathscr{P}_{0}
,

and achieves the the following type-II error exponent simultaneously for all
\bm{P}_{1}\in\mathscr{P}_{1}
:

e_{1}(\bm{P}_{1})\geq\inf_{\bm{Q}\in\mathscr{P}_{0}\cup\Gamma_{0}\cup\bar{\Omega}(\bm{P}_{1})}\sum_{i=1}^{s}\alpha_{i}\mathrm{D}\mkern-1.5mu\left(Q_{i}\middle\|P_{1,i}\right),

(10)

where

\displaystyle\bar{\Omega}(\bm{P}_{1})

\displaystyle=\left\{\bm{Q}\in\mathcal{S}(\bm{P}_{1})\,\middle|\,\bar{g}^{\bm{P}_{1}}(\bm{Q})\leq 0\right\},

(11)

\displaystyle\bar{g}^{\bm{P}_{1}}(\bm{Q})

\displaystyle=\inf_{\bm{P}_{0}^{\prime}\in\mathrm{cl}(\mathscr{P}_{0})\cap\mathcal{S}(\bm{P}_{1})}\Big(\sum_{i=1}^{\ell}\alpha_{i}\mathrm{D}\mkern-1.5mu\left(Q_{i}\middle\|P_{0,i}^{\prime}\right)-\bar{\lambda}(\bm{P}_{0}^{\prime})\Big).

(12)

To prove
Proposition

2
, we propose a two-phase test that satisfies the universality constraints.
Following the same idea as in the converse part, to ensure the expected stopping time is bounded by
n
, the test stops at time
n-1
if the empirical distributions of the observed sequences are close to any distributions in
\mathscr{P}_{0}
or
\mathscr{P}_{1}
.
The decision rule follows the optimal fixed-length test in
[
7
]
to meet the type-I error exponent constraint. The contribution to the type-II error exponent in this part unsurprisingly corresponds to
\mathscr{P}_{0}
and
\Gamma_{0}
.

On the other hand, if the empirical distributions are not close to any possible underlying distributions, it means that the samples are inadequate to make a good estimation.
Again, motivated by the idea in the converse part, we aim to approach the case when the distributions behind sequentially observed samples are known. Intuitively, if we observe infinitely many samples, then the distributions behind those sequences can be known by the law of large numbers.
Thus in this case the test continues to take more samples until time
n^{2}
, which can be viewed as mimicking having infinitely many samples.
The decision rule again leverages the idea of fixed-length test in
[
7
]
. In this part, the contribution to the type-II error exponent corresponds to
\bar{\Omega}(\bm{P}_{1})
.

Observe that
\bar{\Omega}(\bm{P}_{1})
is the only part that (
10
) in
Proposition

2
differs from (
6
) in
Proposition

1
.
In particular,
<
in (
7
) is replaced by
\leq
in (
11
), and
g^{\bm{P}_{1}}(\bm{Q})
in (
8
) is replaced by
\bar{g}^{\bm{P}_{1}}(\bm{Q})
in (
12
), where the infimum is taken over
\mathscr{P}_{0}\cap\mathcal{S}(\bm{P}_{1})
and
\mathrm{cl}(\mathscr{P}_{0})\cap\mathcal{S}(\bm{P}_{1})
respectively. Also note that in (
12
),
\lambda
is replaced by its extension
\bar{\lambda}
so that the definition is valid.
The reason behind this gap is that in the proof of
Proposition

1
,
we assume the distributions of the last
s-\ell
sequences are known, which is possible only if infinitely many samples are observed.
However, in the proof of
Proposition

2
, the constructed test only observes finitely many samples (in the order of
n^{2}
), so those distributions are just approximated instead of perfectly known. As a result, the closure emerges when taking limit.
The two conditions in
Proposition

2
are also added to keep the proof tractable when taking limit. Specifically, the first condition is to avoid the case that KL divergence being possibly infinity on the boundary of the probability simplex. The second condition means the threshold function
\lambda
should be continuous even on the boundary of its domain.
The detailed proof of
Proposition

2
can be found in
Section

VI
.

Let us now investigate when the converse and achievability can coincide. In the following lemma, we first show that if the two conditions in
Proposition

2
hold,
\Omega(\bm{P}_{1})

can be replaced with another set
\Omega^{\prime}(\bm{P}_{1})
which differs from
\bar{\Omega}(\bm{P}_{1})

only in substituting
\bar{g}^{\bm{P}_{1}}(\bm{Q})
with
g^{\bm{P}_{1}}(\bm{Q})
. The proof is given in Appendix
B-A
.

Lemma 1
.

Suppose the two conditions in
Proposition

2
hold, then

\inf_{\bm{Q}\in\Omega(\bm{P}_{1})}\sum_{i=1}^{s}\alpha_{i}\mathrm{D}\mkern-1.5mu\left(Q_{i}\middle\|P_{1,i}\right)=\inf_{\bm{Q}\in\Omega^{\prime}(\bm{P}_{1})}\sum_{i=1}^{s}\alpha_{i}\mathrm{D}\mkern-1.5mu\left(Q_{i}\middle\|P_{1,i}\right),

(13)

where
\Omega^{\prime}(\bm{P}_{1})=\left\{\bm{Q}\in\mathcal{S}(\bm{P}_{1})\,\middle|\,g^{\bm{P}_{1}}(\bm{Q})\leq 0\right\}
.

To proceed, further note that
g^{\bm{P}_{1}}(\bm{Q})
in (
8
) can be rewritten as

g^{\bm{P}_{1}}(\bm{Q})=\inf_{\bm{P}_{0}^{\prime}\in\mathscr{P}_{0}\cap\mathcal{S}(\bm{P}_{1})}\Big(\sum_{i=1}^{\ell}\alpha_{i}\mathrm{D}\mkern-1.5mu\left(Q_{i}\middle\|P_{0,i}^{\prime}\right)-\bar{\lambda}(\bm{P}_{0}^{\prime})\Big).

(14)

In general,
\mathscr{P}_{0}\cap\mathcal{S}(\bm{P}_{1})\subseteq\mathrm{cl}(\mathscr{P}_{0}\cap\mathcal{S}(\bm{P}_{1}))\subseteq\mathrm{cl}(\mathscr{P}_{0})\cap\mathcal{S}(\bm{P}_{1})
. Hence,

\displaystyle g^{\bm{P}_{1}}(\bm{Q})

\displaystyle=\inf_{\bm{P}_{0}^{\prime}\in\mathscr{P}_{0}\cap\mathcal{S}(\bm{P}_{1})}\Big(\sum_{i=1}^{\ell}\alpha_{i}\mathrm{D}\mkern-1.5mu\left(Q_{i}\middle\|P_{0,i}^{\prime}\right)-\bar{\lambda}(\bm{P}_{0}^{\prime})\Big)

(15)

\displaystyle\geq\bar{g}^{\bm{P}_{1}}(\bm{Q})

\displaystyle=\inf_{\bm{P}_{0}^{\prime}\in\mathrm{cl}(\mathscr{P}_{0})\cap\mathcal{S}(\bm{P}_{1})}\Big(\sum_{i=1}^{\ell}\alpha_{i}\mathrm{D}\mkern-1.5mu\left(Q_{i}\middle\|P_{0,i}^{\prime}\right)-\bar{\lambda}(\bm{P}_{0}^{\prime})\Big).

(16)

As we will show in
Lemma

4
, since the KL divergence and
\bar{\lambda}
are continuous, taking infimum over
\mathscr{P}_{0}\cap\mathcal{S}(\bm{P}_{1})
in (
14
) is the same as taking infimum over
\mathrm{cl}(\mathscr{P}_{0}\cap\mathcal{S}(\bm{P}_{1}))
. Thus we can obtain a sufficient condition for which the converse and achievability match, that is,
\mathrm{cl}(\mathscr{P}_{0}\cap\mathcal{S}(\bm{P}_{1}))=\mathrm{cl}(\mathscr{P}_{0})\cap\mathcal{S}(\bm{P}_{1})
.
This sufficient condition will be leveraged to prove the main results for binary classification problems in
Section

IV
.

Let us summarize the above sufficient condition in the following proposition for convenience.

Proposition 3

(Characterization of the optimal type-II error exponent)
.

Suppose the two conditions in
Proposition

2
hold and
\mathrm{cl}(\mathscr{P}_{0}\cap\mathcal{S}(\bm{P}_{1}))=\mathrm{cl}(\mathscr{P}_{0})\cap\mathcal{S}(\bm{P}_{1})
, then the optimal type-II error exponent of all tests that satisfy the universality constraints in
Propositions

1
and
2
is
e_{1}^{*}(\bm{P}_{1})
in (
6
), and it can be achieved by a sequence of tests that do not depend on the actual
\bm{P}_{1}
.

Before we proceed to the specialization to the binary classification problem, let us close this sub-section by taking a look at a special case to better understand the general result. When
\ell=0
, all the samples arrive sequentially with a fixed ratio. In this case,
it can be shown that the optimal type-II error exponent can be achieved and simplified, even if
\mathrm{cl}(\mathscr{P}_{0}\cap\mathcal{S}(\bm{P}_{1}))\neq\mathrm{cl}(\mathscr{P}_{0})\cap\mathcal{S}(\bm{P}_{1})
.

Corollary 1

(Fully-sequential composite hypothesis testing)
.

Suppose the two conditions in
Proposition

2
hold and
\ell=0
, then the optimal type-II error exponent of all tests that satisfy the universality constraints in
Propositions

1
and
2
is

e_{1}^{*}(\bm{P}_{1})=\inf_{\bm{Q}\in\mathscr{P}_{0}\cup\Gamma_{0}}\sum_{i=1}^{s}\alpha_{i}\mathrm{D}\mkern-1.5mu\left(Q_{i}\middle\|P_{1,i}\right),

(17)

and it can be achieved by a sequence of tests that do not depend on the actual
\bm{P}_{1}
.

Proof.

Since
\mathscr{P}_{0}
and
\mathscr{P}_{1}
are disjoint, we have
\mathcal{S}(\bm{P}_{1})=\{\bm{P}_{1}\}
and
\mathscr{P}_{0}\cap\mathcal{S}(\bm{P}_{1})=\emptyset
.
If
\mathrm{cl}(\mathscr{P}_{0})\cap\mathcal{S}(\bm{P}_{1})=\emptyset
, simply apply
Proposition

3
. Otherwise,
\mathrm{cl}(\mathscr{P}_{0})\cap\mathcal{S}(\bm{P}_{1})=\{\bm{P}_{1}\}
, and thus
\bm{P}_{1}\in\mathrm{cl}(\mathscr{P}_{0})
. With the help of
Lemma

4
(presented later in
Section

VI
), we can observe that

\displaystyle 0\leq e_{1}^{*}(\bm{P}_{1})

\displaystyle\leq\inf_{\bm{Q}\in\mathscr{P}_{0}\cup\Gamma_{0}}\sum_{i=1}^{s}\alpha_{i}\mathrm{D}\mkern-1.5mu\left(Q_{i}\middle\|P_{1,i}\right)

(18)

\displaystyle\leq\inf_{\bm{Q}\in\mathscr{P}_{0}}\sum_{i=1}^{s}\alpha_{i}\mathrm{D}\mkern-1.5mu\left(Q_{i}\middle\|P_{1,i}\right)

(19)

\displaystyle=\inf_{\bm{Q}\in\mathrm{cl}(\mathscr{P}_{0})}\sum_{i=1}^{s}\alpha_{i}\mathrm{D}\mkern-1.5mu\left(Q_{i}\middle\|P_{1,i}\right)=0,

(20)

where the last equality holds because
\bm{P}_{1}\in\mathrm{cl}(\mathscr{P}_{0})
.
∎

Remark 2
.

In the fixed-length setup (
\ell=s
), the result in

]
shows matching converse and achievability without the additional conditions in
Proposition

2
. It is worth notice that if
\mathscr{P}_{0}
is bounded away from the boundary of
\mathscr{D}
, then the optimal type-II error exponent can be rewritten as the following:

e^{*}_{1,\mathsf{fix}}(\bm{P}_{1})=\inf_{\begin{subarray}{c}\bm{Q}\in\mathscr{D}\\
g_{1}(\bm{Q})\leq 0\end{subarray}}\sum_{i=1}^{s}\alpha_{i}\mathrm{D}\mkern-1.5mu\left(Q_{i}\middle\|P_{1,i}\right).

(21)

This result follows from the same argument as in
Lemma

1
and some observations in

.

IV
Main Results on Universal Classification with Empirically Observed Statistics

Now we switch our focus back to the binary classification problem and present the optimal type-II error exponents in different sequential setups.
To present the results, we first introduce two divergences.
For
P,Q\in\mathcal{P}(\mathcal{X})
, the Rényi Divergence of order
\frac{\alpha}{1+\alpha}
of
P
from
Q
can be expressed as

\mathrm{D}_{\frac{\alpha}{1+\alpha}}\mkern-1.5mu\left(P\middle\|Q\right)=\min_{V\in\mathcal{P}(\mathcal{X})}\{\alpha\mathrm{D}\mkern-1.5mu\left(V\middle\|P\right)+\mathrm{D}\mkern-1.5mu\left(V\middle\|Q\right)\},

(22)

and the minimizer is
V^{*}
, where

V^{*}(x)=\frac{P(x)^{\frac{\alpha}{1+\alpha}}Q(x)^{\frac{1}{1+\alpha}}}{\sum_{x^{\prime}\in\mathcal{X}}P(x^{\prime})^{\frac{\alpha}{1+\alpha}}Q(x^{\prime})^{\frac{1}{1+\alpha}}}\quad\text{for }x\in\mathcal{X}.

(23)

The
\alpha
-weighted generalized Jensen-Shannon (GJS) divergence of
Q
from
P
is defined as

\textstyle\mathrm{GJS}\mkern-1.5mu\left(P,Q,\alpha\right)=\alpha\mathrm{D}\mkern-1.5mu\left(P\middle\|\frac{\alpha P+Q}{\alpha+1}\right)+\mathrm{D}\mkern-1.5mu\left(Q\middle\|\frac{\alpha P+Q}{\alpha+1}\right).

(24)

We start with the
\mathsf{Fully\text{-}Sequential}
setup where all the samples arrive sequentially.

Theorem 1

(
\mathsf{Fully\text{-}Sequential}
)
.

Given
\lambda:\mathcal{D}_{\varepsilon}\to(0,\infty)
and let
\{\Phi_{n}\}
be a sequence of
\mathsf{Fully\text{-}Sequential}
tests such that for any underlying distributions
(P_{0},P_{1})\in\mathcal{D}_{\varepsilon}
,

•

for each
\Phi_{n}=(\tau_{n},\delta_{n})
,
\mathbb{E}_{\theta}[\tau_{n}|P_{0},P_{1}]\leq n\ \forall\,\theta\in\{0,1\}
,

•

e_{0}(P_{0},P_{1})\geq\lambda(P_{0},P_{1})
.

Then for any
(P_{0},P_{1})\in\mathcal{D}_{\varepsilon}
,
e_{1}(P_{0},P_{1})
is upper bounded by

e_{1,\mathsf{seq}}^{*}(P_{0},P_{1}):=\min\left\{\mathrm{D}_{\frac{\alpha}{1+\alpha}}\mkern-1.5mu\left(P_{0}\middle\|P_{1}\right),\ \kappa(P_{0},P_{1})\right\},

(25)

where
\kappa(P_{0},P_{1})=

\displaystyle\inf_{\begin{subarray}{c}(Q_{0},Q_{1})\in\mathcal{D}_{\varepsilon}\\
g_{1}(Q_{1},Q_{0},Q_{1})<0\end{subarray}}\Big(\mathrm{D}\mkern-1.5mu\left(Q_{1}\middle\|P_{1}\right)+\alpha\mathrm{D}\mkern-1.5mu\left(Q_{0}\middle\|P_{0}\right)+\beta\mathrm{D}\mkern-1.5mu\left(Q_{1}\middle\|P_{1}\right)\Big),

(26)

and
g_{1}(Q,Q_{0},Q_{1})=

\displaystyle\inf_{(P_{0}^{\prime},P_{1}^{\prime})\in\mathcal{D}_{\varepsilon}}\Big(\mathrm{D}\mkern-1.5mu\left(Q\middle\|P_{0}^{\prime}\right)

\displaystyle+\alpha\mathrm{D}\mkern-1.5mu\left(Q_{0}\middle\|P_{0}^{\prime}\right)

\displaystyle+\beta\mathrm{D}\mkern-1.5mu\left(Q_{1}\middle\|P_{1}^{\prime}\right)-\lambda(P_{0}^{\prime},P_{1}^{\prime})\Big).

(27)

Moreover, the upper bound
e_{1,\mathsf{seq}}^{*}(P_{0},P_{1})
can be achieved simultaneously for all
(P_{0},P_{1})\in\mathcal{D}_{\varepsilon}
.

Theorem

1
is proved as a corollary of
Proposition

3
by identifying it as a special case of the general composite hypothesis testing problem. Detailed derivation can be found in
Section

IV-A
.
In (
25
),
\mathrm{D}_{\frac{\alpha}{1+\alpha}}\mkern-1.5mu\left(P_{0}\middle\|P_{1}\right)
corresponds to the region of true distributions under ground truth
0
, that is
\mathscr{P}_{0}
in the general setup.
On the other hand,
\kappa(P_{0},P_{1})
corresponds to the region of true distributions under ground truth
1
that falls in the
\lambda
-balls centered at distributions under ground truth
0
, that is
\Gamma_{0}
in the general setup.

Next we look at the
\mathsf{Semi\text{-}Sequential\text{-}1}
setup where the testing samples
X_{k}
’s arrive sequentially and the numbers of
P_{0}
/
P_{1}
-training samples are fixed.

IV-C
2
Efficient Tests

Following
[
7
,
10
]
, we consider a special class of tests.
A sequence of tests is said to be
efficient

[
7
]
or
universally exponentially consistent

[
14
]
if the error probabilities decay to zero exponentially for all the underlying distributions as the number of samples goes to infinity.
Under our problem formulation, the formal definition of efficient tests is provided as follows.

Definition 1

(Efficient Tests)
.

Given
\lambda:\mathcal{D}_{\varepsilon}\to(0,\infty)
and let
\{\Phi_{n}\}
be a sequence of tests such that for any underlying distributions
(P_{0},P_{1})\in\mathcal{D}_{\varepsilon}
,

•

for each
\Phi_{n}=(\tau_{n},\delta_{n})
,

\mathbb{E}_{\theta}[\tau_{n}|P_{0},P_{1}]\leq n\ \forall\,\theta\in\{0,1\}
,

•

e_{0}(P_{0},P_{1})\geq\lambda(P_{0},P_{1})
.

We say
\{\Phi_{n}\}
is efficient if the type-II error exponent
e_{1}(P_{0},P_{1})>0
for all
(P_{0},P_{1})\in\mathcal{D}_{\varepsilon}
.

In order to have tests satisfying the universality constraints and being efficient, the constraint function
\lambda
has to satisfy certain conditions, and this will lead to further simplification of the optimal error exponents.
In the following, we will introduce a necessary condition and the consequent results. Then we state a sufficient condition and show some numerical examples.

Proposition 6
.

Given
\lambda:\mathcal{D}_{\varepsilon}\to(0,\infty)
, if there exists a sequence of tests satisfying the universality constraints on the expected stopping time and the type-I error exponent,
furthermore being efficient; then for all
(P_{0},P_{1})\in\mathcal{D}_{\varepsilon}
,
\lambda(P_{0},P_{1})\leq\mathrm{D}_{\frac{\beta}{1+\beta}}\mkern-1.5mu\left(P_{1}\middle\|P_{0}\right)
, and hence
\kappa(P_{0},P_{1})=\infty
. Thus

\displaystyle e_{1,\mathsf{seq}}^{*}(P_{0},P_{1})

\displaystyle=\mathrm{D}_{\frac{\alpha}{1+\alpha}}\mkern-1.5mu\left(P_{0}\middle\|P_{1}\right),

(64)

\displaystyle e_{1,\mathsf{semi1}}^{*}(P_{0},P_{1})

\displaystyle=\min\left\{\mathrm{D}_{\frac{\alpha}{1+\alpha}}\mkern-1.5mu\left(P_{0}\middle\|P_{1}\right),\ \mu(P_{0},P_{1})\right\},

(65)

\displaystyle e_{1,\mathsf{semi2}}^{*}(P_{0},P_{1})

\displaystyle=\min\left\{\mathrm{D}_{\frac{\alpha}{1+\alpha}}\mkern-1.5mu\left(P_{0}\middle\|P_{1}\right),\ \nu(P_{0},P_{1})\right\}.

(66)

Following the proof in
[
10
]
, it can be shown that the error exponents of efficient tests are upper bounded by the Rényi divergence, which imposes upper bounds on
\lambda(P_{0},P_{1})

As a result,
g_{1}(Q_{1},Q_{0},Q_{1})\geq 0

\kappa(P_{0},P_{1})=\infty

Detailed proof is provided in Appendix
C-C

VII-B

Assumptions on the Underlying Distributions

Apart from the constraint function, another possible relaxation is about the assumption on the underlying distributions. In the achievability part, it is assumed that
\mathscr{P}_{0}
is bounded away from the boundary of
\mathscr{D}
. If we allow distributions to come from
\mathrm{int}(\mathscr{D})
, it is possible that the sequence we construct in the proof converges to some distributions that do not have full support and that causes problems such as infinite KL divergences.

In
Proposition

2

Proposition

1

\mathrm{cl}(\mathscr{P}_{0})\cap\mathcal{S}(\bm{P}_{1})=\mathrm{cl}(\mathscr{P}_{0}\cap\mathcal{S}(\bm{P}_{1}))

\bm{Q}\in\mathrm{cl}(\mathscr{P}_{0})\cap\mathcal{S}(\bm{P}_{1})
that cannot be approximated by points in
\mathscr{P}_{0}\cap\mathcal{S}(\bm{P}_{1})
, then our current proposed tests may result in a sequence converging to that point and lead to smaller type-II error exponents. It is unclear whether it is the converse or achievability bound that is too loose. As mentioned before, as long as the number of samples are finite, the underlying distributions cannot be perfectly known. Since the error exponent is an asymptotic notion, attempts to universally achieve the converse bound may further jeopardize the performance when the sample size is small. Note that it is also possible that the assumption of perfectly known distributions in the proof of converse is too strong.

VII-E

Different Sequential Setups for Binary Classification

Previously, we focus on
\mathsf{Fixed\text{-}Length}
,
\mathsf{Semi\text{-}Sequential\text{-}1}
,
\mathsf{Semi\text{-}Sequential\text{-}2}

X,T_{0}
are sequentially observed and

has fixed length.
In this case, the optimal type-II error exponent can be shown to be the same as that in the
\mathsf{Fully\text{-}Sequential}
setup,

-training samples does not further improve the error exponents.

However, when

2

\displaystyle\min\Big\{\mathrm{D}_{\frac{\alpha}{1+\alpha}}\mkern-1.5mu\left(P_{0}\middle\|P_{1}\right),\ \kappa(P_{0},P_{1}),

\displaystyle\hskip 28.45274pt\inf_{Q\in\mathcal{P}(\mathcal{X}):\alpha\mathrm{D}\mkern-1.5mu\left(Q\middle\|P_{1}\right)\leq\bar{\lambda}(P_{1},P_{1})}\alpha\mathrm{D}\mkern-1.5mu\left(Q\middle\|P_{0}\right)\Big\}.

If

\mathrm{D}_{\frac{\alpha}{1+\alpha}}\mkern-1.5mu\left(P_{0}\middle\|P_{1}\right)

\bar{\lambda}(P_{1},P_{1})=0
. But in general we cannot show the optimality of the achievability result.

VIII
Conclusion

In this work, we consider a unified framework for binary classification with universality constraints on the expected stopping time and the type-I error exponent. Furthermore, we extend to a more general composite hypothesis testing problem and provide upper and lower bounds on the error exponents, along with a sufficient condition such that the bounds match. Applying the results to binary classification, we compare the optimal error exponents in different sequential setups in a fair manner to show the benefit of sequentiality. Under constant type-I error exponent constraint, sequentiality in taking testing samples suffices to achieve strict gain over fixed-length setup. On the other hand, for efficient tests, we derive necessary and sufficient condition such that sequentiality in taking training samples results in additional gain.

Throughout this work, we focus on distributions on fixed and finite alphabets.
It is interesting whether this kind of tests can be extended to more general parametric distributions such as exponential families. Since the method of types is heavily used in this work and is only applicable for distributions on finite support, an alternative tool for continuous distributions is required.
In particular, kernel-based tests such as those based on the maximum mean discrepancy (MMD) metric are suitable for achievability and have been explored in the literature
[
15
,
16
,
17
]
.
Another more practical direction is to consider data with high dimension or growing alphabet size.
In practice, our test is difficult to compute as it involves solving a lot of optimization problems. So one may attempt to design a test with lower computational complexity and simpler statistics.
Also, our focus is on the error exponents, which is an asymptotic notion. Although the test is asymptotically optimal, there is no performance guarantee for small sample size. A possible direction is to study the sample complexity under the assumption that the two underlying distributions are
\varepsilon
-separated (total variation at least
\varepsilon
), as in
[
13
]
.

\alpha<1
. Arbitrarily pick
(P_{0},P_{1})\in\mathcal{D}_{\varepsilon}
, and now the goal is to find
P_{1}^{\prime}\in\mathcal{P}_{\varepsilon}\setminus\{P_{1}\}
such that
\alpha\mathrm{D}\mkern-1.5mu\left(V_{\alpha}\middle\|P_{1}\right)<\mathrm{D}\mkern-1.5mu\left(V_{\beta}\middle\|P_{1}\right)<\frac{1}{\beta}\mathrm{D}\mkern-1.5mu\left(V_{\alpha}\middle\|P_{1}\right)
. This is possible since
\alpha<\frac{1}{\beta}
and
V_{\alpha}\neq P_{1}
.
The closed-form expression of
V_{\beta}
can be derived and notice that it is continuous in
P_{1}^{\prime}
. Hence
\mathrm{D}\mkern-1.5mu\left(V_{\beta}\middle\|P_{1}\right)
is continuous in
P_{1}^{\prime}
. When
P_{1}^{\prime}=P_{1}
,
\mathrm{D}\mkern-1.5mu\left(V_{\beta}\middle\|P_{1}\right)=0
. When
P_{1}^{\prime}=P_{0}
, since
\alpha\leq\beta
, it can be shown that
\mathrm{D}\mkern-1.5mu\left(V_{\alpha}\middle\|P_{1}\right)\leq\mathrm{D}\mkern-1.5mu\left(V_{\beta}\middle\|P_{1}\right)
.
By the intermediate value theorem, there exists
P_{1}^{\prime}
between
P_{1}
and
P_{0}
with the desired property.

Proof of Lemma
4

For the first part, clearly,
\inf_{x\in A}f(x)\geq\inf_{x\in\mathrm{cl}(A)}f(x)
. For
y\in\mathrm{cl}(A)
, choose a sequence
\{y_{n}\}
such that
y_{n}\in A
and
y_{n}\to y
. By the continuity of
f
and the definition of infimum, we have

f(y)=\lim_{n\to\infty}f(y_{n})\geq\inf_{x\in A}f(x).

(274)

Since this holds for all
y\in\mathrm{cl}(A)
, it follows that
\inf_{x\in\mathrm{cl}(A)}f(x)\geq\inf_{x\in A}f(x)
.

For the second part, observe that for any
\epsilon>0
, we have
\inf_{x\in\mathcal{B}^{\prime}_{\epsilon}(A)\cap S}f(x)\leq\inf_{x\in\mathrm{cl}(A)}f(x)<\infty
. Also,
\inf_{x\in\mathcal{B}^{\prime}_{\epsilon}(A)\cap S}f(x)
is non-decreasing as
\epsilon\to 0
. Hence we know the limit exists. Assume

\lim_{\epsilon\to 0}\inf_{x\in\mathcal{B}^{\prime}_{\epsilon}(A)\cap S}f(x)=a<\inf_{x\in\mathrm{cl}(A)}f(x).

(275)

For each
n\in\mathbb{N}
, there exists

such that
f(y_{n})\leq\inf_{x\in\mathcal{B}^{\prime}_{1/n}(A)\cap S}f(x)+1/n
. Since
y_{n}\in\mathcal{B}^{\prime}_{1/n}(A)
, there exists
x_{n}\in A
such that
\left\lVert y_{n}-x_{n}\right\rVert_{1}\leq 1/n
. Consider a convergent subsequence

y\in S
denote the limit point. Since

.

f(y)=\lim_{i\to\infty}f(y_{n_{i}})\leq a<\inf_{x\in\mathrm{cl}(A)}f(x),

(276)
