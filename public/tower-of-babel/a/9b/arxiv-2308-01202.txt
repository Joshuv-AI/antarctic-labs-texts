The Bartnik quasi-local mass conjectures
Michael T. Anderson

Abstract
. This paper is a tribute to Robert Bartnik and his work and conjectures on quasi-local mass. We present a framework in which to clearly analyse Bartnik’s static vacuum extension conjecture. While we prove that this conjecture is not true in general, it remains a fundamental open problem to understand the realm of its validity.



In Memory of Robert Bartnik

1.
Introduction

This paper is a tribute to Robert Bartnik and his work on quasi-local mass in General Relativity. I met Robert in person only
twice, at the Newton Institute in 2005 and then a few years later in Oberwolfach and my personal interactions with him were sadly somewhat limited.
Robert’s conjectures on the realization of the quasi-local mass, related to the structure of the space of static vacuum solutions of the Einstein equations
have had a profound impact on a significant portion of my research over the last 20 years. Personally, I was not trained early in GR and came to the
subject rather indirectly through a study of the geometrization problem for 3-manifolds (the Thurston conjecture). In that approach to geometrization,
a surprising connection arose between the degeneration of metrics of controlled scalar curvature and the structure of static vacuum Einstein metrics in 3
dimensions.

1

The singularity models of such degeneration are static vacuum Einstein metrics, much like the singularity models of Ricci flow are
Ricci solitons. These two equations are superficially somewhat similar and it would be of interest to understand any deeper relations between them.

I hope this paper may contribute in a small way to a deeper understanding and appreciation of the Bartnik quasi-local mass conjectures.

The Bartnik mass of a bounded domain
\Omega
with smooth boundary
\partial\Omega
is a very natural and direct localization of the global
ADM mass
m_{ADM}
. The original definition of Bartnik is the following, cf.
[
17
]
,
[
18
]
. Let
\Omega
be a 3-dimensional domain with smooth
connected boundary
\partial\Omega
(we will usually assume
\partial\Omega\simeq S^{2}
) and let
g_{\Omega}
be a smooth Riemannian metric on

\Omega
, smooth up to
\partial\Omega
, with non-negative scalar curvature,
s_{g_{\Omega}}\geq 0
. Let
(M,g)
be an asymptotically flat (AF) extension
of
(\Omega,g_{\Omega})
; thus
{\partial M}=\partial\Omega
and the union
\Omega\cup M
is a smooth, complete AF Riemannian manifold with non-negative and
integrable scalar curvature. Assume in addition that
(M,g)
has no horizons, i.e.
(M,g)
has no minimal surfaces surrounding
{\partial M}
.
Let
{\mathcal{P}}_{\Omega}
denote the set of such AF extensions
(M,g)
. The Bartnik mass of the smooth bounded domain
(\Omega,g_{\Omega})
is then defined by

(1.1)

m_{B}(\Omega,g_{\Omega})=\inf\{m_{ADM}(M,g):(M,g)\in{\mathcal{P}}_{\Omega}\},

where the infimum is taken over all
(M,g)\in{\mathcal{P}}_{\Omega}
.

Bartnik observed in
[
17
]
,
[
18
]
that an AF extension
(M,g)
of
\partial\Omega
with
\widehat{M}=\Omega\cup M
which
realizes the infimum in (
1.1
) will in general only be Lipschitz along the “seam”
\partial\Omega={\partial M}
. By a
simple and elegant argument using the
2^{\rm nd}
variational formula for area, he showed that a minimizer should obey
the boundary conditions

(1.2)

\gamma_{\partial\Omega}=\gamma_{{\partial M}},\ \ \ \ H_{\partial\Omega}=H_{{\partial M}},

where
\gamma
is the induced metric on the boundary and
H
is the mean curvature of
{\partial M}
with respect to the unit normal pointing into
M
,
both with respect to
g_{\Omega}
and
g
respectively. The relation (
1.2
)
implies that the scalar curvature is defined as a non-negative distribution across the seam
{\partial M}=\partial\Omega
.
2
2

2

Actually this holds for

H_{\partial\Omega}\geq H_{{\partial M}}
and this condition is sometimes used instead of (
1.2
), cf.
[
29
]
.
Standard minimal surface
arguments show that if
H_{{\partial M}}\leq 0
then any extension
(M,g)
has a horizon, so that it is common practice to assume

(1.3)

H=H_{{\partial M}}>0.

As discussed in more detail in §2, there are several further reasons why the restriction (
1.3
) is essential. In the following, we will
always assume (
1.3
), unless explicitly noted otherwise.

The quasi-local data
(\gamma,H)
of
\Omega
on
\partial\Omega={\partial M}
are now called Bartnik boundary data. There are a number of modifications or
variations of the definition of the Bartnik mass
m_{B}
; we refer to
[
29
]
and further references therein for a careful and detailed
discussion.

By general principles, it is to be expected that an extension
(M,g)
realizing the infimum in (
1.1
) satisfies strong
conditions. In
[
17
]
,
[
18
]
, Bartnik presented a natural physical argument that extensions
(M,g)
realizing the infimum in
(
1.1
) should be solutions of the static vacuum Einstein equations. Briefly, any dynamical gravitational
field carries energy and so mass and so an extension of minimal mass should
have no gravitational dynamics, i.e. be time-independent. For similar reasons, a minimal-mass extension should have no mass coming
from matter sources, and so be vacuum. A time-independent vacuum solution which is time-symmetric (
K=0
) is static vacuum.

The static vacuum Einstein equations are the equations for a pair
(g,u)
on
M
where
u
is a potential function (forming
the lapse function of the space-time
{\mathcal{M}}=I\times M
) given by

(1.4)

u{\rm Ric}_{g}=D^{2}u,\ \ \Delta u=0,

where
{\rm Ric}_{g}
is the Ricci curvature,
D^{2}u
is the Hessian of
u
and
\Delta u
is the Laplacian of
u
, all with respect to
g
.
These equations are equivalent to the statement that the space-time metric

(1.5)

g_{{\mathcal{M}}}=-u^{2}dt^{2}+g

is Ricci-flat, i.e. a vacuum solution of the Einstein equations.
3
3

3

This holds in both Lorentizian and Riemannian signature.

It is important both mathematically and physically to add the requirements that
u>0
in
M
and
u\rightarrow 1
at infinity.

A clearer approach as to why the static vacuum equations (
1.4
) arise as minimizers was suggested by Bartnik in
[
19
]
, following
a proposal by Brill-Deser-Fadeev
[
21
]
regarding the positive mass theorem. Thus consider the Regge-Teitelboim Hamiltonian (with
zero shift) given by

(1.6)

{\mathcal{H}}_{RT}=-\int_{M}us_{g}dv_{g}+16\pi m_{ADM},

where
s_{g}={\rm tr}_{g}{\rm Ric}_{g}
is the scalar curvature.
The first term corresponds to the Einstein-Hilbert action while the mass term is introduced to give a well-defined, and in particular
differentiable variational problem on the space of fields. The configuration space of fields is here given by AF static metrics as in
(
1.5
), but not necessarily vacuum, i.e. pairs
(g,u)
with
u>0
; the AF conditions are described more precisely in §2. The
Hamiltonian
{\mathcal{H}}_{RT}
is thus a smooth function on such static pairs
(g,u)
.

Let

{\mathcal{C}}=\{g:R_{g}=0\}.

This is the vacuum constraint set (in the time-symmetric case
K=0
). Let
{\mathcal{C}}_{(\gamma,H)}\subset{\mathcal{C}}
be the subset where the
boundary data
(\gamma,H)
induced by
g
is fixed. A necessary first step in the Bartnik program is to prove that
{\mathcal{C}}_{(\gamma,H)}
is a smooth (Banach)
manifold; this was proved to be the case in
[
14
]
. The Hamiltonian
{\mathcal{H}}_{RT}
then
restricts to be the smooth function

{\mathcal{H}}_{RT}=16\pi m_{ADM}:{\mathcal{C}}_{(\gamma,H)}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}

on
{\mathcal{C}}_{(\gamma,H)}
. Thus, critical points of the ADM mass on
{\mathcal{C}}_{(\gamma,H)}
are just the critical points of
{\mathcal{H}}_{RT}
on
{\mathcal{C}}_{(\gamma,H)}
. It is then
straightforward to verify that such critical points are exactly static vacuum Einstein metrics. Further, cf.
[
14
]
, one has
u>0

everywhere and
u\rightarrow 1
at infinity, at least for critical points realizing the infimum of
m_{ADM}
on
{\mathcal{C}}_{(\gamma,H)}
, i.e. the Bartnik mass.

There is a useful analogy, cf.
[
18
]
,
[
20
]
,
[
27
]
, of the Bartnik mass with the gravitational capacity of a body
\Omega\subset\mathbb{R}^{3}

in Newtonian gravity, or a charged body in electrostatics. Here one minimizes the Dirichlet energy,

(1.7)

E(v)=\int_{M}|dv|^{2},

over
M=\mathbb{R}^{3}\setminus\Omega
with boundary conditions
v=1
at
\partial\Omega
and
v\rightarrow 0
at infinity.
4
4

4

In (
1.7
) and below
the volume form will often be dropped from the notation when its choice is obvious.
(One could also
set
v^{\prime}=1-v
with
v^{\prime}\rightarrow 1
at infinity). Classical results show that the infimum of (
1.7
) is realized by a unique harmonic
function
v_{0}
,
\Delta v_{0}=0
on
M
;
v_{0}
represents the gravitational potential of the single layer
\partial\Omega
. The capacity
of
\Omega
, equal to the total mass or charge up to a constant, is then given by

(1.8)

E(\Omega)=\inf E(v)=-\int_{{\partial M}}N(v_{0}),

where
N
is the unit normal into
M
at
{\partial M}
.

Partly based on the Newtonian analogy, Bartnik
[
17
]
,
[
18
]
, made the bold conjecture that a minimizer
(M,g)
in (
1.1
)
also always exists and is unique.

Conjecture I (Bartnik Minimization Conjecture).
For any given smooth boundary data
(\gamma,H)
,
H>0
, of a domain
\Omega
,
\partial\Omega\simeq S^{2}
,
the infimum in (
1.1
) is realized by a unique (up to isometry) AF extension
(M,g)
, i.e.

m_{ADM}(M,g)=m_{B}.

Moreover, there is a positive potential function
u>0
on
M\simeq\mathbb{R}^{3}\setminus B
with
u\rightarrow 1
at infinity such that the triple
(M,g,u)

is a solution of the static vacuum Einstein equations (
1.4
) with boundary data
(\gamma,H)
.

The validity of this conjecture would ensure that the Bartnik mass is well-behaved as a function of the boundary data
(\gamma,H)
coming from
the solid body
(\Omega,g_{\Omega})
. For example, it would follow that the Bartnik mass is a smooth function of the boundary data
(\gamma,H)
; in fact
the mass would be effectively computable via the expression

m_{B}=\frac{1}{4\pi}\int_{{\partial M}}N(u),

where
(M,g,u)
is the unique static vacuum extension of
(\gamma,H)
.

If true, Conjecture I suggests the following conjecture
[
17
]
,
[
18
]
, which thus serves as a test
of the minimization conjecture but is also of independent interest in geometric PDE theory.

Conjecture II (Bartnik Static Extension Conjecture).
Given smooth boundary data
(\gamma,H)
,
H>0
, on
{\partial M}\simeq S^{2}
, there
exists a unique (up to isometry) AF solution
(M,g,u)
,
M\simeq\mathbb{R}^{3}\setminus B
,
u>0
,
u\rightarrow 1
at infinity, of the static
vacuum Einstein equations (
1.4
) which induces the data
(\gamma,H)
at
{\partial M}
.

Conjecture II may be considered as a pure PDE problem: it concerns the unique global solvability of a non-linear elliptic boundary
value problem. Conjecture I does not logically imply Conjecture II since a static vacuum solution may not minimize the Bartnik mass.
Nevertheless, we expect that any counterexample to Conjecture I leads also to counterexamples to Conjecture II. Of course
counterexamples to Conjecture II are counterexamples to Conjecture I.

The static vacuum Einstein equations are seemingly among the simplest set of geometric (diffeomorphism invariant) PDE for a general
metric
g
, either on a Lorentzian or Riemannian 4-manifold. In the Lorentzian or General Relativity setting, the simplest vacuum
Einstein solutions are static, i.e. time independent with vanishing initial momentum and so have a hypersurface-orthogonal time-like
Killing field.
5
5

5

The focus here is on static case; for recent progress on the more difficult stationary case, cf.
[
3
]
.

Similarly, Einstein metrics on 4-manifolds are of fundametal interest in Riemannian geometry and the existence of a
global non-vanishing Killing field reduces the Einstein PDE system to the simpler system (
1.4
) on a 3-manifold
M
.
While the equations (
1.4
) lie at the crossroads or intersection of Lorentzian and Riemannian geometry, gaining an understanding
of the space of solutions and of Conjectures I and II has remained very difficult. One could impose a further symmetry, i.e. Killing field
to simplify the problem further to 2 dimensions. This leads to the class of axi-symmetric or Weyl solutions, discussed further in
detail in §4: however, even in this case, Conjectures I and II remain very challenging.

For Conjecture II, the source of these difficulties is of course the non-linear nature of the PDE (
1.4
) and the boundary data
(\gamma,H)
.
This is also a coupled system of PDE, adding significantly to the complexity. To gain some general perspective, it is worth comparing
the global existence and uniqueness problem for static vacuum Einstein metrics with other natural geometric problems. In fact,
global existence and uniqueness for solutions of nonlinear elliptic geometric PDE is rather rare and is much more the exception
than the rule. The list of examples is huge, so we mention only the following illustrative examples:

Global existence and uniqueness:

\bullet
Kähler-Einstein metrics. Among the few situations where global existence and uniqueness hold, the most celebrated is Yau’s solution
of the Calabi problem for compact Kähler manifolds with
c_{1}=0
and the Aubin-Yau theorem in the case
c_{1}<0
, as well as the more
recent work of Chen-Donaldson-Sun in the more difficult positive case
c_{1}>0
. These existence proofs use a version of the continuity
method, discussed briefly in §2.

\bullet
Weyl embedding problem. Any smooth metric of positive Gauss curvature on
S^{2}
admits a smooth isometric embedding
into
\mathbb{R}^{3}
as the boundary of a convex body, unique up to rigid motion of
\mathbb{R}^{3}
. Again, the existence proof of this result by Nirenberg
uses the continuity method.

Global existence, non-uniqueness:

\bullet
The Yamabe problem. Global existence was established by variational methods through the combined work of Yamabe, Trudinger,
Aubin and Schoen. However, it is well-known that uniqueness, either of constant scalar curvature metrics or of Yamabe minimizers in a
conformal class, fails in general.

\bullet
Minimal varieties. There is a large and very satisfactory theory for existence of minimal varieties spanning a given boundary in an
ambient Riemannian manifold, using either parametric methods in low dimensions (Plateau problem) or non-parametric methods (geometric
measure theory) in all dimensions. However, it is again well-known that uniqueness fails in many situations.

Failure of global existence and uniqueness:

\bullet
Nirenberg problem. A very simple sounding problem is the Nirenberg problem of determining the existence and uniqueness of metrics on

S^{2}
conformal to the round metric with prescribed Gauss curvature. Here, both existence and uniqueness fail in general and the number of
solutions depends in a very complicated way on the structure of the Gauss curvature function
K:S^{2}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
.

\bullet
Vacuum Einstein constraint equations in GR. The main method of solving these constraint equations is the conformal method of
Lichnerowicz, Choquet-Bruhat and York. Here one has full existence and uniqueness results for constant mean curvature (CMC) data, but
failure of both existence and uniqueness for data far from CMC data.

One could easily go on and on; but the lesson is that non-linear geometric problems usually have complicated global behavior.

We note that there is now also a large and growing literature of estimates of the Bartnik mass
m_{B}(\gamma,H)
from below and above in terms of the
boundary data
(\gamma,H)
, as well as comparisons of
m_{B}
with other notions of quasi-local mass, such as the Hawking, Brown-York, or Wang-Yau masses.
These estimates and comparisons may be viewed as analogs of the extensive and well-known estimates of the capacity of a domain
\Omega\subset\mathbb{R}^{3}
,
or of the first eigenvalue
\lambda_{1}
of a compact Riemannian manifold, in terms of other geometric data.

However, the theory behind the capacity of a domain or of the first eigenvalue of the Laplacian is simple and easy to establish; they are realized by
a harmonic function or a first eigenfunction whose existence and uniqueness is easy to establish, since the Laplacian is a single scalar, linear PDE.
The focus is this paper is instead on the theory underlying the definition of Bartnik mass, in particular on the foundational Conjectures I and II.
The Bartnik mass formally exists as a number, but one should understand its structure and global behavior as a function of the boundary data

(\gamma,H)
.

In accord with most of the examples discussed above, it turns out that both Conjectures I and II are false in full generality.
For example, we prove:

Theorem 1.1
.

There is no static vacuum Einstein metric
(M,g,u)
smooth up to
{\partial M}
with boundary data
(\gamma,H)
, where
\gamma
is any
smooth metric on
S^{2}
for which the Gauss curvature
K_{\gamma}
is a Morse function on
S^{2}
and

0<H<H_{0},

where
H_{0}
is a positive constant, sufficiently small depending on
\gamma
.

We refer to Theorem 3.5 below for the exact statement. We also discuss in §3 why this non-existence result also leads to
expected local non-uniqueness for Conjecture II. Conjecture I was previously proved to be false in certain (quite different)
situations in
[
14
]
.

It remains then a fundamental challenge to identify conditions or restrictions on the boundary data
(\gamma,H)
which
ensure the validity of the Bartnik Conjectures.

A brief summary of the contents of this paper is as follows. In §2, we discuss the basic framework in which to analyse Conjecture II
and show that the local structures involved are very well behaved. In §3, we turn to global issues of existence and uniqueness and prove
in particular Theorem 1.1. A large class of relatively explicit examples of static vacuum Einstein metrics are the Weyl metrics, discussed
in detail in §4. Finally, in an Appendix, §5, we prove a regularity result for (local) minimal surfaces in static vacuum Einstein metrics needed
for the proof of Theorem 3.5.

2.
Local Behavior

Let
{\mathcal{B}}
be the set of Bartnik boundary data
(\gamma,H)
on
S^{2}
with
H>0
as in (
1.3
). Let
{\mathcal{E}}
be the set of isometry classes of
static vacuum Einstein solutions with
H>0
at the boundary
{\partial M}
.
6
6

6

It is an open question whether
{\mathcal{E}}
is connected or not. Thus

{\mathcal{E}}
is understood to be the component containing the standard flat exterior metric
\mathbb{R}^{3}\setminus B^{3}(1)
, with
g
the Euclidean
metric and
u\equiv 1
.
Thus
{\mathcal{E}}
is the set of equivalence classes of AF static vacuum
Einstein metrics
(M,g,u)
, modulo the equivalence relation

(g_{1},u_{1})\sim(g_{2},u_{2})

if there is a diffeomorphism
\psi:M\rightarrow M
with
\psi={\rm Id}
on
{\partial M}
and
\psi\rightarrow{\rm Id}
at infinity, (cf. (
2.4
) below), such that

\psi^{*}g_{2}=\psi^{*}g_{1}
and
\psi^{*}u_{2}=u_{1}
. One has a natural
Bartnik boundary map

(2.1)

\Pi_{B}:{\mathcal{E}}\rightarrow{\mathcal{B}},

(g,u)\rightarrow(\gamma,H).

Thus the static extension Conjecture II is equivalent to the statement that
\Pi_{B}
is a bijection. This means that the space of solutions
(M,g,u)
(up to
isometry) should be set-theoretically parametrized by its boundary data
(\gamma,H)
. However, it is natural to introduce topology at this point.
Well-posedness in PDE also typically assumes stability properties; small perturbations of the data (boundary data in this situation, initial or
initial
+
boundary data in the case of parabolic or hyperbolic PDE) implies small perturbations of the solution; this corresponds to
continuity of the inverse map
\Pi_{B}^{-1}
in (
2.1
).

The set
{\mathcal{B}}
of boundary data may be naturally topologized in several ways. For convenience, we choose here the Hölder space topology,

{\mathcal{B}}={\rm Met}^{m,\alpha}(S^{2})\times C_{+}^{m-1,\alpha}(S^{2}).

Here
{\rm Met}^{m,\alpha}(S^{2})
is the space of
C^{m,\alpha}
Riemannian metrics on
S^{2}
and
C_{+}^{m-1,\alpha}(S^{2})
is the space of positive
C^{m-1,\alpha}

functions on
S^{2}
. The space
{\mathcal{B}}
has the structure of a Banach manifold (an open subset of a linear Banach space)
7
7

7

In place of

C^{m,a}
, it is actually better to choose the so-called little Hölder spaces
c^{m,\alpha}
, i.e. the closure of
C^{\infty}
in the
C^{m,\alpha}
Hölder norm,
which has the structure of a separable Banach space; however, this is not an essential distinction in the discussion to follow.
.

Next, let
{\rm Met}_{\delta}^{m,\alpha}(M)
be the space of
C^{m,\alpha}
Riemannian metrics
g
on
M\simeq\mathbb{R}^{3}\setminus B
which are
asymptotically flat of order
\delta
, where
\delta\in(\frac{1}{2},1)
, i.e. for
r
sufficiently large,

(2.2)

|g-g_{Eucl}|\leq cr^{-\delta},\ \ |\partial^{k+\alpha}g|\leq cr^{-\delta-k-\alpha},

1\leq k\leq m
.
Similarly, let
C_{+}^{m,\alpha}(M)
denote the space of positive potential functions
u
on
M
which are asymptotically flat of order
\delta
:

(2.3)

|u-1|\leq cr^{-\delta},\ \ |\partial^{k+\alpha}u|\leq cr^{-\delta-k-\alpha}.

The pair
(g,u)
assemble to form the static or time-independent metric on the 4-manifold
{\mathcal{M}}
, as in (
1.5
).
A
C^{m+1,\alpha}
diffeomorphism
\psi:M\rightarrow M
is AF if

(2.4)

|\psi-{\rm Id}|\leq cr^{-\delta},\ \ |\partial^{k+\alpha}\psi|\leq cr^{-\delta-k-\alpha},

for
1\leq k\leq m+1
.

The product
{\rm Met}_{\delta}^{m,\alpha}(M)\times C_{+}^{m,\alpha}(M)
then also has the structure of a Banach manifold. Let
\mathbb{E}\subset{\rm Met}_{\delta}^{m,\alpha}(M)\times C_{+}^{m,\alpha}(M)
be the subset of solutions of the static vacuum Einstein equations (
1.4
) with

H>0
at
{\partial M}
. It is proved in
[
15
]
that
\mathbb{E}
is a smooth Banach submanifold of
{\rm Met}_{\delta}^{m,\alpha}(M)\times C_{+}^{m,\alpha}(M)
.
Moreover, the group
{\rm Diff}_{1}^{m+1,\alpha}(M)
of
C^{m+1,\alpha}
AF diffeomorphisms of
M
equal to the identity on the boundary

{\partial M}
and asymptotic to
{\rm Id}
at infinity as in (
2.4
), acts smoothly, freely and properly on
\mathbb{E}
, with a smooth local slice.
Consequently, the quotient

(2.5)

{\mathcal{E}}=\mathbb{E}/{\rm Diff}_{1}^{m+1,\alpha}(M),

representing isometry classes of solutions, is also a smooth Banach manifold.

In addition, the Bartnik boundary map
\Pi_{B}
in (
2.1
) is a smooth Fredholm map, of Fredholm index 0, cf. again
[
15
]
.
Thus the linearization or derivative
D_{(g,u)}\Pi_{B}
at any point
(M,g,u)
is a Fredholm map of Banach spaces. This
means that the linear map
D\Pi_{B}
has closed range and finite dimensional kernel and cokernel. In more detail, let

K
be the finite dimensional kernel of
D\Pi_{B}
at some point
(M,g,u)\in{\mathcal{E}}
. The closed subspace
K
admits a closed
complement
D
,

T_{(g,u)}{\mathcal{E}}=D\oplus K,

and similarly the image,
W={\rm Im}D\Pi_{B}
is a closed complemented subspace of
T_{(\gamma,H)}{\mathcal{B}}
. Let
C
denote the finite dimensional
complement. Then
dimC=dimK=k<\infty
since
D\Pi_{B}
has Fredholm index
0
. The restricted map

D_{(g,u)}\Pi|_{D}:D\rightarrow W,

is an isomorphism.

If
dimK=0
, then
D\Pi_{B}
itself is an isomorphism and so the inverse function theorem for Banach manifolds implies
\Pi_{B}
is a
local diffeomorphism near
(M,g,u)
. Suppose
dimK=k>0
. Then the implicit function theorem (also known as the constant rank theorem)
for Banach manifolds states that there is a local submanifold
{\mathcal{U}}
of codimension
k
in
{\mathcal{E}}
, with
T_{(g,u)}{\mathcal{U}}\oplus K=T_{(g,u)}{\mathcal{E}}

and a local submanifold
{\mathcal{V}}
of codimension
k
in the boundary data space
{\mathcal{B}}
, with
T_{(\gamma,H)}{\mathcal{V}}=W
such that

(2.6)

\Pi_{B}|_{{\mathcal{U}}}:{\mathcal{U}}\rightarrow{\mathcal{V}}

is a diffeomorphism. Thus, near any given solution
(M,g,u)
, one has local existence (and local uniqueness) of solutions, parametrized
by boundary data
(\gamma,H)
in an open set of a local submanifold of codimension
k
in
{\mathcal{B}}
.

We see then that the local structure of the space of solutions
{\mathcal{E}}
and the local behavior of the map
\Pi_{B}
relating solutions with boundary
data are both very well behaved. However, this structure of the mapping
\Pi_{B}
breaks down at the locus
H=0
(minimal surface boundary)
basically due to the black hole uniqueness theorem. To see this, let
\bar{\mathcal{E}}
be the closure of
{\mathcal{E}}
in the
C^{m,\alpha}
norm. This consists of
static vacuum Einstein metrics
(M,g,u)
with
u\geq 0
on
{\partial M}
.
8
8

8

It does not really make sense in this framework to consider metrics
where “
u<0
” somewhere.
Similarly, let
\widetilde{\mathcal{B}}
be the full space of boundary data, without the restriction that
H>0
. One still has the
Bartnik boundary map

(2.7)

\Pi_{B}:\bar{\mathcal{E}}\rightarrow\widetilde{\mathcal{B}},

(M,g,u)\rightarrow(\gamma,H).

Proposition 2.1
.

Let
(M,g,u)
be an AF static vacuum Einstein metric in
\bar{\mathcal{E}}
and let
\Sigma
be a minimal surface surrounding
{\partial M}
,

Proof.

\int_{\Sigma}|df|^{2}-(|A|^{2}+{\rm Ric}(N,N))f^{2}\geq 0,

Again, the static vacuum Einstein equations imply that

on

\Sigma

Since by the constraint equation (

Now consider the (so-called) optical metric

Points

) if it is not a regular point.

An important locus of singularities of a smooth index 0 Fredholm map
{\mathcal{F}}:{\mathcal{M}}\rightarrow{\mathcal{N}}
between Banach manifolds is a fold locus. This is given
by a (local) hypersurface
{\mathcal{H}}\subset{\mathcal{M}}
(a codimension 1 submanifold) where
{\mathcal{H}}
consists of singular points of
{\mathcal{F}}
with

dim{\rm Ker}\,D{\mathcal{F}}=1
and the map
{\mathcal{F}}
is 2-1 in an open neighborhood of
{\mathcal{H}}
in
{\mathcal{M}}
off of

; compare with (

), (

)
and the discussion following (

) below. In the context of (Riemannian) Einstein metrics, the most well-known example of such fold
behavior is the Hawking-Page phase transition for the AdS Schwarzschild metrics
[
26
]
. Similar fold behavior holds for the the interior
Dirichlet boundary map for Ricci-flat Schwarzschild metrics
[
2
]
. Such behavior also occurs for the general interior Bartnik boundary
map
[
10
]
, i.e. for the analog of (
2.1
) where

is replaced by a compact (interior) manifold with boundary.

It is natural to conjecture that generically, i.e. at least on an open dense subset of
{\mathcal{E}}
, the linearization
D\Pi_{B}
is an isomorphism,
i.e. the regular or non-degenerate points in the domain

are generic. This issue has been widely considered and analysed in many
other geometric PDE (and ODE) problems. For example, for geodesics in Riemannian manifolds, this is the statement that the regular points
of the exponential map

are open and dense in

; generically, two points in
M
are not conjugate along a geodesic, i.e. there
are no Jacobi fields vanishing at the end points. Similarly for minimal submanifolds, for a generic boundary in fixed Riemannian manifold,
(or for a generic Riemannian metric), minimal surfaces have no Jacobi fields vanishing on the boundary.

In fact, based on an analogy with the monotonicity of the first eigenvalue of the Laplacian on bounded domains with Dirichlet boundary
condition with respect to inclusion of domains, White
[
41
]
proved that the set of minimal immersions with non-trivial Jacobi fields
vanishing on the boundary is of codimension 1 in the space of all minimal immersions
(M,{\partial M})\rightarrow(N,g_{N})
. This is of course a
much stronger conclusion than generic. The proof in
[
41
]
uses a variational approach, together with a normal interior variation of
the domain of the minimal surface and the Calderon unique continuation theorem for elliptic systems. A similar result was proved for the
interior Bartnik problem in
[
10
]
using an analogous approach, but based on a unique continuation property for Einstein metrics and
their linearization; this is considerably more difficult partly due to gauge issues.

Using essentially this same approach but with some modifications, An-Huang
[
4
]
,
[
5
]
have recently shown that the regular points
of
\Pi_{B}
are indeed generic in many cases.

As discussed above, while the spaces

and the boundary map

, consider the

by the

by the

(2.13)

\pi:S\rightarrow\mathbb{R},

For

is small; for most values in

there is no solution. The space of boundary data realized by solutions

In the example above, the degree of

is

the following simple modification. Let

be the graph of the function

over the interval
(-\frac{\pi}{2},\frac{\pi}{2})
and consider

(2.14)

\pi:T\rightarrow\mathbb{R},

discussed in more detail for the map

in

One of the most effective methods of proving global existence and uniqueness is the continuity method, as mentioned above in the works on the
solution to the Calabi conjecture and the solution to the Weyl embedding problem. In the current context, this method can be described as follows.
Choose boundary data for which a static vacuum solution is known to exist; for instance
(\gamma_{0},H_{0})=(\gamma_{+1},2)
, which is realized by the exterior
flat metric
M=\mathbb{R}^{3}\setminus B^{3}(1)
,
g=g_{Eucl}
is the Euclidean metric and
u=1
. Given any boundary data
(\gamma,H)\in{\mathcal{B}}
, choose a
path from
(\gamma_{0},H_{0})
to
(\gamma,H)
in
{\mathcal{B}}
; for instance

(\gamma_{s},H_{s})=(1-s)(\gamma_{0},H_{0})+s(\gamma,H).

To prove there is a solution in
{\mathcal{E}}
with boundary data
(\gamma,H)
, one shows that the set of
s
for which there is a solution is both open and
closed in
[0,1]
. The openness property amounts to showing that solutions
(M,g_{s},u_{s})
with
\Pi_{B}(M,g_{s},u_{s})=(\gamma_{s},H_{s})
are always
non-degenerate. In case the degeneracy locus is non-empty, for example a collection of fold hypersurfaces in
{\mathcal{E}}
, then a very useful
replacement or generalization of the method of continuity is the degree theory, e.g. the Leray-Schauder degree or more generally
the Smale degree
[
38
]
. This does not require the non-degeneracy property, but to establish a well-defined degree does require
the properness of the map
\Pi_{B}
, which is the analog of the closedness condition above.

3.
Global Behavior

The key issue in understanding the global behavior of
\Pi_{B}
is to understand how well the boundary data
(\gamma,H)
controls the
behavior of any possible solution
(M,g,u)
with such boundary data. As always with elliptic problems, this requires apriori estimates on the
geometry of solutions
(M,g,u)
in terms of
(\gamma,H)
. Proving such estimates is equivalent to proving that
\Pi_{B}
is a proper map,
i.e. the inverse image
(\Pi_{B})^{-1}(K)
of compact sets
K\subset{\mathcal{B}}
are compact sets in the domain
{\mathcal{E}}
. We point out that
Fredholm maps are always locally proper, cf.
[
38
]
.

As a starting point of this analysis, we recall that one does have optimal apriori estimates of solutions
(M,g,u)
in the interior of
M
,
away from
{\partial M}
. Let
t(x)=dist_{g}(x,{\partial M})
be the distance function to the boundary
{\partial M}
and let
\nu=\log u
. We recall that, by
definition,
u>0
in
M
. By
[
6
]
, there is a universal constant

K<\infty
such that

(3.1)

|{\rm Rm|}(x)\leq\frac{K}{t^{2}(x)},\ \ |d\nu(x)|\leq\frac{K}{t(x)},

for any static vacuum solution
(M,g,u)
; here
{\rm Rm}
is the Riemann curvature tensor of
g
. The estimates (
3.1
) are scale invariant
and such scale-invariant estimates also hold for all higher derivatives. It follows (from the Cheeger-Gromov compactness theorem) that if one
has weak uniform control on the global geometry of the locus
L_{1}=\{t=1\}
in
(M,g)
, namely a lower bound on its area and upper bound
on its diameter,

(3.2)

{\rm area}_{g}L_{1}\geq v>0,\ \ {\rm diam}_{g}L_{1}\leq D<\infty,

then one obtains global uniform control on
(M,g,u)
away from
{\partial M}
. Under the bounds (
3.2
),
any sequence of static vacuum solutions
(M,g_{i},u_{i})\in{\mathcal{E}}
has a subsequence which converges, in
C_{\delta}^{\infty}
and away from the
boundary
{\partial M}
and modulo AF diffeomorphisms, to a limit AF static vacuum solution
(M,g,u)
, cf.
[
7
]
,
[
15
]
for further
details. In other words, one has good compactness properties away from the boundary - irrespective of the boundary conditions.

Thus, the issue is the behavior of solutions arbitrarily near the boundary
{\partial M}
.

The conjecture that
\Pi_{B}
in (
2.1
) is a bijection (Conjecture II) suggests the closely related conjecture that
\Pi_{B}
is a
diffeomorphism. However, this turns out not to be the case. It was observed by the author in
[
15
]
that
\Pi_{B}
is not a proper map, and
so in particular is not a homeomorphism. The reason for this is simple: the structure of a manifold-with-boundary
(M,{\partial M})
may
degenerate with uniform control on the boundary data
(\gamma,H)
. In other words, the embedded boundary
{\partial M}
in
M

may “degenerate” from an embedding to an immersion.

As a simple illustration, consider for instance any fixed static vacuum metric
(M,g,u)
with boundary
{\partial M}
and boundary data

(\gamma,H)\in{\mathcal{B}}
. Let
\Sigma
be any embedded
2
-sphere
S^{2}\subset M
homologous to the boundary
{\partial M}
with
H_{\Sigma}>0
. One
may then cut off
M
at
\Sigma
to obtain a new exterior solution
M^{\prime}
with
\partial M^{\prime}=\Sigma
and
corresponding boundary data
(\gamma_{\Sigma},H_{\Sigma})\in{\mathcal{B}}
at
\Sigma
. Suppose however
\Sigma
is an
immersed

2
-sphere in
(M,g)

homologous to
{\partial M}
. This again has induced “boundary” data
(\gamma_{\Sigma},H_{\Sigma})
from
(M,g)
, but
\Sigma
is not the natural smooth boundary
of an exterior manifold-with-boundary
M^{\prime}
. If the immersed surface
\Sigma
is isotopic to the boundary
{\partial M}
in
M
with
H_{\Sigma}>0
,
then at least in many cases one may easily find curves of spheres
\Sigma_{s}
in
(M,g)
which smoothly deform an embedded sphere
to an immersed sphere, with boundary data
(\gamma_{s},H_{s})
smoothly controlled for all
s
and with
H_{s}
remaining uniformly positive.
In this process, the ambient metric
g
and potential
u
remain fixed up to
{\partial M}
; only the domain
M_{s}
on which they are
defined is changing. Thus, control of the “boundary data”
(\gamma_{s},H_{s})
does not control the structure of the manifold-with-boundary
M_{s}
.
On the other hand, when
(M,g)
is an extension of a smooth interior body
(\Omega,g_{\Omega})
, the corresponding interior geometry

(\Omega_{s},g_{\Omega,s})
is well-controlled and does not degenerate at all.

Remark 3.1
.

It was proved in
[
14
]
that this type of degeneration leads to counterexamples to the Bartnik minimization conjecture, Conjecture I.
There exists a large family of immersed spheres
S^{2}
in
\mathbb{R}^{3}
whose boundary data
(\gamma,H)\in{\mathcal{B}}
are not realized by a static vacuum
extension of minimal mass; cf. also
[
12
]
for further discussion and conjectures.

Remark 3.2
.

One immediate consequence of this degeneration is a kind of symmetry breaking of the gauge group. As discussed in §2, the
natural gauge group for the Bartnik boundary value problem is the group
{\rm Diff}_{1}(M)
of AF diffeomorphisms
of
M
equal to the identity on the boundary
{\partial M}
. This corresponds to isometric metrics, and preserves
the boundary data
(\gamma,H)
. Suppose then
(\gamma,H)
are realized as boundary data of a static vacuum solution

(M,g,u)
, with
{\partial M}
embedded. Then clearly so are
(\varphi^{*}\gamma,\varphi^{*}H)
, for any diffeomorphism
\varphi
of

{\partial M}\simeq S^{2}
. Namely any such diffeomorphism extends to an AF diffeomorphism, also called
\varphi
, of
M

and the triple
(M,\varphi^{*}g,\varphi^{*}u)
is a solution of the static vacuum Einstein equations with boundary data
(\varphi^{*}\gamma,\varphi^{*}H)
. If however

\Sigma
is merely an immersed “boundary” in a larger space
M
, then this may not be the case. It is
no longer clear that if
(\gamma,H)
are the boundary data of
\Sigma
, then
(\varphi^{*}\gamma,\varphi^{*}H)
are realized as
boundary values of a static vacuum solution, for general diffeomorphisms
\varphi
of
\Sigma\simeq S^{2}
.
Thus the symmetry group of the problem has been reduced in the immersed case.

This breakdown of diffeomorphism invariance of the problem implies that one must work in a
suitable fixed gauge. One can no longer deal with metrics up to isometry (i.e. moduli spaces of
metrics or solutions to the equations) but only with metrics in a fixed gauge, i.e. (local) coordinate
system. It is worth noting that the Weyl metrics, discussed in detail in §4, do have a preferred global gauge (coordinate system).

Let (
M,g_{i},u_{i})\in{\mathcal{E}}
be a sequence of static vacuum solutions for which the boundary data
(\gamma_{i},H_{i})\in{\mathcal{B}}
converge to a limit

(\gamma,H)
,
H\geq 0
in
\widetilde{\mathcal{B}}
. There are only two ways that the sequence can degenerate, i.e. fail to have a subsequence which
converges to a limit
(M,g,u)\in\widetilde{\mathcal{E}}
, cf. (
2.7
)). First the structure of an exterior manifold with boundary may break down as
above; this occurs only if there is no uniform lower bound on the distance to the cut locus of the
g_{i}
-normal exponential map of
{\partial M}
into

(M,g_{i})
. Second, the norm of the Riemann curvature
{\rm Rm}
of
g_{i}
may tend to infinity at or arbitrarily near
{\partial M}
,

(3.3)

|{\rm Rm}_{g_{i}}(x_{i})|\rightarrow\infty,

for some sequence such that
t(x_{i})\rightarrow 0
, (cf.
[
15
]
). Of course it is possible that both of these behaviors occur simultaneously.
We note that a special case of the analysis given in
[
15
]
shows that (
3.3
) does not occur if there are uniform lower and
upper bounds on the potential
u
at
{\partial M}
, i.e. constants
m,M
such that

0<m\leq u_{i}\leq M<\infty\ \ {\rm on}\ \ {\partial M}.

There is one, seemingly natural, condition that can be used to rule out both of these possibilities of degeneration at once. Thus, recall that the
boundary
{\partial M}
is called outer-minimizing in
(M,g)
if for any surface
S\subset M
surrounding
{\partial M}
, i.e.
S
is homologous to
{\partial M}

in
M
, one has

(3.4)

\area_{g}S\geq\area_{g}{\partial M}.

The boundary
{\partial M}
is strictly outer-minimizing if strict inequality holds in (
3.4
), for all
S\neq{\partial M}
.
Note that by the first variational formula for area, (
3.4
) implies (
1.3
),
H\geq 0
at
{\partial M}
. It is obvious but important to
note that the outer-minimizing condition depends on the global structure of the solution
(M,g)
; it cannot be expressed only in terms
of the boundary data
(\gamma,H)
.

The following result was proved by the author in
[
15
]
.

Theorem 3.3
.

Let
(M,g_{i},u_{i})\in\bar{\mathcal{E}}
be a sequence of static vacuum Einstein metrics with boundary data
(\gamma_{i},H_{i})
with

H_{i}\geq 0
,
m\geq 4
, such that

(g_{i},H_{i})\rightarrow(\gamma,H),

in
\widetilde{\mathcal{B}}
. Suppose also that
{\partial M}
is outer-minimizing in
(M,g_{i})
for each
i
:

area_{g_{i}}S\geq area_{g_{i}}{\partial M},

for all
S
surrounding
{\partial M}
.
Then a subsequence converges in
\widetilde{\mathcal{E}}
to a limit static vacuum Einstein metric
(M,g,u)\in\bar{\mathcal{E}}
with boundary data
(\gamma,H)
.

Thus, one has good compactness properties under an outer-minimizing condition.

In light of Theorem 3.3, it might seem beneficial to restrict to static vacuum solutions with (strictly) outer-minimizing boundary. Let then

{\mathcal{E}}^{out}\subset{\mathcal{E}}
be the space of static vacuum solutions for which the boundary is strictly outer-minimizing:

(3.5)

{\rm area}_{g}S>{\rm area}_{g}{\partial M},

for
S\neq{\partial M}
. This is clearly an open condition so that
{\mathcal{E}}^{out}
remains a smooth Banach manifold. The map

(3.6)

\Pi_{B}:{\mathcal{E}}^{out}\rightarrow{\mathcal{B}},

is still well-defined, smooth and Fredholm, of index
0
.

In fact it is not unusual in the literature to modify the definition of the Bartnik mass by replacing the no horizon condition (no minimal
surfaces surrounding the boundary) by an outer-minimizing condition. Thus define

(3.7)

m_{B}^{out}(\Omega,g_{\Omega})=\inf\{m_{ADM}(M,g):(M,g)\in{\mathcal{P}}_{\Omega}^{out}\},

where
{\mathcal{P}}_{\Omega}^{out}
is the space of strictly outer-minimizing extensions of
(\Omega,g_{\Omega})
or equivalently of the boundary data
(\gamma,H)
;
cf.
[
29
]
for a detailed discussion. Similarly, the minimizing and static extension Conjectures I and II are then
rephrased to assert the existence and uniqueness of static vacuum solutions with strictly outer-minimizing boundary. One advantage of
this modified definition is the fundamental estimate of Huisken-Illmanen
[
27
]
,

m_{B}^{out}(\gamma,H)\geq m_{H}(\gamma,H)=\sqrt{\frac{{\rm area}_{g}{\partial M}}{16\pi}}(1-\frac{1}{16\pi}\int_{{\partial M}}H^{2}),

comparing the outer-minimizing Bartnik mass with the Hawking mass of the boundary.

The problem with these reformulations say of Conjecture II is that one needs the strict inequality (
3.5
) to have a satisfactory
manifold structure to the space
{\mathcal{E}}^{out}
of solutions. Working instead with the (weak) outer-minimizing condition leads, at best,
to the structure of a Banach manifold with boundary; this is not suitable to try to apply general existence methods, such as
the method of continuity or the more general degree theory techniques discussed briefly in §2.

On the other hand, in passing to limits of minimizing sequences, (assuming such limits exist), the strict outer-minimizing property is not preserved;
limits can be expected to be only (weakly) outer-minimizing. Thus for a limit minimizer to be an admissible extension, one needs to work with the
(closed) weak outer-minimizing condition. In a similar vein, the map
\Pi_{B}
in (
3.6
) cannot possibly be proper.
One can easily find sequences
(M,g_{i},u_{i})\subset{\mathcal{E}}^{out}
with
(\gamma_{i},H_{i})\rightarrow(\gamma,H)\in{\mathcal{B}}
smoothly, but which
have no limit
(M,g,u)
in
{\mathcal{E}}^{out}
. In fact it is easy to find such sequences of boundaries in a fixed background solution
(M,g,u)
)
with limit only weakly outer-minimizing

\area_{g}S\geq\area_{g}{\partial M}.

Thus, neither the weak or strict outer-minimizing condition seems satisfactory. We are not aware of any way out of this quandary.

Remark 3.4
.

One might raise the same objection with the no-horizon condition itself; if a static vacuum solution had a horizon, (i.e. a minimal
surface surrounding
{\partial M}
), then it could not be an admissible extension and so could not realize the Bartnik mass as in Conjecture I.
One would have to consider only static vacuum solutions without horizons, which would be very awkward. Fortunately,
Proposition 2.1 rules out this situation.

Of course, the main trouble here is that the outer-minimizing property (strict or weak) cannot be phrased in terms of the boundary data
(\gamma,H)
.
There has been some speculation that suitable conditions on the boundary data
(\gamma,H)
would ensure that static vacuum solutions
with such boundary data have strict outer-minimizing boundary; a common suggestion, based on the resolution of the Weyl embedding problem,
is the condition

K_{\gamma}>0\ \ {\rm and}\ \ H>0.

However, the next result shows that this cannot possibly work, at least for
H
small.

Using Theorem 3.3, we prove the following non-existence result. This may be viewed as a stable version of the black hole uniqueness theorem,
as in Proposition 2.1, with
H
small replacing
H=0
.

Theorem 3.5
.

Let
\gamma
be a smooth metric on
S^{2}
such that the Gauss curvature
K_{\gamma}
is a Morse function
in the domain
K_{\gamma}>0
, i.e.
K_{\gamma}
has only non-degenerate critical points when
K_{\gamma}>0
.

Let
\mu(p)=\min|\lambda_{i}(p)|
, where
\lambda_{i}(p)\neq 0
are the eigenvalues of the Hessian
D^{2}K_{\gamma}
at a critical point
p
of
K_{\gamma}
,

K_{\gamma}(p)>0
. Let also
C=||\gamma||_{C^{m,\alpha}}+||H||_{C^{m-1,\alpha}}
, for some
m\geq 4
.

Then there is a constant
H_{0}>0
, depending only on
C
and a positive lower bound for
\mu_{0}=\min_{p}\mu(p)>0
, such that:
for any smooth function
H
satisfying

(3.8)

0<H<H_{0},

pointwise, the Bartnik boundary data
(\gamma,H)
are not the boundary data of a static vacuum Einstein metric
(M,g,u)\in{\mathcal{E}}
. Thus
for such
(\gamma,H)\in{\mathcal{B}}
,

\Pi_{B}^{-1}(\gamma,H)=\emptyset.

We note that at least a large class of boundary data satisfying (
3.8
) above do have positive scalar curvature fill-ins, i.e. are
boundary data of compact domains
(\Omega,g_{\Omega})
with positive scalar curvature.

Proof.

The proof is by contradiction. Thus, let
(\gamma_{i},H_{i})\in{\mathcal{B}}
, (
H_{i}>0
), be a sequence of boundary data
converging in
\widetilde{\mathcal{B}}
to
(\gamma,0)\notin{\mathcal{B}}
and satisfying the assumptions of Theorem 3.5. Suppose there is a sequence of
static vacuum solutions
(M,g_{i},u_{i})
,
u_{i}>0
with Bartnik boundary data
(\gamma_{i},H_{i})
. We then need to derive a
contradiction.

In case the boundary
{\partial M}
is (weakly) outer-minimizing in
(M,g_{i})
for
i
sufficiently large, Theorem 3.5 is proved in
[
12
]
,
based on the discussion in
[
14
]
. We sketch the argument for completeness. Since
{\partial M}
is outer-minimizing, Theorem 3.3 implies
that a subsequence of
(M,g_{i},u_{i})
converges in
\widetilde{\mathcal{E}}
to a limit solution
(M,g,u)\in\widetilde{\mathcal{E}}
of the static vacuum Einstein
equations with
u\geq 0
and
u\rightarrow 1
at infinity. The Bartnik boundary data
of
(M,g,u)
is given by

\lim_{i\rightarrow\infty}(\gamma_{i},H_{i})=(\gamma,0).

By the black hole uniqueness theorem as in Proposition 2.1, the only static vacuum solution
(M,g,u)
with

u\geq 0
,
u\rightarrow 1
at infinity and smooth horizon boundary
H=0
is the Schwarzschild metric with boundary data
(\gamma_{2m},0)
.
Since the Gauss curvature
K_{\gamma_{2m}}\equiv(2m)^{-2}
is not a Morse function, this gives a contradiction. Namely the smooth convergence
of
(M,g_{i},u_{i})
up to the boundary implies that
K_{i}
is smoothly close to the constant
(2m)^{-2}
; however, the eigenvalues of the
Hessian
D^{2}K_{i}
are bounded away from
0
, say at
\max K_{i}
.

Now in general, consider a static vacuum solution
(M,g,u)
(say equal to some
(M,g_{i},u_{i})
above) with boundary data
(\gamma,H)
with

H>0
but close to
0
. Let
\Sigma
be the outer-minimizing hull of
{\partial M}
in
M
, cf.
[
27
]
. This is the embedded surface surrounding

{\partial M}
in
(M,g)
of smallest area. Note first that by Proposition 2.1,
\Sigma\subset M
cannot be disjoint from
{\partial M}
, so

\Sigma\cap{\partial M}\neq\emptyset.

Let
U\subset{\partial M}
be the interior of
{\partial M}\cap\Sigma
and let
V=\Sigma\setminus\bar{U}\subset\Sigma
. Then (again by Proposition 2.1),
both
U
and
V
are non-empty open sets; of course, neither
U
nor
V
is assumed to be connected. By the outer-minimizing property, one has

H=0\ \ {\rm on}\ \ V.

The surface
\Sigma
is
C^{1,1}
at the seam or corner
\partial U\cap\partial V
, smooth away from
\partial U\cap\partial V
and has non-negative
distributional mean curvature
H
across
\partial U\cap\partial V
, cf.
[
27
]
.

Now given
(M,g_{i},u_{i})
as above, consider the new sequence of static vacuum solutions
(\widehat{M}_{i},g_{i},u_{i})
where

\widehat{M}_{i}\subset M
is the region of
M
exterior to
\Sigma_{i}
, so
\partial\widehat{M}_{i}=\Sigma_{i}
. The analysis to follow will be to show that
that the same arguments as before where
{\partial M}_{i}
was outer-minimizing can be applied to the new sequence
(\widehat{M}_{i},g_{i},u_{i})
to
again obtain a contradiction.

Let
\gamma_{U_{i}}=g_{i}|_{U_{i}}
and
\gamma_{V_{i}}=g|_{V_{i}}
. The metric
\gamma_{U_{i}}
is uniformly controlled in
C^{m,\alpha}
by hypothesis,
since
U_{i}\subset{\partial M}_{i}
. Now assume first, for simplicity, that the sequence
(V_{i},\gamma_{V_{i}})
is uniformly bounded in
C^{4,\alpha}

(modulo diffeomorphisms), so that the Gauss curvature
K_{i}
of
(V_{i},\gamma_{V_{i}})
is uniformly bounded in
C^{2,\alpha}
. The same
then holds for the induced metric
\widehat{\gamma}_{i}
on
\partial\widehat{M}_{i}
, away from the seam or corner
\partial V_{i}\cap\partial U_{i}\subset\Sigma_{i}
.
Thus away from the corners, one has
H_{i}\rightarrow 0
in
C^{3,\alpha}
with
\widehat{\gamma}_{i}
bounded in
C^{4,\alpha}
.
One may then still apply the proof of Theorem 3.3 in this setting to conclude that the curvature
{\rm Rm}_{g_{i}}
and its derivatives
up to order
2+\alpha
are uniformly bounded in
\widehat{M}_{i}
, up to the boundary
\partial M_{i}
. Thus, in a subsequence,
(\widehat{M}_{i},\widehat{g}_{i},\widehat{u}_{i})

converges in
C^{4,\alpha}
away from corners to a round Schwarzschild metric, cf. also the proof of Proposition 2.1 and Remark 2.2. In
particular, this convergence holds in
U_{i}\subset{\partial M}_{i}
and so gives the same contradiction as above - even if components of
U_{i}

shrink to points for instance.

Now the surfaces
(V_{i},\gamma_{V_{i}})
are stable minimal surfaces in
(M,g_{i})
. In the Appendix, we prove a regularity result for such stable
minimal surfaces in static vacuum Einstein manifolds which implies that if both the scalar curvature
s_{V_{i}}
of
(V_{i},\gamma_{V_{i}})
and the
full Riemann curvature
{\rm Rm}
of
(M,g_{i})
are uniformly bounded along
V_{i}
,

(3.9)

|s_{V_{i}}|+|{\rm Rm}_{g_{i}}|\leq C

on
V_{i}
, then in fact
(V_{i},\gamma_{V_{i}})
is uniformly bounded in
C^{\infty}
modulo diffeomorphisms. Note this statement is standard for
the trivial static vacuum Einstein metric
\mathbb{R}^{3}
. It also follows easily in regions of
V_{i}
where
t_{i}=dist_{g_{i}}({\partial M}_{i},\cdot)
is bounded
away from
0
, by the estimates (
3.1
). Thus, the proof follows just as above if (
3.9
) holds, and so it suffices to
prove (
3.9
).

We prove (
3.9
) by a blow-up argument. The discussion below applies to each
(\widehat{M}_{i},g_{i},u_{i})
but we drop the index
i
from the
notation; the estimates below are then understood to hold uniformly, for all
i
large.

To begin, the
2^{\rm nd}
variation of area gives

(3.10)

\int_{\Sigma}(|df|^{2}+(N(H)+H^{2})f^{2})dv_{\gamma_{\Sigma}}\geq 0,

and the normal variation of
H
is given by
N(H)=-|A|^{2}-{\rm Ric}(N,N)
. By the Hamiltonian constraint (Gauss) equation (
5.1
),

|A|^{2}-H^{2}+s_{\gamma}=-2{\rm Ric}(N,N)
,
so that

N(H)=-{\tfrac{1}{2}}(|A|^{2}+H^{2}-s_{\gamma_{\Sigma}}).

Choosing
f=1
in (
3.10
) thus gives the bound

\int_{\Sigma}|A|^{2}\leq\int_{\Sigma}s_{\gamma_{\Sigma}}+H^{2}=4\pi\chi(\Sigma)+\int_{\Sigma}H^{2}.

Since
H^{2}\rightarrow 0
uniformly, it follows that one has the uniform bound

(3.11)

\int_{\Sigma}|A|^{2}\leq 8\pi+1.

Note this bound is scale invariant. Next, again from the Gauss equation, cf. (
5.3
), one has

|A|^{2}-H^{2}+s_{\gamma}=2u^{-1}(\Delta u+HN(u)).

Integrating over
\Sigma
and applying divergence formula gives

\int_{\Sigma}|d^{T}\nu|^{2}\leq 8\pi-\int_{\Sigma}HN(\nu),

where
d^{T}\nu
is the tangential gradient of
\nu
on
\Sigma
. On the domain
V\subset\Sigma
,
H=0
while on the locally
outer-minimizing complement
U\subset{\partial M}
,
HN(\nu)
converges to a well-defined limit by Theorem 3.3, cf.
[
15
]

for details. It follows that

(3.12)

\int_{\Sigma}|d^{T}\nu|^{2}\leq C,

for some uniform
C
, independent of
i
.

Next, for the blow-up argument, choose points
p_{i}\in\Sigma_{i}
realizing the maximum of

(3.13)

Q_{i}(x)=|s_{\gamma_{V_{i}}}(x)|+|{\rm Rm}_{g_{i}}(x)|,

x\in\Sigma_{i}
. Without loss of generality, assuming
Q_{i}(p_{i})\rightarrow\infty
, we rescale the metrics
g_{i}
(and correspondingly
\gamma_{V_{i}}
) by

\bar{g}_{i}=Q_{i}(p_{i})g_{i},

so that

(3.14)

\bar{Q}_{i}\leq 1\ \ {\rm and}\ \ \bar{Q}_{i}(p_{i})=1,

on
\Sigma_{i}
. Clearly one has
p_{i}\in V_{i}
. We also renormalize
u
to
\bar{u}_{i}=u_{i}/u(p_{i})
. Again by (the proof of)
Theorem 3.3,
|{\rm Rm}_{\bar{g}_{i}}|
is uniformly bounded on
(\widehat{M}_{i},\bar{g}_{i})
.

To prove that a subsequence
(\widehat{M}_{i},\bar{g}_{i},p_{i})
converges modulo diffeomorphisms, (i.e. in the Cheeger-Gromov sense),
in
C^{1,\alpha}
, we need to rule out the possibility of collapse of the metrics, i.e. the collapse of the volume of unit balls at
p_{i}
.

Lemma 3.6
.

(Non-collapse). For the rescaled sequence
(\widehat{M}_{i},\bar{g}_{i},p_{i})
, there is a uniform lower bound

(3.15)

{\rm vol}_{\bar{g}_{i}}B_{p_{i}}(1)\geq v_{0}>0.

Similarly, the boundary geometry
(\Sigma_{i},\bar{\gamma}_{i},p_{i})
does not collapse:

(3.16)

{\rm area}_{\bar{\gamma}_{i}}D_{p_{i}}(1)\geq a_{0}>0.

Proof.

First, as discussed in the Appendix, cf. (
5.5
), for any
(M,g,u)\in{\mathcal{E}}
, the conformally equivalent metric
\widetilde{g}=u^{2}g

has positive Ricci curvature. The metric
\widetilde{g}
is AF and hence by the Bishop-Gromov volume comparison theorem, there is a
uniform scale-invariant lower bound on the volume of
r
-balls in
(M,\widetilde{g})
:

\frac{{\rm vol}_{\widetilde{g}}B(r)}{r^{3}}\geq v_{0}>0.

Now apply this to the rescaled metrics
(\widehat{M}_{i},\bar{g}_{i},\bar{u}_{i})
based at
p_{i}
. Since
\bar{u}_{i}
is uniformly bounded
above and below in
(B_{p_{i}}(1),\bar{g}_{i})
, (cf. (
5.8
)), the metrics
\bar{g}_{i}
and
\widetilde{g}_{i}
are uniformly quasi-isometric in this region.
This proves (
3.15
).

To see that the boundary geometry also cannot collapse, recall that
(\Sigma_{i},\bar{\gamma}_{i})
has uniformly bounded (Gauss)
curvature. If the geometry is collapsing at
p_{i}
, then the geometry of a large geodesic disc
D_{p_{i}}(R)
about
p_{i}

in
(\Sigma_{i},\bar{\gamma}_{i})
is that of a long cylinder which has a foliation by short (geodesic) loops. Since the ambient curvature is
uniformly bounded, if
\ell
denotes the length of the short loops in
(\Sigma_{i},\bar{\gamma}_{i})
near
p_{i}
, then
|A|\sim\ell^{-1}
and hence

\int_{I}|A|^{2}dv_{\gamma}\sim\int_{I}\ell\ell^{-2}\sim\ell^{-1}.

Since
A
is bounded in
L^{2}
by (
3.11
) and since (
3.11
) is scale-invariant, this gives a uniform lower bound on
\ell
,
proving (
3.16
).

∎

The discussion above proves that, after passing to a subsequence, the sequence
(\widehat{M}_{i},\bar{g}_{i},\bar{u}_{i},p_{i})
converges in the
pointed
C^{1,\alpha}
topology, modulo diffeomorphisms, to a complete non-compact limit
(M_{\infty},\bar{g}_{\infty},\bar{u}_{\infty},p)
, with
complete, non-compact boundary
(S,\gamma_{S},\bar{u}_{\infty},p)
. The rest of the argument to follow is to prove that the limit is flat,
with flat boundary
S
. By results in the Appendix, cf. Proposition 5.2, the convergence to the limit is in the strong
C^{2}
topology,
giving a contradiction to the continuity of
Q
in (
3.14
) under
C^{2}
convergence. This will complete the proof of Theorem 3.5.

All the discussion to follow takes place on the limit
M_{\infty}
and
S
so we drop the bar and
\infty
below to simplify the notation.

On the blow-up limit boundary
S
, we have
A
and
d\nu
are in
L^{2}(S,\gamma_{S})
, by (
3.11
) and (
3.12
). Moreover, the
ambient curvature
|{\rm Rm}|
is uniformly bounded along
S
. As above,
D(r)
denotes a geodesic
r
-disc in
(S,\gamma_{S})
about
p
.

Lemma 3.7
.

The limit
(S,\gamma_{S})
has at most quadratic area growth, i.e.

(3.17)

{\rm area}_{\gamma_{S}}D(r)\leq Vr^{2},

for some
V<\infty
.

Proof.

On
(S,\gamma_{S})
, we have

(3.18)

|A|^{2}+s_{\gamma_{S}}=2u^{-1}\Delta u.

Let
v(r)
be the length of the boundary
S(r)=\partial D(r)
. Integrating (
3.18
) over
D(r)
and applying the Gauss Bonnet
and divergence theorems gives

4\pi-2v^{\prime}(r)=-\int_{D(r)}|A|^{2}+2\int_{D(r)}|d^{T}\nu|^{2}+2\int_{S(r)}\partial_{r}\nu.

By (
3.11
), this gives

v^{\prime}(r)\leq C+\int_{S(r)}|d^{T}\nu|.

On intervals where
v^{\prime}(r)\leq C
, (
3.17
) holds (by integration), so assume only

v^{\prime}(r)\leq C\int_{S(r)}|d^{T}\nu|.

By the Hölder inequality,
\int_{S(r)}|d^{T}\nu|\leq(\int_{S(r)}|d^{T}\nu|^{2})^{1/2}v(r)^{1/2}
, so that

((\sqrt{v^{\prime}(r)})^{\prime})^{2}\leq C\int_{S(r)}|d^{T}\nu|^{2}
. Let
q(r)=\sqrt{v^{\prime}(r)}
, so that via (
3.12
) we obtain

\int_{0}^{r}(q^{\prime})^{2}\leq C.

Then
q(r)\leq\int_{0}^{r}q^{\prime}(r)\leq(\int_{0}^{r}(q^{\prime}(r)^{2})^{1/2}r^{1/2}\leq Cr^{1/2}
, so that again
v(r)\leq Cr
.
This proves the result.

∎

Lemma 3.8
.

The surface
(S,\gamma_{S})\subset(M,g)
is AF in the weak sense that

Q(x_{k})\rightarrow 0,

on any sequence
x_{k}\rightarrow\infty
in
S
.

Proof.

Let
x_{k}
be any divergent sequence in
S
and consider the geometry of pointed manifolds
(S,\gamma_{S},x_{k})
in regions about
x_{k}
.
By Lemma 3.6, this sequence does not collapse, and so has a
C^{1,\alpha}
limit (in a subsequence). Also,
A\rightarrow 0
and
d\nu\rightarrow 0
in

L_{loc}^{2}
, by (
3.11
) and (
3.12
). By the regularity estimates in the Appendix,
A
is uniformly bounded in
L^{\infty}
,
as is
u\in L_{loc}^{2,p}
on the boundary
S
and in
M
, cf. (
5.7
), (
5.8
). Hence
N(u)
is also uniformly bounded.
By the constraint equation (
5.4
),

\delta A-A(d\nu)=u^{-1}dN(u).

The term
A(d\nu)\rightarrow 0
in
L_{loc}^{2}
, while the term
\delta A\rightarrow 0
in
L_{loc}^{-1,2}
. It follows that
dN(u)\rightarrow 0
in
L_{loc}^{-1,2}

and hence
N(u)\rightarrow const
weakly in
L_{loc}^{2}
. Since
N(u)
is also uniformly bounded in
L^{\infty}
,
N(u)\rightarrow const
strongly in

L_{loc}^{2}
. It follows that on the limit (which is smooth by regularity results from Appendix), one has the limit data

A=0,\ \ u=const,\ \ N(u)=const.

Similarly,
NN(u)=0
on the limit, etc. Since
\Delta_{g}u=0
on
M
, unique continuation for the Laplacian implies that
D^{2}u=0

on
(M,g)
so that
u
is an affine function and the metric
g
is flat. Since
A=0
, the boundary metric
\gamma
is also flat.

It follows that
Q\equiv 0
on the limit. By the strong convergence (Proposition 5.2) in the Appendix, it follows that

,
as claimed.

∎

It follows, by repeated use of Lemma 3.8 at various blow-down scales that all tangent cones at infinity of
(S,\gamma_{S})
are flat, with flat
ambient geometry, i.e.
(D(r),\frac{1}{r^{2}}\gamma_{S})
converges to a flat metric away from the origin
p
and similarly with
g_{S}
in
place of
\gamma_{S}
. Now apply the stability inequality (
3.10
) on the complete
(S,\gamma_{S})
. By the well-known log-cutoff trick,
(cf.
[
24
]
for example) one has

\int_{S}|A|^{2}-s_{\gamma_{S}}\leq 0,

so that by the Gauss-Bonnet theorem,

(3.19)

\int_{S}|A|^{2}\leq\int_{S}s_{\gamma}=2\pi-\lim_{r\rightarrow\infty}v^{\prime}(r).

This implies first that
\lim_{r\rightarrow\infty}\frac{v(r)}{r}\leq 2\pi
. Suppose strict inequality holds,
\lim_{r\rightarrow\infty}\frac{v(r)}{r}<2\pi
. The tangent
cone at infinity
C
is
S
is then a flat cone, of cone angle
\alpha<2\pi
, which bounds a flat static vacuum solution. By the scale-invariant
apriori estimates (
3.1
), the limit static vacuum solution is smooth away from the boundary cone
C
, so cannot be of the form

C\times\mathbb{R}^{+}
, which is singular in the interior: any singularity of the cone
C
cannot propagate into the exterior region. On the
other hand, if
C
is a singular cone,
\alpha<2\pi
, in an ambient smooth flat geometry, then one has
\int_{C}|A|^{2}=\infty
, again a
contradiction. (Alternately, such cones are not outer-minimizing in the ambient geometry).

It follows that

(3.20)

\lim_{r\rightarrow\infty}\frac{v(r)}{r}=2\pi,

and hence by (
3.19
),

\int_{S}|A|^{2}=0,

so that
A=0
on
S
. The potential equation (
3.18
) then becomes

K=u^{-1}\Delta u.

Integrating this over
D(r)
and taking the limit gives

\lim_{r\rightarrow\infty}[\int_{D(r)}|d^{T}\nu|^{2}+\int_{S(r)}\partial_{r}\nu]=0,

since
\lim_{r\rightarrow\infty}\int_{D(r)}K=0
. As in the proof of Lemma 3.7, this implies

\int_{S}|d^{T}\nu|^{2}=0,

so that
u=const
on
S
. Thus the Cauchy data
(\gamma,A,u,N(u))
for the static vacuum Einstein equations equal that of a flat solution
with affine potential. By unique continuation as in the proof of Lemma 3.8, it follows that the limit is flat, with flat boundary, so that

Q=0
on
S
. This again contradicts the strong convergence to the limit, cf. Proposition 5.2.

∎

is a Morse function is a sufficient but we don’t believe a necessary condition. It is natural to conjecture
that Theorem 3.5 holds for all

sufficiently small depending only on lower and upper bounds for the distance of

to a round metric in say

.

It follows in particular that the mass

cannot be realized by boundary data

with

Remark 3.9
.

Theorem 3.5 should be contrasted with the following result, proved in

; the proof also strongly uses the outer-minimizing property:
for any given boundary data
(\gamma,H)\in{\mathcal{B}}

such that

In this Appendix, we first collect several standard facts regarding static vacuum Einstein metrics. Following this, we prove a regularity result for
minimal surfaces in static vacuum spaces used in the proof of Theorem 3.5.

On a Riemannian manifold
(M,g)
with (local) boundary
({\partial M},\gamma)
the Gauss and Gauss-Codazzi equations (also known as the Hamiltonian and
momentum constraint equations) are:

(5.1)

|A|^{2}-H^{2}+s_{\gamma}=s_{g}-2{\rm Ric}(N,N),

(5.2)

\delta A+dH=-{\rm Ric}(N,\cdot),

where
N
is the unit normal to
{\partial M}
and
\delta
is the divergence operator on
{\partial M}
. For static vacuum Einstein metrics,
s_{g}=0
and

{\rm Ric}(N,N)=u^{-1}NN(u)
. Using the standard relation
\Delta_{g}v=NN(v)+HN(v)+\Delta_{{\partial M}}v
, (
5.1
) gives

(5.3)

2u^{-1}(\Delta_{{\partial M}}u+HN(u))=|A|^{2}-H^{2}+s_{\gamma}.

Similarly, the static vacuum equations give
{\rm Ric}(N,\cdot)=u^{-1}D^{2}u(N,\cdot)=u^{-1}(dN(u)-A(du))
. It follows that

(5.4)

\delta(uA)+udH=-dN(u).

or equivalently

\delta(uA-N(u)\gamma)=-udH.

Next, consider the conformally related metric
\widetilde{g}=u^{2}g
. Standard formulas for conformal change of metric show that the static vacuum Einstein
equations (
1.4
) are equivalent to the system

(5.5)

{\rm Ric}_{\widetilde{g}}=2d\nu\cdot d\nu,\ \ \Delta_{\widetilde{g}}\nu=0,

where
\nu=\log u
. Two advantages of these equations are that the Ricci curvature
{\rm Ric}_{\widetilde{g}}
is positive, and second, the Ricci curvature is lower
order (1st order) in
\nu
, which is not the case for (
1.4
).

Next we turn to the regularity properties of local minimal surfaces
(V,\gamma)
in static vacuum Einstein metrics
(M,g,u)
. To begin, as in (
3.9
),
assume a bound on
Q
in (
3.13
), so

(5.6)

|s_{\gamma}|+|{\rm Rm}_{g}|\leq C,

on
(V,\gamma)
.

We first discuss what uniform control the bound (
5.6
) gives on the geometry of
(M,g)
and
(V,\gamma)
.
It follows from the Cheeger-Gromov compactness theorem (or more simply from the uniformization theorem in 2 dimensions) that

\gamma
is controlled, in harmonic coordinates, in
C^{1,\alpha}
. Similarly,
g
is also controlled in
C^{1,\alpha}
, cf. also
[
13
]
. (In case the

Since

5.1
) that
A
is uniformly bounded in

,

|A|\leq C,\ \ {\rm on}\ \ V.

Next, assume (as in §3) that

is normalized at a base point

so that

. It then follows from (
5.3
) and elliptic regularity
for the Laplacian on

that

is uniformly bounded in
L_{loc}^{2,p}
, for any

(5.7)

|u|_{L_{loc}^{2,p}(V)}\leq C.

Since
\Delta_{g}u=0
on
(M,g)
, elliptic regularity for the Laplacian on

implies that

is

and so

V
. Hence

is bounded in

and so in
C^{\alpha}
on
V
. Finally, it then follows from the momentum constraint (

)
and elliptic regularity for the

The main step in proving regularity is the following result. Recall
\widetilde{g}=u^{2}g

Lemma 5.1

The system

(5.10)

It suffices to prove the result for the linearized system and for deformations
