Proof of a conjecture of Bourn and Willenbring concerning a family of palindromic polynomials
Rebecca Bourn, William Q. Erickson

Abstract
. We prove a conjecture of Bourn and Willenbring (2020) regarding the palindromicity and unimodality of certain polynomials N n ​ ( t ) N_{n}(t) . These polynomials arise as the numerators of generating functions in the context of the one-dimensional earth mover’s distance (EMD). Our proof reveals a connection to recent work by Defant et al. (2023) on the Wiener index of minuscule lattices, which we reinterpret combinatorially to obtain explicit formulas for the coefficients of N n ​ ( t ) N_{n}(t) and for the expected value of the EMD.

1.
Introduction

1.1.
Background

In
[
2
]
, Bourn and Willenbring derive a recursive formula for the expected value of the one-dimensional earth mover’s distance (EMD).
For the purposes of this paper, it is enough to know that the EMD (also called the first Wasserstein distance) measures the distance between two histograms (or more generally, probability distributions), by computing the minimum amount of “work” required to transform one into the other (see
[
14
]
).
The EMD can also be viewed as the solution to the classical
transportation problem
(often named after various combinations of Hitchcock, Monge, Kantorovich, and Koopmans), and has an ever-widening range of important applications in mathematics along with the physical and social sciences.
We recommend Villani’s monumental reference
[
16
]
for further reading.

While the main result in
[
2
]
extends to probability distributions on the set
[n]\coloneqq\{1,\ldots,n\}
, their methodology is decidedly combinatorial.
In particular, rather than probability distributions, the authors consider discrete histograms with
n
bins and
s
data points — in other words, the set of (weak) integer
compositions
of
s
into
n
parts, denoted by

\mathcal{C}(s,n)\coloneqq\Big\{(\alpha_{1},\ldots,\alpha_{n})\in(\mathbb{Z}_{\geq 0})^{n}\;\Big|\;\textstyle\sum_{i}\alpha_{i}=s\Big\}.

Then, temporarily allowing ordered pairs of compositions with different numbers of parts (
p
and
q
), they define a bivariate generating function

(1.1)

H_{pq}(z,t)\coloneqq\sum_{s=0}^{\infty}\Bigg(\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\alpha\in\mathcal{C}(s,p),\\
\beta\in\mathcal{C}(s,q)\phantom{,}\end{subarray}}z^{{\rm EMD}(\alpha,\beta)}\Bigg)t^{s}

which tracks the EMD values while summing over all possible numbers
s
of data points.
Upon differentiating with respect to
z
and evaluating at
z=1
, they obtain another generating function in
t
, which is the subject of the present paper:

\displaystyle H^{\prime}_{pq}(t)

\displaystyle\coloneqq\frac{\partial}{\partial z}H_{pq}(z,t)\bigg|_{z=1}

(1.2)

\displaystyle=\sum_{s=0}^{\infty}\Bigg(\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\alpha\in\mathcal{C}(s,p),\\
\beta\in\mathcal{C}(s,q)\phantom{,}\end{subarray}}\hskip-8.61108pt{\rm EMD}(\alpha,\beta)\Bigg)t^{s}.

It is then shown
[
2
]
*Prop. 6 that this generating function has the rational form

H^{\prime}_{pq}(t)=\frac{N_{pq}(t)}{(1-t)^{p+q}},

where
N_{pq}(t)
is a recursively defined polynomial (see Section
2.1
below).

1.2.
Main result

In practice, of course, one is interested in the special case
p=q
, in which case we replace both parameters by
n
, and write
H^{\prime}_{n}(t)\coloneqq H^{\prime}_{nn}(t)
and
N_{n}(t)\coloneqq N_{nn}(t)
.
The main result in this paper is a proof of the conjecture
[
2
]
*Conj. 1:

Conjecture 1
.

For all positive integers
n
, the polynomial
N_{n}(t)
is palindromic and unimodal.

Given a polynomial
f(t)=\sum_{k=a}^{b}f_{k}t^{k}
with total degree
d\coloneqq a+b
(assuming
f_{a}\neq 0
and
f_{b}\neq 0
), we say that
f(t)
is
palindromic
if
f_{k}=f_{d-k}
for all
a\leq k\leq b
.
This is equivalent to the condition
f(t)=t^{d}f(1/t)
.
Moreover,
f(t)
is said to be
unimodal
if

f_{a}\leq f_{a+1}\leq\cdots\leq f_{m}\geq\cdots\geq f_{b-1}\geq f_{b}

for some index
m
.
The key to our proof of palindromicity of the polynomials
N_{n}(t)
is a combinatorial interpretation of the recursion defining
N_{pq}(t)
, in terms of the symmetric difference of pairs of Young diagrams bounded by certain rectangles (see Theorem
3.1
).
The palindromic property of
N_{n}(t)
then becomes clear from the fact that the size of the symmetric difference is unchanged by reflecting both diagrams.

1.3.
Connections to other work

In Section
4
we point out a surprising connection to a recent paper by Defant et al.
[
4
]
.
In particular, the distance defined on a minuscule lattice of Type A measures the symmetric difference between Young diagrams, and the
Wiener index
is the sum of all these pairwise distances.
By aligning this fact with our combinatorial view in Theorem
3.1
, we realize that the Wiener index formula in
[
4
]
yields the coefficients of
N_{n}(t)
, giving an explicit description of these once-mysterious polynomials. (See Theorem
4.1
.)
This leads to a straightforward proof (Corollary
4.2
) of the unimodality of
N_{n}(t)
, thereby settling Conjecture
1
.

As it turns out, the Wiener index in
[
4
]
gives the explicit coefficient formula not only for the numerator
N_{n}(t)
, but also for the series expansion
H^{\prime}_{n}(t)
.
This is sufficient to close the recursion in
[
2
]
for the expected value of the one-dimensional EMD on
\mathcal{C}(s,n)\times\mathcal{C}(s,n)
, and the closed form is surprisingly simple (see Theorem
4.3
):

(1.3)

\mathbb{E}\Big[{\rm EMD}(\alpha,\beta)\Big]=\frac{s(n-1)}{4s+4n-2}\cdot\frac{\binom{2s+2n}{2s+1}}{\binom{s+n-1}{s}^{2}}.

We emphasize that this result complements two similar extensions of the problem from the original paper
[
2
]
.
First, the authors of
[
10
]
derive a non-recursive formula for the expected value of the EMD, but on pairs of probability distributions rather than discrete histograms. (See our Remark
4.4
, where we recover this result by taking a single limit.)
Second, the authors of
[
8
]
present a non-recursive approach in the discrete setting, which is less direct than (
1.3
) but also more flexible.
Specifically, their formula gives the entries of a certain matrix, which (upon taking the trace of its product with an arbitrary Monge cost matrix
C
) yields the expected value of the modified EMD determined by
C
.

1.4.
Open problems

In Section
5
we conclude with two conjectures of our own.
First, it seems that the values
N_{n}(1)
are sums of symmetric differences in a quite different context, previously studied in
[
12
]
.
We are especially interested in finding a bijective proof of this conjecture.
Second, we conjecture that
N_{n}(t)
has only real roots, for any
n
.
(At the time of submission, these conjectures have been proved in the preprint
[
5
]
.)

2.
Combinatorial preliminaries

2.1.
Recursive definition of
N_{pq}(t)

Recall from (
1.1
) the generating function
H_{pq}(z,t)
, and consider the specialization evaluated at
z=1
:

(2.1)

H_{pq}(1,t)=\sum_{s=0}^{\infty}|\mathcal{C}(s,p)|\cdot|\mathcal{C}(s,q)|\cdot t^{s}.

In
[
2
]
this is rewritten in the rational form

H_{pq}(1,t)=\frac{W_{pq}(t)}{(1-t)^{p+q-1}},

where the coefficients of the numerator are well known to be given by

(2.2)

W_{pq}(t)=\sum_{k=0}^{\min\{p,q\}-1}\binom{p-1}{k}\binom{q-1}{k}t^{k}.

By first proving a recursion for
H_{pq}(t)
and then manipulating generating functions, the authors of
[
2
]
derive the following recursive definition of
N_{pq}\coloneqq N_{pq}(t)
in their equation (4.3):

(2.3)

N_{pq}=N_{p-1,q}+N_{p,q-1}-(1-t)N_{p-1,q-1}+|p-q|\cdot t\,W_{pq},

with initial values
N_{0,q}=N_{p,0}=N_{1,1}=0
.
Below we list the polynomials
N_{n}(t)\coloneqq N_{nn}(t)
for the first few values of
n
:

\displaystyle N_{1}(t)

\displaystyle=0,

\displaystyle N_{2}(t)

\displaystyle=2t,

\displaystyle N_{3}(t)

\displaystyle=8t+8t^{2},

\displaystyle N_{4}(t)

\displaystyle=20t+56t^{2}+20t^{3},

\displaystyle N_{5}(t)

\displaystyle=40t+216t^{2}+216t^{3}+40t^{4},

\displaystyle N_{6}(t)

\displaystyle=70t+616t^{2}+1188t^{3}+616t^{4}+70t^{5},

\displaystyle N_{7}(t)

\displaystyle=112t+1456t^{2}+4576t^{3}+4576t^{4}+1456t^{5}+112t^{6},

\displaystyle N_{8}(t)

\displaystyle=168t+3024t^{2}+14040t^{3}+22880t^{4}+14040t^{5}+3024t^{6}+168t^{7}.

Remark 2.1
.

In
[
7
]
*eqn. ​(1.4.4), the series
H_{pq}(1,t)
is shown to be the Hilbert series of the first Wallach representation of the Lie algebra
\mathfrak{su}(p,q)
.
It can also be viewed as the Hilbert series of the determinantal variety consisting of
p\times q
matrices of rank at most 1; or again, as the Hilbert series of the Stanley–Reisner ring of the order complex
\Delta_{pq}
on the poset
[p]\times[q]
.
In this last context, the coefficient of
t^{k}
in the numerator
W_{pq}(t)
equals the number of facets of
\Delta_{pq}
whose restrictions have cardinality
k
; equivalently, this is the number of lattice paths from
(1,1)
to
(p,q)
containing exactly
k
“turns” from the north to the east.
This is a special case of lattice path enumeration as detailed in
[
11
]
*Prop. 28 and Fig. 8, for example.
See also
[
1
]
, which introduced these methods in the context of the harmonic oscillator.

partition

of
\lambda

\lambda_{1}
the
width
of
\lambda

\lambda

For nonnegative integers
a,b
, let

{\rm Par}(a\times b)\coloneqq\Big\{\text{partitions $\lambda$}\;\Big|\;\text{$\lambda$ has length $\leq a$ and width $\leq b$}\Big\}.

It is convenient to regard
{\rm Par}(a\times b)
as the set of Young diagrams fitting inside a rectangle with
a
rows and
b
columns.
The number of such partitions is well known
[
15
]
*Prop. 1.2.1 to be given by the binomial coefficient

(2.4)

|{\rm Par}(a\times b)|=\binom{a+b}{a}.

Clearly
\lambda\mapsto\lambda^{\prime}
is a bijection between
{\rm Par}(a\times b)
and
{\rm Par}(b\times a)
.
Note that
{\rm Par}(0\times b)={\rm Par}(a\times 0)=\{\varnothing\}
, where
\varnothing
is the empty Young diagram.

Given two Young diagrams
\lambda,\mu
, their
symmetric difference
is

\lambda\ominus\mu\coloneqq(\lambda\cup\mu)\setminus(\lambda\cap\mu),

where as usual
\lambda\cup\mu
denotes the set of boxes occurring in either diagram, and
\lambda\cap\mu
denotes the set of boxes occurring in both diagrams.
Hence
\lambda\ominus\mu
(often written as
\lambda\triangle\mu
) is the set of boxes which occur in exactly one of the two diagrams.

Lemma 2.2
.

We have
{\rm S}_{{}_{\ominus\!}}(a,b\mid c,d)={\rm S}_{{}_{\ominus\!}}(b,a\mid d,c)
.

Proof.

Recalling the bijection
\lambda\mapsto\lambda^{\prime}
, we see that
{\rm S}_{{}_{\ominus\!}}(b,a\mid d,c)
is obtained from (
2.5
) by replacing
\lambda
with
\lambda^{\prime}
and
\mu
with
\mu^{\prime}
.
Since
\lambda^{\prime}\ominus\mu^{\prime}
is just the set of boxes obtained by reflecting the set
\lambda\ominus\mu
across the main diagonal, we have
|\lambda\ominus\mu|=|\lambda^{\prime}\ominus\mu^{\prime}|
.
∎

Lemma 2.3
.

For positive integers
k,\ell,m
, we have

(2.6)

\displaystyle\begin{split}{\rm S}_{{}_{\ominus\!}}(k,\ell\mid k,m)&={\rm S}_{{}_{\ominus\!}}(k,\ell-1\mid k,m)+{\rm S}_{{}_{\ominus\!}}(k,\ell\mid k,m-1)-{\rm S}_{{}_{\ominus\!}}(k,\ell-1\mid k,m-1)\\
&\hskip 10.0pt+{\rm S}_{{}_{\ominus\!}}(k-1,\ell\mid k-1,m)\\
&\hskip 20.0pt+|\ell-m|\cdot|{\rm Par}((k-1)\times\ell)|\cdot|{\rm Par}((k-1)\times m)|.\end{split}

Proof.

This can be seen by the inclusion–exclusion principle, as follows.
We claim that

(2.7)

\sum_{\mathclap{\begin{subarray}{c}(\lambda,\mu)\\
\in\>{\rm Par}(k\times\ell)\times{\rm Par}(k\times m)\setminus Q\end{subarray}}}|\lambda\ominus\mu|=\text{first line of the right-hand side of~\eqref{lemma equation},}

where
Q
is the subset containing those pairs
(\lambda,\mu)
such that
\lambda
has
\ell
nonempty columns and
\mu
has
m
nonempty columns.
It is clear that any pair
(\lambda,\mu)\in Q
is not counted in the first line of (
2.6
); to prove the claim (
2.7
), we observe that each pair
(\lambda,\mu)\in{\rm Par}(k\times(\ell-1))\times{\rm Par}(k\times(m-1))
is counted in
both
of the first two terms, which we correct by subtracting the third term.

Now we claim that the last two lines on the right-hand side of (
2.6
) equal

(2.8)

\sum_{\mathclap{(\lambda,\mu)\in Q}}|\lambda\ominus\mu|.

We start with the third line.
Note that
(\lambda,\mu)\in Q
if and only if
\lambda
has width
\ell
and
\mu
has width
m
.
Hence each pair
(\lambda,\mu)\in Q
, if we restrict our attention to the top row of each diagram, contributes
|\ell-m|
to (
2.8
).
Moreover, we have

|Q|=|{\rm Par}((k-1)\times\ell)|\cdot|{\rm Par}((k-1)\times m)|,

since each
(\lambda,\mu)\in Q
is determined by the unique pair
(\hat{\lambda},\hat{\mu})
where

\displaystyle\hat{\lambda}

\displaystyle\in{\rm Par}((k-1)\times\ell)\text{ is the complement of the top row in $\lambda$,}

\displaystyle\hat{\mu}

\displaystyle\in{\rm Par}((k-1)\times m)\text{ is the complement of the top row in $\mu$.}

Hence we have

(2.9)

\sum_{\mathclap{(\lambda,\mu)\in Q}}|\lambda\ominus\mu|=\underbrace{|\ell-m|\cdot|Q|}_{\text{third line of~\eqref{lemma equation}}}+\underbrace{\sum_{(\lambda,\mu)\in Q}\hskip-4.30554pt|\hat{\lambda}\ominus\hat{\mu}|.}_{\text{second line of~\eqref{lemma equation}}}

The result (
2.6
) follows, upon comparing (
2.5
) with (
2.7
) and (
2.9
).
∎

3.
Main result

Theorem 3.1
.

Let
N_{pq}(t)
be the polynomial defined recursively in (
2.3
).
Then

(3.1)

N_{pq}(t)=\sum_{k=1}^{\min\{p,q\}}{\rm S}_{{}_{\ominus\!}}(k,p-k\mid k,q-k)\cdot t^{k},

where the coefficients
{\rm S}_{{}_{\ominus\!}}(-)
are defined in (
2.5
).

Proof.

Recall from (
2.3
) the recursion defining
N_{pq}(t)
, which we expand below (labeling each term for clarity later in the proof):

(3.2)

N_{pq}=\underbrace{N_{p-1,q}}_{\mathbf{A}}+\underbrace{N_{p,q-1}}_{\mathbf{B}}-\underbrace{N_{p-1,q-1}}_{\mathbf{C}}+\;t\cdot\underbrace{N_{p-1,q-1}}_{\mathbf{D}}+\;t\cdot\underbrace{|p-q|\cdot W_{pq}}_{\mathbf{E}},

with initial values
N_{0,q}=N_{p,0}=N_{1,1}=0
.

We begin by verifying the initial values.
Clearly when
p
or
q
is zero, the sum in (
3.1
) is empty.
If
p=q=1
, then there is a single summand where
k=1
, namely

{\rm S}_{{}_{\ominus\!}}(1,0\mid 1,0)\cdot t.

Since
{\rm Par}(1\times 0)
contains only the empty partition, the expression above is zero, as required.

It remains to verify the recursion itself.
As our induction hypothesis, assume that (
3.1
) is valid for all
N_{p^{\prime}q^{\prime}}
with
p^{\prime}<p
or
q^{\prime}<q
.
It suffices to show, for all
k
, that the coefficient of
t^{k}
in the right-hand side of (
3.1
), namely
{\rm S}_{{}_{\ominus\!}}(k,p-k\mid k,q-k)
, equals the coefficient of
t^{k}
in the right-hand side of (
3.2
).
That is, writing
[t^{k}]f
to denote the coefficient of
t^{k}
in a polyomial
f(t)
,
we need to show that

\displaystyle{\rm S}_{{}_{\ominus\!}}(k,p-k\mid k,q-k)

\displaystyle=[t^{k}](\mathbf{A}+\mathbf{B}-\mathbf{C}+t\mathbf{D}+t\mathbf{E})

(3.3)

\displaystyle=[t^{k}]\mathbf{A}+[t^{k}]\mathbf{B}-[t^{k}]\mathbf{C}+[t^{k-1}]\mathbf{D}+[t^{k-1}]\mathbf{E}.

For ease of notation (and to align with Lemma
2.3
), set
\ell\coloneqq p-k
and
m\coloneqq q-k
.
The required coefficients in
\mathbf{A},\ldots,\mathbf{D}
follow directly from (
3.1
) via the induction hypothesis:

\displaystyle[t^{k}]\mathbf{A}

\displaystyle=[t^{k}]N_{p-1,q}

\displaystyle={\rm S}_{{}_{\ominus\!}}(k,\ell-1\mid k,m),

\displaystyle[t^{k}]\mathbf{B}

\displaystyle=[t^{k}]N_{p,q-1}

\displaystyle={\rm S}_{{}_{\ominus\!}}(k,\ell\mid k,m-1),

\displaystyle[t^{k}]\mathbf{C}

\displaystyle=[t^{k}]N_{p-1,q-1}

\displaystyle={\rm S}_{{}_{\ominus\!}}(k,\ell-1\mid k,m-1),

\displaystyle[t^{k-1}]\mathbf{D}

\displaystyle=[t^{k-1}]N_{p-1,q-1}

\displaystyle={\rm S}_{{}_{\ominus\!}}(k-1,\ell\mid k-1,m).

The coefficient
[t^{k-1}]\mathbf{E}
can be expressed by means of previous identities:

\displaystyle[t^{k-1}]\mathbf{E}

\displaystyle=|p-q|\cdot[t^{k-1}]W_{pq}

\displaystyle=|p-q|\cdot\textstyle\binom{p-1}{k-1}\binom{q-1}{k-1}

by (
2.2
)

\displaystyle=|(\ell+k)-(m+k)|\cdot\textstyle\binom{\ell+k-1}{k-1}\binom{m+k-1}{k-1}

\displaystyle=|\ell-m|\cdot|{\rm Par}((k-1)\times\ell)|\cdot|{\rm Par}((k-1)\times m)|

\displaystyle\text{by~\eqref{size Par ab}}.

Upon substituting these five coefficients back into (
3.3
), we obtain the equation in Lemma
2.3
, which verifies (
3.3
) as desired.
Finally, to determine the range of the sum in (
3.1
), we observe that
{\rm S}_{{}_{\ominus\!}}(k,p-k\mid k,q-k)
is nonzero only when
1\leq k\leq\min\{p,q\}
.
∎

The following corollary settles the palindromic part of Conjecture 1 from
[
2
]
.

Corollary 3.2
.

For all positive integers
n
, the polynomial
N_{n}(t)
is palindromic.

Proof.

Since
p=q=n
, the coefficient of
t^{k}
in (
3.1
) equals
{\rm S}_{{}_{\ominus\!}}(k,0\mid k,0)=0
; therefore
N_{n}(t)
has degree
n-1
, and total degree
1+(n-1)=n
.
By the definition of palindromicity, we need to show that

{\rm S}_{{}_{\ominus\!}}(k,n-k\mid k,n-k)={\rm S}_{{}_{\ominus\!}}(n-k,k\mid n-k,k)

for all
1\leq k\leq n-1
, and this follows directly from Lemma
2.2
.
∎

4.
Explicit formulas via minuscule lattices of Type A

Armed with the combinatorial interpretation in Theorem
3.1
, we show in this section that a recent result by Defant et al.
[
4
]
, concerning the Wiener index of minuscule lattices, yields the explicit formula for the coefficients of
N_{n}(t)
.
We use this to prove the unimodality of
N_{n}(t)
.
The connection goes even further.
By reinterpreting the results of
[
4
]
, we are now able to close the recursion in
[
2
]
for the expected value of the one-dimensional EMD.

4.1.
Wiener index of minuscule lattices of Type A

The minuscule lattices form a special class of Hasse diagrams, in which the underlying poset is the set of weights of a minuscule representation of a complex simple Lie algebra
\mathfrak{g}
.
(In this paper we appeal only to the combinatorial structure of certain of these lattices, and therefore the following paragraph may be omitted without loss of continuity.)

A
minuscule weight
of a complex simple Lie algebra
\mathfrak{g}
is a minimal element
\varpi
in the poset of dominant integral weights of
\mathfrak{g}
(in the partial order whereby
\varpi\leq\varpi^{\prime}
if
\varpi^{\prime}-\varpi
is a nonnegative sum of simple roots).
For
\varpi
minuscule, and
V_{\varpi}
the irreducible representation of
\mathfrak{g}
with highest weight
\varpi
, the
set of weights of
V_{\varpi}
is precisely the Weyl group orbit of
\varpi
, and its Hasse diagram is said to be a
minuscule lattice
; see
[
13
]
for details.
Minuscule lattices have various incarnations in the theory of Hermitian symmetric pairs
(\mathfrak{g},\mathfrak{k})
.
For example, minuscule lattices are the Hasse diagrams (with respect to the Bruhat order) of the minimal-length representatives of the right cosets of the Weyl group of
\mathfrak{k}
inside the Weyl group of
\mathfrak{g}
.
Equivalently, minuscule lattices are Hasse diagrams of the poset of lower-order ideals of the positive noncompact roots of
\mathfrak{g}
.
See
[
6
]
for an expansive exposition on the subject.

Let
G=(V,E)
be a finite connected graph, and let
d(x,y)
denote the distance in
G
between
x,y\in V
.
The
Wiener index
of
G
is defined to be the sum of the distances between all ordered pairs of vertices:

d(G)\coloneqq\sum_{\mathclap{(x,y)\in V\times V}}d(x,y).

By extension, if
P
is a finite poset, then
d(P)
is defined to be the Wiener index of the Hasse diagram of
P
.

When
\mathfrak{g}
is of Type A (in the Killing–Cartan classification), a minuscule lattice is the Hasse diagram of the poset of order ideals in a rectangle, say of dimensions
a\times b
, ordered by inclusion.
In
[
4
]
, this poset is denoted by
P_{a,b}
.
The key fact, for our purposes, is that
P_{a,b}\cong{\rm Par}(a\times b)
as posets, if we order Young diagrams by inclusion.
In other words, in
{\rm Par}(a\times b)
, we declare
\mu\leq\lambda
if
\lambda
is obtained by adding boxes to
\mu
.
Hence
\varnothing
is the minimal element of
{\rm Par}(a\times b)
, while
(b^{a})
is the maximal element.
1
1

1

This fact can be restated in somewhat different, but quite standard, combinatorial language:
if
\mathcal{Y}
denotes
Young’s lattice
(the Hasse diagram of all Young diagrams ordered by inclusion), then we can identify
P_{a,b}\cong{\rm Par}(a\times b)
with the sublattice of
\mathcal{Y}
generated by the rectangular Young diagram with
a
rows and
b
columns.

It is easy to see (and is noted in
[
4
]
*§1.3) that distance on the Hasse diagram of
P_{a,b}
measures the symmetric difference of the corresponding Young diagrams;
after all, neighboring elements in the Hasse diagram differ by exactly one box.
Therefore, given
\lambda,\mu\in{\rm Par}(a\times b)
, we have
d(\lambda,\mu)=|\lambda\ominus\mu|
.

The upshot of this is that the Wiener index of
{\rm Par}(a\times b)
equals the sum of the sizes of the symmetric differences of all ordered pairs.
Translating between
[
4
]
and our present paper, this means that

(4.1)

d(P_{a,b})={\rm S}_{{}_{\ominus\!}}(a,b)\coloneqq{\rm S}_{{}_{\ominus\!}}(a,b\mid a,b).

(From now on we will need only the specialized shorthand
{\rm S}_{{}_{\ominus\!}}(a,b)
, since we consider ordered pairs of elements from the common set
{\rm Par}(a\times b)
.)
By manipulating the generating functions of certain Motzkin paths, the authors of
[
4
]
derive the following formula (Thm. 1.2) for the Wiener index of
P_{a,b}
:

(4.2)

d(P_{a,b})=\frac{ab}{4a+4b+2}\binom{2a+2b+2}{2a+1}.

Combining this result with (
4.1
) and our Theorem
3.1
, we obtain the following explicit formula for the coefficients of
N_{n}(t)
:

Theorem 4.1
.

For all positive integers
n
, we have

N_{n}(t)=\frac{1}{4n+2}\cdot\sum_{k=1}^{n-1}k(n-k)\binom{2n+2}{2k+1}\>t^{k}.

We now prove the second part of Conjecture
1
:

Corollary 4.2
.

For all positive integers
n
, the polynomial
N_{n}(t)
is unimodal.

Proof.

Using Theorem
4.1
, it suffices to show that both factors
k(n-k)
and
\binom{2n+2}{2k+1}
are nondecreasing as functions of
k
, for
1\leq k<\lfloor n/2\rfloor
.
In fact, both factors are strictly increasing.
In particular, it is well known that the binomial coefficients
\binom{a}{b}
are increasing as functions of
b
, for
b<a/2
, and therefore
\binom{2(n+1)}{2k+1}
is increasing for
k<n/2
.
Moreover, treating
k(n-k)
as a real function of
k
, we have
\frac{d}{dk}[k(n-k)]=n-2k
, which is positive for all
k<n/2
.
This completes the proof.
∎

4.2.
Expected value of the EMD

Not only does the formula (
4.2
) lead to the complete description of
N_{n}(t)
in Theorem
4.1
, but we now show that it also solves the recursion for the expected value of the EMD, which was the main result of
[
2
]
.

The paper
[
9
]
*eqn. (10) employs the bijection

\displaystyle\mathcal{C}(s,n)

\displaystyle\longrightarrow{\rm Par}(s\times(n-1)),

\displaystyle\alpha

\displaystyle\longmapsto\Big((n-1)^{\alpha_{1}},(n-2)^{\alpha_{2}},\ldots,2^{\alpha_{n-2}},1^{\alpha_{n-1}}\Big),

where the exponents denote repeated parts in a partition.
Now let
\alpha,\beta\in\mathcal{C}(s,n)
, and suppose that
\alpha\mapsto\lambda
and
\beta\mapsto\mu
under this bijection.
It is shown in
[
9
]
*Prop. 3.1 that the EMD is precisely the size of the symmetric difference of the corresponding Young diagrams:

{\rm EMD}(\alpha,\beta)=|\lambda\ominus\mu|.

Comparing this with (
1.2
) and (
2.5
), it follows that

(4.3)

[t^{s}]H^{\prime}_{n}(t)=\sum_{(\alpha,\beta)}{\rm EMD}(\alpha,\beta)=\sum_{(\lambda,\mu)}|\lambda\ominus\mu|\eqqcolon{\rm S}_{{}_{\ominus\!}}(s,n-1),

where
\alpha,\beta\in\mathcal{C}(s,n)
and
\lambda,\mu\in{\rm Par}(s\times(n-1))
.
Therefore, the expected value of the EMD is simply
{\rm S}_{{}_{\ominus\!}}(s,n-1)
divided by the square of
|\mathcal{C}(s,n)|
, or equivalently, divided by the square of
|{\rm Par}(s\times(n-1))|
.

Theorem 4.3
.

Let
(\alpha,\beta)\in\mathcal{C}(s,n)\times\mathcal{C}(s,n)
be chosen uniformly at random.
Then

\mathbb{E}\Big[{\rm EMD}(\alpha,\beta)\Big]=\frac{s(n-1)}{4s+4n-2}\cdot\frac{\binom{2s+2n}{2s+1}}{\binom{s+n-1}{s}^{2}}.

Proof.

We have

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\Big[{\rm EMD}(\alpha,\beta)\Big]

\displaystyle\coloneqq\sum_{(\alpha,\beta)}{\rm EMD}(\alpha,\beta)\Big/|\mathcal{C}(s,n)|^{2}

\displaystyle={\rm S}_{{}_{\ominus\!}}(s,n-1)\Big/\textstyle\binom{s+n-1}{s}^{2}

by (
4.3
) and (
2.4
)

\displaystyle=\frac{s(n-1)}{4s+4(n-1)+2}\cdot\textstyle\binom{2s+2(n-1)+2}{2s+1}\Big/\binom{s+n-1}{s}^{2}

by (
4.1
) and (
4.2
),

which yields the expression in the theorem.
∎

Remark 4.4
.

The explicit formula in Theorem
4.3
gives us an easy way to compute the expected value of the EMD on ordered pairs of probability distributions on
[n]
:
namely, divide by
s
so that
\alpha/s
and
\beta/s
are probability distributions with rational values, and then take the limit as
s\rightarrow\infty
.
This was the method used in
[
2
]
to convert their recursive formula from compositions to probability distributions.
In this case, starting with the expression in Theorem
4.3
, it can be shown that

\lim_{s\rightarrow\infty}\frac{1}{s}\cdot\mathbb{E}\Big[{\rm EMD}(\alpha,\beta)\Big]=\frac{\sqrt{\pi}\,(n-1)\,\Gamma(n)}{4\,\Gamma\!\!\left(n+\frac{1}{2}\right)}.

This is, although somewhat disguised, equivalent to the expected value formula derived in
[
10
]
*Thm. 1, namely

\frac{2^{2n-3}(n-1)}{(2n-1)!}\,(n-1)!\,^{2},

which was obtained analytically by solving the recursion in
[
2
]
.

The following symmetric difference identity is proved in
