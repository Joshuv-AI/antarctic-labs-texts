The nonparametric Behrens-Fisher problem in small samples
Claus P. Nowak, Markus Pauly, Edgar Brunner

Abstract
While there appears to be a general consensus in the literature on the definition of the estimand and estimator associated with the Wilcoxon-Mann-Whitney test, it seems somewhat less clear as to how best to estimate the variance. In addition to the Wilcoxon-Mann-Whitney test, we review different proposals of variance estimators consistent under both the null hypothesis and the alternative. Moreover, in case of small sample sizes, an approximation of the distribution of the test statistic based on the t t -distribution, a logit transformation and a permutation approach have been proposed. Focussing as well on different estimators of the degrees of freedom as regards the t t -approximation, we carried out simulations for a range of scenarios, with results indicating that the performance of different variance estimators in terms of controlling the type I error rate largely depends on the heteroskedasticity pattern and the sample size allocation ratio, not on the specific type of distributions employed. By and large, a particular t t -approximation together with Perme and Manevski’s variance estimator best maintains the nominal significance level



Keywords
Brunner-Munzel test, Wilcoxon-Mann-Whitney test

1
Introduction

In the biomedical context, nonparametric methods are frequently indicated by ordered categorical data such as pain or clinical severity scores. In order to nonparametrically test the null hypothesis of whether two unpaired samples produce similar outcomes, the Wilcoxon-Mann-Whitney test
(
Mann and Whitney 1947
;
Wilcoxon 1945
;
Wilcoxon 1947
)
is arguably the one most commonly used in practice.

Usually, the estimand related to the Wilcoxon-Mann-Whitney test is defined as the probability

p=\mathbb{P}(X_{1}<X_{2})+\nicefrac{{1}}{{2}}\cdot\mathbb{P}(X_{1}=X_{2}),

where
X_{1}\sim F_{1}
and
X_{2}\sim F_{2}
denote two independent random variables corresponding to the two samples. The quantity
p
is referred to as nonparametric relative effect of
X_{2}
with respect to
X_{1}

(
Brunner and Munzel 2000
;
Brunner et al. 2018
)
, probabilistic index
(
Thas et al. 2012
)
or Mann-Whitney parameter
(
Fay et al. 2018
)
. In the setting of a parallel two-arm clinical trial, one may regard the random variables
X_{1}
and
X_{2}
as responses from treatment arms 1 and 2 respectively. Assuming that lower values imply a more beneficial outcome, one may interpret
p
as the probability that a patient on arm 1 will fare better than one on arm 2, including
\nicefrac{{1}}{{2}}
times the probability of equal outcomes.

While the literature seems to agree that the most suitable estimator of
p
, which we will refer to as
\widehat{p}
, are the corresponding relative frequencies arising from all pairwise comparisons of the sample data, the question of how best to estimate the variance of
\widehat{p}
does not appear to be quite that settled.

The variance estimator employed in the Wilcoxon-Mann-Whitney test is unbiased and consistent, but only under the assumption of equal distributions, i.e.,
F_{1}=F_{2}
. Hence the Wilcoxon-Mann-Whitney test can neither be inverted to produce confidence intervals nor does it directly address the nonparametric Behrens-Fisher problem. In that regard, assume both distribution
F_{1}
and
F_{2}
are symmetric with the same centre of symmetry but heterskedastic such as two normal distributions with the same expectation but different variances, yielding a Mann-Whitney parameter of
p=\nicefrac{{1}}{{2}}
. To test the null hypothesis
H_{0}:p=\nicefrac{{1}}{{2}}
,
Shirahata 1993
considers a number of variance estimators of
\widehat{p}
under the assumption that both distributions
F_{1}
and
F_{2}
are continuous while
Bamber 1975
proposed an unbiased variance estimator for general
F_{1}
and
F_{2}
, be they continuous, discreet or neither. Moreover,
DeLong et al. 1988
,
Brunner and Munzel 2000
as well as
Perme and Manevski 2019
put forward variance estimators consistent for arbitrary
F_{1}
and
F_{2}
as well.

In small samples,
Brunner and Munzel 2000
suggest the use of a
t
-approximation analogous to the Satterthwaite-Smith-Welch approach
(
Satterthwaite 1946
;
Smith 1936
;
Welch 1937
)
as regards the parametric Behrens-Fisher problem. Using different degrees of freedom and different variance estimators allowing for ties, we carry out simulation studies for a range of scenarios to gauge the performance of the resulting tests in terms of the type I error rate and power. In addition, we consider a permutation test proposed by
Pauly et al. 2016
.

This manuscript proceeds as follows. In Section
2
we review nonparametric theory and give definitions of the test statistics, whose empirical behaviour we examine as regards type I error rates and power in Section
3
and close with a discussion of the results in Section
4
.
All proofs and derivations as well as more and more detailed simulation results are given in the appendix.

2
Nonparametric model

We start with notation from nonparametric theory convenient for stating variance formulas and test statistics. Then we go over the variance associated with the Wilcoxon-Mann-Whitney test, as well as variance estimators consistent for arbitrary distributions
F_{1}
and
F_{2}
. For sake of completeness, we will also briefly mention Shirahata’s [
Shirahata 1993
] formulas. With the asymptotic normality of the resulting test statistics already established
(
Brunner et al. 2018
)
, we will discuss different estimators for the degrees of freedom in a small sample
t
-approximation as well as as the permutation approach developed by
Pauly et al. 2016
.

2.1
Notation

Let
X
denote a univariate random variable defined on a probability space
(\Omega,\mathcal{A},\mathbb{P})
, which stands for real-valued or ordered categorical responses. As is commonly done, we call

\displaystyle F^{-}(x)

\displaystyle=\mathbb{P}(X<x)\quad\text{ the left-continuous},

\displaystyle F^{+}(x)

\displaystyle=\mathbb{P}(X\leq x)\quad\text{ the right-continuous},

\displaystyle F(x)

\displaystyle=\mathbb{P}(X<x)+\nicefrac{{1}}{{2}}\cdot\mathbb{P}(X=x)\quad\text{ the normalised}

version of the cumulative distribution function of
X

(
Lévy 1925
;
Ruymgaart 1980
;
Brunner and Munzel 2000
)
.

For a particular sample of observations
X_{1},\dots,X_{n}\overset{iid}{\sim}F
, we further denote by

\displaystyle\widehat{F}(x)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{j=1}^{n}c(x,X_{j}),\quad c(x,X_{j})=\begin{cases}0&\text{ if }x<X_{j}\\
\nicefrac{{1}}{{2}}&\text{ if }x=X_{j}\\
1&\text{ if }x>X_{j}\end{cases},

the normalised version of the empirical cumulative distribution function. Moreover, we call

\displaystyle R_{i}=\nicefrac{{1}}{{2}}+\sum_{j=1}^{n}c(X_{i},X_{j}),\quad i=1,\dots,n,

the mid-rank of
X_{i}
among the observations
X_{1},\dots,X_{n}
.

As for two independent random variables
X_{1}\sim F_{1}
and
X_{2}\sim F_{2}
, the Mann-Whitney parameter
as given in the Introduction has the following integral representation, i.e.,

\displaystyle p=\mathbb{P}(X_{1}<X_{2})+\nicefrac{{1}}{{2}}\cdot\mathbb{P}(X_{1}=X_{2})=\int F_{1}dF_{2}.

We say that

•

X_{1}
tends to smaller values than
X_{2}
if
p>\nicefrac{{1}}{{2}}
,

•

X_{1}
tends to larger values than
X_{2}
if
p<\nicefrac{{1}}{{2}}
,

•

X_{1}
and
X_{2}
are stochastically comparable if
p=\nicefrac{{1}}{{2}}
.

For a more comprehensive treatment of nonparametric theory we refer to
Brunner et al. 2018
.

Throughout the remainder of this manuscript we will focus on a parallel two-arm clinical trial with responses

\displaystyle X_{1i}

\displaystyle\overset{iid}{\sim}F_{1},\quad i=1,\dots,n_{1},

\displaystyle X_{2j}

\displaystyle\overset{iid}{\sim}F_{2},\quad j=1,\dots,n_{2},

from treatment arms 1 and 2 respectively.
With
N=n_{1}+n_{2}
, we can estimate the nonparametric relative effect
p
by

\displaystyle\widehat{p}

\displaystyle=\int\widehat{F}_{1}d\widehat{F}_{2}=\frac{1}{n_{1}}\frac{1}{n_{2}}\sum_{j=1}^{n_{2}}\sum_{i=1}^{n_{1}}c(X_{2j},X_{1i})=\frac{1}{N}(\bar{R}_{2\bullet}-\bar{R}_{1\bullet})+\nicefrac{{1}}{{2}},

with
\bar{R}_{g\bullet}=\frac{1}{n_{g}}\sum_{i=1}^{n_{g}}R_{gi}
,
g=1,2
, where
R_{gi}
is the mid-rank of
X_{gi}
among all
N
observations
X_{11},\dots,X_{1n_{1}},X_{21},\dots,X_{2n_{2}}
.

To address the nonparametric Behrens-Fisher problem, we consider the null hypothesis
H_{0}:p=\nicefrac{{1}}{{2}}
against
H_{1}:p\neq\nicefrac{{1}}{{2}}
. Unsurprisingly, all resulting test statistics are based on the deviation of the Mann-Whitney parameter estimate from
\nicefrac{{1}}{{2}}
, i.e.,

\widehat{p}-\nicefrac{{1}}{{2}}.

For asymptotic results, we let both sample sizes tend to infinity such that neither vanishes, i.e.,
n_{g}/N\rightarrow\gamma_{g}>0
for both
n_{1}\rightarrow\infty
and
n_{2}\rightarrow\infty
,
g=1,2
. Moreover, we assume
0<p<1
and that there exists no
x
such that
\mathbb{P}(X_{11}=x)=1
or
\mathbb{P}(X_{21}=x)=1
, i.e., excluding the degenerate cases of completely separated samples and one-point distributions.

To obtain asymptotic standard normal test statistics, it remains to define suitable estimators of
\mathbb{V}(\widehat{p})
, which is the purpose of the next subsection.

2.2
Variance estimators

Under the assumption of equal distributions, i.e.,
F=F_{1}=F_{2}
, the variance estimand
\mathbb{V}(\widehat{p})
takes the following form,

\sigma_{WMW}^{2}=\frac{\sigma_{R}^{2}}{Nn_{1}n_{2}},\text{ with }\sigma^{2}_{R}=N\{(N-2)\int F^{2}dF-\frac{N-3}{4}\}-\frac{N}{4}\int(F^{+}-F^{-})dF.

If
F=F_{1}=F_{2}
holds, a consistent and unbiased estimator of the variance
\sigma_{WMW}^{2}
is given by

\widehat{\sigma}^{2}_{WMW}=\frac{\widehat{\sigma}_{R}^{2}}{Nn_{1}n_{2}},\text{ with }\widehat{\sigma}^{2}_{R}=\frac{N^{3}}{N-1}(\int\widehat{F}^{2}d\widehat{F}-\nicefrac{{1}}{{4}})=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{g=1}^{2}\sum_{i=1}^{n_{g}}(R_{gi}-\frac{N+1}{2})^{2},

(1)

resulting in
T_{WMW}=(\widehat{p}-\nicefrac{{1}}{{2}})/\widehat{\sigma}_{WMW}\xrightarrow{\,\mathcal{D}\,}\mathcal{N}(0,1)
, which is nothing but the Wilcoxon-Mann-Whitney test allowing for ties
(
Brunner et al. 2018
)
. In the context of this manuscript, we feel it more tenable to regard the Wilcoxon-Mann-Whitney test as a way of testing the null hypothesis formulated in terms of
p
, i.e.,
H_{0}:p=\nicefrac{{1}}{{2}}
, whereas
F_{1}=F_{2}
amounts to an additional assumption on the model under the null.

As for arbitrary distributions
F_{1}
and
F_{2}
,
Bamber 1975
as well as
(
Brunner et al. 2021a
)
provide a formula of the variance estimand
\mathbb{V}(\widehat{p})
, which reads in our notation as

\sigma^{2}_{N}=\frac{\tau_{0}+(n_{2}-1)\tau_{1}+(n_{1}-1)\tau_{2}-(n_{1}+n_{2}-1)p^{2}}{n_{1}n_{2}},

where

\tau_{0}=p-\nicefrac{{1}}{{4}}\cdot\int(F_{1}^{+}-F_{1}^{-})dF_{2}
,
\tau_{1}=\int(1-F_{2})^{2}dF_{1}
, and
\tau_{2}=\int F_{1}^{2}dF_{2}
.

Bamber 1975
as well as
Brunner et al. 2021a
propose an unbiased variance estimator of
\sigma^{2}_{N}
as well, namely,

\widehat{\sigma}_{N}^{2}=\frac{n_{2}\widehat{\tau}_{1}+n_{1}\widehat{\tau}_{2}-\widehat{\tau}_{0}-(n_{1}+n_{2}-1)\widehat{p}^{2}}{(n_{1}-1)(n_{2}-1)},

(2)

with

\widehat{\tau}_{0}=\widehat{p}-\nicefrac{{1}}{{4}}\cdot\int(\widehat{F}_{1}^{+}-\widehat{F}_{1}^{-})d\widehat{F}_{2}
,
\widehat{\tau}_{1}=\int(1-\widehat{F}_{2})^{2}d\widehat{F}_{1}
, and
\widehat{\tau}_{2}=\int\widehat{F}_{1}^{2}d\widehat{F}_{2}
.
For a computationally more efficient expression of
\widehat{\sigma}_{N}^{2}
in terms of ranks, see
Brunner et al. 2021a
.

Brunner and Munzel 2000
derived an estimator of
\sigma_{N}^{2}
similar in structure to the variance estimator of the two-sample
t
-test under heteroskedasticity, i.e.,

\widehat{\sigma}_{BM}^{2}=\frac{\widehat{\sigma}^{2}_{1}}{n_{1}}+\frac{\widehat{\sigma}^{2}_{2}}{n_{2}},\text{ where }\widehat{\sigma}^{2}_{1}=\frac{n_{1}}{n_{1}-1}(\widehat{\tau}_{1}-\widehat{p}^{2})\text{ and }\widehat{\sigma}^{2}_{2}=\frac{n_{2}}{n_{2}-1}(\widehat{\tau}_{2}-\widehat{p}^{2}),

(3)

with
\widehat{\tau}_{1}
and
\widehat{\tau}_{2}
as given in (
2
). For a computationally more efficient rank representation of
\widehat{\sigma}_{1}^{2}
and
\widehat{\sigma}_{2}^{2}
, see
Brunner and Munzel 2000
. Note that the estimator
\widehat{\sigma}_{BM}^{2}
is identical to the one given in
DeLong et al. 1988
.

Perme and Manevski 2019
propose yet another estimator for
\sigma_{N}^{2}
, which they refer to as exact, i.e.,

\widehat{\sigma}_{PM}^{2}=\frac{\widehat{p}(1-\widehat{p})+(n_{2}-1)\widehat{\sigma}_{1}^{2}+(n_{1}-1)\widehat{\sigma}_{2}^{2}}{n_{1}n_{2}},

(4)

with
\widehat{\sigma}_{1}^{2}
and
\widehat{\sigma}_{2}^{2}
as just defined in (
3
).

In the Introduction, we have vaguely hinted at the consistency of the variance estimators. More precisely, the dominating terms
\widehat{\tau}_{1}
,
\widehat{\tau}_{2}
,
\widehat{p}^{2}
are consistent for
\tau_{1}
,
\tau_{2}
,
p^{2}
and they converge, as weighted with the sample sizes in
\widehat{\sigma}^{2}_{N}
(
2
),
\widehat{\sigma}^{2}_{BM}
(
3
),
\widehat{\sigma}^{2}_{PM}
(
4
), to zero in probability with the same speed.

2.3
Shirahata’s formulas for continuous distributions

Assuming that ties cannot occur almost surely,
Shirahata 1993
discusses the following four estimators, i.e., an unbiased one, a bootstrap estimator, an estimator by
Fligner and Policello 1981
, and a jackknife estimator, which in our notation read as

\displaystyle\widehat{\sigma}_{U}^{2}

\displaystyle=\frac{n_{2}\int(1-\widehat{F}_{2}^{-})^{2}d\widehat{F}_{1}+n_{1}\int(\widehat{F}_{1}^{+})^{2}d\widehat{F}_{2}-\int\widehat{F}_{1}^{+}d\widehat{F}_{2}-(n_{1}+n_{2}-1)(\int\widehat{F}_{1}^{+}d\widehat{F}_{2})^{2}}{(n_{1}-1)(n_{2}-1)},

\displaystyle\widehat{\sigma}_{B}^{2}

\displaystyle=\frac{(n_{2}-1)\int(1-\widehat{F}_{2}^{-})^{2}d\widehat{F}_{1}+(n_{1}-1)\int(\widehat{F}_{1}^{+})^{2}d\widehat{F}_{2}+\int\widehat{F}_{1}^{+}d\widehat{F}_{2}-(n_{1}+n_{2}-1)(\int\widehat{F}_{1}^{+}d\widehat{F}_{2})^{2}}{n_{1}n_{2}},

\displaystyle\widehat{\sigma}_{FP}^{2}

\displaystyle=\frac{\int(1-\widehat{F}_{2}^{-})^{2}d\widehat{F}_{1}}{n_{1}}+\frac{\int(\widehat{F}_{1}^{+})^{2}d\widehat{F}_{2}}{n_{2}}-\frac{\int\widehat{F}_{1}^{+}d\widehat{F}_{2}+(n_{1}+n_{2}+1)(\int\widehat{F}_{1}^{+}d\widehat{F}_{2})^{2}}{n_{1}n_{2}},

\displaystyle\widehat{\sigma}_{J}^{2}

\displaystyle=\frac{\int(1-\widehat{F}_{2}^{-})^{2}d\widehat{F}_{1}}{n_{1}-1}+\frac{\int(\widehat{F}_{1}^{+})^{2}d\widehat{F}_{2}}{n_{2}-1}-\frac{(n_{1}+n_{2}-2)(\int\widehat{F}_{1}^{+}d\widehat{F}_{2})^{2}}{(n_{1}-1)(n_{2}-1)}.

In case of continuous distributions, however, it follows

\int\widehat{F}_{1}^{+}d\widehat{F}_{2}=\int\widehat{F}_{1}d\widehat{F}_{2}=\widehat{p}=\widehat{\tau}_{0}
,

\int(1-\widehat{F}_{2}^{-})^{2}d\widehat{F}_{1}=\int(1-\widehat{F}_{2})^{2}d\widehat{F}_{1}=\widehat{\tau}_{1}
,

\int(\widehat{F}_{1}^{+})^{2}d\widehat{F}_{2}=\int\widehat{F}_{1}^{2}d\widehat{F}_{2}=\widehat{\tau}_{2}
,
so that then we can express the variance estimators as follows

\displaystyle\widehat{\sigma}_{U}^{2}

\displaystyle=\frac{n_{2}\widehat{\tau}_{1}+n_{1}\widehat{\tau}_{2}-\widehat{\tau}_{0}-(n_{1}+n_{2}-1)\widehat{p}^{2}}{(n_{1}-1)(n_{2}-1)}=\widehat{\sigma}^{2}_{N},

\displaystyle\widehat{\sigma}_{B}^{2}

\displaystyle=\frac{(n_{2}-1)\widehat{\tau}_{1}+(n_{1}-1)\widehat{\tau}_{2}+\widehat{\tau}_{0}-(n_{1}+n_{2}-1)\widehat{p}^{2}}{n_{1}n_{2}},

\displaystyle\widehat{\sigma}_{FP}^{2}

\displaystyle=\frac{\widehat{\tau}_{1}}{n_{1}}+\frac{\widehat{\tau}_{2}}{n_{2}}-\frac{\widehat{\tau}_{0}+(n_{1}+n_{2}+1)\widehat{p}^{2}}{n_{1}n_{2}},

\displaystyle\widehat{\sigma}_{J}^{2}

\displaystyle=\frac{\widehat{\tau}_{1}}{n_{1}-1}+\frac{\widehat{\tau}_{2}}{n_{2}-1}-\frac{(n_{1}+n_{2}-2)\widehat{p}^{2}}{(n_{1}-1)(n_{2}-1)}=\widehat{\sigma}_{BM}^{2}.

2.4
Degrees of freedom

Analogous to the parametric Behrens-Fisher problem,
Brunner and Munzel 2000
propose a Satterthwaite-Smith-Welch
t
-approximation
(
Satterthwaite 1946
;
Smith 1936
;
Welch 1937
)
for small samples with degrees of freedom estimated by

df=\frac{\{\widehat{\sigma}_{1}^{2}/{n_{1}}+\widehat{\sigma}_{2}^{2}/{n_{2}}\}^{2}}{\widehat{\sigma}_{1}^{4}/\{n_{1}^{2}(n_{1}-1)\}+\widehat{\sigma}_{2}^{4}/\{n_{2}^{2}(n_{2}-1)\}},

(5)

where
\widehat{\sigma}_{1}^{2}
and
\widehat{\sigma}_{2}^{2}
are defined as in (
3
). In addition, we will consider the degrees of freedom

\displaystyle df_{1}

\displaystyle=\frac{\{\widehat{\sigma}_{1}^{2}/({n_{1}-1})+\widehat{\sigma}_{2}^{2}/({n_{2}-1})\}^{2}}{\widehat{\sigma}_{1}^{4}/\{(n_{1}-1)^{2}(n_{1}-2)\}+\widehat{\sigma}_{2}^{4}/\{(n_{2}-1)^{2}(n_{2}-2)\}},

(6)

\displaystyle df_{2}

\displaystyle=\frac{\{\widehat{\sigma}_{1}^{2}/({n_{1}-2})+\widehat{\sigma}_{2}^{2}/({n_{2}-2})\}^{2}}{\widehat{\sigma}_{1}^{4}/\{(n_{1}-2)^{2}(n_{1}-3)\}+\widehat{\sigma}_{2}^{4}/\{(n_{2}-2)^{2}(n_{2}-3)\}},

(7)

\displaystyle df_{3}

\displaystyle=\frac{2}{1/(n_{1}-1)+1/(n_{2}-1)},

(8)

\displaystyle df_{4}

\displaystyle=\frac{\widehat{\sigma}_{N}^{4}}{\widehat{\sigma}_{1\mid N}^{4}/(n_{1}-1)+\widehat{\sigma}_{2\mid N}^{4}/(n_{2}-1)},

(9)

with
\widehat{\sigma}_{N}^{2}
as in (
2
) and

\widehat{\sigma}_{1\mid N}^{2}=\frac{n_{2}\widehat{\tau}_{1}-\nicefrac{{1}}{{2}}\cdot\widehat{\tau}_{0}-(n_{2}-\nicefrac{{1}}{{2}})\widehat{p}^{2}}{(n_{1}-1)(n_{2}-1)}
,

\widehat{\sigma}_{2\mid N}^{2}=\frac{n_{1}\widehat{\tau}_{2}-\nicefrac{{1}}{{2}}\cdot\widehat{\tau}_{0}-(n_{1}-\nicefrac{{1}}{{2}})\widehat{p}^{2}}{(n_{1}-1)(n_{2}-1)}
.

The intuition behind using (
6
) and (
7
) in small samples is similar to (
5
), we merely assume that there were one (or two) fewer observations in each of the two groups, with
\widehat{\sigma}_{1}^{2}
and
\widehat{\sigma}_{2}^{2}
remaining unchanged as in (
5
). On the other hand, formulas (
8
) and (
9
) are
loosely based on a Box-type
(
Box 1954
;
Brunner et al. 2018
)
approximation as regards the unbiased variance estimator.

Another way to address the liberal behaviour of the tests is to employ a variance stabilising transformation, such as the
logit
function, or a permutation approach as described in Section
2.5

(
Brunner et al. 2018
;
Pauly et al. 2016
)
.

2.5
Test statistics

Collecting the test statistics with regard to the null hypothesis
H_{0}:p=\nicefrac{{1}}{{2}}
allowing for ties, we have

\displaystyle T_{WMW}

\displaystyle=(\widehat{p}-\nicefrac{{1}}{{2}})/\widehat{\sigma}_{WMW},

(10)

\displaystyle T_{N}

\displaystyle=(\widehat{p}-\nicefrac{{1}}{{2}})/\widehat{\sigma}_{N},

(11)

\displaystyle T_{BM}

\displaystyle=(\widehat{p}-\nicefrac{{1}}{{2}})/\widehat{\sigma}_{BM},

(12)

\displaystyle T_{PM}

\displaystyle=(\widehat{p}-\nicefrac{{1}}{{2}})/\widehat{\sigma}_{PM}.

(13)

For the Wilcoxon-Mann-Whitney test (
10
) we use the standard normal distribution to compute
p
-values, for the other tests, (
11
) to (
13
), a central
t
-distribution with degrees of freedom given as in (
5
) to (
9
). As already alluded to, we will additionally consider the following test statistics based on a
logit
transformation using the delta method, i.e.,

\displaystyle T_{N}^{logit}

\displaystyle=\widehat{p}(1-\widehat{p})\cdot\ln\{\widehat{p}/(1-\widehat{p})\}/\widehat{\sigma}_{N},

(14)

\displaystyle T_{BM}^{logit}

\displaystyle=\widehat{p}(1-\widehat{p})\cdot\ln\{\widehat{p}/(1-\widehat{p})\}/\widehat{\sigma}_{BM},

(15)

\displaystyle T_{PM}^{logit}

\displaystyle=\widehat{p}(1-\widehat{p})\cdot\ln\{\widehat{p}/(1-\widehat{p})\}/\widehat{\sigma}_{PM}.

(16)

As with the Wilcoxon-Mann-Whitney test, we employ the standard normal distribution to obtain
p
-values for (
14
) to (
16
).

Moreover, we will make use of the studentised permutation approach suggested by
Pauly et al. 2016
. To this end, we randomly allocate
n_{1}
out of the entire
N=n_{1}+n_{2}
observations from the whole sample as originating from the first distribution
F_{1}
, with the remaining
n_{2}
responses regarded as having been drawn from
F_{2}
. Repeating this procedure, say
n_{perm}=10\,000
times, and computing one of the test statistics as in (
11
) to (
16
) each time, we obtain a permutation distribution on which to base rejection of the null hypothesis.
More formally, we relabel the entire data
(X_{11},\dots,X_{1n_{1}},X_{21},\dots,X_{2n_{2}})\eqqcolon(X_{1},\dots,X_{N})
and define a random variable
\pi
uniformly distributed on
S_{N}
which is the set of all permutations of
1,\dots,N
. For a particular data set at hand, we then use the permuted pooled sample
(X_{\pi(1)},\dots,X_{\pi(N)})
– with the first
n_{1}
and last
n_{2}
components considered as belonging to samples 1 and 2 respectively – to compute (
11
) to (
16
), yielding the permuted versions

\widetilde{T}_{N},\,\,\widetilde{T}_{BM},\,\,\widetilde{T}_{PM},\,\,\widetilde{T}_{N}^{logit},\,\,\widetilde{T}_{BM}^{logit},\,\,\widetilde{T}_{PM}^{logit}.

(17)

With
T_{N}
denoting the test statistic as in (
11
) based on the original data and
\widetilde{T}_{N}^{1},\dots,\widetilde{T}_{N}^{n_{perm}}
the corresponding test statistics of the
n_{perm}
random permutations, we calculate the two-sided
p
-value as follows,

2\min(p_{1},p_{2}),\,\text{ where }\,p_{1}=\frac{1}{n_{perm}}\sum_{k=1}^{n_{perm}}\mathbf{1}(\widetilde{T}_{N}^{k}\leq T_{N})\,\text{ and }\,p_{2}=\frac{1}{n_{perm}}\sum_{\ell=1}^{n_{perm}}\mathbf{1}(\widetilde{T}_{N}^{\ell}\geq T_{N}).

As for the other test statistics, the permutation based
p
-values are computed in a completely analogous manner.

3
Simulations

As the methods treated in Section
2
are of asymptotic nature, we explore their applicability for finite sample sizes in a range of scenarios. In that regard, we consider the null hypothesis
H_{0}:p=\nicefrac{{1}}{{2}}
against
H_{1}:p\neq\nicefrac{{1}}{{2}}
at a two-sided nominal significance level of
\alpha=0.05
. We first present simulation results for the asymptotic tests as defined in (
10
) to (
16
). As far as the test statistics (
11
) to (
13
) are concerned, we only report rejection rates for degrees of freedom
df_{2}
(
7
) in the main manuscript as they outperformed the other versions. Simulations of permutation tests as in (
17
) being computationally much more expensive, we restrict our focus to some select scenarios as outlined in Section
3.2
.

In extreme cases as alluded to earlier, some variance estimates might actually turn out to be zero or negative as would be the case in two completely separated samples. Since this happened very rarely and has virtually no bearing on the results, we relegate the discussion of exception handling to the appendix.

3.1
Asymptotic tests

First we generate data from normal distributions, namely
X_{gi}\overset{iid}{\sim}\mathcal{N}(\mu_{g},\sigma_{g}^{2})
,
g=1,2
,
i=1,\dots,n_{g}
.
To gauge the type I error rate of the different tests, we set
\mu_{1}=\mu_{2}=0
and perform 100 000 simulation runs for each scenario, giving rise to a Monte Carlo error of about
0.0006
based on a 95%-precision interval for a nominal significance level of
\alpha=0.05
. The results depicted in Table
1
indicate that Perme and Manveski’s test
T_{PM}
(
13
) with
df_{2}
degrees of freedom (
7

Now we choose an ordinal 5-point-distribution with categories
\mathcal{C}_{1}<\mathcal{C}_{2}<\mathcal{C}_{3}<\mathcal{C}_{4}<\mathcal{C}_{5}
. As in
Brunner et al. 2021b
, the probabilities of each category occurring are derived through a latent Beta distribution, i.e., we consider Beta random variables
Y_{gi}\overset{iid}{\sim}\mathcal{B}(\alpha_{g},\beta_{g})
,
g=1,2
,
i=1,\dots,n_{g}
, with shape parameters
\alpha_{g},\beta_{g}>0
, such that the expectation and variance of
Y_{gi}
are given by

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}(Y_{gi})

\displaystyle=\frac{\alpha_{g}}{\alpha_{g}+\beta_{g}},

\displaystyle\mathbb{V}(Y_{gi})

\displaystyle=\frac{\alpha_{g}\beta_{g}}{(\alpha_{g}+\beta_{g})^{2}(\alpha_{g}+\beta_{g}+1)}.

Then we discretise
Y_{gi}
to the random variable
X_{gi}
,
g=1,2
,
i=1,\dots,n_{g}
, as follows

\displaystyle X_{gi}

\displaystyle=\mathcal{C}_{k}\text{ if }Y_{gi}\in\left[0.2(k-1),0.2k\right[\text{ for }k=1,\dots,5.

Consequently, the probability mass function of
X_{gi}
is nothing but

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}(X_{gi}=\mathcal{C}_{k})

\displaystyle=\mathbb{P}(0.2(k-1)\leq Y_{gi}<0.2k)\text{ for }k=1,\dots,5.

(

) with

) best controls the nominal type I error rate. Moreover, the power graphs in Figure

Discussion

In this manuscript, we reviewed different variance estimators for the Mann-Whitney parameter and, more generally, different ways of how to approximate its sampling distribution in small samples. To stick to the unbiased variance estimator appears to be somewhat ill-advised. Indeed, in almost all scenarios Perme and Manevski’s
T_{PM}
(
13
) with degrees of freedom
df_{2}
(
7
) seems preferable in terms of controlling the type I error rate. Of course, Perme and Manevski’s variance estimator is not unbiased and the particular choice of degrees of freedom lack a sound theoretical justification, even if they are consistent.

In heteroskedastic settings, the Wilcoxon-Mann-Whitney test
T_{WMW}
(
10
) performs poorly, particularly in case of unequal sample sizes, a pattern which also very slightly emerges when using the permutation approach (
17
) by

Appendix

This appendix consists of two main parts. First, we discuss the different variance estimators, translate them into our notation and briefly outline our approach to the Box-type degrees of freedom as regards the unbiased variance estimator. Second, we focus on the simulations, dealing with exception handling and providing more detailed results and results from settings not considered in the main manuscript.

Part I – Variance estimators and degrees of freedom

First we extend our notation of nonparametric theory, then we discuss the variance estimand and its different estimators. Lastly, we briefly explain how we arrived at the Box-type formulas of the degrees of freedom.

General notation

Let
X
denote a random variable representing ordered categorical or real data, defined on some probability space
(\Omega,\mathcal{A},\mathbb{P})
. Then we define for each possible value
x
the following versions of distribution functions

\displaystyle F^{+}(x)

\displaystyle=\mathbb{P}(X\leq x),

\displaystyle F^{-}(x)

\displaystyle=\mathbb{P}(X<x),

\displaystyle F(x)

\displaystyle=\mathbb{P}(X<x)+\nicefrac{{1}}{{2}}\cdot\mathbb{P}(X=x).

Now suppose we have a sample of observations
X_{1},\dots,X_{n}\overset{iid}{\sim}F(x)
. The empirical distribution functions then take the form

\displaystyle\widehat{F}^{+}(x)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}c^{+}(x,X_{i}),\quad c^{+}(x,X_{i})=\begin{cases}0&\text{ if }x<X_{i}\\
1&\text{ if }x\geq X_{i}\end{cases},

\displaystyle\widehat{F}^{-}(x)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}c^{-}(x,X_{i}),\quad c^{-}(x,X_{i})=\begin{cases}0&\text{ if }x\leq X_{i}\\
1&\text{ if }x>X_{i}\end{cases},

\displaystyle\widehat{F}(x)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}c(x,X_{i}),\quad c(x,X_{i})=\begin{cases}0&\text{ if }x<X_{i}\\
\nicefrac{{1}}{{2}}&\text{ if }x=X_{i}\\
1&\text{ if }x>X_{i}\end{cases}.

Now we define the nonparametric relative effect and give some of its properties, which we will use in the derivations later on.

Let
X_{ij}\sim F_{i}(x)
,
i=1,2
,
j=1,\dots,n_{i}
be independent random variables. Then the nonparametric relative effect is given by

\displaystyle p=\mathbb{P}(X_{1j}<X_{2j^{\prime}})+\nicefrac{{1}}{{2}}\cdot\mathbb{P}(X_{1j}=X_{2j^{\prime}})=\int F_{1}dF_{2}

for all
j=1,\dots,n_{1}
and
j^{\prime}=1,\dots,n_{2}
. In particular, if
F=F_{1}=F_{2}
, then
\int FdF=\nicefrac{{1}}{{2}}
.

Let
X_{ij}\sim F_{i}(x)
,
i=1,\dots,d
,
j=1,\dots,n_{i}
be independent real-valued random variables. Then for all
i,i^{\prime}=1,\dots,d
as well as
j=1,\dots,n_{i}
and
j^{\prime}=1,\dots,n_{i^{\prime}}
it holds

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}(\widehat{F}_{i}(x))=\mathbb{E}(c(x,X_{ij}))

\displaystyle=F_{i}(x),

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}(\widehat{F}_{i}(X_{i^{\prime}j^{\prime}}))=\mathbb{E}(c(X_{i^{\prime}j^{\prime}},X_{ij}))

\displaystyle=\int F_{i}dF_{i^{\prime}}.

We will also be using the survival functions

\displaystyle S^{+}(x)

\displaystyle=\mathbb{P}(X\geq x),

\displaystyle S^{-}(x)

\displaystyle=\mathbb{P}(X>x),

\displaystyle S(x)

\displaystyle=\mathbb{P}(X>x)+\nicefrac{{1}}{{2}}\cdot\mathbb{P}(X=x),

with their empirical counterparts
\widehat{S}^{+}(x)
,
\widehat{S}^{-}(x)
, and
\widehat{S}(x)
defined accordingly. Note that

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}(\widehat{S}_{i}(x))=\mathbb{E}(c(X_{ij},x))

\displaystyle=S_{i}(x),

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}(\widehat{S}_{i}(X_{i^{\prime}j^{\prime}}))=\mathbb{E}(c(X_{ij},X_{i^{\prime}j^{\prime}}))

\displaystyle=\int S_{i}dF_{i^{\prime}}.

Variance estimand

In our notation, we have for the general case of arbitrary
F_{1}
and
F_{2}
,

\displaystyle\sigma^{2}_{N}

\displaystyle=\mathbb{V}(\widehat{p})=\frac{\tau_{0}+(n_{2}-1)\tau_{1}+(n_{1}-1)\tau_{2}-(n_{1}+n_{2}-1)p^{2}}{n_{1}n_{2}},

where

\displaystyle\tau_{0}

\displaystyle=\int F_{1}dF_{2}-\nicefrac{{1}}{{4}}\cdot\underbrace{\int(F_{1}^{+}-F_{1}^{-})dF_{2}}_{=\mathbb{P}(X_{1}=X_{2})\eqqcolon\beta}=p-\nicefrac{{1}}{{4}}\cdot\beta,

\displaystyle\tau_{1}

\displaystyle=\int S_{2}^{2}dF_{1}=\int(1-F_{2})^{2}dF_{1},

\displaystyle\tau_{2}

\displaystyle=\int F_{1}^{2}dF_{2}.

If both distributions coincide, i.e.,
F_{1}=F_{2}
, and are continuous, then it holds
\tau_{0}=\nicefrac{{1}}{{2}}
,
\tau_{1}=\tau_{2}=\nicefrac{{1}}{{3}}
, yielding
\sigma^{2}_{N}=\frac{n_{1}+n_{2}+1}{12n_{1}n_{2}}
.

In the unpublished preprint of
Brunner et al. 2021a
, we find formula (1.9), i.e.,

\displaystyle\sigma^{2}_{N}

\displaystyle=\frac{(n_{2}-1)\sigma_{1}^{2}+(n_{1}-1)\sigma_{2}^{2}+p(1-p)-\nicefrac{{1}}{{4}}\cdot\beta}{n_{1}n_{2}},

where

\displaystyle\sigma_{1}^{2}

\displaystyle=\mathbb{V}(F_{2}(X_{11}))=\int\{F_{2}-(1-p)\}^{2}dF_{1}=\int(S_{2}-p)^{2}dF_{1}=\int S_{2}^{2}dF_{1}-2p\int S_{2}dF_{1}+p^{2}=\tau_{1}-p^{2},

\displaystyle\sigma_{2}^{2}

\displaystyle=\mathbb{V}(F_{1}(X_{21}))=\int(F_{1}-p)^{2}dF_{2}=\int F_{1}^{2}dF_{2}-2p\int F_{1}dF_{2}+p^{2}=\tau_{2}-p^{2}.

So it should be evident that both definitions of
\sigma_{N}^{2}
are equivalent.

Bamber’s [
Bamber 1975
] definition of
\sigma_{N}^{2}
, which he calls
\sigma_{a}^{2}
, is equivalent as well. Assuming that
X
refers to sample 1 and
Y
to sample
2
, it holds
B_{YYX}=4\tau_{1}-4p+1
as well as
B_{XXY}=4\tau_{2}-4p+1
. With
F_{X}=F_{1}
and
F_{Y}=F_{2}
and taking
\mathbb{P}(Y_{1},Y_{2}<X)
to mean
\mathbb{P}(Y_{1}<X,Y_{2}<X)
, we can deduce

\displaystyle B_{YYX}

\displaystyle=\mathbb{P}(Y_{1}<X,Y_{2}<X)+\mathbb{P}(X<Y_{1},X<Y_{2})-2\mathbb{P}(Y_{1}<X<Y_{2})

\displaystyle=\int\mathbb{P}(Y_{1}<x,Y_{2}<x)dF_{X}(x)+\int\mathbb{P}(Y_{1}>x,Y_{2}>x)dF_{X}(x)-2\int\mathbb{P}(Y_{1}<x,Y_{2}>x)dF_{X}(x)

\displaystyle=\int\mathbb{P}(Y_{1}<x)\mathbb{P}(Y_{2}<x)dF_{X}(x)+\int\mathbb{P}(Y_{1}>x)\mathbb{P}(Y_{2}>x)dF_{X}(x)-2\int\mathbb{P}(Y_{1}<x)\mathbb{P}(Y_{2}>x)dF_{X}(x)

\displaystyle=\int\{F_{Y}^{-}(x)\}^{2}dF_{X}(x)+\int\{S_{Y}^{-}(x)\}^{2}dF_{X}(x)-2\int\{F_{Y}^{-}(x)S_{Y}^{-}(x)\}dF_{X}(x)

\displaystyle=\int\{S_{Y}^{-}(x)-F_{Y}^{-}(x)\}^{2}dF_{X}(x)

\displaystyle=\int\{S_{Y}^{-}(x)+\nicefrac{{1}}{{2}}\cdot\mathbb{P}(Y=x)-F_{Y}^{-}(x)-\nicefrac{{1}}{{2}}\cdot\mathbb{P}(Y=x)\}^{2}dF_{X}(x)

\displaystyle=\int\{S_{Y}(x)-F_{Y}(x)\}^{2}dF_{X}(x)

\displaystyle=\int\{2S_{Y}(x)-1\}^{2}dF_{X}(x)

\displaystyle=4\int S_{Y}^{2}dF_{X}-4\int S_{Y}dF_{X}+1

\displaystyle=4\int S_{2}^{2}dF_{1}-4\int S_{2}dF_{1}+1

\displaystyle=4\tau_{1}-4p+1.

By the same token, it holds

\displaystyle B_{XXY}

\displaystyle=\mathbb{P}(X_{1}<Y,X_{2}<Y)+\mathbb{P}(Y<X_{1},Y<X_{2})-2\mathbb{P}(X_{1}<Y<X_{2})

\displaystyle=\int\mathbb{P}(X_{1}<y,X_{2}<y)dF_{Y}(y)+\int\mathbb{P}(X_{1}>y,X_{2}>y)dF_{Y}(y)-2\int\mathbb{P}(X_{1}<y,X_{2}>y)dF_{Y}(y)

\displaystyle=\int\mathbb{P}(X_{1}<y)\mathbb{P}(X_{2}<y)dF_{Y}(y)+\int\mathbb{P}(X_{1}>y)\mathbb{P}(X_{2}>y)dF_{Y}(y)-2\int\mathbb{P}(X_{1}<y)\mathbb{P}(X_{2}>y)dF_{Y}(y)

\displaystyle=\int\{F_{X}^{-}(y)\}^{2}dF_{Y}(y)+\int\{S_{X}^{-}(y)\}^{2}dF_{Y}(y)-2\int\{F_{X}^{-}(y)S_{X}^{-}(y)\}dF_{Y}(y)

\displaystyle=\int\{F_{X}^{-}(y)-S_{X}^{-}(y)\}^{2}dF_{Y}(y)

\displaystyle=\int\{S_{X}^{-}(y)+\nicefrac{{1}}{{2}}\cdot\mathbb{P}(X=y)-F_{X}^{-}(y)-\nicefrac{{1}}{{2}}\cdot\mathbb{P}(X=y)\}^{2}dF_{Y}(y)

\displaystyle=\int\{F_{X}(y)-S_{X}(y)\}^{2}dF_{Y}(y)

\displaystyle=\int\{2F_{X}(y)-1\}^{2}dF_{Y}(y)

\displaystyle=4\int F_{X}^{2}dF_{Y}-4\int F_{X}dF_{Y}+1

\displaystyle=4\int F_{1}^{2}dF_{2}-4\int F_{1}dF_{2}+1

\displaystyle=4\tau_{2}-4p+1.

Note that in our notation
N_{X}=n_{1}
and
N_{Y}=n_{2}
as well as
A=p
, so that

\displaystyle\sigma_{a}^{2}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{4N_{X}N_{Y}}\left\{\mathbb{P}(X\neq Y)+(N_{X}-1)B_{XXY}+(N_{Y}-1)B_{YYX}-4(N_{X}+N_{Y}-1)(A-\nicefrac{{1}}{{2}})^{2}\right\}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{4n_{1}n_{2}}\left\{1-\beta+(n_{1}-1)(4\tau_{2}-4p+1)+(n_{2}-1)(4\tau_{1}-4p+1)-4(n_{1}+n_{2}-1)(p-\nicefrac{{1}}{{2}})^{2}\right\}

\displaystyle=\frac{(n_{1}-1)\tau_{2}+(n_{2}-1)\tau_{1}-(n_{1}+n_{2}-1)p^{2}}{n_{1}n_{2}}

\displaystyle\phantom{xxxxxxxxxxxxxxx}+\frac{1-\beta-(n_{1}+n_{2}-2)(4p-1)+4(n_{1}+n_{2}-1)(p-\nicefrac{{1}}{{4}})}{4n_{1}n_{2}}

\displaystyle=\frac{(n_{1}-1)\tau_{2}+(n_{2}-1)\tau_{1}-(n_{1}+n_{2}-1)p^{2}}{n_{1}n_{2}}+\frac{4p-\beta}{4n_{1}n_{2}}

\displaystyle=\frac{\tau_{0}+(n_{1}-1)\tau_{2}+(n_{2}-1)\tau_{1}-(n_{1}+n_{2}-1)p^{2}}{n_{1}n_{2}}=\sigma^{2}_{N}.

As for Perme and Manevski [
Perme and Manevski 2019
], they define

\displaystyle\mathbb{V}(\widehat{\theta})

\displaystyle=\frac{\theta(1-\theta)}{mn}+\frac{n-1}{nm}\mathbb{V}(S_{Y}(X))+\frac{m-1}{mn}\mathbb{V}(S_{X}(Y)),

which in our notation should read as

\displaystyle\mathbb{V}(\widehat{p})

\displaystyle=\frac{p(1-p)}{n_{1}n_{2}}+\frac{n_{2}-1}{n_{1}n_{2}}\mathbb{V}(S_{2}^{-}(X_{11}))+\frac{n_{2}-1}{n_{1}n_{2}}\mathbb{V}(S_{1}^{-}(X_{21})),

However, this formula assumes that
F_{X}
and
F_{Y}
are both continuous. Perme and Manevski say as much in the supplementary material, i.e., “For better clarity of all the derivations, we shall assume that both
F_{X}
and
F_{Y}
are continuous (the extension of formulae to the case of ties is then straightforward)”. In the main paper they state “This work focuses on continuous random variables
X
and
Y
. In practice, the data may often be documented on a discrete scale and thus ties can occur. Therefore, we shall always extend the definition to the case of ties.” Nonetheless, Perme and Manevski seemingly do not explicitly set out what they consider to be the variance estimand in case of ties.
In any event, in case of continuity we have

\displaystyle\mathbb{V}(S_{2}^{-}(X_{11}))

\displaystyle=\mathbb{V}(S_{2}(X_{11}))=\mathbb{V}(1-F_{2}(X_{11}))=\mathbb{V}(F_{2}(X_{11}))=\sigma_{1}^{2},

\displaystyle\mathbb{V}(S_{1}^{-}(X_{21}))

\displaystyle=\mathbb{V}(S_{1}(X_{21}))=\mathbb{V}(1-F_{1}(X_{21}))=\mathbb{V}(F_{1}(X_{21}))=\sigma_{2}^{2},

\displaystyle\beta

\displaystyle=0.

Thus Perme and Manevski’s formula is equal to our definition of
\sigma^{2}_{N}
so long as both distributions
F_{1}
and
F_{2}
are continuous.

Variance estimation

Our plug-in estimators for
\tau_{0}
,
\tau_{1}
,
\tau_{2}
,
p^{2}
are given by

\displaystyle\widehat{\tau}_{0}

\displaystyle=\widehat{p}-\nicefrac{{1}}{{4}}\cdot\widehat{\beta},\text{ with }\,\widehat{\beta}=\frac{1}{n_{1}}\frac{1}{n_{2}}\sum_{j=1}^{n_{2}}\sum_{i=1}^{n_{1}}\mathbb{I}(X_{2j}=X_{1i}),\quad\mathbb{I}(X_{2j}=X_{1i})=\begin{cases}1&\text{ if }X_{2j}=X_{1i}\\
0&\text{ if }X_{2j}\neq X_{1i}\end{cases},

\displaystyle\widehat{\tau}_{1}

\displaystyle=\int\widehat{S}_{2}^{2}d\widehat{F}_{1}=\frac{1}{n_{1}}\sum_{i=1}^{n_{1}}\{\widehat{S}_{2}(X_{1i})\}^{2}=\frac{1}{n_{1}}\sum_{i=1}^{n_{1}}\{\frac{1}{n_{2}}\sum_{j=1}^{n_{2}}c(X_{2j},X_{1i})\}^{2},

\displaystyle\widehat{\tau}_{2}

\displaystyle=\int\widehat{F}_{1}^{2}d\widehat{F}_{2}=\frac{1}{n_{2}}\sum_{j=1}^{n_{2}}\{\widehat{F}_{1}(X_{2j})\}^{2}=\frac{1}{n_{2}}\sum_{j=1}^{n_{2}}\{\frac{1}{n_{1}}\sum_{i=1}^{n_{1}}c(X_{2j},X_{1i})\}^{2},

\displaystyle\widehat{p}^{2}

\displaystyle=(\int\widehat{F}_{1}d\widehat{F}_{2})^{2}=\left(\frac{1}{n_{1}}\frac{1}{n_{2}}\sum_{j=1}^{n_{2}}\sum_{i=1}^{n_{1}}c(X_{2j},X_{1i})\right)^{2}.

It can readily be seen that
\mathbb{E}(\widehat{\tau}_{0})=\tau_{0}
since
\widehat{p}
and
\widehat{\beta}
are unbiased.
As regards
\widehat{\tau}_{1}
and
\widehat{\tau}_{2}
, we find

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}(\widehat{\tau}_{1})

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{n_{1}}\sum_{i=1}^{n_{1}}\{\frac{1}{n_{2}}\frac{1}{n_{2}}\sum_{j=1}^{n_{2}}\sum_{j^{\prime}=1}^{n_{2}}\mathbb{E}(c(X_{2j},X_{1i})c(X_{2j^{\prime}},X_{1i}))\}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{n_{1}}\frac{1}{n_{2}}\frac{1}{n_{2}}\sum_{i=1}^{n_{1}}\sum_{j=1}^{n_{2}}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}j^{\prime}=1\\
j^{\prime}\neq j\end{subarray}}^{n_{2}}\mathbb{E}(c(X_{2j},X_{1i})c(X_{2j^{\prime}},X_{1i}))+\frac{1}{n_{1}}\frac{1}{n_{2}}\frac{1}{n_{2}}\sum_{i=1}^{n_{1}}\sum_{j=1}^{n_{2}}\mathbb{E}(c(X_{2j},X_{1i})c(X_{2j},X_{1i}))

\displaystyle=\frac{n_{2}-1}{n_{2}}\int S_{2}^{2}dF_{1}+\frac{1}{n_{2}}\mathbb{E}(c(X_{21},X_{11})^{2})=\frac{n_{2}-1}{n_{2}}\tau_{1}+\frac{1}{n_{2}}\tau_{0}.

In a similar vein, it follows

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}(\widehat{\tau}_{2})

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{n_{2}}\sum_{j=1}^{n_{2}}\{\frac{1}{n_{1}}\frac{1}{n_{1}}\sum_{i=1}^{n_{1}}\sum_{i^{\prime}=1}^{n_{1}}\mathbb{E}(c(X_{2j},X_{1i})c(X_{2j},X_{1i^{\prime}}))\}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{n_{2}}\frac{1}{n_{1}}\frac{1}{n_{1}}\sum_{j=1}^{n_{2}}\sum_{i=1}^{n_{1}}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}i^{\prime}=1\\
i^{\prime}\neq i\end{subarray}}^{n_{1}}\mathbb{E}(c(X_{2j},X_{1i})c(X_{2j},X_{1i^{\prime}}))+\frac{1}{n_{2}}\frac{1}{n_{1}}\frac{1}{n_{1}}\sum_{j=1}^{n_{2}}\sum_{i=1}^{n_{1}}\mathbb{E}(c(X_{2j},X_{1i})c(X_{2j},X_{1i}))

\displaystyle=\frac{n_{1}-1}{n_{1}}\int F_{1}^{2}dF_{2}+\frac{1}{n_{1}}\mathbb{E}(c(X_{21},X_{11})^{2})=\frac{n_{1}-1}{n_{1}}\tau_{2}+\frac{1}{n_{1}}\tau_{0}.

As for
\widehat{p}^{2}
, we now look to

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}(\widehat{p}^{2})

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}(\frac{1}{n_{1}}\frac{1}{n_{2}}\sum_{j=1}^{n_{2}}\sum_{i=1}^{n_{1}}c(X_{2j},X_{1i})\frac{1}{n_{1}}\frac{1}{n_{2}}\sum_{j^{\prime}=1}^{n_{2}}\sum_{i^{\prime}=1}^{n_{1}}c(X_{2j^{\prime}},X_{1i^{\prime}}))

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{n_{1}}\frac{1}{n_{2}}\frac{1}{n_{1}}\frac{1}{n_{2}}\sum_{j=1}^{n_{2}}\sum_{i=1}^{n_{1}}\sum_{j^{\prime}=1}^{n_{2}}\sum_{i^{\prime}=1}^{n_{1}}\mathbb{E}(\underbrace{c(X_{2j},X_{1i})}_{\eqqcolon\zeta_{ij}}\underbrace{c(X_{2j^{\prime}},X_{1i^{\prime}})}_{\eqqcolon\zeta_{i^{\prime}j^{\prime}}})

Looking at the quadruple sum produces

\displaystyle\sum_{j^{\prime}=1}^{n_{2}}\sum_{i^{\prime}=1}^{n_{1}}\sum_{j=1}^{n_{2}}\sum_{i=1}^{n_{1}}\mathbb{E}(\zeta_{ij}\zeta_{i^{\prime}j^{\prime}})

\displaystyle\hskip 10.0pt=\sum_{j^{\prime}=1}^{n_{2}}\sum_{i^{\prime}=1}^{n_{1}}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}j=1\\
j\neq j^{\prime}\end{subarray}}^{n_{2}}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}i=1\\

Therefore, we have

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}(\widehat{p}^{2})=\frac{(n_{2}-1)(n_{1}-1)}{n_{1}n_{2}}p^{2}+\frac{n_{2}-1}{n_{1}n_{2}}\tau_{1}+\frac{n_{1}-1}{n_{1}n_{2}}\tau_{2}+\frac{1}{n_{1}n_{2}}\tau_{0}.

An unbiased estimator of
\sigma_{N}^{2}
should then take the form

\displaystyle\widehat{\sigma}^{2}_{N}=\frac{n_{2}\widehat{\tau}_{1}+n_{1}\widehat{\tau}_{2}-\widehat{\tau}_{0}-(n_{1}+n_{2}-1)\widehat{p}^{2}}{(n_{1}-1)(n_{2}-1)}.

To check the unbiasedness of
\sigma_{N}^{2}
, consider

\displaystyle(n_{1}

\displaystyle-1)(n_{2}-1)\mathbb{E}(\widehat{\sigma}_{N}^{2})

\displaystyle=n_{2}\mathbb{E}(\widehat{\tau}_{1})+n_{1}\mathbb{E}(\widehat{\tau}_{2})-\mathbb{E}(\widehat{\tau}_{0})-(n_{1}+n_{2}-1)\mathbb{E}(\widehat{p}^{2})

\displaystyle=(n_{2}-1)\tau_{1}+\tau_{0}+(n_{1}-1)\tau_{2}+\tau_{0}-\tau_{0}

\displaystyle\hskip 90.0pt-\frac{n_{1}+n_{2}-1}{n_{1}n_{2}}\{(n_{1}-1)(n_{2}-1)p^{2}+(n_{2}-1)\tau_{1}+(n_{1}-1)\tau_{2}+\tau_{0}\}

\displaystyle=\left(1-\frac{n_{1}+n_{2}-1}{n_{1}n_{2}}\right)\left\{\tau_{0}+(n_{2}-1)\tau_{1}+(n_{1}-1)\tau_{2}\right\}-\frac{(n_{1}-1)(n_{2}-1)}{n_{1}n_{2}}(n_{1}+n_{2}-1)p^{2}.

Now since
\left(1-\frac{n_{1}+n_{2}-1}{n_{1}n_{2}}\right)=\frac{(n_{1}-1)(n_{2}-1)}{n_{1}n_{2}}
, it follows that

\mathbb{E}(\widehat{\sigma}_{N}^{2})=\sigma^{2}_{N}

Brunner form of the unbiased variance estimator

Now we want to have a closer look at the estimator in (2.39) derived as in the unpublished preprint of
Brunner et al. 2021a
,

\displaystyle\widehat{\sigma}_{N}^{2}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{n_{1}(n_{1}-1)n_{2}(n_{2}-1)}\left(\sum_{i=1}^{2}\sum_{k=1}^{n_{i}}\left(R_{ik}-R_{ik}^{(i)}-\left[\bar{R}_{i\bullet}-\frac{n_{i}+1}{2}\right]\right)^{2}-n_{1}n_{2}\left[\widehat{\theta}(1-\widehat{\theta})-\frac{1}{4}\widehat{\beta}\right]\right),

where
\widehat{\theta}=\int\widehat{F}_{2}d\widehat{F}_{1}=1-\widehat{p}

First recall the following identities as regards the rank representations,

\displaystyle n_{2}\widehat{F}_{2}(X_{1i})

\displaystyle=R_{1i}-R_{1i}^{(1)},

\displaystyle 1-\widehat{p}=\int\widehat{F}_{2}d\widehat{F}_{1}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{n_{1}}\sum_{i=1}^{n_{1}}\widehat{F}_{2}(X_{1i})=\frac{1}{n_{2}}(\bar{R}_{1\bullet}-\frac{n_{1}+1}{2}),

\displaystyle n_{1}\widehat{F}_{1}(X_{2j})

\displaystyle=R_{2j}-R_{2j}^{(2)},

\displaystyle\widehat{p}=\int\widehat{F}_{1}d\widehat{F}_{2}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{n_{2}}\sum_{j=1}^{n_{2}}\widehat{F}_{1}(X_{2j})=\frac{1}{n_{1}}(\bar{R}_{2\bullet}-\frac{n_{2}+1}{2}),

where
R_{1i}^{(1)}
and
R_{2j}^{(2)}
denote the so-called internal ranks with respect to the first and second sample. That is to say

is the mid-rank of

whereas

is the mid-rank of
X_{2j}

X_{21},\dots,X_{2n_{2}}

Together with the equality

\displaystyle\int\widehat{F}_{2}^{2}d\widehat{F}_{1}-(\int\widehat{F}_{2}d\widehat{F}_{1})^{2}

\displaystyle=\int(1-\widehat{S}_{2})^{2}d\widehat{F}_{1}-(1-\int\widehat{S}_{2}d\widehat{F}_{1})^{2}

\displaystyle V_{i\bullet}

\displaystyle\widehat{\mathbb{V}}(S_{Y}(X))

\displaystyle\widehat{\mathbb{V}}(S_{X}(Y))

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{n_{2}-1}\sum_{j=1}^{n_{2}}(\widehat{F}_{1}(X_{2j})-\widehat{p})^{2}=\frac{1}{n_{2}-1}\sum_{j=1}^{n_{2}}(\widehat{F}_{1}^{2}(X_{2j})-2\widehat{p}\widehat{F}_{1}(X_{2j})+\widehat{p}^{2})

in other words, the variance estimator proposed by DeLong et al. (

Brunner and Munzel 2000
).

exact

\displaystyle\widehat{\mathbb{V}}_{DLe}(\widehat{\theta})=\widehat{\mathbb{V}}_{DLe}(\widehat{p})

\displaystyle=\frac{\widehat{p}(1-\widehat{p})+(n_{2}-1)\widehat{\sigma}_{1}^{2}+(n_{1}-1)\widehat{\sigma}_{2}^{2}}{n_{1}n_{2}}

\displaystyle\zeta_{21}

\displaystyle\zeta_{12}

\displaystyle\zeta_{22}

\displaystyle=\theta^{2}=(\int F_{1}^{+}dF_{2})^{2}.

\displaystyle\sigma^{2}

To begin with, we summarise the pertinent results of Box-type [

First, we consider independent normal random variables

\bar{\mathbf{X}}=(\sum_{i=1}^{n_{1}}X_{1i}/n_{1}\;\;\;\sum_{j=1}^{n_{2}}X_{2j}/n_{2})^{\top}

\displaystyle\mathbf{S}_{N}

\displaystyle=\mathbb{C}\mathrm{ov}(\sqrt{N}\bar{\mathbf{X}})=\bigoplus_{i=1}^{2}N\sigma_{i}^{2}/n_{i}=N\begin{pmatrix}\sigma_{1}^{2}/n_{1}&0\\
0&\sigma_{2}^{2}/n_{2}\\

\displaystyle=N\begin{pmatrix}\widehat{\sigma}_{1}^{2}/n_{1}&0\\
0&\widehat{\sigma}_{2}^{2}/n_{2}\\
\end{pmatrix},

where
\widehat{\sigma}_{g}^{2}=\frac{1}{n_{g}-1}\sum_{i=1}^{n_{g}}(X_{gi}-\bar{X}_{g\bullet})^{2}
,

.
Furthermore we will need

\displaystyle\mathbf{\Lambda}=\left(\begin{pmatrix}n_{1}&0\\
0&n_{2}\\
\end{pmatrix}-\begin{pmatrix}1&0\\
0&1\\
\end{pmatrix}\right)^{-1}=\begin{pmatrix}1/(n_{1}-1)&0\\
0&1/(n_{2}-1)\\
\end{pmatrix}.

As for the contrast matrix (centering matrix), we have

\displaystyle\mathbf{C}=\begin{pmatrix}1&0\\
0&1\\
\end{pmatrix}-\lx@nobreakspace\frac{1}{2}\begin{pmatrix}1&1\\
1&1\\
\end{pmatrix}=\frac{1}{2}\begin{pmatrix}1&-1\\
-1&1\\
\end{pmatrix},

resulting in

\displaystyle\mathbf{T}

\displaystyle=\mathbf{C}^{\top}(\mathbf{C}\mathbf{C}^{\top})^{-}\mathbf{C}=\mathbf{C},

\displaystyle\mathbf{D}_{T}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\begin{pmatrix}1&0\\
0&1\\
\end{pmatrix},

since
\mathbf{C}
is positive semi-definite, symmetric and idempotent.

Then the approximate degrees of freedom for the two sample
t
test are given by

\displaystyle f_{0}

\displaystyle=\left[{\rm tr}\left(\mathbf{D}_{T}\mathbf{S}_{N}\right)\right]^{2}/{\rm tr}\left(\mathbf{D}_{T}^{2}\mathbf{S}_{N}^{2}\mathbf{\Lambda}\right),

\displaystyle\widehat{f}_{0}

\displaystyle=\left[{\rm tr}\left(\mathbf{D}_{T}\widehat{\mathbf{S}}_{N}\right)\right]^{2}/{\rm tr}\left(\mathbf{D}_{T}^{2}\widehat{\mathbf{S}}_{N}^{2}\mathbf{\Lambda}\right),

which then simplifies to

\displaystyle\mathbf{D}_{T}\widehat{\mathbf{S}}_{N}

\displaystyle=\frac{N}{2}\begin{pmatrix}1&0\\
0&1\end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix}\widehat{\sigma}_{1}^{2}/n_{1}&0\\
0&\widehat{\sigma}_{2}^{2}/n_{2}\end{pmatrix}=\frac{N}{2}\begin{pmatrix}\widehat{\sigma}_{1}^{2}/n_{1}&0\\
0&\widehat{\sigma}_{2}^{2}/n_{2}\end{pmatrix},

\displaystyle\left[{\rm tr}\left(\mathbf{D}_{T}\widehat{\mathbf{S}}_{N}\right)\right]^{2}

\displaystyle=\frac{N^{2}}{4}\left(\widehat{\sigma}_{1}^{2}/n_{1}+\widehat{\sigma}_{2}^{2}/n_{2}\right)^{2},

\displaystyle=\frac{N^{2}}{4}\begin{pmatrix}1&0\\

0&1/(n_{2}-1)\\
\end{pmatrix}

\displaystyle=\frac{N^{2}}{4}\left(\frac{\widehat{\sigma}_{1}^{4}}{n_{1}^{2}(n_{1}-1)}+\frac{\widehat{\sigma}_{2}^{4}}{n_{2}^{2}(n_{2}-1)}\right),

yielding

\widehat{f}_{0}=\left(\widehat{\sigma}_{1}^{2}/n_{1}+\widehat{\sigma}_{2}^{2}/n_{2}\right)^{2}/\left(\frac{\widehat{\sigma}_{1}^{4}}{n_{1}^{2}(n_{1}-1)}+\frac{\widehat{\sigma}_{2}^{4}}{n_{2}^{2}(n_{2}-1)}\right)
.

For the degrees of freedom proposed by Brunner and Munzel, the derivation is completely analogous. To this end, consider

X_{11},\dots,X_{1n_{1}}\sim F_{1}
as well as

X_{21},\dots,X_{2n_{2}}\sim F_{2}
,
N=n_{1}+n_{2}
,
\bar{\mathbf{X}}=(\sum_{i=1}^{n_{1}}F_{2}(X_{1i})/n_{1}\;\;\;\sum_{j=1}^{n_{2}}F_{1}(X_{2j})/n_{2})^{\top}
. Then we have

\displaystyle\mathbf{S}_{N}

\displaystyle=\mathbb{C}\mathrm{ov}(\sqrt{N}\bar{\mathbf{X}})=\bigoplus_{i=1}^{2}N\sigma_{i}^{2}/n_{i}=N\begin{pmatrix}\sigma_{1}^{2}/n_{1}&0\\
0&\sigma_{2}^{2}/n_{2}\\
\end{pmatrix},

\displaystyle\widehat{\mathbf{S}}_{N}

\displaystyle=N\begin{pmatrix}\widehat{\sigma}_{1}^{2}/n_{1}&0\\
0&\widehat{\sigma}_{2}^{2}/n_{2}\\
\end{pmatrix},

with variances
\sigma_{1}^{2}=\mathbb{V}(F_{2}(X_{11}))=\tau_{1}-p^{2}
,
\sigma_{2}^{2}=\mathbb{V}(F_{1}(X_{21}))=\tau_{2}-p^{2}
, and estimators

\widehat{\sigma}_{1}^{2}=\frac{n_{1}}{n_{1}-1}(\widehat{\tau}_{1}-\widehat{p}^{2})
and
\widehat{\sigma}_{2}^{2}=\frac{n_{2}}{n_{2}-1}(\widehat{\tau}_{2}-\widehat{p}^{2})
, yielding
\widehat{f}_{0}=\left(\widehat{\sigma}_{1}^{2}/n_{1}+\widehat{\sigma}_{2}^{2}/n_{2}\right)^{2}/\left(\frac{\widehat{\sigma}_{1}^{4}}{n_{1}^{2}(n_{1}-1)}+\frac{\widehat{\sigma}_{2}^{4}}{n_{2}^{2}(n_{2}-1)}\right)
.

The idea behind the newly adjusted degrees of freedom is to derive them similarly as for the Brunner-Munzel [
Brunner and Munzel 2000
] test, but using the empirical distribution functions to compute the mean vector instead of the theoretical ones, i.e.,
\bar{\mathbf{X}}=(\sum_{i=1}^{n_{1}}\widehat{F}_{2}(X_{1i})/n_{1}\;\;\;\sum_{j=1}^{n_{2}}\widehat{F}_{1}(X_{2j})/n_{2})^{\top}
.

To obtain the entries of
\mathbf{S}_{N}=\mathbb{C}\mathrm{ov}(\sqrt{N}\bar{\mathbf{X}})
, we will first consider the following quantities

\displaystyle\psi_{1}^{2}

\displaystyle=\mathbb{V}(\widehat{F}_{2}(X_{11}))=\mathbb{V}(1-\widehat{S}_{2}(X_{11}))=\mathbb{V}(\widehat{S}_{2}(X_{11}))=\mathbb{E}((\widehat{S}_{2}(X_{11}))^{2})-(\mathbb{E}(\widehat{S}_{2}(X_{11})))^{2},

\displaystyle\psi_{1\mid 1}

\displaystyle=\mathbb{C}\mathrm{ov}(\widehat{F}_{2}(X_{11}),\widehat{F}_{2}(X_{12}))=\mathbb{C}\mathrm{ov}(\widehat{S}_{2}(X_{11}),\widehat{S}_{2}(X_{12})),

\displaystyle\psi_{2}^{2}

\displaystyle=\mathbb{V}(\widehat{F}_{1}(X_{21}))=\mathbb{E}((\widehat{F}_{1}(X_{21}))^{2})-(\mathbb{E}(\widehat{F}_{1}(X_{21})))^{2},

\displaystyle\psi_{2\mid 2}

\displaystyle=\mathbb{C}\mathrm{ov}(\widehat{F}_{1}(X_{21}),\widehat{F}_{1}(X_{21})),

\displaystyle\psi_{12}

\displaystyle=\mathbb{C}\mathrm{ov}(\widehat{F}_{2}(X_{11}),\widehat{F}_{1}(X_{21}))=-\mathbb{C}\mathrm{ov}(\widehat{S}_{2}(X_{11}),\widehat{F}_{1}(X_{21}))

\displaystyle=-\mathbb{E}(\widehat{S}_{2}(X_{11}),\widehat{F}_{1}(X_{21}))+\mathbb{E}(\widehat{S}_{2}(X_{11}))\mathbb{E}(\widehat{F}_{1}(X_{21})).

So we now consider

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}(\widehat{S}_{2}(X_{11}))

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{n_{2}}\sum_{\ell=1}^{n_{2}}\mathbb{E}(c(X_{2\ell},X_{11}))=\int F_{1}dF_{2}=p,

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}(\widehat{F}_{1}(X_{21}))

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{n_{1}}\sum_{k=1}^{n_{1}}\mathbb{E}(c(X_{21},X_{1k}))=\int F_{1}dF_{2}=p.

Moreover,

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}((\widehat{S}_{2}(X_{11}))^{2})

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{n_{2}^{2}}\sum_{\ell=1}^{n_{2}}\sum_{\ell^{\prime}=1}^{n_{2}}\mathbb{E}(c(X_{2\ell},X_{11})c(X_{2\ell^{\prime}},X_{11}))

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{n_{2}^{2}}\sum_{\ell=1}^{n_{2}}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\ell^{\prime}=1\\
\ell^{\prime}\neq\ell\end{subarray}}^{n_{2}}\mathbb{E}(c(X_{2\ell},X_{11})c(X_{2\ell^{\prime}},X_{11}))+\frac{1}{n_{2}^{2}}\sum_{\ell=1}^{n_{2}}\mathbb{E}(c(X_{2\ell},X_{11})c(X_{2\ell},X_{11}))

\displaystyle=\frac{n_{2}-1}{n_{2}}\int S_{2}^{2}dF_{1}+\frac{1}{n_{2}}\mathbb{E}(c(X_{21},X_{11})^{2})=\frac{n_{2}-1}{n_{2}}\tau_{1}+\frac{1}{n_{2}}\tau_{0},

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}(\widehat{S}_{2}(X_{11})\widehat{S}_{2}(X_{12}))

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{n_{2}^{2}}\sum_{\ell=1}^{n_{2}}\sum_{\ell^{\prime}=1}^{n_{2}}\mathbb{E}(c(X_{2\ell},X_{11})c(X_{2\ell^{\prime}},X_{12}))

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{n_{2}^{2}}\sum_{\ell=1}^{n_{2}}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\ell^{\prime}=1\\
\ell^{\prime}\neq\ell\end{subarray}}^{n_{2}}\mathbb{E}(c(X_{2\ell},X_{11})c(X_{2\ell^{\prime}},X_{12}))+\frac{1}{n_{2}^{2}}\sum_{\ell=1}^{n_{2}}\mathbb{E}(c(X_{2\ell},X_{11})c(X_{2\ell},X_{12}))

\displaystyle=\frac{n_{2}-1}{n_{2}}p^{2}-\frac{1}{n_{2}}\int F_{1}^{2}dF_{2}=\frac{n_{2}-1}{n_{2}}p^{2}+\frac{1}{n_{2}}\tau_{2},

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}((\widehat{F}_{1}(X_{21}))^{2})

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{n_{1}^{2}}\sum_{k=1}^{n_{1}}\sum_{k^{\prime}=1}^{n_{1}}\mathbb{E}(c(X_{21},X_{1k})c(X_{21},X_{1k^{\prime}}))

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{n_{1}^{2}}\sum_{k=1}^{n_{1}}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}k^{\prime}=1\\
k^{\prime}\neq k\end{subarray}}^{n_{1}}\mathbb{E}(c(X_{21},X_{1k})c(X_{21},X_{1k^{\prime}}))+\frac{1}{n_{1}^{2}}\sum_{k=1}^{n_{1}}\mathbb{E}(c(X_{21},X_{1k})c(X_{21},X_{1k}))

\displaystyle=\frac{n_{1}-1}{n_{1}}\int F_{1}^{2}dF_{2}+\frac{1}{n_{2}}\mathbb{E}(c(X_{21},X_{11})^{2})=\frac{n_{1}-1}{n_{1}}\tau_{2}+\frac{1}{n_{1}}\tau_{0},

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}(\widehat{F}_{1}(X_{21})\widehat{F}_{1}(X_{22}))

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{n_{1}^{2}}\sum_{k=1}^{n_{1}}\sum_{k^{\prime}=1}^{n_{1}}\mathbb{E}(c(X_{21},X_{1k})c(X_{22},X_{1k^{\prime}}))

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{n_{1}^{2}}\sum_{k=1}^{n_{1}}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}k^{\prime}=1\\
k^{\prime}\neq k\end{subarray}}^{n_{1}}\mathbb{E}(c(X_{21},X_{1k})c(X_{22},X_{1k^{\prime}}))+\frac{1}{n_{1}^{2}}\sum_{k=1}^{n_{1}}\mathbb{E}(c(X_{21},X_{1k})c(X_{22},X_{1k}))

\displaystyle=\frac{n_{1}-1}{n_{1}}p^{2}dF_{2}+\frac{1}{n_{2}}\int S_{2}^{2}dF_{1}=\frac{n_{1}-1}{n_{1}}p_{2}+\frac{1}{n_{1}}\tau_{1},

as well as

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}(\widehat{S}_{2}(X_{11}),\widehat{F}_{1}(X_{21}))

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{n_{1}n_{2}}\sum_{k=1}^{n_{1}}\sum_{\ell=1}^{n_{2}}\mathbb{E}(c(X_{2\ell},X_{11})c(X_{21},X_{1k}))

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{n_{1}n_{2}}\sum_{k=2}^{n_{1}}\sum_{\ell=2}^{n_{2}}\mathbb{E}(c(X_{2\ell},X_{11})c(X_{21},X_{1k}))+\frac{1}{n_{1}n_{2}}\mathbb{E}(c(X_{21},X_{11})c(X_{21},X_{11}))

\displaystyle\phantom{xx}+\frac{1}{n_{1}n_{2}}\sum_{k=2}^{n_{1}}\mathbb{E}(c(X_{21},X_{11})c(X_{21},X_{1k}))+\frac{1}{n_{1}n_{2}}\sum_{\ell=2}^{n_{2}}\mathbb{E}(c(X_{2\ell},X_{11})c(X_{21},X_{11}))

\displaystyle=\frac{(n_{1}-1)(n_{2}-1)p^{2}+\tau_{0}+(n_{1}-1)\tau_{2}+(n_{2}-1)\tau_{1}}{n_{1}n_{2}}.

Collecting terms, we have

\displaystyle\psi_{1}^{2}

\displaystyle=\frac{n_{2}-1}{n_{2}}\tau_{1}+\frac{1}{n_{2}}p-\frac{1}{4n_{2}}\beta-p^{2}=\frac{1}{n_{2}}\left[(n_{2}-1)(\tau_{1}-p^{2})+p-p^{2}-\frac{1}{4}\beta\right]

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{n_{2}}\left[(n_{2}-1)\sigma_{1}^{2}+p(1-p)-\frac{1}{4}\beta\right],

\displaystyle\psi_{2}^{2}

\displaystyle=\frac{n_{1}-1}{n_{1}}\tau_{2}+\frac{1}{n_{1}}p-\frac{1}{4n_{1}}\beta-p^{2}=\frac{1}{n_{1}}\left[(n_{1}-1)(\tau_{2}-p^{2})+p-p^{2}-\frac{1}{4}\beta\right]

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{n_{1}}\left[(n_{1}-1)\sigma_{2}^{2}+p(1-p)-\frac{1}{4}\beta\right],

\displaystyle\psi_{1\mid 1}

\displaystyle=\frac{n_{2}-1}{n_{2}}p^{2}+\frac{1}{n_{2}}\tau_{2}-p^{2}=\frac{1}{n_{2}}(\tau_{2}-p^{2})=\frac{1}{n_{2}}\sigma_{2}^{2},

\displaystyle\psi_{2\mid 2}

\displaystyle=\frac{n_{1}-1}{n_{1}}p^{2}+\frac{1}{n_{1}}\tau_{1}-p^{2}=\frac{1}{n_{1}}(\tau_{1}-p^{2})=\frac{1}{n_{1}}\sigma_{1}^{2},

which are the same terms as in equations (2.32), (2.33), (2.34), and (2.35) of Brunner et al. [
Brunner et al. 2021a
] multiplied by either
n_{2}^{2}
or
n_{1}^{2}
. Now we turn to

\displaystyle\psi_{12}

\displaystyle=p^{2}-\frac{(n_{1}-1)(n_{2}-1)p^{2}+\tau_{0}+(n_{1}-1)\tau_{2}+(n_{2}-1)\tau_{1}}{n_{1}n_{2}}

\displaystyle=-\frac{(n_{1}-1)\tau_{2}+(n_{2}-1)\tau_{1}+\tau_{0}-(n_{1}+n_{2}-1)p^{2}}{n_{1}n_{2}}=-\sigma_{N}^{2}=-\mathbb{V}(\widehat{p}),

Now, since

\displaystyle\mathbb{V}(\frac{1}{n_{1}}\sum_{i=1}^{n_{1}}\widehat{F}_{2}(X_{1i}))

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{n_{1}^{2}}\sum_{i=1}^{n_{1}}\mathbb{V}(\widehat{F}_{2}(X_{1i}))+\frac{1}{n_{1}^{2}}\sum_{k=1}^{n_{1}}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}k^{\prime}=1\\
k^{\prime}\neq k\end{subarray}}^{n_{1}}\mathbb{C}\mathrm{ov}(\widehat{F}_{2}(X_{1k}),\widehat{F}_{2}(X_{1k^{\prime}}))

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{n_{1}^{2}}n_{1}\psi_{1}^{2}+\frac{1}{n_{1}^{2}}n_{1}(n_{1}-1)\psi_{1\mid 1}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{n_{1}n_{2}}\left[(n_{2}-1)\sigma_{1}^{2}+p(1-p)-\frac{1}{4}\beta\right]+\frac{n_{1}-1}{n_{1}n_{2}}\sigma_{2}^{2}=\sigma_{N}^{2},

\displaystyle\mathbb{V}(\frac{1}{n_{2}}\sum_{j=1}^{n_{2}}\widehat{F}_{1}(X_{2j}))

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{n_{2}^{2}}\sum_{j=1}^{n_{2}}\mathbb{V}(\widehat{F}_{1}(X_{2j}))+\frac{1}{n_{2}^{2}}\sum_{\ell=1}^{n_{2}}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\ell^{\prime}=1\\
\ell^{\prime}\neq\ell\end{subarray}}^{n_{2}}\mathbb{C}\mathrm{ov}(\widehat{F}_{1}(X_{2\ell}),\widehat{F}_{1}(X_{2\ell^{\prime}}))

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{n_{2}^{2}}n_{2}\psi_{2}^{2}+\frac{1}{n_{2}^{2}}n_{2}(n_{2}-1)\psi_{2\mid 2}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{n_{1}n_{2}}\left[(n_{1}-1)\sigma_{2}^{2}+p(1-p)-\frac{1}{4}\beta\right]+\frac{n_{2}-1}{n_{1}n_{2}}\sigma_{1}^{2}=\sigma_{N}^{2},

and finally

\displaystyle\mathbb{C}\mathrm{ov}(\frac{1}{n_{1}}\sum_{i=1}^{n_{1}}\widehat{F}_{2}(X_{1i}),\frac{1}{n_{2}}\sum_{j=1}^{n_{2}}\widehat{F}_{1}(X_{2j}))=\frac{1}{n_{1}n_{2}}\sum_{i=1}^{n_{1}}\sum_{j=1}^{n_{2}}\mathbb{C}\mathrm{ov}(\widehat{F}_{2}(X_{1i}),\widehat{F}_{1}(X_{2j}))=\frac{n_{1}n_{2}}{n_{1}n_{2}}\psi_{12}=-\sigma_{N}^{2}.

Therefore, we have

\displaystyle\mathbf{S}_{N}

\displaystyle=\mathbb{C}\mathrm{ov}(\sqrt{N}\bar{\mathbf{X}})=N\sigma_{N}^{2}\begin{pmatrix}1&-1\\
-1&1\\
\end{pmatrix},

\displaystyle\widehat{\mathbf{S}}_{N}

\displaystyle=N\widehat{\sigma}_{N}^{2}\begin{pmatrix}1&-1\\
-1&1\\
\end{pmatrix}.

If we are to derive the degrees of freedom in an analogous manner as before, we should get

\displaystyle\mathbf{D}_{T}\widehat{\mathbf{S}}_{N}

\displaystyle=\frac{N\widehat{\sigma}_{N}^{2}}{2}\begin{pmatrix}1&0\\
0&1\end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix}1&-1\\
-1&1\end{pmatrix}=\frac{N\widehat{\sigma}_{N}^{2}}{2}\begin{pmatrix}1&-1\\
-1&1\end{pmatrix}

\displaystyle\left[{\rm tr}\left(\mathbf{D}_{T}\widehat{\mathbf{S}}_{N}\right)\right]^{2}

\displaystyle=N^{2}\widehat{\sigma}_{N}^{4},

\displaystyle\mathbf{D}_{T}^{2}\widehat{\mathbf{S}}_{N}^{2}\mathbf{\Lambda}

\displaystyle=\frac{N^{2}\widehat{\sigma}_{N}^{4}}{4}\begin{pmatrix}1&0\\
0&1\end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix}2&-2\\
-2&2\end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix}1/(n_{1}-1)&0\\
0&1/(n_{2}-1)\\
\end{pmatrix}

\displaystyle=\frac{N^{2}\widehat{\sigma}_{N}^{4}}{2}\begin{pmatrix}1/(n_{1}-1)&-1/(n_{2}-1)\\
-1/(n_{1}-1)&1/(n_{2}-1)\\
\end{pmatrix}

\displaystyle{\rm tr}\left(\mathbf{D}_{T}^{2}\widehat{\mathbf{S}}_{N}^{2}\mathbf{\Lambda}\right)

\displaystyle=\frac{N^{2}\widehat{\sigma}_{N}^{4}}{2}\left(\frac{1}{n_{1}-1}+\frac{1}{n_{2}-1}\right),

yielding

\widehat{f}_{0}=2/\left(\frac{1}{n_{1}-1}+\frac{1}{n_{2}-1}\right)
.

Furthermore, we now consider a simple heuristic alternative which may be viewed as a middle ground of the Brunner-Munzel approach and the approach just developed. To this end, recall the Brunner form of the unbiased variance estimator, i.e.,

\displaystyle\widehat{\sigma}_{N}^{2}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{n_{1}(n_{1}-1)n_{2}(n_{2}-1)}\left(\sum_{i=1}^{2}\sum_{k=1}^{n_{i}}\left(R_{ik}-R_{ik}^{(i)}-\left[\bar{R}_{i\bullet}-\frac{n_{i}+1}{2}\right]\right)^{2}-n_{1}n_{2}\left[\widehat{\theta}(1-\widehat{\theta})-\frac{1}{4}\widehat{\beta}\right]\right).

We now split this unbiased estimator into two “symmetric” parts,

\displaystyle\widehat{\sigma}_{1\mid N}^{2}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{n_{1}(n_{1}-1)n_{2}(n_{2}-1)}\left(\sum_{k=1}^{n_{1}}\left(R_{1k}-R_{1k}^{(1)}-\left[\bar{R}_{1\bullet}-\frac{n_{1}+1}{2}\right]\right)^{2}-\frac{1}{2}n_{1}n_{2}\left[\widehat{\theta}(1-\widehat{\theta})-\frac{1}{4}\widehat{\beta}\right]\right),

\displaystyle\widehat{\sigma}_{2\mid N}^{2}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{n_{1}(n_{1}-1)n_{2}(n_{2}-1)}\left(\sum_{\ell=1}^{n_{2}}\left(R_{2\ell}-R_{2\ell}^{(2)}-\left[\bar{R}_{2\bullet}-\frac{n_{2}+1}{2}\right]\right)^{2}-\frac{1}{2}n_{1}n_{2}\left[\widehat{\theta}(1-\widehat{\theta})-\frac{1}{4}\widehat{\beta}\right]\right),

such that
\widehat{\sigma}_{1\mid N}^{2}+\widehat{\sigma}_{2\mid N}^{2}=\widehat{\sigma}_{N}^{2}
. Moreover, we artificially set the covariances zero so that now we have

\displaystyle\widehat{\mathbf{S}}_{N}

\displaystyle=N\begin{pmatrix}\widehat{\sigma}_{1\mid N}^{2}&0\\
0&\widehat{\sigma}_{2\mid N}^{2}\\
\end{pmatrix}.

With this artificial covariance matrix, we now compute the degrees of freedom similar to before,

\displaystyle\mathbf{D}_{T}\widehat{\mathbf{S}}_{N}

\displaystyle=\frac{N}{2}\begin{pmatrix}1&0\\
0&1\end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix}\widehat{\sigma}_{1\mid N}^{2}&0\\
0&\widehat{\sigma}_{2\mid N}^{2}\\
\end{pmatrix}=\frac{N}{2}\begin{pmatrix}\widehat{\sigma}_{1\mid N}^{2}&0\\
0&\widehat{\sigma}_{2\mid N}^{2}\\
\end{pmatrix},

\displaystyle\left[{\rm tr}\left(\mathbf{D}_{T}\widehat{\mathbf{S}}_{N}\right)\right]^{2}

\displaystyle=\frac{N^{2}}{4}\left(\widehat{\sigma}_{1\mid N}^{2}+\widehat{\sigma}_{2\mid N}^{2}\right)^{2},

\displaystyle\mathbf{D}_{T}^{2}\widehat{\mathbf{S}}_{N}^{2}\mathbf{\Lambda}

\displaystyle=\frac{N^{2}}{4}\begin{pmatrix}1&0\\
0&1\end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix}\widehat{\sigma}_{1\mid N}^{4}&0\\
0&\widehat{\sigma}_{2\mid N}^{4}\\
\end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix}1/(n_{1}-1)&0\\
0&1/(n_{2}-1)\\
\end{pmatrix}

\displaystyle{\rm tr}\left(\mathbf{D}_{T}^{2}\widehat{\mathbf{S}}_{N}^{2}\mathbf{\Lambda}\right)

\displaystyle=\frac{N^{2}}{4}\left(\widehat{\sigma}_{1\mid N}^{4}/(n_{1}-1)+\widehat{\sigma}_{2\mid N}^{4}/(n_{2}-1)\right),

yielding

\widehat{f}_{0}=\left(\widehat{\sigma}_{1\mid N}^{2}+\widehat{\sigma}_{2\mid N}^{2}\right)^{2}/\left(\widehat{\sigma}_{1\mid N}^{4}/(n_{1}-1)+\widehat{\sigma}_{2\mid N}^{4}/(n_{2}-1)\right)=\widehat{\sigma}_{N}^{4}/\left(\widehat{\sigma}_{1\mid N}^{4}/(n_{1}-1)+\widehat{\sigma}_{2\mid N}^{4}/(n_{2}-1)\right)
.

Part II – Simulations

Now, we will briefly discuss how we dealt with cases where the variance estimates turned out to be zero or negative.
Thereafter, we will present simulation results in more detail and for more settings than in the main manuscript.

Exception handling

The fact that
\widehat{\sigma}^{2}_{WMW}\leq 0
can only occur when all outcomes for patients on both treatment arms coincide, that is to say,

x_{11}=\dots=x_{1n_{1}}=x_{21}=\dots=x_{2n_{2}},

yielding
\int\widehat{F}^{2}d\widehat{F}=\nicefrac{{1}}{{4}}
and consequently
\widehat{\sigma}_{WMW}^{2}=0
.
With the Mann-Whitney parameter
p
remaining unchanged, we then pretended that the last observation was different,

x_{11}=\dots=x_{1n_{1k}}=x_{21}=\dots\neq x_{2n_{2k}},

yielding
\widehat{\sigma}^{2}_{WMW}=\nicefrac{{1}}{{4n_{1}n_{2}}}
and thus
T_{WMW}=0
leading to nonrejection of the null hypothesis. We likewise set
T_{N}
and
T_{BM}
to zero as well in such cases, although we replaced the variances in a different manner, i.e., we always used
\max(\nicefrac{{1}}{{n_{1}^{2}n_{2}^{2}}}\,,\,\widehat{\sigma}^{2}_{N})
instead of
\widehat{\sigma}^{2}_{N}
and
\max(\nicefrac{{1}}{{n_{1}^{2}n_{2}^{2}}}\,,\,\widehat{\sigma}^{2}_{BM})
instead of
\widehat{\sigma}^{2}_{BM}
. Note that if all values in both samples coincide, we would have
\widehat{p}^{2}=\widehat{\tau}_{0}=\widehat{\tau}_{1}=\widehat{\tau}_{2}=\nicefrac{{1}}{{4}}
, yielding
\widehat{\sigma}_{N}^{2}=\widehat{\sigma}_{BM}^{2}=0
. Moreover,
\widehat{\sigma}_{PM}^{2}=\nicefrac{{1}}{{4n_{1}n_{2}}}
.

These lower bounds for the unbiased and Brunner-Munzel variance estimates are motivated by the opposite degenerate case, i.e., completely separated samples without ties such as

x_{11}<\dots<x_{1n_{1k}}<x_{21}<\dots<x_{2n_{2k}}\,\,\,\text{ or }\,\,\,x_{11}>\dots>x_{1n_{1k}}>x_{21}>\dots>x_{2n_{2k}},

such that either
\widehat{p}^{2}=\widehat{\tau}_{0}=\widehat{\tau}_{1}=\widehat{\tau}_{2}=1
or
\widehat{p}^{2}=\widehat{\tau}_{0}=\widehat{\tau}_{1}=\widehat{\tau}_{2}=0
, producing
\widehat{\sigma}_{N}^{2}=\widehat{\sigma}_{BM}^{2}=\sigma_{PM}^{2}=0
. Taking a similar approach as before (see also Brunner et al.
Brunner et al. 2018
and
Brunner et al. 2021a
), we then pretended the sample was slightly different, i.e.,

x_{11}<\dots<x_{1(n_{1}-1)}<x_{21}<x_{1n_{1}}<x_{22}<\dots<x_{2n_{2k}},

or

x_{11}>\dots>x_{1(n_{1}-1)}>x_{21}>x_{1n_{1}}>x_{22}>\dots>x_{2n_{2k}},

yielding a slight change in the effect estimate
\widehat{p}=1-\nicefrac{{1}}{{n_{1}n_{2}}}
or
\widehat{p}=\nicefrac{{1}}{{n_{1}n_{2}}}
respectively. In this changed settings, we would have
\widehat{\sigma}_{N}^{2}=\widehat{\sigma}_{BM}^{2}=\nicefrac{{1}}{{n_{1}^{2}n_{2}^{2}}}
. As regards the
logit
transformation, we employed the changed Mann-Whitney effect estimates as well so that the resulting test statistics would not be undefined. As for Perme and Manveski’s [
Perme and Manevski 2019
] approach in completely separated samples, we used their new “shift method” to construct confidence intervals for
p
and rejected the null hypothesis if and only if the number
\nicefrac{{1}}{{2}}
was not an element of this confidence interval.

As far as the degrees of freedom in separated samples are concerned, we assumed
\widehat{\sigma}_{1}^{2}=\widehat{\sigma}_{2}^{2}>0
, giving rise to
df=\{N^{2}(n_{1}-1)(n_{2}-1)\}/\{n_{1}^{2}(n_{1}-1)+n_{2}^{2}(n_{2}-1)\}
. The other degrees of freedom were chosen accordingly.

However, since we only considered scenarios with
\min(n_{1},n_{2})\geq 7
, one might just as well have opted to always reject the null hypothesis in case of completely separated samples and never to reject it when all values coincide – no matter which test statistic is at issue. It would have virtually never resulted in a different decision.
