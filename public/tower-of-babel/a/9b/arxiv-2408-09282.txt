Spectral approximation for substitution systems
Ram Band, Siegfried Beckus, Felix Pogorzelski and Lior Tenenbaum

Abstract
. We study periodic approximations of aperiodic Schrödinger operators on lattices in Lie groups with dilation structure. The potentials arise through symbolic substitution systems that have been recently introduced in this setting. We characterize convergence of spectra of associated Schrödinger operators in the Hausdorff distance via properties of finite graphs. As a consequence, new examples of periodic approximations are obtained. We further prove that there are substitution systems that do not admit periodic approximations in higher dimensions, in contrast to the one-dimensional case. On the other hand, if the spectra converge, then we show that the rate of convergence is necessarily exponentially fast. These results are new even for substitutions over ℤ d \mathbb{Z}^{d} .

1.1.
The underlying dynamical system

Let
\Gamma
be a countable discrete group. Important examples are
\Gamma={\mathbb{Z}}^{d}
or the discrete Heisenberg group
\Gamma=H_{3}(2\mathbb{Z})
. Suppose that the potential
V:\Gamma\to{\mathbb{R}}
takes only finitely many values. Then the finite set
{\mathcal{A}}:=\{V(\gamma)\,|\,\gamma\in\Gamma\}
is called the
alphabet
. The potential
V
can be seen as an element of the product space
{\mathcal{A}}^{\Gamma}=\big\{\omega:\Gamma\to{\mathcal{A}}\big\}
, which we call the
configuration space
. A specific metric on
{\mathcal{A}}^{\Gamma}
is defined as follows: suppose that there is a left-invariant metric
d_{\Gamma}
on
\Gamma
, i.e.
d_{\Gamma}(\gamma\eta_{1},\gamma\eta_{2})=d_{\Gamma}(\eta_{1},\eta_{2})
for all
\gamma,\eta_{1},\eta_{2}\in\Gamma
. For instance,
\Gamma=\mathbb{Z}^{d}
can be equipped with the Euclidean metric, or the Heisenberg group
\Gamma=H_{3}(\mathbb{Z})
can be equipped with the metric inherited from the Cygan-Korányi norm, see equation (
3.1
) below. Then

(1.2)

{\mathrm{d}_{c}}(\omega,\rho):=\inf\Big\{\frac{1}{r+1}\Big|\;r\geq 0\;\textrm{such that}\;\omega|_{B(e,r)}=\rho|_{B(e,r)}\Big\},\qquad\omega,\rho\in{\mathcal{A}}^{\Gamma},

defines an ultra metric on the configuration space
{\mathcal{A}}^{\Gamma}
. Here
B(e,r):=\{\gamma\in\Gamma\,|\,d_{\Gamma}(\gamma,e)<r\}
denotes the open ball of radius
r
in
\Gamma
around the neutral element
e\in\Gamma
.
The group
\Gamma
acts continuously on
{\mathcal{A}}^{\Gamma}
via left translations

(\gamma\omega)(\eta):=\omega(\gamma^{-1}\eta)\quad\text{for all}\quad\gamma,\eta\in\Gamma.

Thus,
({\mathcal{A}}^{\Gamma},\Gamma)
defines, via the previously defined action, a dynamical system.
A nonempty set
\Omega\subseteq{\mathcal{A}}^{\Gamma}
is called
invariant
if
\gamma\Omega\subseteq\Omega
for all
\gamma\in\Gamma
. Then the
space of subshifts
is defined by

{\mathcal{J}}:=\Big\{\Omega\subseteq{\mathcal{A}}^{\Gamma}|\;\Omega\textrm{ is invariant, closed, nonempty}\Big\}.

Particular elements of interest in
{\mathcal{J}}
are the orbit closures
\overline{\text{$\mathchar 525$}(\omega)}
for
\omega\in{\mathcal{A}}^{\Gamma}
where
\text{$\mathchar 525$}(\omega):=\{\gamma\omega\,|\,\gamma\in\Gamma\}
. We are mainly interested in the subshift generated by the potential
V
, namely
\overline{\text{$\mathchar 525$}(V)}\in{\mathcal{J}}
. The set
{\mathcal{J}}
is naturally equipped with a metric – the Hausdorff metric inherited from
{\mathrm{d}_{c}}
– that encodes spectral properties
[
BBDN18
,
BBC19
,
BP20
,
BT25
]
.

We first recall the definition of the Hausdorff distance. Let
(X,d)
be a metric space and
{\mathcal{K}}(X)
be the set of all nonempty compact subsets of
X
. Then, the
Hausdorff metric
between two
A,B\in{\mathcal{K}}(X)
is defined by

d_{H}(A,B)=\max\Big\{\underset{a\in A}{\sup}\;\dist(a,B),\underset{b\in B}{\sup}\;\dist(b,A)\Big\},

where
\dist(a,B):=\inf_{b^{\prime}\in B}d(a,b^{\prime})
. By definition, the Hausdorff metric is induced from the underlying metric
d
on
X
. We are mainly interested in the case

•

X={\mathbb{R}}
with Hausdorff metric
d_{H}
on
{\mathcal{K}}({\mathbb{R}})
induced by the Euclidean distance
|\cdot|
; and

•

X={\mathcal{A}}^{\Gamma}
with Hausdorff metric
\delta_{H}
induced by the metric
{\mathrm{d}_{c}}
and its restriction to
{\mathcal{J}}\subseteq{\mathcal{K}}({\mathcal{A}}^{\Gamma})
.

Note that
({\mathcal{J}},\delta_{H})
is itself a compact metric space since
{\mathcal{J}}\subseteq{\mathcal{K}}({\mathcal{A}}^{\Gamma})
is closed and
{\mathcal{K}}({\mathcal{A}}^{\Gamma})
is compact
, see
[
BBDN18
, Proposition 4]
. The Hausdorff metric
\delta_{H}
on
{\mathcal{J}}
can be expressed by local patches of configurations (see equation (
1.3
) below), which we briefly introduce.

For
M\subseteq\Gamma
, a map
P:M\to{\mathcal{A}}
is called a
patch
with
support

M
. If
M
is finite, we say
P
is a finite patch. The
set of all patches
is denoted by
\mathrm{Pat}({\mathcal{A}}^{\Gamma})
. Note that
\mathrm{Pat}({\mathcal{A}}^{\Gamma})
includes the empty patch
\text{\o}\in{\mathcal{A}}^{\emptyset}
and infinite patches such as elements of
{\mathcal{A}}^{\Gamma}
.
A patch
P\in{\mathcal{A}}^{M}
is a
subpatch
of
Q\in{\mathcal{A}}^{K}
(
P\prec Q
) if there is a
\gamma\in\Gamma
such that
\gamma M\subseteq K
and
Q(\gamma\eta)=P(\eta)
for all
\eta\in M
.
By convention, the empty patch
\text{\o}\in{\mathcal{A}}^{\emptyset}
is a subpatch of any configuration
\omega\in{\mathcal{A}}^{\Gamma}
.
Clearly, the relation
\prec
defined on patches is a partial order.
For
\omega\in{\mathcal{A}}^{\Gamma}
, the associated dictionary
W(\omega)
is the set of all finite subpatches of
\omega\in{\mathcal{A}}^{\Gamma}
.
Similarly, one defines
W(\Omega):=\bigcup_{\omega\in\Omega}W(\omega)
for a subshift
\Omega\in{\mathcal{J}}
. For a finite set
M\subseteq\Gamma
, define
W(\omega)_{M}:=W(\omega)\cap{\mathcal{A}}^{M}
for
\omega\in{\mathcal{A}}^{\Gamma}
and
W(\Omega)_{M}:=W(\Omega)\cap{\mathcal{A}}^{M}
for
\Omega\in{\mathcal{J}}
. With this at hand, we get

(1.3)

\delta_{H}(\Omega_{1},\Omega_{2})=\inf\left\{\left.\frac{1}{r+1}\,\right|\,r\geq 0\;\textrm{such that}\;W(\Omega_{1})_{B(e,r)}=W(\Omega_{2})_{B(e,r)}\right\},\qquad\Omega_{1},\Omega_{2}\in{\mathcal{J}},

from our choice of the
{\mathrm{d}_{c}}
on
{\mathcal{A}}^{\Gamma}
and the definition of the Hausdorff metric. Note that by convention
W(\Omega)_{B(e,0)}=\{\text{\o}\}
.

A
Schrödinger operator
H
with finite range
is a family of self-adjoint operators
H_{\omega}:\ell^{2}(\Gamma)\to\ell^{2}(\Gamma)
for
\omega\in{\mathcal{A}}^{\Gamma}
defined by

(1.4)

(H_{\omega}\psi)(\gamma):=\sum_{\eta\in B}t_{\eta}\big(\gamma^{-1}\omega\big)\psi(\gamma\eta),\qquad\psi\in\ell^{2}(\Gamma),\gamma\in\Gamma,

where
B\subseteq\Gamma
is finite with
B=B^{-1}
while
t_{\eta}:{\mathcal{A}}^{\Gamma}\to{\mathbb{C}},\eta\in B,
are continuous and satisfy that
t_{\eta}(\omega)=\overline{t_{\eta^{-1}}(\eta\omega)}
, where
\overline{z}
is the complex conjugate of
z
. The latter conditions guarantee that
H_{\omega}
is self-adjoint for each
\omega\in{\mathcal{A}}^{\Gamma}
.
Thus, the spectrum
\sigma(H_{\omega})
of
H_{\omega}
is a compact subset of
{\mathbb{R}}
.
We say
H
is
strongly pattern equivariant
if the coefficients
t_{\eta}:{\mathcal{A}}^{\Gamma}\to{\mathbb{C}},\eta\in B,
are continuous and take finitely many values.
These conditions are equivalent to
t_{\eta}
being locally constant. Thus, the value
t_{\eta}(\omega)
depends only on the patch
\omega|_{B(e,r)}
for suitable
r>0
.
The operator defined in (
1.1
) is a strongly pattern equivariant Schrödinger operator with finite range using the viewpoint that
V\in{\mathcal{A}}^{\Gamma}
.

Recently, it was shown that the convergence of dynamical systems in
({\mathcal{J}},\delta_{H})
is tightly connected to the convergence of the spectrum of such operators in
({\mathcal{K}}({\mathbb{R}}),d_{H})

[
BBDN18
,
BBC19
,
BT25
]
and other spectral quantities
[
BP20
]
if
\Gamma
is amenable.
This is the starting point of this work.

Let
\Omega\in{\mathcal{J}}
. To study the spectral properties of Schrödinger operators associated with
\omega\in\Omega
, one aims to find periodic approximations.
Here, a configuration
\omega_{0}\in{\mathcal{A}}^{\Gamma}
is
periodic
, if its orbit
\text{$\mathchar 525$}(\omega_{0})
is finite. Then
\Omega
is
periodically approximable
if there is a sequence of periodic
\omega_{n}\in{\mathcal{A}}^{\Gamma}
such that
\lim_{n\to\infty}\delta_{H}\big(\text{$\mathchar 525$}(\omega_{n}),\Omega\big)=0
. In combination with the results obtained in
[
BBDN18
,
BBC19
,
BT25
]
, we obtain periodic approximations of the associated Schrödinger operators and their spectra. Thus, the question arises when a subshift is periodically approximable.

Our main results hold for a quite general setup. Concretely, we consider subshifts defined via substitutions on lattices in homogeneous Lie groups that were recently introduced

BHP21
]
. Since the formal statements need some more introduction, we start presenting the main results along a guiding example, namely the table tiling substitution. Later we also provide an example on a non-abelian group

– the discrete Heisenberg group, see Section

It is worth emphasizing that we only have edges between patches that are not

Since we only have finitely many patches of size

2.1.
Symbolic substitution systems

Following
[
BHP21
]
, one needs some geometric data – a so-called dilation datum – and combinatorial data – a so-called substitution datum – to describe a substitution on
{\mathcal{A}}^{\Gamma}
.
For convenience of the reader, we borrowed the notation of
[
BHP21
]
.

A tuple
{\mathcal{D}}=\big(G,d,(D_{\lambda})_{\lambda>0},\Gamma,V\big)
is called a
dilation datum
, if

(D1)

G
is a
1
-connected, locally compact, second countable, Hausdorff group and
d
is a proper (i.e. closed balls with finite radius are compact), left-invariant metric on
G
inducing the given topology on
G
;

(D2)

(D_{\lambda})_{\lambda>0}
is a one-parameter group of automorphisms of
G
, called the underlying dilation family, such that

d\big(D_{\lambda}(g),D_{\lambda}(h)\big)=\lambda\cdot d(g,h)\quad\text{for all}\quad\lambda>0,\;\text{and}\;g,h\in G;

(D3)

\Gamma<G
is a uniform lattice satisfying that
D_{\lambda}[\Gamma]\subseteq\Gamma
for some
\lambda>1
and
V
is a Borel measurable, relatively compact left-fundamental domain for
\Gamma
such that
e
is an element in the interior of
V
.

Recall that a subgroup
\Gamma<G
is called a
uniform lattice
of a locally compact second countable Hausdorff group
G
, if
\Gamma
is discrete and co-compact. A set
V\subseteq G
is called a
left-fundamental domain
for a uniform lattice
\Gamma
, if
G=\bigsqcup_{\gamma\in\Gamma}\gamma V
. Since we will only consider left-fundamental domains in this work, which in addition are Borel and relatively compact, we will refer to those simply as
fundamental domains
.

For a fundamental domain
V
,
n\in{\mathbb{N}}
,
\lambda>1
and
\emptyset\neq M\subseteq\Gamma
, we recursively define for
n\in{\mathbb{N}}
,

(2.1)

V_{\lambda}(0,M):=M\cdot V\qquad\text{and}\qquad V_{\lambda}(n,M):=D_{\lambda}\Big[\big(V(n-1,M)\cap\Gamma\big)\cdot V\Big].

Moreover, set
V_{\lambda}(n):=V_{\lambda}(n,\{e\})
for
n\in{\mathbb{N}}
, where
e
is the neutral element in
G
.
The intersection of
\Gamma
with these sets are precisely the support of the iterates
S^{n}(P)
of a patch
P\in{\mathcal{A}}^{M}
, see Proposition
2.4
(
b
) below.

Let
{\mathcal{D}}=(G,d,(D_{\lambda})_{\lambda>0},\Gamma,V)
be a dilation datum. Then
\lambda_{0}>1
is called
stretch factor associated with
{\mathcal{D}}
if
D_{\lambda_{0}}[\Gamma]\subseteq\Gamma
and
\lambda_{0}
is sufficiently large relative to
V
. Here,
\lambda_{0}
is called
sufficiently large relative to
V
if there exist a constant
C_{-}>0
, an integer
s\in{\mathbb{N}}_{0}:={\mathbb{N}}\cup\{0\}
and a
z\in\Gamma
such that for all
n\in{\mathbb{N}}
,

D_{\lambda_{0}}^{n}\big[B\big(z,C_{-})\big]\subseteq V_{\lambda_{0}}(s+n).

If the underlying dilation datum
{\mathcal{D}}
being referred to is clear, we will call
\lambda_{0}
just a
stretch factor
. From now on, we use the notation
V(n,M)=V_{\lambda_{0}}(n,M)
and
V(n)=V_{\lambda_{0}}(n)
for
n\in{\mathbb{N}}_{0}
and
M\subseteq\Gamma
where
\lambda_{0}>1
is a fixed stretch factor.

A group
\Gamma
that is part of a dilation datum as above is called a
homogeneous substitution lattice
. Restricting the metric
d
to
\Gamma\times\Gamma
yields a left-invariant, proper metric
d_{\Gamma}
on
\Gamma
. By
[
BHP21
, Proposition 3.36]
, every homogeneous substitution lattice has
exact polynomial growth
with respect to
d_{\Gamma}
, i.e., there exist constants
C>0
and
\kappa>0
such that

\lim_{r\to\infty}\frac{|B^{\Gamma}(r)|}{Cr^{\kappa}}=1,

where
B^{\Gamma}(r)=\big\{\gamma\in\Gamma:\,d_{\Gamma}(e,\gamma)\leq r\big\}
. The constant
\kappa
only depends on
G
, and is called the
homogeneous dimension
. As a consequence, every homogeneous substitution lattice
\Gamma
is amenable, compare with
[
BHP24
, Proposition 4.4. (a)]
.

A
substitution datum

{\mathcal{S}}:=({\mathcal{A}},\lambda_{0},S_{0})
over a dilation datum
{\mathcal{D}}:=\big(G,d,(D_{\lambda})_{\lambda>0},\Gamma,V\big)
consists of

(S1)

a finite set
{\mathcal{A}}
called the
alphabet
;

(S2)

a stretch factor
\lambda_{0}>1
associated with
{\mathcal{D}}
;

(S3)

a map
S_{0}:{\mathcal{A}}\to{\mathcal{A}}^{D_{\lambda_{0}}[V]\cap\Gamma}
called the
substitution rule
.

Definition 2.1
.

A substitution on
{\mathcal{A}}^{\Gamma}
is a dilation datum
{\mathcal{D}}
together with a substitution datum
{\mathcal{S}}
.

S
.
We are mainly interested in primitive substitutions implying that the associated subshift
\Omega(S)
is minimal. Here a subshift
\Omega\in{\mathcal{J}}
is called
minimal
, if
\overline{\text{$\mathchar 525$}(\omega)}=\Omega
for all
\omega\in\Omega
.

Definition 2.7

(Primitive substitution)
.

A substitution on
{\mathcal{A}}^{\Gamma}
with substitution map
S
is called primitive, if there is an
L\in{\mathbb{N}}
such that for all
a,b\in{\mathcal{A}}
,
a\prec S^{L}(b)
.

In fact, if the substitution is primitive, then the subshift
\Omega(S)
falls into a specific class of minimal subshifts introduced next.

Definition 2.8

(Linearly repetitive subshifts)
.

A subshift
\Omega\in{\mathcal{J}}
is called linearly repetitive, if there is a constant
C\geq 1
such that for every
r\geq 1
,
R\geq Cr
and
P\in W(\Omega)_{B(e,R)}
, we have

Q\prec P\quad\text{for all}\quad Q\in W(\Omega)_{B(e,r)}.

The smallest constant
C_{\mathrm{LR}}=C\geq 1
satisfying the previous condition is called the linear repetitivity constant.

Let us summarize the previously discussed properties of the subshift associated with a substitution.

Theorem 2.9
.

Consider a substitution on
{\mathcal{A}}^{\Gamma}
with substitution map
S
. Then
\Omega(S)\in{\mathcal{J}}
is a subshift. If the substitution is primitive, then
\Omega(S)
is minimal and linearly repetitive. In this case
W(S)=W(\omega)
holds for all
\omega\in\Omega(S)
.

Proof.

In the case of block substitutions (defined in Section
3.1
) this is well-known see e.g.
[
Que10
, Chapter 5]
and
[
BG13
, Chapter 4]
. In the general case, this is proven in
[
BHP21
, Theorem 7.4]
.
∎

2.2.
Substitution graphs and testing domains

We introduce substitution graphs generalizing the graph that was introduced earlier for the table tiling substitution. Recall the basic graph theoretic notions introduced in Section
3.1
.

Definition 2.10

(Substitution graph)
.

Consider a substitution on
{\mathcal{A}}^{\Gamma}
with substitution map
S
. For a finite nonempty set
K\subseteq\Gamma
and an

, we define the substitution graph
G_{S}(K;n)
by the directed graph with vertex set
{\mathcal{V}}={\mathcal{A}}^{K}
and the edge set
{\mathcal{E}}
defined by

(P,Q)\in{\mathcal{E}}\quad:\Longleftrightarrow\quad Q\prec S^{n}(P)\textrm{ and }P,Q\not\in W(S).

We once again emphasize that we only have edges between patches that are not
S
-legal (illegal). In particular, if all patches
{\mathcal{A}}^{K}
are
S
-legal, then
G_{S}(K;n)
has no edges. More precisely, the
S
-legal patches are isolated vertices in
G_{S}(K;n)
.
Therefore, for any
\omega\in\mathcal{A}^{\Gamma}
, this graph keeps track of the illegal
K
-patches in
S^{n}(\omega)
coming from the
K
-patches in
\omega
.
For a correct choice of
K
and
n
, this is the crucial part to determining whether
\overline{Orb\big(S^{n}(\omega_{0})\big)}
converges to
\Omega(S)
or does not.

In
[
Ten24a
]
, the reader can find some graphs plotted for
\Gamma={\mathbb{Z}}
.
The graph

associated with the table tiling substitution (Section

) is a substitution graph with
G_{S}(T,N_{T})
for

and
N_{T}=1
.

We will be interested in the substitution graph

where

satisfies two additional geometric properties with respect to the underlying dilation datum and the stretch factor
\lambda_{0}
.
This leads to the concept of a testing tuple.
Therefore, recall the notion of the supports

for
n\in{\mathbb{N}}
and

recursively defined in equation (

).

Definition 2.11
.

A tuple
(T,N_{T})
is called a testing tuple (for

with associated stretch factor

(a)

If

.

Note that the testing tuple depends only on the dilation datum

and the associated stretch factor

and the substitution rule.

Moreover, if

is a testing tuple, then

is also a testing tuple for all

, see Proposition

(

).

Example 2.13
.

Recall the table tiling substitution introduced in Section

fitting in our setting (see Proposition

). Then a possible testing tuple of the table tiling is
(T,1)

.

The notion of testing tuple is fundamental to check the convergence in our main Theorem

. In particular, it is a crucial ingredient in Lemma

below.

In fact, the existence of a testing tuple is no restriction as it always exists, see Proposition

. We postpone the details for now but let us note the following. While a testing tuple always exists, it can be hard to find a “minimal” (in terms of cardinality) testing domain. We provide in Section

an algorithm

]
to reduce the size of the testing domain and apply it in the case of the Heisenberg group.
Note that the smaller the set

2.3.
First main result: Characterization of the convergence and its consequences

We now have all at hand to formulate our first main result.

Theorem 2.14
.

Consider a primitive substitution on
{\mathcal{A}}^{\Gamma}
with substitution map
S
. For a testing tuple
(T,N_{T})
and
\omega_{0}\in{\mathcal{A}}^{\Gamma}
, the following assertions are equivalent.

(i)

For all Schrödinger operators
H
with finite range, we have

\lim\limits_{n\to\infty}\sigma(H_{S^{n}(\omega_{0})})=\sigma(H_{\omega}),\qquad\omega\in\Omega(S),

(ii)

\lim\limits_{n\to\infty}\delta_{H}\big(\overline{\text{$\mathchar 525$}(S^{n}(\omega_{0}))},\Omega(S)\big)=0
.

(iii)

Each directed path in
G_{S}(T,N_{T})
, starting in a vertex of
W(\omega_{0})_{T}\subseteq{\mathcal{A}}^{T}
, does not contain a closed subpath.

(iv)

Each directed path in
G_{S}(T,N_{T})
, starting in a vertex of
W(\omega_{0})_{T}\subseteq{\mathcal{A}}^{T}
, is of length strictly less than
\big|{\mathcal{A}}^{T}\big|
.

In particular, if
W(\omega_{0})_{T}\subseteq W(S)
, then these equivalent conditions are satisfied.

We prove the theorem in Section
4.2
.

The equivalence of (
i
) and (
ii
) is a consequence of
[
BBDN18
, Corollary 1]
using that every homogeneous substitution lattice is amenable. Our main contribution is the equivalence of (
ii
) to (
iii
) and (
iv
). These conditions are of particular interest as they can be checked algorithmically as demonstrated on some examples in Sections
1.2
and
3.2
. Note further that one can consider a larger class of operators associated with the dynamical systems, see
[
BBDN18
]
.

The assertions in Theorem
2.14
are particularly interesting in situations where one can numerically compute the spectrum explicitly for the approximations
H_{S^{n}(\omega_{0})}
. This is for instance the case if
\Gamma={\mathbb{Z}}^{d}
and
S^{n}(\omega_{0})
is periodic (i.e.
\text{$\mathchar 525$}(S^{n}(\omega_{0}))
is finite) using the Floquet-Bloch theory.
We provide in Proposition
3.3
an example of a primitive substitution on the Heisenberg group admitting periodic approximations. Together with the results on the bottom of the spectrum for periodic graphs given in
[
Ric23
]
, our result might provide an approach to access the bottom of the spectrum in this case. This will be the subject of future investigations.

This motivates the task of finding conditions for the existence of periodic approximations. In order to use Theorem
2.14
, we need the following.

Proposition 2.15
.

Consider a substitution on
{\mathcal{A}}^{\Gamma}
with substitution map
S
. If
\omega_{0}\in{\mathcal{A}}^{\Gamma}
is periodic, then
S^{n}(\omega_{0})
is periodic for all
n\in{\mathbb{N}}
. More precisely, if
M\subseteq\Gamma
is finite satisfying
\text{$\mathchar 525$}(\omega_{0})=\{\eta\omega_{0}\,|\,\eta\in M\}
, then

\text{$\mathchar 525$}(S^{n}(\omega_{0}))=\big\{\eta S^{n}(\omega_{0})\,|\,\eta\in(D^{n}[V]\cap\Gamma)\cdot D^{n}[M]\big\},\qquad n\in{\mathbb{N}}.

Proof.

Recall that
V
is the fundamental domain of a uniform lattice and
D:=D_{\lambda_{0}}
is the dilation with the stretch factor
\lambda_{0}>1
.

Let
H:=\mathrm{Stab}(\omega_{0}):=\{\gamma\in\Gamma\,|\,\gamma\omega_{0}=\omega_{0}\}
be the stabilizer of
\omega_{0}
, which is a subgroup of
\Gamma
. Since
\text{$\mathchar 525$}(\omega_{0})
is finite, there is a finite set
M\subseteq\Gamma
such that
\text{$\mathchar 525$}(\omega_{0})=\{\eta\omega_{0}\,|\,\eta\in M\}
.

We first show that
M\cdot H=\Gamma
. Clearly,
M\cdot H\subseteq\Gamma
and so let
\gamma\in\Gamma
. Then there is an
\eta\in M
such that
\gamma\omega_{0}=\eta\omega_{0}
or equivalently
\eta^{-1}\gamma\omega_{0}=\omega_{0}
. By definition of the stabilizer, we conclude
\eta^{-1}\gamma\in H
, namely
\gamma\in\eta H\subseteq M\cdot H
.

Next, we prove that
S^{n}(\omega_{0})
is periodic. Let
n\in{\mathbb{N}}
. Set
V_{n}:=D^{n}[V^{-1}]\cap\Gamma
, which satisfies
V_{n}\cdot D^{n}[\Gamma]=\Gamma
, since
V^{-1}\cdot\Gamma=(\Gamma\cdot V)^{-1}=G
and
D
is an automorphism. Hence,

\Gamma=V_{n}\cdot D^{n}[M\cdot H]=V_{n}\cdot D^{n}[M]\cdot D^{n}[H],

follows using again that
D
is an automorphism. Define
M_{n}:=V_{n}\cdot D^{n}[M]\subseteq\Gamma
, which is a finite set as
V_{n}
and
M
are finite. Then for all
\gamma\in\Gamma
, there is an
\eta\in M_{n}
and a
\gamma^{\prime}\in H
such that
\gamma=\eta D^{n}(\gamma^{\prime})
. Thus, Proposition
2.4
and
\gamma^{\prime}\in H
lead to

\gamma S^{n}(\omega_{0})=\eta D^{n}(\gamma^{\prime})S^{n}(\omega_{0})=\eta S^{n}(\gamma^{\prime}\omega_{0})=\eta S^{n}(\omega_{0}).

Hence,
\text{$\mathchar 525$}(S^{n}(\omega_{0}))=\{\eta\omega_{0}\,|\,\eta\in M_{n}\}
and so
S^{n}(\omega_{0})
is periodic since
M_{n}
is finite.
∎

Corollary 2.16
.

Consider a primitive substitution on
{\mathcal{A}}^{\Gamma}
with substitution map
S
.
If
\omega_{0}\in{\mathcal{A}}^{\Gamma}
is periodic and satisfies one of the equivalent conditions in Theorem
2.14
, then
\Omega(S)
is periodically approximable.

Proof.

By Theorem
2.14
,
\lim_{n\to\infty}\overline{\text{$\mathchar 525$}(S^{n}(\omega_{0}))}=\Omega(S)
holds. Since
\omega_{0}
is periodic, we conclude that
S^{n}(\omega_{0})
is also periodic, see Proposition
2.15
. In particular,
\overline{\text{$\mathchar 525$}(S^{n}(\omega_{0}))}=\text{$\mathchar 525$}(S^{n}(\omega_{0}))
holds since finite sets are closed.
∎

According to
[
BBDN20
, Proposition 2.11]
, every subshift associated with a primitive substitution on
{\mathcal{A}}^{\mathbb{Z}}
is periodically approximable. Moreover, one can conclude from Theorem
2.14
that so-called self-correcting substitutions, see
[
GM13
, Definition 2.7]
, are periodically approximable. However, in general primitive substitutions do not give rise to periodically approximable subshifts in higher dimensions.
This is a direct consequence of Theorem
2.14
and a result from
[
DLS05
,
Oll08
,
Bal09
]
see a summary in
[
JV20
]
. In order to do so, we call a subshift
\Omega\in{\mathcal{J}}

strongly aperiodic
if
\mathrm{Stab}(\omega):=\{\gamma\in\Gamma\,|\,\gamma\omega=\omega\}=\{e\}
for every
\omega\in\Omega
. Note that substitution subshifts are a typical class used to generate strongly aperiodic subshifts, see e.g.
[
Sol98
,
Que10
,
BG13
,
BHP21
]
.

Corollary 2.17
.

There exists a strongly aperiodic, minimal subshift
\Omega(S)
associated with a primitive substitution on
{\mathcal{A}}^{{\mathbb{Z}}^{2}}
such that
\Omega(S)
is not periodically approximable.

Proof.

We need to introduce some notations used in
[
JV20
]
.
A subset
\tau\subseteq{\mathcal{A}}^{T}
with
T=\{0,1\}^{2}
is called a
tileset
. A tileset
\tau
is
intrinsically substitutive (with factor
2
)
, if there is a substitution on
{\mathcal{A}}^{{\mathbb{Z}}^{2}}
with substitution rule
S_{0}:{\mathcal{A}}\to{\mathcal{A}}^{T}
and substitution map
S
satisfying

•

\Omega(S)=\Omega(\tau):=\{\omega\in{\mathcal{A}}^{{\mathbb{Z}}^{2}}\,|\,W(\omega)_{T}\subseteq\tau\}
,

•

for
P\in{\mathcal{A}}^{M}
with
M\subseteq{\mathbb{Z}}^{2}
finite, we have
S(P)\in W(S)
if and only if
P\in W(S)
,

•

for all
\omega\in\Omega(\tau)
, there is a
\rho\in{\mathcal{A}}^{{\mathbb{Z}}^{2}}
and a
\gamma\in{\mathbb{Z}}^{2}
such that
\omega=\gamma S(\rho)
.

Note that
\Omega(\tau)
is also called a subshift of finite type in the literature. Moreover, this substitution is actually a block substitution falling into our framework, see Section
3.1
.

According to
[
DLS05
,
Oll08
]
(see also
[
JV20
, Proposition 8]
), there exists an intrinsically substitutive tileset
\tau
with substitution map
S
such that
\Omega(S)
is strongly aperiodic. Due to
[
Bal09
, Lemme 1.33]
this substitution is primitive. It is left to prove that
\Omega(S)
is not periodically approximable. This can be done using (only) the first property of an intrinsically substitutive tilset together with its existence. In fact, by equation (
1.3
) and Theorem
2.14
,
\Omega(S)
is periodically approximable if and only if there is a periodic
\omega_{0}\in{\mathcal{A}}^{{\mathbb{Z}}^{2}}
such that
W(\omega_{0})_{T}\subseteq W(S)_{T}\subseteq\tau
. By definition of
\Omega(\tau)=\Omega(S)
, we conclude
\omega_{0}\in\Omega(S)
. This contradicts that
\Omega(S)
is strongly aperiodic. Summing up,
\Omega(S)
is minimal, strongly aperiodic and not periodically approximable.
∎

Corollary
2.16
provides a sufficient condition for subshifts that are periodically approximable generalizing the results in
[
BBDN20
, Proposition 2.11]
and
[
Bec16
, Theorem 6.2.3]
.
With the previous theorem at hand, we provide a verifiable condition for primitive substitution systems in general to admit such periodic approximations.
Moreover, Theorem
2.14
extends the result
[
Bec16
, Theorem 6.2.3]
significantly.

Corollary
2.17
shows that there are substitutions on
{\mathcal{A}}^{{\mathbb{Z}}^{2}}
that are not periodically approximable. Based on this, the question arises if such a behavior also occurs for non-abelian substitution subshifts. Specifically, are there primitive substitutions over a non-abelian group that are not periodically approximable?

2.4.
Second main result: Exponential rate of convergence for the spectra

The exponential convergence of the spectra for the table tiling substitution (Proposition
1.3
), can be witnessed in large geometric generality. This is the core of the second main result of this paper, concerning the qualitative behavior of the convergence rate of
\sigma(H_{S^{n}(\omega_{0})})
to the spectrum
\sigma(H_{\rho})
for
\rho\in\Omega(S)
.

These estimates are obtained by estimating the rate of the convergence of the subshifts
\overline{\text{$\mathchar 525$}(S^{n}(\omega_{0}))}
to
\Omega(S)
in
({\mathcal{J}},\delta_{H})
. This leads to the spectral estimates using the recent works
[
BBC19
,
BT25
]
. The exponential decay obtained is given by the associated stretch factor
\lambda_{0}>1
.

Theorem 2.18
.

Consider a primitive substitution on
{\mathcal{A}}^{\Gamma}
with substitution map
S
.
Then there exist a
C>0
and an
M_{1}\geq 0
such that if
\omega_{0}\in{\mathcal{A}}^{\Gamma}
satisfies one of the equivalent conditions in Theorem
2.14
, then

\delta_{H}\big(\overline{\text{$\mathchar 525$}(S^{n}(\omega_{0}))},\Omega(S)\big)\leq\frac{C}{\lambda_{0}^{n}},\qquad n\geq M_{1}.

The constants
C
and
M_{1}
can be estimated more explicitly, see Proposition
5.6
below. It is also shown there that for specific initial configurations
\omega_{0}
these constants can be significantly reduced, which is desirable for practical purposes, see e.g. Corollary
5.7
. We prove the theorem in Section
5.2
.

Corollary 2.19
.

Consider a primitive substitution on
{\mathcal{A}}^{\Gamma}
with substitution map
S
.
If
H
is a strongly pattern equivariant Schrödinger operator with finite range, then there exist a
C^{\prime}>0
and an
N_{0}\in{\mathbb{N}}
such that for all
\omega_{0}\in{\mathcal{A}}^{\Gamma}
satisfying one of the equivalent conditions in Theorem
2.14
, we have

d_{H}\big(\sigma(H_{S^{n}(\omega_{0})}),\sigma(H_{\omega})\big)\leq\frac{C^{\prime}}{\lambda_{0}^{n}},\qquad\omega\in\Omega(S),\;n\geq N_{0}.

Proof.

Since
S
is primitive,
\Omega(S)
is minimal (Theorem
2.9
) and so
\sigma(H_{\omega})=\sigma(H_{\rho})
holds for all
\omega,\rho\in\Omega(S)
, see e.g.
[
Len99
, Proposition 1.2.2]
in the abelian and
[
Bec16
, Theorem 3.6.8]
in the non-abelian case.
Now the estimate follows directly from Theorem
2.18
together with
[
BT25
, Theorem 1.3 (b)]
(see also
[
BBC19
]
in the case
\Gamma={\mathbb{Z}}^{d}
) using that all the coefficients are locally constant. Note that we can apply
[
BT25
]
as
\Gamma
has exact polynomial growth in our setting, see
[
BHP21
, Proposition 3.36]
.
∎

Remark 2.20
.

We note that the spectral estimate holds for a larger class of operators even with infinite range, see
[
BBC19
,
BT25
]
for more details. Moreover, if the coefficients

.

3.
Applications of the theory

In this section we provide explicit dilation data and substitution data to apply our main results. We give one class of abelian substitutions – block substitutions, and another non-abelian example, namely a substitution over the discrete Heisenberg group.

3.1.
Block substitutions

We study a special class of substitutions in the abelian setting
\Gamma={\mathbb{Z}}^{d}
and prove that they are contained in the general class of substitutions introduced before. In particular, the table tiling substitution presented in Section
1.2
is an interesting example of block substitutions. The following two statements show that the theory developed in Section
2
is applicable for block substitutions and henceforth also for the table tiling substitution.

Let
{\mathcal{A}}
be an alphabet. A
block substitution on
{\mathcal{A}}^{{\mathbb{Z}}^{d}}
is defined by a vector
\vec{m}=(m_{1},\ldots,m_{d})\in{\mathbb{N}}^{d}
with
m_{j}>1
for all
1\leq j\leq d
and a
substitution rule

S_{0}:{\mathcal{A}}\to{\mathcal{A}}^{K_{\vec{m}}}\quad\textrm{where}\quad K_{\vec{m}}:={\mathbb{Z}}^{d}\cap\prod_{j=1}^{d}\big[-\frac{m_{j}}{2},\frac{m_{j}}{2}\big).

It is standard to extend a substitution rule to a map
S:\mathrm{Pat}({\mathcal{A}}^{{\mathbb{Z}}^{d}})\to\mathrm{Pat}({\mathcal{A}}^{{\mathbb{Z}}^{d}})
by acting letter wise, see e.g.
[
Que10
, Chapter 5.1]
or
[
BG13
, Chapter 4]
. For instance, this is sketched in Figure
1
for the table tiling substitution. Thanks to the following proposition, block substitutions are a special case of the substitutions introduced in the previous section.

Proposition 3.1
.

Consider a block substitution with alphabet
{\mathcal{A}}
, vector
\vec{m}=(m_{1},\ldots,m_{d})\in{\mathbb{N}}^{d}
with
m_{j}>1
for all
1\leq j\leq d
and substitution rule
S_{0}:{\mathcal{A}}\to{\mathcal{A}}^{K_{\vec{m}}}
. Then the associated substitution map
S
conincides with the substitution map arising from

•

the dilation datum
{\mathcal{D}}:=\big({\mathbb{R}}^{d},d_{\vec{m}},(D_{\lambda})_{\lambda>0},{\mathbb{Z}}^{d},V\big)
with
V=\big[-\frac{1}{2},\frac{1}{2}\big)^{d}
,
\lambda_{0}=\min\{m_{j}\,|\,1\leq j\leq d\}>1
, metric

d_{\vec{m}}(x,y):=\max_{1\leq j\leq d}|x_{j}-y_{j}|^{\frac{1}{\alpha_{j}}},\qquad x,y\in{\mathbb{R}}^{d},\alpha_{j}:=\frac{\log(m_{j})}{\log(\lambda_{0})}\geq 1\;\textrm{ for }\;1\leq j\leq d,

and the dilation family defined by

D_{\lambda}(x):=\big(\lambda^{\alpha_{1}}x_{1},\ldots,\lambda^{\alpha_{d}}x_{d}\big)\qquad\textrm{for }\quad x=(x_{1},\ldots,x_{d})\in{\mathbb{R}}^{d};

•

the substitution datum
{\mathcal{S}}=({\mathcal{A}},\lambda_{0},S_{0})
with
K_{\vec{m}}=D_{\lambda_{0}}[V]\cap{\mathbb{Z}}^{d}
.

In particular, we have
D_{\lambda_{0}}(x)=(m_{1}x_{1},\ldots,m_{d}x_{d})
for
x\in{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
and
\lambda_{0}
is sufficiently large relative to
V
with respect to the constant

C_{-}:=2\lambda_{0}-3
and
s=4
.

Proof.

Clearly,
d_{\vec{m}}
is a proper left-invariant metric inducing the Euclidean topology on
{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
and

d_{\vec{m}}(D_{\lambda}(x),D_{\lambda}(y))=\max_{1\leq j\leq d}\big|\lambda_{0}^{\alpha_{j}}x_{j}-\lambda_{0}^{\alpha_{j}}y_{j}\big|^{\frac{1}{\alpha_{j}}}=\lambda_{0}d_{\vec{m}}(x,y).

It is straightforward to check that
D_{\lambda}:{\mathbb{R}}^{d}\to{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
defines a group automorphism. Finally, the choice of
\alpha_{j}
implies
\log\big(\lambda_{0}^{\alpha_{j}}\big)=\log(m_{j})
. We conclude that
\lambda_{0}^{\alpha_{j}}=m_{j}
proving
D_{\lambda_{0}}(x)=(m_{1}x_{1},\ldots,m_{d}x_{d})
for
x\in{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
and
D_{\lambda_{0}}[V]\cap{\mathbb{Z}}^{d}=K_{\vec{m}}
.
Thus,
{\mathcal{D}}
defines a dilation datum and
{\mathcal{S}}
a substitution datum if we prove that
\lambda_{0}
is sufficiently large relative to
V
with respect to
C_{-}:=2\lambda_{0}-3
and
s=4
.

In order to do so, we apply Lemma
2.3
(b) for
s=4
.
Therefore set
r_{+}=1
satisfying
\overline{V}\subseteq B(e,r_{+})
.
A short computation gives
V(4)=\prod_{j=1}^{d}[a_{j},b_{j})
with
b_{j}-a_{j}=m_{j}^{4}
for each
1\leq j\leq d
. Let
z_{j}\in{\mathbb{Z}}

4.
Proof of the first main result

In this section, we prove Theorem
2.14
. Throughout this section, we fix a substitution on
{\mathcal{A}}^{\Gamma}
with substitution map
S
. More precisely, let
{\mathcal{D}}=\big(G,d,(D_{\lambda})_{\lambda>0},\Gamma,V\big)
be the associated dilation datum and
{\mathcal{S}}=({\mathcal{A}},\lambda_{0},S_{0})
be the associated substitution datum. We use the notation

4.1.
Sufficient condition

We start by generalizing an unpublished result on block substitutions on
{\mathbb{Z}}^{d}
by one of the authors
[
Bec16
, Theorem 6.2.3]
providing a sufficient condition for the convergence of the subshifts. The special case

was treated in
[
BBDN20
, Corollary 5.5]
.

Lemma 4.1
.

Let
(T,N_{T})
be a testing tuple of a substitution on
{\mathcal{A}}^{\Gamma}
with substitution map
S
. For
r>0
, let
N_{r}(T)\geq 0
be such that it satisfies Definition
2.11
(
a
). Then for each
r>0
and for all
\omega_{0}\in{\mathcal{A}}^{\Gamma}
with
W(\omega_{0})_{T}\subseteq W(S)
, we have

W\big(S^{n}(\omega_{0})\big)_{B(e,r)}\subseteq W(S)\quad\text{for all}\quad n\geq N_{r}(T).

Proof.

Let
r>0
and
P\in W(S^{n}(\omega_{0}))_{B(e,r)}
. Hence, there is an
x\in\Gamma
such that
P=S^{n}(\omega_{0})|_{xB(e,r)}
.
By the choice of
N_{r}(T)
, for each
n\geq N_{r}(T)
, there is a
\gamma:=\gamma(n,x)\in\Gamma
satisfying

xB(e,r)\subseteq D^{n}\big(\gamma\big)V(n,T)=V\big(n,\gamma T\big)

using Proposition
2.4
(
c
). Then Proposition
2.4
implies

P=\big(S^{n}(\omega_{0})\big)|_{xB(e,r)}\prec\big(S^{n}(\omega_{0})\big)|_{V(n,\gamma T)\cap\Gamma}=S^{n}\Big(\omega_{0}|_{\gamma T}\Big).

Since
W(\omega_{0})_{T}\subseteq W(S)
, the patch
\omega_{0}|_{\gamma T}
is
S
-legal and so
P\in W(S)
.
∎

When our starting configuration
\omega_{0}
and our substitution satisfy rather mild conditions, we are guaranteed that our approximations
S^{n}(\omega_{0})
eventually contain all legal patches for any fixed sized support. Recall that every letter
a\in{\mathcal{A}}
is viewed as patch with support
\{e\}
.

Lemma 4.2
.

Consider a substitution on
{\mathcal{A}}^{\Gamma}
with associated substitution map
S
.
If for
\omega_{0}\in{\mathcal{A}}^{\Gamma}
, there is an
n_{0}\in{\mathbb{N}}
such that
{\mathcal{A}}\subseteq W(S^{n}(\omega_{0}))
for all
n\geq n_{0}
, then for each
r>0
, there exists an
M_{r}\geq n_{0}
satisfying

W\big(S^{n}(\omega_{0})\big)\supseteq W(S)_{B(e,r)}\quad\text{for all}\quad n\geq M_{r}.

In particular, if the substitution rule
S_{0}
is primitive, then the constant
M_{r}
can be chosen independently of
\omega_{0}
.

Proof.

Let
r>0
. By definition of
S
-legal patches, for each
Q\in W(S)_{B(e,r)}
, there exists an
m_{Q}\in\mathbb{N}
and a letter
a_{Q}\in{\mathcal{A}}
such that
Q\prec S^{m_{Q}}(a_{Q})
. Since
W(S)_{B(e,r)}
is finite, the following maximum exists

m_{r}:=\max_{Q\in W(S)_{B(e,r)}}m_{Q}.

Set
M_{r}:=n_{0}+m_{r}
.
Let
n\geq M_{r}
. For
Q\in W(S)_{B(e,r)}
, let
a_{Q}\in{\mathcal{A}}
be chosen as before. Since
{\mathcal{A}}\subseteq W(S^{n}(\omega_{0}))
for
n\geq n_{0}
, there is a letter
b\in{\mathcal{A}}
such that
b\prec\omega_{0}
and
a_{Q}\prec S^{n-n_{Q}}(b)
holds since
n-m_{r}\geq n_{0}
. Hence,

Q\prec S^{n_{Q}}(a_{Q})\prec S^{n_{Q}}\big(S^{n-n_{Q}}(b)\big)=S^{n}(b)\prec S^{n}(\omega_{0})

follows implying
Q\in W\big(S^{n}(\omega_{0})\big)
since
\prec
is transitive. Since
Q\in W(S)_{B(e,r)}
was arbitrary, we conclude
W(S)_{B(e,r)}\subseteq W(S^{n}(\omega_{0}))
for
n\geq M_{r}
.

The case when the substitution rule
S_{0}
is primitive follows then directly from the definition by setting
n_{0}=L
, for the
L\in{\mathbb{N}}
in Definition
2.7
.
∎

With this at hand we can now generalize
[
Bec16
, Theorem 6.2.3]
to our setting, and establish a sufficient condition for convergence of the iterative approximations.

Proposition 4.3
.

Let
(T,N_{T})
be a testing tuple for a primitive substitution on
{\mathcal{A}}^{\Gamma}
with substitution map
S
. If for
\omega_{0}\in{\mathcal{A}}^{\Gamma}
,
W(\omega_{0})_{T}\subseteq W(S)
, then

\lim_{n\to\infty}\overline{\text{$\mathchar 525$}\big(S^{n}(\omega_{0})\big)}=\Omega(S)\text{ in }{\mathcal{J}}.

Proof.

Due to equation (
1.3
), it suffices to prove that for every

, there exists an
N_{r}(T)
such that
W(S^{n}(\omega_{0}))_{B(e,r)}=W(S)_{B(e,r)}
if

. The existence of

follows from Lemma

and Lemma

.
∎

As a consequence of the previous proposition, we conclude that if any subsequence of iterative approximations converges then the whole sequence converges.

Corollary 4.4
.

Let
{\mathcal{S}}
be a primitive substitution datum over a dilation datum
{\mathcal{D}}
. Let
\omega_{0}\in{\mathcal{A}}^{\Gamma}
and denote
\Omega_{n}:=\overline{\text{$\mathchar 525$}\big(S^{n}(\omega_{0})\big)}

(i)

The iterative approximation sequence,

\Omega(S)
.

(ii)

There exists a subsequence
\big(\Omega_{n_{k}}\big)_{k=1}^{\infty}
converging to
\Omega(S)

Proof.

(i)
\Rightarrow
(ii): This is obvious.

(ii)
\Rightarrow
(i): Using Proposition
4.3
, it suffices to show that there is an

such that

, where

is a testing domain. Since

∎

Lemma 5.1

5.3.
A lower bound on the rate of convergence

In this subsection, we will prove a lower bound on the rate of convergence of
\delta_{H}\big(\overline{\text{$\mathchar 525$}(S^{n}(\omega_{0}))},\Omega(S)\big)
if
\omega_{0}
is periodic. To this end, we need the concept of lower box counting dimension of a subshift
\Omega
. If
\Omega\in{\mathcal{J}}
is a subshift, then the
lower box counting dimension
is defined by

\underline{\dim}_{B}(\Omega):=\liminf_{r\to\infty}\frac{\log\big(|W(\Omega)_{B(e,r)}|\big)}{\log(r)}=\liminf_{r\to\infty}\frac{\log\big(|W(\Omega)_{B(e,r)}|\big)}{-\log(\frac{1}{2(r+1)})}.

Let us shortly explain why we call this the lower box counting dimension.
Recall that
{\mathcal{A}}^{\Gamma}
is a totally disconnected compact metric space with metric
{\mathrm{d}_{c}}
defined in equation (
1.2
). In order to define the box counting dimension, one counts the number of sets of diameter at most
\frac{1}{2(r+1)}
that are needed to cover the set
\Omega
. By the choice of the metric this is exactly the number of different patches with support
B(e,r)
that
\Omega
admits, namely the patch counting function
|W(\Omega)_{B(e,r)}|
.
One can show that for a linearly repetitive subshift
\Omega
, the patch counting function is at most of the order
r^{\kappa}
where
\kappa
is the homogeneous dimension defined in Section
2.1
. This shows that the box counting dimension of
\Omega(S)
is at most
\kappa
.
We point out at this point that
\underline{\dim}_{B}(\Omega)
is also called
lower power entropy
, see e.g.
[
Pet16
, Section 2.5]
.
We note that due to the choice of metric in equation (
1.2
), this is not the standard box dimension one considers on subshifts. In fact, the standard Hausdorff dimension of
\Omega(S)
on
\mathbb{Z}^{d}
is
0
, since the entropy of a subshift is the same as its Hausdorff dimension, cf.
[
Sim15
, Theorem 4.2]
or
[
Fur67
, Proposition III.1]
.

Proposition 5.8
.

Let
S
be the substitution map of a substitution on
{\mathcal{A}}^{\Gamma}
with dilation datum
{\mathcal{D}}=\big(G,d,(D_{\lambda})_{\lambda>0},\Gamma,V\big)
and primitive substitution datum
{\mathcal{S}}=({\mathcal{A}},\lambda_{0},S_{0})
. Let
\kappa>0
be the homogeneous dimension of
\Gamma
.
If
\underline{\dim}_{B}(\Omega(S))>0
, then for each periodic
\omega_{0}\in{\mathcal{A}}^{\Gamma}
, there exists a constant
C(\omega_{0})>0
such that

\delta_{H}\big(\text{$\mathchar 525$}(S^{n}(\omega_{0})),\Omega(S)\big)\geq C(\omega_{0})\left(\frac{1}{\lambda_{0}^{n}}\right)^{\frac{\kappa}{\underline{\dim}_{B}(\Omega(S))}}\quad\mbox{ for all }n\in{\mathbb{N}}.

Proof.

Since
\omega_{0}
is periodic, there is a finite set
M\subseteq\Gamma
such that
\text{$\mathchar 525$}(\omega_{0})=\{\eta\omega_{0}\,|\,\eta\in M\}
. By Proposition
2.15
, we have

\text{$\mathchar 525$}(S^{n}(\omega_{0}))=\big\{\eta S^{n}(\omega_{0})\,|\,\eta\in(D^{n}[V]\cap\Gamma)\cdot D^{n}[M]\big\},\qquad n\in{\mathbb{N}}.

Since
V
is relatively compact, there is an
r_{+}>0
such that
V\subseteq B(e,r_{+})
. Thus,
D^{n}[V]\cap\Gamma\subseteq B(e,r_{+}\lambda_{0}^{n})
.
Since
\Gamma
has exact polynomial growth, see Section
2.1
, there is a constant
C_{\Gamma}>0
such that
|B(e,r)\cap\Gamma|\leq C_{\Gamma}r^{\kappa}
for all
r\geq 1
.
With all this at hand, we estimate

|\text{$\mathchar 525$}(S^{n}(\omega_{0}))|\leq|D^{n}[M]|\cdot|D^{n}[V]\cap\Gamma|\leq|M|\cdot|B(e,r_{+}\lambda_{0}^{n})\cap\Gamma|\leq|M|\cdot C_{\Gamma}\cdot r_{+}^{\kappa}\cdot\lambda_{0}^{n\kappa}.

Suppose that
\delta_{H}\big(\text{$\mathchar 525$}(S^{n}(\omega_{0})),\Omega(S)\big)<\frac{1}{r+1}
for
r>1
. Then
W(S^{n}(\omega_{0}))_{B(e,r)}=W(S)_{B(e,r)}
follows from equality (
1.3
). Note that if
P,Q\in W(S^{n}(\omega_{0}))_{B(e,r)}
are different, then there are two different
\omega_{P},\omega_{Q}\in\text{$\mathchar 525$}(S^{n}(\omega_{0}))
satisfying
\omega_{P}|_{B(e,r)}=P
and
\omega_{Q}|_{B(e,r)}=Q
. Hence,
|\text{$\mathchar 525$}(S^{n}(\omega_{0}))|\geq|W(S^{n}(\omega_{0}))_{B(e,r)}|
follows. Combining this with the previous considerations yields

|M|\cdot C_{\Gamma}\cdot r_{+}^{\kappa}\cdot\lambda_{0}^{n\kappa}\geq|\text{$\mathchar 525$}(S^{n}(\omega_{0}))|\geq|W(S^{n}(\omega_{0}))_{B(e,r)}|=|W(S)_{B(e,r)}|.

The last term can be estimated using the lower box counting dimension. In particular, there is a constant
C_{B}>0
such that
|W(S)_{B(e,r)}|\geq C_{B}r^{\underline{\dim}_{B}(\Omega(S))}
. Hence, we obtain

|M|\cdot C_{\Gamma}\cdot r_{+}^{\kappa}\cdot\lambda_{0}^{n\kappa}\geq C_{B}r^{\underline{\dim}_{B}(\Omega(S))}.

To conclude, we proved the implication

\delta_{H}\big(\text{$\mathchar 525$}(S^{n}(\omega_{0})),\Omega(S)\big)<\frac{1}{r+1}\;\textrm{for}\;r>1\qquad\Longrightarrow\qquad r\leq\left(\frac{|M|\lambda_{0}^{n\kappa}}{C_{1}}\right)^{\frac{1}{\underline{\dim}_{B}(\Omega(S))}}=:R(n)

where
C_{1}:=\frac{C_{B}}{C_{\Gamma}\cdot r_{+}^{\kappa}}
. Since
\lambda_{0}>1
,
R(n)
diverges implying
R_{0}:=\min_{n\in{\mathbb{N}}}R(n)
exists and is positive. Thus, there is a
C_{2}>1
(independent of
n\in{\mathbb{N}}
) such that
(C_{2}-1)R_{0}>1
. Hence,
(C_{2}-1)R(n)>1
follows for all
n\in{\mathbb{N}}
implying

r(n):=C_{2}R(n)-1>R(n).

Then the previous considerations imply by contraposition that

\delta_{H}\big(\text{$\mathchar 525$}(S^{n}(\omega_{0})),\Omega(S)\big)\geq\frac{1}{r(n)+1}\geq\frac{1}{C_{2}}\left(\frac{C_{1}}{|M|}\right)^{\frac{1}{\underline{\dim}_{B}(\Omega(S))}}\left(\frac{1}{\lambda_{0}^{n}}\right)^{\frac{\kappa}{\underline{\dim}_{B}(\Omega(S))}}

proving the claim with

C(\omega_{0}):=\frac{1}{C_{2}}\left(\frac{C_{1}}{|M|}\right)^{\frac{1}{\underline{\dim}_{B}(\Omega(S))}}.

∎

Remark 5.9
.

For
\Gamma=\mathbb{Z}^{d}
one can adjust
[
Len04
, Lemma 2.2]
to the symbolic setting in order to conclude
|W(\Omega)_{B(e,r)}|\geq Cr^{d}
if
r\geq 1
is large enough. Thus the lower box counting dimension of
\Omega(S)
satisfies
\underline{\dim}_{B}(\Omega(S))=d=\kappa
in this case.
We conclude that if
S
is a substitution map of a primitive non-periodic substitution on
{\mathcal{A}}^{{\mathbb{Z}}^{d}}
(or a block substitution), then Proposition
5.8
asserts

\delta_{H}\big(\text{$\mathchar 525$}(S^{n}(\omega_{0})),\Omega(S)\big)\geq C(\omega_{0})\frac{1}{\lambda_{0}^{n}}

for all periodic
\omega_{0}\in{\mathcal{A}}^{{\mathbb{Z}}^{d}}
, where
C(\omega_{0})>0
is a constant depending on
\omega_{0}
. This shows in particular that our upper bound proven in Proposition
5.6
is asymptotically optimal in this case.

6.
Algorithm to reduce testing domains

In order to apply Theorem
2.14
to specific examples, it is important to compute the graphs
G_{S}(T,N_{T})
. The smaller the testing domain is, the smaller the graphs become. Therefore minimizing the testing domain is computationally crucial. We provide a general algorithm for this purpose and apply it to the Heisenberg group. The Python source code, along with its documentation can be found in the GitHub repository
[
Ten24b
]
.

We remind the reader that a testing tuple always exists by Proposition
5.3
. Furthermore, a testing tuple depends on a dilation datum
{\mathcal{D}}
with associated stretch factor
\lambda_{0}>1
. We continue using the notation
D:=D_{\lambda_{0}}
.

Lemma 6.1
.

Consider a dilation datum
{\mathcal{D}}
with associated stretch factor
\lambda_{0}>1
. Let
T_{0}\subseteq\Gamma
be a testing domain of the substitution, and let
T\subseteq\Gamma
be finite. Then
T
is a testing domain of the substitution if and only if there exists an
N_{0}\in\mathbb{N}
such that for all
x\in\Gamma
, there exists a
\gamma=\gamma(x)\in\Gamma
satisfying

(6.1)

xT_{0}\subseteq D^{N_{0}}(\gamma)V(N_{0},T).

Proof.

If
T
is a testing domain then (
6.1
) follows immediately from Definition
2.11
(
a
), using that
T_{0}
is finite. Thus, it is contained in a ball
B(e,r_{0})
for some
r_{0}>0
. Now suppose the inclusion (
6.1
) holds with
N_{0}\in{\mathbb{N}}
and we prove that
T
is a testing domain. Let
r>0
. Since
T_{0}
is a testing domain, there is an
N_{r}(T_{0})\in{\mathbb{N}}
satisfying Definition
2.11
(
a
). Set
N_{r}(T):=N_{r}(T_{0})+N_{0}
. Let
x\in\Gamma
and
n\geq N_{r}(T)
. Since
n-N_{0}\geq N_{r}(T_{0})
, and
T_{0}
is a testing domain, there is an
\eta=\eta(x,n-N_{0})\in\Gamma
such that

xB(e,r)\subseteq D^{n-N_{0}}(\eta)V(n-N_{0},T_{0})=V(n-N_{0},\eta T_{0}),

where we used Proposition
2.4
(
c
) in the last equality. By equation (
6.1
), there is an
\gamma=\gamma(\eta)\in\Gamma
such that
\eta T_{0}\subseteq D^{N_{0}}(\gamma)V(N_{0},T)=V(N_{0},\gamma T)
. Combined with the previous considerations, Proposition
2.4
(
c
) and Lemma
5.1
(
a
),(
b
) imply that

xB(e,r)\subseteq V(n-N_{0},\eta T_{0})\subseteq V\big(n-N_{0},V(N_{0},\gamma T)\cap\Gamma\big)=V(n-N_{0}+N_{0},\gamma T)=D^{n}(\gamma)V(n,T).

Thus,
T
satisfies Definition
2.11
(
a
), namely
T
is a testing domain.
∎

We aim at showing that it is sufficient to consider only finitely many
x
to invoke Lemma
6.1
, see Lemma
6.3
and Proposition
6.4
below. To this end, we use the following observation.

Lemma 6.2
.

Consider a dilation datum
{\mathcal{D}}
with associated stretch factor
\lambda_{0}>1
. Then for
n\in{\mathbb{N}}
, the sets
D^{n}[V]
and
V(n)
(defined in equation (
2.1
)) are fundamental domains of
D^{n}[\Gamma]
.

Proof.

We first prove that
D^{n}[V]
is a fundamental domain for
D^{n}[\Gamma]
. Since
D
is an automorphism, observe

D^{n}[\Gamma]\cdot D^{n}[V]=D^{n}[\Gamma\cdot V]=D^{n}[G]=G.

In addition, for
\gamma,\eta\in\Gamma
,

D^{n}(\gamma)D^{n}[V]\cap D^{n}(\eta)D^{n}[V]\neq\emptyset\quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad D^{n}[\gamma V]\cap D^{n}[\eta V]\neq\emptyset\quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad\gamma V\cap\eta V\neq\emptyset

holds proving the claim. Next, we show that
V(n)
is a fundamental domain of
D^{n}[\Gamma]
by induction. For the induction base, observe that

D^{1}[V]=D[V]=D\big[(V\cap\Gamma)\cdot V\big]=V(1).

Therefore,
V(1)
is a fundamental domain of
D(\Gamma)
by the previous considerations. For the induction step, suppose that
V(n)
is a fundamental domain of
D^{n}[\Gamma]
. Thus, we have

D^{n}[\Gamma]\cdot(V(n)\cap\Gamma)=\bigcup_{\gamma\in\Gamma}D^{n}(\gamma)\big(V(n)\cap\Gamma\big)=\Big(\bigcup_{\gamma\in\Gamma}D^{n}(\gamma)V(n)\Big)\cap\Gamma=D^{n}[\Gamma]\cdot V(n)\cap\Gamma=\Gamma.

Since
D
is an automorphism, we conclude

\displaystyle D^{n+1}[\Gamma]\cdot V(n+1)

\displaystyle=D^{n+1}[\Gamma]\cdot D\big[(V(n)\cap\Gamma)\cdot V\big]=D\big[{D^{n}[\Gamma]\cdot\big((V(n)\cap\Gamma)}\cdot V\big)\big]

\displaystyle=D\big[\underbrace{\big(D^{n}[\Gamma]\cdot(V(n)\cap\Gamma)\big)}_{=\Gamma}\cdot V\big]=D\big[G\big]=G.

In addition, Proposition
2.4
(
c
) and Lemma
5.1
(
c
) imply for distinct
\gamma,\eta\in\Gamma
that

D^{n+1}(\gamma)V(n+1)\cap D^{n+1}(\eta)V(n+1)=V(n+1,\{\gamma\})\cap V(n+1,\{\eta\})=\emptyset.

This finishes the induction.
∎

Lemma 6.3
.

Consider a dilation datum
{\mathcal{D}}
with associated stretch factor
\lambda_{0}>1
. Let
n\in{\mathbb{N}}
and let
V_{n}
be a relatively compact fundamental domain for
D^{n}[\Gamma]
. Then, the following statements are equivalent for finite sets
T_{1},T_{2}\subseteq\Gamma
.

(i)

For every
x\in\Gamma
, there exists a
\gamma(x)\in\Gamma
satisfying
xT_{1}\subseteq D^{n}(\gamma(x))V(n,T_{2})
.

(ii)

For every
x\in V_{n}\cap\Gamma
, there exists a
\gamma(x)\in\Gamma
satisfying
xT_{1}\subseteq D^{n}(\gamma(x))V(n,T_{2})
.

Proof.

Clearly
(i)
implies
(ii)
. Suppose
(ii)
holds and let
x\in\Gamma
. Since
G=\bigsqcup_{\eta\in\Gamma}D^{n}(\eta)V_{n}
, there exist
\eta\in\Gamma
and
y\in V_{n}\cap\Gamma
, such that
x=D^{n}(\eta)y
. Applying
(ii)
for
y
, there exists a
\gamma(y)\in\Gamma
satisfying
yT_{1}\subseteq D^{n}(\gamma(y))V(n,T_{2})
. Hence,

xT_{1}=D^{n}(\eta)yT_{1}\subseteq D^{n}\big(\eta\gamma(y)\big)V(n,T_{2}).

Setting
\gamma(x):=\eta\gamma(y)
shows that
(i)
holds.
∎

According to
[
BHP21
, Theorem 5.13]
, for every dilation datum
{\mathcal{D}}
with associated stretch factor
\lambda_{0}>1
, there is a constant
C_{+}>0
satisfying
V(n)\subseteq B(e,\lambda_{0}^{n}C_{+})
for all
n\in{\mathbb{N}}
. This will be used in the following proposition to obtain a computable sufficient condition for testing domains.

Proposition 6.4
.

Consider a dilation datum
{\mathcal{D}}
with associated stretch factor
\lambda_{0}>1
and
C_{+}>0
as above.
Let
T_{0}\subseteq\Gamma
be a testing domain of the substitution with
e\in T_{0}
. Then the following assertions are equivalent for a finite set
T\subseteq\Gamma
and
R_{T}>0
satisfying
T\subseteq B(e,R_{T})
.

(i)

The set
T
is a testing domain of the substitution.

(ii)

There exists an
N_{0}\in{\mathbb{N}}
such that for all
x\in D^{N_{0}}[V]\cap\Gamma
, there is a
\gamma_{x}\in B\big(D^{-N_{0}}(x),R_{T}+C_{+}\big)\cap\Gamma
such that
xT_{0}\subseteq D^{N_{0}}(\gamma_{x})V(N_{0},T)
.

Proof.

For each
n\in{\mathbb{N}}
,
D^{n}[V]
is a fundamental domain for
D^{n}[\Gamma]
by Lemma
6.2
. By Lemma
6.1
and Lemma
6.3
, the statement
(i)
is equivalent to the fact that for all
x\in D^{N_{0}}[V]\cap\Gamma
, there exists
\gamma_{x}\in\Gamma
such that
xT_{0}\subseteq D^{N_{0}}(\gamma_{x})V(N_{0},T)
.
Thus, it suffices to prove that
\gamma_{x}
can be chosen in the set
B\big(D^{-N_{0}}(x),R_{T}+C_{+}\big)
.

Let us first note that
x\in D^{N_{0}}(\gamma_{x})V(N_{0},T)
, since
e\in T_{0}
.
Proposition
2.4
(
c
) and Lemma
5.1
(
c
) imply
V(N_{0},T)\subseteq\bigsqcup_{\eta\in T}D^{N_{0}}(\eta)V(N_{0})
.
Recall that
T\subseteq B(e,R_{T})
,
D^{N_{0}}\big[B(e,r)\big]=B(e,\lambda_{0}^{N_{0}}r)
for
r>0
and
V(N_{0})\subseteq B\big(e,\lambda_{0}^{N_{0}}C_{+}\big)
. Relying on these facts, we conclude that

\displaystyle x\in D^{N_{0}}(\gamma_{x})V(N_{0},T)\subseteq D^{N_{0}}(\gamma_{x})\Bigg(\bigsqcup_{\eta\in T}D^{N_{0}}(\eta)V(N_{0})\Bigg)

\displaystyle\subseteq D^{N_{0}}(\gamma_{x})\Bigg(B\big(e,\lambda_{0}^{N_{0}}R_{T}\big)\cdot B\big(e,\lambda_{0}^{N_{0}}C_{+}\big)\Bigg)

\displaystyle\subseteq D^{N_{0}}(\gamma_{x})D^{N_{0}}\bigg[B\big(e,R_{T}+C_{+}\big)\bigg],

using that the metric
d
is left-invariant for the last inclusion.
Hence,
\gamma_{x}\in D^{-N_{0}}(x)\big(B(e,R_{T}+C_{+})\big)^{-1}
follows.
Since
d
is a left-invariant metric, we have
B(e,r)^{-1}=B(e,r)
implying

\gamma_{x}\in D^{-N_{0}}(x)\cdot B(e,R_{T}+C_{+})=B\big(D^{-N_{0}}(x),R_{T}+C_{+}\big).

∎

We now use Proposition
6.4
to propose an algorithm (Algorithm

Let

Let

such that

to check if
