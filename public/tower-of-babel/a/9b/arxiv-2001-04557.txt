A stable algorithm for divergence-free radial basis functions in the flat limit
Kathryn P. Drake, Grady B. Wright

Abstract
The direct method used for calculating smooth radial basis function (RBF) interpolants in the flat limit becomes numerically unstable. The RBF-QR algorithm bypasses this ill-conditioning using a clever change of basis technique. We extend this method for computing interpolants involving matrix-valued kernels, specifically surface divergence-free RBFs on the sphere, in the flat limit. Results illustrating the effectiveness of this algorithm are presented for a divergence-free vector field on the sphere from samples at scattered points.



1
Introduction

Tangential vector fields to a sphere are important in many areas of geophysical sciences, from the surface of the ocean to the ionosphere
[
1
]
. Often the values of these vector fields may only be known at “scattered” locations, e.g., from measurement taken from rawinsondes, airplanes, buoys, remote sensing devices, or from output from certain numerical models (cf.
[
2
, §4]
), and values of the field must be approximated at other locations, e.g., on a grid or mesh. Additionally, these tangential fields may satisfy certain physical constraints, such as being surface divergence-free (div-free) or curl-free, that must be preserved in the approximation. A radial basis function (RBF) technique was developed exactly for these applications in the papers
[
3
,
4
]
. The idea is to construct a positive definite kernel from a radial basis function in such a way that shifts of the kernel can be linearly combined to yield a div-free or curl-free interpolant of the underlying field.
This technique has the added benefits that it gives a well-posed interpolant for scattered data, is devoid of any coordinate singularities, and naturally allows a scalar potential for the field to be extracted
[
4
]
.

When using a smooth RBF that features a shape parameter,
\varepsilon
, to construct these div-free or curl-free kernels, one often finds that the best accuracy of the interpolated field is achieved when
\varepsilon
is small, corresponding to a flat kernel, but that the direct way of computing the interpolant (often called RBF-Direct) is prohibitively ill-conditioned. This is exactly analogous to the standard RBF interpolation problem for scalar functions. In the scalar setting, three distinctly different numerical algorithms have been presented thus far in the literature to bypass this ill-conditioning and open up the complete range of
\varepsilon
that can be considered. These are the RBF-RA method
[
5
,
6
]
, the RBF-QR method
[
7
,
8
,
9
]
, and the RBF-GA method
[
10
]
. The present short note focuses on the RBF-QR method from
[
8
]
, which is specific to RBF interpolation on the sphere. This algorithm exploits the Mercer expansion of the scalar RBFs in terms of spherical harmonics to change the interpolation basis so that the the ill-conditioning associated with small
\varepsilon
is analytically removed. It also shows that scalar RBF interpolants converge to spherical harmonic interpolants in the flat limit (i.e.
\varepsilon\rightarrow 0
). We show how this algorithm, which we call the vector RBF-QR, can be extended to bypass the ill-conditioning associated with surface div-free RBF interpolation for small
\varepsilon
and demonstrate that these interpolants converge to div-free vector spherical harmonic interpolants as
\varepsilon\rightarrow 0
. The algorithm also extends naturally to the surface curl-free case, but the sake of brevity we leave out these details and refer the reader to
[
11
]
.

The rest of the paper is organized as follows: In Section 2, we introduce some notation and background on div-free RBF interpolation and scalar/vector spherical harmonics. Section 3 contains the description of the new vector RBF-QR algorithm and Section 4 contains a numerical example illustrating the stability of the algorithm in the flat limit. We conclude with some brief comments in Section 5.

2
Notation and preliminaries

2.1
Surface div-free vector fields

Any
C^{1}
tangential velocity field
\mathbf{u}
on
\mathbb{S}^{2}
that is surface div-free can be written as

\displaystyle\mathbf{u}=\nabla^{\ast}\times(\psi\widehat{\mathbf{r}})=-\underbrace{\hat{\mathbf{r}}\times\nabla^{\ast}}_{\mathbf{L}^{\ast}}\psi,

where
\nabla^{\ast}
denotes the gradient in spherical coordinates,
\hat{\mathbf{r}}
is the unit radial vector in spherical coordinate basis, and
\psi
is some
C^{2}
scalar-valued function on
\mathbb{S}^{2}
. The function
\psi
is called the
stream function
and is unique up to a constant for a given
\mathbf{u}

[
12
, Proposition 2.1]
.

The operator
\mathbf{L}^{\ast}
, which is sometimes called the surface-curl operator or the
rot
operator, can be written entirely in extrinsic (Cartesian) coordinates as
\mathbf{L}=\hat{\mathbf{n}}\times\nabla
, where
\nabla
is the standard
\mathbb{R}^{3}
gradient in the Cartesian basis, and
\hat{\mathbf{n}}
is the unit normal vector to
\mathbb{S}^{2}
in the Cartesian basis
[
3
]
. Here we have dropped the
\ast
from
\mathbf{L}
to indicate it is defined in extrinsic coordinates. For scalar-valued functions
\psi
that can be extended smoothly from
\mathbb{S}^{2}
to
\mathbb{R}^{3}
, we can generate a tangential velocity field that is surface div-free using
\mathbf{u}=\mathbf{L}\psi
. The operator
\mathbf{L}
can be simplified further by noting that
\hat{\mathbf{n}}
at a point
\mathbf{x}=(x,y,z)
on
\mathbb{S}^{2}
is just
\mathbf{x}
. In what proceeds, we use the following notation for the operator
\mathbf{L}
:

\displaystyle\mathbf{L}_{\mathbf{x}}=\underbrace{\begin{pmatrix}0&-z&y\\
z&0&-x\\
-y&x&0\end{pmatrix}}_{\displaystyle Q(\mathbf{x})}\nabla_{\mathbf{x}},

(1)

where
Q(\mathbf{x})
applied to a vector in
\mathbb{R}^{3}
gives the cross product of
\mathbf{x}
with that vector and the subscripts are used to indicate what variable each operator is applied to. We note that one of the main benefits of working in extrinsic coordinates is that artificial coordinate singularities (e.g. pole singularities) can be avoided.

2.2
Surface div-free RBF interpolation

Surface div-free RBF interpolation is similar to scalar RBF interpolation in the sense that one constructs interpolants from linear combinations of shifts of a kernel at each of the given data sites; for a review of scalar RBF interpolation, we refer the reader to
[
13
]
. The difference between the two approaches is that surface div-free RBF interpolants use a matrix-valued kernel
\Phi_{\rm div}
whose columns are surface div-free. A detailed discussion of the construction of
\Phi_{\rm div}
is given in
[
3
]
. For the sake of brevity, we do not repeat this derivation, but only present the final result. Let
\phi
be a scalar-valued, radial kernel on
\mathbb{R}^{3}
with at least two continuous derivatives and let
\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y}\in\mathbb{S}^{2}
, then
\Phi_{\rm div}
is constructed using the operator
\mathbf{L}
in (
1
) as

\Phi_{\rm div}(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y})=\mathbf{L}_{\mathbf{x}}^{\phantom{T}}\mathbf{L}_{\mathbf{y}}^{T}\phi\left(\|\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{y}\|\right)=-Q(\mathbf{x})\left(\nabla_{\mathbf{x}}^{\phantom{T}}\nabla_{\mathbf{y}}^{T}\phi\left(\|\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{y}\|\right)\right)Q(\mathbf{y})=Q(\mathbf{x})\left(\nabla_{\mathbf{x}}^{\phantom{T}}\nabla_{\mathbf{x}}^{T}\phi\left(\|\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{y}\|\right)\right)Q(\mathbf{y}),

(2)

where
\|\cdot\|
denotes the vector two-norm and we have used the fact that the
Q
matrix in (
1
) is anti-symmetric and
\nabla_{\mathbf{y}}^{T}\phi\left(\|\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{y}\|\right)=-\nabla_{\mathbf{x}}^{T}\phi\left(\|\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{y}\|\right)
. It is straightforward to show that for any
\mathbf{c}\in\mathbb{R}^{3}
that is tangent to
\mathbb{S}^{2}
at
\mathbf{y}
, the vector
\Phi_{\rm div}(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y})\mathbf{c}
is surface div-free in
\mathbf{x}
and centered at the point
\mathbf{y}
; see Figure
1
for an illustration.

\mathbf{s}(\mathbf{x})=\sum_{j=1}^{n}\Phi_{\rm div}(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y}_{j})\mathbf{c}_{j},

(3)

where the coefficients
\mathbf{c}_{j}\in\mathbb{R}^{3}

A naïve approach to solving for the

by imposing
\mathbf{s}(\mathbf{y}_{j})=\mathbf{u}_{j}

will lead to a singular system of equations since each

(and correspondingly

) can be expressed using only two degrees of freedom rather than three. To see this, let

\mathbf{y}_{j}
, where
\mathbf{n}_{j}
is the outward normal to
\mathbb{S}^{2}
,
\mathbf{b}_{j}
is a unit tangent vector, and
\mathbf{a}_{j}=\mathbf{n}_{j}\times\mathbf{b}_{j}
. Then we can write
\mathbf{c}_{j}
in this basis as
\mathbf{c}_{j}=\alpha_{j}\mathbf{a}_{j}+\beta_{j}\mathbf{b}_{j}
, where
\alpha_{j}=\mathbf{a}_{j}^{T}\mathbf{c}_{j}
and
\beta_{j}=\mathbf{b}_{j}^{T}\mathbf{c}_{j}
.
Using this result, we can express (
3
) as

\displaystyle\mathbf{s}(\mathbf{x})=\sum_{j=1}^{n}\Phi_{\rm div}(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y}_{j})\underbrace{\left[\alpha_{j}\mathbf{a}_{j}+\beta_{j}\mathbf{b}_{j}\right]}_{\displaystyle\mathbf{c}_{j}},

(4)

and write the interpolation conditions as
\mathbf{a}_{i}^{T}\mathbf{s}(\mathbf{y}_{i})=\mathbf{a}_{i}^{T}\mathbf{u}_{i}=:\gamma_{i}
and
\mathbf{b}_{i}^{T}\mathbf{s}(\mathbf{y}_{i})=\mathbf{b}_{i}^{T}\mathbf{u}_{i}=:\delta_{i}
, which leads to the
2n
-by-
2n
system of equations

\displaystyle\sum_{j=1}^{n}\underbrace{\left(\begin{bmatrix}\mathbf{a}_{i}^{T}\\
\mathbf{b}_{i}^{T}\end{bmatrix}\Phi_{\rm div}(\mathbf{y}_{i},\mathbf{y}_{j})\begin{bmatrix}\mathbf{a}_{j}&\mathbf{b}_{j}\end{bmatrix}\right)}_{\displaystyle A_{i,j}}\begin{bmatrix}\alpha_{j}\\
\beta_{j}\end{bmatrix}=\begin{bmatrix}\gamma_{i}\\
\delta_{i}\end{bmatrix},\quad 1\leq i\leq n.

(5)

The interpolation matrix that arises from this system (with entries given by
A_{i,j}
) is positive definite if
\Phi_{\rm div}
is constructed from an appropriately chosen scalar-valued
\phi

[
3
]
, such as any of those listed in Table
13
. Simplifications of the entries of this matrix in terms of derivatives of
\phi
are given in
[
4
]
.

In this work, we choose
\mathbf{a}_{j}
and
\mathbf{b}_{j}
, for the point
\mathbf{y}_{j}=(x_{j},y_{j},z_{j})
, to be the standard meridional and zonal vectors, which can be expressed in Cartesian coordinates as

\mathbf{a}_{j}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{1-z_{j}^{2}}}\begin{bmatrix}-z_{j}x_{j}\\
-z_{j}y_{j}\\
1-z_{j}^{2}\end{bmatrix},\quad\mathbf{b}_{j}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{1-z_{j}^{2}}}\begin{bmatrix}-y_{j}\\
x_{j}\\
0\end{bmatrix}.

(6)

If
\mathbf{y}_{j}=[0,0,\pm 1]
, then we can pick any orthogonal vectors in the
xy
-plane.

We conclude by noting that once the coefficients
\mathbf{c}_{j}
are determined in (
4
), a stream-function
\psi(\mathbf{x})
can obtained for the interpolated field using (
2
)

\displaystyle\mathbf{s}(\mathbf{x})=\underbrace{Q(\mathbf{x})\nabla_{\mathbf{x}}}_{\displaystyle\mathbf{L}_{\mathbf{x}}}\biggl(\underbrace{\sum_{j=1}^{n}\nabla_{\mathbf{x}}^{T}\phi\left(\|\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{y}_{j}\|\right)Q(\mathbf{y}_{j})\mathbf{c}_{j}}_{\displaystyle\psi(\mathbf{x})}\biggr).

(7)

Approximation results for
\psi
in reconstructing the underlying stream function for the field are given in
[
4
]
.

Similar to scalar RBF interpolation, we refer to the method of computing the div-free RBF interpolant by solving the system (
5
) as RBF-Direct. For
\Phi_{\rm div}
that are built from scalar kernels that depend on a shape parameter
\varepsilon
, like those in Table
1
, the RBF-Direct approach becomes extremely ill-conditioned as
\varepsilon\rightarrow 0
. However, as in the scalar RBF case, the interpolant remains well-conditioned and the RBF-QR algorithm allows us to stably compute it.

2.3
Spherical harmonics

The RBF-QR algorithm presented in Section 3 relies on spherical harmonic expansions, both scalar and vector ones, so we briefly review these expansions here. We refer the reader to more detailed discussions in
[
15
]
and
[
16
]
, for the scalar and vector case, respectively.

Let
Y_{\mu}^{\nu}
denote the scalar spherical harmonic of integer degree
\mu\geq 0
and integer order
-\mu\leq\nu\leq\mu
on
\mathbb{S}^{2}
. These functions are the eigenfunctions of the Laplace-Beltrami operator
\Delta_{\mathbb{S}^{2}}
, i.e.,
\Delta_{\mathbb{S}^{2}}Y_{\mu}^{\nu}=-\mu(\mu+1)Y_{\mu}^{\nu}
. We use the real-form of the spherical harmonics functions in Cartesian coordinates, which for
\mathbf{x}=(x,y,z)\in\mathbb{S}^{2}
are given as

\displaystyle Y_{\mu}^{\nu}(\mathbf{x})=\begin{cases}\sqrt{\frac{2\mu+1}{4\pi}}\sqrt{\frac{(\mu-\nu)!}{(\mu+\nu)!}}P_{\mu}^{\nu}(z)\cos\left(\nu\tan^{-1}\left(\frac{y}{x}\right)\right),&\text{ }\nu=0,1,\dots,\mu,\\
\sqrt{\frac{2\mu+1}{4\pi}}\sqrt{\frac{(\mu-\nu)!}{(\mu+\nu)!}}P_{\mu}^{\nu}(z)\sin\left(-\nu\tan^{-1}\left(\frac{y}{x}\right)\right),&\text{ }\nu=-\mu,\dots,-1\end{cases},

(8)

where
P_{\mu}^{\nu}(z)
are the associated Legendre functions of degree
\mu
and order
\nu
. The spherical harmonics form a complete, orthonormal set of basis functions for the space of square-integrable functions on
\mathbb{S}^{2}
, which we denote by
L^{2}(\mathbb{S}^{2})

[
15
]
. Thus, any function
f\in L^{2}(\mathbb{S}^{2})
can be uniquely represented as

\displaystyle f(\mathbf{x})=\sum_{\mu=0}^{\infty}\sum_{\nu=-\mu}^{\mu}\hat{f}_{\mu,\nu}Y_{\mu}^{\nu}(\mathbf{x}),\;\text{where}\;\;\hat{f}_{\mu,\nu}=\int_{\mathbb{S}^{2}}f(\mathbf{x})Y_{\mu}^{\nu}(\mathbf{x})dS.

(9)

Vector spherical harmonics are the vectorial analogue of scalar spherical harmonics and can be used for representing vector-valued functions on
\mathbb{S}^{2}
. There are three types of these vector harmonic functions: one that is normal to
\mathbb{S}^{2}
, one that is tangent to
\mathbb{S}^{2}
and surface curl-free, and one that is tangent to
\mathbb{S}^{2}
and surface div-free
[
16
]
. We focus on the latter as they are the ones used in this paper. Both tangent vector spherical harmonics are the eigenfunctions of the vector Laplace-Beltrami operator.

The surface div-free vector spherical harmonics can be constructed in Cartesian coordinates by applying operator
\mathbf{L}
in (
1
) to the scalar spherical harmonic functions (
8
) as follows
[
16
]
:

\mathbf{w}_{\mu}^{\nu}(\mathbf{x})=\mathbf{L}_{\mathbf{x}}Y_{\mu}^{\nu}(\mathbf{x}),\;\mu>0,\;-\mu\leq\nu\leq\mu.

(10)

These can be normalized as
\overline{\mathbf{w}}_{\mu}^{\nu}=\frac{1}{\mu(\mu+1)}\mathbf{w}_{\mu}^{\nu}(vx)
so they are orthonormal with respect to the
L^{2}(\mathbb{S}^{2})
-vector inner product
\langle\mathbf{f},\mathbf{g}\rangle=\int_{\mathbb{S}^{2}}\mathbf{f}^{T}\mathbf{g}dS
,
where
\mathbf{f}
and
\mathbf{g}
are tangent vector fields on
\mathbb{S}^{2}
.
The surface div-free vector spherical harmonics form a complete orthonormal set of vector basis functions for the space of surface div-free vector fields that are tangent to
\mathbb{S}^{2}
and square integrable. We denote this space as
L_{\rm div}(\mathbb{S}^{2})
. Any function
\mathbf{u}\in L_{\rm div}^{2}(\mathbb{S}^{2})
can be expanded in these harmonics as

\mathbf{u}(\mathbf{x})=\sum_{\mu=1}^{\infty}\sum_{\nu=-\mu}^{\mu}\hat{u}_{\mu,\nu}\overline{\mathbf{w}}_{\mu}^{\nu}(\mathbf{x}),\;\text{where}\;\;\hat{u}_{\mu,\nu}=\int_{\mathbb{S}^{2}}\mathbf{f(\mathbf{x})}^{T}\overline{\mathbf{w}}_{\mu}^{\nu}(\mathbf{x})dS.

(11)

Note here that the sum in this expansion excludes
\mu=0
since
Y_{0}^{0}
is annihilated by
\mathbf{L}
.

As discussed in Section
2.2
, the interpolation problem on the sphere requires that we utilize the tangent basis vectors at
\mathbf{x}\in\mathbb{S}^{2}
, i.e.,
\mathbf{a}_{\mathbf{x}}
and
\mathbf{b}_{\mathbf{x}}
. Therefore, it is relevant to introduce the following notation for the non-normalized surface div-free and curl-free vector spherical harmonics in terms of these basis vectors:

\displaystyle\textnormal{meridional, div-free :}\;G_{\mu}^{\nu}(\mathbf{x})=\mathbf{a}_{\mathbf{x}}^{T}\mathbf{L}_{\mathbf{x}}Y_{\mu}^{\nu}(\mathbf{x}),\qquad\textnormal{zonal, div-free :}\;H_{\mu}^{\nu}(\mathbf{x})=\mathbf{b}_{\mathbf{x}}^{T}\mathbf{L}_{\mathbf{x}}Y_{\mu}^{\nu}(\mathbf{x}).

(12)

2.4
Spherical harmonic expansions of smooth radial kernels

\varepsilon

The central idea behind the vector RBF-QR algorithm will also be to replace the matrix-valued basis with a better basis built from vector spherical harmonic expansions. We can use (
2
) and (
13
) to expand the matrix-valued kernel
\Phi_{\rm div}
in terms of vector spherical harmonics as follows:

\displaystyle\Phi_{\rm div}(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y}_{j})=\sum_{\mu=1}^{\infty}\sideset{}{{}^{\prime}}{\sum}_{\nu=-\mu}^{\mu}\{\varepsilon^{2\mu}c_{\mu,\varepsilon}\mathbf{L}_{\mathbf{x}}Y_{\mu}^{\nu}(\mathbf{x})\}\left(\left.\mathbf{L}_{\mathbf{y}}Y_{\mu}^{\nu}(\mathbf{y})\right|_{\mathbf{y}=\mathbf{y}_{j}}\right)^{T}=\sum_{\mu=1}^{\infty}\sideset{}{{}^{\prime}}{\sum}_{\nu=-\mu}^{\mu}\{\varepsilon^{2\mu}c_{\mu,\varepsilon}\mathbf{w}_{\mu}^{\nu}(\mathbf{x})\}\left(\mathbf{w}_{\mu}^{\nu}(\mathbf{y}_{j})\right)^{T}.

(14)

Here we have used the non-normalized div-free vector spherical harmonics defined in (
10
) for simplicity.

The separation of the expansion (
14
) of a smooth matrix-valued kernel
\Phi_{\rm div}
in terms of increasing powers of
\varepsilon
is essential to the RBF-QR algorithm. It is these powers of
\varepsilon
and not the coefficients
c_{\mu,\varepsilon}
that lead ill-conditioning in the matrix-valued basis for small
\varepsilon
in the RBF-Direct method. The RBF-QR algorithm analytically factors out the effects of these powers of
\varepsilon
from the basis.

3
Vector RBF-QR algorithm

Recall from Section
2.2
that in order to interpolate div-free vector fields tangent to the sphere with the matrix-valued div-free interpolant, we must represent the coefficient vectors and target field samples in terms of the orthonormal tangent basis vectors (e.g., using (

) we saw that this is equivalent to representing the kernel
\Phi_{\rm div}
in terms of these basis vectors. We will denote this kernel as

\vdots&\vdots&\vdots\\

\end{bmatrix}}_{\displaystyle B^{\infty}}\underbrace{\begin{bmatrix}\varepsilon^{2}&&\\

&&\varepsilon^{2}\\
&&&\ddots\vskip-7.22743pt\\

The next step is to truncate these infinite matrices to a vector spherical harmonic degree value
\mu=\mu_{\text{trunc}}
. There are two stipulations for this truncation degree. First,
\mu_{\text{trunc}}
must be large enough that the entries in
\widetilde{P}_{\rm div}
are approximated to machine precision; we refer to this value as
\mu_{\text{eps}}
. The second is that
\mu_{\text{trunc}}
must be large enough that the number of columns in the truncated
B^{\infty}
matrix is greater than or equal to
2n
; we refer to this value as
\mu_{0}
. This condition on
\mu_{0}
is given explicitly as
\mu_{0}=\left\lceil\sqrt{2n+1}-1\right\rceil
since the number of vector spherical harmonic terms in a truncated expansion of
\mu_{0}
is
\mu_{0}(\mu_{0}+2)
. Putting the truncation requirements together gives the condition
\mu_{\text{trunc}}\geq\max(\mu_{0},\mu_{\text{eps}})
. We denote the truncated matrix-matrix product (
19
) as

\displaystyle\widetilde{P}_{\rm div}\approx\begin{bmatrix}\\
&&&B&&&\\

\end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix}\\
&&E&&\\

\end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix}\\

\end{bmatrix}.

The matrix

m=\mu_{\text{trunc}}(\mu_{\text{trunc}}+2)
columns and has the block form

\displaystyle B=\left[\begin{array}[]{c|c|c|c|c|c|c}B_{1}&B_{2}&\cdots&B_{\mu_{0}}&B_{\mu_{0}+1}&\cdots&B_{\mu_{\text{trunc}}}\end{array}\right]

\begin{bmatrix}\frac{c_{\mu,\varepsilon}}{2}G_{\mu}^{0}(\mathbf{y}_{i})\;\\
\frac{c_{\mu,\varepsilon}}{2}H_{\mu}^{0}(\mathbf{y}_{i})\end{bmatrix}\;\qquad j=\mu+1,\end{matrix}\right.\;\;j=1,\ldots,2\mu+1,\;i=1,\ldots,n.

The diagonal

, where

\displaystyle E_{1}=\begin{bmatrix}&\varepsilon^{2}I_{3}\\
&&\varepsilon^{4}I_{5}\\
&&&\ddots\\

&&\ddots\\

(22)

and

\displaystyle Y^{T}=\left[\begin{array}[]{c|c|c|c|c|c|c}Y_{1}^{T}&Y_{2}^{T}&\cdots&Y_{\mu_{0}}^{T}&Y_{\mu_{0}+1}^{T}&\cdots&Y_{\mu_{\text{trunc}}}^{T}\end{array}\right],

where block

is given by

\displaystyle\left(Y_{\mu}\right)_{j,1}=G_{\mu}^{j-(\mu+1)}(\mathbf{x}),\quad\left(Y_{\mu}\right)_{j,2}=H_{\mu}^{j-(\mu+1)}(\mathbf{x}),\quad j=1,\ldots,2\mu+1.

In the flat limit, the truncated basis is still highly ill-conditioned because of the powers of
\varepsilon
in (
19
) (recall that the expansion coefficients
c_{\mu,\varepsilon}
do not affect the conditioning). However, all of these powers of
\varepsilon
are confined to the
E
matrix. To develop a better conditioned basis, we need to factor out the ill-effects of the powers of
\varepsilon
. To simplify the description of this step of the algorithm, we make the assumption that
n=\mu_{0}(\mu_{0}+2)/2
so that the sub-matrix block

\displaystyle\left[\begin{array}[]{c|c|c|c}B_{1}&B_{2}&\cdots&B_{\mu_{0}}\end{array}\right]

of

in (
3
) and diagonal matrix

in (

) are square and of size
2n
-by-
2n
. We also assume that the interpolation node set
\{\mathbf{y}_{j}\}_{j=1}^{n}
is unisolvent with respect to the vector spherical harmonics of degree
\mu_{0}
so that (
3
) is invertible. This restriction means that the interpolation matrix associated with the vector spherical harmonic basis of degree
\mu_{0}
is invertible. When using “scattered” node sets, we have never encountered a situation where the point set fails to be unisolvent.

The final step of the vector RBF-QR algorithm starts with a QR factorization on
B
in (
20
), which gives

\widetilde{P}_{\rm div}\approx Q\underbrace{\left[R_{1}\,|\,R_{2}\right]}_{\displaystyle R}\left[\begin{array}[]{c|c}E_{1}&\\
\hline\cr&E_{2}\\
\end{array}\right]Y.

(24)

Here we have partitioned

into
R_{1}
and
R_{2}
, where
R_{1}
is
2n
-by-
2n
upper-triangular, and
R_{2}
is an
2n
-by-
(m-2n)
full matrix. Since the sub-block (
3
) is invertible by our assumption on the node set, we know
R_{1}
is also invertible. This together with the block structure of

allows us to re-write (
24
) as

\displaystyle\widetilde{P}_{\rm div}\approx QR_{1}\left[I_{2n}\,|\,R_{1}^{-1}R_{2}\right]\left[\begin{array}[]{c|c}E_{1}&\\
\hline\cr&E_{2}\\
\end{array}\right]Y=QR_{1}\left[E_{1}\,|\,R_{1}^{-1}R_{2}E_{2}\right]Y=QR_{1}E_{1}\underbrace{\left[I_{2n}\,|\,E_{1}^{-1}R_{1}^{-1}R_{2}E_{2}\right]}_{\displaystyle\widetilde{B}_{\rm div}}Y.

Since left matrix-multiplication is just a linear combination of the columns of the matrices, it follows from this new expression that any element in the span of the columns of
\widetilde{P}_{\rm div}^{T}
(i.e., the span of the original basis containing shifts of
\widetilde{\Phi}_{\rm div}
) can be represented to machine precision by a linear combination of the columns of
(\widetilde{B}_{\rm div}Y)^{T}
.

The form of
\widetilde{B}_{\rm div}Y
in (

\varepsilon
because it involves computing

. However, this matrix and
E_{2}
are diagonal so we can analytically remove the division by small
\varepsilon
using
[

, Lemma 5.1.2]
to arrive at

\widetilde{B}_{\rm div}Y=\Bigl[I_{2n}\,|\,E_{1}^{-1}R_{1}^{-1}R_{2}E_{2}\Bigr]=\Bigl[I_{2n}\,|\,\left(R_{1}^{-1}R_{2}\right)\circ\underbrace{(E_{1}^{-1}J_{2n,m-2n}E_{2})}_{\displaystyle\widetilde{E}}\Bigr],

(27)

where
J_{\sigma,\tau}
is a
\sigma
-by-
\tau
matrix of
1
’s,
\circ
denotes the Hadamard product (or entry-wise multiplication), and the entries of
\widetilde{E}
can be determine explicitly as

\widetilde{E}=\left[\begin{array}[]{c|c|cc|c}\varepsilon^{2\mu_{0}}J_{3,2\mu_{0}+3}&\varepsilon^{2\mu_{0}+2}J_{3,2\mu_{0}+5}&\cdots&\cdots&\varepsilon^{2\mu_{\text{trunc}}-2}J_{3,2\mu_{\text{trunc}}+1}\\

\hline\cr\varepsilon^{4}J_{2\mu_{0}-1,2\mu_{0}+3}&\varepsilon^{6}J_{2\mu_{0}-1,2\mu_{0}+5}&\cdots&\cdots&\varepsilon^{2\mu_{\text{trunc}}-2\mu_{0}+2}J_{2\mu_{0}-1,2\mu_{\text{trunc}}+1}\\

(28)

The columns
(\widetilde{B}_{\rm div}Y)^{T}
in (
27
) can now be used as a stable basis for the space spanned by
\{\widetilde{{\Psi}}_{\rm div}(\cdot,\mathbf{y}_{j})\}_{j=1}^{n}
for small
\varepsilon
. We note that each element of this basis consists of a div-free vector spherical harmonic of some degree
\leq\mu_{0}
plus some
\mathcal{O}(\varepsilon^{2})
combination of div-free vector spherical harmonics of degree
>\mu_{0}
. This implies that matrix-valued div-free RBF interpolant will converge to a div-free vector spherical harmonic interpolant of degree
\mu_{0}
in limit
\varepsilon\rightarrow 0
, which is analogous to a scalar RBF interpolant converging to a spherical harmonic interpolant in the flat limit
[
8
]
.

Remark 3.1
.

Note that just as with the scalar RBF-QR algorithm
[
8
]
, it is possible to include the spherical harmonic coefficients
c_{\mu,\varepsilon}
in the diagonal matrices
E_{1}
and
E_{2}
and generate a similar analytical simplification for
\widetilde{E}
. This has the added advantage of removing all
\varepsilon
dependence in the actual QR numerical computation and allows the central part of the vector RBF-QR to be performed independent of the radial kernel used.

Remark 3.2
.

When the condition
2n=\mu_{0}(\mu_{0}+2)
is not met, the
R_{1}
matrix in (
24
) will not be square. The algorithm then needs to be modified to move columns from
R_{1}
to
R_{2}
to make
R_{1}
square. This requires a similar move of the corresponding diagonals of
E_{1}
to
E_{2}
. In this case, the form of
\widetilde{E}
in (
28
) also needs to change to include some
\varepsilon^{0}
terms.

Remark 3.3
.

More sophisticated techniques for selecting the truncation level
\mu_{\rm trunc}
are discussed in
[
20
]
for the scalar case and may also be adopted here. These methods can reduce the overall computational time without reducing the approximation properties of the interpolants based on the stable basis.

5
Concluding remarks

The vector RBF-QR algorithm presented here can be used to bypass the ill-conditioning associated with surface div-free RBF interpolation on a sphere in the
\varepsilon\rightarrow 0
limit. This allows for more comprehensive studies of how
\varepsilon
affects the accuracy of the interpolants. The derivation of the algorithm additionally demonstrates the connection between these interpolants and div-free vector spherical harmonics in the flat limit. The algorithm can also be applied straightforwardly to surface curl-free RBF interpolation, since in this case, one simply has to replace the matrix-valued kernel
\Phi_{\rm div}
with the kernel
\Phi_{\rm curl}=(P({\mathbf{x}})\nabla_{\mathbf{x}})(P({\mathbf{y}})\nabla_{\mathbf{y}})^{T}\phi(\|\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{y}\|)
, where
P(\boldsymbol{\xi})=(I-\boldsymbol{\xi}\boldsymbol{\xi}^{T})

[
11
]
. Div-free and curl-free RBFs are also available for interpolation in
\mathbb{R}^{d}

[
23
]
. For this problem it may be possible to extend the RBF-QR algorithms
[
7
,
9
]
developed for scalar RBF interpolation in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
. However, the algorithm in
[
7
]
is limited to the Gaussian kernel and the algorithm in
[
9
]
is limited to kernels with known Mercer series expansions.
