Subdimensional criticality: condensation of lineons and planons in the X-cube model
Ethan Lake, Michael Hermele

Abstract
We study quantum phase transitions out of the fracton ordered phase of the ℤ N \mathbb{Z}_{N} X-cube model. These phase transitions occur when various types of sub-dimensional excitations and their composites are condensed. The condensed phases are either trivial paramagnets, or are built from stacks of d = 2 d=2 or d = 3 d=3 deconfined gauge theories, where d d is the spatial dimension. The nature of the phase transitions depends on the excitations being condensed. Upon condensing dipolar bound states of fractons or lineons, for N ≥ 4 N\geq 4 we find stable critical points described by decoupled stacks of d = 2 d=2 conformal field theories. Upon condensing lineon excitations, when N > 4 N>4 we find a gapless phase intermediate between the X-cube and condensed phases, described as an array of d = 1 d=1 conformal field theories. In all these cases, effective subsystem symmetries arise from the mobility constraints on the excitations of the X-cube phase and play an important role in the analysis of the phase transitions.

To do this it is convenient to perform a duality mapping on the XC model, with the dual representation being formulated in terms of spins living on the dual lattice. In what follows, we will use sans serif symbols to denote elements of the dual lattice, with
\mathsf{i},\mathsf{l},\mathsf{p},
and
\mathsf{c}
standing for dual vertices, links, plaquettes, and cubes, respectively.
Our duality mapping involves matter qubits
\mathsf{x}_{\mathsf{i}},\mathsf{z}_{\mathsf{i}}
living on the vertices of the dual lattice, and gauge qubits
\mathcal{X}_{\mathsf{p}},\mathcal{Z}_{\mathsf{p}}
living on the plaquettes of the dual lattice. Sometimes it will be convenient to use
\mathsf{l}
to refer to a unit vector along the given link, and
\mathsf{p}
to refer to a unit vector normal to the given plaquette, so that for instance
\mathsf{l}{\,\parallel\,}{\mathbf{\hat{z}}}
denotes a link parallel to
{\mathbf{\hat{z}}}
, while
\mathsf{p}{\,\parallel\,}{\mathbf{\hat{z}}}
denotes a plaquette with normal in the
{\mathbf{\hat{z}}}
direction.

The duality works in much the same way as that between the
\mathbb{Z}_{2}
toric code and
\mathbb{Z}_{2}
gauge theory coupled to Ising matter,
Kitaev 2003
with the operators mapping as

X_{l}\rightarrow\mathcal{X}_{\mathsf{p}},\qquad Z_{l}\rightarrow\mathcal{Z}_{\mathsf{p}}\prod_{\mathsf{i}\in\mathsf{p}}\mathsf{z}_{\mathsf{i}},

(5)

where
\mathsf{p}
is the dual lattice plaquette dual to
l
, and the product is over the four corners of
\mathsf{p}
. In the dual formulation, there is a Gauss’ law constraint at each vertex
\mathsf{i}

\mathsf{x}_{\mathsf{i}}=\prod_{\mathsf{p}\ni\mathsf{i}}\mathcal{X}_{\mathsf{p}},

(6)

where the product is over the twelve dual plaquettes meeting
\mathsf{i}
at a corner. The Hamiltonian in this representation is

H_{XC}^{\prime}=-K\sum_{\mathsf{i}}\mathsf{x}_{\mathsf{i}}-g\sum_{\mathsf{c},a}A^{a}_{\mathsf{c}}.

(7)

Here
A^{a}_{\mathsf{c}}
is a product of
\mathcal{Z}_{\mathsf{p}}
over four of the six plaquettes in the cube
\mathsf{c}
, excluding the two plaquettes normal to the
{\mathbf{\hat{a}}}
direction.

We will now modify this model to include a separate field for fracton dipoles. As such dipoles naturally live on the links of the dual lattice, we introduce a qubit on each link with Pauli operators
X_{\mathsf{l}},Z_{\mathsf{l}}
(not to be confused with the Pauli operators in the original formulation of the XC model, which live on the direct lattice), where
X_{\mathsf{l}}
is the dipole number operator and
Z_{\mathsf{l}}
creates a fracton dipole at the link
\mathsf{l}
.

Since single fractons carry gauge charge, a dipole at
\mathsf{l}
should carry gauge charge on the vertices at the two ends of
\mathsf{l}
. We therefore modify the Gauss law constraint to be

\mathsf{x}_{\mathsf{i}}\prod_{\mathsf{l}\ni i}X_{\mathsf{l}}=\prod_{\mathsf{p}\ni\mathsf{i}}\mathcal{X}_{\mathsf{p}},

(8)

where the products are over all the links and plaquettes neighboring the vertex
\mathsf{i}
.
With this Gauss law two fractons at adjacent vertices can be converted into a dipole via the gauge-invariant operator
\mathsf{z}_{\mathsf{i}}Z_{\langle\mathsf{i}\mathsf{j}\rangle}\mathsf{z}_{\mathsf{j}}
, and two dipoles with parallel dipole moments can be created on opposite edges
\mathsf{l},\mathsf{l}^{\prime}
of a plaquette
\mathsf{p}
via the gauge-invariant operator
Z_{\mathsf{l}}\mathcal{Z}_{\mathsf{p}}Z_{\mathsf{l}^{\prime}}
.

To analyze what happens when we condense the dipoles, it will be notationally helpful to fix a standard set of links
\mathsf{l}_{1},\dots,\mathsf{l}_{4}
for each plaquette of a given orientation. Our conventions for each plaquette are shown in
7
, and are such that for any plaquette
\mathsf{l}_{1}
is always parallel to
\mathsf{l}_{2}
, and
\mathsf{l}_{3}
is always parallel to
\mathsf{l}_{4}
.

We now consider the following Hamiltonian:

\displaystyle H_{con}

\displaystyle=-K\sum_{\mathsf{i}}\mathsf{x}_{\mathsf{i}}-g\sum_{\mathsf{i},a}A^{a}_{\mathsf{i}}

(9)

\displaystyle-h\sum_{\mathsf{l}}X_{\mathsf{l}}-\lambda\sum_{\mathsf{p}}(Z_{\mathsf{l}_{1}}\mathcal{Z}_{\mathsf{p}}Z_{\mathsf{l}_{2}}+Z_{\mathsf{l}_{3}}\mathcal{Z}_{\mathsf{p}}Z_{\mathsf{l}_{4}}).

The last term proportional to
\lambda
is a kinetic energy term for the dipoles, and allows dipoles on links parallel to
{\mathbf{\hat{a}}}
to move in planes normal to
{\mathbf{\hat{a}}}
.

When
h/\lambda\gg 1
, we are in the regime where both the dipoles and fractons are gapped, and the gauge field
\mathcal{Z}
is deconfined. In this limit we are clearly in the same phase as
H_{XC}
. In the following we will instead be interested in the limit
h/\lambda\ll 1
, as in this limit the dipoles are condensed. In this limit the second term in (
9
) can be dropped without changing the low-energy physics. This is because
A^{a}_{\mathsf{i}}
can be obtained by taking products of the dipole kinetic terms, so that if these terms are set to
1
then so are the
A^{a}_{\mathsf{i}}
terms.

We will now show that in the phase where dipoles are condensed, the Hamiltonian (
9
) can be transformed into the standard Hamiltonian of
\mathbb{Z}_{2}
gauge theory, plus an unimportant decoupled paramagnet arising from the plaquette gauge fields. For simplicity and to work at a solvable point within the dipole-condensed phase, we set
h=g=0
. Note that if we ignore the plaquette variables
\mathcal{X}_{\mathsf{p}}
, the Gauss law (
8
) becomes the ordinary
\mathbb{Z}_{2}
gauge theory Gauss law constraint. Our strategy will therefore be to
arrive at the
\mathbb{Z}_{2}
gauge theory by performing a unitary transformation designed to eliminate the
\mathcal{X}_{\mathsf{p}}
variables appearing in (
8
).

To begin, for each plaquette, define the CNOT-like operators

U_{\mathsf{p}}\equiv\left(\frac{1+Z_{\mathsf{l}_{1}}Z_{\mathsf{l}_{2}}}{2}\right)+\left(\frac{1-Z_{\mathsf{l}_{1}}Z_{\mathsf{l}_{2}}}{2}\right)X_{\mathsf{p}}.

(10)

Note that
U_{\mathsf{p}}=U^{\dagger}_{\mathsf{p}}
is unitary, and is
not
gauge invariant — thus we can use it to transform both the Hamiltonian and the gauge constraint.

The unitary
U_{\mathsf{p}}
conjugates the various Pauli operators as

\displaystyle U_{\mathsf{p}}\mathcal{Z}_{\mathsf{p}}U_{\mathsf{p}}

\displaystyle=\mathcal{Z}_{\mathsf{p}}Z_{\mathsf{l}_{1}}Z_{\mathsf{l}_{2}}

(11)

\displaystyle U_{\mathsf{p}}X_{\mathsf{l}_{1,2}}U_{\mathsf{p}}

\displaystyle=X_{\mathsf{l}_{1,2}}\mathcal{X}_{\mathsf{p}}

\displaystyle U_{\mathsf{p}}X_{\mathsf{l}_{3,4}}U_{\mathsf{p}}

\displaystyle=X_{\mathsf{l}_{3,4}}\text{,}

with other conjugations trivial.

Noting that
[U_{\mathsf{p}},U_{\mathsf{p}^{\prime}}]=0
, we now perform a unitary conjugation by the operator

\mathcal{U}\equiv\prod_{\mathsf{p}}U_{\mathsf{p}}.

(12)

While the support of
U_{\mathsf{p}}
operators on adjacent plaquettes does overlap,
\mathcal{U}
can be written as a depth-two quantum circuit by dividing the plaquettes into two subsets.
The transformed Hamiltonian then takes the form

\displaystyle H^{\prime}_{con}

\displaystyle=\mathcal{U}H_{con}\mathcal{U}

(13)

\displaystyle=-K\sum_{\mathsf{i}}\mathsf{x}_{\mathsf{i}}-\lambda\sum_{\mathsf{p}}\mathcal{Z}_{\mathsf{p}}-\lambda\sum_{\mathsf{p}}\mathcal{Z}_{\mathsf{p}}\prod_{\mathsf{l}\in\mathsf{p}}Z_{\mathsf{l}}

Here the first term proportional to
\lambda
comes from conjugating terms which hop dipoles between
\mathsf{l}_{1}
and
\mathsf{l}_{2}
links, while the second comes from terms which hop dipoles between
\mathsf{l}_{3}
and
\mathsf{l}_{4}
links. The product in the final term is over the four links in the perimeter of
\mathsf{p}
.

To compute the transformation of the gauge constraint, note that for a given vertex
\mathsf{i}
, for each of the twelve plaquettes meeting
\mathsf{i}
, one of the links
\mathsf{l}_{1},\mathsf{l}_{2}
has
\mathsf{i}
as an endpoint. When the
X_{\mathsf{l}}
operator on this link is conjugated by
U
, it produces by (
11
) an
\mathcal{X}_{\mathsf{p}}
operator, which cancels one of the
\mathcal{X}_{\mathsf{p}}
’s appearing in the Gauss law. The transformed gauge constraint is therefore

\mathsf{x}_{\mathsf{i}}=\prod_{l\ni i}X_{\mathsf{l}},

(14)

which is obtained by conjugating both sides of (
8
) by
\mathcal{U}
. This is exactly the gauge constraint of
\mathbb{Z}_{2}
gauge theory.

It only remains to deform
H^{\prime}_{con}
to the standard solvable Hamiltonian for the deconfined phase of
\mathbb{Z}_{2}
gauge theory. This can obviously be done without closing the gap simply by freezing out the now gauge-invariant
\mathcal{Z}_{\mathsf{p}}
degrees of freedom in (
13
), which are gapped and have no dynamics. More precisely, we may introduce a parameter
s\in[0,1]
and write

\displaystyle H^{\prime}_{con}(s)

\displaystyle\equiv-K\sum_{\mathsf{i}}\mathsf{x}_{\mathsf{i}}-\lambda\sum_{\mathsf{p}}\mathcal{Z}_{\mathsf{p}}

(15)

\displaystyle-\lambda\sum_{\mathsf{p}}[(1-s)\mathcal{Z}_{\mathsf{p}}+s]\prod_{\mathsf{l}\in\mathsf{p}}Z_{\mathsf{l}}.

Clearly at
s=0
we have
H^{\prime}_{con}(0)=H^{\prime}_{con}
, the ground state does not change as a function of
s
, and the gap remains open. At
s=1
we have

H^{\prime}_{con}(1)=-K\sum_{\mathsf{i}}\mathsf{x}_{\mathsf{i}}-\lambda\sum_{\mathsf{p}}\mathcal{Z}_{\mathsf{p}}-\lambda\sum_{\mathsf{p}}\prod_{\mathsf{l}\in\mathsf{p}}Z_{\mathsf{l}}.

(16)

This is nothing but the usual
\mathbb{Z}_{2}
gauge theory in its deconfined phase, stacked with a trivial paramagnet.

Note that the gauge field variables in the condensed phase are precisely the dipole creation operators. This can be argued intuitively: in the condensed phase, single fractons can only move with the help of string operators built from dipoles, and in the gauge theory description these strings become the Wilson lines which render isolated fractons gauge invariant.

III.2
Condensing a subset of fracton dipoles

Identifying the nature of the phases obtained when condensing only a subset of the fracton dipoles can be done similarly. We give the details here in the case
N=2
.

III.2.1
Condensing a single orientation of dipoles

Suppose first that we condense dipoles with only a single orientation of dipole moment, which given our conventions is most conveniently chosen to be
{\mathbf{\hat{z}}}
. This can be done simply by using the Hamiltonian (
9
), but only including
X_{\mathsf{l}}
,
Z_{\mathsf{l}}
dipole fields, and the corresponding hopping terms, for links parallel to
{\mathbf{\hat{z}}}
. We thus consider the Hamiltonian

\displaystyle H_{con;z}

\displaystyle=-K\sum_{\mathsf{i}}\mathsf{x}_{\mathsf{i}}-g\sum_{\mathsf{c}}(A^{x}_{\mathsf{c}}+A^{y}_{\mathsf{c}})

(17)

\displaystyle-\lambda\sum_{\mathsf{p}\perp{\mathbf{\hat{z}}}}Z_{\mathsf{l}_{1}}\mathcal{Z}_{\mathsf{p}}Z_{\mathsf{l}_{2}}\text{,}

where we have left out
A^{z}_{\mathsf{c}}
in the second term on the grounds that it can be written as a product of dipole kinetic energy terms, and where the last term involves a sum over plaquettes with normal vectors perpendicular to
{\mathbf{\hat{z}}}
. Note that the pairs
\mathsf{l}_{1},\mathsf{l}_{2}
are parallel to
{\mathbf{\hat{z}}}
for both orientations of plaquettes with
\mathsf{p}\perp{\mathbf{\hat{z}}}
; see figure
7
.
The Gauss’ law constraint is an appropriate anisotropic version of (
8
), viz.

\mathsf{x}_{\mathsf{i}}\prod_{\mathsf{l}{\,\parallel\,}{\mathbf{\hat{z}}}\ni\mathsf{i}}X_{\mathsf{l}}=\prod_{\mathsf{p}\ni\mathsf{i}}\mathcal{X}_{\mathsf{p}},

(18)

where the product over
\mathsf{l}
on the left-hand side is over the two
z
-axis links
\mathsf{l}
that touch the vertex
\mathsf{i}
.

We now perform a unitary transformation with the operator
\mathcal{U}_{z}=\prod_{\mathsf{p}\perp{\mathbf{\hat{z}}}}U_{\mathsf{p}}
, which is the same as the operator
\mathcal{U}
defined above, except for the omission of plaquettes with unit normal along
{\mathbf{\hat{z}}}
. After this conjugation, we have

\displaystyle H^{\prime}_{con;z}

\displaystyle=\mathcal{U}_{z}H_{con;z}\mathcal{U}_{z}

(19)

\displaystyle=-K\sum_{\mathsf{i}}\mathsf{x}_{\mathsf{i}}-g\sum_{\mathsf{c}}(A^{x}_{\mathsf{c}}+A^{y}_{\mathsf{c}})\prod_{\mathsf{l}{\,\parallel\,}{\mathbf{\hat{z}}}\in\mathsf{c}}Z_{\mathsf{l}}-\lambda\sum_{\mathsf{p}\perp{\mathbf{\hat{z}}}}\mathcal{Z}_{\mathsf{p}}\text{.}

Importantly, the second term is modified by the transformation, which adds a product of
Z_{\mathsf{l}}
over the four
z
-axis links
\mathsf{l}
contained in the cube
\mathsf{c}
. This can clearly be deformed to

\displaystyle H^{\prime\prime}_{con;z}

\displaystyle=-K\sum_{\mathsf{i}}\mathsf{x}_{\mathsf{i}}-g\sum_{\mathsf{c}}[\prod_{\mathsf{p}{\,\parallel\,}{\mathbf{\hat{z}}}\in\mathsf{c}}\mathcal{Z}_{\mathsf{p}}][\prod_{\mathsf{l}{\,\parallel\,}{\mathbf{\hat{z}}}\in\mathsf{c}}Z_{\mathsf{l}}]-\lambda\sum_{\mathsf{p}\perp{\mathbf{\hat{z}}}}\mathcal{Z}_{\mathsf{p}}\text{,}

(20)

where the first product in the second term is over the two plaquettes on the boundary of the cube
c
with normal vectors along
{\mathbf{\hat{z}}}
.
Under conjugation by
\mathcal{U}_{z}
, the Gauss’ law transforms to

\mathsf{x}_{\mathsf{i}}\prod_{\mathsf{l}{\,\parallel\,}{\mathbf{\hat{z}}}\ni\mathsf{i}}X_{\mathsf{l}}=\prod_{\mathsf{p}{\,\parallel\,}{\mathbf{\hat{z}}}\ni\mathsf{i}}\mathcal{X}_{\mathsf{p}}\text{.}

(21)

Here the product on the right-hand side is over the four plaquettes with normals along
{\mathbf{\hat{z}}}
that meet
\mathsf{i}
at a corner.

The Hamiltonian (
20
) together with the above Gauss constraint is nothing but the “anisotropic model with lineons and planons” discussed in Sec. 5.7 of Ref.
Shirley et al. 2019
(mapped to a generalized gauge theory in the usual way), with the two types of lineon excitations identified in Ref.
Shirley et al. 2019
corresponding here to single fractons and
\mathfrak{e}^{z}
lineons.

III.2.2
Condensing two orientations of dipoles

Consider now condensing dipoles with dipole moments oriented along
{\mathbf{\hat{z}}}
and
{\mathbf{\hat{x}}}
. By exchanging dipoles with the condensate, the fractons in the condensed phase will be able to move along both the
{\mathbf{\hat{z}}}
and
{\mathbf{\hat{x}}}
directions, and hence the natural expectation is to identify the condensed phase with a decoupled stack of
\mathbb{Z}_{2}
gauge theories.

This is indeed what happens. We include
X_{\mathsf{l}}
,
Z_{\mathsf{l}}
for
\mathsf{l}
parallel to
{\mathbf{\hat{z}}}
and
{\mathbf{\hat{x}}}
, but not
{\mathbf{\hat{y}}}
. The Gauss’ law is thus

\mathsf{x}_{\mathsf{i}}\prod_{\mathsf{l}{\,\parallel\,}{\mathbf{\hat{z}}},{\mathbf{\hat{x}}}\ni\mathsf{i}}X_{\mathsf{l}}=\prod_{\mathsf{p}\ni\mathsf{i}}\mathcal{X}_{\mathsf{p}},

(22)

where the product on the left-hand side is over the
x
and
z
links touching
\mathsf{i}
.
For the Hamiltonian we take (
9
) and include only kinetic terms for dipoles oriented along the
{\mathbf{\hat{z}}},{\mathbf{\hat{x}}}
directions. We have, referring to figure
7
,

\displaystyle H_{con;zx}

\displaystyle=-K\sum_{\mathsf{i}}\mathsf{x}_{\mathsf{i}}-\lambda\sum_{\mathsf{p}{\,\parallel\,}{\mathbf{\hat{x}}},{\mathbf{\hat{z}}}}Z_{\mathsf{l}_{1}}\mathcal{Z}_{\mathsf{p}}Z_{\mathsf{l}_{2}}

(23)

\displaystyle-\lambda\sum_{\mathsf{p}{\,\parallel\,}{\mathbf{\hat{y}}}}\mathcal{Z}_{\mathsf{p}}(Z_{\mathsf{l}_{1}}Z_{\mathsf{l}_{2}}+Z_{\mathsf{l}_{3}}Z_{\mathsf{l}_{4}}).

We have again set
h=g=0
, noting that the
A^{a}_{\mathsf{c}}
terms can be obtained as products of the dipole kinetic energy terms that are present. Conjugating with
\mathcal{U}
as in the analysis of the case where all dipoles condense, we obtain

\mathcal{U}H_{con;zx}\mathcal{U}=-K\sum_{\mathsf{i}}\mathsf{x}_{\mathsf{i}}-\lambda\sum_{\mathsf{p}}\mathcal{Z}_{\mathsf{p}}-\lambda\sum_{\mathsf{p}{\,\parallel\,}{\mathbf{\hat{y}}}}\mathcal{Z}_{\mathsf{p}}\prod_{\mathsf{l}\in\mathsf{p}}Z_{\mathsf{l}},

(24)

while the Gauss law becomes

\mathsf{x}_{\mathsf{i}}=\prod_{\mathsf{l}{\,\parallel\,}{\mathbf{\hat{z}}},{\mathbf{\hat{x}}}\ni\mathsf{i}}X_{\mathsf{l}}\text{.}

(25)

After eliminating the now-trivial
\mathcal{Z}_{\mathsf{p}}
degrees of freedom, this indeed describes a set of decoupled deconfined
\mathbb{Z}_{2}
gauge theories, stacked along the
{\mathbf{\hat{y}}}
direction.

III.3
Phase transitions

We now turn to studying the critical points that occur between the
\mathbb{Z}_{N}
X-cube phase and the condensed phases described above. We will first focus on what happens when we only condense fracton dipoles with a single orientation of dipole moment, which we take to be along
{\mathbf{\hat{z}}}
.

To analyze the critical points we can ignore the fracton matter spins
\mathsf{x},\mathsf{z}
, as single fractons remain gapped across the transition. In addition, excitations of the generalized gauge field are gapped across the transition, so we may also ignore fluctuations of the generalized gauge field
{\mathcal{Z}}_{\mathsf{p}}
. Indeed, we may set
{\mathcal{Z}}_{\mathsf{p}}=1
and focus on an effective Hamiltonian for the dipole matter alone, provided that we remember to only consider correlation functions of gauge-invariant operators. Since for now we are only interested in condensing
\mathfrak{d}^{z}_{\mathfrak{m}}
dipoles, we only need to retain the corresponding kinetic terms. Thus we can consider the Hamiltonian

H_{stack}=-h\sum_{\mathsf{l}{\,\parallel\,}{\mathbf{\hat{z}}}}X_{\mathsf{l}}-\lambda\sum_{\mathsf{p}\perp{\mathbf{\hat{z}}}}Z_{\mathsf{l}_{1}}^{\dagger}Z_{\mathsf{l}_{2}}+h.c,

(26)

where the
\mathsf{l}_{1},\mathsf{l}_{2}
links are parallel to
{\mathbf{\hat{z}}}
(see figure
7
), and where we are again working with
\mathbb{Z}_{N}
spins. Since only the spins on the links parallel to
{\mathbf{\hat{z}}}
enter into the above Hamiltonian, we see that
H_{stack}
is simply a decoupled stack of 2d
\mathbb{Z}_{N}
clock models, with one clock model at each
z
-coordinate of the lattice.

In this effective matter theory, the
\mathbb{Z}_{N}
planar conservation laws governing mobility of lineons (see Sec.
II
) lead to a subsystem symmetry. In particular, the number of dipoles in each
xy

plane is separately conserved modulo
N
.
6
6

6

Strictly speaking, this conservation only holds for local terms; non-local processes that add a dipole to every plane are allowed, because each dipole carries two opposite-sign
\mathbb{Z}_{N}
charges in neighboring
xy
-planes. A more general statement is the the difference in number of dipoles between any two planes is conserved.
From a formal perspective, this symmetry arises from the fact that any local term in the matter theory can be consistently coupled to the generalized gauge field if and only if it is symmetry-invariant.

The critical point of the Hamiltonian (
26
) is then the same as a decoupled stack of critical 2d
\mathbb{Z}_{N}
clock model layers, with the added restriction
– coming from gauge invariance – of the effective subsystem symmetry. The allowed operators are precisely those that are invariant under independent
\mathbb{Z}_{N}
transformations on each layer.

III.3.1
N=2

What happens at the critical point depends on the value of
N
.
First, consider the case of
N=2
. Here the decoupled critical point is built from Ising
∗
CFTs on each layer, where as usual the
*
denotes the restriction to the
\mathbb{Z}_{2}
-neutral part of the spectrum. The most relevant way to couple different layers together is through their energy operators, via a perturbation to the fixed-point action of the form

\delta S=\sum_{\alpha\neq\beta}g_{\alpha,\beta}\int d^{2}x\,d\tau\,\varepsilon_{\alpha}\varepsilon_{\beta},

(27)

where
\varepsilon_{\alpha}
is the energy operator on layer
\alpha
. Since the scaling dimension of the energy operator in the 2d Ising model is
\Delta_{\varepsilon}\approx 1.41<3/2
,
Poland et al. 2019
these couplings are slightly relevant at the decoupled fixed point. In appendix
B
we argue that there is in fact no stable fixed point to this RG flow, with the result being an instability towards a first-order transition.

III.3.2
N=3

When
N=3
, we have a 3-state Potts model on each layer. The transition in the 3-state Potts model in three space-time dimensions is well-known to be first order, and hence we do not obtain any stable fixed points in this case.

III.3.3
N=4

The case of
N=4
can be understood by noting that the
\mathbb{Z}_{4}
clock model can be mapped to a pair of two decoupled Ising models, written in terms of two
\mathbb{Z}_{2}
spins
Z_{1},Z_{2}
(see e.g.
Ortiz et al. 2012
).
7
7

7

To see this, we write the
\mathbb{Z}_{4}
clock matrix
Z
in terms of two
\mathbb{Z}_{2}
Pauli matrices
Z_{1}\equiv\sigma^{z}\otimes\mathbf{1},Z_{2}\equiv\mathbf{1}\otimes\sigma^{z}
as

Z=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}(e^{i\pi/4}Z_{1}+e^{-i\pi/4}Z_{2}).

(28)

The real matrix
X+X^{\dagger}
also has a simple representation in terms of
X_{1}\equiv\sigma^{x}\otimes\mathbf{1}
and
X_{2}=\mathbf{1}\otimes\sigma^{x}
, with

X+X^{\dagger}=X_{1}+X_{2}.

(29)

It is then easy to check that in terms of the
\mathbb{Z}_{2}
variables, the
\mathbb{Z}_{4}
clock model Hamiltonian splits as
H_{1}+H_{2}
, where
H_{1}
(
H_{2}
) is an Ising chain Hamiltonian for the
Z_{1}
(
Z_{2}
) variables.

The (gauged)
\mathbb{Z}_{4}
symmetry acts in this representation as

\mathbb{Z}_{4}\,:\,Z_{1}\rightarrow Z_{2},\qquad Z_{2}\rightarrow Z_{1}X_{1},

(30)

and as such at the critical point it exchanges the two energy operators
\varepsilon_{1}
and
\varepsilon_{2}
.
The operator
\varepsilon_{1}+\varepsilon_{2}
is therefore gauge-invariant, and since the Ising models are decoupled
\varepsilon_{1}+\varepsilon_{2}
has the same dimension as the energy operator in a single Ising model. Therefore according to the discussion of the
N=2
case above, the decoupled fixed point is again unstable.

III.3.4
N>4

When
N>4
, we describe the critical point on each layer with the action

\displaystyle S

\displaystyle=\int d\tau\,d^{2}x\,\Big(|\partial\psi|^{2}+t|\psi|^{2}+\frac{u}{4}|\psi|^{4}

(31)

\displaystyle+\frac{g\Lambda^{4-N}}{N!}(\psi^{N}+(\psi^{*})^{N})+\cdots\Big),

with
\psi
a complex scalar field.
It is known from Monte Carlo simulations that the
\mathbb{Z}_{N}
anisotropy term is irrelevant as long as
N>4
,
Scholten and Irakliotis 1993
;
Hove and Sudbo 2003
so that the phase transitions on each layer are in the universality class of the 2+1D XY
∗
model. The most relevant gauge-invariant coupling between the layers is the energy-energy coupling. The energy operator in this case is known to have scaling dimension
Poland et al. 2019

\Delta_{\varepsilon}\approx 1.51>3/2,

(32)

and so the decoupled fixed point consisting of a decoupled stack of 2+1D XY
∗
models is (barely) stable.

III.3.5
No particle-hole symmetry

For
N>2
, the discussion above strictly speaking pertains only to models which possess a
\mathbb{Z}_{2}
particle-hole symmetry
C
, which acts on the spins of the
\mathbb{Z}_{N}
clock models (as well as on the original spins of the XC model) by conjugation with the
N\times N
matrix
C
, where

C=\begin{pmatrix}1&&&\\
&&&1\\
&&\scalebox{-1}[1]{$\ddots$}&\\
&1&&\end{pmatrix},\qquad C\mathsf{z}C=\mathsf{z}^{\dagger},\quad C\mathsf{x}C=\mathsf{x}^{\dagger}.

(33)

In terms of the coarse-grained field
\psi
employed above in (
31
), this sends
C:\psi\rightarrow\psi^{*}
.

This symmetry can be broken for
N>2
by letting the parameters
h,\lambda
in (
26
) be complex. We are then prompted to consider an action on each layer of the form

\displaystyle S

\displaystyle=\int d\tau\,d^{2}x\,\Big(\psi^{*}\partial_{\tau}\psi+\frac{1}{2m}|\nabla\psi|^{2}+t|\psi|^{2}+\frac{u}{4}|\psi|^{4}

(34)

\displaystyle+\frac{g\Lambda^{4-N}}{N!}(\psi^{N}+(\psi^{*})^{N})+\cdots\Big),

where the
C
-breaking first term, which leads to non-relativistic
z=2
scaling, is included instead of
|\partial_{\tau}\psi|^{2}
on the grounds of it being more relevant. Since 2+1D is the upper critical dimension for the above model, the RG flow can be accurately computed through a perturbative analysis of the above action (
34
).

The non-relativistic nature of the theory means that the 1-loop computation of the
\beta
functions is exact,
8
8

8

For example, in momentum-shell regularization with a cutoff
\Lambda
, the only diagrams depending on
\ln(\Lambda)
are those built from concatenations of one-loop bubbles, meaning that the full beta functions are determined by the one-loop terms.
giving

\beta_{u}=-u^{2}/2,\qquad\beta_{g}=(4-N)g-ug/2.

(35)

The anisotropy is therefore relevant at
N=3
, marginally irrelevant at
N=4
, and irrelevant for
N>4
. When
N=3
Ginzburg-Landau theory predicts a first-order transition due to the shape of the potential for
\psi
, and so this case can be ignored. For
N\geq 4
however, we obtain a nontrivial critical theory describable by the non-relativistic XY
∗
model.

We therefore need to understand the stability of a decoupled stack of non-relativistic XY
∗
models.

9

9

orthogonal metal phase transitions studied in Ref.
Zou and Senthil 2016
The most relevant gauge-invariant couplings are again those which couple the energy operators on different layers; in the present notation they read

\delta S=\sum_{\alpha,\beta}g_{\alpha,\beta}\int d\tau\,d^{2}x\,|\psi_{\alpha}|^{2}|\psi_{\beta}|^{2}.

(36)

The beta function for
g_{\alpha,\beta}
is similarly exactly computable from the 1-loop term, which gives

\beta_{g_{\alpha,\beta}}=-\frac{1}{2}(g_{\alpha,\beta})^{2}.

(37)

Due to the non-relativistic nature of the theory, none of the
g_{\alpha,\beta}
s mix with each other. As a result, the decoupled fixed point is stable, provided that all of the
g_{\alpha,\beta}
s are positive.
10
10

10

Note that getting a stable fixed point does not require fine-tuning: we just need to that
g_{\alpha,\beta}>0
for all
\alpha,\beta
; we do not require that each of the
g_{\alpha,\beta}
be tuned to any one particular value.

Before moving on, we add a brief aside about a subtlety in the above discussion of stability. In the argument above, we have restricted our attention only to the
g_{\alpha,\beta}
couplings, which are marginal. In principle however, the irrelevant operators we have neglected may drive some of the
g_{\alpha,\beta}
negative during the initial stages of the RG flow. If this occurs generically, the stability of the proposed fixed point would be called into question.

This worry is not a serious issue in the present context, however. First, note that due to the non-relativistic scaling, the only irrelevant operators which can renormalize
g_{\alpha,\beta}
are those containing
\psi^{*}_{\alpha}\psi_{\alpha}\psi^{*}_{\beta}\psi_{\beta}
and no other field operators, the least irrelevant of which is
(\psi_{\alpha}^{*}\nabla_{i}\psi_{\alpha})(\psi_{\beta}^{*}\nabla^{i}\psi_{\beta})
, which has an RG eigenvalue of
-2
. Unless the bare value of this term is significantly larger than that of
g_{\alpha,\beta}
,
g_{\alpha,\beta}
will never be driven negative during the flow.
Therefore if we make the (reasonable) assumption that the bare values of these irrelevant operators are not significantly larger than the bare values of the appropriate
g_{\alpha,\beta}
, the above fixed point is indeed stable.
11
11

11

It is instructive to compare the story here with the Kohn-Luttinger instability that occurs in Fermi liquids.
Shankar 1994
In that scenario, irrelevant operators lead to instabilities by renormalizing certain marginal couplings. This occurs however only due to the fact that the bare values of certain marginal terms are naturally exponentially smaller than the bare values of the irrelevant operators which renormalize them. In the present setting, by contrast, the bare values of the irrelevant terms and of the marginal terms they renormalize both scale similarly with the distance between the
\alpha
and
\beta
layers.

III.3.6
Condensing multiple species of dipoles

So far we have only been concerned with condensing a single species of dipole, but the same analysis can be applied when simultaneously condensing multiple species. The starting point for the critical theory is simply multiple decoupled stacks of critical
\mathbb{Z}_{N}
clock models, with one stack oriented along the dipole moment vector of each of the condensing dipoles. The effective matter theory again has a subsystem symmetry, where (for local terms) the number of
z
-dipoles is conserved modulo
N
in each
xy
-plane, and similarly for
x
- and
y
-dipoles. Dipoles with different orientations of dipole moment are separately conserved, because they carry
\mathbb{Z}_{N}
planar charges in distinct planes. The discussion of stability of the decoupled critical point is not modified, because the couplings between perpendicular planes are always less relevant than the couplings between parallel planes.

IV
Lineon dipole condensation

In this section we discuss what happens when lineon dipoles are condensed.
The analysis is similar in many respects to the case of fracton dipoles treated in the previous section, although the phenomenology is slightly richer due to fact that there are three different types of lineons, as opposed to only a single type of fracton.
As in the previous section, we will proceed by first using a generalized gauge theory to identify the nature of the condensed phases, and then later turn to discussing the nature of the phase transitions.

IV.1
Condensed phases and dual gauge theories

IV.3
Phase transitions

Because we are again condensing excitations that can move in 2d planes, with the number of excitations in each plane separately conserved modulo
N
(for local processes), the critical points to analyze are the same as those that appeared in section
III.3
. The stability analysis of the critical points is identical to the previous fracton dipole case, and so we will not repeat ourselves.

V
Lineon condensation

In this section we will discuss what happens when we condense one or more species of the lineons
\mathfrak{e}^{x,y,z}
. We will see that when we condense a single species of lineon the resulting phase is a stack of deconfined 2d
\mathbb{Z}_{N}
gauge theories, while if more species are condensed the resulting phase is trivial. If
N>4
, we find that the system enters an intermediate gapless phase upon condensation of lineons, described as an array of strongly-coupled Luttinger liquids. The transition from the XC phase into the gapless phase is expected to be of Berezinskii-Kosterlitz-Thouless (BKT) type.

V.1
Condensed phases and dual Hamiltonians

Before getting into details let us discuss our expectations for the condensed phases.
Consider first condensing a single type of lineon, for example
\mathfrak{e}^{z}
.
In the electric sector, the fusion rule
\mathfrak{e}^{x}\mathfrak{e}^{y}\mathfrak{e}^{z}=1
means that the condensed phase contains a single type of electric excitation
\mathfrak{e}^{x}\sim(\mathfrak{e}^{y})^{\dagger}
, which is able to move in both the
x
and
y
directions by fusing with the
\mathfrak{e}^{z}
condensate.
In the magnetic sector, the
\mathfrak{e}^{z}
condensate leads to confinement of fractons. Consider first fracton membrane operators lying in the
xy
-plane. Such operators do not commute with string operators of the condensate
\mathfrak{e}^{z}
lineons, and thus acquire a surface tension. Naively,
xz
- and
yz
-plane fracton membrane operators do not acquire any tension, and indeed they do not feel the
\mathfrak{e}^{z}
condensate. However, in the presence of the condensate, the edges of such membranes running along the
z
-direction become locally detectable, via processes that create
e.g.
a pair of
\mathfrak{e}^{x}
lineons, and move one of them in a small loop encircling the edge before annihilating the lineons to vacuum (the movement around the loop creates no additional excitations due to the presence of the
\mathfrak{e}^{z}
condensate). Nothing prevents adding terms to the Hamiltonian that effect such processes, giving a line tension to the
z
-direction edges of the
xz
- and
yz
-membrane operators.
These effects result in confinement of
\mathfrak{d}^{x,y}_{\mathfrak{m}}
dipoles, while leaving
\mathfrak{d}^{z}_{\mathfrak{m}}
dipoles deconfined.
The excitation content is thus the same as a stack of 2d
\mathbb{Z}_{N}
gauge theories oriented along the
{\mathbf{\hat{z}}}
direction. Indeed, we will see momentarily that this is the correct identification.

When we further condense a second species of lineon, all of the lineons become condensed due to the fusion rule
\mathfrak{e}^{x}\mathfrak{e}^{y}\mathfrak{e}^{z}=1
, and consequently the magnetic sector is entirely confined. We therefore obtain a trivial paramagnet.

Now suppose we condense both

V.2
Phase transitions for
N\leq 4

We now examine the nature of the phase transitions that occur when the lineons condense. We will first consider only the simplest case when a single species of lineon condenses, which as in the previous section we will take to be
\mathfrak{e}^{z}
. We will therefore take
\lambda^{a}=(0,0,\lambda)
, and consider the condensation transition that occurs as a function of
g/\lambda
.

In this limit,
\mathsf{x}^{x},\mathsf{x}^{y}
both commute with the Hamiltonian (
74
), and to study the phase transition we may work in the
\mathsf{x}^{x}=\mathsf{x}^{y}=1
subspace. In this subspace the Hamiltonian (
74
) clearly reduces to an array of 1d
\mathbb{Z}_{N}
clock model wires oriented along the
{\mathbf{\hat{z}}}
direction, all of which are coupled to the (non-fluctuating)
\mathbb{Z}_{N}
gauge field
\mathcal{Z}_{\langle ij\rangle}
. In this limit, the critical point is simply described by a decoupled array of 1d critical clock models, with the critical fluctuations occurring on each wire independently.

Of course the limit where the
\mathbb{Z}_{N}
chains are completely decoupled is very fine-tuned, and we are interested in the fate of the decoupled fixed point under generic perturbations respecting the effective subsystem symmetry. This can be determined by performing an RG analysis – if all of the perturbations which couple different chains are irrelevant, the decoupled fixed point will correctly describe the phase transition in a finite region of parameter space. If some of the perturbations are relevant, they may drive the system into a nontrivial coupled fixed point, or may lead to the transition being driven first order. Using our knowledge of the critical properties of the 1d
\mathbb{Z}_{N}
clock model, the RG analysis can be performed straightforwardly. Two classes of terms need to be considered, those which are a) invariant under separate
\mathbb{Z}_{N}
transformations on each wire or are b) built from the ring exchange terms of (
73
).

V.2.1
N=2

First, consider the case where
N=2
. The decoupled critical point is then described by an array of critical Ising CFTs. Energy-energy interactions are an important class of couplings between pairs of chains. Labeling the chains by
w
and assuming translation symmetry, these couplings give us a perturbation to the decoupled fixed point which can be written as

\delta S_{\varepsilon}=\sum_{w\neq w^{\prime}}C_{w-w^{\prime}}\int dz\,d\tau\,\varepsilon_{w}\varepsilon_{w^{\prime}},

(76)

where
\varepsilon_{w}
is the energy operator on the chain
w
(note that since there is no
\varepsilon^{2}
operator in the 1d Ising CFT, we only sum over
w^{\prime}\neq w
).
Since the critical fluctuations are only in the
z
-
\tau
spacetime plane, the relevance of
\delta S
is obtained by comparing the scaling dimension of
\varepsilon_{w}\varepsilon_{w^{\prime}}
to 2.
Since the dimension of the energy operator in the 1d Ising model is 1, the couplings
C_{w,w^{\prime}}
are all marginal at tree level.

However, it is unnecessary to actually compute the beta functions for the
C_{w-w^{\prime}}
couplings. This is because there is another gauge-invariant coupling which is relevant, which as we will see destabilizes all of the decoupled critical points when
N\leq 4
. This is the ring-exchange term (
73
), which at the critical point is written as (dropping the
\mathcal{Z}
gauge fields as they do not affect calculations of scaling dimensions at the critical point)

\displaystyle\delta S_{r}

\displaystyle=r\sum_{i,a}\int\int dz\,d\tau\,R_{i}^{a}

(77)

\displaystyle\rightarrow r\sum_{w_{1,\dots,4}\in\square}\int dz\,d\tau\,\sigma_{w_{1}}\sigma_{w_{2}}\sigma_{w_{3}}\sigma_{w_{4}},

where
\sigma_{w}
is the spin operator on wire
w
and the notation
w_{1,\dots,4}\in\square
means a sum over configurations of four wires
w_{1,\dots,4}
whose
x,y
coordinates form the corners of a square with unit normal along
{\mathbf{\hat{z}}}
. Since the scaling dimension of
\sigma
is
\Delta_{\sigma}=1/8
, the scaling dimension of the above term is

\Delta_{r}=4\Delta_{\sigma}=\frac{1}{2}<2,

(78)

which is very relevant. Higher-order contributions to the beta function for
r
do not lead to fixed points accessible at small couplings. We therefore conclude that the transition is likely generically first-order, in agreement with suggestions from numerics.
Slagle and
Kim 2017a

The same conclusion holds when mutiple species of lineons condense simultaneously.

V.2.2
N=3

Now consider
N=3
. In this case the putative decoupled critical point corresponds to an array of
\mathbb{Z}_{3}
clock models.
At the critical point of the
\mathbb{Z}_{3}

\sigma

\Delta_{\sigma}=2/15

\Delta_{\varepsilon}=4/5

V.2.3
N=4

Next up is
N=4

While in this case there is a one-parameter critical line of fixed points (the Ashkin-Teller line), the scaling dimension of the spin operator on each chain is always
\Delta_{\sigma}=1/8

V.3.2
General derivative couplings

That the decoupled fixed point is destabilized by the ring-exchange terms does not necessarily mean that there do not exist stable fixed points described by the quadratic action (
80
) — it only means that any such fixed point must owe its existence to interchain derivative couplings which frustrate ring-exchange processes. In this section we show numerically that a relatively simple choice for the derivative couplings can be made which renders the Gaussian fixed point stable with respect to low-body finite-range cosines. Whether or not the chosen couplings ensure stability with respect to
all
possible deformations is a more complicated question, which we leave to future work. However, we also argue that there exist other, more complicated, choices of interchain couplings that result in a truly stable fixed point.

We begin by Fourier transforming in the

With the aforementioned caveats about stability in mind, we thus arrive at a scenario where the XC model is separated from the condensed phase by a massless critical phase extending over a finite region of parameter space. The transition from the XC phase to the intermediate massless region is likely to be of BKT type, since the operator which becomes relevant in the XC phase is the cosine

Finally, we note that although in this section we have only considered the transitions that occur as a single species of lineon is condensed, the generalization to the case where multiple species condense simultaneously is straightforward, due to the fact that couplings between perpendicular wires are always less relevant than those between parallel wires. VI
Discussion

In this paper we have discussed various types of condensation transitions in the
\mathbb{Z}_{N}
XC model, with the phases on both sides of the transitions described in terms of various types of gauge theories.
We have identified continuous condensation phase transitions, and intermediate gapless phases, provided that
N>4
, although our analysis does not rule out the existence of continuous transitions for smaller
N
.
Clearly there is a lot more work along these lines that may be done.

One obvious extension of the present work is to consider similar decoupled critical points in other fracton models, and to understand them using our existing knowledge of 1d and 2d critical phenomena. This strategy is likely to yield many other interesting examples.

One interesting question to ask is then whether or not there exists a continuous phase transition between a fracton phase and a phase without fracton order, where the critical modes at the transition fluctuate in the full four-dimensional spacetime. In fact, one such critical point was proposed in Ref.
Vijay 2017
. This work discussed the transition between the XC model and decoupled stacks of two-dimensional
\mathbb{Z}_{N}
gauge theories. Based on a duality between a model of coupled
\mathbb{Z}_{N}
gauge theory layers and a 3d
\mathbb{Z}_{N}
gauge theory, it was proposed that this transition is first order for
N\leq 4
, but that there may be a continuous transition out of the XC phase for
N>4
. This transition is dual to that between the deconfined phase of 3d
\mathbb{Z}_{N}
gauge theory and a massless Coulomb phase, which was claimed to be continuous in early Monte Carlo studies.
Creutz et al. 1979
However, we believe that this transition is in fact likely weakly first order. Indeed, starting from the Coulomb phase, one may obtain the
\mathbb{Z}_{N}
gauge theory by condensing electric particles with charge
N
. If we assume a second order transition, the critical point can presumably be described by a charge-
N
Higgs field coupled to a
U(1)
gauge field. The Higgs transition with
n\lesssim 365
flavors in 3d is however generically made first order by fluctuations,
Halperin et al. 1974
and hence the transition from the XC model to the intermediate massless phase is also likely to be weakly first order.

One possible route to a continuous transition with 3d character could potentially lie in Higgs transitions into the XC phase, of the type studied in Refs.
Ma et al. 2018
;
Bulmash and Barkeshli 2018
The nonstandard dispersion of the matter fields in these examples may help to stabilize against a fluctuation-induced first-order transition, although the ultimate character of the phase transition may also end up being quasi 2d. Another possible route lies in finding a theory where the decoupled fixed point is unstable, but can be shown to flow to a stable fixed point with nontrivial couplings between different planes / chains. We leave a more detailed treatment of these possibilities to future work.

Appendix B
RG flow for stacked Ising models

In this appendix we argue that
L
stacked layers
of critical 2d Ising models are always unstable
with respect to interlayer interactions that preserve the
\mathbb{Z}_{2}^{L}
subsystem symmetry

\mathbb{Z}_{2}^{L}:\psi_{i}\mapsto f_{i}\psi_{i},\qquad f_{i}\in\{1,-1\},

(101)

where
\psi_{i}
is the Ising field on layer
i
.

We will address this question within the context of the
\varepsilon
expansion.
Our starting point is then the critical action

\displaystyle S

\displaystyle=\int d^{3}x\,\Big(\,\frac{1}{2}\sum_{i=1}^{L}(\partial\psi_{i})^{2}+\frac{\Lambda}{8}\sum_{i,j=1}^{L}\psi_{i}^{2}\psi_{j}^{2}g_{i,j}\Big),

(102)

where the couplings
g_{i,j}
are symmetric and dimensionless,
\Lambda
is a UV cutoff, and where the absence of mass terms denotes tuning to the critical point.

Enumerating all the symmetric RG fixed points for even moderately small

is essentially impossible, since the symmetry group
\mathbb{Z}_{2}^{L}
possesses many quartic invariants.
However, since we are only interested in symmetric fixed points which are
stable
(viz. those which have no relevant quartic terms), the situation becomes much more tractable. This is because we may take advantage of powerful results about the properties of 1-loop beta functions, which follow from interpreting the RG flow as a gradient flow on the space of quartic couplings.

In the following we will make use of two facts. The first is that for any subgroup
H\subset O(L)
(we will be concerned with
H=\mathbb{Z}_{2}^{L}
), there is
at most one

H

Michel 1984
;
Zinn-Justin 2002
The second is that the action of

These two facts mean that if the
g_{i,j}
couplings describe a stable symmetric fixed point, the
g_{i,j}
must be invariant under the action of any
O(L)
transformation which preserves the
\mathbb{Z}_{2}^{L}
symmetry (otherwise the fixed point would not be unique). In particular, consider the action of
\sigma\in S_{L}\subset O(L)
on a given
\mathbb{Z}_{2}^{L}
-invariant coupling

\sigma

for all

. This means that any symmetric stable fixed point will have couplings of the form

g_{i,j}^{*}=(g_{0}-h)\delta_{i,j}+h

(103)

for some constants

. If

Indeed, it is not hard to explicitly compute the one-loop

\displaystyle\beta_{g_{0}}

\displaystyle=g_{0}-\frac{9}{2}g_{0}^{2}-\frac{L-1}{2}h^{2}

(104)

\displaystyle\beta_{h}

\displaystyle=h-3g_{0}h-\frac{L+2}{2}h^{2}.

There are three fixed points to these equations. One is the decoupled fixed point where

and the cubic fixed point

(105)

It is then straightforward to check that both fixed points are unstable (provided that

The above discussion has focused on the RG flows obtained in a perturbative expansion about the free fixed point.
One might imagine a possible way out by first introducing a strong deformation that drives the system to a different fixed point, around which the RG analysis is modified. The simplest possibility is to add the term

\delta S=\sum_{i}g_{pair}\int d^{2}x\,d\tau\,\varepsilon_{2i}\varepsilon_{2i+1}.

(106)

In the absence of further couplings between pairs of layers, this term drives the system to a decoupled stack of 2d XY models.

15

15
