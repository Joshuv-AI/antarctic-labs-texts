On separability in discrete geometry
Károly Bezdek and Zsolt Lángi

Abstract
. A problem of Erdős ( [ 31 ] ) and a theorem of G. Fejes Tóth and L. Fejes Tóth ( [ 27 ] ) initiated the study of non-separable arrangements of convex bodies and the investigation of totally separable packings of convex bodies with both topics analyzing the concept of separability from the point view of discrete geometry. This article surveys the progress made on these and some closely related problems and highlights the relevant questions that have been left open.



2010 Mathematics Subject Classification
52-02, 52A20, 52A38, 52A40, 52C07, 52C10, 52C17, 52C45

1.
Introduction

Separation by hyperplanes is a basic and often used concept in convex geometry (
[
48
]
).
So, perhaps not surprisingly, separability properties of families of convex sets play an important role
in discrete geometry as well. In particular, there are a number of recent results
that have been discovered in response to the questions about non-separable arrangements (resp., totally separable packings) raised by Erdős
[
31
]
(resp., G. Fejes Tóth and L. Fejes Tóth
[
27
]
).
Thus, we felt that it is timely to survey them and orient the readers attention to those questions that have been left open
and seem to have the potential to motivate further research. It is convenient, as well as natural, to group the results into three sections, each representing a topic of independent interest.
Section
2
discusses the state of the art of minimal coverings of non-separable arrangements of convex bodies (resp., spherical caps) in Euclidean spaces (resp., spherical spaces). Section
3
surveys dense totally separable translative packings of convex bodies in Euclidean spaces and densest totally separable packings of congruent spherical caps in the spherical plane. Finally, Section
4
gives an overview on bounds for contact numbers of locally separable sphere packings in Euclidean spaces.

Throughout, we use some standard notations and concepts from convex geometry (
[
48
]
) as well as discrete geometry (
[
20
]
). This includes the following basic ones. The Euclidean norm of a vector (i.e., a point)
\mathbf{p}
in the
d
-dimensional Euclidean
space
{\mathbb{E}}^{d}
is denoted by
|\mathbf{p}|:=\sqrt{\langle\mathbf{p},\mathbf{p}\rangle}
, where
\langle\cdot,\cdot\rangle
stands for the
standard inner product. We denote the
(d-1)
-dimensional unit sphere centered at the origin

\mathbf{o}\in{\mathbb{E}}^{d}
by
{\mathbb{S}}^{d-1}:=\{\mathbf{u}\in{\mathbb{E}}^{d}\;:\;|\mathbf{u}|=1\}
. We shall use
{\rm vol}_{d}(\cdot)
for the
d
-dimensional volume (i.e., Lebesgue measure) of sets in
{\mathbb{E}}^{d}
and use
{\rm area}(\cdot)
instead of
{\rm vol}_{2}(\cdot)
in
{\mathbb{E}}^{2}
. The closed
d
-dimensional Euclidean ball of radius
\rho>0
centered at
\mathbf{p}\in{\mathbb{E}}^{d}
is denoted
by
\mathbf{B}[\mathbf{p},\rho]:=\{\mathbf{q}\in{\mathbb{E}}^{d}\;:\;|\mathbf{p}-\mathbf{q}|\leq\rho\}
with
{\rm vol}_{d}(\mathbf{B}[\mathbf{p},\rho])=\rho^{d}\kappa_{d}
, where
\kappa_{d}:={\rm vol}_{d}\left({\mathbf{B}[\mathbf{o},1]}\right)
.
Moreover,
{\rm bd}(\cdot),{\rm int}(\cdot)
, and
{\rm conv}(\cdot)
refer to the boundary, interior, and the convex hull, respectively, of the corresponding sets. Furthermore, let
R(\mathbf{C})
(resp.,
r(\mathbf{C})
) denote the circumradius (resp., inradius) of the compact set
\mathbf{C}
with non-empty interior in
\mathbb{E}^{d}
, which is the radius of the smallest (resp., a largest)
d
-dimensional ball that contains (resp., is contained in)
\mathbf{C}
.

2.
Minimal coverings of non-separable arrangements

2.1.
Non-separable arrangements in Euclidean spaces

The following theorem was conjectured by Erdős and proved by A. W. Goodman and R. E. Goodman in
[
31
]
.

Theorem 2.1
.

Let the disks
\mathbf{B}[\mathbf{x}_{1},\tau_{1}]\subset{\mathbb{E}}^{2},\dots,\mathbf{B}[\mathbf{x}_{n},\tau_{n}]\subset{\mathbb{E}}^{2}
have the following property: No line of
{\mathbb{E}}^{2}
divides the disks
\mathbf{B}[\mathbf{x}_{1},\tau_{1}],\dots,\mathbf{B}[\mathbf{x}_{n},\tau_{n}]
into two non-empty families without touching or intersecting at least one disk. Then the disks
\mathbf{B}[\mathbf{x}_{1},\tau_{1}],\dots,\mathbf{B}[\mathbf{x}_{n},\tau_{n}]
can be covered by a disk of radius
\tau:=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\tau_{i}
.

The proof of A. W. Goodman and R. E. Goodman
[
31
]
is based on the following statement.

Lemma 2.2
.

Let
\mathcal{F}=\left\{[x_{i}-\tau_{i},x_{i}+\tau_{i}]:\tau_{i}>0,i=1,2,\ldots,n\right\}
be a family of closed intervals in
{\mathbb{R}}
such that
\bigcup\mathcal{F}
is single closed interval in
{\mathbb{R}}
. Let
x:=\left(\sum_{i=1}^{n}\tau_{i}x_{i}\right)/\left(\sum_{i=1}^{n}\tau_{i}\right)
. Then the interval
\left[x-\sum_{i=1}^{n}\tau_{i},x+\sum_{i=1}^{n}\tau_{i}\right]
covers
\bigcup\mathcal{F}
.

With the help of Lemma
2.2
it is shown in
[
31
]
that

\bigcup_{i=1}^{n}\mathbf{B}[\mathbf{x}_{i},\tau_{i}]\subset\mathbf{B}\left[\frac{\sum_{i=1}^{n}\tau_{i}\mathbf{x}_{i}}{\sum_{i=1}^{n}\tau_{i}},\sum_{i=1}^{n}\tau_{i}\right],

finishing the proof of Theorem
2.1
.

For a completely different proof of Theorem
2.1
see Theorem 6.1 of K. Bezdek and Litvak
[
15
]
.

Notice that the value of
\tau
in Theorem
2.1
(as a function of
\tau_{1},\dots,\tau_{n}
) is the smallest possible. Namely, if the centers of the disks
\mathbf{B}_{1},\dots,\mathbf{B}_{n}
lie on a line such that the consecutive disks are tangent to each other, then the radius of the smallest disk containing them is equal to
\sum_{i=1}^{n}\tau_{i}
.

Recall that a
convex domain
of
{\mathbb{E}}^{2}
is a compact convex set with non-empty interior. Hadwiger
[
33
]
extended Theorem
2.1
by introducing the concept of
non-separable system
as follows: A system of convex domains
\mathbf{K}_{i}\subset{\mathbb{E}}^{2}
,
i=1,\dots,n
is called
separable
if there is a line of
{\mathbb{E}}^{2}
, which is disjoint from each
\mathbf{K}_{i}
and which divides
{\mathbb{E}}^{2}
into two open half planes each containing at least one
\mathbf{K}_{i}
. If no such line exists, we call the system
non-separable
. Projections on lines and Cauchy’s perimeter formula combined with Theorem
2.1
yield the following inequalities.

Theorem 2.3
.

Let
\mathbf{K}_{i}
,
i=1,\dots,n
be a non-separable system of convex domains in
{\mathbb{E}}^{2}
. If
\mathbf{K}_{0}:={\rm conv}\left(\cup_{i=1}^{n}\mathbf{K}_{i}\right)
and
{\rm per}\left(\mathbf{K}_{i}\right),{\rm diam}\left(\mathbf{K}_{i}\right)
, and
R\left(\mathbf{K}_{i}\right)
denote perimeter, diameter, and circumradius, respectively, of the convex domain
\mathbf{K}_{i}
,
i=0,1,\dots,n
, then

(1)

{\rm per}\left(\mathbf{K}_{0}\right)\leq\sum_{i=1}^{n}{\rm per}\left(\mathbf{K}_{i}\right),{\rm diam}\left(\mathbf{K}_{0}\right)\leq\sum_{i=1}^{n}{\rm diam}\left(\mathbf{K}_{i}\right),\ {\rm and}\ R\left(\mathbf{K}_{0}\right)\leq\sum_{i=1}^{n}R\left(\mathbf{K}_{i}\right).

It is natural to extend the problem of Erdős to higher dimensions as follows.

Definition 1
.

Let
\mathbf{K}
be a convex body in
{\mathbb{E}}^{d}
and let
\mathcal{K}:=\{\mathbf{x}_{i}+\tau_{i}\mathbf{K}\ |\ \mathbf{x}_{i}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{d},\tau_{i}>0,i=1,2,\ldots,n\}
, where
d\geq 2
and
n\geq 2
.
Assume that
\mathcal{K}
is a
non-separable family
, in short, an
NS-family
, meaning that every hyperplane intersecting
{\rm conv}\left(\bigcup\mathcal{K}\right)
intersects a member of
\mathcal{K}
in
{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
, i.e., there is no hyperplane disjoint from
\bigcup\mathcal{K}
that strictly separates some elements of
\mathcal{K}
from all the other elements of
\mathcal{K}
in
{\mathbb{E}}^{d}
. Then, let
\lambda(\mathcal{K})>0
denote the smallest positive value
\lambda
such that a translate of

\lambda\left(\sum_{i=1}^{n}\tau_{i}\right)\mathbf{K}
covers
\bigcup\mathcal{K}
.

In terms of Definition
1
, Theorem
2.1
states that if
\mathcal{C}
is an arbitrary NS-family of finitely many homothetic ellipses in
{\mathbb{E}}^{2}
, then
\lambda(\mathcal{C})\leq 1
. In
[
10
]
, the authors showed that Theorem
2.1
holds not only for balls, but also for any centrally symmetric convex body. In other words, Theorem
2.1
extends to balls of finite dimensional normed spaces. (Here we recall that a set
S\subset{\mathbb{E}}^{d}
is centrally symmetric with center
\mathbf{q}\in{\mathbb{E}}^{d}
if
S=2\mathbf{q}-S
, and
\mathbf{o}
-symmetric if
S=-S
.)

Theorem 2.4
.

For all
d\geq 2
and
n\geq 2
, and for every
\mathbf{o}
-symmetric convex body
\mathbf{K}_{0}
and every NS-family
\mathcal{K}:=\{\mathbf{x}_{i}+\tau_{i}\mathbf{K}_{0}\ |\ \mathbf{x}_{i}\in{\mathbb{E}}^{d},\tau_{i}>0,i=1,2,\ldots,n\}
the inequality
\lambda(\mathcal{K})\leq 1
holds.

The proof of Theorem
2.4
in
[
10
]
is a short one, which we recall next.

Proof.

Let
\mathbf{x}=\left(\sum_{i=1}^{n}\tau_{i}\mathbf{x}_{i}\right)/\left(\sum_{i=1}^{n}\tau_{i}\right)
, and set
\mathbf{K}^{\prime}=\mathbf{x}+\left(\sum_{i=1}^{n}\tau_{i}\right)\mathbf{K}_{0}
. We prove that
\mathbf{K}^{\prime}
covers
\bigcup\mathcal{K}
. For any line
L
through the origin
\mathbf{o}
, let
\proj_{L}:{\mathbb{R}}^{d}\to L
denote the orthogonal projection onto
L
, and let
h_{\mathcal{K}}:\mathbb{S}^{d-1}\to{\mathbb{R}}
and
h_{\mathbf{K}^{\prime}}:\mathbb{S}^{d-1}\to{\mathbb{R}}
denote the support functions of
{\rm conv}\left(\bigcup\mathcal{K}\right)
and
\mathbf{K}^{\prime}
, respectively (for the definition of support function, see
[
48
]
).
Then
\proj_{L}\left(\bigcup\mathcal{K}\right)
is a single interval, which, by Lemma
2.2
, is covered by
\proj_{L}\left(\mathbf{K}^{\prime}\right)
.
Thus, for any
\mathbf{u}\in\mathbb{S}^{d-1}
, we have that
h_{\mathcal{K}}(\mathbf{u})\leq h_{\mathbf{K}^{\prime}}(\mathbf{u})
, which readily implies that
\bigcup\mathcal{K}\subseteq\mathbf{K}^{\prime}
.
∎

Perhaps, it is not surprising that A. W. Goodman and R. E. Goodman
[
31
]
put forward the following conjecture: For every convex body
\mathbf{K}
in
{\mathbb{E}}^{d}
and every NS-family
\mathcal{K}:=\{\mathbf{x}_{i}+\tau_{i}\mathbf{K}\ |\ \mathbf{x}_{i}\in{\mathbb{E}}^{d},\tau_{i}>0,i=1,2,\ldots,n\}
the inequality
\lambda(\mathcal{K})\leq 1
holds for all
d\geq 2
and
n\geq 2
. However, the authors of
[
10
]
found the following counterexample to it.

Figure 1.
A counterexample in the plane for three triangles.

Place three regular triangles
{\mathcal{T}}=\{\mathbf{T}_{1},\mathbf{T}_{2},\mathbf{T}_{3}\}
of unit side lengths into a regular triangle
\mathbf{T}
of side length
2+2/\sqrt{3}=3.154700\ldots>3
such that

•

each side of
\mathbf{T}
contains a side of
\mathbf{T}_{i}
, for
i=1,2,3
, respectively (cf. Figure
1
),

•

for
i=1,2,3
, the vertices of
\mathbf{T}_{i}
contained in a side of
\mathbf{T}
divide this side into three segments of lengths
2/3+1/\sqrt{3}
,
1
, and
1/3+1/\sqrt{3}
, in counter-clockwise order.

A simple computation yields that the convex hull of any two of the small triangles touches the third triangle, and hence, no two of them can be strictly separated from the third one. Thus,
\lambda({\mathcal{T}})=2/3+2/(3\sqrt{3})=1.0515\ldots>1
and
{\mathcal{T}}
is a counterexample to the above conjecture of A. W. Goodman and R. E. Goodman for
n=3
in
{\mathbb{E}}^{2}
. Note that for any value
n\geq 4
, placing
n-3
sufficiently small triangles inside
\mathbf{T}
yields a counterexample for
n
positive homothetic copies of a triangle in
{\mathbb{E}}^{2}
. As in
[
10
]
, for
d\geq 2
let
\lambda_{\rm simplex}^{(d)}
denote the supremum of
\lambda(\mathcal{K})
, where
\mathcal{K}
runs over the NS-families of finitely many positive homothetic
d
-simplices in
{\mathbb{E}}^{d}
. It was proved in
[
10
]
that
\lambda_{\rm simplex}^{(d)}
is a non-decreasing sequence of
d
. Moreover, Figure
2
shows how to extend the configuration in Figure
1
to
{\mathbb{E}}^{3}
, implying that
\lambda_{\rm simplex}^{(d)}\geq\lambda_{\rm simplex}^{(2)}\geq 2/3+2/(3\sqrt{3})=1.0515\ldots>1
for all
d\geq 3
. The special role of
\lambda_{\rm simplex}^{(d)}
is highlighted by the following statement proved by the authors in
[
10
]
.

Figure 2.
A counterexample in
{\mathbb{R}}^{3}
for three tetrahedra. The dotted lines denote the intersection of a plane touching all three tetrahedra with the smallest tetrahedron containing them.

Lemma 2.5
.

For all
d\geq 2
,
\lambda_{\rm simplex}^{(d)}=\sup_{\mathcal{K}}\lambda(\mathcal{K})
, where
\mathcal{K}
runs over the NS-families of finitely many positive homothetic copies of an arbitrary convex body
\mathbf{K}
in
{\mathbb{E}}^{d}
.

As the proof of Lemma
2.5
in
[
10
]
is a short application of Lutwak’s containment theorem (
[
38
]
) and might be of independent interest, we recall it here.

Proof.

As
\lambda_{\rm simplex}^{(d)}\leq\sup_{\mathcal{K}}\lambda(\mathcal{K})
, it is sufficient to show that for every convex body
\mathbf{K}
in
{\mathbb{E}}^{d}
and every NS-family
\mathcal{K}=\{\mathbf{x}_{i}+\tau_{i}\mathbf{K}\ |\ \mathbf{x}_{i}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{d},\tau_{i}>0,i=1,2,\ldots,n\}
a translate of
\lambda_{\rm simplex}^{(d)}(\sum_{i=1}^{n}\tau_{i})\mathbf{K}
covers
\bigcup\mathcal{K}
. Now, according to Lutwak’s containment theorem (
[
38
]
) if
\mathbf{K}_{1}
and
\mathbf{K}_{2}
are convex bodies in
{\mathbb{E}}^{d}
such that every circumscribed simplex of
\mathbf{K}_{2}
has a translate that covers
\mathbf{K}_{1}
, then
\mathbf{K}_{2}
has a translate that covers
\mathbf{K}_{1}
. (Here a circumscribed simplex of
\mathbf{K}_{2}
means a
d
-simplex of
{\mathbb{E}}^{d}
that contains
\mathbf{K}_{2}
such that each facet of the
d
-simplex meets
\mathbf{K}_{2}
.) Thus, if
\Delta(\mathbf{K})
is a circumscribed simplex of
\mathbf{K}
, then
\lambda_{\rm simplex}^{(d)}(\sum_{i=1}^{n}\tau_{i})\Delta(\mathbf{K})
is a circumscribed simplex of
\lambda_{\rm simplex}^{(d)}(\sum_{i=1}^{n}\tau_{i})\mathbf{K}
and
\mathbf{x}_{i}+\tau_{i}\Delta(\mathbf{K})
is a circumscribed simplex of
\mathbf{x}_{i}+\tau_{i}\mathbf{K}
for all
i=1,2,\ldots,n
. Furthermore,
\{\mathbf{x}_{i}+\tau_{i}\Delta(\mathbf{K})\ |\ \mathbf{x}_{i}\in{\mathbb{E}}^{d},\tau_{i}>0,i=1,2,\ldots,n\}
is an NS-family and therefore
\lambda_{\rm simplex}^{(d)}(\sum_{i=1}^{n}\tau_{i})\Delta(\mathbf{K})
has a translate that covers
\bigcup\{\mathbf{x}_{i}+\tau_{i}\Delta(\mathbf{K})\ |\ \mathbf{x}_{i}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{d},\tau_{i}>0,i=1,2,\ldots,n\}\supseteq\bigcup\mathcal{K}
, which completes the proof.
∎

In connection with the question whether Theorem
2.4
can be extended to non-symmetric convex bodies, it was proved in
[
10
]
that if
\mathcal{K}=\{\mathbf{x}_{i}+\tau_{i}\mathbf{K}:\mathbf{x}_{i}\in{\mathbb{E}}^{d},\tau_{i}>0,i=1,2,\ldots,n\}
is an arbitrary NS-family of positive homothetic copies of the convex body
\mathbf{K}
in
{\mathbb{E}}^{d}
, then
\lambda(\mathcal{K})\leq d
holds for all
n\geq 2
and
d\geq 2
. This upper bound was recently improved by Akopyan, Balitskiy, and Grigorev
[
1
]
as follows.

Theorem 2.6
.

Let
\mathcal{T}=\{\mathbf{x}_{i}+\tau_{i}\mathbf{T}:\mathbf{x}_{i}\in{\mathbb{E}}^{d},\tau_{i}>0,i=1,2,\ldots,n\}
be an arbitrary family of positive homothetic copies of the
d
-dimensional simplex
\mathbf{T}
in
{\mathbb{E}}^{d}
such that any hyperplane parallel to a facet of
\mathbf{T}
and intersecting
{\rm conv}\left(\bigcup\mathcal{T}\right)
intersects a member of
\mathcal{T}
. Then it is possible to cover
\bigcup\mathcal{T}
by a translate of
(d+1)/2\cdot\left(\sum_{i=1}^{n}\tau_{i}\right)\mathbf{T}
. Moreover, the factor
(d+1)/2
cannot be improved.

Clearly, Lemma
2.5
and Theorem
2.6
imply the following corollary.

Corollary 2.7
.

If
\mathcal{K}=\{\mathbf{x}_{i}+\tau_{i}\mathbf{K}:\mathbf{x}_{i}\in{\mathbb{E}}^{d},\tau_{i}>0,i=1,2,\ldots,n\}
is an arbitrary NS-family of positive homothetic copies of the convex body
\mathbf{K}
in
{\mathbb{E}}^{d}
, then
\lambda(\mathcal{K})\leq(d+1)/2
holds for all
n\geq 2
and
d\geq 2
.

Hence, the following problem remains open.

Problem 1
.

Find
\sup_{\mathcal{K}}\lambda(\mathcal{K})
for any given
d\geq 2
, where
\mathcal{K}
runs over the NS-families of finitely many positive homothetic copies of an arbitrary convex body
\mathbf{K}
in
{\mathbb{E}}^{d}
. In
particular, is there an absolute constant
c>0
such that
\sup_{\mathcal{K}}\lambda(\mathcal{K})\leq c
holds for all
d\geq 2
?

2.2.
More on non-separable translative families of convex bodies

In this subsection we raise a number of isoperimetric-type questions for NS-families of translates of convex bodies, and prove some partial results about them.

Problem 2
.

Let
\mathbf{K}
be a convex body in
{\mathbb{E}}^{d}
. Find the maxima of the quantities
\mathrm{vol}_{d}({\rm conv}\left(\bigcup\mathcal{F})\right)
,
\mathrm{svol}_{d-1}\left({\rm conv}(\bigcup\mathcal{F})\right)
,
r\left({\rm conv}(\bigcup\mathcal{F})\right)
and
w\left({\rm conv}(\bigcup\mathcal{F})\right)
, where
\mathcal{F}
runs over all NS-families of
n>1
translates of
\mathbf{K}
, and
\mathrm{svol}_{d-1}(\cdot)
and
w(\cdot)
denote the surface volume and the minimal width, respectively, of the corresponding convex body.

Remark 1
.

It is easy to see that for any NS-family
\mathcal{F}=\{\mathbf{x}_{i}+\lambda_{i}\mathbf{K}:\lambda_{i}>0,i=1,2,\ldots,n\}
of positive homothetic copies of a convex body
\mathbf{K}
in
{\mathbb{E}}^{d}
, we have
\diam\left({\rm conv}(\mathcal{F})\right)\leq\left(\sum_{i=1}^{n}\lambda_{i}\right)\diam(\mathbf{K})
. Indeed, recall that the diameter of a convex body coincides with its longest orthogonal projection to a line, and hence, the inequality follows from the property that the orthogonal projection of an NS-family of convex bodies to any line is a closed segment. Moreover, we have equality, if and only if,
\mathbf{K}
has a diameter
[\mathbf{p},\mathbf{q}]
such that for any
2\leq i\leq n
,
\mathbf{x}_{i-1}+\lambda_{i-1}\mathbf{q}=\mathbf{x}_{i}+\lambda_{i}\mathbf{p}
.

We start the investigation of Problem
2
with the following variant of Kirchberger’s theorem (
[
48
]
).

Theorem 2.8
.

Let
\mathcal{F}=\{\mathbf{K}_{i}:i=1,2,\ldots,n\}
be a family of convex bodies in
{\mathbb{E}}^{d}
. Let the subfamilies
\mathcal{F}_{1}
and
\mathcal{F}_{2}
of
\mathcal{F}
satisfy
\mathcal{F}_{1}\cap\mathcal{F}_{2}=\emptyset
,
\mathcal{F}_{1}\cup\mathcal{F}_{2}=\mathcal{F}
. Then there is a hyperplane (strictly) separating
\bigcup\mathcal{F}_{1}
from
\bigcup\mathcal{F}_{2}
if and only if for any subfamily
\mathcal{G}
of
\mathcal{F}
of cardinality at most
d+2
,
\bigcup(\mathcal{F}_{1}\cap\mathcal{G})
can be (strictly) separated by a hyperplane from
\bigcup(\mathcal{F}_{2}\cap\mathcal{G})
.

Proof.

For any
i=1,2,\ldots,n
with
\mathbf{K}_{i}\in\mathcal{F}_{1}
, let
\hat{\mathbf{K}}_{i}\subset\mathbb{E}^{d+1}
be the set of points
(\mathbf{y},\alpha)
with
\mathbf{y}\in{\mathbb{E}}^{d}
and
\alpha\in{\mathbb{R}}
such that
\mathbf{K}_{i}
is contained in the closed half space
\{\mathbf{x}\in{\mathbb{E}}^{d}:\langle\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y}\rangle\leq\alpha\}
. Then
\hat{\mathbf{K}}_{i}
is convex. Indeed, let
(\mathbf{y}_{1},\alpha_{1}),(\mathbf{y}_{2},\alpha_{2})\in\hat{\mathbf{K}}_{i}
, and
0\leq t\leq 1
. Consider any
\mathbf{x}\in\mathbf{K}
. Then for
s=1,2
,
\langle\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y}_{s}\rangle\leq\alpha_{s}
, and

\langle\mathbf{x},t\mathbf{y}_{1}+(1-t)\mathbf{y}_{2}\rangle=t\langle\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y}_{1}\rangle+(1-t)\langle\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y}_{2}\rangle\leq t\alpha_{1}+(1-t)\alpha_{2},

implying that
t(\mathbf{y}_{1},\alpha_{1})+(1-t)(\mathbf{y}_{2},\alpha_{2})\in\hat{\mathbf{K}}_{i}
.
It follows similarly that for any
i=1,2,\ldots,n
with
\mathbf{K}_{i}\in\mathcal{F}_{2}
, the set
\hat{\mathbf{K}}_{i}\subset\mathbb{E}^{d+1}
of points
(\mathbf{y},\alpha)
with
\mathbf{y}\in{\mathbb{E}}^{d}
and
\alpha\in{\mathbb{R}}
such that
\mathbf{K}_{i}
is contained in the closed half space
\{\mathbf{x}\in{\mathbb{E}}^{d}:\langle\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y}\rangle\geq\alpha\}
, is convex. Thus, the assertion for nonstrict separation follows from Helly’s theorem, and a straightforward modification shows it for strict separation.
∎

Corollary 2.9
.

Let
\mathcal{F}=\{\mathbf{K}_{i}:i=1,2,\ldots,n\}
be a family of convex bodies in
{\mathbb{E}}^{d}
. Then
\mathcal{F}
is non-separable if and only if the following holds: for any partition of
\mathcal{F}
into subfamilies
\mathcal{F}_{1}
and
\mathcal{F}_{2}
, there is a subfamily
\mathcal{G}
of
\mathcal{F}
of cardinality at most
d+2
such that
\bigcup(\mathcal{F}_{1}\cap\mathcal{G})
is not strictly separated from
\bigcup(\mathcal{F}_{2}\cap\mathcal{G})
by a hyperplane.

Our next theorem answers Problem
2
for the case of three Euclidean unit disks.

Theorem 2.10
.

Let
\mathcal{F}=\{\mathbf{B}[\mathbf{x}_{i},1]:i=1,2,3\}
be an NS-family of three unit disks in
\mathbb{E}^{2}
. Let
\mathbf{T}={\rm conv}\{\mathbf{x}_{1},\mathbf{x}_{2},\mathbf{x}_{3}\}
and
\mathbf{C}=\mathbf{T}+\mathbf{B}[\mathbf{o},1]
. Then

(a)

\area(\mathbf{C})\leq\pi+4+3\sqrt{3}
, with equality if and only if
\mathbf{T}
is a regular triangle whose heights are of length
2
.

(b)

\perim(\mathbf{C})\leq 2\pi+8
, with equality if an only if
\mathbf{T}
is a segment of length
4
.

(c)

r(\mathbf{C})\leq 16/3
, with equality if and only if
\mathbf{T}
is a regular triangle whose heights are of length
2
.

(d)

w(\mathbf{C})\leq 4
, with equality if and only if
\mathbf{T}
is a regular triangle whose heights are of length
2
.

Proof.

First, we prove (a). By Steiner’s formula (see
[
48
, Chapter 4]
), we have

\area(\mathbf{C})=\pi+\area(\mathbf{T})+\perim(\mathbf{T}).

First, consider the case that
\mathbf{T}
has a non-acute angle
\gamma
. Then the property that
\mathcal{F}
is non-separable is equivalent to the property that the two sides of
\mathbf{T}
enclosing
\gamma
have lengths at most
2
. Thus, we may assume that these lengths are both equal to
2
. In this case

\area(\mathbf{C})=\pi+4+4\sin\frac{\gamma}{2}+2\sin\gamma.

We denote the expression on the right-hand side by
A_{0}(\gamma)
.
Since the sine function is strictly concave on
(0,\pi)
, it follows that
A_{0}(\gamma)
is strictly concave on
[\pi/2,\pi)
, and it has a unique maximum.
Thus, by differentiating, we obtain that
A_{0}(\gamma)
is maximal if and only if
\gamma=\frac{2\pi}{3}
.

Next, consider the case that
\mathbf{T}
has no obtuse angles. In this case the property that
\mathcal{F}
is non-separable is equivalent to the property that no height of
\mathbf{T}
has length greater than
2
. For
i=1,2,3
, let the side of
\mathbf{T}
opposite of
\mathbf{x}_{i}
be denoted by
S_{i}
, and its length by
a_{i}
. Assume that
a_{1}\leq a_{2}\leq a_{3}
. Let
h_{i}
denote the length of the height of
\mathbf{T}
through
\mathbf{x}_{i}
. Since
a_{i}h_{i}=2\area(\mathbf{T})
for all values of
i
, it follows that
h_{3}\leq h_{2}\leq h_{1}
, and we may assume that
h_{1}=2
.
Note that if we move
\mathbf{x}_{1}
on the line parallel to
[\mathbf{x}_{2},\mathbf{x}_{3}]
and away from the bisector of the segment, then
h_{1}
, as well as
\area(\mathbf{T})
, do not change, but
\perim(\mathbf{T})
increases. Thus, if
\area(\mathbf{C})
is maximal, then
h_{2}=h_{1}=2
, or
\mathbf{T}
has a right angle at
\mathbf{x}_{3}
. By the argument in the previous paragraph, it is sufficient to consider the case that
h_{1}=h_{2}=2
.

Let
\pi/3\leq\gamma\leq\pi/2
denote the angle of
\mathbf{T}
at
\mathbf{x}_{3}
. Then
a_{1}=a_{2}=2/\sin\gamma
, and

\area(\mathbf{C})=\pi+\frac{4}{\sin\gamma}+\frac{4\sin\frac{\gamma}{2}}{\sin\gamma}+\frac{2}{\sin\gamma}=\pi+\frac{6}{\sin\gamma}+\frac{2}{\cos\frac{\gamma}{2}}.

We denote the expression on the right-hand side by
A_{1}(\gamma)
. By differentiation, we obtain that if
A_{1}(\gamma)
is maximal, then
\gamma=\pi/3
or
\gamma=\pi/2
. Thus, (a) follows from the inequalities
A_{1}\left(\pi/3\right)>A_{0}\left(2\pi/3\right)>A_{1}\left(\pi/2\right)
.

Next, we prove (b).
Observe that

\perim(\mathbf{C})=2\pi+\perim(\mathbf{T}).

Following the method of the proof of (a), we have that if
\mathbf{T}
is not acute, then, by the triangle inequality,
\perim(\mathbf{T})\leq 8
. Similarly, if
\mathbf{T}
has no obtuse angles, than the maximality of
\perim(\mathbf{C})
yields that the two shortest sides of
T
have equal lengths, and the length of the corresponding heights is
2
. In this case, denoting by
\gamma
the angle enclosed by the two legs,

\perim(\mathbf{T})=\frac{4}{\sin\gamma}+\frac{4\sin\frac{\gamma}{2}}{\sin\gamma}.

By differentiating, it follows that the right-hand side is maximal if
\gamma=\frac{\pi}{2}
, and thus, if
\mathbf{T}
is not obtuse, then
\perim(\mathbf{T})\leq 4+2\sqrt{2}<8
, implying (b).

To prove (c), we observe that
w(\mathbf{C})=w(\mathbf{T})+2
and that
w(\mathbf{T})
coincides with the length of the shortest height of
\mathbf{T}
.
To prove (d), we note that
r(\mathbf{C})=r(\mathbf{T})+2
, and apply a case analysis like in the proofs of (a) and (b).
∎

To present our next result, we need a definition. We note that the same concept for a family of segments was defined by Siegel in
[
49
]
.

Definition 2
.

Let
\mathcal{F}=\{\mathbf{K}_{i}:i=1,2,\ldots,n\}
be a family of
n
convex bodies in
{\mathbb{E}}^{d}
.
If we can relabel the indices of the elements such that for any
i=2,3,\ldots,n
,
\mathbf{x}_{i}+\mathbf{K}
intersects
{\rm conv}\bigcup_{j=1}^{i-1}(\mathbf{x}_{j}+\mathbf{K})
, we say that
\mathcal{F}
is
a successively non-separable family
, in short, an
SNS-family
.

Remark 2
.

Every SNS-family is non-separable, but there are NS-families that are not successively non-separable.

Proof.

Let
\mathcal{F}=\{\mathbf{K}_{i}:i=1,2,\ldots,n\}
be an SNS-family, where the indices are chosen to satisfy the condition in Definition
2
. For contradiction, suppose that there is a hyperplane
H
strictly separating some of the elements of
\mathcal{F}
from the rest of the elements. Let
i>1
denote the smallest index such that
\mathbf{K}_{i}
is strictly separated from
\mathbf{K}_{1}
. Then
H
strictly separates
\mathbf{K}_{i}
from
\bigcup_{j=1}^{i-1}\mathbf{K}_{j}
, and thus, also from
{\rm conv}\bigcup_{j=1}^{i-1}\mathbf{K}_{j}
, contradicting our assumption that
\mathcal{F}
is an SNS-family. As an example of an NS-family that is not sucessively non-separable, we refer the reader to the configuration in Figure

Theorem 2.11
.

Let
\mathcal{F}
be an SNS-family of
n\geq 1
unit disks in
\mathbb{E}^{2}
. Then

\perim\left({\rm conv}\bigcup\mathcal{F}\right)\leq 2\pi+4n-4,

with equality, if and only if, the centerpoints of the elements of
\mathcal{F}
are all collinear and the distance between any two consecutive centerpoints on this line is
2
.

Note that Theorem
2.11
is trivial for

. Thus, it readily follows from Lemma
2.12
by applying induction on

Lemma 2.12
.

Let
\mathbf{K}
be the convex hull of finitely many unit disks. Let
\mathbf{B}=\mathbf{B}[\mathbf{x},1]
satisfy
\mathbf{B}\cap\mathbf{K}\neq\emptyset
. Then

\perim({\rm conv}(\mathbf{K}\cup\mathbf{B}))\leq\perim(\mathbf{K})+4,

with equality, if and only if,
\mathbf{B}
is tangent to
\mathbf{K}
, and the centers of all unit disks generating
\mathbf{K}
, and that of
\mathbf{B}
, are collinear.

In the proof we denote the arclength of a curve
\Gamma
by
\ell(\Gamma)
.

Proof of Lemma
2.12
.

Without loss of generality, we may assume that
\mathbf{B}
is tangent to
\mathbf{K}
at some point
\mathbf{p}
. Then
{\rm bd}({\rm conv}(\mathbf{K}\cup\mathbf{B}))
consists of an arc in
{\rm bd}(\mathbf{K})
, two segments
[\mathbf{p}_{i},\mathbf{q}_{i}]
, where
i=1,2
, connecting a point
\mathbf{p}_{i}\in{\rm bd}(\mathbf{K})
and a point
\mathbf{q}_{i}\in{\rm bd}(\mathbf{B})
, and a unit circle arc in
{\rm bd}(\mathbf{B})
connecting
\mathbf{q}_{1}
and
\mathbf{q}_{2}
.

Let
\mathbf{q}
denote the point of
\mathbf{B}
antipodal to
\mathbf{p}
, and note that
\mathbf{q}\in{\rm bd}(\mathbf{K}\cup\mathbf{B})
. For
i=1,2
, let
\Gamma_{i}
denote the arc of
{\rm bd}(\mathbf{K})
inside
{\rm conv}(\mathbf{K}\cup\mathbf{B})
from
\mathbf{p}
to
\mathbf{p}_{i}
, and let
\Delta_{i}
denote the union of
[\mathbf{p}_{i},\mathbf{q}_{i}]
and the shorter arc
\Theta_{i}
of
{\rm bd}(\mathbf{B})
from
\mathbf{q}
to
\mathbf{q}_{i}
. We need to show that for

\ell(\Delta_{i})\leq\ell(\Gamma_{i})+2
.

In order to do it, observe that
{\rm bd}(\mathbf{K})
consists of segments and unit circle arcs. Furthermore, the total length of the unit circle arcs in an arc in
{\rm bd}(K)
is equal to the total turning angle of the arc as we move from one endpoint to the other one. Since at
\mathbf{p}_{i}
,
\mathbf{K}
and
{\rm conv}(\mathbf{K}\cup\mathbf{B})
have the same tangent line and the tangent line of
\mathbf{K}
at
\mathbf{p}
and that of

are parallel, the unit circle arcs in

\Theta_{i}
into finitely many arcs, and translating each part by a vector. Thus, we can translate the segments and unit circle arcs in

to obtain the union of the translate

of

to
\mathbf{p}_{i}

is the translate of
\mathbf{q}_{i}
by
\mathbf{p}-\mathbf{q}
.
Thus, by the triangle inequality, we have

\ell(\Delta_{i})=\ell(\Theta_{i})+|\mathbf{q}_{i}-\mathbf{p}_{i}|\leq\ell(\Theta_{i}^{\prime})+|\mathbf{p}_{i}-\mathbf{q}_{i}^{\prime}|+|\mathbf{q}_{i}^{\prime}-\mathbf{q}_{i}|\leq\Gamma_{i}+2.

Equality here implies that
\mathbf{q}_{i},\mathbf{q}_{i}^{\prime},\mathbf{p}_{i}

2.3.
Non-separable arrangements in spherical spaces

In order to state the main results of this section we need to recall some definitions. A closed cap, in short, a
cap
, of spherical radius
\alpha
, for
0\leq\alpha\leq\pi
, is the set of points with spherical distance at most
\alpha
from a given point in
{\mathbb{S}}^{d-1}\subset{\mathbb{E}}^{d}
. A
great sphere
of
{\mathbb{S}}^{d-1}
is an intersection of
{\mathbb{S}}^{d-1}
with a hyperplane of
{\mathbb{E}}^{d}
passing through the origin
\mathbf{o}\in{\mathbb{E}}^{d}
. Following the terminology of Polyanskii
[
45
]
, we say that a great sphere
avoids
a collection of caps in
{\mathbb{S}}^{d-1}
if it does not intersect any cap of the collection. Finally, we say that a finite collection of caps is
non-separable
if it does not have a great sphere that avoids the caps such that both hemispheres bounded by it contain at least one cap. Based on these concepts Polyanskii
[
45
]
proved the following extension of Theorem
2.1
to spherical spaces.

Theorem 2.15
.

Let
\mathcal{F}
be a non-separable family of caps of spherical radii
\alpha_{1},\dots,\alpha_{n}
in
{\mathbb{S}}^{d-1}
,
d\geq 2
. If
\alpha_{1}+\dots+\alpha_{n}<\pi/2
, then
\mathcal{F}
can be covered by a cap of radius
\alpha_{1}+\dots+\alpha_{n}
in
{\mathbb{S}}^{d-1}
.

It is shown in
[
45
]
that Theorem
2.15
is equivalent to Theorem
2.16
below. Polyanskii’s proof of Theorem
2.16
uses ideas from
[
3
]
,
[
4
]
, and
[
5
]
. Recall that a closed zone, in short, a
zone
, of width
2\alpha
with
0\leq\alpha\leq\pi/2
in
{\mathbb{S}}^{d-1}
is the set of points with spherical distance at most
\alpha
from a given great sphere in
{\mathbb{S}}^{d-1}
. As a
spherically convex body
is the intersection of
{\mathbb{S}}^{d-1}
with a
d
-dimensional closed convex cone of
{\mathbb{E}}^{d}
different from
{\mathbb{E}}^{d}
, a zone of width
2\alpha
for
0<\alpha<\pi/2
is not a spherically convex body.

Theorem 2.16
.

Let
\mathbf{Z}_{1},\dots,\mathbf{Z}_{n}\subset{\mathbb{S}}^{d-1}
,
d\geq 2
be zones of width
2\alpha_{1},\dots,2\alpha_{n}
, respectively, such that
\alpha_{1}+\dots+\alpha_{n}<\pi/2
. If
{\mathbb{S}}^{d-1}\setminus\left(\bigcup_{i=1}^{n}\mathbf{Z}_{i}\right)
possesses at most one pair of antipodal open connected components, then
\bigcup_{i=1}^{n}\mathbf{Z}_{i}
can be covered by a zone of width
2\alpha_{1}+\dots+2\alpha_{n}
.

According to
[
45
]
, Didid suggested to investigate the following more general problem, which he phrased as a conjecture. In what follows, the
inradius
of a spherically convex body is the spherical radius of the largest cap contained in it.

Conjecture 2
.

Let
\mathbf{Z}_{1}\subset,\dots,\mathbf{Z}_{n}\subset{\mathbb{S}}^{d-1}
,
d>2
be zones of width
2\beta_{1},\dots,2\beta_{n}
, respectively. If
{\mathbb{S}}^{d-1}\setminus\left(\bigcup_{i=1}^{n}\mathbf{Z}_{i}\right)

consists of
2m
spherically convex open connected components with inradii
\gamma_{1},\dots,\gamma_{2m}
, respectively, then
2\beta_{1}+\dots 2\beta_{n}+\gamma_{1}+\dots+\gamma_{2m}\geq\pi
.

Remark 4
.

The first named author and Schneider
[
17
]
proved that if a cap of spherical radius
\alpha\geq\pi/2
is covered by a finite family of spherically convex bodies in
{\mathbb{S}}^{d-1}
,
d>2
, then the sum of the inradii of the spherically convex bodies in the family is at least
\alpha
. This theorem implies Conjecture
2
for the case when
\beta_{1}=\dots=\beta_{n}=0
, i.e.,
\mathbf{Z}_{1},\dots,\mathbf{Z}_{n}
are great spheres of
{\mathbb{S}}^{d-1}
.

In connection with Remark
4
, we call the reader’s attention to Problem 7.3.5 of
[
21

, which is still open and so, we include it here.

Problem 3
.

Prove or disprove that if a cap of spherical radius
0<\alpha<\pi/2
is covered by a finite family of spherically convex bodies in
{\mathbb{S}}^{d-1}
,
d>2
, then the sum of the inradii of the spherically convex bodies in the family is at least
\alpha
.

.

Jiang and Polyanskii
[
36

proved that if a finite family of zones covers
{\mathbb{S}}^{d-1}

3.
Dense totally separable packings

3.1.
Finding the densest totally separable (finite or infinite) translative packings in the Euclidean plane

The concept of totally separable packings was introduced by G. Fejes Tóth and L. Fejes Tóth
[
27
]
as follows.

Definition 3
.

A packing
\mathcal{F}
of convex domains in
{\mathbb{E}}^{2}
is called a
totally separable packing
, in short, a
TS-packing
, if any two members of
\mathcal{F}
can be separated by a line which is disjoint from the interiors of all members of
\mathcal{F}
.

We shall use the following notion and notation.

Definition 4
.

Let
\mathcal{F}
be a packing of convex bodies in
{\mathbb{E}}^{d}
. The quantity

\limsup_{\rho\to\infty}\frac{{\rm vol}_{d}(\mathbf{B}[\mathbf{o},\rho]\cap\bigcup\mathcal{F}}{{\rm vol}_{d}(\mathbf{B}[\mathbf{o},\rho])}

is called the
upper density
of
\mathcal{F}
. If we replace
\limsup
by
\liminf
in the above definition, we obtain the
lower density
of
\mathcal{F}
. If the upper and the lower density of
\mathcal{F}
are equal, we call this common value the
density
of
\mathcal{F}
.

Definition 5
.

Let
\mathbf{K}
be a convex domain in
{\mathbb{E}}^{2}
. Then let
\square^{*}(\mathbf{K})
(resp.,
\square(\mathbf{K})
) denote a minimal area circumscribed quadrilateral (resp., parallelogram) of
\mathbf{K}
.

G. Fejes Tóth and L. Fejes Tóth
[
27
]
put forward the problem of finding the largest density of TS-packings by congruent copies of a given convex domain in
{\mathbb{E}}^{2}
. They have solved this problem for centrally symmetric convex domains as follows.

Theorem 3.1
.

Let
\mathbf{K}
be a convex domain in
{\mathbb{E}}^{2}
and let
\mathbf{Q}\subset{\mathbb{E}}^{2}
be a convex quadrilateral that contains
n>1
congruent copies of
\mathbf{K}
forming a TS-packing in
\mathbf{Q}
.
Then
{\rm area}(\mathbf{Q})\geq n\cdot{\rm area}(\square^{*}(\mathbf{K}))
.

According to a theorem of Dowker
[
26
]
, if
\mathbf{K}\subset{\mathbb{E}}^{2}
is a centrally symmetric convex domain, then among the least area convex quadrilaterals containing
\mathbf{K}
there is a parallelogram. Clearly, this observation and Theorem
3.1
imply the following corollary.

Corollary 3.2
.

Let
\mathbf{K}
be a centrally symmetric convex domain and let
\mathcal{P}
be an arbitrary TS-packing by congruent copies of
\mathbf{K}
. Then for the (upper) density
\delta(\mathcal{P})
of
\mathcal{P}
(i.e., for the fraction
\delta(\mathcal{P})
of
{\mathbb{E}}^{2}
covered by the members of
\mathcal{P}
) we have that

\delta(\mathcal{P})\leq\frac{{\rm area}(\mathbf{K})}{{\rm area}(\square(\mathbf{K}))}.

Equality is attained for the lattice TS-packing of
\mathbf{K}
with fundamental parallelogram
\square(\mathbf{K})
.

The line of research started in
[
27
]
has been continued by the authors in
[
12
]
. The following close relative of Corollary
3.2
was proved in
[
12
]
.

Theorem 3.3
.

If
\delta_{sep}(\mathbf{K})
denotes the largest (upper) density of TS-packings by translates of the convex domain
\mathbf{K}
in
{\mathbb{E}}^{2}
, then

(2)

\delta_{sep}(\mathbf{K})=\frac{{\rm area}(\mathbf{K})}{{\rm area}(\square(\mathbf{K}))}.

Remark 6
.

It is worth pointing out that by (
2
) of Theorem
3.3
, the densest TS-packing by translates of a convex domain is attained by
a lattice packing.

The following finite TS-packing analogue of Theorem

was also proved in

\mathbf{K}
or

3.2.
Finding the densest totally separable packings of a given number of congruent spherical caps in the spherical plane

The Tammes problem, which is one of the best-known problems of discrete geometry, was originally proposed by the Dutch botanist Tammes in 1930. It is about finding the arrangement of
N
points on the unit sphere
{\mathbb{S}}^{2}
such that it maximizes the minimum distance between any two points. Equivalently, for given
N>1
, one is asking for the largest
r
such that there exists a packing of
N
spherical caps with angular radius
r
in the unit sphere
{\mathbb{S}}^{2}
. For a short overview of this problem see
[
13
]
, and for a comprehensive overview we refer the interested reader to the recent article
[
40
]
.

The following definition is a natural extension to
{\mathbb{S}}^{2}
of the Euclidean analogue notion of Definition
3
.

Definition 7
.

A family of spherical caps of
{\mathbb{S}}^{2}
is called a
totally separable packing
in short, a
TS-packing
if any two spherical caps can be separated by a great circle of
{\mathbb{S}}^{2}
which is disjoint from the interior of each spherical cap in the packing.

The analogue of the Tammes problem for TS-packings, called the
separable Tammes problem
, was raised in
[
13
]
.

Definition 8
.

For given
k>1
find the largest
r>0
such that there exists a TS-packing of
k
spherical caps with angular radius
r
in
{\mathbb{S}}^{2}
. Let us denote this
r
by
r_{\rm STam}(k,{\mathbb{S}}^{2})
.

Remark 11
.

It was noted in
[
13
]
that
r_{\rm STam}(2k^{\prime}-1,{\mathbb{S}}^{2})=r_{\rm STam}(2k^{\prime},{\mathbb{S}}^{2})
holds for any integer
k^{\prime}>1
.

The following TS-packings are special solutions of the separable Tammes problem (
[
13
]
). Consider three mutually orthogonal great circles on
{\mathbb{S}}^{2}
. These divide the sphere into eight regular spherical triangles of side length
\pi/2
. The family of inscribed spherical caps of these triangles is a TS-packing of eight spherical caps of radius
\arcsin(1/\sqrt{3})(\approx 35.26^{\circ})
. We call such a family an
octahedral TS-packing
(Figure
7
). Similarly, the side lines of a regular spherical triangle of side length
\arccos(1/4)\approx 75.52^{\circ}
divide the sphere into two regular spherical triangles of side length
\arccos(1/4)
, and six isosceles spherical triangles of side lengths
\pi-\arccos(1/4),\pi-\arccos(1/4)
and
\arccos(1/4)
.
The inscribed spherical caps of the six isosceles triangles form a TS-packing of six spherical caps of radius
\arctan(3/4)\approx 36.87^{\circ}
, which we call a

(Figure

).

{\mathbb{S}}^{2}
.

On the one hand, it is easy to check that

and

. On the other hand, Problem
8
is solved for

in
[

]

Definition 9
.

Let

be fixed. A TS-packing of

For

The value of

is not known for any

Problem 5

Compute the exact value of

On the other hand, in

]
lower and upper bounds were given for

Theorem 3.9

(i)

We have

for all

(ii)

Notice that the upper bound on

stated in Part (i) is about
\sqrt{\pi/k}\approx 1.772/\sqrt{k}
as

Problem 6

Does the limit

Prove or disprove that if a

Remark 13

A positive answer to Problem

Next, recall the following elegant theorem of Böröczky Jr.

: Consider the convex hull
\mathbf{Q}
of

-separable translative packings using

Definition 15
.

Let
\mathbf{C}
be an
\mathbf{o}
-symmetric convex body of
\mathbb{E}^{d}
. Furthermore, let

denote the norm generated by
\mathbf{C}
.
Now, let

{\mathcal{P}}_{{\rm sep}}:=\{\mathbf{c}_{i}+\mathbf{C}\ |\ i\in I\ {\rm with}\ |\mathbf{c}_{j}-\mathbf{c}_{k}|_{\mathbf{C}}\geq 2\ {\rm for\ all}\ j\neq k\in I\}

of (finitely or infinitely many) non-overlapping translates of

\{\mathbf{c}_{j}+\mathbf{C}\ |\ \mathbf{c}_{j}+\mathbf{C}\subseteq\mathbf{c}_{i}+\rho\mathbf{C}\}

). Finally, let
\delta_{{\rm sep}}(\rho,\mathbf{C})
denote the largest upper density of all
\rho
-separable translative packings of
\mathbf{C}
in
\mathbb{E}^{d}
, i.e., let

\delta_{{\rm sep}}(\rho,\mathbf{C}):=\sup_{{\mathcal{P}}_{\rm sep}}\left(\limsup_{\lambda\to+\infty}\frac{\sum_{\mathbf{c}_{i}+\mathbf{C}\subset\mathbf{W}_{\lambda}^{d}}{\rm vol}_{d}(\mathbf{c}_{i}+\mathbf{C})}{{\rm vol}_{d}(\mathbf{W}_{\lambda}^{d})}\right)\ ,

where

denotes the
d

\mathbf{o}
in
\mathbb{E}^{d}
with edges parallel to the coordinate axes of
\mathbb{E}^{d}
.

Remark 14
.

Let
\delta(\mathbf{C})
(resp.,

\mathbf{o}
-symmetric convex body

Recall that the mean

\frac{r(\mathbf{Q})}{R(\mathbf{Q})}\geq 1-\frac{\omega}{n^{\frac{2}{d(d+3)}}}\hbox{ for }\omega=\lambda(d)\left(\frac{\rho R(\mathbf{C})}{r(\mathbf{C})}\right)^{\frac{2}{d+3}},

4.
On contact numbers for separable translative packings

For an overview on contact graphs and contact numbers of packings we refer the interested reader to the recent survey article
[
9
]
. Here we focus on the latest developments for contact numbers of totally (resp., locally) separable translative packings that are not discussed in
[
9
]
.

Let us recall the definition of contact graphs and contact numbers for packings.

Definition 16
.

The
contact graph

G(\mathcal{P})
of a packing
\mathcal{P}
of convex bodies in
{\mathbb{E}}^{d}
,
d>1
is the simple graph whose vertices correspond to the members of the packing, and whose two vertices are connected by an edge if the two members touch each other. The number of edges of
G(\mathcal{P})
is called the
contact number

c(\mathcal{P})
of
\mathcal{P}
.

The concept of locally separable (sphere) packing was introduced by the first named author in
[
22
]
.

Definition 17
.

We call the packing
\mathcal{P}
of convex bodies in
{\mathbb{E}}^{d}
,
d>1
a
locally separable packing
, in short, an
LS-packing
if each member of
\mathcal{P}
together with the members of
\mathcal{P}
that are tangent to it form a TS-packing.

Clearly, any TS-packing is also an LS-packing, but not necessarily the other way around. Moreover, it is worth noting that any
\rho
-separable packing by translates of a convex body for
\rho=3
is a translative LS-packing and vice versa.

4.1.
On contact numbers for totally separable translative packings

The concept of
separable Hadwiger number
(resp.,
maximum separable contact number
) was introduced in
[
8
]
(resp.,
[
16
]
) as follows.

Definition 18
.

Let
\mathbf{K}
be a convex body of
\mathbb{E}^{d}
,
d>1
. The
separable Hadwiger number

H_{\rm sep}(\mathbf{K})
of
\mathbf{K}
is the maximum number of translates of
\mathbf{K}
that all are tangent to
\mathbf{K}
, and together with
\mathbf{K}
, form a TS-packing in
\mathbb{E}^{d}
. Moreover, let
c_{\rm sep}(\mathbf{K},n)
denote the largest contact number of a TS-packing of
n>1
traslates of
\mathbf{K}
in
\mathbb{E}^{d}
.

Clearly,
H_{\rm sep}(\mathbf{K})
is the maximum degree in the contact graph of any totally separable packing of
\mathbf{K}
. It is proved in
[
8
]
that
H_{\rm sep}(\mathbf{K})=4
for any smooth convex domain
\mathbf{K}
in
\mathbb{E}^{2}
. Moreover, the first named author, Khan, and Oliwa
[
8
]
showed that
c_{\rm sep}(\mathbf{K},n)=\lfloor 2n-2\sqrt{n}\rfloor
for any
n>1
and any smooth strictly convex domain
\mathbf{K}
in
{\mathbb{E}}^{2}
. In an elegant paper, Naszódi and Swanepoel
[
41
]
have completed the characterization of the values of
H_{\rm sep}(\mathbf{K})
and
c_{\rm sep}(\mathbf{K},n)
for every convex domain
\mathbf{K}
in
\mathbb{E}^{2}
. In order to state their result we need to recall the following notion. The convex domain
\mathbf{K}\subset{\mathbb{E}}^{2}
is called a
quasi hexagon
if there exists a parallelogram
\mathbf{P}
containig
\mathbf{K}
such that some two opposite vertices of
\mathbf{P}
lie on the boundary of
\mathbf{K}
, and such that each edge of
\mathbf{P}
has a translate contained in
\mathbf{K}
. Clearly, triangles, parallelograms, affine regular hexagons, and quarter circular disks are quasi hexagons.

Theorem 4.1
.

Let
n>1
and
\mathbf{K}
be a convex domain in
{\mathbb{E}}^{2}
.

(i)

If
\mathbf{K}
is a parallelogram, then
H_{\rm sep}(\mathbf{K})=8
and
c_{\rm sep}(\mathbf{K},n)=\lfloor 4n-\sqrt{28n-12}\rfloor
.

(ii)

If
\mathbf{K}
is a quasi hexagon but not a parallelogram, then
H_{\rm sep}(\mathbf{K})=6
and
c_{\rm sep}(\mathbf{K},n)=\lfloor 3n-\sqrt{12n-3}\rfloor
.

(iii)

If
\mathbf{K}
is not a quasi hexagon, then
H_{\rm sep}(\mathbf{K})=4
and
c_{\rm sep}(\mathbf{K},n)=\lfloor 2n-2\sqrt{n}\rfloor
.

The analogue problem in high dimensions seems to be wide open. We close this section with a higher dimensional partial result proved by the first named author and Naszódi
[
16
]
. For further partial results we refer
the interested reader to
[
41
]
.

Theorem 4.2
.

Let
n>1
and
\mathbf{K}
be a smooth convex body in
{\mathbb{E}}^{d}
.

(i)

For
d\in\{2,3,4\}
, we have
H_{\rm sep}(\mathbf{K})=2d
and
c_{\rm sep}(\mathbf{K},n)\leq dn-n^{(d-1)/d}f(\mathbf{K})
, where
f(\mathbf{K})
depends on
\mathbf{K}
only.

(ii)

For all
d\geq 5
, we have
H_{\rm sep}(\mathbf{K})\leq 2^{d+1}-3
and
c_{\rm sep}(\mathbf{K},n)\leq(1/2)nH_{\rm sep}(\mathbf{K})\leq(2^{d+1}-3)n/2
.

4.3.
Upper bounding the contact numbers of locally separable unit sphere packings in dimensions larger than
\mathbf{2}

In higher dimensions we know less than in the plane. At present the best upper bound for contact numbers of LS-packings of congruent balls in
{\mathbb{E}}^{d}
,
d\geq 3
is the one published by the first named author in
[
22
]
. We shall need the following notation.
Let
\mathcal{P}:=\{\mathbf{B}^{d}[\mathbf{c}_{i},1]|i\in I\}
be an arbitrary (finite or infinite) packing of unit balls in
{\mathbb{E}}^{d}
,
d\geq 3
and let
\mathbf{V}_{i}:=\{\mathbf{x}\in{\mathbb{E}}^{d}|\ |\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{c}_{i}|\leq|\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{c}_{j}|\ {\rm for\ all}\ j\neq i,j\in I\}
denote the Voronoi cell assigned to
\mathbf{B}^{d}[\mathbf{c}_{i},1]
for
i\in I
. Recall (
[
47
]
) that the Voronoi cells
\{\mathbf{V}_{i}|i\in I\}
form a face-to-face tiling of
{\mathbb{E}}^{d}
. Then let the largest density of the unit ball
\mathbf{B}^{d}[\mathbf{c}_{i},1]
in its truncated Voronoi cell
\mathbf{V}_{i}\cap\mathbf{B}^{d}[\mathbf{c}_{i},\sqrt{d}]
be denoted by
\hat{\delta}_{d}
, i.e., let

\hat{\delta}_{d}:=\sup_{\mathcal{P}}\left(\sup_{i\in I}\frac{\omega_{d}}{{\rm vol}_{d}\left(\mathbf{V}_{i}\cap\mathbf{B}^{d}[\mathbf{c}_{i},\sqrt{d}]\right)}\right),

where
\mathcal{P}
runs through all possible unit ball packings of
{\mathbb{E}}^{d}
. We are now ready to state the upper bound from
[
22
]
.

Theorem 4.5
.

Let
\mathcal{P}
be an arbitrary LS-packing of
n>1
unit balls in
{\mathbb{E}}^{d}
,
d\geq 3
. Then

(14)

c(\mathcal{P})\leq\left\lfloor dn-\left(d^{-\frac{d-3}{2}}\hat{\delta}_{d}^{-\frac{d-1}{d}}\right)n^{\frac{d-1}{d}}\right\rfloor.

Remark 17
.

Recall the following classical result of Rogers
[
46
]
(which was rediscovered by Baranovskii
[
6
]
and extended to

[
24
]

\mathcal{P}:=\{\mathbf{B}^{d}[\mathbf{c}_{i},1]|i\in I\}
be an arbitrary packing of unit balls in
{\mathbb{E}}^{d}
,
d>1
with
\mathbf{V}_{i}
standing for the Voronoi cell assigned to
\mathbf{B}^{d}[\mathbf{c}_{i},1]
for
i\in I
. Furthermore, take a regular
d
-dimensional simplex of edge length
2
in
{\mathbb{E}}^{d}
and then draw a
d
-dimensional unit ball around each vertex of the simplex. Finally, let
\sigma_{d}
denote the ratio of the volume of the portion of the simplex covered by balls to the volume of the simplex. Then

\frac{\omega_{d}}{{\rm vol}_{d}\left(\mathbf{V}_{i}\cap\mathbf{B}^{d}[\mathbf{c}_{i},\sqrt{\frac{2d}{d+1}}]\right)}\leq\sigma_{d}

holds for all
i\in I
and therefore
\hat{\delta}_{d}\leq\sigma_{d}
for all
d\geq 3
. The latter inequality and (
14
) yield that if
\mathcal{P}
is an arbitrary LS-packing of
n>1
unit balls in
{\mathbb{E}}^{d}
,
d\geq 3
, then

c(\mathcal{P})\leq\left\lfloor dn-\left(d^{-\frac{d-3}{2}}\hat{\delta}_{d}^{-\frac{d-1}{d}}\right)n^{\frac{d-1}{d}}\right\rfloor\leq\left\lfloor dn-\left(d^{-\frac{d-3}{2}}{\sigma}_{d}^{-\frac{d-1}{d}}\right)n^{\frac{d-1}{d}}\right\rfloor,

where
\sigma_{d}\sim\frac{d}{e}2^{-d/2}
(
[
46
]
).

.

We note that the density upper bound

of Rogers has been improved by the first named author

\frac{\omega_{d}}{{\rm vol}_{d}\left(\mathbf{V}_{i}\cap\mathbf{B}^{d}[\mathbf{c}_{i},\sqrt{\frac{2d}{d+1}}]\right)}\leq\hat{\sigma}_{d}

holds for all

and
d\geq 8
and therefore
\hat{\delta}_{d}\leq\hat{\sigma}_{d}
for all

, where

\mathcal{P}
is an arbitrary LS-packing of

{\mathbb{E}}^{d}
,

c(\mathcal{P})\leq\left\lfloor dn-\left(d^{-\frac{d-3}{2}}\hat{\delta}_{d}^{-\frac{d-1}{d}}\right)n^{\frac{d-1}{d}}\right\rfloor\leq\left\lfloor dn-\left(d^{-\frac{d-3}{2}}{\hat{\sigma}}_{d}^{-\frac{d-1}{d}}\right)n^{\frac{d-1}{d}}\right\rfloor.
