Brain memory working. Optimal control behavior for improved Hopfield-like models
Franco Cardin, Alberto Lovison, Amos Maritan, Aram Megighian

Abstract
Recent works have highlighted the need for a new dynamical paradigm in the modeling of brain function and evolution. Specifically, these models should incorporate non-constant and asymmetric synaptic weights T i ​ j T_{ij} in the neuron-neuron interaction matrix, moving beyond the classical Hopfield framework. Krotov and Hopfield proposed a non-constant yet symmetric model, resulting in a vector field that describes gradient-type dynamics, which includes a Lyapunov-like energy function. Firstly, we will outline the general conditions for generating a Hopfield-like vector field of gradient type, recovering the Krotov-Hopfield condition as a particular case. Secondly, we address the issue of symmetry, which we abandon for two key physiological reasons: (1) actual neural connections have a distinctly directional character (axons and dendrites), and (2) the gradient structure derived from symmetry forces the dynamics towards stationary points, leading to the recognition of every pattern. We propose a novel model that incorporates a set of limited but variable controls | ξ i ​ j | ⩽ K |\xi_{ij}|\leqslant K , which are used to adjust an initially constant interaction matrix, T i ​ j = A i ​ j + ξ i ​ j T_{ij}=A_{ij}+\xi_{ij} . Additionally, we introduce a reasonable controlled variational functional for optimization. This allows us to simulate three potential outcomes when a pattern is submitted to the learning system: (1) if the dynamics converges to an existing stationary point without activating controls, the system has recognized an existing pattern; (2) if a new stationary point is reached through control activation, the system has learned a new pattern; and (3) if the dynamics wanders without reaching any stationary point, the system is unable to recognize or learn the submitted pattern. An additional feature (4) models the processes of forgetting and restoring memory.

Proposal One: Gradient Vector Field

We have shown that the proposal (

Proposition 1

Given the symmetry of

Proposal Two: An Alternative Perspective through Optimal Control

Within the Hopfield framework, we address the challenges posed by a non-constant and non-symmetric synaptic matrix
T_{ij}
using a different approach.
We propose a constitutive structure for the synaptic matrix defined as:

T_{ij}(\xi)=A_{ij}+\xi_{ij}

(31)

where
A_{ij}
is a constant (not necessarily symmetric) “ancestral” synaptic matrix and
\xi_{ij}
are bounded adjustments satisfying:

|\xi_{ij}|\leqslant K\ \ (\mathrm{when}\ A_{ij}\neq 0).

(32)

We hypothesize a
\xi
-controlled Hopfield dynamics as follows:

\dot{u}_{i}(t)=X_{i}(u(t),\xi(t))=\sum_{j=1}^{N}\big(A_{ij}+\xi_{ij}(t)\big)g(u_{j}(t))-u_{i}(t),\qquad u_{i}(0)=u^{(0)}_{i},

(33)

where the bounded controls
\xi_{ij}(t)
are implemented by the brain based on the input pattern
u^{(0)}
and on a “principle of economy” (discussed in (
36
) below) to achieve pre-existing states (recognition) or to create new states (recording) with minimal control expenditure. The model may struggle to reach existing equilibria or create new ones under the structural constraints defined by (
32
), leading to a situation termed

. Additionally, it allows for the potential to

existing memories (see Remark
4

(iv)
).

It is well known that the neuron-neuron connections in the brain are not a fully connected network. We therefore adopt a more realistical model consisting in a
sparse network where the set of active connections

is significantly smaller than the total possible connections (i.e., the network is sparse
\#C\ll N\times N
). Recent studies
[

suggest modulating
\xi_{ij}
between synaptic connections in a set

. This approach naturally accounts for

A_{jk}=0
but possibly
\xi_{jk}\neq 0
), as discussed in the context of brain plasticity
[
15
]

Given the infrequent activation of silent synapses, we propose a stricter bound on the strength of newly introduced connections:

\mathrm{for}\ (i,j)\in M\setminus C:|\xi_{ij}|\leqslant k\ll K.

(34)

Estimates suggest that
N\approx 10^{11}
and
10^{13}\,\lesssim\,M\,\lesssim\,10^{15}\ll N\times N\cong 10^{22}
.

We unify the constraints (

) and (

|\xi|\leqslant(K,k)\,.

(35)

The variables
\xi_{ij}
are viewed as controls that perturb and modify the ancestral matrix

towards equilibrium (subject to the limits imposed by (

(Infinite Horizon Optimal Control Problem (
[

For a chosen

The integrand function

Remark 3
.

The variational principle translates the brain’s mechanism of searching for or constructing equilibria with minimal deviations from the original synaptic conductivities. We extend the time to the entire interval

because, typically, achieving equilibrium may require infinite time. However, practically, we can approximate the equilibrium very quickly. For instance, consider the equation

By manipulating this equation, we obtain:

v(u(t,x,\xi^{*}))e^{-\lambda t}\Big|_{t=0}-v(u(t,x,\xi^{*}))e^{-\lambda t}\Big|_{t>0}=\int_{0}^{t}\ell(u(s,x,\xi^{*}),\xi^{*}(s))e^{-\lambda s}ds\,,

Thus, we can express the integral as:

\int_{0}^{t}\left(\frac{d}{dt}\left(v(u(s,x,\xi))e^{-\lambda s}\right)+\ell(u(s,x,\xi),\xi(s))e^{-\lambda s}\right)\,ds=0\,

for almost every
t\geqslant 0
. This leads to the differential equation:

\frac{d}{dt}\left(v(u(t,x,\xi))e^{-\lambda t}\right)=-\ell(u(t,x,\xi),\xi(t))e^{-\lambda t}.

(43)

The presence of a global Lyapunov function appears inconsistent with the actual behavior of the mind. Therefore, relation (
43
) indicates a weak Lyapunov phenomenon, where the
e^{-\lambda t}
-discounted value function
v
has a time-rate that decreases in line with the negative
e^{-\lambda t}
-discounted Lagrangian
-\ell
. Rewriting gives:

\frac{d}{dt}v(u(t,x,\xi^{*}))=\lambda v(u(t,x,\xi^{*}))-\ell(u(t,x,\xi^{*}),\xi^{*}(t))\,.

(44)

Moreover, under the assumption that as
t\to+\infty
we approach an equilibrium
x^{*}
, (with
X(x^{*},0)
and
\xi(t)
not necessarily vanishing), we find:

\lim_{t\to+\infty}u(t,x,\xi^{*})=x^{*}\,,\ \lim_{t\to+\infty}\xi^{*}(t)=0\,,\ X(x^{*},0)=0\,,

This leads to the expected asymptotic behavior of the time-rate of
v
:

\lim_{t\to+\infty}\frac{d}{dt}v(u(t,x,\xi^{*}))=\lim_{t\to+\infty}\left(\lambda v(u(t,x,\xi^{*}))-\ell(u(t,x,\xi^{*})\,,\xi^{*}(t))\right)=0\,,

(45)

since
\ell(x^{*},0)=0
and
v(x^{*})=0
.

Finally, we can interpret the earlier relation using the so-called Witten deformation derivative
[
1
,
16
]
, as discussed in the literature. We define:

D_{t}^{(\lambda)}f:=\Big(e^{\lambda t}\frac{d}{dt}e^{-\lambda t}\Big)f,

which allows us to rewrite (
43
) as:

D_{t}^{(\lambda)}v=-\ell\leqslant 0\,.

Pareto Optimization of a Multiobjective Cost Functional

In the cost functional to be minimized (
36
):

\inf_{|\xi(\cdot)|\leqslant(K,k)}J(x,\xi)=\inf_{|\xi(\cdot)|\leqslant(K,k)}\int_{0}^{+\infty}\underbrace{\left(|X\big(u(t,x,\xi),\xi(t)\big)|^{2}+|\xi(t)|^{2}\right)}_{\ell(t)}e^{-\lambda t}\,dt,

(46)

we can identify two competing objectives:

1.

Minimizing the norm of the vector field
X
to quickly approach equilibria.

2.

Minimizing the activation of the control field
|\xi|
during the search for equilibria.

The trade-off between these objectives can be controlled by introducing a parameter
\mu\in[0,1]
and reformulating the cost functional as follows:

\displaystyle\inf_{|\xi(\cdot)|\leqslant(K,k)}J_{\mu}(x,\xi)=\inf_{|\xi(\cdot)|\leqslant(K,k)}\int_{0}^{+\infty}\left[\ell_{\mu}(t)\right]e^{-\lambda t}dt,\

(47)

\displaystyle\mathrm{where,}\quad\hfill\ \ell_{\mu}(t):=(1-\mu)|X\big(u(t,x,\xi),\xi(t)\big)|^{2}+\mu|\xi(t)|^{2}.

(48)

This suggests that the brain’s wide range of possible reactions and outcomes in different situations, which can vary moment to moment, may result from an optimization process involving multiple objectives. The diversity in outcomes could thus reflect a set of Pareto optima.
When
\mu
is close to
1
, the cost penalizes controls
\xi
more, favoring approaches to existing equilibria (i.e., recognizing existing patterns). Conversely, small values of
\mu
allow for larger controls
\xi
, promoting the exploration of new equilibria that do not exist in the original configuration of
X
.
Thus, by tuning
\mu
, the functional cost captures both ”conservative” and ”innovative” strategies, which may represent predefined brain settings adopted in different contexts or moments to either enhance solution diversity or facilitate pattern recognition.
A coupling between optimal control theory and Pareto multiobjective optimization has been explored in
[
12
]
.
