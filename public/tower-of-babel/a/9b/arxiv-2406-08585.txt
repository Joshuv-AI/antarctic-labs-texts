Abstract
. We consider the Monge–Kantorovich problem between two random measures. More precisely, given probability measures ℙ 1 , ℙ 2 ∈ 𝒫 ⁡ ( 𝒫 ⁡ ( M ) ) \mathbb{P}_{1},\mathbb{P}_{2}\in\mathcal{P}(\mathcal{P}(M)) on the space 𝒫 ⁡ ( M ) \mathcal{P}(M) of probability measures on a smooth compact manifold, we study the optimal transport problem between ℙ 1 \mathbb{P}_{1} and ℙ 2 \mathbb{P}_{2} where the cost function is given by the squared Wasserstein distance W 2 2 ​ ( μ , ν ) W_{2}^{2}(\mu,\nu) between μ , ν ∈ 𝒫 ⁡ ( M ) \mu,\nu\in\mathcal{P}(M) . Under appropriate assumptions on ℙ 1 \mathbb{P}_{1} , we prove that there exists a unique optimal plan and that it takes the form of an optimal map. An extension of this result to cost functions of the form h ​ ( W 2 ​ ( μ , ν ) ) h(W_{2}(\mu,\nu)) , for strictly convex and strictly increasing functions h h , is also established. The proofs rely heavily on a recent result of Dello Schiavo [ 20 ] , which establishes a version of Rademacher’s theorem on Wasserstein spaces.

1.
Introduction

This paper focuses on optimal transport when the ambient space itself is the
infinite-dimensional space of probability measures on a Riemannian manifold, endowed with the Wasserstein
metric.

Assume that
X
and
Y
are metric spaces. Given probability measures
\mu\in\mathcal{P}(X)
and
\nu\in\mathcal{P}(Y)
, and a cost function
c:X\times Y\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
, a fairly general version of Monge’s original
formulation of the optimal transport problem is as follows
[
18
]
:

(1)

\inf_{\stackrel{{\scriptstyle T:X\rightarrow Y,}}{{T_{\#}\mu=\nu}}}\,\int_{X}c(x,T(x))\,d\mu,

where the infimum is over measurable maps
T:X\rightarrow Y
pushing
\mu
forward to
\nu,

T_{\#}\mu=\nu
; that is,
\mu(T^{-1}(A))=\nu(A)
for all Borel
A\subseteq Y
.

Non-linearity and a lack of compactness make this problem challenging on its own. Kantorovich’s
relaxed version
[
14
]
sidesteps these issues by seeking transport
plans
rather
than maps. Explicitly, his formulation of the problem is:

(2)

\inf_{\gamma\in\Pi(\mu,\nu)}\int_{X\times Y}c(x,y)\,d\gamma,

where
\Pi(\mu,\nu)
is the set of all measures on
X\times Y
such that
(\pi_{X})_{\#}\gamma=\mu
,
and
(\pi_{Y})_{\#}\gamma=\nu
, where
\pi_{X}
and
\pi_{Y}
are the canonical projections. Kantorovich’s
problem amounts to an infinite-dimensional linear program, and, in contrast to (
1
), it is straightforward to show that a solution
\gamma
, known as an
optimal plan
, exists
under reasonable assumptions on
c
,
\mu
and
\nu
.

Problems (
1
) and (
2
) have generated a lot of research
over the past several decades, and have many applications, reviewed in the monographs of Villani

[
22
,
23
]
and Santambrogio
[
19
]
. In many of these
applications, the cost function
c(x,y)=d^{2}(x,y)
is the metric distance squared on a common metric
space
X=Y
, and we focus on this case in what follows. We highlight here two important themes, which
are pertinent to this paper. First, the problem induces an extended distance
W_{2}
on the space

\mathcal{P}(X)
of probability measures on
X
. This distance is known as the Wasserstein distance, and

(\mathcal{P}(X),W_{2})
as the Wasserstein space. Second, for many choices of
X
, under additional
assumptions on
\mu
, it is possible to prove that the solution to (
2
) is
unique, and concentrates on the graph of a function
T:X\rightarrow X
pushing
\mu
forward to
\nu
.
This
T
in turns solves the Monge problem (
1
), and is known as an
optimal
map
. This result was first established on convex Euclidean domains by Brenier

[
5
]
, and has since been extended by many authors to a wide variety of other geometric
situations, notably including Riemannian manifolds by McCann
[
17
]
, as well as the
Heisenberg group
[
2
]
, general sub-Riemannain manifolds
[
9
]
,
Alexandrov spaces
[
3
]
, and several infinite-dimensional spaces which we will touch
on in more detail shortly.

Our purpose here is to establish a similar result when the underlying metric space
X
is itself the
Wasserstein space over a compact Riemannian manifold

1

M
,
(X,d)=(\mathcal{P}(M),W_{2})
. The optimal
transport problem on Wasserstein spaces is natural, as it can be thought of as quantifying the
distance between two random (probability) measures. In addition, we note that a substantial amount of
recent research on the applied side of optimal transport has focused on situations where the marginals

\mu
and
\nu
in optimal transport problems on finite dimensional spaces

and

, or other measures
of interest, are not known with certainty; see for instance

. This uncertainty or ambiguity is expressed in different ways depending on
the precise context, but it seems reasonable to conjecture that problems where marginal uncertainty is
reflected by a

of possible marginals on Wasserstein space (instead of precisely known
prescribed marginals) may be relevant in some of these applications. This leads naturally to our problem.

Let us say a few words about the proof of our main result. For general choices of
X
, it is well
understood that one can only establish the existence of optimal maps under certain regularity assumptions
on
\mu
; namely, one needs to exclude the possibility of
\mu

appropriate sense. Although more refined notions exist (see
[
12

for a sharp

), a straightforward sufficient hypothesis (a version of which
we will adopt here) is that
\mu
is absolutely continuous with respect to an appropriate reference
measure. From a technical perspective, this hypothesis arises in the proof of the existence of optimal
maps by ensuring that we can differentiate Kantorovich potentials (defined in the next section)
\mu

almost everywhere. Since these potentials are well known to be locally Lipschitz, Rademacher’s theorem
makes this work when the reference measure is, for example, the Lebesgue measure on

\mathbb{R}^{n}
, or Riemannian volume on a Riemannian manifold. It is also worth mentioning the case of
the Wiener space, which, like our setting here, is infinite-dimensional, where Monge solutions
were established in
[
8
]
and
[
15
]
. In that case, the natural reference
measure is the Wiener measure. Existence and uniqueness of optimal maps were proven more
generally on separable Hilbert spaces in
[
1
]
, where all non-degenerate Gaussian
measures play the role of the reference measure in a certain sense, and on configuration spaces in
[
6
]
.

In our particular infinite-dimensional setting
X=\mathcal{P}(M)
, the choice of an appropriate
reference measure is non-trivial. However, recent work of Dello Schiavo establishes a version of
Rademacher’s theorem on Wasserstein spaces for a class of reference measures satisfying certain
assumptions
[
20
]
. Here, we show that with this result in hand, the fairly
standard proof strategy for the existence of optimal maps on many metric spaces can indeed be adapted to
the Wasserstein space.

The paper is organized as follows. In the following section, we review some basic facts and notation on
optimal transport and Riemannian geometry which we will use later on. In Section 3 we recall relevant
information on the space of probability measures on a Riemannian manifold, and we state and prove our
main theorem on the existence and uniqueness of optimal maps on this space in Section 4. Section 5 is
reserved for an extension of this result to more general cost functions.

2.
Preliminaries

2.1.
Basic tools for optimal transport

A key tool in the analysis of optimal transport plans is the Kantorovich dual problem, which reads:

(3)

\sup_{\begin{subarray}{c}\phi\in C(X),\psi\in C(Y)\end{subarray}}\left\{\int_{X}{\phi(x)\,d\mu}+\int_{Y}{\psi(y)\,d\nu};\quad\phi(x)+\psi(y)\leq c(x,y)\right\}.

Solutions
\varphi
and
\psi
to this problem are called
Kantorovich potentials
. They can be
chosen to have a particular structure, expressed in the following definition.

Definition 1
.

A function
\phi:X\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
is called
c
-concave
if

\phi(x)=\psi^{c}(x):=\inf_{y\in Y}c(x,y)-\psi(y)

for some function
\psi:Y\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
. Similarly, a function
\psi:Y\rightarrow\mathbb{R}

is called
c
-concave if

\psi(y)=\phi^{c}(y):=\inf_{x\in X}c(x,y)-\phi(x)

for some
\phi:X\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
.

The following well known theorem, asserting duality between (
2
) and
(
3
), makes precise the remarks above about the connection between (
2
), (
3
) and the notion of
c
-concavity, and will play a crucial
role in this paper.

Theorem 2
.

Let
\mu,\nu
be Borel probability measures on compact metric spaces,
X
and
Y
, respectively, and
c:X\times Y\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
a continuous cost function.

Then the infimal value in (
2
) equals the supremal value in (
3
), and there exists a minimizer
\gamma
in (
2
) and a maximizer

(\phi,\psi)=(\psi^{c},\phi^{c})
in (
3
) such that both
\phi
and
\psi
are

c
-concave.

Furthermore, for all solutions
\gamma
to (
2
) and
(\phi,\psi)
in
(
3
) we have

\phi(x)+\psi(y)=c(x,y)\qquad\gamma\text{ almost everywhere}.

2.2.
Riemannian manifolds

In this section, we introduce some notation and recall some known facts on Riemannian manifolds which we
will need later on. Let
(M,g)
be a compact connected Riemannian manifold with Riemannian distance

d(x,y):=d_{g}(x,y)
. For the rest of this work, we will quite closely follow notation in

[
20
]
. We denote by
\mathrm{dvol_{g}}
the canonical volume measure on
M
and the set of
all smooth functions on
M
by
C^{\infty}(M)
. Moreover, we denote by
\mathfrak{X}^{0}
the space of all continuous
vector fields on
M
; that is, sections of the tangent bundle
TM
, endowed with the supremum norm

\left\lVert w\right\rVert_{\mathfrak{X}^{0}}:=\sup_{M}\left|w(x)\right|_{g}.

We denote by
\mathfrak{X}^{\infty}
the algebra of smooth vector fields on
M
. Since

is compact, every smooth
vector field

generates a unique global flow
\left({}^{w,t}\right)_{t\in\mathbb{R}}
; that is, each
{}^{w,t}:M\to M
is a smooth orientation-preserving diffeomorphism such that

(4)

\dot{\psy}^{w,t}(x)=w({}^{w,t}(x))\quad\&\quad{}^{w,0}(x)=x,\qquad x\in M,

where
\dot{\psy}^{w,t}(x)
is the velocity of the curve

.

By Theorem
2
, the Kantorovich problem on
M
with the cost function

c(x,y)=d(x,y)^{2}
always admits a solution. Moreover, the following famous theorem of McCann

[
17
]
implies the existence and uniqueness of an optimal map whenever the source measure
lies in the set
\mathcal{P}^{\mathrm{ac}}(M)
of probability measures which are absolutely continuous with respect to
\mathrm{dvol_{g}}
.

Theorem 3

(McCann 2001)
.

Let
\mu\in\mathcal{P}^{\mathrm{ac}}(M)
and
\nu\in\mathcal{P}(M)
. Then the solution to (
2
) on
X=Y=M
with
c(x,y)=d^{2}(x,y)
is
unique and concentrated on the graph of a function
T:M\rightarrow M
. This
T
is in turn the unique
solution to (
1

T(x)=\exp_{x}(-\nabla\phi(x))
for a

\frac{d^{2}}{2}
-concave function
\phi:M\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
.

3.
The space of Borel probability measures on a Riemannian manifold

We now turn our attention to the space
\mathcal{P}(M)

3.1.
Topological properties of
\mathcal{P}(M)

Since

is a compact metric
space, the space of all finite signed Borel measures on

is a weak

induces a bounded linear functional

on
\mathcal{P}(M)
which is called a

. When

is
called a

. Motivated by studies on configuration spaces

[
6

Definition 4
.

A function

is called a “cylinder” function if there exists a positive integer
k>0

on
\mathbb{R}^{k}
and

such

u(\mu)=F(f_{1}^{*}\mu,f_{2}^{*}\mu,\cdots,f_{k}^{*}\mu).

We denote the set of all cylinder functions on
\mathcal{P}(M)
by
\mathcal{FC}^{\infty}
.

The fact that the Kantorovich problem on
M
always admits a solution implies that for every two measures

\mu,\nu\in\mathcal{P}(M)
, the cost of the transportation is a finite value. In fact, this value induces a metric

W_{2}(\mu,\nu):=\left(\int_{X\times Y}d(x,y)^{2}\,d\gamma(x,y)\right)^{\frac{1}{2}},

where
\gamma
is optimal in (
2

\mathcal{P}(M)
, known as the

-Wasserstein metric

-Wasserstein metric metrizes the weak

and turns it into a compact metric space itself. This space

7
).
These conditions are not strictly necessary, however; there are examples of references measures violating
them for which the Rademacher property holds, including the Dirichlet-Ferguson measure

[
7
]

3

Note, however, that Theorem
13
does not apply if

\mathbb{P}_{0}
is the Dirichlet-Ferguson measure, as it concentrates on purely atomic measures, so that the
assumption
\mathbb{P}_{1}(\mathcal{P}^{\mathrm{ac}}(M))=1
is violated for any measure
\mathbb{P}_{1}
which is absolutely continuous with
respect to
\mathbb{P}_{0}
.
. We therefore state our result under the Radmacher property assumption rather than
Assumption
7
, making it potentially more widely applicable.

Theorem 13
.

Let
\mathbb{P}_{1},\mathbb{P}_{2}
be Borel probability measures on
X:=\mathcal{P}(M)
and
Y:=\mathcal{P}(M)
, respectively,
\mathcal{P}(M)
be
equipped with a reference measure
\mathbb{P}_{0}
satisfying the Rademacher property and

c(\mu,\nu)=W_{2}^{2}(\mu,\nu)
for every
\mu,\nu\in\mathcal{P}(M)
. Assuming that
\mathbb{P}_{1}
is absolutely continuous
with respect to
\mathbb{P}_{0}
and
\mathbb{P}_{1}(\mathcal{P}^{\mathrm{ac}}(M))=1
, the optimal transportation problem (
2
) between
(X,\mathbb{P}_{1})
and
(Y,\mathbb{P}_{2})
under this cost function admits a unique solution

\mathbb{P}_{1,2}
which is concentrated on the graph of a map
T:X\to Y
; that is,
T(\mu)=\nu
for

\mathbb{P}_{1,2}

\mathrm{a.e.}\,(\mu,\nu)
and
T_{\#}\mathbb{P}_{1}=\mathbb{P}_{2}
. Furthermore,
T
is the unique solution to
(
1
).

Proof
.

Let
\mathbb{P}_{1,2}\in\mathcal{P}(\mathcal{P}(M)\times\mathcal{P}(M))
solve the Kantorovich problem (
2
) and
U
and
V
be
W_{2}^{2}
-concave functions solving the dual problem. We then
have, by Theorem
2
for all
\mu,\nu\in\mathcal{P}(M)
,

(9)

U(\mu)+V(\nu)\leq W_{2}^{2}(\mu,\nu),

with equality
\mathbb{P}_{1,2}
almost everywhere.
U
is Lipschitz by Lemma
12
. Fix a countable
W=\{w_{i}\}_{i}\subseteq\mathfrak{X}^{\infty}
which is dense in
\mathfrak{X}^{0}
with respect to the
sup norm. Then Corollary
11
implies that (
8
)
holds
\mathbb{P}_{0}
, and therefore
\mathbb{P}_{1}
, almost everywhere for all
w\in W
. Fix any
\mu

where this holds; our goal is to show that there is only one
\nu
where equality holds in (
9
). To this end, suppose equality holds for some fixed
\nu
. For a fixed
w\in W
,
letting
\mu_{t}=\Psi^{w,t}(\mu)
, consider the function

\alpha(t)=U(\mu_{t})-W_{2}^{2}(\mu_{t},\nu).

Since
\alpha(t)\leq-V(\nu)
for all
t
by (
9
), and
\alpha(0)=V(\nu)
by
assumption (as
\mu_{0}=\mu)
,
\alpha
is maximized at
t=0
. Corollary
11
implies the differentiability of
t\mapsto U(\mu_{t})
at
t=0
and Theorem

6
implies that
t\mapsto W_{2}^{2}(\mu_{t},\nu)
is differentiable at
t=0
. It
follows that
\alpha
is differentiable at
0
, and

(10)

\int_{M}\langle\textbf{\text{D}}U(\mu)(x)-2\nabla\phi_{\nu}(x),w(x)\rangle_{g}\,d\mu(x)=\alpha^{\prime}(0)=0,

where
\textbf{\text{D}}U
is as in Theorem
10
and
\phi_{\nu}
is the Kantorovich potential
corresponding to the optimal transport between
\mu
and
\nu
. Since this holds for all
w\in W
, it
holds for all
w\in\mathfrak{X}^{0}
by density, which implies that

\nabla\phi_{\nu}(x)=\frac{1}{2}\textbf{\text{D}}U(\mu)(x)

for
\mu
almost every
x
. But Theorem
3
then implies that

\nu
is uniquely determined by
\mu
as desired; more precisely, we must have

\nu=\exp\left(-\frac{1}{2}\textbf{\text{D}}U(\mu)\right)_{\#}\mu=:T(\mu).

This shows that
\mathbb{P}_{1,2}
concentrates on the graph of the transport map
T
. Uniqueness of the
optimal
\mathbb{P}_{1,2}
in (
2
) then follows by a very standard argument.
If
\mathbb{P}_{1,2}
and
\mathbb{P}_{1,2}^{\prime}
are both optimal, then by linearity so is

\frac{1}{2}[\mathbb{P}_{1,2}+\mathbb{P}_{1,2}^{\prime}]
. The argument above implies that
\mathbb{P}_{1,2},\mathbb{P}_{1,2}^{\prime}
and
\frac{1}{2}[\mathbb{P}_{1,2}+\mathbb{P}_{1,2}^{\prime}]
are all concentrated on maps
over
\mathbb{P}_{1}
, which is impossible unless
\mathbb{P}_{1,2}=\mathbb{P}_{1,2}^{\prime}
.

\square

Remark 14
.

The assumption

5.
Extension to more general functions of the Wasserstein distance

The result of Theorem
13
can be further extended to a class of cost functions, namely,
the class of all functions of the form
c(\mu,\nu)=h(W_{2}(\mu,\nu))
where
h:\mathbb{R}_{+}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
is
C^{2}
-smooth, strictly increasing and strictly convex, as the following theorem asserts.

Theorem 15
.

Let
\mathbb{P}_{1},\mathbb{P}_{2}
be Borel probability measures on
X=Y=\mathcal{P}(M)
, where
\mathcal{P}(M)
is equipped with a reference
measure
\mathbb{P}_{0}
satisfying the Rademacher property, such that
\mathbb{P}_{1}
is absolutely continuous with
respect to
\mathbb{P}_{0}
and
\mathbb{P}_{1}(\mathcal{P}^{\mathrm{ac}}(M))=1
. Let the cost function be defined by
c(\mu,\nu)=h(W_{2}(\mu,\nu))

for every
\mu,\nu\in\mathcal{P}(M)
where
h:\mathbb{R}_{+}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
is
C^{2}
-smooth, strictly
increasing and strictly convex. Then the solution to (
2
) is unique and
concentrated on the graph of a map
T:X\to Y
such that
T_{\#}\mathbb{P}_{1}=\mathbb{P}_{2}
, and
T
is the unique solution
to (
1
).

Proof
.

Since much of the proof is very similar to the proof of Theorem
13
, we only outline
the differences. First note that we can write

c(\mu,\nu)=h(W_{2}(\mu,\nu))=\bar{h}(W_{2}^{2}(\mu,\nu)).

for
\bar{h}(s)=h(\sqrt{s})
. Then, for a curve
\mu_{t}
of measures as in Theorem

6
, (
6
) combined with the chain rule implies

(11)

\dfrac{d}{dt}\Big|_{t=0}c(\mu_{t},\nu)=2\bar{h}^{\prime}(W_{2}^{2}(\mu,\nu))\int_{M}\langle\nabla\phi(x),w(x)\rangle_{g}\,d\mu(x).

Letting
\mathbb{P}
solve (
2
) and
U
,
V
be
W_{2}^{2}
-concave solutions to
(
3
), a similar argument to the proof of Theorem
13
, implies that
for
\mathbb{P}_{1}
almost every
\mu
and
\nu
we have

(12)

\displaystyle\textbf{\text{D}}U(\mu)(x)=2\bar{h}^{\prime}(W_{2}^{2}(\mu,\nu))\nabla\phi_{\nu}(x)\qquad\mu\text{-a.e.}\,

What is left to show is the fact that for fixed
\mu
the above equation uniquely determines
\nu
; that
is

(13)

\displaystyle\bar{h}^{\prime}(W_{2}^{2}(\mu,\nu_{1}))\nabla\phi_{\nu_{1}}=\bar{h}^{\prime}(W_{2}^{2}(\mu,\nu_{2}))\nabla\phi_{\nu_{2}}\implies\nabla\phi_{\nu_{1}}=\nabla\phi_{\nu_{2}},

where
\phi_{\nu_{i}}
is the Kantorovich potential for transport between
\mu
and
\nu_{i}
.
To prove that the above implication holds, we first take the squared norm of both sides of
(
13
) and integrate with respect to
\mu
to obtain

(14)

[\bar{h}^{\prime}(W_{2}^{2}(\mu,\nu_{1}))]^{2}\int_{M}\left|\nabla\phi_{\nu_{1}}\right|^{2}\,d\mu=[\bar{h}^{\prime}(W_{2}^{2}(\mu,\nu_{2}))]^{2}\int_{M}\left|\nabla\phi_{\nu_{2}}\right|^{2}\,d\mu.

Observe that
d_{g}^{2}(x,\exp_{x}(-\nabla\phi(x))=\left|\nabla\phi(x)\right|^{2}
, and therefore

W_{2}^{2}(\mu,\nu)=\int_{M}\left|\nabla\phi\right|^{2}\,d\mu
. This means that (
14
) implies

(15)

\bar{h}^{\prime}(W_{2}^{2}(\mu,\nu_{1}))W_{2}(\mu,\nu_{1})=\bar{h}^{\prime}(W_{2}^{2}(\mu,\nu_{2}))W_{2}(\mu,\nu_{2}).

Now note that

(16)

h(s)=\bar{h}(s^{2})\implies h^{\prime}(s)=2s\bar{h}^{\prime}(s^{2}),

and since
h
is assumed to be strictly convex, the mapping
s\mapsto h^{\prime}(s)=2s\bar{h}^{\prime}(s^{2})
is strictly
increasing. So (
15
) implies that
W_{2}(\mu,\nu_{1})=W_{2}(\mu,\nu_{2})
. If

\bar{h}^{\prime}(W_{2}^{2}(\mu,\nu_{1}))\neq 0
, we can then cancel this common factor in
(
13
) to obtain the desired result. On the other hand, if

\bar{h}^{\prime}(W_{2}^{2}(\mu,\nu_{1}))=0
, we must have
h^{\prime}(W_{2}(\mu,\nu_{1}))=0
by (
16
). As

h
is strictly increasing and strictly convex, we can only have
h^{\prime}(s)=0
at
s=0
, so that

0=W_{2}(\mu,\nu_{1})=W_{2}(\mu,\nu_{2})
, which of course implies
\nu_{1}=\nu_{2}

(=\mu)
in this case as well.
