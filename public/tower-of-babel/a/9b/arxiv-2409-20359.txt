Abstract
. This paper examines minimal hypersurfaces in sub-Riemannian Heisenberg groups. We extend the celebrated Simons formula and Kato inequality to the sub-Riemannian setting, and we apply them to obtain integral curvature estimates for stable hypersurfaces. These results lead to structural conditions that imply a Bernstein-type rigidity theorem for smooth, non-characteristic hypersurfaces in the second Heisenberg group.

53C17, 53A10, 35R03

1.
Introduction

In this paper, we investigate minimal hypersurfaces within sub-Riemannian Heisenberg groups. Our main contribution is the extension to the sub-Riemannian setting of the celebrated
Simons formula
for the Laplacian of the second fundamental form of a minimal hypersurface, discovered by Simons in
[
71
]
. Morover, we provide an improved
Kato inequality
for the gradient of the second fundamental form of a minimal hypersurface, extending its Riemannian version first established by Schoen, Simon and Yau in
[
68
]
. Combining the previous results, we derive integral curvature bounds for stable hypersurfaces. Finally, we apply them to uncover structural conditions under which complete, stable hypersurfaces in the second Heisenberg group are flat from the sub-Riemannian viewpoint, providing a first Bernstein-type rigidity result in Heisenberg groups of higher dimension. The solution to the Euclidean Bernstein problem played a pivotal role in advancing the understanding of the geometric structure of minimal hypersurfaces. Therefore, it seems appropriate to briefly recall the historical origins of this subject.

The Euclidean Bernstein problem

The
Bernstein problem
, originally solved by Bernstein in
{\mathbb{R}}^{2}
(cf.
[
6
]
), consists in characterizing entire solutions
u:{\mathbb{R}}^{n}\to{\mathbb{R}}
to the minimal surface equation

(1.1)

\divv\left(\dfrac{Du}{\sqrt{1+|Du|^{2}}}\right)=0.

Thanks to the combined contributions of Fleming (cf.
[
37
]
), De Giorgi (cf.
[
30
]
), Almgren (cf.
[
2
]
), Simons (cf.
[
71
]
) and Bombieri, De Giorgi
and Giusti (cf.
[
7
]
), we know that, for
n\leqslant 7
, entire solutions to (
1.1
) are affine functions, or, equivalently, their graphs are hyperplanes. In contrast, for
n\geqslant 8
, there exist entire analytic solutions to (
1.1
) that are not affine.
Indeed, the well-known monotonicity formula for the perimeter density allows to reduce the solution to the Bernstein problem in
{\mathbb{R}}^{n+1}
to the existence of singular minimal cones in
{\mathbb{R}}^{n}
(cf.
[
37
,
30
]
), which occurs if and only if
n\geqslant 8
(cf.
[
2
,
71
,
7
]
).
The very same approach is suitable for the solution to a way more general formulation of the Bernstein problem, i.e. the characterization of global perimeter minimizers in
{\mathbb{R}}^{n}
. In this setting, when
n\leqslant 7
, the unique non-empty global perimeter minimizers in
{\mathbb{R}}^{n}
are half-spaces, while there are counterexamples when
n\geqslant 8
.
We refer to
[
45
]
for a detailed account on the Bernstein problem in the Euclidean space.

An alternative approach

A new approach to the Bernstein problem
was proposed by Schoen, Simon and Yau in their seminal paper
[
68
]
, where the authors solved the latter in the class of complete, stable hypersurfaces satisfying suitable volume growth assumptions and under the additional constraint
n\leqslant 6
. This second approach can be summarized in the following steps.

1.

Combining the celebrated
Simons identity
for minimal hypersurfaces
S\subseteq{\mathbb{R}}^{n}
(cf.
[
71
]
), namely

(1.2)

\Delta^{S}h=-|h|^{2}h,

with the
Kato-type inequality

(1.3)

\left(1+\frac{2}{n-1}\right)\left|\nabla^{S}|h|^{2}\right|^{2}\leqslant 4|h|^{2}|\nabla^{S}h|^{2}

(cf.
[
68
]
), one provides a lower bound for
\Delta^{S}|h|^{2}
of the form

(1.4)

2|h|^{2}\Delta^{S}|h|^{2}\geqslant\left(1+\frac{2}{n-1}\right)\left|\nabla^{S}|h|^{2}\right|^{2}-4|h|^{6}.

Here,
h
is the second fundamental form associated to
S
,
\Delta^{S}
is the tangential Laplacian and
\nabla^{S}
is the tangential gradient.

2.

Owing to (
1.4
), one establishes
L^{p}
-estimates for stable hypersurfaces such as

(1.5)

\int_{S}|h|^{p}\varphi^{p}\,d\sigma\leqslant C\int_{S}|\nabla^{S}\varphi|^{p}\,d\sigma,

where
C
is a geometric constant,
p
lies in a range of exponents which depends on the dimension
n
,
\sigma
is the surface measure and
\varphi
is a smooth test function.

3.

Assuming that
S
satisfies volume growth conditions of the form

(1.6)

\sigma(S\cap B_{r}(p))=O\left(r^{n-1}\right)

as
r\to\infty
, one exploits (
1.5
) to show that
S
is totally geodesic, meaning that
h\equiv 0
.

4.

Complete, totally geodesic Euclidean hypersurfaces are hyperplanes.

In particular, since for
n\leqslant 7
boundaries of perimeter minimizers are smooth, complete, stable hypersurfaces and satisfy (
1.6
) (cf.
[
50
]
), this new approach yields a new solution to the Bernstein problem when
n\leqslant 6
.
Although this second approach fails to solve the Bernstein problem in its full generality, as the case
n=7
is not covered, it is originally stated in the more general setting of Riemannian manifolds satisfying suitable curvature constraints. We point out that, in the Euclidean setting, Bellettini (cf.
[
5
]
) has recently extended Schoen-Simon-Yau’s result up to
n=7
. This second approach has the advantage of being applicable to the solution of the so-called
stable Bernstein problem
, i.e. the characterization of complete, stable hypersurfaces, thus without a priori requiring that they are boundaries of global perimeter minimizers. While the thee-dimensional version of the latter has been solved by do Carmo and Peng (cf.
[
32
]
), Fischer-Colbrie and Schoen (cf.
[
36
]
) and Pogorelov (cf.
[
60
]
) via
ad hoc
techniques, Schoen-Simon-Yau’s approach reduces the solution to the higher dimensional case to the establishment of volume growth estimates as in (
1.6
). Following this approach, Chodosh and Li (cf.
[
18
,
19
]
) and Catino, Mastrolia and Roncoroni (cf.
[
12
]
), Chodosh, Li, Minter and Stryker (cf.
[
20
]
) and Mazet (cf.
[
52
]
) recently solved the stable Bernstein problem in
{\mathbb{R}}^{4}
,
{\mathbb{R}}^{5}
and
{\mathbb{R}}^{6}
respectively. We refer to
[
31
,
23
]
for some recent developments in the Riemannian setting.

The sub-Riemannian Bernstein problem

Like its Euclidean and Riemannian precursors, the sub-Riemannian Bernstein problem is an intriguing topic within the broader framework of sub-Riemannian geometry and it fits into the more general context of studying minimal hypersurfaces in sub-Riemannian structures (cf.
[
14
,
15
,
16
,
28
,
34
,
42
,
61
,
43
,
46
,
56
,
57
,
62
,
70
]
and references therein). This research area is particularly relevant in the sub-Riemannian Heisenberg group
\mathbb{H}^{n}
,
which constitutes a prototypical model in the setting of Carnot groups (cf.
[
8
]
), sub-Riemannian manifolds (cf.
[
1
]
), CR manifolds (cf.
[
11
]
) and Carnot-Carathéodory spaces (cf.
[
47
]
).
We briefly recall that the
n
-th Heisenberg group
(\mathbb{H}^{n},\cdot)
is
\mathbb{R}^{2n+1}
endowed with the group law

p\cdot p^{\prime}=(z,t)\cdot(z^{\prime},t^{\prime})=(\bar{x},\bar{y},t)\cdot(\bar{x}^{\prime},\bar{y}^{\prime},t^{\prime})=\left(\bar{x}+\bar{x}^{\prime},\bar{y}+\bar{y}^{\prime},t+t^{\prime}+\sum_{j=1}^{n}\left(x_{j}^{\prime}y_{j}-x_{j}y_{j}^{\prime}\right)\right),

where we denoted points
p\in\mathbb{R}^{2n+1}
by
p=(z,t)=(\bar{x},\bar{y},t)=(x_{1},\ldots,x_{n},y_{1},\ldots,y_{n},t)
.
With this operation,
\mathbb{H}^{n}
is a Carnot group, whose associated
horizontal distribution
, which we denote by
{\mathcal{H}}
, is generated by the left-invariant vector fields

Z_{j}=X_{j}=\frac{\partial}{\partial x_{j}}+y_{j}\frac{\partial}{\partial t}\qquad\text{and}\qquad Z_{n+j}=Y_{j}=\frac{\partial}{\partial x_{j}}-x_{j}\frac{\partial}{\partial t}

for
j=1,\ldots,n
. A vector field which is tangent to
{\mathcal{H}}
at every point is called
horizontal
. If we denote by
T
the left-invariant vector field
\frac{\partial}{\partial t}
, then
X_{1},\ldots,X_{n},Y_{1},\ldots,Y_{n},T
constitutes a global frame of left-invariant vector fields. The only nontrivial commutation relations are

[X_{j},Y_{j}]=-[Y_{j},X_{j}]=-2T

for any
j=1,\ldots,n
.

\mathbb{H}^{n}
inherits a sub-Riemannian structure by fixing be the unique Riemannian metric
\langle\cdot,\cdot\rangle
which makes
X_{1},\ldots,X_{n},Y_{1},\ldots,Y_{n},T
orthonormal. Moreover,
\mathbb{H}^{n}
can be endowed with an appropriate affine connection
\nabla
, the so-called
pseudohermitian connection
, which is metric but not torsion-free, and in a sense realizes it as a flat sub-Riemannian model. These tools both provide an intrinsic definition of perimeter, the so-called
horizontal perimeter
, and enhance the study of the extrinsic geometry of submanifolds in
{\mathbb{H}}^{n}
. Consequently, the sub-Riemannian formulation of the Bernstein problem appears just as natural as its Riemannian counterpart. Nevertheless, its essence is substantially different from the Euclidean one.
First of all, a suitable generalization of the Euclidean monotonicity formula is known to hold only for particular classes of hypersurfaces (cf.
[
27
]
), whereas its validity for general hypersurfaces remains a major open problem in the field. This obstruction prevents the possibility to follow either the classical approach (cf,
[
71
]
) or the more recent one developed in
[
5
]
. Moreover, there are several examples of minimal cones, both smooth (cf.
[
66
,
15
,
46
]
) and with low regularity (cf.
[
62
,
54
]
) which are not flat from the intrinsic viewpoint of
\mathbb{H}^{n}
.
This new phenomenon is caused by the fact that an hypersurface
S\subseteq{\mathbb{H}}^{n}
, although smooth from a classical differential standpoint, may be intrinsically irregular due to the presence of the so-called
characteristic points
, i.e. those points
p\in S
for which the tangent space
T_{p}S
coincides with the horizontal distribution
{\mathcal{H}}_{p}
. For the above-mentioned reasons, the first approach we have described in the Euclidean setting does not appear to be suitable for this context, neither in
{\mathbb{H}}^{1}
nor in higher dimension. Nevertheless, by means of
ad hoc
techniques, the Bernstein problem in
{\mathbb{H}}^{1}
is now largely solved.
Danielli, Garofalo, Nhieu and Pauls (cf.
[
29
]
) and Hurtado, Ritoré and Rosales (cf.
[
49
]
, and cf. also
[
14
,
15
,
66
,
28
]
) proved that a complete, two-sided, connected, stable
C^{2}
-surface in
{\mathbb{H}}^{1}
must be a
vertical plane
(without characteristic points), a
horizontal plane
(with one characteristic point) or the
hyperbolic paraboloid

t=x_{1}y_{1}
(with a line of characteristic points). In general, we recall that a
vertical hyperplane
in
{\mathbb{H}}^{n}
is an Euclidean hyperplane which is tangent to
T
at every point.
The hyperbolic paraboloid, which is a cone for the intrinsic geometry of
\mathbb{H}^{n}
, can be easily lifted to provide smooth, non-flat, minimal cones for any
n\geqslant 2
(cf.
[
59
]
). A special class of surfaces without characteristic points, which we call
non-characteristic
, is that of
intrinsic graphs
as introduced by Franchi, Serapioni and Serra Cassano in
[
38
]
.
In this setting, Barone Adesi, Serra Cassano and Vittone (cf.
[
4
]
) had previously shown that entire, stable intrinsic graphs associated to a
C^{2}
-function are vertical planes. We point out that, differently from the Euclidean setting, stability plays a role even when dealing with intrinsic graphs. Indeed, Danielli, Garofalo and Nhieu exhibited striking examples of entire, minimal intrinsic graphs which are unstable (cf.
[
26
]
). In the non-characteristic setting, the same conclusion was achieved by Galli and Ritoré (cf.
[
40
]
) in the class of non-characteristic, complete, two-sided, connected and stable
C^{1}
-surfaces. The
C^{1}
-regularity assumption was later improved to Euclidean Lipschitz regularity by Nicolussi Golo and Serra Cassano (cf.
[
55
]
) and by the first author and Ritoré (cf.
[
44
]
).
We point out that the best possible regularity to guarantee the above rigidity in
{\mathbb{H}}^{1}
is still an open problem (cf.
[
73
]
for some developments in this direction).
On the other hand, although in
{\mathbb{H}}^{1}
there are counterexamples to the regularity of perimeter minimizers, some evidences (cf. e.g.
[
10
]
) suggest that it is reasonable to study the higher dimensional Bernstein problem in the smooth category.
Therefore, in light of the above results and considerations, the appropriate intrinsic formulation of the Bernstein conjecture in arbitrary dimension reads as follows.

Sub-Riemannian stable Bernstein problem
.

Is it true that smooth, complete, two-sided, connected, stable non-characteristic hypersurfaces
S\subseteq{\mathbb{H}}^{n}
are vertical hyperplanes?

While, as we have just noticed, the problem in
{\mathbb{H}}^{1}
is fairly well understood, very little can be said in the higher dimensional case.
In
[
4
]
, the authors provide a negative answer to this question for
n\geqslant 5
,
essentially by lifting the Euclidean analytic counterexamples available in
{\mathbb{R}}^{n+1}
when
n\geqslant 8
. However, the purely Euclidean character of these counterexamples suggests that the dimensional bound
n\geqslant 5
might not be optimal.
In any case, the validity of this long-standing conjecture in the remaining cases
{\mathbb{H}}^{2}
,
{\mathbb{H}}^{3}
and
{\mathbb{H}}^{4}
remains a completely open problem.
Motivated by the above considerations, in this paper we develop the geometric tools underlying Schoen-Simon-Yau’s approach. As a consequence, we discover some reasonable assumptions, which we now briefly describe, to solve the sub-Riemannian Bernstein’s conjecture.

Simons formulas

A compelling reason to be interested in the extrinsic geometry of hypersurfaces moves from recent results proved by the last two authors of this paper (cf.
[
59
]
). Namely, complete, non-characteristic, embedded hypersurfaces
S\subseteq{\mathbb{H}}^{n}
with vanishing symmetric horizontal second fundamental form
\tilde{h}
are vertical hyperplanes.
One of the main contributions of this paper consists in the establishment of a full sub-Riemannian counterpart of the Simons identity (
1.2
) for
\Delta^{{\mathcal{H}},S}h
, where
\Delta^{{\mathcal{H}},S}
is the
horizontal tangential Laplacian
of
S
, which relates the latter to the stability function
q
appearing in (
1.7
) with the aid of appropriate sub-Riemannian Gauss-Codazzi equations (cf.
Proposition

3.13
).

Theorem 1.1

(Simons formula)
.

Let
S
be a smooth, immersed, minimal hypersurface in
{\mathbb{H}}^{n}
. Then

(1.9)

\begin{split}\Delta^{{\mathcal{H}},S}h(X,Y)&=-qh(X,Y)+8\alpha^{2}h(X,Y)\\
&\quad+4\hess^{{\mathcal{H}},S}\alpha(\pi(J(X)),Y)+4\hess^{{\mathcal{H}},S}\alpha(X,\pi(J(Y)))\\
&\quad+\Big(16\alpha\pi(J(X))\alpha-8\alpha^{2}h(X,J(\nu))+4\left(\nabla_{X}\nu\right)\alpha\Big)\langle Y,J(\nu)\rangle\\
&\quad-2X\alpha h(Y,J(\nu))-2Y\alpha h(X,J(\nu))\\
&\quad+2\alpha h(Y,\nabla_{\pi(J(X))}\nu)-2\alpha\langle\nabla_{X}\nabla_{J(\nu)}\nu,Y\rangle-4\alpha^{2}h(\pi(J(X)),\pi(J(Y)))\\

lack of sub-Riemannian

requires some delicate

Section

). Although a result of this kind holds in arbitrary dimension, the specific structure of

allows to provide a precise description of the last term appearing in (

(1.11)

\begin{split}\frac{1}{2}\hat{\Delta}^{{\mathcal{H}},S}|\tilde{h}|^{2}&=|\nabla^{{\mathcal{H}},S}\tilde{h}|^{2}-q|\tilde{h}|^{2}+4\alpha^{2}|\tilde{h}|^{2}-8\alpha^{2}\left|\nabla_{J(\nu)}\nu\right|^{2}+2\alpha^{2}\ell^{2}\\
&\quad+4\left(J(\nu)\alpha+\alpha^{2}\right)\left(2|\tilde{h}|^{2}-4\left|\nabla_{J(\nu)}\nu\right|^{2}+\ell^{2}\right)\\
&\quad+\left(8J(\nu)\alpha+6\alpha^{2}\right)\left(\left|\nabla_{J(\nu)}\nu\right|^{2}-\ell^{2}\right)\end{split}

on the non-characteristic part of

. In turn, the structure of (

) and (

\begin{split}\frac{1}{2}\hat{\Delta}^{{\mathcal{H}},S}|\tilde{h}|^{2}\geqslant|\nabla^{{\mathcal{H}},S}\tilde{h}|^{2}-q|\tilde{h}|^{2}+\alpha^{2}\left(4|\tilde{h}|^{2}-6\ell^{2}\right)\end{split}

on the non-characteristic part of

.
We point out that the lower bound provided by (

), (

) and (

Kato inequalities

All the aforementioned contracted Simons inequality are characterized by the presence of the gradient term
|\nabla^{{\mathcal{H}},S}\tilde{h}|
. While the latter always admits the trivial lower bound

(1.13)

|\nabla^{{\mathcal{H}},S}|\tilde{h}|^{2}|^{2}\leqslant 4|\tilde{h}|^{2}|\nabla^{{\mathcal{H}},S}\tilde{h}|^{2},

the minimality of
S
, coupled with (

⁢
P
1

) and (

⁢
P
2

), allows to improve (
1.13
) to a parametric sub-Riemannian Kato inequality in the spirit of (

).

Theorem 1.3

(Improved Kato inequalities)
.

Let
S
be a smooth, immersed hypersurface in
{\mathbb{H}}^{n}
. Then (
1.13
) holds. Assume that
S
is minimal. Assume (

⁢
P
1

) and (

⁢
P
2

).
Then

(1.14)

\left(1+\frac{k}{2n-1}\right)|\nabla^{{\mathcal{H}},S}|\tilde{h}|^{2}|^{2}\leqslant 4|\tilde{h}|^{2}|\nabla^{{\mathcal{H}},S}\tilde{h}|^{2}+4\alpha^{2}|\tilde{h}|^{2}\left((4k-2)|\tilde{h}|^{2}+(2+2kn-2k-4n)\ell^{2}\right)

on the non-characteristic part of
S
for any
k\in[0,2]
.

Even just focusing on the second Heisenberg group, the last terms appearing respectively in (
1.12
) and (
1.14
) constitute a crucial novelty compared to the Euclidean setting. Indeed, the presence of these two

-remainders
is essentially due to the fact that, differently from the Euclidean setting, the lack of torsion-freeness of

prevents

from being a

(cf.
Proposition

3.14
). Consequently, the parametric nature of the improved Kato inequality is crucial in managing to control these additional terms. While the
\alpha^{2}
-reminder in (

) is constrained by (

⁢
P
3

), the freedom to choose

-reminder. Roughly speaking, for a better control on the latter we need to pay a worse contribution from the former. A careful combination of (

1.14
) (cf.

5.4
), allows to establish the sub-Riemannian counterpart of (

2|\tilde{h}|^{2}\hat{\Delta}^{{\mathcal{H}},S}|\tilde{h}|^{2}\geqslant\left(1+\frac{k}{3}\right)|\nabla^{S}|\tilde{h}|^{2}|^{2}-4q|\tilde{h}|^{4}+4\alpha^{2}|\tilde{h}|^{2}g_{S,k}.

Here

Once (

Theorem 1.4

(Integral curvature estimates in
{\mathbb{H}}^{2}
)
.

Let

1.1.
An application to the Bernstein problem

Heuristically, the application of (

to be chosen as close as possible to its upper bound, since the bigger is the latter, the higher is the dimension

in which we can apply this approach. Indeed, under natural sub-Riemannian volume growth assumptions of the form

as

. All in all, then, we would like to choose

large enough to ensure that

, but still small enough to ensure that

. Regarding the first condition, it is easy to verify that even the optimal choice

2.
Preliminaries

2.1.
The Heisenberg group

In the following, we denote by
\Gamma(T{\mathbb{H}}^{n})
and
\Gamma({\mathcal{H}})
the families of smooth vector fields and of smooth horizontal vector fields respectively.
The
complex structure

J:\Gamma(T{\mathbb{H}}^{n})\longrightarrow\Gamma(T{\mathbb{H}}^{n})
is the unique
C^{\infty}({\mathbb{H}}^{n})
-linear map which satisfies

J(X_{i})=Y_{i},\qquad J(Y_{i})=-X_{i}\qquad\text{and}\qquad J(T)=0

for any
i=1,\ldots,n
. In particular, observe that

(2.1)

J(J(X))=-X\qquad\text{and}\qquad\langle X,J(X)\rangle=0

for any
X\in\Gamma({\mathcal{H}})
. The latter, together with the distribution
{\mathcal{H}}
, realizes
{\mathbb{H}}^{n}
as a
pseudohermitian manifold
(cf.
[
14
, Appendix]
).
We recall that
\langle\cdot,\cdot\rangle
is the unique Riemannian metric which makes
X_{1},\ldots,X_{n},Y_{1},\ldots,Y_{n},T
orthonormal.
Restricting it to
{\mathcal{H}}
, and still denoting this restriction by
\langle\cdot,\cdot\rangle
,
\mathbb{H}^{n}
inherits a
sub-Riemannian structure
which realizes it as a
sub-Riemannian manifold.
We denote by
|\cdot|
the norm induced by
\langle\cdot,\cdot\rangle
.
Moreover, we denote by
\nabla
the so-called
pseudohermitian connection
(cf. e.g.
[
64
]
), i.e. the unique
metric connection
(cf.
[
33
]
) whose torsion tensor is

(2.2)

\nabla_{X}Y-\nabla_{Y}X-[X,Y]=2\langle J(X),Y\rangle T

for any
X,Y\in\Gamma(T{\mathbb{H}}^{n})
. Although
\nabla
is not a torsion-free connection, it has the advantage of vanishing along left-invariant vector fields, meaning that

(2.3)

\nabla_{Z_{i}}Z_{j}=0

for any
i,j=1,\ldots,2n+1
(cf.
[
63
]
). In view of (
2.3
), it is easy to check that

X\in\Gamma(T{\mathbb{H}}^{n}),\,Y\in\Gamma({\mathcal{H}})\qquad\implies\qquad\nabla_{X}Y\in\Gamma({\mathcal{H}}).

Moreover, denoting by
R
the curvature tensor associated with
\nabla
, i.e.

R(X,Y)Z=\nabla_{X}\nabla_{Y}Z-\nabla_{Y}\nabla_{X}Z-\nabla_{[X,Y]}Z

for any
X,Y,Z\in\Gamma(T{\mathbb{H}}^{n})
, (
2.3
) implies that
R\equiv 0
, whence
{\mathbb{H}}^{n}
is flat from the pseudohermitian standpoint.
The pseudohermitian connection is related to the complex structure by

(2.4)

\nabla_{X}J(Y)=J(\nabla_{X}Y).

for any
X,Y\in\Gamma(T\mathbb{H}^{n})
(cf. e.g.
[
35
]
).
Given a function
f\in C^{\infty}({\mathbb{H}}^{n})
, we denote by

\nabla f=\sum_{j=1}^{2n+1}\left(Z_{j}f\right)Z_{j}\qquad\text{and}\qquad\nabla^{\mathcal{H}}f=\sum_{j=1}^{2n}\left(Z_{j}f\right)Z_{j}\qquad

the gradient and the horizontal gradient associated with the pseudohermitian connection
\nabla
.

2.2.
Carnot-Carathéodory structure

An absolutely continuous curve
\Gamma:[a,b]\longrightarrow\mathbb{H}^{n}
is
horizontal
if

(2.5)

\dot{\Gamma}(t)\in{\mathcal{H}}_{\Gamma(t)}

for a.e.
t\in[a,b]
, and it is
sub-unit
if it is horizontal and
|\dot{\Gamma}(t)|\leqslant 1
for a.e.
t\in[a,b]
. If we define

d(p,q):=\inf\{b\,:\,\Gamma:[0,b]\longrightarrow\mathbb{H}^{n}\text{ is sub-unit, $\Gamma(0)=p$ and $\Gamma(b)=q$}\},

then Chow-Rashevskii theorem (cf. e.g.
[
8
]
) implies that
d
is a distance on
\mathbb{H}^{n}
, the so-called
Carnot-Carathéodory distance
. The metric space
(\mathbb{H}^{n},d)
is a prototype of Carnot-Carathéodory space.
For any
r>0
and any
p\in{\mathbb{H}}^{n}
, we let
B_{r}(p)
be the open ball of radius
r
centered at
p
induced by
d
.

2.3.
Perimeter and perimeter minimizers

Let
\Omega\subseteq\mathbb{H}^{n}
be open and
E\subseteq\mathbb{H}^{n}
measurable. The
horizontal perimeter
(or
{\mathbb{H}}
-perimeter
) of
E
in
\Omega
(cf. e.g.
[
38
,
39
,
41
]
) is defined by

P_{\mathbb{H}}(E,\Omega):=\sup\left\{\int_{E}\divv_{{\mathcal{H}}}(\bar{\varphi})\,d\mathcal{L}^{2n+1}\,:\,\bar{\varphi}\in C^{1}_{c}(\Omega,\mathcal{H}),\,|\bar{\varphi}|_{p}\leqslant 1\text{ for any }p\in\Omega\right\},

where by
C^{1}_{c}(\Omega,{\mathcal{H}})
we denote the class of compactly supported horizontal vector fields defined on
\Omega
, and where
\divv_{{\mathcal{H}}}
is the so-called
horizontal divergence
, defined by

\divv_{{\mathcal{H}}}\left(\sum_{j=1}^{n}(\varphi_{j}X_{j}+\varphi_{n+j}Y_{j})\right):=\sum_{j=1}^{n}(X_{j}\varphi_{j}+Y_{j}\varphi_{n+j})

for any
\sum_{j=1}^{n}(\varphi_{j}X_{j}+\varphi_{n+j}Y_{j})\in C^{1}(\Omega,{\mathcal{H}})
. We say that
E
is an
\mathbb{H}
-Caccioppoli set
whenever
P_{\mathbb{H}}(E,\Omega)<+\infty
for any bounded open set
\Omega\subseteq\mathbb{H}^{n}
. Finally, we recall (cf. e.g.
[
69
]
) that an
\mathbb{H}
-Caccioppoli set
E
is an
\mathbb{H}
-perimeter minimizer
in
\Omega
whenever

P_{\mathbb{H}}(E,\Omega)\leqslant P_{\mathbb{H}}(F,\Omega)

for any
\Omega\Subset\mathbb{H}^{n}
and for any
\mathbb{H}
-Caccioppoli set
F
such that
E\Delta F\Subset\Omega
. When
E
is an
\mathbb{H}
-perimeter minimizer in
\Omega={\mathbb{H}}^{n}
, we refer to it as
global
\mathbb{H}
-perimeter minimizer
.

3.
Geometric properties of non-characteristic hypersurfaces

3.1.
Non-characteristic hypersurfaces

Let
S\subseteq{\mathbb{H}}^{n}
be a smooth, immersed hypersurface without boundary. We recall (cf. e.g.
[
69
]
) that a point
p\in S
is called
characteristic
when

{\mathcal{H}}_{p}=T_{p}S,

and is called
non-characteristic
otherwise. In the latter case, the
horizontal tangent space

{\mathcal{H}}T_{p}S={\mathcal{H}}_{p}\cap T_{p}S

is a
(2n-1)
-dimensional vector space. The set of characteristic points of
S
is denoted by
S_{0}
and is called the
characteristic set
of
S
. When
S_{0}=\emptyset
,
S
is called
non-characteristic
, and the
horizontal tangent distribution

{\mathcal{H}}TS
is actually a constant-rank sub-bundle of
TS
. According to the previous notation, we denote by
\Gamma(TS)
and by
\Gamma({\mathcal{H}}TS)
the families of smooth vector fields which are tangent to
S
and which are horizontal and tangent to
S
respectively. In the following, unless otherwise specified, we assume that
S
is a smooth, immersed, non-characteristic hypersurface without boundary. When our statements are of local nature, we assume without loss of generality that
S
is two-sided and embedded. We denote by
N
its Riemannian unit normal, and by
N^{\mathbb{H}}
its projection onto
{\mathcal{H}}
, that is

N^{\mathbb{H}}=N-\langle N,T\rangle T.

Being
S
non-characteristic, then
N^{\mathbb{H}}(p)\neq 0
for any
p\in S
, so that the
horizontal unit normal

\nu=\frac{N^{\mathbb{H}}}{|N^{\mathbb{H}}|}

is well-defined on the whole
S
. Notice that the horizontal unit normal can be characterized to be the unique unitary horizontal vector field which is orthogonal to any horizontal tangent vector field. When
E
is an
{\mathbb{H}}
-Caccioppoli set in
{\mathbb{H}}^{n}
with boundary of class
C^{1}
, it is known (cf. e.g.
[
25
]
) that
P_{\mathbb{H}}(E,\cdot)=|N^{\mathbb{H}}|\mathcal{H}^{2n}\llcorner\partial E
, being
\mathcal{H}^{2n}
the standard
(2n)
-dimensional Hausdorff measure. Therefore, if
S
is a two-sided hypersurface as above, in the following we adopt the notation

\sigma_{\mathcal{H}}=|N^{\mathbb{H}}|\mathcal{H}^{2n}\llcorner S

to denote the relevant sub-Riemannian hypersurface measure as introduced e.g. in
[
25
,
51
]
.
Let
d^{S}
be the signed Carnot-Carathéodory distance from
S
. When
S
is embedded,
d^{S}
is smooth and satisfies the eikonal equation
|\nabla^{\mathcal{H}}d^{S}|=1
in a neighborhood of
S
(cf.
[
64
]
). In this case, we assume that
\nu
is defined in a neighborhood of
S
by

(3.1)

\nu=\nabla^{\mathcal{H}}d^{S}.

When
\nu
is locally extended as in (
3.1
), it follows that

(3.2)

Z_{k}(\nu_{h})=Z_{h}(\nu_{k})

for any
h,k=1,\ldots,2n
such that
|h-k|\neq n
, and

(3.3)

X_{k}(\nu_{n+k})=Y_{k}(\nu_{k})-2\alpha\qquad\text{and}\qquad Y_{k}(\nu_{k})=X_{k}(\nu_{n+k})+2\alpha

for any
k=1,\ldots,n
, where here and in the following, according to
[
17
,
13
]
, we adopt the notation
\alpha=Td^{S}
.
In particular, an easy computation (cf.
[
59
]
) reveals that

(3.4)

\nabla_{\nu}\nu=-2\alpha J(\nu),

Moreover, the Riemannian normal
N
can be locally extended by letting

(3.5)

N=\frac{1}{\sqrt{1+\alpha^{2}}}\nu+\frac{\alpha}{\sqrt{1+\alpha^{2}}}T.

Let us provide a more precise description of the tangent space to
S
. First, (
2.1
) implies that
J(\nu)\in\Gamma({\mathcal{H}}TS)
.
Moreover, denoting by
{\mathcal{H}}^{\prime}TS
the distribution defined by

{\mathcal{H}}^{\prime}T_{p}S={\mathcal{H}}T_{p}S\cap J\left({\mathcal{H}}T_{p}S\right)

for any
p\in S
, it is easy to check that it is a
(2n-2)
-dimensional sub-bundle of
{\mathcal{H}}TS
, and that the latter can be orthogonally decomposed as

{\mathcal{H}}TS={\mathcal{H}}^{\prime}TS\oplus\spann J(\nu).

Finally, (
3.5
) implies that the vector field
\mathcal{S}
defined by

\mathcal{S}=T-\alpha\nu

belongs to
\Gamma(TS)
and satisfies
\langle\mathcal{S},X\rangle=0
for any
X\in\Gamma({\mathcal{H}}TS)
. Therefore, the tangent space to
S
admits the orthogonal decomposition

TS={\mathcal{H}}^{\prime}TS\oplus\spann J(\nu)\oplus\spann\mathcal{S}.

In the following, we denote by
\pi:\Gamma({\mathcal{H}})\longrightarrow\Gamma({\mathcal{H}}TS)
the projection map

(3.6)

\pi(X)=X-\langle X,\nu\rangle\nu=\sum_{i=1}^{2n-1}\langle X,E_{i}\rangle E_{i}

for any
X\in\Gamma({\mathcal{H}})
and any local orthonormal frame
\mathrm{E}_{1},\ldots,\mathrm{E}_{2n-1}
of
{\mathcal{H}}TS
. In particular,

(3.7)

\pi(J(X))\in\Gamma({\mathcal{H}}^{\prime}TS)\qquad\text{and}\qquad J(\pi(J(X)))=-X+\langle X,J(\nu)\rangle J(\nu).

Let
X\in\Gamma({\mathcal{H}}TS)
. By (
2.2
),

(3.8)

[J(\nu),X]=\nabla_{J(\nu)}X-\nabla_{X}J(\nu)\in\Gamma({\mathcal{H}}TS).

In addition,

(3.9)

\langle[J(\nu),X],X\rangle=\langle\nabla_{X}\nu,\pi(J(X))\rangle.

The horizontal unit normal
\nu
evolves along
\mathcal{S}
as follows.

Proposition 3.1
.

Let
S
be a smooth, immersed, non-characteristic hypersurface without boundary. Then

(3.10)

\nabla_{\mathcal{S}}\nu=\nabla^{\mathcal{H}}\alpha+2\alpha^{2}J(\nu).

In particular, if
X\in\Gamma({\mathcal{H}}TS)
, then

(3.11)

\langle\nabla_{\mathcal{S}}\nu,X\rangle=X\alpha+2\alpha^{2}\langle J(\nu),X\rangle.

Proof.

We assume without loss of generality that
S
is embedded. Let
\nu
be extended as in (
3.1
). Then, recalling (
3.4
),

\nabla_{\mathcal{S}}\nu=\nabla_{T}\nu-\alpha\nabla_{\nu}\nu=\sum_{j=1}^{2n}T(Z_{j}d)Z_{j}+2\alpha^{2}J(\nu)=\sum_{j=1}^{2n}Z_{j}\alpha Z_{j}+2\alpha^{2}J(\nu)=\nabla^{\mathcal{H}}\alpha+2\alpha^{2}J(\nu).

Finally, (
3.11
) easily follows.
∎

3.2.
Tangent pseudohermitian connection

The
tangent pseudohermitian connection
, denoted by
\nabla^{S}:\Gamma(TS)\times\Gamma({\mathcal{H}}TS)\longrightarrow\Gamma({\mathcal{H}}TS)
, is defined by

\nabla^{S}_{X}Y=\nabla_{X}Y-\langle\nabla_{X}Y,\nu\rangle\nu

for any
X\in\Gamma(TS)
and any
Y\in\Gamma({\mathcal{H}}TS)
. An easy computation reveals that
\nabla^{S}
is a well-defined affine connection, and that it is metric in the sense that

(3.12)

X\langle Y,Z\rangle=\langle\nabla^{S}_{X}Y,Z\rangle+\langle Y,\nabla^{S}_{X}Z\rangle

for any
X\in\Gamma(TS)
and any
Y,Z\in\Gamma({\mathcal{H}}TS)
.
Accordingly, the torsion tensor
\tor_{\nabla^{S}}(X,Y):\Gamma({\mathcal{H}}TS)\times\Gamma({\mathcal{H}}TS)\longrightarrow\Gamma(TS)
is defined by

\tors(X,Y)=\nabla^{S}_{X}Y-\nabla^{S}_{Y}X-[X,Y].

We stress that we are not requiring
\tors(X,Y)
to be horizontal, so that, by Frobenious theorem,
\tors
is well-defined. The latter admits the following explicit expression.

Proposition 3.2
.

Let
X,Y\in\Gamma({\mathcal{H}}TS)
. Then

(3.13)

\tors(X,Y)=2\langle J(X),Y\rangle\mathcal{S}.

Proof.

Let
X,Y\in\Gamma({\mathcal{H}}TS)
.
If
X=\sum_{j=1}^{2n}X^{j}Z_{j}
and
Y=\sum_{j=1}^{2n}Y^{j}Z_{j}
, then

\begin{split}-\langle[X,Y],\nu\rangle&\overset{\eqref{pseudotorsion}}{=}2\langle J(X),Y\rangle\langle\nu,T\rangle+\langle\nabla_{X}\nu,Y\rangle-\langle\nabla_{Y}\nu,X\rangle\\
&\overset{\eqref{phflat}}{=}\sum_{i,j=1}^{2n}X^{i}Y^{j}(Z_{i}\nu_{j}-Z_{j}\nu_{i})\\
&\overset{\eqref{propvh2},\eqref{propvh3}}{=}-2\alpha\sum_{i=1}^{n}X^{i}Y^{n+i}+2\alpha\sum_{i=1}^{n}X^{n+i}Y^{i}\\
&=-2\alpha\langle J(X),Y)\rangle.\end{split}

In particular,
\tors(X,Y)=\torn(X,Y)-\langle\torn(X,Y),\nu\rangle\nu-\langle[X,Y],\nu\rangle\nu=2\langle J(X),Y\rangle\mathcal{S}
.
∎

If
f\in C^{\infty}(S)
, we denote by

\nabla^{{\mathcal{H}},S}f=\sum_{j=1}^{2n-1}\left(\mathrm{E}_{j}f\right)\mathrm{E}_{j}

the horizontal tangential gradient associated with the connection
\nabla^{S}
, where
\mathrm{E}_{1},\ldots,\mathrm{E}_{2n-1}
is any local orthonormal frame of
{\mathcal{H}}TS
. More generally, if
p\in\mathbb{N}
and
T
is a horizontal
(p,0)
-tensor field (cf.
[
33
]
), meaning that
T:\Gamma({\mathcal{H}}TS)^{p}\longrightarrow C^{\infty}(S)
is a
C^{\infty}(S)
-multilinear map, the
(p+1,0)
tensor field
\nabla^{S}T:\Gamma(TS)\times\Gamma({\mathcal{H}}TS)^{p}\longrightarrow C^{\infty}(S)
is defined by

\nabla^{S}_{X}T(X_{1},\ldots,X_{p})=X\left(T(X_{1},\ldots,X_{p})\right)-T\left(\nabla^{S}_{X}X_{1},\ldots,X_{p}\right)-\ldots-T\left(X_{1},\ldots,\nabla^{S}_{X}X_{p}\right)

for any
X\in\Gamma(TS)
and any
X_{1},\ldots,X_{p}\in\Gamma({\mathcal{H}}TS)
. According to the above notation, we denote by
\nabla^{{\mathcal{H}},S}T
the restriction of
\nabla^{S}T
to
\Gamma({\mathcal{H}}TS)^{p+1}
. As a general fact,
\nabla^{S}
verifies the Leibniz-type rule

(3.14)

\nabla^{S}_{X}(TU)=U\nabla^{S}_{X}T+T\nabla^{S}_{X}U

for any
X\in\Gamma(TS)
and any couple of tensor fields
T,U
. If
T,U
are as above, we set

\langle T,U\rangle=\sum_{i_{1},\ldots,i_{p}=1}^{2n-1}T\left(\mathrm{E}_{i_{1}},\ldots,\mathrm{E}_{i_{p}}\right)U\left(\mathrm{E}_{i_{1}},\ldots,\mathrm{E}_{i_{p}}\right)\qquad\text{and}\qquad|T|^{2}=\sum_{i_{1},\ldots,i_{p}=1}^{2n-1}T\left(\mathrm{E}_{i_{1}},\ldots,\mathrm{E}_{i_{p}}\right)^{2}

for any local orthonormal frame
\mathrm{E}_{1},\ldots,\mathrm{E}_{2n-1}
of
{\mathcal{H}}TS
.
The
horizontal tangential Hessian
of
T
is the
(p+2,0)
-tensor field
\hess^{{\mathcal{H}},S}T:\Gamma({\mathcal{H}}TS)^{p+2}\longrightarrow C^{\infty}(S)
defined by

\hess^{{\mathcal{H}},S}T(X,Y,X_{1},\ldots,X_{p})=\nabla^{S}_{X}\nabla^{S}_{Y}T(X_{1},\ldots,X_{p})

for any
X,Y,X_{1},\ldots,X_{p}\in\Gamma({\mathcal{H}}TS)
. Finally, the
horizontal tangential Laplacian
of
T
is the
(p,0)
-tensor field
\Delta^{{\mathcal{H}},S}T:\Gamma({\mathcal{H}}TS)^{p}\longrightarrow C^{\infty}(S)
defined by

\Delta^{{\mathcal{H}},S}T(X_{1},\ldots,X_{p})=\trace\hess^{{\mathcal{H}},S}T(\cdot,\cdot,X_{1},\ldots,X_{p})=\sum_{j=1}^{2n-1}\hess^{{\mathcal{H}},S}T(\mathrm{E}_{j},\mathrm{E}_{j},X_{1},\ldots,X_{p})

for any
X_{1},\ldots,X_{p}\in\Gamma({\mathcal{H}}TS)
and any local orthonormal frame
\mathrm{E}_{1},\ldots,\mathrm{E}_{2n-1}
of
{\mathcal{H}}TS
. We denote by
R^{S}:\Gamma({\mathcal{H}}TS)\times\Gamma({\mathcal{H}}TS)\times\Gamma({\mathcal{H}}TS)\longrightarrow\Gamma({\mathcal{H}}TS)
the curvature tensor associated with
\nabla^{S}
, that is

R^{S}(X,Y)Z=\nabla^{S}_{X}\nabla_{Y}^{S}Z-\nabla^{S}_{Y}\nabla^{S}_{X}Z-\nabla^{S}_{[X,Y]}Z

for any
X,Y,Z\in\Gamma({\mathcal{H}}TS)
,
and with some abuse of notation we let

R^{S}(X,Y,Z,W)=\langle\nabla^{S}_{X}\nabla_{Y}^{S}Z-\nabla^{S}_{Y}\nabla^{S}_{X}Z-\nabla^{S}_{[X,Y]}Z,W\rangle

for any
X,Y,Z,W\in\Gamma({\mathcal{H}}TS)
.
The horizontal Hessian is affected by
R^{S}
as follows.

Proposition 3.3
.

Let
T:\Gamma({\mathcal{H}}TS)\times\Gamma({\mathcal{H}}TS)\longrightarrow C^{\infty}(S)
be a
(2,0)
-tensor field.
Then

(3.15)

\begin{split}\hess^{{\mathcal{H}},S}T(Y,X,Z,W)&=\hess^{{\mathcal{H}},S}T(X,Y,Z,W)\\
&\quad+T(R^{S}(X,Y)Z,W)+T(Z,R^{S}(X,Y)W)\\
&\quad+2\langle J(X),Y\rangle\left(\nabla^{S}_{\mathcal{S}}T\right)(Z,W)\end{split}

for any
X,Y,Z,W\in\Gamma({\mathcal{H}}TS)
.

Proof.

Notice that

\begin{split}\hess^{{\mathcal{H}},S}T(X,Y,Z,W)&=XY(T(Z,W))-X(T(\nabla^{S}_{Y}Z,W))-X(T(Z,\nabla^{S}_{Y}W))\\
&-\nabla^{S}_{X}Y(T(Z,W))+T(\nabla^{S}_{\nabla^{S}_{X}Y}Z,W)+T(Z,\nabla^{S}_{\nabla^{S}_{X}Y}W)\\
&-Y(T(\nabla^{S}_{X}Z,W))+T(\nabla^{S}_{Y}\nabla^{S}_{X}Z,W)+T(\nabla^{S}_{X}Z,\nabla^{S}_{Y}W)\\
&-Y(T(Z,\nabla^{S}_{X}W))+T(\nabla^{S}_{Y}Z,\nabla^{S}_{X}W)+T(Z,\nabla^{S}_{Y}\nabla^{S}_{X}W)\end{split}

and, in the same way,

\begin{split}\hess^{{\mathcal{H}},S}T(Y,X,Z,W)&=YX(T(Z,W))-Y(T(\nabla^{S}_{X}Z,W))-Y(T(Z,\nabla^{S}_{X}W))\\
&-\nabla^{S}_{Y}X(T(Z,W))+T(\nabla^{S}_{\nabla^{S}_{Y}X}Z,W)+T(Z,\nabla^{S}_{\nabla^{S}_{Y}X}W)\\
&-X(T(\nabla^{S}_{Y}Z,W))+T(\nabla^{S}_{X}\nabla^{S}_{Y}Z,W)+T(\nabla^{S}_{Y}Z,\nabla^{S}_{X}W)\\
&-X(T(Z,\nabla^{S}_{Y}W))+T(\nabla^{S}_{X}Z,\nabla^{S}_{Y}W)+T(Z,\nabla^{S}_{X}\nabla^{S}_{Y}W).\end{split}

Hence

\begin{split}\hess^{{\mathcal{H}},S}&T(Y,X,Z,W)-\hess^{{\mathcal{H}},S}T(X,Y,W,Z)=\tor_{\nabla}^{S}(X,Y)(T(Z,W))\\
&\quad+T(\nabla^{S}_{X}\nabla^{S}_{Y}Z-\nabla^{S}_{Y}\nabla^{S}_{X}Z-\nabla^{S}_{\nabla^{S}_{X}Y-\nabla^{S}_{Y}X}Z,W)\\
&\quad+T(Z,\nabla^{S}_{X}\nabla^{S}_{Y}W-\nabla^{S}_{Y}\nabla^{S}_{X}W-\nabla^{S}_{\nabla^{S}_{X}Y-\nabla^{S}_{Y}X}W)\\
&=\tor_{\nabla^{S}}(X,Y)(T(Z,W))-T(\nabla^{S}_{\tor_{\nabla^{S}}(X,Y)}Z,W)-T(Z,\nabla^{S}_{\tor_{\nabla^{S}}(X,Y)}W)\\
&\quad+T(R^{S}(X,Y)Z,W)+T(Z,R^{S}(X,Y)W)\\
&=\nabla^{S}_{\tors(X,Y)}T(Z,W)+T(R^{S}(X,Y)Z,W)+T(Z,R^{S}(X,Y)W)\\
&\overset{\eqref{torsionofnablas}}{=}2\langle J(X),Y\rangle\nabla^{S}_{\mathcal{S}}T(Z,W)+T(R^{S}(X,Y)Z,W)+T(Z,R^{S}(X,Y)W).\\
\end{split}

∎

3.3.
Second fundamental forms and mean curvature

In the current literature, different types of second fundamental form are available in the sub-Riemannian Heisenberg group (cf. e.g.
[
48
,
25
,
17
,
63
,
64
]
). The
horizontal shape operator

A:\Gamma(TS)\longrightarrow\Gamma({\mathcal{H}}TS)
and the
symmetric horizontal shape operator

\tilde{A}:\Gamma({\mathcal{H}}TS)\longrightarrow\Gamma({\mathcal{H}}TS)
are defined respectively by

A(X)=\nabla_{X}\nu\qquad\text{and}\qquad\tilde{A}(X)=\nabla_{X}\nu+\alpha J^{\prime}(X)

for any
X\in\Gamma({\mathcal{H}}TS)
, where
J^{\prime}=J\text{ on }{\mathcal{H}}^{\prime}TS
and
J^{\prime}(J(\nu))=0
.
It is easy to check that
A
and
\tilde{A}
are well-defined. Accordingly, the
horizontal second fundamental form

h
and the
symmetric horizontal second fundamental form

\tilde{h}
are the horizontal
(2,0)
-tensor fields defined by

h(X,Y)=\langle A(X),Y\rangle\qquad\text{and}\qquad\tilde{h}(X,Y)=\langle\tilde{A}(X),Y\rangle

for any
X,Y\in\Gamma({\mathcal{H}}TS)
.
As in the Riemannian setting, the horizontal second fundamental form
h
relates the connections
\nabla
and
\nabla^{S}
by the identity

\nabla_{X}Y=\nabla^{S}_{X}Y-h(X,Y)\nu

for any
X,Y\in\Gamma({\mathcal{H}}TS)
. It is well known that
\tilde{h}
is symmetric, while, when
n\geqslant 2
,
h
may not be symmetric (cf. e.g.
[
25
,
63
]
). More precisely,
h
and
\tilde{h}
are related in the following way.

Proposition 3.4
.

Let
X,Y\in\Gamma({\mathcal{H}}TS)
. Then

(3.16)

\tilde{h}(X,Y)=h(X,Y)+\alpha C(X,Y)=\frac{h(X,Y)+h(Y,X)}{2},

where
C:\Gamma({\mathcal{H}}TS)\times\Gamma({\mathcal{H}}TS)\longrightarrow C^{\infty}(S)
, which we will refer to as
commutation tensor
, is the skew-symmetric horizontal
(2,0)
-tensor field defined by

C(X,Y)=\langle J(X),Y\rangle.

In particular

(3.17)

h(Y,X)=h(X,Y)+2\alpha C(X,Y).

Finally,

(3.18)

|h|^{2}=|\tilde{h}|^{2}+2(n-1)\alpha^{2}.

Proof.

Let
X,Y\in\Gamma({\mathcal{H}}TS)
. By definition of
h
and
\tilde{h}
we have that
\tilde{h}(X,Y)=h(X,Y)+\alpha C(X,Y).

Moreover, since
\tilde{h}
is symmetric and
C
is skew-symmetric in view of (
2.1
), then

\tilde{h}(X,Y)=\tilde{h}(Y,X)=h(Y,X)+\alpha C(Y,X)=h(Y,X)-\alpha C(X,Y),

whence (
3.16
) and (
3.17
) follow. Finally, (
3.18
) follows from
[
59
, Proposition 5.4]
.
∎

According to its Riemannian counterpart, the
horizontal mean curvature

H
is then defined by

(3.19)

H=\trace h=\trace\tilde{h}=\divv_{\mathcal{H}}\nu,

the last identity following from
[
25
]
. In the following, we say that
S
is
minimal
whenever
H\equiv 0
.

Remark 3.5
.

Although we have defined the horizontal shape operator
A
only for horizontal tangent vector fields, we emphasize that
A(\mathcal{S})
is nevertheless well-defined and admits an explicit expression by virtue of (
3.10
). Therefore, we shall use the notation
h(\mathcal{S},X)
for any given
X\in\Gamma({\mathcal{H}}TS)
.

3.4.
Eigenvectors

Fix
p\in S
. Since
\tilde{h}_{p}
is symmetric, then it is diagonalizable. Therefore, in the following, we denote by
\mathrm{F}_{1},\ldots,\mathrm{F}_{2n-1}
any local orthonormal frame of
{\mathcal{H}}TS
around
p
such that

(3.20)

\tilde{h}_{p}(\mathrm{F}_{i}|_{p},\mathrm{F}_{j}|_{p})=\lambda_{i}\delta_{i,j}

for any
i,j=1,\ldots,2n-1
, where
\lambda_{1},\ldots,\lambda_{2n-1}
are the eigenvalues of
\tilde{h}_{p}
. When
S
satisfies milder assumptions, more can be said about
\mathrm{F}_{1},\ldots,\mathrm{F}_{2n-1}
. According to
[
17
,
13
]
, in the following we adopt the notation
\ell=h(J(\nu),J(\nu))
, and we let
\mathfrak{X}=\nabla_{J(\nu)}\nu-\ell J(\nu)
.
As we know from
[
13
]
, if
p\in S
, then
\mathfrak{X}|_{p}=0
if and only if
\tilde{A}|_{p}({\mathcal{H}}^{\prime}T_{p}S)\subseteq{\mathcal{H}}^{\prime}T_{p}S
. In particular, when

(
\mathrm{P}1
)

\mathfrak{X}\equiv 0,

J(\nu)|_{p}
is an eigenvector of
\tilde{h}_{p}
for any
p\in S
. Therefore, when (

⁢
P
1

) holds and
\mathrm{F}_{1},\ldots,\mathrm{F}_{2n-1}
is as in (
3.20
), we may always assume that

(3.21)

\mathrm{F}_{1},\ldots,\mathrm{F}_{n-1},\ldots,\mathrm{F}_{n+1},\ldots,\mathrm{F}_{2n-1}\in\Gamma({\mathcal{H}}^{\prime}TS)\qquad\text{and}\qquad\mathrm{F}_{n}=J(\nu).

A relevant class of hypersurfaces which satisfy (

⁢
P
1

) is that of
horizontally umbilic hypersurfaces
introduced in
[
13
]
.
In the context of non-characteristic minimal hypersurfaces, (

⁢
P
1

) holds, e.g., for the following relevant examples.

Example 3.6

(Vertical hyperplanes)
.

Vertical hyperplanes trivially satisfy (

⁢
P
1

), as
\nabla_{J(\nu)}\nu\equiv 0
.

Example 3.7

(Catenoidal hypersurfaces,
\mathrm{I}
)
.

A non-trivial example (cf.
[
65
]
) is provided by the class of hypersurfaces of revolution around the vertical axis, say
(S_{E})_{E>0}
, whose profile is given by the vertical symmetrization of the curve
(s,t_{E}(s))
, where

t_{E}:\left[E^{\frac{1}{2n-1}},\infty\right]\longrightarrow[0,\infty],\qquad t_{E}(s)=\int_{E^{\frac{1}{2n-1}}}^{s}\frac{E\tau}{\sqrt{\tau^{4n-2}-E^{2}}}\,d\tau.

Each
S_{E}
is complete and minimal. Moreover, it is non-characteristic, as it does not intersect the vertical axis (cf.
[
65
]
). Since hypersurfaces of revolution are horizontally umbilic (cf.
[
13
]
),
S_{E}
satisfies (

⁢
P
1

). In this case, differently from
Example

3.6
, a tedious computation shows that

(3.22)

\nabla_{J(\nu)}\nu=-2E|z|^{-4}J(\nu),

where we have set
|z|^{2}=x_{1}^{2}+\cdots+x_{n}^{2}+y_{1}^{2}+\cdots+y_{n}^{2}.

On the other hand, we have the following important example in the class of minimal, characteristic hypersurfaces.

Example 3.8

(Hyperbolic paraboloids,
\mathrm{I}
)
.

Let
S\subseteq{\mathbb{H}}^{n}
be the vertical graph of the function

u(x_{1},\ldots,x_{n},,y_{1},\ldots,y_{n})=x_{1}y_{1}+x_{2}y_{2}+\cdots+x_{n}y_{n}.

S
is a complete, minimal hypersurface with
S_{0}=\{(z,t)\in{\mathbb{H}}^{n}\,:\,x_{1}=\cdots=x_{n}=0\}
. A direct computation reveals that
\nabla_{J(\nu)}\nu\equiv 0
on
S\setminus S_{0}
, whence
S
verifies (

⁢
P
1

). We stress that
S
is not horizontally umbilic, since its characteristic points are not isolated (cf.
[
13
, Proposition 4.1]
).

3.5.
Tangential Laplace-Beltrami operators

The general approach described in
Section

3.2
allows to associate to a function
f\in C^{\infty}(S)
a natural notion of tangential Laplacian, namely

(3.23)

\Delta^{{\mathcal{H}},S}f=\trace\hess^{{\mathcal{H}},S}f.

On the other hand, the authors of
[
25
]
considered a Laplace-Beltrami operator on
S
of the form

(3.24)

\sum_{i=1}^{2n}\nabla^{{\mathbb{H}},S}_{i}\nabla^{{\mathbb{H}},S}_{i}f,

where the
horizontal tangential derivatives

\nabla^{{\mathbb{H}},S}_{i}f=Z_{i}f-\langle\nabla^{\mathcal{H}}f,\nu\rangle\nu_{i}

for any
i=1,\ldots,2n
do not depend on the smooth extension of
f
(cf.
[
25
]
).
As pointed out in
[
25
]
, the operator defined in (
3.24
), differently from the Riemannian framework, is not in general self-adjoint. To this aim, the authors of
[
25
]
introduced a
modified
version of (
3.24
), the so-called
modified horizontal tangential Laplacian

(3.25)

\hat{\Delta}^{{\mathcal{H}},S}f=\sum_{i=1}^{2n}\nabla^{{\mathbb{H}},S}_{i}\nabla^{{\mathbb{H}},S}_{i}f+2\alpha\left\langle\nabla^{\mathcal{H}}f,J(\nu)\right\rangle.

The most relevant feature of
\hat{\Delta}^{{\mathcal{H}},S}
is that it is indeed self-adjoint (cf.
[
25
, Corollary 11.4]
), so that the following integration-by-parts formula holds.

Proposition 3.9
.

Let
\varphi\in C^{1}_{c}(S)
and
\psi\in C^{2}(S)
. Then

(3.26)

\int_{S}\varphi\,\hat{\Delta}^{{\mathcal{H}},S}\psi\,d\sigma_{\mathcal{H}}=-\int_{S}\langle\nabla^{{\mathcal{H}},S}\varphi,\nabla^{{\mathcal{H}},S}\psi\rangle\,d\sigma_{\mathcal{H}}

In order to exploit (
3.26
), in the next proposition we show that (
3.23
) and
(
3.24
) agree.

Proposition 3.10
.

Let
f\in C^{2}(S)
. Set
g_{\mathbb{H}}^{i,j}=\delta_{i,j}-\nu_{i}\nu_{j}
for any
i,j=1,\ldots,2n
. Then

\Delta^{{\mathcal{H}},S}f=\sum_{i=1}^{2n}\nabla^{{\mathbb{H}},S}_{i}\nabla^{{\mathbb{H}},S}_{i}f=\sum_{i,j=1}^{2n}g_{\mathbb{H}}^{i,j}Z_{i}Z_{j}f-H\langle\nabla^{\mathcal{H}}f,\nu\rangle,

Proof.

Recalling that
|\nu|=1
,

\begin{split}\sum_{i=1}^{2n}\nabla^{{\mathbb{H}},S}_{i}\nabla^{{\mathbb{H}},S}_{i}f&=\sum_{i=1}^{2n}Z_{i}Z_{i}f-\langle\nabla^{\mathcal{H}}f,\nu\rangle\sum_{i=1}^{2n}Z_{i}\nu_{i}-\sum_{i,j=1}^{2n}Z_{i}Z_{j}f\nu_{i}\nu_{j}-\sum_{i,j=1}^{2n}Z_{j}fZ_{i}\nu_{j}\nu_{i}\\
&\quad-\sum_{i}^{2n}\langle\nabla^{\mathcal{H}}Z_{i}f,\nu\rangle\nu_{i}+\sum_{i=1}^{2n}\langle\nabla^{\mathcal{H}}(\langle\nabla^{\mathcal{H}}f,\nu\rangle\nu_{i}),\nu\rangle\nu_{i}\\
&\overset{\eqref{hvarieespressioni}}{=}\sum_{i,j=1}^{2n}g_{\mathbb{H}}^{i,j}Z_{i}Z_{j}f-H\langle\nabla^{\mathcal{H}}f,\nu\rangle-\sum_{i,j=1}^{2n}Z_{j}fZ_{i}\nu_{j}\nu_{i}-\sum_{i,j=1}^{2n}Z_{j}Z_{i}f\nu_{i}\nu_{j}\\
&\quad+\sum_{i,j,k=1}^{2n}Z_{j}Z_{k}f(\nu_{i})^{2}\nu_{j}\nu_{k}+\sum_{i,j,k=1}^{2n}Z_{k}fZ_{j}\nu_{k}(\nu_{i})^{2}\nu_{j}+\sum_{i,j,k=1}^{2n}Z_{k}f\nu_{k}Z_{j}\nu_{i}\nu_{i}\nu_{j}\\
&=\sum_{i,j=1}^{2n}g_{\mathbb{H}}^{i,j}Z_{i}Z_{j}f-H\langle\nabla^{\mathcal{H}}f,\nu\rangle-\sum_{i,j=1}^{2n}Z_{j}fZ_{i}\nu_{j}\nu_{i}-\sum_{i,j=1}^{2n}Z_{j}Z_{i}f\nu_{i}\nu_{j}\\
&\quad+\sum_{j,k=1}^{2n}Z_{j}Z_{k}f\nu_{j}\nu_{k}+\sum_{j,k=1}^{2n}Z_{k}fZ_{j}\nu_{k}\nu_{j}\\
&=\sum_{i,j=1}^{2n}g_{\mathbb{H}}^{i,j}Z_{i}Z_{j}f-H\langle\nabla^{\mathcal{H}}f,\nu\rangle.\end{split}

Let now
\mathrm{E}_{1},\ldots,\mathrm{E}_{2n-1}
be a local orthonormal frame of
{\mathcal{H}}TS
. Set
a_{i}^{j}=\langle\mathrm{E}_{i},Z_{j}\rangle
for any
i=1,\ldots,2n-1
and any
j=1,\ldots,2n
.
Then

(3.27)

\sum_{k=1}^{2n}a_{i}^{k}a_{j}^{k}=\delta_{ij},\qquad\sum_{k=1}^{2n}a_{i}^{k}\nu_{k}=0\qquad\text{and}\qquad\sum_{k=1}^{2n-1}a_{k}^{l}a_{k}^{m}=g_{\mathbb{H}}^{l,m}

for any
i,j=1,\ldots,2n-1
and any
l,m=1,\ldots,2n
. We conclude that

\begin{split}\Delta^{{\mathcal{H}},S}f&=\sum_{j=1}^{2n-1}E_{j}(E_{j}f)-\sum_{j=1}^{2n-1}\nabla^{S}_{E_{j}}E_{j}f\\
&=\sum_{j=1}^{2n-1}\sum_{h,k=1}^{2n}a_{j}^{h}a_{j}^{k}Z_{h}Z_{k}f+\sum_{j=1}^{2n-1}\sum_{h,k=1}^{2n}a_{j}^{h}Z_{h}(a_{j}^{k})Z_{k}f\\
&\quad-\sum_{j=1}^{2n-1}\langle\nabla_{E_{j}}E_{j},\nabla f\rangle+\sum_{j=1}^{2n-1}\langle\nabla_{E_{j}}E_{j},\nu\rangle\langle\nabla f,\nu\rangle\\
&\overset{\eqref{coeffequations}}{=}\sum_{h,k=1}^{2n}g_{\mathbb{H}}^{h,k}Z_{h}Z_{k}f+\sum_{j=1}^{2n-1}\sum_{h,k=1}^{2n}a_{j}^{h}Z_{h}(a_{j}^{k})Z_{k}f\\
&\quad-\sum_{j=1}^{2n-1}\sum_{h,k=1}^{2n}a_{j}^{h}\langle\nabla_{Z_{h}}a_{j}^{k}Z_{k},\nabla f\rangle-H\langle\nabla f,\nu\rangle\\
&=\sum_{h,k=1}^{2n}g_{\mathbb{H}}^{h,k}Z_{h}Z_{k}f-H\langle\nabla f,\nu\rangle,\end{split}

whence the thesis follows.
∎

3.6.
The commutation tensor

We know from
Proposition

3.4
how the commutation tensor
C
intervenes in the lack of commutativity of
h
. Next we discuss how it affects the commutation of the covariant derivative of
h
. First,
C
evolves along tangent vector fields as follows.

Proposition 3.11
.

Let
X\in\Gamma(TS)
and let
Y,Z\in\Gamma({\mathcal{H}}TS)
. Then

(3.28)

\left(\nabla^{S}_{X}C\right)(Y,Z)=C(Z,\nu)h(X,Y)-C(Y,\nu)h(X,Z).

Proof.

Let
X,Y,Z
be as in the statement. Then, by (
2.4
),

\begin{split}\nabla^{S}_{X}C(Y,Z)&=X\langle J(Y),Z\rangle+\langle\nabla^{S}_{X}Y,J(Z)\rangle-\langle J(Y),\nabla^{S}_{X}Z\rangle\\
&=\langle\nabla_{X}J(Y),Z\rangle+\langle J(Y),\nabla_{X}Z\rangle+\langle\nabla_{X}Y,J(Z))-\langle J(Y),\nabla_{X}Z\rangle\\
&\quad-\langle\nabla_{X}Y,\nu\rangle\langle J(Z),\nu\rangle+\langle\nabla_{X}Z,\nu\rangle\langle J(Y),\nu\rangle\\
&=C(Z,\nu)h(X,Y)-C(Y,\nu)h(X,Z).\end{split}

∎

Thanks to
Proposition

3.4
and
Proposition

3.11
, we describe the lack of commutativity of
\nabla^{S}h
.

Proposition 3.12
.

Let
X,Y,Z\in\Gamma({\mathcal{H}}TS)
. Then

(3.29)

\nabla^{S}_{X}h(Y,Z)=\nabla^{S}_{X}h(Z,Y)+2(X\alpha)C(Z,Y)+2\alpha C(Y,\nu)h(X,Z)-2\alpha C(Z,\nu)h(X,Y).

Proof.

Fix
X,Y,Z
as in the statement. Then, by
Proposition

3.4
and
Proposition

3.11
,

\begin{split}\nabla^{S}_{X}h(Y,Z)&=X(h(Y,Z))-h(\nabla^{S}_{X}Y,Z)-h(Y,\nabla^{S}_{X}Z)\\
&=X(h(Z,Y))+2X\alpha C(Z,Y)+2\alpha X(C(Z,Y))\\
&\quad-h(Z,\nabla^{S}_{X}Y)-2\alpha C(Z,\nabla_{X}^{S}Y)-h(\nabla^{S}_{X}Z,Y)-2\alpha C(\nabla^{S}_{X}Z,Y)\\
&=\nabla^{S}_{X}h(Z,Y)+2X\alpha C(Z,Y)+2\alpha\nabla^{S}_{X}C(Z,Y)\\
&=\nabla^{S}_{X}h(Z,Y)+2X\alpha C(Z,Y)+2\alpha C(Y,\nu)h(X,Z)-2\alpha C(Z,\nu)h(X,Y).\end{split}

∎

3.7.
Gauss-Codazzi equations

With the next result we derive the sub-Riemannian counterpart of the classical Gauss-Codazzi equations. We refer to
[
67
]
for a proof, which we include anyway for the sake of completeness.

Proposition 3.13

(Gauss-Codazzi equations)
.

Let
X,Y,Z,W\in\Gamma({\mathcal{H}}TS)
. Then the
Gauss equation

(3.30)

R^{S}(X,Y,Z,W)=h(Y,Z)h(X,W)-h(X,Z)h(Y,W)

and the
Codazzi equation

(3.31)

\left(\nabla^{S}_{Y}h\right)(X,Z)=\left(\nabla^{S}_{X}h\right)(Y,Z)+2C(X,Y)h(\mathcal{S},Z)

hold.

Proof.

We know that
\nabla_{X}Y=\nabla^{S}_{X}Y-h(X,Y)\nu.

Hence

\begin{split}\nabla_{X}\nabla_{Y}Z&=\nabla_{X}\nabla_{Y}^{S}Z-\nabla_{X}(h(Y,Z)\nu)=\nabla^{S}_{X}\nabla^{S}_{Y}Z-h(X,\nabla^{S}_{Y}Z)\nu-X(h(Y,Z))\nu-h(Y,Z)A(X).\end{split}

Similarly,

-\nabla_{Y}\nabla_{X}Z=-\nabla^{S}_{Y}\nabla^{S}_{X}Z+h(Y,\nabla^{S}_{X}Z)\nu+Y(h(X,Z))\nu+h(X,Z)A(Y)

Moreover,

-\nabla_{[X,Y]}Z=-\nabla^{S}_{[X,Y]}Z+h([X,Y],Z)\nu

Summing the three equations term by term we get that

\begin{split}0&\overset{R\equiv 0}{=}R^{S}(X,Y)Z+h(X,Z)A(Y)-h(Y,Z)A(X)\\
&\quad+\left(Y(h(X,Z))-h(\nabla_{Y}^{S}X,Z)-h(X,\nabla^{S}_{Y}Z)\right)\nu\\
&\quad\left(-X(h(Y,Z))+h(\nabla^{S}_{X}Y,Z)+h(Y,\nabla^{S}_{X}Z)\right)\nu\\
&\quad+h(\nabla_{Y}^{S}X-\nabla^{S}_{X}Y+[X,Y],Z)\nu\\
&=R^{S}(X,Y)Z+h(X,Z)A(Y)-h(Y,Z)A(X)\\
&\quad+\nabla^{S}_{Y}h(X,Z)\nu-\nabla^{S}_{X}h(Y,Z)\nu-h(\tor_{\nabla^{S}}(X,Y),Z)\nu\\
&=R^{S}(X,Y)Z+h(X,Z)A(Y)-h(Y,Z)A(X)\\
&\quad+\nabla^{S}_{Y}h(X,Z)\nu-\nabla^{S}_{X}h(Y,Z)\nu-2\langle J(X),Y\rangle h(S,Z)\nu.\end{split}

The thesis follows projecting the previous identity either on
W
or on
\nu
.
∎

3.8.
Variation formulas

Let
S\subseteq\mathbb{H}^{n}
be a smooth, embedded, non-characteristic hypersurface without boundary. Assume in addition that
S
is two-sided. Let
\Omega\subseteq{\mathbb{H}}^{n}
be an open bounded set such that
\Omega\cap S\neq\emptyset
, and let
\xi\in C^{1}_{c}(\Omega)
. Then it is known (cf.
[
53
,
22
,
67
,
72
]
)
that

(3.33)

\frac{d}{dt}\sigma_{{\mathcal{H}},t}(\Omega)\Big|_{t=0}=\int_{S}H\xi\,d\sigma_{\mathcal{H}}

and

(3.34)

\frac{d^{2}}{dt^{2}}\sigma_{{\mathcal{H}},t}(\Omega)\Big|_{t=0}=\int_{S}\left(|\nabla^{{\mathcal{H}},S}\xi|^{2}-\xi^{2}\left(q-H^{2}\right)\right)\,d\sigma_{\mathcal{H}},

where

(3.35)

q=\sum_{h,k=1}^{2n}Z_{h}(\nu_{k})Z_{k}(\nu_{h})+4\langle\nabla\alpha,J(\nu)\rangle+4n\alpha^{2}

and where by
\sigma_{{\mathcal{H}},t}
we denote the horizontal surface measure associated with the smooth variation
E_{t}
along the vector field
\xi\nu
.
Observe that
q
does not depend on the chosen unitary extension of
\nu
(cf.
[
59
]
). Moreover, in view of
[
59
, Proposition 5.1]
and (
3.18
),

(3.36)

\begin{split}q&=|h|^{2}+4\langle\nabla\alpha,J(\nu)\rangle+4\alpha^{2}=|\tilde{h}|^{2}+4\langle\nabla\alpha,J(\nu)\rangle+2(n+1)\alpha^{2}.\end{split}

As customary (cf. e.g.
[
40
]
) we say that
S
is
area stationary
whenever the quantity in (
3.33
) vanishes for any
\Omega
and
\xi
as above, and that
S
is
stable
if it is area stationary and the quantity in (
3.34
) is non-negative for any
\Omega
and
\xi
as above. Notice that
S
is minimal if and only if it is area stationary. In particular, when
S
is stable, the
stability inequality

(3.37)

\int_{S}q\xi^{2}\,d\sigma_{\mathcal{H}}\leqslant\int_{S}|\nabla^{{\mathcal{H}},S}\xi|^{2}\,d\sigma_{\mathcal{H}}

holds for any
\xi\in C^{1}_{c}(S)
.

Proposition 4.1

To prove (

), notice that

\tilde{h}(\mathrm{F}_{j},\pi(J(\mathrm{F}_{j})))=\sum_{k=1}^{2n-1}\langle\pi(J(\mathrm{F}_{j})),\mathrm{F}_{k}\rangle\tilde{h}(\mathrm{F}_{j},\mathrm{F}_{k})=\langle J(\mathrm{F}_{j}),\mathrm{F}_{j}\rangle\tilde{h}(\mathrm{F}_{j},\mathrm{F}_{j})=0,

\begin{split}\tilde{h}\left(\nabla^{S}_{X}\mathrm{F}_{i},\mathrm{F}_{i}\right)=\sum_{k=1}^{2n-1}\left\langle\nabla^{S}_{X}\mathrm{F}_{i},\mathrm{F}_{k}\right\rangle\tilde{h}\left(\mathrm{F}_{k},\mathrm{F}_{i}\right)=\lambda_{i}\left\langle\nabla^{S}_{X}\mathrm{F}_{i},\mathrm{F}_{i}\right\rangle=0,\end{split}

whence (
4.3
) follows. Moreover, (
4.4
) follows since

\begin{split}\sum_{i=1}^{2n-1}Y\Big(\tilde{h}\Big(\nabla^{S}_{X}&\mathrm{F}_{i},\mathrm{F}_{i}\Big)\Big)(p)=\sum_{i,k=1}^{2n-1}Y\left(\left\langle\nabla^{S}_{X}\mathrm{F}_{i},\mathrm{F}_{k}\right\rangle\tilde{h}\left(\mathrm{F}_{k},\mathrm{F}_{i}\right)\right)(p)\\
&=\sum_{i,k=1}^{2n-1}\tilde{h}_{p}(\mathrm{F}_{i},\mathrm{F}_{k})Y\left(\left\langle\nabla^{S}_{X}\mathrm{F}_{i},\mathrm{F}_{k}\right\rangle\right)(p)+\sum_{i,k=1}^{2n-1}\left\langle\nabla^{S}_{X}\mathrm{F}_{i},\mathrm{F}_{k}\right\rangle Y(\tilde{h}(\mathrm{F}_{i},\mathrm{F}_{k}))(p)\\

&=0,\end{split}

the semi-last equality following by the symmetry of
\tilde{h}
. Finally,

\begin{split}\sum_{i=1}^{2n-1}\Big(\tilde{h}(\nabla^{S}_{X}&\nabla^{S}_{Y}\mathrm{F}_{i},\mathrm{F}_{i})+\tilde{h}(\nabla^{S}_{Y}\mathrm{F}_{i},\nabla^{S}_{X}\mathrm{F}_{i})\Big)\\
&=\sum_{i,j=1}^{2n-1}\langle\nabla^{S}_{X}\nabla^{S}_{Y}\mathrm{F}_{i},\mathrm{F}_{j}\rangle\tilde{h}(\mathrm{F}_{i},\mathrm{F}_{j})+\sum_{i,j,k=1}^{2n-1}\langle\nabla^{S}_{Y}\mathrm{F}_{i},\mathrm{F}_{j}\rangle\langle\nabla^{S}_{X}\mathrm{F}_{i},\mathrm{F}_{k}\rangle\tilde{h}(\mathrm{F}_{j},\mathrm{F}_{k})\\
&\overset{\eqref{diagontildeh}}{=}\sum_{i=1}^{2n-1}\lambda_{i}\langle\nabla^{S}_{X}\nabla^{S}_{Y}\mathrm{F}_{i},\mathrm{F}_{i}\rangle+\sum_{i,j=1}^{2n-1}\lambda_{j}\langle\nabla^{S}_{Y}\mathrm{F}_{i},\mathrm{F}_{j}\rangle\langle\nabla^{S}_{X}\mathrm{F}_{i},\mathrm{F}_{j}\rangle\\
&\overset{\eqref{metricequationnablas}}{=}-\sum_{i=1}^{2n-1}\lambda_{i}\langle\nabla^{S}_{Y}\mathrm{F}_{i},\nabla^{S}_{X}\mathrm{F}_{i}\rangle+\sum_{i,j=1}^{2n-1}\lambda_{j}\langle\nabla^{S}_{Y}\mathrm{F}_{i},\mathrm{F}_{j}\rangle\langle\nabla^{S}_{X}\mathrm{F}_{i},\mathrm{F}_{j}\rangle\\

From one hand, exchanging the indices in the previous equation, we get that

From the other hand, recalling (

Therefore, combining (
4.6
) and (

4.5
) follows.

&\overset{\eqref{comesetildehmetrica}}{=}\sum_{j=1}^{2n-1}\tilde{h}(\mathrm{F}_{j},\mathrm{F}_{j})\Delta^{{\mathcal{H}},S}(\tilde{h}(\mathrm{F}_{j},\mathrm{F}_{j}))-4\sum_{i,j=1}^{2n-1}\tilde{h}(\mathrm{F}_{j},\mathrm{F}_{j})\mathrm{F}_{i}\tilde{h}(\nabla^{S}_{\mathrm{F}_{i}}\mathrm{F}_{j},\mathrm{F}_{j})\\
&\quad+2\sum_{i,j=1}^{2n-1}\tilde{h}(\mathrm{F}_{j},\mathrm{F}_{j})\tilde{h}(\nabla^{S}_{\mathrm{F}_{i}}\nabla^{S}_{\mathrm{F}_{i}}\mathrm{F}_{j},\mathrm{F}_{j})+2\sum_{i,j=1}^{2n-1}\tilde{h}(\mathrm{F}_{j},\mathrm{F}_{j})\tilde{h}(\nabla^{S}_{\mathrm{F}_{i}}\mathrm{F}_{j},\nabla^{S}_{\mathrm{F}_{i}}\mathrm{F}_{j}).\end{split}

Notice that

\begin{split}-4\sum_{i,j=1}^{2n-1}&\tilde{h}(\mathrm{F}_{j},\mathrm{F}_{j})\mathrm{F}_{i}\tilde{h}(\nabla^{S}_{\mathrm{F}_{i}}\mathrm{F}_{j},\mathrm{F}_{j})=-4\sum_{i,j,k=1}^{2n-1}\tilde{h}(\mathrm{F}_{j},\mathrm{F}_{j})\mathrm{F}_{i}\left(\langle\nabla^{S}_{\mathrm{F}_{i}}\mathrm{F}_{j},\mathrm{F}_{k}\rangle\tilde{h}(\mathrm{F}_{k},\mathrm{F}_{j})\right)\\
&\overset{\eqref{diagontildeh}}{=}-4\sum_{i,j=1}^{2n-1}\tilde{h}(\mathrm{F}_{j},\mathrm{F}_{j})^{2}\mathrm{F}_{i}\left(\langle\nabla^{S}_{\mathrm{F}_{i}}\mathrm{F}_{j},\mathrm{F}_{j}\rangle\right)-4\sum_{i,j,k=1}^{2n-1}\tilde{h}(\mathrm{F}_{j},\mathrm{F}_{j})\mathrm{F}_{i}\tilde{h}(\mathrm{F}_{k},\mathrm{F}_{j})\langle\nabla^{S}_{\mathrm{F}_{i}}\mathrm{F}_{j},\mathrm{F}_{k}\rangle\\
&\overset{\eqref{metricequationnablas}}{=}-4\sum_{i,j,k=1}^{2n-1}\tilde{h}(\mathrm{F}_{j},\mathrm{F}_{j})\mathrm{F}_{i}\tilde{h}(\mathrm{F}_{j},\mathrm{F}_{k})\langle\nabla^{S}_{\mathrm{F}_{i}}\mathrm{F}_{j},\mathrm{F}_{k}\rangle.\end{split}

Moreover,

\begin{split}2\sum_{i,j=1}^{2n-1}\tilde{h}(\mathrm{F}_{j},\mathrm{F}_{j})\tilde{h}(\nabla^{S}_{\mathrm{F}_{i}}\nabla^{S}_{\mathrm{F}_{i}}\mathrm{F}_{j},\mathrm{F}_{j})&\overset{\eqref{diagontildeh}}{=}2\sum_{i,j=1}^{2n-1}\tilde{h}(\mathrm{F}_{j},\mathrm{F}_{j})^{2}\langle\nabla^{S}_{\mathrm{F}_{i}}\nabla^{S}_{\mathrm{F}_{i}}\mathrm{F}_{j},\mathrm{F}_{j}\rangle\\
&\overset{\eqref{metricequationnablas}}{=}-2\sum_{i,j=1}^{2n-1}\tilde{h}(\mathrm{F}_{j},\mathrm{F}_{j})^{2}\langle\nabla^{S}_{\mathrm{F}_{i}}\mathrm{F}_{j},\nabla^{S}_{\mathrm{F}_{i}}\mathrm{F}_{j}\rangle\\
&=-2\sum_{i,j,k=1}^{2n-1}\tilde{h}(\mathrm{F}_{j},\mathrm{F}_{j})^{2}\langle\nabla^{S}_{\mathrm{F}_{i}}\mathrm{F}_{j},\mathrm{F}_{k}\rangle^{2}\\
&\overset{\eqref{metricequationnablas}}{=}-2\sum_{i,j,k=1}^{2n-1}\tilde{h}(\mathrm{F}_{k},\mathrm{F}_{k})^{2}\langle\nabla^{S}_{\mathrm{F}_{i}}\mathrm{F}_{j},\mathrm{F}_{k}\rangle^{2}.\\
\end{split}

Finally,

\begin{split}2\sum_{i,j=1}^{2n-1}\tilde{h}(\mathrm{F}_{j},\mathrm{F}_{j})\tilde{h}(\nabla^{S}_{\mathrm{F}_{i}}\mathrm{F}_{j},\nabla^{S}_{\mathrm{F}_{i}}\mathrm{F}_{j})&=2\sum_{i,j,k=1}^{2n-1}\tilde{h}(\mathrm{F}_{j},\mathrm{F}_{j})\tilde{h}(\mathrm{F}_{k},\mathrm{F}_{k})\langle\nabla^{S}_{\mathrm{F}_{i}}\mathrm{F}_{j},\mathrm{F}_{k}\rangle^{2}.\end{split}

Therefore we infer that

(4.12)

|\nabla^{{\mathcal{H}},S}\tilde{h}|^{2}+\sum_{j=1}^{2n-1}\tilde{h}(\mathrm{F}_{j},\mathrm{F}_{j})\left(\Delta^{{\mathcal{H}},S}\tilde{h}\right)(\mathrm{F}_{j},\mathrm{F}_{j})=\sum_{i,j,k=1}^{2n-1}\left(\mathrm{F}_{i}\tilde{h}(\mathrm{F}_{j},\mathrm{F}_{k})\right)^{2}+\sum_{j=1}^{2n-1}\tilde{h}(\mathrm{F}_{j},\mathrm{F}_{j})\Delta^{{\mathcal{H}},S}(\tilde{h}(\mathrm{F}_{j},\mathrm{F}_{j})).

We conclude noticing that

\begin{split}\frac{1}{2}\Delta^{{\mathcal{H}},S}|\tilde{h}|^{2}&=\frac{1}{2}\sum_{i,j,k=1}^{2n-1}\nabla^{S}_{\mathrm{F}_{i}}\nabla^{S}_{\mathrm{F}_{i}}\left(\tilde{h}(\mathrm{F}_{j},\mathrm{F}_{k})^{2}\right)\\
&=\sum_{i,j,k=1}^{2n-1}\nabla^{S}_{\mathrm{F}_{i}}\left(\tilde{h}(\mathrm{F}_{j},\mathrm{F}_{k})\mathrm{F}_{i}\tilde{h}(\mathrm{F}_{j},\mathrm{F}_{k})\right)\\
&=\sum_{i,j,k=1}^{2n-1}\left(\mathrm{F}_{i}\tilde{h}(\mathrm{F}_{j},\mathrm{F}_{k})\right)^{2}+\sum_{j=1}^{2n-1}\tilde{h}(\mathrm{F}_{j},\mathrm{F}_{j})\Delta^{{\mathcal{H}},S}(\tilde{h}(\mathrm{F}_{j},\mathrm{F}_{j}))\\
&\overset{\eqref{moltoutilepersimonsmoduloeq}}{=}|\nabla^{{\mathcal{H}},S}\tilde{h}|^{2}+\sum_{j=1}^{2n-1}\tilde{h}(\mathrm{F}_{j},\mathrm{F}_{j})\left(\Delta^{{\mathcal{H}},S}\tilde{h}\right)(\mathrm{F}_{j},\mathrm{F}_{j}).\end{split}

∎

Proposition 4.4
.

Let
\mathrm{E}_{1},\ldots,\mathrm{E}_{2n-1}
be any local orthonormal frame of
{\mathcal{H}}TS
. It holds that

(4.13)

\langle\nabla^{S}|\tilde{h}|^{2},J(\nu)\rangle=4\alpha\left|\nabla_{J(\nu)}\nu\right|^{2}-4\alpha|\tilde{h}|^{2}+2\sum_{j,k=1}^{2n-1}\tilde{h}(\mathrm{E}_{j},\mathrm{E}_{k}),\langle\nabla_{\mathrm{E}_{j}}\nabla_{J(\nu)}\nu,\mathrm{E}_{k}\rangle.

Proof.

Take
\mathrm{F}_{1},\ldots,\mathrm{F}_{2n-1}
as in (
3.20
). Then

\begin{split}\langle\nabla^{S}|\tilde{h}|^{2},J(\nu)\rangle&=2\sum_{j,k=1}^{2n-1}\tilde{h}(\mathrm{F}_{j},\mathrm{F}_{k})J(\nu)\left(\tilde{h}(\mathrm{F}_{j},\mathrm{F}_{k})\right)\\
&\overset{\eqref{diagontildeh}}{=}2\sum_{j=1}^{2n-1}\tilde{h}(\mathrm{F}_{j},\mathrm{F}_{j})J(\nu)\left(\tilde{h}(\mathrm{F}_{j},\mathrm{F}_{j})\right)\\
&=2\sum_{j=1}^{2n-1}\tilde{h}(\mathrm{F}_{j},\mathrm{F}_{j})\langle\nabla_{J(\nu)}\nabla_{\mathrm{F}_{j}}\nu,\mathrm{F}_{j}\rangle+2\sum_{j=1}^{2n-1}\tilde{h}(\mathrm{F}_{j},\mathrm{F}_{j})\langle\nabla_{\mathrm{F}_{j}}\nu,\nabla_{J(\nu)}\mathrm{F}_{j}\rangle\\
&\overset{R\equiv 0,\,\eqref{pseudotorsion}}{=}2\sum_{j=1}^{2n-1}\tilde{h}(\mathrm{F}_{j},\mathrm{F}_{j})\langle\nabla_{\mathrm{F}_{j}}\nabla_{J(\nu)}\nu,\mathrm{F}_{j}\rangle+2\sum_{j=1}^{2n-1}\tilde{h}(\mathrm{F}_{j},\mathrm{F}_{j})\langle\nabla_{[J(\nu),\mathrm{F}_{j}]}\nu,\mathrm{F}_{j}\rangle\\
&\quad+2\sum_{j=1}^{2n-1}\tilde{h}(\mathrm{F}_{j},\mathrm{F}_{j})\langle\nabla_{\mathrm{F}_{j}}\nu,\nabla_{\mathrm{F}_{j}}J(\nu)\rangle+2\sum_{j=1}^{2n-1}\tilde{h}(\mathrm{F}_{j},\mathrm{F}_{j})\langle\nabla_{\mathrm{F}_{j}}\nu,[J(\nu),\mathrm{F}_{j}]\rangle\\
&\overset{\eqref{jcommutaeq},\eqref{inhsiiiiiiiii}}{=}2\sum_{j,k=1}^{2n-1}\tilde{h}(\mathrm{F}_{j},\mathrm{F}_{k})\langle\nabla_{\mathrm{F}_{j}}\nabla_{J(\nu)}\nu,\mathrm{F}_{k}\rangle+4\sum_{j=1}^{2n-1}\tilde{h}(\mathrm{F}_{j},\mathrm{F}_{j})\tilde{h}([J(\nu),\mathrm{F}_{j}],\mathrm{F}_{j}).\end{split}

We conclude noticing that

\begin{split}4\sum_{j=1}^{2n-1}\tilde{h}(\mathrm{F}_{j},\mathrm{F}_{j})\tilde{h}([J(\nu),\mathrm{F}_{j}],\mathrm{F}_{j})&\overset{\eqref{diagontildeh}}{=}4\sum_{j=1}^{2n-1}\tilde{h}(\mathrm{F}_{j},\mathrm{F}_{j})^{2}\langle[J(\nu),\mathrm{F}_{j}],\mathrm{F}_{j}\rangle\\
&\overset{\eqref{commutatoreesecondaforma}}{=}4\sum_{j=1}^{2n-1}\tilde{h}(\mathrm{F}_{j},\mathrm{F}_{j})^{2}h(\mathrm{F}_{j},\pi(J(\mathrm{F}_{j})))\\
&\overset{\eqref{hnonsimejjej}}{=}-4\alpha\sum_{j=1}^{2n-1}\tilde{h}(\mathrm{F}_{j},\mathrm{F}_{j})^{2}+4\alpha\sum_{j=1}^{2n-1}\langle J(\nu),\mathrm{F}_{j}\rangle^{2}\tilde{h}(\mathrm{F}_{j},\mathrm{F}_{j})^{2}\\
&=-4\alpha|\tilde{h}|^{2}+4\alpha\left|\nabla_{J(\nu)}\nu\right|^{2}.\end{split}

∎

5.
Simons formulas and Kato inequalities

5.1.
The full Simons identity

This section constitutes the core of the paper. Namely, we establish Simons formulas and Kato inequalities for minimal hypersurfaces in
{\mathbb{H}}^{n}
.
First, we provide the proof
Theorem

1.1
, establishing a full Simons identity for the
(2,0)
-horizontal tensor field
\Delta^{{\mathcal{H}},S}h
associated with a minimal hypersurface and generalizing its Celebrated Riemannian counterpart (cf.
[
71
]
) to the sub-Riemannian setting. Namely, we show that

(5.1)

\begin{split}\Delta^{{\mathcal{H}},S}h(X,Y)&=-qh(X,Y)+8\alpha^{2}h(X,Y)\\
&\quad+4\hess^{{\mathcal{H}},S}\alpha(\pi(J(X)),Y)+4\hess^{{\mathcal{H}},S}\alpha(X,\pi(J(Y)))\\
&\quad+\Big(16\alpha\pi(J(X))\alpha-8\alpha^{2}h(X,J(\nu))+4\left(\nabla_{X}\nu\right)\alpha\Big)\langle Y,J(\nu)\rangle\\
&\quad-2X\alpha h(Y,J(\nu))-2Y\alpha h(X,J(\nu))\\
&\quad+2\alpha h(Y,\nabla_{\pi(J(X))}\nu)-2\alpha\langle\nabla_{X}\nabla_{J(\nu)}\nu,Y\rangle-4\alpha^{2}h(\pi(J(X)),\pi(J(Y)))\\
&\quad+2\alpha\left\langle J\left(\nabla_{X}\nu\right),\nabla_{Y}\nu\right\rangle\end{split}

for any
X,Y\in\Gamma({\mathcal{H}}TS)
.

Proof of
Theorem

1.1
.

Let
\mathrm{E}_{1},\ldots,\mathrm{E}_{2n-1}
be a local orthonormal frame of
{\mathcal{H}}TS
. Let
h_{\mathcal{S}}
and
C_{\nu}
be the
(1,0)
-tensor fields defined by
h_{\mathcal{S}}(Z)=h(\mathcal{S},Z)
and
C_{\nu}(Z)=C(Z,\nu)

for any
Z\in\Gamma({\mathcal{H}}TS)
. Then

\begin{split}\Delta^{{\mathcal{H}},S}&=\sum_{i=1}^{2n-1}\nabla^{S}_{\mathrm{E}_{i}}(\nabla^{S}_{\mathrm{E}_{i}}h(X,Y))\\
&\overset{\eqref{codazzipersimons}}{=}\sum_{i=1}^{2n-1}\nabla^{S}_{\mathrm{E}_{i}}(\nabla^{S}_{X}h(\mathrm{E}_{i},Y)+2C(X,\mathrm{E}_{i})h_{\mathcal{S}}(Y))\\
&=\sum_{i=1}^{2n-1}\hess^{{\mathcal{H}},S}h(\mathrm{E}_{i},X,\mathrm{E}_{i},Y)+\underbrace{\sum_{i=1}^{2n-1}\nabla^{S}_{\mathrm{E}_{i}}(2C(X,\mathrm{E}_{i})h_{\mathcal{S}}(Y))}_{\mathrm{I}}\\
&\overset{\eqref{commutatensoreconriemannpersimons}}{=}\sum_{i=1}^{2n-1}\hess^{{\mathcal{H}},S}h(X,\mathrm{E}_{i},\mathrm{E}_{i},Y)+\underbrace{\sum_{i=1}^{2n-1}h(R^{S}(X,\mathrm{E}_{i})\mathrm{E}_{i},Y)+\sum_{i=1}^{2n-1}h(\mathrm{E}_{i},R^{S}(X,\mathrm{E}_{i})Y)}_{\mathrm{II}}\\
&\quad+\underbrace{\sum_{i=1}^{2n-1}2C(X,\mathrm{E}_{i})\nabla^{S}_{\mathcal{S}}h(\mathrm{E}_{i},Y)}_{\mathrm{III}}+\,\mathrm{I}\\
&=\sum_{i=1}^{2n-1}\nabla^{S}_{X}(\nabla^{S}_{\mathrm{E}_{i}}h(\mathrm{E}_{i},Y))+\mathrm{I}+\mathrm{II}+\mathrm{III}\\
&\overset{\eqref{simmetryandnabla}}{=}\sum_{i=1}^{2n-1}\nabla^{S}_{X}(\nabla^{S}_{\mathrm{E}_{i}}h(Y,\mathrm{E}_{i}))+\underbrace{\sum_{i=1}^{2n-1}\nabla^{S}_{X}(2\mathrm{E}_{i}\alpha C(Y,\mathrm{E}_{i}))}_{\mathrm{IV}}+\underbrace{\sum_{i=1}^{2n-1}\nabla^{S}_{X}(2\alpha C_{\nu}(\mathrm{E}_{i})h(\mathrm{E}_{i},Y))}_{\mathrm{V}}\\
&\quad\underbrace{-\sum_{i=1}^{2n-1}\nabla^{S}_{X}(2\alpha C_{\nu}(Y)h(\mathrm{E}_{i},\mathrm{E}_{i}))}_{\mathrm{VI}}+\mathrm{I}+\mathrm{II}+\mathrm{III}\\
&\overset{\eqref{codazzipersimons}}{=}\underbrace{\sum_{i=1}^{2n-1}\nabla^{S}_{X}(\nabla^{S}_{Y}h(\mathrm{E}_{i},\mathrm{E}_{i}))}_{\mathrm{VII}}+\underbrace{\sum_{i=1}^{2n-1}\nabla^{S}_{X}(2C(Y,\mathrm{E}_{i})h_{\mathcal{S}}(\mathrm{E}_{i}))}_{\mathrm{VIII}}+\mathrm{I}+\mathrm{II}+\mathrm{III}+\mathrm{IV}+\mathrm{V}+\mathrm{VI}\\
&=\mathrm{I}+\mathrm{II}+\mathrm{III}+\mathrm{IV}+\mathrm{V}+\mathrm{VI}+\mathrm{VII}+\mathrm{VIII}.\end{split}

We compute
\mathrm{I},\ldots,\mathrm{VIII}
. Let
\mathrm{G}_{1},\ldots,\mathrm{G}_{2n-1}
be a local orthonormal frame of
{\mathcal{H}}TS
such that

(5.2)

\mathrm{G}_{n}=J(\nu)\qquad\text{and}\qquad J(\mathrm{G}_{1})=\mathrm{G}_{n+1},\ldots,J(\mathrm{G}_{n-1})=\mathrm{G}_{2n-1}.

Computation of
\mathrm{I}
.

\begin{split}\mathrm{I}&=\sum_{i=1}^{2n-1}\nabla^{S}_{\mathrm{G}_{i}}(2C(X,\mathrm{G}_{i})h_{\mathcal{S}}(Y))\\
&\overset{\eqref{leibnizpertensori}}{=}\sum_{i=1}^{2n-1}2h(\mathcal{S},Y)\nabla^{S}_{\mathrm{G}_{i}}C(X,\mathrm{G}_{i})+\sum_{i=1}^{2n-1}2C(X,\mathrm{G}_{i})\nabla^{S}_{\mathrm{G}_{i}}h_{\mathcal{S}}(Y)\\
&\overset{\eqref{commutatortensorpersimons},\eqref{proiezionepersimons}}{=}\sum_{i=1}^{2n-1}2h(\mathcal{S},Y)C(\mathrm{G}_{i},\nu)h(\mathrm{G}_{i},X)-\sum_{i=1}^{2n-1}2h(\mathcal{S},Y)C(X,\nu)h(\mathrm{G}_{i},\mathrm{G}_{i})+2\nabla^{S}_{\pi(J(X))}h_{\mathcal{S}}(Y)\\
&\overset{H\equiv 0}{=}\sum_{i=1}^{2n-1}2h(\mathcal{S},Y)C(\mathrm{G}_{i},\nu)h(\mathrm{G}_{i},X)+2\nabla^{S}_{\pi(J(X))}h_{\mathcal{S}}(Y)\\
&\overset{\eqref{baseadattatapersimons}}{=}-2h(\mathcal{S},Y)h(J(\nu),X)+2\nabla^{S}_{\pi(J(X))}h_{\mathcal{S}}(Y).\end{split}

Notice that

\begin{split}-2h(\mathcal{S},Y)h(J(\nu),X)\overset{\eqref{comecommutah}}{=}-2h(\mathcal{S},Y)h(X,J(\nu))\overset{\eqref{accaesse}}{=}-2Y\alpha h(X,J(\nu))-4\alpha^{2}h(X,J(\nu))\langle Y,J(\nu)\rangle.\end{split}

On the other hand,

\begin{split}2\nabla^{S}_{\pi(J(X))}h_{\mathcal{S}}(Y)&=2\pi(J(X))(h(\mathcal{S},Y))-2h(\mathcal{S},\nabla^{S}_{\pi(J(X))}Y)\\
&\overset{\eqref{accaesse}}{=}2\pi(J(X))(Y\alpha+2\alpha^{2}\langle Y,J(\nu)\rangle-2\nabla^{S}_{\pi(J(X))}Y\alpha-4\alpha^{2}\langle\nabla^{S}_{\pi(J(X))}Y,J(\nu)\rangle\\
&=2\hess^{{\mathcal{H}},S}\alpha(\pi(J(X)),Y)+8\alpha\pi(J(X))\alpha\langle Y,J(\nu)\rangle\\
&\quad+4\alpha^{2}\pi(J(X))\langle Y,J(\nu)\rangle-4\alpha^{2}\langle\nabla_{\pi(J(X))}Y,J(\nu)\rangle\\
&=2\hess^{{\mathcal{H}},S}\alpha(\pi(J(X)),Y)+8\alpha\pi(J(X))\alpha\langle Y,J(\nu)\rangle+4\alpha^{2}\langle Y,\nabla_{\pi(J(X))}J(\nu)\rangle\\
&\overset{\eqref{jcommutaeq}}{=}2\hess^{{\mathcal{H}},S}\alpha(\pi(J(X)),Y)+8\alpha\pi(J(X))\alpha\langle Y,J(\nu)\rangle-4\alpha^{2}\left\langle J(Y),\nabla_{\pi(J(X))}\nu\right\rangle\\
&=2\hess^{{\mathcal{H}},S}\alpha(\pi(J(X)),Y)+8\alpha\pi(J(X))\alpha\langle Y,J(\nu)\rangle-4\alpha^{2}h(\pi(J(X)),\pi(J(Y))).\end{split}

In conclusion,

\begin{split}\mathrm{I}&=2\hess^{{\mathcal{H}},S}\alpha(\pi(J(X)),Y)+8\alpha\pi(J(X))\alpha\langle Y,J(\nu)\rangle-4\alpha^{2}h(\pi(J(X)),\pi(J(Y)))\\
&\quad-2Y\alpha h(X,J(\nu))-4\alpha^{2}h(X,J(\nu))\langle Y,J(\nu)\rangle.\end{split}

Computation of
\mathrm{VI}
.

\begin{split}\mathrm{VI}&=-\sum_{i=1}^{2n-1}\nabla^{S}_{X}(2\alpha C_{\nu}(Y)h(\mathrm{E}_{i},\mathrm{E}_{i}))\\
&=-\sum_{i=1}^{2n-1}\nabla_{X}^{S}(2\alpha C_{\nu}(Y))h(\mathrm{E}_{i},\mathrm{E}_{i})-2\alpha C(Y,\nu)\sum_{i=1}^{2n-1}\nabla^{S}_{X}h(\mathrm{E}_{i},\mathrm{E}_{i})\\
&=-\nabla^{S}_{X}(2\alpha C_{\nu}(Y))H-2\alpha C(Y,\nu)\trace\nabla^{S}_{X}h(\cdot,\cdot)\\
&\overset{\eqref{nablahtrace}}{=}-\nabla_{X}^{S}(2\alpha C_{\nu}(Y))H-2\alpha C(Y,\nu)XH\\
&\overset{H\equiv 0}{=}0.\end{split}

Computation of
\mathrm{VII}
.

Thanks to
Proposition

4.2
, we infer that

\mathrm{VII}=\trace\hess^{{\mathcal{H}},S}h(X,Y,\cdot,\cdot)=\hess^{{\mathcal{H}},S}H(X,Y)\overset{H\equiv 0}{=}0.

Computation of
\mathrm{VIII}
.

\begin{split}\mathrm{VIII}&=\trace\nabla^{S}_{X}(2C(Y,\cdot)h_{\mathcal{S}}(\cdot))\\
&=\sum_{i=1}^{2n-1}\nabla^{S}_{X}(2C(Y,\mathrm{G}_{i})h_{\mathcal{S}}(\mathrm{G}_{i}))\\
&\overset{\eqref{leibnizpertensori}}{=}\sum_{i=1}^{2n-1}2h(\mathcal{S},\mathrm{G}_{i})\nabla^{S}_{X}C(Y,\mathrm{G}_{i})+\sum_{i=1}^{2n-1}2C(Y,\mathrm{G}_{i})\nabla^{S}_{X}h_{\mathcal{S}}(\mathrm{G}_{i})\\
&\overset{\eqref{commutatortensorpersimons}}{=}\sum_{i=1}^{2n-1}2h(\mathcal{S},\mathrm{G}_{i})C(\mathrm{G}_{i},\nu)h(X,Y)-\sum_{i=1}^{2n-1}2h(\mathcal{S},\mathrm{G}_{i})C(Y,\nu)h(X,\mathrm{G}_{i})+\sum_{i=1}^{2n-1}2C(Y,\mathrm{G}_{i})\nabla^{S}_{X}h_{\mathcal{S}}(\mathrm{G}_{i})\\
&\overset{\eqref{baseadattatapersimons},\eqref{proiezionepersimons}}{=}-2h(\mathcal{S},J(\nu))h(X,Y)-2h(\mathcal{S},\nabla_{X}\nu)C(Y,\nu)+2\nabla^{S}_{X}h_{\mathcal{S}}(\pi(J(Y))).\\
\end{split}

Notice that

-2h(\mathcal{S},J(\nu))h(X,Y)\overset{\eqref{accaesse}}{=}\left(-2\langle\nabla\alpha,J(\nu)\rangle-4\alpha^{2}\right)h(X,Y).

On the other hand,

-2h(\mathcal{S},\nabla_{X}\nu)C(Y,\nu)=2\left(\nabla_{X}\nu\right)\alpha\langle Y,J(\nu)\rangle+4\alpha^{2}h(X,J(\nu))\langle Y,J(\nu)\rangle.

Finally,

\begin{split}2\nabla^{S}_{X}h_{\mathcal{S}}(\pi(J(Y)))&=2X\left(h(\mathcal{S},\pi(J(Y)))\right)-2h(\mathcal{S},\nabla^{S}_{X}\pi(J(Y)))\\
&\overset{\eqref{accaesse},\eqref{pijeixinhprmo}}{=}2\hess^{{\mathcal{H}},S}\alpha(X,\pi(J(Y)))+4\alpha^{2}\langle\pi(J(Y)),\nabla_{X}J(\nu)\rangle\\
&=2\hess^{{\mathcal{H}},S}\alpha(X,\pi(J(Y)))-4\alpha^{2}h(X,J(\pi(J(Y))))\\
&\overset{\eqref{pijeixinhprmo}}{=}2\hess^{{\mathcal{H}},S}\alpha(X,\pi(J(Y)))+4\alpha^{2}h(X,Y)-4\alpha^{2}h(X,J(\nu))\langle Y,J(\nu)\rangle.\end{split}

Putting the previous equations together, we conclude that

\mathrm{VIII}=-2\langle\nabla\alpha,J(\nu)\rangle h(X,Y)+2\left(\nabla_{X}\nu\right)\alpha\langle Y,J(\nu)\rangle+2\hess^{{\mathcal{H}},S}\alpha(X,\pi(J(Y))).

Computation of
\mathrm{II}
.

\begin{split}\mathrm{II}&=\sum_{i,j=1}^{2n-1}R^{S}(X,\mathrm{G}_{i},\mathrm{G}_{i},\mathrm{G}_{j})h(\mathrm{G}_{j},Y)+\sum_{i,j=1}^{2n-1}R^{S}(X,\mathrm{G}_{i},Y,\mathrm{G}_{j})h(\mathrm{G}_{i},\mathrm{G}_{j})\\
&\overset{\eqref{gaussssssub}}{=}\sum_{i,j=1}^{2n-1}h(\mathrm{G}_{i},\mathrm{G}_{i})h(X,\mathrm{G}_{j})h(\mathrm{G}_{j},Y)-\sum_{i,j=1}^{2n-1}h(X,\mathrm{G}_{i})h(\mathrm{G}_{i},\mathrm{G}_{j})h(\mathrm{G}_{j},Y)\\
&\quad+\sum_{i,j=1}^{2n-1}h(\mathrm{G}_{i},Y)h(X,\mathrm{G}_{j})h(\mathrm{G}_{i},\mathrm{G}_{j})-\sum_{i,j=1}^{2n-1}h(X,Y)h(\mathrm{G}_{i},\mathrm{G}_{j})^{2}\\
&\overset{H\equiv 0}{=}\sum_{i,j=1}^{2n-1}h(\mathrm{G}_{i},Y)h(X,\mathrm{G}_{j})(h(\mathrm{G}_{i},\mathrm{G}_{j})-h(\mathrm{G}_{j},\mathrm{G}_{i}))-|h|^{2}h(X,Y)\\
&\overset{\eqref{comecommutah}}{=}2\alpha\sum_{i,j=1}^{2n-1}h(\mathrm{G}_{i},Y)h(X,\mathrm{G}_{j})C(\mathrm{G}_{j},\mathrm{G}_{i})-|h|^{2}h(X,Y)\\
&\overset{\eqref{comecommutah}}{=}2\alpha\sum_{i,j=1}^{2n-1}h(Y,\mathrm{G}_{i})h(X,\mathrm{G}_{j})C(\mathrm{G}_{j},\mathrm{G}_{i})+4\alpha^{2}\sum_{i,j=1}^{2n-1}h(X,\mathrm{G}_{j})C(Y,\mathrm{G}_{i})C(\mathrm{G}_{j},\mathrm{G}_{i})-|h|^{2}h(X,Y).\\
\end{split}

Notice that

\begin{split}\sum_{i,j=1}^{2n-1}h(Y,\mathrm{G}_{i})h(X,\mathrm{G}_{j})C(\mathrm{G}_{j},\mathrm{G}_{i})&=\sum_{j=1}^{2n-1}h(X,\mathrm{G}_{j})\left\langle\nabla_{Y}\nu,\sum_{i=1}^{2n-1}\langle J(\mathrm{G}_{j}),\mathrm{G}_{i}\rangle\mathrm{G}_{i}\right\rangle\\
&\overset{\eqref{metricequationnablas}}{=}\sum_{j=1}^{2n-1}h(X,\mathrm{G}_{j})\langle\nabla_{Y}\nu,J(\mathrm{G}_{j})\rangle\\
&=-\left\langle\nabla_{X}\nu,\sum_{j=1}^{2n-1}\langle J(\nabla_{Y}\nu),\mathrm{G}_{j}\rangle\mathrm{G}_{j}\right\rangle\\
&=-\langle\nabla_{X}\nu,J(\nabla_{Y}\nu)\rangle+\langle\nabla_{X}\nu,\nu\rangle\langle J(\nabla_{Y}\nu),\nu\rangle\\
&\overset{\eqref{metricequationnablas}}{=}-\langle\nabla_{X}\nu,J(\nabla_{Y}\nu)\rangle.\end{split}

On the other hand,

\begin{split}\sum_{i,j=1}^{2n-1}h(X,\mathrm{G}_{j})C(Y,\mathrm{G}_{i})&C(\mathrm{G}_{j},\mathrm{G}_{i})=\sum_{j=1}^{2n-1}h(X,\mathrm{G}_{j})\left\langle J(Y),\sum_{i=1}^{2n-1}\langle J(\mathrm{G}_{j}),\mathrm{G}_{i}\rangle\mathrm{G}_{i}\right\rangle\\
&=\sum_{j=1}^{2n-1}h(X,\mathrm{G}_{j})\left\langle J(Y),J(\mathrm{G}_{j})\right\rangle-\sum_{j=1}^{2n-1}h(X,\mathrm{G}_{j})\left\langle J(Y),\nu\right\rangle\langle J(\mathrm{G}_{j}),\nu\rangle\\
&=\sum_{j=1}^{2n-1}h(X,\mathrm{G}_{j})\left\langle Y,\mathrm{G}_{j}\right\rangle+h(X,J(\nu))\left\langle J(Y),\nu\right\rangle\\
&=h(X,Y)-h(X,J(\nu))\left\langle Y,J(\nu)\right\rangle.\end{split}

Therefore we conclude that

\mathrm{II}=\left(-|h|^{2}+4\alpha^{2}\right)h(X,Y)-4\alpha^{2}h(X,J(\nu))\left\langle Y,J(\nu)\right\rangle-2\alpha\langle\nabla_{X}\nu,J(\nabla_{Y}\nu)\rangle.

Computation of
\mathrm{IV}
.

\begin{split}\mathrm{IV}&=\sum_{i=1}^{2n-1}2\nabla^{S}_{X}(\mathrm{G}_{i}\alpha)C(Y,\mathrm{G}_{i})+\sum_{i=1}^{2n-1}2\mathrm{G}_{i}\alpha\nabla^{S}_{X}C(Y,\mathrm{G}_{i})\\
&\overset{\eqref{commutatortensorpersimons}}{=}2\nabla^{S}_{X}\left(\pi(J(Y))\alpha\right)+\sum_{i=1}^{2n-1}2G_{i}\alpha C(\mathrm{G}_{i},\nu)h(X,Y)-\sum_{i=1}^{2n-1}2\mathrm{G}_{i}\alpha C(Y,\nu)h(X,\mathrm{G}_{i})\\
&=2\hess^{{\mathcal{H}},S}\alpha(X,\pi(J(Y)))-2\langle\nabla\alpha,J(\nu)\rangle h(X,Y)+2\langle Y,J(\nu)\rangle\left(\nabla_{X}\nu\right)\alpha.\end{split}

Computation of
\mathrm{V}

\begin{split}\mathrm{V}&=\sum_{i=1}^{2n-1}2X\alpha C(\mathrm{G}_{i},\nu)h(\mathrm{G}_{i},Y)+\sum_{i=1}^{2n-1}2\alpha X(C(\mathrm{G}_{i},\nu)h(\mathrm{G}_{i},Y))\\
&\quad-\sum_{i=1}^{2n-1}2\alpha C(\nabla^{S}_{X}\mathrm{G}_{i},\nu)h(\mathrm{G}_{i},Y)-\sum_{i=1}^{2n-1}2\alpha C(\mathrm{G}_{i},\nu)h(\nabla^{S}_{X}\mathrm{G}_{i},Y)-\sum_{i=1}^{2n-1}2\alpha C(\mathrm{G}_{i},\nu)h(\mathrm{G}_{i},\nabla^{S}_{X}Y)\\
&\overset{\eqref{comecommutah}}{=}-2X\alpha h(Y,J(\nu))-2\alpha Xh(J(\nu),Y)+\sum_{i=1}^{2n-1}2\alpha\langle\nabla^{S}_{X}\mathrm{G}_{i},J(\nu)\rangle h(\mathrm{G}_{i},Y)\\
&\quad+2\alpha h(\nabla^{S}_{X}J(\nu),Y)+2\alpha h(J(\nu),\nabla^{S}_{X}Y)\\
&=-2X\alpha h(Y,J(\nu))-2\alpha X\langle\nabla_{J(\nu)}\nu,Y\rangle-\sum_{i=1}^{2n-1}2\alpha\langle\mathrm{G}_{i},\nabla^{S}_{X}J(\nu)\rangle h(\mathrm{G}_{i},Y)\\
&\quad+2\alpha h(\nabla^{S}_{X}J(\nu),Y)+2\alpha h(J(\nu),\nabla^{S}_{X}Y)\\
&=-2X\alpha h(Y,J(\nu))-2\alpha X\langle\nabla_{J(\nu)}\nu,Y\rangle+2\alpha h(J(\nu),\nabla^{S}_{X}Y)\\
&=-2X\alpha h(Y,J(\nu))-2\alpha\langle\nabla^{S}_{X}\nabla_{J(\nu)}\nu,Y\rangle\\
&=-2X\alpha h(Y,J(\nu))-2\alpha\langle\nabla_{X}\nabla_{J(\nu)}\nu,Y\rangle.\end{split}

Computation of
\mathrm{III}
.

\begin{split}\mathrm{III}&=2\nabla^{S}_{\mathcal{S}}h(\pi(J(X)),Y)\\
&=2\mathcal{S}\langle\nabla_{\pi(J(X))}\nu,Y\rangle-2\langle\nabla_{\nabla^{S}_{\mathcal{S}}\pi(J(X))}\nu,Y\rangle-2\langle\nabla_{\pi(J(X))}\nu,\nabla^{S}_{\mathcal{S}}Y)\\
&=2\langle\nabla^{S}_{\mathcal{S}}\nabla_{\pi(J(X))}\nu,Y\rangle+2\langle\nabla_{\pi(J(X))}\nu,\nabla^{S}_{\mathcal{S}}Y\rangle-2\langle\nabla_{\nabla^{S}_{\mathcal{S}}\pi(J(X))}\nu,Y\rangle-2\langle\nabla_{\pi(J(X))}\nu,\nabla^{S}_{\mathcal{S}}Y)\\
&=2\langle\nabla_{\mathcal{S}}\nabla_{\pi(J(X))}\nu,Y\rangle-2\langle\nabla_{\nabla_{\mathcal{S}}\pi(J(X))}\nu,Y\rangle+2\langle\nabla_{\mathcal{S}}\pi(J(X)),\nu\rangle\langle\nabla_{\nu}\nu,Y\rangle\\
&=2\langle R(\mathcal{S},\pi(J(X)))\nu,Y\rangle+2\langle\nabla_{\pi(J(X))}\nabla_{\mathcal{S}}\nu,Y\rangle+2\langle\nabla_{[\mathcal{S},\pi(J(X))]}\nu,Y\rangle\\
&\quad-2\langle\nabla_{\nabla_{\mathcal{S}}\pi(J(X))}\nu,Y\rangle+2\langle\nabla_{\mathcal{S}}\pi(J(X)),\nu\rangle\langle\nabla_{\nu}\nu,Y\rangle\\
&\overset{R\equiv 0}{=}2\langle\nabla_{\pi(J(X))}\nabla_{\mathcal{S}}\nu,Y\rangle+2\langle\nabla_{[\mathcal{S},\pi(J(X))]-\nabla_{\mathcal{S}}\pi(J(X))}\nu,Y\rangle+2\langle\nabla_{\mathcal{S}}\pi(J(X)),\nu\rangle\langle\nabla_{\nu}\nu,Y\rangle\\
&\overset{\eqref{nablaessenu},\eqref{propvh5}}{=}2\langle\nabla_{\pi(J(X))}\nabla^{\mathcal{H}}\alpha,Y\rangle+4\langle\nabla_{\pi(J(X))}\alpha^{2}J(\nu),Y\rangle+2\langle\nabla_{\tor_{\nabla}(\pi(J(X)),\mathcal{S})}\nu,Y\rangle\\
&\quad-2\langle\nabla_{\nabla_{\pi(J(X))}\mathcal{S}}\nu,Y\rangle+4\alpha\langle Y,J(\nu)\rangle h(\mathcal{S},\pi(J(X))).\end{split}

Notice that

2\langle\nabla_{\pi(J(X))}\nabla^{\mathcal{H}}\alpha,Y\rangle=2\langle\nabla^{S}_{\pi(J(X))}\nabla^{\mathcal{H}}\alpha,Y\rangle=2\hess^{{\mathcal{H}},S}\alpha(\pi(J(X)),Y).

Moreover,

\begin{split}4\langle\nabla_{\pi(J(X))}\alpha^{2}J(\nu),Y\rangle&=8\alpha\langle Y,J(\nu)\rangle\pi(J(X))\alpha+4\alpha^{2}\langle\nabla_{\pi(J(X))}J(\nu),Y\rangle\\
&=8\alpha\langle Y,J(\nu)\rangle\pi(J(X))\alpha-4\alpha^{2}h(\pi(J(X)),\pi(J(Y))).\\
\end{split}

In addition,

\begin{split}2\langle\nabla_{\tor_{\nabla}(\pi(J(X)),\mathcal{S})}\nu,Y\rangle&\overset{\eqref{pseudotorsion}}{=}-4\langle\pi(J(X)),J(\mathcal{S})\rangle\langle\nabla_{T}\nu,Y\rangle\\
&=-4\langle\pi(J(X)),J(T)\rangle\langle\nabla_{T}\nu,Y\rangle+4\alpha\langle\pi(J(X)),J(\nu)\rangle\langle\nabla_{T}\nu,Y\rangle\\
&\overset{\eqref{pijeixinhprmo}}{=}0.\end{split}

Observe that

\begin{split}-2\langle\nabla_{\nabla_{\pi(J(X))}\mathcal{S}}\nu,Y\rangle&\overset{\eqref{phflat}}{=}2\langle\nabla_{\nabla_{\pi(J(X))}\alpha\nu}\nu,Y\rangle\\
&=2\pi(J(X))\alpha\langle\nabla_{\nu}\nu,Y\rangle+2\alpha\langle\nabla_{\nabla_{\pi(J(X))}\nu}\nu,Y\rangle\\
&\overset{\eqref{propvh5}}{=}-4\alpha\pi(J(X))\alpha\langle Y,J(\nu)\rangle+2\alpha h(\nabla_{\pi(J(X))}\nu,Y)\\
&\overset{\eqref{comecommutah}}{=}-4\alpha\pi(J(X))\alpha\langle Y,J(\nu)\rangle+2\alpha h(Y,\nabla_{\pi(J(X))}\nu)+4\alpha^{2}h(\pi(J(X)),\pi(J(Y))).\end{split}

Finally,

4\alpha\langle Y,J(\nu)\rangle h(\mathcal{S},\pi(J(X)))\overset{\eqref{accaesse},\eqref{pijeixinhprmo}}{=}4\alpha\pi(J(X))\alpha\langle Y,J(\nu)\rangle.

In conclusion, we infer that

\mathrm{III}=2\hess^{{\mathcal{H}},S}\alpha(\pi(J(X)),Y)+8\alpha\pi(J(X))\alpha\langle Y,J(\nu)\rangle+2\alpha h(Y,\nabla_{\pi(J(X))}\nu).

The thesis follows from adding the terms that we have just computed.
∎

Proof.

In this section, we establish the sub-Riemannian analogue of

Proof of

.

We recall that we are assuming

for a given

and for any

Let us recall (cf.

for any

and any

Fix

,

and a test function

in the stability inequality (

\begin{split}\int_{S}&q\left(A(\delta)^{\beta}\varphi\right)^{2}\,d\sigma_{\mathcal{H}}\overset{\eqref{stabilityinequality}}{\leqslant}\int_{S}\left|\nabla^{{\mathcal{H}},S}\left(A(\delta)^{\beta}\varphi\right)\right|^{2}\,\,d\sigma_{\mathcal{H}}\\

\end{split}

Therefore, factoring out, we deduce that

(6.3)

\frac{\beta}{4}\left(\frac{k}{2n-1}+\beta-1\right)\int_{S}\varphi^{2}A(\delta)^{2\beta-4}|\nabla^{{\mathcal{H}},S}|\tilde{h}|^{2}|^{2}\,d\sigma_{\mathcal{H}}\leqslant\int_{S}(\beta-1)q\left(A(\delta)^{\beta}\varphi\right)^{2}+A(\delta)^{2\beta}|\nabla^{{\mathcal{H}},S}\varphi|^{2}\,d\sigma_{\mathcal{H}}.

Assume first that

\beta-1\geqslant 0

) and combining Cauchy-Schwarz and Young inequalities,

\begin{split}\int_{S}&q\left(A(\delta)^{\beta}\varphi\right)^{2}\,d\sigma_{\mathcal{H}}\overset{\eqref{stabilityinequality}}{\leqslant}\int_{S}\left|\nabla^{{\mathcal{H}},S}\left(A(\delta)^{\beta}\varphi\right)\right|^{2}\,\,d\sigma_{\mathcal{H}}\\
&=\int_{S}\frac{\beta^{2}}{4}\varphi^{2}A(\delta)^{2\beta-4}|\nabla^{{\mathcal{H}},S}|\tilde{h}|^{2}|^{2}+\beta\varphi A(\delta)^{2\beta-2}\langle\nabla^{{\mathcal{H}},S}|\tilde{h}|^{2},\nabla^{{\mathcal{H}},S}\varphi\rangle+A(\delta)^{2\beta}|\nabla^{{\mathcal{H}},S}\varphi|^{2}\,d\sigma_{\mathcal{H}}\\

for any given

\begin{split}\frac{\beta}{4}&\left(\frac{k}{2n-1}+\beta-1\right)\int_{S}\varphi^{2}A(\delta)^{2\beta-4}|\nabla^{{\mathcal{H}},S}|\tilde{h}|^{2}|^{2}\,d\sigma_{\mathcal{H}}\leqslant\frac{\beta}{4}(\beta-1)(\beta+\varepsilon)\int_{S}\varphi^{2}A(\delta)^{2\beta-4}\left|\nabla^{{\mathcal{H}},S}|\tilde{h}|^{2}\right|^{2}\,d\sigma_{\mathcal{H}}\\

so that, factoring out and dividing by

Notice that

for any

Finally, recalling (

⁢
P
3

\begin{split}\int_{S}\varphi^{2}|\tilde{h}|^{2\beta+2}\,d\sigma_{\mathcal{H}}&\overset{\eqref{segnodiq}}{\leqslant}\int_{S}q\left(A(\delta)^{\beta}\varphi\right)^{2}\,d\sigma_{\mathcal{H}}\\
&\overset{\eqref{secondssydelta}}{\leqslant}\frac{\beta}{4}(\beta+\hat{\varepsilon})\int_{S}\varphi^{2}A(\delta)^{2\beta-4}\left|\nabla^{{\mathcal{H}},S}|\tilde{h}|^{2}\right|^{2}\,d\sigma_{\mathcal{H}}+\left(1+\frac{\beta}{\hat{\varepsilon}}\right)\int_{S}A(\delta)^{2\beta}\left|\nabla^{{\mathcal{H}},S}\varphi\right|^{2}\,d\sigma_{\mathcal{H}}\\

Since the constant

is independent on

∎

7.
The Bernstein problem

As an important applications of our curvature estimates, we solve the Bernstein problem in the class of hypersurfaces

satisfying (

Proposition 7.1
.

Let

be a global perimeter minimizer with smooth, non-characteristic boundary. Then there exists a constant

for any

and any

Remark 7.2

With regard to the above volume growth condition, notice that

, where

is both the
homogeneous
and the

dimension of
\mathbb{H}^{n}
(cf.
[
69
]
).

In order to apply
Theorem

1.4
, we need to ensure the validity of (
6.1
) for suitable values of

.
To this aim, we see that

g_{S,k}(p)\overset{\eqref{doveedefinitafis}}{=}(6-4k)|\tilde{h}_{p}|^{2}-2k\ell(p)^{2}\overset{\eqref{newnewboundperelle}}{\geqslant}\left(6-\frac{16}{3}k\right)|\tilde{h}_{p}|^{2}\geqslant 0

provided that

.
Before stating our main result, we point out that, in view of
[
3
]
, when
n\geqslant 2
the notion of completeness for an embedded hypersurface

can be equivalently given by the restriction of the ambient metric on
S
or by the intrinsic metric of
S
, provided that the latter are induced by the Euclidean, Riemannian or sub-Riemannian structure of
{\mathbb{H}}^{n}
. Therefore, in the following we will talk without ambiguity of complete hypersurfaces.

Theorem 7.3
.

Let
S\subseteq{\mathbb{H}}^{2}
be a smooth, complete, connected, embedded, two sided non-characteristic hypersurface. Assume that
S
is stable. Assume that
S
verifies (

⁢
P
1

), (

⁢
P
2

) and (

⁢
P
3

). Assume in addition that there exists
p\in S
and a constant

such that

(7.2)

\lim_{r\to+\infty}\frac{\sigma_{\mathcal{H}}(S\cap B_{r}(p))}{r^{2n+1}}\leqslant c.

Then
S
is a vertical hyperplane.

Proof.

Fix
p\in S
as in the statement. Let
(R_{j})_{j}
be a sequence of positive numbers such that
\lim_{j\to\infty}R_{j}=+\infty
. In this way, up to a subsequence, we deduce from
(
7.2
) that

\sigma_{\mathcal{H}}(S\cap B_{2R_{j}}(p))\leqslant\hat{c}R_{j}^{2n+1}

for any
j\in\mathbb{N}
and a suitable constant
\hat{c}>0
. In view of
[
21

, it is possible to find a positive constant
\tilde{C}>0
and a sequence of non-negative functions

such that

\varphi_{j}\equiv 1\text{ in }B_{R_{j}}(p),\qquad\varphi_{j}\equiv 0\text{ in }{\mathbb{H}}^{2}\setminus B_{2R_{j}}(p)\qquad\text{and}\qquad|\nabla^{\mathcal{H}}\varphi_{j}|\leqslant\frac{\tilde{C}}{R_{j}}.

We wish to apply
Theorem

1.4
to the sequence
(\varphi_{j})_{j}
. To this aim, since
S
is complete, then

is compact in
S
for any
j\in\mathbb{N}
, whence
(\varphi_{j})_{j}\subseteq C^{1}_{c}(S)
.
Fix
k\in\left[0,\frac{9}{8}\right]

7.1
), (
6.1
) holds.
Therefore, for any fixed
