Dissipation-enabled bosonic Hamiltonian learning via new information-propagation bounds
Tim Möbus, Andreas Bluhm, Matthias C. Caro, Albert H. Werner, Cambyse Rouzé

Abstract
Reliable quantum technology requires knowledge of the dynamics governing the underlying system. This problem of characterizing and benchmarking quantum devices or experiments in continuous time is referred to as the Hamiltonian learning problem. In contrast to multi-qubit systems, learning guarantees for the dynamics of bosonic systems have hitherto remained mostly unexplored. For m m -mode Hamiltonians given as polynomials in annihilation and creation operators with modes arranged on a lattice, we establish a simple moment criterion in terms of the particle number operator which ensures that learning strategies from the finite-dimensional setting extend to the bosonic setting, requiring only coherent states and heterodyne detection on the experimental side. We then propose an enhanced procedure based on added dissipation that even works if the Hamiltonian time evolution violates this moment criterion: With high success probability it learns all coefficients of the Hamiltonian to accuracy ε \varepsilon using a total evolution time of 𝒪 ⁡ ( ε − 2 ​ log ⁡ ( m ) ) \mathcal{O}(\varepsilon^{-2}\log(m)) . Our protocol involves the experimentally reachable resources of projected coherent state preparation, dissipative regularization akin to recent quantum error correction schemes involving cat qubits stabilized by a nonlinear multi-photon driven dissipation process, and heterodyne measurements. As a crucial step in our analysis, we establish our moment criterion and a new Lieb-Robinson type bound for the evolution generated by an arbitrary bosonic Hamiltonian of bounded degree in the annihilation and creation operators combined with photon-driven dissipation. Our work demonstrates that a broad class of bosonic Hamiltonians can be efficiently learned from simple quantum experiments, and our bosonic Lieb-Robinson bound may independently serve as a versatile tool for studying evolutions on continuous variable systems.

The setup

We consider an
m

We assume the Hamiltonian to be a sum of two-body interactions on the edges of

That is, the Hamiltonian of the system is assumed to take the following form:

\displaystyle H\coloneqq\sum_{e\in E}\,H_{e}\,,

(1)

where for any edge

\displaystyle H_{e}\coloneqq\sum_{k,\ell,k^{\prime},\ell^{\prime}=0}^{d}\,\lambda^{(e)}_{k\ell k^{\prime}\ell^{\prime}}\,(a_{i}^{\dagger})^{k}\,a_{i}^{\ell}\,(a_{j}^{\dagger})^{k^{\prime}}\,a_{j}^{\ell^{\prime}}\,.

(2)

Here,

In what follows and to simplify the exposition, we will assume that

Our first assumption is that we have access to incoherent copies of the evolution

\displaystyle a_{i}|\alpha\rangle=\alpha_{i}|\alpha\rangle\,,\qquad i\in[m],\,\alpha_{i}\in\mathbb{C}\,.

(3)

Given

\displaystyle g_{e}(\alpha,\beta)

III
Taylor expansion and particle number control for many-body CV Hamiltonians

Before discussing the construction of our estimator in detail, let us first recall the situation of a local Hamiltonian associated to a locally finite-dimensional quantum system, e.g. a quantum lattice spin system where each vertex corresponds to a single qubit. There, the Hamiltonian learning task is that of learning the coefficients of the many-qubit Hamiltonian
H_{\operatorname{qubit}}
in a local Pauli basis.
Therefore, as observed in
Haah2021Optimal
;
franca2022efficient
;
Gu2022Practical
;
caro2022learning
, for a fixed edge
e=\{i,j\}
, it is sufficient to learn coefficients of the form
\lambda_{\sigma_{e}}\coloneqq\operatorname{tr}\big([H^{\operatorname{qubit}},\rho]\sigma_{e}\big)
, where
\sigma_{e}=\sigma_{i}\otimes\sigma_{j}\otimes I_{e^{c}}
denotes the tensor product of two arbitrary Pauli matrices
\sigma_{i}
and
\sigma_{j}
supported on the vertices
i
and
j
of the graph, and where the state
\rho
can be chosen to be a tensor product of biased qubit states of the form
\frac{I\pm\sigma_{k}}{2}

A simple Taylor expansion of the evolution up to second order gives, denoting
\rho_{t}\coloneqq e^{-itH^{\operatorname{qubit}}}\rho e^{itH^{\operatorname{qubit}}}

\displaystyle\Big|\lambda_{\sigma_{e}}-\frac{\operatorname{tr}\big[(\rho_{t}-\rho)\sigma_{e}\big]}{t}\Big|\leq\frac{t}{2}\max_{s\leq t}\Big|\operatorname{tr}\big[\rho_{s}(\mathcal{H}^{\operatorname{qubit}})^{2}(\sigma_{e})\big]\Big|\,,

(5)

where
-\mathcal{H}^{\operatorname{qubit}}(O)=i[H^{\operatorname{qubit}},O]
denotes the generator of the unitary evolution in the Heisenberg picture. The latter bound can be further simplified due to the locality of
H_{\operatorname{qubit}}
, which leads to an approximation of the coefficient
\lambda_{\sigma_{e}}
via a relative difference with additive error at most

\displaystyle\frac{t}{2}\Big\|\big[H^{\operatorname{qubit}}_{e\partial\partial},\big[H^{\operatorname{qubit}}_{e\partial},\sigma_{e}\big]\big]\Big\|_{\infty}\,.

(6)

Above,
H^{\operatorname{qubit}}_{e\partial}
refers to the interactions intersecting sites
i
and
j
, whereas
H^{\operatorname{qubit}}_{e\partial\partial}
refers to those interactions that intersect
i
,
j
, as well as the support of
H_{e\partial}^{\operatorname{qubit}}
. Since the operator norm above is independent of the overall size of the graph, on can therefore learn
\lambda_{\sigma_{e}}
as long as
t=\mathcal{O}(\varepsilon)
. As we mentioned in the introduction, combining this with a standard estimator of time-evolved averages
\operatorname{tr}[\rho_{t}\sigma_{e}]
results in a sub-optimal sample complexity of order
\mathcal{O}(\varepsilon^{-4}\ln(m))
, and therefore a total evolution time of order
\mathcal{O}(\varepsilon^{-3}\ln(m))
. Nevertheless, even this basic approach does not directly carry over to our continuous variable setting. This is due to the intrinsic unboundedness of the Hamiltonian in (
1
).

Instead, we need to consider a state-dependent upper bound similar to (
5
). We recall that the local observables considered in our setting are the two-mode effect operators
P_{\beta}^{(e)}\coloneqq\frac{1}{\pi^{2}}|\beta\rangle\langle\beta|_{e}\otimes I_{e^{c}}
corresponding to a local heterodyne measurement. Initializing the evolution on the pure coherent state
\rho\coloneqq|\alpha\rangle\langle\alpha|
, a reasoning identical to the one leading to (
5

\big|\frac{1}{\pi^{2}}\,g(\alpha,\beta)|\langle{\alpha_{e}}|{\beta}\rangle|^{2}-t^{-1}\operatorname{tr}\big[(\rho_{t}-\rho)P_{\beta}^{(e)}\big]\big|\leq\frac{t}{2}\max_{s\leq t}\big|\operatorname{tr}\big[\rho_{s}\mathcal{H}^{2}(P_{\beta}^{(e)})\big]\big|
, for
\rho_{s}\coloneqq e^{-itH}\rho e^{itH}
and
-\mathcal{H}(O)=i[H,O]
. As for the qubit case,
\mathcal{H}^{2}(P_{\beta}^{(e)})
simplifies into a double commutator involving Hamiltonian terms
H_{e^{\prime}}
corresponding to edges
e^{\prime}
at distance at most
1
to
e
, where the distance between two sets of vertices is defined as the minimal distance between any two vertices belonging to each of the sets. Let us also denote the Hamiltonians involved by
H_{e\partial}=\sum_{\operatorname{dist}(e,e^{\prime})=0}H_{e^{\prime}}
and
H_{e\partial\partial}=\sum_{\operatorname{dist}(e,e^{\prime})\leq 1}H_{e^{\prime}}
.
The resulting bound can be further controlled in terms of the moments of the time-evolved particle number observable:

\displaystyle\Big|\operatorname{tr}\big[\rho_{s}\mathcal{H}^{2}(P_{\beta}^{(e)})\big]\Big|

\displaystyle\leq\big\|\mathcal{H}_{e\partial}\circ\mathcal{H}_{e\partial\partial}(\rho_{s})\big\|_{1}

\displaystyle=\mathcal{O}(1)\cdot L^{2}(d+1)^{8}\,\gamma^{2}M^{(2d)}_{e\partial\partial}(s)\,,

(7)

where
\|X\|_{1}\coloneqq\operatorname{tr}|X|
denotes the trace norm and where, given a region
R\subset V
and an integer
k\in\mathbb{N}

M^{(k)}_{R}(s)\coloneqq\operatorname{tr}\big[\rho_{s}\prod_{i\in R}(I+N_{i})^{k}\big]
, for
N_{i}=a_{i}^{\dagger}a_{i}
denoting the particle number observable at site
i

For example, for the celebrated Bose-Hubbard model

;
Kuwahara2020

\displaystyle M_{R}^{(k)}(s)=\mathcal{O}(1)

(8)

for
k,|R|,s=\mathcal{O}(1)
. Our reasoning above implies that the finite-dimensional method described at the beginning of this section extends to the CV case whenever such a moment bound holds.

Dissipative regularizers

However, the moment condition (
8
) is not shared by all Hamiltonians of the form given in (
1

The generator of this process takes the following Lindblad form: Given

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}^{(\alpha,p)}_{V}\coloneqq\sum_{j\in V}\,\mathcal{L}[L_{j}^{(\alpha_{j},p)}],\,

Importantly, note that the parameters

\displaystyle M_{R}^{(k)}(t)\leq e^{\mathcal{O}(k|R|\ln(k|R|)t)}\,.

Plugging the bound (

VII
Justifying the modified evolution

It remains to justify the modification of the Hamiltonian evolution by the one with added dissipation introduced in

Here, we provide a mathematical justification by extending Trotter-Kato’s product formula to the case of unbounded generators, which shows that the evolution generated by the sum

VIII
Conclusion

We have established the first learning algorithm with rigorous guarantees for a broad class of bosonic Hamiltonians, namely those given as polynomials in annihilation and creation operators.
This extends recent progress in Hamiltonian learning to the experimentally and theoretically significant realm of CV quantum systems.
Novel LR-type bounds for dissipatively regularized evolutions of CV systems played a crucial role in our procedure and in themselves form a relevant contribution to the toolbox of CV quantum many-body physics.

A central question left open by our work is whether the total evolution time of bosonic Hamiltonian learning can be improved to achieve the Heisenberg scaling for our broad class of Hamiltonians. Recent progress in the case of many-qubit systems suggests that this will require more involved experimental setups involving long-time evolution interleaved with control operations
huang2023learning
;
dutkiewicz2023advantage
. Whether and how such techniques can be combined with our approach based on dissipative regularization is an interesting direction for future research.

In the following, we are concerned with Markovian evolutions, that is, semigroups of completely positive, trace-preserving maps, over bosonic systems that are generated by a Hamiltonian and a dissipative part (or Lindbladian). Given a possibly unbounded operator
L
and a self-adjoint operator
H
that can both be expressed in terms of polynomials of the creation and annihilation operators over a suitably chosen domain, we denote the superoperators

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}[L]\coloneqq L(.)L^{\dagger}-\frac{1}{2}\,\{L^{\dagger}L,\,.\}\,\qquad\text{ and }\qquad\mathcal{H}[H]\coloneqq-i[H,\,.\,]\,.

Formally, the evolutions in the Schödinger picture considered in this work will be exponentials of sums of superoperators of the form of
\mathcal{L}
and
\mathcal{H}
, i.e.

\displaystyle\mathcal{P}_{t}\coloneqq e^{t(\sum_{i}\mathcal{L}[L_{i}]+\mathcal{H}[H])}\,,\qquad t\in\mathbb{R}_{+}\,,

for some unbounded operators
L_{i}
and a self-adjoint operator
H
on
\mathcal{H}_{m}
. In this work, the operators
L_{i}
and
H
will be further assumed to be polynomials in the creation and annihilation operators on
\mathcal{H}_{m}
. We also introduce a useful set of conditions on
\widetilde{\mathcal{L}}\coloneqq\sum_{i}\mathcal{L}[L_{i}]+\mathcal{H}[H]
:

Assumption 1

(Photon-number moment stability)
.

For any
k>0
, there are

such that, for any state

\displaystyle\operatorname{tr}\Big[\widetilde{\mathcal{L}}(\rho)\bigotimes_{j=1}^{m}(N_{j}+I)^{k}\Big]\leq-c_{k}\operatorname{tr}\Big[\rho\bigotimes_{j=1}^{m}(N_{j}+I)^{k}\Big]+\mu_{k}\,.

Under

(29)

Here, the Sobolev norm is defined via

\mathcal{W}^{2k}(X)\coloneqq\bigotimes_{j=1}^{m}(N_{j}+I)^{k/2}X\bigotimes_{j=1}^{m}(N_{j}+I)^{k/2}\equiv\bigotimes_{j=1}^{m}\mathcal{W}_{j}^{2k}(X)

\|\cdot\|_{W^{2k,1}}\coloneqq\|\mathcal{W}^{2k}(.)\|_{1}\,.

(31)

More generally, for any region

\displaystyle\|\cdot\|_{W^{2k,1}(R)}\coloneqq\|\mathcal{W}^{2k}_{R}(.)\|_{1}\,,\qquad\text{ where }\quad\mathcal{W}^{2k}_{R}\coloneqq\bigotimes_{j\in R}\mathcal{W}_{j}^{2k}\,.

(32)

For later use, we also define the following operator

\mathcal{W}_{\Sigma}^{2k}(X)\coloneqq\sum_{j=1}^{m}(N_{j}+I)^{k/2}X(N_{j}+I)^{k/2}\,.

(33)

For a quantum state
\rho

\big\|\mathcal{W}^{k/m}(\rho)\big\|_{1}=\operatorname{tr}\Big[\sqrt{\rho}\bigotimes_{j=1}^{m}(N_{j}+I)^{k/(2m)}\sqrt{\rho}\Big]\leq\frac{1}{m}\operatorname{tr}\Big[\sqrt{\rho}\sum_{j=1}^{m}(N_{j}+I)^{k/2}\sqrt{\rho}\Big]=\frac{1}{m}\big\|\mathcal{W}_{\Sigma}^{k}(\rho)\big\|_{1}\,.

Lemma 7
.

For any

\displaystyle\||\alpha\rangle\langle\alpha|\|_{W^{2k,1}}\leq 2^{k}\left(\frac{k}{\ln(k/|\alpha|^{2}+1)}\right)^{k}

Proof.

It is well-known that the state

generates a Poisson distribution over the spectrum of

\displaystyle\||\alpha\rangle\langle\alpha|\|_{W^{2k,1}}

\displaystyle=\langle\alpha|(N+I)^{k}|\alpha\rangle

\displaystyle=e^{-|\alpha|^{2}}\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\,\frac{|\alpha|^{2n}}{n!}\,(n+1)^{k}

\displaystyle=\sum_{l=0}^{k}\binom{k}{l}\,\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}n^{l}\,\frac{|\alpha|^{2n}}{n!}e^{-|\alpha|^{2}}

where we have used a simple bound on the

Ahle2022

A.3.1
Bosonic Hamiltonian

The goal of this article is to learn Hamiltonians that can be expressed as polynomials of annihilation and creation operators with some known local structure. More precisely, we consider an
m
-mode bosonic system, where each mode is associated to a vertex
v\in V
of a graph

, whose edges

\displaystyle\mathcal{H}_{E}=-i[H_{E},\,\cdot\,]\qquad\text{where}\quad H_{E}\coloneqq\sum_{e\in E}\,H_{e}~\,,\text{for $e=\{i,j\}$,}\quad H_{e}\coloneqq\sum_{k,\ell,k^{\prime},\ell^{\prime}=0}^{d}\,\lambda^{(e)}_{k\ell k^{\prime}\ell^{\prime}}\,(a_{i}^{\dagger})^{k}\,a_{i}^{\ell}\,(a_{j}^{\dagger})^{k^{\prime}}\,a_{j}^{\ell^{\prime}}

for some complex coefficients

with
|\lambda^{(e)}_{k\ell k^{\prime}\ell^{\prime}}|\leq L
. Note that we can fix

, due to gauge freedom.
By uniqueness of the decomposition and self-adjointness of each

, we deduce that
\lambda^{(e)}_{k\ell k^{\prime}\ell^{\prime}}=\overline{\lambda}^{(e)}_{\ell k\ell^{\prime}k^{\prime}}
. Here,

denotes to the creation operator at vertex

is its associated creation operator.
To avoid superfluous technicalities, we further assume that

R\subset V
of vertices, we define the associated subgraph
(R,E_{R})
, where the set of edges is

The dynamics generated by

do not satisfy

in general fail to be approximable by their finite Taylor sums, even on smooth inputs such as coherent states. Similarly, finite propagation bounds such as cluster expansion and Lieb-Robinson estimates are not applicable for such Hamiltonians.
As these tools play a crucial role in the analysis of the sample optimal protocols in

huang2023learning

A.3.2
Dissipative regularizers

To overcome this issue, we introduce a class of local, Sobolev preserving dissipative generators, which we call
dissipative regularizers
.
These generators, which arise from the theory of continuous variables quantum error correcting codes
Guillaud.2019
;
Guillaud.2023
, take the following form: Given
\alpha\in\mathbb{C}^{m}
and
p\in\mathbb{N}
,

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}_{V}^{(\alpha,p)}\coloneqq\sum_{j\in V}\mathcal{L}[L_{j}^{(\alpha_{j},p)}],\qquad\text{ where }\quad L_{j}^{(\alpha_{j},p)}\coloneqq a_{j}^{p}-\alpha_{j}^{p}I\,.

(35)

On a subgraph
(R,E_{R})
, we denote the dissipation by
\mathcal{L}_{R}^{(\alpha,p)}
and the overall generators of the evolutions on
V
and
R
are denoted by

\widetilde{\mathcal{L}}^{(\alpha)}=\mathcal{L}_{V}^{(\alpha,p)}+\mathcal{H}_{E}\,,\qquad\qquad\text{respectively}\qquad\qquad\widetilde{\mathcal{L}}_{R}=\mathcal{L}_{R}^{(\alpha,p)}+\mathcal{H}_{E_{R}}\,.

(36)

While
\widetilde{\mathcal{L}}_{R}
also depends on
\alpha
, we do not make this dependence explicit notationally to avoid overloading the notation.
As we will see in section
, the added dissipation permits a decoupling of the evolution over separated regions, which can thus be analyzed independently when choosing a suitable parameter vector
\alpha
for the input coherent state as well as for the jump operators
L_{i}^{(\alpha_{i},p)}
.

Lemma 11
.

For
\alpha,\beta>0
and
a>b>0
, the polynomial
p(X)=-\alpha X^{a}+\beta X^{b}
satisfies

\displaystyle\sup_{X\geq 0}\,p(X)\leq\left(\frac{\beta b}{\alpha a}\right)^{\frac{b}{a-b}}\,\beta\,.

Proof.

Searching for zeroes of the derivative of
p
, we see that it attains its maximum on
[0,\infty)
at
X_{0}=\left(\tfrac{\beta b}{\alpha a}\right)^{1/(a-b)}
. Plugging this into the polynomial, we get
p(X_{0})=\left(\tfrac{\beta b}{\alpha a}\right)^{\frac{b}{a-b}}\,\beta\,\left(1-\tfrac{b}{a}\right)\leq\left(\tfrac{\beta b}{\alpha a}\right)^{\frac{b}{a-b}}\,\beta.

∎
