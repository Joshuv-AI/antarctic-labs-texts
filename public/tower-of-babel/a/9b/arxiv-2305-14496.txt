Optimal learning via Moderate Deviations Theory
Arnab Ganguly, Tobias Sutter

Abstract
This paper proposes a statistically optimal approach for learning a function value using a confidence interval in a wide range of models, including general non-parametric estimation of an expected loss described as a stochastic programming problem or various SDE models. More precisely, we develop a systematic construction of highly accurate confidence intervals by using a moderate deviation principle-based approach. It is shown that the proposed confidence intervals are statistically optimal in the sense that they satisfy criteria regarding exponential accuracy, minimality, consistency, mischaracterization probability, and eventual uniformly most accurate (UMA) property. The confidence intervals suggested by this approach are expressed as solutions to robust optimization problems, where the uncertainty is expressed via the underlying moderate deviation rate function induced by the data-generating process. We demonstrate that for many models these optimization problems admit tractable reformulations as finite convex programs even when they are infinite-dimensional.

Introduction

Proper understanding of quantitative disciplines like physics, systems biology, mathematical finance, etc. requires building accurate mathematical models and calibrating them according to available data. The model and the associated data can be in a wide array of formats ranging from i.i.d. observations from a probability distribution to data from a stochastic process (e.g., solution of a stochastic dynamical system) having intricate temporal correlation structure.
Calibration of the model according to the available data often depends on robust and accurate estimation of relevant cost functions. This in turn requires construction of appropriate confidence intervals for the cost function, which is the main theme of this paper. More precisely, given a function
J:\Theta\to\mathbb{R}
and an unknown model parameter
\theta\in\Theta
, the paper aims to learn the function value
J(\theta)
via a novel confidence interval estimation procedure.

As mentioned,
J
is typically interpreted as a
cost function
associated with a model. For example, in systems biology, the term
J(\theta)
may describe the energy cost required to steer a chemical species in a reaction system to the desired configuration in finite time, where the chemical reaction dynamics depend on an unknown parameter
\theta

[
68
]
. Problems in decision theory often involve working with the long-run average cost
J(\theta)
of a discrete-time Markov Decision Process under a policy
\pi_{\theta}
parameterized by
\theta
, i.e.,

J(\theta)=\limsup_{m\to\infty}\frac{1}{m}\sum_{j=0}^{m-1}\mathbb{E}_{\theta}[c(X_{t_{j}})],

where
c:\mathcal{S}\to\mathbb{R}
denotes some cost-per-stage function and
\mathbb{E}_{\theta}
denotes the expectation of the
\mathcal{S}
-valued Markov Decision Process under policy
\pi_{\theta}
; see
[
42
]
for a rigorous construction. Estimating
J(\theta)
of the form above, when the underlying transition kernel is unknown, is a key building block in reinforcement learning
[
51
]
. Depending on how the data is generated, the reinforcement learning jargon refers to the estimation problem of
J(\theta)
as policy evaluation
[
10
,
65
]
or off-policy evaluation
[
58
,
29
,
48
]
.

The parameter
\theta
in this paper can be either finite or infinite-dimensional, and we specifically note that the term ‘parameter’ is used here in a rather broad sense. In particular, by taking
\Theta
to be the space of probability measures (or a subclass of it), our framework covers so-called non-parametric models.
An important example in this direction is the non-parametric estimation of an expected loss of a stochastic programming problem. Taking
\Theta
to be a suitable subclass of
\mathcal{P}(\mathcal{S})
, the space of probability measures on
\mathcal{S}\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}
, the aim is to estimate

J(\theta)=\min_{z\in\mathcal{Z}}\mathbb{E}_{\theta}[\ell(X,z)]

(1)

for a given loss function
\ell:\mathcal{S}\times\mathcal{Z}\to\mathbb{R}
and an unknown probability measure
\theta\in\Theta
. Such cost functions and their variations are at the heart of a large class of stochastic optimization problems
[
63
]
, and constructing suitable confidence intervals for
J(\theta)
is necessary for producing distributionally robust solutions (for example, see
[
22
,
6
,
12
]
).

In this paper, we consider a general function
J:\Theta\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
and do not restrict ourselves to a specific structure. For estimating the quantity
J(\theta)
from the available data via a confidence interval (CI) denoted as
\widehat{\mathcal{I}}_{T}
, the traditional approach involves finding a CI
\widehat{\mathcal{I}}_{T}
with
(1-\alpha)\%
confidence for a fixed threshold
\alpha
; specifically, for a fixed
\alpha
,
\widehat{\mathcal{I}}_{T}
needs to satisfy
\mathbb{P}_{\theta}(J(\theta)\in\widehat{\mathcal{I}}_{T})\geqslant 1-\alpha
for all
\theta\in\Theta
.
The construction of an ‘ideal’ CI of course also needs to take into account other optimality and accuracy properties besides a chosen high-level of confidence. In the world of CIs corresponding to a fixed threshold
\alpha
,
uniformly most accurate confidence intervals
(UMA-CIs) are often the ideal prototypes. A UMA-CI of level
1-\alpha
is a confidence interval
\widehat{\mathcal{I}}_{T}
such that
J(\theta)
lies in
\widehat{\mathcal{I}}_{T}
with probability at least
1-\alpha
for all
\theta\in\Theta
, and in addition, any other
1-\alpha
confidence interval
\widehat{\mathcal{I}}^{\prime}_{T}
is such that
\mathbb{P}_{\theta}(J(\theta^{\prime})\in\widehat{\mathcal{I}}_{T})\leqslant\mathbb{P}_{\theta}(J(\theta^{\prime})\in\widehat{\mathcal{I}}^{\prime}_{T})
for all
\theta^{\prime}\in\Theta
such that
J(\theta^{\prime})\neq J(\theta)
, i.e., confidence intervals with small probabilities of covering wrong values of
J(\theta)
are preferred, see
[
47
, Section 3.5]
.
UMA-CIs are typically constructed via uniformly most powerful (UMP) tests. In the case of simple hypotheses testing with known likelihoods, the Neyman-Pearson lemma
[
47
, Section 3.2]
ensures that the likelihood ratio defines a UMP test statistic, which then leads to a UMA-CI. The Neyman-Pearson lemma cannot be used when it is necessary to work with approximate likelihood functions. More importantly, except for some toy problems, the Neyman-Pearson lemma is no longer applicable for composite hypotheses testing (where the alternate hypothesis consists of a family of probability measures), which is what is required for UMA-CIs.
Indeed the requirement of uniformly small
\mathbb{P}_{\theta}(J(\theta^{\prime})\in\widehat{\mathcal{I}}_{T})
for every
\theta^{\prime}
such that
J(\theta^{\prime})\neq J(\theta)
and each
T
is too strong, and it is not possible to characterize cases where UMA-CIs will exist.

It is thus necessary to work with limiting notions of accuracy and optimality, which is what we use in the paper. The most common way to obtain a CI,
\widehat{\mathcal{I}}_{T}
, with asymptotic confidence
(1-\alpha)
, i.e.,
\lim_{T\rightarrow\infty}\mathbb{P}_{\theta}(J(\theta)\in\widehat{\mathcal{I}}_{T})\geqslant 1-\alpha
, is through a CLT. Given an estimator
\widehat{\theta}_{T}
, the CLT method gives an interval of the form
[J(\widehat{\theta}_{T})-\widehat{\sigma}_{T}z/\sqrt{T},J(\widehat{\theta}_{T})+\widehat{\sigma}_{T}z/\sqrt{T}]
with
\widehat{\sigma}_{T}
being a suitable estimate of variance of
\widehat{\theta}_{T}
. The constant
z
depends on the threshold
\alpha
and the limiting distribution of
\sqrt{T}(\widehat{\theta}_{T}-\theta)/\sqrt{\text{Var}(\widehat{\theta}_{T})}
, which is typically standard normal. For the case of standard sample average estimate
\widehat{\theta}_{T}=\frac{1}{T}\sum_{s=1}^{T}X_{s}
of population mean
\theta
based on i.i.d. data points
\{X_{s}:s=1,2,\ldots,T\}
(with
\mathbb{E}[X_{s}]=\theta
), Edgeworth corrected CIs achieving higher accuracy than the typical CLT based CI have been studied in
[
39
,
1
,
40
]
with the last paper studying uniform coverage bounds (over a suitable class of probability distributions) of these CIs.

In contrast, our work takes a different route and focuses on
exponentially accurate
CIs for which the the error probability,
\mathbb{P}_{\theta}(J(\theta)\notin\widehat{\mathcal{I}}_{T})
, decays exponentially fast with a chosen rate
r>0
and speed
b_{T}\rightarrow\infty
. There is almost nothing available in the literature on such intervals. For
\mathbb{R}^{d}
-valued i.i.d. random variables
\{X_{s}:s=1,2,\dots,T\}
having density
f
, the paper
[
54
]
constructs an exponentially accurate interval for certain kernel density estimators
\widehat{f}_{T}
. Our goal in this paper is much more ambitious — we aim to build exponentially accurate CIs in a general framework covering datasets from a wide variety of different models. Of course the construction of an exponentially accurate CI is just part of the goal — it is important to construct one which exhibits various optimilaity properties among its peers. Minimality of such an interval
\widehat{\mathcal{I}}^{\star}_{T}
, that is, requiring
\widehat{\mathcal{I}}^{\star}_{T}\subset\widehat{\mathcal{I}}_{T}
for any other CI
\widehat{\mathcal{I}}_{T}
satisfying the same exponential accuracy condition at least for large
T
, is a natural criterion in this respect. However, such a stringent perception of minimality is almost always impossible to achieve, and in most cases, it is necessary to restrict the search for optimal CIs within a smaller class and view optimality through a bit different lens. Towards this end, we search for an ideal candidate in the class of CIs that are based on a given point estimator
\widehat{\theta}_{T}
. More precisely, the confidence intervals of interest to us are the form
\widehat{\mathcal{I}}_{T}=\mathcal{I}_{T}(\widehat{\theta}_{T})
where

\mathcal{I}_{T}(\theta)=[\underaccent{\bar}{H}_{T,r}(\theta),\bar{H}_{T,r}(\theta)]
, with
\underaccent{\bar}{H}_{T,r},\bar{H}_{T,r}
belonging to suitable classes of functions from
\mathcal{V}
to
\mathbb{R}
. Here
\mathcal{V}
is the space where the estimator
\widehat{\theta}_{T}
takes its values and could be larger than the parameter set
\Theta
. The primary objective of the paper is to develop a systematic approach for constructing an interval-family
\{\mathcal{I}^{\star}_{T}(\cdot)=[\underaccent{\bar}{J}_{T,r}(\cdot),\bar{J}_{T,r}(\cdot)]:T>0\}
that is an ideal choice from multiple angles. At this stage it is useful to make a note about a couple of terminologies that we will use in the paper. For convenience, we will refer to
\{\mathcal{I}_{T}=[\underaccent{\bar}{H}_{T,r},\bar{H}_{T,r}]:T>0\}
as an
interval-family
, although a longer term,
interval-function-family
, perhaps is more appropriate. Notice that for each point
u
in the domain of the functions
\underaccent{\bar}{H}_{T,r}
and
\bar{H}_{T,r}
(with
\underaccent{\bar}{H}_{T,r}\leqslant\bar{H}_{T,r}
pointwise) and
T>0
,
\mathcal{I}_{T}(u)=[\underaccent{\bar}{H}_{T,r}(u),\bar{H}_{T,r}(u)]
is a legitimate interval, and the term
confidence interval
arising in this context will be reserved for the (random) interval,
\mathcal{I}_{T}(\widehat{\theta}_{T})=[\underaccent{\bar}{H}_{T,r}(\widehat{\theta}_{T}),\bar{H}_{T,r}(\widehat{\theta}_{T})]
.

We are now ready to list the accuracy criteria that we want our optimal interval-family
\{\mathcal{I}^{\star}_{T}=[\underaccent{\bar}{J}_{T,r},\bar{J}_{T,r}]:T>0\}
to satisfy.

(i)

Exponential accuracy
: Given a rate
r\in(0,\infty)
and speed
\{b_{T}\}_{T\in\mathbb{N}}
such that
b_{T}\rightarrow\infty
of exponential decay, the confidence interval
\mathcal{I}^{\star}_{T}(\widehat{\theta}_{T})
is such that

\mathbb{P}_{\theta}\left(J(\theta)\notin\mathcal{I}^{\star}_{T}(\widehat{\theta}_{T})\right)=O(e^{-rb_{T}})\quad\forall\theta\in\Theta.

(ii)

Minimality
: Any interval
\mathcal{I}_{T}(\theta)=[\underaccent{\bar}{H}_{T,r}(\theta),\bar{H}_{T,r}(\theta)]
with the property
(i)
will eventually (as
T
is sufficiently large) contain
\mathcal{I}^{\star}_{T}(\theta)=[\underaccent{\bar}{J}_{T,r}(\theta),\bar{J}_{T,r}(\theta)]
for every
\theta\in\Theta
; that is, for every
\theta
,
\underaccent{\bar}{J}_{T,r}(\theta)
will be eventually bigger than
\underaccent{\bar}{H}_{T,r}(\theta)
, and
\bar{J}_{T,r}(\theta)
eventually smaller than
\bar{H}_{T,r}(\theta)
.

(iii)

Consistency
: For every
\theta\in\Theta
the interval
\mathcal{I}^{\star}_{T}
is such that under
\mathbb{P}_{\theta}
, the resulting CI
\mathcal{I}^{\star}_{T}(\widehat{\theta}_{T})=[\underaccent{\bar}{J}_{T,r}(\widehat{\theta}_{T}),\bar{J}_{T,r}(\widehat{\theta}_{T})]\to\{J(\theta)\}
either almost surely or in probability as
T\to\infty
.

(iv)

Mischaracterization probability
: Consider any fixed
\theta\in\Theta
. Then the interval family
\mathcal{I}^{\star}_{T}
is such that there is a substantial chance of values of the cost function other than the true value to fall outside the CI,
\mathcal{I}^{\star}_{T}(\widehat{\theta}_{T})
. We ensure this by requiring that for any
\tilde{\theta}\in\Theta
such that
J(\tilde{\theta})\neq J(\theta)
, the probability
\mathbb{P}_{\theta}(J(\tilde{\theta})\notin\mathcal{I}^{\star}_{T}(\widehat{\theta}_{T}))
does not decay exponentially fast.

(v)

Eventually UMA
:
Any interval family
\{\mathcal{I}_{T}\}
with the property
(i)
will eventually (as
T
is sufficiently large) be such that for any
\theta\in\Theta

\mathbb{P}_{\theta}\left(J(\tilde{\theta})\in\mathcal{I}^{\star}_{T}(\widehat{\theta}_{T})\right)\leqslant\mathbb{P}_{\theta}\left(J(\tilde{\theta})\in\mathcal{I}_{T}(\widehat{\theta}_{T})\right)\quad\forall\tilde{\theta}\in\Theta:J(\tilde{\theta})\neq J(\theta).

These criteria will be stated and discussed rigorously in Section
3
— see Problem
3.7
and its solution presented in our main results, Theorems
3.10
and
3.11
. Here, we just add a few high-level remarks about these five criteria.
The criterion
(iv)
is an asymptotic version of what in the literature is often referred to as an
unbiased confidence interval
, see
[
47
, Section 5.5]
.
Both criteria
(ii)
and
(v)
pertain to the smallness of the size of the desired interval, but one does not imply the other. While
(ii)
ensures the optimality of the functions
\underaccent{\bar}{J}_{T,r},\bar{J}_{T,r}
defining the lower and upper bounds of the interval-family,
(v)
encodes the requirement that the actual CI,
\mathcal{I}^{\star}_{T}(\widehat{\theta}_{T})=[\underaccent{\bar}{J}_{T,r}(\widehat{\theta}_{T}),\bar{J}_{T,r}(\widehat{\theta}_{T})]
needs to be smaller than any other competing intervals.
While nonasymptotic versions of the criteria
(v)
as well as
(iv)
are standard textbook concepts and their corresponding versions at least for CIs with usual asymptotic level
(1-\alpha)
is well-known, except for simple models, their uses for establishing optimality of a CI in limiting regimes are rather rare. Even rarer is the criterion
(ii)
, which, to the best of our knowledge, has not been used before besides
[
64
]
.

We now discuss the conditions that we need for the above properties.
Of course, consistency of the underlying estimator
\widehat{\theta}_{T}
is a bare minimum requirement for consistency of the CI. In fact, we need more information about the convergence rate of
\widehat{\theta}_{T}
to
\theta
to get
(i)
and
(ii)
. It is not hard to see that exponential decay with rate
r
and speed
b_{T}
in the error probability in
(i)
needs an exponential decay for probabilities of the scaled deviations,
a_{T}(\widehat{\theta}_{T}-\theta)
, with the same speed and suitable scaling factor
a_{T}
. Mathematically, this means we need
\widehat{\theta}_{T}
to satisfy a
large deviation asymptotics
with speed
b_{T}
and rate function
I
in a scaling regime determined by
a_{T}=\sqrt{T/b_{T}}
. Here we note that the phrase
large deviation principle (LDP)
is typically reserved for the scaling regime corresponding to
a_{T}=1
and the phrase
moderate deviation principle (MDP)
is used for the scaling regime,
1\ll a_{T}\ll\sqrt{T}
(or equivalently,
1\ll b_{T}\ll T
). This paper focuses exclusively on the MDP scaling regime. It is clear that an MDP, by analyzing the deviations that are neither too near the limit nor too far from it, bridges the gap between a central limit theorem and the usual LDP. The rate function
I^{M}
of the MDP of
\widehat{\theta}_{T}
lies at the core of constructing the optimal interval. Moderate deviation asymptotics have been frequently used in mathematical statistics with early works focusing on sample averages based on i.i.d. sequences and arrays
[
61
,
52
,
2
,
23
]
. More general framework including that of general topological spaces, weakly dependent sequences, Markov processes, multiscale systems, stochastic PDEs have been considered in various works, a very limited list of references for which is
[
34
,
35
,
25
,
19
,
69
,
36
,
3
,
20
,
21
,
49
,
26
,
37
,
38
,
18
,
32
]
.
MDP for different statistical estimators used in regression analysis, time-series analysis, inference of specific stochastic differential equations have been obtained in
[
27
,
55
,
70
,
33
,
50
]
.

Further related work.

The notion of minimality
(ii)
in combination with exponential accuracy is inspired by the recent work
[
64
]
, where the goal is to derive high confidence upper bound estimates
\bar{H}_{T,r}(\widehat{\theta}_{T})
to
J(\theta)
similar to
(i)
, but with speed
b_{T}=T
, while ensuring a slightly modified concept to
(ii)
regarding minimality. The estimator
\bar{H}_{T,r}(\widehat{\theta}_{T})
in
[
64
]
relies on
\{\widehat{\theta}_{T}\}
satisfying a large deviation principle and, importantly, the function
\bar{H}_{T,r}
does not depend on time. Consequently, the resulting estimator lacks asymptotic consistency, i.e.,
\bar{H}_{T,r}(\widehat{\theta}_{T})
does not converge to
J(\theta)
as
T\to\infty
. Another limitation of
[
64
]
is that the results rely on specific properties of the large deviation rate function, such as the “radial monotonicity" (see
[
64
, Definition 3.3]
), which is a restrictive assumption. Our approach works under much milder assumptions on the underlying rate function
I^{M}
. The paper
[
64
]
generalizes an approach restricted to i.i.d. data with finite support
[
66
]
.
A more recent work
[
9
]
extends the approach of
[
66
]
intending to achieve asymptotic consistency of the high-confidence upper bound estimates by exploiting a moderate deviation principle ensuring asymptotic consistency of the estimators. This work, however, is restricted to the setting of finite state i.i.d. processes and uses a slightly different notion of minimality in
(ii)
compared to us.

When
J(\theta)
is described by an expected loss with respect to a probability measure
\theta
, i.e.,
J(\theta)=\mathbb{E}_{\theta}[\ell(X)]
, and
\widehat{\theta}_{T}
denotes the empirical probability measure constructed from
T
i.i.d. samples drawn from
\theta
,
[
46
,
28
]
discussed how to construct functions
\underaccent{\bar}{H}_{T,r}
and
\bar{H}_{T,r}
such that the corresponding CI,
\mathcal{I}_{T}(\widehat{\theta}_{T})=[\underaccent{\bar}{H}_{T,r}(\widehat{\theta}_{T}),\bar{H}_{T,r}(\widehat{\theta}_{T})]
, provides an asymptotically exact coverage corresponding to a fixed threshold
\alpha
, i.e.,
\lim_{T\to\infty}\mathbb{P}_{\theta}(J(\theta)\notin\mathcal{I}_{T}(\widehat{\theta}_{T}))=\alpha
, and such that
\mathcal{I}_{T}(\widehat{\theta}_{T})\to\{J(\theta)\}
as
T\to\infty
.
This of course differs from our framework of exponential accuracy as described in
(i)
. Moreover,
[
46
,
28
]
did not study optimality of their proposed intervals.

The above CIs are of the form

\underaccent{\bar}{H}_{T,r}(\widehat{\theta}_{T})=\inf_{\theta\in\Theta}\{J(\theta):\mathsf{d}_{T}(\widehat{\theta}_{T},\theta)\leqslant r\},\quad\bar{H}_{T,r}(\widehat{\theta}_{T})=\sup_{\theta\in\Theta}\{J(\theta):\mathsf{d}_{T}(\widehat{\theta}_{T},\theta)\leqslant r\},

(2)

where
\mathsf{d}_{T}
is some distance-like function. Interestingly, the functions
\underaccent{\bar}{J}_{T,r},\bar{J}_{T,r}
defining the optimal confidence interval bounds in our paper are also of the above form, with the noteworthy feature that
\mathsf{d}_{T}
depends on the data-generating process through the MDP rate function. Intuitively, the constraint
\mathsf{d}_{T}(\widehat{\theta}_{T},\theta)\leqslant r
forces the optimization variable
\theta
to be “close" to the estimator
\widehat{\theta}_{T}
in a sense that will be made precise within this paper. In other words, an estimator of the form
\bar{H}_{T,r}(\widehat{\theta}_{T})
in (
2
) describes the worst-case value of
J
that can attain in a neighborhood close to the estimator
\widehat{\theta}_{T}
. Estimators of the form (
2
) are popular objects in robust optimization
[
8
,
11
]
and distributionally robust optimization
[
45
,
59
]
. While there is a vast amount of literature on the choice of
\mathsf{d}_{T}
, it is usually chosen ad hoc and common examples are scaled
\phi
-divergences (e.g., relative entropy) or integral probability metrics (e.g., Wasserstein distances). Then, for a fixed structure of
\mathsf{d}_{T}
and asymptotically fixed threshold
\alpha
, the statistical properties of
\bar{H}_{T,r}(\widehat{\theta}_{T})
are analyzed, see
[
46
,
28
,
53
,
15
]
.
In particular, to ensure consistency of the two estimators
\underaccent{\bar}{H}_{T,r}(\widehat{\theta}_{T})
and
\bar{H}_{T,r}(\widehat{\theta}_{T})
, the ambiguity set
\{\theta\in\Theta:\mathsf{d}_{T}(\widehat{\theta}_{T},\theta)\leqslant r\}
asymptotically should reduce to a singleton described by the true unknown parameter
\theta

[
28
,
14
]
.
Our approach is fundamentally different. We use a top-down approach — starting from a list of natural criteria,
(i)
-
(v)
, that one expects the functions,
\underaccent{\bar}{J}_{T,r}
and

, defining the lower and upper bounds of the optimal CI to satisfy, we describe a method to construct them. In our case, the data-generating process, or the rate function of the underlying MDP to be precise, naturally induces the optimal structure of
\mathsf{d}_{T}

2
Discussion of standard approaches to estimation

We start by discussing whether standard estimators of
J(\theta)
can be adapted to work in our desired setting of exponential accuracy.

Limitation of the canonical estimator:

From the point estimation perspective, a natural question is if given an estimator
\widehat{\theta}_{T}
of
\theta
and a function
J
, the canonical estimator
J(\widehat{\theta}_{T})
is an ideal choice for estimating
J(\theta)
. The estimator
J(\widehat{\theta}_{T})
is of course consistent (assuming that
\widehat{\theta}_{T}
is consistent, and
J
is continuous), but the answer, perhaps counter-intuitively, is “not always". Indeed
J(\widehat{\theta}_{T})
is not a good candidate for estimating
J(\theta)
in situations where the consequences of the true cost being greater than the estimated one are catastrophic. For a specific scenario, consider the constrained optimization problem of the form

\displaystyle\min_{x\in\mathcal{E}_{c}}g(x),\quad\text{ where }\ \mathcal{E}_{c}=\{x\in\mathcal{E}:J(\theta,x)\leqslant c\}.

(3)

In absence of knowledge of
\theta
, one needs to use a suitable estimator
\widehat{J}_{T}(x)
for
J(\theta,x)
. Accuracy of the approximation of the original solution by the solution of the data-driven version of the problem,

\min_{x\in\mathcal{E}_{c}}g(x)
subject to
\widehat{J}_{T}(x)\leqslant c,
as well as its robustness requires the estimator
\widehat{J}_{T}(x)
not only to be consistent but be such that the probability of
J(\theta,x)
being larger than
\widehat{J}_{T}(x)
is very small. This ensures that with high probability the estimated constraint region,
\{x:\widehat{J}_{T}(x)\leqslant c\}
, is an accurate representation of actual constraint region,
\mathcal{E}_{c}
, and is in fact contained in it for large
T
.

In other words, in situations like above we really need an upper confidence bound
\widehat{J}_{T}
for
J(\theta)
, which, besides being a consistent estimator of
J(\theta)
, will ensure that
\mathbb{P}_{\theta}(J(\theta)>\widehat{J}_{T})
is small – exponentially small in our framework; that is, for a given rate of decay
r
and speed
b_{T}\rightarrow\infty
, we seek an estimator
\widehat{J}_{T}\equiv\widehat{J}_{T,r}

\mathbb{P}_{\theta}\left(J(\theta)\notin(-\infty,\widehat{J}_{T}]\right)=\mathbb{P}_{\theta}\left(J(\theta)>\widehat{J}_{T}\right)=O(e^{-rb_{T}}).

Rigorously stated, the upper bound needs to ensure that

\limsup_{T\to\infty}\frac{1}{b_{T}}\log\mathbb{P}_{\theta}\left(J(\theta)>\widehat{J}_{T}\right)\leqslant-r\quad\forall\theta\in\Theta.

(4)

But
\widehat{J}_{T}\equiv J(\widehat{\theta}_{T})
does not satisfy (
4
). To see this first note that the following trichotomy holds:

\lim_{T\to\infty}\mathbb{P}_{\theta}\left(J(\theta)>J(\widehat{\theta}_{T})+\kappa\right)=\begin{cases}1,&\quad\text{ if }\kappa<0\\
\frac{1}{2},&\quad\text{ if }\kappa=0\\
0,&\quad\text{ if }\kappa>0.\end{cases}

(5)

Here the first and the last equality simply follow from consistency of
J(\widehat{\theta}_{T})
, the second requires the additional assumption that
\widehat{\theta}_{T}
satisfies a CLT (with respect to
\mathbb{P}_{\theta}
, for every
\theta
) with some asymptotic covariance matrix
S(\theta)
and
J
is continuous. Indeed in this case

\lim_{T\to\infty}\mathbb{P}_{\theta}\left(J(\theta)>J(\widehat{\theta}_{T})\right)=\lim_{T\to\infty}\mathbb{P}_{\theta}\left(\sqrt{T}(J(\widehat{\theta}_{T})-J(\theta))<0\right)=\frac{1}{2}

and consequently for
\kappa\leqslant 0
,

\displaystyle\limsup\limits_{T\to\infty}\frac{1}{b_{T}}\log\mathbb{P}_{\theta}\left(J(\theta)>J(\widehat{\theta}_{T})+\kappa\right)=0.

While adding a fixed bias
\kappa
to
J(\widehat{\theta}_{T})
may result in the exponential decay of error probabilities, the desirable property of consistency
(iii)
is lost. Even if we decide to forgo consistency, it is not clear what an allowable size of
\kappa
representing the fixed or asymptotic bias is for a given problem.

CLT Approach:
It is thus imperative that the parameter
\kappa\equiv\kappa_{T}
is scaled with time
T
no matter which lens we use to view the problem. Indeed for the interval estimation, a natural approach is to search for CIs of the form
[J(\widehat{\theta}_{T})-\kappa_{T},J(\widehat{\theta}_{T})+\kappa_{T}]
(or
(-\infty,J(\widehat{\theta}_{T})+\kappa_{T}]
for the one-sided upper CI problem). A common way to achieve an asymptotic level
(1-\alpha)
confidence interval for a
fixed
level
\alpha
is through the CLT. Assume that
\widehat{\theta}_{T}
satisfies a CLT with limiting covariance matrix
S(\theta)
, that is,
\sqrt{T}(\widehat{\theta}_{T}-\theta)\overset{d}{\longrightarrow}\mathcal{N}(0,S(\theta))
(w.r.t.
\mathbb{P}_{\theta}
). If
J
is continuously differentiable at the point
\theta
, then standard laws of probability guarantee that

\frac{\sqrt{T}(J(\widehat{\theta}_{T})-J(\theta))}{\sqrt{\nabla J(\widehat{\theta}_{T})^{\top}S(\widehat{\theta}_{T})\nabla J(\widehat{\theta}_{T})}}\overset{d}{\longrightarrow}\mathcal{N}(0,1).

Consequently, a central limit confidence interval (CLT-CI) of asymptotic level
\alpha
is given by

\mathcal{I}^{\mathsf{CLT}}_{T,\alpha}(\widehat{\theta}_{T})=\Big[J(\widehat{\theta}_{T})-\kappa^{\mathsf{CLT}}_{T}(\alpha),J(\widehat{\theta}_{T})+\kappa^{\mathsf{CLT}}_{T}(\alpha)\Big],

(6)

with

\kappa^{\mathsf{CLT}}_{T}(\alpha)\equiv\Phi^{-1}(1-\alpha/2)\sqrt{\nabla J(\widehat{\theta}_{T})^{\top}S(\widehat{\theta}_{T})\nabla J(\widehat{\theta}_{T})}/\sqrt{T},

where
\Phi
is the distribution function of a standard normal random variable. This guarantees that
\lim_{T\rightarrow\infty}\mathbb{P}_{\theta}(J(\theta)\notin\mathcal{I}^{\mathsf{CLT}}_{T,\alpha}(\widehat{\theta}_{T}))\leqslant\alpha
. But a CLT-based interval does not lead to the desired exponential decay of the error probability, although a practitioner can intuitively use
\alpha=e^{-rb_{T}}
with the hope that the resulting CI,
\mathcal{I}^{\mathsf{CLT}}_{T,\alpha=e^{-rb_{T}}}(\widehat{\theta}_{T})
, at least satisfies the
exponentially high confidence estimate

(i)
in the introduction, i.e.,
\mathbb{P}_{\theta}(J(\theta)\notin\mathcal{I}^{\mathsf{CLT}}_{T,\alpha=e^{-rb_{T}}}(\widehat{\theta}_{T}))=O(e^{-rb_{T}})
, if not
(ii)
. However, this is purely a heuristic technique and is not mathematically guaranteed to work. The level
\alpha
needs to be
constant
(with respect to time) for the CLT-CI in (
6
) — one cannot simply substitute
\alpha=e^{-rb_{T}}
and rigorously justify that the above
high confidence estimate
holds for
\mathcal{I}^{\mathsf{CLT}}_{T,\alpha}(\widehat{\theta}_{T})
.
Moreover, achieving the guarantees
(iv)
or
(v)
via this CLT-CI remains entirely unclear.

The reason that the above method does not assure exponential decay of error probabilities is because the CLT is meant to look at deviations near the mean which are of order
O(1/\sqrt{T})
. This is not where exponential decay occurs; it occurs when the deviations from the mean are larger - of the order
O(1/a_{T})
with
a_{T}\ll\sqrt{T}
. It is thus natural that a rigorous methodology for constructing the optimal interval estimators satisfying the properties
(i)
-
(v)
needs to be based on large deviation analysis rather than the CLT. As customary, we will reserve the phrase
large deviation principle (LDP)
when
a_{T}=1
and
moderate deviation principle (MDP)
(which also falls under the realm of large deviation analysis) when
1\ll a_{T}\ll\sqrt{T}
.
This is, however, easier said than done and many of the notions involving optimality need to be carefully developed.

\mathcal{I}^{\mathsf{CLT}}_{T,\alpha=e^{-rb_{T}}}(\widehat{\theta}_{T})
with our optimal CI in Figure
1
.

Example 1

(Estimation of asymptotic variance of an Ornstein-Uhlenbeck process)
.

Consider an Ornstein-Uhlenbeck (OU) process defined as the SDE

\mathrm{d}X_{t}=-\theta X_{t}\mathrm{d}t+\mathrm{d}W_{t},\quad X_{0}=0,

(7)

where
\theta\in\Theta=\mathbb{R}_{>0}
is an unknown drift parameter and
W
a Brownian motion
independent of
X_{0}
. Our goal is to find a CI for the asymptotic variance of the process (see
[
43
, Example 6.8]
) given by

J(\theta)=\lim_{T\to\infty}\frac{1}{T}\sum_{t=0}^{T-1}\left(\mathbb{E}_{\theta}[X_{t}^{2}]-\mathbb{E}_{\theta}[X_{t}]^{2}\right)=\frac{1}{2\theta}.

(8)

We base the estimation of the CI on the maximum likelihood estimator (MLE)
\widehat{\theta}_{T}\in\mathcal{V}=\mathbb{R}
, which is constructed from a single trajectory of data
\{X_{t}\}_{t\in[0,T]}
by

\widehat{\theta}_{T}=-\frac{\int_{0}^{T}X_{t}\mathrm{d}X_{t}}{\int_{0}^{T}X^{2}_{t}\mathrm{d}t}=-\frac{X_{T}^{2}-T}{2\int_{0}^{T}X^{2}_{t}\mathrm{d}t}.

(9)

Here the second equality, easily obtained through stochastic integration by parts, describes an alternative computable expression of the MLE without the stochastic integral (see
[
44
, Remark 2.1]
).
Our MDP based approach, which we will describe shortly, in this case leads to the confidence interval

\mathcal{I}^{\star}_{T,r}(\widehat{\theta}_{T})\!=\![J(\widehat{\theta}_{T})\!+\!\kappa^{-}_{T},J(\widehat{\theta}_{T})\!+\!\kappa^{+}_{T}],\quad\kappa^{\pm}_{T}=\frac{1}{2\widehat{\theta}_{T}^{2}}\left(r_{T}\pm\sqrt{r_{T}^{2}+2\widehat{\theta}_{T}r_{T}}\right),

(10)

where
r_{T}=rb_{T}/T
.
The length of this interval is
\sqrt{rb_{T}/T(2\widehat{\theta}_{T}+rb_{T}/T)}/\widehat{\theta}_{T}^{2}=O\left(\sqrt{b_{T}/T}\right)
. The details of this calculation will be presented in Example
2
, but here it is instructive to compare this interval with the heuristic CLT-CI,
\mathcal{I}^{\mathsf{CLT}}_{T,\alpha=e^{-rb_{T}}}(\widehat{\theta}_{T})=[J(\widehat{\theta}_{T})-\kappa^{\mathsf{CLT}}_{T}(e^{-rb_{T}}),J(\widehat{\theta}_{T})+\kappa^{\mathsf{CLT}}_{T}(e^{-rb_{T}})]
, even though we know that in general it is not theoretically guaranteed to give the exponential decay.

It is known that the MLE (
9
) satisfies the following CLT,
\sqrt{T}(\widehat{\theta}_{T}-\theta)\stackrel{{\scriptstyle d}}{{\longrightarrow}}\mathcal{N}(0,\frac{1}{2\theta})
as
T\rightarrow\infty
,
[
5
]
. Thus the heuristic CLT-CI corresponding to
O(e^{-rb_{T}})
-decay for error probability is given as in (
6
) with

\kappa^{\mathsf{CLT}}_{T}(\alpha)\equiv\frac{\Phi^{-1}(1-\alpha/2)}{2^{3/2}\widehat{\theta}_{T}^{5/2}\sqrt{T}},\qquad\alpha=e^{-rb_{T}}.

(11)

Therefore, the length of this interval
{\mathcal{I}}^{\mathsf{CLT}}_{T,\alpha=e^{-rb_{T}}}(\widehat{\theta}_{T})
in (
6
) scales as

2\kappa^{\mathsf{CLT}}_{T}(\alpha)=2\frac{\Phi^{-1}(1-\alpha/2)}{2^{3/2}\widehat{\theta}_{T}^{5/2}\sqrt{T}}=2\frac{|\Phi^{-1}(\alpha/2)|}{2^{3/2}\widehat{\theta}_{T}^{5/2}\sqrt{T}}=O(\sqrt{b_{T}/T}),

(12)

where the second equality used the symmetry of the standard normal density and the third equality used the approximation,
\Phi^{-1}(\alpha)\approx\sqrt{-2\log(\alpha)}
for small
\alpha
(see
[
62
]
), here
\alpha=e^{-rb_{T}}
. Thus the lengths of both the MDP based optimal CI (
10
) and the heuristic CLT-CI (
11
) appear to be of the same order, but it does not tell the whole story – the difference comes down to the constant factors hidden in
O(\sqrt{b_{T}/T})
. The MDP based interval being minimal by construction will eventually be smaller than any other interval satisfying the same exponential decay. Thus even if
\mathcal{I}^{\mathsf{CLT}}_{T,\alpha=e^{-rb_{T}}}(\widehat{\theta}_{T})
gives exponential decay it is bound to be eventually larger than the MDP-CI. Since both the intervals in this case are symmetric around
J(\theta)
, we can simply check this by comparing the lengths of the two intervals. Indeed simulation of the two intervals - graphically visualized in Figure
1
confirms this. Figure
2
visualizes the trichonometry described in (
5
).

3
Mathematical framework, problem description and main results

Let
X
be an
\{\mathcal{F}_{t}\}
-adapted stochastic process on a filtered probability space
(\Omega,\mathcal{F},\{\mathcal{F}_{t}\},\mathbb{P})
with state space
\mathcal{S}\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}
.
We denote the
\sigma
-algebra generated by the process
X_{[0,T]}
by
\mathcal{F}_{T}\subset\mathcal{F}
. We assume that the probability measure
\mathbb{P}
belongs to a parametrized family
\mathcal{P}=\{\mathbb{P}_{\theta}\ :\ \theta\in\Theta\}
. As mentioned in the introduction our optimal interval estimator of the cost function
J(\theta)
will be based upon a given estimator
\{\widehat{\theta}_{T}\}
. The assumptions on the parameter space
\Theta
and the estimator
\{\widehat{\theta}_{T}\}
will be mentioned shortly.

An
estimator

\widehat{\theta}=\{\widehat{\theta}_{T}\}
is simply a stochastic process with state space
\mathcal{V}
adapted to the filtration
\{\mathcal{F}_{T}\}
. Recall that
\widehat{\theta}
is an
weakly consistent
estimator for
\theta_{0}\in\Theta
if
\widehat{\theta}_{T}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mathbb{P}_{\theta_{0}}}}{{\rightarrow}}\theta_{0}
as
T\rightarrow\infty
and is
strongly consistent
if the convergence is
\mathbb{P}_{\theta_{0}}
-a.s.
In many situations an estimator
\widehat{\theta}=\{\widehat{\theta}_{T}\}
might not take values in the parameter space
\Theta
at least for all
T>0
, even when it is consistent. With this in mind we introduce the following assumption.

Assumption 3.1
.

There exists a normed vector space
\mathcal{V}
equipped with a norm
\left\|\cdot\right\|
such that

(i)

\Theta\subset\mathcal{V}
; the state space of
\widehat{\theta}
is
\mathcal{V}
, i.e.,
\widehat{\theta}_{T}
is a
\mathcal{V}
-valued random variable for all
T>0
;

(ii)

either

(a)

\Theta\subset\mathcal{V}
is open, or

(b)

the estimator
\widehat{\theta}_{T}
takes values in
\Theta
for all
T>0
.

The topology on
\mathcal{V}
is the one induced by the norm
\left\|\cdot\right\|
. Note that we are not assuming that
\left\|\cdot\right\|
is complete, that is,
\mathcal{V}
might not be Banach.

This section will formalize the main problem of interest by rigorously defining the notions of exponential accuracy, minimality and other optimality criteria described in
(i)
-
(v)
in the introduction. This in turn requires a few definitions.

Definition 3.2

(Eventually smaller)
.

Let
\mathcal{W}
be a metric space and
\mathcal{M}(\mathcal{W},\mathbb{R})
the class of measurable functions from
\mathcal{W}
to
\mathbb{R}
. A family of functions

\{\phi_{T}:T>0\}\subset\mathcal{M}(\mathcal{W},\mathbb{R})
is called

(i)

eventually smaller
than another family
\{\psi_{T}:T>0\}\subset\mathcal{M}(\mathcal{W},\mathbb{R})

at
w\in\mathcal{W}
if

\displaystyle\limsup_{T\rightarrow\infty}(\phi_{T}(w)-\psi_{T}(w))\leqslant 0;

(ii)

eventually smaller
than
\{\psi_{T}:T>0\}\subset\mathcal{M}(\mathcal{W},\mathbb{R})
if the former is eventually smaller than the latter at all
w\in\mathcal{W}
;

(iii)

eventually and uniformly smaller
than
\{\psi_{T}:T>0\}
on
compact sets
if for any compact subset
\mathcal{W}_{0}\subset\mathcal{W}
,

\displaystyle\limsup_{T\rightarrow\infty}\sup_{w\in\mathcal{W}_{0}}(\phi_{T}(w)-\psi_{T}(w))\leqslant 0.

Definition 3.3

(Minimal interval-family)
.

Let
\mathcal{W}
be a metric space, and
\mathfrak{F}_{0}(\mathcal{W},\mathbb{R})
and
\mathfrak{F}_{1}(\mathcal{W},\mathbb{R})
two collections of function-families from
\mathcal{W}
to
\mathbb{R}
. Denote by

\displaystyle\mathbb{I}(\mathfrak{F}_{0},\mathfrak{F}_{1},\mathcal{W})\equiv

\displaystyle\ \big\{\{\mathcal{I}_{T}=[\ell_{T},u_{T}]:T>0\}:\{\ell_{T}:T>0\}\in\mathfrak{F}_{0}(\mathcal{W},\mathbb{R}),

\displaystyle\hskip 14.22636pt\{u_{T}:T>0\}\in\mathfrak{F}_{1}(\mathcal{W},\mathbb{R})\big\}

the collections of bounded interval-families whose lower and upper end points are defined by function-families from
\mathfrak{F}_{0}(\mathcal{W},\mathbb{R})
and
\mathfrak{F}_{1}(\mathcal{W},\mathbb{R})
.
Let
\mathbb{I}_{0}(\mathfrak{F}_{0},\mathfrak{F}_{1},\mathcal{W})\subset\mathbb{I}(\mathfrak{F}_{0},\mathfrak{F}_{1},\mathcal{W})
.

(i)

An interval-family
\left\{\mathcal{I}^{\star}_{T}=[\ell^{\star}_{T},u^{\star}_{T}]:T>0\right\}\in\mathbb{I}_{0}(\mathfrak{F}_{0},\mathfrak{F}_{1},\mathcal{W})
is a
pointwise minimal interval-family
of the subcollection
\mathbb{I}_{0}(\mathfrak{F}_{0},\mathfrak{F}_{1},\mathcal{W})
, if for any other interval-family
\{\mathcal{I}_{T}=[\ell_{T},u_{T}]:T>0\}\in\mathbb{I}_{0}(\mathfrak{F}_{0},\mathfrak{F}_{1},\mathcal{W})
the family
\{\ell^{\star}_{T}:T>0\}
is eventually larger than
\{\ell_{T}:T>0\}
and
\{u^{\star}_{T}:T>0\}
is eventually smaller than
\left\{u_{T}:T>0\right\}
.

(ii)

An interval-family
\left\{\mathcal{I}^{\star}_{T}=[\ell^{\star}_{T},u^{\star}_{T}]:T>0\right\}\in\mathbb{I}_{0}(\mathfrak{F}_{0},\mathfrak{F}_{1},\mathcal{W})
is a
locally uniform minimal interval-family
of the subcollection
\mathbb{I}_{0}(\mathfrak{F}_{0},\mathfrak{F}_{1},\mathcal{W})
, if for any other interval-family
\{\mathcal{I}_{T}=[\ell_{T},u_{T}]:T>0\}\in\mathbb{I}_{0}(\mathfrak{F}_{0},\mathfrak{F}_{1},\mathcal{W})
the family
\{\ell^{\star}_{T}:T>0\}
is eventually and uniformly larger than
\{\ell_{T}:T>0\}
on compact sets of
\mathcal{W}
and
\{u^{\star}_{T}:T>0\}
is eventually and uniformly smaller than
\left\{u_{T}:T>0\right\}
on compact sets of
\mathcal{W}
.

(iii)

If
\widetilde{\mathcal{W}}\subset\mathcal{W}
and
\left\{\mathcal{I}^{\star}_{T}=[\ell^{\star}_{T},u^{\star}_{T}]:T>0\right\}\in\mathbb{I}_{0}(\mathfrak{F}_{0},\mathfrak{F}_{1},\mathcal{W})
, then
\left\{\mathcal{I}^{\star}_{T}\big|_{\widetilde{\mathcal{W}}}\right\}
, the interval-family restricted to
\widetilde{\mathcal{W}}
, is a
pointwise minimal interval-family
of
\mathbb{I}_{0}(\mathfrak{F}_{0},\mathfrak{F}_{1},\mathcal{W})\big|_{\widetilde{\mathcal{W}}}
if for any other family of intervals
\left\{\mathcal{I}_{T}=[\ell_{T},u_{T}]:T>0\right\}

\in\mathbb{I}_{0}(\mathfrak{F}_{0},\mathfrak{F}_{1},\mathcal{W})
, the family
\left\{\ell^{\star}_{T}\big|_{\widetilde{\mathcal{W}}}:T>0\right\}
is eventually larger than
\left\{\ell_{T}\big|_{\widetilde{\mathcal{W}}}:T>0\right\}
and
\left\{u^{\star}_{T}\big|_{\widetilde{\mathcal{W}}}:T>0\right\}
is eventually smaller than
\left\{u_{T}\big|_{\widetilde{\mathcal{W}}}:T>0\right\}.

An interval-family
\left\{\mathcal{I}^{\star}_{T}\big|_{\widetilde{\mathcal{W}}}\right\}
will be called a
locally uniform minimal interval-family
of
\mathbb{I}_{0}(\mathfrak{F}_{0},\mathfrak{F}_{1},\mathcal{W})\big|_{\widetilde{\mathcal{W}}}
if for any other family of intervals
\left\{\mathcal{I}_{T}=[\ell_{T},u_{T}]:T>0\right\}

\in\mathbb{I}_{0}(\mathfrak{F}_{0},\mathfrak{F}_{1},\mathcal{W})
, the family
\left\{\ell^{\star}_{T}\big|_{\widetilde{\mathcal{W}}}:T>0\right\}
is uniformly and eventually larger than
\left\{\ell_{T}\big|_{\widetilde{\mathcal{W}}}:T>0\right\}
on compact sets of
\widetilde{\mathcal{W}}
and
\left\{u^{\star}_{T}\big|_{\widetilde{\mathcal{W}}}:T>0\right\}
is uniformly and eventually smaller than
\left\{u_{T}\big|_{\widetilde{\mathcal{W}}}:T>0\right\}
on compact sets of
\widetilde{\mathcal{W}}
.

If
\mathfrak{F}_{0}=\mathfrak{F}_{1}
, we will use the symbol
\mathbb{I}(\mathfrak{F}_{0},\mathcal{W})
instead of
\mathbb{I}(\mathfrak{F}_{0},\mathfrak{F}_{1},\mathcal{W})
for simplicity.
In a similar way, collections of one-sided lower and upper bounded interval-families,
\mathbb{I}^{L}(\mathfrak{F}_{0},\mathcal{W})
and
\mathbb{I}^{U}(\mathfrak{F}_{1},\mathcal{W})
as well as pointwise and locally uniform minimal interval-families in some subcollections
\mathbb{I}^{L}_{0}(\mathfrak{F}_{0},\mathcal{W})
and
\mathbb{I}^{U}_{0}(\mathfrak{F}_{1},\mathcal{W})
and their restrictions on a subset
\widetilde{\mathcal{W}}
can be defined.

The following definition considers a limiting version of the classical equicontinuity. Slight variants of this notion have been used under different names, e.g.,
asymptotic equicontinuity

[
60
, Section 5]
. We introduce it here as
eventual equicontinuity
.

Definition 3.4

(Eventually equicontinuous)
.

(i)

A family of functions
\{\phi_{T}:\mathcal{W}\rightarrow\mathbb{R},T>0\}
is called
eventually equi-upper semicontinuous
(ev-equi-u.s.c.) at
w_{0}
, if
\limsup\limits_{T\rightarrow\infty,w\rightarrow w_{0}}\left(\phi_{T}(w)-\phi_{T}(w_{0})\right)\leqslant 0
. Equivalently, for every
\varepsilon>0
, there exist
\delta=\delta(w_{0},\varepsilon)>0
and
T_{0}=T_{0}(w_{0},\varepsilon)>0
such that for all
w\in B(w_{0},\delta)
and
T\geqslant T_{0}
,
\phi_{T}(w)-\phi_{T}(w_{0})<\varepsilon.

The family is called
ev-equi-u.s.c.
if it is ev-equi-u.s.c. at all
w\in\mathcal{W}
.

(ii)

A family of functions
\{\phi_{T}:\mathcal{W}\rightarrow\mathbb{R},T>0\}
is called
eventually equicontinuous at
w_{0}
, if
\limsup\limits_{T\rightarrow\infty,w\rightarrow w_{0}}\left|\phi_{T}(w)-\phi_{T}(w_{0})\right|=0
. Equivalently, for every
\varepsilon>0
, there exist
\delta=\delta(w_{0},\varepsilon)>0
and
T_{0}=T_{0}(w_{0},\varepsilon)>0
such that for all
w\in B(w_{0},\delta)
and
T\geqslant T_{0}
,
\left|\phi_{T}(w)-\phi_{T}(w_{0})\right|<\varepsilon.

The family is called eventually equicontinuous if it is eventually equicontinuous at all
w\in\mathcal{W}
.

(iii)

A family
\{\phi_{T}:\mathcal{W}\rightarrow\mathbb{R},T>0\}
is eventually equi-lower semicontinuous (ev-equi-l.s.c.) if
\{-\phi_{T}:T>0\}
is ev-equi-u.s.c.

For a metric space

As mentioned in the introduction we search for optimal CI among the class of intervals which are exponentially accurate in the sense that the associated error probability decays exponentially with a certain rate and speed, see

Definition 3.5

(Exponentially accurate)
.

Let
r>0
and
b_{T}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle T\rightarrow\infty}}{{\longrightarrow}}\infty
. An interval-family

is called

with error rate

, if

\displaystyle\limsup\limits_{T\to\infty}\frac{1}{b_{T}}\log\mathbb{P}_{\theta}\left(J(\theta)\notin\mathcal{I}_{T,r}(\widehat{\theta}_{T})\right)\leqslant-r,\quad\forall\theta\in\Theta\subset\mathcal{V}.

(13)

Denote by

the collection of interval-families
\{\mathcal{I}_{T,r}\equiv[\underaccent{\bar}{H}_{T,r},\bar{H}_{T,r}]:T>0\}
with both
\{\underaccent{\bar}{H}_{T,r}:T>0\},\{\bar{H}_{T,r}:T>0\}\in\mathcal{E}(\mathcal{V},\mathbb{R})
which are exponentially accurate with error rate
r
and speed
b_{T}
(that is, which satisfy (

)). Similarly,
\mathbb{I}_{r}(\mathcal{L},\mathcal{U},\mathcal{V})
will denote the subcollection of interval-families from
\mathbb{I}(\mathcal{L},\mathcal{U},\mathcal{V})
that are exponentially accurate with error rate

and speed

.

A common criterion to characterize the “optimal" confidence interval among those with fixed error-threshold is the notion of
uniformly most accurate
(UMA) confidence intervals. We consider an appropriately adjusted asymptotic version of this concept for our problem.

Definition 3.6

(Eventually UMA)
.

Fix an exponential rate parameter
r>0
and a speed of decay
\{b_{T}:T>0\}
. An interval family
\{\widetilde{\mathcal{I}}_{T,r}:T>0\}\in\mathbb{I}_{r}(\mathcal{E},\mathcal{V})
is called
eventually UMA
in the collection
\mathbb{I}_{r}(\mathcal{E},\mathcal{V})
, if for any other interval family
\{\mathcal{I}_{T,r}:T>0\}\in\mathbb{I}_{r}(\mathcal{E},\mathcal{V})
,

\limsup_{T\to\infty}\left[\mathbb{P}_{\theta}\left(J(\tilde{\theta})\in\widetilde{\mathcal{I}}_{T,r}(\widehat{\theta}_{T})\right)-\mathbb{P}_{\theta}\left(J(\tilde{\theta})\in\mathcal{I}_{T,r}(\widehat{\theta}_{T})\right)\right]\leqslant 0\quad\forall\theta,\tilde{\theta}\in\Theta:J(\tilde{\theta})\neq J(\theta).

Intuitively, in the long run confidence intervals with small probabilities of covering the wrong function value
J(\tilde{\theta})
are preferred.
Finally, we can precisely state the key problem tackled in this paper.

Problem 3.7

Problem
3.7
is the rigorous formulation of the interval criteria
(i)
-
(v)
mentioned in the introduction.
Property
(d)
of Problem
3.7
, which is the mathematical formulation of the criteria
(iv)
, states that the probability of the “wrong value" of the cost function

falling outside our confidence interval does not decay at an exponential rate. This is of course a natural criterion for optimality - we want the CI

does not exceed the given confidence

in the literature, for example, see
[

We next need the following Arzela-Ascoli type result adapted for eventual equicontinuity. The proof essentially follows the same steps used to prove the original Arzela-Ascoli theorem with some obvious adjustments and is given in the Appendix for sake of completeness.

Lemma 4.7
(Arzela-Ascoli for eventual equicontinuity).

Let
\mathcal{W}
be a metric space. Suppose that the function-family

\displaystyle\lim_{T\rightarrow\infty}\sup_{w\in\mathcal{W}_{0}}|\phi_{T}(w)-\phi_{0}(w)|=0

-

(ii)

. Since

5.2
Cox-Ingersoll-Ross process

Consider a Cox–Ingersoll–Ross (CIR) process defined as the SDE

\mathrm{d}X_{t}=(\delta-\theta X_{t})\mathrm{d}t+\sigma\sqrt{X_{t}}\ \mathrm{d}W_{t},\quad X_{0}=0,

(36)

where
\delta>0
,
\sigma>0
are known parameters and
\theta\in\Theta=\mathbb{R}_{>0}
is unknown.
The goal is to build an estimator for the asymptotic variance of the process described as

\displaystyle\lim_{t\to\infty}\mathbb{E}[X_{t}]=\delta/\theta,\qquad\lim_{t\to\infty}\text{Var}[X_{t}]=\ \frac{\sigma^{2}\delta}{2\theta^{2}}\equiv J(\theta).

The confidence interval for the asymptotic variance
J(\theta)
is built via the MLE for the unknown parameter
\theta
given by

\widehat{\theta}_{T}=\frac{\delta T-X_{T}}{\int_{0}^{T}X_{t}\mathrm{d}t},

where
\widehat{\theta}_{T}\in\mathcal{V}=\mathbb{R}
. In this setting, clearly Assumption
3.1
holds.
As for the Ornstein-Uhlenbeck process in Example
1
, a heuristic derivation of a confidence interval can be obtained via the CLT. Recall that the MLE
\widehat{\theta}_{T}
satisfies the following CLT
[
56
]

\displaystyle\sqrt{T}(\widehat{\theta}_{T}-\theta)\overset{d}{\longrightarrow}\mathcal{N}(0,S(\theta)\equiv\sigma^{2}\theta/\delta).

Notice that
J^{\prime}(\theta)^{2}S(\theta)=\sigma^{6}\delta/\theta^{5}.

Thus by (
6
), the CLT-CI of level
\alpha
is given by

\displaystyle{\mathcal{I}}^{\mathsf{CLT}}_{T,\alpha}(\widehat{\theta}_{T})=\Big[J(\widehat{\theta}_{T})-\kappa^{\mathsf{CLT}}_{T}(\alpha),J(\widehat{\theta}_{T})+\kappa^{\mathsf{CLT}}_{T}(\alpha)\Big],

(37)

with

\kappa^{\mathsf{CLT}}_{T}(\alpha)\equiv\frac{\sigma^{3}\delta^{1/2}\Phi^{-1}(1-\alpha/2)}{\widehat{\theta}_{T}^{5/2}\sqrt{T}}.

The heuristic CLT-CI (
37
) for
\alpha=e^{b_{T}T}
is compared with the optimal CI (
17
) proposed by Theorem
3.10
. To derive (
17
), note in a fist step that the MLE
\widehat{\theta}_{T}
satisfies an MDP.

Theorem 5.2
.

Let
\{a_{T}\}
be such that
a_{T}\rightarrow\infty
, and
b_{T}\equiv T/a_{T}^{2}\rightarrow\infty
. For any
\theta\in\Theta
the stochastic process
\{a_{T}(\widehat{\theta}_{T}-\theta)\}
satisfies an LDP with speed
b_{T}
and rate function
I_{\theta}^{M}:\mathcal{V}\to[0,\infty]
defined as

I^{M}_{\theta}(\vartheta)=\frac{\vartheta^{2}\delta}{2\sigma^{2}\theta}.

We introduce a change of variables by defining
Z_{t}=\frac{4}{\sigma^{2}}X_{t}
and note that in the new coordinates, the CIR process (
36
) can be written as

\mathsf{d}Z_{t}=\left(\frac{4\delta}{\sigma^{2}}-\theta Z_{t}\right)\mathsf{d}t+2\sqrt{Z_{t}}\mathsf{d}W_{t}.

(38)

In this form, it has been shown
[
33
]
that the estimator

\widehat{\theta}^{\prime}_{T}=\frac{Z_{T}-\frac{4\delta}{\sigma^{2}}T}{2\int_{0}^{T}Z_{t}\mathsf{d}t}

converges to
\theta^{\prime}=-\frac{1}{2}\theta
and
satisfies an MDP with rate function
I^{M}_{\theta^{\prime}}(\vartheta)=\frac{\vartheta^{2}2\delta}{\sigma^{2}\theta}
. By applying the change of coordinate again, we observe that

-2\widehat{\theta}^{\prime}_{T}=\frac{\frac{4\delta}{\sigma^{2}}T-Z_{T}}{\int_{0}^{T}Z_{t}\mathsf{d}t}=\frac{\delta T-X_{T}}{\int_{0}^{T}X_{t}\mathsf{d}t}=\widehat{\theta}_{T}.

The contraction principle states that
\widehat{\theta}_{T}
satisfies an MDP with rate function given as

I^{M}_{\theta}(\vartheta)=I^{M}_{\theta^{\prime}}\left(-\vartheta/2\right)=\frac{\vartheta^{2}\delta}{2\sigma^{2}\theta},

which completes the proof.
∎Theorem
5.2
ensures that Assumption
3.8
is satisfied and accordingly Theorem
3.10
can be applied (with
\delta=0
) quantifying an optimal interval estimator for
J(\theta)
. The corresponding interval (
17
) is expressed as

\displaystyle\bar{J}^{0}_{T,r}(\widehat{\theta}_{T})

\displaystyle=\max\limits_{\theta>0}\left\{\frac{\delta\sigma^{2}}{2\theta^{2}}\ :\ \frac{\delta(\widehat{\theta}_{T}-\theta)^{2}}{2\sigma^{2}\theta}\leqslant r_{T}\right\}

(39a)

\displaystyle=\max\limits_{\bar{\theta}>0}\left\{\frac{\delta\sigma^{2}}{2}\bar{\theta}^{2}\ :\ \bar{\theta}^{2}\widehat{\theta}_{T}^{2}+\bar{\theta}(-2\widehat{\theta}_{T}-\frac{2\sigma^{2}r_{T}}{\delta})+1\leqslant 0\right\}

and

\displaystyle\underaccent{\bar}{J}^{0}_{T,r}(\widehat{\theta}_{T})

\displaystyle=\min\limits_{\bar{\theta}>0}\left\{\frac{\delta\sigma^{2}}{2}\bar{\theta}^{2}\ :\ \bar{\theta}^{2}\widehat{\theta}_{T}^{2}+\bar{\theta}(-2\widehat{\theta}_{T}-\frac{2\sigma^{2}r_{T}}{\delta})+1\leqslant 0\right\},

(39b)

where
r_{T}=r/a_{T}^{2}
.
The optimization problems (
39

39

\widehat{\theta}_{T}<0

\widehat{\theta}_{T}\to\theta>0

If

\displaystyle\underaccent{\bar}{J}^{0}_{T,r}(\theta^{\prime})=\frac{\delta\sigma^{2}}{2(\theta^{-})^{2}},\qquad\bar{J}^{0}_{T,r}(\theta^{\prime})=\frac{\delta\sigma^{2}}{2(\theta^{+})^{2}},

\displaystyle\theta^{\pm}=\frac{1}{\theta^{\prime 2}}\left(\theta^{\prime}+\frac{\sigma^{2}r_{T}}{\delta}\pm\sqrt{\left(\theta^{\prime}+\sigma^{2}r_{T}/\delta\right)^{2}-\theta^{\prime 2}}\right).

5.3
Non-parametric i.i.d. model

Consider a setting where
\mathcal{S}\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}
is compact and where the samples
\{X_{t}\}_{t\in\mathbb{N}}\subset\mathcal{S}
are serially independent and distributed according to
\theta^{\star}\in\Theta
, for
\Theta=\mathcal{P}(\mathcal{S})
. The empirical probability distribution is given by

\widehat{\theta}_{T}=\frac{1}{T}\sum_{t=1}^{T}\delta_{X_{t}},

(40)

where
T\in\mathbb{N}
and model space is
\Theta=\mathcal{P}(\mathcal{S})
. We denote by

\mathcal{V}=\mathcal{M}(\mathcal{S})
the vector space of finite signed measures on
\mathcal{S}
. We equip
\mathcal{V}
with the Kantorovich-Rubinstein (KR) norm, and note that the topology on
\mathcal{P}(\mathcal{S})
induced by the KR-norm coincides with the topology of weak convergence
[
16
, Section 3.2]
.
Clearly Assumption
3.1
is satisfied.
For any
\theta\in\Theta
, introduce the function
I_{\theta}^{M}:\mathcal{M}(\mathcal{S})\to[0,\infty]
defined as

I_{\theta}^{M}(\vartheta)=\begin{cases}\frac{1}{2}\int_{\mathcal{S}}\left(\frac{\mathsf{d}\vartheta}{\mathsf{d}\theta}\right)^{2}\mathsf{d}\theta,&\quad\vartheta\ll\theta,\ \vartheta(\mathcal{S})=0\\
\infty,&\quad\text{o.w.}\end{cases}

(41)

Then
[
23
,
30
]
gives the MDP counterpart of Sanov’s Theorem which has actually been proved in the stronger
\tau
-topology.

Theorem 5.4
(Moderate deviations empirical measure—i.i.d. setting).

Let
X_{1},\ldots,X_{T}
be
\mathcal{S}
-valued i.i.d. random variables. Consider a sequence
\{a_{T}\}
such that
a_{T}\to\infty
and
b_{T}\equiv T/a^{2}_{T}\to\infty
. Let the estimator
\{\widehat{\theta}_{T}\}
be defined as in (
40
).
Then, the stochastic process
\{a_{T}(\widehat{\theta}_{T}-\theta)\}
satisfies an LDP with speed
\{b_{T}\}
and convex, good rate function (
41
).

Consider a function
J
expressing an expected loss

J(\theta)\equiv\mathbb{E}_{\theta}[\ell(X)]=\int_{\mathcal{S}}\ell(x)\mathsf{d}\theta(x),

(42)

where
\ell:\mathcal{S}\to\mathbb{R}
is some bounded loss function. Note that
J
is uniformly continuous and
Theorem
5.4
ensures that Assumption
3.8
holds. Therefore, Theorem
3.10
proposes to estimate
J(\theta)
via the interval family
\{\mathcal{I}^{\delta,\star}_{T,r}=[\underaccent{\bar}{J}^{\delta}_{T,r},\bar{J}^{\delta}_{T,r}]:T>0\}
given by (
16
). In this example, we need to consider
\delta>0
as the rate function (
41
) is not continuous. The functions
\underaccent{\bar}{J}^{\delta}_{T,r}
and
\bar{J}^{\delta}_{T,r}
according to (
16
) are described by convex optimization problems that are infinite-dimensional and therefore cannot be readily solved with a computer.
The functions
\underaccent{\bar}{J}^{\delta}_{T,r}
and
\bar{J}^{\delta}_{T,r}
for small
\delta
can be closely approximated by
\underaccent{\bar}{J}^{0}_{T,r}
and
\bar{J}^{0}_{T,r}
. Interestingly, we show in the following, that the functions
\underaccent{\bar}{J}^{0}_{T,r}
and
\bar{J}^{0}_{T,r}
can be equivalently expressed as tractable finite-dimensional optimization problems by exploiting duality of convex optimization
[
4
]
.

Proposition 5.5
(Computation of
\underaccent{\bar}{J}^{0}_{T,r}
and
\bar{J}^{0}_{T,r}
).

If
r>0
and
\bar{\ell}=\max_{t\in\{1,\dots T\}}\ell(X_{t})
denotes the worst-case loss function, then the two functions
\underaccent{\bar}{J}^{0}_{T,r}
and
\bar{J}^{0}_{T,r}
given by (
17
) admit the dual representations

\displaystyle\underaccent{\bar}{J}^{0}_{T,r}({\widehat{\theta}_{T}})

\displaystyle=-\min_{\alpha\in\mathcal{A}}\left\{\alpha-\frac{1}{2r_{T}+1}\left(\frac{1}{T}\sum_{t=1}^{T}\sqrt{\alpha+\ell(X_{t})}\right)^{2}\right\},

\displaystyle\bar{J}^{0}_{T,r}({\widehat{\theta}_{T}})

\displaystyle=\min_{\alpha\in\mathcal{B}}\left\{\alpha-\frac{1}{2r_{T}+1}\left(\frac{1}{T}\sum_{t=1}^{T}\sqrt{\alpha-\ell(X_{t})}\right)^{2}\right\},

where
\mathcal{A}=\{\alpha\in\mathbb{R}\ :\ \bar{\ell}\leqslant\alpha\leqslant\bar{\ell}(2r_{T}+1)/(2r_{T})+\frac{1}{2r_{T}}J({{\widehat{\theta}_{T}}})\}
,
\mathcal{B}=\{\alpha\in\mathbb{R}\ :\ \bar{\ell}\leqslant\alpha\leqslant\bar{\ell}(2r_{T}+1)/(2r_{T})-\frac{1}{2r_{T}}J({{\widehat{\theta}_{T}}})\}
and
r_{T}=r/a_{T}^{2}
.

The duality formula is shown for the upper bound
\bar{J}^{0}_{T,r}
as the lower bound
\underaccent{\bar}{J}^{0}_{T,r}
is derived analogously. To start, note that using the form of the rate function (
41
) the optimization problem (
17
) can be expressed as

\bar{J}^{0}_{T,r}({\widehat{\theta}_{T}})=\sup_{\theta\in\Theta}\left\{\mathbb{E}_{\theta}[\ell(X)]\ :\ \mathsf{D}_{\phi}(\theta,\widehat{\theta}_{T})\leqslant 2r_{T}\right\},

(43)

where
r_{T}=r/a_{T}^{2}
and
\mathsf{D}_{\phi}
denotes the
\phi
-divergence
[
57
, Chapter 8]
corresponding to the
\chi^{2}
-distance, i.e., for
\phi(t)=1/t\cdot(t-1)^{2}
. Note that the conjugate of
\phi
is given
\phi^{*}(s)=2-2\sqrt{1-s}
for
s<1

[
7
]
.
With this formulation, the dual convex program to (
43
) is expressed as
[
28
, Lemma 3]

\displaystyle\bar{J}^{0}_{T,r}({\widehat{\theta}_{T}})

\displaystyle=\inf_{\lambda\geqslant 0,\eta\in\mathbb{R}}\left\{\mathbb{E}_{\widehat{\theta}_{T}}\left[\lambda\phi^{*}\left(\frac{\ell(X)-\eta}{\lambda}\right)\right]+2r_{T}\lambda+\eta\right\}

\displaystyle=\inf_{\lambda\geqslant 0,\eta\in\mathbb{R}}\left\{\eta+2r_{T}\lambda+\frac{\lambda}{T}\sum_{t=1}^{T}\left(2-2\sqrt{\frac{\lambda-\ell(X_{t})+\eta}{\lambda}}\right)\ :\ \frac{\bar{\ell}-\eta}{\lambda}\leqslant 1\right\}

\displaystyle=\min_{\alpha\geqslant\bar{\ell},\nu\geqslant 0}\left\{\alpha-\nu+2r_{T}\nu+\frac{\nu}{T}\sum_{t=1}^{T}\left(2-2\sqrt{\frac{\alpha-\ell(X_{t})}{\nu}}\right)\right\}

\displaystyle=\min_{\nu\geqslant 0,\alpha\geqslant\bar{\ell}}\left\{-2\sqrt{\nu}\frac{1}{T}\sum_{t=1}^{T}\sqrt{\alpha-\ell(X_{t})}+\alpha+\nu(2r_{T}+1)\right\},

where we have used the variable transformation
\alpha=\lambda+\eta
and
\nu=\lambda
.
We denote the objective function in the optimization problem above by
g(\alpha,\nu)
. It can be readily seen that
g
is jointly convex and continuous on
[\bar{\ell},\infty)\times[0,\infty)
. Moreover,
g(\alpha,0)=\alpha
and
\lim_{\nu\to\infty}g(\alpha,\nu)=\infty
for all
\alpha\geqslant\bar{\ell}
, hence for any
\alpha\geqslant\bar{\ell}
there exists
\nu^{\star}(\alpha)\in\arg\min_{\nu\geqslant 0}g(\alpha,\nu)
. Applying the first-order optimality conditions

\displaystyle\frac{\partial g(\alpha,\nu)}{\partial\nu}=-\frac{1}{\sqrt{\nu}}\frac{1}{T}\sum_{t=1}^{T}\sqrt{\alpha-\ell(X_{t})}+2r_{T}+1=0

\displaystyle\quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad\nu^{\star}(\alpha)=\frac{1}{(2r_{T}+1)^{2}}\left(\frac{1}{T}\sum_{t=1}^{T}\sqrt{\alpha-\ell(X_{t})}\right)^{2}.

Hence,

\displaystyle g(\alpha,\nu^{\star}(\alpha))=\frac{-1}{(2r_{T}+1)}\left(\frac{1}{T}\sum_{t=1}^{T}\sqrt{\alpha-\ell(X_{t})}\right)^{2}+\alpha.

(44)

Equation (
44
) implies that
g(\alpha,\nu^{\star}(\alpha))\leqslant\alpha
for all
\alpha
and therefore

\displaystyle g(\alpha^{\star},\nu^{\star}(\alpha^{\star}))\leqslant\bar{\ell}.

(45)

Moreover, applying Jensen’s inequality to (
44
) gives the lower bound

\displaystyle g(\alpha,\nu^{\star}(\alpha))\geqslant\frac{-1}{(2r_{T}+1)}\left(\frac{1}{T}\sum_{t=1}^{T}(\alpha-\ell(X_{t}))\right)+\alpha\qquad\forall\alpha.

(46)

Finally, combining (
45
) and (
46
) for
\alpha=\alpha^{\star}
leads to

\displaystyle\frac{-1}{(2r_{T}+1)}\left(\frac{1}{T}\sum_{t=1}^{T}(\alpha^{\star}-\ell(X_{t}))\right)+\alpha^{\star}\leqslant\bar{\ell}

\displaystyle\quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad\alpha^{\star}\frac{2r_{T}}{2r_{T}+1}\leqslant\bar{\ell}-\frac{1}{2r_{T}+1}J({\widehat{\theta}_{T}}),

where we used the fact that
J({\widehat{\theta}_{T}})=\frac{1}{T}\sum_{t=1}^{T}\ell(X_{t})
. Hence,

\displaystyle\alpha^{\star}\leqslant\frac{2r_{T}+1}{2r_{T}}\bar{\ell}-\frac{1}{2r_{T}}J(\widehat{\theta}_{T}),

which completes the proof.
∎

Remark 5.6

(Finite state setting—comparison to
[
9
]
)
.

In the special setting where the random variables are finitely supported, i.e.,
\mathcal{S}=\{1,\dots,d\}
, the upper confidence bound (
17
) reduces to

\displaystyle\bar{J}^{0}_{T,r}(\widehat{\theta}_{T})=\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}\sup\limits_{\theta\in\Theta}&J(\theta)\\
\mathrm{s.t.}&\frac{1}{2}\sum_{i=1}^{d}\frac{(\widehat{\theta}_{T}-\theta)_{i}^{2}}{\theta_{i}}\leqslant\frac{r}{a_{T}^{2}}.\end{array}\right.

In the finite state i.i.d. setting, the recent work
[
9
]
considers a slightly modified optimality criteria. Namely, a notion similar to that of eventually smaller (see Definition
3.2
), where a family of functions
\{\phi_{T}:T>0\}\subset\mathcal{F}(\Theta,\mathbb{R})
is defined to be ”eventually smaller" than another family
\{\psi_{T}:T>0\}\subset\mathcal{F}(\Theta,\mathbb{R})
if

\limsup_{T\to\infty}\left|\frac{\phi_{T}(\theta)-f(\theta)}{\psi_{T}(\theta)-f(\theta)}\right|\leqslant 1\quad\forall\theta\in\Theta,

for some fixed function
f:\Theta\to\mathbb{R}
. Under this modified optimality criteria,
[
9
]
shows that the optimal upper confidence bound is a modified DRO predictor

\displaystyle\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}\sup\limits_{\theta\in\Theta}&J(\theta)\\
\mathrm{s.t.}&\frac{1}{2}\sum_{i=1}^{d}\frac{(\widehat{\theta}_{T}-\theta)_{i}^{2}}{(\widehat{\theta}_{T})_{i}}\leqslant\frac{r}{a_{T}^{2}}.\end{array}\right.

Remark 5.7

(Stochastic Programming)
.

The setting here can actually be generalized, where instead of estimating
J(\theta)
of the form (
42
), we consider a general stochastic program, i.e.,
J(\theta)
given by (
1

L

J(\theta)=\min_{z\in\mathcal{Z}}\mathbb{E}_{\theta}[L(X,z)].

Then, the corresponding interval family is

\displaystyle\bar{J}_{T,r}^{0}(\theta^{\prime})

\displaystyle=\sup_{\theta\in\Theta}\bigg\{\min_{z\in\mathcal{Z}}\mathbb{E}_{\theta}[L(X,z)]:I_{\theta}^{M}(a_{T}(\theta^{\prime}-\theta))\leqslant r\bigg\}

6
Concluding reflections on decision making

As already indicated by (
1
) and Remark
5.7
, the framework proposed in this article can be extended to tackle data-driven decision making problems, more precisely to estimate the optimal value
J(\theta)
of a stochastic programming problem. With slight abuse of notation, we denote

J(\theta)=\min_{z\in\mathcal{Z}}J(\theta,z)=\min_{z\in\mathcal{Z}}\mathbb{E}_{\theta}[\ell(X,z)],

(47)

for
\mathcal{Z}\subset\mathbb{R}^{m}
. According to Remark
5.7
, our paper directly proposes optimal confidence intervals for
J(\theta)
. For future work, we aim to study the derivation of a pair comprising a decision
\widehat{z}_{T}\in\mathcal{Z}
and a corresponding CI
\widehat{\mathcal{I}}_{T}
with the goal that
J(\theta,\widehat{z}_{T})
is “close to" the optimal value
J(\theta)
and
\widehat{\mathcal{I}}_{T}
provides an exponentially accurate estimate for
J(\theta,\widehat{z}_{T})
, i.e.,

\limsup_{T\to\infty}\frac{1}{b_{T}}\log\mathbb{P}_{\theta}\left(J(\theta,\widehat{z}_{T})\notin\widehat{\mathcal{I}}_{T}\right)\leqslant-r\quad\forall\theta.

In addition, the CI
\widehat{\mathcal{I}}_{T}
must admit the other optimality criteria
(ii)
-
(v)
studied in this paper. Stochastic programming problems (
47
) are the key objects in supervised learning, and statistical learning theory
[
67
,
41
]
aims to quantify estimation guarantees for the corresponding optimizers.
Constructing optimal confidence intervals via MDP rate functions in the context of supervised learning problems has not been considered yet. This paper provides the methods and techniques for this goal.

Another class of problems that can be addressed as instances of (
47
) are offline (or batch) reinforcement learning problems
[
10
,
65
,
48
]
, where previously collected data from an unknown dynamical system is available and where one aims to answer the question of how this data can be utilized to estimate an optimal (or approximately optimal) policy.
Offline reinforcement learning is difficult as it requires the learning algorithm to derive a sufficient understanding of the underlying unknown dynamical system entirely from a fixed data set, i.e., without the possibility to interact further with the underlying dynamical system. When deriving confidence intervals for the learned offline policy, the key challenge is to systematically find a balance between increased generalization and avoiding unwanted
behaviors outside of the distribution, i.e., the available data.

A
Some definitions

For the following definitions, we will consider a generic stochastic process,
\Psi=\{\Psi_{T}\}
, defined on a probability space
(\Omega,\mathcal{F},\mathbb{P})
.

Definition A.1

(Large deviation principle)
.

A stochastic process
\Psi=\{\Psi_{T}\}
taking values in a metric space
\mathcal{W}
satisfies an LDP with a rate function
I^{L}:\mathcal{W}\to[0,\infty]
and speed
\{b_{T}\}
where
\lim_{T\to\infty}b_{T}=\infty
, if for all Borel sets
\mathcal{D}\subseteq\mathcal{W}
we have

\displaystyle-\inf_{s\in{\mathcal{D}}^{\circ}}\,I^{L}(s)

\displaystyle\leqslant\liminf_{T\to\infty}~\frac{1}{b_{T}}\log\mathbb{P}\left(\Psi_{T}\in\mathcal{D}\right)

(48)

\displaystyle\leqslant\limsup_{T\to\infty}~\frac{1}{b_{T}}\log\mathbb{P}\left(\Psi_{T}\in\mathcal{D}\right)\leqslant-\inf_{s\in\overline{\mathcal{D}}}\,I^{L}(s).

The rate function can be taken to be l.s.c., and under this assumption it is unique. A rate function
I^{L}
associated with an LDP is called
good
if its sublevel set,
\{w\in\mathcal{W}:I^{L}(w)\leqslant a\}
, is compact for each
a\geqslant 0
. The goodness property of rate function holds for large deviation asymptotics in a wide variety of models.
[
24
]
is a comprehensive source for the theory of large deviations.

Definition A.2

(Moderate deviation principle)
.

Let
\Psi=\{\Psi_{T}\}
be a stochastic process taking values in a metrizable topological vector space
\mathcal{V}
. Suppose that
\Psi_{T}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mathbb{P}}}{{\longrightarrow}}\psi_{0}
as
T\rightarrow\infty
for some deterministic
\psi_{0}\in\mathcal{V}
.

\Psi
satisfies a moderate deviation principle (MDP) with a rate function
I^{M}:\mathcal{V}\to[0,\infty]
and speed
b_{T}
, if
\{a_{T}(\Psi_{T}-\psi_{0})\}
with
a_{T}=\sqrt{T/b_{T}}
satisfies an LDP in
\mathcal{V}
with the rate function
I^{M}
and speed
\{b_{T}\}
.

B
Auxiliary Proofs

Fix an
\varepsilon>0
. First by uniform continuity of
\phi_{0}
on the compact set
\mathcal{W}_{0}
, choose
\tilde{\delta}_{0}
such that
|\phi_{0}(w)-\phi_{0}(\tilde{w})|<\varepsilon/3
for all
w,\tilde{w}\in\mathcal{W}_{0}
satisfying
|w-\tilde{w}|<\tilde{\delta}_{0}.
Next by eventual equicontinuity for every
w^{\prime}\in\mathcal{W}_{0}
, there exist
\delta(w^{\prime},\varepsilon)
and
T_{0}(w^{\prime},\varepsilon)
such that for all
w\in B(w^{\prime},\delta(w^{\prime},\varepsilon))
, and
T\geqslant T_{0}(w^{\prime},\varepsilon)
,
\left|\phi_{T}(w)-\phi_{T}(w^{\prime})\right|<\varepsilon/3.
Now by compactness of
\mathcal{W}_{0}
, there exist a finite set
\{w^{\prime}_{i}:i=1,2,\ldots,m\}\subset\mathcal{W}_{0}
such that
\mathcal{W}_{0}\subset\cup_{i=1}^{m}B(w^{\prime}_{i},\delta(w^{\prime}_{i},\varepsilon)\wedge\tilde{\delta}_{0}).

By pointwise convergence of
\phi_{T}
, for each
i=1,2,\ldots,m
, let
\tilde{T}_{0,i}
be such that for all
T>\tilde{T}_{0,i},

|\phi_{T}(w^{\prime}_{i})-\phi_{0}(w^{\prime}_{i})|<\varepsilon/3
. Set
T_{0}=\vee_{i=1}^{m}(T_{0}(w^{\prime}_{i},\varepsilon)\vee\tilde{T}_{0,i})
. Choose a
w\in\mathcal{W}_{0}
. Then
w\in B(w^{\prime}_{k},\delta(w^{\prime}_{k},\varepsilon)\wedge\tilde{\delta}_{0})
for some
k
. Now notice that for
T>T_{0}
,

\displaystyle|\phi_{T}(w)-\phi_{0}(w)|\leqslant

\displaystyle\ |\phi_{T}(w)-\phi_{T}(w^{\prime}_{k})|+|\phi_{T}(w^{\prime}_{k})-\phi_{0}(w^{\prime}_{k})|+|\phi_{0}(w^{\prime}_{k})-\phi_{0}(w)|

\displaystyle\leqslant

\displaystyle\ \varepsilon/3+\varepsilon/3+\varepsilon/3=\varepsilon.

∎

To justify the second assertion of Remark
3.9
, we proceed in two steps.

Only if part:

Assume that condition
(ii)(ii)-(a)
holds and consider a sequence
\{(\theta_{n},\vartheta_{n})\}\subset\mathscr{I}_{m_{0}}(\Theta_{0}).

Since
\Theta_{0}
is bounded,
\{\theta_{n}\}
is clearly bounded.
Suppose the sequence
\{\vartheta_{n}\}
is not bounded, then there exists a subsequence
\{n_{k}\}
such that
\left\|\vartheta_{n_{k}}\right\|\stackrel{{\scriptstyle k\rightarrow\infty}}{{\rightarrow}}\infty
. But then Condition
(ii)(ii)-(a)
guarantees that
\lim_{k\rightarrow\infty}I^{M}_{\theta_{n_{k}}}(\vartheta_{n_{k}})=\infty.
This violates the fact that
\{(\theta_{n_{k}},\vartheta_{n_{k}})\}\subset\mathscr{I}_{m_{0}}(\Theta_{0})
(that is,
I^{M}_{\theta_{n_{k}}}(\vartheta_{n_{k}})\leqslant m_{0}
for all
k\in\mathbb{N}
).

If part:
Suppose that Condition
(ii)(ii)-(a)
does not hold. Then there exist
\{\theta^{0}_{n}\}\subset\Theta,\{\vartheta_{n}\}\subset\mathcal{V}
such that
\sup_{n}\left\|\theta^{0}_{n}\right\|<\infty
and
|\vartheta_{n}|\stackrel{{\scriptstyle n\rightarrow\infty}}{{\rightarrow}}\infty
, and for all
n\geqslant 1
,
I^{M}_{\theta^{0}_{n}}(\vartheta_{n})\leqslant m_{0}
for some constant
m_{0}>0
. But then it is clear that for the bounded set
\Theta_{0}=\{\theta^{0}_{n}\}
,
\mathscr{I}_{m_{0}}(\Theta_{0})
is not bounded.

∎
