Detecting affine equivalences between certain types of parametric curves, in any dimension
Juan Gerardo Alcázar, Hüsnü Anıl Çoban, Uğur Gözütok

Abstract
Two curves are affinely equivalent if there exists an affine mapping transforming one of them onto the other. Thus, detecting affine equivalence comprises, as important particular cases, similarity, congruence and symmetry detection. In this paper we generalize previous results by the authors to provide an algorithm for computing the affine equivalences between two parametric curves of certain types, in any dimension. In more detail, the algorithm is valid for rational curves, and for parametric curves with non-rational but meromorphic components admitting a rational inverse. Unlike other algorithms already known for rational curves, the algorithm completely avoids polynomial system solving, and uses bivariate factoring, instead, as a fundamental tool. The algorithm has been implemented in the computer algebra system Maple , and can be freely downloaded and used.



1
Introduction.

We say that two curves are
affinely equivalent
if one of them is the image of the other curve by means of an affine mapping. If the affine mapping preserves angles, then the two curves are
similar
, i.e. both correspond to the same shape and differ only in position and
/
or scaling. If the affine mapping preserves distances, the curves are
congruent
, so they differ only in position. Finally, if the two curves coincide, finding the self-congruences of the curve is equivalent to computing its
symmetries
.

Because of the nature of the problem, it has received some attention in the applied fields of Computer Aided Geometric Design, Pattern Recognition and Computer Vision. In the last years, the problem has been also addressed in the Computer Algebra field, and this paper follows this trend. Examples of papers where this question has been studied are
[
2
,
4
,
7
]
; for other related papers, the interested reader can check the bibliographies of
[
2
,
4
,
7
]
. These papers address the problem for
rational
curves, i.e. parametric curves whose components are quotients of polynomial functions, and aim to the more general question of checking projective, and not just affine, equivalence. While
[
2
,
7
]
provide solutions for this problem, with different strategies, for curves in any dimension and using as a fundamental tool polynomial system solving, the paper
[
4
]
addresses the question only for space rational curves, but employing bivariate factoring as an alternative to solving polynomial systems; this leads to better timings and performance. To do this, in
[
4
]
two
rational invariants
, i.e. two functions rationally depending on the parametrizations to be studied which stay invariant under projective transformations, are found and used.

In this paper we generalize the ideas of
[
4
]
in three different ways. First, while the development of the invariants in
[
4
]
was more of an “art" than of a “craft", here we provide a complete algorithm to generate such invariants. Second, the technique is valid for curves in any dimension, and not just space curves, which was the case addressed in
[
4
]
: we provide an algorithm, that can be downloaded from
[
5
]
, to generate the corresponding invariants for any dimension, that needs to be executed just once for each dimension. Third, our strategy is valid not only for rational curves, but also for non-algebraic, parametric curves with meromorphic components under certain conditions, so we can apply the algorithm for helices or 3D spirals, for instance, whenever some hypotheses are fulfilled. In order to also include this type of non-algebraic curves, we stick to affine equivalences, and not projective equivalences, although our ideas could be developed in a projective setting for rational curves. Notice that strategies like
[
2
,
7
]
, based on polynomial system solving, cannot handle non-algebraic curves because we would need to solve systems involving analytic functions.

The idea in
[
2
,
4
,
7
]
is to compute the equivalences between the curves by determining first an associated function in the parameter space. If the curve parametrizations are rational and
proper
, i.e. generically injective, the fact that the Möbius functions are the birational transformations of the complex line guarantees that the associated function is a Möbius function. Thus, one aims to compute first the associated Möbius functions, and derives the equivalences between the curves from them. However, the Möbius functions are also the bi-meromorphic transformations of the complex line, which opens up the possibility, which we explore in this paper, of solving the problem for more general, not necessarily rational parametrizations. However, in order to do this we need that these parametrizations have meromorphic
global
inverses. For proper rational parametrizations, it is well-known that a global inverse exists, and is rational. For non-rational parametrizations, guaranteeing the existence of a global inverse is difficult. For this reason,
in the case of non-rational curves we restrict ourselves to certain types of parametrizations: despite of the fact that these parametrizations are not rational, we can guarantee that they have a global,
rational
inverse, therefore meromorphic, so our ideas can be applied.

Although the implementation of our algorithm is relatively simple, and can be downloaded from
[
5
]
jointly with the examples worked out in this paper, its development is involved. Because of this, we present the main ideas and results behind the algorithm, as well as several examples, in Section
2
and Section
3
, so that the reader only interested in using the algorithm can get a full idea by the end of Section
3
. Justifying the correctness of the algorithm requires to justify three steps that we refer to as steps (i), (ii) and (iii): while (i) and (iii) are more accessible, and are fully justified in Section
3
, justifying step (ii), which is the step where we generate the invariants, requires major work and is postponed to Section
4
. Nevertheless, the intuitive idea behind the justification of step (ii) is also given in Section
3
. We close the paper with our conclusion and some open questions in Section
5
. Some technical proofs are provided in Appendix I.

2
Background, statement of the problem and required tools.

2.1
Background, auxiliary results and statement of the problem

Let us start by describing the kind of parametric curves we will work with. The key idea is that they must be parametric curves with rational inverses. However, we will make precise their structure so that we can algorithmically verify that this requirement is satisfied.

We need two ingredients to do this; the first ingredient is a meromorphic function
\xi:{\mathbb{C}}\to{\mathbb{C}}
. Defining

\Pi:{\mathbb{C}}\to{\mathbb{C}}^{2},\mbox{ }\Pi(z)=(z,\xi(z)),

we observe that
\Pi
is an invertible function over its image, which is the graph
{\mathcal{G}}_{\xi}
of the function
\xi
,

{\mathcal{G}}_{\xi}=\{(z,\xi(z))|z\in{\mathbb{C}}\}\subset{\mathbb{C}}^{2}.

Indeed, for
(z,\omega)\in{\mathbb{C}}^{2}
,
\omega=\xi(z)
, we have
\Pi^{-1}(z,\omega)=z
. The second ingredient is a rational mapping
\Phi:{\mathbb{C}}^{2}\to{\mathbb{C}}^{n}
. If we compose these two mappings, we get a new mapping

{\bm{p}}=\Phi\circ\Pi:{\mathbb{C}}\to{\mathbb{C}}^{n}

(1)

which provides a parametrization
{\bm{p}}(z)=\Phi(z,\xi(z))
of a curve
{\bm{C}}\subset{\mathbb{C}}^{n}
, which is the image of
{\mathcal{G}}_{\xi}
under
\Phi
. Notice that
{\bm{p}}
is a vector function with meromorphic components. Of course if
\xi
is a rational function,
{\bm{p}}
is just a rational parametrization. We will also assume that the curve defined by
{\bm{p}}
is not contained in a hyperplane.

What we really need is the condition that the restriction
\Pi|_{{\mathcal{G}}_{\xi}}
is birational, so that
{\bm{p}}
has a rational inverse. If
\xi
is a rational function, what implies that
{\bm{p}}(z)
is a rational parametrization, we will just assume that
{\bm{p}}(z)
is
proper
, i.e. generically injective, in which case
{\bm{p}}^{-1}
exists and is rational. So let us provide sufficient conditions to guarantee this also in the case when
\xi
is not a rational function. In that case, we will assume that
\Phi
is a birational mapping. Recall that the
cardinality
of the fiber of
\Phi
, which we denote by
\#(\Phi)
, is the number of points in the preimage of
\Phi({\bf q})
with
{\bf q}\in{\mathbb{C}}^{2}
a generic point. Then, the birationality of
\Phi
is equivalent to
\#(\Phi)=1
(see for instance Proposition 7.16 in
[
6
]
), so we can check this condition by just picking a random point
{\bf q}
, and computing the number of points in the preimage of
\Phi({\bf q})
. Furthermore, we have the following lemma, inspired by
[
13
,
14
]
.

Lemma 1
.

Let
\Phi:{\mathbb{C}}^{2}\to{\mathbb{C}}^{n}
be a birational mapping. Then the set of points
{\bf q}\in{\mathbb{C}}^{2}
such that
\#(\Phi({\bf q}))>1
is included in an algebraic variety
{\mathcal{V}}\subset{\mathbb{C}}^{2}
of dimension at most 1.

Proof.

Let

\Phi(x_{1},x_{2})=(\Phi_{1}(x_{1},x_{2}),\ldots,\Phi_{n}(x_{1},x_{n}))=(y_{1},\ldots,y_{n}).

If
\Phi
is birational, then
\Phi^{-1}
exists and is rational, i.e.

\Phi^{-1}(y_{1},\ldots,y_{n})=(\Psi_{1}(y_{1},\ldots,y_{n}),\Psi_{2}(y_{1},\ldots,y_{n}))

where for
i=1,2
,

\Psi_{i}(y_{1},\ldots,y_{n})=\dfrac{A_{i}(y_{1},\ldots,y_{n})}{B_{i}(y_{1},\ldots,y_{n})}

with
A_{i},B_{i}
polynomials. Then the set of points
{\bf q}\in{\mathbb{C}}^{2}
such that
\#(\Phi({\bf q}))>1
is included in the set
{\mathcal{V}}\subset{\mathbb{C}}^{2}
where
\Phi^{-1}
is not defined,
{\mathcal{V}}
being the union of the sets defined by
(B_{i}\circ\Phi)(x_{1},x_{2})=0
with
i=1,2
, and
N_{i}(x_{1},x_{2})=0
,
i=1,2
, where
N_{i}
is the denominator of
(A_{i}\circ\Phi)(x_{1},x_{2})
. Notice that
{\mathcal{V}}
is an algebraic planar curve.
∎

Corollary 2
.

Assume that
\Phi
is a birational mapping, and
\xi
is a meromorphic, not algebraic function. Then
{\bm{p}}(z)
is invertible over
{\bm{C}}=\Phi({\mathcal{G}}_{\xi})
, and the inverse
{\bm{p}}^{-1}
has rational components.

Proof.

Since by assumption
\#(\Phi)=1
,
\Phi^{-1}
exists and is rational. Next from Lemma
1
we have that
\#(\Phi)
is constant except perhaps for the points of an algebraic variety
{\mathcal{V}}\subset{\mathbb{C}}^{2}
of dimension at most one. Since
\xi
is not an algebraic function
{\mathcal{G}}_{\xi}
is not an algebraic curve. Therefore, by the Identity Theorem (see Theorem 3.1.9 in
[
8
]
)
{\mathcal{G}}_{\xi}\cap{\mathcal{V}}
is either finite, or infinite but without any accumulation point. Thus, for a generic point
{\bf q}\in{\mathcal{G}}_{\xi}
we get that the cardinality of
\Phi({\bf q})
is
1
. Therefore
\Phi^{-1}
is well-defined for almost all points in
\Phi({\mathcal{G}}_{\xi})
, and
{\bm{p}}^{-1}=\Pi^{-1}\circ\Phi^{-1}
. Since
\Phi^{-1},\Pi^{-1}
are rational,
{\bm{p}}^{-1}
is rational as well.
∎

Example 1
.

To illustrate the assumptions that we need, we provide now some examples of plane and space curves satisfying these requirements.

(1)

Catenary.
Consider the curve
{\bm{C}}
parametrized by

(z,\mbox{cosh}(z)),

where
cosh
denotes the hyperbolic cosine (see Fig.
1
, left). Here
\xi(z)=\mbox{cosh}(z)
and
\Phi(x,y)=(x,y)
, which is clearly birational.

(2)

Image of the graph of the exponential curve under an inversion.
Let the curve
{\bm{C}}
(see Fig.
1
, middle) be parametrized by

\left(\dfrac{z}{z^{2}+e^{2z}},\dfrac{e^{z}}{z^{2}+e^{2z}}\right)

Here
\xi(z)=e^{z}
and

\Phi(x,y)=\left(\dfrac{x}{x^{2}+y^{2}},\dfrac{y}{x^{2}+y^{2}}\right),

(3)

3D spiral.
Consider the curve
{\bm{C}}
parametrized by
(z\cos(z),z\sin(z),z)
, which is a 3D spiral (see Fig.

, right). Writing

the parametrization of

\left(z\dfrac{e^{2{\bf i}z}+1}{2e^{{\bf i}z}},z\dfrac{e^{2{\bf i}z}-1}{2{\bf i}e^{{\bf i}z}},z\right).

Then,
\xi(z)=e^{{\bf i}z}
, and

\Phi(x,y)=\left(y\dfrac{x^{2}+1}{2x},y\dfrac{x^{2}-1}{2{\bf i}x},y\right),

Even though for technical reasons we will assume that the parameter space of the curve
{\bm{C}}
parametrized by Eq. (
1
) is
{\mathbb{C}}
, we will mostly work with real curves, i.e. curves with infinitely many real points. Next, given two curves
{\bm{C}}_{1},{\bm{C}}_{2}\subset{\mathbb{C}}^{n}
parametrized by
{\bm{p}}(z),{\bm{q}}(z)
as in Eq. (
1
), we say that
{\bm{C}}_{1},{\bm{C}}_{2}
are
affinely equivalent
if there exists a mapping
f:{\mathbb{C}}^{n}\to{\mathbb{C}}^{n}
,
f({\bm{x}})=A{\bm{x}}+{\bm{b}}
with
A\in{\mathcal{M}}_{n\times n}({\mathbb{C}})
, i.e.
A
is an
n\times n
matrix (in general, over the complex),
A
non-singular,
{\bm{b}}\in{\mathbb{C}}^{n}
, such that
f({\bm{C}}_{1})={\bm{C}}_{2}
; furthermore, we say that
f
is an
affine equivalence
between
{\bm{C}}_{1},{\bm{C}}_{2}
. We are interested in real affine equivalences, so in our case we will be mostly looking for
A\in{\mathcal{M}}_{n\times n}({\mathbb{R}})
,
{\bm{b}}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{n}
. If
{\bm{C}}_{1}={\bm{C}}_{2}={\bm{C}}
and
A
is
orthogonal
, i.e.
A^{T}A=I
, with
I
the identity matrix, we say that
f
is a
symmetry
of
{\bm{C}}
.

Now we are ready to state the problem that we want to solve.

Problem:
Given two curves
{\bm{C}}_{1},{\bm{C}}_{2}\subset{\mathbb{C}}^{n}
, not contained in hyperplanes, parametrized by mappings
{\bm{p}}(z),{\bm{q}}(z)
as in Eq. (
1
) with meromorphic components, admitting rational inverses
{\bm{p}}^{-1},{\bm{q}}^{-1}
, compute the affine equivalences, if any, between
{\bm{C}}_{1},{\bm{C}}_{2}
.

In order to solve this problem, we will make use of the following result, which corresponds to a similar result used in
[
4
,
7
]
, adapted to our case. We recall here that a
Möbius transformation
is a transformation

\varphi:{\mathbb{C}}\to{\mathbb{C}},\quad\varphi(z)=\dfrac{az+b}{cz+d},\quad ad-bc\neq 0.

Theorem 3
.

Let
{\bm{C}}_{1},{\bm{C}}_{2}\subset{\mathbb{C}}^{n}
be two parametric curves defined by
{\bm{p}}(z),{\bm{q}}(z)
as in Eq. (
1
), where
{\bm{p}}(z),{\bm{q}}(z)
are mappings with meromorphic components, admitting rational inverses
{\bm{p}}^{-1},{\bm{q}}^{-1}
. If
f({\bm{x}})=A{\bm{x}}+{\bm{b}}
is an affine equivalence between
{\bm{C}}_{1},{\bm{C}}_{2}
then there exists a Möbius transformation
\varphi(z)
satisfying that

f\circ{\bm{p}}={\bm{q}}\circ\varphi,

(2)

i.e. making commutative the following diagram:

\textstyle{{\bm{C}}_{1}\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces}
\scriptstyle{f}
\textstyle{{\bm{C}}_{2}}
\textstyle{{\mathbb{C}}\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces}
\scriptstyle{{\bm{p}}}
\scriptstyle{\varphi}
\textstyle{{\mathbb{C}}\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces}
\scriptstyle{{\bm{q}}}

(3)

Proof.

Since
{\bm{q}}^{-1}
exists,
\varphi={\bm{q}}^{-1}\circ f\circ{\bm{q}}
is well-defined. Furthermore, since
{\bm{q}}^{-1}
is rational,
{\bm{q}}^{-1}\circ f
is also a rational function and therefore
\varphi=({\bm{q}}^{-1}\circ f)\circ{\bm{p}}
is meromorphic. Since
{\bm{p}}
exists and is rational,
\varphi^{-1}={\bm{p}}^{-1}\circ f^{-1}\circ{\bm{q}}
is also meromorphic, so
\varphi
is a bi-meromorphic function, and therefore it must be a Möbius transformation (see Remark 2 in
[
1
]
).
∎

Remark 1
.

Theorem
3
also works with meromorphic parametrizations
{\bm{p}},{\bm{q}}
admitting
global
meromorphic inverses. However, guaranteeing the existence of a global meromorphic inverse is a really hard problem. This is the reason why we restrict ourselves to
parametrizations, rational or not rational, where this condition is easy to check. Notice that the parametrizations we work with here have rational inverses, so certainly they have global meromorphic inverses.

2.2

Additional tools

In this subsection we recall two notions that we will be using later in the paper. The first one is the
Schwartzian derivative
: given a holomorphic function
f:{\mathbb{C}}\to{\mathbb{C}}
, the Schwartzian derivative
[
12
]

S(f)
of
f
is

S(f)(z)=\dfrac{f^{\prime\prime\prime}(z)}{f^{\prime}(z)}-\dfrac{3}{2}\left(\dfrac{f^{\prime\prime}(z)}{f^{\prime}(z)}\right)^{2}.

The Schwartzian derivative of any Möbius transformation is identically zero. The following lemma is a consequence of this.

Lemma 4
.

Let
\omega:=\varphi(z)
a Möbius transformation, and let
\omega^{(k)}
denote the
k
-th derivative of
\omega
with respect to
k
. For
k\geq 3
,

\omega^{(k)}=\dfrac{k!}{2^{k-1}}\dfrac{(\omega^{\prime\prime})^{k-1}}{(\omega^{\prime})^{k-2}}.

(4)

Proof.

Since the Schwartzian derivative of a Möbius transformation is identically zero, we get that

\omega^{\prime\prime\prime}=\dfrac{3}{2}\dfrac{(\omega^{\prime\prime})^{2}}{\omega^{\prime}},

which corresponds to Eq. (
4
) for
k=3
. Then the result follows by induction on
k
.
∎

The second tool that we will need is
Faà di Bruno’s formula

[
9
]
for the derivatives of high order of a composite function. Given a vector function
{\bm{u}}:={\bm{u}}(z)
and a scalar function
\omega:=\omega(z)
, Faà di Bruno’s formula provides the derivatives of order
k\geq 1
of the composite function
{\bm{u}}(\omega)
with respect to
z
. Although there are other formulations, we will use the expression

\dfrac{d^{k}({\bm{u}}(\omega))}{dz^{k}}=\sum_{m=1}^{k}{\bm{u}}^{(m)}(\omega)B_{k,m}(\omega^{\prime},\omega^{\prime\prime},\ldots,\omega^{(k+1-m)}),

(5)

where the
B_{k,m}
are the incomplete (or partial)
Bell polynomials

[
10
]
, well-known in combinatorics,

B_{k,m}(x_{1},x_{2},\ldots,x_{k+1-m})=\sum\dfrac{k!}{\ell_{1}!\cdots\ell_{k+1-m}!}\prod_{i=1}^{k+1-m}\left(\dfrac{x_{i}}{i!}\right)^{\ell_{i}},

(6)

where the sum is taken over all sequences
\ell_{1},\ell_{2},\ldots,\ell_{k+1-m}
of non-negative integers such that

\sum_{i=1}^{k+1-m}\ell_{i}=m,\mbox{ }\sum_{i=1}^{k+1-m}i\ell_{i}=k.

(7)

In particular,

B_{k,k}(x_{1})=(x_{1})^{k},

(8)

and we will assume the convention that
B_{k,m}=0
when
k<m
.

3

Development of the method, algorithm and examples

3.1

Overall strategy and first step

We want to exploit Eq. (
2
) to first find the Möbius transformation
\varphi
, if any, and then derive
f
from
\varphi
. If we expand Eq. (
2
), we get

A{\bm{p}}(z)+{\bm{b}}=({\bm{q}}\circ\varphi)(z).

(9)

Our overall strategy will consist of three steps, that we will refer to as steps (i), (ii), (iii):

(i)

Find initial invariants:
we start by constructing certain functions
I_{1},\ldots,I_{n}
satisfying that
I_{i}({\bm{p}})=I_{i}({\bm{q}}\circ\varphi)
, which are
rational
in the sense that they are rational functions of
{\bm{p}}
and its derivatives. Since by Eq. (
9
) we observe that
{\bm{q}}\circ\varphi
is the image of
{\bm{p}}
under an affine mapping
f({\bm{x}})=A{\bm{x}}+{\bm{b}}
, we say that
I_{1},\ldots,I_{n}
are
affine invariants
, i.e. functions depending on a parametrization (and its derivatives) that stay the same when an affine transformation is applied.

(ii)

Find Möbius-commuting invariants:
we say that a function
F
depending on a parametrization
{\bm{u}}={\bm{u}}(z)
and its derivatives is
Möbius-commuting
if for any Möbius function we have

F({\bm{u}}\circ\varphi)=F({\bm{u}})\circ\varphi.

The functions
I_{i}
found in step (i) are not, in general, Möbius-commuting. Thus, in a second step we will compute Möbius-commuting functions
F_{1},\ldots,F_{n-1}
from the
I_{i}
, also rational. The
F_{j}
not only satify that
F_{j}({\bm{p}})=F_{j}({\bm{q}}\circ\varphi)
for
j=1,\ldots,n-1
, but they also satisfy that
F_{j}({\bm{q}}\circ\varphi)=F_{j}({\bm{q}})\circ\varphi
. In turn, for
j=1,\ldots,n-1
we have

F_{j}({\bm{p}})=F_{j}({\bm{q}})\circ\varphi.

Notice that while we have
n
initial invariants
I_{i}
, we have
n-1
Möbius-commuting invariants.

(iii)

Compute
\varphi
using bivariate factoring, and derive
f
from
\varphi
:
setting
\omega:=\varphi(z)
, the equalities
F_{j}({\bm{p}})=F_{j}({\bm{q}})\circ\varphi
, after clearing denominators, are translated into
n-1
conditions
M_{j}(z,\omega)=0
, with
j=1,\ldots,n-1
. Then the Möbius function
\varphi
corresponds to a common factor of all the
M_{j}
, and the affine equivalence itself,
f({\bm{x}})=A{\bm{x}}+{\bm{b}}
, follows from Eq. (
9
).

In this subsection we will present step (i); the remaining steps will be described in the next section. Also, in the rest of the paper we will use the notation
[{\bm{w}}_{1},\cdots,{\bm{w}}_{n}]
for an
n\times n
matrix whose columns are
{\bm{w}}_{1},\ldots,{\bm{w}}_{n}\in{\mathbb{C}}^{n}
, and
\|{\bm{w}}_{1},\cdots,{\bm{w}}_{n}\|
for the determinant of the matrix
[{\bm{w}}_{1},\cdots,{\bm{w}}_{n}]
.

The description of step (i) is analogous to Section 3.2 in
[
4
]
. Thus, here we focus on the main ideas, and refer the interested reader to
[
4
]
for details and proofs. Going back to Eq. (
2
), let us write
{\bm{u}}:={\bm{p}}(z)
,
{\bm{v}}:=({\bm{q}}\circ\varphi)(z)
, so that Eq. (
9
) becomes simply
A{\bm{u}}+{\bm{b}}={\bm{v}}
. Repeatedly differentiating this equation with respect to
z
yields
AD({\bm{u}})=D({\bm{v}})
where

D({\bm{u}})=\left[{\bm{u}}^{\prime},{\bm{u}}^{\prime\prime},\cdots,{\bm{u}}^{(n)}\right],\mbox{ }D({\bm{v}})=\left[{\bm{v}}^{\prime},{\bm{v}}^{\prime\prime},\cdots,{\bm{v}}^{(n)}\right],

i.e.
D({\bm{u}}),D({\bm{v}})
are matrices whose columns consist of the first
n
derivatives of
{\bm{u}},{\bm{v}}
with respect to
z
. Whenever
{\bm{p}},{\bm{q}}
and therefore
{\bm{u}},{\bm{v}}
are not contained in hyperplanes,
D({\bm{u}}),D({\bm{v}})
are invertible
[
15
]
. Thus, we can write
A=D({\bm{v}})(D({\bm{u}}))^{-1}
. Differentiating this equality with respect to
z
, and taking into account that
A
is a constant matrix, we get that

\dfrac{d(D({\bm{v}})(D({\bm{u}}))^{-1})}{dz}=0.

Expanding the derivative in the left-hand side of the above equation we arrive at

(D({\bm{u}}))^{-1}\dfrac{dD({\bm{u}})}{dz}=(D({\bm{v}}))^{-1}\dfrac{dD({\bm{v}})}{dz}.

(10)

Denoting

U=(D({\bm{u}}))^{-1}\dfrac{dD(u)}{dz},\mbox{ }V=(D({\bm{v}}))^{-1}\dfrac{dD(v)}{dz},

one can check that

\begin{array}[]{cc}U=\begin{bmatrix}0&0&\cdots&0&\dfrac{\|{\bm{u}}^{(n+1)},{\bm{u}}^{\prime\prime},\cdots,{\bm{u}}^{(n)}\|}{\|{\bm{u}}^{\prime},{\bm{u}}^{\prime\prime},\cdots,{\bm{u}}^{(n)}\|}\\
0&1&\cdots&0&\dfrac{\|{\bm{u}}^{\prime},{\bm{u}}^{(n+1)},\cdots,{\bm{u}}^{(n)}\|}{\|{\bm{u}}^{\prime},{\bm{u}}^{\prime\prime},\cdots,{\bm{u}}^{(n)}\|}\\
\vdots&\vdots&\ddots&\vdots&\vdots\\
0&0&\cdots&1&\dfrac{\|{\bm{u}}^{\prime},{\bm{u}}^{\prime\prime},\cdots,{\bm{u}}^{(n+1)}\|}{\|{\bm{u}}^{\prime},{\bm{u}}^{\prime\prime},\cdots,{\bm{u}}^{(n)}\|}\end{bmatrix},V=\begin{bmatrix}0&0&\cdots&0&\dfrac{\|{\bm{v}}^{(n+1)},{\bm{v}}^{\prime\prime},\cdots,{\bm{v}}^{(n)}\|}{\|{\bm{v}}^{\prime},{\bm{v}}^{\prime\prime},\cdots,{\bm{v}}^{(n)}\|}\\
0&1&\cdots&0&\dfrac{\|{\bm{v}}^{\prime},{\bm{v}}^{(n+1)},\cdots,{\bm{v}}^{(n)}\|}{\|{\bm{v}}^{\prime},{\bm{v}}^{\prime\prime},\cdots,{\bm{v}}^{(n)}\|}\\
\vdots&\vdots&\ddots&\vdots&\vdots\\
0&0&\cdots&1&\dfrac{\|{\bm{v}}^{\prime},{\bm{v}}^{\prime\prime},\cdots,{\bm{v}}^{(n+1)}\|}{\|{\bm{v}}^{\prime},{\bm{v}}^{\prime\prime},\cdots,{\bm{v}}^{(n)}\|}\end{bmatrix}.\end{array}

(11)

Next let us define

A_{i}({\bm{u}}):=\|{\bm{u}}^{\prime},\cdots,{\bm{u}}^{(i-1)},{\bm{u}}^{(n+1)},{\bm{u}}^{i+1},\cdots,{\bm{u}}^{(n)}\|,\mbox{ }\Delta(\bm{u}):=\|\bm{u}^{\prime},\bm{u}^{\prime\prime},\cdots,{\bm{u}}^{n}\|.

(12)

Thus,
A_{i}({\bm{u}})
is the result of replacing
{\bm{u}}^{(i)}
in
\|{\bm{u}}^{\prime}{\bm{u}}^{\prime\prime}\cdots{\bm{u}}^{(n)}\|
by
{\bm{u}}^{(n+1)}
. Finally, for
i=1,\ldots,n
, let

I_{i}(\bm{u}):=\dfrac{A_{i}(\bm{u})}{\Delta(\bm{u})},

(13)

which correspond to the entries of the last column of
U
; notice that whenever
{\bm{u}},{\bm{v}}
are not contained in hyperplanes
\Delta({\bm{u}})
is not identically zero
[
15
]
, so the
I_{i}
are well defined.

By Eq. (
10
),
U,V
are equal and therefore their last columns coincide. Thus,
I_{i}({\bm{u}})=I_{i}({\bm{v}})
for
i=1,\ldots,n
, i.e.
I_{i}({\bm{p}})=I_{i}({\bm{q}}\circ\varphi)
, which by Theorem
3
is a necessary condition for affine equivalence. The following result, analogous to Theorem 7 in
[
4
]
and which can be proved, using Theorem
3
, in a similar way, shows that this condition is also sufficient.

Theorem 5
.

Let
\bm{C}_{1},\bm{C}_{2}\subset{\mathbb{C}}^{n}
be two curves, not contained in a hyperplane, parametrized by mappings
\bm{p},\bm{q}
with meromorphic components, admitting rational inverses. If
\bm{C}_{1},\bm{C}_{2}
are affinely equivalent then there exists a Möbius transformation
\varphi
such that

I_{i}({\bm{p}})=I_{i}({\bm{q}}\circ\varphi)

(14)

for
i=1,\ldots,n
.

In order to carry out step (ii), which will be addressed in the next section, we need an auxiliary invariant,
I_{0}
, defined as

I_{0}({\bm{u}}):=\dfrac{\|{\bm{u}}^{\prime},\cdots,{\bm{u}}^{(n-1)},{\bm{u}}^{(n+2)}\|}{\|{\bm{u}}^{\prime},{\bm{u}}^{\prime\prime},\cdots,{\bm{u}}^{(n)}\|}.

(15)

The following lemma proves that
I_{0}
lies in the differential field spanned by
I_{1},\ldots,I_{n}
. The proof of this lemma is provided in Appendix I.

Lemma 6
.

I_{0}=\dfrac{dI_{n}}{dz}+I_{n-1}+I_{n}^{2}
.

Since, according to Lemma
6
,
I_{0}
is generated by
I_{1},\ldots,I_{n}
, the result in Theorem
5
also holds when we add
I_{0}
to the list of the
I_{i}
s.

Corollary 7
.

Let
\bm{C}_{1},\bm{C}_{2}\subset{\mathbb{C}}^{n}
be two curves, not contained in a hyperplane, parametrized by mappings
\bm{p},\bm{q}
with meromorphic components, admitting rational inverses. If
\bm{C}_{1},\bm{C}_{2}
are affinely equivalent then there exists a Möbius transformation
\varphi
such that

I_{i}({\bm{p}})=I_{i}({\bm{q}}\circ\varphi)

(16)

for
i\in\{0,1\ldots,n\}
.

3.2

Second step (overview) and third step

The
I_{i}
developed in the previous section are not Möbius-commuting, i.e.
I_{i}({\bm{q}}\circ\varphi)\neq I_{i}({\bm{q}})\circ\varphi
; in other words, calling
\omega:=\varphi(z)
,
I_{i}({\bm{q}}(\omega))\neq I_{i}({\bm{q}})(\omega)
. For instance, in the case
n=3
, expanding
I_{i}({\bm{q}}(\omega))
for
i=1,2,3
we get that

\begin{array}[]{rcl}\omega^{\prime 3}I_{1}(\bm{q}(\omega))&=&3\omega^{\prime\prime 3}+\dfrac{3}{2}\omega^{\prime 2}\omega^{\prime\prime 2}I_{3}({\bm{q}})(\omega)-\omega^{\prime 4}\omega^{\prime\prime}I_{2}({\bm{q}})(\omega)+\omega^{\prime 6}I_{1}({\bm{q}})(\omega)\\
\omega^{\prime 2}I_{2}(\bm{q}(\omega))&=&-9\omega^{\prime\prime 2}+\omega^{\prime 4}I_{2}({\bm{q}})(\omega)-3\omega^{\prime 2}\omega^{\prime\prime}I_{3}({\bm{q}})(\omega)\\
\omega^{\prime}I_{3}(\bm{q}(\omega))&=&6\omega^{\prime\prime}+\omega^{\prime 2}I_{3}({\bm{q}})(\omega),\\
\end{array}

(17)

where
\omega^{\prime},\omega^{\prime\prime}
are the first and second derivatives of
\omega=\varphi(z)
with respect to
z
; to produce these equalities, we have taken into account the definition of
I_{1},I_{2},I_{3}
as quotients of determinants, the Chain Rule, and the fact that, because of Eq. (
4
) in Lemma
4
, the derivatives of
\omega
of order higher than 3 can be written in terms of
\omega^{\prime},\omega^{\prime\prime}
. However, by eliminating
\omega^{\prime},\omega^{\prime\prime}
in Eq. (
17
), one can show that

\dfrac{\left[36I_{1}(\bm{q}(\omega))+6I_{2}(\bm{q}(\omega))I_{3}(\bm{q}(\omega))+I_{3}(\bm{q}(\omega))^{3}\right]^{2}}{\left[4I_{2}(\bm{q}(\omega))+I_{3}(\bm{q}(\omega))^{2}\right]^{3}}=\dfrac{\left[36I_{1}({\bm{q}})(\omega)+6I_{2}({\bm{q}})(\omega)I_{3}({\bm{q}})(\omega)+I_{3}({\bm{q}})(\omega)^{3}\right]^{2}}{\left[4I_{2}({\bm{q}})(\omega)+I_{3}({\bm{q}})(\omega)^{2}\right]^{3}},

(18)

so that

F=\dfrac{\left[36I_{1}({\bm{q}})+6I_{2}({\bm{q}})I_{3}({\bm{q}})+I_{3}^{3}({\bm{q}})\right]^{2}}{\left[4I_{2}({\bm{q}})+I_{3}^{2}({\bm{q}})\right]^{3}}

(19)

is Möbius-commuting, i.e.
F({\bm{q}}\circ\varphi)=F({\bm{q}})\circ\varphi
.

One can certainly manipulate Eq. (
17
) by hand to get rid of
\omega^{\prime},\omega^{\prime\prime}
, reach Eq. (
18
), and therefore find the invariant in Eq. (
19
). However, we want to produce invariants like the one in Eq. (
19
) in an algorithmic fashion, and for any dimension: that is the task in step (ii). The rough idea, as in Eq. (
17
), is to get rid of the derivatives
\omega^{(k)}
,
k=1,2,\ldots,n+2
, in the system consisting of the expressions

I_{i}({\bm{q}}(\omega))=\xi_{i}\left(I_{0}({\bm{q}})(\omega),\ldots,I_{n}({\bm{q}})(\omega),\omega^{\prime},\omega^{\prime\prime},\ldots,\omega^{(n+2)}\right),

where
\xi_{i}
is the result of expanding
I_{i}({\bm{q}}(\omega))
, with
i=0,1,\ldots,n
. The process is involved, and will be detailed in Section
4
, but as a final product of this process we get closed expressions for these invariants (see Theorem
16
in the next Section
4
), that we denote
F_{1},\ldots,F_{n-1}
. The generation of the Möbius-commuting invariants, for any dimension
n
, is implemented in
[
5
]
, which can be freely downloaded, and can be done just once for each dimension
n
. In Table

we spell the invariants for low dimension,
2\leq n\leq 4
.

Next let us address step (iii). Let
F_{j}
be a Möbius-commuting invariant,
j\in\{1,\ldots,n-1\}
. Since
F_{j}
is a rational function of the
I_{i}
,
F_{j}
is also an affine invariant, i.e. from Theorem
5
we get that
F_{j}({\bm{p}})=F_{j}({\bm{q}}\circ\varphi)
. Therefore, in terms of the variables
z
and
\omega:=\varphi(z)
, and taking into account that
F_{j}({\bm{q}}\circ\varphi)=F_{j}({\bm{q}})\circ\varphi
, we deduce that
F_{j}({\bm{p}})(z)=F_{j}({\bm{q}})(\omega)
. Then we have the following result.

Proposition 8
.

Let
\bm{C}_{1},\bm{C}_{2}\subset{\mathbb{C}}^{n}
be two curves, not contained in a hyperplane, parametrized by mappings
\bm{p},\bm{q}
with meromorphic components, admitting rational inverses. Then
\bm{C}_{1},\bm{C}_{2}
are affinely equivalent if and only if there exists a Möbius transformation
\varphi
such that

F_{j}({\bm{p}})(z)-F_{j}({\bm{q}})(\omega)=0,

(20)

for
j\in\{1,2,\ldots,n-1\}
with
\omega=\varphi(z)
, such that
D({\bm{q}}\circ\varphi)(D({\bm{p}}))^{-1}(z)
is a constant matrix
A
and
{\bm{b}}=({\bm{q}}\circ\varphi-A{\bm{p}})(z)
is a constant vector. Furthermore,
f({\bm{x}})=A{\bm{x}}+{\bm{b}}
is an affine equivalence between
{\bm{C}}_{1},{\bm{C}}_{2}
.

Proof.

(\Rightarrow)
Let
f
be an affine equivalence between
\bm{C}_{1},\bm{C}_{2}
. By Theorem
3
, there exists a Möbius function
\varphi
such that
f\circ{\bm{p}}={\bm{q}}\circ\varphi
. By Corollary
7
we have that
I_{i}({\bm{p}})(z)=I_{i}({\bm{q}}(\omega))
for all
i\in\{0,\ldots,n\}
. Since the
F_{j}
are rational functions of the
I_{i}
,
I_{i}({\bm{p}})(z)=I_{i}({\bm{q}}(\omega))
yields
F_{j}({\bm{p}})(z)=F_{i}({\bm{q}})(\omega)
for
j\in\{1,2,\ldots,n-1\}
. Finally, writing
f({\bm{x}})=A{\bm{x}}+{\bm{b}}
, the condition
f\circ{\bm{p}}={\bm{q}}\circ\varphi
implies that
A{\bm{p}}(z)+{\bm{b}}={\bm{q}}(\varphi(z))
, so
{\bm{b}}=({\bm{q}}\circ\varphi-A{\bm{p}})(z)
, which is a constant vector. Furthermore, by differentiating the condition
A{\bm{p}}(z)+{\bm{b}}={\bm{q}}(\varphi(z))
(see Subsection
3.1
) we deduce that
A=D({\bm{q}}\circ\varphi)(D({\bm{p}}))^{-1}(z)
.
(\Leftarrow)
Let
\varphi
be a Möbius transformation satisfying
F_{i}({\bm{p}}(z))-F_{i}({\bm{q}})(\omega)=0
for
\omega=\varphi(z)
. If
A=D({\bm{q}}\circ\varphi)(D({\bm{p}}))^{-1}(z)
is a constant matrix, then
D(A{\bm{p}})(z)=D({\bm{q}}\circ\varphi)(z)
, so
A{\bm{p}}(z)-({\bm{q}}\circ\varphi)(z)
is a constant, equal to
-{\bm{b}}
. Therefore,
A{\bm{p}}(z)+{\bm{b}}={\bm{q}}(\varphi(z))
. But this equality implies that
A{\bm{p}}(z)+{\bm{b}}
, which is the image of
\bm{C}_{1}
under the affine mapping
f({\bm{x}})=A{\bm{x}}+{\bm{b}}
, and
{\bm{q}}(z)
, parametrize the same curve, namely
\bm{C}_{2}
. Thus,
f({\bm{x}})=A{\bm{x}}+{\bm{b}}
is an affine equivalence between
{\bm{C}}_{1}
and
{\bm{C}}_{2}
.
∎

3.3

Algorithm and examples

To finally turn Proposition
8
into an algorithm, let
M_{j}(z,\omega)
be obtained by clearing denominators in
F_{j}({\bm{p}})(z)-F_{j}({\bm{q}})(\omega)
. We need to request that
M_{j}(z,\omega)
is not identically zero, which amounts to requiring that not all the
F_{j}
are constant: this can happen, and an example is the circular helix
{\bm{p}}(z)=(\mbox{cos}(z),\mbox{sin}(z),z)
. If
M_{j}(z,\omega)
is not zero, then
M_{j}(z,\omega)=0
defines an analytic curve in the plane
z,\omega
. Now if

\varphi(z)=\dfrac{az+b}{cz+d}

(21)

is a Möbius function satisfying Proposition
8
, calling
\omega=\varphi(z)
we get that all the points
(z,\omega)
of the curve

\omega(cz+d)-(az+d)=0,

which is an irreducible analytic curve, are also points of the curve
M_{j}(z,\omega)
. As a consequence of Study’s Lemma (see Section 6.13 of
[
3
]
),
H(z,\omega)=\omega(cz+d)-(az+d)
must be a factor of
M_{j}(z,\omega)
; we say that
H(z,\omega)=\omega(cz+d)-(az+d)
is a
Möbius-like
factor of
M_{j}(z,\omega)
, and that the Möbius function
\varphi
in Eq. (
21
) is
associated
with
H(z,\omega)
. So we have the following theorem, which follows from Proposition
8
.

Theorem 9
.

Let
\bm{C}_{1},\bm{C}_{2}\subset{\mathbb{C}}^{n}
be two curves, not contained in a hyperplane, parametrized by mappings
\bm{p},\bm{q}
with meromorphic components, admitting rational inverses and where not all the
F_{j}
are constant. Then
\bm{C}_{1},\bm{C}_{2}
are affinely equivalent if and only if there exists a Möbius-like factor
H(z,\omega)
common to
M_{j}(z,\omega)
,
j=1,\ldots,n-1
such that the corresponding associated Möbius function
\varphi
satisfies that: (1)
D({\bm{q}}\circ\varphi)(D({\bm{p}}))^{-1}(z)
is a constant matrix
A
, (2)
{\bm{b}}=({\bm{q}}\circ\varphi-A{\bm{p}})(z)
is a constant vector. Furthermore, in that case
f({\bm{x}})=A{\bm{x}}+{\bm{b}}
is an affine equivalence between
{\bm{C}}_{1},{\bm{C}}_{2}
.

Thus, we get the following procedure
AffineEquivalences
to find the affine equivalences between the curves
\bm{C}_{1},\bm{C}_{2}
defined by
{\bm{p}},{\bm{q}}
.

AffineEquivalences

Input:
Two parametrizations
\bm{p}
and
\bm{q}
with meromorphic components, admitting rational inverses.

Output:
Either the list of affine equivalences between the curves, or the warning
The curves are not affinely equivalent

1:

procedure
AffEq
(
\bm{p},\bm{q}
)

2:

Compute
M_{j}(x,z)
,
j=1,\ldots,n-1
, by clearing denominators in
F_{j}({\bm{p}})(z)-F_{j}({\bm{q}})(\omega)
.

3:

if all the
M_{j}
are identically zero then

4:

return
Failure: all the Möbius-commuting invariants are constant

5:

else

6:

Compute the common factor
L(x,z)
of the
M_{j}(x,z)
.

7:

Let
\mathcal{L}
be the list of Möbius-like factors of
L(x,z)

8:

if
\mathcal{L}=\emptyset
then

9:

return
The curves are not affinely equivalent

10:

else

11:

for
\varphi\in\mathcal{L}
do

12:

Check whether or not
A=D(\bm{q}(\varphi))D(\bm{p})^{-1}
,
{\bm{b}}={\bm{q}}\circ\varphi-A{\bm{p}}
are constant

13:

In the affirmative case, return
f({\bm{x}})=A{\bm{x}}+{\bm{b}}
.

If
{\bm{p}},{\bm{q}}
are rational, the
M_{j}(x,z)
are rational and
H(z,\omega)
is a factor of
\mbox{gcd}(M_{1}(z,\omega),\ldots,M_{n-1}(z,\omega))
. However, the computer algebra system
Maple
, where we implemented the procedure (see
[
5
]
), can compute
H(z,\omega)
also in the case when
{\bm{p}},{\bm{q}}
are not rational, but satisfy the hypotheses of the procedure. In this last case, we ask
Maple
to solve
H(z,\omega)
for
\omega
to find the Möbius functions.

.

4

Justification of step (ii): computation of Möbius-commuting invariants

Step (ii) in the strategy presented at the beginning of Section
3.1
corresponds to the computation of what we called Möbius-commuting invariants. The deduction of these invariants is involved; so in order to develope our reasoning, we will distinguish three small substeps, that we present in separate subsections:

(ii.1)

Rewriting high order derivatives of
\omega
and rewriting Faà di Bruno’s formula.

(ii.2)

Expansion of
I_{i}({\bm{q}}(\omega))
for
i=1,\ldots,n-1
.

(ii.3)

Computation of Möbius-commuting invariants.

Here we recall the notation
\omega=\varphi(z)
, and the notation
\omega^{\prime},\omega^{\prime\prime},\ldots,\omega^{(k)}
for the derivatives of
\omega
with respect to
z
. Recall also from Subsection
3.2
that the rough idea in step (ii) is to eliminate the derivatives of
\omega
from the expressions resulting from expanding
I_{i}({\bm{q}}(\omega))
. In order to do that, first we will rewrite all the derivatives
\omega^{(k)}
in terms of just
\omega^{\prime}
; we will do that in step (ii.1) with the help of the expansion of
I_{n}({\bm{q}}(\omega))
, for which we will make use of the tools introduced in Subsection
2.2
and, in particular, Faà di Bruno’s formula. Then, in step (ii.2), we will compute an expansion form for
I_{i}({\bm{q}}(\omega))
for
i=1,\ldots,n-1
; this is the hardest part, where we will need to make use, again, of Faà di Bruno’s formula, rewritten in an advantageous form in substep (ii.1), and some combinatorics. Finally, in step (ii.3), we will make use of
I_{0}({\bm{q}}(\omega))
, the auxiliary invariant introduced in Eq. (
15
) to finally eliminate
\omega^{\prime}
, the only derivative of
\omega
left after substep (ii.1), and compute the Möbius-commuting invariants.

4.1

Substep
(ii.1)

Our first step in order to eliminate the derivatives
\omega^{(k)}
is to write all of them in terms of
\omega^{\prime}
; later, we will use this to rewrite Faà di Bruno’s formula, introduced in Section
2.2
, in an alternative form that will be useful in substep (ii.2). In order to do this, we will take advantage of the expansion of
I_{n}({\bm{q}}(\omega))
: in general, expanding
I_{i}({\bm{q}}(\omega))
for
i=0,1,\ldots,n-1
is messy and will be the hardest part, deferred for substep (ii.2), but expanding
I_{n}({\bm{q}}(\omega))
is much more accesible. So let focus on this. From Eq. (
13
), we need to expand
\Delta({\bm{q}}(\omega))
and
A_{n}({\bm{q}}(\omega))
. In both cases we will make use of Faà di Bruno’s formula. First, from Eq. (
12
)

\Delta({\bm{q}}(\omega))=\left\|\dfrac{d}{dz}({\bm{q}}(\omega)),\dfrac{d^{2}}{dz^{2}}({\bm{q}}(\omega)),\ldots,\dfrac{d^{n}}{dz^{n}}({\bm{q}}(\omega))\right\|.

(22)

From Faà di Bruno’s formula, Eq. (
7
) and Eq. (
8
), we have

\dfrac{d}{dz}({\bm{q}}(\omega))=\omega^{\prime}{\bm{q}}^{\prime}(\omega),

and for
k\geq 2
,

\dfrac{d^{k}}{dz^{k}}({\bm{q}}(\omega))=\bullet_{k-1}+(\omega^{\prime})^{k}{\bm{q}}^{(k)}(\omega),

(23)

where
\bullet_{k-1}
is a linear combination of the derivatives of
{\bm{q}}
up to order
k-1
, evaluated at
\omega
. By expanding the determinant in Eq. (
22
) as a sum of determinants, we observe that all the determinants including terms of the
\bullet_{k-1}
,
k=2,\ldots,n
, must be zero. Thus, we are left with one determinant, whose columns
k=1,2,\ldots,n
are

(\omega^{\prime})^{k}{\bm{q}}^{(k)}(\omega),

and we get the following result.

Lemma 10
.

\Delta({\bm{q}}(\omega))=(\omega^{\prime})^{\frac{n(n+1)}{2}}\Delta{\bm{q}}(\omega).

To expand
I_{n}({\bm{q}}(\omega))
we also need
A_{n}({\bm{q}}(\omega))
. From Eq. (
12
),

A_{n}({\bm{q}}(\omega))=\left\|\dfrac{d({\bm{q}}(\omega))}{dz},\ldots,\dfrac{d^{n-1}({\bm{q}}(\omega))}{dz^{n-1}},\dfrac{d^{n+1}({\bm{q}}(\omega))}{dz^{n+1}}\right\|.

(24)

Furthermore, from Faà di Bruno’s formula,

\dfrac{d^{n+1}({\bm{q}}(\omega))}{dz^{n+1}}=\bullet_{n-1}+{\bm{q}}^{(n)}(\omega)B_{n+1,n}(\omega^{\prime},\omega^{\prime\prime})+{\bm{q}}^{(n+1)}(\omega)B_{n+1,n+1}(\omega^{\prime}),

(25)

where
\bullet_{n-1}
contains a linear combination of the derivatives of
{\bm{q}}
up to order
n-1
, evaluated at
\omega
. Also, from Eq. (
5
),

{\bm{q}}^{(n)}(\omega)B_{n+1,n}(\omega^{\prime},\omega^{\prime\prime})=\dfrac{n(n+1)}{2}(\omega^{\prime})^{n-1}\omega^{\prime\prime}{\bm{q}}^{(n)}(\omega),

(26)

and

{\bm{q}}^{(n+1)}(\omega)B_{n+1,n+1}(\omega^{\prime})=(\omega^{\prime})^{n+1}{\bm{q}}^{(n+1)}(\omega).

(27)

Now let us substitute Eq. (
23
), for
k=1,\ldots,n-1
, and Eqs. (
25
)-(
26
)-(
27
), into Eq. (
24
). Expanding as a sum of determinants, we observe again that the determinants including some term of the
\bullet_{k-1}
,
k=2,\ldots,n
must be zero. Thus, we arrive at

A_{n}({\bm{q}}(\omega))=\dfrac{n(n+1)}{2}(\omega^{\prime})^{\frac{n(n+1)}{2}-1}\omega^{\prime\prime}\Delta({\bm{q}})(\omega)+(\omega^{\prime})^{\frac{n(n+1)}{2}+1}A_{n}({\bm{q}})(\omega).

Dividing the last equality by
\Delta({\bm{q}}(\omega))
and using Lemma
10
, we reach the following result.

Lemma 11
.

\displaystyle{I_{n}({\bm{q}}(\omega))=\dfrac{n(n+1)}{2}\dfrac{\omega^{\prime\prime}}{\omega^{\prime}}+\omega^{\prime}I_{n}({\bm{q}})(\omega)}.

Notice that the formula for
I_{n}({\bm{q}}(\omega))
in Lemma
11
is linear in
\omega^{\prime\prime}
. This allows us to write

\omega^{\prime\prime}=\dfrac{2\omega^{\prime}}{n(n+1)}(I_{n}({\bm{q}}(\omega))-\omega^{\prime}I_{n}({\bm{q}})(\omega)).

(28)

Furthermore, using Eq. (
28
) and invoking Lemma
4
in Section
2.2
, we can write all the derivatives of
\omega
in terms of just
\omega^{\prime}
, which was one of the goals of this substep. We formulate this as a corollary of Lemma
11
.

Corollary 12
.

\omega^{(k)}=\dfrac{k!}{n^{k-1}(n+1)^{k-1}}\omega^{\prime}(I_{n}({\bm{q}}(\omega))-\omega^{\prime}I_{n}({\bm{q}})(\omega))^{k-1}
,
k\geq 2
.

Finally, let us use Corollary
12
to rewrite Faà di Bruno’s formula. In order to do this, let us introduce the notation

K_{n}:=I_{n}({\bm{q}}(\omega)),\mbox{ }G_{n}:=I_{n}({\bm{q}})(\omega),\mbox{ }\Phi:=K_{n}-\omega^{\prime}G_{n}.

(29)

Thus, using Corollary
12
and the above notation, the Bell polynomial in Eq. (
5
) can be written as

B_{k,m}(\omega^{\prime},\omega^{\prime\prime},\ldots,\omega^{(k+1-m)})=B_{k,m}\left(\omega^{\prime},\dfrac{2!}{n(n+1)}\omega^{\prime}\Phi,\ldots,\ldots,\dfrac{(k+1-m)!}{n^{k-m}(n+1)^{k-m}}\omega^{\prime}\Phi^{k-m}\right),

(30)

where only
\omega^{\prime}
is involved. Let

L(k,m)=\binom{k-1}{m-1}\dfrac{k!}{m!}

be the
Lah number

L(k,m)
(see for instance
[
10
]
), and let us denote

\tilde{B}_{k,m}=\dfrac{1}{n^{k-m}(n+1)^{k-m}}L(k,m).

(31)

Then we have the following result, proved in Appendix I.

Lemma 13
.

B_{k,m}(\omega^{\prime},\omega^{\prime\prime},\ldots,\omega^{(k+1-m)})=\tilde{B}_{k,m}\omega^{\prime m}\Phi^{k-m}
.

Using Lemma
13
we can rewrite Faà di Bruno’s formula as

\dfrac{d^{k}({\bm{q}}(\omega))}{dt^{k}}=\sum_{m=1}^{k}{\tilde{B}_{k,m}{\omega^{\prime}}^{m}\Phi^{k-m}{\bm{q}}^{(m)}(\omega)}.

(32)

We will use this in the next substep. Additionally, notice that
\tilde{B}_{k,k}=1
; also, we will assume the convention that
\tilde{B}_{k,m}=0
when
k<m
.

4.2

Substep
(ii.2)

The goal of this substep is to expand the
I_{i}({\bm{q}}(\omega))
, for
i=1,\ldots,n-1
, defined in Eq. (
13
). Since from Lemma
10
we already know
\Delta({\bm{q}}(\omega))
, we need to analyze, for
i\neq n
,

A_{i}({\bm{q}}(\omega))=\left\|\underbrace{\dfrac{d({\bm{q}}(\omega))}{dz},\ldots,\dfrac{d^{i-1}({\bm{q}}(\omega))}{dz^{i-1}}}_{i-1},\dfrac{d^{n+1}({\bm{q}}(\omega))}{dz^{n+1}},\underbrace{\dfrac{d^{i+1}({\bm{q}}(\omega))}{dz^{i+1}},\ldots,\dfrac{d^{n}({\bm{q}}(\omega))}{dz^{n}}}_{n-i}\right\|.

(33)

By using Faà di Bruno’s formula in Eq. (
32
), we know that each column, i.e. each derivative, in Eq. (
33
) is a linear combination of derivatives, where the number of terms is equal to the order of the derivative. Furthermore, we observe that for the first
i-1
columns, only the last term, i.e. the term involving the highest derivative, matters, since the other terms lead to vanishing determinants when expanding
A_{i}({\bm{q}}(\omega))
as a sum of determinants. Thus, we can write

A_{i}({\bm{q}}(\omega))=\left\|\underbrace{\omega^{\prime}{\bm{q}}^{\prime}(\omega),\ldots,(\omega^{\prime})^{i-1}{\bm{q}}^{(i-1)}(\omega)}_{i-1},\dfrac{d^{n+1}({\bm{q}}(\omega))}{dz^{n+1}},\underbrace{\dfrac{d^{i+1}({\bm{q}}(\omega))}{dz^{i+1}},\ldots,\dfrac{d^{n}({\bm{q}}(\omega))}{dz^{n}}}_{n-i}\right\|.

(34)

Next let us express Eq. (
34
) as a sum of determinants, using Faà di Bruno’s formula in Eq. (
32
). In order to do this, we consider the following two subsets:

1.

{\mathcal{P}}
represents the set consisting of the permutations of
\{i,i+1,\ldots,n\}
. Notice that
\#\{i,i+1,\ldots,n\}=n-i+1
, where
\#
denotes here the cardinal of a set.

2.

{\mathcal{Q}}
represents the set consisting of the combinations of
n-i
items from
\{i,i+1,\ldots,n\}
; thus, each element of
{\mathcal{Q}}
skips exactly one element,
r\in\{i,i+1,\ldots,n\}
. We will denote, also, the set of the permutations of the set
\{i,i+1,\ldots,n\}-\{r\}
by
{\mathcal{P}}_{r}
.

Now when expanding
A_{i}({\bm{q}}(\omega))
as a sum of determinants, and ignoring vanishing determinants, we get sums of terms like

\left\|\omega^{\prime}{\bm{q}}^{\prime}(\omega),\ldots,(\omega^{\prime})^{i-1}{\bm{q}}^{(i-1)}(\omega),\Box_{\ell_{1}}{\bm{q}}^{(\ell_{1})}(\omega),\Box_{\ell_{2}}{\bm{q}}^{(\ell_{2})}(\omega),\ldots,\Box_{\ell_{n-i+1}}{\bm{q}}^{(\ell_{n-i+1})}(\omega)\right\|,

(35)

where
1,\ldots,i-1,\ell_{1},\ell_{2},\ldots,\ell_{n-i+1}
are
n
different numbers (otherwise the determinant is zero), the
\Box_{\ell_{k}}
are coefficients to be discussed, and
\{1,\ldots,i-1,\ell_{1},\ell_{2},\ldots,\ell_{n-i+1}\}
is a subset of
\{1,2,\ldots,i-1,i,i+1,\ldots,n,n+1\}
. We are going to split the determinants in Eq. (
35
) into two different groups, according to
\ell_{1}\neq n+1
or
\ell_{1}=n+1
. The sums of the determinants of each group are denoted
A^{(I)}_{i}({\bm{q}}(\omega))
,
A^{(II)}_{i}({\bm{q}}(\omega))
, so
A_{i}({\bm{q}}(\omega))=A^{(I)}_{i}({\bm{q}}(\omega))+A^{(II)}_{i}({\bm{q}}(\omega))
.

\ell_{1}\neq n+1

Here we have the determinants where we do not pick the term in
{\bm{q}}^{(n+1)}(\omega)
of the expansion, using Faà di Bruno’s formula, of
\dfrac{d^{n+1}({\bm{q}}(\omega))}{dz^{n+1}}
, the
i
-th colum of Eq. (
34
). In this case,
\{\ell_{1},\ell_{2},\ldots,\ell_{n-i+1}\}\in{\mathcal{P}}
; thus, the columns of the determinant in Eq. (
35
) coincide, up to a permutation, with the columns of
\Delta{\bm{q}}(\omega)
, so Eq. (
35
) is equal to

\alpha\|{\bm{q}}^{\prime}(\omega),\ldots,{\bm{q}}^{(i-1)}(\omega),{\bm{q}}^{(\ell_{1})}(\omega),\ldots,{\bm{q}}^{(\ell_{n-i+1})}(\omega)\|=\alpha(-1)^{\sigma}\Delta{\bm{q}}(\omega),

(36)

where
\alpha
is a coefficient to be discussed, and
(-1)^{\sigma}
denotes the signature of the permutation

\{i,i+1,\ldots,n\}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\sigma}}{{\to}}\{\ell_{1},\ell_{2},\ldots,\ell_{n-i+1}\}.

We have exactly
\#{\mathcal{P}}
terms like this in the expansion of
A_{i}({\bm{q}}(\omega))
.

Let us now discuss the value of the coefficient
\alpha
. In Eq. (
35
), for
k=1,\ldots,i-1
each derivative
{\bm{q}}^{(k)}(\omega)
appears multiplied by
(\omega^{\prime})^{k}
. Additionally, from Eq. (
32
), we deduce that the derivative
{\bm{q}}^{(\ell_{1})}(\omega)
appears multiplied by
\tilde{B}_{n+1,\ell_{1}}\Phi^{(n+1)-\ell_{1}}
, the derivative
{\bm{q}}^{(\ell_{2})}(\omega)
appears multiplied by
\tilde{B}_{i+1,\ell_{2}}\Phi^{(i+1)-\ell_{2}}
, etc. and the derivative
{\bm{q}}^{\ell_{n-i+1}}(\omega)
appears multiplied by
\tilde{B}_{n,\ell_{n-i+1}}\Phi^{n-\ell_{n-i+1}}
. Taking these constants out of the determinant in Eq. (
35
), we get that

\alpha=(\omega^{\prime})^{\frac{n(n+1)}{2}}\tilde{B}_{n+1,\ell_{1}}\tilde{B}_{i+1,\ell_{2}}\cdots\tilde{B}_{n,\ell_{n-i+1}}\Phi^{(n+1)-i}.

Here we need to take into account that:

(1)

The power of
\omega^{\prime}
in the above expression comes from

\omega^{\prime}(\omega^{\prime})^{2}\cdots(\omega^{\prime})^{i-1}(\omega^{\prime})^{\ell_{1}}\cdots(\omega)^{\ell_{n-i+1}}.

Since
\{\ell_{1},\ell_{2},\ldots,\ell_{n-i+1}\}\in{\mathcal{P}}
, the sum of
1,2,\ldots,i-1,\ell_{1},\ldots,\ell_{n-i+1}
coincides with the sum of the first
n
natural numbers.

(2)

The power of
\Phi
is the result of the sum

[(n+1)-\ell_{1}]+[(i+1)-\ell_{2}]+\cdots+[n-\ell_{n-i+1}]=n+1-i.

(37)

Thus, when summing over the elements of
{\mathcal{P}}
, the sum of the determinants in the expansion of
A_{i}({\bm{q}}(\omega))
with
\ell_{1}\neq n+1
yields

A^{(I)}_{i}({\bm{q}}(\omega))=\sum_{\sigma\in{\mathcal{P}}}(-1)^{\sigma}(\omega^{\prime})^{\frac{n(n+1)}{2}}\tilde{B}_{n+1,\ell_{1}}\tilde{B}_{i+1,\ell_{2}}\cdots\tilde{B}_{n,\ell_{n-i+1}}\Phi^{(n+1)-i}\Delta{\bm{q}}(\omega),

(38)

which we can also write as

A^{(I)}_{i}({\bm{q}}(\omega))=(\omega^{\prime})^{\frac{n(n+1)}{2}}\Phi^{(n+1)-i}\Delta{\bm{q}}(\omega)\underbrace{\sum_{\sigma\in{\mathcal{P}}}(-1)^{\sigma}\tilde{B}_{n+1,\ell_{1}}\tilde{B}_{i+1,\ell_{2}}\cdots\tilde{B}_{n,\ell_{n-i+1}}}.

(39)

The underbraced expression corresponds exactly to the definition of an
(n-i+1)\times(n-i+1)
determinant, whose
j
-th column consists of the values of
\tilde{B}_{n+1,n+1-j},\tilde{B}_{n,n+1-j},\ldots,\tilde{B}_{i+1,n+1-j}
, where
\tilde{B}_{k,m}=0
when
k<m
, and
\tilde{B}_{k,k}=1
. We call this determinant
M^{n+1,i+1}
, so

A^{(I)}_{i}({\bm{q}}(\omega))=(\omega^{\prime})^{\frac{n(n+1)}{2}}\Phi^{(n+1)-i}\Delta{\bm{q}}(\omega)M^{n+1,i+1}.

(40)

\ell_{1}=n+1

Here we have the determinants where we pick the term in
{\bm{q}}^{(n+1)}(\omega)
of the expansion, using Faà di Bruno’s formula, of
\dfrac{d^{n+1}({\bm{q}}(\omega))}{dz^{n+1}}
, the
i
-th colum of Eq. (
34
). In other words, we have determinants like Eq. (
35
), where the
i
-th column is
(\omega^{\prime})^{n+1}{\bm{q}}^{(n+1)}(\omega)
, and
\{\ell_{2},\ldots,\ell_{n-i+1}\}\in{\mathcal{Q}}
, so Eq. (
35
) is equal to

\beta\|{\bm{q}}^{\prime}(\omega),\ldots,{\bm{q}}^{(i-1)}(\omega),{\bm{q}}^{(n+1)}(\omega),{\bm{q}}^{(\ell_{2})},\ldots,{\bm{q}}^{(\ell_{n-i+1})}(\omega)\|,

(41)

where
\alpha
is a coefficient to be discussed. Since each combination of
{\mathcal{Q}}
skips exactly one element of
\{i,i+1,\ldots,n\}
, say
r
, the value that we have in the above determinant, leaving
\alpha
aside, coincides with
A_{r}({\bm{q}})(\omega)
up to
(-1)^{r}(-1)^{\sigma}
. The factor
(-1)^{r}
comes from the fact that to get
A_{r}({\bm{q}})(\omega)
we need to take
{\bm{q}}^{(n+1)}(\omega)
to the
\ell_{r}
-th column, while the factor
(-1)^{\sigma}
denotes the signature of a permutation of the elements in
\{i,i+1,\ldots,n\}-\{r\}
so that we reach exactly
A_{r}({\bm{q}})(\omega)
. Thus, Eq. (
41
) can be written as

\beta(-1)^{r}(-1)^{\sigma}A_{r}({\bm{q}})(\omega).

(42)

To discuss the value of the coefficient
\beta
, we argue as in the case
\ell_{1}\neq n+1
. The difference is that in Eq. (
35
), using Eq. (
32
) we have
\ell_{1}=n+1
and the derivative
{\bm{q}}^{(n+1)}(\omega)
appears multiplied by
(\omega^{\prime})^{n+1}\tilde{B}_{n+1,n+1}
. Again arguing as in the case
\ell_{1}\neq n+1
, we get that

\beta=(\omega^{\prime})^{L}\tilde{B}_{n+1,n+1}\tilde{B}_{i+1,\ell_{2}}\cdots\tilde{B}_{n,\ell_{n-i+1}}\Phi^{N}.

The power
L
equals the sum of
1,2,\ldots,n+1
minus
r
, so

L=\dfrac{n(n+1)}{2}+n+1-r.

On the other hand,

N=[(i+1)-\ell_{2}]+\cdots+[n-\ell_{n-i+1}]=[(i+1)+\cdots+n]-[\ell_{2}+\cdots+\ell_{n-i+1}].

Since
\{\ell_{2},\ldots,\ell_{n-i+1}\}\in{\mathcal{Q}}
, and the list
\{\ell_{2},\ldots,\ell_{n-i+1}\}
is the result of removing
r
from the list
\{i,i+1,\ldots,n\}
and reordering it,
\ell_{2}+\cdots+\ell_{n-i+1}=[i+(i+1)+\cdots+n]-r
, and hence
N=r-i
. Thus, Eq. (
42
) turns into

(\omega^{\prime})^{\frac{n(n+1)}{2}}(-1)^{r}(\omega^{\prime})^{n+1-r}\Phi^{r-i}A_{r}({\bm{q}})(\omega)(-1)^{\sigma}\tilde{B}_{n+1,n+1}\tilde{B}_{i+1,\ell_{2}}\cdots\tilde{B}_{n,\ell_{n-i+1}}.

(43)

Now to get the sum of all the determinants corresponding to
\ell_{1}=n+1
, i.e.
A^{(II)}_{i}({\bm{q}}(\omega))
, we need to sum Eq. (
43
) over the permutations
{\mathcal{P}}_{r}
, and then over the combinations
{\mathcal{Q}}
. However, this last sum is nothing else than the sum over
r
, from
r=i
to
r=n
. Thus, we get that

A^{(II)}_{i}({\bm{q}}(\omega))=(\omega^{\prime})^{\frac{n(n+1)}{2}}\sum_{r=i}^{n}(-1)^{r}(\omega^{\prime})^{n+1-r}\Phi^{r-i}A_{r}({\bm{q}})(\omega)\underbrace{\sum_{\sigma\in{\mathcal{P}}_{r}}(-1)^{\sigma}\tilde{B}_{n+1,n+1}\tilde{B}_{i+1,\ell_{2}}\cdots\tilde{B}_{n,\ell_{n-i+1}}}.

(44)

The underbraced expression corresponds, as in the case
\ell_{1}\neq n+1
, to an
(n-i+1)\times(n-i+1)
determinant
M^{n+1,i+1}_{r}
defined in the following way:

1.

If
j<r
, the
j
-th column of
M^{n+1,i+1}_{r}
consists of the values
\tilde{B}_{n+1,n+1-j},\tilde{B}_{n,n+1-j},\ldots,\tilde{B}_{i+1,n+1-j}
.

2.

If
j\geq r
, the
j
-th column of
M^{n+1,i+1}_{r}
consists of the values
\tilde{B}_{n+1,n-j},\tilde{B}_{n,n-j},\ldots,\tilde{B}_{i+1,n-j}
,

where we recall that
\tilde{B}_{k,k}=1
, and
\tilde{B}_{k,m}=0
when
k<m
. We will assume the convention

M^{n+1,i+1}_{0}=M^{n+1,i+1},\mbox{ and }M^{n+1,i+1}_{n+1}=(-1)^{n},

(45)

where
M^{n+1,i+1}
, without any subindex, was the underbraced determinant in Eq. (
39
). Furthermore, one can check that

M_{n-i+1}^{n+1,i+1}=M^{n,i+1}.

(46)

Therefore,

A^{(II)}_{i}({\bm{q}}(\omega))=(\omega^{\prime})^{\frac{n(n+1)}{2}}\sum_{r=i}^{n}(-1)^{r}(\omega^{\prime})^{n+1-r}\Phi^{r-i}A_{r}({\bm{q}})(\omega)M^{n+1,i+1}_{r}.

(47)

Since
A_{i}({\bm{q}}(\omega))=A^{(I)}_{i}({\bm{q}}(\omega))+A^{(II)}_{i}({\bm{q}}(\omega))
, finally we get

A_{i}({\bm{q}}(\omega))=(\omega^{\prime})^{\frac{n(n+1)}{2}}\Phi^{(n+1)-i}\Delta{\bm{q}}(\omega)M^{n+1,i+1}+(\omega^{\prime})^{\frac{n(n+1)}{2}}\sum_{r=i}^{n}(-1)^{r}(\omega^{\prime})^{n+1-r}\Phi^{r-i}A_{r}({\bm{q}})(\omega)M^{n+1,i+1}_{r},

(48)

and to find
I_{i}({\bm{q}}(\omega))
, which was the goal of this substep, we just need to divide Eq. (
48
) by
\Delta({\bm{q}}(\omega))
. Taking Lemma
10
into account, we deduce the following result.

Lemma 14
.

I_{i}({\bm{q}}(\omega))=\Phi^{(n+1)-i}M^{n+1,i+1}+\sum_{r=i}^{n}(-1)^{r}(\omega^{\prime})^{n+1-r}\Phi^{r-i}I_{r}({\bm{q}})(\omega)M^{n+1,i+1}_{r}.

(49)

Here we recall, from Eq. (
29
), that
\Phi:=K_{n}-\omega^{\prime}G_{n}
, where
K_{n}:=I_{n}({\bm{q}}(\omega))
,
G_{n}:=I_{n}({\bm{q}})(\omega)
.

4.3

Substep
(ii.3)

In this last substep, we will make use of Eq. (
49
), for
i=1,\ldots,n-1
, to get rid of
\omega^{\prime}
and find Möbius-commuting invariants. Notice that because of the notation introduced in Eq. (
29
), when expanding the powers of
\Phi
we get powers of
\omega^{\prime}
as well. We want to reach an expression where the coefficient of each power of
\omega^{\prime}
is explicit. To do that, we first express Eq. (
49
) as one sum, using the convention in Eq. (
45
); here we recover the notation in Eq. (
29
), and assume by convention
G_{n+1}:=-1
. Thus,

K_{i}=\sum_{r=i}^{n+1}(-1)^{r}(\omega^{\prime})^{n+1-r}(K_{n}-\omega^{\prime}G_{n})^{r-i}G_{r}M^{n+1,i+1}_{r}.

(50)

Calling
p=n-r+1
, we get

K_{i}=\sum_{p=0}^{n-i+1}(-1)^{n+1-p}(\omega^{\prime})^{p}(K_{n}-\omega^{\prime}G_{n})^{n+1-i-p}G_{n+1-p}M^{n+1,i+1}_{n+1-p}.

(51)

Reordering the sum above and using the binomial expansion for
(K_{n}-s^{\prime}G_{n})^{j}
, we reach

K_{i}=\sum_{j=0}^{n-i+1}{\sum_{k=0}^{j}{(-1)^{k}\binom{j}{k}M_{j}^{n+1,i+1}{\omega^{\prime}}^{n-i+1-k}G_{n}^{j-k}K_{n}^{k}G_{j+i}}},

(52)

where we recall that
G_{n+1}=-1
.

Next we need to reorder the indexes above so that the coefficient of the powers of
\omega^{\prime}
is explicit. We can do it by rearranging the indices
j
and
k
as follows

K_{i}=\sum_{k=0}^{n-i+1}{(-1)^{k}K_{n}^{k}\omega^{\prime n-i+1-k}\sum_{j=k}^{n-i+1}{\binom{j}{k}M_{j}^{n+1,i+1}G_{n}^{j-k}G_{j+i}}}.

(53)

We observe that for each
i
, where recall that
i=1,2,\ldots,n-1
, the degree of
K_{i}
as a polynomial in
\omega^{\prime}
is
n-i+1
. In particular, the degree of
K_{1}
is
n
, and the degree of
K_{n-1}
is
2
. Considering

\alpha_{1}:=\omega^{\prime},\alpha_{2}:=(\omega^{\prime})^{2},\ldots,\alpha_{n}:=(\omega^{\prime})^{n},

we notice that when collecting Eq. (
53
) for
i=1,\ldots,n-1
, we get a linear system
{\mathcal{S}}
in
\alpha_{1},\alpha_{2},\ldots,\alpha_{n}
consisting of
n
unknowns and
n-1
equations. However, one can prove that for all
i
the coefficient of
\alpha_{1}:=\omega^{\prime}
is always zero, i.e.,
K_{i}
does not depend on
\alpha_{1}:=\omega^{\prime}
: indeed, from the formula in Eq. (
53
), for any
i
, the coefficient of
\omega^{\prime}
corresponds to the indices
k=n-i
and
j=n-i,j=n-i+1
, which yields

(-1)^{n-i}G_{n}K_{n}^{n-i}\left(M_{n-i}^{n+1,i+1}-(n-i+1)M_{n-i+1}^{n+1,i+1}\right).

But this number is zero, as stated in the following lemma, which is proven in Appendix I.

Lemma 15
.

M_{n-i}^{n+1,i+1}-(n-i+1)M_{n-i+1}^{n+1,i+1}=0
.

Now we are almost ready to find our Möbius-commuting invariants. Because of Lemma
15
, the system
{\mathcal{S}}
is in fact a system with
n
unknowns, namely
\alpha_{2},\ldots,\alpha_{n}
. Furthermore,
{\mathcal{S}}
is upper triangular. Using back substitution, we find the solution of
{\mathcal{S}}
as

\alpha_{k}:=(\omega^{\prime})^{k}=\dfrac{\sum_{i=0}^{k}{M_{i}^{n+1,n+2-k}K_{n-k+1+i}K_{n}^{i}}}{\sum_{i=0}^{k}{M_{i}^{n+1,n-k+2}G_{n-k+1+i}G_{n}^{i}}},\quad 2\leq k\leq n,

(54)

where we assume that
K_{n+1}=G_{n+1}=-1
. Note that, for all
k\in\{2,3,\ldots,n\}
,
\alpha_{k}
is a rational function of the
K_{j},G_{j}
whose numerator only depends on the
K_{j}
and the denominator only depends on the
G_{j}
.

Next we need to invoke the additional invariant
I_{0}
, introduced in Eq. (
15
), that we did not use so far. The expansion of
I_{0}({\bm{q}}(\omega))
can be directly found as in the case of
I_{n}({\bm{q}}(\omega))
; denoting
K_{0}:=I_{0}({\bm{q}}(\omega))
,
G_{0}:=I_{0}({\bm{q}})(\omega)
, we get that

K_{0}=\dfrac{1}{2}\dfrac{n+2}{n}K_{n}^{2}+\omega^{\prime 2}(G_{0}-\dfrac{1}{2}\dfrac{n+2}{n}).

(55)

Now if we isolate
(\omega^{\prime})^{2}
in Eq. (
55
), introduce
k=2
in Eq. (
54
) and set the obtained expressions to be equal, we get that

\dfrac{K_{0}-\dfrac{1}{2}\dfrac{n+2}{n}K_{n}^{2}}{K_{n-1}+M_{2}^{n+1,n}K_{n}^{2}}=\dfrac{G_{0}-\dfrac{1}{2}\dfrac{n+2}{n}G_{n}^{2}}{G_{n-1}+M_{2}^{n+1,n}G_{n}^{2}}.

(56)

Getting back to the notation
K_{i}:=I_{i}({\bm{q}}(\omega))
and
G_{i}:=I_{i}({\bm{q}})(\omega)
, we can rewrite Eq. (
56
) as

\dfrac{I_{0}({\bm{q}}(\omega))-\dfrac{1}{2}\dfrac{n+2}{n}I_{n}^{2}({\bm{q}}(\omega))}{I_{n-1}({\bm{q}}(\omega))+M_{2}^{n+1,n}I_{n}^{2}({\bm{q}}(\omega))}=\dfrac{I_{0}({\bm{q}})(\omega)-\dfrac{1}{2}\dfrac{n+2}{n}I_{n}^{2}({\bm{q}})(\omega)}{I_{n-1}({\bm{q}})(\omega)+M_{2}^{n+1,n}I_{n}^{2}({\bm{q}})(\omega)}.

(57)

But Eq. (
57
) is expressing, exactly, that

F_{1}({\bm{q}}):=\dfrac{I_{0}({\bm{q}})-\dfrac{1}{2}\dfrac{n+2}{n}I_{n}^{2}({\bm{q}})}{I_{n-1}({\bm{q}})+M_{2}^{n+1,n}I_{n}^{2}({\bm{q}})}

(58)

is Möbius-commuting, i.e. that
F_{1}({\bm{q}}(\omega))=F_{1}({\bm{q}})(\omega)
. We can find other invariants in a similar way, by considering
k=3,\ldots,n
in Eq. (
54
) and eliminating
\omega^{\prime}
with the help of Eq. (
55
). So we have proven the following theorem.

Theorem 16
.

Let
F_{1}({\bm{q}})
be the expression in Eq. (
58
), and for
k=3,\ldots,n
, let

F_{k-1}({\bm{q}}):=\dfrac{\left(\sum_{i=0}^{k}{M_{i}^{n+1,n-k+2}I_{n-k+1+i}({\bm{q}})I_{n}^{i}({\bm{q}})}\right)^{e_{k}/k}}{\left(I_{n-1}({\bm{q}})+M_{2}^{n+1,n}I_{n}^{2}({\bm{q}})\right)^{e_{k}/2}},\quad 3\leq k\leq n,

(59)

where
e_{k}
is, for
3\leq k\leq n
, the least common multiple of
2,k
, i.e.
e_{k}=\emph{lcm}(2,k)
. Then, the
F_{\ell}
, for
\ell=1,\ldots,n-1
, are Möbius-commuting, i.e.
F_{\ell}({\bm{q}}(\omega))=F_{\ell}({\bm{q}})(\omega)
.

5

Conclusion

We have presented an algorithm, generalizing the algorithm in
[
4
]
, to compute the affine equivalences, if any, between two parametric curves in any dimension. Our strategy relies on bivariate factoring, and avoids polynomial system solving. The algorithm works for rational curves and also non-algebraic parametric curves with meromorphic components, admitting a rational inverse. We have implemented the algorithm in
Maple
, and evidence of its performance has been presented.

The algorithm works whenever not all the Möbius-commuting invariants are constant. This happens generically, but identifying the curves where this does not occur, as well as providing a solution to the problem for this special case, are questions that we pose here as open problems.

Additionally, in the case of non-algebraic curves, right now we need some hypotheses that are not always satisfied: for instance, planar curves like the cycloid, or the tractrix, or classical planar spirals, do not satisfy our hypotheses. However, we have observed that the algorithm seems to work also for many of those curves, which makes us think that our hypotheses could be relaxed. This requires more theoretical work regarding analytic curves.

It would be desirable to extend our ideas to the case of rational surfaces
/
hypersurfaces. This probably requires some extra hypotheses, e.g. non-existence of base points or special types of surfaces
/
hypersurfaces, that allow us to guess the type of transformation that we have in the parameter space: such transformation would play a role similar to the role played by Möbius transformations here. These are questions that we would like to address in the future.
