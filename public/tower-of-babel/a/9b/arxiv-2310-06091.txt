A trion in a magnetic field revisited
I. A. Aleksandrov, A. Kudlis, I. A. Shelykh

Abstract
We revisit the problem of a two dimensional trion in an external magnetic field. We demonstrate that the approximations used previously for finding the energy spectrum of this system break down in the experimentally accessible range of magnetic fields. It is shown that the neglect of the Coulomb-induced mixing of different Landau levels corresponding to non-interacting particles leads to a strong underestimation of the trion binding energies even at extremely high magnetic fields (hundreds of Tesla). Moreover, proper account of the Coulomb effects for certain values of the parameters can lead to the appearance of additional discrete trion states which were overlooked previously. Finally, we provide a database of the matrix elements necessary for calculation of the magnetotrion spectra for a wide class of materials.



I
Introduction

Understanding of magneto-optical spectra of various quasi-two-dimensional (2D) electron-hole (
e
–
h
) complexes in external magnetic fields is an important problem in condensed matter theory
Miura 2008
;
Arora 2021
. The resonances corresponding to such composite quasiparticles in magneto-optical spectra are routinely observed in semiconductor quantum wells and 2D materials.
These experimental observations give strong motivation for theorists to provide a corresponding quantitative description together with an unambiguous classification of the spectra. The developed theoretical techniques should be sufficiently flexible and allow one to examine a broad variety of materials with different effective masses of carriers and types of the screening in experimentally relevant diapasons of magnetic fields.

The fundamental basis for theoretical description of magneto-optic response was laid by L. D. Landau decades ago in his seminal work introducing the concept of Landau levels (LLs) for a single charged particle in an external homogeneous magnetic field
Landau 1930
. The next step was consideration of an exciton, a neutral composite particle consisting of an electron and a hole bound together by the Coulomb attraction
Akimoto and Hasegawa 1967
;
Edelstein et al. 1989
;
MacDonald and Ritchie 1986
;
Potemski et al. 1991
;
Yang and Sham 1987
;
Bodnar et al. 2017
. In this case, it was demonstrated that center-of-mass coordinates of an exciton can be separated from the internal degrees of freedom
Gor’kov and Dzyaloshinskii 1968
;
Avron and Herbst 1978
. This allowed one to build the theory of a magnetoexciton using a perturbative approach
Lerner and Lozovik 1980
, WKB approximation
Akimoto and Hasegawa 1967
, and brute force numerical modelling
Edelstein et al. 1989
, and provide the classification of the corresponding spectra
MacDonald and Ritchie 1986
.

Besides excitons, electron-hole complexes containing more than two particles, such as biexcitons
He et al. 2016
;
Stevens et al. 2018
;
Torche and Bester 2021
and trions
Finkelstein and Bar-Joseph 1995
;
Shields et al. 1995
;
Sanvitto et al. 2002
;
Smoleński et al. 2019
;
Munteanu et al. 2000
;
Andronikov et al. 2005
;
Klein et al. 2022
;
Van Tuan et al. 2022
;
Wang et al. 2020
;
Li et al. 2020
can be formed. The latter case is of particular interest as a trion (
T
), being a composite particle consisting of two holes and one electron (
\textup{X}^{+}
) or two electrons and one hole (
\textup{X}^{-}
), possesses a net electric charge, and its center-of-mass dynamics should be thus strongly affected by an external magnetic field
Stébé et al. 1987
;
Chapman et al. 1998
;
Finkelstein et al. 1995
;
Wojs and Hawrylak 1995
;
Palacios et al. 1996
;
Whittaker and Shields 1997
;
Stébé et al. 1998
;
Kozhevnikov et al. 1997
;
Hayne et al. 1999
;
Riva et al. 2002
;
Riva et al. 2001
;
Kuznetsov et al. 2018
;
Filikhin et al. 2018
;
Chang and Chang 2022
;
Chang and Chang 2021
;
Stébé and Moradi 2000
;
Kaur et al. 2019
. Although for the case of a magnetotrion the center-of-mass coordinates cannot be straightforwardly separated as in the case of an exciton, one can take advantage of an another exact symmetry, namely magnetic translations, to reduce the number of the degrees of freedom
Schmelcher and Cederbaum 1991
. An additional quantum number characterizing trion states can be thus introduced, together with the total orbital angular momentum
M_{z}
along the magnetic-field direction, and the spins of electrons
S_{e}
and holes
S_{h}

Dzyubenko and Sivachenko 2000
.

A number of different techniques were employed to analyze magnetotrion spectra quantitatively, such as variational methods with Slater-type orbitals as trial functions
Chang and Chang 2021
;
Chang and Chang 2022
and a stochastic variational approach
Riva et al. 2002
;
Riva et al. 2001
. These methods, however, possess an important drawback as they do not present a clear classification of the energy levels. Another possible approach is based on the perturbative treatment where the mixing of the LLs due to the the Coulomb interaction is supposed to be small
Dzyubenko and Sivachenko 2000
;
Dzyubenko 2000
;
Dzyubenko 2001
;
Kuznetsov et al. 2018
. However, as we will show below, this approach neglects an entire class of the important matrix elements, and not only gives wrong values of trion energies, but even fails to reproduce the correct number of the discrete states in the experimentally relevant range of magnetic fields. Revisiting the problem is thus a relevant task.

When considering a trion in an external magnetic field, one should analyze the regimes of strong and weak fields separately. Indeed, on the one hand, in the case of a weak magnetic field, the simplest natural approximation would be to consider a trion as a single particle with the net charge
Q_{\textup{T}}=e
and a mass equal to the total mass of the particles forming it,
m_{\textup{T}}=2m_{e}+m_{h}
. In this limit, the Coulomb interaction binding the particles together dominates over the magnetic field effects, so that

E_{\textup{T}}\gg\hbar\omega_{\textup{T}}=\frac{\hbar|e|B}{m_{\textup{T}}c},

(1)

where
E_{\textup{T}}
is the binding energy of a trion and
\omega_{\textup{T}}=|e|B/(m_{\textup{T}}c)
is the trion cyclotron frequency.

On the other hand, in the case of strong magnetic fields, one can consider three particles interacting with a magnetic field as almost independent ones. It was argued that the Coulomb interaction in this case can be treated as a perturbation leading to a weak mixing of the LLs corresponding to the individual particles. This approach is expected to be valid if
Dzyubenko and Sivachenko 2000
;
Dzyubenko 2000
;
Dzyubenko 2001

\hbar\omega_{e},\,\hbar\omega_{h},\,|\hbar\omega_{e}-\hbar\omega_{h}|\gg\frac{e^{2}}{\varepsilon\lambda}=\frac{e^{2}}{\varepsilon}\sqrt{\frac{|e|B}{\hbar c}},

(2)

where
\omega_{e}
and
\omega_{h}
are the cyclotron frequencies of an electron and a hole, respectively,
\varepsilon
is a permittivity of a medium, and
\lambda=\sqrt{\hbar c/(|e|B)}
, with is a magnetic length. This corresponds to fields

B\gg\frac{|e|^{3}m_{e,h}^{2}c}{\varepsilon^{2}\hbar^{2}}.

(3)

Here we employ the Gaussian-cgs units (the SI expressions can be obtained by substituting
\varepsilon\to 4\pi\varepsilon\varepsilon_{0}
and omitting
c
). The condition (
2
) seems to have a clear physical meaning, being equivalent to the requirement that the characteristic mixing energy between the LLs were much smaller than the distance between them.

However, one can note that there are certain problems with this criterion. First, Eq. (
3
) imposes a very strong restriction on the values of the magnetic field strength. For instance, in the case of GaAs, it corresponds to
B\sim 1000~\textup{T}
or stronger, which is out of the experimentally accessible range.

Second, even if the condition (
3
) is satisfied, one can argue that the mixing of the LLs should be taken into account anyway. Indeed, from the viewpoint of the perturbation theory, the corresponding second-order energy correction is proportional to the square of the Coulomb matrix element (which scales as
\sqrt{B}
), divided by the distance between the LLs (which is proportional to
B
). This combination is thus independent on the magnetic field, which means that the increase of the latter does not reduce the role of the mixing terms.

These two considerations motivated us to go beyond the approximation used in Refs.
Dzyubenko and Sivachenko 2000
;
Dzyubenko 2000
;
Dzyubenko 2001
and treat the Coulomb interaction terms in the magnetotrion problem exactly. In this paper, we present the corresponding development of the operator method introduced in Refs.
Dzyubenko and Sivachenko 2000
;
Dzyubenko 2000
;
Dzyubenko 2001
generalizing it for the case when mixing of the LLs is accounted for, and provide a reader with all necessary formulae for all relevant matrix elements. We show that the approach used previously considerably underestimates the trion binding energy and, moreover, fails to reproduce completely the discrete part of the spectrum.

The paper has the following structure. In Sec.
II
, we briefly outline our theoretical approach (detailed derivations are given in the Appendices). In Sec.
III
, we provide a quantitative analysis of trion energy spectra in the cases of GaAs and CdTe quantum wells (QWs) for various values of the magnetic field strength. Conclusions are summarized in Sec.
IV
.

Throughout the text, we denote the electron (hole) charge and mass by
e<0
and
m_{e}
(
-e
and
m_{h})
.

II
Theoretical description

In this section, we discuss the main theoretical aspects of the calculations of trion energy spectra. We briefly describe the symmetries of the problem, the basis used, and present the final expressions for the matrix elements of the full trion Hamiltonian. In the main text we present only the most important steps of the derivation, whereas detailed description of the calculation procedures is given in the Appendices. We tried to make the paper self-consistent, so Appendices contain all relevant expressions for the cases of individual electrons, excitons, trions, and unbound electron-exciton pairs placed in external magnetic fields (see Appendix A, B, C, and D, respectively).

The Hamiltonian of a three-particle system involving a pair of two dimensional (2D) electrons with coordinates
\mathbf{r}_{1}
and
\mathbf{r}_{2}
and a hole with coordinates
\mathbf{r}_{h}
reads

\hat{H}^{(\textup{T})}=\hat{H}_{0}+\hat{V}_{eh}+\hat{V}_{ee},

(4)

where

\displaystyle\hat{H}_{0}

\displaystyle=\sum_{j=1,2}\frac{\hat{\boldsymbol{\pi}}_{j}^{2}}{2m_{e}}+\frac{\hat{\boldsymbol{\pi}}_{h}^{2}}{2m_{h}},

(5)

\displaystyle\hat{V}_{eh}

\displaystyle=-\sum_{j=1,2}\frac{e^{2}}{\lambda}V(|\mathbf{r}_{j}-\mathbf{r}_{h}|),

(6)

\displaystyle\hat{V}_{ee}

\displaystyle=\frac{e^{2}}{\lambda}V(|\mathbf{r}_{1}-\mathbf{r}_{2}|).

(7)

Here the momenta operators are obtained by the canonical substitution
\hat{\boldsymbol{\pi}}_{j}=-i\hbar\boldsymbol{\nabla}_{j}-(e/c)\boldsymbol{A}(\boldsymbol{r}_{j})
,
\hat{\boldsymbol{\pi}}_{h}=-i\hbar\boldsymbol{\nabla}_{h}+(e/c)\mathbf{A}(\mathbf{r}_{h})
,
\lambda=\sqrt{\hbar c/(|e|B)}
, and
V(r)
is a dimensionless function describing the interaction potential. In the current paper, we focus on the case of conventional semiconductor QWs and thus employ the standard expression for a Coulomb potential,

V(r)=\frac{\lambda}{\varepsilon r}.

(8)

We did not wright explicitly the terms, correspopnding to the direct action of the magnetic field on spins of individual electrons and holes, as their account is trivial.

In contrast to the case of a single charged particle (an electron or a hole), where the spectrum is given by the discrete harmonic oscillator series (LLs), the spectrum of a multi-particle complex is substantially modified by the Coulomb interaction, and the problem cannot be solved analytically. Therefore, we first construct the basis consisting of the eigenfunctions of the free particle Hamiltonian
\hat{H}_{0}
and then diagonalize the matrix of the total Hamiltonian
\hat{H}^{(\textup{T})}
numerically. When choosing the basis, we fully take into account the complete symmetries of the system, which allows us to substantially reduce the calculation costs.

In the case of the three interacting particles, the operator of the
magnetic translations
defined as
Lerner and Lozovik 1980
;
Dzyubenko and Sivachenko 2000
;
Dzyubenko 2000
;
Dzyubenko 2001

\hat{\mathbf{K}}=\sum_{j=1,2}\hat{\boldsymbol{\pi}}_{j}+\hat{\boldsymbol{\pi}}_{h}+\frac{e}{c}\sum_{j=1,2}\mathbf{B}\times\mathbf{r}_{j}-\frac{e}{c}\,\mathbf{B}\times\mathbf{r}_{h}

(9)

commutes with the Hamiltonian
\hat{H}^{(\textup{T})}
. Note that this commutator is proportional to the net electric charge of the system, so the components of the corresponding operator for an exciton do commute (see Appendix
B
). However, the
x
and
y
components of
\hat{\mathbf{K}}
do not commute with each other,
[\hat{K}_{x},\,\hat{K}_{y}]=i|e|\hbar B/c
, which means that in the case of a trion, when constructing the basis, one can take advantage of only one integral of motion (for instance, either
\hat{K}_{x}
or
\hat{K}_{y}
). Instead of choosing a specific component of the vector
\hat{\mathbf{K}}
(either
\hat{K}_{x}
or
\hat{K}_{y}
), one can use the operator
\hat{\mathbf{K}}^{2}

Dzyubenko 2000
, which commutes with the Hamiltonian as well.

Let us perform the following coordinate transformation:

\displaystyle\mathbf{R}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}\,\big(\mathbf{r}_{1}+\mathbf{r}_{2}\big),\quad\mathbf{r}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}\,\big(\mathbf{r}_{1}-\mathbf{r}_{2}\big).

(10)

In the most straightforward approach the basis functions corresponding to the case of non-interacting particles can be chosen as:

\varphi^{(\textup{T})}_{n_{1}m_{1}n_{2}m_{2}n_{h}m_{h}}(\mathbf{r},\mathbf{R},\mathbf{r}_{h})\\
=\varphi^{(e)}_{n_{1}m_{1}}(\mathbf{r})\varphi^{(e)}_{n_{2}m_{2}}(\mathbf{R})\varphi^{(h)}_{n_{h}m_{h}}(\mathbf{r}_{h}),

(11)

where
\varphi^{(h)}_{nm}(\mathbf{r})=[\varphi^{(e)}_{nm}(\mathbf{r})]^{*}
correspond to single particle wavefunctions in a magnetic field (see Appendix
A
). The quantum number
n=0,1,2,\dots
is the LL number, and
m=0,1,2,\dots
defines the angular momentum projection. However, this basis set completely disregards the translational symmetry of the system as the corresponding functions are characterized only by a well-defined projection of the total angular momentum
M_{z}=(n_{1}-m_{1})+(n_{2}-m_{2})-(n_{h}-m_{h})
and total electron spin
S_{e}
[
S_{e}=0
(spin-singlet state) for even
n_{1}-m_{1}
and
S_{e}=1
(spin-triplet state) for odd
n_{1}-m_{1}
; see Appendix
C
].

To account for the translational symmetry, one can perform a unitary transformation making the basis functions (
11
) also eigenfunctions of
\hat{\mathbf{K}}^{2}

Dzyubenko 2000
(see Appendix
C
for details), and get the following set of the basis functions

\displaystyle\psi_{n_{1}n_{2}n_{h}ml}(\mathbf{r},\mathbf{R},\mathbf{r}_{h})

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\sqrt{N_{n_{1}n_{2}n_{h}ml}}}\left[\dfrac{1}{\sqrt{2}}\left(\xi-\partialderivative{\xi^*}\right)\right]^{n_{1}}\left[\dfrac{1}{\sqrt{2}}\left(\xi_{R}-\partialderivative{\xi^*_R}\right)\right]^{n_{2}}\left[\dfrac{1}{\sqrt{2}}\left(\xi^{*}_{h}-\partialderivative{\xi_h}\right)\right]^{n_{h}}\psi_{ml}(\mathbf{r},\mathbf{R},\mathbf{r}_{h}),

(12)

where

\displaystyle\psi_{ml}(\mathbf{r},\mathbf{R},\mathbf{r}_{h})=\left(\xi^{*}\right)^{m}\left(\xi_{h}\right)^{l}\tilde{\Phi}^{(\textup{T})}_{0}\!(\mathbf{r},\!\mathbf{R},\mathbf{r}_{h})

(13)

and

\displaystyle\tilde{\Phi}^{(\textup{T})}_{0}\!(\mathbf{r},\!\mathbf{R},\mathbf{r}_{h})\!=\!\dfrac{\exp{-\xi\xi^*\!-\!\xi_R\xi^*_R\!-\!\xi_h\xi^*_h\!+\!\sqrt{2}\xi_h\xi^*_R}}{\sqrt{2}\left(2\pi\right)^{3/2}\lambda^{3}}.

(14)

is a normalized wave function of the ground state. The normalization factors
N_{n_{1}n_{2}n_{h}ml}
are calculated in Appendix
C
. The dimensionless coordinates read:
\xi=z/(2\lambda)
,
\xi_{R}=z_{R}/(2\lambda)
, and
\xi_{h}=z_{h}/(2\lambda)
, where
z=x+{i\mkern 1.0mu}y
. The angular momentum projection of the state (
12
) reads

M_{z}=n_{1}-m+n_{2}-(n_{h}-l),

(15)

while the spin quantum number is
S_{e}=0
(
S_{e}=1
) if
n_{1}-m
is even (odd). Note that the energies are degenerate with respect to the quantum number
k
associated with the operator
\mathbf{K}^{2}
, so we have set
k=0
.

The matrix elements of the full Hamiltonian (
4
) are defined by the following expression:

\displaystyle H^{(\textup{T})}_{n_{1}n_{2}n_{h}ml;n_{1}^{\prime}n_{2}^{\prime}n_{h}^{\prime}m^{\prime}l^{\prime}}

\displaystyle=E^{(\textup{T},0)}_{n_{1}+n_{2},n_{h}}\delta_{n_{1}n_{1}^{\prime}}\delta_{n_{2}n_{2}^{\prime}}\delta_{n_{h}n_{h}^{\prime}}\delta_{mm^{\prime}}\delta_{ll^{\prime}}

\displaystyle+V^{(eh)}_{n_{1}n_{2}n_{h}ml;n_{1}^{\prime}n_{2}^{\prime}n_{h}^{\prime}m^{\prime}l^{\prime}}

\displaystyle+V^{(ee)}_{n_{1}n_{2}n_{h}ml;n_{1}^{\prime}n_{2}^{\prime}n_{h}^{\prime}m^{\prime}l^{\prime}},

(16)

where

E^{(\textup{T},0)}_{n_{e},n_{h}}=\hbar\omega_{e}\bigg(1+n_{e}\bigg)+\hbar\omega_{h}\bigg(\frac{1}{2}+n_{h}\bigg)

(17)

corresponds to the LLs of non-interacting particles,
n_{e}=n_{1}+n_{2}
and
\omega_{e,h}=|e|B/(m_{e,h}c)
. The Coulomb interaction matrix elements
V^{(eh)}_{n_{1}n_{h}ml;n_{1}^{\prime}n_{2}^{\prime}n_{h}^{\prime}m^{\prime}l^{\prime}}
and
V^{(ee)}_{n_{1}n_{2}n_{h}ml;n_{1}^{\prime}n_{2}^{\prime}n_{h}^{\prime}m^{\prime}l^{\prime}}
are bulky and given in Appendic C ( Eqs. (
96
) and (
108
), respectively).

In Ref.
Dzyubenko 2000
the Coulomb mixing was taken into account only within the subspaces corresponding to
n_{e}=n_{e}\textasciiacute
and
n_{h}=n_{h}\textasciiacute
, i.e. it was thus assumed, that
n_{e},n_{h}
remain good quantum numbers of the full interaction problem. It was argued that this approximation should be valid in the case of very strong magnetic fields, where the magnetic energy dominates over the Coulomb energy. However, as was discussed in the Introduction [see Eq. (
3
) and below], this approach can hardly be justified in any physically relevant situation and exact treatment of the Coulomb mixing is necessary.

The resulting spectrum contains discrete levels and continuous regions, corresponding to the states of unbound electron-exciton pairs. To identify the onset of the energy continuum, we additionally perform the independent calculations for the case of an uncoupled electron and an exciton (see Appendix
D
).

S_{e}
into
(2S_{h}+1)(2S_{e}+1)=2(2S_{e}+1)
sublevels.

IV
Conclusion

In this study, we revisited the problem of a 2D trion in an external magnetic field. We demonstrated, that the mixing between different Landau levels induced by Coulomb interaction plays crucial role in the determination of the energy spectrum of the problem and can not be neglected even in the limit of very strong magnetic fields. This is because magnetic field increases not only the distance between the Landau levels, but Coulomb matrix elements as well via decrease of the magnetic length. We have shown that proper account of the Coulomb mixing not only leads to the substantial increase of magnetotrion binding energy, but can also lead to qualitative reshaping of the spectrum, resulting in the appearance of additional discrete energy states.

Appendix A
Single electron in a magnetic field

Here and in Appendices
B
and
C
, we first briefly outline how the energy spectrum can be computed and then provide a detailed theoretical description of the corresponding problems. In Appendix
D
, we give a direct recipe for calculating the spectrum for an unbound electron-exciton pair, since we presented all the necessary theoretical calculations in the previous sections.

A.1
Energy spectrum

In the case of a single electron (hole) in a magnetic field, the energy is given by a simple formula describing the spectrum of a harmonic oscillator. The corresponding wave functions
\varphi^{(e)}_{nm}(\mathbf{r})
are constructed explicitly and form a basis in which the Hamiltonian is diagonal:

\displaystyle H^{(e)}_{n^{\prime};n}=E^{(e)}_{n}\delta_{nn^{\prime}},

(19)

where
n,n^{\prime}=0,1,2,\dots
and the eigenvalues correspond to the Landau levels (LLs),

E_{n}^{(e)}=\hbar\omega_{e}\bigg(\frac{1}{2}+n\bigg).

(20)

Here
\omega_{e}=|e|B/(m_{e}c)
is the electron cyclotron frequency. The energy is degenerate with respect to the second quantum number
m
. The
z
projection of the orbital angular momentum is
m_{z}=n-m
.

A.2
Details

We start with the Hamiltonian, which in the case of a single electron interacting with a constant uniform magnetic field reads

\hat{H}^{(e)}=\frac{\hat{\boldsymbol{\pi}}_{e}^{2}}{2m_{e}},

(21)

where
\hat{\boldsymbol{\pi}}_{e}=-i\hbar\boldsymbol{\nabla}-(e/c)\mathbf{A}(\mathbf{r})
and we employ a symmetric gauge,
\mathbf{A}(\mathbf{r})=(\mathbf{B}\times\mathbf{r})/2
with
\mathbf{B}
directed along the
z
axis. We assume that the particle is confined within the
xy
plane and its quantum state is described by a wave function depending solely on
x
and
y
(or polar coordinates
r
and
\varphi
).

In the external field specified above, the Hamiltonian (
21
) obviously commutes with the operator of the angular momentum projection
\hat{\ell}_{z}=-i\hbar\partial_{\varphi}
, so the eigenfunctions of
H^{(e)}
will also be characterized by the corresponding quantum number
m_{z}
, i.e., one can utilize the ansatz
\psi(r,\varphi)=\mathrm{e}^{im_{z}\varphi}R(r)
. The ordinary differential equation for
R(r)
can then be solved analytically (see, e.g., Ref.
Landau and Lifshitz 1977
). Here we will use another approach based on the formalism of creation and annihilation operators (see, e.g., Ref.
Dzyubenko 2000
).

First, let us introduce the one-particle normalized vacuum state in the coordinate representation:

\Phi^{(e)}_{0}(\mathbf{r})=\langle\mathbf{r}|0\rangle=\frac{1}{\lambda\sqrt{2\pi}}\,\mathrm{e}^{-r^{2}/(4\lambda^{2})},

(22)

where

\lambda=\sqrt{\frac{\hbar c}{|e|B}}

(23)

is the so-called magnetic length, which was also introduced in the main text. It is convenient to use a complex variable
z=r\mathrm{e}^{i\varphi}=x+iy
and its complex conjugate
z^{*}
. We will also need the following creation operators in the coordinate representation:

\displaystyle\hat{A}_{e}^{\dagger}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}\bigg(\frac{z}{2\lambda}-2\lambda\,\frac{\partial}{\partial z^{*}}\bigg),

(24)

\displaystyle\hat{B}_{e}^{\dagger}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}\bigg(\frac{z^{*}}{2\lambda}-2\lambda\,\frac{\partial}{\partial z}\bigg),

(25)

Together with the annihilation operators
\hat{A}_{e}
and
\hat{B}_{e}
, they obey the relations
[\hat{A}_{e},\,\hat{A}_{e}^{\dagger}]=1
,
[\hat{B}_{e},\,\hat{B}_{e}^{\dagger}]=1
, and
[\hat{A}_{e},\,\hat{B}_{e}]=[\hat{A}_{e},\,\hat{B}_{e}^{\dagger}]=0
. These operators allow one to generate the following wave functions:

\varphi^{(e)}_{nm}(\mathbf{r})=\frac{1}{\sqrt{n!m!}}\,(\hat{A}_{e}^{\dagger})^{n}(\hat{B}_{e}^{\dagger})^{m}\Phi^{(e)}_{0}(\mathbf{r}),

(26)

where
n
,
m=0
,
1
,
2
, … One can explicitly verify that these functions form an orthonormal and complete set of the Hamiltonian eigenfunctions and the corresponding energies are given by Eq. (
20
). Instead of the non-negative integers
n
and
m
, one can also label the solutions by the following numbers:

\displaystyle\tilde{n}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\mathrm{min}\,(n,\,m),

(27)

\displaystyle\tilde{m}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle m-n.

(28)

These allow one to provide the following explicit form of the functions (
26
):

\displaystyle\varphi^{(e)}_{nm}(\mathbf{r})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{(-1)^{\tilde{n}}}{\lambda}\sqrt{\frac{2^{|\tilde{m}|}\tilde{n}!}{2\pi(\tilde{n}+|\tilde{m}|)!}}\bigg(\frac{r}{2\lambda}\bigg)^{|\tilde{m}|}

(29)

\displaystyle\times

\displaystyle\mathrm{e}^{-i\tilde{m}\varphi}L^{|\tilde{m}|}_{\tilde{n}}\bigg(\frac{r^{2}}{2\lambda^{2}}\bigg)\,\mathrm{e}^{-r^{2}/(4\lambda^{2})},

where
L^{|\tilde{m}|}_{\tilde{n}}
are the Laguerre polynomials. In terms of
n
and
m
, the explicit form (
29
) strongly depends on whether
n>m
or not, so using
\tilde{n}
and
\tilde{m}
is often more convenient. The angular momentum projection reads
m_{z}=-\tilde{m}=n-m
.

Appendix B
Exciton
(\textup{X})
in a magnetic field

In the case of two- and theree-particle systems considered in what follows, the free LLs are modified due to the Coulomb interaction and the Hamiltonian eigenfunctions cannot be found analytically. Next, we will discuss how the energy eigenvalues can be obtained by diagonalizing the corresponding matrix.

B.1
Spectrum calculation recipe

The exciton energy spectrum turns out to be striped — it contains continuous regions separated by empty areas. To evaluate the energies, one has first to separate the center-of-mass motion by introducing a conserved vector
\mathbf{K}
(see the next section for details). Using then the basis set (
39
), one obtains the matrix elements in the form

H^{(\textup{X})}_{\mathbf{K},n^{\prime}m^{\prime};nm}=E^{(\textup{X},0)}_{nm}\delta_{nn^{\prime}}\delta_{mm^{\prime}}+V_{\mathbf{K},n^{\prime}m^{\prime};nm},

(30)

where
n
and
m
are non-negative integer numbers,
E^{(\textup{X},0)}_{nm}
is defined in Eq. (
40
), while the expression for
V_{\mathbf{K},n^{\prime}m^{\prime};nm}
is presented in Eq. (
52
). The Hamiltonian is block-diagonal in
M_{z}=n-m
, due to the angular momentum conservation. Having constructed the matrix (
30
), we should diagonalize it varying
\mathbf{K}
and
M_{z}
. In fact, the matrix elements depend only on the magnitude
|\mathbf{K}|
and
M_{z}
. Within numerical computations, one has to make sure that for given
|\mathbf{K}|
and
M_{z}
, the quantum numbers
n
and
m
change in a sufficiently large range, so that the results converge to a desired accuracy.

B.2
Details

As will be seen below, in the case of a two-dimensional electron-hole (
e
–
h
) system, it is possible to separate the center-of-mass coordinates and make use of the one-particle solutions (
29
). The two-particle Hamiltonian has the form

\hat{H}^{(\textup{X})}=\frac{\hat{\boldsymbol{\pi}}_{e}^{2}}{2m_{e}}+\frac{\hat{\boldsymbol{\pi}}_{h}^{2}}{2m_{h}}-\frac{e^{2}}{\lambda}V(|\mathbf{r}_{e}-\mathbf{r}_{h}|),

(31)

where
\hat{\boldsymbol{\pi}}_{e}=-i\hbar\boldsymbol{\nabla}_{e}-(e/c)\mathbf{A}(\mathbf{r}_{e})
,
\hat{\boldsymbol{\pi}}_{h}=-i\hbar\boldsymbol{\nabla}_{h}+(e/c)\mathbf{A}(\mathbf{r}_{h})
, and
V(r)
is a dimensionless function governing the interaction potential [for the usual Coulomb interaction without the screening effects, it reads
V(r)=\lambda/(\varepsilon r)
]. Although the interparticle-interaction law has nothing to do with the magnetic length, its typical values does involve
\lambda
due to the localization of the wave functions in the external magnetic field [for instance, the matrix elements of
V(r)=\lambda/(\varepsilon r)
computed in the basis of the Landau wave functions are
\lambda
-independent].

Let us now take advantage of the translational symmetry of the problem and separate the center-of-mass motion. As was shown in Ref.
Gor’kov and Dzyaloshinskii 1968
, the Hamiltonian (
31
) commutes with the following vector operator:

\displaystyle\hat{\mathbf{K}}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\hat{\boldsymbol{\pi}}_{e}+\hat{\boldsymbol{\pi}}_{h}+\frac{e}{c}\,\mathbf{B}\times(\mathbf{r}_{e}-\mathbf{r}_{h})

(32)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-i\hbar\boldsymbol{\nabla}_{e}-i\hbar\boldsymbol{\nabla}_{h}+\frac{e}{c}\,\big[\mathbf{A}(\mathbf{r}_{e})-\mathbf{A}(\mathbf{r}_{h})\big].

Since
\hat{K}_{x}
and
\hat{K}_{y}
also commute with each other, the Hamiltonian eigenfunctions can be characterized by the corresponding vectors
\mathbf{K}
consisting of two quantum numbers
K_{x}
and
K_{y}
. Note that in the absence of the external field,
\mathbf{K}
is the total momentum of the particles. Let us introduce the center-of-mass position
\mathbf{R}
and relative position
\mathbf{r}
:

\displaystyle\mathbf{R}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{m_{e}\mathbf{r}_{e}+m_{h}\mathbf{r}_{h}}{m_{e}+m_{h}},

(33)

\displaystyle\mathbf{r}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\mathbf{r}_{e}-\mathbf{r}_{h}.

(34)

The operator (
32
) takes the form

\hat{\mathbf{K}}=-i\hbar\boldsymbol{\nabla}_{R}+\frac{e}{2c}\,\mathbf{B}\times\mathbf{r}.

(35)

The Hamiltonian (
31
) now reads

\displaystyle\hat{H}^{(\textup{X})}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-\frac{\hbar^{2}}{2m_{eh}}\,\Delta_{R}-\frac{\hbar^{2}}{2\mu}\,\Delta_{r}

(36)

\displaystyle+

\displaystyle\frac{ie\hbar}{2c}\Bigg\{\frac{1}{m_{eh}}\big(\mathbf{B}\times\mathbf{r}\big)\boldsymbol{\nabla}_{R}+\frac{1}{\mu}\Big[\mathbf{B}\times\big(\mathbf{R}+\gamma\mathbf{r}\big)\Big]\boldsymbol{\nabla}_{r}\Bigg\}

\displaystyle+

\displaystyle\frac{e^{2}B^{2}}{8\mu c^{2}}\bigg(R^{2}+\beta r^{2}+2\gamma\mathbf{r}\mathbf{R}\bigg)-\frac{e^{2}}{\lambda}V(r),

where
m_{eh}=m_{e}+m_{h}
,
\mu=m_{e}m_{h}/m_{eh}
,
\gamma=(m_{h}-m_{e})/m_{eh}
, and
\beta=(m_{e}^{3}+m_{h}^{3})/m_{eh}^{3}
. The wave function can then be represented as
Gor’kov and Dzyaloshinskii 1968

\varphi^{(\textup{X})}_{\mathbf{K}}(\mathbf{r},\mathbf{R})=\mathrm{exp}\bigg[\frac{i}{\hbar}\bigg(\mathbf{K}-\frac{e}{2c}\,\mathbf{B}\times\mathbf{r}\bigg)\mathbf{R}\bigg]\\
{}\times\mathrm{exp}\bigg(\frac{i\gamma}{2\hbar}\,\mathbf{K}\mathbf{r}\bigg)\varphi^{(\textup{X})}(\mathbf{r}-\mathbf{r}_{0}).

(37)

It is convenient to choose
\mathbf{r}_{0}=-c(\mathbf{B}\times\mathbf{K})/(eB^{2})
in order to obtain the following equation for
\varphi^{(\textup{X})}(\mathbf{r})
:

\bigg[-\frac{\hbar^{2}}{2\mu}\,\Delta+\frac{ie\hbar\gamma}{2\mu c}\,(\mathbf{B}\times\mathbf{r})\boldsymbol{\nabla}+\frac{e^{2}B^{2}}{8\mu c^{2}}\,r^{2}\\
{}-\frac{e^{2}}{\lambda}V(|\mathbf{r}+\mathbf{r}_{0}|)-E\bigg]\varphi^{(\textup{X})}(\mathbf{r})=0.

(38)

Note that the wave function
\varphi^{(\textup{X})}(\mathbf{r})
does not involve
\mathbf{K}
.

First, we will neglect the Coulomb interaction
V
and find the corresponding two-particle wave functions and energy eigenvalues (free LLs). The total Hamiltonian
\hat{H}^{(\textup{X})}
will be then considered in this basis, and the corresponding matrix elements are given by Eq. (
30
). The solutions of Eq. (
38
) without the
V
term are exactly those given in Eq. (
29
), so the normalized two-particle wave functions within the zeroth order with respect to the Coulomb interaction have the form

\varphi^{(\textup{X})}_{\mathbf{K},nm}(\mathbf{r},\mathbf{R})=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi\hbar}}\mathrm{exp}\bigg[\frac{i}{\hbar}\bigg(\mathbf{K}-\frac{e}{2c}\,\mathbf{B}\times\mathbf{r}\bigg)\mathbf{R}\bigg]\\
{}\times\mathrm{exp}\bigg(\frac{i\gamma}{2\hbar}\,\mathbf{K}\mathbf{r}\bigg)\varphi^{(e)}_{nm}(\mathbf{r}-\mathbf{r}_{0}).

(39)

The corresponding energies involve the cyclotron frequency with the reduced mass,
\omega_{0}=|e|B/(\mu c)
, and read

\displaystyle E^{(\textup{X},0)}_{nm}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\hbar\omega_{0}\bigg(\frac{1}{2}+\frac{1+\gamma}{2}\,n+\frac{1-\gamma}{2}\,m\bigg)

(40)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\hbar\omega_{0}\bigg(\frac{1}{2}+\tilde{n}+\frac{|\tilde{m}|-\gamma\tilde{m}}{2}\bigg).

Here the superscript “0” indicates that the corresponding energies are obtained to zeroth order in the Coulomb interaction. Note that
\omega_{0}=\omega_{e}+\omega_{h}
, where
\omega_{h}=|e|B/(m_{h}c)
. In the heavy-hole limit
m_{h}=\infty
, one obtains
\gamma=1
,
\mu=m_{e}
,
\omega_{h}=0
and recovers the electron LLs (
20
). The angular momentum projection of the two-particle system is again
M_{z}=-\tilde{m}=n-m
.

Next, one has to compute the matrix elements of the
V
operator in Eq. (
31
) using the basis functions (
39
). The integration over
\mathbf{R}
is trivial and the matrix is diagonal with respect to
\mathbf{K}
:

V_{\mathbf{K}^{\prime},n^{\prime}m^{\prime};\mathbf{K},nm}\equiv-(e^{2}/\lambda)\big\langle\varphi^{(\textup{X})}_{\mathbf{K}^{\prime},n^{\prime}m^{\prime}}\big|V(r)\big|\varphi^{(\textup{X})}_{\mathbf{K},nm}\big\rangle\\
=-(e^{2}/\lambda)\delta(\mathbf{K}-\mathbf{K}^{\prime})\int\differential\mathbf{r}\big[\varphi^{(e)}_{n^{\prime}m^{\prime}}(\mathbf{r})\big]^{*}V(|\mathbf{r}+\mathbf{r}_{0}|)\varphi^{(e)}_{nm}(\mathbf{r})\\
\equiv\delta(\mathbf{K}-\mathbf{K}^{\prime})V_{\mathbf{K},n^{\prime}m^{\prime};nm}.

(41)

There are several technical difficulties which one encounters in evaluating the integral over
\mathbf{r}
. First, note that the function
V(|\mathbf{r}+\mathbf{r}_{0}|)
is symmetric about
\mathbf{r}=-\mathbf{r}_{0}
, whereas the wave functions are symmetric about the origin (apart from the angular part
\mathrm{e}^{-i\tilde{m}\varphi}
). Second, the radial parts of the wave functions involve special functions (Laguerre polynomials), which complicates the calculations. Moreover, the explicit form of the electrostatic potential may also be highly complex.

In the present study, we compute integrals containing monomials instead of the Laguerre polynomials and then combine the results taking into account the corresponding coefficients. In order to do this, it is necessary to inspect the structure of the wave functions. First, it is convenient to introduce non-normalized wave functions, which will be denoted by
\psi
(normalized functions are denoted by
\varphi
). Second, we rewrite the creation operators (
24
) in terms of a new dimensionless variable
\xi=z/(2\lambda)
:

\displaystyle\hat{A}^{\dagger}_{e}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}\left(\xi-\partialderivative{\xi^*}\right),\quad\hat{B}^{\dagger}_{e}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}\left(\xi^{*}-\partialderivative{\xi}\right).

(42)

The vacuum state (
22
) in terms of
\xi
reads

\displaystyle\Phi^{(e)}_{0}(\mathbf{r})=\frac{1}{\lambda\sqrt{2\pi}}\,\mathrm{e}^{-\xi\xi^{*}}.

(43)

The arbitrary wave function can be obtained via

\displaystyle\varphi^{(\textup{X})}_{\mathbf{K},nm}(\mathbf{r},\mathbf{R})=\phi^{(\textup{X})}_{\mathbf{K}}(\mathbf{r},\mathbf{R})\dfrac{\psi_{nm}(\mathbf{r})}{\sqrt{N_{nm}}},

(44)

where

\phi^{(\textup{X})}_{\mathbf{K}}(\mathbf{r},\mathbf{R})=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi\hbar}}\mathrm{exp}\bigg(\frac{i\gamma}{2\hbar}\,\mathbf{K}\mathbf{r}\bigg)\\
\times\mathrm{exp}\bigg[\frac{i}{\hbar}\bigg(\mathbf{K}-\frac{e}{2c}\,\mathbf{B}\times\mathbf{r}\bigg)\mathbf{R}\bigg],

(45)

and the relative-motion wave function has the form

\psi_{nm}(\mathbf{r})=\frac{1}{2^{(n+m)/2}}\bigg(\xi-\partialderivative{\xi^*}\bigg)^{n}\bigg(\xi^{*}-\partialderivative{\xi}\bigg)^{m}\Phi^{(e)}_{0}(\mathbf{r}).

(46)

The part of Eq. (
45
) concerning the center-of-mass motion already includes the necessary normalization factor, so
N_{nm}
should be evaluated as

\displaystyle N_{nm}=\int\!\differential{\vb{r}}\psi_{nm}^{*}(\mathbf{r})\psi_{nm}(\mathbf{r}).

(47)

Applying operators
\hat{A}^{\dagger}_{e}
and
\hat{B}^{\dagger}_{e}
to the vacuum state according to Eq. (
46
), we obtain the following structure of the wave function
\psi_{nm}
:

\displaystyle\psi_{nm}(\mathbf{r})=\sum_{a_{1},a_{2}}B^{nm}_{a_{1}a_{2}}(\xi^{*})^{a_{1}}(\xi)^{a_{2}}\Phi^{(e)}_{0}(\mathbf{r}).

(48)

The coefficients
B^{nm}_{a_{1}a_{2}}
can be found directly by means of Eq. (
46
). We have implemented the corresponding procedure within the
Wolfram Mathematica
subroutine, which is included in the Supplemental Material (for the details see Appendix
I
).

Taking into account Eq. (
48
), the normalization factor can be calculated as follows (we do not display the summation indices explicitly):

N_{nm}=\sum\sum B^{nm*}_{a_{1}^{\prime}a_{2}^{\prime}}B^{nm}_{a_{1}a_{2}}\\
\times\int\!\differential{\vb{r}}\big[\Phi^{(e)}_{0}(\mathbf{r})\big]^{*}\Phi^{(e)}_{0}(\mathbf{r})(\xi^{*})^{a_{1}+a_{2}^{\prime}}(\xi)^{a_{2}+a_{1}^{\prime}}\\
=\sum\sum B^{nm*}_{a_{1}^{\prime}a_{2}^{\prime}}B^{nm}_{a_{1}a_{2}}I^{(\textup{X},\textup{N})}_{a_{1}+a_{2}^{\prime},a_{2}+a_{1}^{\prime}}.

(49)

Here we have defined the normalization master integral (MI)
I^{(\textup{X},\textup{N})}_{p_{1},p_{2}}
involving a monomial:

\displaystyle I^{(\textup{X},\textup{N})}_{p_{1},p_{2}}=\int\!\differential{\vb{r}}\big[\Phi^{(e)}_{0}(\mathbf{r})\big]^{*}\Phi^{(e)}_{0}(\mathbf{r})(\xi^{*})^{p_{1}}(\xi)^{p_{2}}=\dfrac{1}{2\pi\lambda^{2}}\int\limits_{0}^{\infty}\differential{r}r\mathrm{e}^{-r^{2}/(2\lambda^{2})}\left(\dfrac{r}{2\lambda}\right)^{p_{1}+p_{2}}\int\limits_{0}^{2\pi}\differential{\varphi}\mathrm{e}^{{i\mkern 1.0mu}\varphi(p_{2}-p_{1})}=\dfrac{p_{1}!}{2^{p_{1}}}\,\delta_{p_{1}p_{2}}.

(50)

With the aid of the normalization factor (
49
), one can construct now the normalized wave functions

\displaystyle\varphi^{(e)}_{nm}(\mathbf{r})=\dfrac{\psi_{nm}(\mathbf{r})}{\sqrt{N_{nm}}},

(51)

which coincide with the expression (
29
), and also obtain the full two-particle wave function (
44
).

Having described how to calculate the normalization factor
N_{nm}
using the normalization MI (
50
), we can proceed in the same way and compute the matrix element of the electron-hole interaction
V_{\mathbf{K},n^{\prime}m^{\prime};nm}
, defined in Eq. (
41
):

V_{\mathbf{K},n^{\prime}m^{\prime};nm}=-\frac{e^{2}}{\lambda}\int\differential{\vb{r}}\big[\varphi^{(e)}_{n^{\prime}m^{\prime}}(\mathbf{r})\big]^{*}V(|\mathbf{r}+\mathbf{r}_{0}|)\varphi^{(e)}_{nm}(\mathbf{r})\\
=\dfrac{\sum\sum B^{nm*}_{a_{1}^{\prime}a_{2}^{\prime}}B^{nm}_{a_{1}a_{2}}I^{(\textup{X},eh)}_{a_{1}+a_{2}^{\prime},a_{2}+a_{1}^{\prime}}}{\sqrt{N_{n^{\prime}m^{\prime}}}\sqrt{N_{nm}}}.

(52)

Here we have introduced the electron-hole interaction MI
I^{(\textup{X},eh)}_{p_{1}p_{2}}
:

I^{(\textup{X},eh)}_{p_{1}p_{2}}=-\frac{e^{2}}{\lambda}\int\!\differential{\vb{r}}\big[\Phi^{(e)}_{0}(\mathbf{r})\big]^{*}\Phi^{(e)}_{0}(\mathbf{r})V(|\mathbf{r}+\mathbf{r}_{0}|)(\xi^{*})^{p_{1}}(\xi)^{p_{2}}=-\dfrac{1}{2\pi}\dfrac{1}{2^{p_{1}+p_{2}}}\frac{e^{2}}{\lambda}\left(\dfrac{r_{0}}{\lambda}\right)^{2+p_{1}+p_{2}}\\
\times\int\limits_{0}^{\infty}\differential{x}x^{p_{1}+p_{2}+1}\mathrm{exp}\bigg[{-\left(\dfrac{r_{0}}{\lambda}\right)^{2}\dfrac{x^{2}}{2}}\bigg]\int\limits_{0}^{2\pi}\differential{\varphi}\mathrm{e}^{{i\mkern 1.0mu}\varphi(p_{2}-p_{1})}V(r_{0}\sqrt{1+x^{2}+2x\cos{\varphi}}),

(53)

which in the case of the Coulomb potential
V(r)=\lambda/(\varepsilon r)
reads

\displaystyle I^{(\textup{X},eh,\textup{C})}_{p_{1}p_{2}}=-\dfrac{E_{0}}{\pi\sqrt{2\pi}}\dfrac{1}{2^{p_{1}+p_{2}}}\left(\dfrac{r_{0}}{\lambda}\right)^{1+p_{1}+p_{2}}\int\limits_{0}^{\infty}\differential{x}x^{p_{1}+p_{2}+1}\mathrm{exp}\bigg[{-\left(\dfrac{r_{0}}{\lambda}\right)^{2}\dfrac{x^{2}}{2}}\bigg]\int\limits_{0}^{2\pi}\differential{\varphi}\dfrac{\exp[{i\mkern 1.0mu}\varphi(p_{2}-p_{1})]}{\sqrt{1+x^{2}+2x\cos{\varphi}}}.

(54)

Here
E_{0}=\sqrt{\pi/2}(e^{2}/\varepsilon\lambda)
. With this expression in hands, we are now able to calculate an arbitrary matrix element (
30
).

Appendix C
Trion (T) in a magnetic field

C.1
Spectrum calculation recipe

In contrast to exciton, this three-particle system is not electrically neutral, so one can only partially separate the center-of-mass degrees of freedom (for the details see the next section). Instead of a conserved vector
\mathbf{K}
, one has in this case an integer quantum number
k
which can be set to zero due to the degeneracy of the energy levels. The matrix elements read

\displaystyle H^{(\textup{T})}_{n_{1}n_{2}n_{h}ml;n_{1}^{\prime}n_{2}^{\prime}n_{h}^{\prime}m^{\prime}l^{\prime}}

\displaystyle=E^{(\textup{T},0)}_{n_{1}+n_{2},n_{h}}\delta_{n_{1}n_{1}^{\prime}}\delta_{n_{2}n_{2}^{\prime}}\delta_{n_{h}n_{h}^{\prime}}\delta_{mm^{\prime}}\delta_{ll^{\prime}}

\displaystyle+V^{(eh)}_{n_{1}n_{2}n_{h}ml;n_{1}^{\prime}n_{2}^{\prime}n_{h}^{\prime}m^{\prime}l^{\prime}}

\displaystyle+V^{(ee)}_{n_{1}n_{2}n_{h}ml;n_{1}^{\prime}n_{2}^{\prime}n_{h}^{\prime}m^{\prime}l^{\prime}},

(55)

where each index is a non-negative integer,
E^{(\textup{T},0)}_{n_{e},n_{h}}
is given in Eq. (
95
). The matrix elements of the interparticle interaction are displayed in Eqs. (
96
) and (
108
). Note that in what follows, we will compute the whole matrix by independently varying all of the indices and taking into account the Coulomb mixing of the free LLs, i.e. incorporating the
V
terms in Eq. (
16
) with
n_{1}\neq n_{1}^{\prime}
,
n_{2}\neq n_{2}^{\prime}
, and
n_{h}\neq n_{h}^{\prime}
.

C.2
Details

A three-particle system involving two electrons with positions
\mathbf{r}_{1}
and
\mathbf{r}_{2}
and a hole with position
\mathbf{r}_{h}
is described by the following Hamiltonian:

\hat{H}^{(\textup{T})}=\hat{H}_{0}+\hat{V}_{eh}+\hat{V}_{ee},

(56)

where

\displaystyle\hat{H}_{0}

\displaystyle=\sum_{j=1,2}\frac{\hat{\boldsymbol{\pi}}_{j}^{2}}{2m_{e}}+\frac{\hat{\boldsymbol{\pi}}_{h}^{2}}{2m_{h}},

(57)

\displaystyle\hat{V}_{eh}

\displaystyle=-\sum_{j=1,2}\frac{e^{2}}{\lambda}V(|\mathbf{r}_{j}-\mathbf{r}_{h}|),

(58)

\displaystyle\hat{V}_{ee}

\displaystyle=\frac{e^{2}}{\lambda}V(|\mathbf{r}_{1}-\mathbf{r}_{2}|).

(59)

Here
\hat{\boldsymbol{\pi}}_{j}=-i\hbar\boldsymbol{\nabla}_{j}-(e/c)\boldsymbol{A}(\boldsymbol{r}_{j})
. In what follows, we will again construct first the eigenfunctions of the Hamiltonian
\hat{H}_{0}
without the interparticle interaction and then use the corresponding wave functions as a basis to subsequently diagonalize the total Hamiltonian
\hat{H}^{(\textup{T})}
.

The most straightforward approach is to employ the one-particle solutions (
26
) and simply multiply the functions depending on
\mathbf{r}_{1}
,
\mathbf{r}_{2}
, and
\mathbf{r}_{h}
, respectively. However, this basis set completely disregards the translational symmetry of the system as the functions have only a well-defined projection of the total angular momentum
M_{z}
. First, let us inspect a three-particle generalization of the vector operator (
32
):

\hat{\mathbf{K}}=\sum_{j=1,2}\hat{\boldsymbol{\pi}}_{j}+\hat{\boldsymbol{\pi}}_{h}+\frac{e}{c}\sum_{j=1,2}\mathbf{B}\times\mathbf{r}_{j}-\frac{e}{c}\,\mathbf{B}\times\mathbf{r}_{h}.

(60)

This operator (so-called operator of the
magnetic translations

Lerner and Lozovik 1980
;
Dzyubenko and Sivachenko 2000
;
Dzyubenko 2000
;
Dzyubenko 2001
) commutes with both
\hat{H}_{0}
and
\hat{H}^{(\textup{T})}
, but the crucial difference between an exciton and trion is that in the latter case the
x
and
y
components of
\hat{\mathbf{K}}
do not commute with each other. It turns out that
[\hat{K}_{x},\,\hat{K}_{y}]=i|e|\hbar B/c
and generally this commutator is proportional to the total electric charge of the system (an exciton is neutral). This means that one can take advantage only of one integral of motion (for instance, either
K_{x}
or
K_{y}
). In fact, it is convenient to consider the operator
\hat{\mathbf{K}}^{2}

Dzyubenko 2000
. It obviously commutes with the Hamiltonian. Let us normalize the vector operator according to

\hat{\mathbf{k}}=\sqrt{\frac{c}{|e|\hbar B}}\,\hat{\mathbf{K}},

(61)

so that
[\hat{k}_{x},\,\hat{k}_{y}]=i
. Introducing then operator
\hat{\varkappa}=(i\hat{k}_{x}-\hat{k}_{y})/\sqrt{2}
, one obtains

\displaystyle[\hat{\varkappa},\,\hat{\varkappa}^{\dagger}]=1,

(62)

\displaystyle\hat{\mathbf{K}}^{2}=2\hat{\varkappa}^{\dagger}\hat{\varkappa}+1.

(63)

Therefore, the eigenvalues of
\hat{\mathbf{K}}^{2}
are
2k+1
, where
k=0
,
1
, … The solutions of the Schrödinger equation can be characterized by the quantum number
k
, which allows one to partially separate the center-of-mass motion. The question now is how to use this symmetry in practical calculations, i.e., how to construct the eigenfunctions of
\hat{\mathbf{K}}^{2}
.

Let us briefly outline the procedure proposed in Ref.
Dzyubenko 2000
. First, we perform the following coordinate transformation:

\displaystyle\mathbf{R}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}\,\big(\mathbf{r}_{1}+\mathbf{r}_{2}\big),\quad\mathbf{r}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}\,\big(\mathbf{r}_{1}-\mathbf{r}_{2}\big).

(64)

Introducing then the three-particle vacuum state

\Phi^{(\textup{T})}_{0}(\mathbf{r},\mathbf{R},\mathbf{r}_{h})=\Phi^{(e)}_{0}(\mathbf{r})\Phi^{(e)}_{0}(\mathbf{R})\Phi^{(e)}_{0}(\mathbf{r}_{h}),

(65)

one can generate
naive
solutions by the corresponding creation operators of the form (
24
) and (
25
) and obtain:

\varphi^{(\textup{T})}_{n_{1}m_{1}n_{2}m_{2}n_{h}m_{h}}(\mathbf{r},\mathbf{R},\mathbf{r}_{h})\\
=\varphi^{(e)}_{n_{1}m_{1}}(\mathbf{r})\varphi^{(e)}_{n_{2}m_{2}}(\mathbf{R})\varphi^{(h)}_{n_{h}m_{h}}(\mathbf{r}_{h}),

(66)

where
\varphi^{(h)}_{nm}(\mathbf{r})=[\varphi^{(e)}_{nm}(\mathbf{r})]^{*}
. The
z
projection of the angular momentum equals
M_{z}=(n_{1}-m_{1})+(n_{2}-m_{2})-(n_{h}-m_{h})
. Moreover, the reflection
\mathbf{r}\to-\mathbf{r}
, which is equivalent to exchanging the positions of the electrons, yields the factor
(-1)^{n_{1}-m_{1}}
, so the spin wave function of the electron subsystem (not displayed here explicitly) is even (odd) if
n_{1}-m_{1}
is odd (even). In other words, the total electron spin is
S_{e}=0
(spin-singlet state) for even
n_{1}-m_{1}
and
S_{e}=1
(spin-triplet state) for odd
n_{1}-m_{1}
. As was shown in Ref.
Dzyubenko 2000
, the functions (
66
) can be unitary transformed, so that they become eigenfunctions of
\hat{\mathbf{K}}^{2}
, i.e., they gain additional quantum number
k
preserving
M_{z}
and
S_{e}
.

To perform this unitary transformation, let us again introduce dimensionless variables
\xi=z/(2\lambda)
,
\xi_{R}=z_{R}/(2\lambda)
, and
\xi_{h}=z_{h}/(2\lambda)
and consider the following creation operators:

\displaystyle\hat{A}^{\dagger}_{e}(\mathbf{r})

\displaystyle=\dfrac{\left(\xi-\partialderivative{\xi^*}\right)}{\sqrt{2}},\ \ \hat{B}^{\dagger}_{e}(\mathbf{r})=\dfrac{\left(\xi^{*}-\partialderivative{\xi}\right)}{\sqrt{2}},

(67)

\displaystyle\hat{A}^{\dagger}_{e}(\mathbf{R})

\displaystyle=\dfrac{\left(\xi_{R}-\partialderivative{\xi_R^*}\right)}{\sqrt{2}},\ \ \hat{B}^{\dagger}_{e}(\mathbf{R})=\dfrac{\left(\xi_{R}^{*}-\partialderivative{\xi_R}\right)}{\sqrt{2}},

(68)

\displaystyle\hat{A}^{\dagger}_{h}(\mathbf{r}_{h})

\displaystyle=\dfrac{\left(\xi_{h}^{*}-\partialderivative{\xi_h}\right)}{\sqrt{2}},\ \ \hat{B}^{\dagger}_{h}(\mathbf{r}_{h})=\dfrac{\left(\xi_{h}-\partialderivative{\xi_h^*}\right)}{\sqrt{2}}.

(69)

Let us note that

\hat{\varkappa}^{\dagger}=\sqrt{2}\hat{B}^{\dagger}_{e}(\mathbf{R})-\hat{B}_{h}(\mathbf{r}_{h}).

(70)

This relation can be interpreted as a Bogolyubov transformation of
B^{\dagger}_{e}(\mathbf{R})
:

\hat{\widetilde{B}}^{\dagger}_{e}(\mathbf{R})=\hat{\varkappa}^{\dagger}=\sqrt{2}\hat{B}^{\dagger}_{e}(\mathbf{R})-\hat{B}_{h}(\mathbf{r}_{h})=\hat{S}\hat{B}^{\dagger}_{e}(\mathbf{R})\hat{S}^{\dagger},

(71)

The corresponding unitary operator is

\displaystyle\hat{S}=\exp{- \Theta\left[ \hat{B}_e(\vb{R})\hat{B}_h(\vb{r}_h)-\hat{B}^\dag_h(\vb{r}_h)\hat{B}^\dag_e(\vb{R})\right] },

(72)

where
\sinh\Theta=1
and
\cosh\Theta=\sqrt{2}
. Another operator that does not commute with
\hat{S}
and should be transformed is
\hat{B}^{\dagger}_{h}(\mathbf{r}_{h})
:

\hat{\widetilde{B}}^{\dagger}_{h}(\mathbf{r}_{h})=\sqrt{2}\hat{B}^{\dagger}_{h}(\mathbf{r}_{h})-\hat{B}_{e}(\mathbf{R}).

(73)

In addition to the operators, the wave functions themselves also have to be transformed. Thus, up to the normalization factor the new basis is

\displaystyle\!\!\!\!\hat{A}^{{\dagger}}_{e}(\mathbf{r})^{n_{1}}\!\hat{A}^{\dagger}_{e}(\mathbf{R})^{n_{2}}\!\hat{A}^{\dagger}_{h}(\mathbf{r}_{h})^{n_{h}}\!\hat{\widetilde{B}}^{\dagger}_{e}(\mathbf{R})^{k}\!\hat{B}^{\dagger}_{e}(\mathbf{r})^{m}\!\hat{\widetilde{B}}_{h}^{\dagger}(\mathbf{r}_{h})^{l}\Psi^{(\textup{T})}_{0},\!\!\!

(74)

where we used a new vacuum state
\Psi^{(\textup{T})}_{0}=\hat{S}\Phi^{(\textup{T})}_{0}
. In this basis, three well-defined
quantum numbers are
k
,
M_{z}
, and
S_{e}
, i.e. the Hamiltonian is block-diagonal in terms of these quantum numbers. The projection of the total angular momentum reads

\displaystyle M_{z}=\underbrace{n_{1}-m}_{\textup{from $\mathbf{r}$}}+\underbrace{n_{2}-k}_{\textup{from $\mathbf{R}$}}-\underbrace{(n_{h}-l)}_{\textup{from $\mathbf{r}_{h}$}}.

(75)

The spin quantum number is
S_{e}=0
(
S_{e}=1
) if
n_{1}-m
is even (odd). Moreover, hereinafter we will assume
k=0
since the Hamiltonian
H^{(\textup{T})}
commutes with
\widetilde{B}^{\dagger}_{e}(\mathbf{R})
. The matrix elements do not depend on
k
, so the energy also turns out to be
k
-independent.

In order to obtain an explicit form of the new vacuum state, it is necessary to rewrite the expression for the operator
\hat{S}
. Using the Baker–Campbell–Hausdorff formula (see, e.g., Ref.
Kirzhnits 1967
) for the operator equality
\mathrm{exp}[\Theta(\hat{a}+\hat{b})]=\mathrm{exp}(\Theta\hat{d})
, one can present the operator
\hat{S}
in the following form:

\hat{S}=\mathrm{exp}\left[\tanh\Theta\hat{B}^{\dagger}_{h}(\mathbf{r}_{h})\hat{B}^{\dagger}_{e}(\mathbf{R})\right]\\
\times\exp{-\ln{(\cosh\Theta)}\!\left[ \hat{B}^\dag_e(\vb{R})\hat{B}_e(\vb{R})+\hat{B}^\dag_h(\vb{r}_h)\hat{B}_h(\vb{r}_h)+1\right]}\\
\times\mathrm{exp}\left[-\tanh\Theta\hat{B}_{e}(\mathbf{R})\hat{B}_{h}(\mathbf{r}_{h})\right].

(76)

The new vacuum state then reads

\displaystyle\Psi^{(\textup{T})}_{0}=\hat{S}\Phi^{(\textup{T})}_{0}=\dfrac{1}{\cosh\Theta}\mathrm{exp}\left[\tanh\Theta\hat{B}^{\dagger}_{h}(\mathbf{r}_{h})\hat{B}^{\dagger}_{e}(\mathbf{R})\right]\Phi^{(\textup{T})}_{0}.

(77)

The state (
65
) is given by

\displaystyle\Phi^{(\textup{T})}_{0}(\mathbf{r},\mathbf{R},\mathbf{r}_{h})=\dfrac{\mathrm{exp}(-\xi\xi^{*}-\xi_{R}\xi^{*}_{R}-\xi_{h}\xi^{*}_{h})}{\left(2\pi\right)^{3/2}\lambda^{3}}.

(78)

Taking into account the explicit form of the operators
B^{\dagger}_{h}(\mathbf{r}_{h})
and
B^{\dagger}_{e}(\mathbf{R})
, one obtains

\Psi^{(\textup{T})}_{0}=\exp[\dfrac{1}{2\sqrt{2}}\left(\xi_{h}-\partialderivative{\xi^*_h}\right)\left(\xi^{*}_{R}-\partialderivative{\xi_R}\right)]\\
\quad\quad\times\dfrac{\exp(-\xi\xi^*-\xi_R\xi^*_R-\xi_h\xi^*_h)}{\sqrt{2}\left(2\pi\right)^{3/2}\lambda^{3}}.

(79)

Let us label the function
\sqrt{2}\Psi^{(\textup{T})}_{0}(\mathbf{r},\mathbf{R},\mathbf{r}_{h})
as a new normalized vacuum state
\tilde{\Phi}^{(\textup{T})}_{0}(\mathbf{r},\mathbf{R},\mathbf{r}_{h})
, which reads

\displaystyle\tilde{\Phi}^{(\textup{T})}_{0}\!(\mathbf{r},\!\mathbf{R},\mathbf{r}_{h})\!=\!\dfrac{\exp{-\xi\xi^*\!-\!\xi_R\xi^*_R\!-\!\xi_h\xi^*_h\!+\!\sqrt{2}\xi_h\xi^*_R}}{\sqrt{2}\left(2\pi\right)^{3/2}\lambda^{3}}.\!\!

(80)

Now one can generate an arbitrary wave function by means of the creation operators in Eq. (
74
). Regarding the normalization of the wave functions, we prefer to keep only factor
1/[\left(2\pi\right)^{3/2}\lambda^{3}]
explicitly in contrast to other normalization factors which will be taken into account at the very end. Thus, we first employ the operator
\hat{B}^{\dagger}_{e}(\mathbf{r})^{m}\hat{\widetilde{B}}_{h}^{\dagger}(\mathbf{r}_{h})^{l}
. Note that

\displaystyle\hat{\widetilde{B}}^{\dagger}_{h}(\mathbf{r}_{h})^{l}\tilde{\Phi}^{(\textup{T})}_{0}\!(\mathbf{r},\!\mathbf{R},\mathbf{r}_{h})\!=\dfrac{\left(\xi_{h}\right)^{l}\tilde{\Phi}^{(\textup{T})}_{0}\!(\mathbf{r},\!\mathbf{R},\mathbf{r}_{h})\!}{\sqrt{2}}.

(81)

Then, we obtain

\hat{B}^{\dagger}_{e}(\mathbf{r})^{m}\hat{\widetilde{B}}^{\dagger}_{h}(\mathbf{r}_{h})^{l}\tilde{\Phi}^{(\textup{T})}_{0}\!(\mathbf{r},\!\mathbf{R},\mathbf{r}_{h})\\
=2^{(m-1)/2}\left(\xi^{*}\right)^{m}\left(\xi_{h}\right)^{l}\tilde{\Phi}^{(\textup{T})}_{0}\!(\mathbf{r},\!\mathbf{R},\mathbf{r}_{h}).

(82)

It is convenient to introduce the following function:

\displaystyle\psi_{ml}(\mathbf{r},\mathbf{R},\mathbf{r}_{h})=\left(\xi^{*}\right)^{m}\left(\xi_{h}\right)^{l}\tilde{\Phi}^{(\textup{T})}_{0}\!(\mathbf{r},\!\mathbf{R},\mathbf{r}_{h}).\!

(83)

According to Eq. (
74
), the general wave function up to the normalization factor reads

\displaystyle\psi_{n_{1}n_{2}n_{h}ml}(\mathbf{r},\mathbf{R},\mathbf{r}_{h})

\displaystyle=\left[\dfrac{1}{\sqrt{2}}\left(\xi-\partialderivative{\xi^*}\right)\right]^{n_{1}}\left[\dfrac{1}{\sqrt{2}}\left(\xi_{R}-\partialderivative{\xi^*_R}\right)\right]^{n_{2}}\left[\dfrac{1}{\sqrt{2}}\left(\xi^{*}_{h}-\partialderivative{\xi_h}\right)\right]^{n_{h}}\psi_{ml}(\mathbf{r},\mathbf{R},\mathbf{r}_{h}).

(84)

Let us now calculate the normalization factor:

\displaystyle N_{n_{1}n_{2}n_{h}ml}=\langle\psi_{n_{1}n_{2}n_{h}ml}\text{\textbar}\psi_{n_{1}n_{2}n_{h}ml}\rangle.

(85)

This expression involves three 2D integrals (over
\mathbf{r}
,
\mathbf{R}
, and
\mathbf{r}_{h})
. Any wave function can be represented as the following linear combination involving products of
\tilde{\Phi}^{(\textup{T})}_{0}(\mathbf{r},\mathbf{R},\mathbf{r}_{h})
and monomials:

\displaystyle\psi_{n_{1}n_{2}n_{h}ml}(\mathbf{r},\mathbf{R},\mathbf{r}_{h})=\sum C^{n_{1}n_{2}n_{h}ml}_{a_{1}a_{2}b_{1}b_{2}c_{1}c_{2}}(\xi^{*})^{a_{1}}(\xi)^{a_{2}}

\displaystyle\quad\quad\times(\xi_{h})^{b_{1}}(\xi_{h}^{*})^{b_{2}}(\xi_{R})^{c_{1}}(\xi_{R}^{*})^{c_{2}}\tilde{\Phi}^{(\textup{T})}_{0}\!(\mathbf{r},\!\mathbf{R},\mathbf{r}_{h}).

(86)

Therefore,

\displaystyle N_{n_{1}n_{2}n_{h}ml}

\displaystyle=\sum\sum C^{n_{1}n_{2}n_{h}ml*}_{a^{\prime}_{1}a^{\prime}_{2}b^{\prime}_{1}b^{\prime}_{2}c^{\prime}_{1}c^{\prime}_{2}}C^{n_{1}n_{2}n_{h}ml}_{a_{1}a_{2}b_{1}b_{2}c_{1}c_{2}}\int\!\differential{\vb{r}}\int\!\differential{\vb{R}}\int\!\differential{\vb{r}_h}\big|\tilde{\Phi}^{(\textup{T})}_{0}\!(\mathbf{r},\!\mathbf{R},\mathbf{r}_{h})\big|^{2}

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad\times(\xi^{*})^{a_{1}+a_{2}^{\prime}}(\xi)^{a_{2}+a_{1}^{\prime}}(\xi_{h})^{b_{1}+b_{2}^{\prime}}(\xi_{h}^{*})^{b_{2}+b_{1}^{\prime}}(\xi_{R})^{c_{1}+c_{2}^{\prime}}(\xi_{R}^{*})^{c_{2}+c_{1}^{\prime}}

\displaystyle=\sum\sum C^{n_{1}n_{2}n_{h}ml*}_{a^{\prime}_{1}a^{\prime}_{2}b^{\prime}_{1}b^{\prime}_{2}c^{\prime}_{1}c^{\prime}_{2}}C^{n_{1}n_{2}n_{h}ml}_{a_{1}a_{2}b_{1}b_{2}c_{1}c_{2}}I^{(\textup{T},\textup{N})}_{a_{1}+a_{2}^{\prime},a_{2}+a_{1}^{\prime},b_{1}+b_{2}^{\prime},b_{2}+b_{1}^{\prime},c_{1}+c_{2}^{\prime},c_{2}+c_{1}^{\prime}},

(87)

where we define the master integral
I^{(\textup{T},\textup{N})}_{p_{1}p_{2}q_{1}q_{2}r_{1}r_{2}}
as

\displaystyle I^{(\textup{T},\textup{N})}_{p_{1}p_{2}q_{1}q_{2}r_{1}r_{2}}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{(2\pi)^{3}\lambda^{6}}\int\!\differential{\vb{r}}\int\!\differential{\vb{R}}\int\!\differential{\vb{r}_h}(\xi^{*})^{p_{1}}(\xi)^{p_{2}}(\xi_{h})^{q_{1}}(\xi_{h}^{*})^{q_{2}}(\xi_{R})^{r_{1}}(\xi_{R}^{*})^{r_{2}}

\displaystyle\times\mathrm{exp}\Big[-2(\xi\xi^{*}+\xi_{R}\xi^{*}_{R}+\xi_{h}\xi^{*}_{h})+\sqrt{2}(\xi_{h}\xi^{*}_{R}+\xi_{h}^{*}\xi_{R})\Big].

(88)

The expansion coefficients
C^{n_{1}n_{2}n_{h}ml}_{a_{1}a_{2}b_{1}b_{2}c_{1}c_{2}}
for an arbitrary wave function in terms of monomials
(\xi^{*})^{a_{1}}(\xi)^{a_{2}}(\xi_{h})^{b_{1}}(\xi_{h}^{*})^{b_{2}}(\xi_{R})^{c_{1}}(\xi_{R}^{*})^{c_{2}}
can be obtained automatically. We attach the corresponding subroutine in
Wolfram Mathematica
format. Thus, we should evaluate only the master integral (
88
). First, taking into account
\xi=r\mathrm{e}^{{i\mkern 1.0mu}\varphi}/(2\lambda)
, we integrate out the
\mathbf{r}
-dependent part:

\displaystyle\int\differential{\vb{r}}\left(\xi^{*}\right)^{p_{1}}\left(\xi\right)^{p_{2}}\exp(-2\xi\xi^*)=\int\limits^{\infty}_{0}r\differential{r}\int\limits^{2\pi}_{0}\differential{\varphi}\exp[{i\mkern 1.0mu}\varphi(p_{2}-p_{1})]\left(\dfrac{r}{2\lambda}\right)^{p_{1}+p_{2}}\exp(-\dfrac{r^2}{2\lambda^2})=2\pi\delta_{p_{1}p_{2}}\dfrac{p_{1}!\lambda^{2}}{2^{p_{1}}}.

Equation (
88
) takes the form

I^{(\textup{T},\textup{N})}_{p_{1}p_{2}q_{1}q_{2}r_{1}r_{2}}=\dfrac{2\pi\delta_{p_{1}p_{2}}p_{1}!}{(2\pi)^{3}2^{p_{1}}\lambda^{4}}\int\limits^{\infty}_{0}R\differential{R}\int\limits^{\infty}_{0}r_{h}\differential{r_h}\left(\dfrac{r_{h}}{2\lambda}\right)^{q_{1}+q_{2}}\left(\dfrac{R}{2\lambda}\right)^{r_{1}+r_{2}}\int\limits_{0}^{2\pi}\differential{\varphi_h}\int\limits_{0}^{2\pi}\differential{\varphi_R}\exp[{i\mkern 1.0mu}\varphi_{h}(q_{1}-q_{2})]\exp[{i\mkern 1.0mu}\varphi_{R}(r_{1}-r_{2})]\\
\times\exp[-\dfrac{R^{2}}{2\lambda^{2}}-\dfrac{r_{h}^{2}}{2\lambda^{2}}+\dfrac{r_{h}R\cos{(\varphi_R-\varphi_h)}}{\sqrt{2}\lambda^{2}}].

(89)

Using the Jacobi-Anger relation
\mathrm{exp}({i\mkern 1.0mu}z\cos{\varphi})=\!\!\!\!\sum\limits_{n=-\infty}^{\infty}\!\!\!\!{i\mkern 1.0mu}^{n}J_{n}(z)\mathrm{exp}({i\mkern 1.0mu}n\varphi)
, we can integrate over the angles as follows:

\displaystyle\int\limits_{0}^{2\pi}\differential{\varphi_R}\int\limits_{0}^{2\pi}\differential{\varphi_h}\exp[{i\mkern 1.0mu}\varphi_{h}(q_{1}-q_{2})]\exp[{i\mkern 1.0mu}\varphi_{R}(r_{1}-r_{2})]\exp[{i\mkern 1.0mu}n(\varphi_{R}-\varphi_{h})]=(2\pi)^{2}\delta_{0,q_{1}-q_{2}-n}\delta_{0,r_{1}-r_{2}+n}.

(90)

Let us assume that
q_{1}\geq q_{2}
. One obtains

\displaystyle I^{(\textup{T},\textup{N})}_{p_{1}p_{2}q_{1}q_{2}r_{1}r_{2}}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\lambda^{4}}\,\delta_{p_{1}p_{2}}\delta_{q_{1}-q_{2},r_{2}-r_{1}}\,\dfrac{p_{1}!}{2^{p_{1}}}\,{i\mkern 1.0mu}^{q_{1}-q_{2}}\int\limits^{\infty}_{0}\differential{R}R\left(\dfrac{R}{2\lambda}\right)^{r_{1}+r_{2}}\exp(-\dfrac{R^2}{2\lambda^2})

\displaystyle\times\int\limits^{\infty}_{0}\differential{r_h}r_{h}\left(\dfrac{r_{h}}{2\lambda}\right)^{q_{1}+q_{2}}\exp(-\dfrac{r_h}{2\lambda^2})J_{q_{1}-q_{2}}\left(-{i\mkern 1.0mu}\dfrac{r_{h}R}{\lambda^{2}\sqrt{2}}\right)=\dfrac{\delta_{p_{1}p_{2}}\delta_{q_{1}-q_{2},r_{2}-r_{1}}p_{1}!}{2^{p_{1}+q_{1}+r_{1}+\frac{q_{1}-q_{2}}{2}-2}}\int\limits^{\infty}_{0}\differential{\left(\dfrac{R}{\sqrt{2}\lambda}\right)}\left(\dfrac{R}{\sqrt{2}\lambda}\right)

\displaystyle\times\int\limits^{\infty}_{0}\differential{\left(\dfrac{r_h}{\sqrt{2}\lambda}\right)}\left(\dfrac{r_{h}}{\sqrt{2}\lambda}\right)\sum\limits^{\infty}_{m=0}\dfrac{1}{2^{m}m!}\dfrac{1}{(q_{1}-q_{2}+m)!}\exp(-\dfrac{r^2_h}{2\lambda^2})\exp(-\dfrac{R^2}{2\lambda^2})\left(\dfrac{r_{h}}{\sqrt{2}\lambda}\right)^{2q_{1}+2m}

\displaystyle\times\left(\dfrac{R}{\sqrt{2}\lambda}\right)^{2r_{1}+2m+2q_{1}-2q_{2}}=\dfrac{\delta_{p_{1}p_{2}}\delta_{q_{1}-q_{2},r_{2}-r_{1}}p_{1}!}{2^{p_{1}+q_{1}+r_{1}+\frac{q_{1}-q_{2}}{2}}}\int\limits^{\infty}_{0}\differential{x}\int\limits^{\infty}_{0}\differential{y}\sum\limits^{\infty}_{m=0}\dfrac{y^{q_{1}+m}\exp(-y)x^{m+r_{1}+q_{1}-q_{2}}\exp(-x)}{2^{m}m!\left(q_{1}-q_{2}+m\right)!}=

\displaystyle=\dfrac{\delta_{p_{1}p_{2}}\delta_{q_{1}-q_{2},r_{2}-r_{1}}p_{1}!}{2^{p_{1}+q_{1}+r_{1}+\frac{q_{1}-q_{2}}{2}}}\sum\limits_{m=0}^{\infty}\dfrac{\left(q_{1}+m\right)!(r_{1}+m+q_{1}-q_{2})!}{m!2^{m}\left(q_{1}-q_{2}+m\right)!}=\dfrac{\delta_{p_{1}p_{2}}\delta_{q_{1}-q_{2},r_{2}-r_{1}}p_{1}!q_{1}!r_{2}!}{(q_{1}-q_{2})!2^{p_{1}+q_{1}+r_{1}+\frac{q_{1}-q_{2}}{2}}}

\displaystyle\times{}_{2}F_{1}\left(q_{1}+1,r_{2}+1,q_{1}-q_{2}+1;\dfrac{1}{2}\right).

(91)

If
q_{1}<q_{2}
, then one can perform complex conjugation of the initial expression for
I^{(\textup{T},\textup{N})}_{p_{1}p_{2}q_{1}q_{2}r_{1}r_{2}}
and calculate it via the same steps as described above. The result can also be obtained by simply substituting
q_{1}\leftrightarrow q_{2}
and
r_{1}\leftrightarrow r_{2}
. Then, for universality, we introduce
q_{>}=\textup{max}(q_{1},q_{2})
,
r_{>}=\textup{max}(r_{1},r_{2})
, and
r_{<}=\textup{min}(r_{1},r_{2})
. In terms of these quantities, the master integral reads

\displaystyle I^{(\textup{T},\textup{N})}_{p_{1}p_{2}q_{1}q_{2}r_{1}r_{2}}=\!\dfrac{\delta_{p_{1}p_{2}}\delta_{q_{1}-q_{2},r_{2}-r_{1}}p_{1}!q_{>}!r_{>}!}{|q_{1}-q_{2}|!2^{p_{1}+q_{>}+r_{<}+\frac{|q_{1}-q_{2}|}{2}}}\!\ _{2}F_{1}\!\!\left(\!q_{>}\!+\!1,r_{>}\!+\!1,|q_{1}\!-\!q_{2}|\!+1;\!\dfrac{1}{2}\!\right).

(92)

Having obtained the expression for the normalization factor

, we introduce the normalized wave functions as

\displaystyle\varphi^{(\textup{T})}_{n_{1}n_{2}n_{h}ml}(\mathbf{r},\mathbf{R},\mathbf{r}_{h})=\dfrac{\psi_{n_{1}n_{2}n_{h}ml}(\mathbf{r},\mathbf{R},\mathbf{r}_{h})}{\sqrt{N_{n_{1}n_{2}n_{h}ml}}}.

With these wave functions, we can calculate the matrix elements of interest. First, it immediately follows that the matrix elements of the operator

read

\langle\varphi^{(\textup{T})}_{n_{1}n_{2}n_{h}ml}\text{\textbar}\hat{H}_{0}\text{\textbar}\varphi^{(\textup{T})}_{n_{1}^{\prime}n_{2}^{\prime}n_{h}^{\prime}m^{\prime}l^{\prime}}\rangle\\
=\delta_{n_{1}n_{1}^{\prime}}\delta_{n_{2}n_{2}^{\prime}}\delta_{n_{h}n_{h}^{\prime}}\delta_{mm^{\prime}}\delta_{ll^{\prime}}E^{(\textup{T},0)}_{n_{1}+n_{2},n_{h}},

(94)

where

E^{(\textup{T},0)}_{n_{e}n_{h}}=\hbar\omega_{e}\bigg(1+n_{e}\bigg)+\hbar\omega_{h}\bigg(\frac{1}{2}+n_{h}\bigg).

(95)

Next, one has to compute the matrix elements involving the interparticle-interaction terms. Let us start with the contribution
\hat{V}_{eh}
. Taking into account Eq. (

), we obtain

\displaystyle V^{(eh)}_{n_{1}n_{2}n_{h}ml;n_{1}^{\prime}n_{2}^{\prime}n_{h}^{\prime}m^{\prime}l^{\prime}}\!\!=\!\!\langle\varphi^{(\textup{T})}_{n_{1}n_{2}n_{h}ml}\text{\textbar}\hat{V}_{eh}\text{\textbar}\varphi^{(\textup{T})}_{n_{1}^{\prime}n_{2}^{\prime}n_{h}^{\prime}m^{\prime}l^{\prime}}\rangle\!=\!\!\dfrac{\sum\sum C^{n_{1}n_{2}n_{h}ml*}_{a^{\prime}_{1}a^{\prime}_{2}b^{\prime}_{1}b^{\prime}_{2}c^{\prime}_{1}c^{\prime}_{2}}C^{n_{1}n_{2}n_{h}ml}_{a_{1}a_{2}b_{1}b_{2}c_{1}c_{2}}I^{(\textup{T},eh)}_{a_{1}+a_{2}^{\prime},a_{2}+a_{1}^{\prime},b_{1}+b_{2}^{\prime},b_{2}+b_{1}^{\prime},c_{1}+c_{2}^{\prime},c_{2}+c_{1}^{\prime}}}{\sqrt{N_{n_{1}n_{2}n_{h}ml}}\sqrt{N_{n_{1}^{\prime}n_{2}^{\prime}n_{h}^{\prime}m^{\prime}l^{\prime}}}},

(96)

where

I^{(\textup{T},eh)}_{p_{1}p_{2}q_{1}q_{2}r_{1}r_{2}}=-\dfrac{e^{2}}{\lambda}\!\int\!\!\differential{\vb{r}}\!\!\int\!\!\differential{\vb{R}}\!\!\int\!\!\differential{\vb{r}}_{h}(\xi^{*})^{p_{1}}(\xi)^{p_{2}}(\xi_{h})^{q_{1}}(\xi_{h}^{*})^{q_{2}}(\xi_{R})^{r_{1}}(\xi_{R}^{*})^{r_{2}}\\
\times\dfrac{\exp{-2(\xi\xi^*+\xi_R\xi^*_R+\xi_h\xi^*_h)+\sqrt{2}(\xi_h\xi^*_R+\xi_h^*\xi_R)}}{\left(2\pi\right)^{3}\lambda^{6}}\left[V(|\mathbf{r}_{h}-\mathbf{r}_{1}|)+V(|\mathbf{r}_{h}-\mathbf{r}_{2}|)\right].

(97)

It is convenient to introduce the variable
\widetilde{\mathbf{r}}=\sqrt{2}\mathbf{r}_{h}-\mathbf{R}
. We perform further calculations for an arbitrary spherically symmetric potential, while the Coulomb potential will be considered only at the very end of our calculations. In terms of
\widetilde{\mathbf{r}}

Appendix D
Exciton and a free electron
(\textup{X},e)

When solving the three-particle problem discussed in Appendix
C
, one obtains discrete energy levels corresponding to bound trion states and a continuous spectrum. The lower boundary of the latter indicates the energy at which the trion disintegrates and turns into a combination of an exciton and a free electron. Since these two constituents do not interact with each other, the free electron contributes to the total energy according to Eq. (
20
), while the exciton contribution can be extracted from the matrix problem (
30
). Let the free-electron LL be characterized by quantum number
n_{0}
. The Hamiltonian is diagonal with respect to
n_{0}
because the Coulomb interaction relates solely to the exciton part. The matrix has the following form:

+V_{\mathbf{K},n^{\prime}-n_{0},m^{\prime};n-n_{0},m}.

(116)

Here the second term is present only for
n_{0}\leqslant\mathrm{min}\,(n,\,n^{\prime})
. To obtain the lower energy level of the continuous spectrum, one also has to minimize the resulting energy by varying
|\mathbf{K}|
. If the Coulomb mixing among different LLs of the three-particle problem is neglected, then for given
n_{e}\equiv n_{1}+n_{2}
and
n_{h}
in Eq. (
55
) one should choose
n=n^{\prime}=n_{e}
and
m=m^{\prime}=n_{h}
in Eq. (
116
). In order to fully take into account the Coulomb interaction, it is necessary to vary
n
,
n^{\prime}
,
m
,
m^{\prime}
in Eq. (
116
) from zero to sufficiently large numbers, so the energy eigenvalues converge.

Appendix H
Convergence of energy levels and extrapolation procedure

Our predictions should converge with respect to the number of the LLs incorporated within the model Hilbert space. It turns out that even within the setup that takes into account all levels up to the fifth ones (
n_{e}=0,\dots,5
and
n_{h}=0,\dots,5
), we cannot conclude that the convergence has been achieved. In order to overcome this problem, we utilize an extrapolation procedure described below. In Fig.
8
, we demonstrate the energy of one of the lowest trion levels and the lowest exciton level as functions of the number of LLs included within the model Hilbert space in the case of GaAs at
B=10~\textup{T}
. We depict first the energies calculated in the zero approximation (
n^{\textup{max}}_{e}=n^{\textup{max}}_{h}=0

Dzyubenko and Sivachenko 2000

n^{\textup{max}}_{e}=n^{\textup{max}}_{h}=5
, while for the exciton problem we took into acoount the states up to
n^{\textup{max}}_{e}=n^{\textup{max}}_{h}=9
. One observes that the convergence is very slow and, based on the existing number of points, it is difficult to precisely determine the values of the true trion and exciton levels. However, using these points, we performed an extrapolation procedure allowing us to obtain more accurate predictions. In Fig.
8

We note that although extrapolating the data can never yield absolutely accurate values, it is not possible to extract the energy levels without extrapolation since the convergence is unlikely to be achieved by a brute-force approach, i.e, by simply increasing the dimension of the model Hilbert space via including additional LLs, due to the slow convergence rate and computational limitations.
