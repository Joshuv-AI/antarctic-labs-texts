2.
A condition for
W(T)
to be in an ellipse

Let
\delta\in[0,1)
. We have introduced the open and closed elliptical sets
G_{\delta}
and
K_{\delta}
defined in equations (
1.28
) and (
1.1
).
Their common boundary is

\Gamma_{\delta}=K_{\delta}\setminus G_{\delta}=\{x+iy\in\mathbb{C}\,:\,\frac{x^{2}}{(1+\delta)^{2}}+\frac{y^{2}}{(1-\delta)^{2}}=1\}.

We shall make extensive use of the Zhukovskii map, which relates an annulus to the elliptical region
G_{\delta}
. It is widely used in engineering applications, especially in aerodynamics, in the design of aerofoils
[
13
, page 677]
.

Lemma 2.1
.

Let
\delta\in[0,1)
. If
f:\mathbb{C}\cup\{\infty\}\to\mathbb{C}\cup\{\infty\}
is defined by

f(z)=1/z+\delta z,

then

(i)

f
maps
\mathbb{D}
in a 1-1 manner onto
(\mathbb{C}\cup\{\infty\})\setminus K_{\delta}
,

(ii)

for
\delta\in(0,1)
,
f
maps
A_{\delta}=\{z\in\mathbb{C}\,:\,1<|z|<1/\delta\}
in a 2 -1 manner onto
G_{\delta}
,
except for the points
\pm 2\sqrt{\delta}\in G_{\delta}
, whose pre-images in
A_{\delta}
are the singleton sets
\{\pm 1/\sqrt{\delta}\}
.

(iii)

for
\delta\in(0,1)
,
f
maps
\{z\in\mathbb{C}\,:\,|z|>1/\delta\}
in a 1 -1 manner onto
(\mathbb{C}\cup\{\infty\})\setminus K_{\delta}
, and

(iv)

f
maps
\mathbb{T}
onto
\Gamma_{\delta}
and
f^{\prime}(z)\neq 0
for all
z\in\mathbb{T}
.

Proposition 2.2
.

Let
\delta\in[0,1)
.
Let
T\in\mathcal{B}(\mathcal{H})
with
\sigma(T)\subseteq K_{\delta}
. Then, for

f
as described in Lemma
2.1
,

G(z)=\frac{zf^{\prime}(z)}{T-f(z)}

is an analytic
\mathcal{B}(\mathcal{H})
-valued function on
\mathbb{D}\setminus\{0\}
which extends to an analytic function on
\mathbb{D}
satisfying
G(0)=1
. Furthermore,

W(T)\subseteq K_{\delta}\iff\forall_{z\in\mathbb{D}}\ \re G(z)\geq 0.

(2.3)

Proof.

By assumption,
\sigma(T)\subseteq K_{\delta}
. By Lemma
2.1
(i),

f
maps
\mathbb{D}\setminus\{0\}
to the resolvent set of
T
.
Hence
(T-f(z))^{-1}
is analytic on
\mathbb{D}\setminus\{0\}
. Thus,

G
is analytic on
\mathbb{D}\setminus\{0\}
. Furthermore,

\lim_{z\to 0}G(z)=\lim_{z\to 0}\frac{zf^{\prime}(z)}{T-f(z)}=\lim_{z\to 0}\frac{\delta z^{2}-1}{zT-1-\delta z^{2}}=1,

so the singularity of
G
at 0 is removable, with
G(0)=1
.

Now observe that Lemma
2.1
(iv) implies that for each
\theta
,
f(e^{i\theta})\in\Gamma_{\delta}
and
ie^{i\theta}f^{\prime}(e^{i\theta})
points tangent to
\Gamma_{\delta}
at
f(e^{i\theta})
. Furthermore, Lemma
2.1
(i) implies that
ie^{i\theta}f^{\prime}(e^{i\theta})
is oriented in the clockwise direction. Consequently, for each
\theta
,
e^{i\theta}f^{\prime}(e^{i\theta})
is an inward pointing normal to
\Gamma_{\delta}
at
f(e^{i\theta})
.

The observations in the preceding paragraph imply that for each
\theta
, the set
H_{\theta}
of complex numbers defined by

H_{\theta}=\{\mu\in\mathbb{C}\,:\,\re\overline{e^{i\theta}f^{\prime}(e^{i\theta})}(\mu-f(e^{i\theta}))\geq 0\},

is the supporting half plane to
K_{\delta}
at
f(e^{i\theta})
. Since
W(T)\subseteq K_{\delta}
if and only if
W(T)\subseteq H_{\theta}
for all
\theta
, and
W(T)\subseteq H_{\theta}
if and only if

\re\overline{e^{i\theta}f^{\prime}(e^{i\theta})}(T-f(e^{i\theta}))\geq 0,

it follows that

W(T)\subseteq K_{\delta}\iff\forall_{\theta\in\mathbb{R}}\ \re\overline{e^{i\theta}f^{\prime}(e^{i\theta})}(T-f(e^{i\theta}))\geq 0.

(2.4)

Note

\overline{e^{i\theta}f^{\prime}(e^{i\theta})}(T-f(e^{i\theta}))=|f^{\prime}(e^{i\theta})|^{2}\frac{T-f(e^{i\theta})}{e^{i\theta}f^{\prime}(e^{i\theta})}.

If
X
is an invertible operator, then

\re X^{-1}=\frac{1}{2}(X^{-1}+(X^{-1})^{*})=X^{-1}\frac{1}{2}(X+X^{*})(X^{-1})^{*}=X^{-1}\re X(X^{-1})^{*}.

Thus

\re X\geq 0\ \text{if and only if}\ \re X^{-1}\geq 0.

(2.5)

Hence, by the equivalences (
2.4
) and (
2.5
),

\displaystyle W(T)\subseteq K_{\delta}

\displaystyle\iff\forall_{\theta\in\mathbb{R}}\ \re\frac{e^{i\theta}f^{\prime}(e^{i\theta})}{T-f(e^{i\theta})}\geq 0

(2.6)

\displaystyle\iff\re G(z)\geq 0\ \text{for all}\ z\in\mathbb{T}\;\text{(by the definition of}\,G).

(2.7)

Finally, let us show that
\re G(z)\geq 0
for all
z\in\mathbb{D}
if and only if

\re G(z)\geq 0
for all
z\in\mathbb{T}
.
We have already shown that
G

is an analytic
\mathcal{B}(\mathcal{H})
-valued function on
\mathbb{D}
and is continuous
on
\overline{\mathbb{D}}
. It is immediate from the continuity of
G
that if

\re G(z)\geq 0
for all
z\in\mathbb{D}
then

\re G(z)\geq 0
for all
z\in\mathbb{D}^{-}
, and so, in particular for all
z\in\mathbb{T}
.
Conversely, suppose that, for all
z\in\mathbb{T}
,
\re G(z)\geq 0.

Take the composition of the inverse
C^{-1}
of the Cayley transform
C:\mathbb{D}\to\mathbb{H}
with
G
, where
\mathbb{H}
denotes the right half plane
\{z\in\mathbb{C}:\re z>0\}
.
Then
C^{-1}\circ G
is an analytic
\mathcal{B}(\mathcal{H})
-valued function on
\mathbb{D}
, continuous on
\overline{\mathbb{D}}
and maps
\mathbb{T}
to the unit ball of
\mathcal{B}(\mathcal{H})
. By the Maximum Principle, for all
z\in\mathbb{D}
,

\|C^{-1}\circ G(z)\|\leq 1.

Hence, for all
z\in\mathbb{D}
,
\re G(z)\geq 0.

The statement (
2.3
) follows.
∎

3.
Strange dilation on an ellipse

In this section we shall combine the Herglotz Representation Theorem for operator-valued functions with Proposition
2.2
to obtain an analog of Berger’s Theorem for
K_{\delta}
.

We first recall the following generalization of the Herglotz Representation Theorem due to Naimark
[
17
,
16
]
.

Theorem 3.1
.

Let
\mathcal{H}
be a Hilbert space and assume that
V
is an analytic
\mathcal{B}(\mathcal{H})
-valued function defined on
\mathbb{D}
satisfying
\re V(z)\geq 0
for all
z\in\mathbb{D}
. Then there exist a Hilbert space
\mathcal{K}
, an isometry
I:\mathcal{H}\to\mathcal{K}
, and a unitary operator
U\in\mathcal{B}(\mathcal{K})
such that

V(z)=I^{*}\frac{1+zU}{1-zU}I\quad\text{ for all }z\in\mathbb{D}.

We shall massage
G
to produce a closely related function
F
, which has the virtue that it gives rise to a dilation theorem for operators with numerical range in an elliptical region that is closely analogous to Berger’s strange dilation theorem (see Theorem
1.6
above).

Lemma 3.2
.

Let
\delta\in[0,1)
.
Let
T\in\mathcal{B}(\mathcal{H})
with
\sigma(T)\subseteq K_{\delta}
. Then

F(z)=\frac{1-\tfrac{1}{2}zT}{1-zT+\delta z^{2}},\qquad z\in\mathbb{D},

(3.3)

is an analytic
\mathcal{B}(\mathcal{H})
-valued function on
\mathbb{D}
satisfying
F(0)=1
.
Moreover, for every
z\in\mathbb{D}
,

F(z)=\frac{1}{2}\ (G(z)+1),

(3.4)

where

G(z)=\frac{zf^{\prime}(z)}{T-f(z)}\;\;\text{and}\;\;f(z)=1/z+\delta z.

Proof.

Note that, for all
z\neq 0
,

1-zT+\delta z^{2}=z(\frac{1}{z}+\delta z-T)=z(f(z)-T).

Hence, for
z\in\mathbb{D}\setminus\{0\}
,

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}\ (G(z)+1)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\ \left(\frac{zf^{\prime}(z)}{T-f(z)}+1\right)=\frac{1}{2}\ \left(\frac{z(-1/z^{2}+\delta)}{T-f(z)}+1\right)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\ \left(\frac{-1/z+\delta z-1/z-\delta z+T)}{T-f(z)}\right)=\frac{1}{2}\ \left(\frac{-2/z+T}{T-f(z)}\right)

\displaystyle=\frac{1-\frac{1}{2}zT}{z(f(z)-T)}=F(z).

By Proposition
2.2
,
G
is an analytic
\mathcal{B}(\mathcal{H})
-valued function on
\mathbb{D}\setminus\{0\}
, with a removable singularity at
0
, satisfying
G(0)=1
.
Hence,
F
is a well defined analytic function on
\mathbb{D}\setminus\{0\}
, with a removable singularity at
0
, satisfying
F(0)=1
.
∎

We shall now prove Theorem
1.8
from the Introduction.

Theorem 3.5
.

Let
\delta\in[0,1)
.
Let
T\in\mathcal{B}(\mathcal{H})
be an operator satisfying
\sigma(T)\subseteq K_{\delta}
,
and let

be defined by the formula

Then
W(T)\subseteq K_{\delta}
if and only if there exists a Hilbert space
\mathcal{K}\supseteq\mathcal{H}
, an isometry
I:\mathcal{H}\to\mathcal{K}
, and a unitary operator

F(z)=I^{*}\frac{1}{1-zU}I\quad\text{ for all }z\in\mathbb{D}.

(3.6)

Proof.

Proposition
2.2
and Theorem
3.1
imply that
W(T)\subseteq K_{\delta}
if and only if, for the analytic function

\mathcal{K}

Note that, for all

\frac{1}{2}\ \left(\frac{1+zU}{1-zU}+1\right)=\frac{1}{1-zU}.

Hence, for all

I^{*}\frac{1}{1-zU}I=I^{*}\frac{1}{2}\ \left(\frac{1+zU}{1-zU}+1\right)I=\frac{1}{2}\ \left(I^{*}\frac{1+zU}{1-zU}I+1\right).

Definition 3.8

Let

Proposition 3.10
.

Let
\delta\in(0,1)
, let
\mu\in G_{\delta}
, and let

4.

X^{1}X^{2}=\delta
, we have
X^{2}=\delta(X^{1})^{-1}
, and so (possibly after renumbering)
\lambda^{2}_{j}=\delta(\lambda^{1}_{j})^{-1}
for
j=1,\dots,n
, and an eigenvector of
X^{2}
corresponding to
\lambda^{2}_{j}
is the eigenvector
e_{j}
of
X^{1}
corresponding to
\lambda^{1}_{j}
. Further,
T=X^{1}+X^{2}=X^{1}+\delta(X^{1})^{-1}
, and so
e_{j}
is an eigenvector of
T
corresponding to the eigenvalue
\lambda^{1}_{j}+\delta(\lambda^{1}_{j})^{-1}
.
∎

We denote by
\mathcal{P}
the set of polynomials over
\mathbb{C}
. Note that if
T\in\mathcal{B}(\mathcal{H})
has a generic prepair then
T
has exactly
2^{n}
generic prepairs.
If
T
is generic for
K_{\delta}
, then for each choice of generic prepair
X=(X^{1},X^{2})
one can define a pair of
residues

A=(A^{1},A^{2})
to be the unique pair of operators in the algebra generated by
T
satisfying

\frac{1-\frac{1}{2}zT}{1-zT+\delta z^{2}}=\frac{A^{1}}{1-zX^{1}}+\frac{A^{2}}{1-zX^{2}}\quad\mbox{ for all }z\in\mathbb{D}.

(4.11)

It is easy to verify that

A^{1}=\frac{1}{2}\ \ \text{ and }\ \ A^{2}=\frac{1}{2}

for all choices of generic prepairs. The following lemma gives a valuable formula which relates prepairs and residues to strange dilations.

Lemma 4.12
.

Let
\delta\in(0,1)
.
Suppose that
T\in\mathcal{B}(\mathcal{H})
is generic for
K_{\delta}
and
X
is a generic prepair for
T
with residues
A
. If
(\mathcal{K},I,U)
is a strange dilation of
T
, then

A^{1}p(X^{1})+A^{2}p(X^{2})=I^{*}p(U)I

for all
p\in\mathcal{P}
.

Proof.

Under the assumptions of the lemma, since
(\mathcal{K},I,U)
is a strange dilation of
T
, then the formula

\frac{1-\tfrac{1}{2}zT}{1-zT+\delta z^{2}}=I^{*}\frac{1}{1-zU}I

(4.13)

holds for all
z\in\mathbb{D}
. The formula (
4.11
) implies that, for all
z\in\mathbb{D}
,

\frac{\tfrac{1}{2}}{1-zX^{1}}+\frac{\tfrac{1}{2}}{1-zX^{2}}=I^{*}\frac{1}{1-zU}I.

If we expand the analytic functions in this formula into power series and equate coefficients we obtain the relations

(X^{1})^{k}+(X^{2})^{k}=2I^{*}U^{k}I,\;\;\;k=1,2,\dots.

(4.14)

The lemma follows if one takes linear combinations of equations of the form (
4.14
).
∎

5.
Cutting down strange dilations

We have shown in Proposition
3.10
that if
T
is the operator of multiplication by a scalar
\mu\in G_{\delta}

on a finite- or infinite-dimensional Hilbert space
\mathcal{H}
, then necessarily, for any strange dilation
(\mathcal{K},I,U)
of
T
,
\mathcal{K}
is infinite dimensional.
One can obtain a
finite
-dimensional dilation of
T
if one is
willing to replace the unitary
U
in Definition
3.8
with a contraction
Y
.
This idea is formalized in the following definition.

Definition 5.1
.

Let
\delta\in(0,1)
and let
T\in\mathcal{B}(\mathcal{H})
where
\mathcal{H}
is a finite-dimensional Hilbert space and assume that
\sigma(T)\subseteq K_{\delta}
. We say that a triple
(\mathcal{L},E,Y)
is
an even stranger dilation of
T
relative to
K_{\delta}

if
\mathcal{L}
is a Hilbert space with
\dim\mathcal{L}=2\dim\mathcal{H}
,
E:\mathcal{H}\to\mathcal{L}
is an isometry,
Y\in\mathcal{B}(\mathcal{L})
is a contraction,
and

\frac{1-\frac{1}{2}zT}{1-zT+\delta z^{2}}=E^{*}\frac{1}{1-zY}E\quad\text{ for all }z\in\mathbb{D}.

(5.2)

We may omit the phrase “relative to
K_{\delta}
” when it is clear from the context.

Proposition 5.3
.

Let
\delta\in(0,1)
, let
T\in\mathcal{B}(\mathcal{H})
and let
\dim\mathcal{H}
be finite. Assume that
T
is generic for
K_{\delta}
. Then
T
has a strange dilation relative to
K_{\delta}
if and only if
T
has an even stranger dilation relative to
K_{\delta}
.

Proof.

First assume that
T
has a strange dilation
(\mathcal{K},I,U)
relative to
K_{\delta}
, as in Definition
3.8
. Fix a generic prepair
X=(X^{1},X^{2})
of
T
with residue
A=(A^{1},A^{2})
and assume that
e_{i}
is an eigenvector of
T
corresponding to the eigenvalue
\mu_{i}
, as in Definition
4.1
and

\lambda_{i}^{r}
is the corresponding eigenvalue of
X^{r}
, so that

X^{r}e_{i}=\lambda_{i}^{r}e_{i}\qquad\mbox{ for }r=1,2\;\;\mbox{ and }\;i=1,\ldots,n.

(5.4)

For
i=1,\ldots,n
, let

\mathcal{I}_{i}=\{p\in\mathcal{P}\,:\,p(\lambda_{i}^{r})=0,r=1,2\}

and define closed linear subspaces
\mathcal{M}_{i}
and
\mathcal{N}_{i}
in
\mathcal{K}
by

\mathcal{M}_{i}=\overline{\text{span}\{p(U)Ie_{i}\,:\,p\in\mathcal{P}\}}

and

\mathcal{N}_{i}=\overline{\text{span}\{p(U)Ie_{i}\,:\,p\in\mathcal{I}_{i}\}}.

Note that
\mathcal{M}_{i}
and
\mathcal{N}_{i}
are invariant for
U
,
\mathcal{N}_{i}\subseteq\mathcal{M}_{i}
, and that
\mathcal{N}_{i}
has codimension at most 2 in
\mathcal{M}_{i}
.

Let

\mathcal{M}=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\mathcal{M}_{i}\qquad\text{ and }\qquad\mathcal{N}=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\mathcal{N}_{i}.

Since for each
i
, both
\mathcal{M}_{i}
and
\mathcal{N}_{i}
are invariant for
U
, so also
\mathcal{M}
and
\mathcal{N}
are invariant for
U
. Consequently, if we define a Hilbert space
\mathcal{L}
by

\mathcal{L}=\mathcal{M}\ominus\mathcal{N}=\{y\in\mathcal{M}\,:\,y\perp\mathcal{N}\},

and
Y\in\mathcal{B}(\mathcal{L})
by

Y=P_{\mathcal{L}}U|\mathcal{L},

then

p(Y)=P_{\mathcal{L}}p(U)|\mathcal{L}

(5.5)

for all
p\in\mathcal{P}
. Also, since for each
i
,
\mathcal{N}_{i}
has codimension at most 2 in
\mathcal{M}_{i}
,

\dim\mathcal{L}\leq 2n.

(5.6)

Now notice that if
x=\sum_{i}c_{i}e_{i}\in\mathcal{H}
, then

Ix=\sum_{i}c_{i}Ie_{i}\in\sum_{i}\mathcal{M}_{i}=\mathcal{M}.

Therefore,
\ran I\subseteq\mathcal{M}
. Also observe that if
p\in\mathcal{I}_{i}
and
y=p(U)Ie_{i}
, then by Lemma
4.12

2I^{*}y=2I^{*}p(U)Ie_{i}=(A^{1}p(X^{1})+A^{2}p(X^{2}))e_{i}=p(\lambda_{i}^{1})A^{1}e_{i}+p(\lambda_{i}^{2})A^{2}e_{i}=0.

Therefore,
\mathcal{N}_{i}\subseteq\ker I^{*}
for each
i
. Consequently,
\mathcal{N}\subseteq\ker I^{*}
, or equivalently,
\ran I\subseteq\mathcal{N}^{\perp}
. As we have both
\ran I\subseteq\mathcal{M}
and
\ran I\subseteq\mathcal{N}^{\perp}
,

\ran I\subseteq\mathcal{L}.

(5.7)

Now observe that if we define
E:\mathcal{H}\to\mathcal{L}
, by the formula

E(x)=P_{\mathcal{L}}I(x)

then inclusion (
5.7
) implies that
E
is an isometry. Also, using formula (
5.5
), we deduce that if
k\geq 0
, then

I^{*}U^{k}I=(I^{*}P_{\mathcal{L}})(P_{\mathcal{L}}U^{k}|\mathcal{L})(P_{\mathcal{L}}I)=(I^{*}P_{\mathcal{L}})(P_{\mathcal{L}}U|\mathcal{L})^{k}(P_{\mathcal{L}}I)=E^{*}Y^{k}E.

(5.8)

Therefore, if
z\in\mathbb{D}
,

\displaystyle\frac{1-\frac{1}{2}zT}{1-zT+\delta z^{2}}=

\displaystyle I^{*}\frac{1}{1-zU}I=\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}z^{k}I^{*}U^{k}I

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}z^{k}E^{*}Y^{k}E=E^{*}\frac{1}{1-zY}E,

that is, the formula (
5.2
) holds for all
z\in\mathbb{D}
.

To summarize, we have shown that
E
is an isometry and the formula (
5.2
) holds for all
z\in\mathbb{D}
. It is clear that
Y=P_{\mathcal{L}}U|\mathcal{L}
is a contraction, since
U
is unitary.

There remains to show that
\dim\mathcal{L}=2\dim\mathcal{H}
. For each
i=1,\ldots,n
and
r=1,2
, choose
\chi_{i}^{r}\in\mathcal{P}
such that for each
i
and
r
.

\chi_{i}^{r}(\lambda_{i}^{s})=\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}1&\mbox{if }s=r\\
0&\mbox{if }s\not=r\end{array}.\right.

Note that if
p\in\mathcal{P}
, then, by equation (
5.8
) and by Lemma
4.12
,

\displaystyle E^{*}\big(p(Y)\chi_{i}^{r}(Y)Ie_{i}\big)

\displaystyle=\big(A^{1}p(X^{1})\chi_{i}^{r}(X^{1})+A^{2}p(X^{2})\chi_{i}^{r}(X^{1})\big)e_{i}

\displaystyle=\big(\delta^{1}_{r}p(\lambda_{i}^{1})A^{1}+\delta^{2}_{r}p(\lambda_{i}^{2})A^{2}\big)e_{i},

(5.9)

where
\delta^{k}_{r}
is the Kronecker symbol.
We claim that the set
S
of vectors in
\mathcal{L}
defined by

S=\{\chi_{i}^{r}(Y)Ie_{i}\,:\,i=1,\ldots,n,\ \ r=1,2\}

is linearly independent. For if
c_{i}^{r}
are complex numbers with

\sum_{i,r}c_{i}^{r}\ \chi_{i}^{r}(Y)Ie_{i}=0,

and
p\in\mathcal{P}
, then by equation (
5
),

\displaystyle 0

\displaystyle=E^{*}p(Y)\sum_{i,r}c_{i}^{r}\ \chi_{i}^{r}(Y)Ie_{i}

\displaystyle=\sum_{i,r}c_{i}^{r}\ E^{*}p(Y)\chi_{i}^{r}(Y)Ie_{i}

\displaystyle=\sum_{i,r}c_{i}^{r}\ \big(\delta^{1}_{r}p(\lambda_{i}^{1})A^{1}+\delta^{2}_{r}p(\lambda_{i}^{2})A^{2}\big)e_{i}.

But since
p\in\mathcal{P}
is arbitrary and the residues
A^{1}
and
A^{2}
are invertible, this calculation shows that the set
S
is linearly independent. In particular,
\dim\mathcal{L}\geq 2n
. Hence, in light of inequality (
5.6
),
\dim\mathcal{L}=2n
, as was to be proved.

Now assume that
(\mathcal{L},E,Y)
is an even stranger dilation of
T
relative to
K_{\delta}
. As in particular,
Y
is a contraction, by the Nagy Dilation Theorem, there exists a Hilbert Space
\mathcal{K}
, an isometry
F:\mathcal{L}\to\mathcal{K}
, and a unitary
U\in\mathcal{B}(\mathcal{K})
such that

p(Y)=F^{*}p(U)F

for all
p\in\mathcal{P}
. Consequently, if we set
I=FE
, then it follows that
(\mathcal{K},I,U)
is a strange dilation of
T
relative to
K_{\delta}
.
∎

6.
The structure of even stranger dilations

In this section we characterize generic operators
T
with
W(T)\subseteq K_{\delta}
using even stranger dilations of
T
.

Lemma 6.1
.

Let
\delta\in(0,1)
and let
T\in\mathcal{B}(\mathcal{H})
be generic for
K_{\delta}
. Assume that
X=(X^{1},X^{2})
is a generic prepair for
T
. If
(\mathcal{K},E,Y)
is an even stranger dilation of
T
relative to
K_{\delta}
, then
Y
is similar to
X^{1}\oplus X^{2}
, that is, there exists an invertible operator
S\in\mathcal{B}(\mathcal{H}\oplus\mathcal{H},\mathcal{K})

such that
Y=S(X^{1}\oplus X^{2})S^{-1}
.

Proof.

If
(\mathcal{K},E,Y)
is an even stranger dilation of
T
relative to
K_{\delta}
, then equations (
4.11
) and (
5.2
) imply that

\frac{\frac{1}{2}}{1-zX^{1}}+\frac{\frac{1}{2}}{1-zX^{2}}=E^{*}\frac{1}{1-zY}E\quad\text{ for all }z\in\mathbb{D}.

(6.2)

Let
S_{1}=1/\sigma(X^{1})\cup 1/\sigma(X^{2})
and
S_{2}=1/\sigma(Y)
. Since
\dim\mathcal{H}<\infty
and
\dim\mathcal{K}=2\dim\mathcal{H}
,
S_{1}
and
S_{2}
are finite sets. Furthermore, the left hand side of equation (
6.2
) is analytic on
\mathbb{C}\setminus S_{1}
and the right hand side of equation (
6.2
) is analytic on
\mathbb{C}\setminus S_{2}
. Since
\frac{\frac{1}{2}}{1-zX^{1}}+\frac{\frac{1}{2}}{1-zX^{2}}
and
E^{*}\frac{1}{1-zY}E
are analytic functions on
\mathbb{C}\setminus(S_{1}\cup S_{2})
that agree on a nonempty open set, they agree on
\mathbb{C}\setminus(S_{1}\cup S_{2})
.
Therefore, equation
(
6.2
)
holds for all
z\in\mathbb{C}\setminus(S_{1}\cup S_{2})
.

Fix
\lambda\in\sigma(X^{1})
with corresponding eigenvector
e
. Then since
\sigma(X^{1})
and
\sigma(X^{2})
are disjoint,
\|(z-X^{1})^{-1}e\|
is unbounded and
\|(z-X^{2})^{-1}e\|
is bounded on
\{z\in\mathbb{C}\,:\,0<|z-\lambda|<\varepsilon\}
for all
sufficiently small
\varepsilon>0
. Consequently, equation (
6.2
) implies that
\|(z-Y)^{-1}e\|
is unbounded on
\{z\in\mathbb{C}\,:\,0<|z-\lambda|<\varepsilon\}

for all sufficiently small
\varepsilon>0
. Therefore,
\lambda\in\sigma(Y)
.

The previous paragraph showed that
\sigma(X^{1})\subseteq\sigma(Y)
. By a similar argument it follows that
\sigma(X^{2})\subseteq\sigma(Y)
as well. Since
X=(X^{1},X^{2})
is a generic prepair,
\sigma(X^{1})\cup\sigma(X^{2})
consists of
2\dim\mathcal{H}
points and
\dim\mathcal{K}=2\dim\mathcal{H}
. Let
\dim\mathcal{H}=n
,

\sigma(X^{1})=\{\lambda_{i}:i=1,2,\dots,n\}\;\text{ and }\;\sigma(X^{2})=\{\lambda_{i}:i=n+1,\;n+2,\dots,2n\}.

There are vectors

u_{1},\dots,u_{2n}\in\mathcal{H}\oplus\mathcal{H}

such that

(X^{1}\oplus X^{2})u_{i}=\lambda_{i}u_{i}\;\text{ for}\;i=1,2,\dots,2n.

Since
Y
acts on a
2n
-dimensional space and
\sigma(X^{1})\cup\sigma(X^{2})\subseteq\sigma(Y)
, it follows that

\sigma(Y)=\sigma(X^{1})\cup\sigma(X^{2})
. Hence there are vectors

v_{1},\dots,v_{2n}\in\mathcal{K}

such that

Yv_{i}=\lambda_{i}v_{i}\;\text{ for}\;i=1,2,\dots,2n.

The vectors
u_{i},i=1,\dots,2n,
and
v_{i},i=1,\dots,2n,
comprise bases for
\mathcal{H}\oplus\mathcal{H}
and
\mathcal{K}
respectively, since they are both
2n
in number and correspond to distinct eigenvalues.
Define an operator

S:\mathcal{H}\oplus\mathcal{H}\to\mathcal{K}\;\text{ by }\;Su_{i}=v_{i}\;\text{ for}\;i=1,2,\dots,2n.

Then

YSu_{i}=Yv_{i}=\lambda_{i}v_{i}=\lambda_{i}Su_{i},

and so

S^{-1}YSu_{i}=S^{-1}(Yv_{i})=S^{-1}(\lambda_{i}v_{i})=\lambda_{i}u_{i}=(X^{1}\oplus X^{2})u_{i}\;\text{ for}\;i=1,2,\dots,2n.

It follows that
S\in\mathcal{B}(\mathcal{H}\oplus\mathcal{H},\mathcal{K})
is an invertible operator
such that
Y=S(X^{1}\oplus X^{2})S^{-1}
.
∎

Lemma 6.3
.

Let
\delta\in(0,1)
and let

T\in\mathcal{B}(\mathcal{H})
be generic for
K_{\delta}
. Assume that
X=(X^{1},X^{2})
is a generic prepair for
T
. Then
(\mathcal{K},E,Y)
is an even stranger dilation of
T
relative to
K_{\delta}
if and only if there exist an invertible
S\in\mathcal{B}(\mathcal{H}\oplus\mathcal{H},\mathcal{K})
and
C\in\mathcal{B}(\mathcal{H},\mathcal{H}\oplus\mathcal{H})
such that

Y=S(X^{1}\oplus X^{2})S^{-1}\ \ \text{ and }\ \ E=SC,

(6.4)

and where
S
and
C
satisfy the following three conditions.

(i)

(X^{1}\oplus X^{2})^{*}S^{*}S(X^{1}\oplus X^{2})\leq S^{*}S.

(ii)

If we decompose
C
as

C=\begin{bmatrix}C^{1}\\
C^{2}\end{bmatrix},

then, for each
i=1,2
,
C^{i}
is invertible and commutes with
X^{i}
and
T
.

(iii)

If
C
is decomposed as in Condition (ii) above, then

S^{*}S\begin{bmatrix}C^{1}\\
C^{2}\end{bmatrix}=\frac{1}{2}\begin{bmatrix}{(C^{1})^{*}}^{-1}\\
{(C^{2})^{*}}^{-1}\end{bmatrix}.

Proof.

First assume that
(\mathcal{K},E,Y)
is an even stranger dilation of
T
.
The formula

\frac{1-\tfrac{1}{2}zT}{1-zT+\delta z^{2}}=E^{*}\frac{1}{1-zY}E

(6.5)

holds for all
z\in\mathbb{D}
. The formula (
4.11
) implies that, for all
z\in\mathbb{D}
,

\frac{\frac{1}{2}}{1-zX^{1}}+\frac{\frac{1}{2}}{1-zX^{2}}=E^{*}\frac{1}{1-zY}E.

If we expand the analytic functions in this formula into power series and equate coefficients we obtain

\frac{1}{2}(X^{1})^{k}+\frac{1}{2}(X^{2})^{k}=E^{*}Y^{k}E,\quad k=1,2,\dots.

Let us take linear combinations of the last equations to get

\frac{1}{2}p(X^{1})+\frac{1}{2}p(X^{2})=E^{*}p(Y)E

(6.6)

for all
p\in\mathcal{P}
.

By Lemma
6.1
, there exists an invertible
S\in\mathcal{B}(\mathcal{H}\oplus\mathcal{H},\mathcal{K})
such that

Y=S(X^{1}\oplus X^{2})S^{-1}.

(6.7)

Fix such an
S
and define
C\in\mathcal{B}(\mathcal{H},\mathcal{H}\oplus\mathcal{H})
by
C=S^{-1}E
. Clearly, equation (
6.4
) holds. Also, as
\|Y\|\leq 1
,
Y^{*}Y\leq 1
, which implies that Condition (i) holds.

To see that Condition (ii) holds, let
B\in\mathcal{B}(\mathcal{H}\oplus\mathcal{H},\mathcal{H})
be defined by
B=E^{*}S
. If we decompose
B
as

B=\begin{bmatrix}B^{1}&B^{2}\end{bmatrix},

then, by equations (
6.6
), (
6.7
) and the definitions of
B
and
C
, it follows that for all
p\in\mathcal{P}
,

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}p(X^{1})+\frac{1}{2}p(X^{2})

\displaystyle=E^{*}p(Y)E

\displaystyle=BS^{-1}p(Y)SC

\displaystyle=Bp(X^{1}\oplus X^{2})C

\displaystyle=B^{1}p(X^{1})C^{1}+B^{2}p(X^{2})C^{2}.

(6.8)

By assumption,
X
is a generic prepair for
T
, and so
\sigma(X^{1})
and
\sigma(X^{2})
are disjoint and finite. Hence there exists a polynomial
p_{0}
such that
p_{0}(z)=1
for all
z\in\sigma(X^{1})
and
p_{0}(z)=0\;
for all
z\in\sigma(X^{2})
. In equation (
6
)
replace
p
by
pp_{0}
and observe that

p_{0}(X^{1})=1
and
p_{0}(X^{2})=0
, and so equation (
6
) becomes

\frac{1}{2}p(X^{1})=B^{1}p(X^{1})C^{1}

(6.9)

for all
p\in\mathcal{P}
.
Similarly,

\frac{1}{2}p(X^{2})=B^{2}p(X^{2})C^{2}

(6.10)

for all
p\in\mathcal{P}
.
Let
p=1
in equations (
6.9
) and (
6.10
), to get

B^{1}C^{1}=\frac{1}{2}\ \ \text{ and }\ \ B^{2}C^{2}=\frac{1}{2},

which implies that
C^{1}
and
C^{2}
are invertible.
Let
p=z
in equations (
6.9
) and (
6.10
), to get

\frac{1}{2}X^{1}=\frac{1}{2}{C^{1}}^{-1}X^{1}C^{1}\ \ \text{ and }\ \ \frac{1}{2}X^{2}=\frac{1}{2}{C^{2}}^{-1}X^{2}C^{2},

(6.11)

which implies that
C^{1}
commutes with
X^{1}
and
C^{2}
commutes with
X^{2}
.
By equation (
4.9
),

T=X^{1}+\delta(X^{1})^{-1}\;\;\text{and}\;\;T=X^{2}+\delta(X^{2})^{-1}.

Therefore,

C^{1}
and
C^{2}
commute with
T
.

Finally, to see that Condition (iii) holds, let
B
be as in the previous paragraph and compute that

\displaystyle S^{*}S\begin{bmatrix}C^{1}\\
C^{2}\end{bmatrix}

\displaystyle=S^{*}E

\displaystyle=(E^{*}S)^{*}

\displaystyle=\begin{bmatrix}B^{1}&B^{2}\end{bmatrix}^{*}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\begin{bmatrix}{(C^{1})^{*}}^{-1}\\
{(C^{2})^{*}}^{-1}\end{bmatrix}.

Now assume that
S\in\mathcal{B}(\mathcal{H}\oplus\mathcal{H},\mathcal{K})
is invertible,
C\in\mathcal{B}(\mathcal{H},\mathcal{H}\oplus\mathcal{H})
, and that
S
and
C
satisfy Conditions (i), (ii), and (iii). We wish to show that if
Y
and
E
are defined by formulae (
6.4
), then
(\mathcal{K},E,Y)
is an even stranger dilation of
T
. First observe that since
S
is invertible,
\dim\mathcal{K}=2\dim\mathcal{H}
. Also note that as
Y=S(X^{1}\oplus X^{2})S^{-1}
, Condition (i) implies that
Y^{*}Y\leq 1
, so that
\|Y\|\leq 1
. There remains to show that
E
is an isometry and that equation (
5.2
) holds.

Using Condition (iii) we see that

E^{*}E=C^{*}(S^{*}SC)=\begin{bmatrix}{(C^{1})^{*}}&{(C^{2})^{*}}\end{bmatrix}\Big(\frac{1}{2}\begin{bmatrix}{(C^{1})^{*}}^{-1}\\
{(C^{2})^{*}}^{-1}\end{bmatrix}\Big)=1.

Therefore
E
is an isometry.

To prove that equation (
5.2
) holds, fix
z\in\mathbb{D}
and note that

\displaystyle E^{*}\frac{1}{1-zY}E

\displaystyle=(C^{*}S^{*})\frac{1}{1-zS(X^{1}\oplus X^{2})S^{-1}}(SC)

\displaystyle=(C^{*}S^{*})S\frac{1}{1-z(X^{1}\oplus X^{2})}S^{-1}(SC)

S\in\mathcal{B}(\mathcal{H})
such that if
C
is defined by the equation

C=\begin{bmatrix}1&0\\
0&S\end{bmatrix}Z_{T}\begin{bmatrix}1&0\\
0&S\end{bmatrix}^{-1},

(8.17)

then
C
is a
(\delta,\mathcal{H})
-germinator.

A converse to Proposition
8.16
hinges on the inversion of equation (
8.6
) which we turn to now.

Lemma 8.18
.

If
T\in\mathcal{B}(\mathcal{H})
is generic for
K_{\delta}
, then

Z_{T}+\frac{\delta}{Z_{T}}=T\oplus T.

Proof.

By equation (
8.9
),
Z_{T}
is
J-equivalent
to
\delta Z_{T}^{-1}
.
Thus

\displaystyle Z_{T}+\frac{\delta}{Z_{T}}

\displaystyle=Z_{T}+JZ_{T}J

(8.19)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\begin{bmatrix}T&\sqrt{T^{2}-4\delta}\\
\sqrt{T^{2}-4\delta}&T\end{bmatrix}+\frac{1}{2}\begin{bmatrix}T&-\sqrt{T^{2}-4\delta}\\
-\sqrt{T^{2}-4\delta}&T\end{bmatrix}

\displaystyle=T\oplus T.

∎

Lemma 8.20
.

If
C
is a
(\delta,\mathcal{H})
-germinator, then

C+\frac{\delta}{C}=C_{1}\oplus C_{2},

where
C_{2}
is similar to
C_{1}
.

Proof.

Since
C
is a
(\delta,\mathcal{H})
-germinator,
\frac{\delta}{C}=JCJ
, and so

\displaystyle C+\frac{\delta}{C}

\displaystyle=C+JCJ

(8.21)

\displaystyle=\begin{bmatrix}C_{11}&C_{12}\\
C_{21}&C_{22}\end{bmatrix}+\begin{bmatrix}C_{11}&-C_{12}\\
-C_{21}&C_{22}\end{bmatrix}

\displaystyle=2C_{11}\oplus 2C_{22}.

Accordingly, define
C_{1}=\frac{1}{2}C_{11}
and
C_{2}=\frac{1}{2}C_{22}
.

Noting that

\begin{bmatrix}\delta&0\\
0&\delta\end{bmatrix}=(\frac{\delta}{C})\ C=\begin{bmatrix}C_{11}&-C_{12}\\
-C_{21}&C_{22}\end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix}C_{11}&C_{12}\\
C_{21}&C_{22}\end{bmatrix}=\begin{bmatrix}C_{11}^{2}-C_{12}C_{21}&C_{11}C_{12}-C_{12}C_{22}\\
C_{22}C_{21}-C_{21}C_{11}&C_{22}^{2}-C_{21}C_{12}\end{bmatrix},

we see from the 1-2 entry that

C_{11}C_{12}-C_{12}C_{22}=0.

Therefore, the relation (
8.15
)

0\not\in\sigma(C_{12}),

implies that
C_{12}
is invertible and

C_{1}=\tfrac{1}{2}C_{11}=\tfrac{1}{2}C_{12}C_{22}C_{12}^{-1}=C_{12}C_{2}C_{12}^{-1}.

Thus
C_{2}
is similar to
C_{1}
.
∎

Proposition 8.22
.

Let
\mathcal{H}
be a finite-dimensional Hilbert space and
\dim\mathcal{H}=n
, and let
C\in\mathcal{B}(\mathcal{H}\oplus\mathcal{H})

be a
(\delta,\mathcal{H})
-germinator, then there exists an invertible
S\in\mathcal{B}(\mathcal{H})
and a
T\in\mathcal{W}_{\delta}(\mathcal{H})
such that
T
is generic for
K_{\delta}
and the equation

C=\begin{bmatrix}1&0\\
0&S\end{bmatrix}Z_{T}\begin{bmatrix}1&0\\
0&S\end{bmatrix}^{-1}

(8.23)

holds.

Proof.

First observe using equation (
8.14
) that if

C\begin{pmatrix}u\\
v\end{pmatrix}=\lambda\begin{pmatrix}u\\
v\end{pmatrix},

then

C\begin{pmatrix}u\\
-v\end{pmatrix}=\frac{\delta}{\lambda}\begin{pmatrix}u\\
-v\end{pmatrix}.

Thus, using the fact (
8.13
) that
C
has
2n
distinct eigenvalues,
we may decompose the set of eigenvalues of
C
into a disjoint union

\bigcup_{i=1}^{n}\ \{\lambda_{i},\frac{\delta}{\lambda_{i}}\}.

Since all eigenvalues of
C
lie in
R_{\delta}
,
\sigma(C)\subseteq R_{\delta}
.

Furthermore, if
\begin{pmatrix}u_{i}\\
v_{i}\end{pmatrix}
and
\begin{pmatrix}u_{i}\\
-v_{i}\end{pmatrix}
are the eigenvectors corresponding to
\lambda_{i}
and
\delta/\lambda_{i}
respectively, then the statement (
8.13
), that
C
has
2n
distinct eigenvalues, implies that
\{u_{1},u_{2},\ldots,u_{n}\}
is a basis for
\mathcal{H}
. Therefore, there exist
S,T\in\mathcal{B}(\mathcal{H})
satisfying

Su_{i}=v_{i}\qquad\text{and}\qquad Tu_{i}=(\lambda_{i}+\frac{\delta}{\lambda_{i}})u_{i},\qquad\ \ \ \ i=1,2,\ldots,n,

(8.24)

and the operator
Q
defined by

Qu_{i}=(\lambda_{i}-\frac{\delta}{\lambda_{i}})u_{i},\qquad\ \ \ \ i=1,2,\ldots,n

is a square root of
T^{2}-4\delta
that commutes with
T
.
Observe that
T
is the restriction of
C+\frac{\delta}{C}
to
\mathcal{H}\oplus\{0\}
which is the span of
u_{1},\dots,u_{n}
,
and so

\sigma(T)\subseteq\sigma(\pi(C))\subseteq\pi(R_{\delta})\subseteq K_{\delta}.

By the definition of
T
, the eigenvalues of
T
are the points
\mu_{i}=\pi(\lambda_{i}),i=1,\dots,n
.
Since
C
is a
(\delta,\mathcal{H})
-germinator,
C
has
2n
distinct eigenvalues
\lambda_{i},\frac{\delta}{\lambda_{i}},i=1,\dots,n
. Therefore the equation
\pi(\lambda)=\mu_{i}
has two distinct solutions in
R_{\delta}
for each
i
. Hence
T
is generic for
K_{\delta}
.

To see that the equation (
8.23
) holds it suffices to show that the two operators

\begin{bmatrix}1&0\\
0&S\end{bmatrix}^{-1}C\qquad\text{ and }\qquad Z_{T}\begin{bmatrix}1&0\\
0&S\end{bmatrix}^{-1}

agree on the eigenvectors of
C
(which the relation (
8.13
) guarantees to be a basis for
\mathcal{H}\oplus\mathcal{H}
). But for fixed
i
,

\displaystyle\begin{bmatrix}1&0\\
0&S\end{bmatrix}^{-1}C\begin{pmatrix}u_{i}\\
v_{i}\end{pmatrix}

\displaystyle=\lambda_{i}\begin{bmatrix}1&0\\
0&S\end{bmatrix}^{-1}\begin{pmatrix}u_{i}\\
v_{i}\end{pmatrix}=\lambda_{i}\begin{pmatrix}u_{i}\\
u_{i}\end{pmatrix}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\begin{bmatrix}T&Q\\
Q&T\end{bmatrix}\begin{pmatrix}u_{i}\\
u_{i}\end{pmatrix}=Z_{T}\begin{pmatrix}u_{i}\\
u_{i}\end{pmatrix}

\displaystyle=Z_{T}\begin{bmatrix}1&0\\
0&S\end{bmatrix}^{-1}\begin{pmatrix}u_{i}\\
v_{i}\end{pmatrix},

and likewise,

\displaystyle\begin{bmatrix}1&0\\
0&S\end{bmatrix}^{-1}C\begin{pmatrix}u_{i}\\
-v_{i}\end{pmatrix}

\displaystyle=\frac{\delta}{\lambda_{i}}\begin{bmatrix}1&0\\
0&S\end{bmatrix}^{-1}\begin{pmatrix}u_{i}\\
-v_{i}\end{pmatrix}=\frac{\delta}{\lambda_{i}}\begin{pmatrix}u_{i}\\
-u_{i}\end{pmatrix}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\begin{bmatrix}T&Q\\
Q&T\end{bmatrix}\begin{pmatrix}u_{i}\\
-u_{i}\end{pmatrix}=Z_{T}\begin{pmatrix}u_{i}\\
-u_{i}\end{pmatrix}

\displaystyle=Z_{T}\begin{bmatrix}1&0\\
0&S\end{bmatrix}^{-1}\begin{pmatrix}u_{i}\\
-v_{i}\end{pmatrix}.

Therefore,

\begin{bmatrix}1&0\\
0&S\end{bmatrix}^{-1}C=Z_{T}\begin{bmatrix}1&0\\
0&S\end{bmatrix}^{-1}

and equation (
8.23
) holds. By Theorem
8.3
,
W(T)\subseteq K_{\delta}
.
∎

Evidently, Theorem
8.3
may be restated in the following way.

Theorem 8.25
.

Let
\delta\in(0,1)
and let
T\in\mathcal{B}(\mathcal{H})
be generic for
K_{\delta}
. Then
T\in\mathcal{W}_{\delta}(\mathcal{H})
if and only if
there exists a
(\delta,\mathcal{H})
-germinator
C\in\mathcal{B}(\mathcal{H}\oplus\mathcal{H})
and an invertible operator
S\in\mathcal{B}(\mathcal{H})
such that

C=\begin{bmatrix}1&0\\
0&S\end{bmatrix}Z_{T}\begin{bmatrix}1&0\\
0&S\end{bmatrix}^{-1}.

(8.26)

Moreover, for any such
(\delta,\mathcal{H})
-germinator
C
,

T=\left(C+\frac{\delta}{C}\right)\big|_{\mathcal{H}\oplus\{0\}}.

(8.27)

Proof.

By Proposition
8.16
, if
T\in\mathcal{W}_{\delta}(\mathcal{H})
is generic for
K_{\delta}
, then exists an invertible
S\in\mathcal{B}(\mathcal{H})
such that if
C
is defined by the equation

C=\begin{bmatrix}1&0\\
0&S\end{bmatrix}Z_{T}\begin{bmatrix}1&0\\
0&S\end{bmatrix}^{-1},

(8.28)

then
C
is a
(\delta,\mathcal{H})
-germinator.

Conversely, suppose that
C\in\mathcal{B}(\mathcal{H}\oplus\mathcal{H})
is a
(\delta,\mathcal{H})
-germinator and there is an invertible operator
S\in\mathcal{B}(\mathcal{H})
such that

C=\begin{bmatrix}1&0\\
0&S\end{bmatrix}Z_{T}\begin{bmatrix}1&0\\
0&S\end{bmatrix}^{-1}.

(8.29)

By the definition of a
(\delta,\mathcal{H})
-germinator,
\|C\|\leq 1
, and so equation

\big\|\begin{bmatrix}1&0\\
0&S\end{bmatrix}Z_{T}\begin{bmatrix}1&0\\
0&S\end{bmatrix}^{-1}\big\|=\|C\|\leq 1.

(8.30)

is satisfied. Thus, by Theorem
8.3
,
T\in\mathcal{W}_{\delta}(\mathcal{H})
.

Note that, by equation (
8.26
),

C=\begin{bmatrix}1&0\\
0&S\end{bmatrix}Z_{T}\begin{bmatrix}1&0\\
0&S\end{bmatrix}^{-1}=\frac{1}{2}\begin{bmatrix}T&QS^{-1}\\
SQ&STS^{-1}\end{bmatrix}.

Thus, by Lemma
8.20
, since
C\in\mathcal{B}(\mathcal{H}\oplus\mathcal{H})
is a
(\delta,\mathcal{H})
-germinator,

C+\frac{\delta}{C}=2C_{11}\oplus 2C_{22}=\begin{bmatrix}T&0\\
0&STS^{-1}\end{bmatrix}.

Therefore,

T=\left(C+\frac{\delta}{C}\right)\big|_{\mathcal{H}\oplus\{0\}}.

∎

9.
A parametrization of the operators
T
with
W(T)\subseteq{K}_{\delta}

In view of Theorem
8.25
it is relatively straightforward to parametrize

\mathcal{W}_{\delta}(\mathcal{H})
.

Lemma 9.1
.

Let
\delta\in(0,1)
, let
\mathcal{H}
be a Hilbert space of finite dimension
n
and let
C\in\mathcal{B}(\mathcal{H}\oplus\mathcal{H})
. Suppose that the operator
C
satisfies the relations

\|C\|\leq 1\text{ and }\frac{\delta}{C}=JCJ.

(9.2)

Then
C=\sqrt{\delta}XJ
for some
X
having the block matrix representation

X=\begin{bmatrix}1&E\\
0&-1\end{bmatrix},

(9.3)

with respect to the decomposition

\mathcal{H}\oplus\mathcal{H}=\ker(1-X)\oplus\big(\ker(1-X)\big)^{\perp},

(9.4)

and where the operator
E:\big(\ker(1-X)\big)^{\perp}\to\ker(1-X)
satisfies

\|E\|\leq\frac{1}{\sqrt{\delta}}-\sqrt{\delta}.

(9.5)

Proof.

Suppose that
C
satisfies the relations (
9.2
). Then
\delta=CJCJ
. Let
X=\delta^{-\tfrac{1}{2}}CJ
. Then
X^{2}=1
, so that, for any
u\in\mathcal{H}\oplus\mathcal{H},(1+X)(1-X)u=(1-X)(1+X)u=(1-X^{2})u=0
. It follows that

\ran(1+X)\subseteq\ker(1-X),\quad\ran(1-X)\subseteq\ker(1+X).

Since

x=\tfrac{1}{2}(1+X)x+\tfrac{1}{2}(1-X)x\quad\text{ for all }x\in\mathcal{H}\oplus\mathcal{H},

it follows that
\ker(1-X)+\ker(1+X)=\mathcal{H}\oplus\mathcal{H}
. Since clearly
\ker(1-X)\cap\ker(1+X)=\{0\}
,

\mathcal{H}\oplus\mathcal{H}
is the vector space direct sum of
\ker(1-X)
and
\ker(1+X)
.
Let us write

V_{-}=\ker(1-X),\quad V_{+}=\ker(1+X)

for the eigenspaces of
X
corresponding to the eigenvalues
1,-1
respectively. The operator matrix of
X
relative to the orthogonal decomposition
\mathcal{H}\oplus\mathcal{H}=V_{-}\oplus V_{-}^{\perp}
has the form

X\sim\begin{pmatrix}1&E\\
0&X_{22}\end{pmatrix}

for some operators
E:V_{-}^{\perp}\to V_{-}
and
X_{22}:V_{-}^{\perp}\to V_{-}^{\perp}
.
Since
X^{2}=1
, we have also
X_{22}^{2}=1
and therefore
\sigma(X_{22})\subseteq\{1,-1\}
, by the spectral mapping theorem. However,
V_{-}^{\perp}
does not contain any eigenvector of
X
corresponding to the eigenvalue
1
, and so
\sigma(X_{22})=\{-1\}
. Thus
1-X_{22}
is invertible,
and so

0=\big(1-X_{22}\big)^{-1}\big(1-X_{22}^{2}\big)=1+X_{22},

that is,
X_{22}=-1
.
The expression of
X
by an operator matrix with respect to the direct decomposition
\mathcal{H}\oplus\mathcal{H}=V_{-}\oplus V_{-}^{\perp}
becomes

X\sim\begin{pmatrix}1&E\\
0&-1\end{pmatrix}.

Since
\|C\|\leq 1
,
\|X\|=\|\delta^{-\tfrac{1}{2}}CJ\|\leq\delta^{-\tfrac{1}{2}}
, and so
\delta X^{*}X\leq 1
. Thus

0\leq 1-\delta X^{*}X=\begin{bmatrix}1-\delta&-\delta E\\
-\delta E^{*}&1-\delta-\delta E^{*}E\end{bmatrix}

(9.6)

For general matrices
A,B,D
of appropriate sizes the identity

\begin{bmatrix}1&0\\
-B^{*}A^{-1}&1\end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix}A&B\\
B^{*}&D\end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix}1&-A^{-1}B\\
0&1\end{bmatrix}=\begin{bmatrix}A&0\\
0&D-B^{*}A^{-1}B\end{bmatrix},

is valid whenever the operator
A
is invertible. Select
A=1-\delta,B=-\delta E,D=1-\delta-\delta E^{*}E
to deduce that

\begin{bmatrix}1-\delta&-\delta E\\
-\delta E^{*}&1-\delta-\delta E^{*}E\end{bmatrix}\mbox{ is congruent to }\begin{bmatrix}1-\delta&0\\
0&1-\delta-\frac{\delta}{1-\delta}E^{*}E\end{bmatrix}.

It follows from the inequality (
9.6
) that
1-\delta-\frac{\delta}{1-\delta}E^{*}E\geq 0
, so that

E^{*}E\leq\frac{(1-\delta)^{2}}{\delta}=\big(\frac{1}{\sqrt{\delta}}-\sqrt{\delta}\big)^{2}.

Hence
\|E\|\leq\frac{1}{\sqrt{\delta}}-\sqrt{\delta}
.
∎

Lemma 9.7
.

Let
\delta\in(0,1)
.
If
C
is a
(\delta,\mathcal{H})
-germinator, then there exist
A,F\in\mathcal{B}(\mathcal{H})
such that
A
is a self-adjoint contraction,

\|F\|\leq\frac{1}{\sqrt{\delta}}-\sqrt{\delta},

(9.8)

and

C_{11}=\sqrt{\delta}\ \big(A+\tfrac{1}{2}\sqrt{1+A}\ F\ \sqrt{1-A}\big)

(9.9)

Proof.

Using Lemma
9.1
we see that

C_{11}=\sqrt{\delta}\ V^{*}(XJ)V

where
X
is as in the relations (
9.3
), (
9.5
), and

V:\mathcal{H}\to\ker(1-X)\oplus\big(\ker(1-X)\big)^{\perp}

is an isometry. Decomposing
V
into a block
2\times 1
matrix

V=\begin{bmatrix}V_{1}\\
V_{2}\end{bmatrix}:\mathcal{H}\to\ker(1-X)\oplus\big(\ker(1-X)\big)^{\perp}

we obtain the formula

C_{11}=\sqrt{\delta}\ (V_{1}^{*}V_{1}-V_{2}^{*}V_{2}-V_{1}^{*}EV_{2}^{*}).

(9.10)

If we set
A=V_{1}^{*}V_{1}-V_{2}^{*}V_{2}
, then as
V_{1}^{*}V_{1}+V_{2}^{*}V_{2}=1
,

A
is a self-adjoint contraction, as required by the statement of the lemma.
Furthermore,

V_{1}^{*}V_{1}={\frac{1+A}{2}}\qquad\text{ and }\qquad V_{2}^{*}V_{2}={\frac{1-A}{2}}

so that
V_{1}
and
V_{2}
have the polar decompositions

V_{1}=U_{1}\sqrt{\frac{1+A}{2}}\qquad\text{ and }\qquad V_{2}=U_{2}\sqrt{\frac{1-A}{2}}

where
U_{1}
and
U_{2}
are Hilbert space isomorphisms. In terms of these new operators, equation (
9.10
) becomes

C_{11}=\sqrt{\delta}\ \big(A-\tfrac{1}{2}\sqrt{1+A}\ U_{1}^{*}EU_{2}\ \sqrt{1-A}\big).

(9.11)

Finally, if we define
F=-U_{1}^{*}EU_{2}
, equation (
9.11
) becomes equation (
9.9
) and as
U_{1}
and
U_{2}
are Hilbert space isomorphisms, equation (
9.5
) implies equation (
9.8
).
∎

Lemma 9.12
.

Let
\delta\in(0,1)
and let
\mathcal{H}
be a finite dimensional Hilbert space. If
T\in\mathcal{W}_{\delta}(\mathcal{H})
is generic for
K_{\delta}
, then there exist a pair of contractions
A,Y\in\mathcal{B}(\mathcal{H})
such that

A
is self-adjoint and

T=2\sqrt{\delta}A+(1-\delta)\sqrt{1+A}\ Y\sqrt{1-A}.

(9.13)

Proof.

Assume that
T\in\mathcal{W}_{\delta}(\mathcal{H})
is generic for
K_{\delta}
.
By Theorem
8.25
, there exists a
(\delta,\mathcal{H})
-germinator
C
such that (
8.26
) holds. In particular,

T=2C_{11}.

By Lemma
9.7
, there exist
A,F\in\mathcal{B}(\mathcal{H})
such that
A
is a self-adjoint contraction such that (
9.8
) and (
9.9
) hold.
If we define

Y=\frac{\sqrt{\delta}}{1-\delta}F,

then
\|F\|\leq 1
and the equation (
9.13
) holds.
∎

For
c\in\mathbb{C}
and
r\geq 0
we let
D_{c}(r)
denote the closed disc in the complex plane centered at
c
of radius
r
, that is,

D_{c}(r)=\{z\in\mathbb{C}\,:\,|z-c|\leq r\}.

The following lemma describes the radii of the maximal discs in
K_{\delta}
centered on the major axis of
K_{\delta}
.

Lemma 9.14
.

Let
\delta\in(0,1)
.
If, for
t\in[-(1+\delta),1+\delta]
, we define

\rho(t)=\sup\ \{r\,:\,D_{(t,0)}(r)\subseteq K_{\delta}\},

then

\rho(t)=\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}(1-\delta)\sqrt{1-\frac{t^{2}}{4\delta}}&\mbox{if }t\in[-\frac{4\delta}{1+\delta},\frac{4\delta}{1+\delta}\ ]\\
1+\delta-|t|&\mbox{if }t\in[-(1+\delta),1+\delta]\setminus[-\frac{4\delta}{1+\delta},\frac{4\delta}{1+\delta}\ ]\end{array}\right..

Proof.

For
t\in[-\frac{4\delta}{1+\delta},\frac{4\delta}{1+\delta}\ ]
, the maximal disc always touches the boundary of
K_{\delta}
in two distinct points symmetric about the
x
-axis and the formula for
\rho(t)
can be justified by Lagrange multipliers. For
t\not\in[-\frac{4\delta}{1+\delta},\frac{4\delta}{1+\delta}\ ]
, the maximal disc touches the boundary of
K_{\delta}
in a single point
\pm(1+\delta)
, which explains the formula for
\rho(t)
in that case.
∎

It was slightly surprising to the authors that the role played by the transition points
\pm\frac{4\delta}{1+\delta}
in the preceding lemma was not played by the foci (
\pm 2\sqrt{\delta}
). However, the foci of
K_{\delta}
appear in the following corollary to Lemma
9.14
.

Lemma 9.15
.

Let
\delta\in(0,1)
.
If
t\in[-2\sqrt{\delta},2\sqrt{\delta}]
and
z\in\mathbb{C}
, then

|z|\leq(1-\delta)\sqrt{1-\frac{t^{2}}{4\delta}}\implies t+z\in K_{\delta}.

Proof.

As
\pm\frac{4\delta}{1+\delta}\in[-2\sqrt{\delta},2\sqrt{\delta}]
, there are two cases, both of which follow immediately from Lemma
9.14
.
∎

Proposition 9.16
.

Let
\delta\in(0,1)
.
If
\mathcal{H}
is a Hilbert space (not assumed to be finite dimensional),
A
and
Y
are contractions in
\mathcal{B}(\mathcal{H})
,
A
is self-adjoint, and
T\in\mathcal{B}(\mathcal{H})
is defined by the formula,

T=2\sqrt{\delta}A+(1-\delta)\sqrt{1+A}\ Y\sqrt{1-A},

then
W(T)\subseteq K_{\delta}
.

Proof.

Fix
v\in\mathcal{H}
with
\|v\|=1
. We need to show that

\langle Tv,v\rangle\in K_{\delta}.

(9.17)

We observe that if we set
a=\langle Av,v\rangle

\displaystyle\langle Tv,v\rangle

\displaystyle=2\sqrt{\delta}a+(1-\delta)\langle\sqrt{1+A}\ Y\sqrt{1-A}v,v\rangle

\displaystyle=2\sqrt{\delta}a+(1-\delta)\langle Y\sqrt{1-A}v,\sqrt{1+A}v\rangle.

Hence, as
Y
is a contraction,

\displaystyle|\langle Tv,v\rangle-2\sqrt{\delta}a|^{2}

\displaystyle=(1-\delta)^{2}\ \big|\langle Y\sqrt{1-A}v,\sqrt{1+A}v\rangle\big|^{2}

\displaystyle\leq(1-\delta)^{2}\ \|\sqrt{1-A}v\|^{2}\ \|\sqrt{1+A}v\|^{2}

\displaystyle=(1-\delta)^{2}\ \langle(1-A)v,v\rangle\ \langle(1+A)v,v\rangle

\displaystyle=(1-\delta)^{2}(1-a^{2}).

Therefore,

\langle Tv,v\rangle\in D_{2\sqrt{\delta}a}(\ (1-\delta)\sqrt{1-a^{2}}\ ),

which implies via Lemma
9.15
(with
t=2\sqrt{\delta}a
) that equation (
9.17
) holds.
∎

We close this section by noting that, in the finite dimensional case, Lemma
9.12
and Proposition
9.16
can be combined to yield the following generalization of a theorem of Ando that parametrizes the operators with numerical range in
\mathbb{D}^{-}
(
[
4
, Theorem 1]
).

Theorem 9.18
.

Let
\delta\in(0,1)
.
Let
\mathcal{H}
be a finite dimensional Hilbert space and assume that
T\in\mathcal{B}(\mathcal{H})
.
W(T)\subseteq K_{\delta}
if and only if there exist a pair of contractions
A,Y\in\mathcal{B}(\mathcal{H})
such that

A
is self-adjoint and

T=2\sqrt{\delta}A+(1-\delta)\sqrt{1+A}\ Y\sqrt{1-A}.

(9.19)

Proof.

The theorem follows from Lemma
9.12
and Proposition
9.16
provided only that we may remove the genericity assumption in Lemma
9.12
. Accordingly assume
T\in\mathcal{W}_{\delta}(\mathcal{H})
. Choose a generic sequence
\{T_{k}\}
such that
T_{k}\in\mathcal{W}_{\delta}(\mathcal{H})
for all
k
and
T_{k}\to T
as
k\to\infty
in the weak operator topology. By Lemma
9.12
, for each
k
there exist contractions
A_{k},Y_{k}\in\mathcal{B}(\mathcal{H})
such that
A_{k}
is self-adjoint and

T_{k}=2\sqrt{\delta}A_{k}+2(1-\delta)\sqrt{\frac{1+A_{k}}{2}}\ Y_{k}\sqrt{\frac{1-A_{k}}{2}}.

(9.20)

By passing to a subsequence if necessary we may assume that there exist
A,Y\in\mathcal{B}(\mathcal{H})
such that

A_{k}\to A\qquad\text{and}\;\;Y_{k}\to Y\;\;\;\text{as}\;\;k\to\infty.

(9.21)

Evidently
A_{k}
and
Y_{k}
contractive for all
k

10.

and Douglas-Paulsen operators

In this section we present yet another consequence of Theorem

Consider a number
\delta\in(0,1)
.
In
[
11

R. G. Douglas and V. Paulsen developed a dilation theory for operators

on some Hilbert space

such that
\delta^{2}\leq T^{*}T\leq 1

More exposition and references on such operators can be found in
[

Proposition 10.1
.

Let
\delta\in(0,1)
.
Fix a finite-dimensional Hilbert space
\mathcal{H}
and let
X\in\mathcal{B}(\mathcal{H})
be a Douglas-Paulsen operator with parameter
\delta
. Then
W(\pi(X))\subseteq K_{\delta}
.

Proof.

Let
\mathcal{H}
be
n
-dimensional and notice that
\sigma(X)\subseteq R_{\delta}^{-}
. Set
T=\pi(X)=X+\delta/X
. We first prove the proposition under the assumptions that

\sigma(X)\cap\sigma(\delta/X)=\emptyset

(10.2)

and both
X
and
\delta/X
have
n
distinct eigenvalues.
The hypotheses imply that
(X,\delta/X)
is a generic prepair for
T
.
We observe that the fact

\sigma(T)=\sigma(\pi(X))=\pi(\sigma(X))\subseteq\pi(R_{\delta}^{-})\subseteq K_{\delta}

ensures (with the aid of Lemma
4.10
) that
T
is generic for
K_{\delta}
.
Furthermore, as

T=X+\delta/X\qquad

and
X-\delta/X
is an operator that commutes with
T
and satisfies
(X-\delta/X)^{2}=T^{2}-4\delta
, we may choose
Q=X-\delta/X
in the definition of
Z_{T}
, equation (
7.5
), and we have

\displaystyle Z_{T}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\begin{bmatrix}T&Q\\
Q&T\end{bmatrix}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\begin{bmatrix}X+\delta/X&X-\delta/X\\
X-\delta/X&X+\delta/X\end{bmatrix}

\displaystyle=U\begin{bmatrix}X&0\\
0&\delta/X\end{bmatrix}U,

where

U=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}\begin{bmatrix}1&1\\
1&-1\end{bmatrix}.

Consequently, as
\|X\|,\|\delta/X\|\leq 1
and
U
is unitary,
\|Z_{T}\|\leq 1
. Hence
condition (
8.4
) holds (with
S=1
) and Theorem
8.3
implies that
W(T)\subseteq K_{\delta}
.

To relax the assumptions (
10.2
) and the assumption that

has
n
distinct eigenvalues, assume that
X
is now a general Douglas-Paulsen operator with parameter
\delta
. Choose a sequence
\{X_{k}\}
such that

is a Douglas-Paulsen operator with parameter
\delta
,

has

distinct eigenvalues, the stated assumptions hold for all

and

as

for the weak operator topology.
By the special case that has been proved above,

for all

.
∎

Proposition
10.1
asserts that
\pi:X\mapsto X+\delta/X
maps the class of Douglas-Paulsen operators with parameter

on a Hilbert space

into the class of operators on

whose numerical ranges are contained in

.

Fact 10.3
.

The map
\pi
is not onto, even in the case of a

Proof.

Consider the operator

\sqrt{\delta}-\frac{1}{\sqrt{\delta}}\end{pmatrix}

Suppose that

\displaystyle X\begin{bmatrix}v_{1}&v_{2}\end{bmatrix}

\displaystyle=\begin{bmatrix}v_{1}&v_{2}\end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix}\sqrt{\delta}&0\\
0&-\sqrt{\delta}\end{bmatrix},\;\text{ and so}

\displaystyle X

\displaystyle=\begin{bmatrix}1&2\\
0&\sqrt{\delta}-\frac{1}{\sqrt{\delta}}\end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix}\sqrt{\delta}&0\\
0&-\sqrt{\delta}\end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix}1&2\\

j=1,\dots,m
,

\mathcal{F}_{\gamma}=\mathcal{F}_{\mathrm{bbc}}(c,r).

The rest of the proof follows from Corollary
11.7
and is similar to the case (i).
∎

Our goal is now to prove Proposition
11.16
which shows the supremum in the definition of
\|\varphi\|_{\mathrm{bfd}}
, instead of being taken over all operators in the B. and F. Delyon family, can equivalently be taken only over the operators in the family that act on a finite-dimensional Hilbert space.
Since the family
\mathcal{F}_{\mathrm{bfd}}(G_{\delta})
is not of the form
\mathcal{F}_{\gamma}
for any
\gamma
, we cannot deduce the desired equality directly from Corollary
11.7
. We therefore approximate the bfd norm by the bbc norm corresponding to a suitable bbc pair, and then use Proposition
11.9
to show that the required inequality holds.

The approximation result we need, which is
[
2
, Lemma 9.76]
, is the following.

Lemma 11.13
.

Let
C
be a smoothly bounded open strictly convex set in
\mathbb{C}
and
\varepsilon>0
. If
\mathcal{H}
is a Hilbert space,
T\in\mathcal{B}(\mathcal{H})
, and
\overline{W(T)}\subseteq C
, then there exists a bbc pair
(c,r)
such that

C\subseteq D(c,r)\subseteq C+\varepsilon\mathbb{D}

(11.14)

and

T\in\mathcal{F}_{\mathrm{bbc}}(c,r).

(11.15)

It is easy to see that
G_{\delta}
is indeed a smoothly bounded open strictly convex set in
\mathbb{C}
.

Proposition 11.16
.

Let
\delta\in(0,1)
and let
\varphi\in{\rm Hol}(G_{\delta})
. The following equation holds:

\|\varphi\|_{\mathrm{bfd}}=\sup_{T\in\mathcal{F}_{\mathrm{bfd}}(G_{\delta}),\;T\text{ is a matrix}}\|\varphi(T)\|.

(11.17)

Proof.

It is obvious that

\sup_{T\in\mathcal{F}_{\mathrm{bfd}}(G_{\delta})}\|\varphi(T)\|\geq\sup_{T\in\mathcal{F}_{\mathrm{bfd}}(G_{\delta}),\;T\text{ is a matrix}}\|\varphi(T)\|.

Therefore, equation (
11.17
) will follow if we can show that

\sup_{T\in\mathcal{F}_{\mathrm{bfd}}(G_{\delta})}\|\varphi(T)\|\leq\sup_{T\in\mathcal{F}_{\mathrm{bfd}}(G_{\delta}),\;T\text{ is a matrix}}\|\varphi(T)\|.

(11.18)

Notice that

s\in[0,1]\text{ and }T\in\mathcal{F}_{\mathrm{bfd}}(G_{\delta})\implies sT\in\mathcal{F}_{\mathrm{bfd}}(G_{\delta}).

Also, as
\varphi(sz)
converges uniformly to
\varphi(z)
on compact subsets of
G_{\delta}
as
s\to 1-
, it follows by the continuity of the Riesz-Dunford Functional Calculus that

\|\varphi(T)-\varphi(sT)\|\to 0\text{ as }s\to 1-

whenever
T\in\mathcal{F}_{\mathrm{bfd}}(G_{\delta})
. These two facts imply that

\sup_{T\in\mathcal{F}_{\mathrm{bfd}}(G_{\delta})}\|\varphi(T)\|=\sup_{T\in\mathcal{F}_{\mathrm{bfd}}(G_{\delta}),\,s\in[0,1)}\|\varphi(sT)\|.

Consequently, inequality (
11.18
) will follow if we can show that

\sup_{T\in\mathcal{F}_{\mathrm{bfd}}(G_{\delta}),\ s\in[0,1)}\|\varphi(sT)\|\leq\sup_{T\in\mathcal{F}_{\mathrm{bfd}}(G_{\delta}),\;T\text{ is a matrix}}\|\varphi(T)\|.

(11.19)

To prove inequality (
11.19
) fix
T\in\mathcal{F}_{\mathrm{bfd}}(G_{\delta})
, and let
s\in[0,1)
. Let
C=sG_{\delta}
. As
C^{-}
is a compact subset of
G_{\delta}
, there exists
\varepsilon>0
such that

C+\varepsilon\mathbb{D}\subseteq G_{\delta}.

(11.20)

Also, as
\overline{W(T)}\subseteq G_{\delta}
,
\overline{W(sT)}\subseteq C
. Consequently, by Lemma
11.13
(with
T
replaced by
sT
) there exists a bbc pair
(c,r)
such that

C\subseteq D(c,r)\subseteq C+\varepsilon\mathbb{D}

(11.21)

and

sT\in\mathcal{F}_{\mathrm{bbc}}(c,r).

(11.22)

Therefore, by Proposition
11.9
, we have

\|\varphi(sT)\|\leq\|\varphi\|_{\mathrm{bbc}}=\sup_{X\in\mathcal{F}_{\mathrm{bbc}}(c,r),\;X\text{ is a matrix}}\|\varphi(X)\|.

Hence, if
\eta>0
, there is a matrix
M\in\mathcal{F}_{\mathrm{bbc}}(c,r)
such that

\|\varphi(M)\|>\|\varphi\|_{\mathrm{bbc}}-\eta,

and so

\|\varphi(sT)\|\leq\|\varphi\|_{\mathrm{bbc}}<\|\varphi(M)\|+\eta.

(11.23)

But, as
M\in\mathcal{F}_{\mathrm{bbc}}(c,r)
, in particular,
\overline{W(M)}\subseteq D(c,r)
and the inclusions (
11.21
) and (
11.20
) imply that
D(c,r)\subseteq G_{\delta}
. Therefore,
M\in\mathcal{F}_{\mathrm{bfd}}(G_{\delta})
.

To summarize, in the previous two paragraphs we have shown that if
T\in\mathcal{F}_{\mathrm{bfd}}(G_{\delta})
,
s\in[0,1)
, and
\eta>0
, then there exists a matrix
M\in\mathcal{F}_{\mathrm{bfd}}(G_{\delta})
such that the inequality (
11.23
) holds. This proves the inequality (
11.19
) and completes the proof of Proposition
11.16
.
∎

Now observe that if
\varphi\in{\rm Hol}(G_{\delta})
then we may define
\pi^{\sharp}(\varphi)\in{\rm Hol}(R_{\delta})
by the formula

\pi^{\sharp}(\varphi)(\lambda)=\varphi(\pi(\lambda))\qquad\text{ for all }\lambda\in R_{\delta}.

We record the following simple fact from complex analysis without proof.

Lemma 11.24
.

Let
\delta\in(0,1)
and let

\psi\in{\rm Hol}(R_{\delta})
. Then
\psi\in\ran\pi^{\sharp}
if and only if
\psi
is
symmetric
with respect to the involution
\lambda\mapsto\delta/\lambda
of
R_{\delta}
, that is, if and only if
\psi
satisfies

\psi(\delta/\lambda)=\psi(\lambda)

for all
\lambda\in R_{\delta}
.

The following theorem gives an intimate connection between the
\|\cdot\|_{\mathrm{dp}}
and
\|\cdot\|_{\mathrm{bfd}}
norms.

Theorem 11.25
.

Let
\delta\in(0,1)
.
The mapping
\pi^{\sharp}
is an isometric isomorphism from
{\rm H}^{\infty}_{\mathrm{bfd}}(G_{\delta})
onto the set of symmetric functions with respect to the involution
\lambda\mapsto\delta/\lambda
in
{\rm H}^{\infty}_{\mathrm{dp}}(R_{\delta})
, so that, for all
\varphi\in{\rm Hol}(G_{\delta})
,

\|\varphi\|_{\mathrm{bfd}}=\|\varphi\circ\pi\|_{\mathrm{dp}}.

(11.26)

Proof.

To see that the equation (
11.26
) holds it suffices to prove
that, for fixed
\varphi\in{\rm Hol}(G_{\delta})
the following inequalities are satisfied:

\|\pi^{\sharp}(\varphi)\|_{\mathrm{dp}}\leq\|\varphi\|_{\mathrm{bfd}}

(11.27)

and

\|\pi^{\sharp}(\varphi)\|_{\mathrm{dp}}\geq\|\varphi\|_{\mathrm{bfd}}.

(11.28)

To prove the inequality (
11.27
) fix a matrix
X\in\mathcal{F}_{\mathrm{dp}}(\delta)
.
By Proposition
10.1
,
\pi(X)\in\mathcal{F}_{\mathrm{bfd}}(G_{\delta})
.
Hence,

\displaystyle\|\pi^{\sharp}(\varphi)(X)\|

\displaystyle=\|\varphi(\pi(X))\|

\displaystyle\leq\sup_{T\in\mathcal{F}_{\mathrm{bfd}}(G_{\delta})}\|\varphi(T)\|=\|\varphi\|_{\mathrm{bfd}}.

Consequently, by Proposition
11.9
,

\|\pi^{\sharp}(\varphi)\|_{\mathrm{dp}}=\sup_{X\in\mathcal{F}_{\mathrm{dp}}(\delta),\;X\;\text{is a matrix}}\|\pi^{\sharp}(\varphi)(X)\|\leq\|\varphi\|_{\mathrm{bfd}}.

To prove the inequality (
11.28
), that
\|\varphi\|_{\mathrm{bfd}}\leq\|\varphi\circ\pi\|_{\mathrm{dp}}
, we use Proposition
10.4
. Indeed, let
\varphi\in{\rm Hol}(G_{\delta})
, by Proposition
11.16
, the following equation holds:

\|\varphi\|_{\mathrm{bfd}}=\sup_{T\in\mathcal{F}_{\mathrm{bfd}}(G_{\delta}),\;T\text{ is a matrix}}\|\varphi(T)\|.

(11.29)

Consider any matrix
T\in\mathcal{F}_{\mathrm{bfd}}(G_{\delta})
. We have
\overline{W(T)}\subseteq G_{\delta}\subset K_{\delta}
, and so Proposition
10.4
applies that there exists
X\in\mathcal{B}(\mathcal{H}\oplus\mathcal{H})
such that
\|X\|\leq 1
and
\|X^{-1}\|\leq 1/\delta
and

T=(X+\delta X^{-1})|\ \mathcal{H}\oplus\{0\}
.
Thus
T
is a restriction of
\pi(X)
to an invariant subspace

\mathcal{N}=\mathcal{H}\oplus\{0\}
for
\pi(X)
.
Therefore,

\displaystyle\|\varphi(T)\|

\displaystyle=\|\varphi(\pi(X)|\mathcal{N})\|

\displaystyle\leq\|\varphi(\pi(X))\|=\|\pi^{\sharp}(\varphi)(X)\|

\displaystyle\leq\|\pi^{\sharp}(\varphi)\|_{\mathrm{dp}}.

Take the supremum of both sides over all matrices
T\in\mathcal{F}_{\mathrm{bfd}}(G_{\delta})
to infer that

\|\varphi\|_{\mathrm{bfd}}=\sup_{T\in\mathcal{F}_{\mathrm{bfd}}(G_{\delta}),\;T\;\text{is a matrix}}\|\varphi(T)\|\leq\|\pi^{\sharp}(\varphi)\|_{\mathrm{dp}}.

(11.30)

We claim now that the mapping
\pi^{\sharp}
maps
{\rm H}^{\infty}_{\mathrm{bfd}}(G_{\delta})
surjectively onto the set of functions in
{\rm H}^{\infty}_{\mathrm{dp}}(R_{\delta})
that are symmetric with respect to the involution
\lambda\mapsto\delta/\lambda
.
For consider any function
f\in{\rm H}^{\infty}_{\mathrm{dp}}(R_{\delta})
that is symmetric with respect to the involution
\lambda\mapsto\delta/\lambda
, that is, such that
f(\delta/\lambda)=f(\lambda)
for all
\lambda\in R_{\delta}
. By Lemma
11.24
, there exists a function
\varphi\in{\rm Hol}(G_{\delta})
such that
\pi^{\sharp}\varphi=f
. By the inequality (
11.30
),
\|\varphi\|_{\mathrm{bfd}}\leq\|f\|_{\mathrm{dp}}<\infty
.
Hence
\varphi\in{\rm H}^{\infty}_{\mathrm{bfd}}(G_{\delta})
and
\pi^{\sharp}\varphi=f
.
∎

12.
The Banach algebra
{\rm H}^{\infty}_{\mathrm{bfd}}(G_{\delta})
and the symmetrized bidisc

In this section we shall give an interpretation of the B. and F. Delyon norm on
{\rm Hol}(G_{\delta})
in terms of the solution to a certain extremal problem in two complex variables. Let
G
denote the symmetrized bidisc, the open set in
\mathbb{C}^{2}
defined by either of the equivalent formulas

G=\rho(\mathbb{D}^{2})

where
\rho:\mathbb{C}^{2}\to\mathbb{C}^{2}
, the
symmetrization map
, is defined by

\rho(z)=(z_{1}+z_{2},z_{1}z_{2}),\qquad z=(z_{1},z_{2})\in\mathbb{C}^{2},

or

G=\{(s,p)\in\mathbb{C}^{2}\,:\,|s-\bar{s}p|<1-|p|^{2}\}\;\text{ (see \cite[cite]{[\@@bibref{}{AY04}{}{}, Theorem 2.1]})}.

The following simple lemma demonstrates that
G_{\delta}
may be viewed as a slice of
G
.

Lemma 12.1
.

For
\delta\in(0,1)
,

(s,\delta)\in G\iff s\in G_{\delta}.

Proof.

Observe that

\displaystyle(s,\delta)\in G

\displaystyle\Leftrightarrow\;\text{there exist}\;z,w\in\mathbb{D}\;\text{such that}\;s=z+w,\delta=zw

\displaystyle\Leftrightarrow\text{ there exists }z\in\mathbb{C}\text{ such that }\delta<|z|<1\text{ and }s=z+\delta/z

\displaystyle\Leftrightarrow s\in\text{ the image of }R_{\delta}\text{ under the map }z\mapsto z+\delta/z

\displaystyle\Leftrightarrow s\in G_{\delta}\;\text{ (see Lemma \ref{ell.lem.10})}.

∎

Evidently, the lemma implies that if
\Phi\in{\rm Hol}(G)
, then we may define
\varphi\in{\rm Hol}(G_{\delta})
by the formula

\varphi(\mu)=\Phi(\mu,\delta)\quad\text{ for all }\mu\in G_{\delta}.

(12.2)

Lemma
12.1
prompts the question: if
\varphi\in{\rm Hol}(G_{\delta})
, does there exist a function
\Phi\in{\rm Hol}(G)
such that equation (
12.2
) holds? Or, in other words. such that
\varphi
is the restriction of
\Phi
to
G_{\delta}
?
A classical result relevant to this question is Cartan’s Extension Theorem
[
7
]
, which states that if
V
is an analytic variety in a domain of holomorphy
U
and
\varphi
is a holomorphic function on
V
then there is a holomorphic function
\Phi
on
U
such that the restriction of
\Phi
to
V
is
\varphi
.

The symmetrized bidisc
G
is a domain of holomorphy in
\mathbb{C}^{2}
and
G_{\delta}
is a variety in
G
, so
Cartan’s theorem applies to prove that there exists a function
\Phi\in{\rm Hol}(G)
such that
\varphi
is the restriction of
\Phi
to
G_{\delta}
; however, Cartan’s theorem gives no information about bounds on
\Phi
, even when
\varphi
is bounded on
G_{\delta}
. It is therefore natural to pose the following extremal problems:

Problem 12.3
.

Given
\varphi\in\in H^{\infty}_{\mathrm{bfd}}(G_{\delta})
, what is the minimum
m_{\varphi}
of
\|\Phi\|_{H^{\infty}(G)}
over all
\Phi\in H^{\infty}(G)
such that
\varphi
is the restriction of
\Phi
to
G_{\delta}
? For which
\varphi
is
m_{\varphi}
finite?

The next two propositions combine to show that the minimum
m_{\varphi}
is
\|\varphi\|_{\mathrm{bfd}}
, so that
m_{\varphi}
is finite if and only if
\varphi\in{\rm H}^{\infty}_{\mathrm{bfd}}(G_{\delta})
.

Proposition 12.4
.

Let
\delta\in(0,1)
.
If
\Phi\in{\rm H}^{\infty}(G)
and
\varphi
is defined as in equation (
12.2
), then
\varphi\in{\rm H}^{\infty}_{\mathrm{bfd}}(G_{\delta})
and
\|\varphi\|_{\mathrm{bfd}}\leq\|\Phi\|_{H^{\infty}(G)}
.

Proof.

Let
\Psi=\Phi\circ\rho
and define
\psi:R_{\delta}\to\mathbb{C}
by

\psi(\lambda)=\Psi(\lambda,\delta/\lambda)
. Then Theorem 9.56 from
[
2
]
implies that

\psi\in{\rm H}^{\infty}_{\mathrm{dp}}(R_{\delta})
and
\|\psi\|_{\mathrm{dp}}\leq\|\Psi\|_{H^{\infty}(G)}
. But for
\lambda\in R_{\delta}
,

\displaystyle\psi(\lambda)

\displaystyle=\Psi(\lambda,\delta/\lambda)

\displaystyle=\Phi(\lambda+\delta/\lambda,\delta)

\displaystyle=\varphi(\pi(\lambda)),

so that
\psi=\pi^{\sharp}(\varphi)
. Hence, using Theorem
11.25
, we deduce that

\|\varphi\|_{\mathrm{bfd}}=\|\psi\|_{\mathrm{dp}}\leq\|\Psi\|_{H^{\infty}(G)}=\|\Phi\|_{H^{\infty}(G)}.

∎

Proposition 12.5
.

If
\varphi\in{\rm H}^{\infty}_{\mathrm{bfd}}(G_{\delta})
, then there exists
\Phi\in{\rm H}^{\infty}(G)
such that
\|\Phi\|_{\infty}=\|\varphi\|_{\mathrm{bfd}}
and equation (
12.2
) holds.

Proof.

Let

\psi=\pi^{\sharp}(\varphi).

(12.6)

By Theorem
11.25
,
\psi
is a symmetric function in
{\rm H}^{\infty}_{\mathrm{dp}}(R_{\delta})
and
\|\psi\|_{\mathrm{dp}}=\|\varphi\|_{\mathrm{bfd}}
. Hence, by
Theorem 9.56 in
[
2
]
, there exists
\Psi_{0}\in{\rm H}^{\infty}(\mathbb{D}^{2})
such that

\|\Psi_{0}\|_{\infty}=\|\varphi\|_{\mathrm{bfd}}

(12.7)

and

\Psi_{0}(\lambda,\delta/\lambda)=\psi(\lambda),\qquad\lambda\in R_{\delta}.

(12.8)

If we define
\Psi\in{\rm H}^{\infty}(\mathbb{D}^{2})
by

\Psi(z_{1},z_{2})=\frac{\Psi_{0}(z_{1},z_{2})+\Psi_{0}(z_{2},z_{1})}{2},\qquad z\in\mathbb{D}^{2},

then (
12.7
) implies that

\|\Psi\|_{\infty}\leq\|\varphi\|_{\mathrm{bfd}}

(12.9)

and, as
\psi
is symmetric, (
12.8
) implies that

\Psi(\lambda,\delta/\lambda)=\psi(\lambda),\qquad\lambda\in R_{\delta}.

(12.10)

Now, from the definition, we see that
\Psi
a symmetric function on
\mathbb{D}^{2}
. Therefore, by the Waring-Lagrange theorem for
{\rm H}^{\infty}(\mathbb{D}^{2})

[
2
, Theorem 7.1]
, there exists
\Phi\in{\rm H}^{\infty}(G)
satisfying

\|\Phi\|_{\infty}=\|\Psi\|_{\infty}

(12.11)

and

\Phi(z_{1}+z_{2},z_{1}z_{2})=\Psi(z_{1},z_{2}),\qquad z\in\mathbb{D}^{2}.

(12.12)

We claim that
\Phi
satisfies the conditions of the proposition.

We first show that (
12.2
) holds. Letting
\mu=\pi(\lambda)
, we have

\displaystyle\Phi(\mu,\delta)

\displaystyle=\Phi(\lambda+\delta/\lambda,\lambda\ (\delta/\lambda))

\displaystyle=\Psi(\lambda,\delta/\lambda)\qquad\qquad\text{by equation}\;\eqref{sym.70}

\displaystyle=\psi(\lambda)\qquad\qquad\qquad\text{by equation}\;\eqref{sym.50}

\displaystyle=\pi^{\sharp}(\varphi)(\lambda)\qquad\qquad\ \text{by equation}\;\eqref{sym.15}

\displaystyle=\varphi(\mu).

To see that
\|\Phi\|_{\infty}=\|\varphi\|_{\mathrm{bfd}}
observe that the relations (
12.9
) and (
12.11
) imply that
\|\Phi\|_{\infty}\leq\|\varphi\|_{\mathrm{bfd}}
. As equation (
12.2
) holds, the reverse inequality,

\|\Phi\|_{\infty}\geq\|\varphi\|_{\mathrm{bfd}}
, follows from Proposition
12.4
.
∎

Propositions
12.5
and
12.4
combine to yield the following statement.

Theorem 12.13
.

Let
\delta\in(0,1)
. For any
\varphi\in{\rm Hol}(G_{\delta})
, the minimum of
\|\Phi\|_{H^{\infty}(G)}
over all functions
\Phi\in H^{\infty}(G)
that extend the function
(s,\delta)\mapsto\varphi(s)
is
\|\varphi\|_{\mathrm{bfd}}
. In particular, an analytic function
\varphi
on
G_{\delta}
has a bounded extension
\Phi
to
G
if
and only if
\varphi\in H^{\infty}_{\mathrm{bfd}}(G_{\delta})
.

13.
Appendix on the numerical range and dilations

In this appendix we recall some known results on the numerical range
W(T)
of an operator
T
and in particular the relationship between the inclusion of
W(T)
in a prescribed disc and the existence of a dilation of
T
of a particular type.

The notion of a dilation of an operator has proved fruitful in the study of operators.
It is a crucial element of the “harmonic analysis of operators on Hilbert space”, the theory established by B. Sz.-Nagy and C. Foias in
[
14
]
. Consider an operator
T
on a Hilbert space
\mathcal{H}
, and let
\mathcal{H}
be decomposed into an orthogonal direct sum of three subspaces
\mathcal{H}_{1},\mathcal{H}_{2},\mathcal{H}_{3}
. Represent
T
by an operator matrix with respect to this decomposition:

T\sim\begin{bmatrix}T_{11}&T_{12}&T_{13}\\
T_{21}&T_{22}&T_{23}\\
T_{31}&T_{32}&T_{33}\end{bmatrix}.

(13.1)

Here of course
T_{ij}
denotes the operator
P_{i}T|\mathcal{H}_{j}
, where
P_{i}
is the orthogonal projection from
\mathcal{H}
to
\mathcal{H}_{i}
and
\cdot|\mathcal{H}_{j}
denotes restriction to
\mathcal{H}_{j}
. In general the properties of the block entries
T_{ij}
are related in quite subtle ways to the properties of the operator
T
,
but in the special case that the block matrix (
13.1
) is lower triangular, in the sense that the operators
T_{21},T_{31},T_{32}
are all zero (or equivalently, that
\mathcal{H}_{1}
and
\mathcal{H}_{1}\oplus\mathcal{H}_{2}
are invariant subspaces of
\mathcal{H}
for
T
), the relation is more straightforward. Observe in particular that, if
T
has a block upper triangular matrix with respect to the orthogonal decomposition
\mathcal{H}=\mathcal{H}_{1}\oplus\mathcal{H}_{2}\oplus\mathcal{H}_{3}
, say

T\sim\begin{bmatrix}T_{11}&T_{12}&T_{13}\\
0&T_{22}&T_{23}\\
0&0&T_{33}\end{bmatrix},

(13.2)

then, for every positive integer
n
,

T^{n}\sim\begin{bmatrix}T_{11}^{n}&*&*\\
0&T_{22}^{n}&*\\
0&0&T_{33}^{n}\end{bmatrix},

where the stars denote unspecified entries. Thus, in this block triangular case,
(T_{22})^{n}
is the compression of
T^{n}
to
\mathcal{H}_{2}
for all
n\geq 1
:
T_{22}^{n}=P_{2}T^{n}|\mathcal{H}_{2}
. This property brings us to the terminology of
dilations
.

One says that an operator
X\in\mathcal{B}(\mathcal{K})
is a dilation of an operator
T\in\mathcal{B}(\mathcal{H})
if
\mathcal{H}
is a closed subspace of
\mathcal{K}
and, for every positive integer
n
,
T^{n}
is the compression to
\mathcal{H}
of
X^{n}
, or, in other words, if
T^{n}=P_{\mathcal{H}}X^{n}|\mathcal{H}
for all
n\geq 1
.
The preceding paragraph shows that, if
T
has the block upper triangular form (
13.2
) with respect to the orthogonal decomposition
\mathcal{H}=\mathcal{H}_{1}\oplus\mathcal{H}_{2}\oplus\mathcal{H}_{3}
, then
T
is a dilation of
T_{22}
.

According to a lemma of D. Sarason
[
19
, Lemma 0]
, a converse statement also holds:

Lemma 13.3
.

Let
X
be an operator on a Hilbert space
\mathcal{K}
and suppose
X
is a dilation of an operator
T
on a closed subspace
\mathcal{H}
of
\mathcal{K}
. Then there exist closed subspaces
\mathcal{H}_{1}
and
\mathcal{H}_{3}
of
\mathcal{K}
such that
\mathcal{K}
is the orthogonal direct sum of
\mathcal{H}_{1}
,
\mathcal{H}
and
\mathcal{H}_{3}
,
X
has an upper triangular operator matrix with respect to the decomposition
\mathcal{K}=\mathcal{H}_{1}\oplus\mathcal{H}\oplus\mathcal{H}_{3}
, and
T
is the
(2,2)
block in the operator matrix of
X
.

In the above statement of the lemma, the triangularity assertion about
X
is equivalent to
both
\mathcal{H}_{1}
and
\mathcal{H}_{1}\oplus\mathcal{H}
being invariant subspaces of
\mathcal{K}
for
X
.
Here is a proof (which Sarason attributes to C. Foias) of Lemma
13.3
.

Proof.

Let
\mathcal{M}
be the smallest closed
X
-invariant subspace of
\mathcal{K}
containing
\mathcal{H}
and let
P
and
Q
be the orthogonal projections from
\mathcal{K}
onto
\mathcal{H}
and
\mathcal{M}
respectively. Let
\mathcal{H}_{1}=\mathcal{M}\ominus\mathcal{H}
, so that
Q-P
is the orthogonal projection onto
\mathcal{H}_{1}
. We assert that
\mathcal{H}_{1}
is
X
-invariant, or equivalently that

(Q-P)X(Q-P)=X(Q-P).

Since clearly
QXQ=XQ
and
QXP=XP
, it is enough to show that
PXP=PXQ
. Now for
y\in\mathcal{H}
and for
m,n\geq 1
, the dilation condition implies that

PX^{n}PX^{m}y=PX^{n}T^{m}y=T^{n}T^{m}y=T^{n+m}y=PX^{n+m}y.

But
\mathcal{M}
is the closed span of the vectors
X^{m}y
with
y\in\mathcal{H}
and
m\geq 1
. We may conclude that
PX^{n}Px=PX^{n}x
for all
x\in\mathcal{M}
, and so
PXP=PXQ
, as required. Thus
\mathcal{H}_{1}
and
\mathcal{M}
are invariant subspaces for
X
. Choice of
\mathcal{H}_{3}=\mathcal{M}^{\perp}
completes the orthogonal decomposition
\mathcal{K}=\mathcal{H}_{1}\oplus\mathcal{H}\oplus\mathcal{H}_{3}
with respect to which
X
has an upper triangular operator matrix, with
T
in the
(2,2)
-position.
∎

In the theory of operators on Hilbert space the most important result concerning dilations is the
Nagy dilation theorem
, which asserts that any contraction
T
on a Hilbert space
\mathcal{H}
has a dilation
U
on some Hilbert space
\mathcal{K}\supseteq\mathcal{H}
such that
U
is a unitary operator on
\mathcal{K}
.
There are many proofs of this result (see
[
14
, page 52]
). One of the shortest proofs is to deduce it from the following generalization of the Herglotz Representation Theorem due to Naimark
[
17
,
16
]
.

Theorem 13.4
.

Let
\mathcal{H}
be a Hilbert space and assume that
V
is an analytic
\mathcal{B}(\mathcal{H})
-valued function defined on
\mathbb{D}
satisfying
\re V(z)\geq 0
for all
z\in\mathbb{D}
. Then there exist a Hilbert space
\mathcal{K}
, an isometry
I:\mathcal{H}\to\mathcal{K}
, and a unitary operator
U\in\mathcal{B}(\mathcal{K})
such that

V(z)=I^{*}\frac{1+zU}{1-zU}I

for all
z\in\mathbb{D}
.

Indeed, if
T
is a contraction then, for any
z\in\mathbb{D}
,

\re\frac{1+zT}{1-zT}=2(1-zT)^{-1}(1-|z|^{2}TT^{*})(1-\bar{z}T^{*})^{-1}>0.

It follows from Naimark’s theorem that there exist a Hilbert space
\mathcal{K}
, an isometry
I:\mathcal{H}\to\mathcal{K}
, and a unitary operator
U\in\mathcal{B}(\mathcal{K})
such that

\frac{1+zT}{1-zT}=I^{*}\frac{1+zU}{1-zU}I\quad\text{ for all }z\in\mathbb{D}.

Expanding both sides of the equation as power series in
z
, we have

1+2zT+2z^{2}T^{2}+2z^{3}T^{3}+\dots=I^{*}(1+2zU+2z^{2}U^{2}+2z^{3}U^{3}+\dots)I\quad\text{ for all }z\in\mathbb{D},

and on equating coefficients of
z^{n}
we deduce that
T^{n}=I^{*}U^{n}I
for all positive integers
n
, so that
U
is a dilation of
T
. The lemma of Sarason proved above then gives us a geometric interpretation of the relation between
U
and
T
:
T
is the compression of
U
to a semi-invariant subspace of
U
, which is to say,
T
is the compression of
U
to the orthogonal complement of of one invariant subspace of
U
in another. In the notation of the above proof of Sarason’s Lemma,
T
is the compression of
U
to
\mathcal{M}\ominus\mathcal{H}_{1}
, while both
\mathcal{M}
and
\mathcal{H}_{1}
are invariant subspaces for
U
, which makes
\mathcal{H}=\mathcal{M}\ominus\mathcal{H}_{1}
a semi-invariant subspace of
U
.
