On smooth and peaked traveling waves in a local model for shallow water waves
Spencer Locke, Dmitry E. Pelinovsky

Abstract
. We introduce a new model equation for Stokes gravity waves based on conformal transformations of Euler’s equations. The local version of the model equation is relevant for dynamics of shallow water waves. It allows us to characterize the traveling periodic waves both in the case of smooth and peaked waves and to solve the existence problem exactly, albeit not in elementary functions. Spectral stability of smooth waves with respect to co-periodic perturbations is proven analytically based on the exact count of eigenvalues in a constrained spectral problem.

1.
Introduction

An irrotational motion of incompressible two-dimensional surface water waves can be fully described by means of evolution equations for two canonical variables in one spatial coordinate. This formalism was originated by V. Zakharov
[
48
]
in the context of stability of traveling periodic waves.

One approach to develop this formalism systematically is based on the Dirichlet-to-Neumann (D-N) operator
[
9
]
. The two nonlinear evolution equations closed with the D-N operator have been studied in many works on water waves, including the recent study of modulational instability of traveling periodic waves
[
6
]
. See also
[
10
,
26
,
36
]
for other recent works where three more methods have been explored in the same context.

Another approach to obtain a closed system of two nonlinear evolution equations for water waves is based on a conformal transformation which maps the fluid domain with a variable surface profile to a fixed rectangular domain. This formalism was introduced
in
[
4
,
41
]
and has been explored in the context of traveling periodic waves in
[
11
,
49
,
8
]
and more recently in
[
12
,
13
,
14
,
27
,
33
]
. Our work contributes to the analysis of the nonlinear evolution equations obtained in the latter approach.

The approach based on conformal transformations has been used to tackle many mathematical problems related to water waves such as the existence of standing waves
[
43
,
44
]
and bifurcations of quasi-periodic wave solutions from the standing periodic waves
[
45
,
46
,
47
]
. Holomorphic coordinates were used for analysis of well-posedness of the water wave equations
[
21
,
23
]
. The particular problem addressed in our work
is the coexistence of smooth and peaked traveling periodic waves
for different intervals of wave speeds as well as the linear stability of waves with smooth profiles.

1.1.
A new model equation

The purpose of this paper is to introduce a new model equation which shares the same solutions as the traveling wave reduction of Euler’s equations in
[
4
]
but simplifies the time evolution and, particularly, the linear stability analysis
of the traveling periodic waves. This model equation can be written in the following nonlocal form,

2cT_{h}^{-1}\partial_{t}\eta=(c^{2}K_{h}-1)\eta-\eta K_{h}\eta-\frac{1}{2}K_{h}\eta^{2},

(1.1)

where
\eta=\eta(u,t)\in\mathbb{R}
is the surface elevation in the reference frame moving with the constant wave speed
c>0
,
t\in\mathbb{R}
is time, and
u
is the spatial coordinate defined on the periodic domain
\mathbb{T}=\mathbb{R}\backslash(2\pi\mathbb{Z})
. The spatial coordinate
u
arises after the conformal transformation of the fluid domain with variable surface elevation
\eta
to a rectangle
[-h,0]\times[-\pi,\pi]
, where
h>0
is the fluid depth.
The linear skew-adjoint operator
T_{h}^{-1}
in
L^{2}(\mathbb{T})
is defined by the Fourier symbol

\widehat{\left(T_{h}^{-1}\right)}_{n}=\left\{\begin{array}[]{cl}-i\coth(hn),&\quad n\in\mathbb{Z}\backslash\{0\},\\
0,&\quad n=0,\end{array}\right.

(1.2)

whereas the linear, self-adjoint, positive
operator
K_{h}=T_{h}^{-1}\partial_{u}
in
L^{2}(\mathbb{T})
is defined by the Fourier symbol

\widehat{\left(K_{h}\right)}_{n}=\left\{\begin{array}[]{cl}n\coth(hn),&\quad n\in\mathbb{Z}\backslash\{0\},\\
0,&\quad n=0.\end{array}\right.

(1.3)

Appendix
A
explains how the nonlocal evolution equation (
1.1
) arises in the context of the original Euler’s equations.

Let us obtain the conserved quantities for the nonlocal model (
1.1
).
Taking the mean value of (
1.1
), we get the constraint

\oint\eta(1+K_{h}\eta)du=0,

(1.4)

which represents the zero-mean constraint for the surface elevation
\eta
in
the physical spatial coordinate. Furthermore, differentiating (
1.1
) in
u
, multiplying by
\eta
and integrating over the period of
\mathbb{T}
yields

\displaystyle c\frac{d}{dt}\oint\eta K_{h}\eta du

\displaystyle=\oint(c^{2}\eta K_{h}\eta_{u}-\eta\eta_{u}-\eta\eta_{u}K_{h}\eta-\eta^{2}K_{h}\eta_{u}-\eta K_{h}\eta\eta_{u})du

\displaystyle=\oint\partial_{u}\left(\frac{1}{2}c^{2}\eta K_{h}\eta-\frac{1}{2}\eta^{2}-\eta^{2}K_{h}\eta\right)du=0,

(1.5)

where we have used self-adjointness of
K_{h}
in
L^{2}(\mathbb{T})
for every solution with
\eta,\eta_{u},\eta\eta_{u}
in the domain of
K_{h}
. It follows from (
1.4
) and (
1.5
) that the nonlocal evolution equation (
1.1
) admits two conserved quantities

\oint\eta du\qquad\mbox{\rm and}\qquad\oint\eta K_{h}\eta du.

(1.6)

In addition, the evolution equation (
1.1
) can be written in the
Hamiltonian form

2cT_{h}^{-1}\partial_{t}\eta=\Lambda^{\prime}_{c}(\eta),\quad{\rm with}\;\;\Lambda_{c}(\eta):=\frac{1}{2}\oint\left[c^{2}\eta K_{h}\eta-\eta^{2}(1+K_{h}\eta)\right]du,

(1.7)

where
\Lambda_{c}(\eta)
is the action related to the conserved energy of the fluid. Critical points of
\Lambda_{c}
in the corresponding energy space satisfy the Euler–Lagrange equation

(c^{2}K_{h}-1)\eta=\frac{1}{2}K_{h}\eta^{2}+\eta K_{h}\eta,

(1.8)

which is known as Babenko’s equation because it
coincides with the traveling wave reduction of Euler’s equations after the conformal transformation
[
4
]
. In the context
of the evolution equation (
1.1
) with
u
defined
in the reference frame moving with the wave speed
c
, solutions of (
1.8
) correspond to the time-independent solutions of (
1.1
).

1.2.
Local model and main results

In the deep water limit (
h\to\infty
), we have from (
1.2
) and (
1.3
) that

\lim_{h\to\infty}T_{h}^{-1}=-\mathcal{H}\qquad{\rm and}\qquad\lim_{h\to\infty}K_{h}=-\mathcal{H}\partial_{u},

where
\mathcal{H}
is the periodic Hilbert transform defined by the Fourier symbol

\widehat{\mathcal{H}}_{n}=i\;{\rm sgn}(n),\qquad n\in\mathbb{Z}.

(1.9)

This work explores the shallow water limit (
h\to 0
), when
we replace
T_{h}^{-1}
and
K_{h}
by
-\partial_{u}
and
-\partial_{u}^{2}
respectively. In other words, we study herein the local evolution equation

2c\partial_{u}\partial_{t}\eta=(c^{2}-2\eta)\partial^{2}_{u}\eta-(\partial_{u}\eta)^{2}+\eta.

(1.10)

Appendix
B
describes how the local model (
1.10
) arises
from
T_{h}^{-1}
and
K_{h}
as
h\to 0
and compares it with other phenomenological models for fluid dynamics.

It is important to emphasize that (
1.10
) is not
the asymptotic reduction of (
1.1
) as
h\to 0
but rather
a toy model to understand existence and linear stability of traveling
periodic waves in the shallow water limit.

The local equation (
1.10
) without the last term was derived in
[
24
]
in a different (geometric) context and has been referred to as the Hunter–Saxton equation
[
25
]
. The same equation (
1.10
) with the last term was also discussed in
[
1
,
2
]
in connection to the high-frequency limit of the Camassa–Holm equation, one of the toy models for physics of fluids with smooth and peaked waves. Integrability of
(
1.10
) was established in
[
22
]
together with other peaked wave equations such as the reduced Ostrovsky and short-pulse equations.
Some traveling wave solutions of this and similar equations were studied with
Hirota’s bilinear method in
[
35
]
.

Next we discuss the time evolution and the conserved quantities for the local model (
1.10
). Taking the mean value of (
1.10
) for smooth
2\pi
-periodic solutions and integrating by parts yields the constraint

\oint\left[\eta+(\partial_{u}\eta)^{2}\right]du=0,

(1.11)

which corresponds to (
1.4
) also after integration by parts.
Let
\Pi_{0}:L^{2}(\mathbb{T})\rightarrow L^{2}(\mathbb{T})|_{\{1\}^{T}}
be a projection operator to the periodic functions with zero mean. The evolution equation (
1.10
) can be written in the form

2c\partial_{t}\eta=(c^{2}-2\eta)\partial_{u}\eta+\Pi_{0}\partial_{u}^{-1}\Pi_{0}\left[(\partial_{u}\eta)^{2}+\eta\right],

(1.12)

where
\Pi_{0}\partial_{u}^{-1}\Pi_{0}
is uniquely defined on the zero-mean functions in
L^{2}(\mathbb{T})
with the zero-mean constraint. The evolution equation (
1.12
) is a nonlocal version of the inviscid Burgers equation. The initial-value problem for the inviscid Burgers equation is locally well-posed in
H^{1}_{\rm per}(\mathbb{T})\cap W^{1,\infty}(\mathbb{T})
.
Since the mapping

\Pi_{0}\partial_{u}^{-1}\Pi_{0}\left[(\partial_{u}\eta)^{2}+\eta\right]:H^{1}_{\rm per}(\mathbb{T})\cap W^{1,\infty}(\mathbb{T})\to H^{1}_{\rm per}(\mathbb{T})\cap W^{1,\infty}(\mathbb{T})

is bounded on every bounded subset, there exists a unique local solution
of the evolution equation (
1.12
) for every initial data
in
H^{1}_{\rm per}(\mathbb{T})\cap W^{1,\infty}(\mathbb{T})
.

To get the conserved quantities, we multiply (
1.10
) by
\partial_{u}\eta
and integrate over the period for smooth
2\pi
-periodic solutions
\eta
. This implies the conservation of

Q(\eta):=\oint(\partial_{u}\eta)^{2}du,

(1.13)

and in view of the constraint (
1.11
), the conservation of

M(\eta):=\oint\eta du.

(1.14)

The conserved quantities (
1.13
) and (
1.14
) correspond to (
1.6
). Furthermore, similar to (
1.7
), we can write (
1.12
) in the Hamiltonian form

2c\partial_{t}\eta=-\Pi_{0}\partial_{u}^{-1}\Pi_{0}\left[\frac{1}{2}c^{2}Q^{\prime}(\eta)-\frac{1}{2}H^{\prime}(\eta)\right],

(1.15)

where
H
is the third conserved quantity given by

H(\eta):=\oint\left[\eta^{2}+2\eta(\partial_{u}\eta)^{2}\right]du.

(1.16)

The existence of traveling periodic waves in the local model (
1.10
) is defined by the second-order equation

(c^{2}-2\eta)\eta^{\prime\prime}-(\eta^{\prime})^{2}+\eta=0,\qquad u\in\mathbb{T},

(1.17)

where
\eta=\eta(u)
is the
2\pi
-periodic wave profile satisfying the constraint (
1.11
).
The linear stability of the traveling wave with the profile
\eta

is defined by the linearized equation

2c\partial_{t}\hat{\eta}=-\Pi_{0}\partial_{u}^{-1}\Pi_{0}\mathcal{L}\hat{\eta},\qquad\mathcal{L}:=-\partial_{u}(c^{2}-2\eta)\partial_{u}-1+2\eta^{\prime\prime},

(1.18)

where
\hat{\eta}=\hat{\eta}(u,t)
is the perturbation to the traveling wave with the profile
\eta=\eta(u)
satisfying the orthogonality condition

\langle 1-2\eta^{\prime\prime},\hat{\eta}\rangle=0
with the standard inner product in
L^{2}(\mathbb{T})
. The orthogonality condition
\langle 1-2\eta^{\prime\prime},\hat{\eta}\rangle=0
follows by expanding the nonlinear constraint (
1.11
) near
\eta
.

The following two theorems describe the main results of this work. Our results are formulated for the single-lobe periodic solutions with an even profile
\eta
which possesses a single maximum on
\mathbb{T}
placed at
u=0
. Such single-lobe periodic solutions are often referred to as Stokes waves.

Theorem 1
.

There exist
c_{*}:=\frac{\pi}{2\sqrt{2}}
and
c_{\infty}\in(c_{*},\infty)
such that the stationary equation (
1.17
) admits a unique single-lobe solution with the profile
\eta\in C^{\infty}_{\rm per}(\mathbb{T})
for every
c\in(1,c_{*})
such that

\|\eta\|_{L^{\infty}}\to 0\quad\mbox{\rm as}\;\;c\to 1

(1.19)

and a single-lobe solution with the profile
\eta\in C^{0}_{\rm per}(\mathbb{T})
for every
c\in(c_{*},c_{\infty})
satisfying

\eta(u)=\frac{c^{2}}{2}-A(c)|u|^{2/3}+\mathcal{O}(|u|^{4/3})\quad\mbox{\rm as}\;\;u\to 0,

(1.20)

for some constant
A(c)>0
. At
c=c_{*}
, there exists a unique single-lobe solution with the profile
\eta\in C^{0}_{\rm per}(\mathbb{T})\cap W^{1,\infty}(\mathbb{T})
given explicitly as

\eta(u)=\frac{1}{16}(\pi^{2}-4\pi|u|+2u^{2}),\qquad u\in[-\pi,\pi]

(1.21)

and extended as a
2\pi
-periodic function on
\mathbb{T}
.

.

The special solution (

) has a peaked profile with a finite jump

Remark 4
.

The singular behavior (

) corresponds to the singularity of the limiting Stokes wave with a

[

\max_{u\in\mathbb{T}}\eta(u)=\eta(0)=\frac{c^{2}}{2}

Theorem 2
.

Consider the unique single-lobe solution with the profile
\eta\in C^{\infty}_{\rm per}(\mathbb{T})
in Theorem
1
for
c\in(1,c_{*})
.
For every initial data
\hat{\eta}_{0}\in H^{1}_{\rm per}(\mathbb{T})
satisfying

\langle 1,\hat{\eta}_{0}\rangle=0\quad\mbox{\rm and}\quad\langle\eta^{\prime\prime},\hat{\eta}_{0}\rangle=0,

(1.22)

there exists a unique solution
\hat{\eta}\in C^{0}(\mathbb{R},H^{1}_{\rm per}(\mathbb{T}))
of the linearized equation (
1.18
)
with
\hat{\eta}|_{t=0}=\hat{\eta}_{0}
and a unique
a\in C^{1}(\mathbb{R},\mathbb{R})
such that

\|\hat{\eta}(\cdot,t)-a(t)\eta^{\prime}\|_{H^{1}_{\rm per}}\leq C\|\hat{\eta}_{0}\|_{H^{1}_{\rm per}},\quad|a^{\prime}(t)|\leq C\|\hat{\eta}_{0}\|_{H^{1}_{\rm per}},\quad t\in\mathbb{R},

(1.23)

where
C>0
is independent of
\hat{\eta}_{0}
.

Remark 5
.

Constraints (
1.22
) are preserved in the time
evolution of the linearized equation (
1.18
) because they are linearizations of the conserved quantities (
1.13
) and (
1.14
).
In view of the constraint
\langle 1-2\eta^{\prime\prime},\hat{\eta}\rangle=0
imposed on solutions of the linearized equation (
1.18
), only one constraint
in (
1.22
) is linearly independent. Imposing

\langle\eta^{\prime\prime},\hat{\eta}_{0}\rangle=0
is equivalent to the requirement
that the perturbation
\hat{\eta}
does not change the conserved quantity
Q
in (
1.13
) up to the linear approximation. The bound (
1.23
) expresses the concept of linear orbital stability of the orbit
\{\eta(\cdot+\mathfrak{u})\}_{\mathfrak{u}\in\mathbb{T}}
of the traveling periodic wave with the profile
\eta
.

Remark 6
.

The linear orbital stability of Theorem
2
implies spectral stability
of the traveling periodic wave in the sense that the spectrum of the associated linearized operator
\partial_{u}^{-1}\mathcal{L}
in
L^{2}(\mathbb{T})
belongs to
i\mathbb{R}
. It is also worthwhile to point out that an eigenfunction
\hat{\eta}_{0}
of the spectral stability problem

\partial_{u}^{-1}\mathcal{L}\hat{\eta}_{0}=\lambda_{0}\hat{\eta}_{0},\qquad\eta_{0}\in H^{1}_{\rm per}(\mathbb{T})

(1.24)

for every nonzero eigenvalue
\lambda_{0}\in\mathbb{C}\backslash\{0\}
must satisfy the two constraints in (
1.22
). The spectral stability problem in the form (
1.24
) was also considered in
[
40
]
.

Remark 7
.

The proof of Theorem
2
relies on the construction
of the coercive quadratic form
\langle\mathcal{L}\hat{\eta},\hat{\eta}\rangle
for
\hat{\eta}\in H^{1}_{\rm per}(\mathbb{T})
under the three constraints:

\langle 1,\hat{\eta}\rangle=\langle\eta^{\prime},\hat{\eta}\rangle=\langle\eta^{\prime\prime},\hat{\eta}\rangle=0.

The quadratic form is invariant in the time evolution of the linearized equation (
1.18
). This yields the energetic stability of the traveling periodic wave, ensuring that the periodic wave with the profile
\eta
is a local minimizer of the energy
H
subject to fixed
Q
and
M
in
H^{1}_{\rm per}(\mathbb{T})
. If local well-posedness of the nonlinear evolution equation (
1.12
) can be shown in
H^{1}_{\rm per}(\mathbb{T})
, then the energetic stability implies the nonlinear orbital stability of the traveling periodic wave. However, the local well-posedness of (
1.12
) holds only in
H^{1}_{\rm per}(\mathbb{T})\cap W^{1,\infty}(\mathbb{T})
and the control of
\|\partial_{u}\hat{\eta}\|_{L^{\infty}}
for the perturbation
\hat{\eta}
does not follow from the conserved quantities (
1.13
), (
1.14
), and (
1.16
).

1.3.
Discussion

The local model (
1.10
) shows a pattern of the existence and stability of traveling periodic waves parameterized by the wave speed
c
. There is a continuum of wave speeds for the smooth waves with profile
\eta\in C^{\infty}(\mathbb{T})
bifurcating from the linear limit in (
1.19
) and a continuum of wave speeds for the cusped waves with the profile
\eta\in C^{0}(\mathbb{T})
satisfying (
1.20
). The two continuous families are connected together at a particular value of the wave speed
c=c_{*}
for which the wave is peaked with the profile
\eta\in C^{0}(\mathbb{T})\cap W^{1,\infty}(\mathbb{T})
. The same phenomenon is observed in the Camassa–Holm equation
[
15
,
29
]
and the Degasperis–Procesi equation
[
19
,
28
]
.

It is rather remarkable that exactly the same
|u|^{2/3}
singularity in the limiting wave profile with

\max_{u\in\mathbb{T}}\eta(u)=\eta(0)=\frac{c^{2}}{2}

is recovered by the local model (
1.10
) as predicted by the full model for any depth
h

[
37
]
. After the conformal transformation, this singularity yields the limiting Stokes wave with the
120^{o}
angle in the physical coordinates. More precise details of such singular profiles are beyond the current capacities of the asymptotic
[
32
]
or numerical
[
12
,
33
]
methods. The local model (
1.10
) gives precise conclusions that the
|u|^{2/3}
singularity is obtained in a range of wave speeds
c
and that the borderline wave profile
\eta\in H^{1}_{\rm per}(\mathbb{T})\cap W^{1,\infty}(\mathbb{T})
has a peaked profile at
c=c_{*}
before getting the
|u|^{2/3}
singularity for
c>c_{*}
. This might be an artefact of the local model (
1.10
) since the nonlocal models typically predict only
|u|^{2/3}
singularity in the fluid models, see
[
30
]
.

Stability of the traveling periodic waves with singular profiles is a complicated problem, which is out of reach in the current analytical and numerical methods in the nonlocal models
[
13
,
27
]
. The local model (
1.10
) is a promising candidate for showing linear instability of the peaked wave (based on a similar analysis in
[
17
,
18
]
and
[
34
]
) and for attacking linear instability of the cusped wave with the
|u|^{2/3}
singularity.

The remainder of this paper is organized as follows. Section
2
contains the proof of Theorem
1
on the existence of smooth and peaked periodic waves. Section
3
gives the proof of Theorem
2
on linear stability of the smooth periodic waves. Appendix
A
reviews the Euler’s equations after the conformal transformation and discusses how the nonlocal model equation (

) arises. Appendix

describes the local model (
1.10
) in the context of other phenomenological models for dynamics of fluid surfaces.

2.
Existence of smooth and peaked traveling periodic waves

Here we consider the single-lobe periodic solutions of the second-order equation (
1.17
). Recall that a single-lobe periodic solution has the even profile
\eta
with a single maximum on
\mathbb{T}
placed at
u=0
. Theorem
1
is proven by using the period function for the planar Hamiltonian systems used in a similar context in
[
15
,
16
,
19
,
31
]
.

We start with the first-order invariant of the second-order equation (
1.17
) given by the following lemma.

Lemma 1
.

For every solution
\eta\in C^{2}(a,b)
of the second-order equation
(
1.17
) with
-\infty\leq a<b\leq\infty
, the following function

E(\eta,\eta^{\prime}):=\frac{1}{2}(c^{2}-2\eta)(\eta^{\prime})^{2}+\frac{1}{2}\eta^{2}

(2.1)

is constant for
u\in(a,b)
.

Proof.

It is based on the elementary computation:

\displaystyle\frac{d}{du}E(\eta,\eta^{\prime})

\displaystyle=(c^{2}-2\eta)\eta^{\prime}\eta^{\prime\prime}-(\eta^{\prime})^{3}+\eta\eta^{\prime}

\displaystyle=\eta^{\prime}\left[(c^{2}-2\eta)\eta^{\prime\prime}-(\eta^{\prime})^{2}+\eta\right]

\displaystyle=0,

since
\eta\in C^{2}(a,b)
satisfies (
1.17
).
∎

The next lemma explores the phase portrait for the second-order equation (
1.17
) on the phase plane
(\eta,\eta^{\prime})
obtained from the level curves of
E(\eta,\eta^{\prime})
in (
2.1
). We obtain the existence of smooth and singular solutions in terms of the level
\mathcal{E}
of
E(\eta,\eta^{\prime})
.

Lemma 2
.

For every
c>0
, there exists
\mathcal{E}_{c}:=\frac{c^{4}}{8}
such that every periodic solution to (
1.17
) with profile
\eta\in C^{\infty}(\mathbb{R})
belongs to
E(\eta,\eta^{\prime})=\mathcal{E}
with
\mathcal{E}\in(0,\mathcal{E}_{c})
. For
E(\eta,\eta^{\prime})=\mathcal{E}_{c}
, the only solution to (
1.17
) is the parabola

\eta(u)=-\frac{c^{2}}{2}+\frac{(u-u_{0})^{2}}{8},

(2.2)

with arbitrary
u_{0}\in\mathbb{R}
. For
E(\eta,\eta^{\prime})=\mathcal{E}
with
\mathcal{E}\in(\mathcal{E}_{c},\infty)
, there exist no bounded solutions to (
1.17
) with profile
\eta\in C^{\infty}(\mathbb{R})
.

Proof.

The only equilibrium point of (
1.17
) is the center
(\eta,\eta^{\prime})=(0,0)
, which corresponds to the minimum of
E(\eta,\eta^{\prime})
if
c>0
. Every periodic solution to (
1.17
) belongs to the period annulus, which is the largest punctured neighbourhood of the center
(0,0)
consisting entirely of periodic orbits.

The phase portrait from the level curves of
E(\eta,\eta^{\prime})=\mathcal{E}
is shown on Figure
3
. Each level curve defines the profile
\eta
from integration of

\left(\frac{d\eta}{du}\right)^{2}=\frac{2\mathcal{E}-\eta^{2}}{c^{2}-2\eta}.

(2.3)

The vertical line corresponds to
\eta=\frac{c^{2}}{2}
and divides the phase plane into two half planes. For
\eta>\frac{c^{2}}{2}
, the level curves of

Lemma 3

Let
\eta\in C^{0}(u_{-},u_{+})
be a solution for the level curve
E(\eta,\eta^{\prime})=\mathcal{E}
for
\mathcal{E}\in(\mathcal{E}_{c},\infty)
such that

\eta(u)\to\pm\frac{c^{2}}{2}

-\infty<u_{-}<u_{+}<\infty
and the solution satisfies

\eta(u)=\frac{c^{2}}{2}-\frac{3^{2/3}}{2^{2/3}}(\mathcal{E}-\mathcal{E}_{c})^{1/3}|u-u_{\pm}|^{2/3}+\mathcal{O}(|u-u_{\pm}|^{4/3})\qquad\mbox{\rm as}\;\;u\to u_{\pm}.

(2.5)

Proof.

We consider the level curve
E(\eta,\eta^{\prime})=\mathcal{E}
with
\mathcal{E}\in(\mathcal{E}_{c},\infty)
for
\eta<\frac{c^{2}}{2}
. Then, we have

\displaystyle\left(\frac{d\eta}{du}\right)^{2}

\displaystyle=\frac{2\mathcal{E}-\eta^{2}}{c^{2}-2\eta}

\displaystyle=\frac{2(\mathcal{E}-\mathcal{E}_{c})}{c^{2}-2\eta}+\frac{1}{2}c^{2}+\frac{1}{4}(2\eta-c^{2})

\displaystyle=\frac{2(\mathcal{E}-\mathcal{E}_{c})}{c^{2}-2\eta}+\mathcal{O}(1)\quad\mbox{\rm as}\;\;\eta\to\frac{c^{2}}{2}.

This yields

\sqrt{c^{2}-2\eta}\left[1+\mathcal{O}(|c^{2}-2\eta|)\right]\frac{d\eta}{du}=\pm\sqrt{2(\mathcal{E}-\mathcal{E}_{c})}\quad\mbox{\rm as}\;\;u\to u_{\pm}\mp 0.

Integrating in
u
yields

\sqrt{(c^{2}-2\eta)^{3}}\left[1+\mathcal{O}(|c^{2}-2\eta|)\right]=\mp 3\sqrt{2(\mathcal{E}-\mathcal{E}_{c})}(u-u_{\pm})\quad\mbox{\rm as}\;\;u\to u_{\pm}\mp 0,

which results in the expansion (
2.5
).
It remains to prove that
[u_{-},u_{+}]
is compact. This follows from the bounds

\displaystyle u_{+}-u_{-}

\displaystyle=2\int_{-\sqrt{2\mathcal{E}}}^{c^{2}/2}\frac{\sqrt{c^{2}-2\eta}}{\sqrt{2\mathcal{E}-\eta^{2}}}d\eta

\displaystyle\leq 2\sqrt{c^{2}+2\sqrt{2\mathcal{E}}}\int_{-\sqrt{2\mathcal{E}}}^{c^{2}/2}\frac{d\eta}{\sqrt{2\mathcal{E}-\eta^{2}}}<\infty,

since the integral in the upper bound is finite.
∎

Lemma 4

For every

\displaystyle T(\mathcal{E},c)

In addition, we have

Proof.

For the smooth periodic solutions, we use the change of variables

We start with the count of the negative eigenvalues of

Lemma 5
.

Let
\eta\in C^{\infty}_{\rm per}(\mathbb{T})
be the profile of the single-lobe solution in Theorem

Proof.

Since

\eta^{\prime\prime}\in L^{\infty}(\mathbb{T})

For fixed

1.17

u

\mathcal{L}\eta^{\prime}=0

\eta^{\prime}\in H^{2}_{\rm per}(\mathbb{T})

\mathcal{L}
admits a zero eigenvalue with the spatially odd eigenfunction
\eta^{\prime}
.
To consider the second linearly independent solution of
\mathcal{L}\hat{\eta}=0
, we define the family
\{\eta(u;\mathcal{E})\}_{\mathcal{E}\in(0,\mathcal{E}_{c})}
of spatially even, smooth periodic solutions of the second-order equation (

\eta(0;\mathcal{E})=\sqrt{2\mathcal{E}},\quad\partial_{u}\eta(0;\mathcal{E})=0

\eta(T(\mathcal{E},c);\mathcal{E})=\sqrt{2\mathcal{E}},\quad\partial_{u}\eta(T(\mathcal{E},c);\mathcal{E})=0,

where

is the period function (
2.6
) satisfying (
2.8
). Let
\mathcal{E}(c)
be the root of the period function
T(\mathcal{E},c)=2\pi
in the proof of Theorem

such that
\eta(u)=\eta(u;\mathcal{E}(c))\in C^{\infty}_{\rm per}(\mathbb{T})

1.17
) in
\mathcal{E}
along the family

\{\eta(u;\mathcal{E})\}_{\mathcal{E}\in(0,\mathcal{E}_{c})}

Remark 8
.

One can prove the assertion of lemma
5
by using small-amplitude expansions. The periodic solution with the profile
\eta\in C^{\infty}_{\rm per}(\mathbb{T})
is expanded near the trivial solution, see (

\eta=a\cos(u)-a^{2}\sin^{2}(u)+\mathcal{O}(a^{3}),\quad c^{2}=1+\frac{1}{2}a^{2}+\mathcal{O}(a^{4}),

\mathcal{L}
along the solution family has one negative eigenvalue
-1+\mathcal{O}(a^{2})

with

being the third eigenvalue. Since
{\rm Ker}(\mathcal{L})={\rm span}(\eta^{\prime})
along the family of smooth periodic solutions for

, the inertia index of

remains the same for

.

The next lemma specifies the criterion for the constrained linear operator

\mathcal{L}|_{\mathcal{X}_{c}}
to be positive, where
\mathcal{L}|_{\mathcal{X}_{c}}=\mathcal{L}|_{\{1,\eta^{\prime\prime}\}^{\perp}}
is defined by the two constraints in (

Lemma 6
.

Let
\mathcal{L}:H^{2}_{\rm per}(\mathbb{T})\subset L^{2}(\mathbb{T})\to L^{2}(\mathbb{T})
be given by (
3.1
) as in Lemma
5
and
\mathcal{X}_{c}
be the constrained subspace of
L^{2}(\mathbb{T})
given by (

\mathcal{L}|_{\mathcal{X}_{c}}
has a simple zero eigenvalue and no negative eigenvalues, with the rest of its spectrum being bounded away from zero, if and only if the mapping

is monotonically decreasing for
c\in(1,c_{*})

Proof.

By Proposition 2 in
[
15
]
, we construct the
2

2

\mathcal{X}_{c}

A:=\left[\begin{matrix}\langle\mathcal{L}^{-1}1,1\rangle&\langle\mathcal{L}^{-1}1,\eta^{\prime\prime}\rangle\\
\langle\mathcal{L}^{-1}\eta^{\prime\prime},1\rangle&\langle\mathcal{L}^{-1}\eta^{\prime\prime},\eta^{\prime\prime}\rangle\end{matrix}\right].

The inverse operator
\mathcal{L}^{-1}
on
{\rm span}(1,\eta^{\prime\prime})
is well-defined since

{\rm Ker}(\mathcal{L})={\rm span}(\eta^{\prime})\perp{\rm span}(1,\eta^{\prime\prime}).

Differentiating (
1.17
) in

yields

\mathcal{L}\partial_{c}\eta=2c\eta^{\prime\prime},

(3.4)

where
\partial_{c}\eta
is defined along the family of
2\pi
-periodic solutions
\{\eta\}_{c\in(1,c_{*})}
. The family is smooth in
c
since

T(\mathcal{E},c)
is
C^{1}
on
(0,\mathcal{E}_{c})\times(0,c_{*})

and
\mathcal{E}(c)
is
C^{1}
on
(0,c_{*})
due to the implicit function theorem for

with
\partial_{\mathcal{E}}T(\mathcal{E}(c),c)<0
. In addition, we have
\mathcal{L}1=2\eta^{\prime\prime}-1

\mathcal{L}\left(c^{-1}\partial_{c}\eta-1\right)=1.

(3.5)

By using (
3.4
) and (
3.5

A=\left[\begin{matrix}c^{-1}\langle\partial_{c}\eta,1\rangle-2\pi&c^{-1}\langle\partial_{c}\eta,\eta^{\prime\prime}\rangle\\
(2c)^{-1}\langle\partial_{c}\eta,1\rangle&(2c)^{-1}\langle\partial_{c}\eta,\eta^{\prime\prime}\rangle\end{matrix}\right].

Since

is symmetric, we have
\langle\partial_{c}\eta,1\rangle=2\langle\partial_{c}\eta,\eta^{\prime\prime}\rangle

\det(A)=-\frac{\pi}{2c}\langle\partial_{c}\eta,1\rangle=-\frac{\pi}{2c}\mathcal{M}^{\prime}(c),

[

•

\mathcal{M}^{\prime}(c)>0

\det(A)<0

\mathcal{L}|_{\mathcal{X}_{c}}
admits one simple negative and a simple zero eigenvalue.

•

If
\mathcal{M}^{\prime}(c)=0

\det(A)=0

{\rm tr}(A)<0

A
has one negative and one zero eigenvalue so that
\mathcal{L}|_{\mathcal{X}_{c}}
admits a double zero eigenvalue and no negative eigenvalues.

•

If
\mathcal{M}^{\prime}(c)<0
, then
\det(A)>0
, hence

has two negative eigenvalues so that
\mathcal{L}|_{\mathcal{X}_{c}}
admits a simple zero eigenvalue and no negative eigenvalues.

The last case yields the assertion of the lemma.
∎

Remark 9

Due to constraint (
1.11
), we have

M(\eta)=\oint\eta du=-\oint(\partial_{u}\eta)^{2}du=-Q(\eta),

(3.6)

so that the criterion in Lemma
6
is equivalent to the mapping of

(1,c_{*})\ni c\mapsto\mathcal{Q}(c):=\oint(\eta^{\prime})^{2}du

being monotonically increasing for
c\in(1,c_{*})
.

Since
\mathcal{M}(c)\to 0
as
c\to 1
by (
1.19
)
and
\mathcal{M}(c)<0
by (
3.6
), it is clear that

\mathcal{M}^{\prime}(c)<0
for
c\gtrsim 1
. The next lemma asserts that

\mathcal{M}^{\prime}(c)<0
for every
c\in(1,c_{*})
.

Lemma 7
.

The mapping (
3.3
) is monotonically decreasing for every
c\in(1,c_{*})
.

Proof.

Differentiating
T(\mathcal{E}(c),c)=2\pi
in
c
yields

\partial_{c}T(\mathcal{E}(c),c)+\mathcal{E}^{\prime}(c)\partial_{\mathcal{E}}T(\mathcal{E}(c),c)=0,

where

\displaystyle\partial_{c}T(\mathcal{E},c)

\displaystyle=2c\int_{-\sqrt{2\mathcal{E}}}^{\sqrt{2\mathcal{E}}}\frac{d\eta}{\sqrt{2\mathcal{E}-\eta^{2}}\sqrt{c^{2}-2\eta}},

\displaystyle\partial_{\mathcal{E}}T(\mathcal{E},c)

\displaystyle=-\mathcal{E}^{-1}\int_{-\sqrt{2\mathcal{E}}}^{\sqrt{2\mathcal{E}}}\frac{\eta d\eta}{\sqrt{2\mathcal{E}-\eta^{2}}\sqrt{c^{2}-2\eta}},

and we have used (
2.11
) with the substitution
\eta=\sqrt{2\mathcal{E}}x
. By using the same substitution, we define
\mathcal{M}(c)\equiv\mathcal{M}(\mathcal{E}(c),c)
with

\mathcal{M}(\mathcal{E},c):=2\int_{-\sqrt{2\mathcal{E}}}^{\sqrt{2\mathcal{E}}}\frac{\eta\sqrt{c^{2}-2\eta}d\eta}{\sqrt{2\mathcal{E}-\eta^{2}}}=2\sqrt{2\mathcal{E}}\int_{-1}^{1}\frac{x\sqrt{c^{2}-2\sqrt{2\mathcal{E}}x}dx}{\sqrt{1-x^{2}}},

from which we obtain

\displaystyle\partial_{c}\mathcal{M}(\mathcal{E},c)

\displaystyle=2c\int_{-\sqrt{2\mathcal{E}}}^{\sqrt{2\mathcal{E}}}\frac{\eta d\eta}{\sqrt{2\mathcal{E}-\eta^{2}}\sqrt{c^{2}-2\eta}},

\displaystyle\partial_{\mathcal{E}}\mathcal{M}(\mathcal{E},c)

\displaystyle=\mathcal{E}^{-1}\int_{-\sqrt{2\mathcal{E}}}^{\sqrt{2\mathcal{E}}}\frac{\eta\sqrt{c^{2}-2\eta}d\eta}{\sqrt{2\mathcal{E}-\eta^{2}}}-\mathcal{E}^{-1}\int_{-\sqrt{2\mathcal{E}}}^{\sqrt{2\mathcal{E}}}\frac{\eta^{2}d\eta}{\sqrt{2\mathcal{E}-\eta^{2}}\sqrt{c^{2}-2\eta}}.

Since
\partial_{\mathcal{E}}T(\mathcal{E}(c),c)<0
, we obtain

\displaystyle\mathcal{M}^{\prime}(c)

\displaystyle=\partial_{c}\mathcal{M}(\mathcal{E}(c),c)+\mathcal{E}^{\prime}(c)\partial_{\mathcal{E}}\mathcal{M}(\mathcal{E}(c),c)

\displaystyle=\frac{2c}{\mathcal{E}(c)|\partial_{\mathcal{E}}T(\mathcal{E}(c),c)|}\Delta(\mathcal{E}(c),c),

where

\displaystyle\Delta(\mathcal{E},c)

\displaystyle:=\left(\int_{-\sqrt{2\mathcal{E}}}^{\sqrt{2\mathcal{E}}}\frac{\eta d\eta}{\sqrt{2\mathcal{E}-\eta^{2}}\sqrt{c^{2}-2\eta}}\right)^{2}

\displaystyle\quad+\left(\int_{-\sqrt{2\mathcal{E}}}^{\sqrt{2\mathcal{E}}}\frac{d\eta}{\sqrt{2\mathcal{E}-\eta^{2}}\sqrt{c^{2}-2\eta}}\right)\left(\int_{-\sqrt{2\mathcal{E}}}^{\sqrt{2\mathcal{E}}}\frac{\eta(c^{2}-3\eta)d\eta}{\sqrt{2\mathcal{E}-\eta^{2}}\sqrt{c^{2}-2\eta}}\right).

We show that
\Delta(\mathcal{E},c)<0
, which implies that
\mathcal{M}^{\prime}(c)<0
. Indeed, since

\frac{\eta\sqrt{c^{2}-2\eta}}{\sqrt{2\mathcal{E}-\eta^{2}}}<\frac{|\eta|\sqrt{c^{2}+2|\eta|}}{\sqrt{2\mathcal{E}-\eta^{2}}},\quad\eta\in(0,\sqrt{2\mathcal{E}}),

we have

\left(\int_{-\sqrt{2\mathcal{E}}}^{\sqrt{2\mathcal{E}}}\frac{d\eta}{\sqrt{2\mathcal{E}-\eta^{2}}\sqrt{c^{2}-2\eta}}\right)\left(\int_{-\sqrt{2\mathcal{E}}}^{\sqrt{2\mathcal{E}}}\frac{\eta\sqrt{c^{2}-2\eta}d\eta}{\sqrt{2\mathcal{E}-\eta^{2}}}\right)<0.

The remaining part of
\Delta(\mathcal{E},c)
is also negative since

\displaystyle\quad\left(\int\frac{\eta d\eta}{\sqrt{2\mathcal{E}-\eta^{2}}\sqrt{c^{2}-2\eta}}\right)^{2}-\left(\int\frac{d\eta}{\sqrt{2\mathcal{E}-\eta^{2}}\sqrt{c^{2}-2\eta}}\right)\left(\int\frac{\eta^{2}d\eta}{\sqrt{2\mathcal{E}-\eta^{2}}\sqrt{c^{2}-2\eta}}\right)

\displaystyle=\int\int\frac{\eta_{1}\eta_{2}-\eta_{2}^{2}}{\sqrt{2\mathcal{E}-\eta_{1}^{2}}\sqrt{c^{2}-2\eta_{1}}\sqrt{2\mathcal{E}-\eta_{2}^{2}}\sqrt{c^{2}-2\eta_{2}}}d\eta_{1}d\eta_{2}

\displaystyle=-\frac{1}{2}\int\int\frac{(\eta_{1}-\eta_{2})^{2}}{\sqrt{2\mathcal{E}-\eta_{1}^{2}}\sqrt{c^{2}-2\eta_{1}}\sqrt{2\mathcal{E}-\eta_{2}^{2}}\sqrt{c^{2}-2\eta_{2}}}d\eta_{1}d\eta_{2}<0,

where the integrations are defined on
[-\sqrt{2\mathcal{E}},\sqrt{2\mathcal{E}}]
.
Hence
\Delta(\mathcal{E}(c),c)<0
and the assertion of the lemma has been proven.
∎

We are now ready to prove Theorem
2
.

Proof of Theorem
2
.

First, we prove that the two constraints (
1.22
) are preserved in the time evolution of the linearized equation (
1.18
).
Since
\Pi_{0}\partial_{u}^{-1}\Pi_{0}
is defined on zero-mean functions with the zero-mean constraint, taking the mean value of (
1.18

2c\frac{d}{dt}\langle 1,\hat{\eta}\rangle=0.

Multiplying (
1.18

\eta^{\prime\prime}
and integrating by parts, we obtain
for any solution
\hat{\eta}\in C^{0}(\mathbb{R},H^{1}_{\rm per}(\mathbb{T}))

\displaystyle 2c\frac{d}{dt}\langle\eta^{\prime\prime},\hat{\eta}\rangle

\displaystyle=\langle(c^{2}-2\eta)\eta^{\prime\prime},\partial_{u}\hat{\eta}\rangle-\langle(1-2\eta^{\prime\prime})\eta^{\prime},\hat{\eta}\rangle

\displaystyle=\langle(\eta^{\prime})^{2}-\eta,\partial_{u}\hat{\eta}\rangle-\langle(1-2\eta^{\prime\prime})\eta^{\prime},\hat{\eta}\rangle

\displaystyle=\langle\eta^{\prime}-2\eta^{\prime}\eta^{\prime\prime},\hat{\eta}\rangle-\langle(1-2\eta^{\prime\prime})\eta^{\prime},\hat{\eta}\rangle

\displaystyle=0,

where (
1.17
) has been used with
\eta\in C^{\infty}_{\rm per}(\mathbb{T})
. Hence the two constraints (
1.22

\hat{\eta}\in C^{0}(\mathbb{R},H^{1}_{\rm per}(\mathbb{T}))

If we further decompose

then

for

Next, existence and uniqueness of solutions

\hat{\eta}(\cdot,0)=\hat{\eta}_{0}

[

]
.

\displaystyle\langle\mathcal{L}w(\cdot,t),w(\cdot,t)\rangle=\langle\mathcal{L}\hat{\eta}(\cdot,t),\hat{\eta}(\cdot,t)\rangle=\langle\mathcal{L}\hat{\eta}_{0},\hat{\eta}_{0}\rangle\leq\beta\|\hat{\eta}_{0}\|^{2}_{H^{1}_{\rm per}},

Appendix A
Euler equations after a conformal transformation

Let
y=\eta(x,t)
be the profile for the free surface of an incompressible and irrotational fluid in the
2\pi
-periodic domain and assume a flat bottom at
y=-h_{0}
, where the vertical velocity vanishes. For a proper definition of the fluid depth
h_{0}
, we add the zero-mean constraint on the free surface, that is,

\oint\eta(x,t)dx=0,

(A.1)

which is invariant in the time evolution of Euler’s equations.

Let
\varphi(x,y,t)
be the velocity potential, which satisfies the Laplace equation in the time-dependent spatial domain

D_{\eta}:=\left\{(x,y)\in\mathbb{R}^{2}:\quad-\pi\leq x\leq\pi,\quad-h_{0}\leq y\leq\eta(x,t)\right\}

subject to the periodic boundary conditions at
x=\pm\pi
and the Neumann boundary condition at
y=-h_{0}
. The formulation of the water wave problem is completed by two additional (kinematic and dynamic) conditions
at the free surface
y=\eta(x,t)
:

\displaystyle\left.\begin{array}[]{r}\displaystyle\eta_{t}+\varphi_{x}\eta_{x}-\varphi_{y}=0,\\
\displaystyle\varphi_{t}+\frac{1}{2}(\varphi_{x})^{2}+\frac{1}{2}(\varphi_{y})^{2}+\eta=0,\end{array}\right\}\qquad\mbox{\rm at}\;\;y=\eta(x,t),

where the gravitational constant
g
is set to unity for convenience.

The method of conformal transformations is used to map the spatial domain
D_{\eta}
to the flat domain

\mathcal{D}:=\left\{(u,v)\in\mathbb{R}^{2}:\quad-\pi\leq u\leq\pi,\quad-h\leq v\leq 0\right\},

where
h
may be different from
h_{0}
. The transformation is based on the conformal mapping
x+iy=z(u+iv,t)
, where
w:=u+iv
is a new complex variable and
z\in C^{\omega}(\mathcal{D})
is a holomorphic function, the real and imaginary parts of which satisfy the Cauchy–Riemann equations

\frac{\partial x}{\partial u}=\frac{\partial y}{\partial v},\qquad\frac{\partial x}{\partial v}=-\frac{\partial y}{\partial u}.

To preserve the flat bottom
y=-h_{0}
at
v=-h
, one needs to add
the Neumann condition
\partial_{v}x|_{v=-h}=0
, which ensures that

y(u,-h,t)=-h_{0}
is
u
-independent. In addition, we require

x-u
and
y-v
be
2\pi
-periodic functions of
u\in\mathbb{T}:=\mathbb{R}\backslash(2\pi\mathbb{Z})
to ensure that
x(\pi,v,t)-x(-\pi,v,t)=2\pi
.

Abusing notations we refer to
x=x(u,t)
and
y=\eta(u,t)
at the top boundary of
\mathcal{D}
:

x(u,t)+i\eta(u,t)=z(u,t),\qquad\mbox{\rm at }\;\;v=0.

Similarly, we abuse notations for the velocity potential
\varphi(u,v,t)
and define

\xi(u,t):=\varphi(u,v=0,t)

on the flat top boundary of
\mathcal{D}
.
Since the conformal transformation preserves the periodic boundary conditions and the zero vertical velocity condition at
v=-h
, the Laplace equation
can be solved with the following Fourier series

\varphi(u,v,t)=\sum_{n\in\mathbb{Z}}\hat{\xi}_{n}(t)e^{inu}\;\frac{\cosh(n(v+h))}{\cosh(nh)},

where
\hat{\xi}_{n}(t)
is the Fourier coefficient for
\xi(u,t)=\varphi(u,v=0,t)
. Similarly, we obtain

\displaystyle x(u,v,t)

\displaystyle=u+\sum_{n\in\mathbb{Z}}\hat{x}_{n}(t)e^{inu}\;\frac{\cosh(n(v+h))}{\cosh(nh)},

\displaystyle y(u,v,t)

\displaystyle=v+\hat{\eta}_{0}+\sum_{n\in\mathbb{Z}}\hat{x}_{n}(t)e^{inu}\;i\;\frac{\sinh(n(v+h))}{\cosh(nh)}.

It follows from
y(u,-h,t)=-h_{0}
that
\hat{\eta}_{0}=h-h_{0}
. If
\hat{\eta}_{0}(t)=\frac{1}{2\pi}\oint\eta(u,t)du
depends on time
t
, so is
h(t)
which satisfies
\partial_{t}\eta(u,-h,t)-h^{\prime}(t)\partial_{v}\eta(u,-h,t)
for all
u\in\mathbb{T}
.

Reducing the Fourier series for
x(u,v,t)
and
y(u,v,t)
on
v=0

yields

\displaystyle x(u,t)

\displaystyle=u+\hat{x}_{0}(t)+\sum_{n\in\mathbb{Z}\backslash\{0\}}\hat{\eta}_{n}(t)e^{inu}\;(-i)\coth(nh),

\displaystyle\eta(u,t)

\displaystyle=\hat{\eta}_{0}(t)+\sum_{n\in\mathbb{Z}\backslash\{0\}}\hat{\eta}_{n}(t)e^{inu},

with the correspondence
\hat{\eta}_{n}(t)=i\tanh(nh)\hat{x}_{n}(t)
for
n\in\mathbb{Z}\backslash\{0\}
.

Let us introduce the nonlocal operator
T_{h}
with the Fourier symbol given by

\widehat{\left(T_{h}\right)}_{n}=i\tanh(hn),\qquad n\in\mathbb{Z},

so that
\eta=\hat{\eta}_{0}+T_{h}(x-u)
. The inverse of
T_{h}
is only defined on the zero-mean functions with the Fourier symbol given by

\widehat{\left(T_{h}^{-1}\right)}_{n}=\left\{\begin{array}[]{cl}-i\coth(hn),&\quad n\in\mathbb{Z}\backslash\{0\},\\
0,&\quad n=0.\end{array}\right.

Inverting
\eta=\hat{\eta}_{0}+T_{h}(x-u)
yields
x=u+\hat{x}_{0}+T_{h}^{-1}\eta
and

x_{u}=1+K_{h}\eta,

where
K_{h}:=T_{h}^{-1}\partial_{u}
is a linear, self-adjoint, positive operator on
L^{2}(\mathbb{T})
. We set
\hat{x}_{0}=0
in
x=u+\hat{x}_{0}+T_{h}^{-1}\eta
without loss of generality.

The equations of motion can be derived from the following Lagrangian, see
[
11
]
and
[
12
, Appendix A]
for
h=\infty
,

\mathcal{L}(\xi,\eta,x):=\oint\xi(\eta_{t}x_{u}-\eta_{u}x_{t})du+\frac{1}{2}\oint\xi T_{h}\xi_{u}du-\frac{1}{2}\oint\eta^{2}x_{u}du+\oint f(\eta-T_{h}(x-u))du,

where
f
is the Lagrange multiplier with the zero mean value
\oint fdu=0
. Variation of
\mathcal{L}
in
\xi
,
\eta
, and
x

yields the system of equations

\left\{\begin{array}[]{l}\eta_{t}x_{u}-\eta_{u}x_{t}+T_{h}\xi_{u}=0,\\
-\xi_{t}x_{u}+\xi_{u}x_{t}-\eta x_{u}+f=0,\\
\xi_{t}\eta_{u}-\xi_{u}\eta_{t}+\eta\eta_{u}+T_{h}f=0,\end{array}\right.

(A.4)

with the additional constraint due to the reduction
h=h_{0}+\frac{1}{2\pi}\oint\eta du
:

\frac{1}{2}\oint\xi(\partial_{h}T_{h})\xi_{u}du-\oint f(\partial_{h}T_{h})(x-u)du=0.

(A.5)

Taking mean values in each equation of system (
A.4
) and integrating by parts yields three conserved quantities:

\displaystyle M_{1}(\eta)

\displaystyle=\oint\eta x_{u}du=\oint\eta(1+K_{h}\eta)du=0,

(A.6)

\displaystyle M_{2}(\xi,\eta)

\displaystyle=\oint\xi x_{u}du=\oint\xi(1+K_{h}\eta)du,

(A.7)

\displaystyle M_{3}(\xi,\eta)

\displaystyle=\oint\xi\eta_{u}du,

(A.8)

where the constraint
M_{1}(\eta)=0
follows from the zero-mean constraint (
A.1
) in physical coordinates. We express
f
from the second equation of system (
A.4
), substitute it into the third equation, and invert
T_{h}
on the periodic functions with zero mean. This transforms the system (
A.4
) to the following system of two equations for
\xi
and
\eta
:

\left\{\begin{array}[]{l}\eta_{t}x_{u}-\eta_{u}x_{t}+T_{h}\xi_{u}=0,\\
\xi_{t}x_{u}-\xi_{u}x_{t}+\eta(1+K_{h}\eta)+T_{h}^{-1}(\xi_{t}\eta_{u}-\xi_{u}\eta_{t}+\eta\eta_{u})=0.\end{array}\right.

(A.9)

The constraint (
A.5
) is rewritten in the equivalent form:

\frac{1}{2}\oint\xi(\partial_{h}T_{h})\xi_{u}du+\oint T_{h}^{-1}(\xi_{t}\eta_{u}-\xi_{u}\eta_{t}+\eta\eta_{u})(\partial_{h}T_{h})(T_{h}^{-1}\eta)du=0.

(A.10)

The conserved energy of system (
A.9
) is given by

H(\xi,\eta)=\oint\left[\xi T_{h}\xi_{u}-\eta^{2}(1+K_{h}\eta)\right]du.

(A.11)

To derive (
A.11
), we multiply the first equation of system (
A.9
) by
\xi_{t}
and the second equation by
\eta_{t}
, integrate over the period, and subtract one equation from another. After integration by parts, we get
\frac{d}{dt}H(\xi,\eta)=0
if and only if

-\frac{1}{2}h^{\prime}(t)\oint\xi(\partial_{h}T_{h})\xi_{u}du-h^{\prime}(t)\oint\eta\eta_{u}(\partial_{h}T_{h}^{-1})\eta du-\oint(\xi_{t}\eta_{u}-\xi_{u}\eta_{t})(x_{t}-T_{h}^{-1}\eta_{t})du=0.

Since
x_{t}-T_{h}^{-1}\eta_{t}=h^{\prime}(t)(\partial_{h}T_{h}^{-1})\eta
,
\partial_{h}T_{h}^{-1}=-T_{h}^{-1}(\partial_{h}T_{h})T_{h}^{-1}
, and
T_{h}^{-1}
is skew-adjoint, the last constraint is identical to the constraint (
A.10
) for every
h^{\prime}(t)
. This proves the conservation of
H(\xi,\eta)
.

The conserved quantities (
A.6
), (
A.7
), (
A.8
), and (
A.11
) coincide with the conserved quantities for Euler’s equation in physical coordinates
[
5
]
, see also
[
11
]
for
h=\infty
.

In order to introduce the scalar model (
1.1
), we rewrite (
A.9
) in the reference frame moving with the wave speed
c
:

\left\{\begin{array}[]{l}\eta_{t}x_{u}-\eta_{u}x_{t}+T_{h}\xi_{u}-c\eta_{u}=0,\\
\xi_{t}x_{u}-\xi_{u}x_{t}+\eta(1+K_{h}\eta)-c\xi_{u}+T_{h}^{-1}(\xi_{t}\eta_{u}-\xi_{u}\eta_{t}+\eta\eta_{u})=0,\end{array}\right.

(A.12)

where
u
now stands for
u-ct
and we have used the chain rule with

\displaystyle\xi_{t}

\displaystyle\to\xi_{t}-c\xi_{u},

\displaystyle\eta_{t}

\displaystyle\to\eta_{t}-c\eta_{u},

\displaystyle x_{t}

\displaystyle\to x_{t}+c-cx_{u}.

We introduce a change of variables by

\xi=cT_{h}^{-1}\eta+\zeta,

(A.13)

after which the system (
A.12
) can be rewritten in the form:

Appendix B
Introducing the local model

One popular model for fluid motion is the intermediate long-wave (ILW) equation written in the form

\partial_{t}\eta+\eta\partial_{u}\eta=\mathcal{K}_{h}(\partial_{u}\eta)

(B.1)

where
\mathcal{K}_{h}
is defined by the Fourier symbol

\widehat{\left(\mathcal{K}_{h}\right)}_{n}=\left\{\begin{array}[]{cl}n\coth(hn),&\quad n\in\mathbb{Z}\backslash\{0\},\\
h^{-1},&\quad n=0.\end{array}\right.

In comparison with (
1.3
), we have the correspondence

\mathcal{K}_{h}=K_{h}+\frac{1}{2\pi h}\oint\;\cdot\;du.

The ILW equation (
B.1
) is integrable by inverse scattering and many results on well-posedness and dynamics of nonlinear waves have been obtained for this fluid model,
see review in
[
39
]
. In the shallow water limit
h\to 0
, the scaling transformation

\eta(u,t):=h\tilde{\eta}(\tilde{u},\tilde{t}),\quad\tilde{u}:=u+h^{-1}t,\quad\tilde{t}:=ht

recovers formally the Korteweg–de Vries (KdV) equation

\partial_{\tilde{t}}\tilde{\eta}+\tilde{\eta}\partial_{\tilde{u}}\tilde{\eta}+\frac{1}{3}\partial_{\tilde{u}}^{3}\tilde{\eta}=0

due to the asymptotic expansion

\mathcal{K}_{h}=\frac{1}{h}-\frac{1}{3}h\partial_{u}^{2}+\mathcal{O}(h^{3}).

For completeness, in the deep water limit
h\to\infty
, the ILW equation (
B.1
) becomes the Benjamin–Ono (BO) equation

\partial_{t}\eta+\eta\partial_{u}\eta+\mathcal{H}(\partial^{2}_{u}\eta)=0,

where
\mathcal{H}
is the periodic Hilbert transform defined by (
1.9
). Both the KdV and BO equations are also integrable by inverse scattering.

To obtain the local evolution equation (
1.10
) from the nonlocal model (
1.1
), we replace
K_{h}
given by (
1.3
) with

\tilde{K}_{h}=K_{h}+\frac{1}{2\pi h}\oint\;\cdot\;du-\frac{1}{h}=\mathcal{K}_{h}-\frac{1}{h}.

The difference between
\tilde{K}_{h}
and
K_{h}
appears in the local term
1/h
. It is removed from the mean term in the definition of
K_{h}
and from all Fourier modes in the definition of
\tilde{K}_{h}
. Since
K_{h}=T_{h}^{-1}\partial_{u}
, we can similarly define

\tilde{K}_{h}=\tilde{T}_{h}^{-1}\partial_{u}
and expand asymptotically as
h\to 0
:

\tilde{K}_{h}=-\frac{1}{3}h\partial_{u}^{2}+\mathcal{O}(h^{3}),\qquad\tilde{T}_{h}^{-1}=-\frac{1}{3}h\partial_{u}+\mathcal{O}(h^{3}).

By using the scaling transformation

\eta(u,t):=h^{-1}\tilde{\eta}(\tilde{u},\tilde{t}),\qquad u:=3^{-1/2}\tilde{u},\qquad t:=3^{-1/2}h^{1/2}\tilde{t},\qquad c=h^{-1/2}\tilde{c},

we obtain the formal limit of the model equation (
1.1
) as
h\to 0

in the form

-2\tilde{c}\partial_{\tilde{u}}\partial_{\tilde{t}}\tilde{\eta}=\left(-\tilde{c}^{2}\partial_{\tilde{u}}^{2}-1\right)\tilde{\eta}+\tilde{\eta}\partial_{\tilde{u}}^{2}\tilde{\eta}+\frac{1}{2}\partial_{\tilde{u}}^{2}\tilde{\eta}^{2}.

(B.2)

Expanding the derivatives, changing the sign, and removing the tilde notations yields the local model (
1.10
).
