On Extensions of Frobenius-Kähler and Sasakian Lie Algebras
M.C. Rodríguez-Vallarte (1) , G. Salgado (1) , O.A. Sánchez-Valenzuela (2)

Abstract
. Extensions of Lie algebras equipped with Sasakian or Frobenius-Kähler geometric structures are studied. Conditions are given so that a double extension of a Sasakian Lie algebra be Sasakian again. Conditions are also given for obtaining either a Sasakian or a Frobernius-Kähler Lie algebra upon respectively extending a Frobernius-Kähler or a Sasakian Lie algebra by adjoining a derivation of the source algebra. Low-dimensional examples are included.

1.
Introduction

There are two well known mechanisms to extend a given Lie algebra
\mathfrak{g}
.
One is through central extensions using
2
-cocycles

\theta\in(\wedge^{2}\mathfrak{g}^{*})\otimes V
with values in some
\mathfrak{g}
-module
V
.
The other is through semidirect sums
by adjoining to
\mathfrak{g}
a Lie subalgebra of its Lie algebra of derivations

\operatorname{Der}(\mathfrak{g})
. Both of them have been thoroughly studied and they coincide
when the
2
-cocycle is trivial and the adjoined Lie subalgebra
of
\operatorname{Der}(\mathfrak{g})
is abelian and acts trivially on
\mathfrak{g}
.
In both cases one ends up with the direct sum of two Lie algebras.

When the two procedures are applied successively, the result is
known as a
double extension
(of
\mathfrak{g}
) and it was originally
introduced by V. Kaç in
[
13
]
. The simplest example of
this process starts with a Lie algebra
\mathfrak{g}
. Then perform
a central extension on it via a
2
-cocycle

\theta
with values in the underlying field viewed as the
trivial
1
-dimensional
\mathfrak{g}
-module. Then adjoin to the
resulting central extension, a derivation of the central extension itself.

The relevance of this process lies in the fact that,
when applied to a Lie algebra equipped with some kind of geometric structure
(e.g., an invariant metric, a symplectic structure, a hermitian structure,
a contact structure, etc.), the resulting Lie algebra inherits
the same kind of geometric structure.
See
[
13
]
,
[
10
]
,
[
15
]
,
[
19
]
,
[
20
]
.

As a matter of fact, the double extension process has been extended
from the realm of Lie algebras to almost any kind of algebraic structure;
i.e., Leibniz algebras, Poisson algebras, Kähler Lie algebras; Hom-Lie algebras,
and their
\mathbb{Z}_{2}
-graded versions; say, Lie superalgebras; Leibniz superalgebras;
Hom-Lie superalgebras; etc.
In other words, the double extension technique has
proved to be useful and effective.
See
[
1
]
,
[
4
]
,
[
5
]
,
[
6
]
,
[
14
]
.

For example, it can be proved that if
\mathfrak{g}
comes
equipped with a symplectic form
\omega:\mathfrak{g}\times\mathfrak{g}\to\mathbb{R}

—a fact referred to by saying that
(\mathfrak{g},\omega)

is a symplectic Lie algebra
—
the central extension of
\mathfrak{g}
by the
2
-cocycle defined by
\omega

is a contact Lie algebra.
Similarly, it has been proved in
[
2
]
that the
central extension of a Kähler Lie algebra through the

2
-cocycle of its symplectic structure, is a Sasakian Lie algebra.
It is also known that any contact Lie algebra with non-trivial center
is a central extension of a symplectic Lie algebra (see
[
11
]
).
On the other hand, it is proved
in
[
2
]
that any Sasakian Lie algebra
with non-trivial center is a central extension of a Kähler Lie algebra.
Unfortunately, it has not been possible so far to prove
similar results starting with either contact Lie algebras or Sasakian
Lie algebras (see Proposition
2.5
).
It has been proved in
[
3
]
, however, that it is possible
to go from contact Lie algebras to Frobenius Lie algebras (i.e.,
symplectic Lie algebras whose symplectic form is exact) and
viceversa, while in
[
19
]
conditions are given so that
a double extension of a contact Lie algebra be again
a contact Lie algebra. It is then natural to try and see
whether these kind of results can also be obtained for
either the class of Kähler Lie algebras or for the class
of Sasakian Lie algebras.

Since double extensions of Kähler Lie algebras have already
been studied (see
[
9
]
and
[
10
]
), the purpose of
this work is to focus on the conditions needed on a double
extension of a Sasakian Lie algebra, to be Sasakian again.
We shall also prove that certain subclass of Kähler Lie algebras,
once extended by a derivation, always yield Sasakian Lie algebras
and viceversa.

This work is organized as follows: We first recall in §2 the basic
definitions of Kähler and Sasakian Lie algebras, together with
the main results of the double extension process. It is
proved in §3 a Theorem that gives the conditions under which a double extension
of a Sasakian Lie algebra is Sasakian again.
Section §4 deals with extensions of Frobenius-Kähler Lie algebras
and of Sasakian Lie algebras by a derivation, and the relationship
between them. Finally, §5 gives a few non-trivial low-dimensional
examples of the results we have obtained in §3 and §4.

2.
Frobenius-Kähler and Sasakian Lie Algebras

2.1.
Basic definitions

Let
\mathfrak{g}
be a Lie algebra with Lie bracket
[\,\cdot\,,\,\cdot\,]:\mathfrak{g}\times\mathfrak{g}\to\mathfrak{g}
.
Any
2
-cocycle
\theta\in\wedge^{2}\mathfrak{g}^{*}
defines a

central extension of

\mathfrak{g}
in the following way:
If
z\notin\mathfrak{g}
, consider the vector space

\mathfrak{g}_{\theta}:=\mathfrak{g}\oplus\langle z\rangle
, and define

[\,\cdot\,,\,\cdot\,]_{\theta}:\mathfrak{g}_{\theta}\times\mathfrak{g}_{\theta}\to\mathfrak{g}_{\theta}

by means of

[x,y]_{\theta}=[x,y]_{\mathfrak{g}}+\theta(x,y)z
, for any
x
and
y
in
\mathfrak{g}
,
and
[x,z]_{\theta}=0
, for any
x
in
\mathfrak{g}
.
Since
\theta\in\wedge^{2}\mathfrak{g}^{*}
is a
2
-cocycle,
[\,\cdot\,,\,\cdot\,]_{\theta}

is in fact a Lie bracket on
\mathfrak{g}_{\theta}
. The pair
(\mathfrak{g}_{\theta},[\,\cdot\,,\,\cdot\,]_{\theta})

is called
the central extension of
\mathfrak{g}
by the
2
-cocycle
\theta
.
Moreover, it is proved in
[
8
]
that
\mathfrak{g}_{\theta_{1}}\simeq\mathfrak{g}_{\theta_{2}}

if and only if the cohomology classes
[\theta_{1}]
and
[\theta_{2}]

are the same in
H^{2}(\mathfrak{g},\mathbb{F})
, where
\mathbb{F}
is the base field.

One may also consider the case in which
\mathfrak{g}
is extended by
adjoining a single derivation
D\in\operatorname{Der}(\mathfrak{g})
. Such an extension
has the vector space decomposition
\mathfrak{g}(D)=\langle D\rangle\oplus\mathfrak{g}
,
and its Lie bracket is defined in such a way that

[D,x]_{\mathfrak{g}(D)}=D(x)=-[x,D]_{\mathfrak{g}(D)}
, for any
x\in\mathfrak{g}
, and
so as to coincide with the Lie bracket
[\,\cdot\,,\,\cdot\,]
on
\mathfrak{g}

when applied to any pair of elements
x
and
y
in
\mathfrak{g}
.
This type of extension is actually the semidirect sum,

\mathfrak{g}(D)=\langle D\rangle\ltimes\mathfrak{g}
,
and it is called
the extension of
\mathfrak{g}
by the (chosen) derivation

D\in\operatorname{Der}(\mathfrak{g})
.
In particular, an element
z\in\mathfrak{g}(D)
gets defined in a unique way,
having the property that
[z,x]_{\mathfrak{g}(D)}=D(x)
, for any
x\in\mathfrak{g}
;
i.e., one may assert that there is a
z\in\mathfrak{g}(D)
, such that,

\operatorname{ad}(z)|_{\mathfrak{g}}=D
.

Now, the two types of extensions just described can be performed
one after the other. Say, start with a Lie algebra
(\mathfrak{g},[\,\cdot\,,\,\cdot\,])

and a
2
-cocycle
\theta\in\wedge^{2}\mathfrak{g}^{*}
, so as to produce the
central extension
(\mathfrak{g}_{\theta},[\,\cdot\,,\,\cdot\,]_{\theta})
. Then adjoin
a derivation
D\in\operatorname{Der}(\mathfrak{g}_{\theta})
to produce the Lie algebra

\left(\mathfrak{g}(D;\theta),[\,\cdot\,,\,\cdot\,]_{\mathfrak{g}(D,\theta)}\right)
,
by further extending the Lie bracket
[\,\cdot\,,\,\cdot\,]_{\theta}
, to the vector space,

\mathfrak{g}(D,\theta):=\langle D\rangle\oplus\mathfrak{g}_{\theta}
, letting

[\,\cdot\,,\,\cdot\,]_{\mathfrak{g}(D,\theta)}:\mathfrak{g}(D,\theta)\times\mathfrak{g}(D,\theta)\to\mathfrak{g}(D,\theta)
,
be skew-symmetric and given by,

[u,v]_{\mathfrak{g}(D,\theta)}=[u,v]_{\theta}
for any
u
and
v
in
\mathfrak{g}_{\theta}
,
and
[D,v]_{\mathfrak{g}(D,\theta)}=D(v)
for any
v
in
\mathfrak{g}_{\theta}
.
The process of going from
\mathfrak{g}
to
\mathfrak{g}(D,\theta)
is known as

the double extension of
\mathfrak{g}
by the pair
(D,\theta)
,
and actually,
\mathfrak{g}(D,\theta)=\langle D\rangle\ltimes\mathfrak{g}_{\theta}
.
We remark that the double extension process was first
introduced by V. Kaç in
[
13
]
.

We shall be dealing with
Frobenius Lie algebras

and with
contact Lie algebras
.
Let
(\mathfrak{g},[\,\cdot\,,\,\cdot\,])
be a Lie algebra.
Given a linear form

\varphi\in\mathfrak{g}^{*}
, its associated

Kirillov form

B_{\varphi}

is the bilinear form

B_{\varphi}:\mathfrak{g}\times\mathfrak{g}\to\mathbb{F}
, defined by,

B_{\varphi}(x,y)=\varphi([x,y]),\quad\text{for all\ }\,x,y\in\mathfrak{g}.

Clearly,
B_{\varphi}
is skew-symmetric.
A Lie algebra
(\mathfrak{g},[\,\cdot\,,\,\cdot\,])

is a Frobenius Lie algebra

if
B_{\varphi}
is non-degenerate (see
[
17
]
).
Equivalently, if
(d\varphi)^{n}\neq 0
.
We shall also use the notation
(\mathfrak{g},\varphi)
for
a Frobenius Lie algebra, assuming that
\varphi\in\mathfrak{g}^{*}

has this property.
In particular, a Frobenius Lie algebra
(\mathfrak{g},\varphi)

satisfies
\operatorname{dim}(\mathfrak{g})=2n
for some
n\in\mathbb{N}
,
and it is also
an exact symplectic Lie algebra

whose symplectic form is
\omega:=B_{\varphi}=-d\varphi
.
Moreover, there is a unique
x_{P}\in\mathfrak{g}
,
called
the principal element associated to

\varphi
,
satisfying,
\varphi\,\circ\,\operatorname{ad}(x_{P})=\varphi
(see
[
17
]
).
It has been proved in
[
3
]

that
there is a codimension
1

unimodular contact ideal

\mathfrak{h}
in
\mathfrak{g}
, such that,

\mathfrak{g}=\langle x_{P}\rangle\ltimes\mathfrak{h}.

Recall that
(\mathfrak{g},[\,\cdot\,,\,\cdot\,])

is a contact Lie algebra
,
if
\operatorname{dim}(\mathfrak{g})=2n+1
for some
n\in\mathbb{N}
,
and if it comes equipped with a
linear form
\alpha\in\mathfrak{g}^{*}
, called
the contact
1
-form
,
satisfying
\alpha\wedge(d\alpha)^{n}\neq 0
.
Equivalently, in terms of the Kirillov form, there is
an element
\xi\in\mathfrak{g}
,
called
the Reeb vector
, satisfying
\alpha(\xi)=1
,
and
\operatorname{Rad}(B_{\alpha})=\langle\xi\rangle
, where

\operatorname{Rad}(B_{\alpha})=\{x\in\mathfrak{g}\mid B_{\alpha}(x,y)=0,\ \,\forall\ y\in\mathfrak{g}\}

is
the radical of

B_{\alpha}
.
We shall also use the notation
(\mathfrak{g},\alpha)
for
a contact Lie algebra, assuming that
\alpha\in\mathfrak{g}^{*}

has the required property property, and refer to

\alpha
as its
contact structure
.

Now, there are, at least,
two different ways of producing
a contact Lie algebra from a Frobenius Lie algebra
(\mathfrak{g},\varphi)
:

(1)

Use the symplectic
2
-form
\omega=-d\varphi

as the defining 2-cocycle for the central extension of
\mathfrak{g}

by a central element
z\notin\mathfrak{g}
.
In this case
the contact 1-form

\alpha\in(\mathfrak{g}\oplus\langle z\rangle)^{*}
,
is
\alpha=z^{*}
.

Note:
For a given
z
in a Lie algebra
\mathfrak{G}
, we follow the convention of
writing,
z^{*}\in\mathfrak{G}^{*}
for a linear functional such that
z^{*}(z)=1
, and use
later the freedom of choosing the subspace
\operatorname{Ker}(z^{*})\subset\mathfrak{G}
as needed.

(2)

Find a derivation
D\in\operatorname{Der}(\mathfrak{g})
, satisfying
\varphi\,\circ\,D=\varphi
.
Then,
\mathfrak{g}(D):=\langle D\rangle\ltimes\mathfrak{g}
becomes a contact Lie algebra
with contact 1-form
\alpha\in\mathfrak{g}(D)^{*}
, given by,

\alpha=\varphi+\lambda\,D^{*}
,
for almost any non-zero scalar
\lambda
.
Moreover, in this case there is
a codimension
1
ideal
\mathfrak{h}
in
\mathfrak{g}
, and
\mathfrak{g}
itself
is a codimension
1
ideal in
\mathfrak{g}(D)
.
Thus,
\mathfrak{g}(D)\rhd\mathfrak{g}\rhd\mathfrak{h}
(see
[
3
]
).

Kähler structures.
In what follows, we shall assume that all
the Lie algebras
\mathfrak{g}
we deal with, are defined over the real field
\mathbb{R}
. A

Kähler structure in a Lie algebra

(\mathfrak{g},[\,\cdot\,,\,\cdot\,])
is a triple,

(g,J,\omega)
, where,

(1)

g:\mathfrak{g}\times\mathfrak{g}\to\mathbb{R}
is a positive-definite
symmetric bilinear form on
\mathfrak{g}
.

(2)

J:\mathfrak{g}\to\mathfrak{g}
is a linear endomorphism satisfying,

(2.1)

J\circ J=-\operatorname{Id}_{\mathfrak{g}}
;

(2.2)

g(Jx,y)+g(x,Jy)=0
, for all
x
and
y
in
\mathfrak{g}
;

(2.3)

N_{J}(x,y)=0
, for all
x
and
y
in
\mathfrak{g}
, where

N_{J}
is the Nijenhuis tensor of
J
, given by,

N_{J}(x,y)=-[x,y]+[Jx,Jy]-J[x,Jy]-J[Jx,y];

(3)

\omega:\mathfrak{g}\times\mathfrak{g}\to\mathbb{R}
, defined through,

\omega(x,y)=g(x,Jy)
, for all
x
and
y
in
\mathfrak{g}
, satisfies,

d\omega=0
.

Comments.

(1)
In view of (3), it
suffices to define a Kähler structure on a given
Lie algebra
(\mathfrak{g},[\,\dot{\,},\,\cdot\,])
in terms of
either, the pair
(g,J)
, or the pair
(\omega,J)
.
Also, if defined as a pair
(g,J)
, the condition (2.2),
is equivalent to
g(Jx,Jy)=g(x,y)
, for all
x
and
y
in
\mathfrak{g}
,
given the hypothesis (2.1).
In this context,
g
is called a

Hermitian metric.

(2)
Recall that a linear endomorphism
J\in\operatorname{End}(\mathfrak{g})

satisfying (2.1) is called an
almost complex structure on

\mathfrak{g}
.
An almost complex structure
J\in\operatorname{End}(\mathfrak{g})
satisfying (2.3) is called

a complex structure.

Finally, a pair
(g,J)
satisfying (1), (2.1), (2.2) and (3) is called an

almost Kähler structure
.

Sasakian structures.

A
Sasakian structure in a Lie algebra

(\mathfrak{g},[\,\cdot\,,\,\cdot\,])
is a 4-tuple,

(g,\xi,\alpha,\Phi)
, where,

(1)

g:\mathfrak{g}\times\mathfrak{g}\to\mathbb{R}
is a positive-definite
symmetric bilinear form on
\mathfrak{g}
.

(2)

\xi\in\mathfrak{g}
and
\alpha\in\mathfrak{g}^{*}
are dual to each other,
in the sense that
\alpha(\xi)=1
.

(3)

\Phi:\mathfrak{g}\to\mathfrak{g}
is a linear endomorphism satisfying:

(3.1)

\Phi\,\circ\,\Phi=-\operatorname{Id}_{\mathfrak{g}}+\alpha\otimes\xi
;

(3.2)

g(\Phi x,\Phi y)=g(x,y)-\alpha(x)\,\alpha(y)
, for all
x
and
y
in
\mathfrak{g}
;

(3.3)

g(x,\Phi y)=d\alpha(x,y)
, for all
x
and
y
in
\mathfrak{g}
;

(3.4)

N_{\Phi}=-d\alpha\otimes\xi
, where,

N_{\Phi}(x,y)=\Phi^{2}[x,y]+[\Phi x,\Phi y]-\Phi[x,\Phi y]-\Phi[\Phi x,y],

for all
x
and
y
in
\mathfrak{g}
.

Comments.

(1)
The positive-definite
symmetric bilinear form
g:\mathfrak{g}\times\mathfrak{g}\to\mathbb{R}
of a Kähler
(or of a Sasakian) structure, need not be

\operatorname{ad}
-invariant; that is,
g
does not necessarily
satisfy
the invariance condition

g([x,y],z)=g(x,[y,z])
, for all
x
,
y
and
z
in
\mathfrak{g}
.

(2)
The conditions

\Phi\,\circ\,\Phi=-\operatorname{Id}_{\mathfrak{g}}+\alpha\otimes\xi
,
and
\alpha(\xi)=1
, imply that
\Phi(\xi)=0
,
\alpha\circ\Phi=0
,
and
(\Phi|_{\operatorname{Ker}(\alpha)})^{2}=-\operatorname{Id}_{\mathfrak{g}}
.
Thus, one might as well use the notation
J:=\Phi|_{\operatorname{Ker}(\alpha)}

(see
[
7
]
).

Remark 2.1
.

It is proved in
[
7
]
(Chapter 4) that if
some kind of structure is given on a Lie algebra
(\mathfrak{g},[\,\cdot\,,\,\cdot\,])

(or more generally on a differentiable manifold
M
),
there is always a way to produce an adapted positive-definite inner product
on
\mathfrak{g}
(or Riemannian metric on
M
) compatible with the given structure.
Thus,
(1)
if
(\mathfrak{g},\omega)
is a symplectic Lie algebra,
there is always a way to produce an almost complex structure

J\in\operatorname{End}(\mathfrak{g})
and a positive-definite symmetric bilinear form

g:\mathfrak{g}\times\mathfrak{g}\to\mathbb{R}
, satisfying
\omega(x,y)=g(x,Jy)
;

(2)
if
(\mathfrak{g},\alpha)
is a contact Lie algebra,
there is always a way to produce a linear endomorphism

\Phi\in\operatorname{End}(\mathfrak{g})
and a positive-definite symmetric bilinear form

g:\mathfrak{g}\times\mathfrak{g}\to\mathbb{R}
,
satisfying
d\alpha(x,y)=g(x,\Phi y)
;
or,
(3)
if
(\xi,\alpha,\Phi)
is a given triple on
\mathfrak{g}

with
\xi\in\mathfrak{g}
and
\alpha\in\mathfrak{g}^{*}
satisfying,
\alpha(\xi)=1
,
and
\Phi\in\operatorname{End}(\mathfrak{g})
, satisfying
\Phi^{2}=-\operatorname{Id}_{\mathfrak{g}}+\alpha\otimes\xi
,
and
N_{\Phi}=-d\alpha\otimes\xi
, there is always a way to produce a
positive-definite symmetric bilinear form

g:\mathfrak{g}\times\mathfrak{g}\to\mathbb{R}
, satisfying

g(\Phi x,\Phi y)=g(x,y)-\alpha(x)\alpha(y)
.
In other words,
the existence of an adapted positive-definite
symmetric bilinear form
g:\mathfrak{g}\times\mathfrak{g}\to\mathbb{R}
, is always guaranteed.

Therefore, it is safe to remove
g
from the defining data
of Kähler and Sasakian structures and take the adapted
g

for granted.
From now on, we shall also say that
(\mathfrak{g},[\,\cdot\,,\,\cdot\,])

is a Kähler Lie algebra
(respectively,
a Sasakian Lie algebra
)
if
(J,\omega)
is a Kähler structure (respectively, if

(\xi,\alpha,\Phi)
is a Sasakian structure) defined on it.

We now quote two important results that relate
Kähler and Sasakian Lie algebras:

Theorem 2.2

(See
[
2
]
)
.

Let
(\xi,\alpha,\Phi)
be a Sasakian structure on a Lie algebra
(\mathfrak{g},[\,\cdot\,,\,\cdot\,])

with non-trivial one-dimensional center generated by
\xi
, and let
\mathfrak{h}=\operatorname{Ker}(\alpha)
.
Let
J=\Phi|_{\mathfrak{h}}
and let
\omega

be obtained through
d\alpha
from
the
\mathfrak{h}
-component of the Lie bracket on
\mathfrak{g}
.
Then,
(J,\omega)
is a Kähler structure on
(\mathfrak{h},[\,\cdot\,,\,\cdot\,]|_{\mathfrak{h}\times\mathfrak{h}})
.

In other words, this result states that Sasakian Lie algebras
with non-trivial center generated by
\xi
, must be central extensions
of Kähler Lie algebras.

Theorem 2.3

(See
[
21
]
)
.

Let
(J,\omega)
be an almost Kähler structure
on the Lie algebra
(\mathfrak{h},[\,\cdot\,,\,\cdot\,])
,
and let
(\alpha,\xi,\Phi)
be the corresponding
contact structure on the central extension

\mathfrak{h}_{\omega}=\mathfrak{h}\oplus\langle\xi\rangle

by the
2
-cocycle defined by
\omega
.
Then the Nijenhuis torsion
N_{\Phi}
on
\mathfrak{h}_{\omega}

is given in terms of the Nijenhuis tensor
N_{J}
on
\mathfrak{h}
as,

N_{\Phi}(X,Y)=N_{J}(x,y)-d\alpha(X,Y)\,\xi,

where,
X=x+\lambda\,\xi
and
Y=y+\mu\,\xi
,
for any pair of scalars
\lambda
and
\mu
and any pair of elements

x
and
y
in
\mathfrak{h}
.

One concludes from this result that
N_{J}=0
, if and only if,
N_{\Phi}=-d\alpha\otimes\xi
.
Therefore, if the almost complex structure
J
in
\mathfrak{h}
is integrable, then

\mathfrak{g}=\mathfrak{h}_{\omega}
, as given in Theorem

2.3
, is a Sasakian Lie algebra; in other words:

Corollary 2.4
.

The central extension of a Kähler Lie algebra defined by the

2
-cocycle given by its symplectic form
\omega
, is a Sasakian Lie algebra.
Furthermore, any Sasakian Lie algebra with non trivial center
is obtained as such a central extension
of a Kähler Lie algebra.

Observation.

On the other hand, the next result shows that a
Kähler structure
(J,\omega)
can never be obtained
on a central extension of a Lie algebra
(\mathfrak{g},[\,\cdot\,,\,\cdot\,])

equipped with a Sasakian structure
(\xi,\alpha,\Phi)

in such a way that its symplectic form
\omega
be an extension of
d\alpha

and its almost complex structure
J
be an extension of
\Phi
.
More concisely:

Proposition 2.5
.

Let
(\xi,\alpha,\Phi)
be a Sasakian structure on a given Lie algebra
(\mathfrak{g},[\,\cdot\,,\,\cdot\,])
.
Then, no central extension of
\mathfrak{g}
produces a Kähler structure
(J,\omega)

whose complex structure satisfies
J|_{\operatorname{Ker}({\alpha})}=\Phi
,
and whose symplectic form satisfies
\omega|_{\operatorname{Ker}({\alpha})}=-d\alpha
.

Proof.

Assume that the central extension of
(\mathfrak{g},[\,\cdot\,,\,\cdot\,])
by a
2
-cocycle
\theta

produces a Kähler Lie algebra
(\mathfrak{g}_{\theta},[\,\cdot\,,\,\cdot\,]_{\theta})
.
Let
z
be the central element added by the central extension;
i.e.,
[x,y]_{\theta}=[x,y]_{\mathfrak{g}}+\theta(x,y)z
, for any
x
and
y
in
\mathfrak{g}
.
Assume that the complex structure
J:\mathfrak{g}_{\theta}\to\mathfrak{g}_{\theta}
is defined by,

J(x)=\Phi(x)
, for any
x\in\operatorname{Ker}(\alpha)
, and
J(\xi)=z
, and
J(z)=-\xi
.
Since
J
must be integrable, we have,

\displaystyle 0

\displaystyle=N_{J}(x,y)=N_{\Phi}(x,y)+d\alpha(x,y)\xi

\displaystyle+(\theta(x,y)+\theta(\Phi(x),\Phi(y)))z+(\theta(\Phi(x),y)+\theta(x,\Phi(y)))\xi.

Therefore, for any
x
and
y\in\operatorname{Ker}(\alpha)
, one has,

\theta(x,y)+\theta(\Phi(x),\Phi(y))=0,\quad\text{and}\quad\theta(\Phi(x),y)+\theta(x,\Phi(y))=0.

Since
\Phi^{2}=-\operatorname{Id}
in
\operatorname{Ker}(\alpha)
, it must be true that

\theta(x,y)=0
for any
x
and
y\in\operatorname{Ker}(\alpha)
. Similarly,

N_{J}(x,\xi)=0
for any
x\in\operatorname{Ker}(\alpha)
implies that

\theta(x,\xi)=0
. It follows that
\theta\equiv 0
.
This implies that
dz^{*}=0
.

Now, the hypothesis that the symplectic form on
\mathfrak{g}_{\theta}

should be given by the extension of
d\alpha
, means that,

\omega=d\alpha+\xi^{*}\wedge z^{*}.

But then,
d\omega=0
if and only if
d\xi^{*}=0
, which is impossible
(see Theorem 13 in
[
12
]
).
∎

2.2.
Double extensions with integrable
J
’s

Suppose
\mathfrak{h}
is a real Lie algebra with a symplectic form

\omega:\mathfrak{h}\times\mathfrak{h}\to\mathbb{R}
which
is not necessarily exact as for Frobenius Lie algebras.
Suppose that
J
is a complex structure on
\mathfrak{h}
; i.e.,
N_{J}=0
.
Suppose that
\mathfrak{g}=\mathfrak{h}(D,\omega)

is the double extension of
\mathfrak{h}
by the
2
-cocycle
\omega
and a derivation
D
of the
central extension with central element
\xi
. Define
\bar{J}:\mathfrak{g}\to\mathfrak{g}
as,

\bar{J}(x)=J(x),\ \ \forall\,x\in\mathfrak{h};\quad\bar{J}(\xi)=D;\quad\bar{J}(D)=-\xi.

Proposition 2.6
.

Under the hypotheses above,
N_{\bar{J}}=0
if and only if,

\bar{J}(Dx)=D(Jx)
, for any
x\in\mathfrak{h}
.

Proof.

Clearly, for any
x
and
y
in
\mathfrak{h}
, one has
N_{\bar{J}}(x,y)=0
.
It is also easy to see that
N_{\bar{J}}(\xi,D)=0
. Similarly,

N_{\bar{J}}(x,\xi)=0
if and only if
\bar{J}(Dx)=D(Jx)
.
Finally,
N_{\bar{J}}(x,D)=0
if and only if
\bar{J}(D(Jx))=D(x)
, for any
x\in\mathfrak{h}
.
Using the fact that
J^{2}=-\operatorname{Id}_{\mathfrak{h}}
in the last equality, we conclude that

N_{\bar{J}}(x,D)=0
if and only if
\bar{J}(D(x))=D(Jx)
.
∎

3.
Double extensions of Kähler and Sasakian Lie algebras

We have seen that
in most cases
the extension processes
on Lie algebras having some kind of geometric structures,
can be performed in such a way so as to get compatible
geometric structures in the obtained extensions.
This is the guiding principle that we have followed in previous works
(e.g.,
[
16
]
,
[
19
]
or
[
20
]
) and that we shall
follow throughout this and the next section.

For example, it is now known that the double extension process applied to a given
Kähler Lie algebra
(\mathfrak{g},[\,\cdot\,,\,\cdot\,])
, produces again a Kähler Lie algebra
(see
[
9
]
or
[
10
]
). It is for this reason that in this section
we shall focus our interest in the double extension process, but
applied to Sasakian Lie algebras.

3.1.
Sasakian double extension

Let
\bar{\alpha}
be a contact structure
on a Lie algebra
(\mathfrak{g},[\,\cdot\,,\,\cdot\,])

with Reeb vector
\bar{\xi}\in\mathfrak{g}

and let
\mathfrak{g}(\theta,D)=\langle D\rangle\ltimes\mathfrak{g}_{\theta}
,
be a double extension of
\mathfrak{g}
, where
\mathfrak{g}_{\theta}
is a central
extension of
\mathfrak{g}
by a
2
-cocycle
\theta\in\wedge^{2}\mathfrak{g}^{*}
,
introducing a central element
z\in\mathfrak{g}_{\theta}
, with
z\notin\mathfrak{g}
,
and
D\in\operatorname{Der}(\mathfrak{g}_{\theta})
. Let
\lambda
be a non-zero scalar,
and consider,
\alpha=\bar{\alpha}+\lambda z^{*}\in\mathfrak{g}(\theta,D)^{*}
.
It has been proved in
[
19
]
that if

\alpha(D(z))\neq 0,

then
(\mathfrak{g}(\theta,D),\alpha)
is a contact Lie algebra.
As a matter of fact, it suffices to consider the case
\lambda=1
;
that is,
\alpha=\bar{\alpha}+z^{*}
, since
z
can be scaled as needed.
The Reeb vector would then have to be of the form,

\xi=u+a\,\bar{\xi}+b\,z
,
for some pair of scalars
a
and
b
in
\mathbb{R}
,
satisfying
a+b=1
,
and some vector
u\in\operatorname{Ker}(\bar{\alpha})
.
Fix for a moment a vector
w=c\,\bar{\xi}+d\,z\in\operatorname{Ker}(\alpha)
,
for some pair of scalars
c
and
d
, such that
c+d=0
.
Therefore, the double extension
has the vector space decomposition

\mathfrak{g}(\theta,D)=\operatorname{Ker}(\bar{\alpha})\oplus\langle w,D\rangle\oplus\langle\xi\rangle
.

Now, given
(\mathfrak{g},[\,\cdot\,,\,\cdot\,])
, with contact
1
-form
\bar{\alpha}

and Reeb vector
\bar{\xi}
,
assume there is a linear map
\bar{\Phi}\in\operatorname{End}(\mathfrak{g})
that turns

(\bar{\alpha},\bar{\xi},\bar{\Phi})
into a Sasakian structure on it.
We may extend
\bar{\Phi}
to
\Phi\in\operatorname{End}(\mathfrak{g}(\theta,D))

by letting,

[formula]

\displaystyle\Phi(x)

\displaystyle:=\bar{\Phi}(x),\quad\text{if}\quad x\in\operatorname{Ker}(\bar{\alpha});

\displaystyle\Phi(w)

\displaystyle:=D,\qquad\ \ \Phi(D):=-w;

\displaystyle\Phi(\xi)

\displaystyle:=0.

Observe that the equations

\Phi(\xi)=0
and
\Phi(w)=D
, are equivalent to,

\displaystyle a\,\Phi(\bar{\xi})+b\,\Phi(z)

\displaystyle=-\Phi(u),

\displaystyle c\,\Phi(\bar{\xi})+d\,\Phi(z)

\displaystyle=D.

If
\delta:=ad-bc\neq 0
, then,

\Phi(\bar{\xi})=-\frac{1}{\delta}\left(b\,D+d\,\Phi(u)\right),\quad\text{and,}\quad\Phi(z)=\frac{1}{\delta}\left(a\,D+c\,\Phi(u)\right).

We may now proceed to state and prove the main result of this section
using the notation and hypotheses just introduced:

Theorem 3.1
.

Let
(\bar{\xi},\bar{\alpha},\bar{\Phi})
be a Sasakian structure
on a given Lie algebra
(\mathfrak{g},[\,\cdot\,,\,\cdot\,])
, and let

\alpha
be a contact structure on a double extension

\mathfrak{g}(\theta,D)
of
\mathfrak{g}
, with
D\in\operatorname{Der}(\mathfrak{g}_{\theta})

and let
\xi\in\mathfrak{g}(\theta,D)
be its Reeb vector.
Let
z
be the central element introduced by the given
2
-cocycle
\theta
.
Write
\alpha=\bar{\alpha}+z^{*}
, and assume that the Reeb vector
has the form,
\xi=u+a\,\bar{\xi}+b\,z
,
for some pair of scalars
a
and
b
in
\mathbb{R}
,
satisfying
a+b=1
,
and some vector
u\in\operatorname{Ker}(\bar{\alpha})
.
Also assume that
w=c\,\bar{\xi}+d\,z\in\operatorname{Ker}(\alpha)
,
so that
c+d=0
.
Define
\Phi\in\operatorname{End}(\mathfrak{g}(\theta,D))
in terms of
\bar{\Phi}
as in (1) above.
Then,
(\mathfrak{g}(\theta,D),\xi,\alpha,\Phi)
is a Sasakian Lie algebra
if and only if,

(1)

\theta(\bar{\Phi}x,y)+\theta(y,\bar{\Phi}y)=0
, for any

x
and
y
in
\operatorname{Ker}(\bar{\alpha})
;

(2)

u
and
\bar{\xi}
belong to
\operatorname{Rad}(\theta)
;

(3)

D\circ\bar{\Phi}=\Phi\circ D
, on
\operatorname{Ker}(\bar{\alpha})
;

(4)

\operatorname{ad}_{\mathfrak{g}}(u)=-\bar{\Phi}\circ\operatorname{ad}_{\mathfrak{g}}(u)\circ\bar{\Phi}
;

(5)

c\,[\bar{\xi},u]+\Phi(D(\xi))=0
.

We shall prove this Theorem by a series of observations and
calculations stated as lemmas. We start by noting that
what needs to be proved is that,

\Phi^{2}=-\operatorname{Id}+\alpha\otimes\xi,

so that,
N_{\Phi}=-d\alpha\,\otimes\,\xi
,
if and only if,

M(U,V):=-[U,V]+[\Phi U,\Phi V]-\Phi([\Phi U,V])-\Phi([U,\Phi V])=0,

for any
U
and
V
in
\mathfrak{g}(\theta,D)
.
What we shall find in the following lemmas is the set of conditions
(1)-(5) above, in terms of what is needed for

M(U,V)
to vanish for any
U
and
V
in
\mathfrak{g}(\theta,D)
.
Thus we start with the following:

Lemma 3.2
.

For any
x
and
y
in
\operatorname{Ker}(\bar{\alpha})
,

M(x,y)=0\quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad\theta(\bar{\Phi}x,y)+\theta(x,\bar{\Phi}y)=0.

Proof.

Take
U=x
and
V=y
with
x
and
y
in
\operatorname{Ker}(\bar{\alpha})
. Then,
it is straightforward to see that,

\displaystyle M(x,y)

\displaystyle=-[x,y]+[\Phi x,\Phi y]-\Phi([\Phi x,y])-\Phi([x,\Phi y])

\displaystyle=-[x,y]_{\mathfrak{g}}-\theta(x,y)\,z+[\bar{\Phi}x,\bar{\Phi}y]_{\mathfrak{g}}+\theta(\bar{\Phi}x,\bar{\Phi}y)\,z

\displaystyle-\bar{\Phi}([\bar{\Phi}x,y]_{\mathfrak{g}})+\theta(\bar{\Phi}x,y)\,\Phi(z)

\displaystyle-\bar{\Phi}([x,\bar{\Phi}y]_{\mathfrak{g}})+\theta(x,\bar{\Phi}y)\,\Phi(z)

\displaystyle=-[x,y]_{\mathfrak{g}}+[\bar{\Phi}x,\bar{\Phi}y]_{\mathfrak{g}}-\bar{\Phi}([\bar{\Phi}x,y]_{\mathfrak{g}})-\bar{\Phi}([x,\bar{\Phi}y]_{\mathfrak{g}})

\displaystyle+\left(\,\theta(\bar{\Phi}x,\bar{\Phi}y)-\theta(x,y)\,\right)\,z

\displaystyle+\left(\,\theta(\bar{\Phi}x,y)+\theta(x,\bar{\Phi}y)\,\right)\,\Phi(z).

Now,
-[x,y]_{\mathfrak{g}}+[\bar{\Phi}x,\bar{\Phi}y]_{\mathfrak{g}}-\bar{\Phi}([\bar{\Phi}x,y]_{\mathfrak{g}})-\bar{\Phi}([x,\bar{\Phi}y]_{\mathfrak{g}})=0
,
because
x
and
y
lie in
\operatorname{Ker}(\bar{\alpha})\subset\mathfrak{g}
, and
\mathfrak{g}
is
Sasakian. On the other hand,
\theta(\bar{\Phi}x,\bar{\Phi}y)-\theta(x,y)=0
,
if and only if,

\theta(\bar{\Phi}x,y)+\theta(x,\bar{\Phi}y)=0
.
In particular, it suffices that one or the other vanishes to conclude the
truth of the statement.

∎

Lemma 3.3
.

Assume that
w=c\,\bar{\xi} +d\,z\in\operatorname{Ker}(\alpha)
, for
appropriate choices of the scalars
c
and
d
, and let

x
be in
\operatorname{Ker}(\bar{\alpha})
. Then,

M(x,w)=0\quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad\begin{cases}(a)\ \bar{\xi}\in\operatorname{Rad}(\theta),\ \ \text{and,}\\
(b)\ D\circ\bar{\Phi}=\Phi\circ D,\,\,\text{on\ }\operatorname{Ker}(\bar{\alpha}).\end{cases}

Proof.

Asume that
x\in\operatorname{Ker}(\bar{\alpha})
and write,
w=c\,\bar{\xi}+d\,z\in\operatorname{Ker}(\alpha)
.
Then,

\displaystyle M(x,c\,\bar{\xi}+d\,z)

\displaystyle=-[x,c\,\bar{\xi}+d\,z]+[\Phi(x),\Phi(c\,\bar{\xi}+d\,z)]

\displaystyle-\Phi([\Phi(x),c\,\bar{\xi}+d\,z])-\Phi([x,\Phi(c\,\bar{\xi}+d\,z)])

\displaystyle=-c\,[x,\bar{\xi}]+c\,[\Phi(x),\Phi(\bar{\xi})]+d\,[\Phi(x),\Phi(z)]

\displaystyle-c\,\Phi([\Phi(x),\bar{\xi}])-c\,\Phi([x,\Phi(\bar{\xi})])-d\,\Phi([x,\Phi(z)])

\displaystyle=-c\,\left([x,\bar{\xi}]_{\mathfrak{g}}+\theta(x,\bar{\xi})\,z\right)+c\,\left([\bar{\Phi}(x),-\frac{1}{\delta}(b\,D+d\,\Phi(u))]\right)

\displaystyle+d\,[\bar{\Phi}(x),\frac{1}{\delta}(a\,D+c\,\Phi(u))]-c\,\Phi\left([\bar{\Phi}(x),\bar{\xi}]_{\mathfrak{g}}+\theta(\bar{\Phi}(x),\bar{\xi})\,z\right)

\displaystyle-c\,\Phi\left([x,-\frac{1}{\delta}(b\,D+d\,\Phi(u)]\right)-d\,\Phi\left([x,\frac{1}{\delta}(a\,D+c\,\Phi(u)]\right)

\displaystyle=-c\,([x,\bar{\xi}]_{\mathfrak{g}}+\bar{\Phi}([\bar{\Phi}(x),\bar{\xi}]_{\mathfrak{g}})+\left(\frac{ad}{\delta}-\frac{bc}{\delta}\right)[\bar{\Phi}(x),D]

\displaystyle+\left(\frac{bc}{\delta}-\frac{ad}{\delta}\right)\Phi([x,D])-\frac{cd}{\delta}[\bar{\Phi}(x),\Phi(u)]

\displaystyle+\frac{cd}{\delta}[\bar{\Phi}(x),\Phi(u)]+\frac{cd}{\delta}\Phi([x,\Phi(u)])-\frac{cd}{\delta}\Phi([x,\Phi(u)])

\displaystyle-c\,\theta(x,\bar{\xi})\,z-c\,\theta(\bar{\Phi}(x),\bar{\xi})\,\Phi(z)

\displaystyle=-c\,([x,\bar{\xi}]_{\mathfrak{g}}+\bar{\Phi}([\bar{\Phi}(x),\bar{\xi}]_{\mathfrak{g}})+[\bar{\Phi}(x),D]-\Phi([x,D])

\displaystyle-c\,\theta(x,\bar{\xi})\,z-c\,\theta(\bar{\Phi}(x),\bar{\xi})\,\Phi(z).

Now,
\bar{\Phi}([\bar{\Phi}(x),\bar{\xi}]_{\mathfrak{g}})=c\,[x,\bar{\xi}]_{\mathfrak{g}}

because
\mathfrak{g}
is Sasakian. Whence,

M(x,c\,\bar{\xi}+d\,z)=[\bar{\Phi}(x),D]-\Phi([x,D])-c\,\theta(x,\bar{\xi})\,z-c\,\theta(\bar{\Phi}(x),\bar{\xi})\,\Phi(z).

Therefore, if
\bar{\xi}\in\operatorname{Rad}(\theta)
,
we conclude that
M(x,c\bar{\xi}+dz)=0
, if and only if,

D(\bar{\Phi}(x))=\Phi(D(x))
, for all
x\in\operatorname{Ker}(\bar{\alpha})
.
∎

Lemma 3.4
.

For any
x\in\operatorname{Ker}(\bar{\alpha})
,

M(x,D)=0\quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad\begin{cases}(a)\ \bar{\xi}\in\operatorname{Rad}(\theta),\ \ \text{and,}\\
(b)\ D\circ\bar{\Phi}=\Phi\circ D,\,\,\text{on\ }\operatorname{Ker}(\bar{\alpha}).\end{cases}

Proof.

Assume
x\in\operatorname{Ker}(\bar{\alpha})
, and write
\Phi(D)=-c\,\bar{\xi}-d\,z
, as before.
It follows that,

\displaystyle M(x,D)

\displaystyle=-[x,D]+[\Phi(x),\Phi(D)]-\Phi([\Phi(x),D])-\Phi([x,\Phi(D)]

\displaystyle=D(x)-c\,[\bar{\Phi}(x),\bar{\xi}]-\Phi([\bar{\Phi}(x),D])+c\,\Phi([x,\bar{\xi}])

\displaystyle=D(x)+\Phi([\bar{\Phi}(x),D])-c\,\left([\bar{\Phi}(x),\bar{\xi}]_{\mathfrak{g}}+\theta(\bar{\Phi}(x),\bar{\xi})\,z\right)

\displaystyle+c\,\Phi([x,\bar{\xi}]_{\mathfrak{g}}+\theta(x,\bar{\xi})\,z)

\displaystyle=D(x)-\Phi([\Phi(x),D])

\displaystyle+c\,(\Phi([x,\bar{\xi}]_{\mathfrak{g}})-[\bar{\Phi}(x),\bar{\xi}])

\displaystyle+c\,(\theta(x,\bar{\xi})\,\Phi(z)-\theta(\bar{\Phi}(x),\bar{\xi})\,z.

Since
\mathfrak{g}
is Sasakian,
\Phi([x,\bar{\xi}]_{\mathfrak{g}})=[\bar{\Phi}(x),\bar{\xi}]
,
and we are left with,

M(x,D)=D(x)-\Phi([\Phi(x),D])+c\,(\theta(x,\bar{\xi})\,\Phi(z)-\theta(\bar{\Phi}(x),\bar{\xi})\,z.

It follows as before that if
\bar{\xi}\in\operatorname{Rad}(\theta)
, then

M(x,D)=0
, if and only if,
D(x)=\Phi([\Phi(x),D])
; equivalently,
if and only if,
\Phi(D(x))=D(\bar{\Phi}(x))
.
∎

Lemma 3.5
.

Let
x
be in
\operatorname{Ker}(\bar{\alpha})
, and write
\xi=u+a\,\bar{\xi}+b\,z
,
with
u\in\operatorname{Ker}(\bar{\alpha})
, as before. Then,

M(x,\xi)=0\quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad\begin{cases}(a)\ u\text{\,\,and\,\,\,}\bar{\xi}\in\operatorname{Rad}(\theta),\ \ \text{and,}\\
(b)\ \operatorname{ad}_{\mathfrak{g}}(u)=-\bar{\Phi}\circ\operatorname{ad}_{\mathfrak{g}}(u)\circ\bar{\Phi}.\end{cases}

Proof.

Assume
x\in\operatorname{Ker}(\bar{\alpha})
. We know in that,
\Phi(\xi)=0
.
Whence,

\displaystyle M(x,\xi)

\displaystyle=-[x,\xi]+[\Phi(x),\Phi(\xi)]-\Phi([\Phi(x),\xi])-\Phi([x,\Phi(\xi)])

\displaystyle=-[x,u]-a\,[x,\bar{\xi}]-\Phi([\bar{\Phi}(x),u+a\,\bar{\xi}+b\,z])

\displaystyle=-([x,u]_{\mathfrak{g}}+\theta(x,u)\,z)-a\,([x,\bar{\xi}]_{\mathfrak{g}}+\theta(x,\bar{\xi})\,z)

\displaystyle-\Phi([\bar{\Phi}(x),u])-a\,\Phi([\bar{\Phi}(x),\bar{\xi}]))

\displaystyle=-([x,u]_{\mathfrak{g}}+\theta(x,u)\,z)-a\,([x,\bar{\xi}]_{\mathfrak{g}}+\theta(x,\bar{\xi})\,z)

\displaystyle-\Phi([\bar{\Phi}(x),u]_{\mathfrak{g}}+\theta(\bar{\Phi}(x),u)\,z)-a\,\Phi([\bar{\Phi}(x),\bar{\xi}]))

\displaystyle=-[x,u]_{\mathfrak{g}}-\Phi([\bar{\Phi}(x),u]_{\mathfrak{g}})

\displaystyle-(\theta(x,u)\,z+\theta(\bar{\Phi}(x),u)\,\Phi(z))-a\,\theta(x,\bar{\xi})\,z

\displaystyle-a\,([x,\bar{\xi}]_{\mathfrak{g}}+\Phi([\bar{\Phi}(x),\bar{\xi}])).

Since
\mathfrak{g}
is Sasakian,
[x,\bar{\xi}]_{\mathfrak{g}}=-\Phi([\bar{\Phi}(x),\bar{\xi}])
.
Under the assumption that
u
and
\bar{\xi}
belong to
\operatorname{Ker}(\theta)
,
it follows that

M(x,\xi)=0
, if and only if,

-[x,u]_{\mathfrak{g}}-\Phi([\bar{\Phi}(x),u]_{\mathfrak{g}})=0
.
∎

Lemma 3.6
.

If
w=c\,\bar{\xi}+d\,z\in\operatorname{Ker}(\alpha)
, then
M(w,D)=0
.

Proof.

Since
\Phi(w)=D
, and
\Phi(D)=-w
, it trivially follows that
M(w,D)=0
.

∎

Lemma 3.7
.

Given
w=c\,\bar{\xi}+d\,z\in\operatorname{Ker}(\alpha)
, and
\xi=u+a\,\bar{\xi}+b\,z
,
with
u\in\operatorname{Ker}(\bar{\alpha})
,

M(w,\xi)=0\quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad\begin{cases}(a)\ \bar{\xi}\in\operatorname{Rad}(\theta),\ \ \text{and,}\\
(b)\ c\,[\bar{\xi},u]_{\mathfrak{g}}+\Phi(D(\xi))=0.\end{cases}

Proof.

Using the fact that
\Phi(\xi)=0
, and the fact that
\Phi(w)=D
, we have,

\displaystyle M(w,\xi)

\displaystyle=-[w,\xi]+[\Phi(w),\Phi(\xi)]-\Phi([\Phi(w),\xi])-\Phi([w,\Phi(\xi)])

\displaystyle=-[c\,\bar{\xi}+d\,z,u+a\,\bar{\xi}+b\,z]-\Phi([D,\xi])

\displaystyle=-c\,[\bar{\xi},u]-\Phi(D(\xi))

\displaystyle=-c\,([\bar{\xi},u]_{\mathfrak{g}}+\theta(\bar{\xi},u)\,z)-\Phi(D(\xi)).

If
\bar{\xi}\in\operatorname{Ker}(\theta)
, the assertion follows.

∎

Lemma 3.8
.

Write,

\xi=u+a\,\bar{\xi}+b\,z
,
with
u\in\operatorname{Ker}(\bar{\alpha})
. Then,

M(D,\xi)=0\quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad\begin{cases}(a)\ \bar{\xi}\in\operatorname{Rad}(\theta),\ \ \text{and,}\\
(b)\ c\,[\bar{\xi},u]+\Phi(D(\xi))=0.\end{cases}

Proof.

Using the fact that,
\Phi(D)=-w
, and
\Phi(\xi)=0
, we have,

\displaystyle M(D,\xi)

\displaystyle=-[D,\xi]+[\Phi(D),\Phi(\xi)]-\Phi([\Phi(D),\xi])-\Phi([D,\Phi(\xi)])

\displaystyle=-D(\xi)+\Phi([c\,\bar{\xi}+d\,z,u+a\,\bar{\xi}+b\,z])

\displaystyle=-D(\xi)+c\,\Phi([\bar{\xi},u])

\displaystyle=-D(\xi)+c\,\Phi([\bar{\xi},u]_{\mathfrak{g}}+\theta(\bar{\xi},u)\,z).

Therefore, for
\bar{\xi}\in\operatorname{Rad}(\theta)
, one has,

M(D,\xi)=0
, if and only if,
-D(\xi)+c\Phi([\bar{\xi},u]_{\mathfrak{g}})=0
,
and this equation is equivalent to,
c\,[\bar{\xi},u]+\Phi(D(\xi))=0
.

∎

We have thus proved
Theorem 3.1
.
qed

4.
Extensions of Frobenius-Kähler and Sasakian Lie algebras

We are now interested in the following problem:
Given a Kähler (respectively, Sasakian) structure
on a Lie algebra
(\mathfrak{g},[\,\cdot\,,\,\cdot\,])
,
under what conditions can we
conclude that the extension

\mathfrak{g}(D)=\langle D\rangle\oplus\mathfrak{g}
, with
D\in\operatorname{Der}(\mathfrak{g})

carries a Sasakian (respectively, Kähler) structure?
This section gives precisely these conditions.

4.1.
From Frobenius-Kähler to Sasakian Lie algebras

Let
(J,\omega)
be a Kähler structure on the
Lie algebra
(\mathfrak{g},[\,\cdot\,,\,\cdot\,])
.
One says that,
\mathfrak{g}

is a Frobenius-Kähler Lie algebra

if there is some
\alpha\in\mathfrak{g}^{*}
, such that
\omega=-d\alpha
,
thus turning the pair
(\mathfrak{g},\alpha)
into a Frobenius Lie algebra.

Now, let
D\in\operatorname{Der}(\mathfrak{g})
be such that
\alpha\circ D=0
and
[D,J]=0
, and let

\mathfrak{g}(D):=\langle D\rangle\ltimes\mathfrak{g}
,
be the extension of
\mathfrak{g}
by
D
. Let
\xi\in\mathfrak{g}(D)
be
such that
D(x)=[\xi,x]
, for any
x\in\mathfrak{g}
.

With the notation and hypotheses just introduced, we shall
now state the following:

Theorem 4.1
.

Let
(J,\omega)
be a Frobenius-Kähler structure on the
Lie algebra
(\mathfrak{g},[\,\cdot\,,\,\cdot\,])
,
with
\omega=-d\alpha
for some
\alpha\in\mathfrak{g}^{*}
.
Let
D\in\operatorname{Der}(\mathfrak{g})
be chosen so that

\alpha\circ D=0
and
D\circ J=J\circ D
.
Then, the triple
(\xi,\bar{\alpha},\Phi)
turns
\mathfrak{g}(D)

into a Sasakian Lie algebra.

Proof.

Let
\bar{\alpha}:=\alpha+\xi^{*}\in\mathfrak{g}(D)^{*}
. Then,

(\mathfrak{g}(D),\bar{\alpha})
is a contact Lie algebra.
This follows easily because
d\xi^{*}=0
and
-d\alpha=\omega
.
Therefore,
\bar{\alpha}\wedge(d\bar{\alpha})^{n}=\xi^{*}\wedge\omega^{n}\neq 0
.
Now define
\Phi:\mathfrak{g}(D)\to\mathfrak{g}(D)
by means of

\Phi(x)=J(x)+\bar{\alpha}(x)\xi
, for any
x\in\mathfrak{g}
and
\Phi(\xi)=0
.
This definition of
\Phi
turns
\xi
into the Reeb vector
associated to
\bar{\alpha}
. Now,
J^{2}=-\operatorname{Id}
, implies that,

\Phi^{2}=-\operatorname{Id}+\bar{\alpha}\otimes\xi
.
Thus, all what we need to prove is that,

N_{\Phi}=-d\bar{\alpha}\otimes\xi.

To prove this fact, recall first that
for any pair
u
and
v
in
\mathfrak{g}(D)
,

N_{\Phi}(u,v)=\Phi^{2}[u,v]+[\Phi(u),\Phi(v)]-\Phi([\Phi u,v])-\Phi([u,\Phi v]).

Since,
\Phi^{2}[u,v]=-[u,v]+\bar{\alpha}([u,v])\xi
, the proof of (2)
comes down to show that, for any
u
and
v
in
\mathfrak{g}(D)
,

-[u,v]+[\Phi(u),\Phi(v)]-\Phi([\Phi u,v])-\Phi([u,\Phi v])=0.

For
u=x
, and
v=y
, both in
\mathfrak{g}
, one has,

\displaystyle-[x,y]

\displaystyle+[\Phi(x),\Phi(y)]-\Phi([\Phi x,y])-\Phi([x,\Phi y])=

\displaystyle=[Jx,Jy]+\bar{\alpha}(x)[\xi,Jy]+\bar{\alpha}(y)[Jx,\xi]

\displaystyle-J([Jx,y])-\bar{\alpha}([Jx,y])\xi-\bar{\alpha}(x)J([\xi,y])-\bar{\alpha}(x)\bar{\alpha}([\xi,y])\xi

\displaystyle-J([x,Jy])-\bar{\alpha}([x,Jy])\xi-\bar{\alpha}(y)J([x,\xi])-\bar{\alpha}(y)\bar{\alpha}([x,\xi])\xi

\displaystyle=N_{J}(x,y)+\bar{\alpha}(x)[D,J](y)-\bar{\alpha}(y)[J,D](x)

\displaystyle+\left(d\alpha(Jx,y)+d\alpha(x,Jy)+\bar{\alpha}(x)d\bar{\alpha}(\xi,y)+\bar{\alpha}(x)d\bar{\alpha}(x,\xi)\right)\xi

\displaystyle=0.

On the other hand, for
u=x\in\mathfrak{g}
, and
v=\xi
, one has,

\displaystyle N_{\Phi}(x,\xi)

\displaystyle=\Phi^{2}[x,\xi]+[\Phi(x),\Phi(\xi)]-\Phi([\Phi x,\xi])-\Phi([x,\Phi\xi])

\displaystyle=-[x,\xi]-\Phi([Jx+\bar{\alpha}(x)\xi,\xi])

\displaystyle=-[x,\xi]-\Phi([Jx,\xi])=-[x,\xi]-J([Jx,\xi])

\displaystyle=-[x,\xi]+J(D(Jx))=-[x,\xi]-[\xi,x]

\displaystyle=0.

∎

4.2.
From Sasakian to Frobenius-Kähler Lie algebras

Theorem 4.2
.

Let
(\xi,\alpha,\Phi)
be a Sasakian structure
on the Lie algebra
(\mathfrak{g},[\,\cdot\,,\,\cdot\,])
.
Let
D\in\operatorname{Der}(\mathfrak{g})
be such that
\alpha\circ D=\alpha
, and
assume that
[\Phi,D]|_{\operatorname{Ker}(\alpha)}=0
. Consider the Lie algebra

\mathfrak{g}(D)=\langle D\rangle\ltimes\mathfrak{g}
, and let
x_{P}
be
the unique element in
\mathfrak{g}(D)
such that,

[x_{P},x]=D(x)
, for any
x\in\mathfrak{g}
. Now define

J:\mathfrak{g}(D)\to\mathfrak{g}(D)
by letting

J(x)=\Phi(x)+\alpha(x)x_{P}
, and
J(x_{P})=-\xi
.
Further define,
\bar{\alpha}=\alpha+x_{P}^{*}\in\mathfrak{g}(D)^{*}
.
Then,
(\mathfrak{g}(D),\bar{\alpha})

is a Frobenius-Kähler Lie algebra.

Proof.

The condition
\alpha\circ D=\alpha
implies that

\bar{\alpha}=\alpha+x_{P}^{*}
is a Frobenius structure
on
\mathfrak{g}(D)
(see
[
3
]
),
making
\omega=-d\bar{\alpha}
symplectic.
We shall now prove that for the defined
J:\mathfrak{g}(D)\to\mathfrak{g}(D)
,

N_{J}(u,v)=0
, for any
u
and
v
in
\mathfrak{g}(D)
.
Observe that
\mathfrak{g}(D)
has the vector space decomposition,

\mathfrak{g}(D)=\operatorname{Ker}(\alpha)\oplus\langle\xi\rangle\oplus\langle x_{P}\rangle.

Assume first that
u=x
and
v=y
lie in
\operatorname{Ker}(\alpha)
. Then,

\displaystyle N_{J}(x,y)

\displaystyle=-[x,y]+[Jx,Jy]-J([Jx,y])-J([x,Jy])

\displaystyle=\Phi^{2}([x,y])-\alpha([x,y])\xi+[\Phi(x)+\alpha(x)x_{P},\Phi(y)+\alpha(y)x_{P}]

\displaystyle-J([\Phi(x)+\alpha(x)x_{P},y])-J([x,\Phi(y)+\alpha(y)x_{P}])

\displaystyle=\Phi^{2}([x,y])-\alpha([x,y])\xi+[\Phi(x),\Phi(y)]+\alpha(y)[\Phi(x),x_{P}]

\displaystyle+\alpha(x)[x_{P},\phi(y)]-\Phi([\Phi(x),y])-\alpha([\Phi(x),y])x_{P}

\displaystyle-\alpha(x)\Phi([x_{P},y])-\alpha(x)\alpha([x_{P},y])x_{P}-\Phi([x,\Phi(y)])

\displaystyle-\alpha([x,\Phi(y)])x_{P}-\alpha(y)\Phi([x,x_{P}])-\alpha(y)\alpha([x,x_{P}])x_{P}

\displaystyle=N_{\Phi}(x,y)+d\alpha(x,y)+\alpha(y)([\Phi(x),x_{P}]-\Phi([x,x_{P}]))

\displaystyle+\alpha(x)([x_{P},\Phi(y)]-\Phi([x_{P},y]))

\displaystyle-(\alpha(y)\alpha([x,x_{P}])+\alpha(x)\alpha([x_{P},y]))x_{P}

\displaystyle-(\alpha([x,\Phi(y)])+\alpha([\Phi(x),y])x_{P}

\displaystyle=0.

On the other hand, now assume

u=x\in\operatorname{Ker}(\alpha)
and
v=x_{P}
. Then,

\displaystyle N_{J}(x,x_{P})

\displaystyle=-[x,x_{P}]+[Jx,Jx_{P}]-J([Jx,x_{P}])-J([x,Jx_{P}])

\displaystyle=[x,x_{P}]+[\Phi(x)+\alpha(x)x_{P},-\xi]-J([\Phi(x)+\alpha(x)x_{P},x_{P}])

\displaystyle-J([x,-\xi])

\displaystyle=-[x,x_{P}]-[\Phi(x),\xi]-\alpha(x)\xi-\Phi([\Phi(x),x_{P}])-\alpha([\Phi(x),x_{P}])x_{P}

\displaystyle+\Phi([x,\xi])+\alpha([x,\xi])x_{P}

\displaystyle=-([x,x_{P}]+\Phi([\Phi(x),x_{P}]))-([\Phi(x),\xi]-\Phi([x,\xi]))

\displaystyle+(\alpha([x,\xi])-\alpha([\Phi(x),x_{P}]))x_{P}-\alpha(x)\xi

\displaystyle=0.

Finally, since
[x_{P},\xi]=\xi
,
J(\xi)=x_{P}
, and
J(x_{P})=-\xi
,
one trivially has,
N_{J}(x_{P},\xi)=0
.
∎

Corollary 4.3
.

Let
(\alpha,J)
be a Frobenius-Kähler structure on the Lie algebra

(\mathfrak{g},[\,\cdot\,,\,\cdot\,])
with symplectic form
\omega=-d\alpha
.
Let
\mathfrak{g}\oplus\langle\xi\rangle

be the central extension of
\mathfrak{g}
by a central element
\xi
via the
2
-cocycle
defined by
\omega
. Suppose there is a derivation

D\in\operatorname{Der}(\mathfrak{g}\oplus\langle\xi\rangle)
satisfying
[D,J]=0
,
and
\xi^{*}\circ D=\xi^{*}
.
Then the double extension
\mathfrak{g}(D,\omega)
of
\mathfrak{g}

is a Frobenius- Kähler Lie algebra.

Notation.

Let
(\alpha,J)
be a Frobenius-Kähler structure
on a Lie algebra
(\mathfrak{g},[\,\cdot\,,\,\cdot\,])

and let
x_{P}
be its principal vector.
We know that
\mathfrak{g}=\langle x_{P}\rangle\ltimes\mathfrak{h}
,
where
\mathfrak{h}
is a codimension
1
unimodular contact ideal
(see
[
16
]
) whose contact structure is just the
restriction
\alpha|_{\mathfrak{h}}
.
Let
i:\mathfrak{h}\hookrightarrow\mathfrak{g}
be the natural inclusion and
let
\xi
be the Reeb vector associated to
\bar{\alpha}:=i^{*}\alpha
.
Then
\mathfrak{h}
can be decomposed as
\mathfrak{h}=\langle\xi\rangle\oplus\operatorname{Ker}(\bar{\alpha})

and one may define the linear endomorphism
\Phi:\mathfrak{h}\to\mathfrak{h}
, by letting

\Phi(\xi)=0
and
\Phi(x)=J(x)
for any
x\in\operatorname{Ker}(\bar{\alpha})
.
Arguing as before, we can state the following:

Theorem 4.4
.

With the hypotheses of the last paragraph,

(\xi,\alpha,\Phi)
is a Sasakian structure on
\mathfrak{h}

if and only if
[\operatorname{ad}(\xi),\Phi]=0
.

Remark 4.5
.

In any Frobenius-Kähler Lie algebra it is true that

[x_{P},\xi]=\xi
.
If
J(\xi)=x_{P}
, it follows that
[\operatorname{ad}(\xi),\Phi]=0
if and only if

[\operatorname{ad}(x_{P}),\Phi]=0
, but
[\operatorname{ad}(x_{P}),\Phi]
is zero only
on
\operatorname{Ker}(\bar{\alpha})
.

Corollary 4.6
.

With the notation and hypotheses above,

\mathfrak{h}
is a Sasakian Lie algebra if and only if

[\operatorname{ad}(x_{P}),\Phi]=0
on
\operatorname{Ker}(\bar{\alpha})
.

5.
Examples

Here are two examples of what we have done so far.

Let
\mathfrak{h}_{3}=\operatorname{Span}_{\mathbb{R}}\{e_{1},e_{2},e_{3}\}
be the
Heisenberg Lie algebra with its Lie bracket defined by

[e_{1},e_{2}]=e_{3}
,
and
[e_{i},e_{3}]=0
, for
i=1
or
2
.
Let
\{e^{1},e^{2},e^{3}\}
be the dual basis to
\{e_{1},e_{2},e_{3}\}
.
It is well known that
\mathfrak{h}_{3}
is a Sasakian Lie algebra.
Its contact structure is defined by
\alpha=e^{3}\in\mathfrak{h}_{3}^{*}
.
Its Reeb vector is defined by
\xi=e_{3}
, and the linear
endomorphism
\Phi:\mathfrak{h}_{3}\to\mathfrak{h}_{3}
may be defined by

\Phi(e_{1})=e_{2}
,
\Phi(e_{2})=-e_{1}
, and
\Phi(e_{3})=0
.

Let
D\in\operatorname{Der}(\mathfrak{h}_{3})
be defined
in such a way that
\alpha\circ D=\alpha
, for
\alpha=e^{3}
.
Write,
D(e_{j})=D_{1j}e_{1}+D_{2j}e_{2}+D_{3j}e_{3}
,
for
1\leq j\leq 3
. Then,

\alpha\circ D=\alpha\quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad D_{3j}=\delta_{3j}.

Moreover, since
D\in\operatorname{Der}(\mathfrak{h}_{3})
, one may compute

D([e_{i},e_{j}])
for
1\leq i<j\leq 3
and conclude that

D_{11}+D_{22}=1
, and
D_{13}=0=D_{23}
.
In particular, we may fix
D\in\operatorname{Der}(\mathfrak{h}_{3})
satisfying

\alpha\circ D=\alpha
by letting,

D(e_{1})=\displaystyle{\frac{1}{2}}\,e_{1},\qquad D(e_{2})=\displaystyle{\frac{1}{2}}\,e_{2},\qquad D(e_{3})=\,e_{3}.

Let
e_{4}
be the
unique element in
\mathfrak{h}_{3}(D)
satisfying
[e_{4},e_{i}]=D(e_{i})
for
any
1\leq i\leq 3
, so that
x_{P}=e_{4}
. Whence,
[x_{P},\xi]=\xi
;
equivalently,
[e_{4},e_{3}]=e_{3}
.

Now, compute
d\alpha
. It is clear that,

\displaystyle de^{3}(e_{1},e_{2})

\displaystyle=-e^{3}([e_{1},e_{2}])=-1,

\displaystyle de^{3}(e_{3},e_{4})

\displaystyle=-e^{3}([e_{3},e_{4}])=1,

\displaystyle de^{3}(e_{i},e_{j})

\displaystyle=0,\quad\text{for all others\ \ }1\leq i<j\leq 4.

Therefore,

d\alpha=de^{3}=e^{1}\wedge e^{2}-e^{3}\wedge e^{4}.

It follows that
\mathfrak{h}_{3}(D)
is a Frobenius-Kähler Lie algebra with
symplectic (exact) form,

\omega=-d\alpha=-e^{1}\wedge e^{2}+e^{3}\wedge e^{4}
.

Remark.

We are following the convention that, for any Lie algebra
\mathfrak{g}
,
each
\chi\in\mathfrak{g}^{*}
defines a degree
-1
derivation
i_{\chi}\in\operatorname{Der}(\wedge\mathfrak{g})
,
with the property that,
i_{\chi}(x)=\chi(x)
for any
x\in\mathfrak{g}
. Then,
using the universal property of the exterior algebra
\wedge\mathfrak{g}^{*}
,
one may identify
i_{\chi_{1}}\circ\cdots\circ i_{\chi_{k}}
with

{\chi_{1}}\wedge\cdots\wedge{\chi_{k}}\in\wedge\mathfrak{g}^{*}
, for a collection
of elements
\chi_{j}\in\mathfrak{g}^{*}
,
1\leq j\leq\operatorname{dim}(\mathfrak{g})
. In particular,

\displaystyle(\chi_{1}\wedge\chi_{2})(x_{1}\wedge x_{2})

\displaystyle=(i_{\chi_{1}}\circ i_{\chi_{2}})(x_{1}\wedge x_{2})

\displaystyle=i_{\chi_{1}}(i_{\chi_{2}}(x_{1})\,x_{2}-i_{\chi_{2}}(x_{2})\,x_{1})

\displaystyle=i_{\chi_{1}}({\chi_{2}}(x_{1})\,x_{2}-{\chi_{2}}(x_{2})\,x_{1})

\displaystyle=\chi_{2}(x_{1})\,\chi_{1}(x_{2})-\chi_{2}(x_{2})\,\chi_{1}(x_{1})

\displaystyle=-\det{\begin{pmatrix}\chi_{1}(x_{1})&\chi_{1}(x_{2})\\
\chi_{2}(x_{1})&\chi_{2}(x_{2})\\
\end{pmatrix}}.

We now want to define a complex structure
J\in\operatorname{End}(\mathfrak{h}_{3}(D))

by extending
\Phi(e_{1})=J(e_{1})=e_{2}
, and
\Phi(e_{2})=J(e_{2})=-e_{1}
,
in such a way so as to adapt it to the underlying Hermitian metric
so that
\omega(x,y)=g(x,Jy)
. Therefore, define
J(e_{3})=-e_{4}

and
J(e_{4})=e_{3}
.

Remark 5.1
.

The Lie algebra
\mathfrak{h}_{3}(D)
just described, is the Lie algebra
denoted by
D_{4,1/2}
in
[
18
]
.

Construction.

Now, starting with the Frobenius-Kähler Lie algebra
\mathfrak{h}_{3}(D)
,
we shall now produce a
5
-dimensional Sasakian Lie algebra
with trivial center. In order to do this, it suffices to choose a
derivation
E\in\operatorname{Der}(\mathfrak{h}_{3}(D))
, satisfying

E(e_{1})=-e_{2}
,
E(e_{2})=e_{1}
, and,
E(e_{i})=0
for
i=3,4
.
Observe that
\alpha\circ E=0
, and that
[E,J]=0
. Therefore,

\mathfrak{h}_{3}(D,E):=(\mathfrak{h}_{3}(D))(E)
is a Sasakian Lie algebra
(see
Theorem
4.1
).

Remark 5.2
.

The Lie algebra
\mathfrak{h}_{3}(D,E)
just described, is the Lie algebra
denoted by
\mathfrak{g}_{0}
in
[
2
]
.

On the other hand, since
\mathfrak{h}_{3}(D)
is a Frobenius-Kähler Lie algebra
with Frobenius structure given by
\alpha=e^{3}
, we may produce the
central extension of
\mathfrak{h}_{3}(D)
defined by the
2
-cocyle

\omega=-d\alpha
.
This construction yields again a Sasakian Lie algebra; this time, however,
with non-trivial center.

Remark 5.3
.

The central extension of
\mathfrak{h}_{3}(D)
defined by the 2-cocycle

\omega=-d\alpha
, is the Lie algebra denoted by

\mathfrak{g}_{5}
in
Theorem 10
of
[
2
]
.

Observe that if one starts with a Sasakian Lie algebra
\mathfrak{g}

defined by a given triple
(\xi,\alpha,\Phi)
, it seems easier to
construct first a Frobenius-Kähler Lie algebra
\mathfrak{g}(D)
through
a principal derivation
D\in\operatorname{Der}(\mathfrak{g})
satisfying
[D,\Phi]=0
,
and then proceed to find a central extension of
\mathfrak{g}(D)

by means of the
2
-cocycle defined by its symplectic
form
\omega
. In this way one would be producing
a
reversed double extension,
in the sense that
one would adjoin the derivation
D
first to obtain
the Frobenius-Kähler Lie algebra
\mathfrak{g}(D)
, and perform
the central extension by its
2
-cocyle afterwards.
That is, the operations are done exactly in the reverse
order as they were originally performed by V. Kaç to
produce his double extension procedure.

Remark 5.4
.

It has been proved in
[
3
]
that there are contact Lie algebras
that do not admit principal derivations. Thus, if one starts with such
a Lie algebra that furthermore happens to be Sasakian,
then one cannot perform on it the reversed double extension
procedure to produce again a new (2 dimensions bigger) Sasakian Lie algebra.
