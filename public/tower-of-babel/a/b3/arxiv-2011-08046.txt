Risk-Constrained Thompson Sampling for Gaussian CVaR Bandits
Joel Q. L. Chang, Qiuyu Zhu, Vincent Y. F. Tan

Abstract
The multi-armed bandit (MAB) problem is a ubiquitous decision-making problem that exemplifies the exploration-exploitation tradeoff. Standard formulations exclude risk in decision making. Risk notably complicates the basic reward-maximising objective, in part because there is no universally agreed definition of it. In this paper, we consider a popular risk measure in quantitative finance known as the Conditional Value at Risk (CVaR). We explore the performance of a Thompson Sampling-based algorithm CVaR-TS under this risk measure. We provide comprehensive comparisons between our regret bounds with state-of-the-art L/UCB-based algorithms in comparable settings and demonstrate their clear improvement in performance. We show that the regret bounds of CVaR-TS approach instance-dependent lower bounds for the same problem in some parameter regimes. We also perform numerical simulations to empirically verify that CVaR-TS significantly outperforms other L/UCB-based algorithms in terms of regret and also in flagging an infeasible instance as feasible and vice versa.



1
Introduction

The multi-armed bandit (MAB) problem analyses sequential decision making, where the learner has access to partial feedback. This problem is applicable to a variety of real-world applications, such as clinical trials, online advertisement, network routing, and resource allocation. In the well-known stochastic MAB setting, a player chooses among
K
arms, each characterised by an independent reward distribution. During each period, the player plays one arm and observes a random reward from that arm, incorporates the information in choosing the next arm to select. The player repeats the process for a horizon containing
n
periods. In each period, the player faces a dilemma whether to explore the potential value of other arms or to exploit the arm that the player believes offers the highest estimated reward.

In each of the above-mentioned applications, risk is not taken into account. In this regard, the MAB problem should be explored in a more sophisticated setting, where the player wants to maximise one’s reward while minimising one’s risk incurred, subject to a “maximum risk” condition. This paper proposes the first Thompson sampling-based learning algorithm which minimises the
Conditional Value at Risk (CVaR)
risk measure
(
Rockafellar & Uryasev 2000
)
, also known as the
expected shortfall (ES)
.

1.1
Related Work

Various analyses of MABs involving risk measures have been undertaken.

Sani et al. 2012
considered the
mean-variance
as their risk measure. Each arm
i
was distributed according to a Gaussian with mean
\mu_{i}\in[0,1]
and variance
\sigma_{i}^{2}\in[0,1]
. The authors provided an LCB-based algorithm with accompanying regret analyses.

Galichet et al. 2013
proposed the Multi-Armed Risk-Aware Bandit (
MaRaB
) algorithm with the objective of minimising the number of pulls of risky arms, using CVaR as their risk measure.

Vakili & Zhao 2016
showed that the instance-dependent and instance-independent regrets in terms of the mean-variance of the reward process over a horizon
n
are lower bounded by
\Omega(\log n)
and
\Omega(n^{2/3})
respectively.

Sun et al. 2017
considered contextual bandits with risk constraints, and developed a meta algorithm utilizing the online mirror descent algorithm which achieves near-optimal regret in terms of minimizing the total cost.

Huo & Fu 2017
studied applications of risk-aware MAB into portfolio selection, achieving a balance between risk and return.

Khajonchotpanya et al. 2020
similarly used CVaR as their risk measure and proposed the CVaR-UCB algorithm, which chooses the arm with the highest gap with respect to the arm with the highest CVaR. Note that the authors associated the highest CVaR to the highest reward, while our paper considers the highest CVaR to result in the highest loss. Thus, the discussions in
Khajonchotpanya et al. 2020
apply analogously.

The paper closest to our work is that by
Kagrecha et al. 2020a
, who regard a large CVaR as corresponding to a large loss incurred by the user. They proposed and analysed RC-LCB and considered the scenario where users have a predetermined risk tolerance level
\tau
. Any arm whose CVaR exceeds
\tau
is deemed infeasible. This motivates the authors to define three different regrets, each corresponding to the number of times the non-optimal arms of different classes (e.g., feasible/infeasible) were pulled. This problem formulation also includes that of
Khajonchotpanya et al. 2020
in the “infeasible instance” case. Having drawn inspiration from
Kagrecha et al. 2020a
in terms of the problem setup, in Section
2
, we provide definitions of these classes of arms later which were originally defined in their paper.

Zhu & Tan 2020
provided a Thompson sampling-based algorithm for the scenario in
Sani et al. 2012
, where the arm distributions are Gaussian or Bernoulli. Our paper seeks to explore the efficacy of Thompson sampling in the problem setting proposed by
Kagrecha et al. 2020a
and to demonstrate, theoretically and empirically, the improvement of Thompson sampling over confidence bound-based approaches. Finally, we note the independent and contemporaneous work by
Baudry et al. 2020
on designing and analyzing Thompson sampling-based algorithms
\alpha
-NPTS for
bounded
rewards and
\alpha
-Multinomial-TS for
discrete multinomial distributions
; we consider a different class of reward distributions (Gaussian whose support is unbounded) here and also
three
different regret criteria (Definition
3
).

2
Problem formulation

In this section we define the CVaR MAB problem. Throughout the paper, denote
[m]=\left\{{1,\dots,m}\right\}
for any
m\in\mathbb{N}
and
{(x)}^{+}=\max\left\{{0,x}\right\}
for
x\in\mathbb{R}
.

Definition 1
.

For any random variable
X
, given a confidence level
\alpha\in[0,1)
, we define the Value at Risk (VaR) and Conditional Value at Risk (CVaR) metrics of
X
by

\displaystyle v_{\alpha}(X)

\displaystyle={\inf\left\{{v\in\mathbb{R}:\mathbb{P}(X\leq v)\geq\alpha}\right\}},\ \text{and}

(1)

\displaystyle c_{\alpha}(X)

\displaystyle={v_{\alpha}(X)+\frac{1}{1-\alpha}\mathbb{E}\big[{(X-v_{\alpha}(X))}^{+}}\big].

(2)

As explained by
Kagrecha et al. 2020a
,
v_{\alpha}(X)
is the worst case loss corresponding to a confidence level
\alpha
, which is usually taken to be in the set
[0.90,1)

(
Rockafellar & Uryasev 2000
)
, where
X
is the loss associated with a portfolio. Working with a continuous cumulative distribution function (CDF)
F_{X}(\cdot)
of
X
that is strictly increasing over its support, direct computations give
c_{\alpha}(X)=\mathbb{E}[X|X\geq v_{\alpha}(X)]
, and thus
c_{\alpha}(X)
can be interpreted as the expected loss given that the loss exceeds
v_{\alpha}(X)
.
We remark that CVaR is usually preferred to VaR as a risk measure since it is coherent
(
Artzner et al. 1999
)
and satisfies more mathematically useful properties for analysis. In our paper, we will be working with Gaussian random variables
X\sim\mathcal{N}(\mu,\sigma^{2})
whose CDF satisfies the continuity and strict monotonicity assumptions of
F_{X}(\cdot)
. Letting
\Phi
denote the CDF of
Z\sim\mathcal{N}(0,1)
, direct computations using (
1
) and (
2
) yield

\displaystyle c_{\alpha}(Z)

\displaystyle={\frac{1}{(1-\alpha)\sqrt{2\pi}}\exp\Big(-\frac{1}{2}\left({\Phi^{-1}(\alpha)}\right)^{2}}\Big),

(3)

\displaystyle c_{\alpha}(X)

\displaystyle={\mu\Big(\frac{\alpha}{1-\alpha}\Big)+\sigma c_{\alpha}(Z)}.

(4)

In the rest of the paper, we denote
c_{\alpha}^{*}=c_{\alpha}(Z)
. Intuitively, by (
3
), as
\alpha\to 1^{-}
, we have
c_{\alpha}^{*}\to+\infty
, i.e., the CVaR of the standard Gaussian arm increases without bound, and the arm gets riskier as the user demands higher confidence. However, since
(\sigma c_{\alpha}^{*})/\big(\mu\cdot{\frac{\alpha}{1-\alpha}}\big)\to 0
as
\alpha\to 1^{-}
, we have that
\frac{\alpha}{1-\alpha}\to+\infty
faster than
c_{\alpha}^{*}
. Thus, for
\alpha
sufficiently close to
1^{-}
,
c_{\alpha}(X)\approx\mu\big(\frac{\alpha}{1-\alpha}\big)
and the problem reduces to a standard (risk-neutral, cost minimization or reward maximization)
K
-armed MAB problem.

Consider a
K
-armed MAB
\nu=({\nu(i)})_{i\in[K]}
and a player with a known risk threshold
\tau>0
that represents her risk appetite. The CVaR MAB problem is played over a horizon of length
n
. Roughly speaking, our goal is to choose the arm with the lowest average loss, subject to an upper bound on the risk (measured by the CVaR) associated with the arm. For any arm
i
, the loss associated with arm
i
has distribution
\nu(i)
and
X(i)
denotes a random variable with distribution
\nu(i)
. Furthermore,
\mu(i)=\mu_{i}
denotes the mean of
X(i)
, and
c_{\alpha}(i)
denotes the CVaR of
X(i)
, as defined in (
4
). Similar to
Kagrecha et al. 2020a
, we define feasible and infeasible instances as follows.

Definition 2

(
Kagrecha et al. 2020a
)
.

An
instance
of the risk-constrained MAB problem is defined by
(\nu,\tau)
. We denote the set of feasible arms (whose CVaR
\leq\tau
) as
\mathcal{K}_{\tau}=\left\{{i\in[K]:c_{\alpha}(i)\leq\tau}\right\}
. The instance
(\nu,\tau)
is said to be
feasible
(resp.
infeasible
) if
\mathcal{K}_{\tau}\neq\emptyset
(resp.
\mathcal{K}_{\tau}=\emptyset
).

In a feasible instance, an arm
i
is
optimal
if
c_{\alpha}(i)\leq\tau
and
\mu_{i}=\min_{j\in\mathcal{K}_{\tau}}\mu_{j}
. Suppose arm
1
is optimal and
\mathrm{arg\ min}_{i\in\mathcal{K}_{\tau}}\mu_{i}=\left\{{1}\right\}
for simplicity. Define the set
{\cal M}:=\left\{{i\in{[K]}\backslash{\left\{{1}\right\}}:\mu_{i}>\mu_{1}}\right\}
.
Arm
i
is said to be

\begin{cases}\text{\emph{a suboptimal arm}}&\text{if $i\in{\cal M}\cap\mathcal{K}_{\tau}$,}\\
\text{\emph{an infeasible arm}}&\text{if $i\in\mathcal{K}_{\tau}^{c}$,}\\
\text{\emph{a deceiver arm}}&\text{if $i\in{\cal M}^{c}\cap\mathcal{K}_{\tau}^{c}$.}\end{cases}

For a suboptimal arm
i
, we define the
suboptimality gap
as
\Delta(i)=\mu_{i}-\mu_{1}>0
. For an infeasible arm
i
, we define the
infeasibility gap
as
\Delta_{\tau}(i,\alpha)=c_{\alpha}(i)-\tau>0
.

In an infeasible instance, without loss of generality, set arm
1
as an
optimal
arm, that is,
c_{\alpha}(1)=\min_{i\in[K]}c_{\alpha}(i)
. For simplicity we suppose
\mathrm{arg\ min}_{i\in[K]}c_{\alpha}(i)=\left\{{1}\right\}
. We define the
risk gap
for an arm
i
that is not optimal by
\Delta_{r}(i,\alpha)=c_{\alpha}(i)-c_{\alpha}(1)>0
. We remark that it is in this infeasible instance that the risk gap is defined naturally by
Khajonchotpanya et al. 2020
. With the three gaps defined, we can now define three regrets in different settings.

Definition 3
.

Let
T_{i,n}
denote the number of times arm
i
was pulled in the first
n
rounds.

1.

For a feasible instance, let

\mathcal{K}^{*}=\left\{{i\in[K]:c_{\alpha}(i)\leq\tau\ \text{and}\ \mu_{i}=\min_{j\in\mathcal{K}_{\tau}}\mu_{j}}\right\}

denote the set of optimal arms (and without loss of generality suppose
1\in\mathcal{K}^{*}
). The
suboptimality regret
of policy
\pi
over
n
rounds is

\mathcal{R}_{n}^{\mathsf{sub}}(\pi)=\sum_{i\in{\mathcal{K}_{\tau}}\backslash{\mathcal{K}^{*}}}\mathbb{E}[T_{i,n}]\Delta(i),

(5)

and the
infeasibility regret
of policy
\pi
over
n
rounds is

\mathcal{R}_{n}^{\mathsf{inf}}(\pi)=\sum_{i\in\mathcal{K}_{\tau}^{c}}\mathbb{E}[T_{i,n}]\Delta_{\tau}(i,\alpha).

(6)

2.

For an infeasible instance, let

\mathcal{K}^{*}=\left\{{i\in[K]:c_{\alpha}(i)=\min_{j\in[K]}c_{\alpha}(j)}\right\}

denote the set of optimal arms (and without loss of generality suppose
1\in\mathcal{K}^{*}
). The
risk regret
of policy
\pi
over
n
rounds is

\mathcal{R}_{n}^{\mathsf{risk}}(\pi)=\sum_{i\in{[K]}\backslash{\mathcal{K}^{*}}}\mathbb{E}[T_{i,n}]\Delta_{\mathrm{r}}(i,\alpha).

(7)

In the following, we design and analyse CVaR-TS which aims to simultaneously minimize the three regrets in (
5
), (
6
), and (
7
). To assess the optimality of CVaR-TS, we define the notion of consistency in this risk-constrained setting.

Definition 4
.

A policy
\pi
is suboptimality-consistent if
\mathcal{R}_{n}^{\mathsf{sub}}(\pi)=o(n^{a})
for any
a>0
. Similarly,
\pi
is infeasibility-consistent (resp. risk-consistent) if
\mathcal{R}_{n}^{\mathsf{inf}}(\pi)=o(n^{a})
(resp.
\mathcal{R}_{n}^{\mathsf{risk}}(\pi)=o(n^{a})
) for any
a>0
.

3
The CVaR-TS Algorithm

In this section, we introduce the CVaR Thompson Sampling (CVaR-TS) algorithm for Gaussian bandits with bounded variances, i.e.,
\nu\in{\cal E}_{\cal N}^{K}(\sigma_{\max}^{2})
where

\displaystyle{\cal E}_{\cal N}^{K}(\sigma_{\max}^{2})

\displaystyle=\big\{\nu=(\nu_{1},\dots,\nu_{K}):

\displaystyle\qquad\nu_{i}\sim{\cal N}(\mu_{i},\sigma_{i}^{2}),\sigma_{i}^{2}\leq\sigma_{\max}^{2},\forall\,i\in[K]\big\}

for some
\sigma_{\max}^{2}>1
.
Instead of choosing the arm based on the optimism in the face of uncertainty principle as in
Kagrecha et al. 2020a
, the algorithm samples from the posteriors of each arm, then chooses the arm according to a multi-criterion procedure.

As is well known, a crucial step of Thompson sampling algorithms is the updating of parameters based on Bayes rule. Denote the mean and precision of the Gaussian by
\mu
and
\phi
respectively. If
(\mu,\phi)\sim\mathrm{Normal}\text{-}\mathrm{Gamma}(\mu,T,\alpha,\beta)
, then
\phi\sim\mathrm{Gamma}(\alpha,\beta)
, and
\mu|\phi\sim\mathcal{N}(\mu,1/(\phi T))
. Since the Normal-Gamma distribution is the conjugate prior for the Gaussian with unknown mean and variance, we use Algorithm
1
to update
(\mu,\phi)
.

Regret Bounds of CVaR-TS and Lower Bounds

We present our regret bounds in the following theorems. We then compare our bounds to those of other competing algorithms. Most comparisons of results are with respect to Theorem
1

Theorem 1
.

Fix

\alpha\in(1/2,1)

\displaystyle\limsup_{n\to\infty}\frac{\mathcal{R}_{n}^{\mathsf{risk}}(\text{CVaR-TS})}{\log n}\leq\sum_{i\in{[K]}\backslash{\mathcal{K}^{*}}}C_{\alpha,\xi}^{i}\Delta_{\mathrm{r}}(i,\alpha),

where

\displaystyle A_{\alpha,\xi}^{i}

\displaystyle=\frac{2\alpha^{2}}{\xi^{2}{(1-\alpha)}^{2}\Delta_{r}^{2}(i,\alpha)},

(8)

\displaystyle B_{\alpha,\xi}^{i}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{h\left({\frac{\sigma_{i}^{2}{(c_{\alpha}^{*})}^{2}}{{\left({\sigma_{i}c_{\alpha}^{*}-(1-\xi)\Delta_{\mathrm{r}}(i,\alpha)}\right)}^{2}}}\right)},\quad\mbox{and}

\displaystyle h(x)

\displaystyle=\textstyle\frac{{1}}{{2}}(x-1-\log x).

Proof Outline of Theorem
1
and Extensions

We outline the proof of Theorem
1
, since it is the most involved. Theorem
2
follows by a straightforward substitution and Theorem
3
is analogous to the proof of Thompson sampling for standard MAB. Denote the sample CVaR (the Thompson sample) as
\hat{c}_{\alpha}(i,t)=\theta_{i,t}\Big(\frac{\alpha}{1-\alpha}\Big)+\frac{1}{\sqrt{\kappa_{i,t}}}c_{\alpha}^{*}
. Fix
\varepsilon>0
and define
E_{i}(t):=\left\{{\hat{c}_{\alpha}(i,t)>c_{\alpha}(1)+\varepsilon}\right\}

the event that the Thompson sample mean of arm
i
is
\varepsilon
-riskier than a certain threshold or, more precisely,
\varepsilon
-higher than the optimal arm (which has the lowest CVaR, quantifying the expected loss). Intuitively, event
E_{i}(t)
is highly likely to occur when the algorithm has explored sufficiently. However, the algorithm does not choose arm
i
when
E_{i}^{c}(t)
occurs with small probability under Thompson sampling. We can split
\mathbb{E}[T_{i,n}]
into two parts as follows.

Lemma 1

(
Lattimore & Szepesvári 2020
)
.

Let
\mathbb{P}_{t}(\,\cdot\,)=\mathbb{P}(\,\cdot\,|A_{1},X_{1},\dots,A_{t-1},X_{t-1})
be the probability measure conditioned on the history up to time
t-1
and
G_{is}=\mathbb{P}_{t}(E_{i}^{c}(t)|T_{i,t}=s)
, where
E_{i}(t)
is any specified event for arm
i
at time
t
. Denote
\Lambda_{1}=\mathbb{E}\big[\sum_{s=0}^{n-1}\big\{\frac{1}{G_{1s}}-1\big\}\big]
and
\Lambda_{2}=\sum_{s=0}^{n-1}\mathbb{P}\left({G_{is}>\frac{1}{n}}\right)
. Then
\mathbb{E}[T_{i,n}]\leq\Lambda_{1}+\Lambda_{2}+1
.

It remains to upper bound
\Lambda_{1}
and
\Lambda_{2}
. The techniques used to upper-bound
\Lambda_{1}
resemble those in
Zhu & Tan 2020
, and are relegated to the supplementary material. To upper bound
\Lambda_{2}
, we split the event
E_{i}^{c}(t)=\left\{{\hat{c}_{\alpha}(i,t)\leq c_{\alpha}(1)+\varepsilon}\right\}
into

\displaystyle\Psi_{1}(\xi)

\displaystyle=\left\{{\left({\theta_{i,t}\!-\!\mu_{i}}\right)\left({\textstyle\frac{{\alpha}}{{1\!-\!\alpha}}}\right)\!\leq\!-\xi(\Delta_{\mathrm{r}}(i,\alpha)\!-\!\varepsilon)}\right\},

\displaystyle\Psi_{2}(\xi)

\displaystyle=\left\{{\left({\textstyle\frac{{1}}{{\sqrt{\kappa_{i,t}}}}-\sigma_{i}}\right)c_{\alpha}^{*}\leq(-1+\xi)(\Delta_{\mathrm{r}}(i,\alpha)-\varepsilon)}\right\}.

That is,
E_{i}^{c}(t)\subseteq\Psi_{1}(\xi)\cup\Psi_{2}(\xi)
. We then use the union bound which yields
\mathbb{P}(E_{i}^{c}(t))\leq\mathbb{P}(\Psi_{1}(\xi))+\mathbb{P}(\Psi_{2}(\xi))
and concentration bounds to upper bound
\mathbb{P}(\Psi_{1}(\xi))
and
\mathbb{P}(\Psi_{2}(\xi))
. A good choice of the free parameter
\xi
allows us to allocate “weights” to the bounds on
\mathbb{P}(\Psi_{1}(\xi))
and
\mathbb{P}(\Psi_{2}(\xi))
which then yields
A_{\alpha,\xi}^{i}
and
B_{\alpha,\xi}^{i}
(in (
8
) and (

Extensions and Improvements to Existing Works

The Thompson sample of the Gaussian CVaR is of the form
af(\theta)+bg(1/\kappa)
, where
\theta
and
\kappa
follow a Gaussian and Gamma distribution respectively,
f(x)=x,g(x)=\sqrt{x}
are bijective on their natural domains and have inverses
f^{-1},g^{-1}
respectively, and
a=\alpha/(1-\alpha),b=c_{\alpha}^{*}
depend on
\alpha
. By splitting the Thompson sample
\hat{c}_{\alpha}(i,t)
into two parts as described in the previous paragraph, and using
f^{-1}
and
g^{-1}
, we reduced the problem to upper bounding probabilities of the form
\mathbb{P}(\theta\leq\cdot)
and
\mathbb{P}(\kappa\geq\cdot)
and concentration bounds can then be readily applied. This hints that for general risk measures on Gaussian bandits with Thompson samples of the form
af(\theta)+bg(1/\kappa)
, a similar strategy can be used to establish tight regret bounds. For example, the
entropic risk

(
Lee et al. 2020
;
Howard & Matheson. 1972
)
with parameter
\gamma
of a Gaussian random variable
X\sim{\cal N}(\mu,\sigma^{2})
is
\frac{1}{\gamma}\log\mathbb{E}[\exp(-\gamma X)]=-\mu+(\gamma/2)\sigma^{2}
with a corresponding Thompson sample
-\theta+(\gamma/2)(1/\kappa)
, where
(f(x),g(x),a,b)=(x,x,-1,\gamma/2)
, and our techniques are also immediately amenable to the entropic risk. Similarly, the
mean-variance
risk measure
(
Lee et al. 2020
;
Zhu & Tan 2020
)
with parameter
\rho
of
X\sim{\cal N}(\mu,\sigma^{2})
is
\rho\mu-\sigma^{2}
, with a corresponding Thompson sample
\rho\theta-1/\kappa
, where
(f(x),g(x),a,b)=(x,x,\rho,-1)
. In fact, using our techniques and, in particular arguments, similar to Lemmas
6
and
8
, we can immediately strengthen the regret bound of MVTS in
Zhu & Tan 2020
from being dependent on
O((\mu_{i}-\mu_{1})^{-2})
to
O((\mathrm{MV}_{i}-\mathrm{MV}_{1})^{-2})
. If instead
f
and
g
take very different forms, establishing regret bounds might be more challenging and require new analytical techniques.

7
Conclusion

This paper applies Thompson sampling
(
Thompson 1933
)
to provide a solution for CVaR MAB problems
(
Galichet et al. 2013
;
Kagrecha et al. 2020b
;
Khajonchotpanya et al. 2020
)
which were largely approached from the L/UCB perspectives previously. The regret bounds are notable improvements over those obtained by the state-of-the-art L/UCB techniques, when the bandits are Gaussians satisfying certain assumptions. We show that the bounds also coincide with instance-dependent lower bounds when
\alpha\to 1^{-}
. We corroborated the theoretical results through simulations and verified that under the conditions predicted by the theorems, the gains over previous approaches are significant.

Noting the similarity of the mean-variance of a Gaussian arm when
\rho\to+\infty
and the CVaR of the same arm when
\alpha\to 1^{-}
, we believe there is a unifying theory of risk measures for Gaussian MABs; see
Cassel et al. 2018
,
Lee et al. 2020
, and
Xi et al. 2020
. Furthermore, most papers consider sub-Gaussian bandits, while we focus on Gaussian distributions. This is perhaps why we obtain better regret bounds in some regimes. Further work includes analysing Thompson sampling of Gaussian MABs under general risk measures and exploring the performance of Thompson sampling for CVaR sub-Gaussian bandits.

), we have the expected number of pulls on arm
i
up to round
n
given by

\mathbb{E}[T_{i,n}]\leq\frac{2\log(n)}{{(\mu_{i}-\mu_{1}-\varepsilon)}^{2}}+\frac{C_{9}^{\prime}}{\varepsilon^{4}}+\frac{C_{10}^{\prime}}{\varepsilon^{2}}+C_{11}^{\prime}.

Setting
\varepsilon={(\log n)}^{-\frac{1}{8}}
, we get the following result for the suboptimality regret:

\limsup_{n\to\infty}\frac{\mathcal{R}_{n}^{\mathsf{sub}}(\text{CVaR-TS})}{\log n}\leq\sum_{i\in{\mathcal{K}_{\tau}}\backslash{\mathcal{K}^{*}}}\frac{2}{{(\mu_{i}-\mu_{1})}^{2}}\Delta(i).

∎

Appendix D
Proof of Theorem
4

Proof of Theorem
4
:

Let
\pi
be any (risk-, infeasibility-, or suboptimality-)consistent algorithm. We will prove the theorem in two parts, when the instances are infeasible and feasible respectively.

1.

Fix the threshold level
\tau\in\mathbb{R}
. For any arm
i
with distribution
\nu(i)\sim{\cal N}(\mu_{i},\sigma_{i}^{2})
in a given infeasible instance
(\nu,\tau)
, define
{\cal S}_{i}=\left\{{\nu^{\prime}(i)\in{\cal E}_{\cal N}^{K}:c_{\alpha}(\nu^{\prime}(i))<c_{\alpha}(1)}\right\}
and

\displaystyle\eta(i,\alpha)

\displaystyle=\inf_{\nu^{\prime}(i)\in{\cal S}_{i}}\left\{{\mathrm{KL}(\nu(i),\nu^{\prime}(i))}\right\}=\inf_{\nu^{\prime}(i)\in{\cal S}_{i}}\left\{{\log\frac{\sigma_{i}^{\prime}}{\sigma_{i}}+\frac{\sigma_{i}^{2}+{(\mu_{i}-\mu_{i}^{\prime})}^{2}}{2{(\sigma_{i}^{\prime})}^{2}}-\frac{1}{2}}\right\},

where
\nu^{\prime}(i)\sim{\cal N}(\mu_{i}^{\prime},{(\sigma_{i}^{\prime})}^{2})
and the KL-divergence of two Gaussians is well-known. By Theorem 4 in
Kagrecha et al. 2020a
,
the expected number of pulls of a non-optimal arm
i
is characterised by

\liminf_{n\to\infty}\frac{\mathbb{E}[T_{i,n}]}{\log n}\geq\frac{1}{\eta(i,\alpha)}.

Fix
\varepsilon>0
and consider the arm
\nu^{\prime}(i)
with distribution
{\cal N}\left({\mu_{i}-\frac{\sqrt{2}}{\xi\sqrt{A_{\alpha,\xi}^{i}}}-\varepsilon,\sigma_{i}^{2}}\right)
. Then a direct computation gives

\displaystyle c_{\alpha}(\nu^{\prime}(i))-c_{\alpha}(1)

\displaystyle=\left({\mu_{i}-\frac{\sqrt{2}}{\xi\sqrt{A_{\alpha,\xi}^{i}}}-\varepsilon}\right)\left({\frac{\alpha}{1-\alpha}}\right)+\sigma_{i}c_{\alpha}^{*}-\mu_{1}\left({\frac{\alpha}{1-\alpha}}\right)-\sigma_{1}c_{\alpha}^{*}

\displaystyle=\mu_{i}\left({\frac{\alpha}{1-\alpha}}\right)-\Delta_{\mathrm{r}}(i,\alpha)-\mu_{1}\left({\frac{\alpha}{1-\alpha}}\right)+(\sigma_{i}-\sigma_{1})c_{\alpha}^{*}-\varepsilon\left({\frac{\alpha}{1-\alpha}}\right)

\displaystyle=-\Delta_{\mathrm{r}}(i,\alpha)+\Delta_{\mathrm{r}}(i,\alpha)-\varepsilon\left({\frac{\alpha}{1-\alpha}}\right)

\displaystyle=-\varepsilon\left({\frac{\alpha}{1-\alpha}}\right)

\displaystyle<0.

Thus,
c_{\alpha}(\nu^{\prime}(i))<c_{\alpha}(1)
and
\nu^{\prime}(i)\in{\cal S}_{i}
. Furthermore,

\displaystyle\mathrm{KL}(\nu(i),\nu^{\prime}(i))

\displaystyle={\log\frac{\sigma_{i}}{\sigma_{i}}+\frac{\sigma_{i}^{2}+\left({\mu_{i}-\left({\mu_{i}-\frac{\sqrt{2}}{\xi\sqrt{A_{\alpha,\xi}^{i}}}-\varepsilon}\right)}\right)^{2}}{2\sigma_{i}^{2}}-\frac{1}{2}}=\frac{1}{\xi^{2}\sigma_{i}^{2}A_{\alpha,\xi}^{i}}-\frac{\varepsilon}{2\sigma_{i}^{2}}\left({\frac{2\sqrt{2}}{\xi\sqrt{A_{\alpha,\xi}^{i}}}-\varepsilon}\right).

By the definition of
\eta
,

\eta(i,\alpha)\leq\lim_{\varepsilon\to 0^{+}}\left[{\frac{1}{\xi^{2}\sigma_{i}^{2}A_{\alpha,\xi}^{i}}-\frac{\varepsilon}{2\sigma_{i}^{2}}\left({\frac{2\sqrt{2}}{\xi\sqrt{A_{\alpha,\xi}^{i}}}-\varepsilon}\right)}\right]=\frac{1}{\xi^{2}\sigma_{i}^{2}A_{\alpha,\xi}^{i}}\quad\Longrightarrow\quad\frac{1}{\eta(i,\alpha)}\geq\xi^{2}\sigma_{i}^{2}A_{\alpha,\xi}^{i}.

Hence,

\liminf_{n\to\infty}\frac{\mathcal{R}_{n}^{\mathsf{risk}}(\pi)}{\log n}=\sum_{i\in{[K]}\backslash{\mathcal{K}^{*}}}\left({\liminf_{n\to\infty}\frac{\mathbb{E}[T_{i,n}]}{\log n}}\right)\Delta_{\mathrm{r}}(i,\alpha)\geq\sum_{i\in{[K]}\backslash{\mathcal{K}^{*}}}\xi^{2}\sigma_{i}^{2}A_{\alpha,\xi}^{i}\Delta_{\mathrm{r}}(i,\alpha).

2.

Fix the threshold level
\tau\in\mathbb{R}
. For any arm
i
with distribution
\nu(i)\sim{\cal N}(\mu_{i},\sigma_{i}^{2})
in a given feasible instance
(\nu,\tau)
, define
{\cal V}_{i}=\left\{{\nu^{\prime}(i)\in{\cal E}_{\cal N}^{K}:\mu(\nu^{\prime}(i))<\mu_{1}\ \text{and}\ c_{\alpha}(\nu^{\prime}(i))\leq\tau}\right\}
and

\displaystyle\eta(i,\alpha)

\displaystyle=\inf_{\nu^{\prime}(i)\in{\cal V}_{i}}\left\{{\mathrm{KL}(\nu(i),\nu^{\prime}(i))}\right\}=\inf_{\nu^{\prime}(i)\in{\cal V}_{i}}\left\{{\log\frac{\sigma_{i}^{\prime}}{\sigma_{i}}+\frac{\sigma_{i}^{2}+{(\mu_{i}-\mu_{i}^{\prime})}^{2}}{2{(\sigma_{i}^{\prime})}^{2}}-\frac{1}{2}}\right\},

where
\nu^{\prime}(i)\sim{\cal N}(\mu_{i}^{\prime},{(\sigma_{i}^{\prime})}^{2})
and the KL-divergence of two Gaussians is well-known. By Theorem 4 in
Kagrecha et al. 2020a
,
the expected number of pulls of a non-optimal arm
i
is characterised by

\liminf_{n\to\infty}\frac{\mathbb{E}[T_{i,n}]}{\log n}\geq\frac{1}{\eta(i,\alpha)}.

Suppose arm
i
is infeasible. Fix
\varepsilon>0
, and consider the arm
\nu^{\prime}(i)
with distribution
{\cal N}\left({\mu_{i}-\frac{\sqrt{2}}{\xi\sqrt{D_{\alpha,\xi}^{i}}}-\varepsilon,\sigma_{i}^{2}}\right)
. Then
\mu(\nu^{\prime}(i))<\mu_{1}
trivially, and a direct computation gives

\displaystyle c_{\alpha}(\nu^{\prime}(i))-\tau

\displaystyle=\left({\mu_{i}-\frac{\sqrt{2}}{\xi\sqrt{D_{\alpha,\xi}^{i}}}-\varepsilon}\right)\left({\frac{\alpha}{1-\alpha}}\right)+\sigma_{i}c_{\alpha}^{*}-\tau

\displaystyle=\mu_{i}\left({\frac{\alpha}{1-\alpha}}\right)-\Delta_{\tau}(i)+\sigma_{i}c_{\alpha}^{*}-\tau-\varepsilon\left({\frac{\alpha}{1-\alpha}}\right)

\displaystyle=\mu_{i}\left({\frac{\alpha}{1-\alpha}}\right)-c_{\alpha}(i)+\sigma_{i}c_{\alpha}^{*}-\varepsilon\left({\frac{\alpha}{1-\alpha}}\right)

\displaystyle=-\varepsilon\left({\frac{\alpha}{1-\alpha}}\right)<0.

Thus,
c_{\alpha}(\nu^{\prime}(i))<\tau
and
\nu^{\prime}(i)\in{\cal V}_{i}
. By similar computations as in Part 1,

\displaystyle\mathrm{KL}(\nu(i),\nu^{\prime}(i))

\displaystyle={\log\frac{\sigma_{i}}{\sigma_{i}}+\frac{\sigma_{i}^{2}+\left({\mu_{i}-\left({\mu_{i}-\frac{\sqrt{2}}{\xi\sqrt{D_{\alpha,\xi}^{i}}}-\varepsilon}\right)}\right)^{2}}{2\sigma_{i}^{2}}-\frac{1}{2}}=\frac{1}{\xi^{2}\sigma_{i}^{2}D_{\alpha,\xi}^{i}}-\frac{\varepsilon}{2\sigma_{i}^{2}}\left({\frac{2\sqrt{2}}{\xi\sqrt{A_{\alpha,\xi}^{i}}}-\varepsilon}\right).

By the definition of
\eta
,

\eta(i,\alpha)\leq\lim_{\varepsilon\to 0^{+}}\left[{\frac{1}{\xi^{2}\sigma_{i}^{2}D_{\alpha,\xi}^{i}}-\frac{\varepsilon}{2\sigma_{i}^{2}}\left({\frac{2\sqrt{2}}{\xi\sqrt{A_{\alpha,\xi}^{i}}}-\varepsilon}\right)}\right]=\frac{1}{\xi^{2}\sigma_{i}^{2}D_{\alpha,\xi}^{i}}\quad\Longrightarrow\quad\frac{1}{\eta(i,\alpha)}\geq\xi^{2}\sigma_{i}^{2}D_{\alpha,\xi}^{i}.

Hence,

\liminf_{n\to\infty}\frac{\mathcal{R}_{n}^{\mathsf{inf}}(\pi)}{\log n}=\sum_{i\in\mathcal{K}_{\tau}^{*}}\left({\liminf_{n\to\infty}\frac{\mathbb{E}[T_{i,n}]}{\log n}}\right)\Delta_{\tau}(i)\geq\sum_{i\in\mathcal{K}_{\tau}^{c}}\xi^{2}\sigma_{i}^{2}D_{\alpha,\xi}^{i}\Delta_{\tau}(i).

Now, suppose arm
i
is feasible but suboptimal.
Fix
\varepsilon>0
and consider the arm
\nu^{\prime}(i)
with distribution
{\cal N}(\mu_{i}-\sigma_{i}\Delta(i)-\varepsilon,\sigma_{i}^{2})
.
Then a direct computation gives

\displaystyle c_{\alpha}(\nu^{\prime}(i))-\tau

\displaystyle=(\mu_{i}-\sigma_{i}\Delta(i)-\varepsilon)\left({\frac{\alpha}{1-\alpha}}\right)+\sigma_{i}c_{\alpha}^{*}-\tau

\displaystyle=\mu_{i}\left({\frac{\alpha}{1-\alpha}}\right)-\tau-\sigma_{i}\Delta(i)\left({\frac{\alpha}{1-\alpha}}\right)+\sigma_{i}c_{\alpha}^{*}-\varepsilon\left({\frac{\alpha}{1-\alpha}}\right)

\displaystyle=c_{\alpha}(i)-\tau-\sigma_{i}\Delta(i)\left({\frac{\alpha}{1-\alpha}}\right)-\varepsilon\left({\frac{\alpha}{1-\alpha}}\right)<0.

Thus,
c_{\alpha}(\nu^{\prime}(i))<\tau
and
\nu^{\prime}(i)\in{\cal V}_{i}
. By similar computations as in the previous parts,

\displaystyle\mathrm{KL}(\nu(i),\nu^{\prime}(i))

\displaystyle={\log\frac{\sigma_{i}}{\sigma_{i}}+\frac{\sigma_{i}^{2}+\left({\mu_{i}-\left({\mu_{i}-\sigma_{i}\Delta(i)-\varepsilon}\right)}\right)^{2}}{2\sigma_{i}^{2}}-\frac{1}{2}}

\displaystyle=\frac{\sigma_{i}^{2}\Delta^{2}(i)}{2\sigma_{i}^{2}}-\frac{\varepsilon}{2\sigma_{i}^{2}}\left({2\sigma_{i}\Delta(i)-\varepsilon}\right)

\displaystyle=\frac{\Delta^{2}(i)}{2}-\frac{\varepsilon}{2\sigma_{i}^{2}}\left({2\sigma_{i}\Delta(i)-\varepsilon}\right).

By the definition of
\eta
,

\eta(i,\alpha)\leq\lim_{\varepsilon\to 0^{+}}\left[{\frac{\Delta^{2}(i)}{2}-\frac{\varepsilon}{2\sigma_{i}^{2}}\left({2\sigma_{i}\Delta(i)-\varepsilon}\right)}\right]=\frac{\Delta^{2}(i)}{2}\quad\Longrightarrow\quad\frac{1}{\eta(i,\alpha)}\geq\frac{2}{\Delta^{2}(i)}.

Hence,

\liminf_{n\to\infty}\frac{\mathcal{R}_{n}^{\mathsf{sub}}(\pi)}{\log n}=\sum_{i\in{\mathcal{K}_{\tau}}\backslash{\mathcal{K}^{*}}}\left({\liminf_{n\to\infty}\frac{\mathbb{E}[T_{i,n}]}{\log n}}\right)\Delta(i)\geq\sum_{i\in{\mathcal{K}_{\tau}}\backslash{\mathcal{K}^{*}}}\frac{2}{\Delta(i)}.

∎
