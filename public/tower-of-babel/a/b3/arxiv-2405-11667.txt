The Limits and Potentials of Local SGD for Distributed Heterogeneous Learning with Intermittent Communication
Kumar Kshitij Patel, Margalit Glasgow, Ali Zindari, Lingxiao Wang, Sebastian U. Stich, Ziheng Cheng, Nirmit Joshi, Nathan Srebro

Abstract
Local SGD is a popular optimization method in distributed learning, often outperforming other algorithms in practice, including mini-batch SGD. Despite this success, theoretically proving the dominance of local SGD in settings with reasonable data heterogeneity has been difficult, creating a significant gap between theory and practice ( Wang et al., 2022 ) . In this paper, we provide new lower bounds for local SGD under existing first-order data heterogeneity assumptions, showing that these assumptions are insufficient to prove the effectiveness of local update steps. Furthermore, under these same assumptions, we demonstrate the min-max optimality of accelerated mini-batch SGD, which fully resolves our understanding of distributed optimization for several problem classes. Our results emphasize the need for better models of data heterogeneity to understand the effectiveness of local SGD in practice. Towards this end, we consider higher-order smoothness and heterogeneity assumptions, providing new upper bounds that imply the dominance of local SGD over mini-batch SGD when data heterogeneity is low.

Towards this end, several works

1. Existing first-order heterogeneity assumptions are insufficient for local SGD.

We provide a new lower bound for local SGD (Theorem
1
) in the general convex setting. Our core finding can be stated as:

There is a smooth, convex, and quadratic problem instance such that local SGD can not get arbitrarily close to the shared optimum of the clients with finite communication.

Our lower bound precludes dominance of local SGD over mini-batch SGD under the following heterogeneity assumption considered by several existing works
(
Koloskova et al., 2020
;
Woodworth et al., 2020a
;
Glasgow et al., 2022
)
.

Assumption 1

(Bounded First-Order Heterogeneity at Optima)
.

A set of objectives
\{F_{m}\}_{m\in[M]}
satisfy
\zeta_{\star}
-first-order heterogeneity at the optima if for all minimizers of the average objective
x^{\star}\in\arg\min_{x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}}F(x)
,

\frac{1}{M}\sum_{m\in[M]}\left\lVert\nabla F_{m}(x^{\star})\right\rVert_{2}^{2}\leq\zeta_{\star}^{2}.

We show that the above assumption, while a natural measure of data-heterogeneity, can not avoid some basic pathologies of distributed learning with convex objectives. Our lower bound also precludes any dominance of local SGD over mini-batch SGD under another similar assumption postulated by
Wang et al. (2022)
(Assumption
7
in Appendix
C
).

2. Accelerated mini-batch SGD is min-max optimal when machines have shared optima.

We provide a new algorithm-independent lower bound (Theorem
2
) under Assumption
1
(and Assumption
7
), showing that accelerated mini-batch SGD is min-max optimal under this assumption! This conclusion is surprising, and while it further deepens the theory-practice disparity, it concludes a recent line of work on identifying the min-max optimal algorithm in the general convex setting under related first-order heterogeneity assumptions
(
Khaled et al., 2020
;
Koloskova et al., 2020
;
Woodworth et al., 2020a
;
Glasgow et al., 2022
;
Wang et al., 2022
)
.

Together our lower bounds (Theorems
1
and
2
) imply that existing first-order assumptions are insufficient to understand the effectiveness of local SGD, in that they do not characterize a measure of data heterogeneity, reducing which would allow local update steps to be useful. This motivates us to consider higher-order assumptions in addition to first-order ones.

3. Local SGD shines under higher-order heterogeneity and smoothness assumptions.

We provide a new upper bound (Theorem
3
) for local SGD improving the analysis of
Woodworth et al. (2020a)
by capturing the effect of second-order heterogeneity (Assumption
3
) and third-order smoothness (Assumption
4
). While our upper bound depends on the restrictive Assumption
2
of
Woodworth et al. (2021)
, it relaxes local SGD’s dependence on the assumption, i.e., our analysis implies a benefit of local updates with much larger data heterogeneity than that implied by the analysis of
Woodworth et al. (2020a)
. This hints that higher-order assumptions indeed enhance the benefit of local updates. We explore this further by considering the special case when all the client objectives are strongly convex quadratics. In this setting, we provide a new upper bound (Theorem
4

1
and
3
control the discrepancy between the fixed point of local SGD and the minimizer of
F
in problem (
1
).

Remark 4

(Local SGD’s Fixed Point)

Denote the set of optima of machine

Middling Utility of First-order Heterogeneity Assumptions in the Convex Setting

Our lower bounds in this section are based on the key insight presented in Remark
2

S^{\star}=\cap_{m\in[M]}S_{m}^{\star}\neq\emptyset
(see Remark
4

\sigma=0
and the objectives of the machines for all

are given by,

\displaystyle F_{1}(x)=\frac{H}{2}\left(x[1]-x^{\star}[1]\right)^{2}\text{ and }F_{2}(x)=\frac{H}{2}\left(x[2]-x^{\star}[2]\right)^{2},

(7)

where

\displaystyle x_{R}=x^{\star}\left(1-\left(1-\frac{\beta}{2}\left(1-(1-\eta H)^{K}\right)\right)^{R}\right).

\displaystyle x_{R}=x^{\star}\left(1-\left(\frac{1+(1-\eta H)^{K}}{2}\right)^{R}\right).

Remark 5

(The Role of the Outer Step-size)
.

Note that for Example

in a single communication round when

and provides a separation between vanilla local SGD and local SGD with two step-sizes.

In the following proposition, we extend this idea to obtain a lower bound for any outer step size

6
)). Finally, note that the proposition can not benefit from simultaneous realizability, i.e., non-empty
\cap_{m\in[M]}S_{m}^{\star}
. This highlights the hardness of general convex optimization in the intermittent communication model. To prove the above proposition for arbitrary step sizes, we reduce the local GD updates over a single communication round to a single gradient descent (GD) update on
F
and then use the following lemma (proved in Appendix
B
) for GD.

Lemma 1
.

Let
F(x)
be a convex quadratic function whose Hessian has top eigenvalue
H
, bottom eigenvalue
\mu
, and condition number
\kappa=\frac{H}{\mu}\geq 6
. Let
\hat{x}_{R}
be the
R^{th}
gradient descent iterate initialized at zero. Then for any
B
, there exists some
x^{\star}
with
\|x^{\star}\|_{2}\leq B
such that for any step size
\eta
,
F(\hat{x}_{R})-F(x^{\star})\geq HB^{2}\frac{1}{\kappa}e^{-R/\kappa}
. In particular, if
\kappa=\Omega(R)
, then
F(\hat{x}_{R})-F(x^{\star})\geq\Omega\left(\frac{HB^{2}}{R}\right)
.

To get a lower bound in terms of
\zeta_{\star}
from Assumption
2
as well as understand the effect of noise level
\sigma
, we combine proposition
2
with the previous lower bound by
Glasgow et al. (2022)
, to get the following lower bound result.

Theorem 1
.

For any
K\geq 2,R,M,H,B,\sigma,\zeta_{\star}
, there exists a problem instance in
\mathcal{P}^{H,B,\sigma}_{\zeta_{\star}}
such that the final iterate
x_{R}
of local SGD initialized at zero with any step size satisfies:

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[F(x_{R})\right]-F(x^{\star})

\displaystyle\succeq\frac{HB^{2}}{R}+\frac{(H\sigma^{2}B^{4})^{1/3}}{K^{1/3}R^{2/3}}+\frac{\sigma B}{\sqrt{MKR}}+\frac{(H\zeta_{\star}^{2}B^{4})^{1/3}}{R^{2/3}}.

Combining Theorem
1
with the upper bound by
Koloskova et al. (2020)
, we characterize the optimal convergence rate for local SGD for problems in class
\mathcal{P}_{\zeta_{\star}}^{H,B,\sigma}
. Thus, our lower bound concludes the line of work trying to analyze local SGD under the first-order heterogeneity assumption on the set
S^{\star}

(
Koloskova et al., 2020
;
Woodworth et al., 2020a
;
Glasgow et al., 2022
)
.

Remark 6

(Proposed Assumption of
Wang et al. (2022)
)
.

Proposition
2
also implies that the first-order assumption of
Wang et al. (2022)
(see Assumption
7
and discussion in Appendix
C
) can not be used to show a domination of local SGD over mini-batch SGD. This is because when
S^{\star}\neq\emptyset
, the data heterogeneity assumption of
Wang et al. (2022)
implies zero heterogeneity.

Overall, the results of this section imply that none of the existing assumptions besides the Assumption
2
on the unbounded domain
\mathbb{R}^{d}
provides the necessary control on data heterogeneity, for local SGD to beat mini-batch SGD in the general convex setting.

3.1

The Min-max Optimality of Mini-batch SGD

While we can not show the effectiveness of local SGD on the class
\mathcal{P}_{\zeta_{\star}}^{H,B,\sigma}
, can we say which algorithm is min-max optimal for this class of problems? It turns out that the answer is surprisingly simple: accelerated large mini-batch SGD
(
Ghadimi and Lan, 2012
)
! To show this, we prove the following new algorithm-independent lower bound, which does not improve with a small
\zeta_{\star}
.

Theorem 2

(Algorithm independent lower bound)
.

For any
K\geq 2,R,M,H,B,\sigma,\zeta_{\star}
, there exists a problem instance in the class
\mathcal{P}^{H,B,\sigma}_{\zeta_{\star}}
, s.t. the final iterate
\hat{x}
of any distributed zero-respecting algorithm (see Appendix
B.5
) initialized at zero with
R
rounds of communication and
K
stochastic gradient computations per machine per round satisfies,

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[F(\hat{x})\right]-F(x^{\star})\succeq\frac{HB^{2}}{R^{2}}+\frac{\sigma B}{\sqrt{MKR}}\,.

(10)

The above lower bound fully characterizes the min-max complexity of distributed optimization on the class
\mathcal{P}_{\zeta_{\star}}^{H,B,\sigma}
, showing that accelerated mini-batch SGD is the min-max optimal algorithm. This conclusion is surprising, but it closes a recent line of work investigating the intermittent communication setting under Assumption
2

(
Khaled et al., 2020
;
Karimireddy et al., 2020b
;
Koloskova et al., 2020
;
Woodworth et al., 2020a
;
Glasgow et al., 2022
;
Wang et al., 2022
)
. The above lower bound also implies the min-max optimality of large mini-batch SGD under Assumption
7
proposed by
Wang et al. (2022)
(see Appendix
C
).

Compared to
Woodworth et al. (2021)
who showed that for problem class
\mathcal{P}_{hom}^{H,B,\sigma}
(and by implication for
\mathcal{P}_{\zeta=0}^{H,B,\sigma}
), either accelerated SGD on a single machine or accelerated local SGD can improve over accelerated mini-batch SGD, we have shown that for the problem class
\mathcal{P}_{\zeta_{\star}=0}^{H,B,\sigma}
such an improvement is not possible
2
2

2

Note that on Example (
7
) also showed that single machine SGD must incur a sub-optimality of order
HB^{2}
, making it much worse than local SGD, and highlighting that
\mathcal{P}^{H,B,\sigma}_{hom}
is a very special sub-class of
\mathcal{P}_{\zeta=0}^{H,B,\sigma}
with regards to the utility of collaboration.
. Thus, we can not hope to show the benefit of using (accelerated) local SGD on any super-class of
\mathcal{P}_{\zeta_{\star}=0}^{H,B,\sigma}
. In other words, we need additional assumptions on top of Assumption
2
for
\mathcal{X}=S^{\star}
to avoid the pathologies of our hard instances. In the remainder of this paper we will explore how additional higher-order assumptions can help improve local SGD’s convergence.

Woodworth et al. (2020a)
did show such a result over the “nearly homogeneous" class
\mathcal{P}^{H,B,\sigma}_{\zeta(\mathbb{R}^{d})}
(see Table
2
). This prompts us to ask the following question:

Can local SGD dominate mini-batch SGD on the class
\mathcal{P}^{H,B,\sigma}_{\zeta(\mathbb{B}_{2}(D))}
, where
\mathbb{B}_{2}(D)
is the
L_{2}
ball of radius
D
around
0
for some sufficiently large
D
, and small
\zeta(\mathbb{B}_{2}(D))
?

Intuitively, if our problem has a low (but meaningful) heterogeneity, and we can ensure that the local SGD iterates never leave the ball
\mathbb{B}_{2}(D)
, for some large enough
D
, then we can recover the result of
Woodworth et al. (2020a)
, to show the effectiveness of local SGD on a much larger problem class
\mathcal{P}_{\zeta(\mathbb{B}_{2}(D))}^{H,B,\sigma}
. To enable this, we would need the following second-order heterogeneity assumption, which controls how far the hessians on each machine can be from the average hessian:

Assumption 3

(Bounded Second-order Heterogeneity)
.

A set of doubly-differentiable objectives
\{F_{m}\}_{m\in[M]}
satisfy
\tau
-second-order heterogeneity if for all
x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
,

\sup_{m,n\in[M]}\left\lVert\nabla^{2}F_{m}(x)-\nabla^{2}F_{n}(x)\right\rVert_{2}^{2}\leq\tau^{2}.

Remark 7
.

Assumption
3
has recently received attention in the non-convex setting
(
Karimireddy et al., 2020a
;
Murata and Suzuki, 2021
;
Patel et al., 2022
)
.
Patel et al. (2022)
show that it is possible to show the dominance of local update algorithms, essentially local SGD with variance reduction over mini-batch algorithms when
\tau
is much smaller than
H
.

Assumption
3
can always be satisfied by choosing
\tau\geq 2H
for smooth functions. We will denote the problems satisfying Assumption
3
by
\mathcal{P}_{\tau}^{H,B,\sigma}
. Given Assumption
3
, we note that on the set
\mathbb{B}_{2}(D)
, we can bound
\zeta
in terms of
\tau,\zeta_{\star}
and
D
using the following upper bound (proved in Appendix
D
).

Proposition 3
.

Given a problem instance in the class
\mathcal{P}_{\zeta_{\star}}^{H,B,\sigma}~\cap~\mathcal{P}_{\tau}^{H,B,\sigma}
with objectives
\{F_{m}\}_{m\in[M]}
,

\displaystyle\sup_{x\in\mathbb{B}_{2}(D),m\in[M]}\left\lVert\nabla F_{m}(x)-\nabla F(x)\right\rVert_{2}^{2}\leq(\zeta_{\star}+\tau D)^{2}.

The above proposition implies that for some level of first-order heterogeneity
\zeta
when
\tau
is small, we can allow for a much larger
D
. Alternatively, if we know our algorithm will be inside a ball
\mathbb{B}_{2}(D)
, the smaller the second-order heterogeneity of our problem, the smaller the bound on its first-order heterogeneity. In the extreme case of
\tau=0
, we can replace
\zeta
with
\zeta_{\star}
(i.e., the converse of Proposition
1
)! And as a sanity check, in the homogeneous setting the right hand side is zero. Finally, we will also define a third-order smoothness assumption in synergy with Assumption
3
.

Assumption 4

(Third-order Smoothness)
.

The average function
F
has a
Q
Lipschitz hessian if

\forall\ x,y\in\mathbb{R}^{d},\quad\left\lVert\nabla^{2}F(x)-\nabla^{2}F(y)\right\rVert_{2}\leq Q\left\lVert x-y\right\rVert_{2}\kern 5.0pt.

The above assumption intuitively upper bounds the gap between a function and its second-order approximation and is meaningful for smooth functions only when for a given
x,y\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
,
H\gg Q\left\lVert x-y\right\rVert_{2}
, i.e., the bound implied by smoothness is “loose”. For a sanity check, note that when
Q=0
, then
F
must be a quadratic function. To analyze functions with third-order smoothness, we also need control over the fourth moment of the stochastic noise on each machine, akin to existing works
(
Yuan and Ma, 2020
)
. In particular in addition to the assumptions in (
3
) we also assume that for each machine
m\in[M]
and for all
x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
,

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{z\sim\mathcal{D}_{m}}\left[\left\lVert\nabla f(x;z)-\nabla F_{m}(x)\right\rVert_{2}^{4}\right]\leq\sigma^{4}\kern 5.0pt.

(11)

We will denote the problems satisfying Assumptions
3
and
4
and the above additional fourth moment bound, in addition to other assumptions in the paper by
\mathcal{P}^{H,Q,B,\sigma}_{\tau}
.

Remark 8
.

For quadratic problems in the class
\mathcal{P}^{H,B,\sigma}_{hom}
,
Woodworth et al. (2020b)
showed that accelerated local SGD is min-max optimal. In particular, their result implies that local SGD can achieve an arbitrary accuracy for a fixed number of communication rounds
R
by increasing the number of local steps
K
per round. Given this result, several works
(
Yuan and Ma, 2020
;
Bullins et al., 2021
)
have tried to use higher-order smoothness assumptions to interpolate between the min-max optimal bounds for the quadratic and convex homogeneous setting (Table
2
).

We can obtain the following informal convergence result for the problem class
\mathcal{P}^{H,Q,B,\sigma}_{\tau}\cap\mathcal{P}^{H,B,\sigma}_{\zeta_{\star}}
. We state the full version of the above bound, and the step-size used to obtain it, in Appendix
D
.

Theorem 3

(Informal)
.

For any
K,R,M\geq 1
and
H,B,Q,\sigma,\tau\geq 0
consider a problem instance in the class
\mathcal{P}^{H,Q,B,\sigma}_{\tau}~\cap~\left(\mathcal{P}^{H,B,\sigma}_{\zeta(\mathbb{B}_{2}(D))}~\cup~\mathcal{P}^{H,B,\sigma}_{\zeta_{\star}}\right)
, where we assume
D
is large-enough such that the local SGD iterates stay inside the ball
\mathbb{B}_{2}(D)
. Then, for a fixed inner stepsize of
\eta\leq\frac{1}{2H}
, and outer stepsize of
\beta=1
, we have the following convergence rate (up to logarithmic factors) for local SGD (initialized at zero):

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[F\left(\frac{1}{MKR}\sum_{m=1}^{M}\sum_{r=1}^{R}\sum_{k=0}^{K-1}x^{m}_{r,k}\right)-F(x^{\star})\right]\preceq\frac{HB^{2}}{KR}+\frac{\sigma B}{\sqrt{MKR}}+\frac{(\tau\sigma B^{3})^{1/2}}{K^{1/4}R^{1/2}}+\frac{(Q\sigma^{2}B^{5})^{1/3}}{K^{1/3}R^{2/3}}

\displaystyle+\min\left\{\frac{(\tau\zeta B^{3})^{1/2}}{R^{1/2}}+\frac{(Q\zeta^{2}B^{5})^{1/3}}{R^{2/3}},\frac{(\tau\zeta_{\star}B^{3})^{1/2}}{R^{1/2}}+\frac{(Q\zeta_{\star}^{2}B^{5})^{1/3}}{R^{2/3}}+\frac{(\tau^{2}DB^{3})^{1/2}}{R^{1/2}}+\frac{(Q\tau^{2}D^{2}B^{5})^{1/3}}{R^{2/3}}\right\}\,.

Remark 9
.

Note that the bound implied by Proposition
3
is a worst-case bound for problems in the class
\mathcal{P}_{\zeta_{\star}}^{H,B,\sigma}\cap\mathcal{P}_{\tau}^{H,B,\sigma}
. And in general, there might exist an upper bound
\zeta(\mathbb{B}_{2}(D))\ll\zeta_{\star}+\tau D
. For this reason, we have stated our upper bounds in terms of
\zeta
as well as only in terms of
\tau,\zeta_{\star},D
.

To prove Theorem
3
we first obtain a convergence rate in the strongly convex setting (Theorem
5
) and then use the standard convex to strongly convex reduction by adding appropriate regularization to our original problem. To the best of our knowledge, Theorem
3
is the only upper bound for local SGD for problems in the class
\mathcal{P}^{H,Q,B,\sigma}_{\tau}~\cap~\mathcal{P}^{H,B,\sigma}_{\zeta(\mathbb{B}_{2}(D))}
even for
D=\infty
, i.e.,
\mathcal{X}=\mathbb{R}^{d}
in Assumption
2
. Note that the upper bound
can
benefit from second-order heterogeneity, i.e., Assumption
3
. In the extreme case, i.e., the homogeneous setting where
\tau,\zeta=0
, our rate recovers the convergence rate by
Yuan and Ma (2020)
(see Table
2
). To compare our result to existing results in the heterogeneous setting by
Woodworth et al. (2020a)
, and to highlight the benefit of Assumptions
3
and
4
, we will now compare requirements on
\zeta(\mathbb{B}_{2}(D))
to reach some target sub-optimality
\epsilon
. For simplicity, we will assume that
K
is large enough to ignore all the terms in the convergence bound with
K
in the denominator. Then the requirements on
\zeta
are,

\displaystyle\zeta_{old}\preceq\frac{\epsilon^{3/2}R}{H^{1/2}B^{2}}\qquad\text{ v/s }\qquad\zeta_{our}\preceq\min\left\{\frac{\epsilon^{3/2}R}{(QB)^{1/2}B^{2}},\frac{\epsilon^{2}R}{\tau B^{3}}\right\}.

(12)

In the regime when
Q,\tau
are small, this highlights that our requirements are much less stringent on
\zeta
. The main limitation of our upper bound, however, is that we assume there exists a
D
such that,

\sup_{m\in[M],r\in[R],k\in[0,K-1]}\left\lVert x_{r,K}^{m}\right\rVert_{2}\leq D,

and prove the upper bound in terms of such a
D
. In fact, we require this for all (random) sequences of local SGD iterates. This is a strong assumption, but we believe it can be avoided by choosing appropriate
\eta,\beta
in terms of problem-dependent quantities such as
\zeta_{\star},\tau,Q
so that we can control the norm of local SGD iterates (with high probability). We leave this task for future work. For now, we conjecture the following result.

Conjecture 1

(Effectiveness of Local SGD)
.

Local SGD can dominate mini-batch SGD over the problem class
\mathcal{P}_{\tau}^{H,Q,B,\sigma}~\cap~\mathcal{P}_{\zeta_{\star}}^{H,B,\sigma}
in the regime when
\tau,QB\ll H
and
\sigma,\zeta_{\star}
are small.

Remark 10

(Limitations of mini-batch SGD in the worst case)
.

In our setting, mini-batch SGD can not improve from additional assumptions on top of convexity and smoothness (at least in the worst case). To illustrate this, we assume that all machines share the same objective function
F
, a quadratic function that will be determined later. In this simple setup, note that all data heterogeneity measures are zero; since the objective is quadratic,
Q=0
as well. Essentially, mini-batch SGD reduces to optimizing
F
with
R
mini-batch updates, each with batch size
MK
. Using Lemma
1
and standard sample complexity lower bounds for mean-estimation (also a quadratic problem), we can argue that the convergence rate for mini-batch SGD in this setting is
\frac{HB^{2}}{R}+\frac{\sigma B}{\sqrt{MKR}}
, which does not benefit from
\tau,\zeta_{\star},Q=0
. On the other hand, we conjecture above that local SGD can significantly benefit from these low measures of heterogeneity and sharpness. Thus, if the conjecture is accurate, we would identify a regime of low data heterogeneity where local SGD dominates mini-batch SGD.

Remark 11

(Breaking our lower bound)
.

Another motivation for our conjecture is the observation that in Example
7
as well as our construction for proving Theorem
1
, the Hessians on the different machines must be dissimilar. We can not cause the machines to drift between communication rounds if they are not dissimilar, which is, in fact, not possible when
\tau=0
. This is precisely why we believe controlling the distance between the machines’ Hessians can break our lower bound.

While we do not yet know how to prove Conjecture
1
for the entire class
\mathcal{P}_{\tau}^{H,Q,B,\sigma}~\cap~\mathcal{P}_{\zeta_{\star}}^{H,B,\sigma}
, we will prove a very special case of this conjecture in the next section for strongly convex quadratic objectives on each machine which lie in the problem class
\mathcal{P}_{\tau}^{H,Q=0,B,\sigma=0}~\cap~\mathcal{P}_{\zeta_{\star}}^{H,B,\sigma=0}
. Quadratic objectives are analytically simpler, and we rely on a fixed-point perspective to understand the behavior of local gradient descent.

, for any choice of
\eta,\beta>0
that ensures convergence of local GD,

. This is unsurprising because with
K=1
, local GD reduces to GD on the averaged objective, so the fixed point must be a stationary point of

, i.e.,
x^{\star}
. We also observe in Figure

that as
\eta
decreases as a function of
K

K\to\infty
we recover the correct fixed point. This is also expected, as the effect of local updates diminishes with smaller
\eta

2. The incorrect fixed point.

On the other hand when
K\to\infty
and we pick
\eta=\omega{\frac{1}{KH}}
but
\eta<\frac{1}{H}
we get that
\lim_{K\to\infty}x_{\infty}(K,\eta,\beta)=\bar{x}^{\star}
. This makes intuitive sense (at least for
\beta=1
) because for a large step-size
\eta

\bar{x}^{\star}
.
In fact, using Assumptions

and
5
(a strongly convex variant of Assumption

on

), we can show that the distances between

,

and

are bounded. Specifically, we consider the following assumption,

Assumption 5

(Bounded Optima Heterogeneity)
.

A set of

-smooth and strongly convex objectives
\{F_{m}\}_{m\in[M]}

-optima-heterogeneity if,

\frac{1}{M}\sum_{m\in[M]}\left\lVert x_{m}^{\star}-x^{\star}\right\rVert_{2}^{2}\leq\frac{\zeta_{\star}^{2}}{H^{2}},

where
x_{m}^{\star}
is the optimum of

for all
m\in[M]
and

is the optimum of the average objective
F
.

Note that using smoothness along with Assumption

implies Assumption
2
for
\mathcal{X}=S^{\star}
. Intuitively, when the machines have unique optima, the most natural measure of heterogeneity is the distance between these optima and the optima of the average objective, which the above assumption measures
3
3

3

A problem instance with objectives of the form (

) satisfying Assumption

lies in the problem class

.
. This assumption enables us to prove the following upper bound on the fixed-point discrepancy of local gradient descent.

Consider a problem instance of the form (

) with

Theorem 4

Assuming

\displaystyle\left\lVert x_{R}-x^{\star}\right\rVert_{2}

\displaystyle\preceq Be^{-\frac{KR}{\kappa}}+\frac{\zeta_{\star}\tau}{H\mu}.

Comparing this bound to the rate for GD (i.e., the noiseless variant of mini-batch SGD), we see that when
\zeta_{\star},\tau
are small, local GD has a much better optimization term v/s the optimization term
Be^{-R/\kappa}
of GD. This implies that we have identified a
\mu

\mathcal{P}_{\tau}^{H,Q,B,\sigma=0}~\cap~\mathcal{P}_{\zeta_{\star}}^{H,B,\sigma=0}

5.1

Two Stage Algorithms

Based on the discussion in this section, a natural question is how to reduce the fixed-point discrepancy of local SGD. One approach based on the observation in Figure
2
is to reduce the inner step-size of local SGD as iterations proceed so that we get the benefit of more aggressive updates at the beginning of training but can also avoid the fixed-point discrepancy at the end of training. Note that reducing the inner step size actually reduces local SGD to mini-batch SGD in the limit. Thus, an even easier approach to fixing the fixed point issue is sharply transitioning from running local SGD to mini-batch SGD (which has no fixed point issues) at the end of training.

Such a two-stage algorithm has been considered by
Hou et al. (2021)
, motivated by the results of
Woodworth et al. (2020a)
. Theorem
4
provides a more fine-grained perspective on this two-stage algorithm by capturing the effect of
\zeta_{\star},\tau
as opposed to
\zeta(\mathbb{R}^{d})
in
Hou et al. (2021)
. In particular, we run local GD for the first phase until the optimization (first) term in Theorem
4
dominates the convergence, i.e.,
R_{1}=\theta{\frac{\kappa}{K}\ln{\frac{H\mu}{\zeta_{\star}\tau B}}}
rounds. At this point, we switch to running GD, i.e., the noiseless variant of mini-batch SGD, for
R_{2}=\theta{\kappa\ln{\frac{\zeta_{\star}\tau}{H\mu\epsilon}}}
, for some target accuracy
\epsilon
. Thus, in total, the communication complexity of this algorithm is given by,

R(\epsilon)=R_{1}+R_{2}=\theta{\kappa\ln{{\frac{H\mu}{\zeta_{\star}\tau}\cdot\frac{1}{B}}^{1/K}{\frac{\zeta_{\star}\tau}{H\mu}\cdot\frac{1}{\epsilon}}}}.

This communication complexity exemplifies the effect of local update steps, where we notice that in the limit
K\to\infty
the communication complexity of the two-stage algorithm local GD->GD is much better than running GD alone as long as
\frac{\zeta_{\star}\tau}{\mu}<<HB
.

5.2

Interpreting the Heterogeneity Assumptions

So far, we have introduced several heterogeneity assumptions in this paper. We end this discussion by considering a simple linear regression model to highlight how these assumptions might appear in real-world problems relating to actual distribution shifts between clients. We will consider a linear regression problem, where each data point is a co-variate label pair
z_{m}:=(\beta_{m},y_{m})\sim\mathcal{D}_{m}
on each client such that:

•

The covariates are sampled from a Gaussian distribution, i.e.,
\beta_{m}\sim\mathcal{N}(\mu_{m},\Sigma_{m})\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
; and

•

The labels are generated as
y_{m}\sim\left\langle x_{m}^{\star},\beta_{m}\right\rangle+\mathcal{N}(0,\sigma^{2}_{noise})\in\mathbb{R}
for some ground truth model
x_{m}^{\star}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
on machine
m
.

The goal of each client is to minimize the mean squared error, i.e.,
f(x;(\beta_{m},y_{m}))=\frac{1}{2}{y_{m}-\left\langle x,\beta_{m}\right\rangle}^{2}
and the expected loss function is given by,

\displaystyle F_{m}(x)

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}_{z_{m}\sim\mathcal{D}_{m}}[f(x;z_{m})]\kern 5.0pt,

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}_{\beta_{m}\sim\mathcal{N}(\mu_{m},\Sigma_{m}),\xi_{m}\sim\mathcal{N}(0,\sigma^{2}_{noise})}\left[\frac{1}{2}{\left\langle x_{m}^{\star}-x,\beta_{m}\right\rangle+\xi_{m}}^{2}\right]\kern 5.0pt,

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}_{\beta_{m}\sim\mathcal{N}(\mu_{m},\Sigma_{m})}\left[\frac{1}{2}{\left\langle x_{m}^{\star}-x,\beta_{m}\right\rangle}^{2}\right]+\mathbb{E}_{\xi_{m}\sim\mathcal{N}(0,\sigma^{2}_{noise})}\left[\frac{1}{2}\xi_{m}^{2}\right]\kern 5.0pt,

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}(x-x_{m}^{\star})^{T}\mathbb{E}_{\beta_{m}\sim\mathcal{N}(\mu_{m},\Sigma_{m})}\left[\beta_{m}\beta_{m}^{T}\right](x-x_{m}^{\star})+\frac{1}{2}\sigma_{noise}^{2}\kern 5.0pt,

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}(x-x_{m}^{\star})^{T}{\mu_{m}\mu_{m}^{T}+\Sigma_{m}}(x-x_{m}^{\star})+\frac{1}{2}\sigma_{noise}^{2}\kern 5.0pt,

which fits in the model of
H
-smooth and strongly convex quadratic functions we have considered in this section, as long as
0\prec\mu_{m}\mu_{m}^{T}+\Sigma_{m}\preceq H.I_{d}
. Interestingly, Assumptions
3
and
5
have a very nice interpretation in this setting,

•

Note that for any
m,n\in[M]
,

\left\lVert A_{m}-A_{n}\right\rVert_{2}\leq(\left\lVert\mu_{m}\right\rVert_{2}+\left\lVert\mu_{n}\right\rVert_{2})\left\lVert\mu_{m}-\mu_{n}\right\rVert_{2}+\left\lVert\Sigma_{m}-\Sigma_{n}\right\rVert_{2}=:\tau_{m,n},

Thus this problem satisfies Assumption
3
with
\tau^{2}:=\sup_{m,n}\tau^{2}_{m,n}
which in turn can be thought of as a measure of the
“co-variate shift"
between the machines.

•

Similarly, note that for any
m,n\in[M]
,

\left\lVert x_{m}^{\star}-x_{n}^{\star}\right\rVert_{2}^{2}\leq 2\left\lVert x_{m}^{\star}-x^{\star}\right\rVert_{2}^{2}+2\left\lVert x_{n}^{\star}-x^{\star}\right\rVert_{2}^{2},

for
x\in S^{\star}
. Thus Assumption
5
controls the average
“concept-shift"
between the clients as,

\frac{1}{M^{2}}\sum_{m,n\in[M]}\left\lVert x_{m}^{\star}-x_{n}^{\star}\right\rVert_{2}^{2}\leq\frac{4}{M}\sum_{m\in[M]}\left\lVert x_{m}^{\star}-x^{\star}\right\rVert_{2}^{2}\leq 4\frac{\zeta_{\star}^{2}}{H^{2}}.

In light of this interpretation, note that Theorem
4
implies that the local SGD can approach a fast convergence rate, i.e., not suffer due to the fixed point discrepancy, as long as either the co-variate shift (
\tau
) or the concept shift (
\zeta_{\star}
) is low. We believe this provides a useful interpretation for these heterogeneity assumptions, and this simple learning problem might pave the way to a better understanding of distributed learning algorithms. In particular, we posit the following problem, which is largely open:

Open Problem 1
.

For the distributed linear regression problem described in this setting, and for different regimes of heterogeneity/distribution shift parameters
\tau,\zeta_{\star}
as well as problem dependent parameters
H,\mu,\sigma,B
identifying the min-max optimal algorithms.

Answering the above problem requires analyzing other algorithms, such as the two-stage algorithm mentioned in the previous sub-section, reconciling existing literature in low heterogeneity regimes, including the homogeneous regime, and identifying relevant lower bounds for the problem. Most importantly, the insights from answering the above question might have many useful prescriptions for the practice of distributed/federated learning in the face of different natures of heterogeneity, such as co-variate/concept shift. We hope to address this problem more comprehensively in future work.

6

Discussion

In this paper, we further the discussion around the convergence rate of local SGD and establish the min-max optimal convergence rate for the much-studied problem class
\mathcal{P}^{H,B,\sigma}_{\zeta_{\star}}
. While the lower bound results of our paper seem to paint a negative picture for local SGD, we believe their real message is to help us identify the additional heterogeneity assumptions required to demonstrate the effectiveness of local SGD. Based on the paper’s upper bound results and our current insights, we believe that combining first-order heterogeneity assumptions with higher-order assumptions is key to understanding the “real" behavior of local SGD. Towards this end, we believe the central message of our paper is Conjecture
1
, stated in Section
3
.
Proving this conjecture is an important future direction, and we believe it can both enhance our understanding of local SGD and aid in designing a better algorithm for practice.

3.

at the
r^{th}
communication round, the machines only communicate vectors in

\left\{\bigcup_{n\in[M],s\in[r],l\in[K]}supp\left(\mathcal{O}_{F_{n}}(I_{s,l}^{n};z_{s,l}^{n}\sim\mathcal{D}_{n})\right)\right\},

where
supp\left(.\right)
denotes the co-ordinate support/span of its arguments. We denote this class of algorithms by
\boldsymbol{\mathcal{A}_{ZR}}
. Furthermore, if all the oracle inputs are the same between two communication rounds, i.e.,
I_{r,k}^{m}=I_{r}\in\mathcal{I}
for all
m\in[M],k\in[K],r\in[R]
, then we say that the algorithm is centralized, and denote this class of algorithms by
\boldsymbol{\mathcal{A}_{ZR}^{cent}}\subset\mathcal{A}_{ZR}
.

This class captures a very wide variety of distributed optimization algorithms, including mini-batch SGD
[
Dekel et al., 2012
]
, accelerated mini-batch SGD
[
Ghadimi and Lan, 2012
]
, local SGD
[
McMahan et al., 2016a
]
, as well as all the variance-reduced algorithms
[
Karimireddy et al., 2020a
,
Zhao et al., 2021
,
Khanduri et al., 2021
]
. Non-distributed zero-respecting algorithms are those whose iterates have components in directions about which the algorithm has no information, meaning that, in some sense, it is just “wild guessing”. We have also defined the smaller class of centralized algorithms
\mathcal{A}_{ZR}^{cent}
. These algorithms query the oracles at the same point within each communication round and use the combined

oracle queries each round to get a “mini-batch” estimate of the gradient. Thus, the class
\mathcal{A}_{ZR}^{cent}
includes algorithms such as mini-batch SGD
[
Dekel et al., 2012
,
Woodworth et al., 2020a
]
, but doesn’t include local-update algorithms in

such as local-SGD.

Appendix C

On the Assumption of
Wang et al. [2022]

We will now consider the first-order assumption introduced by
Wang et al. [2022]
. As discussed in Section
2
, Assumption
2
is very restrictive when
\mathcal{X}=\mathbb{R}^{d}
.
Wang et al. [2022]
claim that even with
\mathcal{X}=S^{\star}
, the optima of
F(x)
, Assumption (
2
) (and by implication Assumption
6
) can be restrictive in some settings. They propose an alternative assumption that instead tries to capture how much the local iterates move when initialized at an optimum of the averaged function
F
.

Assumption 7

(Movement at Optima)
.

Given an inner step-size
\eta
, local steps
K
, and
x^{\star}\in\arg\min_{x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}}F(x)
assume,

\frac{1}{M\eta K}\left\lVert\sum_{m\in[M]}x^{\star}-\hat{x}^{m}_{K}\right\rVert_{2}\leq\rho,

where
\hat{x}^{m}_{K}
is the iterate on machine
m
after making
K
local steps (using exact gradients) initialized at
x^{\star}
.

Unlike all other assumptions, note that
\rho
in Assumption
7
can be a function of the hyper-parameters
\eta
and
K
despite normalizing with
\eta K
.
Wang et al. [2022]
argue that Assumption
7
is much less restrictive than the other first-order assumptions (Assumption
2
with
\mathcal{X}=\mathbb{R}^{d}
or with
\mathcal{X}=S^{\star}
). And, when the client’s objectives are strongly convex, they show a provable domination over large-mini-batch SGD in a regime of low heterogeneity (see the discussion in Section
3
). However, it is unclear if the assumption is useful in the general convex setting, where we often empirically see that local SGD outperforms mini-batch SGD. We show in Section
3
that this assumption is not useful in the convex setting because of the following connection with Assumption
2
for
\mathcal{X}=S^{\star}
.

Proposition 6
.

If the functions of the machines
\{F_{m}\}_{m\in[M]}
satisfy Assumption
2
for
\mathcal{X}=S^{\star}
with
\zeta_{\star}
, then we have,

\left\lVert\frac{1}{M\eta K}\sum_{m\in[M]}x^{\star}-\hat{x}^{m}_{K}\right\rVert_{2}\leq\zeta_{\star}{{1+\eta H}^{K-1}-1}.

Proof.

Define
\phi(k):=\left\lVert\frac{1}{M\eta K}\sum_{m\in[M]}x^{\star}-\hat{x}^{m}_{k}\right\rVert_{2}
where
\hat{x}^{m}_{k}
is the
k
th gradient descent iterate on machine
m
initialized at
\hat{x}^{m}_{0}=x^{\star}
. Then note the following,

\displaystyle\phi(K)

\displaystyle=\left\lVert\frac{1}{M\eta K}\sum_{m\in[M]}x^{\star}-\hat{x}^{m}_{K}\right\rVert_{2},

\displaystyle=\left\lVert\frac{1}{M\eta K}\sum_{m\in[M]}x^{\star}-\hat{x}^{m}_{K-1}+\eta\nabla F_{m}(\hat{x}^{m}_{K-1})\right\rVert_{2},

\displaystyle\leq\left\lVert\frac{1}{M\eta K}\sum_{m\in[M]}x^{\star}-\hat{x}^{m}_{K-1}\right\rVert_{2}+\left\lVert\frac{1}{MK}\sum_{m\in[M]}\nabla F_{m}(\hat{x}^{m}_{K-1})\right\rVert_{2},

\displaystyle=\phi(K-1)+\left\lVert\frac{1}{MK}\sum_{m\in[M]}\nabla F_{m}(\hat{x}^{m}_{K-1})-\nabla F_{m}(x^{\star})\right\rVert_{2},

\displaystyle\leq\phi(K-1)+\frac{1}{MK}\sum_{m\in[M]}\left\lVert\nabla F_{m}(\hat{x}^{m}_{K-1})-\nabla F_{m}(x^{\star})\right\rVert_{2},

\displaystyle\leq\phi(K-1)+\frac{H}{MK}\sum_{m\in[M]}\left\lVert\hat{x}^{m}_{K-1}-x^{\star}\right\rVert_{2},

\displaystyle=\phi(K-1)+\frac{H}{K}\delta(K-1),

(24)

where we define
\delta(k):=\frac{1}{M}\sum_{m\in[M]}\left\lVert\hat{x}^{m}_{k}-x^{\star}\right\rVert_{2}
. Now we consider another recursion on
\delta(k)
to introduce the
\zeta_{\star}
assumption:

\displaystyle\delta(k)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{M}\sum_{m\in[M]}\left\lVert\hat{x}^{m}_{k}-x^{\star}\right\rVert_{2},

\displaystyle\leq\frac{1}{M}\sum_{m\in[M]}\left\lVert\hat{x}^{m}_{k-1}-x^{\star}\right\rVert_{2}+\frac{\eta}{M}\sum_{m\in[M]}\left\lVert\nabla F_{m}(\hat{x}_{m}^{k-1})\right\rVert_{2},

\displaystyle\leq\frac{1}{M}\sum_{m\in[M]}\left\lVert\hat{x}^{m}_{k-1}-x^{\star}\right\rVert_{2}+\frac{\eta}{M}\sum_{m\in[M]}{\left\lVert\nabla F_{m}(\hat{x}_{m}^{k-1})-\nabla F_{m}(x^{\star})\right\rVert_{2}+\left\lVert\nabla F_{m}(x^{\star})\right\rVert_{2}},

\displaystyle\leq\frac{(1+\eta H)}{M}\sum_{m\in[M]}\left\lVert\hat{x}^{m}_{k-1}-x^{\star}\right\rVert_{2}+\eta\zeta_{\star},

\displaystyle\leq(1+\eta H)\delta(k-1)+\eta\zeta_{\star},

\displaystyle\leq\frac{\zeta_{\star}}{H}{{1+\eta H}^{k}-1}.\qquad\qquad(\delta(0)=0)

(25)

Plugging (
25
) back into
24
we get,

\displaystyle\phi(K)

\displaystyle\leq\phi(K-1)+\frac{\zeta_{\star}}{K}{{1+\eta H}^{K-1}-1},

\displaystyle=\zeta_{\star}{{1+\eta H}^{K-1}-1},

which proves the claim.
∎

Remark 13
.

When Assumption
2
for
\mathcal{X}=S^{\star}
can be satisfied with
\zeta_{\star}=0
, we can satisfy Assumption
7
with
\rho=0
.

As might be evident already, Proposition
2
has implications for the heterogeneity assumption by
Wang et al. [2022]
. This is because the construction used in the proposition satisfies Assumption
7
with
\rho=0
. In particular, in the general convex setting under Assumption
7
, local SGD can not hope to benefit in the optimization term from a large
K
. This implies that it is
not possible
to explain the effectiveness of local SGD by controlling the movement of the local iterates at the optima, as postulated by
Wang et al. [2022]
. Note that Theorem
2
also applies to Assumption
7
. Thus, large mini-batch SGD is also min-max optimal under Assumptions
2
,
4
, and
7
for convex objectives.

To reconcile this negative result with the upper bound of
Wang et al. [2022]
, we note that their analyses crucially rely on the strong convexity of each machine’s objective and not just the strong convexity of the average function, something which is lacking in our hard instance in Proposition
2
. This highlights that controlling the client drift for local SGD between communication rounds in the general convex setting is much more challenging due to potential directions of no information on each machine (both
A_{1},A_{2}
are rank one in Proposition
2
).

C.1

Beating Mini-batch SGD

Finally, we note that for quadratic functions of the form discussed in Section
5
, we can bound the average movement at optimum as follows,

\displaystyle\rho

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{M\eta K}\left\lVert\sum_{m\in[M]}x^{\star}-x_{K}^{m}\right\rVert_{2}=\frac{1}{M\eta K}\left\lVert\sum_{m\in[M]}x^{\star}-x_{m}^{\star}-(I-\eta A_{m})^{K}(x_{\star}-x_{m}^{\star})\right\rVert_{2}\kern 5.0pt,

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{M\eta K}\left\lVert\sum_{m\in[M]}{I-(I-\eta A_{m})^{K}}(x_{\star}-x_{m}^{\star})\right\rVert_{2}\kern 5.0pt,

\displaystyle\leq\frac{1}{M\eta K}\sum_{m\in[M]}\left\lVert{I-(I-\eta A_{m})^{K}}\right\rVert_{2}\left\lVert x_{\star}-x_{m}^{\star}\right\rVert_{2}\leq\frac{1-(1-\eta L)^{K}}{\eta K}\zeta_{\star}\kern 5.0pt.

If
\eta=\frac{1}{2HK^{p}}
for
p<1
, and
K\to\infty
we get that,

\displaystyle\lim_{K\to\infty}\rho

\displaystyle\leq\lim_{K\to\infty}\frac{1-(1-\eta H)^{K}}{\eta K}\zeta_{\star}\kern 5.0pt,

\displaystyle\leq\lim_{K\to\infty}\frac{1-{1-\frac{1}{2K^{p}}}^{K}}{K^{1-p}}\cdot 2L\zeta_{\star}\kern 5.0pt,

\displaystyle=0\kern 5.0pt.

On the other hand, if
\eta=\frac{1}{2HK}
, then we get that,

\displaystyle\lim_{K\to\infty}\rho

\displaystyle\leq\lim_{K\to\infty}\frac{1-(1-\eta H)^{K}}{\eta K}\zeta_{\star}\kern 5.0pt,

\displaystyle\leq\lim_{K\to\infty}{1-{1-\frac{1}{2K}}^{K}}\cdot 2L\zeta_{\star}\kern 5.0pt,

\displaystyle={1-\frac{1}{\sqrt{e}}}\cdot 2L\zeta_{\star}\kern 5.0pt.

This suggests that the Corollary 2 in
Wang et al. [2022]
must use
\eta=\Omega{\frac{1}{K}}
to ensure that
\rho
can reach arbitrarily small values. Unfortunately the corollary can only be obtained by setting
\eta<\min\left\{\frac{1}{\mu K},\frac{1}{L}\right\}
. Thus, in their existing result, it seems
impossible
to improve the convergence w.r.t. MB-SGD unconditionally. This also highlights that our result in Theorem
4
is much cleaner, depends on only problem-dependentth parameters, and can improve over mini-batch SGD.

Lemma 13

(Stepsize Tuning)
.

Our upper bound has the following recursive form:

\displaystyle r_{t+1}\leq(1-A\eta_{t})r_{t}-\eta_{t}e_{t}+C\eta_{t}^{2}+E\eta_{t}^{3}+G\eta_{t}^{5}\,,

(35)

where
\{r_{t}\}_{t\geq 0}
and
\{e_{t}\}_{t\geq 0}
are two non-negative sequences and
\eta_{t}\leq\frac{1}{D}
for a
D>0
. With the following choice of parameters we recover our recursive upper bound used in the proof of Theorem
5
(see below):

\displaystyle r_{t}=\left\lVert\bar{x}_{t}-x^{\star}\right\rVert_{2}^{2},\quad e_{t}=\Bigr[F(\bar{x}_{t})-F(x^{\star})\Bigr],\quad C=\frac{\sigma^{2}}{M},\quad D=2H,\quad A=\frac{\mu}{2}\,,

\displaystyle E=\frac{24K\tau^{2}\sigma^{2}}{\mu}+\frac{48K^{2}\tau^{2}\zeta^{2}}{\mu}\,,\quad G=\frac{36K^{2}Q^{2}\sigma^{4}}{\mu}+\frac{144Q^{2}K^{4}\zeta^{4}}{\mu}\,.

The recursion (
35
) can be solved as follows:

\displaystyle\frac{1}{W_{T}}\sum_{t=0}^{T}w_{t}e_{t}+Ar_{T+1}

\displaystyle\leq Dr_{0}\exp{\left(\frac{-AT}{D}\right)}+\tilde{\mathcal{O}}\left(\frac{C}{AT}\right)+\tilde{\mathcal{O}}\left(\frac{E}{A^{2}T^{2}}\right)+\tilde{\mathcal{O}}\left(\frac{G}{A^{4}T^{4}}\right)\,,

where we define the coefficient
w_{t}=(1-A\eta)^{-(t+1)}
and
W_{T}=\sum_{t=0}^{T}w_{t}
.

Proof.

The proof is similar to the works
[
Stich, 2019
,
Koloskova et al., 2020
]
. We divide both sides of the recursive inequality by
\eta_{t}
and rearrange the terms:

\displaystyle e_{t}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{(1-A\eta_{t})}{\eta_{t}}r_{t}-\frac{1}{\eta_{t}}r_{t+1}+C\eta_{t}+E\eta_{t}^{2}+G\eta_{t}^{4}

Note that learning rate is fixed
\eta_{t}=\eta
. Then we multiply both sides of the inequality by
w_{t}
and sum over
t
which gives us:

\displaystyle\frac{1}{W_{T}}\sum_{t=0}^{T}w_{t}e_{t}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{1}{W_{T}}\sum_{t=0}^{T}\left[\frac{(1-A\eta)^{-(t+1)}(1-A\eta)}{\eta}r_{t}-\frac{(1-A\eta)^{-(t+1)}}{\eta}r_{t+1}\right]+C\eta+E\eta^{2}+G\eta^{4}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{W_{T}}\sum_{t=0}^{T}\left[\frac{(1-A\eta)^{-t}}{\eta}r_{t}-\frac{(1-A\eta)^{-(t+1)}}{\eta}r_{t+1}\right]+C\eta+E\eta^{2}+G\eta^{4}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{W_{T}}\sum_{t=0}^{T}\left[\frac{w_{t-1}}{\eta}r_{t}-\frac{w_{t}}{\eta}r_{t+1}\right]+C\eta+E\eta^{2}+G\eta^{4}

\displaystyle=\left[\frac{r_{0}}{\eta W_{T}}-\frac{w_{T}}{\eta W_{T}}r_{T+1}\right]+C\eta+E\eta^{2}+G\eta^{4}\,.

So we have:

\displaystyle\frac{1}{W_{T}}\sum_{t=0}^{T}w_{t}e_{t}+\frac{w_{T}}{\eta W_{T}}r_{T+1}\leq\frac{r_{0}}{\eta W_{T}}+C\eta+E\eta^{2}+G\eta^{4}\,.

Also using the fact that
W_{T}\leq\frac{w_{T}}{a\eta}
and
W_{T}\geq(1-A\eta)^{-(T+1)}
we have:

\displaystyle\frac{1}{W_{T}}\sum_{t=0}^{T}w_{t}e_{t}+ar_{T+1}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{(1-A\eta)^{-(T+1)}r_{0}}{\eta}+C\eta+E\eta^{2}+G\eta^{4}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{r_{0}\exp{\left(-a\eta(T+1)\right)}}{\eta}+C\eta+E\eta^{2}+G\eta^{4}\,.

Now we consider two cases:

1)

\frac{1}{D}\geq\frac{\ln\left(\max\left\{2,\frac{A^{2}T^{2}r_{0}}{C}\right\}\right)}{AT}
: We choose
\eta=\frac{\ln\left(\max\left\{2,\frac{A^{2}T^{2}r_{0}}{C}\right\}\right)}{AT}
. With this choice we get a rate of:

\displaystyle\frac{1}{W_{T}}\sum_{t=0}^{T}w_{t}e_{t}+Ar_{T+1}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{r_{0}AT}{\ln\left(\max\left\{2,\frac{A^{2}T^{2}r_{0}}{C}\right\}\right)}\exp\left(-\ln\left(\max\left\{2,\frac{A^{2}T^{2}r_{0}}{C}\right\}\right)\right)+\tilde{\mathcal{O}}\left(\frac{C}{AT}\right)

\displaystyle\quad+\tilde{\mathcal{O}}\left(\frac{E}{A^{2}T^{2}}\right)+\tilde{\mathcal{O}}\left(\frac{G}{A^{4}T^{4}}\right)

\displaystyle\leq\tilde{\mathcal{O}}\left(\frac{r_{0}A}{T^{2}}\right)+\tilde{\mathcal{O}}\left(\frac{C}{AT}\right)+\tilde{\mathcal{O}}\left(\frac{E}{A^{2}T^{2}}\right)+\tilde{\mathcal{O}}\left(\frac{G}{A^{4}T^{4}}\right)\,.

2)

\frac{1}{D}\leq\frac{\ln\left(\max\left\{2,\frac{A^{2}T^{2}r_{0}}{C}\right\}\right)}{AT}
: We choose
\eta=\frac{1}{D}
. With this choice we get a rate of:

\displaystyle\frac{1}{W_{T}}\sum_{t=0}^{T}w_{t}e_{t}+Ar_{T+1}

\displaystyle\leq Dr_{0}\exp{\left(\frac{-AT}{D}\right)}+\frac{C}{D}+\frac{E}{D^{2}}+\frac{G}{D^{4}}

\displaystyle\leq Dr_{0}\exp{\left(\frac{-AT}{D}\right)}+\tilde{\mathcal{O}}\left(\frac{C}{AT}\right)+\tilde{\mathcal{O}}\left(\frac{E}{A^{2}T^{2}}\right)+\tilde{\mathcal{O}}\left(\frac{G}{A^{4}T^{4}}\right)\,.

∎

Proof.

The proof follows a similar approach to
Yuan and Ma [2020]
while we assume that we have heterogeneity. In the following proof, we use the same notation as the work
Woodworth et al. [2020a]
. We drop the subscript
r
for simplicity and use
t
instead which is in the range
[0,KR-1]
. We use the notation
x^{m}_{t}
as the parameters of client
m

t
and
g^{m}_{t}
as the stochastic gradient on client
m
at time step
t
. Also because
\beta=1

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{t}\left[\left\lVert\bar{x}_{t+1}-x^{\star}\right\rVert_{2}^{2}\right]

D.5

Proof of Corollary
3

We use the regularization technique to derive the convergence rate for the convex case from the strongly-convex result. This technique is standard in the literature, see e.g.
[
Hazan and others, 2016
]
. For the sake of completeness, we repeat the argument here.

Let
F(x)
be a convex function. We construct a regularized version of this function
F_{\mu}(x)
as:

\displaystyle F_{\mu}(x)=F(x)+\frac{\mu}{2}\left\lVert x-x_{0}\right\rVert_{2}^{2}\,.

Next we define:

\displaystyle x^{\star}_{\mu}

\displaystyle=\underset{x}{\arg\min}\hskip 2.84526ptF_{\mu}(x)\,,

\displaystyle x^{\star}

\displaystyle=\underset{x}{\arg\min}\hskip 2.84526ptF(x)\,.

We have that
F_{\mu}(x^{\star}_{\mu})\leq F_{\mu}(x^{\star})
. Then we upper bound the function sub-optimality for the convex function
F(x)
:

\displaystyle F(\bar{x}_{t})-F(x^{\star})

\displaystyle\leq F_{\mu}(\bar{x}_{t})-\frac{\mu}{2}\left\lVert\bar{x}_{t}-x_{0}\right\rVert_{2}^{2}-F_{\mu}(x^{\star})+\frac{\mu}{2}\left\lVert x^{\star}-x_{0}\right\rVert_{2}^{2}

\displaystyle\leq F_{\mu}(\bar{x}_{t})-F_{\mu}(x^{\star})+\frac{\mu}{2}\left\lVert x^{\star}-x_{0}\right\rVert_{2}^{2}

\displaystyle\leq F_{\mu}(\bar{x}_{t})-F_{\mu}(x^{\star}_{\mu})+\frac{\mu}{2}\left\lVert x^{\star}-x_{0}\right\rVert_{2}^{2}

\displaystyle\leq F_{\mu}(\bar{x}_{t})-F_{\mu}(x^{\star}_{\mu})+\frac{\mu}{2}B^{2}\,.

The last step is to tune the
\mu
. We assume that we want to achieve
\epsilon
-accuracy when running local SGD on the convex function
F
, so we have:

\displaystyle F(\bar{x}_{t})-F(x^{\star})

\displaystyle\leq\left(F_{\mu}(\bar{x}_{t})-F_{\mu}(x^{\star}_{\mu})\right)+\frac{\mu}{2}B^{2}\leq\epsilon\,.

To satisfy this condition, it is enough to ensure that both terms in the rate are less than
\frac{\epsilon}{2}
. We solve this for the regularization term, and we get:

\displaystyle\frac{\mu}{2}B^{2}\leq\frac{\epsilon}{2}\,,

which results in:

\displaystyle\mu\leq\frac{\epsilon}{B^{2}}\,.

Now we proceed by replacing
\mu
in all terms and deriving the rate.

Proof.

By assuming
\mu=\frac{\epsilon}{B^{2}}
, we will find the condition on
K
and
R
to reach
\epsilon
-accuracy using Theorem
5
. For simplicity, we drop all constant numbers which do not affect the rate. To recall, in the strongly convex case we had proven a rate of:

\displaystyle HB^{2}\exp\left(-\frac{\mu KR}{H}\right)+\tilde{\mathcal{O}}\left(\frac{\sigma^{2}}{\mu MKR}\right)+\tilde{\mathcal{O}}\left(\frac{\tau^{2}\sigma^{2}}{\mu^{3}KR^{2}}\right)+\tilde{\mathcal{O}}\left(\frac{\tau^{2}\zeta^{2}}{\mu^{3}R^{2}}\right)+\tilde{\mathcal{O}}\left(\frac{Q^{2}\sigma^{4}}{\mu^{5}K^{2}R^{4}}\right)+\tilde{\mathcal{O}}\left(\frac{Q^{2}\zeta^{4}}{\mu^{5}R^{4}}\right)\,.

For the first term we solve the inequality:

\displaystyle HB^{2}\exp\left(-\frac{\epsilon KR}{HB^{2}}\right)\leq\epsilon\quad\Longrightarrow\quad KR\geq\frac{HB^{2}}{\epsilon}\ln\left(\frac{\epsilon}{HB^{2}}\right)=\tilde{\mathcal{O}}\left(\frac{HB^{2}}{\epsilon}\right)\,.

For the second term we solve the inequality:

\displaystyle\frac{\sigma^{2}B^{2}}{\epsilon MKR}\leq\epsilon\quad\Longrightarrow\quad KR\geq\frac{\sigma^{2}B^{2}}{M\epsilon^{2}}\,.

For the third term we solve the inequality:

\displaystyle\frac{\tau^{2}\sigma^{2}B^{6}}{\epsilon^{3}KR^{2}}\leq\epsilon\quad\Longrightarrow\quad KR^{2}\geq\frac{\tau^{2}\sigma^{2}B^{6}}{\epsilon^{4}}\,.

For the fourth term we solve the inequality:

\displaystyle\frac{\tau^{2}\zeta^{2}B^{6}}{\epsilon^{3}R^{2}}\leq\epsilon\quad\Longrightarrow\quad R^{2}\geq\frac{\tau^{2}\zeta^{2}B^{6}}{\epsilon^{4}}\,.

For the fifth term we solve the inequality:

\displaystyle\frac{Q^{2}\sigma^{4}}{\mu^{5}K^{2}R^{4}}\leq\epsilon\quad\Longrightarrow\quad K^{2}R^{4}\geq\frac{Q^{2}\sigma^{4}B^{10}}{\epsilon^{6}}\,.

And for the last term:

\displaystyle\frac{Q^{2}\zeta^{4}B^{10}}{\epsilon^{5}R^{4}}\leq\epsilon\quad\Longrightarrow\quad R^{4}\geq\frac{Q^{2}\zeta^{4}B^{10}}{\epsilon^{6}}\,.

Finally, the rate would be the maximum of above terms which we can write:

\displaystyle\tilde{\mathcal{O}}\left(\max\left\{\frac{HB^{2}}{\epsilon},\frac{\sigma^{2}B^{2}}{M\epsilon^{2}},\frac{\tau^{2}\sigma^{2}B^{6}}{\epsilon^{4}},\frac{\tau^{2}\zeta^{2}B^{6}}{\epsilon^{4}},\frac{Q^{2}\sigma^{4}B^{10}}{\epsilon^{6}},\frac{Q^{2}\zeta^{4}B^{10}}{\epsilon^{6}}\right\}\right)\leq

\displaystyle\tilde{\mathcal{O}}\left(\frac{HB^{2}}{\epsilon}+\frac{\sigma^{2}B^{2}}{M\epsilon^{2}}+\frac{\tau^{2}\sigma^{2}B^{6}}{\epsilon^{4}}+\frac{\tau^{2}\zeta^{2}B^{6}}{\epsilon^{4}}+\frac{Q^{2}\sigma^{4}B^{10}}{\epsilon^{6}}+\frac{Q^{2}\zeta^{4}B^{10}}{\epsilon^{6}}\right)\,.

Or in terms of
K
and

\displaystyle\tilde{\mathcal{O}}\left(\frac{HB^{2}}{KR}+\frac{\sigma B}{\sqrt{MKR}}+\frac{(\tau\sigma B^{3})^{1/2}}{K^{1/4}R^{1/2}}+\frac{(\tau\zeta B^{3})^{1/2}}{R^{1/2}}+\frac{(Q\sigma^{2}B^{5})^{1/3}}{K^{1/3}R^{2/3}}+\frac{(Q\zeta^{2}B^{5})^{1/3}}{R^{2/3}}\right)\,.

It’s worth mentioning that during the process of deriving rate for convex regime from strongly convex regime, we also ignore some logarithmic factors which is why we used the notation
\tilde{\mathcal{O}}(.)
. In fact the term
\frac{\ln\left(\max\left\{2,\frac{M\mu^{2}T^{2}B^{2}}{\sigma^{2}}\right\}\right)}{\mu T}
(some constants are dropped for simplicity) that we use for tuning the stepsize depends on
\mu

\mu=\frac{\epsilon}{B^{2}}
this term becomes
\frac{B^{2}\ln\left(\max\left\{2,\frac{M\epsilon^{2}T^{2}}{B^{2}\sigma^{2}}\right\}\right)}{\epsilon T}
which will be used to upper bound the terms in our rate which are in the form of
\frac{C}{D}+\frac{E}{D^{2}}+\frac{G}{D^{4}}
(refer to
D.3

\frac{1}{D}\leq\frac{B^{2}\ln\left(\max\left\{2,\frac{M\epsilon^{2}T^{2}}{B^{2}\sigma^{2}}\right\}\right)}{\epsilon T}

And to achieve

E.1

Proof of Proposition

Assuming the local SGD algorithm converges, i.e., the hyper-parameters are set to achieve that and
R\to\infty
, we would like to calculate its fixed point. Let’s denote the fixed point by
x_{\infty}
. Then
x_{\infty}

\displaystyle x_{\infty}=x_{\infty}+\frac{\beta}{M}\sum_{m\in[M]}\Delta^{m}(x_{\infty})\equiv\sum_{m\in[M]}\Delta^{m}(x_{\infty})=0,

where
\Delta^{m}(x_{\infty})
is the update on machine

for a communication round starting at the fixed point
x_{\infty}
. Note that the above equation does not depend on
\beta
. Also, note that this is very similar to the average drift assumption of

. Unwinding the update, we get the following,

\displaystyle\sum_{m\in[M]}\sum_{k\in[K]}A_{m}(I-\eta A_{m})^{k-1}(x_{\infty}-x_{m}^{\star})=0,

\displaystyle\Leftrightarrow^{\text{if $\eta=0$}}x_{\infty}=\frac{1}{M}\sum_{m\in[M]}A^{-1}A_{m}x_{m}^{\star}=x^{\star},

\displaystyle\Leftrightarrow^{\text{if $\eta>0$}}x_{\infty}=\frac{1}{M}\sum_{m\in[M]}C^{-1}C_{m}x_{m}^{\star},

where
C_{m}:=I-(I-\eta C_{m})^{K}
, and
C:=\frac{1}{M}\sum_{m\in[M]}C_{m}
. Note that
x_{\infty}(\eta,K)
is a function of
\eta,K
and is unaffected by the choice of
\beta
. The above proof also highlights a key limitation of the assumption
7
; they assume that local SGD is going to converge to
x^{\star}
as opposed to any other fixed point, defining the average drift at the optimum. This makes their analysis more complicated than it should have been.

\displaystyle\quad+(I-\eta A_{n})^{K}-(I-\eta A_{n})^{K}\kern 5.0pt,

\displaystyle=(I-\eta A_{m})^{K}+\sum_{k\in[K-1]}(I-\eta A_{n})^{k}(I-\eta A_{m})^{K-k}-\sum_{k\in[K]}(I-\eta A_{n})^{k}(I-\eta A_{m})^{K-k}\kern 5.0pt,

\displaystyle=\sum_{k\in[0,K-1]}(I-\eta A_{n})^{k}(I-\eta A_{m})^{K-k}-\sum_{k\in[K]}(I-\eta A_{n})^{k}(I-\eta A_{m})^{K-k}\kern 5.0pt,

\displaystyle=\sum_{k\in[K]}(I-\eta A_{n})^{k-1}(I-\eta A_{m})^{K-k+1}-\sum_{k\in[K]}(I-\eta A_{n})^{k}(I-\eta A_{m})^{K-k}\kern 5.0pt,

\displaystyle=\sum_{k\in[K]}\left[(I-\eta A_{n})^{k-1}(I-\eta A_{m})^{K-k+1}-(I-\eta A_{n})^{k}(I-\eta A_{m})^{K-k}\right]\kern 5.0pt,

\displaystyle=\sum_{k\in[K]}\left[\eta(I-\eta A_{n})^{k-1}(A_{n}-A_{m})(I-\eta A_{m})^{K-k}\right],

which upon taking the norm implies,

\displaystyle\left\lVert(I-\eta A_{m})^{K}-(I-\eta A_{n})^{K}\right\rVert_{2}

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{k\in[K]}\eta\left\lVert I-\eta A_{n}\right\rVert_{2}^{k-1}\left\lVert A-n-A_{m}\right\rVert_{2}\left\lVert I-\eta A_{m}\right\rVert_{2}^{K-k},

\displaystyle\leq\eta\sum_{k\in[K]}(1-\eta\mu)^{k-1}\tau(1-\eta\mu)^{K-k},

\displaystyle\leq\eta\tau K(1-\eta\mu)^{K-1},

which finishes the proof of the inequality and hence the proof as well.
∎

E.3

Proof of Theorem
4

Having identified the convergence point of local SGD in the previous section as
x_{\infty}(\eta,K)
, in this section, we will characterize the rate of convergence to this point.

Proof.

We first write the inner loop iterated on machine
m
as follows,

\displaystyle x_{r,K}^{m}

\displaystyle=x_{r,K-1}^{m}-\eta{A_{m}(x_{r,K-1}^{m}-x_{m}^{\star})},

\displaystyle=x_{m}^{\star}+{I-\eta A_{m}}^{K}{x_{r,0}^{m}-x_{m}^{\star}},

\displaystyle=x_{m}^{\star}+{I-\eta A_{m}}^{K}{x_{r-1}-x_{m}^{\star}}.

Subtracting
x_{r-1}
from both sides,

\displaystyle x_{r,K}^{m}-x_{r-1}

\displaystyle=x_{m}^{\star}-x_{r-1}+{I-\eta A_{m}}^{K}{x_{r-1}-x_{m}^{\star}},

\displaystyle={I-{I-\eta A_{m}}^{K}}{x_{m}^{\star}-x_{r-1}}.

With this in hand, we can write the update across communication rounds as follows,

\displaystyle x_{r}

\displaystyle=x_{r-1}+\frac{\beta}{M}\sum_{m\in[M]}{x_{r,K}^{m}-x_{r-1}},

\displaystyle=x_{r-1}+\frac{\beta}{M}\sum_{m\in[M]}{I-{I-\eta A_{m}}^{K}}{x_{m}^{\star}-x_{r-1}}.

Subtracting the fixed point
\hat{x}
on both sides, we get,

\displaystyle x_{r}-\hat{x}

\displaystyle=x_{r-1}-\hat{x}+\frac{\beta}{M}\sum_{m\in[M]}{I-{I-\eta A_{m}}^{K}}{x_{m}^{\star}-x_{r-1}},

\displaystyle=x_{r-1}-\hat{x}+\frac{\beta}{M}\sum_{m\in[M]}C_{m}{x_{m}^{\star}-x_{r-1}},

\displaystyle={I-\beta C}x_{r-1}-x_{\infty}+\frac{\beta B}{M}\sum_{m\in[M]}C^{-1}C_{m}x_{m}^{\star},

\displaystyle={I-\beta C}x_{r-1}-x_{\infty}+\beta Cx_{\infty},

\displaystyle={I-\beta C}{x_{r-1}-x_{\infty}}.

Unrolling this recursion for
R
communication rounds,

\displaystyle x_{R}-x_{\infty}={I-\beta B}^{R}{x_{0}-x_{\infty}}.

Noting the initialization
x_{0}=0
we get,

\displaystyle x_{R}

\displaystyle={I-{I-\beta C}^{R}}x_{\infty},

\displaystyle={I-{I-\frac{\beta}{M}\sum_{m\in[M]}{I-{I-\eta A_{m}}^{K}}}^{R}}x_{\infty},

\displaystyle={I-{(1-\beta)\cdot I+\frac{\beta}{M}\sum_{m\in[M]}{I-\eta A_{m}}^{K}}^{R}}x_{\infty}.

In particular, we can bound the distance between
x_{R}
and
x_{\infty}
as follows,

\displaystyle\left\lVert x_{R}-x_{\infty}\right\rVert_{2}

\displaystyle=\left\lVert{I-\beta C}^{R}{x_{0}-x_{\infty}}\right\rVert_{2},

\displaystyle\leq\left\lVert I-\beta C\right\rVert_{2}^{R}\left\lVert x_{\infty}\right\rVert_{2},

\displaystyle=\left\lVert I-\beta\cdot\frac{1}{M}\sum_{m\in[M]}{I-{I-\eta A_{m}}^{K}}\right\rVert_{2}^{R}\left\lVert x_{\infty}\right\rVert_{2},

If we ensure that
\beta<1/\left\lVert C\right\rVert_{2}
, then the distance contracts with more communication rounds. Note that,

\displaystyle\left\lVert C\right\rVert_{2}

\displaystyle=\left\lVert\frac{1}{M}\sum_{m\in[M]}{I-{I-\eta A_{m}}^{K}}\right\rVert_{2},

\displaystyle=\left\lVert I-\frac{1}{M}\sum_{m\in[M]}{I-\eta A_{m}}^{K}\right\rVert_{2},

\displaystyle\leq\max_{m\in[M]}\left\lVert I-(I-\eta A_{m})^{K}\right\rVert_{2},

\displaystyle\leq 1-(1-\eta H)^{K}.

This implies that we will have contraction with communication if
\beta<\frac{1}{1-(1-\eta H)^{K}}
. In this regime, we can calculate the overall convergence as follows,

\displaystyle\left\lVert x_{R}-x_{\infty}\right\rVert_{2}

\displaystyle\leq\left\lVert I-\beta C\right\rVert_{2}^{R}\left\lVert x_{\infty}\right\rVert_{2},

\displaystyle\leq(1-\beta\left\lVert C^{-1}\right\rVert_{2}^{-1})^{R}\left\lVert x_{\infty}\right\rVert_{2},

\displaystyle\leq{1-\beta{1-{1-\eta\mu}^{K}}}^{R}\left\lVert x_{\infty}\right\rVert_{2},

\displaystyle\leq{1-\beta{1-{1-\eta\mu}^{K}}}^{R}\left\lVert C^{-1}\right\rVert_{2}\left\lVert\frac{1}{M}\sum_{m\in[M]}C_{m}x_{m}^{\star}\right\rVert_{2},

\displaystyle\leq{1-\beta{1-{1-\eta\mu}^{K}}}^{R}\cdot\frac{1-{1-\eta L}^{K}}{1-{1-\eta\mu}^{K}}B\kern 5.0pt.

We can note that when
\beta=1
this simplifies to,

\displaystyle\left\lVert x_{R}-x_{\infty}\right\rVert_{2}

\displaystyle\leq{1-\eta\mu}^{KR}\cdot\frac{1-{1-\eta L}^{K}}{1-{1-\eta\mu}^{K}}B\kern 5.0pt.

When
\beta=\frac{1}{c}\cdot\frac{1}{1-(1-\eta L)^{K}}
for some
c>1
this reduces to,

\displaystyle\left\lVert x_{R}-x_{\infty}\right\rVert_{2}

\displaystyle\leq{1-\frac{1}{c}\cdot\frac{1-{1-\eta\mu}^{K}}{1-{1-\eta L}^{K}}}^{R}\cdot\frac{1-{1-\eta L}^{K}}{1-{1-\eta\mu}^{K}}B,

\displaystyle={1-\frac{1}{c\kappa^{\prime}}}^{R}\kappa^{\prime}B,

where we define,

\kappa^{\prime}:=\frac{1-{1-\eta L}^{K}}{1-{1-\eta\mu}^{K}}.

This finishes the convergence proof for local GD. To finish the proof of the theorem, we combine this result with the fixed point discrepancy implied by Proposition
5
.
∎
