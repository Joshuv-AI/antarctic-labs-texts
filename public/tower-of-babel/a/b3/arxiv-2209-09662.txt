Stability of the vortex in micromagnetics and related models
Xavier Lamy Elio Marconi

Abstract
We consider line-energy models of Ginzburg-Landau type in a two-dimensional simply-connected bounded domain. Configurations of vanishing energy have been characterized by Jabin, Otto and Perthame: the domain must be a disk, and the configuration a vortex. We prove a quantitative version of this statement in the class of C 1 , 1 C^{1,1} domains, improving on previous results by Lorent. In particular, the deviation of the domain from a disk is controlled by a power of the energy, and that power is optimal. The main tool is a Lagrangian representation introduced by the second author, which allows to decompose the energy along characteristic curves.

Introduction

1.1
Models

Several models arising in a variety of physical applications (micromagnetics, smectic liquid crystals, blistering) have in common that, as a characteristics length scale
\varepsilon
tends to 0, bounded-energy configurations converge to two-dimensional vector fields
m\colon\Omega\to{\mathbb{R}}^{2}
satisfying the eikonal equation

\displaystyle|m|=1\text{ a.e. in }\Omega,\qquad\nabla\cdot(\mathbf{1}_{\Omega}m)=0\text{ in }{\mathbb{R}}^{2}.

(1)

Here
\Omega\subset{\mathbb{R}}^{2}
is a smooth, simply connected bounded domain, and the divergence constraint on the trivially extended field
\mathbf{1}_{\Omega}m
amounts to
\nabla\cdot m=0
in
\Omega
and
m\cdot n_{\partial\Omega}=0
on
\partial\Omega
, where
n_{\partial\Omega}
is the exterior unit normal (and the last condition makes sense whenever
m
admits a strong trace on
\partial\Omega
). Examples of such models include:

•

The Aviles-Giga functional, introduced in
[
4
]
as a simplified model for smectic liquid crystals and proposed as a model for thin film blisters in
[
32
]
(see the introduction of
[
21
]
for other applications),

\displaystyle E^{AG}_{\varepsilon}(m;\Omega)=\frac{\varepsilon}{2}\int_{\Omega}|\nabla m|^{2}+\frac{1}{2\varepsilon}\int_{\Omega}(1-|m|^{2})^{2},

(2)

\displaystyle m\colon\Omega\to{\mathbb{R}}^{2},\qquad\nabla\cdot(\mathbf{1}_{\Omega}m)=0\text{ in }{\mathbb{R}}^{2}.

Note that the Aviles-Giga functional is more often expressed in terms of
u
such that
\nabla^{\perp}u=m
in
\Omega
and
u=0
on
\partial\Omega
, however in a simply connected domain the two formulations are equivalent.

•

A micromagnetics model studied in
[
36
,
37
]
,

\displaystyle E^{RS}_{\varepsilon}(m;\Omega)=\frac{\varepsilon}{2}\int_{\Omega}|\nabla m|^{2}+\frac{1}{2\varepsilon}\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{2}}|H|^{2},

(3)

\displaystyle m\colon\Omega\to\mathbb{S}^{1}\subset{\mathbb{R}}^{2},\qquad H\colon{\mathbb{R}}^{2}\to{\mathbb{R}}^{2},

\displaystyle\nabla\times H=0\text{ and }\nabla\cdot(H+1_{\Omega}m)=0\text{ in }{\mathbb{R}}^{2}.

•

A more general micromagnetics model studied in
[
1
]
,

\displaystyle E^{ARS}_{\varepsilon}(m;\Omega)=\frac{\varepsilon}{2}\int_{\Omega}|\nabla m|^{2}+\frac{1}{2\varepsilon}\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{2}}|H|^{2}+\frac{1}{2c_{\varepsilon}}\int_{\Omega}|m_{3}|^{2},

(4)

\displaystyle m\colon\Omega\to\mathbb{S}^{2}\subset{\mathbb{R}}^{3},\quad 0<c_{\varepsilon}\leq\varepsilon^{1+\delta},

\displaystyle H\colon{\mathbb{R}}^{2}\to{\mathbb{R}}^{2},\qquad\nabla\times H=0\text{ and }\nabla\cdot\left(H+(m_{1},m_{2})\mathbf{1}_{\Omega}\right)=0\text{ in }{\mathbb{R}}^{2}.

For all these models, sequences of bounded energy as
\varepsilon\to 0
are precompact in
L^{2}(\Omega)

[
1
,
2
,
11
,
36
]
, and limits of converging subsequences satisfy the eikonal equation (
1
). (In the case of (

so we can identify it with an
{\mathbb{R}}^{2}
-valued map.)

A large literature is devoted to understanding the behavior of minimizers
m_{\varepsilon}
of
E^{AG}_{\varepsilon}(\cdot;\Omega)
as
\varepsilon\to 0
.
In particular it is conjectured in
[
32
]
that the minimizers
m_{\varepsilon}
converge to
m_{\ast}=\nabla^{\perp}\dist(\cdot,\partial\Omega)
when
\Omega
is convex (counterexamples in nonconvex domains are given in
[
17

A positive answer is obtained in
[
19
]
when
\Omega
is a disk, and in
[
29
]
for some special domains including ellipses (under the additional boundary constraint
m_{\lfloor\partial\Omega}=-in_{\partial\Omega}
).
For
E_{\varepsilon}^{RS}
much more is known: that conjecture has been verified
[
37
]
, and limits of non-minimizing sequences also have a well-understood structure
[
30
]
.

In
[
19
]
the authors characterize zero-energy states, that is, limits of sequences with energy (
2
) converging to 0 as
\varepsilon\to 0
. In addition to the eikonal equation (
1
), zero-energy states satisfy the kinetic equation

\displaystyle e^{is}\cdot\nabla_{x}\mathbf{1}_{m(x)\cdot e^{is}>0}=0\qquad\text{in }\Omega,\text{ for all }s\in{\mathbb{R}}.

(5)

This is also valid for zero-energy states of (
3
)
[
19
]
and of (
4
)
[
1
]
(see Appendix
A
). It is shown in
[

that, if a smooth bounded simply connected domain

admits a zero energy state, that is, a solution of (
1
) and (

), then
\Omega
must be a disk
\Omega=B_{R}(x_{0})
, and
m
must be a vortex
m(x)=\pm i(x-x_{0})/|x-x_{0}|
, or equivalently

. Various generalizations can be found in
[
6
,
13
,
22
,
23
,
28
]
.

1.2
Main results

The main purpose of this work is to provide a quantitative version of the characterization of zero-energy states from
[
19
]
: estimate how much
\Omega
differs from a disk and
m
from a vortex, in terms of the energy of an approximating sequence
m_{\varepsilon}\to m
. Previous results in this direction are proven in
[
25
,
26
]
. Under the assumption that
\Omega
is a
C^{2}
convex domain renormalized to satisfy
\diam(\Omega)=2
, it is shown in
[
26
]
that there exists
x_{*}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{2}
such that

\displaystyle|\Omega\Delta B_{1}(x_{*})|+\int_{\Omega}\left|m+i\frac{x-x_{*}}{|x-x_{*}|}\right|^{2}dx\leq CE^{AG}_{\varepsilon}(m;\Omega)^{\delta},

(6)

\displaystyle\text{whenever }\nabla\cdot m=0\text{ in }\Omega\quad\text{and }m\cdot\tau=-1\text{ on }\partial\Omega,

for some absolute constants
C>0
,
\delta=2^{-9}
, and
\tau=in_{\partial\Omega}
a unit tangent to
\partial\Omega
.
Note that the boundary condition
m\cdot\tau=-1
, commonly imposed in the study of the Aviles-Giga functional (see e.g.
[
2
,
5
,
7
,
21
]
), is more restrictive than the condition
m\cdot n_{\partial\Omega}=0
enforced in (
2
) (which is natural in micromagnetics models).

Our goal is to obtain an estimate similar to (
6
), but with a sharp exponent
\delta
, in the limit
\varepsilon\to 0
.
To present our results in a unified setting, we consider the energy functional

\displaystyle F_{\varepsilon}(m;\Omega)

\displaystyle=\frac{\varepsilon}{2}\int_{\Omega}|\nabla m|^{2}+\frac{1}{2\varepsilon}\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{2}}|H|^{2}+\frac{1}{2\varepsilon}\int_{\Omega}(1-|m|^{2})^{2}+\frac{1}{2\varepsilon}\int_{\Omega}|m_{3}|^{4},

(7)

\displaystyle m\colon\Omega\to{\mathbb{R}}^{3},\quad H\colon{\mathbb{R}}^{2}\to{\mathbb{R}}^{2},

\displaystyle\nabla\times H=0\text{ and }\nabla\cdot\left(H+(m_{1},m_{2})\mathbf{1}_{\Omega}\right)=0\text{ in }{\mathbb{R}}^{2}.

This functional satisfies
F_{\varepsilon}\leq E_{\varepsilon}^{AG},E^{RS}_{\varepsilon},E_{\varepsilon}^{ARS}
(for
F_{\varepsilon}\leq E_{\varepsilon}^{ARS}
, note that any
m\in\mathbb{S}^{2}
satisfies
|m_{3}|^{4}\leq|m_{3}|^{2}
) and the compactness proof of
[
11
]
applies (see Appendix
A
) to show that any sequence
(m_{\varepsilon})
with bounded energy
F_{\varepsilon}(m_{\varepsilon};\Omega)\leq C
is precompact in
L^{2}(\Omega)
, and its limits
m=\lim m_{\varepsilon}
are
{\mathbb{R}}^{2}
-valued and satisfy the eikonal equation (
1
).
(Similar functionals are considered in
[
18
]
.)
We obtain a sharp bound for the
L^{2}
-distance between the unit normal to
\partial\Omega
and the unit normal to a disk, in terms of the limit of
F_{\varepsilon}(m_{\varepsilon};\Omega)
.

Theorem 1.1
.

Let
\Omega\subset{\mathbb{R}}^{2}
be a simply connected open set of class
C^{1,1}
with
\mathcal{H}^{1}(\partial\Omega)=2\pi
and

\sup_{\partial\Omega}|\kappa|\leq K
for some
K>0
, where
\kappa
denotes the curvature of
\partial\Omega
. There exists
c>0
depending only on
K
such that

\displaystyle\inf_{x_{*}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{2}}\int_{\partial\Omega}\left|n_{\partial\Omega}(x)-\frac{x-x_{*}}{|x-x_{*}|}\right|^{2}d\mathcal{H}^{1}(x)\leq c\,\liminf_{\varepsilon\to 0}\;\inf_{H^{1}(\Omega;{\mathbb{R}}^{3})}F_{\varepsilon}(\cdot;\Omega),

(8)

where
F_{\varepsilon}
is the functional defined in (
7
).

Moreover, this estimate is sharp:

Proposition 1.2
.

There exist a family of convex domains
\{\Omega_{N}\}_{N\geq 3}
of class
C^{1,1}
with uniformly bounded curvature such that

\displaystyle\frac{c_{1}}{N^{2}}

\displaystyle\geq\inf_{x_{*}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{2}}\int_{\partial\Omega_{N}}\left|n_{\partial\Omega_{N}}(x)-\frac{x-x_{*}}{|x-x_{*}|}\right|^{2}d\mathcal{H}^{1}(x)

\displaystyle\geq c_{2}\,\liminf_{\varepsilon\to 0}\;\inf_{H^{1}(\Omega;{\mathbb{R}}^{3})}F_{\varepsilon}(\cdot;\Omega_{N})\geq\frac{c_{3}}{N^{2}},

for some absolute constants
c_{1},c_{2},c_{3}>0
.

Remark 1.3
.

The estimate (
8
) is sharp also when replacing
\inf F_{\varepsilon}
with any of the (larger)
\inf E_{\varepsilon}^{AG}
,
\inf E^{RS}_{\varepsilon}
or
\inf E_{\varepsilon}^{ARS}
, where the infimums are taken over all admissible maps for the corresponding functionals as described in (
2
), (
3
) and (
4
).
This will be clear from the explicit description of the
\Omega_{N}
’s in §
6.3
.

As corollaries of Theorem
1.1
and its proof we obtain two other estimates, which are however probably not sharp. The first corollary provides a bound on the distance of the boundary
\partial\Omega
to the boundary of a disk, which is perhaps a more natural way of measuring how close
\Omega
is to a disk.

Corollary 1.4
.

Let
\Omega
be as in Theorem
1.1
. Then

\displaystyle\inf_{x_{*}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{2}}\dist(\partial\Omega,\partial B_{1}(x_{*}))\leq c\,\liminf_{\varepsilon\to 0}\;\inf F_{\varepsilon}(\cdot;\Omega)^{\frac{1}{2}}.

for some constant
c=c(K)>0
.

The second corollary provides a bound on the distance of a limiting map
m
from a vortex.

Corollary 1.5
.

Let
\Omega
be as in Theorem
1.1
and
m=\lim m_{\varepsilon}
as
\varepsilon\to 0
, where
(m_{\varepsilon})
is a sequence of admissible maps for the functional
F_{\varepsilon}
. Then there exists
\alpha\in\{\pm 1\}
and
x_{*}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{2}
such that

\displaystyle\int_{\Omega}\left|m(x)-\alpha\,i\frac{x-x_{*}}{|x-x_{*}|}\right|^{4}\,dx\leq c\,\,\liminf_{\varepsilon\to 0}F_{\varepsilon}(m_{\varepsilon};\Omega)^{\frac{2}{3}},

for some constant
c=c(K)>0
.

Remark 1.6
.

In comparison with the estimate (
6
) for
E_{\varepsilon}^{AG}
from
[
26
]
, we don’t require
\Omega
to be convex, and impose only the boundary condition
m\cdot n_{\partial\Omega}=0
on limit maps.
However, we only obtain bounds in the limit
\varepsilon\to 0
, while (
6
) is valid for any fixed
\varepsilon>0
.
Note that the constant
c
in (
8
) depends on
K
, while the constant
C
in (
6
) is absolute;
on the other hand it is not possible to obtain an absolute constant if we drop the assumption of
\Omega
being convex.
Indeed if
\Omega_{\delta}=B_{1}((-1+\delta,0))\cup B_{1}((1-\delta,0))

(or rather, a mollification of this domain at scale much smaller than
\delta
),
then
\liminf_{\varepsilon\to 0}\inf F_{\varepsilon}(\cdot,\Omega_{\delta})
tends to 0 as
\delta\to 0
.
This can be checked by using the solution of (
1
) in
\Omega_{\delta}
given by
m_{\delta}=i\nabla d_{\delta}
, where
d_{\delta}(x)=\dist(x,\partial\Omega_{\delta})
, and the upper bound (see e.g.
[
7
,
34
]
)
\liminf_{\varepsilon\to 0}\inf F_{\varepsilon}(\cdot,\Omega_{\delta})\leq C\int_{J_{\delta}}|[m_{\delta}]|^{3}d\mathcal{H}^{1}
, where
J_{\delta}
is the jump set of
m_{\delta}
.

Our proofs of Theorem
1.1
and its corollaries rely on a generalization of the zero-energy kinetic equation (
5
) to limits
m=\lim_{\varepsilon\to 0}m_{\varepsilon}
of bounded energy sequences:

\displaystyle e^{is}\cdot\nabla_{x}\mathbf{1}_{m(x)\cdot e^{is}>0}=\partial_{s}\sigma,\qquad\sigma\in\mathcal{M}(\Omega\times{\mathbb{R}}/2\pi{\mathbb{Z}}),

(9)

\displaystyle|\sigma|(\Omega\times{\mathbb{R}}/2\pi{\mathbb{Z}})\leq c_{0}\liminf_{\varepsilon\to 0}F_{\varepsilon}(m_{\varepsilon};\Omega),

where
c_{0}>0
is an absolute constant.
This kinetic formulation, inspired by the field of scalar conservation laws
[
24
]
, was first obtained in
[
20
]
for the Aviles-Giga functional (
2
) (see also
[
12
]
) and in
[
37
]
for the micromagnetics model (

). It also applies to the more general functional

(see Appendix

). It is worth noting that it implies that

admits strong traces along
1

[
38
]
or
[
10
]
), and in particular along
\partial\Omega
.

Among the measures
\sigma
satisfying (
9
),
we consider the measure
\sigma_{\mathrm{min}}
with minimal total variation

|\sigma|(\Omega\times{\mathbb{R}}/2\pi{\mathbb{Z}})
(the uniqueness of
\sigma_{\min}
is proven in
[
29
]
), and set

\nu=(p_{x})_{\sharp}|\sigma_{\min}|,

(10)

where
p_{x}:\Omega\times{\mathbb{R}}/2\pi{\mathbb{Z}}\to\Omega
denotes the standard projection. In particular we have

\displaystyle\nu(\Omega)=|\sigma_{\min}|(\Omega\times{\mathbb{R}}/2\pi{\mathbb{Z}})\leq c_{0}\liminf_{\varepsilon\to 0}F_{\varepsilon}(m_{\varepsilon};\Omega).

(11)

With these notations we may reformulate our main estimate as follows.

Theorem 1.7
.

Let
\Omega\subset{\mathbb{R}}^{2}
be a simply connected open set of class
C^{1,1}
with
\mathcal{H}^{1}(\partial\Omega)=2\pi
and

\sup_{\partial\Omega}|\kappa|\leq K
for some
K>0
, where
\kappa
denotes the curvature of
\partial\Omega
.
If there exists
m\colon\Omega\to{\mathbb{R}}^{2}
solving the eikonal equation (
1
) and the kinetic formulation (

), then

\displaystyle\inf_{x_{*}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{2}}\int_{\partial\Omega}\left|n_{\partial\Omega}(x)-\frac{x-x_{*}}{|x-x_{*}|}\right|^{2}d\mathcal{H}^{1}(x)\leq c\,\nu(\Omega),

(12)

for some constant
c>0
depending only on
K
.

Theorem
1.7
implies Theorem
1.1
thanks to (
11
).
Similarly, Corollaries
1.4
and
1.5
will be consequences of the estimates

\displaystyle\dist(\partial\Omega,\partial B_{1}(x_{*}))

\displaystyle\leq c\,\nu(\Omega)^{\frac{1}{2}},

(13)

\displaystyle\int_{\Omega}\left|m(x)-\alpha\,i\frac{x-x_{*}}{|x-x_{*}|}\right|^{4}\,dx

\displaystyle\leq c\,\,\nu(\Omega)^{\frac{2}{3}},

(14)

for some
x_{*}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{2}
and
\alpha\in\{\pm 1\}
. Next we briefly describe our strategy to prove Theorem
1.7
.

1.3
Strategy of proof

1.3.1
A basic geometric argument

At the heart of our estimates is the following basic geometric argument. Assume
m
is a zero-energy state, that is, a solution of (
1
) and (
5
), and assume moreover that
m=-\tau
on
\partial\Omega
. Suppose there are three boundary points
x_{k}\in\partial\Omega
,
k=1,2,3
, and three directions
e^{i\alpha_{k}}
with the following properties:

1.

the three lines
x_{k}+e^{i\alpha_{k}}{\mathbb{R}}
intersect at a point
z_{0}\in\Omega
,

2.

the direction
e^{i\alpha_{k}}
points in the half-cirle determined by the direction
m=-\tau
at
x_{k}
, i.e.
e^{i\alpha_{k}}\cdot\tau(x_{k})<0
,

3.

the three directions
e^{i\alpha_{k}}
are not contained in the same half-circle.

Such configuration is made impossible by the kinetic equation (
5
), because
\mathbf{1}_{m\cdot e^{i\alpha_{k}}>0}
must be constant along the line
x_{k}+{\mathbb{R}}e^{i\alpha_{k}}
. By the second property, its constant value must be one for
k=1,2,3
, which implies that
m(z_{0})
has positive scalar product with the three directions
e^{i\alpha_{k}}
, which is impossible by the third property.
(To make this rigorous actually requires a bit of care and ‘almost everywhere’ statements, as in
[
19
]
.)
So there are no triplets of points satisfying that condition, and this can be seen to imply that
\partial\Omega
must be a circle, as it forces the normal lines at any three boundary points to be concurrent.

1.3.2
A quantitative version

Our strategy is to make that basic geometric argument quantitative.
Let
a(x_{1},x_{2},x_{3})\geq 0
quantify the above properties:
a>0
if there are three lines from
x_{k}
with directions
e^{i\alpha_{k}}
intersecting well inside
\Omega
, with
e^{i\alpha_{k}}\cdot\tau(x_{k})\leq-a
and the three directions are not contained in the
a
-neighborhood of any half-circle.
Note that this is a purely geometric quantity, defined without any reference to a map
m
.
Let
m
satisfy the eikonal equation (
1
) and kinetic equation (
9
) with a non-zero dissipation measure
\nu(\Omega)=|\sigma_{\min}|(\Omega\times{\mathbb{R}}/2\pi{\mathbb{Z}})
.
Compared to the above basic geometric argument, the assumptions on
m
are relaxed in two ways:
\nu(\Omega)>0
, and the trace
m_{\lfloor\partial\Omega}
can take values into
\{\pm\tau\}
.
Then we show that

\displaystyle\int_{\partial\Omega^{3}}a(x_{1},x_{2},x_{3})^{2}\,d(\mathcal{H}^{1})^{\otimes 3}\leq c\,\nu(\Omega)+c\,\mathcal{H}^{1}(\{m_{\lfloor\partial\Omega}=\tau\}),

(15)

provided
\Omega
is a priori close enough to a disk.
This a priori condition will be satisfied if
\nu(\Omega)
is small enough thanks to a compactness argument and the characterization of zero-energy states
[
19
]
.
To deal with the trace issue, (
15
) needs to be complemented with the estimate

\displaystyle\mathcal{H}^{1}(\{m_{\lfloor\partial\Omega}=\tau\})\leq c\,\nu(\Omega),

(16)

provided the left-hand side is a priori small enough.
Again, this a priori condition can be obtained by means of a compactness argument and the characterization of zero-energy states.
(The compactness argument tells us that, for small
\nu(\Omega)
, one of the complementary subsets
\{m_{\lfloor\partial\Omega}=\tau\}
or
\{m_{\lfloor\partial\Omega}=-\tau\}
is small, here we consider without loss of generality only the first case.)
Finally Theorem
1.1
is obtained by
estimating the deviation of
n_{\partial\Omega}
from the disk’s normal
with the geometric quantity
a
, which relies on purely geometric considerations (that is, independent of the map
m
).

1.3.3
Lagrangian representation

The quantitative estimate (
15
) is our main new ingredient.
It relies on the Lagrangian representation introduced by the second author in
[
29
,
31
]
,
which allows to decompose the dissipation
\nu(\Omega)
along Lagrangian trajectories.
Roughly speaking, the dissipation created by one trajectory is the amount by which it deviates from being a straight line. In particular, absence of dissipation (
\nu=0
) is equivalent to Lagrangian trajectories being straight lines.
With this interpretation in mind, the intuition behind the proof of (
15
) can be explained as follows.
Assume for simplicity that
m_{\lfloor\partial\Omega}=-\tau
.
The basic geometric argument outlined above implies that Lagrangian trajectories meeting three boundary points
x_{k}
with directions close to
e^{i\alpha_{k}}
cannot be straight lines if
a>0
:
they must therefore create dissipation.
More precisely, for intervals of directions of order
a
around each
e^{i\alpha_{k}}
, at least one of the corresponding three trajectories should deviate of order
a
from being a straight line, and summing these contributions provides a dissipation of order
a^{2}
, as expressed by (
15
).
Many technical details are however needed to make this intuition rigorous.
In particular, trajectories cannot be considered individually, but in ‘packets’ inside which only a certain amount of trajectories follow that intuition.
Similar arguments are used to prove the trace estimate (
16
).

2
Geometric estimates

Here and in the rest of the article, we fix
B_{R}(x_{0})
a maximal disk contained in
\Omega
.
As explained in the introduction, the proofs of our main results rely on a geometric quantity

defined for triples of boundary points.

Definition 2.1
.

Given
\hat{x}=(x_{1},x_{2},x_{3})\in\partial\Omega^{3}
, we define
a(\hat{x})\geq 0
as the maximal value
a\geq 0

for which there are

\alpha_{1},\alpha_{2},\alpha_{3}\in{\mathbb{R}}/2\pi{\mathbb{Z}}
such that

1.

the lines
x_{k}+e^{i\alpha_{k}}{\mathbb{R}}
are concurrent in
B_{R/2}(x_{0})
, namely there are
t_{1},t_{2},t_{3}\in{\mathbb{R}}

x_{1}+t_{1}e^{i\alpha_{1}}=x_{2}+t_{2}e^{i\alpha_{2}}=x_{3}+t_{3}e^{i\alpha_{3}}=z_{0}\in B_{R/2}(x_{0});

2.

\min(0,\tau(x_{k})\cdot e^{i\alpha_{k}})\leq-a
for
k=1,2,3
;

3.

a\leq\max\{l(\alpha_{1},\alpha_{2},\alpha_{3})-\pi,0\}
, where

denotes the length of the shortest interval in
{\mathbb{R}}/2\pi{\mathbb{Z}}

\alpha_{1},\alpha_{2},\alpha_{3}
.

Note that each direction

by the inner radius of the

Proposition 2.2

2.1
A few preliminary geometric facts

First we show that boundary points close to the maximal inscribed circle must have a unit normal close to radial (with respect to the inscribed circle’s center).

Lemma 2.3
.

Let
\Omega
be a
C^{1,1}
simply connected domain with
\mathcal{H}^{1}(\partial\Omega)=2\pi
,
\sup_{\partial\Omega}|\kappa|\leq K
and denote by
B_{R}(x_{0})
a maximal disk contained in
\Omega
. Then
1/K\leq R\leq 1
and for every
x\in\partial\Omega
we have

\left|\tau(x)\cdot\frac{x-x_{0}}{|x-x_{0}|}\right|\leq 2\sqrt{K\dist(x,\partial B_{R}(x_{0}))}.

Proof of Lemma
2.3
.

The isoperimetric inequality ensures
R\leq 1
.
For a proof of the property
R\geq 1/K
we refer to
[
33
,
15
]
.
Let us consider an arc-length parametrization
g:{\mathbb{R}}/2\pi{\mathbb{Z}}\to{\mathbb{R}}^{2}
of
\partial\Omega
and let
\psi\colon{\mathbb{R}}/2\pi{\mathbb{Z}}\to{\mathbb{R}}
be defined by

\psi(s)=|g(s)-x_{0}|-R=\dist(g(s),\partial B_{R}(x_{0})).

In particular we have

\begin{split}\psi^{\prime}(s)&=\frac{\dot{g}(s)\cdot(g(s)-x_{0})}{|g(s)-x_{0}|}=\tau(g(s))\cdot\frac{g(s)-x_{0}}{|g(s)-x_{0}|},\\
\psi^{\prime\prime}(s)&=\frac{\ddot{g}(s)\cdot(g(s)-x_{0})}{|g(s)-x_{0}|}-\frac{|\dot{g}(s)\cdot(g(s)-x_{0})|^{2}}{|g(s)-x_{0}|^{3}}.\end{split}

(18)

and therefore

\|\psi^{\prime\prime}\|_{L^{\infty}}\leq K+\frac{1}{R}\leq 2K.

Now consider, for any
a>2
, the function

\displaystyle\varphi(s)=a\,\|\psi^{\prime\prime}\|_{L^{\infty}}\psi(s)-\psi^{\prime}(s)^{2},

which is
C^{1}
with derivative

\displaystyle\varphi^{\prime}(s)

\displaystyle=a\,\|\psi^{\prime\prime}\|_{L^{\infty}}\psi^{\prime}(s)-2\psi^{\prime}(s)\psi^{\prime\prime}(s)

\displaystyle=\left(a\,\|\psi^{\prime\prime}\|_{L^{\infty}}-2\psi^{\prime\prime}(s)\right)\psi^{\prime}(s).

The first factor is positive since
a>2
. Hence at a minimal point
s_{0}
of
\varphi
one must have
\psi^{\prime}(s_{0})=0
and so
\varphi(s_{0})=a\,\|\psi^{\prime\prime}\|_{L^{\infty}}\psi(s_{0})\geq 0
. Therefore
\varphi
is a nonnegative function. As this is valid for any
a>2
we deduce that

\displaystyle\psi^{\prime}(s)^{2}\leq 2\|\psi^{\prime\prime}\|_{L^{\infty}}\psi(s)\leq 4K\,\psi(s).

Taking the square root and recalling the expression of
\psi
and
\psi^{\prime}
concludes the proof.
∎

The next lemma ensures that
a(\hat{x})
is meaningful whenever
\hat{x}\in E(\eta)^{3}
for sufficiently small
\eta>0
.

Lemma 2.4
.

Let
\Omega
be a
C^{1,1}
simply connected domain with
\mathcal{H}^{1}(\partial\Omega)=2\pi
and
\sup_{\partial\Omega}|\kappa|\leq K
.
There exists
\eta_{0}>0
depending only on
K
such that, for all
x\in E(\eta_{0})
and any
z\in B_{2R/3}(x_{0})
, the segment
[z,x]
is included in
\Omega\cup\{x\}
.

Proof of Lemma
2.4
.

Let
x\in E(\eta_{0})
, and write
x_{0}=x+re^{i\theta_{0}}
for some
r\in[R,(1+\eta_{0})R]
and
\theta_{0}\in{\mathbb{R}}
. Denote by
L_{\theta}=x+[0,\infty)e^{i\theta}
the half line from
x
in direction
\theta
. This half-line has a nontrivial intersection with
B_{2R/3}(x_{0})
if and only if
\theta\in(\theta_{0}-\alpha,\theta_{0}+\alpha)+2\pi{\mathbb{Z}}
,
where
\alpha=\arcsin(2R/(3r))=\arcsin(2/3)+\mathcal{O}(\eta_{0})\leq\pi/4
if
\eta_{0}
is small enough.
By definition (
17
) of
E(\eta)
and of
E_{*}
, for all
z\in B_{2R/3}(x_{0})\cap L_{\theta_{0}}
the segment
[z,x]
is included in
\Omega\cup\{x\}
.
If the conclusion of Lemma
2.4
is not true, by continuity we may therefore find
\theta_{1}\in(\theta_{0}-\alpha,\theta_{0}+\alpha)
,
\theta_{1}\neq\theta_{0}
, and
z_{1}\in B_{2R/3}(x_{0})\cap L_{\theta_{1}}
such that
[z_{1},x]
is not included in
\Omega\cup\{x\}
, while that property holds for
z\in B_{2R/3}(x_{0})\cap L_{\theta}
if
\theta\in(\theta_{0},\theta_{1})
.
This implies the existence of
y\in[z_{1},x]\cap\partial\Omega\setminus\{x\}
, with tangent vector
\tau(y)=e^{i\theta_{1}}
and
|x-y|\lesssim R\eta_{0}
.
In particular we have
(y-x_{0})/|y-x_{0}|=e^{i\theta}
with
|\theta-\theta_{0}|\lesssim R\eta_{0}
, and by Lemma
2.3
applied to the boundary point
y
we infer

\displaystyle|e^{i\theta_{1}}\cdot e^{i\theta_{0}}|^{2}\lesssim KR\eta_{0}\lesssim c_{0}K\eta_{0}.

As
|e^{i\theta_{1}}\cdot e^{i\theta_{0}}|^{2}\geq\cos^{2}\alpha\geq 1/2
this implies
\eta_{0}\gtrsim 1/K
, hence choosing
\eta_{0}=1/(CK)
for a large enough absolute constant

ensures the validity of Lemma
2.4
.
∎

We also remark that, for a connected component of
\partial\Omega\cap\overline{B}_{(1+\eta)R}(x_{0})
to be contained in

, it is sufficient that one of its elements belongs to
E(\eta)
.

Lemma 2.5
.

Let
\eta\in[0,1/(4K)]
and assume that
\bar{x}=g(\bar{s})\in E(\eta)
and
s_{1},s_{2}\in{\mathbb{R}}
are such that

\bar{s}\in[s_{1},s_{2}]\qquad\mbox{and}\qquad|g(s)-x_{0}|\leq(1+\eta)R\quad\forall s\in[s_{1},s_{2}].

Then
g((s_{1},s_{2}))\subset E(\eta)
.

Proof of Lemma
2.5
.

Since we assume that
|g(s)-x_{0}|\leq(1+\eta)R
for all
s\in[s_{1},s_{2}]
, it only remains to show that
g((s_{1},s_{2}))\subset E_{*}
, that is, for all
s\in(s_{1},s_{2})
the line interval
\{(1-t)x_{0}+tg(s)\colon 0<t<1\}
is contained in
\Omega
. Consider the largest interval
I\subset(s_{1},s_{2})
containing
\bar{s}
and such that
g(s)\in E_{*}
for all
s\in I
. Note that
I
is open and non-empty. Assume by contradiction that
(s_{1},s_{2})\setminus I\neq\emptyset
, and denote by
\tilde{s}\in(s_{1},s_{2})
an extremity of
I
. Then by maximality of
I
the line interval
\{(1-t)x_{0}+tg(\tilde{s})\colon 0<t<1\}
intersects
\partial\Omega
: there exists
t\in(0,1)
such that
\tilde{x}=(1-t)x_{0}+tg(\tilde{s})\in\partial\Omega
. By definition of
I
, locally near
\tilde{x}
the
C^{2}
curve
\partial\Omega
stays on one side of the line
x_{0}+{\mathbb{R}}(g(\tilde{s})-x_{0})
, hence it must be tangent to that line. Therefore we have
\tau(x)=\pm(\tilde{x}-x_{0})/|\tilde{x}-x_{0}|
. By Lemma
2.3
, and since
R\leq|\tilde{x}-x_{0}|<|g(\tilde{s})-x_{0}|\leq(1+\eta)R
this implies
2\sqrt{K\eta R}>1
, in contradiction with the assumption that
\eta\in[0,1/(4K)]
and the fact that

(by isoperimetric inequality).
∎

Finally we remark that the function
a
is Lipschitz.

Lemma 2.6
.

The function
a
is Lipschitz on
\partial\Omega^{3}
(with respect to the geodesic distance), with Lipschitz constant
L\lesssim K
.

Proof.

Let
\hat{x}=(x_{1},x_{2},x_{3})\in\partial\Omega^{3}
, and
\hat{\alpha}=(\alpha_{1},\alpha_{2},\alpha_{3})\in({\mathbb{R}}/2\pi{\mathbb{Z}})^{3}
as in the definition of
a(\hat{x})
. Denote by
z_{0}\in B_{R/2}(x_{0})
the intersection point of the three lines
x_{k}+e^{i\alpha_{k}}{\mathbb{R}}
. Let
\hat{x}^{\prime}=(x^{\prime}_{1},x^{\prime}_{2},x^{\prime}_{3})\in\partial\Omega^{3}
. Since
z_{0}
lies at a distance at least
R/2
of each
x_{k}
, the three concurrent lines connecting
x^{\prime}_{k}
to
z_{0}
are of the form
x^{\prime}_{k}+e^{i\alpha^{\prime}_{k}}{\mathbb{R}}
for some
\hat{\alpha}^{\prime}=(\alpha^{\prime}_{1},\alpha^{\prime}_{2},\alpha^{\prime}_{3})\in({\mathbb{R}}/2\pi{\mathbb{Z}})^{3}
such that

\displaystyle|\alpha_{k}-\alpha^{\prime}_{k}|\lesssim\frac{1}{R}|x_{k}-x_{k}^{\prime}|\lesssim K\dist(\hat{x},\hat{x}^{\prime})\qquad\forall k\in\{1,2,3\}.

Therefore we have

\displaystyle\max(l(\hat{\alpha}^{\prime})-\pi,0)

\displaystyle\geq\max(l(\hat{\alpha})-\pi,0)-CK\dist(\hat{x},\hat{x}^{\prime})

\displaystyle\geq a(\hat{x})-CK\dist(\hat{x},\hat{x}^{\prime}),

for some absolute constant
C>0
. Moreover by definition of
K
we have

\displaystyle|\tau(x_{k})-\tau(x^{\prime}_{k})|\lesssim K\dist(\hat{x},\hat{x}^{\prime})\qquad\forall k\in\{1,2,3\},

and therefore

\displaystyle\tau(x^{\prime}_{k})\cdot e^{i\alpha_{k}^{\prime}}

\displaystyle\leq\tau(x_{k})\cdot e^{i\alpha_{k}}+C|\tau(x_{k})-\tau(x_{k}^{\prime})|+C|\alpha_{k}-\alpha_{k}^{\prime}|

\displaystyle\leq-a(\hat{x})-CK\dist(\hat{x},\hat{x}^{\prime}).

This shows that

\displaystyle a(\hat{x}^{\prime})\geq a(\hat{x})-CK\dist(\hat{x},\hat{x}^{\prime}).

Exchanging the roles of
\hat{x}
and
\hat{x}^{\prime}
we conclude that
|a(\hat{x}^{\prime})-a(\hat{x})|\lesssim K\dist(\hat{x},\hat{x}^{\prime})
.
∎

Proof of Proposition
2.2

We start by remarking that the distance between
\partial\Omega
and a unit circle is controlled by the
L^{1}
-difference of their normals.

Lemma 2.7
.

If
\Omega
is a simply connected
C^{1}
domain such that
\mathcal{H}^{1}(\partial\Omega)=2\pi
, then for any
x_{*}\in\Omega
such that
\Omega
is strictly star-shaped around
x_{*}
, we have

\displaystyle\dist(\partial\Omega,\partial D_{1}(x_{*}))

\displaystyle\leq\int_{\partial\Omega}\left|n_{\partial\Omega}(x)-\frac{x-x_{*}}{|x-x_{*}|}\right|\,d\mathcal{H}^{1}(x)

Proof of Lemma
2.7
.

We choose coordinates in which
x_{*}=0
and denote

\displaystyle n_{*}(x)=\frac{x-x_{*}}{|x-x_{*}|}=\frac{x}{|x|}.

First we claim that

\displaystyle\left|\tau_{\partial\Omega}(x)\cdot n_{*}(x)\right|\leq\left|n_{\partial\Omega}(x)-n_{*}(x)\right|.

(19)

To prove (
19
), note that since
\Omega
is strictly star-shaped around
x_{*}
, i.e.
n_{\partial\Omega}\cdot n_{*}>0
on
\partial\Omega
, we have

\displaystyle n_{\partial\Omega}\cdot n_{*}=\sqrt{1-(\tau_{\partial\Omega}\cdot n_{*})^{2}}

Hence we deduce

\displaystyle|n_{\partial\Omega}-n_{*}|^{2}=2-2\,n_{\partial\Omega}\cdot n_{*}=2-2\sqrt{1-(\tau_{\partial\Omega}\cdot n_{*})^{2}}.

Estimate (
19
) follows from this identity and the convexity inequality

\displaystyle 2-2\sqrt{1-t}\geq t\qquad\forall t\in[0,1].

Let
g\in C^{1}({\mathbb{R}}/2\pi{\mathbb{Z}};{\mathbb{R}}^{2})
denote a counterclockwise arc-length parametrization of
\partial\Omega
, and let
r_{\min}=\min|g|
,
r_{\max}=\max|g|
be the respective radii of the maximal centered disk contained in
\Omega
and of the minimal centered disk containing
\Omega
. As

\displaystyle\frac{d}{ds}|g(s)|=\dot{g}(s)\cdot\frac{g(s)}{|g(s)|}=\tau_{\partial\Omega}(g(s))\cdot n_{*}(g(s)),

we infer, using also (
19
),

\displaystyle r_{\max}-r_{\min}\leq\frac{1}{2}\int_{\partial\Omega}|\tau_{\partial\Omega}\cdot n_{*}|\,d\mathcal{H}^{1}\leq\frac{1}{2}\int_{\partial\Omega}|n_{\partial\Omega}-n_{*}|\,d\mathcal{H}^{1}.

(20)

Note that
r_{\min}\leq 1
thanks to the isoperimetric inequality, so if
r_{\max}\geq 1
then
(
20
) directly implies the conclusion of Lemma
2.7
. In what follows we may therefore assume
r_{\max}<1
. As
\Omega
is strictly star-shaped around
x_{*}=0
, the map

\displaystyle g_{*}(s)=\frac{g(s)}{|g(s)|},\qquad s\in{\mathbb{R}}/2\pi{\mathbb{Z}},

defines a one-to-one parametrization of the unit circle
\partial D_{1}
. In particular we must have

\displaystyle\int_{{\mathbb{R}}/2\pi{\mathbb{Z}}}|\dot{g}_{*}|=2\pi.

On the other hand direct calculation shows

\displaystyle|g|\,\dot{g}_{*}=\dot{g}-(\dot{g}\cdot g_{*})\,g_{*},

hence

\displaystyle\int_{{\mathbb{R}}/2\pi{\mathbb{Z}}}(1-|g|)|\dot{g}_{*}|

\displaystyle=2\pi-\int_{{\mathbb{R}}/2\pi{\mathbb{Z}}}|\dot{g}-(\dot{g}\cdot g_{*})\,g_{*}|

\displaystyle\leq 2\pi-\int_{{\mathbb{R}}/2\pi{\mathbb{Z}}}|\dot{g}|+\int_{{\mathbb{R}}/2\pi{\mathbb{Z}}}|\dot{g}\cdot g_{*}|

\displaystyle=\int_{\partial\Omega}|\tau_{\partial\Omega}\cdot n_{*}|\,d\mathcal{H}^{1}.

As
0<1-r_{\max}\leq 1-|g|
, this implies

\displaystyle 2\pi(1-r_{\max})=(1-r_{\max})\int_{{\mathbb{R}}/2\pi{\mathbb{Z}}}|\dot{g}_{*}|\leq\int_{\partial\Omega}|\tau_{\partial\Omega}\cdot n_{*}|\,d\mathcal{H}^{1}.

Together with (
20
) this gives

\displaystyle 1-r_{\min}\leq\left(\frac{1}{2}+\frac{1}{2\pi}\right)\int_{\partial\Omega}|\tau_{\partial\Omega}\cdot n_{*}|\,d\mathcal{H}^{1}\leq\int_{\partial\Omega}|n_{\partial\Omega}-n_{*}|\,d\mathcal{H}^{1},

proving Lemma
2.7
also in the case
r_{\max}<1
.
∎

we deduce
|z_{j}-z_{1}|^{2}\lesssim\int_{\partial\Omega^{3}}a^{2}\,d(\mathcal{H}^{1})^{\otimes 3}
for all
j=1,\ldots,24
, and therefore (
23
) implies

\displaystyle\int_{\partial\Omega}|n_{\partial\Omega}-n_{1}|^{2}\lesssim\int_{\partial\Omega^{3}}a^{2}\,d(\mathcal{H}^{1})^{\otimes 3}.

Taking
x_{*}=z_{1}
and applying Lemma
2.7
and Cauchy-Schwarz’ inequality, this concludes the proof of Proposition
2.2
.
∎

3
Lower bound on the dissipation

In this section we prove the following.

Proposition 3.1
.

Let
\Omega
and
m
be as in Theorem
1.7
. We have the estimate

\displaystyle\int_{E(\eta_{*})^{3}}a^{2}d(\mathcal{H}^{1})^{\otimes 3}\leq C\,\nu(\Omega)+C\,\mathcal{H}^{1}(\{m_{\partial\Omega}=\tau\}),

where
\eta_{*}=\min(\eta_{0}/2,1/(8K))
, for
\eta_{0}
as in Lemma
2.4
, and
C>0
is a constant depending only on
K
.

3.1
Lagrangian representation

In order to prove Proposition
3.1
, we introduce the notion of Lagrangian representation for entropy solutions of the eikonal equation from
[
29
]
.

Given
T>0
we let

\displaystyle\Gamma=\Big\{(\gamma,t^{-}_{\gamma},t^{+}_{\gamma})\colon

\displaystyle 0\leq t^{-}_{\gamma}\leq t^{+}_{\gamma}\leq T,

\displaystyle\gamma=(\gamma_{x},\gamma_{s})\in{\mathrm{BV}}((t^{-}_{\gamma},t^{+}_{\gamma});\Omega\times{\mathbb{R}}/2\pi{\mathbb{Z}}),\gamma_{x}\mbox{ is Lipschitz}\Big\}.

We will always consider the right-continuous representative of the component
\gamma_{s}
and we will write
\gamma(t^{-}_{\gamma})
instead of
\lim_{t\to t^{-}_{\gamma}}\gamma(t)
and
\gamma(t^{+}_{\gamma})
instead of
\lim_{t\to t^{+}_{\gamma}}\gamma(t)
.
For every
t\in(0,T)
we consider the section

\Gamma(t):=\left\{\left(\gamma,t^{-}_{\gamma},t^{+}_{\gamma}\right)\in\Gamma:t\in\left(t^{-}_{\gamma},t^{+}_{\gamma}\right)\right\}

and we denote by

\displaystyle\begin{array}[]{crl}e_{t}\colon&\Gamma(t)&\longrightarrow\Omega\times{\mathbb{R}}/2\pi{\mathbb{Z}}\\
&(\gamma,t^{-}_{\gamma},t^{+}_{\gamma})&\longmapsto\gamma(t),\end{array}

the evaluation map at time
t
.

Definition 3.2
.

Let
\Omega
be a
C^{1,1}
open set and
m
solving (
1
) and (
9
).
We say that a finite non-negative Radon measure
\omega\in\mathcal{M}(\Gamma)
is a
Lagrangian representation
of
m
if the following conditions are satisfied:

1.

for every
t\in(0,T)
we have

(e_{t})_{\sharp}\left[\omega\llcorner\Gamma(t)\right]=\mathbf{1}_{E_{m}}\,{\mathcal{L}}^{2}\times{\mathcal{L}}^{1},

(25)

where
E_{m}\subset\Omega\times{\mathbb{R}}/2\pi{\mathbb{Z}}
is the ‘epigraph’

\displaystyle E_{m}=\left\{(x,s)\in\Omega\times{\mathbb{R}}/2\pi{\mathbb{Z}}\colon m(x)\cdot e^{is}>0\right\};

2.

the measure
\omega
is concentrated on curves
(\gamma,t^{-}_{\gamma},t^{+}_{\gamma})\in\Gamma
solving the characteristic equation:

\dot{\gamma}_{x}(t)=e^{i\gamma_{s}(t)}\qquad\text{for a.e. }t\in(t^{-}_{\gamma},t^{+}_{\gamma});

(26)

3.

we have the integral bound

\int_{\Gamma}{\mathrm{TV}}_{(0,T)}\gamma_{s}d\omega(\gamma)<\infty;

4.

for
\omega
-a.e.
(\gamma,t^{-}_{\gamma},t^{+}_{\gamma})\in\Gamma
we have

t^{-}_{\gamma}>0\Rightarrow\gamma_{x}(t^{-}_{\gamma})\in\partial\Omega,\qquad\mbox{and}\qquad t^{+}_{\gamma}<T\Rightarrow\gamma_{x}(t^{+}_{\gamma})\in\partial\Omega.

(27)

A useful property of the Lagrangian formulation is the possibility of decomposing the entropy dissipation measure
\nu
along the characteristics detected by
\omega
.
More precisely, from
[
29
]
we have

Proposition 3.3
.

Let
\Omega
be a
C^{1,1}
open set,
m
solving (
1
) and (
9
), and
T>0
. Then there is a Lagrangian representation
\omega
of
m
such that for every Borel set
A\subset[0,T]
and
B\subset\Omega
it holds

\int_{\Gamma}\mu_{\gamma}(\{t\in A:\gamma_{x}(t)\in B\})\,d\omega(\gamma)={\mathcal{L}}^{1}(A)\nu(B),

where
\mu_{\gamma}=|D_{t}\gamma_{s}|
.

Remark 3.4
.

Note that
\gamma_{s}
takes values into
{\mathbb{R}}/2\pi{\mathbb{Z}}
,
so a few precisions about the meaning of the measure
\mu_{\gamma}=|D_{t}\gamma_{s}|
are in order.
It should actually be understood as the measure
|D_{t}\hat{\gamma}_{s}|
where
\hat{\gamma}_{s}\in BV(I_{\gamma};{\mathbb{R}})
is such that
\gamma_{s}(t)=\hat{\gamma}_{s}(t)+2\pi{\mathbb{Z}}
for all
t\in I_{\gamma}
, and the jumps of
\hat{\gamma}_{s}
are such that
|\hat{\gamma}_{s}(t+)-\hat{\gamma}_{s}(t-)|=\dist_{{\mathbb{R}}/2\pi{\mathbb{Z}}}(\gamma_{s}(t-),\gamma_{s}(t+))
(see e.g.
[
16
, Theorem 1]
for the existence of such a lifting, which is however not necessary to define the measure
\mu_{\gamma}
).

We will also use that, thanks to property (
27
) and the trace properties of
m
, the pushforward of
\omega
under evaluation at initial time
t_{\gamma}^{-}
is related to the
\mathcal{H}^{1}_{\lfloor\partial\Omega}
in the following way.

Lemma 3.5
.

Denote
\Gamma_{ini}=\{t_{\gamma}^{-}>0\}\subset\Gamma
and

\displaystyle P_{ini}\colon\Gamma_{ini}\to(0,T)\times\partial\Omega\times{\mathbb{R}}/2\pi{\mathbb{Z}},\quad\gamma\mapsto(t_{\gamma}^{-},\gamma(t_{\gamma}^{-})),

then the pushforward measure
\mu_{ini}=P_{ini}\sharp\omega_{\lfloor\Gamma_{ini}}
is given by

\displaystyle d\mu_{ini}(t,x,s)

\displaystyle=\mathbf{1}_{m(x)\cdot e^{is}>0}\mathbf{1}_{i\tau(x)\cdot e^{is}>0}\,(i\tau(x)\cdot e^{is})\,dt\,d\mathcal{H}^{1}_{\lfloor\partial\Omega}(x)\,ds.

Proof of Lemma
3.5
.

The argument is similar to
[
8
, Lemma 3.1]
.
Let
F\in C^{1}_{c}((0,T)\times\partial\Omega\times{\mathbb{R}}/2\pi{\mathbb{Z}})
, and denote also by
F
a
C^{1}
extension to
(0,T)\times\overline{\Omega}\times{\mathbb{R}}/2\pi{\mathbb{Z}}
.

For small enough
h>0
we may find a
C/h
-Lipschitz function
G_{h}\colon{\mathbb{R}}^{2}\to[0,1]
, with
C
depending only on
K
, such that

\displaystyle\mathbf{1}_{x\in\Omega,\,\dist(x,\partial\Omega)\leq h}\leq G_{h}(x)\leq\mathbf{1}_{x\in\Omega},

and

\displaystyle\nabla G_{h}\to i\tau\,d\mathcal{H}^{1}_{\lfloor\partial\Omega}\qquad\text{as }h\to 0.

Thanks to the trace property of
m
and the Lagrangian property (
25
) we have

\displaystyle\int_{(0,T)\times\partial\Omega\times{\mathbb{R}}/2\pi{\mathbb{Z}}}F(t,x,s)\,\mathbf{1}_{m(x)\cdot e^{is}>0}\,(i\tau(x)\cdot e^{is})\,dt\,d\mathcal{H}^{1}_{\lfloor\partial\Omega}(x)\,ds

\displaystyle=\lim_{h\to 0^{+}}\int_{(0,T)\times\Omega\times{\mathbb{R}}/2\pi{\mathbb{Z}}}F(t,x,s)\,\mathbf{1}_{m(x)\cdot e^{is}>0}\,(e^{is}\cdot\nabla G_{h}(x))\,dt\,dx\,ds

\displaystyle=\lim_{h\to 0^{+}}\int_{\Gamma}A_{h}(\gamma)\,d\omega(\gamma),

where

\displaystyle A_{h}(\gamma)

\displaystyle=\int_{t_{\gamma}^{-}}^{t_{\gamma}^{+}}F(t,\gamma_{x}(t),\gamma_{s}(t))\,\,(e^{i\gamma_{s}(t)}\cdot\nabla G_{h}(\gamma_{x}(t))\,dt

\displaystyle=\int_{t_{\gamma}^{-}}^{t_{\gamma}^{+}}F(t,\gamma_{x}(t),\gamma_{s}(t))\,\,\frac{d}{dt}\left[G_{h}(\gamma_{x}(t))\right]\,dt.

For the last equality we used the characteristic equation (
26
). Then we integrate by parts: since
G_{h}(\gamma_{x}(t_{\gamma}^{-}))=0
if
t_{\gamma}^{-}>0
and
G_{h}(t_{\gamma}^{+})=0
if
t_{\gamma}^{+}<T
we obtain

\displaystyle A_{h}(\gamma)

\displaystyle=-\int_{t_{\gamma}^{-}}^{t_{\gamma}^{+}}G_{h}(\gamma_{x}(t))D\Phi_{\gamma}(dt),

\displaystyle\text{where }\Phi_{\gamma}(t)

\displaystyle=F(t,\gamma_{x}(t),\gamma_{s}(t)).

In particular we have the convergence

\displaystyle A_{h}(\gamma)\longrightarrow A_{0}(\gamma)=\int_{t_{\gamma}^{-}}^{t_{\gamma}^{+}}\mathbf{1}_{\gamma_{x}(t)\in\Omega}D\Phi_{\gamma}(dt),

as
h\to 0^{+}
. By definition of the Lagrangian representation, for
\omega
-a.e.
\gamma\in\Gamma
we have
\gamma_{x}(t)\in\Omega
for all
t\in(t_{\gamma}^{-},t_{\gamma}^{+})
, so

\displaystyle A_{0}(\gamma)

\displaystyle=-\int_{t_{\gamma}^{-}}^{t_{\gamma}^{+}}D\Phi_{\gamma}(dt)=\Phi_{\gamma}(t_{\gamma}^{-})-\Phi_{\gamma}(t_{\gamma}^{+})

\displaystyle=F(t_{\gamma}^{-},\gamma_{x}(t_{\gamma}^{-}),\gamma_{s}(t_{\gamma}^{-}))-F(t_{\gamma}^{+},\gamma_{x}(t_{\gamma}^{+}),\gamma_{s}(t_{\gamma}^{+})).

Thanks to the domination
|A_{h}(\gamma)|\leq\|\nabla F\|_{\infty}(1+TV(\gamma_{s}))
,
we deduce

\displaystyle\int_{(0,T)\times\partial\Omega\times{\mathbb{R}}/2\pi{\mathbb{Z}}}F(t,x,s)\,\mathbf{1}_{m(x)\cdot e^{is}>0}\,(i\tau(x)\cdot e^{is})\,dt\,d\mathcal{H}^{1}_{\lfloor\partial\Omega}(x)\,ds

\displaystyle=\int_{\Gamma}\left(F(t_{\gamma}^{-},\gamma_{x}(t_{\gamma}^{-}),\gamma_{s}(t_{\gamma}^{-}))-F(t_{\gamma}^{+},\gamma_{x}(t_{\gamma}^{+}),\gamma_{s}(t_{\gamma}^{+}))\right)\,d\omega(\gamma).

We apply this to

\displaystyle F(t,x,s)=f(t,x,s)\phi_{\varepsilon}(x,s),\quad\mathbf{1}_{i\tau(x)\cdot e^{is}>\varepsilon}\leq\phi_{\varepsilon}(x,s)\leq\mathbf{1}_{i\tau(x)\cdot e^{is}>0},

where
f\in C_{c}^{1}((0,T)\times\partial\Omega\times{\mathbb{R}}/2\pi{\mathbb{Z}})
and
\phi_{\varepsilon}\in C^{1}(\partial\Omega\times{\mathbb{R}}/2\pi{\mathbb{Z}})
.
Since for
\omega
-a.e.
\gamma\in\Gamma
it holds
\gamma_{x}(t)\in\Omega
for all
t\in(t_{\gamma}^{-},t_{\gamma}^{+})
,
by the characteristic speed constraint (
26
), we have
i\tau(\gamma_{x}(t_{\gamma}^{+}))\cdot e^{i\gamma_{s}(t_{\gamma}^{+})}\leq 0
if
t_{\gamma}^{+}<T
, so
\phi_{\varepsilon}(\gamma_{x}(t_{\gamma}^{+}),\gamma_{s}(t_{\gamma}^{+}))=0
, and we deduce

\displaystyle\int_{(0,T)\times\partial\Omega\times{\mathbb{R}}/2\pi{\mathbb{Z}}}f(t,x,s)\phi_{\varepsilon}(x,s)\,\mathbf{1}_{m(x)\cdot e^{is}>0}\,(i\tau(x)\cdot e^{is})\,dt\,d\mathcal{H}^{1}_{\lfloor\partial\Omega}(x)\,ds

\displaystyle=\int_{\Gamma}f(t_{\gamma}^{-},\gamma_{x}(t_{\gamma}^{-}),\gamma_{s}(t_{\gamma}^{-}))\phi_{\varepsilon}(\gamma_{x}(t_{\gamma}^{-}),\gamma_{s}(t_{\gamma}^{-}))\,d\omega(\gamma).

By dominated convergence as
\varepsilon\to 0
this implies

\displaystyle\int_{(0,T)\times\partial\Omega\times{\mathbb{R}}/2\pi{\mathbb{Z}}}f(t,x,s)\,\mathbf{1}_{i\tau(x)\cdot e^{is}>0}\,\mathbf{1}_{m(x)\cdot e^{is}>0}\,(i\tau(x)\cdot e^{is})\,dt\,d\mathcal{H}^{1}_{\lfloor\partial\Omega}(x)\,ds

\displaystyle=\int_{\Gamma}f(t_{\gamma}^{-},\gamma_{x}(t_{\gamma}^{-}),\gamma_{s}(t_{\gamma}^{-}))\,\mathbf{1}_{i\tau(\gamma_{x}(t_{\gamma}^{-}))\cdot e^{i\gamma_{s}(t_{\gamma}^{-}))}>0}\,d\omega(\gamma).

As above, for
\omega
-a.e.
\gamma\in\Gamma_{ini}
we have
i\tau(\gamma_{x}(t_{\gamma}^{-}))\cdot e^{i\gamma_{s}(t_{\gamma}^{-}))}\geq 0
.
Indeed the curves that enter tangentially into
\Omega
, namely for which
i\tau(\gamma_{x}(t_{\gamma}^{-}))\cdot e^{i\gamma_{s}(t_{\gamma}^{-}))}=0
,
are
\omega
-negligible (see
[
8
, (3.5)]
for details).
In particular for
\omega
-a.e.
\gamma\in\Gamma_{ini}
we have
i\tau(\gamma_{x}(t_{\gamma}^{-}))\cdot e^{i\gamma_{s}(t_{\gamma}^{-}))}>0
and we infer

\displaystyle\int_{(0,T)\times\partial\Omega\times{\mathbb{R}}/2\pi{\mathbb{Z}}}f(t,x,s)\,\mathbf{1}_{i\tau(x)\cdot e^{is}>0}\,\mathbf{1}_{m(x)\cdot e^{is}>0}\,(i\tau(x)\cdot e^{is})\,dt\,d\mathcal{H}^{1}_{\lfloor\partial\Omega}(x)\,ds

\displaystyle=\int_{\Gamma_{ini}}f(t_{\gamma}^{-},\gamma_{x}(t_{\gamma}^{-}),\gamma_{s}(t_{\gamma}^{-}))\,d\omega(\gamma),

for any
f\in C_{c}^{1}((0,T)\times\partial\Omega\times{\mathbb{R}}/2\pi{\mathbb{Z}})
. By approximation this is valid for any
f\in C_{c}^{0}(0,T)\times\partial\Omega\times{\mathbb{R}}/2\pi{\mathbb{Z}})
, concluding the proof of Lemma
3.5
.
∎

3.2
Proof of Proposition
3.1

Before proving Proposition
3.1
we set some notations and definitions.

•

We apply Proposition
3.3
for some fixed
T\geq 3\pi
, and let
h\in L^{1}(\partial\Omega)
be defined by the relation

\displaystyle\int_{E}h\,d\mathcal{H}^{1}=\int_{\{\gamma:\gamma_{x}(t^{-}_{\gamma})\in E\}}|D_{t}\gamma_{s}|(I_{\gamma})\,d\omega_{h}(\gamma),

(28)

that is,
h(x)
encodes the entropy dissipation generated (via Proposition
3.3
) by the curves of the Lagrangian representation emanating from

.

•

We denote by
W
the ‘wrong trace’ set
W=\{m=\tau\}\subset\partial\Omega
, by
\mathcal{M}\mathbf{1}_{W}
the maximal function

\displaystyle\mathcal{M}\mathbf{1}_{W}(x)=\sup_{r>0}\frac{1}{r}\int_{I_{r}(x)}\mathbf{1}_{W}d\mathcal{H}^{1},

where
I_{r}(x)=g([t-r,t+r])
for
x=g(t)
,
and for any
\varepsilon>0
we define the set

\displaystyle G_{\varepsilon}=\{\mathcal{M}\mathbf{1}_{W}<\varepsilon\}\subset\partial\Omega,

(29)

of boundary points where the proportion of wrong traces is less than

at any scale around that point. Note that the Hardy-Littlewood inequality ensures that
\mathcal{H}^{1}(\partial\Omega\setminus G_{\varepsilon})\lesssim\varepsilon^{-1}\mathcal{H}^{1}(W)
.

The main, and most technical, part of Proposition
3.1
’s proof is encoded in the following lemma.

Lemma 3.6
.

There exist
C,c,\varepsilon>0
, depending only on
K
, such that, for any
\hat{x}\in(E(\eta_{*})\cap G_{\varepsilon})^{3}
with
a(\hat{x})>0
,
the quantity

provides the following lower bound on the entropy dissipation:

\displaystyle\frac{C}{T}\int_{I(\hat{x},a(\hat{x}))}(h(x_{1})+h(x_{2})+h(x_{3}))\,d(\mathcal{H}^{1})^{\otimes 3}\geq a(\hat{x})^{5},

where

\displaystyle I(\hat{x},a(\hat{x}))

\displaystyle=I_{1}\times I_{2}\times I_{3},\qquad I_{k}=I_{c\,a(\hat{x})}(x_{k})=g([\bar{s}_{k}-c\,a(\hat{x}),\bar{s}_{k}+c\,a(\hat{x})]),

and
x_{k}=g(\bar{x}_{k})
for

.

Proof of Lemma
3.6
.

Let
\hat{x}\in(E(\eta_{*})\cap G_{\varepsilon})^{3}
with
a(\hat{x})>0

First we choose the constant
c=c(K)>0
appearing in the definition of
I(\hat{x},a(\hat{x}))
in order to ensure

\displaystyle e^{i\alpha_{k}}\cdot\tau(x)\leq-\frac{a(\hat{x})}{2}\;\forall x\in I_{k},\quad\text{and }I_{k}\subset E(2\eta_{*}),

where
\alpha_{k}
are the angles in the definition of
a(\hat{x})
.
The first condition can be imposed because
\tau
is
K
-Lipschitz and
e^{i\alpha_{k}}\cdot\tau(x_{k})\leq-a(\hat{x})
, and the second thanks to Lemma
2.5
, since
2\eta_{*}\leq 1/(4K)
(as imposed in the statement of Proposition
3.1
).

We denote by
z_{0}
the intersection point as in the definition of
a(\hat{x})
, and consider the three cylinders

\mathcal{C}_{k}:=B_{c_{1}a(\hat{x})}(z_{0})\times[\alpha_{k}-c_{2}a(\hat{x}),\alpha_{k}+c_{2}a(\hat{x})]\qquad\mbox{for }k=1,2,3,

where
c_{1},c_{2}>0
are small constants depending on
K
and chosen to ensure that:

•

for any
(s_{1},s_{2},s_{3})\in\Pi_{k=1}^{3}[\alpha_{k}-c_{2}a(\hat{x}),\alpha_{k}+c_{2}a(\hat{x})]
, the shortest interval in
{\mathbb{R}}/2\pi{\mathbb{Z}}
containing

has length
l>\pi
;

•

for all
(x,s)\in\mathcal{C}_{k}
, there is a boundary point
y\in I_{k}
such that
x=y+te^{is}
for some
t\in{\mathbb{R}}
, the segment
[x,y]
is contained in
\overline{\Omega}
, and
\tau(y)\cdot e^{is}<0
.

The last property is possible since
2\eta_{*}\leq\eta_{0}
with
\eta_{0}
as in Lemma
2.4
.

Claim.
For every
k=1,2,3
we have at least one of the following two properties:

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}^{3}(\mathcal{C}_{k}\cap E_{m})\geq\frac{3}{4}\mathcal{L}^{3}(\mathcal{C}_{k}),\qquad\text{or }\quad

\displaystyle\frac{1}{T}\int_{I_{k}}h\,d\mathcal{H}^{1}\gtrsim a(\hat{x})^{3}.

In other words, either most of the elements of
\mathcal{C}_{k}
belong to the ‘epigraph’
E_{m}
, or there must occur entropy dissipation of order
a(\hat{x})
.

We first prove the statement assuming the Claim.
Since
\mathcal{H}^{1}(I_{k})=2c\,a(\hat{x})
for every

, then

\int_{I(\hat{x},a(\hat{x}))}(h(x_{1})+h(x_{2})+h(x_{3}))\,d(\mathcal{H}^{1})^{\otimes 3}=2c\,a(\hat{x})^{2}\sum_{k=1}^{3}\int_{I_{k}}h\,d\mathcal{H}^{1}.

In view of the Claim, in order to conclude the proof it is sufficient to check that
the first property
\mathcal{L}^{3}(\mathcal{C}_{k}\cap E_{m})\geq(3/4)\mathcal{L}^{3}(\mathcal{C}_{k})
cannot be satisfied for all
k=1,2,3
.
Assume by contradiction that this is the case:
for every
k=1,2,3
, let

A_{k}=\{x\in B_{c_{1}a(\hat{x})}(z_{0}):\mathcal{L}^{1}(\{s:(x,s)\in\mathcal{C}_{k}\cap E_{m}\})>0\}.

In particular we have
\mathcal{L}^{2}(A_{k})\geq\frac{3}{4}\mathcal{L}^{2}(B_{c_{1}a(\hat{x})}(z_{0}))
so that

\mathcal{L}^{2}(A_{1}\cap A_{2}\cap A_{3})\geq\frac{1}{4}\mathcal{L}^{2}(B_{c_{1}a(\hat{x})}(z_{0}))>0.

But the definition of
a(\hat{x})
implies that
A_{1}\cap A_{2}\cap A_{3}=\emptyset
. Indeed for every triple
(s_{1},s_{2},s_{3})\in\prod_{k=1}^{3}[\alpha_{k}-c_{2}a(\hat{x}),\alpha_{k}+c_{2}a(\hat{x})]
,
on the one hand the choice of

ensures that
there is no
\alpha\in{\mathbb{R}}/2\pi{\mathbb{Z}}
such that
e^{i\alpha}\cdot e^{is_{k}}>0
for every
k=1,2,3
, and on the other hand for
\mathcal{L}^{3}
-a.e.
(x,s)\in E_{m}
we have

m(x)\cdot e^{is}>0
. So this gives a contradiction.

It remains to prove the Claim.
For
k=1,2,3
, we consider the set of curves
G_{k}\subset\Gamma
defined as follows.
If the direction
e^{i\alpha_{k}}
enters
\Omega
,
G_{k}
consists of the curves which enter
\Omega

in a way that the ‘free characteristic’ (i.e. straight line) entering with the same initial direction intersects the cylinder
\mathcal{C}_{k}
.
If the direction
e^{i\alpha_{k}}
exits
\Omega
,
G_{k}
consists of the curves which exit
\Omega

in a way that the free characteristic exiting with the same final direction intersects the cylinder
\mathcal{C}_{k}
.
Explicitly:

\displaystyle G_{k}=\Bigg\{\gamma\in\Gamma\colon\exists\bar{t}\in\left[\frac{T}{3},\frac{2}{3}T\right],

\displaystyle\left(\gamma_{x}(t^{-}_{\gamma})+e^{i\gamma_{s}(t^{-}_{\gamma})}(\bar{t}-t^{-}_{\gamma}),\gamma_{s}(t_{\gamma}^{-})\right)\in\mathcal{C}_{k}\Bigg\}

\displaystyle\hskip 100.00015pt\text{if }(x_{k}-z_{0})\cdot\tau(x_{k})>0,

\displaystyle G_{k}=\Bigg\{\gamma\in\Gamma\colon\exists\bar{t}\in\left[\frac{T}{3},\frac{2}{3}T\right],

\displaystyle\left(\gamma_{x}(t^{+}_{\gamma})+e^{i\gamma_{s}(t^{+}_{\gamma})}(\bar{t}-t^{+}_{\gamma}),\gamma_{s}(t_{\gamma}^{+})\right)\in\mathcal{C}_{k}\Bigg\}

\displaystyle\hskip 100.00015pt\text{if }(x_{k}-z_{0})\cdot\tau(x_{k})<0.

Moreover for
\gamma\in G_{k}
we denote by
t_{\mathcal{C}_{k}}(\gamma)
the time spent by
\gamma
in
\mathcal{C}_{k}
, and by
\tilde{t}_{\mathcal{C}_{k}}(\gamma)
the time spent in
\mathcal{C}_{k}
by the corresponding (entering or exiting) free characteristic.
Explicitly:

\displaystyle t_{\mathcal{C}_{k}}(\gamma)

\displaystyle=\mathcal{L}^{1}\left(\left\{t\in\left[\frac{T}{3},\frac{2}{3}T\right]:\gamma(t)\in\mathcal{C}_{k}\right\}\right)

\displaystyle\tilde{t}_{\mathcal{C}_{k}}(\gamma)

\displaystyle=\mathcal{L}^{1}\left(\left\{t\in\left[\frac{T}{3},\frac{2}{3}T\right],\left(\gamma_{x}(t^{-}_{\gamma})+e^{i\gamma_{s}(t^{-}_{\gamma})}(t-t^{-}_{\gamma}),\gamma_{s}(t_{\gamma}^{-})\right)\in\mathcal{C}_{k}\right\}\right)

\displaystyle\hskip 200.0003pt\mbox{if }(x_{k}-z_{0})\cdot\tau(x_{k})>0,

\displaystyle\tilde{t}_{\mathcal{C}_{k}}(\gamma)

\displaystyle=\mathcal{L}^{1}\left(\left\{t\in\left[\frac{T}{3},\frac{2}{3}T\right],\left(\gamma_{x}(t^{+}_{\gamma})+e^{i\gamma_{s}(t^{+}_{\gamma})}(\bar{t}-t^{+}_{\gamma}),\gamma_{s}(t_{\gamma}^{+})\right)\in\mathcal{C}_{k}\right\}\right)

\displaystyle\hskip 200.0003pt\mbox{if }(x_{k}-z_{0})\cdot\tau(x_{k})<0,

Since
T\geq 3\pi
, the choices of

ensure that

\displaystyle(1-\beta)\frac{T}{3}\mathcal{L}^{3}(\mathcal{C}_{k})\leq\int_{G_{k}}\tilde{t}_{\mathcal{C}_{k}}d\omega\leq\frac{T}{3}\mathcal{L}^{3}(\mathcal{C}_{k}),

(30)

where

\displaystyle\beta\lesssim\frac{\mathcal{H}^{1}(\{x\in I_{k}(\hat{x},a(\hat{x}))\colon m(x)=\tau(x)\})}{\mathcal{H}^{1}(I_{k}(\hat{x},a(\hat{x})))}\lesssim\varepsilon.

To prove (
30
), assume without loss of generality that
(x_{k}-z_{0})\cdot\tau(x_{k})>0
. Then, by Fubini theorem, we have

\displaystyle\int_{G_{k}}\tilde{t}_{\mathcal{C}_{k}}d\omega

\displaystyle=\int_{T/3}^{2T/3}\omega\left(A_{t}\right)\,dt,

\displaystyle\text{where}\quad A_{t}

\displaystyle=\left\{\gamma\in\Gamma\colon\left(\gamma_{x}(t^{-}_{\gamma})+e^{i\gamma_{s}(t^{-}_{\gamma})}(t-t^{-}_{\gamma}),\gamma_{s}(t_{\gamma}^{-})\right)\in\mathcal{C}_{k}\right\}.

Since
T/3\geq\pi
and
\Omega
has diameter
<\pi
, for
t\in[T/3,2T/3]
any
\gamma\in A_{t}
satisfies
t_{\gamma}^{-}>0
, and moreover

and

.
Invoking Lemma

\displaystyle\omega(A_{t})=\int_{\alpha_{k}-c_{2}a(\hat{x})}^{\alpha_{k}+c_{2}a(\hat{x})}\int_{I_{k}}\int_{0}^{2T/3}

\displaystyle\mathbf{1}_{y+(t-t_{ini})e^{is}\in B_{c_{1}a(\hat{x})}(z_{0})}

\displaystyle\cdot\mathbf{1}_{m(y)\cdot e^{is}>0}(i\tau(y)\cdot e^{is})\,dt_{ini}\,d\mathcal{H}^{1}(y)\,ds.

For any

,
so we deduce

\displaystyle\omega(A_{t})=\int_{\alpha_{k}-c_{2}a(\hat{x})}^{\alpha_{k}+c_{2}a(\hat{x})}\int_{B_{c_{1}a(\hat{x})}(z_{0})}\mathbf{1}_{m(x_{s}(z))\cdot e^{is}>0}\,dz\,ds,

where

. For

\displaystyle\beta=\sup_{z\in B_{c_{1}a(\hat{x})}(z_{0})}\frac{\mathcal{L}^{1}(\{s\in[\alpha_{k}-c_{1}a(\hat{x}),\alpha_{k}+c_{2}a(\hat{x})]\colon m(x_{s}(z))=\tau(x_{s}(z))\})}{\mathcal{L}^{1}([\alpha_{k}-c_{1}a(\hat{x}),\alpha_{k}+c_{2}a(\hat{x})])}.

.
The second inequality

in (

.

Moreover, since for every

, then

\int_{G_{k}}t_{\mathcal{C}_{k}}d\omega\leq\frac{T}{3}\mathcal{L}^{3}(\mathcal{C}_{k}\cap E_{m}).

(31)

We now estimate
\tilde{t}_{\mathcal{C}_{k}}-t_{\mathcal{C}_{k}}
in terms of the entropy dissipation from the curves in

.
Assume without loss of generality that

.

Denote by

for some

.
Finally denote by

For every

We write

\displaystyle\int_{G_{k}}(\tilde{t}_{\mathcal{C}_{k}}-t_{\mathcal{C}_{k}})d\omega

\displaystyle\leq\int_{G_{k}\setminus\tilde{G}_{k}}\tilde{t}_{\mathcal{C}_{k}}d\omega+\int_{\tilde{G}_{k}\setminus\tilde{G}_{k}^{*}}(\tilde{t}_{\mathcal{C}_{k}}-t_{\mathcal{C}_{k}})d\omega+\int_{\tilde{G}_{k}^{*}}\tilde{t}_{\mathcal{C}_{k}}d\omega,

(34)

Third, by definition of

\displaystyle\int_{\tilde{G}_{k}^{*}}\tilde{t}_{\mathcal{C}_{k}}d\omega

\displaystyle\leq\sup\tilde{t}_{\mathcal{C}_{k}}\,\omega(\tilde{G}_{k}^{*})\leq\frac{2c_{1}a(\hat{x})}{c_{6}a(\hat{x})}\int_{\tilde{G}_{k}^{*}}|\mu_{\gamma}|(0,T)d\omega(\gamma)

\displaystyle\leq\frac{2c_{1}}{c_{6}}\int_{I_{k}(\hat{x},a(\hat{x}))}h\,d\mathcal{H}^{1},

so plugging this and (

\displaystyle\int_{G_{k}}(\tilde{t}_{\mathcal{C}_{k}}-t_{\mathcal{C}_{k}})d\omega

\displaystyle\leq\frac{2T}{3}c_{4}\left(\frac{1}{c_{1}}+\frac{1}{c_{2}}\right)\mathcal{L}^{3}(\mathcal{C}_{k})+\frac{T}{3}\frac{c_{5}}{\sqrt{c_{1}c_{4}}}\mathcal{L}^{3}(\mathcal{C}_{k})

\displaystyle\quad+\frac{2c_{1}}{c_{6}}\int_{I_{k}(\hat{x},a(\hat{x}))}h\,d\mathcal{H}^{1}

We may choose

and

\displaystyle\frac{2T}{3}c_{4}\left(\frac{1}{c_{1}}+\frac{1}{c_{2}}\right)\mathcal{L}^{3}(\mathcal{C}_{k})+\frac{T}{3}\frac{c_{5}}{\sqrt{c_{1}c_{4}}}\mathcal{L}^{3}(\mathcal{C}_{k})\leq\frac{T}{24}\mathcal{L}^{3}(\mathcal{C}_{k})

so that by (

) and (

), we deduce

\displaystyle\frac{T}{3}\mathcal{L}^{3}(\mathcal{C}_{k}\cap E_{m})

\displaystyle\geq\int_{G_{k}}\tilde{t}_{\mathcal{C}_{k}}d\omega-\int_{G_{k}}(\tilde{t}_{\mathcal{C}_{k}}-t_{\mathcal{C}_{k}})d\omega

\displaystyle\geq\frac{T}{3}\left(\frac{7}{8}-c\varepsilon\right)\mathcal{L}^{3}(\mathcal{C}_{k})-\frac{2c_{1}}{c_{6}}\int_{I_{k}(\hat{x},a(\hat{x}))}h\,d\mathcal{H}^{1},

for some absolute constant

. Choosing

we deduce

\mathcal{L}^{3}(\mathcal{C}_{k}\cap E_{m})\geq\frac{3}{4}\mathcal{L}^{3}(\mathcal{C}_{k})+\frac{1}{16}\mathcal{L}^{3}(\mathcal{C}_{k})-\frac{6c_{1}}{c_{6}T}\int_{I_{k}(\hat{x},a(\hat{x}))}h\,d\mathcal{H}^{1}.

This estimate implies the claim: if
\mathcal{L}^{3}(\mathcal{C}_{k}\cap E_{m})\leq\frac{3}{4}\mathcal{L}^{3}(\mathcal{C}_{k})
then

\int_{I_{k}(\hat{x},a(\hat{x}))}h\,d\mathcal{H}^{1}\geq(c_{6}T/(6c_{1}))\mathcal{L}^{3}(\mathcal{C}_{k})/16=(\pi Tc_{1}c_{2}c_{6}/48)\,a(\hat{x})^{3}
.
∎

Now Proposition
3.1
follows from Lemma
3.6
via a covering argument.

Proof of Proposition
3.1
.

Denote by
L
the Lipschitz constant of
a
from Lemma
2.6
.
Let
\varepsilon>0
be fixed as in Lemma
3.6
, and consider the covering

\displaystyle\{I(\hat{x},a(\hat{x})):\hat{x}\in(E(\eta_{0})\cap G_{\varepsilon})^{3}\}

of the set
X^{*}:=\{\hat{x}\in(E(\eta_{0})\cap G_{\varepsilon})^{3}\colon a(x)>0\}
. Since for every
\hat{x}\in X^{*}
, the diameter of
I(\hat{x},a(\hat{x}))
is
\lesssim a(\hat{x})
and the function
a
is Lipschitz by Lemma
2.6
, we have

\displaystyle\int_{I(\hat{x},a(\hat{x}))}a^{2}\,d(\mathcal{H}^{1})^{\otimes 3}\lesssim a(\hat{x})^{2}(\mathcal{H}^{1})^{\otimes 3}(I(\hat{x},a(\hat{x})))\lesssim a(\hat{x})^{5}.

(35)

By Besicovitch covering theorem, there is a subcovering

\displaystyle\{I(\hat{x}_{i},a(\hat{x}_{i})):i\in I\}

of
X^{*}
with finite overlap.
By (
35
), Lemma
3.6
and the finite overlap property we obtain

\displaystyle\int_{X^{*}}a^{2}\,d(\mathcal{H}^{1})^{\otimes 3}

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{i\in I}\int_{I(\hat{x}_{i},a(\hat{x}_{i}))}a^{2}d(\mathcal{H}^{1})^{\otimes 3}\lesssim\sum_{i\in I}a(\hat{x}_{i})^{5}

\displaystyle\lesssim\sum_{i\in I}\int_{I(\hat{x}_{i},r_{+}(\hat{x}_{i}))}\left(h(x_{1})+h(x_{2})+h(x_{3})\right)d(\mathcal{H}^{1})^{\otimes 3}

\displaystyle\lesssim\int_{\partial\Omega^{3}}\left(h(x_{1})+h(x_{2})+h(x_{3})\right)d(\mathcal{H}^{1})^{\otimes 3}\lesssim\int_{\partial\Omega}h\,d\mathcal{H}^{1}.

The definition (
28
) of

and Proposition
3.3
ensure that

, so we deduce

\displaystyle\int_{(E(\eta_{0})\setminus G_{\varepsilon})^{3}}a^{2}\,d(\mathcal{H}^{1})^{\otimes 3}\lesssim\nu(\Omega),

which implies, since

,

\displaystyle\int_{E(\eta_{0})^{3}}a^{2}\,d(\mathcal{H}^{1})^{\otimes 3}\lesssim\nu(\Omega)+\mathcal{H}^{1}(\partial\Omega\setminus G_{\varepsilon}).

Recalling the definition (

) of

, thanks to the the Hardy-Littlewood inequality the last term is
\lesssim\varepsilon^{-1}\mathcal{H}^{1}(\{m=\tau\})

.
∎

4
Compactness argument

In this section we use the characterization of zero-energy states
[
19
]
and a compactness argument to ‘initialize’ our analysis of the previous sections:
if
\nu(\Omega)
is small enough, then
\Omega
must be close to a disk and
m
close to a vortex.

Lemma 4.1
.

For any
K,\varepsilon>0
, there exists
\delta=\delta(\varepsilon,K)>0
with the following property.
If
\Omega
is a
C^{1,1}
simply connected domain with
\mathcal{H}^{1}(\partial\Omega)=2\pi
,
\sup_{\partial\Omega}|\kappa|\leq K
which admits a map
m
solving
(
1
) and (
9
) and its dissipation measure
\nu
defined in (
10
) satisfies
\nu(\Omega)\leq\delta
, then

\displaystyle\dist(\partial\Omega,\partial B_{1}(x_{0}))+\sup_{x\in\partial\Omega}\left|n_{\partial\Omega}(x)-\frac{x-x_{0}}{|x-x_{0}|}\right|\leq\varepsilon,

(36)

for some
x_{0}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{2}
, and there is
\alpha\in\{\pm 1\}
such that

\displaystyle\int_{\Omega}\mathbf{1}_{\dist(\cdot,\partial\Omega)\leq\frac{1}{2K}}|m-\alpha\tau_{\partial\Omega}\circ\pi_{\partial\Omega}|\,dx\leq\varepsilon,

(37)

where
\pi_{\partial\Omega}(x)\in\partial\Omega
is the nearest-point projection of
x
onto
\partial\Omega
, well-defined for
\dist(x,\partial\Omega)\leq 1/(2K)
.

Proof of Lemma
4.1
.

Assume by contradiction that there exists a sequence of
C^{2}
simply connected domains
\Omega_{k}
such that
\mathcal{H}^{1}(\partial\Omega_{k})=2\pi
,
\sup_{\partial\Omega_{k}}|\kappa|\leq K
with maps
m_{k}\colon\Omega_{k}\to\mathbb{S}^{1}
satisfying
\nabla\cdot m_{k}=0
in
\Omega
,
m_{k}\cdot n_{\partial\Omega_{k}}=0
on
\partial\Omega_{k}
and
\nu_{m_{k}}(\Omega_{k})=\delta_{k}\to 0
, such that

\displaystyle\inf_{x_{0}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{2}}\left\{\dist(\partial\Omega_{k},\partial B_{1}(x_{0}))+\sup_{x\in\partial\Omega_{k}}\left|n_{\partial\Omega_{k}}(x)-\frac{x-x_{0}}{|x-x_{0}|}\right|\right\}

\displaystyle\geq\varepsilon,

or
\pi_{\partial\Omega_{k}}(x)
is not well-defined (that is, not unique) for
\dist(x,\partial\Omega_{k})\leq 1/(2K)
, or

\displaystyle\min_{\alpha\in\{\pm 1\}}\int_{\Omega_{k}}\mathbf{1}_{\dist(\cdot,\partial\Omega_{k})\leq\frac{1}{2K}}|m-\alpha\tau_{\partial\Omega_{k}}\circ\pi_{\partial\Omega_{k}}|\,dx

\displaystyle\geq\varepsilon.

Let
g_{k}\in C^{2}({\mathbb{R}}/2\pi{\mathbb{Z}};{\mathbb{R}}^{2})
be a counterclockwise arc-length parametrization of
\partial\Omega_{k}
. Up to a translation we may assume that
\int_{{\mathbb{R}}/2\pi{\mathbb{Z}}}g_{k}(t)\,dt=0
.
Since
|\ddot{g}_{k}|\leq K
there exists a subsequence (which we don’t relabel) such that
g_{k}
convergence in
C^{1}({\mathbb{R}}/2\pi{\mathbb{Z}};{\mathbb{R}}^{2})
to a curve
g\in C^{1,1}({\mathbb{R}}/2\pi{\mathbb{Z}};{\mathbb{R}}^{2})
with
|\dot{g}|=1
. The curve
g
can self-intersect, but not self-cross, so at a multiple point all tangents must be parallel.

Each domain
\Omega_{k}
contains a disk of radius
\geq 1/K

[
15
, Proposition 2.1]
, so
{\mathbb{R}}^{2}\setminus g({\mathbb{R}}/2\pi{\mathbb{Z}})
has an open bounded simply connected component containing a disk of radius
\geq 1/K
, which we denote by
\Omega
.
Since
\partial\Omega\subset g({\mathbb{R}}/2\pi{\mathbb{Z}})
, the boundary
\partial\Omega
is
C^{1}
except at multiple points of the
C^{1}
curve
g
.
We distinguish two types of singular points: a singular point
z\in\partial\Omega
is of type I if there exists
\delta>0
such that all connected components
\omega_{\delta}
of
\Omega\cap B_{\delta}
are such that
\partial\omega_{\delta}\cap\partial\Omega
is
C^{1}
, and of type II otherwise. See Figure
2
.
The rest of the proof is divided in 4 steps.

Figure 2:
The point
z_{1}
is a type I singularity, while
z_{2}
is a type II singularity.

Step 1.
Singular points of type II are isolated in
\partial\Omega
.

Let
z_{0}\in\partial\Omega
a singular point. In particular it is a multiple point:
g^{-1}(\{z_{0}\})
contains strictly more than one element.
If
t_{1}\in g^{-1}(\{z_{0}\})
, then
g(t)\neq z_{0}
for
|t-t_{1}|<2\pi/K
because
|\dot{g}|=1
and
|\ddot{g}|\leq K
. This implies that
g^{-1}(\{z_{0}\})
is finite,
g^{-1}(\{z_{0}\})=\{t_{1},\ldots,t_{N}\}
for some
N\geq 2
. Since
g
cannot self-cross, we have
\dot{g}(t_{j})\in\{\pm\tau\}
for all
j=1,\ldots,N
and one fixed
\tau=\dot{g}(t_{1})\in\mathbb{S}^{1}
.

We consider, for
\delta>0
small enough, the open set
g^{-1}(B_{\delta}(z_{0}))
. From a Taylor expansion around each
t_{j}
, we deduce the existence of
\eta=\eta(K)>0
such that for
j=1,\ldots,N
, the subset
g^{-1}(B_{\delta}(z_{0}))\cap(t_{j}-\eta,t_{j}+\eta)
is an open interval
I_{\delta}^{j}
. For small enough
\delta>0
, the open set
g^{-1}(B_{\delta}(z_{0}))
is exactly the union of these
N
open intervals.
Maybe I would give for granted this proof, but since it is already there, we can also keep it. We prove this by contradiction: otherwise, there would exist a sequence
\delta\to 0
and
t_{\delta}
such that
|g(t_{\delta})-z_{0}|<\delta
but
t_{\delta}\notin I_{j}^{\delta}
for any
j=1,\ldots,N
.
Extracting a subsequence
t_{\delta}\to t_{*}
, we must have
g(t_{*})=z_{0}
, so
t_{*}\in\{t_{1},\ldots,t_{N}\}
,
and therefore
t_{\delta}\in(t_{j}-\eta,t_{j}+\eta)
for some
j\in\{1,\ldots,N\}
and all small enough
\delta
.
By the above property of
\eta
this implies
t_{\delta}\in I_{\delta}^{j}
and gives a contradiction, so
g^{-1}(B_{\delta}(z_{0}))
is indeed the union of the
N
intervals
I_{\delta}^{j}
.

We choose coordinates
(x,y)
in which
z_{0}=0
and
\tau=e_{1}
.
Then for small enough
\delta>0
we have

\displaystyle g({\mathbb{R}}/2\pi{\mathbb{Z}})\cap B_{\delta}=\bigcup_{j=1}^{N}\{y=f_{j}(x)\}\cap B_{\delta},

for some
C^{1}
functions
f_{1}\leq\cdots\leq f_{N}
such that
f_{j}(0)=f_{j}^{\prime}(0)=0
for
j=1,\ldots,N
.
We have
z_{0}=0\in\partial\Omega
, and any
(x_{0},y_{0})\in B_{\delta}\cap\Omega
must satisfy

y_{0}\notin\{f_{1}(x_{0}),\ldots,f_{N}(x_{0})\}
.
Moreover, if
f_{j}(x_{0})<y_{0}<f_{j+1}(x_{0})
for some
j\in\{1,\ldots,N-1\}
,
we deduce that
f_{j}(x)<f_{j+1}(x)
for all
x\in(0,x_{0})

since
\Omega
is connected.
Therefore, possibly taking a smaller value of
\delta
, the connected components of

\Omega\cap B_{\delta}
are among the sets

\displaystyle\{y<f_{1}(x)\}\cap B_{\delta},

\displaystyle\{y>f_{N}(x)\}\cap B_{\delta},

\displaystyle\{f_{j}(x)<y<f_{j+1}(x)\}\cap\{x>0\}\cap B_{\delta},

\displaystyle\{f_{j}(x)<y<f_{j+1}(x)\}\cap\{x<0\}\cap B_{\delta}.

Note that the singular point
z_{0}=0
is of type I if and only if the two last types of connected components do not arise. Moreover, if
z_{0}
is of type II, this description of
\Omega\cap B_{\delta}
shows that
\partial\Omega\cap B_{\delta}
contains no other singular points of type II. This proves Step 1.

Step 2.
There exists
m\colon\Omega\to\mathbb{S}^{1}
such that
\nabla\cdot m=0
in
\Omega
,
\nu(\Omega)=0
, with a strong
L^{1}
trace
m_{\lfloor\partial\Omega}
satisfying
m_{\lfloor\partial\Omega}\cdot n_{\partial\Omega}=0
a.e. on
\partial\Omega
.
More precisely, this makes sense in any
C^{1}
portion of
\partial\Omega
, singular points of type II are negligible by Step 1, and around a singular point of type I,
\Omega
is, in adapted coordinates, locally of the form
\{y<f(x)\}\cup\{y>\tilde{f}(x)\}
for some
C^{1}
functions
f\leq\tilde{f}
with
f(0)=\tilde{f}(0)=f^{\prime}(0)=\tilde{f}^{\prime}(0)=0
, and the trace
m_{\partial\Omega}
may differ from one side to another, but both traces satisfy
m\cdot n_{\partial\Omega}=0
a.e. for any choice of unit normal
n_{\partial\Omega}
.

For any
z\in\Omega
and
B_{r}(z)\subset\Omega
, the sequence
m_{k\lfloor D_{r}(z)}
has bounded entropy production and is therefore compact in
L^{1}(D_{r}(z))

[
11
]
, so
m_{k\lfloor\Omega}
is compact in
L^{1}_{\mathrm{loc}}(\Omega)
. After extracting a subsequence converging in
L^{1}_{loc}
and a.e., its limit
m\colon\Omega\to\mathbb{S}^{1}
satisfies
\nabla\cdot m=0
in
\Omega
and
\nabla\cdot\Phi(m)=0
for any entropy
\Phi
(see Appendix
A
), so
\nu(\Omega)=0
.
This last property implies that
m
has an
L^{1}
trace along any
C^{1}
portion of
\partial\Omega
, and along both sides of any portion of
\partial\Omega
around a singular point of type I (see e.g.
[
19
, § 3.2]
).
It remains to prove that this trace satisfies
m_{\lfloor\partial\Omega}\cdot n_{\partial\Omega}=0
a.e. on
\partial\Omega
.

To that end consider first a
C^{1}
point
z_{0}\in\partial\Omega
, and a disk
B_{2r}(z_{0})
such that
\partial\Omega\cap B_{2r}(z_{0})=g(I)
for some open interval
I
. Possibly choosing a smaller
r
and adapted coordinates
(x,y)
in which
z_{0}=0
and
g(I)
is close to horizontal, we write
\Omega\cap B_{2r}
as a subgraph

\displaystyle\Omega\cap B_{2r}=\{y<f(x)\}\cap B_{2r},

where
f(0)=f^{\prime}(0)=0
and
f
is
C^{1}
.
Since
g_{k}\to g
in
C^{1}
, we can write
g_{k}(I)
as a graph
\{y=f_{k}(x)\}
for some functions
f_{k}
converging to
f
in
C^{1}
, and define

\displaystyle\widetilde{\Omega}_{k}=\{y<f_{k}(x)\}\cap B_{r}.

Then we have
\widetilde{\Omega}_{k}\subset\Omega_{k}
and
B_{r}\cap\partial\widetilde{\Omega}_{k}\subset\partial\Omega_{k}
, so
m_{k}\cdot n_{\partial\widetilde{\Omega}_{k}}=0
on

, implying that
\nabla\cdot(m_{k}\mathbf{1}_{\widetilde{\Omega}_{k}})=0
in
B_{r}
.
By dominated convergence we have

m_{k}\mathbf{1}_{\widetilde{\Omega}_{k}}\to m\mathbf{1}_{\Omega\cap B_{r}}
in
L^{1}(B_{r})
. We deduce that
\nabla\cdot(m\mathbf{1}_{\Omega})=0
in
B_{r}
, which implies that
m_{\lfloor\partial\Omega}\cdot n_{\partial\Omega}=0
a.e. on
\partial\Omega\cap B_{r}
.
This is valid around any
C^{1}
point of
\partial\Omega
.
Around a singular point
z_{0}
of type I, the same argument can be applied in both connected components of
\Omega\cap B_{2r}(z_{0})
.
And singular points of type II are isolated by Step 1, so
m_{\lfloor\partial\Omega}\cdot n_{\partial\Omega}=0
a.e. on
\partial\Omega
.

Step 3.
There are no singular points of type II.

If
z_{0}\in\partial\Omega
is a singular point of type II, then by the analysis in Step 1 we may choose coordinates
(x,y)
in
which
z_{0}=0
and there exist
\delta>0
and a connected component

of

\displaystyle\omega_{\delta}=\{f_{1}(x)<y<f_{2}(x)\}\cap\{x>0\}\cap B_{\delta},

.
For all small

Therefore, considering

.
The proof of

implies that, for every point

such that

is defined and

is contained in

must be equal to

.
By a continuation argument, we deduce that the graphs of

and

are, until they meet, arcs of circles centered at

, but this contradicts the fact that they have the same tangent at their intersection point. This contradiction proves that

contains no singular points of type II.

Step 4.
Conclusion.

If there are no singular points of type I,

\Omega
is
C^{1}
with bounded curvature and

ensures the existence of a tangent inscribed disk centered at a focal point: explicitly we have a disk

and a tangency point

, such that for any

and

doesn’t have a local minimum at

.
In fact it can be checked that the proof of
[

, Proposition 2.1]
works even if

has singular points of type I. (One may also approximate

domains, apply
[

, Proposition 2.1]
and pass to the limit.)
So we do have
a disk
B_{r}(z_{*})\subset\Omega
and a tangency point
z_{b}=g(t_{0})\in\partial B_{r}(z_{*})\cap\partial\Omega
, such that for any
\varepsilon>0
and
z_{\varepsilon}=z_{*}-\varepsilon(z_{b}-z_{*})
, the function

doesn’t have a local minimum at

.
The derivative of that function cannot be nondecreasing near

, and this implies the existence of

arbitrarily close to

such that the normal line to
\partial\Omega

.
Denote by

Applying again the argument in the proof of

, we have the following geometric property: for every point

is contained in

.
Let

be the connected component of
t_{0}
in the open set of all

The estimate (

Proposition 5.1

Let

simply connected domain with

,

. For any map

) and (

We start by showing a preliminary lemma, which is a more precise version of

, Lemma 3.1]

(see also

, Lemma 22]

As in

, Lemma 2.7]
we denote by

the full measure set of curves

such that for a.e.

is a Lebesgue point of

.

Lemma 5.2
.

Let

,
\bar{\gamma}\in\Gamma_{g}
and
\bar{t}\in(t^{-}_{\bar{\gamma}},t^{+}_{\bar{\gamma}})
be such that

and denote by
(t^{-}_{r},t^{+}_{r})
the connected component of
\gamma_{x}^{-1}(B_{r}(\bar{\gamma}_{x}(\bar{t})))
in

such that for every

;

2.

\mathcal{L}^{2}(\{x\in B_{r}(\bar{\gamma}_{x}(\bar{t})):e^{i\bar{\gamma}_{s}(\bar{t})}\cdot m(x)\geq-2\beta\})\geq c\beta r^{2}

Proof of Lemma
5.2
.

We let
\bar{x}=\bar{\gamma}_{x}(\bar{t})
,
\bar{s}=\bar{\gamma}_{s}(\bar{t})
, and
\mathcal{C}_{\bar{\gamma}}
be the image curve
\mathcal{C}_{\bar{\gamma}}=\bar{\gamma}_{x}((t_{r}^{-},t_{r}^{+}))\subset\Omega
. For

we denote by

the unit tangent vector determined by the parametrization
\bar{\gamma}
. In particular,
\tau_{\bar{\gamma}}(\bar{x})=e^{i\bar{s}}
.

For
\mathcal{H}^{1}
-a.e.
x\in\mathcal{C}_{\bar{\gamma}}\cap B_{r/2}(\bar{x})
we have
m(x)\cdot\tau_{\bar{\gamma}}(x)\geq 0
, therefore recalling that
\beta\in\big(\mathrm{Osc}_{(t^{-}_{r},t^{+}_{r})}\gamma_{s},\pi/4\big)
one of the following holds:

\displaystyle m(x)\cdot e^{is}e^{i\bar{s}}>0\quad\forall s\in\left(\frac{5\beta}{4},\frac{7\beta}{4}\right),

or

\displaystyle m(x)\cdot e^{is}e^{i\bar{s}}>0\quad\forall s\in\left(-\frac{7\beta}{4},-\frac{5\beta}{4}\right).

One of these two conditions must be satisfied for at least half the points in
\mathcal{C}_{\bar{\gamma}}\cap B_{r/2}(\bar{x})
, and we assume without loss of generality that

\displaystyle\mathcal{H}^{1}\left(\left\{x\in\mathcal{C}_{\bar{\gamma}}\cap B_{r/2}(\bar{x}):m(x)\cdot e^{is}e^{i\bar{s}}>0\ \forall s\in\left(\frac{5\beta}{4},\frac{7\beta}{4}\right)\right\}\right)\geq\frac{r}{2}.

(39)

We define

\displaystyle I(\gamma)

\displaystyle=\{t\in(0,T):\gamma_{x}(t)\in B_{r}(\bar{x})\},

\displaystyle I^{\prime}(\gamma)

\displaystyle=\{t\in I(\gamma):\gamma_{s}(t)\in(\bar{s}+\beta,\bar{s}+2\beta)\},

and

.
We moreover consider

\displaystyle N(\gamma)=\#\left\{t\in(0,T):\gamma_{x}(t)\in\mathcal{C}_{\bar{\gamma}}\cap B_{r/2}(\bar{x}),\gamma_{s}(t)\in\left(\bar{s}+\frac{5\beta}{4},\bar{s}+\frac{7\beta}{4}\right)\right\}.

(40)

This cardinal is finite for
\omega
-a.e.
\gamma\in\Gamma
thanks to
[
8
, Proposition 3.3]
,
and we denote by

the elements of the above set.
We show that for every
\gamma\in\Gamma
we have

\frac{\mu_{\gamma}(I(\gamma))}{\beta}+\frac{T(\gamma)}{r}\geq\frac{1}{4}N(\gamma),

(41)

where

can be interpreted as the entropy dissipation along
\gamma
thanks to Proposition
3.3
.
It follows from the characteristic equation (
26
) that for every
i=1,\ldots N(\gamma)
there is a neighbourhood

of

of size at least

such that

The neighborhoods

are not necessarily disjoint, but if

, then (

and
\mu_{\gamma}([t_{i}(\gamma),t_{i+1}(\gamma)])\geq\beta/4
.
This establishes (
41

Next we integrate (
41
) with respect to
\omega
. From Proposition
3.3
we deduce

\int_{\Gamma}\mu_{\gamma}(I(\gamma))\,d\omega\leq\nu(B_{r}(\bar{x}))T,

and from the Lagrangian property (

\displaystyle\int_{\Gamma}T(\gamma)\,d\omega

\displaystyle\leq T\mathcal{L}^{3}(\{(x,s)\in B_{r}(\bar{x})\times(\bar{s}+\beta,\bar{s}+2\beta)\colon m(x)\cdot e^{is}>0\})

\displaystyle\leq T\beta\mathcal{L}^{2}(\{x\in B_{r}(\bar{x})\colon m(x)\cdot e^{i\bar{s}}>-2\beta\}).

Therefore integrating (

\displaystyle\frac{1}{T}\int_{\Gamma}N(\gamma)\,d\omega\leq\frac{4}{\beta}\nu(B_{r}(\bar{x}))+\frac{4\beta}{r}\mathcal{L}^{2}(\{x\in B_{r}(\bar{x}):m(x)\cdot e^{i\bar{s}}>-2\beta\}).

(42)

To estimate from below the left-hand side of (

) we use its link with the Lagrangian flux across the curve
\mathcal{C}_{\bar{\gamma}}
. Specifically,
for any

, we have

\displaystyle\int f(t,x,s)\mathbf{1}_{m(x)\cdot e^{is}>0}(i\tau_{\bar{\gamma}}(x)\cdot e^{is})\,ds\,d\mathcal{H}^{1}_{\lfloor\mathcal{C}_{\bar{\gamma}}}(x)\,dt=\int_{\Gamma}\langle F_{\gamma},f\rangle\,d\omega(\gamma),

\displaystyle\langle F_{\gamma},f\rangle

\displaystyle=\sum_{t\in X^{+}}f(t,\gamma_{x}(t),\gamma_{s}(t^{+}))-\sum_{t\in X^{-}}f(t,\gamma_{x}(t),\gamma_{s}(t^{-}))

\displaystyle X^{+}

\displaystyle=\left\{t\in(t_{\gamma}^{-},t_{\gamma}^{+})\colon\gamma_{x}(t)\in\mathcal{C}_{\bar{\gamma}},\;i\tau_{\bar{\gamma}}(\gamma_{x}(t))\cdot e^{i\gamma_{s}(t)}>0\right\}

\displaystyle X^{-}

\displaystyle=\left\{t\in(t_{\gamma}^{-},t_{\gamma}^{+})\colon\gamma_{x}(t)\in\mathcal{C}_{\bar{\gamma}},\;i\tau_{\bar{\gamma}}(\gamma_{x}(t))\cdot e^{i\gamma_{s}(t^{-})}<0\right\}.

corresponds to intersection times of
\gamma
with

where
\gamma
exits

in direction of the normal

, and the set
X^{-}
to intersection times where

enters

in the opposite direction. Note that these two sets may not be disjoint since

could ‘bounce’ on

.
The proof of this flux formula is similar to the proof of Lemma
3.5
for the boundary flux, and details are provided in
[

, Theorem 1.4]
in a very similar setting.
Applying this flux formula to

\displaystyle f(t,x,s)\approx\mathbf{1}_{t\in(0,T)}\mathbf{1}_{x\in B_{r/2}(\bar{x})}\mathbf{1}_{s\in(\bar{s}+5\beta/4,\bar{s}+7\beta/4)},

we see that there are no contributions from

and obtain

\displaystyle\int_{\Gamma}N(\gamma)d\omega

\displaystyle=T\int\mathbf{1}_{m(x)\cdot e^{is}>0}(i\tau(x)\cdot e^{is})\mathbf{1}_{s\in[\bar{s}+\frac{5\beta}{4},\bar{s}+\frac{7\beta}{4}]}\,ds\,d\mathcal{H}^{1}_{\lfloor\mathcal{C}_{\bar{\gamma}}\cap B_{r/2}(\bar{x})}(x).

Using also (

\displaystyle\frac{1}{T}\int_{\Gamma}N(\gamma)d\omega

\displaystyle\geq\sin\left(\frac{\beta}{4}\right)\frac{\beta}{2}\frac{r}{2}\geq\frac{1}{8\pi}\beta^{2}r.

Combining this with (

\frac{1}{32\pi}\beta^{2}r\leq\frac{1}{\beta}\nu(B_{r}(\bar{x}))+\frac{\beta}{r}\mathcal{L}^{2}(\{x\in B_{r}(\bar{x}):m(x)\cdot e^{i\bar{s}}>-2\beta\}).

This implies the statement of Lemma
5.2
.
∎

.

(43)

small to be chosen later and assume

This yields

\displaystyle\int E\wedge B

\displaystyle\lesssim\|\chi(\Sigma(m_{1})-\Sigma(m_{2}))\|_{L^{4}(\Omega)}\|\nabla\cdot(\chi(\Sigma(m_{1})-\Sigma(m_{2}))\|_{W^{-1,4/3}(\Omega)}

\displaystyle\lesssim\|\chi(m_{1}-m_{2})\|_{L^{4}(\Omega)}\|\nabla\cdot(\chi(\Sigma(m_{1})-\Sigma(m_{2}))\|_{W^{-1,4/3}(\Omega)}.

we used
|\nabla\Sigma|\lesssim 1
for the last inequality. Moreover, thanks to
[
28

we have

\displaystyle E\wedge B=\chi^{2}\det(\Sigma(m_{1})-\Sigma(m_{2}))\gtrsim\chi^{2}|m_{1}-m_{2}|^{4}\geq\chi^{4}|m_{1}-m_{2}|^{4}.

Therefore we deduce from the previous inequality that

\displaystyle\|\chi(m_{1}-m_{2})\|_{L^{4}}^{3}\lesssim\|\nabla\cdot(\chi(\Sigma(m_{1})-\Sigma(m_{2}))\|_{W^{-1,4/3}(\Omega)}.

(45)

Next we compute, for any
\zeta\in W_{0}^{1,4}(\Omega)\subset C_{0}(\Omega)
,

\displaystyle\left\langle\nabla\cdot(\chi(\Sigma(m_{1})-\Sigma(m_{2})),\zeta\right\rangle

\displaystyle=\int\zeta\nabla\chi\cdot(\Sigma(m_{1})-\Sigma(m_{2}))+\langle\nabla\cdot\Sigma(m_{1})-\nabla\cdot\Sigma(m_{2}),\chi\zeta\rangle

\displaystyle\lesssim\|\zeta\|_{\infty}\|\nabla\chi(m_{1}-m_{2})\|_{L^{1}}+\|\zeta\|_{\infty}\|\nabla\cdot\Sigma(m_{1})-\nabla\cdot\Sigma(m_{2})\|_{\mathcal{M}(\Omega)}

\displaystyle\lesssim c_{0}(\Omega)\|\nabla\zeta\|_{L^{4}}\left(\|\nabla\chi(m_{1}-m_{2})\|_{L^{1}}+\|\nabla\cdot\Sigma(m_{1})-\nabla\cdot\Sigma(m_{2})\|_{\mathcal{M}(\Omega)}\right),

where
c_{0}(\Omega)
is the norm of the Sobolev embedding
W_{0}^{1,4}(\Omega)\subset L^{\infty}(\Omega)
. By definition of
W^{-1,4/3}=(W_{0}^{1,4})^{\prime}
this implies

\displaystyle\|\nabla\cdot(\chi(\Sigma(m_{1})-\Sigma(m_{2}))\|_{W^{-1,4/3}(\Omega)}

\displaystyle\lesssim c_{0}(\Omega)\left(\|\nabla\chi(m_{1}-m_{2})\|_{L^{1}}+\|\nabla\cdot\Sigma(m_{1})-\nabla\cdot\Sigma(m_{2})\|_{\mathcal{M}(\Omega)}\right).

Plugging this into (
45
) we obtain

\displaystyle\|\chi(m_{1}-m_{2})\|_{L^{4}}^{3}\lesssim c_{0}(\Omega)\left(\|\nabla\chi(m_{1}-m_{2})\|_{L^{1}}+\|\nabla\cdot\Sigma(m_{1})-\nabla\cdot\Sigma(m_{2})\|_{\mathcal{M}(\Omega)}\right).

Finally, choosing
\chi=\chi_{\varepsilon}
such that
\chi_{\varepsilon}(x)=1
for
\dist(x,\partial\Omega)>\varepsilon
and

for
\varepsilon\to 0
and using the trace property of
m_{1},m_{2}
we obtain the result.
∎

Appendix A
Entropy productions, compactness and kinetic formulation

The kinetic formulation (
9
) is intimately linked to the notion of entropy, also borrowed from conservation laws, and introduced in
[
11
]
for the eikonal equation. A smooth map
\Phi\colon\mathbb{S}^{1}\to{\mathbb{R}}^{2}
is an entropy for the eikonal equation (
1
) if and only if it preserves the divergence-free quality of smooth solutions :

\displaystyle(|m|=1\text{ and }\nabla\cdot m=0)\quad\Rightarrow\quad\nabla\cdot\Phi(m)=0,

for any open
\Omega\subset{\mathbb{R}}^{2}
and smooth
m\colon\Omega\to{\mathbb{R}}^{2}
.
Direct calculation shows that this is equivalent to the existence of a smooth function
\lambda_{\Phi}\colon{\mathbb{R}}/2\pi{\mathbb{Z}}\to{\mathbb{R}}
such that

\displaystyle\frac{d}{d\theta}\Phi(e^{i\theta})=\lambda_{\Phi}(\theta)ie^{i\theta}\qquad\forall\theta\in{\mathbb{R}}.

To any
f\in C^{\infty}({\mathbb{R}}/2\pi{\mathbb{Z}})
one may associate an entropy
\Phi_{f}
given by

\displaystyle\Phi_{f}(z)=\int_{{\mathbb{R}}/2\pi{\mathbb{Z}}}f(s)\mathbf{1}_{z\cdot e^{is}>0}\,ds\qquad\forall z\in\mathbb{S}^{1},

(48)

and the kinetic formulation (
9
) is equivalent to

\displaystyle\langle\nabla\cdot\Phi_{f}(m),\zeta\rangle=-\langle\sigma,f^{\prime}(s)\zeta(x)\rangle\qquad\forall\zeta\in C_{c}^{\infty}(\Omega),f\in C^{\infty}({\mathbb{R}}/2\pi{\mathbb{Z}}).

(49)

An entropy
\Phi
, whenever extended to
{\mathbb{R}}^{2}
by setting
\widehat{\Phi}(re^{i\theta})=\eta(r)\Phi(e^{i\theta})
for some fixed real-valued cut-off function
\eta\in C_{c}^{\infty}(0,\infty)
with
\eta(1)=1
, satisfies (see e.g.
[
19
,
9
]
)

\displaystyle\nabla\cdot\widehat{\Phi}(m)=\Psi(m)\cdot\nabla(1-|m|^{2})+\alpha(m)\nabla\cdot m\qquad\forall m\in H^{1}(\Omega;{\mathbb{R}}^{2}),

where
\Psi\colon{\mathbb{R}}^{2}\to{\mathbb{R}}^{2}
and
\alpha\colon{\mathbb{R}}^{2}\to{\mathbb{R}}
are such that

\displaystyle\|\nabla\Psi\|_{C^{1}({\mathbb{R}}^{2})}+\|\nabla\alpha\|_{C^{1}({\mathbb{R}}^{2})}\leq c\|\lambda_{\Phi}\|_{C^{1}({\mathbb{R}}/2\pi{\mathbb{Z}})},

for some constant
c>0
depending only on the cut-off function
\eta
. Applying this to
m^{\prime}_{\varepsilon}=(m^{1}_{\varepsilon},m^{2}_{\varepsilon})
for some sequence
m_{\varepsilon}\in H^{1}(\Omega;{\mathbb{R}}^{3})
with
F_{\varepsilon}(m_{\varepsilon})\leq C
, we find

\displaystyle\nabla\cdot\widehat{\Phi}(m^{\prime}_{\varepsilon})

\displaystyle=\nabla\cdot\left[\Psi(m^{\prime}_{\varepsilon})(1-|m^{\prime}_{\varepsilon}|^{2})-\alpha(m^{\prime}_{\varepsilon})H_{\varepsilon}\right]

\displaystyle\quad-(1-|m^{\prime}_{\varepsilon}|^{2})\nabla\cdot\Psi(m^{\prime}_{\varepsilon})-H_{\varepsilon}\cdot\nabla[\alpha(m^{\prime}_{\varepsilon})]\qquad\text{ in }\mathcal{D}^{\prime}(\Omega),

(50)

where
H_{\varepsilon}\colon{\mathbb{R}}^{2}\to{\mathbb{R}}^{2}
is the curl-free vector field such that
\nabla\cdot H_{\varepsilon}=-\nabla\cdot(\mathbf{1}_{\Omega}m^{\prime}_{\varepsilon})
.
Boundedness of the energy
F_{\varepsilon}(m_{\varepsilon};\Omega)
implies that the first line in the right-hand side of (
A
) tends to
0
in
H^{-1}(\Omega)
, while the second line is bounded in
L^{1}(\Omega)
.
One can then argue exactly as in
[
11
]
, to deduce that
\nabla\cdot\widehat{\Phi}(m^{\prime}_{\varepsilon})
is precompact in
H^{-1}(\Omega)
and that
m^{\prime}_{\varepsilon}
is precompact in
L^{2}(\Omega)
.
This gives the precompactness of
m_{\varepsilon}
since
m_{\varepsilon}^{3}\to 0
in
L^{2}(\Omega)
.
Moreover taking the limit
\varepsilon\to 0
in (
A
) along a converging subsequence
m_{\varepsilon}\to m
, one infers

\displaystyle\langle\dv\Phi(m),\zeta\rangle\lesssim\|\zeta\|_{\infty}\|\lambda_{\Phi}\|_{C^{1}}\liminf_{\varepsilon\to 0}F_{\varepsilon}(m_{\varepsilon};\Omega)\qquad\forall\zeta\in C_{c}^{\infty}(\Omega).

Using the arguments of
[
12
, § 3.1]
(see
[
27
, Appendix B]
for more details), this estimate provides the existence of
\sigma\in\mathcal{M}(\Omega\times{\mathbb{R}}/2\pi{\mathbb{Z}})
satisfying (
9
).

Appendix B
On the sharp lower bound for
E_{\varepsilon}^{ARS}

The analysis recalled in Appendix
A
provides an energy lower bound
in terms of the kinetic dissipation measure of the limit map.
In the case of
E_{\varepsilon}^{ARS}
(
4
) from
[
1
]
, these arguments can be refined to obtain a sharp lower bound: for any
m=\lim m_{\varepsilon}
we have

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}\nu(\Omega)\leq\liminf_{\varepsilon\to 0}E_{\varepsilon}^{ARS}(m_{\varepsilon};\Omega),

(51)

where
\nu
is the minimal kinetic dissipation measure associated to
m
as defined in (
10
). Moreover this lower bound is sharp if
m\in BV(\Omega;{\mathbb{R}}^{2})
, in the sense of
\Gamma
-convergence: there exists
m_{\varepsilon}\to m
such that

\displaystyle\limsup_{\varepsilon\to 0}E_{\varepsilon}^{ARS}(m_{\varepsilon};\Omega)\leq\frac{1}{2}\nu(\Omega).

(52)

The sharp lower bound (
51
) is contained in
[
1
]
, but not explicitly stated, so we briefly recall here why it is valid. The key step is
[
1
, Lemma 2.2]
, which ensures the existence of
\tilde{m}_{\varepsilon}\in W^{1,p}(\Omega;\mathbb{S}^{1})
for
1\leq p<2
, such that
\tilde{m}_{\varepsilon}\to m
and

\displaystyle\int_{\Omega}|\nabla\tilde{m}_{\varepsilon}|\cdot|\tilde{H}_{\varepsilon}|\,dx\leq E_{\varepsilon}^{ARS}(m_{\varepsilon};\Omega)+o(1).

(53)

Here
\tilde{H}_{\varepsilon}\colon{\mathbb{R}}^{2}\to{\mathbb{R}}^{2}
is the curl-free vector field such that
\nabla\cdot\tilde{H}_{\varepsilon}=-\nabla\cdot(\mathbf{1}_{\Omega}\tilde{m}_{\varepsilon})
. The gain provided by this lemma is that
\tilde{m}_{\varepsilon}
takes values into
\mathbb{S}^{1}
, so one can directly compute entropy productions (without using an extension
\widehat{\Phi}
as in the previous section). Specifically, for an entropy
\Phi
we have

\displaystyle\nabla\cdot\Phi(\tilde{m}_{\varepsilon})

\displaystyle=\lambda_{\Phi}(\tilde{m}_{\varepsilon})\nabla\cdot\tilde{m}_{\varepsilon}

\displaystyle=-\nabla\cdot\left[\lambda_{\Phi}(\tilde{m}_{\varepsilon})\tilde{H}_{\varepsilon}\right]+\lambda_{\Phi}^{\prime}(\tilde{m}_{\varepsilon})H_{\varepsilon}\cdot\nabla\tilde{m}_{\varepsilon}\qquad\text{in }\mathcal{D}^{\prime}(\Omega).

Here
\lambda_{\Phi}(e^{i\theta})=ie^{i\theta}\cdot(d/d\theta)\Phi(e^{i\theta})
as in Appendix
A
. As in Appendix
A
this implies

\displaystyle|\nabla\cdot\Phi(m)|(\Omega)\leq\|\lambda_{\Phi}^{\prime}\|_{\infty}\liminf_{\varepsilon\to 0}E_{\varepsilon}^{ARS}(m_{\varepsilon};\Omega).

This is also the argument in Step 1 of the proof of
[
1
, Theorem 1]
.
A natural refinement of that argument (see e.g. the proof of
[
22
, Proposition 2]
) leads to

\displaystyle\left(\bigvee_{\Phi\in S}|\nabla\cdot\Phi(m)|\right)(\Omega)\leq\liminf_{n\to\infty}E_{\varepsilon}^{ARS}(m_{\varepsilon};\Omega),

where
S
is any class of entropies
\Phi
with
\|\lambda_{\Phi}^{\prime}\|_{\infty}\leq 1
, and
\bigvee
denotes the lowest upper bound measure of a family of measures
[
3
, Definition 1.68]
. Applying this to entropies
\Phi_{f}
as in (
48
), which satisfy

\lambda_{\Phi_{f}}(\theta)=f(\theta+\pi/2)+f(\theta-\pi/2)
, we deduce

\displaystyle\left(\bigvee_{|f^{\prime}|\leq 1/2}|\nabla\cdot\Phi_{f}(m)|\right)(\Omega)\leq\liminf_{n\to\infty}E_{\varepsilon}^{ARS}(m_{\varepsilon};\Omega).

Recalling (
49
) and (
10
), we see that the left-hand side is equal to
(1/2)\nu(\Omega)
, which proves (
51
).

For a
BV
map
m
, we let
J_{m}
denote its jump set and
m^{\pm}
the traces of
m
along
J_{m}
.
Then, the calculations in
[
29
, Corollary 3.4]
imply that we have

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}\nu(\Omega)

\displaystyle=\int_{J_{m}}c(|m_{+}-m_{-}|)\,d\mathcal{H}^{1},

\displaystyle\text{where }c(2\sin X)

\displaystyle=\begin{cases}2\left|\sin X-X\cos X\right|&\text{if }0\leq X\leq\pi/4,\\
2\left|(X-\pi/2)\cos X-\sin X+\sqrt{2}\right|&\text{if }\pi/4\leq X\leq\pi/2.\end{cases}

This is exactly the expression of the lower bound in
[
1
, Theorem 1]
.
Moreover, that lower bound is shown to be optimal in
[
1
, Theorem 2]
, in the sense that the energy cost

corresponds to the asymptotic energy per unit-length of an ideal wall transition between to limit values
m^{\pm}
with
|m^{+}-m^{-}|=2\sin X
.
This implies the
\Gamma
-upper bound (
52
) using e.g. the techniques in
[
35
]
.

\delta\in(0,1/(8K))
, there exist

x_{1},x_{2}\in E(2\delta^{2})
such that

\displaystyle\frac{x_{1}-z}{|x_{1}-z|}=e^{i\theta_{1}}\frac{\tilde{x}}{|\tilde{x}|}\quad\text{for some }\theta_{1}\in(-c_{2}\delta,-c_{1}\delta t),

\displaystyle\frac{x_{2}-z}{|x_{2}-z|}=e^{i\theta_{2}}\frac{\tilde{x}}{|\tilde{x}|}\quad\text{for some }\theta_{2}\in(c_{1}\delta t,c_{2}\delta),

\displaystyle\tau(x_{1})\cdot\frac{x_{1}-z}{|x_{1}-z|}\leq-c_{3}\delta t,\qquad\tau(x_{2})\cdot\frac{x_{2}-z}{|x_{2}-z|}\geq c_{3}\delta t.

These will be used in Step 5 as illustrated by Figure

.

The blue arrows denote the directions
e^{i\alpha_{1}},e^{i\alpha_{2}},e^{i\alpha_{3}}

Write
\tilde{x}=g(\tilde{s})
for some
\tilde{s}\in{\mathbb{R}}
. The map
g
is
1
-Lipschitz and
|g(\tilde{s})|=R
, so by Lemma
2.5
we have
g(s)\in E_{*}
for
|s-\tilde{s}|\leq\delta\leq 1/(4K)
.

Consider the
C^{1}
function
\hat{\theta}\colon{\mathbb{R}}\to{\mathbb{R}}
such that
\hat{\theta}(\tilde{s})=0
and

\displaystyle\frac{g(s)}{|g(s)|}=e^{i\hat{\theta}(s)}\frac{\tilde{x}}{|\tilde{x}|}.

As in Step 2 we have

\displaystyle\hat{\theta}^{\prime}(s)=\frac{1}{|g(s)|}\frac{ig(s)}{|g(s)|}\cdot\dot{g}(s).

Since
|g(\tilde{s})|=R
and
g
is 1-Lipschitz, Lemma
2.3
implies

\displaystyle\left(\frac{g(s)}{|g(s)|}\cdot\dot{g}(s)\right)^{2}

\displaystyle\leq 4K|s-\tilde{s}|\leq\frac{1}{2}\qquad\text{for }|s-\tilde{s}|\leq\delta\leq\frac{1}{8K}.

We deduce as in Step 2 that
\hat{\theta}^{\prime}(s)\geq 1/(2\pi)
for
|s-\tilde{s}|\leq\delta
. Using also
|g|\geq R
we therefore have

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2\pi}\leq\hat{\theta}^{\prime}(s)\leq\frac{1}{R}\qquad\text{for }|s-\tilde{s}|\leq\delta.

On the other hand, setting

\displaystyle\varphi(s)=|g(s)-z|^{2}=\left|\frac{|g(s)|}{R}e^{i\hat{\theta}(s)}\tilde{x}-t\tilde{x}\right|^{2},

we have

\displaystyle\varphi(s)-\varphi(\tilde{s})

\displaystyle=\left|\frac{|g(s)|}{R}\tilde{x}-t\tilde{x}-\frac{|g(s)|}{R}(1-e^{i\hat{\theta}(s)})\tilde{x}\right|^{2}-(R-Rt)^{2}

\displaystyle=(|g(s)|-Rt)^{2}-(R-Rt)^{2}+|g(s)|^{2}|1-e^{i\hat{\theta}(s)}|^{2}

\displaystyle\quad-2|g(s)|(|g(s)|-Rt)(1-\cos\hat{\theta}(s))

\displaystyle=(|g(s)|-Rt)^{2}-(R-Rt)^{2}+2Rt|g(s)|(1-\cos\hat{\theta}(s))

\displaystyle\geq 2tR^{2}(1-\cos\hat{\theta}(s)),

where the last inequality follows from
|g|\geq R
and
t\leq 1
.
As
\hat{\theta}(\tilde{s})=0
and
|\hat{\theta}^{\prime}|\leq 1/R
, for
|s-\tilde{s}|\leq\delta
we have

|\hat{\theta}(s)|\leq\delta/R\leq\delta K\leq 1/8
, and this implies
1-\cos\hat{\theta}(s)\geq\hat{\theta}(s)^{2}/4
, so

\displaystyle\varphi(s)-\varphi(\tilde{s})

\displaystyle\geq\frac{t}{2}R^{2}\hat{\theta}(s)^{2}\qquad\text{for }|s-\tilde{s}|\leq\delta.

Using moreover that
\hat{\theta}^{\prime}\geq 1/(2\pi)
we deduce

\displaystyle\varphi(s)-\varphi(\tilde{s})\geq\frac{tR^{2}}{8\pi^{2}}(s-\tilde{s})^{2}\qquad\text{for }|s-\tilde{s}|\leq\delta.

Hence there exist
s_{1}\in(\tilde{s}-\delta,\tilde{s})
,
s_{2}\in(\tilde{s},\tilde{s}+\delta)
such that

\displaystyle\varphi^{\prime}(s_{1})

\displaystyle=-\frac{1}{\delta}(\varphi(\tilde{s}-\delta)-\varphi(\tilde{s}))\leq-\frac{R^{2}}{8\pi^{2}}\delta t,

\displaystyle\varphi^{\prime}(s_{2})

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\delta}(\varphi(\tilde{s}+\delta)-\varphi(\tilde{s}))\geq\frac{R^{2}}{8\pi^{2}}\delta t.

Since
\varphi^{\prime}(s)=2\tau(g(s))\cdot(g(s)-z)
and

|g(s)-z|\leq\pi
,
setting
x_{1}=g(s_{1})
,
x_{2}=g(s_{2})

we obtain

\displaystyle\tau(x_{1})\cdot\frac{x_{1}-z}{|x_{1}-z|}

\displaystyle=\frac{\varphi^{\prime}(s_{1})}{2|g(s_{1})-z|}\leq-c_{3}\delta t

\displaystyle\tau(x_{2})\cdot\frac{x_{2}-z}{|x_{2}-z|}

\displaystyle=\frac{\varphi^{\prime}(s_{2})}{2|g(s_{2})-z|}\geq c_{3}\delta t,\qquad c_{3}=\frac{R^{2}}{16\pi^{3}}.

This proves the last assertion of Step 4. Moreover, since
g^{\prime}(\tilde{s})=0
,
\delta\in(0,1/(8K)
and
|\ddot{g}|\leq K
, the points
x_{1},x_{2}
lie outside of the disk of radius
1/K
tangent to
D_{R}(x_{0})
at
\tilde{x}
, and since
R\geq 1/K
we infer that they are at distance at most
2K\delta^{2}
from
D_{R}(x_{0})
, and thanks to Lemma
2.5
they belong to
E(2K\delta^{2}/R)\subset E(2\delta^{2})
.

It remains to show that

\displaystyle\frac{x_{j}-z}{|x_{j}-z|}=e^{i\theta_{j}}\frac{\tilde{x}}{|\tilde{x}|}\quad\text{for }j=1,2,\quad-c_{2}\delta<\theta_{1}<-c_{1}\delta t,\;c_{1}\delta t<\theta_{2}<c_{2}\delta.

By definition of
\hat{\theta}
we know that

\displaystyle\frac{x_{j}}{|x_{j}|}=e^{i\hat{\theta}(s_{j})}\frac{\tilde{x}}{|\tilde{x}|},

so we relate
\hat{\theta}(s_{j})
to
\theta_{j}
and estimate
\hat{\theta}(s_{j})
. To do the first, consider, for any fixed
s\in{\mathbb{R}}
, the
C^{1}
function
\alpha_{s}\colon[0,1/2]\to{\mathbb{R}}
such that
\alpha_{s}(0)=\hat{\theta}(s)
and

\displaystyle\frac{g(s)-t\tilde{x}}{|g(s)-t\tilde{x}|}=e^{i\alpha_{s}(t)}\frac{\tilde{x}}{|\tilde{x}|}.

That way, we have
\hat{\theta}(s_{j})=\alpha_{s_{j}}(0)
and can choose
\theta_{j}=\alpha_{s_{j}}(t)
. Moreover we have

\displaystyle\alpha_{s}^{\prime}(t)=-\frac{i(g(s)-t\tilde{x})}{|g(s)-t\tilde{x}|^{2}}\cdot\tilde{x}=\frac{g(s)\cdot(i\tilde{x})}{|g(s)-t\tilde{x}|^{2}}=\frac{R|g(s)|}{|g(s)-t\tilde{x}|^{2}}\sin\hat{\theta}(s).

Note that since
\hat{\theta}(\tilde{s})=0
and
1/(2\pi)\leq\hat{\theta}^{\prime}\leq 1/R
we have

\displaystyle 0<\mathrm{sign}(s-\tilde{s})\hat{\theta}(s)\leq\frac{\delta}{R}\leq\delta K\leq\frac{1}{8}\quad\text{for }|s-\tilde{s}|\leq\delta.

In particular, using
|g|\leq\pi
,
|g-t\tilde{x}|\geq R/2
and
|\sin\hat{\theta}|\leq|\hat{\theta}|
, we deduce

\displaystyle 0<\mathrm{sign}(s-\tilde{s})\alpha_{s}^{\prime}(t)\leq\frac{4\pi}{R^{2}}\delta\leq\frac{\pi}{2}K\qquad\text{for }|s-\tilde{s}|\leq\delta,

hence, recalling
\theta_{j}-\hat{\theta}(s_{j})=\int_{0}^{t}\alpha_{s_{j}}^{\prime}
, we infer

\displaystyle-(1+\pi/2)K\delta\leq\theta_{2}\leq\hat{\theta}(s_{1})<0<\hat{\theta}(s_{2})\leq\theta_{2}\leq(1+\pi/2)K\delta.

The proof of Step 4 will be complete once we show that
|\hat{\theta}(s_{j})|\geq c_{1}\delta t
for
j=1,2
.
Because
\varphi^{\prime}(\tilde{s})=0
and

\displaystyle|s_{j}-\tilde{s}|\geq\frac{|\varphi^{\prime}(s_{j})|}{4K}\geq 2\pi c_{1}t,\quad c_{1}=\frac{R^{2}}{32\pi^{2}K}.

Combining this with
\hat{\theta}^{\prime}\geq 1/(2\pi)
on

Step 5.
We choose

\displaystyle\frac{x_{1}-z}{|x_{1}-z|}=e^{i\alpha_{1}},\quad\frac{x_{2}-z}{|x_{2}-z|}=-e^{i\alpha_{2}},\quad\frac{y-z}{|y-z|}=-e^{i\alpha_{3}}.

By definition, the three lines

Moreover by Step 4 we have

\displaystyle\tau(x_{j})\cdot e^{i\alpha_{j}}\leq-c_{3}\delta t\qquad\text{for }j=1,2.

The function

Recall from Step 3 that we have

\displaystyle\frac{\tilde{x}}{|\tilde{x}|}=e^{i\tilde{\theta}_{0}}\frac{y}{|y|}\qquad\text{ with }\frac{\eta R}{8\pi^{2}K}\leq\tilde{\theta}_{0}\leq\pi-\frac{\eta R}{8\pi^{2}K}.
