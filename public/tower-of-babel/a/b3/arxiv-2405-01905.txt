Schwarz Methods for Nonlocal Problems
Matthias Schuster, Christian Vollmann, Volker Schulz

Abstract




Abstract.

The first domain decomposition methods for partial differential equations were already developed in 1870 by H. A. Schwarz. Here we consider a nonlocal Dirichlet problem with variable coefficients, where a nonlocal diffusion operator is used. We find that domain decomposition methods like the so-called Schwarz methods seem to be a natural way to solve these nonlocal problems. In this work we show the convergence for nonlocal problems, where specific symmetric kernels are employed, and present the implementation of the multiplicative and additive Schwarz algorithms in the above mentioned nonlocal setting.

Keywords.
Schwarz methods, nonlocal diffusion, finite element method, domain decomposition.

Introduction

Nonlocal operators can be helpful to describe physical phenomena, where long range interactions occur. Therefore, in these cases, nonlocal models are better suited than the classical approach via partial differential equations. Nonlocal operators have already been applied in many fields, such as image denoising
[
3
,
11
]
, anomalous diffusion
[
2
,
27
,
6
]
, peridynamics
[
26
,
14
]
and stochastic processes
[
21
,
8
]
, just to name a few.

Moreover, nonlocal interface problems as well as models that contain domain-dependent parameters have become popular in recent years, see e.g.,
[
23
,
4
,
12
]
. These domain-dependent parameters may naturally suggest a domain decomposition which leads to domain decomposition methods that are widely used in the case of partial differential equations and have been investigated in, e.g.,
[
20
,
28
]
.
In the matter of nonlocal equations the approach of finite element tearing and interconnecting (FETI) has been successfully applied as described in
[
15
,
31
]
. Another class of domain decomposition methods are Schwarz algorithms, that were initially developed in
[
24
]
for partial differential equations. An abstract framework for the Schwarz approach can be found in
[
19
,
17
,
18
]
.

Although FETI is a very efficient way to solve nonlocal equations, Schwarz methods have several advantages. They are easier to implement and it is straightforward to incorporate black-box solvers, whereas for FETI the finite element formulation on overlapping domains needs to be changed. Moreover, we could observe that Schwarz methods can also converge in case of a nonsymmetric kernel as shown in an experiment in Chapter
7.3
. Lastly, if the parameter
\delta
, which determines the the range of nonlocal interaction and thus the width of the nonlocal boundary regarding one (sub-)domain (see Section
2
), is quite huge, FETI cannot decompose the domain in many subdomains. In this case, GMRES with a Schwarz preconditioner might be faster, especially if we include the set-up times of both solvers.

Schwarz methods have also been utilized to solve an energy-based Local-to-Nonlocal(LtN) coupling in
[
1
]
. Additionally, in
[
1
]
they showed the convergence of the multiplicative Schwarz method for this energy-based LtN coupling by applying
[
19
, Theorem I.1]
. In Section
4
we present the convergence of the multiplicative Schwarz method for two cases of nonlocal problems in a similar fashion by referring to
[
19
, Theorem I.2]
. Furthermore, we point out that the Schwarz formulation solves linear, quadratic and cubic patch tests. These patch tests often serve as a criterion to evaluate and therefore compare Local-to-Nonlocal couplings. We refer to the review paper
[
10
]
for more information on LtN couplings. Additionally we test GMRES against two preconditioned versions of GMRES that result from the multiplicative and additive Schwarz method combined with the finite element method.

We start by introducing nonlocal Dirichlet problems and nonlocal problems that involve Neumann boundary conditions in Chapter
2
. After that we describe the multiplicative and additive Schwarz method for the previously mentioned nonlocal problems in Chapter
3
. Next, in Chapter
4
, we prove the convergence of this multiplicative Schwarz method to the unique solution under the assumption that the kernel is symmetric. Furthermore, we present the finite element method as a way to numerically solve nonlocal problems in Chapter
5
.
Here, we also illustrate how Schwarz methods are applied for this discrete framework. In Chapter
6
we shortly introduce patch tests, which are used to evaluate Local-to-Nonlocal or Nonlocal-to-Nonlocal couplings. In the last Chapter
7
we start by testing the Schwarz methods on two examples. The first one is a nonlocal Dirichlet problem, where we use a singular symmetric kernel, and the second example is a nonlocal problem with Neumann boundary, where we employ a constant kernel. Then, we investigate an example of a nonlocal Dirichlet problem with a nonsymmetric kernel that additionally satisfies the patch test. Lastly, we study two versions of preconditioned GMRES, that are connected to the Schwarz method for the finite element formulation, and we compare these two approaches to a GMRES without preconditioning.

Problem Formulation

Let
\Omega\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}
be an open and bounded domain and
\{\Omega_{i}\}_{i=1}^{n}
be a partition of
\Omega
, where
\Omega_{i}\subset\Omega
is open for
i=1,...,n
, and that satisfies

\displaystyle\overline{\Omega}=\bigcup_{i=1}^{n}\overline{\Omega}_{i}\text{ and }\Omega_{i}\cap\Omega_{j}=\emptyset\text{ for }i,j=1,...,n\text{ and }i\neq j.

(1)

From now on we denote by
\gamma:\mathbb{R}^{d}\times\mathbb{R}^{d}\rightarrow[0,\infty)
a nonnegative (interaction) kernel and define the nonlocal boundary
\mathcal{I}
of
\Omega
as follows

\displaystyle\mathcal{I}:=\{\mathbf{y}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}\setminus\Omega:\int_{\Omega}\gamma(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y})~d\mathbf{x}>0\}.

Based on the partition
\{\Omega_{i}\}_{i=1}^{n}
we can express the kernel
\gamma
as

\displaystyle\gamma=\sum_{i,j=1}^{n}\gamma_{ij}\chi_{\Omega_{i}\times\Omega_{j}}+\sum_{i=1}^{n}\gamma_{i\mathcal{I}}\chi_{\Omega_{i}\times\mathcal{I}}+\sum_{i=1}^{n}\gamma_{\mathcal{I}i}\chi_{\mathcal{I}\times\Omega_{i}},

(2)

with potentially different nonnegative kernels

\displaystyle\gamma_{ij}:\Omega_{i}\times\Omega_{j}\rightarrow[0,\infty),\gamma_{i\mathcal{I}}:\Omega_{i}\times\mathcal{I}\rightarrow[0,\infty)\text{ and }\gamma_{\mathcal{I}i}:\mathcal{I}\times\Omega_{i}\rightarrow[0,\infty),\text{ for }i,j=1,...,n.

Then, we define the nonlocal diffusion operator
-\mathcal{L}
, which can be found in, e.g.,
[
8
,
5
,
7
,
29
,
30
]
, as

\displaystyle-\mathcal{L}u(\mathbf{x})=\int\limits_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\left(u(\mathbf{x})-u(\mathbf{y})\right)\gamma(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y})~d\mathbf{y}.

(3)

2.1
Nonlocal Dirichlet Problems

One class of problems, that we focus on, are nonhomogeneous steady-state Dirichlet problems with nonlocal boundary constraints given by

\displaystyle\begin{split}-\mathcal{L}u&=f\text{ on }\Omega\\
u&=g\text{ on }\mathcal{I},\end{split}

(4)

where the forcing term
f
may vary across the various parts of the decomposition (
1
), i.e., there exist possibly mutually different
f_{i}:\Omega_{i}\rightarrow\mathbb{R},\text{ for }i=1,...,n,
such that

\displaystyle f=\sum_{i=1}^{n}f_{i}\chi_{\Omega_{i}}.

(5)

Similar types of nonlocal problems can also be found in, e.g.,
[
5
,
23
]
. Moreover, the kernel
\gamma
is assumed to be symmetric and is supposed to fulfill the following conditions

(K1)

There exist
\delta>0
and
\phi:\mathbb{R}^{d}\times\mathbb{R}^{d}\rightarrow[0,\infty)
with
\gamma(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y})=\phi(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y})\chi_{B_{\delta}(\mathbf{x})}(\mathbf{y})
.

(K2)

There exist
0<\gamma_{0}<\infty
and
\epsilon\in(0,\delta)
such that
\gamma_{0}\leq\gamma(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y})
for
\mathbf{y}\in B_{\epsilon}(\mathbf{x})
and
\mathbf{x}\in\Omega
.

In order to derive a weak formulation, we multiply the first equation of the left-hand side of (
4
) by a test function
v:\Omega\cup\mathcal{I}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
and then integrating over
\Omega
yields

\displaystyle\left(-\mathcal{L}u,v\right)_{L^{2}(\Omega)}

\displaystyle=\left(f,v\right)_{L^{2}(\Omega)}

\displaystyle\Leftrightarrow\int_{\Omega}v(\mathbf{x})\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}(u(\mathbf{x})

\displaystyle-u(\mathbf{y}))\gamma(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y})~d\mathbf{y}d\mathbf{x}=\int_{\Omega}f(\mathbf{x})v(\mathbf{x})~d\mathbf{x}.

(6)

By applying Fubini’s theorem and by considering
v(\mathbf{y})=0
on
\mathcal{I}
the first integral of the left-hand side can be expressed as

\displaystyle\int_{\Omega}v(\mathbf{x})\int_{\Omega\cup\mathcal{I}}(u(\mathbf{x})-u(\mathbf{y}))\gamma(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y})~d\mathbf{y}d\mathbf{x}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\iint_{(\Omega\cup\mathcal{I})^{2}}(v(\mathbf{x})-v(\mathbf{y}))(u(\mathbf{x})-u(\mathbf{y}))\gamma(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y})~d\mathbf{y}d\mathbf{x}.

Thus we now define the nonlocal bilinear form

\displaystyle A(u,v)

\displaystyle:=\frac{1}{2}\iint_{(\Omega\cup\mathcal{I})^{2}}(v(\mathbf{x})-v(\mathbf{y}))(u(\mathbf{x})-u(\mathbf{y}))\gamma(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y})~d\mathbf{y}d\mathbf{x}

\displaystyle=\sum_{i,j=1}^{n}\frac{1}{2}\int_{\Omega_{i}}\int_{\Omega_{j}}\left(v(\mathbf{x})-v(\mathbf{y})\right)\left(u(\mathbf{x})-u(\mathbf{y})\right)\gamma_{ij}(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y})~d\mathbf{y}d\mathbf{x}

\displaystyle~~~~+\sum_{i=1}^{n}\int_{\Omega_{i}}\int_{\mathcal{I}}(v(\mathbf{x})-v(\mathbf{y}))(u(\mathbf{x})-u(\mathbf{y}))\gamma_{i\mathcal{I}}(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y})~d\mathbf{y}d\mathbf{x}

and the linear functional

\displaystyle F(v)

\displaystyle:=\int_{\Omega}f(\mathbf{x})v(\mathbf{x})~d\mathbf{x}=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\int_{\Omega_{i}}f_{i}(\mathbf{x})v(\mathbf{x})~d\mathbf{x},

where we used the definition of the forcing term (
5
) and that the kernel can be expressed as a sum of kernels as in (
2
).
Moreover, with
A
we can define the following semi-norm and norm, respectively,

\displaystyle\begin{split}|u|_{V(\Omega\cup\mathcal{I})}&:=\sqrt{A(u,u)},\\
||u||_{V(\Omega\cup\mathcal{I})}&:=|u|_{V(\Omega\cup\mathcal{I})}+||u||_{L^{2}(\Omega\cup\mathcal{I})}.\end{split}

(7)

This enables us to define the following spaces

\displaystyle V(\Omega\cup\mathcal{I})

\displaystyle:=\{u\in L^{2}(\Omega\cup\mathcal{I}):||u||_{V(\Omega\cup\mathcal{I})}<\infty\}\text{ and}

\displaystyle V_{c}(\Omega\cup\mathcal{I})

\displaystyle:=\{u\in V(\Omega\cup\mathcal{I}):u=0\text{ on }\mathcal{I}\}.

We now present a variational formulation of problem (
4
):

Definition 2.1
.

Given
f\in L^{2}(\Omega)
and
g\in V(\Omega\cup\mathcal{I})
, if
u\in V(\Omega\cup\mathcal{I})
solves

\displaystyle\begin{split}A(u,v)&=F(v)\text{ for all }v\in V_{c}(\Omega\cup\mathcal{I})\text{ and}\\
u&-g\in V_{c}(\Omega\cup\mathcal{I}),\end{split}

(8)

then
u
is called weak solution of (
4
).

Since
A(u,v)=A(u-g,v)+A(g,v)
and
u-g\in V_{c}(\Omega\cup\mathcal{I})
,
problem (
8
) can also be reformulated as a homogeneous Dirichlet problem

\displaystyle\begin{split}&\textit{Given $f\in L^{2}(\Omega)$ and $g\in V(\Omega\cup\mathcal{I})$, find }\tilde{u}\in V_{c}(\Omega\cup\mathcal{I}):\\
&A(\tilde{u},v)=F(v)-A(g,v)\textit{ for all }v\in V_{c}(\Omega\cup\mathcal{I}),\\
&\textit{then }u:=\tilde{u}+g\in V(\Omega\cup\mathcal{I})\textit{ is a weak solution of \eqref{weak_formulation}.}\end{split}

(9)

Remark 2.2
.

In this work, we show the convergence of the multiplicative Schwarz method for nonlocal Dirichlet problems for symmetric kernels, that fulfill (K1) and (K2). Additionally, the kernels are assumed to satisfy the requirements of one of the following two classes, where the well-posedness of the corresponding nonlocal Dirichlet problem (
8
) is shown, e.g., in
[
7
]
:

•

Integrable kernels:

There exist constants
0<\gamma_{1},\gamma_{2}<\infty
with

\displaystyle\gamma_{1}\leq\inf_{\mathbf{x}\in\Omega}\int_{\Omega\cup\mathcal{I}}\gamma(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y})~d\mathbf{y}\text{ and }\sup_{\mathbf{x}\in\Omega}\int_{\Omega\cup\mathcal{I}}\left(\gamma(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y})\right)^{2}~d\mathbf{y}\leq\left(\gamma_{2}\right)^{2}.

Then the solution
u\in V(\Omega\cup\mathcal{I})
for (
8
) exists, is unique and in this case the spaces
\left(V_{c}(\Omega\cup\mathcal{I}),|\cdot|_{V(\Omega\cup\mathcal{I})}\right)
and
\left(L_{c}^{2}(\Omega\cup\mathcal{I}),||\cdot||_{L^{2}(\Omega\cup\mathcal{I})}\right)
as well as
\left(V(\Omega\cup\mathcal{I}),||\cdot||_{V(\Omega\cup\mathcal{I})}\right)
and
\left(L^{2}(\Omega\cup\mathcal{I}),||\cdot||_{L^{2}(\Omega\cup\mathcal{I})}\right)
are equivalent.

•

Singular kernels:

There exist constants
0<\gamma_{*},\gamma^{*}<\infty
and
s\in(0,1)
with

\displaystyle\gamma_{*}<\gamma(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y})||\mathbf{x}+\mathbf{y}||_{2}^{d+2s}<\gamma^{*}\text{ for all }\mathbf{y}\in B_{\delta}(\mathbf{x})\text{ and }\mathbf{x}\in\Omega.

Then the solution to (
8
) exists, is unique and the spaces
\left(V_{c}(\Omega\cup\mathcal{I}),|\cdot|_{V(\Omega\cup\mathcal{I})}\right)
and

\left(H^{s}_{c}(\Omega\cup\mathcal{I}),|\cdot|_{H^{s}(\Omega\cup\mathcal{I})}\right)
as well as
\left(V(\Omega\cup\mathcal{I}),||\cdot||_{V(\Omega\cup\mathcal{I})}\right)
and
\left(H^{s}(\Omega\cup\mathcal{I}),||\cdot||_{H^{s}(\Omega\cup\mathcal{I})}\right)
are equivalent.

2.2
Nonlocal Problems with Robin Boundary Conditions

Besides solving a nonlocal Dirichlet problem, we are also interested in solving nonlocal problems that involve a Neumann boundary condition, i.e., find a solution
u
for

\displaystyle\begin{split}-\mathcal{L}u(\mathbf{x})+\kappa(\mathbf{x})u(\mathbf{x})&=f(\mathbf{x})\text{ for }\mathbf{x}\in\Omega,\\
\mathcal{N}u(\mathbf{y})&=g^{N}(\mathbf{y})\text{ for }\mathbf{y}\in\mathcal{I}^{N},\\
u(\mathbf{y})&=g^{D}(\mathbf{y})\text{ for }\mathbf{y}\in\mathcal{I}^{D},\end{split}

(10)

where

\displaystyle\mathcal{I}=\mathcal{I}^{N}\dot{\cup}\mathcal{I}^{D},\ \kappa

\displaystyle:\Omega\rightarrow[\alpha,\beta]\text{ with }0<\alpha\leq\beta<\infty,\ \gamma:\mathbb{R}^{d}\times\mathbb{R}^{d}\rightarrow[0,\infty)\text{ measurable and}

\displaystyle\mathcal{N}(\mathbf{y})

\displaystyle:=\int_{\Omega\cup\mathcal{I}}\left(u(\mathbf{x})-u(\mathbf{y})\right)\gamma(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y})~d\mathbf{y}\text{ for }\mathbf{y}\in\mathcal{I}^{N}.

(11)

Here,
\mathcal{N}
is called nonlocal Neumann operator. For problems of type (
10
), we only assume
\gamma:\mathbb{R}^{d}\times\mathbb{R}^{d}\rightarrow[0,\infty)
to be a measurable and symmetric function.
Furthermore, if
\gamma(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y})=0
for
\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y}\in\mathcal{I}
, the Neumann operator reduces to

\displaystyle\mathcal{N}(\mathbf{y})=\int_{\Omega}\left(u(\mathbf{x})-u(\mathbf{y})\right)\gamma(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y})~d\mathbf{y}\text{ for }\mathbf{y}\in\mathcal{I}^{N}.

(12)

In the case of
\mathcal{I}^{N}=\mathcal{I}
and (
12
) there exists a unique weak solution to problem (
10
) according to
[
30
]
. In the following we show existence of a unique solution to problem (
10
) for the more general case (
10
) by following the approach in
[
30
]
.
Therefore, in order to get a variational formulation for the problem (
10
), we multiply the first two equations of (
10
) by a test function
v:\Omega\cup\mathcal{I}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
, integrate over
\Omega
, or
\mathcal{I}^{N}
respectively, and add up the two equations. Then we get

\displaystyle\left(-\mathcal{L}u+\kappa u,v\right)_{L^{2}(\Omega)}+\left(\mathcal{N}u,v\right)_{L^{2}(\mathcal{I}^{N})}=\left(f,v\right)_{L^{2}(\Omega)}+\left(g^{N},v\right)_{L^{2}(\mathcal{I}^{N})}.

(13)

By assuming
v=0
on
\mathcal{I}^{D}
and by using similar computations as in Section
2
we can reformulate (
13
) as

\displaystyle\begin{split}&\frac{1}{2}\int_{\Omega\cup\mathcal{I}}\int_{\Omega\cup\mathcal{I}}\left(v(\mathbf{x})-v(\mathbf{y})\right)\left(u(\mathbf{x})-u(\mathbf{y})\right)\gamma(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y})~d\mathbf{y}d\mathbf{x}+\int_{\Omega}\kappa(\mathbf{x})v(\mathbf{x})u(\mathbf{x})~d\mathbf{x}\\
&=\int_{\Omega}f(\mathbf{x})v(\mathbf{x})~d\mathbf{x}+\int_{\mathcal{I}^{N}}g^{N}(\mathbf{x})v(\mathbf{x})~d\mathbf{x}.\end{split}

(14)

Next, we define the spaces

\displaystyle\mathcal{V}(\Omega,\mathcal{I}^{N},\mathcal{I}^{D})

\displaystyle:=\{v\in L^{2}(\Omega\cup\mathcal{I}):||v||_{\mathcal{V}(\Omega,\mathcal{I}^{N},\mathcal{I}^{D})}<\infty\},\text{ and}

\displaystyle\mathcal{V}_{c}(\Omega,\mathcal{I}^{N},\mathcal{I}^{D})

\displaystyle:=\{v\in\mathcal{V}(\Omega,\mathcal{I}^{N},\mathcal{I}^{D})\text{ with }v=0\text{ on }\mathcal{I}^{D}\},\text{ where}

\displaystyle||v||^{2}_{\mathcal{V}(\Omega,\mathcal{I}^{N},\mathcal{I}^{D})}

\displaystyle:=\int_{\Omega}v(\mathbf{x})^{2}~d\mathbf{x}+\int_{\Omega\cup\mathcal{I}}\int_{\Omega\cup\mathcal{I}}\left(v(\mathbf{x})-v(\mathbf{y})\right)^{2}\gamma(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y})~d\mathbf{y}d\mathbf{x}.

Moreover, set

\displaystyle\langle u,v\rangle_{\mathcal{V}(\Omega,\mathcal{I}^{N},\mathcal{I}^{D})}:=\int_{\Omega}u(\mathbf{x})v(\mathbf{x})~d\mathbf{x}

\displaystyle+\int_{\Omega\cup\mathcal{I}}\int_{\Omega\cup\mathcal{I}}\left(u(\mathbf{x})-u(\mathbf{y})\right)\left(v(\mathbf{x})-v(\mathbf{y})\right)\gamma(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y})~d\mathbf{y}d\mathbf{x},

which is a semi-inner product for
\mathcal{V}_{c}(\Omega,\mathcal{I}^{N},\mathcal{I}^{D})
. We further define the quotient space

\displaystyle V(\Omega,\mathcal{I}^{N},\mathcal{I}^{D})

\displaystyle:=\mathcal{V}(\Omega,\mathcal{I}^{N},\mathcal{I}^{D})/Z,\text{ and }V_{c}(\Omega,\mathcal{I}^{N},\mathcal{I}^{D}):=\mathcal{V}_{c}(\Omega,\mathcal{I}^{N},\mathcal{I}^{D})/Z,\text{ where}

\displaystyle Z

\displaystyle:=\{v\in\mathcal{V}_{c}(\Omega,\mathcal{I}^{N},\mathcal{I}^{D}):v(\mathbf{x})=0\text{ for a.e. }\mathbf{x}\in\Omega\cup\mathcal{I}\}\text{ and set}

\displaystyle\langle[u],[v]\rangle_{V(\Omega,\mathcal{I}^{N},\mathcal{I}^{D})}

\displaystyle:=\langle u,v\rangle_{\mathcal{V}(\Omega,\mathcal{I}^{N},\mathcal{I}^{D})}\text{ and }||[v]||_{V(\Omega,\mathcal{I}^{N},\mathcal{I}^{D})}=||v||_{\mathcal{V}(\Omega,\mathcal{I}^{N},\mathcal{I}^{D})}.

In the following we write
u
instead of

The first line of (

(15)

Now, we can show the following theorem, which is similar to
[

Theorem 2.4
.

The space
\mathcal{V}_{c}(\Omega,\mathcal{I}^{N},\mathcal{I}^{D})
is complete regarding
||\cdot||_{\mathcal{V}(\Omega,\mathcal{I}^{N},\mathcal{I}^{D})}
. As a consequence,
V_{c}(\Omega,\mathcal{I}^{N},\mathcal{I}^{D})
is a Hilbert space with respect to
||\cdot||_{V(\Omega,\mathcal{I}^{N},\mathcal{I}^{D})}
.

Proof.

Let
(v_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
be a Cauchy sequence in
\mathcal{V}_{c}(\Omega,\mathcal{I}^{N},\mathcal{I}^{D})
. Then, due to the completeness of
L^{2}(\Omega)
, respectively
L^{2}((\Omega\cup\mathcal{I}^{N})\times(\Omega\cup\mathcal{I}^{N}))
, there exist functions
\tilde{v}\in L^{2}(\Omega)
and
w\in L^{2}((\Omega\cup\mathcal{I}^{N})\times(\Omega\cup\mathcal{I}^{N}))
, such that

\displaystyle\lim_{k\rightarrow\infty}\int_{\Omega}\left(v_{k}(\mathbf{x})-\tilde{v}(\mathbf{x})\right)^{2}~d\mathbf{x}=0\text{ and}

\displaystyle\lim_{k\rightarrow\infty}\int_{\Omega\cup\mathcal{I}^{N}}\int_{\Omega\cup\mathcal{I}^{N}}\left((v_{k}(\mathbf{x})-v_{k}(\mathbf{y}))\sqrt{\gamma(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y})}-w(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y})\right)^{2}~d\mathbf{y}d\mathbf{x}=0.

Analogously to
[
30

one can show that there exists a subsequence
\left(v_{n_{l}}\right)_{l\in\mathbb{N}}
of
(v_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
and an extension
v:\Omega\cup\mathcal{I}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
of
\tilde{v}
, i.e.,
v=\tilde{v}
on
\Omega
, with
v=0
on
\mathcal{I}^{D}
and

\displaystyle\lim_{l\rightarrow\infty}v_{n_{l}}(\mathbf{x})=v(\mathbf{x})\text{ for a.e. }\mathbf{x}\in\Omega\cup\mathcal{I}.

Consequently, we derive

\displaystyle w(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y})=\lim_{l\rightarrow\infty}\left(v_{n_{l}}(\mathbf{x})-v_{n_{l}}(\mathbf{y})\right)\sqrt{\gamma(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y})}=\left(v(\mathbf{x})-v(\mathbf{y})\right)\sqrt{\gamma(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y})}\text{ for a.e. }(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y})\in(\Omega\cup\mathcal{I})^{2}.

Therefore,
v\in\mathcal{V}_{c}(\Omega,\mathcal{I}^{N},\mathcal{I}^{D})
and we can conclude

\displaystyle\lim_{l\rightarrow\infty}||v_{n_{l}}-v||_{\mathcal{V}(\Omega,\mathcal{I}^{N},\mathcal{I}^{D})}=0\text{ and }\lim_{n\rightarrow\infty}||v_{n}-v||_{\mathcal{V}(\Omega,\mathcal{I}^{N},\mathcal{I}^{D})}=0.

∎

Set
|||u|||_{V(\Omega,\mathcal{I}^{N},\mathcal{I}^{D})}^{2}:=\mathcal{B}(u,u)+\int\limits_{\Omega}\kappa(\mathbf{x})u^{2}(\mathbf{x})~d\mathbf{x}

u\in V_{c}(\Omega,\mathcal{I}^{N},\mathcal{I}^{D})
since

\displaystyle\min\{\frac{1}{2},\alpha\}||u||^{2}_{V(\Omega,\mathcal{I}^{N},\mathcal{I}^{D})}\leq|||u|||_{V(\Omega,\mathcal{I}^{N},\mathcal{I}^{D})}^{2}\leq\max\{\frac{1}{2},\beta\}||u||^{2}_{V(\Omega,\mathcal{I}^{N},\mathcal{I}^{D})}.

(17)

By applying the Riesz representation theorem we directly get the following conclusion.

Corollary 2.5
.

Given
f\in L^{2}(\Omega)
,
g^{N}\in L^{2}(\mathcal{I}^{N})
and
g^{D}\in V(\Omega,\mathcal{I}^{N},\mathcal{I}^{D})
, there exists a unique function
\tilde{u}\in V_{c}(\Omega,\mathcal{I}^{N},\mathcal{I}^{D})
that satisfies (
16
), i.e.,
u=\tilde{u}+g^{D}
is a weak solution for (
10
).

3
Schwarz Methods

The first domain decomposition method was already formulated by Hermann Amandus Schwarz around 1869 and is now known as the Schwarz alternating method
[
24
,
25
]
. It is an iterative method which was originally used to solve the Laplace equation on a domain by decomposing the domain into two overlapping domains where the Laplace equation could be easily solved on each. In every iteration the former solution regarding the problem on the other domain was used as a boundary condition on the boundary that was part of the other domain.

In this section we formulate the additive and multiplicative Schwarz method for the nonlocal problem (
4
) in the same manner. Therefore, we denote the nonlocal boundary regarding the subdomains
\Omega_{i}
of the decomposition (
1
) by

\displaystyle\mathcal{I}_{i}:=\{\mathbf{y}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}\setminus\Omega_{i}:\int_{\Omega_{i}}\gamma(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y})d\mathbf{x}>0\}.

3.1
Schwarz Methods for Nonlocal Dirichlet Problems

Given the solution
u
of the nonlocal problem (
8
), for
i=1,...,n
we set
u_{i}:=u|_{\Omega_{i}\cup\mathcal{I}_{i}},
and observe that
u_{i}
is a solution of

\displaystyle\textit{Find a weak solution }u_{i}

\displaystyle\in V(\Omega_{i}\cup\mathcal{I}_{i})\textit{ subject to }

\displaystyle\begin{split}-\mathcal{L}u_{i}&=f_{i}\textit{ on }\Omega_{i},\\
u_{i}&=u_{j}\textit{ on }\mathcal{I}_{i}\cap\Omega_{j},\textit{ for }j=1,...,n\textit{ and }j\neq i,\\
u_{i}&=g\textit{ on }\mathcal{I}_{i}\cap\mathcal{I}.\end{split}

(18)

In this case,
u|_{\Omega_{j}\cup\mathcal{I}_{j}}\in V(\Omega_{j}\cup\mathcal{I}_{j})
for
j=1,...,n
and
j\neq i
serve as the boundary data.

On the other hand, as we will show in Section
4.1
, if we have weak solutions
\{u_{i}\}_{i=1}^{n}
for (
18
), the function

u:=\sum_{i=1}^{n}u_{i}\chi_{\Omega_{i}}+g\chi_{\mathcal{I}}

solves the nonlocal problem (
4
).

Remark 3.1
.

Since we derive (
18
) from (
4
) and (
8
), respectively, the boundary data of the problem on
\Omega_{i}
is an element of
V(\Omega_{i}\cup\mathcal{I}_{i})
and can naturally be extended to a function of
V(\Omega\cup\mathcal{I})
. Therefore, we expand the boundary
\mathcal{I}_{i}
to
\hat{\mathcal{I}}_{i}:=(\Omega\setminus\Omega_{i})\cup\mathcal{I}
and restrict ourselves to boundary data in
V(\Omega_{i}\cup\hat{\mathcal{I}}_{i})
. Consequently, we will make use of the spaces
V(\Omega_{i}\cup\hat{\mathcal{I}}_{i})=V(\Omega\cup\mathcal{I})
and
V_{c}(\Omega_{i}\cup\hat{\mathcal{I}}_{i})=\{u\in V(\Omega\cup\mathcal{I}):u=0\text{ on }\hat{\mathcal{I}}_{i}=\left(\Omega\setminus\Omega_{i}\right)\cup\mathcal{I}\}
in order to formulate a weak formulation for (
18
).

Thus, for
v_{i}\in V_{c}(\Omega_{i}\cup\hat{\mathcal{I}}_{i})
and
u\in V(\Omega\cup\mathcal{I})
the bilinear form
A
reduces to the corresponding bilinear operator that only contains integrals over
\left(\Omega_{i}\cup\mathcal{I}_{i}\right)\times\left(\Omega_{i}\cup\mathcal{I}_{i}\right)
as follows

\displaystyle A(u,v_{i})=

\displaystyle\sum_{l,j=1}^{n}\frac{1}{2}\int_{\Omega_{l}}\int_{\Omega_{j}}\left(u(\mathbf{x})-u(\mathbf{y})\right)\left(v_{i}(\mathbf{x})-v_{i}(\mathbf{y})\right)\gamma(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y})~d\mathbf{y}d\mathbf{x}

\displaystyle+\sum_{j=1}^{n}\int_{\Omega_{j}}u(\mathbf{x})v_{i}(\mathbf{x})\int_{\mathcal{I}}\gamma(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y})~d\mathbf{y}d\mathbf{x}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\int_{\Omega_{i}}\int_{\Omega_{i}}\left(u(\mathbf{x})-u(\mathbf{y})\right)\left(v_{i}(\mathbf{x})-v_{i}(\mathbf{y})\right)\gamma(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y})~d\mathbf{y}d\mathbf{x}

\displaystyle+\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}j=1\\
j\neq i\end{subarray}}^{n}\int_{\Omega_{i}}\int_{\Omega_{j}}v_{i}(\mathbf{x})\left(u(\mathbf{x})-u(\mathbf{y})\right)\gamma(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y})~d\mathbf{y}d\mathbf{x}+\int_{\Omega_{i}}u(\mathbf{x})v_{i}(\mathbf{x})\int_{\mathcal{I}}\gamma(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y})~d\mathbf{y}d\mathbf{x},

where we used the symmetry of
\gamma
in the last step. Analogously,
F
can in this case be written as an operator that only integrates over
\Omega_{i}
, i.e.,

\displaystyle F(v_{i})=

\displaystyle\int_{\Omega}fv_{i}~d\mathbf{x}=\int_{\Omega_{i}}f_{i}v_{i}~d\mathbf{x}.

Then, we define the variational formulation for the subproblem on
\Omega_{i}
as:

\displaystyle\begin{split}\textit{Given }f\in L^{2}(\Omega)\textit{ and }&\textit{boundary data }g\in V(\Omega_{i}\cup\hat{\mathcal{I}}_{i})=V(\Omega\cup\mathcal{I}),\\
\textit{ find }u_{i}\in V(\Omega_{i}\cup\hat{\mathcal{I}}_{i})&=V(\Omega\cup\mathcal{I})\textit{ such that }\\
A(u_{i},v_{i})&=F(v_{i})\textit{ for all }v_{i}\in V_{c}(\Omega_{i}\cup\hat{\mathcal{I}}_{i}),\\
u_{i}-g&\in V_{c}(\Omega_{i}\cup\hat{\mathcal{I}}_{i}).\end{split}

(19)

In the following we show two popular Schwarz methods to solve the problem (
18
) in an iterative manner. In both cases we start with initial guesses
\{u_{i}^{0}\}_{i=1}^{n}
.
In the multiplicative Schwarz method, as shown in Algorithm

, we use the most recent solutions in every iteration as the boundary data, i.e., for the subproblem
i
the solutions
u_{j}^{k+1}
for
0\leq j<i
from the current iteration are employed.

Another approach is to only use the solutions of the former outer iteration

instead of utilizing

for the subproblem

Remark 3.2
.

If we decompose

in only two domains

and

the additive Schwarz method basically consists of two multiplicative Schwarz methods, where one starts by solving the subproblem on

and the second one begins by computing the solution on

.
Therefore, the application of the additive Schwarz methods can only be faster, if the domain

In Chapter

3.2

In this section we apply the Schwarz Method on a nonlocal problem with Neumann boundary conditions as introduced in Chapter
2.2
For ease of presentation, we assume
\mathcal{I}^{N}=\mathcal{I}
(and consequently
\mathcal{I}^{D}=\emptyset
). The case, where

with

, can be handled analogously. Thus, we consider the following problem.

\displaystyle\begin{split}\textit{Find a weak solution }u&\in V(\Omega,\mathcal{I},\emptyset)\textit{ to}\\

(20)

where
\gamma(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y})=0
for

, i.e., we use the nonlocal Neumann operator (

Moreover, let
\{\mathcal{I}_{i}^{N}\}_{i=1}^{n}
be a partition of the nonlocal boundary

such that

\displaystyle\overline{\mathcal{I}}=\left(\bigcup_{i=1}^{n}\overline{\mathcal{I}}_{i}^{N}\right),~\mathcal{I}_{i}^{N}\cap\mathcal{I}_{j}^{N}=\emptyset\text{ for }i,j=1,...,n,~i\neq j\text{ and}

\displaystyle\mathcal{I}^{N}_{j}=\emptyset\textit{ for }j\in\mathbb{S}.

Thus, the partition

can be partially in another subdomain

can contain parts of the nonlocal Dirichlet Boundary and the nonlocal Neumann operator is in this case of type (

Again, if we have a weak solutions

Again, analogously to the nonlocal Dirichlet case as described in Remark

corresponding to the subproblem on

to the domain

j\neq i\end{subarray}}^{n}\left(\Omega_{j}\cup\mathcal{I}_{j}^{N}\right)=\left(\Omega\cup\mathcal{I}\right)\setminus\left(\Omega_{i}\cup\mathcal{I}_{i}^{N}\right)}
. Then,

and, due to the symmetry of

\displaystyle\mathcal{B}(u_{i},v_{i})+\int_{\Omega}\kappa(\mathbf{x})u_{i}(\mathbf{x})v_{i}(\mathbf{x})~d\mathbf{x}=

\displaystyle+\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}j=1\\
j\neq i\end{subarray}}^{n}\int_{\Omega_{i}\cup\mathcal{I}_{i}^{N}}\int_{\Omega_{j}\cup\mathcal{I}_{j}^{N}}v_{i}(\mathbf{x})\left(u_{i}(\mathbf{x})-u_{i}(\mathbf{y})\right)\gamma(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y})~d\mathbf{y}d\mathbf{x}

\displaystyle\textit{Given }f\in L^{2}(\Omega),g^{N}\in L^{2}(\mathcal{I}^{N})\textit{ and }g^{D}\in V(\Omega_{i},\mathcal{I}_{i}^{N}

\displaystyle,\hat{\mathcal{I}}_{i}),\textit{ find }u_{i}\in V(\Omega_{i},\mathcal{I}_{i}^{N},\hat{\mathcal{I}}_{i})\textit{ with }

4
Well-posedness of the Multiplicative Schwarz Method

In this section we show that the multiplicative Schwarz methods as described in Chapters

and

converge. Therefore we recall the first part of
[

, Theorem I.2]
:

Theorem 4.1
.

Given closed subspaces
\{\mathbb{V}_{i}\}_{i=1}^{n}
of a Hilbert space
\mathbb{V}:=\overline{\oplus_{i=1}^{n}\mathbb{V}_{i}}
, orthogonal projections
\mathcal{P}_{i}:\mathbb{V}\rightarrow\mathbb{V}
onto the subspace
\mathbb{V}_{i}
and a sequence
\left(u^{l}\right)_{l=1}^{\infty}
, where
\mathcal{P}_{i}(u^{l-1})=u^{l}
for
l=km+i
given some data
u^{0}
. Then
u^{l}
converges to a unique
u\in\mathbb{V}
.

The version of Theorem
4.1
for two domains (see
[
19
, Theorem I.1]
) has also been used to show the convergence of the multiplicative Schwarz method for an energy-based Local-to-Nonlocal coupling (see
[
1
]
).

Remark 4.2
.

The second part of
[
19
, Theorem I.2]
states that if
\mathbb{V}=\oplus_{i=1}^{n}\mathbb{V}_{i}
holds, then there exists a constant
\epsilon\in(0,1)
such that

\displaystyle||u^{l}-u||_{\mathbb{V}}\leq\epsilon^{l}||u^{0}-u||_{\mathbb{V}}.

Linear convergence of the Schwarz methods in the nonlocal framework of Chapter
3
can also be observed in the numerical experiments in Chapter

and is proven in the discrete case(see Remark

). However, the proof of the assumption
\mathbb{V}=\oplus_{i=1}^{n}\mathbb{V}_{i}
is left to future work.

4.1
Well-posedness of the Multiplicative Schwarz Method for Nonlocal Dirichlet Problems

Here in this subsection, we assume the kernel
\gamma
to be as described in Remark

, i.e.,
\gamma
fulfills conditions (K1) and (K2) and is either a singular symmetric or an integrable symmetric kernel.
As seen in (
9
) we only need to consider the case

on
\mathcal{I}
since the case
g\neq 0
on
\mathcal{I}
is a direct consequence.
Given Algorithm

we can define for every subproblem in (

\displaystyle\mathcal{S}^{f}_{i}:\left(V_{c}(\Omega\cup\mathcal{I}),|\cdot|_{V(\Omega\cup\mathcal{I})}\right)\rightarrow\left(V_{c}(\Omega\cup\mathcal{I}),|\cdot|_{V(\Omega\cup\mathcal{I})}\right),

and

is the weak solution of the subproblem on
\Omega_{i}
as described in (

) given

and
f
, i.e.,
\mathcal{S}_{i}
maps the boundary data
g
onto the solution
u_{i}
on
\Omega_{i}
.

We start by showing the convergence for
f\equiv 0
.
In this case, we set
\mathcal{S}_{i}:=\mathcal{S}^{f}_{i}
.

Lemma 4.3
.

The operator
\mathcal{S}_{i}
is linear and bounded as follows

\displaystyle||u_{i}||_{V(\Omega\cup\mathcal{I})}\leq 2|g|_{V(\Omega\cup\mathcal{I})}\text{ for }u_{i}=\mathcal{S}_{i}(g).

Proof.

Since the linearity of
\mathcal{S}_{i}
is trivially true, we only have to proof the boundedness of
\mathcal{S}_{i}
. As a consequence of
f\equiv 0
and (
9
) in the second step of the following calculation and by applying Cauchy-Schwarz in the third step we derive

\displaystyle|u_{i}-g|_{V(\Omega\cup\mathcal{I})}^{2}=A(u_{i}-g,u_{i}-g)\leq|A(g,u_{i}-g)|

\displaystyle\leq\sqrt{A(g,g)}\sqrt{A(u_{i}-g,u_{i}-g)}=|g|_{V(\Omega\cup\mathcal{I})}|u_{i}-g|_{V(\Omega\cup\mathcal{I})}\text{ and therefore }|u_{i}-g|_{V(\Omega\cup\mathcal{I})}\leq|g|_{V(\Omega\cup\mathcal{I})}.

Then, we conclude

\displaystyle|u_{i}|_{V(\Omega\cup\mathcal{I})}\leq|u_{i}-g|_{V(\Omega\cup\mathcal{I})}+|g|_{V(\Omega\cup\mathcal{I})}\leq 2|g|_{V(\Omega\cup\mathcal{I})}.

∎

Further, we define spaces

\displaystyle\mathbb{V}_{i}(\Omega\cup\mathcal{I}):=\{u_{i}\in V_{c}(\Omega\cup\mathcal{I}):u_{i}=\mathcal{S}_{i}(u_{i})\}~~\text{ and }~~\mathbb{V}(\Omega\cup\mathcal{I}):=\overline{\oplus_{i=1}^{n}\mathbb{V}_{i}(\Omega\cup\mathcal{I})},

(23)

that is equipped with the the inner product

\displaystyle\langle u,v\rangle_{\mathbb{V}(\Omega\cup\mathcal{I})}:=A\left(u,v\right)\text{ and the norm }||u||_{\mathbb{V}(\Omega\cup\mathcal{I})}:=\sqrt{\langle u,u\rangle}_{\mathbb{V}(\Omega\cup\mathcal{I})}.

Then
\langle\cdot,\cdot\rangle_{\mathbb{V}(\Omega\cup\mathcal{I})}
is linear in the first argument, symmetric and positive-definite, see Remark
2.2
.
Here, every
u_{i}\in\mathbb{V}_{i}(\Omega\cup\mathcal{I})
is a solution to (
19
) on
\Omega_{i}
given
u_{i}\in V(\Omega_{i}\cup\hat{\mathcal{I}}_{i})
as boundary data on
\hat{\mathcal{I}}_{i}
and
f\equiv 0
.

Lemma 4.4
.

Let the spaces
\mathbb{V}_{i}(\Omega\cup\mathcal{I})
and
\mathbb{V}(\Omega\cup\mathcal{I})
be as defined in (
23
) for
i=1,...,n
. Then, the space

\mathbb{V}_{i}(\Omega\cup\mathcal{I})
is a closed subspace for every
i=1,...,n
. Moreover,
\mathbb{V}(\Omega\cup\mathcal{I})
is a closed subspace of
V_{c}(\Omega\cup\mathcal{I})
and therefore a Hilbert space.

Proof.

1.

\mathbb{V}_{i}(\Omega\cup\mathcal{I})
is closed under linear combinations: Given
u_{i},\psi_{i}\in\mathbb{V}_{i}(\Omega\cup\mathcal{I})
and
\lambda\in\mathbb{R}
, then
\lambda u_{i}+\psi_{i}\in V_{c}(\Omega\cup\mathcal{I})
and we get

\displaystyle A(\lambda u_{i}+\psi_{i},v_{i})

\displaystyle=\lambda A(u_{i},v_{i})+A(\psi_{i},v_{i})=0\text{ for all }v_{i}\in V_{c}(\Omega_{i}\cup\hat{\mathcal{I}}_{i})

\displaystyle\Rightarrow\lambda u_{i}+\psi_{i}\in\mathbb{V}_{i}(\Omega\cup\mathcal{I}).

2.

\mathbb{V}_{i}(\Omega\cup\mathcal{I})
is a closed subspace regarding
||\cdot||_{\mathbb{V}(\Omega\cup\mathcal{I})}
: Given a sequence
\{u_{i}^{m}\}_{m=1}^{\infty}
such that
u_{i}^{m}\in\mathbb{V}_{i}(\Omega\cup\mathcal{I})
for all
m\in\mathbb{N}
and
\{u_{i}^{m}\}_{m=1}^{\infty}
is a Cauchy sequence regarding
||\cdot||_{\mathbb{V}(\Omega\cup\mathcal{I})}
.
Then, there exists a unique function
u_{i}\in\mathbb{V}(\Omega\cup\mathcal{I})
with
\lim\limits_{m\rightarrow\infty}|u_{i}^{m}-u_{i}|_{V(\Omega\cup\mathcal{I})}=0
, because
\left(u_{i}^{m}\right)_{m=1}^{\infty}
is a Cauchy sequence in the Hilbert space
\left(V_{c}(\Omega\cup\mathcal{I}),|\cdot|_{V(\Omega\cup\mathcal{I})}\right)
. Consequently, the continuity of
\mathcal{S}_{i}
implies
u_{i}\in\mathbb{V}_{i}(\Omega\cup\mathcal{I})
since

\displaystyle|u_{i}-\mathcal{S}_{i}(u_{i})|_{V_{c}(\Omega\cup\mathcal{I})}

\displaystyle\leq|u_{i}-u_{i}^{m}|_{V(\Omega\cup\mathcal{I})}+|\mathcal{S}_{i}(u_{i}^{m})-\mathcal{S}_{i}(u_{i})|_{V(\Omega\cup\mathcal{I})}

\displaystyle\leq 3|u_{i}-u_{i}^{m}|_{V(\Omega\cup\mathcal{I})}\rightarrow 0\quad\text{for }m\rightarrow\infty.

5.1
Finite Element Approximation

In order to solve problem (
8
) we use the finite element method. Therefore we need a triangulation of finite (polyhedral) elements
\mathcal{T}^{h}=\{\mathcal{E}_{j}\}_{j=1}^{J}
with vertices
\{\mathbf{x}_{k}\}_{k=1}^{K}

for

and
\mathbf{x}_{k}\in\mathcal{I}
for

Furthermore, we utilize continuous piecewise linear basis functions
\{\phi_{k}\}_{k=1}^{K}
that satisfy
\phi_{k}(\mathbf{x}_{l})=\delta_{kl}

. Moreover, we define finite dimensional function spaces as

\displaystyle V^{h}=span\{\phi_{k}:k=1,...,K\}\text{ and }V_{c}^{h}=span\{\phi_{k}:k=1,...,K_{\Omega}\}.

Thus, we can project

to a function

\displaystyle\mathbf{u}_{i}^{h}:=u(\mathbf{x}_{i}),\ \text{for all }i=1,...,K,

\displaystyle\mathbf{u}^{h}(\mathbf{x})=\sum_{i=1}^{K}\mathbf{u}_{i}^{h}\phi_{i}(\mathbf{x}).

With the projection
\mathbf{g}^{h}=\sum_{j=K_{\Omega}+1}^{K}\mathbf{g}_{i}\phi_{i}
of the boundary data

Find

\displaystyle A(\mathbf{u}_{\Omega}^{h},\phi_{i})

\displaystyle=F(\phi_{i})

\displaystyle\text{for all }i=1,...,K_{\Omega},

\displaystyle\Leftrightarrow\sum_{k=1}^{K_{\Omega}}A(\phi_{k},\phi_{i})\mathbf{u}_{k}^{h}

\displaystyle=\mathbf{f}_{i}^{h}-\sum_{j=K_{\Omega}+1}^{K}A(\phi_{j},\phi_{i})\mathbf{g}_{j}^{h},

\displaystyle\text{for all }i=1,...,K_{\Omega},

(26)

where

\displaystyle\mathbf{f}=\left(\mathbf{f}_{i}^{h}\right)_{1\leq i\leq K_{\Omega}},\mathbf{A}=\left(\mathbf{a}_{ij}\right)_{1\leq i,j\leq K_{\Omega}},\text{ and }\mathbf{A}_{\mathcal{I}}=\left(\mathbf{a}_{ij}\right)_{1\leq i\leq K_{\Omega};K_{\Omega}+1\leq j\leq K},

\displaystyle\mathbf{A}\mathbf{u}^{h}=\mathbf{f}-\mathbf{A}_{I}\mathbf{g}^{h}.

(27)

6

In this section we show that Schwarz methods for nonlocal Dirichlet problems, where we employ nonlocal diffusion operators, can be interpreted as a Nonlocal-to-Nonlocal coupling method, that satisfies linear, quadratic and cubic patch tests.
Analogously to
[
10
, Definition 1 and 2]
, we define the linear and higher-order patch test for the coupling of two nonlocal operators as follows:

Definition 6.1
.

Given two nonlocal operators
-\mathcal{L}_{1}
and
-\mathcal{L}_{2}
and a linear function

u^{*}(\mathbf{x}):=c_{0}+c_{1}\mathbf{x}
, where
c_{0},c_{1}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
and
u^{*}
is a solution of

\displaystyle-\mathcal{L}_{i}u=0\text{ on }\Omega,\quad u(\mathbf{x})=c_{0}+c_{1}\mathbf{x}\text{ on }\mathcal{I}\text{ for }i=1,2.

(29)

Then, a coupling method passes the linear patch test, if
u^{*}
is also a solution of the coupled problem with the same boundary condition.

Definition 6.2
.

Given two nonlocal operators
-\mathcal{L}_{1}
and
-\mathcal{L}_{2}
, and a polynomial
u^{*}(\mathbf{x}):=\sum_{|\boldsymbol{\alpha}|=0}^{p}c_{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}\mathbf{x}^{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}
with degree
p\in\{2,3\}
, multi-indices
\boldsymbol{\alpha}\in\mathbb{N}_{0}^{d}
and
c_{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}\in\mathbb{R}
. Additionally, suppose that
u^{*}
is a solution of

\displaystyle-\mathcal{L}_{i}u=f^{poly}\text{ on }\Omega,\quad u(\mathbf{x})=\sum_{|\boldsymbol{\alpha}|=0}^{p}c_{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}\mathbf{x}^{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}\text{ on }\mathcal{I}\text{ for }i=1,2,

(30)

where
f^{poly}
is a polynomial with degree
p-2
. Then, a coupling method passes the quadratic(cubic) patch test, if
p=2
(
p=3
) and
u^{*}
is also a solution of the coupled problem with the same boundary condition.

Given a solution
u^{*}
of a nonlocal Dirichlet problem (
29
) or (
30
), and the right-hand side
f^{poly}
of the corresponding linear, quadratic or cubic patch test, we can directly follow that
u^{*}
is also the solution to the Schwarz formulation (
18
) since the Dirichlet boundary conditions on every
\mathcal{I}_{i}
holds and

\displaystyle-\mathcal{L}_{i}u^{*}=f^{poly}\text{ on }\Omega_{i}\text{ for }i=1,...,n.

Therefore the patch test is trivially fulfilled. Thus, we can also interpret the Schwarz method as a coupling method. As we will demonstrate in Section
7
, we can, e.g., couple a singular and a constant kernel.

Next, we present a class of kernels where the corresponding nonlocal operator is equivalent to the Laplace operator
-\Delta u(\mathbf{x}):=\sum_{i=1}^{d}\partial_{i}^{2}u(\mathbf{x})
in case of polynomials up to degree three
p(\mathbf{x})=\sum_{|\boldsymbol{\alpha}|=0}^{3}c_{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}\mathbf{x}^{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}
.
Hence, given a radially symmetric kernel
\gamma_{\delta}(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y}):=\gamma_{\delta}(|\mathbf{y}-\mathbf{x}|)\chi_{B_{\delta}(\mathbf{x})}(\mathbf{y})
that satisfies

\displaystyle\int_{B_{\delta}(0)}\mathbf{z}_{i}^{2}\gamma_{\delta}(|\mathbf{z}|)~d\mathbf{z}=2,

(31)

we derive for such polynomials
p
that

\displaystyle-\mathcal{L}p(\mathbf{x})

\displaystyle=\int_{B_{\delta}(\mathbf{x})}\left(p(\mathbf{x})-p(\mathbf{y})\right)\gamma_{\delta}(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y})~d\mathbf{y}=\int_{B_{\delta}(0)}\left(p(\mathbf{x})-p(\mathbf{x}+\mathbf{z})\right)\gamma_{\delta}(|\mathbf{z}|)~d\mathbf{y}

\displaystyle=-\int_{B_{\delta}(0)}\sum_{\boldsymbol{\alpha}=1}^{3}\frac{D^{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}p(\mathbf{x})}{|\boldsymbol{\alpha}|!}\mathbf{z}^{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}\gamma_{\delta}(|\mathbf{z}|)~d\mathbf{z}=-\int_{B_{\delta}(0)}\frac{1}{2}\sum_{i=1}^{d}\partial_{i}^{2}p(\mathbf{x})\mathbf{z}_{i}^{2}\gamma_{\delta}(|\mathbf{z}|)~d\mathbf{z}=-\Delta p(\mathbf{x}),

where we used in the last step that the function
\mathbf{z}^{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}
is an odd function regarding one
x_{i}
-axis, if
|\boldsymbol{\alpha}|
is an odd number. Furthermore, if
\boldsymbol{\alpha}=(\boldsymbol{\alpha}_{1},...,\boldsymbol{\alpha}_{d})
with
|\boldsymbol{\alpha}|=2
,
\boldsymbol{\alpha}_{i}=\boldsymbol{\alpha}_{j}=1
and
i\neq j
, the function
\mathbf{z}^{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}=\mathbf{z}_{i}\mathbf{z}_{j}
is an odd function regarding the
x_{j}
-axis. Since
B_{\delta}(0)
is a symmetric domain regarding every
x_{i}
-axis, the integrals, in which these functions
\mathbf{z}^{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}
appear, vanish. In Section
7.3
we use different kernels that satisfy the conditions (
31
) in a numerical example, where the nonlocal Schwarz formulation passes the linear patch test.

8
Conclusion

We have shown in this paper how the multiplicative and additive Schwarz method can be applied to nonlocal Dirichlet problems and how they can be utilized to solve nonlocal problems with Neumann boundary condition. In the first case we showed the convergence of the multiplicative version of the Schwarz algorithm for two widely used classes of symmetric kernels and in the second case we only needed symmetric kernels to prove that the multiplicative Schwarz approach converges.
Additionally, coupling nonlocal operators with the Schwarz method fulfills trivially nonlocal patch tests. In the last section we provided examples for all discussed problems in this work. Additionally, we observed in the patch test experiments in Chapter
7.3
, that the Schwarz approach can also work for nonsymmetric kernels in practice. Further, we investigated preconditioned GMRES variants that resulted from the multiplicative or additive Schwarz algorithm in the finite element setting, where we noticed, that especially the block-Gauß-Seidel preconditioned GMRES version only needs a small amount of iterations.
