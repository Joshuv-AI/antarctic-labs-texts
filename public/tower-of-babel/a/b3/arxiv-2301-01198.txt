New applications of the Mellin transform to automorphic L L –functions
Laurent Clozel

Abstract




Introduction

In an earlier paper
[
2
]
, and its Appendix, written with Peter Sarnak, we have obtained universal lower bounds on certain quadratic integrals of automorphic
L
–functions. For instance, if
\pi
is a cuspidal unitary representation of
GL(m,\mathbb{A}_{\mathbb{Q}})
, and
L(s)=L(s,\pi)
:

(0.1)

\int_{-\infty}^{+\infty}\Big|\frac{L(1/2+it)}{1/2+it}\Big|^{2}dt>\frac{\pi}{2}.

Cf
[
2
, Theorem D]
. In this article, we follow some questions that arose naturally in this context.

The first one was suggested by a remark of Sarnak, according to which we cannot have such universal bounds for short intervals, e.g. for the integral on
[-1,1]
in (0.1); at least such cannot be obtained if
m
is allowed to vary. See the Introduction to
[
2
]
, § 2.

The argument does not succeed if
m
is fixed; neither did the author succeed in finding, for
m
fixed, an absolute bound on a short interval. This remains an interesting problem.

In Chapter 2, we obtain a universal lower bound, for
m
fixed, for the integral on an interval
[-A\log C,A\log C]
where
C
is the analytic conductor. Even then, we could not obtain a fixed lower bound. Rather, we prove that this integral is larger than
c(\log C)^{-1/2}
where
c>0
is an absolute constant. This may not be optimal, but it is commensurable with the Lindelöf conjecture. (We hope that an analytic number theorist familiar with the use of the Mellin transform, as in the proof of the approximate functional equation, will be able substantially to improve this result.) Moreover, we can shift the ordinate and obtain a general result on an interval
[X-T,X+T]
where
X
is arbitrary and
T
is of the order of
\log X
. See Theorem 2.2, Theorem 2.3. The proof relies on a theorem of Molteni
[
14
]
, further improved by Xiannan Li
[
12
]
.

In Chapter 3, we follow a lead from
[
2
, §3.3]
. There we considered Vinogradov’s Conjecture on the order of the first quadratic non–residue and we showed that it followed directly, via the Mellin transform, from the Lindelöf conjecture (including in the
q
–aspect) for the associated Dirichlet
L
–function. (This may have been well–known to experts).

A similar problem is, given a non trivial representation
\rho
of a Galois group
\mathrm{Gal}(E/F)
of number fields, to determine “the” first prime
\mathfrak{p}
of
F
— i.e. one of smallest norm — where
\rho
is unramified and
\rho(\mathrm{Frob}_{\mathfrak{p}})\not=1
,
\mathrm{Frob}_{\mathfrak{p}}
being a Frobenius element. The proof of
[
2
]
extends naturally. We have treated first the case where
\rho
is a one–dimensional character, in Chapter 3, § 1–3. See Theorem 3.1. When
\rho
is non–Abelian, the proof is more delicate and leads us to introduce the unramified variant
\mathcal{L}(s,\rho)
of the Artin
L
–function
L(s,\rho)
. See Theorem 3.2.

In §3.6, we show that our estimate is, in some cases, better that the known unconditional estimates. (The relevant result here is the recent one of Zaman
[
21
]
) However we have to assume that
F
is a Galois extension of
\mathbb{Q}
and, more crucially, that
L(s,\rho)
is holomorphic. Note that we do not have to assume that
\rho
is associated to an automorphic representation.

In Chapter 4, we have reviewed some odds and ends concerning the results and arguments of
[
2
]
. In particular we point out that they apply to Rankin
L
–functions — not only to the standard
L
–functions of
[
2
]
; and we develop the remarks made in
[
2
]
about the relation between estimates on the summation function
A_{0}(x)
— see § 2.1— and subconvexity for
L(s,\pi)
.

Finally, in Chapter 1, we have recalled some “well–known” results concerning the growth of
L
–functions in the critical strip, in term of the analytic conductor. Fortunately we could rely on the very clear exposition of Harcos
[
5
]
. For the convenience of the reader, we also collect some formulas for special functions.

We use notation standard in analytic number theory, in particular Landau’s symbols
\ll,O(\ )
, indexed when we want to specify the dependence of the implicit constants. We use
f\prec g
(
f,g
positive functions) for
f/g\rightarrow 0
. We use
A
for an absolute constant (in the context), not always the same in different occurrences.

In this preliminary chapter we have grouped together the majorations and formulas which will be used in the text, and crucially in using an “absolute” version, due to Molteni, of the Friedlander–Iwanieč estimate. We briefly recall our set–up, which is that of

\pi

\pi=\pi_{1}\times\pi_{2}\times\cdots\times\pi_{r}
(parabolic induction) of cuspidal representations
\pi_{i}
of
GL(m_{i},\mathbb{A})
,

L(s,\pi)=\prod_{i=1}^{r}L(s,\pi_{i}).

is the standard
L
–function. The completed
L

\Lambda(s,\pi)=D^{s/2}\ L(s,\pi_{\infty})\ L(s,\pi),

where

\geq 1

\Lambda(s,\pi)=\varepsilon(\pi)\Lambda(1-s,\tilde{\pi}).

For details and a review of the other
L

[

We do not assume, as we did in
[

\pi_{\infty}

L(1-s,\pi)=\varepsilon(\pi)\gamma(s)L(s,\tilde{\pi})

and

\Gamma(s,\pi_{\infty})=\prod_{j=1}^{m}\Gamma\Big(\frac{s+c_{j}}{2}\Big)

Re(c_{j})\geq\frac{1}{m^{2}+1}-\frac{1}{2}\qquad\cite[cite]{[\@@bibref{}{LRS}{}{}]}.

Following Iwanieč and Sarnak, we associate to

and its analytic conductor

(1.2)

C=C(\pi)=D(\pi)\prod_{j=1}^{m}(2+|c_{j}|)

as well as

C(s)=C(\pi,s)=C(\pi)\ (1+|s|)^{m}.

(There is a finer version of

; cf
[

for a discussion of this.)
We will need uniform estimates, in terms of these data, for
\gamma(s)
and

.

(Harcos) For
\sigma>\frac{1}{m}-\frac{1}{m^{2}+1}
,

1.3

Here we insert a few remarks on the analytic conductor. It is not true that, for
A>0
fixed, there exist a finite number of representations
\pi
such that
C(\pi)\leq A
. Indeed, if
m=1
, and
\pi=\chi
is a Dirichlet character of conductor
D
, twisted by
|\,|^{a}
,
(a\in i\mathbb{R})
, the analytic conductor is
D(2+|a|)
. This phenomenon persists in higher rank
m
, but is essentially due to the center
GL(1)
of
GL(m)
.

Proposition 1.2
.

For any
A>0
, there exist a finite number of cuspidal representations
\pi
of
GL(m,\mathbb{A})
whose central character
\omega
verifies
\omega|_{\mathbb{R}_{+}^{\times}}=1
and such that
C(\pi)<A
.

Since
D(\pi)<C(\pi)
there is a finite number of possibilities for
D(\pi)
. Let
S
denote the connected component of 1 in the center
Z(\mathbb{R})\cong\mathbb{R}^{\times}
of
GL(m,\mathbb{R})
. Consider the space
\mathcal{L}=L_{cusp}^{2}(S\,GL(m,\mathbb{Q})\backslash GL(m,\mathbb{A}))
of
L^{2}
–cusp forms invariant by
S
. The Hecke algebra
\bigotimes\limits_{v}\mathcal{H}_{v}
(where
\mathcal{H}_{\infty}=C_{c}^{\infty}(GL(n,\mathbb{R}))
and, for
v=p
finite,
\mathcal{H}_{p}
is the algebra of compactly supported smooth functions) acts on
L_{cusp}^{2}
by right translations, and it is well–known that this action is trace–class. Fix
D\geq 1
, and let
K(D)\subset GL(m,\mathbb{A}_{f})
be the congruence subgroup defined by Jacquet, Piaterskii-Shapiro and Shalika
[
8
, §5, Théorème]
, and
\varphi_{D}\in\bigotimes\limits_{p}\mathcal{H}_{p}
its characteristic function. Then
\varphi_{D}
projects
\mathcal{L}
onto its subspace composed of the
K(D)
–invariants in the cuspidal representations
\pi
(with
\omega_{\pi}|_{\mathbb{R}_{+}^{\times}}=1)
. This is an infinite sum of representations
\rho
of
GL(m,\mathbb{R})
; a function
\varphi_{\infty}\in C_{c}^{\infty}(GL(m,\mathbb{R}))
acts on it by a trace–class operator. In particular, any compact subset of the unitary dual of
GL(m,\mathbb{R})
contains only a finite number of such representations. But the set of representations
\rho
such that
C_{\infty}(\rho)=\prod(2+|c_{j}|)\leq A
is compact. (See
[
2
, § 2.2]
for the relation between the
c_{j}
and the Langlands parameters of
\pi_{\infty}
.)

Assume again
\pi
cuspidal. For
a\in i\,\mathbb{R}
, let
\pi[a]=\pi\otimes|\det|^{a}
. We can choose
a
such that
\pi[a]
has trivial central character on
\mathbb{R}_{+}^{\times}
. In view of this, it is useful to know the relation between
C(\pi)
and
C(\pi[a])
.

Again we refer to
[
2
, § 2.2]
. The representation
\pi_{\infty}
is associated to a sum of real characters

\nu(x)=(sgnx)^{\varepsilon}|x|^{c}\qquad(x\in\mathbb{R}_{+}^{\times});\varepsilon=0,1)

and of characters of
\mathbb{C}^{\times}
:

\mu(z)=z^{p}(\bar{z})^{q},\ p-q\in\mathbb{Z}.

(The result of Luo–Rudnick–Sarnak
[
13
]
implies simple bounds on
Re(c)
,
Re(p+q)
.)

The datum
c_{j}
associated to
\nu
is
c+\varepsilon
; the data
c_{j}
associated to
\mu
are

\begin{array}[]{lll}(q,q+1)&\mathrm{if}&Re(p-q)<0\\
(p,p+1)&\mathrm{if}&Re(p-q)>0.\end{array}

If we twist by
|\ |^{a}
, the
c_{j}
are transformed into
c_{j}+a
. Therefore

(1.3)

C(\pi[a])\leq C(\pi)(1+|a|)^{m}.

We also remark that if
C(\pi)=2^{m}
,
D(\pi)=1
and the

are equal to 0. Under this assumption, the number of
\pi
is therefore finite.

Finally, it is of interest to know when
\omega|_{\mathbb{R}_{+}^{\times}}=1
in terms of the
c_{j}
. However the relation is not direct. The real characters
\nu_{j}
contribute
c_{j}=c+\varepsilon
, where
\nu_{j}(x)=x^{c}
,
x>0
;
A
complex character
\mu(z)
determines a representation
\pi_{2}
of
GL(2,\mathbb{R})
of central character
x^{p+q}

(x>0)
, and
c_{j}
,
c_{j^{\prime}}
equal to
(q,q+1)
or
(p,p+1)
. One checks that
c_{j}+c_{j^{\prime}}=p+q+n

is the ramification of
\pi_{2}
. Therefore the central character of
\pi_{\infty}
, on
\mathbb{R}_{+}^{\times}
, is
x^{A}
,

A=\sum c_{j}-Ram(\pi_{\infty}),

where

Ram(\pi_{\infty})=\sum\varepsilon+\sum n

is the ramification of
\pi_{\infty}
. To obtain a representation with
\omega|_{\mathbb{R}_{+}^{\times}}=1
, we have to twist by
|\ det|^{-A/m}
. The new conductor is (very roughly) bounded by

C(\pi)(1+|A/m|)^{m}.

1.4

For the reader’s convenience we recall a few facts on special functions. We will use the functions

\begin{array}[]{rl}Erf(x)&=\displaystyle\int_{0}^{x}e^{-t^{2}}dt,\\
\vskip 5.69054pt\cr Erfc(x)&=\displaystyle\int_{x}^{\infty}e^{-t^{2}}dt=\frac{1}{2}\sqrt{\pi}-Erf(x).\end{array}

See
[
3
, p. 147]
. In particular we have the asymptotic expansion of
Erfc(x)
for
x\gg 0
:

Erfc(x)=\frac{1}{2}e^{-x^{2}}(x^{-1}+O(x^{-3})).

We will also need an estimate for

I(a,T)=\int_{T}^{\infty}e^{-t^{2}}t^{a}dt\qquad(T>0).

This is best computed as an incomplete gamma function. With

\begin{array}[]{rl}\Gamma(a,x)&=\displaystyle\int_{x}^{\infty}e^{-t}t^{a}\frac{dt}{t}\,,\\
\vskip 5.69054pt\cr I(a,T)&=\displaystyle\int_{T}^{\infty}e^{-t^{2}}t^{a+1}\frac{dt}{t}\\
\vskip 5.69054pt\cr&=\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}\int_{T^{2}}^{\infty}e^{-s}s^{\frac{a+1}{2}}\frac{ds}{s}\\
\vskip 5.69054pt\cr&=\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}\Gamma\Big(\frac{a+1}{2},T^{2}\Big)\end{array}

and the asymptotic expansion
[
3
, p. 135]

\Gamma(a,x)=x^{a-1}e^{-x}(1+O(\frac{1}{x}))

now yields

I(a,T)=\frac{1}{2}T^{a-1}e^{-T^{2}}(1+O(T^{-2})).

The quadratic integral of
\frac{L(1/2+it)}{1/2+it}
on short intervals

2.1

In this section we assume
m
fixed; we consider
\pi
verifying the assumptions in (1.1). We write

\begin{array}[]{rl}L(s)&=L(s,\pi),\\
\vskip 5.69054pt\cr L(s,\pi)&=\displaystyle\sum a_{n}n^{-s},\\
\vskip 5.69054pt\cr A_{0}(x)&=\displaystyle\sum_{n\leq x}a_{n}\quad(x\geq 1).\end{array}

We assume given
\xi
,
\nu>0
and consider only
\pi
such that
\nu\geq\sigma_{c}(\pi)
where
\sigma_{c}(\pi)
is the abscissa of convergence of
L(s,\pi)
. We assume that we have, for
x\geq 1
and any
\varepsilon
, an estimate

(2.1)

A_{0}(x)\ll_{\varepsilon}C^{\xi}\,x^{\nu+\varepsilon}

where the implicit constant depends only on
\varepsilon
, and
C=C(\pi)
. For a suitable function
f
on
\mathbb{R}_{+}^{\times}
, we define

\widetilde{\mathcal{M}}f(s)=\int_{0}^{\infty}f(x)x^{-s}\frac{dx}{x}.

Lemma 2.1
.

(cf.
\cite[cite]{[\@@bibref{}{Cl}{}{}, \lx@sectionsign~3.3]})
For
Re(s)>\nu
, the integral defining
\widetilde{\mathcal{M}}A_{0}(s)
is absolutely convergent and
\widetilde{\mathcal{M}}A_{0}(s)=\frac{L(s)}{s}
.

Indeed

\begin{array}[]{l}\hbox to28.45274pt{}\displaystyle\int_{0}^{X}\Big(\sum_{n\leq x}a_{n}\Big)n^{-s}\frac{dx}{x}\\
\vskip 5.69054pt\cr=\displaystyle\sum_{n\leq X}a_{n}\int_{n}^{X}x^{-s-1}dx\\
\vskip 5.69054pt\cr=\displaystyle\frac{1}{s}\sum_{n\leq X}a_{n}n^{-s}-\frac{1}{s}\Big(\sum_{n\leq X}a_{n}\Big)X^{-s}.\end{array}

Since
Re(s)>\sigma_{c}(\pi)
, the first sum converges to
L(s,\pi)
for
X\rightarrow\infty
. The second is dominated by
X^{\nu+\varepsilon/2}X^{-\nu-\varepsilon}\rightarrow 0
.

For
\sigma>\nu
, we then have

(2.2)

\int_{1}^{\infty}x^{-\sigma}A_{0}(x)x^{-it}\frac{dx}{x}=\frac{L(\sigma+it)}{\sigma+it}.

In order to obtain a minoration of the
L^{2}
–norm of
\frac{L(\frac{1}{2}+it)}{1/2+it}
on an interval
[-T,T]
, we consider its scalar product with a Gaussian. Thus let, for
\alpha>0
:

g_{\alpha}(t)=e^{-\pi\alpha t^{2}}.

We extend
g_{\alpha}
to a function of
s
, equal to
g_{\alpha}(t)
for
s=1/2+it
. Thus

G_{\alpha}(s)=e^{\pi\alpha(s-1/2)^{2}}.

This is a function of rapid decrease in
t

(s=\sigma+it)
, uniformly in any vertical strip. In particular, for
\sigma>\nu
:

(2.3)

\int_{\sigma}\frac{L(s)}{s}G_{\alpha}(s)ds=i\int_{-\infty}^{+\infty}\frac{L(1/2+it)}{1/2+it}g_{\alpha}(t)dt

since
L(s)
and
G_{\alpha}(s)
have no poles. We estimate the left–hand side using the Fourier transform. We have by (2.2)

\int_{0}^{\infty}e^{-\sigma X}A_{0}(e^{X})e^{-itX}dX=\frac{L(\sigma+it)}{\sigma+it},

i.e.

\frac{L(\sigma+it)}{\sigma+it}=\mathcal{F}(e^{-\sigma X}A_{0}(e^{X}))

where

\mathcal{F}h(t)=\int_{-\infty}^{+\infty}h(X)e^{-itX}dX.

The inverse transformation is

(2.4)

\mathcal{F}^{-1}k(X)=\frac{1}{2\pi}\int_{-\infty}^{+\infty}k(t)e^{itX}dt.

The scalar product formula is

\int k_{1}(X)\overline{k_{2}(X)}dX=\frac{1}{2\pi}\int h_{1}(t)\bar{h}_{2}(t)dt.

However, (2.3) is a bilinear product, and then:

\int h_{1}(t)h_{2}(t)dt=2\pi\int k_{1}(X)k_{2}(-X)dX.

Therefore the left–hand side of (2.3) is equal to the product of
i=\sqrt{-1}
and of

(2.5)

2\pi\int_{0}^{\infty}e^{-\sigma X}A_{0}(e^{X})\hat{G}_{\alpha}(-X)dX

where
\hat{G}_{\alpha}(X)
is given by (2.4) applied to
k(t)=G_{\alpha}(\sigma+it)
. Now

(2.6)

G_{\alpha}(\sigma+it)=e^{\pi\alpha(\sigma-1/2)^{2}}e^{-\pi\alpha t^{2}}e^{2\pi\alpha(\sigma-1/2)it}.

We have

\mathcal{F}^{-1}k(X)=\frac{1}{2\pi}\mathcal{F}_{0}^{-1}k(\frac{X}{2\pi})

where
\mathcal{F}_{0}
,
\mathcal{F}_{0}^{-1}
denote the usual Fourier transforms, normalised by
2\pi
. Now

\begin{array}[]{rl}\mathcal{F}_{0}^{-1}(f(t)e^{2i\pi\beta t})&=\mathcal{F}_{0}^{-1}f(X+\beta),\\
\mathcal{F}_{0}^{-1}(e^{-\pi\alpha t^{2}})&=\dfrac{1}{\sqrt{\alpha}}e^{-\frac{\pi}{\alpha}X^{2}}\end{array}

so
\mathcal{F}_{0}^{-1}G_{\alpha})(X)
is, by (2.6), equal to

\begin{array}[]{c}e^{\pi\alpha(\sigma-1/2)^{2}}\dfrac{1}{\sqrt{\alpha}}e^{-\frac{\pi}{\alpha}(X+\alpha(\sigma-1/2))^{2}}\\
=\dfrac{1}{\sqrt{\alpha}}e^{-\frac{\pi}{\alpha}X^{2}}e^{-2\pi X(\sigma-1/2)},\end{array}

and

\mathcal{F}^{-1}G_{\alpha}(-X)=\frac{1}{2\pi\sqrt{\alpha}}e^{-\frac{X^{2}}{4\pi\alpha}}e^{(\sigma-1/2)X}.

2.2

We now consider the integral (2.5), equal to

(2.7)

\begin{array}[]{c}\displaystyle\frac{1}{\sqrt{\alpha}}\int_{0}^{\infty}e^{-\sigma X}A_{0}(e^{X})e^{-\frac{X^{2}}{4\pi\alpha}}e^{(\sigma-1/2)X}dX\\
=\displaystyle\frac{1}{\sqrt{\alpha}}\int_{0}^{\infty}A_{0}(e^{X})e^{-\frac{X^{2}}{4\pi\alpha}}e^{-\frac{X}{2}}dX.\end{array}

(It is, as it should be in view of the translation of complex integrals, independent of
\sigma
.)

For
1\leq x\leq 2
,
A_{0}(x)=1
. We first obtain a lower bound for

I_{1}:=\frac{1}{\sqrt{\alpha}}\int_{0}^{\log 2}e^{-\frac{X^{2}}{4\pi\alpha}}e^{-\frac{X}{2}}dX.

In the sequel, Landau’s symbols
O(\ ),\ll,\ldots,
unindexed, are used when the implicit constants are absolute. We have

I_{1}\geq\sqrt{1/2}\frac{1}{\sqrt{\alpha}}\int_{0}^{\log 2}e^{-\frac{X^{2}}{4\pi\alpha}}dX.

We set
X=\sqrt{4\pi\alpha}Y
; the integral is then

\begin{array}[]{rl}&2\sqrt{\pi}\displaystyle\int_{0}^{\log 2/2\sqrt{\pi\alpha}}e^{-Y^{2}}dY\\
=&\displaystyle 2\sqrt{\pi}Erf\Big(\frac{\log 2}{2\sqrt{\pi\alpha}}\Big)\\
=&2\sqrt{\pi}\displaystyle\Big(\frac{1}{2}\sqrt{\pi}-Erfc\Big(\frac{\log 2}{2\sqrt{\pi\alpha}}\Big)\Big).\end{array}

For
x\rightarrow\infty
,
Erfc(x)=\frac{1}{2x}e^{-x^{2}}(1+O(\frac{1}{x^{2}}))
. For small
\alpha
, then,

I_{1}\geq\pi\sqrt{1/2}-O\Big(\sqrt{\alpha}\ e^{-\frac{\log^{2}2}{4\pi\alpha}}\Big)

and
I_{1}\geq\frac{\pi}{2}:=2c
for
\alpha
sufficiently small.

We now have to estimate the remainder, dominated by

I_{2}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{\alpha}}\int_{\log 2}^{\infty}C^{\xi}\ e^{(\nu+\varepsilon-1/2)X}e^{-\frac{X^{2}}{4\pi\alpha}}dX.

Write
\theta=\nu+\varepsilon-1/2
, and set

Y=X-2\pi\theta\alpha.

The exponential term in the integrand is then

\exp\Big(-\frac{Y^{2}}{4\pi\alpha}\Big)e^{\pi\theta^{2}\alpha}.

We can neglect the constant since
\alpha
will be small. Thus
I_{2}
is dominated by

\begin{array}[]{rl}I_{3}&=\displaystyle\frac{1}{\sqrt{\alpha}}\int_{\ell}^{\infty}C^{\xi}e^{-\frac{1}{4\pi\alpha}X^{2}}dX,\\
&\ell=\log 2-2\pi\theta\alpha.\end{array}

for small
\alpha
, so the part relative to
[\ell,\log 2]
is dominated by
C^{\xi}\sqrt{\alpha}\cdot e^{-a_{1}/\alpha}
with
a_{1}>0
fixed. We now consider

I_{4}=C^{\xi}\int_{\log 2}^{\infty}\frac{1}{\sqrt{\alpha}}e^{-\frac{1}{4\pi\alpha}X^{2}}dX.

The integral is equal to
2\sqrt{\pi}

Erfc(\frac{\log 2}{2\sqrt{\pi\alpha}})
(see the formulas in § 1); it admits an expression

(2.8)

\sqrt{\pi}\ e^{-\frac{\log^{2}2}{4\pi\alpha}}\Big(\frac{2\sqrt{\pi\alpha}}{\log 2}+O(\alpha^{3/2})\Big)

for small
\alpha
. This (multiplied by
C^{\xi}
) is of the same order as the integral on the small segment.

We want to ensure that this is dominated by
I_{1}
, which is implied by

\sqrt{\alpha}\ e^{-\frac{a_{2}}{\alpha}}C^{\xi}\leq a_{3}

where
a_{3}
is a small constant, i.e.

\frac{1}{2}\log\alpha-\frac{a_{2}}{\alpha}+\xi\log C\leq-a_{4}

(a_{4}>0)
. We may assume
\alpha\leq 1
, so we seek

(2.9)

\frac{a_{2}}{\alpha}\geq a_{4}+\xi\log C.

Since
C\geq 2^{m}
,
\xi\log C>a_{5}>0
and

\frac{1}{a_{4}+\xi\log C}>\frac{1}{N\xi\log C}

for
N
such that
a_{4}<(N-1)\xi\log C
, so
N
is determined by constants independent of
\pi
.

Thus (2.9) is verified if

(2.10)

\alpha\leq\frac{a_{5}}{\log C}.

We summarise the result

Proposition 2.1
.

Assume
\pi
cuspidal, and
\sigma_{c}(\pi)\leq\nu
. There exist positive constants
c
,
b_{1}
(depending only on
\xi
,
\nu
) such that if
C=C(\pi)
and

\alpha\leq\frac{b_{1}}{\log C},

then

\Big|\int_{-\infty}^{+\infty}\frac{L(1/2+it,\pi)}{1/2+it}g_{\alpha}(t)dt\Big|\geq c.

2.3

We can now apply the previous proof by using a theorem of Molteni
[
14
]
.

Theorem 2.1
.

(Molteni). Assume
\pi
is a cuspidal, unitary representation of
GL(m,\mathbb{A})
. Then

\sum_{n\leq x}|a_{n}|=O_{\varepsilon}(C^{\varepsilon}x^{1+\varepsilon})\qquad(x\geq 1).

(The implicit constant depends only on
m
and
\varepsilon
.) In fact Molteni proves a stronger result:

\sum_{n\leq x}\frac{|a_{n}|}{n}=O(C^{\varepsilon}x^{\varepsilon}).

Thus, in the previous proof, we may take
\nu=1
,
\xi=\varepsilon
. (This has been improved by Xiannan Li
[
12
]
; the improved estimate seems irrelevant to us, but the theorem extends to automorphic representations satisfying the conditions in §1.1) The assumption
\sigma_{c}(\pi)\leq\nu
is of course satisfied.

We have given an exposition of the proof valid for other exponents, because of the following fact. Consider only representations
\pi
that are tempered, i.e. satisfy the Ramanujan Conjecture. Assume moreover
\pi_{\infty}
self–dual. Then by a result of Friedlander and Iwanieč, (2.1) is satisfied with
\xi=\frac{1}{m+1}
,
\nu=\frac{m-1}{m+1}
. (See
[
4
]
; the self–duality condition is implicit there, and made explicit in
[
2
]
.) However Friedlander and Iwanieč consider only the arithmetic conductor, so (2.1) is replaced by

A_{0}(x)\ll D^{\frac{1}{m+1}}x^{\frac{m-1}{m+1}+\varepsilon}.

They must also assume that
\pi_{\infty}
is bounded, i.e.
C_{\infty}(\pi)=\prod(2+|c_{i}|)
bounded. Their estimate with respect to
x
is better, but this does not seem to play any role in the present proof.

The fact that these representations
\pi
verify
\sigma_{c}(\pi)\leq\frac{m-1}{m+1}
is proved in
[
2
]
.

We will unfortunately have to assume the Ramanujan conjecture in the next paragraph.

2.4

In order to exploit Proposition 2.1 to obtain a lower bound on a “short” interval, we must now estimate the tail

I_{5}(T)=\int_{T}^{\infty}\frac{L(1/2+it)}{1/2+it}g_{\alpha}(t)dt

(and the opposite one). In order to have uniform estimates, we must now assume that
\pi
verifies the Ramanujan Conjecture. We now have

L(\frac{1}{2}+it)\ll C(t)^{\frac{1}{4}+\varepsilon}.

Fix
g_{\alpha}(t)=e^{-\pi\alpha t^{2}}
with
\alpha=\frac{b_{1}}{\log C}
. For
T\geq 1
,

(2.11)

I_{5}(T)\ll C^{\frac{1}{4}+\varepsilon}\int_{T}^{\infty}e^{-\pi\alpha t^{2}}t^{\frac{m}{4}-1+\varepsilon}dt.

We need an estimate for

\int_{T}^{\infty}e^{-\pi\alpha t^{2}}t^{a}dt;

as recalled in Chapter 1,

\begin{array}[]{c}\displaystyle\int_{T}^{\infty}e^{-\pi\alpha t^{2}}t^{a}dt=\frac{1}{2}\pi^{-1}\alpha^{-1}T^{a-1}e^{-\pi\alpha T^{2}}\cdot\\
\cdot\displaystyle\Big(1+O\Big(\frac{1}{\pi\alpha}T^{-2}\Big)\Big).\end{array}

We want to ensure that
|I_{5}(T)|\leq\frac{1}{4}c
; i.e.

C^{\frac{1}{4}+\varepsilon}\alpha^{-1}T^{a-1}e^{-\pi\alpha T^{2}}\Big(1+O\Big(\frac{1}{\alpha}T^{-2}\Big)\Big)\ll 1.

We assume provisionally that
T
can be chosen such that
\frac{1}{\alpha}T^{-2}
is small. Then this is implied by

(2.12)

\frac{1}{2}\log C-\log\alpha+(a-1)\log T-\pi\alpha T^{2}\leq-X

where
X
is a positive constant; here
a=\frac{m}{4}-1+\varepsilon
; we replace
a
by a value such that
a-1>0
. With
\alpha=\frac{b_{1}}{\log C}
, we want to ensure

\pi\alpha T^{2}-a\log T\geq X-\log\alpha+\frac{1}{2}\log C,

which yelds

\pi b_{1}T^{2}-a\log C\log T\geq\log C(X^{\prime}+\log\log C+\frac{1}{2}\log C).

Assume
T^{2}=A_{1}\log^{2}C
for a large positive constant. The left–hand side is then

\pi b_{1}A_{1}\log^{2}C-a\log C(\log\log C+\log\sqrt{A_{1}})\geq A_{2}\log^{2}C

for
A_{1}
sufficiently large and
C\geq 2^{m}
, and this dominates the right–hand side. Moreover,
\frac{1}{\alpha}T^{-2}\ll(\log C)^{-1}A_{1}^{-2}\leq\frac{1}{2}
for
A_{1}
sufficiently large (independently of
C
), as assumed previously. Therefore:

Lemma 2.2
.

For
T=A\log C
, where
A
is a sufficiently large positive constant, and
\alpha=\frac{b_{1}}{\log C}
,

\Big|\int_{|t|\geq T}\frac{L(1/2+it)}{1/2+it}g_{\alpha}(t)dt\Big|\leq\frac{c}{2}.

Now the absolute value of the integral for
|t|\leq T
is
\geq\frac{c}{2}
. Thus

(2.13)

\overset{}{\underset{t\in[-T,T]}{\mathrm{Sup}}}\Big|\frac{L(1/2+it)}{1/2+it}\Big|\geq\frac{c}{2}\ \Big|\int_{t\leq T}g_{\alpha}(t)dt\Big|^{-1}

(2.14)

\Big\|\frac{L(1/2+it)}{1/2+it}\Big\|_{2}\ \Big\|g_{\alpha}(t)\Big\|_{2}\geq\frac{c}{2}

where the
L^{2}
–norms are computed in
[-T,T]
.

We now have to estimate the integral, and quadratic integral, of
g_{\alpha}(t)
on
[-T,T]
. We have

\begin{array}[]{rl}\displaystyle\int_{0}^{T}e^{-\pi\alpha t^{2}}dt&=\displaystyle\int_{0}^{\sqrt{\pi\alpha}T}e^{-s^{2}}(\sqrt{\pi\alpha})^{-1}ds\\
&\displaystyle=a_{6}(\log C)^{1/2}\int_{0}^{a_{7}(\log C)^{1/2}}e^{-s^{2}}ds\\
&\asymp(\log C)^{1/2}\end{array}

and the same estimate is true for the quadratic integral. Therefore we obtain

Theorem 2.2
.

There exist absolute, positive constants
A
,
c_{1}
,
c_{2}
such that for any cuspidal
\pi
verifying the Ramanujan Conjecture

(i)
\overset{}{\underset{t\in[-T,T]}{\mathrm{Sup}}}\Big|\dfrac{L(1/2+it),\pi}{1/2+it}\Big|\geq c_{1}(\log C)^{-1/2}

(ii)
\displaystyle\int_{-T}^{T}\Big|\dfrac{L(1/2+it),\pi}{1/2+it}\Big|^{2}dt\geq c_{2}(\log C)^{-1/2}

if
T\geq A\log C
,
C=C(\pi)
.

It may be noticed that in
(i)
the Lindelöf Conjecture yields an estimate
\ll(\log C)^{\varepsilon}
(for
T
of order
A\log C
).

We have used the cuspidality of
\pi
only in order to rely on Molteni’s result; Xianan Li’s result makes this unnecessary.

We can now use the remarks in §1.3 on the translation of integrals to obtain lower bounds on

on short intervals, not necessarily centered in

. We have

\begin{array}[]{c}L(s,\pi[X]=L(s+iX,\pi)\\
\vskip 2.84526pt\cr C(\pi[X])\leq C(\pi)(1+|X|)^{m}\\
\vskip 2.84526pt\cr\log C(\pi[X])\leq\log C(\pi)+m\log(1+|X|).\end{array}

Let

S=\overset{}{\underset{t\in[-T,T]}{\mathrm{Sup}}}\Big|\frac{L(1/2+it,\pi[X])}{1/2+it}\Big|.

By Theorem 2.2, for
T\geq A\log C(\pi[X])
:

S\geq c_{1}(\log C+m\log(1+|X|))^{-1/2}.

However

S=\overset{}{\underset{t\in[-T,T]}{\mathrm{Sup}}}\Big|\frac{L(1/2+i(t+X),\pi}{1/2+it}\Big|\leq 2\overset{}{\underset{t\in[-T,T]}{\mathrm{Sup}}}|L(1/2+i(t+X,\pi)|

Thus we have the following result on “short intervals”.

Theorem 2.3
.

There exist absolute, positive constants
A
,
c_{3},c_{4}>0
such that for any cuspidal
\pi
verifying the Ramanujan Conjecture

(i)
{\underset{t\in[X-T,X+T]}{\mathrm{Sup}}}|L(1/2+it,\pi)|\geq c_{3}\cdot(\log C(\pi)+m\log(1+|X|))^{-1/2}.

(ii)
\displaystyle\int_{X-T}^{X+T}\Big|\dfrac{L(1/2+it,\pi}{1/2+i(t-X)}\Big|^{2}dt\geq c_{4}(\log C(\pi)+m\log(1+|X|))^{-1/2}

if
T\geq A(\log C+\log(1+|X|)
,
C=C(\pi)
.

The absolute constants depend only on
m
.

Sarnak suggested that the estimate (ii) should be compared with the lower bound on short intervals obtained by Ramachandra in
[
18
]
. (Ramachandra notes that his proof, written for the Riemann zeta function, will extend to
L
-functions with Euler products. See
[
18
, Remark 3]
.) In fact, because of the large denominator in the integral, our lower bound seems better than the one that follows obviously from Ramachandra’s. We assume
X
positive and large and
T
small with respect to
X
. (In the next lines the constant
A
depends on the formula.) Then Ramachandra’s estimate is

\displaystyle\int_{X}^{X+T}|L(1/2+it)|^{2}dt\geq AT\log T,\\
\\
\ T\geq A\log\log X

while here we obtain

\displaystyle\int_{X-T}^{X+T}\Big|\dfrac{L(1/2+it,\pi}{1/2+i(t-X)(}\Big|^{2}dt\geq A(\log C+\log X)^{-1/2}

for
T\geq A(\log C+\log X).

For fixed
C
, our condition on
T
implies Ramachandra’s. We consider
T\approx A\log X
. The denominator in our formula is smaller than
T^{2}
. Thus Ramachandra’s bound implies for the integral

\displaystyle\int_{X-T}^{X+T}\Big|\dfrac{L(1/2+it,\pi}{1/2+i(t-X)}\Big|^{2}dt

a lower bound in
AT^{-1}\log T
. But for
T\approx A\log X
,
(\log X)^{-1}\log\log X
is dominated by our bound
(\log X)^{-1/2}
. Thus, for fixed
C
, the results do not seem commensurable. (It would be interesting to know how the conductor enters in Ramachandra’s formula.)

3

The first non–trivial Frobenius element in a Galois representation

3.1

In this chapter we consider a non–trivial, irreducible representation
\rho:\mathrm{Gal}(E/F)\rightarrow GL(m,\mathbb{C})
, where
E/F
is a Galois extension of number fields. If
\rho
is unramified at a prime
\mathfrak{p}
of
F
, we can consider the image
\rho(\mathrm{Frob}_{\mathfrak{p}})
, a conjugacy class in
GL(m,\mathbb{C})
. We want to obtain a lower bound
\beta
on the first value of
N\mathfrak{p}
such that
\rho
is unramified and
\rho(\mathrm{Frob}_{\mathfrak{p}})\not=1
. Of course, this is complicated by the presence of ramification, i.e., there may be a ramified prime
\mathfrak{q}
such that
N\mathfrak{q}\leq\beta
. If
m=1
, this applies to Artin characters of
\mathbb{A}_{F}^{\times}
.

3.2

We first consider the Abelian case,
m=1
. If
F=\mathbb{Q}
, we are looking at primitive Dirichlet characters
\mod\,q=D
. If
L(s,\chi)\ll(q|s|)^{\mu+\varepsilon}
for
Re(s)=\frac{1}{2}
, it was shown in
[
2
, § 3.3]
that
\beta\ll q^{2\mu+\varepsilon}
. Since Petrow and Young
[
17
]
have proved the Weyl bound
\mu\leq\frac{1}{6}
, we obtain by this simple application of the Mellin transform

(3.1)

\mathrm{For}\ \chi\ \mathrm{a\ primitive\ Dirichlet\ character}\mod q,\ \beta(x)\overset{}{\underset{\varepsilon}{\ll}}q^{1/3+\varepsilon}.

Of course this is much weaker than Burgess’s bound (for
q
prime)

\beta(\chi)\overset{}{\underset{\varepsilon}{\ll}}q^{\frac{1}{4\sqrt{e}}+\varepsilon}.

However, we can now extend the method to Artin characters of
\mathbb{A}_{F}^{\times}
for an arbitrary number field
F
.

So let
\chi
be a character of finite order, and
\rho
the
1
–dimensional representation of
\mathrm{Gal}(F_{\chi}/F)
associated to
\chi
by class field theory. We consider the
L
–function
L(s,\chi)
as a
L
–function over
\mathbb{Q}
. All the conditions in the paper of Friedlander–Iwaniec are met — this will also be the case for a Galois representation of degree
>1
. We therefore have the properties of
L(s,\chi)
recalled in § 2.3.

Rather than the formulas in
[
2
]
, we use the Mellin transform as in § 2.1. Thus let

\begin{array}[]{rl}L(s,\chi)&=\displaystyle\sum_{n}a_{n}n^{-s},\\
\vskip 5.69054pt\cr A_{0}(x)&=\displaystyle\sum_{n\leq x}a_{n}\end{array}

and

(3.2)

\widetilde{\mathcal{M}}\,A_{0}(x)=\int_{1}^{\infty}A_{0}(x)x^{-s}\frac{dx}{x}.

Let
\nu_{0}
be the exponent of Friedlander–Iwaniec:

\nu_{0}=\frac{d-1}{d+1},\ d=[F:\mathbb{Q}].

so

(3.3)

A_{0}(x){\underset{d,\varepsilon}{\ll}}D^{\frac{1}{d+1}}x^{\nu_{0}+\varepsilon}\quad(x\geq 1).

We note that the Archimedean data
(c_{j})
associated to
\chi
are uniformly bounded, so the constant depends only on
(d,\varepsilon)
. Moreover (3.2) is obtained by Friedlander–Iwaniec for
x\geq D^{1/2}
(see a correction in
[
2
, § 2.5]
) but it is trivial for
x\leq D^{1/2}
since
|a_{n}|\ll n^{\varepsilon^{\prime}}
for any
\varepsilon^{\prime}>o
.

The integral in (3.2) is absolutely convergent for
Re(s)>\nu_{0}
, say
Re(s)=\nu>\nu_{0}
. The function
A_{0}(x)x^{-\nu}
then belongs to
L^{2}(\mathbb{R}_{+}^{\times},\frac{dx}{x})
. It follows (cf. Titchmarsh
[
19
]
) that
\frac{L(s)}{s}
is
L^{2}
on the line
Re(s)=\nu
, and that

(3.4)

\int_{1}^{\infty}|x^{-\nu}A_{0}(x)|^{2}\frac{dx}{x}=\frac{1}{2\pi}\int_{\nu}\Big|\frac{L(s)}{s}\Big|^{2}\ |ds|.

However, if
\nu
is close to
\nu_{0}
, the convergence of the right–hand side does not follow from convexity: see
[
2
, § 3.2]
; we will review this in Chapter 4. As a consequence we cannot use Proposition 1.1 to estimate the right–hand side.

By Proposition 1.1, we have
\mu(\nu)\leq\frac{d}{2}(1-\nu)
, so
\mu(\nu)<\frac{1}{2}
if
\nu>1-\frac{1}{d}
. The convergence of the right–hand side then follows from convexity and we have again by Proposition 1.1:

Lemma 3.1
.

For
\nu>1-\frac{1}{d}
,

\int_{\nu}\Big|\frac{L(s)}{s}\Big|^{2}\,|ds|\ll D^{1-\nu+\varepsilon}.

3.3

We now want to obtain a lower bound for the left–hand side of (3.4). We consider the integral on
[1,\beta]
where
\beta
is the norm of the first unramified prime such that
\chi(\mathfrak{p})\not=1
.

Before doing so we recall the expression of the “absolute” conductor
D
. Let
D_{F}
be the absolute value of the discriminant of
F
; let
\mathfrak{f}(\chi)
be the conductor of
\chi
(seen as a character of
\mathbb{A}_{F}^{\times}
), an ideal of
F
. Then

(3.5)

D=D_{F}\ N_{F/\mathbb{Q}}\mathfrak{f}(\chi).

We need an expression for
A_{0}(x)
, for
x<\beta
. It is then given by

a_{n}=\sum_{N\mathfrak{a}=n}a(\mathfrak{a})

where
\sum a(\mathfrak{a})N\mathfrak{a}^{-s}
is the expression of
L(s,\chi)
as an
L
–function over
F
, and
N\mathfrak{a}<\beta
, which implies that the factorization of
\mathfrak{a}
involves only primes
\mathfrak{p}
with
N\mathfrak{p}<\beta
. We have

L_{\mathfrak{p}}(s,\chi)=(1-N\mathfrak{p}^{-s})^{-1}

if
\chi
is unramified at
\mathfrak{p}
and
N\mathfrak{p}<\beta
,

L_{\mathfrak{q}}(s,\chi)=1

is
\chi
is ramified at
\mathfrak{q}
. If
\mathfrak{a}=\prod\mathfrak{q}_{i}^{\alpha_{i}}\prod\mathfrak{p}_{j}^{\beta_{j}}

(where we use
\{\mathfrak{q}_{i}\}
to denote all the ramified primes), this implies that

a(\mathfrak{a})=0

if
\alpha_{i}>0
for some
i
, and
a(\mathfrak{a})=1
otherwise. Thus, up to
x=\beta
,
A_{0}(x)
is a variant of the summation function for
\zeta_{F}
:

(3.6)

A_{0}(x)=\sum_{n\leq x}\sum_{N\mathfrak{a}=n\atop(\mathfrak{q}_{i},\mathfrak{a})=1}1

where the condition
(\mathfrak{q}_{i},\mathfrak{a})=1
is imposed for all
i
. Let
B_{0}(x)
be the summation function for
\zeta_{F}
. The difference
B_{0}-A_{0}
is then

(3.7)

\sum_{n\leq x}\sum_{N\mathfrak{a}=n}{}^{\prime}1

where
\sum^{\prime}
is restricted by the condition

(3.8)

\exists~~i:\mathfrak{q}_{i}|\mathfrak{a}.

At this point it is necessary to extend the main result of
[
4
]
. Very generally, assume
\pi
is a tempered representation of
GL(m,\mathbb{A})
; assume
\pi
is a product
\pi_{1}\times\cdots\pi_{r}
of cuspidal representations (with at most a factor
m_{i}=1
,
\pi_{i}=1
); assume
\pi_{\infty}
self-dual. Let
A_{0}(x)
be the associated summation function. Let
\kappa
be the residue of
L(s,\pi)
at 1. We write
\mathbf{D}
for the conductor of
\pi
.

Proposition 3.1
.

For
x\geq 1
,

A_{0}(x)=\kappa x+O(\mathbf{D}^{\frac{1}{m+1}+\varepsilon}x^{\frac{m-1}{m+1}+\varepsilon})

where the implicit constant depends only on
m
,
\varepsilon
and the Archimedean parameters of
\pi
.

For the function
\zeta_{F}
, the implicit constant depends only on
d
and
\varepsilon
.

For
x\geq\mathbf{D}^{1/2}
this is the result of
[
4
]
. For
x\leq\mathbf{D}^{1/2}
, it was already noticed in § 3.2 that
A_{0}(x)\ll\mathbf{D}^{\frac{1}{m+1}}x^{\frac{m-1}{m+1}+\varepsilon}
. (This does not depend on the fact that
L(s)
is holomorphic.) If
x\leq\mathbf{D}^{1/2}
,
\mathbf{D}^{\frac{1}{m+1}}x^{-\frac{2}{m+1}}\geq 1
and it suffices to check:

\kappa\ll(\mathbf{D}x)^{\varepsilon}

which is true since
\kappa\ll\mathbf{D}^{\varepsilon}
, cf.
[
7
, p. 100]
. (For a zêta function, this is the easy part of the Brauer–Siegel theorem.)

We can now apply this to
B_{0}
:

B_{0}(x)=\kappa x+O(D_{F}^{\frac{1}{d+1}+\varepsilon}x^{\frac{d-1}{d+1}+\varepsilon}),

\kappa=Res_{s=1}\zeta_{F}(s)
. Now fix
i
and consider the sum (3.7), the condition being
\mathfrak{q}_{i}|\mathfrak{a}
. By a change of variables,

\sum_{N\mathfrak{a}\leq x\atop\mathfrak{q}_{i}|\mathfrak{a}}1=\sum_{N\mathfrak{b}\leq\frac{x}{q_{i}}}1

where
q_{i}=N\mathfrak{q}_{i}
and
\mathfrak{b}
ranges over integral ideals. This can be expressed as

\kappa\frac{x}{q_{i}}+O(D_{F}^{\frac{1}{d+1}+\varepsilon}(x/q_{i})^{\frac{d-1}{d+1}+\varepsilon}).

However, care has to be exercised. This is certainly true for
x\geq q_{i}
, however for
x<q_{i}
the sum is empty and we have to check that

\kappa\frac{x}{q_{i}}\ll D_{F}^{\frac{1}{d+1}+\varepsilon}(x/q_{i})^{1-\frac{2}{d+1}+\varepsilon}

(uniformly when
d
is fixed.) This reduces to

\kappa\ll D_{F}^{\frac{1}{d+1}+\varepsilon}(x/q_{i})^{-\frac{2}{d+1}+\varepsilon};

but
x/q_{i}\leq 1
and
\kappa\ll D_{F}^{\varepsilon}
.

Now return to the sum (3.7). By the inclusion–exclusion principle, it is equal to

\sum S_{i}-\sum S_{ij}+\sum S_{ijk}-\cdots

where

S_{ij\cdots\ell}=\sum_{N\mathfrak{a}\leq x\atop\mathfrak{q}_{i}\cdots\mathfrak{q}_{\ell}|\mathfrak{a}}1.

The same argument yields, with
\mathfrak{q}=\mathfrak{q}_{i}\cdots\mathfrak{q}_{\ell}
,
q=q_{i}\cdots q_{\ell}
:

S_{ij\cdots\ell}=\kappa\frac{x}{q_{i}}+O\Big(D_{F}^{\frac{1}{d+1}+\varepsilon}(x/q)^{\frac{d-1}{d+1}+\varepsilon}\Big).

The previous argument remains correct for
x\leq q
, with the same uniformity.

Let us write
\xi=\frac{d-1}{d+1}+\varepsilon
. We now have

(3.9)

A_{0}(x)=\prod_{i}\Big(1-\frac{1}{q_{i}}\Big)\kappa x+O(\prod(1+q_{i}^{-\xi})D_{F}^{\frac{1}{d+1}+\varepsilon}x^{\xi}).

In (3.9), we can (brutally) replace
D_{F}
by
D\geq D_{F}
.
We have to evaluate
Q=\prod(1-\frac{1}{q_{i}})
and
R=\prod(1+q_{i}^{-\xi})
.

Let
p_{i}
be the prime divisor of
q_{i}
. Then

Q\geq\prod_{i}\Big(1-\frac{1}{p_{i}}\Big).

There may be several primes associated to
p_{i}
, but fewer than
d=[F:\mathbb{Q}]
. Thus

Q\geq\prod_{j}\Big(1-\frac{1}{p_{j}}\Big)^{d}

where the
p_{j}
are now distinct. But
p_{j}
divides
N\mathfrak{f}_{\chi}
, so
p_{j}\leq N=N\mathfrak{f}_{\chi}
and

Q\geq\prod_{p\leq N}\Big(1-\frac{1}{p}\Big)^{d}.

Since
\prod\limits_{p\leq N}(1-\frac{1}{p})=\frac{e^{-\gamma}}{\log N}(1+O(\frac{1}{\log N}))
we deduce that
Q\geq A\,\log(N\mathfrak{f}_{\chi})^{-d}
. (If
\chi
is everywhere unramified,
Q=1
.).

Consider now
R=\prod(1+q_{i}^{-\xi})
. Again,
R\leq\prod\limits_{i}(1+p_{i}^{-\xi})\leq(\prod\limits_{j}(1+p_{j}^{-\xi}))^{d}
, with the same notation. But
\prod(1+p_{j}^{-\xi})=\sigma_{-\xi}(p_{1}\cdots p_{j})<\sigma_{0}(p_{1}\cdots p_{j})\ll(p_{1}\cdots p_{j})^{\varepsilon}\ll(N\mathfrak{f}_{\chi})^{\varepsilon}
and the same estimate obtains for
R
.

Now fix
\nu>1-\frac{1}{d}
and consider the integral

\int_{1}^{\beta}|x^{-\nu}A_{0}(x)|^{2}\frac{dx}{x}.

By (3.9) this is a sum of three terms, one of them exact:

I_{1}=\int_{1}^{\beta}(Q\kappa x^{1-\nu})^{2}\frac{dx}{x},

so

(3.10)

I_{1}\asymp Q^{2}\kappa^{2}\beta^{2-2\nu}\qquad(\beta\geq 2).

The second term is dominated by

The third term is dominated by

(rescaling
\varepsilon\cdots

<-1

We now compare

and

is dominant, i.e.

We now assume that the extension

is Galois. By the Brauer–Siegel theorem,
\kappa\gg D_{F}^{-\varepsilon}
, the implicit constant depending only on
d
. By our estimate on
Q
, we must have

which is true, upon changing

\beta^{\frac{2}{d+1}}\succ D^{\frac{1}{d+1}+\varepsilon},

i.e.

\beta\gg D^{\frac{1}{2}+\varepsilon}.

Now consider

and

, we must have

\beta^{2-2\nu}\succ D^{\frac{2}{d+1}+\varepsilon};

for

We now compare

The conclusion is:
