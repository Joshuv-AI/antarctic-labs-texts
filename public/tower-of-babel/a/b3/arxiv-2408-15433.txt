Decoherence and Brownian motion of a polarizable particle near a surface
Clemens Jakubec, Christopher Jarzynski, Kanu Sinha

Abstract
We analyze the classical and quantized center-of-mass motion of a polarizable particle interacting with the fluctuations of the electromagnetic (EM) field in the presence of a medium. As a polarizable particle is immersed in a thermal environment, the momentum impulses imparted by the field fluctuations lead to momentum diffusion and drag for the particle’s classical center of mass. When considering the quantized center-of-mass motion of the particle, these very fluctuations gain information about its position, leading to decoherence in the position basis. We derive a position localization master equation for the particle’s quantized center of mass, and examine its classical center-of-mass momentum diffusion, elucidating correspondences between classical and quantum Brownian motion of polarizable particles near media.

Model

III
Quantized Center-of-Mass Dynamics

III.1
QBM master equation

We now describe the quantized center-of-mass dynamics resulting from the interaction between the particle and fluctuations of the EM field. The Hamiltonian
\hat{H}_{I}^{(2)}
for the interaction between the particle’s center of mass and the fluctuations of the field can be written in the interaction picture as:

\displaystyle\tilde{H}_{I}^{(2)}\left(t\right)\equiv

\displaystyle e^{-i\left(\hat{H}_{M}+\hat{H}_{F}\right)t/\hbar}\hat{H}_{I}^{(2)}e^{i\left(\hat{H}_{M}+\hat{H}_{F}\right)t/\hbar}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-\tilde{{\mathbf{r}}}(t)\cdot\tilde{\mbc{B}}(t),

(7)

where we define the operators
\tilde{{\mathbf{r}}}(t)\equiv e^{-i\hat{H}_{M}t/\hbar}\hat{\mathbf{r}}e^{i\hat{H}_{M}t/\hbar}
and

\displaystyle\tilde{\mbc{B}}(t)

\displaystyle\equiv e^{-i\hat{H}_{F}t/\hbar}\hat{\mbc{B}}e^{i\hat{H}_{F}t/\hbar}=

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\left[\partial_{i}\hat{\mathbf{P}}\left({\mathbf{r}}_{0},t\right)\cdot\hat{\mathbf{E}}\left({\mathbf{r}}_{0},t\right)+\hat{\mathbf{P}}\left({\mathbf{r}}_{0},t\right)\cdot\partial_{i}\hat{\mathbf{E}}\left({\mathbf{r}}_{0},t\right)\right]

(8)

with
\hat{\mbc{B}}=\left[\frac{1}{2}\mathbf{\nabla}\left(\hat{{\mathbf{P}}}\left({\mathbf{r}}_{0}\right)\cdot\hat{{\mathbf{E}}}\left({\mathbf{r}}_{0}\right)\right)\right]
as the bath operator.
The dynamics of the center-of-mass density matrix
\hat{\rho}_{M}=\int d^{2}r\int d^{2}r^{\prime}\rho_{M}({\mathbf{r}},{\mathbf{r}}^{\prime},t)\left|{\mathbf{r}}\right\rangle\left\langle{\mathbf{r}}^{\prime}\right|
, with
\left\{{\mathbf{r}},{\mathbf{r}^{\prime}}\right\}
pertaining to motion of the particle around
{\mathbf{r}}_{0}
, is given by the second-order Born-Markov master equation
[
11
]
:

\displaystyle\frac{{d}\hat{\rho}_{M}\left(t\right)}{{d}t}=-\frac{i}{\hbar}\mathrm{Tr}_{B}\left[\tilde{H}_{I}^{(2)}\left(t\right),\hat{\rho}_{M}(0)\otimes\hat{\rho}_{B}\right]

\displaystyle-\frac{1}{\hbar^{2}}\mathrm{Tr}_{B}\int_{0}^{\infty}\mathrm{d}\tau\left[\tilde{H}_{I}^{(2)}\left(t\right),\left[\tilde{H}_{I}^{(2)}\left(t-\tau\right),\hat{\rho}_{M}\left(t\right)\otimes\hat{\rho}_{B}\right]\right]

(9)

The first term in Eq. (
9
) is typically neglected on account of representing a physically irrelevant modification to the system Hamiltonian; here it corresponds to the Casimir-Polder force along the direction of motion
[
34
]
.

We can simplify the above master equation to obtain the QBM master equation in the Born-Markov approximation. After transforming back to the Schrödinger picture we get:

\displaystyle\frac{{d}\hat{\rho}_{M}(t)}{{d}t}=

\displaystyle-\frac{i}{\hbar}\left[\hat{H}_{M},\hat{\rho}_{M}(t)\right]-\frac{i}{\hbar}\sum_{i}C^{(1)}_{i}\left[\hat{r}_{i},\hat{\rho}_{M}(0)\right]-\frac{1}{2\hbar^{2}}\int_{0}^{\infty}\mathrm{d}\tau\sum_{ij}\left[i\mathcal{D}_{ij}(\tau)\left[\hat{r}_{i},\left\{\hat{r}_{j},\hat{\rho}_{M}(t)\right\}\right]\right.

\displaystyle\left.-i\mathcal{D}_{ij}\left(\tau\right)\frac{\tau}{M}\left[\hat{r}_{i},\left\{\hat{p}_{j},\hat{\rho}_{M}(t)\right\}\right]-\mathcal{N}_{ij}(\tau)\left[\hat{r}_{i},\left[\hat{r}_{j},\hat{\rho}_{M}(t)\right]\right]+\mathcal{N}_{ij}\left(\tau\right)\frac{\tau}{M}\left[\hat{r}_{i},\left[\hat{p}_{j},\hat{\rho}_{M}(t)\right]\right]\right],

(10)

where the indices
i,j
indicate displacements and momenta along the respective axes. The first-order Casimir-Polder force, as well as the dissipation and noise kernels, are defined via the expectation value and two-time correlation functions of the bath operator
\tilde{\mbc{B}}(t)
as:

\displaystyle C^{(1)}_{i}\equiv

\displaystyle\left\langle{\tilde{\mathcal{B}}}_{i}\left(t\right)\right\rangle

(11)

\displaystyle\mathcal{D}_{ij}\left(\tau\right)\equiv

\displaystyle i\left\langle\left[{\tilde{\mathcal{B}}}_{i}\left(t\right),{\tilde{\mathcal{B}}}_{j}\left(t-\tau\right)\right]\right\rangle

(12)

\displaystyle\mathcal{N}_{ij}\left(\tau\right)\equiv

\displaystyle\left\langle\left\{{\tilde{\mathcal{B}}}_{i}\left(t\right),{\tilde{\mathcal{B}}}_{j}\left(t-\tau\right)\right\}\right\rangle.

(13)

Ignoring the last term in Eq. (
10
) under the Markov approximation, the master equation simplifies to:

\displaystyle\frac{{d}\hat{\rho}_{M}}{{d}t}\approx-\frac{i}{\hbar}\left[\hat{H}_{M},\hat{\rho}_{M}(t)\right]-\frac{i}{\hbar}\sum_{i}C^{(1)}_{i}\left[\hat{r}_{i},\hat{\rho}_{M}(0)\right]-

\displaystyle\frac{i}{\hbar}\sum_{ij}C_{ij}^{(2)}\left[\hat{r}_{i}\hat{r}_{j},\hat{\rho}_{M}(t)\right]-\frac{i}{\hbar}\sum_{ij}\Gamma_{ij}\left[\hat{r}_{i},\left\{\hat{p}_{j},\hat{\rho}_{M}(t)\right\}\right]-

\displaystyle\sum_{ij}\Lambda_{ij}\left[\hat{r}_{i},\left[\hat{r}_{j},\hat{\rho}_{M}(t)\right]\right].

(14)

The first term corresponds to the unitary evolution under the free-particle Hamiltonian. The second term corresponds to the first-order Casimir-Polder potential; the third term represents a harmonic potential resulting from the second-order Casimir-Polder interaction between the particle and medium
[
35
]
, with

\displaystyle C_{ij}^{(2)}\equiv\frac{1}{2\hbar}\int_{0}^{\infty}\mathrm{d}\tau\mathcal{D}_{ij}\left(\tau\right).

(15)

The fourth term represents dissipation or friction on the quantized center of mass with
[
11
]
:

\displaystyle\Gamma_{ij}\equiv\frac{1}{2M\hbar}\int_{0}^{\infty}\mathrm{d}\tau\,\tau\mathcal{D}_{ij}\left(\tau\right).

(16)

The last term stands for decoherence that acts to localize the center of mass in the position basis, with a localization rate
[
11
]
:

\displaystyle\Lambda_{ij}\equiv\frac{1}{2\hbar^{2}}\int_{0}^{\infty}\mathrm{d}\tau\mathcal{N}_{ij}\left(\tau\right).

(17)

We will now investigate these effects for the specific example of a polarizable particle near a planar medium.

III.3
Fokker-Planck Equation

To illustrate how decoherence and dissipation (
\Lambda_{ij}
and
\Gamma_{ij}
) are linked to Brownian motion for the classical center of mass, we now derive a phase-space equation from the master equation. To this end, we first write Eq. (

\displaystyle\frac{{d}\rho_{M}({\mathbf{r}},{\mathbf{r}}^{\prime},t)}{{d}t}=

\displaystyle\frac{i\hbar}{2M}\left(\frac{\partial^{2}}{\partial r_{i}^{2}}-\frac{\partial^{2}}{\partial r_{i}^{{}^{\prime}2}}\right)\rho_{M}({\mathbf{r}},{\mathbf{r}}_{i}^{\prime},t)-\frac{i}{\hbar}\sum_{i}C_{i}^{(1)}\left(r_{i}-r_{i}^{\prime}\right)\rho_{M}({\mathbf{r}},{\mathbf{r}}^{\prime},0)-\frac{i}{\hbar}\sum_{ij}C_{ij}^{(2)}\left(r_{i}r_{j}-r_{i}^{\prime}r_{j}^{\prime}\right)\rho_{M}({\mathbf{r}},{\mathbf{r}}^{\prime},t)-

\displaystyle\sum_{ij}\Gamma_{ij}\left(r_{i}-r_{i}^{\prime}\right)\left(\frac{\partial}{\partial r_{j}}-\frac{\partial}{\partial r_{j}^{\prime}}\right)\rho_{M}({\mathbf{r}},{\mathbf{r}}^{\prime},t)-\sum_{ij}\Lambda_{ij}\left(r_{i}-r_{i}^{\prime}\right)\left(r_{j}-r_{j}^{\prime}\right)\rho_{M}({\mathbf{r}},{\mathbf{r}}^{\prime},t)

(26)

Now we take the Wigner-Weyl transform of the above equation by making the substitution
r_{i}=\bar{r}_{i}+\frac{a_{i}}{2}
and
r_{i}^{\prime}=\bar{r}_{i}-\frac{a_{i}}{2}
and using
W({\mathbf{r}},{\mathbf{p}})=\int d{\mathbf{a}}e^{i{\mathbf{p}}\cdot{\mathbf{a}}}/\hbar\rho({\mathbf{r}}+{\mathbf{a}}/2,{\mathbf{r}}-{\mathbf{a}}/2)

[
11
,
9
]
. After relabeling
\bar{r}_{i}
as
r_{i}
we obtain the Quantum Fokker-Planck equation:

\displaystyle\frac{\partial W}{\partial t}=

\displaystyle-\sum_{i}\frac{p_{i}}{M}\frac{\partial W}{\partial r_{i}}-\sum_{i}C_{i}^{(1)}\frac{\partial W}{\partial p_{i}}-2\sum_{ij}C_{ij}^{(2)}r_{i}\frac{\partial W}{\partial p_{j}}

\displaystyle+\sum_{ij}2\Gamma_{ij}\frac{\partial(p_{j}W)}{\partial p_{i}}+\sum_{ij}\Lambda_{ij}\hbar^{2}\frac{\partial W}{\partial p_{i}\partial p_{j}}.

(27)

Analogous to the master equation Eq. (
14
), the second and the third terms correspond to the first- and second-order Casimir-Polder potentials.
The fourth term corresponds to friction and the fifth term represents the momentum diffusion coefficient of the quantized center of mass, which is therefore related to the decoherence coefficient in Eq. (
14
) as:

\displaystyle 2\hbar^{2}\Lambda_{ij}=\frac{\left\langle\Delta\hat{p}_{i}\Delta\hat{p}_{j}\right\rangle_{q}}{\Delta t},

(28)

where the subscript ‘
q
’ refers to a quantum expectation value. The above relation can be seen as a manifestation of the complementarity of the position and momentum variables. Such a relation was established by
[
38
]
for general QBM dynamics.

Classically the diffusion coefficient is defined as:

\displaystyle D_{ij}=\frac{\left\langle\Delta p_{i}\Delta p_{j}\right\rangle_{c}}{\Delta t},

(29)

where the subscript ‘
c
’ denotes a classical average. Using the Ehrenfest theorem
[
39
]
to form a correspondence between classical and quantum expectation values, establishes a relation between momentum diffusion for the classical center of mass and decoherence of the quantized center of mass:

\displaystyle\frac{\left\langle\Delta p_{i}\Delta p_{j}\right\rangle_{c}}{\Delta t}=\frac{\left\langle\Delta\hat{p}_{i}\Delta\hat{p}_{j}\right\rangle_{q}}{\Delta t}\implies D_{ij}=2\hbar^{2}\Lambda_{ij}.

(30)

In essence, this relation confirms that both decoherence (the measurement of the system by the environment) and momentum diffusion arise due to the scattering of thermal photons.
Although Eq. (
21
) and Eq. (
20
) are derived under the assumption of a planar medium, all the conclusions drawn in this section hold for an arbitrary macroscopic body.

We can motivate the result
D_{ij}=2\hbar^{2}\Lambda_{ij}
(Eq. (
30
)) through a back-of-the-envelope calculation, treating momentum diffusion as the cumulative effect of many random “kicks” delivered to a Brownian particle.
These kicks produce momentum changes
\cdots\delta{\bf p}_{n-1},\,\delta{\bf p}_{n},\,\delta{\bf p}_{n+1}\cdots
, which sum to
\Delta{\bf p}\equiv\sum_{n}\delta{\bf p}_{n}
over a time
\Delta t
.
For sufficiently many kicks,
\Delta{\bf p}
becomes a random sample from the Gaussian distribution

\displaystyle\eta(\Delta{\bf p})=\sqrt{\frac{|K|}{2\pi\Delta t}}\exp\left[-\frac{\sum_{ij}K_{ij}\Delta p_{i}\Delta p_{j}}{2\Delta t}\right]\,,

(31)

where
K\equiv D^{-1}
is the inverse of the momentum diffusion matrix given by
D_{ij}=\left\langle\Delta p_{i}\Delta p_{j}\right\rangle/\Delta t
.

To evaluate the decoherence produced by these kicks, note that when a quantum system undergoes a sudden momentum change
\delta{\bf p}
, its density matrix
\hat{\rho}
transforms as

\hat{\rho}\rightarrow e^{-i\delta{\bf p}\cdot\hat{\bf r}/\hbar}\,\hat{\rho}\,e^{i\delta{\bf p}\cdot\hat{\bf r}/\hbar}\,.

Many such changes, summing to
\Delta{\bf p}
over time
\Delta t
, produce the change (in the position representation)

\displaystyle\rho({\bf r},{\bf r}^{\prime},t+\Delta t)=e^{-i\Delta{\bf p}\cdot({\bf r}-{\bf r}^{\prime})/\hbar}\rho({\bf r},{\bf r}^{\prime},t)\,,

(32)

with
\Delta{\bf p}\equiv\sum_{n}\delta{\bf p}_{n}
.
Averaging over
\eta(\Delta{\bf p})
(Eq. (
31
)) leads to the expression

\displaystyle\rho({\bf r},

\displaystyle{\bf r}^{\prime},t+\Delta t)=\left\langle e^{-i\Delta{\bf p}\cdot({\bf r}-{\bf r}^{\prime})/\hbar}\right\rangle\rho({\bf r},{\bf r}^{\prime},t)

\displaystyle=e^{-\sum_{ij}D_{ij}(r_{i}-r_{i}^{\prime})(r_{j}-r_{j}^{\prime})\Delta t/2\hbar^{2}}\rho({\bf r},{\bf r}^{\prime},t)\,,

(33)

describing decoherence in the position representation.

Now consider the effects of the decoherence term in Eq. (
26
):

\displaystyle\frac{\partial}{\partial t}\rho({\bf r},{\bf r}^{\prime},t)=-\sum_{ij}\Lambda_{ij}(r_{i}-r_{i}^{\prime})(r_{j}-r_{j}^{\prime})\rho({\bf r},{\bf r}^{\prime},t)\,.

(34)

Upon integrating, we obtain the expression

\displaystyle\rho({\bf r},{\bf r}^{\prime},t+\Delta t)=e^{-\sum_{ij}\Lambda_{ij}(r_{i}-r_{i}^{\prime})(r_{j}-r_{j}^{\prime})\Delta t}\rho({\bf r},{\bf r}^{\prime},t)\,,

(35)

which has the same form as Eq. (
33
).
Comparing the two gives
D_{ij}=2\hbar^{2}\Lambda_{ij}
.

IV
Classical Center-of-Mass Brownian Motion

We now turn to the Brownian motion properties of the classical center-of-mass of the particle interacting with the thermal field near a surface.
The force acting on the classical center-of-mass of the particle along the
i
-axis is derived from the first-order Hamiltonian
\hat{H}_{I}^{(1)}
as:

\displaystyle\hat{F}_{i}=-{\partial_{i}\tilde{H}_{I}^{(1)}}=\tilde{\mathcal{B}}_{i}(t),

(36)

which is the same as the
i^{\mathrm{th}}
component of the bath operator in Eq. (
8
). The operator nature of the force results from the quantized EM field exerting a force on the particle’s classical center of mass. The resulting force is averaged over the thermal density matrix of the field, leading to a net momentum impulse of zero, such that
\left\langle\Delta p_{i}\right\rangle=-\int_{0}^{\Delta t}\mathrm{d}\tau\left\langle\tilde{\mathcal{B}}_{i}(\tau)\right\rangle=0
.
The corresponding momentum correlator is thus found from the two-time correlation of the forces as:

\displaystyle\left\langle\Delta p_{i}\Delta p_{j}\right\rangle=\int_{0}^{\Delta t}\mathrm{d}\tau_{1}\int_{0}^{\Delta t}\mathrm{d}\tau_{2}\left\langle\tilde{\mathcal{B}}_{i}\left(\tau_{1}\right)\tilde{\mathcal{B}}_{i}\left(\tau_{2}\right)\right\rangle.

(37)

We see that the momentum diffusion for the particle’s classical center-of-mass diffusion can be found as the rate of increase in the momentum uncertainty with time,
D_{ij}\equiv\frac{\left\langle\Delta p_{i}\Delta p_{j}\right\rangle}{\Delta t}
, as (see Appendix
D
for details):

\displaystyle D_{ij}=\frac{4\hbar^{2}\mu_{0}^{2}}{\pi}\int_{0}^{\infty}\mathrm{d}\omega\omega^{4}n_{\mathrm{th}}\left(\omega\right)\left(n_{\mathrm{th}}\left(\omega\right)+1\right)\left|\alpha\left(\omega\right)\right|^{2}

\displaystyle\sum_{p,k=\left\{x,y,z\right\}}\mathrm{Im}\left[\bar{\bar{G}}_{pk}\left({\mathbf{r}}_{0},{\mathbf{r}}_{0},\omega\right)\right]\mathrm{Im}\left[\partial_{i}\bar{\bar{G}}_{pk}\left({\mathbf{r}}_{0},{\mathbf{r}}_{0},\omega\right)\partial_{j}\right].

(38)

As shown in the previous section, the above expression for momentum diffusion is related to the decoherence rate in Eq. (
21
) as
D_{ij}=2\hbar^{2}\Lambda_{ij}=2\hbar^{2}\Lambda\delta_{ij}
.

The above momentum diffusion of a polarizable particle moving through blackbody radiation is accompanied by a drag force of the form
\left(F_{\mathrm{drag}}\right)_{i}=-\xi_{ij}v_{j}

[
5
]
, with
v_{j}
as the particle’s classical center-of-mass velocity, and
\xi_{ij}
as the friction coefficient. Using the fluctuation-dissipation theorem
D_{ij}=2\xi_{ij}k_{B}T
, we obtain the friction coefficient as:

\displaystyle\xi_{ij}=\frac{2\hbar^{2}\mu_{0}^{2}}{k_{B}T\pi}\int_{0}^{\infty}\mathrm{d}\omega\omega^{4}n_{\mathrm{th}}\left(\omega\right)\left(n_{\mathrm{th}}\left(\omega\right)+1\right)\left|\alpha\left(\omega\right)\right|^{2}

\displaystyle\sum_{p,k=\left\{x,y,z\right\}}\mathrm{Im}\left[\bar{\bar{G}}_{pk}\left({\mathbf{r}}_{0},{\mathbf{r}}_{0},\omega\right)\right]\mathrm{Im}\left[\partial_{i}\bar{\bar{G}}_{pk}\left({\mathbf{r}}_{0},{\mathbf{r}}_{0},\omega\right)\partial_{j}\right].

(39)

As before, using planar symmetry, the diffusion and drag coefficients,
D_{ij}=\left(D^{\mathrm{free}}+D^{\mathrm{s}}\right)\delta_{ij}
and
\xi_{ij}=\gamma\delta_{ij}=\left(\xi^{\mathrm{free}}+\xi^{\mathrm{s}}\right)\delta_{ij}
, respectively, can be separated into free-space (
D^{\mathrm{free}},\xi^{\mathrm{free}}
) and surface-induced (
D^{\mathrm{s}},\xi^{\mathrm{s}}
) components. In the free-space limit, we recover:

\displaystyle D^{\mathrm{free}}=\frac{\hbar^{2}}{9\pi^{3}\epsilon_{0}^{2}c^{8}}\int_{0}^{\infty}\mathrm{d}\omega\omega^{8}\left|\alpha(\omega)\right|^{2}n_{\mathrm{th}}(\omega)\left(n_{\mathrm{th}}(\omega)+1\right)

(40)

\displaystyle\xi^{\mathrm{free}}=

\displaystyle\frac{\hbar^{2}}{18k_{B}T\pi^{3}\epsilon_{0}^{2}c^{8}}\int_{0}^{\infty}\mathrm{d}\omega\omega^{8}\left|\alpha(\omega)\right|^{2}n_{\mathrm{th}}(\omega)\left(n_{\mathrm{th}}(\omega)+1\right),

(41)

in agreement with
[
10
]
.

V
Discussion

To summarize, we establish a connection between the Brownian motion of the quantum and classical center of mass a neutral, polarizable particle interacting with the fluctuations of a thermal EM field near a medium (Fig.
1
(a)). We derive a second-order Born-Markov QBM master equation for the particle’s quantized center of mass near an arbitrary macroscopic body, which encompasses the thermal Casimir-Polder force, position decoherence and dissipation (Eq. (
14
)). We analyze this master equation for the specific case wherein the motion of the particle is restricted to a plane parallel to a planar medium, demonstrating that the decoherence and dissipation of the particle increase with the medium losses (Eq. (
21
) and (
20
)). Further deriving the Quantum Fokker-Planck equation corresponding to the QBM master equation (Eq.(
27
)), we link the quantized center-of-mass decoherence
\Lambda
to the classical center of mass momentum diffusion
D
via the simple relation
D=2\hbar^{2}\Lambda
. This relation relies on the position decoherence form of the QBM master equation in the ‘long-wavelength limit’, and is a manifestation of the position-momentum complementarity and Ehrenfest theorem.
Subsequently, we calculate an explicit expression for the momentum diffusion and classical drag of the polarizable particle in the presence of a planar medium (Eq. (
38
) and (
39
)).

In the free space limit our decoherence expression agrees with
[
9
,
10
]
, and the classical momentum diffusion and thermal drag near surfaces are in agreement with
[
12
,
13
]
. The connection between decoherence and diffusion highlighted in Sec.
III.3
has been previously pointed out by
[
38
]
for general QBM dynamics.

Loss of spatial coherence of particles near surfaces is pertinent to matter-wave interferometry. Previous works have investigated the effect of fluctuation-induced van der Waals interactions between particles and gratings which causes dephasing
[
40
,
15
]
. Dephasing or phase averaging also occurs when the system interacts with an ensemble of EM sources
[
41
]
. This statistical effect occurs as the phases induced on the system state fluctuate due to a changing environment, leading to an apparent loss of coherence after averaging over many runs of the experiment.

In a recent work, it was illustrated that a polarizable particle in uniformly accelerated motion experiences momentum fluctuations in accord with its observed Unruh-Davies temperature
[
42
]
. The relation between momentum fluctuations and center-of-mass decoherence suggests that a uniformly accelerating particle prepared in spatial superposition should decohere at a rate determined by the effective Unruh-Davies temperature.
Such decoherence and Brownian motion in accelerated frames of reference can further correspond to decoherence of particles in the vicinity of black hole event horizons via the equivalence principle, a subject of growing recent interest
[
43
,
44
,
45
,
46
]
.

Appendix A
The Electromagnetic Green tensor

The electromagnetic Green tensor
\bar{\bar{G}}\left({\mathbf{r}}_{1},{\mathbf{r}}_{2},\omega\right)
represents the propagator for the EM field in the presence of media, accounting for how a photon at frequency
\omega
propagates between positions
{\mathbf{r}}_{1}
and
{\mathbf{r}}_{2}
. The Green tensor can be found as the solution of the Helmholtz equation
[
33
]
,

\displaystyle\left(\nabla_{1}\times\frac{1}{\mu({\mathbf{r}}_{1},\omega)}\nabla_{1}\times-\frac{\omega^{2}}{c^{2}}\epsilon\left({\mathbf{r}}_{1},\omega\right)\right)\bar{\bar{G}}({\mathbf{r}}_{1},{\mathbf{r}}_{2},\omega)={\mathbf{\delta}}\left({\mathbf{r}}_{1}-{\mathbf{r}_{2}}\right),

(42)

where
\epsilon({\mathbf{r}},\omega)
and
\mu({\mathbf{r}},\omega)
represent the spatially dependent dielectric permittivity and magnetic permeability in the presence of a medium. The Green tensor encompasses the effects of surface geometry, optical and material properties of the medium, thereby modifying the density of states of the EM environment. For a general medium the Green tensor can always be split up into a free space Green tensor
\bar{\bar{G}}_{\mathrm{0}}\left({\mathbf{r}}_{1},{\mathbf{r}}_{2},\omega\right)
and a scattering Green tensor
\bar{\bar{G}}_{\mathrm{s}}\left({\mathbf{r}}_{1},{\mathbf{r}}_{2},\omega\right)
.

The free space Green tensor describes the EM field when
\epsilon({\mathbf{r}},\omega)=\epsilon_{0}
and
\mu({\mathbf{r}},\omega)=\mu_{0}
, i.e. when there are no media present, and between the points
{\mathbf{r}}_{1}
and
{\mathbf{r}}_{2}
is given as:

\displaystyle\bar{\bar{G}}_{\mathrm{0}}\left({\mathbf{r}}_{1},{\mathbf{r}}_{2},\omega\right)=\frac{e^{ikr}}{4\pi k^{2}r^{3}}\left\{f(kr)\mathbb{1}-h(kr){\mathbf{e}}_{r}\otimes{\mathbf{e}}_{r}\right\}

(43)

where
f\left(x\right)\equiv 1-ix-x^{2}
,

h\left(x\right)\equiv 3-3ix-x^{2}
,
r=\left|{\mathbf{r}}_{1}-{\mathbf{r}}_{2}\right|
, and
{\mathbf{e}}_{r}={\mathbf{r}}/r
.
\bar{\bar{G}}_{\mathrm{0}}\left({\mathbf{r}}_{1},{\mathbf{r}}_{2},\omega\right)
includes all the processes that do not involve scattering of any surfaces and is thus independent of any surface properties and only depends in the distance between
{\mathbf{r}}_{1}
and
{\mathbf{r}}_{2}
.

The surface Green tensor, which describes the response of the electric field in the presence of a planar surface, is given by:

\displaystyle\bar{\bar{G}}_{\mathrm{s}}\left({\mathbf{r}}_{1},{\mathbf{r}}_{2},\omega\right)=\frac{1}{8\pi}\int_{0}^{\infty}dk^{\parallel}\frac{k^{\parallel}}{\kappa^{\perp}}e^{-\kappa^{\perp}z_{+}}\left[\begin{pmatrix}J_{0}(k^{\parallel}x)+J_{2}(k^{\parallel}x)&0&0\\
0&J_{0}(k^{\parallel}x)-J_{2}(k^{\parallel}x)&0\\
0&0&0\end{pmatrix}r_{s}\right.

\displaystyle\left.+\frac{c^{2}}{\omega^{2}}\begin{pmatrix}\kappa{{}^{\perp}2}\left[J_{0}(k^{\parallel}x)-J_{2}(k^{\parallel}x)\right]&0&2k^{\parallel}\kappa^{\perp}J_{1}(k^{\parallel}x)\\
0&\kappa^{\perp 2}\left[J_{0}(k^{\parallel}x)+J_{2}(k^{\parallel}x)\right]&0\\
-2k^{\parallel}\kappa^{\perp}J_{1}(k^{\parallel}x)&0&2k^{\parallel 2}J_{0}(k^{\parallel}x)\end{pmatrix}r_{p}\right]

(44)

where
k^{\parallel}
,
\kappa^{\perp}
are the wavevectors parallel and perpendicular to the surface with
\omega^{2}/c^{2}=k^{\parallel 2}-\kappa^{\perp 2}
,
z_{+}=z_{1}+z_{2}
,
x=x_{1}-x_{2}
,
J_{0}(k^{\parallel}x)
and
J_{2}(k^{\parallel}x)
are the cylindrical Bessel function of the zeroth and second kind and
r_{s}
,
r_{p}
are the Fresnel coefficients for s and p-polarized light.

Appendix B
Casimir-Polder Potentials

The interaction between the fluctuating field and the polarization induced by it in the particle, leads to the Casimir-Polder potential. The second-order master equation approach used in this paper yields Casimir-Polder potential up to second order in the polarizability
\alpha(\omega)
. The order refers to the number of scattering events taken into account. First order thus means that no scattered fields are considered, whereas second order means that fields that scatter off the particle once are taken into account. The first-order Casimir-Polder potential is given by
[
31
]
:

\displaystyle\mathcal{U}_{\mathrm{CP}}^{(1)}=-\frac{\hbar\mu_{0}}{2\pi}\int_{0}^{\infty}d\omega\alpha(\omega)\omega^{2}(2n_{\mathrm{th}}(\omega)+1)\mathrm{Tr}\left[\mathrm{Im}\ \bar{\bar{G}}({\mathbf{r}}_{0},{\mathbf{r}}_{0},\omega)\right].

(45)

The first term in Eq. (
9
) gives rise to the first-order Casimir-Polder force, defined as the derivative of the above Casimir-Polder potential along the
i
axis, i.e.,
C^{(1)}_{i}\equiv\partial_{i}\mathcal{U}_{\mathrm{CP}}^{(1)}
.
When the motion of the particle is restricted to the x-y plane, the first derivative of the Casimir-Polder potential is zero because
\mathcal{U}_{\mathrm{CP}}^{(1)}
only varies in the z-direction via the scattering Green tensor (see App.
A
).

The second order Casimir-Polder potential
\mathcal{U}_{\mathrm{CP}}^{(2)}
is given by
[
35
]
:

\displaystyle\mathcal{U}_{\mathrm{CP}}^{(2)}=\frac{\hbar\mu_{0}^{2}}{2\pi}\sum_{pk=\left\{x,y,z\right\}}\int_{0}^{\infty}d\omega\omega^{4}\left|\alpha(\omega)\right|^{2}\left(2n_{\mathrm{th}}(\omega)+1\right)\left[\mathrm{Re}\ \bar{\bar{G}}_{pk}\left({\mathbf{r}}_{0},{\mathbf{r}}_{0},\omega\right)\mathrm{Im}\ \bar{\bar{G}}_{pk}\left({\mathbf{r}}_{0},{\mathbf{r}}_{0},\omega\right).\right]

(46)

It is a second-order potential and therefore represents processes that involve scattering off the particle. The second term in Eq. (
14
) corresponds to the second derivative of the above potential along
i
and
j
axes, i.e.,
C^{(2)}_{ij}\equiv\partial_{i}\mathcal{U}_{\mathrm{CP}}^{(2)}\partial_{j}
.
