1.
Introduction

Understanding the arithmetic of varieties over global fields constitutes one of the most fundamental and difficult ambitions of number theory. If
X\subset\mathbb{P}^{n-1}
is a variety over a global field
K
, then one key aspect entails understanding the counting function

N_{X}(P)\coloneqq\#\{\bm{x}\in X(K)\colon H(\bm{x})<P\}

for a suitable height function
H\colon X(K)\to\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}
. The Hardy–Littlewood circle method is a versatile tool to study
N_{X}(P)
and has been successful in producing an asymptotic formula in many situations. The circle method works particularly well when
n
is large compared to the degree of
X
and so the main challenge lies in reducing the number of admissible variables. In this work we shall focus on the case when
X=V(F_{1},F_{2})\subset\mathbb{P}^{n-1}
is a non-singular complete intersection of a cubic and a quadric hypersurface over
K=\mathbb{F}_{q}(t)
. To state our main result, we take a smooth weight function
w\colon\mathbb{F}_{q}((t^{-1}))^{n}\to\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}
that is supported around a suitable point
\bm{x}_{0}\in\mathbb{F}_{q}((t^{-1}))^{n}
with
F_{1}(\bm{x}_{0})=F_{2}(\bm{x}_{0})=0
and consider the counting function

N(P)\coloneqq\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\bm{x}\in\mathbb{F}_{q}[t]^{n}\\
F_{1}(\bm{x})=F_{2}(\bm{x})=0\end{subarray}}w\left(\frac{\bm{x}}{t^{P}}\right)\quad\text{as }P\to\infty.

In addition, we set
|\bm{x}|=\max q^{\deg x_{i}}
for
\bm{x}\in\mathbb{F}_{q}[t]^{n}
and for
P\in\mathbb{R}
, we write
\widehat{P}=q^{P}
.

Theorem 1.1
.

Let
X\subset\mathbb{P}^{n-1}
be a non-singular complete intersection of a cubic and a quadric hypersurface over
\mathbb{F}_{q}(t)
. If
n\geq 26
and
\cha(\mathbb{F}_{q})>3
, then there exists
\delta>0
such that

N(P)=c\widehat{P}^{n-5}+O\left(\widehat{P}^{n-5-\delta}\right),

for some
c>0
.

To put our result into context, Browning–Dietmann–Heath-Brown
[
5
]
proved the analogue of Theorem
1.1
over
K=\mathbb{Q}
for
n\geq 29
. Another result in this direction when both
F_{1}
and
F_{2}
are diagonal is due to Wooley
[
34
,
33
]
. Again working over
\mathbb{Q}
, he restricted the range of possible integer solutions to those having only few prime factors. Appealing to the theory of smooth Weyl sums this allowed him to provide an asymptotic formula for the number of such restricted solutions for
n\geq 13
whenever
F_{1}
and
F_{2}
have at least
7
and
5
non-zero coefficients respectively.

Let us now review some results in the general situation of non-singular complete intersections
X=V(F_{1},\dots,F_{R})\subset\mathbb{P}^{n-1}
over a global field
K
, where
d_{i}=\deg F_{i}
for
i=1,\dots,R
. Provided
n>d_{1}+\cdots+d_{R}
, the variety
X
is Fano — a class of varieties for which Manin and his collaborators
[
10
]
made a precise conjecture about the asymptotic behaviour of the counting function
N_{X}(P)
. Originally the conjecture was only stated over number fields, but it was later generalised to global function fields by Peyre
[
28
]
. In our case, the order of growth established in Theorem
1.1
agrees with this prediction. The constant
c
appearing in Theorem
1.1
plays an important role in the qualitative understanding of the set of rational points
X(K)
and has obtained a conjectural interpretation by Peyre
[
27
]
. Recall that
X
is said to satisfy the Hasse principle if the existence of
K_{\nu}
-rational points for every place
\nu
of
K
is sufficient to guarantee that
X(K)
is non-empty. In addition, we say that
X
satisfies weak approximation if the image under the diagonal embedding

X(K)\hookrightarrow\prod_{\nu}X(K_{\nu})

is dense, where we endow the right hand side with the product topology coming from the analytic topology on
X(K_{\nu})
. Whenever the circle method yields the Hasse principle, we can often adapt the argument to give weak approximation.

The application of the circle method to the study of rational points goes back to pioneering work of Birch
[
1
]
. For
K=\mathbb{Q}
, he gave an asymptotic formula for
N_{X}(P)
provided
d\coloneqq d_{1}=\cdots=d_{R}
and
n-\sigma^{*}>(d-1)2^{d-1}R(R+1)
, where
\sigma^{*}
is the dimension of the Birch singular locus, and thereby verified that
X
satisfies the Hasse principle. Schmidt
[
29
]
showed how to handle to complete intersections defined by forms of differing degrees. This was subsequently improved by Browning and Heath-Brown
[
6
]
and by Myerson
[
24
]
for generic complete intersections. Myerson was able to drop the genericity assumption for quadratic
F_{i}

[
22
]
and cubic
F_{i}

[
23
]
. However, his work only improves Birch’s result for large values of
R
. In a different direction, Northey and Vishe
[
25
]
developed a procedure that opens up the possibility for a Kloostermann refinement for systems of two forms with
d_{1}+d_{2}>5
and used it to give an asymptotic for
N_{X}(P)
and verify the Hasse principle for non-singular intersections of two cubic forms if
n\geq 39
.

In the function field setting the Hasse principle is a straightforward consequence of Lang–Tsen theory if
n>d_{1}^{2}+\cdots+d_{R}^{2}
. Indeed, with this condition
[
13
, Theorem 3.6]
shows the existence of a rational point of
X
, so that the Hasse principle is vacuously true. However, establishing weak approximation or an asymptotic formula for the number of rational points of bounded height remains a substantial challenge. Building on work of Kubota
[
19
]
, Lee
[
20
]
showed that Birch’s work can be translated to the function field setting and further demonstrated that weak approximation holds under the same constraints on the number of variables provided
\cha(K)>d
. Specialising again to the case when
X
is the non-singular intersection of a cubic and a quadric hypersurface, Tsen’s Theorem shows that
X
posseses a
K
-rational point as soon as
n>13
. Although there should be no problem in translating the work of Browning–Dietmann–Heath-Brown
[
5
]
and of Wooley
[
34
]
,
[
33
]
, currently there are no results available regarding weak approximation for complete intersections of cubic and quadric hypersurfaces. Our second main result remedies this deficiency.

Theorem 1.2
.

Let
X\subset\mathbb{P}^{n-1}
be a non-singular complete intersection of a cubic and a quadric hypersurface over
\mathbb{F}_{q}(t)
. If
n\geq 26
and
\cha(\mathbb{F}_{q})>3
, then
X
satisfies weak approximation.

The restriction on the characteristic in both Theorems
1.1
and
1.2
arises naturally in applications of the circle method. Typically, it comes from applications of Weyl differencing, which renders any estimates trivial when the characteristic is smaller than the degrees of the equations. In our situation we have to study both quadratic and cubic exponential sums that we can only bound satisfactorily when
\cha(K)>3
.

Browning and Vishe
[
4
]
have found a way to use the circle method over
\mathbb{F}_{q}(t)
to obtain crude geometric information about the space of rational curves of fixed degree inside a hypersurface in sufficiently many variables compared to its degree. If one is willing to make all the estimates uniform in
q
, then our work is likely to give access to the analogous properties when
X
is the intersection of a cubic and a quadric hypersurface.

Using a geometric approach, Tian
[
31
]
has verified the Hasse principle for non-singular cubic hypersurfaces
X\subset\mathbb{P}^{n-1}
when
\cha(K)>5
and
n\geq 6
and weak approximation for non-singular intersections of quadratic forms when
\cha(K)>2
and
n\geq 6
. It would be interesting to see whether his methods carry over to say something useful about intersections of cubic and quadric hypersurfaces.

When the degree of a form
F
is small, the delta method developed by Duke, Friedlander and Iwaniec
[
9
]
and further refined by Heath-Brown
[
15
]
is capable of dealing with significantly fewer variables than the classical circle method. In particular, it has been successfully applied to quadratic forms
[
15
]
and cubic forms
[
17
]
. Over
\mathbb{F}_{q}(t)
the delta method turns out to be much simpler thanks to the non-archimedean nature and was successfully incorporated by Browning and Vishe
[
5
]
. However, until recently it was unclear how to construct an analogue of the delta method for systems of equations. Vishe
[
32
]
made substantial progress by developing a 2-dimensional analogue of the delta method over
K=\mathbb{F}_{q}(t)
that enabled him to produce an asymptotic formula for the number of rational points of bounded height on non-singular intersections of two quadratic forms in
n\geq 9
variables when
\cha(K)>2
. His innovation serves as the main input for our work and we shall now proceed to outline the main steps of our proof.

Outline of proof

In
[
32
]
the main new input is the development of a two-dimensional version of a Farey dissection over
\mathbb{F}_{q}(t)
. While Vishe’s version only allows one to put squares around the approximating rationals, our application requires lopsided boxes in order to take into account the different degrees of the forms
F_{1}
and
F_{2}
. In Section
2
we shall modify his development to accommodate our needs. We expect that the argument would carry over inductively to higher dimensions. In the case of hypersurfaces the delta method is particularly useful when the degree is at most 3. Unless one appeals to a similar strategy as devised by Marmon and Vishe
[
21
]
to deal with quartic hypersurfaces, it seems that when one considers intersections of two hypersurfaces, our situation is just at the barrier. That is, when the sum of the degrees of the individual hypersurfaces exceeds 5, it does not seem to give any improvements compared to the classical circle method.

Once we have achieved the Farey dissection, a standard application of the Poisson summation formula leads us to study certain oscillatory integrals and exponential sums. We provide upper bounds for the oscillatory integrals in Section
5
and for the exponential sums and averages thereof in Sections
6
and
7
. When the modulus is square-free, we estimate the exponential sums by appealing to work of Katz
[
18
]
, that ultimately relies on Deligne’s resolution of the Riemann hypothesis over finite fields
[
8
]
and obtain cancellations when summing over the numerators. This is usually referred to as a “Kloosterman refinement”. As in
[
32
]
and
[
2
]
it would have been desirable to obtain a double Kloosterman refinement, in which we extract cancellations when summing over both the numerators and denominators. In
[
32
]
and
[
2
]
the corresponding exponential sums are multiplicative and their averages over the denominator can be studied via the associated
L
-functions that satisfy a suitable version of the Riemann hypothesis. In our setting, we consider exponential sums associated to linear combinations of the cubic and quadratic form. That these are not homogeneous only allows for a “twisted” form of multiplicativity and it is not clear how to associate an
L
-function to study their averages. There remains the substantial task of providing estimates for exponential sums when the modulus is not square-free. We are unable to give upper bounds directly, but rather study averages of them over the dual variable in Section
7
. The underlying arguments go back to work of Heath-Brown
[
16
]
, but are significantly more complicated in our situation.

Notation

\ll,\gg
and the big-

2.
Farey dissection

Vishe’s strategy is to find a suitable family of lines in the unit square so that when we consider rational points on these lines they cover the whole square and at the same time stay sufficiently far away from each other to ensure an exact partition. Before reviewing his results in more detail, we need some notation. Let
K=\mathbb{F}_{q}(t)
and
\mathcal{O}=\mathbb{F}_{q}[t]
be its ring of integers. We denote by
K_{\infty}\coloneqq\mathbb{F}_{q}((t^{-1}))
the completion of
K
with respect to the absolute value induced by
t^{-1}
. It naturally comes with a norm extending the absolute value
|f|=q^{\deg f}
on
\mathcal{O}
. More explicitly, any non-zero
\alpha\in K_{\infty}
can be written uniquely as
\alpha=\sum_{i\leq N}\alpha_{i}t^{i}
with
\alpha_{N}\neq 0
and
\alpha_{i}\in\mathbb{F}_{q}
, in which case
|\alpha|=q^{N}
. We also define
\{\alpha\}\coloneqq\sum_{i\leq-1}\alpha_{i}t^{i}
to be the
fractional part
of
\alpha
and
[\alpha]\coloneqq\alpha-\{\alpha\}
to be the
integer part
of
\alpha
.

Furthermore,
K_{\infty}
is a locally compact Hausdorff group with respect to addition and so comes with a Haar measure
\differential\alpha
that we normalise in such a way that the unit interval
\mathbb{T}\coloneqq\{\alpha\in K_{\infty}\colon|\alpha|<1\}
has measure 1. We extend the norm and the Haar measure to
K_{\infty}^{n}
via

|\underline{\alpha}|\coloneqq\max_{i=1,\dots,n}|\alpha_{i}|\quad\text{and}\quad\differential\underline{\alpha}\coloneqq\differential\alpha_{1}\dots\differential\alpha_{n}

for
\underline{\alpha}=(\alpha_{1},\dots,\alpha_{n})\in K_{\infty}^{n}
. If
\underline{c}=(c_{1},c_{2})\in\mathcal{O}^{2}
, then we say that
\underline{c}
is
primitive
if
(c_{1},c_{2})=1
and either
c_{1}
is monic or
c_{1}=0
and
c_{2}
is monic. For
d,k\in\mathcal{O}
with
(d,k)=1
and
\underline{c}\in\mathcal{O}^{2}
primitive we define the affine line

(2.1)

L_{1}(d\underline{c},k)\coloneqq\{\underline{x}\in K_{\infty}^{2}\colon d\underline{c}\cdot\underline{x}=k\}

and the
generalised line

(2.2)

L(d\underline{c})\coloneqq\{\underline{a}/r\in\mathbb{T}^{2}\cap K^{2}\colon(\underline{a},r)=1,\underline{a}/r\in L_{1}(d\underline{c},k)\text{ for some }k\in\mathcal{O}\text{ with }(k,d)=1\}.

Note that since
(k,d)=1
, we must have
d\mid r
if
\underline{a}/r\in L(d\underline{c})
with
(\underline{a},r)=1
. We refer to
|d\underline{c}|
as the
height
of
L(d\underline{c})
. For
\underline{x}\in\mathbb{T}^{2}
and
R\in\mathbb{Z}
, we let
B(\underline{x},\widehat{R})\coloneqq\{\underline{\theta}\in\mathbb{T}^{2}\colon|\underline{\theta}-\underline{x}|<\widehat{R}\}
be the ball of radius
\widehat{R}
centered around
\underline{x}
. Similarly, for
\underline{\widehat{R}}=(\widehat{R}_{1},\widehat{R}_{2})\in\mathbb{Z}
we set

R(\underline{x},\underline{\widehat{R}})\coloneqq\{\underline{\theta}\in\mathbb{T}^{2}\colon|\theta_{i}-x_{i}|<\widehat{R}_{i}\text{ for }i=1,2\}

to be a rectangle of sidelengths
\widehat{R}_{1}
and
\widehat{R}_{2}
centered around
\underline{x}
. In addition,
\underline{\widehat{R}}^{-1}
denotes the vector
(\widehat{R}_{1}^{-1},\widehat{R}_{2}^{-1})
. We are now in a position to state Vishe’s partition of the unit square.

Theorem 2.1
.

Let
Q\geq 1
. Then

\mathbb{T}^{2}=\bigsqcup_{\begin{subarray}{c}r\text{ monic}\\
|r|\leq\widehat{Q}\end{subarray}}\bigsqcup_{\begin{subarray}{c}d\mid r\text{ monic}\\
\underline{c}\in\mathcal{O}^{2}\text{ primitive}\\
|r|\widehat{Q}^{-1/2}\leq|d\underline{c}|\leq|r|^{1/2}\\
|dc_{2}|<|r|^{1/2}\end{subarray}}\sideset{}{{}^{\prime}}{\bigsqcup}_{\begin{subarray}{c}|\underline{a}|<|r|\\
\underline{a}/r\in L(d\underline{c})\end{subarray}}B(\underline{a}/r,|r|^{-1}\widehat{Q}^{-1/2}),

where
\bigsqcup^{\prime}
indicates that we only consider vectors
\underline{a}
such that
(\underline{a},r)=1
.

A few remarks are in order to explain the conditions on the lines in the theorem. First, from a standard application of Dirichlet’s approximation theorem one obtains

(2.3)

\mathbb{T}^{k}=\bigcup_{\begin{subarray}{c}|r|\leq\widehat{Q}\\
r\text{ monic}\end{subarray}}\sideset{}{{}^{\prime}}{\bigcup}_{|\underline{a}|<|r|}B(\underline{a}/r,|r|^{-1}\widehat{Q}^{-1/k})

for any
Q>0
and
k\geq 1
. Furthermore, using the pigeon-hole principle one can show that that any
\underline{a}/r\in K^{2}
with
|\underline{a}|<|r|
and
(\underline{a},r)=1
lies on a line
L(d\underline{c})
with
|d\underline{c}|\leq|r|^{1/2}
and
|dc_{2}|<|r|^{1/2}
. It also clear from the definition (
2.2
) that we must have
d\mid r
. So the key condition in Theorem
2.1
is
|r|\widehat{Q}^{-1/2}\leq|d\underline{c}|
. This guarantees that

(i)

rational points on an individual line stay sufficiently far away from each other,

(ii)

rational points on distinct lines stay sufficiently far away from each other,

(iii)

distinct lines don’t intersect at rationals with small denominator.

With (i)–(iii) at hand it only remains to show that we can still cover
\mathbb{T}^{2}
with balls centered on rationals
\underline{a}/r
on lines
L(d\underline{c})
such that
|r|\widehat{Q}^{-1/2}\leq|d\underline{c}|
. This is a consequence of one-dimensional Diophantine approximation, where in fact (
2.3
) already provides an exact partition of the unit interval.

We will follow this blueprint closely to obtain an analogue of Theorem
2.1
that allows for lopsided boxes. This requires us to go through most of Vishe’s steps again, since we have to modify some of the proofs to get control over the distance between the individual coordinates of rational vectors. We begin with a two-dimensional version of Dirichlet’s approximation theorem with rectangles.

Lemma 2.2
.

let
R_{1}\geq R_{2}\geq 1
be integers. Then

\mathbb{T}^{2}=\bigcup_{\begin{subarray}{c}|r|\leq\widehat{R}_{1}\widehat{R}_{2}\\
r\text{ monic}\end{subarray}}\sideset{}{{}^{\prime}}{\bigcup}_{\begin{subarray}{c}|\underline{a}|<|r|\end{subarray}}R(\underline{a}/r,|r|^{-1}\underline{\widehat{R}}^{-1}).

Proof.

For any
\underline{x}\in\mathbb{T}^{2}
the rectangle
R(\underline{x},\underline{\widehat{R}}^{-1})
has volume
(\widehat{R}_{1}\widehat{R}_{2})^{-1}
. We can therefore write

(2.4)

\mathbb{T}^{2}=\bigsqcup_{i=1}^{\widehat{R}_{1}\widehat{R}_{2}}R(\underline{x}_{i},\underline{\widehat{R}}^{-1})

for some
\underline{x}_{i}\in\mathbb{T}^{2}
. Observe that there are
\widehat{R}_{1}\widehat{R}_{2}q
polynomials
r\in\mathcal{O}
with
|r|\leq\widehat{R}_{1}\widehat{R}_{2}
. In particular, if
\underline{x}\in\mathbb{T}^{2}
and
r
runs through all
r\in\mathcal{O}
with
|r|\leq\widehat{R}_{1}\widehat{R}_{2}
, then two of them, say
r_{1}\neq r_{2}
, must satisfy

\{r_{i}\underline{x}\}\in R(\underline{x}_{j},\underline{\widehat{R}}^{-1}),

for
i=1,2
and some
1\leq j\leq\widehat{R}_{1}\widehat{R}_{2}
, where
\{\cdot\}
denotes the fractional part. If we let
r=r_{1}-r_{2}
, then this implies

r\underline{x}-\underline{a}\in R(0,(\widehat{R}_{1}^{-1},\widehat{R}_{2}^{-2})),

where
\underline{a}
is the integer part of
(r_{1}-r_{2})\underline{x}
. We can divide through by
(\underline{a},r)
to ensure that
(\underline{a},r)=1
and also multiply by a unit if necessary to guarantee that
r
is monic.
∎

Next we show that every rational lies on a generalised line of suitable height. This is the analogue of
[
32
, Lemma 3.1]
, where the difference is that we allow the extra parameter
T
.

Lemma 2.3
.

Let
T\geq 1
. Then for any
\underline{a}/r\in\mathbb{T}^{2}
with
|\underline{a}|<|r|
and
(\underline{a},r)=1
, there exists
d\mid r
monic and
\underline{c}\in\mathcal{O}^{2}
primitive such that
|dc_{1}|\leq\widehat{T}|r|^{1/2}
,
|dc_{2}|<\widehat{T}^{-1}|r|^{1/2}
and
\underline{a}/r\in L(d\underline{c})
.

Proof.

The set

\{\underline{c}\in\mathcal{O}^{2}\colon|c_{1}|\leq\widehat{T}|r|^{1/2},|c_{2}|<\widehat{T}^{-1}|r|^{1/2}\}

has cardinality strictly bigger than
|r|
. In particular, there exists two distinct vectors
\underline{c}_{1},\underline{c}_{2}
in this set such that
\underline{c}_{1}\cdot\underline{a}\equiv\underline{c}_{2}\cdot\underline{a}\mod r
. Let
\underline{c}^{\prime}=\underline{c}_{1}-\underline{c}_{2}
. It then follows that
\underline{c}^{\prime}\cdot\underline{a}=k^{\prime}r
for some
k^{\prime}\in\mathcal{O}
. Now let
d^{\prime}=\gcd(c_{1},c_{2})
,
d=d^{\prime}/\gcd(d^{\prime},k^{\prime})
,
\underline{c}=\underline{c}^{\prime}/d^{\prime}
and
k=k^{\prime}/\gcd(d^{\prime},k^{\prime})
. We then have
d\underline{c}\cdot\underline{a}=kr
. Moreover, by construction
(d,k)=1
, which also implies
d\mid r
. We can further guarantee that
\underline{c}
is primitive and
d
monic by multiplying with a unit and changing
k
if necessary.
∎

For any
\underline{c}=(c_{1},c_{2})\in\mathcal{O}^{2}
we let
\underline{c}^{\bot}=(-c_{1},c_{1})
. We also need the following result, which is
[
32
, Lemma 3.5]
, about the distribution of rational points on an individual line.

Lemma 2.4
.

Let
d\in\mathcal{O}
be monic and
\underline{c}\in\mathcal{O}^{2}
be primitive. Then for every
\underline{a}/r\in L(\underline{c})
there exists a unique
a\in\mathcal{O}
with
|a|<|r|
,
(a,r)=1
and a unique
\underline{d}\in\mathcal{O}^{2}
with
|\underline{d}|<|\underline{c}|
such that
\underline{a}/r=\frac{a}{r}\underline{c}^{\bot}+\underline{d}
. Moreover, for every
\underline{a}/r\in L(d\underline{c})
there exists a unique
\underline{a}^{\prime}/(r/d)\in L(\underline{c})
with
|\underline{a}^{\prime}|<|r/d|
and a unique
\underline{d}^{\prime}\in\mathcal{O}^{2}
with
|\underline{d}|^{\prime}<|d|
such that
\underline{a}/r=\underline{a}^{\prime}/r+\underline{d}^{\prime}/d
.

Next we turn to studying the distance between rational points on lines of the form
L(\underline{c})
. The following result is the analogue of
[
32
, Lemma 3.6]
.

Lemma 2.5
.

Let
\underline{c}\in\mathcal{O}^{2}
be primitive and
\underline{a}/r\neq\underline{a}^{\prime}/r^{\prime}\in L(\underline{c})
. Then

\left|\frac{a_{i}}{r}-\frac{a_{i}^{\prime}}{r^{\prime}}\right|\geq\frac{|c_{i}^{\bot}|}{|rr^{\prime}|}\text{ for both }i=1,2\quad\text{or}\quad\max_{i=1,2}\left\{|c_{i}|\left|\frac{a_{i}}{r}-\frac{a_{i}^{\prime}}{r^{\prime}}\right|\right\}\geq 1.

The first case happens if
\underline{a}/r,\underline{a}^{\prime}/r^{\prime}\in L_{1}(\underline{c},k)
for some
k\in\mathcal{O}
. Moreover, if

\underline{a}/r
is an element of
L(\underline{c})\cap L_{1}(\underline{c},k)
with
|\underline{c}|\leq|r|^{2}
, there exists
\underline{b}/r_{1}\in L(\underline{c})\cap L_{1}(\underline{c},k)
such that
|r_{1}|=q|r|
and
|a_{i}/r-b_{i}/r_{1}|=|c_{i}^{\bot}|/|rr_{1}|
.

Proof.

We begin with the first part of the lemma. Since
\underline{a}/r,\underline{a}^{\prime}/r^{\prime}\in L(\underline{c})
, it follows from the definition of
L(\underline{c})
that there exist
k,k^{\prime}\in\mathcal{O}
such that
(\underline{a}/r-\underline{a}^{\prime}/r^{\prime})\cdot\underline{c}=k-k^{\prime}
. If
k\neq k^{\prime}
, then by the ultrametric property we have
\max_{i=1,2}{|a_{i}/r-a_{i}^{\prime}/r^{\prime}||c_{i}|}\geq 1
, which is sufficient. Thanks to Lemma
2.4
we can also write
\underline{a}/r=\frac{a}{r}\underline{c}^{\bot}+\underline{d}
and
\underline{a}^{\prime}/r^{\prime}=\frac{a^{\prime}}{r^{\prime}}\underline{c}^{\bot}+\underline{d}^{\prime}
. If
k=k^{\prime}
, then the conditions
|\underline{d}|,|\underline{d}^{\prime}|<|\underline{c}|
and the fact that
\underline{c}
is primitive imply that
\underline{d}=\underline{d}^{\prime}
. Therefore, we have
|a_{i}/r-a^{\prime}_{i}/r^{\prime}|=|a/r-a^{\prime}/r||c_{i}^{\bot}|\geq|c_{i}^{\bot}|/|rr^{\prime}|
. Furthermore, we have
k=k^{\prime}
if and only if
\underline{a}/r,\underline{a}^{\prime}/r^{\prime}\in L_{1}(\underline{c},k)
.

For the second part of the lemma, one can check that Vishe’s proof of
[
32
, Lemma 3.6]
in fact gives control over the distance of both coordinates of
\underline{a}/r-\underline{b}/r_{1}
. Moreover, he requires
|\underline{c}|^{2}\leq|r|
, but his proof shows that
|\underline{c}|\leq|r|^{2}
is in fact sufficient.

∎

We can also extend this result to arbitrary lines.

Lemma 2.6
.

Let
d\in\mathcal{O}
be monic and
\underline{c}\in\mathcal{O}^{2}
be primitive. Then for
\underline{a}/r\neq\underline{a}^{\prime}/r^{\prime}\in L(d\underline{c})
, at least one of the following must hold:

(i)

\left|\frac{a_{i}}{r}-\frac{a^{\prime}_{i}}{r^{\prime}}\right|\geq\frac{|dc_{i}^{\bot}|}{|rr^{\prime}|}\text{ for both }i=1,2
,

(ii)

\left|\frac{\underline{a}}{r}-\frac{\underline{a}^{\prime}}{r^{\prime}}\right|\geq\max\{|r|^{-1},|r^{\prime}|^{-1}\}
,

(iii)

\max_{i=1,2}\left\{|dc_{i}|\left|\frac{a_{i}}{r}-\frac{a^{\prime}_{i}}{r^{\prime}}\right|\right\}\geq 1.

Moreover, if
\underline{a}/r\in L_{1}(d\underline{c},k)
, then there exists
\underline{a}/r\neq\underline{b}/r_{2}\in L_{1}(d\underline{c},k)
such that

{|a_{i}/r-b_{i}/r_{2}|\leq|dc_{i}^{\bot}|/|rr_{2}|}

for both
i=1,2
.

Proof.

We begin with the first part of the statement. Recall from Lemma
2.4
that we can write
\underline{a}/r=\underline{a}_{1}/r+\underline{d}/d
and
\underline{a}^{\prime}/r^{\prime}=\underline{a}_{2}/r^{\prime}+\underline{d}^{\prime}/d
where
\underline{a}_{1}/(r/d),\underline{a}_{2}/(r^{\prime}/d)\in L(\underline{c})
and
\underline{d},\underline{d}^{\prime}\in\mathcal{O}^{2}
. We thus have

\left|\frac{a_{i}}{r}-\frac{a^{\prime}_{i}}{r^{\prime}}\right|=\left|\frac{a_{1,i}}{r}-\frac{a_{2,i}}{r^{\prime}}+\frac{d_{i}-d_{i}^{\prime}}{d}\right|

for
i=1,2
. If
\underline{d}\neq\underline{d}^{\prime}
, then this is clearly at least
1/|d|\geq\max\{|r|^{-1},|r^{\prime}|^{-1}\}
for one of
i=1,2
, since
d\mid r,r^{\prime}
. One the other hand, if
\underline{d}=\underline{d}^{\prime}
, we can use Lemma
2.5
: In its first case we obtain
|a_{1,i}/(r/d)-a_{2,i}/(r^{\prime}/d)|\geq|d^{2}||c_{i}^{\bot}|/|rr^{\prime}|
for both
i=1,2
, which implies

|a_{i}/r-a_{i}^{\prime}/r^{\prime}|\geq|dc_{i}^{\bot}|/|rr^{\prime}|\quad\text{for}\quad i=1,2,

whereas in the second case
\max_{i=1,2}\{|c_{i}||a_{1,i}/(r/d)-a_{2,i}/(r/d)|\}\geq 1
, which implies

\max_{i=1,2}|dc_{i}||a_{i}/r-a^{\prime}_{i}/r^{\prime}|\geq 1.

For the second part of the lemma, we use Lemma
2.4
to write
\underline{a}/r=\underline{b}^{\prime}/r+\underline{d}^{\prime}/d
, where
\underline{b}^{\prime}/(r/d)\in L_{1}(\underline{c},k)
for some
k\in\mathcal{O}
. It follows from the second part of Lemma
2.5
that there exists
\underline{b}^{\prime\prime}/r_{1}\in L_{1}(\underline{c},k)
with
|r_{1}|=q|r/d|
and
|b^{\prime}_{i}/(r/d)-b^{\prime\prime}_{i}/r_{1}|=|dc_{i}^{\bot}|/|rr_{1}|
for
i=1,2
. Now set
\underline{b}/r_{2}=\underline{b}^{\prime\prime}/(r_{1}d)+\underline{d}^{\prime}/d
. We then have
d\underline{c}\cdot\underline{b}/r_{2}=k+\underline{c}\cdot\underline{d}^{\prime}=d\underline{c}\cdot\underline{a}/r
, so that
\underline{b}/r_{2}\in L_{1}(d\underline{c},k)
. Moreover, we also have
|b_{i}/r_{2}-a_{i}/r|=|b_{i}^{\prime\prime}/r_{1}-b^{\prime}_{i}/(r/d)|/|d|=|c_{i}^{\bot}|/|rr_{1}|\leq|dc_{i}^{\bot}|/|rr_{2}|
, where we used that
|r_{2}|\leq|r_{1}d|
.
∎

We also need the following lemma, which is the second part of
[
32
, Lemma 3.9]
.

Lemma 2.7
.

Let
\underline{a}/r\in L(d\underline{c})\cap L(d^{\prime}\underline{c}^{\prime})
, where
d,d^{\prime}\in\mathcal{O}
are monic and
\underline{c},\underline{c}^{\prime}\in\mathcal{O}^{2}
are primitive. If
|c_{1}c_{2}^{\prime}|,|c_{2}c_{1}^{\prime}|<|r/(dd^{\prime})|
, then
d\underline{c}=d^{\prime}\underline{c}^{\prime}
.

Note that in
[
32
, Lemma 3.9]
the extra condition
|d\underline{c}|^{2},|d^{\prime}\underline{c}^{\prime}|^{2}\leq|r|
is required, but this is in fact not used in the proof.
The next lemma is concerned with the distance between rational points that lie on distinct lines.

Lemma 2.8
.

Let
\underline{a}/r\in L(d\underline{c})
and
\underline{a}^{\prime}/r^{\prime}\in L(d^{\prime}\underline{c}^{\prime})
with
d\underline{c}\neq d^{\prime}\underline{c}^{\prime}
,
|dd^{\prime}c_{1}c_{2}^{\prime}|<|rr^{\prime}|^{1/2}
and
|dd^{\prime}c_{2}c_{1}^{\prime}|<|rr^{\prime}|^{1/2}
.
Then we have
|a_{i}/r-a^{\prime}_{i}/r^{\prime}|\geq|dc_{i}r^{\prime}|^{-1}
for one of
i=1,2
and
|a_{i}/r-a^{\prime}_{i}/r^{\prime}|\geq|d^{\prime}c^{\prime}_{i}r|^{-1}
for one of
i=1,2
.

Proof.

First note that
\underline{a}/r
and
\underline{a}^{\prime}/r^{\prime}
must be distinct by Lemma
2.7
. By the second part of Lemma
2.6
there exists
\underline{b}/r_{1}\in L(d\underline{c})
such that
|a_{i}/r-b_{i}/r_{1}|\leq|dc_{i}^{\bot}|/|rr_{1}|
. Let

C=\begin{pmatrix}\underline{a}/r-\underline{b}/r_{1}\\
\underline{a}/r-\underline{a}^{\prime}/r^{\prime}\end{pmatrix}.

Since
\underline{a}^{\prime}/r^{\prime}\not\in L(d\underline{c})
, it follows that
\det(C)\neq 0
. It is therefore clear that
|\det(C)|\geq|rr^{\prime}r_{1}|
. This implies that
|a_{i}/r-a^{\prime}_{i}/r^{\prime}|\geq 1/|dc_{i}r^{\prime}|
for one of
i=1,2
. Finally we can replace the role of
r
and
r^{\prime}
to obtain the second inequality of the statement.
∎

We now have all ingredients at hand to prove the main result of this section.

Theorem 2.9
.

Let
R_{1}\geq R_{2}\geq 1
be integers. If we set
T=({R}_{1}-{R}_{2})/2
, then

(2.5)

\mathbb{T}^{2}=\bigsqcup_{\begin{subarray}{c}|r|\leq\widehat{R}_{1}\widehat{R}_{2}\\
r\text{ monic}\end{subarray}}\bigsqcup_{\begin{subarray}{c}d\mid r\text{ monic}\\
\underline{c}\in\mathcal{O}^{2}\text{ primitive}\\
|dc_{1}|\leq\widehat{T}|r|^{1/2},|dc_{2}|<\widehat{T}^{-1}|r|^{1/2}\\
\max\{\widehat{R}_{i}|dc_{i}^{\bot}|\}\geq|r|\end{subarray}}\sideset{}{{}^{\prime}}{\bigsqcup}_{\begin{subarray}{c}|\underline{a}|<|r|\\
\underline{a}/r\in L(d\underline{c})\end{subarray}}R(\underline{a}/r,|r|^{-1}\underline{\widehat{R}}^{-1}).

Proof.

We first show the union on the right hand side of (
2.5
) is disjoint. Let
\underline{a}/r\neq\underline{a}^{\prime}/r^{\prime}
appear on the right hand side of (
2.5
) and suppose
|r^{\prime}|\geq|r|
. We now have to distinguish a few cases. First, if
\underline{a}/r,\underline{a}^{\prime}/r^{\prime}\in L(d\underline{c})
for some
L(d\underline{c})
appearing on the right hand side of (
2.5
), then in case (i) of Lemma
2.6
we have

\displaystyle\left|\frac{a_{i}}{r}-\frac{a_{i}^{\prime}}{r^{\prime}}\right|\geq\frac{|dc_{i}^{\bot}|}{|rr^{\prime}|}\geq\frac{1}{\widehat{R}_{i}|r|}

for one of
i=1,2
, where we used that
|dc_{i}^{\bot}|\geq\widehat{R}_{i}^{-1}|r^{\prime}|
for one of
i=1,2
. This is clearly sufficient to show
R(\underline{a}/r,|r|^{-1}\underline{\widehat{R}}^{-1})
is disjoint from
R(\underline{a^{\prime}}/r^{\prime},|r^{\prime}|^{-1}\underline{\widehat{R}}^{-1})
. On the other hand, case (ii) of Lemma
2.6
yields
\left|\underline{a}/r-\underline{a}^{\prime}/r^{\prime}\right|\geq\max\{|r|^{-1},|r^{\prime}|^{-1}\}
. However, then the rectangles around
\underline{a}/r
and
\underline{a}^{\prime}/r^{\prime}
must be disjoint since
\widehat{R}_{1}\geq\widehat{R}_{2}\geq 1
. If case (iii) in Lemma
2.6
holds for
i=1
, then
dc_{1}\neq 0
and

\displaystyle\left|\frac{a_{1}}{r}-\frac{a_{1}^{\prime}}{r^{\prime}}\right|\geq\frac{1}{|dc_{1}|}\geq\frac{\widehat{R}_{2}|dc_{1}|}{\widehat{R}_{1}|r|}\geq\frac{1}{\widehat{R}_{1}|r|}.

A similar calculation shows that if the inequality holds for
i=2
, then we obtain
|a_{2}/r-a_{2}^{\prime}/r^{\prime}|\geq\widehat{R}_{2}^{-1}|r|^{-1}
. This finishes the case
\underline{a}/r,\underline{a}^{\prime}/r^{\prime}\in L(d\underline{c})
. Next we are concerned about the case
\underline{a}/r\in L(d\underline{c})
,
\underline{a}^{\prime}/r^{\prime}\in L(d^{\prime}\underline{c}^{\prime})
with
d\underline{c}\neq d^{\prime}\underline{c}^{\prime}
. Our constraints on
d\underline{c},d^{\prime}\underline{c}^{\prime}
guarantee that the requirements in Lemma
2.8
are met, and so we get
|a_{i}/r-a_{i}/r^{\prime}|\geq|dc_{i}r|^{-1}
for one of
i=1,2
. Note that
|dc_{1}|\leq(\widehat{R}_{1}/\widehat{R}_{2})^{1/2}|r|^{1/2}\leq\widehat{R}_{1}
, since
|r|\leq\widehat{R}_{1}\widehat{R}_{2}
. Similarly we get
|dc_{2}|\leq\widehat{R}_{2}
, so that
|a_{i}/r-a_{i}/r^{\prime}|\geq|dc_{i}r|^{-1}\geq\widehat{R}_{i}^{-1}|r|^{-1}
, which is sufficient. Finally, it remains to show that every rational
\underline{a}/r
in the right hand side of (
2.5
) appears precisely once. This is a consequence of Lemma
2.7
. Therefore, we have established that the right hand side of (
2.5
) is a disjoint union.

Now we show that if
\underline{a}/r\in\mathbb{T}^{2}
is a rational with
|\underline{a}|<|r|\leq\widehat{R}_{1}\widehat{R}_{2}
and
(\underline{a},r)=1
, then there exists a rational
\underline{a}^{\prime}/r^{\prime}
appearing on the right hand side of (
2.5
) such that
R(\underline{a}/r,|r|^{-1}\underline{\widehat{R}}^{-1})\subset R(\underline{a}^{\prime}/r^{\prime},|r^{\prime}|^{-1}\underline{\widehat{R}}^{-1})
. By Lemma
2.3
this is enough to show the equality of sets in (
2.5
). It follows from Lemma
2.3
that there exist
d\in\mathcal{O}
monic and
\underline{c}\in\mathcal{O}^{2}
primitive such that
d\mid r
,
\underline{a}/r\in L(d\underline{c})
and
|dc_{1}|\leq T|r|^{1/2}
,
|dc_{2}|<T^{-1}|r|^{1/2}
. If
\max\{\widehat{R}_{i}|dc_{i}^{\bot}|\}\geq|r|
we are done. Otherwise, let
M=\max\{\widehat{R}_{i}|c_{i}^{\bot}|\}
so that
M<|r|
. Lemma
2.4
allows to write
\underline{a}/r=\frac{a}{r}\underline{c}^{\bot}+\underline{d}/d
for some
a\in\mathcal{O}
and
\underline{d}\in\mathcal{O}^{2}
, where
a/(r/d)\in L(\underline{c})
. The one dimensional Dirichlet approximation theorem implies the existence of a rational
a^{\prime}/r_{1}
such that
|a^{\prime}|<|r_{1}|\leq M|d|^{-1}
,
(a^{\prime},r_{1})=1
and
|a/(r/d)-a^{\prime}/r_{1}|<|r_{1}|^{-1}|d|M^{-1}
. Now set
\underline{a}^{\prime}/r^{\prime}=\frac{a^{\prime}}{r_{1}d}\underline{c}^{\bot}+\underline{d}/d
. We then have
d\underline{c}\cdot\underline{a}^{\prime}/r^{\prime}=\underline{c}\cdot\underline{d}=d\underline{c}\cdot\underline{a}/r
, which implies
\underline{a}^{\prime}/r^{\prime}\in L(d\underline{c})
and
d\mid r^{\prime}
. Moreover, by construction we have
|r^{\prime}|\leq|dr_{1}|\leq M
. We also have
|a_{i}/r-a^{\prime}_{i}/r^{\prime}|=|d|^{-1}|a_{i}/(r/d)-a^{\prime}/r_{1}|<|r_{1}|^{-1}M^{-1}\leq|r^{\prime}|^{-1}\widehat{R}_{i}^{-1}
for both
i=1,2
. This completes the proof of Theorem
2.9
.

2.1
with

.

The following corollary will be useful when evaluating the main contribution to our asymptotic formula.

Corollary 2.10
.

Let
R_{1}\geq R_{2}\geq 1
be integers. Then

\displaystyle\bigsqcup_{\begin{subarray}{c}|r|\leq\widehat{R}_{2}\\
r\text{ monic}\end{subarray}}\bigsqcup_{\begin{subarray}{c}d\mid r\text{ monic}\\
\underline{c}\in\mathcal{O}^{2}\text{ primitive}\\
|dc_{1}|\leq\widehat{T}|r|^{1/2}\\
|dc_{2}|<\widehat{T}^{-1}|r|^{1/2}\end{subarray}}\sideset{}{{}^{\prime}}{\bigsqcup}_{\begin{subarray}{c}|\underline{a}|<|r|\\
\underline{a}/r\in L(d\underline{c})\end{subarray}}R(\underline{a}/r,|r|^{-1}\underline{\widehat{R}}^{-1})=\bigsqcup_{\begin{subarray}{c}|r|\leq\widehat{R}_{2}\\
r\text{ monic}\end{subarray}}\sideset{}{{}^{\prime}}{\bigsqcup}_{\begin{subarray}{c}|\underline{a}|<|r|\end{subarray}}R(\underline{a}/r,|r|^{-1}\underline{\widehat{R}}^{-1})

Proof.

Clearly the left hand side of the claimed equality is contained in the right hand side. Moreover, by Lemma
2.3
the right hand side is contained in the left hand side. Theorem
2.9
implies that the left hand side is disjoint, since the condition
\max\{\widehat{R}_{i}|dc_{i}^{\bot}|\}\geq|r|
is vacuously true for
|r|\leq\widehat{R}_{2}
. It remains to prove that the right hand side is disjoint. Suppose that
\underline{\alpha}\in R(\underline{a}_{1}/r_{1},|r_{1}|^{-1}\underline{\widehat{R}}^{-1})\cap R(\underline{a}_{2}/r_{2},|r_{2}|^{-1}\underline{\widehat{R}}^{-1})
with
\underline{a}_{1}/r_{1}\neq\underline{a}_{2}/r_{2}
. We then have

\frac{1}{|r_{1}r_{2}|}\leq\left|\frac{\underline{a}_{1}}{r_{1}}-\frac{\underline{a}_{2}}{r_{2}}\right|=\left|\left(\frac{\underline{a}_{1}}{r_{1}}-\underline{\alpha}\right)+\left(\underline{\alpha}-\frac{\underline{a}_{2}}{r_{2}}\right)\right|<\max\left\{\frac{1}{|r_{1}|\widehat{R}_{2}},\frac{1}{|r_{2}|\widehat{R}_{2}}\right\},

which is impossible since
|r_{1}|,|r_{2}|\leq\widehat{R}_{2}
.
∎

3.
Geometry

If the complete intersection
X\subset\mathbb{P}^{n-1}
is defined by a cubic form
F_{1}
and a quadratic form
F_{2}
, then it is also defined by
F_{1}+LF_{2}
and
F_{2}
for any linear form
L\in\mathcal{O}[x_{1},\dots,x_{n}]
. In particular, given the degree of freedom we have, it is reasonable to expect that we can define
X
as the intersection of a non-singular cubic hypersurface and a quadratic hypersurface. This is indeed the case as demonstrated in Lemmas 3.2–3.3 of
[
5
]
, whose proof we adjust to cope with positive characteristic.

Lemma 3.1
.

Let
X\subset\mathbb{P}^{n-1}
be a non-singular complete intersection of a cubic and a quadratic hypersurface over
K
. Then
X=V(F_{1},F_{2})
, where
F_{1}\in\mathcal{O}[x_{1},\dots,x_{n}]
is a non-singular cubic form and
F_{2}
is a quadratic form of rank at least
n-1
.

Proof.

Suppose
X=V(G_{1},G_{2})
, where
G_{1}
is a cubic and
G_{2}
a quadratic form respectively. For
U=\mathbb{P}^{n-1}\setminus V(G_{2})
define the morphism

\varphi\colon U\to\mathbb{P}^{n},\quad(x_{1}\colon\cdots\colon x_{n})\mapsto(G_{1}(\bm{x})\colon x_{1}G_{2}(\bm{x})\cdots\colon x_{n}G_{2}(\bm{x})).

Assume for a moment that there exists a hyperplane
H\subset\mathbb{P}^{n}
defined over
K
such that
\varphi^{-1}(H)
is smooth. This means that there exist
\lambda_{0},\dots,\lambda_{n}\in K
such that

F_{1}=\lambda_{0}G_{1}+\lambda_{1}x_{1}G_{2}+\cdots+\lambda_{n}x_{n}G_{n}

satisfies
U\cap\{F_{1}=0\}
is smooth. However,
U\cap\{F_{1}=0\}=\{F_{1}=0\}\setminus\{G_{2}=0\}
, from which it follows that
F_{1}
is non-singular since
X
is non-singular.

To prove the existence of the claimed
\lambda_{i}
’s, Browning–Dietmann–Heath-Brown appeal to Bertini’s Theorem, which does not hold in general in positive characteristic. However, it follows from work of Spreafico
[
30
, Corollary 4.3]
that the fiber above a general hyperplane
H\subset\mathbb{P}^{n}
is smooth provided the induced extension of residues fields
\kappa(x)/\kappa(\varphi(x))
is separable for any
x\in U
. Let
Y=V(F_{1}-x_{0}F_{2})\subset\mathbb{P}^{n}
. Then a little thought reveals that
\varphi
factors into

U\rightarrow Y\setminus V(F_{2})\rightarrow Y\rightarrow\mathbb{P}^{n},

where the first arrow is an isomorphism, the second an open embedding and the third a closed immersion. Both open embeddings and closed immersions are unramified, as is the composition of unramified morphisms. It follows that
\varphi
is unramified and hence all the residue field extensions are separable.

It remains to show that
G_{2}
has rank at least
n-1
. Aiming at a contradiction, suppose the opposite holds. This implies that
G_{2}
is singular along a line. However, this line will intersect
\{F_{1}=0\}
in a point that will then be a singular point of
X
, which is impossible.
∎

To deal with the exponential integrals appearing in our work, we will have to concentrate our weight function near a point such that linear combinations of its Hessian associated with
F_{1}
and the matrix underlying the quadratic form
F_{2}
always have large rank. This is only possible when
\cha(K)>3
and so we assume this holds for the rest of our work. Let us now fix a symmetric matrix
M\in\mathcal{O}^{n\times n}
such that
F_{2}(\bm{x})=\bm{x}^{t}M\bm{x}
. Moreover, for
\bm{x}\in K_{\infty}^{n}
we shall denote by
H(\bm{x})=(\frac{\partial F_{1}}{\partial x_{i}\partial x_{j}})_{1\leq i,j\leq n}
the Hessian of
F_{1}
evaluated at
\bm{x}
.

Lemma 3.2
.

For
1\leq k\leq n-1
, let
V_{k}
be the Zariski closure of

V^{\prime}_{k}\coloneqq\{\bm{x}\in\mathbb{A}^{n}\colon\text{rk}(t_{1}H(\bm{x})+t_{2}M)\leq k\text{ for some }(t_{1},t_{2})\in\mathbb{A}^{2}\setminus\{\bm{0}\}\}

inside
\mathbb{A}^{n}
. Then
\dim V_{k}\leq k+1
.

Proof.

For
1\leq k\leq n-1
consider the incidence correspondence

\displaystyle I

\displaystyle\coloneqq\{(\bm{x},\bm{y})\in\mathbb{A}^{n}\times\mathbb{A}^{n}\colon\text{rk}(H(\bm{x})\bm{y},M\bm{y})\leq 1\}.

Let
V
be an irreducible component of
V_{k}
. Since
V\times\{\bm{0}\}\subset I
and
V\times\{\bm{0}\}
is irreducible, there exists an irreducible component
W
of
I
containing
V\times\{\bm{0}\}
. Then the projection of
I
onto the first factor restricts to a surjective morphism
\psi\colon W\to V
of irreducible varieties. We can therefore apply Chevalley’s theorem
[
12
, Proposition 14.109]
to deduce the existence of an open dense subset
U\subset V
such that
\dim\psi^{-1}(x)=\dim W-\dim V
for all
x\in V
. Note that since
V^{\prime}_{k}
is dense in
V_{k}
and
V
is an irreducible component of
V_{k}
, we must have
U\cap V_{k}\neq\emptyset
. In addition, by the definition of
V_{k}^{\prime}
we have
\dim\psi^{-1}(x)\geq n-k
for all
x\in V_{k}^{\prime}
, so that we must also have
\dim\psi^{-1}(x)\geq n-k
for all
x\in U
.

Our next task is to bound the dimension of
I
. For this, let
\Delta\colon\mathbb{A}^{n}\to\mathbb{A}^{n}\times\mathbb{A}^{n}
the diagonal embedding, that is
\bm{x}\mapsto(\bm{x},\bm{x})
, and let
S=\Delta(\mathbb{A}^{n})\cap I
. If
\bm{x}\in S\cap V(F_{1})
, then there exists
(t_{1},t_{2})\in\mathbb{A}^{2}\setminus\{\bm{0}\}
such that
t_{1}\nabla F_{1}(\bm{x})+t_{2}\nabla F_{2}(\bm{x})=0
. Suppose first that
t_{2}=0
. It follows immediately from Euler’s identity that
F_{1}(\bm{x})=0
and hence
\bm{x}=\bm{0}
, because
F_{1}
is non-singular by assumption. On the other hand, if
t_{2}\neq 0
, then after taking the inner product with
\bm{x}
, we get
F_{2}(\bm{x})=0
, so that
\bm{x}
is a singular point on the affine cone of the non-singular complete intersection of
F_{1}
and
F_{2}
, which again implies
\bm{x}=\bm{0}
. Altogether we obtain
0\geq\dim S\cap V(F_{1})\geq\dim S+\dim V(F_{1})-n
and hence
\dim S\leq 1
.

Having established an upper bound for
\dim S
, we are now in a position to get control over
\dim I
. From what we have just shown, it follows that

1\geq\dim I\cap\Delta(\mathbb{A}^{n})\geq\dim I+\dim\mathbb{A}^{n}-2n=\dim I-n

and thus
\dim I\leq n+1
, from which we immediately deduce
\dim W\leq n+1
. Combining this with the information about the dimension of the fibers of
\psi
, we obtain for any
x\in U
the inequality
n-k\leq\dim\psi^{-1}(x)=\dim W-\dim V_{k}
and therefore
\dim V_{k}\leq k+1
as claimed.
∎

Corollary 3.3
.

There exists
\bm{x}_{0}\in K_{\infty}^{n}
such that
F_{1}(\bm{x}_{0})=F_{2}(\bm{x}_{0})=0
,
\text{rk}(H(\bm{x}_{0}))\geq n-2
and
\text{rk}(t_{1}H(\bm{x})+t_{2}M)\geq n-3
for all
(t_{1},t_{2})\in K_{\infty}^{2}\setminus\{\bm{0}\}
.

Proof.

Let
X^{\prime}\subset\mathbb{A}^{n}
be the affine cone of the non-singular complete intersection
X=V(F_{1},F_{2})\subset\mathbb{P}^{n-1}
. It follows from Lemma
3.2
that

is a Zariski closed subset in
\mathbb{A}^{n}
of dimension at most
n-3
. Similarly, Heath-Brown

[
16
, Lemma 2]
has shown that

W=\{\bm{x}\in\mathbb{A}^{n}\colon\rk(H(\bm{x}))\leq n-3\}

has dimension at most

. Since
X
is non-singular, it follows that
X^{\prime}(K_{\infty})
is Zariski dense in

. In particular, the fact that

implies that

is non-empty and any point contained therein satisfies the conditions required in the statement of the corollary.
∎

We also need strong upper bounds for the number of integral points on an affine hypersurface, which are a special case of

in the

Theorem 3.4

Let

whose degree

. Then there exists a constant

4.
Activation of the circle method

In this section we collect the remaining facts needed to get the circle method started. Recall from Lemma
3.1
that we can assume
X=V(F_{1},F_{2})
with
F_{1}\in\mathcal{O}[x_{1},\dots,x_{n}]
a non-singular cubic form and
F_{2}\in\mathcal{O}[x_{1},\dots,x_{n}]
a quadratic form of rank at least
n-1
. We shall fix such a choice of
F_{1}
and
F_{2}
once and for all and write
\underline{F}=(F_{1},F_{2})
. Moreover, we assume
M\in\text{Mat}_{n\times n}(\mathcal{O})
is a symmetric matrix such that
F_{2}(\bm{x})=\bm{x}^{t}M\bm{x}
.
In what follows, for
F\in K_{\infty}[x_{1},\dots,x_{n}]
we refer to the maximum of the absolute values of the coefficients of
F
as the height of
F
and denote it by
H_{F}
. We extend this definition to pairs of polynomials by
H_{\underline{G}}\coloneqq\max\{H_{G_{1}},H_{G_{2}}\}
.

Corollary
3.3
implies that there exists
\bm{x}_{0}\in K_{\infty}^{n}
such that

(4.1)

\displaystyle\begin{split}&F_{1}(\bm{x}_{0})=F_{2}(\bm{x}_{0})=0,\\
&\text{rk}(H(\bm{x}_{0}))\geq n-2\text{ and}\\
&\text{rk}(\gamma_{1}H(\bm{x}_{0})+\gamma_{2}M)\geq n-3\text{ for all }(\gamma_{1},\gamma_{2})\in K_{\infty}^{2}\setminus\{\bm{0}\},\end{split}

where
H(\bm{x}_{0})
denotes the Hessian of the cubic form
F_{1}
evaluated at
\bm{x}_{0}
. These properties are clearly invariant under scaling and so we may additionally assume
|\bm{x}_{0}|<H_{\underline{F}}^{-1}
. We will then work with the weight function
w\colon K_{\infty}^{n}\to\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}
defined by

(4.2)

w(\bm{x})\coloneqq\chi_{\mathbb{T}}(t^{L}(\bm{x}-\bm{x}_{0})),

where
L
is a large but fixed integer, whose exact value will be determined throughout our work. The non-archimedean nature of
K_{\infty}
ensures that
\text{rk}(H(\bm{x}))\geq n-2
and
|\bm{x}|<1/H_{\underline{F}}
whenever
w(\bm{x})\neq 0
and
L
is sufficiently large. Moreover, we have seen in the proof of Corollary
3.3
that the set of points
\bm{x}\in K_{\infty}^{n}
satisfying
\rk(\gamma_{1}H(\bm{x})+\gamma_{2}M)\geq n-3
for all
(\gamma_{1},\gamma_{2})\in K_{\infty}^{2}\setminus\{\bm{0}\}
is Zariski dense in
K_{\infty}^{n}
. In particular, if
L
is large enough, we can guarantee that any
\bm{x}\in\supp(w)
satisfies the third property in (
4.1
). Let us now fix
\bm{b}\in\mathcal{O}^{n}
and
N\in\mathcal{O}
such that
N\mid F_{1}(\bm{b}),F_{2}(\bm{b})
. The counting function we consider is now given by

N(P)\coloneqq\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\bm{x}\in\mathcal{O}^{n}\\
F_{1}(\bm{x})=F_{2}(\bm{x})=0\\
\bm{x}\equiv\bm{b}\>(N)\end{subarray}}w\left(\frac{\bm{x}}{t^{P}}\right).

Any
\alpha\in K_{\infty}
can be written as a Laurent series
\alpha=\sum\alpha_{i}t^{i}
, where
\alpha_{i}\neq 0
for only finitely many
i>0
. We can now define a character

\psi\colon K_{\infty}\to\mathbb{C}^{\times},\quad\alpha\mapsto e_{p}\left(\Tr_{\mathbb{F}_{q}/\mathbb{F}_{p}}(\alpha_{-1})\right),

where as usual
e_{p}(\cdot)=\exp(2\pi i \cdot/p)
. The starting point for the circle method is the orthogonality relation

(4.3)

\int_{\mathbb{T}}\psi(\alpha x)\differential\alpha=\begin{cases}1&\text{if }x=0,\\
0&\text{else,}\end{cases}

for any
x\in\mathcal{O}
as proved in
[
3
, Corollary 5.6]
. Combined with Theorem
2.9
this immediately implies

(4.4)

N(P)=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}|r|\leq\widehat{R}_{1}\widehat{R}_{2}\\
r\text{ monic}\end{subarray}}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}d\mid r\text{ monic}\\
\underline{c}\in\mathcal{O}^{2}\text{ primitive}\\
|dc_{1}|\leq\widehat{T}|r|^{1/2},|dc_{2}|<\widehat{T}^{-1}|r|^{1/2}\\
\max\{\widehat{R}_{i}|dc_{i}^{\bot}|\}\geq|r|\end{subarray}}\sideset{}{{}^{\prime}}{\sum}_{\begin{subarray}{c}|\underline{a}|<|r|\\
\underline{a}/r\in L(d\underline{c})\end{subarray}}\int_{|\theta_{1}|<|r|^{-1}\widehat{R}_{1}^{-1}}\int_{|\theta_{2}|<|r|^{-1}\widehat{R}_{2}^{-1}}S\left(\frac{\underline{a}}{r}+\underline{\theta}\right)\differential\theta_{2}\differential\theta_{1},

where

(4.5)

\bm{x}\equiv\bm{b}\>(N)\end{subarray}}\psi(\alpha_{1}F_{1}(\bm{x})+\alpha_{2}F_{2}(\bm{x}))w(\bm{x}/t^{P}),

for

and
\sum^{\prime}
indicates the condition

and transform (

\underline{c}\in\mathcal{O}^{2}\text{ primitive}\\

where

,

(4.7)

S_{d\underline{c},r,\bm{b},N}(\bm{v})\coloneqq\sideset{}{{}^{\prime}}{\sum}_{\underline{a}/r\in L(d\underline{c})}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}|\bm{x}|<|r_{M}|\\

and

(4.8)

I_{s}(\underline{\theta},\bm{v})\coloneqq\int_{K_{\infty}^{n}}w(\bm{x})\psi\left(t^{3P}\theta_{1}F_{1}(\bm{x})+t^{2P}\theta_{2}F_{2}(\bm{x})+\frac{t^{P}\bm{v}\cdot\bm{x}}{s}\right)\differential\bm{x}

for

.

In our work we will assume throughout that

\widehat{R}_{1}\asymp\widehat{P}^{4/3}\quad\text{and}\quad\widehat{R}_{2}\asymp\widehat{P}^{1/3},

. Let us now separate the terms from (

(1)

\displaystyle\ll\widehat{P}^{n-9}\sum_{|r|\leq\widehat{R}_{1}\widehat{R}_{2}}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}|dc_{1}|\leq\widehat{T}|r|^{1/2}\\

Lemma 4.1

Let

and

Remark
.

Lee states Lemma 4.3.6 without the appearance of the quadratic form that features in the definition of

We now wish to apply Lemma

. However, recall from

\displaystyle\ll\sum_{|r|=\widehat{Y}}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}|d|\leq\widehat{Y}\\

and (

5.
Exponential integrals

To get control over
I_{s}(\underline{\theta},\bm{v})
, we consider for
\bm{w}\in K_{\infty}^{n}
,
\underline{\gamma}\in K_{\infty}^{2}
and
G_{1},G_{2}\in K_{\infty}[x_{1},\dots,x_{n}]
the following oscillatory integral

J_{\underline{G}}(\underline{\gamma},\bm{w})\coloneqq\int_{\mathbb{T}^{n}}\psi\left(\underline{\gamma}\cdot\underline{G}(\bm{x})+\bm{w}\cdot\bm{x}\right)\differential\bm{x},

where we henceforth adopt the notation
\underline{\gamma}\cdot\underline{G}(\bm{x})=\gamma_{1}G_{1}(\bm{x})+\gamma_{2}G_{2}(\bm{x})
. The main ingredients to deal with the exponential integrals appearing in our work are
[
32
, Lemma 2.1–2.2]
, which we recall here for our convenience.

Lemma 5.1
.

We have
J_{\underline{G}}(\underline{\gamma},\bm{w})=0
if
|\bm{w}|>\max\{1,|\gamma_{1}|H_{G_{1}},|\gamma_{2}|H_{G_{2}}\}
.

Lemma 5.2
.

Let
\Omega=\{\bm{x}\in\mathbb{T}^{n}\colon|\gamma_{1}\nabla G_{1}(\bm{x})+\gamma_{2}\nabla G_{2}(\bm{x})+\bm{w}|\leq H_{\underline{G}}\max\{1,|\underline{\gamma}|^{1/2}\}\}
. Then

J_{\underline{G}}(\underline{\gamma},\bm{w})=\int_{\Omega}\psi\left(\underline{\gamma}\cdot\underline{G}(\bm{x})+\bm{w}\cdot\bm{x}\right)\differential\bm{x}.

Moreover, our analysis of the singular integral appearing in the main term of the asymptotic formula for
N(P)
makes use of Lemma 5.5 in
[
3
]
.

Lemma 5.3
.

Let
x\in\mathbb{T}
and
Z\geq 1
. Then

\int_{|\theta|<\widehat{Z}^{-1}}\psi(x\theta)\differential\theta=\begin{cases}\widehat{Z}^{-1}&\text{if }|x|<\widehat{Z}\\
0&\text{else.}\end{cases}

To begin our treatment of
I_{s}(\underline{\theta},\bm{v})
, note that by the definition of
w
in (
4.2
) we have

(5.1)

\displaystyle\begin{split}I_{s}(\underline{\theta},\bm{v})&=\widehat{L}^{-n}\psi\left(\frac{t^{p}\bm{v}\cdot\bm{x}_{0}}{s}\right)\int_{\mathbb{T}^{n}}\psi\left(t^{3P}\theta_{1}G_{1}(\bm{y})+t^{2P}\theta_{2}G_{2}(\bm{y})+\frac{t^{p}\bm{v}\cdot\bm{y}}{t^{L}s}\right)\differential\underline{\theta}\\
&=\widehat{L}^{-n}\psi\left(\frac{t^{p}\bm{v}\cdot\bm{x}_{0}}{s}\right)J_{\underline{G}}(t^{3P}\theta_{1},t^{2P}\theta_{2},(t^{P}\bm{v}t^{-L}/s)),\end{split}

where
G_{i}(\bm{y})=F_{i}(\bm{x}_{0}+t^{-L}\bm{y})
for
i=1,2
and we applied the change of variables
\bm{y}=t^{L}(\bm{x}-\bm{x}_{0})
. It is clear that
G_{i}
is a polynomial with coefficients in
K_{\infty}
and
H_{\underline{G}}\leq H_{\underline{F}}
. Therefore, it follows from Lemma
5.1
that

(5.2)

I_{s}(\underline{\theta},\bm{v})=0\quad\text{if}\quad|\bm{v}|>\widehat{L}H_{\underline{F}}|s|\frac{\max\{1,\widehat{P}^{3}|\theta_{1}|,\widehat{P}^{2}|\theta_{2}|\}}{\widehat{P}}.

Before we can derive upper bounds for
I_{s}(\underline{\theta},v)
from Lemma
5.2
, we need a preliminary step.

Lemma 5.4
.

Let
C\subset K^{2}_{\infty}
be compact and bounded away from
\bm{0}
. If we define
A(\underline{\gamma},\bm{x})
to be the maximum of the absolute values of the
(n-3)\times(n-3)
-minors of
\gamma_{1}H(\bm{x})+\gamma_{2}M
, then

A(\underline{\gamma},\bm{x})\gg_{C,w,\underline{F}}1

for
\underline{\gamma}\in C
and
\bm{x}\in\supp(w)
.

Proof.

Suppose by contradiction that the statement of the lemma is false. Then there exists a sequence
(\underline{\gamma}_{k},\bm{x}_{k})\in C\times\supp(w)
such that
A(\underline{\gamma}_{k},\bm{x}_{k})\leq 1/k
for all
k\geq 1
. Since
C\times\supp(w)
is compact, we can pass to a convergent subsequence with limit
(\underline{\gamma}^{\prime},\bm{x}^{\prime})\in C\times\supp(w)
. However, since the map
(\underline{\gamma},\bm{x})\mapsto A(\underline{\gamma},\bm{x})
is continuous, this implies that every
(n-3)\times(n-3)
minor of
\gamma^{\prime}_{1}H(\bm{x}^{\prime})+\gamma^{\prime}_{2}M
vanishes. Therefore,
\text{rk}(\gamma^{\prime}_{1}H(\bm{x}^{\prime})+\gamma^{\prime}_{2}M)\leq n-4
, which is a contradiction since any
\bm{x}\in\supp(w)
satisfies the third condition in
(
4.1
)
.
∎

When
\underline{\Theta}=(\Theta_{1},\Theta_{2})\in\mathbb{Z}^{2}
, then we shall henceforth adopt the convention that
|\underline{\gamma}|=\underline{\widehat{\Theta}}
means
|\theta_{1}|=\widehat{\Theta}_{1}
and
|\theta_{2}|=\widehat{\Theta}_{2}
. We finally have all the ingredients at hand to provide an upper bound for an average of
I_{s}(\underline{\theta},\bm{v})
over
\underline{\theta}
.

Proposition 5.5
.

Let
\underline{\Theta}\in\mathbb{Z}^{2}
,
\bm{v}\in\mathcal{O}^{n}
,

be monic and put
\widehat{Z}=\max\{1,\widehat{P}^{3}\widehat{\Theta}_{1},\widehat{P}^{2}\widehat{\Theta}_{2}\}
. Then if

6.
Exponential sums: Pointwise estimates

The aim of this section is to collect estimates for the complete exponential sums
S_{d\underline{c},r,\bm{b},N}(\bm{v})
defined in (
4.7
). These sums enjoy a twisted multiplicativity property, which essentially reduces the task of estimating them to the case of prime power moduli. For
r,R\in\mathcal{O}
, we adopt the notation

r\mid R^{\infty}

to mean that every prime divisor of
r
also divides
R
.

Lemma 6.1
.

Suppose
d\mid r
and
r=r_{1}r_{2}
with
(r_{1},r_{2})=1
. If we write
N=N_{1}N_{2}N_{3}
, where
N_{i}\mid r_{i}^{\infty}
for
i=1,2
and
(r,N_{3})=1
, and let
s_{i}=r_{i}N_{i}/(r_{i},N_{i})
for
i=1,2
, then there exist
\bm{b}^{\prime}\in(\mathcal{O}/N_{3}\mathcal{O})^{n}
and
t_{i}\in(\mathcal{O}/s_{i}\mathcal{O})^{\times}
for
i=1,2
such that

S_{d\underline{c},r,\bm{b},N}(\bm{v})=S_{d_{1}\underline{c},r_{1},\bm{b},N_{1}}(t_{1}\bm{v})S_{d_{2}\underline{c},r_{2},\bm{b},N_{2}}(t_{2}\bm{v})\psi\left(\frac{-\bm{v}\cdot\bm{b}^{\prime}}{N_{3}}\right).

where
d=d_{1}d_{2}
with
d_{i}\mid r_{i}
for
i=1,2
.

Proof.

By construction,
s_{1},s_{2}
and
N_{3}
are pairwise coprime so that
r_{N}=s_{1}s_{2}N_{3}
. In particular, if
\bm{y}_{i}
runs through a complete sets of residues modulo
s_{i}
for
i=1,2
and
\bm{y}_{3}
modulo
N_{3}
, then

\bm{x}=s_{2}N_{3}\bm{y}_{1}+s_{1}N_{3}\bm{y}_{2}+s_{1}s_{2}\bm{y}_{3}

constitutes a complete set of residues modulo
r
. Next, for
\underline{a}/r\in L(d\underline{c})
, we write
\underline{a}=r_{2}\underline{a}_{1}+r_{1}\underline{a}_{2}
, where
|\underline{a}_{i}|<|r_{i}|
and
(\underline{a}_{i},r_{i})=1
. It is then clear that

\psi\left(\frac{\underline{a}\cdot\underline{F}(\bm{x})}{r}\right)=\psi\left(\frac{\underline{a}_{1}\cdot\underline{F}(s_{2}N_{3}\bm{y_{1}})}{r_{1}}\right)\psi\left(\frac{\underline{a}_{2}\cdot\underline{F}(s_{1}N_{3}\bm{y}_{2})}{r_{2}}\right)

and

\psi\left(\frac{-\bm{v}\cdot\bm{x}}{r_{N}}\right)=\psi\left(\frac{-\bm{v}\cdot\bm{y}_{1}}{s_{1}}\right)\psi\left(\frac{-\bm{v}\cdot\bm{y}_{2}}{s_{2}}\right)\psi\left(\frac{-\bm{v}\cdot\bm{y}_{3}}{N_{3}}\right).

Moreover, it is demonstrated in the proof of Lemma 5.2 in
[
32
]
that
\underline{a}/r\in L(d\underline{c})
if and only if
\underline{a}_{i}/r_{i}\in L(d_{i}\underline{c})
. The result now follows after the change of variables
\bm{x}_{1}=s_{2}N_{3}\bm{y}_{1}
and
\bm{x}_{2}=s_{1}N_{3}\bm{y}_{2}
and taking
t_{1}\equiv(s_{2}N_{3})^{-1}\>(s_{1})
,
t_{2}\equiv(s_{1}N_{3})^{-1}\>(s_{2})
and
\bm{b}^{\prime}\equiv(s_{1}s_{2})^{-1}\bm{b}\>(s_{3})
.
∎

In some cases we will obtain estimates for the sums
S_{d\underline{c},r,\bm{b},N}(\bm{v})
by considering their relatives

(6.1)

T(\underline{a},r,\bm{v})\coloneqq\sum_{|\bm{x}|<|r|}\psi\left(\frac{a_{1}G_{1}(\bm{x})+a_{2}G_{2}(\bm{x})-\bm{v}\cdot\bm{x}}{r}\right)

for appropriate polynomials
G_{1},G_{2}\in\mathcal{O}[x_{1},\dots,x_{n}]
. These sums satisfy the following twisted multiplicativity property.

Lemma 6.2
.

Let
r=r_{1}r_{2}
with
(r_{1},r_{2})=1
. Then

T(\underline{a},r,\bm{v})=T(\underline{a}_{r_{2}},r_{1},\bm{v})T(\underline{a}_{r_{1}},r_{2},\bm{v}),

where
\underline{a}_{s}\coloneqq(s^{2}a_{1},sa_{2})
for

.

Proof.

As
\bm{x}_{i}
runs over a full set of residues mod
r_{i}
,
\bm{x}=r_{2}\bm{x}_{1}+r_{1}\bm{x}_{2}
runs over a full set of residues mod
r
. Moreover, using Taylor’s formula it is easy to see that

\psi\left(\frac{a_{i}F_{i}(\bm{x})}{r}\right)=\psi\left(\frac{a_{i}r_{2}^{4-i}F_{i}(\bm{x}_{1})}{r_{1}}\right)\psi\left(\frac{a_{i}r_{1}^{4-i}F_{i}(\bm{x}_{2})}{r_{2}}\right)

for
i=1,2
and

\psi\left(\frac{-\bm{v}\cdot\bm{x}}{r}\right)=\psi\left(\frac{-\bm{v}\cdot\bm{x}_{1}}{r_{1}}\right)\psi\left(\frac{-\bm{v}\cdot\bm{x}_{2}}{r_{2}}\right),

from which the statement of the lemma follows.
∎

For our investigation we shall also need a good understanding of the distribution of rational points
\underline{a}/r
on an individual line
L(d\underline{c})
when
r
is fixed. By Lemma

it suffices to consider the case
r=\varpi^{k}
and
d=\varpi^{m}
with

. The following lemma summarises the content of equations (6.9)–(6.11) of

.

Lemma 6.3
.

If
1\leq m<k
, then modulo

we have the following equality of sets

\displaystyle\{\underline{a}\colon\underline{a}/\varpi^{k}\in L(\varpi^{m}\underline{c})\}=

\displaystyle\{a\underline{c}^{\bot}+\varpi^{k-m}\underline{d}\colon|a|<|\varpi|^{k-m},(a,\varpi)=1,|\underline{d}|<|\varpi|^{m}\}\setminus

\displaystyle\{a\underline{c}^{\bot}+\varpi^{k-m+1}\underline{d}\colon|a|<|\varpi|^{k-m+1},|\underline{d}|<|\varpi|^{m-1},(a,\varpi)=1\}

and for

\displaystyle\{\underline{a}\colon\underline{a}/\varpi^{k}\in L(\varpi^{k}\underline{c})\}=

\displaystyle\{\underline{d}\colon(\underline{d},\varpi)=1,|\underline{d}|<|\varpi|^{k}\}\setminus

\displaystyle\{a\underline{c}^{\bot}+\varpi\underline{d}\colon(a,\varpi)=1,|a|<|\varpi|,|\underline{d}|<|\varpi|^{k-1}\}.

Moreover, when

In particular, we have

.

6.1.
Square-free moduli

We will now deal with
S_{d\underline{c},r,\bm{b},N}(\bm{v})
when
r
is square-free. A key player in our estimates is the dual form
F_{1}^{*}\in\mathcal{O}[x_{1},\dots,x_{n}]
, whose zero locus parameterises hyperplanes that have singular intersection with the projective hypersurface defined by
F
. It is well known that
F_{1}^{*}
is absolutely irreducible and of degree
3\times 2^{n-2}
.
We begin our treatment by assuming that
d=1
. In this case Lemma
6.3
tells us that
S_{\underline{c},\varpi,\bm{0},1}(\bm{v})
equals the familiar exponential sum

S_{\varpi}(\bm{v})\coloneqq\sideset{}{{}^{\prime}}{\sum}_{a\>(\varpi)}\sum_{\bm{x}\>(\varpi)}\psi\left(\frac{aF_{\underline{c}}(\bm{x})-\bm{v}\cdot\bm{x}}{\varpi}\right),

where
F_{\underline{c}}(\bm{x})=-c_{2}F_{1}(\bm{x})+c_{1}F_{2}(\bm{x})
. Let
\mathbb{F}_{\varpi}=\mathcal{O}/\varpi\mathcal{O}
be the residue field of
\varpi
. Our main ingredient is the following special case of a result due to Katz
[
18
, Theorem 4]
.

Theorem 6.4
.

Let
X\subset\mathbb{P}^{n}_{\mathbb{F}_{\varpi}}
be a complete intersection of dimension
r
defined by forms of degrees
d_{1},\dots,d_{n-r}
and let
L,H\in H^{0}(\mathbb{P}^{n},O_{\mathbb{P}^{n}}(1))
. If the following conditions are met:

(i)

X\cap L\cap H
has dimension
r-2

(ii)

the singular locus of
X\cap L
has dimension
\varepsilon

(iii)

the singular locus of
X\cap L\cap H
has dimension
\delta\geq\varepsilon
,

then there exists a constant
C>0
depending only on
n,d_{1},\dots,d_{n-r}
such that for
f=H/L
it holds that

\sum_{\bm{x}\in X[1/L]}\psi\left(\frac{f(\bm{x})}{\varpi}\right)\leq C|\varpi|^{(r+1+\delta)/2},

where
X[1/L]
is the affine variety defined as the complement of the hyperplane cut out by
L
in
X
.

Remark
.

Katz states Theorem 4 for arbitrary closed subvarieties of projective space that are geometrically integral or equidimensional and Cohen-Macualy. However, our assumption that
X
is a complete intersection implies that
X
is Cohen-Macauly and equidimensional, thereby allowing us to state the simplified version above.

Suppose that
\varpi\nmid\bm{v}c_{2}
. Using orthogonality of characters we see that

S_{\varpi}(\bm{v})=|\varpi|\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\bm{x}\in\mathbb{F}_{\varpi}^{n}\\
F_{\underline{c}}(\bm{x})=0\end{subarray}}\psi\left(\frac{-\bm{v}\cdot\bm{x}}{\varpi}\right).

Let
F(x_{0},\bm{x})\in\mathcal{O}[x_{0},x_{1},\dots,x_{n}]
be the homogenization of
F_{\underline{c}}
, that is

F(x_{0},\bm{x})=-c_{2}F_{1}(\bm{x})+x_{0}c_{1}F_{2}(\bm{x}),

and define
X=V(F)\subset\mathbb{P}_{\mathbb{F}_{\varpi}}^{n}
to be the projective variety cut out by the reduction of
F
modulo
\varpi
. Note that the point
(1,0,\dots,0)
will always be a singularity of
X
.
Moreover, we also set
L(x_{0},\bm{x})=x_{0}
and
H(\bm{x})=-\bm{v}\cdot\bm{x}
. In our situation we thus have
X\cap L=V(F_{1})
and
X\cap L\cap H=V(F_{1},\bm{v}\cdot\bm{x})
. In particular,
\delta=\varepsilon=-1
provided
\varpi\nmid\Delta_{F_{1}}F_{1}^{*}(\bm{v})
, where
\Delta_{F_{1}}
is the discriminant of
F_{1}
. Indeed, the condition
\varpi\nmid\Delta_{F_{1}}
guarantees that the reduction of
F_{1}
modulo
\varpi
is non-singular and
\varpi\nmid F_{1}^{*}(\bm{v})
implies that
V(F_{1},\bm{v}\cdot\bm{x})\subset\mathbb{P}^{n-1}_{\mathbb{F}_{\varpi}}
is non-singular.
We can thus apply Theorem
6.4
with

to deduce that

|\varpi|^{-1}|S_{\varpi}(\bm{v})|\leq C|\varpi|^{(n-1)/2},

where
C
is a constant that only depends on the degrees of
F_{1}
and
F_{2}
and
n
. Absorbing the primes
\varpi\mid\Delta_{F_{1}}
into the constant and invoking Lemma

, we have thus established the following result.

Lemma 6.5

Suppose that

(r,F_{1}^{*}(\bm{v})c_{2})=1

for some
t_{i}\in(\mathcal{O}/r_{i}\mathcal{O})^{\times}
. After absorbing the primes
\varpi\mid\Delta_{F_{1}}
or for which the reduction of
F_{2}
has rank strictly less than
n-1
into the constant, it now follows from the estimates we just recorded and Lemma
6.3
that

\displaystyle S_{d_{1}\underline{c},r_{1},\bm{0},1}(t_{1}\bm{v})

\displaystyle=\sum_{\underline{a}/r\in L(d_{1}\underline{c})}T(\underline{a},r_{1},t_{1}\bm{v})\leq C^{\omega(r_{1})}|d_{1}||r_{1}|^{(n+3)/2},

for some constant
C>0
.

We can now estimate
S_{d_{2}\underline{c},r_{2},\bm{b},M}(t_{2}\bm{v})
trivially to arrive at the following result.

Lemma 6.6
.

Suppose
r
is square-free and
d\mid r
. Then there exists a constant
C>0
depending only on
\deg F_{1},F_{2},\Delta_{F_{1}}
and
N
such that

S_{d\underline{c},r,\bm{b},N}(\bm{v})\leq C^{\omega(r)}|d||r|^{(n+3)/2}.

Moreover, if
(c_{2},r)=1
, then

S_{\underline{c},r,\bm{0},1}(\bm{v})\leq C^{\omega(r)}|r|^{n/2+1}.

6.2.
Square-full moduli

To satisfactorily deal with square-full moduli, we begin with the case
r=\varpi^{2}
. Our main ingredient is the following result due to Heath-Brown
[
14
]
. It should be noted that Heath-Brown’s proves his result solely over the integers. However, it is a routine exercise and the required adaptions are minor to check that his argument holds over
\mathbb{F}_{q}[t]
as well. Let
F\in\mathcal{O}[x_{1},\dots,x_{n}]
be a polynomial of degree
d
with
\cha(\mathbb{F}_{q})>d
and suppose the reduction of the top degree part of
F
defines a smooth projective hypersurface modulo
\varpi
. Then it holds that

(6.4)

\sum_{\bm{x}\>(\varpi^{2})}\psi\left(\frac{F(\bm{x})}{\varpi^{2}}\right)\leq(d-1)^{n}|\varpi|^{n}.

After absorbing the contribution from the primes dividing
\Delta_{F_{1}}
into the constant and employing Lemma
6.3
, we arrive at the following estimate.

Lemma 6.7
.

If
\varpi\nmid a_{1}
, then

T(\underline{a},\varpi^{2},\bm{v})\ll_{\Delta_{F_{1}}}|\varpi|^{n}.

In particular, we also have

S_{\underline{c},\varpi^{2},\bm{0},1}(\bm{v})\ll_{\Delta_{F_{1}}}|\varpi|^{2+n}

provided
\varpi\nmid c_{2}
.

Since
F_{2}
is a quadratic form, there exists a matrix
G\in\GL_{n}(K)
with entries in
\mathcal{O}
such that after the change of variables
\bm{y}=G\bm{x}
one has

F_{2}(\bm{y})=\sum_{i=1}^{n}b_{i}y_{i}^{2}\quad\text{with }b_{2}\dots b_{n}\neq 0.

If
\varpi\mid\det(G)
, we may still locally diagonalise
F_{2}
with a matrix
G_{\varpi}\in\GL_{n}(K_{\varpi})
that has coefficients in
\mathcal{O}_{\varpi}
, so that after the change of variables
\bm{y}=G_{\varpi}\bm{x}
we have

F_{2}(\bm{y})=\sum_{i=1}^{n}b_{\varpi,i}y_{i}^{2}\quad\text{with }b_{\varpi,2}\cdots b_{\varpi,n}\neq 0.

Let us define

\Delta_{F_{2}}=\begin{cases}b_{1}\cdots b_{n}\prod_{\varpi\mid\det(G)}\varpi^{\nu_{\varpi}(b_{\varpi,1}\cdots b_{\varpi,n})}&\text{if }\rk(M)=n,\\
b_{2}\cdots b_{n}\prod_{\varpi\mid\det(G)}\varpi^{\nu_{\varpi}(b_{\varpi,2}\cdots b_{\varpi,n})}&\text{if }\rk(M)=n-1.\end{cases}

and

(6.5)

v_{\varpi}=\nu_{\varpi}(\Delta_{F_{2}}).

We then have the following result
[
5
, Lemma 6.2]
.

Lemma 6.8
.

Let
k\geq 2
and suppose
\varpi^{1+v_{\varpi}}\mid a_{1}
. Then for any
\bm{v}\in\mathcal{O}^{n}
we have

T(\underline{a},\varpi^{k},\bm{v})\ll_{F_{2}}|\varpi|^{(n+1)/2}.

Lemma 6.2 in
[
5
]
is again only proved for the analogous sum over the integers. Moreover, they assume that
F_{2}
is diagonal from the beginning, a difference we take care of by the diagonalisation process above. The proof goes through verbatim in our setting, and so we shall not repeat it here.

Corollary 6.9
.

Let
r\in\mathcal{O}
be such that
\varpi\mid r
implies
\varpi^{2}\parallel r
. Then there exists a constant
C>0
depending on
F_{1}
,
F_{2}
and
N
such that

S_{d\underline{c},r,\bm{b},N}(\bm{v})\leq C^{\omega(r)}|d||r|^{(n+3)/2}.

Proof.

Write
r=r_{1}r_{2}
with coprime
r_{1},r_{2}\in\mathcal{O}
such that
(r_{1},N)=1
and
r_{2}\mid N^{\infty}
. As in the proof of Lemma
6.6
it suffices to obtain and upper bound for the sum
S_{d_{1}\underline{c},r_{1},\bm{0},1}(t_{1}\bm{v})
, where
t_{1}\in(\mathcal{O}/r_{1}\mathcal{O})^{\times}
, and estimate the sum corresponding to
r_{2}
trivially. By definition we then have

S_{d_{1}\underline{c},r_{1},\bm{0},1}(t_{1}\bm{v})=\sum_{\underline{a}/r\in L(d_{1}\underline{c})}T(\underline{a},r_{1},t_{1}\bm{v}).

Using the mulitplicativity property recorded in Lemma
6.2
, we can now invoke Lemma
6.7
in conjunction with Lemma
6.8
to obtain
T(\underline{a},r_{1},t_{1}\bm{v})\leq C^{\omega(r_{1})}|r_{1}|^{(n+1)/2}
. Lemma
6.3
provides us with an upper bound for the number of
\underline{a}
’s such that
\underline{a}/r_{1}\in L(d_{1}\underline{c})
that completes the proof.
∎

6.3.
The case
c_{2}=0
.

We now consider separately the case
c_{2}=0
. This can only occur if
c_{1}=1
and so to ease notation, we write
\underline{c}_{0}\coloneqq(1,0)
. By Lemma
6.1
we can reduce to the case when
r=\varpi^{k}
and
d=\varpi^{m}
with
m\leq k
and we again begin our treatment assuming that
r=\varpi
. When
d=1
, Lemma
6.6
already provides sufficiently good upper bounds. However, when
d=\varpi
, we have to do better and establishing an estimate that is superior to Lemma
6.6
is our first goal.
For
k\geq 1
, let us define

\rho_{1}(\varpi^{k})=\#\{\bm{x}\>(\varpi)\colon F_{1}(\bm{x})\equiv F_{2}(\bm{x})\equiv 0\>(\varpi^{k})\}\quad\text{and}\quad\rho_{2}(\varpi^{k})=\#\{\bm{x}\>(\varpi)\colon F_{2}(\bm{x})\equiv 0\>(\varpi^{k})\}.

By Lemma
6.3
we have

\displaystyle S_{\varpi\underline{c}_{0},\varpi,\bm{0},1}(\bm{v})

\displaystyle=\sideset{}{{}^{\prime}}{\sum}_{a_{1}\>(\varpi)}\sum_{a_{2}\>(\varpi)}\sum_{\bm{x}\>(\varpi)}\psi\left(\frac{a_{1}F_{1}(\bm{x})+a_{2}F_{2}(\bm{x})-\bm{v}\cdot\bm{x}}{\varpi}\right)

\displaystyle=|\varpi|\Biggl(|\varpi|\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\bm{x}\>(\varpi)\\
F_{1}(\bm{x})\equiv F_{2}(\bm{x})\equiv 0\>(\varpi)\end{subarray}}\psi\left(\frac{-\bm{v}\cdot\bm{x}}{\varpi}\right)-\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\bm{x}\>(\varpi)\\
F_{2}(\bm{x})\equiv 0\>(\varpi)\end{subarray}}\psi\left(\frac{-\bm{v}\cdot\bm{x}}{\varpi}\right)\Biggr).

If
\varpi\mid\bm{v}
, then the expression above simplifies to

S_{\varpi\underline{c}_{0},\varpi,\bm{0},1}(\bm{v})=|\varpi|(|\varpi|\rho_{1}(\varpi)-\rho_{2}(\varpi)).

Since the reduction of
X
modulo
\varpi
is non-singular for
|\varpi|
sufficiently large, we have

\rho_{1}(\varpi)=|\varpi|^{n-2}+O\left(|\varpi|^{(n-1)/2}\right),

as follows for example from Equation (3.12) in
[
2
]
.
Moreover, because
F_{2}
has rank at least
n-1
, it holds that

\rho_{2}(\varpi)=|\varpi|^{n-1}+O\left(|\varpi|^{(n+1)/2}\right).

So that in total we have

(6.6)

S_{\varpi\underline{c}_{0},\varpi,\bm{0},1}(\bm{v})\ll|\varpi|^{(n+3)/2}

whenever
\varpi\mid\bm{v}
, where the implied constant only depends on
F_{1}
and
F_{2}
. Let us now deal with the opposite case
\varpi\nmid\bm{v}
. We want to apply Theorem
6.4
to our situation. For this, we define
X^{\prime}_{\varpi}=V(F_{1},F_{2})\subset\mathbb{P}^{n}=\text{Proj}(\mathbb{F}_{\varpi}[x_{0},x_{1},\dots,x_{n}])
. In addition, we set
L(x_{0},\bm{x})=x_{0}
and
H(x_{0},\bm{x})=-\bm{v}\cdot\bm{x}
. Provided
|\varpi|
is sufficiently large,
X^{\prime}_{\varpi}
is a complete intersection of dimension
n-2
with the only singularity at
(1:0:\cdots:0)\in\mathbb{P}^{n}
. Moreover, we have
X^{\prime}_{\varpi}\cap L=V(F_{1},F_{2})\subset\mathbb{P}^{n-1}
, which is non-singular. It follows from a result of Zak and Fulton–Lazarsfeld
[
11
, Remark 7.5]
that
X^{\prime}_{\varpi}\cap H\cap L=V(F_{1},F_{1})\cap H\subset\mathbb{P}^{n-1}
has at worst isolated singularities, so that in the notation of Theorem
6.4
we have
\varepsilon=-1
and
\delta\leq 0
. In particular,

\displaystyle\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\bm{x}\>(\varpi)\\
F_{1}(\bm{x})\equiv F_{2}(\bm{x})\equiv 0\>(\varpi)\end{subarray}}\psi\left(\frac{-\bm{v}\cdot\bm{x}}{\varpi}\right)\ll|\varpi|^{(n-1)/2}.

Combining this with (
6.3
), we infer

\displaystyle S_{\varpi\underline{c}_{0},\varpi,\bm{0},1}(\varpi)

\displaystyle=|\varpi|^{2}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\bm{x}\>(\varpi)\\
F_{1}(\bm{x})\equiv F_{2}(\bm{x})\equiv 0\>(\varpi)\end{subarray}}\psi\left(\frac{-\bm{v}\cdot{x}}{\varpi}\right)-\sum_{a_{2}\>(\varpi)}\sum_{\bm{x}\>(\varpi)}\psi\left(\frac{a_{2}F_{2}(\bm{x})-\bm{v}\cdot\bm{x}}{\varpi}\right)

\displaystyle\ll|\varpi|^{(n+3)/2}+|\varpi|^{(n+3)/2}.

Estimating the contribution from the primes
\varpi\mid N
trivially, and using Lemma
6.1
, it thus follows from (
6.6
) that

(6.7)

|S_{d\underline{c}_{0},d,\bm{b},N}(\bm{v})|\leq C^{\omega(d)}|d|^{(n+3)/2}

for some constant
C>0
that only depends on
F_{1},F_{2}
and
N
.

We also require strong upper bounds for the sums

S_{1}=S_{\varpi\underline{c}_{0},\varpi^{2},\bm{0},1}(\bm{v})\quad\text{and}\quad S_{2}=S_{\varpi^{2}\underline{c}_{0},\varpi^{2},\bm{0},1}(\bm{v}).

Let us begin with the former. In this case Lemma
6.3
implies

\displaystyle S_{1}

\displaystyle=\sideset{}{{}^{\prime}}{\sum}_{|a|<|\varpi|}\sum_{|\underline{d}|<|\varpi|}\sum_{|\bm{x}|<|\varpi|^{2}}\psi\left(\frac{(a+\varpi d_{2})F_{2}(\bm{x})+\varpi d_{1}F_{1}(\bm{x})-\bm{v}\cdot\bm{x}}{\varpi^{2}}\right)

\displaystyle=|\varpi|\sideset{}{{}^{\prime}}{\sum}_{|a|<|\varpi|^{2}}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}|\bm{x}|<|\varpi|^{2}\\
F_{1}(\bm{x})\equiv 0\>(\varpi)\end{subarray}}\psi\left(\frac{aF_{2}(\bm{x})-\bm{v}\cdot\bm{x}}{\varpi^{2}}\right)

\displaystyle=|\varpi|^{3}\Biggl(\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}|\bm{x}|<|\varpi|^{2}\\
F_{1}(\bm{x})\equiv 0\>(\varpi)\\
F_{2}(\bm{x})\equiv 0\>(\varpi^{2})\end{subarray}}\psi\left(\frac{-\bm{v}\cdot\bm{x}}{\varpi^{2}}\right)-|\varpi|^{-1}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}|\bm{x}|<|\varpi|^{2}\\
F_{1}(\bm{x})\equiv 0\>(\varpi)\\
F_{2}(\bm{x})\equiv 0\>(\varpi)\end{subarray}}\psi\left(\frac{-\bm{v}\cdot\bm{x}}{\varpi^{2}}\right)\Biggr)

\displaystyle=|\varpi|^{3}\left(\Sigma_{1}-|\varpi|^{-1}\Sigma_{2}\right)

say. The conditions
F_{1}(\bm{x})\equiv 0\>(\varpi)
and
F_{2}(\bm{x})\equiv 0\>(\varpi^{2})
are invariant under scaling
\bm{x}
by any
b
with
(b,\varpi)=1
and so we deduce that that

\displaystyle\Sigma_{1}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{|\varpi|^{2}(1-|\varpi|^{-1})}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}|\bm{x}|<|\varpi|^{2}\\
F_{1}(\bm{x})\equiv 0\>(\varpi)\\
F_{2}(\bm{x})\equiv 0\>(\varpi^{2})\end{subarray}}\sideset{}{{}^{\prime}}{\sum}_{|b|<|\varpi|^{2}}\psi\left(\frac{b\bm{v}\cdot\bm{x}}{\varpi^{2}}\right)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{1-|\varpi|^{-1}}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\bm{x}\>(\varpi)\\
F_{1}(\bm{x})\equiv F_{2}(\bm{x}\equiv 0\>(\varpi)\\
\bm{v}\cdot\bm{x}\equiv 0\>(\varpi)\end{subarray}}(\rho_{1}(\bm{x})-|\varpi|^{-1}\rho_{2}(\bm{x})),

where

\rho_{1}(\bm{x})\coloneqq\#\{\bm{y}\>(\varpi^{2})\colon\bm{y}\equiv\bm{x}\>(\varpi),F_{2}(\bm{x})\equiv\bm{v}\cdot\bm{x}\equiv 0\>(\varpi^{2})\}

and

\rho_{2}(\bm{x})\coloneqq\#\{\bm{y}\>(\varpi^{2})\colon\bm{y}\equiv\bm{x}\>(\varpi),F_{2}(\bm{x})\equiv 0\>(\varpi^{2})\}.

Running the exact argument again yields

\Sigma_{2}=\frac{1}{1-|\varpi|^{-1}}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\bm{x}\>(\varpi)\\
F_{1}(\bm{x})\equiv F_{2}(\bm{x})\equiv 0\>(\varpi)\\
\bm{v}\cdot\bm{x}\equiv 0\>(\varpi)\end{subarray}}(\rho_{1}^{\prime}(\bm{x})-|\varpi|^{n-1}),

where

\rho_{1}^{\prime}(\bm{x})\coloneqq\#\{\bm{y}\>(\varpi^{2})\colon\bm{y}\equiv\bm{x}\>(\varpi),\bm{v}\cdot\bm{x}\equiv 0\>(\varpi^{2})\}.

Suppose that
\bm{y}=\bm{x}+\varpi\bm{z}
. Then
\bm{y}
is counted by
\rho_{1}(\bm{x})
if and only if
{\varpi\mid(\bm{x}^{t}M\bm{y}+\varpi^{-1}F_{2}(\bm{x}))}
and
\varpi\mid(\bm{v}\cdot\bm{z}+\varpi^{-1}\bm{v}\cdot\bm{x})
. Whereas
\bm{y}
is counted by
\rho_{2}(\bm{x})
if and only if
{\varpi\mid(\bm{x}^{t}M\bm{y}+\varpi^{-1}F_{2}(\bm{x}))}
. In particular, we see that
\rho_{1}(\bm{x})-|\varpi|^{-1}\rho_{2}(\bm{x})=0

unless

(6.8)

\rk\begin{pmatrix}\bm{v}\\
M\bm{x}\end{pmatrix}=\rk(M\bm{x})\mod\varpi.

Note that for
\bm{x}\not\equiv 0\>(\varpi)
and
|\varpi|
sufficiently large this can only happen if
\bm{v}
and
M\bm{x}
are proportional, since then the non-singularity of
X
implies that
M\bm{x}\not\equiv 0\>(\varpi)
.
In particular, if (
6.8
) holds and
\bm{x}\not\equiv 0\>(\varpi)
, then
\rho_{1}(\bm{x})-|\varpi|^{-1}\rho_{2}(\bm{x})=|\varpi|^{n-1}-|\varpi|^{n-2}
, while if
\bm{v}\equiv 0\>(\varpi)
, then
\rho_{1}(\bm{0})-|\varpi|^{-1}\rho_{2}(\bm{0})=|\varpi|^{n}-|\varpi|^{n-1}
. Moreover, we have
\rho_{1}^{\prime}(\bm{x})=|\varpi|^{n-1}
unless
\varpi\mid\bm{v}
, in which case
\rho_{1}(\bm{x})=|\varpi|^{n}
. In total, we thus have

S_{1}=|\varpi|^{3}\left(\frac{|\varpi|^{n-1}}{(1-|\varpi|^{-1})^{2}}\mathcal{N}_{1}-\frac{|\varpi|^{n-1}}{(1-|\varpi|^{-1})^{2}}\mathcal{N}_{2}\right)+O\left(|\varpi|^{n+3}\right).

The error term takes care of the contribution from
\bm{x}\equiv\bm{0}\>(\varpi)
in
\Sigma_{1}
and
\Sigma_{2}
, and where we have defined

\mathcal{N}_{1}\coloneqq\#\{\in\mathbb{F}_{\varpi}^{n}\setminus\{\bm{0}\}\colon\bm{v}\cdot\bm{x}=F_{1}(\bm{x})=F_{2}(\bm{x})=0,\eqref{Eq: RankConditionExpsum}\text{ holds}\}

and

\mathcal{N}_{2}\coloneqq\#\{\bm{x}\in\mathbb{F}_{\varpi}^{n}\setminus\{\bm{0}\}\colon F_{1}(\bm{x})=F_{2}(\bm{x})=0,\bm{v}\equiv\bm{0}\>(\varpi)\}.

Let us first deal with the case
\varpi\mid\bm{v}
. It follows that
\mathcal{N}_{1}=\mathcal{N}_{2}
and thus

S_{1}=O(|\varpi|^{n+3}).

Suppose next that
\varpi\nmid\bm{v}
. In this case
\mathcal{N}_{2}=0
and (
6.8
) holds if and only if
M\bm{x}
and
\bm{v}
are proportional. Since
M
has rank at least
n-1
, this can happen for at most
O(|\varpi|^{2})
choices of
\bm{x}
, so that

\displaystyle S_{1}\ll|\varpi|^{n+2}\mathcal{N}_{1}+|\varpi|^{n+3}\ll|\varpi|^{n+4}.

In total we have therefore established that

(6.9)

S_{1}\ll|\varpi|^{n+4}.

Let us now turn to the sum
S_{2}
. It follows from Lemma
6.3
that

S_{2}\leq\sideset{}{{}^{\prime}}{\sum}_{a_{1}\>(\varpi^{2})}\sum_{a_{2}\>(\varpi^{2})}\left|\sum_{\bm{x}\>(\varpi^{2})}\psi\left(\frac{a_{1}F_{1}(\bm{x})+a_{2}F_{2}(\bm{x})-\bm{v}\cdot\bm{x}}{\varpi^{2}}\right)\right|.

Since
(a_{1},\varpi)=1
, we can apply Lemma
6.7
to deduce that the sum over
\bm{x}
is
O(|\varpi|^{n})
and hence

(6.10)

S_{2}\ll|\varpi|^{n+4},

which completes our treatment of
S_{1}
and
S_{2}
.

Finally, when
\varpi\nmid\Delta_{F_{2}}
and
0\leq m<k
, we can invoke Lemma
6.8
to deduce that

(6.11)

S_{\varpi^{m}\underline{c}_{0},\varpi^{k},\bm{0},1}(\bm{v})\ll|\varpi|^{m+k(n+3)/2}.

Using Lemma
6.1
and estimating the contribution from
N
trivially, we see that the following result summarises the content of (
6.7
) and (
6.9
)–(
6.11
).

Proposition 6.10
.

Let
d_{1},d_{2},d_{3}\in\mathcal{O}
be square-free. Then there exists a constant
C>0
depending on
F_{1},F_{2}
and
N
such that

S_{d_{1}d_{2}d_{3}^{2}\underline{c}_{0},d_{1}d_{2}^{2}d_{3}^{2},\bm{b},N}(\bm{v})\leq C^{\omega(d_{1}d_{2}d_{3})}|d_{1}|^{(n+3)/2}|d_{2}d_{3}|^{n+4}.

In addition, let
d,r\in\mathcal{O}
be both monic such that
d\mid r
and
(r,\Delta_{F_{2}})=1
. If
\nu_{\varpi}(d)<\nu_{\varpi}(r)
for all
\varpi\mid d
, then

S_{d\underline{c}_{0},r,\bm{0},1}(\bm{v})\leq C^{\omega(r)}|d||r|^{(n+3)/2}.

7.
Exponential sums: Averages

We also need to deal with certain averages over exponential sums. Our first ingredient is the following result, which we apply to our situation in the corollary directly afterwards.

Lemma 7.1
.

Let
\bm{v}_{0}\in K_{\infty}^{n}
and
V\geq 1
. If
r
is cube-full and
\underline{a}\in\mathcal{O}^{2}
is such that
|(a_{1},r)|\ll 1
, then

\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\bm{v}\in\mathcal{O}^{n}\\
|\bm{v}-\bm{v}_{0}|<\widehat{V}\end{subarray}}|T(\underline{a},r,\bm{v})|\ll_{F_{1}}|r|^{n/2+\varepsilon}\left(\widehat{V}^{n}+|r|^{n/3}\right).

The Lemma we just stated follows from equation (6.9) in
[
2
]
. There only the case when
(a_{1},r)=1
is considered. However, as explained in the paragraph after Lemma 6.4 in
[
5
]
, the argument leading to the estimate continues to hold when
|(a_{1},r)|\ll 1
after employing some minor modifications.

Corollary 7.2
.

Let
\bm{v}_{0}\in K_{\infty}^{n}
and
V\geq 1
. Suppose
r
is cube-full such that
d\mid r
and define

P_{1}(r)\coloneqq\{\varpi^{k}\parallel r\colon(\varpi,\Delta_{F_{2}}c_{2})=1,\varpi^{k}\nmid d\}\quad\text{ and }\quad P_{2}(r)=\{\varpi^{k}\parallel r\colon(\varpi,\Delta_{F_{2}})=1,\varpi^{k}\mid d\}.

If we write
r=r_{1}r_{2}
, where

(7.1)

r_{1}=\begin{cases}\prod\limits_{\varpi^{k}\in P_{1}(r)}\varpi^{k}&\text{if }c_{2}\neq 0\\
\prod\limits_{\varpi^{k}\in P_{2}(r)}\varpi^{k}&\text{else}\end{cases}

and
r_{2}=r/r_{1}
, then

\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\bm{v}\in\mathcal{O}^{n}\\
|\bm{v}-\bm{v}_{0}|<\widehat{V}\end{subarray}}|S_{d\underline{c},r,\bm{b},N}(\bm{v})|\ll_{F_{1},F_{2},N}|d||r|^{n/2+1+\varepsilon}|r_{2}|^{1/2}\left(\widehat{V}^{n}+|r|^{n/3}\right).

Note that since
d\mid r
, the condition
\varpi^{k}\nmid d
in the definition of
P_{1}(r)
means that every prime factor of
d
that divides
r_{1}
in fact properly divides
r_{1}
when
c_{2}\neq 0
.

Proof.

Denote the sum to be estimated by
S
. After making the change of variables
\bm{x}=\bm{y}N+\bm{b}
, we obtain the identity

S_{d\underline{c},r,\bm{b},N}(\bm{v})=\psi\left(\frac{-\bm{b}\cdot\bm{v}}{r_{N}}\right)\sum_{\underline{a}/r\in L(d\underline{c})}T(\underline{a},r/(r,N),\bm{v})

with underlying polynomials
G_{i}(\bm{y})=(r,N)^{-1}F_{i}(N\bm{y}+\bm{b})
for
i=1,2
in the definition (
6.1
). Since
N\mid F_{i}(\bm{b})
, it follows that
G_{i}
has coefficients in
\mathcal{O}
. Moreover, the cubic part of
G_{1}
is given by the non-singular polynomial
g_{0}(\bm{y})=(r,N)^{-1}N^{3}F_{1}(\bm{y})
. We now factor
r/(r,N)
into its cube-free part
t
and its cube-full part
s
. Since
r
is cube-full, we must have
|t|\leq|N|
. Using Lemma
6.2
and estimating the contribution from the sum corresponding to
t
trivially, we see that

(7.2)

|S_{d\underline{c},r,\bm{b},N}(\bm{v})|\leq|N|^{n}\sum_{\underline{a}/r\in L(d\underline{c})}|T(\underline{a}_{t},s,\bm{v})|.

Next we write
s=s_{1}s_{2}
, where

s_{1}=\prod_{\begin{subarray}{c}\varpi^{k}\parallel s\\
\varpi^{1+v_{\varpi}}\nmid a_{1}\end{subarray}}\varpi^{k}\quad\text{and}\quad s_{2}=\prod_{\begin{subarray}{c}\varpi^{k}\parallel s\\
\varpi^{1+v_{\varpi}}\mid a_{1}\end{subarray}}\varpi^{k},

with
v_{\varpi}
defined in (
6.5
). It then follows from Lemma
6.8
that
T(\underline{a}_{ts_{1}},s_{2},\bm{v})\ll|s_{2}|^{(n+1)/2+\varepsilon}
. Therefore, after applying Lemma
6.2
and Lemma
7.1
together with the identity (
7.2
), we obtain

\displaystyle S

\displaystyle\ll|r|^{\varepsilon}\sum_{\underline{a}/r\in L(d\underline{c})}|s_{1}|^{n/2}|s_{2}|^{(n+1)/2}\left(\widehat{V}^{n}+|s_{1}|^{n/3}\right)

\displaystyle\ll\widehat{P}^{n}\widehat{Y}^{5/2-n/2+\varepsilon}\widehat{\Theta}_{1}\widehat{\Theta}_{2}\left(\widehat{V}^{n}\widehat{Z}^{-n/2}+\widehat{Y}^{n/3}\right)

\displaystyle=\widehat{Y}^{5/2+n/2+\varepsilon}\widehat{\Theta}_{1}\widehat{\Theta}_{2}\widehat{Z}^{n/2}+\widehat{P}^{n}\widehat{Y}^{5/2-n/6+\varepsilon}\widehat{\Theta}_{1}\widehat{\Theta}_{2}

\displaystyle\ll\widehat{P}^{5n/6-5/3}\widehat{Y}^{1/2+\varepsilon}+\widehat{P}^{n-5/3+(1-\eta)(1/2-n/6+\varepsilon)}.

The first term is
O(\widehat{P}^{5n/6-5/6+\varepsilon})
, which is satisfactory as soon as
n\geq 26
. Moreover,
n-5/3+(1/2-n/6)<n-5
if
n\geq 24
. In particular, the second term is sufficiently small provided
\varepsilon
is small.

If
\widehat{Y}\leq\widehat{P}^{1-\eta}
we instead estimate the integral
I_{r_{N}}(\underline{\theta},\bm{v})
directly via Lemma
5.2
to obtain

\displaystyle E_{1,b}(Y,\Theta_{1},\Theta_{2})

\displaystyle\ll\frac{\widehat{P}^{n}}{\widehat{Y}^{n}}\widehat{\Theta}_{1}\widehat{\Theta}_{2}\widehat{Z}^{1-n/2-\mu}\Sigma(\widehat{V}),

where

\mu=\begin{cases}1/2&\text{if }\widehat{P}\widehat{\Theta}_{1}\ll\widehat{\Theta}_{2}\\
0&\text{else.}\end{cases}

We can now apply (
8.21
) with
\widehat{W}=\widehat{V}
to deduce that

\displaystyle E_{1,b}(Y,\Theta_{1},\Theta_{2})

\displaystyle\ll\widehat{P}^{n}\widehat{Y}^{5/2-n/2+\varepsilon}\widehat{\Theta}_{1}\widehat{\Theta}_{2}\widehat{Z}^{1-n/2-\mu}\left(\widehat{V}^{n}+\widehat{Y}^{n/3}\right)

\displaystyle=\widehat{Y}^{5/2+n/2+\varepsilon}\widehat{\Theta}_{1}\widehat{\Theta}_{2}\widehat{Z}^{1+n/2-\mu}+\widehat{P}^{n-5}\widehat{Y}^{5/2-n/6+\varepsilon}\widehat{Z}^{3-n/2-\mu}.

The second term is satisfactory by (
8.10
) if
n>15
, so we may assume the first one dominates. Recall that we already dealt with the case when
\widehat{Y}\widehat{\Theta}_{1}\geq\widehat{P}^{-\delta}
, where
\delta=8(n-16)/(3n-24)
, since we assume (
2
) after (
4.10
) does not hold. So we may suppose the contrary is true. There are now two cases: First, we assume that
\widehat{\Theta}_{2}\ll\widehat{P}\widehat{\Theta}_{1}
. In this situation we have
\widehat{Z}\ll\widehat{P}^{3-\delta}\widehat{Y}^{-1}
and
\mu=0
, so that

\displaystyle E_{1,b}(Y,\Theta_{1},\Theta_{2})\ll\widehat{P}^{(3-\delta)(1+n/2)+1-2\delta}\widehat{Y}^{-1/2+\varepsilon}.

A rather involved computation or a check with a computer algebra program verifies that
(3-\delta)(1+n/2)+1-2\delta<n-5
if
n\geq 25
, which is satisfactory.

The only case that remains is when
\widehat{P}\widehat{\Theta}_{1}\ll\widehat{\Theta}_{2}
, in which case
\mu=1/2
and hence

E_{1,b}(Y,\Theta_{1},\Theta_{2})\ll\widehat{P}^{5n/6+5/6-5/3}\widehat{Y}^{\varepsilon},

which is satisfactory for
n>25
.
