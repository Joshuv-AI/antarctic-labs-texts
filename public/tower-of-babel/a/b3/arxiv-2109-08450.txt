Energetic solutions for the coupling of associative plasticity with damage in geomaterials
Vito Crismale

Abstract
. We prove existence of globally stable quasistatic evolutions, referred to as energetic solutions, for a model proposed by Marigo and Kazymyrenko in 2019. The behaviour of geomaterials under compression is studied through the coupling of Drucker-Prager plasticity model with a damage term tuning kinematical hardening. This provides a new approach to the modelling of geomaterials, for which non associative plasticity is usually employed. The kinematical hardening is null where the damage is complete, so there the behaviour is perfectly plastic. We analyse the model combining tools from the theory of capacity and from the treatment of linearly elastic materials with cracks.

1.
Introduction

The theory of elasto-plasticity provides a common framework to model
the behaviour of
materials displaying permanent deformations when a critical stress threshold is reached, even if these deformations could be caused by
many different physical mechanisms:
for instance, for crystalline materials, such as metals, plastic deformation is mainly due to dislocations, atomic defects inside the lattice, while for granular materials
like concrete, rocks, and soils it is mostly the result of relative sliding of the
microparticles composing them.
Such intrinsic difference between materials
reflects in the choice
of the elasto-plastic model to adopt;
in particular, granular materials (we refer to them also as geomaterials) usually undergo permanent volumetric changes, in contrast to crystalline materials.

The general setting of elasto-plasticity, in small-strain assumptions,
is based on an additive decomposition of the
total strain

\mathrm{E}u\in{\mathbb{M}^{n\times n}_{sym}}
, that is the symmetrized gradient of the
displacement field

u\colon\Omega\to{{\mathbb{R}}}^{n}
,
\Omega\subset{{\mathbb{R}}}^{n}
being the
reference domain
, into
elastic strain
and
plastic strain
:

\mathrm{E}u=e+p.

The
stress tensor

\sigma\colon\Omega\to{\mathbb{M}^{n\times n}_{sym}}
depends linearly on
e
according to Hooke’s law

\sigma={\mathbb{C}}e,

{\mathbb{C}}
being the fourth order positive definite Hooke’s tensor, it is constrained to lie in a fixed closed and convex set
K\subset{\mathbb{M}^{n\times n}_{sym}}
, referred to as
constraint set
, and satisfies equilibrium conditions involving the external loads.
When
\sigma
is in the interior of
K
, the deformations are reversible (after a loading cycle the material has the same configuration), so the behaviour is elastic and no plasticity is produced. Conversely, a plastic flow may be produced when
\sigma\in\partial K
(that is,
\sigma
reaches the
yield surface
): if plasticity flows in the cone of normal directions to
K
at
\sigma\in\partial K
, that is
\dot{p}\in N_{K}(\sigma)
, the plasticity is said
associative
, otherwise
non associative
(e.g., if
\dot{p}\in N_{\widetilde{K}(\sigma)}(\sigma)
, for
\sigma\in\partial K
and
K,\,\widetilde{K}(\sigma)\subset{\mathbb{M}^{n\times n}_{sym}}
different closed convex sets).

Associative plasticity is largely employed for crystalline materials. Moreover, these materials usually do not develop permanent volumetric changes: then

\frac{\mathrm{tr}\,p}{n}
, the hydrostatic part of
p
related to irreversible volumetric deformation, is null and
K
does not depend on the
mean stress

\sigma_{m}:=\frac{\mathrm{tr}\sigma}{n}\in{\mathbb{R}}
, but only on
\sigma_{D}:=\sigma-\sigma_{m}\,\mathrm{Id}\in{\mathbb{M}}^{n{\times}n}_{D}
(here
\mathrm{tr}
denotes the trace of a matrix and
{\mathbb{M}}^{n{\times}n}_{D}
the deviatoric matrices, that is the matrices with null trace). For these materials thus
K\subset{\mathbb{M}}^{n{\times}n}_{D}
, and the corresponding associative models are said
of
Prandtl-Reuss type
(we refer to
[
50
,
20
]
for their reference mathematical treatment).

Differently, granular materials display volumetric plastic deformations, depending on the hydrostatic pressure. In fact, an irreversible rearrangement of the microparticles may be caused by applying a triaxial test, namely a shear compression plus a compression normal to the shear plane; as a result, for geomaterials the volume could increase even in compression, providing an example of
dilatance
. In the shear compression, the volume may increase with that of the internal voids between the microparticles, interpreted as microcracks, due to a less efficient particle organisation; in the normal compression a part of these microcracks may be closed, preventing a free sliding and thus the free relaxation to the initial configuration in unloading.

Most of the models for geomaterials are formulated in the realm of non associative plasticity, see e.g.
[
35
,
26
,
18
,
55
]
. (We refer the reader to the variational treatment of non associative plasticity in
[
21
,
6
,
30
]
and to the related
[
54
]
). The reason to put aside associative plasticity is that, when modifying Prandtl-Reuss plasticity accounting for the dependence of
K
on
\sigma_{m}
, too important dilatancy effects appear, regardless of the form of
K
.

A different approach to the modelling of geomaterials
has been
proposed by Marigo and Kazymyrenko in
[
39
]
. Basing on a micromechanical analysis, they consider the coupling of an associative elasto-plastic model with damage, tuning kinematical hardening.
The
damage variable

\alpha\colon[0,T]\to[0,1]
is associated to the distribution density
of closed microcracks, and reflects into an internal blocked energy depending on plastic strain, being related
to irreversible deformations.
Assuming Coulomb law for the sliding with friction between the lips of closed microcracks, plasticity follows a Drucker–Prager law, that is

K=\{\sigma\in{\mathbb{M}^{n\times n}_{sym}}\colon\tau\sigma_{m}+|\sigma_{D}|-k\leq 0\},

for
\tau
,
k>0
. Then the plastic dissipation from a plastic strain
q
is the relaxation of

\int_{\Omega}H(p(t)-q){\,\mathrm{d}x},\qquad H(\xi)=\sup_{\sigma\in K}\sigma\colon\xi

for
p(t)

a
bounded Radon measure and the
stored energy at time
t
is the sum of the elastic energy of the sound material plus the kinematical hardening term (depending on
\alpha
)

{\mathcal{Q}}(e(t))+{\widetilde{Q}}(\alpha(t),p(t))=\frac{1}{2}\int_{\Omega}{\mathbb{C}}e(x):e(x){\,\mathrm{d}x}+\int_{\Omega}{\mathbb{C}}_{1}(\alpha(x))p(x)\colon p(x){\,\mathrm{d}x},

for
{\mathbb{C}}_{1}
a fourth-order tensor
positive definite except for
\alpha=0
, corresponding to the fully damaged material, while formally
{\mathbb{C}}_{1}
is

+\infty
for
\alpha=1
, meaning that the sound material does not display plasticity.
The growth of the microcracks is modelled by the terms usually present in gradient damage models, corresponding to Ambrosio-Tortorelli approximation for a fixed thickness parameter

D(\alpha(t))+\|\nabla\alpha(t)\|_{L^{2}}^{2},

cf.
[
48
]
,
[
1
, Section 4]
,
[
39
, Remark 3.1]
, and the coupling between Coulomb law for the sliding of crack lips with Griffith law for crack propagation in
[
5
,
56
]
. Damage is assumed to be an irreversible process, consequently
\alpha
is nonincreasing in time. The reference experiment is the triaxial test; here the external loading is
represented by a suitable duality between the displacement and a loading term
{\mathcal{L}}(t)
. Remarkably, the simulations discussed in
[
39
]
reproduce well the phenomena expected for geomaterials, in particular the dilatance.

The main result of the present work is the proof of existence of quasistatic evolutions for the model of
[
39
]
, fulfilling the notion of

energetic solutions
à la Mielke-Theil (cf.
[
46
,
47
,
44
]
). This notion is based on a global stability condition (qs1), which prescribes that at each time the current configuration minimises the sum of the total internal energy and the dissipation potential, and on an energy-dissipation balance (qs2) between the total variation in time of the internal energy, the total dissipated energy, and the work of the external loadings.
The precise assumptions and the functional framework of the existence result stated below, are described in Section
2
.

Theorem 1.1
.

Assume the conditions (
2.1
), (
D
), (
2.5
), (
2.7
), (
K
), (
2.11
), (
IC
), and the definitions in Section
2
. Then there exists an evolution
\alpha\colon{[0,T]}\to H^{1}(\Omega)
,
u\colon{[0,T]}\to BD(\Omega)
,
e\colon{[0,T]}\to{L^{2}(\Omega;{\mathbb{M}^{n\times n}_{sym}})}
,
p\colon{[0,T]}\to{M_{b}(\Omega\cup\partial_{D}\Omega;{\mathbb{M}^{n\times n}_{sym}})}
, such that

(qs0)

\alpha(s)\leq\alpha(t)
a.e. in
\Omega
for every
0\leq s\leq t\leq T
;

(qs1)

(u(t),e(t),p(t))\in\mathrm{A}(w(t))
for every
t\in{[0,T]}
and

\begin{split}{\mathcal{Q}}(e(t))&+D(\alpha(t))+\|\nabla\alpha(t)\|_{L^{2}}^{2}+{\widetilde{Q}}(\alpha(t),p(t))-\langle{\mathcal{L}}(t),u(t)\rangle\\
&\leq{\mathcal{Q}}(\eta)+D(\beta)+\|\nabla\beta\|_{L^{2}}^{2}+{\widetilde{Q}}(\beta,q)+{\mathcal{H}}(q-p(t))-\langle{\mathcal{L}}(t),v\rangle\end{split}

for every
\beta\leq\alpha(t)
,
\beta\geq 0
,
(v,\eta,q)\in\mathrm{A}(w(t))
;

(qs2)

p\colon{[0,T]}\to{M_{b}(\Omega\cup\partial_{D}\Omega;{\mathbb{M}^{n\times n}_{sym}})}
has bounded variation and for every
t\in{[0,T]}

\begin{split}{\mathcal{Q}}(e(t))&+D(\alpha(t))+\|\nabla\alpha(t)\|_{L^{2}}^{2}+{\widetilde{Q}}(\alpha(t),p(t))+{\mathcal{V}}_{\mathcal{H}}(p;0,t)-\langle{\mathcal{L}}(t),u(t)\rangle\\
&={\mathcal{Q}}(e_{0})+D(\alpha_{0})+\|\nabla\alpha_{0}\|_{L^{2}}^{2}+{\widetilde{Q}}(\alpha_{0},p_{0})-\langle{\mathcal{L}}_{0},u_{0}\rangle+\int_{0}^{t}\langle\sigma(s),\mathrm{E}\dot{w}(s)\rangle\,\mathrm{d}s\\
&\hskip 10.00002pt-\int_{0}^{t}\{\langle\dot{{\mathcal{L}}}(s),u(s)\rangle+\langle{\mathcal{L}}(s),\dot{w}(s)\rangle\}\,\mathrm{d}s.\end{split}

Moreover, for every
t\in{[0,T]}
except at most countable many, it holds that:

\alpha
and
e
are strongly continuous from
[0,T]
into
H^{1}(\Omega)
and
{L^{2}(\Omega;{\mathbb{M}^{n\times n}_{sym}})}
;
u
and
p
are weakly
∗
continuous in
BD(\Omega)
and
{M_{b}(\Omega\cup\partial_{D}\Omega;{\mathbb{M}^{n\times n}_{sym}})}
;
\sqrt{{\mathbb{C}}_{1}(\alpha(\cdot))}p(\cdot)
is strongly continuous in
L^{2}(\Omega;{\mathbb{M}^{n\times n}_{sym}})
.

We point out that we are exactly in the setting proposed in
[
39
]
for the variational formulation. In particular,
{\mathbb{C}}_{1}(0)=0
and the gradient damage term is taken in the
L^{2}
norm. These constitutive choices give weaker regularity than in other coupled damage-plasticity models, which requires a careful mathematical treatment.

The degeneracy of
{\mathbb{C}}_{1}
at the maximal damage state corresponds to complete damage. For this model, it means that in
\{\alpha=0\}
the material satisfies Drucker-Prager perfect plasticity and
p
is a bounded Radon measure, since it is controlled only linearly.
Such assumption is then crucial to observe plastic shear bands, otherwise one would have a kinematical hardening even in
\{\alpha=0\}
and no concentration of plastic strain.
Furthermore, the
L^{2}
damage gradient term is the one usually adopted in simulations and in mechanical models, e.g.
[
48
,
1
,
55
]
; the fact that the damage field is not continuous but only in
H^{1}
is a source of analytical issues, since it in general multiplies some functions of the measure
p
, which is in duality with continuous fields.
We remark that it could be also interesting for future applications to deal with the case where the switching between perfect plasticity and plasticity with hardening is governed by the values of a Sobolev field, as in the present framework.

In previous treatments of quasistatic evolutions for coupled small-strain plasticity-damage models
[
12
,
13
,
15
]
and in many elasticity-damage models (see e.g.
[
43
,
53
,
37
,
36
]
) the damage is assumed incomplete: in those works the Hooke’s tensor
{\mathbb{C}}
, depending on
\alpha
, is positive definite
for any
\alpha\in[0,1]
.
Here
{\mathbb{C}}
is independent of
\alpha
but the relevant mixed term including the damage variable degenerates. Restricting to elasticity-damage models, in
[
8
,
45
,
41
,
33
]
some cases of complete damage are addressed, and also in the dynamic plasticity-damage model
[
22
]
Hooke’s tensor is only positive semidefinite.
Moreover, in plasticity-damage models the
L^{2}
regularisation in the damage gradient is at the moment not enough, unless having the presence of strain gradient
[
13
]
or further assumptions on elastic strain
[
16
]
: an
L^{p}
regularisation is required for
p>n

[
12
,
22
]
or for
p=n

[
15
]
.

Following De Giorgi’s Minimising Movement approach to quasistatic evolutions
[
2
]
, time-continuous evolutions are approximated by discrete-time ones, constructed by solving incremental minimisation problems.
The interaction between damage in
H^{1}
and plasticity plays a crucial role in the lower semicontinuity of the kinematical hardening term (see Theorem
3.1
), needed in incremental minimisation problems and both in passing to the limit the discrete stability to get (qs1) and in the lower energy inequality in (qs2); moreover, it enters also in a continuity condition to get (qs1), see Theorem
4.1
.

Theorems
3.1
and
4.1
are the main intermediate results needed to prove Theorem
1.1
: their proof strategies employ fine properties of Sobolev functions, derived from capacitary arguments, combined with tools from free discontinuity problems in linear elasticity. Indeed, we observe that even the definition of
{\widetilde{Q}}
is well posed since
p
could concentrate on sets of dimension at least
n{-}1
, and the capacitary precise representative
\widetilde{\alpha}
of
\alpha
is well defined up to sets of dimension
s
for any
s>n{-}2
.

In Theorem
3.1
we use a blow-up procedure, at every
x_{0}\in\Omega
with
\alpha(x_{0})>0
. If the rescaled damage variables
\beta_{k}
converge uniformly to
\alpha(x_{0})>0
, then the rescaled plastic strains
q_{k}
(for which
\int_{B_{1}}{\mathbb{C}}_{1}(\beta_{k})q_{k}\colon q_{k}\,\mathrm{d}x
is bounded) would be bounded in
L^{2}
and the semicontinuity would be direct from Ioffe-Olech Theorem. Since
\beta_{k}
converge weakly in
H^{1}
to
\alpha(x_{0})
, the convergence is uniform up to an exceptional set of small
s
-capacity,
s\in(1,2)
. The idea is to combine the fact that the exceptional set could be taken with small perimeter, following Lahti
[
38
]
, with the theory of
GSBD
functions
[
19
]
, namely those corresponding to displacements for linearly elastic materials outside a
(n{-}1)
-dimensional crack. We see the boundary of the exceptional set of small capacity as a discontinuity surface of the rescaled displacements
v_{k}
, that are regarded as functions equibounded in
GSBD
, since the absolutely continuous parts of their symmetric gradients are bounded in
L^{2}
and their discontinuity sets have bounded surface measure. Using lower semicontinuity results in
GSBD
we get lower semicontinuity outside the exceptional set.

Furthermore, to exclude that the measure
p
concentrates on
\{\widetilde{\alpha}>0\}
, we resort to a slicing argument. Also here, the result would be direct under uniform convergence of damage
\alpha_{k}
to
\alpha
, using that the sets
\{\alpha>\delta\}
are open and well approximated by
\{\alpha_{k}>\delta\}
.
We stress the fact that in non associative plasticity regularity assumptions are needed even to solve the minimisation problems
[
6
]
or to prove existence of weak evolutions
[
30
]
: these are obtained through convolutions with fixed kernels.

The
GSBD
-lower semicontinuity together with Lahti’s capacitary estimate is applied also in Theorem
4.1
, without blow-up.
We believe that this strategy could be useful in other problems in plasticity-damage and for other couplings in different contexts.

Another issue arising when following the framework of
[
39
]
is that the triaxial test requires mixed Dirichlet-Neumann boundary conditions. These mixed conditions could be imposed trough a suitable notion of stress-plastic strain duality, well defined when the constraint set
K
is bounded in the direction of deviatoric matrices, see e.g.
[
20
]
. Conversely, this is a problem when dealing with geomaterials, so that fully Dirichlet boundary conditions are usually considered in mathematical works, see
[
7
, Introduction]
.
Here, besides safe load condition, we require a further assumption on the external loads; remarkably, this is satisfied in the case of triaxial test. These assumptions guarantee coercivity in
p
in the incremental minimisation problems and permit us to obtain a weak formulation of evolution accounting for Neumann conditions in an integral form.
In the last part of the paper we show that, under regularity assumptions on the evolution, one recovers the differential properties in
[
39
]
, to which we refer for the full set of conditions and for further details on their mechanical interpretation.

We notice that we follow
[
39
]
, but with the same techniques one could treat the case of a

L^{\gamma}
gradient damage regularisation,
\gamma>1
, in place of
L^{2}
. Moreover, taking
{\mathbb{C}}_{1}(0)
positive definite or
{\mathbb{C}}_{1}(1)<+\infty
simplifies the analysis.

One could also repeat the same arguments for the existence of evolutions when adding a plastic strain gradient term of the type
\|\nabla p\|_{L^{2}}^{2}
in the total energy (starting by minimising the functional in (
3.1
) plus
\|\nabla p\|_{L^{2}}^{2}
); actually, this term simplifies the analysis since it improves the regularity of
p
.
Moreover, a strain gradient regularisation seems also suitable to improve time regularity.
In the current setting, quasistatic evolutions
are not proven to be absolutely continuous in time, as usual in coupled plasticity-damage models. Different notions of evolutions have been proposed to deal with time discontinuity, such as the one of
BV
solutions
[
42
]
, obtained trough a vanishing-viscosity technique à la Efendiev-Mielke
[
27
]
(see
[
14
,
17
]
for plasticity-damage models). We plan to address these variants of the model
in future works.

The damage variable and the associated dissipation.
In addition to

During the evolution, the damage variable is forced to decrease.

is a functional

(D)

The total energy includes also a damage gradient term

Capacity.

For the notion of capacity we refer, e.g., to
[
29
,
34
]
. We recall here the definition and some properties.

Let
1\leq\gamma<+\infty
and let
\Omega
be a bounded, open subset of
{{\mathbb{R}}}^{n}
. For every subset
B\subset\Omega
, the
\gamma
-capacity
of
E
in
\Omega
is defined by

\mathrm{Cap}_{\gamma}(E,\Omega):=\inf\Big\{\int\limits_{\Omega}|\nabla v|^{\gamma}{\,\mathrm{d}x}\colon v\in W^{1,\gamma}_{0}(\Omega),\ v\geq 1\text{ a.e.\ in a neighbourhood of }E\Big\}\,.

A set
E\subset\Omega
has
\gamma
-capacity zero
if
\mathrm{Cap}_{\gamma}(E,\Omega)=0
(actually, the definition does not depend on the open set
\Omega
containing
E
). A property is said to hold
\mathrm{Cap}_{\gamma}
-quasi everywhere
(abbreviated as
\mathrm{Cap}_{\gamma}
-q.e.) if it holds for a set of
\gamma
-capacity zero.

If
1<\gamma\leq n
and
E
has
\gamma
-capacity zero, then
{\mathcal{H}}^{s}(E)=0
for every
s>n-\gamma
.

A function
\alpha\colon\Omega\to{\mathbb{R}}
is
\mathrm{Cap}_{\gamma}
-
quasicontinuous
if for every
\varepsilon>0
there exists a set
E_{\varepsilon}\subset\Omega
with
\mathrm{Cap}_{\gamma}(E_{\varepsilon},\Omega)<\varepsilon
such that the restriction
\alpha|_{\Omega\setminus E_{\varepsilon}}
is continuous. Note that if
\gamma>n
, a function
\alpha
is
\mathrm{Cap}_{\gamma}
-quasicontinous if and only if it is continuous.

Every function
\alpha\in W^{1,\gamma}(\Omega)
admits a
\mathrm{Cap}_{\gamma}
-
quasicontinuous representative

\tilde{\alpha}
, i.e., a
\mathrm{Cap}_{\gamma}
-quasicontinuous function
\tilde{\alpha}
such that
\tilde{\alpha}=\alpha

{\mathcal{L}}^{n}
-a.e. in
\Omega
. The
\mathrm{Cap}_{\gamma}
-quasicontinuous representative is essentially unique, that is, if
\tilde{\beta}
is another
\mathrm{Cap}_{\gamma}
-quasicontinuous representative of
\alpha
, then
\tilde{\beta}=\tilde{\alpha}

\mathrm{Cap}_{\gamma}
-q.e. in
\Omega
. It satisfies (see
[
29
, Theorem 4.8.1]
)

(2.4)

\lim_{\rho\to 0}\frac{1}{|B_{\rho}(x_{0})|}\int\limits_{B_{\rho}(x_{0})}\!|\alpha(x)-\tilde{\alpha}(x_{0})|{\,\mathrm{d}x}=0\,\quad\text{for $\mathrm{Cap}_{\gamma}$-q.e.\ $x_{0}\in\Omega$}\,.

Recalling
[
28
, Theorem 7]
, if
\alpha_{k}\rightharpoonup\alpha
in
W^{1,\gamma}(\Omega)
, up to passing to a subsequence we have that for every
\widetilde{\gamma}\in[1,\gamma)
and every
\varepsilon>0
there exists a relatively open set
E_{\varepsilon}\subset\Omega
such that

(2.5)

\widetilde{\alpha}_{k}\to\widetilde{\alpha}\quad\text{uniformly on }\Omega\setminus E_{\varepsilon},\qquad\mathrm{Cap}_{\widetilde{\gamma}}(E_{\varepsilon},\Omega)<\varepsilon.

The stored energy.
The stored energy is the sum of two contributions: besides the classical expression of the elastic energy depending only on the elastic strain through Hooke’s tensor, the model devised in
[
39
]
displays a further term depending both on the damage variable and the plastic strain.

The elastic energy of the sound material.

For every
e\in L^{2}(\Omega,{\mathbb{M}^{n\times n}_{sym}})
, when the damage variable assumes constant value 0,
the
elastic energy
is given by

{\mathcal{Q}}(e):=\frac{1}{2}\int_{\Omega}{\mathbb{C}}e(x):e(x){\,\mathrm{d}x}=\frac{1}{2}\langle{\mathbb{C}}e,e\rangle_{L^{2}(\Omega;{\mathbb{M}^{n\times n}_{sym}})},

with
{\mathbb{C}}
the Hooke’s tensor, or any fourth order tensor satisfying the symmetry conditions

(
{\mathbb{C}}
.1)

{\mathbb{C}}_{ijkl}={\mathbb{C}}_{klij}={\mathbb{C}}_{jikl}\quad\text{for all }i,j,k,l\in\{1,\dots,n\}

and the coercivity-continuity assumption

(
{\mathbb{C}}
.2)

\gamma_{1}|\xi|^{2}\leq{\mathbb{C}}\xi:\xi\leq\gamma_{2}|\xi|^{2}\quad\text{for every}\,\xi\in{\mathbb{M}^{n\times n}_{sym}},

where
\gamma_{1},\,\gamma_{2}
are positive constants.
In particular, this implies

(2.6)

|{\mathbb{C}}\xi|\leq 2\gamma_{2}|\xi|.

The kinematical hardening term.

The latter term in the elastic energy has the form

(2.7)

\int_{\Omega}{\mathbb{C}}_{1}(\alpha(x))p(x)\colon p(x){\,\mathrm{d}x},\quad\color[rgb]{0,0,0}p=0\text{ in }\{\alpha=1\}\color[rgb]{0,0,0}

with
{\mathbb{C}}_{1}
a fourth order tensor satisfying

(
{\mathbb{C}}_{1}
.1)

\displaystyle\color[rgb]{0,0,0}{\mathbb{C}}_{1}\in C([0,1);Lin({\mathbb{M}^{n\times n}_{sym}};{\mathbb{M}^{n\times n}_{sym}})),\color[rgb]{0,0,0}

(
{\mathbb{C}}_{1}
.2)

\displaystyle\gamma(\alpha)|\xi|^{2}\leq{\mathbb{C}}_{1}(\alpha)\xi:\xi\quad\text{for every}\,\alpha\in[0,1],\xi\in{\mathbb{M}^{n\times n}_{sym}},

(
{\mathbb{C}}_{1}
.3)

\displaystyle{\mathbb{C}}_{1}(\alpha)=0\quad\text{if and only if }\alpha=0,

(
{\mathbb{C}}_{1}
.4)

\displaystyle\alpha\mapsto{\mathbb{C}}_{1}(\alpha)\xi:\xi\quad\text{is nondecreasing for every}\,\xi\in{\mathbb{M}^{n\times n}_{sym}},

(
{\mathbb{C}}_{1}
.5)

\displaystyle\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\lim_{\alpha\to 1^{-}}{\mathbb{C}}_{1}(\alpha)\xi\cdot\xi=+\infty\quad\text{uniformly w.r.t.\ }\xi\in{\mathbb{M}^{n\times n}_{sym}},\color[rgb]{0,0,0}

so that we may assume

(
{\mathbb{C}}_{1}
.6)

\gamma\colon[0,1]\to{\mathbb{R}}^{+}\text{ nondecreasing with }\gamma(\alpha)=0\text{ if and only if }\alpha=0.

This represents the energy blocked by the contact with friction of the lips of the cracks, and it results in a hardening behaviour. Its derivation is based on a micro-mechanical approach.
Observe that
{\mathbb{C}}_{1}
is not coercive uniformly with respect to
\alpha\in[0,1]
since
{\mathbb{C}}_{1}(0)=0
, in contrast to the assumptions on
{\mathbb{C}}(\alpha)
in the incomplete damage models.

The functional in (
2.7
) is well defined if
\alpha\in L^{1}(\Omega;[0,1])
and
p\in{L^{2}(\Omega;{\mathbb{M}^{n\times n}_{sym}})}
. Since in our model the natural space for
p
is

{M_{b}(\Omega\cup\partial_{D}\Omega;{\mathbb{M}^{n\times n}_{sym}})}
, we extend the definition to
\alpha\in H^{1}(\Omega;[0,1])
and to those
p\in{M_{b}(\Omega\cup\partial_{D}\Omega;{\mathbb{M}^{n\times n}_{sym}})}
for which there exist
u\in BD(\Omega)
,
e\in L^{2}(\Omega)
,
w\in H^{1}(\Omega)
satisfying (
2.2
), by setting

{\widetilde{Q}}(\alpha,p):=\begin{cases}\int_{\Omega\setminus\{\alpha=1\}}\hskip-30.00005pt{\mathbb{C}}_{1}(\alpha(x))p(x)\colon p(x){\,\mathrm{d}x}\quad\text{if }|p^{s}|(\{x\in\Omega\cup\partial_{D}\Omega\colon\widetilde{\alpha}(x)>0\})=0,\,p\,\chi_{\{\alpha=1\}}=0,\\
+\infty\quad\text{otherwise,}\end{cases}

where
\widetilde{\alpha}
is the quasicontinuous representative of
\alpha\in H^{1}(\Omega)
and
\widetilde{\alpha}
is defined in
\partial\Omega
through the trace of
\alpha
(we could consider any
H^{1}
extension of
\alpha
in an open set
\Omega^{\prime}\supset\overline{\Omega}
).
Notice that
\widetilde{\alpha}
is well defined up to a negligible set with respect to
\mathrm{Cap}_{2}
; by (

) and properties of

functions,

may concentrate up to sets of nonnegative
{\mathcal{H}}^{n-1}
measure, and not on
\mathrm{Cap}_{\delta}
-negligible sets, for any

. Then it is meaningful to write
|p^{s}|(\{\widetilde{\alpha}>0\})
.
Observe also that
p\in L^{1}(\{\widetilde{\alpha}>0\})
has to be 0 in
\{\alpha=1\}
. This formally corresponds to take
{\mathbb{C}}_{1}(1)=+\infty
, as requested in
[
39
, Section 3.3]
.

The constraint set and its support function.

Let
K
be a closed convex set in
{\mathbb{M}^{n\times n}_{sym}}
containing a ball of radius
r_{H}>0
, that is

(K)

\{\sigma\in{\mathbb{M}^{n\times n}_{sym}}\colon|\sigma|\leq r_{H}\}\subset K\,.

Let us consider the support function
H\colon{\mathbb{M}^{n\times n}_{sym}}\to{\mathbb{R}}^{+}\cup\{0\}

H(\xi):=\sup_{\sigma\in K}\sigma\colon\xi\,,

which, by (
K
), satisfies
H(p)\geq r_{H}|p|
for all

.
We remark that in the applications considered in
[
39
]
, namely the Drucker-Prager criterion (or some variants like Mohr-Coulomb or Hoek-Brown criteria) with kinematical hardening coupled with damage,

is unbounded in the direction of negative hydrostatic matrices: it has the form

K:=\{\sigma\in{\mathbb{M}^{n\times n}_{sym}}\colon\tau\sigma_{m}+\kappa(\sigma_{D})-k\leq 0\}\,,

with
\kappa\colon{\mathbb{M}}^{n{\times}n}_{D}\to[0,+\infty)
convex and positively 1-homogeneous,
\kappa(0)=0
, and

,

two constants.

The plastic potential.

Basing on the theory of convex functions of measures developed in
[
32
]
, we define the non-negative Borel measure

\textbf{H}(p):=\int_{\Omega\cup\partial_{D}\Omega}H\biggl(\frac{dp}{d|p|}(x)\biggr)\,d|p|(x)\,,\quad\text{for }B\subset\Omega\cup\partial_{D}\Omega\text{ Borel.}

If
\textbf{H}(p)
has finite mass, namely it is a bounded Radon measure, we introduce the

plastic potential

{\mathcal{H}}\colon{M_{b}(\Omega\cup\partial_{D}\Omega;{\mathbb{M}^{n\times n}_{sym}})}\to{\mathbb{R}}
by

(2.8)

{\mathcal{H}}(p):=\textbf{H}(p)(\Omega\cup\partial_{D}\Omega)\,.

In this case, the results in
[
23
,
24
]
(see in particular
[
24
, Theorem 2.1]
) give that
H
is expressed through duality formulas. For a bounded smooth open set
\Omega^{\prime}
such that
\Omega\subset\Omega^{\prime}
and
\Omega^{\prime}\cap\partial\Omega=\partial_{D}\Omega
, extending any
p
by 0 on
\Omega^{\prime}\setminus\Omega
, it holds that

(2.9)

\int_{\Omega^{\prime}}\varphi\,\mathrm{d}[\textbf{H}(p)]=\sup_{\sigma\in C_{c}^{\infty}(\Omega^{\prime};K)}\int_{\Omega^{\prime}}\varphi\,\sigma\colon\mathrm{d}p=\sup_{\sigma\in C_{c}(\Omega^{\prime};K)}\int_{\Omega^{\prime}}\varphi\,\sigma\colon\mathrm{d}p=\sup_{\sigma\in L^{1}(\Omega^{\prime},|p|+{\mathcal{L}}^{n};K)}\int_{\Omega^{\prime}}\varphi\,\sigma\colon\mathrm{d}p

for any
\varphi\in C_{c}(\Omega^{\prime})
,
\varphi\geq 0
, and

(2.10)

{\mathcal{H}}(p)=\sup_{\sigma\in C_{c}^{\infty}(\Omega^{\prime};K)}\int_{\Omega^{\prime}}\sigma\colon\mathrm{d}p=\sup_{\sigma\in C_{c}(\Omega^{\prime};K)}\int_{\Omega^{\prime}}\sigma\colon\mathrm{d}p=\sup_{\sigma\in L^{1}(\Omega^{\prime},|p|+{\mathcal{L}}^{n};K)}\int_{\Omega^{\prime}}\sigma\colon\mathrm{d}p,

where
L^{1}(\Omega^{\prime},|p|+{\mathcal{L}}^{n};K)
denotes the space of integrable functions with respect to
|p|+{\mathcal{L}}^{n}
with values in
K
.
Moreover, Reshetnyak Theorem (see
[
4
, Theorem 2.38]
) implies that
{\mathcal{H}}
is sequentially weakly
∗
-lower semicontinuous in
{M_{b}(\Omega\cup\partial_{D}\Omega;{\mathbb{M}^{n\times n}_{sym}})}
.

The plastic dissipation.

We introduce now a term which represents the plastic dissipation in a given time interval.

A function
p\colon[0,T]\to{M_{b}(\Omega\cup\partial_{D}\Omega;{\mathbb{M}^{n\times n}_{sym}})}
will be regarded as a function defined on the time interval
[0,T]

with values in the dual of the separable Banach space
C(\Omega\cup\partial_{D}\Omega;{\mathbb{M}^{n\times n}_{sym}})
, that can be identified with the space of functions in
C(\overline{\Omega};{\mathbb{M}^{n\times n}_{sym}})
vanishing on
\overline{\partial_{N}\Omega}
.
For every
s,t\in[0,T]
with

s\leq t
the total variation of
p
on
[s,t]
is defined by

{\mathcal{V}}(p;s,t)=\sup\biggl\{\sum_{j=1}^{N}\|p(t_{j})-p(t_{j-1})\|_{1}\,\Big|\,s=t_{0}<t_{1}<\dots<t_{N}=t,\,N\in{\mathbb{N}}\biggr\}\,.

For every partition
{\mathcal{P}}
of
[s,t]
, namely
{\mathcal{P}}:=\{t_{i}\}_{0\leq i\leq N}
with
s=t_{0}<t_{1}<\dots<t_{N}=t
, we define

{\mathcal{V}}^{\mathcal{P}}_{\mathcal{H}}(p;s,t):=\sum_{i=1}^{N}{\mathcal{H}}(p(t_{i})-p(t_{i-1}))\,.

The
{\mathcal{H}}
-variation of
p
on
[s,t]

is denoted by
{\mathcal{V}}_{\mathcal{H}}(p;s,t)
and is defined through

(2.11)

\begin{split}{\mathcal{V}}_{\mathcal{H}}(p;s,t):=\sup&\biggl\{\sum_{j=1}^{N}{\mathcal{H}}(p(t_{j})-p(t_{j-1}))\,\Big|\,s=t_{0}<t_{1}<\dots<t_{N}=t,\,N\in{\mathbb{N}}\biggr\}\\
=\sup&\bigl\{{\mathcal{V}}_{\mathcal{H}}^{{\mathcal{P}}}(p;s,t)|\,{\mathcal{P}}\text{ partition of $[s,t]$}\bigr\}.\end{split}

We recall that this notion has been introduced in
[
20
, Appendix]
.

The prescribed boundary displacement and the external loading.
We assume that the prescribed boundary displacement
w
depends on time and satisfies the regularity assumption

(Load.1)

w\in AC({[0,T]};H^{1}({{\mathbb{R}}}^{n};{{\mathbb{R}}}^{n})),

so that the
time derivative

t\mapsto\dot{w}(t)
belongs to
L^{1}([0,T];H^{1}({{\mathbb{R}}}^{n};{{\mathbb{R}}}^{n}))
and
its strain
t\mapsto E\dot{w}(t)
belongs to

L^{1}([0,T];L^{2}({{\mathbb{R}}}^{n};{\mathbb{M}^{n\times n}_{sym}}))
. The prescribed boundary value will be
the trace on
\partial_{D}\Omega
of
w
(again denoted by
w
).
As for the volume force
f
, we assume that

(Load.2)

f\in AC({[0,T]};L^{n}({{\mathbb{R}}}^{n};{{\mathbb{R}}}^{n})).

In the presence of Neumann boundary conditions, the traction is a function

(Load.3)

g\in AC({[0,T]};L^{\infty}(\partial_{N}\Omega)).

We define the
total load

{\mathcal{L}}\in AC({[0,T]};BD(\Omega)^{\prime})
by

(Load.4)

\langle{\mathcal{L}}(t),v\rangle:=\int_{\Omega}f(t)\cdot v{\,\mathrm{d}x}+\int_{\partial_{N}\Omega}g(t)\cdot v{\,\mathrm{d}{\mathcal{H}}^{n-1}},

for every
v\in BD(\Omega)
. Notice that
v\in L^{1^{*}}(\Omega;{{\mathbb{R}}}^{n})
and
v\in L^{1}(\partial\Omega;{{\mathbb{R}}}^{n})
, by embedding properties of
BD
functions.
If we assume only Dirichlet boundary conditions, (
Load.4
) reads as
\langle{\mathcal{L}}(t),v\rangle:=\int_{\Omega}f(t)\cdot v{\,\mathrm{d}x}
.
For the main properties of absolutely continuous functions with values in reflexive Banach spaces we refer to
[
9
, Appendix]
.

We assume the following
safe load
condition: there exists
\varrho\in AC({[0,T]};L^{n}(\Omega;{{\mathbb{R}}}^{n}))
and a constant
\tau_{0}>0
such that for every
t\in{[0,T]}

(Load.5)

\begin{cases}-\mathrm{div}\,\varrho(t)=f(t)\quad\text{a.e.\ in }\Omega\,,\qquad[\varrho(t)\nu]=g(t)\quad{\mathcal{H}}^{n-1}\text{-a.e.\ in }\partial_{N}\Omega,\\
\varrho(t)+\tau\in K\quad\text{for every }|\tau|\leq\tau_{0}.\end{cases}

This is a standard assumption to study existence results for quasistatic evolutions in plasticity. It results in coercivity for the plastic strain in the incremental minimisation problem.
In the condition
[\varrho(t)\nu]=g(t)\quad{\mathcal{H}}^{n-1}\text{-a.e.\ in }\partial_{N}\Omega
,
[\varrho(t)\nu]
is the distribution on
\partial\Omega
defined by

\langle[\varrho(t)\nu],\psi\rangle:=\int_{\Omega}\mathrm{div}\,\varrho(t)\psi{\,\mathrm{d}x}+\int_{\Omega}\varrho(t)\mathrm{E}\psi{\,\mathrm{d}x}\,.

Notice that when
\mathrm{div}\,\varrho\in L^{q}(\Omega;{{\mathbb{R}}}^{n})
,
\varrho\in L^{q}(\Omega;{\mathbb{M}^{n\times n}_{sym}})
, then
[\varrho\nu]
is in the dual space of
W^{q^{\prime},1-q^{\prime}}(\partial\Omega)
.

When dealing with Neumann boundary conditions in the present case, where the constraint set is unbounded in the deviatoric matrices, we enforce a further condition. We have to require that

(Load.6)

|\varrho(t)\colon p|\leq C_{\varrho}|p|\qquad\text{in }{M_{b}(\Omega\cup\partial_{D}\Omega;{\mathbb{M}^{n\times n}_{sym}})},

for
C_{\varrho}>0
independent of
t\in[0,T]
. It is not clear in general how to determine the fields
\varrho
satisfying the safe load condition. We notice that in the case of null forces the safe load condition is satisfied for
\varrho\equiv 0
, and when only a constant pressure
\mathrm{pr}(t)
is applied on
\partial_{N}\Omega
with some Dirichlet conditions on
\partial_{D}\Omega
(with null volume loadings), as in the case of the triaxial test,
\varrho(t)
could be taken constant for every
t
, so that (
Load.6
) is satisfied if
\mathrm{pr}(t)
is bounded uniformly in time. In the case of Dirichlet boundary conditions, we drop the condition on
[\varrho(t)\nu]
in (
Load.5
), besides (
Load.6
).

By (
Load.5
) and (
Load.6
),
we obtain
for every
t\in{[0,T]}
and
(u,e,p)\in\mathrm{A}(w(t))
the following integration by parts formula

(2.12)

\langle\mathcal{L}(t),u\rangle=\langle\varrho(t),e-\mathrm{E}w(t)\rangle_{L^{2}}+\int_{\Omega\cup\partial_{D}\Omega}\varrho(t)\colon\mathrm{d}p+\langle\mathcal{L}(t),w(t)\rangle.

3.
The minimisation problem and the discrete time solutions

In this section we study the minimisation problems entering the time discrete approximation of quasistatic evolutions for the present model. This approximation follows the general scheme of minimising movements.

At each incremental time step, the updated approximate solution is obtained by minimising, among the admissible configurations for the updated external loading, the sum of the internal energy terms, of the loading, and of the dissipation from the approximate solution at the previous time step.

More precisely, for every
k\in{\mathbb{N}}
a sequence of subdivisions
(t_{k}^{i})_{0\leq i\leq k}
of the
interval
[0,T]
is introduced, with

\displaystyle 0=t_{k}^{0}<t_{k}^{1}<\dots<t_{k}^{k-1}<t_{k}^{k}=T\,,

\displaystyle\displaystyle\lim_{k\to\infty}\,\max_{1\leq i\leq k}(t_{k}^{i}-t_{k}^{i-1})=0\,.

Starting from
(\alpha_{k}^{0},u_{k}^{0},e_{k}^{0},p_{k}^{0}):=(\alpha_{0},u_{0},e_{0},p_{0})\in{H^{1}(\Omega;[0,1])}\times\mathrm{A}(w(0))

\min_{0\leq\alpha\leq\alpha_{k}^{i-1},\,(u,e,p)\in\mathrm{A}(w_{k}^{i})}\Big\{{\mathcal{Q}}(e)+D(\alpha)+{\|\nabla\alpha\|_{2}^{2}}+{\widetilde{Q}}(\alpha,p)+{\mathcal{H}}(p-p_{k}^{i-1})-\langle{\mathcal{L}}_{k}^{i},u\rangle\Big\}.

∎

Remark 4.5
