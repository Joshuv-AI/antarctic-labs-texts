Index concepts for linear differential-algebraic equations in finite and infinite dimensions
Mehmet Erbay, Birgit Jacob, Kirsten Morris, Timo Reis, Caren Tischendorf

Abstract
Different index concepts for linear differential-algebraic equations are defined in the general Banach space setting, and compared. For regular finite-dimensional linear differential-algebraic equations, all these indices exist and are equivalent. For infinite-dimensional systems, the situation is more complex. It is proven that although some indices imply others, in general they are not equivalent. The situation is illustrated with a number of examples.



1
Introduction

In this article we take a closer look at the index terms for infinite-dimensional differential-algebraic systems (DAE) of the form

\frac{{\rm d}}{{\rm d}t}Ex(t)=Ax(t)+f(t),\quad t\geq 0,

(1)

where
E\colon\mathcal{X}\to\mathcal{Z}
is a bounded linear operator (denoted by
E\in\mathcal{L}(\mathcal{X},\mathcal{Z})
),
(A,\mathcal{D}(A))
is a closed and densely defined linear operator from
\mathcal{X}
to
\mathcal{Z}
and
f\colon[0,\infty)\to\mathcal{Z}
. Throughout this article,
\mathcal{X}
and
\mathcal{Z}
are Banach spaces and the DAE (
1
) is assumed to be regular. That is,

\rho(E,A)\coloneqq\left\{\lambda\in\mathbb{C}\,\middle|\,(sE-A)^{-1}\in\mathcal{L}(\mathcal{Z},\mathcal{X})\right\}\not=\emptyset.

By a
solution
of (
1
) we mean a
classical solution
, that is, a function
x\colon[0,\infty)\rightarrow\mathcal{D}(A)
such that
Ex(\cdot)
is continuously differentiable as a function with values in
\mathcal{Z}
, and (
1
) is satisfied for every
t\geq 0
.

The index of a DAE can be defined in a number of various ways. Examples include the differentiation index, the nilpotency index, the resolvent index and the radiality index
[
4
,
10
,
18
,
19
]
. Not all indices are defined in the infinite-dimensional case. For instance, the nilpotency index of a DAE demands a Weierstraß form (defined formally below), which is not always available.

Our aim in writing this paper is to ’collect’ all the index terms that are applicable in the infinite-dimensional case, and to characterize and compare them to each other. In particular, we investigate the resolvent index
p_{\rm res\vphantom{d}}^{(E,A)}
, the chain index
p_{\rm chain}^{(E,A)}
, the radiality index
p_{\rm rad}^{(E,A)}
, the nilpotency index
p_{\rm nilp}^{(E,A)}
, the differentiation index
p_{\rm diff}^{(E,A)}
and the perturbation index
p_{\rm pert}^{(E,A)}
. Several of these indices have not previously been defined for infinite-dimensional systems.

One of our main results is that if all the indices mentioned in the previous paragraph exist then

\displaystyle p_{\rm rad}^{(E,A)}+1\geq p_{\rm res\vphantom{d}}^{(E,A)}\geq p_{\rm nilp}^{(E,A)}=p_{\rm diff}^{(E,A)}=p_{\rm chain}^{(E,A)}.

If in addition the operator
A_{1}
in the Weierstraß form generates a
C_{0}
-semigroup, then

\displaystyle p_{\rm rad}^{(E,A)}+1\geq p_{\rm res\vphantom{d}}^{(E,A)}\geq p_{\rm nilp}^{(E,A)}=p_{\rm diff}^{(E,A)}=p_{\rm chain}^{(E,A)}=p_{\rm pert}^{(E,A)}.

Furthermore, Proposition
6.9
implies that in the finite-dimensional case, equality holds in all these bounds.

We conclude the introduction with some notation.
For a
\lambda\in\rho(E,A)
we call
(\lambda E-A)^{-1}
,
R^{E}(\lambda,A)\coloneqq(\lambda E-A)^{-1}E
and
L^{E}(\lambda,A)\coloneqq E(\lambda E-A)^{-1}
the
resolvent
,
right-
E
resolvent
and
left-
E
resolvent
of
A
respectively.

2
Weierstraß form

Consider differential-algebraic systems
of the form (
1
). Let also
\tilde{\mathcal{X}},\tilde{\mathcal{Z}}
be Banach spaces,
\tilde{E}\in\mathcal{L}(\tilde{\mathcal{X}},\tilde{\mathcal{Z}})
and
\tilde{A}\colon\mathcal{D}(\tilde{A})\subseteq\tilde{\mathcal{X}}\to\tilde{\mathcal{Z}}
closed and densely defined.

Definition 2.1
.

Two differential-algebraic systems
\frac{{\rm d}}{{\rm d}t}Ex=Ax
and
\frac{{\rm d}}{{\rm d}t}\tilde{E}x=\tilde{A}x
are
equivalent
, denoted by
(E,A)\sim(\tilde{E},\tilde{A})
, if there are two bounded isomorphisms
P\colon\mathcal{X}\to\tilde{\mathcal{X}}
,
Q\colon\mathcal{Z}\to\tilde{\mathcal{Z}}
, such that
E=Q^{-1}\tilde{E}P
and
A=Q^{-1}\tilde{A}P
.

Definition 2.2
.

A bounded operator
N\in\mathcal{L}(\mathcal{X})
is called
nilpotent
, if there exists a
p\in\mathbb{N}
, such that
N^{l}\neq 0
for all
l<p
and
N^{p}=0
.
p
is called the
degree of nilpotency
.

This definition may be slightly different in other references. For example, in
[
18
]
the degree of nilpotency is
p-1
and not
p
.

Definition 2.3
.

The DAE (
1
) has a
Weierstraß form
, if there exists a Hilbert space
\mathcal{Y}=\mathcal{Y}^{1}\oplus\mathcal{Y}^{2}
, such that

(E,A)\sim\left(\begin{bmatrix}I_{\mathcal{Y}^{1}}&0\\
0&N\end{bmatrix},\begin{bmatrix}A_{1}&0\\
0&I_{\mathcal{Y}^{2}}\end{bmatrix}\right),

(2)

where
N\colon\mathcal{Y}^{2}\to\mathcal{Y}^{2}
is a bounded linear nilpotent operator,
A_{1}\colon D(A_{1})\subseteq\mathcal{Y}^{1}\to\mathcal{Y}^{1}
is a linear operator and
I_{\mathcal{Y}^{i}}
indicates the identity operator on the associated subspace
\mathcal{Y}^{i}
,
i=1,2
.

This form is also known variously as the
quasi-Weierstraß form

[
2
]
or
Weierstraß canonical form

[
10
]
. In finite dimensions the operator
A_{1}
is generally a Jordan matrix
J
. In this case, the Weierstraß form is unique up to isomorphisms and therefore the nilpotency degree of
N
is uniquely determined. To be more precise, assume that
(E,A)
has two different Weierstraß forms
\left(\big[\begin{smallmatrix}I&0\\
0&N_{i}\end{smallmatrix}\big],\big[\begin{smallmatrix}J_{i}&0\\
0&I\end{smallmatrix}\big]\right)
,
i=1,2
. Then the sizes of the Jordan blocks
J_{1},J_{2}
and of the nilpotent operators
N_{1},N_{2}
coincide, as well as the degree of nilpotency of these nilpotent operators
[
10
, Lem. 2.10]
.

In the next few sections we will go through a variety of different index term and generalise/adapt them for the infinite-dimensional case. Most of the terms are already known in the finite-dimensional case.

3
Resolvent index

The
resolvent index
has already been defined in
[
4
, p. 5]
,
[
19
, p. 8]
and
[
5
, ch. 6.1]
. It has the advantage that it does not require a Weierstraß form. Thus, this definition can be easily extended to the infinite-dimensional case. The only difficulty encountered in calculating this index is the calculation of the resolvent and its growth rate, which is a greater hurdle in the infinite-dimensional case.

Definition 3.1

(
resolvent index
)
.

The
resolvent index
of
(E,A)
is the smallest integer
p_{\rm res\vphantom{d}}^{(E,A)}\in\mathbb{N}_{0}
, such that there exists a
\omega\in\mathbb{R}
,
C>0
with
(\omega,\infty)\subseteq\rho(E,A)
and

\left\|(\lambda E-A)^{-1}\right\|\leq C\left|\lambda\right|^{p_{\rm res\vphantom{d}}^{(E,A)}-1}

(3)

for all
\lambda\in(\omega,\infty)
. The resolvent index is called a
complex resolvent index
, denoted by
p_{\rm c,res}^{(E,A)}\in\mathbb{N}_{0}
, if
\mathbb{C}_{{\rm Re}\,>\omega}\subseteq\rho(E,A)
and (
3
) holds for
p_{\rm c,res}^{(E,A)}
.

Note that the resolvent index can also defined in a weaker form as seen in
[
5
, ch. 5& 6]
. Clearly,
p_{\rm res\vphantom{d}}^{(E,A)}\leq p_{\rm c,res}^{(E,A)}
. The next proposition shows that this index is uniquely defined.

Proposition 3.2
.

The resolvent index, given that it exists, is uniquely defined. To be more precise, let
(E,A)\sim(\tilde{E},\tilde{A})
. Then
p_{\mathrm{res}}^{(E,A)}=p_{\mathrm{res}}^{(\tilde{E},\tilde{A})}
and
p_{\mathrm{c,res}}^{(E,A)}=p_{\mathrm{c,res}}^{(\tilde{E},\tilde{A})}
.

Proof.

Since
(E,A)\sim(\tilde{E},\tilde{A})
there exists two isomorphisms
P\colon\mathcal{X}\to\tilde{\mathcal{X}}
,
Q\colon\mathcal{Z}\to\tilde{\mathcal{Z}}
with

E=Q^{-1}\tilde{E}P\quad\text{and}\quad A=Q^{-1}\tilde{A}P.

Assume that
(E,A)
has resolvent index
p_{\rm res\vphantom{d}}^{(E,A)}
. That is, there exists a
C>0
,
\omega\in\mathbb{R}
, such that
(\omega,\infty)\subseteq\rho(E,A)
and

\|(\lambda E-A)^{-1}\|\leq C\left|\lambda\right|^{p_{\rm res\vphantom{d}}^{(E,A)}-1},\quad\lambda>\omega.

Since

(\lambda\tilde{E}-\tilde{A})^{-1}\tilde{E}=P(\lambda E-A)^{-1}EQ^{-1},\quad\lambda>\omega

it follows that
(\omega,\infty)\subseteq\rho(E,A)\subseteq\rho(\tilde{E},\tilde{A})
and

\|(\lambda\tilde{E}-\tilde{A})^{-1}\|\leq\left\|P\right\|\left\|(\lambda E-A)^{-1}\right\|\left\|Q^{-1}\right\|\leq\tilde{C}\left|\lambda\right|^{p_{\rm res\vphantom{d}}^{(E,A)}-1},\quad\lambda>\omega,

for
\tilde{C}\coloneqq C\left\|P\right\|\left\|Q^{-1}\right\|
. Thus, the resolvent index of
(\tilde{E},\tilde{A})
is at most
p_{\rm res\vphantom{d}}^{(E,A)}
. The other estimate follows from an equivalent argument and switching
(E,A)
and
(\tilde{E},\tilde{A})
. The statement concerning the complex resolvent index follows similarly.
∎

Next, we will show the existence of the (complex) resolvent index for a special class of systems, namely
\mathcal{X}=\mathcal{Z}
,
E
is non-negative and
A
is dissipative. Note that we call
E
non-negative, denoted by
E\geq 0
, if
\langle Ex,x\rangle\geq 0
for all
x\in\mathcal{X}
and we call
A
dissipative, if
{\rm Re}\,\langle Ax,x\rangle\leq 0
for all
x\in\mathcal{D}(A)
. Such systems, are known as
port-Hamiltonian DAEs
or
abstract dissipative DAEs
(see
[
5
, ch. 7]
and
[
12
]
).

Theorem 3.3
.

Let
\mathcal{X}=\mathcal{Z}
,
E\in\mathcal{L}(\mathcal{X})
be non-negative self-adjoint and
A\colon\mathcal{D}(A)\subseteq\mathcal{X}\to\mathcal{X}
be dissipative. If there exists a
\omega>0
, such that
(\omega,\infty)\subseteq\rho(E,A)
, then
p_{\mathrm{res}}^{(E,A)}\leq 2
. If also
\mathbb{C}_{{\rm Re}\,>\omega}\subseteq\rho(E,A)
, then
p_{\mathrm{c,res}}^{(E,A)}\leq 3
.

Proof.

For any
x\in\mathcal{X}
define
z=(\lambda E-A)^{-1}x.
By using the dissipativity of
A
and the positivity of
E
we deduce that

\displaystyle{\rm Re}\,\langle(\lambda E-A)^{-1}x,x\rangle=\langle z,Ez\rangle{\rm Re}\,\lambda-{\rm Re}\,\langle z,Az\rangle\geq 0

for all
\lambda\in\rho(E,A)\cap\mathbb{C}_{{\rm Re}\,>0}
, . Thus, for every
x\in\mathcal{X}
the function

f_{x}\colon\lambda\mapsto\langle(\lambda E-A)^{-1}x,x\rangle+\left\|x\right\|^{2}

is positive real. Let
\mu>\omega
. Using
[
6
, Thm. 3]
implies

\displaystyle\left|f_{x}(\lambda)\right|\leq\left|f_{x}(\mu)\right|\frac{\left|\lambda\right|^{2}+\left|\mu\right|^{2}+3\left|\lambda\mu\right|}{\mu{\rm Re}\,\lambda}\leq\left|f_{x}(\mu)\right|\frac{5}{\mu}\frac{\left|\lambda\right|^{2}}{{\rm Re}\,\lambda},\quad\lambda\in\mathbb{C}_{{\rm Re}\,>\mu}.

Hence, together with the Riesz representation theorem we derive

\displaystyle\left\|(\lambda E-A)^{-1}\right\|=\sup_{\begin{subarray}{c}x,y\in\mathcal{X}\\
\|x\|=\|y\|=1\end{subarray}}\left|\langle(\lambda E-A)^{-1}x,y\rangle\right|\leq\sup_{\begin{subarray}{c}x\in\mathcal{X}\\
\left\|x\right\|=1\end{subarray}}2\left|f_{x}(\mu)\right|\leq K\frac{\left|\lambda\right|^{2}}{{\rm Re}\,\lambda},

for all
\lambda\in\mathbb{C}_{{\rm Re}\,>\omega}
with
K=(\left\|(\mu E-A)^{-1}\right\|+1)\frac{10}{\mu}
. Thus, the complex resolvent index is at most
3
and if
\lambda>\omega
is real, then
\frac{\left|\lambda\right|^{2}}{{\rm Re}\,\lambda}=\lambda
and the resolvent index is at most
2
.
∎

Example 3.4
.

(
p_{\rm res\vphantom{d}}^{(E,A)}=2
and
p_{\mathrm{c,res}\vphantom{d}}^{(E,A)}=3
.)

Define
A={\rm diag\,}(A_{0},A_{1},A_{2},\ldots)
with

\displaystyle A_{0}=\begin{bmatrix}0&-1\\
1&0\end{bmatrix},\quad A_{k}=\begin{bmatrix}0&\sqrt{k^{4}+1}\\
-\sqrt{k^{4}+1}&-2\end{bmatrix},\quad k\in\mathbb{N},

and
\mathcal{D}(A)\coloneqq\{x\in\ell^{2}\mid Ax\in\ell^{2}\}
. Then
A
can be extended to
\ell^{2}
, which will denoted by
A_{-1}
. Define
E\in\mathcal{L}(\ell^{2})
,
B\colon\mathbb{R}\to\mathcal{D}(A^{\ast})^{\prime}
and
C\colon\mathcal{D}(A)\to\mathbb{R}
with
E={\rm diag\,}(E_{0},E_{1},E_{2},\ldots)
and
B=(B_{0},B_{1},B_{2},\ldots)^{T}=C^{\ast}
, whereby

\displaystyle E_{0}

\displaystyle=\begin{bmatrix}1&0\\
0&0\end{bmatrix},

\displaystyle E_{k}

\displaystyle=\begin{bmatrix}1&0\\
0&1\end{bmatrix},\quad k\in\mathbb{N},

\displaystyle B_{0}

\displaystyle=\begin{bmatrix}0\\
1\end{bmatrix},

\displaystyle B_{k}

\displaystyle=\begin{bmatrix}0\\
k^{\frac{5}{4}}\end{bmatrix},\quad k\in\mathbb{N}.

Consider the following system

\displaystyle\frac{{\rm d}}{{\rm d}t}\underbrace{\begin{bmatrix}E&0&0\\
0&0&0\\
0&0&0\end{bmatrix}}_{\mathcal{E}}\begin{bmatrix}x_{1}\\
x_{2}\\
x_{3}\end{bmatrix}=\underbrace{\begin{bmatrix}A_{-1}&B&0\\
-C&0&I\\
0&-I&0\end{bmatrix}}_{\mathcal{A}}\begin{bmatrix}x_{1}\\
x_{2}\\
x_{3}\end{bmatrix}

on
\mathcal{X}=\ell^{2}\times\mathbb{R}\times\mathbb{R}
. Obviously,
\mathcal{E}
is non-negative and self-adjoint and, by its construction,
\mathcal{A}
with maximal domain is dissipative. Thus,
(\mathcal{E},\mathcal{A})
satisfies the conditions of Theorem
3.3
.

It will now be shown that
p_{\rm res\vphantom{d}}^{(\mathcal{E},\mathcal{A})}=2
and
p_{\rm c,res}^{(\mathcal{E},\mathcal{A})}=3
. For
s\in\rho(\mathcal{E},\mathcal{A})

(s\mathcal{E}-\mathcal{A})^{-1}=\begin{bmatrix}(sE-A)^{-1}&0&(sE-A)^{-1}B\\
0&0&I\\
-C(sE-A)^{-1}&-I&C(sE-A)^{-1}B\end{bmatrix}.

It can be shown that

G(s)\coloneqq C(sE-A)^{-1}B=s+\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}\frac{k^{\frac{5}{2}}s}{s^{2}+2s+k^{4}+1},

a function that George Weiss (Tel Aviv) scribbled on paper for one of the authors. Thanks for that!

Since
G(s)\geq s
for
s\geq 0
, the resolvent
(s\mathcal{E}-\mathcal{A})^{-1}
grows at least linearly along the real axis and together with Theorem
3.3
this implies
p_{\rm res\vphantom{d}}^{(\mathcal{E},\mathcal{A})}=2
.

Now consider growth in the entire right-hand-plane. Letting
\sigma>0
and
s_{n}\coloneqq\sigma+in^{2}
for
n\in\mathbb{N}
,

\displaystyle{\rm Re}\,G(s_{n})

\displaystyle=\sigma+\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}\frac{k^{\frac{5}{2}}(\sigma((1+\sigma)^{2}+k^{4})+(2+\sigma)n^{4})}{((1+\sigma)^{2}+(k^{4}-n^{4}))^{2}+4(1+\sigma)^{2}n^{4}}

\displaystyle\geq\sigma+\frac{n^{\frac{5}{2}}(\sigma((1+\sigma)^{2}+n^{4})+(2+\sigma)n^{4})}{(1+\sigma)^{4}+4(1+\sigma)^{4}n^{4}}

\displaystyle\geq\frac{2n^{\frac{5}{2}}n^{4}}{(1+\sigma)^{4}(1+4n^{4})}=\frac{2n^{\frac{5}{2}}}{5(1+\sigma)^{4}}\frac{5n^{4}}{1+4n^{4}}

\displaystyle\geq\frac{2n^{\frac{5}{2}}}{5(1+\sigma)^{4}},\quad n\in\mathbb{N}.

By choosing an
c>0
and
N\in\mathbb{N}
such that
|s_{n}|^{\frac{5}{4}}\leq c\frac{2n^{\frac{5}{2}}}{5(1+\sigma)^{4}}
for every
n\geq N
, one obtains

\left|G(s_{n})\right|\geq{\rm Re}\,G(s_{n})\geq\frac{1}{c}\left|s_{n}\right|^{\frac{5}{4}},\quad n\geq N.

Hence, the growth of
G(s_{n})
is more than quadratic along lines
{\rm Re}\,s=\sigma
parallel to the imaginary axis for every
\sigma>0
. Therefore, using Theorem
3.3
it follows that
p_{\mathrm{c,res}}^{(E,A)}=3
.

Example 3.5
.

(A class of well-posed systems with
p_{\rm res\vphantom{d}}^{(E,A)}\leq 2
)

Let
A_{o}
with
\mathcal{D}(A_{o})\subset\mathcal{W}
generate a
C_{0}
-semigroup on
\mathcal{W}
, where
\mathcal{W}
is a Hilbert space. We indicate the growth bound of the semigroup by
\omega
. For
b\in\mathcal{W}
and
c\in\mathcal{D}(A_{o}^{*})
with
\langle b,c\rangle\not=0
define the operators
Bu=bu
where
u\in\mathbb{C}
and
Cz=\langle z,c\rangle
for any
z\in\mathcal{W}
. We define the DAE on
\mathcal{Z}=\mathcal{W}\times\mathbb{C}
by

\frac{{\rm d}}{{\rm d}t}\underbrace{\begin{bmatrix}I&0\\
0&0\end{bmatrix}}_{\eqqcolon E}x(t)=\underbrace{\begin{bmatrix}A_{o}&B\\
C&0\end{bmatrix}}_{\eqqcolon A}x(t)\quad t\geq 0.

(4)

Defining for
s\in\rho(A_{o}),

G(s)=\langle(sI-A_{o})^{-1}b,c\rangle

we obtain

(sE-A)^{-1}=\begin{bmatrix}(sI-A_{o})^{-1}-(sI-A_{o})^{-1}BG(s)^{-1}C(sI-A_{o})^{-1}&(sI-A_{o})^{-1}BG(s)^{-1}\\

Note that the condition

.
Thus, the resolvent of

Since

\lim_{s\to\infty}sG(s)=\langle c,b\rangle,

4
Chain index

The concept of Wong sequences
[
20
]
are needed to define the chain index. For
\mathcal{X}=\mathcal{Z}=\mathbb{K}^{n}
(
n\in\mathbb{N}
) we define

\displaystyle\mathcal{V}_{0}

\displaystyle\coloneqq\mathbb{K}^{n},

\displaystyle\mathcal{V}_{i+1}

\displaystyle\coloneqq A^{-1}(E(V_{i})),

\displaystyle\quad i\in\mathbb{N},

\displaystyle\mathcal{W}_{0}

\displaystyle\coloneqq\left\{0\right\},

\displaystyle\mathcal{W}_{i+1}

\displaystyle\coloneqq E^{-1}(A(W_{i})),

\displaystyle\quad i\in\mathbb{N}.

Thus, there exists
k,l\in\mathbb{N}
such that
\mathcal{V}_{i+1}\subsetneq\mathcal{V}_{i}
,
0\leq i<l
,
\mathcal{W}_{j}\subsetneq\mathcal{W}_{j+1}
,
0\leq j<k
and
\mathcal{V}_{l}=\mathcal{V}_{l+r}
,
\mathcal{W}_{k}=\mathcal{W}_{l+r}
for all
r\in\mathbb{N}

[
2
, p. 3]
. Let
x_{k}\in\mathcal{W}_{k}
. Then, there exists a
x_{k-1}\in\mathcal{W}_{k-1}
with
Ex_{k}=Ax_{k-1}
. Inductively, one obtains

\displaystyle\begin{split}Ex_{1}&=0,\\
Ex_{2}&=Ax_{1},\\
&\;\;\vdots\\
Ex_{k}&=Ax_{k-1},\end{split}

(5)

x_{r}\in\mathcal{W}_{r}
,
0\leq r\leq k
, which is called a
chain of length
k
. The concept of such a chain is not new and can be found in
[
2
, p. 15]
for the finite-dimensional case or
[
18
, p. 18]
for arbitrary Banach spaces. It should be mentioned that the latter reference does not call (
5
) a chain.

Definition 4.1

(
chain index
)
.

We call
(x_{1},\ldots,x_{p})\in\mathcal{D}(A)^{p-1}\times\mathcal{X}
,
p\in\mathbb{N}
, a
chain of

(E,A)
of length
p
, if

x_{1}\in{\rm ker}\,E\setminus\{0\}
,
x_{k}\notin{\rm ker}\,A
,
k=1,\ldots,p-1
, and

Ex_{k+1}=Ax_{k},\quad k=1,\ldots,p-1.

(6)

The
chain index

p_{\rm chain}^{(E,A)}\in\mathbb{N}_{0}
of
(E,A)
is the supremum over all chain lengths of
(E,A)
.

With this definition we exclude the case where the chain index can be infinite. Such an example is obtained when
(E,A)
has a form as in (
2
), whereby
N
is quasi-nilpotent. For such systems the chain index is not defined.

Proposition 4.2
.

The chain index is, given that it exists, uniquely defined. To be more specific, let
(E,A)\sim(\tilde{E},\tilde{A})
. Then
p_{\mathrm{chain}}^{(E,A)}=p_{\mathrm{chain}}^{(\tilde{E},\tilde{A})}
.

Proof.

Since
(E,A)\sim(\tilde{E},\tilde{A})
there exists two isomorphisms
P\colon\mathcal{X}\to\tilde{\mathcal{X}}
,
Q\colon\mathcal{Z}\to\tilde{\mathcal{Z}}
, such that

E=Q^{-1}\tilde{E}P\quad\text{and}\quad A=Q^{-1}\tilde{A}P.

Let
p_{\rm chain}^{(E,A)}\in\mathbb{N}
. Thus, there exists a chain
(x_{1},\ldots,x_{p_{\rm chain}^{(E,A)}})
, such that

Ex_{1}=0,Ex_{2}=Ax_{1},\ldots,Ex_{p_{\rm chain}^{(E,A)}}=Ax_{p_{\rm chain}^{(E,A)}-1}.

Since
E=Q^{-1}\tilde{E}P
,
A=Q^{-1}\tilde{A}P
and
Q
is an isomorphism,

\tilde{E}Px_{1}=0,\tilde{E}Px_{2}=\tilde{A}Px_{1},\ldots,\tilde{E}Px_{p_{\rm chain}^{(E,A)}}=\tilde{A}Px_{p_{\rm chain}^{(E,A)}-1}.

Because
P
is an isomorphism,
Px_{1}\neq 0
and thus
Px_{1}\in{\rm ker}\,\tilde{E}\backslash\{0\}
. If we assume that
Px_{k}\in{\rm ker}\,\tilde{A}
for any
1\leq k\leq p_{\rm chain}^{(E,A)}-1
, then
0=Q^{-1}\tilde{A}Px_{k}=Ax_{k}
, which would be a contradiction to
x_{k}\in\mathcal{X}\backslash{\rm ker}\,A
. Therefore,
Px_{k}\notin{\rm ker}\,\tilde{A}
for all
1\leq k\leq p_{\rm chain}^{(E,A)}-1
. Hence,
(Px_{1},\ldots,Px_{p_{\rm chain}^{(E,A)}})
denotes a chain of
(\tilde{E},\tilde{A})
of length
p_{\rm chain}^{(E,A)}
. Therefore, the chain index of
(\tilde{E},\tilde{A})
is bounded from below by
p_{\rm chain}^{(E,A)}
and, equivalently, the chain index of
(E,A)
is bounded from below by
p_{\rm chain}^{(\tilde{E},\tilde{A})}
.
∎

5
Radiality index

In this section we introduce a less well-known index, the
radiality index
.

Definition 5.1

(
radiality index
)
.

The
radiality index
of
(E,A)
is the smallest number
p_{\rm rad}^{(E,A)}\in\mathbb{N}_{0}
, such that there exists a
\omega\in\mathbb{R}
,
C>0
with
(\omega,\infty)\subseteq\rho(E,A)
and

\displaystyle\begin{split}\left\|(\lambda_{0}E-A)^{-1}E\cdot\ldots\cdot(\lambda_{p_{\rm rad}^{(E,A)}}E-A)^{-1}E\right\|&\leq C\prod_{k=0}^{p_{\rm rad}^{(E,A)}}\frac{1}{\left|\lambda_{k}-\omega\right|},\\
\left\|E(\lambda_{0}E-A)^{-1}\cdot\ldots\cdot E(\lambda_{p_{\rm rad}^{(E,A)}}E-A)^{-1}\right\|&\leq C\prod_{k=0}^{p_{\rm rad}^{(E,A)}}\frac{1}{\left|\lambda_{k}-\omega\right|}\end{split}

(7)

for all
\lambda_{0},\ldots,\lambda_{p_{\rm{rad}}^{(E,A)}}>\omega
. The radiality index is called a
complex radiality index
, denoted by
p_{\rm c,rad}^{(E,A)}
, if
\mathbb{C}_{{\rm Re}\,>\omega}\subseteq\rho(E,A)
and (
7
) holds for
p_{\rm{rad}}^{(E,A)}
.

Clearly,
p_{\rm rad}^{(E,A)}\leq p_{\rm c,rad}^{(E,A)}
. The radiality index
p=p_{\rm rad}^{(E,A)}\in\mathbb{N}_{0}
, originally known as
weak
(E,p)
-radiality

[
18
, p. 21]
, is not a commonly used index in finite-dimensional problems. However, the radiality index is one of the most useful index terms in infinite dimensions (see
[
18
,
8
]
).

To get a better understanding for this definition one can start by looking at the radiality index with
p_{\rm rad}^{(E,A)}=0
,
\mathcal{X}=\mathcal{Z}
and
E=I_{\mathcal{X}}
. Then, (
7
) translates into the well known Hille-Yosida type estimate. This suggests that the radiality index implies further well-posedness results. In fact, as soon as slightly stronger assumptions, namely strong
(E,p)
-radiality, are considered, one obtains not only the well-posedness of the system, but also the existence of a Weierstraß-form
[
18
, Sec. 2.5 & 2.6]
.

Proposition 5.2
.

The radiality index is unique. In fact, let
(E,A)\sim(\tilde{E},\tilde{A})
. Then
p_{\mathrm{rad}}^{(E,A)}=p_{\mathrm{rad}}^{(\tilde{E},\tilde{A})}
and
p_{\mathrm{c,rad}}^{(E,A)}=p_{\mathrm{c,rad}}^{(\tilde{E},\tilde{A})}
.

Proof.

Since
(E,A)\sim(\tilde{E},\tilde{A})
there exists two isomorphisms
P\colon\mathcal{X}\to\tilde{\mathcal{X}}
,
Q\colon\mathcal{Z}\to\tilde{\mathcal{Z}}
, such that

E=Q^{-1}\tilde{E}P\quad\text{and}\quad A=Q^{-1}\tilde{A}P.

Assume, that
(E,A)
has radiality index
p_{\rm rad}^{(E,A)}
. Thus, there exists positive constants
C>0
,
\omega>0
, such that
(\omega,\infty)\subseteq\rho(E,A)
and

\displaystyle\left\|(\lambda_{0}E-A)^{-1}E\cdot\ldots\cdot(\lambda_{p_{\rm rad}^{(E,A)}}E-A)^{-1}E\right\|

\displaystyle\leq\frac{C}{(\lambda_{0}-\omega)\cdot\ldots\cdot(\lambda_{p_{\rm rad}^{(E,A)}}-\omega)},

\displaystyle\left\|E(\lambda_{0}E-A)^{-1}\cdot\ldots\cdot E(\lambda_{p_{\rm rad}^{(E,A)}}E-A)^{-1}\right\|

\displaystyle\leq\frac{C}{(\lambda_{0}-\omega)\cdot\ldots\cdot(\lambda_{p_{\rm rad}^{(E,A)}}-\omega)},

for all
\lambda_{0},\ldots,\lambda_{p_{\rm rad}^{(E,A)}}\in\rho(E,A)
. Since

P(\lambda E-A)^{-1}Q^{-1}=(\lambda\tilde{E}-\tilde{A})^{-1}

for all
\lambda\in\rho(E,A)
we derive
(\omega,\infty)\subseteq\rho(E,A)\subseteq\rho(\tilde{E},\tilde{A})
and therefore

\displaystyle\|(\lambda_{0}\tilde{E}-\tilde{A})^{-1}\tilde{E}\cdot\ldots

\displaystyle\cdot(\lambda_{p_{\rm rad}^{(E,A)}}\tilde{E}-\tilde{A})^{-1}\tilde{E}\|

\displaystyle\leq\left\|P\right\|\|(\lambda_{0}E-A)^{-1}E\cdot\ldots\cdot(\lambda_{p_{\rm rad}^{(E,A)}}E-A)^{-1}E\|\|P^{-1}\|

\displaystyle\leq\frac{\tilde{C}}{(\lambda_{0}-\omega)\cdot\ldots\cdot(\lambda_{p_{\rm rad}^{(E,A)}}-\omega)},

\displaystyle\|\tilde{E}(\lambda_{0}\tilde{E}-\tilde{A})^{-1}\cdot\ldots

\displaystyle\cdot\tilde{E}(\lambda_{p_{\rm rad}^{(E,A)}}\tilde{E}-\tilde{A})^{-1}\|

\displaystyle\leq\left\|Q\right\|\|E(\lambda_{0}E-A)^{-1}\cdot\ldots\cdot E(\lambda_{p_{\rm rad}^{(E,A)}}E-A)^{-1}\|\left\|Q^{-1}\right\|

\displaystyle\leq\frac{\tilde{C}}{(\lambda_{0}-\omega)\cdot\ldots\cdot(\lambda_{p_{\rm rad}^{(E,A)}}-\omega)}

for all
\lambda_{0},\ldots,\lambda_{\rm rad}^{(E,A)}>\omega
, for
\tilde{C}\coloneqq 2C\max\{\left\|P\right\|,\left\|P^{-1}\right\|,\left\|Q\right\|,\left\|Q^{-1}\right\|\}
. Consequently,
(\tilde{E},\tilde{A})
has at most radiality index
p_{\rm rad}^{(E,A)}
. By an identical argument,
(E,A)
has at most radiality index
p_{\rm rad}^{(\tilde{E},\tilde{A})}
. The equality
p_{\mathrm{c,rad}}^{(E,A)}=p_{\mathrm{c,rad}}^{(\tilde{E},\tilde{A})}
is shown in the same way.
∎

The following example shows that in general existence of the resolvent index does not imply that the radiality index exists.

Example 5.5
.

(A system where the resolvent index exists, but the radiality index does not.)
Let
\mathcal{X}=L^{2}(0,\infty)\times\mathbb{R}
and define

\displaystyle E=\begin{bmatrix}I&0\\
0&0\end{bmatrix},\quad A=\begin{bmatrix}\partial_{x}&0\\
\delta_{0}&1\end{bmatrix}

with
\mathcal{D}(A)\coloneqq H^{1}_{0}(0,\infty)\times\mathbb{R}
, where
\delta_{0}\colon L^{2}(0,\infty)\to\mathbb{R},x\mapsto x(0)
. Let
\lambda>0
and
\Big(\begin{smallmatrix}f\\
g\end{smallmatrix}\Big)\in\mathcal{X}
. Then

\displaystyle(\lambda E-A)\begin{pmatrix}x\\
y\end{pmatrix}=\begin{pmatrix}f\\
g\end{pmatrix}

if and only if

\displaystyle\begin{pmatrix}x\\
y\end{pmatrix}=\begin{pmatrix}\int_{\cdot}^{\infty}\mathrm{e}^{\lambda(\cdot-s)}f(s)\,\mathrm{d}s\\
-g-\int_{0}^{\infty}\mathrm{e}^{-\lambda s}f(s)\,\mathrm{d}s\end{pmatrix}.

Hence,

\displaystyle(\lambda E-A)^{-1}\begin{pmatrix}f\\
g\end{pmatrix}=\begin{pmatrix}\int_{\cdot}^{\infty}\mathrm{e}^{\lambda(\cdot-s)}f(s)\,\mathrm{d}s\\
-g-\int_{0}^{\infty}\mathrm{e}^{\lambda(0-s)}f(s)\,\mathrm{d}s\end{pmatrix}

and
\left\|(\lambda E-A)^{-1}\right\|\leq M=M\lambda^{0}
for a
M>0
for all
\lambda\geq 0
. Thus,
(E,A)
has resolvent index
p_{\rm res}^{(E,A)}=1
. Furthermore, we have

\displaystyle\left((\lambda E-A)^{-1}E\right)^{p+1}\begin{pmatrix}f\\
g\end{pmatrix}=\begin{pmatrix}\int_{\cdot}^{\infty}\left(\int_{s_{p+1}}^{\infty}\left(\ldots\left(\int_{s_{2}}^{\infty}\mathrm{e}^{\lambda(\cdot-s_{1})}f(s_{1})\,\mathrm{d}s_{1}\right)\ldots\right)\,\mathrm{d}s_{p}\right)\,\mathrm{d}s_{p+1}\\
-\int_{0}^{\infty}\left(\int_{s_{p+1}}^{\infty}\left(\ldots\left(\int_{s_{2}}^{\infty}\mathrm{e}^{\lambda(0-s_{1})}f(s_{1})\,\mathrm{d}s_{1}\right)\ldots\right)\,\mathrm{d}s_{p}\right)\,\mathrm{d}s_{p+1}\end{pmatrix}.

Let
f(\tau)=\tau\mathrm{e}^{-\lambda\tau}
. Then,
\left\|f\right\|_{L^{2}}=\frac{1}{\lambda^{\frac{3}{2}}}
and

\displaystyle\begin{split}\left\|\left((\lambda E-A)^{-1}E\right)^{p+1}\right\|&\geq\left\|\left((\lambda E-A)^{-1}E\right)^{p+1}\begin{pmatrix}\lambda^{\frac{3}{2}}f\\
0\end{pmatrix}\right\|\\
&\geq\left|\int_{0}^{\infty}\left(\int_{s_{p+1}}^{\infty}\left(\cdots\left(\int_{s_{2}}^{\infty}\mathrm{e}^{-2\lambda s_{1}}s_{1}\,\mathrm{d}s_{1}\right)\right)\,\mathrm{d}s_{p}\right)\,\mathrm{d}s_{p+1}\right|\\
&=\frac{p+1}{2^{p+1}}\frac{1}{\lambda^{p+1-\frac{3}{2}}}\end{split}

(11)

for every
p\in\mathbb{N}
. Consequently, the radiality index does not exist. (Because if it exists, then there would exist
C>0
,
\omega>0
, such that

\displaystyle\left\|\left((\lambda E-A)^{-1}E\right)^{p+1}\right\|\leq C\frac{1}{(\lambda-\omega)^{p+1}}

for all
\lambda>\omega
, which would contradict (
11
).)

6
Nilpotency index

Next, we are going to look at what is probably the best known index, the
nilpotency index
(also known as the
Weierstraß-index
). As the alternative term suggests, the most important part of the definition is the existence of the Weierstraß form.

Definition 6.1

(
nilpotency index
)
.

Assume, that the DAE (
1
) has a Weierstraß form given by (
2
). Then, the
nilpotency index
of
(E,A)
, denoted by
p_{\rm nilp}^{(E,A)}\in\mathbb{N}_{0}
, is the nilpotency degree of
N
if it is present and
0
if
N
is absent. In the latter case one has
(E,A)\sim(I_{\mathcal{Y}^{1}},A_{1})
in (
2
).

Proposition 6.2
.

Assume that the DAE (
1
) has a Weierstraß form. Let
\lambda\in\mathbb{C}
such that
\lambda E-A
is bijective (which exists by our regularity assumption on (
1
)). Then the nilpotency index of
(E,A)
is
is the smallest number
k\in\mathbb{N}
, such that

{\rm ker}\,\big((\lambda E-A)^{-1}E\big)^{k}={\rm ker}\,\big((\lambda E-A)^{-1}E\big)^{k+1}.

In particular, the nilpotency index is well-defined.

Proof.

Assume that

P\colon\mathcal{X}\to\mathcal{Y}^{1}\times\mathcal{Y}^{2}
,
Q\colon\mathcal{Z}\to\mathcal{Y}^{1}\times\mathcal{Y}^{2}
, such that

E=Q^{-1}\begin{bmatrix}I_{\mathcal{Y}^{1}}&0\\
0&N\end{bmatrix}P,\quad A=Q^{-1}\begin{bmatrix}A_{1}&0\\
0&I_{\mathcal{Y}^{2}}\end{bmatrix}P.

Then the result follows, since for all
k\in\mathbb{N}
,

\big((\lambda E-A)^{-1}E\big)^{k}=Q\begin{bmatrix}(\lambda I_{\mathcal{Y}^{1}}-A_{1})^{-k}&0\\
0&(\lambda N-I_{\mathcal{Y}^{2}})^{-1}N^{k}\end{bmatrix}Q^{-1}.\qed

According to this definition, the nilpotency index is always a natural number (including
0
).
This means that the nilpotency index can never be
\infty
, since we require a nilpotent operator
N
in (
2
). To handle this more general situation, one would have to replace the nilpotency of
N
with
quasi-nilpotency
, namely with
\sigma(N)=\{0\}
. For example,
N\colon\ell^{2}\to\ell^{2}
,
(x_{1},x_{2},,x_{3},\ldots)\mapsto(0,\frac{x_{1}}{2^{1}},\frac{x_{2}}{2^{2}},\frac{x_{3}}{2^{3}},\ldots)
.

A disadvantage of the nilpotency index is that it requires the Weierstraß form. In finite dimensions one only needs the system to be regular to obtain such a form, see, for instance,
[
10
, Def. 2.9]
for a constructive procedure. There is no standard procedure to obtain the Weierstraß form for infinite-dimensional systems, and, in fact, it has not been proven that such a form always exists.

Proposition 6.3
.

(a)

If the nilpotency index exists, then the chain index also exists with
p_{\rm nilp}^{(E,A)}=p_{\rm chain}^{(E,A)}
.

(b)

If the nilpotency index and resolvent index exist, then
p_{\rm res\vphantom{d}}^{(E,A)}\geq p_{\rm nilp}^{(E,A)}
.

(c)

If the nilpotency index and radiality index exist, then

We start with the proof of Part (
a
).
Since

, with

E=Q^{-1}\begin{bmatrix}I_{\mathcal{Y}^{1}}&0\\
0&N\end{bmatrix}P\quad\text{and}\quad A=Q^{-1}\begin{bmatrix}A_{1}&0\\
0&I_{\mathcal{Y}^{2}}\end{bmatrix}P.

(12)

For simplicity we will show, that

(because then the rest follows from Proposition
4.2
and
6.2
). Let

x_{k,2}\end{smallmatrix}\right)
,

is not possible. Thus, the existence of a Weierstraß form implies that the chain index also exists and that it is at most

. In order to prove that these two index-terms are equal, we need to show that there does exists a chain of length

We now prove Part (

7
Differentiation index

The differentiation index is based on taking formal derivatives up to a certain order which leads to an ordinary differential equation. For details, see, for instance,
[
11
, Sec. 3.10]
and
[
10
, Sec. 3.3]
. Here we present a generalization of this concept to abstract differential-algebraic equations of the form
(
1
)
with the restriction that only the case where
f\equiv 0
is considered. An
extension to nonzero
f
is a subject for future research.
Using the fact that the operators
E
and
A
do not depend on time, the
k
-th formal derivative of (
1
) with
f=0
is

\frac{{\rm d}}{{\rm d}t}E\frac{d^{k}}{dt^{k}}x(t)=A\frac{d^{k}}{dt^{k}}x(t).

The collection of the first
\mu
formal derivatives of (
1
) is

\underbrace{\begin{bmatrix}A&-E\\
&A&-E\\
&&\ddots&\ddots\\
&&&A&-E\end{bmatrix}}_{\eqqcolon M_{\mu}}\begin{pmatrix}x(t)\\
\frac{{\rm d}}{{\rm d}t}x(t)\\
\vdots\\
\frac{d^{\mu}}{dt^{\mu}}x(t)\\
\frac{d^{\mu+1}}{dt^{\mu+1}}x(t)\end{pmatrix}=0

(26)

which will be referred to as a
derivative array of order

\mu
. The operator
M_{\mu}
maps from
\mathcal{D}(A)^{\mu+1}\times\mathcal{X}
to
\mathcal{Z}^{\mu+1}
.

Definition 7.1

(
Differentiation index
)
.

Let
\mathcal{X},\mathcal{Z}
be Banach spaces, and let a linear differential-algebraic equation

\frac{{\rm d}}{{\rm d}t}Ex(t)=Ax(t)
be given, where
E\in\mathcal{L}(\mathcal{X},\mathcal{Z})
,

(A,\mathcal{D}(A))
is a closed and densely defined linear
operator from
\mathcal{X}
to
\mathcal{Z}
. The DAE
\frac{{\rm d}}{{\rm d}t}Ex(t)=Ax(t)
has differentiation index
p_{\rm diff}^{(E,A)}\in\mathbb{N}_{0}
, if the following holds:

(a)

There exists a Banach space
\tilde{\mathcal{X}}
, such that
\tilde{\mathcal{X}}
is densely embedded into
\mathcal{X}
, an operator
(S,\mathcal{D}(S))

which is the generator of a strongly continuous semigroup on
\tilde{\mathcal{X}}
,
and the operator
M_{p_{\rm diff}^{(E,A)}}
as defined in (
26
) satisfies

\begin{pmatrix}x_{0}\\
x_{1}\\
\vdots\\
x_{p_{\rm diff}+1}\end{pmatrix}\in{\rm ker}\,M_{p_{\rm diff}^{(E,A)}}\quad\Longrightarrow\,x_{0}\in\mathcal{D}(S)\text{ with }x_{1}=Sx_{0}.

(b)

The number
p_{\rm diff}^{(E,A)}
is minimal with the properties in (a).

We call

\frac{{\rm d}}{{\rm d}t}x(t)=Sx(t)

(27)

an

of (

.

Before establishing a connection between the solutions of the abstract completion ODE and the DAE itself, we introduce a lemma that characterizes solutions.
This result is a direct consequence of the integration by parts formula. It is therefore omitted.

Lemma 7.2

Let

be Banach spaces, and let a linear differential-algebraic equation

(b)

Finally, we define the perturbation index for DAEs on possibly infinite-dimensional spaces. As the name indicates, the perturbation index is a measure of sensitivity of solutions with respect to perturbations of the problem.

Remark 8.4
.

In
[
15
]
, a class of linear infinite-dimensional differential-algebraic systems was examined, which do not necessarily have a nilpotency index. It was revealed that a perturbation analysis necessitates the consideration of stronger norms for the initial value. These norms may correspond to a type of spatial perturbation index for systems governed by partial differential-algebraic equations.

9
Summary

The purpose of this paper was, as explained in the introduction, to review the common indices for DAEs and to extend them to infinite-dimensional DAEs where this has not already been done. Rigorous definitions were given for the nilpotency, resolvent, radiality, chain, differentiation and perturbation indices. The definitions of the differentiation and perturbation indices are as far as we know, new in the Banach space setting. Unlike finite-dimensional DAEs, the existence of an index is not guaranteed, and furthermore the various indices are not equivalent for general DAEs. Each index reveals different information about the system. We were able to show that in some cases existence of a particular index will imply existence of some other other indices. One implication is that the chain index is the least restrictive index since existence of any of the other indices implies existence of the chain index, as well as a bound on the chain index.

This work has revealed a number of open questions for DAEs on Banach spaces. Under what conditions does a particular index exist? If two indices exist, when are they equal? Which indices are more useful for the study of DAEs? It is hoped that this paper will inspire research on these and related questions.
