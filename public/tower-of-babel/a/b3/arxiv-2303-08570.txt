Abstract
. We provide a direct proof of existence and uniqueness of weak solutions to a broad family of strongly nonlinear elliptic equations with lower-order terms. The leading part of the operator satisfies general growth conditions settling the problem in the framework of fully anisotropic and inhomogeneous Musielak–Orlicz spaces generated by an N N -function M : Ω × ℝ d → [ 0 , ∞ ) M:\Omega\times{\mathbb{R}}^{d}\to{[0,\infty)} . Neither ∇ 2 \nabla_{2} nor Δ 2 \Delta_{2} conditions are imposed on M M . Our results cover among others problems with anisotropic polynomial, Orlicz, variable exponent, and double phase growth.



1.
Introduction

In this paper we investigate the following strongly nonlinear problem

(1.1)

\left\{\begin{array}[]{cl}-\DIV\big({\mathcal{A}}(x,\nabla u)+\Phi(u)\big)+b(x,u)=\DIV F&\qquad\mathrm{in}\qquad\Omega,\\
u(x)=0&\qquad\mathrm{on}\qquad\partial\Omega,\end{array}\right.

where
\Omega
is a bounded Lipschitz domain in
{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
,
d>1
.
The growth and coercivity of the vector field
{\mathcal{A}}
are assumed to be controlled by a generalized anisotropic
N
-function
M:\Omega\times{\mathbb{R}}^{d}\to{[0,\infty)}
,
\Phi\in L^{\infty}(\Omega,{\mathbb{R}}^{d})
is a continuous vector field,
b
is a function satisfying a sign condition without any extra growth conditions and the vector field
F
has growth dictated by the conjugate function to
M
. The considered framework is described in
[
11
]
. We briefly recall the definitions of the involved
N
-functions, conjugates, and Musielak–Orlicz spaces in Section
2
. In the present study we do not impose
\nabla_{2}
nor
\Delta_{2}
condition on
M
, but we assume the modular density of smooth functions in the related Sobolev-type space that can be inferred from balanced behaviour of
M
. In this setting we show the existence of weak solutions by a direct reasoning that does not rely on a regularisation of isotropic equation. The result is supplied with a short proof of uniqueness.

Study of nonlinear problems was initiated in 1960s by Browder
[
6
]
, Hess
[
32
]
, Leray and Lions
[
34
]
and many others. In the beginning the considered operators were assumed to satisfy polynomial growth and coercivity conditions. The typical example of such an operator is the
p
-Laplacian
-\DIV{\mathcal{A}}(x,\nabla u)=-\Delta_{p}u=-\DIV(|\nabla u|^{p-2}\nabla u)
for
1<p<\infty
. Henceforth, the problem was stated in the reflexive setting of the classical Sobolev spaces. In recent years, there has been an increasing interest in the study of existence of solutions for elliptic problems of nonstandard growth. See
[
7
,
30
,
37
]
and references therein for a pretty complete account of new developments. Nowadays, the field of nonlinear elliptic boundary value problems aims to embrace investigations on problems admitting

•

general growth – when the power function governing the growth of the operator is substituted by an
N
-function
M(x,\xi)=M(|\xi|)
, which do not necessarily satisfy the so-called
\Delta_{2}
-condition (being a necessary condition for an Orlicz space
L_{M}
to be reflexive);

•

inhomogeneity – when the growth of the operator could be controlled by an
x
-dependent function e.g.
M(x,\xi)=|\xi|^{p(x)}
(which results in the lack of the density of smooth functions in
L^{p(\cdot)}
, if
p(\cdot)
is not regular enough);

•

anisotropy – when the growth of the operator is governed by a function depending on the full vector of
\xi
, not just its length
|\xi|
. For example,
M(x,\xi)=|\xi_{1}|^{p}\log(1+|\xi_{1}-\xi_{2}|)
for
\xi=(\xi_{1},\xi_{2})
.

There are a lot of important ideas introduced to overcome the abovementioned challenges. The difficulty caused by the lack of reflexivity of
L_{M}
under non-doubling regime was avoided by the idea of the complementary systems in Orlicz–Sobolev spaces. Contributions in this direction were initiated by Donaldson
[
14
]
and continued by Gossez
[
19
,
20
,
21
]
and Mustonen and Tienari
[
38
]
. We refer to
[
2
,
3
,
10
,
26
]
for analysis of problems in anisotropic Orlicz spaces governed by possibly fully anisotropic modular function, which is independent of the spacial variable. None of these contributions, however, provides a direct proof of existence to PDEs. Note that the complementary systems are very homogeneous by their nature. In the typical inhomogeneous case of variable exponent problems, the setting fall into the realm of reflexive spaces, and consequently the general theory exposed e.g. in
[
33
]
yields existence to the problems with regular data. In
[
18
,
29
,
36
]
isotropic, separable, and reflexive Musielak–Orlicz spaces are employed and
[
15
]
concerns separable, but not reflexive Musielak–Orlicz spaces. Existence to problems that are in the same time of general growth, inhomogeneous, and fully anisotropic were studied in
[
9
,
11
,
12
,
24
,
25
,
31
,
35
,
42
]
, but none of them provide a direct proof. Anisotropic problems with lower-order terms are less understood – we can only refer to
[
13
,
27
]
, but they do not cover our generality of the problem.

We are interested in a short and compact proofs of the existence and uniqueness of solutions to (
1.1
) under possibly general regime under the framework of
[
11
]
. Let us lay out our structural conditions for capturing inhomogeneous and anisotropic problems more precisely.

Assumptions.
We consider a vector field
{\mathcal{A}}:\Omega\times{\mathbb{R}}^{d}\to{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
belonging to a Musielak–Orlicz class. Namely, we assume that a function
{\mathcal{A}}:\Omega\times{\mathbb{R}}^{d}\to{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
satisfies the following conditions:

(A1)

{\mathcal{A}}
is a Carathéodory’s function;

(A2)

{\mathcal{A}}(x,0)=0
for almost every
x\in\Omega
and there exists an
N
-function
M:\Omega\times{\mathbb{R}}^{d}\to{[0,\infty)}
and constants
c_{1}^{\mathcal{A}},c_{2}^{\mathcal{A}},c_{3}^{\mathcal{A}},c_{4}^{\mathcal{A}}>0
such that for all
\xi\in{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
we have

{\mathcal{A}}(x,\xi)\cdot\xi\geq M(x,c_{1}^{\mathcal{A}}\xi)-h_{1}(x)

and

c_{2}^{\mathcal{A}}M^{\ast}(x,c_{3}^{\mathcal{A}}{\mathcal{A}}(x,\xi))\leq M(x,c_{4}^{\mathcal{A}}\xi)+h_{2}(x),

where
M^{\ast}
is the conjugate to
M
and
h_{1},h_{2}\in L^{1}(\Omega)
;

(A3)

For all
\xi,\eta\in{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
and for almost every
x\in\Omega
we have

({\mathcal{A}}(x,\xi)-{\mathcal{A}}(x,\eta))\cdot(\xi-\eta)\geq 0.

Moreover, we assume

(P)

\Phi:{\mathbb{R}}\to{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
is bounded and continuous;

(b)

b:\Omega\times{\mathbb{R}}\to{\mathbb{R}}
is a Carathéodory’s function, which is nondecreasing with respect to the second variable, and such that
b(\cdot,s)\in L^{1}(\Omega)
and
b(\cdot,s)\sign(s)\geq 0
for every
s\in{\mathbb{R}}
.

We point out that we do not control the growth of
M
with respect to the second variable by any kind of doubling condition or a power function. Instead, we rely on the density of smooth functions in a relevant function space to study problem (
1.1
), namely

V_{0}^{1}L_{M}(\Omega):=\{\phi\in W_{0}^{1,1}(\Omega):\ \ \nabla\phi\in L_{M}(\Omega)\}\,,

where
L_{M}(\Omega)
is the inhomogeneous and fully anisotropic Musielak–Orlicz spaces defined in Section
2
. The space
E_{M}
is a closure in
L_{M}
-norm of the set of bounded functions. If
M
does not satisfy growth condition of doubling type,
E_{M}
is a proper subspace of
L_{M}
. There is a clear condition proven to be sufficient in
[
4
]
, but since our reasoning does not involve the condition in any other way, we assume density and recall the condition below the statement of our main theorem.

Our main theorem is an existence and uniqueness result for problem (
1.1
).

Theorem 1.1
.

Let
\Omega
be a bounded Lipschitz domain in
{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
. Suppose that an
N
-function
M
is regular enough so that the set of smooth functions is dense in
V^{1}_{0}L_{M}(\Omega)
in the modular topology. Assume further that
F\in E_{M^{\ast}}(\Omega)
,
{\mathcal{A}}
satisfies assumptions (A1), (A2) and (A3),
\Phi
satisfies (P), and
b
satisfies (b). Then there exists at least one weak solution to the problem (
1.1
). Namely, there exists a function
u\in V^{1}_{0}L_{M}(\Omega)
satisfying

\int_{\Omega}{\mathcal{A}}(x,\nabla u)\cdot\nabla v+\Phi(u)\cdot\nabla v+b(x,u)v\,dx=\int_{\Omega}F\cdot\nabla v\,dx

for all
v\in V^{1}_{0}L_{M}(\Omega)\cap L^{\infty}(\Omega)
.

If, additionally,
s\mapsto b(\cdot,s)
is strictly increasing and
\Phi
is Lipschitz continuous, then the weak solution is unique.

Remark 1.2
.

Our result is valid in the case of bounded data. In fact, for each
g\in L^{\infty}(\Omega)
, we know that there exists
F:\Omega\to{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
, such that
g=\DIV F
and
F\in E_{M^{\ast}}(\Omega)
. The fact one can take
F\in E_{M^{\ast}}(\Omega)
is a consequence of properties of Bogovski operator. This is explained in
[
11
, Remark 4.1.7]
with the use of
[
40
, Lemma II.2.1.1]
.

In order to ensure the density one may assume regularity of
M
. Note that the smooth functions are dense in
V^{1}_{0}L_{M}(\Omega)
in the modular topology if the following balance condition holds, see
[
4
]
.

Condition
\mathsf{(B)}
.
Given an
N
-function
M:\Omega\times{\mathbb{R}}^{d}\to{[0,\infty)}
suppose there exists a constant
C_{M}>1
such that for every ball
B\subset\Omega
with
|B|\leq 1
, every
x\in B,
and for all
\xi\in{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
such that
|\xi|>1
and
M(x,C_{M}\xi)\in[1,\tfrac{1}{|B|}]
there holds
\sup_{y\in B}M(y,\xi)\leq M(x,C_{M}\xi)
.

Note that in the isotropic and doubling regime this condition is known to be sufficient to the boundedness of the maximal operator. Moreover, when
d=1
they are equivalent
[
28
]
. Condition
\mathsf{(B)}
is essentially less restrictive than the isotropic one from
[
1
]
or the anisotropic ones used in
[
24
,
11
]
. Following
[
4
,
28
]
, we give examples of
N
-functions satisfying the above balance condition.

Example 1.1
.

The following
N
-functions satisfy the balance condition
\mathsf{(B)}
.

(1)

Variable exponent case:
M(x,\xi)=|\xi|^{p(x)}
,
where
p(x):\Omega\rightarrow[p^{-},p^{+}]
is log-Hölder continuous and
1<p^{-}\leq p(\cdot)\leq p^{+}\leq\infty
; see the proof of
[
28
, Proposition 7.1.2]
.

(2)

Double phase case:
M(x,\xi)=|\xi|^{p}+a(x)|\xi|^{q}
, with
1<p\leq q<\infty
,
0\leq a\in C^{0,\alpha}(\Omega)
,
\alpha\in(0,1]
and
\frac{p}{q}\leq 1+\frac{\alpha}{n}
; see the proof of
[
28
, Proposition 7.2.2]
.

(3)

Anisotropic variable case:
M(x,\xi)=\sum_{i=1}^{n}|\xi_{i}|^{p_{i}(x)}
,
where
p_{i}(x):\Omega\rightarrow[p_{i}^{-},p_{i}^{+}]
are log-Hölder continuous and
1<p^{-}_{i}\leq p_{i}(\cdot)\leq p_{i}^{+}\leq\infty
; see
[
4
, Subsection 4.4]
.

(4)

Anisotropic double phase case:
M(x,\xi)=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\left(|\xi_{i}|^{p_{i}}+a_{i}(x)|\xi_{i}|^{q_{i}}\right)
, where
1<p_{i}\leq q_{i}<\infty
,
0\leq a_{i}\in C^{0,\alpha_{i}}(\Omega)
,
\alpha_{i}\in(0,1]
, and
\frac{p_{i}}{q_{i}}\leq 1+\frac{\alpha_{i}}{n}
; for this example, as well as anisotropic multi-phase case (also with Orlicz phases), see
[
4
, Subsection 4.4]
.

Taking into account
[
10
]
and
[
4
, Section 4]
one can provide an explicit condition that implies
\mathsf{(B)}
even in the case when the anisotropic function
M(x,\xi)
does
not
admit a so-called orthotropic decomposition
\sum_{i=1}^{d}M_{i}(x,\xi_{i})
even after an affine change of variables.

Let us comment on the choice of method of the proof and its importance. We point out that there are some related results in the literature. For instance
[
23
]
is devoted to the existence of the weak solutions to a problem of the form (
1.1
) with
\Phi\equiv 0
and
b\equiv 0
. Since the equation is posed in the inhomogeneous and fully anisotropic space setting, where no conditions of doubling-type are assumed, the underlying space needs to be equipped with some approximation property. For this a compatibility condition might be imposed. The main idea of the proof of existence in
[
23
]
is to introduce a regularised problem with solutions in the classical Orlicz–Sobolev space, make use of the theory of pseudo-monotone operators due to
[
20
,
22
,
38
]
, and pass to the limit. This scheme was later applied also e.g. in
[
2
,
11
,
24
]
in the proofs of existence to problems without lower-order terms. A direct proof was a puzzling lack in this theory even in the absence of the lower-order terms.

Note that the framework of
[
20
,
38
]
is isotropic and describes the admitted behaviour of lower-order terms by the means of functions governing the operator. It is hard to provide natural counterpart of assumptions on the lower-order terms to get existence for possibly broad class of problems via this approach. Let us point out that no ‘good’ embeddings of fully anisotropic Musielak–Orlicz–Sobolev spaces into Musielak–Orlicz spaces are known. In turn, so far one is not equipped with relevant growth conditions that can be applied to the lower-order terms due to the embeddings.

Interested in the existence of solutions to as broad as possible class of problems and in a straightforward reasoning that will be relevant for the general properties of the function setting and problems with lower-order terms, we provide a direct proof of existence of weak solutions to (
1.1
). Our method is based on the Galerkin approximation. To be precise, we take a sequence of solutions to finite dimensional problems and show that it has a subsequence converging sufficiently well to our weak solution. Despite the idea is classical, the proof is not trivial because of two reasons. The first of them results from the fact that the understanding of the dual pairing is complicated (
L_{M^{*}}
is not dual to
L_{M}
, but only associate). The second reason comes from the fact that in the general Orlicz–Sobolev spaces the set of smooth functions is not dense in the norm topology. Assuming the balanced behaviour of
M
one is equipped with the best possible density in the framework of Musielak–Orlicz–Sobolev spaces, namely with the modular density of smooth functions, cf.
[
4
]
.

Despite we follow the schemes of the proofs that can be found in
[
11
,
27
,
39
]
, the compactness arguments are much more delicate. We take into account the anisotropy and inhomogeneity not considered in
[
39
]
, introduce general lower-order terms unlike
[
11
]
, and relax the doubling growth condition of
[
27
]
, where assuming
M^{*}\in\Delta_{2}
is essential. In turn, most of the limit passages needed to be justified in a different way than in the above-mentioned literature. Since no theory of operator classes or regularization of the equation is used in our proof, we do not apply directly ideas of
[
13
,
19
,
20
,
21
,
38
]
. This enables us to dismiss imposing growth conditions on the lower-order terms. Summing up, we provide a detailed direct proof of the existence result supplied with uniqueness shown by choosing a relevant sequence of test functions.

2.
Preliminaries

By
\Omega
we always mean a bounded domain of
{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
with Lipschitz regular boundary. If not specified, a constant
C
is a positive constant, possibly changing line by line. For a measurable set
A
, we denote by
\chi_{A}
its characteristic function.
By
C^{\infty}_{c}(\Omega)
we mean the set of compactly supported smooth functions over
\Omega
.

Our framework is anisotropic Musielak–Orlicz spaces based on
N
-functions. Readers are advised to turn to
[
11
]
for a thorough resource. In order to introduce the main definitions let us define a Young function in the classical way. We say a function
m:[0,\infty)\to[0,\infty)
is a Young function if it is convex, satisfies
m(s)=0\Leftrightarrow s=0
and is superlinear at zero and infinity, i.e.

\lim_{s\to 0^{+}}\frac{m(s)}{s}=0\quad\text{and}\quad\lim_{s\to\infty}\frac{m(s)}{s}=\infty.

A function
M(x,\xi):\Omega\times\mathbb{R}^{d}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
is called an
N
-function if

•

it is a convex function with respect to
{\xi}
;

•

it is a Carathéodory function satisfying
M(x,0)=0
;

•

M(x,\xi)=M(x,-\xi)
for a.e.
x\in\Omega
;

•

there exist two Young functions
m_{1},m_{2}:[0,\infty)\to[0,\infty)
such that for a.e.
x\in\Omega
it holds

\displaystyle m_{1}(|\xi|)\leq M(x,\xi)\leq m_{2}(|\xi|).

The conjugate function
M^{*}
to an
N
-function
M
is defined by

M^{\ast}(x,\eta):=\displaystyle\sup_{\xi\in\mathbb{R}^{d}}\left[\xi\cdot\eta-M(x,\xi)\right]\quad\mbox{for any}~~\eta\in\mathbb{R}^{d}~~\mbox{and}~~\mbox{a.e.}~~x\in\Omega.

In that case, the Fenchel–Young inequality reads

\displaystyle\xi\cdot\eta\leq M(x,\xi)+M^{\ast}(x,\eta)\quad\mbox{for all}~~~\xi,\eta\in\mathbb{R}^{d}~~~\mbox{and}~~\mbox{a.e.}~~x\in\Omega.

For an
N
-function we define the general Musielak–Orlicz class
\mathcal{L}_{M}(\Omega)
as the set of all measurable functions
\xi:\Omega\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^{d}
satisfying

\int_{\Omega}M(x,\xi(x))\,dx<\infty\,.

The Musielak–Orlicz space
L_{M}(\Omega)
is the smallest linear hull of
\mathcal{L}_{M}(\Omega)
equipped with the Luxemburg norm

\|\xi\|_{L_{M}(\Omega)}:=\inf\left\{\lambda>0:\int_{\Omega}M\left(x,\frac{\xi(x)}{\lambda}\right)dx\leq 1\right\}.

The space
E_{M}(\Omega)
is the closure in
L_{M}
-norm of the set of bounded functions.
Equivalently,
L_{M}(\Omega)
and
E_{M}(\Omega)
are defined as sets of functions
\xi:\Omega\to{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
satisfying

\int_{\Omega}M(x,\lambda\xi(x))\,dx<\infty

for some
\lambda\in{\mathbb{R}}
and for every
\lambda\in{\mathbb{R}}
, respectively
[
11
, Lemma 3.1.8]
. We also note that
(E_{M}(\Omega))^{\ast}=L_{M^{\ast}}(\Omega)
and
(E_{M^{\ast}}(\Omega))^{\ast}=L_{M}(\Omega)

[
11
, Theorem 3.5.3]
but no other duality relations are expected.

Next we define various notions of convergence. The first is called modular convergence and it generates a more suitable topology to our possibly non-reflexive spaces than the norm topology.
A sequence
\left\{\xi_{i}\right\}^{\infty}_{i=1}
converges modularly to
{\xi}
in
L_{M}(\Omega)
, which we denote as
{\xi_{i}}\xrightarrow{M}\xi
, if

\int_{\Omega}M\left(x,\frac{\xi_{i}-\xi}{\lambda}\right)dx\xrightarrow{i\rightarrow\infty}0

for some
\lambda>0
.

Let us point out that smooth functions are not dense in our Sobolev space in the norm topology, but they are dense in the modular topology.

Lemma 2.1

(Theorem 1 in
[
4
]
)
.

Assume that
\Omega
is a Lipschitz domain and
M
is an
N
-function satisfying the Balance condition (
B
). Then for any
\phi\in V^{1}_{0}L_{M}(\Omega)
, there exists a sequence
\left\{\phi_{\delta}\right\}_{\delta>0}\in C^{\infty}_{c}(\Omega)
satisfying
\phi_{\delta}\rightarrow{\phi}
in
L^{1}(\Omega)
and
\nabla\phi_{\delta}\xrightarrow{M}\nabla\phi
. Additionally, if
\phi
is bounded, then
\|\phi_{\delta}\|_{L^{\infty}(\Omega)}\leq C(\Omega)\|\phi\|_{L^{\infty}(\Omega)}
for every
\delta>0
.

Again, the possible non-reflexivity forces us to consider replacements for weak convergence. Let
X
and
Y
be subsets of
L^{1}(\Omega)
not necessarily related by duality. We say
f_{n}\to f
for
\sigma(X,Y)
if

\int_{\Omega}f_{n}g\,dx\xrightarrow{n\to\infty}\int_{\Omega}fg\,dx

for all
g\in Y
. If
X=L_{M}(\Omega)
and
Y=E_{M^{\ast}}(\Omega)
, we recover the weak-
\ast
convergence and can also denote
f_{n}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\ast}}{{\rightharpoonup}}f
.

Another consequence of modular topology is the following fact.

Lemma 2.2

(Lemma 3.4.6 in
[
11
]
)
.

Let
M
be an
N
-function and
\{\xi_{n}\}^{\infty}_{n=1}
,
\xi\in L_{M}(\Omega)
. If
\xi_{n}\xrightarrow{M}\xi
in
L_{M}(\Omega)
then, up to a subsequence,
\xi_{n}\xrightarrow{n\rightarrow\infty}\xi
in
\sigma(L_{M},L_{M^{\ast}})
.

Next, we shall give some definitions and preliminary lemmas related to
N
-functions and Musielak–Orlicz spaces. The first one compares a modular and a norm of a function.

Lemma 2.3

(Lemma 3.1.14 in
[
11
]
)
.

Let
M
be an
N
-function.

(1)

If
\xi\in L_{M}(\Omega)
and
\|\xi\|_{L_{M}(\Omega)}\leq 1
, then

\int_{\Omega}M(x,\xi(x))\,dx\leq\|\xi\|_{L_{M}(\Omega)}.

(2)

If
\xi\in L_{M}(\Omega)
and
\|\xi\|_{L_{M}(\Omega)}>1
, then

\int_{\Omega}M(x,\xi(x))\,dx\geq\|\xi\|_{L_{M}(\Omega)}.

Throughout the paper we take advantage of the corollary of the Vitali convergence theorem. For that we require the concept of uniform integrability.

Definition 2.4

(Uniform integrability)
.

Let
\Omega\subset{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
have finite measure. A sequence
\{f_{n}\}\subset L^{1}(\Omega)
is uniformly integrable if
for any
\varepsilon>0
there exists a
\delta>0
such that for every measurable set
E
, if
|E|<\delta
, then
\int_{E}|f_{n}|\,dx<\varepsilon
for every
n\in\mathbb{N}
.

The following consequence of in the Vitali convergence theorem results from
[
16
, Theorem 8.23]
. Since we assume that
\Omega
has a finite measure, we use pointwise convergence instead of convergence in measure.

Lemma 2.5
.

If
f_{n},f\in L^{1}(\Omega),

n=1,2,\dots
, and
|\Omega|<\infty
,
f_{n}\to f
almost everywhere in
\Omega
and
\{f_{n}\}
is uniformly integrable in
L^{1}(\Omega)
, then
f_{n}\to f
in
L^{1}(\Omega)
.

Since
N
-functions are superlinear, they allow us to detect uniform integrability.

Lemma 2.6

(Lemma 3.4.2 in
[
11
]
)
.

Suppose
M
is an
N
-function and let
\{\xi_{n}\}_{n=1}^{\infty}
be a sequence of measurable functions
\xi_{n}:\Omega\to{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
satisfying

\sup_{n\in\mathbb{N}}\int_{\Omega}M(x,\xi_{n}(x))dx<\infty.

Then the sequence
\{\xi_{n}\}_{n=1}^{\infty}
is uniformly integrable in
L^{1}(\Omega,{\mathbb{R}}^{d})
.

Lastly we give a few miscellaneous and useful results.

Lemma 2.7

(Lemma 8.22 in
[
11
]
)
.

Suppose
f_{k},f\in L^{\infty}(\Omega)
and
g_{k}\rightharpoonup g
in
L^{1}(\Omega)
. Assume further that there exists a
C>0
such that
\sup_{k\in\mathbb{N}}\|f_{k}\|_{L^{\infty}(\Omega)}<C
and
f_{k}\to f
almost everywhere in

. Then

\lim_{k\to\infty}\int_{\Omega}f_{k}g_{k}\,dx=\int_{\Omega}fg\,dx.

Lemma 2.8
.

Let
\Phi:{\mathbb{R}}\to{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
be continuous and belong to
L^{\infty}(\Omega,{\mathbb{R}}^{d})
. Let
u\in W^{1,1}_{0}(\Omega)
. Then

\int_{\Omega}\Phi(u)\cdot\nabla u\,dx=0.

Proof.

Since
\Phi
is continuous, for each of its components
\Phi_{k}
there exists a function
G_{k}(t)=\int_{0}^{t}\Phi_{k}(s)\,ds
so that
(G_{k}(u))^{\prime}=\Phi_{k}(u)\dfrac{\partial u}{\partial x_{k}}
and
G_{k}(0)=0
. Since each
G_{k}
is defined as an integral, all of them are absolutely continuous. Therefore, by denoting as
dS
the
(n-1)
-dimensional Hausdorff measure we can use integration by parts and note that
u=0
on
\partial\Omega
to get

\displaystyle\int_{\Omega}\Phi(u)\cdot\nabla u\,dx

\displaystyle=\sum_{k=1}^{d}\int_{\Omega}\Phi_{k}(u)\frac{\partial u}{\partial x_{k}}\,dx=\sum_{k=1}^{d}\int_{\Omega}\dfrac{\partial}{\partial x_{k}}(G_{k}(u))\,dx

\displaystyle=\sum_{k=1}^{d}\left\{\int_{\partial\Omega}G_{k}(u)\cdot 1\,dS-\int_{\Omega}G_{k}(u)\cdot 0\,dx\right\}=0.

∎

Lemma 2.9

(Section 9.1 in
[
17
]
)
.

Let
s:{\mathbb{R}}^{d}\to{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
be a continuous mapping and

s(x)\cdot x\geq 0\quad\mbox{if}\quad|x|=r

for some
r>0
. Then there is a point
x
with
|x|\leq r
such that
s(x)=0
.

Lemma 2.10

(Monotonicity trick, Theorem 4.1.1 in
[
11
]
)
.

Suppose
{\mathcal{A}}:\Omega\times{\mathbb{R}}^{d}\to{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
satisfies growth and coercivity conditions (A1) and (A2) for an
N
-function
M:\Omega\times{\mathbb{R}}^{d}\to[0,\infty)
. Let
h\in L_{M^{\ast}}(\Omega)
and
\xi\in L_{M}(\Omega)
satisfy

\int_{\Omega}\big(h-{\mathcal{A}}(x,\eta)\big)\cdot(\xi-\eta)\,dx\geq 0\quad\text{for all }\eta\in{\mathbb{R}}^{d}.

Then
h={\mathcal{A}}(x,\xi)
almost everywhere in
\Omega
.

3.
Galerkin solutions

In this section we discuss finite dimensional approximations of our problem (
1.1
) and their solutions, called Galerkin solutions. In the proof of Theorem
1.1
the existence of weak solutions is found as a limit of Galerkin solutions when the dimension of the approximating problem is increased. In the lemmas below we show the existence and boundedness properties of Galerkin solutions required for compactness arguments. The proofs follow similar scheme as in
[
11
, Theorem 4.1.2]
, but here we skip the structural assumption
M^{*}\in\Delta_{2}
that influence the functional analysis of the underlying space, and include the analysis of lower order terms.

Since
C^{\infty}_{c}(\Omega)
is separable and dense in
C^{1}_{c}(\Omega)
we can extract a sequence of
\{w_{i}\}_{i=1}^{\infty}\subset C^{\infty}_{c}(\Omega)
such that
\overline{\text{span}\{w_{1},w_{2},\dots\}}^{C^{1}_{c}}=C^{1}_{c}(\Omega)
. We denote the finite dimensional spaces as
V_{n}:=\text{span}\{w_{1},\dots w_{n}\}
.

Definition 3.1
.

For every
n=1,2,\dots
and spaces
V_{n}
defined above, we say a function
u_{n}\in V_{n}
is called a Galerkin solution to the problem (
1.1
) if

(3.1)

\int_{\Omega}{\mathcal{A}}(x,\nabla u_{n})\cdot\nabla\omega+\Phi(u_{n})\cdot\nabla\omega+b(x,u_{n})\omega\,dx=\int_{\Omega}F\cdot\nabla\omega\ dx.

holds for every
\omega\in V_{n}
.

Lemma 3.2

(Existence of Galerkin solutions)
.

Let
\Omega\subset{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
be a bounded Lipschitz domain. Assume
{\mathcal{A}}
satisfies (A1), (A2) and (A3) and
M:\Omega\times{\mathbb{R}}^{d}\to[0,\infty)
is an
N
-function. Let
F\in E_{M^{\ast}}(\Omega)
,
b:\Omega\times{\mathbb{R}}\to{\mathbb{R}}
satisfies condition (b) and
\Phi:{\mathbb{R}}\to{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
satisfy (P). Then for every
n\in\mathbb{N}
, there exists a Galerkin solution in the sense of (
3.1
).

Proof.

Every
u_{n}\in V_{n}
can be written in a form
u_{n}=\sum_{k=1}^{n}\alpha_{k}w_{k}
for
n\in\mathbb{N}
, where
\alpha_{k}\in{\mathbb{R}}
. We show that there exist such
\{\alpha_{k}\}_{k=1}^{n}
such that

\int_{\Omega}{\mathcal{A}}(x,\nabla u_{n})\cdot\nabla w_{k}+\Phi(u_{n})\cdot\nabla w_{k}+b(x,u_{n})w_{k}\,dx=\int_{\Omega}F\cdot\nabla w_{k}\,dx.

We define
\vec{\alpha}=(\alpha_{1},\alpha_{2},\dots\alpha_{n})\in{\mathbb{R}}^{n}
, a mapping
s:{\mathbb{R}}^{n}\to{\mathbb{R}}^{n}
given coordinate by the formula

s_{j}(\vec{\alpha}):=\int_{\Omega}{\mathcal{A}}\left(x,\sum_{k=1}^{n}\alpha_{k}\nabla w_{k}\right)\cdot\nabla w_{j}+\Phi\left(\sum_{k=1}^{n}\alpha_{k}w_{k}\right)\cdot\nabla w_{j}+b\left(x,\sum_{k=1}^{n}\alpha_{k}w_{k}\right)w_{j}-F\cdot\nabla w_{j}\,dx

and

\omega(\vec{\alpha}):=\sum_{k=1}^{n}\alpha_{k}w_{k}.

In order to prove that
s
takes the value zero, first we show that
s
is continuous and for that we choose a sequence
\{\vec{a}^{i}\}_{i=1}^{\infty}
such that
\vec{a}^{i}\to\vec{\alpha}
in
{\mathbb{R}}^{n}
. Consider

\begin{split}|s_{j}(\vec{a}^{i})-s_{j}(\vec{\alpha})|=&\bigg|\int_{\Omega}h_{i,j}(x)\,dx\bigg|\end{split}

for all
j=1,\dots,n
and every
i\in\mathbb{N}
, where

h_{i,j}(x):=\big({\mathcal{A}}(x,\nabla\omega(\vec{a}^{i}))-{\mathcal{A}}(x,\nabla\omega(\vec{\alpha}))\big)\cdot\nabla w_{j}+\big(\Phi(\omega(\vec{a}^{i}))-\Phi(\omega(\vec{\alpha}))\big)\cdot\nabla w_{j}+\big(b(x,\omega(\vec{a}^{i}))-b(x,\omega(\vec{\alpha}))\big)w_{j}.

Since
{\mathcal{A}}
and
b
are Carathéodory functions and
\Phi
is continuous, we see that for almost every
x\in\Omega
we have
h_{i,j}(x)\to 0
as
i\to\infty
. We show uniform integrability of the three terms in
h_{i,j}
one by one starting with
{\mathcal{A}}
. Assumption (A2) shows that

c_{2}M^{\ast}(x,c_{3}{\mathcal{A}}(x,\nabla\omega(\vec{a}^{i})))\leq M(x,c_{4}\nabla\omega(\vec{a}^{i}))+h_{2}(x)

and for
\vec{a}^{i}=(a^{i}_{1},\dots,a^{i}_{n})

\displaystyle\int_{\Omega}M(x,c_{4}\nabla\omega(\vec{a}^{i}))\,dx

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{k=1}^{n}\frac{|a^{i}_{k}|}{|\vec{a}^{i}|}\int_{\Omega}M(x,c_{4}|\vec{a}^{i}|\nabla w_{k})\,dx\leq n\max_{k\in\{1,\dots,n\}}\int_{\Omega}M(x,c_{4}|\vec{a}^{i}|\nabla w_{k})\,dx

and this is finite since
\{\vec{a}^{i}\}_{i=1}^{\infty}
is bounded
{\mathbb{R}}^{n}
and
w_{k}\in C_{c}^{\infty}(\Omega)
, see
[
11
, Lemma 3.1.8]
. Now Lemma
2.6
shows the uniform integrability of
\{{\mathcal{A}}(\cdot,\nabla\omega(\vec{a}^{i}))\}_{i=1}^{\infty}
in
L^{1}(\Omega,{\mathbb{R}}^{d})
. As
{\mathcal{A}}(\cdot,\nabla\omega(\vec{\alpha}))\in L^{1}(\Omega,{\mathbb{R}}^{d})
and
\nabla w_{k}\in L^{\infty}(\Omega,{\mathbb{R}}^{d})
and it follows that
\big({\mathcal{A}}(x,\nabla\omega(\vec{a}^{i}))-{\mathcal{A}}(x,\nabla\omega(\vec{\alpha}))\big)\cdot\nabla w_{j}
is uniformly integrable in
L^{1}(\Omega)
.

Moreover, we notice that
|\Phi(\cdot)|\in L^{\infty}({\mathbb{R}})
and
|\nabla w_{k}|\in L^{\infty}(\Omega)
. Thus for any set
E\subset\Omega

\left|\int_{E}\Phi(\omega(\vec{a}^{i}))\cdot\nabla w_{k}\,dx\right|\leq\max_{k\in\{1,\dots,n\}}\int_{E}\||\Phi(\omega(\vec{a}^{i}))|\|_{L^{\infty}({\mathbb{R}})}\||\nabla w_{k}|\|_{L^{\infty}(\Omega)}\,dx<C|E|.

Furthermore by the fact that
s\mapsto b(\cdot,s)
is non-decreasing, convergence of
\vec{a}^{i}
and boundedness of
\{w_{k}\}
we get

\displaystyle\left|\int_{E}\big(b(x,\omega(\vec{a}^{i}))\big)w_{k}\,dx\right|\leq n\max_{k\in\{1,\dots,n\}}\left\{\|w_{k}\|_{L^{\infty}(\Omega)}\int_{E}|b(x,|\vec{a}^{i}|\,w_{k})|\,dx\right\}<C|E|.

Thus all the terms in
h_{i,j}
are uniformly integrable in
L^{1}(\Omega)
and
h_{i,j}\to 0
almost everywhere, so it follows from Vitali convergence theorem (Lemma
2.5
) that

|s(\vec{a}^{i})-s(\vec{\alpha})|\leq n\max_{j\in\{1,\dots,n\}}\int_{\Omega}|h_{i,j}|\,dx\to 0

as
i\to\infty
. Thus
s
is continuous.

Our next aim is to show that there exists a vector
\vec{\alpha}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{n}
such that
s(\vec{\alpha})=0
. For this we will apply Lemma
2.9
. Therefore we need to prove that
s(\vec{\alpha})\cdot\vec{\alpha}\geq 0
if
|\vec{\alpha}|=R
for some
R>0
. From (A2), Young’s inequality, the facts that
\Phi(\omega(\vec{\alpha}))\cdot\nabla\omega(\vec{\alpha})=0
and
b
satisfies (b), and Lemma
2.3
(2) we get

\displaystyle s(\vec{\alpha})\cdot\vec{\alpha}

\displaystyle=\int_{\Omega}{\mathcal{A}}(x,\nabla\omega(\vec{\alpha}))\cdot\nabla\omega(\vec{\alpha})+b(x,\omega(\vec{\alpha}))\omega(\vec{\alpha})-F\cdot\nabla\omega(\vec{\alpha})\,dx

\displaystyle\geq\frac{1}{2}\int_{\Omega}M(x,c_{1}^{\mathcal{A}}\nabla\omega(\vec{\alpha}))\,dx-h_{1}(x)\,dx-\int_{\Omega}M^{\ast}\left(x,\frac{2}{c_{1}}F\right)\,dx

\displaystyle\geq\frac{1}{2}(c_{1}^{\mathcal{A}}\|\nabla\omega(\vec{\alpha})\|_{L_{M}(\Omega)}-1)-C.

Note that
\vec{\alpha}\mapsto\|\nabla\omega(\vec{\alpha})\|_{L_{M}(\Omega)}
is a continuous function and therefore it attains its minimum on the unit sphere
S_{1}\subset{\mathbb{R}}^{n}
at some
\vec{\beta}=(\beta_{1},\dots,\beta_{n})\in S_{1}
.

We show that
\|\nabla\omega(\vec{\beta})\|_{L_{M}(\Omega)}>0
. Assume the contrary that
\|\nabla\omega(\vec{\beta})\|_{L_{M}(\Omega)}=0
. Thus
\|\sum_{k=1}^{n}\beta_{k}\nabla w_{k}\|_{L^{1}(\Omega)}=0
and by Poincaré inequality we also have
\|\sum_{k=1}^{n}\beta_{k}w_{k}\|_{L^{1}(\Omega)}=0
(recall that
w_{k}\in C_{c}^{\infty}(\Omega)
). This implies that
\sum_{k=1}^{n}\beta_{k}w_{k}=0
almost everywhere in
\Omega
, i.e.
\beta_{k}=0
for each
k=1,\dots,n
since functions
w_{k}
are linearly independent. Since
\vec{\beta}\in S_{1}
this is contradiction so
\|\nabla\omega(\vec{\beta})\|_{L_{M}(\Omega)}>0
.

For our choice of
\vec{\beta}
we have

\|\nabla\omega(\vec{\alpha})\|_{L_{M}(\Omega)}\geq|\alpha|\left\|\nabla\omega\left(\frac{\vec{\alpha}}{|\vec{\alpha}|}\right)\right\|_{L_{M}(\Omega)}\geq|\vec{\alpha}|\|\nabla\omega(\vec{\beta})\|_{L_{M}(\Omega)}>0.

This means that
\|\nabla\omega(\vec{\alpha})\|_{L_{M}(\Omega)}\to\infty
when
|\vec{\alpha}|\to\infty
. Thus
s(\vec{\alpha})\cdot\vec{\alpha}\geq 0
if
|\vec{\alpha}|=R
for some
R>0
. From the definition of
s
we see that for
u_{n}=\sum_{k=1}^{n}\alpha_{k}w_{k}
we have

\int_{\Omega}{\mathcal{A}}(x,\nabla u_{n})\cdot\nabla\omega+\Phi(u_{n})\cdot\nabla\omega+b(x,u_{n})\omega\,dx=\int_{\Omega}F\cdot\nabla\omega\,dx

and thus the Galerkin solution
u_{n}
exists.
∎

Lemma 3.3

(Uniform boundedness of Galerkin solutions)
.

Let
\Omega\subset{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
be a bounded Lipschitz domain. Assume
{\mathcal{A}}
satisfies (A1), (A2), (A3) and
M:\Omega\times{\mathbb{R}}^{d}\to[0,\infty)
is an
N
-function. Let
F\in E_{M^{\ast}}(\Omega)
,
b:\Omega\times{\mathbb{R}}\to{\mathbb{R}}
satisfy condition (b) and
\Phi:{\mathbb{R}}\to{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
satisfy (P). Then there exists a constant
C
independent of
n
such that for every Galerkin solution
u_{n}
it holds

\int_{\Omega}{\mathcal{A}}(x,\nabla u_{n})\cdot\nabla u_{n}\,dx\leq C;\quad\|\nabla u_{n}\|_{L_{M}(\Omega)}\leq C;\quad\int_{\Omega}b(x,u_{n})u_{n}\,dx\leq C.

Proof.

We test the equation (
3.1
) by
u_{n}
, which is an admissible test function due to Lemma
3.2
, to get

\int_{\Omega}{\mathcal{A}}(x,\nabla u_{n})\cdot\nabla u_{n}+\Phi(u_{n})\cdot\nabla u_{n}+b(x,u_{n})u_{n}\,dx=\int_{\Omega}F\cdot\nabla u_{n}\,dx.

Using condition (A2) we get an estimate

\frac{1}{2}\int_{\Omega}M(x,c_{1}^{\mathcal{A}}\nabla u_{n})-h_{1}(x)\,dx\leq\frac{1}{2}\int_{\Omega}A(x,\nabla u_{n})\cdot\nabla u_{n}\,dx.

Moreover, Fenchel–Young inequality and the definition of
N
functions allow us to infer that

\displaystyle\int_{\Omega}\frac{4}{c_{1}^{\mathcal{A}}}F\cdot\frac{c_{1}^{\mathcal{A}}}{4}\nabla u_{n}dx

\displaystyle\leq\int_{\Omega}M\left(x,\frac{c_{1}^{\mathcal{A}}}{4}\nabla u_{n}\right)\,dx+\int_{\Omega}M^{\ast}\left(x,\frac{4}{c_{1}^{\mathcal{A}}}F\right)\,dx

\displaystyle\leq\frac{1}{4}\int_{\Omega}M(x,c_{1}^{\mathcal{A}}\nabla u_{n})dx+\int_{\Omega}M^{\ast}\left(x,\frac{4}{c_{1}^{\mathcal{A}}}F\right)\,dx.

And thus, according to
M(x,\xi)\geq 0
for almost every
x\in\Omega
and every
\xi\in{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
,
b(x,u_{n})u_{n}\geq 0
as
b
satisfies condition (b), and
\Phi(u_{n})\cdot\nabla u_{n}=0
by Lemma
2.8
we have

\displaystyle\frac{1}{4}\int_{\Omega}M(x,c_{1}^{\mathcal{A}}\nabla u_{n})dx+\frac{1}{2}\int_{\Omega}{\mathcal{A}}(x,\nabla u_{n})\cdot\nabla u_{n}dx+\int_{\Omega}\Phi(u_{n})\cdot\nabla u_{n}dx+\int_{\Omega}b(x,u_{n})u_{n}\,dx

\displaystyle\quad\leq\int_{\Omega}M^{\ast}\left(x,\frac{4}{c_{1}^{\mathcal{A}}}F\right)+\frac{1}{2}\|h_{1}(x)\|_{L^{1}(\Omega)}.

Since
F\in E_{M^{\ast}}(\Omega)
by assumption, the right-hand side of the latter inequality is finite and we infer that

\int_{\Omega}{\mathcal{A}}(x,\nabla u_{n})\cdot\nabla u_{n}\,dx\leq C

and

\int_{\Omega}b(x,u_{n})u_{n}\,dx\leq C.

Furthermore, by Lemma
2.3

\|\nabla u_{n}\|_{L_{M}(\Omega)}\leq\frac{1}{c_{1}^{\mathcal{A}}}\left(\int_{\Omega}M(x,c_{1}^{\mathcal{A}}\nabla u_{n})\,dx+1\right)\leq C,

where
C
is independent of
n
.
∎

The proof of the next fact follows almost the same lines as
[
11
, Lemma 3.8.2]
, but we correct some flaws and include it for readers’ convenience.

Lemma 3.4
.

Suppose
M
is an
N
-function and
{\mathcal{A}}:\Omega\times{\mathbb{R}}^{d}\to{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
satisfies (A1), (A2), (A3) and suppose
\|{\mathcal{A}}(\cdot,\xi)\cdot\xi\|_{L^{1}(\Omega)}\leq\tilde{c}
. Then there exists a constant
C>0
depending only on the parameters from (A1), (A2) and
\tilde{c}
such that

\|{\mathcal{A}}(\cdot,\xi)\|_{L_{M^{\ast}}(\Omega)}<C
.

Proof.

Let
w\in E_{M}(\Omega)
. Since
{\mathcal{A}}
is a Carathéodory’s function, we see that
{\mathcal{A}}(x,w)
is bounded and thus belongs to
E_{M^{\ast}}(\Omega)
. Now by a direct calculation and (A3)

\displaystyle\int_{\Omega}{\mathcal{A}}(x,\xi)\cdot w\,dx

\displaystyle=-\int_{\Omega}({\mathcal{A}}(x,\xi)-{\mathcal{A}}(x,w))\cdot(\xi-w)\,dx+\int_{\Omega}{\mathcal{A}}(x,\xi)\cdot\xi\,dx

\displaystyle\quad-\int_{\Omega}{\mathcal{A}}(x,w)\cdot(\xi-w)\,dx

\displaystyle\leq\int_{\Omega}{\mathcal{A}}(x,\xi)\cdot\xi\,dx-\int_{\Omega}{\mathcal{A}}(x,w)\cdot(\xi-w)\,dx.

The first term on the right-hand side is bounded by assumption.
For the second term we use Fenchel–Young inequality, (A2) and convexity to estimate

\displaystyle-\int_{\Omega}{\mathcal{A}}(x,w)\cdot(\xi-w)\,dx

\displaystyle=-\frac{2}{c_{1}^{\mathcal{A}}c_{3}^{\mathcal{A}}}\int_{\Omega}c_{3}^{\mathcal{A}}{\mathcal{A}}(x,w)\cdot\left(\frac{c_{1}^{\mathcal{A}}(\xi-w)}{2}\right)\,dx

\displaystyle\leq\frac{2}{c_{1}^{\mathcal{A}}c_{3}^{\mathcal{A}}}\int_{\Omega}{M^{\ast}}(x,c_{3}^{\mathcal{A}}{\mathcal{A}}(x,w))+M\left(x,\frac{c_{1}^{\mathcal{A}}(\xi-w)}{2}\right)\,dx

\displaystyle\leq\frac{2}{c_{1}^{\mathcal{A}}c_{3}^{\mathcal{A}}}\int_{\Omega}\frac{1}{c_{2}^{\mathcal{A}}}M(x,c_{4}^{\mathcal{A}}w)+\frac{1}{c_{2}^{\mathcal{A}}}h_{2}(x)+M(x,c_{1}^{\mathcal{A}}\xi)+M(x,c_{1}^{\mathcal{A}}w)\,dx

\displaystyle\leq\frac{2}{c_{1}^{\mathcal{A}}c_{3}^{\mathcal{A}}}\left[\left(\frac{1}{c_{2}^{\mathcal{A}}}+1\right)\int_{\Omega}M(x,\max\{{c_{1}^{\mathcal{A}},c_{4}^{\mathcal{A}}}\}w)\,dx\right.

\displaystyle\left.\qquad\qquad\ +\int_{\Omega}{\mathcal{A}}(x,\xi)\cdot\xi+h_{1}(x)+\frac{1}{c_{2}^{\mathcal{A}}}h_{2}(x)\,dx\right].

In turn,

\displaystyle\int_{\Omega}{\mathcal{A}}(x,\xi)\cdot w\,dx\leq\frac{2}{c_{1}^{\mathcal{A}}c_{3}^{\mathcal{A}}}

\displaystyle\left[\left(\frac{1}{c_{2}^{\mathcal{A}}}+1\right)\int_{\Omega}M(x,\max\{{c_{1}^{\mathcal{A}},c_{4}^{\mathcal{A}}}\}w)\,dx\right.

(3.2)

\displaystyle\left.\ +\left(\frac{c_{1}^{\mathcal{A}}c_{3}^{\mathcal{A}}}{2}+1\right)\int_{\Omega}{\mathcal{A}}(x,\xi)\cdot\xi+h_{1}(x)+\frac{1}{c_{2}^{\mathcal{A}}}h_{2}(x)\,dx\right]\,.

Provided
\max\{{c_{1}^{\mathcal{A}},c_{4}^{\mathcal{A}}}\}\|w\|_{L_{M}(\Omega)}\leq 1
, by Lemma
2.3
(1), we know that

\displaystyle\int_{\Omega}M(x,\max\{{c_{1}^{\mathcal{A}},c_{4}^{\mathcal{A}}}\}w)\,dx\leq\max\{{c_{1}^{\mathcal{A}},c_{4}^{\mathcal{A}}}\}\|w\|_{L_{M}(\Omega)}\leq 1.

Using it in (
3.2
) we obtain

\displaystyle\|{\mathcal{A}}(x,\xi)\|_{(E_{M}(\Omega))^{\ast}}

\displaystyle=\max\{c_{1}^{\mathcal{A}},c_{4}^{\mathcal{A}}\}\sup\left\{\int_{\Omega}{\mathcal{A}}(x,\xi)\cdot w\,dx:\quad\max\{c_{1}^{\mathcal{A}},c_{4}^{\mathcal{A}}\}\|w\|_{L_{M}(\Omega)}\leq 1\right\}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{2\max\{c_{1}^{\mathcal{A}},c_{4}^{\mathcal{A}}\}}{c_{1}^{\mathcal{A}}c_{3}^{\mathcal{A}}}\left[\left(\frac{1}{c_{2}^{\mathcal{A}}}+1\right)+\left(\frac{c_{1}^{\mathcal{A}}c_{3}^{\mathcal{A}}}{2}+1\right)\int_{\Omega}{\mathcal{A}}(x,\xi)\cdot\xi+h_{1}(x)+\frac{1}{c_{2}^{\mathcal{A}}}h_{2}(x)\,dx\right]

\displaystyle\leq C.

Thus
{\mathcal{A}}(x,\xi)
stays bounded in
L_{M^{\ast}}(\Omega)
.
∎

4.
Existence and uniqueness for problem (
1.1
)

We are ready to prove our main Theorem.

Proof of Theorem
1.1
.

We base our proof on Galerkin approximations. We fix spaces
V_{n}
and functions
u_{n}
as in Definition
3.1
.
We shall prove that there exists a weak solution of the problem (
1.1
) being a limit of a subsequence of the Galerkin solutions
\{u_{n}\}
. We divide our proof into four steps.

Step 1
According to the Lemma
3.2
, we get

(4.1)

\int_{\Omega}{\mathcal{A}}(x,\nabla u_{n})\cdot\nabla\omega+\Phi(u_{n})\cdot\nabla\omega+b(x,u_{n})\omega\,dx=\int_{\Omega}F\cdot\nabla\omega\,dx\quad\text{
for all $\omega\in V_{k}$, $k\leq n$.}

By Lemma
3.3
we have that
\|\nabla u_{n}\|_{L_{M}(\Omega)}\leq C
and
\|{\mathcal{A}}(\cdot,\nabla u_{n})\cdot\nabla u_{n}\|_{L^{1}(\Omega)}\leq C
, where
C
is independent of
n
.
By the Banach–Alaoglu Theorem, we know that
\{\nabla u_{n}\}_{n}
is weakly-
\ast
compact in
L_{M}(\Omega)
. By Lemma
2.6
and the fact that
M
is an
N
-function, we see that
\{\nabla u_{n}\}_{n}
is uniformly integrable in
L^{1}(\Omega,{\mathbb{R}}^{d})
. The Dunford–Pettis Theorem
[
5
, Theorem 4.3]
implies that
\left\{\nabla u_{n}\right\}_{n}
is relatively compact in the weak topology of
L^{1}(\Omega,{\mathbb{R}}^{d})
. Taking into accont the Rellich-Kondrashev theorem and Poincaré inequality there exists a function
u\in W^{1,1}_{0}(\Omega)
such that

u_{n}\rightharpoonup u\quad
in
\quad W^{1,1}(\Omega)
.

Since the weak-
\ast
limit is unique, we see that additionally
\nabla u\in V^{1}_{0}L_{M}(\Omega)
and

\nabla u_{n}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle*}}{{\rightharpoonup}}\nabla u\quad
for
\quad\sigma(L_{M},E_{M^{\ast}})
.

Also, by Lemma
3.4
, we see that
{\mathcal{A}}(\cdot,\nabla u_{n})
is bounded in
L_{M^{\ast}}(\Omega)
and a similar argument shows the existence of
h\in L_{M^{\ast}}(\Omega)
such that

(4.2)

{\mathcal{A}}(x,\nabla u_{n})\stackrel{{\scriptstyle*}}{{\rightharpoonup}}h\quad\text{ for }\quad\sigma(L_{M^{\ast}},E_{M}).

As
b
is a Carathéodory’s function, we get
b(x,u_{n})\to b(x,u)
almost everywhere in
\Omega
. Fix
\varepsilon>0
and choose
\ell
large enough to guarantee
\frac{C}{\ell}<\frac{\varepsilon}{2}
for
C
from Lemma
3.3
. Since
b(\cdot,\ell)\in L^{1}(\Omega)
is a given function independent of
n
, for any subset
E\subset\Omega
with
|E|
small enough, we have
\|b(\cdot,\ell)\|_{L^{1}(E)}<\frac{\varepsilon}{2}
. Now

\displaystyle\int_{E}b(x,u_{n})\,dx

\displaystyle=\int_{E\cap\{|u_{n}|<\ell\}}b(x,u_{n})\,dx+\int_{E\cap\{|u_{n}|\geq\ell\}}b(x,u_{n})\,dx

\displaystyle\leq\int_{E\cap\{|u_{n}|<\ell\}}|b(x,\ell)|\,dx+\int_{E\cap\{|u_{n}|\geq\ell\}}\frac{1}{|u_{n}|}|b(x,u_{n})u_{n}|\,dx

\displaystyle\leq\int_{E}|b(x,\ell)|\,dx+\int_{\{|u_{n}|\geq\ell\}}\frac{1}{\ell}|b(x,u_{n})u_{n}|\,dx

\displaystyle\leq\int_{E}|b(x,\ell)|\,dx+\frac{C}{\ell}<\varepsilon.

Therefore
\{b(\cdot,u_{n})\}
is uniformly integrable.
By Vitali convergence theorem (Lemma
2.5
) we have

(4.3)

b(\cdot,u_{n})\to b(\cdot,u)\text{ in }L^{1}(\Omega).

As
\Phi
is continuous and belongs to
L^{\infty}(\Omega,{\mathbb{R}}^{d})
, we immediately see that

(4.4)

\Phi(u_{n})\to\Phi(u)
weakly in
L^{1}(\Omega,{\mathbb{R}}^{d})

due to dominated convergence theorem.

Having (
4.2
), (
4.4
), and (
4.3
), we can pass to the limit in (
4.1
) with
n
. In turn we get

(4.5)

\langle F,\nabla\omega\rangle=\int_{\Omega}h\cdot\nabla\omega+\Phi(u)\cdot\nabla\omega+b(x,u)\omega\,dx\quad\text{for any $\omega\in V_{k}$, $k\in\mathbb{N}$}.

Step 2
(extending the class of test functions): Let
\varphi\in C_{c}^{\infty}(\Omega)
be arbitrary and
\varphi_{j}\in V_{j}
be a sequence of smooth function such that
\varphi_{j}\to\varphi
in
C^{1}_{c}(\Omega)
. Due to (
4.5
) we have

(4.6)

\displaystyle\begin{split}\langle F,\nabla\varphi_{j}\rangle&=\int_{\Omega}h\cdot\nabla\varphi_{j}+\Phi(u)\cdot\nabla\varphi_{j}+b(x,u)\varphi_{j}\,dx\quad\text{for any $j$.}\end{split}

Now
h\cdot\nabla\varphi_{j}\to h\cdot\nabla\varphi
almost everywhere and by uniform convergence of
\{\nabla\varphi_{j}\}
we have for large enough
j

\int_{\Omega}h\cdot\nabla\varphi_{j}\ dx\leq\int_{\Omega}h\cdot\nabla\varphi+1\,dx<\infty.

Since (
4.6
),
\Phi(u)
is bounded,
b(\cdot,u)\in L^{1}(\Omega)
, by dominated convergence theorem applied similarly also to all the other terms in we have

(4.7)

\displaystyle\begin{split}\langle F,\nabla\varphi\rangle=\lim_{j\to\infty}\langle F,\nabla\varphi_{j}\rangle&=\lim_{j\to\infty}\int_{\Omega}h\cdot\nabla\varphi_{j}+\Phi(u)\cdot\nabla\varphi_{j}+b(x,u)\varphi_{j}\,dx\\
&=\int_{\Omega}h\cdot\nabla\varphi+\Phi(u)\cdot\nabla\varphi+b(x,u)\varphi\,dx.\end{split}

Let
v\in V^{1}_{0}L_{M}(\Omega)\cap L^{\infty}(\Omega)
and
\{v_{k}\}\subset C_{c}^{\infty}(\Omega)
be a sequence such that
\nabla v_{k}\to\nabla v
modularly in
L_{M}(\Omega)
,
v_{k}\to v
in
L^{1}(\Omega)
and
\|v_{k}\|_{L^{\infty}(\Omega)}\leq c\|v\|_{L^{\infty}(\Omega)}
with
c
independent of
k
. This is possible because of the modular density assumption (Lemma
2.1
).

The modular convergence of
\{\nabla v_{k}\}
implies that
\nabla v_{k}\to\nabla v
for
\sigma(L_{M},L_{M^{\ast}})
by Lemma
2.2
. Note that

(4.8)

F\in E_{M^{\ast}}(\Omega)\subset L_{M^{\ast}}(\Omega)
,
\quad h\in L_{M^{\ast}}(\Omega)
,
\quad\Phi(u)\in L^{\infty}(\Omega,{\mathbb{R}}^{d})\subset E_{M^{\ast}}(\Omega)

and since
b(\cdot,u)\in L^{1}(\Omega)
, Lemma
2.7
implies that

(4.9)

\lim_{k\to\infty}\int_{\Omega}b(x,u)v_{k}\,dx\to\int_{\Omega}b(x,u)v\,dx.

Using (
4.8
) and (
4.9
) we can extend (
4.7
) as

(4.10)

\displaystyle\begin{split}\langle F,\nabla v\rangle=\lim_{k\to\infty}\langle F,\nabla v_{k}\rangle&=\lim_{k\to\infty}\int_{\Omega}h\cdot\nabla v_{k}+\Phi(u)\cdot\nabla v_{k}+b(x,u)v_{k}\,dx\\
&=\int_{\Omega}h\cdot\nabla v+\Phi(u)\cdot\nabla v+b(x,u)v\,dx\end{split}

for test functions
v\in V^{1}_{0}L_{M}(\Omega)\cap L^{\infty}(\Omega)
.

Step 3
(
h={\mathcal{A}}(x,\nabla u)
almost everywhere): Let
w\in L^{\infty}(\Omega,{\mathbb{R}}^{d})
be arbitrary. By (A3) we have

\displaystyle 0

\displaystyle\leq\limsup_{n\to\infty}\int_{\Omega}({\mathcal{A}}(x,\nabla u_{n})-{\mathcal{A}}(x,w))\cdot(\nabla u_{n}-w)\,dx

\displaystyle=\limsup_{n\to\infty}\bigg(\int_{\Omega}{\mathcal{A}}(x,\nabla u_{n})\cdot\nabla u_{n}\,dx-\int_{\Omega}{\mathcal{A}}(x,\nabla u_{n})\cdot w\,dx

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad-\int_{\Omega}{\mathcal{A}}(x,w)\cdot\nabla u_{n}\,dx+\int_{\Omega}A(x,w)\cdot w\,dx\bigg)

\displaystyle\leq I_{1}+I_{2}+I_{3}+I_{4}.

Let us handle the integrals one by one. Before we estimate the
I_{1}
, we would like to point out that, as
u_{n}\to u
a.e. in
\Omega
,
b:\Omega\times{\mathbb{R}}\to{\mathbb{R}}
is a Carathéodory’s function
satisfying the sign condition, using the Fatou lemma, we have

\int_{\Omega}b(x,u)u\,dx\leq\liminf_{n\to\infty}\int_{\Omega}b(x,u_{n})u_{n}\,dx\leq C.

In the case of
I_{1}
, as
u_{n}

is a Galerkin solution,
F\in E_{M^{\ast}}(\Omega)
, taking the test function as
u_{n}
in (
3.1
) and combining with Lemma
2.8
, we deduced that

\displaystyle\begin{split}I_{1}&=\limsup_{n\to\infty}\int_{\Omega}{\mathcal{A}}(x,\nabla u_{n})\cdot\nabla u_{n}\,dx\\
&=\limsup_{n\to\infty}\int_{\Omega}\bigg(F\cdot\nabla u_{n}-b(x,u_{n})u_{n}-\Phi(u_{n})\cdot\nabla u_{n}\bigg)\,dx\\
&\leq\limsup_{n\to\infty}\int_{\Omega}F\cdot\nabla u_{n}\,dx-\liminf_{n\to\infty}\int_{\Omega}b(x,u_{n})u_{n}\,dx\\
&=\int_{\Omega}F\cdot\nabla u\,dx-\liminf_{n\to\infty}\int_{\Omega}b(x,u_{n})u_{n}\,dx.\end{split}

According to the fact that
\nabla T_{k}(u)\to\nabla u
modularly in
L_{M}(\Omega)
, we obtain

\displaystyle\begin{split}&\int_{\Omega}F\cdot\nabla u\,dx-\liminf_{n\to\infty}\int_{\Omega}b(x,u_{n})u_{n}\,dx=\lim_{k\to\infty}\int_{\Omega}F\cdot\nabla T_{k}(u)\,dx-\liminf_{n\to\infty}\int_{\Omega}b(x,u_{n})u_{n}\,dx.\end{split}

In addition, in the step
2
, we have used the approximation theory (lemma
2.1
) to extend the test function of (
4.10
) to hold for any
v\in V^{1}_{0}L_{M}(\Omega)\cap L^{\infty}(\Omega)
, so taking the test function as
T_{k}(u)
in (
4.10
) and combining with Lemma
2.8
, we obtain

\displaystyle\begin{split}&\lim_{k\to\infty}\int_{\Omega}F\cdot\nabla T_{k}(u)\,dx-\liminf_{n\to\infty}\int_{\Omega}b(x,u_{n})u_{n}\,dx\\
&\quad=\lim_{k\to\infty}\int_{\Omega}h\cdot\nabla T_{k}(u)+b(x,u)T_{k}(u)+\Phi(u)\nabla T_{k}(u)\,dx-\liminf_{n\to\infty}\int_{\Omega}b(x,u_{n})u_{n}\,dx\\
&\quad=\int_{\Omega}h\cdot\nabla u\,dx+\int_{\Omega}b(x,u)u\,dx-\liminf_{n\to\infty}\int_{\Omega}b(x,u_{n})u_{n}\,dx\\
&\quad\leq\int_{\Omega}h\cdot\nabla u\,dx.\end{split}

Therefore, we deduce that

(4.11)

\displaystyle I_{1}=\limsup_{n\to\infty}\int_{\Omega}{\mathcal{A}}(x,\nabla u_{n})\cdot\nabla u_{n}\,dx\leq\int_{\Omega}h\cdot\nabla u\,dx.

For
I_{2}
, recalling the fact (
4.2
), it is easy to check that

(4.12)

\ I_{2}=\limsup_{n\to\infty}\int_{\Omega}-{\mathcal{A}}(x,\nabla u_{n})\cdot w\,dx=-\int_{\Omega}h\cdot w\,dx.

In addition, according to the fact that
{\mathcal{A}}(\cdot,w)\in L^{\infty}(\Omega,{\mathbb{R}}^{d})
, we have

(4.13)

I_{3}=\limsup_{n\to\infty}\int_{\Omega}-{\mathcal{A}}(x,w)\cdot\nabla u_{n}\,dx=-\int_{\Omega}{\mathcal{A}}(x,w)\cdot\nabla u\,dx.

Lastly,

I_{4}
does not depend on
n
or
k
.

All in all, combining (
4.11
)–(
4.13
) we see that

\displaystyle 0\leq\int_{\Omega}(h-{\mathcal{A}}(x,w))\cdot(\nabla u-w)\,dx.

Now the monotonicity trick (Lemma
2.10
) yields that
h={\mathcal{A}}(x,\nabla u)
almost everywhere in
\Omega
. Plugging this to (
4.10
) we see that
u_{n}
is a weak solution to equation (
1.1
).

Step 4
(uniqueness): We prove uniqueness of the weak solution under the additional assumptions that
s\mapsto b(x,s)
is strictly increasing for almost every
x\in\Omega
and
\Phi
is Lipschitz continuous. We take an approximation of a Heaviside function and pass to the limit, see
[
8
,
27
,
41
]
for similar proofs.

Assume that there exists two weak solutions
u^{1}
and
u^{2}
. Let us take an approximation of a Heaviside function
H(t)=\chi_{\{t\geq 0\}}
given by

\displaystyle H_{\delta}(t):=\begin{cases}0,&\ t<0,\\
\tfrac{1}{\delta}t,&\ 0\leq t\leq\delta,\\
1,&\ t>\delta.\end{cases}

Let
k\in\mathbb{N}
be arbitrary and test the equation (
1.1
) with
\varphi=H_{\delta}(u^{1}-u^{2})\in V^{1}_{0}L_{M}(\Omega)\cap L^{\infty}(\Omega)
to get

\int_{\Omega}\big({\mathcal{A}}(x,\nabla u^{i})+\Phi(u^{i})\big)\cdot\nabla\varphi+b(x,u^{i})\varphi\,dx=\int_{\Omega}F\cdot\nabla\varphi\,dx,\quad i=1,2\,.

Subtracting the equations we receive

\displaystyle J_{1}+J_{2}+J_{3}

\displaystyle:=\frac{1}{\delta}\int_{\{0\leq u^{1}-u^{2}\leq\delta\}}\big({\mathcal{A}}(x,\nabla u^{1})-{\mathcal{A}}(x,\nabla u^{2})\big)\cdot\nabla(u^{1}-u^{2})\,dx

\displaystyle\quad+\frac{1}{\delta}\int_{\{0\leq u^{1}-u^{2}\leq\delta\}}\big(\Phi(u^{1})-\Phi(u^{2})\big)\cdot\nabla(u^{1}-u^{2})\,dx

\displaystyle\quad+\int_{\Omega}(b(x,u^{1})-b(x,u^{2}))H_{\delta}(u^{1}-u^{2})\,dx=0.

We note that
J_{1}\geq 0
for each
\delta
due to (A3). For
J_{2}
we estimate with Lipschitz continuity of
\Phi

\displaystyle J_{2}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{1}{\delta}\int_{\{0\leq u^{1}-u^{2}\leq\delta\}}|\Phi(u^{1})-\Phi(u^{2})||\nabla(u^{1}-u^{2})|\,dx\leq\frac{1}{\delta}\int_{\{0\leq u^{1}-u^{2}\leq\delta\}}L_{\Phi}\delta|\nabla(u^{1}-u^{2})|\,dx\to 0

as
\delta\to 0
as we integrate over a shrinking domain. Lastly we note that due to Lemma
2.7
we have

\displaystyle\lim_{\delta\to 0}J_{3}=\int_{\Omega}(b(x,u^{1})-b(x,u^{2}))H(u^{1}-u^{2})\,dx.

Dropping the non-negative
J_{1}
and taking into account limits of
J_{2}
and
J_{3}
we see that

\displaystyle\int_{\Omega}(b(x,u^{1})-b(x,u^{2}))H(u^{1}-u^{2})\,dx\leq 0

so that
(b(x,u^{1})-b(x,u^{2}))H(u^{1}-u^{2})=0
almost everywhere due to sign condition of
b
.
Since
b
is strictly increasing with respect to the second variable we see that
u^{1}\leq u^{2}
. Considering
\varphi=H_{\delta}(u^{2}-u^{1})
yields the opposite inequality and thus
u^{1}=u^{2}
.
∎
