Abstract
. In the standard SIR model, infected vertices infect their neighbors at rate λ \lambda independently across each edge. They also recover at rate γ \gamma . In this work we consider the SIR- ω \omega model where the graph structure itself co-evolves with the SIR dynamics. Specifically, S − I S-I connections are broken at rate ω \omega . Then, with probability α \alpha , S S rewires this edge to another uniformly chosen vertex; and with probability 1 − α 1-\alpha , this edge is simply dropped. When α = 1 \alpha=1 the SIR- ω \omega model becomes the evoSIR model introduced in Jiang et al. 2019 , where they proved that the probability of a major outbreak in the evoSIR model evolving on an Erdős-Rényi( n n , μ / n \mu/n ) graph converges to 0 as λ \lambda approaches the critical infection rate λ c \lambda_{c} . On the other hand, numerical experiments in Jiang et al. 2019 revealed that, as λ → λ c \lambda\to\lambda_{c} , (conditionally on a major outbreak) the fraction of infected vertices may not converge to 0 0 , leading to a discontinuous phase transition . Ball and Britton 2022 considered the SIR- ω \omega model on an Erdős-Rényi( n n , μ / n \mu/n ) graph and proved that, if ω ⁡ ( 2 ​ α − 1 ) > γ ​ and ​ μ > 2 ​ ω ​ α ω ⁡ ( 2 ​ α − 1 ) − γ , \omega(2\alpha-1)>\gamma\mbox{ and }\mu>\frac{2\omega\alpha}{\omega(2\alpha-1)-\gamma}, then the phase transition of the final epidemic size is discontinuous. In this work we prove that the above condition is also necessary for this discontinuity. We achieve this by proving that the time-changed SIR- ω \omega process converges to a singular ODE system.

1.
Introduction

In the SIR model, individuals are in one of three states:
S=
susceptible,
I=
infected,
R=
removed (cannot be infected). In this work we consider the SIR model on a graph
G
that can be thought of as the social structure of the underlying community: vertices represent individuals and edges connections between them.
S-I
edges become
I-I
at rate
\lambda
, i.e., after a time with an Exponential(
\lambda
) distribution. An individual remains infected for an amount of time which has an Exponential(
\gamma
) distribution. Once individuals leave the infected state, they enter the removed state and stay removed forever.

In recent years there has been a surge of interest in epidemic processes on
dynamic
networks
Chatterjee et al. 2022
;
Hilário et al. 2022
;
Ball and Britton 2022
;
Linker and Remenik 2020
;
Durrett and Yao 2022
, i.e., the graph structure will not remain static but rather evolve with time.
Britton et al. 2016
introduced the
SIR-
\omega
model
where the graph is allowed to co-evolve with the epidemic. In addition to the dynamics in the standard SIR model, in the SIR-
\omega
model
S-I
edges are broken at rate
\omega
. With probability
1-\alpha
the susceptible individual drops this edge, and with probability
\alpha

S
re-connects to another individual chosen uniformly at random from the other
n-2
individuals in the graph. Mathematical analysis for this model has been done in
Ball and Britton 2022
;
Britton et al. 2016
;
Leung et al. 2018
. When
\alpha=1
, the SIR-
\omega
model is also called the
evoSIR model
, where ‘evo’ stands for ‘evolving’. The evoSIR model was studied in
Jiang et al. 2019
and the authors there proved that, similarly to the case of static graphs, as the infection rate
\lambda\searrow\lambda_{c}
, i.e.,
\lambda
approaches the critical value
\lambda_{c}
from above, the probability of a major outbreak converges to 0. (For
\lambda<\lambda_{c}
, the probability of a major outbreak is 0, so we do not need to consider the limit of
\lambda\nearrow\lambda_{c}
.) In other words, the transition for the probability of a major outbreak is always continuous. However, as discovered by simulations in
Jiang et al. 2019
, the evoSIR model exhibits
discontinuous phase transitions
in terms of the final epidemic size for certain values of parameters of the model. More precisely, when
\lambda
approaches
\lambda_{c}
, conditionally on a major outbreak the fraction of infected individuals
doesn’t
converge to 0. This is in sharp contrast with the case of SIR epidemics on static Erdős-Rényi graphs (and the configuration model), where the phase transition for the fraction of infecteds is always continuous. To avoid potential confusion, when we say ‘phase transition’ below we always refer to the transition for the final epidemic size conditionally on a major outbreak.

While the SIR-
\omega
/evoSIR model may not be actually used in real epidemic preventions, it is interesting in at least two aspects. First, the evoSIR model can increase the epidemic threshold (see (
1.20
)) while keeping the total number of edges in the network unchanged, which can be a bit surprising at first sight. Second, from the mathematical point view, apart from the phase transition phenomenon, as we will see below the SIR-
\omega
model provides an example of a particle system with a singular limit, which is interesting in itself.

For the simpler
SI
epidemic where individuals stay infected forever (i.e., no recoveries),
Ball and Britton 2022
and
Durrett and Yao 2022
give an (almost) necessary and sufficient condition for discontinuous phase transitions of the final epidemic size in the
SI-
\omega
model
on Erdős-Rényi graphs and the
evoSI
model on the configuration model, respectively. Note that Erdős-Rényi graphs are contiguous to the configuration model with Poisson degree distribution. In fact,
Ball and Britton 2022
found out the limit of scaled final epidemic size, which can be expressed as a solution to a certain equation (see
(
Ball and Britton 2022
, Theorem 2.5 and equation (2.8))
).

However, the situation becomes much more complicated for SIR epidemics on evolving graphs. The paper
Ball and Britton 2022
give two conditions for continuous and discontinuous phase transitions of the SIR-
\omega
model. But there is a gap between these two conditions. In addition, not much is known about the final epidemic size. See Section
1.2.3
for more details.

In this work we give a necessary and sufficient condition for the continuity of the phase transition of the SIR-
\omega
model (Theorem
1.1
), resolving a conjecture in
(
Ball and Britton 2022
, Remark 2.4)
. For the case of continuous phase transitions we also prove the convergence of (scaled) final epidemic size (Theorem
1.4
). A major step in the proof is to show that the (time-changed version of) SIR-
\omega
process converges to the ODE system (
1.8
)-(
1.11
), which contains a discontinuity point. This barrier forbids direct applications of ODE approximation techniques and causes many technicalities.

The rest of the introduction is organized as follows. We state our main results in Section
1.1
. Section
1.2
is devoted to a quick review of related work. Section
1.3
contains the proof strategies as well and the organization of this paper.

1.1.
Main results

We start from some terminologies and definitions. The term
final epidemic size
, denoted by
\Lambda^{(n)}=\Lambda^{(n)}(\alpha,\lambda,\gamma,\mu,\omega)
, when the graph has size
n
, refers to the total number of individuals that have ever been infected. For SIR epidemics it is the number of vertices that are eventually removed. We say a
major outbreak
occurs if the epidemic infects more than
\epsilon n
individuals (
n
is the size of the total population) for some
\epsilon>0
independent of
n
, i.e.,
\Lambda^{(n)}\geq\epsilon n
. The critical infection rate
\lambda_{c}
is the smallest infection rate such that a major outbreak occurs with probability bounded away from 0 as
n\to\infty
. Mathematically,

\lambda_{c}=\inf\{\lambda>0:\liminf_{n\to\infty}\mathbb{P}(\Lambda^{(n)}\geq\epsilon n)>0\mbox{ for some }\epsilon>0\}.

The phase transition for the final epidemic size is said to be continuous if for any
\epsilon>0
,

(1.1)

\lim_{\lambda\to\lambda_{c}}\mathbb{P}(\Lambda^{(n)}>n\epsilon|\mbox{a major outbreak occurs})=0.

If (
1.1
) fails for some
\epsilon_{0}>0
, then the phase transition is said to be discontinuous.

As mentioned in the beginning of this paper, the SIR-
\omega
model has four parameters:
\gamma
representing the recovery rate,
\lambda
the infection rate,
\omega
the rate of dropping or rewiring and
\alpha
the probability of rewiring.
We focus on the Erdős-Rényi(
n
,
\mu/n
) graph, which is a random graph on
n
vertices where each pair gets connected with probability
\mu/n
.

It has been proved in
Jiang et al. 2019
for the evoSIR model and
Ball and Britton 2022
for the SIR-
\omega
model that

(1.2)

\lambda_{c}=\frac{\omega+\gamma}{\mu-1}.

Turning to the phase transition of the final epidemic size, Ball and Britton proved in
(
Ball and Britton 2022
, Theorem 2.4)
that if

(1.3)

\omega(2\alpha-1)>\gamma\mbox{ and }\mu>\frac{2\omega\alpha}{\omega(2\alpha-1)-\gamma},

then there exists
r_{0}=r_{0}(\mu,\gamma,\omega,\alpha)>0
such that

(1.4)

\lim_{n\to\infty}\mathbb{P}(\Lambda^{(n)}>nr_{0}|\mbox{a major outbreak occurs})=1\mbox{ for all }\lambda>\lambda_{c}.

They also showed that the phase transition is continuous when

(1.5)

\omega(3\alpha-1)\leq\gamma\mbox{ or }\mu\leq\frac{2\omega\alpha}{\omega(3\alpha-1)-\gamma}.

Note that there is a gap between (
1.3
) and (
1.5
). They conjectured in
(
Ball and Britton 2022
, Remark 2.4)
that (
1.3
) should be both necessary and sufficient for a discontinuous phase transition. The following theorem of our work confirms this conjecture.

Theorem 1.1
.

Consider the SIR-
\omega
model starting with one infected individual and the rest being susceptible.
Suppose

(1.6)

\omega(2\alpha-1)\leq\gamma\mbox{ or }\mu\leq\frac{2\omega\alpha}{\omega(2\alpha-1)-\gamma},

then for any
\epsilon>0
, there exists
\lambda_{1}>\lambda_{c}
such that

(1.7)

\lim_{n\to\infty}\mathbb{P}(\Lambda^{(n)}<\epsilon n|\mbox{a major outbreak occurs})=1\mbox{ for all }\lambda\in(\lambda_{c},\lambda_{1}).

Combining (
1.7
) and (
1.4
), the phase transition for the final epidemic size of the SIR-
\omega
model near
\lambda_{c}
is discontinuous if and only if (
1.3
) holds.

We emphasize that, while the gap between (
1.3
) and (
1.5
) may seem to be not very large, overcoming it requires new ideas that are quite different from
Ball and Britton 2022
. To start we re-use the infrastructure built in
Ball and Britton 2022
. Suppose the underlying graph has
n
vertices, then we set

•

S^{(n)}(t)
: the number of susceptible vertices at time
t
,

•

I^{(n)}(t)
: the number of infected vertices at time
t
,

•

I^{(n)}_{E}(t)
: the number of infectious (i.e., infected–susceptible) edges at time
t
,

•

\tau^{(n)}
: the first time when
I_{E}^{(n)}
reaches 0,

•

W^{(n)}(t)
: the number of susceptible–susceptible edges at time
t
that were created by rewiring,

•

{\mathbf{X}}^{(n)}(t)=(S^{(n)}(t),I^{(n)}(t),I^{(n)}_{E}(t),W^{(n)}(t))
.

Note that
\tau^{(n)}
can be understood as the
terminal time
since no more vertices can be infected after
\tau^{(n)}
. Also, recall that we use
\Lambda^{(n)}
to denote the final epidemic size, which is equal to
n-S^{(n)}(\tau^{(n)})
. As will be clear soon,
{\mathbf{X}}^{(n)}
is more amenable to analysis after a
random time-transform
. Specifically, we multiply all transition rates of the SIR-
\omega
process (which is a Markov chain) by
n/(\lambda I_{E}^{(n)}(t))
. We use a hat to denote the quantities in the time-changed process. For instance,
\widehat{\tau}^{(n)}
is the terminal time in the time-transformed model. Note that
{\mathbf{X}}^{(n)}(0)=\widehat{{\mathbf{X}}}^{(n)}(0)
. Also,
\widehat{\Lambda}^{(n)}
is the final epidemic size in the time-changed model, which is necessarily equal to
\Lambda^{(n)}
. We emphasize that
these notations are fixed throughout the paper
.

Our goal is to give a deterministic approximation for
\widehat{\mathbf{X}}(t)
. Consider the following system of equations:

(1.8)

\displaystyle\frac{\mathrm{d}\widehat{s}}{\mathrm{d}t}

\displaystyle=-1,

(1.9)

\displaystyle\frac{\mathrm{d}\widehat{i}}{\mathrm{d}t}

\displaystyle=-\frac{\gamma}{\lambda}\frac{\widehat{i}}{\widehat{i}_{E}}+1,

(1.10)

\displaystyle\frac{\mathrm{d}\widehat{i}_{E}}{\mathrm{d}t}

\displaystyle=-1-\frac{\gamma}{\lambda}+\mu\widehat{s}-\frac{\widehat{i}_{E}}{\widehat{s}}+2\frac{\widehat{w}}{\widehat{s}}-\frac{\omega}{\lambda}(1-\alpha+\alpha(1-\widehat{i})),

(1.11)

\displaystyle\frac{\mathrm{d}\widehat{w}}{\mathrm{d}t}

\displaystyle=\frac{\omega}{\lambda}\alpha\widehat{s}-2\frac{\widehat{w}}{\widehat{s}}.

Here
\widehat{i}_{E}
and
\widehat{w}
are non-negative functions, while
\widehat{s}
are
\widehat{i}
are non-negative and upper bounded by
1
. For
\widehat{s}
and
\widehat{w}
, they are well defined up to time
t=\widehat{s}(0)
; for
\widehat{i}
and
\widehat{i}_{E}
, the appropriate domain is
t<t_{*}
(see (
1.14
) below). As will be clear later, these equations can be obtained by dividing the right-hand sides of equations (
1.21
)-(
1.24
) by
\lambda i_{E}(t)
. In fact, Ball and Britton also mentioned this system of equations in
(
Ball and Britton 2022
, Section 6.5.1)
, but they didn’t make use of this to analyze the SIR-
\omega
model. Note that (
1.9
) contains a singularity when
\widehat{i}_{E}=0
.
Ball and Britton 2022
got around the singularity issue by analyzing other auxiliary models.

Note that
(\widehat{s}(t),\widehat{w}(t))
already forms a closed sub-system, which can be explicitly solved for any initial condition with
\widehat{w}(0)=0
as follows (for all
t<\widehat{s}(0)
)

(1.12)

\displaystyle\widehat{s}(t)

\displaystyle=\widehat{s}(0)-t,

(1.13)

\displaystyle\widehat{w}(t)

\displaystyle=\frac{\omega\alpha}{\lambda}(\widehat{s}(0)-t)^{2}\log\frac{\widehat{s}(0)}{\widehat{s}(0)-t}.

We will show in Theorem
1.2
below that the system (
1.8
)-(
1.11
) admits a unique solution under initial conditions that are relevant to the evolution of the SIR-
\omega
model.

•

Positive initial condition
:

\widehat{i}(0)\in(0,1),\ \widehat{s}(0)\in(0,1-\widehat{i}(0)),\ \widehat{i}_{E}(0)>0,\ \widehat{w}(0)=0.

•

Zero initial condition
:

\widehat{s}(0)=1,\ \widehat{i}(0)=\widehat{i}_{E}(0)=0,\ \widehat{w}(0)=0.

The positive initial condition corresponds to a positive fraction of individuals initially infected while the zero initial condition is useful for analyzing the case of one initially infected node.

We denote the solution to (
1.8
)-(
1.11
) by
\widehat{\mathbf{x}}(t):=(\widehat{s}(t),\widehat{i}(t),\widehat{i}_{E}(t),\widehat{w}(t))
.
For any solution
\widehat{{\mathbf{x}}}(t)
, we let
t_{*}
to be the first time after 0 when
\widehat{i}_{E}(t)
hits 0, i.e.,

(1.14)

t_{*}:=\inf\{0<t\leq\widehat{s}(0):\widehat{i}_{E}(t)=0\}.

Here we use the convention that
\inf\emptyset=\widehat{s}(0)
. We shall show in Section
3.2
that
t_{*}<\widehat{s}(0)
.

We now state the existence and uniqueness theorem up to time
t_{*}
.

Theorem 1.2
.

For either positive initial condition or zero initial condition (in this case assuming additionally that
\lambda>\lambda_{c}
), there exists a unique solution
\widehat{\mathbf{x}}(t)
to the system (
1.8
) -(
1.11
) up to time
t_{*}
.

With Theorem
1.2
established, we can now state the convergence theorem for the stochastic process
\widehat{\mathbf{X}}/n
.

Theorem 1.3
.

For either positive initial condition or zero initial condition, suppose

\widehat{\mathbf{X}}^{(n)}(0)/n\xrightarrow{\mathbb{P}}\widehat{\mathbf{x}}(0),

then

(1.15)

\sup_{t\leq\widehat{s}(0)\wedge\widehat{\tau}^{(n)}}\left(\left|\frac{1}{n}\widehat{S}^{(n)}(t)-\widehat{s}(t)\right|+\left|\frac{1}{n}\widehat{W}^{(n)}(t)-\widehat{w}(t)\right|\right)\xrightarrow{\mathbb{P}}0,

and

(1.16)

\sup_{t\leq t_{*}\wedge\widehat{\tau}^{(n)}}\left\|\frac{1}{n}\widehat{\mathbf{X}}^{(n)}(t)-\widehat{\mathbf{x}}(t)\right\|_{2}\xrightarrow{\mathbb{P}}0.

Theorem
1.3
can be used to derive the convergence of the scaled final epidemic size
\Lambda^{(n)}/n
under (
1.6
).

Theorem 1.4
.

Assume that (
1.6
) holds. Let
t_{*}
be given by (
1.14
).

(i)

For positive initial condition: suppose
\widehat{\mathbf{X}}^{(n)}(0)/n\xrightarrow{\mathbb{P}}\widehat{\mathbf{x}}(0)
where
\widehat{i}(0)>0
and
\widehat{i}_{E}(0)>0
. Then,

(1.17)

\frac{\Lambda^{(n)}}{n}\xrightarrow{\mathbb{P}}1-\widehat{s}(0)+t_{*}\quad\mbox{as}\quad n\to\infty.

(ii)

For zero initial condition: suppose
\lambda>\lambda_{c}
, and for each
n
the epidemic starts from one infected vertex with the rest being susceptible.
Then conditionally on a major outbreak,

(1.18)

\frac{\Lambda^{(n)}}{n}\xrightarrow{\mathbb{P}}t_{*}\quad\mbox{as}\quad n\to\infty.

Remark 1.5
.

Note that the right-hand sides of (
1.17
) and (
1.18
) can be written as
1-\widehat{s}(t_{*})
.
Theorem
1.4
thus verifies
(
Ball and Britton 2022
, Conjecture 2.1)
under the condition (
1.6
). We conjecture that this condition is not essential, i.e., Theorem
1.4
holds without assuming (
1.6
).

Theorem
1.1
follows from Theorem
1.4
via a direct computation. See Section
6
.

We end this section with a comment regarding the SIR-
\omega
model on random graphs generated from the configuration model.

Remark 1.6
.

While we believe that a similar phase transition should occur for the SIR-
\omega
model on the configuration model, we do find some nontrivial barriers in adapting the proof of Theorem
1.1
to that case. See also Remark

below.

1.2.
Previous work

1.2.1.
SIR on static graphs

So far the SIR model on several random graph models (including Erdős-Rényi graphs and the configuration model) has been well understood. For the case of Erdős-Rényi(
n
,
\mu/n
) graphs, let
T
be a random variable with the Exponential(
\lambda
) distribution, and consider the functions

G_{0}(z)=\mathbb{E}\exp\left(-\mu(1-z)(1-e^{-\lambda T})\right),\quad G_{1}(z)=\exp\left(-\mu\frac{\lambda}{\lambda+\gamma}(1-z)\right).

The following theorem is well known. See, e.g.,
Neal 2003
.

Theorem 1.7
.

The critical value for SIR model on static Erdős-Rényi(
n
,
\mu/n
) is given by
\lambda_{c}=\gamma/(\mu-1)
. For
\lambda>\lambda_{c}
,
the probability of a major outbreak converges to
1-z_{0}
where
z_{0}<1
is the solution to the equation
G_{0}(z)=z
, i.e.,

\lim_{n\to\infty}\mathbb{P}(\Lambda^{(n)}\geq\epsilon n)=1-z_{0}

for all small enough
\epsilon>0
.
In addition, conditionally on a major outbreak, the scaled final epidemic size
\Lambda^{(n)}/n
converges to
1-z_{1}
, where
z_{1}<1
is the solution to the equation
G_{1}(z)=z
. In particular,
z_{0}\neq z_{1}
.

It follows from this theorem that the phase transition for final epidemic size of the SIR model on static Erdős-Rényi graphs is continuous, since we have
1-z_{1}\to 0
as
\lambda\to\lambda_{c}
.

Similar results hold for the configuration model. Take any degree sequence
(D_{1},\ldots,D_{n})
, denoted by
\mathbf{D}_{n}
, the configuration model
\mathbb{CM}(n,\mathbf{D}_{n})
is constructed by uniformly randomly pairing
D_{1},\ldots,D_{n}
half-edges attached to vertices
1,\ldots,n
, respectively. Two paired half-edges form a single edge. If
D_{i},1\leq i\leq n
, are i.i.d. sampled from a distribution
D
(with minor adjustment to make sure that the sum
\sum_{i=1}^{n}D_{i}
is even), then we may write
\mathbb{CM}(n,D)
for
\mathbb{CM}(n,\mathbf{D}_{n})
. It is known that, when
\mathbb{E}(D^{2})<\infty
, the local neighborhood of
\mathbb{CM}(n,D)
resembles a two-stage Galton-Watson tree where the offspring distribution of the root is
D
while the offspring distribution of later generations is
D^{*}-1
(
D^{*}
is the size-biased version of
D
). See, e.g.,
(
van der Hofstad 2017
, Chapter 7)
for a detailed introduction to the configuration model. Then for the SIR model on
\mathbb{CM}(n,D)
,
\lambda_{c}=\gamma m_{1}/(m_{2}-2m_{1})
where
m_{i}
is the
i
-th moment of
D
. In addition, both the probability of a major outbreak and the scaled final epidemic size (conditionally on a major outbreak) converge to a deterministic quantity which goes to 0 as
\lambda\searrow\lambda_{c}
. See, e.g.,
Janson et al. 2014
for statements and proofs of these results.

1.2.2.
SI on evolving graphs

As mentioned before, in SI type models infected vertices will not recover.

For the SI-
\omega
epidemics on Erdős-Rényi(
n,\mu/n
) graphs,
Ball and Britton 2022
found out the limit of
\Lambda^{(n)}/n
(which can be expressed as the solution to an explicit equation) and gave a necessary and sufficient condition for the phase transition to be discontinuous. See
(
Ball and Britton 2022
, Theorem 2.5 and Theorem 2.6)
. More precisely, they showed that the phase transition for the fraction of infected is discontinuous if and only if
\alpha>1/3
and
\mu>3\alpha/(3\alpha-1)
.

When
\alpha=1
, the SI-
\omega
model is also called the evoSI model.
Durrett and Yao 2022
analyzed the phase transition of final epidemic size in the evoSI model with underlying graph
\mathbb{CM}(n,D)
. Let
\mu_{i}
be the
i
-th factorial moment of
D
, i.e.,

\mu_{i}=\mathbb{E}[D(D-1)\cdots(D-i+1)].

Set

(1.19)

\Delta=-\frac{\mu_{3}}{\mu_{1}}+3(\mu_{2}-\mu_{1}).

They showed that the phase transition is continuous if
\Delta<0
and discontinuous if
\Delta>0
. Note that when
D\sim\mathrm{Poisson}(\mu)
and
\alpha=1
,
\Delta=-\mu^{2}+3(\mu^{2}-\mu)
. Thus, the condition
\Delta>0
corresponds to
\mu>3/2
, which is the condition given by Ball and Britton by setting
\alpha=1
.

1.2.3.
SIR on evolving graphs

Jiang et al. 2019
studied the evoSIR model (SIR-
\omega
with
\alpha=1
) on Erdős-Rényi(
n
,
\mu/n
) graphs. They showed in
(
Jiang et al. 2019
, Theorem 1)
that the critical infection rate for evoSIR model is give by

(1.20)

\lambda_{c}=\frac{\gamma+\omega}{\mu-1}.

In addition, the probability of a major outbreak converges to
1-z^{\prime\prime}_{0}
where
z^{\prime\prime}_{0}<1
is the solution to the equation
G_{2}(z)=z
with
G_{2}
defined by

G_{2}(z)=\exp\left(-\mu\frac{\lambda}{\lambda+\omega+\gamma}(1-z)\right).

Consequently, as
\lambda
approaches
\lambda_{c}
, the probability of a major outbreak also converges to 0.
The authors there also used numerical experiments to discover that for certain parameters the phase transition for final epidemic size is discontinuous. See Figure
1.1
.

\mu=5
,
\omega=4
and

. In this case
\lambda_{c}=1.25
. The top curve is the simulation of final size of evoSIR. The bottom curve is the final size of the delSIR (SIR-

) epidemic with the same parameters. Other two curves are approximations of evoSIR, which are discussed in

.

SIR-
\omega
model is introduced the in
Britton et al. 2016
.
Ball and Britton 2022
analyzed the SIR-
\omega
model on an Erdős-Rényi(
n
,
\mu/n
) graph using a novel construction that couples the graph and the epidemic. We will explain the construction in Section
2
. Using this construction, they proved the convergence of
\mathbf{X}^{(n)}(t)/n
to a system of ODEs over any finite time horizon. Specifically, let
\mathbf{x}(t)=(s(t),i(t),i_{E}(t),w(t))
be the solution of the following system of equations:

(1.21)

\displaystyle\frac{\mathrm{d}s}{\mathrm{d}t}

\displaystyle=-\lambda i_{E},

(1.22)

\displaystyle\frac{\mathrm{d}i}{\mathrm{d}t}

\displaystyle=-\gamma i+\lambda i_{E},

(1.23)

\displaystyle\frac{\mathrm{d}i_{E}}{\mathrm{d}t}

1.3.
Proof strategies and organization of the paper

Our proof relies on the construction of the SIR-
\omega
model given in
Ball and Britton 2022

S^{(n)}(t)
. As pointed out in
Ball and Britton 2022
, a common approach to analyze epidemic models is performing a suitable time-transform so that the system becomes more solvable. In the case of the SIR-
\omega

)-(

\widehat{i}_{E}
near 0. Thus, one cannot directly apply classical results (e.g.,

\widehat{\tau}^{(n)}
and the fraction of infected vertices

.

We now give a quick overview of the proof of (
1.16
) in the case of positive initial condition to illustrate the idea to overcome this barrier. Fix any
\epsilon>0
, we can divide the interval
[0,t_{*}]
into
[0,t_{*}-\epsilon]
and
[t_{*}-\epsilon,t_{*}]
. We note that
\widehat{i}_{E}
is bounded away from 0 in

) for

. To deal with the other time interval
[t_{*}-\epsilon,t_{*}]
, we need two observations:

•

\widehat{i}(t_{*}-\epsilon)
and
\widehat{i}_{E}(t_{*}-\epsilon)
can be arbitrarily small by taking

to be small. It follows that
\widehat{I}^{(n)}(t_{*}-\epsilon)/n
and
\widehat{I}^{(n)}_{E}(t_{*}-\epsilon)/n
must also be small with high probability, by the convergence in the interval

that we just proved.

•

The evolution equations for
\widehat{I}^{(n)}
and
\widehat{I}^{(n)}_{E}

4.24
) and (

), imply that the upward drift for
\widehat{I}^{(n)}
and
\widehat{I}^{(n)}_{E}
is not large.

Combining these two observations, we conclude that
\widehat{I}^{(n)}/n
and
\widehat{I}^{(n)}_{E}/n
remain small in the time interval

. Since
\widehat{i}
and
\widehat{i}_{E}
are also small in this interval, we finish the proof of (

In order to obtain the convergence of

and prove Theorem

is negative or 0 at

. However, this seems not trivial. To overcome this issue, we just consider the two cases

and

To prove Theorem

we also need to separate the condition (

2.
Construction of the SIR-
\omega
process

The key to our proof is the following construction for the SIR-
\omega
model on Erdős-Rényi graphs, proposed in
Ball and Britton 2022
. Initially, there are
I^{(n)}(0)
infected vertices and
I_{E}^{(n)}(0)
edges attached to these vertices. For each of these edges it has a ‘free’ endpoint which is undetermined at this stage. No edges are attached to other vertices. There are three types of Poisson process occurring on vertices or edges. (When there is an arrival in the Poisson process, we also say that Poisson clock rings.) Below we use the term
infected edges
to denote the edges that are connected to infected vertices and have a free endpoint. We also let
\mathcal{W}^{(n)}(t)
be the pool of rewired susceptible edges, i.e., edges that have been rewired at least once and are susceptible at time
t
. Note that if an edge in the pool
\mathcal{W}^{(n)}(t-)
becomes an infected edge at time
t
, then it will exit
\mathcal{W}^{(n)}(t)
, though it may joint the pool again at some later time.

•

Recovery Poisson processes for each infected vertex with rate
\gamma
. Each infected vertex recovers with rate
\gamma
. When the recovery clock rings, the infected vertex becomes recovered and all edges attached to this infected vertex are no longer infected.

•

Rewiring/dropping Poisson processes for each infected edge with rate
\omega
. Each infected edge has an independent Poisson process with rate
\omega
on it. Whenever the clock rings, this edge is dropped with probability
1-\alpha
and rewired to a uniformly chosen vertex excluding itself with probability
\alpha
. Suppose at time
t
such a clock rings on an edge
e
attached to vertex
x
and that edge decides to be rewired. Then vertex
x
loses
e
at time
t
.

–

If
e
is rewired to an infected vertex
y
, then the number of infected edges of
y
increases by 1. This case occurs with probability
(I(t)-1)/(n-1)
.

–

If
e
is rewired to a susceptible vertex (which we don’t specify at this moment), then
e
is added to
\mathcal{W}^{(n)}(t)
and its cardinality
W^{(n)}(t)=W^{(n)}(t-)+1
.

–

If
e
is rewired to a recovered vertex, then the effect is similar to dropping.

•

Infection Poisson processes for each infected edge with rate
\lambda
. Each infected edge is connected to a (uniformly chosen) susceptible vertex with rate
\lambda
. Suppose infected edge
e
is connected to susceptible vertex
y
. Then
y
immediately becomes infected. We now attach a random number of edges to
y
(which become infected edges immediately) given by

(2.1)

\mbox{Poisson}\left(\frac{S^{(n)}(t)}{n}\mu\right)+\mbox{Binomial}\left(W^{(n)}(t-),\frac{2}{S^{(n)}(t-)}\right).

We also delete all infected edges (other than those newly added to
y
) with probability
1/S^{(n)}(t-)
, independently.

To explain the construction, note that we have required infected edges to be paired to susceptible vertices only. The definition of Erdős-Rényi graphs implies that the number of edges (excluding rewired edges) between
y
and susceptible vertices at time
t
is close in distribution to Poisson(
S^{(n)}(t)\mu/n
). On the other hand, the number of rewired susceptible edges in the pool
\mathcal{W}^{(n)}(t-)
that have an endpoint as
y
has a
\mbox{Binomial}(W^{(n)}(t-),2/S^{(n)}(t-))
distribution. The reason of deleting infected edges with probability
1/S^{(n)}(t-)
is to remove all infected edges that connect to
y
(so that all of the remaining infected edges connect to susceptible vertices).

It has been proved in
(
Ball and Britton 2022
, Section 2.3)
that the process just constructed (which we temporarily call the coupled SIR-
\omega
model) has the same law as the original SIR-
\omega
model on Erdős-Rényi(
n
,
\mu/n
) graphs, after conditioning on the event

(2.2)

\begin{split}\Omega_{\ref{simple}}:=\{&\mbox{The graph induced from the construction}\\
&\mbox{of the couple SIR-$\omega$ model is a simple graph}\}.\end{split}

In addition, Ball and Britton showed that

\liminf_{n\to\infty}\mathbb{P}(\Omega_{\ref{simple}})>0.

Set

(2.3)

\Omega_{\ref{simple0}}:=\{\mbox{A major outbreak occurs in the coupled SIR-$\omega$ model}\}.

Using the techniques in the proof of
(
Janson et al. 2014
, Theorems 2.7 and 2.10)
, we can show that

(2.4)

\liminf_{n\to\infty}\mathbb{P}(\Omega_{\ref{simple0}}\cap\Omega_{\ref{simple}})>0.

Equation (
2.4
) implies that it suffices to prove Theorem
1.1
for the coupled SIR-
\omega
model. To see this, we use a subscript ‘old’ to denote the original SIR-
\omega
model and ‘new’ to denote the coupled SIR-
\omega
model. Suppose (
1.7
) holds for the coupled model, then for
\lambda<\lambda_{1}
,

(2.5)

\lim_{n\to\infty}\mathbb{P}(\Lambda^{(n)}_{\textrm{new}}>\epsilon n)=0.

By (
2.4
) and (
2.5
),

\limsup_{n\to\infty}\mathbb{P}(\Lambda^{(n)}_{\textrm{new}}>\epsilon n|\Omega_{\ref{simple0}}\cap\Omega_{\ref{simple}})\leq\limsup_{n\to\infty}\frac{\mathbb{P}(\Lambda^{(n)}_{\textrm{new}}>\epsilon n)}{\mathbb{P}(\Omega_{\ref{simple0}}\cap\Omega_{\ref{simple}})}=0.

Since the original model and the coupled model are equivalent upon conditioning on
\Omega_{\ref{simple}}
,

\lim_{n\to\infty}\mathbb{P}(\Lambda^{(n)}_{\textrm{old}}>\epsilon n|\Omega_{\ref{simple0}})=\lim_{n\to\infty}\mathbb{P}(\Lambda^{(n)}_{\textrm{new}}>\epsilon n|\Omega_{\ref{simple0}}\cap\Omega_{\ref{simple}})=0.

This proves Theorem
1.1
for the original model. Hence, in this paper we will not distinguish between the original SIR-
\omega
model and the coupled SIR-
\omega
model.

.

his coupling construction of SIR-

In the rest of this section we will state several implications of the construction just given, which will be used in later sections. In the proofs of Lemmas
2.2
-

Lemma 2.2
.

Let
A_{e}
be the total number of edges appearing in the SIR-
\omega
process. Then exists a constant
C>0
such that

\mathbb{P}(A_{e}\geq 2\mu n)\leq\frac{C}{n}.

Proof.

From the construction of the SIR-
\omega
model,
A_{e}
is bounded from above the sum of two random variables

I_{E}^{(n)}(0)+\sum_{i=1}^{n-I^{(n)}(0)}F_{i}\mbox{ where }F_{i}\mbox{ i.i.d. }\sim\mbox{Poisson}(\mu).

Clearly
I_{E}^{(n)}(0)
is bounded from above by the sum of
I^{(n)}(0)n
independent Bernoulli(
\mu/n
) variables. Let
U_{1}
have the distribution Binomial(
n^{2}
,
\mu/n
) and
U_{2}
the distribution Binomial(
n^{2}/2
,
\mu/n
).
For the case
I^{(n)}(0)>n/2
,

A_{e}\mbox{ is stochastically dominated by }U_{1}+\sum_{i=1}^{n/2}F_{i}.

For the case
I^{(n)}(0)\leq n/2
,

A_{e}\mbox{ is stochastically dominated by }U_{2}+\sum_{i=1}^{n}F_{i}.

We have

\mathbb{E}(U_{1})=\mu n,\quad\textrm{Var}(U_{1})\leq\mu n.

Thus,

(2.6)

\mathbb{P}(U_{1}\geq 5\mu n/4)=\mathbb{P}(U_{1}-\mathbb{E}(U_{1})\geq\mu n/4)\leq\frac{\textrm{Var}(U_{1})}{(\mu n/4)^{2}}\leq\frac{C}{n}.

We also have

(2.7)

\mathbb{P}\left(\sum_{i=1}^{n/2}F_{i}\geq 3n\mu/4\right)=\mathbb{P}\left(\sum_{i=1}^{n/2}(F_{i}-\mu)\geq n\mu/4\right)\leq\frac{\mathrm{Var}(Y_{1})n/2}{(n\mu/4)^{2}}\leq\frac{C}{n}.

Combining (
2.6
) and (
2.7
),

\begin{split}\mathbb{P}\left(A_{e}\geq 2\mu n,I^{(n)}(0)\geq n/2\right)&\leq\mathbb{P}\left(U_{1}+\sum_{i=1}^{n/2}F_{i}\geq 2\mu n\right)\\
&\leq\mathbb{P}(U_{1}\geq 5\mu n/4)+\mathbb{P}\left(\sum_{i=1}^{n/2}F_{i}\geq 3n\mu/4\right)\\
&\leq\frac{C}{n}+\frac{C}{n}\leq\frac{2C}{n}.\end{split}

This proves Lemma
2.2
for the case
I^{(n)}(0)\geq n/2
. The case
I^{(n)}(0)\leq n/2
can be proved similarly. Combining these two cases we deduce Lemma
2.2
.
∎

By Lemma
2.2
,
\mathbb{P}(A_{e}\geq 2\mu n)\to 0
. From now on we work on the event
\{A_{e}\leq 2\mu n\}
.

Lemma 2.3
.

The number of times that any given edge is rewired is stochastically dominated by Geometric(
\alpha\omega/(\omega+\lambda)
). Consequently, the number of rewired edges that any given vertex receives is stochastically dominated by Binomial(
2\mu n
,
2\omega/(\lambda n)
).

Proof.

The rewiring/dropping Poisson processes and infection Poisson processes occur independently for each edge in the construction of the SIR-
\omega
model. By properties of Poisson processes, the probability that rewiring/dropping occurs before infection is
\omega/(\omega+\lambda)
. Also note that the probability of rewiring is
\alpha
in the rewiring/dropping Poisson processes. As a result, for any infected edge
e
,

\mathbb{P}(\mbox{edge }e\mbox{ is rewired before it infects other vertex or gets recovered})\leq\alpha\omega/(\omega+\lambda).

To see this, first note that all other Poisson processes (other than the rewiring/dropping and infection Poisson processes associated with
e
) will either prevent
e
from rewiring, or have no effect on its rewiring. On the other hand, the probability that the rewiring Poisson clock rings first before dropping or infection Poisson clocks is equal to
\alpha\omega/(\omega+\lambda)
.

Since whether each attempt to rewire succeeds or not is independent, the number of times that any given edge is rewired is stochastically dominated by a Geometric distribution with success probability
\alpha\omega/(\omega+\lambda)
. On the other hand, when a rewiring occurs, the probability of any given vertex
x
being selected to receive the rewired edge is at most
1/(n-1)
. As a result, the probability that any given edge
e
has been rewired to any given vertex
x
is bounded from above by

1-\mathbb{E}\left[\left(1-\frac{1}{n-1}\right)^{H}\right]\mbox{ where }H\sim\mbox{Geometric}(\alpha\omega/(\omega+\lambda)),

which is further bounded by (for large
n
)

\mathbb{E}\left(\frac{H}{n-1}\right)=\frac{1}{n-1}\left(\left(1-\frac{\alpha\omega}{\omega+\lambda}\right)^{-1}-1\right)\leq\frac{\omega}{(n-1)\lambda}\leq\frac{2\omega}{\lambda n}.

Since we are working on the event
\{A_{e}\leq 2\mu n\}
, we know that the number of edges that have the potential to be rewired is less than or equal to
2\mu n
. Since the infection Poisson processes and rewiring/dropping Poisson processes are independent for different edges, the total number of rewired edges that any given vertex receives is stochastically dominated by

\mbox{Binomial}\left(2\mu n,\frac{2\omega}{\lambda n}\right).

∎

Lemma 2.4
.

Let
N_{i}
be the number of edges added to vertex
i
when it first becomes infected. Then
N_{i}
is stochastically dominated by the independent sum

\mbox{Poisson}(\mu)+\mbox{Binomial}(2\mu n,\omega/(\lambda n)).

Consequently, for some constant
C_{\ref{ni4}}
we have

(2.8)

\mathbb{E}(N_{i}^{4})\leq C_{\ref{ni4}}.

Similarly, setting
\widehat{N}_{i}
to be the number of edges of vertex
i
when it becomes recovered, then

(2.9)

\mathbb{E}(\widehat{N}_{i}^{4})\leq C_{\ref{ni4}}.

Proof.

Let
\mathfrak{t}_{i}
be the time when
i
first becomes infected.
Then
N_{i}
is equal to the independent sum

\mbox{Poisson}\left(\frac{S^{(n)}(\mathfrak{t}_{i})}{n}\mu\right)+\mbox{Binomial}\left(W^{(n)}(\mathfrak{t}_{i}-),\frac{2}{S^{(n)}(\mathfrak{t}_{i}-)}\right),

where the second part in the sum stands for the number of edges rewired to vertex
i
when
i
first becomes infected. By Lemma
2.3
we see
N_{i}
is stochastically dominated by the independent sum Poisson(
\mu
)+Binomial(
2\mu n
,
2\omega/(\lambda n)
). Set
V\sim\mbox{Poisson}(\mu)
,
B\sim\mbox{Binomial}(2\mu n
,
2\omega/(\lambda n))
. In addition,
V
and
B
are independent. Then we have

\mathbb{E}(N_{i}^{4})\leq\mathbb{E}((V+B)^{4}).

Note that the fourth moment of Poisson(
\mu
) is clearly finite, and

\mathbb{E}(B^{4})\leq\sum_{j=1}^{4}(2\mu n)^{j}\left(\frac{2\omega}{\lambda n}\right)^{j}\leq C.

Hence, for some sufficiently large constant

,

\mathbb{E}((V+B)^{4})\leq\mathbb{E}(8(V^{4}+B^{4}))\leq C_{\ref{ni4}},

which then implies that

and proves (

). Equation (

) can be proved in exactly the same way as (

∎

In this section we prove the existence and uniqueness of the solution to the system (

)-(
1.11
). Note that we have already given closed form expressions for
\widehat{s}(t)
and
\widehat{w}(t)
in (
1.12
) and (
1.13
) (which are necessarily unique). Hence, it remains to deal with
\widehat{i}
and
\widehat{i}_{E}
. We treat the case of positive initial condition in Section
3.1
, and the case of zero initial condition in Section
3.2
.

We now show that, the
t_{*}
defined in (
1.14
) is strictly smaller than
\widehat{s}(0)
. To see this, we use an argument by contradiction. Assume that
t_{*}=\widehat{s}(0)
. Then
\widehat{i}_{E}(t)>0
for all
0<t<\widehat{s}(0)
. Equations (
1.10
), (
1.12
) and (

\epsilon>0
so that

\frac{\mathrm{d}\widehat{i}_{E}(t)}{\mathrm{d}t}\leq-1-\frac{\widehat{i}_{E}(t)}{\widehat{s}(t)}

holds true for all
t\in(\widehat{s}(0)-\epsilon,\widehat{s}(0))
. It follows that

(3.1)

\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}t}\left(\frac{\widehat{i}_{E}(t)}{\widehat{s}(t)}\right)=\frac{1}{\widehat{s}(t)}\left(\frac{\mathrm{d}\widehat{i}_{E}(t)}{\mathrm{d}t}+\frac{\widehat{i}_{E}(t)}{\widehat{s}(t)}\right)\leq-\frac{1}{\widehat{s}(t)}.

Integrating (

) from
\widehat{s}(0)-\epsilon
to

\frac{\widehat{i}_{E}(t)}{\widehat{s}(t)}-\frac{\widehat{i}_{E}(\widehat{s}(0)-\epsilon)}{\widehat{s}(\widehat{s}(0)-\epsilon)}\leq\int^{t}_{\widehat{s}(0)-\epsilon}\frac{-1}{\widehat{s}(u)}\mathrm{d}u=\log\left(\frac{\widehat{s}(0)-t}{\epsilon}\right),

which goes to

. We have thus arrived at a contradiction. Therefore, we must have

.

3.1.
Existence and uniqueness for positive initial condition

In this case the existence and uniqueness for the pair
(\widehat{i},\widehat{i}_{E})
follows from standard results in ODE theory. Indeed, the right-hand sides of (
1.9
) and (
1.10
) are Lipschitz functions of
\widehat{i}
and
\widehat{i}_{E}
when
\widehat{i}_{E}
is bounded away from 0. Existence theory for ODE implies that, given any solution
(\widehat{i},\widehat{i}_{E})
defined for small
t>0
, one can extend its domain to
t_{*}
where
\lim_{t\to t_{*}}\widehat{i}_{E}(t)=0
. We set
\widehat{i}_{E}(t_{*})=0
.

As for the uniqueness, assume that there are two solution
(\widehat{i}_{1},\widehat{i}_{E,1})
and
(\widehat{i}_{2},\widehat{i}_{E,2})
. Given any
\delta>0
,

\begin{split}b_{\delta,1}&=\inf\{t\geq 0:\widehat{i}_{E,1}\leq\delta\},\\
b_{\delta,2}&=\inf\{t\geq 0:\widehat{i}_{E,2}\leq\delta\},\\
b_{\delta}&=b_{\delta,1}\wedge b_{\delta,2}.\end{split}

We define
t_{*,1}
and
t_{*,2}
to be the first time after 0 that
\widehat{i}_{E,1}
and
\widehat{i}_{E,2}
hit 0, respectively. Then
b_{\delta,1}\leq t_{*,1}<\widehat{s}(0)
. Hence,
b_{\delta}<\widehat{s}(0)
. By (
1.9
) and (
1.10
), for
t\leq b_{\delta}
,

(3.2)

\begin{split}\left|\widehat{i}_{E,1}(t)-\widehat{i}_{E,2}(t)\right|&\leq\int_{0}^{t}\frac{\left|\widehat{i}_{E,1}(u)-\widehat{i}_{E,2}(u)\right|}{\widehat{s}(u)}\mathrm{d}u+\frac{\omega\alpha}{\lambda}\int_{0}^{t}\left|\widehat{i}_{1}(u)-\widehat{i}_{2}(u)\right|\mathrm{d}u\\
&\leq\left(\frac{1}{\widehat{s}(0)-b_{\delta}}+\frac{\omega\alpha}{\lambda}\right)\int_{0}^{t}\left(\left|\widehat{i}_{E,1}(u)-\widehat{i}_{E,2}(u)\right|+\left|\widehat{i}_{1}(u)-\widehat{i}_{2}(u)\right|\right)\mathrm{d}u,\end{split}

and

(3.3)

\begin{split}\left|\widehat{i}_{1}(t)-\widehat{i}_{2}(t)\right|&\leq\frac{\gamma}{\lambda}\int_{0}^{t}\left|\frac{\widehat{i}_{1}(u)}{\widehat{i}_{E,1}(u)}-\frac{\widehat{i}_{2}(u)}{\widehat{i}_{E,2}(u)}\right|\mathrm{d}u\\
&\leq\frac{\gamma}{\lambda}\ \int_{0}^{t}\frac{\left|\widehat{i}_{E,2}\widehat{i}_{1}(u)-\widehat{i}_{2}(u)\widehat{i}_{E,1}(u)\right|}{\widehat{i}_{E,1}(u)\widehat{i}_{E,2}(u)}\mathrm{d}u\\
&\leq\frac{\gamma}{\lambda}\ \int_{0}^{t}\left(\frac{\left|\widehat{i}_{1}(u)-\widehat{i}_{2}(u)\right|}{\widehat{i}_{E,1}(u)}+\frac{\widehat{i}_{2}(u)\left|\widehat{i}_{E,1}(u)-\widehat{i}_{E,2}(u)\right|}{\widehat{i}_{E,1}(u)\widehat{i}_{E,2}(u)}\right)\mathrm{d}u\\
&\leq\frac{\gamma}{\lambda}\left(\frac{1}{\delta}+\frac{1}{\delta^{2}}\right)\int_{0}^{t}\left(\left|\widehat{i}_{E,1}(u)-\widehat{i}_{E,2}(u)\right|+\left|\widehat{i}_{1}(u)-\widehat{i}_{2}(u)\right|\right)\mathrm{d}u.\end{split}

Combining (
3.2
) and (

), we see that for all

and some constant

,

\left|\widehat{i}_{E,1}(t)-\widehat{i}_{E,2}(t)\right|+\left|\widehat{i}_{1}(t)-\widehat{i}_{2}(t)\right|\leq C\int_{0}^{t}\left(\left|\widehat{i}_{E,1}(u)-\widehat{i}_{E,2}(u)\right|+\left|\widehat{i}_{1}(u)-\widehat{i}_{2}(u)\right|\right)\mathrm{d}u.

By Gronwall’s inequality we deduce that, for all
t\leq b_{\delta}

\widehat{i}_{E,1}(t)=\widehat{i}_{E,2}(t)\mbox{ and }\widehat{i}_{1}(t)=\widehat{i}_{2}(t).

Since
\delta
can be taken to be arbitrarily small, we conclude that
t_{*,1}=t_{*,2}
, and for all
t\leq t_{*,1}
,

\widehat{i}_{E,1}(t)=\widehat{i}_{E,2}(t)\mbox{ and }\widehat{i}_{1}(t)=\widehat{i}_{2}(t).

This proves the uniqueness part.

3.2.
Existence and uniqueness for zero initial condition

We start from the comparison principle for the system (

)-(
1.10
) under positive initial condition.

Lemma 3.1
.

Suppose that there are two solutions
(\widehat{i}_{1},\widehat{i}_{E,1})
and
(\widehat{i}_{2},\widehat{i}_{E,2})
such that

\widehat{i}_{1}(0)\leq\widehat{i}_{2}(0),\ \widehat{i}_{E,1}(0)\leq\widehat{i}_{E,2}(0)\mbox{ and }\widehat{i}_{E,1}(0)>0.

Then for all

\widehat{i}_{1}(t)\leq\widehat{i}_{2}(t)\mbox{ and }\widehat{i}_{E,1}(t)\leq\widehat{i}_{E,2}(t).

Proof.

Denote the right-hand side of (

) and (

)
by

and
F_{2}(\widehat{i},\widehat{i}_{E},t)
,
respectively. In other words,

and

are defined by

and

F_{2}(x,y,t)=-1-\frac{\gamma}{\lambda}+\mu(\widehat{s}(0)-t)-\frac{y}{\widehat{s}(0)-t}+2\frac{\omega\alpha}{\lambda}(\widehat{s}(0)-t)\log\frac{\widehat{s}(0)}{\widehat{s}(0)-t}-\frac{\omega}{\lambda}(1-\alpha+\alpha(1-x)).

Here we have substituted the explicit expressions of (

) and (

) into the right-hand side of (

It is clear that for any

Thus, the system (

(

∎

Remark 3.2

Proof of the existence

Recall that for zero initial condition

.) Set

We claim that

(3.4)

\liminf_{\epsilon\to 0}t^{\epsilon}_{*}>0.

Proof of (
3.4
).

Using (
1.10
), we see that for some

(3.5)

\left|\widehat{i}^{\prime}_{E,\epsilon}\right|\leq C_{\ref{hati'}},\quad\forall\ \epsilon\leq 1,\ t\leq 1/2.

Hence,

(3.6)

\begin{split}\frac{\mathrm{d}\widehat{i}_{E,\epsilon}}{\mathrm{d}t}&=-1-\frac{\gamma}{\lambda}-\frac{\omega}{\lambda}+\mu(1-t)-\frac{\widehat{i}_{E,\epsilon}}{1-t}+\frac{2\widehat{w}}{\widehat{s}}+\frac{\omega\alpha}{\lambda}\widehat{i}_{\epsilon}\\

Thanks to the assumption

By (

∎

Define

Clearly

) and (

)-(

1.10

\limsup_{t\to 0}\frac{\widehat{i}(t)}{\widehat{i}_{E}(t)}<\infty.

\widehat{i}(t)\leq t,\quad\forall t\geq 0.

3.2.2.
Proof of the uniqueness

We now prove the uniqueness part. Suppose that
(\widehat{i}_{1}(t),\widehat{i}_{E,1}(t))
and
(\widehat{i}_{2}(t),\widehat{i}_{E,2}(t))
are two solutions that satisfy (

)-(
1.10

Step 1
: We first show that either

(3.11)

\widehat{i}_{1}(t)\leq\widehat{i}_{2}(t),\ \widehat{i}_{E,1}(t)\leq\widehat{i}_{E,2}(t),\quad\forall\ t\leq t_{*,1}\wedge t_{*,2},

or

(3.12)

\widehat{i}_{1}(t)\geq\widehat{i}_{2}(t),\ \widehat{i}_{E,1}(t)\geq\widehat{i}_{E,2}(t),\quad\forall\ t\leq t_{*,1}\wedge t_{*,2},

To see this, we consider two cases.

•

Case ①: either

\widehat{i}_{1}(t)\leq\widehat{i}_{2}(t),\quad\forall\ t\in(0,t_{*,1}\wedge t_{*,2}),

or

\widehat{i}_{1}(t)\geq\widehat{i}_{2}(t),\quad\forall\ t\in(0,t_{*,1}\wedge t_{*,2}).

Using Remark
3.3
we see that either (
3.11
)
or (
3.12
) holds true.

•

Case ②: the opposite of Case ①. Without loss of generality let us assume that for some
0<t_{3}<t_{1}<t_{*,1}\wedge t_{*,2}
, we have

\widehat{i}_{1}(t_{3})>\widehat{i}_{2}(t_{3})
and
\widehat{i}_{1}(t_{1})<\widehat{i}_{2}(t_{1})
. There must exist

such that
\widehat{i}_{1}(t_{2})=\widehat{i}_{2}(t_{2})

3.1
and Remark
3.2
, we must have
\widehat{i}_{E,2}(t_{2})>\widehat{i}_{E,1}(t_{2})
, which, in turn, implies that
\widehat{i}_{E,2}(t_{3})>\widehat{i}_{E,1}(t_{3})
. Now we define

t_{4}=\sup\{0\leq t\leq t_{3}:\widehat{i}_{1}(t)=\widehat{i}_{2}(t)\mbox{ or }\widehat{i}_{E,1}(t)=\widehat{i}_{E,2}(t)\}.

We then have
0\leq t_{3}<t_{3}
. Moreover, for all
t_{4}<t<t_{3}
, we have

(3.13)

\widehat{i}_{1}(t)>\widehat{i_{2}}(t)\mbox{ and }\widehat{i}_{E,1}(t)<\widehat{i}_{E,2}(t).

Now there are two possible scenarios:

–

Scenario 1:
\widehat{i}_{1}(t_{4})=\widehat{i}_{2}(t_{4})
and
\widehat{i}_{E,1}(t_{4})\leq\widehat{i}_{E,2}(t_{4})
. Using this and equations (
1.9
) and (
3.13
),

(3.14)

\begin{split}0&=(\widehat{i}_{1}(t_{3})-\widehat{i}_{1}(t_{4}))-(\widehat{i}_{2}(t_{3})-\widehat{i}_{2}(t_{4}))\\
&=\frac{\gamma}{\lambda}\int_{t_{4}}^{t_{3}}\left(\frac{\widehat{i}_{2}(u)}{\widehat{i}_{E,2}(u)}-\frac{\widehat{i}_{1}(u)}{\widehat{i}_{E,1}(u)}\right)\mathrm{d}u<0,\end{split}

which is a contradiction.

–

Scenario 2:
\widehat{i}_{1}(t_{4})>\widehat{i}_{2}(t_{4})
and
\widehat{i}_{E,1}(t_{4})=\widehat{i}_{E,2}(t_{4})
. Then we have
t_{4}\in(0,t_{3})
. Using Lemma
3.1
, we get
\widehat{i}_{1}(t_{2})\geq\widehat{i}_{2}(t_{2})
. But this contradicts with the assumption
\widehat{i}_{1}(t_{2})<\widehat{i}_{2}(t_{2})
in Case ②.

Since both scenarios are impossible, we conclude that Case ② cannot occur.

We have now finished the proof of (
3.11
) and (
3.12
).

Step 2
: Without loss of generality we assume that (
3.11
) holds true. Using (
1.10
),

(3.15)

\begin{split}\widehat{i}_{E,2}(t)-\widehat{i}_{E,1}(t)&\leq\int_{0}^{t}\frac{\omega\alpha}{\lambda}(\widehat{i}_{2}(u)-\widehat{i}_{1}(u))\mathrm{d}u.\end{split}

Define the function

H(t):=\sup_{0\leq u\leq t}\left|\widehat{i}_{1}(u)-\widehat{i}_{2}(u)\right|.

For

), (
3.10
), (

) and (

\begin{split}\widehat{i}_{2}(t)-\widehat{i}_{1}(t)&=\frac{\gamma}{\lambda}\int_{0}^{t}\left(\frac{\widehat{i}_{1}(u)}{\widehat{i}_{E,1}(u)}-\frac{\widehat{i}_{2}(u)}{\widehat{i}_{E,2}(u)}\right)\mathrm{d}u\\
&\leq\frac{\gamma}{\lambda}\int_{0}^{t}\left(\frac{\widehat{i}_{1}(u)}{\widehat{i}_{E,1}(u)}-\frac{\widehat{i}_{1}(u)}{\widehat{i}_{E,2}(u)}\right)\mathrm{d}u\\

It follows that

, applying the Gronwall’s ineqaulity we deduce that

4.
Approximations of the SIR-
\omega
process by differential equations

The proof of the convergences in Theorem
1.3
is based on the general strategy of tightness and uniqueness argument. The starting point is the Dynkin’s formula (see, e.g., Chapter 4, Proposition 1.7 in
Ethier and Kurtz 1986
), which says that, if
V(t)
is a Markov chain, then for any function
f
in the domain of
L
(the Markov generator for
V(t)
), we have

f(V(t))=f(V(0))+\int_{0}^{t}Lf(V(s))\mathrm{d}s+M(t),

where
M(t)
is a martingale. This is also called Dynkin’s decomposition. Suppose for a sequence of Markov chains
V^{(n)}
and a function
f
, the martingale term
M^{(n)}(t)
converges to
0
as
n\to\infty
, and the drift part
Lf(V^{(n)}(t))
can be approximated by
g(f(V^{n}(t)),t)
for some function
g
. Then, under the tightness of
f(V^{(n)}(t))
, its possible limit point
u(t)
satisfies

u(t)=u(0)+\int_{0}^{t}g(u(s),s)\mathrm{d}s.

If this equation has a unique solution for any initial condition, then
f(V^{(n)}(t))
convergence to
u(t)
in probability. We will apply the tightness/uniqueness framework to
\widehat{S}^{(n)},\widehat{W}^{(n)},\widehat{I}^{(n)},\widehat{I}^{(n)}_{E}
.

Section
4
is organized into three parts. We first prove (
1.15
) in Section
4.1
, which is composed of three subsections
4.1.1
-
4.1.3
. In Section
4.1.1
, we figure out the Dynkin’s decomposition of
\widehat{S}^{(n)}
and find that the drift term is deterministic. Thus, by controlling its martingale part, we prove the convergence of
\widehat{S}^{(n)}/n
. In Section
4.1.2
we prove
\widehat{W}^{(n)}
converges to
\widehat{w}
for
t\leq\widehat{s}(0)-\epsilon
. In this regime, it is relatively easy to control the drift part in the Dynkin’s decomposition of
\widehat{W}^{(n)}
and thus deduce its tightness. In Section
4.1.3
we lift this restriction, by controlling the oscillations of
\widehat{W}^{(n)}/n
and
\widehat{w}
in the interval
t\in[\widehat{s}(0)-\epsilon,\widehat{s}(0)]
.

Section
4.2
deals with the convergences of
(\widehat{I}^{(n)}/n,\widehat{I}_{E}^{(n)}/n)
under the condition
\widehat{i}(0),\widehat{i}_{E}(0)>0
. We write down the evolution equations for
\widehat{I}^{(n)}
and
\widehat{I}_{E}^{(n)}
in Section
4.2.1
. In Section
4.2.2
we first state the convergence of
(\widehat{I}^{(n)}/n,\widehat{I}^{(n)}_{E}/n)
up to the time when
\widehat{I}_{E}^{(n)}
drops below
\delta n
for some small
\delta>0
. Then we remove this restriction by again estimating the fluctuations of
\widehat{I}^{(n)}
and
\widehat{I}^{(n)}_{E}
when
t
is near
t_{*}\wedge\widehat{\tau}^{(n)}
.

Finally, in Section
4.3
we prove (
1.16
) for zero initial condition case where
\widehat{i}(0)=\widehat{i}_{E}(0)=0
.

4.1.
Proof of (
1.15
): convergence of
\widehat{S}^{(n)}(t)/n
and
\widehat{W}^{(n)}(t)/n

4.1.1.
Proof of the convergence of
\widehat{S}^{(n)}(t)/n
to
\widehat{s}(t)

We start from the analysis of
\widehat{S}^{(n)}(t)
. Corresponding to the three types of Poisson processes, we can divide the change of
\widehat{S}^{(n)}(t)
into three categories.

•

Change of
\widehat{S}^{(n)}(t)
due to recovery Poisson processes is 0.

•

Change of
\widehat{S}^{(n)}(t)
due to rewiring/dropping Poisson processes is 0.

•

Change of
\widehat{S}^{(n)}(t)
due to infection Poisson Processes is
-1
. (Hereafter, the minus sign in the change of certain quantity means that quantity will decrease.) The rate for this to occur is

\lambda\widehat{I}^{(n)}_{E}(t)\times\frac{n}{\lambda\widehat{I}^{(n)}_{E}(t)}=n.

(The factor
\frac{n}{\lambda\widehat{I}^{(n)}_{E}(t)}
comes from the fact that we are considering the time-changed dynamics.)

Collecting these three types of changes and applying Dynkin’s formula, we see that

(4.1)

\widehat{S}^{(n)}(t)=\widehat{S}^{(n)}(0)+\int_{0}^{t}(-n)\mathrm{d}u+M^{(n)}_{1}(t),

where
M^{(n)}_{1}(t)
is a martingale. Note that we can also define
M^{(n)}_{1}(t)
for
t\geq\widehat{\tau}^{(n)}
(
\widehat{\tau}^{(n)}
is the first time that
\widehat{I}^{(n)}_{E}
reaches 0) by simply setting
M^{(n)}_{1}(t)=M_{1}(\widehat{\tau}^{(n)})
. Then
M^{(n)}_{1}(t)
is a martingale defined for all
t\geq 0
.

By (
4.1
), the quadratic variation process of
M^{(n)}_{1}(t)
,
\langle M^{(n)}_{1}(t),M^{(n)}_{1}(t)\rangle
, is equal to the quadratic variation process of
\widehat{S}^{(n)}(t)
. The quadratic variation process of
\widehat{S}^{(n)}(t)
can be bounded using previous analysis for the change of
\widehat{S}^{(n)}(t)
. In particular, since the clocks in infection Poisson processes can effectively ring at most
n
times, we have

(4.2)

\langle\widehat{S}^{(n)}(t),\widehat{S}^{(n)}(t)\rangle=\sum_{t^{\prime}\leq t}(\widehat{S}^{(n)}(t^{\prime})-\widehat{S}^{(n)}(t^{\prime}-))^{2}\leq n\times 1^{2}=n,\quad\forall\ t\leq\widehat{\tau}^{(n)}.

Consequently,

\sup_{t\geq 0}\mathbb{E}\left(M^{(n)}_{1}(t)^{2}\right)\leq n.

By
L^{2}
maximal inequality applied to the martingale
M^{(n)}_{1}(t)
, we have

(4.3)

\mathbb{E}\left(\sup_{0\leq t\leq\widehat{\tau}^{(n)}}M^{(n)}_{1}(t)^{2}\right)\leq 4n.

Markov inequality then implies that

(4.4)

\mathbb{P}\left(\sup_{t\leq\widehat{\tau}^{(n)}}\left|M^{(n)}_{1}(t)\right|>n^{2/3}\right)\leq\frac{4n}{n^{4/3}}\leq 4n^{-1/3}.

Dividing both sides of (
4.1
) by
n
, we get

(4.5)

\frac{\widehat{S}^{(n)}(t)}{n}=\frac{\widehat{S}^{(n)}(0)}{n}-t+\frac{M^{(n)}_{1}(t)}{n}.

Recall that we have assumed that
\widehat{S}^{(n)}(0)/n\xrightarrow{\mathbb{P}}\widehat{s}(0)
. Combining (
4.4
) and (
4.5
),

(4.6)

\sup_{t\leq\widehat{\tau}^{(n)}}\left|\widehat{S}^{(n)}(t)/n-(\widehat{s}(0)-t)\right|\xrightarrow{\mathbb{P}}0,

as desired.

4.1.2.
Proof of the convergence of
\widehat{W}^{(n)}(t)/n
to
\widehat{w}(t)
for
t
away from
\widehat{s}(0)

Now we analyze the evolution equation of
\widehat{W}^{(n)}(t)
.

•

Change of
\widehat{W}^{(n)}(t)
due to recovery Poisson processes is 0.

•

Change of
\widehat{W}^{(n)}(t)
due to rewiring/dropping Poisson processes is 1 with probability
\alpha\widehat{S}^{(n)}(t)/n
, and 0 with probability
1-\alpha\widehat{S}^{(n)}(t)/n
. Thus, the mean of the change is
\alpha\widehat{S}^{(n)}(t)/n
. The rate for such an event to occur is

\omega\widehat{I}^{(n)}_{E}(t)\times\frac{n}{\lambda\widehat{I}^{(n)}_{E}(t)}=\frac{n\omega}{\lambda}.

•

Change of
\widehat{W}^{(n)}(t)
due to infection Poisson processes is

-\mbox{Binomial}(\widehat{W}^{(n)}(t),2/\widehat{S}^{(n)}(t)).

The mean of the change is
-2\widehat{W}^{(n)}(t)/\widehat{S}^{(n)}(t)
.
The rate for such an event to occur is

\lambda\widehat{I}^{(n)}_{E}(t)\times\frac{n}{\lambda\widehat{I}^{(n)}_{E}(t)}=n.

Hence, we can write, by Dynkin’s formula,

(4.7)

\widehat{W}^{(n)}(t)=\widehat{W}^{(n)}(0)+\int_{0}^{t}\left(\frac{\alpha\widehat{S}^{(n)}(u)}{n}\frac{n\omega}{\lambda}-n\frac{2\widehat{W}^{(n)}(u)}{\widehat{S}^{(n)}(u)}\right)\mathrm{d}u+M^{(n)}_{2}(t).

We first control the martingale
M^{(n)}_{2}(t)
. Using our analysis for the change of
\widehat{W}^{(n)}(t)
,

\langle M^{(n)}_{2}(t),M^{(n)}_{2}(t)\rangle=\langle\widehat{W}^{(n)}(t),\widehat{W}^{(n)}(t)\rangle\leq\sum_{i=1}^{n}N_{i}^{2}+Q(t),

where
Q(t)
is the number of arrivals of rewiring/dropping Poisson processes by time
t
and
N_{i}
is the number of edges added to vertex
i
when vertex
i
first becomes infected. Since
Q(t)
has total rate
\omega n/\lambda
,

(4.8)

\mathbb{E}(Q(t))\leq\frac{\omega nt}{\lambda}.

By Lemma
2.4
we see

(4.9)

\mathbb{E}\left(\sum_{i=1}^{n}N_{i}^{2}\right)\leq Cn.

Combining (
4.8
) and (
4.9
) we see that there exists some constant
C
such that for all
t\leq 2
,

\mathbb{E}\langle M^{(n)}_{2}(t),M^{(n)}_{2}(t)\rangle\leq Cn.

Similarly to (
4.4
) we deduce that

(4.10)

\mathbb{P}\left(\sup_{t\leq\widehat{\tau}^{(n)}\wedge 2}\left|M^{(n)}_{2}(t)\right|>n^{2/3}\right)\leq\frac{Cn}{n^{4/3}}\leq Cn^{-1/3}.

To finish the proof of (
1.15
), we need to show

(4.11)

\sup_{t\leq\widehat{s}(0)\wedge\widehat{\tau}^{(n)}}\left|\widehat{W}^{(n)}(t)/n-\widehat{w}(t)\right|\xrightarrow{\mathbb{P}}0.

We first prove a weaker version of (
4.11
).
Fix any
\epsilon>0
. We now prove

(4.12)

\sup_{0\leq t\leq(\widehat{s}(0)-\epsilon)\wedge\widehat{\tau}^{(n)}}\left|\widehat{W}^{(n)}(t)/n-\widehat{w}(t)\right|\xrightarrow{\mathbb{P}}0.

Proof of (
4.12
).

We prove (
4.12
) in two steps. First we show that
\widehat{W}^{(n)}(t)/n
is a tight sequence in D[0,
\widehat{s}(0)-\epsilon
], the space of Càdlàg functions on the interval [0,
\widehat{s}(0)-\epsilon
]. Then we show the uniqueness of possible sequential limit.

Since
\widehat{W}^{(n)}(0)\to\widehat{w}(0)
, we know
\{\widehat{W}^{(n)}(0)/n\}_{n\geq 1}
is a tight sequence of random variables. To establish tightness of
\{\widehat{W}^{(n)}(t)/n,0\leq t\leq 1-\epsilon\}_{n\geq 1}
, we need to show that for any fixed
\epsilon^{\prime},\delta>0
, there exist
\theta>0
and an integer
n_{0}
so that for all
n\geq n_{0}
,

(4.13)

\mathbb{P}\left(\sup_{\left|t_{1}-t_{2}\right|\leq\theta,t_{1}<t_{2}\leq\widehat{s}(0)-\epsilon}\left|\frac{\widehat{W}^{(n)}(t_{1})}{n}-\frac{\widehat{W}^{(n)}(t_{2})}{n}\right|\geq\delta\right)\leq\epsilon^{\prime}.

Assuming (
4.13
) for the moment, we see that
\{\widehat{W}^{(n)}(t)/n,0\leq t\leq\widehat{s}(0)-\epsilon\}
, as an element of D[0,
\widehat{s}(0)-\epsilon
], satisfies condition (ii) of Proposition 3.26 in
Jacod and Shiryaev 2003
. Consequently,
\{\widehat{W}^{(n)}(t)/n,0\leq t\leq\widehat{s}(0)-\epsilon\}_{n\geq 1}
is a tight sequence.

Now we show that any sequential limit of
\widehat{W}^{(n)}(t)/n
coincides with
\widehat{w}(t)
. By the tightness of
\{\widehat{W}^{(n)}(t)/n,t\geq 0\}_{n\geq 1}
, we see for any subsequence of
\widehat{W}^{(n)}(t)/n
we can extract a further subsequence that converges in distribution to a process
\check{w}(t)
with continuous sample path. By the Skorokhod representation theorem we can assume the convergence is actually in the almost sure sense, and we can also assume that
\widehat{S}^{(n)}(t)/n
converges a.s. to
\widehat{s}(t)
in the interval [0,
\widehat{s}(0)-\epsilon
] and
M^{(n)}_{2}(t)/n
converges to 0 a.s. It remains to prove that the limit point
\check{w}(t)
coincides with
\widehat{w}(t)
, which is then necessarily independent of the subsequence. But this is clearly the case. Indeed, by dividing both sides of (
4.7
) by
n
and then sending
n
to
\infty
, we obtain that

\check{w}(t)=\widehat{w}(0)+\int_{0}^{t}\frac{\omega}{\lambda}\alpha\widehat{s}(u)\mathrm{d}u-\int_{0}^{t}2\frac{\check{w}(u)}{\widehat{s}(u)}\mathrm{d}u,

for
t\leq\widehat{s}(0)-\epsilon
. This equation has a unique solution
\check{w}(t)=\widehat{w}(t)
.

It remains to prove (
4.13
). We have

(4.14)

\frac{\widehat{W}^{(n)}(t_{2})}{n}-\frac{\widehat{W}^{(n)}(t_{1})}{n}=\int_{t_{1}}^{t_{2}}\left(\frac{\alpha\omega\widehat{S}^{(n)}(u)}{\lambda n}-\frac{2\widehat{W}^{(n)}(u)}{\widehat{S}^{(n)}(u)}\right)\mathrm{d}u+\frac{M^{(n)}_{2}(t_{2})-M^{(n)}_{2}(t_{1})}{n}.

We define the event

(4.15)

\Omega_{\ref{s>ep}}:=\{\widehat{S}^{n}(t)\geq\epsilon n/2,\ \forall t\leq(\widehat{s}(0)-\epsilon)\wedge\widehat{\tau}^{(n)}\}\cap\{\sup_{t\leq\widehat{\tau}^{(n)}\wedge 2}\left|M^{(n)}_{2}(t)\right|\leq n^{2/3}\}.

By (
4.6
) and (
4.10
),
\mathbb{P}(\Omega_{\ref{s>ep}})\to 1
as
n\to\infty
. On
\Omega_{\ref{s>ep}}
, for
t_{1}<t_{2}<\widehat{s}(0)-\epsilon
, using (
4.14
),

\begin{split}&\left|\frac{\widehat{W}^{(n)}(t_{2})}{n}-\frac{\widehat{W}^{(n)}(t_{1})}{n}\right|\\
\leq&(t_{2}-t_{1})\left(\frac{\alpha\omega}{\lambda}+\frac{2\mu n}{\epsilon n/2}\right)+\frac{2}{n}\sup_{t\leq\widehat{\tau}^{(n)}\wedge 1}\left|M^{(n)}_{2}(t)\right|\\
\leq&C\left((t_{2}-t_{1})+n^{-1/3}\right).\end{split}

This proves (
4.13
) and thus also completes the proof of (
4.12
).
∎

4.1.3.
Proof of the convergence of
\widehat{W}^{(n)}(t)/n
to
\widehat{w}(t)
for all
t\leq\widehat{s}(0)\wedge\widehat{\tau}^{(n)}

Having proved (
4.12
), we now use it to show the original statement (
1.15
). In other words, given any
\epsilon^{\prime}>0
, we need to show

(4.16)

\lim_{n\to\infty}\mathbb{P}\left(\sup_{0\leq t\leq\widehat{s}(0)\wedge\widehat{\tau}^{(n)}}\left|\frac{\widehat{W}^{(n)}(t)}{n}-\widehat{w}(t)\right|>\epsilon^{\prime}\right)=0.

Denote the oscillation of a function
h(t)
in any given interval
[a,b]
by

(4.17)

\mbox{Osc}\left[h(t),[a,b]\right]:=\sup_{a\leq u\leq v\leq b}\left|h(u)-h(v)\right|.

Observe that

(4.18)

\begin{split}&\sup_{0\leq t\leq\widehat{s}(0)\wedge\widehat{\tau}^{(n)}}\left|\frac{\widehat{W}^{(n)}(t)}{n}-\widehat{w}(t)\right|\\
\leq&\sup_{0\leq t\leq(\widehat{s}(0)-\epsilon)\wedge\widehat{\tau}^{(n)}}\left|\frac{\widehat{W}^{(n)}(t)}{n}-\widehat{w}(t)\right|+\mbox{Osc}\left[\frac{\widehat{W}^{(n)}(t)}{n},[(\widehat{s}(0)-\epsilon)\wedge\widehat{\tau}^{(n)},\widehat{s}(0)\wedge\widehat{\tau}^{(n)}]\right]\\
&+\mbox{Osc}[\widehat{w}(t),[\widehat{s}(0)-\epsilon,\widehat{s}(0)]]\\
:=&I_{1}^{(n)}+I_{2}^{(n)}+I_{3}^{(n)}.\end{split}

Fix any
\epsilon^{\prime}>0
, we need to find
\epsilon
so that

(4.19)

\lim_{n\to\infty}\mathbb{P}(I_{1}^{(n)}>\epsilon^{\prime}/3)=\lim_{n\to\infty}\mathbb{P}(I_{2}^{(n)}>\epsilon^{\prime}/3)=0\mbox{ and }I_{3}^{(n)}\leq\epsilon^{\prime}/3.

Once (
4.19
) is proved, (
4.16
) follows from the union bound.

Equation (
4.12
) already implies that
\mathbb{P}(I_{1}^{(n)}>\epsilon^{\prime}/3)\to 0
for any fixed
\epsilon
and
\epsilon^{\prime}
, so we only need to estimate
I_{2}^{(n)}
and
I_{3}^{(n)}
. From (
1.13
) we have the explicit expression for
\widehat{w}(t)
:

\widehat{w}(t)=\frac{\omega\alpha}{\lambda}(\widehat{s}(0)-t)^{2}\log\frac{\widehat{s}(0)}{\widehat{s}(0)-t}.

We can compute its derivative

\widehat{w}^{\prime}(t)=\frac{\omega\alpha}{\lambda}\left(2(t-\widehat{s}(0))\log\frac{\widehat{s}(0)}{\widehat{s}(0)-t}+(\widehat{s}(0)-t)\right).

From this we see that the derivative of
\widehat{w}(t)
is uniformly bounded.

\sup_{0\leq t\leq\widehat{s}(0)}\left|\widehat{w}^{\prime}(t)\right|\leq C.

It follows that

I_{3}^{(n)}=\mbox{Osc}[\widehat{w},[\widehat{s}(0)-\epsilon,\widehat{s}(0)]]\leq C\epsilon.

Also note that

\widehat{w}(\widehat{s}(0)-\epsilon)=\frac{\omega\alpha}{\lambda}\epsilon^{2}\log(\widehat{s}(0)/\epsilon)\leq C\epsilon.

Hence, by choosing
\epsilon
small enough we can make

(4.20)

I_{3}^{(n)}\leq\epsilon^{\prime}/3\mbox{ and }\widehat{w}(\widehat{s}(0)-\epsilon)\leq\epsilon^{\prime}/3.

It remains to control
I_{2}^{(n)}
. Note that

\displaystyle I_{2}^{(n)}\left\{\begin{array}[]{l}=0,\ \text{ if }\ \widehat{\tau}^{(n)}<\widehat{s}(0)-\epsilon;\\
\leq\sup_{\widehat{s}(0)-\epsilon\leq t\leq\widehat{s}(0)\wedge\widehat{\tau}^{(n)}}\widehat{W}^{(n)}(t)/n,\ \text{ if }\ \widehat{\tau}^{(n)}\geq\widehat{s}(0)-\epsilon.\end{array}\right.

Define the event

(4.21)

\Omega_{\ref{ws0-ep}}=\{\widehat{\tau}^{(n)}<\widehat{s}(0)-\epsilon\}\cup\{\widehat{\tau}^{(n)}\geq\widehat{s}(0)-\epsilon,\widehat{W}(\widehat{s}(0)-\epsilon)>\epsilon^{\prime}n/12\}.

By (
4.12
),

\lim_{n\to\infty}\mathbb{P}\left(\Omega_{\ref{ws0-ep}}\right)=1.

By (
4.14
), on
\Omega_{\ref{s>ep}}\cap\Omega_{\ref{ws0-ep}}\cap\{\widehat{\tau}^{(n)}\geq\widehat{s}(0)-\epsilon\}
, for all
\widehat{s}(0)-\epsilon\leq t\leq\widehat{s}(0)\wedge\widehat{\tau}^{(n)}
,

(4.22)

\begin{split}\frac{\widehat{W}^{(n)}(t)}{n}&\leq\frac{\widehat{W}^{(n)}(\widehat{s}(0)-\epsilon)}{n}+\frac{\alpha\omega}{\lambda}\epsilon+\frac{2\sup_{t}\left|M^{(n)}_{2}(t)\right|}{n}\\
&\leq\frac{\epsilon^{\prime}}{12}+\frac{\alpha\omega}{\lambda}\epsilon+\frac{2n^{2/3}}{n},\end{split}

which is smaller than
\epsilon^{\prime}/3
for
\epsilon
small enough and
n
large enough.

Consequently, on
\Omega_{\ref{s>ep}}\cap\Omega_{\ref{ws0-ep}}
,

I_{2}\leq\epsilon^{\prime}/3
. It follows that

(4.23)

\lim_{n\to\infty}\mathbb{P}(I_{2}^{(n)}>\epsilon^{\prime}/3)\leq\lim_{n\to\infty}\mathbb{P}\left(\left(\Omega_{\ref{s>ep}}\cap\Omega_{\ref{ws0-ep}}\right)^{c}\right)=0,

since both
\mathbb{P}(\Omega_{\ref{s>ep}})
and
\mathbb{P}(\Omega_{\ref{ws0-ep}})
tend to 1 as
n\to\infty
. Equation (
4.19
) now follows from (
4.20
) and (
4.23
). This completes the proof of (
1.15
).

4.2.
Proof of (
1.16
) for positive initial condition

4.2.1.
Analysis of the evolution equations for
\widehat{I}^{(n)}
and
\widehat{I}^{(n)}_{E}
.

We first analyze
\widehat{I}^{(n)}(t)
.

•

Change of
\widehat{I}^{(n)}(t)
due to recovery Poisson processes is
-1
. The rate for such an event to occur is

\gamma\widehat{I}^{(n)}(t)\times\frac{n}{\lambda\widehat{I}^{(n)}_{E}(t)}=\frac{\gamma\widehat{I}^{(n)}(t)n}{\lambda\widehat{I}^{(n)}_{E}(t)}.

•

Change of
\widehat{I}^{(n)}(t)
due to rewiring/dropping Poisson processes is 0.

•

Change of
\widehat{I}^{(n)}(t)
due to infection Poisson processes is 1. The rate for such an event to occur is

\lambda\widehat{I}^{(n)}_{E}(t)\times\frac{n}{\lambda\widehat{I}^{(n)}_{E}(t)}=n.

By Dynkin’s formula again, we can decompose
\widehat{I}^{(n)}(t)
as follows:

(4.24)

\widehat{I}^{(n)}(t)=\widehat{I}^{(n)}(0)+\int_{0}^{t}\left(-\frac{\gamma\widehat{I}^{(n)}(t)n}{\lambda\widehat{I}^{(n)}_{E}(t)}+n\right)\mathrm{d}u+M^{(n)}_{3}(t).

The quadratic variation of the martingale
M^{(n)}_{3}(t)
can be bounded in the similar way to
M^{(n)}_{1}(t)
:

\langle M^{(n)}_{3}(t),M^{(n)}_{3}(t)\rangle=\langle\widehat{I}^{(n)}(t),\widehat{I}^{(n)}(t)\rangle\leq\sum_{i=1}^{n}1^{2}+\sum_{i=1}^{n}1^{2}=2n.

Similarly to (
4.4
),

(4.25)

\mathbb{P}\left(\sup_{t\leq\widehat{\tau}^{(n)}}\left|M^{(n)}_{3}(t)\right|>n^{2/3}\right)\leq\frac{8n}{n^{4/3}}\leq 8n^{-1/3}.

Now we turn to
\widehat{I}^{(n)}_{E}(t)
. Let
\widehat{I}^{(n)}_{E}(i,t)
be the infected edges of vertex
i
at time
t
. If
i
is not infected at time
t
then
\widehat{I}^{(n)}_{E}(i,t)=0
.
We can classify the change of
\widehat{I}^{(n)}_{E}(t)
as follows.

•

Change of
\widehat{I}^{(n)}_{E}(t)
due to recovery Poisson processes is
-\widehat{I}^{(n)}_{E}(i,t)
if vertex
i
recovers. The total rate of change is equal to

-\frac{n}{\lambda\widehat{I}^{(n)}_{E}(t)}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\gamma\widehat{I}^{(n)}_{E}(i,t)=-\frac{n\gamma}{\lambda}.

•

Change of
\widehat{I}^{(n)}_{E}(t)
due to rewiring/dropping Poisson processes is
-1
with probability
1-\alpha+\alpha(1-\widehat{I}^{(n)}(t)/n)
, and 0 with probability
\alpha\widehat{I}^{(n)}(t)/n
.
Thus, the mean of the change is
-(1-\alpha+\alpha(1-\widehat{I}^{(n)}(t)/n))
. The rate for such an event to occur is

\omega\widehat{I}^{(n)}_{E}(t)\times\frac{n}{\lambda\widehat{I}^{(n)}_{E}(t)}=\frac{n\omega}{\lambda}.

•

Change of
\widehat{I}^{(n)}_{E}(t)
due to Infection Poisson processes is equal to

-1+\mbox{Poisson}\left(\frac{(\widehat{S}^{(n)}(t)-1)\mu}{n}\right)+\mbox{Binomial}\left(\widehat{W}^{(n)}(t),2/\widehat{S}^{(n)}(t)\right)-\mbox{Binomial}\left(\widehat{I}^{(n)}_{E}(t)-1,\frac{1}{\widehat{S}^{(n)}(t)}\right).

To see this, recall the description of the Infection Poisson Processes in the third bullet of the construction of the SIR-
\omega
model in Section
2
. The first term ‘-1’ is because each infection will cost an infected edge to infect the susceptible vertex. The appearance of the second and third terms come from (
2.1
). The last term is due to that each infected edge is deleted with probability
1/\widehat{S}^{(n)}(t)
in each infection event.

The mean of the change is

-1+\frac{(\widehat{S}^{(n)}(t)-1)\mu}{n}+\frac{2\widehat{W}^{(n)}(t)}{\widehat{S}^{(n)}(t)}-\frac{\widehat{I}^{(n)}_{E}(t)-1}{\widehat{S}^{(n)}(t)}.

The rate for such an event to occur is

\lambda\widehat{I}^{(n)}_{E}(t)\times\frac{n}{\lambda\widehat{I}^{(n)}_{E}(t)}=n.

We now decompose
\widehat{I}^{(n)}_{E}(t)
into the drift part and martingale part.

(4.26)

\begin{split}\widehat{I}^{(n)}_{E}(t)=&\widehat{I}^{(n)}_{E}(0)+\int_{0}^{t}\left(-\frac{n\gamma}{\lambda}\right)\mathrm{d}u+\int_{0}^{t}\frac{n\omega}{\lambda}\left(-\left(1-\alpha+\alpha\left(1-\frac{\widehat{I}^{(n)}(u)}{n}\right)\right)\right)\mathrm{d}u\\
&+\int_{0}^{t}n\left(-1+\frac{(\widehat{S}^{(n)}(u)-1)\mu}{n}+\frac{2\widehat{W}^{(n)}(u)}{\widehat{S}^{(n)}(u)}-\frac{\widehat{I}^{(n)}_{E}(u)-1}{\widehat{S}^{(n)}(u)}\right)\mathrm{d}u+M^{(n)}_{4}(t).\end{split}

Similarly to the analysis for the martingales
M^{(n)}_{1},M^{(n)}_{2},M^{(n)}_{3}
, we can show that

(4.27)

\mathbb{P}\left(\sup_{t\leq\widehat{\tau}^{(n)}\wedge((\widehat{s}(0)+t_{*})/2)}\left|M^{(n)}_{4}(t)\right|>n^{2/3}\right)\leq C^{\prime}n^{-1/3}.

We deferred the detailed proof of (
4.27
) to Appendix
A
.

4.2.2.

1.16
) for

and
\widehat{I}^{(n)}_{E}

For any
\delta>0
, we can define
\widehat{\tau}^{(n)}_{\delta}

Let

In order to prove (

Lemma 4.1

Proof of Lemma

On the other hand, we claim that the event

\begin{split}\lim_{n\to\infty}\mathbb{P}\left(\widehat{\tau}^{(n)}_{\delta}\leq t_{*}-\epsilon\right)=\lim_{n\to\infty}\mathbb{P}\left(\widehat{\tau}^{(n)}_{\delta}\leq t_{*}-\epsilon,\ \left|\frac{\widehat{I}^{(n)}_{E}(\widehat{\tau}^{(n)}_{\delta})}{n}-{\widehat{i}_{E}(\widehat{\tau}^{(n)}_{\delta})}\right|\geq\frac{a}{2}\right)=0,\end{split}

\left|F^{\prime}(t)\right|
are uniformly bounded for all
\lambda
in a neighborhood of
\lambda_{c}
and
t\leq t_{*}
, there exists a constant
C
such that
\widehat{i}_{E}(t)\leq Ct
and
F(t)\leq F(0)+Ct
. Using this fact together with (
6.1
) and (
6.2
), we see that for some constant
C_{\ref{ie1}}
,

(6.3)

\begin{split}\widehat{i}_{E}(t)&\leq\int_{0}^{t}F(u)\mathrm{d}u+\int_{0}^{t}\frac{\omega\alpha}{\lambda}\widehat{i}(u)\mathrm{d}u,\\
&\leq\int_{0}^{t}(F(0)+Cu)\mathrm{d}u+\frac{\omega\alpha}{\lambda}\int_{0}^{t}\frac{\widehat{i}(u)}{\widehat{i}_{E}(u)}Cu\mathrm{d}u,\\
&\leq F(0)t+C_{\ref{ie1}}t^{2}.\end{split}

Using the expression of
F(t)
and (
5.19
),

(6.4)

\begin{split}F(0)&=-\frac{1+\gamma+\omega}{\lambda}+\mu,\\
F^{\prime}(0)&=-\mu+2\frac{\omega\alpha}{\lambda},\\
F^{\prime\prime}(t)&=\frac{-2\omega\alpha}{\lambda(1-t)}.\end{split}

Note that
F(0)\to 0
as
\lambda\searrow\lambda_{c}
, while
F^{\prime\prime}(t)
is bounded from above by
-2\omega\alpha/\lambda
.

We now divide the proof of Theorem
1.1
into two cases.

6.1.
Proof of Case ①

Suppose either

\omega(2\alpha-1)\leq\gamma
or

\omega(2\alpha-1)>\gamma\mbox{ and }\mu<2\omega\alpha/(\omega(2\alpha-1)-\gamma).

Then by (
5.20
),

-\mu+\frac{2\omega\alpha}{\lambda_{c}}=-\mu+\frac{2\omega\alpha(\mu-1)}{\gamma+\omega}<0.

By (
6.4
) and (
5.20
),

\lim_{\lambda\to\lambda_{c}}F^{\prime}(0)=-\mu+\frac{2\omega\alpha}{\lambda_{c}}<0.

Hence, there exists
\epsilon_{0},\delta_{0}>0
, such that
for
\lambda-\lambda_{c}<\epsilon_{0}
and
t<\delta_{0}
,

(6.5)

F(t)\leq F(0)-C_{\ref{f't6}}t,

for some
C_{\ref{f't6}}>0
.
By (
6.2
), (
6.3
) and (
6.5
), for
t\leq\delta_{0}
,

\begin{split}\widehat{i}_{E}(t)&\leq\int_{0}^{t}F(u)\mathrm{d}u+\int_{0}^{t}\frac{\omega\alpha}{\lambda}\widehat{i}(u)\mathrm{d}u\\
&\leq\int_{0}^{t}(F(0)-C_{\ref{f't6}}u)\mathrm{d}u+\frac{\omega\alpha}{\lambda}\int_{0}^{t}\frac{\widehat{i}(u)}{\widehat{i}_{E}(u)}(F(0)u+C_{\ref{ie1}}u^{2})\mathrm{d}u\\
&\leq F(0)t-\frac{C_{\ref{f't6}}}{2}t^{2}+\frac{\omega\alpha}{\lambda}(F(0)t+C_{\ref{ie1}}t^{2})\frac{\lambda}{\gamma}t\\
&=F(0)t-\left(\frac{C_{\ref{f't6}}}{2}-\frac{\omega\alpha}{\gamma}F(0)\right)t^{2}+\frac{\omega\alpha}{\gamma}t^{3},\end{split}

where we have used
\widehat{i}(u)=(\widehat{i}(u)/\widehat{i}_{E}(u))\widehat{i}_{E}(u)
in the second inequality.
Note that

\lim_{\lambda\searrow\lambda_{c}}F(0)=0\mbox{ and }\lim_{\lambda\to\lambda_{c}}\left(\frac{C_{\ref{f't6}}}{2}-\frac{\omega\alpha}{\gamma}F(0)\right)=\frac{C_{\ref{f't6}}}{2}>0.

Consequently, there exist
\delta_{1}<\delta_{0}
and
\epsilon_{1}<\epsilon_{0}
such that for
t\leq\delta_{1}
and
\lambda<\lambda_{c}+\epsilon_{1}
,

\widehat{i}_{E}(t)\leq F(0)t-\left(\frac{C_{\ref{f't6}}}{2}-\frac{\omega\alpha}{\gamma}F(0)\right)t^{2}+\frac{\omega\alpha}{\gamma}t^{3}\leq F(0)t-\frac{C_{\ref{f't6}}}{4}t^{2}.

From this we obtain that

t_{*}=\inf\{0<t\leq\widehat{s}(0):\widehat{i}_{E}(t)=0\}\leq\frac{4F(0)}{C_{\ref{f't6}}},

which converges to 0 as
\lambda\searrow\lambda_{c}
since
F(0)\to 0
.

6.2.
Proof of Case ②

Suppose
\omega(2\alpha-1)>\gamma
and
\mu=2\omega\alpha/(\omega(2\alpha-1)-\gamma)
. Note that
\alpha
must be positive in this case. By (
6.4
) and (
5.20
), we have

F^{\prime}(0)<0\mbox{ and }\lim_{\lambda\to\lambda_{c}}F^{\prime}(0)=-\mu+\frac{2\omega\alpha}{\lambda_{c}}=0.

Thus, by the third equation in (
6.4
),

F(t)\leq F(0)-\frac{\omega\alpha}{\lambda}t^{2}.

Using (
6.3
), for some constant
C_{\ref{iebd6}}
(independent of
\lambda-\lambda_{c}
),

(6.6)

\begin{split}\widehat{i}_{E}(t)&\leq\int_{0}^{t}F(u)\mathrm{d}u+\int_{0}^{t}\frac{\omega\alpha}{\lambda}\widehat{i}(u)\mathrm{d}u\\
&\leq\int_{0}^{t}\left(F(0)-\frac{\omega\alpha}{\lambda}u^{2}\right)\mathrm{d}u+\frac{\omega\alpha}{\lambda}\int_{0}^{t}\frac{\widehat{i}(u)}{\widehat{i}_{E}(u)}(F(0)u+C_{\ref{ie1}}u^{2})\mathrm{d}u\\
&\leq F(0)t+C_{\ref{iebd6}}(F(0)t^{2}+t^{3}),\end{split}

where we have again used
\widehat{i}(u)=(\widehat{i}(u)/\widehat{i}_{E}(u))\widehat{i}_{E}(u)
in the second inequality. Substituting this bound into (
6.6
) again, we see that

\begin{split}\widehat{i}_{E}(t)&\leq\int_{0}^{t}\left(F(0)-\frac{\omega\alpha}{\lambda}u^{2}\right)\mathrm{d}u+\frac{\omega\alpha}{\gamma}t\left(F(0)t+C_{\ref{iebd6}}(F(0)t^{2}+t^{3})\right)\\
&=F(0)t+\frac{\omega\alpha}{\gamma}F(0)t^{2}+\left(-\frac{\omega\alpha}{3\lambda}+\frac{\omega\alpha}{\gamma}C_{\ref{iebd6}}F(0)\right)t^{3}+\frac{\omega\alpha}{\gamma}C_{\ref{iebd6}}t^{4}.\end{split}

Since

\lim_{\lambda\searrow\lambda_{c}}F(0)=0\mbox{ and }\lim_{\lambda\searrow\lambda_{c}}\left(-\frac{\omega\alpha}{3\lambda}+\frac{\omega\alpha}{\lambda}C_{\ref{iebd6}}F(0)\right)=-\frac{\omega\alpha}{3\lambda_{c}}<0,

we see that there exist
\epsilon_{2}>0
and
\delta_{2}>0
such that for
\lambda<\lambda_{c}+\epsilon_{2}
and
t\leq\delta_{2}
,

(6.7)

\widehat{i}_{E}(t)\leq 2F(0)t-\frac{\omega\alpha}{6\lambda_{c}}t^{3}.

It follows from (
6.7
) that

t_{*}=\inf\{0<t\leq\widehat{s}(0):\widehat{i}_{E}(t)=0\}\leq\sqrt{\frac{12F(0)\lambda_{c}}{\omega\alpha}},

which converges to 0 as
\lambda\searrow\lambda_{c}
since
F(0)\to 0
.

Appendix A
Proof of (
4.27
)

Let
N_{i}
be the number of edges added to vertex
i
when
i
first becomes infected and
\widehat{N}_{i}
the number of infected edges of
i
just before it becomes recovered. The quadratic variation of
M^{(n)}_{4}(t)
can be bounded by

(A.1)

\langle M^{(n)}_{4}(t),M^{(n)}_{4}(t)\rangle=\langle\widehat{I}^{(n)}_{E}(t),\widehat{I}^{(n)}_{E}(t)\rangle\leq\sum_{i=1}^{n}(\widehat{N}_{i}^{2}+N_{i}^{2})+Q(t)+\sum_{j=\widehat{S}^{(n)}(t)}^{n}Z_{j}^{2},

where
Q(t)
is the number of arrivals of rewiring/dropping Poisson processes by time
t
, and
Z_{j}
is distributed as 1+Binomial(
2\mu n
,
1/j
). In addition,
Z_{j},1\leq j\leq n
are independent. By (
4.8
),

(A.2)

\mathbb{E}(Q(t))\leq\frac{\gamma nt}{\lambda}.

By Lemma
2.4
, for some constant
C>0
,

(A.3)

\mathbb{E}\left(\sum_{i=1}^{n}(\widehat{N}_{i}^{2}+{N}_{i}^{2})\right)\leq Cn.

Define the event

(A.4)

\Omega_{\ref{m100}}=\left\{\sup_{t\leq\hat{\tau}}\left|M^{(n)}_{1}(t)\right|\leq(\widehat{s}(0)-t_{*})n/4\right\}.

By (
4.2
) and the Burkholder-Davis-Gundy inequality (see, e.g.,
(
Klebaner 2012
, Theorem 7.34)
with
p=6
), we see that

\mathbb{E}\left(\sup_{t\leq\widehat{\tau}^{(n)}}M^{(n)}_{1}(t)^{6}\right)\leq Cn^{3},

and consequently,

(A.5)

\mathbb{P}(\Omega_{\ref{m100}}^{c})\leq\frac{Cn^{3}}{((\widehat{s}(0)-t_{*})n/4)^{6}}\leq\frac{C^{\prime}}{n^{4}}.

On the event
\Omega_{\ref{m100}}
, by (
4.1
) and (
A.5
),

\inf_{t\leq\widehat{\tau}^{(n)}\wedge((\widehat{s}(0)+t_{*})/2)}\widehat{S}^{(n)}(t)\geq\left(\widehat{s}(0)-\frac{t_{*}+\widehat{s}(0)}{2}\right)n-\frac{\widehat{s}(0)-t_{*}}{4}n\geq\frac{\widehat{s}(0)-t_{*}}{4}n.

Define

(A.6)

Z=\sum_{j=1}^{n}Z_{j}^{2}.

We have

(A.7)

\sum_{j=\widehat{S}^{(n)}(t)}^{n}Z_{j}^{2}\leq Z\mathbf{1}_{\Omega^{c}_{\ref{m100}}}+\sum_{j=(\widehat{s}(0)-t_{*})n/4}^{n}Z_{j}^{2}.

It follows that

(A.8)

\mathbb{E}\left(\sum_{j=\widehat{S}^{(n)}(t)}^{n}Z_{j}^{2}\right)\leq\mathbb{E}\left(Z\mathbf{1}_{\Omega^{c}_{\ref{m100}}}\right)+\mathbb{E}\left(\sum_{j=(\widehat{s}(0)-t_{*})n/4}^{n}Z_{j}^{2}\right).

Observe that the second moment of Binomial(
2\mu n
,
1/j
) is bounded by

(A.9)

\frac{2\mu n}{j}+\left(\frac{2\mu n}{j}\right)^{2}\leq\frac{Cn^{2}}{j^{2}}.

Thus,

(A.10)

\mathbb{E}\left(\sum_{j=(\widehat{s}(0)-t_{*})n/4}^{n}Z_{j}^{2}\right)\leq C\sum_{j=(\widehat{s}(0)-t_{*})n/4}^{n}\frac{n^{2}}{j^{2}}\leq C^{\prime}n.

To control
Z
, similarly to (
A.9
), the fourth moment of Binomial(
2\mu n
,
1/j
) can be bounded by
C(n/j)^{4}
. Therefore,

\mathbb{E}(Z^{2})\leq Cn\mathbb{E}\left(\sum_{j=1}^{n}\frac{n^{4}}{j^{4}}\right)\leq C^{\prime}n^{5}.

By Cauchy-Schwartz inequality, we get

(A.11)

\mathbb{E}\left(Z\mathbf{1}_{\Omega^{c}_{\ref{m100}}}\right)\leq\sqrt{\mathbb{E}(Z^{2})\mathbb{P}(\Omega^{c}_{\ref{m100}})}\leq C\sqrt{n^{5}\cdot n^{-3}}=Cn.

Combining (
A.8
), (
A.10
) and (
A.11
), we see that

(A.12)

\mathbb{E}\left(\sum_{j=\widehat{S}^{(n)}(t)}^{n}Z_{j}^{2}\right)\leq Cn.

Equation (
4.27
) now follows from (
A.1
), (
A.2
), (
A.3
), (
A.12
) and
L^{2}
maximal inequality for martingales.
