A homogeneous Rayleigh quotient with applications in gradient methods Journal: -
Giulia Ferrandi, Michiel E. Hochstenbach

Abstract
Given an approximate eigenvector, its (standard) Rayleigh quotient and harmonic Rayleigh quotient are two well-known approximations of the corresponding eigenvalue. We propose a new type of Rayleigh quotient, the homogeneous Rayleigh quotient, and analyze its sensitivity with respect to perturbations in the eigenvector. Furthermore, we study the inverse of this homogeneous Rayleigh quotient as stepsize for the gradient method for unconstrained optimization. The notion and basic properties are also extended to the generalized eigenvalue problem.

1
Introduction

Let
A
be an
n\times n
symmetric matrix with eigenvalues
\lambda_{1}\leq\cdots\leq\lambda_{n}
. We are interested in the eigenproblem
A\mathbf{x}=\lambda\mathbf{x}
.
Let
\mathbf{u}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
be an approximate eigenvector to
\mathbf{x}
with unit 2-norm.
Traditionally, the Rayleigh quotient

\theta=\frac{\mathbf{u}^{T}\!A\mathbf{u}}{\mathbf{u}^{T}\mathbf{u}}\qquad(=\mathbf{u}^{T}\!A\mathbf{u})

(1)

is the standard approach to determine an eigenvalue approximation corresponding to
\mathbf{u}
, which yields the corresponding eigenvalue when
\mathbf{u}
is an exact eigenvector.
This
\theta
satisfies two (related) optimality properties, a Galerkin condition (cf., e.g.,
[
1
, pp. 13–14]
)

A\mathbf{u}-\theta\mathbf{u}\perp\mathbf{u},

(2)

and a minimum residual condition

\theta=\argmin_{\gamma}\,\|A\mathbf{u}-\gamma\,\mathbf{u}\|.

(3)

A second well-known quantity is the harmonic Rayleigh quotient, which is, for instance, sometimes considered when one is interested in an eigenvalue near a given target
\tau\in\mathbb{R}
. This is defined as (cf., e.g.,
[
2
, p. 294]
)

\widetilde{\theta}_{\tau}=\frac{\mathbf{u}^{T}(A-\tau I)\,A\mathbf{u}}{\mathbf{u}^{T}(A-\tau I)\,\mathbf{u}},

(4)

provided that the denominator is nonzero.
This quotient is also equal to the eigenvalue when
\mathbf{u}
is an eigenvector.
It satisfies two optimality conditions: a Galerkin condition
(A-\widetilde{\theta}_{\tau}I)\,\mathbf{u}\perp(A-\tau I)\,\mathbf{u}
, and a minimum residual condition (or secant condition)
\widetilde{\theta}_{\tau}=\argmin_{\gamma}\|(\gamma-\tau)^{-1}(A-\tau I)\,\mathbf{u}-\mathbf{u}\|
(cf.
[
3
, Prop. 5]
).

In this paper, we mainly focus on the case
\tau=0
; for nonzero
\mathbf{u}^{T}\!A\mathbf{u}
, the harmonic Rayleigh quotient is given by (cf.
[
4
]
)

\widetilde{\theta}=\frac{\mathbf{u}^{T}\!A^{2}\,\mathbf{u}}{\mathbf{u}^{T}\!A\mathbf{u}}.

(5)

For
\tau=0
, the Galerkin condition becomes

A\mathbf{u}-\widetilde{\theta}\mathbf{u}\perp A\mathbf{u},

(6)

and the minimum residual condition is

\widetilde{\theta}=\argmin_{\gamma}\,\|\gamma^{-1}A\mathbf{u}-\mathbf{u}\|.

(7)

Condition (
7
) is easily verified by setting the derivative of
\|\gamma^{-1}A\mathbf{u}-\mathbf{u}\|
with respect to
\gamma
to zero.
Property (
7
) is perhaps less widely known.
Both Properties (
3
) and (
7
) have been exploited as secant conditions to determine a stepsize in gradient-type optimization methods; for instance for the well-known Barzilai and Borwein steplengths
[
5
]
. We return to this topic in Section
5
; see also Vömel
[
6
]
.

As an alternative to (
3
) and (
7
), we propose and study a new
homogeneous
type of Rayleigh quotient in this paper.

We work in the projective space
\mathbb{P}=\mathbb{P}_{1}(\mathbb{R})
, also called the
projective line
. This is a quotient space of
\mathbb{R}^{2}\setminus\{\bf 0\}
, with the following equivalence relation: two points
[\alpha_{1},\alpha_{2}]^{T}
,
[\widetilde{\alpha}_{1},\widetilde{\alpha}_{2}]^{T}\in\mathbb{R}^{2}\setminus\{\bf 0\}
are equivalent if there exists a nonzero
\zeta
such that
[\alpha_{1},\alpha_{2}]^{T}=\zeta\,[\widetilde{\alpha}_{1},\widetilde{\alpha}_{2}]^{T}
. The coordinates of the representative of a class
(\alpha_{1},\alpha_{2})
are called
homogeneous coordinates
.
The pair
(1,0)
corresponds to the point at infinity. All other equivalence classes
(\alpha_{2}\neq 0)
can be identified with a real number
\alpha=\alpha_{1}/\alpha_{2}
.

Homogeneous techniques for eigenvalue problems have already been developed by various authors.
Stewart and Sun
[
7
, p. 283]
exploit homogeneous coordinates for the generalized eigenvalue problem
A\mathbf{x}=\lambda B\,\mathbf{x}
, for
B\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times n}
, since this is “especially convenient for treating infinite eigenvalues”
[
8
]
.
While the standard eigenvalue problem
A\mathbf{x}=\lambda\mathbf{x}
does not involve infinite eigenvalues, the harmonic Rayleigh quotient (
5
) may be infinite.
Dedieu and Tisseur
[
9
,
10
]
exploit homogeneous techniques to study perturbation theory for generalized and polynomial eigenproblems.
Inspired by this, a homogeneous Jacobi–Davidson method for subspace expansion has been proposed in
[
11
]
; this may be viewed as an inexact Newton method.

The contributions and outline of this paper are the following. We define a new
homogeneous Rayleigh quotient
as the minimizer of the residual quantity

\min_{(\alpha_{1},\,\alpha_{2})\,\in\,\mathbb{P}}\ \frac{\|\alpha_{1}\,\mathbf{u}-\alpha_{2}\,A\mathbf{u}\|}{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\sqrt{\alpha_{1}^{2}+\alpha_{2}^{2}}}}.

In Section
2
, we show that there is a closed-form solution to express this homogeneous Rayleigh quotient, and that it can also be obtained from a certain
nonlinear
Galerkin condition.
To quantify the quality of the homogeneous Rayleigh quotient as an approximate eigenvalue, a bound for the (chordal) distance between the homogeneous Rayleigh quotient and the spectrum of
A
is derived.

We compare the homogeneous Rayleigh quotient with the standard and harmonic Rayleigh quotients. Given the relation between the homogeneous residual quantity and the two residual quantities (
3
) and (
7
) (see also Section
2.3
), the homogeneous Rayleigh quotient may be seen as the mediator between the two other Rayleigh quotients. In Section
3
, we highlight this fact in the theoretical and experimental study on the relative errors of the three quotients. Asymptotic bounds on the accuracy of the three quotients are also derived and compared. Interestingly, the homogeneous Rayleigh quotient may be more accurate than its two competitors in some situations.

In Section
4
, we extend the definition of the homogeneous Rayleigh quotient to the generalized eigenvalue problem
(A,B)
, with
A
symmetric and
B
symmetric positive definite (SPD).
Finally, in Section
5
, we propose a new stepsize for gradient methods based on the homogeneous Rayleigh quotient. Conclusions are drawn in Section
6
.

We would like to stress that the stepsize (
31
) presented in Section
5
has been recently proposed first by Li, Zhang, and Xia
[
12
]
(with preliminary version
[
13
]
in 2021).
The results in our Section
5
have been developed independently and the derivation is different: whereas the approach by Li, Zhang, and Xia is via a total least squares technique, ours is based on exploiting homogeneous coordinates.
We will discuss some more details in Section
5
.

2
A homogeneous Rayleigh quotient

For a given approximate eigenvector
\mathbf{u}
, we consider the minimization of the
homogeneous
residual (as mediator of the two residual quantities in (
3
) and (
7
)):

\min_{(\alpha_{1},\,\alpha_{2})\,\in\,\mathbb{P}}\ \frac{\|\alpha_{1}\,\mathbf{u}-\alpha_{2}\,A\mathbf{u}\|}{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\sqrt{\alpha_{1}^{2}+\alpha_{2}^{2}}}}=\min_{\alpha_{1}^{2}+\alpha_{2}^{2}=1}\ \|[\mathbf{u}\ \ -\!\!A\mathbf{u}]{\mbox{\scriptsize$\left[\!\!\begin{array}[]{c}\alpha_{1}\\
\alpha_{2}\end{array}\!\!\right]$}}\|.

(8)

Note that the expression on the left-hand side is well defined on
\mathbb{P}
. The equality between the two optimization problems can be seen by taking
(\alpha_{1},\alpha_{2})\leftarrow(\alpha_{1}^{2}+\alpha_{2}^{2})^{-1/2}\ (\alpha_{1},\alpha_{2})
as representative of the equivalence class. Then we minimize the objective function on
\{[\alpha_{1},\alpha_{2}]^{T}\in\mathbb{R}^{2}\mid\alpha_{1}^{2}+\alpha_{2}^{2}=1\}
.

For nonzero
\alpha_{2}
we call the ratio between the coordinates of the solution
\alpha=\alpha_{1}/\alpha_{2}
the
homogeneous Rayleigh quotient
.

2.1
Key properties of the homogeneous Rayleigh quotient

We discuss some properties of the solution to (
8
). First, let us introduce the
n\times 2
matrix
C=[\mathbf{u}\ \ \,-\!\!A\mathbf{u}]
and the associated
2\times 2
matrix

C^{T}C=\left[\begin{array}[]{cc}\phantom{-}\mathbf{u}^{T}\mathbf{u}&-\mathbf{u}^{T}\!A\mathbf{u}\\
-\mathbf{u}^{T}\!A\mathbf{u}&\phantom{-}\mathbf{u}^{T}\!A^{2}\mathbf{u}\end{array}\right]=\mathbf{u}^{T}\!A\mathbf{u}\cdot\left[\begin{array}[]{cc}\phantom{-}\theta^{-1}&-1\\
-1&\phantom{-}\widetilde{\theta}\end{array}\right],

(9)

where the second equality holds when
\mathbf{u}^{T}\!A\mathbf{u}
is nonzero.
Although we usually impose
\|\mathbf{u}\|=1
, we will not exploit this simplification in view of more general use in Section
5
.

In the next proposition, assuming that
\mathbf{u}^{T}\!A\mathbf{u}\neq 0
, we can provide an explicit formula for the homogeneous Rayleigh quotient
\alpha
. In addition, we show that
\alpha
is an eigenvalue of
A
when
\mathbf{u}
is an eigenvector, and that it is located between the Rayleigh quotient and the harmonic Rayleigh quotient, depending on the sign of
\mathbf{u}^{T}\!A\mathbf{u}
.

Proposition 1
.

Suppose that
\mathbf{u}^{T}\!A\mathbf{u}\neq 0
and denote the smallest eigenvalue of
C^{T}C
by
\mu
. Then the following properties hold.

(i)

The value
\mu
is a simple eigenvalue of
C^{T}C
, or, equivalently,
\sqrt{\mu}
is a simple singular value of
C
; its corresponding eigenvector
[\alpha_{1},\ \alpha_{2}]^{T}
is the unique minimizer of (
8
).

(ii)

The value of
\mu
is

\displaystyle\mu=\tfrac{1}{2}\,\big[\mathbf{u}^{T}\mathbf{u}+\mathbf{u}^{T}\!A^{2}\,\mathbf{u}-\sqrt{\smash[b]{(\mathbf{u}^{T}\mathbf{u}-\mathbf{u}^{T}\!A^{2}\,\mathbf{u})^{2}+4\,(\mathbf{u}^{T}\!A\mathbf{u})^{2}}}\ \big].

(10)

(iii)

For the homogeneous Rayleigh quotient
\alpha
we have

\displaystyle\alpha

\displaystyle=\frac{\alpha_{1}}{\alpha_{2}}=\frac{\mathbf{u}^{T}\!A\mathbf{u}}{\mathbf{u}^{T}\mathbf{u}-\mu}=\frac{\mathbf{u}^{T}\!A^{2}\,\mathbf{u}-\mu}{\mathbf{u}^{T}\!A\mathbf{u}}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2\ \mathbf{u}^{T}\!A\mathbf{u}}\ \big[\,\mathbf{u}^{T}\!A^{2}\,\mathbf{u}-\mathbf{u}^{T}\mathbf{u}+\sqrt{\smash[b]{(\mathbf{u}^{T}\!A^{2}\,\mathbf{u}-\mathbf{u}^{T}\mathbf{u})^{2}+4\,(\mathbf{u}^{T}\!A\mathbf{u})^{2}}}\,\big].

(11)

In terms of the standard and harmonic Rayleigh quotient,

\alpha=\tfrac{1}{2}\,\big[\,\widetilde{\theta}-\theta^{-1}+\sqrt{\smash[b]{(\widetilde{\theta}-\theta^{-1})^{2}+4}}\,\big]=\big(\tfrac{1}{2}\,\big[\,\theta^{-1}-\widetilde{\theta}+\sqrt{\smash[b]{(\theta^{-1}-\widetilde{\theta})^{2}+4}}\,\big]\big)^{-1}.

(iv)

\mu=0
if and only if
\mathbf{u}
is an eigenvector of
A
. If
\mathbf{u}
is an eigenvector, then the corresponding homogeneous Rayleigh quotient
\alpha
is the corresponding eigenvalue.

(v)

0\leq\mu<\min(\mathbf{u}^{T}\mathbf{u},\ \mathbf{u}^{T}\!A^{2}\mathbf{u})
.

(vi)

We have the following bounds:

\begin{cases}\theta\leq\alpha\leq\widetilde{\theta}&\text{if}\quad\mathbf{u}^{T}\!A\mathbf{u}>0,\\[1.42262pt]
\widetilde{\theta}\leq\alpha\leq\theta&\text{if}\quad\mathbf{u}^{T}\!A\mathbf{u}<0.\end{cases}

Proof.

It is well known that the minimizer of (
8
) is the eigenvector corresponding to the smallest eigenvalue of the matrix
C^{T}C
; or, equivalently, to the right singular vector of
C
corresponding to the smallest singular value.
We start with the eigenvalue problem

C^{T}C\,{\mbox{\scriptsize$\left[\!\!\begin{array}[]{c}\alpha_{1}\\
\alpha_{2}\end{array}\!\!\right]$}}=\mu{\mbox{\scriptsize$\left[\!\!\begin{array}[]{c}\alpha_{1}\\
\alpha_{2}\end{array}\!\!\right]$}}.

The eigenvalue
\mu
of (
9
) is simple because
\mathbf{u}^{T}\!A\mathbf{u}
is assumed nonzero, so that the discriminant of the quadratic characteristic polynomial is positive. In particular, this implies that the smallest eigenvector
[\alpha_{1},\ \alpha_{2}]^{T}
is well defined. The result in part (ii) is an explicit expression for the eigenvalue
\mu
. Its corresponding eigenvector
[\alpha_{1},\ \alpha_{2}]^{T}
is used in item (iii) to give an expression
of

the ratio
\alpha=\alpha_{1}/\alpha_{2}
, which gives the homogeneous Rayleigh quotient, that can also be written in terms of the standard and the harmonic Rayleigh quotients.

If
\mu=0
, this means that
C
has rank 1. This happens precisely when
\mathbf{u}
is an eigenvector. Moreover, if
\mathbf{u}
is an eigenvector corresponding to some eigenvalue
\lambda
, equations (
10
) and (
(iii)
) give
\mu=0
and
\alpha=\lambda
respectively. This concludes the proof of part (iv).

The nonnegativity of
\mu
directly follows from the fact that
C^{T}C
is positive semidefinite. Since
\mathbf{u}^{T}\!A\mathbf{u}\neq 0
we have
\sqrt{\smash[b]{(\mathbf{u}^{T}\mathbf{u}-\mathbf{u}^{T}\!A^{2}\,\mathbf{u})^{2}+4\,(\mathbf{u}^{T}\!A\mathbf{u})^{2}}}>|\mathbf{u}^{T}\mathbf{u}-\mathbf{u}^{T}\!A^{2}\,\mathbf{u}|
, and therefore
\mathbf{u}^{T}\mathbf{u}-\mu,\ \mathbf{u}^{T}\!A^{2}\mathbf{u}-\mu>0
.
It follows that
0\leq\mu<\min(\mathbf{u}^{T}\mathbf{u},\,\mathbf{u}^{T}\!A^{2}\mathbf{u})
. In particular, this shows that the sign of
\alpha
only depends on the quantity
\mathbf{u}^{T}\!A\mathbf{u}
. Given this fact, property (vi) is a direct consequence of part (v).
∎

In the exceptional case of
\mathbf{u}^{T}\!A\mathbf{u}=0
, the matrix
C^{T}C
is diagonal, with zero Rayleigh quotient and infinite harmonic Rayleigh quotient.
The homogeneous Rayleigh quotient
\alpha=(\alpha_{1},\alpha_{2})
is either zero (that is,
(0,1)
), or the point at infinity
(1,0)
.
The situation
\mathbf{u}^{T}\!A\mathbf{u}=0
cannot happen if
A
is definite, such as for quadratic optimization problems with an SPD Hessian; see also Section
5
.
Moreover,
\mathbf{u}^{T}\!A\mathbf{u}=0
may not be a very common situation in the context of eigenproblems; this usually means that we are approximating a zero eigenvalue or that
\mathbf{u}
is a poor approximate eigenvector.

Rayleigh quotients have some natural invariance properties. It is easy to check that the standard Rayleigh quotient is invariant under nonzero scalings of
\mathbf{u}
; it is invariant under nonzero scalings of
A
, and it shifts naturally with shifts of
A
.
The harmonic Rayleigh quotient (with target
\tau
) only satisfies the first property, but modified properties still hold, as follows.

Proposition 2
.

For any
\tau\in\mathbb{R}
, the following properties hold:

(i)

\widetilde{\theta}_{\tau}
is invariant under scaling of
\mathbf{u}
:
\widetilde{\theta}_{\tau}(\zeta\,\mathbf{u})=\widetilde{\theta}_{\tau}(\mathbf{u})
for
\zeta\neq 0
.

(ii)

For
\widetilde{\theta}_{\tau}
as function of scaling of
A
we have

\widetilde{\theta}_{\tau}(\zeta\,A)=\zeta\,\widetilde{\theta}_{\zeta^{-1}\,\tau}(A)
for
\zeta\neq 0
.

(iii)

For
\widetilde{\theta}_{\tau}
as function of shifts of
A
we have

\widetilde{\theta}_{\tau}(A-\eta I)=\widetilde{\theta}_{\tau+\eta}(A)-\eta
for
\zeta\in\mathbb{R}
.

Proof.

This is easy to check from (
4
).
∎

As for the homogeneous Rayleigh quotient, it is easy to see (for instance, from (
(iii)
)) that the solution to the minimization problem (
8
) is invariant under nonzero scaling of
\mathbf{u}
: as function of
\mathbf{u}
, we have
\alpha(\zeta\,\mathbf{u})=\alpha(\mathbf{u})
for
\zeta\neq 0
.
Second, while the standard and harmonic Rayleigh quotients are invariant under multiplications
A\to\zeta A
, there is no easy relation between
\alpha(\zeta A)
and
\alpha(A)
. However, in view of Proposition
1
(vi), we establish these bounds:

\begin{cases}\zeta\,\theta(A)\leq\alpha(\zeta A)\leq\zeta\,\widetilde{\theta}(A)&\text{if}\quad\mathbf{u}^{T}\!A\mathbf{u}>0,\\[1.42262pt]
\zeta\,\widetilde{\theta}(A)\leq\alpha(\zeta A)\leq\zeta\,\theta(A)&\text{if}\quad\mathbf{u}^{T}\!A\mathbf{u}<0.\end{cases}

The same upper and lower bounds hold for
\zeta\alpha(A)
. Therefore, if the interval
[\zeta\theta(A),\,\zeta\widetilde{\theta}(A)]
(or
[\widetilde{\zeta}\theta(A),\,\zeta\theta(A)]
) is small, we conclude that
\alpha(\zeta A)\approx\zeta\alpha(A)
.

On the size of this interval, we note that when
\theta\leq\widetilde{\theta}
(which for instance holds in the SPD case), the “relative size” of the interval
[\theta,\,\widetilde{\theta}]
can be expressed in terms of the angle between
\mathbf{u}
and
A\mathbf{u}
, since

(\widetilde{\theta}-\theta)\,/\,\theta=\cos^{-2}(A\mathbf{u},\mathbf{u})-1=\tan^{2}(A\mathbf{u},\mathbf{u})
.
This means that if
\angle(A\mathbf{u},\mathbf{u})
is small, the interval is small, and all three Rayleigh quotients are close.
Still, Example
1
will show that one quotient can still be considerably more accurate than the other two.

Moreover, we have

\frac{\alpha-\theta}{\theta}=\tfrac{1}{2}\,\big[\,1+\tan^{2}(A\mathbf{u},\mathbf{u})-\theta^{-2}+\sqrt{\smash[b]{(1+\tan^{2}(A\mathbf{u},\mathbf{u})-\theta^{-2})^{2}+4\,\theta^{-2}}}\,-2\big].

For
\theta^{-1}\ll\tan(A\mathbf{u},\mathbf{u})
(that is, for large eigenvalues), this expression is close to
\tan^{2}(A\mathbf{u},\mathbf{u})
, which means that
\alpha\approx\widetilde{\theta}
.
In contrast, if
\theta^{-1}\gg\max(\tan(A\mathbf{u},\mathbf{u}),\,1)
(i.e., for small eigenvalues), one may check that
\frac{\alpha-\theta}{\theta}=\mathcal{O}(\theta^{2})\approx 0
, which implies that
\alpha\approx\theta
.
In conclusion, for a small
\angle(A\mathbf{u},\mathbf{u})
, the homogeneous Rayleigh quotient is close to the Rayleigh quotient for small eigenvalues, and close to the harmonic Rayleigh quotient for large ones.
Similar remarks can be made when
\widetilde{\theta}<\theta
.

Simple manipulations of Proposition
1
(vi) also lead to some relations between the relative errors of the three Rayleigh quotients, when the estimated eigenvalues are either
\lambda_{1}
or
\lambda_{n}
. If
A
is SPD, then both the standard and harmonic Rayleigh quotients lie in the spectrum of
A
, i.e.,
\theta,\,\widetilde{\theta}\in[\lambda_{1},\lambda_{n}]
. Thus the following inequalities hold:

\theta-\lambda_{1}\leq\alpha-\lambda_{1}\leq\widetilde{\theta}-\lambda_{1},\qquad\lambda_{n}-\widetilde{\theta}\leq\lambda_{n}-\alpha\leq\lambda_{n}-\theta.

(12)

We conclude that, in the SPD case, the Rayleigh quotient is more accurate when we estimate the smallest eigenvalue of
A
, while the harmonic Rayleigh quotient is more accurate for the largest eigenvalue. The homogeneous Rayleigh quotient lies in between.
This may suggest the perhaps not so well-known result that to approximate large eigenvalues, the harmonic Rayleigh quotient may be preferable over the standard Rayleigh quotient.

For indefinite matrices, while
\theta\in[\lambda_{1},\lambda_{n}]
, the harmonic and the homogeneous Rayleigh quotients can lie outside the spectrum. In particular, (
12
) does not hold if
A
is not SPD. Example
1
shows that the harmonic and homogeneous Rayleigh quotients may overestimate the largest eigenvalues, and the sensitivity of the homogeneous Rayleigh quotient may be smaller than the other two.

Example 1
.

Let
A=\text{diag}(-\frac{2}{3},\ \frac{1}{3},\ 2)
, and consider the eigenvector
\mathbf{x}=[0,\,0,\,1]^{T}
corresponding to
\lambda_{3}=2
. Given
\mathbf{u}=[-0.02,\ 0.01,\ 1]^{T}
as approximation to
\mathbf{x}
, with
\angle(\mathbf{u},\mathbf{x})\approx 2.2\cdot 10^{-2}
, we have that

|\theta(\mathbf{u})-\lambda_{3}|\approx 1.2\cdot 10^{-3},\qquad|\widetilde{\theta}(\mathbf{u})-\lambda_{3}|\approx 3.3\cdot 10^{-4},\qquad|\alpha(\mathbf{u})-\lambda_{3}|\approx 1.6\cdot 10^{-5}.

The quotients are
\theta(\mathbf{u})\approx 1.9988<\lambda_{3}
,
\widetilde{\theta}(\mathbf{u})\approx 2.0003>\lambda_{3}
and
\alpha(\mathbf{u})\approx 2.00002>\lambda_{3}
, so the harmonic and the homogeneous Rayleigh quotients overestimate
\lambda_{3}
. Nevertheless, the homogeneous Rayleigh quotient is an order of magnitude more accurate than the harmonic Rayleigh quotient, and two orders more accurate than the standard Rayleigh quotient.
This example highlights that the homogeneous Rayleigh quotient might be more accurate, especially for exterior eigenvalues of indefinite matrices. We will show another example in Figure
1
in Section
3
.

When the wanted eigenvalue is in the interior of the spectrum, or if
A
is indefinite, it is more difficult to derive general conclusions about the ordering of the relative errors. We will discuss this in more detail in Section
3
.

2.2
A Galerkin condition for the homogeneous Rayleigh quotient

In view of (
2
) and (
6
), the question arises whether there exists a Galerkin (orthogonality) condition based on the span of
\mathbf{u}
and
A\mathbf{u}
for the homogeneous approach. Let us first express the homogeneous Rayleigh quotient as a solution to a quadratic equation. As a direct consequence, this property connects the homogeneous Rayleigh quotient and the harmonic Rayleigh quotient with target.

Proposition 3
.

Let
\mathbf{u}^{T}\!A\mathbf{u}\neq 0
. The homogeneous Rayleigh quotient (
(iii)
) solves

(\mathbf{u}^{T}\!A\mathbf{u})\,\alpha^{2}+(\mathbf{u}^{T}\mathbf{u}-\mathbf{u}^{T}\!A^{2}\mathbf{u})\,\alpha-(\mathbf{u}^{T}\!A\mathbf{u})=0

(13)

and satisfies
\alpha\ \mathbf{u}^{T}\!A\mathbf{u}>0
. In addition, the homogeneous Rayleigh quotient is a harmonic Rayleigh quotient with target (
4
), where
\tau=-\alpha^{-1}
.

Proof.

It is straightforward to check that (
(iii)
) is a solution to (
13
). The solutions to (
13
) have opposite signs since their product is
-1
. Given that
0\leq\mu<\min(\mathbf{u}^{T}\mathbf{u},\,\mathbf{u}^{T}\!A^{2}\mathbf{u})
from Proposition
1
, the homogeneous Rayleigh quotient has the same sign as
\mathbf{u}^{T}\!A\mathbf{u}
, thus it corresponds to the solution to (
13
) for which
\alpha\ \mathbf{u}^{T}\!A\mathbf{u}>0
.
The second part follows immediately from the fact that imposing
\widetilde{\theta}_{-\alpha^{-1}}=\alpha
is equivalent to solving (
13
).
∎

We remark that the second solution of (
13
) is related to the smallest eigenvector of
C^{T}C
, which maximizes (rather than minimizes) the homogeneous residual quantity (
8
).

Another equivalent point of view on this proposition is the following.
Equation (
13
) can also be expressed as

\mathbf{u}^{T}(A+\alpha^{-1}I)\,(A-\alpha I)\,\mathbf{u}=0.

(14)

Therefore, we have the
nonlinear Galerkin condition

(A-\alpha I)\,\mathbf{u}\perp(A+\alpha^{-1}I)\,\mathbf{u}
, or, in homogeneous coordinates,

(\alpha_{2}A-\alpha_{1}I)\,\mathbf{u}\perp(\alpha_{1}A+\alpha_{2}I)\,\mathbf{u}.

This again highlights that
\alpha
can be viewed as a harmonic Rayleigh quotient with
-\alpha^{-1}
as target.

2.3
Properties of the homogeneous residual quantity

There is a connection between the two minimal residual conditions of the standard and harmonic Rayleigh quotients ((
3
) and (
7
)) and the quadratic equation (
13
).
The stationarity conditions for (
3
) and (
7
), respectively, can be stated as

\mathbf{u}^{T}\mathbf{u}-(\mathbf{u}^{T}\!A\mathbf{u})\,\gamma^{-1}=0,\qquad(\mathbf{u}^{T}\!A\mathbf{u})\,\gamma-\mathbf{u}^{T}\!A^{2}\mathbf{u}=0.

This means that (
14
) is a linear combination of the two stationarity conditions of the standard and the harmonic Rayleigh quotients. This fact, together with the bounds derived in Proposition
1
(vi), lets us interpret the homogeneous Rayleigh quotient as a “mediator” between the standard and the harmonic Rayleigh quotient.
Experiments in Section
3.3
will also highlight this mediating behavior.

It is an open question whether it is possible to relate the objective function (
8
) to a combination of the two residuals associated with the standard and the harmonic Rayleigh quotients. Nevertheless, it is possible to show that, when
\mathbf{u}^{T}\!A\mathbf{u}\neq 0
, the homogeneous residual quantity is smaller than the other two residual quantities (see (
3
) and (
7
)).
As remarked after Proposition
1
, this hypothesis excludes the points
(0,1)
and
(1,0)
from the admissible minimizers. Therefore, (
8
) is restricted to
(\alpha_{1},\alpha_{2})\in\mathbb{P}
, with
\alpha_{1},\alpha_{2}\neq 0
. Then we use either
(\gamma,1)
, or
(1,\gamma)
, for a nonzero
\gamma
, as class representatives. We derive the following minima, which are equivalent to (
8
) under
\mathbf{u}^{T}\!A\mathbf{u}\neq 0
:

\min_{\gamma\neq 0}\ \frac{1}{\sqrt{\gamma^{2}+1}}\,\|A\mathbf{u}-\gamma\mathbf{u}\|=\min_{\gamma\neq 0}\ \frac{|\gamma|}{\sqrt{\gamma^{2}+1}}\,\|\gamma^{-1}A\mathbf{u}-\mathbf{u}\|.

(15)

This means that the homogeneous residual quantity corresponds to the standard and harmonic residual quantities multiplied by a factor smaller than one.

From (
15
), we may also relate the homogeneous residual to a measure of distance between the homogeneous Rayleigh quotient and the spectrum of
A
.
As stated in
[
1
, Thm. 4.5.1]
and
[
6
, Thm. 3.7]
, for the standard Rayleigh quotient and the harmonic Rayleigh quotient there exists certain eigenvalues of
A
, say
\lambda
and
\widetilde{\lambda}
, such that

|\lambda-\theta|\leq\frac{\|(A-\theta\,I)\,\mathbf{u}\|}{\|\mathbf{u}\|},\qquad|\widetilde{\lambda}^{-1}-\widetilde{\theta}^{-1}|\leq\frac{\|(\widetilde{\theta}^{-1}I-A)\,\mathbf{u}\|}{\|A\mathbf{u}\|},

provided that
A\mathbf{u}\neq\mathbf{0}
in the second case.
These inequalities hold for any
\theta
and
\widetilde{\theta}
, but the Rayleigh quotients
\theta
and
\widetilde{\theta}
have the advantage of minimizing the corresponding residual quantities.
A similar result can be stated for the homogeneous Rayleigh quotient in terms of the chordal metric (see, e.g.,
[
7
, Ch. 6, Def. 1.20]
). By applying
[
1
, Thm. 4.5.1]
, we get the following result:
for any
\alpha
, there exists an eigenvalue
\lambda
of
A
such that

\frac{|\lambda-\alpha|}{\sqrt{1+\lambda^{2}}\ \sqrt{1+\alpha^{2}}}\leq\frac{1}{\sqrt{1+\lambda^{2}}\ \|\mathbf{u}\|}\cdot\frac{\|(A-\alpha\,I)\,\mathbf{u}\|}{\sqrt{1+\alpha^{2}}},

(16)

where the upper bound is minimized by the choice of the homogeneous Rayleigh quotient for
\alpha
.

Note that, as explained in
[
7
, Ch. 6]
, the chordal metric behaves counterintuitively for large eigenvalues: while the chordal metric is small, the relative error can still be large.
For a fair comparison of the accuracy of the three Rayleigh quotients, we consider asymptotic bounds on their relative error when the vector
\mathbf{u}
approximates an eigenvector in the next section.

3
Sensitivity analysis

We now study the sensitivity of the various Rayleigh quotients with respect to perturbations in the approximate eigenvector
\mathbf{u}
.
Note that this sensitivity is different from that expressed by the condition number
\kappa(\lambda)
of an eigenvalue, which is related to its perturbation as
a
function of changes in the matrix
A
. We note that for a simple eigenvalue
\lambda
of a symmetric
A
, it holds that
\kappa(\lambda)=1
; this means that the eigenvalue is perfectly conditioned (see, e.g.,
[
1
, p. 16]
).

Studying the sensitivity with respect to the approximate eigenvector for the standard and harmonic Rayleigh quotient is certainly not new (cf., e.g.,
[
14
,
15
]
and the references therein), but we derive a new result for the homogeneous Rayleigh quotient, and obtain expressions that allow an easy comparison of the three Rayleigh quotients. We will also comment on the differences between existing results and ours.

3.1
Rayleigh quotient and harmonic Rayleigh quotient

As ansatz, suppose
\mathbf{u}=\mathbf{x}+\mathbf{e}
is an approximate eigenvector, where
\|\mathbf{x}\|=1
,
\mathbf{e}\perp\mathbf{x}
, and
\varepsilon:=\|\mathbf{e}\|
is small.
Then the perturbation of the standard Rayleigh quotient is

\theta(\mathbf{u})=\frac{\mathbf{u}^{T}\!A\mathbf{u}}{\mathbf{u}^{T}\mathbf{u}}=\frac{\lambda+\mathbf{e}^{T}\!A\mathbf{e}}{1+\mathbf{e}^{T}\mathbf{e}}=\lambda\,(1+\mathbf{e}^{T}(\lambda^{-1}A-I)\,\mathbf{e})+\mathcal{O}(\varepsilon^{4}),

(17)

where we use the fact that
(1+t)^{-1}=1-t+\mathcal{O}(t^{2})
for
t\to 0
.
For the harmonic Rayleigh quotient we have

\displaystyle\widetilde{\theta}(\mathbf{u})=\frac{\mathbf{u}^{T}\!A^{2}\,\mathbf{u}}{\mathbf{u}^{T}\!A\mathbf{u}}

\displaystyle=\frac{\lambda^{2}+\mathbf{e}^{T}\!A^{2}\mathbf{e}}{\lambda+\mathbf{e}^{T}\!A\mathbf{e}}=\lambda\,(1+\lambda^{-2}\,\mathbf{e}^{T}\!A^{2}\mathbf{e})\,(1-\lambda^{-1}\,\mathbf{e}^{T}\!A\mathbf{e})+\mathcal{O}(\varepsilon^{4})

\displaystyle=\lambda\,(1+\mathbf{e}^{T}\lambda^{-1}A\,(\lambda^{-1}A-I)\,\mathbf{e})+\mathcal{O}(\varepsilon^{4}).

(18)

For the harmonic Rayleigh quotient with target
\tau
the expression becomes

\widetilde{\theta}_{\tau}(\mathbf{u})=\lambda\,(1+\mathbf{e}^{T}\,(\lambda-\tau)^{-1}(A-\tau I)\,(\lambda^{-1}A-I)\,\mathbf{e})+\mathcal{O}(\varepsilon^{4}).

(19)

In particular, we note that
\theta(\mathbf{x})=\widetilde{\theta}(\mathbf{x})=\widetilde{\theta}_{\tau}(\mathbf{x})=\lambda
. We also point out that we have studied similar expressions for
inverse
Rayleigh quotients (as stepsizes for gradient methods) in
[
3
]
.

To derive the lower and upper bounds on the sensitivity of the approximate eigenvalues, we make use of this standard result for symmetric operators.

Lemma 2
.

Suppose
\mathbf{u}=\mathbf{x}+\mathbf{e}
is an approximate eigenvector corresponding to a simple eigenvalue
\lambda
, with
\|\mathbf{x}\|=1
,
\mathbf{e}\perp\mathbf{x}
, and
\varepsilon=\|\mathbf{e}\|
, of a symmetric
A
. Let
p
be a polynomial. Then

\varepsilon^{2}\,\min_{\lambda_{i}\neq\lambda}\ |p(\lambda_{i})|\ \leq\ |\mathbf{e}^{T}p(A)\,\mathbf{e}|\ \leq\ \varepsilon^{2}\,\max_{\lambda_{i}\neq\lambda}\ |p(\lambda_{i})|.

Proof.

Since
A
is symmetric, it is diagonalizable by an orthogonal transformation, and we may assume that
A=\text{diag}(\lambda_{1},\dots,\lambda_{n})
.
With the notation
\lambda=\lambda_{j}
, it follows that
|\mathbf{e}^{T}\!p(A)\mathbf{e}|=|\sum_{i\neq j}e_{i}^{2}\,p(\lambda_{i})|
, from which the result follows easily.
∎

The above expressions imply the following lower and upper bounds on the sensitivity of the approximate eigenvalues as function of the approximate eigenvector.
The assumption
\lambda\neq 0
in the next propositions may be viewed as non-restrictive: for zero eigenvalues we can consider the absolute error
|\theta(\mathbf{u})-\lambda|
instead.

Proposition 4
.

Suppose
\mathbf{u}=\mathbf{x}+\mathbf{e}
is an approximate eigenvector corresponding to a simple eigenvalue
\lambda\neq 0
of a symmetric
A
, with
\|\mathbf{x}\|=1
,
\mathbf{e}\perp\mathbf{x}
, and
\varepsilon=\|\mathbf{e}\|
. Then, up to
\mathcal{O}(\varepsilon^{4})
-terms, for the sensitivity of the Rayleigh quotient (as function of
\mathbf{u}
) it holds that

\min_{\lambda_{i}\neq\lambda}\frac{|\lambda_{i}-\lambda|}{|\lambda|}\ \varepsilon^{2}\ \lesssim\ \frac{|\theta(\mathbf{u})-\lambda|}{|\lambda|}\ \lesssim\ \max_{\lambda_{i}\neq\lambda}\frac{|\lambda_{i}-\lambda|}{|\lambda|}\ \varepsilon^{2}.

(20)

If
\tau
is not equal to an eigenvalue, then for the harmonic Rayleigh quotient with target
\widetilde{\theta}_{\tau}
:

\min_{\lambda_{i}\neq\lambda}\frac{|(\lambda_{i}-\tau)(\lambda_{i}-\lambda)|}{|\lambda(\lambda-\tau)|}\,\varepsilon^{2}\ \lesssim\ \frac{|\widetilde{\theta}_{\tau}(\mathbf{u})-\lambda|}{|\lambda|}\ \lesssim\ \max_{\lambda_{i}\neq\lambda}\frac{|(\lambda_{i}-\tau)(\lambda_{i}-\lambda)|}{|\lambda(\lambda-\tau)|}\,\varepsilon^{2}.

(21)

In particular, for the sensitivity of the harmonic Rayleigh quotient (i.e.,
\tau=0
) we have

\min_{\lambda_{i}\neq\lambda}\frac{|\lambda_{i}(\lambda_{i}-\lambda)|}{\lambda^{2}}\,\varepsilon^{2}\ \lesssim\ \frac{|\widetilde{\theta}(\mathbf{u})-\lambda|}{|\lambda|}\ \lesssim\ \max_{\lambda_{i}\neq\lambda}\frac{|\lambda_{i}(\lambda_{i}-\lambda)|}{\lambda^{2}}\,\varepsilon^{2}.

Proof.

This follows from (
17
), (
18
), and (
19
), using Lemma
2
with
p(t)=t-\lambda
for (
17
) and
p(t)=t\,(t-\lambda)
for (
19
). The bound for (
17
) is a specific case of (
18
), when
\tau=0
.
∎

We mention that the bound (
20
) has a slightly improved version, as follows (cf., e.g.,
[
15
, Thm. 2.1]
for the smallest eigenvalue).
For this context we introduce
\widetilde{\mathbf{e}}=\frac{\mathbf{e}}{\|\mathbf{e}\|}
of unit length,
\mathbf{u}=\mathbf{x}+\mathbf{e}
as before, and
\widetilde{\mathbf{u}}=\frac{\mathbf{u}}{\|\mathbf{u}\|}
, where
\|\mathbf{u}\|^{2}=1+\varepsilon^{2}
.
We can decompose
\widetilde{\mathbf{u}}=\cos(\widetilde{\mathbf{u}},\mathbf{x})\,\mathbf{x}+\sin(\widetilde{\mathbf{u}},\mathbf{x})\,\widetilde{\mathbf{e}}
. Then an easy computation gives
\widetilde{\mathbf{u}}^{T}\!A\widetilde{\mathbf{u}}-\lambda=\sin^{2}(\widetilde{\mathbf{u}},\mathbf{x})\ \widetilde{\mathbf{e}}^{T}\!(A-\lambda I)\,\widetilde{\mathbf{e}}
and therefore

|\widetilde{\mathbf{u}}^{T}\!A\widetilde{\mathbf{u}}-\lambda|\leq\max_{\lambda_{i}\neq\lambda}|\lambda_{i}-\lambda|\,\sin^{2}(\widetilde{\mathbf{u}},\mathbf{x}).

(22)

To connect the approximate bound (
20
) to (
22
), we note that
\sin^{2}(\widetilde{\mathbf{u}},\mathbf{x})=\frac{\varepsilon^{2}}{1+\varepsilon^{2}}
, which is asymptotically equal to
\varepsilon^{2}
. The bound (
22
) is exact (i.e., not an asymptotic bound as in Proposition
4
) and sharp, but it is asymptotically equal to our expression.

An exact bound for the error of the harmonic Rayleigh quotient with target is provided in
[
14
, Thm. 5.2]
. Under certain assumptions mentioned in
[
14
]
for the target
\tau
and
\varepsilon
, with
\mathbf{x}=\mathbf{x}+\varepsilon\,\widetilde{\mathbf{e}}
, it is shown that

\frac{\mathbf{u}^{T}A(A-\tau I)\,\mathbf{u}}{\mathbf{u}^{T}(A-\tau I)\,\mathbf{u}}-\lambda=\varepsilon^{2}\frac{\widetilde{\mathbf{e}}^{T}(A-\lambda I)(A-\tau I)\,\widetilde{\mathbf{e}}}{(\lambda-\tau)+\varepsilon^{2}\,(\widetilde{\mathbf{e}}^{T}A\widetilde{\mathbf{e}}-\tau)}\leq\varepsilon^{2}\,\max_{\lambda_{i}\neq\lambda}\frac{(\lambda_{i}-\lambda)(\lambda_{i}-\tau)}{(\lambda-\tau)+\varepsilon^{2}(\lambda_{i}-\tau)}.

Since we do not make any assumptions on the target or the value of
\|\mathbf{e}\|
, our upper bound (
21
) includes absolute values. Discarding the
\varepsilon^{2}
-term in the denominator yields an approximate upper bound accurate to
\mathcal{O}(\varepsilon^{4})
-terms. In conclusion, (
21
) is less sharp in some situations but more general, and asymptotically the same as the result of
[
14
]
. Another good reason to consider (
20
) and (
21
) is that approximate bounds for the error of the homogeneous Rayleigh quotient can also be obtained via the second-order approximation of the quotient, as we have done in Proposition
4
. At the end of this section, we will also point out that the three approximate bounds have the same form.

3.2
Homogeneous Rayleigh quotient

We now determine approximate bounds for the sensitivity of the homogeneous Rayleigh quotient.

Proposition 5
.

Suppose
\mathbf{u}=\mathbf{x}+\mathbf{e}
is an approximate eigenvector corresponding to a simple eigenvalue
\lambda\neq 0
of a symmetric
A
, with
\|\mathbf{x}\|=1
,
\mathbf{e}\perp\mathbf{x}
, and
\varepsilon=\|\mathbf{e}\|
. Then, up to
\mathcal{O}(\varepsilon^{4})
-terms, for the sensitivity of the homogeneous Rayleigh quotient (as function of
\mathbf{u}
) it holds that:

\frac{\varepsilon^{2}}{\lambda^{2}+1}\,\min_{\lambda_{i}\neq\lambda}|(\lambda_{i}-\lambda)(\lambda_{i}+\lambda^{-1})|\,\lesssim\,\frac{|\alpha(\mathbf{u})-\lambda|}{|\lambda|}\,\lesssim\,\frac{\varepsilon^{2}}{\lambda^{2}+1}\,\max_{\lambda_{i}\neq\lambda}|(\lambda_{i}-\lambda)(\lambda_{i}+\lambda^{-1})|.

Proof.

From Proposition
1
(iii), the homogeneous Rayleigh quotient can be written as

\alpha=\sqrt{\omega^{2}+1}-\omega,\qquad\omega=(2\ \mathbf{u}^{T}\!A\mathbf{u})^{-1}\,(\mathbf{u}^{T}\mathbf{u}-\mathbf{u}^{T}\!A^{2}\mathbf{u}).

We can express
\omega
in terms of
\lambda
and
\mathbf{e}
as

\omega=\frac{1-\lambda^{2}+\|\mathbf{e}\|^{2}-\|A\mathbf{e}\|^{2}}{2\,(\lambda+\mathbf{e}^{T}A\mathbf{e})}.

Furthermore, when
\|\mathbf{e}\|=\varepsilon\to 0
,

\omega=\tfrac{1}{2}\,\lambda^{-1}\,(1-\lambda^{-1}\,\mathbf{e}^{T}\!A\mathbf{e})\,(1-\lambda^{2}+\|\mathbf{e}\|^{2}-\|A\mathbf{e}\|^{2})+\mathcal{O}(\varepsilon^{4}).

For the square root, we use the first-order approximation
\sqrt{1+t}=1+\frac{1}{2}t+\mathcal{O}(t^{2})
. Discarding
\mathcal{O}(\varepsilon^{4})
-terms yields

\displaystyle\sqrt{\omega^{2}+1}

\displaystyle=\tfrac{1}{2}\,\lambda^{-1}\,(1+\lambda^{-1}\,\mathbf{e}^{T}\!A\mathbf{e})^{-1}\sqrt{(1-\lambda^{2}+\|\mathbf{e}\|^{2}-\|A\mathbf{e}\|^{2})^{2}+4\,(\lambda+\mathbf{e}^{T}A\mathbf{e})^{2}}

\displaystyle=\tfrac{1}{2}\lambda^{-1}(1-\lambda^{-1}\mathbf{e}^{T}A\mathbf{e})\big(1+\lambda^{2}+\tfrac{1-\lambda^{2}}{1+\lambda^{2}}(\|\mathbf{e}\|^{2}-\|A\mathbf{e}\|^{2})+\tfrac{4\lambda}{1+\lambda^{2}}\,\mathbf{e}^{T}\!A\mathbf{e}\big).

Simple computations show that

\displaystyle\alpha

\displaystyle=(1-\lambda^{-1}\mathbf{e}^{T}A\mathbf{e})\Big(\lambda-\frac{\lambda}{\lambda^{2}+1}(\|\mathbf{e}\|^{2}-\|A\mathbf{e}\|^{2})+\frac{2}{\lambda^{2}+1}\mathbf{e}^{T}\!A\mathbf{e}\Big)+\mathcal{O}(\varepsilon^{4})

\displaystyle=\lambda+\frac{\lambda}{\lambda^{2}+1}\,\mathbf{e}^{T}(A+\lambda^{-1}I)(A-\lambda I)\,\mathbf{e}+\mathcal{O}(\varepsilon^{4}).

(23)

The thesis follows from Lemma
2
, with the polynomial
p(t)=(t+\lambda^{-1})(t-\lambda)
.

An alternative method to derive this result is via the Implicit Function Theorem. From (
14
), for the pair
(\alpha(\mathbf{u}),\mathbf{u})
, we have the implicit equation
F(\alpha(\mathbf{u}),\mathbf{u}):=\mathbf{u}^{T}(A-\alpha I)(A+\alpha^{-1}I)\,\mathbf{u}=0
.
Then we know that

\nabla\alpha(\mathbf{u})=-\Big(\frac{\partial F}{\partial\alpha}\Big)^{-1}\,\frac{\partial F}{\partial\mathbf{u}}\\
=2\ [\mathbf{u}^{T}(A+\alpha^{-2}A)\,\mathbf{u}]^{-1}\ (A-\alpha I)(A+\alpha^{-1}I)\,\mathbf{u}.

Since
\nabla\alpha(\mathbf{x})=\mathbf{0}
, we consider the second-order approximation of the homogeneous Rayleigh quotient
\alpha(\mathbf{x}+\mathbf{e})\approx\alpha(\mathbf{x})+\frac{1}{2}\,\mathbf{e}^{T}\nabla^{2}\alpha(\mathbf{x})\,\mathbf{e}
.
The action of
\nabla^{2}\alpha(\mathbf{x})
can be obtained from the first-order approximation of the gradient in
\mathbf{x}
:

\displaystyle\nabla^{2}\alpha(\mathbf{x})\,\mathbf{e}

\displaystyle=\nabla\alpha(\mathbf{x}+\mathbf{e})-\nabla\alpha(\mathbf{x})+\mathcal{O}(\varepsilon^{2})

\displaystyle=\frac{2\,\lambda}{1+\lambda^{2}}\ (A-\lambda I)(A+\lambda^{-1}I)\,\mathbf{e}+\mathcal{O}(\varepsilon^{2}).

This also leads to the expression in (
3.2
).
∎

Interestingly, by replacing
\tau=-\lambda^{-1}
in the approximation of the harmonic Rayleigh quotient with target (
19
), we get the approximation (
3.2
) for the homogeneous Rayleigh quotient.

We now compare the different asymptotic bounds. With a little abuse of notation, let
\theta(\mathbf{u})
be any of the three Rayleigh quotients in
\mathbf{u}
. Then the previous results can be summarized by

\frac{\varepsilon^{2}}{|\lambda|}\ \min_{\lambda_{i}\neq\lambda}|p_{\lambda}(\lambda_{i})|\ \ \lesssim\ \ \frac{|\theta(\mathbf{u})-\lambda|}{|\lambda|}\ \ \lesssim\ \ \frac{\varepsilon^{2}}{|\lambda|}\ \max_{\lambda_{i}\neq\lambda}|p_{\lambda}(\lambda_{i})|,

where the different polynomials
p_{\lambda}(\cdot)
are reported in Table

\lambda\to 0
and
\lambda\to\pm\infty
. This table provides a naive explanation of what we will observe in the experiments: the homogeneous Rayleigh quotient tends to have a similar behavior to the Rayleigh quotient for small eigenvalues, and it follows the harmonic Rayleigh quotient for large eigenvalues.

3.3
Comparison of the Rayleigh quotients

A=\text{diag}(\lambda_{1},\dots,\lambda_{n})

(\lambda,\mathbf{x})

\mathbf{x}

.
To compute a random perturbation
\mathbf{u}=\mathbf{x}+\varepsilon\,\mathbf{e}
, such that
\|\mathbf{e}\|=1
and
\mathbf{x}\perp\mathbf{e}

\mathbf{x}

\mathbf{e}
. Finally we normalize
\mathbf{u}
.

So far we have discussed the properties of the homogeneous Rayleigh quotient and compared it to the well-known standard and harmonic Rayleigh quotient. Now we extend the homogeneous Rayleigh quotient for the generalized eigenvalue problem and present an application of Rayleigh quotients to gradient methods for unconstrained optimization problems.

4
The generalized eigenvalue problem

Let us consider the generalized eigenvalue problem (GEP)
A\mathbf{x}=\lambda B\,\mathbf{x}
, where
A
is symmetric (definite or indefinite), and
B
is SPD. This problem has
n
real eigenvalues
\lambda_{1}\leq\cdots\leq\lambda_{n}
.
Although a common generalized Rayleigh quotient is
\frac{\mathbf{u}^{T}\!A\mathbf{u}}{\mathbf{u}^{T}\!B\,\mathbf{u}}
(see, e.g.,
[
1
, Ch. 15]
), it turns out that, in our context, the most relevant quantities are

\theta=\frac{\mathbf{u}^{T}\!AB\,\mathbf{u}}{\mathbf{u}^{T}\!B^{2}\mathbf{u}},\qquad\widetilde{\theta}=\frac{\mathbf{u}^{T}\!A^{2}\mathbf{u}}{\mathbf{u}^{T}\!AB\,\mathbf{u}},

(25)

for nonzero
\mathbf{u}^{T}\!AB\,\mathbf{u}
.
The Rayleigh quotient
\theta
satisfies
A\mathbf{u}-\theta B\,\mathbf{u}\perp B\,\mathbf{u}
, and, equivalently, it is the solution to
\min_{\gamma}\|A\mathbf{u}-\gamma B\,\mathbf{u}\|
.
The harmonic Rayleigh quotient
\widetilde{\theta}
satisfies
A\mathbf{u}-\theta B\,\mathbf{u}\perp A\mathbf{u}
and solves
\min_{\gamma}\|\gamma^{-1}A\mathbf{u}-B\,\mathbf{u}\|
.
The extension of the homogeneous Rayleigh quotient for the GEP is rather straightforward.
It is the solution to

\min_{(\alpha_{1},\,\alpha_{2})\,\in\,\mathbb{P}}\ \frac{\|\alpha_{1}B\,\mathbf{u}-\alpha_{2}\,A\mathbf{u}\|}{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\sqrt{\alpha_{1}^{2}+\alpha_{2}^{2}}}}=\min_{\alpha_{1}^{2}+\alpha_{2}^{2}=1}\ \|[B\,\mathbf{u}\ \ -\!\!A\mathbf{u}]{\mbox{\scriptsize$\left[\!\!\begin{array}[]{c}\alpha_{1}\\
\alpha_{2}\end{array}\!\!\right]$}}\|.

(26)

As for the standard eigenvalue problem, this amounts to solve a reduced SVD of an
n\times 2
matrix, or an eigenvalue problem involving a
2\times 2
matrix.

The next result is a generalization of Proposition
1
.
Let
C=[B\,\mathbf{u}\ \ -\!\!A\mathbf{u}]
.

Proposition 6
.

Suppose that
\mathbf{u}^{T}\!AB\,\mathbf{u}\neq 0
and denote by
\mu
the smallest eigenvalue of
C^{T}\!C
. Then the following properties hold.

(i)

\mu
is a simple eigenvalue of
C^{T}\!C
, or equivalently
\sqrt{\mu}
is a simple singular value of
C
; its corresponding eigenvector
[\alpha_{1},\,\alpha_{2}]^{T}
is the unique minimizer of (
26
), up to orthogonal transformations.

(ii)

\mu=\tfrac{1}{2}\,\big[\mathbf{u}^{T}\!A^{2}\mathbf{u}+\mathbf{u}^{T}\!B^{2}\mathbf{u}-\sqrt{\smash[b]{(\mathbf{u}^{T}\!A^{2}\mathbf{u}-\mathbf{u}^{T}\!B^{2}\mathbf{u})^{2}+4\,(\mathbf{u}^{T}\!AB\,\mathbf{u})^{2}}}\ \big].

(iii)

For the generalized homogeneous Rayleigh quotient
\alpha
we have

\displaystyle\alpha

\displaystyle=(2\ \mathbf{u}^{T}\!AB\,\mathbf{u})^{-1}\,\big[\,\mathbf{u}^{T}\!A^{2}\,\mathbf{u}-\mathbf{u}^{T}\!B^{2}\mathbf{u}+\sqrt{(\mathbf{u}^{T}\!A^{2}\,\mathbf{u}-\mathbf{u}^{T}\!B^{2}\mathbf{u})^{2}+4\,(\mathbf{u}^{T}\!AB\,\mathbf{u})^{2}}\,\big].

(27)

(iv)

\mu=0
if and only if
\mathbf{u}
is an eigenvector. If
\mathbf{u}
is an eigenvector, then the corresponding homogeneous Rayleigh quotient
\alpha
is the corresponding eigenvalue.

(v)

0\leq\mu<\min(\mathbf{u}^{T}\!A^{2}\mathbf{u},\ \mathbf{u}^{T}\!B^{2}\mathbf{u})
.

(vi)

We have the following inequalities

\begin{cases}\theta\leq\alpha\leq\widetilde{\theta}&\text{if}\quad\mathbf{u}^{T}\!AB\,\mathbf{u}>0,\\
\widetilde{\theta}\leq\alpha\leq\theta&\text{if}\quad\mathbf{u}^{T}\!AB\,\mathbf{u}<0.\end{cases}

Proof.

This proof follows the exact same lines as those of Proposition
1
.
∎

As in Section
2
, we can show that the generalized homogeneous Rayleigh quotient is a solution to a quadratic equation.

Proposition 7
.

Let
\mathbf{u}^{T}\!AB\,\mathbf{u}\neq 0
. The generalized homogeneous Rayleigh quotient (
(iii)
) is the solution to

(\mathbf{u}^{T}\!AB\,\mathbf{u})\,\alpha^{2}+(\mathbf{u}^{T}\!B^{2}\mathbf{u}-\mathbf{u}^{T}\!A^{2}\mathbf{u})\,\alpha-(\mathbf{u}^{T}\!AB\,\mathbf{u})=0,

(28)

which satisfies
\alpha\ \mathbf{u}^{T}\!AB\,\mathbf{u}>0
.

Proof.

The proof is similar to that of Proposition
3
.
∎

We derive a Galerkin condition from Proposition
7
as follows. Since
\mathbf{u}^{T}\!AB\,\mathbf{u}=\mathbf{u}^{T}\!B\,A\mathbf{u}
, (
28
) is equivalent to
\mathbf{u}^{T}\!(\alpha A+B)(A-\alpha B)\,\mathbf{u}=0
.
Therefore, we can write the corresponding
nonlinear
Galerkin condition in homogeneous coordinates as

(\alpha_{2}A-\alpha_{1}B)\,\mathbf{u}\perp(\alpha_{1}A+\alpha_{2}B)\,\mathbf{u}.

Finally, we discuss a bound for the chordal metric, analogous to (
16
). For an SPD
B
, as a generalization to
[
1
, Thm. 4.5.1]
, one can show that there exists a generalized eigenvalue
\lambda=\lambda(A,B)
of
(A,B)
such that
|\lambda-\alpha|\leq(\lambda_{1}(B)\|\mathbf{u}\|)^{-1}\|(A-\alpha\,B)\,\mathbf{u}\|
, where
\lambda_{1}(B)
is the smallest eigenvalue of
B
.
Indeed, if
\alpha
is not an eigenvalue of
(A,B)
,

\displaystyle\|\mathbf{u}\|

\displaystyle=\|(A-\alpha\,B)^{-1}\,(A-\alpha\,B)\,\mathbf{u}\|\geq\lambda_{1}(B)\,\|(B^{-1}A-\alpha\,I)^{-1}\|\,\|(A-\alpha\,B)\,\mathbf{u}\|

\displaystyle\geq\lambda_{1}(B)\,\min_{i}|\lambda_{i}(A,B)-\alpha|\cdot\|(A-\alpha\,B)\,\mathbf{u}\|.

Therefore, for the chordal distance between
\lambda
and
\alpha
we have

\frac{|\lambda-\alpha|}{\sqrt{1+\lambda^{2}}\,\sqrt{1+\alpha^{2}}}\leq\frac{1}{\lambda_{1}(B)}\cdot\frac{1}{\sqrt{1+\lambda^{2}}\,\|\mathbf{u}\|}\cdot\frac{\|(A-\alpha\,B)\,\mathbf{u}\|}{\sqrt{1+\alpha^{2}}}.

This upper bound is minimized for
\alpha
equal to the generalized homogeneous Rayleigh quotient. We notice that this inequality differs from (
16
) by the factor
(\lambda_{1}(B))^{-1}
.

In the next section, we return to the standard homogeneous Rayleigh quotient, and study its use as stepsize in gradient methods.

5
A homogeneous stepsize for gradient methods

In gradient methods for nonlinear optimization, inverse Rayleigh quotients are popular choices for stepsizes. We refer to
[
16
]
for a nice recent review about steplength selection.
We exploit the inverse of the homogeneous Rayleigh quotient as a new
homogeneous stepsize
for gradient-type methods.
Consider the unconstrained minimization of a smooth function
f:\mathbb{R}^{n}\to\mathbb{R}
,

\min_{\mathbf{x}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}}\,f(\mathbf{x}).

Gradient methods are of the form

\mathbf{x}_{k+1}=\mathbf{x}_{k}-\beta_{k}\,\mathbf{g}_{k}=\mathbf{x}_{k}-\alpha_{k}^{-1}\,\mathbf{g}_{k},

where
\mathbf{g}_{k}=\nabla f(\mathbf{x}_{k})
,
\beta_{k}>0
is the stepsize, and
\alpha_{k}
the inverse stepsize.
As usual we write
\mathbf{s}_{k-1}=\mathbf{x}_{k}-\mathbf{x}_{k-1}
and
\mathbf{y}_{k-1}=\mathbf{g}_{k}-\mathbf{g}_{k-1}
.

In
[
5
]
, the Barzilai–Borwein stepsizes

\beta_{k}^{\rm{BB1}}=\frac{\mathbf{s}_{k-1}^{T}\,\mathbf{s}_{k-1}}{\mathbf{s}_{k-1}^{T}\,\mathbf{y}_{k-1}},\qquad\beta_{k}^{\rm{BB2}}=\frac{\mathbf{s}_{k-1}^{T}\,\mathbf{y}_{k-1}}{\mathbf{y}_{k-1}^{T}\,\mathbf{y}_{k-1}}

(29)

have been introduced. The motivation is that, when we approximate the Hessian in
\mathbf{x}_{k-1}
by a scalar multiple of the identity, i.e.,
\nabla^{2}f(\mathbf{x}_{k-1})\approx\gamma\,I
, the corresponding inverse stepsizes satisfy the following least squares secant conditions:

\alpha_{k}^{\rm{BB1}}=\argmin_{\gamma}\|\mathbf{y}_{k-1}-\gamma\,\mathbf{s}_{k-1}\|,\qquad\alpha_{k}^{\rm{BB2}}=\argmin_{\gamma}\|\gamma^{-1}\,\mathbf{y}_{k-1}-\mathbf{s}_{k-1}\|,

(30)

with
\alpha_{k}^{\rm{BB1}}=(\beta_{k}^{\rm{BB1}})^{-1}
and
\alpha_{k}^{\rm{BB2}}=(\beta_{k}^{\rm{BB2}})^{-1}
. Moreover, both stepsizes (
29
) can be seen as inverse Rayleigh quotients of a certain matrix
H_{k}
at
\mathbf{s}_{k-1}
. In fact, for any
f
it holds

\mathbf{y}_{k-1}=\nabla f(\mathbf{x}_{k})-\nabla f(\mathbf{x}_{k-1})=H_{k}\,(\mathbf{x}_{k}-\mathbf{x}_{k-1})=H_{k}\,\mathbf{s}_{k-1},

where
H_{k}:=\int_{0}^{1}\nabla^{2}f((1-t)\,\mathbf{x}_{k-1}+t\,\mathbf{x}_{k})\,dt
is an average Hessian on the line piece between
\mathbf{x}_{k-1}
and
\mathbf{x}_{k}
.
From this relation, it is easy to see that the minimum residual conditions (
3
) and (
7
) are equivalent to the secant conditions (
30
) for
\mathbf{u}=\mathbf{s}_{k-1}
and
A=H_{k}
.

We introduce the homogeneous BB stepsize (HBB) as the inverse of the homogeneous Rayleigh quotient of
H_{k}
in
\mathbf{s}_{k-1}
. The HBB stepsize is given by the quotient
\beta_{k}^{\rm HBB}=\alpha_{2,k}/\alpha_{1,k}
, where the pair
(\alpha_{1,k}
,
\alpha_{2,k})
solves

\argmin_{\alpha_{1}^{2}+\alpha^{2}_{2}=1}\ \|\alpha_{1}\,\mathbf{s}_{k-1}-\alpha_{2}\,\mathbf{y}_{k-1}\|.

As in (
30
), this is equivalent to the minimum residual condition (
8
) for
\mathbf{u}=\mathbf{s}_{k-1}
and
A=H_{k}
.
Therefore (cf. Proposition
1
)

\beta_{k}^{\rm HBB}=\frac{\|\mathbf{s}_{k-1}\|^{2}-\|\mathbf{y}_{k-1}\|^{2}+\sqrt{\smash[b]{(\|\mathbf{s}_{k-1}\|^{2}-\|\mathbf{y}_{k-1}\|^{2})^{2}+4\,(\mathbf{s}_{k-1}^{T}\mathbf{y}_{k-1})^{2}}}}{2\ \mathbf{s}_{k-1}^{T}\mathbf{y}_{k-1}}.

(31)

As mentioned in Section
1
, this stepsize has been proposed first by Li, Zhang, and Xia
[
12
,
13
]
, obtained from a total least squares secant condition.

In this section, we will also introduce an alternating variant of this HBB step.

In Section
5.1
we will carry out some experiments to test the behavior of the HBB stepsize when plugged into a gradient method for general differentiable functions. A pseudocode of this method is provided in Algorithm

.

6
Conclusions

We have introduced a new Rayleigh quotient, the homogeneous Rayleigh quotient
\alpha
, which minimizes the homogeneous residual quantity (
8
). We have shown that the value of
\alpha
lies between the standard and the harmonic Rayleigh quotient, and so does its sensitivity when approximating extreme eigenvalues of an SPD matrix
A
. Regarding the other eigenvalues and the indefinite case, we have provided examples where the homogeneous Rayleigh quotient may be more accurate than the others.
In addition, in all our experiments, we have noticed that the homogeneous Rayleigh quotient is less sensitive than one of the other two Rayleigh quotients. To some extent, the homogeneous Rayleigh quotient seems to leverage the two Rayleigh quotients: it is less sensitive than the harmonic Rayleigh quotient when estimating the smallest eigenvalues, and less sensitive than the standard Rayleigh quotient when estimating the largest eigenvalues (in magnitude).

We have derived a
nonlinear
Galerkin condition for the homogeneous Rayleigh quotient, in contrast to the linear Galerkin conditions for the standard and the harmonic Rayleigh quotient.
All the results extend to the homogeneous Rayleigh quotient for the generalized eigenvalue problem
(A,B)
, when
B
is SPD and
A
is symmetric.

Finally, we have considered the homogeneous Rayleigh quotient as inverse stepsize (HBB) in gradient methods for unconstrained optimization problems.

This stepsize has first and independently been obtained recently from a different angle by Li, Zhang, and Xia
[
12
,
13
]
.

We have also proposed the AHBB steplength as an alternative to the ABB stepsize, based on the homogeneous stepsize. Experiments show that this variant sometimes performs better than the classical BB steplengths and ABB.
