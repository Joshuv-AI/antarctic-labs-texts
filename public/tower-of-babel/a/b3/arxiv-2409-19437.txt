Strongly-Polynomial Time and Validation Analysis of Policy Gradient Methods Thanks: First submitted on 09/27/2024. Authors listed alphabetically.
Caleb Ju, Guanghui Lan

Abstract
This paper proposes a novel termination criterion, termed the advantage gap function, for finite state and action Markov decision processes (MDP) and reinforcement learning (RL). By incorporating this advantage gap function into the design of step size rules and deriving a new linear rate of convergence that is independent of the stationary state distribution of the optimal policy, we demonstrate that policy gradient methods can solve MDPs in strongly-polynomial time. To the best of our knowledge, this is the first time that such strong convergence properties have been established for policy gradient methods. Moreover, in the stochastic setting, where only stochastic estimates of policy gradients are available, we show that the advantage gap function provides close approximations of the optimality gap for each individual state and exhibits a sublinear rate of convergence at every state. The advantage gap function can be easily estimated in the stochastic case, and when coupled with easily computable upper bounds on policy values, they provide a convenient way to validate the solutions generated by policy gradient methods. Therefore, our developments offer a principled and computable measure of optimality for RL, whereas current practice tends to rely on algorithm-to-algorithm or baseline comparisons with no certificate of optimality.

Introduction

Reinforcement learning (RL) generally refers to Markov Decision Processes (MDP) with unknown transition kernels. The increasing interest in applying RL to real-world applications over the last decade is fueled not only by its success in domains like robotics, resource allocation, and optimal control
[
14
,
19
,
3
]
, but more recently in strategic game play (i.e., artificial intelligence for video games) and training large-language models via reinforcement learning from human feedback
[
29
,
36
]
.
Such empirical successes have inspired intensive research on the development of principled MDP and RL algorithms during the last decade.

Depending on the mathematical formulations and the sub-fields from which the technology originates, MDP/RL algorithms can be grouped into
three different categories: dynamic optimization, linear optimization, and nonlinear optimization methods.
Dynamic optimization methods include the classic value iteration and policy iteration, as well as their stochastic variants, e.g., stochastic value iteration and Q-learning
[
41
,
47
,
42
]
. Since certain important MDP/RL problems, such as those in finite state and action spaces, can be formulated as linear programs, several key linear optimization algorithms (e.g., Simplex methods, interior point methods, first-order methods and their stochastic variants)
have been proposed for MDP/RL
[
15
,
46
,
50
,
51
,
37
]
. More recently, nonlinear optimization methods, particularly those based on policy gradient methods,
have attracted much attention in both industry and academia
[
43
,
48
,
29
,
40
,
24
,
1
,
7
]
.
Compared to the previous two categories, these nonlinear programming based methods offer several significant advantages.
First, they can handle large, even continuous, state and action spaces by incorporating value function approximation techniques
[
1
,
43
,
42
,
17
]
.
Second, they can effectively operate in various stochastic environments, such as using generative models or on-policy sampling to access random observations from the transition kernel in RL
[
42
]
. Third, they can efficiently process and even benefit from nonlinear components (e.g., regularization terms) existing in
MDP/RL formulations
[
25
,
8
,
34
]
. On the other hand, while there exists a rich theoretical foundation for classical dynamic optimization and linear optimization methods, theoretic studies for nonlinear policy gradient methods are still lacking behind.

One prominent issue lies in the theoretical convergence guarantees that policy gradient methods provide for their approximate solutions to MDPs.
Although dynamic and linear optimization methods bound the optimality gap at every state, most policy gradient methods bound the optimality gap that is averaged over states with respect to the stationary state distribution of the optimal policy.
Note that this distribution is unknown and problem-dependent. Moreover, the optimality gap averaged over states is only necessary but not sufficient for the optimality gap to be small at every state.
Therefore, the standard notion of approximate optimality in most policy gradient methods is weaker than in dynamic and linear optimization.
In a similar vein, it is known that MDPs can be solved to optimality in strongly-polynomial time using some dynamic optimization approaches (e.g., policy iteration
[
51
]
) as well as linear optimization methods
[
50
,
51
]
. Yet, such strong convergence guarantees are unknown for policy gradient methods.

Another substantial issue exists in the termination for policy gradient methods, especially under the aforementioned stochastic environments for RL.
In fact, this issue is also shared by other dynamic optimization methods, such as stochastic value iteration and Q-learning.
More specifically, the solutions generated by dynamic optimization or policy gradient type algorithms are typically judged by their empirical costs compared to other competing algorithms or some a priori threshold, e.g., based on prior knowledge of the environment or a human baseline
[
29
,
2
,
40
,
13
]
.
Linear programming, on the other hand, provides both easily accessible primal objective values and duality gaps to monitor the progress of the algorithm.
Because such a convenient and computable gap is not currently known for other RL methods, it is in general challenging to determine when a sufficiently good policy has been found, especially for new and complex environments.
Moreover, even obtaining accurate estimates of the objective function is non-trivial in RL, since the underlying MDP and RL algorithm are stochastic.
This is further exacerbated by the fact the cost function in RL, which is the expectation of a random infinite-horizon sum, can have a large variance.
A common solution is to run an RL algorithm across a small number of seeds (e.g., 3 or 5), and then plot confidence intervals or report some statistics of Monte Carlo estimates of the objective function from each seed
[
11
,
40
,
2
]
.
However, scaling this to more seeds to further reduce estimation errors is intractable since even training one seed can take millions of simulation steps for certain RL problems
[
29
]
.

In this paper, we attempt to address these aforementioned issues associated with policy gradient methods, and some of our results can also be extended to other dynamic optimization methods.
Central to our development is a novel termination criterion called the
advantage gap function
;
see (
10
) for a formal definition.
We show the advantage gap function being small is necessary and sufficient for the optimality gap to be small at every state (see Proposition
2
).
This is stronger than previous notions of approximate optimality for policy gradient methods, which only bound the aggregated optimality gap, i.e., the optimality gap averaged over the steady state distribution of the (unknown) optimality policy, denoted by
\nu^{*}
.
We call such strong convergence guarantees
distribution-free
, since they do not depend on the distribution
\nu^{*}
.
Importantly, by incorporating a novel “scheduled” geometrically increasing step size rule, we show that the policy mirror descent (PMD)
[
24
]
, a recently developed first-order method, can achieve linear rates of convergence that are distribution-free.
This is the first time such strong convergence results have been shown for first-order methods.
Additionally, by embedding the advantage gap function into the aforementioned “scheduled” step size rule for solving MDPs without regularization, we can improve the runtime of PMD to be strongly-polynomial.
For the first time, this extends the celebrated result of Ye, who showed the simplex method and Howard’s policy iteration are strongly-polynomial
[
51
]
, to first-order methods.

It turns out the advantage gap function closely approximates the optimality gap in that it can be used to measure a lower bound on the optimality gap at each state and a universal upper bound on the optimality gap for all states. In particular,
we show that stochastic PMD for solving RL can minimize the advantage gap function at a sublinear rate of convergence that is distribution-free.
This result ensures the policy value function and advantage gap provide accessible estimates that closely approximate the objective value and optimality gap for RL, respectively, similar to the primal objective and duality gap in linear programming methods.
Moreover, since both the policy value function and advantage gap function are stochastic in RL and must be estimated by samples, we show their estimation errors can also be reduced at a similar sublinear rate of convergence that is distribution-free.
This ensures the proposed quantities can be reliably estimated and are suitable to use as a termination criterion and performance metric for RL. To the best of our knowledge, this is the first time such validation analysis procedures have been developed for solving these highly nonconvex RL problems, while some related previous studies have been restricted to stochastic convex optimization only
[
22
,
23
]
.

This paper is organized as follows. We introduce the reinforcement learning and the advantage gap function in Section
2
.
With the problem setup done, we also establish some duality theory for policy mirror descent at the end of Section
2
.
Section
3
then establishes distribution-free convergence rates for the deterministic setting, as well as a (relatively simple) modification to obtain strongly-polynomial runtime for solving unregularized MDPs.
Distribution-free convergence is extended to the stochastic setting in Section
4
.
We provide the analysis of online and offline stochastic accurate certificates in Section
5
.
We conclude with preliminary numerical experiments in Section
6
.

1.1
Notation

For a Hilbert space (e.g., real Euclidean space), let
\|\cdot\|
be the induced norm and let
\|\cdot\|_{*}
be its dual norm.
When appropriate, we specify the exact norm (e.g.,
\ell_{2}
norm,
\ell_{1}
norm).

We denote the probability simplex over
n
elements as

\textstyle\Delta_{n}:=\{x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}:\sum_{i=1}^{n}x_{i}=1,x_{i}\geq 0\ \forall i\}.

For any two distributions
p,q\in\Delta_{n}
, we measure their Kullback–Leibler (KL) divergence by

\mathrm{KL}(q||p)=\sum_{i=1}^{n}p_{i}\log\frac{p_{i}}{q_{i}}
.
Observe that the KL divergence can be viewed as a special instance of Bregman’s distance (or prox-function) widely used in the optimization literature.
We define Bregman’s distance associated with a distance generating function
\omega:X\to\mathbb{R}
for some set
X\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{n}
as

\textstyle D(q,p):=\omega(p)-\omega(q)-\langle\nabla\omega(q),p-q\rangle,\ \forall p,q\in X.

The choice of
X=\Delta_{n}
and Shannon entropy
\omega(p):=\sum_{i=1}^{n}p_{i}\log p_{i}
results in Bregman’s distance as the KL-divergence.
In this case, one can show Bregman’s distance is 1-strongly convex w.r.t. the
\ell_{1}
norm:
D(q,p)\geq\|p-q\|_{1}^{2}
.
Another popular choice is
\omega(\cdot)=\frac{1}{2}\|\cdot\|_{2}^{2}
, the Euclidean distance squared and
X\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{n}
.
Bregman’s distance becomes
D(q,p)=\frac{1}{2}\|p-q\|_{2}^{2}
.

2
Markov decision process, a gap function, and connections to (non-)linear programming

An infinite-horizon discounted Markov decision process (MDP) is a five-tuple

(\mathcal{S},\mathcal{A},\mathcal{P},c,\gamma)
, where

\mathcal{S}
is a finite state space,

\mathcal{A}
is a finite action space,
and
\mathcal{P}:\mathcal{S}\times\mathcal{S}\times\mathcal{A}\to\mathbb{R}
is the transition kernel where given a state-action
(s,a)
pair, it reports probability of the next state being
s^{\prime}
, denoted by
\mathcal{P}(s^{\prime}|s,a)
.
The cost is
c:\mathcal{S}\times\mathcal{A}\to\mathbb{R}
and

\gamma\in[0,1)
is a discount factor.
A feasible policy
\pi:\mathcal{A}\times\mathcal{S}\to\mathbb{R}
determines the probability of selecting a particular action at a given state.
We denote the space of feasible policies by
\Pi
.
Now, we write Bregman’s distance between any two policies at state
s
as

\textstyle D_{\pi}^{\pi^{\prime}}(s):=D(\pi(\cdot|s),\pi^{\prime}(\cdot|s))=\omega(\pi^{\prime}(\cdot|s))-\omega(\pi(\cdot|s))-\langle\nabla\omega(\pi(\cdot|s)),\pi^{\prime}(\cdot|s)-\pi(\cdot|s)\rangle.

We measure a policy
\pi
’s performance by the action-value
function
Q^{\pi}:\mathcal{S}\times\mathcal{A}\to\mathbb{R}

defined as

\textstyle Q^{\pi}(s,a):=\mathbb{E}[\sum_{t=0}^{\infty}\gamma^{t}\big[c(s_{t},a_{t})+h^{\pi(\cdot|s_{t})}(s_{t})]\mid s_{0}=s,a_{0}=a,a_{t}\sim\pi(\cdot|s_{t}),s_{t+1}\sim\mathcal{P}(\cdot|s_{t},a_{t})\big],

where the function
h^{\cdot}(s):\Delta_{|\mathcal{A}|}\to\mathbb{R}
is closed strong convex function with modulus
\mu_{h}\geq 0
with respect to (w.r.t.) the policy
\pi(\cdot|s)
, i.e.,

\textstyle h^{\pi(\cdot|s)}(s)-[h^{\pi^{\prime}(\cdot|s)}(s)+\langle(h^{\prime})^{\pi^{\prime}(\cdot|s)}(s,\cdot),\pi(\cdot|s)-\pi^{\prime}(\cdot|s)\rangle\geq\mu_{h}D^{\pi}_{\pi^{\prime}}(s),

(1)

where
\langle\cdot,\cdot\rangle
denotes the inner product over the action space
\mathcal{A}
, and
(h^{\prime})^{\pi^{\prime}(\cdot|s)}(s,\cdot)
denotes a subgradient of
h^{\cdot}(s)
at
\pi^{\prime}(\cdot|s)
.
The generality of
h
allows modeling of the popular entropy regularization, which can induce safe exploration and learn risk-sensitive policies
[
25
,
34
]
, as well as barrier functions for constrained MDPs.
Here we separate
h^{\pi(\cdot|s)}
from the cost
c(s,a)
to take the advantage of
its strong convexity in the design and analysis of algorithms.
Moreover, we define the state-value function

V^{\pi}:\mathcal{S}\to\mathbb{R}
associated with
\pi

as

\textstyle V^{\pi}(s):=\mathbb{E}\big[\sum_{t=0}^{\infty}\gamma^{t}[c(s_{t},a_{t})+h^{\pi_{t}(\cdot|s_{t})}(\cdot|s_{t})]\mid s_{0}=s,a_{t}\sim\pi(\cdot|s_{t}),s_{t+1}\sim\mathcal{P}(\cdot|s_{t},a_{t})\big].

(2)

It can be easily seen from the definitions of
Q^{\pi}
and
V^{\pi}
that

\textstyle V^{\pi}(s)

\textstyle=\sum_{a\in\mathcal{A}}\pi(a|s)Q^{\pi}(s,a)=\langle Q^{\pi}(s,\cdot),\pi(\cdot|s)\rangle

(3)

\textstyle Q^{\pi}(s,a)

\textstyle=c(s,a)+h^{\pi(\cdot|s)}(s)+\gamma\sum_{s^{\prime}\in\mathcal{S}}\mathcal{P}(s^{\prime}|s,a)V^{\pi}(s^{\prime}).

(4)

The main objective in MDP is to find an optimal policy
\pi^{*}:\mathcal{A}\times\mathcal{S}\to\mathbb{R}
such that

V^{\pi^{*}}(s)\leq V^{\pi}(s),\forall\pi(\cdot|s)\in\Delta_{|\mathcal{A}|},\forall s\in\mathcal{S}.

(5)

Sufficient conditions that guarantee the existence of
\pi^{*}
have been intensively studied (e.g.,
[
6
,
37
]
).
Note that (
5
) can be formulated as a nonlinear optimization problem with a single objective function.
Given an initial state distribution
\rho\in\Delta_{|\mathcal{S}|}
, let
f_{\rho}
be defined as

\textstyle f_{\rho}(\pi):=\sum_{s\in\mathcal{S}}\rho(s)\cdot V^{\pi}(s).

(6)

When
\rho
is strictly positive, one can see an optimal solutions to (
5
) is also optimal for (
6
).
While the distribution
\rho
can be arbitrarily chosen, prior policy gradient methods typically select
\rho
to be the
stationary state distribution induced by the optimal policy
\pi^{*}
, denoted by
\nu^{*}:=\nu^{\pi*}

[
24
,
17
,
27
]
.
As such, the problem reduces to
\min_{\pi\in\Pi}f_{\nu^{*}}(\pi)
.
This aggregated objective function is commonly formulated and solved by nonlinear programming approaches such as policy gradient methods.

2.1
Performance difference and advantage function

Given a policy
\pi(\cdot|s)\in\Delta_{|\mathcal{A}|}
, we
define the discounted state visitation distribution
\kappa^{\pi}_{q}:\mathcal{S}\to\mathbb{R}
by

\textstyle\kappa^{\pi}_{q}(s)

\textstyle:=(1-\gamma)\sum_{t=0}^{\infty}\gamma^{t}\mathrm{Pr}^{\pi}\{s_{t}=s|s_{0}=q\},

(7)

where
\mathrm{Pr}^{\pi}\{s_{t}=\cdot|s_{0}=q\}
is the distribution of state
s_{t}
when following policy
\pi
and starting at state
q\in\mathcal{S}
.
In the finite state case, we can also view
\kappa^{\pi}_{q}\in\mathbb{R}^{|\mathcal{S}|}
as a vector.
Clearly,
\kappa^{\pi}_{s}\in\Delta_{|\mathcal{S}|}
, and one can show the lower bound
\kappa^{\pi}_{s}(s)\geq 1-\gamma
.

We now state an important “performance difference” lemma
which tells us the difference on the value functions for two different policies.
The proof has appeared in numerous previous works (e.g.,
[
17
, Lemma 1]
), so we skip it.

Lemma 1
.

Let
\pi
and
\pi^{\prime}
be two feasible policies. Then
we have

\textstyle V^{\pi^{\prime}}(s)-V^{\pi}(s)

\textstyle=\frac{1}{1-\gamma}\sum_{q\in\mathcal{S}}\psi^{\pi}(q,\pi^{\prime}(\cdot|q))\kappa_{s}^{\pi^{\prime}}(q),\forall s\in\mathcal{S},

(8)

where for a given
p\in\Delta_{|\mathcal{A}|}
, the advantage function is defined as

\psi^{\pi}(s,p):=\langle Q^{\pi}(s,\cdot),p\rangle-V^{\pi}(s)+h^{p}(s)-h^{\pi(\cdot|s)}(s).

(9)

The performance difference lemma is striking because it provides an exact characterization between the values of any two policies.
Historically, the advantage function without regularization seems to have first appeared in
[
18
]
, and the generalized form including regularization was shown in
[
17
]
.
In the former, it was presented as an inequality and used to establish a monotonicity-type result to show convergence to optimality.
Similarly, the performance difference lemma was used to show various convergence results in policy gradient methods
[
24
,
20
,
1
,
7
]
.
We take a different approach and use it to derive a computable measure of optimality.

2.2
Advantage gap function and distribution-free convergence

For any policy
\pi\in\Pi
,
the
advantage gap function
is the mapping
g^{\pi}:\mathcal{S}\to\mathbb{R}
defined as

\textstyle g^{\pi}(s):=\max_{p\in\Delta_{|\mathcal{A}|}}\{-\psi^{\pi}(s,p)\}.

(10)

For some common cases, the gap function can be computed exactly.
In the unregularized case of
h^{\cdot}(s)=0
, then
g^{\pi}(s)=\max_{a\in\mathcal{A}}\{-\psi^{\pi}(s,e_{a})\}
, where
e_{a}\in\mathbb{R}^{|\mathcal{A}|}
is the elementary basis vector in Euclidean space, i.e.,
g^{\pi}(s)
is the largest value in the negative advantage function.
When using a negative entropy regularizer
h^{\pi(\cdot|s)}(s)=\sum_{a\in\mathcal{A}}\pi(a|s)\ln\pi(a|s)
, then
[
4
, Section 4.4.10]
provides the closed-form solution that can be easily computed,

\textstyle g^{\pi}(s)=\log(\sum_{a\in\mathcal{A}}\mathrm{exp}\{-Q^{\pi}(s,a)+V^{\pi}(s)\})+h^{\pi(\cdot|s)}(s).

For a general convex regularization
h^{\cdot}(s)
, a closed-formed solution may not exist. We can still approximately evaluate them by solving
|\mathcal{S}|
separable, convex programs,

\textstyle\min_{p\in\Delta_{|\mathcal{A}|}}\{\langle-Q^{\pi}(s,\cdot),p\rangle+h^{p}(s)\},\quad\forall s\in\mathcal{S}.

(11)

When
h^{\cdot}(s)
is simple, i.e., prox-mappings can be computed exactly
[
5
, Section 5.3.2]
, the convex program can be efficiently solved to arbitrary accuracy
\epsilon>0
.
If
h^{\cdot}(s)
is an
L
-smooth convex function, then
O(\epsilon^{-1/2})
iterations of Nesterov’s accelerated gradient method are required
[
33
]
, where we only show dependence on
\epsilon
.
If
h^{\cdot}(s)
is a continuous convex function, then
O(\epsilon^{-1})
iterations of Nesterov’s accelerated gradient method with a smoothing technique are needed
[
31
]
. For simplicity, we assume
g^{\pi}(s)
can be computed exactly, but our forthcoming results can be extended to account for an
\epsilon
error.

We are ready to establish one of our fundamental yet simple results, which says the gap function from (
10
) can be used to estimate both upper and lower bounds on the optimality gap.

Proposition 2
.

For any policy
\pi
,

\textstyle g^{\pi}(s)\leq V^{\pi}(s)-V^{\pi^{*}}(s)

\textstyle\leq\textstyle(1-\gamma)^{-1}\max_{s^{\prime}\in\mathcal{S}}g^{\pi}(s^{\prime}).

Proof.

We first prove the lower bound.
With any
\hat{\pi}(\cdot|s)\in\mathrm{argmax}_{p\in\Delta_{|\mathcal{A}|}}\{-\psi^{\pi}(s,p)\}
, we have

\textstyle-\psi^{\pi}(s,\hat{\pi}(\cdot|s))=\max_{p\in\Delta_{|\mathcal{A}|}}-\psi^{\pi}(s,p)\geq-\psi^{\pi}(s,\pi(\cdot|s))=0.

where the last equality is by the definition given in (
9
). Therefore,

\textstyle V^{\pi}(s)-V^{\pi^{*}}(s)\geq V^{\pi}(s)-V^{\hat{\pi}}(s)

\textstyle\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\eqref{eq:per_diff}}}{{=}}\frac{1}{1-\gamma}\sum_{q\in\mathcal{S}}-\psi^{\pi}(q,\hat{\pi}(\cdot|q))\kappa^{\hat{\pi}}_{s}(q)

\textstyle\kern-42.35896pt\mathrel{\mathop{\geq}\limits^{\begin{subarray}{c}-\psi^{\pi}(s,\hat{\pi}(\cdot|q))\geq 0\\
\text{and}~\eqref{eq:visitation_measure}\end{subarray}}}-\psi^{\pi}(s,\hat{\pi}(\cdot|s)),

which by construction of
\hat{\pi}
establishes the lower bound.

As for the upper bound,
we recall
\kappa_{s}^{\pi^{*}}
from (
7
) is a distribution over states.
So,

\textstyle V^{\pi}(s)-V^{\pi^{*}}(s)

\textstyle\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\eqref{eq:per_diff}}}{{=}}\frac{1}{1-\gamma}\sum_{q\in\mathcal{S}}-\psi^{\pi}(q,{\pi^{*}(\cdot|s)})\kappa^{{\pi^{*}}}_{s}(q)

\textstyle~\leq\frac{1}{1-\gamma}\sum_{q\in\mathcal{S}}\max_{p\in\Delta_{|\mathcal{A}|}}\{-\psi^{\pi}(q,p)\}\kappa^{\pi^{*}}_{s}(q)

\textstyle~\leq\frac{1}{1-\gamma}\max_{s^{\prime}\in\mathcal{S},p\in\Delta_{|\mathcal{A}|}}\{-\psi^{\pi}(s^{\prime},p)\}.

While this paper focuses on finite state and actions, the above proposition can be easily extended to general state and action spaces
[
17
]
.
To do so, the advantage function
\psi^{\pi}(s,p)
must be modified so that it is a function of actions
a\in\mathcal{A}
instead of distributions
p\in\Delta_{|\mathcal{A}|}
. This is because for general action spaces, policies are mappings from states to actions; see
[
17
, Lemma 1]
for a concrete derivation. Let us denote such an advantage function as
\psi^{\pi}(s,a)
. Similarly, the gap function must be modified to maximize over actions in
\mathcal{A}
. Finally, the “max” in the gap function and Proposition
2
is replaced with a supremum. However, unlike the finite space setting, evaluating the gap function to arbitrary accuracy may be intractable in general space. The advantage function
\psi^{\pi}(\cdot,\cdot)
becomes a general infinite-dimensional mapping, so computing it exactly is non-trivial. More importantly,
\psi^{\pi}(s,a)
may be nonconvex in
a\in\mathcal{A}
. Understanding and addressing these issues is beyond the scope of this paper, and we refer the reader to
[
17
]
for further discussions.

We also present a similar result for the aggregation of multiple advantage functions, which will be useful in the stochastic setting where one can only estimate the advantage function.

Proposition 3
.

For a set of (possibly random) policies
\{\pi_{t}\}
,

\textstyle\sum_{t=0}^{k-1}\mathbb{E}_{\{\pi_{t}\}}[V^{\pi_{t}}(s)-V^{\pi^{*}}(s)]

\textstyle\leq\textstyle(1-\gamma)^{-1}\max_{s^{\prime}\in\mathcal{S}}\mathbb{E}_{\{\pi_{t}\}}[g^{\pi_{[k]}}(s^{\prime})],\ \forall s\in\mathcal{S},

where we define the aggregated advantage gap function as

\textstyle g^{\pi_{[k]}}(s):=\max_{p\in\Delta_{|\mathcal{A}|}}\{-\sum_{t=0}^{k-1}\psi^{\pi_{t}}(s,p)\}

(12)

Proof.

Similar to Proposition
2
,

\textstyle\sum_{t=0}^{k-1}[V^{\pi_{t}}(s)-V^{\pi^{*}}(s)]

\textstyle\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\eqref{eq:per_diff}}}{{=}}\frac{1}{1-\gamma}\sum_{q\in\mathcal{S}}\sum_{t=0}^{k-1}-\psi^{\pi_{t}}(q,{\pi^{*}}(\cdot|q))\kappa^{{\pi^{*}}}_{s}(q)

\textstyle~\kern-13.32138pt\mathrel{\mathop{\leq}\limits^{\kappa^{\pi^{*}}_{s}(q)\geq 0}}\frac{1}{1-\gamma}\sum_{q\in\mathcal{S}}\max_{p\in\Delta_{|\mathcal{A}|}}\big\{\sum_{t=0}^{k-1}-\psi^{\pi_{t}}(q,p)\big\}\kappa^{{\pi^{*}}}_{s}(q).

Since
\kappa^{{\pi^{*}}}_{s}
is a deterministic probability distribution, applying expectation yields

\textstyle\sum_{t=0}^{k-1}\mathbb{E}[V^{\pi}(s)-V^{\pi^{*}}(s)]

\textstyle\leq\frac{1}{1-\gamma}\sum_{q\in\mathcal{S}}\kappa^{{\pi^{*}}}_{s}(q)\mathbb{E}\big[\max_{p\in\Delta_{|\mathcal{A}|}}\big\{\sum_{t=0}^{k-1}-\psi^{\pi_{t}}(q,p)\big\}\big]

(13)

\textstyle\leq\frac{1}{1-\gamma}\max_{s\in\mathcal{S}}\mathbb{E}\big[\max_{p\in\Delta_{|\mathcal{A}|}}\big\{\sum_{t=0}^{k-1}-\psi^{\pi_{t}}(s,p)\big\}\big].

Clearly, when the advantage gap function is small, say
g^{\pi}(s)\leq(1-\gamma)\epsilon
for all states
s\in\mathcal{S}
, then
V^{\pi}(s)-V^{\pi^{*}}(s)\leq\epsilon
for all states, which implies
f_{\nu^{*}}(\pi)-f_{\nu^{*}}(\pi^{*})\leq\epsilon
, where we recall the
\nu^{*}
-weighted objective
f_{\nu^{*}}(\pi)=\mathbb{E}_{s\sim\nu^{*}}[V^{\pi}(s)]
.
That is, making the negative advantage function small is a sufficient condition for the
\nu^{*}
-weighted optimality gap to be small.
However, it is not necessary, because

\textstyle g^{\pi}(s)\leq V^{\pi}(s)-V^{\pi^{*}}(s)\leq(\min_{s^{\prime}}\nu^{*}(s^{\prime}))^{-1}[f_{\nu^{*}}(\pi)-f_{\nu^{*}}(\pi^{*})],\quad\forall s\in\mathcal{S},

(14)

where
\nu^{*}(s^{\prime})
can be arbitrarily small for some state
s^{\prime}
.
On the other hand, the previous propositions say making the advantage gap small at every state is necessary and sufficient for the value optimality gap
V^{\pi}(s)-V^{\pi^{*}}(s)
to be small at every state.

Therefore, an algorithm is
distribution-free
or exhibits distribution-free convergence if for every
\epsilon>0
, it outputs a policy
\pi_{k}
such that

\textstyle V^{\pi_{k}}(s)-V^{\pi^{*}}(s)\leq\epsilon,\ \forall s\in\mathcal{S},

(15)

where the iteration complexity
k
can depend on
\epsilon>0

2.3
Convex programming and duality theory of (regularized) RL

For a given distribution
\rho\in\Delta_{|\mathcal{S}|}
and policy
\pi(\cdot|s)\in\Delta_{|\mathcal{A}|}
,
we introduce the weighted visitation
\eta^{\pi}_{\rho}:\mathcal{S}\to\mathbb{R}
,

\textstyle\eta^{\pi}_{\rho}(s)

\textstyle:=(1-\gamma)^{-1}\sum_{q\in\mathcal{S}}\rho(q)\cdot\kappa^{\pi}_{q}(s),

(17)

where recall
\kappa^{\pi}_{q}
is the state-visitation vector from (
7
).
Since
\rho
and
\kappa^{\pi}_{q}
are distributions over states, then
\eta^{\pi}_{\rho}(s)\in[0,(1-\gamma)^{-1}]
for every state
s
. We next state a useful supporting result that re-writes the objective function, and its proof is deferred to Appendix

Lemma 4
.

For any policy
\pi
and distribution over states
\rho
,

\textstyle f_{\rho}(\pi)=\sum_{s\in\mathcal{S}}\rho(s)V^{\pi}(s)=\sum_{s\in\mathcal{S}}[c(s,\pi(\cdot|s))+h^{\pi(\cdot|s)}(s)]\cdot\eta^{\pi}_{\rho}(s).

Policy optimization problems solve problems of the form
\min_{\pi\in\Pi}f_{\rho}(\pi)
.
We will show an equivalent convex optimization problem, which will help derive some primal dual results.
Our problem transformation relies on the following observation
[
37
, Theorem 6.9.1]
.

Lemma 5
.

For any distribution
\rho\in\Delta_{|\mathcal{S}|}
and policy
\pi(\cdot|s)\in\Delta_{|\mathcal{A}|}
, the vector
x^{\pi}(a,s):=\eta^{\pi}_{\rho}(s)\pi(a|s)
satisfies

\textstyle\sum_{a}x(a,s)-\gamma\sum_{s^{\prime},a}\mathcal{P}(s|s^{\prime},a)x(a,s^{\prime})=\rho(s)

(18)

\textstyle\sum_{a}x(a,s)=\eta_{\rho}^{\pi}(s)

(19)

\textstyle x(a,s)\geq 0,~\forall a\in\mathcal{A},s\in\mathcal{S}.

Equivalently, (

(\hat{I}-\gamma P)^{T}x=\rho
for some matrices
\hat{I}
and

.

In view of the lemma, we consider the following (possibly nonlinear) program.
Fix some distribution
\rho\in\Delta_{|\mathcal{S}|}
and policy
\pi(\cdot|s)\in\Delta_{|\mathcal{A}|}
(e.g.,

) in

\textstyle\min_{x}~

\textstyle\{f_{\rho,\pi}(x):=\langle c,x\rangle+\sum_{s\in\mathcal{S}}\eta^{\pi}_{\rho}(s)\bar{h}^{x(\cdot,s)}(s)\}

\textstyle(\hat{I}-\gamma P)^{T}x=\rho

\textstyle x\in X(\rho,\pi):=\{x\in\mathbb{R}^{|\mathcal{A}|\times|\mathcal{S}|}|\sum_{a\in\mathcal{A}}x(a,s)=\eta^{\pi}_{\rho}(s),x\geq\mathbf{0}\},

where

takes probability distribution as inputs (the function

For the unregularized case, i.e.,

Consider the Lagrange function,
L_{\rho,\pi}(x,v):=\langle c,x\rangle+\sum_{s\in\mathcal{S}}\eta^{\pi}_{\rho}(s)\cdot\bar{h}^{x(\cdot,s)}(s)+\langle v,\rho-(\hat{I}-\gamma P)^{T}x\rangle

Lemma 6
.

For any policy
\pi

\textstyle\underline{L}_{\rho,\pi^{\prime}}(V^{\pi})

Proof.

We have

\textstyle\underline{L}_{\rho,\pi^{\prime}}(V^{\pi})

\textstyle-c(s,a)-\big[(\gamma P-\hat{I})V^{\pi}](s,a)

In view of the advantage function (

\textstyle\max_{v\in\mathbb{R}^{|\mathcal{S}|}}

\textstyle\ \rho^{T}v

\textstyle\ \max_{p\in\Delta_{|\mathcal{A}|}}\{\langle-c(s,\cdot)-[(\gamma P-\hat{I})v](s,\cdot),p\rangle-h^{p}(s)\}\leq 0,\ \forall s\in\mathcal{S}.

For the unregularized case, i.e.,

A similar duality result was shown in

3.1
Basic PMD method

We consider a basic policy mirror descent (PMD) method, as first introduced in
[
24
]
.
Starting from an arbitrarily policy
\pi_{0}
,
we compute the state-action value function

in each iteration (equivalently, one can compute the advantage function since the two are equivalent up to a constant additive factor at a fixed state

Then we solve the subproblem (

, step size
\eta_{t}
, and an arbitrary Bregman’s distance

This subproblem is referred to as the prox-mapping.
In some cases, a closed-form solution is known for (

See
[

for more details.

Recall the modulus of strong convexity parameter
\mu_{h}
from (

The following can be derived by the optimality conditions of (

[

Lemma 7
.

Let

be defined according to (

If the step size

\textstyle\langle Q^{\pi_{t}}(s,\cdot),\pi_{t+1}(\cdot|s)\rangle+h^{\pi_{t+1}(\cdot|s)}(s)+\eta_{t}^{-1}D_{\pi_{t}}^{\pi_{t+1}}(s)+(\mu_{h}+{\eta_{t}}^{-1})D_{\pi_{t+1}}^{\pi}(s)

\textstyle\leq\langle Q^{\pi_{t}}(s,\cdot),\pi(\cdot|s)\rangle+h^{\pi(\cdot|s)}(s)+{\eta_{t}}^{-1}D_{\pi_{t}}^{\pi}(s),\ \forall\pi(\cdot|s)\in\Delta_{|\mathcal{A}|},s\in\mathcal{S}.

Next, monotonicity of PMD is shown.
In the sequel, a step size

simply means Bregman’s distance

is set to 0 in the subproblem (

We skip the proof, which can be found in

Lemma 8
.

For any
\eta_{t}\in[0,+\infty)\cup\{1/0\}

V^{\pi_{t+1}}(s)-V^{\pi_{t}}(s)\leq\psi^{\pi_{t}}(s,\pi_{t+1}(\cdot|s))\leq 0

Now we show that a direct application of the PMD method achieves a sublinear rate of convergence of the value function for all states.

Theorem 9
.

Let
\eta_{t}>0
be a non-decreasing step size used in the PMD method. Then

\textstyle V^{\pi_{k}}(s)-V^{\pi^{*}}(s)\leq\frac{\sum_{q\in\mathcal{S}}\kappa^{\pi^{*}}_{s}(q)\cdot[\eta_{0}(V^{\pi_{0}}(q)-V^{\pi^{*}}(q))+D^{\pi^{*}}_{\pi_{0}}(q)]}{\eta_{0}(1-\gamma)k},~\forall s\in\mathcal{S}.

Proof.

We have for any policy
\pi(\cdot|s)\in\Delta_{|\mathcal{A}|}

\textstyle(1-\gamma)[V^{\pi_{t}}(s)-V^{\pi}(s)]

\textstyle\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\eqref{eq:per_diff}}}{{=}}\sum_{q\in\mathcal{S}}\kappa^{\pi}_{s}(q)(-\psi^{\pi_{t}}(q,\pi(\cdot|q)))

\textstyle~\kern-14.3899pt\mathrel{\mathop{\leq}\limits^{\text{Lemma~\ref{lem:pmd_descent}}}}\sum_{q\in\mathcal{S}}\kappa^{\pi}_{s}(q)[-\psi^{\pi_{t}}(q,\pi_{t+1}(\cdot|q))+\eta_{t}^{-1}D_{\pi_{t}}^{\pi}(q)-\eta_{t}^{-1}D_{\pi_{t+1}}^{\pi}(q)]

\textstyle~\kern-14.3899pt\mathrel{\mathop{\leq}\limits^{\text{Lemma~\ref{lem:pmd_monotone}}}}\sum_{q\in\mathcal{S}}\kappa^{\pi}_{s}(q)[V^{\pi_{t}}(q)-V^{\pi_{t+1}}(q)+\eta_{t}^{-1}D_{\pi_{t}}^{\pi}(q)-\eta_{t}^{-1}D_{\pi_{t+1}}^{\pi}(q)].

Fixing
\pi=\pi^{*}
and taking a telescopic sum from
t=0,\ldots,k-1

\textstyle k(1-\gamma)[V^{\pi_{k}}(s)-V^{\pi^{*}}(s)]

\textstyle\kern-14.3899pt\mathrel{\mathop{\leq}\limits^{\text{Lemma~\ref{lem:pmd_monotone}}}}(1-\gamma)\sum_{t=0}^{k-1}[V^{\pi_{t}}(s)-V^{\pi^{*}}(s)]

\textstyle\leq\sum_{q\in\mathcal{S}}\kappa^{\pi^{*}}_{s}(q)[\sum_{t=0}^{k-1}V^{\pi_{t}}(q)-V^{\pi_{t+1}}(q)+\sum_{t=0}^{k-1}\eta_{t}^{-1}D^{\pi^{*}}_{\pi_{t}}(q)-\eta_{t}^{-1}D^{\pi^{*}}_{\pi_{t+1}}(q)]

\textstyle\kern-24.88673pt\mathrel{\mathop{\leq}\limits^{\begin{subarray}{c}V^{\pi_{k}}(s)\geq V^{\pi^{*}}(s)\\
\text{and}~\eta_{t+1}\geq\eta_{t}\end{subarray}}}\sum_{q\in\mathcal{S}}\kappa^{\pi^{*}}_{s}(q)[V^{\pi_{0}}(q)-V^{\pi^{*}}(q)+\eta_{0}^{-1}D_{\pi_{0}}^{\pi^{*}}(q)-\eta_{k-1}^{-1}D^{\pi^{*}}_{\pi_{k}}(q)].

(24)

This result strengthens
[
24

by improving the convergence to be distribution free.
See (
15

Note that a similar sublinear distribution-free rate was already shown by some policy gradient methods
[

]

Now, by choosing a geometrically increasing step size, the averaged optimality gap

3.2
Distribution-free linear convergence for PMD

We will show by directly using PMD with a step size that increases geometrically at fixed intervals, then one can obtain linear convergence of the value function over any state.
We present two step sizes: one for general Bregman’s distances, and one for bounded Bregman’s distances.
Note that this result applies to general convex and strongly convex regularizers, i.e.,
\mu_{h}\geq 0
.

Theorem 10
.

Let
N:=\lceil 4(1-\gamma)^{-1}\rceil
.
By using the step size

\textstyle\eta_{t}={4^{\lfloor t/N\rfloor}\bar{D}_{0}}/\Delta_{0},

\Delta_{0}:=(1-\gamma)^{-1}\max_{s\in\mathcal{S}}g^{\pi_{0}}(s)

\textstyle V^{\pi_{t}}(s)-V^{\pi^{*}}(s)\leq 2^{-\lfloor t/N\rfloor}\Delta_{0},\ \forall s\in\mathcal{S}.

(25)

Proof.

To simplify our analysis, we say epoch
i
is the set of iterations

.
Our proof will be by mathematical induction over epoch
i
.
We will prove for any
s\in\mathcal{S}
and integer

\textstyle V^{\pi_{iN}}(s)-V^{\pi^{*}}(s)

\textstyle\leq 2^{-i}\Delta_{0}

(26)

\textstyle\sum_{q\in\mathcal{S}}\kappa^{\pi^{*}}_{s}(q)D^{\pi^{*}}_{\pi_{iN}}(q)

\textstyle\leq 2^{i}\bar{D}_{0}.

(27)

In view of Lemma
8
, then (

) implies (

For the base case of

, (

) is from Proposition
2

) is from the assumption

We consider
i+1

Applying (

\textstyle\leq\sum_{q\in\mathcal{S}}\kappa^{\pi^{*}}_{s}(q)[V^{\pi_{iN}}(q)-V^{\pi^{*}}(q)]+\frac{1}{\eta^{(i)}}\sum_{q\in\mathcal{S}}\kappa^{\pi^{*}}_{s}(q)D^{\pi^{*}}_{\pi_{iN}}(q)

The main innovation is to perform a larger geometric increase in step size at fixed intervals instead of a slower geometric increase every iteration

3.3

\textstyle\hat{\pi}_{\tau}(\bar{a}|\bar{s})

\textstyle\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\text{Lemma~\ref{lem:psi_ub_and_nonopt}}}}{{\leq}}\frac{|\mathcal{S}||\mathcal{A}|}{(1-\gamma)\rho(\bar{s})}\cdot\frac{f_{\rho}(\hat{\pi}_{\tau})-f_{\rho}(\pi^{*})}{f_{\rho}(\pi_{0})-f_{\rho}(\pi^{*})}\leq\frac{|\mathcal{S}|^{3}|\mathcal{A}|\cdot 2^{-T}}{(1-\gamma)^{2}}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\text{Choice in $T$}}}{{<}}1.

The proposition guarantees after a certain number of iterations, at least one non-optimal action will never be selected by the greedy policy.
To remove all non-optimal actions, we repeat the argument multiple times, leading to the following iteration complexity that is polynomial in only the number of states and actions for any fixed discount
\gamma
.

But before doing so, we first fix Bregman’s distance to the Euclidean distance squared,
D(\cdot,\cdot)=\frac{1}{2}\|\cdot-\cdot\|_{2}^{2}
.
It turns out this choice has several important consequences when choosing the step size and solving the subproblem (

) that make the algorithm strongly-polynomial and also efficient in practice.
Further discussions for this choice will take place after Corollary

.

Theorem 16
.

Fix Bregman’s distance to the Euclidean distance squared.
Let
N:=\lceil 4(1-\gamma)^{-1}\rceil
and
T:=\lceil\log_{2}(|\mathcal{S}|^{3}|\mathcal{A}|/(1-\gamma)^{2})\rceil+1
.
By using the step size

\textstyle\eta_{t}=\begin{cases}2^{t+1}/\Delta_{(NT+1)\lfloor t/(NT+1)\rfloor}&:~(t+1)~\mathrm{mod}~(NT+1)\neq 0\\
+\infty&:~\text{otherwise}\end{cases},

where
\Delta_{t}:=(1-\gamma)^{-1}\max_{s\in\mathcal{S}}g^{\pi_{t}}(s)
,
then for any iteration
\tau\geq|\mathcal{S}|(|\mathcal{A}|-1)(NT+1)
, the greedy policy
\hat{\pi}_{\tau}
is optimal.

Proof.

Recall epoch
i\geq 0
consists of iterations
iN,iN+1,\ldots,(i+1)N-1
(see the proof for Theorem
10
).
Similarly, we say round
\ell\geq 0
consists of epochs
i=\ell T,1+\ell T,\ldots,(\ell+1)T
.
Our goal is to show within a round
\ell

i.e., for any round
\ell\geq 0
and any epoch

within the round,

\textstyle V^{\pi_{iN+\ell\cdot(NT+1)}}(s)-V^{\pi^{*}}(s)

\textstyle\leq 2^{-i}\Delta_{\ell\cdot(NT+1)}.

(32)

By choice in the step size during round
\ell
and
\bar{D}=2
(where
\max_{s\in\mathcal{S},\pi,\pi^{\prime}\in\Pi}D^{\pi}_{\pi^{\prime}}(s)\leq\bar{D}
by choice of Euclidean norm), then one can use an argument similar to the proof for Theorem
11
to establish (
32
).

To complete the proof, we will apply Proposition
15
to every round
\ell
(the proof needs to be slightly modified, where we invoke (
32
) in place of Theorem
11
to show geometric decrease of the optimality gap w.r.t.
\Delta_{\ell\cdot(NT+1)}
).
That is, if the starting policy in a round is non-optimal, then Proposition
15
ensures that applying the greedy step size
\eta_{t}=+\infty
after the round removes (i.e., updates the probability of selecting the action to be 0) at least one non-optimal state-action pair that was not previously removed.
There are at most

non-optimal state-action pairs to remove, and each round and greedy step together involve

Theorem

implies the iteration complexity to find the optimal solution is only polynomial in

and
|\mathcal{A}|
if we assume

is a fixed constant.
This result is new for policy gradient methods.
Note that the greedy step size

corresponds to one iteration of policy iteration. The policy iteration step helps ensure the iteration complexity is independent of the so-called
gap value
,
\underline{A}^{*}:=\min_{(s,a):A^{\pi^{*}}(s,a)>0}A^{\pi^{*}}(s,a)
, which can be arbitrarily small
[
27
]
.
However, if we allow the iteration complexity to depend logarithmically on

, then one can derive an alternative iteration complexity that is (nearly) dimension-independent, with only logarithmic dependence on
|\mathcal{S}|
and
|\mathcal{A}|
. The resulting iteration complexity can possibly be smaller than Theorem
16
’s when the state and action spaces are large. We refer to
[
27
]
for more details on the gap value.

We will now show PMD is a strongly-polynomial algorithm for a fixed
\gamma
, as advertised in the beginning of this section.
Let the five-tuple
\mathcal{M}=(\mathcal{S},\mathcal{A},\mathcal{P},c,\gamma)
define the MDP, and suppose
\mathcal{M}
is rational.
Recall the length of a rational number
p/q
is
\lceil\log(p+1)\rceil+\lceil\log(q+1)\rceil+1
, and the size of the variable is the sum of the length of all its data.
Let
L:=L(\mathcal{M})
be the size of the MDP.
We write “
x
is
\mathrm{poly}(L)
” to mean the variable
x
has size that is a polynomial of
L
.
In view of Theorem
16
, we just need to show subproblem (
23
) can be solved in strongly-polynomial time. If
\pi_{t}
is rational and is
\mathrm{poly}(L)
, then so is the advantage function
A^{\pi_{t}}(s,a)=Q^{\pi_{t}}(s,a)-V^{\pi_{t}}(s)
.
This is because the value function, when viewed as the vector
V^{\pi_{t}}\in\mathbb{R}^{|\mathcal{S}|}
, is the solution to a linear system defined by
\mathcal{M}
and
\pi_{t}

[
37
, Theorem 6.1.1]
, and solving a rational linear system can be done in strongly-polynomial time
[
38
, Theorem 3.3]
.
In view of (
4
), then
Q^{\pi_{t}}
and
A^{\pi_{t}}
are rational and
\mathrm{poly}(L)
as well.
By choice of the Euclidean distance squared,
the subproblem (
23
) is equivalent to the projection problem,

\textstyle\min_{p\in\Delta_{|\mathcal{A}|}}\|p-[\pi_{t}(\cdot|s)-\eta_{t}\cdot Q^{\pi_{t}}(s,\cdot)]\|_{2},\ \forall s\in\mathcal{S}.

When the previous advantage functions are rational and
\mathrm{poly}(L)
, then the gap function
\Delta_{(NT+1)\lfloor t/(NT+1)\rfloor}
from Theorem
16
(which is defined with
A^{\pi_{(NT+1)\lfloor t/(NT+1)\rfloor}}
) is also rational and
\mathrm{poly}(L)
. Furthermore, Proposition
2
ensures
\Delta_{(NT+1)\lfloor t/(NT+1)\rfloor}
is positive whenever
\pi_{(NT+1)\lfloor t/(NT+1)\rfloor}
is non-optimal. Based on these two observations, the step size
\eta_{t}
from Theorem
16
is rational and
\mathrm{poly}(L)
whenever
\eta_{t}<+\infty
(if
\eta_{t}=+\infty
, then subproblem (
23
) corresponds to finding the largest element in
Q^{\pi_{t}}(s,\cdot)
).
So when
Q^{\pi_{t}}(s,\cdot)
and
\pi_{t}(\cdot|s)
are rational and
\mathrm{poly}(L)
, then so are the inputs to the projection problem.
Since projection onto the simplex can be done in strongly-polynomial time
[
9
]
,
this ensures
\pi_{t+1}(\cdot|s)
is rational and
\mathrm{poly}(L)
.
Thus, starting with the uniform distribution of
\pi_{0}(a|s)=|\mathcal{A}|^{-1}
,
a simple successive argument implies PMD solves MDPs in strongly-polynomial time.
We have come to the following conclusion.

Corollary 17
.

Suppose we are given an unregularized MDP problem with rational data and fixed discount factor
\gamma
. By using PMD as described in Theorem
16
and setting
\pi_{0}(\cdot|s)\in\Delta_{|\mathcal{A}|}
as the uniform distribution for all
s\in\mathcal{S}
, then PMD runs in strongly-polynomial time.

We make some remarks about using the Euclidean distance squared for Bregman’s distance.
If we instead used the KL-divergence, which does not have a fixed upper bound, it can grow exponentially within PMD, requiring the step size
\eta_{t}
to grow exponentially, as large as
2^{O(|\mathcal{S}||\mathcal{A}|)}
(see (
27
)).
As the solution to (
23
) under the KL-divergence requires computation of
\exp\{\eta_{t}Q^{\pi_{t}}(s,\cdot)\}

[
24
]
, the large step size
\eta_{t}
will incur memory that is not
\mathrm{poly}(L)
.
Another difficulty is that exponentials are irrational.
Similarly, showing strongly-polynomial runtime for solving MDPs with general regularizations may be impossible, because even solving the subproblem with negative entropy regularization involves taking an exponential.
Still, the linear distribution-free convergence from Theorem
10
yields a polynomial-time algorithm for the original problem (
5
) in the sense of nonlinear programming, where the arithmetic cost per digit of accuracy is
\mathrm{poly}(L)

[
32
]
.

This ends our tour of value convergence of PMD in the deterministic setting, i.e., when the advantage function can be computed exactly.
In the next section, we consider the more realistic stochastic setting where one can only estimate the advantage function.

4
Distribution-free convergence for stochastic PMD

We assume throughout that given a policy
\pi_{t}
, we are given an estimator
\tilde{Q}^{\pi_{t}}
generated by random vectors
\xi_{t}
instead of the true advantage function
Q^{\pi_{t}}
.
Our goal is to show the basic policy mirror descent (PMD) method also can achieve distribution-free convergence when only given
\tilde{Q}^{\pi_{t}}
.

We make the following assumption regarding the underlying noise.
It covers independent and identically distributed (iid) random data and bounded stochastic estimates, as well as non-iid with time-dependent noise (e.g., Markovian noise
[
21
]
) and noise with bounded moments.
This latter setup is more common in reinforcement learning and stochastic optimal control, where data is generated along a single trajectory and subject to some (possibly Gaussian) noise
[
16
]
.

Assumption 18
.

We have
\max_{p\in\Delta_{|\mathcal{A}|}}\|p\|\leq 1
, and
there exists
\varsigma,\sigma,\bar{Q}\geq 0
satisfying

\textstyle\|\mathbb{E}_{\xi_{t}|\xi_{[t-1]}}[\tilde{Q}^{\pi_{t}}]-Q^{\pi_{t}}\|_{*}

\textstyle\leq\varsigma

(33)

\textstyle\mathbb{E}_{\xi_{t}|\xi_{[t-1]}}\|\tilde{Q}^{\pi_{t}}-Q^{\pi_{t}}\|_{*}^{2}

\textstyle\leq\sigma^{2}

(34)

\textstyle\mathbb{E}_{\xi_{t}|\xi_{[t-1]}}\|\tilde{Q}^{\pi_{t}}\|_{*}^{2}

\textstyle\leq\bar{Q}^{2}.

(35)

The assumption on the norm is to simplify results, and it clearly holds for all
\ell_{p}
norms.
The assumption (
33
) bounds the bias, while (
34
) bounds the variance.
In the finite state and action MDP, numerous works have developed efficient methods to satisfy these assumptions
[
26
,
28
,
24
,
21
]
.

We consider another set of assumptions that are crucial to obtain high-probability results.
In contrast to assumptions (
34
) and (
35
), which rely on the second moments, this next set of assumptions bound the moment generating function, as previously appeared in stochastic optimization
[
30
,
22
]
.

Assumption 19
.

We have
\max_{p\in\Delta_{|\mathcal{A}|}}\|p\|\leq 1
, and
there exists
\varsigma,\sigma,\bar{Q}\geq 0
satisfying (
33
) and

\textstyle\mathbb{E}_{\xi_{t}|\xi_{[t-1]}}\mathrm{exp}\{{\|\tilde{Q}^{\pi_{t}}-Q^{\pi_{t}}\|_{*}^{2}}/{\sigma^{2}}\}

\textstyle\leq 2

(36)

\textstyle\mathbb{E}_{\xi_{t}|\xi_{[t-1]}}\mathrm{exp}\{{\|\tilde{Q}^{\pi_{t}}\|_{*}^{2}}/{\bar{Q}^{2}}\}

\textstyle\leq 2.

(37)

Clearly, all these assumptions are satisfied whenever both
Q^{\pi_{t}}
and
\tilde{Q}^{\pi_{t}}
are bounded almost surely.
Equipped with these assumptions, we examine the convergence properties of the stochastic PMD.

4.1
Basic stochastic policy mirror descent

Stochastic policy mirror descent (SPMD) is the same as PMD (Algorithm
1
) except the exact Q-function in (

\textstyle\pi_{t+1}(\cdot|s)

\textstyle=\mathrm{argmin}_{\pi^{\prime}(\cdot|s)\in\Delta_{|\mathcal{A}|}}\{\eta_{t}[\langle\tilde{Q}^{\pi_{t}}(s,\cdot),\pi^{\prime}(\cdot|s)\rangle+h^{\pi^{\prime}(\cdot|s)}(s)]+D^{\pi^{\prime}}_{\pi_{t}}(s)\}.

Note that the results for the remainder of the paper hold for arbitrary Bregman’s distance, such as KL-divergence.
We start with a descent lemma under noise. We skip the proof since similar results can be found in prior works like
[
17

and
[

.

Lemma 20
.

Suppose the regularization

\textstyle h^{\pi(\cdot|s)}(s)-h^{\pi^{\prime}(\cdot|s)}(s)\leq M_{h}\|\pi(\cdot|s)-\pi^{\prime}(\cdot|s)\|,\ \forall s\in\mathcal{S},\ \forall\pi,\pi^{\prime}\in\Pi.

Then for any fixed

\textstyle(1-\gamma)[V^{\pi_{t}}(s)-V^{\pi}(s)]

\textstyle\leq\mathbb{E}_{q\sim\kappa^{\pi}_{s}}[-\eta_{t}^{-1}(1+\eta_{t}\mu_{h})D^{\pi}_{\pi_{t+1}}(q)+\eta_{t}^{-1}D^{\pi}_{\pi_{t}}(q)+\eta_{t}\|\tilde{Q}^{\pi_{t}}(q,\cdot)\|_{*}^{2}+\zeta_{t}(q,\pi)]+\eta_{t}M_{h}^{2},\ \forall s\in\mathcal{S}

The following technical result will be useful to derive high probability bounds.
We defer the proof to Appendix

Lemma 21

Fix an integer

Let

Theorem 22

From these observations, we can deduce

\textstyle(1-\gamma)\sum_{t=0}^{k-1}\eta_{t}\mathbb{E}[V^{\pi_{t}}(s)-V^{\pi^{*}}(s)]

\textstyle~~~\kern-14.3899pt\mathrel{\mathop{\leq}\limits^{\text{Lemma~\ref{lem:pmd_value_descent}}}}\mathbb{E}_{q\sim\kappa^{\pi^{*}}_{s}}\big[D^{\pi^{*}}_{\pi_{0}}(q)+\sum_{t=0}^{k-1}\eta_{t}^{2}(\mathbb{E}\|\tilde{Q}^{\pi_{t}}(q,\cdot)\|_{*}^{2}+M_{h}^{2})+\sum_{t=0}^{k-1}\mathbb{E}\eta_{t}\zeta_{t}(q,\pi^{*})\big]

(40)

\textstyle~~~\kern-58.89696pt\mathrel{\mathop{\leq}\limits^{\begin{subarray}{c}\text{Choice of $\eta_{t}$,}\\
~\eqref{eq:bias_bound},~\eqref{eq:second_moment}\end{subarray}}}\bar{D}_{0}+\alpha^{2}(\bar{Q}^{2}+M_{h}^{2})+2\alpha\varsigma\sqrt{2k}.

Dividing the above by
(1-\gamma)\sum_{t=0}^{k-1}\eta_{t}=(1-\gamma)\alpha\sqrt{k}
, recalling
\sigma^{2}\geq 0
, and substituting our choice of

completes the bound in expectation.

For the second result,
we need a high probability bound on the terms in expectation within (

Recall the error term

from Lemma

To bound the sum over the terms

\textstyle\sum_{t=0}^{k-1}\eta_{t}\zeta_{t}(q,\pi^{*})

\textstyle\leq\textstyle\sigma D_{\|\cdot\|,[0,k-1]}\sqrt{3\log(4|\mathcal{S}|/{\delta})\sum_{t=0}^{k-1}\eta_{t}^{2}}

\textstyle\kern-9.95233pt\mathrel{\mathop{\leq}\limits^{\eta_{t}=\alpha/\sqrt{k}}}2\alpha\sigma\sqrt{3\log(4|\mathcal{S}|/{\delta})}

\textstyle\leq 1+3\alpha^{2}\sigma^{2}\log(4|\mathcal{S}|/\delta),\ \forall q\in\mathcal{S},

To bound the sum over the

we again apply Lemma

\textstyle\sum_{t=0}^{k-1}\eta_{t}^{2}\|\tilde{Q}^{\pi_{t}}(q,\cdot)\|^{2}_{*}

\textstyle\leq\textstyle\bar{Q}^{2}\sum_{t=0}^{k-1}\eta_{t}^{2}+12\bar{Q}^{2}\log({4|\mathcal{S}|}/{\delta})\sqrt{\sum_{t=0}^{k-1}\eta_{t}^{4}}

Plugging the resulting bounds back into (

(1-\gamma)\sum_{t=0}^{k-1}\eta_{t}=(1-\gamma)\alpha\sqrt{k}
, recalling
\sigma^{2}\geq 0
, and then substituting in our choice of

Similar to Theorem
9
, this result extends
[
17
, Theorem 3.6]
to be distribution-free in the stochastic setting.
This result also appears to be the first time distribution-free convergence under Assumption
18
.
In contrast, prior works like the variance-reduced Q-value iteration
[
41
]
show a similar distribution-free convergence with better dependence on
\gamma
, but they require a stronger oracle, where one can generate iid samples of the transition dynamic
\mathcal{P}
at any state-action pair.
Assumption
18
only requires the bias of the estimator to be small and have bounded second moments, which can be done without the stronger oracle using, for example, Monte-Carlo sampling along a single trajectory
[
28
]
.
Finally, we note the upper bound
\bar{D}_{0}
is often known when the initial policy
\pi_{0}
is the uniform distribution over actions at every state.
For example, when Bregman’s distance is the KL-divergence, then
\bar{D}_{0}=\log|\mathcal{A}|

[
24
]
.
If Bregman’s distance is induced by the negative Tsallis entropy with an entropic-index

\bar{D}_{0}=-1+|\mathcal{A}|^{1-p}

[
28
]
.

We now consider strongly convex regularizations, i.e.,
\mu_{h}>0
.
The proof is similar to when
\mu_{h}\geq 0
, except the bias is handled more carefully in the high-probability regime by showing the distance to optimality is decreasing.
Crucially, this avoids the need to shrink the feasible region

23

Due to the technical aspect of the proof, we defer it to Appendix

Theorem 23
.

Suppose Assumption
19
and (
38
) take place.
When
\eta_{t}=\frac{1}{\mu_{h}(t+1)}

\textstyle\textstyle k^{-1}\sum_{t=0}^{k-1}\mathbb{E}[V^{\pi_{t}}(s)-V^{\pi^{*}}(s)]+\frac{\mu_{h}}{1-\gamma}\mathbb{E}_{q\sim\kappa^{\pi^{*}}_{s}}\mathbb{E}[D^{\pi^{*}}_{\pi_{k}}(q)]

\textstyle\leq\frac{\mu_{h}\bar{D}_{0}+\mu_{h}^{-1}(\bar{Q}^{2}+M_{h}^{2})\log(2k)+2\varsigma k}{(1-\gamma)k},\ \forall s\in\mathcal{S},

where
\bar{D}_{0}:=\max_{s,\pi}D^{\pi}_{\pi_{0}}(s)
for an arbitrary Bregman’s distance.
Suppose instead Assumption
19
and (
38
) occur and
\varsigma\leq\sigma/k
.
Then for any
\delta\in(0,1]

\textstyle\mathrm{Pr}\big\{\exists s\in\mathcal{S}:k^{-1}\sum_{t=0}^{k-1}[V^{\pi_{t}}(s)-V^{\pi^{*}}(s)]+\frac{\mu_{h}}{1-\gamma}\mathbb{E}_{q\sim\kappa^{\pi^{*}}_{s}}[D^{\pi^{*}}_{\pi_{k}}(q)]

\textstyle\hskip 60.0pt>\frac{\mu_{h}\bar{D}_{0}+[25\mu_{h}^{-1}(\bar{Q}^{2}+M_{h}^{2})+2\sigma\sqrt{3C(k)}](\log(4k|\mathcal{S}|/\delta))^{3/2}}{(1-\gamma)k}\big\}\leq(k+1)\delta,

This result seems to be the first distribution-free
O(k^{-1})
convergence rate for MDPs with strongly convex regularization.

To summarize, this section shows distribution-free convergence, which implies the expected advantage gap function (Proposition
3
) is small.
But, since this expected value is not known exactly, it is unclear how to use it as a termination criterion.
In the next section, we provide an efficient way to provide accurate estimates of the expected advantage gap function.

5
Validation analysis and last-iterate convergence of SPMD

The main goal of this section is to show one can develop computationally and statistically efficient ways to obtain accuracy estimates of a policy generated by stochastic policy mirror descent.
We call this the
validation step
.
We provide two approaches: one with no additional samples (i.e., the online estimate) and another with a sampling complexity similar to computing the policy itself (i.e., the offline estimate).
This work is based upon
[
22
]
, but it extends the results to RL, where an important difference is that RL is nonconvex in policy space
[
1
]
.

5.1
Online accuracy certificates

We consider the following aggregate upper bound of
V^{\pi^{*}}(s)
and advantage gap function, respectively, at any time step
k\geq 0

\textstyle\textstyle{V^{*}}^{k}(s)

\textstyle:=k^{-1}\sum_{t=0}^{k-1}V^{\pi_{t}}(s)

\textstyle G^{k}(s)

\textstyle:=k^{-1}g^{\pi_{[k]}}(s)\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\eqref{eq:agg_gap_function_definition}}}{{=}}k^{-1}\max_{p\in\Delta_{|\mathcal{A}|}}\{-\sum_{t=0}^{k-1}\psi^{\pi_{t}}(s,p)\}.

(43)

\textstyle:=k^{-1}\max_{p\in\Delta_{|\mathcal{A}|}}\{-\sum_{t=0}^{k-1}\tilde{\psi}^{\pi_{t}}(s,p)\},

Our goal is to show the stochastic estimates converge at an

rate towards their exact counterpart.

k
grows, the observable upper bound
\bar{V}^{k}
and observable gap
\tilde{G}^{k}
approach, in a probabilistic sense, to their exact but unobservable counterparts.
We note that the computable optimality gap
(1-\gamma)^{-1}\max_{q\in\mathcal{S}}\mathbb{E}[G^{k}(q)]
decreases as
O((1-\gamma)^{-1}k^{-1/2})
in the worst-case, which matches the worst-case rate for the true optimality gap from Theorem
22
.

Although the computable optimality gap
{V^{*}}^{k}(s)-(1-\gamma)^{-1}\max_{s^{\prime}\in\mathcal{S}}G^{k}(s^{\prime})
provides a lower bound of
V^{\pi^{*}}(s)
at any state
s\in\mathcal{S}
,
the max operator makes the evaluation of the advantage gap function the same at every state, i.e., it is not adaptive to the state
s
, which can be over-conservative.
Below, we provide one possible alternative lower bound.
Although this may not be a valid lower bound of
V^{\pi^{*}}(s)
for every state
s
, it is close to a lower bound on average taken w.r.t. the states.
In the following, we denote
[\cdot]_{+}:=\max\{0,\cdot\}

and recall
f_{\rho}(\pi):=\mathbb{E}_{s\sim\rho}V^{\pi}(s)
and
\kappa^{\pi}_{\rho}
from (
7
).

Corollary 25
.

For any distribution
\rho
over states, we have

\textstyle\mathbb{E}_{s\sim\rho}[{V^{*}}^{k}(s)-(1-\gamma)^{-1}[G^{k}(s)]_{+}]\leq f_{\rho}(\pi^{*})+(1-\gamma)^{-1}\varepsilon_{k}(\rho),

(55)

where
\kappa^{\pi}_{\rho}(\cdot):=\sum_{s\in\mathcal{S}}\kappa^{\pi}_{s}(\cdot)\rho(s)
and
\varepsilon_{k}(\rho):=\mathbb{E}_{s\sim\kappa^{\pi^{*}}_{\rho}}[{G}^{k}(s)]-\mathbb{E}_{s\sim\rho}[[{G}^{k}(s)]_{+}]
is the overestimation error.
Moreover, under the same assumptions as Theorem
22
for the result in expectation, then

\textstyle\mathbb{E}\varepsilon_{k}(\rho)\leq\frac{4\sqrt{\bar{D}_{0}\cdot(\bar{Q}^{2}+\sigma^{2}+M_{h}^{2})}+4\varsigma\sqrt{2k}}{(1-\gamma)\sqrt{k}}.

Proof.

In view of (
13
) and
G^{k}
from (
43
), we have

\textstyle k^{-1}\sum_{t=0}^{k-1}[f_{\rho}(\pi_{t})-f_{\rho}(\pi^{*})]\leq(1-\gamma)^{-1}\mathbb{E}_{q\sim\kappa^{\pi^{*}}_{\rho}}[G^{k}(q)],

which implies (
55
).
Finally for the last inequality, we use the bound

\textstyle\mathbb{E}G^{k}(s)

\textstyle\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\eqref{eq:exact_gap}}}{{\leq}}k^{-1}\mathbb{E}\sum_{t=0}^{k-1}\max_{p\in\Delta_{|\mathcal{A}|}}\{-\psi^{\pi_{t}}(s,p)\}

\textstyle~\kern-21.74133pt\mathrel{\mathop{\leq}\limits^{\text{Proposition~\ref{prop:gap_functions}}}}k^{-1}\mathbb{E}\sum_{t=0}^{k-1}[V^{\pi_{t}}(s)-V^{\pi^{*}}(s)]\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\text{Theorem~\ref{thm:agg_convergence}}}}{{\leq}}\frac{2\sqrt{\bar{D}_{0}\cdot(\bar{Q}^{2}+\sigma^{2}+M_{h}^{2})}+2\varsigma\sqrt{2k}}{(1-\gamma)\sqrt{k}}.

Since the above holds for any state
s\in\mathcal{S}
, then the same upper bound holds when taking expectation w.r.t.
s\sim\kappa^{\pi^{*}}_{\rho}
, and we complete the proof by observing
[\cdot]_{+}
is nonnegative.

In view of
\max_{s^{\prime}\in\mathcal{S}}G^{k}(s^{\prime})\geq 0
(Proposition
3
), then at any state
s\in\mathcal{S}
,
{V^{*}}^{k}(s)-\max_{s^{\prime}\in\mathcal{S}}\frac{G^{k}(s^{\prime})}{1-\gamma}\leq{V^{*}}^{k}(s)-\frac{[G^{k}(s)]_{+}}{1-\gamma}
.
That is, the lower bound from (
55
) is tighter than the lower bound from Proposition
3
.
Moreover, (
55
) gets closer to becoming a valid lower bound as the iteration
k
increases; in the worst-case, the overestimation error is at most
O(k^{-1/2})
.

To finish, we observe Theorem
24
only says the average (over iterations) function gap – not a single policy’s function gap – can be made arbitrarily small.
While in stochastic convex optimization one can take the ergodic average to derive an optimality gap for a single solution, reinforcement learning over policy space is nonconvex.
So, similar to stochastic nonconvex optimization, one can possibly output a (uniformly) random policy for each state
[
12
,
24
]
.
However, this strategy provides no guarantees about a single policy nor its expected (over the random vectors) value.
In the next section, we argue the last iterate has meaningful convergence properties.

5.2
Last-iterate convergence

The following establishes last-iterate convergence.
We defer the proof to Appendix
C
.

Proposition 26
.

Suppose Assumption
18
and (
38
) take place.
With
\eta_{t}=\sqrt{\frac{\bar{D}_{0}}{(\bar{Q}^{2}+\sigma^{2}+M_{h}^{2})k}}
, then

\textstyle\mathbb{E}[V^{\pi_{k-1}}(s)-V^{\pi^{*}}(s)]\leq\frac{2\sqrt{\bar{D}_{0}\cdot(\bar{Q}^{2}+\sigma^{2}+M_{h}^{2})}\log(2k)}{(1-\gamma)\sqrt{k}}+\frac{4\varsigma(\sqrt{2}+8\sqrt{k})}{1-\gamma},\ \forall s\in\mathcal{S},

where
\bar{D}_{0}:=\max_{s,\pi}D^{\pi}_{\pi_{0}}(s)
for an arbitrary Bregman’s distance.
If we also have
\mu_{h}>0
and
\eta_{t}=\frac{1}{\mu_{h}(t+1)}
, then

\textstyle\mathbb{E}[V^{\pi_{k-1}}(s)-V^{\pi^{*}}(s)]\leq\frac{\mu_{h}\bar{D}_{0}+3\mu_{h}^{-1}(\bar{Q}^{2}+M_{h}^{2})\ln(2k)}{(1-\gamma)k}+\frac{4\varsigma\ln(2k)}{1-\gamma},\ \forall s\in\mathcal{S}.

The rates of convergence match those in Theorem
22
and Theorem
23
up to log factors.
The major advantage is we identified single policy – not the average over iterations – that achieves distribution-free convergence.
A limitation with last-iterate convergence is that we cannot provide the same validation analysis as with the average iterate.
This is because unless we generate more samples w.r.t. the last iterate
\pi_{k-1}
, we cannot reduce the estimation error of
\tilde{Q}^{\pi_{k-1}}
by averaging.
Nevertheless, the proposition serves a valuable purpose. It gives guarantees on the last-iterate, from a statistical perspective.
In the next section, we show how to enhance the accuracy estimate of any chosen policy, e.g. the last-iterate
\pi_{k-1}
.

5.3
Offline accuracy certificates

Consider some policy
\hat{\pi}
(e.g., a random policy or the last-iterate), and we want to assess its solution quality.
Given a set of
N
random samples
\{\xi_{t}\}_{t=0}^{N-1}
(separate from the samples used to obtain
\hat{\pi}
), we form the estimate
\tilde{Q}^{\hat{\pi}}_{t}
from each
\xi_{t}
.
The empirical value and advantage function can be defined as, respectively,
\tilde{V}^{\hat{\pi}}_{t}=\langle\tilde{Q}^{\hat{\pi}}_{t}(s,\cdot),\hat{\pi}(\cdot|s)\rangle
, and
\tilde{\psi}^{\hat{\pi}}_{t}
is defined similarly to (
9
) but with the noisy estimates.
We refer to these quantities and samples as offline, since they are computed after a policy is found.
In contrast, the online ones from subsection
5.1
are generated on-the-fly as one improves the policy.

If for example Assumption
18
takes place with some small bias
\varsigma
,
then the ergodic average

\textstyle\tilde{V}_{N}(s):=N^{-1}\sum_{t=0}^{N-1}\tilde{V}^{\hat{\pi}}_{t}(s)

(56)

provides an estimate of
V^{\hat{\pi}}(s)
.
In particular, the expected value can be bounded by
\mathbb{E}[\tilde{V}_{N}(s)-V^{\hat{\pi}}(s)]\leq\varsigma
(see (
47
)), and the deviation converges as
\mathbb{E}|\tilde{V}_{N}(s)-V^{\hat{\pi}}(s)|\leq\sigma N^{-1/2}+\varsigma
for all states, which can be shown similar to the proof for Theorem
24
.
The main difference between these aforementioned bounds and the ones from Theorem
24
is that the former are taken w.r.t. the value function for a single policy while the latter are w.r.t. the averaged (over policies) value function
\bar{V}^{k}(s)
from (
44
).
Thus, one can obtain better performance estimates on, e.g., the last-iterate policy.
Likewise, one can use the offline samples
\{\xi_{t}\}_{t=0}^{N-1}
to estimate the advantage gap function:

\textstyle\tilde{G}_{N}(s):=N^{-1}\max_{p\in\Delta_{|\mathcal{A}|}}\{-\sum_{t=0}^{N-1}\tilde{\psi}^{\hat{\pi}}(s,p)\}.

(57)

Similar to
\tilde{V}_{N}(s)
, the function
\tilde{G}_{N}(s)
is the noisy advantage gap function w.r.t. a single policy rather than the averaged (over policies) advantage gap
\tilde{G}^{k}(s)
from (
45
).

We will now show by combining both the estimated value function
\tilde{V}_{N}(s)
and advantage gap function
\tilde{G}_{N}(s)
, one can obtain estimates of the optimal value function.

Proposition 27
.

Suppose Assumption
18
and Assumption
19
take place, as well as
\varsigma\leq\sigma/(2N)
.
Denoting
[\cdot]_{+}=\max\{0,\cdot\}
, we then have

\textstyle\mathbb{E}\Big[\big[\big(\tilde{V}_{N}(s)-(1-\gamma)^{-1}\max_{s^{\prime}\in\mathcal{S}}\tilde{G}_{N}(s^{\prime})\big)-V^{\pi^{*}}(s)\big]_{+}\Big]

\textstyle\hskip 20.0pt\leq\sqrt{\frac{\sigma^{2}+N\varsigma^{2}}{N}}+\frac{2\sqrt{\bar{D}_{0}\cdot(\bar{Q}^{2}+\sigma^{2}+M_{h}^{2})}}{(1-\gamma)\sqrt{N}}+\frac{8C\sigma(\log|\mathcal{S}|+1)+4\sqrt{\sigma^{2}+N\varsigma^{2}}}{(1-\gamma)\sqrt{N}},~~~~\forall s\in\mathcal{S},

for some absolute constant
C>0
and
\bar{D}_{0}:=\max_{s,\pi}D^{\pi}_{\pi_{0}}(s)
for an arbitrary Bregman’s distance.
Suppose instead Assumption
19
occurs and
\varsigma\leq\sigma/N
.
Then for any
\delta\in(0,1]
,

\textstyle\mathrm{Pr}\big\{\exists s\in\mathcal{S}:\big(\tilde{V}_{N}(s)-(1-\gamma)^{-1}\max_{s^{\prime}\in\mathcal{S}}\tilde{G}_{N}(s^{\prime})\big)-V^{\pi^{*}}(s)>A_{N}+B_{N}\big\}\leq\delta,

where

A_{N}:=\sigma\sqrt{\frac{3\log(4|\mathcal{S}|/{\delta})}{N}}

and

B_{N}:=\frac{4\sqrt{\sigma^{2}\bar{D}_{0}}+4\sigma\sqrt{3\log(16|\mathcal{S}|/\delta)}+24\sqrt{\sigma^{2}\bar{D}_{0}}\log({32|\mathcal{S}|}/{\delta})}{(1-\gamma)\sqrt{N}}
.

Proof.

We start by bounding

\textstyle\big(\tilde{V}_{N}(s)-(1-\gamma)^{-1}\max_{s^{\prime}\in\mathcal{S}}\tilde{G}_{N}(s^{\prime})\big)-V^{\pi^{*}}(s)

\textstyle\kern-21.74133pt\mathrel{\mathop{\leq}\limits^{\text{Proposition~\ref{prop:gap_functions}}}}\big(\tilde{V}_{N}(s)-(1-\gamma)^{-1}\max_{s^{\prime}\in\mathcal{S}}\tilde{G}_{N}(s^{\prime})\big)-\big(V^{\hat{\pi}}(s)-(1-\gamma)^{-1}\max_{s^{\prime}\in\mathcal{S}}g^{\hat{\pi}}(s^{\prime})\big)

\textstyle\leq|\tilde{V}_{N}(s)-V^{\hat{\pi}}(s)|+(1-\gamma)^{-1}\max_{s^{\prime}\in\mathcal{S}}|\tilde{G}_{N}(s^{\prime})-g^{\hat{\pi}}(s^{\prime})|.

Recall the Q-function error
\delta_{t}(s,a)=Q^{\pi_{t}}(s,a)-\tilde{Q}^{\pi_{t}}(s,a)
.
Since all the terms in the last line above are nonnegative,

\textstyle\mathbb{E}\Big[\big[\big(\tilde{V}_{N}(s)-(1-\gamma)^{-1}\max_{s^{\prime}\in\mathcal{S}}\tilde{G}_{N}(s^{\prime})\big)-V^{\pi^{*}}(s)\big]_{+}\Big]

\textstyle\leq\mathbb{E}|\tilde{V}_{N}(s)-V^{\hat{\pi}}(s)|+(1-\gamma)^{-1}\mathbb{E}\max_{s^{\prime}\in\mathcal{S}}|\tilde{G}_{N}(s^{\prime})-g^{\hat{\pi}}(s^{\prime})|

\textstyle\kern-56.43748pt\mathrel{\mathop{\leq}\limits^{\begin{subarray}{c}\text{Theorem~\ref{thm:validation_analysis}}\\
\text{and}~\eqref{eq:G_deviation_penulatimate}\end{subarray}}}\frac{\sqrt{\sigma^{2}+N\varsigma^{2}}}{\sqrt{N}}+\frac{\mathbb{E}\max_{s\in\mathcal{S}}|N^{-1}\sum_{t=0}^{N-1}\langle{\delta_{t}(s,\cdot)},\hat{\pi}(\cdot|s)-u_{t}(\cdot|s)\rangle|}{1-\gamma}

\textstyle\hskip 10.0pt+\frac{\mathbb{E}\max_{s\in\mathcal{S}}|N^{-1}\sum_{t=0}^{N-1}\langle{-\delta_{t}(s,\cdot)},\hat{\pi}(\cdot|s)-v_{t}(\cdot|s)\rangle|}{1-\gamma}+\frac{2\sqrt{\bar{D}_{0}\cdot(\bar{Q}^{2}+\sigma^{2}+M_{h}^{2})}}{(1-\gamma)\sqrt{N}}

(58)

where the auxiliary variables
\{u_{t}\}
and
\{v_{t}\}
are from (
49
).
It remains to upper bound the second and third terms from the last line above.
Consider the random variable
U(s):=N^{-1}\sum_{t=0}^{N-1}\langle{\delta_{t}(s,\cdot)},\hat{\pi}(\cdot|s)-u_{t}(\cdot|s)\rangle
.
By triangle inequality and the assumption
\max_{p\in\Delta_{|\mathcal{A}|}}\|p\|\leq 1
,
|U(s)|\leq 2N^{-1}\sum_{t=0}^{N-1}\|\delta_{t}(s,\cdot)\|_{*}
.
Then we have for any
\theta>0
,

\textstyle\mathbb{E}\exp\{\theta^{-1}\cdot|U(s)|\}

\textstyle\leq\mathbb{E}\big[\exp\{2(N\theta)^{-1}\sum_{t=0}^{N-1}\|\delta_{t}(s,\cdot)\|_{*}\}\big]

\textstyle=\mathbb{E}_{\xi_{[N-2]}}\big[\mathbb{E}_{|\xi_{[N-2]}}[\exp\{2(N\theta)^{-1}\|\delta_{N-1}(s,\cdot)\|_{*}\}]\cdot\exp\{2(N\theta)^{-1}\sum_{t=0}^{N-2}\|\delta_{t}(s,\cdot)\|_{*}\}\big]

\textstyle\kern-63.61813pt\mathrel{\mathop{\leq}\limits^{\begin{subarray}{c}\text{Assumption~\ref{asmp:high_prob},~\eqref{eq:mgf_quadratic_ub},}\\
\text{and}~\varsigma\leq\sigma/(2N)\end{subarray}}}(1+1/(2N))\exp\{\frac{3\sigma^{2}}{\theta^{2}N^{2}}\}\cdot\mathbb{E}_{\xi_{[N-2]}}\big[\exp\{2(N\theta)^{-1}\sum_{t=0}^{N-2}\|\delta_{t}(s,\cdot)\|_{*}\}\big]

\textstyle\kern-45.10062pt\mathrel{\mathop{\leq}\limits^{\begin{subarray}{c}\text{Repeatedly}\\
\text{apply}~\eqref{eq:mgf_quadratic_ub}\end{subarray}}}(1+1/(2N))^{N}\exp\{\frac{3\sigma^{2}}{\theta^{2}N}\}

\textstyle\leq\sqrt{e}\cdot\exp\{\frac{3\sigma^{2}}{\theta^{2}N}\},

where the third line used the law of total expectation and the fact the sample
\xi_{t-1}
is observed after
\xi_{[t-2]}
.
Fixing
\theta=4\sigma/\sqrt{N}
, we get
\mathbb{E}\exp\{|U(s)|/\theta\}\leq 2
.
Equivalently,
\|U(s)\|_{\psi_{1}}\leq 4\sigma/\sqrt{N}
, where the sub-exponential norm of a random variable
X
is
\|X\|_{\psi_{1}}:=\inf\{t>0:\mathbb{E}\mathrm{exp}\{|X|/t\}\leq 2\}

[
45
, Section 2.7]
.
Moreover, in view of Assumption
18
,
one can use (
48
) to show
\mathbb{E}|U(s)|\leq\sqrt{4(\sigma^{2}+N\varsigma^{2})/N}
for all
s\in\mathcal{S}
.
Therefore,
it follows from
[
24
, Lemma 25]
that

\textstyle\mathbb{E}\max_{s\in\mathcal{S}}\big|N^{-1}\sum_{t=0}^{N-1}\langle{\delta_{t}(s,\cdot)},\hat{\pi}(\cdot|s)-u_{t}(\cdot|s)\rangle\big|

\textstyle=\mathbb{E}\max_{s\in\mathcal{S}}U(s)\leq\frac{4C\sigma(\log|\mathcal{S}|+1)+2\sqrt{\sigma^{2}+N\varsigma^{2}}}{\sqrt{N}},

for some absolute constant
C
.
And a similar bound can be shown with
\delta_{t}
and
u_{t}
replaced by
-\delta_{t}
and
v_{t}
, respectively.
Applying the just-derived inequalities back into (
58
), we arrive at

\textstyle\mathbb{E}\Big[\big[\big(\tilde{V}_{N}(s)-(1-\gamma)^{-1}\max_{s^{\prime}\in\mathcal{S}}\tilde{G}_{N}(s^{\prime})\big)-V^{\pi^{*}}(s)\big]_{+}\Big]

\textstyle\leq\frac{\sqrt{\sigma^{2}+N\varsigma^{2}}}{\sqrt{N}}+\frac{2\sqrt{\bar{D}_{0}\cdot(\bar{Q}^{2}+\sigma^{2}+M_{h}^{2})}}{(1-\gamma)\sqrt{N}}+\frac{8C\sigma(\log|\mathcal{S}|+1)+4\sqrt{\sigma^{2}+N\varsigma^{2}}}{(1-\gamma)\sqrt{N}},

which yields the first bound from the proposition we are trying to prove.

The high probability bound can derived by applying the high-probability bounds from Theorem
24
(which still holds when fixing a single policy rather than taking the average over policies) into (
58
).

A couple remarks are in order.
First, the offline estimate can be applied to a policy
\hat{\pi}
from any RL algorithm.
Second, the above result only bounds the expected nonnegative component because we want to ensure
\tilde{V}_{N}(s)-(1-\gamma)^{-1}\max_{s^{\prime}\in\mathcal{S}}\tilde{G}_{N}(s^{\prime})
is a lower bound of
V^{\pi^{*}}(s)
.
Third, the above proposition requires the additional Assumption
19
for the result in expectation, while Theorem
24
does not.
Without Assumption
19
, there will be an additional
|\mathcal{S}|
dependence, whereas the above result has a milder
\log|\mathcal{S}|
dependence.

7
Conclusion

We provide new convergence guarantees and validation analysis for policy mirror descent (PMD).
For the deterministic case, we introduce a novel step size that allows PMD to obtain the stronger distribution-free linear convergence.
Moreover, by incorporating our proposed advantage gap function into the step size, we improve PMD so it achieves, for the first time, strongly polynomial runtime to get the optimal solution.
For the stochastic setting, we show the stochastic PMD can also achieve the stronger distribution-free sublinear convergence, and it does so with the same constant step size as in previous developments
[
24
]
.
We also pair this convergence analysis with a novel validation analysis,
which can be used to possibly terminate policy gradient methods sooner.
This extends the validation analysis for stochastic convex optimization
[
22
]
to the challenging nonconvex landscape of policy optimization
[
1
]
.
An important future work can be to extend the advantage gap function and its analysis to more realistic general state and action spaces that appear in RL
[
17
]
.
