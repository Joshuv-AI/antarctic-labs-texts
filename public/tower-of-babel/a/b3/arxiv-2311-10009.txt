Efficient quantum algorithm to simulate open systems through a single environmental qubit Preprint: APS/123-QED
Giovanni Di Bartolomeo ⋄, Michele Vischi ⋄, Tommaso Feri ⋄, Angelo Bassi, Sandro Donadi

Abstract
We present an efficient algorithm for simulating open quantum systems dynamics described by the Lindblad master equation on quantum computers, addressing key challenges in the field. In contrast to existing approaches, our method achieves two significant advancements. First, we employ a repetition of unitary gates on a set of n n system qubits and, remarkably, only a single ancillary bath qubit representing the environment. It follows that, for the typical case of m m -locality of the Lindblad operators, we reach an exponential improvement of the number of ancilla in terms of m m and up to a polynomial improvement in ancilla overhead for large n n with respect to other approaches. Although stochasticity is introduced, requiring multiple circuit realizations, the sampling overhead is independent of the system size. Secondly, we show that, under fixed accuracy conditions, our algorithm enables a reduction in the number of trotter steps compared to other approaches, substantially decreasing circuit depth. These advancements hold particular significance for near-term quantum computers, where minimizing both width and depth is critical due to inherent noise in their dynamics.

Introduction

Quantum systems are typically presented as isolated from the surrounding environment: this is often a good approximation in simple cases, such as for single photons or single atoms, but fails in more complex situations. The theory of open quantum systems, which takes into account the effects of the environment, has a long and celebrated history, with key results such as the Linblad-Gorini-Kossakowski-Sudarshan (LGKS) master equation
Gorini et al. 1976
;
Lindblad 1976
. In this context, as standard in physics, simple problems have been well understood theoretically, while complex ones require numerical simulations.

Simulating open quantum systems allows to model and understand real-world quantum phenomena and is crucial in a variety of fields: in quantum chemistry, understanding chemical reactions at a quantum level facilitates the design of new materials and the development of novel molecules
Mohseni et al. 2008
;
Caruso et al. 2009
;
Huelga and Plenio 2013
;
McArdle et al. 2020

;
Lorenzo et al. 2015a
;
Kessler et al. 2012
;
Giorgi et al. 2013
;
Walter et al. 2014
;
Xu et al. 2014
; in quantum optical systems, they help in understanding the behavior of quantum systems made of light, such as quantum lasers and quantum teleportation systems
Bruneau 2014
;
Ciccarello 2017
;
Cilluffo et al. 2020
;
Carollo et al. 2020
; in quantum thermodynamics, they allow to explore energy exchanges, work extraction, and other thermodynamic processes in the quantum regime
Lorenzo et al. 2015b
;
Pezzutto et al. 2016
;
Karevski and Platini 2009
;
Barra and Lledó 2017
;
Lostaglio et al. 2018
;

;

;

;

;

;

;

. Finally, simulating open systems is relevant to the development of quantum technologies, including high-precision quantum sensors
Ciccarello 2017
;
Seah et al. 2019b

;

.

Quantum computers are a privileged tool for simulating open quantum systems, an idea which has gained increasing interest in recent years, showing several challenges that must be addressed. Endeavors in this direction primarily fall into two categories. The first one is based on the concept that, since the quantum devices themselves are open systems subject to noise, one can harness the inherent noise of the computer to simulate the desired open system. Recent promising results have emerged along this line, holding the potential for fruitful outcomes
Guimarães et al. 2023
;
Leppäkangas et al. 2023
;
Sun et al. 2024
. However, this approach is limited in that it can only simulate open systems described by the same master equation that characterizes the device and, moreover, building an adequate noise model of the device itself poses a significant challenge
Georgopoulos et al. 2021
;
Di Bartolomeo et al. 2023
;
Vischi et al. 2023
;
Ketterer and Wellens 2023
;
Martina et al. 2022
. The second line of research is based on the ambition that also for open systems, efficient quantum algorithms can be devised, without relying on the natural noise of the computer
Kliesch et al. 2011
;
Sweke et al. 2015
;
Childs and Tongyang 2017
;
de Jong et al. 2021
;
Cattaneo et al. 2021
;
Cattaneo et al. 2023
;
Cleve and Wang 2019
;
Kamakari et al. 2022
;
Pocrnic et al. 2024
. The present work falls within the latter framework.

In this letter we propose an efficient quantum algorithm that simulates the Lindblad dynamics of the system density matrix
\hat{\rho}_{S}(t)

Gorini et al. 1976
;
Lindblad 1976
:

\displaystyle\frac{\!\operatorname{d}\!}{\!\operatorname{d}\!t}\hat{\rho}_{S}(t)

\displaystyle=-\frac{i}{\hbar}[\hat{H}_{\text{S}},\hat{\rho}_{S}(t)]

(1)

\displaystyle+\sum_{k}\gamma_{k}\Bigl(\hat{L}_{k}\hat{\rho}_{S}(t)\hat{L}_{k}^{\dagger}-\frac{1}{2}\{\hat{L}_{k}^{\dagger}\hat{L}_{k},\hat{\rho}_{S}(t)\}\Bigr),

where
\hat{H}_{\text{S}}
is the system Hamiltonian,
\hat{L}_{k}
are the Lindblad operators that describe the effects of the environment on the system and
\gamma_{k}
are the coupling constants between the system and the environment. We reach two main significant advancements in the field.

First, we show that it is always possible to drive the open system dynamics via a repetition of unitary gates applied to a set of
n
system qubits and crucially only a single ancillary bath qubit, representing the environment. As such, the ancilla overhead is always equal to 1, meaning that it does not scale with the number
n
of qubits neither, in the case of
m
-locality of the Lindblad operators, with
m
. For a comparison, among state of the art implementations, some methods
Sweke et al. 2015
;
de Jong et al. 2021
;
Cattaneo et al. 2021
;
Cattaneo et al. 2023
;
Cleve and Wang 2019
;
Kamakari et al. 2022
use a number of ancillary qubits that scales polynomially with large
n
; our method provides a polynomial improvement, for large
n
, with respect to them. For other methods
Kliesch et al. 2011
the number of ancillary qubits scales exponentially with
m
; in this case we provide an exponential improvement.
The price to pay is stochasticity, i.e. multiple realizations of the quantum circuit, which however is preferable because the sampling overhead does not scale with the number of qubits.

Second, given a fixed accuracy, for small system-environment coupling constants, the approximations that we use allows to implement a number of trotter steps which is smaller than those required in other approaches
Cattaneo et al. 2021
;
Cattaneo et al. 2023
;
Pocrnic et al. 2024
, implying a reduction of the circuit depth.

These two advancements are important in general, and even more for near-term quantum computers where, due to their noisy dynamics, a drastic reduction of both width and depth of the quantum circuit is crucial.

II
The quantum noise formalism

Our approach is based on the quantum noise formalism
Gardiner and Zoller 2004
;
Wiseman and Milburn 2009
;
Zoller and Gardiner 1997
, that we introduce with more details in the appendix
A
below. In this framework, one considers a system in contact with an environment made of thermal baths, each with annihilation operators
\hat{b}_{k}(\omega)
.
The evolution of the state vector
\ket{\Psi(t)}
of the system plus environment is described by the following quantum stochastic differential equation (QSDE)

\displaystyle\!\operatorname{d}\!\ket{\Psi(t)}=

\displaystyle\Bigl[-\frac{i}{\hbar}\hat{H}_{\text{S}}\!\operatorname{d}\!t+\sum_{k}\sqrt{\gamma_{k}}\left(\hat{L}_{k}\,\!\operatorname{d}\!\hat{B}_{k}^{\dagger}(t)-\hat{L}_{k}^{\dagger}\,\!\operatorname{d}\!\hat{B}_{k}(t)\right)

(2)

\displaystyle-\sum_{k}\frac{\gamma_{k}}{2}\hat{L}_{k}^{\dagger}\hat{L}_{k}dt\Bigr]\ket{\Psi(t)},

where
\!\operatorname{d}\!\hat{B}_{k}(t)=\hat{b}_{\text{in},k}(t)\!\operatorname{d}\!t
with
\hat{b}_{\text{in},k}(t)=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi}}\int\!\operatorname{d}\!\omega e^{-i\omega(t-t_{0})}\hat{b}_{k}(t_{0},\omega)
.
The operators
\hat{B}_{k}(t)
can be interpreted as the quantum generalization of a Wiener process
Gardiner et al. 1985
;
Jacobs 2010
and their variances satisfy

\mathbb{E}_{\text{Q}}\Bigl[\!\operatorname{d}\!\hat{B}_{k}(t)\!\operatorname{d}\!\hat{B}_{j}^{\dagger}(t)\Bigr]=\delta_{kj}\!\operatorname{d}\!t

(3)

where
\mathbb{E}_{\tiny\text{Q}}[\,\cdot\,]\equiv\Tr_{\text{E}}(\hat{\rho}_{\text{in}}\,\cdot\,)
with
\hat{\rho}_{\text{in}}=\ket{0_\omega}_{\text{E}}\bra{0_\omega}_{\text{E}}
and
\ket{0_\omega}_{\text{E}}
is the ground state of the thermal baths;
\Tr_{\text{E}}(\cdot)
is the trace over the environment. The mean values of the other combinations of two thermal baths operators are zero.

From Eq. (
2
) one can show (see Appendix
B.1
) that the master equation for the reduced density matrix of the system
\hat{\rho}_{S}(t)=\Tr_{\text{E}}(\ket{\Psi(t)}\bra{\Psi(t)})
is the Lindblad Eq. (
1
).

III
Approximate expression of the unitary evolution

The unravelling in Eq. (
2
) acting on the full system-environment space is linear meaning that knowing the state at time
t
, the state at time
t+\Delta t
is given by

\ket{\Psi(t+\Delta t)}=\hat{N}(\Delta t)\ket{\Psi(t)},

(4)

where
\hat{N}(\Delta t)
is a matrix which is also unitary, thus implementable on a quantum computer.
As we show in Appendix
C
, the unitary evolution
\hat{N}(\Delta t)
in Eq.(
4
) can be obtained by resorting to perturbative techniques
Gardiner et al. 1985
, as Eq. (
2
) in general is not solvable in a closed form. This leads to the following approximate expression

\displaystyle\hat{N}(\Delta t)

\displaystyle=\hat{U}(\Delta t)\text{T}\big[e^{\sum_{k}\sqrt{\gamma_{k}}\hat{S}_{k}(\Delta t)}\big]

(5)

\displaystyle\simeq\hat{U}(\Delta t)\prod_{k}e^{\sqrt{\gamma_{k}}\hat{S}_{k}(\Delta t)},

where
\hat{U}(\Delta t)
is the closed system evolution operator,
\text{T}[\cdot]
is the time ordering operator and the stochastic terms
\hat{S}_{k}(t)
are defined as

\displaystyle\hat{S}_{k}(\Delta t)=\int_{t}^{t+\Delta t}\Bigl(\hat{L}_{k}(s)d\hat{B}_{k}^{\dagger}(s)-\hat{L}_{k}^{\dagger}(s)d\hat{B}_{k}(s)\Bigr),

(6)

and
\hat{L}_{k}(s)=\hat{U}^{\dagger}(s-t)\hat{L}_{k}\hat{U}(s-t)
are the Lindblad operators in interaction picture.

Under this approximation by computing
\hat{\rho}_{S}(t+\Delta t)=\Tr_{E}\big(\hat{N}(\Delta t)\ket{\Psi(t)}\bra{\Psi(t)}\hat{N}^{\dagger}(\Delta t)\big)
one recovers the following final density matrix of the system (see Appendix
D
)

\displaystyle

\displaystyle\hat{\rho}_{S}(t+\Delta t)\simeq\hat{U}(\Delta t)\biggl(\hat{\rho}_{S}(t)+\int_{t}^{t+\Delta t}\!\operatorname{d}\!s\mathcal{D}(s)\hat{\rho}_{S}(t)\biggr)\hat{U}^{\dagger}(\Delta t)

(7)

where

\mathcal{D}(s)\hat{\rho}_{S}(t)=\hat{U}^{\dagger}(s-t)\mathcal{D}\hat{\rho}_{S}(t)\hat{U}(s-t)=\sum_{k}\!\gamma_{k}\bigl(\hat{L}_{k}(s)\hat{\rho}_{S}(t)\hat{L}_{k}^{\dagger}(s)-\frac{1}{2}\bigl\{\hat{L}_{k}^{\dagger}(s)\hat{L}_{k}(s),\hat{\rho}_{S}(t)\bigr\}\bigr)
.
The approximate expression in Eq. (
7
) leads to an approximation error due to the perturbative expansion

\begin{split}\varepsilon_{p}&=\biggl\lVert\hat{U}(\Delta t)\text{T}\bigl[e^{\int_{t}^{t+\Delta t}\!\operatorname{d}\!s\mathcal{D}(s)}\bigr]\hat{U}^{\dagger}(\Delta t)\\
&-\hat{U}(\Delta t)\Bigl(\mathbb{1}+\int_{t}^{t+\Delta t}\!\operatorname{d}\!s\mathcal{D}(s)\Bigr)\hat{U}^{\dagger}(\Delta t)\biggr\rVert_{1\rightarrow 1}\end{split}

(8)

where we use the
1\rightarrow 1

superoperator norm
Kliesch et al. 2011
;
Sweke et al. 2015
. The term in the first line of Eq. (
8
) is the formal solution of the
Lindblad equation in interaction picture, where
\text{T}[\,\cdot\,]
is the time ordering operator.
\varepsilon_{p}
is quantified in Appendix
E
by assuming a
m
-locality condition, namely by considering the decomposition of
\mathcal{D}(s)
into
\mathcal{D}(s)=\sum_{j}^{K}\mathcal{D}_{j}(s)
where each
\mathcal{D}_{j}(s)
acts non trivially on a subset of
m<n
qubits. Then each
\mathcal{D}_{j}(s)
has a maximum of
2^{2m}-1
Lindblad operators. For simplicity we assume that all parameters
\gamma_{k}
have the same order of magnitude
\gamma_{k}=\gamma
. Thus, the approximation error is bounded by

\varepsilon_{p}\leq 2e\Bigl(K(2^{2m}-1)\max_{k,j}\norm{\hat{L}_{k,j}}^{2}_{\infty}\gamma\Delta t\Bigr)^{2},

(9)

where
e
is the Euler number and
K(2^{2m}-1)
is the total number of Lindblad operators which scales polynomially with the number of the system qubits
n
:
K\sim\mathcal{O}(n^{m})
(see Appendix
E
).

IV
Finite representation of the bath operators

If we wish to simulate the evolution given by Eq. (
4
) on quantum computers, a problem arises in terms of how to represent the terms
\!\operatorname{d}\!\hat{B}_{k}(t)
and
\!\operatorname{d}\!\hat{B}_{k}^{\dagger}(t)
,
which are operators acting on an infinite dimensional Hilbert space. To overcome this difficulty we proceed as follows: we
assume that at every given time
\Delta t
the system interacts only with a small portion
of the bath, and being the bath of infinite dimension, the portion with
which the system is interacting changes at each time step. Effectively we couple the system with a single ancillary qubit, representing a part of the bath, evolve the full state for
\Delta t
and then reset the bath qubit to the ground state. This last step can
be seen as changing the part of the bath connected to the system (see Fig.
1
).

This idea corresponds to selecting the following finite representation for the bath operators

\!\operatorname{d}\!\hat{B}_{k}(t)\to\hat{\sigma}_{E}^{-}\!\operatorname{d}\!W_{k}(t),\quad\!\operatorname{d}\!\hat{B}_{k}^{\dagger}(t)\to\hat{\sigma}_{E}^{+}\!\operatorname{d}\!W_{k}(t),

(10)

where
\hat{\sigma}_{E}^{-}=\ket{0}_{E}\bra{1}_{E}
,
\hat{\sigma}_{E}^{+}=\ket{1}_{E}\bra{0}_{E}
and
\!\operatorname{d}\!W_{k}(t)
are classical Wiener processes. By using the latter definitions and applying the following prescriptions

1.

Substitute
\mathbb{E}_{\text{Q}}[\,\cdot\,]
with
\mathbb{E}_{\text{C}}[\Tr_{\text{E}}(\hat{\rho}_{\text{in}}\,\cdot\,)]
, where
\mathbb{E}_{C}[\,\cdot\,]
is the average over classical stochastic processes and
\hat{\rho}_{\text{in}}=|0\rangle_{E}\langle 0|_{E}
is the ground state of the bath qubit,

2.

At each time step
\Delta t
the state is factorized as

\ket{\Psi}=\ket{\psi}_{S}\ket{0}_{\text{E}}
,

3.

The density matrix of the system has to be computed as

\hat{\rho}_{S}=\mathbb{E}_{C}[\Tr_{\text{E}}(\ket{\Psi}\bra{\Psi})]
,

one reproduces Eq. (
3
) and the master equation of the open system in Eq. (
1
). This proves that for a generic
\hat{H}_{S}
and generic Lindbald operators a single bath qubit is enough to reproduce the open dynamics. The complete derivation of the master equation is in Appendix
B.2
. Prescription 2 follows directly from the fact that in the finite dimensional representation the bath qubit must be reset after each time step to preserve Markovianity; the first and the third prescriptions are required to average over classical stochastic processes, thus to obtain the right result.

By assuming the finite representation (
10
) of the bath operators the stochastic terms in Eq. (
6
) become

\hat{S}_{k}(\Delta t)=\int_{t}^{t+\Delta t}\Bigl(\hat{L}_{k}(s)\hat{\sigma}_{E}^{+}-\hat{L}_{k}^{\dagger}(s)\hat{\sigma}_{E}^{-}\Bigr)\!\operatorname{d}\!W_{k}(s).

(11)

For convenience we define
\hat{J}_{k}(s)\equiv\hat{L}_{k}(s)\hat{\sigma}_{E}^{+}-\hat{L}_{k}^{\dagger}(s)\hat{\sigma}_{E}^{-}
. The real
[\hat{S}_{k}(\Delta t)]^{\text{\tiny}{R}}_{ij}
and imaginary part
[\hat{S}_{k}(\Delta t)]^{\text{\tiny}{I}}_{ij}
of the entries of the operators
\hat{S}_{k}(\Delta t)
in Eq. (
11
) are Itô integrals of deterministic functions
[\hat{S}_{k}(\Delta t)]^{\lambda}_{ij}=\int_{t}^{t+\Delta t}\!\operatorname{d}\!W_{k}(s)[\hat{J}_{k}(s)]^{\lambda}_{ij}
for
\lambda=\text{\tiny}{R},\tiny{I}
, that represent Gaussian stochastic processes with means zero
\mathbb{E}\big[[\hat{S}_{k}(\Delta t)]^{\lambda}_{ij}\big]=0
, variances
\mathbb{V}\bigl[[\hat{S}_{k}(\Delta t)]^{\lambda}_{ij}\bigr]=\int_{t}^{t+\Delta t}\!\operatorname{d}\!s([\hat{J}_{k}(s)]^{\lambda}_{ij})^{2}
and covariances
\mathbb{E}\bigl[[\hat{S}_{k}(\Delta t)]^{\lambda}_{ij}[\hat{S}_{k}(\Delta t)]^{\lambda^{\prime}}_{i^{\prime}j^{\prime}}\bigr]=\int_{t}^{t+\Delta t}\!\operatorname{d}\!s[\hat{J}_{k}(s)]^{\lambda}_{ij}[\hat{J}_{k}(s)]^{\lambda^{\prime}}_{i^{\prime}j^{\prime}}
.
In a quantum simulation once all the variances and covariances are computed, the stochastic processes can be sampled to get a single realization of the evolution
\hat{N}(\Delta t)
. Finally by averaging over all the realizations of the final state
\hat{\rho}_{S}(t+\Delta t)=\mathbb{E}_{C}[\Tr_{E}\big(\hat{N}(\Delta t)\ket{\Psi(t)}\bra{\Psi(t)}\hat{N}^{\dagger}(\Delta t)\big)]
, in accordance with the prescriptions, one obtains the same final density matrix in Eq. (
7
).

V
Algorithm implementation

In Alg.
1
we now describe the algorithmic implementation of the approach.

Input:

A Lindblad equation with system Hamiltonian
\hat{H}_{S}
and a set of
m
-local Lindblad operators; a number
\mathcal{N}_{r}
of samples to be performed.

Protocol:

1.

Divide the unitary evolution of the closed system
\hat{U}(T)=e^{-\frac{i}{\hbar}\hat{H}_{S}T}
in
\Delta t
time steps as
\hat{U}(T)=\prod_{j}^{N_{\text{step}}}\hat{U}(\Delta t)
where
T=N_{\text{step}}\Delta t
.;

2.

Compute
\hat{N}(\Delta t)
corresponding to
\hat{U}(\Delta t)
by using Eq. (
5
) in the finite representation of Sec.
IV
;

3.

Apply
\prod_{j}^{N_{\text{step}}}\hat{N}(\Delta t)
making sure to reset the ancilla qubit after the application of each
\hat{N}(\Delta t)
.

4.

Repeat the resulting quantum circuit of point 3 by sampling
\mathcal{N}_{r}
times the stochastic processes inside each
\hat{N}(\Delta t)
.;

Output:

Final probabilities computed by taking the average over all
\mathcal{N}_{r}
circuit realizations.

VI
Resources estimation

In order to simulate the dynamics up to time
T
making an error not greater than
\varepsilon_{global}
, our algorithm needs

\displaystyle\text{N}_{G}

\displaystyle\propto\biggl\lceil\biggl(KJ+K(2^{2m}-1)+1\biggr)\frac{T^{2}K^{2}(\gamma^{2}+\omega^{2})}{\varepsilon_{global}}\biggr\rceil

(14)

\displaystyle\sim\mathcal{O}\Biggl(\biggl\lceil\frac{n^{4m}T^{2}(\gamma^{2}+\omega^{2})}{\varepsilon_{global}}\biggr\rceil\Biggr)

total number of gates. In Eq. (
14
)
KJ
is the number of gates to implement
\hat{U}(\Delta t)
, that is polynomial in
n
for
m
-local Hamiltonians. The term
K(2^{2m}-1)
counts the number of gates needed to implement the product in Eq. (
5
), where
K
scales polynomially with
n
. The term
+1
counts the reset of the ancillary qubit. Thus,
\text{N}_{G}=\text{poly}(n,\gamma T,\omega T,1/\varepsilon_{global})
and therefore our algorithm is efficiently simulatable on a quantum computer
Kliesch et al. 2011
.

Given a fixed accuracy, our approximation (see Eqs. (
5
) and (
7
)) allows to implement a number of trotter steps which is smaller than those required in other approaches
Cattaneo et al. 2021
;
Cattaneo et al. 2023
;
Pocrnic et al. 2024
, implying a reduction of the circuit depth. See Appendix
I
for further details.

In general our ancilla overhead is constant and is always equal to one, thus of order
\mathcal{O}(1)
, as shown above, while many of the state of the art implementations
Sweke et al. 2015
;
de Jong et al. 2021
;
Cattaneo et al. 2021
;
Cattaneo et al. 2023
;
Cleve and Wang 2019
;
Kamakari et al. 2022
have an ancilla overhead proportional to the number of Lindblad operators thus in general of order
\mathcal{O}(2^{2n}-1
) where
n
is the number of system qubits or, assuming a
m
-locality condition, of order
\mathcal{O}(n^{m})
, or if the implementation is based on the vectorization of the density matrix, of order
\mathcal{O}(n)
. Other methods, such as the one presented in
Kliesch et al. 2011
, are able to use the same set of
2^{2m}
ancillary qubits for every
m
-local term in the Lindbladian, i.e. an asymptotic overhead of order
\mathcal{O}(1)
. However these methods need a number of ancillary qubits that is exponential in the
m
-locality, while our algorithm always need a single ancilla regardless of both
n
and
m
. Already in the most simple case of
m
= 2, the algorithms in
Kliesch et al. 2011
need 16 ancillas versus 1 ancilla of our method. This is a relevant achievement especially in the NISQ era.

Finally, our approach needs to evaluate multiple circuits due to the sampling of the stochastic processes and this leads to a
sampling error on the evaluation of expectation values of an
m
-local observable
\hat{O}
given by
\eta\sim\mathcal{O}(1/\sqrt{\mathcal{N}_{r}})
with
\mathcal{N}_{r}
the total number of samples of the classical stochastic processes. Thus
\eta
does not scale asymptotically with the size of the system. We give further details on
\eta
in Appendix

.

VIII
Conclusions

We have presented an algorithm based on the quantum noise formalism tailored for the efficient simulation of open quantum systems on quantum devices. Our approach allows to use a single ancilla qubit of the bath independently of the system size marking a substantial reduction in the total number of circuit qubits. Moreover our approximation, for small environment coupling constants, permits to reduce the total number of steps while maintaining the desired accuracy. For bigger values of the environment coupling constants, our approach still works and converges to the standard first order solution in
\Delta t
. Moreover, in principle, the accuracy can be improved by expanding the approximation to higher orders, see Appendices
C
and
H
.

As future work we expect to apply the algorithm on real quantum computers paired with error mitigation techniques to reduce the impact of the inherent noise of the devices. We will also explore the possibility of introducing non-Markovian effects by relaxing the prescription of resetting the bath qubit after each gate in the time step.

Appendix A
The quantum noise formalism

Our approach is based on the quantum noise formalism
Gardiner and Zoller 2004
;
Wiseman and Milburn 2009
;
Zoller and Gardiner 1997
, that here we briefly introduce. We consider the following Hamiltonian
\hat{H}=\hat{H}_{\text{S}}+\hat{H}_{\text{E}}+\hat{H}_{\text{SE}}
where
\hat{H}_{\text{S}}
is the system Hamiltonian while
\hat{H}_{\text{E}}
and
\hat{H}_{\text{SE}}
are, respectively, the environment Hamiltonian and the system-environment interaction Hamiltonian, that read

\displaystyle\hat{H}_{\text{E}}

\displaystyle=\hbar\sum_{k}\int\!\operatorname{d}\!\omega\omega\hat{b}_{k}^{\dagger}(\omega)\hat{b}_{k}(\omega),

(17)

\displaystyle\hat{H}_{\text{SE}}

\displaystyle=i\hbar\sum_{k}\int\!\operatorname{d}\!\omega\chi_{k}(\omega)\Bigl(\hat{b}_{k}^{\dagger}(\omega)\hat{L}_{k}-\hat{L}_{k}^{\dagger}\hat{b}_{k}(\omega)\Bigr),

(18)

where
\hat{b}_{k}(\omega)
is the annihilation operator of the
k
-th thermal bath,
\omega
is the frequency of the mode and
\hat{L}_{k}
is an arbitrary system operator. By using the Hamiltonian
\hat{H}
one can solve the Heisenberg equation for
\hat{b}_{k}(\omega)
and substitute the solution in the Heisenberg equation for an arbitrary operator
\hat{O}
. In the Markov approximation, namely when
\chi_{k}(\omega)=\sqrt{\gamma_{k}/(2\pi)}
, the Heisenberg equation for the operator
\hat{O}
reads
Gardiner and Zoller 2004
:

\displaystyle\frac{\!\operatorname{d}\!}{\!\operatorname{d}\!t}\hat{O}=\frac{i}{\hbar}[\hat{H}_{\text{S}},\hat{O}]+\sum_{k}\gamma_{k}\Bigl(\hat{L}_{k}^{\dagger}\hat{O}\hat{L}_{k}-\frac{1}{2}\{\hat{L}_{k}^{\dagger}\hat{L}_{k},\hat{O}\}\Bigr)+\sum_{k}\sqrt{\gamma_{k}}\Bigl(\hat{b}_{\text{in},k}^{\dagger}(t)[\hat{O},\hat{L}_{k}]+\hat{b}_{\text{in},k}(t)[\hat{L}_{k}^{\dagger},\hat{O}]\Bigr),

(19)

where
\hat{b}_{\text{in},k}(t)=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi}}\int\!\operatorname{d}\!\omega e^{-i\omega(t-t_{0})}\hat{b}_{k}(t_{0},\omega)
.

Now we consider the ground state
\ket{0_\omega}_{\text{E}}
of the thermal baths and define
\hat{\rho}_{\text{in}}=\ket{0_\omega}_{\text{E}}\bra{0_\omega}_{\text{E}}
. In this way by defining
\mathbb{E}_{\tiny\text{Q}}[\,\cdot\,]\equiv\Tr_{\text{E}}(\hat{\rho}_{\text{in}}\,\cdot\,)
the following relation holds

\mathbb{E}_{\text{Q}}[\hat{b}_{k}(\omega)\hat{b}_{j}^{\dagger}(\omega^{\prime})]=\Tr_{\text{E}}(\hat{\rho}_{\text{in}}\hat{b}_{k}(\omega)\hat{b}_{j}^{\dagger}(\omega^{\prime}))=\delta_{kj}\delta(\omega-\omega^{\prime})

and the mean values of the other combinations of two thermal baths operators are zero. We notice that if we define
\!\operatorname{d}\!\hat{B}_{k}(t)=\hat{b}_{\text{in},k}(t)\!\operatorname{d}\!t
, then by using this latter operator the only non zero mean value is

\mathbb{E}_{\text{Q}}\Bigl[\!\operatorname{d}\!\hat{B}_{k}(t)\!\operatorname{d}\!\hat{B}_{j}^{\dagger}(t)\Bigr]=\delta_{kj}\!\operatorname{d}\!t.

(20)

Thus in force of Eq. (
3
) the operator
\hat{B}_{k}(t)
can be interpreted as the quantum generalization of a Wiener process
Gardiner et al. 1985
;
Jacobs 2010
and from Eq. (
19
) one can build the following quantum stochastic differential equation (QSDE) for the state vector of the system plus environment
\ket{\Psi(t)}

\displaystyle\!\operatorname{d}\!\ket{\Psi(t)}=\Bigl[-\frac{i}{\hbar}\hat{H}_{\text{S}}\!\operatorname{d}\!t+\sum_{k}\sqrt{\gamma_{k}}\left(\hat{L}_{k}\,\!\operatorname{d}\!\hat{B}_{k}^{\dagger}(t)-\hat{L}_{k}^{\dagger}\,\!\operatorname{d}\!\hat{B}_{k}(t)\right)-\sum_{k}\frac{\gamma_{k}}{2}\hat{L}_{k}^{\dagger}\hat{L}_{k}dt\Bigr]\ket{\Psi(t)}.

(21)

Finally from Eq. (
21
) we can compute the master equation for the density matrix of the open quantum system (see Appendix
B.1
) in Eq. (
1
) that is in the Lindblad form
Gorini et al. 1976
;
Lindblad 1976
;
Breuer et al. 2002
, where
\hat{\rho}_{S}
is such that
\hat{\rho}_{S}(t)=\Tr_{\text{E}}(\ket{\Psi(t)}\bra{\Psi(t)})
.

Appendix B
Derivation of the master equation of the system

We show how to recover the Lindblad equation for the system density matrix
\hat{\rho}_{S}
, first following the standard derivation and then in the case of finite representation of the bath operators.

B.1
Standard derivation

We start from the quantum stochastic differential equation (QSDE) in the main text. For simplicity we consider to have a single Lindblad operator
\hat{L}
with coefficient
\gamma
, the generalization to more Lindblad operators being straightforward.
Starting from
\hat{\rho}_{S}=\Tr_{\text{E}}(\ket{\Psi}\bra{\Psi})
, we differentiate on both sides to get

\!\operatorname{d}\!\hat{\rho}_{S}=\Tr_{\text{E}}\bigl(\!\operatorname{d}\!\ket{\Psi}\bra{\Psi}+\ket{\Psi}\!\operatorname{d}\!\bra{\Psi}+\!\operatorname{d}\!\ket{\Psi}\!\operatorname{d}\!\bra{\Psi}\bigr).

(22)

By using the expression for
\!\operatorname{d}\!\ket{\Psi}
, its conjugate for
\!\operatorname{d}\!\bra{\Psi}
, and neglecting terms of order
\mathcal{O}(\!\operatorname{d}\!t^{2})
one gets

\begin{split}\!\operatorname{d}\!\hat{\rho}_{S}=&-\frac{i}{\hbar}[\hat{H}_{\text{S}},\hat{\rho}_{S}]\!\operatorname{d}\!t-\frac{\gamma}{2}\{\hat{L}^{\dagger}\hat{L},\hat{\rho}_{S}\}\!\operatorname{d}\!t+\sqrt{\gamma}\hat{L}\Tr_{\text{E}}\bigl(\!\operatorname{d}\!\hat{B}^{\dagger}\ket{\Psi}\bra{\Psi}\bigr)-\sqrt{\gamma}\hat{L}^{\dagger}\Tr_{\text{E}}\bigl(\!\operatorname{d}\!\hat{B}\ket{\Psi}\bra{\Psi}\bigr)+\sqrt{\gamma}\Tr_{\text{E}}\bigl(\ket{\Psi}\bra{\Psi}\!\operatorname{d}\!\hat{B}\bigr)\hat{L}^{\dagger}\\
&-\sqrt{\gamma}\Tr_{\text{E}}\bigl(\ket{\Psi}\bra{\Psi}\!\operatorname{d}\!\hat{B}^{\dagger}\bigr)\hat{L}+\gamma\hat{L}\Tr_{\text{E}}\bigl(\!\operatorname{d}\!\hat{B}^{\dagger}\ket{\Psi}\bra{\Psi}\!\operatorname{d}\!\hat{B}\bigr)\hat{L}^{\dagger}-\gamma\hat{L}\Tr_{\text{E}}\bigl(\!\operatorname{d}\!\hat{B}^{\dagger}\ket{\Psi}\bra{\Psi}\!\operatorname{d}\!\hat{B}^{\dagger}\bigr)\hat{L}-\gamma\hat{L}^{\dagger}\Tr_{\text{E}}\bigl(\!\operatorname{d}\!\hat{B}\ket{\Psi}\bra{\Psi}\!\operatorname{d}\!\hat{B}\bigr)\hat{L}^{\dagger}\\
&+\gamma\hat{L}^{\dagger}\Tr_{\text{E}}\bigl(\!\operatorname{d}\!\hat{B}\ket{\Psi}\bra{\Psi}\!\operatorname{d}\!\hat{B}^{\dagger}\bigr)\hat{L}=\\
&=-\frac{i}{\hbar}[\hat{H}_{\text{S}},\hat{\rho}_{S}]\!\operatorname{d}\!t-\frac{\gamma}{2}\{\hat{L}^{\dagger}\hat{L},\hat{\rho}_{S}\}\!\operatorname{d}\!t+\gamma\hat{L}\hat{\rho}_{S}\hat{L}^{\dagger}\!\operatorname{d}\!t\end{split}

(23)

where we used the fact that
\!\operatorname{d}\!\hat{B}\ket{\Psi}=0
at all times
Zoller and Gardiner 1997
together with the ciclicity of the trace, which implies that the only term surviving is the second term in the second line, leading to the master equation in the Lindblad form. We mention that this derivation is valid for a generic state
\ket{\Psi}
of the system-environment as far as this state is evolved from an initial state which is factorized with the bath in the ground state i.e.
\ket{\psi(0)}_{S}\ket{0_\omega}_{E}
.

B.2
Derivation in the case of finite representation of the bath operators

We now substite the finite representation of the bath operators in the main text inside the first line of Eq.(
23
) to get

\begin{split}\!\operatorname{d}\!\hat{\rho}_{S}=&\mathbb{E}_{C}\Bigg[-\frac{i}{\hbar}[\hat{H}_{\text{S}},\hat{\rho}_{S}]\!\operatorname{d}\!t-\frac{\gamma}{2}\{\hat{L}^{\dagger}\hat{L},\hat{\rho}_{S}\}\!\operatorname{d}\!t+\sqrt{\gamma}\hat{L}\!\operatorname{d}\!W\Tr_{\text{E}}\bigl(\hat{\sigma}^{+}_{E}\ket{\Psi}\bra{\Psi}\bigr)-\sqrt{\gamma}\hat{L}^{\dagger}\!\operatorname{d}\!W\Tr_{\text{E}}\bigl(\hat{\sigma}^{-}_{E}\ket{\Psi}\bra{\Psi}\bigr)\\
&+\sqrt{\gamma}\Tr_{\text{E}}\bigl(\ket{\Psi}\bra{\Psi}\hat{\sigma}^{-}_{E}\bigr)\hat{L}^{\dagger}\!\operatorname{d}\!W-\sqrt{\gamma}\Tr_{\text{E}}\bigl(\ket{\Psi}\bra{\Psi}\hat{\sigma}^{+}_{E}\bigr)\hat{L}\!\operatorname{d}\!W+\gamma\hat{L}\Tr_{\text{E}}\bigl(\ket{\Psi}\bra{\Psi}\hat{\sigma}^{-}_{E}\hat{\sigma}^{+}_{E}\bigr)\hat{L}^{\dagger}\!\operatorname{d}\!t\\
&-\gamma\hat{L}\Tr_{\text{E}}\bigl(\ket{\Psi}\bra{\Psi}\hat{\sigma}^{+}_{E}\hat{\sigma}^{+}_{E}\bigr)\hat{L}\!\operatorname{d}\!t-\gamma\hat{L}^{\dagger}\Tr_{\text{E}}\bigl(\ket{\Psi}\bra{\Psi}\hat{\sigma}^{-}_{E}\hat{\sigma}^{-}_{E}\bigr)\hat{L}^{\dagger}\!\operatorname{d}\!t+\gamma\hat{L}^{\dagger}\Tr_{\text{E}}\bigl(\ket{\Psi}\bra{\Psi}\hat{\sigma}^{+}_{E}\hat{\sigma}^{-}_{E}\bigr)\hat{L}\!\operatorname{d}\!t\Bigg].\end{split}

(24)

By using the fact that
(\hat{\sigma}_{E}^{\pm})^{2}=0
,
\hat{P}_{0}=\ket{0}_{E}\bra{0}_{E}=\hat{\sigma}^{-}_{E}\hat{\sigma}^{+}_{E}
and
\hat{P}_{1}=\ket{1}_{E}\bra{1}_{E}=\hat{\sigma}^{+}_{E}\hat{\sigma}^{-}_{E}
and that
\mathbb{E}_{C}[dW]=0
, Eq. (
24
) becomes

\begin{split}\!\operatorname{d}\!\hat{\rho}_{S}=&-\frac{i}{\hbar}[\hat{H}_{\text{S}},\hat{\rho}_{S}]\!\operatorname{d}\!t-\frac{\gamma}{2}\{\hat{L}^{\dagger}\hat{L},\hat{\rho}_{S}\}\!\operatorname{d}\!t+\gamma\hat{L}\Tr_{\text{E}}\bigl(\ket{\Psi}\bra{\Psi}\hat{P}_{0}\bigr)\hat{L}^{\dagger}\!\operatorname{d}\!t+\gamma\hat{L}^{\dagger}\Tr_{\text{E}}\bigl(\ket{\Psi}\bra{\Psi}\hat{P}_{1}\bigr)\hat{L}\!\operatorname{d}\!t.\end{split}

(25)

The Lindblad master equation is recovered by using the prescriptions of the main text. Since
\ket{\Psi}=\ket{\psi}_{S}\ket{0}_{\text{E}}
the
\hat{P}_{1}
term give a zero contribution and
\Tr_{\text{E}}\bigl(\ket{\Psi}\bra{\Psi}\hat{P}_{0}\bigr)=\ket{\psi}_{S}\bra{\psi}_{S}\bra{0}_{E}\hat{P}_{0}\ket{0}_{E}=\ket{\psi}_{S}\bra{\psi}_{S}
, recovering the Lindblad equation.

Appendix C
Perturbative solution of the QSDE

To derive the approximate solution of the QSDE in the main text we use a perturbative method known as
small noise expansion

Gardiner et al. 1985
. The following derivation is performed by using the generic expression for
\!\operatorname{d}\!\hat{B}_{t}
and
\!\operatorname{d}\!\hat{B}_{t}^{\dagger}
, but the same results apply when substituting their finite expression and the prescriptions introduced in the main text. For simplicity, let us consider the QSDE with one single Lindblad operator,

\!\operatorname{d}\!\ket{\Psi_t}=\bigg[-\frac{i}{\hbar}\hat{H}\!\operatorname{d}\!t+\epsilon\bigl(\hat{L}\!\operatorname{d}\!\hat{B}_{t}^{\dagger}-\hat{L}^{\dagger}\!\operatorname{d}\!\hat{B}_{t}\bigr)-\frac{\epsilon^{2}}{2}\hat{L}^{\dagger}\hat{L}\!\operatorname{d}\!t\bigg]\ket{\Psi_t},

(26)

where
\epsilon=\sqrt{\gamma}
. The generalization to
2^{2n}-1
Lindblad operators again being straightforward, and let us set the following perturbative expansion:

\ket{\Psi_s}=\ket{\Psi_s^0}+\epsilon\ket{\Psi_s^1}+\epsilon^{2}\ket{\Psi_s^2}+\dots

(27)

Substituting the latter into Eq.(
26
) and equating terms with the same power of
\epsilon
, up to second order we have the following system of QSDEs:

\displaystyle\operatorname{d}\!\ket{\Psi_t^0}=-\frac{i}{\hbar}\hat{H}\ket{\Psi_t^0}\!\operatorname{d}\!t

\displaystyle\operatorname{d}\!\ket{\Psi_t^1}=-\frac{i}{\hbar}\hat{H}\ket{\Psi_t^1}\!\operatorname{d}\!t+\bigl(\hat{L}\!\operatorname{d}\!\hat{B}_{t}^{\dagger}-\hat{L}^{\dagger}\!\operatorname{d}\!\hat{B}_{t}\bigr)\ket{\Psi_t^0}

\displaystyle\operatorname{d}\!\ket{\Psi_t^2}=-\frac{i}{\hbar}\hat{H}\ket{\Psi_t^2}\!\operatorname{d}\!t+\bigl(\hat{L}\!\operatorname{d}\!\hat{B}_{t}^{\dagger}-\hat{L}^{\dagger}\!\operatorname{d}\!\hat{B}_{t}\bigr)\ket{\Psi_t^1}-\frac{1}{2}\hat{L}^{\dagger}\hat{L}\ket{\Psi_t^0}\!\operatorname{d}\!s,

which has to be solved with the initial conditions
\ket{\Psi_0^0}=\ket{\Psi_0}
and
\ket{\Psi_0^j}=0
for
j>0
. The zero-th order differential equation is the deterministic equation given by the Hamiltonian evolution alone, hence its solution is simply
\ket{\Psi_t^0}=\hat{U}_{t}\ket{\Psi_0}
. The solution of the first order QSDE is:

\ket{\Psi_t^1}=\hat{U}_{t}\hat{S}_{t}\ket{\Psi_0},

(28)

where we introduced
\hat{S}_{t}:=\int_{0}^{t}(\hat{L}_{s}\!\operatorname{d}\!\hat{B}_{s}^{\dagger}-\hat{L}_{s}^{\dagger}\!\operatorname{d}\!\hat{B}_{s})
with
\hat{L}_{s}=\hat{U}_{s}^{\dagger}\hat{L}\hat{U}_{s}
. Last, the solution to the second order QSDE is

\ket{\Psi_t^2}=-\hat{U}_{t}\int_{0}^{t}\Big[\frac{1}{2}\hat{L}_{s}^{\dagger}\hat{L}_{s}\!\operatorname{d}\!s-(\hat{L}_{s}\!\operatorname{d}\!\hat{B}_{s}^{\dagger}-\hat{L}_{s}^{\dagger}\!\operatorname{d}\!\hat{B}_{s})\hat{S}_{s}\ \Big]\ket{\Psi_0}.

(29)

Then, the solution at order
\epsilon^{2}
is given by
\ket{\Psi_t}=\hat{N}\ket{\Psi_0}+\mathcal{O}(\epsilon^{3})
, where the evolution operator is
\hat{N}=\hat{U}\hat{N}^{\prime}
, with

\displaystyle\hat{N}^{\prime}=\mathbb{1}+\epsilon\hat{S}_{t}-\frac{\epsilon^{2}}{2}\int_{0}^{t}\hat{L}_{s}^{\dagger}\hat{L}_{s}\!\operatorname{d}\!s+\epsilon^{2}\int_{0}^{t}\bigl(\hat{L}_{s}\!\operatorname{d}\!\hat{B}_{s}^{\dagger}-\hat{L}_{s}^{\dagger}\!\operatorname{d}\!\hat{B}_{s}\bigr)\hat{S}_{s}.

(30)

In order to express the solution in the form given in the main text, we make use of the following equality obtained by using the Itô rule
Gardiner and Zoller 2004
;
Gardiner et al. 1985

\int_{0}^{t}\bigl(\hat{L}_{s}\!\operatorname{d}\!\hat{B}_{s}^{\dagger}-\hat{L}_{s}^{\dagger}\!\operatorname{d}\!\hat{B}_{s}\bigr)\hat{S}_{s}=\frac{1}{2}\bigg[\hat{S}^{2}_{t}+\int_{0}^{t}\!\operatorname{d}\!s\hat{L}_{s}^{\dagger}\hat{L}_{s}-\hat{C}_{t}\bigg],

(31)

where
\hat{C}_{t}=\frac{1}{2}\int_{0}^{t}\bigl[\hat{S}_{s},\hat{L}_{s}\!\operatorname{d}\!\hat{B}_{s}^{\dagger}-\hat{L}_{s}^{\dagger}\!\operatorname{d}\!\hat{B}_{s}\bigr]
. By substituting this expression into Eq.(
30
), we have to second order:

\begin{split}&\hat{N}^{\prime}=\mathbb{1}+\epsilon\hat{S}_{t}+\frac{\epsilon^{2}}{2}\hat{S}_{t}^{2}-\epsilon^{2}\hat{C}_{t}=e^{\epsilon\hat{S}_{t}-\epsilon^{2}\hat{C}_{t}}+\mathcal{O}(\epsilon^{3}).\end{split}

(32)

We can neglect the term
\epsilon^{2}\hat{C}_{t}
which in principle contributes to order
\epsilon^{2}
; this is legitimate because it is a nested Itô integral of non anticipating functions
Gardiner and Zoller 2004
;
Gardiner et al. 1985
, and hence its stochastic average is 0. In this way we obtain the same expression in Eq. (
5
).

Appendix D

\hat{\rho}_{S}(t+\Delta t)=\Tr_{E}(\hat{N}(\Delta t)\ket{\Psi(t)}\bra{\Psi(t)}\hat{N}^{\dagger}(\Delta t))\simeq\hat{U}(\Delta t)\biggl(\hat{\rho}_{S}(t)+\int_{t}^{t+\Delta t}\!\operatorname{d}\!s\mathcal{D}(s)\hat{\rho}_{S}(t)\biggr)\hat{U}^{\dagger}(\Delta t)

\begin{aligned} &\mathbb{E}_{C}\Bigl[\Tr_{E}\Bigl(\prod_{k}e^{\sqrt{\gamma_{k}}\hat{S}_{k}(\Delta t)}\hat{\rho}_{S}(0)\ket{0}\bra{0}\prod_{j}e^{\sqrt{\gamma_{j}}\hat{S}_{j}^{\dagger}(\Delta t)}\Bigr)\Bigr]=\\

Appendix E
Approximation error due to the perturbative expansion

We derive an estimate of the upper bound on the approximation error due to the perturbative expansion used in the main text. To estimate the bound we adopt the superoperator norm
\norm{\mathcal{E}}_{1\rightarrow 1}\equiv\sup_{\norm{\hat{O}}_{1}=1}\norm{\mathcal{E}(\hat{O})}_{1}

Sweke et al. 2015

\norm{\hat{O}}_{1}\equiv\Tr(\sqrt{\hat{O}^{\dagger}\hat{O}})

\mathcal{E}(\,\cdot\,)

The approximation error is quantified by assuming a
m

\displaystyle\varepsilon_{p}

\displaystyle=\norm{\hat{U}(\Delta t)\text{T}\Bigl[e^{\int_t^{t+\Delta t}\D s\mathcal{D}(s)}\Bigr]\hat{U}^{\dagger}(\Delta t) - \hat{U}(\Delta t)\Bigl(\mathbb{1} +\int_t^{t+\Delta t}\D s\mathcal{D}(s)\Bigr)\hat{U}^{\dagger}(\Delta t)}_{1\rightarrow 1}

\displaystyle=\norm{\sum_{k = 2}^{\infty}\frac{1}{k!}\int_{t}^{t+\Delta t}\dots\int_{t}^{t+\Delta t}\D t_1\dots\D t_k \text{T}\Bigl[\mathcal{D}(t_1)\dots\mathcal{D}(t_k)\Bigr]}_{1\rightarrow 1}

\displaystyle\norm{\mathcal{D}_j(s)}_{1\rightarrow 1}=

\displaystyle\sup_{\norm{\hat{O}}_{1}=1}\norm{\sum_{k=1}^{2^{2m}-1}\gamma\Bigl( (\hat{L}_{k,j}(s)\hat{O}\hat{L}_{k,j}^{\dagger}(s)-\frac{1}{2}\{\hat{L}_{k,j}^{\dagger}(s)\hat{L}_{k,j}(s),\hat{O}\}\Bigr)}_{1}

Appendix F
On the locality of the product terms in Eq. (
5
)

We notice that the Lindblad operators in general are strings of operators with the structure
\hat{L}_{k}=\hat{l}_{k}^{(\alpha)}...\hat{l}_{k}^{(\beta)}
where
\alpha
,
\beta
are integer numbers,
\hat{l}_{k}^{(q)}
with
q\in[1,...,\alpha,...,\beta,...,n]
are generic single-qubit operators applied to the q-th qubit. In the case of
m
-locality of the Lindblad equation, the number of
\hat{l}_{k}^{(q)}
in
\hat{L}_{k}
is
m<n
. Thus, by using the first order trotter-Suzuki formula
\hat{U}(\Delta t)\simeq\prod_{\alpha}e^{-\frac{i}{\hbar}\hat{H}_{\alpha}\Delta t}

\hat{L}_{k}(s)=\hat{U}^{\dagger}(s-t)\hat{L}_{k}\hat{U}(s-t)

5

\hat{L}_{k}
acts on a set
\mathbb{A}
of
m
qubits, then all the terms in
\hat{U}
that act on a set of qubits disjoint from
\mathbb{A}
, do not contribute in the expression for
\hat{L}_{k}(s)
. As a result, the locality of
\hat{L}_{k}(s)
depends only on
m
and not on
n
(in general if the locality of
\hat{H}_{\alpha}
is
l\neq m
then the locality of
\hat{L}_{k}(s)
depends on
m
and
l
). A remarkable property of the approach is that we can implement this by using the same unique ancillary bath qubit that has to be reset only after the application of all operators in the right hand side of Eq. (
34
).

Appendix I
Domain of application

The advantage of our protocol is that it is based on a perturbative expansion of the environment coupling constants for which
\Delta t
is not restricted to be infinitesimal. We provide an approximate solution of the Lindblad equation (Eq. (
7
)) which is more accurate in the interval
\Delta t
than just

\hat{\rho}_{S}(t+\Delta t)=\hat{\rho}_{S}(t)+\Bigl(-\frac{i}{\hbar}[\hat{H}_{S},\hat{\rho}_{S}(t)]+\mathcal{D}\hat{\rho}_{S}(t)\Bigr)\Delta t

(46)

that instead requires an infinitesimal
\Delta t
to be valid. Thus, given a fixed target accuracy
\varepsilon_{global}
, when the perturbative expansion holds (
\gamma<<\omega
) our approximation reduces the number of total time steps required to reach the final time
T
with respect to Eq. (
46
). This is also confirmed by the panel (a) in Fig.
2
.

Moreover, our upper bound on
\varepsilon_{global}
(Eq. (
13
)) and on the number of gates
\text{N}_{G}
(Eq. (
14
)) are smaller than those of other approaches
Cattaneo et al. 2021
;
Cattaneo et al. 2023
;
Pocrnic et al. 2024
. In particular our upper bounds contain less terms to leading order in
\gamma\Delta t
and
\omega\Delta t
.

We mention that when the characteristic times of the environmental noise and of the system Hamiltonian evolution become comparable, i.e.
\gamma\sim\omega
, the perturbative approach is not justified anymore. In this regime, Eq. (
7
) reduces to Eq. (
46
) and the stochastic exponents in
\hat{N}(\Delta t)\simeq\hat{U}(\Delta t)\prod_{k}e^{\sqrt{\gamma_{k}}\hat{S}_{k}(\Delta t)}

;

Appendix J
Sampling error

As it is known in the literature
Mølmer et al. 1993
;
Daley 2014
;
Bonnes and Läuchli 2014
, the estimate of the expectation value of an observable
\hat{O}
with finite number of realization of the stochastic processes have a sampling error of the form

\eta=|\langle\hat{O}\rangle-\langle\hat{O}\rangle_{\mathcal{N}_{r}}|=\frac{\Delta\hat{O}(\gamma,\Delta t)}{\sqrt{\mathcal{N}_{r}}}

(47)

where
\langle\hat{O}\rangle=\Tr(\hat{O}\hat{\rho}_S(t+\Delta t))
is the expected value in the limit of infinite samples, where
\hat{\rho}_{S}(t+\Delta t)=\mathbb{E}_{C}\bigl[\Tr_{E}(\hat{N}(\Delta t)\ket{\Psi(t)}\bra{\Psi(t)}\hat{N}^{\dagger}(\Delta t))\bigr]
and
\langle\hat{O}\rangle_{\mathcal{N}_{r}}=\Tr(\hat{O}\hat{\rho}_{\mathcal{N}_{r}}(t+\Delta t))
is the estimate with
\mathcal{N}_{r}
total number of samples of the classical stochastic processes where
\hat{\rho}_{\mathcal{N}_{r}}(t+\Delta t)=\frac{1}{\mathcal{N}_{r}}\sum_{k}^{\mathcal{N}_{r}}\Tr_{E}(\hat{N}_{k}(\Delta t)\ket{\Psi(t)}\bra{\Psi(t)}\hat{N}_{k}^{\dagger}(\Delta t))
. The value
\Delta\hat{O}(\gamma,\Delta t)
is the square root of the sample variance
\Delta\hat{O}^{2}(\gamma,\Delta t)=\frac{1}{\mathcal{N}_{r}}\sum_{k}^{\mathcal{N}_{r}}\Tr^{2}(\hat{O}\Tr_{E}(\hat{N}_{k}(\Delta t)\ket{\Psi(t)}\bra{\Psi(t)}\hat{N}_{k}^{\dagger}(\Delta t)))-\langle\hat{O}\rangle^{2}_{\mathcal{N}_{r}}
which is upper bounded by (see
Mølmer et al. 1993
)

\Delta\hat{O}^{2}(\gamma,\Delta t)\leq\langle\hat{O}^{2}\rangle_{\mathcal{N}_{r}}-\langle\hat{O}\rangle^{2}_{\mathcal{N}_{r}}\simeq\langle\hat{O}^{2}\rangle-\langle\hat{O}\rangle^{2}.

(48)

It can be shown
Mølmer et al. 1993
that for global operators
\hat{O}
the following relation holds

\sqrt{\langle\hat{O}^{2}\rangle-\langle\hat{O}\rangle^{2}}\sim\langle\hat{O}\rangle,

(49)

thus by using Eqs. (
47
) and (
48
) we get

\langle\hat{O}\rangle\pm\eta\leq\langle\hat{O}\rangle\biggl(1\pm 1/\sqrt{\mathcal{N}_{r}}\biggr),

(50)

showing that the numbers of samples needed to reach a target accuracy does not depend on the dimension of the system.
