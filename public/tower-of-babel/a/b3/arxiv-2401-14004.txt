Lattice-induced wavefunction effects on trapped superfluids Preprint: APS/123-QED
Yeyang Zhang

Abstract
Wavefunction effects in uncorrelated systems are characterized by the Berry curvature and quantum metric. Beyond those, we propose gauge-independent tensors describing Bloch wavefunction effects on local interaction between correlated particles. We derive an effective hydrodynamic theory for ultracold bosons in optical lattices. Ground states and collective modes of superfluids in isotropic harmonic traps are solved for highly symmetric lattices. In a dynamic process, the wavefunction effects are featured by the eigenfrequency, amplitude, and phase shift of an excited breathing mode and can be observed in experiments. We also give a tight-binding model of a bipartite square lattice with nontrivial wavefunction effects, where results are estimated with typical experimental parameters. Our discovery advances the connections between the modern band theory and quantum many-body physics.

In this paper, we derive a low-energy effective hydrodynamic theory of locally interacting bosons in an optical lattice with an additional external potential. The spatial variation of the external potential is assumed to be small compared with the lattice, so a generalized effective mass theory with a gradient expansion
Li et al. 2018
applies to the system. Distinct from the single-body external potential, in the second order of momentum, a
\phi^{4}

lattice-induced (Bloch) wavefunction effects
of the system. All of the quantities are invariant under a gauge transformation in the momentum space. One of the second-order tensors is related to inverse participation ratios
Evers and Mirlin 2008
of Bloch wavefunctions in a unit cell. Another is odd under time reversal, whose symmetry is forbidden in the quantum geometric tensor. For a three-dimensional (3D) lattice of the cubic crystal system or a two-dimensional (2D) lattice with a
\mathbb{Z}_{n}
(
n\geq 3
) rotational symmetry, the effective theory becomes isotropic when the external potential is central. Without time-reversal symmetry, the Thomas-Fermi distribution

;
Zhai 2021

Our corrections to the hydrodynamic theory stem from a different physical origin, compared with those due to quantum fluctuations, e.g. the Lee-Huang-Yang correction
Pitaevskii and Stringari 1998
;
Braaten and Pearson 1999

In a weakly interacting limit in 2D or 3D, where interaction becomes small enough while chemical potential keeps in the same order, the quantum fluctuation corrections are negligible compared with the lattice corrections. The classical hydrodynamic description is valid in this limit. The results in this paper are derived therein and can be generalized with the quantum fluctuations.

For measurable predictions, we solve collective modes in a harmonic trap

;
Pérez-García et al. 1996
;
Dalfovo et al. 1999
;
Onofrio et al. 2000

In the leading order of the gradient expansion, values of the scalar and two of the three second-order tensors describing the lattice-induced wavefunction effects can be differentiated experimentally. When the wavefunction effects are weak, they are directly linked to three observables, the frequency, amplitude, and initial phase of a breathing mode. To make a nonzero initial phase, the time-reversal symmetry needs to be broken. As a concrete example of tight-binding models, we consider a bilayer square lattice in a nonuniform synthetic magnetic field

;

;

II
Effective theory of superfluids in lattices

We start from a continuous model of bosonic atoms in
d
dimensions. The Hamiltonian of the system is given by three parts,
H=H_{0}+H_{U}+H_{I}
. Here
H_{0}
is a single-body Hamiltonian with an optical lattice, and it is diagonalized in a Bloch basis (
\hbar=1

\displaystyle H_{0}=\sum_{\mathsf{n}}\int_{\mathrm{BZ}}d^{d}\bm{k}b^{\dagger}_{\mathsf{n},\bm{k}}\epsilon_{\mathsf{n}}(\bm{k})b_{\mathsf{n},\bm{k}},

(1)

where

\mathsf{n}=0

\bm{k}=\bm{0}
, around which there is a quadratic dispersion,
\epsilon_{0}(\bm{k})\approx\frac{k^{2}}{2m}
. Bosonic operators in the real space and Bloch space are linked by Bloch wavefunctions,

\displaystyle a(\bm{r})=\sum_{\mathsf{n}}\int_{\mathrm{BZ}}d^{d}\bm{k}e^{i\bm{k}\cdot\bm{r}}u_{\mathsf{n},\bm{k}}(\bm{r})b_{\mathsf{n},\bm{k}}.

(2)

u_{n,\bm{k}}(\bm{r})
is normalized such that the average of
|u_{0,\bm{k}}(\bm{r})|^{2}
in a unit cell equals
(2\pi)^{-d}
.
H_{U}
is an applied external potential and
H_{I}
is a local repulsive interaction between atoms,

\displaystyle H_{U}=

\displaystyle\int_{\infty}d^{d}\bm{r}a^{\dagger}(\bm{r})U(\bm{r})a(\bm{r}),

\displaystyle H_{I}=

\displaystyle\frac{g}{2}\int_{\infty}d^{d}\bm{r}a^{\dagger}(\bm{r})a^{\dagger}(\bm{r})a(\bm{r})a(\bm{r}).

(3)

In cold-atom systems, it is proper to model the interaction to be short-ranged
Zhai 2021
;
Sadeghpour et al. 2000
;
Chin et al. 2010
even at the length scale of the optical lattice. The spatial variation of
U(\bm{r})
is assumed to be small compared with the lattice, such that the Fourier components of
U(\bm{r})
outside the first Brillouin zone are negligible
Li et al. 2018
. Besides, suppose only the excitation of Bloch states near the band bottom of the lowest band are considered in a low-energy effective theory, so the position index
\bm{r}
of a Wannier basis
b_{\mathsf{n}}(\bm{r})
(i.e. a Fourier transform of the Bloch basis) is approximated to be continuous in an effective theory. Those assumptions require that the typical energy and momentum scales of the external potential are much smaller than those of the chemical potential and band dispersion. Those assumptions apply to ground states and low-energy hydrodynamic collective modes with slowly varying
U(\bm{r})
(see Secs.
III
and
IV
).

Taking hydrodynamic variables, i.e.
b(\bm{r})=\sqrt{n(\bm{r})}e^{i\theta(\bm{r})}
, and letting the field variables depend on time, we get an effective action in the (coarse-grained) Wannier basis by a second-order expansion of the (quasi)momentum
\bm{k}
,

\displaystyle\mathcal{S}=\int dt[\int_{\infty}d^{d}\bm{r}(-n\partial_{t}\theta+\mu n)-H_{0}-H_{U}-H_{I}],

(4)

where the theory has been projected to the lowest band (the band index is omitted) and
\mu
is a chemical potential. The free Hamiltonian of quasiparticles reads

\displaystyle H_{0}=\int_{\infty}d^{d}\bm{r}\frac{1}{2m}\big[\frac{(\bm{\nabla}n)^{2}}{4n}+n(\bm{\nabla}\theta)^{2}\big].

(5)

Hydrodynamic theory

III.2

\bm{\nabla}n_{0}
, the antisymmetric term of Berry curvature vanishes. Then
\theta_{0}(\bm{r})
is determined by

\displaystyle[\frac{n_{0}}{m}\delta_{\alpha\beta}+\frac{g}{2}M^{\prime\prime}_{\alpha\beta}n_{0}^{2}]\partial_{\alpha}\partial_{\beta}\theta_{0}+[\frac{1}{m}\delta_{\alpha\beta}

\displaystyle\!\ \!\ +gM^{\prime\prime}_{\alpha\beta}n_{0}](\partial_{\alpha}n_{0})(\partial_{\beta}\theta_{0})=\frac{1}{2}gS_{\alpha\beta}\partial_{\alpha}\partial_{\beta}n_{0}^{2}.

(29)

When the time-reversal symmetry is broken such that
S_{\alpha\beta}\neq 0
, it is nontrivial to determine
\theta_{0}(\bm{r})
. In the remainder of the paper, we focus on a simple case, where the external potential is central, i.e.
U(\bm{r})=U(r)
. Furthermore, suppose the lattice belongs to the cubic crystal system in 3D or there is a
\mathbb{Z}_{n}
(
n\geq 3
) rotational symmetry in 2D. For those highly symmetric cases,
M^{\prime}_{\alpha}=0
,
\mathsf{g}_{\alpha\beta}=\mathsf{g}\delta_{\alpha,\beta}
,
M^{\prime\prime}_{\alpha\beta}=M^{\prime\prime}\delta_{\alpha,\beta}
,
W_{\alpha\beta}=W\delta_{\alpha,\beta}
,
S_{\alpha\beta}=S\delta_{\alpha,\beta}
;
\bm{\Omega}=\Omega\hat{\bm{e}}_{z}
for 2D and
\bm{\Omega}=\bm{0}
for 3D. The stability of the ground state without
U(r)
requires

\displaystyle\eta\equiv\frac{M^{\prime\prime}m\mu}{M}>-2.

(30)

Suppose the ground-state configuration is isotropic. Then we get the rotation-free part of the velocity for
n(\bm{r})=n(r)
,
\theta(\bm{r})=\theta(r)
,

\displaystyle\tilde{\bm{v}}

\displaystyle\equiv\bm{v}-\frac{1}{2}(\bm{\nabla}U)\times\bm{\Omega}

\displaystyle=(\frac{1}{m}+\frac{gM^{\prime\prime}}{2}n)\bm{\nabla}\theta-gS\bm{\nabla}n.

(31)

The ground state is given by
\tilde{\bm{v}}(n_{0},\theta_{0})=\bm{0}
, as a term
n\bm{\nabla}\theta\cdot(\bm{\nabla}U\times\bm{\Omega})
in the Lagrangian
\mathcal{L}
vanishes. For
\mu>U(r)
, we get

\displaystyle[\frac{1}{m}+\frac{M^{\prime\prime}}{2M}(\mu-U)]\bm{\nabla}\theta_{0}=-\frac{S}{M}\bm{\nabla}U.

(32)

According to Eqs. (
III.2
,
32
), small
\nabla U
justifies the momentum expansion of
\nabla n_{0}
and
\nabla\theta_{0}
.

Eq. (
III.2
) is the same as the Thomas-Fermi distribution
Zhai 2021
;
Li et al. 2017
(except a constant factor
M
). However, Eq. (
32
) gives a correction to the Thomas-Fermi distribution, which cannot be obtained when the interaction between atoms is treated as a mean field
Li et al. 2017
;
Price and Cooper 2013
.
\nabla\theta_{0}
becomes nonzero and in the same order as
\nabla U
. Although an equilibrium distribution of
\theta_{0}(\bm{r})
may not be observed directly, there are observable effects when the equilibrium distribution depends on time due to manipulation of the optical lattice. An observable dynamic process thereby will be discussed in Sec.
IV.2.2
.

III.3
Collective modes with external potential

Consider collective modes near the isotropic static ground state,
n(\bm{r},t)=n_{0}(r)+\delta n(\bm{r},t)
,
\theta(\bm{r},t)=\theta_{0}(r)+\delta\theta(\bm{r},t)
. We take the double expansion of derivatives of
U
and
\delta\theta
in Eqs. (
III
,
III
), and calculate leading-order equations of motion. Similar to the case of
U(r)=0
,
\delta n
is in the same order as
\partial_{t}\delta\theta
,

\displaystyle\partial_{t}\delta\theta=-gM\delta n,

(33)

where we have used the condition that
\partial_{t}\delta\theta=0
when
\delta\theta=\delta n=0
. For
\mu>U(r)
, Eq. (
21
) gives

\displaystyle\partial_{t}^{2}\delta n=

\displaystyle-\bm{\nabla}\cdot(n_{0}\partial_{t}\bm{v})-\bm{\nabla}\cdot(\delta n\partial_{t}\bm{v})

\displaystyle-\bm{\nabla}\cdot[(\partial_{t}\delta n)\frac{1}{2}(\bm{\nabla}U)\times\bm{\Omega}]-\bm{\nabla}\cdot[(\partial_{t}\delta n)\tilde{\bm{v}}]

\displaystyle\approx

\displaystyle-\bm{\nabla}\cdot(n_{0}\partial_{t}\bm{v}).

(34)

Here the first term on the right-hand side is in the same order as
\partial_{t}^{2}\delta n
, which will be verified in Eq. (
III.3
). The second term is neglected because
\delta n
makes it one order higher than
\partial_{t}^{2}\delta n
. The third term is neglect because
\bm{\nabla}U
makes it one order higher than
\partial_{t}^{2}\delta n
, although a correction of the Berry curvature was discussed in previous works
Li et al. 2017
;
Price and Cooper 2013
. The last term is neglected because
\tilde{\bm{v}}(n_{0},\theta_{0})=0
, so terms in
\tilde{\bm{v}}
are proportional to
\nabla\delta\theta
or
\delta n
, which makes the last term one order higher than
\partial_{t}^{2}\delta n
. Then for
\mu>U(r)
,
\delta n(\bm{r},t)
is given by

\displaystyle\partial_{t}^{2}\delta n\approx

\displaystyle-\bm{\nabla}\cdot[\frac{\mu-U}{gM}(\frac{1}{m}\bm{\nabla}\partial_{t}\delta\theta+\frac{g}{2}M^{\prime\prime}\frac{\mu-U}{gM}\bm{\nabla}\partial_{t}\delta\theta)]

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\bm{\nabla}\cdot(\mu-U)[\frac{1}{m}\bm{\nabla}\delta n+\frac{M^{\prime\prime}}{2M}(\mu-U)\bm{\nabla}\delta n]

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{\mu-U}{m}[1+\frac{\eta}{2}(1-\frac{U}{\mu})]\nabla^{2}\delta n

\displaystyle-\frac{\bm{\nabla}U}{m}\cdot[1+\eta(1-\frac{U}{\mu})]\bm{\nabla}\delta n.

(35)

Here we also neglected the terms proportional to
\bm{\nabla}\partial_{t}\delta n
and
(\partial_{t}\delta n)\bm{\nabla}\delta\theta
in
\partial_{t}\bm{v}
, because their orders are one higher than
\bm{\nabla}\partial_{t}\delta\theta
. Compared with Eqs. (
26
,
III.2
), we know that the first term in Eq. (
III.3
) is to replace the constant density
n_{0}
by the density distribution
n_{0}(\bm{r})
. The two terms in Eq. (
III.3
) are both important when the momentum scales of
U
and
\delta n
are in the same order.

III.4
Discussion on quantum fluctuations

In this section, we derive the lattice-induced corrections to the hydrodynamic theory in the classical-field limit, i.e.
\delta\mathcal{S}=0
. Besides, quantum fluctuations may also give corrections. Typically, the quantum-fluctuation corrections are small (e.g. about 1%)
Pitaevskii and Stringari 1998
;
Braaten and Pearson 1999
. However, it should be clarified in what limit our treatment is justified. The ratio between the quantum correction to the particle density
n_{\mathrm{q}}
and classical density
n_{\mathrm{cl}}
vanish in a weakly interacting limit (
d=2,3
),

\displaystyle\frac{n_{\mathrm{q}}}{n_{\mathrm{cl}}}\sim\frac{\xi_{h}^{-d}}{\mu/g}\sim\frac{(m\mu)^{\frac{d}{2}}}{\mu/g}=m^{\frac{d}{2}}\mu^{\frac{d}{2}-1}g\rightarrow 0,

(36)

where the particle density
n=n_{\mathrm{cl}}+n_{\mathrm{q}}
, and
\xi_{h}
is the typical length scale (i.e. healing length
Zhai 2021
) of the superfluid state. Eq. (
36
) guarantees the applicability of the hydrodynamic theory. On the other hand, the lattice-induced corrections may survive in this limit. Suppose the interaction strength
g
decreases while the particle density
n
increases, such that the chemical potential
\mu
keeps in the same order. Then from the classical equations of motion Eqs. (
III
,
III
), or from the results Eqs. (
26
,
III.2
,
32
,
III.3
), we can see that the lattice-induced corrections remain in the same order, despite the suppression of the quantum corrections. Therefore, it is fair to stay on this limit and neglect the quantum fluctuations in this paper. Our derivations can be generalized when further taking the quantum fluctuations into consideration.

IV
Superfluid dynamics in a harmonic trap

In cold-atom systems,
U(r)
is commonly taken as an isotropic harmonic potential
Zhai 2021
,

\displaystyle U(r)=\frac{1}{2}m\omega_{0}^{2}r^{2}\equiv\mu\frac{r^{2}}{R^{2}}.

(37)

Here
\omega_{0}\ll\mu\lesssim J
and
k_{0}\equiv\frac{1}{R}\ll\sqrt{m\mu}\lesssim k_{M}
such that the gradient expansion (i.e. momentum and frequency expansion) is applicable for
r<R
.
J
is the bandwidth of the lowest band and
k_{M}
is the momentum range of the first Broullion zone.

IV.1
Collective modes

Define
\rho(\frac{\bm{r}}{R},t)\equiv\delta n(\bm{r},t)
. Solutions of Eqs. (
III.3
,
37
) depend on
\eta
. For the 3D case with spherical coordinates
\bm{r}=(r,\theta,\phi)
,

\displaystyle\rho(\bm{r},t)=\sum_{\mathsf{n},\ell,\mathsf{m}}\sum_{\xi=\pm}e^{-i\xi\omega_{\mathsf{n},\ell}t}\mathsf{a}_{\mathsf{n},\ell,\mathsf{m},\xi}\rho_{\mathsf{n},\ell}(r)Y_{\ell,\mathsf{m}}(\theta,\phi),

(38)

where
Y_{\ell,m}
are spherical harmonics. Then we get a one-dimensional eigenequation for
\rho_{\mathsf{n},\ell}(r)
with
0<r<1
,

\displaystyle-\omega_{\mathsf{n},\ell}^{2}\rho_{\mathsf{n},\ell}=\frac{1}{2}\omega_{0}^{2}(1-r^{2})[1+\frac{\eta}{2}(1-r^{2})][\frac{d^{2}}{dr^{2}}+\frac{2}{r}\frac{d}{dr}

\displaystyle\!\ \!\ \!\ \!\ -\frac{\ell(\ell+1)}{r^{2}}]\rho_{\mathsf{n},\ell}-\omega^{2}_{0}r[1+\eta(1-r^{2})]\frac{d}{dr}\rho_{\mathsf{n},\ell}.

(39)

For the 2D case with polar coordinates
\bm{r}=(r,\phi)
,

\displaystyle\rho(\bm{r},t)=\sum_{\mathsf{n},\mathsf{m}}\sum_{\xi=\pm}e^{-i\xi\omega_{\mathsf{n},|\mathsf{m}|}t}\mathsf{a}_{\mathsf{n},\mathsf{m},\xi}\rho_{\mathsf{n},|\mathsf{m}|}(r)e^{i\mathsf{m}\phi}.

(40)

Then we get

\displaystyle-\omega_{\mathsf{n},|\mathsf{m}|}^{2}\rho_{\mathsf{n},|\mathsf{m}|}=\frac{1}{2}\omega_{0}^{2}(1-r^{2})[1+\frac{\eta}{2}(1-r^{2})][\frac{d^{2}}{dr^{2}}+\frac{1}{r}\frac{d}{dr}

\displaystyle\!\ \!\ \!\ \!\ -\frac{m^{2}}{r^{2}}]\rho_{\mathsf{n},|\mathsf{m}|}-\omega^{2}_{0}r[1+\eta(1-r^{2})]\frac{d}{dr}\rho_{\mathsf{n},|\mathsf{m}|}.

(41)

In Eqs. (
38
,
40
),
\mathsf{n}
takes nonnegative integers.

When
\eta\neq 0
, eigenfrequencies (eigenenergies)
\omega_{\mathsf{n},\mathsf{j}}
cannot be obtained analytically, where
\mathsf{j}\equiv\ell
for
d=3
and
\mathsf{j}\equiv|\mathsf{m}|
for
d=2
. Nevertheless, when
\eta>-1
, solutions take the form of an infinite series,

\displaystyle\rho_{\mathsf{n},\mathsf{j}}(r)=\sum_{\mathsf{k}=0}^{\infty}\alpha_{\mathsf{n},\mathsf{j},2\mathsf{k}}r^{2\mathsf{k}+\mathsf{j}},

(42)

and the eigenfrequencies are solvable numerically
SM2
. According to an asymptotic analysis of
\alpha_{\mathsf{n},\mathsf{j},2\mathsf{k}}
(see Appendix
B.2
), we can iteratively determine
\frac{\alpha_{\mathsf{n},\mathsf{j},2\mathsf{k}+2}}{\alpha_{\mathsf{n},\mathsf{j},2\mathsf{k}}}
as functions of
\omega_{\mathsf{n},\mathsf{j}}
for
\mathsf{k}\leq\mathsf{k}_{0}
, and obtain
\omega_{\mathsf{n},\mathsf{j}}
(see Fig.
1
) by requiring

\displaystyle\frac{\alpha_{\mathsf{n},\mathsf{j},2\mathsf{k}_{0}+2}}{\alpha_{\mathsf{n},\mathsf{j},2\mathsf{k}_{0}}}=\frac{\mathsf{k}_{0}}{\mathsf{k}_{0}+1}\frac{\eta}{\eta+2},

(43)

with a certain integer
\mathsf{k}_{0}
large enough for convergence. For several low-energy levels, i.e.
\mathsf{n}
and
\mathsf{j}
are not large, the eigenfrequency
\omega_{\mathsf{n},\mathsf{j}}
is in the same order as
\omega_{0}
. According to Eqs. (
33
,
III.3
) with
\omega_{0}\ll\mu
, this justifies the momentum and frequency expansion of
\delta n_{0}
and
\delta\theta_{0}
for the low-energy collective modes. When
\eta=0
, Eq. (
43
) returns to the condition that the series becomes finite
Stringari 1996
, and the numerical results in Fig.
1
are consistent with those without the lattice.

Besides, collective modes are normalizable and satisfy an orthonormal relation
Mathews and Walker 1971
(see Appendix
B.3
for a proof),

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{1}r^{d-1}\rho_{\mathsf{n}_{1},\mathsf{j}}^{*}(r)\rho_{\mathsf{n}_{2},\mathsf{j}}(r)dr=\delta_{\mathsf{n}_{1},\mathsf{n}_{2}}.

(44)

The orthonormal condition is independent of
\eta
, which enables perturbative solutions of the collective modes in Sec.
IV.2.1
.

IV.2
Breathing mode with weak lattice-induced wavefunction effects

From Eqs. (
III.2
,
30
,
32
) and Fig.
1
, in the leading order of the gradient expansion, we know
M_{d}\equiv M-1
,
M^{\prime\prime}
, and
S
are already involved in the dynamics of the superfluid in the isotropic harmonic trap
SM3
. To demonstrate more explicit relations between the three Bloch wavefunction quantities and experimental observables, suppose the lattice-induced wavefunction effects are weak,

\displaystyle M_{d}\equiv M-1\ll 1,\quad m\mu|M^{\prime\prime}|\ll 1,\quad m\mu|S|\ll 1.

(45)

The last condition in Eq. (
45
) can be weakened and substituted by
m\omega_{0}|S|\ll 1
, which has been automatically satisfied by previously imposed conditions
m\mu|S|=\mathcal{O}(1)
and
\omega_{0}\ll\mu
. We will keep the leading order of the three quantities, which leads to
|\eta|\approx|M^{\prime\prime}|m\mu\ll 1
.

The weak wavefunction effects can be realized by a weak lattice potential. They can also be effectively realized in a tight-binding limit by weakly breaking sublattice symmetry, where the integrals of
\bm{r}
in the definition Eq. (
II.2
) should be renormalized and become summations of a sublattice index (see Appendix
C.1
). Here we define the sublattice symmetry physically, i.e. by permuting the sublattices, which may be different from a conventional one
Schnyder et al. 2008
;
Schnyder et al. 2009
;
Kitaev 2009
;
Ryu et al. 2010
.

As an example, we focus on the lattice-induced wavefunction effects on the breathing mode (
\mathsf{n}=1
,
\mathsf{j}=0
)
Zhai 2021
. As explained below Eq. (
43
), the momentum and frequency expansion is valid for the breathing mode. Without the wavefunction effects, (zeroth-order) eigenvalues (
\kappa_{\mathsf{n},\mathsf{j}}\equiv\frac{\omega^{2}_{\mathsf{n},\mathsf{j}}}{\omega_{0}^{2}}
) and normalized eigenfunctions (
\rho_{\mathsf{n},\mathsf{j}}
) of Eqs. (
IV.1
,
IV.1
) for the breathing mode are given by (See Appendix
B.1
and Ref.
Stringari 1996
)

\displaystyle\kappa^{(0)}_{1,0}=\left\{\begin{array}[]{cc}5&\quad\mathrm{for}\quad\mathrm{3D}\\
4&\quad\mathrm{for}\quad\mathrm{2D}\end{array}\right.,

\displaystyle\rho^{(0)}_{1,0}=\left\{\begin{array}[]{cc}\frac{3\sqrt{7}}{2}(1-\frac{5}{3}r^{2})&\quad\mathrm{for}\quad\mathrm{3D}\\
\sqrt{6}(1-2r^{2})&\quad\mathrm{for}\quad\mathrm{2D}\end{array}\right..

IV.2.1
Eigenfrequency

At the first order,
\kappa_{1,0}
should be a linear function of
\eta
, i.e. a linear function of
M^{\prime\prime}
. We can apply a perturbation theory similar to that in quantum mechanics and get the first-order correction to the eigenvalue
\kappa_{1,0}
,

\displaystyle\kappa^{(1)}_{1,0}=\int_{0}^{1}r^{d-1}\rho^{(0)}_{1}\hat{L}^{(1)}\rho^{(0)}_{1}dr=\left\{\begin{array}[]{cc}\frac{10}{9}\eta&\quad\mathrm{for}\quad\mathrm{3D}\\
\eta&\quad\mathrm{for}\quad\mathrm{2D}\end{array}\right.,

where the first-order operator
\hat{L}^{(1)}
with
\ell=0
is given by

\displaystyle\hat{L}^{(1)}=\left\{\begin{array}[]{cc}\begin{array}[]{l}-\frac{\eta}{4}(1-r^{2})^{2}\frac{d^{2}}{dr^{2}}\\
\!\ \!\ \!\ \!\ -\frac{\eta}{2}(1-r^{2})(1-3r^{2})\frac{1}{r}\frac{d}{dr}\end{array}&\quad\mathrm{for}\quad\mathrm{3D}\\
\begin{array}[]{l}-\frac{\eta}{4}(1-r^{2})^{2}\frac{d^{2}}{dr^{2}}\\
\!\ \!\ \!\ \!\ -\frac{\eta}{4}(1-r^{2})(1-5r^{2})\frac{1}{r}\frac{d}{dr}\end{array}&\quad\mathrm{for}\quad\mathrm{2D}\end{array}\right..

So the first-order eigenfrequency of the breathing mode reads

\displaystyle\omega_{1,0}=\left\{\begin{array}[]{cc}\sqrt{5}\omega_{0}(1+\frac{1}{9}m\mu M^{\prime\prime})&\quad\mathrm{for}\quad\mathrm{3D}\\
2\omega_{0}(1+\frac{1}{8}m\mu M^{\prime\prime})&\quad\mathrm{for}\quad\mathrm{2D}\end{array}\right..

IV.2.2
Amplitude and initial phase in a dynamic process

To further show the effects of
M_{d}
and
S
, we consider a dynamic process. Atoms are initially at the ground state with the harmonic potential, satisfying the Thomas-Fermi distribution. There is no lattice or a tight-binding lattice with negligible Bloch wavefunction effects, i.e.
M_{d}=M^{\prime\prime}=S=0
. At time
t=0
, we suddenly open or change the lattice potential to make the weak lattice-induced wavefunction effects. Then the ground-state distribution is substituted by Eqs. (
III.2
,
32
), and the atoms are at an excited state.

Suppose for
t<0
, the chemical potential, the effective mass of atoms, the typical frequency of the harmonic traps, and the traping radius of the atoms are
\tilde{\mu}
,
\tilde{m}
,
\tilde{\omega}
,
\tilde{R}
. For
t>0
, they become
\mu
,
m
,
\omega
,
R
. Because
U(r)
does not change, we have

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}m\omega_{0}^{2}r^{2}=\frac{1}{2}\tilde{m}\tilde{\omega}_{0}^{2}r^{2},

(58)

\displaystyle\mu=\frac{1}{2}m\omega_{0}^{2}R^{2},\quad\tilde{\mu}=\frac{1}{2}\tilde{m}\tilde{\omega}_{0}^{2}\tilde{R}^{2}.

(59)

Besides, suppose the amount of atoms remains invariant during the process,

\displaystyle\frac{\mu}{gM}\int_{0}^{R}(1-\frac{r^{2}}{R^{2}})r^{d-1}dr=\frac{\tilde{\mu}}{g}\int_{0}^{\tilde{R}}(1-\frac{r^{2}}{\tilde{R}^{2}})r^{d-1}dr.

(60)

Combining Eqs. (
58
–
60
) with Eqs. (
III.2
,
32
), we get initial conditions at
t=0
,

\displaystyle\rho(\bm{r},0)\approx\left\{\begin{array}[]{cc}\frac{\mu}{g}M_{d}(\frac{3}{5}-r^{2})&\quad\mathrm{for}\quad\mathrm{3D}\\
\frac{\mu}{g}M_{d}(\frac{1}{2}-r^{2})&\quad\mathrm{for}\quad\mathrm{2D}\end{array}\right.,

\displaystyle\frac{d}{dr}\delta\theta(\bm{r},0)\approx 2m\mu S\frac{r}{R^{2}},

(63)

\displaystyle\partial_{t}\delta n(\bm{r},0)\approx-\bm{\nabla}\cdot(n_{0}\bm{v})\approx-\bm{\nabla}\cdot(\bm{\hat{e}_{r}}\frac{n_{0}}{m}\frac{d}{dr}\delta\theta).

(64)

Eq. (
IV.2.2
) is not valid for
\frac{\tilde{R}}{R}<r<1
. However, that region leads to a higher-order contribution. Eqs. (
63
,
64
) further gives

\displaystyle\partial_{t}\rho(\bm{r},0)=\left\{\begin{array}[]{cc}-\frac{m\mu}{g}\omega_{0}^{2}S(3-5r^{2})&\quad\mathrm{for}\quad\mathrm{3D}\\
-\frac{2m\mu}{g}\omega_{0}^{2}S(1-2r^{2})&\quad\mathrm{for}\quad\mathrm{2D}\end{array}\right..

To satisfy the initial conditions Eqs. (
IV.2.2
,
IV.2.2
), for
t>0
,
\rho(r,t)
takes a form,

\displaystyle\rho(\bm{r},t)=\sum_{\mathsf{n}}\mathsf{b}_{\mathsf{n},0}\rho_{\mathsf{n},0}(r)\mathrm{cos}(\omega_{\mathsf{n},0}t+\varphi_{\mathsf{n},0}),

(67)

where amplitudes (
\mathsf{b}_{\mathsf{n},0}
) and initial phases (
\varphi_{\mathsf{n},0}
) are determined,

\displaystyle\mathsf{b}_{\mathsf{n},0}\mathrm{cos}\varphi_{\mathsf{n},0}=\int_{0}^{1}r^{d-1}\rho(r,0)\rho_{\mathsf{n},0}(r)dr,

(68)

\displaystyle\omega_{\mathsf{n},0}\mathsf{b}_{\mathsf{n},0}\mathrm{sin}\varphi_{\mathsf{n},0}=-\int_{0}^{1}r^{d-1}\partial_{t}\rho(r,0)\rho_{\mathsf{n},0}(r)dr.

(69)

At the leading order, we can take
\rho_{\mathsf{n},0}(r)=\rho_{\mathsf{n},0}^{(0)}(r)
. Then we get the amplitude and initial phase of the breathing mode,

\displaystyle\mathsf{b}_{1,0}\mathrm{cos}\varphi_{1,0}=\left\{\begin{array}[]{cc}\frac{2\mu M_{d}}{5\sqrt{7}g}&\quad\mathrm{for}\quad\mathrm{3D}\\
\frac{\mu M_{d}}{2\sqrt{6}g}&\quad\mathrm{for}\quad\mathrm{2D}\end{array}\right.,

\displaystyle\mathsf{b}_{1,0}\mathrm{sin}\varphi_{1,0}=\left\{\begin{array}[]{cc}\frac{2m\mu\omega_{0}S}{\sqrt{35}g}&\quad\mathrm{for}\quad\mathrm{3D}\\
\frac{m\mu\omega_{0}S}{\sqrt{6}g}&\quad\mathrm{for}\quad\mathrm{2D}\end{array}\right..

The phase shift
\varphi_{1,0}
can be observed by a corresponding time shift
\tau_{1,0}\equiv\frac{\varphi_{1,0}}{\omega_{0}}
. A nonzero phase shift
\varphi_{1,0}
is a characteristic of time-reversal symmetry breaking.

When
M_{d}
and
m\mu S
are in the same order, despite
|\varphi_{1,0}|\ll 1
, it is possible to observe the time shift as
\tau_{1,0}=\mathcal{O}(\mu^{-1})
. Besides, even if
|\varphi_{1,0}|\ll 1
, when the corrections to
\omega_{1,0}
and
\rho_{1,0}
in the next-to-leading order is considered, a correction to
\varphi_{1,0}
is also in the next-to-leading order.

V
Tight-binding example

A nonzero
S
can be generally realized by a synthetic magnetic field breaking the time-reversal symmetry. For a toy model showing essential physics, we consider a 2D tight-binding model of a bilayer square lattice in a nonuniform magnetic field, where the
\mathbb{Z}_{4}
rotational symmetry remains intact,

\displaystyle H_{0}=

\displaystyle\sum_{\bm{i},\bm{j}}(a^{\dagger}_{\bm{i},A},a^{\dagger}_{\bm{i},B})(\bm{h}_{0})_{\bm{i},\bm{j}}\left(\begin{array}[]{c}a_{\bm{j},A}\\
a_{\bm{j},B}\end{array}\right),

VI
Summary and outlook

In this paper, we study lattice-induced wavefunction effects on correlations between bosonic particles in lattices. The hydrodynamic properties of superfluids are influenced by a richer structure of Bloch wavefunctions beyond the single-body quantum geometric tensor. The lattice-induced wavefunction effects can be observed by the dynamic distribution of the particle density.

Our discussions are restricted to the simplest quantum many-body system where the interaction is local and the resulting effective theory is isotropic. The author wishes this paper to promote further exploration and application of wavefunction effects in wider many-body systems.

Besides, the discussions are still based on single-body Bloch wavefunctions. We derive the results at a practical level. Namely, this paper aims to propose specific many-body physical effects of Bloch wavefunctions, instead of to clarify more systematic or mathematical structures of two-body or many-body quantum states. Thus, a better understanding of quantum geometry in many-body systems and corresponding physical consequences remains intended
Hetényi and Lévay 2023
;
Salerno et al. 2023
;
Törmä 2023
.

\displaystyle\!\ \!\ \!\ \!\ +2(\ell+2\mathsf{k})-\kappa-\frac{1}{2}\ell(\ell+1)].

(104)

When
\mathsf{N}
is finite,
\mathsf{N}=\mathsf{n}
and an equation for
r^{\ell+2\mathsf{n}}
gives

\displaystyle\kappa=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}(\ell+2\mathsf{n})(\ell+2\mathsf{n}-1)+2(\ell+2\mathsf{n})-\frac{1}{2}\ell(\ell+1)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle 2\mathsf{n}^{2}+2\mathsf{n}\ell+3\mathsf{n}+\ell.

(105)

Taking Eq. (
B.1.1
) into Eq. (
B.1.1
), we have

\displaystyle\alpha_{2\mathsf{k}+2}=-\frac{(\mathsf{n}-\mathsf{k})(2\mathsf{k}+2\mathsf{n}+2\ell+3)}{(\mathsf{k}+1)(2\mathsf{k}+2\ell+3)}\alpha_{2\mathsf{k}}.

(106)

Below we explain why we cannot take
\mathsf{N}=\infty
. In this case, when
\mathsf{k}\rightarrow\infty
, Eq. (
B.1.1
) gives

\displaystyle\frac{\alpha_{2\mathsf{k}+2}}{\alpha_{2\mathsf{k}}}=\frac{2+\frac{2\ell+3}{\mathsf{k}}}{2+\frac{2\ell+5}{\mathsf{k}}}+\mathcal{O}(\frac{1}{\mathsf{k}^{2}})=1-\frac{1}{\mathsf{k}}+\mathcal{O}(\frac{1}{\mathsf{k}^{2}}).

(107)

We can take a test form of
\alpha_{2\mathsf{k}}
for
\mathsf{k}\rightarrow\infty
,

\displaystyle\alpha_{2\mathsf{k}}=\frac{C}{\mathsf{k}^{\zeta}}(1+\frac{C^{\prime}}{\mathsf{k}^{\zeta^{\prime}}}),

(108)

where
\zeta>0
,
\zeta^{\prime}>0
,
C
and
C^{\prime}
are constant, then

\displaystyle\frac{\alpha_{2\mathsf{k}+2}}{\alpha_{2\mathsf{k}}}=\frac{\mathsf{k}^{\zeta}}{(\mathsf{k}+1)^{\zeta}}\frac{1+\frac{C^{\prime}}{(\mathsf{k}+1)^{\zeta^{\prime}}}}{1+\frac{C^{\prime}}{\mathsf{k}^{\zeta^{\prime}}}}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle(1-\frac{\zeta}{\mathsf{k}})[1+\frac{C^{\prime}}{(\mathsf{k}+1)^{\zeta^{\prime}}}](1-\frac{C^{\prime}}{\mathsf{k}^{\zeta^{\prime}}})+\mathcal{O}(\frac{1}{\mathsf{k}^{\zeta^{\prime}+1}})+\mathcal{O}(\frac{1}{\mathsf{k}^{2}})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle 1-\frac{\zeta}{\mathsf{k}}+\mathcal{O}(\frac{1}{\mathsf{k}^{\zeta^{\prime}+1}})+\mathcal{O}(\frac{1}{\mathsf{k}^{2}}).

(109)

Comparing Eq. (
B.1.1
) with Eq. (
107
), we get
\zeta=1
. Eq. (
108
) can be generalized to show that
\alpha_{2\mathsf{k}}=\frac{C}{\mathsf{k}}+\mathcal{O}(\frac{1}{\mathsf{k}^{2}})
. Then we get

\displaystyle\rho(r\rightarrow 1)=\sum_{\mathsf{k}=0}^{\mathsf{k}_{0}}\alpha_{2\mathsf{k}}+\sum_{\mathsf{k}=\mathsf{k}_{0}}^{\infty}[\frac{C}{\mathsf{k}}+\mathcal{O}(\frac{1}{\mathsf{k}^{2}})]\rightarrow\infty,

(110)

where
\mathsf{k}_{0}
is a constant large enough. It is unphysical to have a divergent
\rho_{\mathsf{n},\ell}
when
r\rightarrow 1
. Note that at least one of
\mathsf{n}
and
\ell
is nonzero in the divergent case, so
n(\bm{r},t)
becomes negative for some spacetime coordinates. To avoid the divergence, we need Eq. (
B.1.1
) to truncate the sequence
\alpha_{2k}
and get a finite
\mathsf{N}
.

In summary, eigenfunctions take a form,

\displaystyle\rho_{\mathsf{n},\ell}=\sum_{\mathsf{k}=0}^{\mathsf{N}}\alpha_{\mathsf{n},\ell,2\mathsf{k}}r^{2\mathsf{k}+\ell},

(111)

where
\alpha_{\mathsf{n},\ell,2\mathsf{k}}
satisfies Eq. (
106
). When
\eta=0
, we get
\mathsf{N}=\mathsf{n}
in Eq. (
111
), but we will show that this is not true for
\eta\neq 0
.

B.1.2
2D case

When
\eta=0
, Eq. (
IV.1
) becomes

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}(1-r^{2})\frac{d^{2}\rho}{dr^{2}}+\frac{1}{2}(1-3r^{2})\frac{1}{r}\frac{d\rho}{dr}

\displaystyle+[\kappa r^{2}-\frac{1}{2}m^{2}(1-r^{2})]\frac{\rho}{r^{2}}=0.

(112)

An analysis of
r\rightarrow 0
gives
\rho(r\rightarrow 0)\rightarrow r^{|\mathsf{m}|}
, then we get

\displaystyle\rho_{\mathsf{n},|\mathsf{m}|}=\sum_{\mathsf{k}=0}^{\mathsf{N}}\alpha_{\mathsf{n},|\mathsf{m}|,2\mathsf{k}}r^{2\mathsf{k}+|\mathsf{m}|}.

(113)

Let us take
\mathsf{m}\geq 0
for simplicity. There is a recursion relation,

\displaystyle\alpha_{2\mathsf{k}+2}[\frac{1}{2}(\mathsf{m}+2\mathsf{k}+2)(\mathsf{m}+2\mathsf{k}+1)+\frac{1}{2}(\mathsf{m}+2\mathsf{k}+2)

\displaystyle\!\ \!\ -\frac{1}{2}\mathsf{m}^{2}]=\alpha_{2\mathsf{k}}[\frac{1}{2}(\mathsf{m}+2\mathsf{k})(\mathsf{m}+2\mathsf{k}-1)

\displaystyle\!\ \!\ \!\ \!\ +\frac{3}{2}(\mathsf{m}+2\mathsf{k})-\kappa-\frac{1}{2}\mathsf{m}^{2}].

(114)

If
\mathsf{N}
is finite, we get
\mathsf{N}=\mathsf{n}
and

\displaystyle\kappa=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}(\mathsf{m}+2\mathsf{n})(\mathsf{m}+2\mathsf{n}-1)+\frac{3}{2}(\mathsf{m}+2\mathsf{n})-\frac{1}{2}\mathsf{m}^{2}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle 2\mathsf{n}^{2}+2\mathsf{n}\mathsf{m}+2\mathsf{n}+\mathsf{m},

(115)

\displaystyle\alpha_{2\mathsf{k}+2}=

\displaystyle\alpha_{2\mathsf{k}}\frac{2(\mathsf{k}-\mathsf{n})(\mathsf{k}+\mathsf{n})+2(\mathsf{k}-\mathsf{n})(\mathsf{m}+1)}{\frac{1}{2}(\mathsf{m}+2\mathsf{k}+2)^{2}-\frac{1}{2}\mathsf{m}^{2}}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\alpha_{2\mathsf{k}}\frac{(\mathsf{k}-\mathsf{n})(\mathsf{k}+\mathsf{n}+\mathsf{m}+1)}{(\mathsf{k}+1)(\mathsf{k}+\mathsf{m}+1)}.

(116)

If
\mathsf{N}=\infty
, then when
\mathsf{k}\rightarrow\infty
, we get

\displaystyle\frac{\alpha_{2\mathsf{k}+2}}{\alpha_{2\mathsf{k}}}=\frac{2\mathsf{k}^{2}+(2\mathsf{m}+2)\mathsf{k}}{2\mathsf{k}^{2}+(2\mathsf{m}+4)\mathsf{k}}+\mathcal{O}(\frac{1}{\mathsf{k}^{2}})=1-\frac{1}{\mathsf{k}}+\mathcal{O}(\frac{1}{\mathsf{k}^{2}}),

(117)

and by the analysis below Eq. (
107
) we know it is impossible.

B.2
With lattice-induced wavefunction effects

B.2.1
3D case

Consider Eq. (
IV.1
) with
\eta\neq 0
. Note that
\eta
can be an
\mathcal{O}(1)
quantity. We have an equation,

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}[(1+\frac{\eta}{2})-(1+\eta)r^{2}+\frac{\eta}{2}r^{4}]\frac{d^{2}\rho}{dr^{2}}

\displaystyle+[(1+\frac{\eta}{2})-2(1+\eta)r^{2}+\frac{3\eta}{2}r^{4}]\frac{1}{r}\frac{d\rho}{dr}

\displaystyle+\{\kappa r^{2}-\frac{1}{2}\ell(\ell+1)[(1+\frac{\eta}{2})-(1+\eta)r^{2}+\frac{\eta}{2}r^{4}]\}\frac{\rho}{r^{2}}=0.

(118)

When
r\rightarrow 0
, we get

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}(1+\frac{\eta}{2})\frac{d^{2}\rho}{dr^{2}}+(1+\frac{\eta}{2})\frac{1}{r}\frac{d\rho}{dr}-\frac{1}{2}\ell(\ell+1)(1+\frac{\eta}{2})\frac{\rho}{r^{2}}=0.

(119)

Eq. (
119
) is identical to Eq. (
101
). Then we can take Eq. (
111
) into Eq. (
B.2.1
), and the difference from before is that we will get a second-order recursion relation, i.e.
\alpha_{2\mathsf{k}+2}
should be determined by
\alpha_{2\mathsf{k}}
and
\alpha_{2\mathsf{k}-2}
,

\displaystyle\sum_{\mathsf{k}=-1}^{\mathsf{N}-1}\alpha_{2\mathsf{k}+2}[\frac{1}{2}(1+\frac{\eta}{2})(\ell+2\mathsf{k}+2)(\ell+2\mathsf{k}+1)

\displaystyle\!\ \!\ \!\ \!\ +(1+\frac{\eta}{2})(\ell+2\mathsf{k}+2)-\frac{1}{2}(1+\frac{\eta}{2})\ell(\ell+1)]r^{\ell+2\mathsf{k}}

\displaystyle\!\ \!\ +\sum_{\mathsf{k}=0}^{\mathsf{N}}\alpha_{2\mathsf{k}}[-\frac{1}{2}(1+\eta)(\ell+2\mathsf{k})(\ell+2\mathsf{k}-1)

\displaystyle\!\ \!\ \!\ \!\ \!\ \!\ -2(1+\eta)(\ell+2\mathsf{k})+\kappa+\frac{1}{2}(1+\eta)\ell(\ell+1)]r^{\ell+2\mathsf{k}}

\displaystyle\!\ \!\ +\sum_{\mathsf{k}=1}^{\mathsf{N}+1}\alpha_{2\mathsf{k}-2}[\frac{\eta}{4}(\ell+2\mathsf{k}-2)(\ell+2\mathsf{k}-3)

\displaystyle\!\ \!\ \!\ \!\ \!\ \!\ +\frac{3}{2}\eta(\ell+2\mathsf{k}-2)-\frac{\eta}{4}\ell(\ell+1)]r^{\ell+2\mathsf{k}}=0.

(120)

However, if
\mathsf{N}
is finite, an equation for
\alpha_{2\mathsf{N}}r^{\ell+2\mathsf{N}+2}
gives

\displaystyle\frac{1}{4}(\ell+2\mathsf{N})(\ell+2\mathsf{N}-1)+\frac{3}{2}(\ell+2\mathsf{N})-\frac{1}{4}\ell(\ell+1)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle(\mathsf{N}+1)\ell+\frac{1}{2}\mathsf{N}(2\mathsf{N}+5)=0,

(121)

whose only solution is
\mathsf{N}=\ell=0
, and an equation for
\alpha_{2\mathsf{N}}r^{\ell+2\mathsf{N}}
gives
\kappa=0
. Besides this trivial zero mode (
\delta n
is a constant), we should take
\mathsf{N}=\infty
.

When
\mathsf{N}=\infty
and
\mathsf{k}\rightarrow\infty
, we can apply an expansion of
\mathsf{k}^{-1}
. At the leading order, we get a recursion relation,

\displaystyle(2+\eta)\alpha_{2\mathsf{k}+2}-2(1+\eta)\alpha_{2\mathsf{k}}+\eta\alpha_{2\mathsf{k}-2}=0,

(122)

which leads to

\displaystyle\alpha_{2\mathsf{k}}=A_{1}+A_{2}(\frac{\eta}{2+\eta})^{\mathsf{k}}.

(123)

So there are two parts of
\alpha_{2\mathsf{k}}
with different decay exponents. Let us concentrate on the case of
\eta>-1
so that
|\frac{\eta}{2+\eta}|<1
. At the next-to-leading order, similar to Eq. (
108
),
A_{1}
and
A_{2}
in Eq. (
123
) are no longer constants but proportional to powers of
\mathsf{k}
. Because Eq. (
122
) is linear, we can solve
A_{1}
and
A_{2}
separately, namely we can take only one of them to be nonzero. Taking
\alpha_{2\mathsf{k}}=\frac{C_{1}}{\mathsf{k}^{\zeta_{1}}}
, we get

\displaystyle(1-\frac{\zeta_{1}}{\mathsf{k}})(1+\frac{\eta}{2})(1+\frac{2\ell+5}{2\mathsf{k}})

\displaystyle-(1+\eta)(1+\frac{2\ell+3}{2\mathsf{k}})

\displaystyle+(1+\frac{\zeta_{1}}{\mathsf{k}})\frac{\eta}{2}(1+\frac{2\ell+1}{2\mathsf{k}})+\mathcal{O}(\frac{1}{\mathsf{k}^{2}})=0,

(124)

so the power
\zeta_{1}
is determined,

\displaystyle\zeta_{1}=(1+\frac{\eta}{2})(\ell+\frac{5}{2})-(1+\eta)(\ell+\frac{3}{2})+\eta(\frac{\ell}{2}+\frac{1}{4})=1.

(125)

Then we get
\alpha_{2\mathsf{k}}=\frac{C_{1}}{\mathsf{k}}+\mathcal{O}(\frac{1}{\mathsf{k}^{2}})
, which leads to the divergence shown in Eq. (
110
). To avoid the divergence, in the asymptotic behavior of
\alpha_{2\mathsf{k}}
shown in Eq. (
123
), only the second part should be nonzero. Taking
\alpha_{2\mathsf{k}}=\frac{C_{2}}{\mathsf{k}^{\zeta_{2}}}(\frac{\eta}{2+\eta})^{\mathsf{k}}
, we get

\displaystyle(1-\frac{\zeta_{2}}{\mathsf{k}})(1+\frac{\eta}{2})(1+\frac{2\ell+5}{2\mathsf{k}})\eta^{2}

\displaystyle-(1+\eta)(1+\frac{2\ell+3}{2\mathsf{k}})\eta(2+\eta)

\displaystyle+(1+\frac{\zeta_{2}}{\mathsf{k}})\frac{\eta}{2}(1+\frac{2\ell+1}{2\mathsf{k}})(2+\eta)^{2}+\mathcal{O}(\frac{1}{\mathsf{k}^{2}})=0,

(126)

so the power
\zeta_{2}
is determined,

\displaystyle\zeta_{2}=[(1+\frac{\eta}{2})(\ell+\frac{5}{2})\eta^{2}-(1+\eta)(\ell+\frac{3}{2})\eta(2+\eta)

\displaystyle\!\ \!\ +\eta(\frac{\ell}{2}+\frac{1}{4})(2+\eta)^{2}][-(1+\frac{\eta}{2})\eta^{2}+\frac{\eta}{2}(2+\eta)^{2}]^{-1}=1.

(127)

Then we get the asymptotic behavior of
\alpha_{2\mathsf{k}}
,

\displaystyle\alpha_{2\mathsf{k}}=[\frac{C_{2}}{\mathsf{k}}+\mathcal{O}(\frac{1}{\mathsf{k}^{2}})](\frac{\eta}{2+\eta})^{\mathsf{k}}.

(128)

To make
C_{1}=0
,
\kappa
should take discrete values. However, because
C_{1}=0
is an asymptomatic condition for the sequence
\alpha_{2\mathsf{k}}
, we can only solve
\kappa
numerically. From the recursion relation for
\mathsf{k}\geq 0
,

\displaystyle[\frac{1}{2}(1+\frac{\eta}{2})(\ell+2\mathsf{k}+2)(\ell+2\mathsf{k}+1)

\displaystyle\!\ \!\ \!\ \!\ +(1+\frac{\eta}{2})(\ell+2\mathsf{k}+2)-\frac{1}{2}(1+\frac{\eta}{2})\ell(\ell+1)]\alpha_{2\mathsf{k}+2}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle[\frac{1}{2}(1+\eta)(\ell+2\mathsf{k})(\ell+2\mathsf{k}-1)

\displaystyle\!\ \!\ \!\ \!\ +2(1+\eta)(\ell+2\mathsf{k})-\kappa-\frac{1}{2}(1+\eta)\ell(\ell+1)]\alpha_{2\mathsf{k}}

\displaystyle-[\frac{\eta}{4}(\ell+2\mathsf{k}-2)(\ell+2\mathsf{k}-3)

\displaystyle\!\ \!\ \!\ \!\ +\frac{3}{2}\eta(\ell+2\mathsf{k}-2)-\frac{\eta}{4}\ell(\ell+1)]\alpha_{2\mathsf{k}-2},

(129)

where
\alpha_{-2}\equiv 0
, when
\alpha_{0}
is normalized to be
1
, we can get
\alpha_{2\mathsf{k}_{0}+2}(\kappa)
and
\alpha_{2\mathsf{k}_{0}}(\kappa)
for some large enough integer
\mathsf{k}_{0}
. Then
\kappa
can be solved by (Eq. (
43
))

\displaystyle\alpha_{2\mathsf{k}_{0}+2}=\alpha_{2\mathsf{k}_{0}}\frac{\mathsf{k}_{0}}{\mathsf{k}_{0}+1}\frac{\eta}{2+\eta}.

(130)

Eq. (
130
) is an equation of order (
\mathsf{k}_{0}+1
), so we will get (
\mathsf{k}_{0}+1
) solutions of
\kappa
. When
k_{0}\rightarrow\infty
, we should get infinite solutions of
\kappa
, which form a discrete spectrum. Note that when
\eta=0
, Eq. (
130
) becomes the condition that
\mathsf{N}
in Eq. (
111
) is finite, which is consistent with our previous result.

B.2.2
2D case

Consider Eq. (
IV.1
) with
\eta\neq 0
,

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}[(1+\frac{\eta}{2})-(1+\eta)r^{2}+\frac{\eta}{2}r^{4}]\frac{d^{2}\rho}{dr^{2}}

\displaystyle\!\ \!\ +\frac{1}{2}[(1+\frac{\eta}{2})-3(1+\eta)r^{2}+\frac{5\eta}{2}r^{4}]\frac{1}{r}\frac{d\rho}{dr}

\displaystyle\!\ \!\ \!\ \!\ +\{\kappa r^{2}-\frac{1}{2}m^{2}[(1+\frac{\eta}{2})-(1+\eta)r^{2}+\frac{\eta}{2}r^{4}]\}\frac{\rho}{r^{2}}=0.

(131)

We still have Eq. (
113
). Taking
\mathsf{m}\geq 0
for simplicity, we get a recursion relation,

\displaystyle[\frac{1}{2}(1+\frac{\eta}{2})(\mathsf{m}+2\mathsf{k}+2)(\mathsf{m}+2\mathsf{k}+1)

\displaystyle\!\ \!\ \!\ \!\ +\frac{1}{2}(1+\frac{\eta}{2})(\mathsf{m}+2\mathsf{k}+2)-\frac{1}{2}(1+\frac{\eta}{2})\mathsf{m}^{2}]\alpha_{2\mathsf{k}+2}

\displaystyle+[-\frac{1}{2}(1+\eta)(\mathsf{m}+2\mathsf{k})(\mathsf{m}+2\mathsf{k}-1)

\displaystyle\!\ \!\ \!\ \!\ -\frac{3}{2}(1+\eta)(\mathsf{m}+2\mathsf{k})+\kappa+\frac{1}{2}(1+\eta)\mathsf{m}^{2}]\alpha_{2\mathsf{k}}

\displaystyle+[\frac{\eta}{4}(\mathsf{m}+2\mathsf{k}-2)(\mathsf{m}+2\mathsf{k}-3)

\displaystyle\!\ \!\ \!\ \!\ +\frac{5}{4}\eta(\mathsf{m}+2\mathsf{k}-2)-\frac{\eta}{4}\mathsf{m}^{2}]\alpha_{2\mathsf{k}-2}=0.

(132)

Then
\mathsf{N}=\infty
unless
\mathsf{m}=0
and
\mathsf{N}=0
, because

\displaystyle\frac{1}{4}(\mathsf{m}+2\mathsf{N})(\mathsf{m}+2\mathsf{N}-1)+\frac{5}{4}(\mathsf{m}+2\mathsf{N})-\frac{1}{4}\mathsf{m}^{2}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\mathsf{m}(2\mathsf{N}+1)+\mathsf{N}(\mathsf{N}+2)\geq 0.

(133)

When
\mathsf{N}=\infty
and
\mathsf{k}\rightarrow\infty
, taking an expansion of
\mathsf{k}^{-1}
, at the leading order we get the same results as Eqs. (
122
,
123
). Taking
\alpha_{2\mathsf{k}}=\frac{C_{1}}{\mathsf{k}^{\zeta_{1}}}
, we get

\displaystyle(1-\frac{\zeta_{1}}{\mathsf{k}})(1+\frac{\eta}{2})(1+\frac{\mathsf{m}+2}{\mathsf{k}})

\displaystyle-(1+\eta)(1+\frac{\mathsf{m}+1}{\mathsf{k}})

\displaystyle+(1+\frac{\zeta_{1}}{\mathsf{k}})\frac{\eta}{2}(1+\frac{\mathsf{m}}{\mathsf{k}})+\mathcal{O}(\frac{1}{\mathsf{k}^{2}})=0,

(134)

then

\displaystyle\zeta_{1}=

\displaystyle(1+\frac{\eta}{2})(\mathsf{m}+2)

\displaystyle\!\ \!\ \!\ \!\ -(1+\eta)(\mathsf{m}+1)+\eta\frac{\mathsf{m}}{2}=1,

(135)

and we should take
C_{1}=0
to make an eigenfunction convergent when

. Taking
\alpha_{2\mathsf{k}}=\frac{C_{2}}{\mathsf{k}^{\zeta_{2}}}(\frac{\eta}{2+\eta})^{\mathsf{k}}
, we get

\displaystyle(1-\frac{\zeta_{2}}{\mathsf{k}})(1+\frac{\eta}{2})(1+\frac{\mathsf{m}+2}{\mathsf{k}})\eta^{2}

\displaystyle-(1+\eta)(1+\frac{\mathsf{m}+1}{\mathsf{k}})\eta(2+\eta)

(136)

so

\displaystyle\zeta_{2}=-[(1+\frac{\eta}{2})(\mathsf{m}+2)\eta^{2}-(1+\eta)(\mathsf{m}+1)\eta(2+\eta)

\displaystyle\!\ \!\ \!\ \!\ +\frac{\eta}{2}\mathsf{m}(2+\eta)^{2}][\eta^{2}+2\eta]^{-1}=1,

(137)

and Eq. (

) is still valid to determine eigenvalues numerically.

B.3
Orthonormal relation

The linear differential operators in Eqs. (
B.2.1
,
B.2.2
) are non-Hermitian if we use the simplest definition of an inner product in a Hilbert space, so we need to apply the Sturm-Liouville theory
Mathews and Walker 1971
to find proper definitions of the inner product. For an eigenfunction of a second-order linear differential operator,

\displaystyle P(r)\frac{d^{2}\rho}{dr^{2}}+Q(r)\frac{d\rho}{dr}+[\kappa W(r)-S(r)]\rho=0,

(138)

it has a Sturm-Liouville form,

\displaystyle\frac{d}{dr}[p(r)\frac{d\rho}{dr}]+[\kappa w(r)-s(r)]\rho=0,

(139)

where

\displaystyle p(r)=e^{\int\frac{Q(r)}{P(r)}dr},\quad w(r)=\frac{W(r)}{P(r)}e^{\int\frac{Q(r)}{P(r)}dr},

\displaystyle s(r)=\frac{S(r)}{P(r)}e^{\int\frac{Q(r)}{P(r)}dr}.

(140)

When a boundary condition is satisfied,

\displaystyle p(r)[\rho_{\mathsf{n}_{2},\ell}\frac{d\rho^{*}_{\mathsf{n}_{1},\ell}}{dr}-\rho^{*}_{\mathsf{n}_{1},\ell}\frac{d\rho_{\mathsf{n}_{2},\ell}}{dr}]\big|_{r=0}^{r=1}=0,

(141)

the eigenfunctions can be normalized to form an orthonormal basis with an inner product,

\displaystyle(\rho_{\mathsf{n}_{1},\ell},\rho_{\mathsf{n}_{2},\ell})\equiv\int_{0}^{1}\frac{w(r)}{w}\rho_{\mathsf{n}_{1},\ell}^{*}(r)\rho_{\mathsf{n}_{2},\ell}(r)dr=\delta_{\mathsf{n}_{1},\mathsf{n}_{2}},

(142)

where
w
is an arbitrary coefficient.

B.3.1
3D case

In the 3D case,

\displaystyle p(r)=e^{\int\frac{Q(r)}{P(r)}dr}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\mathrm{exp}\{\int\frac{(1+\frac{\eta}{2})-2(1+\eta)r^{2}+\frac{3}{2}\eta r^{4}}{\frac{r}{2}[(1+\frac{\eta}{2})-(1+\eta)r^{2}+\frac{\eta}{2}r^{4}]}dr\}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle r^{2}(1-r^{2})(2+\eta-\eta r^{2}),

(143)

\displaystyle w(r)=

\displaystyle\frac{2}{(1+\frac{\eta}{2})-(1+\eta)r^{2}+\frac{\eta}{2}r^{4}}r^{2}(1-r^{2})(2+\eta-\eta r^{2})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle 4r^{2},

(144)

\displaystyle s(r)=\ell(\ell+1)(1-r^{2})(2+\eta-\eta r^{2}).

(145)

Near
r=0
we have got
\rho(r)\sim r^{\ell}
. Near
r=1
, we have required that
\rho(r)
is finite, and by an expansion of
1-r
we have
\frac{d\rho}{dr}=\kappa\rho
, so
\frac{d\rho}{dr}
is also finite. Because
p(0)=p(1)=0
, for two different eigenfunctions
\rho_{\mathsf{n}_{1},\ell}(r)
and
\rho_{\mathsf{n}_{2},\ell}(r)
, the condition Eq. (
141
) is satisfied. So we get the orthonormal relation in Eq. (
44
),

\displaystyle(\rho_{\mathsf{n}_{1},\ell},\rho_{\mathsf{n}_{2},\ell})\equiv\int_{0}^{1}r^{2}\rho_{\mathsf{n}_{1},\ell}^{*}(r)\rho_{\mathsf{n}_{2}}(r)dr=\delta_{\mathsf{n}_{1},\mathsf{n}_{2}},

(146)

where we have taken
w=4
in Eq. (
142
).

B.3.2
2D case

In the 2D case,

\displaystyle p(r)=

\displaystyle\mathrm{exp}\{\int\frac{(1+\frac{\eta}{2})-3(1+\eta)r^{2}+\frac{5}{2}\eta r^{4}}{r[(1+\frac{\eta}{2})-(1+\eta)r^{2}+\frac{\eta}{2}r^{4}]}dr\}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle r(1-r^{2})(2+\eta-\eta r^{2}),

(147)

\displaystyle w(r)=4r,\quad s(r)=\frac{m^{2}}{r}(1-r^{2})(2+\eta-\eta r^{2}).

(148)

Taking
w=4
, we get the orthonormal relation in Eq. (
44
),

\displaystyle(\rho_{\mathsf{n}_{1},\mathsf{m}},\rho_{\mathsf{n}_{2},\mathsf{m}})\equiv\int_{0}^{1}r\rho_{\mathsf{n}_{1},\mathsf{m}}^{*}(r)\rho_{\mathsf{n}_{2},\mathsf{m}}(r)dr=\delta_{\mathsf{n}_{1},\mathsf{n}_{2}}.

(149)

Appendix C
Discussions on tight-binding models

In this Appendix, as a supplement to the continuous model Eqs. (
1
–
II
), we discuss Bloch wavefunction effects in tight-binding models defined in discretized lattices. We first clarify a general tight-binding limit of the continuous model in Subsection
C.1
. We then focus on the simplest example, bipartite tight-binding models, and calculate their Bloch wavefunction quantities in Subsection
C.2
.

C.1
General tight-binding limit

In the tight-binding limit, the integral of
\bm{r}
in a unit cell is substituted by a summation of sublattice indices times the volume of a unit cell. Namely, our Hamiltonian becomes

\displaystyle H_{0}=\Omega_{\mathrm{cell}}^{2}\sum_{\bm{R},\bm{R}^{\prime}}\sum_{\sigma,\sigma^{\prime}=0}^{\mathsf{n}_{s}-1}a^{\dagger}_{\bm{R}+\bm{t}_{\sigma},\sigma}

\displaystyle\!\ \!\ \!\ \!\ \cdot(\bm{H}_{0,\mathrm{eff}})_{\sigma,\sigma^{\prime}}(\bm{R}-\bm{R}^{\prime}+\bm{t}_{\sigma}-\bm{t}_{\sigma^{\prime}})a_{\bm{R}^{\prime}+\bm{t}_{\sigma^{\prime}},\sigma^{\prime}}

\displaystyle\!\ \!\ +\frac{g_{\mathrm{eff}}}{2}\Omega_{\mathrm{cell}}\sum_{\bm{R}}\sum_{\sigma=0}^{\mathsf{n}_{s}-1}a^{\dagger}_{\bm{R}+\bm{t}_{\sigma},\sigma}a^{\dagger}_{\bm{R}+\bm{t}_{\sigma},\sigma}a_{\bm{R}+\bm{t}_{\sigma},\sigma}a_{\bm{R}+\bm{t}_{\sigma},\sigma}

\displaystyle\!\ \!\ +\Omega_{\mathrm{cell}}\sum_{\bm{R}}\sum_{\sigma=0}^{\mathsf{n}_{s}-1}U(\bm{R}+\bm{t}_{\sigma})a^{\dagger}_{\bm{R}+\bm{t}_{\sigma},\sigma}a_{\bm{R}+\bm{t}_{\sigma},\sigma},

(150)

where
\sigma
and
\sigma^{\prime}
are sublattice indices,
\bm{R}
and
\bm{R}^{\prime}
take lattice vectors,
\mathsf{n}_{s}
is the number of sublattices in a unit cell,
\bm{t}_{\sigma}
is the relative position of the
\sigma
-th sublattice in a unit cell. Different from a more commonly used convention that different sublattices in the same unit cell are regarded as in the same position, here we keep
\bm{t}_{\sigma}
in Fourier transforms to make crystal symmetries more explicit. This convention results in discontinuity of a
\bm{k}
-space Hamiltonian at the boundary of the first Brillouin zone, but this is not a problem for us because we only care about the Bloch states near the band bottom.

In Eq. (
C.1
),
U(\bm{R}+\bm{t}_{\sigma})\equiv U(\bm{r}=\bm{R}+\bm{t}_{\sigma})
and its Fourier transform are defined in the continuous space as the general cases of the continuous model,

\displaystyle U(\bm{R}+\bm{t}_{\sigma})=

\displaystyle\int_{\infty}\frac{d^{d}\bm{k}}{(2\pi)^{d}}e^{i\bm{k}\cdot(\bm{R}+\bm{t}_{\sigma})}U_{\bm{k}}

\displaystyle\approx

\displaystyle\int_{\mathrm{BZ}}\frac{d^{d}\bm{k}}{(2\pi)^{d}}e^{i\bm{k}\cdot(\bm{R}+\bm{t}_{\sigma})}U_{\bm{k}}.

(151)

Because now
a_{\sigma}(\bm{R}+\bm{t}_{\sigma})
and
(H_{0,\mathrm{eff}})_{\sigma,\sigma^{\prime}}(\bm{R}-\bm{R}^{\prime}+\bm{t}_{\sigma}-\bm{t}_{\sigma^{\prime}})
are defined in the lattice, to recover the general result of the low-energy effective action in the Bloch basis, their Fourier transforms are defined by

\displaystyle a_{\bm{R}+\bm{t}_{\sigma},\sigma}=\sum_{\mathsf{n}=0}^{\mathsf{n}_{s}-1}\int_{\mathrm{BZ}}\frac{d^{d}\bm{k}}{(2\pi)^{\frac{d}{2}}}e^{i\bm{k}\cdot(\bm{R}+\bm{t}_{\sigma})}u_{\mathsf{n},\bm{k},\sigma}b_{\mathsf{n},\bm{k}},

(152)

\displaystyle(\bm{H}_{0,\mathrm{eff}})_{\sigma,\sigma^{\prime}}(\bm{R}-\bm{R}^{\prime}+\bm{t}_{\sigma}-\bm{t}_{\sigma^{\prime}})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{\mathsf{n},\mathsf{n}^{\prime}=0}^{\mathsf{n}_{s}-1}\int_{\mathrm{BZ}}\frac{d^{d}\bm{k}}{(2\pi)^{d}}e^{i\bm{k}\cdot(\bm{R}-\bm{R}^{\prime}+\bm{t}_{\sigma}-\bm{t}_{\sigma^{\prime}})}(\bm{H}_{0,\mathrm{eff}})_{\sigma,\sigma^{\prime},\bm{k}}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{\mathsf{n},\mathsf{n}^{\prime}=0}^{\mathsf{n}_{s}-1}\int_{\mathrm{BZ}}\frac{d^{d}\bm{k}}{(2\pi)^{d}}e^{i\bm{k}\cdot(\bm{R}-\bm{R}^{\prime}+\bm{t}_{\sigma}-\bm{t}_{\sigma^{\prime}})}u^{*}_{\mathsf{n},\bm{k},\sigma}u_{\mathsf{n},\bm{k},\sigma^{\prime}}(\bm{H}_{0,\mathrm{eff}})_{\mathsf{n},\bm{k}},

(153)

where
\mathsf{n}
is the band index,
u_{\mathsf{n},\bm{k},\sigma}
is the normalized eigenvector of
(\bm{H}_{0,\mathrm{eff}})_{\sigma,\sigma^{\prime},\bm{k}}
in the sublattice space,

\displaystyle\sum_{\sigma=0}^{\mathsf{n}_{s}-1}u^{*}_{\mathsf{n},\bm{k},\sigma}u_{\mathsf{n}^{\prime},\bm{k},\sigma}=\delta_{\mathsf{n},\mathsf{n}^{\prime}}.

(154)

Note that the interaction strength
g_{\mathrm{eff}}
in Eq. (
C.1
) is different from
g
in Eq. (
II
), so we add a subscript “
\mathrm{eff}
” to emphasize this, although they play the same role for a low-energy effective theory. The tight-binding limit makes
M\rightarrow\infty
and
g_{\mathrm{eff}}=\mathcal{O}(gM)\rightarrow\infty
. This is because atoms are constrained at only several points in the unit cell, which significantly enlarges the effective repulsive interaction. Though, as there must be finite widths of Wannier functions in reality, we can renormalize
g_{\mathrm{eff}}
to be finite.

A Bloch wavefunction quantity
M_{\mathsf{n},\mathrm{eff}}
is defined by

\displaystyle M_{\mathsf{n},\mathrm{eff}}(\bm{k})=\mathsf{n}_{s}\sum_{\sigma=0}^{\mathsf{n}_{s}-1}u^{*}_{\mathsf{n},\bm{k},\sigma}u^{*}_{\mathsf{n},\bm{k},\sigma}u_{\mathsf{n},\bm{k},\sigma}u_{\mathsf{n},\bm{k},\sigma},

(155)

and other Bloch wavefunction quantities are defined similarly. When the sublattices become equivalent,
|u_{\mathsf{n},\bm{k},\sigma}|=\frac{1}{\sqrt{\mathsf{n}_{s}}}
and
M_{\mathsf{n},\mathrm{eff}}(\bm{k})=1
; for a general case,
|u_{\mathsf{n},\bm{k},\sigma}|
depends on
\sigma
and
M_{\mathsf{n},\mathrm{eff}}(\bm{k})>1
. We focus on the lowest band, i.e.
\mathsf{n}=0
, whose dispersion is assumed to be
(\bm{H}_{0,\mathrm{eff}})_{0,\bm{k}}=\frac{k^{2}}{2m}+\mathcal{O}(k^{3})
near the band bottom
\bm{k}=\bm{0}
.

In the next subsection, we only consider the tight-binding limit and only discuss the quantities with the subscript “eff”, so we will omit the subscript “eff” for convenience. We also omit the band index in
(\bm{H}_{0})_{\bm{k}}
when denoting a matrix and in
u_{\bm{k},\sigma}
when denoting the lowest band.

C.2
Bloch wavefunction quantities of bipartite tight-binding models

Next, we focus on bipartite lattices (
\mathsf{n}_{s}=2
). For a 2D lattice, suppose there is a
\mathbb{Z}_{n}
(
n\geq 3
) rotational symmetry; for a 3D lattice, suppose it belongs to the cubic crystal system. Near the band bottom
\bm{k}=\bm{0}
, in the sublattice space we can expand the single-body Hamiltonian by Pauli matrices,

\displaystyle(\bm{H}_{0})_{\bm{k}}=

\displaystyle\tilde{h}_{\bm{k}}\mathrm{sin}\Theta_{\bm{k}}\mathrm{cos}\Phi_{\bm{k}}\bm{\sigma}_{x}+\tilde{h}_{\bm{k}}\mathrm{sin}\Theta_{\bm{k}}\mathrm{sin}\Phi_{\bm{k}}\bm{\sigma}_{y}

\displaystyle+\tilde{h}_{\bm{k}}\mathrm{cos}\Theta_{\bm{k}}\bm{\sigma}_{z}+\bar{h}_{\bm{k}}\bm{\sigma}_{0},

(156)

where we neglect a constant which shifts the energy of
\bm{k}=\bm{0}
of the lower band to be zero. Then the lower-band eigenvector is given by

\displaystyle\bm{u}_{\bm{k}}\equiv\left(\begin{array}[]{c}u_{\bm{k},1}\\
u_{\bm{k},2}\end{array}\right)=\left(\begin{array}[]{c}-\mathrm{sin}\frac{\Theta_{\bm{k}}}{2}\\
\mathrm{cos}\frac{\Theta_{\bm{k}}}{2}e^{i\Phi_{\bm{k}}}\end{array}\right).

The Hamiltonian near
\bm{k}=\bm{0}
is constrained by the lattice symmetry,

\displaystyle(\bm{H}_{0})_{\bm{k}}=

\displaystyle(a_{x}-\frac{1}{2}b_{x}k^{2})\bm{\sigma}_{x}+(a_{y}-\frac{1}{2}b_{y}k^{2})\bm{\sigma}_{y}

\displaystyle+(a_{z}-\frac{1}{2}b_{z}k^{2})\bm{\sigma}_{z}+\frac{1}{2}b_{0}k^{2}\bm{\sigma}_{0}+\mathcal{O}(k^{3}).

(161)

For simplicity, we take
a_{x,y,z}\geq 0
,
b_{x,y,z,0}\geq 0
, and it can be generalized to other cases.

If there is a time-reversal symmetry, i.e.
(\bm{H}_{0})^{*}_{\bm{k}}=(\bm{H}_{0})_{-\bm{k}}
, then
a_{y}=b_{y}=0
. If there is an inversion symmetry that exchanges two sublattices (e.g. 2D honeycomb lattice), i.e.
\bm{\sigma}_{x}(\bm{H}_{0})_{\bm{k}}\bm{\sigma}_{x}=(\bm{H}_{0})_{-\bm{k}}
, then
a_{y}=b_{y}=a_{z}=b_{z}=0
. If there is an inversion symmetry that keeps the sublattices invariant (e.g. 2D bipartite square lattice), i.e.
(\bm{H}_{0})_{\bm{k}}=(\bm{H}_{0})_{-\bm{k}}
, it does not give additional constraints to Eq. (
C.2
). Besides, we take the permutation of sublattices as our definition of the sublattice symmetry, i.e.
\bm{\sigma}_{x}(\bm{H}_{0})_{\bm{k}}\bm{\sigma}_{x}=(\bm{H}_{0})_{\bm{k}}
, which is different from a conventional one, i.e.
\bm{\sigma}_{z}(\bm{H}_{0})_{\bm{k}}\bm{\sigma}_{z}=-(\bm{H}_{0})_{\bm{k}}
. The sublattice symmetry requires
a_{y}=b_{y}=a_{z}=b_{z}=0
.

We can calculate the Bloch wavefunction quantities near
\bm{k}=\bm{0}
, which are much simplified by
\partial_{k_{x}}\Theta_{\bm{0}}=\partial_{k_{x}}\Phi_{\bm{0}}=0
,

\displaystyle M_{d}(\bm{k})=M(\bm{k})-1

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle 2(\mathrm{sin}^{4}\frac{\Theta_{\bm{k}}}{2}+\mathrm{cos}^{4}\frac{\Theta_{\bm{k}}}{2})-1=\mathrm{cos}^{2}\Theta_{\bm{k}},

(162)

\displaystyle\partial_{k_{x}}M(\bm{k})=-\mathrm{sin}2\Theta_{\bm{k}}\partial_{k_{x}}\Theta_{\bm{k}},

(163)

\displaystyle M^{\prime\prime}(\bm{0})=\partial_{k_{x}}^{2}M(\bm{0})=-\mathrm{sin}2\Theta_{\bm{0}}\partial_{k_{x}}^{2}\Theta_{\bm{0}},

(164)

\displaystyle\partial_{k_{x}}\bm{u}_{\bm{k}}=\left(\begin{array}[]{c}-\frac{1}{2}(\partial_{k_{x}}\Theta_{\bm{k}})\mathrm{cos}\frac{\Theta_{\bm{k}}}{2}\\
-\frac{1}{2}(\partial_{k_{x}}\Theta_{\bm{k}})\mathrm{sin}\frac{\Theta_{\bm{k}}}{2}e^{i\Phi_{\bm{k}}}+i(\partial_{k_{x}}\Phi_{\bm{k}})\mathrm{cos}\frac{\Theta_{\bm{k}}}{2}e^{i\Phi_{\bm{k}}}\end{array}\right),

\displaystyle\partial_{k_{x}}^{2}\bm{u}_{\bm{0}}=\left(\begin{array}[]{c}-\frac{1}{2}(\partial_{k_{x}}^{2}\Theta_{\bm{0}})\mathrm{cos}\frac{\Theta_{\bm{0}}}{2}\\
-\frac{1}{2}(\partial_{k_{x}}^{2}\Theta_{\bm{0}})\mathrm{sin}\frac{\Theta_{\bm{0}}}{2}e^{i\Phi_{\bm{0}}}+i(\partial_{k_{x}}^{2}\Phi_{\bm{0}})\mathrm{cos}\frac{\Theta_{\bm{0}}}{2}e^{i\Phi_{\bm{0}}}\end{array}\right),

\displaystyle S(\bm{0})=

\displaystyle\mathrm{Im}[2\sum_{\sigma=0}^{1}u^{*}_{\bm{0},\sigma}u^{*}_{\bm{0},\sigma}u_{\bm{0},\sigma}\partial_{k_{x}^{2}}u_{\bm{0},\sigma}

\displaystyle\!\ \!\ \!\ \!\ -M(\bm{0})\sum_{\sigma=0}^{1}u^{*}_{\bm{0},\sigma}\partial_{k_{x}^{2}}u_{\bm{0},\sigma}]

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle 2\mathrm{cos}^{4}\frac{\Theta_{\bm{0}}}{2}\partial_{k_{x}}^{2}\Phi_{\bm{0}}-(1+\mathrm{cos}^{2}\Theta_{\bm{0}})\mathrm{cos}^{2}\frac{\Theta_{\bm{0}}}{2}\partial_{k_{x}}^{2}\Phi_{\bm{0}}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{4}\mathrm{sin}2\Theta_{\bm{0}}\partial_{k_{x}}^{2}\Phi_{\bm{0}}.

(169)

When there is the time-reversal symmetry, we get
\Phi_{\bm{k}}=0
, so
S(\bm{k})=0
. When there is the sublattice symmetry or a 3D inversion symmetry that exchanges the two sublattices, we get
\mathrm{cos}\Theta_{\bm{0}}=0
, so
M_{d}(\bm{0})=M^{\prime\prime}(\bm{0})=S(\bm{0})=0
, where there are no lattice-induced wavefunction effects in the low-energy effective theory.

When the sublattice symmetry and the time-reversal symmetry are weakly broken,
\frac{a_{y}}{a_{x}}
,
\frac{b_{y}}{b_{x}}
,
\frac{a_{z}}{a_{x}}
,
\frac{b_{z}}{b_{x}}
are small. Then at the leading order, we get

\displaystyle m\approx\frac{1}{b_{0}+b_{x}},\quad M_{d}(\bm{0})\approx\frac{a_{z}^{2}}{a_{x}^{2}},

(170)

\displaystyle\Theta_{\bm{k}}=

\displaystyle\frac{\pi}{2}-\mathrm{arctan}\frac{a_{z}-\frac{1}{2}b_{z}k^{2}}{\sqrt{(a_{x}-\frac{1}{2}b_{x}k^{2})^{2}+(a_{y}-\frac{1}{2}b_{y}k^{2})^{2}}},

\displaystyle\Phi_{\bm{k}}=

\displaystyle\mathrm{arctan}\frac{a_{y}-\frac{1}{2}b_{y}k^{2}}{a_{x}-\frac{1}{2}b_{x}k^{2}},

(171)

\displaystyle\partial_{k_{x}}^{2}\Theta_{\bm{0}}=2\frac{\partial\Theta_{\bm{0}}}{\partial k^{2}}=\frac{1}{1+\frac{a_{z}^{2}}{a_{x}^{2}+a_{y}^{2}}}[\frac{b_{z}}{(a_{x}^{2}+a_{y}^{2})^{\frac{1}{2}}}

\displaystyle\!\ \!\ \!\ \!\ -\frac{1}{2}\frac{2a_{z}(a_{x}b_{x}+a_{y}b_{y})}{(a_{x}^{2}+a_{y}^{2})^{\frac{3}{2}}}]\approx\frac{b_{z}}{a_{x}}-\frac{b_{x}a_{z}}{a_{x}^{2}},

(172)

\displaystyle\partial_{k_{x}}^{2}\Phi_{\bm{0}}=

\displaystyle 2\frac{\partial\Phi_{\bm{0}}}{\partial k^{2}}=-\frac{1}{1+\frac{a_{y}^{2}}{a_{x}^{2}}}\frac{a_{x}b_{y}-a_{y}b_{x}}{a_{x}^{2}}

\displaystyle\approx

\displaystyle-\frac{b_{y}}{a_{x}}+\frac{b_{x}a_{y}}{a_{x}^{2}},

(173)

\displaystyle M^{\prime\prime}(\bm{0})\approx

\displaystyle-\frac{2a_{z}}{a_{x}}(\frac{b_{z}}{a_{x}}-\frac{b_{x}a_{z}}{a_{x}^{2}}),

\displaystyle S(\bm{0})\approx

\displaystyle-\frac{a_{z}}{2a_{x}}(\frac{b_{y}}{a_{x}}-\frac{b_{x}a_{y}}{a_{x}^{2}}).

(174)

If
\frac{a_{y}}{a_{x}}
,
\frac{b_{y}}{b_{x}}
,
\frac{a_{z}}{a_{x}}
,
\frac{b_{z}}{b_{x}}
are in the same order,
\mu
and
a_{x}
(or a bandwidth) are in the same order, then without fine-tuning,
M_{d}(\bm{0})
,
m\mu M^{\prime\prime}(\bm{0})
, and
m\mu S(\bm{0})
are in the same order.

For the example given in Eq. (
V
), from Eq. (
V
) we get

\displaystyle b_{0}=2t_{1}a_{0}^{2},\quad a_{x}=-t_{2}-4t_{3}\mathrm{cos}\theta_{3},

\displaystyle b_{x}=-2t_{3}a_{0}^{2}\mathrm{cos}\theta_{3},

(175)

\displaystyle a_{y}=-4t_{3}\mathrm{sin}\theta_{3},\quad b_{y}=-2t_{3}a_{0}^{2}\mathrm{sin}\theta_{3},

\displaystyle a_{z}=\Delta,\quad b_{z}=0.

(176)

We can apply a gauge transformation to take an additional sign for
a_{x,y}
and
b_{x,y}
so that they become positive. The gauge transformation is in the sublattice space instead of the momentum space, which does not affect the Bloch wavefunction quantities. When
\Delta
and
\theta_{3}
are small, taking Eqs. (
C.2
,
C.2
) into Eqs. (
170
,
C.2
) and keeping the leading order, we get the results of Eq. (
V
), where two terms in
S(\bm{0})
have been combined,

\displaystyle S(\bm{0})=

\displaystyle-\frac{\Delta}{2(t_{2}+4t_{3})}[\frac{2t_{3}a_{0}^{2}\theta_{3}}{t_{2}+4t_{3}}-\frac{8t_{3}^{2}a_{0}^{2}\theta_{3}}{(t_{2}+4t_{3})^{2}}]

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-\frac{a_{0}^{2}t_{2}t_{3}\theta_{3}\Delta}{(t_{2}+4t_{3})^{3}}.

(177)
