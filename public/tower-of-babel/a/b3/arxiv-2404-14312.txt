Abstract
The main challenge of large-scale numerical simulation of radiation transport is the high memory and computation time requirements of discretization methods for kinetic equations. In this work, we derive and investigate a neural network-based approximation to the entropy closure method to accurately compute the solution of the multi-dimensional moment system with a low memory footprint and competitive computational time. We extend methods developed for the standard entropy-based closure to the context of regularized entropy-based closures. The main idea is to interpret structure-preserving neural network approximations of the regularized entropy closure as a two-stage approximation to the original entropy closure. We conduct a numerical analysis of this approximation and investigate optimal parameter choices. Our numerical experiments demonstrate that the method has a much lower memory footprint than traditional methods with competitive computation times and simulation accuracy. The code and all trained networks are provided on GitHub 1 1 1 https://github.com/ScSteffen/neuralEntropyClosures , 2 2 2 https://github.com/CSMMLab/KiT-RT .

1
Introduction

Numerically solving kinetic equations is at the heart of many physics-based applications, including neutron transport
[
43
]
, radiation transport
[
10
]
, semiconductors
[
46
]
and rarefied gas dynamics
[
9
]
.
A kinetic equation is a high-dimensional integro-differential equation formulated over a position-momentum phase space that can be as large as six dimensions. The high dimensionality of the phase space presents a severe computational challenge for large-scale numerical simulations. In addition, kinetic equations possess intricate structural properties: Galilean invariance, conserved quantities, and entropy dissipation (which implies hyperbolicity and an H-Theorem)
[
41
]
.
It is key to the stability of a numerical method to explicitly preserve as many structural properties as possible.

Several methods have been proposed to solve kinetic equations, which are generally divided into nodal and modal methods. Nodal methods (also known as discrete velocity or discrete ordinates methods) evaluate the velocity space at specific points, yielding a system of equations only coupled by a quadrature approximation for the integral scattering operator. While computationally efficient, these methods suffer from numerical artifacts, which are called ray effects
[
39
]
when few discrete velocities are used, and on the other hand have a very large memory footprint when many velocities are used.

Modal methods (also known as moment methods) eliminate the velocity dependence of the phase space by computing the moment hierarchy of the kinetic equation. Due to the intrinsic structure of the advection term the moment system requires a closure
[
42
]
. Modal methods differ in the closure model for the kinetic density in the transport term. The classical P
N
closure reconstructs the kinetic density linearly from the moment basis, which is computationally very fast. Its main drawbacks are spurious numerical artifacts such as large oscillations of the solution in the presence of highly anisotropic particle distributions, which may even result in negative solutions in the particle density
[
21
]
. Filtering procedures may mitigate these oscillations
[
47
]
, but ensuring positivity requires a sophisticated limiting strategy
[
38
,
36
,
37
,
25
]
.

A moment closure, which preserves almost all important physical and mathematical properties of the Boltzmann equation (the one exception being the superposition principle, cf. Section
2.2
) is constructed by solving a convex, constrained optimization problem based on the principle of (mathematical) entropy minimization
[
42
]
. Convexity is the mathematical key, because it implies entropy dissipation. The drawback is that this so-called M
N
closure is far more expensive to compute, since an optimization problem has to be solved at each closure evaluation. Furthermore, this optimization problem is ill-conditioned in physically relevant situations, while gradient and Hessian evaluations usually require expensive quadratures.
Making solving entropy-based closures practically feasible has been studied in a series of papers: First experiments were performed in
[
27
]
; the structural properties of the optimization problem were investigated in
[
3
]
; in
[
34
]
, it was demonstrated that entropy-based closures can be scaled onto large HPC systems; the ill-posedness of the optimization problem was addressed by applying regularization techniques
[
2
]
; the regularization introduces a numerical error that can be controlled and balanced with the spatio-temporal discretization error
[
1
]
).

Recently, neural networks have been used to to relieve the computational burden of the optimization problem. One key idea is to use input-convex neural networks to approximate the entropy functional, and thereby close the system
[
49
]
. Further, in
[
51
]
, data sampling strategies and approximation errors of the neural network-based surrogate models have been investigated. Although successfully applied to low-order entropy closures, these neural network-based closures face severe challenges when approximating high-order closures in higher spatial dimensions
[
51
]
. Other approaches use a direct neural network-based modeling of the closure of the moment system. Examples include encoder-decoder networks
[
22
]
, Galilean invariant machine learning methods
[
32
,
17
]
, U-nets that consider global information of the solution field
[
45
]
, and learning the spatial gradient of the highest-order moment
[
30
,
11
]
. Contrary to entropy-based closures, the approach in
[
30
]
requires additional work to ensure hyperbolicity
[
31
]
and correct characteristic speeds
[
29
]
. It also requires training with kinetic data, which can be expensive to obtain.

This work presents a structure-preserving neural network-based surrogate model combining the regularized formulation of the entropy-based closure
[
2
]
with structure-preserving neural network-based closure approximations
[
49
]
. In particular, we explore the difficulties associated with the non-regularized entropy closure in terms of neural network approximation and data sampling. To address these issues, we develop a normalized, regularized framework that generates input convex neural network approximations to the regularized entropy closure, which itself serves as a convex approximation to the non-regularized entropy closure.
We conduct an error analysis of this multi-step approximation, which includes neural network approximation error, regularization error, and scaling errors of the partially regularized closure. Lastly, we perform extensive numerical experiments comparing different neural network approximations for various higher-order entropy closures of different regularization levels in synthetic tests, as well as established simulation benchmarks in the field of radiation transport. The trained network graphs are incorporated into the open-source high-performance solver KiT-RT
[
35
]
.

Entropy-based moment closures

2.1
Kinetic equations

We consider a linear kinetic equation

\partial_{t}f+\mathbf{v}\cdot\nabla_{\mathbf{x}}f=Q(f),

(1)

which describes the evolution of a kinetic distribution function
f(t,\mathbf{x},{\mathbf{v}})
in a many-particle system with phase space dependence space
\mathbf{x}\in\mathbf{X}\subset\mathbb{R}^{3}
, and velocity
{\mathbf{v}}\in\mathbb{S}^{2}=\left\{{\mathbf{v}}\in\mathbb{R}^{3}:\left\lVert{\mathbf{v}}\right\rVert=1\right\}
.
Throughout this paper, we denote the Euclidean norm by
\left\lVert\cdot\right\rVert
. The left-hand side of Eq. (
1
) models particle advection, and the right-hand side models interaction with the background medium of other particles.
We use the linear collision operator in this work, which reads

Q(f)(\mathbf{v})=\int_{\mathbb{S}^{2}}k(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{v}\cdot\mathbf{v}_{*})\left[f({\mathbf{v}}_{*})-f({\mathbf{v}})\right]d{\mathbf{v}}_{*},

(2)

where the collision kernel
k
models the strength of collisions at different velocities.
We consider in this paper a space-dependent, isotropic collision kernel, denoted by
\sigma_{\textup{s}}(\mathbf{x})
, however, the results can be extended to arbitrary collision kernels using the methods of
[
2
]
.
Further, we abbreviate the integral over the velocity space by

\left<\cdot\right>=\int_{\mathbb{S}^{2}}\cdot\;\mathrm{d}\mathbf{v}.

(3)

Equation (
1
) becomes a well-posed problem with suitable boundary and initial conditions.

The linear kinetic equation possesses some key structural properties, which are intricately related to the physical processes and its mathematical existence and uniqueness theory
[
2
,
41
]
. First, the time evolution of the solution is invariant in range, i.e. if
f(0,\mathbf{x},\mathbf{v})\in B
, then
f(t,\mathbf{x},\mathbf{v})\in B
for all
t>0
. In this work, we have
B=[0,\infty)
. Particularly this implies the non-negativity of
f
, if the initial condition
f_{0}
is non-negative.
Second, if
\varphi
is a collision invariant, i.e.

\displaystyle\left<\varphi Q(g)\right>=0,\quad\forall g\in\mathrm{Dom}(Q),

(4)

then the equation

\displaystyle\partial_{t}\left<\varphi f\right>+\nabla_{\mathbf{x}}\cdot\left<{\mathbf{v}}\varphi f\right>=0

(5)

is a local conservation law. Third, for each fixed direction
\mathbf{v}
, the advection operator, i.e. the left-hand side term of Eq. (
1
), is hyperbolic in space and time. Fourth, for
D\subseteq\mathbb{R}_{+}
, any twice continuously differentiable, strictly convex function
\eta:D\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
is called kinetic entropy density. It yields the local entropy dissipation law

\displaystyle\partial_{t}\left<\eta(f)\right>+\nabla_{\mathbf{x}}\cdot\left<{\mathbf{v}}\eta(f)\right>=\left<\eta^{\prime}(f)Q(f)\right>\leq 0.

(6)

Thus the particle system always strives for a state of minimal mathematical (i.e. maximal physical) entropy. The domain
D
of the entropy density is often consistent with the physical bounds
B
of the problem and we assume
D=B=[0,\infty)
in the following.
Next, the solution
f
fulfills the H-theorem that characterizes equilibria of the linear kinetic equation.
Lastly, the solution
f
will be invariant under rotations in
(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{v})
and translations in
\mathbf{x}
, as long as such transformations are allowed by the kernel
k
.

Entropy-based closures

Moment methods encode the velocity dependence of the linear kinetic equation by multiplication of

{\mathbf{m}}(\mathbf{v}):\mathbb{S}^{2}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^{n+1}

The solution of the resulting moment equation is the moment vector

\displaystyle\mathbf{u}_{f}(t,\mathbf{x})=\left<{\mathbf{m}}({\mathbf{v}})f(t,\mathbf{x},{\mathbf{v}})\right>.

Common choices for the basis functions are monomials or spherical harmonics, depending on the application. In the following, we consider spherical harmonics basis functions. Typically, they include the collision invariants defined in Eq. (

The moment vector satisfies the system of transport equations

\displaystyle\partial_{t}\mathbf{u}_{f}(t,\mathbf{x})+\nabla_{\mathbf{x}}\cdot\left<\mathbf{v}\otimes{\mathbf{m}}(\mathbf{v})f\right>=\left<{\mathbf{m}}(\mathbf{v})Q(f)\right>,

(8)

By construction, the advection and collision operators still depend on

Since the kinetic equation dissipates entropy and fulfills a local entropy dissipation law, a suitable closure can be built by reconstructing an ansatz density

The entropy-based closure, or M

\displaystyle\mathcal{R}=\left\{\mathbf{u}:\left<{\mathbf{m}}g\right>=\mathbf{u},\,g\in F_{{\mathbf{m}}}\right\}.

2.3
Approximation of entropy-based closure with neural networks

The entropy-based closure faces practical challenges.
In particular, solving the convex optimization problem of Eq. (
12
) at every time step in each grid cell of a numerical simulation comes at a high computational cost, especially near the boundary of
{\mathcal{R}}
, where the condition number of the Hessian in the optimization (
18
) diverges
[
34
,
3
]
.
This issue motivates the neural network-based entropy closures proposed in
[
49
,
51
]
, where a convex neural network is used to approximate the entropy function
h
and to construct an approximate ansatz to close the moment system (
8
).
In previous work
[
51
]
,
h
is approximated on

\displaystyle\overline{\mathcal{R}}

\displaystyle=\left\{\mathbf{u}\in\mathcal{R}:u_{0}=1\right\}\subset\mathbb{R}^{n+1}

(20)

by an input convex neural network N
θ
with parameters
\theta
, i.e.,
\forall\overline{\mathbf{u}}\in\overline{\mathcal{R}}
,

\displaystyle\text{N}_{\theta}(\overline{\mathbf{u}})\approx h(\overline{\mathbf{u}})\quad\text{ and }\quad\nabla_{\mathbf{u}}\text{N}_{\theta}(\overline{\mathbf{u}})\approx{\bm{\alpha}}_{\overline{{\mathbf{u}}}}.

(21)

This neural network-based closure reduces the computational effort by several orders of magnitude from that of an iterative optimizer
[
51
]
.

Even though the neural network-based closure reduces the computation cost, it still suffers from the numerical difficulties near the boundary of the realizable set.
To facilitate the discussion, we introduce
the reduced, normalized realizable set

\displaystyle\widetilde{\mathcal{R}}

\displaystyle=\left\{\mathbf{w}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}:[1,{\mathbf{w}}^{\top}]^{\top}\in\overline{\mathcal{R}}\right\}\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}\>.

(22)

Note that both
\overline{\mathcal{R}}
, defined in Eq. (
20
), and
\widetilde{\mathcal{R}}
are bounded sets.

Definition 1
.

We define the normalization operator

\displaystyle\overline{(\cdot)}\,\colon\mathbb{R}^{{n+1}}\to\mathbb{R}^{n+1}\>\text{ defined such that }\>\mathbf{u}\mapsto\overline{\mathbf{u}}:=\tfrac{\mathbf{u}}{u_{0}}

(23)

and the “fruncation” operator

\displaystyle(\cdot)_{\#}\colon\mathbb{R}^{n+1}\to\mathbb{R}^{n}\>\text{ defined such that }\>\mathbf{u}\mapsto\mathbf{u}_{\#}:=[\mathbf{u}_{1}^{\top},\dots,\mathbf{u}_{N}^{\top}]^{\top}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}\>.

(24)

Further, repeated application of the fruncation operator

to the moment vector removes moments associated with the (currently) lowest degree basis functions, e.g.
({(\mathbf{u})_{\#}})_{\#}=[\mathbf{u}_{2}^{\top},\dots,\mathbf{u}_{N}^{\top}]^{\top}

Therefore,
\mathbf{u}(=[u_{0},(\mathbf{u}_{\#})^{\top}]^{\top})\in\mathcal{R}
if and only if

\overline{\mathbf{u}}(=\frac{\mathbf{u}}{u_{0}})\in\overline{\mathcal{R}}

{\overline{\mathbf{u}}_{\#}}\in\widetilde{\mathcal{R}}

It is known

becomes unbounded. Furthermore, the condition number of the Hessian for the associated optimization problem grows, and at

the problem is singular. For example, in a reduced, one-dimensional slab geometry, the kinetic density

associated to

is a sum of finitely many

[

48
]
, i.e.,

\displaystyle f({\mathbf{v}})=\sum_{i=1}^{l}c_{i}\delta(p_{i}({\mathbf{v}})),

(25)

where
\delta
is a Dirac distribution,

and each

.
In the following theorem, we describe the limit behavior of

Its proof is given in

Theorem 1
.

Given a slab geometry and Maxwell-Boltzmann kinetic entropy (

.

The divergent behavior of

, as well as the unbounded growth of

heavily

Due to numerical overflow, it is infeasible to sample

near or at

Regularized entropy-based moment closures

To overcome the challenge of approximating the entropy closure near the boundary of the realizable set, we adopt a regularized version of the entropy closure that was introduced in

]

]

3.1
Fully regularized entropy-based closure

In

2
]
, the minimization problem (

\displaystyle\min_{g\in F_{{\mathbf{m}}}}\left<\eta(g)\right>+\frac{1}{2\Gamma}\left\lVert\left<{\mathbf{m}}({\mathbf{v}})g\right>-\mathbf{u}\right\rVert^{2},

(26)

\displaystyle f^{\Gamma}_{\mathbf{u}}=\eta^{\prime}_{*}({\bm{\alpha}}_{\mathbf{u}}^{\Gamma}\cdot{\mathbf{m}})\>,

\displaystyle{\bm{\alpha}}_{\mathbf{u}}^{\Gamma}=\underset{{\bm{\alpha}}\in\mathbb{R}^{n+1}}{\text{argmin}}\left\{\left<\eta_{*}({\bm{\alpha}}\cdot{\mathbf{m}})\right>-{\bm{\alpha}}\cdot\mathbf{u}+\frac{\Gamma}{2}\left\lVert{\bm{\alpha}}\right\rVert^{2}\right\}\>.

\displaystyle H^{\Gamma}({\bm{\alpha}})=\left<{\mathbf{m}}\otimes{\mathbf{m}}\eta_{*}^{\prime\prime}({\bm{\alpha}}\cdot{\mathbf{m}})\right>+\Gamma I\>.

3.3
Properties of the partially regularized closure

In this section, we analyze the moment reconstruction error with the ansatz
f^{\gamma}_{\mathbf{u}}
and show that using

in Eq. (

) to close the moment system preserves the desirable structural properties.

3.3.1
Moment reconstruction error

Closing the moment system (
8
) using the partially regularized ansatz
f^{\gamma}_{\mathbf{u}}
rather than the standard entropy-based ansatz
f_{\mathbf{u}}
(see Eq. (
37
) and Eq. (
11
) for the definitions) results in discrepancies in the flux term, i.e.,
\langle\mathbf{v}\otimes\mathbf{m}f^{\gamma}_{\mathbf{u}}\rangle
vs.
\langle\mathbf{v}\otimes\mathbf{m}f_{\mathbf{u}}\rangle
and the collision term, i.e.,
\langle\mathbf{m}Q(f^{\gamma}_{\mathbf{u}})\rangle
vs.
\langle\mathbf{m}Q(f_{\mathbf{u}})\rangle
.
To facilitate the discussion, we denote, for a given moment
\mathbf{u}
,

\mathbf{u}^{\gamma}=\langle\mathbf{m}f^{\gamma}_{\mathbf{u}}\rangle\quad\text{and}\quad\widetilde{\mathbf{u}}^{\gamma}=\langle\mathbf{m}\widetilde{f}^{\gamma}_{\mathbf{u}}\rangle

(50)

as the moments associated to the ansätze
f^{\gamma}_{\mathbf{u}}
and
\widetilde{f}^{\gamma}_{\mathbf{u}}
defined in Eq. (
37
).
Since the standard entropy-based closure recovers the moment then (up to optimization tolerance),
\mathbf{u}=\langle\mathbf{m}f_{\mathbf{u}}\rangle
.

We provide in Theorem
4
an upper bound on the moment reconstruction error
\|\mathbf{u}^{\gamma}-\mathbf{u}\|
, which can be used to estimate the discrepancies in the flux term under the assumption of Lipschitz continuity
[
1
]
.
In Theorem
4
, we separate the error from partial regularization
\left\lVert\widetilde{\mathbf{u}}^{\gamma}-\mathbf{u}\right\rVert
and the error from the entropy gradient and multiplier discrepancy,
\left\lVert\mathbf{u}^{\gamma}-\widetilde{\mathbf{u}}^{\gamma}\right\rVert
, shown in Theorem
2
.

Definition 4
.

Given any positive finite constant, let
B_{M}:=\left\{\bm{\beta}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}:\left\lVert\bm{\beta}\right\rVert<M\right\}
be the ball of radius
M
in the Euclidean norm on
\mathbb{R}^{n}
.

Theorem 4
.

Let
\gamma>0
,
\mathbf{u}=[u_{0},(\mathbf{u}_{\#})^{\top}]^{\top}\in\mathbb{R}^{n+1}
with
u_{0}>0
, and let
\bm{\beta}_{{\overline{\mathbf{u}}_{\#}}}^{\gamma}
be as defined in Eq. (
42
).
Then

\widetilde{\mathbf{u}}^{\gamma}=\mathbf{u}-u_{0}\gamma\,[0,(\bm{\beta}^{\gamma}_{{\overline{\mathbf{u}}_{\#}}})^{\top}]^{\top}.

(51)

Further, suppose
\bm{\beta}_{{\overline{\mathbf{u}}_{\#}}}^{\gamma}\in B_{M}
, then

\left\lVert\widetilde{\mathbf{u}}^{\gamma}-\mathbf{u}\right\rVert=u_{0}\gamma\,\|(\bm{\beta}^{\gamma}_{{\overline{\mathbf{u}}_{\#}}})\|\leq u_{0}\gamma M,

(52)

and

\left\lVert\mathbf{u}^{\gamma}-\mathbf{u}\right\rVert\leq u_{0}\gamma M+\big(1-\exp(-\frac{\gamma}{2}M^{2})\big)\|\widetilde{\mathbf{u}}^{\gamma}\|\leq u_{0}\left(\gamma M+(n+1)\left(\frac{n+1}{\left|\mathbb{S}^{2}\right|}\right)^{1/2}\left(1-\exp\left(-\frac{\gamma}{2}M^{2}\right)\right)\right),

(53)

where

is the measure of the unit sphere.

Proof.

See
B.7
.
∎

The last error estimate is rather conservative since typically
\left\lVert\widetilde{\mathbf{u}}^{\gamma}\right\rVert\ll u_{0}\,(n+1)\left(\frac{n+1}{\left|\mathbb{S}^{2}\right|}\right)^{1/2}
. In the numerical results (cf. Table
6
) we found that often the gross numerical error is dominated by the regularization error given in Eq. (
52
).

3.3.2
Entropy dissipation and hyperbolicity

We show that by closing the moment system (
8
) using the ansatz
f_{\mathbf{u}}^{\gamma}
given in Eq. (
37
), key structural properties, such as invariance of range, conservation, entropy dissipation, and hyperbolicity, are preserved in the closed moment system.
The invariance of range property follows directly from the fact that

, which is identical to the range for the kinetic density
D=[0,\infty)
considered in this paper.
As in the case analyzed in
[

, the partial regularization here does not affect the collision invariants of the collision operator

and thus preserves the conservation property.
In the following Theorem

.

.

The partially regularized entropy function

. The associated entropy flux is given by

(54)

and

\displaystyle\partial_{t}h^{\gamma}(\mathbf{u})+\nabla_{\mathbf{x}}\cdot\mathbf{j}^{\gamma}(\mathbf{u})\leq 0

holds.
Furthermore, the closed moment system

\displaystyle\partial_{t}\mathbf{u}+\nabla_{\mathbf{x}}\cdot\left<\mathbf{v}\otimes\mathbf{m}f^{\gamma}_{\mathbf{u}}\right>=\left<\mathbf{m}Q(f^{\gamma}_{\mathbf{u}})\right>

(56)

is symmetrizable hyperbolic.

Proof.

See
B.8
.
∎

The entropy dissipation and hyperbolicity results in Theorem

are specific for moment systems closed with ansatz

, which is determined by the gradient of the underlying entropy function

.
When the moment system is closed using the ansatz

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}(\theta;X_{\text{Train}})=\frac{1}{T}\sum_{i=1}^{T}\left|{\hat{h}^{\gamma}(\overline{{\mathbf{u}}}_{\#,i})-\hat{h}^{p}(\overline{{\mathbf{u}}}_{\#,i})}\right|^{2}+\left\lVert{{\bm{\beta}}}_{\overline{\mathbf{u}}_{\#,i}}^{\gamma}-{{\bm{\beta}}}_{\overline{\mathbf{u}}_{\#,i}}^{p}\right\rVert^{2}+\left\lVert{{\overline{\mathbf{u}}_{\#}}}_{,i}-\bm{\psi}^{\gamma}({{\bm{\beta}}}_{\overline{\mathbf{u}}_{\#,i}}^{p})\right\rVert^{2},

(63)

where
T
denotes the size of the training set.
For details of the network architecture, we refer to Section
6.1
. The training workflow for finding
\theta^{*}
is summarized in Algorithm
1
, and inference within a kinetic solver is described in Algorithm

.

4.2
Neural network approximation error and data sampling

In addition to the errors analyzed in Theorem
4
a neural network-based approximation of
h^{\gamma}
introduces an approximation error defined by the inference accuracy of the used neural network. It directly follows for the neural network-based reconstruction
\mathbf{u}^{p}
that

\displaystyle\left\lVert\mathbf{u}^{p}-\mathbf{u}\right\rVert\leq\left\lVert\mathbf{u}^{p}-\mathbf{u}^{\gamma}\right\rVert+\left\lVert\mathbf{u}^{\gamma}-\mathbf{u}\right\rVert,

(64)

where the second term of the right-hand side is specified in Theorem
4
. In the following, we analyze the first term via the approximation error of the associated approximated gradient
\mathbf{g}^{p}_{{\overline{\mathbf{u}}_{\#}}}
under the assumption of global convexity of the neural network approximation
h^{p}
, i.e. an approximation given by Algorithm
2
. Numerical results in Section
6.1
demonstrate that the neural network approximation and regularization error are related, since a higher regularization yields smaller neural network approximation errors while increasing the regularization error and vice versa.
Due to the convexity of the neural network-based approximations of the entropy closure and the sampled moments, it is feasible to estimate the maximum interpolation error of the neural network in the convex hull of the training data, i.e.

\displaystyle\max_{{\overline{\mathbf{u}}_{\#}}\in\mathcal{C}\left(\left\{\overline{\mathbf{u}}_{\#,i}\right\}_{i=1}^{T}\right)}\left\lVert\mathbf{g}^{\gamma}_{{\overline{\mathbf{u}}_{\#}}}-\mathbf{g}^{p}_{{\overline{\mathbf{u}}_{\#}}}\right\rVert,

(65)

where
\mathcal{C}\left(\left\{\overline{\mathbf{u}}_{\#,i}\right\}_{i=1}^{T}\right)
is the convex hull of the training data moments
{\overline{\mathbf{u}}_{\#}}
.
For a given interpolation error tolerance one can employ ansatz (
37
), to derive the error in
\mathbf{u}^{p}
. For details, we refer to
[
51
, Section 4.1]
. The authors of
[

further provide a sampling strategy to minimize the maximal interpolation error (

\displaystyle B_{M,\tau}^{\gamma}=\left\{{{\bm{\beta}}}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}:\left\lVert{{\bm{\beta}}}\right\rVert<M\cup\lambda_{\text{min}}^{\gamma}>\tau\right\}.

(66)

uniformly
[

,

]

adapt Gaussian sampling for non-regularized closures, which stabilizes neural network training, at the expense of a strongly increased interpolation error.

5
Numerical solver

The entropy-based closure is combined with a kinetic scheme
[
21
,
3
,
35
]
. Thus, the moment system of Eq. (
8
) is discretized using a finite volume scheme in space,

\partial_{t}\mathbf{u}_{i}=-\frac{1}{A_{i}}\sum_{j\in N(i)}\mathbf{F}_{\rm{up}}(\mathbf{u}_{j},\mathbf{u}_{i})+\mathbf{G}(t,\mathbf{x}_{i},\mathbf{u}_{i}),

(68)

where
\mathbf{G}(t,\mathbf{x}_{i},\mathbf{u}_{i})
is the discretization of collision and source terms, and
\mathbf{F}_{\rm{up}}(\mathbf{u}_{j},\mathbf{u}_{i})
is the kinetic flux discretized with an upwind scheme. The kinetic flux is computed for all faces of neighboring cells
N(i)
of the current cell
i
of an unstructured mesh. The cell area of the current cell is denoted by
A_{i}
.
We arrive at the kinetic numerical flux

\displaystyle\begin{aligned} \mathbf{F}_{\rm{up}}(\mathbf{u}_{j},\mathbf{u}_{i})=\left<{\mathbf{m}}\mathbf{v}\cdot\mathbf{n}_{i,j}\left[{f_{\mathbf{u}_{i}}}\mathcal{H}\left(\mathbf{v}\cdot\mathbf{n}_{i,j}\right)+f_{\mathbf{u}_{j}}\left(1-\mathcal{H}\left(\mathbf{v}\cdot\mathbf{n}_{i,j}\right)\right)\right]\right>,\end{aligned}

(69)

where
\mathbf{n}_{i,j}
is the outward facing normal of the face between cells
i
and
j
scaled with the cell length and
\mathcal{H}
denotes the Heaviside step function.
The int egral is evaluated with a tensorized Gauss-Legendre quadrature.
We employ a second-order slope reconstruction for unstructured grids with the Barth-Jespersen flux limiter in
[
6
]
to obtain a second-order accurate spatial discretization. The reconstruction of the kinetic density
f_{\mathbf{u}}
uses a generic ansatz.

The second-order Heun’s scheme is used for temporal discretization at time step
k
in grid cell
i
:

\displaystyle\begin{aligned} \mathbf{u}_{i}^{*}&=\mathbf{u}_{i}^{k}-\frac{\Delta t}{A_{i}}\sum_{j\in N(i)}\mathbf{F}_{\rm{up}}(\mathbf{u}_{j}^{k},\mathbf{u}_{i}^{k})+\frac{\Delta t}{A_{i}}\mathbf{G}(\mathbf{u}_{i}^{k}),\\
\mathbf{u}_{i}^{**}&=\mathbf{u}_{i}^{*}-\frac{\Delta t}{A_{i}}\sum_{j\in N(i)}\mathbf{F}_{\rm{up}}(\mathbf{u}_{j}^{*},\mathbf{u}_{i}^{*})+\frac{\Delta t}{A_{i}}\mathbf{G}(\mathbf{u}_{i}^{*}),\\
\mathbf{u}_{i}^{k+1}&=\frac{1}{2}\left(\mathbf{u}_{i}^{k}+\mathbf{u}_{i}^{**}\right).\end{aligned}

(70)

The kinetic scheme is implemented in the open-source radiative transport package KiT-RT
[
35
]
and available on GitHub
9
9

9

https://github.com/CSMMLab/KiT-RT
.

7
Summary and conclusion

We have presented a framework for regularized, structure-preserving neural network surrogate models for the entropy-based closure of the moment system of linear kinetic equations.
Regularization addresses the challenge of training and inference of neural network-based surrogates near the boundary of the realizable set, and thus enables the creation of robust surrogates for high-order closures in two spatial dimensions. We have provided an error analysis for regularized network approximations and put it into the context of numerical errors of commonly used schemes for the kinetic equation.
The presented methods are tested on synthetic and simulation test cases, with applications to radiation transport. The neural network-based entropy closure leads to a computationally competitive simulation method with an advantageous trade-off between memory footprint and numerical error, compared to the P
N
, the traditional M
N
, and the S
N
method.
