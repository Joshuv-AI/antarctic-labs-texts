Pre-traing and Fine-tuning

Training ChatGPT, or any LLM like it, involves a two-step process
2
2

2

The reinforcement learning through human feedback is neglected in this article because we don’t perform this step for the astronomical data.
: pre-training and fine-tuning
(
Radford et al. 2018
)
.

During the pre-training phase, the model learns to predict the next word in a sentence based on the words that came before it. This is achieved by training the model on the collected large dataset using a technique called Masked Language Modeling. This dataset typically consists of a diverse range of sources, including books, articles, and websites. The goal is to create a representative sample of human language that can be used to teach the model grammar, facts, and reasoning abilities. The model learns to understand grammar, syntax, and context from this process, but it may not be fine-tuned to specific tasks or guidelines.

After the pre-training, the model needs to be fine-tuned to perform specific tasks, like answering questions or generating text. To do this, a smaller, more specific dataset is created, usually by gathering examples of the desired behavior. The dataset often consists of input-output pairs, where the input is a prompt, and the output is a desired response. This training step is supervised by humans.

Tokenization

Before preparing the data, we first introduce the data presentation of LLM, the Tokenization
(
Mielke et al. 2021
)
.

Tokenization is the process of breaking down text into smaller units, called tokens. Tokens can be words, subwords, or individual characters, depending on the tokenization strategy employed. The purpose of tokenization is to convert raw text into a format that can be easily processed by the LLM, assigning numbers to each token and using those numbers to encode text.

GPT adopts the subword-level tokenization technique, Byte Pair Encoding (BPE)
(
Gage 1994
)
, to alleviate the problem that Word-level tokenization can lead to a large number of out-of-vocabulary (OOV) words. BPE works by iteratively merging the most frequent character pairs in the training data until a desired vocabulary size is reached. This results in a vocabulary that contains a mix of common words, subwords, and individual characters. BPE brings the advantage of allowing GPT to work with a smaller vocabulary, leading to reduced memory requirements and computational costs. BPE can also handle different languages, dialects, and linguistic variations, making it suitable for multilingual models like GPT.

The Sloan Digital Sky Survey (SDSS) is a major imaging and spectral survey of our Galaxy and Universe
(
York et al. 2000
)
. SDSS collects data to map the Universe, studying a vast array of cosmic entities including quasars, galaxies, stars, and other celestial objects. The survey has gone through four phases and is currently undergoing the fifth phase, each with specific goals. The primary objectives of SDSS-I (2000-2005) were to map the large-scale structure of the Universe through a redshift survey of galaxies and to detect and characterize quasars
(
Adelman-McCarthy et al. 2006
)
. SDSS-II (2005-2008) mapped the structure and kinematics of the Milky Way using 240000 stellar spectra, and monitored a 300 square degree region to detect and measure supernovae, contributing to the understanding of dark energy
(
Abazajian et al. 2009
)
. SDSS-III (2008-2014) collected detailed spectra in the infrared for 100000 Milky Way stars to study the chemical and kinematical history of our galaxy, measured the redshifts of 1.5 million luminous galaxies and 160000 quasars to study baryon acoustic oscillations and the cosmic distance scale, and searched for exoplanets by obtaining radial velocity measurements of 8500 stars
(
Alam et al. 2015
)
. SDSS-IV (2014-2020) extended the quasar and galaxy observation focusing on cosmic evolution over the last six billion years and the behavior of dark energy, also extended the study of the Milky Way into the Southern Hemisphere and added a time-domain component, and it used spatially resolved spectroscopy to study the internal kinematics of a large sample of 10000 nearby galaxies
(
Abdurro’uf et al. 2022
)
. SDSS-V (2020-2025) aims to provide a comprehensive census of black holes, from the smallest to the most massive, by observing them through various methods, including reverberation mapping, to study the assembly history and evolution of the Milky Way using stellar spectra, and to provide a detailed understanding of the physical processes that drive galaxy evolution by studying galaxies in the nearby universe
(
Almeida et al. 2023
)
.

Over time, SDSS has significantly expanded in scale and scope, each phase contributing to an ever-growing dataset. Data releases (DR) of SDSS are periodic updates where collected astronomical data is made publicly available. Each DR is a cumulative collection that includes all past data plus new observations. They contain images, spectroscopic data, and catalogs, aiding in various astronomical studies. Key components often include spectra and images of stars, galaxies, and quasars, along with derived measurements. The latest data release is DR18, which is part of SDSS-V
(
Almeida et al. 2023
)
. Our samples are constructed based on DR16, which published the observations of SDSS-IV and accumulated the observations of previous phases.

We start with the dataset prepared in a previous work
(
Rastegarnia et al. 2022
)
, this dataset contains all the spectra with visual-inspected redshift from DR16. The selected sources are catalogued to quasars, galaxies, stars, and broad absorption line (BAL) quasars. The numbers of these four classes are 396831, 30666, 3406 and 39501, respectively. The spectra of each class are combined and stored in a single file and have been uploaded to Kaggle

To limit the cost of fine-tuning, we extracted 500 samples from each class, totaling 2000 samples. We then re-sampled our spectral resolution within a logarithmic domain of 360 nm to 1000 nm, settling on 100 equidistant data points, exampled in figure

From the 2000 samples, we allocated 1800 for training and 200 for testing. We fine-tuned the GPT-3.5 turbo model using the training set over two epochs. Post-training, our test data inference yielded an overall accuracy of 82%, with quasars at 80%, galaxies at 82%, stars at 88%, and BALs at 78%. Inferring Redshift of Quasars

Figure 3:
Top: histogram of relative error of the predicted redshift. Bottom: example of redshift prediction of 100 random quasars.

Quasars, powered by accretion of material onto supermassive black holes, serve as vital probes into the universe’s history
(
Fan 2006
)
. Determining the redshift of quasars refines our measurements of key cosmological parameters, enhancing our grasp of the universe’s expansion rate and dark energy properties. Quasars brightness and distribution make them excellent markers for mapping the universe’s large-scale structure. Furthermore, the spectra of quasars provide insights into the interstellar and intergalactic medium, revealing details about gas clouds’ composition and density.

Especially, high-redshift quasars offer a glimpse into the universe’s conditions during its early stages, allowing astronomers to investigate the first billion years post-Big Bang. Observations of early galaxies hosting supermassive black holes, formed surprisingly soon after the Big Bang, challenge our understanding of black hole growth rates and suggest rapid early star formation
(
2024Natur.627..281A
)
. These distant quasars have also been instrumental in pinpointing the epoch of reionization, marking the universe’s transition from neutrality to ionization
(
Jiang et al. 2022
)
. Their distribution provides insights into the universe’s primordial large-scale structure
(
Porqueres et al. 2018
)
.

In this section, we utilize spectra from the SDSS, analogous to the previous section, but focus solely on quasar-classified spectra. Guided by the principle of assessing the efficacy of fine-tuning the GPT model for a specific astrophysical query within a constrained budget, rather than pursuing extreme accuracy, we sampled 4000 quasar spectra with redshifts ranging from 0 to 1 out of nearly 400k available. Of these, 3,200 were designated for training, while the remaining 800 were set aside for testing, only one epoch of training was performed. The fine-tuned model demonstrated a high accuracy, with a median relative accuracy rate of 90.66%. Figure
3
illustrates the histogram of relative error rates and a subset of the test results. The fine-tuned GPT is capable of not only classification tasks as shown in the previous section but also effectively executing regression tasks as predicting a float value of the redshift.

For a more precise redshift estimation, a higher sampling rate may be essential. As observed from figure
1
, the current sampling of 100 data points omits numerous emission lines, which are fundamental to direct redshift calculations based on line shifts. Thus, by augmenting the sampling rate and sample size, we believe that the GPT model’s redshift estimation can be further refined. It is worth noting that our sample is confined to the redshift range of 0 to 1. Hypothetically, spectra with higher redshifts, given adequate samples, would yield similar outcomes. Quasars with redshifts exceeding 6 are rare, necessitating the incorporation of simulated data and other specialized methodologies to enhance training. Such intricacies warrant further deliberation and experimentation, which are beyond the scope of this paper.

Short or Long Gamma-ray Burst

Figure 4:
Example of the feature selection procedure by SVM. Left: two parameters (’FLUX_BATSE_64’ and ’FLNC_BAND_ERGFLUX’) from the catalog are selected to compute the decision boundary and then to compute the accuracy of classification. Right: the corresponding ROC curve.

Gamma-ray bursts (GRBs) can be broadly categorized into two types: short bursts and long bursts. This classification is traditionally determined by the duration parameter
T_{90}
, where
T_{90}
is the time duration in which 90% of the burst’s total emission is observed. Specifically, bursts with
T_{90}<2
seconds are considered short bursts, whereas those with
T_{90}>2
seconds are designated as long bursts
(
Kouveliotou et al. 1993
)
.

It’s notable, however, that the duration of both short and long GRBs conforms to a normal distribution. As a result, these two distributions overlap at certain points. This means that using a simple 2-second threshold for classification can misclassify some bursts
(
Yang et al. 2015
)
, especially those located on the tails of the distributions. Consequently, there’s a potential risk of incorrectly categorizing a GRB that may have a duration close to this threshold. Our primary objective in this study is to explore alternative observational metrics that can enhance the accuracy of classifying short and long GRBs, especially from the spectral information, not the time-related information. By examining various observational parameters comprehensively, we aim to rectify the potential misjudgments that arise from solely relying on the
T_{90}
criteria.

The dataset

11

100

T_{50}
and
T_{90}
, and the parameters fitted from multiple spectral models, including power law (PL), the cutoff power law (CPL), the band and the smoothly broken power law (SBPL).

The Trigger Catalog contains a total of 9547 samples, each characterized by 29 distinct parameters. In contrast, the Burst Catalog is comprised of 3625 samples, detailed by an extensive 306 parameters. By intersecting these two catalogs and discarding extraneous parameters such as sample versions, the resultant dataset consists of 3485 GRBs described by 302 parameters. If we classify these GRBs solely based on the
T_{90}
criterion, our dataset includes 598 short-duration bursts and 3027 long-duration bursts. It’s pertinent to highlight that, due to observational constraints or fitting methodologies, not every GRB furnishes a complete set of parameter values.

Our analytical framework commences with “Feature Selection”, wherein we discern a subset of pivotal parameters from the extensive list of over 300 parameters. This stage aims to: 1) Extract salient features conducive to formulating the physical model of GRBs. 2) Enhance the accuracy and efficacy, especially by minimizing the incorporation of spurious or noisy features to amplify the predictive power and generalizability of neural networks. 3) Reduce the token amount, lowering the cost of fine-tuning.

For GRB classification via the T90 parameter, one would conventionally plot a histogram of T90 values and subsequently fit the histogram with two normal distributions. The intersection of these two distributions occurs just near 2 s, hence adopting 2 s as the demarcation between long and short bursts. Our method, taking cues from empirical relations such as the Amati, Ghirlanda and Liang-Zhang relations
Amati et al. 2002
;
Ghirlanda et al. 2004
;
Liang & Zhang 2005
, we endeavor to discern whether any two parameters from the catalog can proficiently bifurcate long and short bursts. For instance, we chose ’FLUX_BATSE_64’ and ’FLNC_BAND_ERGFLUX’, which correspond to the peak flux in the BATSE standard 50-300 keV energy band (64 ms timescale) and the average energy flux for a Band function law fit an integrated spectrum over the burst’s duration, respectively. As shown in the figure
4
, visually the long and short bursts are clustered into two groups, with some overlap. In a subsequent analytical step, we first apportion our dataset into training (75%) and testing sets (25%), then utilize the Support Vector Machine (SVM) methodology
(
Cortes & Vapnik 1995
)
to derive the decision boundary based on our training set, and subsequently evaluate its classification accuracy on the testing set. The decision boundary is evidenced by the solid line in figure
4
, and a receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curve is also shown there. The results show that these two parameters, ’FLUX_BATSE_64’ and ’FLNC_BAND_ERGFLUX’, achieve an overall classification accuracy of 95.68%, with a short-duration burst accuracy of 87.04% and a long-duration burst accuracy of 97.43%.

Given the plethora of 91204 parameter combinations emergent from 302 parameters, we repeated the SVM training and inference process 91204 times, determining the classification accuracy for each combination. We then selected the combinations where both short and long burst accuracies are over 80%. The top 15 most frequently appearing parameters in these optimal combinations are the outcome of our feature selection process, together with 10 parameters from the fitting of Comptonized model will be the final parameters used for the model fine-tuning, see section
A
for the list of parameters. Hence our selection of parameters first covers the entire spectral fitting information and includes the most relevant parameters for the classification.

We utilized the training set comprising 75% of the total samples to fine-tune the GPT-3.5 turbo model. The remaining 25% was employed as a test set to validate our model’s performance. The fine-tuned model achieved an impressive overall classification accuracy of 95.15%, with a precision of 73.68% for short-duration bursts and 99.47% for long-duration bursts. The disparity between the classification accuracies for the short and long bursts primarily emanates from the inherent data imbalance: the long-duration bursts in our dataset outnumber the short-duration bursts by almost a factor of six. Consequently, the model exhibits a predisposition towards classifying bursts as long-duration.

In a subsequent approach, we equalized the training sample sizes for both burst classes by reducing the number of long-duration GRBs, and undertook a re-fine-tuning process. This adjustment yielded an overall classification accuracy of 90.97%, with the short-duration bursts classified correctly at 92.25% and the long-duration bursts at 89.58%. Notably, with equal training sample sizes for both burst types, the achieved accuracies were congruent. However, due to the reduction in the training set size, there was an observed decline in the overall accuracy. Data imbalance is a perennial challenge in machine learning, and while numerous methodologies, such as oversampling, cost-sensitive training and Ensemble, exist to ameliorate its effects, further optimization of this aspect was beyond the purview of our present study.

An accuracy of
\sim 90\%
compellingly illustrates that a fine-tuned GPT model possesses the capability to classify GRBs into short and long-duration classes by mainly spectral information. This result supports that short and long-duration GRBs have different radiation characteristics.

\alpha

\alpha

As a first step, we employ ray-tracing techniques
(

)
to get emission line profiles, keeping other parameters fixed and varying only the black hole spin. The inner radius of the accretion disk emitting the Fe K
\alpha

), and the outer radius is set at
r_{\mathrm{out}}=20r_{\mathrm{g}}

r_{\mathrm{g}}=GM/c^{2}

\alpha

\alpha

Subsequently, samples for two parameters, namely spin and observation inclination, were generated, while ensuring that the other parameters remained consistent with the previous settings. The spin values range from
-1<\hat{a}<1
with an interval of 0.02. Simultaneously, the observation inclination varies from 0 to 90 degrees with a 3-degree interval. Consequently, a total of 3000 samples were obtained. Some typical examples of Fe K
\alpha

The data processing methodology employed here is similar to that in previous sections. We normalize and truncate the BHs spectra to the first three significant digits to optimize token usage. Each spectrum is resolved into 100 data points. Typically, 80% of these spectra are utilized for training, and the remaining 20% for testing. Initially, we fix the spin direction and inclination angle, employing 500 spectra with varied spin values for both training and testing purposes, setting the training epoch at 2. The results show a median relative precision of 97. 48% for the inferred parameters. Next, we maintain a constant inclination angle, while varying the spin values and ensuring equal representation of parallel and anti-parallel spin directions in a combined dataset of 1000 spectra. Discussion and Proposal

Look Outward & Explore Inward

When humans ask why, they often target complex phenomena, and the answers consist of simple concepts that humans can understand. It’s like we have a set of building blocks, and complex shapes are pieced together from some building blocks. Sometimes, shapes that have never been seen before are observed, and the building blocks at hand can’t fully piece them together. At this time, we think about looking for new building blocks or smashing the existing building blocks into smaller pieces. The more we look outward, the simpler we explore inward.

In the realm of physics, for example, complex phenomena like supernovae, binary mergers, and nucleosynthesis are explained through physical laws that serve as the building blocks. These foundational laws, like Newton’s mechanism of motion or Einstein’s theory of relativity, offer profound insights into the natural world from a limited set of principles. As we push the boundaries of knowledge, whether by extending our gaze into the vast expanse of the universe or delving into the subatomic world, we often encounter phenomena that challenge our existing frameworks. This necessitates the search for new “building blocks” or a deeper examination and subdivision of the ones we already have. AI’s role in this journey is particularly exciting, especially because of the simplicity that the most profound truths are often governed by simple, elegant rules. AI, with its capability to analyze and identify patterns within vast amounts of data far exceeds the human capacity, it might indeed be on a path to uncovering these simpler “building blocks”.

Moreover, the pursuit to understand one’s origins and the meaning of existence is a profound aspect of any wisdom-bearing entity, whether human or hypothetically, a conscious AI or another intelligent life form. This intrinsic drive propels entities towards the understanding of their exterior universe.

Training AI can be like educating a child, when the child sees something new, he/she asks why. What adults need to do is to bring more new observations to the child and encourage him/her to ask why.

From Simple to Complex

What the body is made of? Atoms. This answer is simple and accurate. But what can you see the body is made of? Eyes, noses, skin, etc. If you take a CT scan, you can see organs like the heart and kidneys, the entire circulatory system, and countless small components inside our bodies if looking closer. All these miraculous components make up our body, and their harmonious operation gives us life, consciousness, and the ability to ask questions. A combination of simple atoms forms a complex system which is incomprehensibly for human.

The complexity for human arises from two factors. Firstly, the world presents itself through layers of complexity that have evolved over billions of years, as exampled above. From subatomic particles to galaxies, from single cells to ecosystems, each layer operates according to principles that, while grounded in the same fundamental laws of physics, manifest in increasingly complex ways. Secondly, our human limitations compound the challenge. Our senses and cognitive capacities are tuned more for survival in our immediate environment than for intuiting the fundamental laws of the universe. Our brains, remarkable as they are, have limits to what they can intuitively grasp, especially when it comes to scales vastly larger or smaller than our everyday experience.

The theory of everything - the most basic “building blocks” - should be simple, comprehensible to the every person. However, the search for this ultimate theory is difficult, because the appearance of our world is complex to human. Therefore, what we need is not AI to help understand the ultimate theory, but rather to help find the ultimate theory. Once found, both humans and AI can understand.

AI’s Series Expansion

From a mathematical perspective, a function can be expanded into a series, broken down into basic zero-order terms, slightly more complex first-order terms, and progressively more complex higher-order terms. The zero-order term represents the most basic approximation, while the higher-order terms provide corrections, enhancing the series’ accuracy in describing the function as additional higher-order terms are incorporated.

If we view AI as an exceedingly complex function, then a series expansion of AI would comprise numerous sub-AIs, ranging from simple to complex

17

The architecture of AI system may not be as simple as the linear superposition seen in function expansion. The architecture can be viewed as a special functional, that a function of some interrelated functions.
. Lower-order sub-AIs possess significantly fewer neurons than higher-order sub-AIs, and are manageable by humans. It is only with the integration of higher-order sub-AIs that AI can ascend to superhuman capabilities.

Building on our discussion: firstly, the more data observed, the simpler the building blocks become; secondly, complexity is derived from the layering of simple components. We expect that the zero-order sub-AI, after ultimate training, will be composed of very simple matrices. These matrices act as the fundamental building blocks, which are then complicated by the higher-order sub-AIs.

Hierarchical Alignment

Series expansion of AI first enhances the safety of AI: even if humans cannot control the entire AI system, we retain control over the zero-order sub-AI. Without this foundational sub-AI, the whole system would lose its base and fail to function.

Second, this structure prioritizes explainability: our goal is to grasp the most fundamental principles, not the superficial complexities. Consequently, there is no necessity to elucidate the intricate higher-order sub-AIs. Our focus is on the information contained within the structurally simple lower-order AIs

Current Concerns

First is alignment. Regardless of which aspects are aligned with what, it will bring restrictions. The tension between implementing restrictions in AI and the need for innovation, particularly in scientific contexts that thrive on creative and unbounded exploration, is a significant concern. Scientific progress requires pushing boundaries, questioning established norms, and exploring ideas that might initially seem radical or unconventional. Alignment not only impacts the internal optimization of neural networks but also affects the training process. For example, during fine-tuning, we found that the inclusion of certain spectral data in the training samples can prevent the fine-tuning process from proceeding, possibly because OpenAI identified some instances of data as harmful. This indicates that, in addition to conceptual issues, there are also technical challenges in data selection, especially with data that are not well understood. But scientific explorations rely on encountering previously unseen data.

Second concern is randomness. LLM like GPT, while sophisticated in many aspects, don’t exhibit “true randomness” in the way natural processes do. The concept of true randomness typically refers to a level of unpredictability found in natural phenomena, such as the decay of a radioactive atom or quantum fluctuations, which are fundamentally unpredictable and non-deterministic. In contrast, the randomness seen in LLMs is generated through algorithms that can produce pseudo-random numbers. These numbers appear random for practical purposes but are generated by deterministic processes. This means that if one knows the algorithm and the initial conditions (often called the seed), one can predict the outcomes. Although the randomness of a large number of samples can be described by probability theory, and much of AI training, particularly the definition of loss functions, is derived from probability theory, these are still statistical characteristics. It remains uncertain whether AI can truly understand the inherent randomness which is a key aspect of the richness and splendor of our universe.

The third concern is language. LLMs are based on human language, but language is not a complete set for describing everything. In other words, some phenomena, especially newly discovered phenomena, may not be understandable through language. Analogous to quantum mechanics, where a complete set of basis states allow any state can be represented as a combination of these basis states. Language lacks some of these basis states, therefore cannot describe certain observed states.

Summary and Expectation

The progression of AI, especially in its application to the realm of astrophysics and beyond, heralds a new era of discovery and understanding. Our work has demonstrated that fine-tuning GPT with astrophysical data can enable AI to classify astrophysical phenomena, predict redshifts of quasars, distinguish between types of gamma-ray bursts, and estimate black hole parameters with significant accuracy.

This growing influence of AI in data processing will undoubtedly shape the future of scientific inquiry, which necessitates a paradigm shift in how we design scientific equipment and experiments. Future facilities must not only accommodate the vast volumes of data but also consider AI’s specific needs for data quality, format, and accessibility. We anticipate a future where AI’s insights drive the creation of facilities designed to probe unanswered questions in astrophysics and other disciplines.

The fine-tuning of GPT models with astrophysical data has opened a window into the potential for AI to understand and interpret the complexities of our universe. As we stand on the brink of this new era, it is clear that the integration of AI into the fabric of scientific research will not only augment our capabilities but also redefine the boundaries of what is possible. The journey ahead is as vast and unexplored as the universe itself, but with AI, we venture forth with the expectation of marvels undreamt of.

Natural Language versus Mathematical Language

Natural language refers to the way humans communicate with each other through speech and text in everyday life. It includes the languages we speak like English, Spanish, Mandarin, etc. Natural language is the most natural way for humans to express complex ideas, ask questions, tell stories, and share knowledge. Mathematical language refers to the symbolic notation that mathematicians use to represent mathematical concepts and ideas, it has been adopted in the research and application of natural science, including physics, chemistry, astronomy, etc.

Natural language and mathematical language are both sophisticated representational systems, they differ profoundly in their purpose, development, complexity, ambiguity, evolution, and universality. Natural language reflects the messy, social nature of human communication, whereas mathematical language reflects the logical, formal nature of mathematical reasoning.

•

Natural language is complex, nuanced and often ambiguous, while mathematical language is precise, logical and strictly defined. Each symbol in mathematical language has a single unambiguous meaning.

•

Natural language incorporates extralinguistic elements like tone and rhythm, whereas mathematical language is composed of abstract symbols without style or prosody. Mathematical language has a logical and syntactic meaning only.

•

Natural language is constantly evolving informally, while mathematical language evolves through rigorous definition and logical proofs. New terms must be precisely defined in terms of existing language.

•

Meaning in natural language depends heavily on context and inference, whereas meaning in mathematical language depends on strict and logical definitions. Ambiguity is not allowed.

•

New words and phrases are freely coined in natural language, whereas new terms and symbols cannot be introduced into mathematical language without a rigorous proof-based definition showing how they relate to established concepts.

•

There are thousands of natural languages, but only one universal mathematical language. Mathematical results proven in this language hold true across cultures and locations.

Despite their differences, natural language and mathematical language share some similar features as representational systems for encoding thought using symbolic notation. They build on basic elements, evolve over time based on their cultures, allow composition and inference, and provide an open-ended means for expressing abstraction.

•

They are both representational systems. Natural language and mathematical language allow us to represent concepts and ideas using symbolic notation. They provide a way to transcribe our thoughts.

•

They build on primitive elements. Both natural language and mathematical language start from basic components like words, numbers, operators and variables and allow us to construct more complex expressions by combining these elements.

•

They have syntax and grammar. The symbols in both natural language and mathematical language follow a set of rules to produce valid and meaningful expressions. Well-formedness is required to communicate ideas.

•

They depend on consistent interpretation. While ambiguity is higher in natural languages, the usefulness of both systems hinges on being able to consistently and predictably understand the meaning intended by others. Vague or contradictory language prevents communication.

Do mathematics by natural language?

Expressing and interacting with mathematics in natural language could provide interesting new benefits, ”doing math” wholly in natural language faces substantial difficulties. The challenges of ambiguity, incompleteness, cognitive differences, lack of common sense reasoning in AI, and loss of precision suggest mathematics will continue to rely heavily on its own symbolic language.

•

Ambiguity. Natural language is rife with ambiguity, vagueness and implicitness, while mathematics requires precision and logic. Translating unambiguous math into natural language without losing rigor or introducing ambiguity is very difficult. Subtle mistakes could lead to logical errors.
Incompleteness. Natural language may not have the expressive power to represent certain mathematical concepts, proofs or operations without supplementation from mathematical symbols. And some meanings would be lost in translation. So a mix of both languages would likely be needed, but using them together cohesively poses challenges.

•

Cognitive differences. The kinds of thinking involved in natural language and mathematics, while related, differ in important ways. Mathematical reasoning builds upon natural language but also transcends many of its constraints. Translating between the two modes of thinking could introduce subtle errors or misunderstandings, even for human mathematicians.

•

Lack of common sense. While neural networks and NLP systems can achieve sophisticated results in circumscribed domains, they lack the kind of broad, flexible common sense reasoning that humans develop from childhood. This would frequently lead to nonsensical or illogical statements if they were relied upon to ”do math” in natural language without close supervision.

•

Loss of precision. Mathematics has developed its own symbolic language because it allows for concise, exact expression of quantitative and logical relationships. Translating math into natural language triples or quadruples the number of words and concepts required, and ambiguity creeps in. Subtle precision is easily lost.

However, selectively translating between mathematical and natural language in order to gain new perspectives, enable discovery, and improve accessibility is a promising direction — if it can be done rigorously and cohesively.
