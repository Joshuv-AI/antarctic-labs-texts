Deterministic Clustering in High Dimensional Spaces: Sketches and Approximation
Vincent Cohen-Addad, David Saulpic, Chris Schwiegelshohn

Abstract
In all state-of-the-art sketching and coreset techniques for clustering, as well as in the best known fixed-parameter tractable approximation algorithms, randomness plays a key role. For the classic k k -median and k k -means problems, there are no known deterministic dimensionality reduction procedure or coreset construction that avoid an exponential dependency on the input dimension d d , the precision parameter ε − 1 \varepsilon^{-1} or k k . Furthermore, there is no coreset construction that succeeds with probability 1 − 1 / n 1-1/n and whose size does not depend on the number of input points, n n . This has led researchers in the area to ask what is the power of randomness for clustering sketches [Feldman WIREs Data Mining Knowl. Discov’20]. Similarly, the best approximation ratio achievable deterministically without a complexity exponential in the dimension are 1 + 2 1+\sqrt{2} for k k -median [Cohen-Addad, Esfandiari, Mirrokni, Narayanan, STOC’22] and 6.12903 6.12903 for k k -means [Grandoni, Ostrovsky, Rabani, Schulman, Venkat, Inf. Process. Lett.’22]. Those are the best results, even when allowing a complexity FPT in the number of clusters k k : this stands in sharp contrast with the ( 1 + ε ) (1+\varepsilon) -approximation achievable in that case, when allowing randomization. In this paper, we provide deterministic sketches constructions for clustering, whose size bounds are close to the best-known randomized ones. We show how to compute a dimension reduction onto ε − O ⁡ ( 1 ) ​ log ⁡ k \varepsilon^{-O(1)}\log k dimensions in time k O ⁡ ( ε − O ⁡ ( 1 ) + log ⁡ log ⁡ k ) ​ poly ​ ( n ​ d ) k^{O\left(\varepsilon^{-O(1)}+\log\log k\right)}\text{poly}(nd) , and how to build a coreset of size O ⁡ ( k 2 ​ log 3 ​ k ​ ε − O ⁡ ( 1 ) ) O\left(k^{2}\log^{3}k\varepsilon^{-O(1)}\right) in time 2 ε O ⁡ ( 1 ) ​ k ​ log 3 ​ k + k O ⁡ ( ε − O ⁡ ( 1 ) + log ⁡ log ⁡ k ) ​ poly ​ ( n ​ d ) 2^{\varepsilon^{O(1)}k\log^{3}k}+k^{O\left(\varepsilon^{-O(1)}+\log\log k\right)}\text{poly}(nd) . In the case where k k is small, this answers an open question of [Feldman WIDM’20] and [Munteanu and Schwiegelshohn, Künstliche Intell. ’18] on whether it is possible to efficiently compute coresets deterministically. We also construct a deterministic algorithm for computing ( 1 + ε ) (1+\varepsilon) -approximation to k k -median and k k -means in high dimensional Euclidean spaces in time 2 k 2 ​ log 3 ​ k / ε O ⁡ ( 1 ) ​ poly ​ ( n ​ d ) 2^{k^{2}\log^{3}k/\varepsilon^{O(1)}}\text{poly}(nd) , close to the best randomized complexity of 2 ( k / ε ) O ⁡ ( 1 ) ​ n ​ d 2^{(k/\varepsilon)^{O(1)}}nd (see [Kumar, Sabharwal, Sen, JACM 10] and [Bhattacharya, Jaiswal, Kumar, TCS’18]). Clustering is the task of partitioning a dataset in a meaningful way such that similar data elements are in the same part and dissimilar ones in different parts. Clustering problems are among the most studied problems in theoretical computer science.
Clustering applications range from data analysis

1.1
Our Results

We phrase our results in terms of the
(k,z)
-clustering problems, a generalization of both
k
-median and
k
-means: the cost function is
\textsc{cost}(P,\mathcal{S})=\sum_{p\in P}\min_{s\in\mathcal{S}}\text{dist}^{z}(p,s)
, and the goal is to find the solution
\mathcal{S}
that minimizes the cost. Thus,
z=1
is the
k
-median problem,
z=2
is
k
-means.

Our main technical contribution lies in designing sketches to answer
1.2
. We start by showing a dimension reduction result:

Theorem 1.3

(Informal, see
theorem

4.1
)
.

Let
P
be a set of
n
points in
\mathds{R}^{d}
. We can compute in time
k^{\varepsilon^{-O(z)}+\log\log k}\text{poly}(nd)
a sketch of
P
onto
\varepsilon^{-O(z)}\cdot\log k
dimensions that preserves the cost of any
(k,z)
-clustering.

The optimal target dimension, even using randomization, is
\Omega\left(\varepsilon^{-2}\log k\right)
: our result has therefore an optimal dependency in
k
. The best known deterministic result was
O\left(\varepsilon^{-2}\log n\right)
(implicit in the work of
[
MMR19
]
), with a dependency in
n
that we precisely want to avoid.

Building on this dimension reduction result, we show how to construct coresets deterministically:

Theorem 1.4

(Informal, see
5.3
)
.

Let
P
be a set of
n
points in
\mathds{R}^{d}
. We can compute in time
2^{\varepsilon^{-O(z)}k\cdot\log^{3}k}+k^{\varepsilon^{-O(z)}+\log\log k}\text{poly}(nd)

\Omega\subset\mathds{R}^{d+1}

O\left(k^{2}\log^{2}k\varepsilon^{-O(z)}\right)

k

,

\textsc{cost}(\Omega,\mathcal{S}^{\prime})=(1\pm\varepsilon)\textsc{cost}(P,\mathcal{S}),

where

is the set

where we append a 0 coordinate at each center.

This notion of coreset is used in

Those sketches directly imply deterministic streaming algorithm for

In case

1.2
Implication of our work when allowing randomization.

We believe that the sketches we develop have interest beyond computing a
(1+\varepsilon)
-approximation. Indeed, we develop new sketching techniques, that improve over the best known bounds for some regime of parameters, and that are of independent interest.

We remark that, if allowing randomization, our new sketch constructions can be implemented faster and provide useful alternatives to the existing ones. For dimension reduction, the randomized running time of our procedure is the one to compute a Johnson-Lindenstrauss (JL) transform, in general
\tilde{O}(nd)
. However, we can use
any
JL transform to get our result, including the sparse transforms (e.g.,
[
KN14
]
) that allow for further speed-ups. This stands in contrast with the
[
MMR19
]
result, which needs a particular additional property on the JL transform they use, namely sub-gaussian tails, and therefore cannot use the fastest JL embeddings.

More importantly, our coreset construction can be implemented using only uniform sampling. The importance of uniform sampling was illustrated in the recent paper
[
BCJ+22
]
, as it gives an easier way of constructing coreset.
Our construction improves the one of
[
BCJ+22
]
by a factor
k
: if one can compute a coreset of size
T
for any
ring

P\subset\left\{x:\|x\|\in[1/2,1]\right\}
, then for any input one can compute a coreset of size
O\left(T\cdot k\log(1/\varepsilon)/\varepsilon\right)
– as opposed to
O\left(Tk^{2}/\varepsilon\right)
in
[
BCJ+22
]
.

This apply to any metric where the VC-dimension of the uniform function space can be bounded, including shortest-path metric of minor-free graphs and Fréchet distance.

1.3
Our Techniques

Given a coreset for
(k,z)
-clustering, it is quite straightforward to compute a
(1+\varepsilon)
-approximation: one can simply enumerate all partitions of the coreset into
k
parts, evaluate their cost and keep the best partition. The running time is thus
\min(k^{\text{coreset-size}},\text{corest-size}^{kd})
, where the latter bound can be obtained through the result of
[
IKI94
]
.

Therefore, we focus on the construction of a coreset. For that, we first require a dimension reduction tool, that we explain first.

1.3.1
Dimension Reduction

High-level overview.

There currently exist only two known methods that enable deterministic dimension reduction. The first is principal component analysis and its generalizations to other norms. Applying principal component analysis to
k
-means requires a target dimension of
\Omega(k)
in order to preserve the
k
-means cost of each partition
[
CEM+15
]
up to a constant factor and thus this attempt falls short of the
O(\log k)
dimension we are aiming for.

The only known alternative is the conditional expectation method for derandomizing Johnson Lindenstrauss transforms by Engebretsen, Indyk and O’Donnell
[
EIO02
]
.
At a high level, this method selects random bits of the projection one by one, and selects the value of the next bit so as to minimize the number of “bad events”, namely the number of vectors whose lengths are distorted by a too high factor, conditioned on the bit’s value.

When applying this idea naively to a candidate embedding for
k
-clustering, the number of “bad events” becomes the number of clusterings for which the cost is not preserved.
As there exist
n^{dk}
many distinct clusterings (
[
IKI94
]
), this is infeasible in time polynomial in
n,d,k
.
Thus, our goal is to reduce the number of bad events necessary to count, in order to use the conditional expectation method.

For this, our first contribution is to reduce the number of distinct clusterings we need to test for. More precisely, we
show that for any cluster, a
(1+\varepsilon)
approximate center lies in the convex hull of a subset of the cluster of size
O\left(\varepsilon^{-2}\right)
, that we call a
witness set
.
Using a careful discretization argument, we can show that it is possible to enumerate over all candidate solutions in the convex hulls of such witness sets and thereby also to enumerate over all possible centers induced by any clustering. This reduces the clustering problem with arbitrary centers in
\mathds{R}^{d}
to a discrete one, where centers are restricted to a finite set, and it is therefore enough to preserve the distances from input points to the discrete set of centers.
There are
n^{O\left(\varepsilon^{-2}\right)}
many possible witness sets – any set of
O\left(\varepsilon^{-2}\right)
input points. Hence, counting the “bad events” now requires to test
n^{O\left(\varepsilon^{-2}\right)}
many clusterings. This is still infeasible in our target running time, but, crucially, does not depend in the dimension
d
anymore.

To improve further, the idea is to reduce the number of distinct points to
\text{poly}(k,\varepsilon^{-1})
, in order to reduce the number of possible witness sets. A
coreset
is the most natural idea here. However the typical guarantees of coresets are not strong enough for our purposes: as explained in
section

2
, coresets preserve the cost to any set of
k
centers, while our goal here is to preserve the cost of any partition. In turn, we naturally introduce the notion of
partition coreset
, which are small sets preserving the cost of any partition.
We show how to construct deterministically partition coresets of size
k^{\varepsilon^{-O(z)}}
; hence, using the witness sets of this partition coreset, we are able to limit the number of clusterings to
k^{O\left(\varepsilon^{-2}\right)}
. We can therefore list all the potential bad events and count their number, and reduce the dimension to
\varepsilon^{-O(z)}\log k
– log of the number of distances to be preserved.
We point that the notion of partition coreset, more general then the usual coreset definition, may be of independent interest and have further application in the future.

Construction of partition coreset.

To build a partition coreset, a first attempt would be to use a
k^{\prime}
clustering such that its cost is at most an
\varepsilon
-fraction of the cost of an optimal
k
-clustering. In that case, the cost of snapping each input point to its closest center would be negligible, and the cost of any partition would be preserved. Unfortunately, such a set has size at least
\varepsilon^{-d}

in the worst case.
Instead, we show – and this is our main technical contribution – that there exists a
k^{\prime}
clustering such that for each cluster, either the cluster is very cheap, or its cost cannot decrease by adding additional centers. We show such a statement for
k^{\prime}=k^{\varepsilon^{-O(z)}}
. The guarantee we ask on the
k^{\prime}
clustering is somewhat non-standard, as the cost in each cluster should not decrease by adding
k
centers (as opposed to the global cost).
In the first case, the set of centers directly gives a partition coreset. The second case implies essentially that the points in some cluster of the
k^{\prime}
clustering pay the same cost in
any
possible
k
-clustering.
Hence, we can simply replace those points by their center, and encode the cost difference in an extension coordinate.

To illustrate further our argument, suppose that we are given a center
m
such that the cost of clustering to
m
is nearly the same as clustering to the optimum
k
centers. Then, we show that in
any
clustering into
k
parts with centers
c_{1},...,c_{k}
, for almost all points
p
of
c_{i}
’s cluster, one of the following condition is verified:

•

either
p
is really close to
m
compared to
c_{i}
, namely
\|p-m\|\leq\varepsilon\|m-c_{i}\|
,

•

or
c_{i}
is really close to
m
compared to
p
,
\|m-c_{i}\|\leq\varepsilon\|p-m\|

•

or the segment between
p
and
m
is nearly orthogonal to the one between
m
and
c_{i}
.

Each of those conditions implies that
\|p-c_{i}\|\approx\left(\|m-c_{i}\|^{2}+\|p-m\|^{2}\right)^{1/2}
: we can therefore replace the point
p
by
f(p)\in\mathds{R}^{d+1}
, where the first
d
coordinates are the ones of
m
and the
extension coordinate
is
\|p-m\|
. Now, for any possible center
c_{i}
, the distance from
p
to
c_{i}
is roughly the one from
f(p)
to
\begin{pmatrix}c_{i}\\
0\end{pmatrix}
(
c_{i}
with an extension coordinate
0
).
Therefore, the cost of clustering to
\{c_{1},...,c_{k}\}
before the transformation is the same as clustering to
\left\{\begin{pmatrix}c_{1}\\
0\end{pmatrix},...,\begin{pmatrix}c_{k}\\
0\end{pmatrix}\right\}
after the transformation.
Furthermore, after transformation, all points have the same first
d
coordinates (equal to
m
), and all centers are required to have last coordinate zero.
Therefore, we have a small partition coreset for those points satisfying one of the condition.

We deal separately with points satisfying none of them. Using the fact that the cost of clustering to
m
is nearly the same as to clustering to
k
centers, we can show that those points have a very tiny cost to
m
, and are easier to deal with.

Applying that construction to all clusters of the
k^{\prime}
clustering whose cost do not decrease by adding
k
centers, we get a set of points in
\mathds{R}^{d+1}
where the first
d
coordinates are one of the
k^{\prime}
centers.
From this, we manage to build a set of candidate centers capturing all clustering, of size
k^{\prime O(\varepsilon^{-2})}
– henceforth independent of
n
. This concludes our algorithm: To reduce the dimension and preserve the cost of all clustering, it is enough to preserve distances between points of the partition coreset and the discrete set of centers, i.e., between
k^{\varepsilon^{-O(z)}}
many points, which can be done using a derandomized Johnson-Lindenstrauss
projection.

The last missing piece to the argument is to compute effectively this
k^{\prime}
clustering deterministically.
This is not straightforward either, as it requires to compute a
(1+\varepsilon)
-approximation to
(k,z)
-clustering. The standard techniques to do so rely on dimension reduction: using ours leads to a loop in the argument.
Instead, we use another JL construction (specifically, the one of Kane and Nelson
[
KN14
]
) that requires few random bits.
Enumerating over all possible random bits provides a a (somewhat) small set of projections, such that at least one is a good dimension reduction for clustering.
For each of the projections, we can compute a
(1+\varepsilon)
-approximate solution in the projected space, and then lift it back to the original
\mathds{R}^{d}
. For the projections that do not preserve the cost, those solutions in
\mathds{R}^{d}
may be bad: however, for the good projection, this solution will be a
(1+\varepsilon)
-approximation in the original metric as well. Enumerating over all possible solutions computed that way allows therefore to compute a
(1+\varepsilon)
-approximation.
It is then possible to use this algorithm to compute the desired
k^{\prime}
clustering, which concludes our whole algorithm.

1.3.2
Coresets

We show a generic coreset construction, based on a careful preprocessing of the input and VC-dimension techniques. This can be applied to the Euclidean space, using our partition coresets and dimension reduction, to obtain even smaller coresets.

Building on the work of Chen
[
Che09
]
, we show that constructing a coreset reduces to sampling uniformly. For this, we essentially compute a bi-criteria approximation and partition points in exponential rings according to their cost in the bi-criteria. There are
O(k\log n)
rings, and Chen showed that uniform sampling among them yield a coreset. Our key new ingredient here is to show how to go from
O(k\log n)
to only
O(k)
rings.
For this, our key contribution is to start from a structured bi-criteria approximation, in the same spirit as what we did for the partition coreset. In particular, we ensure that the cost of that solution cannot decrease by adding
k
more centers. We infer from that property that the cost of the points “far” from the bi-criteria centers cannot be much different in any solution.
We show that we can replace them by copies of the center, and encode their cost in an additive offset
F
.
This structural observation introduces a clean and elegant way of removing the dependency in
n
from the coreset size.
Similarly to what is done in
[
CSS21
]
(although their techniques are deeply randomized), this informally hints that the “far” points do not affect clustering algorithms.

Next, we observe that instead of sampling points in each ring, it is enough to build an
\varepsilon
-approximation of a particular set system, formed by
k
balls.
It is well known that the VC-dimension of that set system is bounded by
O(kd\log k)
in Euclidean space of dimension
d
: hence, using a standard algorithm, this leads to
\varepsilon
-approximation of size
\tilde{O}(\frac{kd\log k}{\varepsilon^{2}})
.

Unfortunately, the running time of these constructions is exponential in the VC dimension, which is
k\cdot d\log k
in our case. To eliminate the dependency on
d
, we apply first our construction of partition coreset: this reduces the size of the input to
k^{\varepsilon^{-O(z)}}
. We can then embed the input into a low dimensional space via terminal embeddings.
Terminal embeddings are commonly used as a coreset preserving dimension reduction, see
[
BBC+19
,
BJKW21
,
CSS21
,
HV20
]
and the construction by Mahabadi, Makarychev, Makarychev and Razenshteyn
[
MMMR18
]
can be made deterministic. That way, we are able to obtain coresets of size
O(k^{2}\cdot\varepsilon^{-O(1)})
in time
n^{O(\varepsilon^{-2}\log 1/\varepsilon)}+exp(\text{poly}(k,\varepsilon^{-1}))
.

Preliminaries

2.1
Notations

We use
a=(1\pm\varepsilon)b
as an abbreviation for
(1-\varepsilon)b\leq a\leq(1+\varepsilon)b
.
For a
d
-dimensional vector
x
, we define the
\ell_{z}
norm
\|x\|_{z}=\sqrt[z]{\sum_{i=1}^{d}|x_{i}|^{z}}
.
In Euclidean spaces, we let
\text{dist}(\cdot,\cdot)
denote the
\ell_{2}
distance.

Given a metric space
(X,\text{dist})
, a set of points
P\subset X
and
\mathcal{S}\subset X
, we define
\textsc{cost}^{z}(P,\mathcal{S})=\sum_{p\in P}\min_{s\in\mathcal{S}}\text{dist}(p,s)^{z}
.
The
(k,z)
-clustering objective consists of finding a set of at most
k
centers
\mathcal{S}
that minimize
\textsc{cost}^{z}(P,\mathcal{S})
. For simplicity, we often drop the
z
and simply write
\textsc{cost}(P,\mathcal{S})
.

Another view on the problem is to find clusters rather than centers. Given a set of points
P\subset X
, we define
\mu^{z}(P):=\argmin_{s}\textsc{cost}(P,\{s\})
to be the optimal center for
(1,z)
-clustering on
P
. For any partition
\mathcal{C}=\{C_{1},C_{2},...\}
of
P
,
we define
\textsc{P-cost}^{z}(P,\mathcal{C}):=\sum_{C\in\mathcal{C}}\textsc{cost}(C,\mu^{z}(C))
.

In Euclidean spaces, we say a set
P^{\prime}\subset\mathds{R}^{d+1}
is an extension of a multiset
P\subset\mathds{R}^{d}
if for all
p\in P
, there exists
p^{\prime}\in\mathds{R}
such that
P^{\prime}=\left\{\begin{pmatrix}p\\
p^{\prime}\end{pmatrix},p\in\right\}
. The number
p^{\prime}
is called the extension of
p
. If all extensions are
0
, then
P^{\prime}
is the
0
-extension of
P
.

Finally, for any function
f
that maps points of
X
to some other metric, we will abusively write
f(P):=\left\{f(p):p\in P\right\}
, and for a partition
\mathcal{C}
,
f(\mathcal{C})
is the partition
\left\{f(C_{1}),f(C_{2}),...\right\}
.

When clustering extensions, we will enforce centers to have extension coordinate
0
. Therefore, for a set of points
X\subset\mathds{R}^{d+1}
, we define

\mu^{z}_{0}(X):=\argmin_{\begin{subarray}{c}s\in\mathbb{R}^{d+1}\\
s_{d+1}=0\end{subarray}}\textsc{cost}^{z}(X,\{s\})
to be the optimal center for
(1,z)
-clustering on
P
with the constraint that the extension coordinate is
0
, and
\textsc{P-cost}_{0}(P,\mathcal{C})=\sum_{C\in\mathcal{C}}\textsc{cost}^{z}(C,\mu^{z}_{0}(C))
.

2.2
Definitions

We can now define the dimension reduction guarantee we aim to satisfy.

Definition 2.1

(Cost Preserving Sketches for Powers)
.

Let
z
be a positive integer and
\varepsilon>0
a precision parameter.
Let
P\subset\mathds{R}^{d}
, and
f:\mathds{R}^{d}\rightarrow\mathds{R}^{m+1}
.

Then we say that
f
is an
(k,z,\varepsilon)
-cost preserving sketch for
P
if for every partition
\mathcal{C}
of
P
into
k
sets,

\textsc{P-cost}_{0}^{z}(f(P),f(\mathcal{C}))=(1\pm\varepsilon)\textsc{P-cost}^{z}(P,\mathcal{C}).

This definition captures the guarantee satisfied for
(k,z)
-clustering given by
[
MMR19
]
, with the generalization of allowing
f(P)
to have an extension.
Let us briefly compare this definition to cost preserving sketches for
k
-means, defined as follows:

Definition 2.2

(Cost Preserving Sketches for
k
-means
[
CEM+15
]
)
.

Let
\varepsilon>0
be a precision parameter.
Let
P\subset\mathds{R}^{d}
, and
f:\mathds{R}^{d}\rightarrow\mathds{R}^{m}
.

Then we say that
f
is an
(k,2,\varepsilon)
-cost preserving sketch for
P
if for every partition
\mathcal{C}
of
P
into
k
sets,

\textsc{P-cost}^{2}(f(P),f(\mathcal{C}))+F=(1\pm\varepsilon)\textsc{P-cost}^{2}(P,\mathcal{C}).

It turns out that those two definitions are equivalent for the case
z=2
: the offset
F
used for
k
-means is a special case of adding extensions to
f(P)
and requiring the extension coordinate of the centers being set to 0 (i.e.
s_{m+1}=0
). Writing
f(p)=\begin{pmatrix}f^{\prime}(P)\\
p^{\prime}\end{pmatrix}
and
\mu_{0}^{z}(C)=\begin{pmatrix}\mu_{C}\\
0\end{pmatrix}
we indeed have for
z=2

\sum_{C\in\mathcal{C}}\sum_{p\in C}\|f(p)-\mu_{0}^{z}(C)\|^{2}=\sum_{C\in\mathcal{C}}\sum_{p\in C}\text{dist}(f^{\prime}(p),\mu_{C})^{2}+{p^{\prime}}^{2}.

Note that the contribution of the final coordinate is independent of the choice of centers, under the constraint
c_{m+1}=0
. Hence, the offset
F
is equivalent to
\sum_{p\in P}{p^{\prime}}^{2}
. Second,
\mu_{C}
is exactly the mean of
C
, regardless of the extension coordinates: hence,
\textsc{P-cost}^{2}(f(P),f(\mathcal{C}))+F=\textsc{P-cost}_{0}^{2}(f^{\prime}(P),f^{\prime}(\mathcal{C}))+\sum_{p\in P}P^{\prime}
, and the two definitions coincides.

In order to construct our cost preserving sketches, we will rely on partition coreset, defined as follows:

Definition 2.3

(Partition Coresets (see
[
SW18
]
))
.

Let
B
be a multiset consisting of several copies of points in
\mathds{R}^{d}
, with total size
|P|
.

B
is an
(\varepsilon,k,z)
-extension partition coreset of
P
if there exists an extension
B^{\prime}
of
B
and a one-to-one mapping
g:P\rightarrow B^{\prime}
such that for any partition
\mathcal{C}=\{C_{1},...,C_{k}\}
into
k
parts and any centers
\mathcal{S}=\{s_{1},...,s_{k}\}
with
0
-extension
\{s^{\prime}_{1},...,s^{\prime}_{k}\}
, it holds that

\sum_{i=1}^{k}\sum_{p\in C_{i}}\text{dist}(g(p),s^{\prime}_{i})^{z}=(1\pm\varepsilon)\sum_{i=1}^{k}\sum_{p\in C_{i}}\text{dist}(p,s_{i})^{z}

We say that the size of the coreset is the number of distinct points of
B
.

We insist on the fact that the size of an extension partition coreset is the number of distinct points in
B
, not on the extension
B^{\prime}
.

This definition of coreset is somewhat stronger than the commonly found definition that requires the cost of an assignment
\sum_{p\in A}\underset{s\in\mathcal{S}}{\min}\text{dist}(p,s)^{z}
to be approximated for any set of
k
-centers
\mathcal{S}
. In our definition, the points are not necessarily assigned optimally to the center set. This difference is crucial for dimension reduction purposes, as guarantees required for cost preserving sketches are related to partitions as well.

Coresets with extensions where introduced by Sohler and Woodruff
[
SW18
]
for
k
-median only: our definition extends theirs, while providing a stronger guarantee, as cost is preserved for any assignments and not only assignments to the closest center.

Finally, we define the more standard coreset that we will work with in section
5
.
The definition of coreset we consider here allows for an
offset
, originally due to Feldman, Schmidt and Sohler
[
FSS13
]
. For other works using this definition, see
[
BBC+19
,
CEM+15
,
SW18
,
CSS21
,
CLSS22
]
.

Definition 2.4
.

Let
(X,\text{dist})
be a metric space, and
P\subset X
.
An
(\varepsilon,k,z)
-coreset for the
(k,z)
-Clustering problem on
P
is a set
\Omega\subset X
with weights
w:\Omega\rightarrow\mathds{R}_{+}
together with a scalar
F
such that, for any set
\mathcal{S}\subset X^{d}
,
|\mathcal{S}|=k
,

\textsc{cost}^{z}(\Omega,\mathcal{S})+F=(1\pm\varepsilon)\textsc{cost}^{z}(P,\mathcal{S})

As opposed to the previous sketches, those coresets preserve the cost of any set of
centers
, instead of preserving the cost of any clustering. In particular, those centers may not be the optimal ones for the induced partition.

Lastly, a
(\alpha,\beta)
bicriteria algorithm for clustering produces a clustering with
\beta\cdot k
centers and cost
\alpha\cdot OPT
, where
OPT
denotes the cost of an optimal clustering with
k
centers.

2.3
Useful Results

Lemma 2.5

(Triangle Inequality for Powers)
.

Let
a,b,c
be an arbitrary set of points in a metric space with distance function
d
and let
z
be a positive integer. Then for any
\varepsilon>0

d(a,b)^{z}\leq(1+\varepsilon)^{z-1}d(a,c)^{z}+\left(\frac{1+\varepsilon}{\varepsilon}\right)^{z-1}d(b,c)^{z}

and

\left|d(a,b)^{z}-d(a,c)^{z}\right|\leq\varepsilon\cdot d(a,c)^{z}+\left(\frac{z+\varepsilon}{\varepsilon}\right)^{z-1}d(b,c)^{z}.

Proofs of this inequality (and variants thereof) can be found in
[
BBC+19
,
CS17
,
FSS13
,
MMR19
]
)

We will use an algorithm that compute an approximation to
(k,z)
-clustering up to a tiny error, as stated in the following theorem. We prove the theorem
in
section

6
: although key to our algorithms, the techniques to build this approximation are distinct from our main techniques and we deferred the proof to the end.

Theorem 2.6
.

There exists an algorithm running in time
k^{\log\log k+\log(1/\varepsilon)}\cdot\text{poly}(nd)
that compute a set of
O(k\cdot\log(1/\varepsilon)/\varepsilon)
many centers
\mathcal{S}
, such that
\textsc{cost}(P,\mathcal{S})\leq(1+\varepsilon)\textsc{cost}(P,\text{OPT})
, where
OPT
is the best solution with
k
centers.

Derandomizing Random Projections.

The seminal Johnson-Lindenstrauss lemma states that any
n
point set in
d
-dimensional Euclidean space can be (linearly) embedded into a
m\in O(\varepsilon^{-2}\log n)
dimensional space such that all pairwise distances are preserved up to a
(1\pm\varepsilon)
factor.
Most proofs show this guarantee by sampling a matrix from an appropriate distribution. We will use the following derandomization based on the conditional expectation method.

Theorem 2.7

(
[
EIO02
]
)
.

Let
v_{1},\ldots v_{n}
be a sequence of vectors in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
and let
\varepsilon,F\in(0,1]
. Then we can compute in deterministic time
O(nd(\log n\varepsilon^{-1})^{O(1)})
a linear mapping
S:\mathbb{R}^{d}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^{m}
where
m\in O(\varepsilon^{-2}\log 1/F)
such that

(1-\varepsilon)\cdot\|v_{i}\|_{2}\leq\|Sv_{i}\|_{2}\leq(1+\varepsilon)\|v_{i}\|_{2}

for at least a
(1-F)
fraction of the
i
’s.

Note that setting
F<1/n
implies that the guarantee holds for all vectors. Also, we note that if we are given
n
points rather than
n
vectors and wish to preserve the pairwise distances, the running time has a quadratic dependency on
n
as there are
{n\choose 2}
many distance vectors. Since the running time is dominated by computing a bicriteria approximation, we will omit it from the statement of our theorems.

Deterministic Partition Coresets for
(k,z)
-Clustering

3.1
Construction of the Coreset

The main goal of this section is to prove the following theorem:

Theorem 3.1
.

Let
A
be a set of
n
points in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
,
\varepsilon>0
and
k
be an integer. We can compute in deterministic time
k^{\log\log k+\varepsilon^{-O(z)}}\cdot\text{poly}(nd)
an
(\varepsilon,k,z)
-extension partition coreset of
A
with at most
k^{\varepsilon^{-O(z)}}
distinct points.

The key structural lemma we use in this case is as follows. Essentially, it states that if the cost of clustering to a single center cannot be decreased by adding
k
centers, then the center is itself a partition coreset.

Lemma 3.2
.

Let
A
be a set of points in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
and let
m
be a (not necessarily optimal) center for
A
. Let
M
be the multiset of
|A|
copies of
m
.

Suppose that for all set of
k
centers
\mathcal{S}
,

\textsc{cost}(A,\{m\})-\textsc{cost}(A,\mathcal{S})<\alpha\cdot\textsc{cost}(A,\{m\}),

for
\alpha\leq\frac{\varepsilon^{z+6}}{401408\cdot 2^{3z}\cdot z^{z+6}}
.

Then
M
is an
(\varepsilon,k,z)
-extension partition coreset of
A
.

More precisely, define the function
f
that maps
p
to
f(p)=\begin{pmatrix}m\\
\|p-m\|\end{pmatrix}
, and let
M^{\prime}=f(A)
. Then, for any partition
\mathcal{C}=\{C_{1},...,C_{k}\}
of
A
into
k
parts and any centers
c_{1},...,c_{k}
with
0
-extension
c^{\prime}_{1},...,c^{\prime}_{k}
, it holds that

\sum_{i=1}^{k}\sum_{p\in C_{i}}\textsc{cost}(f(p),c^{\prime}_{i})=(1\pm\varepsilon)\sum_{i=1}^{k}\sum_{p\in C_{i}}\textsc{cost}(p,c_{i})

In other terms, the guarantee we have on
M
is that fot any partition
\mathcal{C}
and any centers
c_{1},...,c_{k}
, it holds that

\sum_{i=1}^{k}\sum_{p\in C_{i}}\left(\|p-m\|^{2}+\|m-c_{i}\|^{2}\right)^{z/2}=(1\pm\varepsilon)\sum_{i=1}^{k}\sum_{p\in C_{i}}\|p-c_{i}\|^{z}.

Before proving this lemma, we will show why it implies the theorem.

Proof of Theorem
3.1
.

The algorithm to construct the partition coreset is
algorithm

1
. In the following, let
\beta
, and
\gamma
be constants with a polynomial dependency on
\varepsilon^{z}
that we specify later.

Algorithm 1

PartitionCoreset(
C,m
, depth)

1:

Compute a set
\mathcal{S}
for
(k,z)
-clustering on
C
, with cost
(1+\beta)\textsc{cost}(C,\text{OPT}_{C})
and with
|\mathcal{S}|=O(k\log(1/\beta)/\beta)
, using the algorithm of
theorem

2.6
.

2:

if

\textsc{cost}(C,\{m\})-\textsc{cost}(C,\mathcal{S})\leq\beta\cdot\textsc{cost}(C,\{m\})

then

3:

Return a multiset
M
with
|C|
copies of
m
as a partition coreset for
C
, with extension
M^{\prime}=\left\{\begin{pmatrix}m\\
\|p-m\|\end{pmatrix},p\in C\right\}

4:

else

if
depth =
\gamma

then

5:

Return a multiset
M
with
|C|
copies of
m
as a partition coreset for
C
, with extension
M^{\prime}=\left\{\begin{pmatrix}m\\
0\end{pmatrix},p\in C\right\}

6:

else

7:

Let
C_{1},...,C_{|\mathcal{S}|}
be the clusters induced by
\mathcal{S}
, with centers
c_{1},...,c_{|\mathcal{S}|}

8:

Return
\cup_{i=1}^{|\mathcal{S}|}
PartitionCoreset
(C_{i},c_{i},
depth
+1)
.

We first argue that the algorithm is correct, namely, PartitionCoreset
(P,0,0)
computes a partition coreset. We will then show that it runs in the desired (deterministic) time.

For the correctness, we proceed as follows. Let
(C_{1},m_{1},d_{1}),(C_{2},m_{2},d_{2}),...
be the set of calls to PartitionCoreset that end in line 3. Let
M_{i}
be the set computed for the call with parameters
(C_{i},m_{i},d_{i})
. We show that for any
i
,
M_{i}
is a partition coreset for
C_{i}
.

For this, we can use Lemma
3.2
. Let us drop the subscript
i
and fix
C,m
to be some
C_{i},m_{i}
, and let
\beta=\frac{1}{2}\cdot\frac{\varepsilon^{z+6}}{401408\cdot 2^{3z}\cdot z^{z+6}}
(i.e.: equal to
\alpha/2
with
\alpha
being specified in Lemma
3.2
). Let
\mathcal{S}
be the solution computed line 1 of the algorithm for
C
: for any set of
k
centers
K
, it holds that
\textsc{cost}(C,\mathcal{S})\leq(1+\beta)\textsc{cost}(C,K)
. Therefore, we have:

\displaystyle\textsc{cost}(C,\{m\})-\textsc{cost}(C,K)

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\textsc{cost}(C,\{m\})-\frac{1}{1+\beta}\cdot\textsc{cost}(C,\mathcal{S})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{1+\beta}\left(\textsc{cost}(C,\{m\})-\textsc{cost}(C,\mathcal{S})\right)+\frac{\beta}{1+\beta}\cdot\textsc{cost}(C,\{m\})

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\frac{\beta}{1+\beta}\cdot\textsc{cost}(C,\{m\})+\frac{\beta}{1+\beta}\cdot\textsc{cost}(C,\{m\})

\displaystyle\implies\textsc{cost}(C,\{m\})-\textsc{cost}(C,K)

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle 2\beta\cdot\textsc{cost}(C,\{m\}).

Using Lemma
3.2
, this implies that
M
with its extension is indeed an
\varepsilon
- extension partition coreset for the points in
C
.

We now deal with the other termination criteria, namely when we reach depth
\gamma
. For a level
\ell
, we define
(C^{\ell}_{1},c^{\ell}_{1},\ell),(C^{\ell}_{2},c^{\ell}_{2},\ell),...
be the set of calls to PartitionCoreset with level

, and let
\Gamma=\cup C^{\gamma}_{i}
,
\mathcal{S}_{\Gamma}=\cup c^{\gamma}_{i}
.
One can easily show that
\sum_{i}\textsc{cost}(C^{\ell}_{i},c^{\ell}_{i})\leq\frac{1}{1+\beta}\sum_{i}\textsc{cost}(C^{\ell-1}_{i},c^{\ell-1}_{i})
, as if the cost does not decrease by

then the recursion stops on line 3. Hence, the cost of clustering

to

is

\textsc{cost}(\Gamma,\mathcal{S}_{\Gamma})\leq z^{O(z)}\cdot\left(\frac{1}{1+\beta}\right)^{\gamma}\text{OPT}

Lemma 3.3

\displaystyle\max(\|p-m\|,\|m-c_{i}\|)-\min(\|p-m\|,\|m-c_{i}\|)\geq(1-\varepsilon/(4z))\cdot\max(\|p-m\|,\|m-c_{i}\|)

Using the Mercator series, it holds that

\displaystyle\|p-c_{i}\|^{z}

\displaystyle(\|p-m\|+\|m-c_{i}\|)^{z}

\displaystyle\left(\max(\|p-m\|,\|m-c_{i}\|)-\min(\|p-m\|,\|m-c_{i}\|)\right)^{z}

\displaystyle(1-\varepsilon/(4z))^{z}\cdot\max(\|p-m\|^{z},\|m-c_{i}\|^{z})

\displaystyle(1-\varepsilon/2)\cdot\max(\|p-m\|^{z},\|m-c_{i}\|^{z}).

\displaystyle\left(\|p-m\|^{2}+\|m-c_{i}\|^{2}\right)^{z/2}

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\left((1\pm\varepsilon/(4z))\cdot\max(\|p-m\|^{2},\|m-c_{i}\|^{2})\right)^{z/2}

\displaystyle(1\pm\varepsilon/2)\cdot\max(\|p-m\|^{z},\|m-c_{i}\|^{z})

\displaystyle\left|\left(\|p-m\|^{2}+\|m-c_{i}\|^{2}\right)^{z/2}-\|p-c_{i}\|^{z}\right|

\displaystyle\big|(1\pm\varepsilon/2)\cdot\max(\|p-m\|^{z},\|m-c_{i}\|^{z})-(1\pm\varepsilon/2)\cdot\max(\|p-m\|^{z},\|m-c_{i}\|^{z})\big|

4
Derandomized Dimension Reduction

In this section we will use the deterministic construction of partition coresets to obtain a derandomized dimension reduction for
(k,z)
clustering. The goal is to show that we can obtain a cost preserving sketch with dimension at most
O(\varepsilon^{-O(z)}\log k)
:

Theorem 4.1
.

Let
P\subset\mathds{R}^{d}
.
We can compute an
(k,z,\varepsilon)
-cost preserving sketch of
P
with target dimension
O\left(\varepsilon^{-O(z)}\cdot\log k\right)
in deterministic time
k^{\log\log k+\varepsilon^{-O(z)}}\cdot\text{poly}(nd)
.

In the particular
k
-median case (

), the precise target dimension is
\varepsilon^{-11}\log k
, while it is
\varepsilon^{-12}\log k
for
k
-means. We did not make any particular attempt to optimize the dependency in
\varepsilon
.

Again, the first-thought approach would be to use the deterministic Johnson-Lindenstrauss transform of
[
EIO02
]
(Theorem
2.7
). Unfortunately, merely preserving pairwise distances is not sufficient to preserve the cost to an optimal
(1,z)
-center, except for the special case
z=2
.
Instead, we introduce the following notion.

Definition 4.2

(Witness Sets)
.

Let
P
be a set of
n
points in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
(with a possible extension coordinate), let
c
be an optimal
(1,z)
-center (under the possible constraint that the extension coordinate is 0) and let
\Delta=\frac{\sum_{p\in P}\|p-c\|^{z}}{n}
.
Then a
(D,R,\varepsilon)
-uniform witness set is a subset
S\subset P
such that

•

The convex hull of
S
contains a
(1+\varepsilon)
-approximation of
(1,z)
-clustering on
P
(under the possible constraint that the extension coordinate is 0).

•

The diameter of
S
is at most
D\cdot\sqrt[z]{\Delta}
.

•

The size of

is at most

.

The first important result is proving that the existence of witness sets of small size exist for a specific range of parameters.

Theorem 4.3
.

There exists
\left(\frac{O(z)}{\varepsilon},\varepsilon^{-2}\cdot 2^{O(z)},\varepsilon\right)
witness sets.

We give a full proof of Theorem
4.3
in Section
A
of the appendix. The existence of small witness sets is essentially a consequence of the existence of small coresets. Let us consider how this existence of small witness sets combined with Theorem
3.1
implies that we can deterministically construct a cost preserving sketch for powers.

The main idea is that the property of witness sets allows us
to efficiently “discretize” all candidate solutions in the convex hull of a witness set.
We will show that the dimension reduction preserves the property of a subset of points being a witness set. Furthermore, because witness sets lie in low-dimensional spaces, we can guarantee that the cost for every point in the convex hull of a witness is preserved. Specifically, we use that linear projections preserve subspaces, and in particular the subspace spanned by the points in the convex hull of the witness set. Hence, if the cost of every candidate solution in a witness set stays the same, up to a
(1\pm\varepsilon)
factor, the clustering cost overall also stays the same.

Lemma 4.4
.

Let
P\subset\mathds{R}^{d}

\Pi

of

p^{\prime}\end{pmatrix}-\mu^{z}_{0}(C^{\prime})\right\|^{z},

p^{\prime}\end{pmatrix},p\in C\right\}

C
with the same extensions as
C^{\prime}
.

First,

4
4

4

5
Improved Deterministic Coreset Construction via Uniform VC-dimension

We focus in this section on a different notion of compression, namely coresets. As mentionned in the introduction, our construction works in general metric spaces, and not simply in Euclidean Spaces. In the following, we will work in a metric space

.
Again, the construction is greatly simplified compared to
[
CSS21
]
, to the price of a a slightly worse coreset size.

Clustering of Curves.

Driemel, Nusser, Phillips, and Psarros showed in
[
DNPP21
]
VC dimension bounds for clustering of curves, under the Fréchet and Hausdorff distances.
The input of that problem is a set of curves, each consisting of
m
segments in
\mathds{R}^{d}
, and the centers are restricted to be curves consisting of
\ell
segments in
\mathds{R}^{d}
.

They consider two types of distances for curves: the Hausdorff distance between two curves
X
and
Y
is defined as follows: let
d_{\vec{H}}(X,Y)=sup_{x\in X}\inf_{y\in Y}\|x-y\|
. The Hausforff distance between
X
and
Y
is
\max(d_{\vec{H}}(X,Y),d_{\vec{H}}(Y,X))
. The Fréchet distance is slightly more intricate: for that, we consider parametrized curves, namely a curve is a function
X:[0,1]\rightarrow\mathds{R}^{d}
whose graph is made of
\ell
consecutive segments. The Fréchet distance between two parametrized curves
X
and
Y
is defined as
\min_{f:[0,1]\rightarrow[0,1]}\max_{t\in[0,1]}\|X(f(t))-Y(t)\|
, where the function
f
is restricted to be continuous, non decreasing and with
f(0)=0,f(1)=1
.

For both those distances, Driemel, Nusser, Phillips and Psarros
[
DNPP21
]
showed that the VC dimension of
(P,\mathcal{B})
is bounded by
O\left(kd^{2}\ell^{2}\log(d\ell m)\log k\right)
.
Hence,
theorem

5.2
shows the existence of a coreset of size
\frac{k^{2}d^{2}\ell^{2}\log(d\ell m)\log^{2}k}{\varepsilon^{O(1)}}
. This improves over
[
BR21
]
and
[
BCJ+22
]
, although our construction is not polynomial time in that case.

5.3
A Sketch of the Construction

As explained in the introduction (
section

1.3
), our construction is inspired by the one of Chen
[
Che09
]
and of
[
CSS21
]
. As both of those result, we start by computing a constant-factor approximation, and placing rings of exponential radius around each of its center.

We first use a careful preprocessing step to reduce the number of such rings from
O(k\log n)
as in
[
Che09
]
to
O(k)
. For this, we use a particularly structured constant-factor approximation, that allows us to simply discard the outer rings.

To build coresets in the remaining rings, both
[
Che09
]
and
[
CSS21
]
use uniform sampling.
Instead, we show that building an
\varepsilon^{\prime}
-set-approximation of the set system formed by
k
balls is actually enough: this is doable deterministically, which concludes our algorithm.

The size of an
\varepsilon^{\prime}
-set-approximation can be bounded via the VC-dimension of the set system:

Theorem 5.4

(Theorem 4.5 in Chazelle
[
Cha01
]
)
.

Let
(X,\mathcal{R})
be a range space of VC-dimension
d
. Given any
r>0
, an
r
-set-approximation for
(X,\mathcal{R})
of size
O\left(dr^{-2}\log(d/r)\right)
can be computed deterministically in time
d^{O(d)}\left(r^{-2}\log(d/r)\right)^{d}|X|
.

Theorem 5.5

(Theorem 4.9 in Chazelle
[
Cha01
]
)
.

Let
(X,\mathcal{R})
be a range space of VC-dimension
d
. Given any
r>0
, a uniform sample of size
O\left(r^{-2}(d\log(dr)+\log(1/\delta))\right)
is a
r
-set-approximation for
(X,\mathcal{R})
with probability
1-\delta
.

We will compute
\varepsilon
-set-approximation for the set system
(P,\mathcal{B})
, where
P
is the set of input points and
\mathcal{B}
is defined in the opening paragraph.

5.4
The algorithm

As sketched, the algorithm partitions the input points into rings, and computes then
\varepsilon
-set-approximation in each ring. We state here some useful definitions.

Fix a metric space
(X,\text{dist})
, positive integers
k,z
and a set of clients
P
. For a solution
\mathcal{S}
of
(k,z)
-clustering on
P
and a center

,

than to any other center of
\mathcal{S}
. Note that this is different from the dimension reduction setting of
section

, where the assignment are not necessarily towards the closest center.

Fix as well some
\varepsilon>0
, and let
\mathcal{A}
be any solution for
(k,z)
-clustering on
P
with
k
centers.
Let

be the clusters induced by the centers of
\mathcal{A}
.

•

the average cost of a cluster

•

For all

, the
ring

is the set of points
p\in C_{i}
such that

2^{j}\Delta_{C_{i}}\leq\textsc{cost}(p,\mathcal{A})\leq 2^{j+1}\Delta_{C_{i}}.

•

The
inner ring

(resp.

) of a cluster

consists of the points of

with cost at most
\left(\nicefrac{{\varepsilon}}{{z}}\right)^{z}\Delta_{C_{i}}
(resp. at least
\left(\nicefrac{{z}}{{\varepsilon}}\right)^{2z}\Delta_{C_{i}}
), i.e.,
R_{I}(C_{i}):=\cup_{j\leq z\log(\varepsilon/z)}R_{i,j}
and
R_{O}(C_{i}):=\cup_{j>2z\log(z/\varepsilon)}R_{i,j}
. The
main ring

R_{M}(C_{i})
consists of all the other points of
C_{i}
. For a solution
\mathcal{S}
, we let
R_{I}^{\mathcal{S}}
and
R_{O}^{\mathcal{S}}
be the union of inner and outer rings of the clusters induced by
\mathcal{S}
.

The final algorithm is as follows:

(X,\text{dist})

,

,
\varepsilon

, and
\varepsilon^{\prime}\leq\varepsilon
.

Output:
A coreset with offset. Namely, a set of points
P^{\prime}\subseteq X
, a weight function

F
such that for any set of
k
centers
C

\textsc{cost}(P,C)=(1\pm\varepsilon)\textsc{cost}(P^{\prime},C)+F
.

1.

Greedy seeding:

(a)

Compute a
c_{\mathcal{A}}
-approx
\mathcal{A}
to the
(k,z)
-clustering problem for
P
.
8
8

8

Any solution with
O(k)
clusters and

can be actually used for this step, in order to have a faster algorithm.

(b)

Initialize
\mathcal{G}:=\mathcal{A}
.

(c)

While:
\textsc{cost}(\mathcal{G})\geq\varepsilon\textsc{cost}(\mathcal{A})/c_{\mathcal{A}}

and there is a candidate center

such
that

\left(1-\frac{\varepsilon}{k\cdot c_{\mathcal{A}}}\right)\textsc{cost}(\mathcal{G})\geq\textsc{cost}(\mathcal{G}\cup\{c\})

\mathcal{G}\leftarrow\mathcal{G}\cup\{c\}
.

2.

If

\mathcal{G}
and

where

points served by center
c
in
\mathcal{G}
, and offset

.

3.

Let
F:=\textsc{cost}(R_{O}(\mathcal{G}),\mathcal{G})
be the cost of the outer points of
\mathcal{G}
.

4.

\mathcal{G}

5.

Reducing the number of rings:

For each cluster

.

Output:

•

An instance

•

A coreset consisting of

5.5
Proof of the Greedy Seeding

The outcome of the greedy seeding step,
I_{G}
, satisfies the following lemma:

Lemma 5.6
.

Suppose
\mathcal{G}
is locally stable.
Let
F=\sum\limits_{C\in\mathcal{G}}\sum\limits_{p\in R_{O}(C)}\textsc{cost}(p,\mathcal{G})
be the cost of points in outer rings.
For every solution
\mathcal{S}
, it holds that

|\textsc{cost}(\mathcal{S})-(\textsc{cost}_{I_{G}}(\mathcal{S})+F)|\leq\varepsilon\textsc{cost}(\mathcal{S}).

Before proving
lemma

5.6
, we show that the solution
\mathcal{G}
has the
following properties:

Lemma 5.7
.

\mathcal{G}
contains at most
O(k\cdot z\cdot\log(1/\varepsilon)/\varepsilon)

centers and, in the case where
\mathcal{G}
is locally stable,
for any solution
\mathcal{S}
and any subset
Q\subseteq P
,

\textsc{cost}(Q,\mathcal{G})\leq\textsc{cost}(Q,\mathcal{S})+\varepsilon\text{OPT}
.

Proof.

Note that the cost of
\mathcal{G}
is clearly at most the cost of
\mathcal{A}
, whose cost is at most
c_{\mathcal{A}}
times the cost of
the best solution for the
k
-clustering problem.

Each center added by the greedy step decreases its cost
by a factor
(1-\nicefrac{{\varepsilon}}{{c_{\mathcal{A}}k}})
, and the initial solution
\mathcal{G}
has cost
\textsc{cost}(\mathcal{G})
: since the algorithm stops if the cost
drops below
\varepsilon\textsc{cost}(\mathcal{A})/c_{\mathcal{A}}
, the total number of centers added is at most
c_{\mathcal{A}}k\log(1/\varepsilon)/\varepsilon
The total number of centers is therefore at most
c_{\mathcal{A}}k\log(1/\varepsilon)/\varepsilon
.

Note also that
\textsc{cost}(\mathcal{A})/c_{\mathcal{A}}\leq\text{OPT}
: hence, the inequality
(1-\nicefrac{{\varepsilon}}{{c_{\mathcal{A}}k}})\textsc{cost}(\mathcal{G})\leq\textsc{cost}(\mathcal{G}\cup\{c\})
implies

\textsc{cost}(\mathcal{G})\leq\textsc{cost}(\mathcal{G}\cup\{c\})+\varepsilon\text{OPT}/k
. Hence,
since
\mathcal{G}
is locally stable, there is no candidate center
c
such that
\textsc{cost}(\mathcal{G})-\textsc{cost}(\mathcal{G}\cup\{c\})\geq\varepsilon\text{OPT}/k
. Let
\mathcal{S}
be a solution,
Q
be a subset of clients,
s
be a center of
the solution
\mathcal{S}
and
C_{s}
be its cluster. Since the improvement made by adding
s
is at least
\sum_{p\in C_{s}\cap Q}\textsc{cost}(p,\mathcal{G})-\textsc{cost}(p,\mathcal{S})
, it holds that
\sum_{p\in C_{s}\cap Q}\textsc{cost}(p,\mathcal{G})-\textsc{cost}(p,\mathcal{S})\leq\textsc{cost}(\mathcal{G})-\textsc{cost}(\mathcal{G}\cup\{c\})\leq\varepsilon\text{OPT}/k
.
Summing over the
k
centers of the solution
\mathcal{S}
yields the inequality
\textsc{cost}(Q,\mathcal{S})\leq\textsc{cost}(Q,\mathcal{G})+\varepsilon\text{OPT}
, concluding the lemma.
∎

We can now turn to the proof of
lemma

5.6
, that shows that the the outer rings of solution
\mathcal{G}
can simply be discarded, and their cost added in the offset
F
.

proof of
lemma

5.6
.

Fix a cluster
C
of
\mathcal{G}
, with center
c
.
We first want to show that
\textsc{cost}(C,\mathcal{S})\leq\textsc{cost}_{I_{G}}(C,\mathcal{S})+\textsc{cost}(R_{O}(C),\mathcal{G})+\varepsilon\textsc{cost}(C,\mathcal{S})
. First, this is equivalent to

\textsc{cost}(R_{O}(C),\mathcal{S})\leq|R_{O}(C)|\textsc{cost}(c,\mathcal{S})+\textsc{cost}(R_{O}(C),\mathcal{G})+\varepsilon\textsc{cost}(C,\mathcal{S})
, as all points in
C\setminus R_{O}(C)
have same cost in the original instance and in
I_{G}
.

Using
lemma

2.5
, we have

\textsc{cost}(R_{O}(C),\mathcal{S})\leq(1+\varepsilon)\textsc{cost}(R_{O}(C),\mathcal{G})+(1+z/\varepsilon)^{z-1}|R_{O}(C)|\textsc{cost}(c,\mathcal{S}).

Hence, one needs to bound
|R_{O}(C)|\textsc{cost}(c,\mathcal{S})
. For that, we show that the cost of clients in
R_{O}(C)
can be charged to clients of
R_{I}(C)\cup R_{M}(C)
.

First note by Markov’s inequality,
|R_{O}(C)|\leq\left(\nicefrac{{\varepsilon}}{{z}}\right)^{2z}|C|
.
Hence, one can partition
R_{I}(C)\cup R_{M}(C)
into parts of size at least
s=(|C|-|R_{O}(C)|)/|R_{O}(C)|
, and assign every part to a point in
R_{O}(C)
in a one-to-one correspondence.

For such a point
p\in R_{O}(C)
consider the
s

points
p_{1},\ldots,p_{s}
of the part assigned to it. We show how to charge
\textsc{cost}(c,\mathcal{S})
to the cost of those points. Let
\mathcal{S}(p_{j})
be the center serving
p_{j}
in the solution
\mathcal{S}
. It holds that
\textsc{cost}(c,\mathcal{S})\leq\min_{j}\textsc{cost}(c,\mathcal{S}(p_{j}))
. Averaging, this is at most
\frac{1}{s}\sum_{j=1}^{s}\textsc{cost}(c,\mathcal{S}(p_{j}))
which is due to
lemma

2.5

at most
\frac{1}{s}\sum_{j=1}^{s}(1+\varepsilon)\textsc{cost}(p_{j},\mathcal{G})+(1+z/\varepsilon)^{z-1}\textsc{cost}(p_{j},\mathcal{S})
. Since
1/s\leq 2|R_{O}(C)|/|C|=2\left(\nicefrac{{\varepsilon}}{{z}}\right)^{2z}
,
we conclude (using
2\left(\frac{\varepsilon}{z}\right)(1+\varepsilon)\leq 1
) that:

\displaystyle|R_{O}(C)|\textsc{cost}(c,\mathcal{S})

\displaystyle\leq 2\left(\frac{\varepsilon}{z}\right)^{z+1}(1+\varepsilon)\sum_{p\in R_{I}(C)\cup R_{M}(C)}\textsc{cost}(p,\mathcal{G})+\textsc{cost}(p,\mathcal{S})

\displaystyle\leq\left(\frac{\varepsilon}{z}\right)^{z}(\textsc{cost}(C,\mathcal{G})+\textsc{cost}(C,\mathcal{S})).

(12)

We apply this inequality to bound
\textsc{cost}(R_{O}(C),\mathcal{S})
:

\displaystyle\textsc{cost}(R_{O}(C),\mathcal{S})

\displaystyle\leq(1+\varepsilon)\textsc{cost}(R_{O}(C),\mathcal{G})+(1+z/\varepsilon)^{z-1}|R_{O}(C)|\textsc{cost}(c,\mathcal{S})

\displaystyle\leq\textsc{cost}(R_{O}(C),\mathcal{G})+2\varepsilon(\textsc{cost}(C,\mathcal{G})+\textsc{cost}(C,\mathcal{S}))

Hence, summed over all cluster, this yields

\textsc{cost}(R_{O},\mathcal{S})\leq\textsc{cost}(R_{O},\mathcal{G})+2\varepsilon(\textsc{cost}(\mathcal{G})+\textsc{cost}(\mathcal{S}))

(13)

We now turn to the other direction of the inequality. Using
lemma

5.7
,

\displaystyle\textsc{cost}(R_{O},\mathcal{G})

\displaystyle\leq\textsc{cost}(R_{O},\mathcal{S})+\varepsilon\text{OPT}

\displaystyle\leq\textsc{cost}(R_{O},\mathcal{S})+\varepsilon\textsc{cost}(\mathcal{S})

Combined with
eq.

12
, this yields

\displaystyle\textsc{cost}(R_{O},\mathcal{G})+\textsc{cost}_{I_{G}}(R_{O},\mathcal{S})-\textsc{cost}(R_{O},\mathcal{S})\leq 2\varepsilon(\textsc{cost}(\mathcal{S})+\textsc{cost}(\mathcal{G})).

(14)

Combining
eq.

13
and
eq.

14
, and using that all points not in
R_{O}
have same cost in the original instance and in
I_{G}
concludes the lemma.
∎

5.6
Computing Coresets via
\varepsilon
-set-approximation: Proof of
lemma

5.8

We first show that an
\varepsilon
-set-approximation of the set system
(R_{i,j},\mathcal{B})
is indeed a coreset for
R_{i,j}
. We will then bound the size of such an approximation. In this section, we fix the cluster
C_{i}
and a ring
R_{i,j}
.

Lemma 5.8
.

Let
R_{i,j}
be a ring, and
\Omega
be an
\varepsilon^{\prime}
-set-approximation for
(R_{i,j},\mathcal{B})
with
\varepsilon^{\prime}=20\frac{8^{z}\varepsilon^{2}}{\log(4z/\varepsilon)}
. Then
\Omega
with uniform weights
|R_{i,j}|/|\Omega|
is an
\varepsilon
-coreset for
R_{i,j}
.

We use the same proof technique as
[
CSS21
]
to analyze the algorithm. More precisely, we define ranges of clients: we let
I_{i,j,\ell}
to be the set of points from
R_{i,j}
that pays roughly
(1+\varepsilon^{\prime})^{\ell}
is
\mathcal{S}
, i.e.,
I_{i,j,\ell}:=\{p\in R:(1+\varepsilon^{\prime\prime})^{\ell}\leq\textsc{cost}(p,\mathcal{S})<(1+\varepsilon^{\prime\prime})^{\ell+1}\}
, where
\varepsilon^{\prime\prime}
will be set later.

This analysis distinguishes between three cases:

1.

\ell\leq j+\log_{1+\varepsilon^{\prime\prime}}\varepsilon
, in which case we say that
I_{\ell}
is
tiny
. The union of all tiny ranges is denoted
I_{tiny,\mathcal{S}}
.

2.

j+\log_{1+\varepsilon^{\prime\prime}}\varepsilon\leq\ell\leq j+\log_{1+\varepsilon^{\prime\prime}}(4z/\varepsilon)
, in which case we say
I_{\ell}
is
interesting
.

3.

\ell\geq j+\log_{1+\varepsilon^{\prime\prime}}(4z/\varepsilon)
, in which case we say
I_{\ell}
is
huge
.

The very same proof as Lemma 5 and 7 of
[
CSS21
]
shows that the cost the tiny and huge ranges are preserved. For completeness, we provide statement and proofs in
appendix

C
. For the interesting ranges, we use properties of
\varepsilon^{\prime}
-set-approximation as follows.

Lemma 5.9
.

Let
R_{i,j}
be a ring, and
\mathcal{S}
be a solution such that all huge
I_{\ell}
are empty. Further, let
\Omega
be an
\varepsilon^{\prime}
-set-approximation for
(R,\mathcal{B})
, and uniform weights
|R|/|\Omega|
for point in
\Omega
. Then, if
\varepsilon^{\prime\prime}=\varepsilon/10
and
\varepsilon^{\prime}=20\frac{2^{3z}\varepsilon^{2}}{\log(4z/\varepsilon)}
, it holds that
\textsc{cost}(\Omega,S)=(1\pm\varepsilon)\textsc{cost}(R_{i,j},S)
.

Proof.

Fix some solution
\mathcal{S}
. For simplicity, we drop the subscript
i,j
and let
R:=R_{i,j},I_{\ell}:=I_{i,j,\ell}
.

To show that
\textsc{cost}(\Omega,\mathcal{S})=(1\pm\varepsilon)\textsc{cost}(\mathcal{S})
, we will show the following stronger property: for any
\ell
,

|\textsc{cost}(I_{\ell},\mathcal{S})-\textsc{cost}(I_{\ell}\cap\Omega,\mathcal{S})|\leq\frac{\varepsilon}{\log(4z/\varepsilon)}\textsc{cost}(I_{\ell},\mathcal{S}).

Hence, we fix some integer
\ell
. Note that
I_{\ell}=H_{\mathcal{S},(1+\varepsilon^{\prime\prime})^{\ell+1}}\setminus H_{\mathcal{S},(1+\varepsilon^{\prime\prime})^{\ell}}
, where
H_{\mathcal{S},r}=\{p\in P:\text{dist}(p,\mathcal{S})\geq r\}\in\mathcal{B}
.
Since
\Omega
is an
\varepsilon^{\prime}
-set-approximation for
(P,\mathcal{B})
, it holds that

\displaystyle\left|\frac{|H_{\mathcal{S},(1+\varepsilon^{\prime\prime})^{\ell+1}}|}{|R|}-\frac{|H_{\mathcal{S},(1+\varepsilon^{\prime\prime})^{\ell+1}}\cap\Omega|}{|\Omega|}\right|\leq\varepsilon^{\prime}

\displaystyle\left|\frac{|H_{\mathcal{S},(1+\varepsilon^{\prime\prime})^{\ell}}|}{|R|}-\frac{|H_{\mathcal{S},(1+\varepsilon^{\prime\prime})^{\ell}}\cap\Omega|}{|\Omega|}\right|\leq\varepsilon^{\prime}

Combining those two guarantees with triangle inequality ensure that

\displaystyle\left|\frac{|I_{\ell}|}{|R|}-\frac{|I_{\ell}\cap\Omega|}{|\Omega|}\right|

\displaystyle\leq\left|\frac{|H_{\mathcal{S},(1+\varepsilon^{\prime\prime})^{\ell+1}}|}{|R|}-\frac{|H_{\mathcal{S},(1+\varepsilon^{\prime\prime})^{\ell+1}}\cap\Omega|}{|\Omega|}\right|+\left|\frac{|H_{\mathcal{S},(1+\varepsilon^{\prime\prime})^{\ell}}|}{|R|}-\frac{|H_{\mathcal{S},(1+\varepsilon^{\prime\prime})^{\ell}}\cap\Omega|}{|\Omega|}\right|

\displaystyle\leq 2\varepsilon^{\prime}.

Furthermore, by definition of
I_{\ell}
,
|I_{\ell}|\cdot(1+\varepsilon^{\prime\prime})^{\ell}\leq\textsc{cost}(I_{\ell},\mathcal{S})<|I_{\ell}|\cdot(1+\varepsilon^{\prime\prime})^{\ell+1}
. Similarly, due to the weighting of points in
\Omega
, we have
(1+\varepsilon^{\prime\prime})^{\ell}\frac{|R|\cdot|I_{\ell}\cap R|}{|\Omega|}\leq\textsc{cost}(I_{\ell}\cap\Omega,\mathcal{S})
Combining those equations, we get

\displaystyle\textsc{cost}(I_{\ell},\mathcal{S})

\displaystyle\leq|I_{\ell}|\cdot(1+\varepsilon^{\prime\prime})^{\ell+1}

\displaystyle\leq|I_{\ell}\cap\Omega|\cdot(1+\varepsilon^{\prime\prime})^{\ell+1}\cdot\frac{|R|}{|\Omega|}+2\varepsilon^{\prime}\cdot|R|(1+\varepsilon^{\prime\prime})^{\ell+1}

\displaystyle\leq(1+\varepsilon^{\prime\prime})\textsc{cost}(I_{\ell}\cap\Omega,\mathcal{S})+2\varepsilon^{\prime}\cdot|R|(1+\varepsilon^{\prime\prime})^{\ell+1}.

To bound further the right hand side, we let
q\in I_{\ell}
, so that
(1+\varepsilon^{\prime\prime})^{\ell}\leq\textsc{cost}(q,\mathcal{S})
:

\displaystyle|R|(1+\varepsilon^{\prime\prime})^{\ell}

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{p\in R}\textsc{cost}(q,\mathcal{S})\leq\sum_{p\in R}2^{z-1}\textsc{cost}(p,q)+2^{z-1}\textsc{cost}(p,\mathcal{S})

\displaystyle\leq 2^{z-1}\textsc{cost}(R,\mathcal{S})+2^{z-1}\sum_{p\in R}2^{z-1}\left(\textsc{cost}(p,\mathcal{G})+\textsc{cost}(q,\mathcal{G})\right)

\displaystyle\leq 2^{z}\textsc{cost}(R,\mathcal{S})+2^{3z-1}\textsc{cost}(R,\mathcal{G}),

where the last line uses
\textsc{cost}(q,\mathcal{G})\leq 2^{z}\textsc{cost}(p,\mathcal{G})
, since
p
and
q
are in the same ring.

Combined with the previous inequality, this yields

\textsc{cost}(I_{\ell},\mathcal{S})\leq(1+\varepsilon^{\prime\prime})\textsc{cost}(I_{\ell}\cap\Omega,\mathcal{S})+2^{3z}\varepsilon^{\prime}\cdot\left(\textsc{cost}(R,\mathcal{S})+\textsc{cost}(R,\mathcal{G})\right)
.

Similarly, we get

\displaystyle\textsc{cost}(I_{\ell}\cap\Omega,\mathcal{S})\leq(1+\varepsilon^{\prime\prime})\textsc{cost}(I_{\ell},\mathcal{S})+2^{3z}\varepsilon^{\prime}(\textsc{cost}(R,\mathcal{S})+\textsc{cost}(R,\mathcal{G})).

Summing that over the
\log_{1+\varepsilon^{\prime\prime}}(4z/\varepsilon)
interesting ranges and combining with
lemma

C.1
gives that:

\displaystyle|\textsc{cost}(I_{\ell},\mathcal{S})-\textsc{cost}(I_{\ell}\cap\Omega,\mathcal{S})|

\displaystyle\leq~

\displaystyle\log_{1+\varepsilon^{\prime\prime}}(4z/\varepsilon)\cdot 2^{3z}\varepsilon^{\prime}\cdot(\textsc{cost}(R,\mathcal{S})+\textsc{cost}(R,\mathcal{G}))+\varepsilon^{\prime\prime}\textsc{cost}(R\cap\Omega,\mathcal{S})+\varepsilon^{\prime\prime}\textsc{cost}(R,\mathcal{S}).

Hence, we have:

\displaystyle|\textsc{cost}(I_{\ell},\mathcal{S})-\textsc{cost}(I_{\ell}\cap\Omega,\mathcal{S})|

\displaystyle\leq\log_{1+\varepsilon^{\prime\prime}}(4z/\varepsilon)\cdot 2^{3z}\varepsilon^{\prime}\cdot(\textsc{cost}(R,\mathcal{S})+\textsc{cost}(R,\mathcal{G}))

\displaystyle~~+\varepsilon^{\prime\prime}\textsc{cost}(R\cap\Omega,\mathcal{S})+\varepsilon^{\prime\prime}\textsc{cost}(R,\mathcal{S})

\displaystyle\leq\log_{1+\varepsilon^{\prime\prime}}(4z/\varepsilon)\cdot 2^{3z}\varepsilon^{\prime}\cdot(\textsc{cost}(R,\mathcal{S})+\textsc{cost}(R,\mathcal{G}))

\displaystyle~~+2\varepsilon^{\prime\prime}\textsc{cost}(R,\mathcal{S})+\varepsilon^{\prime\prime}|\textsc{cost}(R,\mathcal{S})-\textsc{cost}(R\cap\Omega,\mathcal{S})|

\displaystyle\leq\left(\log_{1+\varepsilon^{\prime\prime}}(4z/\varepsilon)\cdot 2^{3z}\varepsilon^{\prime}+2\varepsilon^{\prime\prime}\right)\cdot(\textsc{cost}(R,\mathcal{S})+\textsc{cost}(R,\mathcal{G}))

\displaystyle~~+\varepsilon^{\prime\prime}|\textsc{cost}(R,\mathcal{S})-\textsc{cost}(R\cap\Omega,\mathcal{S})|,

which is equivalent to

|\textsc{cost}(R,\mathcal{S})-\textsc{cost}(R\cap\Omega,\mathcal{S})|\leq\frac{\log_{1+\varepsilon^{\prime\prime}}(4z/\varepsilon)\cdot 2^{3z}\varepsilon^{\prime}+2\varepsilon^{\prime\prime}}{(1-\varepsilon^{\prime\prime})}(\textsc{cost}(R,\mathcal{S})+\textsc{cost}(R,\mathcal{G}).

Chosing

∎

Two steps dominate the running time of the algorithm: the greedy seeding, and the computation of the
\varepsilon^{\prime}
-set-approximation. For the latter, a direct combination of

5.4
shows that all the
\varepsilon^{\prime}
-set-approximation can be computed in time
k(D/\varepsilon)^{-O(d)}\cdot|X|
.

Hence, it only remains to bound the running time of the greedy seeding.

In the discrete metric case, this is straightforward: each iteration costs
O(|X|\cdot|P|)
, and there are

many of them (see

).

The Euclidean case requires more work, as it is not possible to enumerate over all candidate center. To implement the greedy seeding, we first use

to reduce the dimension to

shows how to implement the greedy seeding algorithm in time

If the solution computed is low cost in the projected space, it is as well low-cost in the original space. Furthermore,

6.1
Computing a Bicriteria Approximation in Low Dimensional Spaces

In this section, we show the following lemma:

Lemma 6.3
.

There exists an algorithm running in time
n\log n(1/\varepsilon)^{O(d)}
that computes a set of
O(k\cdot\log(1/\varepsilon)/\varepsilon)
many centers
\mathcal{S}
, such that
\textsc{cost}(P,\mathcal{S})\leq(1+\varepsilon)\text{OPT}
.

The algorithm is the standard bicriteria approximation. We provide proofs for completeness: our contribution is presented in
section

6.2
.

•

Start computing an
\alpha
-approximation
S_{0}
to
(k,z)
-clustering on
A
.

•

As long as there exists a center
c
such that
\textsc{cost}(S_{i}\cup\{c\}\leq(1-\frac{\varepsilon}{\alpha k})\textsc{cost}(S_{i})
, do
S_{i+1}\leftarrow S_{i}\cup\{c\}
and
i\leftarrow i+1
.

•

Stop this procedure when there is no such center
c
, or
\textsc{cost}(S_{i})\leq\frac{\varepsilon}{\alpha}\textsc{cost}(S_{0})
, and let
S
be the final solution.

We break the proof of
lemma

6.3
into two parts: first we show the quality of the solution, and then how to implement fast the algorithm.

Lemma 6.4
.

\mathcal{S}
is a
\left((1+\varepsilon),\alpha\log(1/\varepsilon)/\varepsilon+1\right)
-bicriteria approximation for
(k,z)
-clustering on
A
.

Proof.

Each center added decreases the cost
by a factor
(1-\nicefrac{{\varepsilon}}{{\alpha k}})
, and the initial solution
S_{0}
has cost
\textsc{cost}(S_{0})
: since the algorithm stops if the cost
drops below
\varepsilon\textsc{cost}(S_{0})/\alpha
, the total number of centers added is at most
\alpha k\log(1/\varepsilon)/\varepsilon
. The total number of centers is therefore at most
\alpha k\log(1/\varepsilon)/\varepsilon+k
.

In the case where the procedure stops because
\textsc{cost}(S)\leq\frac{\varepsilon}{\alpha}\textsc{cost}(S_{0})
, then
S
has cost at most
\varepsilon
times the optimal cost, since
S_{0}
is an
\alpha
-approximation. In that case, we are done.

In the other case, we note that
\textsc{cost}(S_{0})/\alpha\leq\text{OPT}
: hence, the inequality
(1-\nicefrac{{\varepsilon}}{{\alpha k}})\textsc{cost}(S)\geq\textsc{cost}(S\cup\{c\})
implies

\textsc{cost}(S)\geq\textsc{cost}(S\cup\{c\})+\varepsilon\text{OPT}/k
. Hence, there is no candidate center
c
such that
\textsc{cost}(S)-\textsc{cost}(S\cup\{c\})\geq\varepsilon\text{OPT}/k
.
Summing over the
k
centers of the optimal solution
OPT
yields the inequality
\textsc{cost}(S)-\textsc{cost}(S\cup\text{OPT})\geq\varepsilon\text{OPT}
, hence
S
is a
(1+\varepsilon)
-approximation of
OPT. This concludes the lemma.
∎

It remains to show how to implement that greedy procedure fast. For that, we discretize the set of candidate centers, to be able to check all of them.

For a point
p\in P
and a radius
r

\mathbb{C}_{p,r}
be an
\frac{\varepsilon}{z}\cdot r

.
Given some
\alpha

\Delta

We define
\mathbb{C}:=\cup_{p\in P}\cup_{i=\log(\varepsilon/(\alpha z))}^{\log(n/\alpha)}\mathbb{C}_{p,2^{i/z}\Delta^{1/z}}

\mathbb{C}

Lemma 6.5
.

There exists a solution
\mathcal{S}\in\mathbb{C}^{k}
with cost
(1+3\varepsilon)\text{OPT}
. Furthermore,

Proof.

First, the size of
\mathbb{C}
follows immediately from the size of covers in
\mathds{R}^{d}
: there exists
\varepsilon

\varepsilon^{-O(d)}

Given a cluster
C

be the optimal center for

. We show there exist a good approximation

to
c^{*}
in
\mathbb{C}

For this, let
p_{0}
be the closest point in

First, if

Hence, for any point

\displaystyle|\textsc{cost}(p,c)-\textsc{cost}(p,c^{*})|

\displaystyle\leq\varepsilon\textsc{cost}(p,c^{*})+\left(\frac{\varepsilon+z}{\varepsilon}\right)^{z-1}\textsc{cost}(c,c^{*})

\displaystyle\leq\varepsilon\textsc{cost}(p,c^{*})+\left(\frac{\varepsilon+z}{\varepsilon}\right)^{z-1}\left(\frac{\varepsilon}{2\alpha z}\right)^{z}\Delta

In the other case, it must be that

Let

\displaystyle|\textsc{cost}(p,c)-\textsc{cost}(p,c^{*})|

\displaystyle\leq\varepsilon\textsc{cost}(p,c^{*})+\left(\frac{\varepsilon+z}{\varepsilon}\right)^{z-1}\textsc{cost}(c,c^{*})

6.2
From High to Low dimension, and Vice-versa

Our first step is to reduce the dimension, in order to reduce the number of random bits required by

For that, we use a result from

Using
theorem

It is therefore enough to focus on a space of dimension
\tilde{d}=O\left(\varepsilon^{-2}\log n\right)

Assuming now we are provided a

\mathds{R}^{d}

To apply this idea, we enumerate over all possible seeds of bits of length

O\left(\log\tilde{d}\cdot\log(\varepsilon^{-1}k)\right)=O\left(\log(\varepsilon^{-2}\log n)\log(\varepsilon^{-1}k)\right).

theorem

\mathds{R}^{d}

(1+O(\varepsilon))\text{OPT}

The running time is

•

2^{O\left(\log(\log(n)/\varepsilon)\cdot\log(k/\varepsilon)\right)}=k^{O\left(\log(1/\varepsilon)\right)}\cdot\log(n)^{O(\log(1/\varepsilon))}\cdot 2^{O\left(\log\log n\cdot\log k\right)}\leq k^{O(\log(1/\varepsilon)+\log\log k)}\cdot n^{1+o(1)}
