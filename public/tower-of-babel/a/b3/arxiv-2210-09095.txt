Abstract
The reasoning with qualitative uncertainty measures involves comparative statements about events in terms of their likeliness without necessarily assigning an exact numerical value to these events. The paper is divided into two parts. In the first part, we formalise reasoning with the qualitative counterparts of capacities, belief functions, and probabilities, within the framework of two-layered logics. Namely, we provide two-layered logics built over the classical propositional logic using a unary belief modality 𝙱 \mathtt{B} that connects the inner layer to the outer one where the reasoning is formalised by means of Gödel logic. We design their Hilbert-style axiomatisations and prove their completeness. In the second part, we discuss the paraconsistent generalisations of the logics for qualitative uncertainty that take into account the case of the available information being contradictory or inconclusive. Keywords: qualitative probabilities; comparative belief; two-layered modal logics; Gödel logic; Belnap–Dunn logic; paraconsistent logics



1
Introduction

The main objective of this paper is to provide a logical framework for reasoning with uncertain information, where uncertainty in events is expressed qualitatively rather than quantitatively. This approach has been explored in the case of (qualitative counterparts of) probability, but much less so in the case of weaker notions of uncertainty, creating a gap we aim to fill with this paper.
The most general kind of uncertainty measure usually considered in the literature is
capacity
— a function
\mu:2^{W}\rightarrow[0,1]
on the powerset of a set of events
W\neq\varnothing
which is monotone w.r.t.
\subseteq
, non-trivial (
\mu(W)>\mu(\varnothing)
), and normalized:
\mu(\varnothing)=0
,
\mu(W)=1
(cf.
[
21
,
44
]
). We slightly generalize this notion by relaxing the last condition and will call the resulting function an
uncertainty measure
.

We will employ two-layered logical formalism consisting of two separate layers: the inner layer formalizes reasoning about events, while the outer layer formalizes reasoning about beliefs in the events, the uncertainty measure of choice acting as the interpretation of the belief modality acting on formulas describing events. We will use Gödel many-valued (fuzzy) logic to formalize the qualitative reasoning about the resulting beliefs on the outer layer.
Apart from reasoning about events classically, we also wish to consider cases where the actual information about events available to an agent is faulty in the sense that it can be incomplete or even contradictory. Thus, in the apparatus of two-layered logics, we will employ not only classical logic but also
fuzzy
and
paraconsistent
logics to account for this possibility and reason with non-classical information in a
non-trivial
way.

Weaker uncertainty measures

The most prominent examples of uncertainty measures are probability functions, however, they might be considered too strong to formalise people’s beliefs.
Indeed, probabilities assume that the agents are able to express their certainty in a statement with some number from
[0,1]
and then conduct arithmetical operations with these numbers to obtain the probability assignments of complex events: add or subtract them, multiply them by some given constants, etc. This, however, is not always the case. Indeed, outside of scientific contexts (such as conducting medical tests, forecasting the weather, etc.), one can rarely give a number that corresponds to their certainty in a given statement. Furthermore, a layperson might not necessarily know how to obtain values of complex events, although they are usually able to compare their certainty in those.

In
[
7
]
, this aspect was partially tackled with a logic of monotone coherent belief where not only the event description but also the reasoning was done in the language of the Belnap–Dunn logic (
\mathsf{BD}
).
\mathsf{BD}
, however, lacks the capacity to express statements such as ‘I think that the rain tomorrow is more likely than hailstorm’. Here the agent is a layperson and does not know the precise probabilities of any event. Neither are they likely to say something like ‘I am
73\%
sure that it is going to rain tomorrow’. On the other hand, one often can
compare
their degree of certainty in two given statements or at least be able to say something like ‘I don’t know whether rain or hailstorm is more likely tomorrow’.

For the classical case, one of such uncertainty measures is
qualitative probability
. One of the formal systems axiomatising qualitative probability is the modal logic proposed by Peter Gärdenfors in
[
19
]
. It uses a binary modality
\lesssim
such that
\phi\lesssim\psi
is interpreted as ‘
\phi
is at most as likely as
\psi
’. Furthermore, Gärdenfors’ calculus allows for nesting of
\lesssim
which, in turn, leads to formulas of the form

(\tau\lesssim\phi)\lesssim(\chi\lesssim\psi)

to be read as ‘it is at most as likely that
\tau
is at most as likely as
\phi
as that
\chi
is at most as likely as
\psi
’. These statements, however, are quite cumbersome and rarely used in natural language reasoning when people are comparing their certainty in different statements.

This is why, in this paper, we will present a two-layered logic based on Gödel logic expanded with co-implication
\mathsf{biG}
that formalises qualitative reasoning with uncertainty measures. We will also show how to extend it with additional axioms corresponding to the qualitative counterparts of capacities, belief functions, and probabilities. For the last case, we will also show that our logic, in fact, interprets a certain fragment of the Gärdenfors’ calculus where the
\lesssim
does not nest.

Two-layered logics for uncertainty

Two-layered logics consist of two languages: the one that describes the events such as ‘if it rains, then there should be puddles on the road’ (inner layer), and the one wherein the reasoning about beliefs in these events occurs. The uncertainty measure then acts as a non-nesting modality
\mathtt{M}
. Thus, the formulas of the outer language are built from the atoms of the form
\mathtt{M}\phi
with
\phi
being an inner-layer formula.

One of the first formalisations of a logic for reasoning about classical probability is provided in
[
16
,
17
]
. There, the events are described using classical propositional logic. Then, the reasoning happens with so-called
weight formulas
composed of the following constructions

\sum\limits_{i=1}^{n}a_{i}\cdot\mathtt{w}(\phi_{i})\geqslant c.

Here,
a_{i},c\in\mathbb{Z}
,
\phi_{i}
is a propositional formula, and
\mathtt{w}
a
weight
modality denoting the

of the formula.

Although, the formalisation in
[
17
]
is not a two-layered logic

[
1
]
. There, the outer layer is expressed in the language of Łukasiewicz logic, as was first proposed in
[
25
]
. This removes the need of operating with the Boolean combinations of inequalities directly and allows for a finite and straightforward axiomatisation of the whole framework.

The use of many-valued logics (we refer the reader to
[

]
and
[
9

,

]

[

[

, and uncertainty
[

. Most of the previous work, however, employs logics that are capable of expressing a certain amount of arithmetic (usually, addition and subtraction). As this paper concentrates on the qualitative notion of uncertainty, we will be using Gödel logic and its expansions that can only
compare values
of formulas but not conduct arithmetic operations with them. Still, we will show that this is enough to have a logic that can formalise reasoning about probabilities
qualitatively
.

Two-dimensional treatment of inconsistent information

If we recall the understanding of uncertainty, outlined in the first paragraphs of this section, it is clear that classical logic (unsurprisingly) is not well suited to reasoning with non-classical information. Indeed, an arbitrary statement follows classically from a contradiction. This, however, is counter-intuitive for there may be no connection between those. Likewise, even if one does not have any evidence at all whether
\phi

Plan of the paper

The remainder of the paper is structured as follows. In section
2
, we give all necessary preliminaries. In particular, we explain Belnap–Dunn logic
\mathsf{BD}
, Gödel logic with co-implication
\mathsf{biG}
and its paraconsistent expansions
\mathsf{G}^{2}(\rightarrow,\Yleft)
and
\mathsf{G}^{2}(\rightarrowtriangle,\multimap)
(which we dub collectively as
\mathsf{G}^{2}
). We also provide their axiomatisations and establish the completeness of the calculi for
\mathsf{G}^{2}
.

In section
3
, we show how to axiomatise reasoning with different qualitative uncertainty measures. We begin with the logic we dub
\mathsf{QG}
that formalises the reasoning with arbitrary uncertainty measures using Gödel logic. We then consider axiomatic extensions of
\mathsf{QG}
that formalise qualitative reasoning with capacities and belief functions. Finally, we provide
\mathsf{QPG}
— the two-layered logic of qualitative probabilities. We also recall Gärdenfors’ logic of qualitative probability
\mathsf{QP}
and establish a faithful embedding of ‘simple inequality formulas’ (i.e., formulas constructed as Boolean combinations of
\chi\lesssim\chi^{\prime}
formulas of
\mathsf{QP}
without nesting of
\lesssim
) into
\mathsf{QPG}
.

In section
4
, we develop two paraconsistent expansions of
\mathsf{QG}
. We define semantics, construct calculi and prove their completeness. We use these logics to formalise comparisons of certainty in (potentially) contradictory or incomplete information.

Finally, in section
5
, we recapitulate the results presented in the paper and discuss future work.

Logical preliminaries

2.1
Bi-Gödel logic

Gödel logic is an infinite-valued propositional logic, with its standard algebraic semantics being based on the full
[0,1]
interval, where
1
is the designated value. The truth values are (densely) ordered, and, together with the semantics of Gödel implication, this makes Gödel logic suitable for formalizing comparisons. Gödel logic is one of the three basic t-norm based fuzzy logics, and it is also closely related to intuitionistic logic: it is the logic of linearly ordered Heyting algebras and can also be characterized as the logic of linearly ordered intuitionistic Kripke structures, and axiomatized by extending the intuitionistic logic with the axiom of prelinearity. A more detailed exposition of Gödel logics can be found e.g., in
[
10
]
.

In this subsection, we formulate Gödel logic expanded with a co-implication connective
\Yleft_{\mathsf{G}}
and refer to it as
\mathsf{biG}
(bi-Gödel logic), as it can naturally be obtained by extending the bi-intuitionistic logic with the axioms of prelinearity.

.

Belnap–Dunn logic (
\mathsf{BD}
) or first-degree entailment was introduced in

\mathscr{L}_{\mathsf{BD}}:~\phi\coloneqq p\in\mathtt{Prop}\mid\neg\phi\mid(\phi\wedge\phi)\mid(\phi\vee\phi)

•

•

•

\phi

In this paper, we will present

using an alternative semantics of

Definition 2.4

(Belnap–Dunn models)
.

A

is a tuple
\mathfrak{M}=\langle W,v^{+},v^{-}\rangle
with
W\neq\varnothing
and
v^{+},v^{-}:\mathtt{Prop}\rightarrow{\mathcal{P}}(W)
.

Here, each state of the model can be thought of as a source of information. It is clear that regarding each statement, a source can either say that it is true, false, say nothing at all, or provide a contradictory account. This is formalised in the following definition.

Definition 2.5

(Frame semantics for
\mathsf{BD}
)
.

Let
\phi,\phi^{\prime}\in\mathscr{L}_{\mathsf{BD}}
. For a model
\mathfrak{M}=\langle W,v^{+},v^{-}\rangle
, we define notions of

and
w\vDash^{-}\phi
(

as follows.

\displaystyle w\vDash^{+}p

\displaystyle\text{ iff }w\in v^{+}(p)

\displaystyle w\vDash^{-}p

\displaystyle\text{ iff }w\in v^{-}(p)

\displaystyle w\vDash^{+}\neg\phi

\displaystyle\text{ iff }w\vDash^{-}\phi

\displaystyle w\vDash^{-}\neg\phi

\displaystyle\text{ iff }w\vDash^{+}\phi

\displaystyle w\vDash^{+}\phi\wedge\phi^{\prime}

\displaystyle\text{ iff }w\vDash^{+}\phi\text{ and }w\vDash^{+}\phi^{\prime}

\displaystyle w\vDash^{-}\phi\wedge\phi^{\prime}

\displaystyle\text{ iff }w\vDash^{-}\phi\text{ or }w\vDash^{-}\phi^{\prime}

\displaystyle w\vDash^{+}\phi\vee\phi^{\prime}

\displaystyle\text{ iff }w\vDash^{+}\phi\text{ or }w\vDash^{+}\phi^{\prime}

\displaystyle w\vDash^{-}\phi\vee\phi^{\prime}

\displaystyle\text{ iff }w\vDash^{-}\phi\text{ and }w\vDash^{-}\phi^{\prime}

We denote the

\displaystyle|\phi|^{+}\coloneqq\{w\in W\mid w\vDash^{+}\phi\}

\displaystyle|\phi|^{-}\coloneqq\{w\in W\mid w\vDash^{-}\phi\}.

Convention 2.2
.

To facilitate the presentation, we define
\mathsf{Lit}=\mathtt{Prop}\cup\{\neg p:p\in\mathtt{Prop}\}
and denote

\displaystyle\mathsf{Var}(\phi)

\displaystyle=\{p\in\mathtt{Prop}:p\text{ occurs in }\phi\},

One can see that
\mathsf{BD}
inherits truth and falsity conditions from the classical logic but treats them independently. In particular,
w
claims that
\phi
is false (
w\!\vDash^{-}\!\phi
) whenever it claims that its negation is true (
w\!\vDash^{+}\!\neg\phi
); likewise, a conjunction is false when at least one of its members is false, etc. This is why there are no universally true, nor universally false formulas. Indeed, one can check that any
\phi(p_{1},\ldots,p_{n})
is
neither true nor false
at

if
w\!\nvDash^{+}\!p_{i}
and
w\!\nvDash^{-}\!p_{i}
for all
p_{i}
’s.

Furthermore,
\neg\phi\vee\chi
cannot be treated as a shorthand for implication. First, it lacks modus ponens: if both
\phi
and
\neg\phi\vee\chi
are true, it does not entail that
\chi
is true as well (for if
\phi
is both true and false but
\chi
is not true, then
\neg\phi\vee\chi
is still true). Second, the deduction theorem fails: even if
\phi
entails
\chi
, the sequent
\neg\phi\vdash\chi
is still not valid. In fact, even if
\phi\wedge\phi^{\prime}
entails
\chi
, then it is not always the case that
\phi
entails
\neg\phi^{\prime}\vee\chi
, for instance,
p\wedge q\vdash q
is valid while
p\vdash q\vee\neg q
is not. The converse fails as well:
p\vdash p\vee q
is valid but
p\wedge\neg p\vdash q
is not.

Example 2.1

3
Qualitative reasoning about uncertainty

In many contexts, an agent may not necessarily be able to give a precise number that corresponds to their degree of certainty in a given proposition. Indeed, ‘we are 70% certain that it is going to rain tomorrow’ is relatively natural when giving a weather forecast based on some evidence as well as on previous statistical data. On the other hand, if two different people (say, Paula and Quinn) claim that a recently found dog belongs to themselves only, a statement such as ‘I am 65% confident that the dog is Paula’s’ sounds bizarre.

Nevertheless, in such contexts, there is a chance (if Paula, Quinn, or both present some relevant evidence) for a reasoning agent to compare their claims and pick a more compelling one. In other words, even though, we are not really able to measure our certainty in a given statement
numerically
, we can compare evidence for it with evidence for another one.

Qualitative probability was extensively studied, starting already in the work of de Finetti
[
18
]

\preccurlyeq
on the same set iff

\forall X,Y\subseteq W:X\preccurlyeq Y\Leftrightarrow\mu(X)\leq\mu(Y)

This remained an open problem until the 1950s when Kraft, Pratt and Seidenberg in their seminal paper
[
27
]
showed that the answer is negative. They also provided sufficient conditions, which we will discuss below.
The qualitative counterparts of capacities and belief functions were studied as well (see e.g.
[
43

42

3.3
Logics of qualitative probabilities

The main objective of this section is to provide a two-layered axiomatisation of qualitative probabilities. To do this, we need to transform
\mu\mathbf{KPS}_{m}

introduced in
[

is given by the following grammar.

\mathscr{L}_{\mathsf{QP}}:~\phi\coloneqq p\in\mathtt{Prop}\mid{\sim}\phi\mid(\phi\wedge\phi)\mid(\phi\lesssim\phi).

The semantics uses probabilistic frames and models built upon them.

Definition 3.7

(Frame semantics for

[
19

•

;

•

\|{\sim}\phi\|=U\setminus\|\phi\|
;

•

\|\phi\land\psi\|=\|\phi\|\cap\|\psi\|

•

4
Paraconsistent logics of monotone comparative belief

As we have discussed in the previous section, it is not necessary that an agent be able to assign a number to their certainty in a given statement. Furthermore, it is possible that the evidence regarding a given statement is contradictory or incomplete, whence if we want to compare our certainty in different statements, we need to treat evidence in favour and evidence against independently. Consider, e.g., the following situation: all sources give contradictory information regarding
\phi
but no information regarding
\chi
, both our certainty that
\phi
is true and our certainty in its falsity should be greater than our certainty in either truth or falsity of
\chi
.

These characteristics of evidence can be illustrated in the context of court proceedings. Indeed, the evidence in court has the features listed above: it is rare that one can reliably measure one’s certainty in any given piece thereof, instead, the court tries to establish whose claims are more compelling; the evidence presented by witnesses can be incomplete or inconsistent; in any court proceeding there are two parties, and the non-contradictory evidence can be treated as favouring one of them (or irrelevant to the process).

This is why we analyse these contexts using paraconsistent Gödel logics introduced in
[
5
]
on the outer layer (while
\mathsf{BD}
is the inner-layer logic). Since Gödel logic can be seen as a logic of comparative truth, its paraconsistent expansion with a De Morgan negation
\neg
can be seen as a logic of comparative truth and falsity.

We will treat each atomic modal formula
\mathtt{C}\phi
(read ‘the agent is certain in the truth of
\phi
’) as a piece of evidence. The first coordinate supports one party, and the second the other. Pieces of evidence can be combined in different fashions: we can compare our certainty therein using (co-)implication; choose the more or less certain one with
\vee
and
\wedge
, etc. Gödelian negation represents the countering of a given statement — we show that it is absurd. Finally,
\neg
is the operator that swaps support of truth and support of falsity. But in the context of a court session, if a statement is used as an argument for one party, then its negation is actually an argument for the other. Thus, we posit that
\neg\mathtt{C}\phi
is equivalent to
\mathtt{C}\neg\phi
.

Furthermore, there is a difference between criminal and civil proceedings (as well as arbitrations). Namely, during a criminal proceeding, a defendant is pronounced innocent as long as they were able to present conclusive evidence in their favour or counter the evidence of the prosecution. Furthermore, contradictions are usually interpreted in favour of the defendant. Thus, the two parties in the proceeding are not equal in this respect. On the other hand, both parties in a civil court (say, two relatives settle an inheritance dispute in court) or an arbitration present evidence in their own favour, after which the court determines whose evidence was more compelling.

This difference can be formalised if we recall two
\mathsf{G}^{2}
logics and their entailments.
\mathsf{G}^{2}(\rightarrow,\Yleft)
takes into account
both
coordinates of a given valuation which makes it closer to the reasoning demonstrated in a civil process. On the other hand,
\mathsf{G}^{2}(\rightarrowtriangle,\multimap)
takes into account only the first coordinate. Thus, we can associate each coordinate to a party of the process (for
\mathsf{G}^{2}(\rightarrowtriangle,\multimap)
, the first coordinate stands for the defence, and the second one for the prosecution).

Now, if
\Gamma\cup\{\phi\}
is a set of statements concerning some evidence, then entailment relations can be interpreted as preservation of the degree of certainty from premises to the conclusion. We will call
\mathtt{C}
modality of ‘monotone comparative belief’. Here, ‘monotone’ means that
\mathtt{C}
conforms to the underlying
\mathsf{BD}
entailment in the sense that if
\phi\vdash\chi
is valid in
\mathsf{BD}
,
\mathtt{C}\phi
implies
\mathtt{C}\chi
in the outer-layer logic. ‘Comparative’ relates to the fact that we use Gödel logic which can be thought of as a logic of comparative truth.

9

5
Conclusion

In this paper,
we considered the two-layered logic of qualitative probabilities
\mathsf{QPG}
and its paraconsistent expansions
\mathsf{MCB}
and
\mathsf{NMCB}
that formalise reasoning with uncertain, contradictory, or inconclusive information.
For these logics, we provided Hilbert-style axiomatisations and proved their completeness. We showed that the
\mathsf{SIF}
fragment of Gärdenfors’ logic of qualitative probabilities
\mathsf{QP}
can be faithfully embedded into
\mathsf{QPG}
. We also defined generalisations of both
\mathsf{QPG}
and its paraconsistent expansions and proved some results regarding the correspondence between properties of uncertainty measures and two-layered modal formulas.

Still, several questions remain open. First and foremost, Wong conditions and Kraft–Pratt–Seidenberg conditions we gave in section
3
show which uncertainty measures can be extended to a belief function or a probability measure, respectively, while preserving all orders. In
[
26
]
and
[
6
]
, paraconsistent counterparts of probabilities and belief functions were provided. A natural next step would be to establish their qualitative characterisations and provide a two-layered axiomatisation with
\mathsf{G}^{2}
as the outer logic.

In
[
7
,
26
]
, paraconsistent counterparts of belief functions and probability measures were introduced under the titles of ‘non-standard belief functions’ and ‘non-standard probabilities’. Neither
\mathsf{MCB}
nor
\mathsf{NMCB}
, however, have non-standard belief functions or probabilities as their belief measures. This is why it would be instructive to provide a similar characterisation of non-standard belief functions and probabilities and develop a logic of non-standard qualitative probabilities.

Second, we established a faithful translation of
\mathsf{SIF}
’s into
\mathscr{L}_{\mathsf{QG}}
. A natural question is whether there are any other classes of
\mathscr{L}_{\mathsf{QP}}
formulas that can be faithfully translated into
\mathscr{L}_{\mathsf{QG}}
. The most evident candidates would be formulas without nesting of
\lesssim
and formulas where each variable is in the scope of
\lesssim
.

Finally,
\mathsf{QP}
has finite model property and is decidable. Since proofs in
\mathsf{QG}
and its expansions, as well as those in
\mathsf{MCB}
and
\mathsf{NMCB}
, are just derivations from assumptions in
\mathsf{biG}
and
\mathsf{G}^{2}
,
\mathsf{QG}
,
\mathsf{MCB}
, and
\mathsf{NMCB}
and their expansions are decidable as well. Thus, a straightforward and explicit decision procedure along with the complexity evaluation would be desirable to obtain.

s\in\mathfrak{M}
it holds that

\text{if }\mathfrak{M},s\vDash^{+}[\Gamma]\text{ then }\mathfrak{M},s\vDash^{+}\phi

Theorem A.4

(
\mathcal{H}\mathsf{G}^{2}(\rightarrow,\Yleft)
completeness)
.

Let
\Gamma\cup\{\phi\}\subseteq\mathscr{L}_{\mathsf{G}^{2}(\rightarrow,\Yleft)}
. Then
\Gamma\models\phi
iff there is a derivation of
\phi
from
\Gamma
in
\mathcal{H}\mathsf{G}^{2}(\rightarrow,\Yleft)
s.t.
\mathsf{nec}
is applied only to
\mathcal{H}\mathsf{G}^{2}(\rightarrow,\Yleft)
theorems.

Proof.

Recall first, that adding
\mathsf{prel}
axioms to
\mathcal{H}\mathsf{HB}
produces a calculus for Gödel logic with co-implication which is complete w.r.t. linear frames. We use De Morgan laws to transform every formula into its negation normal form. Now let
\phi^{*}
be a negation normal form of
\phi
where each literal
\neg p
is substituted with a fresh variable
p^{*}
. We know from
[
41
, Lemma 12]
that
\phi
is falsified on a
\mathsf{G}^{2}(\rightarrow,\Yleft)
model iff
\phi^{*}
is falsified on an
\mathsf{HB}
model
over the same frame
. Thus,
\mathcal{H}\mathsf{G}^{2}(\rightarrow,\Yleft)
is complete w.r.t. the class of linear frames.

∎

The completeness result for
\mathcal{H}\mathsf{G}^{2}(\rightarrowtriangle,\multimap)
can be proved in the same manner.

It now remains to show that every

Definition A.6

(Model counterpart of a
\mathsf{G}^{2}
valuation)
.

Let
\mathbf{v}
be a
\mathsf{G}^{2}
valuation on
[0,1]^{\Join}
. A model
\mathfrak{M}_{\mathbf{v}}=\langle\mathbb{Q},\leq_{\mathbf{v}},v^{+}_{\mathbf{v}},v^{-}_{\mathbf{v}}\rangle
is a
counterpart
of
\mathbf{v}
if for its valuations
v^{+}_{\mathbf{v}}
and
v^{-}_{\mathbf{v}}
it holds that:

\displaystyle v^{+}_{\mathbf{v}}(p)=\mathbb{Q}

\displaystyle\text{ iff }\mathbf{v}_{1}(p)=1

\displaystyle v^{-}_{\mathbf{v}}(p)=\mathbb{Q}

\displaystyle\text{ iff }\mathbf{v}_{2}(p)=1

\displaystyle v^{+}_{\mathbf{v}}(p)=\varnothing

\displaystyle\text{ iff }\mathbf{v}_{1}(p)=0

\displaystyle v^{-}_{\mathbf{v}}(p)=\varnothing

\displaystyle\text{ iff }\mathbf{v}_{2}(p)=0

\displaystyle v^{+}_{\mathbf{v}}(p)\subseteq v^{+}_{\mathbf{v}}(q)

\displaystyle\text{ iff }\mathbf{v}_{1}(p)\leqslant\mathbf{v}_{1}(q)

\displaystyle v^{-}_{\mathbf{v}}(p)\subseteq v^{-}_{\mathbf{v}}(q)

\displaystyle\text{ iff }\mathbf{v}_{2}(p)\leqslant\mathbf{v}_{2}(q)

\displaystyle v^{-}_{\mathbf{v}}(p)\subseteq v^{+}_{\mathbf{v}}(q)

\displaystyle\text{ iff }\mathbf{v}_{2}(p)\leqslant\mathbf{v}_{1}(q)

\displaystyle v^{+}_{\mathbf{v}}(p)\subseteq v^{-}_{\mathbf{v}}(q)

\displaystyle\text{ iff }e_{1}(p)\leqslant e_{2}(q)

Lemma A.7
.

Let
\phi,\phi^{\prime}\in\mathscr{L}_{\mathsf{G}^{2}(\rightarrow,\Yleft)}\cup\mathscr{L}_{\mathsf{G}^{2}(\rightarrowtriangle,\multimap)}
,
\mathbf{v}
be a valuation on
[0,1]^{\Join}
, and
\mathfrak{M}_{\mathbf{v}}
be a counterpart of
\mathbf{v}
. Then it holds that

\displaystyle\mathbf{v}_{1}(\phi)=1

\displaystyle\text{ iff }\mathfrak{M}_{\mathbf{v}}\vDash^{+}\phi

\displaystyle\mathbf{v}_{2}(\phi)=1

\displaystyle\text{ iff }\mathfrak{M}_{\mathbf{v}}\vDash^{-}\phi

\displaystyle\mathbf{v}_{1}(\phi)\leqslant\mathbf{v}_{1}(\phi^{\prime})

\displaystyle\text{ iff }v_{\mathbf{v}}^{+}(\phi)\subseteq v_{\mathbf{v}}^{+}(\phi^{\prime})

\displaystyle\mathbf{v}_{2}(\phi)\leqslant\mathbf{v}_{2}(\phi^{\prime})

\displaystyle\text{ iff }v_{\mathbf{v}}^{-}(\phi)\subseteq v_{\mathbf{v}}^{-}(\phi^{\prime})

\displaystyle\mathbf{v}_{1}(\phi)\leqslant\mathbf{v}_{2}(\phi^{\prime})

\displaystyle\text{ iff }v_{\mathbf{v}}^{+}(\phi)\subseteq v_{\mathbf{v}}^{-}(\phi^{\prime})

\displaystyle\mathbf{v}_{2}(\phi)\leqslant\mathbf{v}_{1}(\phi^{\prime})

\displaystyle\text{ iff }v_{\mathbf{v}}^{-}(\phi)\subseteq v_{\mathbf{v}}^{+}(\phi^{\prime})

Proof.

We proceed by induction on
\phi
. Then the basis cases of variables and constants hold by construction.

For the induction steps, we consider only the case of
\phi=\psi\rightarrowtriangle\psi^{\prime}
. The other ones are straightforward or can be obtained in a similar manner. In the following, we let
v^{\circ}_{\mathbf{v}}
to stand for the counterpart of
\mathbf{v}_{j}
.

\displaystyle\mathbf{v}_{1}(\psi\rightarrowtriangle\psi^{\prime})=1

\displaystyle\text{ iff }\mathbf{v}_{1}(\psi)\leqslant\mathbf{v}_{1}(\psi^{\prime})

\displaystyle\text{ iff }v^{+}_{\mathbf{v}}(\psi)\subseteq v^{+}_{\mathbf{v}}(\psi^{\prime})

(by IH)

\displaystyle\text{ iff }\mathfrak{M}_{\mathbf{v}}\vDash^{+}\psi\rightarrowtriangle\psi^{\prime}

\displaystyle\mathbf{v}_{2}(\psi\rightarrowtriangle\psi^{\prime})=1

\displaystyle\text{ iff }\mathbf{v}_{1}(\psi)=1\text{ and }e_{2}(\psi^{\prime})=1

\displaystyle\text{ iff }\mathfrak{M}_{\mathbf{v}}\vDash^{+}\psi\text{ and }\mathfrak{M}_{\mathbf{v}}\vDash^{-}\psi^{\prime}

(by IH)

\displaystyle\text{ iff }\mathfrak{M}_{\mathbf{v}}\vDash^{-}\psi\rightarrowtriangle\psi^{\prime}

\displaystyle\mathbf{v}_{1}(\psi\rightarrowtriangle\psi^{\prime})\leqslant\mathbf{v}_{j}(\phi^{\prime})

\displaystyle\text{ iff }\left[\begin{matrix}\mathbf{v}_{1}(\psi)\leqslant\mathbf{v}_{1}(\psi^{\prime})\\
\text{and}\\
\mathbf{v}_{j}(\phi^{\prime})\geqslant\mathbf{v}_{2}(\mathbf{0})\end{matrix}\right]\text{ or }\left[\begin{matrix}\mathbf{v}_{1}(\psi)>\mathbf{v}_{1}(\psi^{\prime})\\
\text{and}\\
\mathbf{v}_{1}(\psi^{\prime})\leqslant\mathbf{v}_{j}(\phi^{\prime})\end{matrix}\right]

\displaystyle\text{ iff }\left[\begin{matrix}v^{+}_{\mathbf{v}}(\psi)\subseteq v^{+}_{\mathbf{v}}(\psi^{\prime})\\
\text{and}\\
v^{\circ}_{\mathbf{v}}(\phi^{\prime})\supseteq v^{-}_{\mathbf{v}}(\mathbf{0})\end{matrix}\right]\text{ or }\left[\begin{matrix}v^{+}_{\mathbf{v}}(\psi)\supsetneq v^{+}_{\mathbf{v}}(\psi^{\prime})\\
\text{and}\\
v^{+}_{\mathbf{v}}(\psi^{\prime})\subseteq v^{\circ}_{\mathbf{v}}(\phi^{\prime})\end{matrix}\right]

(by IH)

\displaystyle\text{ iff }v^{+}_{\mathbf{v}}(\psi\rightarrowtriangle\psi^{\prime})\subseteq v^{\circ}_{\mathbf{v}}(\phi^{\prime})

\displaystyle\mathbf{v}_{2}(\psi\rightarrowtriangle\psi^{\prime})\leqslant\mathbf{v}_{j}(\phi^{\prime})

\displaystyle\text{ iff }\mathbf{v}_{1}(\psi)\leqslant\mathbf{v}_{j}(\phi^{\prime})\text{ or }\mathbf{v}_{2}(\psi^{\prime})\leqslant\mathbf{v}_{j}(\phi^{\prime})

\displaystyle\text{ iff }v^{+}_{\mathbf{v}}(\psi)\subseteq v^{\circ}_{\mathbf{v}}(\phi^{\prime})\text{ or }v^{-}_{\mathbf{v}}(\psi^{\prime})\subseteq v^{\circ}_{\mathbf{v}}(\phi^{\prime})

(by IH)

\displaystyle\text{ iff }v^{-}_{\mathbf{v}}(\psi\rightarrowtriangle\psi^{\prime})\subseteq v^{\circ}_{\mathbf{v}}(\phi^{\prime})

∎

.

\displaystyle v^{\mathfrak{M}}_{2}(p)=1

\displaystyle v^{\mathfrak{M}}_{1}(p)=0

Lemma A.9
.

Let
\phi,\phi^{\prime}\in\mathscr{L}_{\mathsf{G}^{2}(\rightarrow,\Yleft)}\cup\mathscr{L}_{\mathsf{G}^{2}(\rightarrowtriangle,\multimap)}
. Then, for any
\mathsf{G}^{2}

\displaystyle\mathfrak{M}\vDash^{-}\phi

\displaystyle v^{+}(\phi)\subseteq v^{+}(\phi^{\prime})

\displaystyle\text{ iff }e_{1}^{\mathfrak{M}}(\phi)\leqslant e_{1}^{\mathfrak{M}}(\phi^{\prime})

\displaystyle v^{-}(\phi)\subseteq v^{-}(\phi^{\prime})

Proof.

Analogously to lemma
A.7
.
