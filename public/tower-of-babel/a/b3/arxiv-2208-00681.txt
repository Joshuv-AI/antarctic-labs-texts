The Many Facets of Trust in AI: Formalizing the Relation Between Trust and Fairness, Accountability, and Transparency CCS: Human-centered computing HCI theory, concepts and models CCS: Social and professional topics Computing profession
Bran Knowles, John T. Richards, Frens Kroeger

Abstract
. Efforts to promote fairness, accountability, and transparency are assumed to be critical in fostering Trust in AI (TAI), but extant literature is frustratingly vague regarding this “trust”. The lack of exposition on trust itself suggests that trust is commonly understood, uncomplicated, or even uninteresting. But is it? Our analysis of TAI publications reveals numerous orientations which differ in terms of who is doing the trusting ( agent ), in what ( object ), on the basis of what ( basis ), in order to what ( objective ), and why ( impact ). We develop an ontology that encapsulates these key axes of difference to a) illuminate seeming inconsistencies across the literature and b) more effectively manage a dizzying number of TAI considerations. We then reflect this ontology through a corpus of publications exploring fairness, accountability, and transparency to examine the variety of ways that TAI is considered within and between these approaches to promoting trust.



1.
Introduction

The matter of trust is strongly associated with considerations of fairness, accountability, and transparency (FAT), with trust often construed as an overarching aim that these contribute toward. This relationship is perhaps most clearly represented by the guidelines produced by The European Commission’s High-Level Expert Group on AI, which identifies fairness, accountability, and transparency as three of its seven key requirements for “Trustworthy AI”
(
AI 2019
)
. Similarly, the Montréal Declaration for Responsible AI states the collective impact of realizing its enumerated principles (which allude to FAT though adopting slightly different terminology) as “lay[ing] the foundation for cultivating social trust toward artificially intelligent systems”
(
de Montréal 2018b
)
—their accompanying report
(
de Montréal 2018a
)
using the word “trust” over 40 times. In their review of documents containing ethical principles for AI, Jobin et al.
(
Jobin
et al. 2019
)
identify trust as one of the 11 common principles (featured in 28 out of 84 documents) alongside transparency, fairness, and responsibility/accountability; and specifically found that 12 of these documents viewed transparency as key to fostering trust.

Despite consensus that Trust in Artificial Intelligence (TAI) matters, precious little research has focused specifically on TAI; and the little that does exist typically does not do justice to trust. Research typically assumes that the technology which trust relates to is complex, but trust itself is not; that the term “AI” unhelpfully collapses a wide range of disparate systems, but the term “trust” is understood the same by all. Consequently, trust often remains under- or even altogether undefined, and its dimensions and complexities are overlooked. Fundamental questions remain regarding “what trust is, and how it can be built and maintained”
(
Sutrop
et al. 2019
)
. This paper endeavours to begin closing this gap.

In what follows, we explicate the myriad facets of TAI explored across the literature. In section 2, we analyze an established corpus of TAI publications and uncollapse the manifold ways in which trust and distrust affect the perception and use of AI. The resulting ontology reveals the full richness and import of TAI, highlighting synergies and tensions between various orientations. In section 3, we demonstrate the usefulness of the ontology by reflecting it through a second corpus of FAT publications, using it to expose under-developed dimensions of TAI and diagnose critical disconnects in how researchers justify their TAI efforts.

Contributions:

(1)

We develop a common frame of reference for explicating different approaches to the multifaceted challenge of TAI to promote greater clarity in the discourse.

(2)

We identify core pillars representing prominent and relatively coherent TAI narratives that may be useful for orienting ones own approach to TAI.

(3)

We demonstrate the further usefulness of this common frame by applying it to FAT literature to reveal important differences in conceptions of TAI underlying the three dominant approaches to trust-promotion, as well as potential anomalies deserving of attention.

2.
Surveying the field

The first corpus of texts underlying our review of trust in AI literature was created through a moderated process of selection and group discussion. The working group tasked with compiling a list of texts relevant to the topic of trust in AI was assembled by the Partnership on AI (PAI)
(see the acknowledgements section at the end of this paper).

1

While the conclusions about the state of the literature to date presented in what follows owe a number of impulses to the discussions of the working group, as summarized in a Key Insights document (available: https://tinyurl.com/3pwfjpux), here the authors have tried to go much further in depth of analysis regarding axes of difference within the literature.
It consisted of 30 industry professionals and academics who had significant expertise relevant to the topic of trust in AI, with a more active core group consisting of 10 individuals including the authors of this paper. Working group members were asked to submit their suggestions for relevant articles to an online repository, which was subsequently shared with the group. Members were then asked to highlight texts on whose inclusion in the repository they disagreed; texts were removed if at least two working group members had recommended this independently of each other. After the removal of these texts as well as duplicates, the repository comprised 78 texts, mostly academic in nature, but also including a number of industry reports and some items on the topic which were slightly more journalistic in style. Regular catch-ups and discussions were scheduled for the working group via Zoom. Initial discussions focused on understanding the nature and composition of the corpus of texts assembled. One important topic of discussion was apparent bias within the corpus towards individual concepts (notably: explainability) and whether or not this bias should be corrected. It was decided to expand the corpus further in order to fill in several blind spots which had been identified in group discussions (e.g. conceptual treatments of trust stemming from social science).

2.1.
Agent: Who is doing the (dis)trusting?

We identify three broad types of trustor. The first (and dominant category) are the
users
who knowingly interact with AI during the accomplishment of a task, such as decision making
(
Lepri et al. 2018
;
Bussone et al. 2015
;
Mittelstadt
et al. 2019
;
Ribeiro
et al. 2016
;
Rieder and Simon 2016
)
.
These interactions raise a variety of concerns helpfully taxonomized as “use, misuse, disuse and abuse” of the AI
(
Parasuraman and
Riley 1997
)
. Explorations of use reveal ongoing “re-negotiation” of trust in the AI “through practices of skepticism, assessment, and credibility”
(
Passi and Jackson 2018
)
. Misuse involves the use of otherwise trustworthy AI in ways that make its effects untrustworthy, in some cases leading to catastrophic failures and complete erosion of trust (see
(
Kroeger 2015
)
). Misuse can result from abuse, i.e. implementing AI
“without regard for the consequences for human performance”
(
Parasuraman and
Riley 1997
)
, and can take the form of “neglect”
(
Hancock et al. 2011
)
. Often misuse occurs as a result of over-trusting
(
Wagner
et al. 2018
)
or over-relying on
(
Dzindolet et al. 2003
;
Bussone et al. 2015
;
Lee and See 2004
)
the AI. Interestingly, publications in the corpus present evidence of
automation bias
, whereby people tend to defer to machines in instances of doubt over conflicting information
(
Wagner
et al. 2018
)
, as well as the contradictory impulse of
algorithm aversion
, whereby “people more quickly lose confidence in algorithmic than human forecasters after seeing them make the same mistake”
(
Dietvorst
et al. 2015
)
. Explanations for this seeming incongruity include differences in agents’ predispositions (including both trusting stance
(
Hancock et al. 2011
;
McKnight and
Chervany 2001
;
Touré-Tillery and McGill 2015
)
and risk tolerance
(
Dutton and
Shepherd 2006
)
); membership in an affected group
(
Woodruff et al. 2018
)
; and level of AI expertise, competence, or attentional capacity
(
Hancock et al. 2011
;
Siau and Wang 2018
)
.
5
5

5

We note the particularly wide spectrum of user types explored across the TAI literature—from non-expert to expert, interacting with the AI in low- to high-risk contexts. These differences matter and require careful consideration.

Other literature is concerned with the potential for agents to disuse the AI if believed to be insufficiently trustworthy
(
Lipton 2017
)
, with one means of preventing this being to assist users in predicting when a system might fail
(
Mittelstadt
et al. 2019
;
Ribeiro
et al. 2016
)
(consistent with strategies for maintaining trust between stakeholders
(
Knowles et al. 2014
;
Knowles 2016
)
). Some of the literature suggests that when the AI behaves in ways that do not meet users expectations, some forms of transparency can mitigate loss of trust
(
Kizilcec 2016
)
and empower more effective use of these systems
(
Rader
et al. 2018
)
.

In contrast to the above, the
subject
is generally not using AI with intentionality, nor are they in a position to disuse it. Rather, this agent is affected by the AI’s recommendations
(
Binns et al. 2018
)
or wider impacts on the society in which they live. Often the subject has limited understanding of AI, but may perceive the AI as generally untrustworthy and experience high levels of anxiety about its impacts
(
Cave
et al. 2019
)
. These feelings may stem from subjects’ comparative lack of agency and disproportional benefits accruing to entities who own the AI
(
Kennedy
et al. 2015
)
. Subjects may seek to redress this imbalance by restricting access to their personal data for use by these systems
(
Woolley 2019
)
. To understand and/or avoid unfavorable decisions being made about them by specific AIs, subjects are thought to be interested less in the ‘how’ of the AI and more the ‘why’

(
Wachter
et al. 2017
)
, but may face significant challenges in contesting these decisions
(
Wachter 2018
)
.

Finally, the
controller
is concerned with assuring the trustworthiness of the AI, either as the entity developing
(
Ribeiro
et al. 2016
)
, purchasing (see
(
Hind et al. 2018
)
, omitted from corpus), monitoring/assessing
(
Lepri et al. 2017
)
, or regulating AI (alluded to in but not a particular focus of this corpus). It is important to recognize that these types of controllers can have quite different agendas and capabilities. We have grouped these controllers together because they all determine the characteristics of the AI in a substantial sense, and do so through the application of formalized trustworthiness criteria
(
Doshi-Velez and
Kim 2017
;
Liu
et al. 2018
)
. Problematically, these may not map neatly to criteria
subjects
or
users
apply in evaluating AI’s trustworthiness. Lay notions of fairness, for example, are not always aligned with statistical fairness constructs
(
Binns et al. 2018
;
Binns 2018
)
; moreover, there is an important temporal dimension to fairness requiring ongoing assessment of impacts
(
Liu
et al. 2018
)
. In addition to efforts to bring these into closer alignment
(
Lee and Baykal 2017
;
Lepri et al. 2018
)
(see “goal congruence”
(
Siau and Wang 2018
)
), there is a recognition of a need to bridge the divide between regulators’ and independent auditors’ trustworthiness criteria and that of developers and purchasers
(
Veale
et al. 2018
)
.

2.2.
Object: In what is the agent (dis)trusting?

Most often, the object of trust within TAI is the
technology
itself. Setting aside the enormous variety of AIs, there are different characteristics of the technology that can be (dis)trusted, including “(1) the performance of the technology, (2) its process/attributes, and (3) its purpose”
(
Siau and Wang 2018
)
—roughly relating to how well it works from the perspective of the user
(
Dudley and
Kristensson 2018
;
Sinha and
Swearingen 2002
)
, how it works
(
Rader
et al. 2018
;
Ribeiro
et al. 2016
)
, and what it does
(
Balaram
et al. 2018
;
Lepri et al. 2018
)
. A key route to trust in the technology is through affecting users’ perceptions of performance through model interpretability
(
Chuang
et al. 2012
;
Doshi-Velez and
Kim 2017
;
Lipton 2016
;
Narayanan et al. 2018
)
. Explanations can be provided to compensate for interpretability shortcomings
(
Glass
et al. 2008
;
Kulesza et al. 2013
;
Ribeiro
et al. 2016
;
Yin
et al. 2019
)
, though do not always lead to greater trust
(
Cramer et al. 2008
)
, e.g. if users are given too much information
(
Kizilcec 2016
)
or are not given the right kind of information
(
Bussone et al. 2015
;
Lim
et al. 2009
)
. Explanations necessarily elide certain details about the model’s inner workings which may prove important for being able to appropriately calibrate trust
(
Mittelstadt
et al. 2019
)
. Counterfactual explanations, which convey why one thing happened rather than another, have been proposed as more useful to enabling subjects to act, above and beyond (or without need of) understanding how the AI works
(
Miller 2018
;
Wachter
et al. 2017
)
. Understanding how the AI works can, however, be useful for correcting mistakes
(
Kulesza et al. 2013
)
, and in some cases individuals may wish to modify the algorithm itself to enhance their trust in it
(
Dietvorst
et al. 2016
)
, or defer to humans regarding “human aspects of context”
(
Bellotti and
Edwards 2001
)
(also
(
Lustig and Nardi 2015
)
) when the logical outcome is not necessarily the (morally) “right” outcome
(
Murphy and Woods 2009
)
. Whether or not the technology is functionally trustworthy, as in it can be trusted to work as intended, questions of trust might arise with respect to the application of that technology (“its purpose”), e.g. the trustworthiness of AI for automating driving
(
Waytz
et al. 2014
)
, for medical decision making
(
Lipton 2017
)
, or indeed for any role formerly performed by human actors
(
Lee 2018
)
. An especially important concern is whether the AI can be trusted to promote justice
(
Binns et al. 2018
)
, and an emphasis on understanding “the mechanics of AI” may in fact distract from or confuse deliberations on “the purposes to which AI can be put”
(
Harper 2019
)
(postdating corpus).

A number of publications suggest a complicated intermingling of trust in the
technology
and trust in the
organization

(
Ullman and Malle 2018
)
. Trust in AI is not simply a proxy for trust in the people who developed the AI
(
Marsh et al. 2020
)
; however, trust in AI depends in part or in whole on the perceived trustworthiness of the organization developing or deploying the AI
(
Hengstler
et al. 2016
)
, and the loss of trust in the algorithm has been found to lead to loss of trust in the organization behind it
(
Woodruff et al. 2018
)
. This suggests the applicability of models of trust originating in organizational studies (a number are included in this corpus
(
Bigley and Pearce 1998
;
Kramer 1999
;
Lewicki
et al. 2006
;
Rousseau
et al. 1998
;
Schoorman
et al. 2007
;
Vanneste
et al. 2014
)
), though the aforementioned intermingling of objects of trust necessitates caution in simply appropriating these without careful nuancing. The TAI literature shows that organizational dynamics, such as norms and expectations and relationships with coworkers (a.k.a. “environmental factors” relating to team collaboration
(
Hancock et al. 2011
)
), can influence reliance on AI outputs
(
Lee and See 2004
)
; and that the use of AI in organizational contexts also introduces dynamics of “meta-trust”, or “trust a person has that the other person’s trust in the automation is appropriate”
(
Lee and See 2004
)
. While we believe trust in the
organization
is a useful category, we note that there may be quite important distinctions between types of organizations that influence the nature or quality of trust agents have in them. In particular, in contrast to private sector organizations developing or deploying AI, there is an expectation that public entities are obligated to protect individuals from harm
(
Veale
et al. 2018
)
—particularly as the agent is typically a
subject
of public sector AI rather than a
user
.

Lastly, it can be
structures
that one trusts, or the wider institution comprised of these structures (see “institutional trust”
(
Abdul et al. 2018
;
McKnight and
Chervany 2001
)
).

6

Institutional trust is important in complex societies where the formation of interpersonal trust is impractical (see
(
Kroeger 2017
)
, not in corpus), though it can produce less stable trust
(
Kroeger 2015
)
.

Structures relate to so-called “deterrence-based trust”
(
Rousseau
et al. 1998
)
, i.e. trust that laws and sanctions are sufficient to ensure AI’s trustworthiness.

7

Sanctions can be applied by peers, as in the case of reputation in e-commerce
(
Sabater and
Sierra 2005
)
.

Public anxiety about AI is linked with a perception that those deploying AI (whether private or public entities) operate without restraint
(
Cave
et al. 2018
)
; thus a key question of structures is the strength of oversight mechanisms
(
Woolley 2019
)
and whether AI can be held accountable to legislative controls placed on it
(
Bellotti and
Edwards 2001
;
Banavar 2016
)
. This “macroscopic societal accountability” is not satisfied through well-crafted explanations of single AIs
(
Abdul et al. 2018
)
, but rather through building trust in broader social systems. It may be an indicator of structures in need of trust-building that reliance on big data for decision making
(
Lepri et al. 2017
;
Kennedy
et al. 2015
)
is more prominent in cultures where institutional trust has eroded (also see
(
Passi and Jackson 2018
)
); and that users (e.g. of Bitcoin) “prefer algorithmic authority to the authority of conventional institutions, which they see as untrustworthy”
(
Lustig and Nardi 2015
)
(a finding supported by
(
Sas and
Khairuddin 2017
)
).

2.3.
Basis: Forming (dis)trust on the basis of what?

TAI largely prioritizes a
cognitive
view of trust that emphasizes understanding as the principal mechanism of trust-formation. Transparency, interpretability, and explanation are direct appeals to this cognitive mode; however, certain cognitive limitations may hinder these approaches. For one, these approaches can place unreasonable demands on the explainee’s time and energy
(
Bunt
et al. 2012
;
Glass
et al. 2008
;
Knowles 2016
;
Kulesza et al. 2013
;
Narayanan et al. 2018
)
. Assuming high motivation to understand the information, however, agents may yet be limited in their ability to understand or make use of it
(
Ananny and
Crawford 2018
;
Rader
et al. 2018
;
Wachter
et al. 2017
;
Yin
et al. 2019
)
—explanations are a social interaction, requiring that the explainer understands and responds to the beliefs and needs of the explainee
(
Miller 2018
)
. And even for the most willing and able agent, it is not possible to see what AI does at all times
(
Ananny and
Crawford 2018
)
.

Importantly, a “too narrowly cognitive” view also neglects “emotional and social influences on trust decisions”
(
Kramer 1999
)
.

Social
(“relational”
(
Rousseau
et al. 1998
)
or “interpersonal”
(
Lewicki
et al. 2006
)
) trust arises through interactions which reveal the trustworthiness of actors implicated in the development, deployment, or use of the AI. For example, people have been found to engage with Facebook as a “quasi-person” on the basis of conceptually linked technology and interpersonal characteristics: “competence-functionality, integrity-reliability, and benevolence-helpfulness”
(
Lankton and
McKnight 2011
)
. Much of the TAI literature draws from the legacy of the well-known Ability-Benevolence-Integrity model
(
Schoorman
et al. 2007
)
in articulating key concerns underlying social trust (e.g.
(
Lankton and
McKnight 2011
;
Pu and Chen 2006
;
Ullman and Malle 2018
)
; also
(
Toreini et al. 2020
)
, not in corpus); but
organizational
dynamics can also affect trust, as subjective assessments of interpersonal reliability play out within the context in which the AI is deployed
(
Hancock et al. 2011
;
Lee and See 2004
)
(see also reflections on agency in fostering trust
(
Corbett and
Le Dantec 2018
;
Knowles et al. 2015
)
). Attending to the latter, TAI can draw from the fact that people behave more trustworthy when they know they are going to be held accountable
(
Erickson and
Kellogg 2000
;
Knowles et al. 2014
;
Riegelsberger et al. 2005
)
. Interestingly, Interactive Machine Learning offers an opportunity for users to form a more inter-personal relationship with the AI itself, with each party affecting the behavior of the other
(
Dudley and
Kristensson 2018
)
.

In addition to rational bases for trusting—whether
cognitive
or
social
reasons for believing in the object’s trustworthi-ness—there can be an
affective
dimension “grounded in reciprocated interpersonal care and concern” which moderates trust formation (
(
McAllister 1995
)
, not in corpus). This
affective
trust relates to the “emotional bond among all those who participate in the relationship” and the feelings associated with betrayal of trust
(
Lewis and Weigert 1985
)
. While this could certainly apply to
organizational
objects of trust, it is an interesting question whether, or to what extent, this applies to the human-AI (
technology
) trust dynamic, as there is no presumption of social exchange in the technological relationship
(
Lee and See 2004
)
, nor the reasonable expectation that this bond will compel AI to act more trustworthy
(
Lewis and Weigert 1985
)
.
People are often thrust into relationships with AI which force them to build trust in ways they wouldn’t normally do socially
(
Lee and See 2004
)
, though susceptibility to trusting anthropomorphized AI reveals a desire for individuals to form a familiar social bond
(
Leong and
Selinger 2019
;
Waytz
et al. 2014
)
despite the inherent social limitations of AI
(
Murphy and Woods 2009
)
. Then again, this bias is greatly dependent on whether the agent believes humans are inherently trustworthy as compared with technology
(
Touré-Tillery and McGill 2015
)
, and in which situations
(
Lee and See 2004
;
Lee 2018
)
. Still,
the literature finds evidence that agents respond positively to perceptions that the AI is “honest”
(
Sinha and
Swearingen 2002
)
and respects their autonomy
(
Glass
et al. 2008
)
; and negatively when the system betrays their expectations and/or annoys them
(
Glass
et al. 2008
)
; and representation can be altered to manipulate affective responses
(
Siau and Wang 2018
)
, as with anthropomorphization. When people have limited rational basis for trusting (e.g. lack of access to evidence of trustworthiness, or difficulty comprehending this evidence), the
affective
component may play a greater role in making, or not making, the leap to trust (see
(
Möllering 2001
)
). This is evident in the public’s aversion to “scary” robots
(
Cave
et al. 2018
)
and fear of myriad consequences of AI
(
Balaram
et al. 2018
)
. When trusting in
structures
, perceptions of situational normality (see also “presentational base”
(
Lewis and Weigert 1985
)
), or the general sense that “everything is in the proper order”
(
Siau and Wang 2018
)
, is conducive to trust.

2.4.
Objective: What is the trust goal?

AI is a new addition to the cultural landscape which does not benefit (nor should it) from presumptions of trustworthiness generally afforded to more established social institutions (see
(
Lewis and Weigert 1985
)
). Many agents, therefore, are in the initial trust-forming stage of their relationship with AI
(
Abdul et al. 2018
)
, and their first impressions of performance and process need to be positive to
gain
trust
(
Siau and Wang 2018
)
. Gaining trust is also an objective as it relates to agents who, for whatever reason, begin with low levels of trust
(
Hengstler
et al. 2016
)
. Typically, trust is gained slowly through direct experience of and continual reassessment of trustworthiness

(
Lewicki
et al. 2006
;
Passi and Jackson 2018
;
Schoorman
et al. 2007
;
Vanneste
et al. 2014
)
. While this process works well for
users
, who can respond to encounters with decisions and explanations provided about those decisions and re-calibrate trust accordingly,
subjects
are often not even aware of their interactions with AI and may only become aware of the AI when it negatively impacts them (see
(
Knowles and
Richards 2021
)
, postdating corpus); though one proposed means of gaining subject trust is through public engagement (e.g. “citizens juries” to elicit the public’s thoughts on ethical or unethical uses of AI)
(
Balaram
et al. 2018
)
.

Different mechanisms are involved in
maintaining
trust (see also “continuous trust”
(
Siau and Wang 2018
)
). Maintaining trust involves continuing to nurture trust while simultaneously protecting against the loss of trust (particularly catastrophic loss of trust)
(
Knowles et al. 2015
)
. Interpretability and explainability research tends to be in service of intervening within an interaction that might otherwise reduce a user’s trust (e.g.
(
Lim and Dey 2009
;
Lim
et al. 2009
;
Kulesza et al. 2013
)
). As noted previously, maintaining trust in the
technology
is particularly difficult given people’s intolerance of mistakes made by AI
(
Dietvorst
et al. 2015
)
and issues with users being able to sustain interest in explanations
(
Bunt
et al. 2012
)
.

The objective of
restoring
trust when it is lost may yet again entail different tactics, principally because the agent’s stance may be characterized as distrust
(
Bigley and Pearce 1998
;
McKnight and
Chervany 2001
)
. For example, restoring
subject
trust in AI would involve active dismantling of culturally ingrained narratives and replacing them with more positive or neutral ones
(
Cave
et al. 2019
;
Siau and Wang 2018
)
. Some scholars conceive of distrust as being a state of lower trust than that brought to initial encounters
(
Schoorman
et al. 2007
)
(i.e. in comparison to the
gain
condition), meaning that greater effort is required to raise trust to the desired level.
But distrust is more than just lack of trust; it is an active stance, making it much more difficult to recover from (see strand of literature on trust repair, e.g.
(
Bachmann
et al. 2015
)
, not included in corpus). Distrust often results in disuse, which tends to result in fewer subsequent trust-building experiences (see
(
Bigley and Pearce 1998
)
).
In addition, distrust tends to be associated with negative
affect
arising from trust having been violated
(
Kramer 1999
)
, as well as the entrenchment of suspicion
(
Kramer 1999
)
, and these are not easily resolved through provision of evidence of trustworthiness.

2.5.
Impact: What are the benefits of having trust?

The logic underlying the
epistemic
rationale for the importance of trust in AI is that productive synergy between
user
and AI arises when trust aligns with correct beliefs regarding trustworthiness
(
Bussone et al. 2015
;
Lee and See 2004
;
Wagner
et al. 2018
)
. Helping the agent to form a correct (or at least interactionally beneficial
(
Adar
et al. 2013
)
) mental model is key to ensuring an effective dynamic based on appropriate reliance
(
Bunt
et al. 2012
;
Dzindolet et al. 2003
;
Hancock et al. 2011
;
Lipton 2016
;
Rader
et al. 2018
;
Ribeiro
et al. 2016
)
. This involves both being able to control the AI, e.g. guiding it toward the correct decision, and making sense of the AI output
(
Abdul et al. 2018
)
, particularly in instances when the AI’s decision does not match that of the user. Experimental evidence shows that people trust the AI more and adjust their own predictions to match the AI’s if the model’s accuracy is observed to be high, but are generally unlikely to do so merely on the basis of high stated accuracy
(
Yin
et al. 2019
)
.

Many statements on the importance of trust in AI are instead couched in
instrumental
terms. It is commonly asserted that trust is critical for realizing the full economic and societal benefits afforded by AI, insofar as lack of trust will lead to lack of adoption across the economy
(
Dietvorst
et al. 2015
;
Dutton and
Shepherd 2006
;
Hengstler
et al. 2016
;
Lee 2018
;
Sabater and
Sierra 2005
;
Siau and Wang 2018
)
. While this argument may hold strongly for purchasers (a type of
controller
),
user
and
subject
distrust may not be determinative of non-use
(
Knowles and
Hanson 2018
)
due to various social and/or organizational entanglements with the technology that prohibit disuse. Other instrumental TAI arguments include usage continuance
(
Dietvorst
et al. 2015
;
Dietvorst
et al. 2016
;
Kizilcec 2016
;
Lankton and
McKnight 2011
;
Lim
et al. 2009
;
Hancock et al. 2011
;
Pu and Chen 2006
;
Parasuraman and
Riley 1997
)
, permitting access to data for use within the AI
(
Woolley 2019
)
, and promoting productivity and/or efficiency within organizations using AI
(
Passi and Jackson 2018
;
Vanneste
et al. 2014
)
(see also
(
Knowles et al. 2014
;
McKnight and
Chervany 2000
;
Riegelsberger et al. 2005
)
).

Less commonly, trust in AI is seen to be
intrinsically
valuable. In sociological literature, trust is considered “a functional prerequisite for the possibility of society”
(
Lewis and Weigert 1985
)
, the foundation of all social relationships (see
(
Ullman and Malle 2018
)
); and it enables humans to take action on the basis of expectation despite the inherent unknowability of the future
(
Möllering 2001
)
(see also
(
Knowles et al. 2015
;
Passi and Jackson 2018
)
). Trust is not only an enabler of a functional society, it affords psychological benefits to its members, e.g. mitigating fear when facing vulnerability
(
Kramer 1999
)
, feeling confident about one’s actions despite uncertainty
(
Doshi-Velez and
Kim 2017
;
Lipton 2017
;
Siau and Wang 2018
)
, and experiencing an emotional bond
(
Touré-Tillery and McGill 2015
)
. The
intrinsic
view makes room for healthy distrust as a key to a harmonious relationship with AI: People may be willing to rely on AI while being aware of issues with the AI that pose a threat to this relationship (see
(
McKnight and
Chervany 2001
)
). Regardless of ultimate adoption, distrust tends to signal that “important matters are at stake”
(
Knowles and
Hanson 2018
)
and, if taken seriously, can exert a corrective force on the development of AI which brings it into closer alignment with human values (e.g.
(
Balaram
et al. 2018
;
Binns et al. 2018
;
Lepri et al. 2018
;
Veale
et al. 2018
;
Woodruff et al. 2018
)
). It may be that, given limited agency in disusing AI, not to mention the challenges in developing ethical AI
(
Cave
et al. 2018
)
, distrust may in fact be the most appropriate “mechanism for dealing with risk”
(
McKnight and
Chervany 2001
)
entailed by the technology.

3.1.
Trusting agents in FAT literature

The FAT corpus contributes new (if limited and conflicting) data regarding potential human biases regarding AI. While one paper finds evidence of automation bias in high-impact decision making contexts, with students perceiving automated decision making (ADM) as fairer than human decision making (HDM) for university admissions
(
Marcinkowski et al. 2020
)
, another finds a lack of consensus whether ADM was fairer than a human judge for decisions relating to granting bail
(
Harrison et al. 2020
)
. The corpus also shows continued interest in (in)appropriate reliance
(

et al. 2020
)

(

et al. 2020

3.2.
Objects of trust in FAT literature

The majority of transparency and fairness contributions within the FAT literature are concerned with trust in the
technology
itself (see Figure 2). This is accompanied, however, by critique that a focus on trust in the

perpetuates the problematic notion of model objectivity
(

)
; that, indeed, “trust in a technology is rooted in relationships—not in a technical specification or feature”
(

and trust in the

et al. 2020
)
; discussion of how AI narrows socially constructed categories

; and explorations of the trustworthiness of the human processes involved throughout the AI development pipeline

, model development

)
, data labeling

.

A resounding call from the FAT corpus is for more situated understandings of trust within deployment contexts

(see also

3.3.
Bases of trust in FAT literature

As with the first corpus, a
cognitive
view of trust dominates the transparency, interpretability, and explainability contributions, at least within the FAccT conference archives. And yet, these works reflect waning confidence in the usefulness of explanations to end-users—non-expert end-users rarely trust the explanations
(
Hancox-Li 2020
)
, decision-making by expert end-users is not improved greatly by explanations
(
Jesus et al. 2021
)
, and contrastive explanations that succeed in helping users understand prediction errors nonetheless do not improve trust in the model
(
Lucic
et al. 2020
)
. The community calls for the development of
consistent criteria for evaluating explanations
(
Sokol and Flach 2020
)
, with resultant trust being a key consideration. The corpus clearly acknowledges a
social
(i.e. interactional) component to successful explanations, with works exploring how to make explanations more intuitive so that they ring true
(
Patel
et al. 2021
)
, tailoring explanations to the informational needs of recipients
(
Sokol and Flach 2020
)
(including preserving subject autonomy
(
Barocas
et al. 2020
)
), and even asking whether explanation recipients can contribute to constructing explanations that work for them
(
Hancox-Li and
Kumar 2021
)
.

Notably, the FAT corpus places slightly more emphasis on
social
trust than
cognitive
trust, and proportionally much greater overall emphasis on
social
aspects, perhaps reflecting the social science leanings within the FAT community. Two in-depth explorations of TAI within the corpus
(
Jacovi et al. 2021
;
Toreini et al. 2020
)
draw heavily from
social
trust models—both seeing TAI as “rooted in, but nevertheless not the same as, interpersonal trust as defined by sociologists”
(
Jacovi et al. 2021
)
. The corpus raises a number of new
social
considerations. One is that

(
Abebe et al. 2021
)
. Not only does dissimilarity breed suspicion regarding the
organization’s

(

)
(as in the Ability-Benevolence-Integrity model), it may affect their Ability to deliver TAI. The work highlights the importance of embracing cultural differences in notions of fairness
(
Abebe et al. 2021
;
Harrison et al. 2020
;
Sambasivan et al. 2021
)
(see also
(
Marcinkowski et al. 2020
)
); taking into account the on-the-ground social realities of
users
and
subjects

(
Abebe et al. 2021
;
Green and Viljoen 2020
;
Katell et al. 2020
)
; co-developing measures of risk with affected communities so that they reflect real harms people experience
(
Katell et al. 2020
;
Kulynych et al. 2020
;
Metcalf et al. 2021
)
; and being seen to make good faith efforts to balance different stakeholder interests
(
Malgieri 2020
;
Marcinkowski et al. 2020
)
.

The
affective
dimension remains comparatively under-developed, despite allusions to unaccounted for dynamics limiting trust
users
and

have in both the

and the

(

)

(

(

;

)

to act in the best interest of those affected by their AI, as in a fiduciary model
(

et al. 2020
)
. The corpus is also greatly concerned with

(
Abebe et al. 2021
)
.

3.4.
Trust objective in FAT literature

Maintaining
trust is less robustly linked with the transparency, interpretability, and explainability contributions in the FAT corpus compared to the first corpus. This may be a function of the particular kinds of agents these corpora focus on.
Maintaining
trust is a key objective in relation to agents whose use is required and/or whose trust facilitates improved decision making outcomes
(

;

;
Panigutti
et al. 2020
)

By far the most common objective within the FAT corpus is

technology

proposing the terminology of “calibrating” trust as more apt
(
Zhang
et al. 2020
)
. Indeed, a particularly noticeable feature of the FAT corpus is its exploration not of how fairness, accountability, and transparency promote trust, but how these either can or should promote
distrust

technology
by data scientists without requiring them to understand how it works
(
Hancox-Li and
Kumar 2021
)
. There is a concern that efforts to improve representativeness within datasets can inspire trust, lending the AI “rhetorical authority, merited or not”
(
Chasalow and Levy 2021
)
. It is also argued that AI may promote unwarranted trust in both

and

(

)
. Similarly, China’s Social Credit System attempts to compensate for high levels of distrust in society through dubious attempts to quantify citizens’ trustworthiness
(
Engelmann et al. 2019
)
.

Restoring
trust is a more common objective in this corpus than the first, perhaps resulting from greater attention to the matter of distrust. Two important contributions emerge. The first is that public distrust in
organizations
is seen as contributing to tighter controls over access to demographic data, which limit

ability to evaluate the fairness of algorithms
(
Andrus
et al. 2021
)
. The second is to shine a spotlight on the need for particular care in
restoring
trust in contexts where distrust results from legacies of colonialism, disempowerment, and/or discrimination
(
Abebe et al. 2021
;
Barabas et al. 2020
;
Bogen
et al. 2020
;
Green 2020
;
Park
et al. 2021
;
Sambasivan et al. 2021
;

;

.

(
Hancox-Li 2020
)
, how to determine when datasets are representative in a meaningful way
(
Chasalow and Levy 2021
)
, and whether TAI principles can be implemented in a way that can be audited
(
Malgieri 2020
)
.

As with the TAI corpus, we see the
instrumental
rationale in (limited) works concerned with adoption
(
Lucic
et al. 2020
)
, as well as those noting that
subjects’
distrust of
organizations
presents a barrier to collecting data
(
Andrus
et al. 2021
;
Sambasivan et al. 2021
)
. As to the latter, attending to algorithmic fairness is thus seen as
instrumentally
valuable in “enabling data collection in low-trust environments”
(
Andrus
et al. 2021
)
; whereas other works propose fairness as important for reputational preservation, including preserving trust in institutions (e.g. higher education)
(
Marcinkowski et al. 2020
)
. There remains an implicit concern that distrust will limit uptake of AI. To address this, AI audits have been proposed as a means of engendering trust, but now the FAT community is wrestling with how to instill trust in the audits themselves
(
Wilson et al. 2021
)
.

Intrinsic rationales for TAI are much more prominent in this corpus than in the first corpus, perhaps because
subject
trust, a strong focus in FAT literature, is not easily justified in
instrumental
or
epistemic
terms. It is clearly seen as “fundamental to human flourishing” that people have “peace of mind”
(
Venkatasubramanian and Alfano 2020
)
regarding the use of AI in society. FAT literature emphasizes that this trust will only be earned by effectively mitigating AI harms
(
Green and Viljoen 2020
;
Katell et al. 2020
;
Kulynych et al. 2020
;
Malgieri 2020
)
(i.e. real harms to real people
(
Metcalf et al. 2021
)
), while at the same time ensuring more equitable distribution of AI benefits
(
Washington and
Kuo 2020
)
. While it is common for TAI to frame distrust as largely unwarranted (e.g. see
(
Cave
et al. 2019
)
from the first corpus)—seeing this distrust as possibly arising from a failure to communicate the technology’s trustworthiness—the FAT literature instead proposes distrust as the most appropriate stance towards AI at this time, given that potential harms and inequities have not been satisfactorily resolved.

Figure 2—breaking out the three promotion mechanisms individually—tells an interesting story about the FAT corpus. There are clearly very different orientations to trust between the transparency subset and fairness and accountability ones. And for the most part, these transparency publications conform to expectations, following down the left column the narrative of
optimizing decision making
. It is surprising, however, that here transparency is presented as a mechanism for
gaining
trust more than for
maintaining
it, particularly given that the TAI literature suggests that explanations are generally sought in moments when trustworthiness is suddenly thrown into question (i.e. when the AI does something unexpected).

The fairness literature also largely conforms to expectations, in line with the
maximizing uptake
narrative (middle column), though it seems odd that a predominant object of trust in these papers is
technology

(10 of 23 papers). Even if most often papers focus on
subject
trust in an
organization
, and some of the fairness papers which explore trust in
technology
are concerned about
user
or
controller
trust, we have to question whether it makes sense to discuss
subject
trust in
technology
. Trustworthiness of the AI itself is largely beyond the capability of
subjects
to gauge, and often they are not even in a position to question the trustworthiness of the AI because it acts upon them in ways unseen
(
Knowles and
Richards 2021
)
. Because of this, we might also have expected more of a recognition of the trust
subjects
place in
structures
as a means of ensuring fairness, i.e. freeing them from having to concern themselves with the AI’s trustworthiness
(
Knowles and
Richards 2021
)
. It also seems odd that while trust is seen as
instrumentally
useful and the most common objective in the fairness works is to
gain
trust (see bold boxes), these are rhetorically disconnected within individual publications, the most common connection being between
gain
and
epistemic
(see bold line).

The accountability subset diverges noticeably from the expected narrative (i.e. not proceeding down the right column), leaving us wondering if there is a coherent justification for accountability other than
minimizing harm
. Granted, the accountability subset was the smallest of the three and perhaps more data are needed to see meaningful patterns; but why isn’t the
controller
a more important agent in these publications? Even among the ones looking at
controllers’
trust, there is surprisingly little discussion in the accountability papers (at least the ones that mention “trust” that were included in this corpus) of trust by regulators. This is out of step with calls within the TAI literature for the need not only to regulate AI but, in order to do this effectively, to focus on what trustworthiness criteria regulators would look to (see section 2.1). For that matter, where is the focus on
structures
? It seems odd for
controllers
(if talking about regulators, that is) to trust in
organizations
, particularly on the basis of
social
trust, as opposed to trusting in an organization’s internal governance
structures
, for example their practices in producing algorithmic documentation
(
Raji et al. 2020
)
. Likewise, surely accountability mechanisms provide assurances of trustworthiness, and these assurances, the fact that it is governed by some capable entity, is what subjects would trust
(
Knowles and
Richards 2021
)
. While we cannot answer these questions based on the current analysis, they may perhaps point to some of the confusions that make meaningful interpretation of ‘trust’ difficult, and hence, to areas where the ontology proposed in this paper could help unify the narrative going forward.

4.
Conclusion

In developing an ontology of TAI, it is tempting to consider whether it is a
true
characterization of the literature. This, of course, is hard to say with certainty. The more interesting question, though, is whether it is
useful
characterization. So what does this model do for us?

The first thing it may do is reveal important distinctions that generally go unstated and therefore unacknowledged. Trust may be different enough between the myriad pathways through the model that it makes little sense to compare findings between papers following very different paths. Going forward, having a set of commonly understood referents may allow authors to make their orientations clear, helping to avoid issues of papers talking past each other and potentially muddling insights into how best (or even whether it makes sense) to promote trust in AI for a particular set of agents in a particular context.

At the same time, despite its complexity, the ontology reveals strong connections between certain entities, helping to simplify what otherwise may seem like disjointed discourse. The three pillars are recognizable, if necessarily simplified, ideal-typical categories within TAI. While clearly not all TAI contributions fit within these three pillars, they may be described in contradistinction to these ideal types as a way of progressing the field.

And finally, we have shown that the ontology can be usefully applied as a lens. We were able to more clearly see what aspects of TAI the FAT community has been most interested in and where their contributions lie; see how contributions on fairness, accountability, and transparency differ in terms of their conceptualizations of trust; and even challenge whether certain arguments are coherent and certain mechanisms for promoting trust are appropriately conceived.
