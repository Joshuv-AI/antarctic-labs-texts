Gromov–Hausdorff class: its completeness and cloud geometry
S. A. Bogaty, A. A. Tuzhilin

Abstract
The paper is devoted to the study of the Gromov–Hausdorff proper class, consisting of all metric spaces considered up to isometry. In this class, a generalized Gromov–Hausdorff pseudometric is introduced and the geometry of the resulting space is investigated. The first main result is a proof of the completeness of the space, i.e., that all fundamental sequences converge in it. Then we partition the space into maximal proper subclasses consisting of spaces at a finite distance from each other. We call such subclasses clouds. A multiplicative similarity group operates on clouds, multiplying all the distances of each metric space by some positive number. We present examples of similarity mappings transferring some clouds into another ones. We also show that if a cloud contains a space that remains at zero distance from itself under action of all similarities, then such a cloud contracted to this space. In the final part, we investigate subsets of the real line with respect to their behavior under various similarities.

This work is devoted to the study of the geometry of the Gromov–Hausdorff proper class

The decision to “break the tradition” was implemented in

A class is called a set if it is a member of some other class. If the class is not a member of any other class, then it is called proper. Thus, it is not be possible to determine a topology on a proper class, because otherwise this class itself is an element of the topology and, therefore, must be a set. Nevertheless, for classes in which the cardinality filtering consists of sets, a “topology” can be introduced by assigning a certain topology to each element of the filtering, and requiring a natural matching of these topologies. This is how the concept of a topological class was introduced in

7

What can we say about the geometry and topology of the Gromov–Hausdorff class? In this paper we show that the Gromov–Hausdorff class is complete. Then we split the Gromov–Hausdorff class into “clouds” — maximal proper subclasses, each of which consists of spaces at a finite distance from each other. A cloud containing a one-point metric space includes exactly all bounded metric spaces. All other clouds are made up of unbounded spaces.

To formulate the following result, we introduce some notation. If

It turns out that everything is much more interesting here. We show by constructing a concrete example that for elements of some clouds, multiplication by
\lambda
can lead to a jump to other clouds. In other words, for a positive
\lambda
, the distance between a metric space
X
and
\lambda X
may be infinite. Moreover, if for some
\lambda

\lambda X
jumped out of a cloud containing

, then, when multiplied by another

, the space
\lambda X
can return to the original cloud. There are also such

that never return: when multiplied by any positive

, the space
\lambda X
is at the infinite distance from the space
X
. Naturally, the problem arises of finding out when the space jumps out, when it stays, when it returns, and in general how the corresponding families of

Another interesting objects of the research are spaces that remain isometric to themselves when multiplied by any positive
\lambda

\operatorname{dil}
-invariant. Example of
\operatorname{dil}
-invariant spaces are a normed space
\mathbb{R}^{n}
, a positive orthant, a bouquet of
\operatorname{dil}
-invariant spaces, etc. An interesting problem is to describe both
\operatorname{dil}

It turns out that if a cloud contains a

this was discovered by Ilya Belalov). For a cloud containing spaces jumping out of the cloud when multiplied by some

Thus, in this paper we prove a fundamental fact about the completeness of the Gromov–Hausdorff class and pose the problem of studying clouds for their “stability” w.r.t. multiplication by positive
\lambda

Another question: which clouds are stable and which ones are not? For example, how can one describe the sequences of real numbers that remain in their clouds? It is clear that all arithmetic progressions are of this kind. What about increasing geometric progressions? We present a number of results describing the non-trivial behavior of these progressions.

1
Basic definitions and preliminary results

As noted in the introduction, we will study the Gromov–Hausdorff distance on the proper class of all metric spaces. Let us recall the corresponding definitions.

In von Neumann-Bernays-Gödel (NGB) set theory, all objects are called
classes
. Classes are of two types:
sets
, which are defined as classes that are elements of other classes, and
proper classes
, which are not elemets of any other classes. Many standard operations are defined on the classes, for example, Cartesian product, mappings, etc. In particular, a distance function can be specified on each class. We will use the following terminology:

•

a
distance function
or, in short, a
distance
on a class
\mathcal{A}
is an arbitrary mapping
\rho\colon\mathcal{A}\times\mathcal{A}\to[0,\infty]
, for which always
\rho(x,x)=0
and
\rho(x,y)=\rho(y,x)
(symmetry) hold;

•

if the distance satisfies the triangle inequality, i.e., if
\rho(x,z)\leq\rho(x,y)+\rho(y,z)
is always satisfied, then
\rho
is called
generalized pseudometric
(the word ”generalized” corresponds to the possibility of taking the value
\infty
);

•

if the positive definiteness condition is additionally satisfied for a generalized pseudometric, i.e., if
\rho(x,y)=0
always implies
x=y
, then we call such
\rho

generalized metric
;

•

finally, if
\rho
does not take the value
\infty
, then in the above definitions we will omit the word ‘generalized”, and sometimes, to emphasize the absence of
\infty
, we call this distance
finite
.

As is customary in metric geometry, instead of
\rho(x,y)
we write
|xy|
as a rule.

As we already noted in the introduction, it is impossible to define a topology on a proper class, since otherwise the proper class will be an element of this topology and, therefore, will be a set. Nevertheless, we have proposed a modified construction, which is also called topology, but already defined on proper classes.

Let
\mathcal{A}
be an arbitrary class. Since all its elements are sets, the cardinality is defined for each of them. For each cardinal number
n
, consider a subclass
\mathcal{A}_{n}
consisting of all elements whose cardinalities do not exceed
n
. Then there is a natural filtering:
\mathcal{A}_{m}\subset\mathcal{A}_{n}
for
m\leq n
. This filtering will be called
filtering by sets
if all
\mathcal{A}_{n}
are sets. For a class that is filtered by sets, we define
topology
as a mapping
\tau\colon n\mapsto\tau_{n}
that maps each cardinal number to a topology on
\mathcal{A}_{n}
so that these topologies are
consistent
, i.e., if
m<n
, then the topology
\tau_{m}
is induced from the topology
\tau_{n}
. A class filtered by sets with the topology defined on it will be called a
topological class
.

Note that if we take a set of cardinality
n
as a class
\mathcal{A}
and define a topology
\tau\colon m\to\mathcal{A}_{m}
as described above, then the topology
\tau_{n}
on
\mathcal{A}_{n}=\mathcal{A}
induces topologies on all
\mathcal{A}_{m}
,
m<n
. On all
\mathcal{A}_{m}
,
m>n
, coinciding in this case with
\mathcal{A}
, the topology
\tau_{m}
will coincide with
\tau_{n}
. Thus, in this case we actually have a standard topology on the set
\mathcal{A}
(all other topologies are obtained in the standard way from it), so our construction can be considered as a generalization of the concept of topology to proper classes.

Further, if a generalized pseudometric (or metric) is given on a class, then it turns this class into a topological one in a standard way. The corresponding topology will be called
pseudometric
(
metric
).

It follows from the NGB axioms that if
X
is a set,
\mathcal{A}
is a class, and
f\colon X\to\mathcal{A}
is a mapping, then the image
f(X)
is a set that belongs to some
\mathcal{A}_{n}
. Thus, if we take a topological space as
X
and a topological class as
\mathcal{A}
, then the
continuity
of the mapping
f
is naturally defined, namely, the mapping
f\colon X\to\mathcal{A}_{n}

is continuous
if its restriction
X\mapsto\mathcal{A}_{n}
is continuous for some and, therefore, for any cardinal
n
satisfying the condition
f(X)\subset\mathcal{A}_{n}
.

The latter allows us to introduce in the standard way the definition of a continuous curve
\gamma
, and with it, in the case when the topology is generated by a generalized pseudometric, the concepts of the length
|\gamma|
of a continuous curve
\gamma
, intrinsic pseudometric, as well as strictly intrinsic pseudometric and geodesic class. Namely, a generalized pseudometric on a set-filtered class
\mathcal{A}
will be called
intrinsic
if for every
X,Y\in\mathcal{A}
,
|XY|<\infty
, and for any
\varepsilon>0
there exists a continuous curve
\gamma
connecting
X
and
Y
such that
|\gamma|<|XY|+\varepsilon
.

Let us now recall the definitions we need from metric geometry, namely, the Hausdorff and Gromov–Hausdorff distances.

1.1
Hausdorff distance

Let
X
be an arbitrary metric space,
x\in X
,
r>0
and
s\geq 0
be real numbers. By
U_{r}(x)
and
B_{s}(x)
we denote, respectively, the
open
and
closed balls
centered at the point
x
and with the radii
r
and
s
. If
A
and
B
are non-empty subsets of
X
, then we put
|XA|=|AX|=\inf\bigl\{|xa|:a\in A\bigr\}
and
|AB|=|BA|=\inf\bigl\{|ab|:a\in A,\,b\in B\bigr\}
. Next, we define the
open
r
-neighborhood of the set
A
by setting
U_{r}(A)=\bigl\{x\in X:|xA|<r\bigr\}
. Finally, the
Hausdorff distance
between
A
and
B
is the value

d_{H}(A,B)=\inf\bigl\{r:A\subset U_{r}(B)\ \&\ U_{r}(A)\supset B\bigr\}.

The Hausdorff distance is a generalized pseudometric: it can be infinite, as in the case of the straight line
\mathbb{R}
and any of its points, and also equal to zero between different subsets, for example, between the segment
[0,1]
and the interval
(0,1)
. Nevertheless, the following classical result holds.

Theorem 1.1

(
[
5
]
)
.

On the set
\mathcal{H}(X)
consisting of all non-empty bounded closed subsets of the metric space
X
, the Hausdorff distance is a metric. Moreover,
X
and
\mathcal{H}(X)
simultaneously possess or not the following properties: completeness, total boundedness, compactness, bounded compactness.

1.2
Gromov–Hausdorff distance

We denote by
\operatorname{\mathcal{VG\!H}}
the proper class consisting of all non-empty metric spaces. On this class, we define a distance function called the
Gromov–Hausdorff distance
:

d_{GH}(X,Y)=\inf\bigl\{d_{H}(X^{\prime},Y^{\prime}):X^{\prime},Y^{\prime}\subset Z\in\operatorname{\mathcal{VG\!H}},\,X^{\prime}\approx X,\,Y^{\prime}\approx Y\bigr\},

where for the metric spaces
U
and
V
the expression

means that these spaces are isometric.

The following theorem is well known.

Theorem 1.2

(
[
5
]
)
.

The Gromov–Hausdorff distance is a generalized pseudometric vanishing on each pair of isometric spaces.

This theorem allows us to investigate the Gromov–Hausdorff distance on a “less wild” proper class
\operatorname{\mathcal{G\!H}}
consisting of representatives of isometry classes of all metric spaces, one from each isometry class. This is similar to considering all sets up to bijection, that is, to consider the proper class of all cardinal numbers.

Theorem 1.3

(
[
7
]
)
.

The proper class
\operatorname{\mathcal{G\!H}}
is filtered by sets, so that the Gromov–Hausdorff distance turns it into a topological class. Moreover, the Gromov–Hausdorff distance is an intrinsic generalized pseudometric on
\operatorname{\mathcal{G\!H}}
.

The topological class
\operatorname{\mathcal{G\!H}}
described in Theorem
1.3
will be called the
Gromov–Hausdorff class
. An important subclass in
\operatorname{\mathcal{G\!H}}
is the proper class which consists of all bounded metric spaces, i.e., spaces
X
with finite diameter
\operatorname{diam}X
. This class will be denoted by
\mathcal{B}
.

Let us describe a few simplest properties of the class
\operatorname{\mathcal{G\!H}}
. By
\Delta_{1}\in\operatorname{\mathcal{G\!H}}
we denote the one-point metric space. Also, for a space
X\in\operatorname{\mathcal{G\!H}}
and a real number
\lambda>0
, we denote by
\lambda X
the metric space that is obtained from
X
by multiplying its all distances by
\lambda
. If
\lambda=0
and
\operatorname{diam}X<\infty
, we put
\lambda X=\Delta_{1}
. The transformation
H_{\lambda}\colon\operatorname{\mathcal{G\!H}}\to\operatorname{\mathcal{G\!H}}
,
H_{\lambda}\colon X\mapsto\lambda X
for

, we call

.

Theorem 1.4

(
[
5
]
)
.

For any
X,Y\in\operatorname{\mathcal{G\!H}}
,

(1)

2d_{GH}(\Delta_{1},X)=\dim X
;

(2)

2d_{GH}(X,Y)\leq\max\{\dim X,\dim Y\}
;

(3)

if the diameter of

or

is finite, then

\bigl|\dim X-\dim Y\big|\leq 2d_{GH}(X,Y)
.

(4)

if the diameter of

is finite, then for any

we have
d_{GH}(\lambda X,\mu X)=\frac{1}{2}|\lambda-\mu|\operatorname{diam}X
, whence it immediately follows that the curve

is shortest between any of its points, and the length of such a segment of the curve is equal to the distance between its ends
;

(5)

for any

, we have

. Note that the only space

for which

is isometric to

\lambda>0
, is the one-point space
\Delta_{1}
. Thus, the similarity transformation
H_{\lambda}
is a homothety of the space

centered at the one-point metric space.

2
Completeness of the Gromov–Hausdorff class

The purpose of this subsection is to prove a fundamental property of the Gromov–Hausdorff class.

Theorem 2.1
.

The Gromov–Hausdorff class is complete. In particular, all clouds are complete.

Proof.

Let
X_{1},X_{2},\ldots
be a fundamental sequence. Without loss of generality, we will assume that
2d_{GH}(X_{n},X_{n+1})\leq 1/2^{n}
. For each
n
, choose a correspondence
R_{n}\in\mathcal{R}(X_{n},X_{n=1})
such that
\operatorname{dis}R_{n}<1/2^{n}
. We put
{\bar{X}}=\sqcup_{n=1}^{\infty}
, and for each
x_{1}\in X_{1}
, we call the sequence
x_{1},x_{2}\in{\bar{X}}
a
thread starting at
x_{1}
if for any
n\geq 1
it holds
x_{n+1}\in R_{n}(x_{n})
. We denote the set of all such threads by
N({\bar{X}})
. For threads
\nu=(x_{1},x_{2},\ldots)
and
\nu^{\prime}=(x^{\prime}_{1},x^{\prime}_{2},\ldots)
, consider the sequence of real numbers
|x_{1}x^{\prime}_{1}|,|x_{2}x^{\prime}_{2}|,\ldots
. Since
\operatorname{dis}R_{n}<1/2^{n}
then
\bigl||x_{n}x^{\prime}_{n}|-|x_{n+1}x^{\prime}_{n+1}|\bigr|<1/2^{n}
, whence for any
m\geq 1
we have

\bigl||x_{n}x^{\prime}_{n}|-|x_{n+m}x^{\prime}_{n+m}|\bigr|=\bigl||x_{n}x^{\prime}_{n}|-|x_{n+1}x^{\prime}_{n+1}|+|x_{n+1}x^{\prime}_{n+1}|-\cdots-|x_{n+m}x^{\prime}_{n+m}|\bigr|\\
\leq\sum_{k=1}^{m}\bigl||x_{n+k-1}x^{\prime}_{n+k-1}|-|x_{n+k}x^{\prime}_{n+k}|\bigr|<\sum_{k=1}^{m}\frac{1}{2^{n+k-1}}<\frac{1}{2^{n-1}},

therefore, the sequence of numbers
|x_{1}x^{\prime}_{1}|,|x_{2}x^{\prime}_{2}|,\ldots
is fundamental and, therefore, there is a limit which we denote by
|\nu\nu^{\prime}|
. It is clear that these limits define a distance function on
N({\bar{X}})
. Since for any three threads
\nu=(x_{1},x_{2},\ldots)
,
\nu^{\prime}=(x^{\prime}_{1},x^{\prime}_{2},\ldots)
,
\nu^{\prime\prime}=(x^{\prime\prime}_{1},x^{\prime\prime}_{2},\ldots)
, and for each
n
, the triangle inequalities hold for the points
x_{n}
,
x^{\prime}_{n}
,
x^{\prime\prime}_{n}
, the same is true for the distances between
\nu
,
\nu^{\prime}
, and
\nu^{\prime\prime}
. Thus, we have defined a pseudometric on
N({\bar{X}})
. Having factorized the resulting pseudometric space with respect to the equivalence relation generated by zero distances, we obtain a metric space which we denote by
X
. For a thread
\nu\in N({\bar{X}})
, the class of this equivalence containing
\nu
we denote by
[\nu]
.

For each
n
, consider the relation
R^{\prime}_{n}\subset X\times X_{n}
defined as follows: for each
x
, consider all threads
\nu=(x_{1},x_{2},\ldots,x_{n},\ldots)\in x
and put all
(x,x_{n})
into
R^{\prime}_{n}
. Since each
x_{n}
belongs to some thread, then
R^{\prime}\in\mathcal{R}(X,X_{n})
. Let us estimate the distortion of the correspondence
R^{\prime}
. Choose arbitrary
x,x^{\prime}\in X
, threads
\nu=(x_{1},x_{2},\ldots,x_{n},\ldots)\in x
and
\nu^{\prime}=(x^{\prime}_{1},x^{\prime}_{2},\ldots,x^{\prime}_{n},\ldots)\in x^{\prime}
, then
|\nu\nu^{\prime}|=\lim_{k\to\infty}|x_{k}x^{\prime}_{k}|
, and as shown above,
\bigl||x_{n}x^{\prime}_{n}|-|x_{n+m}x^{\prime}_{n+m}|\bigr|<1/2^{n-1}
, whence
\bigl||x_{n}x^{\prime}_{n}|-|\nu\nu^{\prime}|\bigr|\leq 1/2^{n-1}
. Thus,
\operatorname{dis}R^{\prime}\leq 1/2^{n-1}
, therefore,
X_{n}\xrightarrow{\operatorname{\mathcal{G\!H}}}X
.
∎

3
Contracted and rain clouds

Starting from this section, we will study how the similarity transformation changes the Gromov–Hausdorff distance, in particular, whether the metric space remains in the cloud, or jumps to another one.

Corollary 3.1
.

Suppose that in a cloud
\mathcal{C}
there exists
X\in\mathcal{C}
such that for some
0<\lambda<1
, the condition
\lambda X\in\mathcal{C}
is fulfilled. Then the mapping
H_{\lambda}\colon\operatorname{\mathcal{G\!H}}\to\operatorname{\mathcal{G\!H}}
,
H_{\lambda}\colon Y\mapsto\lambda Y
, transforms the cloud
\mathcal{C}
into itself, and its restriction to
\mathcal{C}
is a contracting mapping. Thus, for any
X,Y\in\operatorname{CC}
, there are limits
X^{\prime}=\lim_{n\to\infty}H_{\lambda}^{n}(X)
,
Y^{\prime}=\lim_{n\to\infty}H_{\lambda}^{n}(Y)
, with
d_{GH}(X^{\prime},Y^{\prime})=0
.

Proof.

For any
Y\in\mathcal{C}
, we have
d_{GH}(\lambda X,\lambda Y)=\lambda d_{GH}(X,Y)<\infty
, therefore
\lambda Y\in\mathcal{C}
, and the mapping
H_{\lambda}
is contracting. Since the class
\operatorname{\mathcal{G\!H}}
is complete by Theorem
2.1
, all sequences
H_{\lambda}^{n}(Z)
converges as
n\to\infty
, and all their limits are at zero distance from each other (the uniqueness result on the limit of a contracting mapping in the case of pseudometric spaces).
∎

For
X
from a cloud
\mathcal{C}
, we denote by
\Lambda_{X}
the set of all
\lambda>0
for which
\lambda X\in\mathcal{C}
. The following result immediately follows from the equality
d_{GH}(\lambda X,\lambda Y)=\lambda d_{GH}(X,Y)
.

Proposition 3.2
.

The set
\Lambda_{X}
does not depend on the choice of
X\in\mathcal{C}
.

Proposition
3.2
allows us to give the following definition: for a cloud
\mathcal{C}
, we put
\Lambda_{\mathcal{C}}=\Lambda_{X}
for arbitrary

and call it the

\mathcal{C}
stabilizer
.

Remark 3.3
.

It is easy to see that the stabilizer
\Lambda_{\mathcal{C}}
of the cloud
\mathcal{C}
is a subgroup in the multiplicative group of positive real numbers. In addition, Example
4.3
(see below) shows that
\Lambda_{\mathcal{C}}
does not preserve the additive structure: the sum of elements from
\Lambda_{\mathcal{C}}
may belong not to
\Lambda_{\mathcal{C}}
.

Example 3.4
.

Let
\mathcal{C}
be a cloud containing
\mathbb{R}^{n}
. Then for any
\lambda>0
, the spaces
\lambda\mathbb{R}^{n}
and

are isometric. This implies that for any

and every

, we have
d_{GH}(\lambda X,\mathbb{R}^{n})=\lambda\,d_{GH}(X,\mathbb{R}^{n})
, so
\lambda X\xrightarrow{\operatorname{\mathcal{G\!H}}}\mathbb{R}^{n}
as
\lambda\to 0+
. In particular,
\Lambda_{\mathcal{C}}=(0,\infty)
.

This example was first considered by Ilya Belalov.

Definition 3.5
.

A metric space
X
is called
\operatorname{dil}
-invariant
if, for all
\lambda>0
, the spaces
X
and
\lambda X
are isometric. In other words,
\operatorname{dil}
-invariance means that all similarities of a metric space are contained in the isometry group
\operatorname{Iso}(X)
of this space.

Problem 3.6
.

Describe all
\operatorname{dil}
-invariant metric spaces.

Definition 3.7
.

If in a cloud
\mathcal{C}
there is a
Z
such that for all
X\in\mathcal{C}
it holds
\lambda X\xrightarrow{\operatorname{\mathcal{G\!H}}}Z
as
\lambda\to 0+
, then such a cloud is called

.

Similarly with Example
3.4
, the following result can be proved.

Corollary 3.8
.

Each cloud
\mathcal{C}
containing a
\operatorname{dil}
-invariant space is contractible. In particular, it holds
\Lambda_{\mathcal{C}}=(0,\infty)
.

The definition of a
\operatorname{dil}
-invariant space can be generalized.

Definition 3.9
.

We say that a metric space
X
is a
generalized
\operatorname{dil}
-invariant
if for any
\lambda>0
, we have
\partial_{GH}(X,\lambda X)=0
.

Corollary 3.10
.

Each cloud
\mathcal{C}
containing a generalized
\operatorname{dil}
-invariant space is contractible. In particular, it satisfies
\Lambda_{\mathcal{C}}=(0,\infty)
.

Problem 3.11
.

Is it true that each cloud
\mathcal{C}
with
\Lambda_{\mathcal{C}}=(0,\infty)
is contractible?

Definition 3.12
.

For
A\subset(0,\infty)
, a metric space
X
is called
A
-invariant
if for all
\lambda\in A
it holds
d_{GH}(X,\lambda X)=0
.

Corollary 3.13
.

Let
\lambda\in(0,1)
and
A=\{\lambda^{r}:r\geq 0,\,r\in{\mathbb{Q}}\}
. Suppose that there exist a cloud
\mathcal{C}
and a metric space
X\in\mathcal{C}
such that for all
\lambda^{r}\in A
the spaces
\lambda^{r}X
belong to
\mathcal{C}
. Let
Z
be the limiting space for the fundamental sequence
X_{n}=H_{\lambda}^{n}(X)
. Then for all
\lambda^{r}\in A
, the space
Z
is also a limiting one for the fundamental sequence
Y_{n}=H_{\lambda^{r}}^{n}(X)=H_{\lambda^{rn}}(X)
. Moreover,
Z
is
A
-invariant.

Proof.

The sequence
\lambda^{rn}
contains a subsequence
\lambda^{n_{i}}
,
n_{i}\in{\mathbb{N}}
. Indeed, if
r=p/q
,
p,q\in{\mathbb{Z}}
,
p\geq 0
,
q>0
, then for
n=qi
we get
\lambda^{rn}=\lambda_{pi}
. It remains to set
n_{i}=pi
. Thus,
d_{GH}(Y_{n_{i}},Z)\to 0
as
i\to\infty
, whence the fundamentality of the sequence
Y_{n}
implies
Y_{n}\xrightarrow{\operatorname{\mathcal{G\!H}}}Z
.

On the other hand, for each
r\in A
, the sequence
\lambda^{rn}
contains a subsequence
\lambda^{n_{i}}\lambda^{r}
,
n_{i}\in{\mathbb{N}}
. Indeed, putting
r=p/q
again and choosing
n
of the form
1+qi
, we get
\lambda^{rn}=\lambda^{r+pi}=\lambda^{pi}\lambda^{r}
. It remains to put
n_{i}=pi
. Thus,
Z
is the limit of the sequence
H_{\lambda}^{n_{i}}(\lambda^{r}X)
. However, the
\lambda^{r}Z
is the limit of this sequence as well. From the “uniqueness of the limit” it follows that
d_{GH}(Z,\lambda^{r}Z)=0
. The proof is complete.
∎

Definition 3.14
.

If
\Lambda_{\mathcal{C}}\neq(0,\infty)
, then we call the cloud
\mathcal{C}

rain
, and each of its elements a
drop
.

4
Rain clouds

Let us show that rain clouds exist by constructing a series of examples of monotonically increasing sequences of real numbers, which, with an induced metric from the real line, are drops in their corresponding clouds. We start with a few general results.

Lemma 4.1
.

Let
X,Y\in\operatorname{\mathcal{G\!H}}
be unbounded spaces for which there exists
R\in\mathcal{R}(X,Y)
with
\operatorname{dis}R<\infty
.
Then for each
x_{0}\in X
,
y_{0}\in Y
, and
r>0
, there exists
(x,y)\in R
such that
x\in X\setminus U_{r}(x_{0})
and
y\in Y\setminus U_{r}(y_{0})
.

Proof.

Suppose the opposite, i.e., for some
x_{0}\in X
and
y_{0}\in Y
, there exists
r>0
such that each
(x,y)\in R
satisfies
\min\bigl\{|xx_{0}|,|yy_{0}|\bigr\}<r
. Since the space
Y
is unbounded, there exists a sequence
y_{k}\in Y
such that
|y_{0}y_{k}|\to\infty
as
k\to\infty
. Since
R
is a correspondence, for every
k
there exists
x_{k}
such that
(x_{k},y_{k})\in R
. But then, for all sufficiently large
k
, we have
|x_{0}x_{k}|<r
. Therefore, choosing such a
k
, we get

\operatorname{dis}R\geq\sup_{l}\bigl||x_{k}x_{l}|-|y_{k}y_{l}|\bigr|=\infty,

a contradiction.
∎

Theorem 4.2
.

Suppose that for unbounded spaces
X,Y\in\operatorname{\mathcal{G\!H}}
, the following condition holds
:

there exist
x_{0}\in X
and
y_{0}\in Y
such that

\Delta(r)=\inf\Bigl\{\bigl||xx^{\prime}|-|yy^{\prime}|\bigr|:x,x^{\prime}\in X\setminus U_{r}(x_{0}),\ y,y^{\prime}\in Y\setminus U_{r}(y_{0}),\ x\neq x^{\prime},y\neq y^{\prime}\Bigr\}\to\infty

for
r\to\infty
. Then for each correspondence
R\in\mathcal{R}
we have
\operatorname{dis}R=\infty
, i.e.,
d_{GH}(X,Y)=\infty
.

Proof.

Suppose the opposite, i.e., let
\operatorname{dis}R<\infty
. Lemma
4.1
implies that for every
r>0
, there exists
(x,y)\in R
with
|x_{0}x|\geq r
and
|y_{0}y|\geq r
. Choose
r^{\prime}>r
such that
r^{\prime}>\max\bigl\{|x_{0}x|,|y_{0}y|\bigr\}
, then the pair
(x^{\prime},y^{\prime})\in R
corresponding
r^{\prime}
satisfies
x\neq x^{\prime}
and
y\neq y^{\prime}
. But then,

\operatorname{dis}R\geq\bigl||xx^{\prime}|-|yy^{\prime}|\bigr|\geq\Delta(r),

and since
r
can be chosen arbitrarily large, we get
\operatorname{dis}R=\infty
, a contradiction.
∎

Example 4.3
.

Let
p
be a prime number greater than
2
, and
X=\{x_{1}=p,\,x_{2}=p^{2},\,x_{3}=p^{3},\ldots\}\subset\mathbb{R}
. Put
Y=2X
and implement it as a sequence
y_{i}=2x_{i}
. Let us show that
d_{GH}(X,Y)=\infty
and thus
X
is a drop in its cloud
\mathcal{C}
. To do that, we use Theorem
4.2
to show that
\Delta(r)\to\infty
as
r\to\infty
.

Choose arbitrary
x,x^{\prime}\in X
,
x\neq x^{\prime}
, and
y,y^{\prime}\in Y
,
y\neq y^{\prime}
, in such a way that

\min\bigl\{|x_{1}x|,\,|x_{1}x^{\prime}|,\,|y_{1}y|,\,|y_{1}y^{\prime}|\bigr\}\geq r.

Then for some positive integers
m
,
l
,
n
,
k
we get
|xx^{\prime}|=p^{m}-p^{l}
,
|yy^{\prime}|=2(p^{n}-p^{k})
. Since
x\neq x^{\prime}
and
y\neq y^{\prime}
, then
m>l
and
n>k
.

Put
s=\min\{m,l,n,k\}
, then the condition
r\to\infty
is equivalent to
s\to\infty
. Further, let

\delta=p^{m}-p^{l}-2p^{n}+2p^{k}=p^{s}z.

Let us show that
z\neq 0
, whence the required will follow, since
s
can be chosen arbitrarily large, and
|z|\geq 1
, therefore,
|\delta|\to\infty
as
s\to\infty
, and
\Delta(r)\to\infty
as
r\to\infty
, respectively, due to the arbitrariness of the choice
x
,
x^{\prime}
,
y
,
y^{\prime}
.

Let
z=0
, then
\Delta=0
, i.e.,
p^{l}(p^{m-l}-1)=2p^{k}(p^{n-k}-1)
. Since
m-l>0
and
n-k>0
, we get
l=k
, whence the equality
2p^{n-k}-p^{m-l}=1
follows. Since
n-k>0
and
m-l>0
, the left side of the equality is a multiple of
p
, a contradiction.

Remark 4.4
.

Note that if in the construction from Example
4.3
we multiply the space
X
by
p
, then the space will remain in the cloud, since
d_{GH}(X,pX)<\infty
. Thus, it is not true that the existence of a metric space “jumping out” of its cloud for some
\lambda
, it follows that for all
\lambda>0
, other than
1
, the space
\lambda X
does not lie in the cloud as well.

5
An example of studying the structure of a cloud stabilizer

Let
\varphi\colon{\mathbb{N}}\to\mathbb{R}
be such a monotonically increasing function of a positive integer argument that
\varphi(n)\geq n
. For a real number
q>1
, consider the set

(1)

X_{\varphi}=\{x_{n}=q^{\varphi(n)}:n\in{\mathbb{N}}\}\subset\mathbb{R}

with the metric induced from
\mathbb{R}
. We denote the cloud that contains the space
X_{\varphi}
by the same symbol.

In the case of the function
\varphi(n)=n
, we denote the space
X_{\varphi}
by
X_{q}
. Recall that by
H_{\lambda}\colon\operatorname{\mathcal{G\!H}}\to\operatorname{\mathcal{G\!H}}
,
\lambda>0
, we denoted the similarity transformation
H_{\lambda}\colon X\mapsto\lambda X
. Sometimes we will refer to the space
X_{q}
as the space
\{x_{n}=q^{n}:n\in{\mathbb{Z}}\}\subset\mathbb{R}
, which defines the same cloud but is invariant under homothety
H_{q}
.

Corollary 5.1
.

If
q\in{\mathbb{N}}
and
\lambda\in\Lambda_{X_{\varphi}}\cap{\mathbb{Q}}
, then for any
M
there exist
n>k>M
and
m>l>M
such that

(2)

\lambda=\frac{q^{\varphi(n)}-q^{\varphi(k)}}{q^{\varphi(m)}-q^{\varphi(l)}}.

Proof.

Let us show that otherwise
d_{GH}(X,\lambda X)=\infty
. To do this, we use Theorem
4.2
to show that
\Delta(r)\to\infty
as
r\to\infty
.

Let
\lambda=a_{2}/a_{1}
for some coprime positive integers
a_{1}
and
a_{2}
.
Choose arbitrary
x,x^{\prime}\in X
,
x\neq x^{\prime}
, and
y,y^{\prime}\in Y
,
y\neq y^{\prime}
such that

\min\bigl\{|x_{1}x|,\,|x_{1}x^{\prime}|,\,|y_{1}y|,\,|y_{1}y^{\prime}|\bigr\}\geq r.

Then for some positive integers
n
,
k
,
m
,
l
we have

|xx^{\prime}|=q^{\varphi(n)}-q^{\varphi(k)}
,
|yy^{\prime}|=\lambda(q^{\varphi(m)}-q^{\varphi(l)})
.
Since
x\neq x^{\prime}
and
y\neq y^{\prime}
, then
n>k
and
m>l
.

Let
s=\min\{n,k,m,l\}
, then the condition
r\to\infty
is equivalent to
s\to\infty
. Further, let

\delta=q^{\varphi(n)}-q^{\varphi(k)}-\lambda(q^{\varphi(m)}-q^{\varphi(l)})=q^{\varphi(s)}z.

Suppose that
z\neq 0
. Since
s>M
can be chosen as large as desired, and the number
a_{1}z
is an integer, then
|a_{1}z|\geq 1
. Then
|\delta|\to\infty
as
s\to\infty
(since the number
a_{1}
is fixed) and, respectively,
\Delta(r)\to\infty
as
r\to\infty
due to the arbitrariness of the choice of
x
,
x^{\prime}
,
y
,
y^{\prime}
.

Therefore,
z=0
, i.e., the number
\lambda
has the required form.
∎

Theorem 5.2
.

For a rational number
\lambda>0
and an integer
q\geq 2
, the similarity transformation
H_{\lambda}
keeps the space
X_{q}=\{q^{n}:n\in{\mathbb{Z}}\}
in the same cloud, i.e.,
\lambda\in\Lambda_{X_{q}}\cap{\mathbb{Q}}
, if and only if
\lambda=q^{\alpha}
for some
\alpha\in{\mathbb{Z}}
.

We split the proof into several elementary steps some of which are undoubtedly well known and presented here for completeness and consistency.

For a given positive integers
n
,
m
, and an integer
q\geq 2
, we describe all solutions of the equation

(3)

a_{1}(q^{n}-1)=a_{2}(q^{m}-1)

in positive integer coprime numbers
a_{1}
,
a_{2}
.

Proposition 5.3
.

For any integer number
q\geq 2
, we have

(4)

\gcd(q^{n}-1,q^{m}-1)=q^{\gcd(n,m)}-1.

Proof.

We use induction on the number
\max(n,m)
. If
n=m
, then the Equality (
4
) is obvious. If
n>m
, then the inductive transition is given by the chain of equalities

(5)

\gcd(q^{n}-1,q^{m}-1)=\gcd(q^{n}-1-q^{n-m}(q^{m}-1),q^{m}-1)=\\
=\gcd(q^{n-m}-1,q^{m}-1)=q^{\gcd(n-m,m)}-1=q^{\gcd(n,m)}-1.

∎

Corollary 5.4
.

If the positive integers
n
and
m
are coprime, then for every integer
q\geq 2
, the numbers

(6)

q^{n-1}+q^{n-2}+\ldots+q+1\text{ and }q^{m-1}+q^{m-2}+\ldots+q+1\\

are also coprime.

Theorem 5.5
.

For given positive integers
n
,
m
, and an integer
q\geq 2
, positive coprime integers
a_{1}
,
a_{2}

solves the Equation
(\ref{eq:4})
if and only if

(7)

\begin{cases}a_{1}=q^{(r_{m}-1)d}+q^{(r_{m}-2)d}+\ldots+q^{d}+1,\\
a_{2}=q^{(r_{n}-1)d}+q^{(r_{n}-2)d}+\ldots+q^{d}+1,\end{cases}

where
d=\gcd(n,m)
,
r_{n}=n/d
, and
r_{m}=m/d
.

Proof.

Substitute
x=q^{d}
into the well-known equality

x^{r}-1=(x-1)(x^{r-1}+\ldots+x+1)

with
r=n/d
and
r=m/d
. Thus, according to Equation (
4
), the Equation (
3
) can be rewritten as

(8)

a_{1}(q^{(r_{n}-1)d}+q^{(r_{n}-2)d}+\ldots+q^{d}+1)=a_{2}(q^{(r_{m}-1)d}+q^{(r_{m}-2)d}+\ldots+q^{d}+1).

From Corollary
5.4
and the condition
\gcd(a_{1},a_{2})=1
the required equalities follow.
∎

Proposition 5.6
.

For a rational number
\lambda>0
and an integer
q\geq 2
, if the similarity transformation
H_{\lambda}
keeps the space
X_{q}=\{q^{n}:n\in{\mathbb{Z}}\}
in the same cloud, i.e., if
\lambda\in\Lambda_{X_{q}}\cap{\mathbb{Q}}
, then

(9)

\lambda=q^{\alpha}\frac{1+q^{d}+\ldots+q^{(r_{2}-2)d}+q^{(r_{2}-1)d}}{1+q^{d}+\ldots+q^{(r_{1}-2)d}+q^{(r_{1}-1)d}}=q^{\alpha}\frac{q^{r_{2}d}-1}{q^{r_{1}d}-1}

for some integer
\alpha
, positive integer
d
, and coprime positive integers
r_{1}
and
r_{2}
.

Proof.

By Corollary
5.1
, there exist
n>k
and
m>l
such that

\lambda=\frac{q^{n}-q^{k}}{q^{m}-q^{l}}=q^{k-l}\frac{q^{n-k}-1}{q^{m-l}-1}
.
∎

Proposition 5.7
.

If the square of the number
\lambda=\frac{q^{n}-1}{q^{m}-1}
looks like the right hand side of Equation
(\ref{eq:10})
, then
\lambda=1
.

Proof.

Without loss of generality, we can assume that
n>m
. Suppose that there exist positive integers
k
and
l
(with
k>l
) such that

\lambda^{2}=\frac{(q^{n}-1)^{2}}{(q^{m}-1)^{2}}=\frac{q^{k}-1}{q^{l}-1}.

Then

(q^{n}-1)^{2}(q^{l}-1)=(q^{m}-1)^{2}(q^{k}-1),

i.e.,

q^{2n+l}-2q^{n+l}+q^{l}-q^{2n}+2q^{n}-1=q^{2m+k}-2q^{m+k}+q^{k}-q^{2m}+2q^{m}-1.

From divisibility by the number
q
, it follows that
m=l
. After reduction, the following condition is obtained:

q^{2n}-2q^{n}+1-q^{2n-m}+2q^{n-m}=q^{m+k}-2q^{k}+q^{k-l}-q^{m}+2

Under the assumptions
n>m
and
k>l
, it turns out that

must be divisible by

.
∎

Proof of Theorem

.

By Proposition

, all rational numbers in the stabilizer are of the form (

Theorem 5.8
.

If a function
\varphi\colon{\mathbb{N}}\to\mathbb{R}
satisfies

, then for any real numbers
q>1
and

, the similarity transformation

keeps the space

.

Let
\lambda\neq 1
. Suppose that the number
q
is an integer, and the number
\lambda
is rational. By Corollary

, there exist

and

\lambda=\frac{q^{\varphi(n)}-q^{\varphi(k)}}{q^{\varphi(m)}-q^{\varphi(l)}}

If

If

If

\lambda=\frac{1-q^{\varphi(k)-\varphi(n)}}{q^{\varphi(m)-\varphi(n)}-q^{\varphi(l)-\varphi(n)}}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle M\to\infty}}{{\longrightarrow}}0.

If
M
is sufficiently large, we get a contradiction with
\lambda>0
.

If
n>m
, then

\lambda=\frac{q^{\varphi(n)-\varphi(m)}-q^{\varphi(k)-\varphi(m)}}{1-q^{\varphi(l)-\varphi(m)}}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle M\to\infty}}{{\longrightarrow}}\infty.

If
M
is sufficiently large, we get a contradiction with

If

are arbitrary, one needs to return to the proof of Corollary
5.1

,

\delta=q^{\varphi(n)}-q^{\varphi(k)}-\lambda(q^{\varphi(m)}-q^{\varphi(l)})=(1-\lambda)q^{\varphi(n)}\bigl(1-\frac{1}{1-\lambda}q^{\varphi(k)-\varphi(n)}+\frac{\lambda}{1-\lambda}q^{\varphi(l)-\varphi(n)}\bigr)\to\pm\infty.

The cases

∎
