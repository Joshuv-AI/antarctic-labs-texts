1.
Introduction

A
N\times N
matrix
{\mathbf{Z}}=(z_{i,j})_{1\leqslant i,j\leqslant N}
is a
magic square
of order
N
if the sum of the entries in each of its rows, columns, and two main diagonals are equal. The concept of magic squares has fascinated mathematicians and laymen for thousands of years. Although the study of these objects dates back millennia, there are still many unresolved problems concerning magic squares. One of the most famous problems in this area concerns the existence of a
3\times 3
magic square where each element is a distinct square number. This problem became popularized by Martin Gardner in 1996, and was listed in Richard Guy’s book,
Unsolved Problems in Number Theory
.

One may also investigate problems related to so called
multimagic
squares. Given
K\geqslant 2
we say a matrix
{\mathbf{Z}}\in{\mathbb{Z}}^{N\times N}
is a
K
-multimagic square of order
N
or
MMS
(K,N)
for short if the matrices

{\mathbf{Z}}^{\circ k}:=(z_{i,j}^{k})_{1\leqslant i,j\leqslant N},

remain magic squares for
1\leqslant k\leqslant K
. Before we can state our problem of interest we must first discuss
trivial
multimagic squares.

It is clear any multiple of the
N\times N
matrix of all ones is trivially a
MMS
(K,N)
for every
K\geqslant 2
. However, this is not the only family of “trivial” multimagic squares one must consider. Suppose
{\mathbf{Z}}
is an
N\times N
matrix in which every row, column, and both main diagonals contain precisely
N
distinct symbols. Such matrices are known as doubly diagonalized Latin squares of order
N
, or
DDLS
(
N
) for short.
DDLS
(
N
) are known to exist for all
N\geqslant 4
, see
[
7
]
. Then for
N\geqslant 4
any mapping of these
N
symbols to the integers yields a
MMS
(K,N)
for every
K\geqslant 2
. Consideration of these “trivial”
MMS
(K,N)
motivates the following definition.

Definition 1.1
.

For
K\geqslant 2
and
N\in{\mathbb{N}}
a
MMS
(K,N)
is called
trivial
if it utilizes
N
or less distinct integers.

We begin by observing that for
N=1
or
2
the only
MMS
(K,N)
are those with every element being equal. Additionally it is known that every
3\times 3
magic square may be parametrized by three variables
a,b,c
as follows

{\mathbf{Z}}(a,b,c)=\left[\begin{matrix}c-b&c+(a+b)&c-a\\
c-(a-b)&c&c+(a-b)\\
c+a&c-(a+b)&c+b\\
\end{matrix}\right].

Solving for
a,b,c
such that
{\mathbf{Z}}(a,b,c)^{\circ 2}
is a magic square one sees that the only solutions are those with
a=b=0
. Thus for
K\geqslant 2
and
1\leqslant N\leqslant 3
we conclude that the only
MMS
(K,N)
are trivial.

In this paper we investigate the minimal value
N_{2}(K)
for which there exists a nontrivial
MMS
(K,N_{2}(K))
. This type of question has been considered in the past by several authors (see
[
2
,
6
,
11
,
14
,
15
]
) via constructions of
normal
multimagic squares. A multimagic square is said to be
normal
if its elements consists of the integers
1,2,\ldots,N^{2}
. We give a brief overview of the best known results for nontrivial
MMS
(K,N)
below.

In this paper we establish via the Hardy-Littlewood circle method the following result.

Theorem 1.2
.

N_{2}(K)\leqslant 2K(K+1)+1
for
K\geqslant 2
.

This beats previously known results as soon as
K\geqslant 4
and shows that
N_{2}(K)
grows at most quadratically in
K
rather than potentially exponential in
K
. One may prove an analogous statement for prime valued
MMS
(K,N)
by reapplying the entirety of the circle method where we detect prime solutions instead of integer solutions. This, however, is not necessary as via an argument due to Granville in

one may apply the Green-Tao theorem and deduce the following.

Corollary 1.3
.

Given

there exists infinitely many nontrivial prime valued

(K,N)

.

One may potentially generalize these results to multimagic

and

for the

N_{d}(K)\ll_{d}K^{2},

where
N_{d}(K)
is the minimal number for which there exists multimagic
d

N^{d-1}+1
or more distinct integers.

2.
Overview of the Paper

Given
K\geqslant 2
and
N>2K(K+1)
, we let
M_{K,N}(P)
denote the number of
MMS
(K,N)
with entries satisfying

\max_{1\leqslant i,j\leqslant N}|z_{i,j}|\leqslant P.

One easily sees that the number of trivial
MMS
(K,N)
counted by
M_{K,N}(P)
is
O_{N}(P^{N})
, thus if one wishes to establish the existence of infinitely many nontrivial
MMS
(K,N)
it is enough to show that

(2.1)

\frac{M_{K,N}(P)}{P^{N}}\to\infty\quad\text{as}\quad N\to\infty.

We begin by considering a general diagonal system of equations in differing degrees. Let
C=(c_{i,j})_{\begin{subarray}{c}1\leqslant i\leqslant r\\
1\leqslant j\leqslant s\end{subarray}}\in{\mathbb{Z}}^{r\times s}
be given, and consider the diagonal system

(2.2)

\sum_{1\leqslant j\leqslant s}c_{i,j}x_{j}^{k}=0\quad(1\leqslant i\leqslant r,\quad 1\leqslant k\leqslant K).

We define
R_{K}(P;C)
to be the number of solutions
{\mathbf{x}}\in{\mathbb{Z}}^{s}
to (
2.2
) where
\max_{j}|x_{j}|\leqslant P
. This class of problems has been investigated in the past by various authors (see
[
3
,
4
]
). However, in their application of the circle method they require the
r\times s
matrix of coefficients
C
to be
highly nonsingular
, i.e., for all
J\subset\{1,\ldots,s\}
with
|J|=r
one should have

\det\left(c_{i,j}\right)_{\begin{subarray}{c}1\leqslant i\leqslant r\\
j\in J\end{subarray}}\neq 0.

Upon examination of these methods, however, one sees that a slightly weaker condition on the matrix
C
would suffice. This weaker condition has been used previously by Brüdern and Cook
[
5
]
, who investigated diagonal systems of a fixed degree
k
. This is in fact crucial because, upon investigation, one sees that the matrix of coefficients associated to multimagic squares is certainly not highly nonsingular.

These considerations lead us to define a notion of when a matrix
C

dominates
a function. We establish in section
3
an asymptotic formula for
R_{K}(P;C)
provided
C
dominates an appropriate function. Before stating our results we must establish some notation.

For a given
r\times s
matrix
C=[{\mathbf{c}}_{1},\ldots,{\mathbf{c}}_{s}]
and any set
J\subset\{1,\ldots,s\}
, we denote by
C_{J}
the submatrix of
C
consisting of the columns
{\mathbf{c}}_{j}
where
j\in J
. For any
a\in{\mathbb{Z}}
and
b\in{\mathbb{N}}
we denote by
{\text{rem}}(a,b)
the remainder of
a
modulo
b
considered as an integer between
0
and
b-1
.

Definition 2.1
.

We say that a matrix
C
dominates a function
f:{\mathbb{N}}\to{\mathbb{R}}^{+}
whenever the inequality

\text{rank}(C_{J})\geqslant\min\left\{f(|J|),r\right\},

holds for all
J\subset\{1,\ldots,s\}
.

Theorem 2.2
.

Let
K\geqslant 2
and suppose that
C\in{\mathbb{Z}}^{r\times s}
satisfies
s>rK(K+1)
. Then, if
C
dominates the function

F(x)=\max\left\{\frac{x-{\text{rem}}(s,r)}{\left\lfloor\frac{s}{r}\right\rfloor},\frac{x-{\text{rem}}(s-1,r)}{\left\lfloor\frac{s-1}{r}\right\rfloor}\right\},

one has that

R_{K}(P;C)=P^{s-\frac{rK(K+1)}{2}}\left(\sigma_{K}(C)+o(1)\right),

where
\sigma_{K}(C)\geqslant 0
is a real number depending only on
K
and
C
. Additionally
\sigma_{K}(C)>0
if there exists nonsingular real and
p
-adic solutions to the system (
2.2
).

Theorem
2.2
can be seen as a relaxation on the condition that
C
be highly nonsingular, which would be equivalent to
C
dominating the identity function.

In section
4
we establish the existence of a matrix
C^{\text{magic}}_{N}\in\{-1,0,1\}^{2N\times N^{2}}
for which
M_{K,N}(P)=R_{K}(P;C^{\text{magic}}_{N})
and prove that this matrix dominates
F(x)
from Theorem
2.2
. This is done via a combinatorial argument and understanding the underlying linear system associated to the matrix
C^{\text{magic}}_{N}
. In section
5
we establish that
\sigma_{K}(C)>0
. This is done by showing that a
DDLS
(N)
with distinct integer symbols is a nonsingular integer solution to the system (
2.2
) with
C=C^{\text{magic}}_{N}
.

Thus from the conclusions made in sections
4
and
5
in combination with Theorem
2.2
we deduce the following.

Theorem 2.3
.

For
K\geqslant 2
and
N>2K(K+1)
there exists a constant
c>0
for which one has the asymptotic formula

M_{K,N}(P)\sim cP^{N(N-K(K+1))}.

Whence by (
2.1
) we finally establish Theorem
1.2
.

3.
Application of the Circle Method

Our basic parameter,
P
, is always assumed to be a large positive integer. Whenever
\varepsilon
appears in a statement, either implicitly or explicitly, we assert that the statement holds for every
\varepsilon>0
. Implicit constants in Vinogradov’s notation
\ll
and
\gg
may depend on
\varepsilon
,
r
,
s
, and the elements of the matrix
C
.

We also make use of the vector notation
{\mathbf{x}}=(x_{1},\ldots,x_{r})
where
r
is dependent on the context of the argument. Whenever the notation
|{\mathbf{x}}|
is used for a vector or matrix we mean the maximal absolute value of the elements in
{\mathbf{x}}
. Any statement regarding matrices is to be understood componentwise. With this in mind, whenever we write an inequality involving a matrix
{\mathbf{a}}
, such as
X\leqslant{\mathbf{a}}\leqslant Y
, we mean that the inequality
X\leqslant a_{i,j}\leqslant Y
holds for all elements of
{\mathbf{a}}
. Similarly, when
q\in{\mathbb{N}}
and
{\mathbf{a}}
is an integer matrix we write
(q,{\mathbf{a}})
to denote the simultaneous greatest common divisor
q
and the elements of
{\mathbf{a}}
.

We use
\|x\|
to refer to the distance to nearest integer of
x
. We will occasionally define functions of matrices which will change depending on the number of columns of the matrix. As is conventional in analytic number theory, we write
e(z)
for
e^{2\pi iz}
. Additionally, we write
[n]
to denote the set of integers from
1
of to
n
.

We now proceed to define our basic exponential generating functions necessary for our application of the Hardy-Littlewood circle method. Whenever we make references to a collection of
rK
many variables, say
{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}
, we will represent these as an
r\times K
matrix

{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}=\left[\begin{matrix}{\alpha}_{1,1}&\cdots&{\alpha}_{1,K}\\
\vdots&\ddots&\vdots\\
{\alpha}_{r,1}&\cdots&{\alpha}_{r,K}\end{matrix}\right]=\left[{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}_{1},\ldots,{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}_{K}\right].

Thus whenever
{\,{\rm d}}{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}
shows up we mean

\prod_{\begin{subarray}{c}1\leqslant i\leqslant r\\
1\leqslant k\leqslant K\end{subarray}}d{\alpha}_{i,k}.

For any matrix
C=[{\mathbf{c}}_{1},\cdots,{\mathbf{c}}_{s}]
of dimension
r\times s
we define

f_{K}({\boldsymbol{\alpha}};C)=\prod_{1\leqslant j\leqslant s}\sum_{|x|\leqslant P}e\left(\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}1\leqslant k\leqslant K\end{subarray}}\left({\boldsymbol{\alpha}}_{k}\cdot{\mathbf{c}}_{j}\right)x^{k}\right),

S_{K}(q,{\mathbf{a}};C)=\prod_{1\leqslant j\leqslant s}\sum_{1\leqslant u\leqslant q}e\left(\frac{1}{q}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}1\leqslant k\leqslant K\end{subarray}}\left({\mathbf{a}}_{k}\cdot{\mathbf{c}}_{j}\right)u^{k}\right),

and

I_{K}(P,{\boldsymbol{{\gamma}}};C)=\prod_{1\leqslant j\leqslant s}\int_{-P}^{P}e\left(\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}1\leqslant k\leqslant K\end{subarray}}\left({\boldsymbol{{\gamma}}}_{k}\cdot{\mathbf{c}}_{j}\right)z^{k}\right){\,{\rm d}}z.

Then by orthogonality it follows that

R_{K}(P;C)=\int_{[0,1)^{r\times K}}f_{K}({\boldsymbol{\alpha}};C){\,{\rm d}}{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}.

For any
0<Q\leqslant P
we define our major arcs to be

{\mathfrak{M}}(Q)=\bigcup_{\begin{subarray}{c}0\leqslant{\mathbf{a}}\leqslant q\leqslant Q\\
(q,{\mathbf{a}})=1\end{subarray}}{\mathfrak{M}}(Q;q,{\mathbf{a}}),

where

{\mathfrak{M}}(Q;q,{\mathbf{a}})=\{{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}\in[0,1)^{r\times K}:|q{\alpha}_{i,k}-a_{i,k}|\leqslant QP^{-k}\}.

Similarly we define the minor arcs to be
{\mathfrak{m}}(Q)=[0,1]^{r\times K}\backslash{\mathfrak{M}}(Q)
.
We also define

{\mathfrak{S}}_{K}(Q;C)=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}0\leqslant{\mathbf{a}}\leqslant q\leqslant Q\\
(q,{\mathbf{a}})=1\end{subarray}}q^{-s}S_{K}(q,{\mathbf{a}};C),

and

J_{K}(Q,P;C)=\int_{\begin{subarray}{c}{\boldsymbol{{\gamma}}}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{r\times K}\\
|{\boldsymbol{{\gamma}}}_{k}|\leqslant QP^{-k}\end{subarray}}I_{K}(P,{\boldsymbol{{\gamma}}};C){\,{\rm d}}{\boldsymbol{{\gamma}}}.

Lemma 3.1
.

Suppose that
Q=P^{\delta}
for some
0<\delta<\frac{1}{3+2rK}
and one has the bound

(3.1)

\int_{{\mathfrak{m}}(Q)}|f_{K}({\boldsymbol{\alpha}};C)|{\,{\rm d}}{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}=o\left(P^{s-\frac{rK(K+1)}{2}}\right).

Then

R_{K}(P;C)={\mathfrak{S}}_{K}(Q;C)J_{K}(Q,P;C)+o\left(P^{s-\frac{rK(K+1)}{2}}\right).

Proof.

We begin by defining a slightly larger set of major arcs

{\mathfrak{N}}(Q)=\bigcup_{\begin{subarray}{c}0\leqslant{\mathbf{a}}\leqslant q\leqslant Q\\
(q,{\mathbf{a}})=1\end{subarray}}{\mathfrak{N}}(Q;q,{\mathbf{a}}),

where

{\mathfrak{N}}(Q;q,{\mathbf{a}})=\left\{{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}\in[0,1)^{r\times K}:|{\alpha}_{i,k}-a_{i,k}/q|\leqslant QP^{-k}\right\}.

Observe that
[0,1)^{r\times K}\backslash{\mathfrak{N}}(Q)\subset{\mathfrak{m}}(Q)
, whence from (
3.1
) it follows that

\int_{[0,1)^{r\times K}\backslash{\mathfrak{N}}(Q)}|f_{K}({\boldsymbol{\alpha}};C)|{\,{\rm d}}{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}=o\left(P^{s-\frac{rK(K+1)}{2}}\right).

Thus we have

\displaystyle R_{K}(P;C)

\displaystyle=\int_{{\mathfrak{N}}(Q)}f_{K}({\boldsymbol{\alpha}};C){\,{\rm d}}{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}+o\left(P^{s-\frac{rK(K+1)}{2}}\right).

Via standard methods it follows easily that for
{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}\in{\mathfrak{N}}(Q;q,{\mathbf{a}})
one has that

f_{K}({\boldsymbol{\alpha}};C)=q^{-s}S_{K}(q,{\mathbf{a}};C)I(P,{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}-{\mathbf{a}}/q;C)+O(Q^{2}P^{s-1}).

Whence upon noting that
\text{mes}({\mathfrak{N}}(Q))\ll Q^{2rK+1}P^{-\frac{rK(K+1)}{2}}
one deduces

\displaystyle\int_{{\mathfrak{N}}(Q)}f_{K}({\boldsymbol{\alpha}};C)d{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}

\displaystyle={\mathfrak{S}}_{K}(Q;C)J_{K}(Q,P;C)+O\left(Q^{3+2rK}P^{s-\frac{rK(K+1)}{2}-1}\right)

\displaystyle={\mathfrak{S}}_{K}(Q;C)J_{K}(Q,P;C)+o\left(P^{s-\frac{rK(K+1)}{2}}\right).

∎

3.1.
The Minor Arcs

As is evident from Lemma
3.1
, we must establish an adequate bound over the minor arcs. For this we make use of the arguments of
[
3
]
without assuming our matrix is highly nonsingular. We will instead make use of the assumption that
C
dominates the function

(3.2)

F(x)=\max\left\{\frac{x-{\text{rem}}(s,r)}{\left\lfloor\frac{s}{r}\right\rfloor},\frac{x-{\text{rem}}(s-1,r)}{\left\lfloor\frac{s-1}{r}\right\rfloor}\right\},

to derive a Weyl type inequality and a mean value bound.

Definition 3.2
.

We say a matrix
C
of dimensions
r\times rn
is partitionable if there exists a partition
\bigsqcup_{1\leqslant l\leqslant n}J_{l}=[rn]
, for which one has

{\text{rank}\,}(C_{J_{l}})=r\text{ for all }1\leqslant l\leqslant n.

In the proofs that follow we make use of the property that our matrix
C
contains a partitionable submatrix of size
r\times r\left\lfloor s/r\right\rfloor
and given any
j_{0}\in[s]
we have that
C_{[s]\backslash\{j_{0}\}}
contains a partitionable submatrix of size
r\times r\left\lfloor(s-1)/r\right\rfloor
. This may be deduced from the property that
C
dominates (
3.2
) in conjunction with
[
9
, Lemma 1]
.

Lemma 3.3
.

Let
s\geqslant r
and suppose
C\in{\mathbb{Z}}^{r\times s}
dominates the function

\frac{x-{\text{rem}}(s,r)}{\left\lfloor\frac{s}{r}\right\rfloor}.

Then there exists
j_{0}\in[s]
and
\sigma>0
for which

\sup_{{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}\in{\mathfrak{m}}(Q)}|f_{K}({\boldsymbol{\alpha}};{\mathbf{c}}_{j_{0}})|\ll PQ^{-\sigma}.

Proof.

By
[
9
, Lemma 1]
,
C
contains a partitionable
r\times rn
submatrix for all
n\leqslant\left\lfloor\frac{s}{r}\right\rfloor
. Since
s\geqslant r
we may take
n=1
, whence without loss of generality we may assume that the first
r
columns of
C
are linearly independent. Let
\sigma<1/(2K)
and define

{\beta}_{j,k}={\mathbf{c}}_{j}\cdot{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}_{k}.

Now suppose that

\left|\sum_{1\leqslant x\leqslant P}e\left(\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}1\leqslant k\leqslant K\end{subarray}}\left({\boldsymbol{\alpha}}_{k}\cdot{\mathbf{c}}_{j}\right)x^{k}\right)\right|>PQ^{-\sigma}

for all
1\leqslant j\leqslant r
. Then by
[
10
, Lemma 2.4]
one has that there exists
q\ll Q^{2K\sigma}
such that

(3.3)

\left\|q{\beta}_{j,k}\right\|\ll Q^{2K\sigma}P^{-k}\quad\text{for all $1\leqslant j\leqslant r$ and $1\leqslant k\leqslant K$}.

Since the first
r
columns of
C
are linearly independent one has that there exists vectors
{\mathbf{v}}_{i}
for
1\leqslant i\leqslant r
which satisfy

{\alpha}_{i,k}={\mathbf{v}}_{i}\cdot({\beta}_{1,k},\ldots,{\beta}_{r,k}),

where
[{\mathbf{v}}_{1},\ldots,{\mathbf{v}}_{r}]=\left([{\mathbf{c}}_{1},\ldots,{\mathbf{c}}_{r}]^{T}\right)^{-1}
. It is important to note here that the elements of
[{\mathbf{v}}_{1},\ldots,{\mathbf{v}}_{r}]
are rational. Let
L
be an integer such that
L[{\mathbf{v}}_{1},\ldots,{\mathbf{v}}_{r}]
is an integer matrix, we then have that

\|Lq{\alpha}_{i,k}\|\leqslant\sum_{1\leqslant j\leqslant r}\|Lv_{i,j}q{\beta}_{j,k}\|\leq\sum_{1\leqslant j\leqslant r}|Lv_{i,j}|\|q{\beta}_{j,k}\|\ll(Q/L)^{2K\sigma}P^{-k},

where the last inequality comes from (
3.3
) and noting that
L\asymp 1
. We deduce that
L{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}\in{\mathfrak{M}}(Q/L)
for large enough
Q
but since
L
is an integer this is equivalent to
{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}\in{\mathfrak{M}}(Q)
. Thus, by the contrapositive, it must be the case that for some
1\leqslant j_{0}\leqslant r
we have the desired bound.
∎

Lemma 3.4
.

Let
s\geqslant rK(K+1)
and suppose
C\in{\mathbb{Z}}^{r\times s}
dominates

\frac{x-{\text{rem}}(s,r)}{\left\lfloor\frac{s}{r}\right\rfloor}.

Then one has the bound

\int_{[0,1)^{r\times K}}|f_{K}({\boldsymbol{\alpha}};C)|{\,{\rm d}}{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}\ll P^{s-\frac{rK(K+1)}{2}+\varepsilon}.

Proof.

By
[
9
, Lemma 1]
, we deduce that
C
contains a partitionable

r\times rK(K+1)

submatrix. Then upon utilizing the trivial bound on exponential sums we may without loss of generality suppose that

C=[C_{1},\ldots,C_{K(K+1)}],

where each
C_{i}
is a nonsingular
r\times r
matrix. Then it is enough to establish the bound

(3.4)

\int_{[0,1)^{r\times K}}|f_{K}({\boldsymbol{\alpha}};C)|{\,{\rm d}}{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}\ll P^{\frac{rK(K+1)}{2}+\varepsilon}.

Via an application of the trivial inequality

(3.5)

|a_{1}\cdots a_{n}|\leqslant|a_{1}|^{n}+\cdots+|a_{n}|^{n},

we see that

\int_{[0,1)^{r\times K}}|f_{K}({\boldsymbol{\alpha}};C)|{\,{\rm d}}{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}\ll\max_{1\leqslant l\leqslant K(K+1)}\Phi_{l}

where

\Phi_{l}=\int_{[0,1)^{r\times K}}|f_{K}({\boldsymbol{\alpha}};C_{l})|^{K(K+1)}{\,{\rm d}}{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}.

For a fixed
l
, the value of the integral is, by orthogonality, bounded above by the number of integer solutions of the system

C_{l}\left[\begin{matrix}\Delta_{k}({\mathbf{x}}_{1},{\mathbf{y}}_{1})\\
\vdots\\
\Delta_{k}({\mathbf{x}}_{r},{\mathbf{y}}_{r})\end{matrix}\right]=\bf 0,

where

\Delta_{k}({\mathbf{x}},{\mathbf{y}})=\sum_{n=1}^{K(K+1)/2}x_{n}^{k}-y^{k}_{n},

and
|{\mathbf{x}}_{j}|,|{\mathbf{y}}_{j}|\leqslant P
.
Since
C_{l}
is nonsingular this implies that

\Delta_{k}({\mathbf{x}}_{j},{\mathbf{y}}_{j})=0,\quad 1\leqslant j\leqslant r,\quad 1\leqslant i\leqslant r,\quad 1\leqslant k\leqslant K.

This is simply
J_{K(K+1)/2,K}(P)^{r}
, where

J_{s,k}(X)=\int_{[0,1)^{k}}\left|\sum_{|x|\leq X}e\left(\left(\alpha_{1}x+\cdots+\alpha_{k}x^{k}\right)\right)\right|^{2s}{\,{\rm d}}\alpha.

By the resolution of Vinogradov’s mean value theorem (see
[
1
,
13
]
) we conclude that

\max_{1\leqslant l\leqslant K(K+1)}\Phi_{l}\ll P^{\frac{rK(K+1)}{2}+\varepsilon}.

Thus we have established (
3.4
).
∎

Note that since
C
dominates the function
F(x)
from (
3.2
) then by Lemma
3.3

there exists
\sigma>0
and a column index
j_{0}\in[s]
for which

\sup_{{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}\in{\mathfrak{m}}(Q)}|f_{K}({\boldsymbol{\alpha}};{\mathbf{c}}_{j_{0}})|\ll PQ^{-\sigma}.

Then
C_{[s]\backslash\{j_{0}\}}
satisfies the conditions of Lemma
3.4
. Thus by an application of Hölder’s inequality one obtains that

\int_{{\mathfrak{m}}(Q)}f_{K}({\boldsymbol{\alpha}};C){\,{\rm d}}{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}\ll P^{s-\frac{rK(K+1)}{2}+\varepsilon}Q^{-\sigma}.

Upon setting
Q=P^{\delta}
for some
0<\delta<\frac{1}{3+2rK}
we establish via Lemma
3.1
that

(3.6)

R_{K}(P;C)={\mathfrak{S}}_{K}(P^{\delta};C)J_{K}(P^{\delta},P;C)+o\left(P^{s-\frac{rK(K+1)}{2}}\right).

3.2.
The Singular Series

We proceed by establishing the absolute convergence of the complete singular series

{\mathfrak{S}}_{K}(C):=\sum_{q=1}^{\infty}q^{-s}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}1\leqslant{\mathbf{a}}\leqslant q\\
(q,{\mathbf{a}})=1\end{subarray}}S_{K}(q,{\mathbf{a}};C).

We begin with a definition. For any
r\times s
matrix
C
we let

A_{K}(q;C)=q^{-s}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}1\leqslant{\mathbf{a}}\leqslant q\\
(q,{\mathbf{a}})=1\end{subarray}}S_{K}(q,{\mathbf{a}};C).

It is then immediately clear that one has absolute convergence of
{\mathfrak{S}}_{K}(C)
as soon as one establishes a bound of the form
A_{K}(q;C)\ll q^{-(1+\sigma)+\varepsilon}
for some
\sigma>0.
We accomplish this by making use of the property that
C
contains a partitionable
r\times rn
matrix for
n=\left\lfloor s/r\right\rfloor\geqslant K(K+1)
. In advance of the next lemma we recall the function
F(x)
defined in (
3.2
).

Lemma 3.5
.

Let
K\geqslant 2
and
C
be a
r\times s
matrix with
s>rK(K+1)
. If
C
dominates
F(x)
then there exists
\sigma>0
for which one has the bound

A_{K}(q;C)\ll q^{-1-\sigma+\varepsilon}.

Proof.

We begin by defining a function which will be useful later. Let
{\mathbf{b}}\in{\mathbb{Z}}^{K\times s}
be given, then we define

S_{K}^{*}(q,{\mathbf{b}})=\prod_{1\leqslant j\leqslant s}\sum_{1\leqslant u\leqslant q}e\left(\sum_{1\leqslant k\leqslant K}b_{k,j}u^{k}\right).

Given this definition one may note that the following holds

S_{K}(q,{\mathbf{a}};C)=S_{K}^{*}(q,{\mathbf{a}}^{T}C).

Via an application of Lemma
3.3
to the minor arcs
{\mathfrak{m}}(q)
, we see that there exists
j_{0}\in[s]
and
\sigma>0
for which

\max_{\begin{subarray}{c}1\leqslant{\mathbf{a}}\leqslant q\\
(q,{\mathbf{a}})=1\end{subarray}}|S_{K}(q,{\mathbf{a}};C_{\{j_{0}\}})|\ll q^{1-\sigma}.

Setting
J_{0}=[s]\backslash\{j_{0}\}
we obtain

(3.7)

A_{K}(q;C)\ll q^{-\sigma}A_{K}(q,C_{J_{0}}).

Then since
C_{J_{0}}
contains a partitionable
r\times rK(K+1)
matrix one may without loss of generality write

C_{J_{0}}=[C_{1},\ldots,C_{K(K+1)},E],

where each
C_{l}
is a nonsingular
r\times r
matrix and
E
is a
r\times(s-1-rK(K+1))
matrix. Via an application of Hölder’s inequality and the trivial inequality (
3.5
) there exists
1\leqslant l_{0}\leqslant K(K+1)
for which we have the asymptotic bound

\displaystyle A_{K}(q;C_{J_{0}})

\displaystyle\ll q^{-rK(K+1)}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}1\leqslant{\mathbf{a}}\leqslant q\\
(q,{\mathbf{a}})=1\end{subarray}}|S_{K}(q,{\mathbf{a}};C_{l_{0}})|^{K(K+1)}

\displaystyle=q^{-rK(K+1)}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}1\leqslant{\mathbf{a}}\leqslant q\\
(q,{\mathbf{a}})=1\end{subarray}}|S_{K}^{*}(q,{\mathbf{a}}^{T}C_{l_{0}})|^{K(K+1)}.

Since the matrix
C_{l_{0}}
is invertible one has that the condition
(q,{\mathbf{a}})=1
implies
(q,{\mathbf{a}}^{T}C_{l_{0}})=O(1)
. Additionally since the matrix
C
here is fixed one similarly has that the elements of
{\mathbf{a}}^{T}C_{l_{0}}
are
O(q)
.
Thus there exists constants
c_{0}
and
c_{1}
depending on at most
C_{J_{0}}
for which the following inequality holds

A_{K}(q;C_{J_{0}})\ll q^{-rK(K+1)}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}|{\mathbf{b}}|\leqslant c_{0}q\\
(q,{\mathbf{b}})\leqslant c_{1}\end{subarray}}|S_{K}^{*}(q,{\mathbf{b}})|^{K(K+1)},

where
{\mathbf{b}}=[{\mathbf{b}}_{1},\ldots,{\mathbf{b}}_{r}]
are
K\times r
matrices. Applying
[
12
, Theorem 7.1]
one obtains has the bound

S_{K}^{*}(q,{\mathbf{b}})\ll\left(\prod_{1\leqslant j\leqslant r}(q,{\mathbf{b}}_{j})\right)^{1/K}q^{r(1-1/K)+\varepsilon}.

Thus we conclude that

A_{K}(q;C_{J_{0}})\ll q^{-r(K+1)+\epsilon}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}|{\mathbf{b}}|\leqslant c_{0}q\\
(q,{\mathbf{b}})\leqslant c_{1}\end{subarray}}\left(\prod_{1\leqslant j\leqslant r}(q,{\mathbf{b}}_{j})\right)^{K+1}.

Since
A_{K}(q;C_{J_{0}})
is multiplicative in
q
we need only establish the bound for the case when
q
is a prime power
p^{h}
.

Given an
r
-dimensional vector
{\mathbf{e}}
of positive integers we let
\Phi(p^{h};{\mathbf{e}})
denote the quantity of
K\times r
matrices,
{\mathbf{b}}
, satisfying

(3.8)

|{\mathbf{b}}|\leqslant c_{0}p^{h}\quad\text{and}\quad(p^{h},{\mathbf{b}}_{j})=p^{e_{j}}.

Note that for any
{\mathbf{b}}
counted by
\Phi(p^{h};{\mathbf{e}})
we have that

\quad(p^{h},{\mathbf{b}})=p^{\min{\mathbf{e}}},

thus we have the equality

\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}|{\mathbf{b}}|\leqslant c_{0}p^{h}\\
(p^{h},{\mathbf{b}})\leqslant c_{1}\end{subarray}}\left(\prod_{1\leqslant j\leqslant r}(p^{h},{\mathbf{b}}_{j})\right)^{K+1}=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}0\leqslant{\mathbf{e}}\leqslant h\\
\min{{\mathbf{e}}}\leqslant\log_{p}(c_{1})\end{subarray}}p^{(K+1)\|{\mathbf{e}}\|_{1}}\Phi(p^{h};{\mathbf{e}}).

Note that for any given choice of
0\leqslant e_{j}\leqslant h
one has that there at most
O(p^{K(h-e_{j})})
valid choices for
{\mathbf{b}}_{j}
satisfying (
3.8
). One then concludes that

\Phi(p^{h};{\mathbf{e}})\ll p^{K(rh-\|{\mathbf{e}}\|_{1})}.

Hence

\displaystyle A_{K}(p^{h};C_{J_{0}})

\displaystyle\ll p^{-h(r-\varepsilon)}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}0\leqslant{\mathbf{e}}\leqslant h\\
\min{{\mathbf{e}}}\leqslant\log_{p}(c_{1})\end{subarray}}p^{\|{\mathbf{e}}\|_{1}}\ll p^{h(-r+(r-1)+\varepsilon)}=p^{-h(1-\varepsilon)}.

Thus we establish that
A_{K}(q;C_{J_{0}})\ll q^{-1+\varepsilon}
. Combining this with (
3.7
) we establish the lemma.
∎

We conclude that
{\mathfrak{S}}_{K}
converges absolutely and there exists
\sigma>0
for which one has

|{\mathfrak{S}}_{K}-{\mathfrak{S}}_{K}(Q)|\ll Q^{-\sigma+\varepsilon}.

3.3.
The Singular Integral

Here we will show that the complete singular integral

(3.9)

J_{K}(P;C):=\lim_{Q\to\infty}\int_{\begin{subarray}{c}{\boldsymbol{{\gamma}}}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{r\times K}\\
|{\boldsymbol{{\gamma}}}_{k}|\leqslant QP^{-k}\end{subarray}}I_{K}(P,{\boldsymbol{{\gamma}}};C){\,{\rm d}}{\boldsymbol{{\gamma}}}

is absolutely convergent. We begin with a slight simplification, note that via a change of variables we obtain

J_{K}(P;C)=P^{s-\frac{rK(K+1)}{2}}J_{K}(1;C).

Thus, to prove (
3.9
) converges absolutely it suffices to show that the integral

J_{K}(1;C)=\lim_{Q\to\infty}\int_{|{\boldsymbol{{\gamma}}}|\leqslant Q}I_{K}(1,{\boldsymbol{{\gamma}}};C){\,{\rm d}}{\boldsymbol{{\gamma}}},

converges absolutely.

Lemma 3.6
.

Let
C
be an
r\times s
with
s>rK(K+1)
and suppose
C
dominates

\frac{x-{\text{rem}}(s,r)}{\left\lfloor\frac{s}{r}\right\rfloor}.

Then one has that

\lim_{Q\to\infty}\int_{|{\boldsymbol{{\gamma}}}|\leqslant Q}I_{K}(1,{\boldsymbol{{\gamma}}};C){\,{\rm d}}{\boldsymbol{{\gamma}}}

converges absolutely.

Proof.

Since
C
contains a partitionable submatrix of size
r\times rK(K+1)
, we may use the trivial estimate over oscillatory integrals and suppose that
C=[C_{1},\ldots,C_{K(K+1)}]
. We begin by first defining a useful function. For any

{\boldsymbol{\xi}}=[{\boldsymbol{\xi}}_{1},\ldots,{\boldsymbol{\xi}}_{r}]\in{\mathbb{R}}^{K\times r}

we let

I_{K}^{*}({\boldsymbol{\xi}})=\prod_{1\leqslant j\leqslant r}\int_{-1}^{1}e\left(\sum_{1\leqslant k\leqslant K}\xi_{k,j}z^{k}\right){\,{\rm d}}z.

Given this definition one may note that the following holds

I_{K}(1,{\boldsymbol{{\gamma}}};C)=I_{K}^{*}({\boldsymbol{{\gamma}}}^{T}C).

Making use of (
3.5
), there exists
1\leqslant l_{0}\leqslant K(K+1)
such that

\displaystyle\int_{|{\boldsymbol{{\gamma}}}|\leqslant Q}|I_{K}(1,{\boldsymbol{{\gamma}}};C)|{\,{\rm d}}{\boldsymbol{{\gamma}}}

\displaystyle\ll\int_{|{\boldsymbol{{\gamma}}}|\leqslant Q}|I_{K}(1,{\boldsymbol{{\gamma}}};C_{l_{0}})|^{K(K+1)}{\,{\rm d}}{\boldsymbol{{\gamma}}}

\displaystyle=\int_{|{\boldsymbol{{\gamma}}}|\leqslant Q}|I_{K}^{*}({\boldsymbol{{\gamma}}}^{T}C_{l_{0}})|^{K(K+1)}{\,{\rm d}}{\boldsymbol{{\gamma}}}.

Since
C_{l_{0}}
is nonsingular we can make a nonsingular linear change of variables
{\boldsymbol{\xi}}={\boldsymbol{{\gamma}}}^{T}C_{l_{0}}
. Additionally, from the argument in
[
12
, Theorem 7.3]
, we deduce that

(3.10)

I_{K}^{*}({\boldsymbol{\xi}})\ll\prod_{1\leqslant j\leqslant r}\min\{1,|{\boldsymbol{\xi}}_{j}|^{-1/K}\}.

Hence

\displaystyle\int_{|{\boldsymbol{{\gamma}}}|\leqslant Q}|I_{K}^{*}({\boldsymbol{{\gamma}}}^{T}C_{l_{0}})|^{K(K+1)}{\,{\rm d}}{\boldsymbol{{\gamma}}}

\displaystyle\ll\int_{|{\boldsymbol{\xi}}|\leqslant Q}|I_{K}^{*}({\boldsymbol{\xi}})|^{K(K+1)}{\,{\rm d}}{\boldsymbol{\xi}}

\displaystyle\ll\prod_{1\leqslant j\leqslant r}\int_{|{\boldsymbol{\xi}}_{j}|\leqslant Q}\min\{1,|{\boldsymbol{\xi}}_{j}|^{-K-1)}\}{\,{\rm d}}{\boldsymbol{\xi}}_{j}

\displaystyle=\left(\int_{\begin{subarray}{c}{\mathbf{x}}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{K}\\
|{\mathbf{x}}|\leqslant Q\end{subarray}}\min\{1,|{\mathbf{x}}|^{-K-1}\}{\,{\rm d}}{\mathbf{x}}\right)^{r}.

Thus it is enough to show that the integral

\lim_{Q\to\infty}\int_{\begin{subarray}{c}{\mathbf{x}}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{K}\\
|{\mathbf{x}}|\leqslant Q\end{subarray}}\min\{1,|{\mathbf{x}}|^{-K-1}\}{\,{\rm d}}{\mathbf{x}}

converges absolutely. This follows immediately by elementary analysis as one sees that

\int_{\begin{subarray}{c}{\mathbf{x}}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{K}\\
|{\mathbf{x}}|\geqslant Q\end{subarray}}\min\{1,|{\mathbf{x}}|^{-K-1}\}{\,{\rm d}}{\mathbf{x}}\ll Q^{-1}.

∎

Upon combining the results of Lemma
3.5
and Lemma
3.6
with (
3.6
) we conclude that

R_{K}(P;C)=P^{s-\frac{rK(K+1)}{2}}\left(\sigma_{K}(C)+o(1)\right),

where
\sigma_{K}(C)={\mathfrak{S}}_{K}(C)J_{K}(1;C)
. Since we have shown the absolute convergence of both the singular series and singular integral it follows from the arguments of
[
4
]
that
\sigma_{K}(C)>0
whenever there exists nonsingular real and
p
-adic solutions to (
2.2
). With this, we have established Theorem
2.2
.

4.
Analyzing the
K
-multimagic square system

Let
N\geqslant 4
and recall from section
2
the quantity
M_{N,K}(P)
. We now establish the existence of a matrix

C^{\text{magic}}_{N}\in\{-1,0,1\}^{2N\times N^{2}}

for which
M_{N,K}(P)=R_{K}(P;C^{\text{magic}}_{N})
. Note that a matrix
{\mathbf{Z}}=(z_{i,j})_{\begin{subarray}{c}1\leqslant i,j\leqslant N\end{subarray}}
is a
MMS
(K,N)
if and only if for all
1\leqslant k\leqslant K
it satisfies the simultaneous conditions

(4.1)

\sum_{1\leqslant i\leqslant N}z_{i,j}^{k}=\sum_{1\leqslant i\leqslant N}z_{i,i}^{k}\quad\text{ for }\quad 1\leqslant j\leqslant N,

(4.2)

\sum_{1\leqslant j\leqslant N}z_{i,j}^{k}=\sum_{1\leqslant j\leqslant N}z_{j,N-j+1}^{k}\quad\text{ for }\quad 1\leqslant i\leqslant N.

One may wonder if these equations are equivalent to those of a
MMS
(K,N)
, indeed it does not seem clear that the main diagonal and anti-diagonal are equal at first glance. One can show that this is implied by the above by simply summing over all
j
in (
4.1
) and noting that this is equal to summing over all
i
in (
4.2
). Upon dividing out a factor of
N
one deduces that (
4.1
) and (
4.2
) imply

\sum_{1\leqslant i\leqslant N}z_{i,i}^{k}=\sum_{1\leqslant j\leqslant N}z_{j,N-j+1}^{k}.

Before we construct a matrix corresponding to this system we must first establish some notational shorthand. Let
{\bf 1}_{n}
, or respectively
{\bf 0}_{n}
, denote a
n
-dimensional vector of all ones, or respectively all zeros. Let
{\mathbf{e}}_{n}(m)
denote the
m
th standard basis vector of dimension
n
. For a fixed
N
we define

D_{1}(N)=\{(i,j)\in[N]^{2}:i=j\},

and

D_{2}(N)=\{(i,j)\in[N]^{2}:i+j=N+1\}.

For each
(i,j)\in[N]^{2}
we define the
2N
-dimensional vectors

{\mathbf{d}}_{i,j}=\begin{cases}({\mathbf{e}}_{N}(i)-{\bf 1_{N}},{\mathbf{e}}_{N}(j)-{\bf 1_{N}})&(i,j)\in D_{1}(N)\cap D_{2}(N)\\
({\mathbf{e}}_{N}(i)-{\bf 1_{N}},{\mathbf{e}}_{N}(j))&(i,j)\in D_{1}(N)\backslash D_{2}(N)\\
({\mathbf{e}}_{N}(i),{\mathbf{e}}_{N}(j)-{\bf 1_{N}})&(i,j)\in D_{2}(N)\backslash D_{1}(N)\\
({\mathbf{e}}_{N}(i),{\mathbf{e}}_{N}(j))&\text{otherwise}\\
\end{cases}

Let
\phi:[N^{2}]\to[N]^{2}
be any fixed bijection, then the
2N\times N^{2}
matrix

C^{\text{magic}}_{N}=C^{\text{magic}}_{N}(\phi)=[{\mathbf{d}}_{\phi(1)},\ldots,{\mathbf{d}}_{\phi(N^{2})}],

corresponds to the system defined by (
4.1
) and (
4.2
) up to some arbitrary relabeling of variables defined by the bijection
\phi
. For any subset
J\subset[N]^{2}
we define
(C^{\text{magic}}_{N})_{J}:=(C^{\text{magic}}_{N})_{\phi^{-1}(J)}
in the sense defined in section
1
.

Before we move on it will be useful to define a notion of equivalence between subsets of columns of a matrix. Let
M
be a
r\times s
matrix, then we say
J_{1},J_{2}\subset[s]
are
M
-isomorphic if

{\text{im}\,}(M)_{J_{1}}={\text{im}\,}(M)_{J_{2}}.

Lemma 4.1
.

Let
N\geqslant 4
, then for any
J\subset[N]^{2}
we have that

{\text{rank}\,}(C^{\text{magic}}_{N})_{J}\geqslant\begin{cases}\min\left\{\left\lceil 2\sqrt{|J|}\right\rceil-1,2N-2\right\}+E_{N}(J)&\text{if }|J|\neq(N-1)^{2}+1,\\
2N-3+E_{N}(J)&\text{if }|J|=(N-1)^{2}+1,\end{cases}

where

E_{N}(J)=\dim\left({\text{im}\,}(C^{\text{magic}}_{N})_{J}\cap{\text{im}\,}\left[\begin{matrix}{\bf 1}_{N}&{\bf 0}_{N}\\
{\bf 0}_{N}&{\bf 1}_{N}\end{matrix}\right]\right).

Proof.

Let

B=\left[\begin{matrix}{\bf 1}_{N}&{\bf 0}_{N}\\
{\bf 0}_{N}&{\bf 1}_{N}\end{matrix}\right],

then by elementary linear algebra, one has the equality

{\text{rank}\,}(C^{\text{magic}}_{N})_{J}+{\text{rank}\,}B={\text{rank}\,}[(C^{\text{magic}}_{N})_{J},B]+\dim\left({\text{im}\,}(C^{\text{magic}}_{N})_{J}\cap{\text{im}\,}B\right).

Thus we deduce

{\text{rank}\,}(C^{\text{magic}}_{N})_{J}={\text{rank}\,}[(C^{\text{magic}}_{N})_{J},B]-2+E_{N}(J).

Thus it suffices to understand the rank of the matrix
[(C^{\text{magic}}_{N})_{J},B]
given
J\subset[N]^{2}
. Via rank preserving elementary column operations we have that

{\text{im}\,}[(C^{\text{magic}}_{N})_{J},B]={\text{im}\,}[A_{J},B],

where

A_{J}=\left[\begin{matrix}{\mathbf{e}}_{N}(i)\\
{\mathbf{e}}_{N}(j)\end{matrix}\right]_{(i,j)\in J}.

This simplification motivates our choice of
B
. Then via the same elementary linear algebra identity used above we further see that

{\text{rank}\,}(C^{\text{magic}}_{N})_{J}={\text{rank}\,}A_{J}-\dim({\text{im}\,}A_{J}\cap{\text{im}\,}B)+E_{N}(J).

Here, an important idea that must be considered is that of
projections
,
equivalent sets
, and
irreducible
sets . Given
J\subset[N]^{2}
we define

\pi_{1}(J)=\{i:(i,j)\in J\}\quad\text{and}\quad\pi_{2}(J)=\{j:(i,j)\in J\}.

We call two sets
J_{1},J_{2}
equivalent if there exists bijections
\phi_{1},\phi_{2}:[N]\to[N]
such that either

J_{1}=\{(\phi_{1}(i),\phi_{2}(j)):(i,j)\in J_{2}\},

or

J_{1}=\{(\phi_{1}(j),\phi_{2}(i)):(i,j)\in J_{2}\}.

Clearly if two sets
J_{1},J_{2}
are equivalent then
{\text{rank}\,}A_{J_{1}}={\text{rank}\,}A_{J_{2}}
. We call a set
J\subset[N]^{2}
irreducible if whenever
J=A\cup B
, where
A
and
B
are nonempty, one either has
\pi_{1}(A)\cap\pi_{1}(B)\neq\emptyset
or
\pi_{2}(A)\cap\pi_{2}(B)\neq\emptyset
. Given this definition we see that if
J_{1}
is irreducible and
J_{2}
is equivalent to
J_{1}
then
J_{2}
is irreducible. It is also not hard to see that if
J,J^{\prime}\subset[N]^{2}
are sets satisfying

\pi_{1}(J)\cap\pi_{1}(J^{\prime})=\emptyset\quad\text{and}\quad\pi_{2}(J)\cap\pi_{2}(J^{\prime})=\emptyset,

then
{\text{rank}\,}A_{J\cup J^{\prime}}={\text{rank}\,}A_{J}+{\text{rank}\,}A_{J^{\prime}}
. Thus given an arbitrary set
J\subset[N]^{2}
, there exists a unique number
l=l(J)
for which

J=\bigsqcup_{1\leqslant k\leqslant l}J_{k},

where each
J_{k}
is irreducible and satisfies

\pi_{1}(J_{i})\cap\pi_{1}(J_{j})=\emptyset\quad\text{and}\quad\pi_{2}(J_{i})\cap\pi_{2}(J_{j})=\emptyset\text{ for }i\neq j.

Thus given our above observations we deduce that

{\text{rank}\,}A_{J}=\sum_{1\leqslant k\leqslant l}{\text{rank}\,}A_{J_{k}}.

We additionally note the trivial observation that

(4.3)

\dim({\text{im}\,}A_{J}\cap{\text{im}\,}B)=1\iff\#\pi_{1}(J)=\#\pi_{2}(J)=N.

From the fundamental relation amongst the columns of
A_{[N]^{2}}
, namely

(4.4)

\left[\begin{matrix}{\mathbf{e}}_{N}(i_{1})\\
{\mathbf{e}}_{N}(j_{1})\end{matrix}\right]-\left[\begin{matrix}{\mathbf{e}}_{N}(i_{1})\\
{\mathbf{e}}_{N}(j_{2})\end{matrix}\right]+\left[\begin{matrix}{\mathbf{e}}_{N}(i_{2})\\
{\mathbf{e}}_{N}(j_{2})\end{matrix}\right]-\left[\begin{matrix}{\mathbf{e}}_{N}(i_{2})\\
{\mathbf{e}}_{N}(j_{1})\end{matrix}\right]={\bf 0}_{2N},

for irreducible
J
we have that

(4.5)

{\text{rank}\,}A_{J}=\#\pi_{1}(J)+\#\pi_{2}(J)-1.

Thus we reduce to the geometric problem of finding a lower bound for
\#\pi_{1}(J)+\#\pi_{2}(J)-1
in terms of
|J|
. Note that if
(n-1)^{2}<|J|\leqslant n(n-1)
then it is not hard to see that

\#\pi_{1}(J)+\#\pi_{2}(J)-1\geqslant 2n-2,

similarly if
n(n-1)<|J|\leqslant n^{2}
then

{\text{rank}\,}A_{J}=\#\pi_{1}(J)+\#\pi_{2}(J)-1\geqslant 2n-1.

It is not hard to verify that this implies

{\text{rank}\,}A_{J}\geqslant 2\sqrt{J}-1.

Thus we deduce

{\text{rank}\,}A_{J}\geqslant\sum_{1\leqslant k\leqslant l}2\sqrt{|J_{k}|}-1,\quad|J|=\sum_{1\leqslant k\leqslant l}|J_{k}|.

Via some optimization with the given constraints and noting that
|J_{k}|\geqslant 1
, one may establish that

{\text{rank}\,}A_{J}\geqslant 2\sqrt{|J|-l+1}+l-2\Rightarrow{\text{rank}\,}A_{J}\geqslant\left\lceil 2\sqrt{|J|-l+1}\right\rceil+l-2.

Note that for
|J|>3
and
l\geqslant 2
one has that

\left\lceil 2\sqrt{|J|}\right\rceil-1\leqslant\left\lceil 2\sqrt{|J|-l+1}\right\rceil+l-2,

with equality only when
l=2
and
|J|=(n-1)^{2}+1
or
|J|=n(n-1)+1
for some
3\leqslant n\leqslant N
. Manually checking the cases when
1\leqslant|J|\leqslant 3
we deduce the bound

{\text{rank}\,}A_{J}\geqslant\left\lceil 2\sqrt{|J|}\right\rceil-1\quad\text{ for all }J\subset[N]^{2}.

Given this lower bound on the rank of
A_{J}
it is now worth determining the sets
J
which achieve this optimal lowest rank, luckily, from the information we have derived thus far this is not a difficult task. From now on, we call a set
J

optimal
if one has that

{\text{rank}\,}A_{J}=\left\lceil 2\sqrt{|J|}\right\rceil-1.

Note that if
J
is an optimal irreducible set then from (
4.3
) and (
4.5
) one must have
{\text{rank}\,}A_{J}=2N-1
in order for
\dim({\text{im}\,}A_{J}\cap{\text{im}\,}B)=1
. This allows us to conclude that

{\text{rank}\,}A_{J}-\dim({\text{im}\,}A_{J}\cap{\text{im}\,}B)\geqslant\min\left\{\left\lceil 2\sqrt{J}\right\rceil-1,2N-2\right\},

when
J
is an optimal irreducible set.

From our above analysis there are only three cases in which an optimal set may not be irreducible, either
1\leqslant|J|\leqslant 3
,
|J|=(n-1)^{2}+1
, or
|J|=n(n-1)+1
for some
3\leqslant n\leqslant N
. We must now establish when these optimal sets may satisfy

\dim({\text{im}\,}A_{J}\cap{\text{im}\,}B)=1.

If
1\leqslant|J|\leqslant 3
, then because we are assuming
N\geqslant 4
we have from (
4.3
) that
\dim({\text{im}\,}A_{J}\cap{\text{im}\,}B)=0
. If
|J|=(n-1)^{2}+1
then it is not difficult to see that every optimal nonirreducible set of this size is equivalent to the set

[n-1]^{2}\sqcup P

where
P\in([n,N]\cap{\mathbb{Z}})^{2}
. From (
4.3
) we see that
\dim({\text{im}\,}A_{J}\cap{\text{im}\,}B)=1
if and only if
J
is equivalent to

[N-1]^{2}\sqcup\{N,N\}\Rightarrow|J|=(N-1)^{2}+1,

in this case we see that

{\text{rank}\,}A_{J}-\dim({\text{im}\,}A_{J}\cap{\text{im}\,}B)\geqslant 2N-3.

Finally, if
|J|=n(n-1)+1
then from the above one has
\dim({\text{im}\,}A_{J}\cap{\text{im}\,}B)=1
if and only if
J
is equivalent to

[N]\times[N-1]\cup\{1,N\},

which is irreducible, thus
J
must be irreducible in this case and our previous bound on the rank holds. We end by noting that because
0\leqslant\dim({\text{im}\,}A_{J}\cap{\text{im}\,}B)\leqslant 1
, the lower bounds we have established for optimal sets hold for all sets.
∎

We now establish our final necessary result.

Lemma 4.2
.

Let
N\geqslant 4
and
J\subset[N]^{2}
. If
|J|>N(N-1)
then
E_{N}(J)=2
.

Proof.

If
N=4
then one may through brute force computation establish that for every
J\subset[4]^{2}
of size
13
one has
{\text{rank}\,}(D_{4})_{J}=8
thus
E_{4}(J)=2
. Henceforth, we will be working under the assumption that
N\geqslant 5
. We also take

\chi_{2}(N)=\begin{cases}1,&\text{when}\,2\nmid N,\\
0,&\text{when}\,2|N.\end{cases}

Let
J\subset[N]^{2}
be of size
N(N-1)+1
, and define
S=J\backslash(D_{1}(N)\cup D_{2}(N))
and set
S^{c}:=J\backslash S
. Due to the size of
J
it must be the case that
|S|\geqslant N+1+\chi_{2}(N)
. We now split into subcases depending on the rank of
(C^{\text{magic}}_{N})_{S^{c}}
.

If
{\text{rank}\,}(C^{\text{magic}}_{N})_{S^{c}}=2N-1
then
{\text{im}\,}(C^{\text{magic}}_{N})_{S^{c}}={\text{im}\,}A_{[N]^{2}}
where
A
is defined in the proof of Lemma
4.1
. It is not hard to see that every
{\mathbf{x}}\in{\text{im}\,}A_{[N]^{2}}
satisfies

{\mathbf{x}}\cdot\left[\begin{matrix}{\bf 1}_{N}\\
-{\bf 1}_{N}\end{matrix}\right]={\bf 0}_{2N}.

It is also clear to see that at least one of the elements of
S
does not lie in
D_{1}(N)\cap D_{2}(N)
, say
(i_{0},j_{0})
. Then one may check that

\left|{\mathbf{d}}_{i_{0},j_{0}}\cdot\left[\begin{matrix}{\bf 1}_{N}\\
-{\bf 1}_{N}\end{matrix}\right]\right|=N\Rightarrow{\mathbf{d}}_{i_{0},j_{0}}\notin{\text{im}\,}A_{[N]^{2}},

whence
{\text{rank}\,}(C^{\text{magic}}_{N})_{J}>{\text{rank}\,}(C^{\text{magic}}_{N})_{S^{c}}=2N-1
and trivially
E_{N}(J)=2
.

Suppose
{\text{rank}\,}(C^{\text{magic}}_{N})_{S^{c}}=2N-2
, then by our previous analysis on the matrix
A
it follows that
S^{c}
is without loss of generality
C^{\text{magic}}_{N}
-isomorphic to the set

\overline{S^{c}}=\{(i,j)\in[N]^{2}\backslash(D_{1}(N)\cup D_{2}(N)):i\neq N\},

thus
|S^{c}|\leqslant N^{2}-3N+2+\chi_{2}(N)
, whence
|S|\geqslant 2N-1-\chi_{2}(N)
.

If
S
contains one element in
D_{1}(N)
, say
(i_{1},j_{1})
, and another in
D_{2}(N)
, say
(i_{2},j_{2})
neither of which belonging to row
N
then we are done. This is because there exists
1\leqslant k\leqslant N
and
l\neq N
such that

{\mathbf{d}}_{i_{1},j_{1}}-\left[\begin{matrix}e_{N}(i_{2})\\
e_{N}(k)\end{matrix}\right]+\left[\begin{matrix}e_{N}(l)\\
e_{N}(k)\end{matrix}\right]-\left[\begin{matrix}e_{N}(l)\\
e_{N}(j_{0})\end{matrix}\right]=-\left[\begin{matrix}{\bf 1}_{N}\\
{\bf 0}_{N}\end{matrix}\right].

Because the 3 latter vectors in the left hand side are in
{\text{im}\,}(C^{\text{magic}}_{N})_{S^{c}}
we obtain

\left[\begin{matrix}{\bf 1}_{N}\\
{\bf 0}_{N}\end{matrix}\right]\in{\text{im}\,}(C^{\text{magic}}_{N})_{J}.

This exact same trick may be done with
(i_{2},j_{2})
to obtain

\left[\begin{matrix}{\bf 0}_{N}\\
{\bf 1}_{N}\end{matrix}\right]\in{\text{im}\,}(C^{\text{magic}}_{N})_{J}.

Note that the above definitely happens if
2N-1-\chi_{2}(N)\geqslant N+2+\chi_{2}(N)
, which is always true for
N\geqslant 5
, hence
E_{N}(J)=2
.

We now consider the case in which
{\text{rank}\,}(C^{\text{magic}}_{N})_{S^{c}}\leqslant 2N-3
. By our previous analysis on the matrix
A
we see that the largest set
S^{c}\subset[N]^{2}\backslash(D_{1}(N)\cup D_{2}(N))
for which
{\text{rank}\,}(C^{\text{magic}}_{N})_{S^{c}}\leqslant 2N-3
has size at most
|S^{c}|\leqslant N^{2}-4N+5+\chi_{2}(N)
, this implies that
|S|\geqslant 3N-4-\chi_{2}(N)
. Note that since
|S|\leqslant 2N-\chi_{2}(N)
this is impossible for
N\geqslant 5
.
∎

Combining Lemma
4.1
and Lemma
4.2
and noting that the for any
1\leqslant m<N^{2}
one must have that

0\leqslant\min_{|J|=m+1}{\text{rank}\,}(C^{\text{magic}}_{N})_{J}-\min_{|J|=m}{\text{rank}\,}(C^{\text{magic}}_{N})_{J}\leqslant 1,

we conclude that for
|J|\neq(N-1)^{2}+1
one has

{\text{rank}\,}(C^{\text{magic}}_{N})_{J}\geqslant\begin{cases}\left\lceil 2\sqrt{|J|}\right\rceil-1&1\leqslant|J|\leqslant N(N-1)-1,\\
|J|-N^{2}+3N-1&N(N-1)-1\leqslant|J|\leqslant N(N-1)+1,\\
2N&N(N-1)+1\leqslant|J|\leqslant N^{2},\end{cases}

and

{\text{rank}\,}(C^{\text{magic}}_{N})_{J}\geqslant 2N-3,

when
|J|=(N-1)^{2}+1
.
Thus one may deduce that the matrix
C^{\text{magic}}_{N}
dominates
F(x)
from (
3.2
) and so we have established the quantitative Hasse principle for
MMS
(K,N)
for all
N>2K(K+1)
. We now go further and focus on establishing the existence of nonsingular local solutions.

5.
Existence of nonsingular local
K
-multimagic squares

We begin by introducing some notation, for any
s
-dimensional vector
{\boldsymbol{\xi}}
, we define
\text{diag}({\boldsymbol{\xi}})
to be the
s\times s
diagonal matrix which has the elements of
{\boldsymbol{\xi}}
as its diagonal entries.

Let us now consider the Jacobian matrix associated to the equations (
2.2
) defined by the matrix
C
evaluated at
{\mathbf{x}}
,

{\mathbf{J}}({\mathbf{x}};C)=\left[\begin{matrix}{\mathbf{J}}_{1}({\mathbf{x}},C)\\
\vdots\\
{\mathbf{J}}_{K}({\mathbf{x}},C)\end{matrix}\right],\text{where}\quad{\mathbf{J}}_{k}=kC\text{diag}({\mathbf{x}})^{k-1}.

If we replace
C
with
C_{J}
for any
J\subset[s]
, then one certainly has that

{\text{rank}\,}\left({\mathbf{J}}({\mathbf{x}};C)\right)\geqslant{\text{rank}\,}\left({\mathbf{J}}({\mathbf{x}}_{J};C_{J})\right).

Thus if we wish to show the Jacobian has full rank it suffices to show that the Jacobian associated to a submatrix has full rank. This lets us reduce to the case in which the matrix
C
is a partitionable
r\times rK
matrix
C=[M_{1},\ldots,M_{K}]
. Next we define the block diagonal matrix

\tilde{C}=\left[\begin{matrix}M_{1}^{-1}&{\bf 0}&\cdots&{\bf 0}\\
{\bf 0}&M_{2}^{-1}&\cdots&{\bf 0}\\
\vdots&\vdots&\ddots&\vdots\\
{\bf 0}&{\bf 0}&\cdots&M_{K}^{-1}\end{matrix}\right].

Note that this matrix is clearly nonsingular, whence

(5.1)

\displaystyle{\text{rank}\,}\left({\mathbf{J}}({\mathbf{x}};C)\right)={\text{rank}\,}\left({\mathbf{J}}({\mathbf{x}};C)\tilde{C}\right)

This simplifies our problem because one has that

{\mathbf{J}}_{k}({\mathbf{x}};C)\tilde{C}=k\left[\text{diag}({\mathbf{x}}_{1})^{k-1},\text{diag}({\mathbf{x}}_{2})^{k-1},\ldots,\text{diag}({\mathbf{x}}_{K})^{k-1}\right],

where

{\mathbf{x}}_{l}=(x_{1+2N(l-1)},\ldots,x_{2Nl}).

Then by swapping rows and columns and utilizing (
5.1
) one may deduce that

{\text{rank}\,}\left({\mathbf{J}}({\mathbf{x}};C)\right)=\sum_{1\leqslant j\leqslant 2N}{\text{rank}\,}V^{\prime}(x_{j},x_{j+2N},\ldots,x_{j+2N(K-1)}),

where

V^{\prime}({\mathbf{y}})=\left[\begin{matrix}1&1&\cdots&1\\
2y_{1}&2y_{2}&\cdots&2y_{K}\\
\vdots&\vdots&\ddots&\vdots\\
Ky_{1}^{K-1}&Ky_{2}^{K-1}&\cdots&Ky_{K}^{K-1}\end{matrix}\right].

Comparing this matrix to a Vandermonde matrix it is not too hard to see

\det(V^{\prime}({\mathbf{y}}))=K!\prod_{1\leqslant i<j\leqslant K}(y_{i}-y_{j}).

Thus we conclude that the Jacobian
{\mathbf{J}}({\mathbf{x}};C)
, defined by
C
evaluated at
{\mathbf{x}}
has full rank if there exists
K
many disjoint ordered subsets

J_{l}=\{j(l,1),\ldots,j(l,r)\}\subset[s]

for
1\leqslant l\leqslant K
which satisfy

{\text{rank}\,}C_{J_{l}}=r\text{ for all }1\leqslant l\leqslant K

and

x_{j(l_{1},n)}\neq x_{j(l_{2},n)}\text{ for all }1\leqslant l_{1},l_{2}\leqslant K\text{ and }1\leqslant n\leqslant r.

By
[
9
, Lemma 1]
and Lemma
4.1
one has that
C^{\text{magic}}_{N}
contains a
2N\times 2NK
partitionable submatrix because
K\leqslant\left\lfloor N/2\right\rfloor
. Suppose
{\mathbf{Z}}=(z_{i,j})_{1\leqslant i,j\leqslant N}
is a
MMS
(K,N)
, hence it satisfies

\left[{\mathbf{d}}_{\phi(1)},\ldots,{\mathbf{d}}_{\phi(N^{2})}\right]\left[\begin{matrix}z_{\phi(1)}\\
\vdots\\
z_{\phi(N^{2})}\end{matrix}\right]={\bf 0}_{N^{2}},

where
\phi
is any bijection from
[N^{2}]
to
[N]^{2}
. Thus if we establish the existence of a bijection
\phi:[N^{2}]\to[N]^{2}
for which one has

(5.2)

{\text{rank}\,}\left[{\mathbf{d}}_{\phi(1+2N(l-1))},{\mathbf{d}}_{\phi(2+2N(l-1))},\ldots,{\mathbf{d}}_{\phi(2Nl)}\right]=2N\quad\text{for all}\quad 1\leqslant l\leqslant K,

and

(5.3)

z_{\phi(n+2N(l_{1}-1))}\neq z_{\phi(n+2N(l_{2}-1))}\text{ for all }1\leqslant l_{1},l_{2}\leqslant K\text{ and }1\leqslant n\leqslant 2N,

then by the above work we may conclude that the Jacobian associated to the
K
-multimagic square system evaluated at
{\mathbf{Z}}
has full rank.

First we must fix suitable disjoint subsets
J_{l}(N)\subset[N]^{2}
of size
2N
for
1\leqslant l\leqslant\left\lfloor N/2\right\rfloor
satisfying

{\text{rank}\,}\left[{\mathbf{d}}_{i,j}\right]_{(i,j)\in J_{l}(N)}=2N.

The explicit definitions of these partitions may not seem straightforward but we will also provide a figure to hopefully illuminate what these partitions look like. Given a fixed
N\geqslant 4
we first define our partition in the case where
N
is even. For every
1\leqslant l\leqslant\left\lfloor N/2\right\rfloor
let

J_{l}(N)=J^{(1)}_{l}(N)\sqcup J^{(2)}_{l}(N),

instead of writing the definition of these sets
J^{(1)}_{l}(N)
and
J^{(2)}_{l}(N)
explicitly, we will instead define conditions under which we may determine that a pair
(i,j)
belong to
J^{(1)}_{l}(N)
or
J^{(2)}_{l}(N)
. If
N
is even then

(i,j)\in J^{(1)}_{l}(N)\iff i-j\equiv 2(l-1)\,\,\text{\rm mod}\,\,N,

and

(i,j)\in J^{(2)}_{l}(N)\iff i-j\equiv 2l-1\,\,\text{\rm mod}\,\,N.

For the odd case we split into further subcases depending on parity of
\frac{N+1}{2}
. If

j\notin\left[2-{\text{rem}}\left(\frac{N+1}{2},2\right),\frac{N+3}{2}\right]

then

(i,j)\in J^{(1)}_{l}(N)\iff i-j\equiv 2(l-1)\,\,\text{\rm mod}\,\,N,

if

j\in\left[2-{\text{rem}}\left(\frac{N+1}{2},2\right),\frac{N+3}{2}\right]\quad\text{and}\quad j\equiv\frac{N+3}{2}\,\,\text{\rm mod}\,\,2

then

(i,j)\in J^{(1)}_{l}(N)\iff i-j+1\equiv 2(l-1)\,\,\text{\rm mod}\,\,N,

and finally if

j\in\left[2-{\text{rem}}\left(\frac{N+1}{2},2\right),\frac{N+3}{2}\right]\quad\text{and}\quad j\equiv\frac{N+1}{2}\,\,\text{\rm mod}\,\,2

then

(i,j)\in J^{(1)}_{l}(N)\iff i-j-1\equiv 2(l-1)\,\,\text{\rm mod}\,\,N.

We define
J^{(2)}_{l}(N)
for the odd case in a similar way. If

j\notin\left[2-{\text{rem}}\left(\frac{N+1}{2},2\right),\frac{N+3}{2}\right]

then

(i,j)\in J^{(2)}_{l}(N)\iff i-j\equiv 2l-1\,\,\text{\rm mod}\,\,N,

if

j\in\left[2-{\text{rem}}\left(\frac{N+1}{2},2\right),\frac{N+3}{2}\right]\quad\text{and}\quad j\equiv\frac{N+3}{2}\,\,\text{\rm mod}\,\,2

then

(i,j)\in J^{(2)}_{l}(N)\iff i-j+1\equiv 2l-1\,\,\text{\rm mod}\,\,N,

and finally if

j\in\left[2-{\text{rem}}\left(\frac{N+1}{2},2\right),\frac{N+3}{2}\right]\quad\text{and}\quad j\equiv\frac{N+1}{2}\,\,\text{\rm mod}\,\,2

then

(i,j)\in J^{(2)}_{l}(N)\iff i-j-1\equiv 2l-1\,\,\text{\rm mod}\,\,N.

We now establish that this partition satisfies the required conditions.

Lemma 5.1
.

Let
N\geqslant 4
be given, then the sets
J_{l}
defined previously for
1\leqslant l\leqslant\left\lfloor N/2\right\rfloor
satisfy

{\text{rank}\,}\left[{\mathbf{d}}_{i,j}\right]_{(i,j)\in J_{l}(N)}=2N.

Proof.

We begin by recalling the equation used in Lemma
4.1
, namely

{\text{rank}\,}(C^{\text{magic}}_{N})_{J}={\text{rank}\,}A_{J}-\dim({\text{im}\,}A_{J}\cap{\text{im}\,}B)+E_{N}(J),

where

A_{J}=\left[\begin{matrix}{\mathbf{e}}_{N}(i)\\
{\mathbf{e}}_{N}(j)\end{matrix}\right]_{(i,j)\in J},\quad B=\left[\begin{matrix}{\bf 1}_{N}&{\bf 0}_{N}\\
{\bf 0}_{N}&{\bf 1}_{N}\end{matrix}\right],

and

E_{N}(J)=\dim\left({\text{im}\,}(C^{\text{magic}}_{N})_{J}\cap{\text{im}\,}B\right).

Note that if we can show that
{\text{im}\,}A_{J_{l}(N)}={\text{im}\,}A
and
E_{N}(J_{l}(N))=2
for all
1\leqslant l\leqslant\left\lfloor N/2\right\rfloor
, then we are done because by our previous analysis this implies that

{\text{rank}\,}A-\dim({\text{im}\,}A\cap{\text{im}\,}B)=2N-2,

and hence
{\text{rank}\,}(C^{\text{magic}}_{N})_{J}=2N
.

A few important observations about these sets
J_{l}(N)
should be noted, for every
N\geqslant 4
and
1\leqslant l\leqslant\left\lfloor N/2\right\rfloor
the sets
J^{(1)}_{l}(N)
and
J^{(2)}_{l}(N)
both have size
N
and contain a single element in every row and column. Additionally
J^{(1)}_{l}(N)\cap D_{2}(N)=\emptyset
and

|J^{(2)}_{l}(N)\cap D_{2}(N)|=\begin{cases}2&2\mid N\\
1&2\nmid N\end{cases}

Note that due to the “ladder like” structure of
J_{l}(N)
we may, via applications of the fundamental relation (
4.4
), deduce that

{\text{im}\,}A_{J_{l}}={\text{im}\,}A.

All that is left to show is that
E_{N}(J_{l})=2
for all
1\leqslant l\leqslant\left\lfloor N/2\right\rfloor
. By the above observations it is not hard to see that

\left[\begin{matrix}{\bf 1}_{N}\\
{\bf 0}_{N}\end{matrix}\right]\in{\text{im}\,}\left({\text{rem}}(N,2)\sum_{(i,j)\in J^{(1)}_{l}(N)}{\mathbf{d}}_{i,j}+\sum_{(i,j)\in J^{(2)}_{l}(N)}{\mathbf{d}}_{i,j}\right)\subset{\text{im}\,}(C^{\text{magic}}_{N})_{J_{l}(N)},

and

\left[\begin{matrix}{\bf 0}_{N}\\
{\bf 1}_{N}\end{matrix}\right]\in{\text{im}\,}\left(\sum_{(i,j)\in J^{(1)}_{l}(N)}{\mathbf{d}}_{i,j},\left[\begin{matrix}{\bf 1}_{N}\\
{\bf 0}_{N}\end{matrix}\right]\right)\subset{\text{im}\,}(C^{\text{magic}}_{N})_{J_{l}}(N),

thus we establish that
E_{N}(J_{l}(N))=2
and we are done.
∎

With this partition in place we may now fix a suitable bijection
\phi:[N^{2}]\to[N]^{2}
which respects this partition. We begin by first fixing a bijection

\tilde{\psi}:J_{1}(N)\to[2N].

We now use the important property that each of our partitions
J_{l}(N)
are precisely a previous partition which has been shifted by two rows. Define

\psi:\bigsqcup_{1\leqslant l\leqslant K}J_{l}(N)\to[2NK]

piecewise via the relation

\psi(i,j)=\tilde{\psi}({\text{rem}}(i-2l+1)+1,j)+2Nl\text{ for }(i,j)\in J_{l}(N).

Finally, we take
\phi
to be any bijection from
[N^{2}]
to
[N]^{2}
which satisfies

\phi\circ\psi^{-1}=\text{Id}_{[2NK]}.

One may refer to figure
2
for an example of such a bijection. This bijection
\phi
satisfies (
5.2
), additionally we note that for any
1\leqslant n\leqslant 2N
the set

\{\phi(n),\phi(n+2N),\ldots,\phi(n+2N(K-1))\}\subset[N]^{2},

lies in a single column. Thus, if we can find a
MMS
(K,N)
, say
{\mathbf{Z}}\in{\mathbb{Z}}^{N\times N}
, which has distinct values along the columns we immediately have that (
5.3
) is satisfied and thus establish the existence of a nonsingular integer solution and hence trivially nonsingular local solutions.
