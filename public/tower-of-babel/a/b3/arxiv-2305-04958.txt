Kinetics of information scrambling in correlated metals: disorder-driven transition from shock-wave to FKPP dynamics
Camille Aron, Éric Brunet, Aditi Mitra

Abstract
Quenched disorder slows down the scrambling of quantum information. Using a bottom-up approach, we formulate a kinetic theory of scrambling in a correlated metal near a superconducting transition, following the scrambling dynamics as the impurity scattering rate is increased. Within this framework, we rigorously show that the butterfly velocity v v is bounded by the light cone velocity v lc v_{\rm lc} set by the Fermi velocity. We analytically identify a disorder-driven dynamical transition occurring at small but finite disorder strength between a spreading of information characterized at late times by a discontinuous shock wave propagating at the maximum velocity v lc v_{\rm lc} , and a smooth traveling wave belonging to the Fisher or Kolmogorov-Petrovsky-Piskunov (FKPP) class and propagating at a slower, if not considerably slower, velocity v v . In the diffusive regime, we establish the relation v 2 / λ FKPP ∼ D el v^{2}/\lambda_{\rm FKPP}\sim D_{\rm el} where λ FKPP \lambda_{\rm FKPP} is the Lyapunov exponent set by the inelastic scattering rate and D el D_{\rm el} is the elastic diffusion constant.



Information scrambling refers to the efficient spreading and loss of information throughout an extended many-body system.
Its characterization is fundamental to the foundations of quantum chaos and has implications in the development quantum computing technologies.
The picture that has emerged from the exact or numerical solutions to a variety of classical and quantum thermalizing models with local interactions is
a ballistic spreading of information at a so-called butterfly velocity.
In dual-unitary circuits, the information scrambling occurs precisely on the light rays propagating at the maximum velocity allowed by causality
Bertini et al. 2019
;
Claeys and Lamacraft 2020
.
In random quantum circuits made of Haar-distributed unitaries,
scrambling dynamics was related to classical growth processes with slower ballistic fronts that broaden either diffusively in
d=1
, or according to fluctuations governed by the Kardar-Parisi-Zhang universality class in
d=2
and
d=3

Nahum et al. 2018
;
Bertini and Piroli 2020
.

Alongside these minimal models, as well as classical
Das et al. 2018
;
Khemani et al. 2018
;
Ruidas and Banerjee 2021
;
Roy et al. 2023
, semi-classical, large
N
or holographic models
Gu et al. 2017a
;
Chowdhury and Swingle 2017
;
Shenker and Stanford 2014
;
Roberts and Swingle 2016
, it is essential to address those questions in realistic situations where exact or numerical solutions are out of reach.
Aleiner, Faoro and Ioffe articulated those questions in the larger framework of electronic transport by deriving a quantum kinetic equation for out-of-time-ordered correlators (OTOCs) within a so-called many-world Keldysh formalism
Aleiner et al. 2016
.
They argued that the scrambling dynamics in metals with either phonon or Coulomb interactions are governed by Fisher or Kolmogorov-Petrovsky-Piskunov (FKPP) equations, resulting in smooth non-broadening scrambling fronts propagating at a butterfly velocity set by the Fermi velocity,
v_{\rm F}
. Interestingly, it was argued that the presence of disorder would significantly reduce this butterfly velocity
Aleiner et al. 2016
;
Patel et al. 2017
;
Swingle and Chowdhury 2017
;
Liao and Galitski 2018
.

Building on the approach of Ref.
Aleiner et al. 2016
, we worked out the kinetics of quantum information scrambling in a paradigmatic model of clean interacting metals in the vicinity of a superconducting phase transition, where electron-electron interactions are dominated by superconducting fluctuations
Aron et al. 2023
.
We found scrambling fronts that travel at
v_{\rm F}
but do not belong to the FKPP universality class. Remarkably, their late-time spatial profiles develop a shock-wave discontinuity at the boundary of the light cone.

In this Letter, we investigate the impact of impurity scattering on the dynamics of the scrambling front. Momentum relaxation is indeed significant in metals with an elastic timescale typically in the tens of femtoseconds,
i.e.
much shorter than the scattering time due to electronic interactions, especially at low temperatures.
We first derive an effective set of two coupled partial differential equations (PDEs) governing the scrambling dynamics at large scales. Then, we analytically elucidate how the traveling-wave solutions that develop at late times are affected by the presence of disorder, from the clean case to the diffusive regime.
We find that the shock-wave phenomenology found in the clean case is robust against weak disorder. However, we unravel a dynamical phase transition that causes the scrambling kinetics to abruptly conform to the FKPP class when the disorder strength exceeds a critical value, see Fig.
1
. We fully characterize the information scrambling in the FKPP regime by working out the profiles of the traveling fronts and their butterfly velocities.

We formulate the kinetic theory of quantum information scrambling by starting from the quantum kinetic equation on the many-world distribution functions
F_{\alpha\beta}(t,{\bm{x}};\omega,{\bm{k}})
, where the indices
\alpha,\beta\in\{u,d\}

Aron et al. 2023

The intraworld components

correspond to the standard electronic distribution functions. We concentrate on the interworld components
F_{\alpha\neq\beta}
which are directly related to four-point OTOCs and to the growth of operators. In the gradient approximation, valid when the microscopic scales set by

\displaystyle[\partial_{t}+\bm{v}_{\bm{k}}\cdot\bm{\nabla}_{\!\!\bm{x}}]F_{\alpha\beta}=I_{\alpha\beta},

(2)

with the velocity
\bm{v}_{\bm{k}}:=\bm{\nabla}_{\!\!\bm{k}}\epsilon_{\bm{k}}

The kinetic theory is considerably simplified by working (i) in terms of the first two components of a partial-wave expansion in
{\bm{k}}

\omega\to\epsilon_{\bm{k}}

i.e.

\displaystyle F_{\alpha\beta}(t,{\bm{x}};\omega,{\bm{k}})\mapsto\epsilon_{\alpha\beta}\left[\phi(t,{\bm{x}})+{\bm{u}}_{\bm{k}}\cdot\bm{\phi}_{1}(t,{\bm{x}})\right],

(3)

The validity of the above ansatz is discussed for the clean case in Ref.
Aron et al. 2023

Discussion.

In the Fermi shock-wave regime,
\kappa<\kappa^{*}
, it is still to be clarified whether the scrambling front discontinuity could be smoothened by corrections to the gradient approximation.
In the FKPP regime,
\kappa>\kappa^{*}
, we found that disorder can considerably reduce the butterfly velocity, corroborating the results of Refs.
Swingle and Chowdhury 2017
;
Patel et al. 2017
: a realistic ratio at room temperatures
\kappa\approx 10^{4}
yields
v\sim 10^{-2}v_{\rm F}\sim 10^{4}
m/s, on par with the typical phonon-mediated sound velocity in metals.
Contrary to the shock-wave velocity, it cannot strictly be interpreted a (slower) effective speed of light since the small tail ahead of the front, while providing a quantum-chaotic exponential regime controlled by the inelastic scattering rate,
surreptitiously undermines the causality of the light-cone structure.
In both the Fermi shock-wave and the FKPP regimes, we found a sharp butterfly front. This is similar to what has been reported for models with a large local Hibert space such as the
O(N)
or the Sachdev-Ye-Kitaev (SYK) models
Chowdhury and Swingle 2017
;
Gu et al. 2017a
, but different from the diffusively broadening fronts obtained for random quantum circuits
Nahum et al. 2018
;
Bertini and Piroli 2020
. The precise conditions under which strong quantum fluctuations and strong disorder could generate relevant perturbations to the FKPP dynamics are still to be elucidated.

In this Appendix, we derive the set of kinetic equations (4) that govern the dynamics of quantum information scrambling in the disordered interacting metal given by the Hamiltonian in Eq. (1).
More information on the procedure for the clean case can be found in Ref.
Aron et al. 2023
.

A.1
Many-world formalism

We work within the many-world formalism, sometimes referred to as the augmented Keldysh formalism. We refer the reader to Ref.
Aleiner et al. 2016
for a pedagogical introduction, see also Ref.
Kamenev 2023
.
Two-point correlators are defined as

\displaystyle{\rm i}G_{\alpha\beta}^{ab}(t,{\bm{x}};t^{\prime},{\bm{x}}^{\prime})

\displaystyle:=\langle\mathrm{T}_{\mathcal{C}}\psi_{\alpha}^{a}(t,{\bm{x}})\psi_{\beta}^{b\dagger}(t^{\prime},{\bm{x}}^{\prime})\rangle,

(21)

with the Keldysh indices
a,b=+,-
, and the world indices
\alpha,\beta=u,d
. We set
\hbar=1
.

\mathrm{T}_{\mathcal{C}}
is the time-ordering operator on the two-world contour
\mathcal{C}
represented in Fig.
4
. The fermionic operators
\psi
and
\psi^{\dagger}
are written in the Heisenberg picture, and
\langle\ldots\rangle:=\mathrm{Tr}\left[\ldots\rho_{0}\right]
where
\rho_{0}
is the initial density matrix with
\mathrm{Tr}\,\rho_{0}=1
.
The system is initially prepared in equilibrium at the temperature
T
with the Gibbs state
\rho_{0}\sim{\rm e}^{-H/T}
where we set
k_{\rm B}=1
.
The equilibrium dynamics ensure that, in the absence of an external perturbation explicitly breaking this invariance, the intraworld dynamics is translational invariant in space and time, and one may work in Fourier space.
This is not the case for interworld quantities (
\alpha\neq\beta
) since we are precisely probing their response with respect to local perturbations. Therefore, we use the Wigner representation for the interworld components,
e.g.

\displaystyle G^{ab}_{\alpha\beta}(\omega,{\bm{k}};t,{\bm{x}}):=\int\!\!{\rm d}{\bm{x}}^{\prime}\!\!\int\!\!{\rm d}t^{\prime}\,{\rm e}^{{\rm i}(\omega t^{\prime}-{\bm{k}}\cdot{\bm{x}}^{\prime})}\,G^{ab}_{\alpha\beta}\left(t+\frac{t^{\prime}}{2},{\bm{x}}+\frac{{\bm{x}}^{\prime}}{2};t-\frac{t^{\prime}}{2},{\bm{x}}-\frac{{\bm{x}}^{\prime}}{2}\right).

(22)

To alleviate the notations, we drop the intraworld indices,
e.g.

G_{uu}=G_{dd}=G
, and we reserve the double indices
\alpha\beta
to interworld quantities,
i.e.
by
\alpha\beta
we mean
\alpha\neq\beta
specifically.
We work in the Keldysh basis with the retarded (
R
) and Keldysh (
K
) Green’s functions which, after using the properties of the contour
\mathcal{C}
, read

\displaystyle\left\{\begin{array}[]{rl}G^{R}&=G^{++}-G^{+-}\\
G^{K}&=G^{++}+G^{--}\end{array}\right.\mbox{ and }\left\{\begin{array}[]{rl}G^{R}_{\alpha\beta}&=0\\
G^{K}_{\alpha\beta}&=2G_{\alpha\beta}^{-+}.\end{array}\right.

The intraworld Schwinger-Dyson equations yield the standard (single-world) theory

\displaystyle\left\{\begin{array}[]{rl}G^{R}(\omega,{\bm{k}})&=\left[\omega-\epsilon({\bm{k}})-\Sigma^{R}(\omega,{\bm{k}})\right]^{-1}\\
G^{K}(\omega,{\bm{k}})&=|G^{R}(\omega,{\bm{k}})|^{2}\Sigma^{K}(\omega,{\bm{k}}).\end{array}\right.

The interworld Schwinger-Dyson equations read

\displaystyle\left\{\begin{array}[]{rl}G_{\alpha\beta}^{R}(\omega,{\bm{k}};t,{\bm{x}})&=0\\
G^{K}_{\alpha\beta}(\omega,{\bm{k}};t,{\bm{x}})&=G^{R}(\omega,{\bm{k}};t,{\bm{x}})\star\Sigma^{K}_{\alpha\beta}(\omega,{\bm{k}};t,{\bm{x}})\star G^{R}(\omega,{\bm{k}};t,{\bm{x}})^{*},\end{array}\right.

where we introduced the Moyal product
\star:=\exp\left[\frac{{\rm i}}{2}(\overleftarrow{\partial_{\omega}}\overrightarrow{\partial_{t}}\!-\!\overleftarrow{\bm{\nabla}_{\!\!\bm{k}}}\cdot\overrightarrow{\bm{\nabla}_{\!\!\bm{x}}}\!-\!\overleftarrow{\partial_{t}}\overrightarrow{\partial_{\omega}}\!+\!\overleftarrow{\bm{\nabla}_{\!\!\bm{x}}}\cdot\overrightarrow{\bm{\nabla}_{\!\!\bm{k}}})\right]
where the left (right) arrow designates a derivative operator acting on the left (right) of the star symbol.
The self-energies (the
\Sigma
’s) entering the Schwinger-Dyson equations (
A.1
) and (
A.1
) will be discussed below in App.
A.2
.

Connection to scrambling.

Let us briefly outline the connection between the interworld two-point Green’s functions and the four-point OTOCs which are commonly used to probe the dynamical signatures of quantum chaos. An example of such an OTOC is

\displaystyle\mathcal{A}(t,{\bm{x}};t^{\prime},{\bm{x}}^{\prime}):=

\displaystyle\mathrm{Tr}\,\left[\psi^{\dagger}(0)\ {\rm e}^{{\rm i}Ht}\psi({\bm{x}}){\rm e}^{-{\rm i}Ht}\ \psi(0)\ {\rm e}^{{\rm i}Ht^{\prime}}\psi^{\dagger}({\bm{x}}^{\prime})\ {\rm e}^{-{\rm i}Ht^{\prime}}\rho_{0}\right]

(31)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\langle\mathrm{T}_{\mathcal{C}}\,\psi_{d}^{-\dagger}(0,0)\psi_{d}^{+}(t,{\bm{x}})\psi_{u}^{-}(0,0)\psi_{u}^{+\dagger}(t^{\prime},{\bm{x}}^{\prime})\rangle.

(32)

At late times, we expect the decoupling

\displaystyle\mathcal{A}(t,{\bm{x}};t^{\prime},{\bm{x}}^{\prime})\simeq n_{0}\,{\rm i}G_{du}^{++}(t,{\bm{x}};t^{\prime},{\bm{x}}^{\prime})\propto G^{K}_{du}(t,{\bm{x}};t^{\prime},{\bm{x}}^{\prime}),

(33)

with
n_{0}:=\mathrm{Tr}\,\left[\psi^{\dagger}(0)\psi(0)\rho_{0}\right]
and where the interworld Green’s function is computed as the solution of the interworld Schwinger-Dyson equations in the presence of a local perturbation to the correlated world solution at
{\bm{x}}=0
and time
t=0
.

A.2
Self-energy contributions

There are two contributions to the electronic self-energy: elastic scattering on the disordered potential and inelastic scattering on the Cooperons. We refer the reader to Chap. 8 in Ref.
Rammer 2007
, specifically around Eqs (8.180) and (8.181), for a detailed discussion of the self-energy contributions to the (intraworld) kinetic equations in a very similar setting. We follow the same approach for the interworld theory and

\displaystyle\left\{\begin{array}[]{rl}\Sigma^{R}&=\Sigma^{R}_{\rm dis}+\Sigma^{R}_{U}\\[3.0pt]
\Sigma^{K}&=\Sigma^{K}_{\rm dis}+\Sigma^{K}_{U}\end{array}\right.\mbox{ and }\left\{\begin{array}[]{rl}\Sigma^{R}_{\alpha\beta}&=\Sigma^{R}\\[3.0pt]
\Sigma^{K}_{\alpha\beta}&=\Sigma^{K}_{{\rm dis}\,\alpha\beta}+\Sigma^{K}_{U\,\alpha\beta},\end{array}\right.

where the disorder contribution
\Sigma_{\rm dis}
and the interacting contribution
\Sigma_{U}
are detailed below. It is to be noted that both contributions to the self-energy feed each other. In particular, the interacting contribution depends implicitly on the disorder strength.

Disorder contribution.

Expanding to the second order in the coupling to the disordered potential, we obtain the self-energy contribution

\displaystyle\left\{\begin{array}[]{rl}\Sigma^{R/K}_{{\rm dis}}(\omega,{\bm{k}})&=g\sum_{{\bm{k}}^{\prime}}G^{R/K}(\omega,{\bm{k}}^{\prime})\\[3.0pt]
\Sigma^{K}_{{\rm dis}\,\alpha\beta}(\omega,{\bm{k}};t,{\bm{x}})&=g\sum_{{\bm{k}}^{\prime}}G^{K}_{\alpha\beta}(\omega,{\bm{k}}^{\prime};t,{\bm{x}}),\end{array}\right.

where
g\delta(\bm{x}-{\bm{x}}^{\prime}):=\langle V({\bm{x}})V({\bm{x}}^{\prime})\rangle-\langle V({\bm{x}})\rangle^{2}
.

Interaction contribution.

We treat the finite electronic interaction in the Cooper channel by means of the random phase approximation (RPA). The intraworld self-energy contributions (
\alpha=\beta
) read

\displaystyle\left\{\begin{array}[]{rl}\,\mathrm{Im}\,\Sigma_{U}^{R}(\omega,{\bm{k}})&=\displaystyle-\frac{1}{2}\sum_{{\bm{k}}^{\prime}}\int\frac{{\rm d}\omega^{\prime}}{2\pi}\bigg\{\,\mathrm{Im}\,D^{R}(\omega^{\prime},{\bm{k}}^{\prime}){\rm i}G^{K}(\omega^{\prime}-\omega,{\bm{k}}^{\prime}-{\bm{k}})-{\rm i}D^{K}(\omega^{\prime},{\bm{k}}^{\prime})\,\mathrm{Im}\,G^{R}(\omega^{\prime}-\omega,{\bm{k}}^{\prime}-{\bm{k}})\bigg\}\\[6.0pt]
\Sigma_{\rm U}^{K}(\omega,{\bm{k}})&=2{\rm i}\,F(\omega)\,\mathrm{Im}\,\Sigma^{R}(\omega,{\bm{k}}),\end{array}\right.

where
F(\omega):=\tanh(\omega/2T)
is dictated by the (fermionic) fluctuation-dissipation theorem.
Within the RPA in the particle-particle channel, the Cooperon propagators are given by

\displaystyle\left\{\begin{array}[]{l}D^{R}(\omega,{\bm{k}})=\left[U^{-1}-\Pi^{R}(\omega,{\bm{k}})\right]^{-1}\\[6.0pt]
D^{K}(\omega,{\bm{k}})=2{\rm i}\,P(\omega)\,\mathrm{Im}\,D^{R}(\omega,{\bm{k}}),\end{array}\right.

where
P(\omega):=\coth(\omega/2T)
is dictated by the (bosonic) fluctuation-dissipation theorem and with the polarization bubbles read

\displaystyle\left\{\begin{array}[]{rl}\,\mathrm{Im}\,\Pi^{R}(\omega,{\bm{k}})&=\displaystyle\sum_{{\bm{k}}^{\prime}}\int\frac{{\rm d}\omega^{\prime}}{2\pi}{\rm i}G^{K}(\omega^{\prime},{\bm{k}}^{\prime})\,\mathrm{Im}\,G^{R}(\omega-\omega^{\prime},{\bm{k}}-{\bm{k}}^{\prime})\\[6.0pt]
\Pi^{K}(\omega,{\bm{k}})&=2{\rm i}\,P(\omega)\,\mathrm{Im}\,\Pi^{R}(\omega,{\bm{k}}),\end{array}\right.

Close to the superconducting transition, the near-critical Cooperon propagator reads

\displaystyle D^{R}(\omega,{\bm{k}})\approx\frac{-1/\rho_{\rm F}}{r-{\rm i}a{\omega}/{T}+\xi^{2}k^{2}+\ldots},

(46)

where
\rho_{\rm F}
is the density of states at the Fermi energy, and the parameter
r\propto(T-T_{\rm c})/T_{\rm c}
is the detuning from the critical temperature
T_{\rm c}
. In addition, the remaining parameters are all positive with
a\sim\mathcal{O}(1)
,
\xi^{2}\sim v_{\rm F}^{2}/T^{2}
, where
v_{\rm F}
is the Fermi velocity.
At criticality
r\to 0
, the Cooperon becomes soft with diverging length scale
l\sim 1/r^{\nu}
and timescale
\sim l^{z}
(here
\nu=1/2
,
z=2
), and the propagator is singular at
\omega=k=0
.

The interworld components read

\displaystyle\Sigma_{U\,\alpha\beta}^{K}(\omega,{\bm{k}};t,{\bm{x}})

\displaystyle=-\frac{{\rm i}}{2}\sum_{{\bm{k}}^{\prime}}\int\frac{{\rm d}\omega^{\prime}}{2\pi}D_{\alpha\beta}^{K}(\omega^{\prime},{\bm{k}}^{\prime};t,{\bm{x}})G_{\beta\alpha}^{K}(\omega^{\prime}-\omega,{\bm{k}}^{\prime}-{\bm{k}};t,{\bm{x}}),

(47)

with

\displaystyle D_{\alpha\beta}^{K}(\omega,{\bm{k}};t,{\bm{x}})

\displaystyle=D^{R}(\omega,{\bm{k}})\star\Pi_{\alpha\beta}^{K}(\omega,{\bm{k}};t,{\bm{x}})\star D^{R}(\omega,{\bm{k}})^{*},

(48)

\displaystyle\Pi_{\alpha\beta}^{K}(\omega,{\bm{k}};t,{\bm{x}})

\displaystyle=\frac{{\rm i}}{2}\sum_{{\bm{k}}^{\prime}}\int\frac{{\rm d}\omega^{\prime}}{2\pi}G_{\alpha\beta}^{K}(\omega^{\prime},{\bm{k}}^{\prime};t,{\bm{x}})G_{\alpha\beta}^{K}(\omega-\omega^{\prime},{\bm{k}}-{\bm{k}}^{\prime};t,{\bm{x}}).

(49)

A.3
Interworld kinetics

We start from the quantum kinetic equation which is a rewrite of the Schwinger-Dyson equations (
A.1
). See,
e.g.
, a standard derivation in Ref
Kamenev 2023
. The interworld Keldysh Green’s function (
\alpha\neq\beta
) is parametrized in terms of the interworld distribution function
F_{\alpha\beta}
:

\displaystyle G^{K}_{\alpha\beta}(\omega,{\bm{k}};t,{\bm{x}})=G^{R}(\omega,{\bm{k}})\star F_{\alpha\beta}(\omega,{\bm{k}};t,{\bm{x}})-F_{\alpha\beta}(\omega,{\bm{k}};t,{\bm{x}})\star G^{A}(\omega,{\bm{k}}),

(50)

To alleviate the notations, we now drop the explicit dependence of
{\bm{x}}
and
t
except when this obscures the meaning.

Gradient approximation.

Expanding the Moyal products to first non-trivial orders, the interworld quantum kinetic equation reads

\displaystyle\left[\partial_{t}+\bm{v}_{\bm{k}}\cdot\bm{\nabla}_{\!\!\bm{x}}\right]F_{\alpha\beta}(\omega,{\bm{k}})

\displaystyle=I_{\alpha\beta}(\omega,{\bm{k}}),

(51)

with the collision integral

\displaystyle I_{\alpha\beta}(\omega,{\bm{k}})

\displaystyle=2\,{\rm Im}\Sigma^{R}(\omega,{\bm{k}})\,F_{\alpha\beta}(\omega,{\bm{k}})+{\rm i}\Sigma^{K}_{\alpha\beta}(\omega,{\bm{k}}).

(52)

Using the expressions for the self-energies in App.
A.2
, we have

\displaystyle I_{\alpha\beta}(\omega,{\bm{k}})

\displaystyle=2\sum_{{\bm{k}}^{\prime}}g({\bm{k}}-{\bm{k}}^{\prime})\mathrm{Im}\,G^{R}(\omega,{\bm{k}}^{\prime})\left[F_{\alpha\beta}(\omega,{\bm{k}})-F_{\alpha\beta}(\omega,{\bm{k}}^{\prime})\right]

\displaystyle\quad+2\sum_{{\bm{k}}^{\prime}{\bm{k}}^{\prime\prime}}\int\frac{{\rm d}\omega^{\prime}}{2\pi}\frac{{\rm d}\omega^{\prime\prime}}{2\pi}|D^{R}(\omega^{\prime},{\bm{k}}^{\prime})|^{2}

\displaystyle\quad\qquad\times\,\mathrm{Im}\,G^{R}(\omega^{\prime\prime},{\bm{k}}^{\prime\prime})\,\mathrm{Im}\,G^{R}(\omega^{\prime}-\omega^{\prime\prime},{\bm{k}}^{\prime}-{\bm{k}}^{\prime\prime})\,\mathrm{Im}\,G^{R}(\omega^{\prime}-\omega,{\bm{k}}^{\prime}-{\bm{k}})

\displaystyle\quad\qquad\times\bigg\{\left[\tanh\left(\frac{\omega^{\prime\prime}}{2T}\right)+\tanh\left(\frac{\omega^{\prime}-\omega^{\prime\prime}}{2T}\right)\right]\left[\coth\left(\frac{\omega^{\prime}}{2T}\right)+\tanh\left(\frac{\omega-\omega^{\prime}}{2T}\right)\right]F_{\alpha\beta}(\omega,{\bm{k}})

\displaystyle\quad\qquad\qquad+F_{\alpha\beta}(\omega^{\prime\prime},{\bm{k}}^{\prime\prime})F_{\alpha\beta}(\omega^{\prime}-\omega^{\prime\prime},{\bm{k}}^{\prime}-{\bm{k}}^{\prime\prime})F_{\beta\alpha}(\omega^{\prime}-\omega,{\bm{k}}^{\prime}-{\bm{k}})\bigg\}.

(53)

One may check that the collision integral vanishes identically at the uncorrelated world solution

\displaystyle F^{\rm uncorr}_{\alpha\beta}(\omega,{\bm{k}})=0,

(54)

as well as at the correlated world solution

\displaystyle F^{\rm corr}_{\alpha\beta}(\omega,{\bm{k}})=\epsilon_{\alpha\beta}+\tanh\left(\omega/2T\right),

(55)

with
\epsilon_{du}=1
and
\epsilon_{ud}=-1
.

Quasi-particle approximation.

In all practical instances,
F_{\alpha\beta}(\omega,{\bm{k}})
appears multiplied by the density of states
\,\mathrm{Im}\,G^{R}(\omega,{\bm{k}})
, see
e.g.
Eq. (
53
). When quasi-particles are well defined, with a dispersion relation
\epsilon_{\bm{k}}
, the density of states is sharply peaked around
\omega=\epsilon_{\bm{k}}
and one may seamlessly exchange
F_{\alpha\beta}(\omega,{\bm{k}})
with the on-shell quasi-particle distribution function
\tilde{F}_{\alpha\beta}({\bm{k}}):=F_{\alpha\beta}(\omega=\epsilon_{\bm{k}},{\bm{k}})
. From now on, we use the tilde notation to denote the on-shell prescription
\omega=\epsilon_{\bm{k}}
.

Partial-wave ansatz.

The kinetic equation in (
51
), with its collision integral in Eq. (
53
), is considerably simplified by working with the following ansatz which implements, altogether, the truncation to the first terms of a partial-wave expansion in the momentum space, the quasi-particle approximation, and the focusing on the physics at the Fermi surface. Restoring the explicit dependence on
{\bm{x}}
and
t
, it reads

\displaystyle\tilde{F}^{\rm ansatz}_{\alpha\beta}(t,{\bm{x}};{\bm{u}}_{\bm{k}})

\displaystyle=\left[\phi(t,{\bm{x}})+{\bm{u}}_{\bm{k}}\cdot\bm{\phi}_{1}(t,{\bm{x}})\right]\,\tilde{F}_{\alpha\beta}^{\rm corr}({\bm{k}})\lvert_{k\to k_{\rm F}}

(56)

\displaystyle=\epsilon_{\alpha\beta}\left[\phi(t,{\bm{x}})+{\bm{u}}_{\bm{k}}\cdot\bm{\phi}_{1}(t,{\bm{x}})\right],

(57)

with the unit vector
{\bm{u}}_{\bm{k}}:={\bm{k}}/k
and where
\phi
and
\bm{\phi}_{1}
are real scalar and vector fields coding, respectively, for the isotropic component and the first anisotropic corrections to the interworld distribution function
\tilde{F}_{\alpha\beta}({\bm{k}})\big\lvert_{k\to k_{\rm F}}
.
Conversely, this amounts to performing a projection on the first two terms of a partial-wave expansion:

\displaystyle\left\{\begin{array}[]{rl}\phi(t,{\bm{x}})&=\displaystyle\frac{1}{S_{d-1}}\epsilon_{\alpha\beta}\int{\rm d}\Omega_{\bm{k}}F_{\alpha\beta}(t,{\bm{x}};\epsilon_{\bm{k}},{\bm{k}})\Big\lvert_{k\to k_{\rm F}}\\[6.0pt]
\bm{\phi}_{1}(t,{\bm{x}})&=\displaystyle\frac{d}{S_{d-1}}\epsilon_{\alpha\beta}\int{\rm d}\Omega_{\bm{k}}{\bm{u}}_{\bm{k}}F_{\alpha\beta}(t,{\bm{x}};\epsilon_{\bm{k}},{\bm{k}})\Big\lvert_{k\to k_{\rm F}},\end{array}\right.

where
{\rm d}\Omega_{\bm{k}}
is the elementary solid angle in the direction of
{\bm{k}}
, and
S_{d-1}:=\int{\rm d}\Omega_{\bm{k}}
is the surface area of the
d-1
-sphere with unit radius. One has
\int{\rm d}\Omega_{\bm{k}}u_{\bm{k}}^{i}u_{\bm{k}}^{j}=

\delta_{ij}\,S_{d-1}/d
.
From Eq. (
51
), we obtain

\displaystyle\left[\partial_{t}\phi+{\bm{u}}_{{\bm{k}}}\cdot\partial_{t}\bm{\phi}_{1}+v_{\rm F}\left({\bm{u}}_{\bm{k}}\cdot\bm{\nabla}_{\!\!\bm{x}}\right)\left(\phi+{\bm{u}}_{\bm{k}}\cdot\bm{\phi}_{1}\right)\right]\epsilon_{\alpha\beta}=\tilde{I}_{{\rm dis}\,\alpha\beta}({\bm{k}})\lvert_{k\to k_{\rm F}}+\tilde{I}_{U\,\alpha\beta}({\bm{k}})\lvert_{k\to k_{\rm F}},

(60)

with

\displaystyle\tilde{I}_{{\rm dis}\,\alpha\beta}({\bm{k}})

\displaystyle=g\bm{\phi}_{1}\cdot\sum_{{\bm{k}}^{\prime}}\mathrm{Im}\,G^{R}(\epsilon_{\bm{k}},{\bm{k}}^{\prime})\left[{\bm{u}}_{\bm{k}}\tilde{F}_{\alpha\beta}^{\rm corr}({\bm{k}})-{\bm{u}}_{{\bm{k}}^{\prime}}\tilde{F}_{\alpha\beta}^{\rm corr}({\bm{k}}^{\prime})\right].

(61)

At the Fermi surface,
k\to k_{\rm F}
, the term
\mathrm{Im}\,G^{R}(\epsilon_{\bm{k}},{\bm{k}}^{\prime})
selects momenta
{\bm{k}}^{\prime}
which also live at the Fermi surface.
We obtain

\displaystyle\tilde{I}_{{\rm dis}\,\alpha\beta}({\bm{k}})\lvert_{k\to k_{\rm F}}

\displaystyle=-\epsilon_{\alpha\beta}\pi g\rho_{\rm F}\,\bm{\phi}_{1}\cdot{\bm{u}}_{\bm{k}}

(62)

\displaystyle=-\epsilon_{\alpha\beta}\bm{\phi}_{1}\cdot{\bm{u}}_{\bm{k}}/\tau_{\rm el},

(63)

where we made use of
\tilde{F}_{\alpha\beta}({\bm{k}})\lvert_{k\to k_{\rm F}}=\epsilon_{\alpha\beta}
with
\epsilon_{du}=-\epsilon_{ud}=1
, and
\rho_{\rm F}
is the total density of states at the Fermi energy.
In the last line, we introduced the elastic scattering rate

\displaystyle\frac{1}{\tau_{\rm el}}:=\pi g\rho_{\rm F}.

(64)

The use of the Born approximation is legitimate far from the localization regimes,
1/{\tau_{\rm el}}\ll E_{{\rm F}}
.
On the interaction side, using the results of Ref.
Aron et al. 2023
, we have

\displaystyle\tilde{I}_{U\,\alpha\beta}({\bm{k}})\lvert_{k\to k_{\rm F}}=\epsilon_{\alpha\beta}\phi(\phi^{2}-1)/\tau_{\rm sc}+\epsilon_{\alpha\beta}(\bm{\phi}_{1}\cdot{\bm{u}}_{\bm{k}})(\gamma\phi^{2}-1)/\tau_{\rm sc}+\ldots

(65)

where the
\ldots
stand for higher-order spherical harmonics and we introduced the (temperature and disorder dependent) inelastic scattering rate

\displaystyle\frac{1}{\tau_{\rm sc}}:=-2\,\mathrm{Im}\,\tilde{\Sigma}^{R}(k_{\rm F}),

(66)

with, explicitly,

\displaystyle{\rm Im}\,\tilde{\Sigma}_{U}^{R}({\bm{k}})

\displaystyle=\sum_{{\bm{k}}^{\prime}{\bm{k}}^{\prime\prime}}\int\frac{{\rm d}\omega^{\prime}}{2\pi}\frac{{\rm d}\omega^{\prime\prime}}{2\pi}|D^{R}(\omega^{\prime},{\bm{k}}^{\prime})|^{2}\,\mathrm{Im}\,G^{R}(\omega^{\prime\prime},{\bm{k}}^{\prime\prime})\,\mathrm{Im}\,G^{R}(\omega^{\prime}-\omega^{\prime\prime},{\bm{k}}^{\prime}-{\bm{k}}^{\prime\prime})\,\mathrm{Im}\,G^{R}(\omega^{\prime}-\epsilon_{\bm{k}},{\bm{k}}^{\prime}-{\bm{k}})

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad\times\left[\tanh\left(\frac{\omega^{\prime\prime}}{2T}\right)+\tanh\left(\frac{\omega^{\prime}-\omega^{\prime\prime}}{2T}\right)\right]\left[\coth\left(\frac{\omega^{\prime}}{2T}\right)+\tanh\left(\frac{\epsilon_{\bm{k}}-\omega^{\prime}}{2T}\right)\right],

(67)

and where the parameter
0<\gamma\leq 1
quantifies the distance to criticality (
\gamma=1
at criticality).

We now project Eq. (
60
) onto the first two terms of the partial-wave expansion, using

\displaystyle\left\{\begin{array}[]{rl}\int{\rm d}\Omega_{\bm{k}}\tilde{I}_{{\rm dis}\,\alpha\beta}({\bm{k}})\lvert_{k\to k_{\rm F}}&=0\\[6.0pt]
\frac{d}{S_{d-1}}\int{\rm d}\Omega_{\bm{k}}{\bm{u}}_{\bm{k}}\tilde{I}_{{\rm dis}\,\alpha\beta}({\bm{k}})\lvert_{k\to k_{\rm F}}&=-\epsilon_{\alpha\beta}\bm{\phi}_{1}/\tau_{\rm el}\end{array}\right.\mbox{ and }\left\{\begin{array}[]{rl}\int{\rm d}\Omega_{\bm{k}}\tilde{I}_{U\,\alpha\beta}({\bm{k}})\lvert_{k\to k_{\rm F}}&=\epsilon_{\alpha\beta}\phi(\phi^{2}-1)/\tau_{\rm sc}\\[6.0pt]
\frac{d}{S_{d-1}}\int{\rm d}\Omega_{\bm{k}}{\bm{u}}_{\bm{k}}\tilde{I}_{U\,\alpha\beta}({\bm{k}})\lvert_{k\to k_{\rm F}}&=\epsilon_{\alpha\beta}\bm{\phi}_{1}(\gamma\phi^{2}-1)/\tau_{\rm sc}.\end{array}\right.

Finally, we obtain the set of coupled PDEs (4) which govern the kinetics of quantum information scrambling,

\displaystyle\left\{\begin{array}[]{rl}\partial_{t}\phi+\frac{v_{\rm F}}{d}\bm{\nabla}_{\!\!\bm{x}}\cdot\bm{\phi}_{1}&=\phi(\phi^{2}-1)/\tau_{\rm sc}\\[6.0pt]
\partial_{t}\bm{\phi}_{1}+v_{\rm F}\bm{\nabla}_{\!\!\bm{x}}\phi&=\bm{\phi}_{1}(\gamma\phi^{2}-1)/\tau_{\rm sc}-\bm{\phi}_{1}/\tau_{\rm el}.\end{array}\right.

Appendix B
Light-cone structure of scrambling dynamics

Here below, we carefully demonstrate that the solutions of the coupled PDEs (5) in
d=1
are strictly causal in the sense that they are bounded by a light cone which propagates at velocity
v_{\rm lc}=1
(
i.e.

v_{\rm lc}=v_{\rm F}/\sqrt{d}
in the original units). In other words, for any
X
and any
\tau>0
, the values of
\phi(\tau,X)
and
\phi_{1}(\tau,X)
depend only on the initial conditions
\phi_{0}(z)
and
{\phi_{1}}_{0}(z)
in the range
z\in[X-\tau,X+\tau]
. Then, with initial conditions such that
\phi_{0}(X)=1
and
{\phi_{1}}_{0}(X)=0
for all
|X|>R_{0}
, as studied in the paper, the solution strictly satisfies

\phi(\tau,X)=1\quad\text{and}\quad\phi_{1}(\tau,X)=0\quad\text{for $|X|>R_{0}+\tau$}.

(74)

Let us first recall the system of equations (5) in dimension
d=1
:

\left\{\begin{array}[]{rl}\partial_{\tau}\phi+\partial_{X}\phi_{1}&=\phi(\phi^{2}-1)\\
\partial_{\tau}\phi_{1}+\partial_{X}\phi&=\phi_{1}\left(\gamma\phi^{2}-1-\kappa\right).\end{array}\right.

(75)

where
0\leq\gamma\leq 1
and
\kappa\geq 0
.
More generally, the light cone property can be proved to hold for any system of the form

\partial_{\tau}\phi+\partial_{X}\phi_{1}=f(\phi,\phi_{1})
,

\partial_{\tau}\phi_{1}+\partial_{X}\phi=g(\phi,\phi_{1})
with
“reasonable” (say, polynomial) functions
f
and
g
, and for any bounded
initial condition. However, in order to simplify the discussion, we only
consider the specific case (
75
)
and initial conditions such that

0\leq\phi_{0}(X)\leq 1,\qquad{\phi_{1}}_{0}(X)=0.

(76)

For such an initial condition, we also show that the solution satisfies the following properties:

•

0\leq\phi(\tau,X)\leq 1
for all
X
and
\tau\geq 0
,

•

if
\phi_{0}(X)
and
\tilde{\phi}_{0}(X)
are two initial conditions satisfying
Eq. (
76
)
such that
\phi_{0}(X)\leq\tilde{\phi}_{0}(X)
for all
X
, then the corresponding solutions satisfy
\phi(\tau,X)\leq\tilde{\phi}(\tau,X)
for all
\tau>0
and all
X
.

B.1
A construction of the solutions
\phi

and
\phi_{1}

To show these properties, we rewrite
Eq. (
75
)
in terms of
\varphi:=\phi+\phi_{1}
and
\psi:=\phi-\phi_{1}
:

\begin{cases}\partial_{\tau}\varphi+\partial_{X}\varphi=\varphi(\gamma\phi^{2}-1-\kappa)+(1-\gamma)\phi^{3}+\kappa\phi\\
\partial_{\tau}\psi-\partial_{X}\psi=\psi(\gamma\phi^{2}-1-\kappa)+(1-\gamma)\phi^{3}+\kappa\phi.\end{cases}

(77)

Going respectively into the
X=z+\tau
and the
X=z-\tau
frame, this
is equivalent to

\begin{cases}\dfrac{{\rm d}}{{\rm d}\tau}\varphi(\tau,z+\tau)=\Big[\varphi(\gamma\phi^{2}-1-\kappa)+(1-\gamma)\phi^{3}+\kappa\phi\Big]_{X=z+\tau}\\[8.61108pt]

Then, this is also equivalent to the integral equations

\begin{cases}\varphi(\tau,z+\tau)=\displaystyle{\rm e}^{\int_{0}^{\tau}{\rm d}s\,[\gamma\phi^{2}(s,z+s)-1-\kappa]}\varphi_{0}(z)+\int_{0}^{\tau}{\rm d}u\,{\rm e}^{\int_{u}^{\tau}{\rm d}s\,[\gamma\phi^{2}(s,z+s)-1-\kappa]}\big[(1-\gamma)\phi(u,z+u)^{3}+\kappa\phi(u,z+u)\big]\\[8.61108pt]

(79)

where
\varphi_{0}(z)=\phi_{0}(z)+{\phi_{1}}_{0}(z)
and
\psi_{0}(z)=\phi_{0}(z)-{\phi_{1}}_{0}(z)
. Note that, from
Eq. (
76
)
,
\varphi_{0}(z)=\psi_{0}(z)=\phi_{0}(z)
, but we only need to assume that
\varphi_{0}
and
\psi_{0}
are both in
[0,1]
,
i.e.
that
\phi_{0}\pm{\phi_{1}}_{0}\in[0,1]
.

Now, define recursively
\varphi_{n}(\tau,X)
and
\psi_{n}(\tau,X)
by

\displaystyle\begin{cases}\varphi_{1}(\tau,X):=0\\
\psi_{1}(\tau,X):=0,\end{cases}

(80)

and

\begin{cases}\varphi_{n+1}(\tau,z+\tau):=\displaystyle{\rm e}^{\int_{0}^{\tau}{\rm d}s\,[\gamma\phi_{n}^{2}(s,z+s)-1-\kappa]}\varphi_{0}(z)+\int_{0}^{\tau}{\rm d}u\,{\rm e}^{\int_{u}^{\tau}{\rm d}s\,[\gamma\phi_{n}^{2}(s,z+s)-1-\kappa]}\big[(1-\gamma)\phi_{n}(u,z+u)^{3}+\kappa\phi_{n}(u,z+u)\big]\\[8.61108pt]

We shall show in

as

\varphi
and

for all

B.2
Properties of the solution

Once the convergence of
\varphi_{n}
and
\psi_{n}
is proved, we have a construction of the solution
\{\varphi,\psi\}
to the system (
77
). This construction allows us to prove all the properties we claimed:

Light cone property

We observe by recursion that
\varphi_{n}
and
\psi_{n}
satisfy the light cone property. Indeed, it is obvious for

; assume now that the property holds for
\varphi_{n}
and
\psi_{n}
, then

(and, similarly,
\phi_{n}(s,z+s)
) in the right hand side of

81
)
only depends on the initial condition in the interval
[z,2u]\in[z,2\tau]
. This implies that
\varphi_{n+1}(\tau,z+\tau)
only depends on the initial condition in the interval
[z,2\tau]
, and this is the light cone property for
\varphi_{n+1}
(and, similarly, for
\psi_{n+1}
).

Then, going to the limit
n\to\infty
, we obtain that
\varphi
and
\psi
, and then
\phi
and
\phi_{1}
, satisfy the light cone property.

Comparison property

Let us consider two initial conditions
\{\varphi_{0}(z),\psi_{0}(z)\}
and
\{\tilde{\varphi}_{0}(z),\tilde{\psi}_{0}(z)\}
, as well as
\{\varphi_{n},\psi_{n},\phi_{n}=\tfrac{1}{2}(\varphi_{n}+\psi_{n})\}
and
\{\tilde{\varphi}_{n},\tilde{\psi}_{n},\tilde{\phi}_{n}=\tfrac{1}{2}(\tilde{\varphi}_{n}+\tilde{\psi}_{n})\}
, the functions respectively obtained from these initial conditions with the recursion (
81
). If
\varphi_{0}\leq\tilde{\varphi}_{0}
and
\psi_{0}\leq\tilde{\psi}_{0}
, it is clear by recursion from
Eq. (
81
)
that, for all
n
, one has
\varphi_{n}\leq\tilde{\varphi}_{n}
,
\psi_{n}\leq\tilde{\psi}_{n}
, and then
\phi_{n}\leq\tilde{\phi}_{n}
. Note that argument makes use of
0\leq\gamma\leq 1
and
\kappa\geq 0
.

Going to the limit
n\to\infty
, we obtain that
\varphi\leq\tilde{\varphi}
,
\psi\leq\tilde{\psi}
, and then
\phi\leq\tilde{\phi}
.

Bounds on
\phi

If
\varphi_{0}\geq 0
and
\psi_{0}\geq 0
, it is immediately clear that, by recursion,
\varphi_{n}\geq 0
and
\psi_{n}\geq 0
, which of course also implies that
\phi_{n}\geq 0
. With slightly more work, it is also clear that if
\varphi_{0}\leq 1
and
\psi_{0}\leq 1
, then by recursion
\varphi_{n}\leq 1
and
\psi_{n}\leq 1
(and
\phi_{n}\leq 1
). Note that argument makes use of
0\leq\gamma\leq 1
and
\kappa\geq 0
.

Taking the limit

, we obtain that

and
0\leq\psi\leq 1
(and then

) if

and

.

Another way to obtain the same result would be to use the comparison property: observing that
\{\varphi=0,\psi=0\}
and

are two solutions of (
77
), then any initial condition “in between” must lead to a solution remaining between 0 and 1.

B.3
Convergence of
\varphi_{n}
and
\psi_{n}

Let us now explain why the series
\varphi_{n}
and
\psi_{n}
converge as
n\to\infty
. To simplify the argument, let us assume that the initial condition satisfies
0\leq\varphi_{0}\leq 1
and
0\leq\psi_{0}\leq 1
; this implies from
App. B.2
that for all
n
,
0\leq\varphi_{n}\leq 1
,
0\leq\psi_{n}\leq 1
, and therefore
0\leq\phi_{n}\leq 1
.
Let us introduce

\Delta_{n}(\tau)=\sup_{X\in\mathbb{R}}\big|\varphi_{n+1}(\tau,X)-\varphi_{n}(\tau,X)\big|+\sup_{X\in\mathbb{R}}\big|\psi_{n+1}(\tau,X)-\psi_{n}(\tau,X)\big|.

(82)

Below, we shall show that
\Delta_{n}(\tau)\to 0
sufficiently quickly as
n\to\infty
to ensure it is summable; specifically, we shall show that, for every
T>0
, one has

\sum_{n\geq 1}\sup_{\tau\in[0,T]}\Delta_{n}(\tau)<\infty.

(83)

Then, writing

\varphi_{n}(\tau,X)=\sum_{p=1}^{n-1}[\varphi_{p+1}(\tau,X)-\varphi_{p}(\tau,X)],

(84)

(remember that
\varphi_{1}(\tau,X)=0
), we see that the series is convergent, as the running term is bounded by
\Delta_{p}(\tau)
. This proves that
\varphi_{n}
(and, similarly,
\psi_{n}
) converges uniformly over all
X
and all
\tau\in[0,T]
to a limiting
\varphi
, as required.

To prove
Eq. (
83
)
, we use
Eq. (
81
)
to bound
\varphi_{n+2}-\varphi_{n+1}
and
\psi_{n+2}-\psi_{n+1}
in terms of
\phi_{n+1}-\phi_{n}
. To do so, we use extensively
0\leq\gamma\leq 1
,
\kappa\geq 0
and
0\leq\phi_{n}\leq 1
. In particular, one has
\gamma\phi_{n}^{2}-1-\kappa\leq 0
.

The mean-value theorem gives the bounds

\big|{\rm e}^{X_{n+1}}-{\rm e}^{X_{n}}\big|\leq|X_{n+1}-X_{n}|\quad\text{if
$X\leq 0$},\qquad|Y_{n+1}^{\alpha}-Y_{n}^{\alpha}|\leq\alpha|Y_{n+1}-Y_{n}|\quad\text{if $|Y|\leq 1$}.

(85)

Furthermore, as can be seen from writing the left-hand side as

\big|({\rm e}^{X_{n+1}}-{\rm e}^{X_{n}})Y_{n+1}+{\rm e}^{X_{n}}(Y_{n+1}-Y_{n})\big|
,

\big|{\rm e}^{X_{n+1}}Y_{n+1}-{\rm e}^{X_{n}}Y_{n}\big|\leq|X_{n+1}-X_{n}|+|Y_{n+1}-Y_{n}|\quad\text{if $X\leq 0$ and $|Y|\leq 1$.}

(86)

Then, we write
\varphi_{n+2}-\varphi_{n+1}
using
Eq. (
81
)
and bound the different terms arising in this difference using the relations (
85
) and (
86
). In practice, we need (for
\alpha=0,1,3
)

\displaystyle\left|{\rm e}^{\int_{u}^{\tau}{\rm d}s[\gamma\phi_{n+1}(s,z+s)^{2}-1-\kappa]}\phi_{n+1}(u,z+u)^{\alpha}-{\rm e}^{\int_{u}^{\tau}{\rm d}s[\gamma\phi_{n}(s,z+s)^{2}-1-\kappa]}\phi_{n}(u,z+u)^{\alpha}\right|

(87)

\displaystyle\leq\int_{u}^{\tau}{\rm d}s\,\gamma\big|\phi_{n+1}(s,z+s)^{2}-\phi_{n}(s,z+s)^{2}\big|+\big|\phi_{n+1}(u,z+u)^{\alpha}-\phi_{n}(u,z+u)^{\alpha}\big|

\displaystyle\leq 2\gamma\int_{u}^{\tau}{\rm d}s\,\big|\phi_{n+1}(s,z+s)-\phi_{n}(s,z+s)\big|+\alpha\big|\phi_{n+1}(u,z+u)-\phi_{n}(u,z+u)\big|

\displaystyle\leq\gamma\int_{u}^{\tau}{\rm d}s\,\Delta_{n}(s)+\tfrac{\alpha}{2}\Delta_{n}(u).

In the last line, we used
|\phi_{n+1}-\phi_{n}|=\frac{1}{2}|\varphi_{n+1}+\psi_{n+1}-\varphi_{n}-\psi_{n}|\leq\frac{1}{2}\Delta_{n}
. Then, from
Eq. (
81
)
,

\displaystyle|\varphi_{n+2}(\tau,z+\tau)-\varphi_{n+1}(\tau,z+\tau)|\leq\varphi_{0}(z)\gamma\int_{0}^{\tau}{\rm d}s\,\Delta_{n}(s)

(88)

\displaystyle+\int_{0}^{\tau}{\rm d}u\,\bigg[(1-\gamma)\Big(\gamma\int_{u}^{\tau}{\rm d}s\,\Delta_{n}(s)+\tfrac{3}{2}\Delta_{n}(u)\Big)+\kappa\Big(\gamma\int_{u}^{\tau}{\rm d}s\,\Delta_{n}(s)+\tfrac{1}{2}\Delta_{n}(u)\Big)\bigg].

We already used that
\varphi_{0}(z)\geq 0
. Recalling that
\varphi_{0}(z)\leq 1
, we obtain

|\varphi_{n+2}(\tau,z+\tau)-\varphi_{n+1}(\tau,z+\tau)|\leq\tfrac{a}{2}\int_{0}^{\tau}{\rm d}s\,\Delta_{n}(s)+\tfrac{b}{2}\int_{0}^{\tau}{\rm d}u\int_{u}^{\tau}{\rm d}s\,\Delta_{n}(s)=\tfrac{1}{2}\int_{0}^{\tau}{\rm d}s\,(a+bs)\Delta_{n}(s),

(89)

with
a=2\gamma+3(1-\gamma)+\kappa
and
b=2(1-\gamma+\kappa)\gamma
. The exact same bound also holds, obviously, for
\psi_{n+2}(\tau,z-\tau)-\psi_{n+1}(\tau,z-\tau)|
. Altogether, we finally obtain

\Delta_{n+1}(\tau)\leq\int_{0}^{\tau}{\rm d}s\,(a+bs)\Delta_{n}(s).

(90)

Obviously, from its definition,
\Delta_{0}(\tau)\leq 2
. Furthermore, we have the relation

\frac{\big(a\tau+\tfrac{1}{2}b\tau^{2})^{n+1}}{(n+1)!}=\int_{0}^{\tau}{\rm d}s\,(a+bs)\frac{\big(as+\tfrac{1}{2}bs^{2})^{n}}{n!}.

(91)

Indeed, as functions of
\tau
, both sides have the same derivative and the same value (zero) at
\tau=0
.
Then, by recursion, one obtains

\Delta_{n}(\tau)\leq 2\frac{\big(a\tau+\tfrac{1}{2}b\tau^{2})^{n}}{n!}.

(92)

Clearly,
Eq. (
83
)
holds, and the proof is complete.

Note that, in practice, we have shown that the system (
75
) admits a solution when the initial conditions satisfy
Eq. (
76
)
. The same methods can be used to show that the solution is unique.

Appendix C
Profile of the Fermi shock wave

In this Appendix, we compute the discontinuity of the Fermi shock wave that develops at the boundary of the light cone and we derive the implicit equation which determines the complete shock wave profile.
We work in the reference frame of the right-moving front by using

\displaystyle\left\{\begin{array}[]{rl}\displaystyle\phi_{+}(\tau,z):=\phi(\tau,z+R_{0}+\tau)&\xrightarrow[\tau\to+\infty]{}f(z)\\[8.61108pt]
\displaystyle\phi_{1+}(\tau,z):=\phi_{1}(\tau,z+R_{0}+\tau)&\xrightarrow[\tau\to+\infty]{}f_{1}(z),\end{array}\right.

where we recall that
R_{0}
is the extension of the initial condition. The light cone property implies that, at all times,
\phi_{+}(\tau,z)=1
and
\phi_{1+}(\tau,z)=0
for
z>0
. Then the limiting shapes also satisfy
f(z)=1
and
f_{1}(z)=0
for
z>0
.
From the coupled PDEs (5), one obtains the evolution equations for
\phi_{+}
and
\phi_{1+}

\displaystyle\left\{\begin{array}[]{rl}\partial_{\tau}\phi_{+}+\partial_{z}(\phi_{1+}-\phi_{+})&=\phi_{+}(\phi_{+}^{2}-1)\\[8.61108pt]
\partial_{\tau}\phi_{1+}-\partial_{z}(\phi_{1+}-\phi_{+})&=\phi_{1+}(\gamma\phi_{+}^{2}-1-\kappa).\end{array}\right.

As
\tau\to\infty
, requiring that
\partial_{\tau}\phi_{+}\to 0
and
\partial_{\tau}\phi_{1+}\to 0
leads to the relations

\displaystyle\partial_{z}(f_{1}-f)=f(f^{2}-1)=f_{1}[1+\kappa-\gamma f^{2}].

(97)

Hence,

\displaystyle f_{1}=f\frac{f^{2}-1}{1+\kappa-\gamma f^{2}},

(98)

and

\displaystyle\partial_{z}\left(f\frac{f^{2}-1}{1+\kappa-\gamma f^{2}}-f\right)=f(f^{2}-1).

(99)

Let us now introduce

L:=f(0^{-}),\qquad M:=-f_{1}(0^{-}).

(100)

From
Eq. (
98
)
, we have

\displaystyle M=L\frac{1-L^{2}}{1+\kappa-\gamma L^{2}}.

(101)

Now, integrating the first line of
Eq. (
C
)
over

yields

\partial_{\tau}\int_{-\epsilon}^{\epsilon}{\rm d}z\,\phi_{+}(\tau,z)-1-\phi_{1+}(\tau,-\epsilon)+\phi_{+}(\tau,-\epsilon)=\int_{-\epsilon}^{\epsilon}{\rm d}z\,\phi_{+}(\tau,z)[\phi_{+}(\tau,z)^{2}-1],

(102)

where we used
\phi_{+}(\tau,\epsilon)=1
and
\phi_{1+}(\tau,\epsilon)=0
. Taking
\tau\to\infty
, and then
\epsilon\to 0
leads to the relation
M+L=1
. Combining with

101
)
gives two solutions for

and
M
(assuming

\displaystyle\left\{\begin{array}[]{rl}L&=1\\[6.0pt]
M&=0\end{array}\right.\mbox{ and }\left\{\begin{array}[]{rl}L&=\frac{\sqrt{1+4\kappa^{*}(1+\kappa)}-1}{2\kappa^{*}}\\[3.0pt]
M&=1-\frac{\sqrt{1+4\kappa^{*}(1+\kappa)}-1}{2\kappa^{*}},\end{array}\right.

where we recall that
\kappa^{*}=\gamma+1
. The expression for

in the second solution is Eq. (9).

For
\kappa>\kappa^{*}
, the second solution in

C
)
leads to

which cannot be correct, see
Appendix B
.
This implies that one must have
L=1
and
M=0
: for
\kappa>\kappa^{*}
, there is no front at late times at position

. As is shown in
Appendix D
, the actual front propagates at a velocity

and has all the properties of an FKPP front.

For
\kappa<\kappa^{*}

jumps from

We can now solve

for
z<0

Appendix D
FKPP dynamics

In this Appendix, we discuss how the set of coupled PDEs (5) can exhibit solutions belonging to the FKPP class
Fisher 1937
;
Kolmogorov et al. 1937
for
\kappa>\kappa^{*}

First, in
App. D.1

D.1
Hidden FKPP equation

In this Appendix, we carefully derive Eq. (11) which results from the coupled PDEs (5). The latter read

\displaystyle\left\{\begin{array}[]{rl}\partial_{\tau}\phi+\bm{\nabla}_{\!\!\bm{X}}\cdot\bm{\phi}_{1}&=\phi(\phi^{2}-1)\\[6.0pt]
\partial_{\tau}\bm{\phi}_{1}+\bm{\nabla}_{\!\!\bm{X}}\phi&=\bm{\phi}_{1}(\gamma\phi^{2}-1)-\kappa\bm{\phi}_{1}.\end{array}\right.

Taking the time derivative of the first one and the spatial derivative of the second one, we have

\displaystyle\left\{\begin{array}[]{rl}\partial^{2}_{\tau}\phi+\bm{\nabla}_{\!\!\bm{X}}\cdot\partial_{\tau}\bm{\phi}_{1}&=(3\phi^{2}-1)\partial_{\tau}\phi\\[6.0pt]
\bm{\nabla}_{\!\!\bm{X}}\cdot\partial_{\tau}\bm{\phi}_{1}+\bm{\nabla}_{\!\!\bm{X}}^{2}\phi&=\bm{\nabla}_{\!\!\bm{X}}\cdot\bm{\phi}_{1}[\gamma\phi^{2}-1-\kappa]+2\gamma\phi\bm{\nabla}_{\!\!\bm{X}}\phi\cdot\bm{\phi}_{1}.\end{array}\right.

Using the second equation to eliminate the mixed derivative term in the first one, this yields

\displaystyle\partial^{2}_{\tau}\phi-\bm{\nabla}_{\!\!\bm{X}}^{2}\phi+\bm{\nabla}_{\!\!\bm{X}}\cdot\bm{\phi}_{1}[\gamma\phi^{2}-1-\kappa]+2\gamma\phi\bm{\nabla}_{\!\!\bm{X}}\phi\cdot\bm{\phi}_{1}=(3\phi^{2}-1)\partial_{\tau}\phi.

(113)

Now,
\bm{\nabla}_{\!\!\bm{X}}\cdot\bm{\phi}_{1}
can be expressed in terms of
\phi
using the first equation in (
D.1
), yielding

\displaystyle\partial^{2}_{\tau}\phi-\bm{\nabla}_{\!\!\bm{X}}^{2}\phi+[-\partial_{\tau}\phi+\phi(\phi^{2}-1)][\gamma\phi^{2}-1-\kappa]+2\gamma\phi\bm{\nabla}_{\!\!\bm{X}}\phi\cdot\bm{\phi}_{1}=(3\phi^{2}-1)\partial_{\tau}\phi.

(114)

We switch to the parameter
\kappa^{*}:=1+\gamma
and reorganize to only have on the
lhs
derivative terms with a constant prefactor.

\left\{\begin{array}[]{rl}\partial_{\tau}^{2}\phi+(\kappa-\kappa^{*})\partial_{\tau}\phi-\bm{\nabla}_{\!\!\bm{X}}^{2}\phi&=\phi(\phi^{2}-1)[(2+\kappa-\kappa^{*})+(1-\kappa^{*})(\phi^{2}-1)]\\
&\qquad+(2+\kappa^{*})(\phi^{2}-1)\partial_{\tau}\phi-2(\kappa^{*}-1)\phi\bm{\phi}_{1}\cdot\bm{\nabla}_{\!\!\bm{X}}\phi\\[3.0pt]
\partial_{\tau}\bm{\phi}_{1}+\bm{\nabla}_{\!\!\bm{X}}\phi&=\bm{\phi}_{1}[(\kappa^{*}-1)\phi^{2}-1-\kappa].\end{array}\right.

(115)

The derivative terms on a
rhs
cancel when
\phi\to 1
and
\bm{\phi}_{1}\to 0
.
Finally, we switch to the field
\delta\phi:=1-\phi
and obtain the following set of coupled PDEs

\displaystyle\left\{\begin{array}[]{rl}\partial_{\tau}^{2}\delta\phi+(\kappa-\kappa^{*})\partial_{\tau}\delta\phi-\bm{\nabla}_{\!\!\bm{X}}^{2}\delta\phi&=\delta\phi(1-\delta\phi)(2-\delta\phi)[(2+\kappa-\kappa^{*})-(1-\kappa^{*})\delta\phi(2-\delta\phi)]\\
&\qquad-(2+\kappa^{*})\delta\phi(2-\delta\phi)\partial_{\tau}\delta\phi-2(\kappa^{*}-1)(1-\delta\phi)\bm{\phi}_{1}\cdot\bm{\nabla}_{\!\!\bm{X}}\delta\phi\\[3.0pt]
\partial_{\tau}\bm{\phi}_{1}-\bm{\nabla}_{\!\!\bm{X}}\delta\phi&=-(2+\kappa-\kappa^{*})\bm{\phi}_{1}-(\kappa^{*}-1)\bm{\phi}_{1}\delta\phi(2-\delta\phi).\end{array}\right.

We now re-express the
rhs
of both equations above by collecting the non-linear terms under the symbols
\mathrm{NL}
and
\mathrm{NL}_{1}
.
This yields

\displaystyle\left\{\begin{array}[]{rl}\partial_{\tau}^{2}\delta\phi+(\kappa-\kappa^{*})\partial_{\tau}\delta\phi-\bm{\nabla}_{\!\!\bm{X}}^{2}\delta\phi&=2(2+\kappa-\kappa^{*})\delta\phi-\mathrm{NL}\\[3.0pt]
\partial_{\tau}\bm{\phi}_{1}-\bm{\nabla}_{\!\!\bm{X}}\delta\phi&=-(2+\kappa-\kappa^{*})\bm{\phi}_{1}-\mathrm{NL}_{1}.\end{array}\right.

The initial conditions are
\delta\phi(\tau=0,{\bm{X}})=\delta\phi_{0}(X)
,
\bm{\phi}_{1}(\tau=0,{\bm{X}})=0
, and
\partial_{\tau}\delta\phi(\tau=0,{\bm{X}})\simeq 2\delta\phi_{0}(X)
.
Notice the dependence on
\bm{\phi}_{1}
in the first equation which has not been fully eliminated but is now entering at a non-linear level. Naturally, one may check that
rhs
vanishes at the correlated- and uncorrelated-world solutions given by (
\delta\phi=\bm{\phi}_{1}=0
) and (
\delta\phi=1
,
\bm{\phi}_{1}=0
), respectively. This concludes the derivation of Eq. (11).

D.2
Properties of the FKPP solutions – Short introduction to FKKP-ology

The FKPP equation was introduced independently in 1937 by Fisher
Fisher 1937
and Kolmogorov-Petrouvsky-Piscounov
Kolmogorov et al. 1937
to describe problems in biology related to evolution. It has been the subject of many works and studies since. An extensive review was published in 2003
van Saarloos 2003
;
Brunet 2016
.
We only consider the one-dimensional
d=1
case, and focus on
\phi(\tau,X)
only, regarding
\phi_{1}
as an intermediate calculation step. We begin by giving a list of defining characteristics that an evolution equation for
\phi(\tau,X)
should satisfy in order to belong to the FKPP class. By evolution equation, here we mean the actual equation for times
\tau>0
, without the input of the specific initial condition at time
\tau=0
.

1.

The equation admits a stable uniform solution and an unstable uniform solution.

In our case,
\phi=1
is an unstable solution (also called the unstable phase), while
\phi=0
is a stable solution. (In both phases, we also need to take
\phi_{1}=0
but, as already mentioned, we focus on
\phi
only.)

2.

The equation admits traveling wave solutions for all velocities
larger or equal than a critical velocity
v^{*}
.

This means that, for all
v\geq v^{*}
, there exists functions
f_{v}(z)
with
f_{v}(\infty)=1
and
f_{v}(-\infty)=0
such that
f_{v}(X-v\tau)
is a solution. Depending on the initial condition, the front
\phi(\tau,X)
properly centered might converge to any of these traveling waves.

3.

The traveling wave solutions decay exponentially towards the unstable phase 1, with a linear prefactor for the critical traveling wave only.

This means that, there is a spatial decay rate
\mu=\mu(v)>0
and
\mu^{*}:=\mu(v^{*})
such that

1-f_{v}(z)\propto\exp({-\mu z})\text{ as }z\to\infty\text{ for }v>v^{*},\quad\text{ and }1-f_{v^{*}}(z)\simeq A\,z\exp({-{\mu^{*}}z})\text{ as
}z\to\infty.

(121)

Nota
: in the main manuscript, we wrote
\mu
and
v
instead
\mu^{*}
and
v^{*}
as there was no possible ambiguity. The traveling waves are defined up to translation: if
f_{v^{*}}(z)
is a traveling wave at velocity
v^{*}
, then so is
f_{v^{*}}(z-\delta)
. In this Appendix, we choose the particular critical traveling wave such that
f_{v^{*}}(z)=1-[A\,z+\epsilon(z)]\exp(-\mu^{*}z)
, where
\epsilon(z)\to 0
as
z\to\infty
. The quantity
A
can be measured numerically.

4.

When
\delta\phi_{0}(X):=1-\phi(0,X)
decays faster than
\exp({-\mu^{*}X})
for large
X
, then the front
\phi(\tau,X)
propagates asymptotically at velocity
v^{*}
and its shape converges to the critical traveling wave.

If we call
m_{\tau}
the position of the front at time
\tau
, this means that
m_{\tau}\sim v^{*}\tau
as
\tau\to\infty
and that

\phi(\tau,m_{\tau}+\delta+z)\to f_{v^{*}}(z)\quad\text{as $\tau\to\infty$,}

(122)

where
\delta
is a constant offset depending on the initial condition and on the precise definition of
m_{\tau}
.

5.

Still assuming that
\delta\phi_{0}(X):=1-\phi(0,X)
decays faster than
\exp({-\mu^{*}X})
for large
X
, the asymptotic position of the front is

m_{\tau}=v^{*}\tau-B\log\tau+a-\frac{E}{\sqrt{\tau}}+\mathcal{O}\Big(\frac{\log\tau}{\tau}\Big)\quad\text{as $\tau\to\infty$}.

(123)

The quantities
B
and
E
are known (see below) and independent of the initial condition.
The terms
-B\log\tau
and
-E/\sqrt{\tau}
are respectively known as the Bramson term
Bramson 1978
;
Bramson 1983
(see also Refs.
Hamel et al. 2013
;
Roberts 2013
) and the Ebert-van Saarloos term
Ebert and van
Saarloos 2000
(see also Refs.
Berestycki et al. 2016
;
Nolen et al. 2019
). The next term, of order
(\log\tau)/\tau
, has also a known coefficient
Berestycki et al. 2017
;
Berestycki et al. 2018
;
Graham 2019
. The constant offset
a
depends on the initial condition and on the precise definitions of
m_{\tau}
.

6.

Still assuming that
\delta\phi_{0}(X):=1-\phi(0,X)
decays faster than
\exp({-\mu^{*}X})
for large
X
, one has for the shape of the front:

1-\phi(\tau,m_{\tau}+\delta+z)\simeq A\,z\exp\left(-\mu^{*}z-C\frac{z^{2}}{\tau}\right)\qquad\text{as $\tau\to\infty$ and $z\propto\sqrt{\tau}$},

(124)

where
A
and
\delta
are the same as in (
121
) and (
122
).
The quantity
C
can be computed (see below).

In our problem, the initial conditions
\delta\phi_{0}(X)
are defined on a finite support of radius
R_{0}
and thus do decay faster than
\exp({-\mu^{*}X})
for large
X
. This implies that points 4., 5., and 6. above do apply. In practice, in order to identify
v^{*}
, the first step is to look for solutions satisfying

1-\phi(\tau,X)=f_{v}(X-v\tau)\propto\exp\left(-\mu(X-v\tau)\right)\quad\text{for large $z:=X-v\tau$}.

(125)

For that purpose, we can consider the equation linearized around
\phi=1
, in the unstable phase. This leads to a relation between
v
and
\mu
, which we can write
v=v(\mu)
. A necessary condition to have an FKPP equation (see point 2. above) is that
v(\mu)
reaches a minimum when
\mu=\mu^{*}
for some finite value
\mu^{*}
:

v^{*}:=\min_{\mu}v(\mu)=v(\mu^{*}).

(126)

For the problem at hand, we inject
Eq. (
125
)
into
Eq. (
D.1
)
, where we recall that
\delta\phi:=1-\phi
.
Neglecting non-linear terms, this leads to

(\mu v)^{2}+(\kappa-\kappa^{*})\mu v-\mu^{2}=2(2+\kappa-\kappa^{*}).

(127)

The solution with
v>0
is

v(\mu)=\frac{\sqrt{(4+\kappa-\kappa^{*})^{2}+4\mu^{2}}-(\kappa-\kappa^{*})}{2\mu}.

(128)

For
\kappa>\kappa^{*}
, one can see that

v(\mu)
reaches a minimum at a finite
\mu^{*}
.
Conversely, for
\kappa\leq\kappa^{*}
,
v(\mu)
is always decreasing and, thus, does not reach a minimum. This indicates that the problem cannot be FKPP if
\kappa\leq\kappa^{*}
, and that it might be FKPP if
\kappa>\kappa^{*}
.

From now on, let us assume that
\kappa>\kappa^{*}
.
A simple computation from
Eq. (
128
)
yields the selected front velocity and spatial decay rate

v^{*}=\frac{2\sqrt{4+2(\kappa-\kappa^{*})}}{4+\kappa-\kappa^{*}},\qquad\mu^{*}=\frac{4+\kappa-\kappa^{*}}{\kappa-\kappa^{*}}\sqrt{4+2(\kappa-\kappa^{*})}.

(129)

This is the solution announced in Eq. (12) of the main manuscript.
For an FKPP front, the behavior of
v(\mu)
around
\mu=\mu^{*}
controls many properties of the propagating front. In particular, the quantities
B
and
E
appearing in (
123
), and the quantity
C
appearing in (
124
) are given by

B=\frac{3}{2\mu^{*}},\qquad E=3\sqrt{\frac{2\pi}{\mu^{*5}\,v^{\prime\prime}(\mu^{*})}},\qquad C=\frac{1}{2\mu^{*}v^{\prime\prime}(\mu^{*})}.

(130)

See for instance Ref.
Bramson 1983
for
B
, and Ref.
Ebert and van
Saarloos 2000
for
E
. The above expression for
C
can be obtained with the methods developed in Ref.
Brunet and Derrida 1997
.

D.3
FKPP equation in the diffusive regime,
\kappa\gg 1
.

Here, we derive the asymptotic FKPP theory when the disorder strength is very large,
\kappa\gg 1
.
We start from the re-formulation of the coupled PDEs in
Eqs. (
115
)
which, we recall, read

\displaystyle\left\{\begin{array}[]{rl}\partial_{\tau}^{2}\phi+(\kappa-\kappa^{*})\partial_{\tau}\phi-\bm{\nabla}_{\!\!\bm{X}}^{2}\phi&=\phi(\phi^{2}-1)[(2+\kappa-\kappa^{*})+(1-\kappa^{*})(\phi^{2}-1)]\\
&\qquad+(2+\kappa^{*})(\phi^{2}-1)\partial_{\tau}\phi-2(\kappa^{*}-1)\phi\bm{\phi}_{1}\cdot\bm{\nabla}_{\!\!\bm{X}}\phi\\[3.0pt]
\partial_{\tau}\bm{\phi}_{1}+\bm{\nabla}_{\!\!\bm{X}}\phi&=\bm{\phi}_{1}[(\kappa^{*}-1)\phi^{2}-1-\kappa].\end{array}\right.

When
\kappa
is large, the exponential spatial decay rate
\mu^{*}
is of order
\sqrt{\kappa}
, see
Eq. (
129
)
. This indicates that the width of the front is small, of order
1/\sqrt{\kappa}
. This suggests rescaling space by working with

{\bm{Y}}:=\sqrt{2\kappa}{\bm{X}}.

(133)

Keeping only the terms of order
\kappa
, we see that the rescaled equation for
\phi
converges as
\kappa\to\infty
to

\partial_{\tau}\phi-2\bm{\nabla}_{\!\!\bm{Y}}^{2}\phi=\phi(\phi^{2}-1),

(134)

which is the FKPP equation.
Notice that the dependence on
\gamma
, the distance to the superconducting transition, has dropped.
A standard analysis of the above FKPP equation, following the steps in
App. D.2
, yields a front traveling at the velocity
4
on the
{\bm{Y}}
scale, and thus with a velocity of order
2\sqrt{2/\kappa}
on the
{\bm{X}}
scale, in agreement with
Eq. (
129
)
.
The radial profile of the critical traveling wave corresponding to

Eq. (
134
)
must satisfy the ODE

2\hat{f}^{\prime\prime}+4\hat{f}^{\prime}+\hat{f}(\hat{f}^{2}-1)=0.

(135)

The prediction is that the late-time shape of the front,
f_{v^{*}}(z)
defined in
Eq. (
122
)
, converges after rescaling to

f_{v^{*}}(Y/\sqrt{2\kappa})\to\hat{f}(Y)\qquad\text{as $\kappa\to\infty$}.

(136)

This is numerically confirmed in Fig. 3 of the main manuscript.
(
Nota
: the solutions to
Eq. (
135
)
are defined up to translation.)

D.4
Numerical evidence of FKPP dynamics at

\kappa>\kappa^{*}
– FKPP-ometry

Here, we present numerical simulations in
d=1
that demonstrate that
\phi(\tau,X)
behaves as an FKPP front as soon as
\kappa>\kappa^{*}
, and not only when
\kappa\gg\kappa^{*}
where we showed in
App. D.3
that the equations for
\phi
were converging to the actual FKPP equation. Specifically, we show that although the equations for
\phi
look quite different from the FKPP equation at finite
\kappa>\kappa^{*}
, the FKPP properties listed in
Eqs. (
122
)
, (
123
) and (
124
) hold. This is in addition to the numerical results presented in Fig. 1 of the main manuscript where we tested the FKPP prediction for the front velocity when
\kappa>\kappa^{*}
.

Discretization and integration schemes

Checking that the late-time
Eq. (
123
)
holds requires a very accurate prediction for the velocity of the front. However, as shown below, the discretization scheme of the equations as well as their integration scheme slightly impact the value of that velocity. This implies that the analytical prediction for the velocity must take into account the specific numerical integration scheme that is used.
Another difficulty is that FKPP fronts are extremely sensitive to the exponential tail ahead of the front, at a distance on the order of
\sqrt{\tau}
ahead of the front, where
\phi(\tau,X)
is very close to 1. This requires a numerical solver that remains very accurate in that region.
For these reasons, we found it easier to write our own solver, which we now describe.

Taking the sum and difference of the two coupled PDEs (5), we obtain

\partial_{\tau}(\phi\pm\phi_{1})\pm\partial_{X}(\phi\pm\phi_{1})=g_{\pm}(\phi,\phi_{1}).

(137)

with the functions
g_{\pm}(\phi,\phi_{1}):=\phi(\phi^{2}-1)\pm\phi_{1}[\gamma\phi^{2}-1-\kappa]
.
This is equivalent to the following set of coupled first-order ODEs,

\frac{{\rm d}}{{\rm d}\tau}\Big[(\phi\pm\phi_{1})(\tau,\pm\tau+z)\Big]=g_{\pm}\left(\phi(\tau,\pm\tau+z),\phi_{1}(\tau,\pm\tau+z)\right).

(138)

We discretize them with a simple Euler scheme, using a common discretization step
a
for both space and time. This reads

(\phi\pm\phi_{1})(\tau+a,\pm(\tau+a)+z)=(\phi\pm\phi_{1})(\tau,\pm\tau+z)+ag_{\pm}\left(\phi(\tau,\pm\tau+z),\phi_{1}(\tau,\pm\tau+z)\right),

(139)

or, writing
X=\pm(\tau+a)+z
,

(\phi\pm\phi_{1})(\tau+a,X)=(\phi\pm\phi_{1})(\tau,X\mp a)+ag_{\pm}\left(\phi(\tau,X\mp a),\phi_{1}(\tau,X\mp a)\right),

(140)

Finally, solving for
\phi
and
\phi_{1}
,

\displaystyle\hskip-5.0pt\left\{\begin{array}[]{rl}\phi(\tau+a,X)=&\frac{\phi(\tau,X-a)+\phi(\tau,X+a)}{2}+\frac{\phi_{1}(\tau,X-a)-\phi_{1}(\tau,X+a)}{2}+a\frac{g_{+}\left(\phi(\tau,X-a),\phi_{1}(\tau,X-a)\right)+g_{-}\left(\phi(\tau,X+a),\phi_{1}(\tau,X+a)\right)}{2}\\[6.0pt]
\phi_{1}(\tau+a,X)=&\frac{\phi(\tau,X-a)-\phi(\tau,X+a)}{2}+\frac{\phi_{1}(\tau,X-a)+\phi_{1}(\tau,X+a)}{2}+a\frac{g_{+}\left(\phi(\tau,X-a),\phi_{1}(\tau,X-a)\right)-g_{-}\left(\phi(\tau,X+a),\phi_{1}(\tau,X+a)\right)}{2}.\end{array}\right.

These equations allow efficient numerical computation of
\phi(na,pa)
for any
n\in\mathbb{N}
and
p\in\mathbb{Z}
as a function of the discretized initial condition
\phi(0,pa)
for
p\in\mathbb{Z}
.
Let us make a few practical remarks:

•

In this scheme, the discretization step
a
is the same for space and time. This guarantees a strict light-cone structure: if the initial condition is zero for
X>R_{0}
, then
\phi(\tau,X)=0
for
X>R_{0}+\tau
.

•

At a given time step, the values on even lattice sites only depend on the values on odd lattice sites at the previous time step, and
vice versa
. In other words, the even and odd lattice sites at a given time step are independent realizations with slightly different initial conditions. Unless the initial conditions are extremely fine-tuned, this leads to a front shape that is not smooth. To avoid this issue, we only display even lattice sites at even time steps.

•

FKPP equations are very sensitive to what happens in the region where the front is close to the unstable phase, here
\phi=1
. As the default representation of reals in a computer (we use the “long double” type) is much better at handling numbers close to 0 rather than numbers close to 1, we simulate
\delta\phi:=1-\phi
rather than
\phi
directly.

•

To save computation time and to avoid instabilities due to loss of precision for numbers too close to 0 or 1, we truncate values at each time step, setting to 0 any value of
\delta\phi
smaller than
10^{-2000}
, and setting to 1 any value larger than
1-10^{-16}
.

The discretized equations (
D.4
) lead to a front that propagates at a velocity slightly different from the one predicted by the continuous equations. In fact, we can redo the analysis of
App. D.2
, looking for a traveling wave solution with an exponential decay
1-\phi(\tau,X)\propto{\rm e}^{-\mu(X-v\tau)}
. This predicts a relation

v(\mu)=\frac{\log\cosh(\mu a)}{\mu a}+\frac{1}{\mu a}\log\!\left[\!1+\frac{a}{2}\left(\sqrt{(4+\kappa-\kappa^{*})^{2}+4\frac{\tanh(\mu a)^{2}}{a^{2}}\Big(1-a(\kappa-\kappa^{*})-2a^{2}(2+\kappa-\kappa^{*})\Big)}\!-\!(\kappa-\kappa^{*})\right)\right],

(143)

which reduces to
Eq. (
128
)
when
a\to 0
.
