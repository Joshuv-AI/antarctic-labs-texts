Convergence and Regularization Properties of Gradient Descent for Deep Residual Networks Alain Rossier’s research was supported through EPSRC Centre for Doctoral Training in Mathematics of Random Systems: Analysis, Modelling and Simulation (EP/S023925/1).
Rama Cont, Alain Rossier, Renyuan Xu

Abstract
We prove linear convergence of gradient descent to a global optimum for the training of deep residual networks with constant layer width and smooth activation function. We show that if the trained weights, as a function of the layer index, admit a scaling limit as the depth increases, then the limit has finite p − p- variation with p = 2 p=2 . Proofs are based on non-asymptotic estimates for the loss function and for norms of the network weights along the gradient descent path. We illustrate the relevance of our theoretical results to practical settings using detailed numerical experiments on supervised learning problems.



1
Introduction

Whether gradient descent methods find globally optimal solutions in the training of neural networks and how trained neural networks generalize are two major open questions in the theory of deep learning. The non-convexity of the loss functions for neural network training may lead to sub-optimal solutions when applying gradient descent methods. It is thus relevant to understand from a theoretical point of view whether specific neural network architectures with a proper choice of learning rates for gradient descent methods can improve the optimization landscape and/or eliminate sub-optimal solutions
[
23
]
. There is some empirical evidence that gradient descent seems to select solutions that generalize well
[
28
]
even without any
explicit
regularization. Hence, it is believed that gradient descent induces an implicit regularization
[
21
]
and characterizing the nature of this regularization is an interesting research question.

In the present work we prove linear convergence of gradient descent to a global minimum for a class of deep residual networks with constant layer width and smooth activation function. Furthermore, we show that under practical assumptions, the trained weights admit a scaling limit as a function of the layer index which has finite
2
-variation. Our result shows that how implicit regularization emerges from gradient descent.
Our proofs are based on non-asymptotic estimates for the loss function and norms of the network weights along the gradient descent path. These non-asymptotic estimates are interesting in their own right and may prove useful to other researchers for the study of dynamics of learning algorithms.

1.1
Convergence and regularization properties of deep learning algorithms

Existing results on convergence and implicit regularization in deep learning exploit three paradigms: over-parametrized neural networks with fixed depth and large width, linear neural networks with sufficiently large depth, and mean-field residual networks.

Under sufficient over-parametrization by width with
fixed depth
, many popular neural network architectures (including feed-forward, convolutional, and residual) with ReLU activation find a global optimum in linear time with respect to the remaining error and the trained network generalizes well
[
1
,
2
]
. However, the associated generalization bounds are intractable, and the amount of over-parametrization implied in these results is often unrealistically large. One can improve the asymptotic analysis
[
29
,
30
]
, but it still falls short of leading to any practical insight. For smooth activation functions,
[
10
]
studied the convergence of gradient descent for various network architectures, including residual networks. They show that for any depth, if the residual layers are wide enough and the learning rate is small enough, gradient descent on the empirical mean-squared loss converges to a solution with zero training loss in linear time. The rate of convergence is proportional to the learning rate and the minimum eigenvalue of the
Gram matrix
.
[
11
]
showed that in the over-parametrized regime, for a suitable initialization with the last layer initialized at zero and other weights initialized uniformly, gradient descent can find a global minimum exponentially fast with high probability.

For
linear
deep neural networks (i.e. with identity activation function),
[
4
]
showed that training with gradient descent is able to learn the positive definite linear transformations using identity initialization.
[
25
]
proposed a new initialization scheme named zero-asymmetric (ZAS) and proved that that under such initialization, for an arbitrary target matrix, gradient descent converges to an
\epsilon
-optimal point in
\mathcal{O}(L^{3}\log(1/\epsilon))
iterations, which scales polynomially with the network depth
L
. Subsequent refinements of the convergence rates and the width requirements have been established in
[
9
,
31
]
. Finally,
[
27
]
showed the implicit regularization of gradient descent for linear fully-connected networks to
l_{2}
max-margin solutions.

Another line of work deals with mean-field residual networks by looking at the continuum limit of residual networks when either the depth
L
or the width
d
goes to infinity.
[
26
]
build on the analysis of
[
8
]
for feed-forward networks to study the average behaviour of randomly initialized residual networks with width tending to infinity. They show that a careful initialization, depending on the depth, may enhance expressivity. Further,
[
20
]
proposed a continuum limit of deep residual networks by letting the depth
L
tends to infinity and showed that every local minimum of the loss landscape is global. This characterization enables them to derive the first global convergence result for multi-layer neural networks in the mean-field regime.

In addition to the network architectures listed above, non-linear neural networks with fixed width and
large but finite
depth are successful and practically more popular
[
15
,
16
]
. It is well-documented that for a fixed number of parameters, going deeper allows the models to capture richer structures
[
12
,
24
]
. However, the theoretical foundations for such networks remain widely open due to their complex training landscape.

1.2
Contributions

We consider a supervised learning problem where we seek to learn an unknown mapping with inputs and outputs in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
using a residual network with constant width
d
and a smooth activation function.
We study the convergence and implicit regularization of gradient descent for the mean-squared error.

•

Linear convergence.
For
\epsilon>0
, we prove that for a residual network of depth
L=\Omega(1/\epsilon)
, we can choose a learning rate schedule such that gradient descent on the training loss converges to a
\epsilon
-optimal solution in
\Theta(\log(1/\epsilon))
iterations.

•

Scaling limit of trained weights.
The trained weights, as a function of the layer, may admit a scaling limit as
L\to\infty
. We prove that such a scaling limit is a matrix-valued function with finite
2
-variation.

•

Non-asymptotic estimates on loss function and weights along the gradient descent path.
In addition to the convergence results mentioned above, we obtain (non-asymptotic) estimates along the gradient descent path for the loss function and various norms of the weights, with tractable bounds.

•

Relevance to practical settings.
We illustrate the relevance of our theoretical results in practical settings using detailed numerical experiments with networks of realistic width and depth.

Our analysis generalizes previous results on
linear
neural networks
[
25
]
to a more general nonlinear setting relevant for learning problems. Our non-asymptotic results stand in contrast to the mean-field analysis
[
20
]
which requires infinite depth. Our tractable bounds improve upon the ones found for networks over-parametrized by width
[
2
,
10
,
11
,
29
,
30
]
, where the trained weights do not leave the lazy training regime
[
6
]
: in our setting, the trained weights are not necessarily staying close to their initialization. A key ingredient in the proof is to study the evolution of various norms for the weights under gradient descent iterations. These estimates are provided in Lemmas
3.3
and
3.4
.

Our theoretical results suggest that initialization of weights at scale
L^{-1}
together with a
L^{-1/2}
scaling of the activation function leads to convergence under a constant learning rate. The overarching principle is to make sure that the gradient stays on the same scale as the weights (here
L^{-1/2}
) during training.
Our analysis also extends, with minimal changes, to the case where linear layers are added at the beginning and the end of the network.

Next, if we assume that the weights
\alpha^{(L)}
are close to each other in neighbouring layers, we can deduce that the gradient of the loss with respect to weights in neighbouring layers are also close to each other. Hence, if we couple this fact with Lemma
3.1
, we can prove a lower bound on the norm of the gradient of the loss with respect to the full weight vector
\alpha^{(L)}
.

Lemma 3.2
.

Under Assumption
2.1
(i)–(iii), let
\alpha^{(L)}\in\mathbb{R}^{L\times d\times d}
such that
L\geq\max(5c_{0},4c_{0}^{2})
,
\left\lVert\alpha^{(L)}\right\rVert_{F,\infty}\leq c_{0}L^{-1/2}
, and
\left\lVert\alpha^{(L)}_{k+1}-\alpha^{(L)}_{k}\right\rVert_{F}\leq 2^{-7/2}N^{-1/2}e^{-4.2c_{0}}L^{-1}
for each
k
. Then,

\left\lVert\nabla_{\alpha^{(L)}}J_{L}\hskip-2.0pt\left(\alpha^{(L)}\right)\right\rVert_{F}^{2}\geq\left(\frac{1}{16}N^{-1}e^{-2c_{0}}-17dc_{0}^{4}e^{6.4c_{0}}L^{-1}\right)J_{L}(\alpha).

Proof.

Fix
L\geq\max(5c_{0},4c_{0}^{2})
. In the proof, we omit the explicit dependence in
L
. We use Lemma
D.1
to estimate the difference of neighbouring gradients:

\displaystyle\frac{\partial J_{L}}{\partial\alpha_{k,mn}}-\frac{\partial J_{L}}{\partial\alpha_{k+1,mn}}

\displaystyle=\frac{\delta_{L}}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}h_{k-1,n}^{x_{i}}\left(\dot{\sigma}_{k,x_{i},m}-\dot{\sigma}_{k+1,x_{i},m}\right)\nabla_{\widehat{y}}\,\ell\left(y_{i},\widehat{y}(x_{i},\alpha)\right)^{\top}M_{k+1}^{x_{i}}e_{m}

\displaystyle+\frac{\delta_{L}^{2}}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\nabla_{\widehat{y}}\,\ell\left(y_{i},\widehat{y}(x_{i},\alpha)\right)^{\top}M_{k+1}^{x_{i}}\xi^{x_{i},\,(L)}_{k,mn},

where
\xi^{x,\,(L)}_{k,mn}
satisfies

\left\lVert\xi^{x,\,(L)}_{k,mn}\right\rVert_{2}^{2}\leq 2\left(h^{x}_{k-1,n}\right)^{2}\left\lVert\alpha_{k+1}-\alpha_{k}\right\rVert_{F}^{2}+2\left\lVert\alpha_{k,n}\right\rVert_{2}^{4}\left\lVert h_{k-1}^{x}\right\rVert_{2}^{4}.

By Lemma
B.1
and the fact that
\sigma^{\prime}
is
1-
Lipschitz by Assumption
2.1

(i)
, we bound further:

\displaystyle\left\lVert\nabla_{\alpha_{k+1}}J_{L}\left(\alpha\right)-\nabla_{\alpha_{k}}J_{L}\left(\alpha\right)\right\rVert_{F}^{2}=\sum_{m,n=1}^{d}\left(\frac{\partial J_{L}}{\partial\alpha_{k,mn}}-\frac{\partial J_{L}}{\partial\alpha_{k+1,mn}}\right)^{2}

\displaystyle\leq 4\sum_{m,n=1}^{d}\frac{1}{LN}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\left(h_{k-1,n}^{x_{i}}\right)^{2}\left\lVert M^{x_{i}}_{k}e_{m}\right\rVert_{2}^{2}\left(\alpha_{k}h^{x_{i}}_{k-1}-\alpha_{k+1}h^{x_{i}}_{k}\right)_{m}^{2}\ell(y_{i},\widehat{y}(x_{i},\alpha))

\displaystyle\qquad+\frac{4}{L^{2}N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}e^{2c_{0}}\left(2de^{2.2c_{0}}\left\lVert\alpha_{k+1}-\alpha_{k}\right\rVert_{F}^{2}+2dc_{0}^{4}e^{4.4c_{0}}L^{-2}\right)\ell(y_{i},\widehat{y}(x_{i},\alpha))

\displaystyle\leq e^{4.2c_{0}}\frac{4}{LN}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\left\lVert\alpha_{k}h^{x_{i}}_{k-1}-\alpha_{k+1}h^{x_{i}}_{k}\right\rVert_{2}^{2}\ell(y_{i},\widehat{y}(x_{i},\alpha))+9dc_{0}^{4}e^{6.4c_{0}}L^{-4}J_{L}(\alpha).

Then, simply note that

\displaystyle\left\lVert\alpha_{k}h^{x_{i}}_{k-1}-\alpha_{k+1}h^{x_{i}}_{k}\right\rVert_{2}^{2}

\displaystyle\leq 2\left\lVert\left(\alpha_{k+1}-\alpha_{k}\right)h^{x_{i}}_{k}\right\rVert_{2}^{2}+2\left\lVert\alpha_{k}\left(h^{x_{i}}_{k}-h^{x_{i}}_{k-1}\right)\right\rVert_{2}^{2}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{1}{64}N^{-1}e^{-6.2c_{0}}L^{-2}+2c_{0}^{4}e^{2.2c_{0}}L^{-3}.

Hence,

\left\lVert\nabla_{\alpha_{k+1}}J_{L}\left(\alpha\right)-\nabla_{\alpha_{k}}J_{L}\left(\alpha\right)\right\rVert_{F}^{2}\leq\left(\frac{1}{16}N^{-1}e^{-2c_{0}}+17dc_{0}^{4}e^{6.4c_{0}}L^{-1}\right)L^{-3}J_{L}(\alpha).

Finally, we use the reverse triangle inequality and Cauchy-Schwarz inequality:

\displaystyle\left\lVert\nabla_{\alpha_{k}}J_{L}\left(\alpha\right)\right\rVert_{F}^{2}

\displaystyle\geq\frac{1}{2}\left\lVert\nabla_{\alpha_{1}}J_{L}\left(\alpha\right)\right\rVert_{F}^{2}-(k-1)\sum_{k^{\prime}=1}^{k-1}\left\lVert\nabla_{\alpha_{k+1}}J_{L}\left(\alpha\right)-\nabla_{\alpha_{k}}J_{L}\left(\alpha\right)\right\rVert_{F}^{2}

\displaystyle\geq\frac{1}{8}N^{-1}e^{-2c_{0}}L^{-1}J_{L}(\alpha)-\frac{(k-1)^{2}}{L^{3}}\left(\frac{1}{16}N^{-1}e^{-2c_{0}}+17dc_{0}^{4}e^{6.4c_{0}}L^{-1}\right)J_{L}(\alpha)

\displaystyle\geq\left(\frac{1}{16}N^{-1}e^{-2c_{0}}-17dc_{0}^{4}e^{6.4c_{0}}L^{-1}\right)L^{-1}J_{L}(\alpha).

The second inequality holds by Lemma
3.1
and
(i)
above. Hence,

\left\lVert\nabla_{\alpha}J_{L}\hskip-2.0pt\left(\alpha^{(L)}\right)\right\rVert_{F}^{2}=\sum_{k=1}^{L}\left\lVert\nabla_{\alpha_{k}}J_{L}(\alpha^{(L)})\right\rVert_{F}^{2}\geq\left(\frac{1}{16}N^{-1}e^{-2c_{0}}-17dc_{0}^{4}e^{6.4c_{0}}L^{-1}\right)J_{L}(\alpha).

∎

It guarantees that for
L\gg 1
, every critical point close to the origin is a global minimum of the objective function, similarly to what is known for linear residual networks

J_{L}\hskip-2.0pt\left(A^{(L)}(T_{L}^{\,\mathrm{decay}})\right)<\epsilon
in
\Theta(\exp(\eta_{0}^{-1}\log 1/\epsilon))

The above convergence rates above are confirmed by our experiments in Section

. Note that gradient descent converges exponentially faster when using constant learning rates rather than decaying ones. This is because the parameters

and the gradients

are already on the same scale

. Note also that Theorem

is not in contradiction with

stating that gradient descent might get stuck at the critical point

that is usually not a global minimizer. Indeed, we force

to have a non-trivial scaling by Assumption

is simply not a point in the parameter space.

Scaling limit of trained weights

In many cases the trained weights, viewed as a function of the layer index

We show that such a limit then admits finite

Proposition 3.7
.

Let

Proof.

Fix a partition
\pi=\left\{0=s_{0}<s_{1}<\ldots<s_{K}=1\right\}
, where the mesh of the partition
\left\lVert\pi\right\rVert
is small enough. In the following,

and

5
Conclusion

We prove linear convergence of gradient descent to a global minimum of the training loss for deep residual networks with constant layer width and smooth activation function. We further show that if the trained weights, as a function of the layer index, admits a scaling limit as the depth of the network tends to infinity, then it has finite
2-
variation.

A natural question to investigate next is the generalization capability of the trained weights obtained by gradient descent, which we characterize in this work. Indeed, it is still an open question whether the weights obtained by gradient descent admit the tightest generalization gap among all the other global minima. Also, our work can be generalized to study other residual architectures (for example with ReLU activation) by looking at alternative norms along the gradient descent path.

Gradient of the loss function with respect to parameters

Let

and
\alpha^{(L)}\in\mathbb{R}^{L\times d\times d}

\ell(y,\widehat{y}(x,\alpha^{(L)}))
with respect to the network parameters
\{\alpha^{(L)}_{k}:k=1,\ldots,L\}

and

. We first observe that

\displaystyle\frac{\partial\ell}{\partial\alpha^{(L)}_{k,mn}}\left(y,\widehat{y}(x,\alpha^{(L)})\right)

\displaystyle=\nabla_{\widehat{y}}\,\ell\left(y,\widehat{y}\left(x,\alpha^{(L)}\right)\right)^{\top}\frac{\partial h_{L}^{(L)}}{\partial h^{(L)}_{k}}\frac{\partial h_{k}^{(L)}}{\partial\alpha^{(L)}_{k,mn}}.

By induction, we obtain

\displaystyle M_{k}^{(L)}\coloneqq\frac{\partial h_{L}^{(L)}}{\partial h_{k}^{(L)}}

\displaystyle=\prod_{j=k+1}^{L}\frac{\partial h_{j}^{(L)}}{\partial h_{j-1}^{(L)}}

\displaystyle=\prod_{j=k+1}^{L}\left(I_{d}+\delta_{L}\frac{\partial}{\partial h_{j-1}^{(L)}}\sigma_{d}\left(\alpha^{(L)}_{j}h_{j-1}^{(L)}\right)\right)

\displaystyle=\prod_{j=k+1}^{L}\left(I_{d}+\delta_{L}\mathrm{diag}\left(\nabla\sigma_{d}\left(\alpha^{(L)}_{j}h_{j-1}^{(L)}\right)\right)\alpha^{(L)}_{j}\right).

(A.1)

We also have

\frac{\partial h_{k}^{(L)}}{\partial\alpha^{(L)}_{k,mn}}=\delta_{L}\sigma^{\prime}\left(\left(\alpha^{(L)}_{k}h_{k-1}^{(L)}\right)_{m}\right)h_{k-1,n}^{(L)}e_{m}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}.

. Regrouping everything, we get

\frac{\partial\ell}{\partial\alpha^{(L)}_{k,mn}}=\delta_{L}\,h_{k-1,n}^{(L)}\,\dot{\sigma}^{(L)}_{k,m}\,\nabla_{\widehat{y}}\,\ell\left(y,\widehat{y}\left(x,\alpha^{(L)}\right)\right)^{\top}M_{k}^{(L)}e_{m}.

(A.2)

Appendix B
Boundedness of hidden states and Jacobians

This section contains two useful results for our analysis.

Lemma B.1
.

Let
\alpha^{(L)}\in\mathbb{R}^{L\times d\times d}
and
c_{\alpha}>0
such that
L\geq 5c_{\alpha}
and

\left\lVert\alpha^{(L)}\right\rVert_{F,\infty}=\max_{k=1,\ldots,L}\left\lVert\alpha^{(L)}_{k}\right\rVert_{F}\leq c_{\alpha}L^{-1/2}.

2.1
(i)–(ii), we have that for all

\left\lVert x\right\rVert_{2}e^{-2c_{\alpha}}\leq\left\lVert h^{x,\,(L)}_{k}\right\rVert_{2}\leq\left\lVert x\right\rVert_{2}e^{1.1c_{\alpha}}\hskip 28.45274pt\mbox{and}\hskip 28.45274pt\left\lVert M^{x,\,(L)}_{k}e_{m}\right\rVert_{2}\leq e^{c_{\alpha}}.

Proof.

We follow the same lines as

\displaystyle\Delta_{j}

\displaystyle\leq 2\delta_{L}\left\lVert\alpha_{j}\right\rVert_{F}+\delta_{L}^{2}\left\lVert\alpha_{j}\right\rVert_{F}^{2}

2.1

(ii)

\nabla^{2}_{\alpha}J_{L}\left(\alpha^{(L)}\right)=H_{\mathrm{psd}}+H+\widetilde{H}+\mathcal{O}\left(L^{-1/2}\right),

where
H_{\mathrm{psd}},H,\widetilde{H}\in\mathbb{R}^{Ld^{2}\times Ld^{2}}
are given by the following formulae:

H_{\mathrm{psd}}=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}H^{x_{i}}_{\mathrm{psd}}\quad H=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}H^{x_{i}}\quad\widetilde{H}=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\widetilde{H}^{x_{i}},

where

\displaystyle\left(H^{x_{i}}_{\mathrm{psd}}\right)_{(k,\,mn)\,(k^{\prime},\,m^{\prime}n^{\prime})}

\displaystyle=\delta_{L}^{2}h^{x_{i}}_{k-1,n}h^{x_{i}}_{k^{\prime}-1,n^{\prime}}\dot{\sigma}_{k,x_{i},m}\dot{\sigma}_{k^{\prime},x_{i},m^{\prime}}\left(M^{x_{i}}_{k}e_{m}\right)^{\top}\left(M^{x_{i}}_{k^{\prime}}e_{m^{\prime}}\right)

\displaystyle H^{x_{i}}_{(k,\,mn)\,(k^{\prime},\,m^{\prime}n^{\prime})}

\displaystyle=\delta_{L}h^{x_{i}}_{k-1,n}h^{x_{i}}_{k-1,n^{\prime}}\ddot{\sigma}_{k,x_{i},m}\left(\widehat{y}(x_{i},\alpha)-y_{i}\right)^{\top}M_{k}^{x_{i}}e_{m}\mathbbm{1}_{m=m^{\prime}}\mathbbm{1}_{k=k^{\prime}}

\displaystyle\widetilde{H}^{x_{i}}_{(k,\,mn)\,(k^{\prime},\,m^{\prime}n^{\prime})}

\displaystyle=\delta_{L}^{2}h^{x_{i}}_{k-1,n}\dot{\sigma}_{k,x_{i},m}\dot{\sigma}_{k,x_{i},m^{\prime}}\left(\widehat{y}(x_{i},\alpha)-y_{i}\right)^{\top}M_{k,-k^{\prime}}^{x_{i}}e_{m}\mathbbm{1}_{m^{\prime}=n^{\prime}}\mathbbm{1}_{k<k^{\prime}}.

Here,
M_{k,-k^{\prime}}^{x_{i}}
is defined as the same product of matrices as
M_{k}^{x_{i}}
in (
A.1
), but without the term
j=k^{\prime}
. By the same reasoning as in Lemma
B.1
, we still have
\left\lVert M_{k,-k^{\prime}}^{x_{i}}e_{m}\right\rVert_{2}\leq e^{c_{\alpha}}
.

We readily see that for each
i
there exists
Q_{i}
such that
H^{x_{i}}_{\mathrm{psd}}=Q_{i}^{\top}Q_{i}
, so
H_{\mathrm{psd}}
is positive semi-definite. The trace of
H_{\mathrm{psd}}
is straightforward to compute.

\displaystyle\mathrm{tr}\left(H_{\mathrm{psd}}\right)=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\mathrm{tr}\left(H^{x_{i}}_{\mathrm{psd}}\right)

\displaystyle=\frac{\delta_{L}^{2}}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\sum_{k,m,n}\left|h_{k-1,n}^{x_{i}}\right|^{2}\left(\dot{\sigma}_{k,x_{i},m}\right)^{2}\left\lVert M^{x_{i}}_{k}e_{m}\right\rVert_{2}^{2}.

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}L^{-1}\sum_{k=1}^{L}\left\lVert h_{k-1}^{x_{i}}\right\rVert_{2}^{2}\left\lVert M^{x_{i}}_{k}\right\rVert_{F}^{2}.

We deduce that by Lemma
B.1
that
\mathrm{tr}\left(H_{\mathrm{psd}}\right)\leq d^{2}e^{4.2c_{\alpha}}
.

The upper bound on the Frobenius norm of
H
and
\widetilde{H}
is no harder.

\displaystyle\left\lVert H^{x_{i}}\right\rVert^{2}_{F}

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{k=1}^{L}\sum_{m=1}^{d}\delta_{L}^{2}\left\lVert h_{k-1}^{x_{i}}\right\rVert_{2}^{4}\ell(y_{i},\widehat{y}(x_{i},\alpha))\left\lVert M^{x_{i}}_{k}e_{m}\right\rVert_{2}^{2}\leq de^{6.4c_{\alpha}}\ell(y_{i},\widehat{y}(x_{i},\alpha)),

\displaystyle\left\lVert\widetilde{H}^{x_{i}}\right\rVert_{F}^{2}

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{k\neq k^{\prime}}\delta_{L}^{4}\left\lVert h_{k-1}^{x_{i}}\right\rVert_{2}^{2}\ell(y_{i},\widehat{y}(x_{i},\alpha))d^{2}e^{2c_{\alpha}}\leq d^{2}e^{4.2c_{\alpha}}\ell(y_{i},\widehat{y}(x_{i},\alpha)).

Hence,
\left\lVert H\right\rVert_{F}\leq\sqrt{2d}e^{3.2c_{\alpha}}J_{L}(\alpha)^{1/2}
and
\lVert\widetilde{H}\rVert_{F}\leq\sqrt{2}de^{2.1c_{\alpha}}J_{L}(\alpha)^{1/2}
. Using Corollary
B.2
and wrapping both terms together, we get

\displaystyle\left\lVert\nabla^{2}_{\alpha}J\left(\alpha^{(L)}\right)\right\rVert_{2}

\displaystyle\leq\left\lVert H_{\mathrm{psd}}\right\rVert_{2}+\left\lVert H\right\rVert_{2}+\lVert\widetilde{H}\rVert_{2}+\mathcal{O}(L^{-1/2})

\displaystyle\leq\mathrm{tr}(H_{\mathrm{psd}})^{1/2}+\left\lVert H\right\rVert_{F}+\lVert\widetilde{H}\rVert_{F}+\mathcal{O}(L^{-1/2})

\displaystyle\leq 5de^{4.3c_{\alpha}}.

∎

that

\displaystyle\left\lVert\mathrm{vec}(\alpha(t))\right\rVert_{2}=\left\lVert\alpha(t)\right\rVert_{F}

\displaystyle=\left(\sum_{k=1}^{L}\left\lVert\alpha_{k}(t)\right\rVert_{F}^{2}\right)^{1/2}\leq c_{\alpha}(t),

\displaystyle\underline{c}(t)J_{L}(\alpha(t))-\mkern 1.0mu\overline{\mkern-1.5muc\mkern 0.0mu}\mkern 1.0mu(t)L^{-1}

\displaystyle=\left\lVert\nabla_{\alpha}J_{L}(\alpha(t))\right\rVert_{F}^{2}\leq 2e^{4.2c_{\alpha}(t)}J_{L}(\alpha(t)).

We want to use Lemma
G.3
with
p=Ld^{2}
,
R=c_{\alpha}
,

x_{0}=\alpha(t)
and
x=\alpha(t)-\eta_{L}(t)\nabla_{\alpha}J_{L}(\alpha(t))
. For this, we need to check two assumptions. The first is an upper bound on the spectral norm of the Hessian of
J_{L}
, which we get from Lemma
C.2
.

H_{\infty}(t)=\sup_{\left\lVert\alpha^{\prime}\right\rVert_{F}\leq\,c_{\alpha}(t)}\left\lVert\nabla^{2}J_{L}(\alpha^{\prime})\right\rVert_{2}\leq 5de^{4.3c_{\alpha}(t)}.

The second is an upper bound on the norm of
x-x_{0}=-\eta_{L}(t)\nabla_{\alpha}J_{L}(\alpha(t))
, which we get from Lemma
C.1
.

\displaystyle\eta_{L}(t)\left\lVert\nabla_{\alpha}J_{L}(\alpha(t))\right\rVert_{2}

\displaystyle\leq\sqrt{2}\eta_{L}(t)e^{2.1c_{\alpha}(t)}J_{L}(\alpha(t))^{1/2}

\displaystyle\leq\sqrt{2}\eta_{L}(t)e^{2.1c_{\alpha}(t)}\left(1+e^{2.2c_{\alpha}(t)}\right)^{1/2}

\displaystyle\leq 2\eta_{L}(t)e^{3.2c_{\alpha}(t)}\leq c_{\alpha}(t),

where the second inequality comes from Corollary
B.2
and the third inequality from the fact that
(1+z)^{1/2}\leq(2z)^{1/2}
for
z\geq 1
. Hence, we can apply Lemma
G.3
and deduce that

\displaystyle J_{L}\left(\alpha(t+1)\right)

\displaystyle=J_{L}\Big(\alpha(t)-\eta_{L}(t)\nabla_{\alpha}J_{L}(\alpha(t))\Big)-J_{L}(\alpha(t))

\displaystyle\leq J_{L}\left(\alpha(t)\right)-\eta_{L}(t)\left\lVert\nabla_{\alpha}J_{L}(\alpha(t))\right\rVert_{F}^{2}+\frac{1}{2}H_{\infty}(t)\eta_{L}(t)^{2}\left\lVert\nabla_{\alpha}J_{L}(\alpha(t))\right\rVert_{2}^{2}

\displaystyle\leq\left(1-\underline{c}(t)\eta_{L}(t)+5d\eta_{L}(t)^{2}e^{8.5c_{\alpha}(t)}\right)J_{L}\left(\alpha(t)\right)+\mkern 1.0mu\overline{\mkern-1.5muc\mkern 0.0mu}\mkern 1.0mu(t)\eta_{L}(t)L^{-1}.

To finish the proof, we apply Lemma
G.4

(i)
with

u_{L}(t)\coloneqq\underline{c}(t)\eta_{L}(t)-5d\eta_{L}(t)^{2}e^{8.5c_{\alpha}(t)}\geq\frac{1}{2}\underline{c}(t)\eta_{L}(t)>0,

and the fact that
1-x\leq e^{-x}
. Hence,

\displaystyle J_{L}(\alpha(T))

\displaystyle\leq\exp\left(-\sum_{t=0}^{T-1}u_{L}(t)\right)J_{L}(\alpha(0))+L^{-1}\sum_{t=0}^{T-1}\mkern 1.0mu\overline{\mkern-1.5muc\mkern 0.0mu}\mkern 1.0mu(t)\eta_{L}(t)

\displaystyle\leq\exp\left(-\frac{1}{2}\sum_{t=0}^{T-1}\underline{c}(t)\eta_{L}(t)\right)J_{L}(\alpha(0))+L^{-1}\sum_{t=0}^{T-1}\mkern 1.0mu\overline{\mkern-1.5muc\mkern 0.0mu}\mkern 1.0mu(t)\eta_{L}(t).

∎

Appendix G
Auxiliary results

Lemma G.1
.

For any
A\in\mathbb{R}^{m\times n}
and
B\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times p}
, we have

\left\lVert AB\right\rVert_{F}\leq\left\lVert A\right\rVert_{2}\left\lVert B\right\rVert_{F}.

Proof.

Let
B=\left[b_{1},\ldots,b_{p}\right]
the columns of
B
. Then
\left\lVert B\right\rVert_{F}^{2}=\sum_{i=1}^{p}\left\lVert b_{i}\right\rVert_{2}^{2}
. We use the fact that the spectral norm is compatible with the Euclidian norm to deduce

\left\lVert AB\right\rVert_{F}^{2}=\sum_{i=1}^{p}\left\lVert Ab_{i}\right\rVert_{2}^{2}\leq\sum_{i=1}^{p}\left\lVert A\right\rVert^{2}_{2}\left\lVert b_{i}\right\rVert^{2}_{2}=\left\lVert A\right\rVert^{2}_{2}\left\lVert B\right\rVert_{F}^{2}.

∎

Lemma G.2
.

Let
x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
and
\left\{A_{k}:k=1,\ldots,L\right\}\subset\mathbb{R}^{d\times d}
such that
\max_{k}\left\lVert A_{k}\right\rVert_{2}<1
. Then

\left\lVert\left[\prod_{k=1}^{L}(I_{d}+A_{k})\right]x\,\right\rVert_{2}\geq\left\lVert x\right\rVert_{2}\prod_{k=1}^{L}(1-\left\lVert A_{k}\right\rVert_{2}).

Proof.

First observe that for
A,B\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times d}
and
x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
, we have
\left\lVert ABx\right\rVert_{2}\geq\sigma_{\min}(A)\left\lVert Bx\right\rVert_{2}
, where
\sigma_{\min}(A)
is the smallest singular value of
A
. This is easy to see, as
\sigma_{\min}(A)^{2}
is the smallest eigenvalue of
A^{\top}A
, so

\left\lVert ABx\right\rVert_{2}^{2}=(Bx)^{\top}A^{\top}A(Bx)\geq\sigma_{\min}(A)^{2}\left\lVert Bx\right\rVert_{2}^{2}.

Observe also that for all
A\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times d}
with
\left\lVert A\right\rVert_{2}<1
, we have
\sigma_{\min}(I_{d}+A)\geq 1-\left\lVert A\right\rVert_{2}>0
. Indeed, there exists
v\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
such that
\left\lVert v\right\rVert_{2}=1
and
v^{\top}(I_{d}+A)v=\sigma_{\min}(I_{d}+A)^{2}
. Hence,

\sigma_{\min}(I_{d}+A)=\left(1+v^{\top}Av\right)^{1/2}\geq\left(1-\left\lVert A\right\rVert_{2}\right)^{1/2}\geq 1-\left\lVert A\right\rVert_{2}.

Combining these two facts, we deduce that

\left\lVert\left[\prod_{k=1}^{L}(I_{d}+A_{k})\right]x\,\right\rVert_{2}\geq\left\lVert x\right\rVert_{2}\prod_{k=1}^{L}\sigma_{\min}(I_{d}+A_{k})\geq\left\lVert x\right\rVert_{2}\prod_{k=1}^{L}(1-\left\lVert A_{k}\right\rVert_{2}).

∎

Lemma G.3
.

Let
f\in C^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{p})
satisfying
\sup_{\left\lVert x\right\rVert_{2}<R}\left\lVert\nabla^{2}f(x)\right\rVert_{2}\leq H_{\infty}
for some
H_{\infty},R>0
. Then, for all
x\in\mathbb{R}^{p}
such that
\left\lVert x-x_{0}\right\rVert_{2}<R
,

\Big|f(x)-f(x_{0})-\langle\nabla_{x}f(x),x-x_{0}\rangle\Big|\leq\frac{H_{\infty}}{2}\left\lVert x-x_{0}\right\rVert_{2}^{2}.

Proof.

We apply the fundamental theorem of calculus for line integrals between
x_{0}
and
x
:

f(x)-f(x_{0})=\int_{0}^{1}\big\langle\nabla_{x}f(x_{0}+t(x-x_{0})),x-x_{0}\big\rangle\mathrm{d}t.

Hence, by Cauchy-Schwartz inequality and by hypothesis,

\displaystyle\Big|f(x)-f(x_{0})-\big\langle\nabla_{x}f(x_{0}),x-x_{0}\big\rangle\Big|

\displaystyle\leq\int_{0}^{1}\left\lVert\nabla_{x}f(x_{0}+t(x-x_{0}))-\nabla_{x}f(x_{0})\right\rVert_{2}\left\lVert x-x_{0}\right\rVert_{2}\mathrm{d}t

\displaystyle\leq\int_{0}^{1}H_{\infty}\left\lVert t(x-x_{0})\right\rVert_{2}\left\lVert x-x_{0}\right\rVert_{2}\mathrm{d}t

\displaystyle=\frac{H_{\infty}}{2}\left\lVert x-x_{0}\right\rVert_{2}^{2}.

∎

Lemma G.4

(Discrete Grönwall inequalities)
.

Let
(u_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}},(v_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}},(w_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}\subset\mathbb{R}_{>0}
. Then

(i)

If
e_{n+1}\leq u_{n}e_{n}+v_{n}
for each
n\geq 0
, then

e_{n}\leq\left(\prod_{n^{\prime}=0}^{n-1}u_{n^{\prime}}\right)e_{0}+\sum_{n^{\prime}=0}^{n-1}\left(\prod_{n^{\prime\prime}=n^{\prime}+1}^{n-1}u_{n^{\prime\prime}}\right)v_{n^{\prime}}.

(ii)

If
g_{0}>0
and
0<g_{n+1}\leq u_{n}g_{n}+w_{n}g_{n}^{1/2}
, then

g_{n}^{1/2}\leq\left(\prod_{n^{\prime}=0}^{n-1}u_{n}^{1/2}\right)g_{0}^{1/2}+\frac{1}{2}\sum_{n^{\prime}=0}^{n-1}\left(\prod_{n^{\prime\prime}=n^{\prime}+1}^{n-1}u_{n^{\prime\prime}}^{1/2}\right)\frac{w_{n^{\prime}}}{u_{n^{\prime}}^{1/2}}.

The first inequality is well-known, but we give proofs for both, for the sake of completeness.

Proof.

To prove (i), we start by defining
\widetilde{e}_{n}=\left(\prod_{n^{\prime}=0}^{n-1}u_{n^{\prime}}\right)^{-1}e_{n}
. Then,

\widetilde{e}_{n+1}-\widetilde{e}_{n}=\left(\prod_{n^{\prime}=0}^{n}u_{n^{\prime}}\right)^{-1}\left(e_{n+1}-u_{n}e_{n}\right)\leq\left(\prod_{n^{\prime}=0}^{n}u_{n^{\prime}}\right)^{-1}v_{n}.

Hence, summing over
n
, we get

\displaystyle e_{n}=\left(\prod_{n^{\prime}=0}^{n-1}u_{n^{\prime}}\right)\widetilde{e}_{n}

\displaystyle\leq\left(\prod_{n^{\prime}=0}^{n-1}u_{n^{\prime}}\right)\left(e_{0}+\sum_{n^{\prime}=0}^{n-1}\left(\prod_{n^{\prime\prime}=0}^{n^{\prime}}u_{n^{\prime\prime}}\right)^{-1}v_{n^{\prime}}\right)

\displaystyle=\left(\prod_{n^{\prime}=0}^{n-1}u_{n^{\prime}}\right)e_{0}+\sum_{n^{\prime}=0}^{n-1}\left(\prod_{n^{\prime\prime}=n^{\prime}+1}^{n-1}u_{n^{\prime\prime}}\right)v_{n^{\prime}}.

To prove (ii), we simply complete the square:
u_{n}g_{n}+w_{n}g_{n}^{1/2}\leq u_{n}\left(g_{n}^{1/2}+\frac{w_{n}}{2u_{n}}\right)^{2}
. Hence,

g_{n+1}^{1/2}\leq u_{n}^{1/2}g_{n}^{1/2}+\frac{w_{n}}{2u_{n}^{1/2}}.

We can thus apply part (i) to
e_{n}=g_{n}^{1/2}
to deduce the result.

∎
